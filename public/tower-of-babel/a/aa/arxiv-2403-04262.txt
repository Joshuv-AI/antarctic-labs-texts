Coderivative-Based Newton Methods in Structured Nonconvex and Nonsmooth Optimization
Pham Duy Khanh, Boris S. Mordukhovich, Vo Thanh Phat

Abstract




Abstract
. This paper proposes and develops new Newton-type methods to solve structured nonconvex and nonsmooth optimization problems with justifying their fast local and global convergence by means of advanced tools of variational analysis and generalized differentiation. The objective functions belong to a broad class of prox-regular functions with specification to constrained optimization of nonconvex structured sums. We also develop a novel line search method, which is an extension of the proximal gradient algorithm while allowing us to globalize the proposed coderivative-based Newton methods by incorporating the machinery of forward-backward envelopes. Applications and numerical experiments, which are provided for nonconvex least squares regression models, Student’s t-regression with an
\ell^{0}
-penalty, and image restoration problems, demonstrate the efficiency of the proposed algorithms.

Key words
. Nonsmooth optimization, variational analysis, generalized Newton methods, local convergence, global convergence, nonconvex structured optimization

Introduction

{intro}

For a function
\varphi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightarrow{\rm I\!R}
, which is twice continuously differentiable (
{\cal C}^{2}
-smooth) around a certain point
\bar{x}
, the
classical Newton method
to solve the unconstrained optimization problem

\mbox{\rm minimize}\quad\varphi(x)\quad\text{subject to }\;x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}

(1.1)

starts with a given initial point
x^{0}\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
and constructs the iterative procedure

x^{k+1}:=x^{k}+d^{k}\;\mbox{ for all }\;k\in{\rm I\!N}:=\big\{1,2,\ldots\big\},

(1.2)

where
d^{k}
is a solution to the linear system

-\nabla\varphi(x^{k})=\nabla^{2}\varphi(x^{k})d^{k},\quad k\in{\rm I\!N},

(1.3)

expressed via the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}\varphi(x^{k})
of
\varphi
at
x^{k}
. In classical optimization, Newton’s algorithm in (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) is well-defined, meaning that the equations in (
1.3
) are solvable for
d^{k}
and the sequence of its iterations
{x^{k}}
quadratically converges to the solution
\bar{x}
of (
1.1
) if
x^{0}
is chosen sufficiently close to
\bar{x}
and if the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}\varphi(\bar{x})
is
positive-definite
. However, this method necessitates information on Hessian matrices, which are unavailable in many practical optimization problems where the cost function may not be
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth. Therefore, the development of generalized Newton methods is required with replacing the Hessian matrix by a certain generalized second-order derivative.

Over the years, various generalized Newton methods have been developed in nonsmooth optimization; we refer the reader to the books
[
11
,
19
,
30
]
and the bibliographies therein for more details.

Our approach in this paper is different from the majority of developments on generalized Newton methods in nonsmooth optimization and related areas. It is based on the machinery of variational analysis and generalized differentiation that is revolved around the
second-order subdifferentials/generalized Hessians
introduced by Mordukhovich
[
37
]
in the dual “derivative-of-derivative” scheme by taking a coderivative of a subgradient mapping; see Section
2
. The importance and benefits of such constructions have been well recognized in variational analysis, optimization, and numerous applications (see, e.g., the books
[
39
,
41
]
with the vast bibliographies therein), while their usage in the design and justification of numerical algorithms, as well as in practical applications to machine learning and other disciplines, has been revealed quite recently in

[
1
,
13
,
24
,
26
,
27
,
41
,
43
]
.

The
main goals
of this paper include developing novel coderivative-based generalized Newton methods to solve broad classes of optimization-related problems that are different from the known achievements in this direction by the methodology, design, imposed assumptions, obtained results, and applications. Let us discuss some major issues and their placements in the paper .

\bullet
In Section
3
(after the basic definitions and preliminary results given in Section
2
), we address solving
subgradient inclusions
of the type
0\in\partial\varphi(x)
, where
\partial\varphi
stands for the
limiting subdifferential
of an extended-real-valued, lower semicontinuous (l.s.c.), and prox-bounded function
\varphi\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}:=(-\infty,\infty]
. Using the aforementioned second-order subdifferential
\partial^{2}\varphi
, we design a generalized Newton algorithm to solve such problems that doesn’t require, in contrast to
[
24
]
, the nonemptiness of the limiting subdifferential
\partial\varphi(x^{k})
along the iterative sequence. The well-posedness and
local superlinear convergence
of the new algorithm to find stationary points are justified under natural assumptions.

\bullet
Section
4
develops a novel Newton-type algorithm to solve
structured optimization problems

\mbox{\rm minimize}\quad\varphi(x):=f(x)+g(x)\quad\text{subject to }\;x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},

(1.4)

where
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to{\rm I\!R}
is a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth function while the
regularizer

g:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
is l.s.c. and prox-bounded. Note that both
f
and
g
are generally
nonconvex
. This makes (
1.4
) appropriate for applications to signal and image processing, machine learning, statistics, control, system identification, etc. The newly proposed algorithm to solve (
1.4
) uses, in contrast to the previous developments in
[
26
,
27
]
, the proximal mapping only for the regularizer
g
, while not for the cost function
\varphi
. This is often easy to compute and enables us to address broader classes in constrained optimization and applications. To proceed efficiently, we use the notion of
normal maps
for (
1.4
) and establish the local superlinear convergence of iterates in the proposed algorithm.

\bullet
Sections
5
and
6
are devoted to the
globalization
of developed algorithms to solve nonconvex structured optimization problems (
1.4
). In Section
5
, we propose and justify a novel
line search method
, which can be viewed as a
generalized proximal gradient
line search. To proceed, we exploit the machinery of
forward-backward envelopes
(FBE) introduced in
[
45
]
. The newly proposed line search algorithm doesn’t involved any generalized second-order constructions and ensures the
global linear convergence
of iterates. Involving the
second-order information
in Section
6
allows us to design the
globalized coderivative-based Newton method
(GCNM), which exhibits the
the global convergence with local superlinear rate
under the assumptions that are less restrictive compered to the previous developments in
[
26
,
27
]
. In particular, we don’t impose the positive-definiteness of the generalized Hessian on the entire space
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
(while only at the solution point in some settings) and don’t restrict ourselves to quadratic convex functions
f
in (
1.4
). This dramatically extends the scope of applications.

\bullet
From an application perspective, Section
7.1
demonstrates the use of GCNM in solving nonconvex problems such as the
\ell_{0}
-
\ell_{2}
regularized least squares regression
and Student’s
t
-regression with an
\ell_{0}
-penalty, both of which frequently arise in machine learning and statistics. For these types of nonsmooth and nonconvex problems, all algorithmic components and parameters are computed via the problem data. Numerical experiments are conducted on randomly generated instances, and the results are compared with other second-order approaches, including the SCD semismooth
∗
Newton method
[
15
]
and the ZeroFPR method with BFGS update
[
58
]
. GCNM is applied to nonconvex image restoration problems to further demonstrate its efficiency.

Preliminaries from Variational Analysis

{sec:pre}

This section overviews some notions and results of first-order and second-order variational analysis and generalized differentiation broadly used in the paper. We refer the reader to the monographs
[
39
,
41
,
55
]
and the more recent papers mentioned below.

Given a set-valued mapping/multifunction
F\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\rm I\!R}^{m}
between finite-dimensional spaces, its (Painlevé-Kuratowski)
outer limit
at
\bar{x}
is defined by

\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}_{x\to\bar{x}}F(x):=\big\{y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;\big|\;\exists\,\mbox{ sequences }\;x_{k}\to\bar{x},\;y_{k}\rightarrow y\;\mbox{ with }\;y_{k}\in F(x_{k}),\;\;k\in{\rm I\!N}:=\{1,2,\ldots\}\big\}.

Using the notation
z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Omega}}{{\to}}\bar{z}
meaning that
z\to\bar{z}
with
z\in\Omega
for a given nonempty set
\Omega\subset{\rm I\!R}^{s}
, the (Fréchet)
regular normal cone
to
\Omega
at
\bar{z}\in\Omega
is

\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(\bar{z}):=\Big\{v\in{\rm I\!R}^{s}\;\Big|\;\limsup_{z\overset{\Omega}{\rightarrow}\bar{z}}\frac{\langle v,z-\bar{z}\rangle}{\|z-\bar{z}\|}\leq 0\Big\},

(2.1)

while the (Mordukhovich)
limiting normal cone
to
\Omega
at
\bar{z}\in\Omega
is defined by

N_{\Omega}(\bar{z}):=\underset{z\overset{\Omega}{\to}\bar{z}}{\mathop{{\rm Lim}\,{\rm sup}}}\;\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(z)=\big\{v\in{\rm I\!R}^{s}\;\big|\;\exists\,z_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Omega}}{{\to}}\bar{z},\;v_{k}\to v\;\text{ as }\;k\to\infty\;\text{ with }\;v_{k}\in\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(z_{k})\big\}.

(2.2)

The (Bouligand-Severi)
tangent/contingent cone
to
\Omega
at
\bar{z}
is

T_{\Omega}(\bar{z}):=\big\{w\in{\rm I\!R}^{s}\;\big|\;\exists\,t_{k}\downarrow 0,\;w_{k}\to w\;\mbox{ as }\;k\to\infty\;\mbox{ with }\;\bar{z}+t_{k}w_{k}\in\Omega\big\}.

(2.3)

Note the duality relation between the regular normal and tangent cones

\widehat{N}_{\Omega}(\bar{z})=\left[T_{\Omega}(\bar{z})\right]^{*}=\big\{v\in{\rm I\!R}^{s}\;\big|\;\langle v,w\rangle\leq 0\;\mbox{ for all }\;w\in T_{\Omega}(\bar{z})\big\}

(2.4)

and observe that the limiting normal cone (
2.2
) cannot be dual, in view of its
intrinsic nonconvexity
, to any tangential approximation of the set in question.

For a set-valued mapping
F\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\rm I\!R}^{m}
, we define its
graph
,
domain, kernel
, and
range
by

\mbox{\rm gph}\,F:=\big\{(x,y)\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\times{\rm I\!R}^{m}\;\big|\;y\in F(x)\big\},\quad\mbox{\rm dom}\,F:=\big\{x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;\big|\;F(x)\neq\emptyset\big\},

\mbox{\rm ker}\,F:=\big\{x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;\big|\;0\in F(x)\big\},\;\mbox{ and }\;{\rm rge\,}F:=\big\{y\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}\;\big|\;\exists\ x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\quad\text{with }\;y\in F(x)\big\},

respectively. The
inverse mapping
of
F
is the set-valued mapping
F^{-1}:{\rm I\!R}^{m}\rightrightarrows{\rm I\!R}^{n}
given by

F^{-1}(y):=\big\{x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;\big|\;y\in F(x)\big\},\quad y\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}.

Based on the normal cones (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) to the graphs, we define the
regular coderivative
and the
limiting
coderivative
of
F
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,F
by, respectively,

\widehat{D}^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})(v):=\big\{u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;\big|\;(u,-v)\in\widehat{N}_{{\rm gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\big\},\quad v\in{\rm I\!R}^{m},

(2.5)

D^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})(v):=\big\{u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;\big|\;(u,-v)\in N_{{\rm gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\big\},\quad v\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}.

(2.6)

The
graphical derivative
of
F
at

(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,F
is defined via the tangent cone (
2.3
) by

DF(\bar{x},\bar{y})(u):=\big\{v\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}\;\big|\;(u,v)\in T_{{\rm gph}\,F}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\big\},\quad u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(2.7)

Due to the regular normal-tangent cone duality (
2.4
), we have the duality correspondence between (
2.5
) and (
2.7
), while the nonconvex-valued limiting coderivative (
2.6
) is not tangentially generated. In the case
where
F(\bar{x})
is the singleton
\{\bar{y}\}
, we omit
\bar{y}
in the notation of (
2.5
)–(
2.7
). Note that if

F\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to{\rm I\!R}^{m}
is
{\cal C}^{1}
-smooth around
\bar{x}
(in fact, just strictly differentiable at this point), then

DF(\bar{x})=\nabla F(\bar{x})\;\mbox{ and
}\;\widehat{D}^{*}F(\bar{x})=D^{*}F(\bar{x})=\nabla F(\bar{x})^{*},

where
\nabla F(\bar{x})^{*}
is the adjoint/transpose
matrix of the Jacobian
\nabla F(\bar{x})
.

To proceed further, recall that the
distance function
associated with
a set
\Omega\subset{\rm I\!R}^{s}
is

{\rm dist}(x;\Omega):=\inf\big\{\|w-x\|\;\big|\;w\in\Omega\big\},\quad x\in{\rm I\!R}^{s},

and define the
Euclidean projector
of
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{s}
to
\Omega
by

\Pi_{\Omega}(x):=\big\{w\in\Omega\;\big|\;\|x-w\|={\rm dist}(x;\Omega)\big\}.

A mapping
\widehat{F}\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\rm I\!R}^{m}
is a
localization
of

F\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\rm I\!R}^{m}
at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{y}\in F(\bar{x})
if there exist neighborhoods
U
of
\bar{x}
and
V
of

\bar{y}
such that we have
\mbox{\rm gph}\,\widehat{F}=\mbox{\rm gph}\,F\cap(U\times V)
. Here are the important well-posedness properties of set-valued mappings used in what follows.

Definition 2.1

(
metric regularity and subregularity of mappings).

{met-reg}

Let
F\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\rm I\!R}^{m}
be a set-valued mapping, and let
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,F
. We say that:

(i)

F
is
metrically regular
around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
with modulus
\mu>0
if there exist neighborhoods
U
of
\bar{x}
and
V
of
\bar{y}
providing the estimate

{\rm dist}\big(x;F^{-1}(y)\big)\leq\mu\,{\rm dist}\big(y;F(x)\big)\;\text{ for all }\;(x,y)\in U\times V.

If in addition
F^{-1}
has a single-valued localization around
(\bar{y},\bar{x})
, then
F
is
strongly metrically regular
around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
with modulus
\mu>0
.

(ii)

F
is
metrically subregular
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
with modulus
\mu>0
if there is a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
such that

{\rm dist}\big(x;F^{-1}(\bar{y})\big)\leq\mu\,{\rm dist}\big(\bar{y};F(x)\big)\;\text{ for all }\;x\in U.

If
F^{-1}(\bar{y})\cap U=\{\bar{x}\}
, then
F
is
strongly metrically subregular at

(\bar{x},\bar{y})

with modulus
\mu>0
. We say that
F
is
strongly metrically subregular around

(\bar{x},\bar{y})
if there exist neighborhoods
U
of
\bar{x}

and
V
of
\bar{y}
such that
F
is strongly metrically subregular at every point
(x,y)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,F\cap(U\times V)
.

Observe that the metric regularity properties in Definition
2.1
(i) are preserved in a neighborhood of the point
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
, i.e., they are
robust
with respect to small perturbations of the reference point, which is not always the case for metric subregularity from Definition
2.1
(ii). Recall that
F\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\rm I\!R}^{m}
, which graph is closed around
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,F
, is
metrically regular
around this point
if and only if
we have the coderivative condition

\mbox{\rm ker}\,D^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y}):=\big\{v\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}\;\big|\;0\in D^{*}F(\bar{x},\bar{y})(v)\big\}=\{0\}

(2.8)

established by Mordukhovich
[
38
, Theorem 3.6]
via his limiting coderivative (
2.6
) and then labeled as the
Mordukhovich criterion
in Rockafellar and Wets
[
55
, Theorems 7.40 and 7.43]
. Broad applications of this result are based on the robustness and
full calculus
available for (
2.6
); see
[
39
,
40
,
55
]
for more details and references. A parallel characterization of the (nonrobust)
strong metric subregularity
property of a closed-graph mapping
F\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\rm I\!R}^{m}
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,F
is given by
\mbox{\rm ker}\,DF(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\{0\}
via the (nonrobust) graphical derivative (
2.7
) of
F
at
(\bar{x},\bar{y})
and is known as the
Levy-Rockafellar criterion
; see
[
9
, Theorem 4E.1]
for more discussions.

Next we consider an extended-real-valued function
\varphi\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
with the domain and epigraph

\mbox{\rm dom}\,\varphi:=\big\{x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;\big|\;\varphi(x)<\infty\big\},\quad\mbox{\rm epi}\,\varphi:=\big\{(x,\alpha)\in{\rm I\!R}^{n+1}\;\big|\;\alpha\geq\varphi(x)\big\}.

The
regular subdifferential
and
limiting subdifferential
of
\varphi
at
\bar{x}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,\varphi
are defined geometrically via the corresponding normal cones to the epigraph by, respectively,

\widehat{\partial}\varphi(\bar{x}):=\big\{v\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;\big|\;(v,-1)\in\widehat{N}_{{\rm\text{\rm epi}}\,\varphi}\big(\bar{x},\varphi(\bar{x})\big)\big\},

(2.9)

\partial\varphi(\bar{x}):=\big\{v\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\;\big|\;(v,-1)\in N_{{\rm\text{\rm epi}}\,\varphi}\big(\bar{x},\varphi(\bar{x})\big)\big\}

(2.10)

Note that the limiting subdifferential (
2.10
) admits various analytic representations and satisfies comprehensive calculus rules that can be found in
[
39
,
40
,
55
]
. Observe the useful
scalarization formula

D^{*}F(\bar{x})(v)=\partial\langle v,F\rangle(\bar{x})\;\mbox{ for all }\;v\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}

(2.11)

connecting the coderivative (
2.6
) of a locally Lipschitzian mapping
F\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to{\rm I\!R}^{m}
and the subdifferential (
2.10
) of the scalarized function
x\mapsto\langle v,F\rangle(x)
whenever
v\in{\rm I\!R}^{m}
.

Following
[
37
]
, we define the
second-order subdifferential
, or
generalized Hessian
,
\partial^{2}\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{v})\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\rm I\!R}^{n}
of
\varphi\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
at
\bar{x}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,\varphi
for
\bar{v}\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
as the coderivative of the subgradient mapping

\partial^{2}\varphi(\bar{x},\bar{v})(u):=\big(D^{*}\partial\varphi\big)(\bar{x},\bar{y})(u)\;\mbox{ for all }\;u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(2.12)

If
\varphi
is
{\cal C}^{2}
-smooth around
\bar{x}
, then we have (no Hessian transposition is needed) that

\partial^{2}\varphi(\bar{x})(u)=\big\{\nabla^{2}\varphi(\bar{x})u\big\}\;\mbox{ for all }\;u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(2.13)

If
\varphi
is of
class
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
around
\bar{x}
(i.e., it is
{\cal C}^{1}
-smooth with a locally Lipschitzian gradient, then

\partial^{2}\varphi(\bar{x})(u)=\partial\big\langle u,\nabla\varphi\big\rangle(\bar{x})\;\mbox{ for all }\;u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}

(2.14)

by (
2.11
). In general, (
2.12
) is a positively homogeneous multifunction, which possesses well-developed
calculus rules
and variety of applications while being
explicitly computed
in terms of the initial data for large classes of extended-real-valued functions that frequently appear in variational analysis, optimization, and numerous applications. We refer the reader to the recent book
[
41
]
for a rather comprehensive account on (
2.12
); see also Section
7.1
for new applications to models of machine learning and statistics.

\varphi\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
broadly used in the paper. The notion of

s
-convexity
for extended-real-valued functions is a major one we intend to investigate in this paper. For the general case of

, it was recently introduced in
[
53
]
, while its earlier versions for
s\geq 0
appeared in
[
51
]
and were later studied and applied in finite-dimensional spaces in
[
21
,
25
,
52
]
, and in infinite-dimensional settings in
[
22
,
23
,
41
]
. The case of

is related to

, a concept originally introduced in
[
48
]
and subsequently developed and widely applied in variational analysis and optimization; see
[
55
]
. Before introducing the notion of variational
s
-convexity, we recall that a function
\varphi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
is

-convex

\varphi-\frac{s}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}
is convex. In particular, when

, it is labeled as
(-s)
-weakly convex

Definition 2.2

(
variationally convex functions).

{vr}

Given
s\in{\rm I\!R}
, a proper l.s.c. function
\varphi\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
is
variationally
s
-convex
at
\bar{x}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,\varphi
for
\bar{v}\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
if there exist an
s
-convex function
\widehat{\varphi}
as well as convex neighborhoods
U
of
\bar{x}
and
V
of
\bar{v}
together with
\varepsilon>0
such that
\widehat{\varphi}\leq\varphi
on

(U\times V)\cap\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\widehat{\varphi}=(U_{\varepsilon}\times V)\cap\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi,\quad\widehat{\varphi}(x)=\varphi(x)\;\text{ at common elements }\;(x,v),

where
U_{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in U\mid\varphi(x)<\varphi(\bar{x})+\varepsilon\}
. When
s\leq 0
,
\varphi
is said to be
(-s)
-level
prox-regular
at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{v}
, while for
s=0

variationally convex
, and for

-strongly variationally convex
at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{v}
.

The next proposition taken from
[

lists the characterizations of variationally convex functions via some properties of their limiting subdifferentials.

Proposition 2.3

(
subgradient characterizations of variational convexity).

{subgradientvar}

Let
\varphi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
be l.s.c. with
\bar{x}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,\varphi
and
\bar{v}\in\widehat{\partial}\varphi(\bar{x})
. Then for any
s\in{\rm I\!R}
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

\varphi
is variationally
s
-convex at
\bar{x}
for
\bar{v}
.

(ii)
There are neighborhoods
U
of
\bar{x}
,
V
of
\bar{v}
, and a number
\varepsilon>0
such that

\langle v_{1}-v_{2},u_{1}-u_{2}\rangle\geq s\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|^{2}\quad\text{for all }\;(u_{1},v_{1}),(u_{2},v_{2})\in\text{\rm gph}\partial\varphi\cap(U_{\varepsilon}\times V),

(2.15)

where
U_{\varepsilon}:=\{x\in U\;|\;\varphi(x)<\varphi(\bar{x})+\varepsilon\}
.

(iii)
There are neighborhoods
U
of
\bar{x}
,
V
of
\bar{v}
, and a number
\varepsilon>0
such that

\varphi(x)\geq\varphi(u)+\langle v,x-u\rangle+\frac{s}{2}\|x-u\|^{2}\quad\text{for all }\;x\in U,\;(u,v)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi\cap(U_{\varepsilon}\times V).

3
Coderivative-Based Newton Method for Subgradient Systems

{sec:general}

In this section, we design and justify a novel generalized Newton method to solve
subgradient inclusions

0\in\partial\varphi(x),\quad x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},

(3.1)

where
\varphi\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
is an l.s.c. and
prox-bounded
function, and where
\partial
stands for the limiting subdifferential (
2.10
). Recall that in the case of a
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth function
\varphi
, the classical Newton method constructs the iterative procedure given in (
1.2
) and (
1.3
). A natural idea, which we explored in our previous publications, is to replace
\nabla\varphi(x^{k})
and
\nabla^{2}\varphi(x^{k})
with a vector
v^{k}\in\partial\varphi(x^{k})
and
\partial^{2}\varphi(x^{k},v^{k})
, respectively. In this way, the iterative sequence
\{x^{k}\}\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}
is constructed by

{x^{k+1}=x^{k}+d^{k}}\quad\text{with }{-v^{k}\in\partial^{2}\varphi(x^{k},v^{k})(d^{k}),\;v^{k}\in\partial\varphi(x^{k}),\quad k\in{\rm I\!N}}.

(3.2)

However, to proceed as in (
3.2
) is not possible if the subgradient sets
\partial\varphi(x^{k})
are empty for some
k\in{\rm I\!N}
, which is not generally excluded. We now propose to replace
x^{k}
with another
approximate vector

\widehat{x}^{k}
that ensures that the limiting subdifferential
\partial\varphi(\widehat{x}^{k})
is nonempty and that
\widehat{x}^{k}
is not far from
x^{k}
. This leads us to the iterative sequence
\{x^{k}\}\subset{\rm I\!R}^{n}
constructed by

{x^{k+1}=\widehat{x}^{k}+d^{k}}\quad\text{with }{-\widehat{v}^{k}\in\partial^{2}\varphi(\widehat{x}^{k},\widehat{v}^{k})(d^{k}),\quad k\in{\rm I\!N}},

(3.3)

where
(\widehat{x}^{k},\widehat{v}^{k})\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
. This general framework plays a crucial role in designing and justifying our new coderivative-based Newton-type algorithms for solving nonsmooth and nonconvex optimization problems in Sections
4
and
6
.
Based on (
3.3
), a key aspect in the development and validation of our Newtonian algorithms is to establish the existence of and explicitly determine a direction
d\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
that satisfies the
generalized Newton system
(or the
second-order subdifferential inclusion
)

-v\in\partial^{2}\varphi(x,v)(d)

(3.4)

for a given
(x,v)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
. Efficient conditions for
solvability
of (
3.4
) presented in
[
24
, Theorems 3.1 and 3.2]
ensure that the proposed algorithm is
well-defined
. Observe that the local closedness assumption for the limiting subdifferential graph imposed below holds in very general settings, especially when
\varphi\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
is subdifferentially continuous; see Section
2
. The following results are drawn from
[
24
, Theorems 3.1 and 3.2]
.

Proposition 3.1

(
solvability of generalized Newton systems).

{solvabilityMOR}

Let
\varphi\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
be such that
\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
is locally closed around
(\bar{x},\bar{v})\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
. The following assertions hold:

(i)
If the subgradient mapping
\partial\varphi\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\rm I\!R}^{n}
is strongly metrically subregular at
(\bar{x},\bar{v})
, then there exists

satisfying the generalized Newton system (
3.4
) for
(x,v):=(\bar{x},\bar{v})
.

(ii)

\partial\varphi
is strongly metrically regular around
(\bar{x},\bar{v})
, then there exists a neighborhood
U\times V
of
(\bar{x},\bar{v})
such that for each
(x,v)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi\cap(U\times V)
, we find a direction
d\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
satisfying the generalized Newton system (
3.4
).

However, Proposition
3.1
only provides a condition that guarantees the existence of a direction

satisfying (
3.4
) without offering explicit methods to find this direction. In practice, an explicit calculation of such a direction is crucial for the implementation of our algorithms. Let us now obtain new sufficient conditions that not only guarantee the existence of the required direction but also furnish a constructive procedure to calculate it for some important classes of functions. Before establishing the theorem, we present a preliminary result from

Theorem 3.3

(

Remark 3.5

(
solving the generalized Newton system).

{findNMremark}

In general, solving the second-order subdifferential inclusion (
3.4
) may be a challenging issue. Nevertheless, our approach offers computational advantages in some rather broad specific scenarios. Consider the following points:

(i)
Theorem
3.3
provides a method to find a solution to (
3.4
) by solving
linear systems generated by quadratic bundles
. Quadratic bundles are defined for nonsmooth and nonconvex functions and have been extensively studied regarding optimality conditions and variational sufficiency in
[
8
,
29
,
52
,
54
]
, with additional calculus rules developed in
[
8
,
28
]
.

(ii)
Theorem
3.4
enables us to obtain a solution to (
3.4
) by solving a
strongly convex optimization subproblem
derived from second subderivatives. Calculus rules for second subderivatives have been thoroughly explored in
[
36
,
55
]
for practical and broader classes of functions overwhelmingly arising in optimization and variational analysis. Moreover, when the cost function is generalized twice differentiable, the subproblem in (
3.6
) reduces to a
quadratic program over a subspace
, for which many efficient numerical algorithms are widely available.

(iii)
When the cost function is the
sum of two nonconvex functions
, which is the primary focus of this paper, Theorem
4.11
offers an alternative approach to finding the Newton direction by computing the Bouligand Jacobian of the proximal mapping
\partial_{B}\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}
. Explicit formulas for these computations are provided in, e.g.,
[
46
, Section 5]
covering a wide range of important optimization problems.

(iv)
In some situations, advantages of the second-order subdifferential inclusion (
3.4
) in coderivative-based Newtonian methods become particularly clear when compared to solving linear equations using the Clarke Jacobian in semismooth Newton methods. This distinction is highlighted in
[
27
, Section 6.1]
. In such cases, the direction generated through (
3.4
) has an
explicit formula
, while semismooth Newton methods require solving a linear system. Computational steps in coderivative-based Newton algorithms depend solely on
vector computations
without the need for matrix calculus. This approach offers significant benefits in terms of computational cost. Our future goal is to identify practically important optimization problems, where the generalized Newton directions generated by (
3.4
) have explicit formulas. We aim to focus on cases where all computational costs involve only vector computations, leading us to a significant reduction in the computational costs of algorithms.

Here is our novel coderivative-based Newton method for solving the subgradient system (

).

(general framework for coderivative-based Newton-type methods)

1:

,
\eta>0

2:

for

If

Remark 3.6

(

{apprst}

The idea in the approximate step of Algorithm

∗

It follows from
[

that the collection of points
(x,v)\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
with
\partial\varphi(x)\neq\emptyset
is
dense
in the subdifferential graph, and thus we can always select
(\widehat{x}^{k},\widehat{v}^{k})\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
arbitrarily close to
(\bar{x},0)
. The issue is about the fulfillment of estimate (

). Let us show how to do it in the following major settings without knowing the solution
\bar{x}
.

(i)
When
\varphi
is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
around
\bar{x}
, there exist a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
and
\ell>0
such that

\|\nabla\varphi(x)-\nabla\varphi(\bar{x})\|\leq\ell\|x-\bar{x}\|\quad\text{for all }\;x\in U.

Supposing that
x^{k}\in U
at the
k^{\text{th}}
iteration, take

\widehat{x}^{k}:=x^{k}
and
\widehat{v}^{k}:=\nabla\varphi(x^{k})
, which gives us

).

(ii)
If
\varphi\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
is continuously prox-regular at
\bar{x}
for
0
with modulus
r>0
, it follows from Proposition
2.5
that for each
\lambda\in(0,r^{-1})
, there is a neighborhood
U_{\lambda}
of
\bar{x}
such that
\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda\varphi}
is single-valued and Lipschitz continuous with modulus

on
U_{\lambda}
. Having

at the

iteration, choose

and
\widehat{v}^{k}

\widehat{x}^{k}:=\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda\varphi}(x^{k})\quad\text{and }\;\widehat{v}^{k}:=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(x^{k}-\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda\varphi}(x^{k})\right).

(3.12)

Therefore, we get the estimates

\displaystyle\|(\widehat{x}^{k},\widehat{v}^{k})-(x^{k},0)\|\leq\left(1+\lambda^{-1}\right)\|x^{k}-\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda\varphi}(x^{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\lambda^{-1}\right)(\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\|+\|\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda\varphi}(x^{k})-\bar{x}\|)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\lambda^{-1}\right)(\|x^{k}-\bar{x}\|+\|\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda\varphi}(x^{k})-\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda\varphi}(\bar{x})\|)

with

Definition 3.7

(

∗

{semi*}

A mapping

if whenever

\langle u^{*},u\rangle=\langle v^{*},v\rangle\;\mbox{ for all }\;(v^{*},u^{*})\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,D^{*}F\big((\bar{x},\bar{y});(u,v)\big).

Recall from

\|\nabla\varphi(x)-\nabla\varphi(\bar{x})+v_{x}\|=o(\|x-\bar{x}\|)

whenever

Lemma 3.8

(
estimates for

{estimateNMC11}

Let
\varphi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to{\rm I\!R}

around

. If

is metrically regular around
(\bar{x},\nabla\varphi(\bar{x}))

and a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
such that for any

satisfying

, we find

with

\|x+d-\bar{x}\|\leq c\|\nabla\varphi(x)+v_{x}\|.

(3.15)

If
\nabla\varphi(\bar{x})=0
and
\nabla\varphi
is semismooth
∗
at
(\bar{x},0)
, then for every
\varepsilon>0
there is a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
such that

\|x+d-\bar{x}\|\leq\varepsilon\|x-\bar{x}\|\quad\text{whenever }\;x\in U\;\mbox{ and }\;-\nabla\varphi(x)\in\partial^{2}\varphi(x)(d).

(3.16)

[
Proof.]
By the metric regularity of
\nabla\varphi
around
(\bar{x},\nabla\varphi(\bar{x}))
and
its characterization from
[
24
, Lemma 5.1]
, we find
c>0
and a neighborhood
U
of

satisfying

\|v\|\geq c^{-1}\|u\|\quad\text{for all }\;v\in\partial^{2}\varphi(x)(u),\;x\in U,\;u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

Using the subadditivity of coderivatives from

tells us that

\partial^{2}\varphi(x)(d)=\partial^{2}\varphi(x)(x+d-\bar{x}+\bar{x}-x)\subset\partial^{2}\varphi(x)(x+d-\bar{x})+\partial^{2}\varphi(x)(\bar{x}-x).

Hence for any

Furthermore, it follows from (

below.

\bullet

Computational aspects.
The sequence generated by (
3.24
) relies heavily on the explicit formula for the Moreau envelope
e_{\lambda}\varphi
, which is often difficult to compute, particularly when
\varphi
is either a composite function, or it is the sum of two nonconvex functions. As such, this method is more valuable for qualitative analysis than for quantitative computations. In contrast, Algorithm
1
involves the computation of first- and second-order subdifferentials at each step without requiring the formulation of Moreau envelopes. This approach takes advantage of well-established subdifferential calculus, and—for many practical classes of extended-real-valued functions—the explicit computations of the second-order subdifferentials are widely accessible; see, e.g.,
[
41
]
.

\bullet

Applicable classes of problems.
Although solving
0\in\partial\varphi(x)
may be transformed in some settings into solving a locally Lipschitz equation
F(z)=0
, such a reformulation is not always straightforward. Moreover, computing
F
iteratively may be infeasible when it requires the explicit form of the Moreau envelope. On the other hand, Algorithm
1
offers a direct approach to solving nonsmooth and nonconvex optimization problems without the need for such reformulations. This generality enables our method to handle problems involving the sum of two nonconvex functions as detailed in the remainder of this paper. Observe, e.g., that for the classes of optimization problems considered in Section
7
, reformulating them as equivalent locally Lipschitz equations is particularly challenging due to the intrinsic nonconvexity of the
\ell_{0}
norm. Moreover, our approach lays a solid foundation for future research on broader classes of nonconvex optimization problems including composite optimization, difference-of-convex programming, and related models. Let us emphasize that subdifferential calculus and computations for many of these structures are already well developed.

We conclude this section with brief discussions on some recent developments of generalized Newton methods to
solve set-valued inclusions involving the subgradient systems (
3.1
).

Remark 3.13

(
comparison with some local Newton-type methods to solve inclusions).

(i)
The semismooth
∗
Newton method of
[
13
]
addresses solving the inclusion
0\in F(x)
for
F:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, which reduces to (
3.1
) when
F:=\partial\varphi
for some
\varphi:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
. The underlying difference between our approach and the method of
[
13
]
is in finding
generalized Newton directions
. To be more specific, the directions
d^{k}
in the semismooth
∗
Newton method is given by

d^{k}:=-A^{-1}B\widehat{v}^{k},\quad k=0,1,\ldots,

(3.26)

where the pair
(\widehat{x}^{k},\widehat{v}^{k})\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
is selected for each
k
from (
3.10
), and where the matrices
A,B\in{\rm I\!R}^{n\times n}
are generated as follows. Choose
(v_{i}^{*},u_{i}^{*})\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,D^{*}\partial\varphi(\widehat{x}^{k},\widehat{v}^{k})
such that the
i
th rows of
A
and
B
are
u_{i}^{*}
and
v_{i}^{*}
, respectively, and that
A
is nonsingular. However, explicit constructions for generating such matrices remain unclear in the case where
F
is a set-valued mapping. In
[
13
]
, such constructions are provided only for the case where
F
is a single-valued and locally Lipschitzian mapping. Meanwhile, our new method provides explicit ways to select generalized Newton directions when
\varphi
belongs to a significantly large class of extended-real-valued functions as discussed in Remark
3.5
. Moreover, the semismooth
∗
Newton method in
[
13
]
is only guaranteed to converge under the assumption of strong metric regularity, while Theorem
3.11
shows that the local superlinear convergence of Algorithm
1
holds under merely metric regularity, which is a less restrictive condition. To overcome this disadvantage of the semismooth
∗
Newton method, one of the possible ideas developed in the more recent paper
[
14
]
is to construct generalized Newton directions by using SCD (subspace containing derivative) mappings instead of coderivatives. This adaption requires a certain SCD regularity, which is weaker than the metric regularity of the subgradient mapping
\partial\varphi
. However, calculus rules for SCD mappings are more limited in comparison with the extensive calculus available for coderivatives.

(ii)
Our previous paper
[
24
]
develops local coderivative-based Newton methods to solve inclusions of the form
0\in\partial\varphi(x)
. The algorithms proposed therein heavily rely on the proximal mapping of
\varphi
, which is not always easy to compute. Moreover, in
[
24
]
we focus on the case where
\varphi=f+g
, with
f
being quadratic and
g
prox-regular. In contrast, our new Algorithm
1
does not require such structural assumptions. We consider a more general setting where
\varphi
may be nonsmooth and nonconvex, and our method only requires the computation of first- and second-order subdifferentials, which enjoy full calculi and can often be determined explicitly.

4
Local Convergence Analysis of Coderivative-Based Newton Method for Structured Nonconvex Optimization

{sec:localNMcompo}

As discussed in Section
3
, Algorithm
1
offers a variety of possibilities to implement the
approximate step
. In this section, we present a way to choose the pair
(\widehat{x}^{k},\widehat{v}^{k})\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
in the approximate step of Algorithm
1
for solving the class of
nonsmooth and nonconvex optimization problems
of the type

\mbox{\rm minimize}\quad\varphi(x):=f(x)+g(x)\quad\text{subject to }\;x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n},

(4.1)

where
f:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to{\rm I\!R}
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth
, and where
g:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
is proper, l.s.c., and
prox-bounded
with threshold
\lambda_{g}\in(0,\infty]
. We start with the corresponding definitions needed in what follows.

Definition 4.1

(
prox-gradient and normal mappings).

{normal map}

Let
\varphi\colon{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to\overline{{\rm I\!R}}
be given in (
4.1
). For each
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{g})
, define the following multifunctions:

(i)
The
prox-gradient map

\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\rm I\!R}^{n}
associated with (
4.1
) is

\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x):=\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}\big(x-\lambda\nabla f(x)\big),\quad x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(4.2)

(ii)
The
normal map

\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\text{\rm nor}}:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\rightrightarrows{\rm I\!R}^{n}
associated with (
4.1
) is

\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\text{\rm nor}}(x):=\Big\{\nabla f(\widehat{x})+\frac{1}{\lambda}(x-\widehat{x})\;\Big|\;\widehat{x}\in\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x)\Big\},\quad x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(4.3)

When the proximal mapping
\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}
is single-valued, the normal map (
4.3
) can be written as

\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\text{\rm nor}}(x)=\nabla f\big(\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x)\big)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\big(x-\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x)\big),\quad x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(4.4)

The terminology “normal map” goes back to variational inequalities being useful there for characterizing solution sets; see, e.g.,
[
11
]
. In the setting of variational inequalities

\text{find }\bar{x}\in\Omega\;\text{ such that }\;\langle F(\bar{x}),x-\bar{x}\rangle\geq 0\;\mbox{ for all }\;x\in\Omega,

(4.5)

where
F:{\rm I\!R}^{n}\to{\rm I\!R}^{n}
is a single-valued operator, and where
\Omega
is a closed and convex set, the normal map associated with (
4.5
) is defined by

\mathcal{F}^{\text{\rm nor}}(x):=F\big(\Pi_{\Omega}(x)\big)+x-\Pi_{\Omega}(x),\quad x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}.

(4.6)

Note that both normal maps in (
4.3
) and (
4.6
) agree when
F:=\nabla f
,
g:=\delta_{\Omega}
, and
\lambda=1
. Various error bounds for variational inequalities based on the normal map (
4.6
) can be found in the book
[
11
]
and the references therein, while new ones by using this normal map have been recently obtained in
[
20
]
for the class of strongly pseudomonotone variational inequalities. One of the first extensions of the normal map to composite problems was introduced by Pieper in his PhD thesis
[
47
]
. His approach combines semismooth Newton steps for solving the nonsmooth equation
F^{\text{nor}}_{\lambda}(x)=0
with a trust-region-type globalization mechanism. The subsequent paper
[
32
]
analyzed local properties of a quasi-Newton variant of Pieper’s normal map-based trust-region method. Pieper’s approach has also been successfully applied in various contexts, particularly in optimal control; see, e.g.,
[
6
,
31
,
56
]
. A more comprehensive convergence analysis, covering both local and global aspects of the normal map trust-region method, was recently presented in
[
44
]
. Additionally, algorithmic extensions of normal map-based techniques to stochastic settings have been proposed in
[
34
,
50
]
. In
[
44
]
, the authors introduce the normal map as an intermediate step to find a solution to the problem of minimizing the sum of a smooth and a nonsmooth convex functions by applying the semismooth Newton method to the equation
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\text{\rm nor}}(z)=0
, where the local Lipschitz continuity of this mapping is ensured by the convexity of the regularizer. In contrast, our approach bypasses this transformation and directly tackles the optimization problem, where the objective function is the sum of two nonconvex functions. In this framework, the normal map plays a critical role in implementing the approximate step of Algorithm
1
and in supporting the development of our new generalized Newton and line-search methods introduced in this section and further detailed in Sections
5
and
6
.

Recall that the stationarity condition
0\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
, where
\varphi
is given in form (
4.1
), is only a necessary condition for the optimality of
\bar{x}
being sufficient if
\varphi
is convex. In what follows, we consider different concepts of stationary and critical points for the function
\varphi
of type (
4.1
) along the lines of general optimization theory.

Definition 4.2

(
stationary and critical points).

{stat}

Let
\varphi
be given in (
4.1
), and let
\bar{x}\in\mbox{\rm dom}\,\varphi
. Then
\bar{x}
is:

(i)

F
(
réchet
)
-stationary point
if
0\in\widehat{\partial}\varphi(\bar{x})
.

(ii)

M
(
ordukhovich
)
-stationary point
if
0\in{\partial}\varphi(\bar{x})
.

(iii)

Critical point
if
\bar{x}\in\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(\bar{x})
for some
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{g})
.

Remark 4.3
.

{crit->Fsta}

If
\bar{x}
is a critical point of (
4.1
), then
\bar{x}
is a F-stationary (and hence M-stationary) point. Indeed, we have from the critical point definition that

\bar{x}\in\text{\rm argmin}\Big\{g(x)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|x-\bar{x}+\lambda\nabla f(\bar{x})\|^{2}\;\Big|\;x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\Big\}.

It then follows from
[
39
, Proposition 1.114]
that

0\in\widehat{\partial}g(\bar{x})+\frac{1}{\lambda}\big(\bar{x}-\bar{x}+\lambda\nabla f(\bar{x})\big)=\nabla f(\bar{x})+\widehat{\partial}g(\bar{x})=\widehat{\partial}\varphi(\bar{x})\subset\partial\varphi(\bar{x}).

If
f
and
g
are convex and
\bar{x}
is a critical point of (
4.1
), then
\bar{x}
is an optimal solution to (
4.1
); see
[
4
, Corollary 27.3]
.

Next we show that the criticality of
\bar{x}
is a necessary optimality condition in (
4.1
).

Proposition 4.4

(
necessary condition for nonconvex optimization problems).

{necessncvopt}

Let
\varphi
be given in (
4.1
), and let
\bar{x}
be an optimal solution to problem (
4.1
). Assume that
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous on
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
with modulus
L_{f}>0
. Then
\bar{x}
is a critical point of (
4.1
), and
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
is single-valued at
\bar{x}
for any
\lambda\in(0,\text{\rm min}\{1/L_{f},\lambda_{g}\})
.

[
Proof.]
Fix
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{g})
. Since
g
is prox-bounded with threshold
\lambda_{g}
, we have by Proposition
2.4
that
\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(\bar{x}-\lambda\nabla f(\bar{x}))
is a nonempty set. Picking any of
\widehat{x}
from this set, let us to show that
\widehat{x}=\bar{x}
. Indeed, the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
ensures by the descent lemma in
[
19
, Lemma A.11]
that

f(\widehat{x})\leq f(\bar{x})+\langle\nabla f(\bar{x}),\widehat{x}-\bar{x}\rangle+\frac{L_{f}}{2}\|\widehat{x}-\bar{x}\|^{2}.

(4.7)

Taking into account that
\widehat{x}\in\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(\bar{x}-\lambda\nabla f(\bar{x}))
, we have the estimate

g(\widehat{x})+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|\widehat{x}-(\bar{x}-\lambda\nabla f(\bar{x}))\|^{2}\leq g(\bar{x})+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|\bar{x}-(\bar{x}-\lambda\nabla f(\bar{x}))\|^{2}=g(\bar{x})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\nabla f(\bar{x})\|^{2}.

(4.8)

Since
\bar{x}
is an optimal solution to (
4.1
) and
g
is proper, it tells us that
g(\bar{x})
is finite, and so is
g(\widehat{x})
by (
4.8
).
Combining (
4.7
) and (
4.8
) provides the conditions

\displaystyle f(\widehat{x})+g(\widehat{x})

\displaystyle\leq f(\bar{x})+\langle\nabla f(\bar{x}),\widehat{x}-\bar{x}\rangle+\frac{L_{f}}{2}\|\widehat{x}-\bar{x}\|^{2}+g(\widehat{x})

\displaystyle=f(\bar{x})+g(\widehat{x})+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|\widehat{x}-(\bar{x}-\lambda\nabla f(\bar{x}))\|^{2}-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\nabla f(\bar{x})\|^{2}+\left(\frac{L_{f}}{2}-\frac{1}{2\lambda}\right)\|\widehat{x}-\bar{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq f(\bar{x})+g(\bar{x})+\left(\frac{L_{f}}{2}-\frac{1}{2\lambda}\right)\|\widehat{x}-\bar{x}\|^{2}\leq f(\widehat{x})+g(\widehat{x})+\left(\frac{L_{f}}{2}-\frac{1}{2\lambda}\right)\|\widehat{x}-\bar{x}\|^{2},

which ensure that
\|\widehat{x}-\bar{x}\|^{2}\leq 0
, and thus
\widehat{x}=\bar{x}
. Since
\widehat{x}
was chosen arbitrarily, the set
\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(\bar{x}-\lambda\nabla f(\bar{x}))
is a singleton, and
\bar{x}
is its unique element as claimed.

Using Remark
4.3
and Proposition
4.4
gives us the implications

\text{optimal solution}\Longrightarrow\text{critical point}\Longrightarrow\text{F-stationary point}\Longrightarrow\text{M-stationary point}.

To proceed further, we involve the normal maps (
4.3
) to characterize critical points.

Proposition 4.5

(
characterization of critical points via normal maps).

{characsol}

Let
\varphi
be given in (
4.1
). Then for each
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{g})
, the following assertions hold:

(i)
If
\bar{x}
is a critical point of (
4.1
), then
0\in\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\text{\rm nor}}(\bar{x}-\lambda\nabla f(\bar{x}))
.

(ii)
If
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous around
\bar{x}
with modulus
L_{f}>0
and if
0\in\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\text{\rm nor}}(\bar{x}-\lambda\nabla f(\bar{x}))
with
\lambda\in(0,1/L_{f})
, then
\bar{x}
is a critical point of (
4.1
).

[
Proof.]
To verify (i), we get by the criticality
\bar{x}
in (
4.1
) that
\bar{x}\in\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(\bar{x})
, which yields

0=\nabla f(\bar{x})+\frac{1}{\lambda}\big(\bar{x}-\lambda\nabla f(\bar{x})-\bar{x}\big)\in\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\text{\rm nor}}\big(\bar{x}-\lambda\nabla f(\bar{x})\big).

To prove (ii), deduce from
0\in\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\text{\rm nor}}(\bar{x}-\lambda\nabla f(\bar{x}))
that there exists
\widehat{x}\in\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(\bar{x})
satisfying

0=\nabla f(\widehat{x})+\frac{1}{\lambda}\big(\bar{x}-\lambda\nabla f(\bar{x})-\widehat{x}\big)=\nabla f(\widehat{x})-\nabla f(\bar{x})+\frac{1}{\lambda}(\bar{x}-\widehat{x}).

(4.9)

Due to the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
with modulus
L_{f}>0
around
\bar{x}
, it follows from (
4.9
) that

\frac{1}{\lambda}\|\widehat{x}-\bar{x}\|=\|\nabla f(\widehat{x})-\nabla f(\bar{x})\|\leq L_{f}\|\widehat{x}-\bar{x}\|,

(4.10)

which tells us by
0<\lambda<1/L_{f}
that
\|\widehat{x}-\bar{x}\|=0
, i.e.,
\widehat{x}=\bar{x}
. Thus we get
\bar{x}\in\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(\bar{x})
.

The next proposition allows us to generate a point in the graph of the subgradient mapping for
\varphi
in (
4.1
) directly from the prox-gradient (
4.2
) and normal (
4.3
) maps. It plays a crucial role in designing and justifying our new algorithm below in this section.

Proposition 4.6

(
generating elements of subgradient graphs).

{xvingraph}

Let
\varphi
be given in (
4.1
). For each
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{g})
and
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
, take
\widehat{x}\in\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x)
and denote

\widehat{v}:=\nabla f(\widehat{x})-\nabla f(x)+\frac{1}{\lambda}(x-\widehat{x}).

Then we have
\widehat{v}\in\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\text{\rm nor}}(x-\lambda\nabla f(x))
and
(\widehat{x},\widehat{v})\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\widehat{\partial}\varphi
.

[
Proof.]
The first inclusion follows immediately from the definitions of the prox-gradient and normal maps. To verify the second one, deduce from
\widehat{x}\in\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x)
that

\widehat{x}\in\text{\rm argmin}\Big\{g(u)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|u-x+\lambda\nabla f(x)\|^{2}\;\Big|\;u\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}\Big\}.

It follows from the elementary calculus rules for regular subgradients
[
40
, Proposition 1.30]
that

0\in\widehat{\partial}g(\widehat{x})+\frac{1}{\lambda}\big(\widehat{x}-x+\lambda\nabla f(x)\big)=\nabla f(x)+\widehat{\partial}g(\widehat{x})+\frac{1}{\lambda}(\widehat{x}-x),

which implies in turn that

\widehat{v}=\nabla f(\widehat{x})-\nabla f(x)+\frac{1}{\lambda}(x-\widehat{x})\in\nabla f(\widehat{x})+\widehat{\partial}g(\widehat{x})=\widehat{\partial}\varphi(\widehat{x}),

and therefore completes the proof of the proposition.

Now we involve the prox-regularity of the regularizer in (
4.1
), which allows us to justify the single-valuedness of the prox-gradient and normal maps.

Proposition 4.7

(
single-valuedness of prox-gradient and normal maps).

{singlevalueproxmap}

Let
\bar{x}
be a critical point of (
4.1
), and let
g
be prox-regular with modulus
r>0
at
\bar{x}
for
-\nabla f(\bar{x})
. Then for each
\lambda\in(0,r^{-1})
, there exists a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
on which the maps
x\mapsto\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x)
and
x\mapsto\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\text{\rm nor}}(x-\lambda\nabla f(x))
are single-valued and Lipschitz continuous on
U
. Moreover, we find
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}>0
such that

\|\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x)-\bar{x}\|\leq\eta_{1}\|x-\bar{x}\|\;\text{ and }\;\|\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\text{\rm nor}}(x-\lambda\nabla f(x))\|\leq\eta_{2}\|x-\bar{x}\|\;\text{ for all }\;x\in U.

(4.11)

[
Proof.]
Since
f
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smooth, there is a neighborhood
\widetilde{U}
of
\bar{x}
such that
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous on
\widetilde{U}
. It follows from Lemma
2.5
that the proximal mapping
\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}
is single-valued and locally Lipschitzian on a neighborhood
U_{\lambda}
of
\bar{x}-\lambda\nabla f(\bar{x})
and that
\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(\bar{x}-\lambda\nabla f(\bar{x}))=\bar{x}
. Choose a neighborhood
U\subset\widetilde{U}
of
\bar{x}
so that

(I-\lambda\nabla f)(U)\subset U_{\lambda}\;\text{and }\;\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}\big((I-\lambda\nabla f)(U)\big)\subset\widetilde{U},

(4.12)

which implies that the prox-gradient map
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
is single-valued and Lipschitz continuous on
U
. By (
4.4
) and (
4.12
), the normal map
x\mapsto\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\text{\rm nor}}(x-\lambda\nabla f(x))
is single-valued and satisfies the equality

\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\text{\rm nor}}\big(x-\lambda\nabla f(x)\big)=\nabla f\big(\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x)\big)-\nabla f(x)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\big(x-\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x)\big)\;\mbox{ for all }\;x\in U,

(4.13)

which also implies that this map is Lipschitz continuous on
U
.

To verify further (
4.11
), let
\ell>0
and
L>0
be Lipschitz moduli of
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
and
\nabla f
on
U
and
\widetilde{U}
, respectively. Since
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(\bar{x})=\bar{x}
, for each
x\in U
we have

\|\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x)-\bar{x}\|=\|\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x)-\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(\bar{x})\|\leq\ell\|x-\bar{x}\|,

which yields the first estimate in (
4.11
) with
\eta_{1}:=\ell
. To verify the second one, for each
x\in U
we deduce from (
4.12
) that
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x)\in\widetilde{U}
. The Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla f
with modulus
L>0
on
\widetilde{U}
leads us to the relationships

\displaystyle\big\|\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\text{\rm nor}}\big(x-\lambda\nabla f(x)\big)\big\|

\displaystyle=\big\|\nabla f\big(\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x)\big)-\nabla f(x)+\frac{1}{\lambda}\big(x-\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x)\big)\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\nabla f\big(\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x)\big)-\nabla f(x)\big\|+\frac{1}{\lambda}\big\|x-\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x)\big\|\leq\big(L+\frac{1}{\lambda}\big)\|\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x)-x\|

\displaystyle\leq\big(L+\frac{1}{\lambda}\big)\big(\|\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x)-\bar{x}\|+\|x-\bar{x}\|\big)\leq\big(L+\frac{1}{\lambda}\big)(1+\ell)\|x-\bar{x}\|,

which justify the second estimate in (
4.11
) with
\eta_{2}:=(L+\lambda^{-1})(1+\ell)
.

The obtained Proposition
4.7
immediately provides the justification of the approximate step in the version of Algorithm
1
for the structured optimization model (
4.1
).

Corollary 4.8

(
approximate step in structured nonconvex optimization).

{apstepncv}

In the setting of Proposition
4.7
, for each
\lambda\in(0,r^{-1})
there exist
\eta>0
and a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
such that whenever
x\in U
, we get a pair
(\widehat{x},\widehat{v})\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
satisfying the estimate

\|(\widehat{x},\widehat{v})-(\bar{x},0)\|\leq\eta\|x-\bar{x}\|,

where the approximation vectors
\widehat{x}
and
\widehat{v}
are selected by

\widehat{x}:=\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x)\;\text{ and }\;\widehat{v}:=\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\text{\rm nor}}(x-\lambda\nabla f(x)).

Let us now discuss how to implement the approximate step in Algorithm
1
to solve the structured optimization problem (
4.1
). The simplest way of choosing
(\widehat{x}^{k},\widehat{v}^{k})\in\mbox{\rm gph}\,\partial\varphi
in Algorithm
1
is

\widehat{x}^{k}:=x^{k}\;\text{ and }\;\widehat{v}^{k}\in\partial\varphi(x^{k}).

The difficulty in the above choice is that we cannot guarantee that
\partial\varphi(x)
is nonempty for an arbitrary
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
. The alternative way is choosing
\widehat{x}^{k}
and
\widehat{v}^{k}
as in (
3.12
). However, one of the difficulties here is to compute exactly the proximal mapping of
\varphi
, which is
the sum of two nonconvex functions. Thanks to Proposition
4.6
and Corollary
4.8
, we can choose
\widehat{x}^{k}
and
\widehat{v}^{k}
as follows:

\widehat{x}^{k}\in\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x^{k}-\lambda\nabla f(x^{k})),\text{ and }\widehat{v}^{k}:=\nabla f(\widehat{x}^{k})-\nabla f(x^{k})+\frac{1}{\lambda}(x^{k}-\widehat{x}^{k}).

(4.14)

Note that
\widehat{x}^{k}
and
\widehat{v}^{k}
in (
4.14
) are always well-defined since
\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x)
is nonempty for any
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
due to the prox-boundedness of
g

5
Generalized Line Search Proximal Gradient Method

{sec:newlinesearch}

In the line of the classical
pure
Newton method, the generalized one from
Section
4
provides fast convergence when the iterative sequence is near the solution point. To get its
globalization
that converges for any starting point in
{\rm I\!R}^{n}
in the nonconvex composite optimization problem (
4.1
), a natural approach is to replace Step 5 in Algorithm
2
by a
damped
step of the form

x^{k+1}:=\widehat{x}^{k}+\tau_{k}d^{k}

(5.1)

with an appropriate stepsize selection
\tau_{k}\in(0,1]
. To accomplish this, we develop in this section a
new line search method
based on the
forward-backward envelope
(FBE)
\varphi_{\lambda}
of the function
\varphi
from (
4.1
), which is defined below to guarantee the
descent property
at each iteration, i.e.,

\varphi_{\lambda}(\widehat{x}^{k}+\tau_{k}d^{k})<\varphi_{\lambda}(x^{k}),\quad k=0,1,\ldots.

(5.2)

To be more specific, we employ the construction of FBE introduced in
[
45
]
for convex composite functions and then largely used to design numerical methods in composite optimization; see. e.g., in
[
27
,
41
,
57
,
58
]
. In general, the FBE
\varphi_{\lambda}
approximates
\varphi
from below while linearizing the smooth part of the function
\varphi
in (
4.1
). This construction plays a crucial role in developing our new line search method to guarantee the
global convergence
of the generalized coderivative-based Newton method presented below in Section
6
.

Definition 5.1

(
forward-backward envelopes).

Let
\varphi
be given in (
4.1
). The
forward-backward envelope

(
FBE
)
of
\varphi
with the parameter value
\lambda>0
is given by

\varphi_{\lambda}(x):=\inf_{y\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}}\Big\{f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+g(y)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|y-x\|^{2}\Big\}.

(5.3)

The following properties are clearly implied by the definition.

(i)

\varphi_{\lambda}(x)\leq\varphi(x)
for all
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
.

(ii)
We have for each
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
,
\lambda\in(0,\lambda_{g})
, and
\widehat{x}\in\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}(x)
that

\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),\widehat{x}-x\rangle+g(\widehat{x})+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|\widehat{x}-x\|^{2},

(5.4)

where
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
is defined in (
4.2
).
Furthermore, FBE (
5.3
) can be equivalently written as

\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=f(x)-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\nabla f(x)\|^{2}+e_{\lambda}g\big(x-\lambda\nabla f(x)\big).

(5.5)

Recall from
[
24
, Proposition 7.1]
that under fairly mild assumptions, the FBE
\varphi_{\lambda}
is
{\cal C}^{1}
-smooth
with the explicit calculation of its gradient and the preservation of
stationary
points of
\varphi
.

Proposition 5.2

(
smoothness of FBEs and stationary points).

{FBEdiff}

Let
f
be of class
\mathcal{C}^{2}
around
\bar{x}

where
0\in\partial\varphi(\bar{x})
, and let
g
be prox-bounded and continuously prox-regular at
\bar{x}
for
-\nabla f(\bar{x})
with modulus
r>0
. Then for all
\lambda\in(0,r^{-1})
, there exists a neighborhood
U
of
\bar{x}
on which the FBE
\varphi_{\lambda}
is continuously differentiable and its gradient mapping is calculated by

\nabla\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda^{-1}\big(I-\lambda\nabla^{2}f(x)\big)\big(x-\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}(x-\lambda\nabla f(x))\big),

(5.6)

where

is the identity matrix. Moreover, if
I-\lambda\nabla^{2}f(x)
is nonsingular for

, we have the equivalence

\nabla\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=0\iff 0\in\partial\varphi(x).

(5.7)

If in addition

Remark 5.3

(
nonsingularity of the shifted matrix and
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
-property of FBEs).

{C11FBE}

(i)
The nonsingularity of the shifted matrix

in Proposition

is guaranteed if we assume in addition that
\nabla f
is Lipschitz continuous on

is positive-definite for all
x\in{\rm I\!R}^{n}
by the choice of
\lambda\in(0,1/L_{f})
. In this case, it follows from (

\nabla\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=0\iff x=\text{\rm Prox}_{\lambda g}\big(x-\lambda\nabla f(x)\big).

(5.8)

(ii)
The FBE

is of class
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
around
\bar{x}
if the mapping

\bar{x}
. Indeed, Lemma

tells us that the Moreau envelope

around

Prior to the design and verification of our new line-search method, we establish some related auxiliary results, which are certainly of their own interest. The first lemma provides a precise formula for representing the generalized Hessian (

Lemma 5.4

(

{calculatepsi}

\bar{x}

[
Proof.]

Remark 5.12

(
comparison with other FBE-based line search methods).

(i)
The primary difference between the line search method in Algorithm
3
and the ZeroFPR method presented in
[
58
]
is that we require the

of
\varphi_{\lambda}
after each iteration, i.e., the condition in (

) must always be satisfied. Additionally, our method relies on the computation of
\widehat{v}^{k}
, the value of the normal map, which is crucial for justifying our globalized coderivative-based Newton method (GCNM) as discussed in Section
6
. This plays an essential role in the convergence analysis of GCNM, whereas ZeroFPR does not require this information. Regarding the convergence results for the iterative sequence of our line search method in Algorithm
3
, the general properties concerning well-definedness and accumulation point behavior are similar to those of ZeroFPR. However, when the accumulation point is isolated, which is crucial for ensuring the superlinear convergence of our generalized Newton method discussed in Section
6
, Theorem
5.11
guarantees the linear convergence of the sequence
{x^{k}}
under Assumption
2
and also ensures the local superlinear convergence if

is chosen by solving the generalized Newton inclusions as suggested in Algorithm
4
. In contrast, to guarantee the superlinear convergence of ZeroFPR, the authors require strict twice epi-differentiability and the second-order epi-derivative of the regularizer

to be generalized quadratic, which is not often satisfied and is difficult to verify; see
[
58
, Theorems 5.10 and 5.11]
for more details. This requirement is not needed in our Assumption

, as we do not rely on the
\mathcal{C}^{2}
-smoothness of FBE around the limiting point, while ZeroFPR requires this condition to apply the classical quasi-Newton methods. Numerical experiments comparing our generalized Newton-type methods with ZeroFPR methods will be presented in Section
7
.

(ii)
Papers
[
26
,
27
]
also employ FBE to establish line search methods for achieving the global convergence. However, the ideas presented in these works are fundamentally different from those in our current paper. In
[
26
,
27
]
, the authors transform nonsmooth optimization problems into
\mathcal{C}^{1,1}
-smooth optimization problems and then apply coderivative-based Newton methods to the transformed problems, with the classical Armijo’s line search designed for continuously differentiable functions. In contrast, our
Algorithm

does not need to reformulate the original optimization problem. Moreover, the developed convergence analysis does not require any convexity assumptions on the functions or the quadratic form of the loss functions as needed in
[

.

Remark 6.2

(
on the modulus of prox-regularity).

The convergence analysis in Theorem
6.1
guarantees the fast local convergence provided that the parameter
\lambda
lies in the interval
0<\lambda<r^{-1}
, where
r
is the modulus of prox-regularity of
g
at the limiting point
\bar{x}
. In principle, this condition requires the explicit knowledge of
\bar{x}
. Although such dependence on local and often unknown constants is somewhat uncommon in globalized numerical methods, in many important cases the value of

can be determined independently of
\bar{x}
. For example, when the regularizer
g
is
weakly convex
with modulus
r>0
, as discussed in
[
5
]
for several important regularizers in machine learning models, the weak convexity modulus also serves as the modulus of prox-regularity at every point. Similarly, when
g
is the
\ell_{0}
-norm, which is
variationally convex
at every point as shown in Theorem
7.3
below, the modulus of prox-regularity is

. In this case,
\lambda
can be chosen as any positive value.

Remark 6.3

(
comparison with other globalized coderivative-based Newton methods).

{rem:comar}

Several globally convergent coderivative-based Newton methods were developed in
[
26
,
27
]
to solve (
4.1
) in the case where

is a convex quadratic function and
g
is l.s.c. and convex. Besides, the generalized damped Newton methods in
[
26
,
27
]
require the positive-definiteness of

on the whole space, which is a restrictive assumption for many practical models. In contrast, our new coderivative-based Newton method in Algorithm

7.24
). It follows from (
7.23
) that
\nabla f
is Lipschitz with constant
L=2\|A\|_{1}\|A\|_{\infty}
, where

\|A\|_{1}=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|a_{ij}|,\quad\|A\|_{\infty}=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|a_{ij}|,\quad A=[a_{ij}]_{i,j=\overline{1,n}}.

Therefore, Assumption
1
is fulfilled for problem (
7.21
). Thus it follows from Theorem
6.1
that if

is an accumulation point of
\{x^{k}\}
, then
\bar{x}

7.21
).

It remains to verify the Q-superlinear convergence of
\{x^{k}\}
provided that
\varphi
is subdifferentially continuous and variationally strongly convex at
\bar{x}
for
0
. This ensures that
\varphi
is continuously prox-regular at
\bar{x}
for
0
, and so
\bar{x}
is a tilt-stable local minimizer of
\varphi
by Proposition
2.7
. As mentioned above, the graph of
\partial g
is the union of finitely many closed convex sets, which ensures that
\partial g
is semismooth
∗
on its graph. Therefore, the superlinear convergence to
\bar{x}
of the iterates
\{x^{k}\}
generated by Algorithm
4
is guaranteed by Theorem
6.1
.

8
Conclusions and Future Research

{sec:conclusion}

In this paper, we propose and develop fast converging local and global generalized Newton methods to solve problems of nonconvex and nonsmooth optimization. The developed algorithms are far-going extensions of the classical Newton method, where the standard Hessian is replaced by its generalized version specifically designed for nonsmooth (of first-order and second-order) functions. This construction is coderivative-generated while giving rise to the names of our generalized Newton methods. The results highlight the good performance of the proposed algorithms across a range of applications including regularized least squares regression, Student’s t-regression models, and image restoration tasks, as demonstrated through comprehensive numerical experiments. In our future research, we plan to extend the spectrum of practical models, which can be solved by using our algorithms and their further developments.
