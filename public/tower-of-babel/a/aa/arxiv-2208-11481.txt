Exponential Inequalities for Some Mixing Processes and Dynamic Systems
Zihao Yuan Zihao.Yuan@ruhr-uni-bochum.de, Holger Dette Holger.Dette@ruhr-uni-bochum.de

Abstract
Many important dynamic systems, time series models or even algorithms exhibit non-strong mixing properties. In this paper, we introduce the general concept of 𝒞 p , ℱ \mathcal{C}_{p,\mathcal{F}} -mixing to cover such cases, where assumptions on the dependence structure become stronger with increasing p ∈ [ 1 , ∞ ] . p\in[1,\infty]. We derive a series of sharp exponential-type (or Bernstein-type) inequalities under this dependence concept for p = 1 p=1 and p = ∞ p=\infty . More specifically, 𝒞 ∞ , ℱ \mathcal{C}_{\infty,\mathcal{F}} -mixing is equal to the widely discussed 𝒞 \mathcal{C} -mixing ( Maume-Deschamps 2006 ) , and we prove a refinement of an Berntsein-type inequality in Hang and Steinwart 2017 for 𝒞 \mathcal{C} -mixing processes under more general assumptions. As there exist many stochastic processes and dynamic systems, which are not 𝒞 \mathcal{C} (or 𝒞 ∞ , ℱ \mathcal{C}_{\infty,\mathcal{F}} )-mixing, we derive Bernstein-type inequalities for 𝒞 1 , ℱ \mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}} -mixing processes as well and we use this result to investigate the convergence rates of plug-in-type estimators of the local conditional mode set for vector-valued output, in particular in situations where the density is less smooth.

Introduction

Concentration inequalities provide finite sample bounds on the deviation of an estimator from its expectation, which is crucial for understanding of the stability and reliability of statistical methods and machine learning algorithms. Hoeffding’s, Bernstein’s, McDiarmid’s or Talagrand’s inequalities are key techniques in proving non-asymptotic statistical guarantees, such as convergence rates of estimators and hypothesis testing procedures, and we refer to the monographs of
Devroye and Lugosi 2001
,
Györfi et al. 2002
,
Devroye et al. 2013
for more details and the references therein. However, a large part of the literature considers such inequalities under the assumption of independent data,
which is often not satisfied in areas such as spatial data regression, time series forecasting, and text or speech recognition. Consequently, numerous researchers have worked on the development of concentration inequalities for dependent data.
Among others, we mention the work of
Bosq 1993
;
Modha and Masry 1996
;
Chen and Shen 1998
;
Merlevède et al. 2011
who derived
and applied Bernstein-type inequalities for
empirical-mean-type statistics
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(Z_{i})
, where
(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a real valued
\alpha
- (strong-),
\beta
- mixing or
\phi
-mixing process and
h
denotes a real-valued measurable function.

Strong- (
\alpha
-)mixing is the weakest among these three mixing concepts, but as pointed out by many authors (see
Rio 2017
,
Doukhan 2012
, among others),
there exist many important time series models and dynamic systems, which are not strong-mixing, and several authors have proposed weaker mixing concepts to address these processes. For example,
Rio 1996
and
Olivier 2010
introduced generalizations of
\phi
-mixing and
Dedecker et al. 2007
developed mixing coefficients extending
\beta
- and
\alpha
- mixing. As these concepts of dependence modeling are still not including many of the commonly considered dynamical systems ,
Maume-Deschamps 2006
introduced

\mathcal{C}
-mixing.
Recently,
Hang and Steinwart 2017

established a Bernstein-type inequality for geometrically
\mathcal{C}
-mixing processes and used this result to prove that several state-of-the-art learning methods can recover the (essentially) optimal rates from the iid case.

In this paper we contribute to this literature from several perspectives. First,
in Section
2
, we introduce the very general concept of
\mathcal{C}_{p,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing (here
p\in[1,\infty]
is parameter and
{\cal F}
is a class of functions defining different mixing coefficients), which incorporates most of the commonly used mixing conditions for stochastic processes and dynamic systems that are not strong mixing. In particular, our most restrictive case,
\mathcal{C}_{\infty,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing is equal to the widely used
\mathcal{C}
-mixing condition
(
Maume-Deschamps 2006
)
. Second, under this dependence structure,
we derive sharp exponential inequalities for the empirical mean
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(Z_{i})
, where
h
is a real-valued measurable function (which can depend on
n
) and
(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a stochastic process taking value in measurable space
(\mathbf{Z},\mathcal{Z})
. More specifically,
for geometrically
\mathcal{C}_{\infty,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing processes we derive a sharper
Bernstein-type inequality. Compared to Theorem 3.1 in
Hang and Steinwart 2017
, our inequality is applicable under even more general assumptions (see Section
3.1
). Third, we also take a step forward by showing a sharp exponential inequality for the empirical mean whose data is collected from a
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing process
taking values in a general measurable space (see Section
3.2
). Deriving such results is challenging as, on one hand, common techniques to bound the Laplace transform are
not applicable, and, on the other hand, many of the available coupling lemmas can not be used, since we are interested in an inequality for processes taking values in a general measurable space.
Fourth, in Section
4
, we demonstrate the applicability of our results and derive the convergence rate of an estimator of the local conditional mode set of a conditional density with respect to Hausdorff loss. Unlike
Chen et al. 2016
, which concentrate on iid processes with a very smooth joint density, we also consider the scenario where the joint density is less smooth at its conditional mode points and where the data comes from a geometrically
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing process.

2
\mathcal{C}_{p,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing processes

In this section, we introduce the concept of “
C_{p,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing” and illustrate its significant generality. Throughout this paper,
we consider random variables defined on a common probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
taking values in a measurable space
(\mathbf{Z},\mathcal{Z})
and the class

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\{f:\mathbf{Z}:\to\mathbb{R}\penalty\ |\penalty\ f\text{ is a measurable such that}\ ||f||<\infty\},

(2.1)

where
\|\cdot\|
is a given (semi-)norm. Note that the (semi-)norm defines the class
{\cal F}
although we do not reflect this in our notation. Similarly, we will consider later functions
f=f_{n}\in{\cal F}
which depend on the sample size without making this explicit in the notation.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
p,q\in[1,\infty]
such that
{1\over p}+{1\over q}=1
.
A
\mathbf{Z}
-valued stochastic process

(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

is called
\mathcal{C}_{p,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing if there exists a decreasing
function
C:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)

such that the inequality

\displaystyle\max_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|Cov(Y,f(Z_{n+k}))|\leq C(k)||Y||_{q}||f||

(2.2)

holds for any
f\in\mathcal{F}
and any
real-valued random variable
Y
which is measurable with respect to the sigma field
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{n}=\sigma(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})
.
C(k)
is called the (
k
-th)
\mathcal{C}_{p,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing coefficient.

(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is called geometrically

\mathcal{C}_{p,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing of order
(\nu,b,\gamma)
, if
C(k)\leq\nu^{-bk^{\gamma}}
for some
\nu>1
,
b,\gamma>0
, and algebraically
\mathcal{C}_{p,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing of order
\gamma
, if
its mixing coefficients satisfy
C(k)\leq k^{-\gamma}
for some
\gamma>0
.

It is quite clear that a
C_{p,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing process is also
C_{p^{\prime},\mathcal{F}}
-mixing for all
p^{\prime}\geq p
.
Therefore, the most restrictive condition is
\mathcal{C}_{\infty,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing which coincides with the definition of
\mathcal{C}
-mixing process introduced in
Maume-Deschamps 2006
and
Hang and Steinwart 2017
.
This mixing concept has turned out to be general enough to include many important non-strong mixing dynamic systems as special cases and we refer to
Dedecker and Prieur 2005
and
Hang and Steinwart 2017
for some review of specific examples.
In order to connect Definition
2.1
with some widely used mixing concepts, we first derive a sufficient condition for a process to be
\mathcal{C}_{p,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing. For this purpose we consider for
B>0
the “ball”

\mathcal{F}_{B}=\{f\in\mathcal{F}:||f||\leq B\}
and introduce the coefficients

\displaystyle C_{p}^{*}(k):=\max_{n}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{B}}\big\|E[f(Z_{n+k})|\mathcal{M}_{1}^{n}]-E[f(Z_{n+k})]\big\|_{p}.

(2.3)

Then, we immediately obtain the following result.

Proposition 2.2
.

If the coefficient
C_{p}^{*}(k)
of the
\mathbf{Z}
-valued stochastic process
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges to
0
,
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
\mathcal{C}_{p,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing, and for any
f\in\mathcal{F}
, the inequality

\displaystyle\max_{n}|\text{Cov}(Y,f(Z_{n+k}))|\leq C(k)||Y||_{q}||f||

holds, where
{1\over p}+{1\over q}=1
and
C(k)=B^{-1}C_{p}^{*}(k)
.

Proposition
2.2
gives a sufficient condition for a process to be
\mathcal{C}_{p,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing. Note that the coefficients in (
2.3
) are essentially
built on the
L_{p}
-norm of the conditional expectation of specific centered random variables, with respect to some sigma algebras.
More precisely, with the notation

\displaystyle C_{p,\mathcal{F}_{B}}(\mathcal{M},Z):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{B}}\big\|E[f(Z)|\mathcal{M}]-E[f(Z)]\big\|_{p},

(2.4)

we have

\displaystyle C_{p}^{*}(k):=\max_{n}C_{p,\mathcal{F}_{B}}({\mathcal{M}_{1}^{n},Z_{n+k}}).

(2.5)

With this point of view, we can investigate the relation between
\mathcal{C}_{p,\mathcal{F}}
- and other
mixing concepts. In fact we will demonstrate that many mixing coefficients proposed in the literature are
special cases of the coefficient (
2.4
).

For this purpose we first review four important mixing coefficients which have been introduced for the case
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbb{R}
by
Dedecker and Prieur 2005
and are defined as follows

\displaystyle\tau(\mathcal{M},Z):=\int\big\|F_{Z|\mathcal{M}}(z)-F_{Z}(z)\big\|_{1}dz,

(2.6)

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}(\mathcal{M},Z):=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\big\|F_{Z|\mathcal{M}}(z)-F_{Z}(z)\big\|_{1},

(2.7)

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}(\mathcal{M},Z):=\big\|\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}|F_{Z|\mathcal{M}}(z)-F_{Z}(z)\big\|_{1},

(2.8)

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}(\mathcal{M},Z):=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\big\|F_{Z|\mathcal{M}}(z)-F_{Z}(z)\big\|_{\infty},

(2.9)

where
F_{Z|\mathcal{M}}(z)=\mathbb{P}(Z\leq z|\mathcal{M})
is the conditional distribution of
Z
given
\mathcal{M}
. With these notations, we can define mixing coefficients
\tau(\mathcal{M}_{1}^{n},Z_{n+k})
,
\tilde{\alpha}(\mathcal{M}_{1}^{n},Z_{n+k})
,
\tilde{\beta}(\mathcal{M}_{1}^{n},Z_{n+k})
and
\tilde{\phi}(\mathcal{M}_{1}^{n},Z_{n+k})
, which can be compared with (
2.5
).
Note that we use the notation
\tilde{\alpha}
,
\tilde{\beta}
and
\tilde{\phi}
to distinguish these coefficients from those appearing in the definition of
\alpha
-(strong-),
\beta
- and
\phi
-mixing
(
Doukhan 2012
, see, for example,)
and
that
\tilde{\alpha}(\mathcal{M},Z)\leq\tilde{\beta}(\mathcal{M},Z)\leq\tilde{\phi}(\mathcal{M},Z)
.
As pointed out in

Dedecker and Prieur 2005
and
Dedecker et al. 2007
,
the coefficients (
2.6
)-(
2.9
) cover many non-strong mixing time series or dynamic systems. To explain how they are included in Definition
2.1
, we note that, by Lemma 1 in
Dedecker and Prieur 2005
,
there exist alternative representations for these coefficients that are

\displaystyle\tau(\mathcal{M},Z)=\Big\|\sup\Big\{\Big|\int fd\mathbb{P}_{Z|\mathcal{M}}(x)-\int fd\mathbb{P}_{Z}(x)\Big|,f\in\text{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R})\Big\}\Big\|_{1},

(2.10)

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}(\mathcal{M},Z)=\sup\big\{\|E[f(Z)|\mathcal{M}]-E[f(Z)]\|_{1}:f\in\text{BV}_{1}(\mathbb{R})\big\},

(2.11)

\displaystyle\tilde{\beta}(\mathcal{M},Z)=\Big\|\sup\Big\{\Big|\int fd\mathbb{P}_{Z|\mathcal{M}}(x)-\int fd\mathbb{P}_{Z}(x)\Big|f\in\text{BV}_{1}(\mathbb{R})\Big\}\Big\|_{1},

(2.12)

\displaystyle\tilde{\phi}(\mathcal{M},Z)=\sup\big\{\|E[f(Z)|\mathcal{M}]-E[f(Z)]\|_{\infty}\big|\penalty\ f\in\text{BV}_{1}(\mathbb{R})\big\}.

(2.13)

Here
\mathbb{P}_{Z|\mathcal{M}}
is the conditional distribution
of the random variable
Z
with respect to
\sigma
-field
{\cal M}
and

\text{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
and
\text{BV}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
are the unit balls in the normed spaces of functions
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
equipped with the Lipschitz-norm
|f|_{\text{Lip}}
and the total variation (
TV
)-norm
|f|_{TV}
, respectively. Apparently, the coefficients
C_{p,\mathcal{F}_{B}}(\mathcal{M},Z)
defined in (
2.4
) can be regarded as a direct generalization of these four mixing coefficients.
Due to Proposition
2.2
, it instantly becomes a special case of our
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing processes defined in Definition
2.1
.

We emphasize that
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing also covers the traditional
\alpha
-mixing for real valued processes. To see this we note,
for real-valued random variables, one can show that
the representation (
2.11
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\tilde{\alpha}(\mathcal{M},Z)

\displaystyle=\sup\{\mathbb{P}(Z\leq z|\mathcal{M})-\mathbb{P}(Z\leq z)\penalty\ |\penalty\ z\in\mathbb{R}\}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(\mathcal{M},\sigma(Z)):=\sup\{\mathbb{P}(Z\in A|\mathcal{M})-\mathbb{P}(Z\in A):A\in\sigma(Z)\}.

Hence, a
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing condition incorporates an
\alpha
-mixing condition defined on a sub-sigma algebra and a sigma-algebra generated by one real-valued random variable.
Consequently, a real-valued
\alpha
-mixing process
(
Doukhan 2012
, see)

is also
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing. Moreover, as pointed out in
Definition 2.5 and Lemma 2.1 in
Dedecker et al. 2007
, these observations are also correct in the case
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Furthermore,

\tau
-mixing processes taking values in a Polish space are
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing as well. This follows from Proposition
2.2
, the inequality

\displaystyle\tau(\mathcal{M},Z)

\displaystyle\geq\sup\left\{\left\|\int fd\mathbb{P}_{Z|\mathcal{M}}(x)-\int fd\mathbb{P}_{Z}(x)\right\|_{1},f\in\text{Lip}_{1}(\mathbb{R})\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup\{||E[f(Z)|\mathcal{M}]-E[f(Z)]||_{1}:f\in\text{Lip}_{1}\}

(2.14)

and the fact that
\tau(\mathcal{M},Z)
can be easily be defined for processes taking values a Polish space
(
Dedecker and Prieur 2005
, see)
.
Summarizing this discussion, besides the classical mixing concepts, Definition
2.1
covers many useful non-strong mixing processes such as contractive Markov models or autoregressive models with a Lipschitz mapping. For more thorough discussion of these examples, we refer the reader to
Dedecker and Prieur 2004
;
Dedecker and Prieur 2005
and Chapter 3 in
Dedecker et al. 2007
.

As illustrated in the previous paragraph, the choice of the class
{\cal F}
and of the (semi-)norm
\|\cdot\|
determine the type of dependence conditions for the stochastic process. Following
Hang and Steinwart 2017
, we consider the (semi-)norm

\displaystyle||f||=||f||_{\infty}+|f|_{SN}

(2.15)

of a function
f:\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{z\in\mathbf{Z}}|f(z)|
and

|\cdot|_{SN}
is a chosen semi-norm (for example,
|f|_{\text{Lip}}
or
|f|_{TV}
).
Hang and Steinwart 2017
made a literature review of some interesting examples of dynamic systems which are covered by the use of
a semi-norms in Definition
2.1
.

3
Exponential Inequalities

In this section, we will derive several new exponential inequalities for the mean
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(Z_{i})

from a
\mathcal{C}_{\infty,\mathcal{F}}
or
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing proces
(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
h:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
is a function such that
E[h(Z_{i})]=0
(
i=1,\ldots,n
) and
h\in{\cal F}
is allowed to depend on
n
. For
\mathcal{C}_{\infty,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing process, we show a slightly sharper inequality under more general conditions
compared to the pioneering work of
Hang and Steinwart 2017
. For (geometrical)
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing process, we show a sharp exponential inequality under a very mild assumption on the characteristic functions of
h(Z_{i})
, which is much weaker than the Cramér condition used in Edgeworth expansion.
To our best knowledge, this is the first sharp inequality under such a general mixing condition. Furthermore, the inequality holds for random variables taking values in an arbitrary measurable space
\mathbf{Z}
and
the value of
t
in the event
\{|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(Z_{i})|>t\}
only appears in the exponential and not in
an additional remainder term. Note that this is desirable property that is often enjoyed only under the
\beta
-mixing (absolute regularity) condition and its special cases.

3.1
\mathcal{C}_{\infty,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing Processes

For a semi-norm of the form (
2.15
) Definition
2.1
with
p=\infty
yields a generic covariance inequality for a

C_{\infty,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing process, that is

\displaystyle\max_{n}|\text{Cov}(Y,f(Z_{n+k}))|\leq C(k)||Y||_{1}(||f||_{\infty}+|f|_{SN}).

(3.1)

This coincides with the definition of
\mathcal{C}
-mixing process in
Hang and Steinwart 2017
(see equation (2.7) therein), who derived
an exponential inequality
for the mean
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(Z_{i})

(where
\mathbb{E}[Z_{i}]=0
). Their work was motivated by the fact that the available results for the concentration of
g(Z_{1},..,Z_{n})-E[g(Z_{1},..,Z_{n})]

(
Dedecker and Prieur 2005
;
Chazottes et al. 2005
;
Chazottes and Gouëzel 2012
, see, for example,)
do not use any variance information and can be improved if the variance of
h(Z_{i})
is small. However, as pointed out by
Hang and Steinwart 2017
,
such situations appear quite frequently in the analysis of learning algorithms.
Another application, where Bernstein-type inequalities reflect the effect of variance are useful, are uniform convergence rates for kernel estimates
in nonparametric regression

(
Hansen 2008
;
Kristensen 2009
;
Li et al. 2012
;
Vogt 2012
, see)
.

Hang and Steinwart 2017
obtained a Bernstein-type inequality using the estimate (
3.1
) iteratively to bound the Laplace transform of averaged partial sums after a “sub-lattice” trick. However, as we point out below, their approach yields an additional but unnecessary logarithmic factor and requires the stationarity of the process
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. We obtain an improvement using a more direct procedure to bound Laplace transforms and making full use of the fact that the upper bound of the covariance inequality (
2.1
) only relies on
L_{1}
-norm of random variable
Y
. Before we state our results, we introduce the following key assumption on the semi-norm
|\cdot|_{SN}
, which is also made in
Hang and Steinwart 2017
.

Assumption 3.1
.

Given a semi-norm
|\cdot|_{SN}
and a function
f
with
|f|_{SN}<\infty
, we assume

\displaystyle|e^{f}|_{SN}\leq||e^{f}||_{\infty}|f|_{SN}.

Example 1
.

Hang and Steinwart 2017
listed some examples of semi-norms which
satisfy Assumption
3.1
and
yield the
\mathcal{C}
-mixing (
\mathcal{C}_{\infty,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing) property of important dynamical systems. These include dynamic systems with piecewise-expanding maps, uniformly hyperbolic attractors and non-uniformly hyperbolic uni-modal maps

(
Viana 1997
, see also)
.
Theorem 2.9 in
Hang and Steinwart 2017
establishes sufficient conditions for
\mathcal{C}
-mixing-type dynamic systems associated with these semi-norms and
for the sake of completeness we list these semi-norms here.

(1)

If
\mathbf{Z}
is any given set we define the semi-norm
|f|_{SN}=0
for any function
f:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
.

(2)

If
\mathbf{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
is open, we define for any continuously differentiable

f:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbb{R}

|f|_{SN}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}||\partial_{i}f||_{\infty},

where
\partial_{i}f
denotes the partial derivative of
f
with respect to its
i
-th variable.

(3)

If
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbb{R}^{D}
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
,
we define for any

\alpha
-Hölder continuous function

f:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle|f|_{SN}=\sup_{z\neq z^{\prime}}\frac{|f(z)-f(z^{\prime})|}{||z-z||_{E}^{\alpha}},

where
||\cdot||_{E}
denotes some norm on
\mathbb{R}^{D}
.

(4)

If
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbb{R}
, we define
for any function

f:\mathbf{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
with bounded total variation
|f|_{SN}=|f|_{TV}

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose that
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

is a
\mathbf{Z}
-valued
geometrically
\mathcal{C}_{\infty,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing stochastic process of order
(\nu,b,\gamma)
. Let
h\in{\cal F}
satisfy Assumption
3.1
,
E[h(Z_{i})]=0
,
E(h^{2}(Z_{i}))\leq\sigma^{2}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
,
||h||_{\infty}\leq A
and
|h|_{SN}\leq B
, for constants
\sigma,A,B>0
. For (arbitrary)
\omega>1
define
N_{0}:=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}:\frac{A+B}{An^{\omega-1}}\leq\frac{1}{3}\}
, then for any
t>0
and
n\geq N_{0}

\mathbb{P}\Big\{\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(Z_{i})\Big|\geq t\Big\}\leq 2e^{2}\exp\Big(-\frac{nt^{2}}{4(\frac{\omega}{{b}}\log_{\nu}n)^{{1}/{\gamma}}(\sigma^{2}+tA)}\Big)\penalty\ .

(3.2)

It is of interest to compare this result with
Theorem 3.1 in
Hang and Steinwart 2017
.
First, note that Theorem
3.2
uses separate bounds
A
and
B
on the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
and the semi-norm
|\cdot|_{SN}
, respectively, and that it is applicable for any
\omega>1
. Therefore, if
A
is a fixed positive constant,
it gives an exponential bound, whenever
|h|_{SN}
is of order
O(n^{\alpha})
, for arbitrary fixed
\alpha>0
. In contrast, Theorem 3.1 in
Hang and Steinwart 2017
provides such a bound only in the case
\alpha\leq 2
. Second, Theorem
3.2
does not require the assumption of a stationary process

(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Third,
the inequality (
3.2
) is on the logarithmic level sharper compared to Theorem 3.1 in
Hang and Steinwart 2017
.

We now continue deriving a concentration inequality for algebraic
\mathcal{C}_{\infty,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing processes.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
\mathbf{Z}
-valued algebraically
\mathcal{C}
-mixing process of order
\gamma
, where
\gamma>0
. For all
h:\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we assume
E[h(Z_{i})]=0
,
E[h^{2}(Z_{i})]\leq\sigma^{2}
, for
i=1,...,n
,

||h||_{\infty}\leq A
and
||h||\leq B\leq n^{\alpha}
, for some
\sigma
,
A
,
B>0

and
\alpha>0
.
If Assumption
3.1
is satisfied for

, we have for any
t>0
and
n\geq N_{1}=\min\{n\in\mathbb{N}:n^{\alpha}>A\}
,

\mathbb{P}\Big\{\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(Z_{i})\Big|\geq t\Big\}\leq C_{1}\exp\Big(-\frac{n^{{\gamma}/{(\gamma+1})}t^{2}}{2(\sigma^{2}+tA)}\Big),

(3.3)

where

.

For algebraically
{\cal C}
-mixing processes there does not exist a directly comparable result.
To our best knowledge,
Blanchard and Zadorozhnyi 2019
is the only work providing a concentration inequality for a sum of
Banach-valued random variables from an
algebraically
\mathcal{C}
-mixing process. In contrast, our Theorem
3.3
provides such an
inequality for the process
(h(Z_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is allowed to take values in any given measurable space
\mathbf{Z}
.
Although both papers consider different scenarios, the derived
bound in (
3.3
)
shows similarities with the bound obtained by
Blanchard and Zadorozhnyi 2019
in their Theorem 3.5 and Proposition 3.6. In principle, their results can be applied
to processes of the form
(h(Z_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. However,
in order to do this, one has to show that the
(h(Z_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
remains a
\mathcal{C}
-mixing process and its
\mathcal{C}
-mixing coefficient is smaller than that of
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. To our best knowledge, there are no general sufficient conditions that guarantee this property.
Showing such results seems to be inherently difficult as indicated by
Dedecker et al. 2007
for
\tilde{\alpha}
-mixing processes.
Up to our knowledge, the most recent progress is that, for a real valued

\tilde{\alpha}
-mixing process

(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, the process
(h(Z_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is still
\tilde{\alpha}
-mixing with not enlarged mixing coefficient, if the function
h
is strictly monotone
(
Dedecker et al. 2007
, see Chapter 3 in)
.

3.2
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing processes

In this section we will derive a sharp exponential inequality reflecting the variance information for a geometrically
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing
\mathbf{Z}
-valued process
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. In this case, we have
p=1
and with (
2.15
) Definition

2.1
yields the
covariance inequality

\displaystyle\max_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\text{Cov}(Y,f(Z_{n+k}))\leq C(k)||Y||_{\infty}(||f||_{\infty}+|f|_{SN}).

(3.4)

Compared with (
3.1
), this inequality is much weaker since only the
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
-norm of the random variable
Y
is involved. This simple difference
makes the derivation of exponential inequalities for
{1\over n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(Z_{i})
from a (general)
\mathbf{Z}
-valued

\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing process
(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
much harder:
First, it is nearly impossible to use similar arguments as given in the proof of Theorems
3.2
and
3.3
, since
the use of the
L_{1}
-norm of
Y
in the application of the covariance inequality is crucial for deriving bounds on the Laplace transform.
Second, the other powerful tool in showing sharp exponential inequalities for weakly dependent processes is coupling. While, there
are many strong coupling lemmas available for processes with mixing coefficients defined for two sigma fields
(see
Merlevede and Peligrad 2002
, for a nice survey)
,
the literature on coupling for processes with
mixing coefficients defined for a sigma field and a random variable is rather scarce. We refer to
Rio 1995
,
Rio 2017
,
Peligrad 2002
and
Dedecker and Prieur 2005
, who derived some result for real valued
\alpha
,
\tilde{\alpha}
- and
\tau
-mixing processes. Some extensions for
\tau
-mixing process taking values in a Polish space can be found
Dedecker et al. 2006
. However, in this paper, we investigate
\mathbf{Z}
-valued
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing processes, where
\mathbf{Z}
is taking values in a general metric space. Thus, the model under consideration is substantially more general (with respect to the image set of the random variables and the dependence concept) as in the cited references. Third, as mentioned before, coupling of
h(Z_{n})
does not always work well, because, to the best of our knowledge, it is not obvious if the mixing coefficients
corresponding to the pair
(\mathcal{M},h(Z))
can be bounded by that corresponding to
(\mathcal{M},Z)
.

Summarizing, the available tools can not be used to derive exponential inequalities valid for the whole class of
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing processes without any additional assumptions on measurable space
\mathbf{Z}
.
However, we will show below that a mild assumption (Assumption
3.5
) on the characteristic function of
h(Z_{i})
is sufficient to bypass this barrier.

Assumption 3.4
.

For the semi-norm
|\cdot|_{SN}
and function
f\in{\cal F}
, we assume

\displaystyle S(u):=|\cos(uf)|_{SN}\lor|\sin(uf)|_{SN}\leq C_{B}|u|^{\eta_{0}}|f|_{SN},

(3.5)

for every
u\in\mathbb{R}
,
where
\eta_{0}\geq 0
and
C_{B}>0
are constants independent of sample size
n
.

By straightforward calculations we can show the semi-norms listed in Example
1
satisfy Assumption
3.4
.
Note that the semi-norm
|\cdot|_{SN}
is determined by the particular
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing condition under consideration and often chosen to reflect smoothness. Roughly speaking, Assumption
3.4

essentially requires that the function
S(u)
diverges at a polynomial rate as
|u|\nearrow\infty
. Another key point is that it does not impose any restriction on the boundedness of the function
f
, which is much more general than Assumption
3.1
.
We finally introduce the following assumption about the characteristic functions

\psi_{h(Z_{i})}(u)=E[\exp(iuh(Z_{i}))]
of the random variables
h(Z_{i})
(
i=1,\ldots,n
).

Assumption 3.5
.

Consider a

\mathbf{Z}
-valued
geometrically
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing process
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
We assume there exists a subset
I_{n}^{\prime}\subset I_{n}:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
such that the following two conditions are satisfied.

1)

There exists constants
0<c<C\leq 1
such that, for every given
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\frac{\text{Card}(I_{n}^{\prime})}{n}\in[c,C]
.

2)

For each
i\in I_{n}^{\prime}
and any fixed
\kappa,\Theta>0
that are independent of
n
,
there exists a constant

N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and a sequence
\{\tau_{n}\}_{n\geq N_{0}}
independent of
i
such that

\tau_{n}\geq\frac{2\kappa(\log n)(b^{-1}\Theta\log n)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}}{n},

(here
\gamma
is is the constant in the definition of the geometrically mixing process)
such that, for any
n\geq N_{0}
,

\displaystyle|\psi_{h(Z_{i})}(u)|\leq 1-\tau_{n},\ \forall\ |u|\in[M_{n},\infty),

(3.6)

where
\{M_{n}\}_{n\geq N_{0}}
is a sequence of increasing positive constants satisfying
M_{n}\leq n^{\beta}
, for some
\beta>0
.

If
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is identically distributed, we only require point 2) above.

Assumption
3.5
is weaker than the famous Cramér condition

{\limsup}_{|t|\rightarrow\infty}|\psi_{h(Z_{i})}(t)|<1,

which is frequently used in the field of Edgeworth expansions

(
Hall 2013
, see, for example,)
.
Note that it is is implied by the weak Cramér condition introduced by
Angst and Poly 2017
. Thus, all the examples provided in this references do not satisfy Cramér’s condition but satisfy (
3.6
). Moreover, for statistical applications, particularly kernel smoothers, we mention the recent work of
Calonico et al. 2018
who introduced an
n
-dependent Cramér condition (see Assumptions S.I.3.3 and S.II.3.3 in the corresponding online supplement), which implies in fact assumption (
3.6
). We further show that, under very general conditions on the kernel, the summands in common kernel density estimator satisfy condition (
3.6
); see Proposition
4.5
below. This allows us to use the following Theorem
3.6
to reveal the concentration phenomenon of kernel density estimators under a geometrically
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing assumption, which is usually a crucial step in obtaining the convergence rates of many density-based plug-in type estimators, like the local conditional mode set estimator discussed in Section
4
. Similar results as in Proposition
4.5
can be derived for
the summands in kernel regression estimators, but the details are omitted for the sake of brevity.

For the statement of the main theorem of this section, we define the decomposition of index set
\{1,\ldots,n\}=\cup_{j=1}^{p_{n}}I_{j}
with
p_{n}\leq n/2
by

\displaystyle I_{j}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\{j+kp_{n}:0\leq k\leq[n/p_{n}]\},&\text{when $1\leq j\leq r_{n}$},\\
&\{j+kp_{n}:0\leq k\leq[n/p_{n}]-1\},&\text{when $r_{n}+1\leq j\leq p_{n}$},\end{aligned}\right.

(3.7)

where
r_{n}=n-p_{n}\lfloor n/p_{n}\rfloor
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Suppose that
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

is a
\mathbf{Z}
-valued
geometrically
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing process of order
(\nu,b,\gamma)
. Let
h\in{\cal F}
such that for
i=1,\ldots,n
the random variable
h(Z_{i})
is centered and satisfies a
Bernstein condition, that is
E|h(Z_{i})|^{k}\leq\frac{1}{2}k!A^{k-2}E|h(Z_{i})|^{2}
for all
k\geq 3

(with a universal constant
A>0
).
Further assume that
|h|_{SN}\leq n^{B}
, for some
B>0
.
Then, under Assumptions
3.4
and
3.5
, there exists an integer
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, for any
n\geq N_{0}
,
t>0
and
\delta>1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(Z_{i})\Big|>t\Big)\leq 2p_{n}\exp\Big(-\frac{nt^{2}}{p_{n}(\sigma^{2}_{\max}+{At}/{3})}\Big)+\frac{C^{\prime}_{\delta}+8\max_{i=1}^{n}E|h(Z_{i})|}{n^{\delta-1}},

(3.8)

where
p_{n}=(b^{-1}\Theta_{\delta}\log_{\nu}n)^{\frac{1}{\gamma}}
,

\sigma_{\max}^{2}=\max_{1\leq j\leq p_{n}}\frac{1}{|I_{j}]}\sum_{k=1}^{|I_{j}]}{\rm Var}\left(h(Z_{j+kp_{n}})\right)

and

\Theta_{\delta}
and

C^{\prime}_{\delta}
are universal constants specified in equation (
A.30
) and (
A
) in the appendix, respectively. Furthermore, if
Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}
are identically distributed, the inequality (
3.8
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\Big|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(Z_{i})\Big|>t\Big)\leq 2p_{n}\exp\Big(-\frac{nt^{2}}{p_{n}({\rm Var}(h(Z_{1}))+{At}/{3})}\Big)+\frac{C^{\prime}_{\delta}+8E|h(Z_{1})|}{n^{\delta-1}}.

(3.9)

We emphasize that the inequalities (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) hold for any
\delta>0
.
This constant only affects the sharpness of the exponential part on the right-hand side up to a constant factor (entering through
p_{n}
). On the other hand, increasing
the constant
\delta
makes the second term smaller (but the constants
C^{\prime}_{\delta}
and
\Theta_{\delta}
increase with
\delta
).
Note also that this remainder is independent of
t
. This makes the estimates (
3.8
) and (
3.9
) different from many exponential inequalities for the mean of dependent random variables, where the remainder term does in fact depend on
t

(
Bosq 2012
, see, for example Theorems 1.3 and 1.4 in)
. As we pointed out before, this is a property often enjoyed by
\beta
-mixing process and its spacial cases only. We also emphasize that the assumptions in Theorem
3.6
on the dependency structure are very general and that our result is valid for any set
\mathbf{Z}
.

4
Conditional Mode Set Estimation

In this section we will use the new exponential inequalities to derive convergence rates for a kernel-based estimator of the local conditional mode set of the density of a stochastic process satisfying a geometrically
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing condition. The conditional mode is an alternative regression method to explore the relationship between an output
Y
and an input
X
. As pointed out by
Chen et al. 2016
, the conditional mode often yields a better reflection of the trend of the output variables and a sharper prediction band. Moreover, it is also more robust with respect to the skewness of the distribution of the error.

Suppose
(X,Y)
is a random vector taking values in set
\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D_{X}}\times\mathbb{R}^{D_{Y}}
with joint density
f_{(X,Y)}
. Suppose that the marginal density
f_{X}
of
X
satisfies

\displaystyle\inf_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{X}}}f_{X}(x)>0,

(4.1)

and define

\displaystyle\Omega_{x_{0}}=\{y\in\mathbb{Y}:(x_{0},y)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{Y}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D_{Y}}.

(4.2)

For any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{X}
, we define the “
local conditional mode set at point
x_{0}
” by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{x_{0}}

\displaystyle=\Big\{y\in\mathbb{Y}:\exists\ \text{cube}\ I_{y}\subset\Omega_{x_{0}}\ \text{such that}\ f_{Y|X}(y|x_{0})=\arg\max_{z\in I_{y}}f_{Y|X}(z|x_{0})\Big\},

\displaystyle=\Big\{y\in\mathbb{Y}:\exists\ \text{cube}\ I_{y}\subset\Omega_{x_{0}}\ \text{such that}\ f_{(X,Y)}(x_{0},y)=\arg\max_{z\in I_{y}}f_{(X,Y)}(x_{0},z)\Big\},

where
f_{Y|X}
denotes the conditional density of
Y
given
X=x_{0}
and the equality between holds because of assumption (
4.1
). Each element of the set
\mathcal{Y}_{x_{0}}
is a local maximizer of the function
f_{(X,Y)}(x_{0},z)
. We introduce the following key assumption on the mode set.

Assumption 4.1
.

For each given
x_{0}\in\mathbb{X}
,
the local conditional mode set
\mathcal{Y}_{x_{0}}
is a finite set with
K_{x_{0}}
elements. We further assume that

\mathbf{K}=\sup_{x_{0}\in\mathbb{X}}K_{x_{0}}<\infty
.

Note that in contrast to most of the literature we do not assume that the local conditional mode is unique. A uniqueness assumption makes the local conditional mode set equal to global mode set and simplifies the analysis of conditional mode estimators substantially.
Under the uniqueness assumption,
Collomb et al. 1986
proposed to estimate the global mode
directly by determining the maximum of a kernel density estimator. Moreover, this assumption implies that the global conditional mode,

\displaystyle\theta(x):=\text{Mode}(Y|X=x_{0})=\arg\max_{y}f_{Y|X}(y|x_{0}),

(4.3)

is a function of
x
and one can develop a framework similar to conditional mean or quantile regression considering the model

\displaystyle Y=\theta(X)+\epsilon,

where the error satisfies
\text{Mode}(\epsilon|X=x)=0.

This setting is obviously more robust to the skewness and outliers and meanwhile several authors have
worked on estimating the function
\theta
under different model assumptions using traditional regression techniques
(see
Lee 1989
;
Lee 1993
;
Kemp and Silva 2012
;
Yao and Li 2014
;
Krief 2017
;
Ota et al. 2019
;
Zhou and Huang 2019
;
Feng et al. 2020
;
Kemp et al. 2020
;
Ullah et al. 2021
;
Ullah et al. 2022
;
Ullah et al. 2023
, among others)
. However, in many applications the uniqueness assumption (
\mathbf{K}=1
) might be too restrictive. If
\mathbf{K}>1
, the function
\theta
in (
4.3
) is a set-valued mapping. Compared to the literature on mode estimation under the uniqueness assumption, the work considering the case
\mathbf{K}>1
is much more scarce. To our best knowledge, the only available theoretical work in this direction is
Chen et al. 2016
who investigated the asymptotic properties of an estimator of the local conditional mode set under the assumption of the existence of the second-order partial derivatives of the joint density of
(X,Y)
. As in
Collomb 1984
, their
set estimator is based on kernel estimators of the joint density and its higher-order partial derivatives.

Nearly all of the cited literature investigates estimators under the assumption of iid data. However, in many applications, such an assumption might not be reasonable. For example, one might be interested in the most likely value of
W_{n+1}
based on the observation of
W_{n}
from a stationary time series
(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. In this case we are interested in the mode set of the conditional density of
Y_{i}=W_{i+1}
given
X_{i}=W_{i}
.
Therefore, in this section, we investigate the convergence rate of the Hausdorff loss of a kernel-based local conditional mode set estimator for dependent data.
Moreover, in contrast to
Chen et al. 2016
,
we do not assume that the conditional density function is differentiable at its local or global maxima and our approach therefore avoids estimating the first and second order partial derivatives of the conditional density. Furthermore, we also consider the case of vector-valued output
Y
.

To be precise, consider a
\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D_{X}}\times\mathbb{R}^{D_{Y}}
-valued stochastic process
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with identical marginal distributions with density
f_{(X;Y)}
. Let
K
denote a (one-dimensional) kernel function supported on the interval
[-1,1]
, and define the product kernels

\displaystyle K_{x_{0},h}(X_{i})

\displaystyle=\prod_{s=1}^{D_{X}}K\Big(\frac{X_{is}-x_{0s}}{h}\Big),\ K_{y,h}(Y_{i})=\prod_{s=1}^{D_{Y}}K\Big(\frac{Y_{is}-y_{s}}{h}\Big).

(4.4)

where
x_{0}=(x_{01},...,x_{0D_{X}})^{\top}
,

X_{i}=(X_{i1},...,X_{iD_{X}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{X}}
,
Y_{i}=(Y_{i1},...,Y_{iD_{Y}})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{D_{Y}}
and
h>0
is a bandwidth. We consider the common product-type estimator

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{(X,Y)}(x_{0},y)=\frac{1}{nh^{D}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{x_{0},h}(X_{i})K_{y,h}(Y_{i}),

(4.5)

for the joint density of the vector
(X_{i},Y_{i})
, where
D=D_{X}+D_{Y}
and define

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{x_{0}}=\{y\in\Omega_{x_{0}}:\ \exists\ \text{cube}\ I_{y}\subset\Omega_{x_{0}}\ \text{such that}\ \hat{f}_{(X,Y)}(x_{0},y)=\arg\max_{z\in I_{y}}\hat{f}_{(X,Y)}(x_{0},z)\},

(4.6)

as the estimator of the set of local conditional modes.

Similar to
Chen et al. 2016
, we
measure the performance of the estimator
\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{x_{0}}
by
the Hausdorff loss

\displaystyle\triangle_{n}(x_{0}):=\max\Big\{\sup_{y\in\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{x_{0}}}d(y,\mathcal{Y}_{x_{0}}),\sup_{y\in\ \mathcal{Y}_{x_{0}}}d(y,\widehat{\mathcal{Y}}_{x_{0}})\Big\},

(4.7)

where

d(y,A)=\inf_{x\in A}||y-x||_{E}
denotes the distance of
y
from the set
A
and
||\cdot||_{E}
is the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{D_{Y}}
. To study
\triangle_{n}(x_{0})
we make the following
regularity assumptions on the joint density
f_{(X,Y)}
.

Assumption 4.2
.

The joint density

f_{(X,Y)}
satisfies the following conditions

(C1)

The joint density
f_{(X,Y)}(x,y)
is bounded on
\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{Y}
and coordinate-wise in the
\alpha
-Hölder class. That is, if
f_{j}
is the function obtained by considering
f
as function of the
j
coordinate and keeping all other coordinates fixed, then
f_{j}
is
\lfloor\alpha\rfloor
times differentiable and the
\lfloor\alpha\rfloor
-th derivative is Hölder continuous of order
(\alpha-\lfloor\alpha\rfloor)
; (
j=1,\ldots,D
);
see Definition 1.2 in
Tsybakov 2009
.

(C2)

For any
x_{0}\in\mathbb{X}
, the function
y\to f_{(X,Y)}(x_{0},y)
is
\beta
-distinguishable on its mode set for some
\beta>0
, that is: for any
y^{*}\in\mathcal{Y}_{x_{0}}
,
\beta
is the smallest positive number such that

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\inf_{\|y^{*}-y\|_{E}<\delta}\frac{|f(x_{0},y^{*})-f(x_{0},y)|}{\|y^{*}-y\|_{E}^{\beta}}\geq c_{y^{*}}>0,

holds for some positive constant
c_{y^{*}}
that does not depend of
y
.

Remark 4.3
.

The constant
\beta
in condition (C2) geometrically describes how “flat” the function is at its conditional mode point. If
\mathbb{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}
and the function
y\to f_{(X,Y)}(x_{0},y)

is
l\geq 1
times continuously differentiable at
y^{*}
, the magnitude of
\beta
is determined by the rate of
\big|\frac{\partial f_{(X,Y)}}{\partial y}(x_{0},y)\big|
as
y\rightarrow y^{*}
.
Furthermore, to illustrate this condition in the case
\mathbb{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}
, we list 3 specific examples.

(1)

If
f_{(X,Y)}(x_{0},y)
is a (mixture) triangular-type or (mixture) exponential-type distributions,
f(x_{0},y)
is distinguishable with
\beta=1
on its mode set.

(2)

Let
X
be uniformly distributed on the interval
[0.4,0.9]
and
w_{1},\ldots,w_{K}

are positive weights with
\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}=1
for some
w_{k}\geq 0
and define

\displaystyle f_{Y|X}(y|x_{0})=\sum_{k=0}^{K}\frac{w_{k}}{2}(1-|y-2k|^{x_{0}})1[|y-2k|\leq 1].

(4.8)

Then
f_{(X,Y)}(x_{0},y)
is distinguishable with the
\beta=x_{0}
(
x_{0}\in[0.4,0.9]
) on its mode set
\{2k:k=0,1,..,K\}
.

(3)

If
f_{(X,Y)}(x_{0},y)
is a (mixture) of Gaussian distributions (with respect to
y
), then
f_{(X,Y)}(x_{0},y)
is distinguishable with
\beta=2
on its mode set.

We further introduce the following crucial assumptions of the kernel functions used in our mode estimator.

Assumption 4.4
.

We assume the kernel
K
in (
4.4
) satisfies the following conditions.

(K1)

K
is a bounded, non-negative and symmetric density with compact support
[-1,1]
.Further,
K
is strictly decreasing on
[0,1]
and, for any
\epsilon_{n}\searrow 0
, there exists a constant
\beta_{1}>0
such that
K(1-\epsilon_{n})\geq\epsilon_{n}^{\beta_{1}}
. We also assume
K^{\prime\prime}
is bounded.

(K2)

We assume
K
is twice differentiable with first and second order derivatives as
K^{\prime}
and
K^{\prime\prime}
, respectively.

(K3)

If
K^{\prime}
is not a negative constant on the interval
[0,1]
, we assume the
|K^{\prime}|
is non-decreasing on
[0,1]
and
|K^{\prime}(\epsilon_{n})|=O(\epsilon_{n}^{\beta_{2}})
, for some
\beta_{2}>0
.

Many widely chosen kernels such as the triangle, Epanechnikov-, Silverman- and truncated Gaussian kernels satisfy this assumption. Moreover, all kernels of the form
(1-|u|^{a})^{b}1[|u|\leq 1]
,
a,b\geq 1
, satisfy Assumption
4.4
. Our first result indicates that, together with condition (C1) in Assumption
4.2
, Assumption
4.4
implies that the
K_{x_{0},h}(X_{i})K_{y,h}(Y_{i})
satisfies condition (
3.6
), which is crucial for the application of Theorem
3.6
.

Proposition 4.5
.

Suppose that
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=((X_{i},Y_{i}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are identically distributed random variables taking value in
\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
with joint density
f_{(X,Y)}(x,y)
. If
h=O(n^{-\eta})
for some
\eta>0
and Assumption
4.4
and condition (C1) of Assumption
4.2
hold, the product kernel
K_{x,h}(X_{1})K_{y,h}(Y_{1})
satisfies Assumption
3.5
, for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{Y}
.

Finally, we also need to specify Assumption
3.4
for the application of Theorem
3.6
. For this purpose, we introduce the following mild assumption.

Assumption 4.6
.

The kernel
K_{x_{0},h}K_{y,h}
defined by the product of the kernels in (
4.4
) satisfies

|K_{x_{0},h}K_{y,h}|_{SN}\leq C_{D}n^{B}
,
where the constants
C_{D}
and
B
do not depend on
n
.

As pointed out in
Hang et al. 2018
, Assumption
4.6
holds for
\mathcal{C}
-mixing (
\mathcal{C}_{\infty,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing) processes. For example, when
|\cdot|_{SN}
is chosen as Lipschitz norm, we can show the growth condition of the Lipschitz norm of the product of kernels is of order
O(\frac{1}{h})
. Therefore, Assumptions
4.4
,
4.6
and condition (C1) of Assumption
4.2
ensure that Theorem
3.6
can be applied to investigate the theoretical guarantee of the local conditional mode set estimator.
The following theorem is the main result of this section and gives the convergence rate of the mode set estimator (
4.6
) with respect to the Hausdorff loss.

Theorem 4.7
.

Suppose
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is a geometrically
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing process of order
(\nu,b,1)
with identically
1
-dimensional marginal distributions
taking values
in set
\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{D_{X}}\times\mathbb{R}^{D_{Y}}
. Let
D=D_{X}+D_{Y}
and

h=(\frac{\log n}{n})^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+D}}
. Then, under Assumptions
4.2
,
4.4
and
4.6
, the Hausdorff loss of the mode estimator (
4.6
) satisfies

\displaystyle\triangle_{n}(x_{0})=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big(\frac{\log n}{n}\Big)^{\frac{\alpha}{\beta(2\alpha+D)}}\Big).

(4.9)

Theorem
4.7
gives a precise statement how the constant
\beta

affects the convergence rate of the Hausdorff loss
\triangle_{n}(x_{0})
.
As we pointed out in Remark

,
\beta
is related the local smoothness of the conditional density at its local mode. A smaller value of

indicates more roughness and leads to a faster rate in (

). Thus, unlike to the estimator of the conditional density at the mode, where more smoothness improves the rate of convergence, the local mode set estimator performs better for smaller values of

, the convergence rate of

is faster than the optimal convergence rates of the density estimator for

. Moreover, in the case

, the statistic (

) provides a consistent estimator of the local conditional mode set, while the method of

is not applicable.

More specifically, these authors proposed an alternative estimate of the local conditional modes set in the case of iid data. Under the assumption of a four times continuously differentiable density
f_{X,Y)}
and the condition

|\frac{\partial f_{(X,Y)}(x_{0},y^{*})}{\partial y^{2}}|>0

they proved the rate

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}\Big(h^{2}+\sqrt{1\over nh^{D+2}}\Big)=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big({1\over n}\Big)^{2/(7+D_{X})}\Big)

(4.10)

where the last equality holds for the bandwidth

. The assumptions in
Chen et al. 2016
correspond to
\alpha=4

and

in condition (C1) and (C2), and, by Theorem

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}}\Big(\Big(\frac{\log n}{n}\Big)^{{2}/{(9+D_{X})}}\Big)

for the estimator proposed in this paper,
which is (slightly) worse than the rate (
4.10
) for the estimator of
Chen et al. 2016
.
In this sense, Theorem
4.7

provides statistical guarantees
for an alternative estimator of the set of local conditional modes from dependent data. In some cases this estimate
also improves the current state of the art
(
Chen et al. 2016
)
for iid data.

5
Conclusions and Discussion

In this paper, we derived sharp exponential inequalities for an empirical-mean-type statistics of the form
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(Z_{i})
,
where
(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
taking values in a measurable space
\mathbf{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}

with a very general dependence structure. We introduced the
concept of
\mathcal{C}_{p,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing
(1\leq p\leq\infty
)
as a general dependence condition that includes most of the currently considered non-strong-mixing processes and dynamical systems as special cases. While a lower value for
p
implies greater generality (which means a weaker dependence condition), the most restrictive condition is obtained for the choice
p=\infty
, which yields
\mathcal{C}_{\infty,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing. This dependence concept coincides with
\mathcal{C}
-mixing which has been discussed before in the literature.

The exponential inequality for
p=\infty
refines previous pioneering results in
Hang and Steinwart 2017
by providing a sharper estimate under more general conditions. For the case
p=1

we provide a sharp exponential inequality
under a very mild assumption on the characteristic function of
h(Z_{i})
for all geometrically
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing processes without requiring specific assumptions on the set
\mathbf{Z}
.
To the best of our knowledge, this result is the first in this direction.
As an illustration we use this inequality to investigate the convergence rate of a local conditional mode set estimator with respect to Hausdorff loss when the data is coming from a geometrically
\mathcal{C}_{1,\mathcal{F}}
-mixing process and the marginal distribution does not necessarily have a differentiable density.
