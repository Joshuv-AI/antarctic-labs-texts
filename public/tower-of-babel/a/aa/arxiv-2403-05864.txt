Abstract
. In the evolving landscape of human-centric systems, personalized privacy solutions are becoming increasingly crucial due to the dynamic nature of human interactions. Traditional static privacy models often fail to meet the diverse and changing privacy needs of users. This paper introduces PEaRL, a system designed to enhance privacy preservation by tailoring its approach to individual behavioral patterns and preferences. While incorporating reinforcement learning (RL) for its adaptability, PEaRL primarily focuses on employing an early-exit strategy that dynamically balances privacy protection and system utility. This approach addresses the challenges posed by the variability and evolution of human behavior, which static privacy models struggle to handle effectively. We evaluate PEaRL in two distinct contexts: Smart Home environments and Virtual Reality (VR) Smart Classrooms. The empirical results demonstrate PEaRL’s capability to provide a personalized tradeoff between user privacy and application utility, adapting effectively to individual user preferences. On average, across both systems, PEaRL enhances privacy protection by 31%, with a corresponding utility reduction of 24%.



1.
Introduction

The rapid advancement of sensor networks and mobile computing has significantly enhanced our ability to observe and analyze human behavior and their interactions with the environment. This progress has facilitated the development of human-aware Internet of Things (IoT) applications
(
Elmalaki et al. 2018a
)
, where the synergy between human behavior and technology transforms IoT design. As a result, human-centric design for IoT systems have emerged
(
Cambeiro 2019
)
, offering a novel paradigm by continuously sensing and adapting to both environmental and human inputs. These systems promise highly personalized user experiences, revolutionizing the landscape of IoT applications.
However, the intimate integration of human behavior with IoT systems introduces significant privacy challenges. The diversity in human behavior patterns, routines, and preferences, which greatly influence the data generated, can lead to varied privacy vulnerabilities. Specifically, this behavioral diversity results in distinct privacy implications, as the behaviors of certain individuals may inadvertently leak sensitive information more than others
(
Taherisadr et al. 2023b
;
Caine 2009
)
. Consequently, designing AI algorithms for human-centric IoT systems requires recognizing that privacy considerations are dynamic and can evolve with shifts in human behavior. Understanding these nuances and tailoring privacy mitigation algorithms accordingly is essential for the responsible deployment of human-centric IoT technologies, acknowledging that privacy is not a one-size-fits-all concept
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Xiao et al. 2023
)
.
To address these challenges, this paper focuses on mitigating privacy leaks in machine learning-based human-centric IoT systems. We particularly examine the use of privacy-aware Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL). Our focus on RL is driven by its ability to adapt to human intentions and responses
(
Sadigh et al. 2017
;
Hadfield-Menell et al. 2016
)
and its ability to accommodate variations in human behavior in both the inter- and intra-human variability
(
Elmalaki et al. 2018b
;
Ahadi-Sarkani and Elmalaki 2021
)
.

While DRL’s ability to personalize IoT systems for individual experiences is well-documented, it raises privacy concerns due to its potential to access private human states
(
Omonkhoa 2021
)
. This issue is particularly pertinent in domains such as healthcare, finance, and autonomous vehicles, where the continuous use of sensitive user data poses significant privacy concerns
(
Hlávka 2020
)
. Therefore, balancing the need for personalized human-centric IoT experiences with protecting individual privacy is a critical challenge.
Previous research has explored privacy vulnerabilities in RL systems, often focusing on the privacy-utility tradeoff
(
Taherisadr et al. 2023b
;
Wang and Hegde 2019
)
. However, the unique context of human-centric IoT systems presents distinct challenges. Recognizing the inherent variability among human subjects, we propose PEaRL, an adaptive personalized DRL approach that leverages an early-exit strategy. This approach aims to tailor the privacy-utility tradeoff to individual variability in human-centric IoT contexts.

Background and Related Work

Privacy in machine learning

Privacy concerns have persisted for many years
(
Petrescu and Krishen 2018
)
, prompting extensive research to address privacy breaches and their mitigation through various strategies. Hong et al.
(
Hong et al. 2022
)
discussed the imperative of balancing privacy and model performance through differentially private learning. Their work introduces dynamic policies, including adjustments to model parameters. These dynamic techniques enhance the performance of the models within privacy constraints.
Game-theoretical methods create an objective function that maximizes utility while minimizing privacy vulnerabilities
(
Jin et al. 2017
)
. Additionally, data encryption has been proposed as a defense against side-channel attacks on communication links between edge and cloud services
(
Mishra et al. 2022
)
. Zhang et al.
(
Zhang et al. 2022a
)
tackle training data privacy in machine learning, shifting focus to attribute privacy and concealing sensitive dataset properties during analysis. They propose definitions and efficient mechanisms for safeguarding specific dataset properties and distribution parameters, laying the foundation for addressing attribute privacy in preserving global properties during analysis. Patel et al.
(
Patel et al. 2022
)
address information leakage in model explanations for algorithmic transparency. They introduce differentially private mechanisms, balancing transparency and data privacy in machine learning.

Privacy-preserving RL

Several facets of privacy-preserving RL challenges have been explored and addressed, including online learning with bandit feedback
(
Malekzadeh et al. 2020
)
, linear contextual bandits
(
Garcelon et al. 2022
)
, and deep reinforcement learning (DRL)
(
Pan et al. 2019
)
. In the tabular setting, Garcelon et al.
(
Garcelon et al. 2021
)
introduced an algorithm that ensures both regret minimization and privacy preservation. In continuous state scenarios, Wang et al.
(
Wang and Hegde 2019
)
devised a Q-learning variant capable of discovering a policy that adheres to differential privacy constraints while optimizing the reward function. Erdemir et al.
(
Erdemir et al. 2020
)
studied the tradeoff between privacy and utility (PUT) in this context, noting that existing approaches often overlook temporal correlations in time-series data. They proposed sharing a distorted version of the user data sequence to mitigate privacy risks. Their approach quantifies privacy risk through mutual information assessment and employs asynchronous actor-critic deep RL to minimize history-dependent mutual information.

In digital forensics, deep learning has significantly enhanced image forgery detection capabilities, yet it remains susceptible to adversarial attacks. Xianboet al.
(
Mo et al. 2023
)
introduce a black-box anti-forensics framework employing RL, which simulates online forensic service dynamics and optimizes attack strategies using a novel reward function. This approach not only challenges existing image forgery detectors but does so with minimal perceptible distortions, showcasing its efficacy and practicality in anti-forensic applications.
(
Maiti et al. 2023
)
introduces a novel algorithm that uses power and load flow measurements to identify vulnerable operating intervals in smart grids. Using a Monte Carlo simulation and deep RL, the algorithm generates undetectable attack vectors that compromise grid stability efficiently. This approach improves security analysis and aids in optimizing grid design and operations. adaPARL
(
Taherisadr et al. 2023b
)
examined the privacy leaks inherent in sequential decision-making setups for human-centric IoT, where interactions with humans introduce variability in behavior and preferences over time. It introduces an adaptive privacy-aware algorithm for such systems, which adjusts to varying human behaviors. adaPARL is designed based on Q-learning and is typically used for discrete state and action spaces.
The drawback of limited state space encourages exploring more advanced RLs.

Early-Exit in human-centric IoT

The concept of early-exit (EE) was initially proposed by Teerapittayanon et al.
(
Teerapittayanon et al. 2016
)
to eliminate the necessity of reducing the size of deep neural network (DNN) models. This is achieved by introducing early-exits within the DNN architecture, which enables the termination of execution as soon as a desired confidence level is reached for a given input sample. In practical terms, specific samples in test datasets and real-world scenarios can be readily processed by a DNN model. In contrast, others might pose challenges, depending on the specific machine learning models employed. Consequently, EE optimizes the inference process for straightforward samples by reducing the number of transformation layers involved, thus reducing overall inference time and computational costs
(
Matsubara et al. 2022
)
. Some work in the literature examined the effects of EE mechanism in IoT and federated learning scenarios, aiming to enhance the robustness of training and inference against privacy attacks, such as Membership Inference Attacks (MIAs)
(
Wu et al. 2023
)
. EE-equipped models in dynamic neural networks adjust their computation path for each sample based on its complexity. Through experiments, they determined that models lacking early-exits are more susceptible to MIAs. The findings highlight the potential of dynamic architectures in enhancing privacy, underscoring the need for further exploration and design in this model category
(
Wu et al. 2023
)
.
Zheng et al. in
(
Li et al. 2022
)
present the privacy analysis of multi-exit networks, focusing on membership leakages and their susceptibility. It demonstrates that the configuration of exits influences vulnerability and introduces a novel hybrid attack that exploits these vulnerabilities, presenting significant privacy risks. A new defense mechanism, TimeGuard, is proposed, effectively mitigating these threats and enhancing privacy protection in multi-exit networks.

While EE strategies have been extensively explored in the context of DNN, their application in RL settings offers a novel perspective on improving the efficiency of learning algorithms. The concept of terminating an episode prematurely, based on intermediate observations or partial trajectories, holds promise in accelerating convergence and optimizing resource utilization. RAPID-RL
(
Kosta et al. 2022
)
introduces a novel approach to enhance deep RL. RAPID-RL framework enhances a standard Deep Q-Network (DQN) with multiple exit branches, enabling the rapid inference of most input states and leading to quicker processing and reduced compute operations. Additionally, they present a sequential training method for these branches, ensuring optimal performance for each while circumventing performance interdependence and creating a unified joint DQN adhering to power constraints. This paper exploits EE for DQN and explores its efficiency as a privacy mitigation technique in human-centric IoT systems with human variability.

3.
Threat model in human-centric IoT Systems

The rapid advancements in the computational power and memory capacity of edge devices have paved the way for executing resource-intensive tasks directly at the edge. This capability allows for sophisticated data processing and the implementation of reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms at the edge, which are essential for real-time decision-making in pervasive environments. However, synchronizing multiple applications often requires centralized decision-making, typically facilitated by a cloud-based server. For instance, the NEST program in Southern California, initiated by the Department of Energy, exemplifies this by sharing HVAC set points with a cloud server to optimize energy consumption
(
Media 2024
)
. Programs like Southern California Edison’s (SCE) Rush Hour Rewards
(
Edison 2024
)
currently operate under similar conditions, where the HVAC setpoint is being set by the cloud server for each registered house in the program.

Our threat model is defined within the context of a HITL IoT system, where edge devices collect sensor data and human inputs. This data is analyzed by a local deep reinforcement learning (DRL) agent, which proposes actions based on the analysis. Rather than executing these actions directly, the edge device forwards them to the cloud. The cloud is tasked with enforcing constraints and synchronizing applications, ultimately determining the final control signals that are sent back to the edge for execution. This edge-cloud architecture is prevalent in many pervasive applications
(
Bovornkeeratiroj et al. 2020
;
Papst et al. 2022
;
Stirapongsasuti et al. 2019
)
.

Our threat model assumes an
“honest-but-curious”
cloud provider as shown in
Figure 1
. While the cloud adheres to the system’s protocols, it may attempt to infer sensitive information from the shared action data. With domain knowledge of the application and access to the time series of recommended actions, the cloud could potentially train machine learning models to exploit this data.

Our primary objective is to limit the cloud-based server’s ability to infer private states, even when it has unlimited computational resources and comprehensive application domain knowledge.
One potential approach to enhancing privacy is homomorphic encryption, which allows data processing in its encrypted form
(
Kim et al. 2023
;
Akhavan Mahdavi et al. 2023
)
. However, this method introduces significant latency and has intrinsic limitations, potentially affecting the system’s responsiveness and efficiency. The complexity of homomorphic encryption restricts the types of analyses that can be performed efficiently on encrypted data, which may impede the effectiveness of cloud-based processing and become an important reason why homomorphic encryption is difficult to put into practice
(
Zhang et al. 2022b
)
. Given the variability in human behavior and the need for precise and adaptive control in pervasive applications, it is crucial to maintain a model capable of operating accurately and locally. Additionally, the computational overhead and latency associated with homomorphic encryption may not be feasible for applications requiring real-time processing and decision-making. Therefore, exploring alternative privacy-preserving strategies is essential to ensure the system’s efficacy without compromising user privacy.

4.
Privacy Leak Metric

We draw from information theory to use mutual information (MI) as a metric for quantifying the correlation between two random variables. In our context, MI measures how effectively an
honest-but-curious
cloud can infer a system state from observed actions
(
Ito et al. 2022
)
. MI establishes a theoretical upper bound on the inferential capability of any learning algorithm. Specifically, lower MI between state and action implies less accurate inference. In the extreme case, an MI of zero indicates that no algorithm can deduce the state from observed actions. Thus, we consider the MI measure
\mathcal{I}_{\pi}(a_{t};s_{t})
for a given policy
\pi(s,a)
as a constraint on the ability to extract information about
s_{t}
by observing
a_{t}
. The information available to the cloud is, therefore, bounded by
\mathcal{I}_{\pi}(a_{t};s_{t})
.

While MI is an application-agnostic approach and provides a quantifiable measure of information leakage
(
Ito et al. 2022
)
, it is inherently an average measure and may not fully capture the complexity of privacy decisions in specific contexts. For instance, MI can be sensitive to noise in the data; if the time series data is noisy, MI might not accurately reflect the true correlation between the series. In our setup, even if sensor data is noisy at the edge, the edge’s local DQN model can generalize inference effectively, mitigating some noise effects. Another concern is that MI may be unsuitable for high-dimensional data, suffering from the “curse of dimensionality,” which makes accurate estimation challenging without large datasets. However, since we focus on human-centric IoT applications with a finite and limited number of states and actions, estimating MI in this context is tractable.

5.
PEaRL algorithm

PEaRL effectively tackles the challenge of balancing the privacy-utility tradeoff in human-centric systems by employing Deep Q-Network (DQN) models. This approach involves training the DQN to determine the optimal network layer for an exit, guided by the dual objectives of maximizing utility and ensuring privacy. By leveraging the variability in human behavior, PEaRL offers a personalized and adaptive mechanism for privacy-aware decision-making during inference. The PEaRL framework is illustrated in
Figure 2
.

5.1.
Training DQN for EE with privacy and utility budgets

PEaRL’s training is structured into two main phases. PHASE 1 aims to develop a foundational DQN with integrated early-exit (EE) branches. The process begins with initializing a single-layer Q-network. Sequential training is then employed, gradually expanding the network by introducing and training new layers with associated EE branches one at a time, while keeping previously trained parameters fixed
(
Kosta et al. 2022
)
. This method ensures that adding new layers does not affect the performance of existing ones, as illustrated in
Figure 3
.

PHASE 2 focuses on optimizing the DQN to balance privacy and utility, adapting to dynamic environments and human behavior. During this phase, confidence labels are assigned to each EE branch based on predefined utility and privacy budgets. Each label can be either “1” or “0,” indicating whether a particular EE satisfies a specific utility or privacy budget (given a label “1”). These labels will determine which EE branches are available for exit.

For utility confidence label (
UCL
) training, a utility replay buffer is created during model interaction with the environment containing the state, action, and utility labels for
B
branches
(s_{t},a_{t},UCL_{1},UCL_{2},...,UCL_{B})
. The utility labels are assigned based on the maximum Q-values in each branch
Q_{i_{max}}(s,a)
relative to the utility budget
u\in(0,1]
and the maximum
Q_{max}
across all of the
B

Q_{max}(s,a)=\max{Q_{i_{max}}(s,a)}\forall i\in B
). The formula used is:

(1)

UCL_{i}(s,a)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }Q_{i_{max}}(s,a)>u\cdot Q_{max}(s,a)\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The intuition behind this approach is that the maximum MI represents the highest level of information leakage observed during the initial training phase when the system is operating. By setting a threshold as a fraction of this maximum, PEaRL can monitor for deviations that indicate reduced information leakage from the worst-case scenario.
Branch selection is then made based on the specific privacy-utility tradeoff determined by

Regarding implementation, utility and privacy paths are augmented to each EE branch, with paths that output binary labels using a binary cross-entropy loss. Separate utility and privacy replay buffers are maintained to capture state, action, and label information. The action selected
a_{t}

5.2.
PEaRL Inference

Upon completing the training, the enhanced DQN, now equipped with confidence paths, is deployed to steer the model’s decision-making process. The algorithm leverages these confidence branches at each decision point to make well-informed choices about the optimal exit. The selection of the early exit (EE) is carefully aligned with the predefined utility (
u
) and privacy (
p
) budgets, as detailed in
Algorithm 2

5.3.
Incooperating Human Variability

As mentioned earlier, human behavior is not fixed, and hence, the labels used (
PCL
and
UCL
) to train the confidence path can change over time. After the trained DQN is deployed, PEaRL accumulates a repository of interactions with the environment, which contains selected actions, state observations, and corresponding rewards associated with the chosen exit branch. This repository is used to monitor variations in MI. If these variations drop below a predetermined threshold, indicating a change in human behavior, PEaRL initiates a retraining phase. The DQN is retrained to adapt to the evolving environment, accommodating variations in user behavior or environmental conditions. The threshold is set as a percentage of the maximum MI (
\mathcal{I}_{max}
) at the point the agent converges to a specific action that maximizes the reward during PHASE 1 of training:
\mathcal{I}_{\text{threshold}}=v\cdot I_{max}
, where
v\in(0,1]
. This continuous training through updating the DQN whenever human behavior or the environment changes is described in

Application 1: Thermal House Model

Recent advancements in smart heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) systems, which integrate human input, aim to improve user satisfaction
(
Jung and Jazizadeh 2017
;
Elmalaki 2021
)
. To achieve this, it is crucial to consider human conditions and preferences when setting the HVAC parameters. For example, body temperature decreases during sleep
(
Barrett et al. 1993
)
, increases during physical exercise
(
Lim et al. 2008
)
, and fluctuates in response to stress and anxiety
(

)

(

;

)
.
Although the primary aim of this section is to evaluate the trade-off between privacy and utility in our proposed PEaRL, we will first provide an overview of the IoT environment design and the Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agent used in this application.

6.1.
Human-Centric System Design

Our study simulates a thermodynamic model of residential houses, accounting for factors such as architectural design, window number, roof pitch angle, and insulation materials. The heating system uses a heater emitting air at
50^{\circ}C
, while cooling is achieved with a cooler emitting air at

. A thermostat maintains the indoor temperature, allowing a deviation of up to

We simulate the behavior of three distinct individuals in three homes by varying their RMV and metabolic rates. Our simulation includes six activity categories: sleeping, relaxing, watching TV, cooking, exercising, and a state called “not at home.” Each individual follows unique lifestyle patterns. Human
H_{1}
maintains a structured weekly routine with minimal randomness,
H_{3}
has an irregular lifestyle with frequent unexpected changes, and

6.2.
DRL Design

We have developed a Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) framework, detailed below, to tailor the HVAC setpoint based on human activity and desired thermal comfort levels.
State Space
\mathcal{S}
is defined as the set of all possible combinations of six distinct human activities and the indoor temperature of the house. Formally, this is expressed as
\mathcal{S}=\{(act,temp):act\in[1,6]\text{ and }temp\in[60,80]\}
, where
act
denotes the current human activity and
temp
represents the current indoor temperature, a continuous variable. Action Space
\mathcal{A}
consists of discrete temperature values within the
[60,80]

7.2.
RL Design

While VR technologies offer significant advantages in educational and workplace training settings, individual responses to VR environments can vary widely. Some users may experience symptoms such as vertigo and cybersickness, which can adversely affect the learning experience. To address these challenges, we define the human state using three key features: alertness level (AL), fatigue level (FL), and vertigo level (VL).

To simplify our state space
\mathcal{S}
, we categorize these features into binary classes. Alertness level (AL) is classified as either “Alert (1)” or “Not Alert (0),” fatigue level (FL) as “Fatigue (1)” or “Vigor (0),” and vertigo level (VL) as “Not Vertigo (1)” or “Vertigo (0).” The human state is thus represented as a tuple:
S=\{(AL,FL,VL):AL\in\{0,1\},FL\in\{0,1\},VL\in\{0,1\}\}
.
Table 2
provides an overview of this state space.

Each feature’s classification is determined by specific thresholds (
\delta_{AL}
,
\delta_{FL}
,
\delta_{VL}
).A feature is classified as “1” if its measured value exceeds the threshold; otherwise, it is classified as “0.” The optimal human state for effective learning is
s_{8}
, characterized by alertness and absence of cybersickness (neither fatigue nor vertigo). Conversely, the least favorable state is
s_{1}
, where the individual is not alert and experiences cybersickness (both fatigue and vertigo). It is important to note that individuals can transition between these states, and the threshold values can be adjusted to fit specific applications and participants
(
Taherisadr et al. 2023a
)
. The procedure for determining the human state

and computing the thresholds is based on the study by Taherisadr et al.
(
Taherisadr et al. 2023a
)

Our VR application’s action space

includes the following actions: (1) providing a break for the user, (2) enabling VR mode by transitioning from 2D to 3D, (3) disabling VR mode by reverting from 3D to 2D, (4) altering the content of the presentation, and (5) maintaining the current state in the learning environment (no change). Notably, activating VR mode can enhance cognitive engagement and improve learning performance. However, due to the risk of cybersickness, the reinforcement learning agent may need to switch back to the standard 2D mode to mitigate symptoms. Additionally, breaks may be necessary to address drowsiness, cybersickness symptoms, or cognitive overload. Therefore,

is a finite set of five discrete actions. The performance of individuals in a quiz determines the reward value

8.
Discussion and Limitations

Privacy leak quantification:
While mutual information (MI) provides a quantifiable, application-agnostic measure of information leakage
(
Ito et al. 2022
)
, its practical application often involves finite datasets. Although MI can theoretically converge to the true value as the number of samples increases, real-world limitations such as computational constraints and data quality can impact the accuracy of the estimate.

While MI is a valuable tool, it may not fully capture the nuances of privacy leaks in all contexts. Other application-agnostic approaches, like differential privacy, k-anonymity, and l-diversity, can also be employed. However, these methods may not always be sufficient for complex privacy scenarios.

Recognizing these limitations, we acknowledge that relying solely on application-agnostic metrics might not provide a complete picture, especially when privacy leaks are highly context-dependent.

Perception of privacy:
Recognizing the complexity of privacy decisions, we acknowledge that our approach, primarily focusing on a specific threat model and privacy leaks while using privacy-utility tradeoff to measure the efficacy of our proposed algorithm PEaRL may not fully capture the broader socio-technical dimensions of privacy. Numerous studies highlighted the multifaceted nature of privacy, which involves cultural, contextual, and behavioral considerations.
In response to this valid critique, as part of our future work, we plan to explore how different definitions of privacy can influence the development and assessment of measures and how these measures intersect with user expectations and societal norms. Such an approach will enrich our analysis, especially as we focus on human-centric IoT systems, and contribute to a more holistic understanding of privacy in technological interventions
(
Ali and Bénabou 2020
)
.

9.
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced PEaRL, which combines deep reinforcement learning (DRL) with an early exit (EE) strategy to provide an adaptive and personalized experience. We exploit the notion of EE to provide personalized privacy. In particular, EE helps address privacy concerns by allowing the system to use different exits when potential risks are detected. This approach is particularly useful in scenarios where user behavior varies, accommodating the individualized nature of privacy requirements. By incorporating this early-exit strategy into the DRL framework, PEaRL aims to manage the privacy-utility tradeoff, considering the specific needs and variations in human behavior within human-centric IoT environments. Across two HITL applications, PEaRL was able to reduce privacy leaks by an average of 31% while maintaining acceptable utility.

Appendix A
Deep Q-Network (
DQN
) for Human-Centric applications

Deep Q-Network (DQN) is a powerful reinforcement learning algorithm that combines deep learning with Q-learning. It learns to make optimal decisions in an environment by approximating the Q-value function,
Q(s,a)
, which estimates the expected future reward for taking action
a
in state
s
. DQN uses a neural network to represent the Q-value function, enabling it to handle complex, high-dimensional state spaces. DQN training involves an iterative process. The agent interacts with the environment, taking actions
a_{t}
and observing the resulting rewards
r_{t}
and new states
s_{t+1}
. These experiences
(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t},s_{t+1})
are stored in a replay buffer. A batch of experiences is sampled from the buffer and fed into the neural network to estimate the Q-values,
\hat{Q}(s_{t},a_{t})
. A separate target network is used to compute stable target Q-values,
Q_{target}(s_{t+1},a)
. The temporal difference (TD) error,
\delta=r_{t}+\gamma\max_{a}Q_{target}(s_{t+1},a)-\hat{Q}(s_{t},a_{t})
, is used to calculate the loss function. This loss is backpropagated to update the neural network’s weights. As the agent continues to learn, its policy improves, leading to more optimal decision-making.

DQN
can model the dynamic interplay between environments and human interaction. Through interaction with the human-centric environment, the
DQN
agent continually updates these Q-value estimates, guided by the reward signals originating from humans. These rewards, whether stemming from explicit annotations or implicit cues, enable the agent to learn between favorable and undesirable actions, ultimately reinforcing the learning of optimal policy
\pi(s,a)
that shape the human-centric adaptation
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
.

Utilizing the clustering on the actions shared with the cloud engine from the branch that maximizes utility (branch 6 for human H1), an honest-but-curious cloud can plot the Within-Cluster Sum of Squares (WCSS) against the number of clusters, as depicted in
Figure 16
. The elbow method is a technique used to determine the optimal number of clusters. By analyzing the WCSS plot, we can identify the “elbow point,” where adding more clusters provides diminishing returns regarding reduced WCSS. In this case, the optimal number of clusters appears to be six for
H_{1}
, as shown
Figure 16
.
