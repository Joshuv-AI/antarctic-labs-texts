Extending Discrete Geometric Singular Perturbation Theory to Non-Hyperbolic Points
S. Jelbart & C. Kuehn

Abstract
We extend the recently developed discrete geometric singular perturbation theory to the non-normally hyperbolic regime. Our primary tool is the Takens embedding theorem , which provides a means of approximating the dynamics of particular maps with the time-1 map of a formal vector field. First, we show that the so-called reduced map , which governs the slow dynamics near slow manifolds in the normally hyperbolic regime, can be locally approximated by the time-1 map of the reduced vector field which appears in continuous-time geometric singular perturbation theory. In the non-normally hyperbolic regime, we show that the dynamics of fast-slow maps with a unipotent linear part can be locally approximated by the time-1 map induced by a fast-slow vector field in the same dimension, which has a nilpotent singularity of the corresponding type. The latter result is used to describe (i) the local dynamics of two-dimensional fast-slow maps with non-normally singularities of regular fold, transcritical and pitchfork type, and (ii) dynamics on a (potentially high - dimensional) local center manifold in n n -dimensional fast-slow maps with regular contact or fold submanifolds of the critical manifold. In general, our results show that the dynamics near a large and important class of singularities in fast-slow maps can be described via the use of formal embedding theorems which allow for their approximation by the time-1 map of a fast-slow vector field featuring a loss of normal hyperbolicity.



Keywords:
geometric singular perturbation theory, discrete dynamical systems, difference equations, singularly perturbed maps, Takens embedding theorem

Introduction

Geometric singular perturbation theory (GSPT)

[
15
,
27
,
32
,
49

25

DGSPT applies to the study of discrete dynamical systems induced by iteration of fast-slow maps or difference equations in the general form

z\mapsto H(z,\varepsilon)=z+h(z)+\varepsilon G(z,\varepsilon),

(1)

where

(k<n)
-dimensional

A recent formulation of GSPT for fast-slow ODE systems in this class can be found in

\begin{split}x&\mapsto x+f(x,y,\varepsilon),\\

The singular theory developed in (I) applies to the identification and analysis of the

Although we shall also derive new results in the normally hyperbolic regime, the primary aim of this work is to make steps towards the extension of DGSPT into the non-normally hyperbolic regime, which is not considered in
[
25

(i)

Fold/contact-type singularities with a single non-trivial multiplier of

(ii)

Due to the coarseness of this ‘classification’, (i) also includes common singularity types such as transcritical and pitchfork singularities. The same is true of (ii)-(iii). For analytical purposes, many authors choose to group singularities in (ii)-(iii) together – they are sometimes referred to collectively as ‘oscillatory singularities’ – because a number of important analytical methods and approaches apply equally to the study of dynamics near both singularity types. They also exhibit dynamical similarities. Numerous authors have shown that many singularities in (ii)-(iii) are associated with

In order to understand the dynamics near singularities in (i) in a more general setting, we adopt an alternative approach which relies on the

We prove and apply a number of formal embedding theorems of this kind in both the normally hyperbolic and non-normally hyperbolic regimes.

The most important result we obtain in the normally hyperbolic regime pertains to the reduced map, which approximates the slow dynamics near normally hyperbolic submanifolds of
S
to leading order in
\varepsilon

The most important result we obtain in the non-normally hyperbolic regime pertains to the dynamics near singularities in a large and important subclass of singularities of type (i), known as
unipotent singularities

\textrm{D}H(z,0)

We then combine these results with (already known) results on the ODE counterparts to these problems, which can be found in
[

Finally, we show that the dynamics near regular contact type singularities

1

In contrast to the original (higher dimensional) map, the linearised problem within the center manifold is unipotent, allowing for the application of the embedding-based methods and results that we derived for unipotent singularities. This allows us to show that the dynamics on the center manifold can be approximated by the time-1 map of a
(k+1)

[

DGSPT and formal embeddings for the slow dynamics

We consider
\varepsilon

DGSPT applies to the class of maps (

Assumption 1

The map (

The

Remark 2.1

The particular form of the factorisation (

Remark 2.2

Consider the map (

DGSPT in the normally hyperbolic regime

2.1.1
The layer map

The limiting problem associated to the fast dynamics is obtained by taking
\varepsilon\to 0
in (
5
), leading to the so-called
layer map

z\mapsto\bar{z}=z+N(z)f(z).

(8)

The critical manifold
S
, recall (
6
), is contained in the fixed point set of (
8
). Since
S
is
(k<n)
-dimensional, the linearisation along
S
, i.e.

\textrm{D}(z+N(z)f(z))=\mathbb{I}_{n}+N(z)\textrm{D}f(z),\qquad z\in S,

(9)

has at least
k
multipliers which are identically equal to
1
. These are referred to as the
trivial multipliers
. We denote the remaining
n-k>0

non-trivial multipliers
by
\mu_{j}(z)
,
j=1,\ldots,n-k
. Linear stability along
S
is characterised and classified in terms of the distribution of the non-trivial multipliers
\mu_{j}(z)
in the complex plane.

Definition 2.3
.

We say that
the map (
8
)
is normally hyperbolic
at a point
z\in S
if there are no non-trivial multipliers on the unit circle, i.e. if

|\mu_{j}(z)|\neq 1,\qquad\forall j=1,\ldots,n-k.

The map is normally hyperbolic along a
subset or submanifold
S_{\textup{nh}}\subseteq S
if it
is normally hyperbolic
at each point in
S_{\textup{nh}}
.

For calculations it is worthy to note that the
n-k
non-trivial multipliers can be identified with the multipliers of the
(n-k)\times(n-k)
square matrix
\mathbb{I}_{n-k}+\textrm{D}f(z)N(z)
, by
[
25
, Prop. 2.10]
. Thus, the problem of checking for normal hyperbolicity, or equivalently, identifying a loss of normal hyperbolicity, reduces to the question of whether or not the spectrum of
\mathbb{I}_{n-k}+\textrm{D}f(z)N(z)
contains points on the unit circle.

The local dynamics of the map (
5
) is also
affected
by
superstability
, which is related to non-invertibility of the map (
5
), and occurs near points on
S

where the linearisation has
at least one non-trivial multiplier equal to zero.

Proposition 2.4
.

Consider a point
z\in S
. The map (
5
) is a diffeomorphism on a neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}
about
z
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for all
\varepsilon\in[0,\varepsilon_{0})
if and only if
\varepsilon_{0}>0
is sufficiently small and

\mu_{j}(z)\neq 0,\qquad\forall j=1,\ldots,n-k.

(10)

Proof.

Let
z\in S
. By the inverse function theorem, the extended map

F(z,\varepsilon):=\begin{pmatrix}z+N(z)f(z)+\varepsilon G(z,\varepsilon)\\
\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}

is a local diffeomorphism on a neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}\times[0,\varepsilon_{0})
about
(z,0)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
if and only if the determinant of the Jacobian

\textrm{D}F(z,0)=\mathbb{I}_{n+1}+\begin{pmatrix}N\textrm{D}f(z)&G(z,0)\\
\mathbb{O}_{1,n}&1\end{pmatrix}

is non-zero. Now let
\{\mu_{i}(z)\}_{i=1}^{n+1}
denote the multipliers of
\textrm{D}f(z,0)
, where the first
n-k
denote the non-trivial multipliers. The remaining multipliers are given by
\mu_{i}(z)=1
, where
i=n-k+1,\ldots,n+1
. Using this fact together with (

\det\textrm{D}f(z,0)=\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}\mu_{i}(z)=\prod_{i=1}^{n-k}\mu_{i}(z)\neq 0,

which implies the result.
∎

It is worthy to note that
the map can be normally hyperbolic
and

\mathcal{G}(z,\varepsilon)
, then
\mathcal{G}(z,\varepsilon)
generates the vector field
\Pi_{\mathcal{N}}^{S_{\textrm{nh}}}G(z,0)
on
\widetilde{S}
when
\varepsilon\to 0
in the sense that the derivative at
\varepsilon=0
is given by

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{\mathcal{G}(z,\varepsilon)-\mathcal{G}(z,0)}{\varepsilon}=\Pi^{S_{\textup{nh}}}_{\mathcal{N}}G(z,0),\qquad z\in\widetilde{S}.

(14)

Intuitively, we think of the dynamics induced by the slow map (
12
) as ‘converging’ to a flow as the distance between iterates, which is generically
O(\varepsilon)
, tends to zero.

Alternatively, we may define the
reduced map
as the leading order approximation for the slow dynamics, i.e. by truncating (
12
) at order
O(\varepsilon)
:

z\mapsto z+\varepsilon\Pi_{\mathcal{N}}^{S_{\textrm{nh}}}G(z,0),\qquad z\in\widetilde{S}.

(15)

Interestingly, the reduced map (
15
) coincides with the Euler discretization of the ODE problem

z^{\prime}=\frac{dz}{dt}=\varepsilon\Pi_{\mathcal{N}}^{S_{\textrm{nh}}}G(z,0),\qquad z\in\widetilde{S},

(16)

with step-size
h=\varepsilon
or, equivalently,

\dot{z}=\frac{dz}{d\tau}=\Pi_{\mathcal{N}}^{S_{\textrm{nh}}}G(z,0),\qquad z\in\widetilde{S}.

(17)

Because of
this close relationship between the slow map (
12
) and the reduced vector field
\Pi^{S_{\textup{nh}}}_{\mathcal{N}}G(z,0)
, a number of important dynamical questions about the reduced map (
15
) can be reformulated as questions about the reduced vector field (
17
). We illustrate the point with the following simple result.

Proposition 2.7
.

There exists an
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_{0})
, t
he reduced map (
15
) has a fixed point at
z=z_{0}
if and only if the reduced vector field (
17
) has an equilibrium at
z=z_{0}
. The multipliers of the linearised reduced map satisfy the hyperbolicity condition
||\mu_{i}|-1|>c>0
for an arbitrarily small but fixed constant
c
if and only if
z_{0}
is a hyperbolic equilibrium of the reduced vector field (
17
).

Proof.

Since

z=z_{0}\mapsto z_{0}+\varepsilon\Pi^{S_{\textrm{nh}}}_{\mathcal{N}}G(z_{0},0)=0\quad\iff\quad\Pi^{S_{\textrm{nh}}}_{\mathcal{N}}G(z_{0},0)=0,

the reduced map (
15
) has a fixed point at
z=z_{0}
if and only if the reduced vector field has an equilibrium at
z=z_{0}
. The multipliers of the reduced map at
z=z_{0}
are given by
\mu_{j}=1+\varepsilon\lambda_{j}
, where
j=1,\ldots,n
and
\lambda_{j}
are the eigenvalues of the matrix
\textrm{D}(\Pi_{\mathcal{N}}^{S_{\textrm{nh}}}G(z,0))(z_{0})
. The hyperbolicity statement follows from the fact that

|\mu_{i}|^{2}=1+2\varepsilon\textup{Re}(\lambda_{i})+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
∎

In the following section we shall derive an even stronger relationship between the map (
12
) – and therefore also the reduced map (
15
) – and the reduced vector field (
17
).

Remark 2.8
.

The maps (
12
) and (
15
) are diffeomorphisms even if the original map (
5
) is not invertible, i.e. (
12
) and (
15
) are also invertible at superstable points on
S
. This feature follows from the fact that (
5
)
|_{\widetilde{S}_{\varepsilon}}
is invertible due to Theorem
2.5
Assertion (iii). It can also be seen as a direct consequence of the inverse function theorem, since the Jacobian matrices associated with the restricted maps in (
12
) and (
15
) are
O(\varepsilon)
-close to
\mathbb{I}_{n}
. To illustrate the point, consider the simple example
(x,y)\mapsto(x^{2},y+\varepsilon)^{\textnormal{T}}
, which can be written in general form (
5
) with
N(x,y)=(1,0)^{\textnormal{T}}
,
f(x,y)=-x+x^{2}
and
G(x,y,\varepsilon)=(0,1)^{\textnormal{T}}
. The critical manifold
S
has a branch along
x=0
with a single non-trivial multiplier
\mu\equiv 0
. Thus
S
is superstable and the map is non-invertible along
x=0
. However, the corresponding reduced map is given by
(0,y)^{\textnormal{T}}=(0,y+\varepsilon)^{\textnormal{T}}
, which is clearly invertible.

Remark 2.9
.

The domain of definition of the reduced map (
15
) can be smoothly extended to the set
S_{\textup{nh}}\cup S_{\textup{ns}}
, where

S_{\textup{ns}}:=\left\{z\in S:\mu_{j}(z)\in S^{1}\setminus\{1\},\ \forall j=1,\ldots n-k\right\},

(18)

and
the subscript “ns” stands for “Neimark-Sacker”. Like
S_{\textup{nh}}
, the set
S_{\textup{nh}}\cup S_{\textup{ns}}
is generically a union of
k
-dimensional submanifolds of
S
satisfying
\overline{S_{\textup{nh}}\cup S_{\textup{ns}}}=S
. Such an extension is possible because the projection operator
\Pi_{\mathcal{N}}^{S}
remains well-defined at
points where the map is
non-normally hyperbolic with
\mu_{j}(z)\neq 1
. Note, however, that
\Pi_{\mathcal{N}}^{S}
is not well-defined on
S\setminus(S_{\textup{nh}}\cup S_{\textup{ns}})
, since the matrix
\textup{D}fN|_{S\setminus(S_{\textrm{nh}}\cup S_{\textup{ns}})}
is not invertible.

2.2
Formal embeddings in the normally hyperbolic regime

Our aim in this section is to derive formal embedding theorems which can be used to approximate the dynamics of the map (
5
) by the time-1 map induced by a flow in the same dimension. We state two different results. The first of these applies to the dynamics in a neighbourhood about in a given point
z\in S_{\textrm{nh}}
. The second can be used in order to relate the dynamics of the reduced map (
15
) to the dynamics of the (continuous-time) reduced vector field defined in (
17
).

We shall assume throughout this section that
0\in S_{\textrm{nh}}
(this can be achieved after a simple coordinate translation), and consider the expansion about
(z,\varepsilon)=(0,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
obtained from (
5
) after appending the trivial map
\varepsilon\to\varepsilon
. We shall also assume that (
5
) is
C^{r}
-smooth with
r\geq 2
. Similarly to the proof of Proposition
2.4
, we write

F(z,\varepsilon):=\begin{pmatrix}H(z,\varepsilon)\\
\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}=A\begin{pmatrix}z\\
\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}+F^{(2)}(z,\varepsilon)+\cdots+F^{(r)}(z,\varepsilon)+o(|(z,\varepsilon)|^{r}),

(19)

where each
F^{(j)}(z,\varepsilon)
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
j\in\{2,\ldots,r\}
, and

A:=\textrm{D}f(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{n}+N\textrm{D}f(0)&G(0,0)\\
\mathbb{O}_{1,n}&1\end{pmatrix}.

(20)

Since every linear transformation on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
has a Jordan-Chevalley decomposition, the matrix
A
can be written as

A=B\left(\mathbb{I}_{n+1}+M\right),

(21)

where
B
is semi-simple,
M
is nilpotent, and
BM=MB
. In particular, there exists a (generally complex) invertible
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrix
\mathcal{S}
such that

A=\mathcal{S}^{-1}\mathcal{D}\mathcal{S}^{-1}+\mathcal{S}^{-1}\left(J(A)-\mathcal{D}\right)\mathcal{S},

(22)

where
\mathcal{D}:=\textup{diag}(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n-k},1,\ldots,1)
is the matrix of (generally complex) multipliers of
A
, which has
k+1
trivial multipliers equal to
1
.
J(A)
denotes the Jordan decomposition of
A
, which is block diagonal with Jordan blocks associated to the non-trivial multipliers in the upper left part, and
\mathbb{I}_{k+1}
in the bottom right block. As usual, the matrix
\mathcal{S}
can be constructed in applications using the eigenvectors associated to
A
. Comparing (
21
) with (
22
), we have

B=\mathcal{S}^{-1}\mathcal{D}\mathcal{S},\qquad M=\mathcal{S}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{D}^{-1}J(A)-\mathbb{I}_{n+1}\right)\mathcal{S}.

(23)

The semi-simple matrix
B
will play an important role in the approximation of the local dynamics of the map (
5
) by a flow. In order to state this result precisely, however, we need two more important notions and an additional assumption.

Definition 2.10
.

Consider a
C^{r}
-smooth function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
with the following finite order Taylor expansion at
z=z_{0}
:

\phi(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{r}\phi^{(j)}(z-z_{0})+o(|z-z_{0}|^{r}),

where
\phi^{(0)}(z-z_{0})=\phi(z_{0})
and the coefficient functions
\phi^{(j)}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
are vectors of degree
j
homogeneous polynomials defined via Taylor expansion.
For each
l=1,\ldots,r
we define the
l
-jet of
\phi(z)
and the truncation operator
j^{l}
at
z=z_{0}
by

j^{l}\phi(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{l}\phi^{(j)}(z-z_{0}).

Definition 2.11
.

Consider a
C^{r}
-smooth map
\phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\phi(0)=0
, where
\Omega
is a neighbourhood of
0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The map
\phi(z)
is said to be in local Poincaré normal form up to order
l
if the corresponding
l
-jet
j^{l}\phi(z)
can be written as a linear combination of “resonant monomials” of the form

z_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{m_{n}}e_{j},

where
z=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
e_{j}
is a standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and the
m_{i}
are positive integers which satisfy
\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\geq 2
and the resonance condition

\lambda_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{n}^{m_{n}}=\lambda_{j}.

Finally, we assume the following local invertibility property.

Assumption 3
.

The origin
0\in S
is not superstable, i.e. the non-trivial multipliers
\mu_{j}(0)
satisfy the local invertibility condition

\mu_{j}(0)\neq 0,\qquad\forall j=1,\ldots,n-k.

If Assumption
3
is satisfied, then Proposition
2.4
ensures that (
5
) is a diffeomorphism on a suitable neighbourhood about
\mathcal{U}\ni 0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We now state and prove two formal embedding theorems, which can be used in order to approximate the dynamics of fast-slow maps close to a normally hyperbolic point
of the map on
S
. The proofs rely on the
Takens embedding theorem
; see Appendix
B
for a formulation based on
[
7
, Thm. 8.1]
which is suitable for our purposes.

Theorem 2.12
.

Assume that
0\in S_{\textup{nh}}
and consider the extended map (
19
), where Assumptions
1
,
2
and
3
apply to
H(z,\varepsilon)
. There exists and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for each
l\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
there exists a neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}_{l}\times[0,\varepsilon_{0})
about
\tilde{z}=(z,\varepsilon)^{\textnormal{T}}=(0,0)^{\textnormal{T}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, a diffeomorphism
\psi_{l}:\mathcal{U}_{l}\times[0,\varepsilon_{0})\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, and a vector field
X:\mathcal{U}_{l}\times[0,\varepsilon_{0})\to\textup{T}\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\cong\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
such that

1.

j^{l}(\psi_{l}\circ F\circ\psi_{l}^{-1})
is a Poincaré normal form of
F
up to order
l
;

2.

X(B\tilde{z})=BX(\tilde{z})
for any
\tilde{z}=(z,\varepsilon)\in\mathcal{U}_{l}\times[0,\varepsilon_{0})
, where

is the semi-simple matrix defined in (

);

3.

j^{l}(\psi_{l}\circ F\circ\psi_{l}^{-1})(\tilde{z})=j^{l}(\Phi_{X}^{1}(B\tilde{z}))
, where
\Phi_{X}^{t}(\tilde{z})
denotes the flow of

by time

4.

The
l
-jet of the vector field
j^{l}X(\tilde{z})
is uniquely determined by the
l
-jet of the map
j^{l}F(\tilde{z})
.

If the matrix
\textup{D}fN(0)
has purely real eigenvalues, the preceding assertions hold for
B=A
, where
A
is the Jacobian matrix defined in (
20
).

Proof.

Assertions 1-4 follow after a direct application of Takens’ embedding theorem; we refer again to Appendix
B
.

If all
n-k
eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}(0)
of the matrix
\textrm{D}fN(0)
are real, then the
n-k
non-trivial multipliers
\mu_{j}(0)=1+\lambda_{j}(0)
corresponding to eigenvalues of the matrix

are also real. Since the remaining
k+1
eigenvalues of
\mathbb{I}_{n}+N\textrm{D}f(0)
are identically
1
, it follows that the matrix
A
defined in (

) has purely real eigenvalues, implying that the semisimple part of
A
is
A
, i.e. that
B=A
.
∎

Theorem
2.12
shows that in a neighbourhood of a normally hyperbolic, non-superstable point, the original map (
5
) is
C^{r}
-conjugate to a map which can be formally approximated by the time-1 flow of an
\varepsilon
-dependent,
C^{r}
-smooth formal vector field in the same dimension. The matrix
B
can be determined algorithmically, and the diffeomorphisms
\psi_{l}
can be determined in a recursive and systematic fashion using normal form theory, see e.g.
[
7
,
19
]
. In practice, however, this is often a non-trivial and computationally expensive task, which is expected to lead to difficulties when it comes to the question of whether Theorem
2.12
can be used as an approximation tool in applications.

From an applied point of view, it may be preferable to consider approximations obtained via a formal embedding of the slow map (
12
), which governs the dynamics on
k
-dimensional slow manifolds
S_{\varepsilon}
. In order to state our results, we denote the right-hand side of the map (
12
)
again
by
\mathcal{G}(z,\varepsilon)
, i.e. we write

\mathcal{G}(z,\varepsilon)=z+\varepsilon\Pi_{\mathcal{N}}^{S_{\textrm{nh}}}G(z,0)+O(\varepsilon^{2}),\qquad z\in\widetilde{S},

(24)

where
\widetilde{S}
is a compact submanifold of
S_{\textrm{nh}}
, and let
\Phi^{t}_{\Pi_{\mathcal{N}}^{S_{\textrm{nh}}}G(z,0)}(z,\varepsilon)
denote the flow induced by the reduced problem (
17
). Our second approximation theorem shows that
\mathcal{G}(z,\varepsilon)
can be locally approximated by the time-
1
map
\Phi^{1}_{\Pi_{\mathcal{N}}^{S_{\textrm{nh}}}G(z,0)}(z,\varepsilon)
.

Theorem 2.13
.

Consider the map (
24
) and let
z\in S_{\textup{nh}}
. There is an
\varepsilon_{0}>0
and
\varepsilon
-independent neighbourhood
\Omega\ni z

S_{\textup{nh}}
such that for all
(z,\varepsilon)\in\Omega\times[0,\varepsilon_{0})
we have

j^{1}\mathcal{G}(z,\varepsilon)=j^{1}\Phi^{1}_{\Pi_{\mathcal{N}}^{S_{\textup{nh}}}G(z,0)}(z,\varepsilon),\qquad j^{l}\mathcal{G}(z,\varepsilon)=j^{l}\Phi^{1}_{\Pi_{\mathcal{N}}^{S_{\textup{nh}}}G(z,0)}(z,\varepsilon)+O(\varepsilon^{2}),

(25)

where the latter holds for all
l=2,\ldots,r
.

The proof of Theorem
2.13
is deferred to Appendix
C
for brevity. The existence of a unique formal embedding, i.e. a unique formal vector field

whose time-
1
map has a Taylor expansion coinciding with the Taylor expansion of the slow map
\mathcal{G}(z,\varepsilon)
, follows from the Takens embedding theorem. However, additional arguments are needed in order to gain information about the particular form of

, and in particular, to show that the unique formal vector field

is locally approximated by the reduced vector field, i.e. that

j^{l}X(z,0)=j^{l}\Pi_{\mathcal{N}}^{S_{\textrm{nh}}}G(z,0)

for each

. This is shown by a recursive argument based on the formal matching of Taylor coefficients of

and
\Phi^{1}_{\Pi_{\mathcal{N}}^{S_{\textrm{nh}}}G(z,0)}(z,\varepsilon)
, where the Taylor coefficients of the latter are obtained via Picard iteration. This is similar to the procedure for approximating a map by a flow applied in e.g.
[
33
,
34
]
.

The practical advantage of Theorem
2.13
over Theorem
2.12
is that preliminary transformations are not necessary (there is no need to identify the transformations
\psi_{l}
appearing in Theorem
2.12
), and that the approximating vector field is known; it is precisely the reduced vector field (
17
). It is also worthy to note that Theorem
2.13
still applies if the original map (
5
) is orientation-reversing, or even non-invertible (Assumption
3
is not necessary). This is a simple consequence of the fact that the dynamics on the slow manifold
\widetilde{S}_{\varepsilon}
itself is orientation-preserving and invertible, even if (
5
) is not invertible on a neighbourhood in about

in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(we refer back to Remark
2.8
).

Remark 2.14
.

Theorem

and its proof in Appendix

for each

. We refer to
[

.

Remark 2.15
.

The
O(\varepsilon^{2})
term in the second equation in (

) is calculated explicitly in the proof of Theorem

, and given by equation (

Remark 2.16
.

There are certain situations in which an exact embedding can be obtained, as opposed to the finite order approximations in Theorems
2.12
and
2.13
. For example, in
[
9
]
the author shows that planar fast-slow maps

3
Formal embeddings in the non-normally hyperbolic regime

We now turn to the study of dynamics in the non-normally hyperbolic regime. We begin in Section
3.1
with basic definitions and a linear classification of local singularities associated to the loss of normal hyperbolicity along
S
.

3.1
Non-normally hyperbolic singularities

As in continuous-time GSPT, local non-normally hyperbolic singularities can often be defined and classified on the ‘singular level’, in our case, in terms of local conditions on the layer and reduced maps (
8
) and (
15
) respectively. For our present purposes if suffices to consider conditions on the layer map, which can be derived using classical bifurcation theory, see e.g.
[
33
]
, since non-normally hyperbolic singularities correspond to bifurcations in the layer map if the problem is viewed in suitable (standard form) coordinates.

In the classification of codimension-
1
non-normally hyperbolic singularities, there are three important cases; see Figure

:

•

Fold/Contact-type singularities
in the submanifold

\mathcal{C}:=\{z\in S:\mu_{1}(z)=1,|\mu_{l}(z)|\neq 1,l=2,\ldots,n-k\},

corresponding to a single real non-trivial multiplier
\mu_{1}(z)
equal to
1
;

•

Flip/Period-doubling-type singularities
in the submanifold

\mathcal{O}_{\textup{f}}:=\{z\in S:\mu_{1}(z)=-1,|\mu_{l}(z)|\neq 1,l=2,\ldots,n-k\},

corresponding to a single real non-trivial multiplier
\mu_{1}(z)
equal to
-1
;

•

Neimark-Sacker/Torus-type singularities
in the submanifold

\mathcal{O}_{\textup{ns}}:=\{z\in S:\mu_{1}(z)=\overline{\mu_{2}(z)}\in S^{1}\setminus\{\pm 1\},|\mu_{l}(z)|\neq 1,l=3,\ldots,n-k\},

corresponding to a pair of complex conjugate non-trivial multipliers
\mu_{1,2}(z)
lying on
S^{1}\setminus\{\pm 1\}
.

We refer to points in
\mathcal{C}
as fold or contact-type singularities, since these are in certain sense the ‘least degenerate’ points in
\mathcal{C}
. This will be made more precise below. However, we emphasise that our terminology includes singularities which would not typically be referred to as fold or contact singularities in
\mathcal{C}
. This includes pitchfork and transcritical singularities as important examples. Similar comments apply to distinctions between different singularity types in
\mathcal{O}_{\textup{f}}
and
\mathcal{O}_{\textup{ns}}
.

\mathcal{C}
. These works demonstrate the applicability of the geometric blow-up method in order to study (in these cases Euler) discretizations of fast-slow ODE systems with non-normally hyperbolic singularities. This approach relies on non-trivial scaling properties of the step-size parameter associated to the discretization, and does not extend directly for the study of general maps, i.e. it does not apply to the study of maps which cannot be obtained via discretization.

3.2
Formal embeddings for unipotent singularities

In this section we derive formal embedding theorems which can be used to approximate the dynamics of (
5
) near
unipotent points
, which form an important subset of singularities in
\mathcal{C}
. Such problems are characterised by the fact that the linearisation of the layer map (
8
) along
S
, as given by (
9
), is ‘close’ to the identity map. More precisely, we consider general fast-slow maps (
5
) under Assumptions
1
,
2
and
3
, locally near a point
z\in S
for which the linearisation (
9
) is unipotent, i.e. for which the matrix
N\textrm{D}f(z)
is nilpotent with
(N\textrm{D}f(z))^{l}=\mathbb{O}_{n,n}
for some integer
l\geq 2
.

Remark 3.3
.

Generically, the Jacobian matrix (
9
) appearing in the linearisation of the layer map associated to a fast-slow map (
5
) will not be unipotent at a point in
\mathcal{C}
if
\textup{codim}(S)=n-k\geq 2
. This follows from the presence of non-zero eigenvalues of the matrix
\textup{D}fN
, which implies that
N\textup{D}f
has non-zero eigenvalues, and therefore that
N\textup{D}f
is not nilpotent (nilpotent matrices have only zero eigenvalues). Nevertheless, as we shall see in Section
5.1
below, the study of dynamics near singularities in
\mathcal{C}
can often be reduced to the study of singularities in fast-slow maps with a codimension-
1
critical manifold after center manifold reduction. In this case, every singularity in
\mathcal{C}
has a corresponding linearisation with a unipotent Jacobian matrix (
9
).

Our first result reduces computational difficulty by establishing an equivalence between nilpotency of the
n\times n
square matrix
N\textrm{D}f|S
and the smaller
(n-k)\times(n-k)
square matrix
\textrm{D}fN|_{S}
. It also shows that nilpotency of
N(z)\textrm{D}f(z)
implies that
z\in\mathcal{C}
, i.e. that nilpotency of
N(z)\textrm{D}f(z)
is correlated with the existence of at least one non-trivial multiplier with
\mu_{j}(z)=1
at a non-normally hyperbolic contact point.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
z\in S
. The
n\times n
matrix
\textup{D}(Nf)(z)=N\textup{D}f(z)
is nilpotent of index
l+1
if and only if the
(n-k)\times(n-k)
matrix
\textup{D}fN(z)
is nilpotent of index
l
, where
l\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. In particular,
\mu_{j}(z)=1
for at least one
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-k\}
.

Proof.

It follows from the proof of
[
25
, Prop. 2.8]
that local coordinates can be chosen near
S
such that

N\textrm{D}f|_{S}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{O}_{k,k}&\tilde{N}^{x}\\
\mathbb{O}_{n-k,k}&\textrm{D}fN\end{pmatrix}\bigg|_{S},

where
\tilde{N}^{x}(z)
is a
k\times(n-k)
matrix with full column rank by Assumption
2
. Direct matrix multiplication yields

(N\textrm{D}f)^{l}|_{S}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{O}_{k,k}&\tilde{N}^{x}(\textrm{D}fN)^{l-1}\\
\mathbb{O}_{n-k,k}&(\textrm{D}fN)^{l}\end{pmatrix}\bigg|_{S},

for all
l\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. It follows that
(\textrm{D}fN)^{l}|_{S}=\mathbb{O}_{n-k,n-k}\implies(N\textrm{D}f)^{l+1}|_{S}=\mathbb{O}_{n,n}
. The converse
(N\textrm{D}f)^{l+1}|_{S}=\mathbb{O}_{n,n}\implies(\textrm{D}fN)^{l}|_{S}=\mathbb{O}_{n-k,n-k}
follows from
\tilde{N}^{x}(\textrm{D}fN)^{l}|_{S}=\mathbb{O}_{k,n-k}
, since
\tilde{N}^{x}(z)
has full column rank.

In order to see that
\mu_{j}(z)=1
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-k\}
we recall that the non-trivial multipliers have the form
\mu_{j}(z)=1+\lambda_{j}(z)
, where
\lambda_{j}(z)
are eigenvalues of
\textrm{D}fN(z)
. Since
\textrm{D}fN(z)
is nilpotent we have
\lambda_{j}(z)=0
for at least one
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-k\}
, thereby implying the result.
∎

We are now in a position to state our main result, namely, a formal embedding theorem which can be used to approximate the dynamics of the map (
5
) in a neighbourhood of a unipotent singularity by the time-1 map of a vector field with known properties. As before, our primary tool for proving the existence of a formal embedding near such points
z\in\mathcal{C}\subset S
is the Takens embedding theorem; we refer
again to Appendix
B
for details.

Theorem 3.5
.

Consider the map (
5
) denoted by
z\mapsto H(z,\varepsilon)
under Assumptions
1
and
2
. Assume additionally that
0\in\mathcal{C}\subseteq S
and that the
(n-k)\times(n-k)
square matrix
\textup{D}fN(0)
is nilpotent with index
l\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
. Then there exists a neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}\ni 0
and an
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
(z,\varepsilon)\in\mathcal{U}\times[0,\varepsilon_{0})
and
m\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
we have

j^{m}H(z,\varepsilon)=j^{m}\Phi^{1}_{V}(z,\varepsilon),

(27)

where
\Phi^{1}_{V}(z,\varepsilon)
denotes the time-
1
map induced by the flow of an
\varepsilon
-family of vector fields
V(z,\varepsilon)
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying

j^{r}V(z,\varepsilon)=\widehat{N}(z)j^{r}f(z)+\varepsilon\widehat{G}(z,\varepsilon),

(28)

where
\widehat{N}(z)
is a
C^{r}
-smooth
n\times(n-k)
matrix with full column rank,
\widehat{G}(z,\varepsilon)
is a
C^{r}
-smooth column vector, and
j^{r}f
is the
r
-jet associated to the Taylor expansion of
f(z)
about
z=0
. We also have that

\widehat{N}(0)=N(0),\qquad\widehat{G}(0,0)=G(0,0).

(29)

The vector field (
28
)
is fast-slow with a
k
-dimensional critical manifold

j^{r}S:=\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:j^{r}f(z)=0\right\}

(30)

which is
C^{r}
-close to
S
on
\mathcal{U}
, i.e.

\textup{dist}(S,j^{r}S):=\sup_{z\in\mathcal{U}}|f(z)-j^{r}f(z)|=o(|z|^{r}).

(31)

Proof.

We want to apply the version of Takens’ embedding theorem provided in Corollary
B.2
. In order to do so, we first append the map (
5
) with the trivial map
\varepsilon\to\varepsilon
and consider the Taylor expansion about
(z,\varepsilon)=(0,0)
, i.e.

\begin{pmatrix}z\\
\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}H(z,\varepsilon)\\
\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}=(\mathbb{I}_{n+1}+\Lambda)\begin{pmatrix}z\\
\varepsilon\end{pmatrix}+H^{(2)}(z,\varepsilon)+H^{(3)}(z,\varepsilon)+\ldots+H^{(r)}(z,\varepsilon)+o(|(z,\varepsilon)|^{r}),

(32)

where

\Lambda:=\begin{pmatrix}N\textrm{D}f(0)&G(0,0)\\
\mathbb{O}_{1,n}&0\end{pmatrix}

and the functions
H^{(l)}(z,\varepsilon)
are homogeneous polynomials of degree
l\geq 2
. Repeated matrix multiplication leads to the expression

\Lambda^{l+2}=\begin{pmatrix}(N\textrm{D}f)^{l+2}(0)&(N\textrm{D}f)^{l+1}(0)G(0,0)\\
\mathbb{O}_{1,n}&0\end{pmatrix}=\mathbb{O}_{n+1,n+1},

where we used the fact that
(\textrm{D}fN)(0)
is nilpotent with index
l
so that by Lemma
3.4
,
(N\textrm{D}f)^{l+1}(0)=\mathbb{O}_{n,n}
. Thus
\Lambda
is also nilpotent, with index
l+2
if
(N\textrm{D}f)^{l}(0)G(0,0)\neq\mathbb{O}_{n,1}
and index
l+1
if
(N\textrm{D}f)^{l}(0)G(0,0)\neq\mathbb{O}_{n,1}
. Moreover, the linearisation
\mathbb{I}_{n+1}+\Lambda
has
n+1
eigenvalues equal to
1
. Therefore, the inverse function theorem applies, and guarantees that the map (
32
) is a local diffeomorphism.

The preceding arguments show that Corollary
B.2
applies. Applying it yields the approximation property (
27
) for a uniquely determined family of formal vector fields
V(z,\varepsilon)
satisfying

j^{r}V(z,\varepsilon)=(N\textrm{D}f)(0)z+\varepsilon G(0,0)+V^{(2)}(z,\varepsilon)+V^{(3)}(z,\varepsilon)+\ldots V^{(r)}(z,\varepsilon),

(33)

where the
V^{(l)}(z,\varepsilon)
are homogeneous polynomials of degree
l=2,\ldots,r
.

We now show that the truncated formal vector field (
33
) is fast-slow with general form (
28
). Notice that (i)
V(0,0)=0
, and (ii)

j^{r}H(z,0)=j^{r}\Phi^{1}_{V}(z,0)=z,

locally for all
z\in j^{r}S
. These two facts imply that
j^{r}V(z,0)=0
for all
z\in j^{r}S
, i.e. that the zero sets (critical manifolds) of the truncated map
j^{r}H(z,0)
and the truncated vector field
j^{r}V(z,0)
coincide in
\mathcal{U}
; both are given by (
30
).
Therefore, Hadamard’s lemma (see again Appendix
A
) implies that

j^{r}V(z,0)=\widehat{N}(z)j^{r}f(z),

for an
n\times(n-k)
matrix
\widehat{N}(z)
with full column rank, as required. This shows that
j^{r}V(z,\varepsilon)
is in the form (
28
), and the linear part of (
33
) implies the two equalities in (
29
). Equation (
31
) follows directly from the definition of
r
-jets and the truncation operator
j^{r}
in Definition
2.10
.
∎

Theorem
3.5
provides a means for approximating the dynamics of the map (
5
) near a unipotent singularity in
\mathcal{C}
, via the time-1 map induced by the flow of an
\varepsilon
-dependent family of fast-slow formal vector fields
V(z,\varepsilon)
in the same dimension. The linear part of
V(z,\varepsilon)
is determined by (
28
) and (
29
), and higher order terms in the series expansion can be determined in a formal but systematic fashion; we refer to Appendices
C
and
D
where this procedure is carried out in detail for particular proofs. Similarly to the problem of explicit determination of the transformations in Theorem
2.12
, this can be a computationally demanding task. Fortunately, an explicit form for the approximating vector field
V(z,\varepsilon)
is not needed in many cases, since we obtain a sufficient amount of information about the qualitative dynamics without an explicit representation. This will be demonstrated with numerous applications in Sections
4
and
5
below. At present, it suffices to emphasise and reiterate the following points:

(i)

The truncated vector field
j^{r}V(z,\varepsilon)
is fast-slow in the general form (
28
);

(ii)

The critical manifold
j^{r}S
of the map
j^{r}H(z,\varepsilon)
coincides with the critical manifold of the vector field
j^{r}V(z,\varepsilon)
, and
j^{r}S
is
C^{r}
-close to
S
, the critical manifold of the original map
H(z,\varepsilon)
;

(iii)

The linear part of
j^{r}V(z,\varepsilon)
is known explicitly, due to (
29
).

Properties (i)-(iii) imply that in many cases, the local defining conditions on the layer map at unipotent, non-normally hyperbolic singularities in
\mathcal{C}\subset S
are satisfied if and only if a set of corresponding conditions are satisfied in the approximating vector field (
28
). In particular, if the map (
5
) has a unipotent, non-normally hyperbolic singularity, then the vector field (
28
) has a nilpotent, non-normally hyperbolic singularity. Moreover, due to the
C^{r}
-closeness of the critical manifolds
j^{r}S
and
S
and the close relationship between the reduced map and the reduced vector field described in Theorem
2.13
, the satisfaction of defining conditions for local unipotent singularities in
\mathcal{C}\subset S
which are formulated in terms of
N
,
f
,
G
and their partial derivatives with respect to
z
, are expected to imply the satisfaction of analogous conditions for vector field (
28
). Thus, unipotent singularities of the map (
5
) will typically imply nilpotent singularities of the corresponding type in the vector field (
28
). This can be a powerful tool in practice, since it
allows for the indirect study of the map (
5
) using methods and techniques which are only developed or applicable in the context of fast-slow ODEs.

Remark 3.6
.

Theorem
3.5
also applies in the
C^{\infty}
case, i.e. for
r=\infty
. In this case, equation (
27
) implies that the Taylor expansions of
H(z,\varepsilon)
and
\Phi_{V}^{1}(z,\varepsilon)
about
(z,\varepsilon)=(0,0)
coincide. It is important to stress, however, that this does not imply an exact embedding

H(z,\varepsilon)=\Phi_{V}^{1}(z,\varepsilon).

(34)

Rather, we have that

H(z,\varepsilon)=\Phi_{V}^{1}(z,\varepsilon)+R(z,\varepsilon),

(35)

for an error term
R(z,\varepsilon)
which is flat (beyond all orders) in
(z,\varepsilon)
(if
r<\infty
then
R(z,\varepsilon)=o(|(z,\varepsilon)|^{r})
). An important example for which the error term cannot be removed arises in the case of saddle-node bifurcation in
1
-dimensional maps
[
23
]
; see also Remark
4.8
below. Terminologically, we distinguish “exact embeddings” (
34
) from “formal embeddings” (
35
), and we say that the latter allow for an approximation of the map by a flow. Similar terminology can be found in e.g.
[
6
,
33
,
34
]
.

4
Fold, transcritical and pitchfork points in 2 dimensions

In this section we consider dynamics near three different types of unipotent singularities in two-dimensional fast-slow maps, i.e. in the lowest dimensional setting. This will help to demonstrate the use of Theorem
3.5
in practice. For simplicity, we consider maps in fast-slow standard form

\begin{pmatrix}x\\

(36)

-smooth with

We consider non-normally hyperbolic singularities of regular fold, transcritical and pitchfork type. We begin with definitions and a description of the limiting dynamics and geometry for
\varepsilon=0

4.1.1
Regular fold points

.

Transcritical points

4.2
Formal embedding near fold, transcritical and pitchfork points

In the following we state and prove a formal embedding theorem which allows for the local approximation of the map (
36
) near a regular fold, transcritical or pitchfork point via the time-1 map induced by a planar system of fast-slow ODEs with a singularity of the corresponding type.

Proposition 4.5
.

Assume that the map (
36
) has a unipotent singularity at
(x,y)=(0,0)\in S
. Then there exists a neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}\ni(0,0)
and an
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
(x,y,\varepsilon)\in\mathcal{U}\times[0,\varepsilon_{0})
and
l\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
we have

j^{l}H(x,y,\varepsilon)=j^{l}\Phi^{1}_{V}(x,y,\varepsilon),

(46)

where
\Phi^{1}_{V}(x,y,\varepsilon)
denotes the time-
1
flow of an
\varepsilon
-family vector fields
V(x,y,\varepsilon)
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The truncated vector field
j^{r}V(x,y,\varepsilon)
is fast-slow in the standard form

j^{r}V(x,y,\varepsilon)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{V}_{1}(x,y,\varepsilon)\\
\varepsilon\mathcal{V}_{2}(x,y,\varepsilon)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}K(x,y)j^{r}\tilde{f}(x,y,0)+O(\varepsilon)\\
\varepsilon(\tilde{g}(0,0,0)+O(x,y,\varepsilon))\end{pmatrix},

(47)

where the function
K(x,y)
satisfies
K(0,0)=1
. The vector field (
47
) has a critical manifold

j^{r}S=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{U}:j^{r}\tilde{f}(x,y,0)=0\right\},

(48)

for which
(0,0)\in j^{r}S
is a nilpotent singularity. In particular,
(0,0)
is a

1.

Regular fold point if
(0,0)
is a regular fold point of the map (
36
);

2.

Transcritical point if
(0,0)
is a transcritical point of the map (
36
);

3.

Pitchfork point if
(0,0)
is a pitchfork point of the map (
36
).

Proof.

We want to apply Theorem
3.5
. In order to do so, we need to check Assumptions
1
,
2
and
3
, and show that
(0,0)
is a unipotent singularity of (
36
).

Following Remark
2.2
, we write the
map
(
36
) in the general form (
5
) by defining

N(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\qquad f(x,y)=\tilde{f}(x,y,0),\qquad G(x,y,\varepsilon)=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon^{-1}\left(\tilde{f}(x,y,\varepsilon)-\tilde{f}(x,y,0)\right)\\
\tilde{g}(0,0,0)\end{pmatrix}.

The point
(0,0)
is a unipotent singularity if
\textrm{D}fN(0,0)
is nilpotent, which in this case (
n-k=1
) is equivalent to
(0,0)\in\mathcal{C}
. Using (
37
), (
40
) or (
43
), we have

\textrm{D}fN(0,0)=\frac{\partial\tilde{f}}{\partial x}(0,0,0)=0,

implying nilpotency. Assumptions
2
and
3
can be verified directly (the latter follows from the inverse function theorem), as can all of the conditions in Assumption
1
except for the requirement that
S
can be regularly embedded as a submanifold in

It follows from (

) that the truncated formal vector field

takes the standard form

\varepsilon\mathcal{V}_{2}(x,y,\varepsilon)\end{pmatrix},

, truncated at order

that the critical manifold is given by (

∎

Thus, the local dynamics near unipotent, non-normally hyperbolic singularities of the map (

Moreover, if the singularity of the map is of regular fold, transcritical or pitchfork type, then the approximating vector field also has a regular fold, transcritical or pitchfork singularity respectively. Since the local dynamics near regular fold, transcritical and pitchfork singularities in planar fast-slow ODE systems are already well understood
[

,
30
]
, Proposition

can be used to approximate the dynamics of the map.

Remark 4.6
.

Every non-normally hyperbolic point in a planar fast-slow ODE system is nilpotent, due to the fact that there are no oscillatory singularities in planar fast-slow systems. Oscillatory singularities can occur in planar fast-slow maps, however, due to the possibility of flip/period-doubling singularities in
\mathcal{O}_{\textup{f}}
. Combining this observation with Proposition
4.5
, we find that every nilpotent singularity of a planar fast-slow ODE system has a corresponding unipotent singularity in a planar fast-slow map, but the converse is not true, i.e. not every non-normally hyperbolic singularity in a planar fast-slow map has a corresponding niplotent singularity in a planar fast-slow ODE system.

Remark 4.7

Results for planar fast-slow maps with regular fold, transcritical and pitchfork singularities obtained after Euler discretizations of a planar fast-slow system with singularities of the corresponding types have been derived using a variant of the geometric blow-up method in

5

z\in\mathcal{C}\subset S
is a regular contact point if

\textup{rk}\left(\textup{D}fN(z)\right)=n-k-1

(50)

and the following genericity conditions are satisfied:

•

Transversality, i.e.
\textup{rk}(\textup{D}f)(z)=n-k
;

•

Nondegeneracy, i.e.
l\cdot((\textup{D}^{2}f)(Nr,Nr)+\textup{D}f\textup{D}N(Nr,r))(z)\neq 0
;

•

Slow regularity, i.e.
Nrl\textup{D}fG(z,0)\neq\mathbb{O}_{n,1}
,

where
l
and
r
denote left and right null vectors of the matrix
\textup{D}fN(z)
respectively, and
(D^{2}f)(Nr,Nr)
and
\textup{D}f\textup{D}N(Nr,r)
are bilinear forms with the following componentwise definitions:

\begin{split}[\textup{D}^{2}f]_{i}(Nr,Nr)&=\sum_{l,m=1}^{n}\sum_{j,s=1}^{n-k}\frac{\partial^{2}f_{i}}{\partial z_{l}\partial z_{m}}(N_{mj}r_{j})(N_{ls}r_{s}),\\
[\textup{D}f\textup{D}N]_{i}(Nr,r)&=\sum_{l,m=1}^{n}\sum_{j,s=1}^{n-k}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial z_{l}}\frac{\partial N_{lj}}{\partial z_{m}}(N_{ms}r_{s})r_{j},\end{split}

for each
i=1,\ldots,n-k
. The definition extends to submanifolds, i.e. a submanifold
\mathcal{C}_{r}\subset S
is called a regular contact (sub)manifold if every
z\in\mathcal{C}_{r}
is a regular contact point.

The case of regular contact along a 1-dimensional submanifold
\mathcal{C}^{r}
of a 2-dimensional critical manifold
S
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is sketched in Figure
6
. The rank condition (
50
) implies that there is a single non-trivial multiplier, which we may denote by
\mu_{1}
, which satisfies
\mu_{1}(z)=1
along a
(k-1)
-dimensional regular contact manifold
\mathcal{C}^{r}\subset S
. The algebraic multiplicity of the zero eigenvalue of the matrix
N\textrm{D}f
is
k+1
at a regular contact point, which is one greater than the geometric multiplicity
k
. Geometrically, this corresponds to a tangency between
S
and the curve(s) containing orbits of the layer problem (
8
). More precisely, if
z\in S
is a regular contact point, then
\textup{range}(N(z))
and
\textup{T}_{z}S
are tangent along the
1
-dimensional line given by the span of the (generalised) null vector
\mathfrak{N}_{1}(z)
of the matrix
\textrm{D}fN(z)
which corresponds to the critical multiplier
\mu_{1}(z)
. The transversality condition implies that
S
is a regularly embedded submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the nondegeneracy condition implies that the tangency described above is quadratic when considered on the nonlinear level, and the slow regularity condition implies that orbits of the reduced vector field
\Pi^{S}_{\mathcal{N}}G(z,0)|_{z\in S}
, which locally approximates the slow dynamics (recall Theorem

), extend to transversal intersections with

Remark 5.2
.

The slow regularity condition appearing in

N\textup{adj}(\textup{D}fN)\textup{D}fG(z,0)\neq\mathbb{O}_{n,1},

Remark 5.3

For fast-slow maps in standard form (

Remark 5.4
.

The transversality condition

Remark 5.5
.

By analogy to

Further consideration of canards are left for future work.

5.1
Center manifold reduction

It was shown in
[

,
49
]
that the mathematical analysis near a regular fold submanifold

(k+1)
-dimensional local local center manifold along
\mathcal{C}^{r}
. A similar feature appears in the context of regular fold submanifolds in fast-slow maps in general form (

We consider maps in general form (
5
) under Assumptions
1
-
2
, which are
C^{r}
-smooth with
r\geq 3
,
\textup{codim}(S)\geq 2
, and a regular contact point at
z=0\in S
. Using Assumption
1
, in particular the assumption that

is a regularly embedded submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we may choose local coordinates
z=(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k}
such that the matrix
\textrm{D}_{y}f
is regular (full rank and invertible) in a neighbourhood of
z=0
. We write

\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}\mapsto H(x,y,\varepsilon)=\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}N^{x}(x,y)\\
N^{y}(x,y)\end{pmatrix}f(x,y)+\varepsilon\begin{pmatrix}G^{x}(x,y,\varepsilon)\\
G^{y}(x,y,\varepsilon)\end{pmatrix},

(52)

where
N^{x}(x,y)
and

are matrices of size

and
(n-k)\times(n-k)
respectively, and
G^{x}(x,y,\varepsilon)
and
G^{y}(x,y,\varepsilon)
are column vectors of size
k\times 1
and
(n-k)\times 1
respectively. Before we apply the center manifold theorem, it is helpful to isolate the
(k+1)
-dimensional center eigenspace
E^{\textup{c}}(0)
. This can be achieved in a two step procedure which is directly analogous to the corresponding derivation in the context of fast-slow ODEs in
[

, Ch. 4.4]

Lemma 5.6
.

The map (
52
) is locally
C^{r}
-conjugate to the map

\widehat{H}(x,u,w,\varepsilon)=\begin{pmatrix}x\\
u\\
w\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{N}^{x}(x,u,w)\\
\widehat{N}^{u}(x,u,w)\\
\widehat{N}^{w}(x,u,w)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
w\end{pmatrix}+\varepsilon\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{G}^{x}(x,u,w,\varepsilon)\\
\widehat{G}^{u}(x,u,w,\varepsilon)\\
\widehat{G}^{w}(x,u,w,\varepsilon)\end{pmatrix}+R(x,u,w,\varepsilon),

(53)

where
(x,u,w)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k-1}
, and the remainder term satisfies

R(x,u,w,\varepsilon)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{O}_{k,1}\\
O(|(u,w,\varepsilon)|^{2})\end{pmatrix}.

The critical manifold of the map (
53
) is contained within the hyperplane defined by
u=0
,
w=0
, and the generalised eigenspace
E^{\textup{c}}(0)
is spanned by the
(x,u)
-coordinates.

Proof.

This can be proven with minor adaptations to the proof of
[
49
, Thm. 4.1]
. The first step is to rectify
S
with a nonlinear coordinate transformation

v=f(x,y),\qquad y=K(x,v).

Note that a local inverse exists due to the implicit function theorem, since
\textrm{D}_{y}f
is regular. In particular, we have
K(x,v)=(\textrm{D}_{y}f)^{-1}\left(v-\textrm{D}_{x}fx\right)+O(|(x,v)|^{2})
. Taylor expansion about
v=0
and
\varepsilon=0
in the new coordinates leads to the map

\widetilde{H}(x,v,\varepsilon)=\begin{pmatrix}x\\
v\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}N^{x}(x,K(x,v))\\
\textrm{D}fN(x,K(x,v))\end{pmatrix}v+\varepsilon\begin{pmatrix}G^{x}(x,K(x,v),\varepsilon)\\
\textrm{D}fG(x,K(x,v),\varepsilon)\end{pmatrix}+R(v,\varepsilon),

(54)

where the remainder function satisfies
R(v,\varepsilon)=(\mathbb{O}_{k,1},O(|(v,\varepsilon)|^{2}))^{\textnormal{T}}
. The advantage is that
S
has been rectified along
v=0
. In particular, the tangent space
\textup{T}_{0}S
is now spanned by the
x
-coordinates, since the Jacobian matrix has the form

\textrm{D}\widetilde{H}(0,0,0)=\mathbb{I}_{n}+\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{O}_{k,k}&N^{x}(x,K(x,v))\\
\mathbb{O}_{n-k,k}&\textrm{D}fN(x,K(x,v))\end{pmatrix}.

The second step is to extract the fast direction along which contact occurs. This can be achieved with a linear transformation of the form

\begin{pmatrix}u\\
w\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}l\\
Q\end{pmatrix}v,\qquad v=\begin{pmatrix}r&P\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
w\end{pmatrix}=ru+Pw,

where
u\in\mathbb{R}
,
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n-k-1}
,
l
and
r
are left and right null vectors of
\textrm{D}fN
at
z=0
, and
Q
,
P
are matrices of size
(n-k-1)\times(n-k)
,
(n-k)\times(n-k-1)
such that

\begin{pmatrix}r&P\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}l\\
Q\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}l\\
Q\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}r&P\end{pmatrix}=\mathbb{I}_{n-k}.

Expressing (
52
) in
(x,u,w)
coordinates, we obtain

\widehat{H}(x,u,w,\varepsilon)=\begin{pmatrix}x\\
u\\
w\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}N^{x}\\
l\textrm{D}fN\\
Q\textrm{D}fN\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}r&P\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
w\end{pmatrix}+\varepsilon\begin{pmatrix}G^{x}\\
l\textrm{D}fG\\
Q\textrm{D}fG\end{pmatrix}+R(x,u,w,\varepsilon),

(55)

where
R(u,w,\varepsilon)=(\mathbb{O}_{k,1},O(|(u,w,\varepsilon)|^{2}))^{\textnormal{T}}
and we have omitted the argument notation in the right-hand side for notational simplicity. The map (
55
) is in the desired form (
53
).

Finally, the Jacobian matrix is upper block triangular of the form

\textrm{D}\widehat{H}(0,0,0,0)=\mathbb{I}_{n}+\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{O}_{k,k}&N^{x}r&N^{x}P\\
\mathbb{O}_{1,k}&0&\mathbb{O}_{1,n-k-1}\\
\mathbb{O}_{n-k-1,k}&\mathbb{O}_{n-k-1,1}&Q\textrm{D}fNP\end{pmatrix},

(56)

which shows that the
(k+1)
-dimensional generalised center eigenspace

is spanned by the
(x,u)
-coordinates.
∎

as required.

The nondegeneracy condition reduces to the requirement that

\sum_{l,m=1}^{k+1}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}(j^{r}\tilde{f})}{\partial\xi_{l}\partial\xi_{m}}(0,0)\mathfrak{N}_{m}(0,0)\mathfrak{N}_{l}(0,0)+\frac{\partial(j^{r}\widetilde{f})}{\partial\xi_{l}}(0,0)\frac{\partial\mathfrak{N}_{l}}{\partial\xi_{m}}(0,0)\mathfrak{N}_{m}(0,0)\right)\neq 0,

(68)

where we introduced the componentwise notation
\xi=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k},\xi_{k+1})=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},u)
and
\mathfrak{N}=(\mathfrak{N}^{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{N}^{x}_{k},\mathfrak{N}^{u})^{\textnormal{T}}
. It follows from the form of
j^{r}\widetilde{f}(x,u)
in (
66
) and the fact that
\mathfrak{N}^{u}(0,0)=\widetilde{N}^{u}(0,0)=0
that

\frac{\partial^{2}(j^{r}\tilde{f})}{\partial\xi_{l}\partial\xi_{m}}(0,0)\mathfrak{N}_{m}(0,0)\mathfrak{N}_{l}(0,0)=0

for all
l,m=1,\ldots,k+1
. Moreover, the terms on the right can be rewritten as

\frac{\partial(j^{r}\widetilde{f})}{\partial\xi_{l}}(0,0)\frac{\partial\mathfrak{N}_{l}}{\partial\xi_{m}}(0,0)\mathfrak{N}_{m}(0,0)=\frac{\partial\widetilde{f}}{\partial\xi_{l}}(0,0)\frac{\partial\mathfrak{N}_{l}}{\partial\xi_{m}}(0,0)\widetilde{N}_{m}(0,0),

for all
l,m=1,\ldots,k+1
. Thus, the nondegeneracy condition in (
68
) reduces to

\sum_{l,m=1}^{k+1}\frac{\partial\widetilde{f}}{\partial\xi_{l}}(0,0)\frac{\partial\mathfrak{N}_{l}}{\partial\xi_{m}}(0,0)\widetilde{N}_{m}(0,0)\neq 0.

(69)

Finally, one can show that

\frac{\partial\mathfrak{N}_{l}}{\partial\xi_{m}}(0,0)=\frac{\partial\widetilde{N}_{l}}{\partial\xi_{m}}(0,0),

(70)

after which (
69
) reduces to the nondegeneracy condition for the restricted map (
58
), which is satisfied. The equality in (
70
) can be obtained by matching coefficients in truncated Taylor expansions of (
58
) and the time-1 map in (
64
). This calculation is standard but lengthy, so we defer it to Appendix
D
for expository reasons.

It remains to check the slow regularity condition. Since
\textrm{D}(j^{r}\widetilde{f})\widehat{N}(0,0)
is a scalar we have that
r=l=1
. Using this together with (
66
), the slow regularity condition reduces on the vector field (
65
) reduces to the slow regularity condition on the map (
58
), and we obtain

\mathfrak{N}\textrm{D}(j^{r}\widetilde{f})\mathfrak{G}(0,0,0)=\widetilde{N}\textrm{D}\widetilde{f}\widetilde{G}(0,0,0)\neq 0,

as required.
∎

shows that the dynamics on the center manifold

can be locally approximated by the time-

map induced by a
(k+1)

(0,0)
. It is significant to note that Theorem

satisfy

|\mu_{j}(0)|<1,\qquad\forall j=2,\ldots,n-k,

due to the local exponential attractivity of

49

(the case

). Although we do not consider the geometry and dynamics in detail here, we conjecture that the approximation result in Theorem

6
Summary and Outlook

The following three features have been fundamental to the success of continuous-time GSPT as an approach to the study of fast-slow ODE systems:

(I)

A systematic geometric formalism for identifying and analysing simpler limiting problems associated to each time-scale;

(II)

A set of perturbation theorems in the normally hyperbolic regime, as provided by Fenichel theory;

(III)

A set of independent results which describe the local dynamics close to non-normally hyperbolic singularities.

The primary aim of this manuscript has been to continue the development of DGSPT, based on the principle that (I)-(III) should also be viewed as cornerstones for a geometric approach to the study of discrete fast-slow systems induced by repeated iteration of a map. One can also view this work as a natural continuation of the work in
[
25
]
, which focused on (I)-(II) via the development of DGSPT for general fast-slow maps (
5
) in the normally hyperbolic regime.

In Section
2.2
we presented two new results in the normally hyperbolic regime, namely Theorems
2.12
and
2.13
. Both of these results can be used in order to approximate the dynamics on slow manifolds via the time-
1
map induced by the flow of a vector field in the same dimension. Theorem
2.13
in particular describes a close relationship to the reduced vector field which appears as the limiting problem for the dynamics on the critical manifold in the continuous-time setting. Not only does this elucidate a close connection to continuous-time theory on the slow time-scale, but, for many purposes, it provides a means of reducing the study of the map to the study of a vector field which is known.

Our primary focus, however, was to contribute to the understanding of dynamics near non-normally hyperbolic points, i.e. we focused primarily on (III). As we saw in Section
3.1
, codimension-
1
non-normally hyperbolic singularities can be split into three important types: fold/contact points in
\mathcal{C}\subset S
, flip/period-doubling points in
\mathcal{O}_{\textup{f}}\subset S
, and torus/Neimark-Sacker points in
\mathcal{O}_{\textup{ns}}\subset S
. These correspond to points at which the matrix
\mathbb{I}_{n-k}+\textrm{D}fN
has a single multiplier at
+1
,
-1
, or a pair of complex conjugate multipliers on
S^{1}\setminus\{\pm 1\}
, respectively. The so-called ‘oscillatory singularities’ in
\mathcal{O}_{\textup{f}}\cup\mathcal{O}_{\textup{ns}}
can often be analysed using techniques which rely on the invertibility of the matrix
\textrm{D}fN
, we refer again to
[
3
,
4
,
16
,
17
,
40
,
41
]
and the references therein. We therefore chose to focus on the loss of normal hyperbolicity near singularities in
\mathcal{C}
, where the matrix
\textrm{D}fN
is singular.

Our most important theoretical result on the dynamics near singularities in
\mathcal{C}
is Theorem
3.5
, which shows that the dynamics near unipotent singularities, which form an important subset of
\mathcal{C}
, can be approximated by the time-
1
map of a formal vector field in the same dimension. This vector field is fast-slow with a nilpotent singularity and a critical manifold
j^{r}S
that is
C^{r}
-close to the critical manifold of the original map. The proof of Theorem
3.5
relied on direct arguments and a suitable application of the Takens embedding theorem.

In order to demonstrate the applicability of Theorem
3.5
, we used it in order to generate results on the geometry and dynamics near non-normally hyperbolic points of regular fold, transcritical and pitchfork type in planar fast-slow maps in standard form; recall Section
4
. We showed in Proposition
4.5
that
2
-dimensional fast-slow maps in standard form (
36
) can be approximated by the time-
1
map of a planar fast-slow ODE system in standard form, and that the approximating vector field has a non-normally hyperbolic point of regular fold, transcritical or pitchfork type if the original map has a non-normally hyperbolic point of the corresponding type. Using this result and the established results from continuous-time theory in
[
29
,
30
]
, we characterised the extension of attracting slow manifolds through a neighbourhood of regular fold, transcritical and pitchfork points in the map (
36
) in Corollaries
4.10
,
4.12
and
4.15
respectively. It is significant to note that the established results in the continuous-time setting, i.e. those in
[
29
,
30
]
, were derived using the geometric blow-up method, which is either not applicable or not yet developed for the study of singularities in general fast-slow maps (with the exception of discretized systems, recall Remarks
3.2
and
4.7
). This points to a major advantage of approximation results like Proposition
4.5
, namely, that they provide a means for analysing certain features of the map using methods that are only developed or applicable in the continuous-time setting.

Finally in Section
5
, we showed that Theorem
3.5
could be also used to approximate the dynamics near regular contact points of fast-slow maps in general nonstandard form (
5
) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, even though contact points in
\mathcal{C}
are not generically unipotent if
\textup{codim}(S)\geq 2
, i.e. if
n-k\geq 2
. The key observation is that fast-slow maps with a codimension-1 regular contact point admit of a local center manifold reduction. The center manifold
W_{\textrm{loc}}^{\textup{c}}
is
(k+1)
-dimensional, and
\textup{codim}(S)=1
in the restricted map
H|_{W_{\textrm{loc}}^{\textup{c}}}
which governs dynamics on
W_{\textrm{loc}}^{\textup{c}}
; this was shown in Lemma
5.7
. The map
H|_{W_{\textrm{loc}}^{\textup{c}}}
is also fast-slow with a regular contact point, but in this case, the contact point is also unipotent. This allowed for the application of Theorem
3.5
, which in turn allowed us to show that the dynamics of
H|_{W_{\textrm{loc}}^{\textup{c}}}
can be approximated by the time-
1
map of a
(k+1)
-dimensional fast-slow system with a regular contact point. This is summarised in Theorem
5.8
.

There are many open questions remaining. For example, Corollaries
4.10
,
4.12
and
4.15
describe the extension of attracting slow manifolds through a neighbourhood of different types of non-normally hyperbolic points, but we did not present results on the dynamics of larger sets of initial conditions. Another open question pertains to the extent to which the approximation error appearing in all of the formal embedding theorems presented herein can be improved. In this regard it is worthy to reiterate that exponentially small errors may be unavoidable, recall Remark
4.8
, which suggests that the details of exponentially small separations which are typical in canard theory, for example, cannot be simply described with formal approximations of the kind presented herein.

More generally, the development of DGSPT is closely tied to Guckenheimer’s program for the development of a ‘global’ GSPT
[
20
]
which is general enough to apply to the study of oscillatory fast-slow systems, i.e. fast-slow ODE systems featuring a manifold or manifolds of limit cycles in the layer problem. The connection arises because DGSPT provides a geometric framework for studying the fast-slow Poincaré maps which arise naturally in this setting. We believe that
[
25
]
and the present manuscript constitute significant progress towards a rigorous geometric theory for global oscillatory fast-slow systems, which has implications for the geometric study of models of complex oscillatory dynamics in a wide range of areas including mathematical neuroscience, biology and chemistry. The exact nature of this connection is left for future work.

Appendix A
Hadamard’s lemma

Here we state
a
version of
Hadamard’s lemma
, see e.g.
[
42
]
for a formulation suited to our purposes. A simple proof can be given using Taylor’s theorem.

Lemma A.1
.

(Hadamard’s lemma)

Let
f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})^{\textnormal{T}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a
C^{1}
-smooth function at
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
which satisfies

f(z)=0,\qquad\textup{rk}(\textup{D}f(z))=m.

For every
C^{1}
-smooth function
\rho:U\to\mathbb{R}
, where
U
is a neighbourhood of
z
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists a neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}\subseteq U
of
z
and continuous functions
\sigma_{j}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
,
j=1,\ldots,m
such that

\rho=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma_{j}f_{j}.

If
\rho
and
f
are
C^{r}
-smooth, there exists a
C^{r}
-smooth choice of
\sigma_{j}
’s.

Appendix B
Takens’ embedding theorem

In the following we state the version of Takens’ embedding theorem given in
[
7
, Theorem 8.1]
(see also
[
19
, Prop. 6.1]
), as well as a corollary which specialises this result for unipotent points.
Takens’ own result can be found in
[
47
, Thm. 4]
. This is similar to the version that we use, except that it applies to scalar diffeomorphisms only. Numerous (albeit simpler) versions of the result predate Takens’ result:
An early reference
where a similar result for scalar diffeomorphisms with linear part identity appears
without proof in
[
5
]
, and later with a proof in
[
36
]
. An early formulation of a similar result in
n
-dimensions
can be found in
[
6
]
.

Consider a
C^{r}
-diffeomorphism
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
r\geq 2
and a fixed point at the origin, i.e.
F(0)=0
. Taylor expansion gives

F(x)=Ax+F^{2}(x)+F^{3}(x)+\dots+F^{r}(x)+o(|x|^{r}),

(71)

where the
F^{l}
are homogeneous polynomials of order
l\in\{2,\ldots,r\}
in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The extension to parameter-dependent maps is straightforward; one simply considers the parameters
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
as variables by appending the trivial map(s)
\lambda\mapsto\lambda
to
F
. Using a Jordan-Chevalley decomposition, the Jacobian matrix
A
can be written as

A=B(\mathbb{I}_{n}+M),

where the matrices
B
and
M
are semi-simple and nilpotent respectively, and
BM=MB
. With this identification, Takens’ theorem can be stated as follows.

Theorem B.1
.

(Takens embedding theorem)

Consider the diffeomorphism (
71
). For each
l\in\{2,\ldots,r\}
there exists a diffeomorphism
\psi_{l}:\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a vector field
X:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\textup{T}\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

(i)

j^{l}(\psi_{l}\circ F\circ\psi_{l}^{-1})
is a Poincaré normal form of
F
up to order
l
;

(ii)

X(Bx)=BX(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
;

(iii)

j^{l}(\psi_{l}\circ F\circ\psi_{l}^{-1})=j^{l}(\Phi_{X}^{1}(Bx))
, where
\Phi^{t}_{X}(x)
denotes the time-
t
flow map induced by
X(x)
;

(iv)

The truncated vector field
j^{l}X(x)
is uniquely determined by
j^{l}F(x)
.

In addition to Theorem
B.1
, we present a well-known corollary for the unipotent case
B=\mathbb{I}_{n}
. This case arises frequently throughout this work. The following formulation can be found in
[
7
, Lem. 8.2]
.

Corollary B.2
.

Assume the same conditions as in Theorem
B.1
and, additionally, that
B=\mathbb{I}_{n}
so that
A=\mathbb{I}_{n}+M
. Then there exists a vector field
X:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\textup{T}\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
j^{r}X(x)
is uniquely determined by
j^{r}F(x)
, and the flow
\Phi^{t}_{X}(x)
satisfies

j^{r}\Phi^{1}_{X}(x)=j^{r}F(x).

\sum_{j_{1}+\ldots+j_{n}=j_{s_{1}}+j_{s_{2}}=2}B^{(2)}_{i,j_{1}\cdots j_{n}0}z_{s_{1}}z_{s_{2}}=0\quad\implies\quad B^{(2)}_{i,j_{1}\cdots j_{n}0}=0.

Substituting
B^{(2)}_{i,j_{1}\cdots j_{n}0}=0
into (
80
) and matching terms with
j_{n+1}=1
, we obtain

\varepsilon\sum_{j_{1}+\ldots+j_{n}=j_{s}=1}B^{(2)}_{i,j_{1}\cdots j_{n}1}z_{s}=\varepsilon\sum_{j_{1}+\ldots+j_{n}=j_{s}=1}b^{(2)}_{i,j_{1}\cdots j_{n}1}z_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{j_{n}}\quad\implies\quad B^{(2)}_{i,j_{1}\cdots j_{n}1}=b^{(2)}_{i,j_{1}\cdots j_{n}1},

whenever
j_{1}+\cdots+j_{n}=1
. This proves the equality of coefficients requirement in (
78
) in the base case
l=2
.

For completeness, we note that substituting the preceding expressions and matching terms at
O(\varepsilon^{2})
gives

B^{(2)}_{i,0\cdots 02}=b^{(2)}_{i,0\cdots 02}-\sum_{j_{1}+\ldots+j_{n}=j_{s}=1}b^{(2)}_{i,j_{1}\cdots j_{n}1}\frac{a_{s}}{2}.

(81)

Induction step:
Now assume as an induction hypothesis that (
78
) holds for all
l=2,\ldots,k
, for some
k<r
. We need to show that it also holds for
l=k+1
. It follows from the form of equation (

) that the matching requirement at order

is

H^{(k+1)}_{i}(x)-\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{F}_{i}^{(k)}(x^{(k)}(\tau))d\tau=0,

for each
i=1,\ldots,n
. In order to evaluate (

), we need to simplify

. At order
k

H^{(k)}_{i}(x)-\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{F}_{i}^{(k)}(x^{(k-1)}(\tau))d\tau=0,

for each
i=1,\ldots,n
. Using the series expressions in (
75
), applying the induction hypothesis and splitting the series into parts which factor
O(\varepsilon^{0})
,
O(\varepsilon)
and
O(\varepsilon^{2})
as in the proof of the base case above, we obtain

\begin{split}&\sum_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{n}=k}b_{i,j_{1}\cdots j_{n}0}^{(k)}\left(z_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{j_{n}}-\int_{0}^{1}z_{1}^{(k)}(\tau)^{j_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{(k)}(\tau)^{j_{n}}d\tau\right)\\
&+\varepsilon\sum_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{n}=k-1}b_{i,j_{1}\cdots j_{n}1}^{(k)}\left(z_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{j_{n}}-\int_{0}^{1}z_{1}^{(k)}(\tau)^{j_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{(k)}(\tau)^{j_{n}}d\tau\right)+O(\varepsilon^{2})=0,\end{split}

\begin{split}&\sum_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{n}=k+1}\left(b_{i,j_{1}\cdots j_{n}0}^{(k+1)}z_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{j_{n}}-B_{i,j_{1}\cdots j_{n}0}^{(k+1)}\int_{0}^{1}z_{1}^{(k)}(\tau)^{j_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{(k)}(\tau)^{j_{n}}d\tau\right)+\\
&\varepsilon\sum_{j_{1}+\cdots+j_{n}=k}\left(b_{i,j_{1}\cdots j_{n}1}^{(k+1)}z_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{j_{n}}-B_{i,j_{1}\cdots j_{n}1}^{(k+1)}\int_{0}^{1}z_{1}^{(k)}(\tau)^{j_{1}}\cdots z_{n}^{(k)}(\tau)^{j_{n}}d\tau\right)+O(\varepsilon^{2})=0.\end{split}

Remark C.2

The (rather direct) method of proof adopted in the section and the next is inspired by approaches to the approximation of maps by flows applied in e.g.

In order to complete the proof of Theorem

, we need to prove the equality (

\frac{\partial\mathfrak{N}^{u}}{\partial x_{m}}(0,0)=\frac{\partial\widetilde{N}^{u}}{\partial x_{m}}(0,0),

for all

, the approximating vector field for

2.13
in Appendix

where

We want to determine the form of the truncated vector field

We have that
