Mixed Preference Optimization: A Two-stage Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedbacks
Qi Gou, Cam-Tu Nguyen

Abstract
There are currently two main approaches to LLM alignment: online Reinforcement Learning (RLHF) with Proximal Preferene Optimization (PPO) and contrastive learning-based methods like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO). By analyzing advantages and disadvantages of PPO and DPO, we propose Mixed Preference Optimization (MPO) that mitigates some weaknesses of both approaches. Specifically, we propose a two-stage training procedure: first train DPO on an easy dataset, and then perform PPO on a difficult set with DPO model being the reference model. Here, the easy (difficult) sets contain samples with completion pairs that are easy (or difficult) to distinguish in terms of human preference. Compared to the vanilla DPO, MPO is less sensitive to noises in preference dataset and less suffer from the distribution shift issue. Compared to vanilla PPO, MPO exploits a better reference model, allowing more effective and stable optimization with a smaller dataset (the small difficult set vs the full set). Experiments are conducted on two public alignment datasets, namely HH-RLHF and TLDR, demonstrating the effectiveness of MPO in terms of reward model-based evaluation, GPT4 and human evaluation.

Currently, there exist two main approaches to LLMs alignment: those that are based on Reinforcement Learning such as RLHF (Reinforcement-Learning with Human Feedbacks)
Stiennon et al. 2020
, and those based on contrastive learning such as DPO
Rafailov et al. 2023
. RLHF has been successfully applied to ChatGPT and contains three main steps: 1) Supervised Finetuning (SFT) LLMs using an instruction-following dataset; 2) Training a reward model that assigns a higher reward for human preferred completions given an instruction; 3) Reinforcement learning using Proximal Preference Optimization (PPO)
Schulman et al. 2017
, of which sampling from the targeted LLMs (for alignment) and labeling with the reward model are two essential components. Recently, contrastive learning based methods (such as DPO) are introduced, replacing the second and third steps of RLHF by directly tuning LLMs on the preference data. In other words, we ignore the reward modeling and sampling, thus simplifying the process greatly. The comparison between RLHF and DPO is demonstrated in Figure
1
, where we skip the SFT stage.

Both RLHF with PPO and DPO have their own disadvantages. On one hand, PPO is complicated, difficult to train and requires intensive memory usage. In order to train PPO more effectively, researchers constrain the search space of LLM by minimizing the KL divergence of the LLM and a reference model (its SFT version). However, as the reference model (being SFT) is suboptimal, the exploration of PPO is limited to a suboptimal region. On the other hand, DPO and other contrastive learning methods suffer from the issue of distribution shift. Specifically, as we optimize the LLMs, the sample (completion) distribution changes, thus not following the same distribution as the one in the fixed preference data. Note that, PPO can avoid this issue by collecting more samples based on the latest policy (see Figure
1
). Additionally, DPO is more susceptible to noises caused by response pairs with similar qualities in the dataset. Although reward model training in RLHF suffers from the same issue, the explicit scores from the reward model allow us to judge if a completion pair (for a given prompt) might be noisy. For instance, Figure
2
(b) shows that more than 50% samples in HH-RLHF dataset exhibit the reward difference of two completions within the range of [0-1], illustrating that this is a common issue. Figure
2
(a) shows that these completion pairs are difficult to be distinguished as the accuracy is significantly lower compared to completion pairs of the other ranges.

With such considerations, we design Mixed Preference Optimization (or MPO) to take the benefits of both worlds, while mitigating some of their disadvantages. Firstly, we conduct a
reward-based data resampling and selection
that exploits a reward model to resample completion pairs based on SFT model, and split the sampled dataset into
\mathcal{D}^{e}
of easy samples and
\mathcal{D}^{h}
of hard samples. Second, we introduce a new curriculum training procedure including 2 training stages: 1) a DPO model is first trained on the easy set to obtain an effective alignment model more quickly; and 2) a PPO model is trained by sampling completions for prompts in the difficult set. During the PPO training phase, we use DPO as the reference model rather than the SFT model as in vanilla PPO, allowing us to train PPO more effectively with less data. Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We show that data quality is essential for both DPO, whereas data quality is correlated to the difference in the reward scores obtained from the reward model in RLHF. We, therefore, develop a simple yet effective data sampling and selection method to effectively train DPO.

•

We propose MPO, which starts from DPO model then trains LLM using PPO. Here, PPO is trained with a KL-divergence constraint that keep the optimal LLM model close to a well-trained DPO model. Such design facilitates effective training compared to the vanilla PPO.

•

The empirical results on two public datasets validate our method effectiveness. Specifically MPO obtain superior performance compared to DPO and PPO accoding to both automatic evaluation methods (reward-based/GPT-based evaluations) and human evaluation.

Related Work

Reinforcement Learning From Human Feedback

Based on PPO
Schulman et al. 2017
,
Ouyang et al. 2022
introduced InstructGPT that utilizes human feedback to train large language models like GPT-3
Mann et al. 2020
, setting the foundation for ChatGPT and GPT-4
Achiam et al. 2023
. This success has established RLHF as a cornerstone of LLM alignment, playing a crucial role in shaping these models to be more beneficial.
Recently, iterative alignment
Touvron et al. 2023b
;
Dubey et al. 2024
;
Wang et al. 2024
is applied to enhance stability during the alignment of LLM with PPO. However, they utilize many new human-labelled preference data at each iteration stage, which is labor-intensive and time-consuming. Our method can make the training process more stable and effective without adding new data. In particular, MPO can be used at each iteration of such process in place of PPO, resulting in more effective models and less computational cost.

Contrastive Learning based Alignment

Figure 3:
MPO architecture, including two parts: reward-based data sampling and selection, and mixed preference optimization.

Several promising methods based on contrastive learning have been introduced for aligning LLMs with human values. DPO
Rafailov et al. 2023
theoretically derives a contrastive learning loss function from RLHF, demonstrating that LLM itself acts as an implicit reward model. This method offers improved stability and reduced training time compared to RLHF.
Yuan et al. 2023
introduces RRHF that directly optimizes the policy model by maximizing the probability difference between chosen and rejected responses. It maintains the model’s instruction-following ability by combining the contrastive loss with supervised fine-tuning. PRO
Song et al. 2023
utilizes list-wise loss, which is an improvement over the point-wise loss used in RRHF, to optimize the likelihood of the partial order of preference data. Calibrated Contrastive Learning
Zhao et al. 2022
;
Zhao et al. 2023
explores various contrastive and regularization losses for optimizing performance. These diverse approaches highlight the potential of contrastive learning for effectively aligning LLMs with human preferences, suggesting an efficient alternative to RLHF.

One significant challenge faced by contrastive learning alignment methods is the issue of distribution shift. Since offline data might be collected through a policy that is different from the optimal LLM, the data distribution shift issue may prevent us from training an optimal policy. SLiC-hf
Zhao et al. 2023
addresses this issue by sample-and-rank, a two-step approach: 1) Sampling a completion from a Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) model; 2) Creating a new preference data where the completion from SFT dataset is the positive response and the sampled completion is negative response. Recently,
Liu et al. 2023
proposed RSO, which directly estimates the data distribution through statistical rejection sampling, leading to improved alignment. Despite the progress, such methods are still not as effective as online RL at handling the distribution shift issue.

MPO vs Previous Studies

Our proposed method, Mixed Preference Optimization (MPO) strategically combines the strengths of DPO and PPO, while trying to mitigate some of their respective limitations. Unlike DPO, MPO can effectively handle the distribution shift issue by resampling. Unlike vanilla PPO, however, MPO exploits the DPO model as a policy model and reference model during online RL stage, enabling more stable and effective online training. As DPO is simple to be trained and PPO of MPO is trained on much less samples, we ensure that the training time of MPO is less than vanilla PPO.

Reward-based Data Re-Sampling and Selection

Reward Modeling

Let
\mathcal{D}=\{(x^{(i)},y^{(i)}_{w},y^{(i)}_{l})\}
denote the preference data. We follow
Rafailov et al. 2023
;
Stiennon et al. 2020
and assume that there exists a latent reward model
r^{*}(x,y)
that assigns higher score for preferred completion
y
. The human preference distribution
p^{*}
can be modeled with Bradley-Terry (BT) model as follows:

p^{*}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}|x)=\frac{\exp{r^{*}(x,y_{1})}}{\exp{r^{*}(x,y_{1})}+\exp{r^{*}(x,y_{2})}}

We can approximate
r^{*}(x,y)
with a (parameterized) reward model
r_{\phi}(x,y)
where
\phi
is the model parameters. Based on the preference dataset
\mathcal{D}
, we can estimate the reward model by minimizing the negative log-likelihood loss as follows:

-E_{(x,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}[\log{\sigma(r_{\phi}(x,y_{w})-r_{\phi}(x,y_{l})}]

3.2
Two Stage Training

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

Following
Rafailov et al. 2023
, we can formalize a maximum likelihood objective for a parameterized policy
\pi_{\theta}
(or the targeted LLM) similar to the reward modeling method but based on the easy set with sampled completions:

-E_{(x,\hat{y}_{w},\hat{y}_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}^{e}}[\log{\sigma(\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,\hat{y}_{w})-\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,\hat{y}_{l})}]

(2)

where
\hat{r}_{\theta}(x,y)=\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(\hat{y}_{w}|x)}{\pi^{SFT}(\hat{y}_{w}|x)}
is the implicit reward defined by the policy model
\pi_{\theta}
, the reference model
\pi^{SFT}
and a constant scale
\beta
. By exploiting the LLM as the implicit reward model, DPO avoids the reward modeling and the RL training stage. As a result, DPO training is simple and converges quickly. Unlike the original DPO, however, MPO only optimizes the policy model with DPO on the easy set
\mathcal{D}^{e}
, of which the completion pairs are resampled from SFT model and judged to be easy to distinguish by the reward model. The reason for such design choice is motivated in introduction and analyzed in depth in Section
4.2
. In the following, we refer to the policy obtained after DPO training as
\pi^{DPO}
.

Proximal Policy Optimization

During the online RL phase, we optimize the policy model
\pi_{\theta}
with the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{\pi_{\theta}}E_{x\sim D^{h},y\sim\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}\{r_{\phi}(x,y)-

\displaystyle\beta\mathbb{D}_{KL}[\pi_{\theta}(y|x)||\pi^{DPO}(y|x)]\}

(3)

where
r_{\phi}(x,y)
is the trained reward model. As online RL samples completion from the current policy (
y\sim\pi_{\theta}(y|x)
) for input prompts
x
, RL training can mitigate the distribution shift issue.

Our RL training phase differs from the one in RLHF
Stiennon et al. 2020
in two aspects. Firstly, the second term in the RL optimization is the KL-divergence between the current policy model and the one obtained from DPO training phase,
\pi^{DPO}
. Additionally, unlike RLHF, we do not search the optimal policy in the trust region around
\pi^{SFT}
, but around
\pi^{DPO}
. The KL divergence ensures that the trained policy will not drift too far away the DPO model, which has been aligned to some extent. Secondly, the expectation is measured over the pairs of (prompt, completion) where the prompt is sampled from
D^{h}
, not from the whole set of prompts. Intuitively, we assume that DPO can effectively help alignment with the easy set, where the completions are clearly distinguished. The exploration in online RL using prompts in the hard set can help reduce the distribution shift and improve alignment with smaller cost (i.e. sample and computational cost) compared to the original PPO.

3.3
Why Mixed Preference Optimization?

Our method is proposed based two main findings: 1) DPO is less effectively trained on the difficult set, which contains difficult to distinguish completion pairs; 2) PPO can be trained more effectively with better reference model, resulting in better performance with less data and (consequently) less computation cost. Both of these findings are from our empirical observations, which can be seen further in section
4.2
. As DPO comes with nice theoretical frameworks
Rafailov et al. 2023
, we can explain the first empirical finding based on the theoretical framework concurrently proposed by
Feng et al. 2024
.

Theorem 1

Feng et al. 2024

We first define two random variables
x_{w}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}\left(y_{w}\mid x\right)}{\pi_{ref}\left(y_{w}\mid x\right)}
and
x_{l}=\frac{\pi_{\theta}\left(y_{l}\mid x\right)}{\pi_{ref}\left(y_{l}\mid x\right)}
, the gradients of DPO with respect to
x_{w}
and
x_{l}
can be calculated as follows
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{DPO}\left(x_{w};x_{l}\right)}{\partial x_{w}}=-\frac{\beta x_{l}^{\beta}}{x_{w}\left(x_{w}^{\beta}+x_{l}^{\beta}\right)}\\
\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{DPO}\left(x_{w};x_{l}\right)}{\partial x_{l}}=\frac{\beta x_{l}^{\beta-1}}{x_{w}^{\beta}+x_{l}^{\beta}}\end{array}\right.

where
\beta
is a hyper-parameter and
\sigma
is the sigmoid function.

Corollary 1

Feng et al. 2024

The ratio
x_{l}/x_{w}
represents the ratio of the increase in the probability of a human-preferred response to the decrease in the probability of a human-dispreferred response
:

\left|\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{DPO}\left(x_{w};x_{l}\right)}{\partial x_{w}}/\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{DPO}\left(x_{w};x_{l}\right)}{\partial x_{l}}\right|=\frac{x_{l}}{x_{w}}

Remark 1

DPO is not effectively trained on the hard set, which contains difficult to distinguish completion pairs.

This remark is the extesion of Remark 2
Feng et al. 2024
. Specifically, as the optimization starts,
x_{w}
tends to increase and
x_{l}
tends to decrease. However, if
y_{w}
is literally similar to
y_{l}
,
\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)
is close to
\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)
, consequently the gradient w.r.t
x_{l}
would counteract the gradient of
x_{w}
, hindering the optimization process
Feng et al. 2024
. Furthermore, when the two completions are similar, it is more likely for human to make annotation error. In other words, the dis-preferred one is mislabeled as preferred, and vice versa. Consequently, the gradient direction is reversed for such pairs, further hindering DPO optimization.

Remark 2

Although the reward model also suffers from noises in the (offline) preference, it is still sufficient to exploit the reward model to judge if a completion pair is difficult to distinguish.

From Equation 1 and Equation 2, we can see that the loss structures for training the reward model and DPO are very similar to each other. However, for reward model, we aim to output a score
r_{\phi}(y|x)\in[0,1]
to indicate the preference level of a completion instead of optimizing LLM for generating preferered responses over disprefered responses. When
y_{w}
is very similar to
y_{l}
, resulting in similar internal representations in the reward model, the reward scores
r_{\phi}(y_{w}|x)
becomes closer to
r_{\phi}(y_{l}|x)
. Here, we apply the similar derivation as Theorem 1 and the reasoning in Remark 1 above. This means that a small reward difference is a good indication of the difficulty level for distinguishing a completion pair. In other words, we can exploit the reward model as a proxy to recognize and remove such pairs, making DPO more effective as suggested in Remark 1. As we can see from Figure
2
in the introduction, the number of pairs with small reward differences ([0-1]) are very common, occupying almost a half of samples in HH-RLHF dataset.

Training Order in MPO

To verify the curriculum learning strategy, we rearrange the two-stage training process of MPO as follows: 1) We first train PPO model on
D^{h}
to obtain
\pi^{PPO}
; 2) We use
\pi^{PPO}
to facilitate the warm-up of the DPO training on
D^{e}
. This model is refereed to as MPO-reverse. The experiment is conducted on HH-RLHF dataset. The results can be seen in Table
6
, where results of PPO and MPO are replicated for cross-reference. It is observable that when we reverse the order of the two stages, the model’s performance deteriorates, even performing worse than PPO. This suggests that curriculum learning plays a crucial role in enhancing the training of MPO. Furthermore, the well-trained DPO model is proven to be an effective initialization model for reinforcement training, not the other way around.

6
Conclusion

This paper investigates the strengths and weaknesses of two common alignment approaches: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Specifically, we analyze the importance of reference models in PPO training, the distribution shift and the influence of data quality in DPO. Inspired from these insights, we propose a simple yet effective alignment method, namely Mixed Preference Optimization (or MPO for short). The main idea is to quickly and effectively train an enhanced DPO as a reference model for PPO. Here, the enhanced DPO model is obtained by mitigating the issues in DPO training. We conducted extensive experiments on two public datasets and the main findings are: 1) By mitigating the distribution shift and noise sensitivity in DPO, MPO significantly outperforms DPO in the alignment performance; 2) By exploiting a better reference model, MPO is easier to train, better in the alignment performance yet requires less samples (consequently, less computation) cost compared to the vanilla PPO. MPO can be used in place of PPO in iterative alignment
Touvron et al. 2023b
for better performance while reducing the sample and computation cost.

Limitations

We do not claim to mitigate all the weaknesses of PPO, and still more computationally expensive compared to DPO. As the training process of MPO containing PPO, it still requires a large amount of computing resources, which is not friendly to researchers with limited training resources. In addition, MPO also requires a reward model, which may cause reward hacking problems.

Ethics Statement

Although our model has undergone an alignment process, it is important to note that, like other large models, there is still a possibility of it generating vulgar language, counterfactual information, or inappropriate content. Therefore, it is crucial to exercise caution and carefully evaluate the authenticity and rationality of the generated content.
