Non-local Optimization: Imposing Structure on Optimization Problems by Relaxation
Nils Müller Tobias Glasmachers Ruhr University Bochum Ruhr University Bochum Germany Institute for Neural Computation Germany mail@nilsmueller.io tobias.glasmachers@ini.rub.de

Abstract
In stochastic optimization, particularly in evolutionary computation and reinforcement learning, the optimization of a function f : Ω → ℝ f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R} is often addressed through optimizing a so-called relaxation θ ∈ Θ ↦ 𝔼 θ ⁡ ( f ) \theta\in\Theta\mapsto\E_{\theta}(f) of f f , where Θ \Theta resembles the parameters of a family of probability measures on Ω \Omega . We investigate the structure of such relaxations by means of measure theory and Fourier analysis, enabling us to shed light on the success of many associated stochastic optimization methods. The main structural traits we derive and that allow fast and reliable optimization of relaxations are the consistency of optimal values of f f , Lipschitzness of gradients, and convexity. We emphasize settings where f f itself is not differentiable or convex, e.g., in the presence of (stochastic) disturbance.

Introduction

The idea of an optimization problem is to find minima (or maxima) of a function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
in a set of possible candidates
\Omega
. Given a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
on
\Omega

{\textstyle\Pr}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
as a generalized candidate of
f
. A generalized candidate
{\textstyle\Pr}
induces a functional to which we assign its expected value
\E(f)
. A parameterization
{\textstyle\Pr_{\cdot}}:\Theta\to\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
of (a subset of) the generalized candidates, where
\Theta
is the parameter set and
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{+}(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
is the set of probability measures on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A})
, results in what is called a

\theta\in\Theta\mapsto\E_{\theta}(f)
.
In this paper, we investigate the central structural properties of relaxed problems that are of interest to optimization: consistency, smoothness, and convexity.

Algorithms.

Our work applies to a broad range of optimization algorithms, including several randomized search heuristics (variants of estimation of distribution methods) for discrete and continuous search spaces. The second half of our paper is heavily influenced by applications in gradient-based optimization, covering in particular information geometric optimization
[
Oll+17

The two most important use-cases in the regime of gradient-based optimization are the following:

•

\Theta

\mathbb{R}^{n}

\theta

{\textstyle\Pr_{\theta}}=k_{\theta}\mu

2
Transforming an Optimization Problem

In this section, we will demonstrate by example, what we will establish in later sections for more general cases and what can be understood as the driving factors for the success of stochastic relaxations. We chose a popular benchmark problem from optimization literature, the Rastrigin function, and multivariate isotropic normal distributions as parameterized probability measures. This setting is typical in stochastic optimization, particularly with evolution strategies. More concretely, we want to illustrate the following traits of this particular stochastic relaxation:

•

Consistency of the minimum of the original problem,

•

Lipschitz continuity of gradients, and

•

convexity.

The above traits will serve to guide our intuition in the subsequent sections.

Example 2.1
.

Consider the Rastrigin function

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\longmapsto f(x):=\left\lVert x\right\rVert^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\cos(\xi_{j}x_{j})\,,

where
\xi_{j},a_{j}>0
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{\leq n}
.
Let
{\textstyle\Pr_{\theta,\sigma}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\theta,\sigma^{2}I)
, where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\sigma>0
. Rastrigin’s function is an example of a highly multi-modal optimization problem. Its global minimum is found at the origin, while an exponentially large (in the problem dimension) number of local minima represent distractors to local optimization methods.

We will now look at the structure of the function

\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\longmapsto\E_{\theta,\sigma}(f)\,,

which we interpret as a relaxation on the original candidate space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
f
. We find the following traits.

•

We can pick a small
\sigma
such that we can approximate the value of
f(x)
with
\E_{x,\sigma}(f)
with arbitrary precision—in particular, at the global minimum of
f
at
0
.

•

Structural traits such as the smoothness of the density of the normal distribution are transferred to
\E_{\cdot}(f)
. Moreover, the gradients of
\theta\mapsto\E_{\theta,\sigma}(f)
are Lipschitz for all
\sigma>0
.

•

As the following arguments will show, we can pick
\sigma^{*}
such that
\theta\mapsto\E_{\theta,\sigma}(f)
is strictly convex for all
\sigma>\sigma^{*}
.

In our specific example we obtain the traits in terms of handy closed-form formulas:

1.

Let
\lambda
denote the Lebesgue measure of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\sigma>0
, we have

\displaystyle\E_{\theta,\sigma}(\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert^{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}^{2}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{n}}}\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\lVert\theta-x\right\rVert^{2}\right)\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)

(definitions)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}x_{k}^{2}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi\sigma^{2})^{n}}}\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left\lVert\theta-x\right\rVert^{2}\right)\lambda(\mathrm{d}x)

(linearity)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}x_{k}^{2}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^{2}}}\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(\theta_{k}-x_{k})^{2}\right)\lambda(\mathrm{d}x_{k})

(Fubini, linearity, and probability measure)

\displaystyle=n\sigma^{2}+\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert^{2}\,.

(Steiner’s Theorem)

2.

Let
j\in\mathbb{N}_{\leq n}
and define
\Xi_{j}:=\xi_{j}e_{j}
, then, using the representation of the characteristic function of the multivariate normal distribution in the third equality, we obtain

\displaystyle\E_{\theta,\sigma}(\cos(\xi_{j}(\cdot)_{j}))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathrm{Re}\left(e^{i\langle\Xi_{j},x\rangle}\right)\,{\textstyle\Pr_{\theta,\sigma}}(\mathrm{d}x)

(definitions)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{i\langle\Xi_{j},x\rangle}\,{\textstyle\Pr_{\theta,\sigma}}(\mathrm{d}x)\bigg)

(definition of the complex integral)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\left(\exp\left(i\langle\Xi_{j},\theta\rangle-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\left\lVert\Xi_{j}\right\rVert^{2}\right)\right)

(characteristic function of the multivariate normal)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\left(\exp(i\xi_{j}\theta_{j})\right)\exp\big(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\xi_{j}^{2}\big)

(definitions and exponential rule)

\displaystyle=\cos(\xi_{j}\theta_{j})\exp\big(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\xi_{j}^{2}\big)\,.

(definition of
\cos
)

Therefore, by linearity of the integral, we have

\displaystyle\E_{\theta,\sigma}(f)

\displaystyle=n\sigma^{2}+\left\lVert\theta\right\rVert^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\cos(\xi_{j}\theta_{j})\exp\big(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\xi_{j}^{2}\big)\,.

Now, we can check strict convexity by looking at the Hessian. The second partial derivatives of the relaxation are given by

\partial_{\theta_{j},\theta_{k}}\E_{\theta,\sigma}(f)=\delta_{jk}\cdot\big(2+a_{j}\xi_{j}^{2}\cos(\xi_{j}\theta_{j})\exp\big(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\xi_{j}^{2}\big)\big)\,,

where
\delta_{jk}
is the Kronecker delta.
Thus, the Hessian is diagonal with strictly positive values at
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if for all
j\in\mathbb{N}_{\leq n}

-2<a_{j}\xi_{j}^{2}\cos(\xi_{j}\theta_{j})\exp\big(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\xi_{j}^{2}\big)\,.

As
\cos{(\xi_{j}\theta_{j})}\geq-1
, we know that this is the case at least if

2>a_{j}\xi_{j}^{2}\exp\big(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\xi_{j}^{2}\big)\iff\tfrac{2}{\xi_{j}^{2}}\log\big(\tfrac{a_{j}\xi_{j}^{2}}{2}\big)<\sigma^{2}.

Picking
{\sigma^{*}}^{2}>\max_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{\leq n}}\tfrac{2}{\xi_{j}^{2}}\log\big(\tfrac{a_{j}\xi_{j}^{2}}{2}\big)
, we obtain the result.

The above result outlines the very structure that can be exploited for optimization by first-order optimization methods
[
Bub15
]
. In some practical settings, a gradient method on the mean parameter
\theta
may be augmented by a manipulation of the standard deviation
\sigma
. However, keeping
\sigma
large improves robustness.

What follows is the general setting used throughout the work. To this end, let
\mathcal{L}^{1}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},{\textstyle\Pr_{\theta}})
denote the at least once Lebesgue integrable, real-valued functions on the measure space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},{\textstyle\Pr_{\theta}})
.

(Stochastic Relaxation)

. In case

3
Consistency in the Codomain

Manipulating an optimization problem raises the question of whether solutions or the cost of solutions will relate to those of the original optimization problem defined by a function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
.

We can observe that some distinguished generalized candidates can be associated with a set of close-to-optimal candidates of
f
by their function value and mass distribution in candidate space. Formally, we define the following.

Definition 3.1

(
f
-consistent)
.

Let
(f,d,\Omega,\mathcal{A},({\textstyle\Pr_{\theta}})_{\theta\in\Theta})
be a relaxation as defined in
Definition

2.1
and let
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
have a unique global minimum at
x^{*}
. We call the relaxation
f
-
consistent
if

\forall x\in\Omega\,,\,\,x\neq x^{*}:\exists\theta^{*}\in\Theta:\,\,\E_{\theta^{*}}(f)<f(x)\,.

We will learn that requiring the Dirac measure at the global optimum of
f
to be a limit candidate of the relaxation in conjunction with a bounding attribute of
f
is sufficient for a relaxation to be
f
-consistent.
A concise way of treating the problem based on measure theory is presented in this section. The results cover a general setting that includes the candidate spaces
\Omega\in\{\mathbb{Z}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
.

Definition 3.2

(

Moreover, probability measures

Lemma 3.1

(Approximation)

(Consistency)
.

(f,d,\Omega,\mathcal{A},({\textstyle\Pr_{\theta}})_{\theta\in\Theta})

Proof.

Pick

(3)

4
Imposing Differentiability

In this section, we derive gradient representations of relaxations. The results in
Theorems

4.2
and
4.2.1
cover cases where the objective function transformed by the relaxation may not be differentiable or no differentiable structure of the candidate space
\Omega
is specified.

First, however, let us look at the simple case where the candidate space
\Omega=\Theta=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the objective function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable, and there is a distinguished probability measure
{\textstyle\Pr}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{B}(\Omega))
. We can construct a family of probability measures by setting

{\textstyle\Pr_{\theta}}(A):={\textstyle\Pr}(A-\theta)

for all
\theta\in\Theta
and for all
A\in\mathcal{A}:=\mathcal{B}(\Omega)
.
This case is critical in practice and particularly interesting due to the following property.

Theorem 4.1

(Preserving Differentiability)
.

Let a relaxation as defined in
Definition

2.1
be given by
(f,\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),({\textstyle\Pr_{\theta}})_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}})
with

•

{\textstyle\Pr_{\theta}}(A)={\textstyle\Pr_{0}}(A-\theta)
for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

•

f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable, and

•

there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mapsto\max\left\lvert(\partial_{r}f)(U_{\varepsilon}(x))\right\rvert
has a
{\textstyle\Pr_{0}}
-integrable upper bound for all
r\in\mathbb{N}_{\leq n}
.

We have
\nabla_{\theta}\E_{\cdot}(f)\equiv\E_{\cdot}(\nabla_{x}f)
. If
\nabla_{x}f
is Lipschitz/uniformly continuous, then
\nabla_{\theta}\E_{\cdot}(f)
is as well.

Remark 4.1
.

The third assumption deviates from the standard assumption of Leibniz’s integral rule, as we have to restrict the parameter set to some neighborhood around any parameter to obtain a bound that fits Leibniz’s integral rule. As differentiability is a local property, this still suffices for the statement of the theorem.

Proof.

Let
\psi(\theta,x):=\psi_{\theta}(x):=x+\theta
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f\,\mathrm{d}{\textstyle\Pr_{\theta}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\textstyle\Pr_{\theta}}(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(x)>t\})\,\mathrm{d}t

(definition Lebesgue integral)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\textstyle\Pr_{0}}\big(\psi_{\theta}^{-1}(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid f(x)>t\})\big)\,\mathrm{d}t

(assumption)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\textstyle\Pr_{0}}(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid(f\circ\psi_{\theta})(x)>t\})\,\mathrm{d}t

(definition inverse)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}f\circ\psi_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}{\textstyle\Pr_{0}}\,.

(definition Lebesgue integral)

Due to the last assumption, we can apply Leibniz’s integral rule
[
Sch05
, p. 91, Theorem 11.5]
, and get

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}\int_{\Omega}f\circ\psi_{\theta}\,\mathrm{d}{\textstyle\Pr_{0}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(\nabla_{\theta}f\circ\psi)(\theta,x)\,{\textstyle\Pr_{0}}(\mathrm{d}x)

(Leibniz integral rule)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(\nabla_{x}f)(x+\theta)\,{\textstyle\Pr_{0}}(\mathrm{d}x)

(definition
\psi

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(\nabla_{x}f)\,\mathrm{d}{\textstyle\Pr_{\theta}}\,.

(above equation)

Combining the results, we obtain for all

, that

\nabla_{\theta}\E_{\theta}(f)=\E_{\theta}(\nabla_{x}f)\,.

In case the gradient of

is also Lipschitz with constant

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{\theta}\E_{\theta^{\prime}}(f)-\nabla_{\theta}\E_{\theta}(f)\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\left\lVert(\nabla_{x}f)(x+\theta^{\prime})-(\nabla_{x}f)(x+\theta)\right\rVert\,{\textstyle\Pr_{0}}(\mathrm{d}x)

(above derivation)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}L\left\lVert\theta^{\prime}-\theta\right\rVert\,{\textstyle\Pr_{0}}(\mathrm{d}x)

(Lipschitzness of
f

\displaystyle=L\left\lVert\theta^{\prime}-\theta\right\rVert\,.

(Lebesgue integral & probability measure)

By an analogous derivation, we obtain continuity of

∎

We will now look again at the abstract, general setting for relaxations. A transfer of smoothness from the probability measures

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(f,\Omega,\mathcal{A},({\textstyle\Pr_{\theta}})_{\theta\in\Theta})
be a relaxation as defined in

be a function for some open

(Partial Differentiability)

If

•

of

w.r.t.

•

for all

exist, and

for all

then the partial derivatives of

\partial_{r}\E_{\gamma(t)}(f)=\int_{\Omega}f\partial_{r}k_{\gamma(t)}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,.

(Continuous Differentiability)

If in addtion, for all

and for all

is continuous, then

(Lipschitz Differentiability)

If in addition, for all

and for all
x\in\Omega
, there exists

s.t. for all

\displaystyle\left\lvert\partial_{r}k_{\gamma(t^{\prime})}(x)-\partial_{r}k_{\gamma(t)}(x)\right\rvert\leq L_{r}(x)\left\lVert t^{\prime}-t\right\rVert\quad\text{and}\quad fL_{r}\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)\,,

then
t\in U\mapsto\nabla\E_{\gamma(t)}(f)
is Lipschitz with constant

\sum_{r=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert f\right\rvert L_{r}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,.

Proof.

(Partial Differentiability)

To obtain the first result, we apply Leibniz’s integral rule
[
Sch05
, p. 91, Theorem 11.5]
. The requirements are fulfilled as per the following arguments.

(a)

For all
t\in U
, we have

x\in\Omega\mapsto f(x)k_{\gamma(t)}(x)\in\mathcal{L}^{1}(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mu)

as

and
k_{\gamma(t)}\mu={\textstyle\Pr_{\gamma(t)}}

\mu
-almost everywhere.

(b)

For all
x\in\Omega
, the function
t\in U\mapsto f(x)k_{\gamma(t)}(x)
is partially differentiable as
t\in U\mapsto k_{\gamma(t)}(x)
is partially differentiable.

(c)

In each coordinate this requirement is directly fulfilled by our initial assumptions.

As
U
is open in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we can apply Leibniz’s integral rule to each coordinate direction.

(Continuous Differentiability)

To obtain the second result, we apply the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, e.g., see
[
Sch05
, p. 89]
, to an arbitrary convergent sequence
(t_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\to t\in U
. Clearly, for all

and for all

\displaystyle\partial_{r}k_{\gamma(t_{j})}(x)\xrightarrow{j\to\infty}\partial_{r}k_{\gamma(t)}(x)\quad\implies\quad f(x)\partial_{r}k_{\gamma(t_{j})}(x)\xrightarrow{j\to\infty}f(x)\partial_{r}k_{\gamma(t)}(x)

\displaystyle\,.

The left side of the above statement is fulfilled by our additional continuity assumption. The boundedness requirement is fulfilled by the assumption of the first statement. Therefore, the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem tells us for all

\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}f\partial_{r}k_{\gamma(t_{j})}\,\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\Omega}f\partial_{r}k_{\gamma(t)}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\,.

Combined with the first result we get that for all

i.e., continuity of the partial differentials. This implies continuity of the differential.

(Lipschitz Differentiability)

To obtain the third result, we observe that for all
r\in\mathbb{N}_{\leq n}
, for all
x\in\Omega
, and for all

\displaystyle\left\lvert\partial_{r}k_{\gamma(t^{\prime})}(x)-\partial_{r}k_{\gamma(t)}(x)\right\rvert\leq L_{r}(x)\left\lVert t^{\prime}-t\right\rVert

\displaystyle\implies

\displaystyle\left\lvert f(x)\partial_{r}k_{\gamma(t^{\prime})}(x)-f(x)\partial_{r}k_{\gamma(t)}(x)\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert f(x)\right\rvert L_{r}(x)\left\lVert t^{\prime}-t\right\rVert

\displaystyle\,.

The left side of the above statement is fulfilled by our additional Lipschitz assumption. Using the result of the first statement and the triangle inequality this implies that for all

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla\E_{\gamma(t^{\prime})}(f)-\nabla\E_{\gamma(t)}(f)\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\int_{\Omega}f\nabla k_{\gamma(t^{\prime})}-f\nabla k_{\gamma(t)}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\left\lVert f\nabla k_{\gamma(t^{\prime})}-f\nabla k_{\gamma(t)}\right\rVert\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\left\lvert f\partial_{r}k_{\gamma(t^{\prime})}-f\partial_{r}k_{\gamma(t)}\right\rvert\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\left\lvert f\right\rvert L_{r}\left\lVert t^{\prime}-t\right\rVert\,\mathrm{d}\mu

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{r=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert f\right\rvert L_{r}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\bigg)\left\lVert t^{\prime}-t\right\rVert\,.

5
Weak Convexity

The notion of convexity of a function is central to continuous optimization. Non-convex optimization problems are much harder to solve in general. However, for families of translated probability measures—including multivariate normal distributions—we show that a function’s convexity is preserved in its relaxation. More importantly, we provide conditions under which non-convex functions result in convex stochastic relaxations. To this end, consider the following class of functions
f

(Preserving Convexity)

6
Conclusion

We established that stochastic relaxations have many favorable properties for optimization in surprisingly general settings. Concretely, we provided mild conditions under which optima are persistent under stochastic relaxation and thereby established the approach’s consistency, which aligns with our intuition. Next to consistency, we have also shown that probability measures with Lipschitz differentiable densities under mild regularity conditions result in relaxations with Lipschitz differentials, allowing favorable convergence properties for first-order optimization methods. However, the arguably most important contributions are the convexity conditions for stochastic relaxations derived in the last section, which offer the possibility of principled global optimization under high-frequency, even stochastic, disturbances. Beyond that, we hope that the insight developed will be useful in furthering state-of-the-art optimization methods.

Future Work.

We believe that some of the most promising directions for future work lie in extending the convexity results for stochastic relaxations based on other methods from harmonic analysis. In practice, particularly in evolution strategies, the decoupling of the relaxation and (numerical) integration methods suggested by the setting at hand could offer the opportunity to design many new optimization methods. The gradient representation of relaxations of differentiable functions likely has powerful applications in problems where gradients are available, such as in gray-box problems in machine learning. Whether convexity results for relaxations hold for probability measures with a support on Lebesgue null sets may also pose an interesting question. These measures can offer strongly reduced computational cost in numerical integration—related algorithms have recently yielded spectacular results
[
LGB19
,
Cho+19
]
. Connecting the convexity results with existing approaches to control the scaling of probability measures during an optimization process is also an important next step.
