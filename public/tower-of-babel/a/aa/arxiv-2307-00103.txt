Gaussian fluctuations for the wave equation under rough random perturbations
Raluca M. Balan, Jingyu Huang, Xiong Wang, Panqiu Xia, Wangjun Yuan

Abstract
In this article, we consider the stochastic wave equation in spatial dimension d = 1 d=1 , with linear term σ ⁡ ( u ) = u \sigma(u)=u multiplying the noise. This equation is driven by a Gaussian noise which is white in time and fractional in space with Hurst index H ∈ ( 1 4 , 1 2 ) H\in(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2}) . First, we prove that the solution is strictly stationary and ergodic in the spatial variable. Then, we show that with proper normalization and centering, the spatial average of the solution converges to the standard normal distribution, and we estimate the rate of this convergence in the total variation distance. We also prove the corresponding functional convergence result.

Introduction

The study of stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) is an active research area in stochastic analysis which has been growing steadily in the last 40 years, using two approaches: the semigroup approach developed by Da Prato and Zabczyk in
[
24
]
, and the random field approach initiated in Walsh’s lecture notes
[
61
]
. The two approaches rely on different infinite-dimensional extensions of Itô’s martingale theory of stochastic integration, and lead to different concepts of solutions. A comparison between the two approaches can be found in
[
22
]
. Classical equations which have been studied using the random field approach are: the stochastic heat equation (SHE), and the stochastic wave equation (SWE). When these equations are perturbed by a space-time Gaussian white noise, random field solutions exist only in spatial dimension
d=1
. A systematic study of SPDEs in higher dimensions was initiated by Dalang in the seminal article
[
20
]
, by considering a spatially-homogeneous Gaussian noise with spatial covariance given by a non-negative-definite function
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
. A typical example is the Riesz kernel
\gamma(x)=|x|^{-\beta}
with
\beta\in(0,d)
. The case
\gamma=\delta_{0}
, where
\delta_{0}
is the Dirac distribution at
0
, corresponds formally to the white noise in space. Subsequent investigations revealed that the solutions to these equations have many interesting properties, such as: intermittency
[
29
]
, Hölder continuity
[
23
]
, strict positivity
[
17
]
, dense blow-up
[
13
]
, to mention just a few. Recent investigations focus on relaxing the conditions on the coefficients of the equation, as for instance in
[
21
,
30
,
58
]
.

In the 1990’s,
fractional Brownian motion
(fBm) became a popular model for the noise in various problems in stochastic analysis. Recall that a fBm is a zero-mean Gaussian process
(B_{x}^{(H)})_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
with covariance

\mathbb{E}[B_{x}^{(H)}B_{y}^{(H)}]=\frac{1}{2}(|x|^{2H}+|y|^{2H}-|x-y|^{2H})\eqqcolon R_{H}(x,y),

where
H\in(0,1)
is the Hurst index. If
H=1/2
, the fBm is a Brownian motion. The paths of fBm are Hölder continuous of order less than
H
, and hence, are smoother or rougher than the Brownian paths, depending on whether
H>1/2
or
H<1/2
. In the “regular” case
H>1/2
, the covariance of fBm can be written as:

R_{H}(x,y)=H(2H-1)\int_{0}^{x}\int_{0}^{y}|u-v|^{2H-2}dudv,

(1.1)

using the Riesz kernel
\gamma(x)=H(2H-1)|x|^{2H-2}
, which is the second derivative
(|x|^{2H})^{\prime\prime}
in the sense of distributions. In the “rough” case
H<1/2
, (
1.1
) does not hold since
(|x|^{2H})^{\prime\prime}
is not a function. In this case, it is useful to work with the spectral representation:

R_{H}(x,y)=c_{H}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{F}1_{[0,x]}(\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}1_{[0,y]}(\xi)}|\xi|^{1-2H}d\xi,

(1.2)

where
c_{H}=\frac{\Gamma(2H+1)\sin(\pi H)}{2\pi}
and
\mathcal{F}\varphi(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-i\xi x}\varphi(x)dx
is the Fourier transform.

The fBm is not a semi-martingale, and Itô calculus cannot be used. Two methods were proposed for developing stochastic analysis with respect to fBm, using either (i) Malliavin calculus, or (ii) pathwise integration (which exploits the Hölder continuity of the paths). Pioneer works in this direction are:
[
1
,
25
,
52
,
62
]
. Method (i) is relevant for the present article, and can be explained briefly as follows: we endow the space
\mathcal{E}
of linear combinations of indicator functions of the form
1_{[0,x]}
with the inner product
\langle 1_{[0,x]},1_{[0,y]}\rangle_{\mathcal{P}_{0}}=R_{H}(x,y)
, so that the map
1_{[0,x]}\mapsto B_{x}^{(H)}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
becomes an isometry; then, the closure
\mathcal{P}_{0}
of
\mathcal{E}
is the domain of the Wiener integral with respect to
B^{(H)}
. In
[
42
]
, Jolis proved that
\mathcal{P}_{0}
coincides with the fractional Sobolev space
W^{\frac{1}{2}-H,2}(\mathbb{R})
, and therefore it is a space of distributions if
H>1/2
, and a space of functions if
H<1/2
. An alternative representation in
[
27
]
for the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{P}_{0}}
was obtained in the case
H<1/2
, namely:

\|\varphi\|_{\mathcal{P}_{0}}^{2}=C_{H}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|^{2}|x-y|^{2H-2}dxdy,

(1.3)

where
C_{H}=\frac{H(1-2H)}{2}
. Relation (
1.3
) is called the
Gagliardo representation
and is very useful for problems in stochastic analysis, and in particular for the present article.

SPDEs with colored noise in time have been considered for the first time in
[
51
]
. Since then, this area has been growing steadily. However, the basic question of existence of solutions to (SHE) or (SWE) with colored (or fractional) noise in time is still an open problem in the case when the noise is multiplied by a Lipschitz function
\sigma(u)
of the solution. The only case when it is known that these equations have unique solutions is the linear case,
\sigma(u)=u
. This case, which is known in the literature as the
parabolic Anderson model
(PAM) for the heat equation, respectively the
hyperbolic Anderson model
(HAM) for the wave equation, is studied using tools from Malliavin calculus, since the solution has an explicit Wiener chaos expansion.
This method was initiated by Hu and Nualart in
[
36
]
. Various properties of the solution have been developed in
[
18
,
32
,
37
]
for (PAM), respectively
[
4
,
5
]
for (HAM), to name just a few of the recent references.

In the present article, we consider the (HAM) driven by a Gaussian noise
\dot{W}
which is white in time and fractional in space with Hurst index
H\in(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2})
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}(t,x)=\dfrac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x)+u(t,x)\dot{W}(t,x),&(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R},\\
u(0,x)=1,\ \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t}(0,x)=0,&\forall x\in\mathbb{R}.\end{cases}

(1.4)

Formally,
\dot{W}
is a zero-mean Gaussian noise with covariance

\mathbb{E}[\dot{W}(t,x)\dot{W}(s,y)]=\delta_{0}(t-s)\gamma(x-y),\quad\mbox{where}\quad\gamma(x)=(|x|^{2H-2})^{\prime\prime}.

Rigorously,
W=\{W(\varphi);\varphi\in\mathcal{D}\}
is a zero-mean Gaussian process defined on a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
and indexed by the set
\mathcal{D}
of infinitely differentiable functions on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
, with compact support. The covariance of
W
is inspired by (
1.2
) (in which we replace
1_{[0,x]}
and
1_{[0,y]}
by smooth functions), and is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W(\varphi)W(\psi)]=c_{H}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathcal{F}\varphi(t,\cdot)(\xi)\overline{\mathcal{F}\psi(t,\cdot)(\xi)}|\xi|^{1-2H}d\xi dt\eqqcolon\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}.

(1.5)

We denote by
\mathcal{H}
the Hilbert space defined as the completion of
\mathcal{D}
with respect to the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
. Then the map
\mathcal{D}\ni\varphi\mapsto W(\varphi)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
becomes an isometry which can be extended to
\mathcal{H}
.
The process
\{W(\varphi);\varphi\in\mathcal{H}\}
is an isonormal Gaussian process, as defined in Malliavin calculus (see
[
50
]
), and
\mathcal{H}
is isomorphic to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathcal{P}_{0})
.
Indicator functions of the form
1_{[0,t]\times[0,x]}
lie in
\mathcal{H}
and the process
\{W_{t}(x)=W(1_{[0,t]\times[0,x]})\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
has the same distribution as
\sqrt{t}B^{(H)}
, since its covariance matches (
1.2
).

A predictable process
u=\{u(t,x);t\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}\}
is a (mild)
solution
to equation (
1.4
) if it satisfies the following integral equation:

u(t,x)=1+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)u(s,y)W(ds,dy),

(1.6)

where
G_{t}
is the fundamental solution to the deterministic wave equation on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}
:

G_{t}(x)\coloneqq\dfrac{1}{2}1_{\{|x|<t\}}.

(1.7)

The integral on the right-hand side of (
1.6
) is an Itô integral, which coincides with the Skorohod integral, as observed in
[
3
]
.

The existence and weak intermittency of the solution to (
1.4
) was proved in
[
2
]
, respectively
[
3
]
. In
[
45
]
, the existence of solution was studied in a more general scenario, where
u
is replaced by a Lipschitz function
\sigma(u)
.
The existence of the solution to (PAM) with the same noise
\dot{W}
as above was obtained in
[
34
]
, while the exact asymptotic behaviour of its moments was established in
[
39
]
. The existence and Hölder continuity of the solution to (PAM) with space-time fractional noise of indices
H_{0}>1/2
in time and
H<1/2
in space was obtained in
[
35
]
, under the condition
H_{0}+H>3/4
. The same problem for (HAM) was studied in
[
59
]
under the assumption
H\in(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{2})
. The (SHE) with the same noise and a general Lipschitz function
\sigma(u)
multiplying the noise was studied in
[
33
]
, under the restriction
\sigma(0)=0
; this condition was later removed in
[
38
]
.

In all these references, the noise is spatially-homogeneous, i.e. it is invariant under translations. This property is transmitted to the solution
u
in the form of strict stationarity of the process
\{u(t,x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
. Without considerable effort, it is possible to prove that this process is also ergodic. The spatial ergodicity of the solution to an SPDE was proved for the first time in
[
14
]
for the SHE with spatially homogeneous Gaussian noise (white noise in time), and a Lipshitz function
\sigma(u)
multiplying the noise.

In the recent years, there has been a lot of interest in examining the asymptotic behaviour of the spatial average:

F_{R}(t)=\int_{-R}^{R}\big(u(t,x)-1\big)dx.

Since
\{u(t,x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
is strictly stationary and ergodic, by Brirkoff and von Neumann mean ergodic theorem,
the following law of large numbers holds:

\frac{F_{R}(t)}{R}\to 0\quad\mbox{a.s. and in $L^{2}(\Omega)$},\quad\mbox{as $R\to\infty$}.

A natural question is to investigate if
F_{R}(t)
satisfies also a central limit theorem. For this, a novel technique was initiated in
[
40
]
, which
combines Stein’s method for normal approximation with tools from Malliavin calculus. This method was originally developed for (SHE) with space-time white noise, and has been rapidly extended to other models. The paramount result is the
Quantitative Central Limit Theorem
(QCLT), which gives an estimate for the total variation distance
d_{TV}
between
F_{R}(t)/\sigma_{R}(t)
and a standard normal random variable
Z
, as a quantifier for the speed of convergence in distribution, when
R\to\infty
.
We recall that the total variation distance between random variables
X
and
Y
is given by:

d_{\rm TV}(X,Y)=\sup_{B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})}|\mathbb{P}(X\in B)-\mathbb{P}(Y\in B)|,

where
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
stands for the collections of all Borel subsets of
\mathbb{R}
. The QCLT is closely related to a study of the order of magnitude of
\sigma_{R}^{2}(t)
, and can be extended to functional convergence.

[

gives the CLT for a function

of the spatial average of the solution to (SHE), while references
[

,

]
studied the QCLT problem for a normalized version of the solution to (PAM) with delta initial condition. In the case of the (SWE) with white noise in time, the spatial ergodicity of the solution was proved in
[

In this paper, we study the Open Problem 1 mentioned above, in the case
\sigma(u)=u
. We first show that the solution is strictly stationary and ergodic in the space variable, then we prove that
\sigma_{R}^{2}\sim R
. The major effort is dedicated to the proof of QCLT. For this, we use the same method as in
[
54
]
for (PAM), which relies on a second-order Poincaré inequality (Proposition 2.4 of
[
54
]
). Due to the Gagliardo representation (
1.3
) of the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{P}_{0}}
, we encounter the problem of estimating the fourth moments of the increments of the Malliavin derivative
Du(t,x)
, and of the rectangular increments of the second Malliavin derivative
D^{2}u(t,x)
. In the case of (PAM), these estimates are obtained in
[
54
]
using highly non-trivial methods, that cannot be applied for (HAM).

Our method is simpler than that of
[
54
]
, and relies on the following key relation

D_{r,z}u(t,x)=u(r,z)v^{(r,z)}(t,x),

(1.8)

between
Du
and the solution
v=v^{(r,z)}
of the integral equation (of Volterra type):

v(t,x)=G_{t-r}(x-z)+\int_{r}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-s}(x-y)v(s,y)W(ds,dy),\quad t\geq r,x\in\mathbb{R}.

(1.9)

Relation (
1.8
) is valid for the solution
u
of any SPDE with white noise in time, constant initial condition, and linear function
\sigma(u)=u
multiplying the noise. But it may not hold for equations with colored noise in time, or time-independent noise. (Nevertheless, in the case of (HAM) with time-independent noise, it is still possible to derive an indirect argument relating
Du
and
v
which leads to estimates for the moments of
Du
and its increments; see
[
8
, Theorem 4.1]
.) Key properties of
v^{(r,z)}
, which lie at the core of our methods, are:

(i) the moments of
v^{(r,z)}(t,x)
are uniformly bounded in
(r,z,t,x)
(see
[
8
, Example B.2]
);

(ii)
v^{(r,z)}
satisfies the following relation (see the proof of Lemma
2.4
below):

\displaystyle v^{(r,z)}(t,x)=2G_{t-r}(x-z)v^{(r,z)}(t,x);

(1.10)

(iii) the increments of
v^{(r,z)}
have translation-invariance properties (see Lemma
2.5
below).

Properties (i) and (ii) may not hold for other models, such as (PAM); see
[
8
, Remark B.3]
.

We are now ready to state the main results of the present article.

Theorem 1.1

(Spatial ergodicity)
.

For any
t>0
, the process
\{u(t,x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
is strictly stationary and ergodic.

Theorem 1.2

(Limiting covariance)
.

For any
t>0
and
s>0

\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[F_{R}(t)F_{R}(s)]}{R}=K(t\wedge s),

(1.11)

where

\displaystyle K(t)=4\pi\sum_{n\geq 2}C_{H}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{\sin^{2}((t_{j+1}-t_{j})|\eta_{j}|)}{|\eta_{j}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}|\eta_{j}-\eta_{j-1}|^{1-2H}|\eta_{n-1}|^{1-2H}d\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n-1}d\boldsymbol{t}_{n},

with
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n-1}=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{n-1})
,
\boldsymbol{t}_{n}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
and by convention
\eta_{0}=0
. In particular,
\sigma_{R}^{2}(t)/R\to K(t)>0
as
R\to\infty
.

Theorem 1.3

(QCLT)
.

For any
t>0
,

d_{\rm TV}\left(\frac{F_{R}(t)}{\sigma_{R}(t)},Z\right)\leq C_{t,H}^{(2)}R^{-1/2},

where
C_{t,H}^{(2)}>0
is a constant depending on
t
and
H
.

Theorem 1.4

(Functional CLT)
.

The process
\{R^{-1/2}F_{R}(t);t\geq 0\}
converges in distribution as
R\to\infty
, to a zero-mean Gaussian process
\{\mathcal{G}(t);t\geq 0\}
with covariance

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(t)\mathcal{G}(s)]=K(t\wedge s).

Theorem
1.1
is derived from an ergodicity criterion credited to Maruyama
[
46
]
. By combining this criterion with the Gaussian-Poincaré inequality (see e.g.,
[
31
,
47
]
), the spatial ergodicity of the solution to (SHE) was investigated in
[
14
]
. For the solution to (SWE), the spatial ergodicity in dimensions
d\leq 3
was proved in
[
56
]
. However, in the paper
[
56
]
, the spatial correlation function has to be a function (or a measure), rendering the result inapplicable in our case, when the correlation is a distribution. Consequently, new methods must be employed to establish the validity of Theorem
1.1
.

The proof of Theorem
1.3
is based on the second-order Gaussian-Poincaré inequality (referenced as in e.g.,
[
12
,
49
,
60
]
) following the strategy initially discovered in
[
4
]
, and further applied/developed in
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
54
]
. It is important to note that several estimates for (PAM) with rough noise in space obtained in
[
54
]
are precise but not essential for achieving the desired goal. Those estimates unnecessarily increase complexity and cannot be applied to (HAM). By thoroughly analyzing the implementation of the second-order Gaussian-Poincaré inequality in the spatial average of SPDE’s, certain unnecessary computations were successfully eliminated in
[
8
]
. This reduction in complexity significantly streamlined the proof and marked the final stage of the progress made in this paper.

This article is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we include the preliminary results, leading to the estimates for Malliavin derivatives of the solution, which are essential for our developments. Section
3
contains the proofs of Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
,
1.3
and
1.4
.

\|\cdot\|_{p}
the
L^{p}(\Omega)
-norm of a random variable on the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. For any positive integer
n
, we make use of the notation
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\coloneqq(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
for an element in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and for any

,

stands for a subset of

T_{n}(t)\coloneqq\{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n});0<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}<t\}
.

2
Preliminary estimates

In this section, we present an overview of (HAM) driven by noise
W
as above, including relevant preliminaries. We reference existing literature for certain results, while also introducing a few novel estimates supported by detailed proofs.

2.1
Properties of the solution

In this section, we include some properties of the solution that will be needed in the sequel, including the spatial strict stationarity mentioned in Theorem
1.1
.

As discussed in
[
59
]
, the solution to equation (
1.4
) has the Wiener chaos expansion:

u(t,x)=1+\sum_{n\geq 1}I_{n}(f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)),

where
I_{n}
is the multiple integral with respect to
W
and the kernel
f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)
is given by:

\displaystyle f_{n}(\boldsymbol{t}_{n},\boldsymbol{x}_{n},t,x)=

\displaystyle f_{n}(t_{1},\dots,t_{n},x_{1},\dots,x_{n},t,x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle G_{t-t_{n}}(x-x_{n})\times\dots\times G_{t_{2}-t_{1}}(x_{2}-x_{1})\mathbf{1}_{T_{n}(t)}(\boldsymbol{t}_{n})

In this case,

\mathbb{E}|u(t,x)|^{2}=\sum_{n\geq 1}n!\|\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}^{\otimes n}}^{2},

where
\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,t,x)
is the symmetrization of
f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)
:

\widetilde{f}_{n}(\boldsymbol{t}_{n},\boldsymbol{x}_{n},t,x)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\rho\in S_{n}}f_{n}(t_{\rho(1)},\ldots,t_{\rho(n)},x_{\rho(1)},\ldots,x_{\rho(n)},t,x),

with
S_{n}
denoting the set of all permutations on
\{1,\dots,n\}
.

The following result was proved in
[
2
,
3
]
; see
[
2
, Theorem 3.9]
or
[
3
, Theorem 3.5]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any
H\in(1/4,1/2)
, equation (
1.4
) has a unique solution. Moreover, for any
p\geq 2
and
t\geq 0
,

\sup_{(r,x)\in[0,t]\times\mathbb{R}}\|u(r,x)\|_{p}<C,

(2.3)

where
C>0
is a constant that depends on
(t,p,H)
.

The following result gives the spatial stationarity of
u
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For any
t>0
, the process
\{u(t,x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
is strictly stationary. In particular, for any
x,h\in\mathbb{R}
,

u(t,x)-u(t,x+h)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}u(t,0)-u(t,h).

Proof.

We show that for any
m\geq 1
and for any
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m},h\in\mathbb{R}

\big(u(t,z_{1}),\ldots,u(t,z_{m})\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\big(u(t,z_{1}+h),\ldots,u(t,z_{m}+h)\big).

For any
j=1,\ldots,m
, the variable
u(t,z_{j}+h)
has the chaos expansion:

\displaystyle u(t,z_{j}+h)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\int_{T_{n}(t)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}

\displaystyle G_{t-t_{n}}(z_{j}+h-x_{n})\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}G_{t_{i+1}-t_{i}}(x_{i+1}-x_{i})

\displaystyle\times W(dt_{1},dx_{1})\ldots W(dt_{n},dx_{n}),

(2.4)

where
T_{n}(t)=\{0<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}<t\}
.
Let
W^{(h)}=\{W^{(h)}(\varphi);\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R})\}
be the shifted noise, where

W^{(h)}(\varphi)=W(\varphi(\cdot-h))
. Performing the formal change of variables
y_{i}=x_{i}-h
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, we see that the multiple integral above is equal to

\displaystyle\int_{T_{n}(t)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}G_{t-t_{n}}(z_{j}-y_{n})\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}G_{t_{i+1}-t_{i}}(y_{i+1}-y_{i})W^{(h)}(dt_{1},dy_{1})\ldots W^{(h)}(dt_{n},dy_{n}).

Since
W
is spatially homogeneous,
W^{(h)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}W
. Therefore, the vector of dimension
m
whose
j
-th component is given by the series (
2.1
) has the same distribution as the vector whose
j
-th component is
u(t,z_{j})
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

For any
t>0
and
p\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big\|u(s,0)-u(s,h)\big\|_{p}^{2}\,|h|^{2H-2}dh\leq C,

where
C>0
is a constant that depends on
(t,H,p)
.

Proof.

Denote by
C
a constant that depends on
(t,H,p)
and may be different in each of its appearances.
Using the uniform bound for
\|u(s,x)\|_{p}
given by (
2.3
), we have:

\int_{|h|>1}\|u(s,0)-u(s,h)\|_{p}|h|^{2H-2}dh\leq C\int_{|h|>1}|h|^{2H-2}dh<\infty.

It remains to treat the integral over the set
|h|\leq 1
.
By using the chaos expansion (
2.1
) for
u(t,0)-u(t,x)
,
and hypercontractivity (see e.g.,
[
48
, Corollary 2.8.14]
), for any
p\geq 2
,

\|u(s,0)-u(s,h)\|_{p}\leq\sum_{n\geq 1}(p-1)^{n/2}\|I_{n}({f}_{n,h}(\cdot,0,s))\|_{2}=\sum_{n\geq 1}(p-1)^{n/2}[J_{n,h}(s)]^{1/2}

(2.5)

where
J_{n,h}(s)\coloneqq n!\|{f}_{n,h}(\cdot,0,s)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}^{2}
.
It follows from (
1.5
) that

\displaystyle J_{n,h}(s)

\displaystyle=c_{H}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(s)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|\mathcal{F}f_{n,h}(\boldsymbol{t}_{n},\cdot,s,x)(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\big|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\xi_{j}|^{1-2H}d\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}

\displaystyle=c_{H}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|1-e^{-i(\xi_{1}+\dots+\xi_{n})h}\big|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\big|\mathcal{F}G_{t_{j+1}-t_{j}}(\xi_{1}+\dots+\xi_{j})\big|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\xi_{j}|^{1-2H}d\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}

\displaystyle=c_{H}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|1-e^{-i\eta_{n}h}\big|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\big|\mathcal{F}G_{t_{j+1}-t_{j}}(\eta_{j})\big|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\eta_{j}-\eta_{j-1}|^{1-2H}d\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}d\boldsymbol{t}_{n},

where by convention
t_{n+1}=s
and
\eta_{0}=0
.
Taking account of the (in)equalities
|a+b|^{1-2H}\leq|a|^{1-2H}+|b|^{1-2H}
for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
, and

x_{1}\prod_{j=2}^{n}(x_{j}+x_{j-1})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}\in A_{n}}x_{j}^{\alpha_{j}},\quad\text{for all }x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

where
A_{n}
is a set of multi-indices
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
such that
\alpha_{1}\in\{1,2\}
,
\alpha_{n}\in\{0,1\}
,
\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n-1}\in\{0,1,2\}
,
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}=n
and
\alpha_{j}+\alpha_{j-1}\in\{1,2,3\}
for any
j=1,\ldots,n-1
. Then the cardinality
{\rm card}(A_{n})=2^{n-1}
(see
[
59
, Page 7]
). Hence

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\eta_{j}-\eta_{j-1}|^{1-2H}\leq\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}\in D_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\eta_{j}|^{\alpha_{j}},

(2.6)

where
D_{n}
is a subset of
A_{n}
fulfilling
\alpha_{j}=(1-2H)\alpha_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,n
.
It follows that

\displaystyle J_{n,h}(s)\leq c_{H}^{n}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}\in D_{n}}\int_{T_{n}(t)}

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\mathcal{F}G_{t_{j+1}-t_{j}}(\eta_{j})||\eta_{j}|^{\alpha_{j}}d\eta_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\times\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|1-e^{-i\eta_{n}h}|^{2}|\mathcal{F}G_{t-t_{n}}(\eta_{n})|^{2}|\eta_{n}|^{\alpha_{n}}d\eta_{n}\right)d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}

As a result of identity (
2.1
) and the fact that
|\eta_{n}|^{\alpha_{n}}\leq 1+|\eta_{n}|^{1-2H}
(since
\alpha_{n}\in\{0,1-2H\}
),

\displaystyle J_{n,h}(s)

\displaystyle\leq C^{n}\sum_{\alpha\in A_{n}}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|1-e^{-i\eta_{n}h}|^{2}|\mathcal{F}G_{t-t_{n}}(\eta_{n})|^{2}(1+|\eta_{n}|^{1-2H})d\eta_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left(\int_{T_{n-1}(t_{n})}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(t_{j+1}-t_{j})^{1-\alpha_{j}}d\boldsymbol{t}_{n-1}\right)dt_{n}

Due to inequality (
2.2
).
and the fact that
\Gamma(an+b+1)\geq c_{a}^{n}(n!)^{a}
for any
a>0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
with some constant
c_{a}
depending only on
a
, it can be proved that

\int_{T_{n-1}(t_{n})}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(t_{j+1}-t_{j})^{1-a_{j}}d\boldsymbol{t}_{n-1}\leq\frac{C^{n}}{(n!)^{2H+1}}t_{n}^{(2H+1)n-2}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle J_{n,h}(s)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C^{n}}{(n!)^{2H+1}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|1-e^{-i\eta_{n}h}|^{2}(1+|\eta_{n}|^{1-2H})\frac{\sin^{2}((t-t_{n})|\eta_{n}|)}{|\eta_{n}|^{2}}t_{n}^{(2H+1)n-2}d\eta_{n}dt_{n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C^{n}}{(n!)^{2H+1}}s^{(2H+1)n-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|1-e^{-i\eta_{n}h}|^{2}(1+|\eta_{n}|^{1-2H})\frac{1}{|\eta_{n}|^{2}}d\eta_{n},

where the last inequality follows from the bound

\sin^{2}((s-t_{n})|\eta_{n}|)\leq 1
.
Using the fact that
|1-e^{-ix}|^{2}=2(1-\cos x)
and the identity

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1-\cos(\xi x))|x|^{-\alpha-1}dx=C_{\alpha}|\xi|^{\alpha},\quad\mbox{for any $\alpha\in(0,2)$},

we obtain that

J_{n,h}(s)\leq\frac{C^{n}}{(n!)^{2H+1}}\big(|h|+|h|^{2H}\big)
.
Plugging this inequality to (
2.5
), we get

\|u(s,0)-u(s,h)\|_{p}\leq(|h|^{1/2}+|h|^{H})\sum_{n\geq 1}(p-1)^{n/2}\frac{C^{n}}{(n!)^{H+1/2}}\eqqcolon C(|h|^{1/2}+|h|^{H}).

This implies

\displaystyle\int_{|h|\leq 1}\|u(s,x)-u(s,x+h)\|_{p}^{2}|h|^{2H-2}dh\leq C\int_{|h|\leq 1}\big(|h|^{1/2}+|h|^{H}\big)^{2}|h|^{2H-2}dh<\infty,

because
H>1/4
. The proof of this lemma is complete.
∎

2.2
(HAM) with delta initial velocity

In this section, we study equation (
1.9
).
We begin by recalling a basic fact which states that the solution to the deterministic wave equation:

\frac{\partial^{2}w}{\partial^{2}t}(t,x)=\frac{\partial^{2}w}{\partial^{2}x}(t,x),\quad t>r,x\in\mathbb{R}

with initial condition
w(r,x)=u_{0}(x)
and
\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}(r,x)=v_{0}(x)
is given by:

w(t,x)=(G_{t-r}*v_{0})(x)+\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(G_{t-r}*u_{0})(x).

When
u_{0}=0
and
v_{0}=\delta_{z}
(for fixed
z\in\mathbb{R}
), this becomes
w(t,x)=G_{t-r}(x-z)
. This observation motivates the definition below.

Consider the following model:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dfrac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial t^{2}}(t,x)=\dfrac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial x^{2}}(t,x)+v(t,x)\dot{W}(t,x),\ t>r,\ x\in\mathbb{R}\\
v(r,\cdot)=0,\ \dfrac{\partial v}{\partial t}(r,\cdot)=\delta_{z},\end{cases}

(2.7)

We say that a random field
v^{(r,z)}=\{v^{(r,z)}(t,x);(t,x)\in[r,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\}
is a (mild)
solution
of equation (
2.7
) if it satisfies the integral equation (
1.9
).

It can be proved that equation (
2.7
) has a unique solution (see e.g.,
[
8
, Example B.2]
). Moreover, this solution has the chaos expansion:

v^{(r,z)}(t,x)=G_{t-r}(x-z)+\sum_{n\geq 1}I_{n}(g_{n}(\cdot,r,z,t,x)),

(2.8)

where
g_{0}(r,z,t,x)=G_{t-r}(x-z)
and for
n\geq 1
,

g_{n}(\boldsymbol{t}_{n},\boldsymbol{x}_{n},r,z,t,x)=G_{t-t_{n}}(x-x_{n})\times\dots\times G_{t_{1}-r}(x_{1}-z).

(2.9)

In addition, for any
p\geq 2
and
t>0
,

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq r\leq s\leq t\\
x,z\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}\big\|v^{(r,z)}(s,x)\big\|_{p}<C,

(2.10)

where
C>0
is a constant depends on
(t,p,H)
.

Lemma 2.4
.

For any
0\leq r\leq s\leq t<\infty
,
x,z\in\mathbb{R}
, and
p\geq 2
, we have:

\big\|v^{(r,z)}(s,x)\big\|_{p}=2G_{s-r}(x-z)\big\|v^{(r,z)}(s,x)\big\|_{p}\leq CG_{s-r}(x-z).

(2.11)

where
C>0
is a constant that depends on
(t,p,H)
.

Proof.

Taking account of the special form (
1.7
) of
G
, we see that
g_{n}(\cdot,r,z,t,x)=2G_{t-r}(x-z)g_{n}(\cdot,r,z,t,x)
. From this, we deduce using the chaos expansion (
2.8
) that (
1.10
) holds.
Thus, (
2.11
) follows from (
2.10
) and (
1.10
). The proof of this lemma is complete.
∎

The next lemma presents some translation-invariance properties of

Lemma 2.5
.

For any
0\leq r\leq t<\infty
and

a)

\displaystyle v^{(r,z)}(t,x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}v^{(r,0)}(t,x-z)

b)

\displaystyle v^{(r,z)}(t,x)-v^{(r,z^{\prime})}(t,x^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}v^{(r,0)}(t,x-z)-v^{(r,z^{\prime}-z)}(t,x^{\prime}-z)

c)

\begin{aligned} &v^{(r,z)}(t,x)-v^{(r,z)}(t,x^{\prime})-v^{(r,z^{\prime})}(t,x)+v^{(r,z^{\prime})}(t,x^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\\

Proof.

a) This is similar to the proof of Lemma
2.2

b) We have the following chaos expansion:

\displaystyle v^{(r,z)}(t,x)-v^{(r,z^{\prime})}(t,x^{\prime})=G_{t-r}(x-z)-G_{t-r}(x^{\prime}-z^{\prime})+

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\int_{r<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}<t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big(G_{t-t_{n}}(x-x_{n})G_{t_{1}-r}(x_{1}-z)-G_{t-t_{n}}(x^{\prime}-x_{n})G_{t_{1}-r}(x_{1}-z^{\prime})\Big)

for

c) The chaos expansion of the term on the left-hand side is:

\displaystyle G_{t-r}(x-z)-G_{t-r}(x^{\prime}-z)-G_{t-r}(x-z^{\prime})+G_{t-r}(x^{\prime}-z^{\prime})+

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{n\geq 1}\int_{r<t_{1}<\ldots<t_{n}<t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big(G_{t-t_{n}}(x-x_{n})-G_{t-t_{n}}(x^{\prime}-x_{n})\big)\prod_{i=1}^{n-1}G_{t_{i+1}-t_{i}}(x_{i+1}-x_{i})

where

Without loss of generality, we assume that

[

D\varphi(F)=\varphi^{\prime}(F)DF
, we see that

Using Minkowski inequality, Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fact that
|\sin(x)|\leq 1

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|D_{r,y}U_{R}-D_{r,z}U_{R}|^{2}=\|D_{r,y}U_{R}-D_{r,z}U_{R}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left(\int_{-R}^{R}\left\|\sin\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{j}u(t,x+\zeta_{j})\right)\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{j}\left(D_{r,y}u(t,x+\zeta_{j})-D_{r,z}u(t,x+\zeta_{j})\right)\right\|_{2}dx\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left(\int_{-R}^{R}\sum_{j=1}^{k}|b_{j}|\big\|D_{r,y}u(t,x+\zeta_{j})-D_{r,z}u(t,x+\zeta_{j})\big\|_{4}dx\right)^{2},

and therefore,

{\rm Var}(U_{R})\leq C\sum_{j=1}^{k}b_{j}^{2}\times\sup_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\int_{-R}^{R}\big\|\Delta_{h}(r,z,t,x+\zeta)\big\|_{4}dx\right)^{2}|h|^{2H-2}dhdzdr,

2.11

\displaystyle\sup_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\int_{-R}^{R}\big\|\Delta_{h}(r,z,t,x+\zeta)\big\|_{4}dx\right)^{2}|h|^{2H-2}dhdz

\displaystyle=\sup_{\zeta\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}\big\|\Delta_{h}(r,z,t,x_{1}+\zeta)\big\|_{4}\big\|\Delta_{h}(r,z,t,x_{2}+\zeta)\big\|_{4}dx_{1}dx_{2}\right)|h|^{2H-2}dhdz

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}(U_{R})\leq CR.

It follows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{R^{2}}\mathrm{Var}(U_{R})\leq CR^{-1}\to 0,\quad\text{as }R\to\infty.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is complete.

3.2
Limiting covariance—Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section, we give the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
Note that

\sigma_{R}^{2}(t)={\rm Var}(F_{R}(t))=\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}\rho_{t}(x-y)dxdy

where

\rho_{t}(x-y)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}\left[\big(u(t,x)-1\big)\big(u(t,y)-1\big)\right]=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n!}\gamma_{n}(t,x-y)

(3.1)

and

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}(t,x-y)\coloneqq(n!)^{2}\langle\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,t,x),\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,t,y)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}

\displaystyle\quad=n!C_{H}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{F}f_{n}(\cdot,t,x)(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})\overline{\mathcal{F}f_{n}(\cdot,t,y)(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n})}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\xi_{j}|^{1-2H}d\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}

\displaystyle\quad=n!C_{H}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-i(\xi_{1}+\ldots+\xi_{n})(x-y)}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\mathcal{F}G_{t_{j+1}-t_{j}}(\xi_{1}+\ldots+\xi_{j})|^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\xi_{j}|^{1-2H}d\boldsymbol{\xi}_{n}d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}

\displaystyle\quad=n!C_{H}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-i\eta_{n}(x-y)}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\sin^{2}((t_{j+1}-t_{j})|\eta_{j}|)}{|\eta_{j}|^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\eta_{j}-\eta_{j-1}|^{1-2H}d\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}d\boldsymbol{t}_{n},

(3.2)

with convention
t_{n+1}=t
and
\eta_{0}=0
.
This shows that

\alpha_{n}(t,x-y)
and
\rho_{t}(x-y)
depend on
x
and
y
only through the difference
x-y
.
In particular,
\{u(t,x);x\in\mathbb{R}\}
is a (wide-sense) stationary process with covariance function
\rho_{t}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

The proof is divided in three steps.

Step 1.

In this step, we prove (
1.11
) in the case
t=s
. We write

\sigma_{R}^{2}(t)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n!}\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}\gamma_{n}(t,x-y)dxdy.

(3.3)

Note that

\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}e^{-i\xi(x-y)}dxdy=\left|\int_{-R}^{R}e^{-i\xi x}dx\right|^{2}=\frac{4\sin^{2}(R|\xi|)}{|\xi|^{2}}=4\pi R\ell_{R}(\xi)

(3.4)

where
\ell_{R}(x)\coloneqq(\pi R|x|^{2})^{-1}\sin^{2}(|x|R)
is an approximation of the identity as
R\to\infty
; see
[
55
, Lemma 2.1]
. On the other hand,
[
59
, Theorem 3.2]
shows that for every
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\int_{T_{n}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigg|e^{-i\eta_{n}(x-y)}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\sin^{2}((t_{j+1}-t_{j})|\eta_{j}|)}{|\eta_{j}|^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\eta_{j}-\eta_{j-1}|^{1-2H}\bigg|d\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}<\infty.

Thus, by Fubini theorem,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}\gamma_{n}(t,x-y)dxdy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C_{H}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}e^{-i\eta_{n}(x-y)}dxdy\right)\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\sin^{2}((t_{j+1}-t_{j})|\eta_{j}|)}{|\eta_{j}|^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\eta_{j}-\eta_{j-1}|^{1-2H}d\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4C_{H}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\sin^{2}(R|\eta_{n}|)}{|\eta_{n}|^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\sin^{2}((t_{j+1}-t_{j})|\eta_{j}|)}{|\eta_{j}|^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\eta_{j}-\eta_{j-1}|^{1-2H}d\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}.

(3.5)

We treat separately the case
n=1
.
From
[
8
, Page 30]
, we know that for any
t_{1}\in[0,t]
,
\theta\in(H,\frac{1}{2})
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{\pi}{4t})
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\sin^{2}(R|\eta_{1}|)}{|\eta_{1}|^{2}}\frac{\sin^{2}((t-t_{1})|\eta_{1}|)}{|\eta_{1}|^{2}}|\eta_{1}|^{1-2H}d\eta_{1}\leq C_{\varepsilon,\theta,H}(t-t_{1})^{2}R^{2\theta}+C_{\varepsilon,H}.

Therefore,

\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R}\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}\gamma_{1}(t,x-y)dxdy\leq 4C_{H}\lim_{R\to\infty}R^{2\theta-1}\int_{0}^{t}\big(C_{\varepsilon,\theta,H}(t-t_{1})^{2}+C_{\varepsilon,H}\big)dt_{1}=0.

Next, we examine the terms corresponding to
n\geq 2
.
For any
n\geq 2
, denote

g_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}(\eta)\coloneqq\frac{\sin^{2}((t-t_{n})|\eta|)}{|\eta|^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{\sin^{2}((t_{j+1}-t_{j})|\eta_{j}|)}{|\eta_{j}|^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\eta_{j}-\eta_{j-1}|^{1-2H}d\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n-1},

with

\displaystyle g_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}(0)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\lim_{\eta\to 0}g_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}(\eta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(t-t_{n})^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{\sin^{2}((t_{j+1}-t_{j})|\eta_{j}|)}{|\eta_{j}|^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}|\eta_{j}-\eta_{j-1}|^{1-2H}|\eta_{n-1}|^{1-2H}d\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n-1},

where the last equality is due to the fact that
\lim_{x\to 0}\sin x/x=1
.
Then,
g_{\cdot}^{(n)}(*)
is a non-negative function on
T_{n}(t)\times\mathbb{R}
, and thus

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\int_{-R}^{R}

\displaystyle\int_{-R}^{R}\gamma_{n}(t,x-y)dxdy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\pi C_{H}^{n}R\int_{T_{n}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\ell_{R}(\eta_{n})\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\sin^{2}((t_{j+1}-t_{j})|\eta_{j}|)}{|\eta_{j}|^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\eta_{j}-\eta_{j-1}|^{1-2H}d\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\pi C_{H}^{n}R\int_{T_{n}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\ell_{R}(\eta_{n})g_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}(\eta_{n})d\eta_{n}d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}=4\pi C_{H}^{n}R\int_{T_{n}(t)}\big(\ell_{R}*g_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}\big)(0)d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}.

(3.6)

Using (
2.6
), we obtain that

\displaystyle g_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}(\eta_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}\in D_{n}}\frac{\sin^{2}((t-t_{n})|\eta_{n}|)}{|\eta_{n}|^{2}}|\eta_{n}|^{\alpha_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\sin^{2}((t_{j+1}-t_{j})|\eta_{j}|)}{|\eta_{j}|^{2}}|\eta_{j}|^{\alpha_{j}}d\eta_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C^{n-1}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}\in D_{n}}\frac{\sin^{2}((t-t_{n})|\eta_{n}|)}{|\eta_{n}|^{2}}|\eta_{n}|^{\alpha_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(t_{j+1}-t_{j})^{1-\alpha_{j}},

(3.7)

where for the last inequality we used (
2.1
). It is not difficult to see that
g_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}
is continuous, locally bounded and integrable on
\mathbb{R}
.
Recall that
\ell_{R}
is an approximation of the identity as
R\to\infty
.
Hence, for any
n\geq 2
,

\lim_{R\to\infty}\big(\ell_{R}*g_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}\big)(0)=g_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}(0).

Combining (
3.3
) and (
3.2
), by using the dominated convergence theorem, we see that

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{\sigma_{R}^{2}(t)}{R}=\lim_{R\to\infty}4\pi\sum_{n\geq 2}C_{H}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(t)}\big(\ell_{R}*g_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}\big)(0)d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}=4\pi\sum_{n\geq 2}C_{H}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(t)}g_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}(0)d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}.

(3.8)

As the dominated convergence theorem is applied in (
3.8
), one needs to justify the applicability. In other words, we have to find a sequence of functions
\{h_{n}\}_{n\geq 2}
on
T_{n}(t)
such that
\big(\ell_{R}*g_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}\big)(0)\leq h_{n}(\boldsymbol{t}_{n})
for all
\boldsymbol{t}_{n}\in T_{n}(t)
and
n\geq 1
, and

\sum_{n\geq 2}C_{H}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(t)}h_{n}(\boldsymbol{t}_{n})d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}<\infty.

(3.9)

In particular, this shows that

K(t)=4\pi\sum_{n\geq 2}C_{H}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(t)}g_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}(0)d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}<\infty.

Thanks to (
3.2
) and the inequality
\frac{\sin^{2}((t-t_{n})|\eta|)}{|\eta|^{2}}\leq(t-t_{n})^{2}
, we have:

\displaystyle\big(\ell_{R}*g_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}\big)(0)

\displaystyle\leq C^{n-1}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}\in D_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(t_{j+1}-t_{j})^{\alpha_{j}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\sin^{2}(R|\eta_{n}|)}{\pi R|\eta|^{2}}\frac{\sin^{2}((t-t_{n})|\eta_{n}|)}{|\eta_{n}|^{2}}|\eta_{n}|^{\alpha_{n}}d\eta_{n}

\displaystyle\leq C^{n-1}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}\in D_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(t_{j+1}-t_{j})^{\alpha_{j}}(t-t_{n})^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\sin^{2}(R|\eta_{n}|)}{\pi R|\eta_{n}|^{2}}|\eta_{n}|^{\alpha_{n}}d\eta_{n}

\displaystyle\leq C^{n}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}\in D_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(t_{j+1}-t_{j})^{1-\alpha_{j}}(t-t_{n})^{2}R^{-\alpha_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{n}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{n}\in D_{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(t_{j+1}-t_{j})^{1-\alpha_{j}}(t-t_{n})^{2}\eqqcolon h_{n}(\boldsymbol{t}_{n}),

for any
R\geq 1
, where identity (
2.1
) is used for the second last line.
As a consequence of identity (
2.2
) and the fact that
\Gamma(an+b+1)\geq C^{n}(n!)^{a}
for any
a>0,b\in\mathbb{R}
, we get:

\displaystyle\int_{T_{n}(t)}h_{n}(\boldsymbol{t}_{n})d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}\leq\frac{C^{n}}{(n!)^{2H+1}}(t^{(1+2H)n+1}+t^{(1+2H)n+2-2H}),

which yields (
3.9
).

Step 2.
In this step, we show that

\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{\sigma_{R}^{2}(t)}{R}>0.

Recall (
3.2
), we have

\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}\gamma_{n}(t,x-y)dxdy>0,

for all
n\geq 2
,
R>0
and
t>0
.
Then, taking account of (
3.2
), we can write

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{\sigma_{R}^{2}(t)}{R}>

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{2R}\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}\gamma_{2}(t,x-y)dxdy=4\pi C_{H}^{2}\int_{0<r<s<t}g^{(2)}_{r,s}(0)drds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\pi C_{H}^{2}\int_{0<r<s<t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\sin^{2}((s-r)\eta_{1})}{|\eta_{1}|^{2}}|\eta_{1}|^{2(1-2H)}d\eta_{1}drds

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\int_{0<r<s<t}(s-r)^{4H-1}drds>0,

where
C>0
is a constant depending on
H
, and the last line is due to (
2.1
) and the fact that
H>1/4
.

Step 3.
In this step, we complete the proof of (
1.11
). Without loss of generality, assume that
s\leq t
. Similarly to (
3.2
),

\mathbb{E}[F_{R}(t)F_{R}(s)]=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n!}\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}\widehat{\gamma}_{n}(t,s,x-y)dxdy,

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\gamma}_{n}(t,s,x-y)

\displaystyle=(n!)^{2}\langle\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,t,x),\widetilde{f}_{n}(\cdot,s,y)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes n}}

\displaystyle=n!C_{H}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(s)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-i\eta_{n}(x-y)}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{\sin^{2}((t_{j+1}-t_{j})|\eta_{j}|)}{|\eta_{j}|^{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\frac{\sin((t-t_{n})|\eta_{n}|)\sin((s-t_{n})|\eta_{n}|)}{|\eta_{n}|^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\eta_{j}-\eta_{j-1}|^{1-2H}d\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n}d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}.

The same argument as for (
3.2
) shows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{-R}^{R}\widehat{\gamma}_{n}(t,s,x-y)dxdy=4\pi C_{H}^{n}R\int_{T_{n}(s)}\big(\ell_{R}*\widehat{g}_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}\big)(0)d\boldsymbol{t}_{n},

where

\displaystyle\widehat{g}_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}(\eta)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\frac{\sin((t-t_{n})|\eta|)\sin((s-t_{n})|\eta|)}{|\eta|^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n-1}}\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{\sin^{2}((t_{j+1}-t_{j})|\eta_{j}|)}{|\eta_{j}|^{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}|\eta_{j}-\eta_{j-1}|^{1-2H}d\boldsymbol{\eta}_{n-1}.

Then, relation (
1.11
) follows by the dominated convergence theorem, as in Step 1. The proof of this theorem is complete.
∎

Remark 3.1
.

There might be an alternative method for proving Theorem
1.2
, which would give a different representation of the limiting covariance. We explain this method here. If

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\rho_{t}(x)|dx<\infty,

(3.10)

then by the dominated convergence theorem

\frac{\sigma_{R}^{2}(t)}{R}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{t}(x)\frac{|B_{R}\cap B_{R}(-x)|}{R}dx\to 2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\rho_{t}(x)dx\quad\mbox{as}\quad R\to\infty

where
B_{R}=[-R,R]
(see
[
55
, Page 27]
). Recalling definition (
3.1
) of
\rho_{t}(x)
, (
3.10
) follows if one can show that

\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n!}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\gamma_{n}(t,x)|dx<\infty.

Unfortunately, we could not prove that
\gamma_{n}(t,\cdot)
is integrable on
\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 3.2
.

Suppose we can exchange the order of integrals arbitrarily and treat the Diract distribution
\delta
as a regular function. Then, we can write

\displaystyle 4\pi C_{H}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(t)}g_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}(0)d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}=

\displaystyle 4\pi C_{H}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\delta_{0}(\eta_{n})g_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}(\eta_{n})d\eta_{n}d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2C_{H}^{n}\int_{T_{n}(t)}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-i\eta_{n}x}\mathbf{1}_{\mathbb{R}}(x)dx\Big)g_{\boldsymbol{t}_{n}}^{(n)}(\eta_{n})d\eta_{n}d\boldsymbol{t}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{n!}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\langle f_{n}(\cdot,t,x),f_{n}(\cdot,t,0)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}dx.

Thus it is natural to conjecture that

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\frac{1}{R}\sigma_{R}^{2}=2\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{Cov}(u(t,x),u(t,0))dx.

(3.11)

Actually, this has been confirmed in parabolic cases (see
[
53
,
55
]
) and also in hyperbolic cases assuming the non-negative correlation in space of driven noises (see
[
4
]
). The aforementioned results rely on the nonnegativity of
\gamma_{n}(t,x)
, which does not hold in our setting. This prevents us to provide a proof for (
3.11
). We expect it can be verified in the future.

3.3
Quantitative CLT—Proof of Theorem
1.3

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.3
.
Applying
[
54
, Proposition 2.4]
,
we get:

d_{TV}\left(\frac{F_{R}(t)}{\sigma_{R}(t)},Z\right)\leq\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{\sigma_{R}^{2}(t)}\sqrt{\mathcal{A}},

where

\mathcal{A}=C_{H}^{3}\int_{[0,t]^{3}}\mathcal{A}_{0}^{*}(r,s,\theta)drdsd\theta,

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0}^{*}(r,s,

\displaystyle\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}\|D_{r,z}F_{R}(t)-D_{r,z^{\prime}}F_{R}(t)\|_{4}\|D_{\theta,w}F_{R}(t)-D_{\theta,w^{\prime}}F_{R}(t)\|_{4}

\displaystyle\times\|D_{(r,z),(s,y)}^{2}F_{R}(t)-D_{(r,z),(s,y^{\prime})}^{2}F_{R}(t)-D_{(r,z^{\prime}),(s,y)}^{2}F_{R}(t)+D_{(r,z^{\prime}),(s,y^{\prime})}^{2}F_{R}(t)\|_{4}

\displaystyle\times\|D_{(\theta,w),(s,y)}^{2}F_{R}(t)-D_{(\theta,w),(s,y^{\prime})}^{2}F_{R}(t)-D_{(\theta,w^{\prime}),(s,y)}^{2}F_{R}(t)+D_{(\theta,w^{\prime}),(s,y^{\prime})}^{2}F_{R}(t)\|_{4}

\displaystyle\times|y-y^{\prime}|^{2H-2}|z-z^{\prime}|^{2H-2}|w-w^{\prime}|^{2H-2}dydy^{\prime}dzdz^{\prime}dwdw^{\prime}.

Since Theorem
1.2
concludes that
\sigma_{R}^{2}(t)\sim CR
as
R\to\infty
, it is enough to show that
\mathcal{A}\leq CR
.

By Minkowski’s inequality,
we get
\mathcal{A}_{0}^{*}(r,s,\theta)\leq\mathcal{A}_{0}(r,s,\theta)
, where
\mathcal{A}_{0}
can be written, after a change of variables, as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0}

\displaystyle(r,s,\theta)=\int_{[-R,R]^{4}}dx_{1}dx_{2}dx_{3}dx_{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{6}}dydy^{\prime}dzdz^{\prime}dwdw^{\prime}dw^{\prime}|y^{\prime}|^{2H-2}|z^{\prime}|^{2H-2}|w^{\prime}|^{2H-2}

\displaystyle\times\big\|\Delta_{z^{\prime}}(r,z,t,x_{1})\big\|_{4}\big\|\Delta_{w^{\prime}}(\theta,w,t,x_{2})\big\|_{4}\big\|\square_{z^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(r,z,s,y,t,x_{3})\big\|_{4}\big\|\square_{w^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(\theta,w,s,y,t,x_{4})\big\|_{4}.

where
\Delta
and
\square
are defined as in (
2.17
) and (
2.4
), respectively.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\leq\mathcal{A}_{1}+\mathcal{A}_{2}+\mathcal{A}_{3}+\mathcal{A}_{4},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}\coloneqq\int_{0<r\vee\theta<s<t}\mathcal{A}_{0}(r,s,\theta)drdsd\theta,\qquad\mathcal{A}_{2}\coloneqq\int_{0<r<s<\theta<t}\mathcal{A}_{0}(r,s,\theta)drdsd\theta,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{3}\coloneqq\int_{0<\theta<s<r<t}\mathcal{A}_{0}(r,s,\theta)drdsd\theta,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{A}_{4}\coloneqq\int_{0<s<r\wedge\theta<t}\mathcal{A}_{0}(r,s,\theta)drdsd\theta.

The estimates for
\mathcal{A}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{4}
are quite similar. Here we only provide a detailed deduction of the estimate for
\mathcal{A}_{1}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0}(r,s,\theta)\leq T_{1}(r,\theta)^{\frac{1}{2}}T_{2}(s,r)^{\frac{1}{2}}\int_{-R}^{R}T_{3}(s,\theta,x_{4})^{\frac{1}{2}}dx_{4}

(3.12)

where

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} T_{1}(r,\theta)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}&\Big(\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big\|\Delta_{z^{\prime}}(r,z,t,x_{1})\big\|_{4}dx_{1}\Big)^{2}\Big(\sup_{w\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big\|\Delta_{w^{\prime}}(\theta,w,t,x_{2})\big\|dx_{2}\Big)^{2}\\
&\times|z^{\prime}|^{2H-2}|w^{\prime}|^{2H-2}dz^{\prime}dw^{\prime}<C,\end{aligned}

\displaystyle T_{2}(s,r)\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\big\|\square_{z^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(r,z,s,y,t,x_{3})\big\|_{4}dx_{3}dz\right)^{2}|y^{\prime}|^{2H-2}|z^{\prime}|^{2H-2}dy^{\prime}dz^{\prime}<C,

and

\displaystyle T_{3}(s,\theta,x_{4})\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\big\|\square_{w^{\prime},y^{\prime}}(\theta,w,s,y,y,x_{4})\big\|_{4}dydw\right)^{2}|y^{\prime}|^{2H-2}|w^{\prime}|^{2H-2}dy^{\prime}dw^{\prime}<C,

by using Proposition
2.11
. This yields immediately that
for any
0<r\vee\theta<s<t
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0}(r,s,\theta)\leq C\int_{-R}^{R}dx_{4}=CR,

and thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{1}=\int_{\{0<r<s\vee\theta<s\}}\mathcal{A}_{0}(r,s,\theta)drdsd\theta\leq CR.

Similar arguments ensure that
\mathcal{A}_{k}\leq CR
for
k=2,3,4
. Therefore,
\mathcal{A}\leq CR
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

3.4
Functional CLT—Proof of Theorem
1.4

In this section, we include the proof of Theorem
1.4
. We fix
T>0
. It suffices to show the following properties:

(1)

The tightness of the collection of
C([0,T])
-valued random variables
\{\sigma_{R}^{-1}F_{R}(r);r\in[0,T]\}_{R\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}
.

(2)

The convergence in distribution of
\{(\sigma_{R}^{-1}F_{R}(t_{1}),\dots,\sigma_{R}^{-1}F_{R}(t_{m})\}_{R\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}
to
(\mathcal{G}(t_{1}),\ldots,\mathcal{G}(t_{m}))
as
R\to\infty
, for all positive integer
m
and for all
0\leq t_{1}\leq\dots\leq t_{m}\leq T
.

Proof of tightness.

By Kolmogorov-Chentsov criterion (
[
43
, Theorem 23.7]
), it is enough to prove that

\displaystyle\|F_{R}(t)-F_{R}(s)\|_{p}\leq CR^{1/2}(t-s)^{1/2},

for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
, where
C>0
is a constant that depends on
(T,p,H)
. Using (
1.6
) and the convention
G_{t}(x)=0
for
t<0
, we write:

u(t,x)-u(s,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\big(G_{t-r}(x-y)-G_{s-r}(x-y)\big)u(r,y)W(dr,dy).

By stochastic Fubini theorem,

F_{R}(t)-F_{R}(s)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{-R}^{R}\big(G_{t-r}(x-y)-G_{s-r}(x-y)\big)dx\right)u(r,y)W(dr,dy).

We use the following inequality: for any predictable process
S
,

\left\|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}}S(t,x)W(dt,dx)\right\|_{p}^{2}\leq C_{p,H}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\|S(t,x)-S(t,y)\|_{p}^{2}|x-y|^{2H-2}dxdydt.

This inequality follows from the Burhholder-Davis-Gundy inequality for the stochastic integral with respect to
W
(given by
[
2
, Theorem 2.9]
) followed by Minkowski inequality for the
\|\cdot\|_{p/2}
-norm. It follows that

\|F_{R}(t)-F_{R}(s)\|_{p}^{2}\leq C_{p,H}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\|S(r,y)-S(r,z)\|_{p}^{2}|y-z|^{2H-2}dydzdr,

with

S(r,y)=\left(\int_{-R}^{R}\big(G_{t-r}(x-y)-G_{s-r}(x-y)\big)dx\right)u(r,y)
.
It follows that:

\displaystyle\|F_{R}(t)-F_{R}(s)\|_{p}^{2}\leq C_{p,H}\big(I_{1}+I_{2}\big),

where

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\int_{-R}^{R}\big(G_{t-r}(x-y)-G_{s-r}(x-y)-G_{t-r}(x-z)+G_{s-r}(x-z)\big)dx\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\times\|u(r,y)\|_{p}^{2}|y-z|^{2H-2}dydzdr

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\int_{-R}^{R}\big(G_{t-r}(x-z)-G_{s-r}(x-z)\big)dx\right)^{2}\|u(r,y)-u(r,z)\|_{p}^{2}|y-z|^{2H-2}dydzdr.

Note that

\displaystyle\int_{-R}^{R}\big(G_{t-r}(x-z)-G_{s-r}(x-z)\big)dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-R}^{R}1_{\{s-r<|x-z|<t-r\}}dx\in[0,t-s].

(3.13)

Thus, due to Lemmas
2.2
and
2.3
, and the fact that
G_{s-r}(x)\leq G_{t-r}(x)
, we deduce that

\displaystyle I_{2}\leq

\displaystyle C(t-s)\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}G_{t-r}(x-z)dz\right)drdx=C(t-s)R\int_{0}^{t}(t-r)dr\leq C(t-s)R.

Next, we treat
I_{1}
. By (
3.13
),

\displaystyle\left|\int_{-R}^{R}\big(G_{t-r}(x-y)-G_{s-r}(x-y)-G_{t-r}(x-z)+G_{s-r}(x-z)\big)dx\right|\leq 2(t-s).

Using this bound and Lemma
2.1
, we find that

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2(t-s)\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\big(|G_{t-r}(x-y)-G_{t-r}(x-z)|+|G_{s-r}(x-y)-G_{s-r}(x-z)|\big)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times|y-z|^{2H-2}dydzdrdx

Note that
|G_{t}(x)-G_{t}(y)|\in\{1/2,0\}
for all
t\geq 0
and
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, thus
|G_{t}(x)-G_{t}(y)|=2|G_{t}(x)-G_{t}(y)|^{2}
. As a result,

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 4(t-s)\bigg(\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|G_{t-r}(x-y)-G_{t-r}(x-z)|^{2}|y-z|^{2H-2}dydzdrdx

\displaystyle+\int_{-R}^{R}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|G_{s-r}(x-y)-G_{s-r}(x-z)|^{2}|y-z|^{2H-2}dydzdrdx\bigg)\leq C(t-s)R,

where the last inequality is due to
[
8
, Inequality (58)]
.
∎

Proof of finite dimensional convergence.

The proof follows the same idea as in
[
4
, Section 4.2]
. More precisely, it suffices to show that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\big(\big\langle DF_{R}(t_{i}),-DL^{-1}F_{R}(t_{j})\big\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\big)\leq CR,\quad\mbox{for any $i,j=1,\ldots,m$}.

This inequality is proved using the same argument as for
\mathcal{A}
in Section
3.3
, based on an estimate for
{\rm Var}(\langle DF,-DL^{-1}G\rangle_{\mathcal{H}})
for two random variables
F
and
G
, which can be deduced similarly to the estimate derived for
F=G
in the proof of
[
54
, Proposition 2.4]
.
∎
