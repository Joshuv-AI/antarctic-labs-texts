Abstract
. In this work, we establish a new method to find critical points of differentiable functionals defined in Banach spaces which belong to a suitable class ( 𝒥 \mathcal{J} ) of functionals. Once given a functional J J in the class ( 𝒥 \mathcal{J} ), the central idea of the referred method consists in defining a real function ζ \zeta of a real variable, called energy function , which is naturally associated to J J in the sense that the existence of real critical points for ζ \zeta guarantees the existence of critical points for the functional J J . As a consequence, we are able to solve some variational elliptic problems, whose associated energy functional belongs to ( 𝒥 \mathcal{J} ) and provide a version of the mountain pass theorem for functionals in the class ( 𝒥 \mathcal{J} ) that allows us to obtain mountain pass solutions without the so-called Ambrosetti-Rabinowitz condition.

1.
Introduction

Over the years, many methods have been developed to attack different classes of partial differential equations. Even though some methods are renowned for their range of applications, it is of course expected that each one of them has its advantages and disadvantages. In fact, the experience shows us that depending on the level of complexity or specificity of a partial differential equation certain approaches are more appropriated and effective than others. In this context, it is crucial to have a diversified theoretical framework which allows that different techniques complement each other in order to provide a more complete understanding of the problems.

In this work, we have established an alternative method that allows us to complement some classical results of the literature, involving differential equations. In fact, this referred method, which we will call
Method of the Energy Function
(MEF), has proved to be effective to treat some relevant classes of variational elliptic partial differential problems, for which, some progress is made in the present article, see Section
4
. For instance, by using the energy function approach, we obtain a version of the Mountain Pass Theorem suitable for problems that do not require the Ambrosetti-Rabinowitz growth condition, see Theorem
3.1
.

In order to give a more clear idea about the subject we are going to treat, let us consider a functional
J:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
in the form

J=\Psi-\Phi
, where
E
is a reflexive Banach space and
\Psi,\Phi\in C^{1}(E,\mathbb{R})
. When
J
is of class
(\mathcal{J})
in
E
, that is,
\Phi
and
\Psi
satisfy certain conditions to be stated in the next section, it is possible to relate
J
with a real
C^{1}
-function
\zeta:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, which we will call the
energy function
.

The main feature on the approach we are about to describe is that it relates the existence of critical points for
\zeta
with the existence of critical points for the functional
J
. More precisely, under appropriated conditions, we can “change” the effort of searching for critical points of functionals defined in infinite-dimensional Banach spaces for the task of finding critical points of a one variable real function, which is certainly more treatable, since it does not require more than basic tools of the classical differential Calculus to be executed.

The idea of trying to relate the energy functional of a certain elliptic problem to a real function was inspired by the work of Arcoya, Santos Júnior and Suárez
[
3
]
, which studied a problem involving a degenerate Kirchhoff operator of the form

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}-m(\|u\|)\Delta u=f(u)&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
\hskip 56.9055ptu=0&\mbox{on}&\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
(
N\geq 1
) is a smooth bounded domain,
m:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are continuous functions satisfying appropriated conditions. The authors in
[
3
]
defined a function
\alpha:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

(1.2)

\alpha(r)=\max_{\|u\|^{2}\leq r\atop u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}\int_{\Omega}F^{\ast}(u)dx,

where

F^{\ast}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f^{\ast}(s)ds,\,\,\mbox{with}\,\,f^{\ast}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}f(0)&\mbox{if}&t<0,\\
f(t)&\mbox{if}&0\leq t<s_{\ast},\\
0&\mbox{if}&s_{\ast}<t,\end{array}\right.

and showed that
\alpha
is differentiable in
(0,\infty)
with

\alpha^{\prime}(r)=\frac{1}{2r}\max_{u\in\mathcal{S}_{r}}\int_{\Omega}f^{\ast}(u)udx,

where

\mathcal{S}_{r}:=\left\{u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega);\|u\|^{2}\leq r\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,\int_{\Omega}F^{\ast}(u)dx=\alpha(r)\right\}.

It is important to point out that it is not obvious or intuitive that
\alpha
, defined as in (
1.2
), is a differentiable, or even continuous, map regarding to
r
, mainly because the function which attains the maximum in (
1.2
) cannot be unique. In fact, in the particular situation considered in
[
3
]
, in order to prove the differentiability of
\alpha
, the authors were inspired by arguments first used in Szulkin and Weth
[
27
]
. Another important detail on
[
3
]
that should be highlighted is that, the map
\alpha
is only used to show that the weak solution found to the problem (
1.1
) is nontrivial and no relation was established between critical points of
\alpha
, or even of any other real function involving
\alpha
, and critical points of the energy functional associated to the problem (
1.1
).

The paper is divided as follows: In Section
2
, we introduce the energy function
\zeta:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
(see (
2.1
)) in an abstract context and give general conditions under which
\zeta
is

and its critical points have a strong connexion with a certain class of critical points of

[

are the following: 1) It provides a new characterization of the mountain pass level as a “maxmin” type constant. In fact, at least in our best knowledge, such a characterization has not previously appeared in the literature; 2) It allows to treat problems involving nonlinearities satisfying conditions weaker than Ambrosetti-Rabinowitz condition
[
2
]
, which have not been required in papers that address the matter, see for instance
[

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,

,
24
,

]
and references there in. Finally, in Section

[

2.
The Method (MEF)

Throughout this article,
E
denotes a reflexive Banach space. We say that a functional
J
belongs to class
(\mathcal{J})
in
E
, if
J:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
J=\Psi-\Phi
and
\Psi,\Phi\in C^{1}(E,\mathbb{R})
satisfy the following hypotheses:

(\Psi_{1})

\Psi
is convex, coercive and weakly lower semicontinuous;

(\Psi_{2})

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}=\{u\in E;\,\Psi(u)\leq r\}
for all
r\geq 0
,
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{0}=\{0\}
and
0\in\mbox{int}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r})
for each
r>0
;

(\Psi_{3})

\Psi^{\prime}(u)u\neq 0
for all
u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
, where
\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}=\{u\in E;\,\Psi(u)=r\}
;

(\Psi_{4})

For each
u\not=0
and
r>0
, there exists a unique
t_{u}(r)>0
such that
t_{u}(r)u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
;

(\Psi_{5})

If
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u_{0}
in
E
and
\Psi(u_{n})\to\Psi(u_{0})
, then
u_{n}\to u_{0}
in
E
;

(\Phi_{1})

\Phi
and
u\mapsto\Phi^{\prime}(u)u/\Psi^{\prime}(u)u
are weakly upper semicontinuous;

(\Phi_{2})

If
\Phi^{\prime}(u)=0
, then
\Phi(u)\leq 0
;

(\Phi_{3})

There exists a sequence
\{u_{n}\}\subset E
such that
u_{n}\rightarrow 0
and
\Phi(u_{n})>0
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Some consequences can be concluded from previous assumptions:

(1)

The coercivity in
(\Psi_{1})
ensures that the boundaries
\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
are nonempty, and it also implies the boundedness of the sets
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
.

(2)

From
(\Psi_{2})
, it follows that
\Psi(u)=0
if, and only if,
u=0
and, moreover,
\Psi(u)>0
if
u\neq 0
.

(3)

Combining information in (2), the coercivity in
(\Psi_{1})
and
(\Psi_{3})
, we conclude that
\Psi^{\prime}(u)u>0
for all
u\neq 0
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose that
\Psi
satisfies
(\Psi_{1})
,
0\in\mbox{int}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r})
for each
r>0
, and
\Phi
is weakly upper semicontinuous and satisfies
(\Phi_{2})-(\Phi_{3})
. Then, for each
r>0
,
\Phi
is bounded from above on
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
and its maximum is attained at the boundary. In other words, there exists
u_{r}\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
such that

\Phi(u_{r})=\max_{u\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}}\Phi(u)=\max_{u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}}\Phi(u).

Proof.

Since
\Psi
satisfies
(\Psi_{1})
, we have that
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
is convex, closed and bounded, and so,
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
is weakly closed and bounded, from where it follows that
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
is weakly compact. On the other hand, as
\Phi
is weakly upper semicontinuous, it is bounded from above on
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
and there exists
u_{r}\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
such that

\Phi(u_{r})=\max_{u\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}}\Phi(u).

Now, we are going to show that
u_{r}\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
. Arguing by contradiction, assume that
u_{r}
belongs to the interior of
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
, which is open, because
\Psi
is continuous and

\mathcal{A}_{r}:=\mbox{int}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r})=\{u\in E;\,\Psi(u)<r\}.

In this case,
u_{r}
is a critical point of
\Phi
, that is,
\Phi^{\prime}(u_{r})=0
. Using (
\Phi_{3}
) together with the fact that
0\in\mbox{int}(\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r})
for each
r>0
, there exists
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
u_{n_{0}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
and

0<\Phi(u_{n_{0}})\leq\max_{u\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}}\Phi(u)=\Phi(u_{r}).

However, from (
\Phi_{2}
), we also have that
\Phi(u_{r})\leq 0
, which is a contradiction, proving that
u_{r}\in\partial\mathcal{A}_{r}
.

∎

Remark 2.2
.

Let us consider the set

\mathcal{G}_{r}:=\left\{u\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}_{r}};\hskip 2.84544pt\Phi(u)=\max_{v\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}_{r}}}\Phi(v)\right\}.

It is an immediate consequence of the proof of Proposition
2.1
that, if
\Psi
satisfies
(\Psi_{1})
and
\Phi
is weakly upper semicontinuous, then the set
\mathcal{G}_{r}
is nonempty. So, it makes sense to consider the hypothesis

(\Phi_{2}^{\prime})

For each
r>0
, there exists
c_{r}>0
, such that
c_{r}\Phi(u)\leq\Phi^{\prime}(u)u
for all
u\in\mathcal{G}_{r}
.

Note that, Proposition
2.1
(as well as all subsequent results that make use of it) still holds true if we replace hypothesis
(\Phi_{2})
by
(\Phi_{2}^{\prime})
. It is important to point out that
(\Phi_{2})
and
(\Phi_{2}^{\prime})
does not imply each other.

From previous remark, the set
\mathcal{G}_{r}
is well-defined. Moreover, by Proposition
2.1
, one has
\mathcal{G}_{r}\subset\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}_{r}}
.

In the following, under the assumptions of Proposition
2.1
, it is possible to introduce a real function of real variable which is naturally related to the functional
J
, in a sense that we will clarify later on (see Theorem
2.8
). This function plays a crucial role in the next results to be presented in this manuscript.

Suppose that the assumptions in Proposition
2.1
hold. We call
energy function
to the function
\zeta:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

(2.1)

\zeta(r):=r-\varphi(r),

where
\varphi(r):=\displaystyle\max_{u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}}\Phi(u)
if
r>0
and
\varphi(0)=\Phi(0)
.

Observe that the energy function and the functional
J
are related by the following equality

(2.2)

\zeta(r)=r-\max_{u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}_{r}}}\Phi(u)=\min_{u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}}J(u)=J(u_{r})\ \mbox{for all $r>0$ and $\zeta(0)=J(0)$}.

In what follows, our main goal is to show that we can change the task of finding critical points of
J
for the simpler task of finding critical points for
\zeta
. In order to establish this relation, we will study the differentiability of function
\varphi
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose that
\Psi
satisfies
(\Psi_{1})
,
(\Psi_{2})
and
(\Psi_{4})
, and
\Phi
is weakly upper semicontinuous and satisfies
(\Phi_{2})-(\Phi_{3})
. Then, function
\varphi:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous.

Proof.

It is enough to take an arbitrary sequence
\{r_{n}\}\subset(0,\infty)
with
r_{n}\rightarrow r_{0}\geq 0
. Before that, observe that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we know from Proposition
2.1
that, there exists a function
u_{n}\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}_{r_{n}}}
such that

\Phi(u_{n})=\max_{u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r_{n}}}\Phi(u)=\max_{u\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r_{n}}}\Phi(u).

We will show that
\{u_{n}\}
is bounded. In fact, since
\{r_{n}\}
is bounded, there exists
R>0
such that
\Psi(u_{n})=r_{n}\leq R
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, showing that
\{u_{n}\}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{R}
, which is a bounded set by
(\Psi_{1})
. Hence, up to a subsequence,

(2.3)

u_{n}\rightharpoonup u_{0}\,\,\mbox{in}\,\,E.

Next, we will consider two cases, namely
r_{0}=0
and
r_{0}>0
.

Case 1:

r_{0}=0
.

In this case, by
(\Psi_{2})
, the lower weak semicontinuity of
\Psi
and (
2.3
), one has

0\leq\Psi(u_{0})\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Psi(u_{n})=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}r_{n}=0.

Therefore,
\Psi(u_{0})=0
and, again, by
(\Psi_{2})
,
u_{0}=0
.

Finally, from
(\Phi_{3})
, there exists
\{v_{n}\}\subset E
with
v_{n}\to 0
and
\Phi(v_{n})>0
. Now, from
(\Psi_{2})
, if necessary, we can renumerate
\{v_{n}\}
in such a way that
v_{n}\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r_{n}}
. Thus, since
\Phi
is continuous and weakly upper semicontinuous, we get

\Phi(0)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\Phi(v_{n})\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(u_{n})\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(u_{n})\leq\Phi(0),

showing that

\varphi(r_{n})=\Phi(u_{n})\to\Phi(0)=\varphi(0).

Case 2:

r_{0}>0
.

Since
\Psi
is weakly lower semicontinuous,

\Psi(u_{0})\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Psi(u_{n})=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}r_{n}=r_{0},

which means that
u_{0}\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r_{0}}
. Thus,

(2.4)

\Phi(u_{0})\leq\varphi(r_{0}).

On the other hand, consider
u_{\ast}\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r_{0}}
such that

\varphi(r_{0})=\Phi(u_{\ast}).

Gathering
(\Psi_{4})
and coercivity of
\Psi
, there exists a bounded sequence
\{t_{n}\}\subset(0,+\infty)
such that
t_{n}u_{\ast}\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r_{n}}
. Then

\Phi\left(t_{n}u_{\ast}\right)\leq\Phi(u_{n}),\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

Claim 2.4
.

t_{n}\rightarrow 1
as
n\to\infty
.

Assume that for some subsequence of
\{t_{n}\}
, still denoted by itself, we have
t_{n}\to t_{0}
. Since
\Psi
is continuous, we must have

\Psi(t_{0}u_{\ast})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Psi(t_{n}u_{\ast})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}r_{n}=r_{0}=\Psi(u_{\ast}),

from where it follows that
t_{0}u_{\ast}\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r_{0}}
. As
u_{\ast}\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r_{0}}
,
(\Psi_{4})
yields that
t_{0}=1
and the Claim is proved.

The claim above permits to conclude that

\varphi(r_{0})=\Phi\left(u_{\ast}\right)=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi\left(t_{n}u_{\ast}\right)\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(u_{n}).

From
(\Phi_{1})
,

(2.5)

\varphi(r_{0})\leq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(u_{n})\leq\Phi(u_{0}).

Employing (
2.4
) and (
2.5
), one finds

\varphi(r_{0})=\Phi(u_{0}).

Again, by
(\Psi_{4})
and coercivity of
\Psi
, there exists a bounded sequence
\{s_{n}\}\subset(0,+\infty)
such that
s_{n}u_{0}\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r_{n}}
and
s_{n}\to 1
. Hence,

\Phi(u_{0})=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(s_{n}u_{0})\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(u_{n}).

On the other hand, as
\Phi
is weakly upper semicontinuous,

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(u_{n})\leq\Phi(u_{0}).

The last two inequalities give

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(u_{n})=\Phi(u_{0}).

Therefore,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\varphi(r_{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(u_{n})=\Phi(u_{0})=\varphi(r_{0}).

∎

Proposition 2.5
.

Suppose that
\Psi
and
\Phi
satisfy
(\Psi_{1})-(\Psi_{3})
and
(\Phi_{1})-(\Phi_{3})
respectively. Then, the functional
u\mapsto\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(u)u}{\Psi^{\prime}(u)u}
attains the maximum in the set

\mathcal{G}_{r}=\left\{u\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}_{r}};\,\Phi(u)=\varphi(r)\right\}.

Proof.

By
(\Psi_{1})
,
\mathcal{G}_{r}
is a bounded set. Now, we will prove that
\mathcal{G}_{r}
is weakly closed. Since
\tilde{\mathcal{A}_{r}}\subset\overline{B}_{R}(0)
for some
R>0
,
\overline{B}_{R}(0)
is weakly closed and
\overline{B}_{R}(0)
is metrizable by the weak topology, it is enough to prove that if
\{u_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{G}_{r}
and
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u_{0}
in
E
, then
u_{0}\in\mathcal{G}_{r}
.
From
(\Psi_{1})
,

(2.6)

\Psi(u_{0})\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Psi(u_{n})=r,

which means that
u_{0}\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
and

\Phi(u_{0})\leq\varphi(r).

On the other hand, since
\Phi
is weakly upper semicontinuous and
\{u_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{G}_{r}
, it follows that

(2.7)

\varphi(r)=\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi(u_{n})\leq\Phi(u_{0}).

So, from (
2.6
), (
2.7
) and Proposition
2.1
, we get
u_{0}\in\mathcal{G}_{r}
. Therefore,
\mathcal{G}_{r}
is weakly closed and bounded and, consequently, weakly compact. Furthermore, by
(\Psi_{3})
and
(\Phi_{1})
, the function
u\mapsto\Phi^{\prime}(u)u/\Psi^{\prime}(u)u
is well defined and is weakly upper semicontinuous, then the maximum of this functional is attained in
\mathcal{G}_{r}
.
∎

We say that a function
u_{r}\in\mathcal{G}_{r}
is an
energy maximum type point
if

\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(u_{r})u_{r}}{\Psi^{\prime}(u_{r})u_{r}}=\max_{u\in\mathcal{G}_{r}}\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(u)u}{\Psi^{\prime}(u)u}.

Observe that an energy maximum type point
u_{r}\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
maximizes at the same time
\Phi
in
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
and
u\mapsto\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(u)u}{\Psi^{\prime}(u)u}
in
\mathcal{G}_{r}
, and, for sure, it is nontrivial when
r>0
.

Before proving the differentiability of function
\varphi
, we need the following lemma about the differentiability of mapping
r\mapsto t_{u}(r)
, for each
u\neq 0
fixed.

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose that
\Psi
is coercive and satisfies
(\Psi_{2})-(\Psi_{4})
. Then, for each
u\neq 0
fixed, the application
r\mapsto t_{u}(r)
, given in
(\Psi_{4})
, is
C^{1}((0,\infty),\mathbb{R})
with

(2.8)

t_{u}^{\prime}(r)=\frac{1}{\Psi^{\prime}(t_{u}(r)u)u},\ \mbox{for all $r>0$}.

Proof.

In what follows, we define
h:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
by
h_{u}(t)=\Psi(tu)
. From the coercivity of
\Psi
and
(\Psi_{2})-(\Psi_{3})
, we infer that
h_{u}(0)=0
,
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}h_{u}(t)=\infty
and
h_{u}
is increasing. Thus, there exists the inverse
h_{u}^{-1}
and from
(\Psi_{4})

t_{u}(r)=h_{u}^{-1}(r),\ \mbox{for all $r>0$}.

The result follows now from the chain’s rule.
∎

After that previous analysis, we are ready to prove that
\varphi
is
C^{1}
in
(0,\infty)
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Suppose that
J=\Psi-\Phi
belongs to class
(\mathcal{J})
in
E
. Then,
\varphi\in C^{1}((0,\infty),\mathbb{R})
and

\varphi^{\prime}(r)=\max_{u\in\mathcal{G}_{r}}\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(u)u}{\Psi^{\prime}(u)u}.

Proof.

Fix
r_{0}>0
and take
u_{r_{0}}\in\mathcal{G}_{r_{0}}
and
u_{r_{0}+r}\in\mathcal{G}_{r_{0}+r}
, with
r>0
. Thereby,

\varphi(r_{0}+r)-\varphi(r_{0})=\Phi(u_{r_{0}+r})-\Phi(u_{r_{0}}).

By
(\Psi_{4})
, there exists a unique
t_{u_{r_{0}}}(r_{0}+r)
such that
t_{u_{r_{0}}}(r_{0}+r)u_{r_{0}}\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r_{0}+r}
. So,

\Phi\left(t_{u_{r_{0}}}(r_{0}+r)u_{r_{0}}\right)-\Phi(u_{r_{0}})\leq\varphi(r_{0}+r)-\varphi(r_{0}).

Since
t_{u_{r_{0}}}(r_{0})=1
, it follows from Lemma
2.6
and Mean Value Theorem that there exists a constant
0<c_{r}<1
such that

\Phi\left(t_{u_{r_{0}}}(r_{0}+r)u_{r_{0}}\right)-\Phi(u_{r_{0}})=r\frac{\Phi^{\prime}\left(t_{u_{r_{0}}}(r_{0}+c_{r}r)u_{r_{0}}\right)u_{r_{0}}}{\Psi^{\prime}(t_{u_{r_{0}}}(r_{0}+c_{r}r)u_{r_{0}})u_{r_{0}}}.

Consequently,

(2.9)

r\frac{\Phi^{\prime}\left(t_{u_{r_{0}}}(r_{0}+c_{r}r)u_{r_{0}}\right)u_{r_{0}}}{\Psi^{\prime}(t_{u_{r_{0}}}(r_{0}+c_{r}r)u_{r_{0}})u_{r_{0}}}\leq\varphi(r_{0}+r)-\varphi(r_{0}).

On the other hand, there exists a unique

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
E
be a reflexive Banach space and
J
be a functional of class
(\mathcal{J})
in
E
. A maximum energy type point
u_{r}\in\mathcal{G}_{r}
is a critical point of the functional
J
, for some
r>0
if, and only if,
r
is a critical point of the energy function
\zeta
.

Proof.

Suppose that
u_{r}
is a maximum energy type point. By Proposition
2.1
, one has that
u_{r}
is a maximum point of
\Phi
restricted to
\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
. From
(\Psi_{3})
and Lagrange multipliers theorem (see, for example,
[
17
, Proposition 14.3]
), there exists
\lambda_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\Phi^{\prime}(u_{r})v=\lambda_{r}\Psi^{\prime}(u_{r})v,\hskip 2.84544pt\mbox{for all}\hskip 2.84544ptv\in E.

As
(\Psi_{3})
occurs, by choosing
v=u_{r}
, we obtain

(2.22)

\lambda_{r}=\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(u_{r})u_{r}}{\Psi^{\prime}(u_{r})u_{r}}.

On the other hand, from (
2.2
), the same function
u_{r}
is also the minimum of the functional
J
restricted to
\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}
. So, again, from
(\Psi_{3})
and Lagrange multipliers theorem, there exists
\gamma_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(2.23)

J^{\prime}(u_{r})v=\gamma_{r}\Psi^{\prime}(u_{r})v,\,\,\mbox{for all}\,\,v\in E,

or equivalently,

\Psi^{\prime}(u_{r})v-\Phi^{\prime}(u_{r})v=\gamma_{r}\Psi^{\prime}(u_{r})v,\,\,\mbox{for all}\,\,v\in E.

By choosing
v=u_{r}
and dividing the expression by
\Psi^{\prime}(u_{r})u_{r}
, we arrive at

1-\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(u_{r})u_{r}}{\Psi^{\prime}(u_{r})u_{r}}=\gamma_{r}.

Finally, employing (
2.22
), we obtain the equality

(2.24)

\gamma_{r}=1-\lambda_{r}.

By Proposition
2.7
and definition of
\zeta
,

\zeta^{\prime}(r)=1-\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(u_{r})u_{r}}{\Psi^{\prime}(u_{r})u_{r}}.

In other words,

(2.25)

\zeta^{\prime}(r)=\frac{J^{\prime}(u_{r})u_{r}}{\Psi^{\prime}(u_{r})u_{r}}.

Therefore, from equality above, if
u_{r}
is a critical point of
J
, then
\zeta^{\prime}(r)=0
. Now suppose that
r
is a critical point of
\zeta
, that is,
\zeta^{\prime}(r)=0
. Then

\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(u_{r})u_{r}}{\Psi^{\prime}(u_{r})u_{r}}=1.

Using (
2.22
), one gets

\lambda_{r}=1.

Then, by (
2.24
),

\gamma_{r}=0

which combines with (
2.23
) to give

J^{\prime}(u_{r})v=0,\,\mbox{for all}\,v\in E,

showing that
u_{r}
is a critical point for
J
.
∎

2.1.
Application to a semilinear problem

Let us consider the following class of semilinear problems

(2.26)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle-\Delta u=f(x,u)&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
\hskip 19.91684ptu=0&\mbox{on}&\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}(N\geq 2)
is a smooth bounded domain and
f:\Omega\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a Carathéodory function.

The classical result of Hammerstein
[
14
]
proves that if
f
satisfies a linear growth condition, and

\limsup_{|t|\to\infty}2F(x,t)/t^{2}<\lambda_{1},\ \mbox{uniformly in $x$},

then problem (
2.26
) has a solution, where
\lambda_{1}
is the first eigenvalue of laplacian operator with Dirichlet boundary condition. Some years later, in 1986, Mawhin, Ward and Willem
[
25
]
improve Hammerstein’s result by allowing the previous upper limit to “touch” the first eigenvalue of laplacian, except on a set of positive measure, that is, the authors consider that there exists
\theta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that

(2.27)

\limsup_{|t|\to\infty}2F(x,t)/t^{2}\leq\theta(x)\leq\lambda_{1},\ \mbox{uniformly in $x$},

with
\theta(x)<\lambda_{1}
on subset of
\Omega
of positive measure. The main goal of this section is to complement the works previously mentioned, by using the method (MEF). To be more precise, we are going to show that if
f
is nonnegative in
(0,\infty)
and positive for small values of
t>0
, then hypothesis (
2.27
) can be relaxed.

In this section, for each
\beta\in L^{s}(\Omega)
with
s>N/2
,
\lambda_{1}(\beta)
stands for the first eigenvalue of

(2.28)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle-\Delta u=\lambda\beta(x)u&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
\hskip 19.91684ptu=0&\mbox{on}&\partial\Omega.\end{array}\right.

Function
f
satisfies the following assumptions:

(f_{1})

There exists
C>0
such that

|f(x,t)|\leq C\left(1+|t|^{p-1}\right),\,\mbox{for all}\,t\in(0,\infty)\,\mbox{and a.e. in \,$\Omega$},

with
1<p<2^{\ast}
if
N\geq 3
and
p>1
if
N=2
;

(f_{2})

f
is nonnegative in
(0,\infty)
and positive in
(0,\varepsilon)
, for some
\varepsilon>0
;

(f_{3})

There exist functions
\alpha_{\ast}
,
\alpha^{\ast}
and
\eta
such that

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\alpha^{\ast},\eta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\ \mbox{and $\lambda_{1}(\alpha_{\ast})<1\leq\lambda_{1}(\eta)$}&\mbox{if}&f(x,0)=0,\\
\hskip 19.91684pt\eta\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)\mbox{ and $1\leq\lambda_{1}(\eta)$}&\mbox{if}&f(x,0)>0,\end{array}\right.

with

\alpha_{\ast}(x)=\liminf_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}2F(x,t)/t^{2},\ \alpha^{\ast}(x)=\limsup_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}2F(x,t)/t^{2},\ \mbox{uniformly in $x$},

and

\eta(x)=\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}2F(x,t)/t^{2},\ \mbox{uniformly in $x$}.

Some considerations are now necessary:

(1)

Observe that, from
(f_{2})
, the primitive
F^{\ast}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}f^{\ast}(x,s)\,ds
is positive and nondecreasing for all
t>0
. Thus,
0\leq\alpha_{\ast}\leq\alpha^{\ast}
and
\eta\geq 0
. Consequently,
(f_{3})
implies that
\alpha_{\ast}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and, by
[
9
]
, there exist positive first eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(\alpha_{\ast})
and
\lambda_{1}(\eta)
.

(2)

If
f(x,0)>0
, then the generalized L’Hospital rule implies that
\alpha_{\ast}=\alpha^{\ast}=\infty
. In fact, in this case

\infty=\liminf_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}f(x,t)/t\leq\liminf_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}2F(x,t)/t^{2}=\alpha_{\ast}(x).

(3)

It is important to point out that inequality
1\leq\lambda_{1}(\eta)
in
(f_{3})
is considerably weaker than (
2.27
). In fact, it is a consequence of Proposition 1.12A in
[
9
]
that (
2.27
) implies
1<\lambda_{1}(\eta)
. On the other hand, there exist functions
\eta
which satisfy
(f_{3})
and are greater than
\lambda_{1}
in some subset
\Omega_{0}\subset\Omega
with positive measure (and so, they are not covered by hypothesis (
2.27
) in
[
25
]
). In effect, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let us consider the set of positive measure

\Omega_{k}=\{x\in\Omega:dist(x,\partial\Omega)<1/k\}

and

\eta_{k}(x)=\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}\chi_{\Omega\backslash\Omega_{k}(x)}+\frac{3\lambda_{1}}{2}\chi_{\Omega_{k}}(x),

where
\chi_{A}
denotes the characteristic function of a measurable set
A\subset\Omega
. It is clear that
\eta_{k}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and

(2.30)

\eta_{k}\to\eta_{\infty}=\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}\ \mbox{in $L^{N/2}(\Omega)$}

with

(2.31)

\lambda_{1}(\eta_{\infty})=2>1.

It follows from (
2.30
), (
2.31
) and
[
9
]
, that

(2.32)

\lambda_{1}(\eta_{k})\to 2.

Thus, from (
2.32
) there exists
k_{\ast}\in\mathbb{N}
such that, defining
\eta_{\ast}:=\eta_{k_{\ast}}
, we have

(2.33)

\lambda_{1}(\eta_{\ast})>1.

Now, consider for example the function

(2.34)

f(x,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}-f(x,-t),&\mbox{if}&t<0,\\
\eta_{\ast}(x)t^{2}&\mbox{if}&0\leq t<1/4,\\
\eta_{\ast}(x)\frac{t^{3}}{(3/16)+t^{2}}&\mbox{if}&t\geq 1/4.\end{array}\right.

We have that

satisfies
(f_{1})-(f_{3})
with
\alpha_{\ast}=\alpha^{\ast}=0
and
\eta=\eta_{\ast}
(see (

\eta(x)=\frac{3\lambda_{1}}{2}>\lambda_{1}\ \mbox{in $\Omega_{k_{\ast}}$}.

In order to find a solution for (
2.26
), we will first prove the existence of a solution to the auxiliary problem

(2.35)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle-\Delta u=f^{\ast}(x,u)&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
\hskip 19.91684ptu=0&\mbox{on}&\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where

(2.36)

f^{\ast}(x,t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}f(x,0),&\mbox{if}&t<0,\\
f(x,t),&\mbox{if}&t\geq 0.\end{array}\right.

For sure,

satisfies
(f_{1})-(f_{3})
.

The functional

associated with the problem (

Now, we are going to show that

in

In the next lemmas, we prove that

Lemma 2.10
.

The functional
\Phi
satisfies
(\Phi_{1})

Proof.

Suppose that

u_{n}\rightharpoonup u\hskip 5.69046pt\mbox{in}\hskip 5.69046ptH_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

By compact embeddings, up to a subsequence, one has

u_{n}\rightarrow u\hskip 5.69046pt\mbox{in}\hskip 5.69046ptL^{p}(\Omega),

u_{n}(x)\to u(x)\quad\mbox{a.e. in}\quad\Omega,

|u_{n}|\leq g(x),\quad\mbox{a.e. in}\quad\Omega.

Therefore,

and by

Hence, by Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem

\Phi(u_{n})=\int_{\Omega}F^{\ast}(x,u_{n})dx\rightarrow\int_{\Omega}F^{\ast}(x,u)dx=\Phi(u),

showing that
\Phi
is weakly continuous. Since

is also continuous and nonnegative,
\Psi^{\prime}(u)u=\|u\|^{2}
is positive and weakly lower semicontinuous, we conclude that

is weakly upper semicontinuous.

∎

Lemma 2.13
.

Let

3.
A version of the Mountain Pass Theorem

In this section, we will provide a version of the mountain pass theorem by using the Method (MEF).

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
E
be a reflexive Banach space and
J
be a functional of class
(\mathcal{J})
in
E
. Suppose that there exist
\alpha,\rho>0
and
w\in E
, with
w\in\mathcal{G}_{R}
, such that

(H_{1})

J(u)\geq\alpha>J(0)
for all
u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\rho}
;

(H_{2})

J(w)<\alpha,
with
R>\rho
.

Then there holds the equality below

(3.1)

c_{\ast}=\max_{r\in[0,R]}\min_{u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}}J(u)\leq\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\max_{t\in[0,1]}J(\gamma(t))=c,

where
\Gamma=\{\gamma\in C([0,1],E);\gamma(0)=0,\gamma(1)=w\}
. Moreover,
c_{\ast}>\max\{J(0),J(w)\}
is a critical value of
J
.

Proof.

Since
J
is a functional of class
(\mathcal{J})
, the energy function

\zeta(r)=\min_{u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}}J(u)

is well-defined. By assumption
(H_{1})
,

\zeta(\rho)=\min_{u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\rho}}J(u)>J(0)=\zeta(0).

On the other hand, from
(H_{2})
,

\zeta(R)=\min_{u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{R}}J(u)\leq J(w)<\min_{u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\rho}}J(u)=\zeta(\rho).

So, due to the geometry of
\zeta
, there exists
r_{\ast}\in(0,R)
such that

\zeta(r_{\ast})=\max_{r\in(0,R)}\zeta(r)=\max_{r\in[0,R]}\zeta(r)\geq\zeta(\rho)>\max\{J(0),J(w)\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\zeta^{\prime}(r_{\ast})=0

Hence, by Theorem
2.8
, the number
c_{\ast}
given below

\max\{J(0),J(w)\}<c_{\ast}=\max_{r\in(0,R)}\zeta(r)=\max_{r\in[0,R]}\zeta(r)=\max_{r\in[0,R]}\min_{u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}}J(u).

is a critical value of
J
.

Next, we will prove that

c_{\ast}=\max_{r\in[0,R]}\min_{u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}}J(u)\leq\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\max_{t\in[0,1]}J(\gamma(t))=c.

Consider a path
\gamma\in\Gamma
and the critical point
r_{\ast}\in(0,R)
given above. Since
\gamma(0)=0\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r_{\ast}}
,
\gamma(1)=w\not\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r_{\ast}}
(otherwise, we would have
J(w)\geq c_{\ast}
) and
\gamma([0,1])
is connected, we have that
\gamma([0,1])\cap\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r_{\ast}}
is a nonempty set. So,

\max_{r\in[0,R]}\zeta(r)=\zeta(r_{\ast})=\min_{u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r_{\ast}}}J(u)\leq\max_{t\in[0,1]}J(\gamma(t)),\,\mbox{for all}\,\gamma\in\Gamma,

from where it follows that

\max_{r\in[0,R]}\zeta(r)\leq\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\max_{t\in[0,1]}J(\gamma(t))=c,

or equivalently,

(3.2)

c_{\ast}=\max_{r\in[0,R]}\min_{u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}}J(u)\leq\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\max_{t\in[0,1]}J(\gamma(t))=c.

∎

Next result gives a sufficient condition for the equality
c=c_{\ast}
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
E
be a reflexive Banach space and
J
be a functional of class
(\mathcal{J})
in
E
satisfying assumptions
(H_{1})-(H_{2})
given in Theorem
3.1
. If the mountain pass level
c
coincides with the infimum of
J
on the Nehari manifold
\mathcal{N}
associated to
J
, that is,

c=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{N}}J(u),\ \mbox{where}\ \mathcal{N}=\{u\in E\backslash\{0\}:J^{\prime}(u)u=0\},

then
c_{\ast}=c=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{N}}J(u)
.

Proof.

Since
c_{\ast}
is a critical value of
J
, from Theorem
3.1
, there exists
u_{\ast}\in E
such that
J(u_{\ast})=c_{\ast}>J(0)
. Therefore,
u_{\ast}\neq 0
, and
u_{\ast}\in\mathcal{N}
. Showing that
c_{\ast}\geq\inf_{u\in\mathcal{N}}J(u)
. The result now follows from inequality (
3.1
).
∎

Situations where
c
coincides with
\inf_{u\in\mathcal{N}}J(u)
are well known in the literature and cover a large class of concrete problems in applications. In these particular cases, Corollary
3.2
provides a new characterization for the mountain pass level constant
c
, then it can be seen as a maxmin level.

Another possible advantage to have a “mountain pass theorem” for maxmin critical levels is in the determination of ground state critical points for
J
, that is, a critical point
w\in\mathcal{N}
satisfying
J(w)=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{N}}J(u)
. Next result gives a step in this direction.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
E
be a reflexive Banach space and
J
be a functional of class
(\mathcal{J})
in
E
satisfying assumptions
(H_{1})-(H_{2})
given in Theorem
3.1
. If,

(3.3)

J(u)\geq J(tu),\ \mbox{for all}\ t>0\ \mbox{and}\ u\in\mathcal{N},

then
J
has a ground state critical point.

Proof.

As we have seen in the proof of previous corollary,

(3.4)

c_{\ast}\geq\inf_{u\in\mathcal{N}}J(u).

To conclude the proof, it is enough to prove the opposite inequality. From Theorem
3.1
, there exists a critical point
u_{\ast}
for
J
satisfying
J(u_{\ast})=c_{\ast}=\zeta(r_{\ast})
. Let
u\in\mathcal{N}
. It follows from
(\Psi_{4})
that there exists
t_{u}(r_{\ast})>0
such that
t_{u}(r_{\ast})u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r_{\ast}}
. Thus, by (
3.3
),

(3.5)

J(u)\geq J(t_{u}(r_{\ast})u)\geq J(u_{\ast})=c_{\ast}.

where the last inequality in (
3.5
) is a consequence of
J(u_{\ast})=\min_{u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r_{\ast}}}J(u)
. Since
u\in\mathcal{N}
is arbitrary in (
3.5
), we conclude that

(3.6)

\inf_{u\in\mathcal{N}}J(u)\geq c_{\ast}.

The result follows now from (
3.4
) and (
3.6
).

∎

4.
Some applications

4.1.
A general nonlinear problem

In this section we are going to investigate the following problem

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle-\Delta u=f(x,u)&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
\hskip 19.91684ptu=0&\mbox{on}&\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}(N\geq 2)
is a smooth bounded domain and

is a Carathéodory function satisfying the growth condition

, introduced in Subsection

.

In
[
2
]
, Ambrosetti and Rabinowitz proved existence of nontrivial solution for (

:

(F_{2})

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{f(x,t)}{|t|}=0
;

(AR)

there are constants

and

such that

0<\nu F(x,t)\leq tf(x,t),|t|\geq r,\ F(x,t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(x,s)\,ds.

Condition (AR) is very important to provide some compactness for Palais-Smale sequences, which is a crucial point in the Mountain Pass Theorem presented in
[
2
]
. However, despite its technical relevance, hypothesis (AR) is quite restrictive and in the last years, many authors have concentrate efforts in the search for weaker conditions that still guarantee the existence of nontrivial solution for (

(4.2)

F(x,t)\geq c_{3}|t|^{\nu}-c_{4},\ x\in\Omega,\ |t|\geq r.

In 1994, Costa and Magalhães
[
8
]
studied (

) under the following assumptions on

\displaystyle\limsup_{|t|\rightarrow\infty}\frac{2F(x,t)}{|t|^{q}}\leq b<\infty

\displaystyle\liminf_{|t|\rightarrow\infty}\frac{H(x,t)}{|t|^{\mu}}\geq a>0

in
\Omega
;

(F_{3})

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{2F(x,t)}{t^{2}}\leq\alpha<\lambda_{1}<\beta\leq\liminf_{|t|\rightarrow\infty}\frac{2F(x,t)}{t^{2}}

x
in
\Omega
,

where

,

and, in practice,

is smaller than

in

. Under hypotheses

,

and
(F_{3})

,

\frac{\sigma(\rho_{n})}{\rho_{n}^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}-\int_{[\rho_{n}v_{n}\leq R]}\frac{F^{\ast}(x,\rho_{n}v_{n})}{(\rho_{n}v_{n})^{2}}v_{n}^{2}\chi_{[v_{n}>0]}dx-\frac{1}{\rho^{2}_{n}}\int_{[\rho_{n}v_{n}>R]}F^{\ast}(x,\rho_{n}v_{n})dx,

It follows from

that there exists

for all

and some

. So, from

) and (

\frac{1}{\rho^{2}_{n}}\int_{[\rho_{n}v_{n}>R]}F^{\ast}(x,\rho_{n}v_{n})dx\to 0.

On the other hand, since

is nonnegative, conditions
(f_{1})
and
(f_{3}^{\prime})
yield that there exists

such that

\left|\frac{F^{\ast}(x,t)}{t^{2}}\right|\leq K,\quad\forall\ |t|\leq R\quad\mbox{and a.e. in }\quad\Omega.

From (
4.11
) and (
4.8
),

(4.12)

\left|\frac{F^{\ast}(x,\rho_{n}v_{n}(x))}{(\rho_{n}v_{n}(x))^{2}}v_{n}^{2}(x)\chi_{[v_{n}>0]}(x)\right|\leq|h(x)|^{2}K\hskip 2.84544pt\hskip 2.84544pt\mbox{a.e.}\hskip 2.84544pt\hskip 2.84544pt\mbox{in}\hskip 2.84544pt\hskip 2.84544pt[\rho_{n}v_{n}(x)\leq R].

If

For the case

to get

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sigma(\rho_{n})}{\rho_{n}^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\alpha(x)v_{0}^{2}dx.

Thus, by

\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sigma(\rho_{n})}{\rho_{n}^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(\alpha)}\right)\|v_{0}\|^{2}>0.

Therefore, there exists
\rho

holds.

Now let

\frac{J(re_{1})}{r^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}-\int_{\Omega}\frac{F^{\ast}(x,re_{1})}{(re_{1})^{2}}e_{1}^{2}dx.

By other side, if

In any case, given

4.2.
A Berestycki-Lions type problem

In 1983, Berestycki and Lions
[
6

were the first to solve the problem

(4.14)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle-\Delta u=g(u)\hskip 2.84544pt\hskip 2.84544pt\mbox{in}\hskip 2.84544pt\hskip 2.84544pt\mathbb{R}^{N},\\
\hskip 11.38092ptu\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\end{array}\right.

without using the Ambrosseti and Rabinowitz condition. However, the method used in
[
6
]
is not valid for non-autonomous problems, even though the function
g(x,u)
is radially symmetric with respect to

In 2009, Azzollini and Pomponio
[
4

, motivated by approach explored in Jeanjean
[
16
]
, considered the problem above with
g(x,t)=f(t)-V(x)t
, which can be rewritten of the form

(4.15)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle-\Delta u+V(x)u=f(u)\hskip 2.84544pt\hskip 2.84544pt\mbox{in}\hskip 2.84544pt\hskip 2.84544pt\mathbb{R}^{N},\\
\hskip 11.38092ptu\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\end{array}\right.

assuming that
N\geq 1
and
V\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R})
satisfying the following conditions:

(V_{1}^{\prime})

V(x)=V(|x|)
;

(V_{2}^{\prime})

V(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and the inequality is strict somewhere;

(V_{3}^{\prime})

\|(\nabla V(\cdot)|\cdot)^{+}\|_{N/2}<2S
, where
\displaystyle S=\inf_{u\in{D}^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\setminus\{0\}}\frac{|\nabla u|_{2}^{2}}{|u|_{2^{\ast}}^{2}}

(V_{4}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\lim_{|x|\rightarrow\infty}V(x)=0
.

In the present section, we intend to use Theorem
3.1
to complement the main result found in
[
4
]
, in the sense we will only consider the following conditions on potential
V
.

(V_{1})

V(x)=V(|x|)
;

(V_{2})

There exist
V_{0},V_{\infty}>0
such that
V_{0}\leq V(x)\leq V_{\infty},\,\forall\,x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
.

By changing
(V_{2}^{\prime})
–
(V_{4}^{\prime})
for
(V_{2})
, it is possible to obtain functions that do not satisfy the conditions of
[
4
]
, but that satisfies our hypotheses, see for example the function

V(x)=\frac{|x|+k_{1}}{|x|+k_{2}},\hskip 2.84544pt\hskip 2.84544pt\mbox{with}\hskip 2.84544pt\hskip 2.84544pt0<k_{1}<k_{2}.

With respect to
f\in C(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
, let us consider the following assumptions:

(f_{1})

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{f(t)}{t}=0\hskip 14.22636pt\mbox{and}\hskip 14.22636pt\limsup_{|t|\rightarrow\infty}\frac{|f(t)|}{|t|^{p-1}}<\infty,\,\,\mbox{where}\,\,2<p<2^{\ast};

(f_{2})

There exists
t_{0}>0
such that

(f_{3})

f(t)>0

In the sequel, we will restrict the functional

(4.16)

H^{1}_{rad}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\hookrightarrow L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\,\,\mbox{with}\,\,2<p<2^{\ast}.

As in the previous section, let us consider the auxiliary problem

(4.17)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle-\Delta u+V(x)u=f^{\ast}(u)&\mbox{in}&\mathbb{R}^{N},\\
\hskip 19.91684ptu\in H^{1}_{rad}(\mathbb{R}^{N}),\end{array}\right.

where

f^{\ast}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0,&\mbox{if}&t<0,\\
f(t),&\mbox{if}&t\geq 0.\end{array}\right.

A simple computation gives that

satisfies the same conditions as

.

Due to
(f_{3})
,
F^{\ast}
does not change sign in

. Then, from
(f_{2})
,

is positive and increasing for all

. The functional

where

Hereafter, we will denote by

the following norm in

By condition
(V_{2})

Next, we will show that

belongs to class

.

Lemma 4.2
.

The functional
\Phi
satisfies

.

Proof.

By

From this inequality,

where

(4.20)

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\frac{P(t)}{Q(t)}=0.

Finally, assuming that

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|Q(u_{n}(x))|dx<\infty.

Therefore, (
4.19

[
6

to get

\Phi(u_{n})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}F^{\ast}(u_{n})dx\rightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}F^{\ast}(u_{0})dx=\Phi(u_{0}),

proving that
\Phi
is weakly continuous. It is possible to obtain the same result for

.

∎

For the other hypotheses of
\Phi
, the procedure is similar to the previous application. Moreover, it is also true that, fixed

u_{0}\in\mathcal{G}_{r}=\left\{u\in\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r};\hskip 2.84544pt\Phi(u)=\max_{u\in\partial\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{r}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}F^{\ast}(u)dx\right\},

Theorem 4.3
.

Under hypotheses
(V_{1})
–
(V_{2})
and
(f_{1})
–

Proof.

In what follows, we will prove that

satisfies
(H_{1})
and
(H_{2})
in Theorem

. Indeed, by (

),

J(u)\geq\|u\|_{\ast}^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}|u|_{2}^{2}-\frac{C}{p}|u|_{p}^{p}.

Fixing

As

, for
\rho
small enough, there exists
\alpha>0
such that

proving
(H_{1})
.

On the other hand, using
(f_{2})
, note that there exists
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}F(\varphi)dx-\frac{V_{\infty}}{2}|\varphi|_{2}^{2}>0.

By taking

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla w|^{2}dx=\frac{1}{t^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left|\nabla\varphi\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)\right|^{2}dx=t^{N-2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}|\nabla\varphi(x)|^{2}dx

4.3.
An anisotropic equation

Consider the problem

(4.23)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{i}(|\partial_{i}u|^{p_{i}-2}\partial_{i}u)=f(u)&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
\hskip 96.73918ptu=0&\mbox{on}&\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
N\geq 2
,
p_{i}
’s are ordered , that is,
1<p_{1}\leq p_{2}\leq...\leq p_{N}
and
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function with
f(0)=0
. In 2009, Di Castro and Montefusco
[
10
]
solved the problem (
4.23
) considering
f(u)=\lambda|u|^{q-2}u
, with
\lambda>0
and
p_{1}<q<p_{N}
. Note that the function chosen by the authors already satisfies the Ambrosseti and Rabinowitz condition. So, our goal is to solve (
4.23
) using a more general function.

In our case,
f
satisfies the following conditions:

(f_{1})

There exists
C>0
such that

|f(t)|\leq C\left(1+|t|^{q-1}\right)\,\mbox{for all}\,t\in(0,\infty),\,\mbox{with}\,\,p_{1}<q<p_{N};

(f_{2})

0\leq\limsup_{t\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{F(t)}{|t|^{p_{N}}}<\frac{\xi}{\Theta^{p_{N}}}\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,\,\,\mathcal{S}\leq\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}\frac{F(t)}{|t|^{p_{N}}},

where
F(x,t)=\int_{0}^{t}f(x,s)ds
and
\mathcal{S},\Theta
and
\xi
are given in (
4.24
), (
4.26
) and (
4.25
) respectively.

(f_{3})

f(t)>0
for all
t>0
.

As function space we will consider the space
W_{0}^{1,\vec{p}}(\Omega)
endowed with the norm

\|u\|_{\vec{p}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\partial_{i}u\|_{p_{i}},

where
\vec{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{N})
.

Recall that
W_{0}^{1,\vec{p}}(\Omega)
is a reflexive Banach space which is compactly embedded in
L^{s}(\Omega)
for all
s\in[1,p_{\infty})
(see
[
11
]
), where

p_{\infty}=\max\{p_{N},\bar{p}^{\ast}\}
,
\bar{p}=\displaystyle N/\left(\displaystyle\sum^{N}_{i=1}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\right)
is the harmonic mean of the
p_{i}
’s and

p^{\ast}:=\displaystyle\frac{N}{\left(\displaystyle\sum^{N}_{i=1}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\right)-1}=\displaystyle\frac{N\overline{p}}{N-\overline{p}}.

Moreover, we fix
\Theta,\xi>0
satisfying

(4.24)

|u|_{p_{N}}\leq\Theta\|u\|_{\vec{p}},\quad\forall u\in W_{0}^{1,\vec{p}}(\Omega)

and

(4.25)

\xi\|u\|^{p_{N}}_{\vec{p}}\leq\frac{1}{p_{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\partial_{i}u\|^{p_{N}}_{p_{i}},\quad\forall u\in W_{0}^{1,\vec{p}}(\Omega).

The existence of such a constant
\Theta
is guaranteed in
[
11
, Theorem 1]
.

Hereafter, we assume that
p_{N}<\bar{p}^{\ast}
, and so, we have the compact embedding
W_{0}^{1,\vec{p}}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{p_{N}}(\Omega)
. Moreover, we will denote by
\mathcal{S}>0
the following constant

(4.26)

\mathcal{S}=\min_{u\in W_{0}^{1,\vec{p}}(\Omega)}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\partial_{i}u\|^{p_{i}}_{p_{i}}\;:\;\|u^{+}\|_{p_{N}}=1\right\}.

Theorem 4.4
.

Under hypotheses
(f_{1})-(f_{3})
, problem (
4.23

Proof.

We will first prove the existence of a solution to the auxiliary problem

(4.27)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\partial_{i}(|\partial_{i}u|^{p_{i}-2}\partial_{i}u)=f^{\ast}(u)&\mbox{in}&\Omega,\\
\hskip 96.73918ptu=0&\mbox{on}&\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
f^{\ast}
is defined by

f^{\ast}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0,&\mbox{if}&t<0,\\
f(t),&\mbox{if}&t\geq 0.\end{array}\right.

Due to
(f_{3})
,
F^{\ast}
does not change sign in
(0,\infty)
. Then, from
(f_{2})
,
F^{\ast}
is positive and increasing for all
t>0
. The functional

J(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{i}u|^{p_{i}}dx-\int_{\Omega}F^{\ast}(u)dx.

In the sequel

Lemma 4.5
.

Hypothesis
(\Psi_{5})
is satisfied in
W_{0}^{1,\vec{p}}(\Omega)
.

Proof.

Let
(u_{n})\in W_{0}^{1,\vec{p}}(\Omega)
with

and
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty}\Psi(u_{n})=\Psi(u)
. Then, by the weak convergence,

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{i}u|^{p_{i}}dx=\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{i}u_{n}|^{p_{i}}dx\geq\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{i}u_{n}|^{p_{i}}dx\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{i}u|^{p_{i}}dx,

hence, for a some subsequence, one has

\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{i}u|^{p_{i}}dx=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{i}u_{n}|^{p_{i}}dx\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{i}u_{n}|^{p_{i}}dx\geq\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{i}u|^{p_{i}}dx,

\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{i}u_{n}|^{p_{i}}dx>\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{i}u|^{p_{i}}dx,

\limsup_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{i}u_{n}|^{p_{i}}dx=\liminf_{n\to+\infty}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{i}u_{n}|^{p_{i}}dx=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\int_{\Omega}|\partial_{i}u|^{p_{i}}dx,\quad\forall i\in\{1,...N\}.

Using the fact that

is uniformly convex and that
\partial_{i}u_{n}\rightharpoonup\partial_{i}u
in

, we derive that

\partial_{i}u_{n}\to\partial_{i}u\quad\mbox{in}\quad L^{p_{i}}(\Omega),\quad\forall i\in\{1,...N\},

showing that

finishing the proof.

∎

Similar to what was done in the previous sections, it is possible to prove that

, we get

where
