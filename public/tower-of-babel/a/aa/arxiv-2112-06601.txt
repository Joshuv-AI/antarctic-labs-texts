New examples of harmonic maps to the hyperbolic plane via Bäcklund transformation
G. Polychrou, E. Papageorgiou, A. Fotiadis, C. Daskaloyannis

Abstract
. We study harmonic maps from a subset of the complex plane to a subset of the hyperbolic plane. In [ 11 ] , harmonic maps are related to the sinh-Gordon equation and a Bäcklund transformation is introduced, which connects solutions of the sinh-Gordon and sine-Gordon equation. We develop this machinery in order to construct new harmonic maps to the hyperbolic plane.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
35A30, 58J70, 58J72

1.
Introduction and Statement of the Results

This article has been motivated by the following open problem: how can we construct a harmonic map explicitly?

The construction of harmonic maps from a Riemann surface to the hyperbolic plane is a classical problem. Many beautiful results have been achieved in this direction, for example, Wolf’s parametrization of Teichmüller space via harmonic maps
[
33
]
, the study of Au-Tam-Wan
[
3
]
and others of harmonic maps to ideal polygons with polynomial Hopf differential, the construction of a harmonic diffeomorphism from the complex plane which solved a conjecture of Schoen
[
8
]
, and so on.

In this paper, our aim is to construct harmonic maps between Riemann surfaces when the curvature in the target is a negative constant, say
-1
. We should emphasize that our construction is local, thus we consider an open simply connected set that contains the origin and we consider the target to be a subset of the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. For that, we develop some of the machinery introduced in
[
11
]
, which connects the harmonic map problem with elliptic versions of the sinh-Gordon and sine-Gordon equation.

Let
w=w(x,y)
be a solution of the sinh-Gordon equation

(1)

\Delta w=2\sinh(2w)

where
\Delta=\partial_{xx}^{2}+\partial_{yy}^{2}
is the Laplacian with the flat metric and let
u=u(z,\bar{z})
be a solution of the Beltrami equation

(2)

\frac{\partial_{\bar{z}}u}{\partial_{z}u}=e^{-2w},

and
z=x+iy
lie in an open simply connected subset
\Omega
of
\mathbb{C}
where the map
u
is a well defined
C^{2}
map. Without loss of generality we assume that
\Omega
contains the origin. Then,
u
is a harmonic map, if the curvature of the target is
-1

[
11
, Theorem 1, Corollary 2]
.

Definition 1
.

For
w
and
u
as above, we say that
u
is the harmonic map that corresponds to the solution
w
of the sinh-Gordon (
1
).

From now on, consider the target surface fixed and realized as the hyperbolic half plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Using a specific coordinate system on the domain (see Section
2
for more details), the harmonic map equation under study becomes

(3)

\frac{\partial_{z}u\partial_{z}\bar{u}}{S^{2}}=1.

This is possible because the Hopf differential is assumed to be non vanishing.

In
[
11
]
, it is proved that the system

(4)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}w-\partial_{y}\theta

\displaystyle=-2\sinh w\sin\theta

(5)

\displaystyle\partial_{y}w+\partial_{x}\theta

\displaystyle=-2\cosh w\cos\theta,

is a
Bäcklund transformation
that connects a solution
w=w(x,y)
of the sinh-Gordon equation (
1
) and a solution
\theta=\theta(x,y)
of the sine-Gordon equation

(6)

\Delta\theta=-2\sin(2\theta).

This motivates us to define the following class of functions.

Definition 2
.

We say that the pair of functions
(w,\theta)
is in the class
(BT)
, if the functions
w
and
\theta
satisfy (
4
)-(
5
). Consequently,
w
is a solution of the sinh-Gordon equation (
1
) and
\theta
is a solution of the sine-Gordon equation (
6
).

From now on we assume that
(w,\theta)\in(BT)
.
Set

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle=I_{1}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\cosh w(t,0)\sin\theta(t,0)dt,

\displaystyle I_{2}

\displaystyle=I_{2}(x,y)=\int_{0}^{y}\sinh w(x,s)\cos\theta(x,s)ds

\displaystyle I_{3}

\displaystyle=I_{3}(x)=\int_{0}^{x}e^{2I_{1}(t)}\cosh w(t,0)\cos\theta(t,0)dt,

\displaystyle I_{4}

\displaystyle=I_{4}(x,y)=e^{2I_{1}(x)}\int_{0}^{y}e^{2I_{2}(x,s)}\sinh w(x,s)\sin\theta(x,s)ds.

We first prove the following result.

Proposition 3
.

Define the function
S
by

\displaystyle S(x,y)=S(0,0)e^{2(I_{1}+I_{2})},

and the function
R
by

\displaystyle R(x,y)=R(0,0)+2S(0,0)(I_{3}-I_{4}).

Then,

u(x,y)=R(x,y)+iS(x,y)

is the harmonic map that corresponds to
w
.
The domain of
R
and
S
is the largest possible open simply connected subset of
\mathbb{C}
containing the origin so that the above expressions make sense.

Remark.

Observe that the function
S
, by its definition preserves the sign of the initial data
S(0,0)
. Also, the image of
u
is a subset of
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. However, these maps are not necessarily injective on their domain, as can be easily seen by Example
11
.

Therefore, given a pair of functions
(w,\theta)
that satisfies the Bäcklund transformation, there is an implicit formula for a harmonic map to the hyperbolic plane that involves the integrals
I_{1},I_{2},I_{3},I_{4}
. We provide an example of a harmonic map, first studied in
[
11
, Section 7]
, that we recover by the algorithm of Proposition
3
.

We next characterize the solutions
w
of the sinh-Gordon equation that are of the form

(7)

w(x,y)=2\arctanh({F(x)}{G(y)}),

for some non-constant functions
F
and
G
. These functions, in general, turn out to be elliptic (only in certain cases they reduce to elementary functions). We also prove that the initial value problem
\Delta w=2\sinh(2w)
,
\partial_{y}w(x,0)=0
, admits a solution of the form (
7
), see Proposition
13
for a class of such solutions. In this case, we determine
\theta
by considering a subclass of
(BT)
.

Definition 4
.

We say that the pair of functions
(w,\theta)
is in the class
(BT_{0})
, when the pair is in
(BT)
and
\partial_{y}w(x,0)=0
,
\theta(0,0)=\frac{\pi}{2}
.

For this selection of
(w,\theta)
, and
w
a solution of the form (
7
), we apply Proposition
3
in order to prove our first main result, which provides an explicit formula for a harmonic map that corresponds to
w
. This is a special case of the more general problem of finding all solutions of the sinh-Gordon equation, and the corresponding harmonic maps. Thus, it is a step (although small) towards the solution of the harmonic map problem.

Next, we state our first main result.

Theorem 5
.

Assume that
w(x,y)=2\arctanh({F(x)}{G(y)})
,
b(x)=\frac{F^{\prime}(x)}{2F(x)}
and
(w,\theta)\in(BT_{0})
.
Define the functions
S
and
R
on the largest possible open simply connected subset of
\mathbb{C}
containing the origin so that

S(x,y)=S(0,0)\frac{e^{2X(x)}(\sin{\theta(x,y)}+b(x))}{1+b(x)},

and

R(x,y)=R(0,0)+S(0,0)\frac{e^{2X(x)}\cos{\theta(x,y)}}{1+b(x)},

make sense, where
X=X(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\cosh w(t,0)dt
.
Then
u(x,y)=R(x,y)+iS(x,y)
is the harmonic map that corresponds to
w
.

Using the machinery of Theorem
5
, we construct an entirely new harmonic map to the hyperbolic plane. At this point, it is worth mentioning that the initial condition
\theta(0,0)=\frac{\pi}{2}
was chosen just to facilitate computations; any other initial condition for
\theta
at the origin would provide a corresponding harmonic map.

In
[
11
]
, the approach pursued by the authors on the construction of harmonic maps, is to first consider a one-soliton (i.e.,
w=w(x)
) solution of the sinh-Gordon equation and then solve the Beltrami equation (
2
) to find a harmonic map that corresponds to
w
. However, if
w(x,y)=2\arctanh({F(x)}{G(y)})
, and both
F,G
are elliptic functions, then this Beltrami equation turns out to be equivalent to a Riccati equation. Therefore, explicit construction of harmonic maps by this approach is rather implausible. Instead, in place of the Beltrami equation, we use the Bäcklund transformation combined with Theorem
5
, to provide an explicit formula for a harmonic map in terms of the functions
w
and
\theta
.

Next, instead of considering solutions to the sinh-Gordon equation first, we investigate the case of solutions to the sine-Gordon equation as a starting point. In particular, we prove that if
\theta=\theta(x)
is a solution to the sine-Gordon equation (
6
), then the function
w
determined by the Bäcklund transformation is of the form (
7
). We then provide an explicit formula for the corresponding harmonic map.

Our second main result is the following.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\theta=\theta(x)
be a solution to the sine-Gordon equation and let
w
be the associated solution to the Bäcklund transformation. Set
a=2\cos\theta(0)+\theta^{\prime}(0)
,
b=2\cos\theta(0)-\theta^{\prime}(0)
, and
k\in\mathbb{C}
such that

\tanh\frac{w(0,0)}{2}=-{\sqrt{\frac{a}{b}}\tan(k)}
. Then, the harmonic map
u=R+iS
that corresponds to
w
is given by

\displaystyle R(x,y)

\displaystyle=R(0,0)+S(0,0)\cosh^{2}\frac{w(0,0)}{2}J\left(\frac{2\cos\theta(x)-\theta^{\prime}(x)}{b}\right)

\displaystyle+2S(0,0)\frac{\sin\theta(x)(\cos(\sqrt{ab}y+2k)-\cos(2k))}{2\cos\theta(0)\cos(2k)-\theta^{\prime}(0)},

\displaystyle S(x,y)

\displaystyle=S(0,0)\frac{2\cos\theta(x)\cos(\sqrt{ab}y+2k)-\theta^{\prime}(x)}{2\cos\theta(0)\cos(2k)-\theta^{\prime}(x)},

where

J(t)=\int_{1}^{t}\frac{u^{2}+\tanh^{2}\frac{w(0,0)}{2}}{u^{2}}\frac{bu^{2}+a}{\sqrt{2(8-ab)u^{2}-a^{2}-b^{2}u^{4}}}du.

The domain of
R
and
S
is the largest possible open simply connected subset of
\mathbb{C}
containing the origin so that the above expressions make sense.

As an application of Theorem
6
, we construct an entirely new example of a harmonic map from an open simply connected set to a subset of the hyperbolic plane, when the Hopf differential is non vanishing.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we discuss some necessary preliminaries.

Let
u:M\rightarrow N
be a map between
Riemann surfaces
(M,g)
,
(N,h)
. The map
u
is locally represented by

u=u(z,\bar{z})=R+iS
, where
z=x+iy
. From now on, we use the standard notation:

\partial_{z}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}-i\partial_{y}),\quad\partial_{\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}+i\partial_{y}).

Recall that isothermal coordinates on a Riemannian manifold are local coordinates where the metric is conformal to the Euclidean metric. The existence of isothermal coordinates on an arbitrary surface with a real analytic metric is a well-known fact, first proved by Gauss (see for instance
[
13
, Section 8, p.396]
).
Consider an isothermal coordinate system
(x,y)
on
M
such that

g=e^{f(z,\bar{z})}|dz|^{2},

where
z=x+iy
, and an isothermal coordinate system
(R,S)
on

N
such that

h=e^{F(u,\bar{u})}|du|^{2},

where
u=R+iS
.
The Gauss curvature on the target is given by the formula

K_{N}(u,\bar{u})=-\frac{1}{2}\Delta F(u,\bar{u})e^{-F(u,\bar{u})}.

In isothermal coordinates, a map between two surfaces is harmonic if and only if it satisfies

(8)

\partial_{z\bar{z}}u+\partial_{u}F(u,\bar{u})\partial_{z}u\partial_{\bar{z}}u=0,

see
[
13
, Section 8, p.397]
. Note that this equation only depends on the conformal structure of
N
.

The Hopf differential of
u
is given by

\Lambda(z)dz^{2}=\left(e^{F(u,\bar{u})}\partial_{z}u\partial_{z}\bar{u}\right)dz^{2}.

It is well-known that if
u
is holomoprhic or antiholomorphic then the zeros of
\partial_{z}u
and
\partial_{\bar{z}}u
are isolated of finite order,
[
29
, Proposition 2.1]
. Due to the local nature of our study,
we may assume from now on that
\Lambda
does not vanish locally
.

We will make use of the following proposition.

Proposition 7
.

A necessary and sufficient condition for a
C^{2}
map
u
with non vanishing Hopf differential, and an almost everywhere non vanishing Jacobian, to be a harmonic map, is that

(9)

e^{F(u,\bar{u})}\partial_{z}u\partial_{z}\bar{u}=e^{-\mu(z)},

where
\mu(z)
is a holomorphic function.

Proof.

We sketch the proof for the reader’s convenience.
Assume first that
u
is a harmonic map. Then the harmonic map equation (
8
) is equivalent to

\partial_{\bar{z}}(e^{F(u,\bar{u})}\partial_{z}u\,\partial_{z}\bar{u})=0.

Then, the claim follows under the assumption that
u
has non vanishing Hopf differential.

For the converse direction, let
e^{-\mu(z)}
be holomorphic. Then, observe that

\text{if}\quad e^{F(u,\bar{u})}\partial_{z}u\,\partial_{z}{\bar{u}}=e^{-\mu(z)}\quad\mbox{then}\quad e^{F(u,\bar{u})}\partial_{\bar{z}}u\,\partial_{\bar{z}}{\bar{u}}=e^{-\overline{\mu(z)}}.

Thus, differentiating these equations in terms of
\bar{z},z
respectively, we find

\partial_{z}\bar{u}\;(\partial^{2}_{z\bar{z}}u+\partial_{u}F\,\partial_{z}u\,\partial_{\bar{z}}u)+\partial_{z}u\;(\partial^{2}_{z\bar{z}}\bar{u}+\partial_{\bar{u}}F\,\partial_{z}\bar{u}\,\partial_{\bar{z}}\bar{u})=0,

and

\partial_{\bar{z}}\bar{u}\;(\partial^{2}_{z\bar{z}}u+\partial_{u}F\,\partial_{z}u\,\partial_{\bar{z}}u)+\partial_{\bar{z}}u\;(\partial^{2}_{z\bar{z}}\bar{u}+\partial_{\bar{u}}F\,\partial_{z}\bar{u}\,\partial_{\bar{z}}\bar{u})=0.

Taking into account that the Jacobian

\|u_{z}\|^{2}-\|u_{\bar{z}}\|^{2}=\partial_{x}\,R\partial_{y}S-\partial_{x}\,S\partial_{y}R

is non vanishing almost everywhere, we deduce that the harmonic map equation hold true almost everywhere. Then, under the
C^{2}
assumption on
u
we deduce that
u
is harmonic everywhere.

∎

A fundamental result in the theory of harmonic maps, which connects the harmonic map problem with the sinh-Gordon equation, is the following.

Proposition 8

(
[
26
]
,
[
33
]
)
.

Let
u:M\rightarrow N
be a harmonic map. Then, it satisfies the Beltrami
equation

\frac{\partial_{\bar{z}}u}{\partial_{z}u}=e^{-2w+i\phi},

and
\phi
is a harmonic function, i.e.
\partial^{2}_{z\bar{z}}\phi=0
. Furthermore, if
\psi
is the conjugate harmonic function to
\phi
, then

K_{N}=-\frac{2\partial^{2}_{z\bar{z}}w}{\sinh 2w}e^{\psi},

where
K_{N}
is the curvature of the target manifold
N
.

On the domain, we can choose a specific coordinate system in order to considerably facilitate the calculations. This
specific
system is defined by the conformal transformation

Z=\int e^{-\mu(z)/2}\,dz.

This transformation is well defined since we work on a simply connected subset and we can assume that the Hopf differential is not vanishing since its zeroes are isolated.
In this specific system the above equations simplify by considering
\mu(z)=0
. In other words, under the assumption that the Hopf differential does not vanish, we may assume that
\mu=0
.
In particular, the harmonic map condition (
9
) becomes

(10)

e^{F(u,\bar{u})}\partial_{z}u\partial_{z}\bar{u}=1.

It follows from Proposition
8
that the harmonic map
u
determined by (
10
) satisfies the Beltrami equation (
2
), where in turn the function
w(z,\bar{z})
occurring in the Beltrami coefficient satisfies the sinh-Gordon equation (
1
). Conversely, as we have already mentioned, if
w
is a solution of the sinh-Gordon equation (
1
) and
u
is a solution of the Beltrami equation (
2
), then,
u
is a harmonic map,
[
11
]
.
As a result, there is a classification of harmonic diffeomorphisms via the classification of the solutions of the sinh-Gordon equation. For further details about the association of the harmonic map problem to the sinh-Gordon equation, we refer to
[
11
, Section 3]
.
From now on, we use the aforementioned specific coordinate system.

We assume that the target is the upper half hyperbolic plane

\mathbb{H}^{2}=\{R+iS:\;R\in\mathbb{R},\;S>0\},

equipped with the hyperbolic metric

h=\frac{dR^{2}+dS^{2}}{S^{2}}

of curvature
-1
.
As mentioned earlier, in the specific coordinate system, the map
u=R+iS
is harmonic if
(
3
) holds true, or equivalently if the following system is satisfied:

(11)

\partial_{x}R\;\partial_{y}R+\partial_{x}S\;\partial_{y}S=0

(12)

\frac{(\partial_{x}R)^{2}+(\partial_{x}S)^{2}-(\partial_{y}R)^{2}-(\partial_{y}S)^{2}}{S^{2}}=4.

Finally, as we have described, our approach has as its starting point the Bäcklund transformation (
4
)-(
5
). For computational reasons, let us point out that it is more convenient to verify that a pair
(w,\theta)
is a solution if the following system holds:

\displaystyle\partial_{x}W\;(1+\Theta^{2})-\partial_{y}\Theta\;(1-W^{2})

\displaystyle=-4W\Theta,

\displaystyle\partial_{y}W\;(1+\Theta^{2})+\partial_{x}\Theta\;(1-W^{2})

\displaystyle=-(1+W^{2})(1-\Theta^{2}),

where
W=\tanh\frac{w}{2}
and
\Theta=\tan\frac{\theta}{2}
.

3.
Proof of Proposition
3

In this section we consider
(w,\theta)\in(BT)
. In other words,
w
and
\theta
are related by the Bäcklund transformation. Our aim is to construct a harmonic map that corresponds to
w
. As usual, the target is assumed to be the upper half-plane equipped with the hyperbolic metric.
We first observe that the following result holds true.

Lemma 9
.

Assume that
(w,\theta)\in(BT)
. Then, there exist functions
R
and
S
such that

(13)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}S

\displaystyle=2S\cosh w\sin\theta

(14)

\displaystyle\partial_{y}S

\displaystyle=2S\sinh w\cos\theta

(15)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}R

\displaystyle=2S\cosh w\cos\theta

(16)

\displaystyle\partial_{y}R

\displaystyle=-2S\sinh w\sin\theta.

Proof.

The existence of the functions
R
and
S
is ensured by the Bäcklund transformation of
w
and
\theta
. Indeed, the compatibility conditions

\partial_{xy}^{2}S=\partial_{yx}^{2}S,\quad\partial_{xy}^{2}R=\partial_{yx}^{2}R

follow by the Bäcklund transformation equations (
4
)-(
5
).
∎

Remark.
The Jacobian of the map
u
is
J(u)=2S^{2}\sinh{2w}
.

Our next goal is to prove the following result, which plays a key role for the proof of Proposition
3
.

Lemma 10
.

Assume that
(w,\theta)\in(BT)
and let
(R,S)
satisfy the system of equations (
13
)-(
16
).
Then,

u(x,y)=R(x,y)+iS(x,y)

is the harmonic map to the hyperbolic plane that corresponds to
w
.

Proof.

Consider

u=R+iS,

and observe that

\partial_{z}u=\frac{1}{2}((\partial_{x}R+\partial_{y}S)+i(\partial_{x}S-\partial_{y}R)).

Then (
13
)-(
16
), imply that

(17)

\partial_{z}u=S(\cosh w+\sinh w)(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)=Se{{}^{w}}e^{i\theta}.

Similarly, we compute

(18)

\partial_{\bar{z}}u=Se^{-w}e^{i\theta}.

By (
17
) and (
18
), it follows that

\frac{\partial_{\bar{z}}u}{\partial_{z}u}=e^{-2w}.

Furthermore, by (
10
), the conformal factor in the target metric is equal to

e^{F(R,S)}=\frac{1}{\partial_{z}u\partial_{z}\bar{u}}=\frac{1}{S^{2}}.

Thus,
u
is a harmonic map to the hyperbolic upper half hyperbolic plane that corresponds to
w
.
∎

Given now
w
and
\theta
, we find the associated harmonic map
u=R+iS
. More precisely, we provide the implicit formulas for
R
and
S
that involve the integrals
I_{1},I_{2},I_{3},I_{4}
.

End of the proof of Proposition
3
.

Our task is now to solve the system (
13
)-(
16
). We begin with (
13
) for
y=0
, which yields

\frac{\partial_{x}S}{S}(x,0)=2\cosh w(x,0)\sin\theta(x,0).

Integrating, we obtain

S(x,0)=S(0,0)e^{2\int_{0}^{x}\cosh w(t,0)\sin\theta(t,0)dt}.

So, (
14
) implies that

S(x,y)=S(0,0)e^{2\int_{0}^{x}\cosh w(t,0)\sin\theta(t,0)dt+2\int_{0}^{y}\sinh w(x,s)\cos\theta(x,s)ds}.

Thus

\displaystyle S(x,y)=S(0,0)e^{2(I_{1}+I_{2})}.

In order now to compute
R(x,y)
, we consider (
15
) for
y=0
, which yields

R(x,0)=R(0,0)+2\int_{0}^{x}S(t,0)\cosh w(t,0)\cos\theta(t,0)dt.

So, (
16
) implies that

\displaystyle\ R(x,y)=R(0,0)

\displaystyle+2\int_{0}^{x}S(t,0)\cosh w(t,0)\cos\theta(t,0)dt

\displaystyle-2\int_{0}^{y}S(x,s)\sinh w(x,s)\sin\theta(x,s)ds,

thus

\displaystyle R(x,y)=R(0,0)+2S(0,0)(I_{3}-I_{4}).

∎

We now provide a concrete example of a harmonic map to show how the algorithm in Proposition
3
can be implemented. In particular, we recover the harmonic map obtained in
[

.

Example 11
.

Suppose that
\tanh{\frac{w(x,y)}{2}}=\frac{2y}{\cosh{2x}}
and consider the case
R(0,0)=0
,
S(0,0)=-\frac{1}{4}
as in
[

4.
The sinh-Gordon equation

In this section we provide a new family of solutions of the sinh-Gordon equation.

The motivation for obtaining solutions of the sinh-Gordon equation is their correspondence to harmonic maps between surfaces, as described in Section
2
. In addition, it is exactly the form of the special solutions to the sinh-Gordon equation considered in this section, that will allow explicit expressions of harmonic maps to hyperbolic plane. We therefore postpone our main goal, the construction of harmonic maps, to the next section.

In
[
11
, Section 5]
, the authors construct harmonic maps when
w
is a one-soliton solution to (
1
). In some sense, they obtain solutions to this sinh-Gordon equation that are of the form

w(x,y)=2\arctanh({F(x)}).

In what follows, we focus on solutions to the sinh-Gordon equation (
1
) that are of the form (
7
), i.e.
w(x,y)=2\arctanh({F(x)}{G(y)})
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
F
and
G
are non-constant functions.

Notice that condition (
7
) is equivalent to
b=\frac{\partial_{x}w(x,y)}{2\sinh{w(x,y)}}=b(x),
where
b(x)=\frac{F^{\prime}(x)}{2F(x)}
.
One can prove that (
7
) is also equivalent to the differential equation

(19)

\partial_{xy}^{2}w(x,y)=\partial_{x}w(x,y)\partial_{y}w(x,y)\coth{w(x,y)}.

Indeed, for the one direction, starting from
\tanh\frac{w}{2}=FG
we get
\partial_{x}w=\frac{F^{\prime}}{F}\sinh w
and
\partial_{y}w=\frac{G^{\prime}}{G}\sinh w
. Thus, differentiating
w_{x}
in
y
and using
w_{y}
, we get (
19
). Conversely, integrating
\frac{\partial^{2}_{xy}w}{\partial_{x}w}=\partial_{y}w\coth w
in
y
yields
\frac{\partial_{x}w}{\sinh w}=b(x)
. Integrating in
x
, we get for some
c(y)
that
\log\left|\tanh\frac{w}{2}\right|=\int b(x)dx+c(y)
, whence the claim follows.
In
[
1
]
, the authors were lead to this equation for geometric reasons related to CMC surfaces. It is an open problem to relate this equation with the geometry of harmonic maps.

The basic idea is that under condition (
7
), we can solve this sinh-Gordon equation by a separation of variables argument. Then, the problem is reduced to solving certain non-linear ODEs, which in turn can be solved by the use of elliptic functions.

We now prove the following result.

Proposition 12
.

If
w
is a solution of the sinh-Gordon equation (
1
)
of the form

w(x,y)=2\arctanh({F(x)}{G(y)}),

then the functions
F,G
satisfy the differential equations

(20)

\displaystyle(F^{\prime}(x))^{2}

\displaystyle=AF^{4}(x)+BF^{2}(x)+C

(21)

\displaystyle(G^{\prime}(y))^{2}

\displaystyle=-CG^{4}(y)-(B-4)G^{2}(y)-A,

where
A,B,C
are arbitrary constants.

Proof.

Set
G(y)=\frac{1}{H(y)}
. Writing
w=2\arctanh\frac{F(x)}{H(y)}
, we find from (
1
) that

\displaystyle\;\frac{F^{\prime\prime}(x)}{F(x)}\left({H^{2}(y)}-{F^{2}(x)}\right)+2F^{\prime}(x)^{2}-\frac{H^{\prime\prime}(y)H(y)-2H^{\prime}(y)^{2}}{H(y)^{2}}\times

(22)

\displaystyle\times\left({H^{2}(y)}-{F^{2}(x)}\right)+2F^{2}(x)\frac{H^{\prime}(y)^{2}}{H^{2}(y)}

\displaystyle=4\left(F^{2}(x)+H^{2}(y)\right).

Differentiating with respect to
x
and
y
, we obtain

2H(y)H^{\prime}(y)\left(\frac{F^{\prime\prime}(x)}{F(x)}\right)^{\prime}+2F(x)F^{\prime}(x)\left(\frac{H^{\prime\prime}(y)}{H(y)}\right)^{\prime}=0,

or

\frac{1}{F(x)F^{\prime}(x)}\left(\frac{F^{\prime\prime}(x)}{F(x)}\right)^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{H(y)H^{\prime}(y)}\left(\frac{H^{\prime\prime}(y)}{H(y)}\right)^{\prime}=4A=\text{constant}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left(\frac{F^{\prime\prime}(x)}{F(x)}\right)^{\prime}=4AF(x)F^{\prime}(x),\quad\left(\frac{H^{\prime\prime}(y)}{H(x)}\right)^{\prime}=4AH(y)H^{\prime}(y).

Integrating twice and taking into account (
22
), we obtain

F^{\prime}(x)^{2}=AF^{4}(x)+BF^{2}(x)+C,

and

H^{\prime}(y)^{2}=-AH^{4}(y)-(B-4)H^{2}(y)-C.

Finally, we find

G^{\prime}(y)^{2}=-CG^{4}(y)-(B-4)G^{2}(y)-A.

∎

An argument similar to the one used in
[
19
]
, reveals that the following result, which is a special case of Proposition
12
, holds true.

Proposition 13
.

Let
w_{0}>0
, and
\alpha,\beta>0
, such that

\alpha+\beta=\cosh{w_{0}}>1.

Consider
f(x)
,
g(y)
such that

\displaystyle(f^{\prime})^{2}

\displaystyle=f^{4}-4\left(1+\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}\right)f^{2}+4^{2}\alpha^{2}

\displaystyle(g^{\prime})^{2}

\displaystyle=g^{4}-4\left(1+\beta^{2}-\alpha^{2}\right)g^{2}+4^{2}\beta^{2},

with
f(0)=0
,
f^{\prime}(0)=-4\alpha
,
g(0)=0
,
g^{\prime}(0)=-4\beta
.
Then the function
w(x,y)
given by

\tanh\frac{w(x,y)}{2}=\tanh\frac{w_{0}}{2}e^{-\int_{0}^{x}f(t)dt}e^{-\int_{0}^{y}g(s)ds},

is such that
\Delta w=2\sinh(2w)
and
w(0,0)=w_{0}
.

Finally, note that in general,
F
and
G
in Proposition
12
, and
f
and
g
in Proposition
13
respectively, are given in terms of elliptic functions, but reduce to elementary functions for certain choices of the coefficients of the ODEs. In particular, we observe that the only case where one of the functions

is elementary is when
|\alpha-\beta|=1
. In this case actually both functions

,
g
end up to be elementary.

6.
One-soliton solutions of the Sine-Gordon equation

In the previous section, we started with a special solution
w
to the sinh-Gordon equation and then determined
\theta
by the Bäcklund transformation, in order to construct harmonic maps by the algorithm proposed in Proposition
3
. We now follow the reverse direction, and start with a special solution
\theta
to the sine-Gordon equation.

More precisely, we are motivated by an example in
[

, where a special solution
\theta=\theta(x)=\arcsin\tanh(2x)
of the sine-Gordon equation is considered. Notice that in the aforementioned example,
\theta(x)
is an elementary function. However, we prove in this section that in general
\theta(x)
is an elliptic function. In addition, we prove that if
\theta=\theta(x)
is a solution to the sine-Gordon equation (
6

determined by the Bäcklund transformation is in the family of solutions that we have studied in Section

w=\arctanh(F(x)G(y))
. Next, we provide an explicit formula for the corresponding harmonic map.

For clarity reasons, we first present the definitions and properties of the elliptic Jacobi functions. The formulation used in this paper is taken from
[
25

.

The elliptic integral of the first kind
F(\phi|n)
and the Jacobi elliptic function
\mathrm{sn}(v|n)
are defined by the formula

F(\phi|n)=v=\int_{0}^{x}\,\dfrac{dt}{\sqrt{\left(1-t^{2}\right)\left(1-nt^{2}\right)}}=\mathrm{sn}^{-1}(x|n),

where

\mathrm{sn}(v|n)=x=\sin\phi.

Define

\mathrm{cn}(v|n)=\cos\phi,\quad\mathrm{dn}(v|n)=\sqrt{1-n\sin^{2}\,\phi}.

We first prove the following result, which provides the characterization for
w
.

Proposition 16
.

If
\theta=\theta(x)
is a solution of the sine-Gordon equation

\Delta\theta=-2\sin(2\theta),

then the associated solution
w
of the sinh-Gordon equation is

\tanh\frac{w(x,y)}{2}=F(x)G(y)=\frac{\sqrt{ab}\tan(\frac{\sqrt{ab}}{2}y+k)}{\theta^{\prime}(x)-2\cos\theta(x)},

where
a=2\cos\theta(0)+\theta^{\prime}(0)
,
b=2\cos\theta(0)-\theta^{\prime}(0)
, and

is such that

\tanh\frac{w(0,0)}{2}=-{\sqrt{\frac{a}{b}}\tan(k)}
.

Remark.
The above formula holds true also for the case

Proof.

Our first task is to determine the function
\theta
. Consider
\theta=\theta(x)
to be a solution of the sine-Gordon equation

\Delta\theta=-2\sin(2\theta).

Then,

\theta^{\prime\prime}=-2\sin(2\theta)

\theta^{\prime}(x)^{2}=-4\sin^{2}\theta(x)+c^{2},

A simple calculation reveals that

\int_{0}^{\theta(x)}\frac{d\psi}{\sqrt{1-\frac{4}{c^{2}}\sin^{2}(\psi)}}=c_{1}+cx,

(28)

\displaystyle\sin\theta(x)=\mathrm{sn}(cx+c_{1}|\frac{4}{c^{2}}),\;\text{ where }c_{1}=\int_{0}^{\theta(0)}\frac{d\psi}{\sqrt{1-\frac{4}{c^{2}}\sin^{2}(\psi)}}.

Moreover, we have

(29)

\displaystyle\cos\theta(x)=\mathrm{cn}(cx+c_{1}|\frac{4}{c^{2}}),\quad\theta^{\prime}(x)=c\;\mathrm{dn}(cx+c_{1}|\frac{4}{c^{2}}),

from which we derive the following differential equation

\theta^{\prime 2}-4\cos^{2}\theta=c^{2}-4.

Note that this implies

We next prove that the function

\displaystyle I(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\sin\theta(s)ds=\int_{0}^{x}\mathrm{sn}(cs+c_{1}|\frac{4}{c^{2}})ds

\displaystyle I_{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\cos\theta(x)\int_{0}^{y}\sinh(w(x,s))ds

\displaystyle=2\cos\theta(x)F(x)\int_{0}^{y}\frac{G(s)}{1-F^{2}(x)G^{2}(s)}ds.

An application of (
37
) and a change of variables, yield

\displaystyle I_{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle=4\cos\theta(x)F(x)\int_{0}^{y}\frac{G(s)}{1-F^{2}(x)G^{2}(s)}\;\frac{G^{\prime}(s)}{bG^{2}(s)+a}ds

\displaystyle=\frac{2\cos\theta(x)F(x)}{aF^{2}(x)+b}\log(\frac{1-F^{2}(x)G^{2}(0)}{1-F^{2}(x)G^{2}(y)}\;\frac{bG^{2}(y)+a}{bG^{2}(0)+a})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log(\frac{1-F^{2}(x)G^{2}(y)}{1-F^{2}(x)G^{2}(0)}\frac{bG^{2}(0)+a}{bG^{2}(y)+a}),

where in the last step we used (
39
), (
30
) and the fact that
ab=4-c^{2}
. A manipulation similar to the one used to derive (
42
) therefore gives

(43)

\exp(2I_{2}(x,y))=\frac{2\cos\theta(x)\cos(\sqrt{ab}y+2k)-\theta^{\prime}(x)}{2\cos\theta(x)\cos(2k)-\theta^{\prime}(x)}.

Then, by Proposition
3
, we have

\displaystyle S(x,y)

\displaystyle=S(0,0)\exp(2I_{1}(x))\exp(2I_{2}(x,y))

\displaystyle=S(0,0)\frac{2\cos\theta(x)\cos(\sqrt{ab}y+2k)-\theta^{\prime}(x)}{2\cos\theta(0)\cos(2k)-\theta^{\prime}(x)}.

We proceed to
I_{3}
. Due to (
34
), it can be written as

\displaystyle I_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\exp(2I_{1}(t))\cosh(w(t,0))\cos\theta(t)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\exp(2I_{1}(t))\cosh(w(t,0))\cot\theta(t)dI(t).

On the one hand, since
F(x)=e^{-2I(x)}
satisfies (
33
) with
A=-\frac{a^{2}}{4}
,
B=\frac{8-ab}{2}
and

C=-\frac{b^{2}}{4}
,
we get

\sin\theta(x)=I^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{F^{\prime}(x)}{F(x)}=\frac{\sqrt{2(8-ab)e^{4I(x)}-a^{2}-b^{2}e^{8I(x)}}}{4e^{2I(x)}},

therefore

\cos\theta(x)=\sqrt{1-I^{\prime}(x)^{2}}=\frac{be^{4I(x)}+a}{4e^{2I(x)}}.

On the other hand, replacing
e^{2I_{1}(x)}
by (
41
), we can write

\displaystyle I_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{\exp(4I(t))+G^{2}(0)}{(1-G^{2}(0))\exp(2I(t))}\frac{be^{4I(x)}+a}{\sqrt{2(8-ab)e^{4I(x)}-a^{2}-b^{2}e^{8I(x)}}}dI(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(1-G^{2}(0))}\int_{1}^{\frac{2\cos\theta(x)-\theta^{\prime}(x)}{b}}\frac{u^{2}+G^{2}(0)}{u^{2}}\frac{bu^{2}+a}{\sqrt{2(8-ab)u^{2}-a^{2}-b^{2}u^{4}}}du,

where we used the change of variables
u=e^{2I(x)}=1/F(x)
and (
40
).
Thus, taking into account (
38
), we find

I_{3}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\cosh^{2}\frac{w(0,0)}{2}\;J\left(\frac{2\cos\theta(x)-\theta^{\prime}(x)}{b}\right),

where
J(t)=\int_{1}^{t}\frac{u^{2}+G^{2}(0)}{u^{2}}\frac{bu^{2}+a}{\sqrt{2(8-ab)u^{2}-a^{2}-b^{2}u^{4}}}du.
Note that
J
can be computed by the use of elliptic integrals.

Finally, for the last integral
I_{4}
we have

\displaystyle I_{4}(x,y)

\displaystyle=e^{2I_{1}(x)}\sin\theta(x)\int_{0}^{y}\exp(2I_{2}(x,s))\sinh(w(x,s))ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}e^{2I_{1}(x)}\tan\theta(x)\int_{0}^{y}e^{(2\cos\theta(x)\int_{0}^{s}\sinh(w(x,\psi))d\psi)}2\cos\theta(x)\sinh(w(x,s))ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}e^{2I_{1}(x)}\tan\theta(x)(e^{2I_{2}(x,y)}-e^{2I_{2}(x,0)})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}e^{2I_{1}(x)}\tan\theta(x)(e^{2I_{2}(x,y)}-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin\theta(x)(\cos(\sqrt{ab}y+2k)-\cos(2k))}{2\cos\theta(0)\cos(2k)-\theta^{\prime}(0)},

where in the last step we used (
42
) and (
43
). The calculation of
R(x,y)=R(0,0)+2S(0,0)(I_{3}(x)-I_{4}(x,y))
is now complete.
∎

Next, using Theorem
6
, we provide an entirely new harmonic map to the hyperbolic plane.

Example 17
.

Consider the case when
\theta(0)=0
,
\theta^{\prime}(0)=1
,
G(0)=0=\tanh\frac{w(0,0)}{2}
,
S(0,0)=1
and
R(0,0)=0
. Then, we compute

\sin\theta(x)=\mathrm{sn}(x|4)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{sn}(2x|\frac{1}{4}),

\tanh(\frac{w(x,y)}{2})=\frac{\sqrt{3}\tan(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}y)}{\theta^{\prime}(x)-2\cos\theta(x)},

and

I(x)=\frac{1}{2}\log(2\cos\theta(x)-\theta^{\prime}(x)).

Therefore,

\displaystyle I_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=I(x),

\displaystyle I_{2}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\log(\frac{2\cos\theta(x)\cos(\sqrt{3}y)-\theta^{\prime}(x)}{2\cos\theta(x)-\theta^{\prime}(x)}),

\displaystyle I_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{1}^{2\cos\theta(x)-\theta^{\prime}(x)}\frac{u^{2}+3}{\sqrt{10u^{2}-u^{4}-9}}du,

\displaystyle I_{4}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sin\theta(x)(\cos(\sqrt{3}y)-1).

Then, the corresponding harmonic map then is
u(x,y)=R(x,y)+iS(x,y)
, where

R(x,y)=J(2\cos\theta(x)-\theta^{\prime}(x))-2\sin\theta(x)(\cos(\sqrt{3}y)-1)

and

S(x,y)=2\cos\theta(x)\cos(\sqrt{3}y)-\theta^{\prime}(x),

where
J(t)=\int_{1}^{t}\frac{u^{2}+G^{2}(0)}{u^{2}}\frac{bu^{2}+a}{\sqrt{2(8-ab)u^{2}-a^{2}-b^{2}u^{4}}}du.

The domain of definition of
u
is the set of

\left\{(x,y):\;\left|\frac{\sqrt{3}\tan(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}y)}{\theta^{\prime}(x)-2\cos\theta(x)}\right|<1\right\}

where the map is a well defined
C^{2}
map whose Jacobian is almost everywhere non vanishing.
