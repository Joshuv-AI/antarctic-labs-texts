Inference on the proportion of variance explained in principal component analysis
Ronan Perry

Abstract
Principal component analysis (PCA) is a longstanding and well-studied approach for dimension reduction. It rests upon the assumption that the underlying signal in the data has low rank, and thus can be well-summarized using a small number of dimensions. The output of PCA is typically represented using a scree plot, which displays the proportion of variance explained (PVE) by each principal component. While the PVE is extensively reported in routine data analyses, to the best of our knowledge the notion of inference on the PVE remains unexplored. In this paper, we consider inference on the PVE. We first introduce a new population quantity for the PVE with respect to an unknown matrix mean. Critically, our interest lies in the PVE of the sample principal components (as opposed to unobserved population principal components); thus, the population PVE that we introduce is defined conditional on the sample singular vectors. We show that it is possible to conduct inference, in the sense of confidence intervals, p-values, and point estimates, on this population quantity. Furthermore, we can conduct valid inference on the PVE of a subset of the principal components, even when the subset is selected using a data-driven approach such as the elbow rule. We demonstrate the proposed approach in simulation and in an application to a gene expression dataset.



Keywords:
Selective Inference, Principal Component Analysis, Proportion of Variance Explained, Scree Plot, Elbow Rule

Introduction

Principal component analysis (PCA)
is a longstanding and well-studied technique for dimension reduction, with applications across disciplines
(
Tipping and Bishop 1999a
;
Jolliffe 2002
;
Bishop 1998
;
Kaiser 1960
;
Krzanowski 1987
;
Besse and de Falguerolles 1993
;
Wold 1978
;
Tipping and Bishop 1999b
;
Muirhead 1982
)
. We begin with an
n\times p
data matrix
X=\begin{pmatrix}X_{1}&\ldots&X_{p}\end{pmatrix}
, consisting of
n
observations and
p
features.
The first principal component loading vector,
v_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, is the unit vector (
\|v_{1}\|_{2}=1)
that maximizes the sample variance of
Xv_{1}
.
The second principal component loading vector,
v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, is the unit vector that maximizes the sample variance
Xv_{2}
, subject to the constraint that
v_{1}^{\top}v_{2}=0
, and so on.
Once the first
k<\min(n,p)
principal component loading vectors have been computed, they can serve a number of purposes:

1.

They can be used for visualization: rather than plotting the
n
observations with respect to the original set of features
X_{1},\ldots,X_{p}
, we instead plot
Xv_{1},Xv_{2},\ldots,Xv_{k}

(
Becht et al. 2019
;
Margulies et al. 2016
)
.

2.

They can be used as features in an unsupervised analysis, such as clustering. That is, we can cluster the rows of the
n\times k
data matrix
\begin{pmatrix}Xv_{1},\ldots,Xv_{k}\end{pmatrix}
, rather than the rows of the original
n\times p
data matrix
X

(
von Luxburg 2007
;
Calhoun et al. 2001
;
Athreya et al. 2018
)
.

3.

They can be used to construct features for a supervised analysis. For instance, in principal components regression, we predict an
n
-dimensional response
Y
on the basis of
Xv_{1},\ldots,Xv_{k}
, as opposed to predicting
Y
using the original features
X_{1},\ldots,X_{p}

(
McGough et al. 2020
;
Hayes et al. 2022
)
.

In each of these cases, we implicitly assume that the first few principal component loading vectors capture the signal in the data, and the remaining dimensions contain mostly noise.
The suitability of this assumption on a particular dataset is typically visualized using a scree plot, which displays
s_{l}^{2}\{X\}/\sum_{l^{\prime}=1}^{p}s_{l^{\prime}}^{2}\{X\}
for
l=1,\ldots,p
, where
s_{1}\{X\},\ldots,s_{p}\{X\}
represent the singular values of
X
in decreasing order. This quantity is referred to as the “proportion of variance explained” (PVE) by the
l
th principal component, and it is often reported in scientific publications
(
Lukk et al. 2010
;
Alter et al. 2000
;
Li 2010
;
Duforet-Frebourg et al. 2016
)
. In fact, in many cases, the PVE is the
only
quantitative summary of PCA reported.
In this paper, we consider inference on the PVE, a problem that — to our knowledge — has not been previously explored. We develop a “population” quantity corresponding to the PVE, as well as tools to construct confidence intervals and conduct hypothesis tests for this quantity.

While in some cases the PVE of all

\min(n,p)
principal components may be of interest, in others interest may focus on a small number of principal components, where this number is selected from the data using a formal or heuristic approach. In fact, this number is often selected by examining the scree plot, and looking for an “elbow” where the PVE decreases
(
Cattell 1966
;
Greenacre et al. 2022
;
Șenbabaoğlu et al. 2014
;
Nguyen and Holmes 2019
;
Li 2010
)
. As demonstrated in
fig.

1
, if the number of principal components to be considered is selected from the data, then valid inference for the PVE must account for the selection process; thus, we extend our approach for inference on the PVE to account for the possibility of data-driven selection of the number of principal components.

Our work builds upon the proposal of
Choi et al. 2017
, which considers inference on the singular values of the data. However, they do not consider the PVE, which is a quantity of much greater scientific interest, and certainly much more widely-reported, than the singular values themselves. Furthermore, they do not account for the possibility that the number of principal components under consideration was selected from the data.

We conduct inference on the PVE conditional on the
k
th sample singular vectors. Furthermore, in the setting where the number of principal components under consideration is selected from the data, we condition also on this selection event. The selective inference framework
(
Fithian et al. 2017
)
has been used to advantage in a number of settings involving data-driven selection events, including high-dimensional regression
(
Lee et al. 2016
;
Panigrahi and Taylor 2023
)
, hierarchical and
k
-means clustering
(
Gao et al. 2022
;
Chen and Witten 2023
;
Yun and Barber 2023
)
, decision trees
(
Neufeld et al. 2022
)
, outlier detection
(
Chen and Bien 2020
)
, and changepoint detection
(
Jewell et al. 2022
;
Hyun et al. 2018
;
Hyun et al. 2021
)
. However, our application of this framework to the setting of inference on the PVE is entirely novel.

In this paper, we fill a major gap involving inference on the PVE, a quantity that is of critical importance in applied settings.
We enable valid inference for a data analysis pipeline that is ubiquitous across virtually all areas of data-driven inquiry, but that has lacked solid methodological or theoretical footing.
Our primary contributions are as follows:

1.

We develop a notion of a “population quantity” corresponding to PVE.

2.

We develop hypothesis tests of this population quantity that control the type 1 error, confidence intervals that attain the nominal coverage, and point estimates.

3.

We extend these inferential tools to the setting where the number of principal components considered was selected using a data-driven rule
r(s\{X\})
based on the singular values
s\{X\}
, e.g., by looking for an “elbow” in the scree plot.

4.

We develop efficient strategies to compute the aforementioned quantities.

2
Preliminaries for inference on the PVE

2.1
A model for PCA

A number of models for PCA have been considered in the literature. The “probabilistic PCA” approach of
Tipping and Bishop 1999a
and
Minka 2000
assumes that each row of the
n\times p
data matrix
X
is independently drawn from a multivariate normal distribution with mean zero and a spiked covariance matrix. Here, instead, we consider a model for PCA in which the low-rank structure is contained in the mean, rather than in the covariance matrix
(
Choi et al. 2017
;
Muirhead 1982
)
. That is, we assume that

X=\Theta+E
,
where
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is a fixed (non-random) mean matrix, and
E\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is a noise matrix with independent and identically-distributed elements,
E_{ij}\sim{\bf{\rm N}}\,(0,\sigma^{2})
. Equivalently,

X\sim\text{MN}(\Theta,I_{n},\sigma^{2}I_{p}),

(2.1)

a matrix normal random variable with mean
\Theta
, independent rows, and columns with covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}I_{p}
. Without loss of generality, we assume that
n\geq p
.

Before performing PCA, it is typical to column-center the data matrix. In Appendix
A.1
, we show that column-centering amounts to a slight re-parametrization of (
2.1
); thus, the discussion that follows applies with or without column-centering.

The singular value decomposition of
X
can be written as

X=U\{X\}\begin{bmatrix}s_{1}\{X\}&\dots&0\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
0&\dots&s_{p}\{X\}\end{bmatrix}V\{X\}^{\top}.

(2.2)

Here,
U\{\cdot\}
and
V\{\cdot\}
are functions mapping to
n\times p
and
p\times p
matrices, respectively, with orthonormal columns: that is,
U\{X\}^{\top}U\{X\}=V\{X\}^{\top}V\{X\}=I_{p\times p}
.
The columns of
V\{X\}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\{X\}\ldots v_{p}\{X\}\end{bmatrix}
are the principal component loading vectors of
X
, while those of
U\{X\}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}\{X\}\ldots u_{p}\{X\}\end{bmatrix}
are the (normalized) principal component score vectors. We can similarly decompose the unknown mean matrix
\Theta
, defining
U\{\Theta\}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}\{\Theta\}\ldots u_{p}\{\Theta\}\end{bmatrix}
,
V\{\Theta\}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}\{\Theta\}\ldots v_{p}\{\Theta\}\end{bmatrix}
, and
s\{\Theta\}=\left(s_{1}\{\Theta\},\dots,s_{p}\{\Theta\}\right)
.

2.2
A population parameter for the PVE

The sample PVE
(
Jolliffe 2002
;
Li 2010
)
of the
k
th principal component of the data matrix
X
is defined as

\mathrm{\widehat{PVE}}_{k}\{X\}=\frac{s^{2}_{k}\{X\}}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{p}s^{2}_{k^{\prime}}\{X\}}.

(2.3)

We will now define a population parameter corresponding to this sample quantity. It is tempting to consider

\frac{s^{2}_{k}\{\Theta\}}{\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{p}s^{2}_{k^{\prime}}\{\Theta\}}=\frac{\left(u_{k}\{\Theta\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{\Theta\}\right)^{2}}{\left\|\Theta\right\|_{F}^{2}}

(2.4)

as a candidate for this population parameter. However, interest lies in the variance explained by the
k
th
sample
singular vectors,
u_{k}\{X\}
and
v_{k}\{X\}
, not the variance explained by the
k
th
population
singular vectors,
u_{k}\{\Theta\}
and
v_{k}\{\Theta\}
: this is because the former are part of the data analysis pipeline and downstream tasks, whereas the latter are not.
Thus, we replace
u_{k}\{\Theta\}
and
v_{k}\{\Theta\}
in
eq.

2.3
with
u_{k}\{X\}
and
v_{k}\{X\}
.

This results in the estimand

\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{X\right\}=\frac{\left(u_{k}\{X\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{X\}\right)^{2}}{\left\|\Theta\right\|_{F}^{2}}.

(2.5)

We will view (
2.5
) as the population quantity corresponding to
\mathrm{\widehat{PVE}}_{k}\{X\}
(
2.3
) in the rest of this paper. Note that under the
global null
in which
\left\|\Theta\right\|_{F}^{2}=0
, we define
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{X\right\}=0
. We interpret
eq.

2.5
as the amount of information that the
k
th singular vectors of
X
contain about the mean
\Theta
, relative to the total amount of signal contained in
\Theta
. By Cauchy-Schwarz,
\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{X\right\}\leq 1
, with equality holding if the sample singular vectors are also population singular vectors. By contrast, of course
\sum_{k=1}^{p}\mathrm{\widehat{PVE}}_{k}\{X\}=1
.

We emphasize that — as suggested by the unusual notation involving
X
— the quantity
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{X\right\}
in (
2.5
) is not a “conventional” parameter: it involves not only the unknown mean parameter
\Theta
but also the data
X
(because
u_{k}\{X\}
and
v_{k}\{X\}
are derived from the data). To conduct inference on
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{X\right\}
, we will need to condition on
u_{k}\{X\}
and
v_{k}\{X\}
. We discuss this in greater depth in
section

2.3
.

2.3
Deriving the conditional density

We now lay the foundation for developing hypothesis tests in
section

3.1
, confidence intervals in
section

3.2
, and point estimates in
section

3.3
. Lemma 1 from
Choi et al. 2017
uses results from
Muirhead 1982
to establish that under the data generating process in (
2.1
), the joint density of
s\{X\},U\{X\}
, and
V\{X\}
takes the form

\displaystyle f\left(s\{X\},U\{X\},V\{X\}\right)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{p}s_{k}^{2}\{X\}-2\sum_{k=1}^{p}s_{k}\{X\}u_{k}\{X\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{X\}+\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}(\Theta^{\top}\Theta)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times J(s\{X\})\,d\mu_{n,p}\left(U\{X\}\right)\,d\mu_{p,p}\left(V\{X\}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\times\prod_{k=1}^{p}\mathbb{I}_{[s_{k+1}\{X\},s_{k-1}\{X\}]}(s_{k}\{X\}),

(2.6)

where we define

J(s\{X\}):=\prod_{k=1}^{p}s_{k}^{n-p}\{X\}\prod_{1\leq k<k^{\prime}\leq p}\left(s^{2}_{k}\{X\}-s^{2}_{k^{\prime}}\{X\}\right),

(2.7)

letting
s_{0}\{X\}:=\infty
,
s_{p+1}\{X\}:=0
, and
\mu_{a,b}(\cdot)
denote the uniform measure over matrices in
\mathbb{R}^{a\times b}
with orthogonal columns.

Recall that we wish to conduct inference on
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{X\right\}
defined in (
2.5
). We make the following observations:

1.

\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{X\right\}
involves the data by way of
u_{k}\{X\}
and
v_{k}\{X\}
. Thus, we will condition on
u_{k}\{X\}
and
v_{k}\{X\}
so that
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{X\right\}
is deterministic.

2.

The numerator of
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{X\right\}
depends on the unknown parameter
\Theta
only by way of
u_{k}\{X\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{X\}
. This quantity is coupled with the
k
th singular value
s_{k}\{X\}
in the joint density in (
2.3
). Since (
2.3
) also involves
u_{k^{\prime}}\{X\}
,
v_{k^{\prime}}\{X\}
, and
s_{k^{\prime}}\{X\}
for
k^{\prime}\neq k
, we will further condition on these quantities.

3.

Typically, a data analyst is interested in the PVE of the
k
th principal component only if
k\leq r(s\{X\})
, where
r(s\{X\})
is the number of principal components selected using a data-driven approach based on the singular values
s\{X\}
. Thus, we condition on the
selection event

k\leq r(s\{X\})
. In the absence of a data-driven selection approach, we define
r(s\{X\}):=p
.

We therefore need to characterize the distribution of
s_{k}\{X\}
given the other singular values,
U\{X\}
,
V\{X\}
, and the selection event
k\leq r(s\{X\})
.

Proposition 2.1

(The conditional density of
s_{k}\{X\}
)
.

Define

s_{[-k]}\{X\}:=\left(s_{1}\{X\},\dots,s_{k-1}\{X\},s_{k+1}\{X\},\dots,s_{p}\{X\}\right),

Under (
2.1
), the
density of
s_{k}\{X\}
, conditional on the values of
U\{X\}
,
V\{X\}
,
s_{[-k]}\{X\}
, and the selection event
k\leq r(s\{X\})
, is

\displaystyle f\left(s_{k}\{X\}~|~U\{X\},V\{X\},s_{[-k]}\{X\},k\leq r(s\{X\})\right)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}s_{k}^{2}\{X\}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}s_{k}\{X\}u_{k}\{X\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{X\}\bigg)\times s_{k}^{n-p}\{X\}\times\Pi_{k^{\prime}\in[p]:k^{\prime}\neq k}\left|s_{k}^{2}\{X\}-s_{k^{\prime}}^{2}\{X\}\right|

\displaystyle\quad\times\mathbb{I}_{[s_{k+1}\{X\},s_{k-1}\{X\}]}(s_{k}\{X\})\times\mathbb{I}[k\leq r(s\{X\})],

(2.8)

where
s_{0}\{X\}:=\infty
,
s_{p+1}\{X\}:=0
.

In
proposition

2.1
,
\mathbb{I}_{A}(\cdot)
denotes an indicator function that equals one if its argument is in the set
A
, and zero otherwise.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, and define

s^{(k)}\{x,t\}:=(s_{1}\{x\},\dots,s_{k-1}\{x\},t,s_{k+1}\{x\},\dots,s_{p}\{x\}),

(2.9)

h\left(t;\delta,s_{[-k]}\{x\}\right):=\exp{\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+\frac{t}{\sigma^{2}}\delta\right)}t^{n-p}\prod_{k^{\prime}\neq k}\left|t^{2}-s_{k^{\prime}}^{2}\{x\}\right|,

(2.10)

and

\mathbb{P}_{\delta,k}(x):=\frac{\int_{s_{k}\{x\}}^{s_{k-1}\{x\}}h\left(t;\delta,s_{[-k]}\{x\}\right)\mathbb{I}\left[r\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)\geq k\right]dt}{\int_{s_{k+1}\{x\}}^{s_{k-1}\{x\}}h\left(t;\delta,s_{[-k]}\{x\}\right)\mathbb{I}\left[r\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)\geq k\right]dt}.

(2.11)

Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\big(s_{k}\{X\}\geq s_{k}\{x\}~\big|~U\{X\}=U\{x\},V\{X\}=V\{x\},s_{[-k]}\{X\}=s_{[-k]}\{x\},k\leq r(s\{X\})\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{u_{k}\{x\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x\},k}(x).

Finally, we arrive at this section’s main result, which sets the stage for the confidence intervals and hypothesis tests in Section
3
.

Theorem 2.3

(Conditional distribution of
\mathbb{P}_{u_{k}\{x\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x\},k}(X)
)
.

Under (
2.1
), for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, and
k
, it holds that

\mathrm{Pr}\left(\mathbb{P}_{u_{k}\{x\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x\},k}(X)\leq\alpha~|~u_{k}\{X\}=u_{k}\{x\},v_{k}\{X\}=v_{k}\{x\},k\leq r(s\{X\})\right)=\alpha.

3
Conducting inference on the PVE

We now develop hypothesis tests, confidence intervals, and point estimates for
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{x\right\}
, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
. All inference in this section will be conditional on

\widetilde{A}_{k}(X,x):=\left\{u_{k}\{X\}=u_{k}\{x\},v_{k}\{X\}=v_{k}\{x\},k\leq r\left(s\{X\}\right)\right\}.

(3.1)

That is, we conduct inference conditional on the
k
th left and right estimated singular vectors, and the fact that at least
k
principal components were selected from the data.

3.1
Hypothesis tests

We consider testing the selective hypothesis
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{X\right\}=0
in (
2.5
), conditional on
\widetilde{A}_{k}(X,x)
in (
3.1
). Since
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{X\right\}=0
if and only if
u_{k}\{X\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{X\}=0
, the following corollary of
theorem

2.3
guarantees selective type 1 error control, in the sense of
Fithian et al. 2017
, for a test based on
\mathbb{P}_{0,k}(X)
defined in (
2.2
).

Corollary 3.1

(Uniformity of p-values under the null)
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, the test that rejects
H_{0,k}(x):\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{x\right\}=0
if
\mathbb{P}_{0,k}(X)\leq\alpha
controls the selective type 1 error at level
\alpha
. That is, under the null hypothesis
H_{0,k}(x):\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{x\right\}=0
,

\mathrm{Pr}\left(\mathbb{P}_{0,k}(X)\leq\alpha~\big|~\widetilde{A}_{k}(X,x)\right)=\alpha.

(3.2)

Remark 1

(Relationship to
Choi et al. 2017
)
.

Comparing the p-value in
corollary

3.1
to that from Theorem 2.1 of
Choi et al. 2017
, we note that the primary difference is that the latter was computed without conditioning on
k\leq r\left(s\{X\}\right)
, i.e., it does not account for the selection of the number of principal components from the data.

3.2
Confidence intervals

We wish to construct a confidence interval for
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{x\right\}
defined in (
2.5
), conditional on
eq.

3.1
. That is, our goal is an interval
\text{CI}_{\alpha,k}(X)
that satisfies

\Pr\left(\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{x\right\}\in\text{CI}_{\alpha,k}(X)\mid\tilde{A}_{k}(X,x)\right)=\Pr\left(\frac{\left(u_{k}\{x\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x\}\right)^{2}}{\left\|\Theta\right\|_{F}^{2}}\in\text{CI}_{\alpha,k}(X)~\bigg|~\tilde{A}_{k}(X,x)\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

First, we note that for positive
\alpha_{1}
, we can obtain a confidence region for the numerator of
eq.

2.5
of the form

\Pr(u_{k}\{x\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x\}\in\text{C}_{\alpha_{1},k}(X)\mid\tilde{A}_{k}(X,x))=1-\alpha_{1}

by inverting the p-values in
corollary

3.1
, i.e.,

\text{C}_{\alpha_{1},k}(X):=\left\{\delta\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{P}_{\delta,k}\left(X\right)\in\left[\alpha_{1}/2,\,1-\alpha_{1}/2\right]\right\}.

(3.3)

More specifically, the conditional density in
proposition

2.1
is a one-parameter exponential family in the parameter
u_{k}\{X\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{X\}
. By
proposition

2.2
,
1-\mathbb{P}_{\delta,k}\left(\cdot\right)
is the CDF of this family and hence has a monotone likelihood ratio in
\delta
. Therefore, the confidence region is the interval
\text{C}_{\alpha_{1},k}(X)=\left[L_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}(X),U_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}(X)\right]
, where
L_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}(X)
and
U_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}(X)
satisfy

\mathbb{P}_{L_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}(X),k}(X)=\alpha_{1}/2,\quad\quad\mathbb{P}_{U_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}(X),k}(X)=1-\alpha_{1}/2.

(3.4)

Under a transformation of
\text{C}_{\alpha_{1},k}(X)
(details left to
section

A.2
), we can obtain a
1-\alpha_{1}
confidence interval for the square
\left(u_{k}\{x\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x\}\right)^{2}
, denoted
\left[\widetilde{L}_{\alpha_{1}/2}^{\mathrm{num}}(X),\widetilde{U}_{\alpha_{1}/2}^{\mathrm{num}}(X)\right]
.

Now, suppose that we also had access to an independent dataset
X^{\prime}
from our model
eq.

2.1
. We could then construct a confidence interval for the denominator of
eq.

2.5
of the form

\Pr\left(\|\Theta\|_{F}^{2}\in[L_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}(X^{\prime}),U_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}(X^{\prime})]\right)=1-\alpha_{2},

(3.5)

for positive
\alpha_{2}
. Noting that (
3.5
) also holds conditional on
\tilde{A}_{k}(X,x)
, it would follow that

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\frac{\left(u_{k}\{x\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x\}\right)^{2}}{\|\Theta\|_{F}^{2}}\in\left[\frac{\widetilde{L}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}(X)}{U_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}(X^{\prime})},\frac{\widetilde{U}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}(X)}{L_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}(X^{\prime})}\right]\bigg|\tilde{A}_{k}(X,x)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Pr\Bigg(\left\{u_{k}\{x\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x\}\in\text{C}_{\alpha_{1},k}(X)\right\}\cap\left\{\|\Theta\|_{F}^{2}\in[L_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}(X^{\prime}),U_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}(X^{\prime})]\right\}\big|\tilde{A}_{k}(X,x)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\alpha_{1})(1-\alpha_{2})>1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}.

(3.6)

We implement this strategy as follows. For a positive constant
c
, we define
X^{(1)}:=X+cE^{\prime}
for
E^{\prime}\sim\text{MN}(0,I_{n},\sigma^{2}I_{p})
, a noisy version of
X
. Similarly, let
x^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
. Then, rather than a confidence interval for
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{x\right\}
conditional on
\widetilde{A}_{k}\left(X,x\right)
, we will instead construct a confidence interval for

\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{x^{(1)}\right\}:=\frac{\left(u_{k}\{x^{(1)}\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x^{(1)}\}\right)^{2}}{\left\|\Theta\right\|_{F}^{2}},

(3.7)

conditional on
\widetilde{A}_{k}\left(X^{(1)},x^{(1)}\right)
.

A confidence interval for the numerator of (
3.7
), conditional on
\widetilde{A}_{k}\left(X^{(1)},x^{(1)}\right)
, takes a form directly analogous to (
3.3
). To obtain a confidence interval for the denominator, let
\sigma^{2}_{c}:=\sigma^{2}\left(1+c^{-2}\right)
and define
X^{(2)}:=X-\frac{1}{c}E^{\prime}\sim\text{MN}(\Theta,I_{n},\sigma^{2}_{c}I_{p})
. Note that
X^{(1)}
and
X^{(2)}
are independent by construction of this data thinning procedure
(
Rasines and Young 2023
;
Neufeld et al. 2024
;
Leiner et al. 2023
)
, and
\sigma^{-2}_{c}\left\|X^{(2)}\right\|_{F}^{2}\sim\chi^{2}_{np}\left(\sigma^{-2}_{c}\left\|\Theta\right\|_{F}^{2}\right)
, i.e., this is a non-central chi-squared random variable. Recalling that
\sigma
is known, we can therefore construct an exact
1-\alpha_{2}
confidence interval for
\left\|\Theta\right\|_{F}^{2}
numerically using the quantiles of the non-central
\chi_{np}^{2}
distribution. We let
\left[L_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}\left(X^{(2)}\right),U_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}\left(X^{(2)}\right)\right]
denote this interval.

Thus, for any
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in(0,1)
, we define the confidence interval

\displaystyle\text{CI}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},k}\left(X^{(1)},X^{(2)}\right):=\left[\frac{\widetilde{L}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right)}{U_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}\left(X^{(2)}\right)},\frac{\widetilde{U}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right)}{L_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}\left(X^{(2)}\right)}\right].

(3.8)

Theorem 3.2

(Selective coverage of the confidence interval)
.

Let
X
be distributed according to
eq.

2.1
, and for a positive constant
c
, define
X^{(1)}:=X+cE^{\prime}
and
X^{(2)}:=X-\tfrac{1}{c}E^{\prime}
for
E^{\prime}\sim\text{MN}(0,I_{n},\sigma^{2}I_{p}))
. For any
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in(0,1)
and
x^{(1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
,

\mathrm{Pr}\left(\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{x^{(1)}\right\}\in\mathrm{CI}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},k}\left(X^{(1)},X^{(2)}\right)~\bigg|~\widetilde{A}_{k}\left(X^{(1)},x^{(1)}\right)\right)\geq 1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}.

(3.9)

Remark 2
.

In practice, we set
c=1
, which equally splits the Fisher information about
\Theta
into
X^{(1)}
and
X^{(2)}
. Furthermore, we set
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=\alpha
to control the type I error at a desired level
\alpha
. We balance
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
to yield empirically narrow average confidence intervals.

3.3
Point estimates

To obtain a point estimate for
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{x\right\}
in
eq.

2.5
conditional on
\widetilde{A}_{k}(X,x)
, we first consider a point estimate for the numerator. To do this, we maximize the likelihood based on the conditional density of
s_{k}\{X\}
in (
2.1
). As this is a function of
\Theta
only through
u_{k}\{x\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x\}
, the point estimate for
u_{k}\{x\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x\}
conditional on
\widetilde{A}_{k}(X,x)
is

\hat{\delta}_{k,\mathrm{MLE}}\left(X\right):=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{\delta\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{h\left(s_{k}\{X\};\delta,s_{[-k]}\{X\}\right)}{\int_{s_{k+1}\{X\}}^{s_{k-1}\{X\}}h\left(t;\delta,s_{[-k]}\{X\}\right)\mathbb{I}\left[r\left(s^{(k)}\{X,t\}\right)\geq k\right]dt},

(3.10)

where
h\left(t;\delta,s_{[-k]}\{X\}\right)
is defined in (
2.10
).

We further note that
\left\|X\right\|_{F}^{2}-np\sigma^{2}
is an unbiased estimator of the denominator of
eq.

2.5
.
However,
\left\|X\right\|_{F}^{2}-np\sigma^{2}
is not unbiased for the denominator of
eq.

2.5
conditional on
\tilde{A}_{k}(X,x)
in (
3.1
).
Thus, as in
section

3.2
, for a positive constant
c
, we define
X^{(1)}:=X+cE^{\prime}
and
X^{(2)}:=X-\tfrac{1}{c}E^{\prime}
for
E^{\prime}\sim\text{MN}(0,I_{n},\sigma^{2}I_{p})
; note that
X^{(1)}
and
X^{(2)}
are independent
(
Rasines and Young 2023
;
Neufeld et al. 2024
;
Leiner et al. 2023
)
.
Our point estimate for
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{x^{(1)}\right\}
conditional on
\widetilde{A}_{k}(X^{(1)},x^{(1)})
is therefore

\mathrm{PVE}_{k,MLE}\left\{X^{(1)},X^{(2)}\right\}:=\frac{\hat{\delta}_{k,MLE}^{2}\left(X^{(1)}\right)}{\left\|X^{(2)}\right\|_{F}^{2}-np\sigma^{2}_{c}},

(3.11)

where
\sigma^{2}_{c}:=\sigma^{2}\left(1+c^{-2}\right)
.

4
Computation of
\mathbb{P}_{\delta,k}(X)

We saw in Section
3
that the quantity
\mathbb{P}_{\delta,k}(X)
, defined in (
2.2
), is key to inference on
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{X\right\}
. Computing
\mathbb{P}_{\delta,k}(X)
in (
2.2
) requires evaluating the indicator variable
\mathbb{I}\left[r\left(s^{(k)}\{X,t\}\right)\geq k\right]
.
If
r(s\{X\})=p
— that is, if we intend to conduct inference on the PVE of all
p
principal components — then this indicator always equals one.
However, if the function
r(\cdot):\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,p\}
corresponds to a data-driven selection event, then we must
characterize the set

\left\{t:k\leq r\left(s^{(k)}\{X,t\}\right)\right\}

(4.1)

in order to compute (
2.2
).
This set will of course depend on the form of
r(\cdot)
.

We now consider two possible choices of
r(\cdot)
.
Both are instantiations of the elbow rule, in the sense that they seek an “elbow” in the scree plot
(
Jolliffe 2002
;
Greenacre et al. 2022
;
Nguyen and Holmes 2019
;
Zhu and Ghodsi 2006
)
.

4.1
Computation with the discrete second derivative rule

The discrete second derivative is defined as

\kappa_{k}(s\{X\}):=s^{2}_{k-1}\{X\}-2s^{2}_{k}\{X\}+s^{2}_{k+1}\{X\},

(4.2)

and we define the
derivative rule
— an elbow rule based on the discrete second derivative — as

r(s\{X\}):=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{k\in\{1,\dots,p-2\}}\kappa_{k+1}(s\{X\}),

(4.3)

which will be unique almost surely.
Thus, the
\left(r(s\{X\})+1\right)
th singular value has the largest discrete second derivative, and so the elbow occurs at the
\left(r(s\{X\})+1\right)
th index. This implies that the last
p-r(s\{X\})
principal components capture predominantly noise, so only the first
r(s\{X\})
principal components are retained for further study.

We now explicitly characterize the set (
4.1
) in the case of the derivative rule (
4.2
)–(
4.3
); it takes the form of one or two compact intervals.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, and
k\leq r(s\{X\})
, for
r(\cdot)
defined in (
4.3
). We define the values

\displaystyle c_{1,k}(X)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\underset{i\in\left\{2,\dots,k-2\right\}}{\max}\kappa_{i}\left(s\{X\}\right)&\mathrm{if}\,k\geq 4\\
-\infty&\mathrm{otherwise}\end{cases},

\displaystyle c_{2,k}(X)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\underset{i\in\left\{k+2,\dots,p-1\right\}}{\max}\kappa_{i}\left(s\{X\}\right)&\mathrm{if}\,k\leq p-3\\
-\infty&\mathrm{otherwise}\end{cases},

where
\kappa_{i}(s\{X\})
is defined in (
4.2
).
Define the intervals

\displaystyle A_{k}(X)

\displaystyle:=[\max\{{\textstyle\frac{1}{3}}\left(s_{k-1}^{2}\{X\}+3s_{k+1}^{2}\{X\}-s_{k+2}^{2}\{X\}\right),

\displaystyle\quad\quad\,\,c_{1,k}(X)+2s_{k+1}^{2}\{X\}-s_{k+2}^{2}\{X\}\},\infty)\quad\mathrm{for}\,k\geq 1,

\displaystyle B_{2}(X)

\displaystyle:=\left({\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\left(s_{1}^{2}\{X\}+s_{3}^{2}\{X\}-c_{2,2}(X)\right),\infty\right),

\displaystyle B_{k}(X)

\displaystyle:=\left[{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\left(s_{k-1}^{2}\{X\}+s_{k+1}^{2}\{X\}-c_{2,k}(X)\right),2s_{k-1}^{2}\{X\}-s_{k-2}^{2}\{X\}+c_{2,k}(X)\right]\quad\mathrm{for}\,k\geq 3.

It holds that

\left\{t:k\leq r\left(s^{(k)}\{X,t\}\right)\right\}=\begin{cases}[0,\infty)&\mathrm{if}\,k=1\\
A_{k}(X)&\mathrm{if}\,c_{1,k}(X)>c_{2,k}(X)\,\mathrm{or}\,|c_{2,k}(X)|=\infty\\
B_{k}(X)&\mathrm{if}\,k\geq 3\,\mathrm{and}\\
&s_{k-2}^{2}\{X\}-2s_{k-1}^{2}\{X\}>-2s_{k+1}^{2}\{X\}+s_{k+2}^{2}\{X\}\\
A_{k}(X)\cup B_{k}(X)&\mathrm{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

Thus,
\left\{t:k\leq r\left(s^{(k)}\{X,t\}\right)\right\}
is either an interval or the union of two intervals.

The key insight of
proposition

4.1
is that to compute
\mathbb{P}_{\delta,k}(X)
in (
2.2
), we can numerically integrate
h(t;\delta,s_{[-k]}\{X\})
in (
2.10
) over either one or two intervals.
We note that in computing
\mathbb{P}_{\delta,k}(X)
, the set
\left\{t:k\leq r\left(s^{(k)}\{X,t\}\right)\right\}
will be intersected with
[s_{k+1}(X),s_{k-1}(X)]
; thus, values outside this interval are not of interest.
proposition

4.1
is illustrated in
fig.

2
.

4.2
Computation with the
Zhu and Ghodsi 2006
elbow rule

Next, we consider an elbow rule motivated by the proposal of

Zhu and Ghodsi 2006
; we call this the
ZG rule
.

Zhu and Ghodsi 2006

separately model the first
k
and last
p-k
squared singular values with Gaussian densities, and then select the index
k
that maximizes the resulting density: that is,

r(s\{X\}):=1+\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,max}}_{k\in\{1,\dots,p-1\}}\ell_{k}(s\{X\})

(4.4)

where

\displaystyle\ell_{k}(s\{X\})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log f_{\Theta}\left(s^{2}_{i}\{X\};\hat{\mu}_{k,1}(s^{2}\{X\}),\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{k}(s^{2}\{X\})\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=k+1}^{p}\log f_{\Theta}\left(s^{2}_{i}\{X\};\hat{\mu}_{k,2}(s^{2}\{X\}),\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{k}(s^{2}\{X\})\right),

(4.5)

and

f(\cdot;\mu,\sigma^{2})
is a normal density with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
. Here
\hat{\mu}_{k,1}
and
\hat{\mu}_{k,2}
are the sample means of the first
k
and last
p-k
squared singular values, respectively, and
\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{k}(s^{2}\{X\})
is a pooled estimate of the variance.

Unlike the derivative rule, the ZG rule in (
4.4
) does not immediately yield a closed-form characterization of the set in (
4.1
). Instead, we will numerically evaluate this set. The likelihood in (
4.2
) is continuous, and for
k\in\left\{2,\dots,p\right\}
, the
k
th singular value is bounded. We can numerically approximate the set
\{t:k\leq r\left(s^{(k)}\{X,t\}\right)\}
by performing a line search over candidate values of
t
between
s_{k+1}\{X\}
and
s_{k-1}\{X\}
, and manually checking whether the condition
k\leq r\left(s^{(k)}\{X,t\}\right)
is satisfied. We then compute
\mathbb{P}_{\delta,k}(X)
via numerical integration over these regions. In practice, (
4.1
) appears to be the union of one or two intervals.

5.3
Hypothesis testing simulations

5.3.1
Selective type 1 error control under the global null

We now investigate whether the test of
H_{0,k}(X):\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{X\right\}=0
developed in Section
3.1
controls the selective type 1 error, defined as (
3.2
). We generate data under the global null hypothesis (i.e.,
\Theta=0_{n\times p}
, so that
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{X\right\}=0
for all
X
and all
k
, by definition) and
\sigma^{2}=1
, and apply the ZG rule to select an index
r(s\{X\})
, and then test
H_{0,k}(X)
at level
\alpha=0.1
for
k\leq r(s\{X\})
.

The qq-plot in the right-hand panel of
fig.

1
displays the distribution of p-values under the global null hypothesis. The plot is stratified by the value of
k
, corresponding to the null hypothesis
H_{0,k}(X):\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{X\right\}=0
tested.
The p-values that account for selection using the ZG rule follow the
45^{\circ}
line, and thus are uniformly distributed under the null. Furthermore, the p-values that do not account for selection using the ZG rule follow the
45^{\circ}
line when
k=1
, i.e., when the first principal component is tested: this is because the ZG rule
always
selects the first principal component, and so no adjustment for the ZG rule is required. However, as
k
increases, the latter p-values become increasingly anticonservative, due to failure to account for the fact that we test
H_{0,k}(X)
only for
k\leq r(s\{X\})
. In other words, the test that does not account for the ZG rule has poorest error control when the ZG rule incorrectly suggests that there is substantial signal in the matrix, i.e., in the exact setting where error control is important. The right hand panel of
fig.

1
clearly indicates that failure to account for the ZG rule leads to anti-conservative p-values, and therefore the corresponding tests have inflated type 1 error. Thus, in the rest of this section, we only show results for tests, confidence intervals, and point estimates that
do
account for selection via the ZG rule.

5.3.2
Selective power under the alternative

To evaluate the selective power of the test of
H_{0,k}(X):\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{X\right\}=0
under the alternative in
section

5.1
, we simulate datasets for
\sigma\in\{0.1,0.2,0.4,0.5,0.7,1\}
(recall that
{\bf{\rm rank}}(\Theta)=5
, with details in
section

B.1
). We define the following metrics:

Detection probability

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{\# [datasets where }r(s\{X\})={\bf{\rm rank}}(\Theta)]}{\text{\# datasets}},

Selective power at
k

\displaystyle=\frac{~\text{\# [datasets where we reject }H_{0,k}(X)\text{ and }k\leq r(s\{X\})]}{\text{\# [datasets where }k\leq r(s\{X\})]}.

The detection probability is the fraction of simulated datasets for which the ZG rule selects the correct rank, whereas the selective power at
k
is the fraction of simulated datasets for which we reject
H_{0,k}(X)
, out of those for which the ZG rule selects at least rank
k
.

In the left-hand panel of
fig.

4
, we plot the detection probability of the ZG rule against the inverse variance. As the variance decreases, the probability that the ZG rule selects the correct rank increases. The right-hand panel displays the selective power of the selective test as a function of the inverse variance, stratified by the value of
k
in the tested null hypothesis
H_{0,k}(X):\mathrm{PVE}_{k}\left\{X\right\}=0
.
We see that for a given value of

6
Discussion

In this paper, we consider inference on the PVE of the principal components of a dataset. While the sample PVE is often reported in scientific papers, we define a population parameter corresponding to this quantity, and propose an associated inferential framework.

The population version of the PVE that we define reflects the PVE of the
unknown population mean
— not the observed data — captured by the principal components. We further provide (i) tests of hypotheses involving the population PVE with valid type 1 error control, (ii) confidence intervals for the population PVE that attain the nominal coverage, and (iii) point estimates for the population PVE.

In this paper, we conduct inference conditional on several events. The motivations for each of these conditioning events are distinct:

1.

The population PVE that we propose is an unusual quantity, as it involves the
k
th singular vectors of
X
. Thus, to make this parameter deterministic we condition on these singular vectors.

2.

As in
Choi et al. 2017
, we further condition on all remaining singular vectors, as well as all but the
k
th singular value, to eliminate nuisance parameters. However, our final inferential guarantees hold even without conditioning on these quantities.

3.

Data analysts typically are interested in the PVE for only a subset of principal components; this subset is often selected via the ZG rule, or via another function of the singular values. Thus, we further condition on the event that a given procedure, such as the ZG rule, selected this particular principal component for inference.

Multiple avenues of future work remain. First, we have assumed knowledge of the variance,
\sigma^{2}
, in (
2.1
). Of course, this quantity is typically unknown and must be estimated. Possible estimators include the scaled squared median of the singular values
(
Gavish and Donoho 2014
)
or the minimum mean of squared residuals identified through cross-validation
(
Choi et al. 2017
)
. Moreover, the power of the proposed approach could be increased by introducing auxiliary randomization, as in
Panigrahi et al. 2023
. Lastly, it is an open question how to best choose the data thinning constant
c
, as well as
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
.

Appendix A
Theory and proofs

A.1
Inference on a centered matrix

In this paper, we have assumed that
X\sim\text{MN}(\Theta,\sigma^{2}I_{n}\otimes I_{p})
(see (
2.1
)). However, in practice, PCA is typically performed on a column-centered matrix,
CX
, where

C:=I_{n}-\frac{1}{n}1_{n}1_{n}^{\top}
is a rank-
(n-1)
projection matrix.
Thus,

CX\sim\text{MN}(C\Theta,C,\sigma^{2}I_{p}).

The results in this paper cannot be directly applied to
CX
, as its distribution does not take the form in (
2.1
).
To be explicit, let
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}^{\Theta}\{X\}
denote the PVE of the
k
th singular vectors of
X
with respect to
\Theta
. The following result shows that we can instead apply the results in this paper to
H^{\top}X
, where
H
is a matrix square root of
C
, to conduct inference on
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}^{C\Theta}\{CX\}
.

Proposition A.1

(Inference after column-centering
X
)
.

Let
X\sim\text{MN}(\Theta,I_{n},\sigma^{2}I_{p})
and define the centering matrix
C:=I_{n}-\frac{1}{n}1_{n}1_{n}^{\top}
.
Furthermore, take
H
to be a
n\times(n-1)
matrix square root of
C
that satisfies
HH^{\top}=C
and
H^{\top}H=I_{n-1}
. Then, (i)

H^{\top}X\sim\text{MN}(H^{\top}\Theta,I_{n-1}\otimes\sigma^{2}I_{p})
;
and (ii)
\mathrm{PVE}_{k}^{H^{\top}\Theta}\{H^{\top}X\}=\mathrm{PVE}_{k}^{C\Theta}\{CX\}
.

Proof.

Claim (i) follows directly from the fact that
X\sim\text{MN}(\Theta,I_{n},\sigma^{2}I_{p})
and that
H^{\top}H=I_{n-1}
.

We will now establish claim (ii).
Recall that
U\{A\}{\rm diag}(s\{A\})V\{A\}^{\top}
denotes the SVD of the matrix
A
.
Thus,

\displaystyle CX=H(H^{\top}X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H\left(U\{H^{\top}X\}{\rm diag}(s\{H^{\top}X\})V\{H^{\top}X\}^{\top}\right)

(A.1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(HU\{H^{\top}X\}\right){\rm diag}(s\{H^{\top}X\})V\{H^{\top}X\}^{\top}.

Furthermore,
\left(HU\{H^{\top}X\})\right)^{\top}HU\{H^{\top}X\}=I_{p}
. Thus, it follows that the SVD of
CX
is closely related to the SVD of
H^{\top}X
: that is,

U\{CX\}=HU\{H^{\top}X\},\quad V\{CX\}=V\{H^{\top}X\},\quad s\{CX\}=s\{H^{\top}X\}.

(A.2)

Thus, the singular values of
CX
and
H^{\top}X
are equal. An important corollary of this is that

\left\|C\Theta\right\|_{F}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}s_{k}^{2}\{C\Theta\}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}s_{k}^{2}\{H^{\top}\Theta\}=\left\|H^{\top}\Theta\right\|_{F}^{2}.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle u_{k}\{CX\}^{\top}\left(C\Theta\right)v_{k}\{CX\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{k}\{H^{\top}X\}^{\top}H^{\top}\left(C\Theta\right)v_{k}\{H^{\top}X\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle u_{k}\{H^{\top}X\}^{\top}\left(H^{\top}\Theta\right)v_{k}\{H^{\top}X\}.

Thus, the quantities

\mathrm{PVE}_{k}^{C\Theta}\{CX\}=\frac{\left(u_{k}\{CX\}^{\top}C\Theta v_{k}\{CX\}\right)^{2}}{\left\|C\Theta\right\|_{F}^{2}}

(A.3)

and

\mathrm{PVE}_{k}^{H^{\top}\Theta}\{H^{\top}X\}=\frac{\left(u_{k}\{H^{\top}X\}^{\top}H^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{H^{\top}X\}\right)^{2}}{\left\|H^{\top}\Theta\right\|_{F}^{2}}

are equal.
∎

has the coverage guarantee

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr}\left[u_{k}\{x^{(1)}\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x^{(1)}\}\in\text{C}_{\alpha_{1},k}\left(X^{(1)}\right)~|~\widetilde{A}_{k}\left(X^{(1)},x^{(1)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathbb{P}_{u_{k}\{x^{(1)}\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x^{(1)}\},\,k}\left(X^{(1)}\right)\in[\alpha_{1}/2,1-\alpha_{1}/2]~|~\widetilde{A}_{k}\left(X^{(1)},x^{(1)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathbb{P}_{u_{k}\{x^{(1)}\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x^{(1)}\},\,k}\left(X^{(1)}\right)\leq 1-\alpha_{1}/2~|~\widetilde{A}_{k}\left(X^{(1)},x^{(1)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad-\mathrm{Pr}\left[\mathbb{P}_{u_{k}\{x^{(1)}\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x^{(1)}\},\,k}\left(X^{(1)}\right)\leq\alpha_{1}/2~|~\widetilde{A}_{k}\left(X^{(1)},x^{(1)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{1}/2)-(\alpha_{1}/2)

\displaystyle=1-\alpha_{1}.

(A.9)

To construct a confidence interval for
\left(u_{k}\{x^{(1)}\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x^{(1)}\}\right)^{2}
, we define
\left[\widetilde{L}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right),\widetilde{U}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right)\right]
where

\displaystyle\widetilde{L}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right)

\displaystyle:=\min\left\{\left|L_{\alpha_{1}/2}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right)\right|,\left|U_{\alpha_{1}/2}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right)\right|\right\}^{2}\mathbb{I}[\mathrm{sign}\left(L_{\alpha_{1}/2}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right)U_{\alpha_{1}/2}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right)\right)=1]

\displaystyle\widetilde{U}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right)

\displaystyle:=\max\left\{\left|L_{\alpha_{1}/2}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right)\right|,\left|U_{\alpha_{1}/2}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right)\right|\right\}^{2}.

The definition of the minimum accounts for the fact that
\left[L_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right),U_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right)\right]
may cover zero; if zero is covered, the bounds have opposite signs and the lower bound of the confidence interval for the square must be zero.

Now recall our confidence interval from
eq.

3.8
is

\text{CI}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},k}\left(X^{(1)},X^{(2)}\right):=\left[\frac{\widetilde{L}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right)}{U_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}\left(X^{(2)}\right)},\frac{\widetilde{U}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right)}{L_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}\left(X^{(2)}\right)}\right].

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(\frac{\left(u_{k}\{x^{(1)}\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x^{(1)}\}\right)^{2}}{\|\Theta\|_{F}^{2}}\in\text{CI}_{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},k}\left(X^{(1)},X^{(2)}\right)\mid\tilde{A}_{k}\left(X^{(1)},x^{(1)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\Pr\Bigg(\left\{\left(u_{k}\{x^{(1)}\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x^{(1)}\}\right)^{2}\in\left[\widetilde{L}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right),\widetilde{U}_{\alpha_{1}}^{\mathrm{num}}\left(X^{(1)}\right)\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cap\left\{\left\|\Theta\right\|_{F}^{2}\in[L_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}\left(X^{(2)}\right),U_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}\left(X^{(2)}\right)]\right\}\mid\tilde{A}_{k}\left(X^{(1)},x^{(1)}\right)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\Pr\Bigg(\left\{u_{k}\{X^{(1)}\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{X^{(1)}\}\in\text{C}_{\alpha_{1},k}\left(X^{(1)}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\cap\left\{\|\Theta\|_{F}^{2}\in[L_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}\left(X^{(2)}\right),U_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}\left(X^{(2)}\right)]\right\}\mid\tilde{A}_{k}\left(X^{(1)},x^{(1)}\right)\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\Pr\left(u_{k}\{x^{(1)}\}^{\top}\Theta v_{k}\{x^{(1)}\}\in\text{C}_{\alpha_{1},k}\left(X^{(1)}\right)\mid\tilde{A}_{k}\left(X^{(1)},x^{(1)}\right)\right)\Pr\left(\|\Theta\|_{F}^{2}\in[L_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}\left(X^{(2)}\right),U_{\alpha_{2}}^{\mathrm{denom}}\left(X^{(2)}\right)]\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\alpha_{1})(1-\alpha_{2})

\displaystyle\geq 1-\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{2}.

∎

A.3
Proofs of Section 4 results

Proof of
proposition

4.1
.

We wish to characterize the set

R(x,k):=\left\{t:k\leq r\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)\right\},

where the function
r(\cdot)
is defined in (
4.3
). Recall that for our quantities to be well defined for all possible elbow-selected values
k\in\{1,\dots,p-2\}
, we have defined
\kappa_{i}(s\{x\})=-\infty
for
i\not\in\{2,\dots,p-1\}
. From (
4.3
), we immediately see that this set can be re-written as

R(x,k)=\left\{t:\max_{i\in\left\{2,\dots,k\right\}}\kappa_{i}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)\leq\max_{i\in\left\{k+1,\dots,p-1\right\}}\kappa_{i}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)\right\},

since this is the event where the maximizer occurs at an index greater than
k
. To define
R(x,k)
more explicitly, we define

c_{1,k}(x):=\begin{cases}\underset{i\in\left\{2,\dots,k-2\right\}}{\max}\kappa_{i}\left(s\{x\}\right)&\text{if }k\geq 4\\
-\infty&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad c_{2,k}(x):=\begin{cases}\underset{i\in\left\{k+2,\dots,p-1\right\}}{\max}\kappa_{i}\left(s\{x\}\right)&\text{if }k\leq p-3\\
-\infty&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

and, recalling the definition of
s^{(k)}\{x,t\}
in
eq.

2.9
, rewrite it as

R(x,k)=\left\{t:\max\left\{\kappa_{k-1}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right),\kappa_{k}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right),c_{1,k}(x)\right\}\leq\max\left\{\kappa_{k+1}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right),c_{2,k}(x)\right\}\right\},

or equivalently as

R(x,k)=A_{k}\cup B_{k},

(A.10)

where we define

\begin{split}A_{k}&:=\left\{t:\max\left\{\kappa_{k-1}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right),\kappa_{k}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right),c_{1,k}(x)\right\}\leq\kappa_{k+1}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)\right\}\\
B_{k}&:=\left\{t:\max\left\{\kappa_{k-1}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right),\kappa_{k}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right),c_{1,k}(x)\right\}\leq c_{2,k}(x)\right\}.\end{split}

(A.11)

How can we interpret
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
? We can see that
A_{k}
restricts the elbow from occurring at index
k+1
while
B_{k}
restricts the elbow from occurring at index
k+2
or later. It is worth considering a couple of edge cases first.

If
k=1
, then by definition
\kappa_{k-1}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)
,
\kappa_{k}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)
, and
c_{1,k}(x)
are all negative infinity. Thus, since singular values are positive,
t
can take any positive value. This is made obvious by the fact that we will always select an index greater than or equal to
1
with the elbow rule.

If
k=2
, then
\kappa_{k}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)
is finite and contributes to a useful bound. The terms
\kappa_{k-1}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)
and
c_{1,k}(x)
are negative infinity and so, recalling the definition of the discrete second derivative in (
4.2
), we can write

\begin{split}A_{2}&:=\left\{t:\kappa_{k}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)\leq\kappa_{k+1}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)\right\}\\
&=\left\{t:s_{k-1}^{2}\{x\}-2t^{2}+s_{k+1}^{2}\{x\}\leq t^{2}-2s_{k+1}^{2}\{x\}+s_{k+2}^{2}\{x\}\right\}\\
&=\left[\frac{1}{3}\left(s_{k-1}^{2}\{x\}+3s_{k+1}^{2}\{x\}-s_{k+2}^{2}\{x\}\right),\infty\right).\end{split}

(A.12)

If
c_{2,k}(x)
is finite, then
B_{2}
is non-empty and we have

\begin{split}B_{2}&:=\left\{t:\kappa_{k}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)\leq c_{2,k}(x)\right\}\\
&=\left\{t:s_{k-1}^{2}\{x\}-2t^{2}+s_{k+1}^{2}\{x\}\leq c_{2,k}(x)\right\}\\
&=\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(s_{k-1}^{2}\{x\}+s_{k+1}^{2}\{x\}-c_{2,k}(x)\right),\infty\right).\end{split}

(A.13)

If
k\geq 3
, then the discrete derivatives of interest are all well defined and we can write
A_{k}
in (
A.11
) as the intersection of sets of solutions to the three inequalities:

(i)

s_{k-2}^{2}\{x\}-2s_{k-1}^{2}\{x\}+t^{2}=\kappa_{k-1}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)\leq\kappa_{k+1}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)=t^{2}-2s_{k+1}^{2}\{x\}+s_{k+2}^{2}\{x\}
,

(ii)

s_{k-1}^{2}\{x\}-2t^{2}+s_{k+1}^{2}\{x\}=\kappa_{k}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)\leq\kappa_{k+1}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)=t^{2}-2s_{k+1}^{2}\{x\}+s_{k+2}^{2}\{x\}
,

(iii)

c_{1,k}(x)\leq\kappa_{k+1}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)=t^{2}-2s_{k+1}^{2}\{x\}+s_{k+2}^{2}\{x\}
.

Rearranging to solve for
t^{2}
in each case —- which is one-to-one with
t
since singular values are non-negative by definition — we first notice that the
t^{2}
terms in (i) cancel, leaving us with the inequality

s_{k-2}^{2}\{x\}-2s_{k-1}^{2}\{x\}\leq-2s_{k+1}^{2}\{x\}+s_{k+2}^{2}\{x\},

(A.14)

which does not involve
t
. If this inequality does not hold, then
A_{k}
in (
A.11
) is the empty set. Otherwise, (ii) and (iii) can be combined into

\max\left\{s_{k-1}^{2}\{x\}+3s_{k+1}^{2}\{x\}-s_{k+2}^{2}\{x\},c_{1,k}(x)+2s_{k+1}^{2}\{x\}-s_{k+2}^{2}\{x\}\right\}\leq t^{2}.

Note that in the event that
c_{1,k}(x)=-\infty
, only inequality (ii) will contribute to the lower bound of
t^{2}
.

We likewise write
B_{k}
in (
A.11
) as the intersection of sets of solutions to the three inequalities:

(i’)

c_{1,k}(x)\leq c_{2,k}(x)
and
|c_{2,k}(x)|<\infty
.

(ii’)

s_{k-2}^{2}\{x\}-2s_{k-1}^{2}\{x\}+t^{2}=\kappa_{k-1}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)\leq c_{2,k}(x)
,

(iii’)

s_{k-1}^{2}\{x\}-2t^{2}+s_{k+1}^{2}\{x\}=\kappa_{k}\left(s^{(k)}\{x,t\}\right)\leq c_{2,k}(x)
.

Similarly to before, if the inequality in (i’) does not hold, then
B_{k}
in (
A.11
) is the empty set. Note that this also is true for the case
k=2
, as seen in the resulting proposition. Otherwise, (ii’) and (iii’) can be combined into

\frac{1}{2}\left(s_{k-1}^{2}\{x\}+s_{k+1}^{2}\{x\}-c_{2,k}(x)\right)\leq t^{2}\leq 2s_{k-1}^{2}\{x\}-s_{k-2}^{2}\{x\}+c_{2,k}(x).

(A.15)

It follows that the set
R(x,k)
is one or both of the intervals
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
, depending on which of conditions (i) and (i’) hold. From (
A.14
) and (
A.15
), we can write
A_{k}
and
B_{k}
as explicit intervals as in the proposition statement.

Finally, we note that at least one of (i) and (i’) must hold. Otherwise, by (
A.11
),
R(x,k)
would be the union of two empty sets and hence itself the empty set. However, we know that
k\leq r(s\{x\})
by definition of
k
in the proposition.
Therefore,
t=s_{k}\{x\}\in R(x,k)
, and thus the set
R(x,k)
is non-empty.
∎
