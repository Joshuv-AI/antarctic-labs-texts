Surface groups among cubulated hyperbolic and one-relator groups
Henry Wilton

Abstract
Let X X be a non-positively curved cube complex with hyperbolic fundamental group. We prove that π 1 ​ ( X ) \pi_{1}(X) has a non-free subgroup of infinite index unless π 1 ​ ( X ) \pi_{1}(X) is either free or a surface group, answering questions of Gromov and Whyte (in a special case) and Wise. A similar result for one-relator groups follows, answering a question posed by several authors. The proof relies on a careful analysis of free and cyclic splittings of cubulated groups.



Surface groups – the fundamental groups of closed, aspherical surfaces – and free groups are often seen as close cousins. One way this can be made precise is to look at their subgroup structures. Let

The proof of Theorem
A
is inspired by the author’s proof of the corresponding statement for graphs of free groups with cyclic edge groups
[
65
]
. That proof relied on Whitehead theory to recognise relative free splittings of free groups, so our first task is to generalise that machinery to the context of non-positively curved cube complexes.

Let
X
be a compact, non-positively curved cube complex. We consider compact, convex subcomplexes
Y
of the universal cover
\widetilde{X}
, and define a
Whitehead complex

\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
. This is a simplicial complex that encodes the intersections of the bounding hyperplanes of
Y
, and provides a topological model for the complement
\widetilde{X}\smallsetminus Y
. We will be especially interested in the minimal cardinality
k
of a cut set of vertices of
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
: if there is such a cut set, then
Y
is called a
k
-cut
. An essential cube complex
X
has a 0-cut if and only if
\pi_{1}(X)
has more than one end.

We analyse the behaviour of the Whitehead complex when
Y
is cut along a hyperplane. It turns out that
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
decomposes as a connect sum in the natural sense for simplicial complexes, in a way that generalises the work of Manning
[
42
]
and Cashen–Macura
[
16
]
in the classical context of Whitehead graphs. Our key technical results analyse how cut sets of vertices behave under the connect sum operation. This enables us to relate cuts to links of vertices. The following criterion, a cubical analogue of Whitehead’s cut-vertex lemma, is a sample application.

Theorem E
.

Let
X
be a compact, non-positively curved cube complex. If the link of every vertex is connected and has no cut simplices then
\pi_{1}(X)
has at most one end.

While 0-cuts detect free splittings, 1-cuts turn out to obstruct analysing splittings over non-trivial subgroups. Fortunately, using recent work of Shepherd
[
55
]
, as long as
\pi_{1}(X)
is one-ended we may assume that
X
has no 1-cuts.

Assuming
\pi_{1}(X)
is one-ended, our next task is to analyse splittings over
\mathbb{Z}
, and to do this we restrict to the case when
\pi_{1}(X)
is hyperbolic. The approach generalises work of Cashen–Macura
[
16
]
and Cashen
[
15
]
, who showed that a line pattern in a free group does not split over
\mathbb{Z}
if and only if there is a bound on the diameters of cut pairs in the Whitehead graph. We prove an analogous result, Theorem
3.13
, which asserts that
\pi_{1}(X)
is cyclically indecomposable if and only if there is a uniform bound on the widths of 2-cuts. The point is that cyclic splittings correspond to
periodic
2-cuts, from which a pigeonhole argument implies a bound on the width of a 2-cut if
\pi_{1}(X)
is cyclically indecomposable. Using the projection-complex machinery of Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara
[
6
]
, and their improved version with Sisto
[
7
]
, we strengthen this to a uniform bound on the widths of
k
-cuts, independent of
k
.

With these technical results in hand, we are ready to prove Theorem
A
. Using the Grushko and JSJ decompositions, and since the case of graphs of free groups with cyclic edge groups is already known, we may reduce to the cyclically indecomposable case. By the above analysis, we may assume that
X
has no 0- or 1-cuts, and that there is a uniform bound
C
on the widths of
k
-cuts. Agol’s theorem
[
1
]
implies that hyperplane stabilisers are separable, so we may pass to a finite-sheeted cover
X_{0}\to X
with the property that, for some hyperplane
H
of
\widetilde{X}
, every
\pi_{1}(X_{0})
-translate of
H
is at distance
>C
from
H
. Cutting
X_{0}
along
H
and passing to a connected component
X^{\prime}
gives a quasiconvex subgroup that is necessarily of infinite index. But now a
0
-cut in
X^{\prime}
would lead to a
k
-cut in
X
of width greater than
C
, which is a contradiction, so
\pi_{1}(X^{\prime})
is not free, as required.

1
Preliminaries

Two kinds of cell complexes will be of particular importance in this paper. On the one hand, our main objects of study are non-positively curved cube complexes and their universal covers, which are CAT(0). On the other hand, we will study them by constructing certain simplicial complexes, called
Whitehead complexes
. In this section, we recall some basic facts about all these classes of complexes. The reader is referred to the standard reference
[
10
]
for background material on cube complexes and other metric polyhedral complexes, and for details of much of what follows.

All complexes that we consider will be locally finite.

1.1
Non-positively curved cube complxes

A
cube complex

X
is a cell complex constructed from a disjoint union of finite-dimensional unit cubes by identifying faces isometrically
[
10
, Example I.7.40(4)]
. Further, we will also assume that cubes are embedded, and that the intersection of any two cubes is either empty or a face (i.e.
X
is
cubical
in the sense of Bridson–Haefliger
[
10
, Definition I.7.32]
); this can always be ensured by subdividing.

The
link

\mathrm{Lk}_{X}(x)
of a point
x
in a metric polyhedral complex
X
is the space of directions at
x
, i.e. the space of germs of geodesics emanating from
x

[
10
, I.7.38]
, and admits a natural angular metric. When
X
is a cube complex and
x
is a vertex,
\mathrm{Lk}_{X}(x)
has the natural structure of a simplicial complex, where the simplices are the corners of the cubes incident at
x
.

As long as a cube complex
X
is finite-dimensional,
X
admits a natural complete geodesic metric in which every
k
-cube is locally isometric to
[0,1]^{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
. We will refer to this metric as the
\ell^{2}
-metric
on
X
. Gromov’s link condition asserts that a polyhedral complex is non-positively curved – i.e. the metric is locally CAT(0) – if and only if the angular metric on the link of any point is CAT(1)
[
27
, 4.2.A]
. Gromov further proved that, when
X
is a cube complex, the link condition is satisfied if and only if the link of every vertex is
flag
, meaning that, for every
k\geq 2
, every
k
-clique in the 1-skeleton of
X
is the 1-skeleton.

The features of a non-positively curved cube complex
X
can often be captured by studying its
hyperplanes
. Let
C
be a
k
-dimensional cube, identified with
[-1/2,1/2]^{k}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{k}
. A
hypercube
of
C
is an intersection of
C
with any coordinate hyperplane of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. The set of hypercubes of cubes in
X
form a natural directed system under inclusion, and gluing along these inclusions gives a cube complex with a natural map to
X
. The path components of this cube complexes are the
hyperplanes
of
X
.

It is immediate from the definition that the natural map from a hyperplane
H
to
X
is a local isometry. Therefore, if
X
is non-positively curved then
H
is too.

Since hyperplanes are constructed by gluing together hypercubes of
X
, each hyperplane
H\to X
admits a natural
(closed) cubical neighbourhood

N(H)
, which is a bundle over
H
with fibre a closed interval. We may write
N_{X}(H)
when we want to emphasise that
H
is a hyperplane of
X
. Likewise,
N(H)
has a natural
interior

\mathring{N}(H)
, also a bundle over
H
with fibre an open interval. The hyperplane
H
is said to be
two-sided
when these bundles are trivial.

If the map
H\to X
is injective we say that
H
is
embedded
. In this case, the interior
\mathring{N}(H)
is also naturally embedded in
X
. The complement
X_{0}=X\smallsetminus\mathring{N}(H)
is a locally convex subcomplex of
X
, and is said to be obtained by
cutting
X
along
H
. In this case, we write
X=X_{0}\cup_{H}
. When
H
is two-sided, this expresses
X
as a
graph of spaces
in the sense of Scott and Wall
[
52
]
. As long as
X
itself is connected, the vertex space
X_{0}
has at most two components, and if there are two then we write
X=X_{1}\cup_{H}X_{2}
in the obvious way.

Finally, we can make use of the following important lemma
[
10
, Proposition II.4.14]
.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
X,Y
be complete, connected, geodesic, metric spaces. If
X
is non-positively curved then any local isometry
Y\to X
is injective on fundamental groups.

Thus, the inclusion of a hyperplane is
\pi_{1}
-injective, so cutting along
H
also decomposes the fundamental group
G=\pi_{1}(X)
as a graph of groups in the sense of Serre
[
53
]
, with a single edge group
\pi_{1}(H)
.

1.2
CAT(0) cube complexes

When
X
is non-positively curved, the natural geodesic metric on the universal cover
\widetilde{X}
is CAT(0), by the Cartan–Hadamard theorem
[
27
, §4]
. This gives us access to the standard tools of CAT(0) geometry detailed in
[
10
]
, which are especially useful when studying a closed, convex subspace
Y\subseteq\widetilde{X}
. For instance, we may define the orthogonal projection
\pi_{Y}:\widetilde{X}\to Y
as in
[
10
, Proposition II.2.4]
.

One important consequence of the uniqueness of geodesics in CAT(0) spaces is that any local isometry between CAT(0) spaces is a global isometric embedding
[
10
, Proposition II.4.14]
. Thus, the map from a hyperplane
H\to X
lifts to a convex embedding of the universal cover
\widetilde{H}\hookrightarrow\widetilde{X}
, and so the hyperplanes of
\widetilde{X}
are the universal covers of the hyperplanes of
X
.

The natural map
N(H)\to X
is also a local isometry. Thus, the map
N(H)\to X
also lifts to an embedding

\widetilde{N(H)}=N(\widetilde{H})\hookrightarrow\widetilde{X}\,.

The interval bundle
N(\widetilde{H})
is necessarily trivial because
\widetilde{H}
is simply connected, so
\widetilde{H}
is embedded and two-sided, and cutting along
\widetilde{H}
realises
\widetilde{X}
as a graph of spaces. Because
\widetilde{X}
is simply connected it follows that
\widetilde{H}
separates it into two, and so we have

\widetilde{X}=\widetilde{U}_{+}\cup_{\widetilde{H}}\widetilde{U}_{-}

for two convex subcomplexes
\widetilde{U}_{\pm}
. These subcomplexes are called the
half-spaces
of
\widetilde{H}
.

It is usually important when studying CAT(0) cube complexes to rule out ‘uninteresting’ half-spaces that are within a bounded distance of the corresponding hyperplane.

Definition 1.2
.

A hyperplane
\widetilde{H}
of a CAT(0) cube complex
\widetilde{X}
is called
inessential
if some half-space of
\widetilde{H}
is within a bounded neighbourhood of
\widetilde{H}
. If
\widetilde{X}
has no inessential hyperplanes then
\widetilde{X}
itself is called
essential
. We will call a non-positively curved complex
X
essential or inessential according to its universal cover.

Fortunately, we may always assume that
X
is essential by invoking a result of Caprace–Sageev
[
14
, Proposition 3.5]
.

Lemma 1.3
.

Every compact, non-positively curved cube complex
X
deformation retracts to an essential, convex subcomplex of its first subdivision.

Hyperplanes also make it possible to define a notion of convex hull for a subset of
\widetilde{X}
.

Definition 1.4
.

For any subset
Y
of a CAT(0) cube complex
\widetilde{X}
, the
cubical convex hull

\Hull(Y)
is defined to be the intersection of the half-spaces of
\widetilde{X}
that contain
Y
.

It is important to be able to control the geometry of convex hulls, and we can do this using a fundamental theorem of Haglund. This theorem makes use of the
\ell^{1}
-metric
on (the vertices of)
\widetilde{X}
.

Definition 1.5
.

Let
\widetilde{X}
be a CAT(0) cube complex. The
\ell^{1}
-metric on the 1-skeleton
X^{(1)}
declares the distance between two points

and

to be equal to the length of the shortest path in
X^{(1)}
between them.

Note that the
\ell^{1}
-metric on
X^{(1)}
is also geodesic, and that the
\ell^{1}
distance between two vertices is exactly the number of hyperplanes that separate them
[
50
, Lemma 3.8]
.

The following lemma proves that orthogonal projection from a convex subcomplex to a hyperplane must land in their intersection, if it is non-empty.

Lemma 1.6
.

Let
\widetilde{X}
be a CAT(0) cube complex, let
Y\subseteq\widetilde{X}
be a convex subcomplex, and let
H
be a hyperplane of
\widetilde{X}
. If
H\cap Y
is non-empty then
\pi_{H}(y)\in Y
, for all
y\in Y
.

Proof.

Let
y\in Y
and
x=\pi_{H\cap Y}(y)
; we shall prove that
x=\pi_{H}(y)
. Since the result is trivial if
y=x
, we may assume that
y\neq x
and choose
z\neq x
in the intersection of the geodesic
[x,y]
with the carrier
N_{Y}(H\cap Y)
. But
x=\pi_{H\cap Y}(z)
so, in the product coordinates on
N_{Y}(H\cap Y)\cong(H\cap Y)\times[-1/2,1/2]
, we have
z=(x,t)
for some
t
. The product coordinates extend naturally to
N_{\widetilde{X}}(H)
, so the geodesic
[x,z]
is orthogonal to every geodesic in
H
through
x
, and hence
[x,y]
is also. Therefore,
x=\pi_{H}(y)
and the result follows.
∎

The next lemma is useful for relating the
\ell^{1}
and
\ell^{2}
distances.

Lemma 1.7
.

Let
Y_{1},Y_{2}
be convex subcomplexes of a CAT(0) cube complex
\widetilde{X}
. Suppose that a geodesic
\gamma
minimises the
\ell^{2}
-distance from

to
Y_{2}
. A hyperplane

separates

from
Y_{2}
if and only if
\gamma
crosses
H
.

Proof.

The necessity is obvious, so it remains to prove that, if
\gamma
crosses
H
, then
H
separates
Y_{1}
from
Y_{2}
. Suppose that
\gamma(0)\in Y_{1}
and
\gamma
crosses
H
at
\gamma(t)
. Because
\gamma
minimises the distance, it makes an angle of at least
\pi/2
with each geodesic in
Y_{1}
through
\gamma(0)
. By Lemma

,
\pi_{H}(\gamma(0))\in Y_{1}
. Hence, if

intersects

then
\gamma(0)
,

and
\pi_{H}(\gamma(0))
form a triangle with two right angles, contradicting the hypothesis that

is CAT(0). Therefore,

is disjoint from

, and likewise from

. Since
\gamma
crosses

once, it follows that

separates
Y_{1}
from

, as required.
∎

1.3
Simplicial complexes

As the above discussion makes clear, our investigations of cube complexes will often be metric. By contrast, when simplicial complexes arise, we will be interested in their combinatorial structure, and especially interested in collections of vertices and simplices that separate. Here, we introduce the necessary terminology.

A_{B}:=A\smallsetminus\mathrm{St}(B)
. The subcomplex
B
is said to
separate

A
if
A_{B}
is disconnected. If
B
is a simplex we say
B
is a
cut simplex
.

In the specific case where
B
is a set of
k

is a
cut set
for

. If
\#B=1
we say

is a

, and if
\#B=2
we say

is a
cut pair
. If

.

2
Free splittings

In 1936, J.H.C. Whitehead explained how to determine whether an element
w
of a free group
F
is primitive. For a fixed basis
B
of
F
, his algorithm uses a graph
\mathrm{Wh}_{B}(w)
which is now known as the
Whitehead graph

[
63
]
. His algorithm can equally be interpreted as searching for free splittings
F\cong\langle w\rangle*F^{\prime}
. Many elaborations and generalisations of Whitehead’s argument have been given over the years by, among others, Lyon
[
41
]
, Stallings
[
59
]
and Stong
[
60
]
. Diao–Feighn, building on work of Gersten
[
26
]
, gave an algorithm to compute the Grushko decomposition of a graph of free groups
[
18
]
.
3
3

3

See the recent preprint of Dicks for a comprehensive history of this topic
[
19
, §2]
.

The power of this point of view is that
global
information about a group
G
– whether or not
G
is free, or splits freely, or most generally the structure of the Grushko decomposition of
G
– is encoded in the
local
information of a Whitehead graph, which can be read off a presentation. The following theorem, which is the result of combining Whitehead’s algorithm with a theorem of Shenitzer
[
54
, Theorem 5]
, exemplifies this idea in the case of one-relator groups.

Theorem 2.1
.

Consider a one-relator group
G=F/\llangle w\rrangle
and fix a basis
B
for
F
. If the Whitehead graph
\mathrm{Wh}_{B}(w)
is connected without cut vertices, then
G
does not split freely.

Note that
\mathrm{Wh}_{B}(w)
is exactly the link of the unique vertex in the natural presentation complex for
G
. Our goal is to develop a similar theory for non-positively curved cube complexes. To this end, our first task is to generalise the notion of Whitehead graph to the context of non-positively curved cube complexes.

2.1
Bounding hyperplanes and Whitehead complexes

In this section, we introduce the
Whitehead complexes
that will be our main technical tool. While the use we put them to here appears to be new, they bear some similarities with
crossing graphs
and
crossing complexes
for CAT(0) cube complexes, which have been studied extensively in a wide variety of contexts – see, for instance,
[
24
,
28
,
45
,
49
]
, among many other examples.

Let
X
be a non-positively curved cube complex, let
\widetilde{X}\to X
be its universal cover and let
Y\subseteq\widetilde{X}
be a convex subcomplex. Equivalently, we may think of
Y
as a CAT(0) complex equipped with a locally isometric combinatorial map
Y\to X
– any such immersion lifts to an embedding
Y\hookrightarrow\widetilde{X}
, and all such embeddings differ by a deck transformation of
X
.

Definition 2.2
.

A hyperplane
H
of
\widetilde{X}

bounds

Y
if it is disjoint from
Y
but no other hyperplane separates
Y
from
H
. Equivalently,
H
and
Y
are disjoint, but
Y
intersects the (closed) regular neighbourhood
N(H)\cong H\times[0,1]
. We may orient the interval factor so that the intersection is of the form
H_{Y}\times\{0\}
, where
H_{Y}
is a convex subcomplex of
H
called the
bounding hyperplane component of
Y
in
H
.

The Whitehead complex associated to such a
Y
encodes the way that the bounding hyperplanes of
Y
intersect each other.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
X
be a non-positively curved cube complex, and
Y
a convex subcomplex of the universal cover
\widetilde{X}
. The
Whitehead complex
of
Y
,
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
, is a simplicial complex defined as follows. The vertices of
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
are the bounding hyperplanes of
Y
. A finite set of bounding hyperplanes
\mathcal{H}=\{H_{0},\ldots,H_{k}\}
spans a
k
-simplex
\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}
in
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
if and only if the intersection
\bigcap_{i=0}^{k}H_{i}
is non-empty.

Remark 2.4
.

The Helly property for hyperplanes of CAT(0) cube complexes, proved by Sageev, asserts that if a set of hyperplanes intersect pairwise then their intersection is non-empty
[
50
, Theorem 4.14]
. Hence, Whitehead complexes are flag.

The name is justified by the following example.

Example 2.5
.

Let
F
be a free group, thought of as the fundamental group of a bouquet of circles
R
. (This is equivalent to choosing a basis
B
for
F
.) The conjugacy class of a non-trivial element
w\in F
can be represented by an immersion
w:S^{1}\to R
. The mapping cylinder
X
of this immersion is naturally a non-positively curved square complex. Let
*
be the one-point space and consider the map
*\to X
with image equal to the unique vertex of
R
. Then
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(*)
is the Whitehead graph
\mathrm{Wh}_{B}(w)
, subdivided once.

More generally, in
[
16
]
, Cashen and Macura defined
generalised Whitehead graphs

\mathrm{Wh}_{B}(T)\{w\}
associated to a word
w
in a free group
F
, for any subtree
T
of the Cayley tree
\widetilde{R}
of
F
. Our complex
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(T)
is the graph obtained from
\mathrm{Wh}_{B}(T)\{w\}
by subdividing once.

The case when
Y
is a vertex provides another important example.

Example 2.6
.

Consider
\tilde{y}
a vertex of
\widetilde{X}
with image
y\in X
. The bounding hyperplanes of
\tilde{y}
are dual to the edges incident at
y
, and a set of such hyperplanes cross exactly when the edges bound a cube with corner
\tilde{y}
. Therefore,
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\tilde{y})\cong\mathrm{Lk}_{X}(y)
as simplicial complexes.

Whitehead complexes are useful because they provide a topological model for the complement of
Y
: we shall see that
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
is homotopy equivalent to the complement
\widetilde{X}\smallsetminus Y
. In order to describe this homotopy equivalence, it is useful to define the
link
of
Y
, and to extend our discussion of bounding hyperplanes to
codimension-
k
hyperplanes
.

Definition 2.7
.

Consider a set of
k
distinct hyperplanes
H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}
of
Y
that pairwise intersect. The intersection

K=\bigcap_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}

is called a
codimension-
k
hyperplane
. It has a closed regular neighbourhood
N(K)\cong K\times[0,1]^{k}
. If the
H_{i}
are all bounding hyperplanes of
Y
then
K
is called a
bounding codimension-
k
hyperplane of
Y
. In this case, we may orient the interval factors so that

N(K)\cap Y=K_{Y}\times\{0\}^{k}

for some convex subcomplex
K_{Y}
, called the
bounding codimension-
k
hyperplane component of
K
in
Y
.

The
link
of
K_{Y}\times\{0\}^{k}
in
K_{Y}\times[0,1]^{k}
consists of unit vectors orthogonal to
K_{Y}\times\{0\}^{k}
, and is naturally of the form
K_{Y}\times\sigma_{K}
, where
\sigma_{K}
is a
(k-1)
-simplex.

Whenever a codimension-
k
hyperplane
K
is contained in a codimension-
l
hyperplane
L
(necessarily with
l\geq k
), there are natural inclusions
K_{Y}\subseteq L_{Y}
and
\sigma_{L}\subseteq\sigma_{K}
, which in turn lead to inclusions

K_{Y}\times\sigma_{L}\subseteq K_{Y}\times\sigma_{K}\,,\,K_{Y}\times\sigma_{L}\subseteq L_{Y}\times\sigma_{L}\,.

(1)

Gluing along these inclusions defines the
link
of the subcomplex
Y
.

Definition 2.8
.

The
link
of a convex subcomplex
Y
of a CAT(0) cube complex
\widetilde{X}
is the cell complex
\mathrm{Lk}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)
defined from the disjoint union

\coprod_{K}K_{Y}\times\sigma_{K}

over all bounding hyperplane components of any codimension, by gluing along the natural inclusions (
1
). As usual, when
\widetilde{X}
is the universal cover of a non-positively curved cube complex
X
, we will write
\mathrm{Lk}_{X}(Y)
.

Remark 2.9
.

The link
\mathrm{Lk}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)
can also be described metrically as the germs of geodesics in
\widetilde{X}
that are orthogonal to
Y
. Thus, orthogonal projection defines a natural map
\hat{\pi}_{Y}:\widetilde{X}\smallsetminus Y\to\mathrm{Lk}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)
.

We can now prove that
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
is homotopy equivalent to the complement of
Y
in two stages.

Lemma 2.10
.

For any convex subcomplex
Y
of a CAT(0) cube complex
\widetilde{X}
, the orthogonal projection
\hat{\pi}_{Y}:\widetilde{X}\smallsetminus Y\to\mathrm{Lk}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)
is a homotopy equivalence.

Proof.

As in the remark, we identify the link
\mathrm{Lk}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)
with the set of germs of geodesics orthogonal to
Y
. Since
Y
is a subcomplex, every such germ extends uniquely to a geodesic of length
1
. Therefore,
\hat{\pi}_{Y}
has a well-defined right-inverse
\rho:\mathrm{Lk}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)\to\widetilde{X}\smallsetminus Y
sending the germ of
\gamma
to
\gamma(1)
. For any
x\notin Y
, let
\gamma_{x}
denote the unique geodesic from
\hat{\pi}_{Y}(x)
to
x
. Then

(x,t)\mapsto\gamma_{x}((1-t)d(x,Y)+t)

defines a homotopy equivalence between the identity and
\rho\circ\hat{\pi}_{Y}
, which completes the proof.
∎

Finally, we relate links to Whitehead complexes by contracting bounding hyperplane components.

Lemma 2.11
.

For any convex subcomplex
Y
of a CAT(0) cube complex
\widetilde{X}
, there is a homotopy equivalence
\mathrm{Lk}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)\simeq\mathrm{Wh}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)
.

Proof.

The link
\mathrm{Lk}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)
is homeomorphic to the realisation of a complex of spaces in the sense of Hatcher, with underlying complex the barycentric subdivision of
\mathrm{Wh}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)
. Each vertex corresponds to a codimension-
k
hyperplane
K
, the associated space is the hyperplane component
K_{Y}
, and the attaching maps are given by the natural inclusions. Since each
K_{Y}
is contractible, it follows that
\mathrm{Lk}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)\simeq\mathrm{Wh}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)
by
[
33
, Proposition 4G.1]
.
∎

The previous two lemmas combine to give the following fundamental result.

Lemma 2.12
.

For any convex subcomplex
Y
of a CAT(0) cube complex
\widetilde{X}
, there is a homotopy equivalence
\widetilde{X}\smallsetminus Y\simeq\mathrm{Wh}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)
.

The lemma can also be deduced from the nerve theorem, by noting that
\mathrm{Wh}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)
is the nerve of a natural open cover of
\widetilde{X}\smallsetminus Y
.

Finally, it will be useful to understand the links of vertices in Whitehead complexes. (Note that here, in the setting of simplicial complexes, we mean link in the sense of Definition
1.10
.)

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
Y
be a convex subcomplex of a CAT(0) cube complex
\widetilde{X}
and let
H
be a bounding hyperplane of
Y
. Then

\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathrm{Wh}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)}(H)\cong\mathrm{Wh}_{H}(H_{Y})\,.

Proof.

Since both simplicial complexes are flag, it suffices to define an isomorphism at the level of 1-skeleta.

The vertices of
\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathrm{Wh}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)}(H)
are, by definition, the bounding hyperplanes of
Y
that cross
H
. For every such hyperplane
K
, the intersection
H\cap K
is a bounding hyperplane of
H_{Y}
in
H
. Conversely, every bounding hyperplane of
H_{Y}
in
H
extends uniquely to a bounding hyperplane of
Y
that crosses
H
. Thus,
K\mapsto H\cap K
defines the required bijection on the 0-skeleta. The bijection extends to an isomorphism of 1-skeleta, because bounding codimension-three hyperplanes of
Y
contained in
H
(which correspond to edges of
\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathrm{Wh}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)}(H)
) are the same thing as bounding codimension-2 hyperplanes of
H_{Y}
in
H
(which correspond to edges of
\mathrm{Wh}_{H}(H_{Y})
).
∎

2.2
Ends and splittings

Invoking a famous theorem of Stallings, the number of
(Freudenthal) ends
of the universal cover of a non-positively curved cube complex
X
detects the free splittings of the fundamental group
[
58
]
. (Note that
\pi_{1}(X)
is necessarily torsion-free.)

Theorem 2.14

(Stallings)
.

Let
X
be a compact, non-positively curved cube complex.

(i)

\mathrm{Ends}(\widetilde{X})=0
if and only if
\pi_{1}(X)
is trivial.

(ii)

\mathrm{Ends}(\widetilde{X})>1
if and only if
\pi_{1}(X)
splits freely.
4
4

4

A non-trivial free splitting corresponds to an action on a simplicial tree without a global fixed point and with trivial edge stabilisers. Note that
\mathbb{Z}\cong 1*_{1}
splits freely.

We will see that the number of ends can be conveniently encoded in the connectivity properties of Whitehead graphs. The next result is prototypical, and deals with the case when
X
is simply connected.

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
X
be a compact, non-positively curved cube complex. There is a compact CAT(0) cube complex
Y
mapping to
X
with
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)=\varnothing
if and only if
\pi_{1}(X)
is trivial.

Proof.

The Whitehead graph
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
is empty if and only if
Y=\widetilde{X}
. Thus such a compact
Y
exists if and only if
\widetilde{X}
is compact, meaning that
\pi_{1}(X)
is finite, i.e. trivial.
∎

In §
1.2
, we saw that is is often convenient to assume that our cube complexes are essential. In fact, when studying free splittings, we will only need a weaker notion.

Definition 2.16
.

A CAT(0) cube complex
\widetilde{X}
is called
freely inessential
if some (necessarily compact) hyperplane
H\subseteq\widetilde{X}
has the property that one of the associated half-spaces is compact. Otherwise,
\widetilde{X}
is called
freely essential
.

Remark 2.17
.

Any compact CAT(0) cube complex is freely inessential, unless it is a point, and any essential cube complex is freely essential.

The next result explains how Whitehead complexes encode free splittings of the fundamental group.

Proposition 2.18
.

Let
\widetilde{X}
be a freely essential CAT(0) cube complex. There is a compact, convex
Y\subseteq\widetilde{X}
such that
\mathrm{Wh}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)
is non-empty and disconnected if and only if
\widetilde{X}
has more than one end.

Proof.

Suppose such a
Y
exists. Since
\mathrm{Wh}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)
is disconnected, Lemma
2.12
implies that
\widetilde{X}\smallsetminus Y
is also disconnected, so
Y
separates
\widetilde{X}
into at least two components. If one of these components is bounded then any bounding hyperplane in the corresponding component of
\mathrm{Wh}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)
has a compact half space, so
\widetilde{X}
is freely inessential. Otherwise, all components are unbounded so
\widetilde{X}
has more than one end.

For the converse direction, suppose that
\widetilde{X}
has more than one end, so there is a compact subcomplex
Y\subseteq\widetilde{X}
such that
\widetilde{X}\smallsetminus Y
has at least two unbounded components. By Theorem
1.8
, we may replace
Y
by its convex hull and assume that
Y
is convex. Since
\widetilde{X}\smallsetminus Y
has at least two components, so does
\mathrm{Wh}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)
by Lemma
2.12
.
∎

The proposition connects Whitehead complexes and free splittings, but the resulting criterion is ineffective because there is no bound on the possible size of a
Y
for which
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
is disconnected. To make the criterion effective, we need to understand how to build up Whitehead complexes inductively by gluing, generalising the
splicing
construction of Manning
[
42
]
used by Cashen–Macura
[
16
]
.

2.3
Connected sums of simplicial complexes

The definition of the connected sum of two simplicial complexes is directly analogous to the definition for manifolds.

Definition 2.19

(Connected sum of complexes)
.

Let
A,B
be simplicial complexes, let
a\in A
,
b\in B
be vertices, and let
\phi:\mathrm{Lk}_{A}(a)\to\mathrm{Lk}_{B}(b)
be an isomorphism. The complex

A\#_{\phi}B:=A_{a}\cup_{\phi}B_{b}

is the
connected sum of
A
to
B
along
\phi
. (Often, we will have a natural identification
v=a=b
, and
\phi
will be implicit. In this case, we may write
A\#_{v}B
instead.)

Remark 2.20
.

Definition
2.19
generalises the
splicing
construction used by Manning to analyse Whitehead graphs in finite-index subgroups
[
42
]
, and exploited further by Cashen and Macura
[
15
,
16
]
. Up to taking first subdivisions, Manning’s definition coincides with Definition
2.19
when restricted to graphs. (Recall from Example
2.5
that the classical Whitehead graph is the first subdivision of our Whitehead complex.)

The next result explains how cutting
Y
along a hyperplane decomposes the Whitehead complex as a connected sum. When a convex subcomplex
Y\subseteq\widetilde{X}
intersects a hyperplane
H
of
\widetilde{X}
, we will abuse notation by identifying
H
with
H\cap Y
. Cutting
Y
along
H
then leads to the decomposition

Y=Y_{1}\cup_{H}Y_{2}\,;

note that
H
is then a bounding hyperplane of both
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
.

Proposition 2.21

(Cutting and connected sum)
.

Let
Y
be a convex subcomplex of a CAT(0) cube complex
\widetilde{X}
. If

Y=Y_{1}\cup_{H}Y_{2}

for some hyperplane
H
of
\widetilde{X}
then

\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)\cong\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{1})\#_{H}\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{2})\,.

Proof.

For notational convenience, write
W_{i}=\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{i})
for
i=1,2
, and
W=\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
. Our first task is to define the isomorphism
\mathrm{Lk}_{W_{1}}(H)\cong\mathrm{Lk}_{W_{2}}(H)
needed to define the connected sum.

A
(k-1)
-simplex
\sigma_{K}
of
W_{i}
corresponds to a bounding codimension-
k
hyperplane
K
of
Y_{i}
. This simplex
\sigma_{K}
is contained in
\mathrm{Lk}_{W_{i}}(H)
exactly when
K
crosses
H
. A codimension-
k
hyperplane that crosses
H
bounds
Y_{1}
if and only it bounds
Y_{2}
, so
\mathrm{Lk}_{W_{1}}(H)
is naturally identified with
\mathrm{Lk}_{W_{2}}(H)
as required. Let
L
denote the natural copy of
\mathrm{Lk}_{W_{1}}(H)\equiv\mathrm{Lk}_{W_{2}}(H)
in
W_{1}\#_{H}W_{2}
.

To see that
W\cong W_{1}\#_{H}W_{2}
, note that the
(k-1
)-simplices of
W_{1}\#_{H}W_{2}
correspond bijectively to bounding codimension-
k
hyperplanes of
Y
. Indeed, the simplices of
W_{1}\#_{H}W_{2}
are of three kinds:

(i)

simplices
\sigma_{K}
in
L
correspond to bounding codimension-
k
hyperplanes of
Y
that cross
H
;

(ii)

simplices
\sigma_{K}
of
W_{1}
that are not adjacent to
H
correspond to bounding codimension-
k
hyperplanes of
Y_{1}
disjoint from
H
;

(iii)

simplices
\sigma_{K}
of
W_{2}
that are not adjacent to
H
correspond to bounding codimension-
k
hyperplanes of
Y_{2}
disjoint from
H
.

Since this list also covers all the codimension-
k
hyperplanes of
Y
, and inclusions are preserved, the result follows.
∎

2.4
Cut-set lemmas in connected sums

To understand splittings of fundamental groups of non-positively curved cube complexes, the key idea is to combine Proposition
2.21
with an understanding of how the connectivity properties of simplicial complexes, in the sense of Definition
1.11
, change under connected sum. The first lemma is prototypical. Let
b_{0}(X)
denote the 0th Betti number of a space
X
, i.e. the number of connected components.

Lemma 2.22
.

Suppose that
A
and
B
are simplicial complexes. Then

b_{0}(A\#_{v}B)=b_{0}(B)

unless
v
is a cut vertex of
A
.

Proof.

We work with reduced homology. Let
L
denote the link of
v
and
C(L)
the cone on
L
. Then
B=B_{v}\cup_{L}C(L)
so, because
C(L)
is contractible, the Mayer–Vietoris theorem gives that

\tilde{H}_{0}(L)\to\tilde{H}_{0}(B_{v})\to\tilde{H}_{0}(B)\to 0

is exact. The connected sum can be written as
A\#_{v}B=A_{v}\cup_{L}B_{v}
, so the Mayer–Vietoris theorem also gives that

\tilde{H}_{0}(L)\to\tilde{H}_{0}(A_{v})\oplus\tilde{H}_{0}(B_{v})\to\tilde{H}_{0}(A\#_{v}B)\to 0

is exact. Unless
v
is a cut vertex in
A
,
\tilde{H}_{0}(A_{v})
is trivial, so the two sequences combine to show that

\tilde{H}_{0}(A\#_{v}B)\cong\operatorname{coker}(\tilde{H}_{0}(L)\to\tilde{H}_{0}(B_{v}))\cong\tilde{H}_{0}(B)

as required.
∎

We will usually only apply the lemma when
B
is connected, in which case it takes the following form.

Lemma 2.23
.

Suppose that
A
and
B
are simplicial complexes. If
B
is connected but
A\#_{v}B
is disconnected, then
v
is a cut vertex in
A
.

Since we will need to apply Lemma
2.23
inductively, we also need to understand when cut vertices can arise, and more generally cut simplices.

Lemma 2.24
.

Suppose that
A
and
B
are connected and a simplex
\sigma
of
A
is a cut simplex of
A\#_{v}B
. Then one of the following holds:

(i)

v
is a cut vertex in
B
;

(ii)

\sigma
is a cut simplex in either
A
or
B
; or

(iii)

\sigma
is contained in the link of
v
, and the simplex
\tau=\langle\sigma,v\rangle
spanned by
\sigma
and
v
together is a cut simplex in both
A
and
B
.

In particular, if
A\#_{v}B
has a cut simplex then either
A
or
B
has a cut simplex.

Proof.

Let
C=A\#_{v}B
for notational convenience. The hypothesis that
\sigma
is a cut simplex of
C
means that
C_{\sigma}
is disconnected. The proof now divides into cases, according to whether
\sigma
is in
A
but not in
B
, or
\sigma
is contained in the canonical copy of the link of
v
.

In the first case, when
\sigma
is in
A
but not in
B
, we have

C_{\sigma}=A_{\sigma}\#_{v}B\,,

so Lemma
2.23
implies that
v
is a cut vertex in
B
, as in item (i), or
A_{\sigma}
is disconnected, i.e.
\sigma
is a cut simplex in
A
.

In the second case, if
\sigma
is in the link of
v
then

C_{\sigma}=A_{\sigma}\#_{v}B_{\sigma}\,,

so Lemma
2.23
leads to two further subcases: either one of
A_{\sigma}
or
B_{\sigma}
is disconnected, or
v
is a cut vertex in both
A_{\sigma}
and
B_{\sigma}
. The former corresponds to item (ii), while the latter case gives item (iii).
∎

The above results will help us to understand free splittings of
\pi_{1}(X)
. More generally, we will need to understand how arbitrary cut sets decompose.

Lemma 2.25
.

Suppose that
U
is a full set of vertices of
A
and
V
is a full set of vertices of
B
, both non-adjacent to a common vertex
v
. If
U\cup V
is a cut set of

then: either

(i)

V
is a cut set in
B
; or

(ii)

U\cup\{v\}
is a cut set in
A
.

Proof.

Again, let
C=A\#_{v}B
for notational convenience. Because
v
is adjacent to neither
U
nor
V
,

C_{U\cup V}=A_{U}\#_{v}B_{V}

which is disconnected by hypothesis. Therefore, by Lemma
2.23
, either
B_{V}
is disconnected, giving item (i), or
v
is a cut vertex in
A_{U}
, giving item (ii).
∎

2.5
Cuts and the generalised Whitehead’s lemma

In this section, we combine the results developed so far to prove a sufficient condition for the fundamental group of a compact, non-positively curved cube complex
X
to be one-ended. These conditions are phrased in terms of
k
-cuts
.

Definition 2.26

(
k
-cuts)
.

Consider a finite CAT(0) cube complex
Y
equipped with an immersion
Y\to X
. If
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
has a finite cut set then
Y
is said to be a
cut
. More precisely, if
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
is
(k-1)
-vertex-connected but not
k
-vertex-connected – i.e. if the smallest cut set for
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
has cardinality
k
– then
Y
is said to be a
k
-cut
. If
\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}\}
is a minimal cut set for a
k
-cut
Y
then we say that
Y
is a cut
between
H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}
.

Our analysis of splittings of fundamental groups of non-positively curved cube complexes will rest on understanding cuts. We start with 0-cuts, which characterise free splittings. The results of §
2.4
explain how 0-cuts decompose.

Lemma 2.27
.

Consider a finite CAT(0) cube complex
Y
equipped with a locally isometric immersion
Y\to X
, and let
Y=Y_{1}\cup_{H}Y_{2}
for some hyperplane
H
of
Y
. If
Y
is a 0-cut then either one of
Y_{1}
or
Y_{2}
is a 0-cut, or both
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
are 1-cuts.

Proof.

The immersion lifts to an embedding of
Y
as a convex subcomplex of the universal cover
\widetilde{X}
, and the hyperplane
H
of
Y
then extends uniquely to a hyperplane of
\widetilde{X}
, still denoted by
H
. Proposition
2.21
implies that

\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)\cong\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{1})\#_{H}\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{2})\,,

so the result follows from Lemma
2.23
.
∎

To iterate the argument, we also need to analyse cut simplices.

Lemma 2.28
.

Consider a finite CAT(0) cube complex equipped with a locally isometric immersion
Y\to X
, and let
Y=Y_{1}\cup_{H}Y_{2}
for some hyperplane
H
of
Y
. If
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
has a cut simplex, then one of
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{1})
or
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{2})
has a cut simplex.

Proof.

This follows in the same way as Lemma
2.27
, using Proposition
2.21
and Lemma
2.24
.
∎

We now have all the tools needed to prove Theorem
E
, which we think of as a version of Whitehead’s lemma for non-positively curved cube complexes.

Proof of Theorem
E
.

Let
Y
be a CAT(0) cube complex, locally isometrically immersed in
X
. We prove that
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
is connected without cut simplices, by induction on the number of vertices of
Y
.

In the base case,
Y
is a single vertex
y
, so
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)\cong\mathrm{Lk}_{X}(y)
by Example
2.6
and there is nothing to prove. If
Y
has more than one vertex, we may choose a (necessarily separating) hyperplane
H
of
Y
and write
Y=Y_{1}\cup_{H}Y_{2}
. By the inductive hypothesis, both
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{1})
and
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{2})
are connected without cut simplices, and therefore so is
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
by Lemma
2.28
.

In conclusion,
X
has no 0-cuts, so Proposition
2.18
implies that
\pi_{1}(X)
has at most one end.
∎

Theorem
E
provides a convenient certificate that
\pi_{1}(X)
does not split freely. However, the sufficient condition that it provides is not necessary.

Example 2.29
.

Let
X_{0}
be the standard 2-torus, seen as a non-positively curved square complex with a single vertex
x_{0}
. Let
X=X_{0}\vee_{x_{0}}I
, where
I
is a unit interval. The link
\mathrm{Lk}_{X}(x_{0})
is the disjoint union of a 4-cycle and a point, and in particular is disconnected. However,
\widetilde{X}
is clearly one-ended.

Examples like
2.29
can be handled by collapsing free edges and free faces. (Free faces manifest themselves as vertices of valence 1 in the link of a vertex.) However, there are also non-positively curved square complexes
X
, with
\widetilde{X}
one-ended, in which the link of a vertex is connected without valence-1 vertices, but contains a cut vertex.

The following example is based on
[
36
, Example 2.1]
.

Example 2.30
.

Consider the rank-two free group
F=\langle a,b\rangle
, thought of as the fundamental group of the wedge of two circles
\Gamma=S^{1}\vee S^{1}
. Consider the element
w=abab^{2}ab^{3}
of
F
and realise the corresponding cyclic word by an immersion of a circle
\gamma:S^{1}\to\Gamma
. Let
X
be the non-positively curved square complex obtained by gluing either end of a cylinder
S^{1}\times[0,1]
to two copies of
\Gamma
, using
\gamma
as the attaching map at each end. The links of the two vertices of
X
are both isomorphic to the first subdivision of the Whitehead graph of
w
, which is connected, without valence-1 vertices, but has a cut vertex.

The fundamental group of
X
is the double

\pi_{1}(X)=F*_{\langle w\rangle}F

by the Seifert–van Kampen theorem. Applying the automorphism
\phi
of
F_{2}
setting
\phi(a)=ab^{-2}
and
\phi(b)=b
sends
w\mapsto w^{\prime}=ab^{-1}a^{2}b
. We may realise
w^{\prime}
by an immersion
\gamma^{\prime}:S^{1}\to\Gamma
and glue along a cylinder as before to obtain a new square complex
X^{\prime}
with
\pi_{1}(X^{\prime})\cong\pi_{1}(X)
. However, the links of the two vertices of
X^{\prime}
are isomorphic to the first subdivision of the Whitehead graph of
w^{\prime}
, which consists of two triangles glued along an edge, and in particular is connected with no cut vertices. Therefore, by Theorem
E
,
\pi(X)\cong\pi_{1}(X^{\prime})
is one-ended, even though the links of
X
have cut vertices.

In the two-dimensional case, a square complex
X
can always be modified so that the sufficient condition of Theorem
E
can be applied. A map of square complexes
\phi:X^{\prime}\to X
is called an
unfolding of
X
if (i) the restriction of
\phi
to 1-skeleta is a combinatorial homotopy equivalence of graphs; and (ii)
\phi
is a homeomorphism on the complements of the 1-skeleta.

Proposition 2.31
.

Let
X
be a compact, non-positively curved square complex. There is an unfolding
\phi:X^{\prime}\to X
such that

X^{\prime}=\Gamma\vee X_{1}\vee\ldots\vee X_{m}\vee S_{1}\vee\ldots\vee S_{n}

is also a compact, non-positively curved square complex and, furthermore:

(i)

\Gamma
is a graph;

(ii)

each
X_{i}
has the property that every link is connected without cut vertices or cut edges;

(iii)

each
S_{j}
is a square.

Proof.

The existence of
X^{\prime}
is
[
66
, Lemma 4.6]
stated for square complexes (see also
[
39
, §3]
). The idea of the proof is an algorithmic unfolding procedure: one simply unfolds an edge of the 1-skeleton whenever the link of a vertex contains a cut vertex.

It remains to prove that
X^{\prime}
is non-positively curved. To do this, it suffices to check Gromov’s link condition, that any cycle
\gamma
in a link of a vertex of
X^{\prime}
should have length at least
2\pi
in the angular metric
[
10
, Lemma II.5.6]
. Because
\phi
is a homeomorphism on the complement of the 1-skeleton, the induced maps on links are injective on edges and hence immersions. Thus,
\gamma
is also an immersed cycle in a link of
X
, so has length at least
2\pi
by the link condition for
X
.
∎

The square complexes
X_{i}
provided by the proposition satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
E
. Thus, the wedge decomposition
X^{\prime}
actually gives the Grushko decomposition of
X
:

\pi_{1}(X)\cong\pi_{1}(\Gamma)*\pi_{1}(X_{1})*\ldots*\pi_{1}(X_{m})\,,

and each
\pi_{1}(X_{i})
is non-trivial and freely indecomposable. Since the proof of Proposition
2.31
is algorithmic, Theorem
E
and the proposition together provide an effective, geometric method to determine the Grushko decomposition of the fundamental group of a non-positively curved square complex.

2.6
Shepherd’s theorem and
1
-cuts

The results of §
2.5
show that 0-cuts detect when an essential cube complex has more than one end, but they also illustrate the fact that the existence of 1-cuts makes it difficult to determine whether or not
\pi_{1}(X)
splits. Therefore, for one-ended
\pi_{1}(X)
, we would like to be able to choose
X
to have no 1-cuts. In the 2-dimensional case, this can be done by
unfolding
the 1-skeleton as in Proposition
2.31
. To handle the general case, however, we can use a recent deep theorem of Shepherd.

A
quarter-space
of a CAT(0) cube complex is the intersection of two transverse half-spaces. A quarter-space is said to be
deep
if it is not contained in a bounded neighbourhood of the intersection of its two defining hyperplanes. The next result is
[
55
, Theorem 1.5]
.

Theorem 2.32

(Shepherd’s theorem)
.

If
G
is one-ended and the fundamental group of a non-positively curved cube complex, then
G=\pi_{1}(X)
for an essential non-positively curved cube complex
X
, such that the universal cover
\widetilde{X}
has the property that all half-spaces of
\widetilde{X}
are one-ended and all quarter-spaces are deep.

The following lemma connects Shepherd’s theorem to 1-cuts.

Lemma 2.33
.

Let
\widetilde{X}
be a one-ended, essential CAT(0) cube complex in which all quarter-spaces are deep. If
\widetilde{X}
has a 1-cut, then some half-space of
\widetilde{X}
has more than one end.

Proof.

Let
Y\subseteq\widetilde{X}
be a 1-cut, i.e.
Y
is compact and convex and
\mathrm{Wh}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)
has a cut vertex
H
. Let
U
be the half-space of
H
that contains
Y
.

We claim that
U
is freely essential. Indeed, suppose that some hyperplane
K
of
U
had a compact half-space
V
. If
K
were disjoint from
H
then
K
would also be a compact hyperplane in
\widetilde{X}
, contradicting the hypothesis that
\widetilde{X}
is essential and one-ended. If
K
crossed
H
then
V
would be a compact quarter-space in
\widetilde{X}
, contradicting the hypothesis that all quarter-spaces are deep.

Now,
\mathrm{Wh}_{U}(Y)
is obtained from
\mathrm{Wh}_{\widetilde{X}}(Y)
by deleting the star of
H
, so is disconnected. Therefore,
U
has more than one end by Proposition
2.18
, as required.
∎

Combining Shepherd’s theorem with the lemma, we may always assume that one-ended cube complexes have no 1-cuts.

Corollary 2.34
.

If
G
is the fundamental group of a compact, non-positively curved cube complex and
G
is one-ended, then
G=\pi_{1}(X)
, where
X
is a compact, essential, non-positively curved cube complex without 0-cuts or 1-cuts.

3
Cyclic splittings

Having understood the free splittings of
\pi_{1}(X)
, we may henceforth assume that
\pi_{1}(X)
is one-ended. Our next task is to adapt the analysis of the previous section to understand splittings of
\pi_{1}(X)
over
\mathbb{Z}
. We restrict attention to the case where
\pi_{1}(X)
is hyperbolic and, for brevity, we will say
hyperbolic cube complex
to mean a compact, connected, non-positively curved cube complex
X
with
\widetilde{X}
(and hence
\pi_{1}(X)
) Gromov-hyperbolic. Let
\delta
be a hyperbolicity constant for
\widetilde{X}
.

Cashen–Macura studied line patterns in free groups, i.e. relatively hyperbolic structures on free groups with cyclic parabolics
[
16
]
. They explained how to recognise cut pairs in Otal’s decomposition space – i.e. the Bowditch boundary – by analysing cut pairs in generalised Whitehead graphs. Cashen went on to use this analysis to recognise relative cyclic splittings
[
15
]
. Barrett used similar ideas to describe an algorithm that computes the Bowditch JSJ decomposition of any hyperbolic group
[
2
]
.

Our first task is to adapt their ideas to the setting of hyperbolic cube complexes.

3.1
Periodic
2
-cuts

Suppose that
\pi_{1}(X)
is one-ended. If every splitting of
\pi_{1}(X)
with cyclic edge groups is trivial, then
\pi_{1}(X)
is said to be
cyclically indecomposable
. The main theorem of this section is a combinatorial characterisation of cyclic indecomposibility. Just as we understood free splittings of
\pi_{1}(X)
in terms of 0-cuts and 1-cuts of
X
, so cyclic splittings can be understood using 2-cuts.

As we will see, cyclic splittings give rise to 2-cuts, but the converse is not quite true: in order to construct a cyclic splitting from a 2-cut, we also need to consider the combinatorial types of the bounding hyperplane components.

Definition 3.1

(Abstract hyperplane component)
.

Let
X
be a compact, non-positively curved cube complex. An
abstract hyperplane component
over
X
consists of the following data:

(i)

a compact, convex subcomplex
K
of a hyperplane
H\subseteq\widetilde{X}
;

(ii)

a non-trivial class
c_{K}
in the reduced 0-cohomology
\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{H}(K);\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
.

Two abstract hyperplane components
K\subseteq H
and
K^{\prime}\subseteq H^{\prime}
are of
isomorphic type
if there is a deck transformation
\phi\in\pi_{1}(X)
such that
\phi(K)=K^{\prime}
, inducing an isomorphism
\mathrm{Wh}_{H}(K)\cong\mathrm{Wh}_{H^{\prime}}(K^{\prime})
under which
c_{K^{\prime}}
pulls back to
c_{K}
.

An
orientation
of an abstract hyperplane component consists of the following extra datum:

(iii)

a choice of one of the two half spaces of the hyperplane
H
.

Two oriented abstract hyperplane components
K\subseteq H
and
K^{\prime}\subseteq H^{\prime}
are of
opposite type
if they are of isomorphic type and, furthermore, the isomorphism
\phi
can be chosen to reverse the chosen orientations.

The reader may find it useful to think of the class
c_{K}
as a non-trivial partition of the connected components of
\mathrm{Wh}_{H}(K)
.

This definition enables us to define a
periodic
2-cut.

Definition 3.2

(Periodic 2-cut)
.

Let
X
be a compact, non-positively curved cube complex without 0- or 1-cuts. Consider a 2-cut
Y
in
X
between disjoint hyperplanes
H,H^{\prime}
. Fix a non-trivial class
c_{Y}\in\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H,H^{\prime}\}};\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
. Define an oriented abstract hyperplane component corresponding to
H
as follows:

(i)

K=H_{Y}
;

(ii)

set
c_{K}
to be the pullback of
c_{Y}
under the inclusion of
\mathrm{Wh}_{H}(K)\cong\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)}(H)
into
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H,H^{\prime}\}}
;

(iii)

the orientation is determined by the half-space of
H
that contains
Y
.

Define an oriented abstract hyperplane component
K^{\prime}
corresponding to
H^{\prime}
in the same way. The 2-cut
Y
is said to be
periodic
if the two oriented abstract hyperplane components are of opposite type, for some choice of
c_{Y}
. In this case, the element
\phi\in\pi_{1}(X)
sending
H\to H^{\prime}
is called the
associated covering transformation of
Y
.

Remark 3.3
.

Since
X
has no 1-cuts,
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H^{\prime}\}}
is connected. The Mayer–Vietoris sequence applied to the decomposition

\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H^{\prime}\}}=\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H,H^{\prime}\}}\cup_{\mathrm{Lk}(H)}C(\mathrm{Lk}(H))

then implies that the natural map

\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H,H^{\prime}\}};\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})\to\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{H}(K);\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})

is injective, so
c_{K}
is indeed non-trivial, as required.

We shall see that periodic 2-cuts characterise cyclic splittings. However, first we need to relate cyclic splittings to Whitehead complexes.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
X
be an essential hyperbolic cube complex with
\pi_{1}(X)
one-ended. Then
\pi_{1}(X)
splits over
\mathbb{Z}
if and only if some infinite cyclic subgroup acts cocompactly on some isometrically embedded subcomplex
\widetilde{Z}
of
\widetilde{X}
with
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z})
disconnected.

Proof.

By the algebraic annulus theorem of Dunwoody–Swenson
[
21
]
, and noting that
\pi_{1}(X)
is torsion-free,
\pi_{1}(X)
splits over a
\mathbb{Z}
subgroup if and only if some subgroup
C\cong\mathbb{Z}
satisfies that the number of relative ends
e(G,C)>1
. Because
\pi_{1}(X)
is hyperbolic,
C
is quasiconvex.

Choosing a base vertex
*
in
\widetilde{X}
, the condition that
e(G,C)>1
implies that, for some
R
, the
R
-neighbourhood of the orbit
C*
separates
\widetilde{X}
into at least two infinite components. Let
\widetilde{Z}
be the cubical convex hull of the
R
-neighbourhood of
C*
, which still separates
\widetilde{X}
into at least two infinite components. By Haglund’s theorem (Theorem
1.8
),
C
acts cocompactly on
\widetilde{Z}
. Because
\widetilde{Z}
separates
\widetilde{X}
into two components,
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z})
is disconnected by Lemma
2.12
, as required.

Conversely, suppose that some
C\cong\mathbb{Z}
acts cocompactly on some convex subcomplex
\widetilde{Z}
with
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z})
disconnected, so
\widetilde{Z}
separates
\widetilde{X}
into two components by Lemma
2.12
. Either some complementary component remains within a bounded distance of
\widetilde{Z}
, in which case some bounding hyperplane of
\widetilde{Z}
is inessential, which is a contradiction, or all complementary components are unbounded away from
\widetilde{Z}
, meaning that
e(G,C)>1
, as required.
∎

In the setting of Lemma
3.4
, it will be useful to know that the number of path components of the,
a priori
infinite, complex
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z})
is finite.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
X
be a compact, non-positively curved cube complex without 0- or 1-cuts. Suppose that some infinite cyclic subgroup
\langle\phi\rangle
of
\pi_{1}(X)
acts cocompactly on some isometrically embedded subcomplex
\widetilde{Z}
of
\widetilde{X}
. Then the number of path components of
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z})
is finite.

Proof.

The compact quotient
Z=\langle\phi\rangle\backslash\widetilde{Z}
has fundamental group
\mathbb{Z}\cong\langle\phi\rangle
, and therefore
H^{1}(Z;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}
. Since the first cohomology is generated by cocycles dual to hyperplanes, some hyperplane
K
of
Z
is crossed by any loop representing the generator
\phi
of
\mathbb{Z}
. Since
\pi_{1}(K)
is contained in the kernel of the dual map

\mathbb{Z}\cong\pi_{1}(Z)\to\mathbb{Z}\,,

it follows that
K
is simply connected, so
K
lifts to a hyperplane (still denoted by
K
) of
\widetilde{Z}
. By
[
14
, Lemma 2.3]
, after replacing
\phi
by a further proper power we may assume that
\phi(K)
is disjoint from
K
.

The hyperplane
K
of
\widetilde{Z}
extends to some hyperplane
H
of
\widetilde{X}
. Cutting
\widetilde{Z}
along the
\langle\phi\rangle
-orbit of
H
decomposes it as an infinite gluing:

\widetilde{Z}=\cdots\cup_{\phi^{-1}(H)}\phi^{-1}(Y)\cup_{H}Y\cup_{\phi(H)}\phi(Y)\cup_{\phi^{2}(H)}\cdots

where
Y
is the compact component resulting from cutting
\widetilde{Z}
along
H
and
\phi(H)
. It follows from this that the Whitehead complex
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z})
can be written as an ascending union

\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z})=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Z_{n})_{\{\phi^{-n}(H),\phi^{n}(H)\}}

where

Z_{n}=\phi^{-n}(Y)\cup_{\phi^{1-n}(H)}\cdots\cup_{\phi^{n-1}(H)}\phi^{n-1}(Y)\,.

In particular,

\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Z_{n})=\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)\#\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Z_{n-1})\#\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)

by Proposition
2.21
. Since
X
has no 0- or 1-cuts,
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
is 1-vertex-connected, so

b_{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Z_{n}))=b_{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Z_{n-1}))=b_{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Z_{0}))

by Lemma
2.22
and induction.

Recall that
K=H\cap\widetilde{Z}=H_{Y}
. Let
L=\mathrm{Wh}_{H}(K)\cong\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)}(H)
(by Lemma
2.13
), which is itself compact. Mayer–Vietoris applied to the decomposition

\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Z_{n})=C(L)\cup_{L}\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Z_{n})_{\{\phi^{-n}(H),\phi^{n}(H)\}}\cup_{L}C(L)

(where, as usual,
C(L)
denotes the cone on
L
) gives the exact sequence

H_{0}(L)\oplus H_{0}(L)\to H_{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Z_{n})_{\{\phi^{-n}(H),\phi^{n}(H)\}})\to H_{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Z_{n}))\to 0\,.

Thus, the number of connected components is bounded independently of
n
:

\displaystyle b_{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Z_{n})_{\{\phi^{-n}(H),\phi^{n}(H)\}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle b_{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Z_{n}))+2b_{0}(L)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle b_{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Z_{0}))+2b_{0}(L)\,.

From this, it follows that
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z})
has finitely many connected components, as required.
∎

It follows from Lemmas
3.4
and
3.5
that periodic 2-cuts characterise cyclic splittings.

Theorem 3.6

(Cyclic splittings and periodic 2-cuts)
.

Let
X
be an essential hyperbolic cube complex. Suppose that
\pi_{1}(X)
is one-ended, and suppose furthermore that
X
has no 0- or 1-cuts. Then
\pi_{1}(X)
is cyclically decomposable if and only if there is a periodic 2-cut
Y\to X
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.4
,
\pi_{1}(X)
is cyclically decomposable if and only if there is a convex
\mathbb{Z}
-periodic subcomplex
\widetilde{Z}\subseteq\widetilde{X}
that separates
\widetilde{X}
. By Lemma
2.12
,
\widetilde{Z}
separates
\widetilde{X}
if and only if
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z})
is disconnected.

Suppose that there is a periodic 2-cut
Y\subseteq\widetilde{X}
between hyperplanes
H,H^{\prime}
. By definition,
H,H^{\prime}
define a cut pair in
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
, and the corresponding oriented abstract hyperplane components have opposite type. Therefore, we may define the amalgamated subcomplex

Y\cup_{\phi(H)}\phi(Y)\,.

Iterating using all powers of
\phi
, we may set
\widetilde{Z}
to be the infinite amalgamation

\cdots\cup_{\phi^{-1}(H)}\phi^{-1}(Y)\cup_{H}Y\cup_{\phi(H)}\phi(Y)\cup_{\phi^{2}(H)}\cdots\,,

which is
\langle\phi\rangle
-invariant and cocompact. By Proposition
2.21
,
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z})
is constructed by iteratively taking connect sums of copies of
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
along copies of
H
, meaning that
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z})
is the infinite union

\cdots\cup_{L}\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H,H^{\prime}\}}\cup_{L}\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H,H^{\prime}\}}\cup_{L}\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H,H^{\prime}\}}\cup_{L}\cdots\,,

where
L=\mathrm{Lk}_{\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)}(H)\cong\mathrm{Wh}_{H}(H_{Y})
by Lemma
2.13
. The Mayer–Vietoris sequence for reduced cohomology (with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
) asserts that

0\to\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z}))\to\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H,H^{\prime}\}})\to\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\tilde{H}^{0}(L)

is exact. The right-hand map sends the non-trivial element

(c_{Y})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H,H^{\prime}\}})

to
(c_{K})-(\phi^{*}(c_{K^{\prime}}))=0
, so
\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z}))
is non-trivial, and
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z})
is indeed disconnected as required.

Conversely, suppose that there is a convex
\mathbb{Z}
-periodic subcomplex
\widetilde{Z}\subseteq\widetilde{X}
with
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z})
disconnected, with the infinite cyclic subgroup
\mathbb{Z}
generated by some covering transformation
\phi
. By Lemma
3.5
,
\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z});\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
is finite so, after replacing
\phi
by a power, we may assume that it acts trivially on
\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z});\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
. As in the proof of Lemma
3.5
,
\widetilde{Z}
has a compact hyperplane
K
that separates the two ends, and extends to a hyperplane
H
of
\widetilde{X}
. After replacing
\phi
by a proper power, we may assume that
\phi(H)
is disjoint from
H
.

Let
\widetilde{Z}_{\pm}
be the two half-spaces of
H
, so

\widetilde{Z}=\widetilde{Z}_{-}\cup_{H}\widetilde{Z}_{+}\,.

We claim that
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z}_{+})
and
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z}_{-})
are connected. Indeed, let
Y
be the compact component of
\widetilde{Z}
that results from cutting along
H
and
\phi(H)
. Then
\widetilde{Z}_{+}
may be written as the ascending union of compact, convex subcomplexes
Y_{n}
where

Y_{n}=Y\cup_{\phi(H)}\phi(Y)\cup_{\phi^{2}(H)}\ldots\cup_{\phi^{n-1}(H)}\phi^{n-1}(Y)

and, correspondingly, the Whitehead complex of
\widetilde{Z}_{+}
may be written as an ascending union

\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z}_{+})=\bigcup_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{n})_{\phi^{n}(H)}\,.

Thus, if
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z}_{+})
were disconnected then it would follow that
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{n})_{\phi^{n}(H)}
was disconnected for some
n
. If so then
Y_{n}
would be a 1-cut in
X
, contradicting the assumption. Thus,
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z}_{+})
is connected and, symmetrically, so is
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z}_{-})
. Since

\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z})=\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z}_{-})\#_{H}\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z}_{+})

is disconnected, it follows that
H
is a cut vertex in both
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z}_{-})
and
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z}_{+})
by Lemma
2.23
.

Now,
H^{\prime}=\phi(H)
is a hyperplane of
\widetilde{Z}_{+}
disjoint from
H
, and cutting along
H^{\prime}
decomposes
\widetilde{Z}_{+}
as

\widetilde{Z}_{+}=Y\cup_{H^{\prime}}\phi(\widetilde{Z}_{+})

where
Y=\widetilde{Z}_{+}\cap\phi(\widetilde{Z}_{-})
; note that
H
is a bounding hyperplane of
Y
. Hence,

\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z}_{+})=\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)\#_{H^{\prime}}\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\phi(\widetilde{Z}_{+}))

and so, since we saw above that
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\phi(\widetilde{Z}_{+}))\cong\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z}_{+})
is connected,
Y
is a 2-cut between
H
and
H^{\prime}
by Lemma
2.25
.

Finally, note that
Y
is a periodic 2-cut. Indeed, setting
H^{\prime}=\phi(H)
, it only remains to construct a suitable
c_{Y}\in\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H,H^{\prime}\}})
that satisfies Definition
3.2
. To do this, as above we consider the exact sequence

0\to\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha}}{{\to}}\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H,H^{\prime}\}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\beta}}{{\to}}\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\tilde{H}^{0}(L)

provided by Mayer–Vietoris, where
L=\mathrm{Wh}_{H}(K)
. Note that
\mathbb{Z}\cong\langle\phi^{*}\rangle
acts on all terms in the exact sequence, and the maps
\alpha
and
\beta
are
\mathbb{Z}
-equivariant.

Since
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z})
is disconnected, there is some non-zero
a\in\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z}))
, and
\alpha(a)=(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is also non-zero because
\alpha
is injective. But
\phi^{*}
acts trivially on
\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z}))
, so
\phi^{*}(a)=a
, and
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is actually a non-zero constant sequence
(b)
. Set
c_{Y}=b
.

Write
i
for the inclusion of
L
into
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H,H^{\prime}\}}
and
j
for the inclusion of
L
into
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\phi^{-1}(Y))_{\{\phi^{-1}(H),H\}}
. Then

\beta(b)=(i^{*}(b)-\phi^{*}\circ j^{*}(b))\,,

so
i^{*}(b)=\phi^{*}(j^{*}(b))
by exactness. Therefore, setting
c_{K}=i^{*}(b)
and
c_{K^{\prime}}=j^{*}(b)
, we have
c_{K}=\phi^{*}(c_{K^{\prime}})
as required.
∎

3.2
A bound on the widths of
2
-cuts

Although Theorem
3.6
provides a useful characterisation of the existence of cyclic splittings, in order to make it effective we need an
a priori
bound on the complexity of the 2-cuts that arise. Such a bound is the main result of this section, and is proved in Theorem
3.13
below. It is phrased in terms of the
width
of a 2-cut.

Definition 3.7

(Width of a
k
-cut)
.

Let
X
be a compact, non-positively curved cube complex, and consider a
k
-cut
Y\to X
between bounding hyperplanes
\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}\}
for
k\geq 2
. The
width
of
Y
is the shortest
\ell^{2}
-distance between any distinct
H_{i}
and
H_{j}
.

The next lemma provides some geometric control on cuts, assuming that there are no 1-cuts.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\widetilde{X}
be a one-ended a CAT(0) cube complex without 0- or 1-cuts. Consider a
k
-cut
Y
between bounding hyperplanes
H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}
. Then
Y\cap\mathrm{Hull}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}\right)
is also a
k
-cut between
H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}
.

Proof.

Let
K_{1},\ldots K_{l}
be the hyperplanes of
Y
that have a half-space
U_{j}
such that
H_{i}\subseteq U_{j}
for all
i
. Write
Y_{n}=Y\cap\bigcap_{j=1}^{n}U_{j}
for each
0\leq n\leq l
. Note that

Y_{l}=Y\cap\mathrm{Hull}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}H_{i}\right)\,.

To prove the lemma, we show by induction on
n
that each
Y_{n}
is a
k
-cut.

In the base case,
Y_{0}=Y
and there is nothing to prove. For the inductive step, let
n\geq 1
. If
K_{n}
is disjoint from
Y_{n-1}
then
Y_{n-1}=Y_{n}
and there is, again, nothing to prove. Otherwise,
K_{n}
decomposes
Y_{n-1}
as

Y_{n-1}=Y_{n}\cup_{K_{n}}Y^{\prime}_{n}

where
Y_{n}=Y_{n-1}\cap U_{n}
. Then
H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}
all bound
Y_{n}
and not
Y^{\prime}_{n}
(since they are disjoint from
K_{n}
, and
K_{n}
cannot separate
Y
from any
H_{i}
). By Proposition
2.21

\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{n-1})\cong\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{n})\#_{K_{n}}\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y^{\prime}_{n})\,,

and
K_{n}
is not a cut vertex in
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y^{\prime}_{n})
because
\widetilde{X}
has no 1-cuts. Therefore
\{H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}\}
is a cut set in
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{n})
by Lemma
2.25
, so
Y_{n}
is also a
k
-cut between
H_{1},\ldots,H_{k}
. Taking
n=l
, the result follows.
∎

We now turn our attention to some CAT(0) geometry of hyperplanes. Consider a 2-cut
Y
between hyperplanes
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, and let
\gamma
be an
\ell^{2}
-geodesic between
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
. In order to prove the desired bound, we need to find long chains of mutually disjoint hyperplanes that cross
\gamma
at an angle that isn’t too acute. A hyperplane
K
is said to be
\epsilon
-almost orthogonal
to
\gamma
if
\gamma
crosses
K
at an angle of at least
\epsilon
.

The next lemma shows that many of the hyperplanes crossed by
\gamma
will be almost orthogonal, for a suitable value of
\epsilon
. The proof uses elementary Euclidean trigonometry.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
d\geq 2
, and let
\gamma
be an
\ell^{2}
-geodesic in a
d
-dimensional CAT(0) cube complex
\widetilde{X}
. Suppose that
\gamma
crosses two hyperplanes
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
consecutively. Then at least one of
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
is
\epsilon_{d}
-almost orthogonal to
\gamma
, where
\epsilon_{d}=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{2\sqrt{d-1}}\right)
.

Proof.

If
\gamma
crosses
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
simultaneously then the result is immediate, because
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
are orthogonal to each other and
\epsilon_{d}<\pi/4
.

Otherwise, suppose that
\gamma(0)\in K_{1}
and
\gamma
crosses
K_{1}
at an angle
\theta<\epsilon_{d}
. Because the (closed) regular neighbourhood
N(K_{1})
of
K_{1}
is isometric to
K_{1}\times[-1/2,1/2]
, we have
\gamma(t)\in N(K_{1})
whenever
|t|\sin\theta\leq 1/2
. In particular, if
\gamma(t_{0})
is in the boundary of
N(K_{1})
then
|t_{0}|=1/(2\sin\theta)
. Let
p
be the orthogonal projection of
\gamma(t_{0})
to
K_{1}
. The three points
\gamma(0)
,
\gamma(t_{0})
,
p
together form a right-angled Euclidean triangle, so

d(\gamma(0),p)=|t_{0}|\cos\theta=\frac{1}{2}\cot\theta\,.

Because
\theta<\epsilon_{d}
, it follows that

d(\gamma(0),p)>\frac{1}{2}\cot\epsilon_{d}=\sqrt{d-1}

which implies that
\gamma(0)
and
p
are separated by a hyperplane in the
(d-1)
-dimensional cube complex
K_{1}
. Since every such hyperplane extends uniquely to a hyperplane in
\widetilde{X}
orthogonal to
K_{1}
, it follows that
\gamma
crosses a hyperplane in
N(K_{1})
orthogonal to
K_{1}
.

The first such hyperplane is by assumption
K_{2}
. Let
\gamma(t_{1})\in K_{2}
and let
q
be the orthogonal projection of
\gamma(0)
to
K_{2}
. Then the three points
\gamma(0)
,
\gamma(t_{1})
,
q
together span a right-angled Euclidean triangle, whence
\gamma
makes an angle

\pi/2-\theta>\pi-\epsilon_{d}>\pi/4>\epsilon_{d}

with
K_{2}
, as required.
∎

The next lemma guarantees that a sufficiently distant pair of hyperplanes will be separated by many mutually disjoint hyperplanes, all themselves almost orthogonal to a geodesic.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
be hyperplanes in a
d
-dimensional CAT(0) cube complex
\widetilde{X}
, and let
\gamma
be a shortest
\ell^{2}
-geodesic between them. For every
n
there is a
C
such that, if the length of
\gamma
is greater than
C
, then there exists a sequence of mutually disjoint hyperplanes

K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}

that each separate
H_{1}
from
H_{2}
, all of which are
\epsilon_{d}
-almost orthogonal to
\gamma
.

Proof.

Let
N
be the Ramsey number
R(n,d+1)
. Recall that the
\ell^{1}
distance between
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
is equal to the number of hyperplanes crossed by
\gamma
. Since the
\ell^{1}
and
\ell^{2}
metrics are quasi-isometric, there is a
C
such that, if
\gamma
has
\ell^{2}
length at least
C
, then it crosses at least
2N
hyperplanes. By Lemma
3.9
, at least
N
of these hyperplanes are
\epsilon_{d}
-almost orthogonal to
\gamma
. Since no set of
d+1
hyperplanes of
\widetilde{X}
pairwise intersect, it follows from the definition of
N
that there is a pairwise disjoint set

\{K_{1},\ldots,K_{n}\}

of
n
hyperplanes that intersect
\gamma

\epsilon_{d}
-almost orthogonally.

Finally, note that any hyperplane
K
that crosses
H_{i}
meets it orthogonally, and therefore if
\gamma
also crossed
K
there would be a compact geodesic triangle in
\widetilde{X}
with two right angles, contradicting the CAT(0) condition. Therefore, each
K_{j}
is disjoint from
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
, and therefore separates them from each other.
∎

We needed to find almost orthogonal hyperplanes because of the following lemma, which will enable us to bound the diameter of a hyperplane intersecting a suitably chosen 2-cut.

Lemma 3.11
.

Suppose that an
\ell^{2}
geodesic
\gamma
intersects a hyperplane
H
at an angle
\theta
. Let
x
be the point of intersection and let
y
be a point in
H
. Then

d(x,y)\sin\theta\leq d(y,\gamma)\,.

In particular, if
H
is
\epsilon
-almost orthogonal to
\gamma
,
d(x,y)\leq d(y,\gamma)/\sin\epsilon
.

Proof.

Let
z
be the closest point on
\gamma
to
y
, and consider the geodesic triangle with vertices
x
,
y
and
z
. Let
\bar{\theta}
be the angle in the Euclidean comparison triangle at the vertex corresponding to
x
, which is at least
\theta
by
[
10
, Proposition 1.7(4)]
. Now, the Euclidean sine rule gives

d(y,z)\geq d(x,y)\sin\bar{\theta}\geq d(x,y)\sin\theta

as required.
∎

The proof of the claimed bound on the widths of 2-cuts uses the pigeonhole principle, applied to isomorphism types of abstract hyperplane components of a given diameter.

Remark 3.12
.

Let
X
be a compact, non-positively curved cube complex, and let
R\geq 0
. The set of isomorphism types of abstract bounding hyperplane components
Z
over
X
of diameter at most
R
is finite. Indeed, the bound on diameter implies that there are only finitely many possible subcomplexes
Z
of hyperplanes
H
of
\widetilde{X}
, up to the action of
\pi_{1}(X)
; since
\mathrm{Wh}_{H}(Z)
is compact for each
Z
, the number of possible cohomology classes in
\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{H}(Z);\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z})
is also finite.

This finiteness is the basis for our argument using the pigeonhole principle.

Theorem 3.13
.

Let
X
be an essential,
d
-dimensional, hyperbolic cube complex without 0-cuts or 1-cuts. Then
\pi_{1}(X)
is cyclically indecomposable if and only if there is a bound on the widths of 2-cuts in
X
.

Proof.

The ‘only if’ direction is similar to the proof of Theorem
3.6
. If
\pi_{1}(X)
has a cyclic splitting then, by Lemma
3.4
, there is an infinite cyclic subgroup
\langle\phi\rangle
acting cocompactly on some isometrically embedded subcomplex
\widetilde{Z}\subseteq\widetilde{X}
with
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(\widetilde{Z})
disconnected. As in the proof of Theorem
3.6
, we can find a hyperplane
H
of
\widetilde{Z}
such that, for any
n
, cutting
\widetilde{Z}
along
H
and
\phi^{n}(H)
gives a 2-cut between
H
and
\phi^{n}(H)
. Since
d(H,\phi^{n}(H))\to\infty
with
n
, it follows that there is no bound on the widths of 2-cuts, as required.

For the converse, let
Y
be a 2-cut between hyperplanes
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
. By Lemma
3.8
, we may replace
Y
by its intersection with
\Hull(H_{1}\cup H_{2})
, and so assume that
Y
is contained in
\Hull(H_{1}\cup H_{2})
. Let
\gamma
be an
\ell^{2}
-geodesic between
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
. It is easy to see that
H_{1}\cup\gamma\cup H_{2}
is
2\delta
-quasiconvex in the
\ell^{2}
metric and therefore, by Theorem
1.8
,
\Hull(H_{1}\cup H_{2})
is contained in the
R
-neighbourhood of
H_{1}\cup\gamma\cup H_{2}
, for some uniform
R
.

Let
M
be the number of isomorphism types of oriented abstract hyperplane components over
X
of diameter at most
2R\sqrt{4d-3}
(which is finite by Remark
3.12
). Let
M^{\prime}
be such that any geodesic crossing
M^{\prime}
hyperplanes has an
\ell^{2}
-length of at least
(1+\sqrt{4d-3})R
, and let
N=M+2M^{\prime}
. By Lemma
3.10
, if the width of
Y
is greater than a constant
C
, then we can find a sequence of
M+1
hyperplanes

K_{0},K_{1},\ldots,K_{M}\,,

all separating
H_{1}
from
H_{2}
, all
\epsilon_{d}
-almost orthogonal to
\gamma
, and such that each
K_{i}
intersects
\gamma
at distance greater than
(1+\sqrt{4d-3})R
from both
H_{1}
and
H_{2}
.

From each
K_{i}
we define an oriented abstract hyperplane component
Z_{i}
as follows. After setting
Z_{i}=K_{i}\cap Y
, we need to define a non-trivial cohomology class
c_{Z_{i}}\in\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{K_{i}}(Z_{i}))
where, as usual, we consider cohomology with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
. Cutting
Y
along
K_{i}
decomposes it as

Y=Y_{1}\cup_{K_{i}}Y_{2}\,,

and Proposition
2.21
then tells us that

\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)=\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{1})\#_{K_{i}}\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{2})\,.

In particular,
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H_{1},H_{2}\}}
contains a copy of
L_{i}=\mathrm{Wh}_{K_{i}}(Z_{i})
, which is canonically isomorphic to the links of
K_{i}
in both
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{1})
and
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{2})
by Lemma
2.13
. Since
Y
is a 2-cut, there is a non-trivial class
c_{Y}
in the reduced cohomology group
\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H_{1},H_{2}\}})
. The cohomology class
c_{Z_{i}}
is then defined as the pullback of
c_{Y}
along the inclusion of
L_{i}
into
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H_{1},H_{2}\}}
. We claim that
c_{Z_{i}}\neq 0
.

Indeed, Mayer–Vietoris applied to the decomposition

\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H_{1},H_{2}\}}=\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{1})_{\{H_{1},K_{i}\}}\cup_{L_{i}}\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{2})_{\{K_{i},H_{2}\}}

shows that the pullback
b
of
c_{Y}
to
\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{1})_{\{H_{1},K_{i}\}})
(without loss of generality) is non-zero. But
c_{Z_{i}}
is the pullback of
b
to
\tilde{H}^{0}(L_{i})
, so if
c_{Z_{i}}=0
then, by Mayer–Vietoris applied to the decomposition

\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{1})_{H_{1}}=\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{1})_{\{H_{1},K_{i}\}}\cup_{L_{i}}C(L_{i})\,,

we see that
b
is the image of a non-trivial cohomology class in
\tilde{H}^{0}(\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{1})_{H_{1}})
, contradicting the hypothesis that
X
has no 1-cuts. Hence,
c_{Z_{i}}\neq 0
as claimed.

We next prove a bound on the diameter of any
Z_{i}
. Indeed, let
x
be the point at which
K_{i}
intersects
\gamma
. Since
Y
is contained in the closed
R
-neighbourhood of
H_{1}\cup\gamma\cup H_{2}
,
Z_{i}
is the union of two closed subsets:

Z_{i}=P\cup Q

where
P=Z_{i}\cap(N_{R}(H_{1})\cup N_{R}(H_{2}))
and
Q=Z_{i}\cap N_{R}(\gamma)
.

By Lemma
3.11
, the distance from any
y\in Q
to
x
is bounded above by

\frac{R}{\sin\epsilon_{d}}=\frac{R}{\sin\cot^{-1}(2\sqrt{d-1})}=R\sqrt{4d-3}\,.

We next argue that
P
is empty. Indeed, if not then there is
y\in P\cap Q
, since
Z_{i}
is connected and
P
and
Q
are both closed. But such a
y
is within distance
R
of
H_{1}\cup H_{2}
and within
R\sqrt{4d-3}
of
x
, so
K_{i}
intersects
\gamma
at distance less than
R(1+\sqrt{4d-3})
from either
H_{1}
or
H_{2}
, contradicting the construction of the
K_{i}
above. Thus,
P
is indeed empty, so
Z_{i}=Q
and
\diam(Z_{i})=\diam(Q)\leq 2R\sqrt{4d-3}
.

Therefore, by the pigeonhole principle and the choice of
M
, there exist distinct
i<j
such that
Z_{i}
and
Z_{j}
are isomorphic oriented abstract hyperplane components. Cutting
Y
along
K_{i}
and
K_{j}
decomposes it as

Y=Y_{1}\cup_{K_{i}}Y^{\prime}\cup_{K_{j}}Y_{2}\,.

Applying Lemma
2.25
twice,
Y^{\prime}
is a 2-cut between
K_{i}
and
K_{j}
, while
Z_{i}
and
Z_{j}
are the corresponding bounding hyperplane components.

Finally, note that the inclusions of
L_{i}=\mathrm{Wh}_{K_{i}}(Z_{i})
and
L_{j}=\mathrm{Wh}_{K_{j}}(Z_{j})
into
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)_{\{H_{1},H_{2}\}}
factor through the inclusion of
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y^{\prime})_{\{K_{i},K_{j}\}}
. Therefore, setting
c_{Y^{\prime}}
to be the pullback of
c_{Y}
to
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y^{\prime})_{\{K_{i},K_{j}\}}
, we see that
c_{Y^{\prime}}
pulls back to both
c_{Z_{i}}
and
c_{Z_{j}}
. This completes the proof that
Y^{\prime}
is a periodic 2-cut.
∎

3.3
From
2
-cuts to
k
-cuts

In the previous section, we proved a uniform bound on the widths of 2-cuts. In this section, we will upgrade this to a uniform bound on the widths of

-cuts, where the bound is independent of

. The key result is Lemma

, which encapsulates the fact that a finite collection of hyperplanes coarsely has the structure of a quasi-tree. I am grateful to Mark Hagen for pointing out that this lemma should be true, and that it would simplify the proof of my argument.

The proof of Lemma

is inspired by the projection-complex machinery of Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara

of convex subcomplexes of a hyperbolic, CAT(0) cube complex
\widetilde{X}
. (In

6
]
this collection would typically be infinite. For us it will be finite, but of unbounded cardinality.) For each
U\in\mathbb{V}
, we consider the closest-point projection
\pi_{U}:\widetilde{X}\to U
. For each
U
, Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara use this to define a
distance function
on pairs of elements of
\mathbb{V}\smallsetminus\{U\}
via

d^{\pi}_{U}(V,W):=\diam(\pi_{U}(V)\cup\pi_{U}(W))\,.

They go on to state
projection complex axioms
for the distance functions
d^{\pi}_{\bullet}
, with respect to a fixed constant
\theta>0
; see
[
7
, §1.3]
for a concise statement of the axioms. The next proposition asserts that, as long as the elements of
\mathbb{V}
are pairwise sufficiently distant from each other, the projection complex axioms are satisfied.

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
\widetilde{X}
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic CAT(0) cube complex and let
\mathbb{V}
be a finite set of convex subcomplexes of
\widetilde{X}
.
There are constants
C
and
\theta>0
, depending only on
\widetilde{X}
, such that, as long as the elements of
\mathbb{V}
are pairwise at distance at least
C
, the following hold for all distinct
U,V,W,Y\in\mathbb{V}
:

(i)

d^{\pi}_{U}(V,W)=d^{\pi}_{U}(W,V)
;

(ii)

d^{\pi}_{U}(V,W)+d^{\pi}_{U}(W,Y)\geq d^{\pi}_{U}(V,Y)
;

(iii)

d^{\pi}_{U}(V,V)\leq\theta
;

(iv)

if
d^{\pi}_{U}(V,W)>\theta
then
d^{\pi}_{V}(U,W)\leq\theta
;

(v)

\#\{Z\in\mathbb{V}\mid d^{\pi}_{Z}(V,W)>\theta\}<\infty

Proof.

Properties (i) and (ii) are obvious, while property (v) is immediate by hypothesis since
\mathbb{V}
is assumed to be finite. Property (iii) is known as the
contracting property
for quasi-convex subspaces of
\delta
-hyperbolic spaces, and a standard exercise shows that it holds for any
\theta\geq 8\delta
as long as
C\geq 2\delta
. Finally, property (iv) is known as the
Behrstock inequality
. A sketch is given in
[
57
, p. 133]
.
∎

In order to construct a quasitree, it is necessary to adjust the distance functions
d^{\pi}_{\bullet}
to strengthen the Behrstock inequality. For this, we follow Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara–Sisto
[
7
]
. From
d^{\pi}_{\bullet}
, they construct a new family of distance functions
d_{\bullet}
with improved properties.

Proposition 3.15
.

For
d^{\pi}_{\bullet}
and
\theta
as above, there are functions
d_{\bullet}
such that

|d^{\pi}_{\bullet}-d_{\bullet}|\leq 2\theta\,,

and that satisfy the following axioms for all distinct
U,V,W,Y\in\mathbb{V}
:

(i)

d_{U}(V,W)=d_{U}(W,V)
;

(ii)

d_{U}(V,W)+d_{U}(W,Y)\geq d_{U}(V,Y)
;

(iii)

d_{U}(V,V)\leq 11\theta
;

(iv)

if
d_{U}(V,W)>11\theta
then
d_{W}(V,Y)=d_{W}(U,Y)
unless
Y=W
;

(v)

\#\{Z\in\mathbb{V}\mid d_{Z}(V,W)>\theta\}<\infty
.

Proof.

This is
[
7
, Proposition 4.14]
.
∎

Remark 3.16
.

Proposition
3.15
(iv) should be thought of as a strengthened version of the Behrstock inequality. Indeed, taking
Y=V
in Proposition
3.15
(iv) shows that, if
d_{U}(V,W)>11\theta
, then
d_{W}(U,V)=d_{W}(V,V)\leq 11\theta
by Proposition
3.15
(iii).

Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara–Sisto use the strengthened projection complex axioms to construct an infinite quasitree
\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{V})
. In our situation,
\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{V})
will be finite, and we will use it to find
U\in\mathbb{V}
onto which the diameter of the projection of all the other elements of
\mathbb{V}
is bounded.

Definition 3.17

(Projection complex)
.

For any
K>0
, the
projection complex

\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{V})
is a graph with vertex set
\mathbb{V}
. Distinct vertices
U,V
are joined by an edge if
d_{W}(U,V)\leq K
for all
W\neq U,V
.

For large enough
K
, they go on to define a
standard path
in
\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{V})
between any
U,V\in\mathbb{V}
; we will let
[U,V]
denote this standard path. Similarly, we will let
(U,V)
denote
[U,V]\smallsetminus\{U,V\}
, etc. (In
[
7
]
, the set of vertices in
(U,V)
is denoted by
\mathbf{Y}_{K}(U,V)
.) We summarise the properties of standard paths in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.18

(Properties of standard paths)
.

Suppose
K\geq 33\theta
. Let
U,V\in\mathbb{V}
. The
standard path

[U,V]
is an embedded interval in
\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{V})
between
U
and
V
. Furthermore, the following hold.

(i)

If
U^{\prime},V^{\prime}\in[U,V]
then
[U^{\prime},V^{\prime}]\subseteq[U,V]
.

(ii)

If
n
is the length of the standard path
[U,V]
then the shortest path in
\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{V})
from
U
to
V
has length at least
\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor+1
.

(iii)

If
W\in(U,V]
then
d_{U}(V,W)\leq K
.

Proof.

The proofs of
[
7
, §2–3]
use a parameter
\theta
that satisfies the
strong projection axioms
. In our context, the strong projection axioms are provided by Proposition
3.15
, so we must replace
\theta
by
11\theta
.

The vertices of
(U,V)
consist of those
W\in\mathbb{V}
such that
d_{W}(U,V)>K
. Setting
W_{1}<W_{2}
if
d_{W_{1}}(U,W_{2})>K
defines a total order on the vertices of
(U,V)
by
[
7
, Proposition 2.3]
, to which we may add
U
as the least element and
V
as the greatest element;
[
7
, Lemma 3.1]
asserts that these are indeed the vertices of an embedded path.

Item (
i
) now follows immediately from
[
7
, Corollary 2.5]
and item (
ii
) is
[
7
, Lemma 3.7]
. For item (
iii
), there is nothing to prove if
W=V
; otherwise
d_{W}(U,V)>K>11\theta
so
d_{U}(V,W)\leq 11\theta<K
by Remark
3.16
.
∎

Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara–Sisto also provide a very precise description of triangles of standard paths.

Lemma 3.19

(Triangles of standard paths)
.

Suppose
K\geq 33\theta
. Let
U,V,W\in\mathbb{V}
. The complement

[U,V]\smallsetminus([U,W]\cup[W,V])

in
\mathcal{P}_{K}(\mathbb{V})
is connected, and contains at most two vertices.

Proof.

This is
[
7
, Lemma 3.6]
.
∎

With these facts in hand, we can prove our desired bound on diameters of projections.

Lemma 3.20
.

Let
\widetilde{X}
be a
\delta
-hyperbolic CAT(0) cube complex and let
\mathbb{V}
be a finite set of unbounded convex subcomplexes of
\widetilde{X}
with at least two elements. There is a constant
C
, depending only on
\widetilde{X}
, such that, as long as the elements of
\mathbb{V}
are pairwise at distance at least
C
, there is
U\in\mathbb{Y}
such that

\diam\left(\bigcup_{V\in\mathbb{V}\smallsetminus U}\pi_{U}(V)\right)\leq 400\theta\,.

Proof.

Let
C
and
\theta
be as in Proposition
3.14
, and consider the projection complex
\mathcal{P}_{33\theta}(\mathbb{V})
, defined using the distance functions
d_{\bullet}
from Proposition
3.15
. Since
\mathbb{V}
is finite, there are finitely many standard paths; fix
[U,V]
of maximal length. Let
V^{\prime}
be the vertex of
[U,V]
adjacent to
U
, and let
W^{\prime}
be any other vertex of
\mathcal{P}_{33\theta}(\mathbb{V})
adjacent to
U
.

First, note that
U
is not contained in the standard path from
W^{\prime}
to
V
. Indeed, if
U
were contained in
[W^{\prime},V]
then, by Proposition
3.18
(
i
),
[U,V]
would be a proper sub-path of
[W^{\prime},V]
, contradicting the choice of
U
. Note also that the standard path
[U,W^{\prime}]
is a single edge, by Proposition
3.18
(
ii
).

Next, consider the triangle of standard paths
[U,W^{\prime}]\cup[W^{\prime},V]\cup[V,U]
. Combining the facts from the previous paragraph with Lemma
3.19
, we see that
V^{\prime}
is joined to
W^{\prime}
by an edge-path of length at most 5, avoiding
U
. By the definition of
\mathcal{P}_{33\theta}(\mathbb{V})
and induction on the length of this edge-path, it follows that
d_{U}(V^{\prime},W^{\prime})\leq 5\times 33\theta=165\theta
.

Now consider an arbitrary
W\neq U
in
\mathbb{V}
and let
W^{\prime}
be the vertex of
[W,U]
adjacent to
U
. Combining the estimate from the previous paragraph with Proposition
3.18
(
iii
) gives

d_{U}(V^{\prime},W)\leq d_{U}(V^{\prime},W^{\prime})+d_{U}(W^{\prime},W)\leq 165\theta+33\theta=198\theta\,.

It follows from Proposition
3.15
that
d^{\pi}_{U}(V^{\prime},W)\leq 200\theta
, and so
d^{\pi}_{U}(W_{1},W_{2})\leq 400\theta
for any
W_{1},W_{2}\neq U
in
\mathbb{V}
, by the triangle inequality. This is the required bound, by the definition of
d^{\pi}_{\bullet}
.
∎

The geometric lemma that we need can now be easily deduced.

Lemma 3.21
.

Let
\widetilde{X}
be a
d
-dimensional,
\delta
-hyperbolic CAT(0) cube complex. There is a constant
D
with the following property. Suppose that
\mathbb{V}
is any finite, non-empty set of unbounded convex subcomplexes of
\widetilde{X}
and that
d(U,V)>D
for every distinct
U,V\in\mathbb{V}
. Then, there exists a hyperplane
H
and
U\in\mathbb{V}
such that
H
separates
U
from every
V\in\mathbb{V}\smallsetminus\{U\}
.

Proof.

Let
C
and
\theta
be as in Lemma
3.20
. By Theorem
1.8
, there is
r
such that, for any subset
Z\subseteq\widetilde{X}
of diameter at most
400\theta
, the diameter of
\Hull(Z)
is at most
r
.

For each
U\in\mathbb{V}
, let
\widehat{U}
be the convex hull of the
(\sqrt{d}+r)
-neighbourhood of
U
, which is contained in the
R
-neighbourhood of
U
, for some uniform
R
, by Theorem
1.8
. Let
\widehat{\mathbb{V}}:=\{\widehat{U}\mid U\in\mathbb{V}\}
and let
D=C+2R
.

As long as the elements of
\mathbb{V}
are at distance at least
D
from each other, it follows that the elements of
\widehat{\mathbb{V}}
are at distance at least
C
from each other. Hence, by Lemma
3.20
, there is
\widehat{U}
such that

\diam\left(\bigcup_{\widehat{V}\in\widehat{\mathbb{V}}\smallsetminus\widehat{U}}\pi_{\widehat{U}}(\widehat{V})\right)\leq 400\theta\,.

Let
W\subseteq\widehat{U}
be the convex hull of
\bigcup_{\widehat{V}\in\widehat{\mathbb{V}}\smallsetminus\widehat{U}}\pi_{\widehat{U}}(\widehat{V})
, so
\diam W\leq r
.

Since
W
contains a point on the frontier of
\widehat{U}
, it follows from the bound on the diameter of
W
and the definition of
\widehat{U}
that
d(U,W)\geq\sqrt{d}
. In particular, by Lemma
1.7
there is a hyperplane
H
that separates
U
from
W
; note that
H
intersects
\widehat{U}
non-trivially by construction.

Finally, consider any
V\neq U
in
\mathbb{V}
. It remains to prove that
H
separates
U
from
V
. Since
H
separates
U
from
W
, it suffices to prove that
H
is disjoint from
V
and does not separate
W
from
V
. Because hyperplanes are convex and
H
intersects
\widehat{U}
, if
H
also intersected
V
then it would intersect
W
, a contradiction. So
H
is disjoint from
V
. If
H
separated
W
from
V
then a shortest geodesic
\gamma
from
V
to
W
would cross
H
by Lemma
1.7
; but
\gamma
is also a shortest geodesic from
V
to
\widehat{U}
, so
H
would separate
V
from
\widehat{U}
, contradicting the fact that
H
intersects
\widehat{U}
.
∎

We can now upgrade our bound on the widths of 2-cuts to a bound on the widths of
k
-cuts.

Theorem 3.22
.

Let
X
be an essential,
d
-dimensional, hyperbolic cube complex without 0-cuts or 1-cuts. Then
\pi_{1}(X)
is cyclically indecomposable if and only if there is a uniform bound, independent of
k
, on the widths of all
k
-cuts with
k\geq 2
.

Proof.

If there is a bound on the widths of
k
-cuts for all
k\geq 2
then, in particular, there is a bound on the widths of all
2
-cuts. Therefore,
X
is cyclically essential and
\pi_{1}(X)
is cyclically indecomposable by Theorem
3.13
.

For the converse implication, let
C
be the bound from Theorem
3.13
. By Theorem
1.8
, there is
R
such that the hull of the
C
-neighbourhood of any convex subset is contained in the
R
neighbourhood of that subset. Let
D
be the constant from Lemma
3.21
.

Let
Y
be a
k
-cut between a collection of
k\geq 2
hyperplanes
\{H_{1},\ldots H_{k}\}
, and suppose that the width of
Y
is greater than
2R+D
. Suppose, furthermore, that
k
is minimal such that there exists a
k
-cut of width greater than
2R+D
.

For each
i
, let
U_{i}
be the convex hull of the
C
-neighbourhood of
H_{i}
. Then the
U_{i}
are pairwise at distance at least
D
.

By Lemma
3.21
, there is a hyperplane
K
that, without loss of generality, separates
U_{1}
from the remaining
H_{i}
. In particular,
K
is at distance at least
C
from
H_{1}
and separates all the other
H_{i}
from
H_{1}
. Thus, cutting
Y
along
K
gives

Y=Y_{1}\cup_{K}Y_{2}

where
H_{1}
bounds
Y_{1}
and the remaining
H_{i}
all bound
Y_{2}
.

By the minimality of
k
,
\{H_{2},\ldots,H_{k}\}
is not a cut set in
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y_{2})
. Therefore, Lemma
2.25
implies that
Y_{1}
is a 2-cut between
H_{1}
and
K
, which is of width at least
C
by construction. By the choice of
C
, it follows that
\pi_{1}(X)
is cyclically decomposable, as required.
∎

4
One-ended subgroups

In this section, we prove both our main theorem about cubulated hyperbolic groups and its corollary about one-relator groups.

4.1
Cubulated hyperbolic groups

With the uniform bound on the widths of cuts in hand, it is easy to prove the main theorem in the cyclically indecomposable case.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X
be a hyperbolic cube complex. If
\pi_{1}(X)
is one-ended and cyclically indecomposable then it has a one-ended quasiconvex subgroup of infinite index.

Proof.

By Corollary
2.34
, we may without loss of generality assume that
X
is essential, without 0- or 1-cuts. Let
C
be the uniform bound on widths of
k
-cuts given by Theorem
3.22
.

Choose any hyperplane
H
of
X
, and fix an elevation
\widetilde{H}
to the universal cover
\widetilde{X}
. Because
\pi_{1}H
is separable in
G=\pi_{1}X
by the work of Agol
[
1
]
and Haglund–Wise
[
32
]
, there is a finite-index subgroup
G_{0}
of
G
such that

d(\widetilde{H},g\widetilde{H})>C

for any
g\in G_{0}\smallsetminus\Stab(\widetilde{H})
.

Let
X_{0}
denote the quotient
G_{0}\backslash\widetilde{X}
and let
H_{0}
be the image of
\widetilde{H}
, which is a hyperplane of
X_{0}
. Let
X^{\prime}
be (a component of) the result of cutting
X_{0}
along
H_{0}
. Since
X^{\prime}
is finite and maps locally isometrically to
X
,
\pi_{1}(X^{\prime})
is a quasiconvex subgroup of
G
. Furthermore, since
\pi_{1}(X^{\prime})
is a vertex-group of a non-trivial graph-of groups decomposition for
G_{0}
, it has infinite index in
G_{0}
, and hence in
G
. It remains to show that
\pi_{1}(X^{\prime})
is one-ended.

With a view to a contradiction, suppose that
\pi_{1}(X^{\prime})
is freely decomposable. By Proposition
2.18
, there is a 0-cut
Y\to X^{\prime}
. But
\mathrm{Wh}_{X^{\prime}}(Y)
is obtained from
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
by deleting the
G_{0}
-translates of
\widetilde{H}
, so some finite set of translates

g_{1}\widetilde{H},\ldots,g_{k}\widetilde{H}

(with
g_{i}\in G_{0}
) defines a cut set in
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
. Since
\mathrm{Wh}_{X}(Y)
has no 0-cut or 1-cuts,
k\geq 2
. Therefore
Y
is a
k
-cut in
\widetilde{X}
of width greater than
C
, contradicting the choice of
C
.
∎

By passing to the Grushko and Bowditch JSJ decompositions, the main theorem follows.

Proof of Theorem
A
.

Since
\pi_{1}(X)
is a finitely generated, torsion-free group, it is freely indecomposable if and only if it is one-ended, by Stallings’ theorem
[
58
]
. If
\pi_{1}(X)
is freely decomposable, Grushko’s theorem implies that either
\pi_{1}(X)
is free or it has a one-ended free factor, which is necessarily a quasiconvex subgroup of infinite index. Therefore, we may assume that
\pi_{1}(X)
is itself one-ended.

Consider the Bowditch JSJ decomposition of
\pi_{1}(X)

[
9
]
. If the decomposition has only one vertex and no edges, then that vertex is either of surface or of rigid type. In the former case,
\pi_{1}(X)
is a surface group. In the latter case,
\pi_{1}(X)
is cyclically indecomposable, so it has a one-ended, quasiconvex subgroup of infinite index by Theorem
4.1
.

Now consider the case where the JSJ decomposition is non-trivial. Every vertex group
V
is quasiconvex
[
9
, Proposition 1.2]
, and in particular finitely generated and hyperbolic. If some vertex group
V
is not itself free, then by Grushko’s theorem it has a one-ended free factor
V^{\prime}
, which provides a one-ended, quasiconvex subgroup, necessarily of infinite index in
\pi_{1}(X)
. On the other hand, if every vertex group is free, then
\pi_{1}(X)
is either a surface group or contains a one-ended, quasiconvex subgroup by
[
65
, Theorem 3]
.
∎

4.2
One-relator groups

Combining the result for cubulated hyperbolic groups with recent joint work of the author with Louder
[
38
,
39
]
, and also work of Linton
[
37
]
and Gardam–Kielak–Logan
[
23
]
, we can also answer Question
0.1
for one-relator groups. The key is Puder’s notion of
primitivity rank

[
48
]
.

Definition 4.2

(Primitivity rank)
.

Let
w
be a non-trivial element of a free group
F
. The
primitivity rank

\pi(w)
is the minimal rank of a subgroup
w\in H\subseteq F

such that
w
is not a primitive element of
H
.

By convention,
\min\varnothing=\infty
, so
\pi(w)=\infty
if and only if
w
is primitive in
F
(and hence in every subgroup containing it). As a result of this convention,
\pi(w)\geq 1
for all
w
. (It can be convenient to extend the definition of
\pi
to the trivial element
1\in F
and set
\pi(1)=0
.)

It has recently been discovered that
\pi(w)
determines several features of the one-relator group
G=F/\llangle w\rrangle
. These results can be summarised in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
F
be free,
w
a non-trivial element of
F
and
G=F/\llangle w\rrangle
. Then:

(i)

\pi(w)=\infty
if and only if
G
is free;

(ii)

\pi(w)=1
if and only if
G
has torsion;

(iii)

if
\pi(w)=2
then
G
has a two-generator, one-relator subgroup
P
that is not free;

(iv)

if
\pi(w)>2
then
G
is hyperbolic and cubulated.

Proof.

Item (i) is a result of the classical theorem of Whitehead that, for non-trivial
w
,
G
is free if and only if
w
is primitive
[
40
, Proposition 5.10]
. It is easy to see that
\pi(w)=1
if and only if
w
is a proper power in
F
; a classical theorem of Karrass–Magnus–Solitar asserts that this is true exactly when
G
has torsion
[
40
, Proposition 5.17]
. Item (iii) is a theorem of Louder and the author
[
38
, Theorem 1.5]
, while item (iv) is a theorem of Linton
[
37
, Theorem 8.2]
(using deep work of Wise
[
69
]
, as well as further work of Louder and the author
[
39
]
).
∎

Remark 4.4
.

The one-relator group
G
is also hyperbolic and cubulated when
\pi(w)=1
, by work of Newman
[
44
]
and Wise
[
69
]
. However, we will not need either of these facts here.

The final piece of the jigsaw is a recent result of Gardam–Kielak–Logan, who handled the two-generator, one-relator case of Question
0.1

[
23
, Theorem 1.5]
.

Theorem 4.5

(Gardam–Kielak–Logan)
.

Let
G
be a two-generator, one-relator group. If every subgroup of infinite index in
G
is free, then
G
is either free or the fundamental group of a closed surface.

Combining these results with Theorem
A
, we obtain the desired result for all one-relator groups.

Proof of Theorem
D
.

A one-relator group
G=F/\llangle w\rrangle
is free if
\pi(w)=0
or
\pi(w)=\infty
. Since
G
is infinite and every subgroup of infinite index is free,
G
is torsion-free, so
\pi(w)>1
. If
\pi(w)>2
then
G
is hyperbolic and cubulated by Theorem
4.3
(iv), so the result follows from Theorem
A
. Thus, it remains to handle the case when
\pi(w)=2
.

In this case,
G
has a two-generator, one-relator subgroup
P
that is not free by Theorem
4.3
(iii). The index of
P
in
G
is finite by hypothesis, and every subgroup of infinite index in
P
is free. By Theorem
4.5
,
P
is either
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
or
\mathbb{Z}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
, so the same is true of
G
by Bieberbach’s theorem.
∎

5
Strebel’s theorem

Let
G
be an
n
-dimensional Poincaré duality group. Strebel proved that any subgroup of infinite index in
G
must have strictly smaller cohomological dimension
[
61
]
, paralleling an older theorem of Whitehead about manifolds
[
64
]
.

Theorem 5.1

(Strebel theorem)
.

Let
G
be a
PD_{n}
group. If
H
is a subgroup of infinite index in
G
, then
\mathrm{cd}(H)<n
.

By Stallings’ theorem, Theorem
A
can be thought of as a converse to Strebel’s theorem when
n=1
and
G
is hyperbolic and cubulated. It is therefore natural to wonder whether, if
G
remains hyperbolic and cubulated, the converse is also true for larger
n
.
6
6

6

This was stated as an open question in an earlier version of this paper.
The purpose of this section is to note that this converse fails. I am grateful to Genevieve Walsh and Macarena Arenas for explaining this to me.

The key point is that Strebel’s theorem is not optimal. Indeed, the following stronger result is given as an exercise in Brown’s book
[
12
, Exercise 4, p. 203]
.

Theorem 5.2

(Strong Strebel theorem)
.

Let
G
be a group of type
FP
and cohomological dimension
n
, and suppose
H^{n}(G,\mathbb{Z}G)
is finitely generated as an abelian group. If
H
is a subgroup of infinite index in
G
, then
\mathrm{cd}(H)<n
.

With the stronger theorem in hand, we can provide an example of a 3-dimensional group, which is not a
PD_{3}
group, but such that every subgroup of infinite index is at most 2-dimensional.

Example 5.3
.

Let
L
be a square-free flag triangulation of the 2-dimensional torus (which exists by a lemma of Dranishnikov
[
20
, Proposition 2.1]
). Let
W_{L}
be the corresponding right-angled Coxeter group, and let
G=[W_{L},W_{L}]
be the commutator subgroup. Then
G
is a hyperbolic, cubulated group, acting properly and cocompactly on the Davis cube complex
\widetilde{X}
; let
X
be the quotient cube complex. The link of every vertex of
\widetilde{X}
, and hence also of
X
, is isomorphic to the torus
L
.

The group
G
has geometric and cohomological dimension 3, but is not a Poincaré duality group. Indeed, the Gromov boundary
\partial_{\infty}G
is the Pontryagin sphere
[
47
, Remark 4.4(1)]
, which is 2-dimensional with
\check{H}^{2}(\partial_{\infty}G;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}
and
\check{H}^{1}(\partial_{\infty}G;\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{\infty}
. By the Bestvina–Mess theorem
[
8
, Corollary 1.3]
,

H^{*}(G;\mathbb{Z}G)\cong\check{H}^{*-1}(\partial_{\infty}G;\mathbb{Z})\,,

so
G
has cohomological dimension 3, and every subgroup of infinite index has cohomological dimension at most 2 by Theorem
5.2
. But
G
is not
PD_{3}
, because
H^{2}(G,\mathbb{Z}G)\cong\check{H}^{1}(\partial_{\infty}G;\mathbb{Z})\neq 0
.

Thus, Theorem
A
should actually be thought of as a conditional converse to Theorem
5.2
in dimension 2.

6
Questions

This final section contains some open questions that are suggested by the results of this paper.

As explained in §
5
, Theorem
A
can be thought of as a converse to the strong Strebel theorem (Theorem
5.2
) in dimension 2, for cubulated hyperbolic groups. Therefore, we can ask whether similar converses hold in higher dimensions.

Question 6.1
.

Let
X
be a hyperbolic cube complex with
G=\pi_{1}(X)
and
\cd(G)=n>2
. If the cohomological dimension of every subgroup of infinite index in
G
is less than
n
, does it follow that
H^{n}(G,\mathbb{Z}G)
is finitely generated as an abelian group?

The proof of Theorem
A
made essential use of the separability of hyperplane stabilisers, which is closely related to the fact that
X
is virtually special. Removing this condition would necessitate a different approach.

Question 6.2
.

Let
X
be a compact, non-positively curved cube complex with every subgroup of infinite index free. Must
\pi_{1}(X)
be either a free group or a surface group?

Combined with Theorem
A
, the well known flat-closing conjecture would imply a positive answer.

In a similar vein, removing the cubulation hypothesis from Theorem
A
would be a major breakthrough. The following question reiterates
[
5
, Questions 1.7 and 1.11]
.

Question 6.3

(Gromov, Whyte)
.

Let
G
be an infinite hyperbolic group. If every subgroup of infinite index in
G
is free, must
G
be either a free group or a surface group?

Of course, if every one-ended hyperbolic group had a surface subgroup, then Question
6.3
would have an affirmative answer.

The next question addresses the slight mismatch between the conclusions of Theorems
A
and
D
.

Question 6.4
.

Let
G
be a two-generator, one-relator group such that every finitely generated subgroup of infinite index is free. Must
G
be isomorphic to either
\mathbb{Z}
or a solvable Baumslag–Solitar group?

(Note that the two two-generator surface groups,
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
\mathbb{Z}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}
, are
BS(1,1)
and
BS(1,-1)
respectively.)

As explained in §
2.5
, any non-positively curved
square
complex can be ‘unfolded’ until Theorem
E
applies, providing an effective geometric computation of the Grushko decomposition. I would like to extend this method to higher-dimensional cube complexes.
7
7

7

Touikan’s algorithm provides a general computation of the Grushko decomposition for any torsion-free group with solvable word problem
[
62
]
. However, a cubical method would be preferable.

The first step is to recognise whether or not the cube complex
X
is essential. Sam Shepherd has explained to me that being essential can be recognised algorithmically: the key observation is that an inessential CAT(0) cube complex contains a half-space that is minimal with respect to inclusion, and the bounding hyperplane of a minimal half-space can be recognised
[
56
]
.

Given essential
X
, the next step is to determine whether or not
\pi_{1}(X)
splits freely. The following question asks whether or not the sufficient condition of Theorem
E
can always be applied.

Question 6.5
.

Let
G
be a one-ended cubulable group. Is
G\cong\pi_{1}(X)
, where
X
is essential and no vertex of
X
has a link with a separating simplex?

Shepherd’s theorem (Theorem
2.32
) implies that there is an
X
without separating vertices in the links, but does not provide information about higher-dimensional simplices. Regardless of the answer to Question
6.5
, it is also important to find an algorithmic condition to recognise whether or not
X
admits 0- or 1-cuts.

Assuming an understanding of free splittings, one may pose similar questions about splittings over
\mathbb{Z}
. It is not clear if there is an analogue of Theorem
E
for cyclic splittings.

Question 6.6
.

Let
X
be a compact, non-positively curved cube complex with
\pi_{1}(X)
one-ended. Is there a condition on the links of vertices of
X
that guarantees that
\pi_{1}(X)
does not split over
\mathbb{Z}
?

For instance, it seems probable that the techniques of this article can be used to prove that, if
X
is a hyperbolic square complex and the links of vertices of
X
are connected without cut vertices or cut pairs, then
\pi_{1}(X)
does not split over
\mathbb{Z}
. But it would be better still to provide a local criterion for higher-dimensional cube complexes, or outside the hyperbolic setting.

As a step towards understanding cyclic splittings outside the hyperbolic setting, the next question asks whether the hyperbolicity hypothesis can be removed from Theorem
3.6
.

Question 6.7
.

Let
X
be a compact, essential, non-positively curved cube complex without 0- or 1-cuts. If
\pi_{1}(X)
splits over
\mathbb{Z}
, must
X
admit a periodic 2-cut?

The converse statement, that
\pi_{1}(X)
splits over
\mathbb{Z}
if
X
admits a periodic 2-cut, follows from the algebraic annulus theorem of Dunwoody–Swenson
[
21
]
.
