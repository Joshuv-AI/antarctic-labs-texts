Brownian Motion with a Singular Drift
Dante DeBlassie

Abstract




We study the effect of a power law drift on Brownian motion in the positive half-line, where the order of the drift at
0
and
\infty
is different.

2000 Mathematical Subject Classification
. Primary 60J60, 60J65, 60J55. Secondary 60F10.

Key Words and Phrases
. Brownian motion, Brownian motion with drift, half-line, exit time, stochastic comparison, large deviations,
h
-transform.

Running Title:
Brownian motion with a singular drift.

1
Introduction

For a real-valued process
X_{t}
, let
\tau_{R}(X)
be the first time the process hits
R
. Specialize
X_{t}
to be a Brownian motion with a power law drift: the process satisfying the stochastic differential equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}dX_{t}=dB_{t}-\beta X_{t}^{-p}\,dt,\quad t<\tau_{0}(X)\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr X_{0}=x>0,\end{array}\right.

where
\beta\not=0
and
p>0
.

Note that when
\beta=0
,
X_{t}
is merely Brownian motion and it is well-known (Feller 1971) that

P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)>t)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{x/\sqrt{t}}e^{-u^{2}/2}\,dt.

When
p=1
, it is known that for
\beta>-\frac{1}{2}
,

P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)>t)=\frac{2^{1/2-\beta}}{\Gamma(1/2+\beta)}\int_{0}^{x/\sqrt{t}}u^{2\beta}e^{-u^{2}/2}\,dt,

and for
\beta\leq-\frac{1}{2}
,

P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)=\infty)=1

(Göing-Jaeschke and Yor (2003)). Using Theorem 1.1 of Chapter 5 in Pinsky (1995), it is not hard to show if
\beta<0
, then

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)=\infty)=1,&\quad\text{for $p>1$}\\
&\\
0<P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)=\infty)<1,&\quad\text{for $p<1$.}\end{array}\right.

DeBlassie and Smits (2009) proved the following results for
\beta>0
.

•

For
p>1
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}E_{x}[\tau_{0}(X)^{q}]<\infty,&\quad\text{if $q<1/2$,}\\
&\\
E_{x}[\tau_{0}(X)^{q}]=\infty,&\quad\text{if $q>1/2$}.\end{array}\right.

•

For
p<1
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-(1-p)/(1+p)}P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)>t)=-\gamma(p,\beta),

where

\gamma(p,\beta)=\tfrac{1}{2}p^{-2p/(1+p)}\beta^{2/(1+p)}\left[B\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1-p}{2p}\right)+B\left(\tfrac{3}{2},\tfrac{1-p}{2p}\right)\right]B\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1-p}{2p}\right)^{-(1-p)/(1+p)}.

In particular, there is a phase transition of sorts as the power
p
passes through the value
1
: roughly power law behavior (with the same power) of the tail versus subexponential behavior (with a different power).

For the last case of
\beta>0
and
0<p<1
, it is not hard to show (we indicate the argument in the next section) that so long as the form of the drift is
\beta x^{-p}
for large values of
x
, one can change the drift to be of the form
\alpha x^{-q}
,
0<q<1,
for small values of
x
, keeping it bounded in between, and still have the process hit
0
almost surely. This holds even if the multiplier
\alpha
is negative. A natural question suggested by this is to determine how much effect this change has on the asymptotic behavior of the time to hit
0
. We were surprised to learn that the behavior near the origin does not influence the time to hit
0
, at least on the logarithmic level.

We now state our results precisely. Consider the diffusion
X_{t}
given by

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}dX_{t}=dB_{t}+b(X_{t})\,dt\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr X_{0}=x>0,\end{array}\right.

where
B_{t}
is one-dimensional Brownian motion and for some
0<M_{1}<M_{2}
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
\beta>0
and
0<p,q<1
,

(1.2)

b(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\alpha x^{-q},&\qquad 0<x\leq M_{1}\\
\text{bounded measurable},&\qquad M_{1}<x<M_{2}\\
-\beta x^{-p},&\qquad M_{2}\leq x.\end{array}\right.

By standard facts, the law of
X_{t}
on the space of continuous paths in
(0,\infty)
exists uniquely up to an explosion time. Given
a\in[0,\infty]
, let

\tau_{a}(X)=\inf\{t>0:X_{t}=a\}.

Below, we will show the explosion time is finite:

(1.3)

P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)<\infty)=1.

Here is our main theorem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Under the condition (
1.2
), the solution
X_{t}
of (
1.1
) satisfies

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-(1-p)/(1+p)}\log P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)>t)=-\,\gamma(p,\beta),

where

(1.4)

\gamma(p,\beta)=\tfrac{1}{2}p^{-2p/(1+p)}\beta^{2/(1+p)}\left[B\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1-p}{2p}\right)+B\left(\tfrac{3}{2},\tfrac{1-p}{2p}\right)\right]B\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1-p}{2p}\right)^{-(1-p)/(1+p)}.

Our result extends to drifts of the form
\alpha(x)x^{q}\ell_{1}(x)
near
0
and
\beta(x)x^{p}\ell_{2}(x)
near
\infty
, where
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are slowly varying at
0
and
\infty
, respectively, and the the coefficients
\alpha(x)
and
\beta(x)
have limiting values
\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{0}>0
. In the context of regular variation, one would say
x^{q}\ell_{1}(x)
is regularly varying at
0
from the right, with index
q
, and
x^{p}\ell_{2}(x)
is regularly varying at infinity, with index
p
.

To be precise, a measurable function
\ell>0
is slowly varying at infinity if for each
\lambda>0
,

\ell(\lambda x)/\ell(x)\to 1,\text{ as $x\to\infty$.}

Similarly, measurable function
\ell>0
is slowly varying at zero from the right if for each
\lambda>0
,

\ell(\lambda x)/\ell(x)\to 1,\text{ as $x\to 0^{+}$.}

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose
\alpha:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
and
\beta:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
are continuous and
p,q\in(0,1)
. Assume:

•

\alpha(x)
is bounded in a neighborhood of
0
and
\lim_{x\to\infty}\beta(x)=\beta_{0}>0
exists;

•

\ell_{1}
is continuous and slowly varying at zero from the right;

•

\ell_{2}
is continuous and slowly varying at infinity.

Suppose for some
0<M_{1}<M_{2}
,

(1.5)

b(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-\alpha(x)x^{q}\ell_{1}(x),&\qquad 0<x\leq M_{1}\\
\text{\rm bounded measurable},&\qquad M_{1}<x<M_{2}\\
-\beta(x)x^{p}\ell_{2}(x),&\qquad M_{2}\leq x.\end{array}\right.

Then the solution
X_{t}
of (
1.1
) satisfies

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{-(1-p)/(1+p)}\log P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)>t)=-\,\gamma(p,\beta_{0}),

where
\gamma(p,\beta_{0})
is from (
1.4
).

In the sequel, for a stochastic process
Z_{t}
in
(0,\infty)
, for
0<\varepsilon<M
, we will write

\tau_{\varepsilon,M}(Z)=\tau_{\varepsilon}(Z)\wedge\tau_{M}(Z).

2
Preliminaries

First we prove zero is hit almost surely.

Lemma 2.1
.

For
X_{t}
as in (
1.1
) with
b
as in (
1.2
),

P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)<\infty)=1\text{ for all $x>0$.}

This will be a consequence of the following special case.

Lemma 2.2
.

For
X_{t}
from (
1.1
), where
b
from (
1.2
) is continuous,

P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)<\infty)=1\text{ for all $x>0$.}

Proof.

This is a consequence of the proof of a very general result (part (ii) in Theorem 1.1 of Chapter 5) in Pinsky (1995). It is a bit cumbersome to verify the hypotheses there, so we will present the proof in our very special case. Let

L=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+b(x)\frac{d}{dx}

be the differential operator corresponding to
X_{t}
. For any
0<r_{1}<r_{2}
, write

\tau_{r_{1},r_{2}}=\tau_{r_{1},r_{2}}(X).

By Theorem 1.1 in Chapter 2 of Pinsky,
\tau_{r_{1},r_{2}}<\infty
a.s.
P_{x}
for any
x\in(r_{1},r_{2})
. Define

f(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\exp\left(-2\int_{0}^{z}b(y)\,dy\right)\,dz,\quad x>0

(note the inner integral is finite because
0<q<1
). Then
f\in C^{2}(0,\infty)
and
Lf=0
. By Itô’s formula,

E_{x}[f(X(\tau_{r_{1},r_{2}}))]=f(x).

This implies

(2.1)

P_{x}(\tau_{r_{1}}<\tau_{r_{2}})=\frac{f(x)-f(r_{2})}{f(r_{1})-f(r_{2})}.

The key fact is that for large
r_{2}
,

\displaystyle f(r_{2})

\displaystyle\geq\int_{M_{2}}^{r_{2}}\exp\left(-2\int_{0}^{z}b(y)\,dy\right)\,dz

\displaystyle=\int_{M_{2}}^{r_{2}}\exp\left(-2\int_{0}^{M_{2}}b(y)\,dy-2\int_{M_{2}}^{z}b(y)\,dy\right)\,dz

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-2\int_{0}^{M_{2}}b(y)\,dy\right)\int_{M_{2}}^{r_{2}}\exp\left(-2\int_{M_{2}}^{z}b(y)\,dy\right)\,dz

\displaystyle=C\int_{M_{2}}^{r_{2}}\exp\left(2\beta\int_{M_{2}}^{z}y^{-p}\,dy\right)\,dz

(where
C<\infty
, since
b
is integrable on any interval
(0,M)
)

\displaystyle=C\int_{M_{2}}^{r_{2}}\exp\left(\tfrac{2\beta}{1-p}\left(z^{1-p}-M_{2}^{1-p}\right)\right)\,dz.

Since
1-p
and
\tfrac{2\beta}{1-p}
are both positive, we see
f(r_{2})\to\infty
as
r_{2}\to\infty
. Using this in (
2.1
) gives
P_{x}(\tau_{r_{1}}<\infty)=1
, and then letting
r_{1}\downarrow 0
gives
P_{x}(\tau_{0}<\infty)=1
, as claimed.
∎

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.
Given
b
as in (
1.2
), since
b
is bounded on
[M_{1},M_{2}]
, we can choose
\widetilde{M_{1}}<M_{1}
and
\widetilde{M_{2}}>M_{2}
, along with a corresponding continuous
\tilde{b}
satisfying (
1.2
) for
\widetilde{M_{1}}
,
\widetilde{M_{2}}
, such that
b\leq\tilde{b}
on
(0,\infty)
. Then for
d\widetilde{X}_{t}=dB_{t}+\tilde{b}(\widetilde{X}_{t})\,dt
,
\widetilde{X}_{0}=x
, by the Comparision Theorem for SDEs (Ikeda and Watanabe (1989), Theorem 1.1 in Chapter VI),
X_{t}\leq\widetilde{X}_{t}
almost surely and so

1=P_{x}(\tau_{0}(\widetilde{X})<\infty)\leq P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)<\infty),

and so
P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)<\infty)=1
, as claimed.
\square

The next results pertain to the case when
b\in C^{3}(0,\infty)
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
b
from (
1.2
) is in
C^{1}
. Then for

(2.2)

h(x)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{x}b(y)\,dy\right),\,x>0,

(2.3)

h(x)
is bounded on
[M_{1},M_{2}]
;

(2.4)

h(x)
is bounded below away from
0
on
[M_{1},M_{2}]
;

(2.5)

h(x)=\exp\left(-\tfrac{\alpha}{1-q}x^{1-q}\right)\text{on $(0,M_{1}]$;}

for some positive constant
C
,

(2.6)

h(x)=C\exp\left(-\tfrac{\beta}{1-p}x^{1-p}\right),\,x>M_{2}.

Proof.

For some constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}b(y)\,dy

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{\alpha}{1-q}x^{1-q}I_{(0,M_{1}]}(x)+\left[C_{1}+\int_{M_{1}}^{x}b(y)\,dy\right]I_{(M_{1},M_{2})}(x)

(2.7)

\displaystyle+\left[C_{2}-\tfrac{\beta}{1-p}x^{1-p}\right]I_{[M_{2},\infty)}(x).

The assertions of the Lemma follow immediately from this.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Suppose
b
from (
1.2
) is in
C^{1}
and
\alpha\geq 0
. Then for

(2.8)

V=-\tfrac{1}{2}(b^{2}+b^{\prime}),

we have
V\leq VI_{(M_{1},\infty)}
.

Proof.

Since
\alpha\geq 0
and
0<q<1
, on the interval
(0,M_{1}]
we have

V(x)=-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2x^{2q}}-\frac{\alpha q}{2x^{q+1}}\leq 0.

The conclusion is immediate.
∎

The next result we will need follows from the formula (1.1) in the proof of Theorem 1.1 from Section 4.1 in the book by Pinsky (1995). To satisfy the hypotheses he requires, we take the drift to be in
C^{3}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose
X_{t}
is from (
1.1
) in the introduction, where
X_{0}=x>0
and
b
given by (
1.2
) is in
C^{3}
. Then for
h
from (
2.2
) and
V
from (
2.8
),

P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)>t)=E_{x}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}V(B_{s})\,ds\right)h(B_{t})I_{\tau_{0}(B)>t}\right].

Proof.

Let
L=\tfrac{1}{2}\tfrac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+V
. Note
h
is positive and
L
-harmonic on
(0,\infty)
. The
h
-transform
L^{h}
of
L
is defined by

L^{h}f=\frac{1}{h}L(hf).

It is easy to show that

L^{h}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+b\frac{d}{dx}.

Let
0<\varepsilon<M<\infty
be such that
x\in(\varepsilon,M)
. Since
V
is bounded on
[\varepsilon,M]
, the formula (1.1) in the proof of Theorem 1.1 in Section 4.1 of Pinsky (1995) holds: for each continuous
f
with compact support in
(\varepsilon,M)
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h(x)}E_{x}

\displaystyle\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}V(B_{s})\,ds\right)(hf)(B_{t})I(\tau_{\varepsilon,M}(B)>t)\right]

\displaystyle=E_{x}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{Lh}{h}(X_{s})\,ds\right)f(X_{t})I(\tau_{\varepsilon,M}(X)>t)\right]

\displaystyle=E_{x}\left[f(X_{t})I(\tau_{\varepsilon,M}(X)>t)\right],

since
Lh=0
. From this we get

\frac{1}{h(x)}E_{x}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}V(B_{s})\,ds\right)h(B_{t})I(\tau_{\varepsilon,M}(B)>t)\right]=P_{x}(\tau_{\varepsilon,M}(X)>t).

By Monotone Convergence and Lemma
2.1
, we can let
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
and
M\uparrow\infty
to get

\frac{1}{h(x)}E_{x}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}V(B_{s})\,ds\right)h(B_{t})I_{\tau_{0}(B)>t}\right]=P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)>t),

as claimed.
∎

3
Lower Bound

The main result of this section is the following lower bound.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X_{t}
be as in (
1.1
), where
b
is from (
1.2
). Then

\liminf_{t\to\infty}t^{-(1-p)/(1+p)}\log P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)>t)\geq-\,\gamma(p,\beta),

where
\gamma(p,\beta)
is from (
1.4
).

We first prove a special case.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
X_{t}
be as in (
1.1
), where
b
from (
1.2
) satisfies
\alpha\geq 0
and
b\in C^{3}
. Then

\liminf_{t\to\infty}t^{-(1-p)/(1+p)}\log P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)>t)\geq-\,\gamma(p,\beta),

where
\gamma(p,\beta)
is from (
1.4
).

The proof of the theorem is long, so we break it up into pieces. By Lemma
2.5

(3.1)

P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)>t)=E_{x}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}V(B_{s})\,ds\right)h(B_{t})I_{\tau_{0}(B)>t}\right].

Define

K_{0}=\{\omega\in C_{0}:\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\omega}_{u}|^{2}\,du<\infty\}

and

(3.2)

F(\omega)=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}|\omega_{u}|^{-2p}\,du+\frac{\beta}{1-p}|\omega_{1}|^{1-p}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\dot{\omega}_{u}^{2}\,du,\quad\omega\in K_{0}.

Let
g\in K_{0}
with
g\geq 0
. Let
\delta>0
be given and set
\tilde{g}=g+\delta
,

(3.3)

\varepsilon=t^{-(1-p)/(1+p)},

and

(3.4)

Z_{u}=B_{u}-\tilde{g}(u)/\sqrt{\varepsilon}.

For
Z_{0}>0
, we have

(3.5)

\tau_{0}(Z)>1\Longrightarrow\tau_{0}(B)>1

(3.6)

\tau_{0}(Z)>1\Longrightarrow B_{u}\geq\tilde{g}(u)/\sqrt{\varepsilon},\,u\in[0,1].

Now scale and use (
3.5
):

\displaystyle P_{x}(\tau_{0}(X)>t)

\displaystyle=E_{x}\left[\exp\left(\vphantom{\int_{0}^{t}}\right.\right.\left.\left.\int_{0}^{t}V(B_{s})\,ds\right)h(B_{t})I_{\tau_{0}(B)>t}\right]

\displaystyle=E_{x/\sqrt{t}}\left[\exp\left(t\int_{0}^{1}V(\sqrt{t}B_{u})\,du\right)h(\sqrt{t}B_{1})I_{\tau_{0}(B)>1}\right]

(3.7)

\displaystyle\geq E_{x/\sqrt{t}}\left[\exp\left(t\int_{0}^{1}V(\sqrt{t}B_{u})\,du\right)h(\sqrt{t}B_{1})I_{\tau_{0}(Z)>1}\right].

Lemma 3.3
.

For
\tau_{0}(Z)>1
and
\varepsilon
from (
3.3
),

t\int_{0}^{1}V(\sqrt{t}B_{u})\left[I_{\sqrt{t}B_{u}<M_{1}}+I_{M_{2}<\sqrt{t}B_{u}}\right]\,du\geq G_{1}(\tilde{g},\varepsilon),

where

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\,G_{1}(\tilde{g},\varepsilon)=-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{g}(u)^{-2p}\,du.

Proof.

By (
2.8
),

V(x)\left[I_{0<x<M_{1}}+I_{M_{2}<x}\right]=-\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2x^{2q}}+\frac{q\alpha}{2x^{q+1}}\right)I_{0<x<M_{1}}-\left(\frac{\beta^{2}}{2x^{2p}}+\frac{p\beta}{2x^{p+1}}\right)I_{M_{2}<x}.

This is nonpositive and increasing, since
\alpha\geq 0
and
\beta>0
. By (
3.6
), for
u\in[0,1]
,
B_{u}\geq\tfrac{\tilde{g}(u)}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}
, and so

V\left(\sqrt{t}B_{u}\right)\geq V\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon}}\,\tilde{g}(u)\right)
. Thus for
\tau_{0}(Z)>1
,

\displaystyle t\int_{0}^{1}V(\sqrt{t}B_{u})

\displaystyle\left[I_{\sqrt{t}B_{u}<M_{1}}+I_{M_{2}<\sqrt{t}B_{u}}\right]\,du\geq t\int_{0}^{1}V\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon}}\,\tilde{g}(u)\right)\left[I_{\sqrt{t}B_{u}<M_{1}}+I_{M_{2}<\sqrt{t}B_{u}}\right]\,du

\displaystyle=-t\int_{0}^{1}\left[\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon}}\,\tilde{g}(u)\right)^{-2q}+\frac{q\alpha}{2}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon}}\,\tilde{g}(u)\right)^{-q-1}\right]I_{\sqrt{t}B_{u}<M_{1}}\,du

\displaystyle\qquad-t\int_{0}^{1}\left[\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon}}\,\tilde{g}(u)\right)^{-2p}+\frac{p\beta}{2}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon}}\,\tilde{g}(u)\right)^{-p-1}\right]I_{M_{2}<\sqrt{t}B_{u}}\,du

\displaystyle\geq-t\int_{0}^{1}\left[\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon}}\,\tilde{g}(u)\right)^{-2q}+\frac{q\alpha}{2}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon}}\,\tilde{g}(u)\right)^{-q-1}\right]I_{\sqrt{t/\varepsilon}\tilde{g}(u)<M_{1}}\,du

\displaystyle\qquad-t\int_{0}^{1}\left[\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon}}\,\tilde{g}(u)\right)^{-2p}+\frac{p\beta}{2}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{t}{\varepsilon}}\,\tilde{g}(u)\right)^{-p-1}\right]\,du

(since
I_{M_{2}<\sqrt{t}B_{u}}\leq 1
and since
\tau_{0}(Z)>1
implies
I_{\sqrt{t}B_{u}<M_{1}}=I_{\sqrt{t}(Z_{u}+\tilde{g}(u)/\sqrt{\varepsilon})<M_{1}}\leq I_{\sqrt{t/\varepsilon}\tilde{g}(u)<M_{1}}
for
u\in[0,1]
)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left[\alpha^{2}\varepsilon^{(2q-p-1)/(1-p)}\tilde{g}(u)^{-2q}+q\alpha\varepsilon^{(q-p)/(1-p)}\tilde{g}(u)^{-q-1}\right]I_{\tilde{g}(u)<M_{1}\varepsilon^{1/(1-p)}}\,du

(3.8)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left[\beta^{2}\varepsilon^{-1}\tilde{g}(u)^{-2p}+p\beta\tilde{g}(u)^{-p-1}\right]\,du,

where we have used (
3.3
) to replace the variable
t
in terms of the variable
\varepsilon
.
Define

G_{1}(\tilde{g},\varepsilon)=\text{RHS\eqref{eq3.8}.}

Then

\displaystyle\varepsilon\,G_{1}(\tilde{g},\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left[\alpha^{2}\varepsilon^{2(q-p)/(1-p)}\tilde{g}(u)^{-2q}+q\alpha\varepsilon^{(1+q-2p)/(1-p)}\tilde{g}(u)^{-q-1}\right]I_{\tilde{g}(u)<M_{1}\varepsilon^{1/(1-p)}}\,du

(3.9)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left[\beta^{2}\tilde{g}(u)^{-2p}+p\beta\varepsilon\tilde{g}(u)^{-p-1}\right]\,du.

Now if
\varepsilon<(\delta/M_{1})^{1-p}
, then for any
u\in[0,1]
,
\tilde{g}(u)\geq\delta>M_{1}\varepsilon^{1/(1-p)}
. Thus the first integral in (
3
) is
0
. The integrand in the second integral is bounded because
\tilde{g}\geq\delta
and
p>0
. It follows that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\varepsilon\,G_{1}(\tilde{g},\varepsilon)=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\beta^{2}\tilde{g}(u)^{-2p}\,du,

as claimed.
∎

Having studied

t\int_{0}^{1}V(\sqrt{t}B_{u})\left[I_{\sqrt{t}B_{u}<M_{1}}+I_{M_{2}<\sqrt{t}B_{u}}\right]\,du,

we now turn attention to

t\int_{0}^{1}V(\sqrt{t}B_{u})\left[I_{M_{1}\leq\sqrt{t}B_{u}\leq M_{2}}\right]\,du.

Lemma 3.4
.

For any positive
C_{1}
and
\gamma
, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\beta^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\omega_{u}+x_{\varepsilon}\right)^{-2p}I_{\left[\varepsilon^{1/(1-p)}M_{2},\infty\right)}(\omega_{u}+x_{\varepsilon})\,du+\tfrac{\beta}{1-p}\left|\omega_{1}+x_{\varepsilon}\right|^{1-p}.

Set

if the integral is finite, otherwise set
J(\omega)=\infty
. Then

is lower semicontinuous on

(4.6)

and if
\omega_{n}\to\omega
in
C_{0}
as
n\to\infty
, then

\mathop{\liminf_{n\to\infty}}_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}J_{\varepsilon}(\omega_{n})\geq J(\omega).

If
\omega\in C_{0}
is absolutely continuous, denote its derivative by
\dot{\omega}_{u}
. Then for

K_{0}=\{\omega\in C_{0}:\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\omega}_{u}|^{2}\,du<\infty\},

by Varadhan’s Theorem (1984, Theorem 2.3),

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\varepsilon\log E^{Q_{\varepsilon}}\left[\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}J_{\varepsilon}(\omega)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq-\inf_{\omega\in C_{0}}\left[J(\omega)+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}(\dot{\omega}_{u})^{2}\,du\right]

\displaystyle=-\inf_{\omega\in K_{0}}\left[J(\omega)+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}|\dot{\omega}_{u}|^{2}\,du\right].

By Theorem 6.1 in DeBlassie and Smits (2007), the infimum is
\gamma(p,\beta)
. This completes the proof.

Lemma 5.1

Since

a.e., it suffices to show for each

(5.3)

\int\left(H^{\prime}(b_{X}-b_{Y})\right)(a)\,da=\int H(a)\,d\mu(a).

To this end, let

be so given. Then denoting the

extensions of

,
b_{Y}
on
(M_{1},M_{2})
to
[M_{1},M_{2}]
by
\tilde{b}_{X}
,
\tilde{b}_{Y}
, respectively,

\displaystyle\int H^{\prime}(b_{X}-b_{Y})

\displaystyle=\int H^{\prime}(\tilde{b}_{X}-\tilde{b}_{Y})I_{(M_{1},M_{2})}

(since

on
(0,M_{1}]\cup[M_{2},\infty)
)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\int H^{\prime}(\tilde{b}_{X}-\tilde{b}_{Y})I_{(M_{1}+\varepsilon,M_{2}-\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\left[(H(\tilde{b}_{X}-\tilde{b}_{Y}))(M_{2}-\varepsilon)-(H(\tilde{b}_{X}-\tilde{b}_{Y}))(M_{1}+\varepsilon)-\int_{M_{1}+\varepsilon}^{M_{2}-\varepsilon}H(\tilde{b}_{X}^{\prime}-\tilde{b}_{Y}^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\left[(H(b_{X}-b_{Y}))(M_{2}-\varepsilon)-\left(H(b_{X}-b_{Y})\right)(M_{1}+\varepsilon)-\int_{M_{1}+\varepsilon}^{M_{2}-\varepsilon}H(b_{X}^{\prime}-b_{Y}^{\prime})\right]

(since

\displaystyle=H(M_{2})(b_{X}-b_{Y})(M_{2}-)-\int_{M_{1}}^{M_{2}}H(b_{X}^{\prime}-b_{Y}^{\prime})

(since

is continuous). This gives (

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
x>0
and let

(5.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}dX_{t}=dB_{t}+b_{X}(X_{t})\,dt,&\quad X_{0}=x\\
\\

and

and

and

(5.5)

\displaystyle\hskip-144.54pt\left.\left.-\tfrac{1}{2}(b_{X}-b_{Y})(M_{2}-)\,\ell_{t}^{M_{2}}(Y)\vphantom{\int_{0}^{t}}\right)I(\tau_{0}(Y)>t)\right],

The idea is similar to the one used by Pinsky (1995) to get the formula cited in Lemma

Let

It follows that

\displaystyle P_{x}(\tau_{\varepsilon,M}(X)>t)

\displaystyle=E_{x}\left[M_{t}I(\tau_{\varepsilon,M}(Y)>t)\right]

\displaystyle=E_{x}\left[\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}(b_{X}-b_{Y})(Y_{s})\,dB_{s}\right.\right.

(5.6)

\displaystyle\qquad\left.\left.-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(b_{X}-b_{Y})^{2}(Y_{s})\,ds\right)I(\tau_{\varepsilon,M}(Y)>t)\right].

Now we eliminate the stochastic integral in (
5
). By Lemma
5.1
, we can apply the Itô-Tanaka formula (Revuz and Yor (1991)) to get for

where

is the left derivative and

is the generalized second derivative of

from (
5.2
) in Lemma

. By our hypotheses on

and
b_{Y}
,

a.e. on
(0,\infty)
. Using this, the occupation times formula (Revuz and Yor (1991)) and Lemma

gives, for

\displaystyle g(Y_{t})

\displaystyle=g(x)+\int_{0}^{t}(b_{X}-b_{Y})(Y_{s})\left[dB_{s}+b_{Y}(Y_{s})\,ds\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\tfrac{1}{2}\left[(b_{X}-b_{Y})(M_{2}-)\,\ell^{M_{2}}_{t}(Y)-\int_{0}^{t}((b_{X}^{\prime}-b_{Y}^{\prime})I_{(M_{1},M_{2})})(Y_{s})\,ds\right]

Solving for the stochastic integral, we get, for

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}(b_{X}-b_{Y})(Y_{s})\,dB_{s}

\displaystyle=g(Y_{t})-g(x)-\int_{0}^{t}((b_{X}-b_{Y})b_{Y})(Y_{s})\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left((b^{\prime}_{X}-b^{\prime}_{Y})I_{(M_{1},M_{2})}\right)(Y_{s})\,ds-\tfrac{1}{2}(b_{X}-b_{Y})(M_{2}-)\,\ell_{t}^{M_{2}}(Y).

Substituting this into the exponential in (

\displaystyle P_{x}(\tau_{\varepsilon,M(X)}>t)

\displaystyle=E_{x}\left[\exp\left(g(Y_{t})-g(x)-\int_{0}^{t}((b_{X}-b_{Y})b_{Y})(Y_{s})\,ds\right.\right.\hskip 72.26999pt

\displaystyle\qquad+\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left((b^{\prime}_{X}-b^{\prime}_{Y})I_{(M_{1},M_{2})}\right)(Y_{s})\,ds-\tfrac{1}{2}(b_{X}-b_{Y})(M_{2}-)\,\ell_{t}^{M_{2}}(Y)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.\left.-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}(b_{X}-b_{Y})^{2}(Y_{s})\,ds\right)I(\tau_{\varepsilon,M}(Y)>t)\right]

\displaystyle=E_{x}\left[\exp\left(g(Y_{t})-g(x)-\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}H(Y_{s})\,ds\right.\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.\left.-\tfrac{1}{2}(b_{X}-b_{Y})(M_{2}-)\,\ell_{t}^{M_{2}}(Y)\vphantom{\int_{0}^{t}}\right)I(\tau_{\varepsilon,M}(Y)>t)\right].

By Monotone Convergence and Lemma
2.1
, upon letting

Proof of Theorem

We need the following bounds due to Potter. See Theorem 1.5.6 in Bingham, Goldie and Teugels (1989).

Theorem 7.1
.

(a) If
\ell
is slowly varying at infinity, then for any choice of
A>1
and
\delta>0
, there is
M>0
such that

\ell(y)/\ell(x)\leq A\max\left((y/x)^{\delta},(y/x)^{-\delta}\right),\quad x,y\geq M.

(b) If
\ell
is slowly varying at zero from the right, then for any choice of
A>1
and
\delta>0
, there is
M>0
such that

\ell(y)/\ell(x)\leq A\max\left((y/x)^{\delta},(y/x)^{-\delta}\right),\quad x,y\leq M.

The following is a consequence of the Representation Theorem for slowly varying functions (Bingham, Goldie and Teugels (1989), Theorem 1.3.1).

Theorem 7.2
.

(a) If
\ell
is slowly varying at infinity, then for each
\delta>0

\lim_{x\to\infty}x^{-\delta}\ell(x)=0\text{ and }\lim_{x\to\infty}x^{\delta}\ell(x)=\infty.

(b) If
\ell
is slowly varying from the right at
0
, then for each
\delta>0

\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}x^{\delta}\ell(x)=0\text{ and }\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}x^{-\delta}\ell(x)=\infty.

We will also need the following fact to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
\delta>0
be so small that

and

. By decreasing

and increasing

if necessary, we have

Proof.

By making

bigger if necessary, by Theorem

(a),

M_{2}^{-\delta}\ell_{2}(M_{2})\leq 1/2<2\leq M_{2}^{\delta}\ell_{2}(M_{2}).

Then by Theorem
