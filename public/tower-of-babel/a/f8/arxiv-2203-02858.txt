Two Channel Filter Banks on Arbitrary Graphs with Positive Semi Definite Variation Operators
Eduardo Pavez, Benjamin Girault, Antonio Ortega, Philip A. Chou

Abstract
We propose novel two-channel filter banks for signals on graphs. Our designs can be applied to arbitrary graphs, given a positive semi definite variation operator, while using arbitrary vertex partitions for downsampling. The proposed generalized filter banks (GFBs) also satisfy several desirable properties including perfect reconstruction and critical sampling, while having efficient implementations. Our results generalize previous approaches that were only valid for the normalized Laplacian of bipartite graphs. Our approach is based on novel graph Fourier transforms (GFTs) given by the generalized eigenvectors of the variation operator. These GFTs are orthogonal in an alternative inner product space which depends on the downsampling and variation operators. Our key theoretical contribution is showing that the spectral folding property of the normalized Laplacian of bipartite graphs, at the core of bipartite filter bank theory, can be generalized for the proposed GFT if the inner product matrix is chosen properly. In addition, we study vertex domain and spectral domain properties of GFBs and illustrate their probabilistic interpretation using Gaussian graphical models. While GFBs can be defined given any choice of a vertex partition for downsampling, we propose an algorithm to optimize these partitions with a criterion that favors balanced partitions with large graph cuts, which are shown to lead to efficient and stable GFB implementations. Our numerical experiments show that partition-optimized GFBs can be implemented efficiently on 3D point clouds with hundreds of thousands of points (nodes), while also improving the color signal representation quality over competing state-of-the-art approaches.

) inner product.

-orthogonality requirement is often an option if it is necessary to achieve some desirable properties. As an example JPEG2000 uses biorthogonal filters that are nearly

-orthogonal because they have finite support and are symmetric, which is desirable for imaging applications and would not be possible with orthogonal filters. Similarly, biorthogonal filter banks on bipartite graphs are preferred over

-orthogonal ones because they have polynomial implementations

Thus, in practice a non

-orthogonal solution can be useful as long as it is sufficient close to being

-orthogonal.
In this work,

Additionally, in

Further analysis of this question for more general scenarios is left for future work.

We discuss related filter banks and multiresolution representations (MRR) on graphs from three perspectives: graph topology, graph matrix, and downsampling sets.

Graph topology.

Several existing filter bank theories are applicable only to graphs with certain types of topology such as: bipartite

,
11
]

[

]

While some of these graph structures appear naturally in some applications
[

51

many cases graphs of interest do not belong to any of these categories.
To apply BFBs to arbitrary graphs one can decompose any graph as a sum of bipartite graphs and apply the filter bank in a separable manner
[
52
]
. Other approaches include bipartite approximation
[
23
]
, bipartite graph learning methods
[
24
,
25
,
26
,
53
,
27
]
, graph oversampling
[
43
]
, and vertex partition optimization
[
54
]
.
Earlier filter bank designs for arbitrary graphs are difficult to invert (e.g., least squares reconstruction is needed)
[
55
,
56
,
57
]
.
More recent MRRs fail to be simultaneously perfect reconstruction and orthogonal
[
44
]
, while others, require full eigendecomposition
[
45
,
58
]
.
Existing approaches that are valid for arbitrary graphs have several disadvantages. On the one hand, approximation-based methods may reduce signal representation quality while also requiring additional computational resources (to select the best bipartite approximation)
[
23
,
52
,
24
,
25
]
.
On the other hand, methods that allow the original graph to be used can do so at
the expense of other desirable features, such as low complexity
[

,
58
]
, perfect reconstruction or orthogonality (in
{\bf I}
or other
{\bf Q}
inner product)
[
54
,
44
]
(see
Table I
for a comparison of some of these approaches). Therefore,
a theoretical formulation leading to critically sampled filter banks for arbitrary graphs, without the aforementioned disadvantages, can be an attractive alternative.

Graph matrix.

The choice of graph matrix, such as the graph Laplacian or the adjacency matrix, whose non-zero pattern encodes the graph structure, is an important design decision
[
59
,
60
]
. Existing filter bank frameworks are built for specific types of graph matrices because of their special algebraic or spectral properties
[
10
,
41
]
, but these choices may not be suitable for a particular application. As an example, BFBs use the normalized Laplacian
[
10
]
, while the random walk Laplacian has been shown to achieve better coding performance
[
28
]
. More recent filter bank approaches
[
46
,
47
]
can be applied to the larger class of positive semi-definite variation operators. However, they lack perfect reconstruction
[
46
]
or require computing a full eigendecomposition of the graph operator
[
47
]
, which significantly limits their application to large graphs.
The proposed GFBs are valid for any positive semi definite graph matrix (i.e., a variation operator), including commonly used graph Laplacians, without compromising on other properties.

Downsampling.

For discrete-time signals, a downsampling by
2
operator keeps “every other sample” and discards the rest. In graphs, there is no obvious notion of “every other vertex” unless the graph is bipartite
[
52
]
. For graph filter banks, vertex partitions of the node set can be chosen under various criteria
[
46
,
54
,
9
]
.
Recently,
[
47
,
61
]
used spectral domain sampling, and while this approach leads to an attractive theory, spectral sampling requires computing all eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the graph matrix, which can have significant computation complexity.

[
46
]
extended sampling theory of graph signals to filter banks but practical implementations of this framework cannot achieve perfect reconstruction. In this work, we show that any partition of the vertex set is a valid downsampling operator. We also propose strategies for optimally choosing these partitions in Section
VI
.

1
Graph signal variation

The graph is equipped with a symmetric positive semi definite variation matrix
{\bf M}=(m_{ij})
, with sparsity pattern determined by the edge set, that is,
m_{ij}=m_{ji}=0
when
(i,j)\notin{\cal E}
and
i\neq j
.
Throughout the paper we will assume that the graph is connected
2
2

2

If the graph is disconnected, each connected component can be treated independently.
and thus
{\bf M}
is an irreducible matrix
[
62
,
63
]
.
The graph signal variation is

\Delta({\bf x})={\bf x}^{\top}{\bf M}{\bf x}.

(1)

Intuitively, given two signals of equal energy, the one with larger variation is the one with higher energy in the higher frequencies of the spectrum.
Popular choices of variation operator are the normalized, combinatorial and generalized Laplacian matrices
[

]
, which we define next.

The adjacency matrix is a non negative symmetric matrix
{\bf W}=(w_{ij})
, where
w_{ij}=0
, whenever
(i,j)\notin{\cal E}
. The degree of node
i
is
d_{i}=\sum_{j\in{\cal V}}w_{ij}
, and the degree matrix is
{\bf D}={\hbox{diag}}(d_{1},\cdots,d_{n})
.
The
combinatorial graph Laplacian
(CGL) is
{\bf L}={\bf D}-{\bf W}
, while the
normalized graph Laplacian
(NGL) is
{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}={\bf D}^{-1/2}{\bf L}{\bf D}^{-1/2}={\bf I}-{\bf D}^{-1/2}{\bf W}{\bf D}^{-1/2}
. A
generalized graph Laplacian
(GGL) is any positive semidefinite matrix with non positive off diagonal entries, which includes the normalized and combinatorial graph Laplacian matrices
[
63
,
64
,
65
,
66
]
. Other variation operators are listed in
[
29
,
67
]
.

2
The
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT
[
29
]

Given a positive definite matrix
{\bf Q}
, we define the
{\bf Q}
inner product between
{\bf x}
and
{\bf y}
, as

\langle{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle_{{\bf Q}}={\bf y}^{\top}{\bf Q}{\bf x},

(2)

with induced
{\bf Q}
-norm
\|{\bf x}\|_{{\bf Q}}=\sqrt{\langle{\bf x},{\bf x}\rangle_{{\bf Q}}}
.
We say
\{{\bf u}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
is a
{\bf Q}
-orthonormal set if it satisfies

\langle{\bf u}_{i},{\bf u}_{j}\rangle_{{\bf Q}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\mbox{if }i=j\\
0&\mbox{if }i\neq j.\end{array}\right.

(3)

In matrix form this corresponds to
{\bf U}^{\top}{\bf Q}{\bf U}={\bf I}
, where
{\bf U}=[{\bf u}_{1},\cdots,{\bf u}_{n}]
, which also implies that
{\bf U}^{-1}={\bf U}^{\top}{\bf Q}
.
Following
[
29
]
, the
({\bf M},{\bf Q})

Graph Fourier Transform
(
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT)
is defined as the
{\bf Q}
-orthonormal set that minimizes the graph signal variation, that is

{\bf u}_{1}=\argmin_{{\bf u}:{\bf u}\neq\mathbf{0},~\|{\bf u}\|_{{\bf Q}}=1}{\bf u}^{\top}{\bf M}{\bf u},

(4)

and for any
2\leq k\leq n

{\bf u}_{k}=\argmin_{{\bf u}:{\bf u}\neq\mathbf{0},~\|{\bf u}\|_{{\bf Q}}=1}{\bf u}^{\top}{\bf M}{\bf u}\textnormal{ s.t. }\langle{\bf u},{\bf u}_{i}\rangle_{{\bf Q}}=0,\forall i<k.

(5)

Note that this definition of the
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT basis is consistent with the traditional definition of GFT (i.e., the
({\bf M},{\bf I})
-GFT). In particular, when
{\bf M}={\bf L}
, the variation of
{\bf x}
is
{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf L}{\bf x}=\sum_{(i,j)\in{\cal E}}w_{ij}(x_{i}-x_{j})^{2}
, and as in that case, the generalized eigenvectors
{\bf u}_{k}
have increasing variation (quantified with the same operator
{\bf M}
) for larger
k
. The only difference is that now the basis vectors are
{\bf Q}
-orthonormal instead of
{\bf I}
-orthonormal.
The
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT is also the solution to the generalized eigendecomposition problem
[
62
,
29
]
:

{\bf M}{\bf u}=\lambda{\bf Q}{\bf u}.

(6)

The matrix of unit
{\bf Q}
-norm generalized eigenvectors is denoted by
{\bf U}=[{\bf u}_{1},\cdots,{\bf u}_{n}]
, with generalized eigenvalues (or graph frequencies) forming a set
\sigma({\bf M},{\bf Q})=\{\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n}\}
where
\lambda_{k}={\bf u}_{k}^{\top}{\bf M}{\bf u}_{k}
and

\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
.
The generalized eigenvalue matrix is
\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}={\hbox{diag}}(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})
.
The
fundamental matrix
is
{\bf Z}={\bf Q}^{-1}{\bf M}
, which is diagonalized by
{\bf U}
, so

{\bf Z}={\bf Q}^{-1}{\bf M}={\bf U}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}{\bf U}^{-1}={\bf U}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}{\bf U}^{\top}{\bf Q}.

(7)

Then we can factorize the variation operator
{\bf M}
as follows:

{\bf M}={\bf Q}{\bf U}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}{\bf U}^{\top}{\bf Q}.

(8)

A graph signal
{\bf x}
has a
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT representation given by:

{\bf x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle{\bf x},{\bf u}_{i}\rangle_{{\bf Q}}{\bf u}_{i}={\bf U}\hat{{\bf x}},

(9)

where
\hat{{\bf x}}={\bf U}^{\top}{\bf Q}{\bf x}
is the
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT of
{\bf x}

[
29
]
.
Thus, (
9
) is the inverse
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT, where
{\bf x}
is reconstructed as a linear combination of the frequency components in
\hat{{\bf x}}
.
Note that the inverse transform is given by
{\bf x}={\bf U}\hat{{\bf x}}
, since
{\bf U}{\bf U}^{\top}{\bf Q}={\bf I}
.

3
Spectral graph filters

Given a function (i.e., a filter kernel)
h:\sigma({\bf M},{\bf Q})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, then a
spectral graph filter
(SGF) is any matrix
{\bf H}
of the form

{\bf H}={\bf U}h({\bf\Lambda}){\bf U}^{\top}{\bf Q}=h({\bf Z}),

(10)

where
h(\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$})={\hbox{diag}}(h(\lambda_{1}),h(\lambda_{2}),\cdots,h(\lambda_{n}))
. When
h(\lambda)=\lambda
we have that

{\bf H}={\bf Z}={\bf Q}^{-1}{\bf M}
and we recover the fundamental matrix. A spectral graph filter is implemented by first applying the
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT to a signal
{\bf x}
, then multiplying each transformed coefficient
\hat{x}_{i}=\langle{\bf x},{\bf u}_{i}\rangle_{{\bf Q}}
by the filter coefficient
h(\lambda_{i})
. The resulting signal is transformed back to the vertex domain with the inverse
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT.
Filtering
{\bf x}
using
h
produces the graph signal
{\bf H}{\bf x}
, since

{\bf H}{\bf x}={\bf U}h(\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$})\hat{{\bf x}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(\lambda_{i})\hat{x}_{i}{\bf u}_{i}.

(11)

When
h
is a polynomial
h(\lambda)=a_{0}+a_{1}\lambda+\cdots+a_{d}\lambda^{d}
for
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the resulting SGF is a polynomial of
{\bf Z}
, thus
{\bf H}=h({\bf Z})=a_{0}{\bf I}+a_{1}{\bf Z}+\cdots+a_{d}{\bf Z}^{d}
,
can be implemented efficiently without eigendecomposition using matrix vector products.
Thus, if
{\bf Z}
is sparse and
d
is
relatively small,
graph filtering with polynomial SGFs
can be applied efficiently on large graphs. Complexity of polynomial graph filters and filter banks is reviewed in more detail in
Section VI
.

III-C

Vertex and frequency domain Hilbert spaces

Both a graph signal,
{\bf x}
, and its
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT,
\hat{{\bf x}}
, can be represented by vectors in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
However, in our formulation they belong to different Hilbert spaces, namely,
({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\bf Q})
for
{\bf x}
and
({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\bf I}})
for
\hat{{\bf x}}
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\bf I}}
is the traditional dot product.
Thus, we can view the
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT,
{\bf U}^{\top}{\bf Q}
, as a mapping from
({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\bf Q})
to
({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\bf I}})
, while the inverse
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT
{\bf U}
is a mapping from
({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\bf I}})
to
({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\bf Q}})
.
Making explicit the Hilbert spaces involved in this mapping allows us to state the following property.

Theorem 1

(Parseval
[
29
]
)
.

Let
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, and let
\hat{{\bf x}}
and
\hat{{\bf y}}
be their respective frequency domain representations, then

\langle{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle_{{\bf Q}}=\langle\hat{{\bf x}},\hat{{\bf y}}\rangle_{{\bf I}}.

(12)

From (
10
) it is clear that SGFs are functions from
({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{{\bf Q}})
to the same Hilbert space.
For the rest of the paper we will use the
{\bf Q}
inner product for graph signals, and the
{\bf I}
inner product for their frequency domain representations.

IV
Two-channel filter banks on graphs

In this section we introduce the two-channel filter bank theory for arbitrary graphs. In
Section IV-A
we formulate the
problem.
Section IV-B
reviews the solution for bipartite graphs from
[
10
]
. We illustrate how to go from bipartite to arbitrary graphs through an example in
Section IV-C
. Our main theoretical result, the generalized spectral folding property is presented in
Section IV-D
. Generalized filter banks are constructed in
Section IV-F
and
Section IV-G
.

IV-A

Problem formulation

A two-channel filter bank (
Figure 1
) is composed of downsampling and upsampling operators on the sets
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
, and two sets of
|{\cal V}|\times|{\cal V}|
matrices, corresponding to the analysis flters (
{\bf H}_{0}
,
{\bf H}_{1}
) and the synthesis filters (
{\bf G}_{0}
,
{\bf G}_{1}
).
Throughout the paper, we will consider critically sampled filter banks.

Definition 1
.

A two-channel filter bank is
critically sampled
if the downsampling sets
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
form a partition of
{\cal V}
.

Without loss of generality we assume that
{\cal A}=\{1,\cdots,|{\cal A}|\}
, and
{\cal B}={\cal V}\setminus{\cal A}
. Downsampling
{\bf x}
on
{\cal A}
keeps the entries
\{x_{i}:i\in{\cal A}\}
and discards the rest, resulting in

{\bf x}_{\cal A}={\bf S}_{\cal A}{\bf x},

(13)

where
{\bf S}_{\cal A}=[{\bf I}_{\cal A},\mathbf{0}]
is a
|{\cal A}|\times|{\cal V}|
selection matrix.
The upsampling operator
{\bf S}_{\cal A}^{\top}
maps the signal back to
{\cal V}
by filling the entries on
{\cal B}
with zeroes. Downsampling followed by upsampling corresponds to

{\bf S}_{\cal A}^{\top}{\bf S}_{\cal A}^{\phantom{\top}}{\bf x}={\bf S}_{\cal A}^{\top}{\bf x}_{{\cal A}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf x}_{\cal A}^{\top}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

(14)

The analysis operator
{\bf T}_{a}
(i.e., filtering followed by downsampling) from
Figure 1
can be written as

{\bf T}_{a}={\bf S}_{\cal A}^{\top}{\bf S}_{\cal A}^{\phantom{\top}}{\bf H}_{0}+{\bf S}_{\cal B}^{\top}{\bf S}_{\cal B}^{\phantom{\top}}{\bf H}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf S}_{\cal A}{\bf H}_{0}\\
{\bf S}_{\cal B}{\bf H}_{1}\end{bmatrix}.

(15)

The outputs of the low pass and high pass channels, approximation,
{\bf a}
, and detail,
{\bf d}
, coefficients, respectively, are

{\bf T}_{a}{\bf x}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf a}^{\top}&{\bf d}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}=\begin{bmatrix}({\bf S}_{\cal A}{\bf H}_{0}{\bf x})^{\top}&({\bf S}_{\cal B}{\bf H}_{1}{\bf x})^{\top}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}.

(16)

The synthesis operator can be expressed as

{\bf T}_{s}={\bf G}_{0}{\bf S}_{\cal A}^{\top}{\bf S}_{\cal A}^{\phantom{\top}}+{\bf G}_{1}{\bf S}_{\cal B}^{\top}{\bf S}_{\cal B}^{\phantom{\top}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf G}_{0}{\bf S}_{\cal A}^{\top}&{\bf G}_{1}{\bf S}_{\cal B}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}.

(17)

Both the analysis and synthesis operators map signals from
({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\bf Q})
to
({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\bf Q})
(see also
Section III-C
), thus
{\bf x},{\bf T}_{a}{\bf x}
, and
{\bf T}_{s}{\bf T}_{a}{\bf x}
are vertex domain signals.
We are interested in perfect reconstruction two-channel filter banks.

Definition 2
.

A two-channel filter bank is
perfect reconstruction
(PR) if
{\bf T}_{s}{\bf T}_{a}{\bf x}={\bf x}
, for all
{\bf x}
.

Since we only consider critically sampled filter banks, the PR condition is equivalent to
{\bf T}_{s}={\bf T}_{a}^{-1}
, thus a necessary and sufficient condition for perfect reconstruction is given by

{\bf T}_{s}{\bf T}_{a}={\bf G}_{0}{\bf S}_{\cal A}^{\top}{\bf S}_{\cal A}{\bf H}_{0}+{\bf G}_{1}{\bf S}_{\cal B}^{\top}{\bf S}_{\cal B}{\bf H}_{1}={\bf I}.

(18)

We also introduce
{\bf Q}
-orthogonal filter banks.

Definition 3
.

A two-channel filter bank is
{\bf Q}
-
orthogonal
, if for every pair of graph signals
{\bf x},{\bf y}
,

\langle{\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle_{{\bf Q}}=\langle{\bf T}_{a}{\bf x},{\bf T}_{a}{\bf y}\rangle_{{\bf Q}}.

(19)

In matrix form, (
19
) is equivalent to
{\bf T}_{a}^{\top}{\bf Q}{\bf T}_{a}={\bf Q}
.

The traditional notion of orthogonal filter banks corresponds to the case
{\bf Q}={\bf I}
.
Critically sampled
{\bf Q}
-orthogonal filter banks are always perfect reconstruction with synthesis operator

{\bf T}_{s}={\bf Q}^{-1}{\bf T}_{a}^{\top}{\bf Q}.

(20)

It is easy to verify that (
20
) also obeys
{\bf T}_{s}^{\top}{\bf Q}{\bf T}_{s}={\bf Q}
.

Remark 1
.

Because the vertex domain and frequency domain Hilbert spaces are different (see
Section III-C
),
{\bf Q}
-orthogonality for filter banks is different than
{\bf Q}
-orthogonality of the
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT. This is because the
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT maps graph signals from their vertex domain representation to their frequency domain representation, that is,
{\bf U}^{\top}{\bf Q}:~({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\bf Q})\rightarrow({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\bf I})
, which results in the relationship of

Theorem 1
(see
[
29
]
).
In contrast, the analysis and synthesis operators of a PR critically sampled filter bank stay in the same Hilbert space
({\mathbb{R}}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\bf Q})
.

We seek two-channel filter banks that can provide good signal representations,
while having efficient implementations on large (arbitrary) graphs. In practice, this can be achieved by designing SGFs
{\bf H}_{i},{\bf G}_{i}
that: (i) have good frequency selectivity and
(ii) can be written as polynomials of
{\bf Z}
. We review bipartite graph solutions
[
10
,
11
]
before introducing our proposed GFBs for arbitrary graphs.

IV-B

Bipartite filter banks
[
10
,
11
]

The necessary and sufficient filter bank design conditions of
[
10
,
11
]

apply to
bipartite graphs.

Definition 4

(Bipartite graph
[
39
]
)
.

A graph
{\cal G}=({\cal V},{\cal E})
is bipartite on a partition of the node set
{\cal A},{\cal B}
, if
for all
(i,j)\in{\cal E}
,
i\in{\cal A}
and
j\in{\cal B}
, or
i\in{\cal B}
and
j\in{\cal A}
.

Some examples of bipartite graphs include
Figure 2
(middle graph),
5(a)
and
7(b)
.
In bipartite filter banks (BFB) the sets
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
are used for downsampling. In a bipartite graph only edges between
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}
exist, so we have:

{\bf L}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf D}_{\cal A}&-{\bf W}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}\\
-{\bf W}_{{\cal B}{\cal A}}&{\bf D}_{\cal B}\end{bmatrix},~{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf I}_{\cal A}&-\tilde{{\bf W}}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}\\
-\tilde{{\bf W}}_{{\cal B}{\cal A}}&{\bf I}_{\cal B}\end{bmatrix}.

(21)

BFBs are implemented using spectral graph filters of the
({\bm{\mathcal{L}}},{\bf I})
-GFT,
that is
{\bf Z}={\bm{\mathcal{L}}}
, and

{\bf H}_{i}=h_{i}({\bm{\mathcal{L}}}),\quad{\bf G}_{i}=g_{i}({\bm{\mathcal{L}}}),\quad i\in\{0,1\}.

(22)

The diagonal matrix
{\bf J}={\bf S}^{\top}_{{\cal A}}{\bf S}_{{\cal A}}^{\phantom{\top}}-{\bf S}^{\top}_{{\cal B}}{\bf S}_{{\cal B}}^{\phantom{\top}}
,
with entries

{\bf J}_{i,i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\mbox{if }i\in{\cal A}\\
-1&\mbox{if }i\in{\cal B},\end{array}\right.

(23)

is used to establish the following property of the normalized Laplacian of bipartite graphs.

Proposition 1

(Spectral folding
[
39
]
)
.

Let
{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}
be the normalized Laplacian of a bipartite graph, we have that

{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}{\bf u}=\lambda{\bf u}
if and only if
{\bf J}{\bf u}
is also an eigenvector with eigenvalue
2-\lambda
.

Thus, eigenvalues come in pairs (
\lambda
,
2-\lambda
) mirrored around the middle frequency
\lambda=1
. For a given
\lambda
, the eigenvector
{\bf u}
and its folded version
{\bf J}{\bf u}
have the same values for entries in
{\cal A}
, while signs are changed for the entries in
{\cal B}
.

Proposition 1
is the key property used by
[
10
,
11
]
to design perfect reconstruction and
{\bf I}
-orthogonal BFBs.

Theorem 2

(Perfect Reconstruction
[
10
]
)
.

A two-channel filter bank on a bipartite graph with spectral graph filters given by (
22
) is PR if and only if,
\forall\lambda\in\sigma({\bm{\mathcal{L}}},{\bf I})

\displaystyle g_{0}(\lambda)h_{0}(\lambda)+g_{1}(\lambda)h_{1}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=2,

(24)

\displaystyle h_{1}(\lambda)g_{1}(2-\lambda)-h_{0}(\lambda)g_{0}(2-\lambda)

\displaystyle=0.

(25)

The proof follows from using
{\bf S}_{\cal A}^{\top}{\bf S}_{\cal A}^{\phantom{\top}}=\frac{1}{2}({\bf I}+{\bf J})
and
{\bf S}_{\cal B}^{\top}{\bf S}_{\cal B}^{\phantom{\top}}=\frac{1}{2}({\bf I}-{\bf J})
in the PR condition (
18
), in combination with
Proposition 1
(see
[
10
]
).

Proposition 1
can also be used to design
{\bf I}
-orthogonal filter banks.

Theorem 3

(Orthogonality
[
10
]
)
.

Under the same conditions of
Theorem 2
, a filter bank is
{\bf I}
-orthogonal if and only if,

\forall\lambda\in\sigma({\bm{\mathcal{L}}},{\bf I})

\displaystyle h_{0}^{2}(\lambda)+h_{1}^{2}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=2,

(26)

\displaystyle h_{1}(\lambda)h_{1}(2-\lambda)-h_{0}(\lambda)h_{0}(2-\lambda)

\displaystyle=0.

(27)

The main advantage of
{\bf I}
-orthogonal over non
{\bf I}
-orthogonal PR filter banks is the energy preservation property (
Definition 3
), essential for compression and de-noising applications. Because no polynomial solutions to (
26
) and (
27
) exist
[
11
]
,
{\bf I}
-orthogonal filter banks cannot be implemented with polynomial graph filters.
Instead, polynomial biorthogonal filter banks with near
{\bf I}
-orthogonality can be designed
[
11
]
.

Proposition 2
.

[
11
,
13
]

Biorthogonal filters defined by

\displaystyle h_{0}(\lambda)=g_{1}(2-\lambda),\quad h_{1}(\lambda)=g_{0}(2-\lambda),

(28)

\displaystyle h_{0}(\lambda)h_{1}(2-\lambda)+h_{0}(2-\lambda)h_{1}(\lambda)=2

(29)

are perfect reconstruction.

In what follows we will show that the PR,
{\bf I}
-orthogonality and biorthogonal conditions are not limited to bipartite graphs and the normalized Laplacian. The key insight is a generalization of
Proposition 1
.

IV-C

From bipartite to arbitrary graphs: Lazy filter bank

{\bf x}
{\bf C}
\downarrow\mkern-6.0mu{\cal B}
\downarrow\mkern-6.0mu{\cal A}
\uparrow\mkern-6.0mu{\cal B}
\uparrow\mkern-6.0mu{\cal A}
2{\bf I}-{\bf C}
+
\hat{{\bf x}}
{\bf d}
{\bf a}

Fig. 3
:
Lazy two-channel filter bank

Before stating our main results, we show how our extension from bipartite to arbitrary graphs would work for the “lazy” filter bank of
Figure 3
, where
{\bf C}
is given by

{\bf C}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf I}_{{\cal A}}&{\bf C}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}\\
{\bf C}_{{\cal B}{\cal A}}&{\bf I}_{{\cal B}}\end{bmatrix}.

(30)

{\bf C}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}
and
{\bf C}_{{\cal B}{\cal A}}
are arbitrary rectangular matrices of dimensions
|{\cal A}|\times|{\cal B}|
and
|{\cal B}|\times|{\cal A}|
, respectively.
It is easy to verify that the lazy filter bank is
perfect reconstruction
when
{\bf C}
is given by (
30
).
Since
{\bf d}={\bf S}_{{\cal B}}{\bf C}{\bf x}
and
{\bf a}={\bf S}_{{\cal A}}{\bf x}
, we have that

\displaystyle\hat{{\bf x}}

\displaystyle={\bf S}_{{\cal B}}^{\top}{\bf d}+(2{\bf I}-{\bf C}){\bf S}_{{\cal A}}^{\top}{\bf a}=({\bf S}_{{\cal B}}^{\top}{\bf S}_{{\cal B}}{\bf C}+(2{\bf I}-{\bf C}){\bf S}_{{\cal A}}^{\top}{\bf S}_{{\cal A}}){\bf x}

\displaystyle=\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}\\
{\bf C}_{{\cal B}{\cal A}}&{\bf I}_{{\cal B}}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}{\bf I}_{{\cal A}}&\mathbf{0}\\
-{\bf C}_{{\cal B}{\cal A}}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}\right){\bf x}={\bf x}.

(31)

If we take
{\bf C}={\bm{\mathcal{L}}}
for a bipartite graph, the lazy filter bank is a special case of a
perfect reconstruction
BFB (
Figure 1
) with filters
{\bf H}_{0}={\bf I}
,
{\bf H}_{1}={\bm{\mathcal{L}}}
,
{\bf G}_{0}=2{\bf I}-{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}
, and
{\bf H}_{1}={\bf I}
,
corresponding to
SGFs of the
({\bm{\mathcal{L}}},{\bf I})
-GFT with
biorthogonal
filter kernels
h_{0}(\lambda)=g_{1}(\lambda)=1
,
h_{1}(\lambda)=\lambda
, and
g_{0}(\lambda)=h_{1}(2-\lambda)
.

In the following subsections we will show that it is also possible to choose
{\bf C}
as a function of the variation operator
{\bf M}
of a non-bipartite graph,
which allows us to generalize the theorems from
Section IV-B
to arbitrary graphs.

IV-D

Spectral folding on arbitrary graphs

We generalize
Proposition 1
to other graphs and variation operators by using the
{\bf Q}
inner product. Using this result, we propose a new
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT and show that it can be used to obtain
Generalized Filter Banks
on arbitrary graphs.
First we define the spectral folding property for a
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT.

Definition 5

(Spectral folding)
.

Given a graph
{\cal G}
with variation operator
{\bf M}\succeq 0
, inner product
{\bf Q}\succ 0
and a partition
{\cal A}
,
{\cal B}
. The
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT has the
spectral folding property
,
if for all generalized eigenpairs
({\bf u},\lambda)
then
({\bf J}{\bf u},(2-\lambda))
is also a generalized eigenpair, that is:

{\bf M}{\bf u}=\lambda{\bf Q}{\bf u}\Leftrightarrow{\bf M}{\bf J}{\bf u}=(2-\lambda){\bf Q}{\bf J}{\bf u},

(32)

where
{\bf J}={\bf S}_{\cal A}^{\top}{\bf S}_{\cal A}^{\phantom{\top}}-{\bf S}_{\cal B}^{\top}{\bf S}_{\cal B}^{\phantom{\top}}
, as in (
23
).

The following theorem completely characterizes the spectral folding property.
The proof can be found in
Section IV-E
.

Theorem 4

(Spectral folding)
.

Given a graph
{\cal G}
with variation operator
{\bf M}\succeq 0
, inner product
{\bf Q}
and a partition
{\cal A}=\{1,\cdots,|{\cal A}|\}
,
{\cal B}={\cal V}\setminus{\cal A}
, for which
{\bf M}_{{\cal A}{\cal A}}
and
{\bf M}_{{\cal B}{\cal B}}
are invertible. The
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT has the spectral folding property if and only if
{\bf Q}
is chosen as

{\bf Q}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf M}_{{\cal A}{\cal A}}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&{\bf M}_{{\cal B}{\cal B}}\end{bmatrix}.

(33)

The condition that
{\bf M}_{{\cal A}{\cal A}}
and
{\bf M}_{{\cal B}{\cal B}}
are non singular is satisfied for any vertex partition if
{\bf M}\succ 0
. This condition also holds if
{\bf M}
is the combinatorial or normalized Laplacian of a connected graph.

Theorem 4
generalizes
Proposition 1
, demonstrating that the spectral folding property is not unique to the
({\bm{\mathcal{L}}},{\bf I})
-GFT of bipartite graphs, and in fact, it is always satisfied if the inner product matrix
{\bf Q}
is chosen as in (
33
).

Using (
33
), the fundamental matrix
{\bf Z}={\bf U}\hbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}{\bf U}^{\top}{\bf Q}
is

{\bf Z}={\bf Q}^{-1}{\bf M}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf I}_{{\cal A}}&{\bf M}_{{\cal A}{\cal A}}^{-1}{\bf M}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}\\
{\bf M}_{{\cal B}{\cal B}}^{-1}{\bf M}_{{\cal B}{\cal A}}&{\bf I}_{{\cal B}}\end{bmatrix},

(34)

so that
{\bf Z}
has the same structure as
{\bf C}
in (
30
) and thus
{\bf Z}
can be used to construct “lazy” filter banks (see
Figure 3
). In
Section IV-F
we apply
Theorem 4
with the fundamental matrix
{\bf Z}
in (
34
) to design GFBs on arbitrary graphs.
To gain some intuition about
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFTs with spectral folding,
we study some of their properties from vertex, spectral and probabilistic perspectives (
Section V
) and propose criteria to choose vertex partitions with favorable properties (
Section VI
).

IV-E

Proof of Theorem
4
(Spectral folding)

First we prove that (
33
) implies the spectral folding property (
Definition 5
).
Since
{\bf M}
and
{\bf Q}
are positive semidefinite, and
{\bf Q}
is non singular, there is a full set of generalized eigenvectors
[
62
]
. Let
{\bf u}=[{\bf u}_{\cal A}^{\top},{\bf u}_{\cal B}^{\top}]^{\top}
and
{\bf M}{\bf u}=\lambda{\bf Q}{\bf u}
, then

\displaystyle{\bf M}_{{\cal A}{\cal A}}{\bf u}_{\cal A}+{\bf M}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}{\bf u}_{\cal B}

\displaystyle=\lambda{\bf Q}_{\cal A}{\bf u}_{\cal A},

(35)

\displaystyle{\bf M}_{{\cal B}{\cal A}}{\bf u}_{\cal A}+{\bf M}_{{\cal B}{\cal B}}{\bf u}_{\cal B}

\displaystyle=\lambda{\bf Q}_{\cal B}{\bf u}_{\cal B}.

(36)

Set
\mathbf{v}={\bf J}{\bf u}=[{\bf u}_{\cal A}^{\top},-{\bf u}_{\cal B}^{\top}]^{\top}
, and using (
35
) and (
36
) we get

\displaystyle{\bf M}{\bf v}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf M}_{{\cal A}{\cal A}}{\bf u}_{\cal A}-{\bf M}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}{\bf u}_{\cal B}\\
{\bf M}_{{\cal B}{\cal A}}{\bf u}_{\cal A}-{\bf M}_{{\cal B}{\cal B}}{\bf u}_{\cal B}\end{bmatrix}

(37)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}2{\bf M}_{{\cal A}{\cal A}}{\bf u}_{\cal A}-\lambda{\bf Q}_{{\cal A}}{\bf u}_{\cal A}\\
\lambda{\bf Q}_{\cal B}{\bf u}_{\cal B}-2{\bf M}_{{\cal B}{\cal B}}{\bf u}_{\cal B}\end{bmatrix}=(2-\lambda){\bf Q}{\bf v}.

(38)

The second equality is implied by
{\bf M}_{{\cal A}{\cal A}}={\bf Q}_{\cal A}
, and
{\bf M}_{{\cal B}{\cal B}}={\bf Q}_{\cal B}
. To prove the other direction of the equivalence in
Definition 5
we set
\gamma=2-\lambda
and repeat the same steps.

Now, we prove that spectral folding implies
(33)
.
Let

{\bf Q}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf Q}_{{\cal A}}&{\bf Q}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}\\
{\bf Q}_{{\cal B}{\cal A}}&{\bf Q}_{{\cal B}}\end{bmatrix}.

(39)

We will first show that
\mathbf{Q}
is block diagonal.
Using the spectral folding assumption and given two generalized eigenvectors
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
with unit
{\bf Q}
-norm, we have that
{\bf J}{\bf u}
and
{\bf J}{\bf v}
are also generalized eigenvectors of unit
{\bf Q}
-norm. In addition,
{\bf J}{\bf u}
is
{\bf Q}
-orthogonal to
{\bf J}{\bf v}
, and
{\bf u}
is
{\bf Q}
-orthogonal to
{\bf v}
.
Therefore, given the matrix of generalized eigenvectors
{\bf U}
,

{\bf U}^{\top}\left({\bf Q}+{\bf J}{\bf Q}{\bf J}\right){\bf U}=2{\bf I}\text{.}

(40)

We replace the right hand side of (
40
) with
2{\bf U}^{\top}{\bf Q}{\bf U}=2{\bf I}
. After simplification we obtain
{\bf J}{\bf Q}{\bf J}={\bf Q}
, meaning that
{\bf Q}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}=\mathbf{0}
, and
{\bf Q}_{{\cal B}{\cal A}}=\mathbf{0}
.

Next, we prove that
{\bf Q}_{\cal A}={\bf M}_{{\cal A}{\cal A}}
and
{\bf Q}_{\cal B}={\bf M}_{{\cal B}{\cal B}}
.
Using the spectral folding property, and given
{\bf u}
, a generalized eigenvector with eigenvalue
\lambda
, we obtain:

\displaystyle{\bf M}({\bf I}+{\bf J}){\bf u}

\displaystyle=(\lambda{\bf Q}+(2-\lambda){\bf Q}{\bf J}){\bf u}

(41)

\displaystyle{\bf M}({\bf I}-{\bf J}){\bf u}

\displaystyle=(\lambda{\bf Q}-(2-\lambda){\bf Q}{\bf J}){\bf u}.

(42)

(
41
) and (
42
) imply

2{\bf M}_{{\cal A}{\cal A}}{\bf u}_{\cal A}=2{\bf Q}_{\cal A}{\bf u}_{\cal A}
and
2{\bf M}_{{\cal B}{\cal B}}{\bf u}_{\cal B}=2{\bf Q}_{\cal B}{\bf u}_{\cal B}
, respectively.
Gathering all these equations for all eigenvectors into matrix form, we obtain
\begin{bmatrix}{\bf M}_{{\cal A}{\cal A}}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&{\bf M}_{{\cal B}{\cal B}}\end{bmatrix}{\bf U}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf Q}_{{\cal A}}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&{\bf Q}_{{\cal B}}\end{bmatrix}{\bf U}
.
Since
{\bf U}
is invertible, we obtain that
{\bf M}_{{\cal A}{\cal A}}={\bf Q}_{\cal A}
and
{\bf M}_{{\cal B}{\cal B}}={\bf Q}_{\cal B}
.

IV-F

Generalized filter banks on arbitrary graphs

We use the spectral folding property (
Definition 5
) and
Theorem 4
to construct perfect reconstruction (
Theorem 5
) and

V
Properties, interpretation and examples

The term
graph Fourier transform
(GFT) associated to the eigenvectors of a graph operator is often justified by the fact that the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) diagonalizes the adjacency matrix of circulant graphs.
More generally, the frequency interpretation of the
({\bf M},{\bf I})
-GFT is justified from its variational definition, i.e., (
4
) and (
5
).
In this section we motivate the use of the proposed
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFTs for graph signal representation, provide insights about its frequency interpretation, and through examples help further understand
the role of
{\bf Q}
.

V-A

Spectral properties of the
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT

V-A
1
Eigenvalue bounds

For any graph with normalized Laplacian
{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}={\bf D}^{-1/2}{\bf L}{\bf D}^{-1/2}
, its eigenvalues belong to the
[0,2]
interval, moreover
\sigma({\bf L},{\bf D})=\sigma({\bm{\mathcal{L}}},{\bf I})\subset[0,2]

[
39
]
. Also, for the
({\bf L},{\bf D})
-GFT and
({\bm{\mathcal{L}}},{\bf I})
-GFT,
\lambda_{n}=2
if and only if the graph is bipartite
[
39
]
. For the
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT there is a similar result.

Proposition 3
.

If the
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT has the spectral folding property then
\sigma({\bf M},{\bf Q})\subset[0,2]
, and
\lambda_{n}=2
if and only if
\lambda_{1}=0
.

Proof.

For all
{\bf x}
,
{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf M}{\bf x}\geq 0
and
{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf J}{\bf M}{\bf J}{\bf x}\geq 0
, therefore

0\leq{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf M}{\bf x}\leq{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf M}{\bf x}+{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf J}{\bf M}{\bf J}{\bf x}=2{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf Q}{\bf x},

(52)

where we use the identity
{\bf M}+{\bf J}{\bf M}{\bf J}=2{\bf Q}
.
Now for any generalized eigenvector
{\bf u}
with unit
{\bf Q}
-norm and eigenvalue
\lambda
, we have
{\bf u}^{\top}{\bf M}{\bf u}=\lambda{\bf u}^{\top}{\bf Q}{\bf u}=\lambda
, and using (
52
) we obtain

0\leq\frac{{\bf u}^{\top}{\bf M}{\bf u}}{{\bf u}^{\top}{\bf Q}{\bf u}}=\lambda\leq 2.

(53)

The statement,
\lambda_{n}=2
if and only if
\lambda_{1}=0
, follows directly from the spectral folding property.
∎

V-A
2
Middle frequency
\lambda=1

In the bipartite case, the subspace associated to the eigenvectors corresponding to
\lambda=1
has the least frequency discrimination because energies at this frequency contribute equally to the low pass and high pass channels. This can be seen from (
27

\lambda=1
, which shows that
h_{0}(1)^{2}=h_{1}(1)^{2}
.
Since the subspace associated to
\lambda=1
has dimension at least
||{\cal A}|-|{\cal B}||
, bipartite graphs with more balanced partitions are usually preferred
[
24
]
.
There is a similar result for the
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT.

Proposition 4
.

If the
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT has the spectral folding property with vertex partition partition
{\cal A},{\cal B}
, then the multiplicity of
\lambda=1
is at least
||{\cal A}|-|{\cal B}||
.

Proof.

We will show that

|\{i:\lambda_{i}=1\}|\geq||{\cal A}|-|{\cal B}||.

(54)

If
\lambda=1
, and
{\bf u}
is the corresponding generalized eigenvector, then
{\bf Z}{\bf u}={\bf u}
, hence the dimension of the null space of
{\bf I}-{\bf Z}
is equal to the multiplicity of
\lambda=1
. Note that
{\bf I}-{\bf Z}
has a the sparsity pattern of a bipartite adjacency matrix, thus
({\bf I}-{\bf Z})^{2}
is block diagonal, and

\displaystyle|\{i:\lambda_{i}=1\}|

\displaystyle=\dim\ker({\bf I}-{\bf Z})

(55)

\displaystyle=n-\rank({\bf Z}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}})-\rank({\bf Z}_{{\cal B}{\cal A}})

(56)

\displaystyle=n-2\rank({\bf M}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}})

(57)

\displaystyle\geq n-2\min(|{\cal A}|,|{\cal B}|)

(58)

\displaystyle=||{\cal A}|-|{\cal B}||.

(59)

The rank of
{\bf I}-{\bf Z}
is the sum of the ranks of the blocks (this can be proven using the fact that t
({\bf I}-{\bf Z})^{2}
is block diagonal). Then the rank of each individual term is equal to the rank of
{\bf M}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}

is non-singular. The rank of a matrix is upper bounded by the minimum between the number of rows and columns. Finally we use the fact that

∎

When

Proposition 5

If

V-B

Vertex domain interpretation

Traditionally, implementations of SGFs using polynomials of the variation operator
{\bf M}
(i.e., the fundamental matrix of the
({\bf M},{\bf I})
-GFT) have been preferred for their
efficiency
(sparse matrix vector products) and interpretability
(localized vertex domain operations).
Since
{\bf Q}
is not diagonal unless the graph is bipartite,
{\bf Z}={\bf Q}^{-1}{\bf M}
can be much denser than
{\bf M}
, and thus the product
{\bf Z}{\bf x}
may no longer be implemented with localized vertex domain operations.

In this section we show that
{\bf Q}^{-1}

{\bf Z}
, can be approximated by polynomials of sparse matrices. In fact, for certain choices of vertex partitions,
{\bf Z}
may be approximately sparse and thus the operation
{\bf Z}{\bf x}
is localized in the vertex domain. We show that this product can be described in terms of vertex domain operations involving bipartite and disconnected graphs. The complexity of SGFs as a function of the vertex partition is studied in
Section VI
.

For simplicity we only consider the combinatorial Laplacian, so that
{\bf M}={\bf L}
.

A probabilistic interpretation of GFBs

Our analysis is based on the lazy filter bank from

.

Assume that
{\bf x}
is a zero mean Gaussian vector with covariance matrix
\Sigma
, and select
{\bf M}=\hbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}^{-1}
as the variation operator. We will show that

{\bf Z}
given by (
34
) is the matrix that produces high pass coefficients
{\bf Z}{\bf x}
with the least norm, hence
{\bf Z}
solves

\min_{{\bf C}}{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf C}{\bf x}\|^{2}_{{\bf I}}],\textnormal{ s.t. }{\bf C}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf I}_{{\cal A}}&{\bf C}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}\\
{\bf C}_{{\cal B}{\cal A}}&{\bf I}_{{\cal B}}\end{bmatrix},

(66)

where
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}={\cal V}\setminus{\cal A}
are fixed, and
{\bf C}
is the high pass filter of the lazy two channel filter bank from
Figure 3
.
Later we study properties of the tree structured iterated lazy filter bank, and provide an alternative probabilistic perspective on its orthogonality.
We can reduce (
66

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf C}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}},{\bf C}_{{\cal B}{\cal A}}}{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{{\cal A}}+{\bf C}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}{\bf x}_{{\cal B}}\|_{{\bf I}}^{2}]+{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{{\cal B}}+{\bf C}_{{\cal B}{\cal A}}{\bf x}_{{\cal A}}\|_{{\bf I}}^{2}]

(67)

\displaystyle=\min_{{\bf C}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}}{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{{\cal A}}+{\bf C}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}{\bf x}_{{\cal B}}\|_{{\bf I}}^{2}]+\min_{{\bf C}_{{\cal B}{\cal A}}}{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{{\cal B}}+{\bf C}_{{\cal B}{\cal A}}{\bf x}_{{\cal A}}\|_{{\bf I}}^{2}].

Minimization of
{\mathbb{E}}[\|{\bf x}_{{\cal A}}+{\bf C}_{{\cal A}{\cal B}}{\bf x}_{{\cal B}}\|_{{\bf I}}^{2}]
corresponds to optimal linear prediction of
{\bf x}_{{\cal A}}
from
{\bf x}_{{\cal B}}
, which fortunately, has a closed form solution for Gaussian distributions.

Proposition 8
.

Given a vertex partition
{\cal A},{\cal B}
, if

VI
Vertex partitioning

In this section
we assume
{\bf M}
is given, and we wish to optimize
{\cal A}
and
{\cal B}={\cal V}\setminus{\cal A}
, as a function of
{\bf M}
.
We start by formulating this problem as an optimization of the condition number of
{\bf Q}
. We provide some theoretical justifications for this choice of objective based on complexity and stability of GFBs. We end the section with our proposed solution.

VI-A

Problem formulation

{\bf J}
defined in (
23
) completely characterizes the vertex partition since
{\bf J}_{ii}=1
if
i\in{\cal A}
, and
{\bf J}_{ii}=-1
if
i\in{\cal B}
. Consequently, we will use a vector
{\bf f}
containing the diagonal terms of
{\bf J}
as an optimization variable.
We propose to minimize
\kappa({\bf Q})=\|{\bf Q}\|\|{\bf Q}^{-1}\|
, the condition number of
{\bf Q}
, subject to spectral folding and balanced partition constraints, namely,

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf f}:{\bf f}_{i}^{2}=1}\kappa({\bf Q})\quad\textnormal{ s.t. }

\displaystyle{\bf Q}=\frac{1}{2}({\bf M}+{\hbox{diag}}({\bf f}){\bf M}{\hbox{diag}}({\bf f}))

(75)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i\in{\cal V}}{\bf f}_{i}\right|\leq 1.

The constraint
{\bf f}_{i}^{2}=1
ensures that the vector
{\bf f}
has entries equal to
1
or
-1
. The downsampling sets are recovered as
{\cal A}=\{i:{\bf f}_{i}>0\}
, and
{\cal B}={\cal A}^{c}
. To ensure the
({\bf M},{\bf Q})
-GFT has the spectral folding property we incorporate the constraint
{\bf Q}=\frac{1}{2}({\bf M}+{\hbox{diag}}({\bf f}){\bf M}{\hbox{diag}}({\bf f}))
. When there is an even number of nodes, the constraint
|\sum_{i\in{\cal V}}{\bf f}_{i}|\leq 1
becomes
\sum_{i\in{\cal V}}{\bf f}_{i}=0
so that
|{\cal A}|=|{\cal B}|
. When the number of nodes is odd, the constraint
|\sum_{i\in{\cal V}}{\bf f}_{i}|\leq 1
becomes
|\sum_{i\in{\cal V}}{\bf f}_{i}|=1
, so that
||{\cal A}|-|{\cal B}||=1
. These constraints guarantee a balanced partition, which is a necessary condition for good frequency selectivity (see
Section V-A
for more details).

VI-B

Justification for condition number minimization

The reason for seeking vertex partitions so that the
{\bf Q}
has a small condition number is due to numerical stability and complexity of GFBs.
For large graphs, performing eigendecomposition to implement a SGF is infeasible due to high computational complexity, thus polynomial graph filters are used instead. For BFBs, a polynomial graph filter of degree
d
can be implemented by sparse matrix vector products (of the normalized Laplacian) with complexity
{\cal O}(d|{\cal E}|)

[
10
,
12
]
.
For arbitrary graphs, polynomials of
{\bf Z}
require the computation of products of the form
{\bf Z}{\bf x}
. However, although
{\bf M}
may be sparse, the fundamental matrix
{\bf Z}={\bf Q}^{-1}{\bf M}
is not. Hence, a naive implementation of the product
{\bf Z}{\bf x}
has complexity
{\cal O}(n^{2})
, while direct computation of
{\bf Z}={\bf Q}^{-1}{\bf M}
has complexity
{\cal O}(n^{3})
due to matrix inversion.
A more efficient approach is to decompose the product
{\bf y}={\bf Z}{\bf x}
into: 1) computing a sparse matrix vector product
{\bf w}={\bf M}{\bf x}
,
and 2) solving a system of equations
{\bf Q}{\bf y}={\bf w}
.
The second step is the computation bottleneck, since it requires solving a linear system.

Large scale positive semi-definite linear system are solved by iterative methods such as the Conjugate Gradient (CG), whose complexity per iteration is that of a sparse matrix vector products with
{\bf Q}
. The condition number of
{\bf Q}
is directly responsible for: 1) the convergence rate of CG algorithms (and thus the total number of iterations), and 2) stability to perturbations of the system
{\bf Q}{\bf y}={\bf w}
. From a computational perspective, the best case scenario occurs when
{\bf Q}
is sparse and has a small condition number. In practice this is attained when
{\bf Q}
is diagonal, and the graph is bipartite, as will be shown in the next subsection.

VI-C

Approximate solution for generalized Laplacians

Because of the non convex constraint
{\bf f}_{i}^{2}=1
, (
75
) is a non convex minimization problem. In this section we obtain an upper bound for
\kappa({\bf Q})
, which allows us to pose an alternative, more tractable optimization problem.
For the rest of this section we assume that
{\bf M}
is a positive semi definite generalized graph Laplacian, that is,
{\bf M}={\bf V}-{\bf W}
, where
{\bf V}
is a diagonal matrix with positive entries and
{\bf W}
is a non negative matrix with zero diagonal.
Since
{\bf Q}
is given by
Theorem 4
, we have that
{\bf Q}={\bf V}-{\bf W}^{bd}\succ 0
, where
{\bf W}^{bd}=(1/2)({\bf W}+{\hbox{diag}}({\bf f}){\bf W}{\hbox{diag}}({\bf f}))
is the block diagonal part of
{\bf W}
. We can bound
\kappa({\bf Q})
with

\kappa({\bf Q})\leq\kappa({\bf V})({1+\rho({\cal A})})/({1-\rho({\cal A})}),

(76)

where
\kappa({\bf V})
is the condition number of
{\bf V}
, and
\rho({\cal A})=\|{\bf V}^{-1/2}{\bf W}^{bd}{\bf V}^{-1/2}\|
. A derivation of this bound is given in Appendix
IX-E
.
When the graph is bipartite,
{\bf f}
can be chosen so that
{\bf Q}
is diagonal resulting in
\rho({\cal A})=0
, making the bound tight.
Since the right side of (
76
) is decreasing with
\rho({\cal A})
, we can minimize
\rho({\cal A})
as a function of the vertex partition.
As a simplification, we use the sequence of bounds for the operator norm of a matrix,
\|{\bf A}\|\leq\|{\bf A}\|_{F}\leq\|{\bf A}\|_{1}=\sum_{i,j}|a_{ij}|
, which results in
\rho({\cal A})\leq\sum_{i,j}(1+{\bf f}_{i}{\bf f}_{j})w_{ij}/\sqrt{v_{i}v_{j}}
. We propose solving instead:

\min_{{\bf f}:{\bf f}_{i}^{2}=1}\sum_{i,j}(1+{\bf f}_{i}{\bf f}_{j})\tilde{w}_{ij}\textnormal{ s.t. }\left|\sum_{i\in{\cal V}}{\bf f}_{i}\right|\leq 1

(77)

where
\tilde{w}_{ij}
is the
ij
entry of
\tilde{{\bf W}}={\bf V}^{-1/2}{\bf W}{\bf V}^{-1/2}
.
Using the identity
\sum_{i,j}{\bf f}_{i}{\bf f}_{j}\tilde{w}_{ij}={\bf f}^{\top}\tilde{{\bf W}}{\bf f}=\sum_{i,j}\tilde{w}_{ij}-{\bf f}^{\top}\tilde{{\bf L}}{\bf f}
, we have that
(
77
) is equivalent to

\max_{{\bf f}:{\bf f}_{i}^{2}=1}{\bf f}^{\top}\tilde{{\bf L}}{\bf f}\text{ s.t. }\left|\sum_{i\in{\cal V}}{\bf f}_{i}\right|\leq 1,

(78)

where
\tilde{{\bf D}}={\hbox{diag}}(\tilde{{\bf W}}\mathbf{1})
, and
\tilde{{\bf L}}=\tilde{{\bf D}}-\tilde{{\bf W}}
. (
78
) is an instance of weighted maximum cut (WMC)
[
71
]
, with an additional balanced partition constraint.
It is well known that WMC is NP hard, thus we consider a spectral partitioning approximation
[
9
,
72
]
, that computes
\tilde{{\bf u}}_{n}=\argmax_{\|{\bf u}\|=1}{\bf u}^{\top}\tilde{{\bf L}}{\bf u}
, and sets
{\bf f}={\hbox{sign}}(\tilde{{\bf u}}_{n}-\tau\mathbf{1})
, and
{\cal A}=\{i\in{\cal V}:{\bf f}_{i}=1\}
.
The parameter
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}
can be tuned to ensure
|\sum_{i\in{\cal V}}{\bf f}_{i}|\leq 1
.

VIII
Conclusion

This paper proposed two-channel filter banks on arbitrary graphs with positive semi definite variation operators
{\bf M}
and arbitrary vertex partitions
{\cal A},{\cal B}
for downsampling. Because of the spectral folding property, previous designs were only valid for the normalized Laplacian of bipartite graphs. Our main contribution is showing that the spectral folding property is satisfied by the generalized eigenvalues and eigenvectors of arbitrary graphs, if the inner product matrix is properly chosen. Based on this, we proposed generalized filter banks (GFB) implemented with spectral graph filters of the generalized eigenvectors. We also studied other theoretical aspects of GFBs including: properties of the generalized eigenvalues, vertex domain localization and probabilistic interpretations. We showed that even though GFBs can use arbitrary vertex partitions for downsampling, partitions that have a large cut (the sum of edge weights from
{\cal A}
to
{\cal B}
is large) lead to computationally efficient and stable implementations. Our numerical results show that indeed, GFBs have localized basis functions and they can be efficiently implemented on large graphs with hundreds of thousands of nodes, while outperforming bipartite filter banks on a signal representation tasks.
Some important directions for future work include:

Tree structured filter banks:
We constructed tree structured GFBs by concatenating two channel GFBs. There is ample room for study of properties of these tree structured filter banks from both probabilistic perspectives (beyond the lazy filter bank case), and the design of variation and downsampling operators. In the supplementary material we obtain frame bounds that reveal some of these multi resolution dependencies.

Vertex partitioning:
Better vertex partitions could be obtained using different approximations to (
75
) or other objective functions, e.g., by using probabilistic models as in
Section V
, or techniques from the graph signal sampling literature
[
74
]
.

IX-A

Proof of
Proposition 6

Recall that
{\bf Q}
from (
33
) obeys

{\bf Q}={\bf D}^{bi}+{\bf L}^{bd}={\bf D}-{\bf W}^{bd}.

(79)

Using this notation, the fundamental matrix can be written

{\bf Z}={\bf I}-({\bf D}-{\bf W}^{bd})^{-1}{\bf W}^{bi}.

(80)

Because
{\bf Q}^{-1}
is non negative, and

{\bf Z}\mathbf{1=0}
, the matrix
({\bf D}-{\bf W}^{bd})^{-1}{\bf W}^{bi}
is right stochastic.
In fact, we can write it as follows

({\bf D}-{\bf W}^{bd})^{-1}{\bf W}^{bi}={\bf Q}^{-1}{\bf W}^{bi}={\bf P}^{bd}{\bf P}^{bi},

(81)

where
{\bf P}^{bi}=({\bf D}^{bi})^{-1}{\bf W}^{bi}
, and
{\bf P}^{bd}=(({\bf D}^{bi})^{-1}({\bf D}^{bi}+{\bf L}^{bd}))^{-1}
, are right stochastic matrices. We can verify this by computing

({\bf D}^{bi})^{-1}{\bf W}^{bi}\mathbf{1}=({\bf D}^{bi})^{-1}{\bf D}^{bi}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}.

(82)

Note that
({\bf P}^{bd})^{-1}
is right stochastic since

\displaystyle({\bf D}^{bi})^{-1}({\bf D}^{bi}+{\bf L}^{bd})\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle=({\bf D}^{bi})^{-1}({\bf D}^{bi}\mathbf{1}+{\bf L}^{bd}\mathbf{1})

\displaystyle=({\bf D}^{bi})^{-1}({\bf D}^{bi}\mathbf{1})=\mathbf{1}.

We have proven that
({\bf P}^{bd})^{-1}\mathbf{1}=\mathbf{1}
, and conclude by right multiplying by
{\bf P}^{bd}
.

Proof of Theorem
5
(Perfect Reconstruction GFB)

The projector onto the eigenspace with eigenvalue
\lambda
is

{\bf P}_{\lambda}=\sum_{i:\lambda_{i}=\lambda}{\bf u}_{i}{\bf u}_{i}^{\top}{\bf Q},

(83)

It is easy to verity that
{\bf P}_{\lambda}^{2}={\bf P}_{\lambda}
, and
{\bf P}_{\lambda}{\bf P}_{\gamma}=\mathbf{0}
when
\lambda\neq\gamma
.
Spectral graph filters can be written using the projectors,

{\bf H}_{i}=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma({\bf M},{\bf Q})}h_{i}(\lambda){\bf P}_{\lambda},\quad{\bf G}_{i}=\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma({\bf M},{\bf Q})}g_{i}(\lambda){\bf P}_{\lambda}.

(84)

Then we have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{0}\mathbf{H}_{0}+\mathbf{G}_{1}\mathbf{H}_{1}=\sum_{\mathclap{\lambda\in\sigma({\bf M},{\bf Q})}}(g_{0}(\lambda)h_{0}(\lambda)+g_{1}(\lambda)h_{1}(\lambda)){\bf P}_{\lambda},

(85)

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{0}{\bf J}\mathbf{H}_{0}-\mathbf{G}_{1}{\bf J}\mathbf{H}_{1}=\sum_{\mathclap{\lambda,\gamma\in\sigma({\bf M},{\bf Q})}}(g_{0}(\lambda)h_{0}(\gamma)-g_{1}(\lambda)h_{1}(\gamma)){\bf P}_{\lambda}{\bf J}{\bf P}_{\gamma}.

(86)

Theorem 4
implies
{\bf J}{\bf P}_{\gamma}={\bf P}_{2-\gamma}{\bf J}
. Using orthogonality, the only terms that survive are those for which
\lambda=2-\gamma
, hence

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}_{0}{\bf J}\mathbf{H}_{0}-\mathbf{G}_{1}{\bf J}\mathbf{H}_{1}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma({\bf M},{\bf Q})}(g_{0}(\lambda)h_{0}(2-\lambda)-g_{1}(\lambda)h_{1}(2-\lambda)){\bf P}_{\lambda}{\bf J}.

(87)

The proof follows directly from these identities. Note that if (
24
) and (
25
) are true, then
\mathbf{G}_{0}{\bf J}\mathbf{H}_{0}-\mathbf{G}_{1}{\bf J}\mathbf{H}_{1}=\mathbf{0}
, and
\mathbf{G}_{0}\mathbf{H}_{0}+\mathbf{G}_{1}\mathbf{H}_{1}=2\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma({\bf M},{\bf Q})}{\bf P}_{\lambda}=2{\bf I}
. For the converse, assume that
\mathbf{G}_{0}\mathbf{H}_{0}+\mathbf{G}_{1}\mathbf{H}_{1}+\mathbf{G}_{0}{\bf J}\mathbf{H}_{0}-\mathbf{G}_{1}{\bf J}\mathbf{H}_{1}=2{\bf I}
. Now let
\gamma\in\sigma({\bf M},{\bf Q})
. Right multiplication by
{\bf P}_{\gamma}
implies (

Analysis and synthesis operators

We assume the input signal is at resolution
L

\ell<L
are called approximation and detail coefficients, and are denoted by
{\bf a}_{\ell}

The sampling sets obey

The synthesis operator is

, and satisfies

, and

After applying (

{\bf x}=\hbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}_{0,L-1}{\bf a}_{L-1}+\hbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}_{1,L-1}{\bf d}_{L-1}=\hbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}_{0}{\bf a}_{0}+\sum_{k=0}^{L-1}\hbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}_{k}{\bf d}_{k}.

Frame bounds

Consider a compression application, where
{\bf c}
and
\hat{{\bf c}}
are the unquantized and quantized coefficients, respectively. We would like to write the reconstruction error

{\bf x}-\hat{{\bf x}}={\bm{\mathcal{T}}}_{s}({\bf c}-\hat{{\bf c}})

(100)

as a function of the quantization error
{\bf c}-\hat{{\bf c}}
. The challenge, is that the operators
{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}_{a}
and
{\bm{\mathcal{T}}}_{s}
are not orthogonal (under any norm). This is true even if each two channel filter bank at resolution
\ell

{\bf Q}_{\ell}

\|{\bf a}_{\ell+1}\|_{{\bf Q}_{\ell+1}}^{2}=\|{\bf a}_{\ell}\|_{{\bf Q}_{0,\ell}}^{2}+\|{\bf d}_{\ell}\|_{{\bf Q}_{1,\ell}}^{2},

where we have defined

{\bf Q}_{0,\ell}={\bf Q}_{\ell+1}({\cal A}_{\ell},{\cal A}_{\ell}),\quad{\bf Q}_{1,\ell}={\bf Q}_{\ell+1}({\cal B}_{\ell},{\cal B}_{\ell}).

(102)

Note that if we wanted to write
\|{\bf a}_{\ell}\|_{{\bf Q}_{0,\ell}}^{2}
as the sum of the energies of
{\bf a}_{\ell-1}
and
{\bf d}_{\ell-1}
, in some norm, and apply recursively (
101

{\bf Q}_{0,\ell}={\bf Q}_{\ell},

(103)

which is satisfied by the normalized Laplacians of bipartite graphs at all resolution levels (since
{\bf Q}_{0,\ell}={\bf Q}_{\ell}={\bf I}

) does not
hold for other variation operators on arbitrary graphs. Fortunately, we can still establish a relation between the norms of the coefficients at different resolutions. We will present a frame bound that relates the norms of
{\bf x}
and its coefficients
{\bf c}
, when the two channel filter bank at level
\ell

{\bf Q}_{\ell}

103
) is violated. First we recall a basic result that relates different vector norms.

Lemma 1
.

[
62
]

Let
{\bf R}_{1}
and
{\bf R}_{2}
be positive definite matrices, then

\alpha\|{\bf x}\|^{2}_{{\bf R}_{2}}\leq\|{\bf x}\|^{2}_{{\bf R}_{1}}\leq\beta\|{\bf x}\|^{2}_{{\bf R}_{2}},

(104)

for any
{\bf x}
, where

\alpha=\inf_{{\bf x}\neq 0}\frac{{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf R}_{1}{\bf x}}{{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf R}_{2}{\bf x}},\quad\beta=\sup_{{\bf x}\neq 0}\frac{{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf R}_{1}{\bf x}}{{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf R}_{2}{\bf x}}.

(105)

Equalities in (

{\bf R}_{1}=c{\bf R}_{2}
for a given constant
c>0
, in which case
\alpha=\beta=c
.
For each
\ell\geq 0

\alpha_{\ell}=\inf_{{\bf x}\neq 0}\frac{{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf Q}_{0,\ell}{\bf x}}{{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf Q}_{\ell}{\bf x}},\quad\beta_{\ell}=\sup_{{\bf x}\neq 0}\frac{{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf Q}_{0,\ell}{\bf x}}{{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf Q}_{\ell}{\bf x}}.

(106)

Also, we define,
A_{L-1}=B_{L-1}=1

A_{\ell}=\prod_{k=\ell+1}^{L-1}\alpha_{k},\quad B_{\ell}=\prod_{k=\ell+1}^{L-1}\beta_{k}.

(107)

We also consider the following inner product matrix

{{\bf Q}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\bf Q}_{0,0}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&{\bf Q}_{1,0}&\ddots&\mathbf{0}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&{\bf Q}_{1,L-1}\end{bmatrix}.

(108)

Now we can revisit (
