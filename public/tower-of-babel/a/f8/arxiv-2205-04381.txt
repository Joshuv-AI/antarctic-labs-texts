Algebraic aspects of connections: from torsion, curvature, and post-Lie algebras to Gavrilov’s double exponential and special polynomials
Mahdi J. Hasan Al-Kaabi, Kurusch Ebrahimi-Fard, Dominique Manchon, Hans Munthe-Kaas

Abstract
. Understanding the algebraic structure underlying a manifold with a general affine connection is a natural problem. In this context, A. V. Gavrilov introduced the notion of framed Lie algebra, consisting of a Lie bracket (the usual Jacobi bracket of vector fields) and a magmatic product without any compatibility relations between them. In this work we will show that an affine connection with curvature and torsion always gives rise to a post-Lie algebra as well as a D D -algebra. The notions of torsion and curvature together with Gavrilov’s special polynomials and double exponential are revisited in this post-Lie algebraic framework. We unfold the relations between the post-Lie Magnus expansion, the Grossman–Larson product and the K K -map, α \alpha -map and β \beta -map, three particular functions introduced by Gavrilov with the aim of understanding the geometric and algebraic properties of the double-exponential, which can be understood as a geometric variant of the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula. We propose a partial answer to a conjecture by Gavrilov, by showing that a particular class of geometrically special polynomials is generated by torsion and curvature. This approach unlocks many possibilities for further research such as numerical integrators and rough paths on Riemannian manifolds.

Let

Definition 1
.

[
32
]

A post-Lie algebra on
\mathbf{k}
is a Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[.\,,.])
together with a bilinear mapping
\rhd
:
\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{g}
compatible with the Lie bracket, in the following sense:

(1)

x\rhd[y,z]=[x\rhd y,z]+[y,x\rhd z]

(2)

[x,y]\rhd z={\mathrm{a}}_{\rhd}(x,y,z)-{\mathrm{a}}_{\rhd}(y,x,z),

for any elements
x,y,z\in\mathfrak{g}
. Here
{\mathrm{a}}_{\rhd}(x,y,z)
is the associator with respect to the product
\rhd
defined by:

{\mathrm{a}}_{\rhd}(x,y,z):=x\rhd(y\rhd z)-(x\rhd y)\rhd z.

Any Lie algebra can be seen as a post-Lie algebra by setting the second product
\rhd
to zero. Another possibility is to take for the second product
\rhd
the opposite of the Lie bracket. A (left) pre-Lie algebra is just an Abelian post-Lie algebra, i.e., a post-Lie algebra with trivial Lie bracket, implying that (
2
) reduces to the (left) pre-Lie identity

0={\mathrm{a}}_{\rhd}(x,y,z)-{\mathrm{a}}_{\rhd}(y,x,z).

In other words, for a (left) pre-Lie algebra the associator is symmetric in the first two entries. On any post-Lie algebra, particular combinations of the Lie bracket and the
\rhd
product yield two other operations, as follows:

(3)

\displaystyle\llbracket x,y\rrbracket

\displaystyle:=x\rhd y-y\rhd x+[x,y],

\displaystyle x\RHD y

\displaystyle:=x\rhd y+[x,y],

for all
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
. From (
1
) and (
2
) above, one can easily deduce that
\big(\mathfrak{g},\llbracket.\,,.\rrbracket\big)
forms a Lie algebra, and the triple
\big(\mathfrak{g},-[.\,,.],\RHD\big)
is another post-Lie algebra
[
7
,
27
]
sharing the same double Lie bracket:

\displaystyle\llbracket x,y\rrbracket

\displaystyle=x\rhd y-y\rhd x+[x,y]

\displaystyle=x\RHD y-y\RHD x-[x,y]

\displaystyle=x\rhd y-y\RHD x.

2.2.1.
The free
D
-algebra generated by a magmatic algebra

Recall that a magmatic algebra consists of a set equipped with a binary operation and no further relations.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(M,\rhd)
be any magmatic algebra. Let
T(M)
be the tensor algebra over
M
with concatenation as product. Extending the magma product
\rhd
to
T(M)
as follows

(22)

\displaystyle x\rhd(VW)

\displaystyle=(x\rhd V)W+V(x\rhd W)

(23)

\displaystyle(xV)\rhd W

\displaystyle={\mathrm{a}}_{\rhd}(x,V,W)

for any element
x\in M
and
V,W\in T(M)
, defines a
D
-algebra structure on
T(M)
.

Proof.

It is clear that (
22
) and (
23
) uniquely define the extended magma product
\rhd
, by induction on the lengths of the elements involved. The tensor algebra is a Hopf algebra with the usual unshuffle coproduct,
\Delta_{\shuffle}(A):=A_{(1)}\otimes A_{(2)}
(we use Sweedler’s notation). The elements in the magmatic algebra
M
are primitive, i.e.,
\Delta_{\shuffle}(x)=x\otimes\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{1}\otimes x
for all
x\in M
. A more explicit formula for the unshuffle coproduct is given by

(24)

\Delta_{\shuffle}(x_{1}\cdots x_{n})=\sum_{I\sqcup J=\{1,\ldots,n\}}x_{I}\otimes x_{J},

with the obvious word notation
x_{I}:=x_{i_{1}}\cdots x_{i_{p}}
for index set
I=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{p}\}
with
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{p}
. The set
\mathop{\hbox{\rm Prim}}\nolimits T(M)
of primitive elements is actually the free Lie algebra
\mathop{\hbox{\rm Lie}}\nolimits(M)
generated by
M
. This is the vector subspace of
T(M)
generated by iterated Lie brackets of elements of
M

[
30
]
. An iteration of (
22
) yields

(25)

U\rhd(VW)=(U_{(1)}\rhd V)(U_{(2)}\rhd W),

which is easily checked on monomials
U=x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
(with
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in M
) by induction on the length
n
. We deduce from (
25
) that the set of
U\in T(M)
such that
L^{\rhd}_{U}
is a derivation is precisely
\mathop{\hbox{\rm Lie}}\nolimits(M)
. It remains to show that (
23
) is valid for any element
x\in\mathop{\hbox{\rm Lie}}\nolimits(M)
. It suffices to check that the set of elements of
T(M)
verifying (
22
) and (
23
), which contains
M
, is a Lie subalgebra. Let us choose two elements
X
and
Y
in
T(M)
verifying (
22
) and (
23
). The claim follows from a straightforward computation detailed in Appendix
A
.
∎

Remark 1
.

It is easily checked that
T(M)
is the free
D
-algebra generated by the magmatic algebra
(M,\rhd)
. In fact, for any
D
-algebra
(D,\diamond,\cdot)
, any magmatic morphism
\psi:M\to(D,\diamond)
can be uniquely extended to an associative algebra morphism
\Psi:T(M)\to D
, which happens to be a
D
-algebra morphism. Similarly,
\mathop{\hbox{\rm Lie}}\nolimits(M)
is the free post-Lie algebra generated by the magmatic algebra
M
.

It is easily seen that
L_{x}^{\rhd}(y):=x\rhd y
is a coderivation with respect to the coproduct
\Delta_{\shuffle}
for any
x\in M
. More generally the coproduct is compatible with the extended magmatic product:

Proposition 1
.

For any
U,V\in T(M)
we have

\Delta_{\shuffle}(U\rhd V)=\Delta_{\shuffle}(U)\rhd\Delta_{\shuffle}(V)=U_{(1)}\rhd V_{(1)}\otimes U_{(2)}\rhd V_{(2)}.

Proof.

We can suppose that
U
is a monomial, and we proceed by induction on its length
\ell
. The details are given in Appendix
A
.
∎

We mention the following result for later use:

Proposition 2
.

For any
U,V,W\in T(M)
the following holds:

(26)

U\rhd(V\rhd W)=\big(U_{(1)}(U_{(2)}\rhd V)\big)\rhd W.

Proof.

We can suppose without loss of generality that
U
is a monomial. We proceed by induction on the length
\ell
of
U
. The details are given in Appendix
A
.
∎

Definition 3
.

The Grossman–Larson product on
T(M)
is defined by

(27)

U\ast V:=U_{(1)}(U_{(2)}\rhd V).

It is easily seen to be associative:

\displaystyle(U\ast V)\ast W

\displaystyle=U_{(1)}(U_{(2)}\rhd V_{(1)})\Big(\big(U_{(3)}(U_{(4)}\rhd V_{(2)})\big)\rhd W\Big)

\displaystyle=U\ast(V\ast W).

This is a straightforward computation using the cocommutativity of the unshuffle coproduct, left to the reader (see the proof of Proposition 3.3 in
[
9
]
).

Proposition 3
.

The Grossman–Larson product (
27
) is compatible with the unshuffle coproduct: for any
U,V\in T(M)
we have

\Delta_{\shuffle}(U\ast V)=\Delta_{\shuffle}(U)\ast\Delta_{\shuffle}(V)=U_{(1)}\ast V_{(1)}\otimes U_{(2)}\ast V_{(2)}.

Proof.

This is a straightforward check using (
27
) and Proposition
1
.
∎

2.2.2.
The free

The following is immediate in view of Remark
1
:

Proposition 4
.

Let
A
be a finite alphabet and
\big(\mathop{\hbox{\rm Mag}}\nolimits(A),\rhd\big)

\mathop{\hbox{\rm Dalg}}\nolimits(A)=T\big(\mathop{\hbox{\rm Mag}}\nolimits(A)\big)

\mathop{\hbox{\rm PostLie}}\nolimits(A)=\mathop{\hbox{\rm Lie}}\nolimits\big(\mathop{\hbox{\rm Mag}}\nolimits(A)\big)

It is known that the free magma generated by

\tau\curvearrowright\Forest{[]}_{a}:=\tau\diamond\Forest{[a]}

\Forest{[b[a]]}=\Forest{[b]}\curvearrowright\Forest{[a]}=\Forest{[b]}\diamond\Forest{[a]}
and

\displaystyle\Forest{[b[a]]}\curvearrowright\Forest{[e[c][d]]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Forest{[b[a]]}\curvearrowright\left(\Forest{[d]}\diamond\Forest{[e[c]]}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\Forest{[b[a]]}\curvearrowright\Forest{[d]}\right)\diamond\Forest{[e[c]]}+\Forest{[d]}\diamond\left(\Forest{[b[a]]}\curvearrowright\Forest{[e[c]]}\right)

\displaystyle\Forest{[d[b[a]]]}\diamond\Forest{[e[c]]}+\Forest{[d]}\diamond\left(\Forest{[e[b[a]][c]]}+\Forest{[e[c[b[a]]]]}\right)=\Forest{[e[c][d[b[a]]]]]}+\Forest{[e[b[a]][c][d]]}+\Forest{[e[c[b[a]]][d]]}\ .

Lemma 2
.

Let
\varphi:\mathop{\hbox{\rm Mag}}\nolimits(A)\to\mathcal{T}^{pl}_{A}
be the unique linear map defined by

\varphi(a):=\Forest{[a]}
for all
a\in A
and
\varphi(\sigma\rhd\tau):=\varphi(\sigma)\curvearrowright\varphi(\tau)
for all
\sigma,\tau\in\mathop{\hbox{\rm Mag}}\nolimits(A)
. The map
\varphi
is an isomorphism, i.e., the magmatic algebras
(\mathcal{T}^{pl}_{A},\curvearrowright)
and
(\mathrm{Mag}(A),\rhd)
are isomorphic.

Proof.

We have just seen that the correspondence
\kappa
, defined by
\kappa(a)=\Forest{[a]}
for all
a\in A
and

\kappa(\tau_{1}\rhd\tau_{2})=\kappa(\tau_{1})\diamond\kappa(\tau_{2})

is a magma isomorphism. This is obviously still true for the correspondence
\overline{\kappa}
analogously defined with the right Butcher product
\diamond
replaced by its left version
{\raise 3.9pt\hbox{$\circ$}}\hskip-1.9pt{\scriptstyle\searrow}\,
, where
\tau_{1}{\raise 3.9pt\hbox{$\circ$}}\hskip-1.9pt{\scriptstyle\searrow}\,\tau_{2}
is the
A
-decorated planar rooted tree obtained by grafting
\tau_{1}
on the root of
\tau_{2}
on the left, for example

\Forest{[c[a][b]]}{\raise 3.9pt\hbox{$\circ$}}\hskip-1.9pt{\scriptstyle\searrow}\,\Forest{[d[e]]}=\Forest{[d[c[a][b]][e]]}\ .

Now both magmatic algebras

As an example, we consider the tree

Then we have

\displaystyle\varphi^{-1}\left(\Forest{[c[a][b]]}\right)

\displaystyle=\varphi^{-1}\big(\Forest{[a]}\curvearrowright(\Forest{[b]}\curvearrowright\Forest{[c]})-(\Forest{[a]}\curvearrowright\Forest{[b]})\curvearrowright\Forest{[c]}\big)

2.3.
The enveloping algebra of a post-Lie algebra

Proposition 5
.

If
(\mathfrak{g},[.\,,.],\rhd)
is a post-Lie algebra, then
\big(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}),\cdot,\rhd\big)
is a
D
-algebra, and the set of primitive elements
\mathfrak{g}=\mathop{\hbox{\rm Prim}}\nolimits\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
is the post-Lie algebra
\mathfrak{d}\big(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})\big)
of the
D
-algebra
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
.

Proof.

Let us consider the
D
-algebra structure on
T(\mathfrak{g})
given in Paragraph
2.2.1
. The ideal
J
generated by
\{\jmath_{x,y}:=x.y-y.x-[x,y],\,x,y\in\mathfrak{g}\}
is also a two-sided ideal for the product
\rhd
. To see this, choose any
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}
and any
U,A,B
in
T(\mathfrak{g})
. By iterating (
22
) we have

U\rhd(A\cdot\jmath_{x,y}\cdot B)=(U_{(1)}\rhd A)\cdot\jmath_{\scriptscriptstyle{U_{(2)}\rhd x,\,U_{(3)}\rhd y}}\cdot(U_{(4)}\rhd B)\in J,

using Sweedler’s notation for the iterated deshuffle coproduct. Starting from
\jmath_{x,y}\rhd U=0
which is a simple consequence of (
23
), we also have
(\jmath_{x,y}\cdot B)\rhd U=0
by Proposition
26
, taking primitiveness of
\jmath_{x,y}
into account. Proposition
26
also proves
(A\cdot\jmath_{x,y}\cdot B)\rhd U\in J
by induction on the length of
A
. Hence the
D
-algebra structure on
T(\mathfrak{g})
gives naturally rise to a
D
-algebra structure on the quotient
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})=T(\mathfrak{g})/J
.
∎

Remark 3
.

From this follows the existence of a pair of adjoint functors between the categories of
D
-algebras and post-Lie algebras

\mathcal{U}(-):\text{postLie}\ \leftrightarrows\ D\text{-algebra}:\mathfrak{g}(-).

In other words, there is a natural isomorphism

\mathrm{Hom}_{\scriptscriptstyle{\text{postLie}}}\big(\mathfrak{g}(A),B\big)\rightarrow\mathrm{Hom}_{\scriptscriptstyle{D\text{-algebra}}}\big(A,\mathcal{U}(B)\big).

Remark 4
.

The Grossman–Larson product also makes sense on
\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
, and is also given by (
27
). From Proposition
3
, it is compatible with the coproduct, making
\big(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}),\ast,\Delta)
a Hopf algebra isomorphic to the enveloping algebra of
(\mathfrak{g},\llbracket.\,,.\rrbracket)
. This has been first established in
[
9
]
, see Proposition 3.3 therein. From Proposition
2
and (
27
) we have

(30)

U\rhd(V\rhd W)=(U\ast V)\rhd W

for any
U,V,W\in\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
.

Note however that both Hopf algebras have different antipodes. As an example, we compare the two antipodes for product
x\cdot y
and
x\ast y
,
x,y\in\mathfrak{g}

\displaystyle S_{*}(x\cdot y)

\displaystyle=-x\cdot y-S_{*}(x)\ast y-S_{*}(y)\ast x

\displaystyle=y\ast x+x\rhd y

\displaystyle=y\cdot x+x\rhd y+y\rhd x

\displaystyle=S(x\cdot y)+x\rhd y+y\rhd x.

and

\displaystyle S(x\ast y)

\displaystyle=-x\ast y-S(x)\cdot y-S(y)\cdot x

\displaystyle=y\cdot x-x\rhd y

\displaystyle=y\ast x-x\rhd y-y\rhd x

\displaystyle=S_{*}(x\ast y)-x\rhd y-y\rhd x.

The following theorem is key to many upcoming computations.

Theorem 2
.

In the Hopf algebra
\big(\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g}),\cdot,\Delta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\shuffle}},\epsilon,S\big)
the product can be expressed in terms of the Grossman–Larson product (
27
) as follows:

(31)

A\cdot B=A_{(1)}\ast\big(S_{*}(A_{(2)})\rhd B\big),\qquad A,B\in U(\mathfrak{g}).

Proof.

We use (
27
) on the righthand side of (
31
), which gives

\displaystyle A_{(1)}\ast\big(S_{*}(A_{(2)})\rhd B\big)

\displaystyle=A_{(1)(1)}\cdot\Big(A_{(1)(2)}\rhd\big(S_{*}(A_{(2)})\rhd B\big)\Big)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{product2}}}{{=}}A_{(1)(1)}\cdot\Big(\big(A_{(1)(2)}\ast S_{*}(A_{(2)})\big)\rhd B\Big)

\displaystyle=A_{(1)}\cdot\Big(\big(A_{(2)(1)}\ast S_{*}(A_{(2)(2)})\big)\rhd B\Big)

\displaystyle=A_{(1)}\cdot\big(m_{\ast}(\text{id}\otimes S_{*})\Delta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\shuffle}}(A_{(2)})\rhd B\big)

\displaystyle=A\cdot B.

In the third equality we used coassociativity.
∎

Remark 5
.

Using (
25
) and
x\in\mathfrak{g}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{U}(\mathfrak{g})
being primitive, i.e.,
\Delta_{\scriptscriptstyle{\shuffle}}(x)=x\otimes 1+1\otimes x

\displaystyle x_{1}\cdots x_{n}

\displaystyle=x_{1}*(x_{2}\cdots x_{n})-x_{1}\rhd(x_{2}\cdots x_{n})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{glp}}}{{=}}x_{1}*(x_{2}\cdots x_{n})-\sum_{i=2}^{n}x_{2}\cdots(x_{1}\rhd x_{i})\cdots x_{n}.

From (

3

3

.

\exp^{*}\big(\chi(y)\big)=\exp^{\textstyle\cdot}(y),

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\exp(\Omega(t))

\displaystyle=\exp(\Omega(t))\text{dexp}_{-\Omega(t)}(\dot{\Omega}(t)).

Using the group-likeness of the two exponentials,
\exp^{*}(\chi(ty))
and
\exp^{\centerdot}(ty)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\exp^{*}(\chi(ty))

\displaystyle=\exp^{\centerdot}(ty){\textstyle\cdot}y

from which we deduce that

\exp^{*}(-\chi(ty))\ast\frac{d}{dt}\exp^{*}(\chi(ty))=\mathrm{dexp}^{*}_{-\chi(ty)}(\dot{\chi}(ty))=\exp^{*}(-\chi(ty))\rhd y.

(48)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{d}{dt}\exp^{*}(-\chi(ty))\Big)\rhd x

2.5.1.
The
K
-map and the
D
-algebra structure

Let
(M,\rhd)
be any magmatic algebra, and let
T(M)
be the tensor algebra over
M
, endowed with the
D
-algebra structure of Paragraph
2.2.1
. For any element
y\in M
, let
\tau^{\rhd}_{y}
be the linear endomorphism of
T(M)
defined by
\tau^{\rhd}_{y}(w)=y\rhd w
for any
w\in T(M)
. By (
22
), it is a derivation. The map

K:T(M)\to T(M)

is recursively defined by
K(1)=1
,
K(y)=y
for any
y\in M
, and

(60)

K(yU)=yK(U)-K\circ\tau^{\rhd}_{y}(U)

for any
y\in M
and
U\in T(M)
. In particular,

K(y_{1}y_{2})=y_{1}y_{2}-y_{1}\rhd y_{2}

and

\displaystyle K(y_{1}y_{2}y_{3})=y_{1}K(y_{2}y_{3})-K\circ\tau^{\rhd}_{y_{1}}(y_{2}y_{3})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{1}y_{2}y_{3}-y_{1}(y_{2}\rhd y_{3})-(y_{1}\rhd y_{2})y_{3}-y_{2}(y_{1}\rhd y_{3})+y_{2}\rhd(y_{1}\rhd y_{3})+(y_{1}\rhd y_{2})\rhd y_{3}.

The map
K
is clearly invertible, as
K(U)-U
is a linear combination of terms of strictly smaller length than the length of
U\in T(M)
. The inverse
K^{-1}
is uniquely determined by
K^{-1}(1)=1
,
K^{-1}(y)=y
for any
y\in M
, and the recursive relation

(61)

(L_{y}+\tau^{\rhd}_{y})\circ K^{-1}=K^{-1}\circ L_{y}

for any
y\in M
, where
L_{y}:T(M)\to T(M)
is defined by
L_{y}(U):=yU
. Recall the Grossman–Larson product (
27
) on
T(M)
given in Definition
3
.

Theorem 3
.

The
K
-map is a unital algebra isomorphism from
T(M)
equipped with the Grossman–Larson product,
\big(T(M),\ast\big)
, onto the tensor algebra
\big(T(M),\cdot\big)
.

Proof.

Recall that for
U,V\in T(M)
we have
U\ast V:=U_{(1)}(U_{(2)}\rhd V).
We prove

K(U\ast V)=K(U)\cdot K(V)

by induction on the length of the tensor
U\in T(M)
. The length zero case is trivial, the length one case is given by (
60
). Now let us compute, with
x\in M
, using the induction hypothesis:

\displaystyle K\big((xU)\ast V\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K(x\ast U\ast V)-K\big((x\rhd U)\ast V)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K(x)\cdot K(U\ast V)-K(x\rhd U)\cdot K(V)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big(xK(U)-K(x\rhd U)\big)\cdot K(V)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle K(xU)\cdot K(V).

∎

2.5.2.

\displaystyle K^{-1}(y_{1}y_{2})

We now recall Gavrilov’s notion of framed Lie algebra

[

A framed Lie algebra is a triple

No compatibility relations of any sort are requested between the magmatic product

[.\,,.]
and

.

, Lemma 6]

There exists a unique series

in the completion

such that

The series

verifies

and

The series

4.
Affine connections on manifolds and covariant derivation

4.1.
Reminders on connections, torsion, curvature and Bianchi identities

Let
E
be a vector bundle on a smooth manifold
\mathcal{M}
, and let
\Gamma(E)
be the
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
-module of its smooth sections. An affine connection on
E
is a bilinear map

\displaystyle\nabla:\mathcal{XM}\times\Gamma(E)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\Gamma(E)

\displaystyle(X,s)

\displaystyle\longmapsto

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}s,

subject to the relations

(84)

\displaystyle\nabla_{fX}s

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f\nabla_{X}s,

(85)

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}(fs)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(X.f)s+f\nabla_{X}s

for any
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
and any
s\in\Gamma(E)
. For clarity, we write
\nabla^{E}
if the vector bundle has to be made precise and we use the convenient notation

X\rhd s:=\nabla_{X}s.

This includes the case of the trivial line bundle
L
, where
\Gamma(L)
then coincides with
C^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
, and the natural connection is given by
X\rhd f:=X.f=\langle Df,X\rangle
. The Leibniz rule (
85
) can therefore be rewritten as follows:

X\rhd(fs)=(X\rhd f)s+f(X\rhd s).

Given a connection on two vector bundles,
E
and
F
, connections on
E\otimes F
and
\mathop{\hbox{\rm Lin}}\nolimits(E,F)=E^{*}\otimes F
are naturally given respectively by the Leibniz rules

(86)

\displaystyle X\rhd(s\otimes s^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(X\rhd s)\otimes s^{\prime}+s\otimes(X\rhd s^{\prime}),

(87)

\displaystyle(X\rhd\varphi)(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X\rhd\varphi(s)-\varphi(X\rhd s).

The curvature of an affine connection is given by

(88)

r(X,Y)s:=X\rhd(Y\rhd s)-Y\rhd(X\rhd s)-{[\hskip-2.9pt[\hskip-2.9pt[\hskip-2.9pt[}X,Y{]\hskip-2.9pt]\hskip-2.9pt]\hskip-2.9pt]}\rhd s

and is also denoted by
R(X,Y,s)
. It is skew symmetric in
(X,Y)
and
C^{\infty}
-linear with respect to each of the three arguments. In the case
E=T\mathcal{M}
, the torsion is given by

(89)

t(X,Y):=X\rhd Y-Y\rhd X-{[\hskip-2.9pt[\hskip-2.9pt[\hskip-2.9pt[}X,Y{]\hskip-2.9pt]\hskip-2.9pt]\hskip-2.9pt]}.

This is skew-symmetric, and
C^{\infty}
-linear with respect to both arguments. The two Bianchi identities are given by

(90)

\displaystyle\oint_{XYZ}R(X,Y,Z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\oint_{XYZ}(X\rhd t)(Y,Z)-\oint_{XYZ}t\big(X,\,t(Y,Z)\big)

(91)

\displaystyle\oint_{XYZ}(X\rhd R)(Y,Z,W)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\oint_{XYZ}R\big(X,\,t(Y,Z),W)

for any
X,Y,Z,W\in\mathcal{XM}
. Here the symbol
\oint
stands for summing over circular permutations of the three arguments. For a detailed account, see e.g.
[
19
]
.

We keep the notations from the previous paragraph and consider the post-Lie algebra associated to a manifold with connection. Let

Theorem 4
.

The map
\rhd:T_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{V})\times\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{R}

\rhd:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{R}\to\mathcal{R}.

-linear morphism

\rho:\mathfrak{g}\to\hskip-10.0pt\to\mathcal{V}.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that

is

-linear in each argument

\displaystyle(f_{1}x_{1}\cdots f_{n}x_{n})\rhd y=f_{1}x_{1}\rhd\big((f_{2}x_{2}\cdots f_{n}x_{n})\rhd y\big)-\big(f_{1}x_{1}\rhd(f_{2}x_{2}\cdots f_{n}x_{n})\big)\rhd y

∎

24

Theorem 5

\mathcal{P}_{\mathop{\hbox{\sevenrm Lie}}\nolimits}(\mathcal{V})=\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{V})

Proof.

The inclusion
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{V})\subset{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathop{\hbox{\sevenrm Lie}}\nolimits}(\mathcal{V})
is obvious. In order to show the reverse inclusion, it suffices to prove that

and
R_{\alpha}
are special polynomials for any Lie monomial
\alpha
. We proceed by induction on the degree: the claim is obvious in degrees one and two, and for

we have from (
112

t_{\alpha}(a.b.c)=R(a.b.c)-(c\rhd t)(a.b)-t\big(t(a.b).c)\big)

and

R_{\alpha}(a.b.c.d)=-(c\rhd R)(a.b.d)-R\big(t(a.b).c.d\big),

which proves the claim. By an iterated use of the Jacobi identity, any Lie monomial of degree
n+1
can be written as a linear combination of Lie monomials of the form

\alpha(x_{1}\cdots x_{n+1})=[x_{j},\beta(x_{1}\cdots\widehat{x_{j}}\cdots x_{n+1})]

where
\beta
is a Lie monomial of degree
n
. For example, consider the equality

\big[[a,b],[c,d]\big]=-\Big[d,\big[[a,b],c\big]\Big]+\Big[c,\big[[a,b],d\big]\Big].

We therefore compute, with
X:=x_{1}\cdots\widehat{x_{j}}\cdots x_{n+1}

\displaystyle\alpha(x_{1}\cdots x_{n+1})=[x_{j},\beta(x_{1}\cdots\widehat{x_{j}}\cdots x_{n+1})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\llbracket x_{j},\beta(X)\rrbracket-x_{j}\rhd\beta(X)+\beta(X)\rhd x_{j}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Dtensor1}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\llbracket x_{j},\beta(X)\rrbracket-\beta(x_{j}\rhd X)+\beta(X)\rhd x_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\llbracket x_{j},t_{\beta}(X)\rrbracket+t_{\beta}(x_{j}\rhd X)-t_{\beta}(X)\rhd x_{j}

\displaystyle\quad+\llbracket x_{j},s_{\beta}(X)\rrbracket-s_{\beta}(x_{j}\rhd X)+s_{\beta}(X)\rhd x_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\llbracket x_{j},t_{\beta}(X)\rrbracket+{[\hskip-2.9pt[\hskip-2.9pt[\hskip-2.9pt[}x_{j},t_{\beta}(X){]\hskip-2.9pt]\hskip-2.9pt]\hskip-2.9pt]}+t_{\beta}(x_{j}\rhd X)-t_{\beta}(X)\rhd x_{j}-{[\hskip-2.9pt[\hskip-2.9pt[\hskip-2.9pt[}x_{j},t_{\beta}(X){]\hskip-2.9pt]\hskip-2.9pt]\hskip-2.9pt]}

\displaystyle\quad+\llbracket x_{j},s_{\beta}(X)\rrbracket-s_{\beta}(x_{j}\rhd X)+s_{\beta}(X)\rhd x_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-s\big(x_{j}.t_{\beta}(X)\big)-t\big(x_{j}.t_{\beta}(X)\big)-(x_{j}\rhd t_{\beta})(X)

\displaystyle\quad+\llbracket x_{j},s_{\beta}(X)\rrbracket-s_{\beta}(x_{j}\rhd X)+s_{\beta}(X)\rhd x_{j}

from which we get

t_{\alpha}(x_{1}\cdots x_{n+1})=t\big(x_{j}.t_{\beta}(X)\big)+(x_{j}\rhd t_{\beta})(X)-R_{\beta}(X.x_{j})

and

\displaystyle R_{\alpha}(x_{1}\cdots x_{n+2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big(\llbracket x_{j},s_{\beta}(X)\rrbracket-s\big(x_{j}.t_{\beta}(X)\big)-s_{\beta}(x_{j}\rhd X)\Big)\rhd x_{n+2}

\displaystyle=

Appendix B
Planar multi-grafting

We use the left grafting representation here (see Remark
2
). We have
T\big((\mathop{\hbox{\rm Mag}}\nolimits(A),\rhd)\big)=(\mathcal{F}^{pl}_{A},\rhd)
, where
\mathcal{F}^{pl}_{A}
is the linear span of ordered forests of planar rooted trees, and
\rhd
is extended by means of (
22
) and (
23
). Recall that any planar rooted tree
\tau\in T^{pl}_{A}
with the root decorated by
a\in A
can be written in terms of the so-called
B^{a}_{+}
-operator, that is,
\tau=B^{a}_{+}[\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{n}]
, for
\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{n}\in\mathcal{F}^{pl}_{A}
. It adds a root decorated by
a\in A
and connects it via an edge to every root in the forest
\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{n}
. For example, denoting the empty tree by
1
, we have

\Forest{[a]}=B^{a}_{+}[1],\quad\Forest{[a[b]]}=B^{a}_{+}[\Forest{[b]}],\quad\Forest{[a[c[b]]]}=B^{a}_{+}[\Forest{[c[b]]}],\quad\Forest{[a[c][b]]}=B^{a}_{+}[\Forest{[c]}\Forest{[b]}].

We will now consider a multivariate extension of the grafting operation by defining the following brace operations

(123)

\rhd^{\scriptscriptstyle{n+1}}:T_{n+1}\big(\mathop{\hbox{\rm Mag}}\nolimits(A)\big)\times\mathop{\hbox{\rm Mag}}\nolimits(A)\to\mathop{\hbox{\rm Mag}}\nolimits(A).

Here,
T_{k}\big(\mathop{\hbox{\rm Mag}}\nolimits(A)\big)
denotes the
k
-th component in the tensor algebra. The multi-grafting in (
123
) is recursively defined for
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in T^{pl}_{A}
and a planar forest
\omega
of length
n
by

(124)

(\tau_{1}\omega)\rhd^{\scriptscriptstyle{n+1}}\tau_{2}:=\tau_{1}\rhd(\omega\rhd^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}\tau_{2})-(\tau_{1}\rhd\omega)\rhd^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}\tau_{2}.

In the case of
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n}\in T^{pl}_{A}
and
\tau_{2}=\Forest{[a]}
this simplifies to

(\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{n})\rhd^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}\Forest{[a]}=(\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{n})\rhd^{\scriptscriptstyle{n}}B_{+}^{a}[1]=B_{+}^{a}[\tau_{1}\cdots\tau_{n}].

Rule (
124
) can be summarised combinatorially as follows: graft the trees
\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n}
in all possible ways onto the tree
\tau
, (i) excluding the grafting of any
\tau_{i}
onto any
\tau_{j}
and (ii) when grafting several trees,
\tau_{j_{1}},\ldots,\tau_{j_{k}}
,
1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{k}\leq n
, onto a vertex
v
of
\tau
, then they must be grafted to the left of the leftmost edge going out from the vertex
v
of
\tau
in such a way, that the order among those trees is preserved. As an example, we consider

\displaystyle\sigma\tau\rhd^{\scriptscriptstyle{2}}\,\Forest{[c[a][b]]}=\

\displaystyle\Forest{[c[\sigma][\tau][a][b]]}+\Forest{[c[\sigma][a[\tau]][b]]}+\Forest{[c[\sigma][a][b[\tau]]]}+\Forest{[c[\tau][a[\sigma]][b]]}+\Forest{[c[a[\sigma][\tau]][b]]}+\Forest{[c[a[\sigma]][b[\tau]]]}+\Forest{[c[\tau][a][b[\sigma]]]}+\Forest{[c[a[\tau]][b[\sigma]]]}+\Forest{[c[a][b[\sigma][\tau]]]}.
