Upper bounds on the Rate of Uniformly-Random Codes for the Deletion Channel
Berivan Isik, Francisco Pernice, Tsachy Weissman

Abstract
We consider the maximum coding rate achievable by uniformly-random codes for the deletion channel. We prove an upper bound that’s within 0.1 of the best known lower bounds for all values of the deletion probability d , d, and much closer for small and large d . d. We give simulation results which suggest that our upper bound is within 0.05 of the exact value for all d d , and within 0.01 0.01 for d > 0.75 d>0.75 . Despite our upper bounds, based on simulations, we conjecture that a positive rate is achievable with uniformly-random codes for all deletion probabilities less than 1. Our results imply impossibility results for the (equivalent) problem of compression of i.i.d. sources correlated via the deletion channel, a relevant model for DNA storage.



1
Introduction

The
binary deletion channel
takes as input a string of bits, and outputs a random subsequence, chosen by deleting each bit of the input independently with a fixed probability
d.
The deletion channel is an example of a channel with
memory
: the action of the channel on different input bits cannot be decoupled, due to the asynchrony between input and output that is introduced. Memory vastly complicates the analysis of the fundamental limits of communication through the deletion channel, i.e., its
capacity
. Indeed, calculating the capacity of this channel as a closed-form expression has been a notorious open problem in information theory for more than fifty years. For channels without memory, the situation is much better understood. A single-letter variational formula for the capacity has been known since Shannon
[
Sha48
]
, and for
symmetric
memoryless channels, the capacity is explicitly given by the mutual information between a uniformly-random (single-letter) input and the corresponding output. This is equivalent to the statement that, for a symmetric memoryless channel, a uniformly-random code achieves capacity with high probability.

Given the success in understanding the capacity of memoryless channels, early research on the deletion channel focused on extending Shannon’s techniques to settings with memory. A major stride in this direction was made by Dobrushin
[
Dob67
]
, who showed that the capacity of the deletion channel, as well as a large class of other channels with memory, is given by the following natural extension of Shannon’s variational formula:

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sup_{X^{n}}I(X^{n};Y^{n}),

(1)

where
I(\cdot\,;\cdot)
is the mutual information, the supremum is over the distributions of the random variable
X^{n}
supported on
\{0,1\}^{n}
, and
Y^{n}
is the output of the binary deletion channel on input
X^{n}
.
1
1

1

The dependence on
d
is suppressed.
Unfortunately, the resulting variational problem is now infinite-dimensional, and a daunting task to compute. And indeed, appealing though it is, Dobrushin’s theorem has proved of little help in obtaining an explicit handle on the capacity of the deletion channel.
2
2

2

A notable exception is the work of Kirsch and Drinea
[
KD10
]
, who improved on the lower bound in
[
DM07
]
via Dobrushin’s theorem and purely information-theoretic arguments.

Faced with the difficulty of the variational problem involved, it is tempting to attempt “plugging in” the uniform distribution as the law of
X^{n},
given the success of this strategy for memoryless symmetric channels. This corresponds to evaluating the performance of uniformly-random codes on the deletion channel. Diggavi and Grossglauser
[
DG01
]
attempted this strategy, and showed that uniformly-random codes can achieve a rate of at least
1-h(d),
for
d\leq 1/2,
where
h:[0,1]\to[0,1]
is the binary entropy function, hence re-proving a lower bound on the deletion channel capacity originally due to Gallager
[
Gal61
]
and Levenshtein
[
Lev65
]
. However, in the same paper, Diggavi and Grossglauser also obtained a better lower bound by generating a code with a non-uniform input distribution. This was later improved upon by a series of papers
[
DM07
,
MD06
,
KD10
,
Dal11
,
RD13
]
, all of whom made use of non-uniform input processes. In fact, we now know that a uniformly-random code
cannot
attain the capacity of the deletion channel, e.g., by the results of Drmota, Szpankowski and Viswanatha
[
DSV12
]
, who showed that the rate achievable by uniformly-random codes is
O((1-d)^{4/3}\log\frac{1}{1-d})
as
d\to 1,
while for optimal codes it’s known to be
\Theta(1-d)

[
MD06
]
.

Nevertheless, in this paper we advocate the view that the rate attainable by uniformly-random codes for the deletion channel is well worth studying in its own right. We offer three justifications, in increasing order of perceived importance by the authors. First, as was pointed out by Mitzenmacher
[
Mit09
]
, and is easy to see, uniformly-random codes have a natural maximum a posteriori (MAP) decoding rule: given a received string
Y,
return the codeword
c
for which
Y
appears as a subsequence the most times. As we illustrate in this paper (and as was implicit in
[
HOS16
]
), this fact can be cast in information-theoretic language, leading to a more concrete characterization of the maximum rate achievable by uniformly-random codes than the limit of the mutual information. Second, as is demonstrated by combining the results of this manuscript with the lower bounds in
[
DG01
,
RD13
,
HOS16
]
, by restricting attention to uniformly-random codes, one can get much tighter lower and upper bounds on the maximum achievable rate. This suggests that a characterization of the maximum rate achievable by uniformly-random codes may be a fruitful milestone in the long road towards understanding the deletion channel capacity. Finally, as we describe in Section
1.1
, the determination of the maximum rate achievable by uniformly-random codes has a
direct
application, in addition to the indirect application of advancing our understanding of the deletion channel capacity. Namely, after a simple transformation, the maximum rate achievable by uniformly-random codes is equivalent to the minimum rate of compression of a uniform source given side information that is correlated to it via the deletion channel. This connection, which was explored in
[
MRT11
,
MRT12
]
, offers a natural generalization of the classical Slepian-Wolf theory of distributed compression of sources with memoryless correlations, to the setting of deletion-based correlations. As such, it has immediate relevance for distributed DNA storage.

1.1
Slepian-Wolf Compression with Deletion Correlations

In this section, we give a brief self-contained overview of the classical Slepian-Wolf theory of distributed compression of correlated sources
[
SW73
]
. For a more comprehensive introduction to the Slepian-Wolf theory, including proofs, we refer the reader to
[
CT06
, Chapter 15.4]
.

Shannon’s theory, among other applications, gives a fundamental limit on the minimum achievable rate of compression of a source
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}:
for an i.i.d. source, the simplest case, it is given by the entropy
H(X_{1}).
It’s natural to ask how this fundamental limit may be extended to the case of multiple sources. It turns out that much about the question is already captured by the case of jointly i.i.d. sources
X:=\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
and
Y:=\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
, where each pair
(X_{i},Y_{i})
is sampled independently from a joint distribution
p(x,y)
, or equivalently, where
X
is i.i.d., and
Y
is the output of a memoryless channel on input
X.
We wish to determine, for any given pair
(R_{X},R_{Y})\in[0,1]^{2}
, whether it’s possible to compress
X
at rate
R_{X}
and
Y
at rate
R_{Y},
i.e., to determine the
achievability region
in the unit square. Slepian and Wolf showed that the achievability region is given by

\{(R_{X},R_{Y}):R_{X}+R_{Y}\geq H(X_{1},Y_{1})\}.

The question becomes even more interesting when one imposes the further restriction that the individual sources
X
and
Y
should be encoded
independently
, in a distributed fashion, such that the encoder of
X
doesn’t have access to
Y
and the encoder of
Y
doesn’t have access to
X,
and then decoded jointly. This is a model, for instance, of a data server with multiple nodes, where communication between nodes is costly and hence each node would like to perform their compression task independently, while taking advantage of the correlations between nodes. Slepian and Wolf’s highly unexpected insight was that, despite the distributed nature of the encoding, the achievability region is

\{(R_{X},R_{Y}):R_{X}\geq H(X_{1}|Y_{1}),R_{Y}\geq H(Y_{1}|X_{1}),R_{X}+R_{Y}\geq H(X_{1},Y_{1})\}.

Given these results, it is of interest to explicitly compute the achievability region for natural choices of sources and correlations. A moment of thought reveals that the problem reduces to calculating
H(X_{1}),H(Y_{1})
,
H(X_{1}|Y_{1})
, and
H(Y_{1}|X_{1})
; the achievability region, in the distributed case, is then given by the convex hull of the points

\begin{cases}(H(X_{1}|Y_{1}),H(Y_{1})),\\
(H(X_{1}),H(Y_{1}|X_{1})),\\
(H(X_{1}|Y_{1}),1),\\
(1,H(Y_{1}|X_{1})),\\
(1,1).\end{cases}

Hence, for sources
X
and
Y
with uniform marginals and memoryless correlations, the problem is as easy and as hard as the determination of the maximum rate achievable by uniform codes over the channels with transition probabilities
p(x|y)
and
p(y|x)
.

Given the subject of this paper, we’d like to extend this from the correlations induced by memoryless channels to those induced by the deletion channel. Ma, Ramchandran and Tse
[
MRT11
]
showed that the Slepian-Wolf results extend almost verbatim (in the “corner point” case where one wants to compress
X
with
Y
as side information) to the case where
X
is uniform and
Y
is the output of the deletion channel on input
X.
Their proof also easily extends to the whole achievability region, e.g., by the information-spectrum version of the Slepian-Wolf theorem
[
MK95
]
,
[
Kog+13
, Section 7.2]
. One simply has to replace all entropies
H(X_{1}),H(X_{1}|Y_{1}),
etc. above by the corresponding entropy rates

H(X):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}H(X_{1}^{n}),\qquad H(X|Y):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}H(X_{1}^{n}|Y_{1}^{n}),\qquad\text{etc.}

Hence, in the setting of distributed compression of uniform sources correlated via deletions, calculating the achievability region amounts to computing
H(X|Y)
and
H(Y|X)
3
3

3

This suffices, since for
X
uniform and
Y
the corresponding output through the deletion channel, it’s trivial that
Y
is also uniform and hence
H(X)=H(Y)=1.
Once again, this is easily seen to be equivalent to computing

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}I(X^{n};Y^{n}(X^{n}))=1-\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}H(X^{n}|Y^{n}(X^{n}))=1-d-\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}H(Y^{n}(X^{n})|X^{n}),

where
X
is uniform and now
Y^{n}(X^{n})
is the output of the deletion channel on input
X^{n},
and
not
the first
n
coordinates of the process
Y
(hence the entropy rate of
Y^{n}(X^{n})
is
1-d
instead of 1). Namely, calculating the minimum rate of Slepian-Wolf coding for uniform sources and deletion correlations is equivalent to evaluating the performance of uniformly-random codes through the deletion channel.

1.2
Our Results

Our main results are efficiently-computable upper bounds on the maximum rate achievable through the deletion channel by uniformly-random codes. By the discussion in Subsection
1.1
, our results imply impossibility results for the distributed compression of deletion-correlated sources. Unlike many of the known lower and upper bounds related to the deletion channel, which often rely on coding-theoretic arguments like side information or specific decoding strategies, our approach is almost entirely based on tools from probability theory.

We now state our results, and then we plot the resulting bounds at the end of this subsection.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
C_{\text{unif}}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
denote the maximum rate achievable through the deletion channel by uniformly-random codes. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
d\in[0,1],
we have

\displaystyle C_{\text{unif}}(d)\leq 1-d-h(d)+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\log(8\pi e\max\{d(1-d)n,1/6\})+\sum_{u=0}^{n}\binom{n}{u}d^{u}(1-d)^{n-u}\Bigg(\frac{1}{n}\log\Pi_{n}^{u}[u]

\displaystyle+h(u/n)+\frac{1}{2n}\log(\max\{2\pi u(n-u)/n,1\})+\frac{\log e}{6n\max\{\min\{u,n-u\},1\}}\Bigg).

where
h
is the binary entropy function, and the function
u\mapsto\Pi_{n}^{u}[u]
can be computed in time
O(n^{3})
and space
O(n^{2}).
In particular, our upper bound can be evaluated in time
O(n^{4})
and space
O(n^{2}).

We note that the explicit algorithm to compute
\Pi_{n}^{u}[u]
is given in Theorem
3.8
. As a second result, we improve upon the runtime of Theorem
1.1
, at the cost of worse error terms.

Theorem 1.2
.

For all
d\in(0,1),

n\in\mathbb{N},
we have

\displaystyle C_{\text{unif}}(d)

\displaystyle\leq 1-d+\inf_{\delta}\Bigg[\frac{1}{n}\log\Pi_{n}^{(d-\delta)n}[(d-\delta)n]+\delta+2^{-nD(d-\delta||d)}+\frac{1}{n}\log(\max\{2\pi(d-\delta)(1-d+\delta)n,1\})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{n}\log(8\pi e\max\{d(1-d)n,1/6\})+\frac{\log e}{3n^{2}\min\{\max\{d-\delta,1-d+\delta\},1\}}\Bigg],

where
u\mapsto\Pi_{n}^{u}[u]
is the same function as in Theorem
1.1
, and
D(\cdot||\cdot):[0,1]^{2}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is the (base 2) KL-divergence for two Bernoulli distributions. The infimum is over the
\delta>0
such that
(d-\delta,d+\delta)\subseteq[0,1]
and
\delta n\in\mathbb{N}.
In particular, this upper bound can be computed in time
O(n^{3})
and space
O(n^{2})
by choosing a constant-sized grid to take the infimum over.

The following corollary, which immediately follows from Theorem
1.2
, simplifies the above expression at a small loss in the error terms.

Corollary 1.3
.

For all
d\in(0,1)
,
\delta>0,n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(d-\delta,d+\delta)\subseteq[0,1],\delta n\in\mathbb{N},
and
n\geq\min\{d-\delta,1-d+\delta\}^{-1},
we have

C_{\text{unif}}(d)\leq 1-d+\frac{1}{n}\log\Pi_{n}^{(d-\delta)n}[(d-\delta)n]+\delta+2^{-nD(d-\delta||d)}+\frac{1}{n}\log(\pi^{2}e^{4/3}n).

Although Theorem
1.2
is asymptotically more efficient than Theorem
1.1
, our numerical calculations, all of which ran within a few hours on a personal computer, were not large enough for this difference to be noticed. Hence our best numerical upper bounds, plotted below, are given by Theorem
1.1
. In addition to our upper bounds and the lower bounds of
[
DG01
,
RD13
,
HOS16
]
, we also plot simulation-based upper and lower bounds on the exact value of
C_{\text{unif}}
for all deletion probabilities. These simulation results suggest that our upper bounds are within 0.05 of the true value of
C_{\text{unif}}
for all values of
d.
For a discussion on how accurate these simulation-based bounds can be taken to be, including the exact method and parameters with which they were calculated, we refer the reader to Section
4
, which can be read immediately after Section
2
. Finally, we mention that, although our upper bound comes very close to zero for
d
large, we conjecture that
C_{\text{unif}}(d)
is positive for all
d<1
(see Section
5

2.3
Convergence Rate

In this subsection, we derive a bound on the speed of convergence of
E_{n}
to
E_{\infty},
as defined in (
3
). Analogous bounds, with a similar proof, have appeared in the literature
[
Dal11
,
FD10
,
KM10
]
.

We go back to our original expression for
E_{n},
namely
E_{n}=\frac{1}{n}H(D|X,Y).
Let
J
be the index of
Y
such that the substring
Y^{J}
is the output of the first half of the deletion pattern
D
on the first half of the input
X,
i.e.
Y^{J}=D^{n/2}(X^{n/2}).
We have

\displaystyle E_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}H(D|X,Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}H(D,J|X,Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}[H(J|X,Y)+H(D|X,Y,J)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}[H(J|X,Y)+2H(D^{n/2}|X^{n/2},Y^{J})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}H(J|X,Y)+E_{n/2}.

Hence

\displaystyle 0\leq E_{n}-E_{n/2}\leq\frac{1}{n}H(J).

Now, again using that for a binomial
J
with variance
v
, we have
H(J)\leq\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi e(v+1/12))

[
Mas88
]
, we get

\displaystyle 0\leq E_{n}-E_{n/2}\leq\frac{1}{2n}\log(\pi e(d(1-d)n+1/6)).

(6)

From this we can deduce a bound on the convergence rate.

Lemma 2.3
.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N},
we have

\displaystyle 0\leq E_{\infty}-E_{n}\leq\frac{1}{2n}\log(8\pi e\max\{d(1-d)n,1/6\}).

Proof.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, by (
6
) we have

\displaystyle 0\leq E_{2^{k}n}-E_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}E_{2^{j}n}-E_{2^{j-1}n}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{2^{j}}\log(2\pi e\max\{d(1-d)2^{j}n,1/6\})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2n}\left[\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{j}{2^{j}}+\log(2\pi e\max\{d(1-d)n,1/6\})\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{2^{j}}\right],

and taking
k\to\infty
yields the lemma.
∎

As a gauge of the speed of convergence guaranteed by Lemma

, note that at

(where our bound is loosest), we have shown

0\leq E_{\infty}-E_{10,000}\leq 0.00087.

3

So far, we have shown convergence rates of

The gap in our upper bound can be qualitatively understood. The only loss we incurred came from our application of Jensen’s inequality at the beginning of this section. Since we’re dealing with an exponential-sized object
|\mathcal{D}(X,Y)|,
its expectation can be wildly swayed by events of exponentially small probability. An example of such an event is that the length of the output
L=|Y|
deviates from its expectation
(1-d)n
by a constant multiple
\delta
of
n.
This happens with probability
O(2^{-\alpha n})
for some
\alpha=\alpha(\delta)>0
, but if the expected increase

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{D}(X,Y)|\;|\;|L-(1-d)n|>\delta n]}{\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{D}(X,Y)|\;|\;|L-(1-d)n|\leq\delta n]}\right)

(14)

is greater than
\alpha,
then the regime where
|L-(1-d)n|>\delta n
will impact the value of
\log\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{D}(X,Y)|.
A moment of thought reveals that (
14
) is small whenever
d
is at a maximum of
d\mapsto E_{n}(d)
; hence in this case the loss in our approximation cannot be explained by deviations of
L
away from its expectation. On the other hand if
E_{n}(d)
is increasing at
d,
then
\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{D}(X,Y)|
will be exponentially swayed upwards by the contribution of the event
L\geq(1-d+\delta)n.
Symmetrically, if
E_{n}
is decreasing at
d,
then
\mathbb{E}|\mathcal{D}(X,Y)|
will be exponentially swayed upwards by the contribution of the event

And indeed, that our bound is loose when

is increasing or decreasing, and close to tight when it’s at a maximum, is exactly what we observe in Figure

3.3
Non-Uniform Measure Upper Bound

At the end of the last section we observed qualitatively that our naive bound was loosened substantially by the exponentially-unlikely event that
||Y|-(1-d)n|=\Omega(n).
Now, what we ultimately want to upper bound is
E_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\log|\mathcal{D}(X,Y)|,
which can’t possibly be swayed by such events by virtue of
\frac{1}{n}\log|\mathcal{D}(X,Y)|
being in
[0,1]
. In this section, we obtain an improved upper bound by “removing” these unlikely events from the expectation altogether.

We begin with a definition.

Definition 3.6
.

For
X\sim Unif\{0,1\}^{n}
and
u\in[n],
we let
Y[u]
denote the random variable obtained by deleting
exactly

u
uniformly-chosen bits from
X.
We distinguish this from the notation
Y(d):=\BDC_{d}(X).

To improve our upper bound on
E_{\infty}
, our approach will be to upper bound

E_{n}^{\prime}=E_{n}^{\prime}[u]:=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\log|\mathcal{D}(X,Y[u])|.

It’s not hard to see that
E_{n}^{\prime}[dn]\to E_{\infty}(d)
as
n\to\infty
(this follows, e.g., by
[
Dal11
, Theorem 1]
), but we’d like to get a non-asymptotic version of that statement so that we can relate our upper bound on
E_{n}^{\prime}
to
E_{\infty}
, and hence
C_{\text{unif}}.
Unfortunately, our arguments from Subsection
2.3
no longer work because the different bits in the deletion pattern are now dependent. We temporarily disregard this issue, and in Subsection
3.3.1
, we focus on upper bounding
E_{n}^{\prime}
using the methods of subsections
3.1
and
3.2
. Then in Subsection
3.3.2
, we show a simple way to circumvent the issue of convergence of
E_{n}^{\prime}
to
E_{\infty},
by directly upper bounding
E_{n}
by an averaging of
E_{n}^{\prime}
values, and then applying the results from Subsection
2.3
to relate
E_{\infty}
to
E_{n}.
This yields a proof of Theorem
1.1
. This method has the advantages of being very simple to prove (once one has an upper bound on
E_{n}^{\prime}
), and of having error terms which sharply decay with
n.
However, it has the disadvantage of making the runtime jump to
O(n^{4}).
In Subsection
3.3.3
, we instead get a non-asymptotic handle on
E_{\infty}
in this setting by relating
E_{n}(d)
to
E_{n}^{\prime}[(d\pm\delta)n],
for
\delta
small. This reduces the runtime to
O(n^{3})
, but introduces error terms which decay more slowly. In practice, the best numerical upper bounds are still obtained by the simpler approach of Subsection
3.3.2

3.3.1
Upper bounding
E_{n}^{\prime}

In this section, we apply the techniques from subsections
3.1
and
3.2
to obtain an upper bound on
E_{n}^{\prime}[u],
for
u\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}
. As in Subsection
3.2
, by Jensen we can write

\displaystyle E_{n}^{\prime}[u]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\log|\mathcal{D}(X,Y[u])|]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{D}(X,Y[u])|].

Letting
D,D^{\prime}\sim_{iid}Ber(u/n)^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\mathcal{D}(X,Y[u])|]

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{u}\mathbb{P}(D(X)=D^{\prime}(X)|w(D)=w(D^{\prime})=u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{u}d^{2u}(1-d)^{2(n-u)}}\mathbb{P}(D(X)=D^{\prime}(X),\;w(D)=w(D^{\prime})=u),

so defining

\displaystyle\Pi_{n}^{\ell}[u]

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}(D(X)=D^{\prime}(X),\;w(D)=w(D^{\prime})=u),\qquad\qquad D,D^{\prime}\sim_{iid}Ber(\ell/n)^{n},

(15)

we have shown

\displaystyle E_{n}^{\prime}[u]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\log\Pi_{n}^{u}[u]-\frac{1}{n}\log\binom{n}{u}+2h(u/n).

Using Lemma
A.2
from the appendix, we get

\displaystyle E_{n}^{\prime}[u]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\log\Pi_{n}^{u}[u]+h(u/n)+\frac{1}{2n}\log(\max\{2\pi u(n-u)/n,1\})+\frac{\log e}{6n\max\{\min\{u,n-u\},1\}}.

(16)

Our remaining task in this section is to efficiently compute
\Pi_{n}^{u}[u].
From Proposition
3.2
, setting
\alpha=\beta=\ell/n,
for
D,D^{\prime}\sim_{iid}Ber(\ell/n)^{n}
we have

\mathbb{P}(D(X)=D^{\prime}(X),w(D)=w(D^{\prime})=u|\mathcal{F}_{n})=\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n}=0,\,w(D)=u}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}P_{j}.

By the same argument as in Subsection
3.2
, we can replace
P_{j}
by the symmetrized process

\displaystyle V_{j}=\begin{cases}1/\sqrt{2}\qquad&\text{if }\xi_{j}\in\{\pm 1\},\\
1&\text{if }\xi_{j}=0\text{ and }W_{j-1}=0,\\
1&\text{if }\xi_{j}=0\text{ and }W_{j-1}\neq 0\text{ and }D_{j}=1,\\
1/2&\text{if }\xi_{j}=0\text{ and }W_{j-1}\neq 0\text{ and }D_{j}=0,\end{cases}

from which we obtain the following corollary of Proposition
3.2
.

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
\Pi_{n}^{\ell}[u]
be defined by (
15
). We have

\displaystyle\Pi_{n}^{\ell}[u]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n}=0,\,w(D)=u}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}V_{j}\right],

where
V_{j}
is defined as above with
D,D^{\prime}\sim Ber(\ell/n)^{n}.

Using this, we obtain a recursive relation analogous to Theorem
3.5
to calculate
\Pi_{n}^{u}[u]
efficiently.

Theorem 3.8
.

Consider the domain
B:=\{(n,k,u):n\geq-1,|k|\leq n+1,0\leq u\leq n+1\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{3},
and its interior
B^{\circ}:=\{(n,k,u):n\geq 0,|k|\leq n,0\leq u\leq n\}.
For
\ell\in\{0,1,\dots,n\},
define a function
S^{\ell}:B\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
by the initial value
S^{\ell}(0,0,0)=1,
the recurrence relation

\displaystyle S^{\ell}(n,k,u)

\displaystyle=(\ell/n)^{2}S^{\ell}(n-1,k,u-1)+\frac{(\ell/n)(1-(\ell/n))}{\sqrt{2}}\left(S^{\ell}(n-1,k-1,u-1)+S^{\ell}(n-1,k+1,u)\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+(1-(\ell/n))^{2}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}_{k\neq 0}\right)S^{\ell}(n-1,k,u).

on
B^{\circ}\setminus\{(0,0,0)\}
, and the boundary condition
S^{\ell}\equiv 0
on
B\setminus B^{\circ}.
Then, if
\Pi_{n}^{\ell}[u]
is defined by (
15
), we have
\Pi_{n}^{\ell}[u]=S^{\ell}(n,0,u).
In particular,
\Pi_{n}^{u}[u]
can be computed in time
O(n^{3})
and space
O(n^{2}).

Proof.

For simplicity, we drop the superscript
\ell
in the notation and set
d=\ell/n.
The proof is analogous to that of Theorem
3.5
. We will prove that, on
B^{\circ},
we have

\displaystyle S(n,k,u)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n}=k,\,w(D)=u}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}V_{j}\right].

(17)

The first part of the theorem then follows from Corollary
3.7
. We proceed by induction on
n.
The validity of (
17
) for the initial condition

W_{0}=0

w(D^{0})=w(\emptyset)=0

For a point
(n,k,u)\in B,
consider the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\sigma(W_{i},D_{i}:i\leq n)

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n}=k,\,w(D)=u}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{n}V_{j}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}V_{j}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n}=k,\,w(D)=u}\cdot V_{n}\;\Bigg|\;\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]\right].

But

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n}=k,\,w(D)=u}\cdot V_{n}\;|\;\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n-1}=k,w(D^{n-1})=u-1}+\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n-1}=k,w(D^{n-1})=u-1}+\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n-1}=k-1,w(D^{n-1})=u-1}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n-1}=k+1,w(D^{n-1})=u})\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n}=k,w(D)=u}\cdot V_{n}\;|\;\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n-1}=k,w(D^{n-1})=u-1}d^{2}\cdot 1+\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n-1}=k,w(D^{n-1})=u-1}d(1-d)\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n-1}=k-1,w(D^{n-1})=u-1}(1-d)d\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n-1}=k+1,w(D^{n-1})=u}(1-d)^{2}\cdot\left(\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n}=0}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n}\neq 0}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n-1}=k,w(D^{n-1})=u-1}d^{2}\cdot 1+\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n-1}=k,w(D^{n-1})=u-1}d(1-d)\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n-1}=k-1,w(D^{n-1})=u-1}(1-d)d\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n-1}=k+1,w(D^{n-1})=u}(1-d)^{2}\cdot\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n}\neq 0}\right).

Finally for points
(n,k,u)
adjacent to the boundary, note that if
(n-1,\ell,t)\in B\setminus B^{\circ},
for

we have
\mathbbm{1}_{W_{n-1}=\ell,w(D^{n-1})=t}\equiv 0
. Thus, by inductive hypothesis, we have (
17

For the second part of the theorem, again the bound on the time complexity is obvious from the first part. The justification on the space bound is the same as in Theorem
3.5

as a function of only the values of the previous strip

O(n^{2})

∎

In the last subsection, we obtained an improvement over the naive bound of Subsection

We first collect a useful lemma.

Lemma 3.9
.

The function

is monotone-increasing.

The (elementary) proof is in the appendix. From this we obtain the following bounds, which are a simple sharpening of
[

Lemma 3.10
.

For all
d,\delta\in[0,1],
we have

\displaystyle\frac{H(X|Y[(d-\delta)n])}{n}-2^{-nD(d-\delta||d)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{H(X|Y(d))}{n}\leq\frac{H(X|Y[(d+\delta)n])}{n}+2^{-nD(d+\delta||d)},

Proof.

For
K\sim Binom(n,d),
we can write

\displaystyle H(X|Y(d))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{k\leftarrow K}[H(X|Y[k])].

But from Lemma

and the fact that

\displaystyle(1-\mathbb{P}(K\leq(d-\delta)n))\frac{H(X|Y[(d-\delta)n])}{n}\leq\frac{H(X|Y(d))}{n}\leq\frac{H(X|Y[(d+\delta)n])}{n}+\mathbb{P}(K\geq(d+\delta)n).

∎

4
Simulating
E_{n}

Given
x
and
y,
the quantity
|\mathcal{D}(x,y)|
can be calculated in time
O(|x||y|)
and auxiliary space
O(|y|)
by a standard dynamic programming algorithm (see, e.g.,
[
Lot05
,
Mit09
]
). We define an array
a
of length equal to
|y|.
We iterate an index
i=0,\dots,|x|-1.
At each fixed value of
i,
the idea is to update our array
a
such that, for each
j=0,\dots,|y|-1,
the value
a[j]
counts the number of times that
y^{j+1}
appears as a subsequence in
x^{i+1}.
For fixed
i
, this can be computed as a function of
x_{i+1},y_{j+1}
, and the previous values of
a[j-1],a[j]
(from iteration
i-1
): if
x_{i+1}=y_{j+1}
, we can now extend all our subsequences
y^{j}
from the previous iteration by appending
x_{i}
to them; hence
a[j]+=a[j-1]
. Otherwise if
x_{i+1}\neq y_{j+1}
, we leave the value
a[j]
unchanged in this iteration. The formal algorithm is below.

Algorithm 1

A standard algorithm to compute the number of times that
y
appears as a subsequence of
x.

Input:

x\in\{0,1\}^{n},y\in\{0,1\}^{m},n\geq m.

a\leftarrow
Array of length
m.

for

i\in\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}

do

for

j\in\{m-1,m-2,\dots,1\}

do

if

x_{i+1}=y_{j+1}

then

a[j]\leftarrow a[j]+a[j-1]

end

if

end

for

if

x_{i+1}=y_{1}

then

a[0]\leftarrow a[0]+1

end

if

end

for

return

a[m-1]

Remark 4.1
.

Note two subtleties. First, we treat the case of
j=0
separately:
a[0]
counts the number of times that the bit
y_{1}
appears in
x
, so here a match
x_{i+1}=y_{1}
results in an increment of
a[0]
by 1. Second, we are iterating over
y
backwards: this is so that our updates of
a[j]
at a fixed outer iteration
i
don’t interfere with each other.

Using this algorithm to calculate
|\mathcal{D}(X,Y)|
for several independently-sampled
(X,Y)
pairs, where
X\sim Unif\{0,1\}^{n}
and
Y=\BDC_{d}(X),
taking log, dividing by
n
, and averaging, we can simulate the value of
E_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\log|\mathcal{D}(X,Y)|
. By Lemma
2.3
, our simulated value of
E_{n}
gives a simulation-based lower bound on
E_{\infty}
, and after an upward shift by
\frac{1}{2n}\log(8\pi e\max\{d(1-d)n,1/6\})
, we obtain a simulation-based upper bound. The simulation-based lower and upper bounds of figures
1
,
3
and
4
were obtained by this method, all with
n=1,000
and by averaging
1,000
independent samples.

It’s natural to ask how accurate these simulation results should be expected to be. A complete answer to this question would require a good understanding of the concentration properties of the random variable
\frac{1}{n}\log|\mathcal{D}(X,Y)|
, which seems like a very challenging problem. Simulation results suggest that it concentrates very strongly (see conjectures
5.1
and
5.2
in the next section). However, we note that
something
can be said just from the fact that
\frac{1}{n}\log|\mathcal{D}(X,Y)|\in[0,1]
almost surely. Namely, letting
Z_{n}^{t}
be the empirical average of
t
independent samples equal in distribution to
\frac{1}{n}\log|\mathcal{D}(X,Y)|,
by Hoeffding’s inequality we have

\mathbb{P}(|Z_{n}^{t}-E_{n}|\geq\gamma)\leq 2e^{-2\gamma^{2}t}

for any
\gamma>0.
This implies, for instance, a
99\%
confidence interval around our simulated lower and upper bounds of width equal to
2\cdot 0.0514
, since
2e^{-2(0.0514)^{2}1000}\approx 0.01
. However, this is of course very weak, and in fact already implied by our upper bounds and the lower bounds of
[
DG01
,
RD13
,
HOS16
]
. This confidence interval could be easily tightened by running larger simulations, but we didn’t attempt to do this since the empirically-observed concentration is so strong.

5
Conjectures and Open Problems

We conclude by collecting some conjectures and open problems with which we hope to motivate further work. We begin with the concentration properties of
\frac{1}{n}\log|\mathcal{D}(X,Y)|,
which we already discussed in the last section. An initial conjecture, which may not be so hard to prove, is the following.

Conjecture 5.1

(Concentration conjecture, weak form.)
.

\frac{1}{n}\log|\mathcal{D}(X,Y)|\to E_{\infty}
almost surely.

The following strengthening implies Conjecture
5.1
by Lemma
2.3
and the Borel-Cantelli lemma.

Conjecture 5.2

(Concentration conjecture, strong form.)
.

For any
\gamma>0,
we have

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\log|\mathcal{D}(X,Y)|-E_{n}\right|\geq\gamma\right)\leq e^{-\Omega(n/\poly(\log(n)))}.

Finally, we turn to what we consider to be a fascinating question.

Question 5.3
.

Determine the value
d^{*}_{\text{unif}}\in[\frac{1}{2},1]
when
C_{\text{unif}}
first becomes equal to zero. Formally, determine

d^{*}_{\text{unif}}:=\sup\{d\in[0,1]:C_{\text{unif}}(d)>0\}.

Based on simulation results, we put forth the following.

Conjecture 5.4
.

d^{*}_{\text{unif}}=1.

We remark that, as far as we know, the state of the art is that
\frac{1}{2}\leq d^{*}_{\text{unif}}\leq 1,
i.e., we don’t have a lower bound that beats 1/2. Given the recent progress on the analogous question for the case of the adversarial deletion channel capacity
[
GHL22
]
, perhaps related techniques could be applied here as well.
