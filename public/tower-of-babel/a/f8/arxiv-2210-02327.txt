On the non-local boundary value problem from the probabilistic viewpoint
Mirko D’Ovidio

Abstract
. We provide a short introduction of new and well-known facts relating non-local operators and irregular domains. Cauchy problems and boundary value problems are considered in case non-local operators are involved. Such problems respectively lead to anomalous behavior on the bulk and on the surface of a given domain. Such a behavior can be considered (in a macroscopic viewpoint) in order to describe regular motion on irregular domains (in the microscopic viewpoint).



1.
Introduction

We present a collection of results about the connection between non-local operators and irregular domains. We are concerned with the interplay between macroscopic and microscopic analysis of Feller motions on bounded and unbounded domains. Our discussion relies on the fact that anomalous motions on regular domains can be considered in order to describe motions on irregular domains. Here, by irregular domains we mean domains with irregular boundaries or interfaces, for example of fractal type.

Fractional Cauchy problems have been considered as models for motions on non-homogeneous domains, fractals for instance. In the same spirit we consider anomalous motions for non-local boundary value problems, that is the motions exhibit anomalous behavior only near the boundary. Non-local boundary conditions therefore introduce models for motions on irregular domains, for example domains with trap boundaries (trap domains, in the sequel).

The presentation will run as follows. First we recall some facts about non-local operators and the associated processes. Sometimes non-local operators are also referred as to generalized fractional operators. However, there is no fractional order to be considered. We move to the next sections by discussing the non-local initial value problems and then the non-local boundary value problems. For the latter, we underline some connection with the case of random Koch domains and, in particular, with the boundary behaviors introduced by the non-local boundary conditions.

2.
Markov processes, time changes, non-local operators

2.1.
Markov Processes

Let
X=\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
with
X_{0}=x\in\Omega
be the Markov process on
\Omega
with generator
(G,D(G))
where
(G,D(G))
is the generator of the semigroup
Q_{t}
, then we have the probabilistic representation

\displaystyle Q_{t}f(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}[f(X_{t})].

We recall that for the Markovian semigroup
Q_{t}
, the generator is defined by

(2.1)

\displaystyle Gf(x)=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{Q_{t}f(x)-f(x)}{t},\quad f\in D(G)

for
f
in some set of real-valued functions and for which the limit exists in some sense. Then we say that
f
belongs to the domain of the generator
D(G)
.
In case
X
is a Feller process for instance, the domain
D(G)
consists of continuous functions vanishing at infinity for which (
2.1
) exists as uniform limit. Examples of Feller processes are given by the Brownian motion (with
Gf=f^{\prime\prime}
), the Poisson process (with
Gf=\lambda(F-1)f
where
Ff(x)=f(x+1)
is the forward operator) and in general, Lévy processes (for which we will consider
G=-\Psi(-\Delta)
to be better defined further on). For the Brownian motion the forward and backward Kolmogorov’s equation have the same reading in terms of heat equations. A Poisson process (started at zero for instance) is driven by
\lambda(1-B)f
where
Bf(x)=f(x-1)
is the backward operator. We skip here a discussion on Lévy processes.

We say that
X
is a Feller process if
X
is a strong Markov process, right-continuous with no discontinuity other than jumps. If
X
is a continuous strong Markov process we say that
X
is a Feller diffusion (or simply, diffusion).

For the process
X
started at
x
with density
p(t,x,y)
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in D)=\int_{D}p(t,x,y)dy,\quad D\subseteq\Omega

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}[f(X_{t})]=\int_{\Omega}f(y)p(t,x,y)dy,\quad t>0,\;x\in\Omega

where
\mathbf{E}_{x}
is the expectation with respect to
\mathbf{P}_{x}
. The function
u(t,x)=Q_{t}f(x)
solves the Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=Gu,\quad u_{0}=f\in D(G).

Proposition 2.2
.

Let us write

\displaystyle\kappa(z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}h(t,z)dt,\quad\textrm{and}\quad\ell(z)=l(z,0),\quad z>0.

Then,

and

are associated Sonine kernels. Moreover,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\kappa(z)\,\ell(1-z)\,dz=\int_{0}^{1}\kappa(z)\,\ell(1-z)\,dz=1.

Proof.

It is enough to consider the Laplace transforms of
\kappa
and

.
∎

We notice that, for a stable subordinator, for which
\Phi(z)=z^{\alpha}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{0}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda H_{t}}dt\right]=\frac{1}{\lambda^{\alpha}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}\frac{x^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}dx

from which we obtain the potential density of

, that is

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}h(t,x)dt=\frac{x^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}=:\kappa(x),\quad x>0.

The inverse of

gives

\displaystyle l(t,0)=\frac{t^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}=:\ell(t),\quad t>0.

Thus, formula (
2.8
) is verified. The convolutions
\kappa*u
and
\ell*u
can be used in order to define respectively the fractional integral and the fractional derivative of a given function

. Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{x}u(x):=\frac{d}{dx}\mathcal{I}^{1-\alpha}_{x}u(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\frac{d}{dx}\int_{0}^{x}u(y)(x-y)^{-\alpha}dy

is the well-known Riemann-Liouville derivative written in terms of the associated fractional integral. Notice that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{x}u(x)=\frac{d}{dx}(u*\ell)(x),\quad\mathcal{I}^{\alpha}_{x}u(x)=(u*\kappa)(x).

We also recall the Caputo-Dzherbashian derivative

\displaystyle D^{\alpha}_{x}u(x):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{x}u^{\prime}(s)(x-s)^{-\alpha}ds=\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}_{x}\big(u(x)-u(0)\big)

where
u^{\prime}=du/ds
. The Caputo-Dzherbashian derivative (also termed Caputo derivative) has been introduced by Caputo in
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
and previously by Dzherbashian in
[
34
,
35
]
. The Riemann-Liouville derivatives have a long history.

The previous operators are obtained by convolution and represent well-known objects in fractional calculus where
\alpha
has a clear meaning in terms of fractional order. Some criticisms have been also reported in the literature about the definition of derivative and integral for some operators (see for example
[
43
]
). In the following, we consider non-local operators for which we do not have a direct link with a fractional order but with a symbol
\Phi
and therefore, with a convolution kernel (singular or not, see the last section). We basically move from the following ”types” of operators:

i)

Marchaud (type) operators,

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{\Phi}_{x-}u(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(u(x)-u(x-y)\big)\phi(dy)

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{\Phi}_{x+}u(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(u(x)-u(x+y)\big)\phi(dy);

ii)

Riemann-Liouville (type) operators,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\Phi}_{x-}u(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\int_{-\infty}^{x}u(x-y)\overline{\phi}(y)dy

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\Phi}_{x+}u(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\int^{\infty}_{x}u(x-y)\overline{\phi}(y)dy;

iii)

Caputo-Dzherbashian (type) operators,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}u(t)=\int_{0}^{t}u^{\prime}(t-s)\overline{\phi}(y)dy

where
u^{\prime}=du/dt
is the derivative of
u(t)
.

These operators coincide in a certain class of functions. A key fact is played by the extension with zero for the negative part of the real line. That is, for those functions

for which
u(x)=0
for
x\leq 0
, a quick check shows that, under the right assumptions for existence of Laplace transforms in each definition, the Laplace symbols coincide.

with
p\geq 1
, we get

\forall\,t>0
(just by applying the Young’s inequality). Moreover, we observe that

and

may be regular more that

.

Let us write

(2.17)

\bar{h}_{f}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}h_{f}(t,x)dt\quad\textrm{and}\quad\bar{l}_{f}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}l_{f}(t,x)dt.

We can immediately check that the Abel (type) equation
f(x)=\mathbf{D}^{\Phi}_{x-}\bar{h}_{f}(x)
gives the elliptic problem associated with (
2.15
). On the other hand, the elliptic problem associated with (
2.16
) exists only if
\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\Phi(\lambda)/\lambda<\infty
. Such a fact is not surprising, indeed by considering

,

\displaystyle\bar{l}_{\mathbf{1}}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{E}_{0}[\mathbf{1}_{(t<H^{\Phi}_{x})}]dt=\mathbf{E}_{0}[H^{\Phi}_{x}]=x\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}.

In case the elliptic problem exists, it takes the form

\displaystyle f(x)=\left(\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\frac{\lambda}{\Phi(\lambda)}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bar{l}_{f}(x).

Now we move to the PDEs connection concerned respectively with non-local equations and non-local boundary conditions.

3.
Non-local initial value problems

First we recall that for

solving the problem

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=Gu,\quad u_{0}=f\in D(G)

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}[f(X_{t})]

where

on

on

as the composition

and

3.1.
Parabolic problems

Let us consider the time non-local Cauchy problem

\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{t}u=Gu,\qquad u_{0}=f\in D(G).

The probabilistic representation of the solution to (

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}[f(X^{H}_{t})]

3.2.
Elliptic problems

We proceed with a short discussion of results which will be useful below. The solution to

\displaystyle-Gu=f\quad on\ E

is given by

\displaystyle u(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\zeta}f(X_{s})ds\right]

where
\zeta
is the lifetime of
X
. We may write

\displaystyle u(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}Q_{t}f(x)dt

which plays an interesting role in the discussion of elliptic problems for time-changed processes (Proposition

and Proposition

). The reader should have in mind formula (
2.14
). If

, then

\displaystyle Q_{t}\mathbf{1}_{E}(x)=\mathbf{P}_{x}(X_{t}\in E)=\mathbf{P}_{x}(t<\tau_{E})

where

is the first exit time of
X
from

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{P}_{x}(t<\tau_{E})dt=\mathbf{E}_{x}[\tau_{E}]

is the mean exit time of
X
from

, that is the solution to

\displaystyle-Gu=\mathbf{1}_{E}.

We move to the non-local problems.

Concerning the process
X^{H}_{t}
, the solution to

\displaystyle\Phi(-G)u=\mathbf{1}_{E}

is given by

\displaystyle u(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}[\tau_{E}^{H}]

where
\tau_{E}^{H}:=\inf\{s>0\,:\,X^{H}_{s}\notin E\}
is the first exit time of
X^{H}
from
E
.

Concerning the process
X^{L}_{t}
, the solution to

\displaystyle-Gu=\Phi^{\prime}(0)\mathbf{1}_{E}

is given by

\displaystyle u(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}[\tau_{E}^{L}]

where
\tau_{E}^{L}:=\inf\{s>0\,:\,X^{L}_{s}\notin E\}
is the first exit time of
X^{L}
from
E
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{x}[\tau_{E}^{L}]=\Phi^{\prime}(0)\,\mathbf{E}_{x}[\zeta]

where
\zeta
is the lifetime of the base process
X_{t}
. If we have Dirichlet boundary condition for example, then
\zeta=\tau_{E}
. We recall that

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(0)=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}

is finite only in some cases and determines the delay of the base process in a sense to be better specified below. We discuss in detail this relation in the next section. Here we only anticipate the following cases as introductory examples. If we have
\Phi(\lambda)=\lambda^{\alpha}
, then the inverse
L_{t}
to a stable subordinator slows down

and the composition

turns out to be delayed. Indeed, we obtain that
\mathbf{E}_{x}[\zeta^{L}]=\infty
. On the other hand, for
a>0
,
b>0

\displaystyle\Phi(\lambda)=a\ln(1+\lambda/b)=\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-\lambda y})ae^{-by}\frac{dy}{y}

is the symbol of the gamma subordinator
H
with density

\mathbf{P}_{0}(H_{t}\in dx)=\frac{b^{at}}{\Gamma(at)}x^{at-1}e^{-bx}dx

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{0}[e^{-\lambda H_{t}}]=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}\mathbf{P}_{0}(H_{t}\in dx)=\left(1+\lambda/b\right)^{-at}=e^{-ta\ln(1+\lambda/b)}.

We have that

\displaystyle\Phi^{\prime}(0)=\frac{b}{a}<\infty

and the mean value of the lifetime
\zeta^{L}
is finite. The time-changed process

is known as variance gamma process or Laplace motion (see
[
21
]
and the references therein). It is a Lévy process with no diffusion component (a pure jump process), the increments are independent and follow a variance-gamma distribution, which is a generalization of the Laplace distribution (modified Bessel function

).

We start with the following definition given in

4.
An irregular domain

Our aim is to underline some relation between non-local operators and the “regularity” of the domains. Such a connection is given in terms of stochastic processes describing the motions in that domains. In order to be clear we present here a discussion based on a simple and instructive case, the fractal Koch domain.

5.1.
Singular kernels

For a bounded subset
\Omega

\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,x)=\Delta u(t,x),\quad t>0,\;x\in\Omega\\
\\
\displaystyle\eta D^{\alpha}_{t}u(t,x)+\sigma\partial_{\bf n}u(t,x)+c\,u(t,x)=0,\quad t>0,\;x\in\partial\Omega\\

\displaystyle u_{f}\to f\quad\textrm{as}\quad t\downarrow 0.

Then, we introduce the space

\displaystyle D_{L}=\left\{\varphi=\varphi_{f|_{\partial\Omega}}:\,\Big|\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}(t,x)\Big|\leq v(x)t^{\alpha-1},\,v\in L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)\right\}.

We also introduce the processes
\bar{X}=\{\bar{X}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}

\partial\Omega

\partial\Omega

on

(X^{+},\gamma)

5.2.
Non-singular kernels

We focus on a famous operator which has been deeply discussed in the last few years. Recently, in a series of papers starting from
[
15
]
, some authors considered the Caputo-Fabrizio operator (for a given constant
a
)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}^{(\alpha)}_{x}u(x)=\frac{M(\alpha)}{1-\alpha}\int_{a}^{x}u^{\prime}(\tau)\exp\left[-\frac{\alpha\,(x-\tau)}{1-\alpha}\right]d\tau

where
M(\alpha)
is a normalizing function such that
M(0)=M(1)=1
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
is the fractional order of the operator. The operator has been introduced as a fractional derivative although the proper definition of fractional operators is now stimulating a deep debate (
[
44
]
). However, the operator
\mathscr{D}^{(\alpha)}_{x}
can be regarded as a non-singular operator and can be included in the class of integro-differential operators. As pointed out in
[
15
]
, we have that
\mathscr{D}^{(\alpha)}_{x}u(x)\to u^{\prime}(x)
as
\alpha\to 1
and
\mathscr{D}^{(\alpha)}_{t}u(x)\to u(x)-u(a)
as
\alpha\to 0
. For the sake of simplicity we assume here that
a=0
and
u(0)=0
. Our results are concerned with the boundary value problem in which the boundary-operator

(5.12)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}^{(\alpha)}_{x}u+c\alpha\,u=0,\qquad c\geq 0

is considered. As a consequence of the properties illustrated before for the Caputo-Fabrizio operator, as
\alpha\to 0
or
\alpha\to 1
we respectively have

(5.13)

\displaystyle u(x)=0\qquad\textrm{or}\qquad u^{\prime}(x)=-c\,u(x)

in place of (
5.12
). The conditions (
5.13
) respectively correspond to Dirichlet or Robin condition. For
\alpha\in[0,1]
and
c\geq 0
, we consider the solution to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=Gu,\quad u_{0}=f

with

\displaystyle(\mathscr{D}^{(\alpha)}_{x}u+c\alpha\,u)|_{x=0}=0

where
G
is the generator of a Markov process on
[0,\infty)
.

In
[
15
]
, the authors provided some properties of
\mathscr{D}^{(\alpha)}_{x}u(x)
. For
a=0
for instance, the Laplace transform reads as follows (as usual we denote by
\widetilde{u}
the Laplace transform of
u
)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}\mathscr{D}^{(\alpha)}_{x}u(x)\,dx=

\displaystyle\frac{M(\alpha)}{1-\alpha}\left(\lambda\widetilde{u}(\lambda)-u(0)\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x}e^{-\frac{\alpha\,x}{1-\alpha}}dx

(5.14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M(\alpha)\frac{1}{\alpha+\lambda(1-\alpha)}\left(\lambda\widetilde{u}(\lambda)-u(0)\right).

From now on, we assume that

(5.15)

\displaystyle M(\alpha)=1\quad\textrm{and}\quad a=0.

The Laplace transform (
5.14
) corresponds to the Laplace transform of the fractional operator
\mathfrak{D}^{\Phi}_{x}
with Lèvy symbol and measure

(5.16)

\displaystyle\Phi(\lambda)=(\theta+1)\frac{\lambda}{\theta+\lambda}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\bar{\phi}((y,\infty))=(\theta+1)e^{-\theta y},\quad\theta=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}

where, as a quick check shows,

\displaystyle\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}=\frac{1}{\alpha}.

From a probability view-point, the symbol
\Phi
characterizes a subordinator
H
, that is a non negative, non-decreasing process. Notice that,
\bar{\phi}((0,\infty))<\infty
if
\alpha<1
and the process
H_{t}
is a step-process, its trajectories are not strictly increasing. The inverse to a subordinator can be defined as

\displaystyle L_{t}=\inf\{s\geq 0\,:\,H_{s}>t\},\quad t>0

from which we also obtain the relation

(5.17)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}_{0}(H_{t}<x)=\mathbf{P}_{0}(L_{x}>t),\quad x,t>0.

As
\alpha\to 1
we obtain
\Phi(\lambda)=\lambda
as expected, the process
H
corresponds to the elementary subordinator
H_{t}=t
. We recall that
L_{t}
may have plateaus only if the process
H_{t}
may have jumps (at least for
\alpha\neq 1
). Since
H_{t}
has non-decreasing paths, the inverse
L_{t}
may have jumps (
L
is not continuous). Thus,
H
is not strictly increasing and
L_{t}
can not be continuous. As
\alpha\to 0
, the ratio
\Phi(\lambda)/\lambda
gives an infinite mean life time and therefore a delayed process on the boundary. This actually corresponds to an absorption at
x=0
whereas the Dirichlet condition suggests a kill. We still have (
[
12
]
)

(5.18)

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}_{0}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}f(L_{t})dt\right]=\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}\mathbf{E}_{0}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda H_{t}}f(t)dt\right].

Thus, for
f=\mathbf{1}_{D}
with
D\subseteq(0,\infty)
,

(5.19)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\mathbf{P}_{0}(L_{t}\in D)dt=\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}\int_{D}e^{-t\Phi(\lambda)}dt.

We also write

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\mathbf{P}_{0}(L_{t}\in dx)dt=\frac{\Phi(\lambda)}{\lambda}e^{-x\Phi(\lambda)}dx

and the density
l
of
L
solves

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}^{(\alpha)}_{t}l=-\partial_{x}l

where
L
is an inverse of
H
with symbol
\Phi(\lambda)=(\theta+1)\frac{\lambda}{\theta+\lambda}
. It can be easily shown that
H
is a compound Poisson process. Indeed, consider the process

\displaystyle H_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{N_{t}}Y_{k}

where
N_{t}\sim Pois((\theta+1)t)
and
Y_{k}
are i.i.d. random variables with
Y_{1}\sim Exp(\theta)
,
\theta>0
. For the reader’s convenience, we recall that, for
N_{t}\perp Y_{1}
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[e^{-\lambda H_{t}}]=

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{N_{t}}e^{-\lambda Y_{1}}\right]=\mathbf{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{E}[e^{-\lambda Y_{1}}]\right)^{N_{t}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\exp\left(-(\theta+1)t\left(1-\mathbf{E}[e^{-\lambda Y_{1}}]\right)\right).

Thus, it turns out that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[e^{-\lambda H_{t}}]=\exp\left(-t(\theta+1)\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-\lambda y})\theta e^{-\theta y}dy\right),\quad\lambda>0.

Our focus is on the boundary condition

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}^{(\alpha)}_{x}u(t,0)+c\alpha u(t,0)=0

corresponding to the drifted process
H_{t}+c\alpha t=:\bar{H}_{t}
. The process
\bar{H}_{t}
may have jumps, never plateaus except for
c=0
. This process is therefore strictly increasing with continuous inverse
\bar{H}_{t}^{-1}
for which (see also
[
7
]
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}(\bar{H}^{-1}_{t}>s)=\mathbf{P}(t<\bar{H}_{s})=\mathbf{P}(t-c\alpha s<H_{s})

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda t}\mathbf{P}(\bar{H}^{-1}_{t}>s)dt=\frac{1}{\lambda}e^{-s(c\alpha\lambda+(\theta+1)\frac{\lambda}{\theta+\lambda})},\quad\lambda>0.

By considering the symbol
\Phi(\lambda)=c\alpha\lambda+\frac{\theta+1}{\theta+\lambda}\lambda
we have the (space) non-local boundary condition discussed in the previous section. The interested reader can also consult
[
6
]
for a discussion on this operator.

Many other cases can be considered. Indeed, the operator

(5.20)

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}^{\Phi}_{x}u(x):=\int_{0}^{x}u^{\prime}\mathscr{K}(x-\tau)d\tau

with the non-singular kernel
\mathscr{K}
can be associated with a compound Poisson process as above. Let us consider
\mathbf{P}(Y_{k}\in dy)=f_{Y_{1}}(y)dy
for the jumps from the boundary where
Y_{k}
takes values in some set
supp(Y_{k})=supp(f_{Y_{1}})\subset(0,\infty)
. We do not consider negative jumps. Then, we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[e^{-\lambda H_{t}}]=\exp\left(-t(\theta+1)\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(1-e^{-\lambda y}\big)f_{Y_{1}}(y)dy\right),\quad\lambda>0

and, for
u
such that
u(x)=0
as
x\leq 0
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}^{\Phi}_{x}u(x)=

\displaystyle(\theta+1)\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(u(x)-u(x-y)\big)f_{Y_{1}}(y)dy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\theta+1)\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{Y_{1}}(y)\int_{0}^{y}\frac{du}{dz}(x-z)dzdy

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(\theta+1)\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{du}{dz}(x-z)\int_{z}^{\infty}f_{Y_{1}}(y)dy\,dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\theta+1)\int_{0}^{x}u^{\prime}(x-z)\,\mathbf{P}(Y_{1}>z)\,dz.

Thus, straightforward calculations lead to (
5.20
) with non-singular kernel

\displaystyle\mathscr{K}(z)=(\theta+1)\,\mathbf{P}(Y_{1}>z),\quad\theta>-1.

A quick example is given by
Y_{1}
distributed as a Mittag-Leffler random variable for which
\mathbf{P}(Y_{1}>y)=E_{\alpha}(-ry^{\alpha})
with
r>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
. It is well-known that for
\alpha=1
we have
Y_{1}\sim Exp(r)
obtaining the case discussed above.
