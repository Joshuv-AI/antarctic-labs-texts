On the Number of Maximum Inner Distance Latin Squares
by Omar A. Garcia

Abstract
The inner distance of a Latin square was defined by myself and six others during an REU in the Summer of 2020 at Moravian College. Since then, I have been curious about its possible connections to other combinatorial mathematics. The inner distance of a matrix is the minimum value of the distance between entries in adjacent cells, where our distance metric is distance modulo n n . Intuitively, one expects that most Latin squares have inner distance 1, for example there probably exists a pair of adjacent cells with consecutive integers. And very few should have maximum inner distance; the maximum inner distance was found by construction for all n ≥ 3 n\geq 3 to be exactly ⌊ n − 1 2 ⌋ \left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor . In this paper we also establish existence for all smaller inner distances. Much of our introductory work is showcased in [ 1 ] , with a primary focus on determining the maximum inner distance for Latin squares and Sudoku Latin squares. In this paper, we focus on enumerating maximum inner distance squares, and calculated the exact number for any n n : Theorem . The number of Latin squares of maximum inner distance, denoted | M ​ i ​ D ​ L ​ S ​ ( n ) | |MiDLS(n)| , equals: | M ​ i ​ D ​ L ​ S ​ ( n ) | = { 4 ​ n if n is odd n ⁡ ( P ​ ( n ) 2 + 2 ​ n ) if n is even |MiDLS(n)|=\begin{cases}4n&\text{if $n$ is odd}\\ n(P(n)^{2}+2n)&\text{if $n$ is even}\end{cases} Where P ⁡ ( n ) = 1 4 ​ n 2 + 2 P(n)=\frac{1}{4}n^{2}+2 if n ≡ 0 ( mod 4 ) n\equiv 0\pmod{4} , and P ⁡ ( n ) = 1 4 ​ n 2 + 1 P(n)=\frac{1}{4}n^{2}+1 if n ≡ 2 ( mod 4 ) n\equiv 2\pmod{4} . The odd case is easy, shown in [ 1 ] . The result for evens is computed in this paper by a connection to graph theory, and direct computation.

To avoid confusion about numbers modulo

The

The

of a Latin rectangle

Enumerating squares of any inner distance is a difficult problem in general: surprisingly the total number of Latin squares of order
n
is only known up to
n=11
. See
[
2
]
for the exact number. Another focus of this paper is formally introducing tools for studying inner distance. Here are a few open questions I would like to see answered: for any fixed order
n
, does the number of squares of inner distance
k
always decrease as
k
increases? What structures (algebraic, combinatorial, etc.) correspond to a Latin square of inner distance
k
? What effect does inner distance have on structures induced by Latin squares?

Here is a general roadmap of this paper: section
2
provides all the necessary (new) tools and definitions for understanding the results in the paper. In particular we define difference rows and difference matrices, which are an equivalent way to think about Latin rows (and thus columns) or Latin squares. In section
3
we specialize to the case where
n
is even and the inner distance is maximized, and compute how many possible difference rows there are by classifying them all. This gives the number of Latin rows of maximum inner distance. Then by studying this classification, we slowly prove that every Latin square of maximum inner distance is either a circulant or back circulant, or something we call a row product.

Theorem 2.1
.

Addition is an equivalence relation on
\mathrm{LS}(n,k)
, with classes of size
n
.

Proof.

Addition preserves inner distance as it preserves distances between adjacent cells, and so is a well defined operation on
\mathrm{LS}(n,k)
. Let
L\sim M
if
M
is an addition of
L
. Then the relation
\sim
satisfies:

1.

Reflexive:
L=L+n
by definition, so
L\sim L
.

2.

Symmetry: If
M=L+i
, then
L=M+(n-i)
, for
i\neq n
, so
M\sim L\iff L\sim M

3.

Transitive: If
M=L+i
, and
N=M+j
, then
N=L+r
where
r\equiv i+j
. Therefore
L\sim M
and
M\sim N
imply
L\sim N
.

Since
L+i\neq L
if
i\neq n
, addition provides equivalence classes of
\mathrm{LS}(n,k)
, each having order
n
. Therefore
n
divides
|\mathrm{LS}(n,k)|
.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

Given any
x\in[1,n]
, and any cell
(i,j)

if and only if there exists a square of inner distance
k

m_{i,j}=x
.

In particular we may assume
m_{1,1}=1
within proofs.

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose
X
is a set of Latin squares such that symbol permutation is a closed operation on
X
. If
m
is the maximum inner distance of
X
, there exists squares of inner distance
k
for all
k\in[1,m]
.

Proof.

The proof is constructive. Suppose the maximum inner distance
m
over
X
is greater than 1. This means there exists a Latin square
L
with inner distance
m>1
, and no square in
X
with inner distance larger than
m
. Within
L
there exists a pair of adjacent symbols with distance
m
, say
x
and
x+m\pmod{n}
. Since symbol permutation is a closed operation, by addition of Latin squares we may assume
x=n
, so that
n
is adjacent to
m
.

The permutation swap
\sigma=(n,1)
, creates a square
\sigma(L)=M\in X
. We wish to show the inner distance of
M
is
m-1
. We do so by checking the distance of every pair of adjacent symbols.

Suppose
y_{1},y_{2}
are adjacent symbols in
L
, thus
\mathrm{dist}(y_{1},y_{2})\geq m
by assumption.

1) If
y_{1},y_{2}\not\in\{1,n\}
, then
y_{1},y_{2}
are unaffected by
\sigma
and are still adjacent in
M
.

2) We cannot have
\{y_{1},y_{2}\}=\{1,n\}
, otherwise
L
has inner distance 1, contradicting
m>1
.

3) Suppose without loss of generality (WLOG),
y_{1}=n
and
y_{2}\neq 1
. Since
\mathrm{dist}(n,y_{2})\geq k
, we have
k\leq y_{2}\leq n-k
, and thus
k-1\leq y_{2}-1\leq n-k-1
. Therefore
\mathrm{dist}(y_{2},1)\geq k-1
. A similar argument holds if
y_{1}=1
and
y_{2}\neq n
.

In particular, a cyclic permutation of consecutive integers
(x,x+1)
changes the inner distance by at most
\pm 1
. In our example, the symbols 1 and
m
are adjacent in

, so

has inner distance
m-1
. The argument only assumes

, so the same holds for all
k\in[2,m]
, thus there exists squares in
X
of any inner distance in
[1,m]
.
∎

Proof.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

be an extended difference row of maximum inner distance. The following patterns never show up as subsequences:

1.

Coming back through the same edge, meaning

2.

Two zeroes bounding

1’s, with

-1’s to either side,
unless

OR

:

(\underbrace{-1,\dots,-1}_{m_{1}^{\prime}},0,\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{m},0,\underbrace{-1,\dots,-1}_{m_{2}^{\prime}})\implies m>m^{\prime}=m_{1}^{\prime}+m_{2}^{\prime}

3.

q
sequences of 1’s with
q-1

\frac{n}{2}
total 1’s,
unless
(1)

and

is odd, or (2)

(\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{m_{1}},0,\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{m_{2}},0,\dots,0,\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{m_{q}})\qquad\text{Where $\sum_{i=1}^{q}m_{i}=\frac{n}{2}$.}

Further, if a zero is attached to the front or back, the sequence is never allowed:

(0,\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{m_{1}},0,\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{m_{2}},0,\dots,0,\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{m_{q}})\qquad(\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{m_{1}},0,\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{m_{2}},0,\dots,0,\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{m_{q}},0)

Proof.

We will call these Rules 1-3. Rule 1 is obvious:
\epsilon_{j}
correspond to taking the edge that ‘adds’

. Therefore, two zeroes in a row, or a 1 following a -1 (or vice versa) correspond to travelling back the same path. Rules 2 and 3 are a generalization: the patterns shown amount to a net difference of 0 between the start and the end of the subsequence, which is only possible if the subsequence is the whole row.

Rule 2: Suppose that
m^{\prime}
-1’s surround two zeroes and
m
1’s. If
m^{\prime}\geq m
, then take a sum of horizontal differences

between two indices

that contain

many -1’s. This amounts to a difference of

, computed by adding the number of -1’s, 0’s, and 1’s. By Lemma

, this is only true if

, so then

.

and

\sum h_{j}\equiv\frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{n}{2}+1\right)+(q-1)\frac{n}{2}=\frac{n}{2}\left(\frac{n}{2}+q-1\right)

This evaluates to zero when

is even, which is true if

and

is odd or if

and

to the partial sum, inducing a difference of zero in the remaining cases, so such a pattern always fails.

.

Step 1

Proof.

Point 0. is true by the classification of cycles, theorem

Theorem 3.7
.

Let

be even, and let

denote the number of Hamiltonian paths in the maximum distance graph. If

.

Proof.

We count how many of each type there are. By Theorem

, there are 2 paths with

for all
n
.

Suppose

\mathbf{n=4k}
: Note there is a unique type 1 row for each possible odd value of
h
in
[1,\frac{n}{2}-1]
, plus the additional row with

,
(0,-1,0,-1,\dots,-1,0,\frac{n}{2}-1)
. So there are

.

(\frac{n}{2}-h)

The 2 coefficient comes from the fact that we need to also count those with

(\text{type 1's})=k+1=\frac{n+2}{4}

3.4
Counting Latin Squares of Maximum Inner Distance

For the rest of the paper, the set of Latin squares of maximum inner distance
\left\lfloor\frac{n-1}{2}\right\rfloor
will be denoted
\mathrm{MiD}(n)
. Assume
L\in\mathrm{MiD}(n)
and as usual
H,V
denote its horizontal and vertical difference matrices. We will overload some notation:
d_{1},d_{2},\dots,d_{n}
denote both the extended difference rows of rows
1,2,\dots,n
, and rows
1,2,\dots,n
of the horizontal difference matrix
H
, which should be clear in context.
d_{i}=(\epsilon_{i,1},\epsilon_{i,2},\dots,\epsilon_{i,n})
, with
\epsilon_{i,n}
denoting the value previously denoted
h
.

There is one more piece to the puzzle: we prove every even ordered Latin square of maximum inner distance is either a product of rows, a circulant, or a back-circulant. We show this is by using the Classification Theorem
3.6
to study how rows behave as neighbors in a Latin square of maximum inner distance.

Definition
.

Let
d_{*}
and
d_{*}^{\prime}
be extended difference rows of maximum inner distance.
d_{*}
and
d_{*}^{\prime}
are

if their induced rows

and

may form a

Latin rectangle with of maximum inner distance.

Occasionally we may also describe

and

as neighbors. For example, in a row product
\prod(d_{*},d_{*}^{\prime})
every row has extended difference row
d_{*}
. So
d_{*}
is its own neighbor. The following rows we will call by name since they are frequently referenced:

1.

The type 1 row with

that
alternates
between in the first
n-1

(0,1,0,\dots,1,0,1-\frac{n}{2})
will be known as

.

2.

The

(1,\dots,1,0,-1,\dots,-1,0)
will be called

.

Much of the theorems in this section are based on the following lemma, relating the horizontal differences within neighbors:

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
d_{*}=(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\dots,\epsilon_{n-1},h)
and
d_{*}^{\prime}=(\epsilon_{1}^{\prime},\epsilon_{2}^{\prime},\dots,\epsilon_{n-1}^{\prime},h^{\prime})

\left|\sum_{j=j_{1}}^{j_{2}}(\epsilon_{j}^{\prime}-\epsilon_{j})\right|\leq 2

or

.

Proof.

The proof falls immediately from Lemma
2.7
Condition 1:

h_{i,j}+v_{i,j+1}\equiv v_{i,j}+h_{i+1,j}\pmod{n}\implies v_{i,j+1}-v_{i,j}\equiv h_{i+1,j}-h_{i,j}\pmod{n}

Which then generalizes to the following:

v_{i,j_{1}}-v_{i,j_{2}}\equiv\sum_{j=j_{1}}^{j_{2}-1}(h_{i,j}-h_{i+1,j})=\sum_{j=j_{1}}^{j_{2}-1}(\epsilon_{i,j}-\epsilon_{i+1,j})\pmod{n}

(6)

Where here and from now on

. In our case

. Assuming

and

are neighbors means that for all valid

we have

; it follows that

, and the result follows for

. The final sentence in the lemma follows from Lemma

Note that

, so for

without contradiction. Note that

always holds, so we must have a case like:

1
\dots
1
-1
\dots
-1

where the entries in between (
\dots
) are equal on both rows. I leave to the reader to verify this tiny case is always a contradiction.
∎

A sneaky use of the Classification Theorem

Lemma 3.9
.

Suppose that

.

Every pair of non-equal neighbors is determined. Further,

Theorem 3.17
.

Every Latin square of even order
n\geq 6
with maximum inner distance is a circulant or back-circulant Latin square, or is a product of rows of maximum inner distance. The number of Latin squares of maximum inner distance is given by
n(P(n)^{2}+2n)
, where:

P(n)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{4}n^{2}+2&\text{if $n=4k$}\\
\frac{1}{4}n^{2}+1&\text{if $n=4k+2$}\end{cases}

is the number of rows of maximum inner distance beginning in the symbol 1.

Proof.

Theorems
3.6
establishes that every row of maximum inner distance can be classified as a type 1 row, type 2 row, or is the cyclic row known as Row C. Some type 2 rows are
cyclic rows
. Theorem
3.7
counts this number as
P(n)
given in the theorem statement above. Lemma
3.14
showed that any pair of non-equal neighboring rows is
determined
, which allowed lemmas
3.12
and
3.15
to show that if a Latin square of maximum inner distance
L
contained a type 1, then
L
is a row product. Theorem
3.16
did the same for non-cyclic type 2’s, and further if
L
contains a cyclic row, it is a row product or a circulant or back-circulant.
∎
