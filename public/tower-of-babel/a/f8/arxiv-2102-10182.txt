Keyboards as a New Model of Computation
Yoan GÉRAN, Bastien LABOUREIX, Corto MASCLE, Valentin D. RICHARD

Abstract
We introduce a new formalisation of language computation, called keyboards. We consider a set of atomic operations (writing a letter, erasing a letter, going to the right or to the left) and we define a keyboard as a set of finite sequences of such operations, called keys. The generated language is the set of words obtained by applying some non-empty sequence of those keys. Unlike classical models of computation, every key can be applied anytime. We define various classes of languages based on different sets of atomic operations, and compare their expressive powers. We also compare them to rational, context-free and context-sensitive languages. We obtain a strict hierarchy of classes, whose expressiveness is orthogonal to the one of the aforementioned classical models. We also study closure properties of those classes, as well as fundamental complexity problems on keyboards.

Introduction

We present a new formalisation of languages, called keyboards. A keyboard
K
is a finite set of keys, which are finite sequences of atomic operations, such as writing or erasing a letter, going one position to the right or to the left… The language of
K
is the set of words obtained by applying a sequence of its keys on an initially empty writing space.

This idea of studying the set generated by a set of algebraic operations is far from new: many works exist on the sets generated by a subset of elements of an algebraic structure, for instance in the context of semigroup and group theory
[
3
,
8
]
, of matrix monoids
[
2
,
10
]
or the theory of codes
[
4
]
. There is however, to the best of the author’s knowledge, no previous work on a model resembling the one presented here.

The atomic operations we use in this paper are the base of other models of computation, such as forgetting automata and erasing automata
[
5
,
6
,
11
]
. The use of those operations was originally to simulate some analysis strategies in linguistics. As a first study of the model, we chose the actions of the operations (backspace and arrows) to behave like an actual keyboard in a text editor.

We can define various classes of languages based on the set of atomic operations we consider, and compare their expressive powers between them, and to well-known classes of languages. We obtain a strict hierarchy of classes, with a wide range of expressiveness and difficulty of comprehension. The expressiveness of keyboards seems to be overall orthogonal to the ones of classical models of computation, which we explain by two key differences with the latter.

First, keyboards are blind and memoryless, in that they do not have states and cannot read the tape at any point. Second, because of this weakness, we can allow operations such as moving in the word or erasing letters without blowing up their expressive power too much.

The main interests of keyboards are: 1. to obtain many deep and complex mathematical questions from a deceptively simple model, and 2. that their expressiveness is very different from the ones of classical models. A language that is simple to express with a keyboard may be more complicated for automata, and vice versa. This paper is meant as a first step in the study and comprehension of keyboards and their languages.

The paper is organised as follows.
In Sections 2 and 3 we establish notations and basic definitions. Section 4 and 5 are dedicated to building properties and tools necessary to the study of keyboards. In Section 6 we dive into the specific properties of each keyboard class, and prove the inclusions of some of them in regular, context-free and context-sensitive languages. Then in Section 7, we study the inclusions between those classes, in particular showing that they are all different. Some complexity results are given in Section 8. Finally, in Section 9 we show that keyboard classes are not stable by union or intersection, and that some (but not all) of them are stable by mirror.

All proofs can be found in the appendices.

4
General properties

In this section, we establish some properties on keyboard. Although most of them are quite intuitive, we take the time to be as formal as possible in order to build solid bases for the study of keyboards.

Our first lemma states that applying a key can only affect a bounded part of the word around the cursor.

Lemma 24

(Locality)
.

Let
t=\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{n}
be a key. If
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle\xrightarrow{t}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u^{\prime}\middle|v^{\prime}}}\right\rangle
, then
u[1,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert u}}\right\rvert-n]
is a prefix of
u^{\prime}
and
v[n+1,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert v}}\right\rvert]
is a suffix of
v^{\prime}
.

Furthermore,
u^{\prime}[1,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert u^{\prime}}}\right\rvert-n]
is a prefix of
u
and
v^{\prime}[n+1,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert v^{\prime}}}\right\rvert]
is a suffix of
v
.

Then we formalize the fact that if the cursor is far enough from the extremities of the word then we do not have edge effects.

Lemma 25

(Effectiveness far from the edges)
.

Let

be a key,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle
a configuration and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{n}\middle|v_{n}}}\right\rangle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle\cdot t
. If

, then

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle\xrightarrow{t}_{\text{e}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{n}\middle|v_{n}}}\right\rangle
, meaning that all the arrows and backspaces are applied effectively.

The two next lemmas bound the variation in length of the configuration when applying a key.

Lemma 26

(Bounds on the lengths)
.

Let
t=\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{n}
be a key,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle

a configuration and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{n}\middle|v_{n}}}\right\rangle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle\cdot t
. Then

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert uv}}\right\rvert-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert t}}\right\rvert_{\mathord{\leftarrow}}+\sum_{x\in A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert t}}\right\rvert_{x}\penalty\ \penalty\ \leq\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert u_{n}v_{n}}}\right\rvert\penalty\ \penalty\ \leq\penalty\ \penalty\ \mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert uv}}\right\rvert+\sum_{x\in A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert t}}\right\rvert_{x}.

In particular
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\vphantom{\frac{a}{b}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert u_{n}v_{n}}}\right\rvert-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert uv}}\right\rvert}}\right\rvert\leq n
.
Moreover
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\vphantom{\frac{a}{b}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert u_{n}}}\right\rvert-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert u}}\right\rvert}}\right\rvert\leq n
and

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert\vphantom{\frac{a}{b}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert v_{n}}}\right\rvert-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert v}}\right\rvert}}\right\rvert\leq n
.

Lemma 27

(Length evolution without left edge effects)
.

Let
t=\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{n}
be a key,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle
a configuration such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert u}}\right\rvert\geq n
. Let
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{n}\middle|v_{n}}}\right\rangle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle\cdot t
, then

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert u_{n}v_{n}}}\right\rvert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert uv}}\right\rvert-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert t}}\right\rvert_{\mathord{\leftarrow}}+\sum_{x\in A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert t}}\right\rvert_{x}.

Then, we obtain the following lemma that can be used to show that some languages
are not recognized by a keyboard.

Lemma 28
.

Let
K
be a keyboard with language
L
. Let
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell_{n}}}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

be the sequence obtained by sorting the lengths of the words in

by increasing order.
Then
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell_{n+1}-\ell_{n}}}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded by

.

Example 29
.

The languages
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a^{n^{2}}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}n\in\mathbb{N}}}\right\}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a^{p}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}p\text{ prime}}}\right\}

are not recognized by a keyboard.

The two following lemmas will be useful when studying effective executions.

Lemma 30

Let

5
Key behaviour

This section aims at providing tools to describe the behaviour of a key. How can we formally express the intuitive fact that the
i
th
symbol of
c\cdot t

was written by
t
or that the
i
th
symbol of
c
was moved by
t
?
We are going to distinguish letters from
t
and
c
in order to keep track of where
t
writes its letters and how the letters of
c
were affected.

Definition 32

(Tracking function)
.

Let
\mathbb{Z}_{t}
and
\mathbb{Z}_{c}
be two duplicates of
\mathbb{Z}
.
We denote by
\overline{k}
the elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{c}
and by
\widehat{k}

the elements of
\mathbb{Z}_{t}
.

We define the tracking functions, one for keys

f_{t}\colon S^{*}\to\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S\cup\mathbb{Z}_{t}}}\right)^{*}
, defined as follows:

f_{t}(\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{n})=\sigma^{\prime}_{1}\ldots\sigma^{\prime}_{n}
where

\sigma^{\prime}_{i}=\begin{cases}\widehat{i}&\text{if $\sigma_{i}\in A$}\\
\sigma_{i}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

and one for configurations
f_{c}\colon\operatorname{\mathcal{C}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)\to\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{c}\times\mathbb{Z}^{*}_{c}
defined by

f_{c}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{1}\ldots a_{k},b_{1}\ldots b_{j}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\overline{-k}\ldots\overline{-1}\middle|\overline{1}\ldots\overline{j}}}\right\rangle.

By applying
f_{t}(t)
to
f_{c}(c)
, we can keep track of which letters of the configuration and of the key were written, erased, or displaced, and where.
We need two copies of
\mathbb{Z}
to differentiate between the symbols of
f_{t}(t)
(added by the key) and
f_{c}(c)
(already in the configuration).

Definition 33
.

Let
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle
be a configuration and
t
a key.
We note
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u^{\prime}\middle|v^{\prime}}}\right\rangle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle\cdot t
and

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle x\middle|y}}\right\rangle=f_{c}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u,v}}\right)\cdot f_{t}(t)
.
We say that
t
writes its
k
th
symbol at position
i

from
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle
if
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle x\middle|y}}\right\rangle_{i}=\widehat{k}
.

Remark 34
.

Let
t=\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{n}
be a key,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle
a
configuration and
1\leq j<k\leq n
integers. Then
t
writes
its
k
th
symbol at position
i
from
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle
if and only if
\sigma_{j+1}\ldots\sigma_{n}
writes its
(k-j)
th
symbol at position
i

from
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle\cdot\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{j}
.
In particular,
t
writes its
k
th
symbol at position
i
from

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle
if and only if
\sigma_{k}\ldots\sigma_{n}
writes its
1
st
symbol from

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle\cdot\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{k-1}
.

We defined an intuitive notion of writing the
k
th
symbol of
t
.
In particular, if
t
writes its
k
th
symbol in
i
th

position from
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle
, then
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u^{\prime}\middle|v^{\prime}}}\right\rangle_{i}=t_{k}
, as stated below.

Proposition 35
.

Let
t=\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{n}
be a key,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle
a
configuration. We note

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{n}\middle|v_{n}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle\cdot t\qquad

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle x_{n}^{\prime}\middle|y_{n}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=f_{c}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u,v}}\right)\cdot t

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{n}^{\prime}\middle|v_{n}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle\cdot f_{t}(t)\qquad

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle x_{n}\middle|y_{n}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=f_{c}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u,v}}\right)\cdot f_{t}(t)

Then
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert u_{n}}}\right\rvert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x_{n}}}\right\rvert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert u_{n}^{\prime}}}\right\rvert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert x_{n}^{\prime}}}\right\rvert

and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert v_{n}}}\right\rvert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert y_{n}}}\right\rvert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert v_{n}^{\prime}}}\right\rvert=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert y_{n}^{\prime}}}\right\rvert
.
And for all

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{n}\middle|v_{n}}}\right\rangle_{j}=a

\displaystyle\text{ or }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle x_{n}\middle|y_{n}}}\right\rangle_{j}=\widehat{k}\text{ and }t_{k}=a

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{n}^{\prime}\middle|v_{n}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle_{j}=a

\displaystyle\text{ or }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u_{n}^{\prime}\middle|v_{n}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle_{j}=\widehat{k}\text{ and }t_{k}=a

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle x_{n}^{\prime}\middle|y_{n}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle_{j}=\overline{k}\text{ and }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle_{k}=a

\displaystyle\text{ or }\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle x_{n}^{\prime}\middle|y_{n}^{\prime}}}\right\rangle_{j}=a

\displaystyle\text{ (iff $a$ already in configuration}

\displaystyle\text{ or $a$ added by $t$).}

Tracking functions are a convenient formalism to show some results on keyboards.

6
Characterisation of the classes

6.1
Languages of
\mathsf{BEK}
(without the arrows)

To begin, we study keyboards that do not contains any arrows.

6.1.1
\mathsf{MK}
and
\mathsf{EK}

\mathsf{MK}
and
\mathsf{EK}
are quite easy to understand. Indeed, since a key of a
minimal keyboard
K
is just a word on
A
,
K\subset A^{*}
.

Remark 47
.

Let
K=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}}}\right\}
be a minimal keyboard.
Then
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K}}\right)=(w_{1}+\cdots+w_{n})^{+}
.

\mathsf{EK}
languages are rather similar.

Lemma 48
.

Let
K=(T,F)
be a
\mathsf{EK}
keyboard. Then
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K}}\right)=T^{*}F
and this
regular expression can be computed in
O(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert K}}\right\rvert)
.

Thus, we can build in linear time a regular expression that recognizes
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K}}\right)
.

6.1.2
\mathsf{BK}
and
\mathsf{BEK}

As a
\mathsf{BEK}
keyboard has no
\mathord{\blacktriangleleft}
operation, the right component of
a configuration in an execution of
K\in\mathsf{BEK}
(starting from

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle\varepsilon\middle|\varepsilon}}\right\rangle
) is always empty. Thus, in this part we will sometimes
denote
u
for the configuration
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|\varepsilon}}\right\rangle
.

Some of the expressiveness of
\mathsf{BEK}
comes from edge effects.
For instance, finite languages are recognized by
\mathsf{BK}
keyboards.

Example 49
.

Let
L
be a finite language and
M=\operatorname*{max}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w}}\right\rvert\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}w\in L}}\right\}
.
Then
L
is recognized by
K=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathord{\leftarrow}^{M}w\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}w\in L}}\right\}
.

These edge effects could make
\mathsf{BEK}
languages quite complex.

Lemma 50

(Normal form)
.

Let
t\in S^{*}
be a key from
\mathsf{BEK}
. Then there exist
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
w\in A^{*}
such that
t\sim\mathord{\leftarrow}^{m}w
. Further,
m
and
w
can be computed from
t
in polynomial time.

Using this normal form, we understand that the action of a
\mathsf{BEK}
key always consists
in deleting a bounded number of letters at the end of the word,
then adding a bounded number of letters. This reminds us of stacks. Following this intuition, we can easily
encode the behavior of a
\mathsf{BEK}
keyboard into a pushdown automaton.

However,
\mathsf{BEK}
is even more narrow since all languages of
\mathsf{BEK}
are regular.

Theorem 51
.

Let
K
be a
\mathsf{BEK}
keyboard. Then,
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}(K)
is regular and we can build
an NFA
\operatorname{\mathcal{A}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K}}\right)
recognizing
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K}}\right)
in polynomial time.

Remark 52
.

There are several ways to prove this result. One of them is to apply the pushdown automaton construction from the proof of
\cref
thm-grek_dans_alg
(presented later in the paper) in the particular case of
\mathsf{BEK}
. The language of the
\mathsf{BEK}
keyboard is then essentially the stack language of this automaton. A slight adaptation of the classical proof that the stack language of a pushdown automaton is rational then yields the result.

We choose to include another proof in this work, as it is elementary, not much longer than the one aforementioned, and seems more elegant to the authors.

6.2
Languages of
\mathsf{BLEK}
(without the right arrow)

In this section, we allow the use of
\mathord{\blacktriangleleft}
. With this symbol, we have the possibility to move
into the word, and then erase or write letters. It opens a new complexity level.

Moreover, we provide a non-regular language of
\mathsf{BLEK}
, hence showing that it is more expressive than
\mathsf{BEK}
(see
\cref
thm-rek_in_rat
).

Example 53
.

Let
K=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{aa\mathord{\blacktriangleleft},bb\mathord{\blacktriangleleft}}}\right\}
. Then,
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{u\widetilde{u}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}u\in(a+b)^{+}}}\right\}
,
that is,
K
recognizes the non-empty palindromes of even length.

Thus, we can represent context-free non-regular languages with

\mathsf{BLEK}
(one can observe that the keyboard of
\cref
ex:palindrome
is actually even in
\mathsf{LK}
).

However, a basic observation helps us to understand the behaviour of a key of
\mathsf{BLEK}
:
as we do not have the symbol
\mathord{\blacktriangleright}
, we cannot go back to the right and all
the letters to the right of the cursor are written forever. The following lemma can be proven easily by induction.

Lemma 54
.

Let
t=\sigma_{1}\ldots\sigma_{n}
be a sequence of atomic operations, and

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle
a configuration. Then,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle\cdot t
is of the form

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u^{\prime}\middle|v^{\prime}v}}\right\rangle
.

Then, we can make some assertions about a key observing its result over a configuration.

Lemma 55

(Independence from position)
.

Let
t
be a key of
\mathsf{BLEK}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle
a configuration. If
t
writes an
a

from
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\langle u\middle|v}}\right\rangle
, then for all configurations

9
Closure properties

In this section we study closure properties of keyboard classes. We selected three operators, the union and the intersection, as they are the most natural closure operators, and the mirror, under which some classes are stable.

Proposition 72

(Mirror)
.

\mathsf{MK}
,
\mathsf{AK}
and
\mathsf{EAK}
are stable by mirror.

\mathsf{EK}
,
\mathsf{BK}
,
\mathsf{BEK}
and
\mathsf{BLK}
are not stable by mirror.

Proposition 73

(Intersection)
.

None of the keyboard language classes are stable by intersection.

Proposition 74

(Union)
.

None of the keyboard language classes are stable by union.

We end this section with an undecidability result, showing that intersecting keyboards can lead to highly complex languages. This shows another link with context-free languages as the emptiness of the intersection of two context-free languages is undecidable as well.

Proposition 75

(Intersection emptiness problem)
.

The following problem is undecidable:

Input:

two
\mathsf{LK}
keyboards.

Output:
Is
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K_{1}}}\right)\cap\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K_{2}}}\right)
empty?

10
Conclusion

A natural question when it comes to models of computation is what we can do without any memory or any information on the current state of the system. We initiated a line of research aiming at studying such
?
blind
?
models. The one we considered here, keyboards, proved to be mathematically complex and interestingly orthogonal in expressiveness to several of the most classical models.
We have established a number of properties of keyboards, as well as a vocabulary facilitating their study. We separated almost all classes and compared their expressiveness, thereby uncovering the lattice of their power.

Future work

As keyboards are a completely new model, there are many open problems we are working on or intend to address.
We conjecture that
\mathsf{EK}
is not included in
\mathsf{BAK}
, but we do not have a proof.
We also conjecture that not all
\mathsf{AK}
languages are algebraic (the language generated by
\{a\mathord{\blacktriangleright}\mathord{\blacktriangleright},b\mathord{\blacktriangleleft}\mathord{\blacktriangleleft}\}
is a candidate as a counter-example) and that
\mathsf{BEAK}
does not contain all rational languages (
a^{*}+b^{*}
being a potential counter-example).
We plan on extending the set of operations to add, for instance, a right erasing operator, symmetric to
\mathord{\leftarrow}
.
It could also be interesting to study the semantics in which we forbid non-effective operations.
Finally, we could equip the model with states and transitions labelled with keys. We would then have more control over which keys are applied at which times, thus increasing the expressiveness of the model and facilitating its study.

Lemma 82
.

Let
K
be a keyboard of
\mathsf{BK}
. We set
E=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w}}\right\rvert-r\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\mathord{\leftarrow}^{r}w\in K}}\right\}

and
p=\operatorname{GCD}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E}}\right)
. Let
x
and
x^{\prime}
be two integers such that
0\leq x,x^{\prime}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert K}}\right\rVert_{\infty}
.
If there exists
\mathord{\leftarrow}^{r_{1}}w_{1},\mathord{\leftarrow}^{r_{2}}w_{2}\in K
such that

•

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w_{1}}}\right\rvert-r_{1}<0<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w_{2}}}\right\rvert-r_{2}
;

•

r_{2}\leq x
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w_{1}}}\right\rvert\leq x^{\prime}
;

•

p
divides
x^{\prime}-x
,

then there exists a sequence of keys
\tau=t_{1}\ldots t_{n}\in K^{*}
such that

x\xrightarrow{\tau}_{\text{e}}x^{\prime}
.

Proof.

The elements of
E
are the numbers of letters written by each key, minus the number of letters it erases. We split
E
between positive integers (corresponding to keys which write more letters than they erase), and negative ones, (keys which erase more than they write). We are not interested in the keys which write as much as they erase for this proof. We set

E_{+}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{i\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}i\in E\text{ and }i>0}}\right\}\text{ and }E_{-}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{i\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}i\in E\text{ and }i>0}}\right\}

and
p^{+}
and
p^{-}
their respective gcds,
p^{+}=\operatorname{GCD}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{+}}}\right)

and
p^{-}=\operatorname{GCD}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{-}}}\right)
. We have
\operatorname{GCD}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p^{+},p^{-}}}\right)=p
, thus, by Bézout’s identity, there exist
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}
such
that
\alpha p^{+}-\beta p^{-}=p
.

We then consider
M
a multiple of
p^{+}
and
p^{-}
greater than
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert K}}\right\rVert_{\infty}

and

D=x^{\prime}+(M^{2}+M)(r_{1}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w_{1}}}\right\rvert)-x-M(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w_{2}}}\right\rvert-r_{2}).

D
is non-negative because
(M^{2}+M)(r_{1}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w_{1}}}\right\rvert)\geq M^{2}+M\geq M\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert K}}\right\rVert+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert K}}\right\rVert\geq M(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w_{2}}}\right\rvert-r_{2})+x
. Further,
p
divides
D
, hence there exist

c\in\mathbb{N}
such that
D=cp
.

We define
A=c\alpha p^{+}+M^{2}
and
B=c\beta p^{-}+M^{2}
. They are
respectively divisible by
p^{+}
and
p^{-}
and we further have

A-B=cp=D
.

We have
A\geq M^{2}\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert K}}\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\geq\operatorname*{max}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{+}}}\right)^{2}
and similarly

B\geq\operatorname*{max}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E_{-}}}\right)^{2}
. A result first proven by Schur
[
1
]
(and then proven again many times) states that given a finite set
S
of positive integers, any number greater than
\operatorname*{max}(S)^{2}
and divisible by
\operatorname{GCD}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S}}\right)
can be written as a linear combination of elements of
S
with natural numbers as coefficients (the bound given by Schur is actually better, but we do not need it here).
There exist non-negative coefficients
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a_{i}}}\right)_{i\in E_{+}}
and

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b_{i}}}\right)_{i\in E_{-}}
such that

\sum_{i\in E_{+}}ia_{i}=A\text{ and }\sum_{i\in E_{-}}ib_{i}=B.

We can now construct the sequence of keys allowing us to go from
x
to
x^{\prime}
effectively.

•

We start from
x
. We apply
M
times the key
\mathord{\leftarrow}^{r_{2}}w_{2}
.
The execution is effective (
r_{2}\leq x
and the key is positive) and leads to
x+M(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w_{2}}}\right\rvert-r_{2})
.

•

For all
i\in E
, let
t_{i}=\mathord{\leftarrow}^{r_{i}}w_{i}
be a key
such that
i=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w_{i}}}\right\rvert-r_{i}
. For all
i\in E_{+}
, we apply
a_{i}

times
t_{i}
(in an arbitrary order). The execution is effective
as we start with more than
M
letters and we only apply positive keys. We obtain configuration
x+M(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w_{2}}}\right\rvert-r_{2})+A
.

•

For all
i\in E_{-}
, we apply
b_{i}
times
t_{i}

(in an arbitrary order). This leads to configuration

x+M(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w_{2}}}\right\rvert-r_{2})+A-B=x+M(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w_{2}}}\right\rvert-r_{2})+D=x^{\prime}+(M^{2}+M)(r_{1}-\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w_{1}}}\right\rvert).

The final configuration is greater than
M
and we applied only negative keys, thus the execution is again effective.

•

Finally, we apply
M+M^{2}
times the key
\mathord{\leftarrow}^{r_{1}}w_{1}
, yielding the final configuration
x^{\prime}
.
As
x^{\prime}\geq r_{1}
and as this key is negative, the execution is effective.

We indeed obtain a sequence of keys

such that

x\xrightarrow{\tau}_{\text{e}}x^{\prime}
.

Lemma 85
.

The following problem is decidable in polynomial time.

\begin{cases}\text{{Input}}:&\text{$K$ a keyboard of $\mathsf{BK}$ and $0\leq x,x^{\prime}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert K}}\right\rVert_{\infty}$}\\
\text{{Output}}:&\text{Does there exist $\tau=t_{1}\ldots t_{n}\in K^{*}$ such that $x\xrightarrow{\tau}_{\text{e}}x^{\prime}$ ?}\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
K
be a keyboard of
\mathsf{BK}
and
0\leq x,x^{\prime}\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert K}}\right\rVert_{\infty}
. We distinguish four cases:

•

If for all
\mathord{\leftarrow}^{r}w\in K
such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w}}\right\rvert-r<0
we have
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w}}\right\rvert>x^{\prime}
, then we construct a graph whose vertices are integers from
0
to
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lVert K}}\right\rVert_{\infty}
and such that
there is a transition from
s
to
s^{\prime}
if and only if there exists
t\in K
such that

s\cdot t=s^{\prime}
. By
\cref
nogoodnegative
, there exists an execution

x\xrightarrow{\tau}_{\text{e}}x^{\prime}
if and only if there is a path from
x
to
x^{\prime}
in that graph.

•

If for all
\mathord{\leftarrow}^{r}w\in K
such that
0<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w}}\right\rvert-r
we have
r>x
, then
we construct the same graph as in the previous case. By
\cref
nogoodpositive
,
there exists
\tau
such that
x\xrightarrow{\tau}_{\text{e}}x^{\prime}
if and only if there is a path from

x
to
x^{\prime}
in that graph.

•

If there exists
\mathord{\leftarrow}^{r_{1}}{w_{1}},\mathord{\leftarrow}^{r_{2}}{w_{2}}\in K
such that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w_{1}}}\right\rvert-r_{1}<0<\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w_{2}}}\right\rvert-r_{2}
,
r_{2}\leq x
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\lvert w_{1}}}\right\rvert\leq x^{\prime}
and

p
divides
x^{\prime}-x
, then, by
\cref
Frobenius
, there exists
\tau
such that

x\xrightarrow{\tau}_{\text{e}}x^{\prime}
.

•

Otherwise,
p
does not divide
x-x^{\prime}
: a straightforward induction on

\tau
shows that for all
\tau\in K^{*}
, if
\tau
acts effectively on
x
, then
p
divides
(x\cdot\tau)-x
and thus
x\cdot\tau\neq x^{\prime}
.

∎

We have all the necessary tools to construct, given a keyboard
K
of
\mathsf{BK}
, an automaton recognizing the language
\operatorname{\mathcal{L}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K}}\right)
.

t=\sigma_{1}\cdots\sigma_{k}\in T
.

s^{u}
for the configuration of the automaton
\operatorname{\mathcal{M}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K}}\right)
in state

with
u
as stack content (from bottom to top).
