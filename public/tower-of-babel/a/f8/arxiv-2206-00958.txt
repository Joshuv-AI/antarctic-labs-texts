Abstract
. In this paper, we classify ( q , q ) (q,q) -biprojective almost perfect nonlinear (APN) functions over 𝕃 × 𝕃 \mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L} under the natural left and right action of GL ⁡ ( 2 , 𝕃 ) \GL(2,\mathbb{L}) where 𝕃 \mathbb{L} is a finite field of characteristic 2 2 . This shows in particular that the only quadratic APN functions (up to 𝖢𝖢𝖹 {\mathsf{CCZ}} -equivalence) over 𝕃 × 𝕃 \mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L} that satisfy the so-called subfield property are the Gold functions and the function κ : 𝔽 64 → 𝔽 64 \kappa:\mathbb{F}_{64}\to\mathbb{F}_{64} which is the only known APN function that is equivalent to a permutation over 𝕃 × 𝕃 \mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L} up to 𝖢𝖢𝖹 {\mathsf{CCZ}} -equivalence. The κ \kappa -function was introduced in (Browning, Dillon, McQuistan, and Wolfe, 2010). Deciding whether there exist other quadratic APN functions (possibly 𝖢𝖢𝖹 {\mathsf{CCZ}} -equivalent to permutations) that satisfy subfield property or equivalently, generalizing κ \kappa to higher dimensions was an open problem listed for instance in (Carlet, 2015) as one of the interesting open problems on cryptographic functions.



1.
Introduction

Almost perfect nonlinear (APN) functions are cryptographically important
functions over a vector space over the finite field of order two that provides
the best resistance against differential cryptanalysis.
Arguably the most important open problem on APN functions is the
question on the existence of APN permutations over even dimensional vector spaces.
There are no APN permutations over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2}
and
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{4}

[
30
]
.
Existence of an APN permutation over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{6}
was shown in
[
8
]
.
The function is
{\mathsf{CCZ}}
-equivalent (see Sections
2
and
3

for the definitions of the concepts that are used in Introduction) to
the quadratic function
\kappa:\mathbb{F}_{2^{6}}\to\mathbb{F}_{2^{6}}
. Note that we will view the
finite field
\mathbb{F}_{2^{2l}}
as a vector space
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{2l}
and also as

\mathbb{F}_{2^{l}}\times\mathbb{F}_{2^{l}}
. When
\kappa
is viewed as a polynomial over the
finite field
\mathbb{F}_{2^{6}}
it falls into the class of Dembowski-Ostrom polynomials
that satisfy the subfield property introduced in
[
8
]
. These
polynomial functions when viewed as functions over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{l}}\times\mathbb{F}_{2^{l}}

form the class of
(q,q)
-biprojective functions. Of course a natural
question that arises is to determine whether Dembowski-Ostrom polynomials
that satisfy the subfield property (or, equivalently, the class of
(q,q)
-biprojective
functions) contain APN functions that are
{\mathsf{CCZ}}
-equivalent to permutations for
l>3
.
The question is viewed as an important problem. For instance it was included
by Carlet in a list of interesting research questions regarding crytographic functions

[
15
, Section 3.7]
as an important subproblem of the major problem of deciding
whether there exists an APN permutation when
l>3
.
The problem also appeared in
[
21
, Problem 15]
.
In this paper, we solve this problem.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
q=2^{k}
,
r=2^{l}
,
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{F}_{2^{l}}
with
0<k<l
and

F:\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}\to\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}

be a
(q,q)
-biprojective function. Then
F
is APN if and only if
\Gcd(k,l)=1
, and

(i)

l
is even and
F{\,{\approx_{\mathfrak{L}}}\,}G_{q+1}
or
F{\,{\approx_{\mathfrak{L}}}\,}G_{q+r}
, or

(ii)

l
is odd,
k
is odd, and
F{\,{\approx_{\mathfrak{L}}}\,}G_{q+1}
, or

(iii)

l
is odd,
k
is even, and
F{\,{\approx_{\mathfrak{L}}}\,}G_{q+r}
, or

(iv)

l=3
and
F{\,{\approx_{\mathfrak{L}}}\,}\kappa
.

We have the following clarifications for the statement of the theorem.

•

The equivalence relation
{\,{\approx_{\mathfrak{L}}}\,}
is introduced by
the action of
\GL(2,\mathbb{L})\times\GL(2,\mathbb{L})
which is a finer notion of
equivalence than that of
{\mathsf{CCZ}}
-equivalence. Thus our results imply

{\mathsf{CCZ}}
-equivalence results.

•

The biprojective maps

with
s\in\{q+1,q+r\}
are
the so-called Gold maps
X\mapsto X^{s}
in the univariate notation
with a suitable identification of the vector spaces.

•

The Gold maps

over
\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}
are APN whenever

\Gcd(j,2l)=1
by
[

]
. APN Gold maps over
\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}
are not

-equivalent to permutations by
[

.
Thus, a

-biprojective APN function

is

and
F{\,{\approx_{\mathfrak{L}}}\,}\kappa
.

•

The special case

was solved in
[

]

,

of
[

,

,

,

•

Another idea to attack the problem is to identify a class of functions
that includes
\kappa
when

and also are
{\mathsf{CCZ}}
-equivalent
to permutations for larger

. This is the case of the so-called butterfly

[

which requires

[

•

The proof is based on three concepts:

2.
Preliminaries

Let
F:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a
vectorial Boolean function
.
A function
F
is said to be
almost perfect nonlinear (APN)

if

F(x)+F(x+a)=b

has zero or two solutions for every

(a,b)\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus\{0\}\times\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. Every vectorial
Boolean function
F:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
can be written as
an evaluation function of a polynomial over
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}[X]
with
(polynomial) degree at most
2^{n}-1
, i.e.,
F:X\mapsto F(X)
where

F(X)=\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}A_{i}X^{i}.

We will not make distinction between functions and their polynomial
representations. The
algebraic degree
of a vectorial Boolean
function is defined to be

\algdeg(F)=\max\{\wt_{2}(i)\ :\ A_{i}\neq 0\},

where
\wt_{2}
denotes the weight of base-
2
representation of an integer.
When we say
quadratic
,
affine
or
linear
, we refer to this
notion of degree. We will need the usual
polynomial degree

as well which will be denoted by

\deg(F)=\max\{i\ :\ A_{i}\neq 0\}

as usual.
The polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}[X]
that correspond to
affine
functions are

M(X)=\sum_{0\leq i\leq n-1}C_{i}X^{2^{i}}+D.

If
D=0
, they are called
linear
functions and are
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear
vector-space endomorphisms of
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
when viewed as a
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-vector space.
As polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}[X]
, they are known as
linearized polynomials
.
We are particularly interested in the class of quadratic polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}[X]
,

Q(X)=\sum_{0\leq i\neq j\leq n-1}B_{ij}X^{2^{i}+2^{j}}+\sum_{0\leq i\leq n-1}C_{i}X^{2^{i}}+D.

The subclass of the above class of polynomials that contain only the
non-affine parts is known as
Dembowski-Ostrom (DO)
polynomials, i.e.,

D(X)=\sum_{0\leq i\neq j\leq n-1}B_{ij}X^{2^{i}+2^{j}}.

When
n=2l
, a further subclass of DO polynomials is important.
The class of polynomials

(1)

R(X)=AX^{q+1}+BX^{2^{l}(q+1)}+CX^{q+2^{l}}+DX^{2^{l}q+1},

where
q=2^{k}
with
0<k<l
, satisfying the
subfield property
,

R(aX)=a^{q+1}R(X),\quad\textrm{ for all }a\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{l}},

contains (up to
{\mathsf{CCZ}}
-equivalence) the only known APN function

\kappa
that is
{\mathsf{CCZ}}
-equivalent to a
permutation for even
n
(we will describe various notions of
equivalences of vectorial Boolean functions further below). Let
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{F}_{2^{l}}
.
Identifying
\mathbb{F}_{2^{2l}}=\mathbb{L}(\xi)=\mathbb{L}\xi+\mathbb{L}\cong\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}
,
we can write the above class of functions

X\mapsto R(X)

as

(x,y)\mapsto R(x,y)

with

\displaystyle R(x,y)

\displaystyle=((a_{0}x^{q+1}+b_{0}x^{q}y+c_{0}xy^{q}+d_{0}y^{q+1}),(a_{1}x^{q+1}+b_{1}x^{q}y+c_{1}xy^{q}+d_{1}y^{q+1}))

\displaystyle=(f(x,y),g(x,y))

where
X=(x\xi+y)
. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
q=2^{k}
with
0<k<l
.

•

Let
f\in\mathbb{L}[x,y]
be a polynomial of the form

f(x,y)=a_{0}x^{q+1}+b_{0}x^{q}y+c_{0}xy^{q}+d_{0}y^{q+1}.

Then
f
is called a
q
-biprojective polynomial
.

•

Let
R:\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}\to\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}
be a function of the form

R:(x,y)\mapsto R(x,y)=(f(x,y),g(x,y))

where
f
and
g
are
q
-biprojective polynomials. Then
R
is called a

(q,q)
-biprojective function
.

We will use the shorthand notation

f=(a_{0},b_{0},c_{0},d_{0})_{q},

and

R=((a_{0},b_{0},c_{0},d_{0})_{q},(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1},d_{1})_{q})=(f,g).

The subfield property can be recognized in this form. For a
(q,q)
-biprojective map
F=(f,g)
, one has

(f(cx,cy),g(cx,cy))=c^{q+1}(f(x),g(y)),\quad\textrm{ for all }c,x,y\in\mathbb{L}.

Remark 2.2
.

One can generalize the concept to

(q,q^{\prime})
-biprojective functions

F:(x,y)\mapsto F(x,y)=((a_{0},b_{0},c_{0},d_{0})_{q},(a_{1},b_{1},c_{1},d_{1})_{q^{\prime}}),

where
q^{\prime}=2^{k^{\prime}}
(here one allows
0\leq k,k^{\prime}<l
).
These functions satisfy a modified form of the subfield property

(f(cx,cy),g(cx,cy))=(c^{q+1}f(x),c^{q^{\prime}+1}g(y)),\quad\textrm{ for all }c,x,y\in\mathbb{L}.

In this paper, we are only interested in
(q,q)
-biprojective functions. See
[
22
,
24
]

for more on
(q,q^{\prime})
-biprojective APN functions and their constructions, equivalences and enumerations.

Define

\displaystyle{\mathsf{D}_{f}^{{0}}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=b_{0}x^{q}+c_{0}x+d_{0}y^{q}+d_{0}y,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{D}_{f}^{{\infty}}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=a_{0}x^{q}+a_{0}x+c_{0}y^{q}+b_{0}y,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{D}_{f}^{{u}}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(a_{0}u+b_{0})x^{q}+(a_{0}u^{q}+c_{0})x+(c_{0}u+d_{0})y^{q}+(b_{0}u^{q}+d_{0})y,

for
u\in{\mathbb{L}^{\times}}
.
The following lemma was proved in
[
22
]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
F=(f,g)
be a
(q,q^{\prime})
-biprojective function. Then
F
is APN if and only if

{\mathsf{D}_{f}^{{u}}}(x,y)=0={\mathsf{D}_{g}^{{u}}}(x,y)

has exactly two solutions for each
u\in{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(\mathbb{L})
.

2.1.
The trace map and Hilbert’s Theorem 90

Let
\mathbb{L}
be the finite field with
p^{l}
elements,
q=p^{k}
for
k>0

and let
\mathbb{D}\subset\mathbb{L}
of order
p^{\delta}
with
\delta=\Gcd(k,l)
.
The
trace map
is defined as

{\mathsf{tr}_{{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{D}}}\left({x}\right)}=\sum_{j=0}^{l/\delta-1}x^{(p^{\delta})^{j}}.

When
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{F}_{p}
then we simply write

{\mathsf{tr}\left({x}\right)}={\mathsf{tr}_{{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{F}_{p}}}\left({x}\right)}.

The following is the finite fields version of Hilbert’s Theorem 90.

Lemma 2.4

(Hilbert’s Theorem 90)
.

Let
\Gcd(j,l)=1
and
a\in\mathbb{L}
. Then

{\mathsf{tr}_{{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{D}}}\left({a}\right)}=0
if and only if
a=x^{(p^{\delta})^{j}}-x
for some
x\in\mathbb{L}
.

The
\mathbb{D}
-linear vector-space endomorphisms of
\mathbb{L}
can be written as

L(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{l/\delta-1}a_{j}x^{(p^{\delta})^{j}},\quad a_{j}\in\mathbb{L},

and are called
\mathbb{D}
-linearized polynomials
. Determining kernels of such
endomorphisms in
\mathbb{L}
, especially of the form
L(x)=ax^{q}-bx
and the
zeroes of its translates
L(x)+c
, is important for this paper. This can
simply be done by observing

ax^{q}-bx=0

for some nonzero
r\in\mathbb{L}
if and only if
r^{q-1}=b/a
. In that case

L(rx)/rb=x^{q}-x
.

Then one can deduce that the zeroes of
L(x)
are
0
and
\epsilon r
for

\epsilon\in{\mathbb{D}^{\times}}
if such
r
exists. Then the case
L(x)+c
can be handled
using Hilbert’s Theorem 90. The following lemma is relevant and will be needed.

Lemma 2.5
.

For a prime
p
,

(i)

\Gcd(p^{k}-1,p^{l}-1)=p^{\Gcd(k,l)}-1
.

(ii)

\Gcd(p^{k}+1,p^{l}-1)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\textrm{if }\frac{l}{\Gcd(k,l)}\textrm{ is odd, and }p=2,\\
2&\textrm{if }\frac{l}{\Gcd(k,l)}\textrm{ is odd, and }p\textrm{ is odd},\\
p^{\Gcd(k,l)}+1&\textrm{if }\frac{l}{\Gcd(k,l)}\textrm{ is even}.\end{array}\right.

2.2.
Difference sets with Singer parameters and multisets

Let
M
be a multiset whose elements are
s_{i}
with repetition
d_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq r
, denoted by

M=\{\!\!\{s_{1}^{[d_{1}]},\ldots,s_{r}^{[d_{r}]}\}\!\!\},

where we write
\mult_{M}(s_{i})=d_{i}
.
When all
s_{i}
have the same repetition number
d
, we write
M=S^{[d]}
,
where
S=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{r}\}
.

For two multisets
S,T
we denote by

S/T=\{\!\!\{s/t\ :\ s\in S,t\in T\}\!\!\}

the
direct division
of two multisets.

A subset
D
of cardinality
k
of a group
G
(written multiplicatively)
of order
v
is said to be a
(v,k,\lambda)
-difference set
if

D/D=\{1\}^{[k]}\cup(G\setminus\{1\})^{[\lambda]}.

We are mostly interested in the cyclic difference sets in the multiplicative
group
{\mathbb{L}^{\times}}
in characteristic
2
. The parameters

(2^{l}-1,2^{l-1},2^{l-2})
and

(2^{l}-1,2^{l-1}-1,2^{l-2}-1)
are called
Singer parameters
, since they
are the parameters of the Singer sets (i.e., hyperplanes of
\mathbb{L}
),

{\overline{\mathcal{H}}}=\{x\in\mathbb{L}\ :\ {\mathsf{tr}\left({x}\right)}=1\},

and
{\mathcal{H}}\setminus\{0\}
where

{\mathcal{H}}=\{x\in\mathbb{L}\ :\ {\mathsf{tr}\left({x}\right)}=0\},

respectively. Dillon-Dobbertin difference sets are another important example of
cyclic difference sets with Singer parameters
[
19
]
(see Lemmas
4.1
,
4.3
)
which we will use frequently in this paper.

2.3.
Equivalences of vectorial Boolean functions

Let
F:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
be a vectorial Boolean function and
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{2^{n}}
.
The widest known notion of equivalence that keeps the APN property invariant
is called the
{\mathsf{CCZ}}
-equivalence

[
16
]
. Define the
graph

of the function
F
by

\Gamma_{F}=\{(x,F(x))\ :\ x\in\mathbb{F}\}.

Then
F
is said to be
{\mathsf{CCZ}}
-equivalent to
F^{\prime}
if there exists
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-linear endomorphisms

A,B,C,D
of
\mathbb{F}
, elements
u,v\in\mathbb{F}
and a permutation
\pi:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
such that

(2)

\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
F(x)\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\pi(x)\\
F^{\prime}(\pi(x))\end{pmatrix}.

In that case we write
F\approx_{{\mathsf{CCZ}}}F^{\prime}
. A narrower notion of equivalence that keeps
the algebraic degree of
F
invariant is called the
extended affine (EA) equivalence
.
We write
F\approx_{{\mathsf{EA}}}F^{\prime}
if

A_{1}\circ F\circ A_{2}(x)+A_{3}(x)=F^{\prime}(x),

for affine maps
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{F}
with
A_{1},A_{2}
bijective. It can easily be shown
that
{\mathsf{EA}}
-equivalence is a special case of
{\mathsf{CCZ}}
-equivalence where one sets
B=0
.
An important theorem for quadratic APN functions is that for two quadratic APN functions

F,G
we have by a result of Yoshiara
[
43
]
,

F\approx_{{\mathsf{EA}}}G\iff F\approx_{{\mathsf{CCZ}}}G.

We can restrict the equivalence even more if, for instance, we want to keep the property
of being a permutation invariant.
The functions
F
and
F^{\prime}
are said to be
linearly equivalent
if

L_{1}\circ F\circ L_{2}=F^{\prime},

for
L_{1},L_{2}\in\GL(n,2)
. Note that this is equivalent to setting
B=C=0
and

u=v=0
in (
2
)
(when only
B=C=0
holds, they are called
affinely equivalent
).
We denote this equivalence by
F\approx_{\GL(n,2)}F^{\prime}
. In the following, we will
even restrict this equivalence to the case where
L_{1},L_{2}\in\GL(2,2^{l})<\GL(2l,2)

when
n=2l
with the obvious motivation that the (left and right) action of

\GL(2,2^{l})
keeps the
(q,q)
-biprojective property of
F(x,y)=(f(x,y),g(x,y))
invariant.

3.
Actions of
\GL(2,\mathbb{L})
and
\PGL(2,\mathbb{L})

In this section we outline the basics of several actions of
\GL(2,\mathbb{L})
and
\PGL(2,\mathbb{L})

on biprojective functions. Most of this section can be considered standard lore.
Let

\mathcal{V}_{q,\mathbb{L}}=\{(a,b,c,d)_{q}\ :\ a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{L}\},

be the set of all
q
-biprojective polynomials.
Let
f,g\in\mathcal{V}_{q,\mathbb{L}}
be two
q
-biprojective polynomials and

\displaystyle F:\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto(f(x,y),g(x,y))

be the associated
(q,q)
-biprojective function.
Define
\mathcal{F}_{q,\mathbb{L}}
to be the set of all
(q,q)
-biprojective functions, i.e.,

\mathcal{F}_{q,\mathbb{L}}=\mathcal{V}_{q,\mathbb{L}}\times\mathcal{V}_{q,\mathbb{L}}.

Let
{\mathfrak{L}}(\mathbb{L})
be the group of all nonsingular
\mathbb{L}
-linear transformations of
\mathbb{L}\times\mathbb{L}
, i.e.,

\GL(2,\mathbb{L})\cong{\mathfrak{L}}(\mathbb{L})=\left\{(x,y)\mapsto(tx+uy,vx+wy)\ :\ t,u,v,w\in\mathbb{L}\ |\ tw-uv\neq 0\right\}.

We are mainly interested in the standard action of
\GL(2,\mathbb{L})\times\GL(2,\mathbb{L})
on

(q,q)
-biprojective functions
F\in\mathcal{F}_{q,\mathbb{L}}
. That is

F^{\prime}(x,y)=L_{1}\circ F\circ L_{2}(x,y).

This action defines an equivalence relation which we denote by

F^{\prime}{\,{\approx_{\mathfrak{L}}}\,}F.

Define also the action of the group
{\mathbb{L}^{\times}}\times\GL(2,\mathbb{L})
on
q
-biprojective polynomials
f\in\mathcal{V}_{q,\mathbb{L}}

where
{\mathbb{L}^{\times}}<\GL(2,\mathbb{L})
acts on
f
by
scaling
, and the action of
\GL(2,\mathbb{L})
is the usual
right action, i.e.,

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\alpha(f\circ L(x,y)),

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(a(tx+uy)^{q+1}+b(tx+uy)^{q}(vx+wy)+c(tx+uy)(vx+wy)^{q}+d(vx+wy)^{q+1}\right),

where
(\alpha,L)\in{\mathbb{L}^{\times}}\times\GL(2,\mathbb{L})
. In this case we say that
f{\,{\sim_{\mathfrak{L}}}\,}f^{\prime}
.

Set
\phi_{f}(x)=f(x,1)
. A polynomial
\phi_{f}\in\mathbb{L}[x]
for
f\in\mathcal{V}_{q,\mathbb{L}}
is
called a
q
-projective polynomial
.
The projective version of the above action on the bivariate
q
-projective polynomial
f
,
on the univariate
q
-projective polynomial
\phi_{f}
can be given using the

fractional linear (Möbius) transformations
over the finite field
\mathbb{L}
, i.e.,

\PGL(2,\mathbb{L})\cong{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathbb{L})=\left\{x\mapsto\frac{tx+u}{vx+w}\ :\ t,u,v,w\in\mathbb{L}\ |\ tw-uv\neq 0\right\}.

Now, define the action

(3)

\phi_{f^{\prime}}(x)=\alpha(vx+w)^{q+1}(\phi_{f}\circ\mu(x)),

for
(\alpha,\mu)\in{\mathbb{L}^{\times}}\times\PGL(2,\mathbb{L})
and

\mu:x\mapsto\frac{tx+u}{vx+w}.

One addresses the zero of the denomiator
(vx+w)
by introducing
\infty=\beta/0

for all
\beta\in{\mathbb{L}^{\times}}
. We define
{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(\mathbb{L})=\mathbb{L}\cup\{\infty\}
.
By defining
\mu(\infty)=t/v
, we see that all
\mu\in{\mathfrak{M}}(\mathbb{L})
permutes

{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(\mathbb{L})
. Note that we view the action as

(4)

\phi_{f^{\prime}}(x)=\alpha\left(a(tx+u)^{q+1}+b(tx+u)^{q}(vx+w)+c(tx+u)(vx+w)^{q}+d(vx+w)^{q+1}\right),

so that
\phi_{f^{\prime}}
is a (
q
-projective) polynomial over
\mathbb{L}[x]
and we do not have to deal with
\infty
(but we will do that later, since it is helpful). We write
\phi_{f}{\,{\sim_{\mathfrak{M}}}\,}\phi_{f^{\prime}}
. The following lemma is straightforward.

Lemma 3.1
.

We have

f{\,{\sim_{\mathfrak{L}}}\,}f^{\prime}\textrm{ if and only if }\phi_{f}{\,{\sim_{\mathfrak{M}}}\,}\phi_{f^{\prime}}.

We will not use
\phi_{f}
to refer to univariate
q
-projective version of
f
and
instead use
f
for both univariate and bivariate functions and polynomials.
We will also use
\mathcal{V}_{q,\mathbb{L}}
as the ambient space of both types of functions/polynomials.

Our aim is to classify the APN functions in
\mathcal{F}_{q,\mathbb{L}}
under the equivalence
{\,{\approx_{\mathfrak{L}}}\,}
.
We say that
\mathcal{S}_{q,\mathbb{L}}\subseteq\mathcal{V}_{q,\mathbb{L}}
is a
representative set
of
\mathcal{V}_{q,\mathbb{L}}

if (denoting by
[f]_{\sim}
the equivalence class of
f
under
\sim
)

[\mathcal{S}_{q,\mathbb{L}}]_{\,{\sim_{\mathfrak{M}}}\,}=\bigcup_{f\in\mathcal{S}_{q,\mathbb{L}}}[f]_{\,{\sim_{\mathfrak{M}}}\,}=\mathcal{V}_{q,\mathbb{L}}.

In the following we will find a representative set
\mathcal{S}_{q,\mathbb{L}}
using univariate

q
-projective polynomials and their equivalence
{\,{\sim_{\mathfrak{M}}}\,}
. Then it will be clear that checking

(f,g)\in\mathcal{S}_{q,\mathbb{L}}\times\mathcal{V}_{q,\mathbb{L}}

is enough for our classification under
{\,{\approx_{\mathfrak{L}}}\,}
since
f{\,{\sim_{\mathfrak{M}}}\,}f^{\prime}
if and only if
f{\,{\sim_{\mathfrak{L}}}\,}f^{\prime}

and if
F=(f,g)\in\mathcal{V}_{q,\mathbb{L}}\times\mathcal{V}_{q,\mathbb{L}}
then

F{\,{\approx_{\mathfrak{L}}}\,}F^{\prime}=(f^{\prime},g^{\prime})\in\mathcal{S}_{q,\mathbb{L}}\times\mathcal{V}_{q,\mathbb{L}}
for some
f^{\prime},g^{\prime}
using the

\mathbb{L}
-linear transformation implied by the equivalence
f{\,{\sim_{\mathfrak{L}}}\,}f^{\prime}
.

3.1.
Roots of projective polynomials

Let
q=p^{k}
,
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{F}_{p^{\delta}}
,
\mathbb{L}=\mathbb{F}_{p^{l}}
where
\delta=\Gcd(k,l)
and

f(x)=ax^{q+1}+bx^{q}+cx+d

be a nonzero
q
-projective polynomial.
If
a=0
, then
f
is an affine polynomial and the set of zeroes of
f
in
\mathbb{L}
, i.e.,

Z^{\prime}_{f}=\{x\in\mathbb{L}\ :\ f(x)=0\}

satisfies
|Z^{\prime}_{f}|\in\{0,1,p^{\delta}
}. To see that, first
observe that

scaling
, i.e.,
f\mapsto\alpha f
for
\alpha\in{\mathbb{L}^{\times}}
,
the
translations

f(x)\mapsto f(x+\beta)
for
\beta\in\mathbb{L}
and
the
dilations

f(x)\mapsto f(\gamma x)
for
\gamma\in{\mathbb{L}^{\times}}

keep the number of zeroes of
f
in
\mathbb{L}
invariant. Then we have only
a few options to consider:

•

f=1

has no
\mathbb{L}
-zeroes —degenerate case (together with omitted
f=0
).

•

f\in\{x,x^{q}\}

has one
\mathbb{L}
-zero.

•

f=x^{q}-cx-d
where
c\neq 0
.

We have

–

if
c=A^{q-1}\in({\mathbb{L}^{\times}})^{q-1}
, then
f
has

*

p^{\delta}

\mathbb{L}
-zeroes if
{\mathsf{tr}_{{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{D}}}\left({d/A^{q}}\right)}=0
, and

*

no
\mathbb{L}
-zeroes if
{\mathsf{tr}_{{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{D}}}\left({d/A^{q}}\right)}\neq 0
; or

–

one
\mathbb{L}
-zero if
c\not\in({\mathbb{L}^{\times}})^{q-1}
,

by Hilbert’s Theorem 90.

Now assume
a\neq 0
. We will show that

|Z^{\prime}_{f}|\in\{0,1,2,p^{\delta}+1\}.

Assume
f
has at least one
\mathbb{L}
-zero
r\in Z^{\prime}_{f}
. Now consider

f^{\prime}(x)=f(x+r)=ax^{q+1}+b^{\prime}x^{q}+c^{\prime}x.

The
reciprocal

f^{\prime\prime}(x)=x^{q+1}f^{\prime}(1/x)
is

f^{\prime\prime}(x)=a+b^{\prime}x+c^{\prime}x^{q},

where
\deg f^{\prime\prime}<q+1
. Now
f^{\prime\prime}
has one fewer
\mathbb{L}
-zeroes than
f^{\prime}

(since we
punctured
the zero of
f^{\prime}
at
0
) and thus we have

|Z^{\prime}_{f^{\prime\prime}}|+1=|Z^{\prime}_{f^{\prime}}|=|Z^{\prime}_{f}|\in\{0,1,2,p^{\delta}+1\}.

This observation, together with the fact that
\PGL(2,\mathbb{L})
is generated by translations, dilations
and inversions (for which the corresponding action is reciprocation),
motivates us to ascribe
f(\infty)=0
if and only if
\deg f<q+1
so that
{\,{\sim_{\mathfrak{M}}}\,}

preserves the number of roots in
{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(\mathbb{L})
.
Now we can define

Definition 3.2
.

The
{\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{L})}
-zeroes
of a
q
-projective polynomial
f
is defined as

Z_{f}=\{x\in{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(\mathbb{L})\ :\ f(x)=0\},

where we define
f(\infty)=0
if and only if
\deg f<q+1
.

Thus we have

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
f\neq 0
be a
q
-projective polynomial. Then,

(i)

|Z_{f}|
is invariant under
{\,{\sim_{\mathfrak{M}}}\,}
, and

(ii)

|Z_{f}|\in\{0,1,2,p^{\delta}+1\}
.

Proof.

Part (ii) is explained before the lemma. We will give a more detailed
proof of Part (i) which, actually, also follows from the previous discussion.

Let
f^{\prime}{\,{\sim_{\mathfrak{M}}}\,}f
via
(\alpha,\mu)\in{\mathbb{L}^{\times}}\times\PGL(2,\mathbb{L})
with

\mu:x\mapsto\frac{tx+u}{vx+w}.
Suppose
v\neq 0
.
Define
T=\mathbb{L}\setminus\{w/v\}
,
which satisfies
\mu(T)=\mathbb{L}\setminus\{t/v\}
.
We see by (
3
) that

x\in T\textrm{ is a zero of $f^{\prime}$ }\iff\mu(x)\textrm{ is a zero of }f.

By (
4
), we get
a=0
if and only if

w/v=\mu^{-1}(\infty)\textrm{ is a zero of $f^{\prime}$ }\iff\infty\textrm{ is a zero of }f

if and only if
\deg f<q+1
. Also by (
4
), since the coefficient of
the
x^{q+1}
term of
f^{\prime}
is
at^{q+1}+bt^{q}v+ctv^{q}+dv^{q+1}=v^{q+1}f(t/v)
,
we have

t/v=\mu(\infty)\textrm{ is a zero of $f$ }\iff\infty\textrm{ is a zero of }f^{\prime}

if and only if
\deg f^{\prime}<q+1
. Combining the three cases we get
\mu(Z_{f^{\prime}})=Z_{f}
and
|Z_{f}|=|Z_{f^{\prime}}|
.
These facts also trivially follow when
v=0
.
∎

Define the sets (suppressing
q
and
\mathbb{L}
from the notation for simplicity),

\displaystyle D_{0}

\displaystyle=\left\{(0,0,0,0)_{q}\right\},

\displaystyle D_{1}

\displaystyle=\left[(0,0,0,1)_{q}\right]_{\,{\sim_{\mathfrak{M}}}\,},

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=D_{0}\cup D_{1},

\displaystyle\Pi_{j}

\displaystyle=\left\{f\in\mathcal{V}_{q,\mathbb{L}}\setminus D\ :\ |Z_{f}|=j\right\},

for

.

Lemma 3.4
.

We have

(i)

\mathcal{V}_{q,\mathbb{L}}=\bigcup_{j\in\{0,1,2,p^{\delta}+1\}}\Pi_{j}\cup D
,

(ii)

\Pi_{1}=[(0,1,-1,-u)_{q}]_{\,{\sim_{\mathfrak{M}}}\,}
where

satisfies
{\mathsf{tr}_{{\mathbb{L}/\mathbb{D}}}\left({u}\right)}=1
,

(iii)

\Pi_{{p^{\delta}+1}}=[(0,1,-1,0)_{q}]_{\,{\sim_{\mathfrak{M}}}\,}
,

Proof.

The proof of Part (i) follows from the discussion before the lemma. For Parts (ii) and (iii), we have to
show that the action is transitive on the sets
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{p^{\delta}+1}
. If
a\neq 0
, as in the
discussion before the lemma, using

first, where

is an
\mathbb{L}
-zero of

,
and then using the reciprocal

, one shows that

with

. Now in both

and one

-root cases,

has the form

with

for some

. One can apply

and then scale

for some

where

and

For the case

∎

4.1.

Now we are going to restrict ourselves to a specific type of

for

The following is an important result on combinatorics of finite fields proved by

]

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

and

The set

is a

(i)

(2^{l}-1,2^{l-1}-1,2^{l-2}-1)
-difference set in
{\mathbb{L}^{\times}}

(ii)

(2^{l}-1,2^{l-1},2^{l-2})

Let

and

\Delta=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\{x^{d}+(x+1)^{d}+1:x\in\mathbb{L}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{2}\}&\textrm{if $l$ is odd, and}\\

The fact that

∎

In the next lemma we show that, since

and

. The map

[

The lemma can be found for general

and

in
[

.
We provide a simple proof for

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
q=2^{k}
,
\Gcd(k,l)=1
and

\displaystyle\rho:\mathbb{L}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{2}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{L}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{2},

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto\frac{x^{q^{2}+1}}{(x^{q}+x)^{q+1}}.

We have

(i)

\{\!\!\{\rho(x)\ :\ x\in{\overline{\mathcal{H}}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{2}\}\!\!\}=I_{1}
,

(ii)

\{\!\!\{\rho(x)\ :\ x\in{\mathcal{H}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{2}\}\!\!\}=I_{3}^{[3]}
,

(iii)

I_{2}=\{0\}
,

(iv)

\{\!\!\{x^{q+1}+x\ :\ x\in\mathbb{L}\}\!\!\}=I_{1}\cup I_{2}^{[2]}\cup I_{3}^{[3]}

Proof.

Let
b\in I_{1}\cup I_{2}\cup I_{3}
and
f=P_{b}=x^{q+1}+x+b
. Let
r\in\mathbb{L}
be
a root of

. Then

\displaystyle f(x+r)

\displaystyle=(x+r)^{q+1}+x+r+b

\displaystyle=x^{q+1}+rx^{q}+(r+1)^{q}x+r^{q+1}+r+b

\displaystyle=x^{q+1}+rx^{q}+(r+1)^{q}x

\displaystyle{\,{\sim_{\mathfrak{M}}}\,}(r+1)^{q}x^{q}+rx+1=f^{\prime\prime}(x)

Then
|Z_{f}|=2
if and only if
|Z_{f^{\prime\prime}}|=1
if and only if

. Thus

and Part
(iii) follows. Otherwise we have

such that

since

is bijective on
\mathbb{L}
by Lemma
2.5
. We have

if and only if
{\mathsf{tr}\left({1/Ar}\right)}=1
and

if and only if
{\mathsf{tr}\left({1/Ar}\right)}=0

Now,
let

satisfy

Let

Now we will introduce the main argument: If

By Lemma
