Critical numerosity in collective behavior
Jacob Calvert

Abstract
. Natural collectives, despite comprising individuals who may not know their numerosity, can exhibit behaviors that depend sensitively on it. This paper proves that the collective behavior of number-oblivious individuals can even have a critical numerosity , above and below which it qualitatively differs. We formalize the concept of critical numerosity in terms of a family of zero–one laws and introduce a model of collective motion, called chain activation and transport (CAT), that has one. CAT describes the collective motion of n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 individuals as a Markov chain that rearranges n n -element subsets of the d d -dimensional grid, m < n m<n elements at a time. According to the individuals’ dynamics, with each step, CAT removes m m elements from the set and then progressively adds m m elements to the boundary of what remains, in a way that favors the consecutive addition and removal of nearby elements. This paper proves that, if d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 , then CAT has a critical numerosity of n c = 2 ​ m + 2 n_{c}=2m+2 with respect to the behavior of its diameter. Specifically, if n < n c n<n_{c} , then the elements form one “cluster,” the diameter of which has an a.s.–finite limit infimum. However, if n ≥ n c n\geq n_{c} , then there is an a.s.–finite time at which the set consists of clusters of between m + 1 m+1 and 2 ​ m + 1 2m+1 elements, and forever after which these clusters grow apart, resulting in unchecked diameter growth. The existence of critical numerosities means that collectives can exhibit “phase transitions” that are governed purely by their numerosity and not, for example, their density or the strength of their interactions. This fact challenges prevalent beliefs about collective behavior; inspires basic scientific questions about the role of numerosity in the behavior of natural collectives; and suggests new functionality for programmable matter, like robot swarms, smart materials, and synthetic biological systems. More broadly, it demonstrates an opportunity to explore the possible behaviors of natural and engineered collectives through the study of random processes that rearrange finite sets.

The existence of critical numerosities means that collectives can exhibit phase transitions—in the sense of abrupt, qualitative changes—in behavior that are mediated
purely by the number of individuals
and not, for example, their density or the nature or strength of their interactions. This fact challenges conventional wisdom about collective behavior. For example, Ouellette and Gordon write that “the properties and functionality of [a collective] arise from the interactions among the individuals. This means that no individual is essential for the group to function”
[
OG21
]
. However, if the collective is at the critical numerosity for a behavior, then
every
individual is essential. For the same reason, the existence of critical numerosities suggests new functionality for programmable matter, like robot swarms. Indeed, an experimentally validated approach to programming matter uses a phase transition inspired by the Ising model; by increasing the strength of attractive interactions between individual robots, the swarm transitions from a dispersed phase to a compact phase that exhibits collective transport
[
LDC+21
]
. Analogously, the existence of critical numerosities could enable programmable matter to exhibit different functionality depending on its numerosity.

Four important properties of CAT are apparent from its definition.

1.

Conservation of numerosity
. CAT conserves the number of elements in the sense of (
1
).

2.

Arbitrarily large diameter
. CAT configurations can have multiple connected components, hence the number of elements does not limit the diameter of the configuration. Specifically, if the configuration
C
has at least three elements, then, for any

\mathbb{P}_{C}

3.

Asymmetric behavior of diameter

(4)

\mathbb{P}_{C}(\mathrm{diam}(\mathscr{C}_{t+1})\leq\mathrm{diam}(\mathscr{C}_{t})+m\,\,\text{for all $t\geq 0$})=1,

for every configuration
C
.
In contrast, the diameter can decrease abruptly. For example, if

4.

\widehat{C}=\big\{D\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}:C=D+x\,\,\text{for some $x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}$}\big\}.

(Cluster formation)

f(V)=\mathrm{diam}(V),\quad g(V)\equiv n,\quad\epsilon=1,\quad\text{and}\quad\cal U=\{V\in\cal V:f(V)\leq b\},

for a yet unspecified
b>0
. By Proposition
1.1
,
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is irreducible on
\cal V
, which is countable. Hence, by Proposition
1.4
,
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{t}
is positive recurrent on
\mathrm{\widehat{P}rog}_{m,n}
if

\displaystyle\forall U:\mathrm{diam}(U)\leq b,\quad\mathbb{E}_{U}\big[\mathrm{diam}(\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{n})\big]

\displaystyle<\infty,\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\forall U:\mathrm{diam}(U)>b,\quad\mathbb{E}_{U}\big[\mathrm{diam}(\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{n})\big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{diam}(U)-n.

The first condition holds because
\mathbb{P}_{U}(\mathrm{diam}(\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{n})\leq\mathrm{diam}(U)+nm)=1
by (
4
). Concerning the second condition, note that Theorem
1.2
applies because
n<2m+2
and, together with (

\mathbb{E}_{U}\big[\mathrm{diam}(\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{n})\big]\leq(1-p)(\mathrm{diam}(U)+n)+p(2n^{2}),

for
p>0
depending on
n
only. Consequently, the second condition holds with
b=(2/p-1)n+2n^{2}
.
∎

Proposition 1.5

(Transience)
.

If
n\geq 2m+2
, then
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{t}
is transient on
\mathrm{\widehat{P}rog}_{m,n}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
1.4
and the Borel–Cantelli lemma, if
C
has
n\geq 2m+2
elements, then
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{t}
makes only finitely many visits to sets in
\mathrm{\widehat{P}rog}_{m,n}
with diameter less than
r>0
,
\mathbb{P}_{C}
–a.s., for every
r
, which implies that
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{t}
is transient.
∎

Theorem
1.5
combines Propositions
1.1
through
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

By standard Markov chain theory, Propositions
1.1
through
1.3
imply the existence of a unique stationary distribution for
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{t}
, supported on
\mathrm{\widehat{P}rog}_{m,n}
, to which it converges from any configuration when
n<2m+2
. Propositions
1.1
and
1.5
imply that
\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{t}
is transient when
n\geq 2m+2
.
∎

Theorem
1.5
reflects a high–level strategy for proving the existence of a critical numerosity—namely, to exhibit a Markov chain that transitions from recurrence to transience, as the numerosity of the initial state increases. The Markov chain dichotomy (e.g.,
[
FMM95
, Theorem 1.2.1]
) explains the virtue of this strategy.
It states that, if a Markov chain
\mathscr{X}
is irreducible on a countable set
\cal T
, then either every state in
\cal T
is recurrent a.s., or every state is transient a.s. This implies that an unbounded function
f:\cal T\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
with finite sublevel sets
\{\{T\in\cal T:f(T)\leq i\}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies

(13)

\liminf_{t\geq 0}f(\mathscr{X}_{t})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{=}}\begin{cases}\inf_{T\in\cal T}f(T)<\infty&\text{$\mathscr{X}$ is recurrent},\\
\infty&\text{$\mathscr{X}$ is transient.}\end{cases}

In particular, the Markov chain dichotomy implies that

\forall T\in\cal T,\quad\mathbb{P}_{T}\left(f(\mathscr{X}_{t})\to\infty\right)\in\{0,1\}.

Now, suppose that
\mathscr{X}
has irreducible components
\{\cal T_{n}\}_{n\geq 2}
, where every element of
\cal T_{n}
is an
n
-element set. For simplicity, assume that
\cal T=\cup_{n\geq 2}\cal T_{n}
. If there is
n_{c}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
such that
\mathscr{X}
is recurrent on
\cal T_{n}
when
n<n_{c}
and
\mathscr{X}
is transient on
\cal T_{n}
when
n\geq n_{c}
, then (
13
) implies that
\{f(\mathscr{X}_{t})\to\infty\}
has a critical numerosity of
n_{c}
.

This observation is relevant to Theorem
1.1
because
\mathrm{diam}
is an unbounded function with finite sublevel sets on
\widehat{\cal S}=\cup_{n>m}\{\widehat{S}:S\subset_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
, i.e., configurations viewed up to translation. According to Proposition
1.1
, the irreducible components of
\mathscr{C}
are
\{\mathrm{\widehat{P}rog}_{m,n}\}_{n>m}
, where each
\mathrm{\widehat{P}rog}_{m,n}
is a subset of
\widehat{\cal S}_{n}=\{\widehat{S}:S\subset_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
and the other elements of
\widehat{\cal S}_{n}
are inessential. By (
13
), if
n>m
, then

\forall\widehat{S}\in\widehat{\cal S}_{n},\quad\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{S}}\big(\mathrm{diam}(\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{t})\to\infty\big)=\begin{cases}0&\text{$\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{t}$ is recurrent on $\mathrm{\widehat{P}rog}_{m,n}$},\\
1&\text{$\widehat{\mathscr{C}}_{t}$ is transient on $\mathrm{\widehat{P}rog}_{m,n}$}.\end{cases}

The same is true of
\mathbb{P}_{S}(\mathrm{diam}(\mathscr{C}_{t})\to\infty)
because CAT is invariant under translation and
\mathrm{diam}(\widehat{S})=\mathrm{diam}(S)
for every configuration
S
. Consequently, Theorem
1.1
follows from Theorem
1.5
.

2.1.
A formula for the CAT transition probabilities

Denote by
\mathcal{A}_{t,j}
and
\mathcal{T}_{t,j}
the
j
th
elements activated and transported at time
t
. We use
\mathcal{A}_{t}
and
\mathcal{T}_{t}
to denote
(\mathcal{A}_{t,j})_{j\leq m}
and
(\mathcal{T}_{t,j})_{j\leq m}
or
\{\mathcal{A}_{t,j}\}_{j\leq m}
and
\{\mathcal{T}_{t,j}\}_{j\leq m}
, depending on the context. Additionally, we use
\cal X_{t}
to denote the concatenation of
\mathcal{A}_{t}
and
\mathcal{T}_{t}
,
(\mathcal{A}_{t,1},\dots,\mathcal{A}_{t,m},\mathcal{T}_{t,1},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{t,m})
.
In these terms,

\mathscr{C}_{t,l}=\begin{cases}\mathscr{C}_{t}\setminus\{\mathcal{A}_{t,j}\}_{j\leq l}&l\leq m,\\
(\mathscr{C}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{t})\cup\{\mathcal{T}_{t,j}\}_{j\leq l-m}&l>m.\end{cases}

In particular,
\mathscr{C}_{t+1}=(\mathscr{C}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{t})\cup\mathcal{T}_{t}
.

We can express the CAT transition probabilities in terms of a family of probability measures on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, indexed by finite, nonempty
U\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
u\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
:

(14)

\mu_{U,u}(v)=\frac{\phi(u,v)}{\sum_{w\in U}\phi(u,w)}\mathbf{1}_{U}(v).

In other words,
\mu_{U,u}
is the probability measure on
U
that is proportional to
\phi(u,\cdot)
.
For ease of notation, we further define
\mu_{U,\infty}(v)=|U|^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{U}(v)
. In terms of these measures, the components of the transition probability are

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}_{t}=x\mid\mathscr{C}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{(\,\mathscr{C}_{t}\setminus\{x_{i}\}_{i<j}\,),\,x_{j-1}}(x_{j})\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{T}_{t}=y\mid\mathscr{C}_{t},\mathcal{A}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\mu_{\partial(\,(\mathscr{C}_{t}\setminus\mathcal{A}_{t})\,\cup\,\{y_{i}\}_{i<j}\,),\,y_{j-1}}(y_{j}),

for
x,y\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{m}
and where
x_{0}=\infty
and
y_{0}=\mathcal{A}_{t,m}
.
The CAT transition probability is

\mathbb{P}(\mathscr{C}_{t+1}=D\mid\mathscr{C}_{t}=C)=\sum_{(x,y):\,C\to D}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{A}_{t}=x,\mathcal{T}_{t}=y\mid\mathscr{C}_{t}\right),

for configurations
C,D
, where
{C\to D}
indicates that the sum ranges over
x,y\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{m}
such that

D=(C\setminus\{x_{i}\}_{i\leq m})\cup\{y_{i}\}_{i\leq m}.

2.2.
Preliminary lemmas

We now prove three basic estimates of the CAT transition probabilities, using a.m.l.g. (
4
) and the definitions of
\phi(u,v)
(
3
) and
\mu_{U,u}(v)
(
14
).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
C_{0},E\subset_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
x\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{2m}
, and
t\geq 1
, and define
\{C_{l}\}_{1\leq l\leq 2m}
according to

(15)

C_{l}=\begin{cases}C_{0}\setminus\{x_{j}\}_{j\leq l}&l\leq m,\\
\big(C_{0}\setminus\{x_{j}\}_{j\leq m}\big)\cup\{x_{j}\}_{j=m+1}^{l}&l>m.\end{cases}

There are
a,b>0
such that, if the following probabilities are positive, then they in fact satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\mathscr{C}_{0,l+1}=C_{l+1}\mid\mathscr{C}_{0,l}=C_{l})

\displaystyle\geq a\|x_{l+1}-x_{l}\|^{-b},

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\mathscr{C}_{1}=C_{2m})

\displaystyle\geq a\mathrm{diam}(\{x_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq 2m})^{-b},\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\mathscr{C}_{t}=E)

\displaystyle\geq a^{t}(t\,\mathrm{diam}(C_{0}))^{-bt}.

Proof.

We consider each bound in turn. First, by the definition of the CAT dynamics,

(16)

\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\mathscr{C}_{0,l+1}=C_{l+1}\mid\mathscr{C}_{0,l}=C_{l})=\begin{cases}\mu_{C_{l},x_{l}}(x_{l+1})&l\leq m\\
\mu_{\partial C_{l},x_{l}}(x_{l+1})&l>m.\end{cases}

By (
3
) and (
14
), if
U\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is finite and nonempty, and if
u,v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, then either
\mu_{U,u}(v)=0
or
\mu_{U,u}(v)\geq|U|^{-1}\|u-v\|^{-\beta}
. Hence, if the conditional probability in (
16
) is positive, then it satisfies

(17)

\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\mathscr{C}_{0,l+1}=C_{l+1}\mid\mathscr{C}_{0,l}=C_{l})\geq(2dn)^{-1}\|x_{l+1}-x_{l}\|^{-\beta},

because
|C_{l}|\leq n
and
|\partial C_{l}|\leq 2dn
.

Second, assume that
\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\mathscr{C}_{1}=C_{2m})>0
, in which case there must be
x\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{2m}
and
\{C_{l}\}_{1\leq l\leq 2m}
that satisfy (
15
) and
\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\mathscr{C}_{0,1}=C_{1},\dots,\mathscr{C}_{0,2m}=C_{2m})>0
. We have

(18)

\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\mathscr{C}_{1}=C_{2m})\geq\prod_{1\leq l\leq 2m-1}\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\mathscr{C}_{0,l+1}=C_{l}\mid\mathscr{C}_{0,l}=C_{l})\geq(2dn)^{-2m}\prod_{1\leq l\leq 2m-1}\|x_{l+1}-x_{l}\|^{-\beta}.

The Markov property implies the first inequality, as well as the fact that
\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\mathscr{C}_{0,l+1}=C_{l+1}\mid\mathscr{C}_{0,l}=C_{l})>0
for each
l\in\llbracket 1,2m-1\rrbracket
. Hence, the second inequality follows from (
17
). Bounding above
\|x_{l+1}-x_{l}\|
by
\mathrm{diam}(\{x_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq 2m})
, we find that

(19)

\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\mathscr{C}_{1}=C_{2m})\geq(2dn)^{-2m}\mathrm{diam}(\{x_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq 2m})^{-2m\beta}.

Third, assume that
\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\mathscr{C}_{t}=C^{\prime})>0
, in which case there is a sequence of configurations
D_{0},\dots,D_{t}
with
D_{0}=C_{0}
and
D_{t}=E
, and for which
\mathbb{P}_{D_{0}}(\mathscr{C}_{1}=D_{1},\dots,\mathscr{C}_{t}=D_{t})>0
. Consider
s\in\llbracket 1,t\rrbracket
. Note that, if
y\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{2m}
and
\{D_{s,l}\}_{1\leq l\leq 2m}
satisfy (
15
) in the place of
x
and
\{C_{l}\}_{1\leq l\leq 2m}
, and if
\mathbb{P}_{D_{s-1}}(\mathscr{C}_{0,1}=D_{s,1},\dots,\mathscr{C}_{0,2m}=D_{s,2m})>0
then the a.m.l.g. property (
4
) implies that

\|y_{l+1}-y_{l}\|\leq\mathrm{diam}(C_{0})+tm\leq 2mt\mathrm{diam}(C_{0}).

Hence, by (

\mathbb{P}_{D_{s-1}}(\mathscr{C}_{1}=D_{s})\geq(2dn(2m)^{\beta})^{-2m}\big(t\,\mathrm{diam}(C_{0})\big)^{-2m\beta}.

By applying the Markov property to each such
s
, we conclude that

(20)

\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\mathscr{C}_{t}=E)\geq(2dn(2m)^{\beta})^{-2m}\big(t\,\mathrm{diam}(C_{0})\big)^{-2m\beta t}.

The three claimed bounds follow from (
17
), (
19
), and (
20
), with
a=(2dn(2m)^{\beta})^{-2m}
and
b=2m\beta
.
∎

The probability measure
\mu_{A,u}
“picks”
v\in A
in proportion to
\phi(u,v)
. If
v
in fact belongs to
B\subset A
, then
\mu_{B,u}(v)\geq\mu_{A,u}(v)
, since there are fewer elements in
B
. The following result further shows that
\mu_{A,u}(v)
is at least a fraction of
\mu_{B,u}(v)
, which is closer to one when the diameter of
B\cup\{u\}
is smaller relative to the distance from
u
to
A\setminus B
. The proof uses simple bounds on
\phi
.

Lemma 2.2

(Ratio estimate)
.

Let
B\subseteq A\subset_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
u\in A^{c}
, and
v\in B
. Then,

(21)

\frac{\mu_{A,u}(v)}{\mu_{B,u}(v)}\geq 1-n\frac{\mathrm{diam}(B\cup\{u\})^{\beta}}{\mathrm{dist}(u,A\setminus B)^{\beta}}.

Proof.

By definition (
14
),

\mu_{A,u}(v)=\frac{\phi(u,v)}{\sum_{z\in B}\phi(u,z)+\sum_{z\in A\setminus B}\phi(u,z)}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{B,u}(v)=\frac{\phi(u,v)}{\sum_{z\in B}\phi(u,z)}.

Since
\frac{1}{1+r}\geq 1-r
when
r\geq 0
,

\frac{\mu_{A,u}(v)}{\mu_{B,u}(v)}\geq 1-\frac{\sum_{z\in A\setminus B}\phi(u,z)}{\sum_{z\in B}\phi(u,z)}.

By assumption,
x\notin A
, so
\phi(u,z)=\|u-z\|^{-\beta}
for every
z\in A
. Hence,

\sum_{z\in A\setminus B}\phi(u,z)\leq n\mathrm{dist}(x,A\setminus B)^{-\beta}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{z\in B}\phi(u,z)\geq\mathrm{diam}(B\cup\{u\})^{-\beta}.

The claimed bound (
21
) follows.
∎

The probability that activation and transport occur according to a particular sequence
x\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{2m}
is a product of
|C_{0}|^{-1}
(to account for the initial activation at
x_{1}
) and probability measures of the form
\mu_{C_{l},x_{l}}(x_{l+1})
and

(

suggests that if we add a set

to

, then the latter components change little, so long as

is relatively far from

and

belongs to

or

. This is the content of the next result; the proof is a simple application of Lemma
2.2
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
C_{0}\in\cal S
, let
D
be a finite subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
such that
\mathrm{dist}(C_{0},D)\geq 2m
, and let
x\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{2m}
satisfy
\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\cal X_{0}=x)>0

(22)

\frac{\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}\cup D}(\cal X_{0}=x)}{\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\cal X_{0}=x)}\geq\frac{|C_{0}|}{|C_{0}\cup D|}\left(1-c|C_{0}\cup D|\frac{\mathrm{diam}(C_{0})^{\beta}}{\mathrm{dist}(C_{0},D)^{\beta}}\right)^{2m}.

Proof.

By the definition of the CAT dynamics,

(23)

\frac{\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}\cup D}(\cal X_{0}=x)}{\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\cal X_{0}=x)}=\frac{|C_{0}|}{|C_{0}\cup D|}\prod_{l=1}^{m-1}\frac{\mu_{C_{l}\cup D,x_{l}}(x_{l+1})}{\mu_{C_{l},x_{l}}(x_{l+1})}\prod_{l=m}^{2m-1}\frac{\mu_{\partial(C_{l}\cup D),x_{l}}(x_{l+1})}{\mu_{\partial C_{l},x_{l}}(x_{l+1})},

where
(C_{l})_{1\leq l\leq 2m}
is defined by (
15
).
We aim to apply Lemma
2.2
with

A=\begin{cases}C_{l}\cup D&1\leq l<m,\\
\partial(C_{l}\cup D)&m\leq l<2m,\end{cases}\quad B=\begin{cases}C_{l}&1\leq l<m,\\
\partial C_{l}&m\leq l<2m,\end{cases}\quad u=x_{l},\quad\text{and}\quad v=x_{l+1},

for each
l
. The hypotheses of Lemma

require that

,
u\in A^{c}
, and
v\in B
. The third requirement is met by (
15
). The first two requirements are met because

Indeed, the second point is due to the assumption that

\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\cal X_{0}=x)>0

By Lemma

\frac{\mu_{C_{l}\cup D,x_{l}}(x_{l+1})}{\mu_{C_{l},x_{l}}(x_{l+1})}\geq 1-|C_{l}\cup D|\frac{\mathrm{diam}(C_{l}\cup\{x_{l}\})^{\beta}}{\mathrm{dist}(x_{l},D)^{\beta}}\geq 1-|C_{0}\cup D|\frac{\mathrm{diam}(C_{0})^{\beta}}{\mathrm{dist}(C_{0},D)^{\beta}}.

The second inequality holds because
C_{l}\subseteq C_{0}

3.
Proof ideas

3.1.
Idea #1: A way to obtain diameter–agnostic bounds

The key feature of Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
is that their conclusions are agnostic of the diameter of the initial state, despite the dependence of the CAT transition probabilities on the distances between elements.
The simple idea underlying these results is that it is typical for CAT to form a more “desirable” configuration when activation is initiated at an undesirable subset. For example, this is true when it is desirable for a configuration to concentrate its elements in a “small” ball or to consist of “large” connected components. This idea leads to diameter–agnostic bounds because activation begins uniformly at random.

3.1.1.
Application to Theorem
1.2

Theorem
1.2
aims to concentrate the elements of a configuration in a ball with a diameter that does not depend on the diameter of the initial state. To obtain a more desirable configuration, we deplete the elements outside of this ball, i.e., through the occurrence of the event

\mathsf{AddToBall}_{x,r}:=\big\{|\mathscr{C}_{1}\cap\cal B_{x}(r+m)|>|\mathscr{C}_{0}\cap\cal B_{x}(r)|\big\},

which is defined for
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
r\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. The key input to the proof of Theorem
1.2
is a lower bound on the probability that this event occurs.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
n\leq 2m+1
and if
C\subset_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
satisfies
|C\cap\cal B_{x}(r)|\in\llbracket m+1,n-1\rrbracket
for some
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
r\geq 1
, then

(24)

\mathbb{P}_{C}(\mathsf{AddToBall}_{x,r})\geq p,

for
p>0
only depending on
r
.

Proof.

The expression of
Idea #1
is the claim that

(25)

\mathbb{P}_{C}(\mathsf{AddToBall}_{x,r}\mid J>1)\geq p,

for some
p>0
only depending on
r
, where
J:=\inf\{j:\mathcal{A}_{0,j}\in\cal B_{x}(r)\}
. To prove (
24
), it suffices to prove (
25
) instead, since

\mathbb{P}_{C}(J>1)=\mathbb{P}_{C}(\mathcal{A}_{0,1}\notin\cal B_{x}(r))\geq\frac{1}{n}.

The inequality follows from the fact that
\mathcal{A}_{0,1}
is uniformly random in
C
, at least one element of which lies outside of
\cal B_{x}(r)
by assumption.

3.1.2.
Application to Theorem

in

Lemma 3.2
.

There is
p>0
such that, if

contains some

\mathbb{P}_{C}(\mathsf{DepleteSmallComps}_{0})\geq p.

Proof.

The expression of

\mathbb{P}_{C}(\mathsf{DepleteSmallComps}_{0}\mid K>1)\geq p,

We claim that

\mathsf{DepleteSmallComps}_{0}\cap\{K>1\}\supseteq\mathsf{DepleteSmallComps}^{\prime}\cap\{K>1\},

3.2.
Idea #2: An increasingly accurate approximation of the CAT dynamics

The key idea of the proof of Theorem
1.4
is that, when a configuration of
n\geq 2m+2
elements consists of clusters of
m+1
or more elements, the CAT dynamics approximately behaves as if the clusters inhabited separate “copies” of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. This approximate dynamics is easier to analyze than the CAT dynamics. Critically, the accuracy of this approximation improves with greater cluster separation. Hence, we can use it to estimate the probability of events that entail sufficiently rapid separation growth, including the event in Theorem
1.4
.

To make this precise, we ‘lift” CAT to a Markov chain
\mathscr{C}_{t}^{I}=(\mathscr{C}_{t}^{i})_{i\in I}
on the state space
\mathrm{Part}
, the collection of ordered partitions of configurations in
\cal S
, each part of which has more than
m
elements. Here,
I
is an interval of the form
\llbracket 1,k\rrbracket
for some
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
. Given
\mathscr{C}_{t}^{I}
, to form
\mathscr{C}_{t+1}^{I}
, we simply apply the CAT dynamics to
\mathscr{C}_{t}:=\cup_{i\in I}\mathscr{C}_{t}^{i}\in\cal S
and update the partition to reflect the new positions of the elements. In other words, we treat the partition like a fixed coloring of the elements, which plays no role in the dynamics. We emphasize that, in the context of a partition
C^{I}
, we use
C
to denote
\cup_{i\in I}C^{i}
.

Next, we define
CopyCAT
to be the Markov chain
\mathscr{D}_{t}^{I}=(\mathscr{D}_{t}^{i})_{i\in I}
on the state space of tuples of configurations,
\mathrm{Tup}=\cup_{i\geq 1}{\cal S}^{i}
, with the following dynamics. Given
\mathscr{D}_{t}^{I}
, to form
\mathscr{D}_{t+1}^{I}
, we apply the CAT dynamics to one entry,
\mathscr{D}_{t}^{J_{t}}
, randomly chosen with a probability proportional to
|\mathscr{D}_{t}^{i}|
. In other words,

(31)

\mathscr{D}_{t+1}^{i}:=\begin{cases}\mathrm{CAT}(\mathscr{D}_{t}^{i})&i=J_{t},\\
\mathscr{D}_{t}^{i}&i\neq J_{t},\end{cases}

where, for any
C,C^{\prime}\in\cal S
and
i\in I
,

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{CAT}(C)=C^{\prime})=\mathbb{P}_{C}(\mathscr{C}_{1}=C^{\prime})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}(J_{t}=i\mid\mathscr{D}_{t})=\frac{|\mathscr{D}_{t}^{i}|}{\sum_{i\in I}|\mathscr{D}_{t}^{i}|}.

We use
\cal Y_{t}
to denote the sequence of elements of
\mathscr{D}_{t}^{I}
that are activated and transported elements at time
t
, i.e., the CopyCAT analogue of
\cal X_{t}
.

Figure 5.
The lifting of CAT corresponds to a coloring which is ignored by the dynamics (left), while a CopyCAT configuration may have elements in different “copies” of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
(right).

To state the key approximation result, we define the separation of
U^{I}\in\mathrm{Tup}
to be

\mathrm{sep}(U^{I}):=\min_{i\in I}\mathrm{dist}(U^{i},U^{\neq i}),

where
U^{\neq i}=\cup_{j\in I\setminus\{i\}}U^{j}
.
For
a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, we define tuples with absolute separation
a
and
relative
(to diameter)
separation

b
by

(32)

\mathrm{Tup}_{a,b}:=\left\{U^{I}\in\mathrm{Tup}:\mathrm{sep}(U^{I})\geq a\quad\text{and}\quad\forall i\in I,\,\,\mathrm{diam}(U^{i})\leq b\log\mathrm{dist}(U^{i},U^{\neq i})\right\}.

Note that
\mathrm{Tup}_{a,b}\subseteq\mathrm{Part}
since
a
is positive. For
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
, and
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we define
\mathrm{Growth}_{a,b,\gamma,\delta;t}
to be the set of sequences
(U_{s}^{I})_{s\geq 0}\in\mathrm{Tup}_{a,b}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that

\forall s\leq t,\quad\mathrm{sep}(U_{s}^{I})\geq\gamma s^{1/2-\delta}.

Additionally, we define
\mathrm{Growth}_{a,b,\gamma,\delta}=\cap_{t\geq 0}\mathrm{Growth}_{a,b,\gamma,\delta;t}
.

Proposition 3.1

(Key approximation result)
.

Let
b,\gamma>0
and
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\beta})
. If
a\geq 2m
is sufficiently large, then

\mathbb{P}_{C^{I}}\left((\mathscr{C}_{t}^{I})_{t\geq 0}\in\mathrm{Growth}_{a,b,\gamma,\delta}\right)\geq(1-o_{a}(1))\,\mathbb{P}_{C^{I}}\left((\mathscr{D}_{t}^{I})_{t\geq 0}\in\mathrm{Growth}_{a,b,\gamma,\delta}\right)

for every
C^{I}\in\mathrm{Tup}_{a,b}
.

The main input to the proof of Proposition
3.1
is a comparison of the one-step transition probabilities of CAT and CopyCAT.

Proposition 3.2

(One-step approximation)
.

Let
b>0
and let
U^{I},V^{I}\in\mathrm{Tup}_{2m,b}
satisfy
\mathbb{P}_{U^{I}}(\mathscr{C}_{1}^{I}=V^{I})>0
. There is
c>0
such that

(33)

\mathbb{P}_{U^{I}}\left(\mathscr{C}_{1}^{I}=V^{I}\right)\geq\left(1-c|U|\,\mathrm{sep}(U^{I})^{-\beta}\big(\log\mathrm{sep}(U^{I})\big)^{\beta}\right)^{2m}\mathbb{P}_{U^{I}}\left(\mathscr{D}_{1}^{I}=V^{I}\right).

Proposition
3.1
is a simple consequence of the Markov property, Proposition
3.2
, and the definition of
\mathrm{Growth}_{a,b,\gamma,\delta}
.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Let
t\geq 0
. By the Markov property and Proposition
3.2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{C^{I}}\left((\mathscr{C}_{s}^{I})_{s\geq 0}\in\mathrm{Growth}_{a,b,\gamma,\delta;t}\right)=

\displaystyle\sum\mathbb{P}_{C^{I}}\left(\mathscr{C}_{s}^{I}=D_{s}^{I},s\geq 0\right)

\displaystyle\geq w_{t}

\displaystyle\sum\mathbb{P}_{C^{I}}\left(\mathscr{D}_{s}^{I}=D_{s}^{I},s\geq 0\right)=w_{t}\mathbb{P}_{C^{I}}\left((\mathscr{D}_{s}^{I})_{s\geq 0}\in\mathrm{Growth}_{a,b,\gamma,\delta;t}\right),

where the sums range over
(D_{s}^{I})_{s\geq 0}\in\mathrm{Growth}_{a,b,\gamma,\delta;t}
. Here,
w_{t}=\prod_{s\leq t}\left(1-cr_{a}(s)^{-\beta}(\log r_{a}(s))^{\beta}\right)^{2m}
, where
c>0
is the number of the same name in (
33
) and
r_{a}(s)=\max\{a,\gamma s^{1/2-\delta}\}
. Since
\mathrm{Growth}_{a,b,\gamma,\delta;t}
decreases with
t
, the continuity of
\mathbb{P}_{C^{I}}
implies that

\mathbb{P}_{C^{I}}\left((\mathscr{C}_{s}^{I})_{s\geq 0}\in\mathrm{Growth}_{a,b,\gamma,\delta}\right)\geq\lim_{t\to\infty}w_{t}\,\mathbb{P}_{C^{I}}\left((\mathscr{D}_{s}^{I})_{s\geq 0}\in\mathrm{Growth}_{a,b,\gamma,\delta}\right).

It is easy to see that, since
\beta>2
, if
\delta<\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\beta}
, then
\sum_{s\geq 0}r_{a}(s)^{-\beta}(\log r_{a}(s))^{\beta}=o_{a}(1)
, which implies that
\lim_{t\to\infty}w_{t}=(1-o_{a}(1))
.
∎

Proposition
3.2
is an application of Lemma
2.3
.

Proof of Proposition
3.2
.

Fix
U^{I},V^{I}\in\mathrm{Tup}_{2m,b}
and define
\cal G
and
\cal H
to be the activation and transport sequences
x\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{2m}
that form
V^{I}
from
U^{I}
under the CAT and CopyCAT dynamics, respectively:

\displaystyle\cal G

\displaystyle:=\left\{x\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{2m}:\{\mathscr{C}_{0}^{I}=U^{I},\cal X_{0}=x\}\subseteq\{\mathscr{C}_{1}^{I}=V^{I}\}\,\,\text{and}\,\,\mathbb{P}_{U^{I}}(\cal X_{0}=x)>0\right\},

\displaystyle\cal H

\displaystyle:=\left\{x\in(\mathbb{Z}^{d})^{2m}:\{\mathscr{D}_{0}^{I}=U^{I},\cal Y_{0}=x\}\subseteq\{\mathscr{D}_{1}^{I}=V^{I}\}\,\,\text{and}\,\,\mathbb{P}_{U^{I}}(\cal Y_{0}=x)>0\right\}.

Observe that
\cal G\supseteq\cal H
because
V^{I}
is separated by more than
m
. Indeed, so long as the clusters are too far apart to intersect after one step of the CopyCAT dynamics, the only difference between
\cal G
and
\cal H
is that there are
x\in\cal G\setminus\cal H
that correspond to activation at more than one cluster.

We calculate

(34)

\mathbb{P}_{U^{I}}(\mathscr{C}_{1}^{I}=V^{I})=\sum_{x\in\cal G}\mathbb{P}_{U^{I}}(\cal X_{0}=x)\geq\sum_{x\in\cal H}\mathbb{P}_{U^{I}}(\cal X_{0}=x)\geq\sum_{x\in\cal H}w_{x}\,\frac{|U^{i_{x}}|}{|U|}\,\mathbb{P}_{U^{i_{x}}}(\cal X_{0}=x),

where
i_{x}
is the cluster of
U^{I}
to which
x_{1}
belongs and

w_{x}=\left(1-c|U|\frac{\mathrm{diam}(U^{i_{x}})^{\beta}}{\mathrm{dist}(U^{i_{x}},U^{\neq i_{x}})^{\beta}}\right)^{2m},

in terms of the quantity
c
from Lemma
2.3
.
The equality in (
34
) holds by definition, the first inequality by
\cal G\supseteq\cal H
, and the second inequality by Lemma
2.3
applied with
C_{0}=U^{i_{x}}
and
D=U^{\neq i_{x}}
. The use of this lemma requires that
\mathrm{dist}(C_{0},D)\geq 2m
and
\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\cal X_{0}=x)>0
. The former requirement is satisfied because
U^{I}
has separation of at least
2m
, while the latter is satisfied because
\mathbb{P}_{U^{I}}(\cal Y_{0}=x)
is positive by virtue of
x\in\cal H
, which implies the same is true of
\mathbb{P}_{C_{0}}(\cal X_{0}=x)
because

\mathbb{P}_{U^{I}}(\cal Y_{0}=x)=\frac{|U^{i_{x}}|}{|U|}\,\mathbb{P}_{U^{i_{x}}}(\cal X_{0}=x)

by (
31

\mathbb{P}_{U^{I}}(\mathscr{D}_{1}^{I}=V^{I})=\sum_{x\in\cal H}\mathbb{P}_{U^{I}}(\cal Y_{0}=x)
, hence by substituting the preceding display into (

The claimed bound (

\min_{x\in\cal H}w_{x}\geq\min_{j\in I}\left(1-c|U|\frac{\big(b\log\mathrm{dist}(U^{j},U^{\neq j})\big)^{\beta}}{\mathrm{dist}(U^{j},U^{\neq j})^{\beta}}\right)^{2m}\geq\left(1-cb^{\beta}|U|\frac{\big(\log\mathrm{sep}(U^{I})\big)^{\beta}}{\mathrm{sep}(U^{I})^{\beta}}\right)^{2m}.

The first inequality holds because
U^{I}\in\mathrm{Tup}_{\cdot,b}
, while the second holds because
\mathrm{dist}(U^{j},U^{\neq j})\geq\mathrm{sep}(U^{I})
and

is decreasing in

.
∎

4.
Diameter collapse

Theorem
1.2
states that, if
n\leq 2m+1
, then the diameter of
\mathscr{C}_{n}
is at most
2n^{2}
with a probability of at least some
p>0
.
We prove Theorem
1.2
with an estimate of
\tau_{x}
, defined for
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as the first time
t
that the number
N_{x}(t)
of elements of
\mathscr{C}_{t}
in the ball
\cal B_{x}(tm+n)
equals
n
:

(35)

\tau_{x}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:N_{x}(t)=n\}\quad\text{where}\quad N_{x}(t):=|\mathscr{C}_{t}\cap\cal B_{x}(tm+n)|.

When
\{\tau_{x}\leq m\}
occurs, the diameter of
\mathscr{C}_{n}
satisfies

(36)

\mathrm{diam}(\mathscr{C}_{n})\leq 2(\tau_{x}m+n)+(n-\tau_{x})m=\tau_{x}m+n(m+2)\leq 2n^{2}.

The first inequality follows from the definition of
\tau_{x}
and a.m.l.g. (
4
). The second follows from bounding
\tau_{x}
by
m
,
m
by
n-1
, and

by

.

4.1.
Application of Lemma
3.1

The next proposition states that

for certain

. The proof is an application of Lemma
3.1
. In the context of (

for such

Proposition 4.1
.

Let

satisfy

for some

such that

\mathbb{P}_{C}(\tau_{x}\leq m)\geq p.

Proof.

For
0\leq j<k\leq m
, denote by

the event that
N_{x}(t)
increases with each step from

to
t=k
, i.e.,
A_{j,k}=\{N_{x}(j)<\cdots<N_{x}(k)\}
. For notational convenience, define

to be the sample space. The key observation is that

(38)

\mathbb{P}_{C}(\tau_{x}\leq m\mid\tau_{x}>0)\geq\mathbb{P}_{C}(A_{0,\tau_{x}}\mid\tau_{x}>0).

Indeed,
\cal B_{x}(n)
contains at least
m+1
elements of the

elements of

by assumption, so

cannot increase more than

times consecutively.

Lemma
3.1
implies that the right hand side of (

) is at least

for some

) holds with

.
To see why, define

according to

\displaystyle f_{j}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}_{C}(A_{j-1,\tau_{x}}\mid\tau_{x}>j-1,A_{0,j-1}),

\displaystyle g_{j}

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}_{C}(A_{j-1,j}\mid\tau_{x}>j-1,A_{0,j-1}),

and note that

In particular,

for each

for each

\mathscr{C}_{j}

belongs to
\llbracket m+1,n-1\rrbracket
, hence

satisfies the hypotheses on

. Consequently, by applying the Markov property at

implies that

for some

.
∎

5.1.
Proof of Theorem

Recall that for

Proposition 5.1
.

for

6.
Cluster formation

In this section, we prove Theorem

6.1.
Staying lemma

We need a basic fact about CAT. If

(70)

\exists V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{m}\}\subset_{m}U:\quad\mathrm{diam}(V)\leq n\quad\text{and}\quad v_{l}\in\partial\big(U\setminus\{v_{l},\dots,v_{m}\}\big),\,\,1\leq l\leq m,

n
-element subsets of

Lemma 6.1

(Staying lemma)
.

Let

n
-element subsets of

C\subset_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}

Since

C\subset_{n}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
By Lemma
2.1
, there is

such that
\mathbb{P}_{U}(\mathscr{C}_{1}=\mathscr{C}_{0})\geq r

for every

that satisfies (

and the fact that

satisfies (

occurs, we have
