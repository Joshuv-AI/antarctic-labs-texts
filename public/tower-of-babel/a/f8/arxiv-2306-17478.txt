Leveraging Observational Data for Efficient CATE Estimation in Randomized Controlled Trials
Amir Asiaee, Chiara Di Gravio, Yuting Mei, Jared D. Huling

Abstract
Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are the gold standard for causal inference, but they are often powered only for average effects, making estimation of heterogeneous treatment effects (HTEs) challenging. Conversely, large-scale observational studies (OS) offer a wealth of data but suffer from confounding bias. Our paper presents a novel framework to leverage OS data for enhancing the efficiency in estimation of conditional average treatment effects (CATEs) from RCTs, while mitigating common biases. We propose an innovative approach to combine RCTs and OS data, expanding the traditionally used control arms from external sources. The framework relaxes the typical assumption of CATE invariance across populations, acknowledging the often unaccounted systematic differences between RCT and OS participants. We demonstrate this through the special case of a linear outcome model, where the CATE is sparsely different between the two populations. The core of our framework relies on learning potential outcome means from OS data, and use them as a nuisance parameter in CATE estimation from RCT data. We further illustrate through experiments that using OS findings reduces the variance of the estimated CATE from RCTs and can decrease the required sample size for detecting HTEs.



1
Introduction

Randomized controlled trials (
RCTs
) are the gold standard for addressing causal questions, such as estimating heterogeneous treatment effects (
HTEs
) – a key area of interest in personalized decision-making. However, RCTs present significant challenges in terms of design, cost, and duration. Furthermore, due to financial and time constraints, RCTs are often limited in size and scope, which hinders their ability to detect HTEs. In contrast, large-scale observational studies (
OS
) provide easily accessible data from various sources, such as administrative databases, cohort studies, biobanks, and electronic health records. Yet, it is well known that causal effects estimation in OS is impossible without relying on untestable assumptions (e.g., unconfoundedness). As a result, recent trends have emerged to combine both data types in order to mitigate biases in causal effect estimation from OS or enhance the efficiency of RCT-based estimators. Successfully merging OS and RCT data holds considerable potential to advance our understanding of HTEs for a target population.

Data integration typically serves to improve the estimated causal effect for a pre-determined target population. When the source population is an RCT and the target population is an OS (with or without measured treatment and outcome), data integration corresponds to generalizing or transporting causal effects from the RCT population to the OS population
[
1
]
. This direction is well-explored in the literature and is not our focus. We refer readers to two recent comprehensive reviews on generalizability and transportability
[
2
,
3
]
. In this work, we address the other direction of data integration, using OS to reduce the variance of estimated CATE from RCT and potentially decrease the required sample size for detecting HTEs.

Data integration methods aimed at increasing precision in an RCT have been discussed in the clinical trials literature since the 1976 seminal paper by Pocock
[
4
]
. Briefly, the idea is to augment the control arm in the current RCT with data from similar control arms observed in previous RCTs and/or OS. These methods refer to the borrowed data as historical controls and assume some form of exchangeability between the current RCT and the historical controls. Integration of RCTs and OS is then performed using two possible methods: static borrowing
[
5
,
6
]
which account for the external data as if they were part of the current RCT, and dynamic borrowing
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
that weight the data to be borrowed based on the degree of agreement between the current RCT and historical controls.

While the RCT literature has primarily focused on utilizing historical control arms from external sources to minimize estimation variance, the full potential of incorporating all relevant arms of observational studies remains largely unexplored, offering significant opportunities for improvement in HTE estimation. Moreover, current integration efforts (either directions) predominantly assume that the conditional average treatment effect (
CATE
) is invariant across populations, implying that CATE is “transportable.” However, this transportability assumption, along with its stricter forms (mean exchangeability or trial participation ignorability)
[
2
]
, often does not hold true in real-world applications, particularly when unrecorded systematic differences exist between RCT and OS participants
[
11
]
.

In this work, we aim to develop a framework for valid and efficient estimation of the CATE, which characterizes HTE, for the RCT population by leveraging the complementary information contained in the OS data. We introduce a general framework that reduces CATE estimation uncertainty in RCT analyses by incorporating information from OS, while relaxing the commonly required assumption of CATE invariance. We instantiate our general framework for the special case of the linear outcome model, wherein the CATE is sparsely different (defined later) between the two populations.

2
Related Work

Decision-making is complex when multiple treatment options are present, as most evidence is represented through Average Treatment Effect (
ATE
). In various applications, such as precision medicine, distinct patient subgroups are expected to show different responses to treatments, necessitating an understanding of HTE. To improve the evidence quality regarding HTE, we aim to merge multiple data sources. However, integrating diverse data sources poses challenges, as data often come from populations with differing covariate distributions, treatment versions, or unmeasured confounders, resulting from design choices or unmeasured inter-study biases. Consequently, conclusions drawn from one study might not directly apply to another population without making certain assumptions about the underlying causes of population differences. We first introduce necessary notation in this section. Afterward, we discuss the commonly adopted assumptions when integrating data and explore their potential limitations.

2.2
Assumptions

Most methods for transportability focus on mitigating changes in covariate distribution (
\mathbb{P}^{r}(x)\neq\mathbb{P}^{o}(x)
) across studies, while assuming the invariance of some component of the outcome model based on one of the following assumptions:

1.

CATE invariance
(
\tau^{r}(x)=\tau^{o}(x)
): Assumes that the causal effects for subgroups are invariant.

2.

Mean exchangeability
(
\forall a:\mu^{r}_{a}(x)=\mu^{o}_{a}(x)
): Assumes that the potential outcome model for each treatment arm is invariant across populations.

3.

Trial participation ignorability
(
Y(A),Y(A^{\prime})\perp S\mid X
): Assumes that units are not preferentially selected to participate in studies based on their potential outcomes.

Assumptions are listed in order of their strength, with the weaker ones being derivable from the stronger ones. It is worth noting that the strongest assumption, trial participation ignorability, is equivalent to “no outcome shift,” meaning
\mathbb{P}^{r}(Y(a)\mid X=x,A=a)=\mathbb{P}^{o}(Y(a)\mid X=x,A=a)
.
Essentially, all of the mainstream assumptions can be derived from the no outcome shift assumption. However, we argue that this assumption is not realistic, and by providing methods that relax it, we lay the foundation for further relaxing other assumptions.

Transportability of the outcome model depends on the similarities between populations in terms of effect modification, treatment versions, and interference between units
[
11
]
. Addressing interference, including changes in the interference pattern, poses a significant challenge in general
[
12
]
. Assuming no interference, controlling for the shift in known (measured) effect modifiers and treatment versions is possible but complicated. However, if there are unmeasured effect modifiers
[
13
]
and/or treatment versions
[
14
]
, they can lead to outcome shift between the populations. For example, differences in outcome models between RCTs and OS can be attributed to factors such as higher fidelity of the intervention in RCTs (e.g., providers in the RCT being more stricter in the application of the treatment), the Hawthorne effect (participants in trials may behave differently due to being monitored), or participation bias (certain socioeconomic groups being more likely to participate, with socioeconomic status not being recorded in the OS). While data integration frameworks have accounted for the shift in covariate distributions across studies, they often overlook potential shifts in patient outcomes caused by unmeasured variations between studies. Our aim is to extend the current integration framework by relaxing this assumption.

Methods for Borrowing Strength from OSs

Under SUTVA
\left(Y_{i}^{\text{obs }}=Y_{i}\left(A_{i}\right)\right)
and conditional ignorability
(Y(-1),Y(+1)\perp A\mid X)
we have the following “Outcome Mean Decomposition” for each population:

\mathbb{E}(Y\mid X,A)=\mathbb{E}(Y(A)\mid X)=\mu(X)+\left\{(1+A)\pi_{+1}(X)-(1-A)\pi_{-1}(X)\right\}\tau(X)/2,

(2)

For example, in an RCT with
\pi_{+1}^{r}(x)=\pi_{-1}^{r}(x)=1/2
the decomposition simplifies to
\mu^{r}(X,A)=\mu_{A}^{r}(X)=\mu^{r}(X)+\frac{A}{2}\tau^{r}(X)
.
Since the treatment
A
is not present in the CFACE definition,
\mu(X)
, it is also known as
treatment-free
or
main
effect.
Guided by the above decomposition, we propose an unbiased CATE estimator for the RCT, that contains a nuisance parameter
m(x)
, which if appropriately set to
\mu^{r}(x)
, yields minimized variance for estimated
\tau^{r}(X)
.

3.1
General Framework

First, we can show that the population CATE is the minimizer of the following objective.

Proposition 1
.

Assume SUTVA, conditional ignorability, and positivity
\left(\forall X,A:\pi_{A}(X)>0\right)
hold. Then, for arbitrary
m(X)
and
\pi_{A}(X)
functions, the minimizer of below objective
\mathcal{L}(f)
is CATE:

\tau(X)=\underset{f}{\operatorname{argmin}}\mathcal{L}(f),\quad\mathcal{L}(f)\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{A(Y-m(X))}{\pi_{A}(X)}-f(X)\right)^{2}\right],

(3)

In objective (
3
), although both
m(X)
and
\pi_{A}(X)
are arbitrary, the latter is fixed in RCTs. Therefore, we have the freedom to select an
m(\cdot)
to minimize the estimated CATE’s variance. Similar objective functions have been proposed in
[
15
]
. Setting
m(X):=\mu(X)
will result in the most efficient CATE estimate:

Theorem 1
.

Setting
m(X):=\mu(X)
results in the most efficient CATE estimate in (
3
). In other words, CFACE minimizes the CATE variance:

\mu(X)=\argmin_{m}\mathbb{V}(\tau(X)),\quad\mathbb{V}(\tau(X))=\mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{A^{2}(Y-m(X))^{2}}{\pi^{2}_{A}(X)}\bigg|X\bigg]-\tau^{2}(X).

Proof.

Only the first term of variance depends on
m(X)
. First order condition implies
\mathbb{E}_{A,Y}((Y-m)/\pi^{2}_{A})=0
, which becomes
\mathbb{E}_{A}\left[\pi^{-2}_{A}(X)\mathbb{E}_{Y}[Y|X,A]\middle|X\right]=m(X)\mathbb{E}_{A}\left[\pi^{-2}_{A}(X)\middle|X\right]
. Replacing the potential outcomes in the LHS and expanding the expectation in the RHS, results in the following formula for the optimal
m(\cdot)
:

\frac{1}{\pi_{+1}(X)}\mathbb{E}[Y(1)|X]+\frac{1}{\pi_{-1}(X)}\mathbb{E}[Y(-1)|X]=m(X)\frac{1}{\pi_{+1}(X)\pi_{-1}(X)}

Solving for
m(X)
completes the proof.
∎

3.1.1
Proposed CATE Estimator in the RCT

We instantiate above results for the RCT data where ignorability and positivity hold by design. The plug-in estimator of
\tau^{r}(x)
is:

\hat{\tau}^{r}\left(x^{r}\right)=\underset{f}{\operatorname{argmin}}\frac{1}{n^{r}}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{r}}\left[\frac{A_{i}^{r}\left(Y_{i}^{r}-m\left(X_{i}^{r}\right)\right)}{\pi_{A_{i}^{r}}^{r}\left(X_{i}^{r}\right)}-f\left(X_{i}^{r}\right)\right]^{2}.

A special case where
f(\cdot)
is linear and
m(X)=0
was proposed in
[
16
]
. To minimize
\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\tau}^{r}(X)\right)
, guided by the above efficiency discussion, it is tempting to set
m(\cdot)
to the plug-in estimator of
\mu^{r}(\cdot)
, i.e.,
m(x):=\hat{\mu}^{r}(x)=\sum_{a}\pi_{-a}^{r}(x)\hat{\mu}_{a}^{r}(x)
where
\hat{\mu}_{a}^{r}(x)
is the estimate of the regression function
{\mu}_{a}^{r}(x)
. But since the RCT is generally limited in sample size, we hope that the CFACE estimated from OS reduces
\mathbb{V}\left(\hat{\tau}^{r}(X)\right)
more than the CFACE estimated from the RCT.
So, we propose,

m(x):=\hat{\mu}^{r}\left(x^{r}\right)\triangleq\sum_{a\in\{-1,+1\}}\pi_{-a}^{r}\left(x^{r}\right)\times\hat{\mu}^{o}\left(x^{r},a\right),

(4)

where
\hat{\mu}^{o}(\cdot,a):\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the mean outcome function estimated from OS but applied to covariates of RCT. Note that here we have abused the notation a little because in CFACE definition, Table

3.1.2
Identifiability Assumptions

Unbiased CATE estimation using
3
only requires SUTVA, positivity, and conditional ignorability. Since we instantiate
3

\mu^{o}(\cdot,a)
and subsequently CFACE

are not necessary, i.e., if

is misspecified or inaccurately estimated, only the variance reduction gain will be smaller.

3.2
Outcome Shift

So far, we have not assumed any relationship between the joint distributions
\mathbb{P}^{r}(X,Y,A)
and
\mathbb{P}^{o}(X,Y,A)
. Going back to the factorization
1
, we observe that from the three factors
\mathbb{P}^{s}(Y(a)\mid X=x,A=a)
,
\mathbb{P}^{s}(a\mid X=x)
, and
\mathbb{P}^{s}(X=x)
only the former is of our concern in (
4
). The treatment assignment probability in (
4
) is from an RCT and is thus known; the covariate distribution is irrelevant mainly because we are interested in CATE estimation and any shift in
\mathbb{P}^{s}(X=x)
only changes the ATE. So assuming no outcome shift
\mathbb{P}^{r}(Y(a)\mid X=x,A=a)=\mathbb{P}^{o}(Y(a)\mid X=x,A=a)
and no unmeasured confounders in the OS (
\hat{\mu}^{o}_{a}(\cdot)=\hat{\mu}^{o}(\cdot,a)

m(X)
is indeed the plug in estimator for CFACE. In such a case, we can even add the RCT data to the OS data and learn only one outcome function per treatment and estimate CFACE with even more samples. But for reasons explained in Section (
2.2
), “no outcome shift” is not a realistic assumption. Here we relax this assumption by allowing the

3.2.1
Sparse Outcome Shift

Here, we assume a working model that posits linearity of potential outcome means
\mu_{a}^{s}(x)
, which implies linearity of CATE, CFACE, and
f(\cdot)
in (
3
). Although this is a parametric assumption, we note that our techniques are generalizable to nonlinear forms through basis function methods. Even if linearity is violated, our approach still results in interpretable parameter estimates even though it utilizes a linear structure for the outcome model. We demonstrate in our numerical experiments that our proposed methods can improve estimation even when the linearity assumption is violated. Further, in contrast to most of the previous works, we do not assume trial participation ignorability, i.e.,
\tau^{r}(x)\neq\tau^{o}(x)
. But we assume that the change in outcome means between studies is sparse. In other words, the outcome means of two studies are
\mu^{o}(x,a)=\gamma_{a}^{o^{T}}x
and
\mu^{r}(x,a)=\mu_{a}^{r}(x)=

\gamma_{a}^{r^{T}}x=\left(\gamma_{a}^{o}+\delta_{a}\right)^{T}x
, where
\delta_{a}
s are sparse. Sparse outcome shift has been previously proposed as a framework for multi-task learning
[
17
,
18
]
. Finally, without loss of generality, we assume RCT being fully randomized:
\pi_{a}^{r}\left(x^{r}\right)=\pi_{a}^{r}
. In this notation, the two CATEs are,
\tau^{r}(x)=\sum_{a}a\gamma_{a}^{rT}x=\sum_{a}a\left(\gamma_{a}^{o}+\delta_{a}\right)^{T}x\neq\sum_{a}a\gamma_{a}^{oT}x=\tau^{o}(x)
.
The objectives for learning the CFACE and CATE become:

\displaystyle\hat{\gamma}_{a}^{o}=\underset{\gamma}{\operatorname{argmin}}\frac{1}{n_{a}^{o}}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{o}}\mathbbm{1}\left(A_{i}^{o}=a\right)\left[Y_{i}^{o}-\gamma^{T}X_{i}^{o}\right]^{2}+\lambda_{1a}\left\|\gamma\right\|_{1}

(5)

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{\delta}_{-1}^{r},\tilde{\delta}_{+1}^{r}\right)=\underset{\left(\delta_{-1},\delta_{+1}\right)}{\operatorname{argmin}}\frac{1}{n_{a}^{r}}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{r}}\mathbbm{1}\left(A_{i}^{r}=a\right)\left[Y_{i}^{r}-\left(\hat{\gamma}_{a}^{o}+\delta_{a}\right)^{T}X_{i}^{r}\right]^{2}+\sum_{a}\lambda_{2a}\left\|\delta_{a}\right\|_{1},\hat{\gamma}_{a}^{r,c}\triangleq\hat{\gamma}_{a}^{o}+\tilde{\delta}_{a}^{r}

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}^{r}=\underset{\delta}{\operatorname{argmin}}\frac{1}{n^{r}}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{r}}\left[\frac{A_{i}^{r}}{\pi_{A_{i}^{r}}^{r}}\left(Y_{i}^{r}-\sum_{a}\pi_{-a}^{r}\hat{\gamma}_{a}^{rT}X_{i}^{r}\right)-\left(\hat{\gamma}_{+1}^{r,c}-\hat{\gamma}_{-1}^{r,c}+\delta\right)^{T}X_{i}^{r}\right]^{2}+\lambda_{3}\|\delta\|_{1}

(7)

where
\mathbbm{1}(\cdot)
is the indicator function,
\sum_{a}\pi_{-a}^{r}(\hat{\gamma}_{a}^{o}+\tilde{\delta}_{a}^{r})^{T}x_{i}^{r}
in (
7
) is the estimated (suggested in (
4
)) and calibrated CFACE, and the
l_{1}
-norm penalization induces sparsity. Note that in (
6
) and (
7
) we estimate separate deltas for
m(x)
and
\tau(x)
to make the results robust to model misspecification. We call step (
6
)
calibration of OS’s outcome models to the RCT
. The overall estimate of the CATE is then
(\hat{\gamma}_{+1}^{o}+\tilde{\delta}_{+1}^{r}-[\hat{\gamma}_{-1}^{o}+\tilde{\delta}_{-1}^{r}]+\hat{\delta}^{r})^{T}x^{r}
.
The purpose of (
5
) is to provide a preliminary estimate of the CFACE in the OS; the purpose of (
6
) is to calibrate the OS estimate of the CFACE to the RCT data. This calibration also results in the following preliminary estimate of the CATE that is calibrated to the RCT data under the sparsely different assumption:
(\hat{\gamma}_{+1}^{o}+\tilde{\delta}_{+1}^{r}-[\hat{\gamma}_{-1}^{o}+\tilde{\delta}_{-1}^{r}])^{T}x^{r}
. If this preliminary CATE estimate is a good estimate of the CATE in the RCT data, the final objective function is redundant (
7
). The objective in (
7
) leverages the observation data in two ways: first, it uses the calibrated CFACE estimate to reduce variance in estimating the CATE for the RCT population and secondly, it also benefits from a preliminary estimate of the CATE in
\hat{\gamma}_{+1}^{o}+\tilde{\delta}_{+1}^{r}-[\hat{\gamma}_{-1}^{o}+\tilde{\delta}_{-1}^{r}]
; if this preliminary estimate is good, then all or most components in
\hat{\delta}^{r}
will be penalized to zero. If this estimate is not perfect, but still approximates the CATE well, then the
\hat{\delta}^{r}
will correct for any such poor approximation. Thus, the observational data is used in two ways and the final step allows for flexible borrowing of information from the preliminary CATE estimate, thereby reducing variance.

3.2.2
Theoretical Justification

Analysis without Calibration.
Assuming that the outcome model is consistently estimated in (
5
), and skipping the calibration step and estimating the
\delta
vectors directly along with CATE, the theoretical justification of the method becomes straightforward. In this case, the counterpart of (
7
) corresponds to two separate LASSO regressions, as discussed in Appendix
6.1
. Under the assumption of correctly specified models, the separated LASSO regression will recover the sparse mean shifts, benefiting from the theoretical guarantees of regular LASSO
[
19
]
.

Misspecification, Calibration, and Interpretability.
It is important to note that under model misspecification (e.g., when the true outcome model is not linear or the shift is not sparse), the performance of the method may suffer. To address this, we propose the use of the calibration step in (
6
), where the
\delta_{a}
vectors are initially trained to better explain the variance of the RCT outcome, resulting in a better
m(X)
for the CATE estimation (
7
) step where a separate
\delta
parameter is used for capturing CATE variation between populations. It is crucial to emphasize that our primary goal is to accurately and efficiently estimate the CATE in the RCT.

Finally, by adhering to the linear model, we make a theoretically grounded tradeoff between interpretability and flexibility. For instance, similar to recent works
[
20
]
, we can demonstrate that (
7
) estimates a linear projection of the true (possibly non-linear) CATE. Consequently, our estimated linear CATE remains interpretable, irrespective of the linearity of the true CATE.

Cross-fitting.
Conducting both steps, (
6
) and (
7
), using the same data from the RCT may give rise to theoretical challenges (e.g., harder consistency or prediction error analysis) as well as practical issues (e.g., overfitting). To mitigate these problems, one solution is to perform estimation in each step using independent sets of samples
[
21
]
. In the causal inference literature, this is achieved through cross-fitting
[
22
]
.
Here, we divide the RCT data into
K
folds and employ one fold for calibration step (
6
) and
K-1
folds for CATE estimation of (
7
). The final
\delta
vector required for CATE estimation is obtained by averaging all
\hat{\delta}^{r}
vectors estimated as solutions of (
7
) across the
K
rounds of cross-fitting.

4
Experiments

We run a series of thorough simulations to test how our proposed methods perform under various conditions. First, we look into the effect of sample splitting on our methods. Our results show that our approach works consistently well, whether we use sample splitting or not. Although the former is easier to justify theoretically, the latter is more efficient computationally. Next, we evaluate how model misspecification impacts CATE estimation. Even when the true outcome model is not strictly linear, our findings show that our method, which uses OS data, still performs strongly. This is especially clear when compared to a non-linear CATE estimators that only use RCT data. Finally, we examine the influence of unobserved effect modifiers affect CATE estimation.

Data Simulated Based on the Proposed Model

For all simulations, potential outcomes are generated (one model for each treatment arm) following the described models. We calculate the true CATE, then generate the observed outcomes by obscuring the unobserved potential outcome according to the treatment assignment.

Simulated OS Data.

To generate simulated data for each arm of the OS, we perform:

•

Covariate Generation:
We generate a covariate vector,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{100}
, from a zero mean multivariate normal distribution with a dense covariance matrix (all off-diagonal elements are non-zero).

•

Arm-specific Outcome Simulation:
For each arm

y^{o}(a)
using the equation
y^{o}(a)=\langle\beta^{o}_{a},x^{o}\rangle+\epsilon
. Here,
\beta^{o}_{a}
represents the true coefficients associated with arm
a
,
x^{o}
is the covariate vector for the given sample, and
\epsilon
is a random noise term. The size of the randomly selected support of
\beta^{o}_{a}
is 10. The values of these non-zero elements are sampled from the interval
\text{Uniform}(-2/3,-1/3)\cup\text{Uniform}(1/3,2/3)
. The noise term
\epsilon
is drawn from
N(0,1/9)
.

•

Treatment Assignment Probabilities:
To determine the probabilities

To simulate the RCT population, we introduce domain and outcome shifts:

We shift the mean of the multivariate distribution of covariates. Specifically, we randomly select 10 covariates (elements of

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced a novel, theoretically justified method for estimating the Conditional Average Treatment Effect (CATE), leveraging data from both observational studies (OS) and randomized controlled trials (RCT). Our extensive simulations demonstrated the superior performance of this approach compared to traditional methods that rely solely on RCT data. Importantly, our method maintains its robust performance even under conditions of model misspecification and in the presence of unmeasured effect modifiers. These findings underscore the efficiency and versatility of our approach in integrating OS and RCT data. By significantly enhancing CATE estimation, our method paves the way for more accurate, personalized decision making across a variety of fields, including healthcare and clinical research.
