Abstraction-based Probabilistic Stability Analysis of Polyhedral Probabilistic Hybrid Systems
Spandan Das, Pavithra Prabhakar

Abstract
In this paper, we consider the problem of probabilistic stability analysis of a subclass of Stochastic Hybrid Systems, namely, Polyhedral Probabilistic Hybrid Systems ( PPHS ) , where the flow dynamics is given by a polyhedral inclusion, the discrete switching between modes happens probabilistically at the boundaries of their invariant regions and the continuous state is not reset during switching. We present an abstraction-based analysis framework that consists of constructing a finite Markov Decision Processes ( MDP ) such that verification of certain property on the finite MDP ensures the satisfaction of probabilistic stability on the PPHS . Further, we present a polynomial-time algorithm for verifying the corresponding property on the MDP . Our experimental analysis demonstrates the feasibility of the approach in successfully verifying probabilistic stability on PPHS of various dimensions and sizes.

Introduction

Stability is a fundamental property of hybrid control systems that stipulates that, small changes in initial state or inputs lead to only small deviations in the behaviors of the system, and that the effect of those perturbations on the system behaviors diminishes over time.
Probabilistic stability
[
27
]
extends this notion to stochastic systems which model uncertainties in the environment.
In this paper, we study probabilistic stability of a certain kind of Stochastic Hybrid Systems (
SHS
), namely, Polyhedral Probabilistic Hybrid Systems (
PPHS
), which are
SHS
where flow rates are constrained by linear inequalities, and the mode switches probabilistically when the continuous state satisfies certain linear constraints.
These systems are powerful due to the non-determinism in the dynamics, and can precisely over-approximate linear hybrid systems
[
25
,
21
]
through a process called hybridization.

While safety analysis of
PPHS

[
24
,
18
,
8
,
1
,
19
]
and stability of polyhedral hybrid systems in the non-probabilistic setting
[
22
,
21
,
23
]
have been extensively investigated, stability analysis remains an open problem.
Classically, stability analysis techniques have been built on the notion of Lyapunov functions
[
3
,
10
,
20
,
30
]
and have been extended to the setting of stochastic systems
[
16
,
31
,
34
,
25
]
.
A detailed study on sufficient conditions for stability of
SHS
based on Lyapunov functions has been performed in
[
29
]
. Almost sure exponential stability
[
6
,
7
,
11
,
15
]
and asymptotic stability in
distribution
[
33
,
32
]
using Lyapunov functions have also been investigated for
SHS
.
While Lyapunov functions provide a certificate of stability, computing them is quite challenging as it involves exploring complex polynomial templates and deducing coefficients of such templates by solving non-linear optimization problems
[
3
,
12
]
.
An alternative but much less explored method involves an abstraction-based analysis, that has shown promise in the non-probabilistic setting
[
22
]
, and more recently in the probabilistic setting in low dimensions
[
9
]
.
In this paper, we present an abstraction-based analysis technique for probabilistic stability analysis of
PPHS
by abstracting the system to a finite Markov Decision Process and checking that an infinite path converges to an equilibrium point in expectation in the abstract system.

Broadly, our approach is to abstract a
PPHS
to a finite Markov Decision Process (
MDP
) with edge weights, and calculate the expected mean payoff of an infinite path under the worst possible policy
[
4
,
28
,
13
,
17
]
.
We show that, if mean payoff of an infinite path of the abstract
MDP
is negative, then the
PPHS
(which has an infinite
MDP
semantics) is stable.
While finding optimal policies for maximum expected mean payoff is computationally expensive, we present a polynomial-time algorithm to compute this maximum expected mean payoff, which suffices for our purpose to deduce stability.
This requires decomposing the
MDP
into communicating
MDP
s, calculating worst case expected mean payoff of a path in each of these
MDP
s and, combining these weights in a suitable manner to obtain the worst case expected mean payoff of a path of the original
MDP

[
26
,
17
,
5
]
.

The main contributions of this paper are:

•

An approach for abstraction of
PPHS
(which has an infinite
MDP
semantics) to a finite
MDP
with edge weights, such that probabilistic stability of
PPHS
can be inferred by checking that the worst case expected mean payoff of a path in the
MDP
is negative.

•

A polynomial time algorithm to compute maximum expected mean payoff of an infinite path of a finite
MDP
.

•

Experimental evaluation on
PPHS
with varying dimensions and sizes.

2.2
Markov Decision Process

A Markov Decision Process (
MDP
) is an abstract model with a set of states
S
and a set of actions

, that selects a distribution from

based on the current state
s\in S
and the current action

Definition 1
(MDP)

A Markov Decision Process (
MDP
) is a tuple
\mathcal{M}=(S,A,T)
such that

•

S
is a nonempty set of states

•

A
is a nonempty set of actions

•

T:S\times A\rightarrow Dist(S)
is a mapping from the set
S\times A
to the set of all distributions on
S
.

At any state

A
.
We use

to denote the probability of going from state
s
to state

when action

T(s,a,s^{\prime})=\alpha(s^{\prime})
where
\alpha=T(s,a)
.

A finite path of an
MDP

\mathcal{M}
is an alternating sequence of states and actions,
\sigma=s_{0},a_{1},s_{1},a_{2},s_{2},\dots,s_{n}
such that for each
0<i\leq n
,

We say

\left|\sigma\right|
),
s_{n}
is the ending state (denoted
{\sigma}_{\textit{end}}
) and
s_{0}
is the starting state (denoted
\sigma_{0}
) of the path
\sigma
.
A state
s_{2}
is said to be reachable from

(denoted
s_{1}\rightsquigarrow s_{2}
) if there is a finite path
\sigma
such that
\sigma_{0}=s_{1}
and
{\sigma}_{\textit{end}}=s_{2}
.

\sigma_{i}
denotes the
i^{th}
state
s_{i}
and

\sigma[i:j]
(
0\leq i\leq j\leq n
) denotes the subpath
s_{i},a_{i+1},s_{i+1},\dots,s_{j}
of the path
\sigma
.
We say a path
\sigma
is an edge if
\left|\sigma\right|=1
and infinite if
\left|\sigma\right|=\infty
.
An edge
e
is reachable from a state

if
s\rightsquigarrow e_{0}
.
The set of all edges, finite paths and infinite paths of an
MDP

\mathcal{M}
are denoted by
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{M}}
(
\mathcal{E}
when
\mathcal{M}
is understood from the context),
\textit{Paths}_{\textit{fin}}(\mathcal{M})
and
\textit{Paths}(\mathcal{M})
respectively.

We assume that for an
MDP

\mathcal{M}
, the next action is determined based on the current history, i.e., the finite path that has been observed until the most recent time point.
Given any finite path of an
MDP

\mathcal{M}
, a policy is a function that determines the next action.

Definition 2
(Policy)

A policy
\rho:\textit{Paths}_{\textit{fin}}(\mathcal{M})\rightarrow A
on an
MDP

\mathcal{M}
is a function from the set of finite paths
\textit{Paths}_{\textit{fin}}(\mathcal{M})
to the set of actions

.

A policy is said to be memoryless if the next action is determined based on the current state only.
Note that, given a memoryless policy
\rho
for an
MDP

\mathcal{M}
, the probability of transition from state

to

is uniquely given by

.
We say a discrete-time Markov chain (

)
[
9
]
is an

with an associated memoryless policy such that, probability of transition between any two states is uniquely defined.

The set of all possible policies of an
MDP

\mathcal{M}
is denoted by
\Gamma_{\mathcal{M}}
. We abuse notation and write

when
\mathcal{M}
is understood from the context. Given an

\mathcal{M}
, a policy

and an initial distribution

on the set of states

, we define probability of a finite path

inductively as
[
13
]
,

T_{\rho}(\sigma)=\begin{cases}d(\sigma_{0})\text{ if }\left|\sigma\right|=0\\
T_{\rho}(\sigma^{\prime})\cdot\rho(\sigma^{\prime})T(s_{\left|\sigma\right|-1},\rho(\sigma^{\prime}),s_{\left|\sigma\right|})\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma[0:\left|\sigma\right|-1]
.
For this work, we will assume that the initial distribution of states of an
MDP
is an indicator function for a unique state

known as the

, i.e.,

iff

.

Weighted Markov Decision Process

We extend

Basically, a

Let us graphically illustrate a sample

,

and
T(s_{i-1},a)=\tau_{i}\in Dist(S)
for all

where,

\tau_{i}(s)=\begin{cases}p_{i}\quad\text{if }i\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)=0\text{ and }s=s_{(i+1)\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 8)}\\
1-p_{i}\quad\text{if }i\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)=0\text{ and }s=s_{(i+2)\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 8)}\\
p_{i}\quad\text{if }i\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)=1\text{ and }s=s_{i\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 8)}\\
1-p_{i}\quad\text{if }i\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)=1\text{ and }s=s_{(i+1)\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 8)}\\
0\quad\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We depict
\mathcal{M}_{W}
in Figure

as a graph where

,

are nodes of the graph, there is a non-deterministic edge from

to
\tau_{j}
(marked red) if

and, there is a probabilistic edge from

to

if
\tau_{j}(s_{i})\neq 0
. Each non-deterministic edge
(s_{i},\tau_{j})
is labeled with the action

. Each probabilistic edge is labeled with its probability.∎

Note that, under a memoryless policy

, a

not only gives a unique probability, but also gives a unique weight of transition from state

to

(given by

We say a Weighted

(

The weight of a finite path

Definition 4
(Mean Payoff)

For a
WMDP

\mathcal{M}_{W}
, the mean payoff
\gamma
is a function from
\textit{Paths}_{\textit{fin}}(\mathcal{M}_{W})
to defined as,

\gamma(\sigma)=\frac{W(\sigma)}{\left|\sigma\right|}.

2.4
Maximum Expected Mean Payoff of WMDP

Given a
WMDP
, we are interested in finding the maximum expected mean payoff of an infinite path under any policy.
Here, we formally state this as a problem and discuss an efficient algorithm for its solution.

Problem 1

Given a
WMDP

\mathcal{M}_{W}
, find the maximum expected mean payoff of an infinite path under any policy, i.e.,

\sup_{\rho\in\Gamma}\left(\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}E^{T^{\rho}_{n}}[\gamma]\right),

where
T^{\rho}_{n}
is a distribution on
\textit{Paths}_{\textit{fin}}(\mathcal{M}_{W})
given by
T^{\rho}_{n}(\sigma)=T_{\rho}(\sigma\mid\left|\sigma\right|=n)
.

We denote
\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}E^{T^{\rho}_{n}}[\gamma]
as
E_{\rho}[\gamma]
.
Now, let us briefly discuss an algorithm for solving the above problem.

Algorithm for calculating maximum expected mean payoff:

Our goal is to solve Problem
1
for a
WMDP
with finite set of states and actions (finite
WMDP
).
Algorithm 2 (MEC-SI) from
[
17
]
solves a similar problem for the class of
WMDP
s with strictly positive weights and the
\limsup
in the expected mean payoff replaced with
\liminf
, that is, it computes
\sup_{\rho\in\Gamma}\left(\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}E^{T^{\rho}_{n}}[\gamma]\right)
.
We want to use this algorithm to solve Problem
1
. In order to do that, the input
WMDP
must be suitably modified to suit the input criterion of Algorithm MEC-SI and, the desired output should be derivable from the output of the algorithm.
Note that,
\limsup
of a real sequence can be found by negating the
\liminf
of the inverted sequence.
Thus, if we negate all weights of the input
WMDP
and find
\sup_{\rho\in\Gamma}\left(\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}E^{T^{\rho}_{n}}[\gamma]\right)
, we are actually finding the maximum expected payoff of the original
WMDP
.
Also, if we shift the outputs of a real valued function by a constant real bias, the expectation gets shifted by the same bias.
Thus, solving Problem
1
for a
WMDP
is the same as solving Problem
1
for the
WMDP
after shifting each weight by a constant, and then removing the constant.
Hence, for a
WMDP
with both positive and negative weights, we can solve Problem
1
by first negating all the weights, then adding a constant bias to each weight to make them all positive, and finally applying Algorithm MEC-SI on the
WMDP
with the modified weights.

Let us now briefly describe Algorithm MEC-SI. The main steps of the algorithm are:

1.

Achieve Maximal End Component (MEC) decomposition
[
5
]
of the input
WMDP
.

2.

For each MEC, find
\sup_{\rho\in\Gamma}\left(\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}E^{T^{\rho}_{n}}[\gamma]\right)
for the induced
WMDP
by strategy iteration.

3.

Construct the MEC-quotient (an
MDP
) using the values obtained in the previous step
[
17
]
.

4.

Find the maximum reaching probability to a particular state of the MEC-quotient
[
17
]
.

Note that, the algorithm works in polynomial time
[
17
,
5
]
if we can solve steps 2 and 4 in polynomial time.
Note that, step 2 cannot be done in polynomial time as strategy iteration is not guaranteed to converge in polynomial time.
Also note that, we don’t actually need to synthesize an optimal strategy, rather, we only need the optimal gain
\sup_{\rho\in\Gamma}\left(\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}E^{T^{\rho}_{n}}[\gamma]\right)
of the MEC.
Let us show how we can do this in polynomial time.
If a
WMDP
is finite and strongly connected (communicating), that is, each state is reachable from every other state, then there is a linear program (LP) formulation for the optimal gain problem
[
26
]
.
Since MECs are strongly connected
[
17
]
, we can obtain optimal gain of an MEC by solving an LP.
Since an LP can be solved in polynomial time, step 2 can actually be completed in polynomial time as well.

If an
MDP
is finite, maximum reaching probability to a state can be solved by solving an LP
[
26
]
.
Since MEC-quotient is a finite
MDP

[
17
]
, we can use this LP formulation for step 4.
Thus, step 4 can also be completed in polynomial time.
So, we have a polynomial-time algorithm for computing maximum expected mean payoff of a
WMDP
.

Example 2

Let us apply our algorithm on the sample
WMDP
described in Example
1
. First, we negate each weight, i.e., the modified weight of a state-action pair
(s_{i},a)
becomes
-W(s_{i},a)
. Now, if
\min_{i\in[8]}(-W(s_{i-1},a))\leq 0
, then we set the constant
c\leftarrow\left|\min_{i\in[8]}(-W(s_{i-1},a))\right|+1
and
c\leftarrow 0
otherwise. We add
c
to each of the modified weights. Thus, the final weight of a state-action pair
(s_{i},a)
becomes
-W(s_{i},a)+c
, which is strictly positive. We now apply Algorithm MEC-SI on the
WMDP
with the modified weights. Note that, the
WMDP
is strongly connected, i.e., it has only one MEC (see
[
17
]
). Thus, we can skip steps 3 and 4 altogether. The maximum expected mean payoff of the sample
WMDP
is simply
-(v-c)
, where
v
is the value obtained from step 2 by solving the linear program for the
WMDP
with modified weights.

In this section, we define Polyhedral Probabilistic Hybrid System (

Definition 5
(
PPHS
)

The Polyhedral Probabilistic Hybrid System (
PPHS
) is defined as the tuple

\mathcal{H}\coloneqq(Q,\mathcal{X},\textit{I},\textit{F},\textit{E})
where,

•

Q
is the set of discrete locations,

•

\mathcal{X}={}^{n}
is the continuous state space for
some
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

•

\textit{I}:Q\rightarrow Poly(n)
is the invariant
function which assigns a positive scaling invariant polyhedral subset of the state space to each location

,

•

\textit{F}:Q\rightarrow Poly(n)
is the Flow function which
associates a flow polyhedron to each location

•

\textit{E}\subseteq Q\times(\cup_{q\in Q}\mathbb{F}(\textit{I}(q)))\times Dist(Q)

is the probabilistic edge relation such that
(q,f,\zeta)\in\textit{E}
where for every
(q,f)
, there is a
at most one
\zeta
such that
(q,f,\zeta)\in\textit{E}
and
f\in\mathbb{F}(\textit{I}(q))
.

f
is called a
Guard
of the location
q
.

Let us describe the semantics of the

.
An execution starts from
(q_{0},x_{0})
where
q_{0}\in Q
and
x_{0}\in\textit{I}(q_{0})
. It evolves continuously for

according to a flow rate that is chosen non-deterministically from
\textit{F}(q_{0})
, until it reaches a facet

of
\textit{I}(q_{0})
.
Then a probabilistic discrete transition is taken if there is an edge

(q_{0},f_{0},\zeta_{0})
and the state

is probabilistically changed

with probability
\zeta_{0}(q_{1})
.
The execution (tree) continues with alternating continuous and

Formally, for

For two locations

from

with probability

\zeta\in Dist(Q)

\lvert\!\lvert x\rvert\!\rvert
captures the distance of state
x
from
0
.

Definition 6
(Semantics of
PPHS
)

Given a
PPHS

\mathcal{H}
, we can construct the
WMDP

{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}\coloneqq(S_{\mathcal{H}},A_{\mathcal{H}},T_{\mathcal{H}},W_{\mathcal{H}})

where,

•

S_{\mathcal{H}}=Q\times\mathcal{X}

•

A_{\mathcal{H}}\subseteq S_{\mathcal{H}}\times S_{\mathcal{H}}
with
((q_{1},x_{1}),(q_{2},x_{2}))\in A
iff
q_{1}=q_{2}
and there is a continuous transition from
x_{1}
to
x_{2}
with respect to
q_{1}
.

•

T_{\mathcal{H}}((q_{2},x_{2})|(q_{1},x_{1}),a)=\zeta(q_{2})=p
if
a=((q_{1},x_{1}),(q_{1},x_{2}))
, there is a discrete transition from
q_{1}
to
q_{2}
with probability
p
via
\zeta
and
f
, and
x_{2}\in f
. In all other cases,
T_{\mathcal{H}}((q_{2},x_{2})|(q_{1},x_{1}),a)=0
.

•

W_{\mathcal{H}}((q_{1},x_{1}),a)=\log\left(\lvert\!\lvert x_{2}\rvert\!\rvert/\lvert\!\lvert x_{1}\rvert\!\rvert\right)
where
a=((q_{1},x_{1}),(q_{1},x_{2}))
.

An infinite path
\sigma=(q_{0},x_{0}),((q_{0},x_{0}),(q_{0},x_{1})),(q_{1},x_{1}),\dots
of the semantics
WMDP

{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
is said to converge to
0
if
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lvert\!\lvert x_{n}\rvert\!\rvert=0
.
Thus,
\sigma
converges if and only if
W_{\mathcal{H}}(\sigma)=-\infty
, if and only if
\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{W_{\mathcal{H}}(\sigma[0:n])}{n}<0

[
9
]
.
Thus,
\sigma
converges if and only if mean payoff of
\sigma
is less than zero.
We say a
WMDP
is stable if an infinite path of it converges in expectation under any policy. A
PPHS
is said to be stable when its semantics
WMDP
is stable.

Definition 7
(Stability of PPHS)

A
WMDP

\mathcal{M}_{W}
is said to be stable if under any policy
\rho
, an infinite path of it converges in expectation, i.e.,

E_{\rho}[\gamma]<0
.
A
PPHS

\mathcal{H}
is said to be stable when its semantics
WMDP

{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
is stable.

For a
WMDP
, an infinite path converges in expectation under all policies, if and only if, maximum expected mean payoff of an infinite path is less than zero. Thus, we have the following characterization for stability of a
PPHS
,

Theorem 3.1

[Characterization of Stability] A
PPHS

\mathcal{H}
is stable iff, maximum expected mean payoff of an infinite path of its semantics
WMDP

{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
is strictly negative, i.e.,

\sup_{\rho\in\Gamma}E_{\rho}[\gamma]<0.

Proof

A
PPHS

\mathcal{H}
is stable when its semantics
WMDP

{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
is stable. Now,

{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
is stable if and only if

E_{\rho}[\gamma]<0

\forall\rho\in\Gamma
, if and only if

\sup_{\rho\in\Gamma}E_{\rho}[\gamma]<0
.
Hence, our claim is proved.∎

However, the algorithm discussed in Section
2.4
cannot be applied to
{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
since it has infinite state and action space.
Hence, we create an abstract
WMDP

\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
from
{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
which is finite in size and, stability of
\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
implies stability of
{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
.

Remark 1

Different notions of stability, such as, exponential stability, Lyapunov stability, Lagrange stability, asymptotic stability, have been explored for non-probabilistic hybrid systems.
These notions have been extended for
SHS

[
29
]
by defining them on expected behavior of the system.
For example, an
SHS
is said to be Lyapunov stable around a point of equilibrium when the system remains within a close neighborhood of the equilibrium point
in the expected case
, when it starts from a point close to the equilibrium point.
We can say that, our notion of stability extends the notion of asymptotic stability in the non-stochastic setting, since we obtain the definition of asymptotic stability in non-stochastic setting by removing the ‘in expectation’ part from Definition
7
.

4
Abstraction of PPHS to WMDP

We now describe the abstract
WMDP

\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
derived from the semantics
WMDP

{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
of a
PPHS

\mathcal{H}
, and show that, stability of
\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
implies stability of
{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
.

Definition 8
(Abstract
WMDP
)

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a
PPHS
and
{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
be its
semantics. We define the
WMDP

\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}=(S^{\text{red}},A^{\text{red}},T^{\text{red}},W^{\text{red}})
as follows,

•

S^{\text{red}}=Q\times\bigcup_{q\in Q}\mathbb{F}(\textit{I}(q))

•

A^{\text{red}}\subseteq S^{\text{red}}\times S^{\text{red}}
with
((q_{1},f_{1}),(q_{2},f_{2}))\in A
iff
q_{1}=q_{2}
and there is a continuous transition from
x_{1}\in f_{1}
to
x_{2}\in f_{2}
with respect to
q_{1}
.

•

T^{\text{red}}((q_{2},f_{2})|(q_{1},f_{1}),a)=T_{\mathcal{H}}((q_{2},x_{2})|(q_{1},x_{1}),a^{\prime})
if
a=((q_{1},f_{1}),(q_{1},f_{2}))
,
x_{1}\in f_{1}
,
x_{2}\in f_{2}
and
T_{\mathcal{H}}((q_{2},x_{2})|(q_{1},x_{1}),a^{\prime})>0
. In all other cases,
T^{\text{red}}((q_{2},f_{2})|(q_{1},f_{1}),a)=0
.

•

W^{\text{red}}((q_{1},f_{1}),((q_{1},f_{1}),(q_{1},f_{2})))=\max\{W_{\mathcal{H}}((q_{1},x_{1}),a^{\prime})\}
, where maximum is taken over all
a^{\prime}
of the form
((q_{1},x_{1}),(q_{2},x_{2}))
such that
x_{1}\in f_{1}
and
x_{2}\in f_{2}
.

Note that,
\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
has finite state space and action space since for any
q\in Q
,
\mathbb{F}(\textit{I}(q))
is finite. Also,
\max\{W_{\mathcal{H}}((q_{1},x_{1}),a^{\prime},(q_{2},x_{2}))\}
,
where maximum is taken over all
((q_{1},x_{1}),a^{\prime},(q_{2},x_{2}))\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{H}}
such that
x_{1}\in f_{1}
and
x_{2}\in f_{2}
, can be calculated by solving linear optimization problems when
\textit{I}(q_{1})
is positive scaling invariant (see
[
22
]
).

Example 4

For example, let us abstract the
PPHS
described in Example
3
. Assuming
(1,0)
to be the initial state and
q_{0}
the initial location,
(q_{0},\{x\geq 0,y=0\})
gives the initialization point for the abstract
WMDP
. A unique action
((q_{0},\{x\geq 0,y=0\}),(q_{0},\{x=0,y\geq 0\}))
leads from the initialization point to the distribution
\tau_{1}
with non-zero weight.
\tau_{1}
has probability
p_{1}
for state
(q_{1},\{x=0,y\geq 0\})
and probability
1-p_{1}
for state
(q_{2},\{x=0,y\geq 0\})
. Other states and transitions are defined similarly. The resulting
WMDP
is depicted by Figure

, where
s_{0}
marks the initialization point.∎

We will now prove that
\mathcal{H}
is stable if
\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
is stable.
This result will lead to a polynomial time algorithm that can verify stability of
PPHS
.

Theorem 4.1

\mathcal{H}
is stable if
\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
is stable.

Proof

Let
\rho
be an arbitrary policy of
{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
. Suppose,
(q_{0},x_{0})
is the initialization point of
{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
and
x_{0}
belongs to the facet
f_{0}
. Then,
(q_{0},f_{0})
is the initialization point of
\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
. We say that a state
(q,x)
of
{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
belongs to a state
(q^{\prime},f^{\prime})
of
\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
, denoted as
(q,x)\in(q^{\prime},f^{\prime})
, if
q=q^{\prime}
and
x\in f^{\prime}
. We define a policy
\hat{\rho}
for
\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
using
\rho
as follows:

•

Let
\hat{\sigma}=(q_{0},f_{0}),((q_{0},f_{0}),(q_{0},f_{1})),(q_{1},f_{1}),\dots,(q_{n},f_{n})
be a finite path of
\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
and the action
((q_{n},f_{n}),(q_{n},f^{\prime}))

\in A^{\text{red}}
.

•

Then,

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(\hat{\sigma})=((q_{n},f_{n}),(q_{n},f^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\text{iff, }\exists\sigma\in\textit{Paths}_{\textit{fin}}({\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}),\sigma_{i}\in\hat{\sigma}_{i}\quad\forall i\in[n],\text{ and }x^{\prime}\in f^{\prime},

where
\rho(\sigma)=((q_{n},x_{n}),(q_{n},x^{\prime}))
.

This implies by construction of
\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
that, probability of a finite path
\hat{\sigma}
under policy
\hat{\rho}
is simply the probability (under
\rho
) of set of all finite paths
\sigma
of
{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
such that
\left|\hat{\sigma}\right|=\left|\sigma\right|
and
\sigma_{i}\in\hat{\sigma}_{i}
for all
i
.
Also note that,
W^{\text{red}}(\hat{\sigma})\geq W_{\mathcal{H}}(\sigma)
for any such
\sigma\in\textit{Paths}_{\textit{fin}}({\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket})
.
In fact, for
\hat{\sigma}\in\textit{Paths}_{\textit{fin}}(\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}})
, let

\Pi(\hat{\sigma})=\{\sigma\in\textit{Paths}_{\textit{fin}}({\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}):\sigma_{i}\in\hat{\sigma}_{i}\text{ }\forall i\};

then, for each
\sigma\in\Pi(\hat{\sigma})
,

\displaystyle\frac{W_{\mathcal{H}}(\sigma)}{\left|\sigma\right|}\leq\frac{W^{\text{red}}(\hat{\sigma})}{\left|\hat{\sigma}\right|}\text{ and,}

\displaystyle T_{\hat{\rho}}(\hat{\sigma})=T_{\rho}\left(\Pi(\hat{\sigma})\right)\quad\text{for all }\hat{\sigma}\in\textit{Paths}_{\textit{fin}}(\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}).

Since for any
\sigma\in\textit{Paths}_{\textit{fin}}({\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket})
, there exists
\hat{\sigma}\in\textit{Paths}_{\textit{fin}}(\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}})
such that
\left|\sigma\right|=\left|\hat{\sigma}\right|
and
\sigma_{i}\in\hat{\sigma}_{i}
for all
i
,

\bigcup_{\hat{\sigma}\in\textit{Paths}_{\textit{fin}}(\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}})}\Pi(\hat{\sigma})=\textit{Paths}_{\textit{fin}}({\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}).

Thus, expected mean payoff of an infinite path of
\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
under
\hat{\rho}
,

\displaystyle E_{\hat{\rho}}[\gamma]=

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{\hat{\sigma}\in\textit{Paths}_{\textit{fin}}(\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}})}\left(\frac{W^{\text{red}}(\hat{\sigma})}{\left|\hat{\sigma}\right|}\right)T_{n}^{\hat{\rho}}(\hat{\sigma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{\hat{\sigma}\in\textit{Paths}_{\textit{fin}}(\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}})}\left(\frac{W^{\text{red}}(\hat{\sigma})}{\left|\hat{\sigma}\right|}\right)T_{n}^{\rho}(\Pi(\hat{\sigma}))

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{\sigma\in\textit{Paths}_{\textit{fin}}({\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket})}\left(\frac{W_{\mathcal{H}}(\sigma)}{\left|\sigma\right|}\right)T_{n}^{\rho}(\sigma)=E_{\rho}[\gamma],

that is, expected mean payoff of an infinite path of
{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
under
\rho
.
Since for any arbitrary policy
\rho
of
{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
, we can define a policy
\hat{\rho}
of
\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
such that
E_{\hat{\rho}}[\gamma]\geq E_{\rho}[\gamma]
, we can say that,

\sup_{\hat{\rho}\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}}}E[\gamma]\geq\sup_{\rho\in\Gamma_{{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}}}E[\gamma].

Thus, stability of
\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
, i.e.,

E_{\hat{\rho}}[\gamma]<0
for all
\hat{\rho}\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}}
, implies

\sup_{\hat{\rho}\in\Gamma_{\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}}}E_{\hat{\rho}}[\gamma]<0
,
which further implies
\sup_{\rho\in\Gamma_{{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}}}E_{\rho}[\gamma]<0
,
which, by Theorem
3.1
implies that
\mathcal{H}
is stable too. Hence, our claim is proved.∎

Using Theorem
4.1
, we can easily device a polynomial time algorithm that can verify stability of a
PPHS

\mathcal{H}
. We simply construct
\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
from
{\llbracket\mathcal{H}\rrbracket}
, which takes polynomial time according to
[
22
]
, and apply the polynomial time algorithm discussed in Section
2.4
on
\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
to find the maximum expected mean payoff of an infinite path of
\mathcal{H}^{\text{red}}
. If the maximum expected mean payoff is less than zero, then we deduce that
\mathcal{H}
is stable.
Note that, our algorithm tests a sufficient condition for stability, i.e., we cannot say that the
PPHS
is unstable if the algorithm does not guarantee stability.

Remark 2

We would like to remark on the generality of this abstraction procedure.
Note that, Theorem
4.1
holds even if we define stability using a different payoff function, like total effective payoff
[
2
]
or, prefix-independent and submixing payoff
[
13
]
.
Not only that, it works for other notions of stability as well, such as almost sure stability.
However, the algorithm for verifying stability of the abstract
WMDP
(Section
2.4
) is not so general and needs to be modified for different notions of stability.

For Linear Switched System with

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have presented an algorithm for stability analysis of an important subclass of Stochastic Hybrid Systems, which we call the Polyhedral Probabilistic Hybrid System (
PPHS
).
Our algorithm is based on abstraction based techniques, where we first abstract the
PPHS
to a finite
WMDP
and then test the abstract
WMDP
for stability.
Verification of stability of the abstract
WMDP
is extremely efficient, since it can be done using a polynomial-time algorithm.
However, the abstraction time increases rapidly with the number of dimensions and always dominates over the verification time.
Hence, the entire process suffers from the curse of dimensionality.
For our future work, we would like to develop compositional methods for analyzing stochastic stability to circumvent this problem.
Two other directions of future research are, exploring probabilistic stability analysis for almost sure notions and for more complex dynamics, including those with stochasticity in the continous dynamics and, analyzing stability in a chosen set of dimensions instead of all the dimensions, which is hard to achieve in reality.
