On The Dynamics Of Variable-Shape Wave Energy Converters
Mohamed A. Shabara, Ossama Abdelkhalik

Abstract
Flexible structures in the wave energy conversion field have recently captured attention. The common method used to tackle the problem of flexibility in wave energy devices is done by accounting for the flexibility (mode shapes) in the hydrodynamic solvers as extra degrees of freedom, not in the structural domain. This work uses the principles of analytical mechanics to derive the equation of motion of spherical variable-shape wave energy converters and describes a methodology to calculate the generalized hydrodynamic forces on the shell. Fluid-Structure Interaction simulations are performed to validate the developed dynamic model and to study the effect of using a flexible buoy in wave energy converters on its trajectory and power production.

The wave energy converters can be classified either based on their position (shoreline, near-shore and offshore devices), or principle of operation, or based on the power take-off (PTO) technique. Drew et al.
[

1.

2.

Point absorbers, have small dimensions relative to the incident wave wavelength.

3.

Terminators, such as the Salter’s duck are devices that lie perpendicular to the predominant wave direction.

Typical optimal control strategies for WECs require reactive power; reactive power flows from the device to water and usually needs a relatively complex Power Take-off (PTO) units. Another aspect is the power quality (fluctuations in the power curve)
[
8
,
9

Variable-shape-buoy wave energy converters (VSB WECs) were recently introduced to tackle this reactive power challenge by either reducing the need for it or completely eliminating the reactive power component. It is worth noting that the difference between a VSB WEC and a variable geometry WEC is the rate of change of the surface of the WEC shape relative to the incident wave forces, i.e., the variable geometry WEC is a Fixed Shape buoy (FSB) WEC that changes its geometry occasionally, on the other hand, the VSB WEC changes their shapes continuously. The wave/WEC interaction produced by the VSB WEC can be tuned to produce more power without the complexity accompanied by the reactive power PTO units.

Flexible Wave Energy Converters (FlexWecs) uses the Distributed embedded energy conversion technology (DEEC-Tec) to harvest energy from the ocean, the structure of these devices use distributed smart materials across their shells to harvest wave energy from the waves

III
Unconstrained Equations of Motion For Flexible Spherical Buoys

The unconstrained equations of motion are here derived as a first step towards writing the constrained equations of motion. The Lagrangian for this buoy system can be written as the summation of three quantities
[
27

:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{D}+\mathcal{L}_{B}+\int_{s}\hat{\mathcal{L}}d\phi

(26)

where

\mathcal{L}_{B}
are related to the discrete coordinates and the boundaries, respectively. ,
\hat{\mathcal{L}}
is the Lagrangian density function and it is a function of the discrete and distributed parameter coordinates. In this work there are no boundary terms in the Lagrangian equation, i.e.,

III-A

The Lagrangian for the discrete coordinates is expressed as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{D}=\mathcal{T}_{D}-\mathcal{\pi}_{D}=\frac{1}{2}{}^{a}\dot{\vec{\bm{r}}}_{sa}^{T}\bm{m}{}^{a}\dot{\vec{\bm{r}}}_{sa}+\frac{1}{2}{\vec{\bm{\omega}}}_{as}^{T}\bm{\mathcal{J}}_{s}{\vec{\bm{\omega}}}_{as}-mg\bm{1}_{3}^{T}\vec{\bm{r}}_{sa}

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{D}}{\partial{\vec{\bm{\omega}}}_{as}}\right)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{J}}_{s}\dot{\vec{\bm{\omega}}}_{as}+\dot{\bm{\mathcal{J}}}_{s}{\vec{\bm{\omega}}}_{as}=0

, is expressed as:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\{(2\pi\rho h)\left\{\dot{u}^{2}+\dot{v}^{2}\right\}r^{2}\sin{\phi}\}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;-\frac{1}{2}\frac{2\pi Eh}{(1-\nu^{2})}\Biggl\{{\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\phi}+v\right)}^{2}+{\left(u\cot(\phi)+v\right)}^{2}

\displaystyle\;\;\;\;\;\;+2\nu{\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\phi}+v\right)}\left(u\cot(\phi)+v\right)\Biggl\}r^{2}\sin{\phi}

(36)

and, the Lagrange equation for the distributed parameters is
[
27
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial\dot{\vec{\bm{r}}}_{dmc}}\right)-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\vec{\bm{r}}_{dmc}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\partial}{\partial\vec{\bm{x}}}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial{\vec{\bm{r}}}_{dmc}^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\vec{\bm{x}}^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial{\vec{\bm{r}}}_{dmc}^{\prime\prime}}\right)=\bm{0}

(37)

where
\vec{\bm{x}}
is the vector of spatial coordinates in the
\hat{\bm{a}}
directions, such that the transformation from the cartesian coordinates to spherical coordinates is as follows:

\displaystyle x_{1}=r\sin{\phi}\leftrightarrows\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}=\frac{1}{r\cos{\phi}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}

(38)

\displaystyle x_{3}=r\cos{\phi}\leftrightarrows\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{3}}=\frac{-1}{r\sin{\phi}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}

(39)

For
{{\vec{r}}_{dmc}}_{1}=u
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial{\dot{u}}}\right)=2\pi\rho hr^{2}\sin{\phi}\ddot{u}

(40)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial u}=-\frac{2\pi Eh}{(1-\nu^{2})}\cot(\phi)\Biggl\{{\left(u\cot(\phi)+v\right)}+\nu{\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\phi}+v\right)}\Biggl\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times r^{2}\sin{\phi}

(41)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{r\cos{\phi}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial{u}^{\prime}}\right)=-\frac{r\pi Eh}{(1-\nu^{2})}\Biggl\{{\left(\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial\phi^{2}}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial\phi}\right)}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\nu\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\phi}\cot(\phi)+u\csc(\phi)+v\right)\Biggl\}-\frac{\pi Eh}{(1-\nu^{2})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\Biggl\{\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\phi}+v\right)+\nu\left(u\cot(\phi)+v\right)\Biggl\}r\tan{\phi}

(42)

For
{{\vec{r}}_{dmc}}_{3}=v
:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial{\dot{v}}}\right)=2\pi\rho hr^{2}\sin{\phi}\ddot{v}

(43)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial v}=-\frac{2\pi Eh}{(1-\nu^{2})}\Biggl\{{\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\phi}+v\right)}+{\left(u\cot(\phi)+v\right)}

\displaystyle\qquad+\left(\nu{\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\phi}+v\right)}+\left(u\cot(\phi)+v\right)\right)\Biggl\}r^{2}\sin{\phi}

(44)

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{r\sin{\phi}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\left(\frac{\partial\hat{\mathcal{L}}}{\partial{v}^{\prime}}\right)=\frac{-1}{r\sin{\phi}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\left(0\right)=0

(45)

Accordingly, the equations of motion associated with the distributed parameter are expressed as:

\displaystyle 2\pi\rho hr^{2}\sin{\phi}\ddot{u}-\frac{2\pi Eh}{(1-\nu^{2})}\cot(\phi)\Biggl\{{\left(u\cot(\phi)+v\right)}

\displaystyle+\nu{\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\phi}+v\right)}\Biggl\}r^{2}\sin{\phi}-\frac{\pi Eh}{(1-\nu^{2})}\times

\displaystyle\left\{{\left(\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial\phi^{2}}+\frac{\partial v}{\partial\phi}\right)}+\nu\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\phi}\cot(\phi)+u\csc(\phi)+v\right)\right\}r

\displaystyle-\frac{r\pi Eh}{(1-\nu^{2})}\left\{{\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\phi}+v\right)}+\nu\left(u\cot(\phi)+v\right)\right\}\tan{\phi}=0

(46)

\displaystyle 2\pi

\displaystyle\rho hr^{2}\sin{\phi}\ddot{v}-\frac{2\pi Eh}{(1-\nu^{2})}\Biggl\{{\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\phi}+v\right)}+{\left(u\cot(\phi)+v\right)}

\displaystyle+\left(\nu{\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\phi}+v\right)}+\left(u\cot(\phi)+v\right)\right)\Biggl\}r^{2}\sin{\phi}=0

(47)

Equations (
46
) and (
47
) are the equations of motion of deformation of axisymmetric homogeneous spherical shell. These equations of motion can not be solved analytically, and an approximate method is used. Most of the equations of motions for continuous systems are usually difficult to obtain; this difficulty arises either from the difficulty in solving the governing equations or from imposing the boundary conditions. Here, an approximate method is implemented to convert the partial differential equations to ordinary differential equations.

Rayleigh-Ritz Approximation

The Rayleigh-Ritz approximation method is applied in this article.
Each component of the displacement vector
\vec{\bm{r}}_{dmc}
is assumed to have the following form
[
22
,
21
]
::

\displaystyle u(\phi,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Psi_{n}^{\phi}(\phi)\eta_{n}(t)=\underbrace{[\Psi_{1}^{\phi}\ldots\Psi_{N}^{\phi}]}_{\bm{\Psi}_{e}^{\phi}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{1}(t)\\
\vdots\\
\eta_{N}(t)\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{\eta}(t)}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Psi}_{e}^{\phi}(\phi)\bm{\eta}(t)

(48)

\displaystyle v(\phi,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\Psi_{n}^{r}(\phi)\eta_{n}(t)=\underbrace{[\Psi_{1}^{r}\ldots\Psi_{n}^{r}]}_{\bm{\Psi}_{e}^{r}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{1}(t)\\
\vdots\\
\eta_{N}(t)\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{\eta}(t)}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Psi}_{e}^{r}(\phi)\bm{\eta}(t)

(49)

where the functions
\Psi_{n}^{\phi}
and
\Psi_{n}^{r}
are trial (admissible) functions of
\phi
and the functions
\eta_{n}
are functions of time
t
,
\forall

n=1,\cdots,N
.
Therefore, the displacement vector can be expressed in the
\hat{\bm{e}}
frame as follows:

\vec{\bm{r}}_{dmc}(\phi,t)=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\Psi}_{e}^{\phi}(\phi)\\
\bm{0}\\
\bm{\Psi}_{e}^{r}(\phi)\end{bmatrix}}_{\bm{\Phi}_{e}}\bm{\eta}(t)=\bm{\Phi}_{e}(\phi)\bm{\eta}(t)

(50)

The Legendre functions of the first kind
P_{n}

[
28
]
can serve as shape functions for the Ritz-Rayleigh method to satisfy the essential geometrical (Dirichlet) boundary conditions
[
2
,
21
,
22
,
25
,
29
,
30
]
, as follows:

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}^{\phi}(\phi)

\displaystyle=A\frac{dP_{n}(\cos(\phi))}{d\phi}\text{, and}

(51)

\displaystyle\Psi_{n}^{r}(\phi)

\displaystyle=A\frac{(1+(1+\nu))\Omega_{n}^{2}}{1-\Omega_{n}^{2}}P_{n}(\cos(\phi))

where the coefficients of the equations above form an eigenvector for the Legendre differential equation, i.e. the constant ”A” can take any real value.
\Omega_{n}^{2}
is a dimensionless frequency parameter expressed as
[
25
]
:

\Omega^{2}_{n}=\frac{1}{2(1-\nu^{2})}(A\pm\sqrt{A^{2}-4mB})

(52)

where
\nu
is the Poisson’s ratio, and

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=n(n+1)-2,\>n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}

(53)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=1+\nu^{2}+\frac{1}{12}[(m+1)^{2}-\nu^{2}]

(54)

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=3(1+\nu)+m+\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{h}{r}\right]^{2}(m+3)(m+1+\nu)

(55)

From
[
25
,
2
]
the natural frequencies in radians per second for spherical shells are calculated using Eq. (
56
)

\omega_{n}^{2}=\frac{E}{r^{2}\rho\Omega_{n}^{2}}

(56)

where
E
is Young’s Modulus of elasticity,
r
is the non-deformed radius of the shell, and
\rho
is the density of the shell material.
When
n=0
, the vibration mode corresponds to the breathing mode (volumetric or pulsating modes) which is a pure radial vibration mode
[
25
,
31
,
30
]
. For
n>0
, the
\pm
sign in Eq. (
52
) yields the modes corresponding to the membrane vibration modes and bending vibration modes (this is demonstrated in Fig.
4
in the results section). The bending vibration modes are obtained when using the negative sign; these modes are sensitive to the
h/r
ratio. On the other hand, the membrane modes are insensitive to the change in the
h/r
ratio. To obtain the approximated equations of motion using the Rayleigh-Ritz method, the approximated displacement vector needs to be substituted in the kinetic and strain energy equations as follows.
The kinetic energy
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{s}}
is approximated by substituting Eq.s (
48
) and (
49
) into Eq. (
19
); to get:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{\bm{s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\bm{\eta}}^{T}\underbrace{\Bigg\{2\pi\rho h\int^{\pi}_{0}\left(\bm{\Psi}_{e}^{{\phi}^{T}}\bm{\Psi}_{e}^{\phi}+\bm{\Psi}_{e}^{{r}^{T}}\bm{\Psi}_{e}^{r}\right)r^{2}\sin\phi d\phi\Bigg\}}_{\bm{M}_{ee}}\dot{\bm{\eta}}

(57)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\bm{\eta}}^{T}\bm{M}_{ee}\dot{\bm{\eta}}

(58)

The strain energy is approximated by first substituting Eqs. (
48
) and (
49
) in Eqs. (
21
) and (
22
) to get:

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\phi\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\left(\frac{\partial\Psi_{e}^{\phi}}{\partial\phi}+\Psi_{e}^{r}\right)\bm{\eta}

(59)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\theta\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\left(\Psi_{e}^{\phi}\cot(\phi)+\Psi^{r}_{e}\right)\bm{\eta}

(60)

Then the strain energy equation in Eq. (
23
) becomes:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\bm{s}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\eta}}^{T}\Bigg[\frac{2\pi Eh}{1-\nu^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\Bigg\{\left(\frac{\partial\Psi_{e}^{\phi}}{\partial\phi}+\Psi_{e}^{r}\right)^{T}\left(\frac{\partial\Psi_{e}^{\phi}}{\partial\phi}+\Psi_{e}^{r}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\Psi_{e}^{\phi}\cot(\phi)+\Psi^{r}_{e}\right)^{T}\left(\Psi_{e}^{\phi}\cot(\phi)+\Psi^{r}_{e}\right)

\displaystyle+\nu\Biggl(\left(\frac{\partial\Psi_{e}^{\phi}}{\partial\phi}+\Psi_{e}^{r}\right)^{T}\left(\Psi_{e}^{\phi}\cot(\phi)+\Psi^{r}_{e}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\Psi_{e}^{\phi}\cot(\phi)+\Psi^{r}_{e}\right)^{T}\left(\frac{\partial\Psi_{e}^{\phi}}{\partial\phi}+\Psi_{e}^{r}\right)\Biggl)\Bigg\}\sin(\phi)d\phi\Bigg]\bm{\eta}

(61)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\eta}}^{T}\bm{K}_{ee}\bm{\eta}

(62)

where

\displaystyle\bm{K}_{ee}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi Eh}{1-\nu^{2}}\int_{0}^{\pi}\Biggl\{\zeta_{\phi\phi}^{T}\zeta_{\phi\phi}+{\zeta_{\theta\theta}}^{T}\zeta_{\theta\theta}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\nu\left(\zeta_{\phi\phi}^{T}\zeta_{\phi\phi}+{\zeta_{\theta\theta}}^{T}\zeta_{\theta\theta}\right)\Biggl\}\sin(\phi)d\phi

(63)

\displaystyle\zeta_{\phi\phi}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\partial\Psi_{e}^{\phi}}{\partial\phi}+\Psi_{e}^{r}\right)

(64)

\displaystyle\zeta_{\theta\theta}

\displaystyle=\left(\Psi_{e}^{\phi}\cot(\phi)+\Psi^{r}_{e}\right)

(65)

Using the above approximate expressions for the kinetic and strain energies, the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}_{s}
is expressed as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{s}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{T}_{\bm{s}}-\mathcal{U}_{\bm{s}}=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\bm{\eta}}^{T}\bm{M}_{ee}\dot{\bm{\eta}}-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{\eta}}^{T}\bm{K}_{ee}\bm{\eta}

(66)

With this approximation, the Lagrangian can be used to write the equations of motion of the flexible buoy in the form:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{s}}{\partial\dot{\bm{\eta}}}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{s}}{\partial{\bm{\eta}}}=\bm{0}

(67)

From Eq. (
66
), one can write:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{s}}{\partial{\bm{\eta}}}

\displaystyle=-\bm{K}_{ee}\bm{\eta},\;\text{and}\;\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{s}}{\partial\dot{\bm{\eta}}}=\bm{M}_{ee}\dot{\bm{\eta}}

\displaystyle\therefore\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{s}}{\partial\dot{\bm{\eta}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\bm{M}_{ee}\ddot{\bm{\eta}}

(68)

Hence, the equation of motion for the shell of a flexible buoy vibrating axisymmetricly is:

\displaystyle\bm{M}_{ee}\ddot{\bm{\eta}}

\displaystyle+\bm{K}_{ee}\bm{\eta}=\bm{0}

(69)

Equation (
69
) can be further extended to include Rayleigh damping as follows:

\bm{M}_{ee}\ddot{\bm{\eta}}+\bm{D}_{ee}\dot{\bm{\eta}}+\bm{K}_{ee}\bm{\eta}=\bm{0}

(70)

where
\bm{D}_{ee}
is a proportional damping matrix which is a function of the mass and stiffness matrices as follows:

\bm{D}_{ee}=\alpha_{d}\bm{M}_{ee}+\beta_{d}\bm{K}_{ee}

where the
\alpha_{d}
and
\beta_{d}
are real scalars named the mass and stiffness matrix multipliers with units 1/sec and sec, respectively
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
. Combining the equations of motion from Eqs. (
32
), (
33
) and (
70
) yields the equation of motion of the flexible buoy:

\displaystyle\bm{M}{\ddot{\vec{\mathbf{x}}}}+(\dot{\bm{M}}+\bm{D}){\dot{\vec{\mathbf{x}}}}+\begin{bmatrix}mg\bm{1}_{3}^{T}&\bm{0}&(\bm{K}_{ee}\bm{\eta})^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}=\bm{0}

(71)

where

\displaystyle\dot{\vec{\mathbf{x}}}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}{}^{a}\dot{\vec{\bm{r}}}_{sa}^{T}&{\vec{\mathbf{\omega}}}_{sa}^{T}&\dot{\bm{\eta}}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}_{(6+N)\times 1}

(72)

\displaystyle\bm{M}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}\{\begin{matrix}\bm{m}&\bm{\mathcal{J}}_{s}&\bm{M}_{ee}\end{matrix}\}_{(6+N)\times(6+N)}

(73)

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{M}}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}\{\begin{matrix}\bm{0}&\dot{\bm{\mathcal{J}}}_{s}&\bm{0}\end{matrix}\}_{(6+N)\times(6+N)}

(74)

\displaystyle\bm{D}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}\{\begin{matrix}\bm{D}_{x}&\bm{D}_{\omega}&\bm{D}_{ee}\end{matrix}\}_{(6+N)\times(6+N)}

(75)

It is noted here that the system mass matrix is a function of time because
\bm{\mathcal{J}}_{s}
is a function of time. The vector
\vec{\bm{\omega}}_{sa}
describes the instantaneous body angular velocities in the body frame
\hat{\bm{s}}
with respect to the inertial frame
\hat{\bm{a}}
. To avoid integrating the direction cosine matrices, the Euler angles are used for orientation (attitude) descriptions. Let
[\bm{B}(\theta)]
be the mapping matrix that converts the angular velocity
\vec{\bm{\omega}}_{sa}
to Euler angle rates
\dot{\bm{\theta}}_{sa}
; hence we can write:

\displaystyle\small\vec{\bm{\omega}}_{sa}

\displaystyle=[\bm{B}(\theta)]^{-1}\dot{\bm{\theta}}_{sa}

(76)

\displaystyle\dot{\vec{\bm{\omega}}}_{sa}

\displaystyle=[\bm{B}(\theta)]^{-1}\ddot{\bm{\theta}}_{sa}+[\dot{\bm{B}}(\theta)]^{-1}\dot{\bm{\theta}}_{sa}

(77)

\displaystyle[\bm{B}(\theta)]^{-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}-\sin{\theta_{2}}&0&1\\
\cos{\theta_{2}}\sin{\theta_{3}}&\cos{\theta_{3}}&0\\
\cos{\theta_{2}}\cos{\theta_{3}}&-\sin{\theta_{3}}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

(78)

\displaystyle[\dot{\bm{B}}(\theta)]^{-1}

\displaystyle=-\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\theta}_{2}\cos{\theta_{2}}&0&0\\
\dot{\theta}_{2}\sin{\theta_{2}}\sin{\theta_{3}}-\dot{\theta}_{3}\cos{\theta_{2}}\cos{\theta_{3}}&\dot{\theta}_{3}\sin{\theta_{3}}&0\\
\dot{\theta}_{2}\sin{\theta_{2}}\cos{\theta_{3}}+\dot{\theta}_{3}\cos{\theta}_{2}\sin{\theta_{3}}&\dot{\theta}_{3}\cos{\theta_{3}}&0\\
\end{bmatrix}

(79)

where Eqs. (
78
) and (
79
) are derived using a 3-2-1 Euler angles sequence. Substituting Eq. (
77
) into the inertia term in (
71
) to get:

\displaystyle\bm{M}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{}^{a}\ddot{\vec{\bm{r}}}_{sa}\\
\dot{\vec{\mathbf{\omega}}}_{sa}\\
\ddot{\bm{\eta}}\end{bmatrix}+(\dot{\bm{M}}+\bm{D})\begin{bmatrix}{}^{a}\dot{\vec{\bm{r}}}_{sa}\\
\vec{\mathbf{\omega}}_{sa}\\
\dot{\bm{\eta}}\end{bmatrix}+\bm{a}

\displaystyle=\bm{M}

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}{}^{a}\ddot{\vec{\bm{r}}}_{sa}\\
[\bm{B}(\theta)]^{-1}\ddot{\bm{\theta}}_{sa}\\
\ddot{\bm{\eta}}^{ee}\end{bmatrix}+\bm{M}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}\\
[\dot{\bm{B}}(\theta)]^{-1}\dot{\bm{\theta}}_{sa}\\
\bm{0}\end{bmatrix}+\bm{D}\begin{bmatrix}{}^{a}\dot{\vec{\bm{r}}}_{sa}\\
\vec{\mathbf{\omega}}_{sa}\\
\dot{\bm{\eta}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle+\begin{bmatrix}mg\bm{1}_{3}\\
\dot{\bm{\mathcal{J}}}_{s}\vec{\mathbf{\omega}}_{sa}\\
\bm{K}_{ee}\bm{\eta}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\bm{M}

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{B}}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}+\bm{M}\dot{\bar{\bm{B}}}\dot{\mathbf{q}}+\bm{D}\dot{\mathbf{q}}+\bm{a}

(80)

where
\mathbf{q}
is the generalized coordinates vector and is defined by Eq. (
81
):

\displaystyle\mathbf{q}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\vec{\bm{r}}_{sa}^{T}&\bm{\theta}_{sa}^{T}&\bm{\eta}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}

(81)

where

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{B}}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}\{\begin{matrix}\bm{1}&[\bm{B}(\theta)]^{-1}&\bm{1}\end{matrix}\},

(82)

\displaystyle\dot{\bar{\bm{B}}}

\displaystyle=\text{diag}\{\begin{matrix}\bm{0}&[\dot{\bm{B}}(\theta)]^{-1}&\bm{0}\end{matrix}\},

(83)

\displaystyle\bm{a}

\displaystyle={\begin{bmatrix}mg\bm{1}_{3}^{T}&(\dot{\bm{\mathcal{J}}}_{s}\vec{\mathbf{\omega}}_{sa})^{T}&(\bm{K}_{ee}\bm{\eta})^{T}\end{bmatrix}}^{T}

(84)

Then the Equation of motion for free buoy is expressed as:

\bar{\bm{B}}^{T}\bm{M}\bar{\bm{B}}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}+\bar{\bm{B}}^{T}(\bm{M}\dot{\bar{\bm{B}}}\dot{\mathbf{q}}+\bm{D}{\bar{{\bm{B}}}}\dot{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{a})=\bm{0}

(85)

The
\mathbf{q}
is a
(6+N)
column vector of generalized coordinates,
\bm{D}
is the system damping matrix,
\bm{D}_{x}
and
\bm{D}_{\omega}
are the transnational and rotational damping coefficients matrices, respectively.

IV
Forced Constrained Equations of Motion

The Lagrange equation for the forced motion for the discrete coordinates is expressed as:

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{\mathbf{q}}}\right)-\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\mathbf{q}}}=\bm{Q}

(86)

where,
\bm{Q}
is a column vector of generalized forces. The external generalized forces on a buoy are the generalized
PTO
force “
\bm{Q}_{pto}
”, generalized buoyant force “
\bm{Q}_{b}
”, generalized radiation forces
Q_{r}
, and generalized hydrodynamic excitation forces “
\bm{Q}_{e}
”.

The WECs can have an internal structure to install a
PTO
unit at an angle
\phi_{c}
on the shell. An example of this the VSB WEC tested in
[
17
]
, where a concert plate was attached at an angle
\phi_{c}=90^{o}
to apply the PTO force.
The elastic surface displacement
\vec{\bm{r}}_{dmc}
at
\phi_{c}=90^{o}
should equal to zero, resulting in the holonomic constraint in Eq. (
87
):

{\vec{\bm{r}}}_{dmc}(\phi_{c})=\bm{\Phi}_{c}\bm{\eta}=\bm{0}

(87)

where
{\bm{\Phi}}_{c}={\bm{\Phi}}_{e}(\phi_{c})
.

Hence, the most general form for the Equation of motion for a Spherical VSB is formed by equating the free vibration equation (Eq. (
85
)) to the external and constraint generalized forces as shown in Eq. (
88
):

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{B}}^{T}\bm{M}\bar{\bm{B}}\ddot{\mathbf{q}}+\bar{\bm{B}}^{T}(\bm{M}\dot{\bar{\bm{B}}}\dot{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle+\bm{D}{\bar{{\bm{B}}}}\dot{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{a})=\bm{Q}_{c}+\bm{Q}

\displaystyle=\bm{A}^{T}\bm{\lambda}+\bm{Q}_{pto}+\bm{Q}_{b}+\bm{Q}_{e}

(88)

where
\bm{Q}_{c}=\bm{A}^{T}\bm{\lambda}
is the generalized constraint vector,
\bm{\lambda}
column matrix of Lagrange multipliers with dimensions (
3\times 1
), and
\bm{A}
is the Jacobian constraint matrix.

To write the constraint in Eq. (
87
) in the generalized coordinates, we write the following transformation:

\displaystyle\bm{0}={\bm{\Phi}}_{c}\bm{\eta}\equiv\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}&\bm{0}&{\bm{\Phi}}_{c}\end{bmatrix}}_{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}_{c}}\bar{\bm{B}}\mathbf{q}=\underbrace{\bar{\bm{\Phi}}_{c}\bar{\bm{B}}}_{\bm{A}}\mathbf{q}

(89)

Eq. (
89
) can be written as
\bm{A}\mathbf{q}=\bm{0}
, where
\bm{A}=\bar{\bm{\Phi}}_{c}\bar{\bm{B}}
.
Reference
[
35
]
shows that the generalized constraint forces for ideal constraints can be expressed as:

\bm{Q}_{c}=\bm{M}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{A}\bm{M}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{\dagger}(\bm{b}-\bm{AM}^{-1}(\bm{Q}-\bar{\bm{a}}))

(90)

where
\bm{b}=-(\ddot{\bm{A}}\mathbf{q}+2\dot{\bm{A}}\dot{\mathbf{q}})
, and
\bar{\bm{a}}=\bar{\bm{B}}^{T}(\bm{M}\dot{\bar{\bm{B}}}\dot{\mathbf{q}}+\bm{D}{\bar{{\bm{B}}}}\dot{\mathbf{q}}+\mathbf{a})
.

It is common in WEC analysis to assume that the WEC is only heaving (moving only in the vertical direction,) and hence we can simplify the equations by assuming that the buoy is not rotating; that is
\dot{\bar{\bm{B}}}=\bm{0}
. In such case, the vector
\bm{b}=\bm{0}
.
Next, the expression for each of the external forces is developed.

IV-A

Power Take-off Unit Force

The PTO can be either active or passive
[
17
]
, in this paper the PTO is assumed a passive damping. To apply the PTO effect for that case one can either include its effect in the
\bm{D}_{x}
matrix in Eq. (
88
), or as an external force in Eq. (
91
).

\vec{\mathbit{f}}^{pto}=-c\>{}^{a}\dot{\vec{\mathit{\bm{r}}}}_{sa,3}\>\bm{a}_{3}

(91)

Where
\vec{\mathbit{f}}^{pto}
is the damping force and
c
is the damping coefficient. Note that the equation above applies to heave only motion. To compute the generalized force, the general transformation takes the form
[
27
]
:

\mathbit{Q}_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{6+N}f_{i}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{r_{i}}}{\partial q_{j}}

(92)

Hence, the generalized force corresponding to the PTO force takes the form:

\mathbit{Q}_{j}^{pto}=\vec{\bm{f}}^{pto}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{r}_{sa}}{\partial q_{j}},\,\,\forall j=1,2,\cdots 6+N

(93)

We can then write the PTO generalized forces using Eq. (
81
) and Eq. (
91
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbit{Q}_{1}^{pto}

\displaystyle=\vec{\bm{f}}^{pto}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{\bm{r}}_{sa}}{\partial\vec{\bm{r}}_{sa,1}}=-c{}^{a}\dot{\vec{\mathit{\bm{r}}}}_{sa,3}\bm{a}_{3}\cdot\bm{1}_{1}=0

\displaystyle\mathbit{Q}_{2}^{pto}

\displaystyle=\vec{\bm{f}}^{pto}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{\bm{r}}_{sa}}{\partial\vec{\bm{r}}_{sa,2}}=-c{}^{a}\dot{\vec{\mathit{\bm{r}}}}_{sa,3}\bm{a}_{3}\cdot\bm{1}_{2}=0

\displaystyle\mathbit{Q}_{3}^{pto}

\displaystyle=\vec{\bm{f}}^{pto}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec{\bm{r}}_{sa}}{\partial\vec{\bm{r}}_{sa,3}}=-c{}^{a}\dot{\vec{\mathit{\bm{r}}}}_{sa,3}\bm{a}_{3}\cdot\bm{1}_{3}=-c{}^{a}\dot{\vec{\mathit{\bm{r}}}}_{sa,3}

\displaystyle\mathbit{Q}_{4}^{pto}

Hydro Forces

This work describes the method of applying the hydrodynamic loads on the shells in the Lagrangian mechanics realm, the hydrodynamic loads can be obtained from BEM solvers or CFD solvers (ex., capytaine, NEMOH and openFOAM).

In this work we assume that the average of the summation of hydrodynamic pressures (excitation, radiation, etc) is sinusoidal. It is assumed that the average hydrodynamic pressures
P_{hydrod}
is uniformly distributed around the submerged buoy’s volume; such that the hydrodynamic force on a submerged partition is expressed as
{f_{hydrod}}_{i}=P_{hydrod}{A}_{i}
, where

i^{th}

The hydrostatic force (buoyant force)

\hat{\bm{a}}_{3}

VII
Conclusion and Future Work

The equations of motion of spherical Variable-Shape Buoy Wave Energy Converters, with axisymmetric deformations, were derived using a Lagrangian formulation in this paper. A Rayleigh-Ritz method along with the classical bending theory for stress-strain relations were used to approximate the equations of motion to finite-dimension equations of motion, in the six degrees of freedom. Holonomic constraints were imposed to limit the buoy to only-heave motion, and to enforce no-deformation at specific locations, to account for the power take-off unit installation flanges. The inner volume of the VSB WEC is assumed to be vented to the atmosphere to exclude any internal pressure variation effect on the buoys’ shell.
The numerical results support the hypothesis of this work which is that a VSB WEC would harvest energy at a significantly higher rate compared to that of a FSB WEC, when both WECs use no reactive power.
The VSB WEC with zero deformation at its highest vertical location harvested more energy than the VSB WEC with unconstrained shell deformations and the VSB WEC with shell constraint at the horizontal midsection.

For future work, one-way and two-way FSI investigations are required to examine the hydrodynamic performance of the VSB WECs in realistic regular and irregular waves environment; in these FSI model, the developed dynamic model would be coupled with a fluid solver (e.g., OpenFoam, Nemoh, WAMIT) such that at every time step the deformed geometry is exported to the fluid solver where the pressure distribution around the VSB WEC shell is calculated. In case of using BEM solvers, expressions for the generalized added mass, damping, hydostatic stiffness and excitation force coefficients can be calculated in the generalized coordinates.
