Full Statistics of Nonstationary Heat Transfer in the Kipnis-Marchioro-Presutti Model
Eldad Bettelheim, Naftali R. Smith, Baruch Meerson

Abstract
We investigate non-stationary heat transfer in the Kipnis-Marchioro-Presutti (KMP) lattice gas model at long times in one dimension when starting from a localized heat distribution. At large scales this initial condition can be described as a delta-function, u ⁡ ( x , t = 0 ) = W ​ δ ​ ( x ) u(x,t=0)=W\delta(x) . We characterize the process by the heat transferred to the right of a specified point x = X x=X by time T T , J = ∫ X ∞ u ⁡ ( x , t = T ) ​ 𝑑 x , J=\int_{X}^{\infty}u(x,t=T)\,dx\,, and study the full probability distribution 𝒫 ⁡ ( J , X , T ) \mathcal{P}(J,X,T) . The particular case of X = 0 X=0 has been recently solved [Bettelheim et al . Phys. Rev. Lett. 128 , 130602 (2022)]. At fixed J J , the distribution 𝒫 \mathcal{P} as a function of X X and T T has the same long-time dynamical scaling properties as the position of a tracer in a single-file diffusion. Here we evaluate 𝒫 ⁡ ( J , X , T ) \mathcal{P}(J,X,T) by exploiting the recently uncovered complete integrability of the equations of the macroscopic fluctuation theory (MFT) for the KMP model and using the Zakharov-Shabat inverse scattering method. We also discuss asymptotics of 𝒫 ⁡ ( J , X , T ) \mathcal{P}(J,X,T) which we extract from the exact solution and also obtain by applying two different perturbation methods directly to the MFT equations.

II
Formulation of the MFT problem

We rescale the variables

,

and

by

,

and

, respectively. As a result,

and

and by

and the conjugate “momentum density” field

We also introduce the (minus) momentum density gradient field

;

;

, the MFT equations can be written as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{x}(\partial_{x}u+2u^{2}v)\,,

(9)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\partial_{x}(-\partial_{x}v+2uv^{2})\,.

(10)

To make this paper more self-contained, a derivation of Eqs. (
9
) and (

The initial condition, after the rescaling, is

u(x,t=0)=\delta(x)\,.

(11)

The rescaled transferred heat at

\int_{X}^{\infty}u(x,t=1)\,dx=J\,.

(12)

The particular case

was solved in Ref.
BSM1
. Being interested in

The constrained optimization problem yields, aside from Eqs. (

where

This MFT formulation possesses a nontrivial symmetry

Once

\displaystyle s=\int_{0}^{1}dt\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,u^{2}v^{2}.

This action determines the probability density

\ln{\mathcal{P}}(J,X,T)\simeq-\sqrt{T}\,s\left(\frac{J}{W},\frac{X}{\sqrt{T}}\right)\,,

which describes the scaling behavior of

scales with time as

, that is sub-diffusively. This scaling coincides with the one universally observed for the tracer position in single file diffusion processes, see Ref.
KMS1
;
KMS2
and references therein. This coincidence is of course not accidental. Indeed, the mathematical formulation of the MFT problem that we have just presented, is very similar to the MFT formulation
for the statistics of the position of a tracer

;
KMS2
. The differences are in the particular

), and in the delta-function initial condition (

At fixed

and small

Solution of the MFT problem

Let us outline the key ideas behind the approach that we shall use to solve the MFT problem: the ISM

;

As explained below, one can then solve for the time evolution of the scattering amplitudes, which turns out to be very simple. By relating the fields

to the scattering amplitudes, at

and

III.1
Scattering amplitudes and their dynamics

Adapting the derivation of Kaup and Newell
KN
to imaginary time and space, we consider the linear system

\begin{cases}\partial_{x}\mathbold{\psi}(x,t,k)=U(x,t,k)\mathbold{\psi}(x,t,k)\,,\\[2.84526pt]
\partial_{t}\mathbold{\psi}(x,t,k)=V(x,t,k)\mathbold{\psi}(x,t,k)\,,\end{cases}

(17)

where
\mathbold{\psi}(x,t,k)
is a column vector of dimension 2, the matrices

\displaystyle U(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-ik/2&-iv\sqrt{ik}\\[2.84526pt]
-iu\sqrt{ik}&ik/2\\
\end{pmatrix},

(18)

\displaystyle V(x,t,k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}k^{2}/2-ikuv&-i(\sqrt{ik})^{3}v+i\sqrt{ik}\,\partial_{x}v-i\sqrt{ik}2v^{2}u\\[2.84526pt]
-i(\sqrt{ik})^{3}u+i\sqrt{ik}\,\partial_{x}u-i\sqrt{ik}2u^{2}v&-k^{2}/2+ikuv\\
\end{pmatrix}

(19)

constitute the Lax pair, and

is a spectral parameter.
It is now straightforward to check that the compatibility condition
\partial_{t}\partial_{x}\mathbold{\psi}=\partial_{x}\partial_{t}\mathbold{\psi}
, which corresponds to

\partial_{t}U-\partial_{x}V+\left[U,V\right]=0,

(20)

is indeed equivalent to Eqs. (
9
) and (
10

It is useful to define the matrix
\mathcal{T}(x,y,t,k)
as the

\partial_{x}\mathcal{T}(x,y,t,k)=U(x,t,k)\mathcal{T}(x,y,t,k)

(21)

with the initial condition
\mathcal{T}(x,x,t,k)=I
(the identity matrix).
At

and

U(x\to\pm\infty,t,k)=\begin{pmatrix}-ik/2&0\\
0&ik/2\end{pmatrix}\,.

(22)

As a result, the entries of
\mathcal{T}(x,y,t,k)
oscillate as a function of

and

with wave numbers
whose magnitude is

at

.
Therefore, it is natural to define the full-space propagator

0&e^{iky/2}\end{pmatrix}.

(23)

The matrix

plays an important role in the ISM, because its (

- and

The time evolution of
G(t,k)
is remarkably simple. Indeed, one finds that the time evolution of the matrix

satisfies:

\partial_{t}\mathcal{T}(x,y,t,k)=V(x,t,k)\mathcal{T}(x,y,t,k)-\mathcal{T}(x,y,t,k)V(y,t,k)\,.

As one can verify directly, Eq. (

) is compatible with (

) (
i.e.

\partial_{t}\partial_{x}\mathcal{T}=\partial_{x}\partial_{t}\mathcal{T}

The matrix

Plugging Eq. (

which in turn, using (

\displaystyle G(t,k)=\begin{pmatrix}a(t,k)&\tilde{b}(t,k)\\[2.84526pt]
b(t,k)&\tilde{a}(t,k)\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a(0,k)&\tilde{b}(0,k)e^{k^{2}t}\\[2.84526pt]
b(0,k)e^{-k^{2}t}&\tilde{a}(0,k)\\
\end{pmatrix}

In Eq. (

V
Discussion

Here we studied the full distribution

of the total heat

in the KMP model at long times for an initially localized heat pulse, thereby extending our previous work

which was limited to

We calculated exactly the rescaled rate function

Furthermore, we studied three different asymptotics of

The distribution

at fixed

has the same long-time dynamical scaling as the distribution of a tracer particle in a single-file transport of interacting particles, as in the SSEP.
It would be interesting to try to apply the ISM to other large-deviation problems for the KMP model, in which non-stationary heat transfer statistics problems were previously solved analytically only in some limiting cases

;
KrMe
;
MS2013
.

The MFT of lattice gases can be viewed as a particular case of the optimal fluctuation method (OFM), a versatile tool which can be used to study large deviations in a broad class of macroscopic systems that also includes paradigmatic surface growth models (such as the KPZ equation), reaction-diffusion systems, and many more
Halperin
;
Langer
;
Lifshitz
;
turb1
;
EK
;
MS2011
;
Fogedby
;
KK
;
MKV
;
HMS
.
Mathematically, the OFM formulation involves a set of coupled nonlinear partial differential equations whose solution gives the optimal history of the system, conditioned on the rare event in question. It is generally very hard to solve these equations exactly.
Therefore, uncovering and exploiting exact integrability of these equations appears to be a way to make a significant progress in some of these systems.
Indeed, the present work is one of several very recent studies

;
KLD1
;
KLD2
;
MMS22
;
KLD3
which use the ISM in order to obtain exact solutions for full statistics of different physical quantities in nonlinear spatio-temporal stochastic systems. We believe that this line of work has a great potential for the future.

Appendix A
Derivation of the MFT equations and boundary conditions

Let us first explain why the effective amplitude of the noise becomes small in the long-time limit
T\gg 1
.
We rescale time and space,
\tilde{t}=t/T
,
\tilde{x}=x/\sqrt{T}
, and define

\tilde{u}\left(\tilde{x},\tilde{t}\right)\equiv u\left(\tilde{x}\sqrt{T},\tilde{t}T\right)
,
and the dimensionless noise term

\tilde{\eta}\left(\tilde{x},\tilde{t}\right)=T^{3/4}\eta\left(\tilde{x}\sqrt{T},\tilde{t}T\right)
.
Under these rescalings, Eq. (
8
) of the main text becomes

\partial_{\tilde{t}}\tilde{u}=\partial_{\tilde{x}}\left(\partial_{\tilde{x}}\tilde{u}+T^{-1/4}\sqrt{2}\,\tilde{u}\tilde{\eta}\right)

(A1)

from which it is clearly observed that the noise is effectively weak in the limit
T\gg 1
. Note, however, that the long-time limit must be taken with constant
X/\sqrt{T}
.

We now derive of the MFT equations, Eqs. (
9
) and (
10
) of the main text.
We first define a “potential”

\psi\left(x,t\right)=\int_{-\infty}^{x}u\left(y,t\right)dy\,.

(A2)

Integrating Eq. (
8
) of the main text with respect to
x
and using the boundary condition
u(x\to-\infty,t)\to 0
, we obtain a Langevin equation for
\psi(x,t)
:

\partial_{t}\psi=\partial_{x}^{2}\psi+\sqrt{2}\,\partial_{x}\psi\,\eta\,.

(A3)

An equivalent description of the dynamics (
A3
) is provided by the path-integral representation. The probability density functional
P[\eta]
of the white Gaussian noise term
\eta(x,t)
is

P\left[\eta\right]\sim\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{T}dt\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}\right)\,.

(A4)

The advantage of using the
\psi
representation is that the noise term in Eq. (
A3
) appears without a spatial derivative, and one can therefore express
\eta
through
\psi
via Eq. (
A3
).
Using this in Eq. (
A4
), one finds that the probability density for a given realization of
\psi(x,t)
is:

-\ln P\left[\psi\right]\simeq S\left[\psi\right]\equiv\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{T}dt\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\left(\frac{\partial_{t}\psi-\partial_{x}^{2}\psi}{\partial_{x}\psi}\right)^{2}\,,

(A5)

where
S\left[\psi\right]
is the action functional.
As explained above, in the limit
\sqrt{T}\gg 1
, the noise is effectively weak. This enables us to calculate
\mathcal{P}(J,T)
via a saddle-point evaluation of the path integral. Within this framework,
-\ln\mathcal{P}\left(J,T\right)
is given by the minimum of the action functional
S
with respect to all realizations
\psi(x,t)
, constrained on the initial condition
u(x,0)=W\delta(x)
and on a given value of the transferred heat

J=W-\psi(X,T)
(where we used the conservation law
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u(x,t=T)\,dx=W
).
The latter constraint on
J
is taken into account by adding the term
-\Lambda\psi\left(0,T\right)
to the action, where
\Lambda
is a Lagrange multiplier, i.e., we minimize the constrained functional

S_{\Lambda}\left[\psi\right]=S\left[\psi\right]-\Lambda\psi\left(0,T\right)=\frac{1}{4}\int_{0}^{T}dt\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\left(\frac{\partial_{t}\psi-\partial_{x}^{2}\psi}{\partial_{x}\psi}\right)^{2}-\Lambda\psi\left(X,T\right)\,.

(A6)

To minimize
S_{\Lambda}
, we must require its linear variation with respect to
\psi(x,t)
to vanish.
A given variation
\psi(x,t)\to\psi(x,t)+\delta\psi(x,t)
causes a variation of
S_{\Lambda}\left[\psi\right]
which, to first order in
\delta\psi
, is given by

\displaystyle\delta S_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{\Lambda}\left[\psi+\delta\psi\right]-S_{\Lambda}\left[\psi\right]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}dt\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\left(\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}\psi-\partial_{t}\psi}{\partial_{x}\psi}\right)\left[\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}\delta\psi-\partial_{t}\delta\psi}{\partial_{x}\psi}-\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}\psi-\partial_{t}\psi}{\left(\partial_{x}\psi\right)^{2}}\partial_{x}\delta\psi\right]-\Lambda\delta\psi\left(X,T\right)

(A7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}dt\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\partial_{x}p\left(\partial_{x}^{2}\delta\psi-\partial_{t}\delta\psi-2\partial_{x}p\partial_{x}\psi\partial_{x}\delta\psi\right)-\Lambda\delta\psi\left(X,T\right)\,,

where we have defined the momentum density gradient

\partial_{x}p=\frac{\partial_{x}^{2}\psi-\partial_{t}\psi}{2\left(\partial_{x}\psi\right)^{2}}\;.

(A8)

Integrating by parts in Eq. (
A7
), we obtain

\displaystyle\delta S_{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}dt\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\left\{\partial_{x}^{3}p+\partial_{xt}p+2\partial_{x}\left[\left(\partial_{x}p\right)^{2}\partial_{x}\psi\right]\right\}\delta\psi

(A9)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\left[\partial_{x}p\left(x,0\right)\delta\psi\left(x,0\right)-\partial_{x}p\left(x,T\right)\delta\psi\left(x,T\right)-\Lambda\delta\left(x-X\right)\delta\psi\left(x,T\right)\right]\,,

where the first two terms in the single integral are the boundary terms originating from the integration by parts in time.
Since
\psi(x,0)
is given (i.e., the initial condition is quenched), the variation
\delta\psi\left(x,0\right)
vanishes.

By requiring the double integral in (
A9
) to vanish for arbitrary
\delta\psi
, one obtains the second MFT equation, Eq. (
10
) in the main text (recalling that
v=-\partial_{x}p
and
u=\partial_{x}\psi
). The first MFT equation, Eq. (
9
) in the main text, follows from Eq. (
A8
) after multiplying by the denominator and then taking a spatial derivative.
Note that, under the rescalings of
x
,
t
and
u
described in the text,
v
and
X
should be rescaled by
1/W
and
\sqrt{T}
, respectively. The MFT equations invariant under these rescalings.
In addition, we must require the single integral in Eq. (
A9
) to vanish for arbitrary
\delta\psi\left(x,T\right)
. This yields the boundary condition

\partial_{x}p\left(x,T\right)=-\Lambda\delta(x-X)\,.

(A10)

After the rescaling, this becomes Eq. (
13
) in the main text, where
\lambda=W\Lambda
is a rescaled Lagrange multiplier, and
X
is rescaled as well.
Finally, using Eq. (
A8
) in (
A5
) one finds that the action can be rewritten as
S=\int_{0}^{T}dt\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,u^{2}v^{2}
which, after the rescaling, leads to
S=\sqrt{T}\,s
where the rescaled action
s
is given by Eq. (
15
) in the main text.
The large parameter
S\sim\sqrt{T}\gg 1
justifies
a posteriori
the saddle-point approximation that we used.

Appendix B
Solving the scattering problem at
t=0

In this appendix, we solve Eq. (
27
) in the main text, obtaining Eq. (
28
). Let us introduce the notation

\mathcal{T}\left(x,y,0,k\right)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{T}_{11}&\mathcal{T}_{12}\\[2.84526pt]
\mathcal{T}_{21}&\mathcal{T}_{21}\end{pmatrix}

(B1)

for the entries of the matrix, with the
x,y,k
dependence suppressed for brevity.
We solve Eq. (
27
) separately for the two columns of the matrix. For the left column, Eq. (
27
) reads

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\mathcal{T}_{11}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{ik}{2}\mathcal{T}_{11}-iv\left(x,0\right)\sqrt{ik}\mathcal{T}_{21}\,,

(B2)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\mathcal{T}_{21}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\delta\left(x\right)\sqrt{ik}\mathcal{T}_{11}+\frac{ik}{2}\mathcal{T}_{21}\,.

(B3)

It is convenient to solve these equations separately in the two regions
x<0
and
x>0
. The general solution reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{11}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}A_{-}e^{-ikx/2}-i\sqrt{ik}B_{-}e^{-ikx/2}e^{iky}I_{v}\left(x,y\right),&x<0,\\[2.84526pt]
A_{+}e^{-ikx/2}-i\sqrt{ik}B_{+}e^{-ikx/2}e^{iky}I_{v}\left(x,y\right),&x>0,\end{cases}

(B4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{21}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}B_{-}e^{ikx/2},&x<0,\\[2.84526pt]
B_{+}e^{ikx/2},&x>0.\end{cases}

(B5)

In order to find the four coefficients
A_{\pm}
and
B_{\pm}
(that depend on
y
and
k
), we use the following four conditions:
(i)
\mathcal{T}_{11}
must be continuous at
x=0
.
(ii)
\mathcal{T}_{21}
has a jump discontinuity at
x=0
, whose magnitude is found by integrating Eq. (
B3
) over an infinitesimally small region around

\left.\mathcal{T}_{21}\right|_{x=0^{+}}-\left.\mathcal{T}_{21}\right|_{x=0^{-}}=-i\sqrt{ik}\left.\mathcal{T}_{11}\right|_{x=0}\,.

(B6)

(iii)
\left.\mathcal{T}_{11}\right|_{x=y}=1
.
(iv)
\left.\mathcal{T}_{21}\right|_{x=y}=0
.
The latter two conditions come from the boundary condition
\mathcal{T}(x,x,t,k)=I
. These conditions yield four linear equations on the coefficients, whose solution then gives the left column of the matrix

For the right column of the matrix, the solution is very similar (replacing

\mathcal{T}\left(x,y,0,k\right)
