On Data Analysis Pipelines and Modular Bayesian Modeling
Erin Lipman, Abel Rodriguez

Abstract
The most common approach to implementing data analysis pipelines involves obtaining point estimates from the upstream modules and then treating these as known quantities when working with the downstream ones. This approach is straightforward, but it is likely to underestimate the overall uncertainty associated with any final estimates. An alternative approach involves estimating parameters from the modules jointly using a Bayesian hierarchical model, which has the advantage of propagating upstream uncertainty into the downstream estimates. However, when modules are misspecified, such a joint model can behave in unexpected ways. Furthermore, hierarchical models require the development of ad-hoc computational implementations that can be laborious and computationally expensive. Cut inference modifies the posterior distribution to prevent information flow between certain parameters and provides a third alternative for statistical inference in data analysis pipelines. This paper presents a unified framework that encompasses two-step, cut, and joint inference in the context of data analysis pipelines with two modules and uses two examples to illustrate the tradeoffs associated with these approaches. Our work shows that cut inference provides both some level of robustness and ease of implementation for data analysis pipelines at a lower cost in terms of statistical inference.

Introduction

Data analysis pipelines, in which a series of algorithms and/or statistical procedures are strung together in such a way that the output generated by those upstream is used as input for those downstream, are widely used across the sciences. For example, in political science, constructs such as legislators’ revealed preferences are often used as either responses or covariates in regression models designed to test hypotheses about legislative behavior (e.g., see
Schickler 2000
,
Schwindt-Bayer and
Corbetta 2004
and
Clark 2012
).

Two-step (or, more generally, multi-step) procedures, in which the products transferred from one module to the next are limited to point estimators, offer both simplicity and modularity in the implementation of data analysis pipelines. Simplicity is particularly appealing to practitioners: a data analysis pipeline can often be put together using existing code and no more than a few function calls. Modularity implies that it is often easy to update the pipeline or perform sensitivity analysis by simply replacing one or two function calls. However, an important drawback of multi-step approaches is that they often ignore the uncertainty associated with the output produced by the upstream modules. For example, the point estimates of legislators’ preferences derived from their voting records are typically plugged directly into regression models and treated as “known” values even though they are only estimates and, therefore, inherently noisy. This often leads to under-coverage and bias downstream (e.g., see
Murphy and Topel 2002
and
Fernández-Val and
Vella 2011
).

An alternative approach to data analysis pipelines is full (Bayesian) hierarchical modeling. The posterior distribution arising from a hierarchical model that incorporates all of the modules will, by construction, fully account for all the uncertainty associated with the analysis. One example of such hierarchical models is presented in
Lipman, Moser and
Rodriguez 2023
, who consider the problem of identifying covariates that might explain differences in legislators’ preferences across various types of votes (e.g., procedural vs. substantive votes). Their model can be seen as consisting of two modules, with the first one designed to infer legislators’ preferences and whether they differ across vote types, and the second one designed to explain the existence of such a difference as a function of various covariates (please see Section
4
). Yet, working with large hierarchical models can be challenging. From a computational point of view, combining code that was originally created for the individual modules is more challenging that creating a traditional pipeline. More importantly, it is well known that misspecification of even a single module in a hierarchical model can cause incorrect estimation of other modules, even if these other modules are correctly specified
(
Plummer 2015
;
Liu and Goudie 2022a
)
, a phenomenon sometimes called
contamination
.

So-called cut posteriors
(
Liu et al. 2009
;
Plummer 2015
;
Jacob et al. 2017
;
Liu and Goudie 2022b
)
provide a middle ground between full and multi-step analysis in Bayesian settings. Cut posteriors modify the posterior distribution so that information is prevented from flowing along certain directions on the graphical model describing the relationships between the parameters and data sources. Most work on cut posteriors has focused on situations in which there are two separate sets of response variables that provide information about a common set of parameters. In that kind of setting, one of the two modules / data sources might be considered more reliable, and the cut posterior is sometimes justified as providing a mechanism to prevent the less reliable module from contaminating the other.

In this paper we investigate the use of cut posteriors in two-module data analysis pipelines. This setting is of interest not only because pipelines are widely used in practice, but also because the underlying graphical model has a structure that is different from that of most of the examples in which cut posteriors have been studied before. To fix ideas, we consider two concrete examples. In the first one, each of the modules in the pipeline corresponds to a linear matrix-variate regression model. This setting allows for the derivation of closed-form expressions for the all posteriors, enabling us to clearly illustrate the statistical trade offs associated with various approaches to data analysis pipelines. For our second example, we develop a feedback-cut version of the
Lipman, Moser and
Rodriguez 2023
model and compare its performance against the full model as well as against a two-step procedure. While no closed-form expressions for the estimates are available in this second example, the non-linearities implicit in the model and the focus on variable selection provide an opportunity to gain additional insight into the behavior all of three approaches.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section
2
provides a general framework for describing and analyzing data pipelines with two modules. Section
3
presents our first illustration which focuses on modules that correspond to matrix-variate linear models. In this context, closed-form expressions are available for the full, two-step and cut distributions, facilitating comparisons. Then, Section
4
introduces a more complex two-module pipeline motivated by an application in political science and compares the performance of all three approaches to inference in its context. Finally, Section
5
summarizes the main takeaway points from the two illustrations and suggests future directions for research.

2
Data analysis pipelines with two modules

Let
\mathbf{Y}
represent the response variable. We assume that the joint distribution of
\mathbf{Y}
can be described by a probability model
p(\mathbf{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\zeta},\mathbf{W})
indexed by a set of (known) covariates
\mathbf{W}
and two sets of parameters:
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
, denoting the key parameters of interest in the first module, and
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}
, the nuisance parameters. We further assume that a prior distribution for the nuisance parameters given the parameters of interest and the covariates,
p(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta},\mathbf{W})
, is available. We refer to the resulting marginal distribution,

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta},\mathbf{W})=\int p(\mathbf{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1},\boldsymbol{\zeta},\mathbf{W})p(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta},\mathbf{W})\text{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1},

(1)

as the first module in the pipeline.

The second module is defined by treating the latent vector
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
as if it were observed. Again, we assume that the joint distribution for
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is given by a probability model
p(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2},\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{X})
, where
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is the key parameter of interest in the second module,
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}
is a nuisance parameter, and
\boldsymbol{X}
is a set of auxiliary data (for example covariates used in a regression model). As before, we assume that a prior
p(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{X})
is available for the nuisance parameters. The second module of our pipeline is then given by

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{X})=\int p(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2},\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{X})p(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{X})\text{d}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}.

(2)

Implicit in this definition of the second module is the assumption that the parameters
(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2})
are independent from the data
\mathbf{Y}
conditioned on
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
. Figure

The ultimate goal of our analysis is to learn
\boldsymbol{\xi}
from the observed
\mathbf{Y}
. Within a Bayesian framework, the most natural approach to answer this question is to base inferences on the marginal posterior distribution,

\displaystyle p_{f}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle=\frac{\int p(\mathbf{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta})p(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi})p(\boldsymbol{\xi})\text{d}\boldsymbol{\zeta}}{\int\int p(\mathbf{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta})p(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi})p(\boldsymbol{\xi})\text{d}\boldsymbol{\zeta}\text{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}}

(3)

\displaystyle=\int p(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta})p(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y})\text{d}\boldsymbol{\zeta},

where

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta})

\displaystyle=\frac{p(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi})p(\boldsymbol{\xi})}{\int p(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi})p(\boldsymbol{\xi})\text{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}},

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle=\frac{p(\mathbf{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta})p(\boldsymbol{\zeta})}{\int p(\mathbf{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta})p(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\text{d}\boldsymbol{\zeta}}

\displaystyle=\frac{p(\mathbf{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta})\int p(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi})p(\boldsymbol{\xi})\text{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}}{\int\int p(\mathbf{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta})p(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi})p(\boldsymbol{\xi})\text{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}\text{d}\boldsymbol{\zeta}},

and
p(\boldsymbol{\xi})
is an appropriate prior on
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. Note that, to simplify notation, we have dropped the explicit dependence on
\mathbf{W}
and
\boldsymbol{X}
.

While this approach is natural from a Bayesian perspective and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms to carry out inference under this full model can usually be derived, it can be challenging to implement for practitioners and may be computationally intensive. This is often true even when using probabilistic programming languages such as
Stan

(
Stan Development Team 2023
)
,
RBugs

(
Thomas et al. 2006
)
or
NIMBLE
,
(
de Valpine et al. 2017
)
, especially in settings where the parameters of interest are discrete (such as when
\boldsymbol{\xi}
indexes a collection of models). Furthermore, it has been noted in the literature that, when at least one of the models is misspecified, inferences obtained from the joint model can be nonsensical (e.g., see
Liu et al. 2009
;
Plummer 2015
;
Jacob et al. 2017
;
Liu and Goudie 2022b
). We provide an additional example of this in Section
4
.

An alternative approach, which is popular among practitioners because of its ease of implementation, involves first generating a point estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
for
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
based on (
1
) (potentially, but not necessarily, through a Bayesian approach to inference), and then treating that point estimator as if it where the true observed value of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
to then estimate
\boldsymbol{\xi}
from (
2
) (again, possibly through a Bayesian procedure). More specifically, introduce a working prior
\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})
that is independent of the parameter of interest
\boldsymbol{\xi}
.
We can combine (
1
) with
\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})
to obtain the working first-level posterior

\displaystyle\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y})=\frac{p(\mathbf{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta})\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})}{\bar{p}(\mathbf{Y})},

(4)

where
\bar{p}(\mathbf{Y})=\int p(\mathbf{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta})\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\text{d}\boldsymbol{\zeta}
. Given an appropriate loss function
L(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\prime})
, this posterior distribution can be used to generate a point estimator

\hat{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(\mathbf{Y})=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\prime}}\int L(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\prime})\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y})\text{d}\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\prime}.

Then, inferences for
\boldsymbol{\xi}
can be based on the working second-level posterior

\displaystyle p_{t}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle=p\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(\mathbf{Y})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{p\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(\mathbf{Y})\mid\boldsymbol{\xi}\right)p\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}\right)}{\int p\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(\mathbf{Y})\mid\boldsymbol{\xi}\right)p\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}\right)\text{d}\boldsymbol{\xi}}

\displaystyle=\int p(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta})\delta_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(\mathbf{Y})}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\text{d}\boldsymbol{\zeta},

where
\delta_{a}(\cdot)
denotes the degenerate distribution placing all its mass at
a
. The joint pseudo-posterior distribution for
(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi})
that is implied by this approach is then given by

\displaystyle p_{t}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})=p\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(\mathbf{Y})\right)\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y}).

(5)

Two-step approaches are much easier to implement than the full model approach: in many settings, software is readily available to carry out inferences based on the first and second level working posteriors, so the whole pipeline can be implemented using just a lines of code. Additionally, estimating the two models separately may be less computationally intensive than estimating the full, joint model (e.g., when point estimators from the either module can be obtained in closed form, or when the mixing times of the individual MCMC algorithms are much lower than that of the MCMC for the hierarchical model). However, by treating
\hat{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
as known rather than as the estimate it is, the uncertainty in the estimation of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is not propagated through to the second module. This leads to underestimation of the uncertainty in the estimation of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and, potentially, to bias as well. Furthermore, the choice of the point estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
based on the first-level posterior
\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y})
is dependent on the choice of loss function. This is particularly problematic because it is not always clear how to select the most appropriate loss function, especially since the problem of estimating
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is not necessarily of primary interest. For example, when
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is a vector of binary indicators, it is common to use a zero-one loss, resulting in point estimates that threshold the posterior probabilities, with the threshold depending on the relative loss assigned to a false positive versus a false negative. The point estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
(and, therefore, the results from the overall procedure) can be highly dependent on the exact value chosen for these relative losses. While a threshold of 0.5 (which implies equal losses for both types of errors) is a common default choice, it is not clear that it is appropriate in every setting.

A third approach is to consider a cut posterior. Its definition starts with the same working first-level posterior
\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y})
as the two-step approach (recall Equation (
4
)), which is used to construct the joint pseudo-posterior

\displaystyle p_{c}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})=p(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta})\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y}).

(6)

The marginal cut posterior for
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is then given by

\displaystyle p_{c}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})=\int p(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta})\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y})\text{d}\boldsymbol{\zeta}.

(7)

While cut posteriors in general require more sophisticated computational approaches than two-step posteriors (e.g., see
Plummer 2015
), the case of data analysis pipelines admits a straightforward computational approach that reduces to first obtaining
B
samples

\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(B)}

from the first level posterior, and then obtaining samples from the second stage posterior by generating
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(b)}\sim p(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(b)})
.
In practice, this involves running a separate MCMC algorithm for each posterior sample
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(b)}
for a relatively short stretch of time. While doing so is more computationally intensive than running a two-step procedure, it is often as easy to implement using pre-existing software. Furthermore, we note that such a procedure can be naively parallelized since the samples
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(b)}
are independent given the samples from the first-stage posterior.

The previous discussion makes it clear that
p_{f}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})
,
p_{c}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})
and
p_{t}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})
can all be written as mixtures of posterior distribution for the second module,
p(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta})
, with respect to slightly different mixing distributions (the “coherent” posterior
p(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y})
, the incoherent first-level working posterior
\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y})
, and a point mass at a point estimator obtained from the first-level working posterior, respectively). Furthermore, because both
p_{t}
and
p_{c}
are based on the working posterior, we do not have to worry about conflicts between the modules as they relate to
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
. However, unlike
p_{t}
,
p_{c}
does allow us to propagate (some) uncertainty from the estimation of
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
to the second stage.

2.1
The cut posterior as an optimal approximation to the full posterior under constraints

Previously, we suggested that cut posteriors might be better than two-step approaches in terms of capturing the true uncertainty associated with the model parameters. In this section, we provide a formal justification for this statement. To do so, consider the class of distributions

\mathcal{F}=\{q(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}):\int q(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi})d\boldsymbol{\xi}=\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y})\},

i.e., the class of all probability distributions for
(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi})
that have the first-level working posterior as their marginal for
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
. The following theorem, which is adapted from
Yu, Nott and Smith 2023
, shows that
p_{c}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi})
is the best approximation to the full posterior
p_{f}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi})
with the class
\mathcal{F}
(in the Kullback–Leibler sense):

Lemma 1
.

For the cut posterior

1.

p_{c}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})=\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}\limits_{q\in\mathcal{F}}\text{KL}\left(q(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi})\mid\mid p_{f}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})\right)

2.

\text{KL}\left(p_{c}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})\mid\mid p_{f}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})\right)\\
\qquad=\text{KL}\left(\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y})\mid\mid p_{f}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y})\right)

The proof can be seen in Section 1 of the supplementary materials Because both
p_{t}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi})
and
p_{c}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi})
both belong to
\mathcal{F}
, this result suggests that, at least in this sense, cut posteriors are superior to two-step posteriors.

Relationship with other relevant literature

The graphical model in Figure

. Selective inference concerns itself with designing procedures with valid statistical properties when carried out after model / hyperparameter (e.g., LASSO penalty) selection. As this description suggests, the focus of selective inference procedures is on how uncertainty downstream the pipeline (on
\boldsymbol{\xi}
) affects inferences carried out upstream the pipeline (on
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
). Our focus in this paper is the opposite: we are interested in the effect that uncertainty upstream in the pipeline (on
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
) affects inferences downstream (on
\boldsymbol{\xi}
). Frequentist work on this version of the problem is, as far as we are aware, limited.

On the other hand, most applications of cut posteriors that we are aware of focus on models that can be described with a DAG similar to that presented in Figure
2
. A key difference is the presence of
\mathbf{Z}
, which corresponds to another set of response variables that provide information about
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. A second difference is the structure of the relationship between
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
. These differences is the structure of the underlying graph have implications on how the cut posterior is defined and justified, and they make our problem distinct from those that have been discussed in the literature previously.

Illustration 1: Matrix-variate Linear Models

We consider first a version of the setup in Section
2
in which
\mathbf{Y}
corresponds to an
N\times J
matrix of continuous responses and the first-level module corresponds to a matrix-variate linear regression

\displaystyle\mathbf{Y}=\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mathbf{W}+\boldsymbol{E},

where
\mathbf{W}
is a
L\times J
full-rank design matrix,
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is a
N\times L
matrix of unknown regression coefficients (
L\ll J
), and
\boldsymbol{E}
is an
N\times J
matrix of observational errors, which are assumed to follow a matrix-variate normal distribution,
\boldsymbol{E}\sim\mathsf{MVN}_{N,J}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{N},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{J})
(please see Section 2 of the supplementary materials). Note that we use
\mathbf{I}_{n}
to denote the
n\times n
identify matrix. For the second module, we consider again a matrix-variate linear regression,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\zeta}=\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\xi}+\boldsymbol{H},

where
\boldsymbol{X}
is an
N\times K
full-rank design matrix,
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is a
K\times L
matrix of regression coefficients (
K\ll N
), and
\boldsymbol{H}\sim\mathsf{MVN}_{N,L}\left(\mathbf{0},\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{N},\mathbf{I}_{L}\right)
.

In the sequel, we assume that both
\sigma^{2}
and
\tau^{2}
are known. This implies that there are no nuisance parameters
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}
or
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}
.
Furthermore, we consider the reference prior for
\boldsymbol{\xi}
,
p(\boldsymbol{\xi})\propto 1
, as well as for the working prior
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
,
\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\propto 1
. In this setting, the full, two-step and cut posteriors can be computed in closed form. We start by discussing the full posterior. Note that, in this setting, we can explicitly integrate
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
, resulting in

\mathbf{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi}\sim\mathsf{MVN}_{N,J}\left(\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{W},\mathbf{I}_{N},\boldsymbol{\Omega}\right)
, where
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{J}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{W}^{T}\mathbf{W}
. Hence, under the reference prior (see Section 2.1 of the supplementary materials),

\displaystyle p_{f}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y}\right)=\mathsf{MVN}_{K,L}\left(\mathbf{M}_{f},\mathbf{U}_{f},\mathbf{V}_{f}\right),

(8)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{f}

\displaystyle=[\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}]^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\mathbf{Y}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{T}\left[\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{T}\right]^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{f}

\displaystyle=[\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}]^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{f}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T}]^{-1}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{L}.

Consider now the two-step and cut posteriors. Under the working prior
p(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\propto 1
, the first-level working posterior is

\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y}\sim\mathsf{MVN}_{N,L}\left(\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{W}^{T}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T}]^{-1},\mathbf{I}_{N},\sigma^{2}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T}]^{-1}\right).

A similar calculation leads to

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta}\sim\mathsf{MVN}_{K,L}\left([\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}]^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\mathbf{Z},[\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}]^{-1},\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{L}\right)

(see Section 2.2 of the supplementary materials).

To construct the two-step posterior, a natural choice for a point estimator is the posterior mean,

\hat{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}(\mathbf{Y})=\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{W}^{T}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T}]^{-1},

which is optimal under squared error loss. This results in

\displaystyle p_{t}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})=\mathsf{MVN}_{K,L}\left(\mathbf{M}_{t},\mathbf{U}_{t},\mathbf{V}_{t}\right).

(9)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{t}

\displaystyle=[\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}]^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{W}^{T}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T}]^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{t}

\displaystyle=[\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}]^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{t}

\displaystyle=\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{L}.

In contrast, the cut posterior is obtained by integrating over the first-level working posterior (see Appendix
), resulting in

\displaystyle p_{c}\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y}\right)=\mathsf{MVN}_{K,L}\left(\mathbf{M}_{c},\mathbf{U}_{c},\mathbf{V}_{c}\right),

(10)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}_{c}

\displaystyle=[\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}]^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{W}^{T}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T}]^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}_{c}

\displaystyle=[\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}]^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{V}_{c}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T}]^{-1}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{L}.

The relative simplicity of these expressions allows us to understand the trade-offs associated with these various posterior distributions. First, note that the posterior mean for
\boldsymbol{\xi}
is the same under
p_{t}
and
p_{c}
, yielding (under squared error loss) the point estimator

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\mathbf{Y})=[\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}]^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{W}^{T}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T}]^{-1}.

This is an unbiased estimator of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. When the variances
\tau^{2}
and
\sigma^{2}
are correctly specified, this estimator has a higher variance than the estimator that arises from
p_{f}
,

\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\mathbf{Y})=[\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}]^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\mathbf{Y}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{T}\left[\mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}\mathbf{W}^{T}\right]^{-1}

(which is the best linear unbiased estimator in this setting). On the other hand, when the values of
\tau^{2}
and
\sigma^{2}
are misspecified (i.e., when the values used to fit the model are different from those in the underlying true data generation mechanism), both
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\mathbf{Y})
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\mathbf{Y})
remain unbiased but the fact that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\mathbf{Y})
is independent of
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
means that the point estimator is robutst to this kind of model misspecification. In contrast, the variance of
\hat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\mathbf{Y})
can potentially be very large (and, in particular, larger than that of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}(\mathbf{Y})
) in cases where the ratio
\sigma^{2}/\tau^{2}
used to fit the model is very different from the true ratio (see Sections 2.1 and 2.2 of the supplementary materials).
This contrasting behavior illustrates how the use of cut posteriors can provide robustness to some types of model misspecification, which might reside on any (or both) of the modules.

Next, note that the posterior variances for
p_{f}
and
p_{c}
are the same, and that they are larger than the variance of
p_{t}
, in the sense that

\displaystyle\tr\left(\left[\sigma^{2}[\mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^{T}]^{-1}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{L}\right]\otimes[\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}]^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad>\tr\left(\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}_{L}\otimes[\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}]^{-1}\right).

This shows how one might expect two-step posteriors to underestimate the uncertainty associated with the estimates of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
. Together, these two observations also suggest that cut posteriors offer the best trade offs between reasonably accurate uncertainty estimation, efficiency in point estimation, and robustness to certain types of model misspecification.

Finally, note that, when
\sigma^{2}/\tau^{2}\to 0
(i.e., when the observational noise in the second module dominates), all three posteriors are very close to each other and, in the limit, are identical to
p_{t}
. This suggests that there are situations in which it might not matter much which procedure is used, but that those situations might require very stringent conditions on the data generation mechanism that should be checked for any particular dataset.

Illustration 2: Hypothesis testing in hierarchical models with latent variables

The previous illustration is useful in providing some intuition about the behavior of the various types of posterior distributions we discussed in Section
2
. However, its relatively simplicity limits the insights that can be gained from it. In this Section, we consider a more elaborate example that is motivated by our own applied work but where closed-form expressions for the posteriors are not available. We start by describing the applied problem that motivates the model.

Recovering the preferences of members of deliberative bodies (such as legislatures or judicial panels) from their voting records is an essential task in various areas of application, but specially in political science. Accordingly, a broad and growing literature on scaling models has developed since the early 1980s (e.g.,
Poole and Rosenthal 1985
;
Poole 2005
;
Jackman 2001
;
Martin and
Quinn 2002
;
Clinton, Jackman and
Rivers 2004
;
Yu and Rodriguez 2021
;
Duck-Mayr and Montgomery 2022
;
Lei and Rodriguez 2023a
). While sometimes used for descriptive purposes (e.g., see
Jenkins 2006
,
Poole and Rosenthal 2006
,
Shor and McCarty 2011
and
Luque and Sosa 2022
), the estimates generated by these models are most often used as inputs for other models whose aim is to test specific substantive theories of member’s behavior (e.g., see
Schickler 2000
,
Schwindt-Bayer and
Corbetta 2004
and
Clark 2012
).

In this line of work, one recurring question is whether members’ preferences can be considered constant across across time or voting domains, and if not, what factors drive the divergent behavior (e.g., see
Martin and
Quinn 2002
,
Jessee and Theriault 2014
,
Lofland, Rodriguez and Moser 2017
,
Moser, Rodríguez and
Lofland 2021
,
Lei and Rodriguez 2023b
and
Lipman, Moser and
Rodriguez 2023
).
In this paper we focus on the problem of explaining differences in legislator behavior on procedural versus substantive votes. Scholars have long been interested in understanding what factors drive legislators to vote differently across domains. Generally speaking, legislators must balance the competing interests of their constituents (e.g., see
López and Jewell 2007
;
Fleisher and Bond 2004
;
Bond and
Fleisher 2002
), their political parties (e.g., see
Hix and
Noury 2016-05
), interest groups (e.g., see
Kau and Rubin 2013
), and their own beliefs. In the specific case of procedural vs. substantive votes, there is broad evidence that factors like party affiliation
(
Patty 2010
;
Carson, Crespin and
Madonna 2014
;
Jessee and Theriault 2014
)
, cross-pressure from constituents and party leaders
(
Kirkland and
Williams 2014
)
, as well as other forms of electoral considerations
(
Shin 2017
)
affect votes on each domain differently.

The data analysis problem at hand can be construed as requiring a two-module pipeline in which the first module is used to identify legislators whose preferences remain constant across domains (which we call “bridges”), and the second module is used to identify variables that might explain whether a particular legislator is a bridge.
Lipman, Moser and
Rodriguez 2023
developed a hierarchical formulation to address this question using what we refer to here as the full posterior. We proceed now to review that model, develop the corresponding two-step and cut posteriors, and explore the differences when all three approaches are applied to various datasets.

4.1
Notation

Let
i=1,\dots,N
index the set of legislators and
j=1,\dots,J
index the votes taken during that session. The response variable
\mathbf{Y}
is an
N\times J
binary matrix of roll call voting data, where
y_{i,j}=1
represents a “yea” vote for the
i
th
legislator on the
j
th
measure and
y_{i,j}=0
represents a “nay” vote. Furthermore, let
w_{j}\in\{0,1\}
be the a (known) indicator for whether the bill is a final passage vote (
w_{j}=1
) or a procedural vote (
w_{j}=0
).

4.2
The first module

The first module in our pipeline is a very slight variation of the hierarchical model introduced in
Lofland, Rodriguez and Moser 2017
. The likelihood for this model is given by

y_{i,j}\mid\mu_{j},\alpha_{j},\beta_{i,0},\beta_{i,1},w_{j}\\
\sim\mathsf{Ber}\left(y_{i,j}\,\bigg|\,\frac{\exp\left\{\mu_{j}+\alpha_{j}\beta_{i,w_{j}}\right\}}{1+\exp\left\{\mu_{j}+\alpha_{j}^{T}\beta_{i,w_{j}}\right\}}\right),

(11)

for
i=1,\ldots,N
and
j=1,\ldots,J
,
where the parameters
\beta_{i,0},\beta_{i,1}\in\mathbb{R}
are the
ideal points
for legislator
i
on procedural and final passage votes, respectively, and the parameters
\mu_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
are unknown parameters that can be understood as the baseline probability of an affirmative vote and the discrimination associated with measure
j
, respectively. The ideal points can in turn be interpreted as the preferred outcomes of legislators in the underlying latent policy space. Understanding the differences in the preferences across domains is at the heart of the our application.

The next level of the hierarchy is given by a joint prior on
(\beta_{i,0},\beta_{i,1})
that explicitly accounts for the possibility that
\beta_{i,0}=\beta_{i,1}
. Such a prior is defined conditionally on the vector of bridging indicators
\boldsymbol{\zeta}=(\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{N})
, with
\zeta_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, so that

(\beta_{i,0},\beta_{i,1})\mid\zeta_{i},\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta},\sigma^{2}_{\beta}\\
{\,\,\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}}\,\,\begin{cases}\mathsf{N}\left(\beta_{i,0}\mid\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta},\sigma^{2}_{\beta}\right)\,\delta_{\beta_{i,0}}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i,1}\right)&\zeta_{i}=1,\\
\mathsf{N}\left(\beta_{i,0}\mid\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta},\sigma^{2}_{\beta}\right)\mathsf{N}\left(\beta_{i,1}\mid\boldsymbol{\rho}_{\beta},\sigma^{2}_{\beta}\right)&\zeta_{i}=0.\\
\end{cases}

(12)

Note that
\zeta_{i}=1
corresponds to the hypothesis
\beta_{i,0}=\beta_{i,1}
, while
\zeta_{i}=0
corresponds to
\beta_{i,0}\neq\beta_{i,1}
.

The specification of the first module is completed by eliciting priors for the parameters
\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{J})
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{J})
and the hyperparameters
\rho_{\beta}
and
\sigma_{\beta}^{2}
(please see Appendix
A
for details on these). Referring back to the notation from Section
2
, we have
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1},\rho_{\beta},\sigma^{2}_{\beta})
, and

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta})

\displaystyle=

(13)

\displaystyle\int\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{J}p(y_{i,j}\mid\mu_{j},\alpha_{j},\beta_{i,0},\beta_{i,1},w_{j})\right\}

\displaystyle\times\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{N}p(\beta_{i,0},\beta_{i,1}\mid\zeta_{i},\rho_{\beta},\sigma^{2}_{\beta})\right\}

\displaystyle\times p(\boldsymbol{\mu})p(\boldsymbol{\alpha})p(\rho_{\beta})p(\sigma^{2}_{\beta})\,\text{d}\boldsymbol{\mu}\,\text{d}\boldsymbol{\alpha}\,\text{d}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0}\,\text{d}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{1}\,\text{d}\rho_{\beta}\,\text{d}\sigma^{2}_{\beta}.

4.3
The second module

The second module of the pipeline relates the vector of bridging indicators
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
to various covariates of interest through a logistic regression:

\displaystyle\zeta_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\eta_{0},\boldsymbol{\eta}\sim\mathsf{Ber}\left(\zeta_{i}\,\bigg|\,\frac{1}{1+\exp\left\{-\left(\eta_{0}+\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{T}\boldsymbol{\eta}\right)\right\}}\right),

(14)

where
\eta_{0}
is an intercept,
\boldsymbol{\eta}=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{K})^{T}
is a
K
-dimensional vector of unknown regression coefficients, and
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is the
i
-th row of the
N\times K
design matrix
\boldsymbol{X}
.

Similarly to the first module, the prior on
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is defined hierarchically. Since our ultimate goal is variable selection, let
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{K})
correspond to a vector of indicator variables such that
\xi_{k}=1
if and only if the
k
-th variable of interest is relevant to explaining whether legislators act as bridges. Then, conditional on
\boldsymbol{\xi}
,
the prior for
\boldsymbol{\eta}
takes the form

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\eta}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi})=N\left(\eta_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\,\Big|\,0,4N\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{T}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\right)^{-1}\right)\prod_{\{k:\xi_{k}=0\}}\delta_{0}(\eta_{k}),

where
\eta_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
denotes the subvector of
\boldsymbol{\eta}
that only includes the coefficients for which
\xi_{k}=1
, and
\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
denotes the matrix whose columns correspond to the variable for which
\xi_{k}=1
. This corresponds to an (approximately) unit information prior for
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}

(
Sabanés Bové and
Held 2011
)
. To complete the model, the intercept
\eta_{0}
is assigned a standard logistic prior (see also next Section). Then, in the notation of Section
2
,
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}=(\eta_{0},\boldsymbol{\eta})
, and the second module is given by

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi})=\int\prod_{i=1}^{N}p(\zeta_{i}\mid\eta_{0},\boldsymbol{\eta},\boldsymbol{x}_{i})p(\boldsymbol{\eta}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{X})p(\eta_{0})\text{d}\boldsymbol{\eta}\,\text{d}\eta_{0}.

(15)

4.4
Priors

In this Section, we discuss the prior on the key parameter of interest
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, as well as the working prior on
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
to be used in the cut and two-step posteriors. For
\boldsymbol{\xi}
, we employ a Beta-Bernoulli prior such that

\displaystyle p(\boldsymbol{\xi})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\cdot}+1\right)\Gamma\left(K-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\cdot}+1\right)}{\Gamma(K+2)}

(16)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\upsilon^{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\cdot}}(1-\upsilon)^{K-\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\cdot}}d\upsilon,

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
denotes the Gamma function and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\cdot}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\xi_{k}
is the number of predictors included in the model
(
Scott and Berger 2006
;
Scott and Berger 2010
)
. Similarly, for the working prior
\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})
, we also consider a Beta-Binomial distribution

p(\boldsymbol{\zeta})=\frac{\Gamma\left(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{\cdot}+1\right)\Gamma\left(N-\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{\cdot}+1\right)}{\Gamma(N+2)},

(17)

where, as before,
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{\cdot}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\zeta_{i}
. Note that, because we assigned
\eta_{0}
a logistic prior, we have

\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})=p(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi}=(0,0,\ldots,0)),

i.e., the working prior corresponds to the “correct” hierarchical prior when no covariates affect the bridging probability. In this case, Lemma
1
implies that

\displaystyle\text{KL}\left(p_{c}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})\mid\mid p_{f}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})\right)

(18)

\displaystyle=\text{KL}\left(\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y})\mid\mid p_{f}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y})\right)

\displaystyle=\text{KL}\left(p_{f}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y},\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{0}_{K})\mid\mid p_{f}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\mathbf{Y})\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\log\left(p_{f}(\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{0}_{K}\mid\mathbf{Y})\right),

where
0_{K}
is the zero vector in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
.

Hence,
\text{KL}\left(p_{c}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})\mid\mid p_{f}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})\right)\to 0
as
p(\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{0}_{K}\mid\mathbf{Y})\to 1
. This relationship implies a sort of consistency that, we believe, is highly desirable and should be kept in mind when selecting the working prior: if the full model strongly supports the exclusion of all covariates from the second module, then posterior inferences for the cut posterior will be (nearly) identical to those from obtained from the joint model.

4.5
Computation

Posterior inference for all three versions of the posterior distribution relies on MCMC algorithms to produce samples
\left(\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1)}\right),\ldots,\left(\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(B)},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(B)}\right)
from the posterior distributions. This Section provides a high-level overview of such algorithms that highlights the similarities and differences. An implementation of the algorithms is available at
https://github.com/e-lipman/ModularBayesUSHouse
.

4.5.1
Full posterior

Computation for the full posterior uses the MCMC sampler introduced in
Lipman, Moser and
Rodriguez 2023
. It is useful to view this algorithm as a two-block sampler that alternately samples the parameters from the first module and those from the second module from their respective full conditional distributions. In particular in iteration
b
, we first sample the parameters of the first module from

p\left(\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(b)},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}^{(b)}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(b-1)},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}^{(b-1)},\cdots\right)\\
\propto p\left(\mathbf{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}^{(b)},\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(b)},\right)p\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}^{(b)}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(b)}\right)\\
\times p\left(\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(b)}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(b-1)},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}^{(b-1)}\right)

(19)

and then update the module 2 parameters

p\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(b)},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}^{(b)}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(b)},\cdots\right)\\
\propto p\left(\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(b)}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(b)},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}^{(b)}\right)p\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}^{(b)}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(b)}\right)p\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(b)}\right)

(20)

Sampling from the conditional distribution in (
19
) is done using a series of Gibb’s steps that rely on the Polya-Gamma data augmentation approach of
Polson, Scott and
Windle 2013
. Sampling from the conditional distribution in (
20
) is achieved using a Metropolis-within-Gibbs sampler
that again relies on a Polya-Gamma augmentation.

4.5.2
Two-step posterior

Computation for the two-step posterior relies on slight variations of the same two blockwise samplers described above. First we generate samples
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(2)},\ldots
from the first-level working posterior, which in our case reduces to sampling from

\bar{p}\left(\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(b)},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}^{(b)}\mid\cdots\right)\\
\propto p\left(\mathbf{Y}\mid\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}^{(b)},\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(b)},\right)p\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}^{(b)}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(b)}\right)\bar{p}\left(\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(b)}\right)

(21)

The MCMC sampler for (
21
) is extremely similar (but not identical) to that for (
19
) (e.g., see
Lofland, Rodriguez and Moser 2017
). We then use a simple zero-one loss function

L(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\prime})=\\
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{a_{1}I(\zeta_{i}=1,\zeta_{i}^{\prime}=0)+a_{2}I(\zeta_{i}=0,\zeta_{i}^{\prime}=1)\right\}

to generate our point estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
, which results in
\hat{\zeta}_{i}=I\left\{\bar{p}\left(\zeta_{i}=1\mid\mathbf{Y}\right)>\frac{a_{2}}{a_{1}+a_{2}}\right\}
. As we mentioned earlier, a common choice is
a_{1}=a_{2}
, leading to ta threshold of
0.5
for the posterior probability. Finally, samples from
p_{t}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mid\mathbf{Y})
can be obtained using the same algorithm to sample from (
20
) by simply fixing
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(b)}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}
for all
b
.

4.5.3
Cut posterior

Computation for the cut posterior relies on exactly the same algorithms used for the two-step posterior, but their output is used is a slightly different way. As for the two-step posterior, our first step is to generate samples
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(2)},\ldots
from (
21
). Then, for each of these samples, we run an independent MCMC algorithm to generate pairs

\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(b,1)},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{2}^{(b,1)}\right),\ldots\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(b,S)},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{2}^{(b,S)}\right)

from

p\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(b,s)},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}^{(b,s)}\mid\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(b)},\cdots\right)\propto\\
p\left(\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{(b)}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(b,s)},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}^{(b,s)}\right)p\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}^{(b,s)}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi}_{2}^{(b,s)}\right)p\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(b,s)}\right)

(recall Equation (
20
)). For a sufficiently large value of
S
, we then set
\left(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(b)},\boldsymbol{\phi}_{2}^{(b)}\right)=\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{(b,S)},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{2}^{(b,S)}\right)
.

Empirical results

We evaluate the three posterior distribution on the the same data presented in
Lipman, Moser and

5
Discussion

While data analysis pipelines are widely used in practice, there are substantial challenges with their use that are often underappreciated. From a Bayesian perspective, it is tempting to argue that integrating the various modules of the pipeline into a single hierarchical model to be jointly fitted to the data is the solution to most of them. However, implementing such big hierarchical models might involve substantial computational challenges, both in terms of algorithm development and of execution time. More importantly, as our two illustrations show, hierarchical Bayesian models are particularly susceptible to model misspecification. The results from our second illustration provide a particularly sharp warning: the full posterior seems to perform quite well for most datasets, but appears to fail dramatically for the 109
th
House, and perhaps for the 100
th
and 105
th
Houses as well. The best solution to this problem, investigating the sources of misspecification and modifying the model accordingly, can be laborious and, in some cases, unfeasible. Our illustrations show that cut inference provides an alternative to full hierarchical inference that can be more robust to model misspecification, while being better at reflecting the true level of uncertainty in the estimates than more traditional multi-step approaches to statistical inference.

Most of the literature on cut inference has focused on models where there are two separate sets of response variables that are assumed to be conditionally independent given a set of shared parameters. As far as we are aware, this paper is the first one to explore cut inference in sequential settings such as those associated with data analysis pipelines. However, much work remains to be done. The result in Theorem
1
is useful in terms of establishing the optimality of cut inference among procedures that share a given marginal distribution for
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
(including multi-step procedures), and the ideas underlying the derivation of Equation (
18
) can be extended to produce bounds for the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the cut and full posteriors in settings where the working prior
\bar{p}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})
is carefully chosen to be compatible with the original
p(\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mid\boldsymbol{\xi})
. However, it would be useful to have tools that would allow us to accurately quantify the impact of cut inference on parameter uncertainty. Exploring potential connections between cut inference and (constrained) variational inference (e.g., see
Blei, Kucukelbir and
McAuliffe 2017
) might provide a path forward in this.
