Posterior contraction in Gaussian process regression using Wasserstein approximations
Anirban Bhattacharya

Abstract




Abstract

We study posterior rates of contraction in Gaussian process regression with unbounded covariate domain. Our argument relies on developing a Gaussian approximation to the posterior of the leading coefficients of a Karhunen–Loève expansion of the Gaussian process. The salient feature of our result is deriving such an approximation in the
L^{2}
Wasserstein distance and relating the speed of the approximation to the posterior contraction rate using a coupling argument. Specific illustrations are provided for the Gaussian or squared-exponential covariance kernel.

Keywords
: kernel regression; Gaussian process; Hermite polynomials; posterior contraction; random design; Wasserstein distance

1
Introduction

Gaussian process (GP) priors
[
23
]
are popularly used in a variety of machine learning applications including regression, classification, density estimation, latent variable modeling, unsupervised learning to name a few. GP priors also share a deep connection with frequentist reproducible kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) based regularization methods; see, for example Chapter 6 of
[
23
]
. Paralleling the development of scalable algorithms for GP regression, there has been substantial progress in recent years in understanding frequentist properties of the posterior arising from a Gaussian process prior. A standard way of evaluating frequentist properties of Bayesian procedures is to consider whether the amount of posterior mass assigned to a neighborhood of the true data-generating parameter (a function in the present setting) converges to one with increasing sample size. If the neighborhood size is fixed, the above phenomenon is termed
posterior consistency
, while if the neighborhood size is allowed to shrink to zero, then the (best possible) shrinking rate is termed the
posterior contraction rate
.
[
16
,
10
]
established posterior consistency of GP priors, while posterior contraction rates in a variety of contexts were derived in
[
28
,
29
,
31
,
32
,
5
,
22
]
among others; see also
[
24
]
for an information-theoretic approach. In particular, it has been established in various contexts that the posterior distribution contracts at an optimal rate (up to a logarithmic term) in a frequentist minimax sense.

The above references exclusively deal with compactly supported functions as parameters, even though the priors in principle are random functions on full Euclidean spaces. In fact, the influential article
[
31
]
remarks that

“ Consistency of a posterior on the full space can be expected only if the tails of the functions are restricted. If they are not, then one would still expect that the posterior restricted to compact subsets contracts at some rate. At the moment there seem to exist no results that would yield such a rate (or even consistency)”
.

In this article, we take a step towards addressing this question borrowing inspiration from the kernel regression literature
[
13
,
25
]
, where a
L^{2}
norm weighted by a possibly unbounded covariate density is commonly used as a measure of discrepancy. We focus on the nonparametric regression model with Gaussian errors

\displaystyle Y_{i}=f(X_{i})+\epsilon_{i},\quad\epsilon_{i}\sim{\mathrm{N}}(0,\sigma^{2}),\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

(1)

where
X_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
are covariates and
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
is an unknown regression function with possibly unbounded domain
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is assigned a zero-mean GP prior. We operate in a random design setting where the covariates are drawn according to a distribution
\rho
on
\mathcal{X}
and study contraction of the posterior in an
L^{2}(\rho)
norm, i.e., the
L^{2}
norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
weighted with respect to the covariate density
\rho
. This choice ensures that the large covariate values are weighted down, which can be considered as a way of restricting the tails of the function as in the comment by
[
31
]
above.

In deriving the posterior rate of contraction, we expand the GP prior via a Karhunen–Loève expansion
[
2
]
and then derive a Gaussian approximation to the posterior distribution of the leading coefficients of the expansion. The Gaussian approximation is derived in an
L^{2}
Wasserstein metric which is particularly suited for the present situation for reasons described in the sequel. Using a careful coupling argument, the speed of such Gaussian approximations are related to the posterior contraction rate; a result which is new to best of our knowledge. Another key ingredient of our method is to control the effect of the truncating the Karhunen–Loève expansion in the posterior. This typically requires bounds on the concentration of the prior around the true function in the sup-norm, which is difficult to control for unbounded covariates. A second contribution of this paper is to develop a general result (Theorem
3.4
)
to bound (with high probability) the integrated log-likelihood ratio from below by a quantity involving prior concentration around the true function in the
L^{2}_{\rho}
norm instead of the sup-norm. We believe this result may be of independent interest in random design Gaussian regression. We may comment here that in addition to dealing with unbounded covariates, the proposed technique has an added advantage of making the bias-variance tradeoff in the posterior explicit as in kernel ridge regression theory.

While we make general assumptions on the covariance kernel to prove our results, verifying them in a specific context requires suitable control over the eigenfunctions of the kernel. This can potentially be a non-trivial exercise, in particular if the covariance kernel involves a parameter which is sample-size dependent. We illustrate this in case of a squared-exponential kernel, for which explicit expressions of the eigenfunctions are available
[
23
]
. We develop precise bounds on the eigenfunctions making the role of a scale parameter explicit, which should be more broadly useful.

For a square matrix
B
,

.

is psd.
For a

. We shall use

For a vector

,

will denote its Euclidean norm. Let
\ell_{2}=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots):\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\theta_{j}^{2}<\infty\}

\Theta_{\alpha}=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\ell_{2}:\sum_{j=1}j^{2\alpha}\theta_{j}^{2}<\infty\}

is a Hilbert space under the inner product
\left\langle g_{1},g_{2}\right\rangle=\int g_{1}(x)g_{2}(x)\rho(x)dx

Throughout

2.1
The
L^{p}
Wasserstein distances

Given two probability measures
P
and
Q
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the total variation distance
d_{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle TV}}}(P,Q):=\sup_{A}|P(A)-Q(A)|
where the supremum is over all Borel subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the Kullback–Leibler divergence
D(P||Q)
are defined in the usual way.
For
p\geq 1
, the
L^{p}
Wasserstein distance with respect to the Euclidean metric (henceforth
W_{p}
in short), denoted
d_{W,p}(P,Q)
, is defined as

\displaystyle d_{W,p}(P,Q)=\inf_{\mbox{joint}(P,Q)}(\mathbb{E}\big\|X-Y\big\|^{p})^{1/p},

(2)

where
\mbox{joint}(P,Q)
denotes all random vectors
(X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that
X\sim P,Y\sim Q
. The Wasserstein distances have their origins in the problem of
optimal transport
; refer to
[
17
,
14
]
for background and properties. Explicit expressions are available for the
W_{2}
distance between two
d
-dimensional Gaussian measures. In particular, if
P\equiv{\mathrm{N}}_{d}(\mu_{1},\Sigma_{1}),Q\equiv{\mathrm{N}}_{d}(\mu_{2},\Sigma_{2})
and
\Sigma_{1}\Sigma_{2}=\Sigma_{2}\Sigma_{1}
, then

\displaystyle d_{W,2}^{2}(P,Q)=\big\|\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}\big\|^{2}+\big\|\Sigma_{1}^{1/2}-\Sigma_{2}^{1/2}\big\|_{F}^{2}.

(3)

For
d=1
, the
W_{2}
distance is identical to the Fréchet distance
[
12
]
.

3
Posterior contraction in random design GP regression

Write the nonparametric regression model (
1
) in vector form as

\displaystyle Y=F+\varepsilon,\quad\varepsilon\sim{\mathrm{N}}(0,\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{n}),

(4)

where
Y=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}
and
F=(f(X_{1}),\ldots,f(X_{n}))^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}
. We shall assume the error variance
\sigma^{2}
to be known throughout this paper. Let
f_{0}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the true data generating function and define
F_{0}=(f_{0}(X_{1}),\ldots,f_{0}(X_{n}))^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}
.

As mentioned in the Introduction, we operate in a random design setting where we assume that the covariates
X_{i}
are independent and identically distributed according to a known density
\rho
on
\mathcal{X}
and
Y_{i}\mid X_{i}\sim{\mathrm{N}}(f_{0}(X_{i}),\sigma^{2})
independently for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Letting
h(y,x)={\mathrm{N}}(y\mid f_{0}(x),\sigma^{2})\rho(x)
, the true joint density of
(Y,X)
is an
n
-fold product of
h
. We shall use
\mathbb{E}_{0}
to denote an expectation with respect to the true joint distribution of
(Y,X)
;
\mathbb{E}_{X}
and
\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}
will respectively denote an expectation with respect to the marginal distribution of
X
and the conditional of
Y
given
X
. Similarly,
\mathbb{P}_{0},\mathbb{P}_{X}
and
\mathbb{P}_{0\mid X}
will denote probabilities under the respective distributions.

Consider a
\mbox{GP}(0,\sigma^{2}K)
prior on
f
, where
K(\cdot,\cdot)
is a positive definite correlation function, i.e.,
K(x,x)=1
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. We shall generically use
\Pi
and
\Pi(\cdot\mid Y,X)
to denote the prior and posterior distribution of
f
.
Under suitable regularity conditions, Mercer’s theorem
[
2
]
guarantees that the kernel
K
admits an eigen-expansion of the form
K(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}\phi_{j}(x)\phi_{j}(x^{\prime})
in
L^{2}_{\rho}(\mathcal{X})
, where
\{\phi_{j}\}
is an orthonormal system in
L^{2}_{\rho}(\mathcal{X})
(
\int\phi_{j}(x)\phi_{l}(x)\rho(x)dx=\delta_{jl}
) and
\{\lambda_{j}\}
the corresponding non-negative eigenvalues, which satisfy

\displaystyle\int K(x,x^{\prime})\phi_{j}(x^{\prime})\rho(x^{\prime})dx^{\prime}=\lambda_{j}\phi_{j}(x),\quad j=1,2,\ldots

(5)

As a concrete example, consider the squared-exponential kernel
K_{a}(x,x^{\prime})=\exp(-a^{2}\big\|x-x^{\prime}\big\|^{2})
indexed by a length-scale parameter
a
. For Gaussian covariate distributions
\rho
, explicit expressions for the eigenfunctions and eigenvalues are known
[
23
]
. Specifically, when the dimension
d=1
, with a Gaussian covariate density

\rho(x)=\sqrt{2b/\pi}\,e^{-2bx^{2}}
and
c=\sqrt{b^{2}+2ba^{2}}
,

\displaystyle\phi_{j}(x)=\frac{(c/b)^{1/4}}{\sqrt{2^{j-1}\,(j-1)!}}\,e^{-(c-b)x^{2}}H_{j-1}(\sqrt{2c}\,x),\hskip 9.24994pt\lambda_{j}=\bigg(\frac{2b}{b+a^{2}+c}\bigg)^{1/2}\,\bigg(\frac{a^{2}}{b+a^{2}+c}\bigg)^{j-1},

(6)

where
H_{k}(x)=(-1)^{n}e^{x^{2}}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}e^{-x^{2}},k=0,1,\ldots
denote the Hermite polynomials
1
1

1

Many references term
H_{k}
s the “physicist’s Hermite polynomial” to distinguish from the “probabilist’s Hermite polynomial”
h_{k}(x)=2^{-k/2}H_{k}(x/\sqrt{2})
. We shall return to the squared-exponential kernel in Section 4.

By the Karhunen–Loève Theorem
[
2
]
, the GP itself can be expanded as

\displaystyle f(x)=\sigma\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\sqrt{\lambda_{j}}\ Z_{j}\phi_{j}(x),

(7)

where
Z_{j}
s are i.i.d.
{\mathrm{N}}(0,1)
. If the series representation above is truncated to the first
k
terms and the resulting random function is denoted by
f^{t}
, then it follows from (
5
) and the orthogonality of the eigenfunctions
\phi_{j}
that
E\big\|f-f^{t}\big\|_{2,\rho}^{2}=\sigma^{2}\sum_{j=k+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}
. The accuracy of the truncation relies on the rate of decay of the eigenvalues, which is related to the smoothness of the GP. For example, if the sample paths of a GP are infinite smooth, then the eigenvalues decay exponentially fast, so that relatively few leading terms in the expansion (
7
) offer a close reconstruction of the original process.

Given a
\mbox{GP}(0,\sigma^{2}K)
prior, we shall consider such truncations of (
7
) to define priors which we refer to as truncated Gaussian process (tGP) priors:

\displaystyle f^{t}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\theta_{j}\phi_{j}(x),\quad\theta_{j}\sim{\mathrm{N}}(0,\sigma^{2}\lambda_{j}).

(8)

Let
\theta^{t}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k_{n}})^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}
denote the
k_{n}
-dimensional vector of coefficients in (
8
) and
\Lambda=\mbox{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k_{n}})
, so that
\theta^{t}\sim{\mathrm{N}}(0,\sigma^{2}\Lambda)
.
One may consider the tGP priors (
8
) as
sieve
approximations to the original GP prior, where the basis functions
\phi_{j}
s and the prior variances
\lambda_{j}
s are determined by the choice of the kernel
K
. We denote such priors by
\mbox{tGP}_{k_{n}}(0,K)
; the truncation level
k_{n}
will be suppressed when clear from the context.

We note here that the tGP prior is solely introduced to obtain theoretical understanding of the original GP prior and the resulting posterior. When working with a tGP prior, one can conveniently direct attention to the coefficient vector
\theta^{t}
, which is finite-dimensional; albeit with the dimension possibly increasing with sample size
n
. In fact, defining the
n\times k_{n}
(random) matrix
\Phi=(\phi_{j}(X_{i}))_{1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq k_{n}}
, one can write model (
4
) equipped with a tGP prior (
8
) as

\displaystyle Y\sim{\mathrm{N}}(F,\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{n}),\quad F=\Phi\theta^{t},\quad\theta^{t}\sim{\mathrm{N}}_{k_{n}}(0,\sigma^{2}\Lambda).

(9)

Using standard Gaussian conjugacy, the posterior distribution of
\theta^{t}
under (
9
) is

\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}^{t}(\cdot\mid X,Y)\equiv{\mathrm{N}}\big({\widetilde{\theta}},{\widetilde{\Sigma}}\big),\quad{\widetilde{\theta}}=(\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi+\Lambda^{-1})^{-1}\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}Y,\quad{\widetilde{\Sigma}}=\sigma^{2}(\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi+\Lambda^{-1})^{-1}.

(10)

From (
10
), the posterior distribution of
F=\Phi\theta^{t}
is also Gaussian.
With a slight abuse of terminology, we shall refer to the posterior distribution (
10
) of
\theta^{t}
as the
tGP posterior
induced by the tGP prior. The role of the tGP in deriving posterior rates of contraction for the original GP prior is made precise through the following general rate theorem for GP priors.
We first state our assumption regarding the true data generating function
f_{0}
and introduce some notations.

(T1)

The true data generating function
f_{0}\in L^{2}_{\rho}(\mathcal{X})
, so that
f_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\theta_{0j}\phi_{j}
with
\theta_{0j}=\left\langle f_{0},\phi_{j}\right\rangle:=\int f_{0}(x)\phi_{j}(x)\rho(x)dx
. The convergence of the infinite sum is in an
L^{2}_{\rho}
sense, i.e.,
\big\|f_{0}-\sum_{j=1}^{J}\theta_{0j}\phi_{j}\big\|_{2,\rho}\to 0
as
J\to\infty
.

Define
f_{0}^{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\theta_{0j}\phi_{j}
,
\theta_{0}^{t}=(\theta_{0j})_{1\leq j\leq k_{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{n}}
. Also define
\big\|\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\frac{\theta_{0j}^{2}}{\lambda_{j}}=\big\|\Lambda^{-1/2}\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|^{2}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Consider model (
1
) with a GP prior
f\sim\mbox{GP}(0,\sigma^{2}K)
, where the kernel
K
has eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{j}\}
and eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{j}\}
with respect to the covariate density
\rho
as in (
5
). Assume the true function
f_{0}
satisfies
\mathrm{{\bf(T1)}}
. For
k_{n}<n
, let
{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}^{t}(\cdot\mid X,Y)
denote the tGP posterior as in (
10
).
Let
\epsilon_{n}\to 0
be a sequence with
n\epsilon_{n}^{2}\to\infty
and
\big\|f_{0}-f_{0}^{t}\big\|_{2,\rho}\lesssim\epsilon_{n}
.
Then, for any
M>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{0}\Pi(\big\|f-f_{0}\big\|_{2,\rho}>M\epsilon_{n}\mid Y,X)\leq T_{1n}+T_{2n},

(11)

where

\displaystyle T_{1n}=\frac{\mathbb{E}_{0}\left\{\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X){\mbox{d}}_{W,2}^{2}\bigg[{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}^{t}(\cdot\mid Y,X),{\mathrm{N}}_{k_{n}}\bigg(\theta_{0}^{t},\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\mathrm{I}_{k_{n}}\bigg)\bigg]\right\}}{M^{2}\epsilon_{n}^{2}/4}+P(\chi_{k_{n}}^{2}>M^{2}n\epsilon_{n}^{2}/4)+\mathbb{P}_{X}(A_{n}^{c}),

(12)

\displaystyle T_{2n}=\mathbb{E}_{0}\Pi(\big\|f-f^{t}\big\|_{2,\rho}>M\epsilon_{n}\mid Y,X).

(13)

In (
12
),
\chi_{r}^{2}
denotes a
\chi^{2}
random variable with
r
degrees of freedom and
A_{n}\subset\mathcal{X}^{n}
is any set in the
\sigma
-field generated by
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.

It immediately follows from (
11
) that for a given
\epsilon_{n}
, if the sequences
T_{1n},T_{2n}\to 0
, then
\epsilon_{n}
is an upper bound to the posterior contraction rate
[
15
]
in the
L^{2}_{\rho}
norm; note that no assumptions regarding the support of the covariate density
\rho
is made. Theorem
3.1
thus relates the posterior contraction rate of a GP prior to (i) the speed of a posterior Wasserstein approximation of the induced tGP prior (
T_{1n}
), and (ii) the associated truncation error (
T_{2n}
). To obtain the best possible rate out of Theorem
3.1
, one needs to choose the truncation level
k_{n}
(and to a lesser extent the set
A_{n}
) in an optimal fashion.
The role of these quantities will become more explicit once we provide manageable bounds to
T_{1n}
and
T_{2n}
in the subsequent sections. To that end, we need to make additional assumptions on the eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{j}\}
and eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{j}\}
of the kernel
K
stated below.
Recall
\Lambda=\mbox{diag}(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k_{n}})
and
\Phi=(\phi_{j}(X_{i}))_{1\leq i\leq n,1\leq j\leq k_{n}}
. Assume

(A1)

\big\|\Lambda^{-1}\big\|_{2}<n/4
.

(A2)

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}|\phi_{j}(x)|\leq L_{n}
for all
j=1,\ldots,k_{n}
, with
L_{n}^{2}k_{n}\log k_{n}<n
.

Assumption
(A1)
typically implies a bound on the growth rate of
k_{n}
; for example, if the eigenvalues decay polynomially,
\lambda_{j}\asymp j^{-2\beta}
for some
\beta>0
, then
\big\|\Lambda^{-1}\big\|_{2}=\lambda_{k_{n}}^{-1}\asymp k_{n}^{2\beta}
and hence
(A1)
is satisfied for all
k_{n}\precsim n^{1/(2\beta+1)}
. Assumption
(A2)
is readily satisfied if all the eigenfunctions
\phi_{j}
are uniformly bounded in magnitude by a constant. However,
(A2)
is more general and allows the sup-norm of the top
k_{n}
eigenfunctions to increase with
n
subject to a growth condition; note that no assumption is made regarding the trailing eigenfunctions. Allowing the sup-norm bound to grow with
n
is important when the kernel is indexed by one or more hyper parameters which may depend on
n
. A specific illustration is provided in the context of the squared-exponential covariance kernel (
6
) in Section 4. It turns out a non-trivial exercise to bound the eigenfunctions (
6
) making the dependence on the bandwidth parameter
a
explicit.

3.1
Wasserstein approximations to tGP posteriors

To bound
T_{1n}
, one primarily needs a handle on the squared
W_{2}
distance between the tGP posterior
{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}^{t}(\cdot\mid Y,X)
in (
10
) and a Gaussian
{\mathrm{N}}_{k_{n}}\big(\theta_{0}^{t},\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{k_{n}}/n\big)
distribution. Inspecting the proof of Theorem
3.1
, it may seem a more obvious choice for
T_{1n}
is

\displaystyle T_{1n}^{*}=\mathbb{E}_{0}d_{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle TV}}}\bigg[{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}^{t}(\cdot\mid Y,X),{\mathrm{N}}_{k_{n}}\bigg(\theta_{0}^{t},\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\mathrm{I}_{k_{n}}\bigg)\bigg]+P(\chi_{k_{n}}^{2}>M^{2}n\epsilon_{n}^{2}/4),

where
d_{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle TV}}}
denotes the total variation distance. However, such approximations in the total variation distance require a
prior flatness
condition
[
7
]
which is not satisfied by the tGP priors. We find that bounding the
W_{2}
distance between the tGP posterior and the asymptotic Gaussian distribution is less demanding in the present setting compared to the total variation distance. However, the connection between such an approximation result in the
W_{2}
distance and posterior contraction rates in the
L^{2}_{\rho}
norm is not immediately clear. We devise a coupling argument to relate the two quantities in the proof of Theorem
3.1
.

The
\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X)
term in
T_{1n}
is introduced as a technical device to control the expectation of the squared Wasserstein distance on
A_{n}
; we appropriately choose
A_{n}
in a way so that
A_{n}^{c}
receives vanishingly small probability under
\mathbb{P}_{X}
. Before proceeding further, we settle with a choice of
A_{n}
in the following Lemma
3.2
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume the eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{j}\}
of the kernel
K
with respect to the covariate density
\rho
satisfy
(A2)
. Define
A_{n}=\{\big\|\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi-n\mathrm{I}_{k_{n}}\big\|_{2}<n/2\}
. Then,
\mathbb{P}_{X}(A_{n}^{c})<k_{n}e^{-Cn/(k_{n}L_{n}^{2})}
.

Remark 3.1
.

On the set
A_{n}
,
\Phi
satisfies

\displaystyle\big\|\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi\big\|_{2}\leq 3n/2,\quad s_{\min}(\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi)\geq n/2,\quad\kappa(\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi)\leq 3,\quad{\rm tr\,}[(\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi)^{-1}]\leq\frac{2k_{n}}{n}.

(14)

Lemma
3.2
follows from a measure concentration phenomenon which under appropriate conditions on the summands ensures that a sum of independent symmetric random matrices is concentrated around its expectation with high probability. We can write
\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi^{(i)}(\phi^{(i)})^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}
with
\phi^{(i)}=(\phi_{j}(X_{i}))_{1\leq j\leq k_{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{n}}
independent for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Using the orthonormality of the eigenfunctions
\{\phi_{j}\}
,
(\mathbb{E}_{X}\phi^{(i)}(\phi^{(i)})^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}})_{jl}=\int\phi_{j}(x)\phi_{l}(x)\rho(x)dx=\delta_{jl}

3.2
Handling the truncation error

We now focus attention on the term

in (

[
15
]
and lower
[
9
]
bounds to the posterior convergence rate. However, a crucial ingredient for the above argument to work is to obtain suitable lower bounds to the log-likelihood ratio integrated with respect to the prior. The only such result that we are aware of in the random design setting is from
[

norm and then use a functional Bernstein inequality to extrapolate to the

norm. Their result requires the prior draws from the GP to be bounded with probability one, which may not be the case for non-compact covariates. In Theorem

to bound (with high probability) the integrated log-likelihood ratio from below by a quantity involving the prior concentration around the true function in the

4
Application to the squared-exponential kernel

As a non-trivial application of the general results in the previous section, we consider Gaussian process regression with a squared-exponential kernel
K_{a}(x,x^{\prime})=\exp(-a^{2}\big\|x-x^{\prime}\big\|^{2})
; a popular choice in machine learning applications. It is well-known that the realizations of a GP with squared-exponential kernel are infinitely smooth and hence are not suitable to model rougher functions. It has only been recently understood
[
28
]
that the parameter
a
plays the role of an “inverse-bandwidth”, and scaling the parameter
a
with the sample size enables better approximation of rougher functions.
[
28
]
motivates this from a rescaling perspective; choosing a large value of
a
is equivalent to tracing the trajectory of a smooth process (with
a=1
) over a larger domain, incurring more roughness. In the regression context (
1
),
[
28
]
derived optimal posterior convergence rates in the empirical
L^{2}
norm using a rescaling
a\equiv a_{n}=n^{1/(2\alpha+1)}
where the true function is
\alpha
-smooth on a compact domain in
\mathbb{R}
. Using a gamma prior on
a
,
[
31
]
extended their result showing that the rate of contraction is adaptive over any
\alpha
-smooth compactly supported function. In a more recent article,
[
22
]
extended the results in
[
28
]
for integrated
L^{1}
norm. All these articles make exclusive use of the reproducing kernel Hilbert space theory from
[
30
]
and bounds on sup-norm small-ball probabilities of Gaussian processes over compact domain
[
20
,
19
,
21
]
.

Figure 1:
The top
50
eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
of the squared-exponential kernel in (
6
) plotted against the index

for
4

. Left panel: the index

The eigen-expansion of the squared-exponential kernel offers a complementary perspective into the rescaling phenomenon. Consider the expression for the eigenvalues of the squared-exponential kernel in (
6
). It is well known that the rate of decay of the eigenvalues is closely connected to the smoothness of the process (
7
). When
a=1
, the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
decay exponentially fast in
j
, indicating the infinite smoothness of the sample paths. Although the rate of decay remains exponential in
j
for any fixed value of
a
, it is effectively slowed down for large values of

; see Figure
1
for an illustration.

In this section, we apply the results developed in Section 4 (specifically Corollary
3.5
) to derive posterior rates of contraction for the above rescaled GP priors with the covariates drawn i.i.d. from a Gaussian density on the real line. To best of our knowledge, no existing posterior contraction rate result for the squared-exponential (or other) kernel allows unbounded covariate support.
Using a tensor-product basis approach, it is possible to extend our results to covariates in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

4.1
Posterior contraction rates

For the remainder of this Section,
\{\phi_{j}\}
and
\{\lambda_{j}\}
denote the eigenfunctions and eigenvalues (
6
) of the squared-exponential kernel with inverse-bandwidth parameter
a
; the dependence on
a
is suppressed for notational convenience. In order to apply Theorem

to the squared-exponential kernel, we need sup-norm bounds on the

leading eigenfunctions

s. Since we are concerned with rescaled processes where the parameter

is sample-size dependent, it is important to precisely characterize the role of

A well-known inequality for the Hermite polynomial is Cramer’s bound
[
26
]
, which states that for any
l\geq 1
,
|H_{l}(z)|\leq C\sqrt{2^{l}\,l!}\,e^{z^{2}/2}
for all

, where
C\leq 2
is a global constant which doesn’t depend on
z
or
l
. A direct use of this bound leads to
|\phi_{j+1}(x)|\lesssim(c/b)^{1/4}e^{bx^{2}}
, which is clearly not sufficient as we are dealing with unbounded covariates. Since the Hermite functions are polynomials, the exponential bound provided by Cramer’s inequality is wasteful in the tails. We derive a bound for the leading eigenfunctions
\phi_{j}
s in Lemma
4.1
below; refer to the Appendix for a proof. We did not find an existing reference proving this result. The main idea is to use Cramer’s bound in a neighborhood of the origin, while for suitably large values of
x
, use a combination of Cramer’s bound with a different bound obtained by exploiting an integral representation of the Hermite polynomials.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\phi_{l}
s be the eigenfunctions of the squared-exponential kernel as in (
6
). Then,

\max_{0\leq j\leq k}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|\phi_{j+1}(x)|\lesssim a^{1/4}e^{bk/a}
for large
a
.

We are now in a position to state the rate theorem.
Set
a_{n}=n^{1/(2\alpha+1)}
in (
6
). We define the true class of functions
\mathcal{F}
with “smoothness

\phi_{j}
with the coefficient vector in the Sobolev class

. Formally,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\{f_{0}:f_{0}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\theta_{0j}\phi_{j},\,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}=(\theta_{01},\theta_{02},\ldots)\in\Theta_{\alpha}\}.

(20)

Theorem 4.2
.

Consider the nonparametric regression model (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{0}\big[\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X)d_{W,2}^{2}(Q_{T},Q_{A})\big]=\mathbb{E}_{X}\big[\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X)\,\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}d_{W,2}^{2}(Q_{T},Q_{A})\big].

(26)

Since
{\widetilde{\Sigma}}
and
\sigma^{2}\mathrm{I}_{k_{n}}/n
(trivially) commute, apply (
3
) to write

\displaystyle d_{W,2}^{2}(Q_{T},Q_{A})=\big\|{\widetilde{\theta}}-\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|^{2}+\big\|{\widetilde{\Sigma}}^{1/2}-\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{I}_{k_{n}}\big\|_{F}^{2}.

(27)

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}d_{W,2}^{2}(Q_{T},Q_{A})=\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}\big\|{\widetilde{\theta}}-\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|^{2}+\big\|{\widetilde{\Sigma}}^{1/2}-\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{I}_{k_{n}}\big\|_{F}^{2},

(28)

since the second term does not involve
Y
. We now proceed to bound each of these two terms in (

) on the set
A_{n}
. To that end, we shall apply Lemma
3.2
and in particular, the consequences of Lemma
3.2
summarized in Remark

multiple times below. We also make use of Lemma

on multiple occasions.

Recall
{\widetilde{\theta}}=(\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi+\Lambda^{-1})^{-1}
and define
\theta_{Y}=(\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}Y
. Using
\big\|a+b\big\|^{2}\leq 2(\big\|a\big\|^{2}+\big\|b\big\|^{2})
, bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}({\widetilde{\theta}}-\theta_{0}^{t})^{2}\lesssim\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}\big\|{\widetilde{\theta}}-\theta_{Y}\big\|^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}\big\|\theta_{Y}-\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|^{2}.

(29)

Let us first deal with
\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}\big\|\theta_{Y}-\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|^{2}
. Let
F_{0}=(f_{0}(X_{1}),\ldots,f_{0}(X_{n}))^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}
, so that
F_{0}=\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}Y

(T1)
, we can write
F_{0}=\Phi\theta_{0}^{t}+R

R_{i}=f_{0}(X_{i})-f_{0}^{t}(X_{i})

\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}\big\|\theta_{Y}-\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}\big\|\theta_{Y}-\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}\theta_{Y}\big\|^{2}+\big\|\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}\theta_{Y}-\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|^{2}

\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}\big\|\theta_{Y}-\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}\theta_{Y}\big\|^{2}=\sigma^{2}{\rm tr\,}[(\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi)^{-1}]\lesssim\sigma^{2}k_{n}/n
on
A_{n}

\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}\theta_{Y}=(\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}F_{0}=\theta_{0}^{t}+(\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi)^{-1}\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}R

1/\sqrt{n}
on

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X)\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}\big\|\theta_{Y}-\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|^{2}\lesssim\sigma^{2}k_{n}/n+\big\|R\big\|^{2}/n.

(30)

We now handle the

A_{1}^{-1}-A_{2}^{-1}=A_{1}^{-1}(A_{2}-A_{1})A_{2}^{-1}

with

. Using a standard result for expectations of quadratic forms,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}\big\|{\widetilde{\theta}}-\theta_{Y}\big\|^{2}=\big\|BF_{0}\big\|^{2}+\sigma^{2}\big\|B\big\|_{F}^{2}.

(31)

So the goal now is to bound each one of
\big\|BF_{0}\big\|^{2}
and
\big\|B\big\|_{F}^{2}
on
A_{n}

on
A_{n}

\big\|\Lambda^{-1}\big\|_{2}<n/4

(A1)

\big\|(\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi)^{-1}\Delta\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}R\big\|\lesssim\big\|\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\big\|/s_{\min}(\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi)\big\|R\big\|\lesssim\big\|R\big\|/\sqrt{n}
on
A_{n}

29

on
A_{n}

\theta_{0}^{s}=\theta_{0}^{t}-\Delta^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\theta_{0}^{t}

\displaystyle\big\|\Lambda^{-1/2}\theta_{0}^{s}\big\|\leq\big\|\Lambda^{-1/2}\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|+\big\|\Lambda^{-1/2}\Delta^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\Lambda^{-1/2}\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|+\big\|\Lambda^{-1/2}(\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi+\Lambda^{-1})^{-1}\Lambda^{-1/2}\big\|_{2}\big\|\Lambda^{-1/2}\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|\lesssim\big\|\Lambda^{-1/2}\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|=\big\|\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|_{\mathbb{H}},

since
\big\|\Lambda^{-1/2}(\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi+\Lambda^{-1})^{-1}\Lambda^{-1/2}\big\|_{2}=\big\|\Delta\big\|_{2}
, which we already know is
\lesssim 1
on
A_{n}
.
Thus, we conclude that
\big\|BF_{0}\big\|^{2}\lesssim\big\|R\big\|^{2}/n+\big\|\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}/n
on
A_{n}

\big\|B\big\|_{F}^{2}\leq k_{n}\big\|B\big\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{k_{n}\big\|\Delta\big\|_{2}^{2}\big\|\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\big\|_{2}^{2}}{s_{\min}^{2}(\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi)}\lesssim\frac{k_{n}}{n}

on
A_{n}
, since we have already shown that
\big\|\Delta\big\|_{2}\lesssim 1
and
\big\|\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\big\|_{2}/s_{\min}(\Phi^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}\Phi)\lesssim 1/\sqrt{n}
on
A_{n}
. Substituting all the inequalities in (

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X)\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}\big\|{\widetilde{\theta}}-\theta_{Y}\big\|^{2}\lesssim\big\|R\big\|^{2}/n+\big\|\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}/n+\sigma^{2}k_{n}/n.

(32)

Substituting the inequalities obtained in (
30
) and (
32
) in (

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X)\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}({\widetilde{\theta}}-\theta_{0}^{t})^{2}\lesssim\sigma^{2}\frac{k_{n}}{n}+\frac{\big\|\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}}{n}+\frac{\big\|R\big\|^{2}}{n}.

(33)

Now we consider the term
\big\|{\widetilde{\Sigma}}^{1/2}-\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}\mathrm{I}_{k_{n}}\big\|_{F}^{2}
in (

{\widetilde{\Sigma}}

on

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}}(X)\,\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}d_{W,2}^{2}(Q_{T},Q_{A})\leq\sigma^{2}\frac{k_{n}}{n}+\frac{\big\|\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}}{n}+\frac{\big\|R\big\|^{2}}{n}.

(34)

Recall from (
26

\mathbb{E}_{X}

Proof of Theorem
3.4

Let

\displaystyle D_{n}=\int\frac{p_{n,F}(Y)}{p_{n,F_{0}}(Y)}\Pi(df),\quad G_{n}=\int\log\bigg\{\frac{p_{n,F}(Y)}{p_{n,F_{0}}(Y)}\bigg\}\Pi(df).

Following a standard argument, it is enough to show the desired lower bound on
\mathbb{P}_{0}(D_{n}\geq e^{-n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{\prime 2}})
for any probability measure
\Pi
supported on
\mathcal{F}_{n}=\{f:\big\|f-f_{0}\big\|_{2,\rho}<\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}\}
. By Jensen’s inequality,
\log D_{n}\geq G_{n}
, so that
\mathbb{P}_{0}(D_{n}\geq e^{-n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}})\geq\mathbb{P}_{0}(G_{n}\geq-n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2})
. Our goal below is to bound
\mathbb{P}_{0}(G_{n}\geq-n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2})
from below, or equivalently, bound
\mathbb{P}_{0}(G_{n}\leq-n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2})
from above.

A simple calculation yields
G_{n}=\mu_{0X}^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}(Y-F_{0})-\sigma_{0X}^{2}/2
, where
\mu_{0X}=\int(F-F_{0})\Pi(df)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\sigma_{0X}^{2}=\int\big\|F-F_{0}\big\|^{2}\Pi(df)
.
Since
Y\sim{\mathrm{N}}(F_{0},\mathrm{I}_{n})
, we have
G_{n}\mid X\sim{\mathrm{N}}(-\sigma_{0X}^{2}/2,\big\|\mu_{0X}\big\|^{2})
. Also, the marginal expectation of
G_{n}
,
\mathbb{E}_{0}G_{n}=-\mathbb{E}_{0X}\sigma_{0X}^{2}/2=-n\sigma_{0}^{2}/2
, where
\sigma_{0}^{2}=\int\big\|f-f_{0}\big\|_{2,\rho}^{2}\Pi(df)
. Since
\Pi
is supported on
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, clearly
\sigma_{0}^{2}\leq\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}
.

The Paley–Zygmund inequality (see, for example,
[
11
]
) states that for any non-negative random variable
Z
with finite second moment and
\delta\in(0,1)
,

P(Z\geq\delta EZ)\geq(1-\delta)^{2}(EZ)^{2}/(EZ^{2})
.
In particular, if
(EZ)^{2}/(EZ^{2})\geq 1-\gamma
for
\gamma>0
small, then

\displaystyle P(Z<\delta EZ)\leq 1-(1-\delta)^{2}(1-\gamma)\lesssim\delta+\gamma.

(35)

We shall invoke (
35
) with the non-negative random variable
Z_{n}=e^{t_{n}G_{n}}
for some
t_{n}\in(0,1/2)
and
\delta_{n}\in(0,1)
to be chosen below. A key ingredient of such an exercise is to obtain a lower bound on
(\mathbb{E}_{0}Z_{n})^{2}/(\mathbb{E}_{0}Z_{n}^{2})
.

By Jensen’s inequality,
\mathbb{E}_{0}Z_{n}\geq e^{t_{n}\mathbb{E}_{0}G_{n}}=e^{-nt_{n}\sigma_{0}^{2}/2}
, which implies
(\mathbb{E}_{0}Z_{n})^{2}\geq e^{-nt_{n}\sigma_{0}^{2}}
. We next need to bound
\mathbb{E}_{0}Z_{n}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{0}e^{2t_{n}G_{n}}
from above. Since
G_{n}\mid X
is conditionally Gaussian, we have sufficient control over the moment generating function
M_{G_{n}}(\lambda)=\mathbb{E}_{0}e^{\lambda G_{n}}
for
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Using the iterative property of conditional expectations, we can write
\mathbb{E}_{0}e^{\lambda G_{n}}=\mathbb{E}_{0X}[\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}(e^{\lambda G_{n}})]
. Recalling
G_{n}\mid X\sim{\mathrm{N}}(-\sigma_{0X}^{2}/2,\big\|\mu_{0X}\big\|^{2})
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{0\mid X}(e^{\lambda G_{n}})=e^{-\lambda\sigma_{0X}^{2}/2}\,e^{\lambda^{2}\big\|\mu_{0X}\big\|^{2}/2}\leq e^{-(\lambda-\lambda^{2})\sigma_{0X}^{2}/2},

where the second step follows since by an application of Cauchy–Schwartz inequality,
\big\|\mu_{0X}\big\|^{2}\leq\sigma_{0X}^{2}
. Since
\lambda\in(0,1)
, the quantity
\lambda-\lambda^{2}
in the exponent is positive. Therefore, by Jensen’s inequality,
\mathbb{E}_{0}e^{\lambda G_{n}}\leq e^{-(\lambda-\lambda^{2})\mathbb{E}_{0X}\sigma_{0X}^{2}/2}=e^{-(\lambda-\lambda^{2})n\sigma_{0}^{2}/2}
. In particular, for any
t_{n}\in(0,1/2)
,

\mathbb{E}_{0}Z_{n}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{0}e^{2t_{n}G_{n}}\leq e^{-n(t_{n}-2t_{n}^{2})\sigma_{0}^{2}}
. Combining this bound with the previously obtained bound
(\mathbb{E}_{0}Z_{n})^{2}\geq e^{-nt_{n}\sigma_{0}^{2}}
, we have
(\mathbb{E}_{0}Z_{n})^{2}/(\mathbb{E}_{0}Z_{n}^{2})\geq e^{-2t_{n}^{2}n\sigma_{0}^{2}}\geq e^{-2t_{n}^{2}n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}}
.

For a slowly decaying sequence
\gamma_{n}
satisfying
\gamma_{n}\to 0
and
\gamma_{n}n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}\to\infty
, set
t_{n}^{2}n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}=\gamma_{n}
. For
n
large enough so that
\gamma_{n}\leq 1
, we have
(\mathbb{E}_{0}Z_{n})^{2}/(\mathbb{E}_{0}Z_{n}^{2})\geq e^{-\gamma_{n}}\geq 1-\gamma_{n}
. From (
35
), we therefore have that for any
0<\delta_{n}>1
,
\mathbb{P}_{0}(Z_{n}\leq\delta_{n}\mathbb{E}_{0}Z_{n})\leq\delta_{n}+\gamma_{n}
. Further,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}(Z_{n}<\delta_{n}\mathbb{E}_{0}Z_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{0}\bigg(G_{n}<\frac{\log\delta_{n}}{t_{n}}+\frac{\log\mathbb{E}_{0}Z_{n}}{t_{n}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{0}\bigg(G_{n}<\frac{\log\delta_{n}}{t_{n}}-\frac{n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}}{2}\bigg),

(36)

where the inequality follows since
(\log\mathbb{E}_{0}Z_{n})/t_{n}\geq\mathbb{E}_{0}G_{n}=-n\sigma_{0}^{2}/2\geq-n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}/2
. Choose
\delta_{n}
so that
(\log\delta_{n}/t_{n})=-n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}/2
, i.e.,
\delta_{n}=e^{-t_{n}n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}/2}=e^{-C\sqrt{\gamma_{n}n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}}}
. From (
5
) and the immediately preceding inequality, we therefore have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{0}(G_{n}\leq-n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2})\leq\delta_{n}+\gamma_{n}=e^{-C\sqrt{\gamma_{n}n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}}}+\gamma_{n}\leq e^{-C\gamma_{n}\sqrt{n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}}}+\gamma_{n}.

(37)

The sequence
\gamma_{n}
is yet to be chosen; we shall do so now by optimizing the right hand side of (
37
). Consider the function
g(x)=x+e^{-Bx}
for
B>0
. The function attains its minimum value on
(0,\infty)
at the point
x=\log B/B
and the minimum value of the function is
(\log B+1)/B
. Therefore, choose
\gamma_{n}=C\log(n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2})/\sqrt{n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}}
; note that for this choice
\gamma_{n}\to 0
and
\gamma_{n}n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}\to\infty
; with this choice we have
\mathbb{P}_{0}(G_{n}\leq-n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2})\leq C\log(n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2})/\sqrt{n\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}}
.

Proof of Theorem
4.2

The proof follows from an application of Corollary
3.5
to the present setting. We assume
\sigma^{2}=1
for this proof.
Also at the very onset, we mention that we replace
\lambda_{j}
by

subsequently, since after some algebra, it can be shown that

. Recall

, we

. Indeed,

It now remains to verify
(C3)
. As noted in the paragraph after (
17
), the numerator in (

) can be expressed as
\Pi(\big\|f-f^{t}\big\|_{2,\rho}^{2}>M^{2}\epsilon_{n}^{2})=\Pi(\sum_{j=k_{n}+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}Z_{j}^{2}>M^{2}\epsilon_{n}^{2})

. Noting that

\sum_{j=k_{n}+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}\asymp e^{-k_{n}/a_{n}}=n^{-2\alpha/(2\alpha+1)}\leq\epsilon_{n}^{2}

\displaystyle\Pi\bigg(\sum_{j=k_{n}+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}Z_{j}^{2}>M^{2}\epsilon_{n}^{2}\bigg)\leq\Pi\bigg\{\sum_{j=k_{n}+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}(Z_{j}^{2}-1)>M^{2}\epsilon_{n}^{2}/2\bigg\}.

(38)

(Z_{j}^{2}-1)
s are mean-zero sub-exponential random variables. By an application of Bernstein’s inequality for linear combinations of mean-zero sub-exponential random variables (Proposition 5.16 of
[
33
]

\displaystyle\Pi\bigg\{\sum_{j=k_{n}+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}(Z_{j}^{2}-1)>M^{2}\epsilon_{n}^{2}/2\bigg\}\leq 2\exp\bigg[-C^{\prime}\min\bigg\{\frac{M^{4}\epsilon_{n}^{4}}{K^{2}\sum_{j=k_{n}+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{2}},\frac{M^{2}\epsilon_{n}^{2}}{K(\max_{j>k_{n}}\lambda_{j})}\bigg\}\bigg]

(39)

where

\sum_{j=k_{n}+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{2}\asymp(1/2a_{n})e^{-2k_{n}/a_{n}}

Next, the term in the denominator of (
18

\Pi(\big\|f-f_{0}\big\|_{2,\rho}\leq\tilde{\epsilon}_{n})={\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}(\big\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\big\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\tilde{\epsilon}_{n})
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\ldots)
with
\theta_{j}\sim{\mathrm{N}}(0,\lambda_{j})
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}=(\theta_{01},\theta_{02},\ldots)
. Set
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}=Cn^{-\alpha/(2\alpha+1)}
for some constant
C
. We show below that

\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}(\big\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\big\|_{\ell_{2}}\leq\tilde{\epsilon}_{n})\geq\exp\{-C^{\prime}n^{1/(2\alpha+1)}\log^{2}n\}.

(40)

We now establish (
40
). Recall
\lambda_{j}\asymp a_{n}^{-1}e^{-j/a_{n}}
.
Let
\theta^{t}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k_{n}})^{{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle T}}}
and recall
\theta_{0}^{t}
defined similarly.
Then,
{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}\big(\big\|\boldsymbol{\theta}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\big\|_{\ell_{2}}<\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}\big)\geq{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}\big(\big\|\theta^{t}-\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|^{2}\leq\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}/2\big)\,{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}\big(\sum_{j=k_{n}+1}^{\infty}(\theta_{j}-\theta_{0j})^{2}\leq\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}/2\big)
. Using
\sum_{j=k_{n}+1}^{\infty}\theta_{0j}^{2}\leq\big\|\theta_{0}\big\|_{\alpha}^{2}k_{n}^{-2\alpha}=o(\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2})
, the second term can be bounded below by
{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}\big(\sum_{j=k_{n}+1}^{\infty}\theta_{j}^{2}\leq\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}/4\big)
. By Markov’s inequality,

\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}\big(\sum_{j=k_{n}+1}^{\infty}\theta_{j}^{2}\leq\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}/4\big)\geq 1-4/\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}\sum_{j=k_{n}+1}^{\infty}E\theta_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle\asymp 1-\frac{4}{a_{n}\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}}\sum_{j=k_{n}+1}^{\infty}e^{-j/a_{n}}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{4e^{-k_{n}/a_{n}}}{\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}}\geq 1/2

(41)

for large
C
. We used above that
\sum_{j=k_{n}+1}^{\infty}e^{-j/a_{n}}\leq\int_{k_{n}}^{\infty}e^{-x/a_{n}}dx=a_{n}e^{-k_{n}/a_{n}}
and
e^{-k_{n}/a_{n}}=n^{-2\alpha/(2\alpha+1)}
. Therefore, it is enough to show the bound (
40
) for
{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}\big(\big\|\theta^{t}-\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|^{2}\leq\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}/2\big)
. By Anderson’s inequality (Lemma
B.2
in Appendix),

\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}\big(\big\|\theta^{t}-\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|^{2}\leq\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}/2\big)\geq e^{-\frac{1}{2}\big\|\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}}\,{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}\big(\big\|\theta_{t}\big\|^{2}\leq\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}/2\big).

(42)

We have already shown that
\big\|\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\lesssim a_{n}
, so that
e^{-\frac{1}{2}\big\|\theta_{0}^{t}\big\|_{\mathbb{H}}^{2}}\geq e^{-C^{\prime}n^{1/(2\alpha+1)}}
. Therefore, suffices to bound
{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}\big(\big\|\theta_{t}\big\|^{2}\leq\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}/2\big)
. Recall
\theta_{j}^{2}/\lambda_{j}\sim\chi_{1}^{2}
, therefore
\theta_{j}^{2}
has a density
(\sqrt{2\pi x})^{-1}a_{n}e^{j/(2a_{n})}\exp(-a_{n}e^{j/a_{n}}x/2)\mathbbm{1}_{(0,\infty)}(x)
.
Let
d{\bf x}
denote
dx_{1}\ldots dx_{k_{n}}
in short and set
D_{n}=a_{n}/\sqrt{2\pi}
. Then,

\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\theta_{i}^{2}\leq\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}/2\bigg)

\displaystyle=D_{n}^{k_{n}}e^{\frac{k_{n}(k_{n}+1)}{4a_{n}}}\int_{\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}x_{j}\leq\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}/2}\prod_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\frac{\exp\big(-a_{n}e^{j/a_{n}}x_{j}/2\big)}{\sqrt{x_{j}}}\ d{\bf x}

\displaystyle\geq\bigg(\frac{D_{n}\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}}{\sqrt{2}}\bigg)^{k_{n}}e^{\frac{k_{n}(k_{n}+1)}{4a_{n}}}\int_{\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}x_{j}\leq 1}\exp\bigg(-\frac{a_{n}e^{k_{n}/a_{n}}}{4}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}x_{j}\bigg)\prod_{j=1}^{k_{n}}x_{j}^{-1/2}d{\bf x}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{D_{n}\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}}{\sqrt{2}}\bigg)^{k_{n}}e^{\frac{k_{n}(k_{n}+1)}{4a_{n}}}\frac{\Gamma(1/2)^{k_{n}}}{\Gamma(k_{n}/2)}\int_{t=0}^{1}\exp\bigg(-\frac{a_{n}e^{k_{n}/a_{n}}}{4}t\bigg)t^{k_{n}/2-1}dt.

(43)

From the first to the second line, we replace
j
by
k_{n}
, perform a change of variable and drop the
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}
term appearing inside the exponent as
\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}<1
. The last equality follows from
the Dirichlet integral formula (Lemma
B.3
in Appendix). Using
\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt{\pi}
and the standard inequality (see, for example,
[
1
]
)
\Gamma(\alpha)\leq\sqrt{2\pi e^{2}}e^{-\alpha}\alpha^{\alpha-1/2}
for
\alpha>1
, we can simplify (
43
) to write

\displaystyle{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{k_{n}}\theta_{i}^{2}\leq\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}/2\bigg)\gtrsim\sqrt{k_{n}}e^{\frac{k_{n}(k_{n}+1)}{4a_{n}}}\bigg(\frac{a_{n}\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}e}{2k_{n}}\bigg)^{k_{n}/2}\int_{t=0}^{1}\exp\bigg(-\frac{a_{n}e^{k_{n}/a_{n}}}{4}t\bigg)t^{k_{n}/2-1}dt.

(44)

The integral in the above display can be bounded from below by
(2e^{-1}/k_{n})(a_{n}e^{k_{n}/a_{n}}/4)^{-k_{n}/2}
. Substituting this bound and simplifying, the lower bound is

\frac{1}{k_{n}}e^{\frac{k_{n}(k_{n}+1)}{4a_{n}}}e^{-\frac{k_{n}^{2}}{2a_{n}}}\bigg(\frac{2e\tilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}}{k_{n}}\bigg)^{k_{n}/2}\gtrsim e^{-C^{\prime}n^{1/(2\alpha+1)}\log^{2}n}.

Combining with (
42

40
) is proved.

Finally, the ratio of the bounds in (
39
) and (
40
) converge to zero by choosing

large enough, completing the proof.
