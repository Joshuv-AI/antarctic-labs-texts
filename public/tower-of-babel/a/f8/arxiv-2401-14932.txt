Super-exponential quantum advantage for finding the center of a sphere
Guanzhong Li, Lvzhou Li

Abstract
This article considers the geometric problem of finding the center of a sphere in vector space over finite fields, given samples of random points on the sphere. We propose a quantum algorithm based on continuous-time quantum walks that needs only a constant number of samples to find the center. We also prove that any classical algorithm for the same task requires approximately as many samples as the dimension of the vector space, by a reduction to an old and basic algebraic result—Warning’s second theorem. Thus, a super-exponential quantum advantage is revealed for the first time for a natural and intuitive geometric problem.



Introduction.
—
A primary goal of quantum computing is to identify problems for which quantum algorithms can offer speedups over their classical counterpart.
Grover’s algorithm
Grover 1996
offers a quadratic speedup for unstructured database search problem.
Shor’s algorithm
Shor 1997
is exponentially faster than the best classical algorithm in factoring integers.
Recent studies have also found exponential speedups in specific problems such as simulating the coupled oscillators
Babbush et al. 2023
, traversing a decorated welded-tree graph using adiabatic quantum computation
Gilyén et al. 2021
, a graph property testing problem in the adjacency list model
Ben-David et al. 2020
, a classification problem for quantum kernel methods based on discrete logarithm problem
Liu et al. 2021
, a specific NP search problem based on random black-box function
Yamakawa and Zhandry 2022
, and so on.
However, to the best of our knowledge, no one has ever found a geometric problem that exhibits sharp quantum speedups.

In this letter, we consider a natural geometric problem of finding the center of a sphere given samples of random points on the sphere.
To illustrate this problem, we begin with an intuitive example: if we want to pin down a circle on a paper, we will need
3
points (
A,B,C
) which are not on the same line, as depicted in Fig.
1
(a);
and if we want to fix an inflatable balloon, we will need
4
points (
A,B,C,D
) which are not on the same plane, as depicted in Fig.
1

As such,
n+1
points are required to determine a sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Figure 1:
Spheres in the
\mathbb{R}^{2}

To accommodate the discrete nature of qubit-based quantum computer, we will replace the continuous vector space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}

\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
q
being a prime number.
Define the length of a vector

Then a sphere with radius

is denoted by
S_{r}+t=\{x+t\mid x\in S_{r}\}
, where

S_{r}:=\{x\mid x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n},l(x)=r\}.

(1)

The problem is then formally described as follows.

Problem 1
.

Find the unknown center

of the sphere

(

S_{r}+t
as possible.

In analogy to sampling a point uniformly random from

, each usage of the state
\ket{S_{r}+t}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{r}}}\sum_{v\in S_{r}+t}\ket{v}
, where
s_{r}:=|S_{r}|
, is regarded as a quantum sample.
In this letter, we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

There is a quantum algorithm that solves Problem
1
with bounded error, and uses
O(\log q)

1

The standard asymptotic notations

However, any classical algorithm that solves Problem

Note that when

This is actually a super-exponential speedup because the classical

To prove the classical lower bound

,
we find unexpectedly that it can be reduced to a basic algebraic theorem proved around 1935 by Warning

We then design the quantum algorithm based on a continuous-time quantum walk (CTQW) on the Euclidean graph on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, where two points

are connected if and only if

Quantum walks are an analogy to classical random walks, and have become a widely adopted paradigm to design quantum algorithms for various problems, such as spatial search

;
Ambainis et al. 2005
;
Chakraborty et al. 2016

;
Li and Li 2023
, group commutativity

, the welded-tree problem

;
Jeffery and Zur 2023
;
Li et al.
, and so on.
There are two types of quantum walks: the CTQW and the discrete-time quantum walk (DTQW).
CTQW is relatively simple, and mainly involves simulating a Hamiltonian
H
that encodes the structure of the graph.
DTQW is more diverse, ranging from the earliest and simplest coined quantum walk

;
Aharonov et al. 2001
to various Markov chain based frameworks
Szegedy 2004
;
Magniez et al. 2011
;
Belovs 2013
;
Krovi et al. 2016
;
Apers et al. 2021
.
The quantum algorithm proposed in this letter is based on CTQW and is different from previous ones with sharp speedups featuring quantum Fourier transform, and thus our result may inspire the discovery of new quantum algorithms with sharp speedups.

Classical lower bound.
—
In the introduction section we have intuitively shown that
n+1
points are required to determine a sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Here we will rigorously prove that any classical algorithm requires
\Omega(n)
samples of points on the sphere
S_{r}+t
to determine the center
t
with high probability.
To prove this classical lower bound, we will use the following lemma, also known as Warning’s second theorem
Asgarli 2018
;
Clark et al. 2017
;
Clark 2018
, attributed to Ewald Warning
Chevalley and Warning 1935
.

Lemma 1

(Warning’s second theorem)
.

Suppose
f_{1},f_{2},\cdots,f_{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}]
are multivariate polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with
d_{i}=\text{deg}(f_{i})
.
Let
d:=d_{1}+\cdots+d_{s}
be the total degree and
V:=\{x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}\mid f_{i}(x)=0,\forall\,1\leq i\leq s\}
be the set of common zeros of
f_{i}
.
Assume
n>d
and
V\neq\emptyset
.
Then
|V|\geq q^{n-d}
.

We can now obtain a relationship between the number of different points that a classical algorithm has sampled on
S_{r}+t
and the upper bound of its success probability.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
c
is a constant such that
0<c<1/2
. If a classical algorithm has sampled less than
cn
different points on
S_{r}+t
,
then it has at most
1/q^{(1-2c)n}
probability of obtaining the correct center
t
.

Proof.

Denoted by
X+t
, where
X:=\{(x_{i1},\cdots,x_{in})\mid i=1,\ldots,s\}\subseteq S_{r}
, the
s\leq cn
different points that the classical algorithm has obtained on
S_{r}+t
.
We claim that there are at least
q^{(1-2c)n}
possible centers
\{t^{\prime}\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
such that
X+t\subseteq S_{r}+t^{\prime}
.
In other words, all of these possible spheres
\{S_{r}+t^{\prime}\}
can lead to the same set of samples
X+t
.
Having obtained only
X+t
, the algorithm cannot distinguish between the possible centers
\{t^{\prime}\}
, where only one of them is the correct center
t
.
As
\left|\{t^{\prime}\}\right|\geq q^{(1-2c)n}
, the possibility of obtaining
t\in\{t^{\prime}\}
is at most
1/q^{(1-2c)n}
.

To prove our claim, note that by letting
\bar{t}:=t^{\prime}-t
, the inclusion
X+t\subseteq S_{r}+t^{\prime}
becomes
X\subseteq S_{r}+\bar{t}
, which results in the following system of polynomial equations over finite fields about variables
\bar{t}=(\bar{t}_{1},\cdots,\bar{t}_{n})
:

f_{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(x_{ij}-\bar{t}_{j})^{2}-r=0,\ 1\leq i\leq s.

(2)

Since
d_{i}=\text{deg}(f_{i})=2
for all
i
, and
\bar{t}=\vec{0}
is a solution (recall that
X\subseteq S_{r}
), the above equations have at least
q^{n-2s}\geq q^{(1-2c)n}
common roots
\bar{t}
by Lemma
1
.
Thus there are at least
q^{(1-2c)n}
possible centers
t^{\prime}=\bar{t}+t
such that
X+t\subseteq S_{r}+t^{\prime}
.
∎

If we let
c=1/3
, then a classical algorithm that has sampled less than
n/3
different points on
S_{r}+t
can only succeed with probability less than
1/q^{n/3}
, which is exponentially small.
Therefore, if a classical algorithm wants to solve Problem
1
with high probability,
\Omega(n)
samples are needed.

Quantum algorithm.
—
The quantum algorithm for Problem
1
is concise and consists of only four steps as shown below.
The main idea is to use CTQW on the Euclidean graph
G
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
to move amplitude from the sphere to its center.
The Euclidean graph
G
has vertex set
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, and two vertices
x,x^{\prime}\in F_{q}^{n}
are connected by an undirected edge if and only if
l(x-x^{\prime})=r
.

The Hamiltonian
\bar{A}
of the CTQW on the Euclidean graph
G
is approximately the adjacency matrix
A
of the Euclidean graph
G
, but with
A
’s largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}=s_{r}
replaced by
0
.
Specifically,
\bar{A}:=A-\lambda_{0}\ket{\widetilde{0}}\bra{\widetilde{0}}
, where
\ket{\tilde{0}}
is the corresponding eigenvector of eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
and it is the equal superposition of all points in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
(see
(
Medrano et al. 1996
, Proposition 2)
for the spectral decomposition of
A
).
Note that
A
is symmetric since the graph
G
is undirected, so
\bar{A}
is a valid Hamiltonian. We will see later from numerical simulation that
A
itself as the Hamiltonian is good enough.

1.

Prepare the quantum sample
\ket{S_{r}+t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{s_{r}}}\sum_{v\in S_{r}+t}\ket{v}
.

2.

Apply a CTQW
e^{i\bar{A}t_{0}}
to
\ket{S_{r}+t}
for time
t_{0}=1/\sqrt{q^{n-1}\log q}
, and then measure in the computational basis obtaining a point in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.

3.

Repeat the above two steps for
O(\log q)
times.

4.

Output the point with the highest frequency.

The following lemma lower bounds the success probability of step 2.
It can be seen as a fine-grained version of
(
Childs et al. 2007
, Lemma 4)
.

Lemma 3
.

The final state
\ket{\psi_{t_{0}}}:=e^{i\bar{A}t_{0}}\ket{S_{r}+t}
of CTQW on the Euclidean graph
G
has the following properties:

\left|\braket{x|\psi_{t_{0}}}\right|\begin{cases}\leq O(q^{-(n-1)/2})&\text{for}\,x\neq t,\\
\geq\frac{h(q,n)}{\sqrt{\log q}}&\text{for}\,x=t,\end{cases}

(3)

where the function
h(q,n)
is monotonically increasing in both
q
and
n
, and
h(127,8)\geq 0.0016
.

From Lemma
3
, the probability to obtain the center
t
is
\Omega(1/\log q)
in step 2, while the probability to obtain any point other than
t
is exponentially small.
Step 3 then guarantees the center
t
to be found with high probability.
Overall, the quantum algorithm needs
O(\log q)
samples.

Proof of Lemma
3
.

As shown in Appendix, the CTQW
e^{i\bar{A}t_{0}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(it_{0})^{k}}{k!}\bar{A}^{k}
has a
(q+1)
-dimensional invariant subspace
\mathcal{H}_{0}
spanned by the following orthonormal basis:

\mathcal{B}_{0}=\{\ket{t},\ket{S_{0}+t},\ket{S_{1}+t},\cdots,\ket{S_{q-1}+t}\},

(4)

where
S_{0}:=\{v\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}:l(v)=0\}\setminus\{\vec{0}\}
(see Eq. (
1
) for

with

).
The following equation
(
Medrano et al. 1996

shows that

for all

\displaystyle s_{r}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}q^{n-1}+\chi((-1)^{\frac{n-1}{2}}r)q^{\frac{n-1}{2}}&n\,\mathrm{odd},r\neq 0,\\
q^{n-1}-\chi((-1)^{\frac{n}{2}})q^{\frac{n-2}{2}}&n\,\mathrm{even},r\neq 0,\\
q^{n-1}-1&n\,\mathrm{odd},r=0,\\
q^{n-1}+\chi((-1)^{\frac{n}{2}})(q-1)q^{\frac{n-2}{2}}-1&n\,\mathrm{even},r=0,\end{cases}

(5)

where
\chi(a)\in\{0,1,-1\}

, or otherwise.

We first consider

Since

\displaystyle\left|\braket{x|\psi_{t_{0}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{r^{\prime}\in[q]}\left|\braket{x|S_{r^{\prime}}+t}\right|

(8)

\displaystyle\geq t_{0}\bra{t}\big(A-\lambda_{0}\ket{\widetilde{0}}\bra{\widetilde{0}}\big)\ket{S_{r}+t}-\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{t_{0}^{k}}{k!}\left|\bra{t}\bar{A}^{k}\ket{S_{r}+t}\right|

From Eq. (

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log q}}(1-\frac{1}{q}-\frac{4}{q^{(n-1)/4}}-g(\sqrt{\log q}))

\tau(S_{r^{\prime}})=S_{r^{\prime}}

The isometry
\tau
