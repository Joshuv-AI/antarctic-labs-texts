Language-Based Security for Low-Level MPC
Christian Skalka, Joseph P. Near

Abstract
. Secure Multi-Party Computation (MPC) is an important enabling technology for data privacy in modern distributed applications. Currently, proof methods for low-level MPC protocols are primarily manual and thus tedious and error-prone, and are also non-standardized and unfamiliar to most PL theorists. As a step towards better language support and language-based enforcement, we develop a new staged PL for defining a variety of low-level probabilistic MPC protocols. We also formulate a collection of confidentiality and integrity hyperproperties for our language model that are familiar from information flow, including conditional noninterference, gradual release, and robust declassification. We demonstrate their relation to standard MPC threat models of passive and malicious security, and how they can be leveraged in security verification of protocols. To prove these properties we develop automated tactics in 𝔽 2 \mathbb{F}_{2} that can be integrated with separation logic-style reasoning.

1.
Introduction

Secure Multi-Party Computation (MPC) protocols support data privacy in
important modern, distributed applications such as privacy-preserving
machine learning
(
Li
et al., 2021
;
Knott et al., 2021
;
Koch
et al., 2020
;
Liu
et al., 2020
)
and Zero-Knowledge proofs in
blockchains
(
Ishai et al., 2009
;
Lu et al., 2019
;
Gao
et al., 2022
;
Tomaz et al., 2020
)
. The security semantics of
MPC include both confidentiality and integrity properties incorporated
into models such as real/ideal (aka simulator) security and universal
composability (UC), developed primarily by the cryptography community

(
Evans et al., 2018
)
. Related proof methods are well-studied

(
Lindell, 2017
)
but mostly manual. Somewhat independently, a
significant body of work in programming languages has focused on
definition and enforcement of confidentiality and integrity

hyperproperties

(
Clarkson and
Schneider, 2010
)
such as
noninterference and gradual release

(
Askarov and
Sabelfeld, 2007
;
Sabelfeld and
Sands, 2009
)
. Following a tradition of
connecting cryptographic and PL-based security models

(
Abadi and Rogaway, 2000
;
Hunt
et al., 2023
)
, recent work has also
recognized connections between MPC security models and hyperproperties
of, e.g., noninterference
(
Haagh et al., 2018
)
, and even leveraged these
connections to enforce MPC security through mechanisms such as
security types
(
Acay
et al., 2021
)
. Major benefits of this
connection in an MPC setting include better language abstractions for
defining protocols and for mechanization and even automation of
security proofs. The goal of this paper is to develop a PL model for
defining a variety of low-level probabilistic MPC protocols, to
formulate a collection of confidentiality and integrity
hyperproperties for our model with familiar information flow
analogs, and to show how these properties can be leveraged for
improved proof automation.

The distinction between high- and low-level languages for MPC is
important. High-level languages such as Wysteria

(
Rastogi
et al., 2014
)
and Viaduct
(
Acay
et al., 2021
)

are designed to provide effective programming of full
applications. These language designs incorporate sophisticated
verified compilation techniques such as orchestration

(
Acay
et al., 2024
)
to guarantee high-level security properties, and
they rely on
libraries
of low-level MPC protocols, such as
binary and arithmetic circuits. These low-level protocols encapsulate
abstractions such as secret sharing and semi-homomorphic encryption,
and must be verified by hand. So, low-level MPC
programming and protocol verification remains a distinct challenge and both
critical to the general challenge of PL design for MPC and
complementary to high-level language design.

The connection between information flow hyperproperties and MPC
security is also complicated especially at a low level. MPC protocols
involve communication between a group of distributed clients called a

federation
that collaboratively compute and publish the result
of some known
ideal functionality

\mathcal{F}
, maintaining
confidentiality of inputs to
\mathcal{F}
without the use of a trusted
third party. However, since the outputs of
\mathcal{F}
are public, some
information about inputs is inevitably leaked. Thus, the ideal
functionality establishes a declassification policy

(
Sabelfeld and
Sands, 2009
)
, which is more difficult to
enforce than pure noninterference. And subtleties of, e.g.,
semi-homomorphic encryption are central to both confidentiality and
integrity properties of protocols and similarly difficult to track
with coarse-grained security types alone.

Nevertheless, as previous authors have observed

(
Brzuska and
Oechsner, 2023
)
, low-level protocol design
patterns such as secret sharing and circuit gate structure have
compositional properties that can be independently verified and then
leveraged in larger proof contexts. We contribute to this line of work
by developing an automated verification technique for subprotocols and
show how it can be integrated as a tactic in a larger security proof.

1.2.
Related Work

Our main focus is on PL design and automated and semi-automated
reasoning about security properties of low-level MPC protocols. Prior
work has considered
automated
verification of

high-level
protocols and
manual
verification of

low-level
protocols—but none offers the combination of
automation and low-level support we consider.
We summarize this comparison in Figure

1
, with the caveat that works vary in the degree of
development in each dimension.

As mentioned above, several high-level languages have been developed
for writing MPC applications, and frequently exploit the connection
between hyperproperties and MPC security. Previous work on analysis
for the SecreC language

(
Almeida et al., 2018
;
Bogdanov
et al., 2014
)
is concerned with
properties of complex MPC circuits, in particular a user-friendly
specification and automated enforcement of declassification bounds in
programs that use MPC in subprograms. The Wys
⋆
language

(
Rastogi
et al., 2019
)
, based on Wysteria
(
Rastogi
et al., 2014
)
, has
similar goals and includes a trace-based semantics for reasoning about
the interactions of MPC protocols. Their compiler also guarantees that
underlying multi-threaded protocols enforce the single-threaded source
language semantics. These two lines of work were focused on passive
security. The Viaduct language

(
Acay
et al., 2021
)
has a well-developed
information flow type system that automatically enforces both
confidentiality and integrity through hyperproperties such as robust
declassification, in addition to rigorous compilation guarantees
through orchestration
(
Acay
et al., 2024
)
. However, these high level
languages lack probabilistic features and other abstractions of
low-level protocols, the implementation and security of which are
typically assumed as a selection of library components.

Various related low-level languages with probabilistic features have
also been developed. The
\lambda_{\mathrm{obliv}}
language

(
Darais
et al., 2019
)
uses a type system to automatically
enforce so-called probabilistic trace obliviousness. But similar to
previous work on oblivious data structures
(
Ye and Delaware, 2022
)
,
obliviousness is related to pure noninterference, not the relaxed form
related to passive MPC security. The Haskell-based security type
system in
(
Mitchell et al., 2012
)
enforces a version of noninterference that is
sound for passive security, but does not verify the correctness of
declassifications and does not consider malicious security. And
properties of real/ideal passive and malicious security for a
probabilistic language have been formulated in EasyCrypt

(
Haagh et al., 2018
)
– though their proof methods, while mechanized, are
fully manual, and their formulation of malicious security is not as
clearly related to robust declassification as is the one we present in
Section
4
.

Program logics for probabilistic languages and specifically reasoning
about properties such as joint probabilistic independence is also
important related work. Probabilistic Separation Logic (PSL)

(
Barthe
et al., 2019
)
develops a logical framework for
reasoning about probabilistic independence (aka separation) in
programs, and they consider several (hyper)properties, such as perfect
secrecy of one-time-pads and indistinguishability in secret sharing,
that are critical to MPC. However, their methods are manual, and
don’t include conditional independence (separation). This
latter issue has been addressed in Lilac
(
Li
et al., 2023
)
. The
application of Lilac-style reasoning to MPC protocols has not
previously been explored, as we do in Section

7
.

Our work also shares many ideas with probabilistic programming
languages designed to perform (exact or approximate) statistical
inference
(
Holtzen et al., 2020
;
Carpenter et al., 2017
;
Wood
et al., 2014
;
Bingham
et al., 2019
;
Albarghouthi et al., 2017
;
De Raedt
et al., 2007
;
Pfeffer, 2009
;
Saad
et al., 2021
)
. Our setting, however, requires
verifying properties beyond inference, including conditional
statistical independence. Recent work by Li et al.
(
Li
et al., 2023
)
proposes a
manual approach for proving such properties, but does not provide
automation.

2.
The
\mathit{Overture}
Protocol Language

The
\mathit{Overture}
language establishes a basic model of synchronous
protocols between a federation of
clients
exchanging values in
the binary field. A model of synchronous communication captures a wide
range of MPC protocols. Concurrency is out of scope in this work but
an avenue for future work. The lack of sophisticated control
structures in
\mathit{Overture}
is intentional, since minimizing features
eases analysis and control abstractions such as function definitions
can be integrated into a metalanguage that generates
\mathit{Overture}

programs (Section
6
).

We identify clients by natural numbers and federations- finite sets of
clients- are always given statically. Our threat model assumes a
partition of the federation into
honest

H
and
corrupt

C
subsets. We model probabilistic programming via a
random
tape
semantics. That is, we will assume that programs can make
reference to values chosen from a uniform random distributions defined
in the initial program memory. Programs aka protocols execute
deterministically given the random tape.

2.2.
Semantics

Memories
are fundamental to the semantics of
\mathit{Overture}
and
provide random tape and secret inputs to protocols, and also record
message sends, public broadcast, and client outputs.

Definition 2.2.

Memories
\sigma

are finite (partial) mappings from variables
x
to values
v\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
. The
domain
of a memory is written

\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)
and is the finite set of variables on which the memory
is defined. We write
\sigma\{x\mapsto v\}
for

x\not\in\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)
to denote the memory
\sigma^{\prime}
such that

\sigma^{\prime}(x)=v
and otherwise
\sigma^{\prime}(y)=\sigma(y)
for all
y\in\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)
. We write
\sigma\subseteq\sigma^{\prime}
iff
\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(\sigma^{\prime})
and
\sigma(x)=\sigma^{\prime}(x)
for all
x\in\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)
. Given any
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
with

\sigma(x)=\sigma^{\prime}(x)
for all
x\in\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)\cap\mathrm{dom}(\sigma^{\prime})
,
we write
\sigma\uplus\sigma^{\prime}
to denote the memory
with domain
X=\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)\cup\mathrm{dom}(\sigma^{\prime})
such

\forall x\in X.(\sigma\uplus\sigma^{\prime})(x)=\begin{cases}\sigma(x)\text{\ if\ }x\in\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)\\
\sigma^{\prime}(x)\text{\ otherwise\ }\end{cases}

In our subsequent presentation we will often want to consider arbitrary
memories that range over particular variables and to restrict

Given a set of variables

\sigma_{X}

2.3.
Example: Passive Secure Addition

Shamir addition leverages homomorphic properties of addition in
arithmetic fields to implement secret addition. If a field value
v_{1}

is uniformly random, then
v_{1}-v_{2}
is an encryption of
v_{2}

where
v_{1}
is an information theoretically secure one-time-pad, which
is exploited for secret sharing, noting that
v_{2}
can be
reconstructed by summing
v_{1}
and
v_{3}\triangleq v_{1}-v_{2}
.

In
\mathit{Overture}
, to privately sum secret values
\texttt{s[}\iota\texttt{]}
, each
client
\iota
in the federation
\{1,2,3\}
samples a value

\texttt{r[}\mathtt{local}\texttt{]}
that can be used as a one-time pad with another random
sample
\texttt{r[}x\texttt{]}
and
\texttt{s[}\iota\texttt{]}
. This yields two secret shares
communicated as messages to the other clients, while each client keeps

\texttt{r[}\mathtt{local}\texttt{]}
as its own share.

\begin{array}[]{lll}\texttt{m[}s1\texttt{]}\texttt{@}2&:=&(\texttt{s[}1\texttt{]}-\texttt{r[}\mathtt{local}\texttt{]}-\texttt{r[}x\texttt{]})\texttt{@}1\\
\texttt{m[}s1\texttt{]}\texttt{@}3&:=&\texttt{r[}x\texttt{]}\texttt{@}1\\
\texttt{m[}s2\texttt{]}\texttt{@}1&:=&(\texttt{s[}2\texttt{]}-\texttt{r[}\mathtt{local}\texttt{]}-\texttt{r[}x\texttt{]})\texttt{@}2\\
\texttt{m[}s2\texttt{]}\texttt{@}3&:=&\texttt{r[}x\texttt{]}\texttt{@}2\\
\texttt{m[}s3\texttt{]}\texttt{@}1&:=&(\texttt{s[}3\texttt{]}-\texttt{r[}\mathtt{local}\texttt{]}-\texttt{r[}x\texttt{]})\texttt{@}3\\
\texttt{m[}s3\texttt{]}\texttt{@}2&:=&\texttt{r[}x\texttt{]}\texttt{@}3\end{array}

This scheme guarantees that messages
are viewed as random noise by any observer
besides
\iota

(
Barthe
et al., 2019
)
. Next, each client
publicly reveals the sum of all of its shares, including its local
share. This step does reveal information about secrets– note in
particular that
\texttt{r[}\mathtt{local}\texttt{]}
is reused and is no longer a one-time-pad:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\texttt{p[}1\texttt{]}&:=&(\texttt{r[}\mathtt{local}\texttt{]}+\texttt{m[}s2\texttt{]}+\texttt{m[}s3\texttt{]})\texttt{@}1\\
\texttt{p[}2\texttt{]}&:=&(\texttt{m[}s1\texttt{]}+\texttt{r[}\mathtt{local}\texttt{]}+\texttt{m[}s3\texttt{]})\texttt{@}2\\
\texttt{p[}3\texttt{]}&:=&(\texttt{m[}s1\texttt{]}+\texttt{m[}s2\texttt{]}+\texttt{r[}\mathtt{local}\texttt{]})\texttt{@}3\end{array}

Finally, each client outputs the sum of each sum of shares, yielding
the sum of secrets. The protocol is correct because the outputs are all the
true sum of secrets, and it is secure because no more information about the
secrets other than that revealed by their sum is exposed.

\begin{array}[]{lll}\texttt{out}\texttt{@}1&:=&(\texttt{p[}1\texttt{]}+\texttt{p[}2\texttt{]}+\texttt{p[}3\texttt{]})\texttt{@}1\\
\texttt{out}\texttt{@}2&:=&(\texttt{p[}1\texttt{]}+\texttt{p[}2\texttt{]}+\texttt{p[}3\texttt{]})\texttt{@}2\\
\texttt{out}\texttt{@}3&:=&(\texttt{p[}1\texttt{]}+\texttt{p[}2\texttt{]}+\texttt{p[}3\texttt{]})\texttt{@}3\end{array}

3.
Security Model

MPC protocols are intended to implement some
ideal
functionality

\mathcal{F}
with per-client outputs. In the
\mathit{Overture}

setting, Given a protocol
\pi
that implements
\mathcal{F}
, with

\mathit{iovars}(\pi)=S\cup M\cup P\cup O
, the domain of
\mathcal{F}

is
\mathit{mems}(S)
and its range is
\mathit{mems}(O)
. Real/ideal security in the MPC
setting means that, given
\sigma\in\mathit{mems}(S)
, a secure protocol

\pi
does not reveal any more information about honest secrets

\sigma_{H}
to parties in
C
beyond what is implicitly declassified by

\mathcal{F}(\sigma)
. Security comes in
passive
and

malicious
flavors, wherein the adversary either follows the
rules or not, respectively. Characterization of both real world
protocol execution and simulation is defined
probabilistically. Following previous work

(
Barthe
et al., 2019
)
we use probability mass functions to
express joint dependencies between input and output variables, as a
metric of information leakage.

3.1.
Probability Mass Functions

We define discrete joint probability mass functions (pmfs) in a
standard manner but develop some notations that are useful for our
presentation. Firstly, whereas distributions typically map
realizations of random variables to values in
[0..1]
, for
convenience and following
(
Barthe
et al., 2019
)
we use
memories to represent realizations, so for example given a pmf
\mathit{P}

over variables
\{\texttt{s[}x\texttt{]}\texttt{@}1,\texttt{m[}y\texttt{]}\texttt{@}2\}
we write
\mathit{P}(\{\texttt{s[}x\texttt{]}\texttt{@}1\mapsto 0,\texttt{m[}y\texttt{]}\texttt{@}2\mapsto 1\})
to
denote the (joint) probability that
\texttt{s[}x\texttt{]}\texttt{@}1=0\wedge\texttt{m[}y\texttt{]}\texttt{@}2=1
. Recall from Section

2.2
that
\uplus
denotes the combination of
memories, so for example
\{\texttt{s[}x\texttt{]}\texttt{@}1\mapsto 0\}\uplus\{\texttt{m[}y\texttt{]}\texttt{@}2\mapsto 1\}=\{\texttt{s[}x\texttt{]}\texttt{@}1\mapsto 0,\texttt{m[}y\texttt{]}\texttt{@}2\mapsto 1\}
.

Definition 3.1.

A
probability mass function

\mathit{P}
is a function
mapping memories in
\mathit{mems}(X)
for given variables
X
to
values in
\mathbb{R}
such that:

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathit{mems}(X)}\mathit{P}(\sigma)\ =\ 1

Now, we can define a notion of marginal and conditional
distributions as follows, which are standard for discrete
probability mass functions.

Definition 3.2.

Given
\mathit{P}
with
\mathrm{dom}(\mathit{P})=\mathit{mems}(X_{2})
, the
marginal distribution

of variables
X_{1}\subseteq X_{2}
in
\mathit{P}
is denoted
{\mathit{P}}_{X_{1}}
and defined as follows:

\forall\sigma\in\mathit{mems}(X_{1})\quad.\quad{\mathit{P}}_{X_{1}}(\sigma)\triangleq\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathit{mems}(X_{2}-X_{1})}\mathit{P}(\sigma\uplus\sigma^{\prime})

Definition 3.3.

Given
\mathit{P}
, the
conditional distribution

of
X_{1}
given
X_{2}
where
X_{1}\cup X_{2}\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(\mathit{P})
and
X_{1}\cap X_{2}=\varnothing

is denoted
\mathit{P}_{({X_{1}}|{X_{2}})}
and defined as follows:

\forall\sigma\in\mathit{mems}(X_{1}\cup X_{2})\ .\ \mathit{P}_{({X_{1}}|{X_{2}})}(\sigma)\triangleq\begin{cases}0\text{\ if\ }{\mathit{P}}_{X_{2}}(\sigma_{X_{2}})=0\\
{\mathit{P}}_{X_{1}\cup X_{2}}(\sigma)/{\mathit{P}}_{X_{2}}(\sigma_{X_{2}})\ \text{\ o.w.}\end{cases}

We also define some convenient syntactic sugarings. The first will allow us to
compare marginal distributions under different realization conditions
(as in, e.g., Definition
4.1
), the others are standard.

Definition 3.4.

Given
\mathit{P}
, for all
\sigma_{1}\in\mathit{mems}(X_{1})
and
\sigma_{2}\in\mathit{mems}(X_{2})
define:

(1)

\mathit{P}_{({X_{1}}|{\sigma_{2}})}(\sigma_{1})\triangleq\mathit{P}_{({X_{1}}|{X_{2}})}(\sigma_{1}\uplus\sigma_{2})

(2)

\mathit{P}(\sigma_{1})\triangleq{\mathit{P}}_{X_{1}}(\sigma_{1})

(3)

\mathit{P}(\sigma_{1}|\sigma_{2})\triangleq\mathit{P}_{({X_{1}}|{X_{2}})}(\sigma_{1}\uplus\sigma_{2})

3.2.
Basic Distribution of a Protocol

Now we can define the probability distribution of a program
\pi
,
that we denote
\mathrm{BD}(\pi)
. Since
\mathit{Overture}
is deterministic the
results of any run are determined by the input values together with
the random tape. And since we constrain programs to not overwrite
views, we are assured that
final
memories contain both a
complete record of all initial secrets as well as views resulting from
communicated information.

Our semantics require that random tapes contain values for all program
values
\texttt{r[}w\texttt{]}\texttt{@}\iota
sampled from a uniform distribution
over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
. Input memories also contain input secret values
and possibly other initial view elements as a result of
pre-processing, e.g., Beaver triples for efficient multiplication,
and/or MACed share distributions as in BDOZ/SPDZ

(
Evans et al., 2018
;
Orsini, 2021
)
. We define

\mathit{runs}(\pi)
as the set of final memories resulting from execution
of
\pi
given any initial memory, and treat all elements of

\mathit{runs}(\pi)
as equally likely. This establishes the basic program
distribution that can be marginalized and conditioned to quantify
input/output information dependencies.

Definition 3.5.

Given
\pi
with
\mathit{secrets}(\pi)=S
and
\mathit{rands}(\pi)=R
and
pre-processing predicate
\mathit{preproc}
on memories, define:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathit{runs}(\pi)\triangleq\\
\{\sigma\mid\exists\sigma_{1}\in\mathit{mems}(R).\exists\sigma_{2}.\mathit{preproc}(\sigma_{2})\wedge(\sigma_{1}\uplus\sigma_{2},\pi)\xrightarrow{}^{*}(\sigma,\varnothing)\}\end{array}

By default,
\mathit{preproc}(\sigma)\iff\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)=S
, i.e.,
the initial memory contains all input secrets in a uniform
marginal distribution. Then the
basic distribution of
\pi
, written
\mathrm{BD}(\pi)
, is
defined such that for all
\sigma\in\mathit{mems}(\mathit{iovars}(\pi)\cup R)
:

\mathrm{BD}(\pi)(\sigma)=1/|\mathit{runs}(\pi)|\ \text{if}\ \sigma\in\mathit{runs}(\pi),\text{otherwise}\ 0

3.3.
Honest and Corrupt Views

Information about honest secrets can be revealed to corrupt clients
through messages sent from honest to corrupt clients, and through
publicly broadcast information from honest clients. Dually,
corrupt clients can impact protocol integrity through the messages
sent from corrupt to honest clients, and through publicly broadcast information
from corrupt clients. We call the former
corrupt views
, and
the latter
honest views
. Generally we let
V
range over sets
of views.

Definition 3.6 (Corrupt and Honest Views).

We let
V
range over
views
which are sets of messages
and reveals. Given a program
\pi
with
\mathit{iovars}(\pi)=S\cup M\cup P\cup O
,
define
\mathit{views}(\pi)\triangleq M\cup P
, and define
V_{H\rhd C}
as
the messages and reveals in
V=M\cup P
sent from honest to corrupt
parties, called
corrupt views
:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}V_{H\rhd C}&\triangleq&\{\ \texttt{p[}w\texttt{]}\mid\ \texttt{p[}w\texttt{]}:=\varepsilon\texttt{@}\iota\in\pi\wedge\iota\in H\ \}\ \cup\\
&&\{\ \texttt{m[}w\texttt{]}\texttt{@}\iota\ \mid\ \texttt{m[}w\texttt{]}\texttt{@}\iota:=\varepsilon\texttt{@}\iota^{\prime}\in\pi\wedge\iota\in C\wedge\iota^{\prime}\in H\ \}\end{array}

and similarly define
V_{C\rhd H}
as the subset of
V
sent from corrupt to honest
parties, called
honest views
:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}V_{C\rhd H}&\triangleq&\{\ \texttt{p[}w\texttt{]}\mid\ \texttt{p[}w\texttt{]}:=\varepsilon\texttt{@}\iota\in\pi\wedge\iota\in C\ \}\ \cup\\
&&\{\ \texttt{m[}w\texttt{]}\texttt{@}\iota\ \mid\ \texttt{m[}w\texttt{]}\texttt{@}\iota:=\varepsilon\texttt{@}\iota^{\prime}\in\pi\wedge\iota\in H\wedge\iota^{\prime}\in C\ \}\end{array}

3.4.
Passive Correctness and Security

In the passive setting we assume that
H
and
C
follow the
rules of protocols and share messages as expected. A first
consideration is whether a given protocol is
correct

with respect to an ideal functionality.

Definition 3.7 (Passive Correctness).

We say that a protocol
\pi
is
passive correct
for a functionality

\mathcal{F}
iff for all
\sigma\in\mathit{mems}(\mathit{secrets}(\pi))

we have
\mathrm{BD}(\pi)(\mathcal{F}(\sigma)\mid\sigma)=1
.

In the passive setting the simulator must construct a probabilistic
algorithm
\mathrm{Sim}
, aka a
simulation
, that is parameterized by
corrupt inputs and the output of an ideal functionality, and that
returns a reconstruction of corrupt views that is probabilistically
indistinguishable from the corrupt views in the real world protocol
execution.

Definition 3.8.

Given
\sigma
, and
v
,we write
\mathrm{Pr}(\mathrm{Sim}(\sigma,v)=\sigma^{\prime})

to denote the probability that
\mathrm{Sim}(\sigma,v)
returns corrupt views

\sigma^{\prime}
as a result. We write
\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{Sim}(\sigma,v))
to
denote the distribution of corrupt views reconstructed by the
simulation, where for
all
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathit{mems}(V)
:

\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{Sim}(\sigma,v))(\sigma^{\prime})\ \triangleq\ \mathrm{Pr}(\mathrm{Sim}(\sigma,v)=\sigma^{\prime})

Then we can define passive security in the real/ideal
model as follows.

Definition 3.9 (Passive Security).

Assume given a program
\pi
that correctly implements an ideal
functionality
\mathcal{F}
, with
\mathit{views}(\pi)=V
. Then
\pi

is
passive secure in the simulator model
iff there exists
a simulation
\mathrm{Sim}
such that for all
partitions of the federation into honest and corrupt sets
H
and
C

and for all
\sigma\in\mathit{mems}(\mathit{secrets}(\pi))
:

\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{Sim}(\sigma_{C},\mathcal{F}(\sigma)))=\mathrm{BD}(\pi)_{({V_{H\rhd C}}|{\sigma})}

3.5.
Malicious Security

In the malicious model we assume that corrupt clients are in
thrall to an adversary
\mathcal{A}
who does not necessarily follow
the rules of the protocol. We model this by positing a
\mathit{rewrite}_{\mathcal{A}}

function which is given a corrupt memory
\sigma_{C}
and expression

\varepsilon
, and returns a rewritten expression that can be interpreted to
yield a corrupt input. We define the evaluation relation that
incorporates the adversary in Figure
3
.

\begin{array}[]{rclr}(\sigma,x:=\varepsilon\texttt{@}\iota;\pi)&\xrightarrow{}_{\mathcal{A}}&(\sigma\{x\mapsto\llbracket\sigma,\varepsilon\rrbracket_{\iota}\},\pi)&\iota\in H\\
(\sigma,x:=\varepsilon\texttt{@}\iota;\pi)&\xrightarrow{}_{\mathcal{A}}&(\sigma\{x\mapsto\llbracket\mathit{rewrite}_{\mathcal{A}}(\sigma_{C},\varepsilon)\rrbracket_{\iota}\},\pi)&\iota\in C\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{rcl@{\qquad}r}(\sigma,\texttt{assert(}\phi(\varepsilon)\texttt{)}\texttt{@}\iota;\pi)&\xrightarrow{}_{\mathcal{A}}&(\sigma,\pi)&\text{if\ }\phi(\llbracket\sigma,\varepsilon\rrbracket_{\iota})\text{\ or\ }\iota\in C\\
(\sigma,\texttt{assert(}\phi(\varepsilon)\texttt{)}\texttt{@}\iota;\pi)&\xrightarrow{}_{\mathcal{A}}&(\sigma,\varnothing)&\text{if\ }\neg\phi(\sigma,\llbracket\sigma,\varepsilon\rrbracket_{\iota})\end{array}

Figure 3.
Adversarial semantics, and semantics of
assert
.

A key technical distinction of the malicious setting is that it
typically incorporates “abort”. Honest parties implement strategies
to detect rule-breaking– aka
cheating
– by using, e.g.,
message authentication codes with semi-homomorphic properties as in
BDOZ/SPDZ
(
Orsini, 2021
)
. If cheating is detected,
the protocol is aborted. To model this, we extend
\mathit{Overture}
with an

assert
command and extend the range of memories with

\bot
. Note that the adversary is free to ignore their own
assertions.

Definition 3.10.

We add assertions of the form
\texttt{assert(}\phi(\varepsilon)\texttt{)}\texttt{@}\iota
to the command
syntax of
\mathit{Overture}
, where
\phi
is a decidable predicate on

\mathbb{F}_{p}
and with operational semantics given in Figure

3
. We also extend the range of memories
\sigma

to
\mathbb{F}_{p}\cup\{\bot\}
.

It is necessary to add
\bot
to the range of memories since
the possibility of abort needs to be reflected in adversarial
runs of a protocol. We can define
\mathit{runs}_{\mathcal{A}}(\pi)

as the “prefix” memories that result from possibly-aborting
protocols, but we also need to “pad out” the memories
of partial runs with
\bot
, as we define in
\mathit{runs}_{\mathcal{A},\bot}(\pi)
,
to properly reflect the contents of views and outputs even in case of abort.

Definition 3.11.

Given program
\pi
with
\mathit{iovars}(\pi)=S\cup V\cup O
and
\mathit{rands}(\pi)=R
, and
any assumed pre-processing predicate
\mathit{preproc}
on memories, define:

\begin{array}[]{c}\mathit{runs}_{\mathcal{A}}(\pi)\triangleq\\
\{\sigma\mid\exists\sigma_{1}\in\mathit{mems}(R).\exists\sigma_{2}.\mathit{preproc}(\sigma_{2})\wedge(\sigma_{1}\uplus\sigma_{2},\pi)\xrightarrow{}^{*}_{\mathcal{A}}(\sigma,\varnothing)\}\end{array}

where by default,
\mathit{preproc}(\sigma)\iff\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)=S
, and also define the following
which pads out undefined views and outputs with
\bot
:

\begin{array}[]{l}\mathit{runs}_{\mathcal{A},\bot}(\pi)\triangleq\\
\qquad\{\sigma\{x_{1}\mapsto\bot,\ldots,x_{n}\mapsto\bot\}\mid\\
\qquad\phantom{\{}\sigma\in\mathit{runs}_{\mathcal{A}}(\pi)\wedge\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}=(V\cup O)-\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)\}\end{array}

Then the
\mathcal{A}
distribution of
\pi
, written
\mathrm{BD}(\pi,\mathcal{A})
, is
defined such that for all
\sigma\in\mathit{mems}(\mathit{iovars}(\pi)\cup R)
:

\mathrm{BD}(\pi,\mathcal{A})(\sigma)=1/|\mathit{runs}_{\mathcal{A},\bot}(\pi)|\ \text{if}\ \sigma\in\mathit{runs}_{\mathcal{A},\bot}(\pi),\text{otherwise}\ 0

Given this preamble, we can define malicious simulation and malicious security
in a standard manner
(
Evans et al., 2018
)
, as follows.

Definition 3.12 (Malicious Simulation).

Given a protocol
\pi
with
\mathit{iovars}(\pi)=S\cup V\cup O
, honest and corrupt
clients
H
and
C
, adversary
\mathcal{A}
, and honest inputs

\sigma\in\mathit{mems}(S_{H})
, the
malicious simulation

\mathrm{Sim}(\sigma)
has three phases:

(1)

In the first phase
\mathrm{Sim}_{1}
,
\mathcal{A}
gives the
simulator some
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathit{mems}(S_{C})
, and the simulator consults an
oracle to compute
\mathcal{F}(\sigma\uplus\sigma^{\prime})\in\mathit{mems}(O)
.

(2)

In the second phase
\mathrm{Sim}_{2}
, the simulator is given the corrupt
outputs
\mathcal{F}(\sigma\uplus\sigma^{\prime})_{C}
, which are again given to

\mathcal{A}
, who decides either to abort or not. If so, then the
simulator is given
\sigma_{\mathit{out}}\triangleq\{\texttt{out}\texttt{@}\iota\mapsto\bot\mid\iota\in H\}

and arbitrary internal state
\varsigma
.
Otherwise the simulator is given
\sigma_{\mathit{out}}\triangleq\mathcal{F}(\sigma\uplus\sigma^{\prime})_{H}

and
\varsigma
.

(3)

In the third phase
\mathrm{Sim}_{3}
, given
\sigma_{\mathit{out}}
and
\varsigma
, the simulator
finally outputs

\sigma_{\mathit{out}}\uplus\sigma_{\mathit{views}}
for some
calculated
\sigma_{\mathit{views}}\in\mathit{mems}(V_{H\rhd C})
.

Definition 3.13 (Malicious Security).

We write
\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{Sim}(\sigma))
to
denote the distribution of honest outputs and corrupt views reconstructed by the
malicious simulation, where for
all
\sigma^{\prime}
:

\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{Sim}(\sigma))(\sigma^{\prime})\ \triangleq\ \mathrm{Pr}(\mathrm{Sim}(\sigma)=\sigma^{\prime})

Then a protocol
\pi
with
\mathit{iovars}(\pi)=S\cup V\cup O
is
malicious
secure
iff for all
H
,
C
,
\mathcal{A}
, and
\sigma\in\mathit{mems}(S_{H})
:

\mathrm{D}(\mathrm{Sim}(\sigma))=\mathrm{BD}(\pi,\mathcal{A})_{({V_{H\rhd C}\cup O_{H}}|{\sigma})}

4.
Security Hyperproperties

In this Section we formulate probabilistic versions of well-studied
hyperproperties of confidentiality and integrity, including
noninterference, gradual release, declassification, and robust
declassification. We follow nomenclature developed in previous work
on characterizing declassification policies in deterministic settings

(
Sabelfeld and
Sands, 2009
)
, but adapt them to our
probabilistic one.

4.1.
Conditional Noninterference

Since MPC protocols release some information about secrets through
outputs of
\mathcal{F}
, they do not enjoy strict noninterference. As
discussed in Section
2
, public reveals and protocol
outputs are fundamentally forms of declassification. But consistent
with other work
(
Haagh et al., 2018
)
, we can formulate a version of
probabilistic noninterference conditioned on output that is sound
for passive security.

Definition 4.1 (Noninterference modulo output).

We say that a program
\pi
with
\mathit{iovars}(\pi)=S\cup V\cup O

satisfies
noninterference modulo output

iff for all
H
and
C
and
\sigma_{1}\in\mathit{mems}(S_{C}\cup O)
and
\sigma_{2}\in\mathit{mems}(V_{H\rhd C})

we have:

\mathrm{BD}(\pi)_{({S_{H}}|{\sigma_{1}})}=\mathrm{BD}(\pi)_{({S_{H}}|{\sigma_{1}\uplus\sigma_{2}})}

This conditional noninterference property implies that
corrupt views give the adversary no better chance of guessing honest
secrets than just the output and corrupt inputs do. So the simulator
can just arbitrarily pick any honest secrets that could have produced
the given outputs and run the protocol in simulation to reconstruct
real world corrupt views. This requires that the simulator can
tractably “pre-image” a given output of a functionality
\mathcal{F}
,
to determine the inputs that could have produced it. This equivalence
class is called a
kernel
in recent work
(
Hunt
et al., 2023
)
.

Definition 4.2.

Given a functionality
\mathcal{F}
and outputs
\sigma_{\mathit{out}}
, their

kernel
, denoted
\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\sigma_{\mathit{out}})
is

\{\sigma\ |\ \mathcal{F}(\sigma)=\sigma_{\mathit{out}}\}
.
We say that
\mathcal{F}
is
pre-imageable
iff
\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\sigma_{\mathit{out}})
for all

\sigma_{\mathit{out}}
can be computed tractably.

A soundness result for passive security can then be given as follows.
It is essentially the same as “perfect passive NI security” explored
in previous work
(
Haagh et al., 2018
)
.

Theorem 4.3.

Assume given pre-imageable
\mathcal{F}
and a protocol
\pi
that
correctly implements
\mathcal{F}
. If
\pi
satisfies noninterference modulo output
then
\pi
is passive secure.

4.2.
Gradual Release

Probabilistic noninterference is related to perfect secrecy and is
preserved by components of cryptographic protocols generally. It can
be expressed using probabilistic independence, aka separation,

(
Darais
et al., 2019
;
Barthe
et al., 2019
)
, and we adopt the
following notation to express independence:

Definition 4.4.

We write
\mathit{P}\vdash X_{1}*X_{2}
iff for all

\sigma\in\mathit{mems}(X_{1}\cup X_{2})
we have

{\mathit{P}}_{X_{1}\cup X_{2}}(\sigma)=\mathit{P}(\sigma_{X_{1}})*\mathit{P}(\sigma_{X_{2}})

In practice, MPC protocols typically satisfy a
gradual
release
property
(
Sabelfeld and
Sands, 2009
)
, where messages
exchanged remain probabilistically separable from secrets, with only
declassification events (reveals and outputs) releasing information
about honest secrets. A key difference is that while these
declassification events essentially define the policy in gradual
release, the ideal functionality sets the release policy for MPC
passive security, so its necessary to show that declassification
events respect these bounds.

Definition 4.5.

Given
H,C
, a protocol
\pi
with
\mathit{iovars}(\pi)=S\cup M\cup P\cup O

satisfies
gradual release
iff

\mathrm{BD}(\pi)\vdash M_{C}*S_{H}
.

4.3.
Integrity and Robust Declassification

Integrity
is an important hyperproperty in security models that admit
malicious adversaries. Consistent with formulations in deterministic settings,
we have already defined protocol confidentiality as the preservation of low equivalence
(of secrets and views), and now we define protocol integrity as the preservation
of high equivalence (of secrets and views). Intuitively, this property says
that any adversarial strategy either “mimics” a passive strategy with some
choice of inputs or causes an abort.

Definition 4.6 (Integrity).

We say that a protocol
\pi
with
\mathit{iovars}(\pi)=S\cup V\cup O
has

integrity
iff for all
H
,
C
, and
\mathcal{A}
,
if
\sigma\in\mathit{runs}_{\mathcal{A}}(\pi)

then there exists
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathit{mems}(S)
with
\sigma_{S_{H}}=\sigma^{\prime}_{S_{H}}
and:

\mathrm{BD}(\pi,\mathcal{A})_{({X}|{\sigma_{S_{H}\cup V_{C\rhd H}}})}=\mathrm{BD}(\pi)_{({X}|{\sigma^{\prime}})}

where
X\triangleq(V_{H\rhd C}\cup O_{H})\cap\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)
.

A first important observation is that integrity preserves protocol correctness
for honest outputs, except for the possibility of abort.

Lemma 4.7.

If a protocol
\pi
with
\mathit{iovars}(\pi)=S\cup V\cup O
is passive correct for

\mathcal{F}
and
has integrity, then for all
H
,
C
,
\mathcal{A}
,
\sigma_{1}\in\mathit{mems}(S_{H})
,

\texttt{out}\texttt{@}\iota\in(O_{H})
, and
\nu\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
, if:

\mathrm{BD}(\pi,\mathcal{A})(\{\texttt{out}\texttt{@}\iota\mapsto\nu\}\mid\sigma_{1})>0

then exists
\sigma_{2}\in\mathit{mems}(S_{C})
such that:

\mathcal{F}(\sigma_{1}\uplus\sigma_{2})(\texttt{out}\texttt{@}\iota)=\nu

The following result establishes that integrity implies malicious
security for protocols that are passive secure (which also subsumes
correctness).

Theorem 4.8.

If a protocol is passive secure and has integrity, then it
is malicious secure.

Proof.

Let
\pi
be some protocol with passive security and integrity
where
\mathit{iovars}(\pi)=S\cup V\cup O
, and let
\mathcal{A}
be some
adversary. Suppose
\sigma\in\mathit{runs}_{\mathcal{A}}(\pi)
.
As integrity requires, and as Lemma
4.7

demonstrates with respect to outputs, the most the adversary can do
in the presence of integrity is to elicit the same responses from
the honest parties– via the strategy
\sigma_{V_{C\rhd H}}
– as
are elicited from some passive run of the protocol using
some
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathit{mems}(S)
where
\sigma^{\prime}_{H}=\sigma_{S_{H}}
,
and perhaps to force an abort after some number of message
exchanges.

Therefore, in simulation,
\mathcal{A}
can provide the simulator
with some
\sigma^{\prime}_{C}
in
\mathrm{Sim}_{1}
which its strategy is “impersonating”,
allowing

In a malicious setting, “detecting cheating” by adding
information-theoretic secure MAC codes to shares is a fundamental
technique pioneered in systems such as BDOZ and SPDZ

(
Damgård and
Orlandi, 2010
;
Damgård et al., 2012
;
Bendlin et al., 2011
;
Orsini, 2021
)

assume a pre-processing phase that distributes shares with MAC codes
to clients. This integrates well with pre-processed Beaver Triples to
implement malicious secure, and relatively efficient, multiplication

(
Evans et al., 2018
)
. Recall that Beaver triples are values
a,b,c
with

\llbracket a*b\rrbracket=c
, unique per multiplication gate, that are secret
shared with clients during pre-processing. Here we consider the
2-party version.

A field value
v
is secret shared among 2 clients in BDOZ in the same
manner as in GMW, but with the addition of a separate MAC values. Each
client
\iota

(v_{\iota},m_{\iota})

v_{\iota}

is a share of
v
reconstructed by addition, i.e.,
v=\llbracket v_{1}+v_{2}\rrbracket
, and
m_{\iota}
is a MAC of
v_{\iota}

m_{\iota}=k+(k_{\Delta}*v_{\iota})
where
keys

k
and

k_{\Delta}
are known only to
\iota^{\prime}\neq\iota
and
k_{\Delta}
. The

local key

k
is unique per MAC, while the
global key

k_{\Delta}
is common to all MACs authenticated by
\iota^{\prime}

semi-homomorphic encryption scheme that supports addition of shares
and multiplication of shares by a constant– for more details the
reader is referred to Orsini
(
Orsini, 2021
)

7.3.
Cheating Detection and Integrity

We can carry out similar proofs of passive security for the protocol
in Figure
8
as for GMW, even using automated tactics
for the protocol in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. But in the case of BDOZ we are
also concerned with malicious security. To demonstrate this, we can
show that the protocol satisfies integrity in the sense of Definition

4.6
. To do so, we observe that it satisfies a stronger
property, that we call cheating detection. Cheating detection says
that the adversary can only execute the protocol honestly, or or else
gets caught (and abort). Note in particular that this is
not

a form of endorsement (the dual of declassification), which allows
the adversary some leeway. Rather, it is a confirmation of high integrity.

Focusing in, we identify the
adversarial inputs
as the messages
sent from the adversary to honest parties on which honest responses
to the adversary may depend. We want to say that these are the messages
that must be legitimate.

Definition 7.8.

Given
\pi
with
\mathit{iovars}(\pi)=S\cup V\cup O
,
let
X_{H}\subseteq\{x\mid x\in(V_{H\rhd C}\cup O_{H})\wedge x\in\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)\}
.
Then the
adversarial inputs to
X_{H}
is the least set

X_{C}\subseteq V_{C\rhd H}
such that
\mathrm{BD}(\pi)\not\vdash X_{C}*X_{H}
.

Now, we can characterize protocols with cheating detection as those where
the adversary can only behave honestly/passively, or else cause abort.

Definition 7.9 (Cheating Detection).

Cheating is detectable
in
\pi
with
\mathit{iovars}(\pi)=S\cup V\cup O
iff
for all
\sigma\in\mathit{runs}_{\mathcal{A}}(\pi)
,
letting
X_{H}=\{x\mid x\in(V_{H\rhd C}\cup O_{H})\wedge x\in\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)\}
,
and letting
X_{C}
be the adversarial inputs to
X_{H}
,
there exists
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathit{runs}(\pi)

with
\sigma_{X_{C}}=\sigma^{\prime}_{X_{C}}
.

It is straightforward to demonstrate that cheating detection has integrity,
since only the “passive” adversary can elicit a response from honest parties.

Lemma 7.10.

If cheating is detectable in
\pi
, then
\pi
has integrity.

In the case of BDOZ, cheating detection is accomplished by the information-theoretic
security of the encryption scheme
(
Evans et al., 2018
)
. Furthermore, the symmetry of
the protocol in Figure
8
ensures that both parties will authenticate
shares, so it is robust to corruption of either party.

8.
Conclusion and Future Work

The
\mathit{Prelude}/\mathit{Overture}
language model developed in this paper, along
with the suite of hyperproperties explored, are a foundation
for defining low-level MPC protocols and exploring methods for security
verification. There are several promising avenues for future
work. Concurrency will be important to capture common MPC idioms such
as commitment and circuit optimizations, and to consider the UC
security model
(
Evans et al., 2018
;
Acay
et al., 2024
)
. To support better
verification, integration of our tactics with a proof assistant is a
compelling direction that resonate with previous work
(
Haagh et al., 2018
)
.

Better automated verification is a main goal for future work.
Security types have been effectively leveraged for high-level
programs, for both passive and malicious security. This suggests that
they can be similarly applied at low levels in a way that is
complementary to high-level systems. Generalizing our automated
analysis in
\mathbb{F}_{2}
to larger arithmetic fields is also a
compelling technical challenge with potential practical benefits,
e.g., application to arithmetic circuits.
