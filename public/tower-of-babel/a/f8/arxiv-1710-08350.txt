Algebra, coalgebra, and minimization in polynomial differential equations
Michele Boreale

Abstract
. We consider reasoning and minimization in systems of polynomial ordinary differential equations ( ode ’s). The ring of multivariate polynomials is employed as a syntax for denoting system behaviours. We endow this set with a transition system structure based on the concept of Lie derivative , thus inducing a notion of ℒ \mathcal{L} - bisimulation . We prove that two states (variables) are ℒ \mathcal{L} -bisimilar if and only if they correspond to the same solution in the ode ’s system. We then characterize ℒ \mathcal{L} -bisimilarity algebraically, in terms of certain ideals in the polynomial ring that are invariant under Lie-derivation. This characterization allows us to develop a complete algorithm, based on building an ascending chain of ideals, for computing the largest ℒ \mathcal{L} -bisimulation containing all valid identities that are instances of a user-specified template. A specific largest ℒ \mathcal{L} -bisimulation can be used to build a reduced system of ode ’s, equivalent to the original one, but minimal among all those obtainable by linear aggregation of the original equations. A computationally less demanding approximate reduction and linearization technique is also proposed.

Introduction

The past few years have
witnessed a surge of interest in computational models based on

equations (

[

), and hybrid systems (e.g.

[

,

).

From a computational point of view, our motivation to study

’s
arises from the following problems.

(1)

Reasoning
: provide methods to automatically prove and discover identities
involving the system variables.

(2)

Reduction
: provide methods to automatically reduce, and possibly minimize,
the number of variables
and equations of a system, in such a way that the reduced system
retains all the relevant information of the original one.

Reasoning may help an expert (a chemist, a biologist, an engineer)
to prove or to disprove certain system properties, even before
actually solving, simulating or realizing the system. Often, the
identities of interest take the form of

conservation laws
. For instance, chemical reactions often
enjoy a mass conservation law, stating that the sum of the concentrations of two or more chemical species,
is a constant. More generally, one would like tools to automatically

discover
all laws of a given form. Pragmatically,
before actually solving or simulating a given system, it can be critical
to reduce the system to a size that can be handled by a
solver or a simulator.

Our goal is showing that these issues can be dealt with by a mix of algebraic and
coalgebraic techniques. We will consider
initial value

problems, specified by a system of
ode
’s of the form
\dot{x}_{i}=f_{i}(x_{1},...,x_{N})
, for
i=1,...,N
, plus initial conditions. The

case where the drifts are multivariate polynomials in the variables

x_{1},..,x_{N}
. Practically, the majority of functions found in
applications is in, or can be encoded into this format (possibly
under restrictions on the initial conditions), including
exponential, trigonometric, logarithmic and rational functions.

A more detailed account of our work follows. We introduce the ring
of multivariate polynomials as a syntax for denoting the

induced by the given initial value problem
(Section
2
). In other words, a behaviour is any
polynomial combination of the individual components

(

We then endow the polynomial ring with a transition system, based on a purely syntactic notion of

(Section

In particular, any two variables

are

the corresponding solutions are the same,

(this generalizes to polynomial behaviours as expected).
This way, one can prove identities between two behaviours, for instance conservation laws, by
exhibiting bisimulations containing the given
pair
. The resulting proof method is greatly enhanced by introducing a
polynomial version of the

technique of
[
36

In order to turn this method into a fully automated proof procedure,
we first characterize

in the polynomial ring that are invariant under Lie-derivation (Section

This characterization leads to an algorithm that, given a user-specified template,
returns the set of all its instances that are valid identities in the system
(Section
5

conservation laws of the system involving terms up to a given degree.
The algorithm implies building an ascending chain of ideals

[
20
]
.
The output of the algorithm is in turn essential to build a
reduced system of

’s, equivalent to the original one,
but featuring a

number of equations and variables, in the class of systems that can be
obtained by linear aggregation from the original one (Section

6

A computationally less demanding

alternative, because it is entirely based on linear algebraic,
hence efficient,
techniques, and produces ‘small’ linear systems. In particular,

are sufficient to guarantee an approximation within

of the original system.
We then illustrate
the results of some simple experiments we have conducted using
a prototype implementation (in Python) of our
algorithms (Section
8

ode
’s
by Cardelli et al.
[
14

and to work in the area of hybrid systems.
We discuss this and other related work, as well as some possible directions for future work, in the concluding section
(Section

In the interest of readability, some proofs and technical
material have been confined to a separate appendix (Appendix

To sum up, we give the following contributions.

(1)

A complete bisimulation-based proof technique to reason on
the polynomial behaviours induced by a system of
ode
’s.

(2)

An algorithm to find all the valid
polynomial identities induced by the given system and fitting a
user-specified template.

(3)

An algorithm to build a reduced, equivalent system that is minimal in the class of all
linear aggregations of the original system.

(4)

An algorithm to build a small linear system, approximating within
O(t^{m})
the original system.

3.
Coalgebraic semantics of polynomial

In this section we show how to endow the
polynomial ring with a transition relation structure, hence giving

We recall that a
(Moore) coalgebra

[

s\,R\,t

(a)

, and (b)
\delta(s)\,R\,\delta(t)

consequence of the general theory of bisimulation that a largest
bisimulation over
S

\sim
,
exists, is the union of all bisimulation relations, and is an
equivalence relation over
S

Given an initial value problem
\mathbf{\Phi}=(F,v_{0})
of the form
(
1

\mathbb{R}[\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}]
is the set of states; (2)

transition function; and (3)

is
the output function. Note that this definition of coalgebra
is merely syntactic, and does not presuppose anything about the
solution of the given initial value problem. When the standard
definition of bisimulation over coalgebras is instantiated to

C_{\mathbf{\Phi}}

{defi}

[

5.
Computing invariants

By Theorem

In the sequel, we will make use of the
following two basic facts about ideals, for whose proof we refer

[

(1)

(2)

The

The ideal membership will be further discussed later on in the section.

The main idea is introduced by the naive algorithm presented
below.

5.1.
A naive algorithm

Suppose we want to decide whether

easy to devise an algorithm that computes the smallest invariant

\big\langle\,\,\cup_{j=0}^{k}\pi_{j}[V]\,\,\big\rangle=\big\langle\,\,\cup_{j=0}^{k}\pi_{j}[B]\,\,\big\rangle
.

Now let
B_{i}
be a finite basis of
V_{i}
, which can be easily built
from the matrix
T_{i}
. By the previous lemma,
\cup_{j=1}^{i}\pi^{(j)}[B_{i}]
is a
finite
set of generators for
J_{i}
: this
solves the representation problem. Concerning the termination
condition, we note that, after checking that actually
V_{i}=V_{i+1}
,
checking
J_{i}=J_{i+1}
reduces to checking that

\displaystyle\pi^{(i+1)}[B_{i}]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\big\langle\,\,\cup_{j=0}^{i}\pi^{(j)}[B_{i}]\,\,\big\rangle\;=\;J_{i}\,.

(19)

To check this inclusion, one can apply standard computer
algebra techniques. For example, one can check if
\pi^{(i+1)}[b]\in J_{i}
for each
b\in B_{i}
, thus solving
|B_{i}|
ideal membership
problems, for one and the same ideal
J_{i}
. As already discussed, this
presupposes the computation of a Gröbner basis for
J_{i}
, a
potentially expensive operation. One advantage of the above
algorithm, over methods proposed in program analysis with
termination conditions based on testing ideal membership (e.g.

[
32
]
),
is that (
19
) is not checked at every iteration, but only when
V_{i+1}=V_{i}

(the latter a relatively inexpensive check). In the Appendix (Subsection
A.2
), we also discuss a
sufficient condition which does not involve Gröbner bases, but
leads to an incomplete algorithm.

{exa}

Consider the initial value problem of Example
2
and
the template
\pi=a_{1}x+a_{2}y+a_{3}z+a_{4}w
. We run the double chain
algorithm with this system and template as inputs. In what follows,

v=(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4})^{T}
will denote a generic vector in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
.
Recall that
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}=(x,y,z,w)^{T}
and
v_{0}=(0,0,1,1)^{T}
.

•

For each
v\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
:
\pi^{(0)}[v](v_{0})=(v_{1}x+v_{2}y+v_{3}z+v_{4}w)(v_{0})=0
if and only if
v\in V_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\{v\,:\,v_{3}=-v_{4}\}
.

•

For each
v\in V_{0}
:
\pi^{(1)}[v](v_{0})=(v_{1}xz+v_{1}z+v_{2}wy+v_{2}z+v_{4}w-v_{4}z)(v_{0})=0
if and only if
v\in V_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\{v\in V_{0}:v_{1}=-v_{2}\}
.

•

For each
v\in V_{1}
:
\pi^{(2)}[v](v_{0})=(v_{2}w^{2}y+v_{2}wy+v_{2}wz-v_{2}xz^{2}-v_{2}xz-v_{2}z^{2}+v_{4}w-v_{4}z)(v_{0})=0
if and only if
v\in V_{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}V_{1}
.

Being
V_{2}=V_{1}
, we also check if
J_{2}=J_{1}
. A basis of
V_{1}
is
B_{1}=\{b_{1},b_{2}\}

with
b_{1}=(-1,1,0,0)^{T}
and
b_{2}=(0,0,-1,1)^{T}
. According to (

),
we have therefore to check if, for
\ell=1,2
:

\displaystyle\pi^{(2)}[b_{\ell}]

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle J_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\big\langle\,\,\{\pi^{(0)}[b_{1}],\pi^{(0)}[b_{2}],\pi^{(1)}[b_{1}],\pi^{(1)}[b_{2}]\}\,\,\big\rangle\,.

With the help of a computer algebra system, one computes a Gröbner basis for
J_{1}
as
G_{1}=\{x-y,z-w\}
.
Then one can reduce
\pi^{(2)}[b_{1}]=w^{2}y+wy+wz-xz^{2}-xz-z^{2}
modulo

and obtain

\pi^{(2)}[b_{1}]=h_{1}(x-y)+h_{2}(z-w)
, with
h_{1}=-z^{2}-z
and

, thus proving that
\pi^{(2)}[b_{1}]\in J_{1}
. One proves similarly that

\pi^{(2)}[b_{2}]\in J_{1}
. This shows that

.

Hence the algorithm terminates with
m=1
and returns
(V_{1},J_{1})
, or,
concretely,
(B_{1},G_{1})
. In particular,

, or
equivalently
x(t)=y(t)
. Similarly for

.

Remark 9

(notational convention: result template)
.

According to Theorem

(a), given a template

and

, checking if

is

, which can be effectively done
knowing the basis
B_{m}
of

concretely returned by the

(another, equivalent possibility, is checking if

, which is built in the minimization phase,

In practice, it is more convenient
to represent the whole set
\pi[V]
returned by the algorithm more compactly, in terms of a

-parameters result template

such that
\pi^{\prime}[{\mathbb{R}}^{m}]=\pi[V]
. For instance, in the previous example, the result template

\displaystyle\pi^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{1}x-a_{1}y+a_{2}z-a_{2}w

, in the precise sense that

.

Remark 10

(linear systems)
.

Consider the case of a
linear
system, that is, when the drifts
f_{i}
in
F
are linear functions of the
x_{i}
’s. Consider
the chain
V_{0}\supseteq V_{1}\supseteq\cdots
in (

then the chain has stabilized, that is

for each

The resulting single chain algorithm boils down to the ‘refinement’ algorithm of

[

.

Remark 11
.

We end this section pointing out that the naive algorithm of
Subsection
5
.1 admits a generalization that works
with templates as well. Specifically, one can regard templates as
polynomials in both the variables
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}
and parameters
\mathbf{a}
,
and compute the invariant ideal in
{\mathbb{R}}[\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a}]

generated by all Lie derivatives of the given template

(further coniderations in the

In any case, the idea of the algorithm in Subsection

.2 is to avoid

6.
Minimization

We present a method for reducing the size of an initial value
problem. The resulting reduced problem, while
equivalent in a precise sense to the original problem, is

minimal
in terms of number of equations and variables, among
all systems that can be obtained by linear aggregations of the
original equations.

The method takes as an input the space
V_{m}
returned by the double chain algorithm in the preceding
section when fed with a certain linear template. The method
itself is quite simple and only
relies on simple linear algebraic operations that can be efficiently automated.

The basic idea is projecting the original
system of equations onto a suitably chosen subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
Consider the
linear template

\displaystyle\pi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{1}\cdot x_{1}+\cdots+a_{N}\cdot x_{N}

(20)

where the
a_{i}
’s are distinct parameters. By applying the
algorithm of the preceding section to this template, we obtain a
subspace
V\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}V_{m}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Consider now the orthogonal
complement of
V
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle

is the usual scalar product between vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and

v,w
denote generic vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
)

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}

\displaystyle V^{\bot}\;=\;\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\,:\,\langle w,v\rangle=0\text{ for each }v\in V\}\,.

We show that the trajectory
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)
lies entirely in
W
, that is

\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)\in W
for each
t
in the open interval of definition, say

D
, of the trajectory. Indeed,
by virtue of Theorem
7
(a), we have that
v\in V
if and only if (here
v=(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{N})^{T}
)

\begin{array}[]{rclclcl}\langle\mathrm{\mathbf{x}},v\rangle&=&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}x_{i}&=&\pi[v]&\in&\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{\Phi}}\,.\end{array}

By definition of
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathbf{\Phi}}
, this means that
v\in V
if and only if

\begin{array}[]{rclclcl}\langle\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t),v\rangle&=&\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}x_{i}(t)&=&\pi[v](t)&=&0\;\;\text{ for each }t\end{array}

that is
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)\in W
for each
t
in the open interval
D
of definition of
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)
. In other words,
v\in V
if and
only if
v
is orthogonal to each
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)
, for
t\in D
, or
V=\{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t):t\in D\}^{\bot}
. This is equivalent to the following crucial lemma.

Lemma 12
.

W=V^{\bot}=\mathrm{span}\{\,\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t):t\in D\,\}
.

The fact that the trajectory
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)
entirely lies in, and in fact generates, the subspace

W
, suggests that we can obtain a more economical representation of

\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)
by adopting a system of coordinates in this subspace. More formally, let
B
be any orthonormal basis of
W
. It
is convenient to represent
B
as a matrix of
l
independent
column vectors,
B=[b_{1}|\cdots|b_{l}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times l}
, where

l\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\dim(W)\leq N
(in fact,
l\leq m+1
as well; this is
discussed in the Appendix, Subsection
A.3
, where an
efficient method for building
B
out of the successive Lie
derivatives of
\pi
is explained). Recall that orthonormality means

B^{T}B=I_{l}
, with
I_{l}
the
l\times l
identity matrix. The
orthogonal projection of any

v
onto
W
has, w.r.t.
B
, coordinates
B^{T}v
, which is of course a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{l}
.
Define now

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(t)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}

\displaystyle B^{T}\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)\;\;\text{ for each }t\in D\,.

(21)

Since each
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)\in W
, each
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)
is a fixpoint of the
projection, so with the above definition we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle BB^{T}\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)

(22)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(t)\,.

From the last equation, it is easy to check that
\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(t)
is a
solution, hence the
unique
analytic solution in a suitable
interval, of the following
reduced
problem
\mathbf{\Psi}=(G,\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(0))
,
where
F
denotes the vector field of the original initial value
problem:

\mathbf{\Psi}:\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\dot{\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}}(t)&=&B^{T}F\left(B\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(t)\right)\\
\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(0)&=&B^{T}\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(0)\,.\end{array}\right.

(23)

In order to check that
\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(t)
as defined in (
21
)
satisfies the first equation of (
23
), observe that,
by definition we have:

\displaystyle\dot{\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B^{T}\dot{\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B^{T}F\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B^{T}F\left(B\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(t)\right)

where the last equality follows from (
22
). The second
equality of (
23
) is trivially seen to be true. Note
that the reduced system (
23
) features
l\leq N

differential equations. In particular, observe that the vector
field
G
of the reduced system is obtained by replacing
each variable
x_{i}
in the original
F

with a linear combination of the variables
y_{j}
’ s, as dictated by
B
, and
then linearly aggregating the resulting
N
terms, as dictated by

B^{T}
. As a consequence, the maximum degree in the reduced
G

does not exceed the maximum degree in the original
F
.

Equation (
22
) naturally extends to any polynomial
behaviour. That is, for any polynomial
p\in\mathbb{R}[\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}]
, we have

p(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t))=p\left(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)\right)=p\left(B\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(t)\right)
. In the
end, we have proven the following result, which shows that we can
exactly recover any behaviour induced by the original system from
the reduced system.

Theorem 13

(exact reduction)
.

Let
\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(t)
be the unique analytic solution of the (reduced)
problem (
23
). Then,
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)=B\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(t)
.
Moreover, for any polynomial behaviour
p(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t))
induced by the
original problem (
1
), we have

p(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t))=p\left(B\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(t)\right)
.

The reduced system is minimal among all systems obtained as linear
aggregations of the original system. In the next result, the
interpretation of the
k
-dimensional vector function
\mathrm{\mathbf{z}}(t)
is
that it may (but need not) arise as the solution of any system with

k
equations.

Theorem 14

(minimality)
.

Assume for some
N\times k
matrix
C
and vector function

\mathrm{\mathbf{z}}(t)
, we have
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)=C\mathrm{\mathbf{z}}(t)
, for each
t\in D
. Then
k\geq l
.

Proof 6.1
.

Assume
k\leq N
(otherwise there is nothing to prove). As the
trajectory
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)
spans
W
(Lemma
12
), which has
dimension
l
, we can form a rank
l
matrix
E
as

E=[\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t_{1})|\cdots|\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t_{l})]=[C\mathrm{\mathbf{z}}(t_{1})|\cdots|C\mathrm{\mathbf{z}}(t_{l})]=C[\mathrm{\mathbf{z}}(t_{1})|\cdots|\mathrm{\mathbf{z}}(t_{l})]
, for suitable points
t_{1},...,t_{l}
. As
in general
\mathrm{rk}(AB)\leq\min\{\mathrm{rk}(A),\mathrm{rk}(B)\}
, we have
\mathrm{rk}(C)\geq l
. But
k\geq\mathrm{rk}(C)
, which implies the thesis.

As a corollary of Theorem
13
, we obtain a further
characterization of
\mathcal{L}
-bisimilarity, which shows that we can
also reason syntactically on polynomial behaviours in terms of the
reduced system. In particular,
\mathcal{L}
-bisimilarity between pairs of
individual variables reduces to plain equality between the
corresponding rows of
B
, which allows one to easily form
equivalence classes of variables if desired. Let us denote by

G=(g_{1},...,g_{l})^{T}
the polynomial vector field of the reduced
system. Note that
G
is expressed in terms of the variables

\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}=(y_{1},...,y_{l})^{T}
, more precisely
G=B^{T}F(B\mathrm{\mathbf{y}})
.

Corollary 15
.

Let
p,q\in\mathbb{R}[\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}]
. Then
p\sim_{\mathbf{\Phi}}q
in
C_{\mathbf{\Phi}}
if and only if

p(B\mathrm{\mathbf{y}})\sim_{\mathbf{\Psi}}q(B\mathrm{\mathbf{y}})
in
C_{\mathbf{\Psi}}
. In particular,
x_{i}\sim_{\mathbf{\Phi}}x_{j}

in if and only if row
i
equals row
j
in
B
.

{exa}

Let us consider again the problem of Example
3
.
Recall from Example
5.4
that the algorithm for
computing invariants
stops with
m=1
returning
V_{1},J_{1}
. In particular,
V\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}V_{1}=\mathrm{span}\{(1,-1,0,0)^{T},\,(0,0,1,-1)^{T}\}
.
It is easily checked
that
W=V^{\bot}=\mathrm{span}\{c_{1},c_{2}\}
, where
c_{1}=(1/\sqrt{2},1/\sqrt{2},0,0)^{T}
and
c_{2}=(0,0,1/\sqrt{2},1/\sqrt{2})^{T}
are orthonormal
vectors.
Hence we let the basis matrix be
B=[c_{1}|c_{2}]
.
By applying (
23
), we build the minimal system in the
variables
\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}=(y_{1},y_{2})^{T}
shown below.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\dot{y}_{1}(t)&=&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}y_{1}(t)y_{2}(t)+y_{2}(t)\\
\dot{y}_{2}(t)&=&y_{2}(t)\\
\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(0)&=&(0,\sqrt{2})^{T}\,.\end{array}\right.

Note that the first and second rows of
B
are equal, as well as the
third and the fourth, which proves (again) that
x(t)=y(t)
and

z(t)=w(t)
.

7.
Approximate reduction and linearization

If one is ready to accept some degree of
approximation, it is possible to build a
reduced system in a simpler, linear form. To this purpose, we will illustrate a method for approximate linearization. The
polynomial behaviours induced by the original and by the reduced
system will in general differ by a term which is
O(t^{m})
,
for some prescribed
m
. This may be useful for analysis of the
beaviour of the system around a chosen operation point
t_{0}
, which
here will be conventionally fixed as
t_{0}=0
. The approximation can
of course be very bad for
t
too far from
0
(see in the concluding section
the discussion on
tpwl
for a strategy to possibly recover global accuracy).
The method is based entirely on simple linear algebraic
manipulations. Another attractive feature is that the reduced
system, besides being linear, is quite small, featuring no more
than
m
equations. We will also give conditions under which the
reduction is exact.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{R}[\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}]
be a set of polynomials of bounded
degree – that is, there is
k
such that
\deg(p)\leq k
for each

p\in S
. For example, one might take
S=\pi[\mathbb{R}^{n}]
, for a
certain template
\pi
with
n
parameters. Assume we are interested
in computing/simulating the set of functions
\{p(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t))\,:\,p\in S\}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
and are ready to accept an approximation error
around 0 of order
m
, for a fixed
m\geq 1
.

The basic idea
is viewing
\mathcal{L}
as a linear operator on the vector space of polynomials, and then taking its projection onto a small,
suitably chosen subspace. Let
\mathcal{M}=\{\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{M}\}
be the set of distinct monomials appearing
in the polynomials of
\cup_{j=0}^{m-1}\mathcal{L}^{(j)}(S)
. Let
U_{\mathcal{M}}
be the set of polynomials that can be generated starting from
\mathcal{M}
. Clearly,
U_{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{d}[\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}]
for some
d
large enough. Moreover, when we regard
polynomials as (column) vectors whose components are indexed by
monomials, totally ordered in some way,
U_{\mathcal{M}}
is isomorphic to

\mathbb{R}^{M}
as a
M
-dimensional vector space over the field

\mathbb{R}
. Let
\mathrm{pr}_{U_{\mathcal{M}}}
be the orthogonal projection from

\mathbb{R}[\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}]
onto
U_{\mathcal{M}}
, and consider the function

\mathrm{pr}_{V}\circ\mathcal{L}:U_{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow U_{\mathcal{M}}
. We denote by
L
the

M\times M
matrix representing
\mathrm{pr}_{U_{\mathcal{M}}}\circ\mathcal{L}
w.r.t. the
canonical basis of
U_{\mathcal{M}}
, that is
\mathcal{M}
. By definition,

(\mathrm{pr}_{U_{\mathcal{M}}}\circ\mathcal{L})_{|S}=\mathcal{L}_{|S}
. This implies that, for each

p\in S
,
\mathcal{L}(p)=Lp
, and more generally that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(j)}(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L^{j}p\ \ \ \text{ ($p\in S$ and $0\leq j\leq m-1$).}

(24)

Moreover, if we evaluate the given monomials at
v_{0}
and let

\displaystyle{\mathbf{\phi}}^{T}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}

\displaystyle(\alpha_{1}(v_{0}),...,\alpha_{M}(v_{0}))

then

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}^{(j)}(p))(v_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi^{T}L^{j}p\ \ \ \text{{\small($p\in S$ and $0\leq j\leq m-1$).}}

(25)

Now consider the subspace of
K_{m}
defined as follows

\displaystyle K_{m}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mathrm{span}\left\{{\mathbf{\phi}},L^{T}{\mathbf{\phi}},...,(L^{T})^{m-1}{\mathbf{\phi}}\right\}\,.

(26)

This is the order-
m

Krylov space
generated by the matrix
L^{T}
and by
{\mathbf{\phi}}
.

Fix now any orthonormal basis of
K_{m}
, which we may represent as a

M\times l
matrix
B=[b_{1}|...|b_{l}]
, for some
l\leq m

(orthonormality means
B^{T}B=I_{l}
). The orthogonal projection from

U_{\mathcal{M}}
onto
K_{m}
is therefore given by
\mathrm{pr}_{K_{m}}(v)=BB^{T}v
.
Consider the function that, taken a vector
v\in U_{\mathcal{M}}
, applies
L^{T}

and then projects onto
K_{m}

\displaystyle v\mapsto\mathrm{pr}_{K_{m}}(L^{T}v)=BB^{T}L^{T}v\,.

When restricted to
K_{m}
, this defines a linear morphism

K_{m}\rightarrow K_{m}
. Call
A
the
l\times l
matrix representing
this morphism w.r.t. the basis
B
. Explicitly,

\displaystyle A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B^{T}L^{T}B\,.

Lemma 16
.

For
0\leq j\leq m-1
, we have
(L^{T})^{j}{\mathbf{\phi}}=BA^{j}B^{T}{\mathbf{\phi}}
.

Proof 7.1
.

This is an easy consequence of the fact that, for any
v\in K_{m}
,

v=BB^{T}v
, and of the definition of
A
.

Let us now introduce a new vector of
l
variables,
\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}=(y_{1},...,y_{l})^{T}
and consider the following
linear
initial value problem,
given by the matrix
A

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\dot{\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}}(t)&=&A\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(t)\\
\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(0)&=&B^{T}{\mathbf{\phi}}\,.\end{array}\right.

(27)

Let us denote by
\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(t)=(y_{1}(t),...,y_{l}(t))^{T}
the (unique) analytic solution of this system. Recall that, given
p\in\mathbb{R}[\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}]
, we let
p(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t))\in\mathcal{A}
denote the function which extends
p\circ\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t)
. The following theorem says that, given
p\in S
, we can reconstruct
p(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t))
, within an approximation of order
m
, by taking a linear combination of the
y_{i}(t)
s, with coefficients given by projecting
p
(as a vector in
U_{\mathcal{M}}
) onto
K_{m}
.

Theorem 17
.

Let
p\in S
and let
\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(t)
be the unique solution of the (reduced)
problem (
27
). Then
p(\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}(t))-p^{T}B\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(t)=O(t^{m})

as
t\rightarrow 0
.

Proof 7.2
.

By definition, the derivatives of
\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(t)
from the
0
-th through the
(m-1)
-th, evaluated at
t=0
, can be written as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B^{T}{\mathbf{\phi}}

9.
Conclusion, further and related work

We have presented a framework for automatic
reasoning and reduction in systems of polynomial
ode
’s. In
particular, we offer algorithms to: (1) compute the most general set
of identities valid in the system that fit a user-specified
template; and, (2)
build a minimal system equivalent to the original
one. These algorithms are based on a mix of simple algebraic and
coalgebraic techniques.

9.1.
Directions for further work

Scalability of our approach is an issue, as, already for simple systems,
the Gröbner basis construction involved in the double chain algorithm
can be computationally quite demanding. Further experimentation,
relying on a well-engineered implementation of the method, and considering sizeable
case studies, is called for in order to assess this aspect.
One would also like to extend the present approach so
as to deal with
regions
of possible initial values, rather
than fixing one such value. This is important, e.g., in the
treatment of hybrid systems (see below). After the short version of the present paper
[
9
]
appeared, some preliminary progress towards this goal has been made, see
[
10
]
. Concerning minimization, we
note that the reduced system may not preserve the structure of the
original one, e.g. as to the meaning of variables: this may be problematic in
certain application domains, such as system biology. In the future,
we intend to investigate this issue. Approximate reductions in
the sense of System Theory
[
1
]
are also worth
investigating. One problem of the approach described in Section
7
is that the reduced system depends on a fixed initial condition
v_{0}
.
Obtaining bounds on the approximation error is another aspect that deserves further investigation.
Some preliminary progress on these issues is reported in
[
11
]
.

9.2.

A.2.
Pseudoideals

We briefly discuss a sufficient condition for establishing the
condition (
19
), that is
J_{i+1}=J_{i}
, which does
not involve Gröbner bases, but only linear algebraic
computations, and can therefore lead to a gain in efficiency. We ill
make use of a bit of new notation. For a set of polynomials
S
and
an integer
k\geq 0
, denote by
\big\langle\,\,S\,\,\big\rangle_{k}
the subset of
\big\langle\,\,S\,\,\big\rangle

generated from
S
by only using multiplier polynomials
h_{j}
of
degree
\leq k
(cf. equation (
8
)); this is a

pseudo ideal of degree
k
, in the terminology of Colón

[
19
]
. We can choose
k\geq 0
and replace
(
19
) by the following stronger condition

\displaystyle\pi^{(i+1)}[B_{i}]

\displaystyle\subseteq

\displaystyle\big\langle\,\,\cup_{j=0}^{i}\pi^{(j)}[B_{i}]\,\,\big\rangle_{k}\,.

(34)

If (
34
) is true then of course also
(
19
) is true, while the converse is not valid in
general. Condition (
