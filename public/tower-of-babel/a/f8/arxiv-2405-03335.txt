Spectral properties of the resolvent difference for singularly perturbed operators Thanks: The work of G.R. was performed at the Saint Petersburg Leonhard Euler International Mathematical Institute and supported by the Ministry of Science and Higher Education (Agreement No. 075–15–2022–287).
Grigori Rozenblum

Abstract
. We obtain order sharp spectral estimates for the difference of resolvents of singularly perturbed elliptic operators 𝐀 + 𝐕 1 {\mathbf{A}}+{\mathbf{V}}_{1} and 𝐀 + 𝐕 2 {\mathbf{A}}+{\mathbf{V}}_{2} in a domain Ω ⊆ ℝ 𝐍 \Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbf{N}} with perturbations 𝐕 1 , 𝐕 2 {\mathbf{V}}_{1},{\mathbf{V}}_{2} generated by V 1 ​ μ , V 2 ​ μ , V_{1}\mu,V_{2}\mu, where μ \mu is a measure singular with respect to the Lebesgue measure and satisfying two-sided or one-sided conditions of Ahlfors type, while V 1 , V 2 V_{1},V_{2} are weight functions subject to some integral conditions. As an important special case, spectral estimates for the difference of resolvents of two Robin realizations of the operator 𝐀 {\mathbf{A}} with different weight functions are obtained. For the case when the support of the measure is a compact Lipschitz hypersurface in Ω \Omega or, more generally, a rectifiable set of Haußdorff dimension d = 𝐍 − 1 d={\mathbf{N}}-1 , the Weyl type asymptotics for eigenvalues is justified.

Introduction

1.1.
Resolvent perturbations

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a self-adjoint second order elliptic differential operator in an open (possibly, unbounded) connected set
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathbf{N}}}
. Suppose that
{\mathbf{V}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{V}}_{2}
are some perturbations of
{\mathbf{A}}
(one of them may be the zero one), so, at least symbolically,

{\mathbf{A}}_{1}={\mathbf{A}}+{\mathbf{V}}_{1},\,{\mathbf{A}}_{2}={\mathbf{A}}+{\mathbf{V}}_{2}.

(1.1)

Generally, the perturbations
{\mathbf{V}}_{1},{\mathbf{V}}_{2}
can be rather singular, and the expressions for
{\mathbf{A}}_{1},{\mathbf{A}}_{2}
in (
1.1

{\mathbf{R}}^{(1,2)}=({\mathbf{A}}_{1}+t)^{-1}-({\mathbf{A}}_{2}+t)^{-1},

(1.2)

where the real number
t

is chosen in such way that both operators in (
1.2
) are invertible, is one of the classical ones in Spectral Theory, with long history. In particular, an efficient method was elaborated and fundamental results were obtained in
[
21
]
,
[
22
]
, where one can find referrals to earlier papers and a motivation for studying this kind of problems, the most important being the needs of the scattering theory. Further on, quite general abstract operator perturbation methods have been developed, see, in particular,
[

[

[

[

,
[

and many other sources. Being applied to concrete differential operators, these abstract perturbation schemes have produced a number of impressive results on the spectral properties, semigroup properties, regularity etc.

One of important directions of study is the investigation of the spectrum of the
difference of resolvents
of operators generated by the same elliptic differential expression with different boundary conditions. For the difference of resolvents of the Dirichlet and Neumann operators, eigenvalue estimates we obtained in the already cited paper
[

Even this, rather particular, topic attracted great attention in the latest two or even three decennia, with scores of papers published. We mention here the papers

One of special singular problems under consideration has been the study of the spectral properties of the difference of resolvents of self-adjoint operators
{\mathbf{A}}_{\iota},

\iota=1,2,
in
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbf{N}}
, corresponding to the Laplacian (or a general symmetric second order elliptic operator
{\mathcal{L}}
) with weighted Robin boundary conditions containing two different weight functions,

\partial_{\nu({\mathcal{L}})}u(X)=V_{\iota}(X)u(X),\,\iota=1,2,X\in\Gamma=\partial\Omega,

where

is the (co)normal derivative at the boundary, associated with
{\mathcal{L}}
. For the singular values

s_{j}\sim({\mathcal{A}}j)^{-\frac{3}{{\mathbf{N}}-1}},\,{\mathcal{A}}=C\int_{\Sigma}|V_{1}(X)-V_{2}(X)|^{\frac{{\mathbf{N}}-1}{3}}\omega(X)\sigma(dX),

(1.3)

where the density
\omega(X)
is determined (in some complicated way, see Theorem 3.5 in
[
41
]
) by the coefficients of the operator
{\mathcal{L}}
at the boundary. We do not need the particular expression for this weight function.

Formula (

1.2.
Singular perturbations

In the present paper, we show that the study of spectral properties of the problems discussed above can be performed in a uniform way, as versions of one and the same construction.
Namely, for a Radon measure
\mu
supported in
\overline{\Omega}
, possibly, singular with respect to the Lebesgue measure, and a
\mu
-measurable function
V
on the support of
\mu
and subject to some summability conditions, the operator
{\mathbf{A}}+V\mu
in
L_{2}(\Omega)
can be effectively defined by means of quadratic forms, and the eigenvalues of the difference of its resolvent and the resolvent of
{\mathbf{A}}
(as well as of the difference of powers of resolvents) can be estimated.
The order of such estimates is determined by the class of Ahlfors regularity of the measure
\mu
(roughly speaking, the local Haußdorff dimension of the support of
\mu
), while the coefficient in the estimates depends on a certain integral norm of the ’weight’ function
V
with respect to the measure
\mu
. In a similar way, the spectrum of the difference of (powers of) resolvents of operators with two different weight functions
V_{1},V_{2}
can be studied, producing eigenvalue estimates and, in certain cases, asymptotics, generalizing (
1.3
).

In this setting, the Robin problem corresponds to the measure
\mu
supported on some set in the boundary of
\Omega,
while the latter type of problems, which we call ’the Schrödinger type’ ones, corresponds to the measure
\mu
supported inside
\Omega.
It is possible, of course, to consider a combination of these perturbations.

Eigenvalue estimates obtained in the present paper involve integral characteristics of the weight functions. This circumstance provides us quite a freedom in extending the existing results on the
eigenvalue asymptotics
to considerably less regular weight functions
V_{\iota}
for the difference of Robin resolvents, compared, e.g., with the results in
[
41
]
. Note that in addition to the asymptotics of singular numbers, studied previously, we are able to separate positive and negative parts of
V_{1}-V_{2}
, thus finding the asymptotics of negative and positive eigenvalues separately. Similarly, for problems with singular potentials supported inside
\Omega
, the eigenvalue asymptotics is found for the measure
\mu

being the Haußdorff measure on a Lipschitz hypersurface in
\Omega
or on some unions of such surfaces (uniformly rectifiable sets). When the set
\Sigma=\hbox{{\rm supp}}\,\mu
is Ahlfors regular of a fractional dimension, asymptotic formulas for eigenvalues are, probably, not accessible in a general case, however, our estimates are order sharp: the general upper estimates are accompanied by lower estimates of the same order.

There is a huge literature on the operator perturbation theory, with extensive machinery, involving boundary triples, generalized boundary pairs, Weyl
M
-functions etc.; this activity has produced quite a lot of impressive results. In our case, where we deal with perturbation of operators defined by means of quadratic forms, using the approach originating in
[
21
]
and developed further in
[
25
]
and in
[
61
]
; the latter is the closest to ours, being much more general. We, however, do not need this approach in its full generality; our perturbation scheme is rather elementary and does not use the above general constructions, giving at the same time convenient explicit formulas.

In dealing with spectral estimates and asymptotics for singular measures, we follow the approach developed recently in the papers
[
46
]
,
[
65
]
,
[
62
]
,
[
67
]
,
[
64
]
concerning spectral properties of Birman-Schwinger type operators with singular weights and use some important results of these papers, especially,
[
67
]
. When needed, we reproduce some formulations from these papers; we explain the strategy of proving asymptotic formulas as well.

In this paper, we restrict ourselves to perturbations and boundary problems for second order uniformly elliptic operator
{\mathcal{L}}
with smooth coefficients. The regularity of the boundary, if it is present, depends on the particular problem under consideration. More general results involving elliptic operators with less regular coefficients and boundaries (like in
[
63
]
), as well as higher order operators, would require essentially more complicated considerations which would dim simple ideas in our approach to spectral problems. We hope to return to these general topics on some other occasion.

There are numerous papers devoted to various aspects of spectral theory of singular perturbations. Our initial attempts to include a complete bibliography led to the reference list filling almost one third of the paper. Therefore, we decided to make the reference list (more or less) reasonably short, so that the interested Reader may find an additional information using reference lists of the papers cited here and the citation lists in the entries of these papers in Math.Sci.Net.

2.
The abstract perturbation formula and related spectral properties

We consider perturbations of a given operator by quadratic forms. The formulas for the resolvent of the perturbed operator are somewhat similar to the ones established in
[
25
]
and, later, in
[
61
]
, however this particular form enables us to apply previously obtained results concerning spectral estimates.

2.1.
Form perturbations

Let
{\mathbf{A}}>0
be a self-adjoint operator in the Hilbert space
\mathfrak{H}.
We suppose that
{\mathbf{A}}\geq c_{0}>0

is defined by the positive quadratic form
{\mathbf{a}}[u]=({\mathbf{B}}u,{\mathbf{B}}u)_{\mathfrak{H}}
with domain
\mathfrak{H}^{1}={\mathcal{D}}({\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}).
Here
{\mathbf{B}}
is a bounded operator acting from the Hilbert space
\mathfrak{H}^{1}
to
\mathfrak{H}
. Of course, there is a considerable freedom in choosing the operator
{\mathbf{B}}

generating one and the same operator
{\mathbf{A}}.
The choice, always possible, is
{\mathbf{B}}={\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

The perturbation is supposed to be defined by means of the quadratic form
{\mathbf{v}}[u]
with domain
\mathfrak{d}[{\mathbf{v}}]\supset{\mathcal{D}}({\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}})\equiv\mathfrak{H}^{1}
and bounded there,

|{\mathbf{v}}[u]|\leq C{\mathbf{a}}[u],u\in\mathfrak{H}^{1}.

(2.1)

Therefore, the quadratic form
{\mathbf{v}}[u]
defines a bounded self-adjoint operator
{\mathbf{V}}
in
\mathfrak{H}^{1}
satisfying

({\mathbf{V}}u,u)_{\mathfrak{H}^{1}}={\mathbf{v}}[u],u\in\mathfrak{H}^{1}.

Under these conditions, we define the perturbed operator
{\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{V}}}
in
\mathfrak{H}
by means of the quadratic form

{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{V}}[u]={\mathbf{a}}[u]+{\mathbf{v}}[u],u\in\mathfrak{H}^{1},

(2.2)

In this paper, we suppose that the quadratic form (
2.2
) is positive in
\mathfrak{H}
.
Then, by the KLMN theorem, this quadratic form is closed in
\mathfrak{H}
and, in fact, defines a lower semi-bounded self-adjoint operator
{\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{V}}}
.
Having the quadratic form definition of the operator
{\mathbf{A}}_{{\mathbf{V}}}
, we are going now to describe conveniently the operator itself.

Set
u={\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}v,

v\in\mathfrak{H}
, in (
2.2
).
We obtain

{\mathbf{v}}[u]={\mathbf{v}}[{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}v].

We suppose that the perturbing quadratic form has the following structure. Let
\mathfrak{G}
be a Hilbert space and
\gamma
be a (boundary) operator,

\gamma:\mathfrak{H}^{1}\to\mathfrak{G}
such that

\|\gamma u\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{G}}\leq C\|u\|^{2}_{\mathfrak{H}^{1}},\,u\in\mathfrak{H}^{1}.

Further on, let
{\mathbf{F}}
be an operator in the space
\mathfrak{G}
such that its domain
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathbf{F}})
contains
\gamma\mathfrak{H}^{1}
and

\|{\mathbf{F}}\gamma u\|_{\mathfrak{G}}\leq C\|u\|_{\mathfrak{H}^{1}}

(note that we do not expect that
{\mathbf{F}}
is a bounded operator in
\mathfrak{G}
.)
Finally, let
{\mathbf{U}}
be a bounded self-adjoint operator in
\mathfrak{G}
.

Under the above conditions, the quadratic form
{\mathbf{v}}
is supposed to be defined by

{\mathbf{v}}[u]=({\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{F}}\gamma u,{\mathbf{F}}\gamma u)_{\mathfrak{G}},u\in\mathfrak{H}^{1}.

(2.3)

The expression (
2.3
) is defined, at least, on
\mathfrak{H}^{1}
and it is bounded with respect to the
\mathfrak{H}^{1}
norm, see (
2.1
). Since
u={\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}v
,
v\in\mathfrak{H}
, (
2.3
) transforms to

{\mathbf{v}}[{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}v]=({\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}v,{\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}v)_{\mathfrak{G}}.

(2.4)

By the conditions imposed above,
{\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is a bounded operator from
\mathfrak{H}
to
\mathfrak{G}.
Therefore, the expression in (
2.4
) can be represented as

{\mathbf{v}}[{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}v]=(({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})v,v)_{\mathfrak{H}}.

(2.5)

Here,
({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}
acts from
\mathfrak{G}
to
\mathfrak{H}
; it is the adjoint operator to
{\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
considered as acting from
\mathfrak{H}
to
\mathfrak{G}.
Since the operator
{\mathbf{U}}
is self-adjoint and bounded, the operator
({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})
is a self-adjoint bounded operator in
\mathfrak{H}
.

We return to
v={\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u
. The expression in (
2.5
) transforms to

{\mathbf{v}}[u]=(({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}){\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u,{\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u)_{\mathfrak{H}},\,u\in\mathfrak{H}^{1}.

Now we consider the operator
{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{V}}
defined in
\mathfrak{H}
by means of the quadratic form (
2.2
),
with
{\mathbf{v}}
as in (
2.3
).
We suppose further on that the form
{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{V}}[u]
is positive in
\mathfrak{H}^{1}
, namely,

{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{v}}[u]\geq C{\mathbf{a}}[u],\,u\in\mathfrak{H}^{1},

(2.6)

with some
C>0
. Under this condition, the operator
1+({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})
is positive, and therefore, we can write, for
u\in\mathfrak{H}^{1},

\displaystyle{\mathbf{a}}_{\mathbf{V}}[u]=({\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u,{\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u)_{\mathfrak{H}}+(({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}){\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u,{\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u)_{\mathfrak{H}}=

(2.7)

\displaystyle((1+({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})){\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u,{\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u)_{\mathfrak{H}}=

\displaystyle((1+({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}}){\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u,(1+({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{\frac{1}{2}}){\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}u)_{\mathfrak{H}}.

The last expression in (
2.7
) still defines the quadratic form of the self-adjoint operator
{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{V}}
, and therefore we have the representation for this operator as the composition of an operator and its adjoint (such composition is always self-adjoint):

{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{V}}=\left[(1+({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}))^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right]^{*}\left[(1+({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}))^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right].

(2.8)

Note again that we do not (and need not to) describe explicitly the domain of this operator.
The operator
{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{V}}
is invertible, since in (
2.8
) it is represented as a composition of two invertible operators. Therefore, for the (bounded)
inverse operator to
{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{V}}
, we obtain the representation

\displaystyle({\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{V}})^{-1}=[(1+({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}))^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}}]^{-1}\times

(2.9)

\displaystyle[((1+({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}))^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{A}}^{\frac{1}{2}})^{*}]^{-1}=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1+({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}))^{-\frac{1}{2}}\times

\displaystyle(1+({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}))^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1+({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}))^{-1}{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1+{\mathbf{T}})^{-1}{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}},\,\,{\mathbf{T}}\equiv{\mathbf{T}}_{\mathbf{V}}=({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}).

We arrive here at the resolvent perturbation formula, which creates the base of our further considerations.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
{\mathbf{A}}
be a positive self-adjoint operator and let
{\mathbf{V}}
be a perturbation as above. Then

\displaystyle{\mathbf{R}}_{{\mathbf{V}}}\equiv{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}-({\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{V}})^{-1}={\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left[1-(1+({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}))^{-1}\right]{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\equiv

(2.10)

\displaystyle{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left[1-(1+{\mathbf{T}}_{{\mathbf{V}}})^{-1}\right]{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

Operators of the form
{\mathbf{T}}
and their analogies have been the base of the study of discrete spectrum of Schrödinger-type operators since 60-s. It is reasonable to call them ’Birman-Schwinger’ operators.

The representation (
2.10
) of the resolvent difference has been derived under the condition that the operator

1+{\mathbf{T}}_{{\mathbf{V}}}\equiv\left[1+({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}{\mathbf{U}}({\mathbf{F}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-\frac{1}{2}})\right]

is positive definite. If we replace the operator
{\mathbf{A}}
by
{\mathbf{A}}-t,

t<0
, (
2.6
) still holds, and (
2.9
) gives the representation for the inverse of
{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{V}}-t,
this means, for the resolvent of the operator
{\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{V}}
at the point
-t
. If there is a family of quadratic forms
{\mathbf{v}}_{\epsilon}
such that the corresponding operators
{\mathbf{T}}_{{\mathbf{V}}_{\epsilon}}
converge in the norm sense to
{\mathbf{T}}_{{\mathbf{V}}}
as
\epsilon\to 0
, then, as soon as
\|{\mathbf{T}}_{{\mathbf{V}}_{\epsilon}}-{\mathbf{T}}_{{\mathbf{V}}}\|
is small enough,
the operator
1+{\mathbf{T}}_{{\mathbf{V}}_{\epsilon}}
is positive definite as well and the resolvent perturbation formula (
2.10
) is valid.

2.2.
Asymptotic spectral characteristics

We are going to study asymptotical spectral properties of compact operators in a Hilbert space. Here we collect some basic notations and facts, mostly well known.
Let
{\mathbf{K}}
be a compact self-adjoint operator in a Hilbert space
\mathfrak{H}
. By
n_{\pm}(\lambda,{\mathbf{K}})
we denote the distribution function of positive (resp., negative) eigenvalues
\pm\lambda^{\pm}_{j}({\mathbf{K}})
of the operator
{\mathbf{K}}
:

n_{\pm}(\lambda,{\mathbf{K}})=\#\{j:\lambda_{j}^{\pm}({\mathbf{K}})>\lambda\}.

For a given exponent
\theta>0
, the asymptotic characteristics of the spectrum of
{\mathbf{K}}
are defined
as

{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}_{\pm}({\mathbf{K}},\theta)=\limsup_{\lambda\to+0}n_{\pm}(\lambda,{\mathbf{K}})\lambda^{\theta},\,{\mathbf{n}}^{\inf}_{\pm}({\mathbf{K}},\theta)=\liminf_{\lambda\to+0}n_{\pm}(\lambda,{\mathbf{K}})\lambda^{\theta}

(2.11)

and

{\mathbf{n}}_{\pm}({\mathbf{K}},\theta)=\lim_{\lambda\to+0}n_{\pm}(\lambda,{\mathbf{K}})\lambda^{\theta},

(2.12)

provided these limits exist.

Similarly, for an
arbitrary
compact operator
{\mathbf{K}}
, the distribution function for its singular numbers
s_{j}({\mathbf{K}})
and their asymptotic characteristics are defined and denoted, as above, but with the symbol
\pm
deleted.

It follows immediately that for a bounded operator
{\mathbf{J}}
,

{\mathbf{n}}(\lambda,{\mathbf{J}}{\mathbf{K}})\leq{\mathbf{n}}(\lambda,\|{\mathbf{J}}\|{\mathbf{K}})={\mathbf{n}}(\|{\mathbf{J}}\|^{-1}\lambda,{\mathbf{K}}),

therefore,

{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}({\mathbf{J}}{\mathbf{K}},\theta)\leq\|{\mathbf{J}}\|^{\theta}{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}({\mathbf{K}},\theta).

The
\limsup
form of the multiplicative Ky Fan inequality

n(\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2},{\mathbf{K}}_{1}{\mathbf{K}}_{2})\leq n(\lambda_{1},{\mathbf{K}}_{1})+n(\lambda_{2},{\mathbf{K}}_{2})

(2.13)

implies in these terms

{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}({\mathbf{K}}_{1}{\mathbf{K}}_{2},\theta)\leq 2{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}({\mathbf{K}}_{1},\theta_{1})^{\frac{\theta}{\theta_{1}}}{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}({\mathbf{K}}_{2},\theta_{2})^{\frac{\theta}{\theta_{2}}}.

(2.14)

Similar inequalities are valid for the product of several operators,
{\mathbf{K}}=\prod_{j=1}^{m}{\mathbf{K}}_{j},

\theta^{-1}=\sum\theta_{j}^{-1}
:

{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}({\mathbf{K}},\theta)\leq m\prod\left({\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}({\mathbf{K}}_{j},\theta_{j})^{\frac{\theta}{\theta_{j}}}\right).

(2.15)

Another simple consequence of the multiplicative Ky Fan inequality is the following:

Lemma 2.2
.

If
{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}({\mathbf{K}}_{j},\theta_{j})<\infty,
for
j=1,3
and
{\mathbf{K}}_{2}
is a compact operator, then

{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}({\mathbf{K}}_{1}{\mathbf{K}}_{2}{\mathbf{K}}_{3},\theta)=0,\,\theta^{-1}=\theta_{1}^{-1}+\theta_{2}^{-1}.

2.3.
Ratios of quadratic forms

We will systematically use operators defined by the ratio of quadratic forms, see, e.g.,
[
23
]
.

If

When we handle several different Hilbert spaces (related, in concrete examples later, with different domains in

[

Lemma 2.3
.

Let

2.4.
Asymptotic perturbations

The major tool in the study of
spectral asymptotics
for problems involving singularities of various kinds is the asymptotic perturbation Lemma by M.Birman and M.Solomyak, see, e.g.,
[
23
]
, Lemma 1.5.
We reproduce it here, in our present notations.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
{\mathbf{K}}
be a compact self-adjoint operator, and there exists a sequence
{\mathbf{K}}_{\epsilon}
,
\epsilon\to 0,
such that

(i):

for some
\theta>0,
the limits
{\mathbf{n}}_{\pm}({\mathbf{K}}_{\epsilon},\theta)=\mathfrak{m}_{\pm,\epsilon}
exist and are finite;

(ii):

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}{\mathbf{n}}^{\sup}({\mathbf{K}}-{\mathbf{K}}_{\epsilon},\theta)=0.

Then the limits
\mathfrak{m}_{\pm}=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathfrak{m}_{\pm,\epsilon}
exist and

{\mathbf{n}}_{\pm}({\mathbf{K}},\theta)=\mathfrak{m}_{\pm}.

If the condition
(i)
above is satisfied for only one of the signs
\pm,
the conclusion of the Lemma holds for this sign.

Note that often, say, in
[
41
]
, Lemma 3.1, the condition
(ii)
above is stated in a stronger form, requiring not the asymptotic but a uniform in
\lambda
estimate for singular numbers of the difference
{\mathbf{K}}-{\mathbf{K}}_{\epsilon}
. In our study, it is the less restrictive condition
(ii)
that can actually be guaranteed, while uniform estimates may be not available.

3.
Elliptic operators with singular perturbations

3.1.
Singular form-perturbations

Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbf{N}}
be a connected domain and
\Sigma
be a compact subset in
\overline{\Omega}
; let
\mu
be a Radon measure supported in
\Sigma
. Further, let
{\mathcal{L}}
be a second order elliptic operator in
\Omega
with smooth coefficients. The corresponding quadratic form is

{\mathbf{a}}[u]=\int_{\Omega}\left[\sum_{j,k}a_{jk}(X)\partial_{j}u\overline{\partial_{k}u}+t|u(X)|^{2}\right]dX,\,t>0,

(3.1)

(where the zero order term is added in order to grant positivity)
with domain
\mathfrak{d}[{\mathbf{a}}]=H^{1}(\Omega)
; this is the
Neumann
realization of
{\mathcal{L}}
; if needed, this will be reflected in the notation of the quadratic form,
{\mathbf{a}}^{\sc\mbox{N}\hskip 1.0pt}
, and the same for the corresponding operator
{\mathbf{A}}={\mathbf{A}}^{\sc\mbox{N}\hskip 1.0pt}.

Along with
H^{1}(\Omega),
we will consider higher order Sobolev spaces
H^{l}(\Omega),
with integer
l>1
as well as, for a domain
\Omega
with compact complement, Hilbert spaces
\mathfrak{H}_{{\mathcal{L}}}^{l}(\Omega)
which locally, near
\partial\Omega,
are contained in
H^{l}

Under certain regularity conditions (specified later on) the restriction
\gamma
of functions in the Sobolev space
H^{l}(\Omega)
to
\Sigma
is a well defined bounded operator from the Sobolev space
H^{l}(\Omega)
to the Hilbert space
\mathfrak{G}=L_{2,\mu}(\Sigma)
with respect to the measure
\mu,
in particular, as explained in Sect.
3.2
, Haußdorff measure
{\mathcal{H}}^{d}
of the proper dimension
d\leq{\mathbf{N}}
. Namely, this restriction, the operator
\gamma
, defined initially on
C(\overline{\Omega})\cap H^{l}(\Omega),
can be extended by continuity to the whole of
H^{l}(\Omega).

For a given real-valued function
V
on
\Sigma,
measurable with respect to the measure
\mu
on
\Sigma
, the function
F
is defined as
F=|V|^{\frac{1}{2}}
, a real
\mu
- measurable function on
\Sigma
. The bounded function
U(X)
on
\Sigma
is defined as the sign of
V(X)
:
U(X)=\hbox{{\rm sign}}\,V(X):=V(X)|V(X)|^{-1}
if
V(X)\neq 0
and
U(X)=0
otherwise. In this setting, the perturbing quadratic form
{\mathbf{v}}[u]
is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathbf{v}}[u]=\int_{\Sigma}V(X)|u(X)|^{2}\mu(dX)=(F\gamma u,UF\gamma u)_{L_{2}(\Sigma,\mu)}:=

\displaystyle({\mathbf{F}}\gamma u,{\mathbf{U}}{\mathbf{F}}\gamma u)_{\mathfrak{G}},\,u\in H^{1}(\Omega),\mathfrak{G}=L_{2,\mu}.

with
{\mathbf{F}},{\mathbf{U}}
being the operators of multiplication by
F,U
; this is a concrete realization of the abstract scheme in Sect.
2
. In order to use this scheme, we need to check the abstract properties required in Sect. 2 for the particular setting, which will be done in this Section.

In this paper, we consider the measure
\mu
having compact support. We will show that
if the ’density’
V
belongs to a certain
L_{p,\mu}
class (with
p
depending on
d,{\mathbf{N}},l
) or, in some cases, the Orlicz class, then the conditions (
2.1
) and (
2.6
) are fulfilled and the above construction fits into the abstract setting of Section
2
.

We consider two different cases in the study of the spectral properties of the difference of resolvents. On the one hand
\Sigma
may be a compact set in the boundary
\Sigma\subset\Gamma=\partial\Omega
, of positive, or possibly, zero surface measure and of Haußdorff dimension
d\in({\mathbf{N}}-2,{\mathbf{N}}-1]
. The domain
\Omega
may be bounded or having bounded complement. Here, the perturbation
{\mathbf{V}}
consists in setting Robin type boundary conditions on
\Sigma,
with measure
V\mu
serving as weight, while keeping intact the Neumann boundary conditions on the remaining part of
\Gamma
. In particular, if
V=0
everywhere on
\Gamma,
the quadratic form
{\mathbf{a}}_{{\mathbf{v}}}
coincides with (
3.1
). Such, unperturbed, operator is the operator of the Neumann problem,
{\mathbf{A}}={\mathbf{A}}^{\sc\mbox{N}\hskip 1.0pt}
. It turns out that the eigenvalue estimates for
({\mathbf{A}}^{\sc\mbox{N}\hskip 1.0pt})^{-1}-({\mathbf{A}}_{\mathbf{V}})^{-1}
have order depending on the Haußdorff dimension of
\Sigma,
in the special case when
d={\mathbf{N}}-1
for
\Sigma
with positive surface measure, the eigenvalue asymptotics holds. These results are valid for the difference of resolvents of two Robin type problems as well.

The second case concerns
\Omega={\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathbf{N}}}
and
\Sigma\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathbf{N}}}
being a compact set of Haußdorff dimension
d\in({\mathbf{N}}-2,{\mathbf{N}}]
with a Haußdorff
{\mathcal{H}}^{d}
– measurable function
V
on
\Sigma.
Here, we consider the difference of resolvents of Schrödinger like operators with, possibly singular (for
d<{\mathbf{N}}
), potentials. In particular, the case fits in this picture when the set
\Sigma
is a Lipschitz hypersurface, and the perturbation
{\mathbf{V}}
consists, in fact, in setting ’transmission type’ condition on
\Sigma
.
In this way, we include here
\delta
-interactions on a hypersurface,
d={\mathbf{N}}-1
, systematically studied in the literature, see, e.g.,
[
10
]
, but we fail to cover
\delta^{\prime}
-type interactions, since this perturbation does not fit into our quadratic form approach. We obtain spectral estimates for the difference of powers of the resolvents as well and its eigenvalue asymptotics.

Further on, we specify the properties of the set
\Sigma
and the weight function
V
, which grant that the abstract conditions in Section
2
are fulfilled.

3.2.
Singular sets and measures

We recall the definition of Ahlfors regular sets and measures.
Let
\Sigma
be a compact set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbf{N}}
and
\mu
be a Radon measure supported in
\Sigma
. For
d\in(0,{\mathbf{N}}]
, the measure
\mu
is called
Ahlfors regular
of dimension
d
(sometimes the word ’
d
-set’ is used for
\Sigma
) if for any
r\in(0,\operatorname{diam\,}(\Sigma)]
and any point
X\in\Sigma,

{\mathcal{A}}_{-}r^{d}\leq\mu(\Sigma\cap B(X,r))\leq{\mathcal{A}}_{+}r^{d},

(3.2)

with some positive constants
{\mathcal{A}}_{\pm}={\mathcal{A}}_{\pm}(\Sigma)
,
B(X,r)
denoting the ball with center at
X
and radius
r
.

This notion is widely used in geometric measure theory and many other topics in Analysis, see, e.g.,
[
27
]
,
[
71
]
. It is known that such measure is equivalent to the
d
-dimensional Haußdorff measure
{\mathcal{H}}^{d}
on
\Sigma
. If the measure
\mu
satisfies (
3.2
) we will write
\mu\in\mathfrak{A}^{d},
or
\Sigma\in\mathfrak{A}^{d}.

For an integer dimension
d
, the most obvious example, the leading one in this paper, is a compact Lipschitz surface or a finite union of such surfaces. A more general construction, valid for fractional
d
as well, is the following (we refer to the exposition in
[
71
]
, Ch.4,5).

Self-similar measures are multi-dimensional generalizations of the well-known Cantor sets with Haußdorff measure of a proper dimension. An affine mapping
S
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathbf{N}}}
is called
similitude
with ratio
\rho
if it is a composition of a translation, a rotation, and a homothety with coefficient
\rho<1
. Let us have a finite collection of similitudes
{\mathcal{S}}=\{S_{j}\},j=1,\dots,N
, with ratios
\rho_{j}.
The compact set
{\mathcal{K}}
is
called invariant with respect to
{\mathcal{S}}
if
{\mathcal{K}}=\cup_{j}S_{j}{\mathcal{K}}
. For any such collection of similitudes, there exists a unique invariant compact set
{\mathcal{K}}
. This set, for every fixed
m
, satisfies

{\mathcal{K}}=\bigcup_{{\mathbf{j}}=(j_{1},\dots,j_{m})}S_{j_{1}}\dots S_{j_{m}}{\mathcal{K}}.

Let
d
be the Haußdorff dimension of
{\mathcal{K}}.
The set
{\mathcal{K}}
is called
selfsimilar
, if, moreover,

{\mathcal{H}}^{d}(S_{j}{\mathcal{K}}\cap S_{j^{\prime}}({\mathcal{K}}))=0,\,\,j\neq j^{\prime}.

(3.3)

A strengthening of (
3.3
) is the
open set condition
: there exists an open set
{\mathcal{O}}
containing
{\mathcal{K}}
such that

S_{j}({\mathcal{O}})\cap S_{j^{\prime}}({\mathcal{O}})=\varnothing,j\neq j^{\prime},\,\,\mbox{and}\,\bigcup_{j}S_{j}({\mathcal{O}})\subset{\mathcal{O}}.

The property of our interest is that if the open set condition is satisfied, then the Haußdorff dimension
d
of
{\mathcal{K}}
equals the only positive solution of

\sum_{j}\rho_{j}^{d}=1,

and the Haußdorff measure
{\mathcal{H}}^{d}
restricted to
{\mathcal{K}}
belongs to
\mathfrak{A}^{d}
. Obviously, a bilipschitz image of a
d
-set is a
d
-set again. More examples one can get by considering a disjoint union of
d
-sets, finite, and under some conditions, infinite.

It was found in
[
67
]
that sometimes, when studying spectral estimates, only one of the inequalities in (
3.2
) is needed. In this connection,
we call the measure (the set) lower-, resp., upper-regular of order
d
if the left, resp., the right inequality in (
3.2
) is satisfied. The corresponding classes of measures will be denoted
\mathfrak{A}^{d}_{-},
resp.,
\mathfrak{A}^{d}_{+}
, so
\mathfrak{A}^{d}=\mathfrak{A}^{d}_{+}\cap\mathfrak{A}^{d}_{-}
. (If the support of the measure
\mu
is not compact, the inequalities in (
3.2
) are supposed to hold for all
r>0
, we, however, restrict our considerations to compact sets.)

3.3.
Regularity of the boundary

For different types of spectral problems under consideration, we will require different regularity level of the domain
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbf{N}}.
For the Schrödinger type problems, where only the local regularity of solutions of the elliptic equations is used,
\Omega
is an arbitrary domain without additional restrictions, and we can consider the whole
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbf{N}}
. For boundary problems with Robin boundary conditions, when considering the difference of resolvents, we suppose that the boundary is present, is compact and is of class
C^{1,1},
this means that the boundary can be represented, locally in properly rotated local co-ordinates
X=(x_{1},x^{\prime})
, as the graph of a function
x_{1}=\psi(x^{\prime})
where first order partial derivatives of the function
\psi
belong to the Lipschitz class. In the latter case, the following local regularity property plays the key role.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain of class
C^{1,1}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbf{N}}
and let
{\mathcal{L}}
be a Hermitian elliptic second order operator in
\Omega
with smooth coefficients. Let
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\Omega
be a bounded domain with
C^{1,1}
boundary such that the boundary of
\Omega
lies in
\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}
. Then for any
f\in L_{2}(\Omega),
the (unique) solution
u
of the Neumann boundary problem

{\mathcal{L}}u+tu=f,\,\,\partial_{\nu({\mathcal{L}})}u_{|_{\partial\Omega}}=0,t>0,

(3.4)

belongs to the Sobolev space
H^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})
and

\|u\|_{H^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\prime})}.

For a smooth boundary, this is the general elliptic regularity fact, presented in a number of standard sources. For the boundary of finite smoothness, as above, this result can be found, with different degree of detalization, in certain advanced treaties on elliptic boundary problems, sometimes in the version for the Dirichlet boundary conditions, with referral to a similar reasoning for the Neumann conditions. We can cite here, e.g.,
[

]

For higher order operators, we suppose, for the sake of brevity, that the boundary is infinitely smooth. For a bounded domain

The domain of a higher power,

Proposition 3.2

Another fact, important in our study, is the possibility of continuation of solutions of the equation (

It suffices to prove Lemma for the whole space; the case of a domain follows immediately by using the Stein extension theorem. Statement (1) can be found, e.g., in

3.5.
A singular measure on the boundary and the operator for the Robin problem

Here we discuss the same kind of inequalities for the measure supported at the boundary of

We recall that the classical Robin boundary problem in a domain

for the elliptic operator

[
8
]

[
53

\displaystyle{\mathbf{a}}_{R,V}=\int_{\Omega}\langle\mathfrak{a}(X)\nabla u(X),\nabla u(X)\rangle dX+t\int_{\Omega}|u(X)|dX+

In more recent papers, a more general setting was considered, with the function

]

[
15
]
,
[
16
]
,
[
53
]
. In our case, this operator will be replaced by a measure on

possibly, singular with respect to
\sigma

Namely, having a measure
\mu
supported in

and a
\mu

{\mathbf{a}}_{V\mu}[u]=\int V(X)|\gamma u(X)|^{2}\mu(dX)

For such quadratic form, the results similar to the ones in the previous subsection are valid, under the condition that the boundary

{\mathbf{v}}_{V\mu}[u]\leq C_{0}\|{\mathbf{J}}u\|^{2}_{H^{l}(\Omega^{\prime})}\leq C_{0}C_{1}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{l}(\Omega)},

In our initial motivation, namely, for the difference of Robin resolvents with different densities, we can prove Weyl-type asymptotic formulas for a measure

When we consider the perturbation contained strictly inside our domain, we can use as approximating, the results on the asymptotics for Lipschitz surfaces, obtained in

As explained above, the asymptotics of eigenvalues of

For the benefit of the Readers, we explain here the structure of the proof, addressing to the above sources for technical details.

We will use Lemma
2.5

of the weight functions, we approximate
in this integral norm over
\Sigma
the given weight functions by more and more regular or more convenient ones, arriving at last to the situation where the eigenvalue asymptotics is already known. We denote

and perform the following steps.

(1)

The function

(2)

The function
V^{(1)}
is approximated (in the above integral norm, again) by a smooth function

such that the set where it is positive and the set where it is negative are separated,
{\rm dist}\,(\hbox{{\rm supp}}\,(V^{(2)}_{+}),\hbox{{\rm supp}}\,(V^{(2)}_{-}))>0.
After this, the property ’sign separation’ established in the papers cited above, is used: for
such

{\mathbf{W}}
do not depend on the negative part,
V^{(2)}_{-}
, and vice versa. Therefore, we can restrict our considerations to a smooth non-negative function

.

(3)

Consider now the spectrum of the (nonnegative) operator

{\mathbf{W}}^{(2)}=[(V^{(2)})^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}]^{*}[(V^{(2)})^{\frac{1}{2}}\gamma{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}].

Here we may commute the (smooth!) function
(V^{(2)})^{\frac{1}{2}}
and the restriction operator
\gamma,

{\mathbf{W}}^{(2)}=\left[\gamma(V^{(2)})^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}\right]^{*}\left[\gamma(V^{(2)})^{\frac{1}{2}}{\mathbf{A}}^{-1}\right].

(6.2)

The nonzero (in fact, positive) eigenvalues of the operator (

) in

(6.3)

acting in

.

(4)

The operator

where

and, therefore, is the integral kernel with singularity at the diagonal. On this stage, we pass from the pseudodifferential setting, where certain regularity of symbols and surfaces is required, to the setting involving integral operators, where considerably less regularity is needed. After the restriction to

(6.4)

(5)

(6)

We can apply now the results of the paper

6.4.
The case of a fractional dimension
d

A natural question arises, whether there are asymptotic eigenvalue formulas for the operator
{\mathbf{R}}^{(1,2)}
with measure
\mu,
Ahlfors regular with a fractional dimension
d
. Our reference to the result by H.Triebel
[
71
]
shows that, generally, the asymptotic
order

\theta=\frac{d}{d-{\mathbf{N}}+4}
is sharp.

In the literature, there are a number of studies of the spectrum of operators of the form
{\mathbf{T}}
above; sometimes they are called Krein-Feller operators. Main interest there is directed to the cases when the measure
\mu
and the density
V
have some regular fractal structure; in such cases, the spectrum can be studied using arithmetic and algebraic relations. The leading examples of measures in
\mathfrak{A}^{d}
with fractional
d
(the construction is valid for integer
d
as well, of course) are the ones obtained as self-similar measures, see Sect. 3.2.

The results existing in the literature concern the spectrum of operators of the form
{\mathbf{T}}.
The author failed while searching for the results for the operator
{\mathbf{W}}.

For
{\mathbf{T}},
even in the one-dimensional case, the asymptotics for
n(\lambda)
can be non-power-like. Namely, see, e.g.,
[
68
]
, where, depending on the arithmetic structure of the self-similar singular measure, the class of measures was described, where the eigenvalue asymptotics is

n({\mathbf{T}},\lambda)\sim\lambda^{-d}\Phi(\log\lambda)+o(\lambda^{-d}),

(6.6)

with
d
being the Haußdorff dimension and
\Phi
being a bounded periodic function. By passing to the direct product, one obtains similar examples with non-power asymptotics in higher dimensions. This effect was also studied in
[
59
]
,
[
47
]
and many other papers in an arbitrary dimension. Thus, at least for now, it seems to be non-realistic to search for eigenvalue asymptotics in a general case, without structural regularity. Nevertheless, our results in Sect.
3
demonstrate that the upper eigenvalues estimates are order sharp.
