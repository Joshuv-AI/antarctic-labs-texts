Abstract
. We simplify the proof of a Gaussian correlation inequality for plurisubharmonic functions found by Barthe and Cordero-Erausquin. The new observation is a second-order integration-by-parts formula in the complex Gaussian setting.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 60E15; Secondary 32U05.

1.
Introduction

A twice continuously differentiable function
f:{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is plurisubharmonic (psh for short) if for all
w\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, the
z
-pointwise non-negativity holds

\sum_{1\leq j,k\leq n}\partial^{2}_{z_{j}\overline{z_{k}}}f(z)w_{j}\overline{w_{k}}\geq 0,

where

\partial_{z_{j}}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x_{j}}-i\partial_{y_{j}})\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{\overline{z_{j}}}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x_{j}}+i\partial_{y_{j}}).

Here,
z=x+iy
with
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
A function
f
defined on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is circular-symmetric if

f(e^{i\theta}w)=f(w),\quad\forall\,\theta\in[-\pi,\pi],\quad\,\forall w\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}.

Consider the standard complex Gaussian measure
d\gamma
on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
:

d\gamma(w)=\pi^{-n}e^{-|w|^{2}}d\ell(w),

where
d\ell
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\simeq{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
.

The following remarkable result was recently established in
[
BCE22
]
.

Theorem 1.1

(Barthe and Cordero-Erausquin)
.

Let
f,g:{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\rightarrow[-\infty,\infty)
be two psh functions (with controlled growth at infinity).
If
f
is circular-symmetric, then

\int fgd\gamma\geq\int fd\gamma\int gd\gamma.

(1.1)

We refer to Barthe
[
Bar17
]
for a nice survey about Gaussian correlation inequality.

2.
Integration by parts

The proof of (
1.1
) in
[
BCE22
]
is similar to the real case for convex functions
(see Y. Hu
[
Hu97
]
and Schmuckenschläger
[
Sch00
]
) for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck operator

{{\mathbb{L}}}f(w):=\frac{1}{4}\Delta f(w)-\frac{1}{2}\langle w,\nabla\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}f(w).

Using that
\langle w,\nabla\rangle_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}}=(x+iy)\cdot(\nabla_{x}-i\nabla_{y})
, we can expand
{\mathbb{L}}
as

{{\mathbb{L}}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{4}\left(\partial^{2}_{x_{j}x_{j}}+\partial^{2}_{y_{j}y_{j}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\left(x_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}+y_{j}\partial_{y_{j}}\right).

Since the plurisubharmonicity involves
\partial\bar{\partial}
-operators, it is then natural to work with

L=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\partial^{2}_{z_{j}\overline{z_{j}}}-\overline{z_{j}}\partial_{\overline{z_{j}}}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{|z|^{2}}\partial_{{z_{j}}}\left(e^{-|z|^{2}}\partial_{\overline{z_{j}}}\right).

For convenience, we introduce the first order vectorial operators

\partial_{\overline{z}}=(\partial_{\overline{z_{1}}},\partial_{\overline{z_{2}}},\cdots,\partial_{\overline{z_{n}}})\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{{z}}=(\partial_{{z_{1}}},\partial_{{z_{2}}},\cdots,\partial_{{z_{n}}}).

We have the following integration by parts formulae.

Lemma 2.1
.

For regular enough functions
f,g:{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}
,

\int(Lf){g}d\gamma=-\int\partial_{\overline{z}}f\cdot{\partial_{z}g}d\gamma=\int f(\overline{L}g)d\gamma,

where
\overline{L}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\partial^{2}_{z_{j}\overline{z_{j}}}-{z_{j}}\partial_{{z_{j}}})
.

Moreover, if
f
is circular-symmetric,

\int(L^{2}f){g}d\gamma=\sum_{1\leq j,k\leq n}\int\partial^{2}_{z_{j}\overline{z_{k}}}f\cdot{\partial^{2}_{z_{k}\overline{z_{j}}}g}d\gamma.

The first part is standard. For the second part we note the commutativity

\partial_{z_{j}}L=L\partial_{z_{j}},\quad\forall\,1\leq j\leq n,

(2.1)

and the following two operator relations

L={{\mathbb{L}}}+\frac{i}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(y_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}-x_{j}\partial_{y_{j}}\right)

and

\overline{L}={{\mathbb{L}}}-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(y_{j}\partial_{x_{j}}-x_{j}\partial_{y_{j}}\right).

Thus,
f
is circular-symmetric implies

Lf=\overline{L}f={\mathbb{L}}f\quad\text{and}\quad L^{2}f={{\mathbb{L}}}^{2}f=\overline{L}Lf.

The second part follows by using (
2.1
) and the two identities in the first part.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that the integration by part formula also implies

\int(-Lf)\overline{f}d\gamma=\int|\partial_{\overline{z}}f|^{2}d\gamma\geq 0.

3.
A shorter proof of Theorem
1.1

Denote by
P_{t}^{{\mathbb{L}}}
(resp.,
P_{t}^{L}
) the semigroup on
L^{2}(d\gamma)
(resp., complex
L^{2}(d\gamma)
).
We follow the strategy of Barthe and Cordero-Erausquin
[
BCE22
]
.
For psh functions
f
and
g
, with
f
also being circular-symmetric, introduce the
{\mathbb{R}}
-valued function

\alpha(t):=\int(P_{t}^{{{\mathbb{L}}}}f)gd\gamma,\quad t\geq 0.

Since
f
is circular-symmetric, we have

\alpha^{\prime\prime}(t)=\int({{\mathbb{L}}}^{2}P_{t}^{{{\mathbb{L}}}}f)gd\gamma=\int(L^{2}P_{t}^{L}f)gd\gamma.

Since
P_{t}^{L}f=P_{t}^{{\mathbb{L}}}f
is also plurisubharmonic and circular-symmetric, by Lemma
2.1

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq j,k\leq n}\int\partial^{2}_{z_{j}\overline{z_{k}}}P_{t}^{L}f\cdot{\partial^{2}_{z_{k}\overline{z_{j}}}g}d\gamma

\displaystyle=\int\text{Tr}\left(D^{2}_{{\mathbb{C}}}(P_{t}^{L}f)D^{2}_{{\mathbb{C}}}g\right)d\gamma\geq 0.

Here
D^{2}_{{\mathbb{C}}}h
denotes the Hermitian matrix
(\partial^{2}_{z_{j}\overline{z_{k}}}h)_{1\leq j,k\leq n}
.
Note that
\alpha
has a bounded limit
\alpha(\infty)
.
The convexity of
\alpha
then implies that it is decreasing on
[0,\infty)

and the correlation inequality (
1.1
) corresponds to
\alpha(0)\geq\alpha(\infty)
.
The proof is complete.

Remark 3.1
.

In conclusion, our proof requires lighter knowledge about
P_{t}^{L}
.
