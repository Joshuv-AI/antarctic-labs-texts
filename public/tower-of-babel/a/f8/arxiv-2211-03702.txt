New counterexamples to the birational Torelli theorem for Calabi–Yau manifolds
Marco Rampazzo

Abstract
. We produce counterexamples to the birational Torelli theorem for Calabi–Yau manifolds in arbitrarily high dimension: this is done by exhibiting a series of non birational pairs of Calabi–Yau ( n 2 − 1 ) (n^{2}-1) -folds which, for n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 even, admit an isometry between their middle cohomologies. These varieties also satisfy an 𝕃 {\mathbb{L}} -equivalence relation in the Grothendieck ring of varieties, i.e. the difference of their classes annihilates a power of the class of the affine line. We state this last property for a broader class of Calabi–Yau pairs, namely all those which are realized as pushforwards of a general ( 1 , 1 ) (1,1) -section on a homogeneous roof in the sense of Kanemitsu, along its two extremal contractions.



1.
Introduction

The global Torelli theorem asserts that the isomorphism class of a
K3
surface is determined by its cohomological data: more precisely, two
K3
surfaces are isomorphic if and only if their integral middle cohomologies, endowed with the intersection pairing, are isometric (see, for instance,
[
Huy16
]
). While similar statements have been formulated for hyperkähler manifolds
[
Ver13
]
, it is a natural question to ask whether a “Torelli-type statement” might exist for Calabi–Yau manifolds. In other words: does the existence of an isometry of middle cohomologies of two Calabi–Yau manifolds (a so-called Hodge-equivalence) imply that such manifolds are isomorphic, or birationally equivalent? The answer to this question has been proven to be negative, at least in low dimension: in
[
OR18
]
and
[
BCP18
]
a counterexample to the “birational” Torelli theorem has been found, in the form of a non-birational but Hodge-equivalent pair of Calabi–Yau threefolds. Shortly after, in
[
Man19
]
a similar example has been given among Calabi–Yau fivefolds. However, to the author’s knowledge, no further counterexamples have been found among Calabi–Yau manifolds of dimension higher than five.

The main goal of this paper is to produce a series of new such counterexamples: namely, for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
greater than two, we construct pairs of Calabi–Yau
(n^{2}-1)
-folds which are not birationally equivalent, but they have isometric middle cohomology if
n
is even. Such varieties are realized in the following way: let
V
be a
(2n+1)
-dimensional complex projective space and
\operatorname{Fl}:=F(n,n+1,V)
the variety parametrizing pairs of subspaces
W
,
W^{\prime}
of
V
, of dimension respectively
n
and
n+1
, such that
W\subset W^{\prime}
. This variety is one of the
homogeneous roofs
classified by Kanemitsu
[
Kan22
, Section 5.1.1]
, i.e. it admits two projective bundle morphisms
p_{-}
and
p_{+}
of the same relative dimension, respectively over
\operatorname{Gr}_{-}:=G(n,V)
and
\operatorname{Gr}_{+}:=G(n+1,V)
, and such that
{\mathcal{O}}(1,1):=p_{-}^{*}{\mathcal{O}}(1)\otimes p_{+}^{*}{\mathcal{O}}(1)
is the Grothendieck line bundle of both the projective bundle structures.

As shown in
[
KR22
]
and
[
Ram20
]
, a general
s\in H^{0}(\Fl,{\mathcal{O}}(1,1))
defines a pair of smooth Calabi–Yau varieties
(Y_{-},Y_{+}):=(Z(p_{-*}s),Z(p_{+*}s))
. We show that such varieties are not isomorphic by proving that any isomorphism
Y_{-}\longrightarrow Y_{+}
must descend from an isomorphism of the (unique) Grassmannians containing them, and ultimately from an automorphism
M
of
H^{0}(\Fl,{\mathcal{O}}(1,1))
satisfying
MSM^{-1}=S^{T}
, where
S
is the matrix associated to
s
as a
(1,1)
-divisor in the product of the Plücker spaces. By an argument similar to
[
OR18
,
Man19
]
we verify that such
M
cannot exist for the general
s
, thus proving that
Y_{-}
and
Y_{+}
are not isomorphic. Finally, we conclude that
Y_{-}
and
Y_{+}
cannot be birationally equivalent. This generalizes some of the results of
[
KR19
]
to higher dimension.

The pairs we discuss are also “
{\mathbb{L}}
-equivalent”, i.e. the difference of their classes in the Grothendieck ring of varieties annihilates a power of the class of the affine line. We state this result for a wider class of pairs: by generalizing a result of Ito–Miura–Okawa–Ueda
[
IMOU19
]
, we prove that every pair of Calabi–Yau varieties associated to a roof in the sense of
[
KR22
,
Ram20
]
is
{\mathbb{L}}
-equivalent. This property is conjectured to be related to derived equivalence
[
KS16
, Conjecture 1.6]
. The main results of this paper are gathered in the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

(Theorem
3.7
)
For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
n\geq 2
, consider the locally trivial
{\mathbb{P}}^{n}
-fibrations
p_{-}:F(n,n+1,2n+1)\longrightarrow G(n,2n+1)
and
p_{+}:F(n,n+1,2n+1)\longrightarrow G(n+1,2n+1)
and a general section
s\in H^{0}(F(n,n+1,2n+1),p_{-}^{*}{\mathcal{O}}(1)\otimes p_{+}^{*}{\mathcal{O}}(1))
. Let
(Y_{-},Y_{+})
be the pair of Calabi–Yau
(n^{2}-1)
-folds defined as
Y_{\pm}:=Z(p_{\pm*}s)
. Then:

(1)

Y_{-}
and
Y_{+}
are not birationally equivalent

(2)

([Y_{-}]-[Y_{+}]){\mathbb{L}}^{n}=0
, i.e.
Y_{-}
and
Y_{+}
are
{\mathbb{L}}
-equivalent

(3)

For
n
even, there is a Hodge isometry
H^{n^{2}-1}(Y_{-},{\mathbb{Z}})\simeq H^{n^{2}-1}(Y_{+},{\mathbb{Z}})
, i.e.
Y_{-}
and
Y_{+}
are Hodge equivalent.

In
[
OR18
, Section 5]
it is discussed how this construction for
n=2
describes a divisor in the moduli space of intersections of general
PGL(10)
-translates of
G(2,5)
. We show how this behavior does not extend to
n>2
.

Notations and conventions

We work over the field of complex numbers.

Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r})\vdash n
be an ordered partition of length
r
, i.e. a non-increasing list of
r
integers such that
n=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}
. Given a vector space
V
of dimension
m\geq r
(or a vector bundle
V
of rank
m\geq r
) by

we denote the Schur power of
V
with respect to
\lambda
. In particular:

(1)

{\mathbb{S}}^{(l)}V=\Sym^{l}V
for any
l\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
{\mathbb{S}}^{(0)}V=\{0\}

(2)

{\mathbb{S}}^{(1^{r})}V=\wedge^{r}V
where
(1^{r})
is the partition of
r
with exactly
r
nonzero terms

(3)

For
\lambda\vdash l
,
\nu\vdash n
we write
{\mathbb{S}}^{\lambda}V\otimes{\mathbb{S}}^{\nu}V=\bigoplus_{\mu\vdash l+n}c_{\lambda\nu}^{\mu}{\mathbb{S}}^{\mu}V
where the integers
c_{\lambda\nu}^{\mu}
are the Littlewood–Richardson coefficients

(4)

By
{\mathbb{S}}^{\lambda}\circ{\mathbb{S}}^{\mu}V
we denote the
inner plethysm
(see
[
Wey03
]
for details).

Let
G
be a simple Lie group and
P\subset G
a parabolic subgroup. Given the rational homogeneous variety
G/P
, we use the notation
{\mathcal{E}}_{\omega}=G\times^{P}{\mathbb{V}}^{P}_{\omega}
for the homogeneous, irreducible vector bundle of rank
r
over
G/P
associated with the
P
-representation of highest weight
\omega
, where
{\mathbb{V}}^{P}_{\omega}
is the rank
r
vector space on which the
P
-representaton of highest weight
\omega
acts. Similarly, we call
{\mathbb{V}}^{G}_{\omega}
the space on which the
G
-representation of highest weight
\omega
acts.

2.
Calabi–Yau pairs of type
A^{G}_{2n}

2.1.
Homogeneous vector bundles on Grassmannians

Consider vector spaces
V_{k}
and
V
, of dimensions respectively
k
and
n
, where
k<n
. By
G(k,V)
we denote the Grassmannian of
k
-dimensional subspaces of
V
, i.e.
G(k,V)=\{A\in\operatorname{Hom}(V_{k},V):\operatorname{rk}A=k\}/GL(V_{k})
where the quotient is taken with respect to the action
(g,A)\mapsto Ag^{-1}
, for
g\in GL(V_{k})
and
A\in\operatorname{Hom}(V_{k},V)
. This is a smooth variety of dimension
nk-k^{2}
, naturally embedded in
{\mathbb{P}}(\wedge^{k}V)
via the Plücker map
\psi_{k}:[A]\longmapsto[\psi_{k}(A)]
, where
\psi_{k}(A)
is the vector of
k
-minors of
A
.

The tautological vector bundle
{\mathcal{U}}
on
G(k,V)
is the rank
k
bundle whose fiber over
[A]
is the vector space
\operatorname{im}(A)\subset V
. It comes with an embedding into the rank
n
trivial vector bundle, giving rise to the tautological short exact sequence on
G(k,V)
:

(2.1)

0\longrightarrow{\mathcal{U}}\longrightarrow V\otimes{\mathcal{O}}\longrightarrow{\mathcal{Q}}\longrightarrow 0.

One has
\wedge^{k}{\mathcal{U}}={\mathcal{O}}(-1)
and
\wedge^{n-k}{\mathcal{Q}}={\mathcal{O}}(1)
.

We also recall that the Grassmannian is a rational homogeneous variety described as
G(k,V)\simeq SL(V)/P_{k}
, where
P_{k}\subset SL(V)
is an appropriate parabolic subgroup. Homogeneous, irreducible vector bundles are in one-to-one correspondence with representations of the Levi subgroup of
P_{k}
, which is
L_{k}=SL(V_{k})\times{\mathbb{C}}^{*}\times SL({V/V_{k}})\subset P_{k}
. We call
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{k-1}
the fundamental weights of the first factor,
\omega_{k}
the one of the second factor and
\omega_{k+1},\dots,\omega_{n-1}
the fundamental weights of the last block. In this way, if we consider two partitions
\lambda,\nu
of appropriate lengths, one can check that:

(2.2)

{\mathbb{S}}^{\lambda}{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}\otimes{\mathbb{S}}^{\nu}{\mathcal{Q}}={\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}

where
\mu=\mu_{1}\omega_{1}+\cdots+\mu_{m-1}\omega_{n-1}
with coefficients given by

(2.3)

\mu_{i}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{i+1}&i<k\\
\lambda_{k}+\nu_{n-k}&i=k\\
\nu_{n-i}-\nu_{n-i+1}&i>k.\end{array}\right.

All homogeneous, irreducuble vector bundles on
G(k,V)
arise in this way. Their cohomology is usually computed via the Borel–Weil–Bott theorem, which we recall in Appendix
A
.

2.2.
Calabi–Yau varieties of type
A^{G}_{2n}
and their properties

Given a vector space
V
of dimension
2n+1
, consider
\operatorname{Gr}:=G(n,V)\subset{\mathbb{P}}:={\mathbb{P}}(\wedge^{n}V)
. By
\operatorname{Gr}_{g}\subset{\mathbb{P}}
we mean the image of
\operatorname{Gr}\subset{\mathbb{P}}
with respect to an isomorphism
g\in\operatorname{Aut}{\mathbb{P}}=PGL(\wedge^{n}V)
(we will often call such variety a
translate
of
\operatorname{Gr}
).

Definition 2.1
.

We say
Y\subset\operatorname{Gr}
is a Calabi–Yau variety of type
A_{2n}^{G}
if
Y=Z(s)
where
s\in H^{0}(\operatorname{Gr},{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee}(2))
is a general section.

Remark 2.2
.

The name refers to
[
Kan22
, Example 5.3]
. In fact, as we will clarify further in this section, any
s
as above is the pushforward of a hyperplane section on a so-called
homogeneous roof
. These special Fano varieties of Picard number two are classified in
[
Kan22
, Section 5.2.1]
.

One immediately computes
\dim Y=n^{2}-1
. The fact that
Y
is indeed a smooth Calabi–Yau variety of Picard rank one is a consequence of
[
Ram20
, Lemma 2.8]
and
[
FKMR21
, Proposition 2.3]
.

The description of
Y
as a smooth, transverse zero locus of a section gives rise to a Koszul exact sequence on
\operatorname{Gr}
:

(2.4)

0\longrightarrow\wedge^{n+1}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2n-2)\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow\wedge^{l}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2l)\longrightarrow\cdots\longrightarrow{\mathcal{Q}}(-2)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{I}}_{Y|\operatorname{Gr}}\longrightarrow 0.

. Then

The purpose of the next results (Lemma

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
Y\subset\operatorname{Gr}
be a Calabi–Yau variety of type

. Then
{\mathcal{Q}}|_{Y}
is slope-stable.

Proof.

We use Hoppe’s criterion
[

, Proposition 1]
: we need to check that the bundle

has no sections for

.

In light of the tensor product of the Koszul resolution for

(Equation

) with

, we must ensure that for every

. This is done in Lemma
A.4
.
∎

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
Y=Z(s)\subset\operatorname{Gr}
be a Calabi–Yau variety of type
A_{2n}^{G}
. Then there exists no nontrivial
g\in\operatorname{Aut}({\mathbb{P}})
such that
Y=Z(\widetilde{s})\subset\operatorname{Gr}_{g}
for
\widetilde{s}\in H^{0}(\operatorname{Gr}_{g},{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee}_{\operatorname{Gr}_{g}}(2))
.

Proof.

By contradiction, pick
g\in\operatorname{Aut}({\mathbb{P}})
as above. By
[
Arr96
, Proposition 2.1]
, the embedding of a subvariety
Y
in
\operatorname{Gr}_{g}
is determined by the isomorphism class of
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\operatorname{Gr}_{g}}|_{Y}
. We will now proceed essentially as in
[
BCP18
, proof of Lemma 2.2]
: observe that
V\simeq H^{0}(\operatorname{Gr}_{g},{\mathcal{Q}}_{\operatorname{Gr}_{g}})
, and that the Plücker embedding of
\operatorname{Gr}_{g}
is given by the isomorphism
\wedge^{n+1}H^{0}(\operatorname{Gr}_{g},{\mathcal{Q}}_{\operatorname{Gr}_{g}})^{\vee}\simeq H^{0}({\mathbb{P}},{\mathcal{O}}(1))
. Since by Lemma
A.6
one has
H^{0}(Y,{\mathcal{Q}}_{\operatorname{Gr}_{g}})\simeq H^{0}(\operatorname{Gr}_{g},{\mathcal{Q}}_{\operatorname{Gr}_{g}})
and
H^{0}(Y,{\mathcal{O}}(1))\simeq H^{0}({\mathbb{P}},{\mathcal{O}}(1))
, the isomorphism class of
{\mathcal{Q}}_{\operatorname{Gr}_{g}}|_{Y}
determines also the Plücker embedding
\operatorname{Gr}_{g}\xhookrightarrow{\,\,\,\,\,}{\mathbb{P}}
, and thus the isomorphism
g
. Since by
2.5
all restrictions of quotient bundles to
Y

∎

2.3.
The main construction

Let us consider the flag variety

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Fl}}

3.
{\mathbb{L}}
-equivalence, Hodge equivalence and non birationality

3.1.
Non birational Calabi–Yau pairs

Let
(Y_{-},Y_{+})
be a Calabi–Yau pair of type
A^{G}_{2n}
and consider a birational equivalence
f:Y_{-}\dashrightarrow Y_{+}
. There is a standard argument to show that
Y_{-}
and
Y_{+}
must be isomorphic (see, for instance,
[
Man19
, Proposition 4.4]
). We summarize it here. Since
Y_{-}
and
Y_{+}
are Calabi–Yau, they are minimal models, and this implies that
f
must be an isomorphism out of codimension two. However our varieties have Picard number one, and therefore
f
identifies the spaces of sections of the respective very ample generators of the Picard groups, yielding a projective equivalence.

The main result of this section is Proposition
3.2
: it states that no isomorphism
f:Y_{-}\longrightarrow Y_{+}
can exist, and thus, by the discussion above,
Y_{-}
and
Y_{+}
cannot be birationally equivalent. We will begin by proving the following lemma, which is necessary for Proposition
3.2
.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists a representation
{\mathbb{S}}^{\lambda}V
such that in the decomposition of the plethysm
{\mathbb{S}}^{\lambda}\circ{\mathbb{S}}^{(1^{n})}V
there is a summand of the form
{\mathbb{S}}^{(k^{2n+1})}V
with multipilcity higher than one.

Proof.

As observed in
[
Man19
, proof of Proposition 4.4]
, if there were no such
\lambda
, then there would be a dense orbit of the action of
GL(V)
on the complete flag variety
F(1,2,\dots,\wedge^{n}V)
. We rule out this possibility by a simple dimension count. Fix
N=\dim\wedge^{n}V
. One has a tower of surjections:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F(1,\dots,\wedge^{n}V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F(2,\dots,\wedge^{n}V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\vdots}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F(N-2,N-1,\wedge^{n}V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces G(N-1,\wedge^{n}V)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{2}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{N-3}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f_{N-2}}

where
f_{k}
is a
{\mathbb{P}}^{k}
-bundle for every
k
. It follows that:

\dim F(1,\dots,\wedge^{n}V)=1+2+\cdots+N-1=\frac{N(N-1)}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\binom{2n+1}{n}\left[\binom{2n+1}{n}-1\right]

Since
GL(V)
has dimension
(2n+1)^{2}
, which for
n>1
is strictly smaller then the number above, the
GL(V)
-orbit of any point of
F(1,\dots,\wedge^{n}V)
cannot be dense.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
S\subset H^{0}(\operatorname{Fl},{\mathcal{O}}(1,1))
be general. Then the associated pair of Calabi–Yau varieties are not birationally equivalent.

Proof.

We can assume that the class of equivalence of a general
S
up to scalar multiplication is an element of
PGL(\wedge^{n}V)
. By Lemma
2.11
, an isomorphism
f:Y_{-}\longrightarrow Y_{+}
would be such that
\widetilde{\iota}_{f}(S)=S
up to rescaling. Hence, by Lemma
2.10
, proving that there is no such isomorphism amounts to find
S
such that there is no
M
satisfying
SM=MS^{T}
, where
M
is chosen among (extensions to
{\mathbb{P}}_{-}
of) of isomorphisms
\operatorname{Gr}_{-}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Gr}_{+}
. Fix
G=PGL(V)\times PGL(V)
. Following the approach of
[
OR18
, proof of Lemma 4.7]
, the proposition is proven if we verify a stronger statement, i.e. that the equivalence class of
S
in
PGL(\wedge^{2}V)
is not in the orbit of the one of
S^{T}
under the following
G
-action:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces PGL(\wedge^{2}V)\times G}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces PGL(\wedge^{2}V)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\displaystyle{[S],[M],[N]}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\displaystyle{[\psi(M)^{-1}S\psi(N)]}}

where
\psi(M)
is the matrix of minors of order
n
of
M
, and the same for
N
. Let us first prove that the general
S\in SL(\wedge^{2}V)
is not in the same
\widetilde{G}
-orbit of
S^{T}
where
\widetilde{G}:=SL(V)\times SL(V)
, since the argument is easier and it can be easily adapted to prove the main result afterwards. To prove this simpler statement is enough to verify the following:

Claim.
There exists a function
h\in{\mathbb{C}}[SL(\wedge^{n}V)]^{\widetilde{G}}
which is not preserved by the involution
\tau:g\longmapsto g^{t}
of
SL(\wedge^{n}V)
.

First note that

{\mathbb{C}}[SL(\wedge^{n}V)]^{\widetilde{G}}={\mathbb{C}}[\wedge^{n}V^{\vee}\otimes\wedge^{n}V]/(\det-1)

and that there is a decomposition

\begin{split}{\mathbb{C}}[\wedge^{n}V^{\vee}\otimes\wedge^{n}V]&=\bigoplus_{k}\Sym^{k}(\wedge^{n}V^{\vee}\otimes\wedge^{n}V)\\
&=\bigoplus_{k}\bigoplus_{\lambda\vdash k}{\mathbb{S}}^{\lambda}\wedge^{n}V^{\vee}\otimes{\mathbb{S}}^{\lambda}\wedge^{n}V.\end{split}

If we now take
\alpha\in\wedge^{n}V^{\vee}
,
\beta\in\wedge^{n}V
and the isomorphism
T:\wedge^{n}V\longrightarrow\wedge^{n}V^{\vee}
defined by a choice of a basis of
V
, we immediately see that for every
\lambda
the action of
\tau
is

\tau:{\mathbb{S}}^{\lambda}\alpha\otimes{\mathbb{S}}^{\lambda}\beta\longmapsto{\mathbb{S}}^{\lambda}T^{-1}(\beta)\otimes{\mathbb{S}}^{\lambda}T(\alpha)

By Lemma
3.1
we can find a partition
\lambda
such that
{\mathbb{S}}^{\lambda}\circ{\mathbb{S}}^{(1^{n})}V
, as a sum of irreducible
GL(V)
-representations, contains a one-dimensional summand of multiplicity higher than one: this implies that
\dim({\mathbb{S}}^{\lambda}\wedge^{n}V)^{SL(V)}>1
. Hence, choosing a general pair
\alpha\in\wedge^{n}V^{\vee}
,
\beta\in\wedge^{n}V
, the function
{\mathbb{S}}^{\lambda}\alpha\otimes{\mathbb{S}}^{\lambda}\beta\in{\mathbb{C}}[\wedge^{n}V^{\vee}\otimes\wedge^{n}V]^{\widetilde{G}}
is not fixed by
\tau
, and the same holds for its image
h
through the quotient map
{\mathbb{C}}[\wedge^{n}V^{\vee}\otimes\wedge^{n}V]^{\widetilde{G}}\longrightarrow{\mathbb{C}}[SL(\wedge^{n}V)]^{\widetilde{G}}
. This settles the claim.

Observe now that choosing
h^{2}
instead of
h
gives rise to a
G
-invariant function which descends to the quotient
{\mathbb{C}}[PGL(\wedge^{2}V)]^{G}
, and which is not fixed by
\tau
.

This proves that
Y_{-}
and
Y_{+}
are not isomorphic, hence they are not projectvely equivalent. By the discussion above (beginning of Section
3.1
), we conclude that they are also not birationally equivalent.
∎

3.2.
Every roof leads to

-equivalent pairs

Let us, for a moment, work in the more general context of homogeneous roofs in the sense of
[

, Section 5.1.1]
. Fix a homogeneous roof

, with

simply connected, simple Lie group and

parabolic. It comes with two projective bundle structures

where

is a Picard rank one rational homogeneous variety, with

. Call

the structure maps of the projective bundle morphisms.

Given

on

, one has
p_{\pm*}{\mathcal{O}}(1,1)={\mathcal{E}}_{\pm}
. For a general section
s\in H^{0}(X,{\mathcal{O}}(1,1)
define
M:=Z(S)\subset X
and
Y_{\pm}:=Z(p_{\pm*}s)\subset X_{\pm}
. Then, by restricting

to

we find morphisms:

(3.1)

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{-}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X_{+}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q_{-}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q_{+}}

As observed in
[

these maps are

projective bundle fibrations and their fibers are:

(3.2)

where

. This leads to the following identities in the Grothendieck ring of varieties:

(3.3)

\displaystyle[X]

\displaystyle=[X_{\pm}](1+{\mathbb{L}}+\cdots+{\mathbb{L}}^{r-1})

(3.4)

\displaystyle[M]

\displaystyle=[X_{\pm}](1+{\mathbb{L}}+\cdots+{\mathbb{L}}^{r-2})+[Y_{\pm}]{\mathbb{L}}^{r-1}

Proposition 3.3
.

Let

be a Calabi–Yau pair associated to a homogeneous roof

as above. Then one has:

(3.5)

([Y_{-}]-[Y_{+}]){\mathbb{L}}^{r-1}=0.

Proof.

By Equation

we immediately write (cf.

, Equation 2.2]

(3.6)

([X_{-}]-[X_{+}])(1+{\mathbb{L}}+\cdots+{\mathbb{L}}^{r-2})+([Y_{-}]-[Y_{+}]){\mathbb{L}}^{r-1}=0

and therefore we are able to conclude if we can show that

. This last equality is essentially
[

, proof of Proposition 2.3]
: the authors address the single case

, but their argument is general enough to be applied in the present context without modification.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Note that the argument of
[

]
is not necessary to prove that Equation

3.3.
Hodge equivalence for the odd-dimensional pairs

Recall the following standard terminology:

Definition 3.5
.

We say that two varieties
Y_{-}
and
Y_{+}
of dimension
d
are Hodge-equivalent if there exists an isometry
H^{d}(Y_{-},{\mathbb{Z}})\longrightarrow H^{d}(Y_{+},{\mathbb{Z}})
with respect to the cup product.

Let us come back to the setting of Diagram
3.1
, where we set
d=\dim G/P_{\pm}
, and consequently
\dim M=d+r-2
and
\dim Y_{\pm}=d-r
. By
[

, Proposition 48]
one has the following decompositions of the middle cohomology group of

(3.7)

H^{d+r-2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})\simeq H^{d-r}(Y_{\pm},{\mathbb{Z}})\oplus H^{d-r+2}(G/P_{\pm},{\mathbb{Z}})\oplus\cdots\oplus H^{d+r-2}(G/P_{\pm},{\mathbb{Z}})

Observe that for

odd, all summands coming from
G/P_{\pm}
have odd degree: however, since
G/P_{\pm}
is rational homogeneous, its cohomology is purely algebraic, thus concentrated in even degree. Therefore, the decompositions of Equation

yield the following isomorphism:

(3.8)

H^{d-r}(Y_{-},{\mathbb{Z}})\simeq H^{d+r-2}(M,{\mathbb{Z}})\simeq H^{d-r}(Y_{+},{\mathbb{Z}})

It is possible to show that Equation

lifts to an isomorphism of

Y_{-}
and
Y_{+}
, by showing that the isomorphisms of Equation
3.8
preserve the cup product. This is the content of the following result, which we state without proof as an immediate corollary of
[

, Proposition 3.4]
:

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
(Y_{-},Y_{+})
be a Calabi–Yau pair of type
A^{G}_{2n}
, with
n
odd. Then there exists an isometry of integral Hodge structures:

(3.9)

H^{n^{2}-1}(Y_{-},{\mathbb{Z}})\longrightarrow H^{n^{2}-1}(Y_{+},{\mathbb{Z}}).

4.
Relation with intersections of translates

In
[
GP01
,
Kap08
,
Kan10
,
Kap11
]
intersections of two translates of
\operatorname{Gr}_{+}\subset{\mathbb{P}}_{+}
have been considered, for
n=2
. With this construction, they described a 51 -dimensional family
{\mathcal{X}}_{25}
of Calabi–Yau threefolds, among which
[
OR18
]
and
[
BCP18
]
constructed counterexamples to the birational Torelli theorem. In
[
OR18
]
, a divisor in
{\mathcal{X}}_{25}
has been described in terms of intersections of “infinitesimal” translates, i.e. zero loci of the normal bundle of
\operatorname{Gr}_{+}
in
{\mathbb{P}}
. These are Calabi–Yau threefolds of type
A^{G}_{4}
: for
Y\in{\mathcal{X}}_{25}
one can check that, while the dimension of the moduli space is
\dim{\mathcal{X}}_{25}=h^{1}(T_{Y})=51
, the number of parameters describing
Y=Z(s)
for
s\in H^{0}(\operatorname{Gr}_{+},{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee}(2))
is
h^{0}(\operatorname{Gr}_{+},{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee}(2))-\dim\operatorname{Aut}\operatorname{Gr}_{+}=75-24=51
, and therefore the
A^{G}_{4}
construction cannot describe the whole family
{\mathcal{X}}_{25}
. While for the general case
V\simeq{\mathbb{C}}^{2n+1}
the intersection of two general translates of
\operatorname{Gr}_{+}
is empty, it is a natural question to ask, for a Calabi–Yau
(n^{2}-1)
-fold of type
A^{G}_{2n}
, whether
h^{1,n^{2}-1}(Y)
and
h^{0}(\operatorname{Gr}_{+},{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee}(2))-\dim\operatorname{Aut}\operatorname{Gr}_{+}
coincide.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
Y\subset\operatorname{Gr}_{+}
be a Calabi–Yau
(n^{2}-1)
-fold of type
A^{G}_{2n}
. Assume
n>2
. Then
h^{1,n^{2}-1}(Y)=h^{0}(\operatorname{Gr}_{+},{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee}(2))-\dim\operatorname{Aut}\operatorname{Gr}_{+}-1
.

Proof.

By Serre duality
h^{1,n^{2}-1}(Y)=h^{n^{2}-1}(Y,\Omega^{1}_{Y})=h^{1}(Y,T_{Y})
. Then, by the normal bundle sequence, we need to compute the cohomology of
T_{\operatorname{Gr}_{+}}|_{Y}\simeq{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{Q}}|_{Y}
and
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee}(2)|_{Y}\simeq\wedge^{n}{\mathcal{Q}}(1)|_{Y}
.

Let us begin wth the latter. The tensor product of the Koszul resolution
2.4
with
\wedge^{n}{\mathcal{Q}}(1)
is given by composing the long exact sequence
2.6
by the short exact sequence defining the ideal sheaf
{\mathcal{I}}_{Y|\operatorname{Gr}_{+}}
. The
l
-th term of such resolution is
\wedge^{l}{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\wedge^{n}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2l+1)
, and for
l>1
it has no cohomology by Lemma
A.3
. For
l=1
one has
{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\wedge^{n}{\mathcal{Q}}(-1)\simeq{\mathbb{S}}^{(2,1^{n-1},0)}{\mathcal{Q}}(-1)\oplus{\mathcal{O}}
where the second summand has cohomology concentrated in degree zero and dimension one, while the first summand does not contribute (again by Lemma
A.3
). For
l=0
the bundle has cohomology concentrated in degree zero. Thus, we deduce that
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee}(2)|_{Y}
has no cohomology in higher degree, while
h^{0}(Y,{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee}(2)|_{Y})=h^{0}(\operatorname{Gr}_{+},{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee}(2))-1
.

Consider now
{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{Q}}|_{Y}
. This object, similarly to the former, has a Koszul resolution whose
l
-th term is
{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\wedge^{l}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2l)
. By tensoring it with the tautological short exact sequence (Equation
2.1
), we obtain the following resolution:

0\longrightarrow\wedge^{n}{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\wedge^{l}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2l-1)\longrightarrow V^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\wedge^{l}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2l)\longrightarrow{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\wedge^{l}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2l)\longrightarrow 0.

Consider the case
l=0
: there it is known that
h^{0}(\operatorname{Gr}_{+},{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{Q}})
is the dimension of the adjoint representation of
SL(V)
, which is
\dim\operatorname{Aut}\operatorname{Gr}_{+}
. For higher values of
l
the computations are covered in Lemma
A.7
, where we conclude that the only contributing term is for
l=n+1
. However, while such term gives
H^{n^{2}-n-1}(\operatorname{Gr},{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\wedge^{n-+}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2n-2)={\mathbb{C}}
, such cohomology does not contribute to
H^{1}(Y,T_{Y})
because the degree is too high compared to the length of the Koszul resolution.

Summing all up, we find
h^{1,n^{2}-1}(Y)=h^{0}(\operatorname{Gr}_{+},{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee}(2))-\dim\operatorname{Aut}\operatorname{Gr}_{+}-1
, thus the proof is concluded.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

One might observe that, doing the same computation for
n=2
, a difference appears in the Koszul resolution of
{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{Q}}|_{Y}
: in fact, while for
n>2
the term
{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}\otimes{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes{\mathcal{Q}}(-2)\simeq{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}\otimes\wedge^{2}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2)\oplus{\mathcal{U}}^{\vee}\otimes\Sym^{2}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2)
has no cohomology, for
n=2
its first direct summand has a non-trivial (one dimensional)
H^{1}
, which is responsible to the difference in the dimension count of Lemma
4.1
, and thus to the fact that the construction as varieties of type
A^{G}_{4}
cannot describe the whole family
{\mathcal{X}}_{25}
.

Appendix A
Borel–Weil–Bott computations

We will gather here all technical statements which come as applications of the Borel–Weil–Bott theorem
[
Bot57
]
, which are needed in the previous sections. Let us begin by recalling the statement of the theorem.

Theorem A.1

(Borel–Weil–Bott)
.

Let
{\mathcal{E}}_{\omega}
be a homogeneous, irreducible vector bundle on a rational homogeneous variety
G/P
, let us call
\rho
the sum of all fundamental weights. Then one and only one of the following statements is true:

(1)

there exists a sequence
s_{p}
of simple Weyl reflections of length
p
such that
s_{p}(\omega+\rho)-\rho
is dominant (i.e. all coefficients of its the expansion in the fundamental weights are non-negative). Then
H^{p}(G/P,{\mathcal{E}}_{\omega})\simeq{\mathbb{V}}^{G}_{s_{p}(\omega+\rho)-\rho}
and all the other cohomology is trivial.

(2)

there is no such
s_{p}
as in point (1). Then
{\mathcal{E}}_{\omega}
has no cohomology.

In the case
G/P=SL(V)/P_{k}\simeq G(k,V)
, which is the only one we are interested in, the Weyl reflection associated to the simple root
\alpha_{i}
acts as follows:

(A.1)

s_{\alpha_{i}}(\omega)_{j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\omega_{j}&j\notin\{i-1;i;i+1\}\\
\omega_{j}-\omega_{i}&j\in\{i-1;i+1\}\\
-\omega_{i}&j=i.\end{array}\right.

Remark A.2
.

Note that an alternative, simpler formulation of the Borel–Weil–Bott theorem for Grassmannians is described in
[
BCP18
, Appendix A]
. However, for the purpose of the computations below, we choose the general formulation. In fact, the combinatorial aspects of the proof of Lemma
A.3
turn out to be far more readable in this language.

Let us switch again to the shorthand notation
\operatorname{Gr}:=G(n,V)
for
V\simeq{\mathbb{C}}^{2n+1}
, and assume
n>2
. All the cohomological vanishings needed in this paper boil down to the content of the following lemma, which we state slightly more generally:

Lemma A.3
.

For
0\leq\lambda_{i}\leq n-1
and
0<i<2n+1
one has:

\begin{split}H^{\bullet}(\operatorname{Gr},{\mathbb{S}}^{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n+1})}{\mathcal{Q}}(-i))&=0\end{split}

Proof.

By the discussion of Section
2.1
the weight associated to
{\mathbb{S}}^{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n+1})}{\mathcal{Q}}(-i)
has the form:

\begin{array}[]{cccccccccc}\omega&=(0^{n-1},&-i,&j_{1}&\dots&j_{n})\end{array}

where
\sum_{k}j_{k}\leq n-1
. Adding the sum of fundamental weights yields:

\begin{array}[]{cccccccccc}\omega+\rho&=(1^{n-1},&1-i,&1+j_{1}&\dots&1+j_{n})\end{array}

Let us now perform, repeatedly, the operation of applying the Weyl reflection which changes sign of the leftmost negative entry. If
i\leq n
, we will eventually find a zero in one of the first
n-1
entries. Then, applying again the same algorithm, we will find a weight with no negative entries and at least a zero, which by Theorem
A.1
implies that
{\mathbb{S}}^{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n+1})}{\mathcal{Q}}(-i)
has no cohomology.

Otherwise, if
i>n
, after exactly
n
repetitions of the operation described above, we find the weight:

\begin{array}[]{cccccccccc}w_{1}\circ\cdots\circ w_{n}(\omega+\rho)&=(i-n,&1^{n-1},&2+j_{1}-i,&1+j_{2}&\dots&1+j_{n})\end{array}

Recall that our assumptions on
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
imply
j_{1}<n
: since
i>n
, it follows that
2+j_{1}-i
cannot be positive. If it is negative, we can repeat the same procedure starting by applying
w_{n+1}
: if
n-1+j_{1}-i\leq 0
we hit again a zero in the one of the first
n
entries and we proceed as above, otherwise we end up with the weight:

\begin{array}[]{cccccccccc}\prod_{t=1}^{n+1}w_{t}\circ\prod_{t=1}^{n}w_{t}(\omega+\rho)&=(j_{1}+i-n,&1^{n},&3+j_{1}+j_{2}-i,&1+j_{3}&\dots&1+j_{n})\end{array}

where again
3+j_{1}+j_{2}-i
is negative. This algorithm can be repeated until we either find a weight with no negative entries and at least one zero (and therefore no cohomology), or in the case
n+1+\sum_{t}j_{t}-i<0
we end up with the weight

\begin{array}[]{cccccccccc}\prod_{t=1}^{2n-1}w_{t}\circ\cdots\circ\prod_{t=1}^{n}w_{t}(\omega+\rho)&=(\sum j_{t}+i-n,&1^{2n-2},&n+1+\sum j_{t}-i).\end{array}

Here, if
\sum j_{t}+3n-1-i<0
, we might get nonvanishing cohomology in degree
\dim G=n^{2}+n
: however, this cannot happen for the assumption
0<i<2n+1
. Therefore, we conclude that
{\mathbb{S}}^{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n+1})}{\mathcal{Q}}(-i)
has no cohomology.∎

Lemma A.4
.

For
0\leq l\leq n+1
, the bundle
\wedge^{k}{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\wedge^{l}{\mathcal{Q}}(-1-2l)
has no cohomology in degree
p<n+1
.

Proof.

By the Littlewood–Richardson formula, the bundle
\wedge^{k}{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\wedge^{l}{\mathcal{Q}}(-1-2l)
decomposes in summands which are of the form we treated in Lemma
A.3
, and hence none of them contributes to the cohomology.
∎

Lemma A.5
.

For
0\leq k\leq n+1
and
p<n+1
one has:

H^{p}(\operatorname{Gr},{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee}(2)\otimes\wedge^{k}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2k))=0.

Proof.

One has, for
0\leq k\leq n+1
:

(A.2)

\begin{split}H^{p}(\operatorname{Gr},{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee}(2)\otimes\wedge^{k}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2k))&=H^{p}(\operatorname{Gr},\wedge^{n}{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\wedge^{k}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2k+1))\\
&=H^{p}(\operatorname{Gr},{\mathbb{S}}^{(2^{k},1^{n-k})}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2k+1))\oplus H^{p}(\operatorname{Gr},\wedge^{k-1}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2k+2)).\\
\end{split}

Both summands are of the type discussed in Lemma
A.3
, and therefore they have no cohomology.
∎

Lemma A.6
.

Consider
Y=Z(s)
, where
s\in H^{0}(\operatorname{Gr},{\mathcal{Q}}_{\operatorname{Gr}}^{\vee}(2))
is general. Then one has:

\begin{split}H^{0}(Y,{\mathcal{O}}(1))&\simeq H^{0}({\mathbb{P}},{\mathcal{O}}(1))\\
H^{0}(Y,{\mathcal{Q}}_{\operatorname{Gr}}^{\vee}(2)|_{Y})&\simeq H^{0}(\operatorname{Gr},{\mathcal{Q}}_{\operatorname{Gr}}^{\vee}(2))\end{split}

Proof.

Since
{\mathbb{P}}:={\mathbb{P}}(\wedge^{n}V)
one has
H^{0}({\mathbb{P}},{\mathcal{O}}(1))=\wedge^{n}V^{\vee}
, and by the Borel–Weil–Bott theorem one easily shows that
H^{0}(\operatorname{Gr},{\mathcal{O}}(1))=\wedge^{n}V^{\vee}
, hence the two spaces are equal. To show that
H^{0}(\operatorname{Gr},{\mathcal{O}}(1))=H^{0}(Y,{\mathcal{O}}(1))
we take the tensor product of the Koszul exact sequence
2.4
by
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
. By Lemma
A.3
all terms have no cohomology except for
{\mathcal{O}}(1)
and
{\mathcal{O}}_{Y}(1)
, and this settles the first claim.

To address the second one, this time we tensor the sequence
2.4
by
{\mathcal{Q}}
: all terms but
{\mathcal{Q}}
and
{\mathcal{Q}}|_{Y}
are of the form we discussed in Lemma
A.3
, and therefore they have no cohomology.
∎

Lemma A.7
.

For
1\leq l\leq n+1
one has
H^{\bullet}(\operatorname{Gr},\wedge^{n}{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\wedge^{l}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2l-1))=H^{\bullet}(\operatorname{Gr},{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\wedge^{l}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2l))=0
, except for
l=n+1
where
H^{n^{2}-n}(\operatorname{Gr},\wedge^{n}{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\wedge^{l}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2l-1))={\mathbb{C}}
.

Proof.

For
l<n
by an expansion similar to Equation
A.2
one can use the Littlewood–Richardson formula to write both the bundles as direct sums of objects of the form we already treated in Lemma
A.3
, therefore the proof reduces to considering the cases
l=n
and
l=n+1
. We start by considering both bundles for
l=n
:

\wedge^{n}{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\wedge^{n}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2n-1)={\mathbb{S}}^{(3,2^{n-1},0)}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2n-1)\oplus{\mathcal{E}}

where
{\mathcal{E}}
is again a sum of terms which do not contribute by Lemma
A.3
. The easiest way to show that the first summand has no cohomology is to use Serre duality:

H^{p}(\operatorname{Gr},{\mathbb{S}}^{(3,2^{n-1},0)}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2n-1))\simeq H^{n^{2}-n-p}(\operatorname{Gr},{\mathbb{S}}^{(3,2^{n-1},0)}{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee})\simeq H^{n^{2}-n-p}(\operatorname{Gr},{\mathbb{S}}^{(3,1^{n-1},0)}{\mathcal{Q}}^{\vee}(-3)),

and to conclude by Lemma
A.3
. The same exact steps prove that
{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\wedge^{n}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2n)
has no cohomology.

Fix now
l=n+1
: the first bundle is

\wedge^{n}{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\wedge^{n+1}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2n-3)=\wedge^{n}{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes{\mathcal{Q}}(-2n-2)={\mathcal{O}}(-2n-1)\oplus{\mathbb{S}}^{(2,1^{n-1},0)}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2n-2).

Here the first summand contributes with
H^{n^{2}-n}(\operatorname{Gr},{\mathcal{O}}(-2n-1))=H^{0}(\operatorname{Gr},{\mathcal{O}})\simeq{\mathbb{C}}
. The second summand, by the same kind of argument, gives no contribution, and the same happens for
{\mathcal{Q}}\otimes\wedge^{n+1}{\mathcal{Q}}(-2n-2)\simeq{\mathcal{Q}}(-2n-1)
.
∎
