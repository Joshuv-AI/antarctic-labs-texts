Sub-Poissonian multiplicity distributions in jets produced in hadron collisions
Han Wei Ang, Maciej Rybczyński, Grzegorz Wilk, Zbigniew Włodarczyk

Abstract
In this work we show that the proper analysis and interpretation of the experimental data on the multiplicity distributions of charged particles produced in jets measured in the ATLAS experiment at the LHC indicates their sub-Poissonian nature. We also show how, by using the recurrent relations and combinants of these distributions, one can obtain new information contained in them and otherwise unavailable, which may broaden our knowledge of the particle production mechanism.

I
Introduction

The experimentally measured multiplicity distributions
P(N)
of the produced particles are the main source of information about the dynamics of their production processes
Kittel
;
Book-BP
. In the theoretical description they are characterized by the generating functions,

G(z)=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}P(N)z^{N},

(1)

such that

P(N)=\frac{1}{N!}\frac{d^{N}G(z)}{dz^{N}}\bigg|_{z=0}.

(2)

Preliminary information about
P(N)
is provided by the moments of this distribution,

m_{k}=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}(N-c)^{k}P(N).

(3)

In many cases, it is sufficient to analyze only the two lowest moments, namely the mean value
\langle N\rangle=m_{1}
being the first raw moment
(c=0)
, and variance
Var(N)=m_{2}
, being the second central moment (
c=\langle N\rangle
).

Another way to characterize
P(N)
is through a recursive formula,

(N+1)P(N+1)=g(N)P(N),

(4)

that connects adjacent values of
P(N)
for the production of
N
and
(N+1)
particles. It is assumed here that every
P(N)
is determined only by the next lower
P(N-1)
value. In other words, a relationship with others
P(N-j)
for
j>1
is indirect. The final algebraic form of
P(N)
is determined by the function
g(N)
. In its simplest form,
g(N)
is assumed to be a linear function of
N
given by
g(N)=\alpha+\beta N
. This form is enough to define commonly known and widely used distributions like Poisson Distribution (PD), for which
\beta=0
), Binomial Distribution (BD) (for which
\beta<0
) or Negative Binomial Distribution (for which
\beta>0
). In general, by selecting the appropriate model, the form
g(N)
can be chosen in such a way that the corresponding
P(N)
describes the experimental data (for example, by introducing higher order terms
HC
or by using its more involved forms
ChK
;
Zg
).

The more promising approach is to use
g(N)
which contains information about the interrelationship between the multiplicity
N
and all smaller multiplicities recursively,

(N+1)P(N+1)=\langle N\rangle\sum^{N}_{j=0}C_{j}P(N-j).

(5)

The memory of that relationship is encoded in coefficients
C_{j}
called
modified combinants
. They were introduced and intensively discussed in
ST
;
Ours1
;
IJMPA
;
Ours2
;
Ours3
;
Ours-EPJA
;
IJMPA-WW
;
Zborovsky
. By inverting the recursion (
5
) we obtain an equation that allows us to determine
C_{j}
from the measured
P(N)
,

\langle N\rangle C_{j}=(j+1)\left[\frac{P(j+1)}{P(0)}\right]-\langle N\rangle\sum^{j-1}_{i=0}C_{i}\left[\frac{P(j-i)}{P(0)}\right]

(6)

(provided we have sufficient statistics). Modified combinants are closely related to
combinants

C^{\star}_{j}
,

C_{j}=\frac{j+1}{\langle N\rangle}C^{\star}_{j+1},

(7)

introduced in
Combinants-1
;
Combinants-2
;
VVP
by means of the generating functions
G(z)
as

\langle N\rangle C^{\star}_{j}=\frac{1}{j!}\frac{d^{j+1}\ln G(z)}{dz^{j+1}}\bigg|_{z=0},

(8)

which have been discussed and used in many publications
Kittel
;
PCarruthers
;
Book-BP
;
CombUse1a
;
CombUse3
;
CombUse4
;
CombUse5
.

Modified combinants are complementary to the commonly used factorial moments,
F_{q}
and cumulant factorial moments,
K_{q}
(see Appendix
A
for details). They differ in that while
C_{j}
’s depend only on multiplicities smaller than their rank,
K_{q}
’s require the knowledge of all
P(N)
’s and are therefore very sensitive to possible limitations of the available phase space
Kittel
;
Book-BP
. However, both
C_{j}
and
K_{q}
share the property of additivity. It turns out that most of the measured multiplicity distributions
P(N)
give oscillatory combinants
C_{j}
with increasing index
j
which arise due to the presence of a BD component in the measured
P(N)

Ours1
;
IJMPA
;
Ours2
;
Ours3
;
Ours-EPJA
;
IJMPA-WW
.

Combinants are believed to be best suited for the study of sparsely populated areas of phase space (while cumulants are better suited for the study of densely populated areas)
Kittel
;
Book-BP
. This feature makes them a potentially important tool for studying
P(N)
in jets where the number of produced particles is small (on the order of
\sim 10
). However, the ATLAS data
AtlasJ
do not include
P(0)
, which is crucial for their determination. This means that in our analysis, we must extrapolate the recurrent relation in Eq. (
4
) to evaluate
P(0)
and use the combinants only for additional verification of our conclusions.

In the next Section we provide details concerning ATLAS data with particular attention to the fact that the measured multiplicity distributions in the jets are clearly sub-Poissonian in character (with details depending on the phase-space covered). This observation will be our main point for further discussion and calculations described in Sections
III
and
IV
. Section
V
summarizes and concludes our work.

g(N)=(N+1)\frac{P(N+1)}{P(N)}=\frac{\alpha}{(N+1)^{\delta}}.

III
Possible explanation: Multiplicity dependent birth and death rates

To interpret the results shown in the previous section, note that Eq. (
13
) actually represents the so-called COM-Poisson distribution introduced by Conway and Maxwell
COM1962
as a model for steady state queuing systems with state-dependent arrival or service rates (in other words, birth-death process with Poisson arrival rate and exponential service rate). It was rediscovered in
Shmuel
where the term
Conway-Maxwell-Poisson
was proposed and a detailed study of its properties and applications was performed. More recent studies can be found in
ChI
;
ChShmuel
. To our best knowledge, this distribution has not been used in the analysis of multiplicity distributions of particles produced in multiparticle production processes.

We will now show that the form of the COM-Poissonian distribution can be obtained from a stochastic Markov process with multiplicity-dependent birth and death rates denoted by
\lambda_{N}
and
\mu_{N}
, respectively
LZH
. Let
P(N,t)
be the probability of having
N
particles at time
t
and let us consider a very general birth-death process given by the following equations:

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!P^{\prime}(0,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lambda_{0}P(0,t)+\mu_{1}P(1,t),

(25)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!P^{\prime}(N,t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left(\lambda_{N}+\mu_{N}\right)P(N,t)+

(26)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{N-1}P(N-1,r)+\mu_{N+1}P(N+1,t).

If we assume the forms

\lambda_{N}=\frac{\lambda}{(N+1)^{a}}\quad{\rm and}\quad\mu_{N}=N^{b}\mu,

(27)

we get

\displaystyle-\left[\frac{\lambda}{(N+1)^{a}}+N^{b}\mu\right]P(N)+

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76219pt+\frac{\lambda}{N^{a}}P(N-1)+(N+1)^{b}\mu P(N+1)=0

(28)

for the steady state, where
P^{\prime}(N,t)=0
. If we denote

\frac{\lambda}{\mu}=\alpha

(29)

we can re-write Eq. (
28
) as

\displaystyle-\frac{\alpha}{(N+1)^{a}}P(N)-N^{b}P(N)+

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76219pt+\frac{\alpha}{N^{a}}P(N-1)+(N+1)^{b}P(N+1)=0

(30)

which leads to the recurrent relation

(N+1)^{b}P(N+1)=\frac{\alpha}{(N+1)^{a}}P(N).

(31)

Further simplifications can be made by writing

a+b=\nu

(32)

which gives us

\frac{P(N+1)}{P(N)}=\frac{\alpha}{(N+1)^{\nu}}.

(33)

For
\delta=\nu-1
, this is just the recurrent form of COM-Poisson distribution defined by Eq. (
12
). The condition in Eq. (
32
) has allowed us to successfully re-parameterize the recurrent relation of Eq. (
33
) using
\alpha
and
\nu=a+b
. This corresponds to the entire class of Markov processes previously characterized by parameters
a
and
b
with the birth and death rates given in Eq. (
27
).

However, to describe the distribution defined by equations (
20
) and (
21
) (which is no longer COM-Poisson distribution) still using the birth-death process we need to add an additional term
\lambda^{\star}
to the birth rate
\lambda_{N}
in the form given by

\lambda_{N}=\frac{\lambda}{(N+1)^{a}}+\lambda^{\star}(N+1)^{(b-1)}.

(34)

If we substitute (
34
) into (
26
) and denote

\frac{\lambda^{\star}}{\mu}=\alpha_{0}

(35)

we get the recurrent relation

\frac{P(N+1)}{P(N)}=\frac{\alpha}{(N+1)^{\nu}}+\frac{\alpha_{0}}{N+1}

(36)

corresponding to Eq. (
20
).
