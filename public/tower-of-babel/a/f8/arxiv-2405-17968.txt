Matroid Semi-Bandits in Sublinear Time
Ruo-Chun Tzeng, Naoto Ohsaka, Kaito Ariu

Abstract
We study the matroid semi-bandits problem, where at each round the learner plays a subset of K K arms from a feasible set, and the goal is to maximize the expected cumulative linear rewards. Existing algorithms have per-round time complexity at least Ω ⁡ ( K ) \Omega(K) , which becomes expensive when K K is large. To address this computational issue, we propose FasterCUCB whose sampling rule takes time sublinear in K K for common classes of matroids: 𝒪 ⁡ ( D ​ polylog ​ ( K ) ​ polylog ​ ( T ) ) \mathcal{O}(D\,\text{polylog}\left({K}\right)\,\text{polylog}\left({T}\right)) for uniform matroids, partition matroids, and graphical matroids, and 𝒪 ⁡ ( D ​ K ​ polylog ​ ( T ) ) \mathcal{O}(D\sqrt{K}\text{polylog}\left({T}\right)) for transversal matroids. Here, D D is the maximum number of elements in any feasible subset of arms, and T T is the horizon. Our technique is based on dynamic maintenance of an approximate maximum-weight basis over inner-product weights. Although the introduction of an approximate maximum-weight basis presents a challenge in regret analysis, we can still guarantee an upper bound on regret as tight as CUCB in the sense that it matches the gap-dependent lower bound by Kveton et al. (2014a) asymptotically.

Introduction

Matroid semi-bandits model many real-world tasks.
An instance of matroid semi-bandit is described by
([K],\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol{\mu})
, where
[K]\triangleq\{1,\cdots,K\}
is the ground set, each
k\in[K]
is associated with a probability distribution
\nu_{k}
with mean
\mu_{k}
, and
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{K}
is the set of bases of a given matroid
\mathcal{M}=([K],\mathcal{I})
of rank
D
.
At each round
t\in[T]
, the learner pulls an action
\boldsymbol{x}(t)\in\mathcal{X}
and observes a
semi-bandit
feedback, i.e.,
y_{k}(t)\sim\nu_{k}
iff
x_{k}(t)=1

This formulation can be used to model online advertisting and news selection
(
Kale et al., 2010
)
with
\mathcal{M}
as a uniform matroid. Ad placement
(
Bubeck et al., 2013
;
Streeter et al., 2009
)
and diversified recommendation
(
Abbassi et al., 2013
)
can be modeled with
\mathcal{M}
as a partition matroid. Network routing
(
Kveton et al., 2014a
)
can be modeled with
\mathcal{M}
as a graphical matroid.
Task assignment
(
Chen et al., 2016
)
can be modeled with
\mathcal{M}
as a transversal matroid.

Popular algorithms include Combinatorial Upper Confidence Bound (
CUCB
)
(
Gai et al., 2012
;
Chen et al., 2013
;
Kveton et al., 2014a
;
Kveton et al., 2015
)
, Combinatorial Thompson Sampling (

)
(
Wang and Chen, 2018
;
Kong et al., 2021
;
Perrault, 2022
)
, and the instance-specifically optimal algorithm KL-based Efficient Sampling for Matroids (

)
(
Talebi and Proutiere, 2016
)
.
All of these algorithms rely on a greedy algorithm (see Algorithm
1
) to determine the action to be pulled. The greedy algorithm takes time at least
\Omega(K)
and at most
\mathcal{O}(K(\log K+\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{member}}))
, where
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{member}}
is the time taken to determine whether
\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{k}\in\mathcal{I}
for some
(\boldsymbol{x},k)\in\mathcal{I}\times[K]
, and
\boldsymbol{e}_{k}
is the
k

vector.
However, when the number
K
of arms is large, performing the greedy algorithm at each round can become expensive. There is a need to develop a matroid semi-bandit algorithm with per-round time complexity sublinear in
K
.

In this work, we present
FasterCUCB
(Algorithm
5
), the first sublinear-time algorithm for matroid semi-bandit.
The design of
FasterCUCB
is based on
CUCB
, but with a much faster sampling rule which takes time sublinear in
K
for many classes of matroids.
For uniform matroids, partition matroids, and graphical matroids, it has per-round time complexity of
\mathcal{O}(D\,\text{polylog}\left({K}\right)\text{polylog}\left({T}\right))
, which is optimal up to a polylogarithmic factor as compared to the trivial lower bound of
\Omega(D)
.
For transversal matroids, the per-round time complexity is
\mathcal{O}(D\sqrt{K}\,\text{polylog}\left({T}\right))
, which is still sublinear in
K
when
D=\mathcal{O}(K^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon})
for any
\epsilon>0
.

FasterCUCB
trades the accuracy for computational efficiency. In other words, the action computed by the sampling rule of
FasterCUCB
is an
approximation
to the optimal solution computed by the sampling rule of
CUCB
. This introduces difficulty in the regret analysis because prior analysis of
CUCB

(
Kveton et al., 2014a
)
requires the exact solution.
What is interesting is that we can still guarantee the same regret upper bound asymptotically as prior analysis of
CUCB
.

To develop a sublinear-time sampling rule,
we present a dynamic algorithm for maintaining maximum-weight base
over inner product weights
(

Our insight for addressing this issue is that

Matroid.

A matroid is described by
\mathcal{M}\triangleq([K],\mathcal{I})
, where
[K]
is called the
ground set
and
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{K}
is the set of
independent sets
satisfying (i)
hereditary property

\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{y}\right)\subset\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)
and
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{I}
, then
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{I}
; and (ii)
augmentation property
, i.e., if
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{I}
and
\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{y}\right)\subset\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)
, then there exists
j\in\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\backslash\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{y}\right)
such that
\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\in\mathcal{I}
.
We said
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{I}
is a basis if
\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)
is
maximal
, i.e., there does not exist
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{I}
such that
\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\subset\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{y}\right)
.
All bases have the same cardinality, which is called the
rank
of the matroid. For
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}
, a maximum-weight basis
\boldsymbol{i}^{\star}\!\left(\boldsymbol{v}\right)\in\argmax_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}v_{k}x_{k}
can be found by a greedy algorithm (Algorithm
1
) in
\mathcal{O}(K(\log K+\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{member}}))
time, where
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{member}}
is the time taken for the membership oracle to determine whether
\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{k}\in\mathcal{I}
for some
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{I}
and some
k\in[K]\backslash\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)
.

Input:

\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
and the bases
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{K}
.

\boldsymbol{v}
in non-increasing order:
v_{\gamma(1)}\geq\cdots\geq v_{\gamma(K)}
;

;
i=1
;

while

\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\|_{0}<D

if

\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{\gamma(i)}\in\mathcal{I}

then

\boldsymbol{x}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{\gamma(i)}

;

Matroid semi-bandits.

An instance of matroid semi-bandit is described by
([K],\mathcal{X},\boldsymbol{\mu})
, where
[K]
is the ground set,
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{K}
is the set of bases of the given matroid
\mathcal{M}\triangleq([K],\mathcal{I})
of rank
D
,
and
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\{0,1\}^{K}
is the set of independent sets.
Each
k\in[K]
is associated with a distribution
\nu_{k}
with mean
\mu_{k}
.
The learner knows the matroid
\mathcal{M}
, and aims to learn the best action
\boldsymbol{i}^{\star}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}\right)\in\argmax_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\langle{\boldsymbol{\mu}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\right\rangle
by playing a game with the environment: At each round
t\in\mathbb{N}
, the learner plays an action
\boldsymbol{x}(t)
, and the environment draws a noisy reward
y_{k}(t)\sim\nu_{k}
for each arm
k\in[K]
and reveals
y_{k}(t)
to the learner iff
k\in\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{x}(t)\right)
.
We assume arms’ rewards are bounded:

Assumption 2.1
.

Assume the support of each arm
\nu_{k}
is a subset of
[a,b]
and
0<a<b<\infty
.

The performance is measured by expected regret:

R(T)\triangleq T\left\langle{\boldsymbol{\mu}},{\boldsymbol{i}^{\star}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}\right)}\right\rangle-\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left\langle{\boldsymbol{\mu}},{\boldsymbol{x}(t)}\right\rangle\right],

which is the difference between the expected cumulative reward of the learner and that of an algorithm who knows
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and always selects the best action
\boldsymbol{i}^{\star}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}\right)
.

Common classes of matroids.

Refer to Chapter 39
(
Schrijver, 2003
)
or Chapter 1
(
Oxley, 2011
)
for more details.

•

A
uniform matroid

([K],\mathcal{I})
of rank
D
has independent sets
\mathcal{I}=\{S\subseteq[K]:\lvert{S}\rvert\leq D\}
and the bases
\mathcal{X}
consist of subsets whose cardinalities are exactly
D
.

•

A
partition matroid

([K],\mathcal{I})
of rank
D
is given a partition
S_{1},\cdots,S_{D}
of the ground set
[K]
, the independent sets
\mathcal{I}=\left\{S:\lvert{S\cap S_{i}}\rvert\leq 1,\forall i\in[D]\right\}
, and the bases
\mathcal{X}
are subsets that choose exactly one element from each of the
D

•

A
graphical matroid
is given a graph
G=(V,E)
with
K

\mathcal{X}
consist of all spanning forests in
G
, and the rank
D
is
\lvert{V}\rvert
minus the number of connected components in
G
.

•

A
transversal matroid
is given a bipartite graph
G=([K]\cup V,E)
with a bipartition
([K],V)
,
\lvert{V}\rvert\leq K
, the independent sets
\mathcal{I}
consist of
S\subseteq[K]
such that there is a matching of
S
to
\lvert{S}\rvert
vertices in
V
, and
\mathcal{X}
is the set of endpoints in
[K]
of all maximum matchings in
G
.

We discuss the query time
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{member}}
of membership oracle in Appendix
C
.
For more examples on semi-bandits under different matroid constraints, we refer the readers to
(
Kveton et al., 2014a
)
for linear matroids, and
(
Kveton et al., 2014b
)

CUCB.

The action selected by
CUCB

(
Gai et al., 2012
;
Chen et al., 2013
;
Kveton et al., 2014b
)
at round
t\in\mathbb{N}
is:

\boldsymbol{x}(t)\in\argmax_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\hat{\mu}_{k}(t-1)+\frac{\lambda_{t}}{\sqrt{N_{k}(t-1)}}\right)x_{k},

(1)

where
\hat{\mu}_{k}(t)\triangleq\frac{1}{N_{k}(t)}\sum_{s=1}^{t}y_{k}(s)\mathbbm{1}\!\left\{{x_{k}(s)=1}\right\}
is the empirical reward estimate,
N_{k}(t)\triangleq\sum_{s=1}^{t}\mathbbm{1}\!\left\{{x_{k}(s)=1}\right\}
is the number of pulls of arm
k
, and
\lambda_{t}>0
controls the confidence width.
The value
\hat{\mu}_{k}(t-1)+\frac{\lambda_{t}}{\sqrt{N_{k}(t-1)}}
is called the
UCB index
of arm
k
.
In
(
Kveton et al., 2014a
)
, Eq. (
1
) is solved by a
\mathcal{O}(K(\log K+\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{member}}))
-time greedy algorithm shown in Algorithm
1
.
In Section
4
, we will develop a faster algorithm for solving Eq. (
1
) with the following reformulation:

\boldsymbol{x}(t)\in\argmax_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle{\boldsymbol{f}_{k}},{\boldsymbol{q}}\right\rangle x_{k},

where
\boldsymbol{f}_{k}=(\hat{\mu}_{k}(t-1),\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}(t-1)}})
and
\boldsymbol{q}=(1,\lambda_{t})
.

Dynamic maintenance of maximum-weight base of a matroid

Here, we review existing dynamic algorithms for maintaining a maximum-weight base of a matroid.
In a standard (fully-)dynamic setting,
we are given a weighted matroid
\mathcal{M}=([K],\mathcal{I})
, where
each arm’s weight dynamically changes over time in an online manner.
The objective is to maintain any (exact or approximate) maximum-weight basis of
\mathcal{M}
over up-to-date arm weights as efficiently as possible.
We use
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{update}}(\mathcal{A})
to denote
the time complexity of a dynamic algorithm
\mathcal{A}

required for updating an (approximate) maximum-weight base according to
the change of a single arm weight.
The best-known bound of

\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{update}}(\mathcal{A})

for each matroid class is summarized as follows:
For graphic matroids,
a maximum-weight basis can be updated
in
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{K})
worst-case time
(
Frederickson, 1985
;
Eppstein et al., 1997
)
and
in
\mathcal{O}(\text{polylog}\left({K}\right))
amortized time
(
Holm et al., 2001
)
.
For laminar matroids
(which include uniform and partition matroids as special cases),
the worst-case time complexity for exact dynamic algorithms is bounded by

\mathcal{O}(\log K)

(
Henzinger et al., 2023
)
.
For transversal matroids,
a
\mathcal{O}(K^{1.407})
-time exact dynamic algorithm is known
(
van den Brand et al., 2019
)
, while
a
(1+\eta)
-approximation dynamic algorithm runs in
\mathcal{O}(\eta^{-2}\sqrt{K})
time
(
Gupta and Peng, 2013
)
.
We can safely assume that, after updating multiple arm weights,
\mathcal{A}
returns an (approximate) maximum-weight basis in
\mathcal{O}(D)
time, where
D
is the rank of a matroid.

4.2
Rounding Arm Features

Here, we apply a rounding technique to arm features
so as to reduce the number of distinct features to consider.
Hereafter, let
\eta\triangleq\frac{\epsilon}{3}
,

(1+\eta)^{2}\leq 1+\epsilon

\epsilon\in(0,1)

Define

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} W&\triangleq\max\left\{\left\lceil\log_{1+\eta}\left(\frac{\alpha_{\text{ub}}}{\alpha_{\text{lb}}}\right)\right\rceil,\left\lceil\log_{1+\eta}\left(\frac{\beta_{\text{ub}}}{\beta_{\text{lb}}}\right)\right\rceil\right\},\\
\mathbb{W}&\triangleq\{-\infty\}\cup[W]=\{-\infty,1,2,3,\ldots,W\}.\end{aligned}

(3)

Since any features are within

\displaystyle\mathrm{dom}_{q,r}\triangleq(\alpha_{\text{lb}}(1+\eta)^{q},\beta_{\text{lb}}(1+\eta)^{r}).

For any feature

Observe easily that for any feature

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\eta}\cdot\langle\mathrm{dom}(\boldsymbol{f}_{k}),\boldsymbol{q}\rangle<\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{k},\boldsymbol{q}\rangle\leq\langle\mathrm{dom}(\boldsymbol{f}_{k}),\boldsymbol{q}\rangle.

4.3
Handling Multiple Queries

From weighting to permutation.

Now we deal with multiple queries.
Our idea is that, if two queries
\boldsymbol{q}_{1},\boldsymbol{q}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}

are “very close” to each other, then
they should derive the common maximum-weight base
(provided that features are fixed).
This intuition can be justified with respect to
orderings
of arms.
For two total orders
\preceq
and
\preceq^{\circ}
over
[K]
,
we say that
\preceq^{\circ}
is
consistent with

\preceq
if

k\preceq^{\circ}k^{\prime}
implies
k\preceq k^{\prime}
for any
k\neq k^{\prime}
.
The following fact is easy to confirm:

Lemma 4.7

(
*
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{K})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}
be
K
arm weights and

\preceq
be a total order over
[K]
such that

k\succeq k^{\prime}
if and only if
w_{k}\geq w_{k^{\prime}}
. Let
\preceq^{\circ}
be a total order over
[K]
that is consistent with
\preceq
.
If
\boldsymbol{x}^{\circ}
is a base of matroid
\mathcal{M}
obtained by running the greedy algorithm
over any ordering of
[K]
consistent with
\preceq^{\circ}
,
it is a maximum-weight base of
\mathcal{M}
over arm
k
’s weight
w_{k}
; namely,
for any base
\boldsymbol{x}
of
\mathcal{M}
,

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{x}^{\circ},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle\geq\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{w}\rangle.

(10)

Lemma

4.7
implies that
any maximum-weight base can be obtained by running the greedy algorithm over some total order
\preceq^{\circ}
;
moreover, we can safely assume that
\preceq^{\circ}
is
strict

(i.e.,
k\prec^{\circ}k^{\prime}
or
k\succ^{\circ}k^{\prime}
for all
k\neq k^{\prime}
), or equivalently,
a
permutation
over
[K]
.
Our strategy for dealing with multiple queries is:
(1) we enumerate all possible permutations in advance, and
(2) we guess a permutation consistent with the arm weights determined based on a query.
To this end, the following question arises:

What kind of and how many permutations are representable given a fixed set of features?

Characterizing representable permutations.

To answer the above question, we characterize representable permutations.
Hereafter, let
\mathfrak{S}_{K}
denote the set of all permutations over
[K]
, and

\boldsymbol{f}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
be any fixed, distinct
K
features.
We say that
a query
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
over
\boldsymbol{f}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}_{K}

represents
a permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{K}
if

\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{\pi(i)},\boldsymbol{q}\rangle>\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{\pi(j)},\boldsymbol{q}\rangle
for all

1\leq i<j\leq K
,
1
1

1

This definition does not allow “ties”; i.e., no pair
k\neq k^{\prime}
satisfies

\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{k},\boldsymbol{q}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{k^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{q}\rangle
.

and that

\pi
is
representable
if such
\boldsymbol{q}
exists.

For a permutation
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{K}
to be representable,
we wish for some query
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
to ensure that
for any
i<j
,
arm
\pi(i)
’s weight is (strictly) higher than arm
\pi(j)
’s weight.
This requirement is equivalent to

\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{\pi(i)}-\boldsymbol{f}_{\pi(j)},\boldsymbol{q}\rangle>0
;
thus, if the following system of linear inequalities is feasible,
any of its solutions
\boldsymbol{q}
represents
\pi
:

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{\pi(i)}-\boldsymbol{f}_{\pi(j)},\boldsymbol{q}\rangle>0\text{\; for all \;}1\leq i<j\leq K.

(11)

Observe now that the above system is feasible
if and only if

the intersection of
\mathcal{P}_{i,j}
for all
i<j
is nonempty, where

\mathcal{P}_{i,j}
is an open half-plane defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{i,j}\triangleq\{\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{\pi(i)}-\boldsymbol{f}_{\pi(j)},\boldsymbol{q}\rangle>0\}.

(12)

Because each
\mathcal{P}_{i,j}
is obtained by dividing
\mathbb{R}^{2}
by
a unique line that
is orthogonal to line
\overleftrightarrow{\boldsymbol{f}_{\pi(i)}\boldsymbol{f}_{\pi(j)}}
and
intersects the origin
\boldsymbol{0}
,
the set of feasible solutions for Eq. (
11
)
is equal to
(the interior of) a
polyhedral cone
defined by the boundaries of a particular pair of
\mathcal{P}_{i,j}
’s.

Here, we characterize the representable permutations by the concept of arrangement of lines.
Let

\mathcal{L}\triangleq\{\boldsymbol{l}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{l}_{K\choose 2}\}
denote
{K\choose 2}
lines,
each of which is orthogonal to line
\overleftrightarrow{\boldsymbol{f}_{k}\boldsymbol{f}_{k^{\prime}}}

for some
k\neq k^{\prime}
and intersects
\boldsymbol{0}
.
Given such
\mathcal{L}
,
a
cell
\mathcal{C}
in arrangement of
\mathcal{L}
is
defined as a maximum connected region of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that does not intersect with
\mathcal{L}

(which is the interior of a polyhedral cone).
Then, for each cell
\mathcal{C}
,
every query
\boldsymbol{q}
in
\mathcal{C}
represents the same permutation
\pi_{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathfrak{S}_{K}

depending only on
\mathcal{C}
; namely,

there is a bijection between
the representable permutations and the cells in arrangement of
\mathcal{L}
.
See
Figure

2
in
Appendix

With this connection in mind,
we demonstrate that reserving a single vector for each cell suffices to cover all representable permutations.
A
minimum hitting set
of the cells in arrangement of
\mathcal{L}

is defined as
any minimum set
\mathcal{H}
of
vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

\mathcal{H}
and each cell have a non-empty intersection.

Lemma 4.8

(
*
)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}

be a minimum hitting set of the cells in arrangement of
\mathcal{L}
.
Then, for any query
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{k},\boldsymbol{h}\rangle>\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{k^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{h}\rangle\implies\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{k},\boldsymbol{q}\rangle\geq\langle\boldsymbol{f}_{k^{\prime}},\boldsymbol{q}\rangle.

(13)

Lemma

4.8
along with

ensure that
for any query
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

there is a vector
\boldsymbol{h}

Generating a minimum hitting set.

Subsequently, we generate a minimum hitting set.
One may think that it requires exponentially long time because the number of permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{K}
is

However,
it turns out that the number of cells in arrangement of

Lemma 4.9

(
*
)
.

The number of cells in arrangement of
\mathcal{L}

\mathcal{O}(K^{2})

Moreover, we can generate a minimum hitting set in
\text{ poly}\left({K}\right)

(by using

in
Appendix

D

By Theorem

5.2

Notation.

Fix

Theorem 5.1
.

Let

\displaystyle+\sum_{k\notin\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{i}^{\star}\right)}\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}}\triangle_{\overline{j},k}\left(\frac{1}{T}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\right)+DbT_{0}.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we have presented
FasterCUCB
, the first sublinear-time algorithm for matroid semi-bandits. Several possible future directions. First, one might extend our approach to speed up UCB-style algorithms for different problems such as combinatorial best-arm identification
(
Chen et al., 2014
;
Du et al., 2021
)
and nonstationary semi-bandits
(
Zhou et al., 2020
;
Chen et al., 2021
)
. Second, another direction is to study the possibility of speeding up other forms of weights, such as those derived from gradients
(
Tzeng et al., 2023
)
and those in the follow-the-perturbed-leader algorithm
(
Neu and Bartók, 2016
)
.

Impact Statement

This paper develops an algorithm for matroid semi-bandits.
The societal consequences of this work indirectly come from the applications of matroid semi-bandits, and none of which we think should be discussed here.

Further Related Works

In this section, we review relevant literatures on combinatorial semi-bandits and sublinear-time bandits. We focus on the stochastic setting.
For ease of comparison, we assume the best action
\boldsymbol{i}^{\star}\in\argmax_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\langle{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right\rangle
is unique, and define

\triangle_{\min}\triangleq\min_{j,k\in[K]:i^{\star}_{j}=1,i^{\star}_{k}=0,\mu_{j}-\mu_{k}>0}(\mu_{j}-\mu_{k})
, and
\triangle\triangleq\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\neq\boldsymbol{i}^{\star}:\left\langle{\boldsymbol{i}^{\star}-\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right\rangle>0}\left\langle{\boldsymbol{i}^{\star}-\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right\rangle
.

Matroid semi-bandits.

Kveton et al. [2014a]
showed an instance such that any uniformly good algorithm

They also showed that

Chen et al., 2013

have a regret bound scaling as
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{(K-D)\log T}{\triangle_{\min}}\right)

Talebi and Proutiere [2016]
showed an instance-specific lower bound

The per-round compleixty of

K

Both

and

rely on the greedy algorithm (Algorithm

[
Perrault et al., 2019

In contrast, our

Combinatorial semi-bandits.

Here, we review works that focus on the standard setting of stochastic combinatorial semi-bandits. These consider a linear reward function and any action sets

Appendix C
Membership Oracles for Different Matroids

In this section, we discuss
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{member}}
for the matroids shown in Section
2
.

•

For uniform matroid, the membership oracle is given
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{I}
and
k\in[K]\backslash\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)
, and has to check whether
\lvert{\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{k}\right)}\rvert\leq D
. Suppose the number
n=\lvert{\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)}\rvert
is maintained. Then, it takes
\mathcal{O}(1)
time to check whether
n+1\leq D
, and hence
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{member}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

•

For partition matroids, the membership oracle is given
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{I}
and
k\in[K]\backslash\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)
, and has to check whether
\lvert{\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{k}\right)\cap S_{i}}\rvert\leq 1
for all
i\in[D]
.
Suppose there is an integer array
A
of size
K
such that
j\in S_{A[j]}
for each
j\in[K]
, and suppose there is an integer array
B
of size
D
such that
B[i]=\sum_{j\in\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)}\mathbbm{1}\!\left\{{j\in S_{i}}\right\}
for each
i\in[D]
.
Then, to decide whether whether
\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{e}_{k}\in\mathcal{I}
, it only requires to check whether
B[A[k]]+1\leq 1
.
This can be implemented in
\mathcal{O}(1)
time, and thus
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{member}}=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

•

For graphical matroids, the membership oracle has to detect if there is a cycle. Using the union-find data structure, whether
\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\cup\{k\}
has a cycle can be detected in
\mathcal{O}(\log K)
time, so we have
\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{member}}=\mathcal{O}(\log K)
. Refer to Section 4.6 in
[
Kleinberg and Tardos, 2006
]
for more detailed explanation.

•

For transversal matroids, there is little discussion about its membership oracle.

and

Suppose a maximum matching

\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{x}\right)\cup V
is maintained.

\displaystyle\frac{\triangle_{\overline{1},k}}{\triangle_{\overline{1},k}(\epsilon)^{2}}+\sum_{j=2}^{d_{k}}\triangle_{\overline{j},k}\left(\frac{1}{\triangle_{\overline{j},k}(\epsilon)^{2}}-\frac{1}{\triangle_{\overline{j-1},k}(\epsilon)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}-1}\frac{\triangle_{\overline{j},k}(\epsilon)-\triangle_{\overline{j+1},k}(\epsilon)}{(\triangle_{\overline{j},k}(\epsilon))^{2}}+\frac{\triangle_{\overline{d_{k}},k}}{\triangle_{\overline{d_{k}},k}(\epsilon)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}-1}\frac{\triangle_{\overline{j},k}(\epsilon)-\triangle_{\overline{j+1},k}(\epsilon)}{\triangle_{\overline{j},k}(\epsilon)\triangle_{\overline{j+1},k}(\epsilon)}+\frac{\triangle_{\overline{d_{k}},k}}{\triangle_{\overline{d_{k}},k}(\epsilon)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}-1}\left(\frac{1}{\triangle_{\overline{j+1},k}(\epsilon)}-\frac{1}{\triangle_{\overline{j},k}(\epsilon)}\right)+\frac{\triangle_{\overline{d_{k}},k}}{\triangle_{\overline{d_{k}},k}(\epsilon)^{2}}\leq\frac{2\triangle_{\overline{d_{k}},k}}{\triangle_{\overline{d_{k}},k}(\epsilon)^{2}},

where the first inequality is due to
\triangle_{\overline{j},k}(\epsilon)\geq\triangle_{\overline{j+1},k}(\epsilon)
, and the second upperbounds the telescoping series:

\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}-1}\left(\frac{1}{\triangle_{\overline{j+1},k}(\epsilon)}-\frac{1}{\triangle_{\overline{j},k}(\epsilon)}\right)=\frac{1}{\triangle_{\overline{d_{k}},k}(\epsilon)}-\frac{1}{\triangle_{\overline{1},k}(\epsilon)}\leq\frac{1}{\triangle_{\overline{d_{k}},k}(\epsilon)}

Hence, we derive an upper bound for the first part relevant to
(I)_{\overline{j},k}
:

\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}}\triangle_{\overline{j},k}(I)_{\overline{j},k}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}}\triangle_{\overline{j},k}T_{0}+\frac{12(b-a)^{2}\triangle_{\overline{d_{k}},k}\log T}{\triangle_{\overline{d_{k}},k}(\epsilon)^{2}}.

\square

Lemma
5.5
.

Let
k\notin\text{ supp}\left(\boldsymbol{i}^{\star}\right)
and
j\in[d_{k}]
. For
T>T_{0}
,

(II)_{\overline{j},k}\leq\frac{1}{T}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}.

Proof of Lemma
5.5
:

Let
\epsilon=\frac{1}{\log^{m}T}
.
Recall

(II)_{\overline{j},k}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\mathbbm{1}\!\left\{{g_{t}(\overline{j})=k,N_{k}(t)>n_{\overline{j},k}}\right\}\right],

where
n_{j,k}=\max\left\{\frac{6(b-a)^{2}\log T}{(\frac{\mu_{j}}{1+\log^{-m}T}-\mu_{k})^{2}},T_{0}\right\}

First, we claim that

g_{t}(\overline{j})=k\implies u_{k}(N_{k}(t-1),T)\geq\frac{\min_{s<t}u_{\overline{j}}(s,t)}{1+\epsilon},

(
14
)

where
u_{k}(s,t)=\tilde{\mu}_{k}(s)+\sqrt{\frac{1.5(b-a)^{2}\log t}{s}}
and
\tilde{\mu}_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t}y_{k}(s)
.

Show Eq. (
14
)
:
Observe that
g_{t}(\overline{j})=k
implies

\left(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3}\right)\left\langle{\boldsymbol{f}_{k}},{\boldsymbol{q}}\right\rangle\geq\left\langle{\mathrm{dom}(\boldsymbol{f}_{k})},{\boldsymbol{q}}\right\rangle\geq\frac{\left\langle{\mathrm{dom}(\boldsymbol{f}_{\overline{j}})},{\boldsymbol{q}}\right\rangle}{1+\frac{\epsilon}{3}}\geq\frac{\left\langle{\boldsymbol{f}_{\overline{j}}},{\boldsymbol{q}}\right\rangle}{1+\frac{\epsilon}{3}},

where Eq. (
8
) is used in the first and the last inequality, and the second inequality is due to Lemma
5.2
and Corollary
4.10
.
By
(1+\frac{\epsilon}{3})^{2}\leq 1+\epsilon
and expanding
\boldsymbol{f}_{k}=(\hat{\mu}_{k}(t-1),\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{k}(t-1)}})
and
\boldsymbol{q}=(1,\sqrt{1.5(b-a)^{2}\log t})
, we have

u_{k}(N_{k}(t-1),t)\\
\geq\frac{u_{\overline{j}}(N_{\overline{j}}(t-1),t)}{1+\epsilon}.

As
\log T>\log t
and
N_{\overline{j}}(t-1)\in[t-1]
, we further derive

\displaystyle u_{k}(N_{k}(t-1),T)\geq\frac{u_{\overline{j}}(N_{\overline{j}}(t-1),t)}{1+\epsilon}\geq\frac{\min_{s<t}u_{\overline{j}}(s,t)}{1+\epsilon},

which shows Eq. (
14
).

Second, let
\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}=\{t\in\{n_{\overline{j},k}+1,\cdots,T\}:g_{t}(\overline{j})=k,N_{k}(t-1)>n_{\overline{j},k}\}
.
From Eq. (
14
), we derive

\displaystyle(II)_{\overline{j},k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=n_{\overline{j},k}+1}^{T}\mathbb{P}\!\left[g_{t}(\overline{j})=k,N_{k}(t-1)>n_{\overline{j},k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=n_{\overline{j},k}+1}\mathbb{P}\!\left[u_{k}(N_{k}(t-1),T)\geq\frac{\min_{s<t}u_{\overline{j}}(s,t)}{1+\epsilon}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=n_{\overline{j},k}+1}\sum_{s<t}\mathbb{P}\!\left[u_{k}(N_{k}(t-1),T)\geq\frac{u_{\overline{j}}(s,t)}{1+\epsilon}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\right],

(22)

where the last inequality uses union bound.

Third, we now upper bound each term
\mathbb{P}\!\left[u_{k}(N_{k}(t-1),T)\geq\frac{u_{\overline{j}}(s,t)}{1+\epsilon}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\right]
in Eq. (
22
).
Remind that

u_{k}(N_{k}(t-1),T)\geq\frac{u_{\overline{j}}(s,t)}{1+\epsilon}\Longleftrightarrow\underbrace{\tilde{\mu}_{k}(N_{k}(t-1))+\frac{\lambda_{T}}{\sqrt{N_{k}(t-1)}}}_{A_{t}}\geq\underbrace{\frac{\tilde{\mu}_{\overline{j}}(s)+\frac{\lambda_{t}}{\sqrt{s}}}{1+\epsilon}}_{B_{t,s}}.

Define the event
\mathcal{E}_{t,s}=\{A_{t}\geq B_{t,s}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\}
.
We will partition the event
\mathcal{E}_{t,s}
by comparing
A_{t}
to
A^{\prime}_{t}=\mu_{k}+\frac{2\lambda_{T}}{\sqrt{N_{k}(t-1)}}
and comparing
B_{t,s}
to
B^{\prime}=\frac{\mu_{\overline{j}}}{1+\epsilon}
as follows:

•

\mathcal{E}_{t,s}\cap\left\{A_{t}\geq A^{\prime}_{t}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\right\}\subseteq\left\{\tilde{\mu}_{k}(N_{k}(t-1))\geq\mu_{k}+\frac{\lambda_{T}}{\sqrt{N_{k}(t-1)}}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\right\}

•

\mathcal{E}_{t,s}\cap\{B_{t,s}\leq B^{\prime}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\}\subseteq\left\{\mu_{\overline{j}}\geq\tilde{\mu}_{\overline{j}}(s)+\frac{\lambda_{t}}{\sqrt{s}}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\right\}

•

\mathcal{E}_{t,s}\cap\{A_{t}<A_{t}^{\prime}\text{ and }B_{t,s}>B^{\prime}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\}\subseteq\left\{\mu_{k}+\frac{2\lambda_{T}}{\sqrt{N_{k}(t-1)}}>\frac{\mu_{\overline{j}}}{1+\epsilon}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\right\}
.
The inclusion is because under the event
\mathcal{E}_{t,s}\cap\{A_{t}<A_{t}^{\prime}\text{ and }B_{t,s}>B^{\prime}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\}
, we have

\mu_{k}+\frac{2\lambda_{T}}{\sqrt{N_{k}(t-1)}}=A_{t}^{\prime}>A_{t}\geq B_{t,s}>B^{\prime}=\frac{\mu_{\overline{j}}}{1+\epsilon},

where the first and last inequalities are due to the event
\{A_{t}<A_{t}^{\prime}\text{ and }B_{t,s}>B^{\prime}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\}
, and the second inequality is due to the event
\mathcal{E}_{t,s}=\{A_{t}\geq B_{t,s}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\}
.

Hence, we have the following inclusion:

\displaystyle\{A_{t}\geq A^{\prime}_{t}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\}\cup\{B_{t,s}\leq B^{\prime}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\}\cup\{A_{t}<A_{t}^{\prime}\text{ and }B_{t,s}>B^{\prime}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\}

\displaystyle=\{t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\}\supset\{A_{t}\geq B_{t,s}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\}=\mathcal{E}_{t,s}.

From union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\!\left[\mathcal{E}_{t,s}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left[\{A_{t}\geq A^{\prime}_{t}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\}\cap\mathcal{E}_{t,s}\right]+\mathbb{P}\!\left[\{B_{t,s}\leq B^{\prime}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\}\cap\mathcal{E}_{t,s}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}\!\left[\{A_{t}<A_{t}^{\prime}\text{ and }B_{t,s}>B^{\prime}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\}\cap\mathcal{E}_{t,s}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left[\mu_{k}+\frac{\lambda_{T}}{\sqrt{N_{k}(t-1)}}\leq\tilde{\mu}_{k}(N_{k}(t-1))\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\!\left[\mu_{\overline{j}}\geq\tilde{\mu}_{\overline{j}}(s)+\frac{\lambda_{t}}{\sqrt{s}}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\right]+\mathbb{P}\!\left[\mu_{k}+\frac{2\lambda_{T}}{\sqrt{N_{k}(t-1)}}>\frac{\mu_{\overline{j}}}{1+\epsilon}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\right].

(23)

In Eq. (
23
), recall
\lambda_{t}=\sqrt{1.5(b-a)^{2}\log t}
and observe that the last term

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\mu_{k}+2\sqrt{\frac{1.5(b-a)^{2}\log T}{N_{k}(t-1)}}\geq\frac{\mu_{\overline{j}}}{1+\epsilon}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\right]\leq\mathbb{P}\!\left[\mu_{k}+2\sqrt{\frac{1.5(b-a)^{2}\log T}{n_{\overline{j},k}+1}}\geq\frac{\mu_{\overline{j}}}{1+\epsilon}\right]=0,

where the inequality is because
t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}
implies
N_{k}(t-1)\geq n_{\overline{j},k}+1
, and the equality is because

n_{\overline{j},k}\geq\frac{6(b-a)^{2}\log T}{(\frac{\mu_{\overline{j}}}{1+\epsilon}-\mu_{k})^{2}}\implies\frac{6(b-a)^{2}\log T}{n_{\overline{j},k}+1}<\left(\frac{\mu_{\overline{j}}}{1+\epsilon}-\mu_{k}\right)^{2}

and also we have
\frac{\mu_{\overline{j}}}{1+\epsilon}-\mu_{k}>0
which is ensured by Lemma
5.3
as
T>T_{0}
.
Finally, from Eq. (
22
) and Eq. (
23
),

\displaystyle(II)_{\overline{j},k}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=n_{\overline{j},k}+1}^{T}\sum_{s<t}\mathbb{P}\!\left[\tilde{\mu}_{k}(N_{k}(t-1))\geq\mu_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{1.5(b-a)^{2}\log T}{N_{k}(t-1)}}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{t=n_{\overline{j},k}+1}^{T}\sum_{s<t}\mathbb{P}\!\left[\mu_{\overline{j}}\geq\tilde{\mu}_{\overline{j}}(s)+\sqrt{\frac{1.5(b-a)^{2}\log t}{s}}\text{ and }t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=n_{\overline{j},k}+1}^{T}\sum_{s<t}\left(\mathbb{P}\!\left[\tilde{\mu}_{k}(t-1)\geq\mu_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{1.5(b-a)^{2}\log T}{t-1}}\right]+\mathbb{P}\!\left[\mu_{\overline{j}}\geq\tilde{\mu}_{\overline{j}}(s)+\sqrt{\frac{1.5(b-a)^{2}\log t}{s}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=n_{\overline{j},k}+1}^{T}\sum_{s<t}\left(e^{-3\log T}+e^{-3\log t}\right),

where the second inequality is because
\{N_{k}(t-1)\}_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{\overline{j},k}}
is strictly increasing (as
N_{k}(t)=N_{k}(t-1)+1
when
g_{t}(\bar{j})=k
) and thus is a subsequence of
\{n_{\overline{j},k}+1,\cdots,T\}
, and the last inequality is due to an application of Hoeffding’s inequality (Lemma
E.2
) with
s=\sqrt{1.5(t-1)(b-a)^{2}\log T}
and
n=t-1
to bound the first term and with
s=\sqrt{1.5s(b-a)^{2}\log t}
and
n=s
to bound the second term.
The proof is completed by evaluating

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{s<t}e^{-3\log T}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{t}{T^{3}}\leq\frac{T(T+1)}{2T^{3}}\leq\frac{1}{T},

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{s<t}e^{-3\log t}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{t}{t^{3}}\leq\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{t^{2}}\leq\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}.

\square

Lemma E.2

(Hoeffding’s inequality)
.

Let
X_{1},\cdots,X_{n}
be independent random variables such that
X_{i}\in[a,b]
for all
i\in[n]
. Then, for all
s>0
,

\mathbb{P}\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}\!\left[X_{i}\right])\geq s\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{2s^{2}}{n(b-a)^{2}}\right).
