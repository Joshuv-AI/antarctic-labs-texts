Ghost distributions on supersymmetric spaces II: basic classical superalgebras
Alexander Sherman

Abstract
. We study ghost distributions on supersymmetric spaces for the case of basic classical Lie superalgebras. We introduce the notion of interlaced pairs, which are those for which both ( 𝔤 , 𝔨 ) (\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k}) and ( 𝔤 , 𝔨 ′ ) (\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}) admit Iwasawa decompositions. For such pairs we define a ghost algebra, generalizing the subalgebra of 𝒰 ​ 𝔤 \mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g} defined by Gorelik. We realize this algebra as an algebra of G G -equivariant operators on the supersymmetric space itself, and for certain pairs, the ‘special’ ones, we realize our operators as twisted-equivariant differential operators on G / K G/K . We additionally show that the Harish-Chandra morphism is injective, compute its image for all rank one pairs, and provide a conjecture for the image when ( 𝔤 , 𝔨 ) (\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k}) is interlaced.



1.
Introduction

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be one of the basic classical Lie superalgebras
\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n)
,
\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n)
,
\mathfrak{d}(1|2;\alpha)
,
\mathfrak{g}(1|2)
, or
\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}(1|3)
. Fix a nondegenerate invariant supersymmetric form
(-,-)
on
\mathfrak{g}
and let
\theta
be an involution of
\mathfrak{g}
which preserves
(-,-)
. Write
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}
for the
(\pm 1)
-eigenspace decomposition with respect to
\theta
, and set
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}\oplus\mathfrak{p}_{\overline{1}}
; in particular
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
is the fixed points of the involution
\delta\circ\theta
, where
\delta(v)=(-1)^{\overline{v}}v
is the canonical grading automorphism. Let
G
be an algebraic supergroup with
\operatorname{Lie}G=\mathfrak{g}
and
G_{0}
reductive, and suppose that
\theta
extends to an involution of
G
and that
K
is a subgroup of
G
with
(G^{\theta})^{\circ}\subseteq K\subseteq G^{\theta}
. Here
(G^{\theta})^{\circ}
denotes the identity component of
G^{\theta}
. Finally we set
K^{\prime}
to be the subgroup of
G
with
K^{\prime}_{0}:=K_{0}
and
\operatorname{Lie}K^{\prime}:=\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
.

In
[
She21a
]
it was shown that the action of
K^{\prime}
on
G/K
enjoys many nice properties, with the foremost being that
\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)=\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{k}
is isomorphic, as a
K^{\prime}
-module, to
\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}}^{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}\operatorname{Dist}(G_{0}/K_{0},eK_{0})
, where algebraically
\operatorname{Dist}(G_{0}/K_{0},eK_{0})=\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}/\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}
. We set
\mathcal{A}_{G/K}
to be the
K^{\prime}
-invariant distributions on
G/K
supported at
eK
, which we call the space of ghost distributions of
G/K
. Then there is an explicit isomorphism of vector spaces
\operatorname{Dist}(G_{0}/K_{0},eK_{0})^{K_{0}}\to\mathcal{A}_{G/K}
.

Write
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}_{\overline{0}}
for a Cartan subspace, and let us suppose that we have an Iwasawa decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}
; then we may define the Harish-Chandra morphism

HC:\mathcal{A}_{G/K}\to S(\mathfrak{a})
. The collection of polynomials
HC(\mathcal{A}_{G/K})\subseteq S(\mathfrak{a})
form a module over
HC(\mathcal{Z}_{G/K})
, and carry representation-theoretic information about branching from
G
to
K^{\prime}
; in particular they detect when an injective indecomposable on the trivial module of
K^{\prime}
appears in highest weight
G
-submodules of
G/K
(see
Lemma

2.9
). The first important result we have is:

Theorem 1.1
.

The map
HC:\mathcal{A}_{G/K}\to S(\mathfrak{a})
is injective.

We can also compute the degree and highest order term of
HC(\gamma)
for
\gamma\in\mathcal{A}_{G/K}
. For representation-theoretic consequences of this result, see
3.6
. Note that
1.1
relies on the existence of a form which
\theta
preserves, and that without this assumption the statement is not true.

1.1.
Interlaced pairs

To further our study of
HC(\mathcal{A}_{G/K})
, we show that certain pairs
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
have a very close relationship to
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k}^{\prime})
. Namely, we say that
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
is interlaced if there exists
t\in A
(where
A=\exp(\mathfrak{a})\subseteq GL(\mathfrak{g})
) such that if we set
\phi=\operatorname{Ad}(t)
then
\phi\theta=\delta\theta\phi
, and further
\phi^{2}=\delta
(
\delta
is the grading operator). This in particular implies that
\phi(\mathfrak{k})=\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
and
\phi(\mathfrak{k}^{\prime})=\mathfrak{k}
. The following remarkable result is crucial for many ideas in our work.

Theorem 1.2
.

A supersymmetric pair
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
is interlaced if and only if both
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
and
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k}^{\prime})
admit Iwasawa decompositions.

Note that the forward direction is clear; for the backward direction we use the classification of supersymmetric pairs of the type we consider along with properties of their reduced root systems.

1.2.
Ghost centre

The property of a pair being interlaced gives it structure that is similar to the diagonal pair
(\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})
. Write
\mathcal{Z}_{G/K}
for the
K
-invariant distributions on
\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)
. Then the space
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}_{G/K}}:=\mathcal{Z}_{G/K}+\mathcal{A}_{G/K}
may be endowed with the natural structure of an algebra, which we call the ghost algebra of
G/K
, and it is such that the map
HC:\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}_{G/K}}\to S(\mathfrak{a})
is an algebra homomorphism. In particular, we obtain in this case that

HC(\mathcal{A}_{G/K})HC(\mathcal{A}_{G/K})\subseteq HC(\mathcal{Z}_{G/K}).

Note that in
[
She21a
]
it was shown that
HC(\mathcal{A}_{G/K})
is always a module over
HC(\mathcal{Z}_{G/K})
, even when
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
is not interlaced.

Remarkably, we may lift
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}_{G/K}}
to an algebra of
G
-equivariant operators on
G/K
:

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose that
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
is interlaced with interlacing automorphism
\phi=\operatorname{Ad}(t)
, and assume that
t^{2}\in K(\Bbbk)
. Then we have an injective morphism of algebras

\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}_{G/K}}\to\operatorname{End}_{G}(\Bbbk[G]),\ \ \ \ u\mapsto\tilde{u}.

See
Section

6.1
for the definition of
\tilde{u}
when
u\in\mathcal{A}_{G/K}
. Note that
\tilde{u}
will
not
be a differential operator when
u\notin\mathcal{Z}_{G/K}
. However for certain interlaced pairs this issue can be remedied, namely when
t
can be chosen to be central in
G_{0}
, as we see next.

1.3.
Special pairs

We call a supersymmetric pair
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
‘special’ if it is interlaced, and the interlacing element
t
can taken to be central in
G_{0}
. The special pairs are exactly
(\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n))
and
(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2r)\times\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2n-2r))
. In this case, for
u\in\mathcal{A}_{G/K}
, we may set

D_{u}:=L_{t^{-1}}^{*}\circ R_{t}^{*}\circ(1\otimes u)\circ a^{*},

where
R_{t},L_{t^{-1}}
are respectively left and right translation by
t
on
G
. Then in Section 7 it is shown that
D_{u}
is an
\operatorname{Ad}(t)
-twisted equivariant differential operator; in particular it is
G_{0}
-equivariant. In fact, there we obtain a subalgebra
\mathcal{D}^{G,\cdot}(G/K)\subseteq\mathcal{D}(G/K)
consisting of differential operators which are
\operatorname{Ad}(z)
twisted equivariant for some
z
in the center of
G_{0}
. Thus we obtain an analogue of the full ghost centre as introduced in
[
She21a
]
.

The action of this algebra on
\mathbb{C}[G/K]
, and in particular its eigenvalues on highest weight functions, would be very interesting to understand. Because they are differential operators, there is some hope that the methods of
[
All12
]
may apply, for instance, to help prove Weyl group invariance.

1.4.
HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
for rank one pairs

In the last section we compute
HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
for all rank one pairs. Here
\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}=(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{k})^{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}
. Here is a list of our results; we use standard notation for root systems, and in particular follow the conventions from
[
She20a
]
.

(i)

(\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n),\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m-1|n)\times\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1))
: let
t=\frac{1}{2}h_{\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{m}}
; then

HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})=\Bbbk[t(t-n+m-1)]\langle t(t-1)\cdots(t-(n-1))\rangle.

(ii)

(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2n))
: let
t=h_{\epsilon_{1}}
, where
\epsilon_{1}(h_{\epsilon_{1}})=1
; then

HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})=\{p\in\Bbbk[t]:p(n+r)=(-1)^{r}p(n-r):1\leq r\leq n\},

or more explicitly:

\Bbbk[t(t-2n)]\langle(t-1)(t-3)\cdots(t-(2n-1)),t(t-2)\cdots(t-2n)\rangle,

(iii)

(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m-1|2n))
,
m\geq 3
: let
t=h_{\epsilon_{1}}
; then

HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})=\Bbbk[t(t-2n+m-2)]\langle(t-1)(t-3)\cdots(t-(2n-1))\rangle.

(iv)

(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n-2)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2))
,
n\geq 2
: let
t\in\mathfrak{a}
be such that
(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})(t)=1
; then

HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})=\Bbbk[t(t+2n-m-1)]\langle(t+1)t(t-1)\cdots(t-(m-2))\rangle

We observe that if
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
is not interlaced, then we do not have any kind of Weyl group (anti-)invariance. The conditions determining
HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
in this general case remain mysterious. However for interlaced pairs we have a coherent conjecture, as is explained in what follows.

1.5.
Conjecture for interlaced pairs

Let
\overline{\Delta}
denote the reduced root system of
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
with respect to a chosen Cartan subspace
\mathfrak{a}
, and set
\overline{\rho}
to be the restricted Weyl vector. Let
\overline{\Delta}_{ev}=\overline{\Delta_{\overline{0}}}\setminus\{0\}
, that is the non-zero projections of even roots to
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
. Set
\overline{\Delta}_{odd}:=\overline{\Delta}\setminus\overline{\Delta}_{ev}
. For
\alpha\in\overline{\Delta}_{ev}
, write
r_{\alpha}
for the reflection on
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
determined by
\alpha
. Let
W
denote the subgroup of
GL(\mathfrak{a})
generated by
r_{\alpha}
, for all
\alpha\in\overline{\Delta}_{ev}
. For
p\in S(\mathfrak{a})
and
w\in W
, we write
(w.p)(\lambda)=p(w(\lambda+\overline{\rho})-\overline{\rho})
.

Conjecture 1.4
.

Assume that
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
is interlaced. Then
HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
is given by the set of
p\in S(\mathfrak{a})
satisfying:

(i)

for
\alpha\in\overline{\Delta}_{ev}
with
n_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\overline{1}}
, we have

(r_{\alpha}.p)=(-1)^{n_{\alpha}}p;

(ii)

for
\alpha\in\overline{\Delta}_{odd}
, and
(\lambda+\overline{\rho},\alpha)=0
, we have

p(\lambda+r\alpha)=(-1)^{r}p(\lambda-r\alpha)

for
1\leq r\leq\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\overline{1}}
.

Remark 1.5
.

We first observe that the above conjecture is valid for all group-like cases
(\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})
, as shown in
[
Gor00
]
. All interlaced cases that we have computed in this paper also satisfy the above conjecture. Further, observe that if
\alpha\in\overline{\Delta}_{odd}
and
(\alpha,\alpha)=0
, then in fact the second condition implies that

(h_{\alpha}+(\overline{\rho},\alpha))|HC(D)

for all
D\in\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
. (See
8.3
for the case when
\alpha
is simple)

Remark 1.6
.

From
[
All12
]
, one may deduce the following description of
HC(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
: it is given by the set of
p\in S(\mathfrak{a})
such that

(i)

w.p=p
for all
w\in W
;

(ii)

for
\alpha\in\overline{\Delta}_{\overline{1}}
, and
(\lambda+\overline{\rho},\alpha)=0
, we have

p(\lambda+r\alpha)=p(\lambda-r\alpha)

for
1\leq r\leq\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha})_{\overline{1}}
.

Thus our conditions are a ‘square-root’ of the conditions on
HC(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
, which is a necessity for interlaced pairs since
HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})^{2}\subseteq HC(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
as explained in
Section

1.2
.

1.6.
Future directions

In future work, we look to prove
1.4
, and understand what occurs in the cases of non-interlaced pairs. Further, for the special pairs we want to compute the structure of the algebra of twisted-equivariant differential operators. In general, the structure of the ghost centre
\widetilde{\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}}
for interlaced pairs is not understood, and should be determined.

1.7.
Summary of sections

In section 2 we recall the setup and results of
[
She21a
]
. Section 3 is devoted to the proof of the injectivity of the Harish-Chandra morphism and its consequences. Section 4 defines the notion of interlaced pairs, and proves
1.2
. Section 5 constructs the ghost algebra of
G/K
for interlaced pairs, and Section 6 explains how to lift to equivariant operators in this case. Section 7 looks at special supersymmetric pairs, showing we may construct a ‘full’ ghost algebra, and explains how to lift these invariant distributions to twisted-equivariant differential operators, giving an algebra of differential operators on
G/K
. Section 8 discusses two tools which may be used to help compute
HC(\mathcal{A}_{G/K})
. Finally Section 9 computes
HC(\mathcal{A}_{G/K})
in every rank one.

2.
Recollections

In what follows,
\Bbbk
denotes an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero.

We work with the same notation and setup as in
[
She21a
]
except that we restrict to certain Lie supergroups and Lie superalgebras. In particular, unless stated otherwise,
\mathfrak{g}
will always denote one of the Lie superalgebras
\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n)
,
\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n)
,
\mathfrak{d}(1,2;a)
,
\mathfrak{g}(1,2)
, or
\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}(1,3)
. Thus
\mathfrak{g}
is quasireductive and admits a nondegenerate, invariant supersymmetric bilinear form which we denote by
(-,-)
. For more on the basic properties of these Lie superalgebras we refer to
[
Mus12
]
. Further,
G
will denote a quasireductive Lie supergroup with
\operatorname{Lie}G=\mathfrak{g}
. For more on quasireductive Lie supergroups we refer to
[
Ser11
]
.

We are again interested in supersymmetric pairs. Throughout,
\theta
will denote an involution of
\mathfrak{g}
which preserves the form
(-,-)
, with fixed point subalgebra
\mathfrak{k}
and
(-1)
-eigenspace
\mathfrak{p}
. In particular
\mathfrak{k}
will be quasireductive and itself admits a nondegenerate, invariant supersymmetric form. We have an explicit classification of the pairs
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
that we consider, given in Section 4. We will assume that
G
is such that
\theta
lifts to an involution of
G
, and by abuse of notation we also denote this involution by
\theta
. Then we let
K
be any quasireductive subgroup of
G
which satisfies
(G^{\theta})^{\circ}\subseteq K\subseteq G^{\theta}
, where
(-)^{\circ}
denotes the connected component of the identity.

We recall that for a given choice of
G,\theta
, and
K
, we set
K^{\prime}
to be the quasireductive subgroup of
G
with
K^{\prime}_{0}=K_{0}
and
\operatorname{Lie}K^{\prime}=\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}:=\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}\oplus\mathfrak{p}_{\overline{1}}
. Such a subgroup exists and is unique by the theory of super Harish-Chandra pairs (see
[
MZ22
]
).

With the above setup, we will consider the homogeneous supervarieties
G/K
and
G/K^{\prime}
, which will be smooth, affine supervarieties (see, for instance,
[
MT18
]
).

2.1.
Convention on actions

Since
G
has a left action on
G/K
as a space,
\Bbbk[G/K]
naturally carries a right action as a
G
-module. The action of
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}
on
\Bbbk[G/K]
is given by

u\mapsto(u\otimes 1)\circ a^{*},

where
a:G\times G/K\to G/K
is the action map. This defines an algebra homomorphism
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\to\mathcal{D}(G/K)^{op}
, where
\mathcal{D}(G/K)
is the algebra of differential operators on
G/K
.

Remark 2.1
.

We work with this right action so that the action on distributions will be a left action. However, note that if
V=L(\lambda)
is a left-module of highest weight
\lambda
for
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}
, then as a right module (obtained via precomposition with the antipode), we have
V=L(-\lambda)
, i.e.
V
becomes a module of highest weight
-\lambda
.

2.2.
Ghost distributions

For an affine supervariety
X
and point
x\in X(\Bbbk)
, we set

\operatorname{Dist}(X,x):=\{\psi:\Bbbk[X]\to\Bbbk:\psi(\mathfrak{m}_{x}^{n})=0\text{ for }n\gg 0\}.

By our conventions above,
\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)
has the natural structure of a left
\mathfrak{g}
-module under precomposition by vector fields. We recall that its structure is given by

\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{k}\cong\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)

from the map

u\mapsto\operatorname{res}_{eK}\circ\left(u\otimes 1\right)\circ a^{*},

where
a:G\times G/K\to G/K
is the action morphism. Since
K_{0}
stabilizes
eK
, the action of
\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}
integrates to an action of
K_{0}
on
\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)
; thus we obtain an action of
K^{\prime}
on
\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)
. The central observation of
[
She21a
]
is the existence of a natural isomorphism of
K^{\prime}
-modules

\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)\cong\operatorname{Ind}_{\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}}^{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}\operatorname{Dist}(G_{0}/K_{0},eK_{0}).

This isomorphism is induced by the natural inclusion
\operatorname{Dist}(G_{0}/K_{0},eK_{0})\subseteq\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)
.

For the pairs we consider, we have that
\Lambda^{\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{p}_{\overline{1}}}\mathfrak{p}_{\overline{1}}
is a trivial
\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}
-module, and thus we have an isomorphism of even vector spaces

\operatorname{Dist}(G_{0}/K_{0},eK_{0})^{K_{0}}\to\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)^{K^{\prime}}

given explicitly by

z\mapsto v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}\cdot z=zv_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}.

Here
v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}
is a nonzero element of
(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}/\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}})^{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}
, which is a one-dimensional vector space (see
[
She21a
]
).

Definition 2.2
.

We define
\mathcal{A}_{G/K}:=\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)^{K^{\prime}}
to be the ghost distributions on
G/K
.

2.3.
The algebraic approach

Notice that one can work purely algebraically and consider
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{k}
as a
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
-module; in this case we set

\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}:=(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{k})^{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}};

if
K
is connected then this agrees with
\mathcal{A}_{G/K}
. In general
\mathcal{A}_{G/K}
is a subspace of
\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
given by the invariants under
K_{0}/K_{0}^{\circ}
. Here, our above isomorphism of
\operatorname{Dist}(G_{0}/K_{0},eK_{0})^{K_{0}}
with
\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)^{K^{\prime}}
becomes an isomorphism

\left(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}/\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}\right)^{\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}}\xrightarrow{\sim}\left(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{k}\right)^{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}},

given by

u\mapsto v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}u.

Remark 2.3
.

For the pairs we consider,
\mathcal{A}_{G/K}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{G/K}
are always purely even vector spaces because
\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{p}_{\overline{1}}
is even (since it admits a nondegenerate symplectic form).

2.4.
Cartan subspaces and the Iwasawa decomposition

We let
\mathfrak{a}\subseteq\mathfrak{p}_{\overline{0}}
denote a Cartan subspace, that is a maximal subspace of
\mathfrak{p}_{\overline{0}}
consisting only of semisimple elements. We may extend
\mathfrak{a}
to a
\theta
-stable Cartan subalgebra of
\mathfrak{g}
, which we call
\mathfrak{h}
, and then we have
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{t}\oplus\mathfrak{a}
according to the
(\pm 1)
-eigenspaces of
\theta
on
\mathfrak{h}
. Notice that
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{h}_{\overline{0}}
because of the choice of Lie superalgebras that we work with.

Write
\Delta\subseteq\mathfrak{h}^{*}
for the roots of
\mathfrak{g}
with respect to
\mathfrak{h}
, and let
\overline{\Delta}\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{*}\setminus\{0\}
denote the collection of nonzero restrictions of roots to
\mathfrak{a}
. Choose a decomposition
\overline{\Delta}=\overline{\Delta}^{+}\sqcup\overline{\Delta}^{-}
into positive and negative roots, and set

\mathfrak{n}=\bigoplus\limits_{\alpha\in\overline{\Delta}^{+}}\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha}.

We say that
\mathfrak{g}
admits an Iwasawa decomposition if for some choice of positive roots in
\overline{\Delta}
as above, we have
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}
. In this case, we choose a decomposition
\Delta=\Delta^{+}\sqcup\Delta^{-}
of positive and negative roots in such a way that the restriction map from
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
to
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
sends
\Delta^{+}
to
\overline{\Delta}^{+}\sqcup\{0\}
. If
\mathfrak{b}
denotes the corresponding Borel subalgebra, then we say that
\mathfrak{b}
is an Iwasawa Borel subalgebra; observe that
\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}\subseteq\mathfrak{b}
and thus
\mathfrak{b}+\mathfrak{k}=\mathfrak{g}
.

By
[
She20a
]
, at least one of
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
or
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k}^{\prime})
admits an Iwasawa decomposition.

2.5.
Highest weight functions

We now assume that
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
admits an Iwasawa decomposition with Iwasawa Borel subalgebra
\mathfrak{b}
. Let
\Lambda\subseteq\mathfrak{a}^{*}
denote the set of weights of rational
\mathfrak{b}
-eigenfunctions on
G/K

•

if we write

Highest weight submodules

We let
\Lambda^{+}\subseteq\Lambda
denote the set of

is Zariski dense in

Lemma 2.5
.

For
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
, set

. Then we have

Proof.

The first equality follows from the Iwasawa decomposition. For the second, we note that we have
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{n}+\mathfrak{c}(\mathfrak{a})+\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
; thus it suffices to show that

is a
\mathfrak{c}(\mathfrak{a})
-eigenvector. By

]

is generated by simple roots of

which are fixed by

, and thus it suffices to show that
\alpha
is a negative simple root of
\mathfrak{b}
with

, then
e_{\alpha}f_{\lambda}=0
, where

.

If
\alpha
is even or odd nonisotropic, then this follows because

and the representation theory of

and
\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2)

\mathfrak{b}

]

2.7.
Harish-Chandra morphism

Under our assumption of an Iwasawa decomposition, we obtain a decomposition of

as
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}=S(\mathfrak{a})\oplus(\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}+\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{k})
. Thus we have

\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)\cong S(\mathfrak{a})\oplus(\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}+\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{k})/\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{k}.

We define the Harish-Chandra morphism to be the projection of
\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)
onto
S(\mathfrak{a})
along
(\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}+\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{k})/\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{k}
, postcomposed with the antipode
\sigma_{\mathfrak{a}}
of
\mathfrak{a}
. The purpose of this postcomposition with the antipode is to make our formulas look familiar to those who work with left
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}
modules as opposed to right
\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}
-modules (see
Remark

2.1
). The following lemma is straightforward.

Lemma 2.6
.

For
\psi\in\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)
we have

\psi(f_{\lambda})=HC(\psi)(-\lambda).

Corollary 2.7
.

Suppose that
\mu,\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
and
f_{\mu}\in V(\lambda)
. Then there exists
c\in\Bbbk
such that
HC(\gamma)(-\mu)=cHC(\gamma)(-\lambda)
for all
\gamma\in\mathcal{A}_{G/K}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.5
, we may write
f_{\mu}=uf_{\lambda}
for some
u\in\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
. Thus for
\gamma\in\mathcal{A}_{G/K}
we have

HC(\gamma)(-\mu)=\gamma(f_{\mu})=\gamma(uf_{\lambda})=\varepsilon(u)\gamma(f_{\lambda})=\varepsilon(u)HC(\gamma)(-\lambda).

Here
\varepsilon:\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}\to\Bbbk
is the counit.
∎

3.
Injectivity of the Harish-Chandra Homomorphism

This section is devoted to the proof and consequences of the following result:

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
admits an Iwasawa decomposition. Then the map

HC:\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}\to S(\mathfrak{a})

is injective.

Proof.

We consider the filtration on
\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)
defined in Sec. 8.6 of
[
She21a
]
, except that we ‘halve’ the indexing; i.e. our filtration on
\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)
will be indexed by half-integers, and if an element previously lied in the
r
th part of the filtration, then it now lies in the
r/2
part of the filtration by definition. Then Lem. 8.18 of
[
She21a
]
tells us that if
f\in\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)
lies in the
r
th part of the filtration, we must have
\deg HC(f)\leq r
.

Now we may represent any element of
\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})}
as
v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}z\in\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}/\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{k}
, where

z\in(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}/\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}})^{\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}}
. Let
\Delta_{\overline{1}}^{+}
denote the collection of odd positive roots, and let
S\subseteq\Delta_{\overline{1}}^{+}
denote the subset of those roots
\alpha
for which
\theta\alpha\neq\alpha
. Then
-\theta
is an involution on
S
, and by Section 6 of
[
She20a
]
, it admits no fixed points. Thus let
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}\in S
be distinct representatives of the orbits of
-\theta
on
S
; then
\mathfrak{p}_{\overline{1}}
admits a basis given by

x_{i}=e_{\alpha_{i}}-\theta e_{\alpha_{i}},\ \ \ \ y_{i}=e_{-\alpha_{i}}-\theta e_{-\alpha_{i}}.

Therefore we may represent
v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}
in the following way

v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}=x_{k}y_{k}\cdots x_{1}y_{1}+l.o.t.

where
l.o.t.
are lower order terms in our filtration, given by monomials in our basis above. Thus we may write

v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}z=x_{k}y_{k}\cdots x_{1}y_{1}z+l.o.t.

Suppose that
z
is of degree
r
; then it suffices to show that

\deg HC(x_{k}y_{k}\cdots x_{1}y_{1}z)=k+r.

In other words we can work up to terms of degree lower than
k+r
. Begin by writing
x_{k}=(e_{\alpha_{k}}+\theta e_{\alpha_{k}})-2e_{\alpha_{k}}
; then since
e_{\alpha_{k}}\in\mathfrak{n}
, we obtain

HC(x_{k}y_{k}\cdots x_{1}y_{1}z)=HC((e_{\alpha_{k}}+\theta e_{\alpha_{k}})y_{k}\cdots x_{1}y_{1}z).

Set
h_{\overline{\alpha_{i}}}=h_{\alpha_{i}}-\theta h_{\alpha_{i}}
; then we have

[e_{\alpha_{k}}+\theta e_{\alpha_{k}},y_{k}]=h_{\overline{\alpha_{k}}}+(1-\theta)[\theta e_{\alpha},e_{-\alpha}].

Thus we have

\displaystyle HC(x_{k}y_{k}\cdots x_{1}y_{1}z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle HC(h_{\overline{\alpha_{k}}}x_{k-1}y_{k-1}\cdots x_{1}y_{1}z)+HC((1-\theta)[\theta e_{\alpha},e_{-\alpha}]x_{k-1}y_{k-1}\cdots x_{1}y_{1}z)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum(\pm)HC(y_{k}\cdots[e_{\alpha_{k}}+\theta e_{\alpha_{k}},w_{i}]y_{i}\cdots z)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle HC(y_{k}\cdots x_{1}y_{1}[e_{\alpha_{k}}+\theta e_{\alpha_{k}},z]).

In the above sum
w_{i}
is either
x_{i}
or
y_{i}
. We will show that all but the first term will have degree strictly less than
r+k
. Starting with the second term, we may write
(1-\theta)[\theta e_{\alpha},e_{-\alpha}]=n+k
, where
n\in\mathfrak{n}
and
k\in\mathfrak{k}
. Then we have

\displaystyle HC((1-\theta)[\theta e_{\alpha},e_{-\alpha}]x_{k-1}y_{k-1}\cdots x_{1}y_{1}z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle HC(x_{k-1}y_{k-1}\cdots x_{1}y_{1}[k,z])

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum HC(x_{k-1}\cdots[k,w_{i}]\cdots y_{1}z).

Since
k
is even,
[k,w_{i}]
will continue to be odd, and thus
HC(x_{k-1}\cdots[k,w_{i}]\cdots y_{1}z)
will be of degree at most
r+k-1
. Further,
[k,z]
will be of filtered degree
r-1
, and thus
HC(x_{k-1}y_{k-1}\cdots x_{1}y_{1}[k,z])
will be of degree at most
r+k-1
as well. This deals with the second term in our large sum.

For the third term, we observe that if
\alpha_{i}\neq\alpha_{k}
, then

[e_{\alpha_{k}}+\theta e_{\alpha_{k}},w_{i}]=(1-\theta)f_{\beta}+(1-\theta)f_{\gamma},

where either
\beta=\alpha_{k}\pm\alpha_{i},\gamma=\alpha_{k}\pm\theta\alpha_{i}
are roots and
f_{\beta},f_{\gamma}
are root vectors, or
\beta,\gamma
are not roots and
f_{\beta},f_{\gamma}
are zero. In any case
\beta,\gamma\neq 0
, and
f_{\beta},f_{\gamma}
will be even root vectors. Thus we may write
[e_{\alpha_{k}}+\theta e_{\alpha_{k}},w_{i}]=n+k
where
n\in\mathfrak{n}_{\overline{0}}
and
k\in\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}
. Now we move
n
all the way to the left and
k
all the way to the right, and because they are even the terms we obtain will all have filtered degree at most
r+k-1
, as desired.

Finally, we observe that
[e_{\alpha_{k}}+\theta e_{\alpha_{k}},z]
is odd and will live in the
r-\frac{1}{2}
part of our filtration. Thus the third and last term in our sum will have degree at most
r+k-1
once again; thus we have shown that

HC(x_{k}y_{k}\cdots x_{1}y_{1}z)=HC(h_{\overline{\alpha_{k}}}x_{k-1}y_{k-1}\cdots x_{1}y_{1}z)+l.o.t.

Because
\mathfrak{h}
normalizes
\mathfrak{n}
, we may continue inductively to obtain that

HC(x_{k}y_{k}\cdots x_{1}y_{1}z)=HC(h_{\overline{\alpha_{k}}}\cdots h_{\overline{\alpha_{1}}}z)+l.o.t.

To finish, we write
z=n+HC(z)+k
, where
n\in\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}
and
k\in\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{k}
, and since
\mathfrak{h}
normalizes
\mathfrak{n}
we find that

HC(x_{k}y_{k}\cdots x_{1}y_{1}z)=(-1)^{k}h_{\overline{\alpha_{k}}}\cdots h_{\overline{\alpha_{1}}}HC(z)+l.o.t.

as desired.
∎

4.2.
Interlacing automorphisms
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})\cong(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k}^{\prime})

In the case that we have an isomorphism of supersymmetric pairs
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})\cong(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k}^{\prime})
, meaning that there exists an automorphism of
\mathfrak{g}
taking
\mathfrak{k}
to
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
, we will show we can take this isomorphism to be of a special form. For a Cartan subspace
\mathfrak{a}
of
\mathfrak{g}
we write
A
for the connected torus in
\operatorname{Inn}(\mathfrak{g})
that it corresponds to, where
\operatorname{Inn}(\mathfrak{g})
denotes the inner automorphisms of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Definition 4.1
.

We say that an automorphism
\phi
of
\mathfrak{g}
interlaces
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
and
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k}^{\prime})
with respect to a Cartan subspace
\mathfrak{a}
if:

(i)

\phi\theta=\delta\theta\phi
;

(ii)

\phi=\operatorname{Ad}(t)
is inner with
t\in A
, we have
\operatorname{Ad}(t^{2})=\delta
,
t^{4}=1
. In particular,
\theta t^{2}=t^{2}
.

In this case we call
\phi
an interlacing automorphism, and say that
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
is interlaced.

In particular an interlacing automorphism satisfies
\phi(\mathfrak{k})=\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
and
\phi(\mathfrak{k}^{\prime})=\mathfrak{k}
. It is easy to check that the inverse of an interlacing automorphism is again an interlacing automorphism. Further, because an interlacing automorphism
\phi
fixes
\mathfrak{a}
pointwise we have
\phi(\mathfrak{n})=\mathfrak{n}
, and thus
\phi
takes the Iwasawa decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}
to another Iwasawa decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}
.

We have the following satisfying theorem.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
be a supersymmetric pair from our above list; then the following are equivalent:

(i)

(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
admits an interlacing automorphism;

(ii)

(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
is conjugate to
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k}^{\prime})
;

(iii)

both
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
and
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k}^{\prime})
admit Iwasawa decompositions.

The proofs of (i)
\implies
(ii) and (ii)
\implies
(iii) are clear. The proof of (iii)
\implies
(1) follows from studying the possible reduced generalized root systems that are obtained in the cases we need to consider. We begin by recalling that the pair
(\Delta,\mathfrak{h}^{*})
may be viewed as an irreducible generalized root system (GRS) in the sense of
[
Ser96
]
. We may write
\Delta_{re}\subseteq\Delta
for the roots that are nonisotropic, so that
\Delta_{re}\subseteq\mathfrak{h}^{*}
defines a root system in the classical sense. In particular it decomposes into irreducible components
\Delta=\Delta_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup\Delta_{k}
, and correspondingly
\mathfrak{h}^{*}=V_{0}\oplus V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{k}
, where
\Delta_{i}\subseteq V_{i}
is an irreducible root system, and
V_{0}=(V_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus V_{k})^{\perp}
.

Now the involution
\theta
induces an automorphism on
\mathfrak{h}^{*}
; if we have
\theta(V_{i})=V_{i}
then we obtain a projection map
p_{i}:\mathfrak{a}^{*}\to\mathfrak{a}^{*}\cap V_{i}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

We have
0\notin\Delta_{\overline{1}}|_{\mathfrak{a}}
if and only if for some
i
we have
\theta V_{i}=V_{i}
and
0\notin p_{i}(\Delta_{\overline{1}})
.

Proof.

The backwards direction is clear; for the forward direction we work case by case. Note that by the proof of Prop. 3.1 of
[
She20a
]
, all pairs have
\theta V_{i}\subseteq V_{i}
for all
i
except possibly when
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(4|2n)
.
∎

Proposition 4.4
.

If
0\notin\Delta_{\overline{1}}|_{\mathfrak{a}^{*}}
, then there exists an
i
such that the following hold:

(i)

\theta V_{i}=V_{i}
;

(ii)

if
A_{i}\subseteq A
denotes the subgroup determined by
V_{i}\cap\mathfrak{a}^{*}
, then there exists
t\in A_{i}
such that
\phi=\operatorname{Ad}(t)
is an interlacing automorphism.

Proof.

We will show that in each case we can find
t\in A
such that
\operatorname{Ad}(t)
acts by
\pm i
on the root spaces of
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
, and by
\pm 1
on root spaces of
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
. In particular we will have
\operatorname{Ad}(t^{-1})=\delta\circ\operatorname{Ad}(t)
. Since
\theta\circ\operatorname{Ad}(t)\circ\theta=\operatorname{Ad}(t^{-1})
, this will give
\theta\operatorname{Ad}(t)=\delta\operatorname{Ad}(t)\theta
, and further it is obvious that
\operatorname{Ad}(t)
fixes
\mathfrak{a}
. Finally,
\operatorname{Ad}(t^{2})
will act by the identity on
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
and have eigenvalue
(-1)
on
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
, implying that it is
\delta
.

The proof is done in cases. First of all we always have
\theta V_{0}=V_{0}
when
V_{0}\neq 0
, and if
0\notin p_{0}(\Delta_{\overline{1}})
then as is shown in
[
Ser96
]
,
A_{0}
is a one-dimensional torus and
\Delta_{\overline{1}}=\Delta_{\overline{1}}^{\prime}\sqcup\Delta_{\overline{1}}^{\prime\prime}
consists of two components on which
A_{0}
acts faithfully by dual characters. Thus we may let
t\in A_{0}
be an order 4 element which acts by
i
on
\Delta_{\overline{1}}^{\prime}
and
(-i)
on
\Delta_{\overline{1}}^{\prime\prime}
. These cases are given by
(\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n))
and
(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2s)\times\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2n-2s))
. (Such pairs are called
special
; they will be studied further in Section 7.)

For the remaining cases we need to take an
i>0
. Write
\overline{\Delta_{j}^{i}}
for the image of
\Delta_{j}
in
\mathfrak{a}^{*}\cap V_{i}
. We will see that there always exists an
i>0
such that the following happens:

•

\theta V_{i}=V_{i}
;

•

the image of
\overline{\Delta_{\overline{1}}^{i}}
is a single Weyl group orbit up to sign; and

•

the lattice generated by
\overline{\Delta_{\overline{0}}^{i}}
is of index two inside of the lattice generated by
\overline{\Delta_{\overline{1}}^{i}}
.

Given the above properties, we may choose
t\in A_{i}
which acts by
\pm i
on
\overline{\Delta_{\overline{1}}^{i}}
and by
\pm 1
on
\overline{\Delta_{\overline{0}}^{i}}
.

Now we go through all the remaining supersymmetric pairs satisfying
0\notin\Delta_{\overline{1}}|_{\mathfrak{a}}

\displaystyle\tilde{u}\tilde{z}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{t}^{*}\circ(1\otimes u)\circ a^{*}\circ(1\otimes z)\circ a^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{t}^{*}\circ(1\otimes(u\circ((1\otimes z)\circ a^{*})))\circ a^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{t}^{*}\circ(1\otimes uz)\circ a^{*}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{uz};

\displaystyle=

Definition 7.1
.

A supersymmetric pair

is called special if our interlacing automorphism
\phi=\operatorname{Ad}(t)
may be taken to act by the identity on

The special supersymmetric pairs are those with properties that are close to the pair
(\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})

(\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n))

Set

to be those automorphisms of
\mathfrak{g}
which fix
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
pointwise. For the cases

The operator

Let

The twisted equivariance is a straightforward check. It remains to check that it defines a differential operator, and for this we use

. First of all notice that since

is

-equivariant, it suffices to prove that there exists

such that for

.

For this we begin by noticing that
\operatorname{res}_{eK}D_{u}=u
; thus if we let

be the degree of

, we have

for all

we have

Theorem 7.11
.

The map

\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)^{G_{z}}\to\mathcal{D}^{G,z^{-1}}(G/K),\ \ \ u\mapsto D_{u}

defines an isomorphism of vector spaces.

Proof.

We already saw that
\operatorname{res}_{eK}D_{u}=u
, so it remains to show that
\operatorname{res}_{eK}:\mathcal{D}^{G,z}(G/K)\to\operatorname{Dist}(G/K,eK)^{G_{z}}
is injective. The proof is almost identical to Prop. 3.4 of
[
She21a
]
, but we give it once again.

Let
D\in\mathcal{D}^{G_{z}}(G/K)
; then we have that

a^{*}\circ D=\operatorname{Ad}(z^{-1})\otimes D\circ a^{*}.

If
\operatorname{res}_{eK}(D)=0
, then
D(f)(eK)=0
for all
f\in\Bbbk[G/K]
, or equivalently
a_{eK}^{*}D(f)(eK)=0
, where
a_{eK}:G\to G/K
is the orbit map at

a_{eK}=a\circ(\operatorname{id}_{G}\times i_{eK})
, so this says that

\displaystyle(\operatorname{id}_{G}\otimes i_{eK})\circ a^{*}(D(f))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\operatorname{Ad}(z^{-1})^{*}\otimes\operatorname{res}_{eK}(D))(a^{*}(f))=0.

Thus

Note that we do not obtain an algebra map

8.1.
Reduction of pair

Let
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
be a supersymmetric pair with an Iwasawa decomposition
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{a}\oplus\mathfrak{n}

\mathfrak{g}(\theta,\mathfrak{a})

\mathfrak{a}
and

\theta

\theta

\mathfrak{g}(\theta,\mathfrak{a})

\mathfrak{k}(\theta,\mathfrak{a})

(-1)

\mathfrak{p}(\theta,\mathfrak{a})

\mathfrak{p}(\theta,\mathfrak{a})_{\overline{1}}=\mathfrak{p}_{\overline{1}}

Clearly

(\mathfrak{n}(\theta,\mathfrak{a}))_{\overline{1}}=\mathfrak{n}_{\overline{1}}

We may consider

Proposition 8.3
.

Let
\alpha
be a simple isotropic root such that
\theta\alpha\neq\alpha
and
\theta\alpha+\alpha\notin\Delta

h_{\alpha}|HC(D)
for all

Proof.

Suppose that
\lambda\in\Lambda^{+}
and that
(\lambda,\alpha)=0
and
V(\lambda)
contains a copy of
I_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}(\Bbbk)
. Then we may write
V(\lambda)=I_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}(\Bbbk)\oplus M
for some
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
-module
M
, and accordingly
f_{\lambda}=g+h
.

Define
e_{-\overline{\alpha}}:=e_{-\alpha}-\theta e_{-\alpha}
. Then
e_{-\alpha}f_{\lambda}=0
by sphericity, so that
e_{-\overline{\alpha}}f_{\lambda}=0
; in particular
e_{-\overline{\alpha}}g=0
. Under our conditions,
[e_{-\overline{\alpha}},e_{-\overline{\alpha}}]=0
, and so by projectivity we must have
g=e_{-\overline{\alpha}}g^{\prime}
for some

. However since
g
generates
I_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}(\Bbbk)
this is impossible, so we obtain a contradiction.

Remark 8.4
.

An interesting (albeit unfortunate) caveat to

is that the hypothesis almost never holds(!). The only supersymmetric pairs for which it does hold are
(\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n),\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m-r|n-s)\times\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(r|s))

This is in great contrast to the classical, even setting where it is always true that

The maps
p
and
q
are projection maps onto subspaces; all horizontal arrows are pullbacks under translation by the appropriate vector, and are obviously isomoprhisms. Finally,
W^{lit}
is the little Weyl group of the supersymmetric space. By Helgason’s theorem (
[
Hel92
]
), the composite map
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}^{i}}\to S(\mathfrak{h}_{i})^{W^{i}_{\rho_{i}}}\to S(\mathfrak{a})^{W^{lit}_{q(\rho^{i})}}
is a surjective map for these pairs. It follows that if the map
p:\mathcal{Z}_{\mathfrak{g}}\to S(\mathfrak{h}_{i})^{W^{i}_{p(\rho)}}
is surjective, then so is the map
qp:\mathcal{Z}_{\mathfrak{g}}\to S(\mathfrak{a})^{W^{lit}_{qp(\rho)}}
, and in particular we would have that the natural map

\mathcal{Z}_{\mathfrak{g}}\to\mathcal{Z}_{G/K}

is surjective in these cases. The following proposition explains when this occurs.

Proposition 8.5
.

The map
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathfrak{g}}\to S(\mathfrak{h}_{i})^{W^{i}_{p(\rho)}}
is surjective if and only if we are in one of the following cases:

•

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2m+1|2n),\partial(1|2;\alpha)
,
\mathfrak{g}(1|2)
;

•

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2m|2n)
with
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}^{i}=\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2n)
;

•

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}(1|3)
with
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}^{i}=\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(2)
.

Proof.

The proof is done case by case; clearly our question is equivalent to when the map
p:S(\mathfrak{h})^{\mathcal{W}}\to S(\mathfrak{h}_{1})^{W^{1}}
is surjective. In
[
Ser99
]
, generators for
S(\mathfrak{h})^{\mathcal{W}}
were described for each of the Lie superalgebras we consider.

(i)

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n)
: generators for
S(\mathfrak{h})^{\mathcal{W}}

\sum\limits_{i}\epsilon_{i}^{k}-\sum\limits_{j}\delta_{j}^{k},\ \ \ k\in\mathbb{N}.

Generators for
\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m)
:

\sum\limits_{i}\epsilon_{i}^{k},\ \ \ k\in\mathbb{N}.

(ii)

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2m+1|2n)
: generators for
S(\mathfrak{h})^{\mathcal{W}}
:

\sum\limits_{i}\epsilon_{i}^{2k}-\sum\limits_{j}\delta_{j}^{2k}.

Generators for
\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{o}(2m+1)
:

\sum\limits_{i}\epsilon_{i}^{2k}.

Generators for
\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2n)
:

\sum\limits_{i}\delta_{i}^{2k}.

(iii)

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2m|2n)
: every element of
S(\mathfrak{h})^{\mathcal{W}}
is of the form:

f_{o}+f_{1}\epsilon_{1}\cdots\epsilon_{m}\prod\limits_{i,j}(\epsilon_{i}^{2}-\delta_{j}^{2}),

where
f_{1}\in S(\mathfrak{h})^{W}
and
f_{0}
lies in the subalgebra generated by the polynomials

\sum\limits_{i}\epsilon_{i}^{2k}-\sum\limits_{j}\delta_{j}^{2k}.

Thus we obtain surjectivity on the component with
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}^{i}=\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2n)
, but the component with
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}^{i}=\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{o}(2m)
has one generator given by
\epsilon_{1}\cdots\epsilon_{m}
, and this will not be in the image.

(iv)

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{d}(1|2;\alpha)
: let
\lambda_{1}=-(1+\alpha)
,
\lambda_{2}=1
, and
\lambda_{3}=\alpha
. Then
S(\mathfrak{h})^{\mathcal{W}}
contains the polynomial

\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}}\epsilon_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}}\epsilon_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{3}}\epsilon_{3}^{2},

so its restriction to any component
\mathfrak{h}_{i}
will generate
S(\mathfrak{h}_{i})^{W^{i}}
.

(v)

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}(1|2)
: every element of
S(\mathfrak{h})^{\mathcal{W}}
is of the form

f_{0}+\prod\limits_{1\leq i\leq 3}(\delta_{i}^{2}-\epsilon_{i}^{2})f_{1},

where
f_{0}\in\Bbbk[3\delta^{2}-2(\epsilon_{1}^{2}+\epsilon_{2}^{2}+\epsilon_{3}^{2})]
, and
f_{1}\in S(\mathfrak{h})^{W}
. If
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}^{i}=\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(2)
then we clearly obtain a surjective map, and if
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}^{i}=\mathfrak{g}(2)
, then generators for
S(\mathfrak{h}^{i})^{W^{i}}
are given by

\epsilon_{1}^{2}+\epsilon_{2}^{2}+\epsilon_{3}^{2},\ \ \ \epsilon_{1}^{2}\epsilon^{2}\epsilon^{2}.

We see these are in the image of the restriction, so we again get surjectivity.

(vi)

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}(1|3)
: every element of
S(\mathfrak{h})^{\mathcal{W}}
is of the form

f_{0}+f_{1}\prod(\delta\pm\epsilon_{1}\pm\epsilon_{2}\pm\epsilon_{3})

where
f_{1}\in S(\mathfrak{h})^{W}
and
f_{0}\in\mathbb{C}[L_{2},L_{6}]
where
L_{2}=\delta^{2}-2(\epsilon_{1}^{2}+\epsilon^{2}+\epsilon_{3}^{2})
, and
L_{6}
is a homogeneous degree 6 polynomial. It follows that we get surjectivity for the component with
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}^{i}=\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(2)
, while for the component with
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}^{i}=\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{o}(7)
we do not get the degree 4 generator
\epsilon_{1}^{4}+\epsilon_{2}^{4}+\epsilon_{3}^{4}
, and thus we do not get surjectivity onto this component.

∎

We obtain the following application of
8.5
.

Theorem 8.6
.

Suppose that
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
is one of the following pairs:

(\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n),\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m-r|n)\times\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(r)),\ m\geq 2r,\ \ \ (\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2m+1|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2m+1-r|2n)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{o}(r)),\ 2m+1\geq 2r,

(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n-2s)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2s)),\ 2n\geq 4s,\ \ \ (\mathfrak{g}(1|2),\mathfrak{d}(1|2,3)).

Then
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathfrak{g}}\to\mathcal{Z}_{G/K}
is surjective, and

HC(\mathcal{A}_{G/K})=S(\mathfrak{a})^{W^{lit}_{\overline{\rho}}}\cdot HC(v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}),

where
S(\mathfrak{a})^{W^{lit}_{\overline{\rho}}}
is the invariance of
S(\mathfrak{a})
under the
\overline{\rho}
-shifted action of the little Weyl group
W^{lit}
, and
\overline{\rho}
is the restriction of
\rho
to
\mathfrak{a}
.

Proof.

The statement that
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathfrak{g}}\to\mathcal{Z}_{G/K}
is surjective is a direct consequence of
8.5
and the diagram above it. For the second statement, we have maps:

\textstyle{HC(\mathcal{Z}_{\mathfrak{g}})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{m}
\textstyle{\mathcal{Z}_{G_{0}/K_{0}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\omega}
\textstyle{HC(\mathcal{A}_{G/K})}

where
m(z)=HC(z)HC(v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}})
, and
\omega(z)=HC(v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}z)
. Of course
\omega
is an isomorphism by construction; on the other hand, by
3.3
,
\deg HC(v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}z)=\deg HC(z)\cdot\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{p}_{\overline{1}}/2
. Of course
m
is also injective, and we have
\deg m(z)=\deg HC(v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}})\deg HC(z)
.

Now by
8.5
and the diagram above it, the subspaces
\{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{G_{0}/K_{0}}:\deg HC(z)\leq r\}
and
\{z\in\mathcal{Z}_{G/K}:\deg HC(z)\leq r\}
have the same dimension. Therefore the image of
m
must be equal to the image of
\omega
, implying that
m
is also surjective.

By the diagram above
8.5
we have that
HC(\mathcal{Z}_{G/K})=S(\mathfrak{a})^{W^{lit}_{\overline{\rho}}}
, finishing the proof.
∎

9.
Rank 1 Computations

In this section we compute
HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
for all supersymmetric pairs that we consider of rank 1, i.e. in which
\operatorname{dim}\mathfrak{a}=1
. This includes the following pairs:

(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2)\times\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2)),\ \ \ (\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|1),\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)),

(\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n),\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m-1|n)\times\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1)),\ m\geq 2,\ \ \ (\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m-1|2n)),\ m\geq 2,

(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n-2)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2)),\ n\geq 2.

We include the first two pairs because they are also of rank one. It is possible that a general formula for
HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
could be proven by reduction to the rank one case, in which case the rank one pairs will be especially important, and these diagonal ones may appear in the process. Notice that
(\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|2),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2))\cong(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2))
, so it is also included.

In the following, for the diagonal pairs
(\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g})
we present the answer in
S(\mathfrak{h})
for a Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{h}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
, since this is equivalent.

Theorem 9.1
.

We have the following computation of
HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
:

(i)

(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2)\times\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2))
: let
\delta(h_{\delta})=1
; then

HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})=(h_{\delta}+\frac{1}{2})\Bbbk[\mathfrak{h}]^{W_{\rho}}.

(ii)

(\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|1),\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|1))
: let
h_{\alpha}
be a coroot of the odd isotropic root; then

HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})=h_{\alpha}\Bbbk[\mathfrak{h}].

(iii)

(\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n),\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m-1|n)\times\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1))
: let
t=\frac{1}{2}h_{\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{m}}
; then

HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})=\Bbbk[t(t-n+m-1)]\langle t(t-1)\cdots(t-(n-1))\rangle.

(iv)

(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2n))
: let
t=h_{\epsilon_{1}}
, where
\epsilon_{1}(h_{\epsilon_{1}})=1
; then

HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})=\{p\in\Bbbk[t]:p(n+r)=(-1)^{r}p(n-r):1\leq r\leq n\},

or more explicitly:

\Bbbk[t(t-2n)]\langle(t-1)(t-3)\cdots(t-(2n-1)),t(t-2)\cdots(t-2n)\rangle.

(v)

(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m-1|2n))
,
m\geq 3
: let
t=h_{\epsilon_{1}}
; then

HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})=\Bbbk[t(t-2n+m-2)]\langle(t-1)(t-3)\cdots(t-(2n-1))\rangle.

(vi)

(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n-2)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2))
,
n\geq 2
: let
t\in\mathfrak{a}
be such that
(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})(t)=1
; then

HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})=\Bbbk[t(t+2n-m-1)]\langle(t+1)t(t-1)\cdots(t-(m-2))\rangle.

The proofs of (i) and (ii) follow from
[
Gor00
]
. The proofs for the rest of the pairs will occupy the rest of the section; we will go through each case individually, using different techniques.
In addition, we have the following computation which we prove and subsumes part (iii):

Theorem 9.2

For the pair
(\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n),\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m-r|n)\times\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(r))
, set

9.1.
(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2n))

We present
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2n)
as

\begin{bmatrix}t&0&\alpha^{t}&-\beta^{t}\\
0&-t&\sigma^{t}&-\tau^{t}\\
\tau&\beta&m&p\\
\sigma&\alpha&q&-m^{t}\end{bmatrix}.

The involution realizing this pair can be given by conjugation by

\begin{bmatrix}0&1&\\
1&0&\\
&&\operatorname{id}\end{bmatrix}.

The Cartan subspace
\mathfrak{a}
is given by the span of the matrix
t
above, and thus
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
is spanned by a single weight
\epsilon
where
\epsilon(t)=1
. We thus identify
S(\mathfrak{a})=\Bbbk[t]
and
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
with
\Bbbk
via
a\epsilon\leftrightarrow a
. We make the following choices:

\mathfrak{n}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&u^{t}&-r^{t}\\
0&0&0&0\\
0&r&0&o\\
0&u&0&0\end{bmatrix}

and

\mathfrak{n}^{-}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&0\\
0&0&v^{t}&-s^{t}\\
s&0&0&0\\
v&0&0&0\end{bmatrix}.

Let
\omega=\sum\limits_{i}s_{i}v_{i}\in\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{n}^{-}
. One can show that for
1\leq r\leq n
we have that
\omega^{r}f_{n+r}
is non-zero of highest weight
(n-r)\epsilon
. Now suppose that we write
\omega^{r}=k+p+m
where
m\in\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}
,
p\in S(\mathfrak{a})
, and
k\in S(\mathfrak{a})(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{k})^{+}
. Then clearly we have

(\omega^{r}f_{n+r})(eK)=p(n+r).

Thus

f_{n-r}=\frac{1}{p(n+r)}\omega^{r}f_{n+r}.

On the other hand, let
\phi\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}})
denote the interlacing automorphism of this pair given by
(-i)\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}_{-1}}\oplus\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}_{0}}\oplus i\operatorname{id}_{\mathfrak{g}_{1}}
. Then as discussed previously,
\phi
fixes
\mathfrak{a}
pointwise, preserves
\mathfrak{n}
, and sends
\mathfrak{k}
to
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
. Thus
\phi(\omega^{r})=k^{\prime}+p+m^{\prime}
where
m^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{n}\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{g}
and
k^{\prime}\in S(\mathfrak{a})(\mathcal{U}\mathfrak{k}^{\prime})^{+}
. On the other hand
\omega\in S^{2}\mathfrak{n}^{-}
, thus
\phi(\omega^{r})=(-1)^{r}\omega^{r}
. Therefore

f_{n-r}=\frac{(-1)^{r}}{p(n+r)}\phi(\omega^{r})f_{n+r}=k^{\prime}f_{n+r}+(-1)^{r}f_{n+r}.

It follows that
HC(D)(n+r)=(-1)^{r}HC(D)(n-r)
for all
1\leq r\leq n
. Let
\mathcal{P}_{n}\subseteq\Bbbk[t]
denote the collection of polynomials given by

\mathcal{P}_{n}:=\{p\in\Bbbk[t]:p(n+r)=(-1)^{r}p(n-r):1\leq r\leq n\}.

Then we have shown that
HC(\mathcal{A})\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{n}
. One can check that
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is of codimension
n
in
\Bbbk[t]
. Here
\mathcal{Z}_{G_{0}/K_{0}}=S(\mathfrak{a})
and
\deg HC(v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}})=n
, so that
HC(\mathcal{A})
is also of codimension
n
in
\Bbbk[t]
, so we obtain that:

HC(\mathcal{A})=\mathcal{P}_{n}:=\{p\in\Bbbk[t]:p(n+r)=(-1)^{r}p(n-r):1\leq r\leq n\}.

Explicitly, we may write this as

\Bbbk[t(t-2n)]\langle(t-1)(t-3)\cdots(t-(2m-1)),t(t-2)\cdots(t-2n)\rangle,

i.e. the
\Bbbk[t(t-2n)]
-module generated by the two written polynomials. Here
t(t-2n)=HC(\Omega)
, where
\Omega
is the Casimir of this space.

(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m-1|2n))

9.3.
(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n-2)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2))

In this case the involution on the root system may be given by
\delta_{1}\leftrightarrow-\delta_{2}
, and thus
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
is spanned by
\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}
. With respect to an Iwaswa Borel subalgebra, the simple module
L(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})
of highest weight
\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}
is a quotient of

, where
\Bbbk^{m|2n}
is the standard module for
\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n)
. One can check directly that it admits a

-coinvariant, and thus appears in

; it follows that
\Lambda^{+}=\{n(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Now we use
Lemma

8.1
to reduce the computation of
HC(v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}})
to the case of
(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|4),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2))
. In this case,
c(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})
has the same central character as
d(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})
if and only if
d=c
or
d=m-3-c
; the map
c\mapsto m-3-c
is exactly the
\rho
-shifted action of the little Weyl group. By

HC(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
is equal to the invariants of the little Weyl group, or equivalently the polynomial algebra in the Casimir. By
5.2
,
HC(v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}})^{2}\in HC(\mathcal{Z}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})
; it follows that the zeros of
HC(v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}})
must be stable under the
\rho
-shifted action of the little Weyl group.

Now assume that
m\geq 2
; then by the above considerations the simple modules of highest weight
c(\delta_{1}+\delta_{2})
must appear for
0\leq c\leq m-2
. All these simple modules appear as subquotients of
(\Bbbk^{m|4})^{\otimes r}
for
r\leq 2(m-2)
; we claim that
I_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}(\Bbbk)
, the injective indecomposable on the trivial module for
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
, does not appear in any such power. Since
\Bbbk^{m|4}
restricts to
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
as
\Bbbk^{m|2}\oplus\Bbbk^{0|2}
, we in fact want to show that
I_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}(\Bbbk)
does not appear in
(\Bbbk^{m|2})^{\otimes r}
for
r\leq 2(m-2)
.

To prove this we use results on the structure of the tensor powers of
\Bbbk^{m|2n}
as a module over
OSp(m|2n)
. There are two injective indecomposable
OSp(m|2n)
-modules
I
whose restriction to
SOSp(m|2n)
is given by
I_{SOSp(m|2n)}(\mathbb{C})
, which are labeled in
[
ES
]
as
P(0,+)
and
P(0,-)
. Thus we want to determine the smallest
r
such that
P(0,\pm)
appear in
(\Bbbk^{m|2n})^{\otimes r}
. By Theorem 12.2 of
[
ES
]
,
P(0,-)
first appears for the first time in
(\Bbbk^{m|2n})^{\otimes m(2n+1)}
. When
n=1
we get
3m
which is indeed larger than
2m-4
.

It remains to deal with
P(0,+)
. By Theorem 7.3 of
[
CH17
]
, the indecomposable summands
R(\lambda)
of
(\Bbbk^{m|2n})^{\otimes r}
are parametrized by certain partitions
\lambda
of size at most
r
. Using Lemma 7.16
[
CH17
]
and the rules of Section 14
[
ES
]
we have that the projective cover of the trivial module is given by the indecomposable representation
R((m-1)^{2n})
. By Corollary 7.14 of
[
CH17
]
,
P(0,+)
occurs as a direct summand in
V^{\otimes 2(m-1)n}
and not in any smaller tensor power. If
n=1
, this means that it does not appear as a direct summand in
(\Bbbk^{m|2n})^{\otimes k}
for
k<2(m-1)
; since
2m-4<2m-2
, we have obtained a sufficient bound.

By
Lemma

2.9
it follows that
HC(v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}})
must vanish on all such
c
; and thus by the above-stated invariance property of its zeroes, we see that
HC(v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}})
vanishes at
c=-1,0,\dots,m-2
; by degree considerations we obtain that (up to a scalar)

HC(v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}})=(t+1)t(t-1)\cdots(t-(m-2)),

where
t=\frac{1}{2}h_{\delta_{1}+\delta_{2}}
.

Now if
m=1
, then we have the pair
(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|4),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2))
; in this case we have (up to a scalar)

HC(v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}})=t+1.

This can be checked directly, or one can use that it is a degree one polynomial whose square is invariant under the
\rho
-shifted action of
W
, which completely determines it up to scalar.

Now we return to the general case
(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n-2)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2))
; the Casimir
\Omega
has (up to scalar)
HC(\Omega)=t(t+2n-m-1)
. From this we obtain:

HC(\mathcal{A}_{(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})})=\Bbbk[t(t+2n-m-1)]\langle(t+1)t(t-1)\cdots(t-(m-2))\rangle.

9.4.
(\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|2n),\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m-r|n)\times\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(r))
,
m\geq 2r

Using
Lemma

8.1
and
8.6
, we reduce the question to the computation of
HC(v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}})
when the pair is
(\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(2r|n),\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(r|n)\times\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(r))
. We present this algebra as

\begin{bmatrix}a&b&\alpha\\
c&d&\beta\\
\phi&\psi&a\end{bmatrix}.

Then we have the involution
\theta
which is the permutation
(1,r+1)(2,r+2)\cdots(r,2r)
. The fixed subalgebra
\mathfrak{k}
is isomorphic to
\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(r|n)\times\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(r)
, explicitly presented as:

\mathfrak{k}=\begin{bmatrix}a&b&\gamma\\
b&a&\gamma\\
\sigma&\sigma&b\end{bmatrix}

where
\gamma
is of size
r\times n
and
\sigma
of size
n\times r
.

The
(-1)
-eigenspace
\mathfrak{p}
is given by

\mathfrak{p}=\begin{bmatrix}t&u&\tau\\
-u&-t&-\tau\\
\eta&-\eta&0\end{bmatrix}.

Thus a Cartan subspace is given by

\mathfrak{a}=\begin{bmatrix}D&0&0\\
0&-D&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}

where
D
is
r\times r
and diagonal. Let

\mathfrak{n}^{+}=\mathfrak{n}=\begin{bmatrix}0&e&\alpha\\
0&0&0\\
0&\psi&0\end{bmatrix},

with positive root system for
\mathfrak{g}
given by

\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{2},\dots,\epsilon_{r-1}-\epsilon_{r},\epsilon_{r}-\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n-1}-\delta_{n},\delta_{n}-\epsilon_{2r},\dots,\epsilon_{r+2}-\epsilon_{r+1}.

We see that for this choice of Borel, the Weyl vector
\rho
restricts to
\mathfrak{a}
as:

\rho|_{\mathfrak{a}}=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{r}\left(\frac{2(r-i)-n+1}{2}\right)(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{r+i}).

Here
\mathfrak{a}^{*}
is spanned by weights of the form:

a_{1}(\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{r+1})+a_{2}(\epsilon_{2}-\epsilon_{r+2})+\dots+a_{r}(\epsilon_{r}-\epsilon_{2r}).

By Prop. 6.11 of
[
She21a
]
, we may write:

v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}}=\prod\limits_{i,j}(\phi_{ij}-\psi_{ij})\prod\limits_{i,j}(\alpha_{ij}-\beta_{ij}).

Now
\psi_{ij}\in\mathfrak{n}^{+}
and for all
i,j
and since
\psi_{ij}
commutes with
\phi_{k\ell}
, the above expression has the same Harish-Chandra projection (up to a sign) as:

\prod\limits_{i,j}\phi_{ij}\prod\limits_{i,j}(\alpha_{ij}-\beta_{ij}).

We additionally have
\alpha_{ij}+\beta_{ij}\in\mathfrak{k}
, so up to scalar the above has the same Harish-Chandra projection as:

\prod\limits_{i,j}\phi_{ij}\prod\limits_{i,j}\alpha_{ij}

From here the proof works by deal with all terms with index 1, and then concluding by induction. Namely, begin by writing the above as

\left(\prod\limits_{(i,j)\neq(1,1)}\phi_{ij}\right)\phi_{11}\alpha_{11}\left(\prod\limits_{(i,j)\neq(1,1)}\alpha_{ij}\right).

If we move
\alpha_{11}
all the way to the left (which we do since it lies in
\mathfrak{n}
), we obtain one term given by

\left(\prod\limits_{(i,j)\neq(1,1)}\phi_{ij}\right)\left(\prod\limits_{(i,j)\neq(1,1)}\alpha_{ij}\right)(h_{\epsilon_{1}-\delta_{1}}+n-r),

and the rest of the terms contain
[\alpha_{11},\phi_{ij}]
for
(i,j)\neq(1,1)
. Now this commutator is nonzero in two cases: in one case
j=1
, and we get the even root vector of weight
\delta_{i}-\delta_{1}
, which lies in
\mathfrak{k}
. Thus we move it all the way to the right; when we move it past root vectors
\phi_{kl}
, we get something nonzero if and only if
k=1
, in which case we get
\phi_{il}
for
i\geq 1
; but then
\phi_{il}
will appear twice, and so we obtain 0. If we move it past
\alpha_{kl}
, we get something nonzero if and only if
\ell=i
, in which case we obtain
\alpha_{ki}
for
k\geq 1
which will appear twice, so we again get 0. Thus these terms all vanish.

The other case is if
i=1
in which case
[\alpha_{11},\phi_{ij}]
is a an even root vector of weight
\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{j}
, which lies in
\mathfrak{n}
. Thus we want to move it all the way to the left; doing so, we pick up new terms only when moving it past
\phi_{kl}
for
l=1
, in which case we obtain
\phi_{kj}
, which will then appear twice, so the term becomes 0. Thus only our first term written above survives.

One may now continue and apply the same argument after moving
\alpha_{12}
all the way to the right in order to obtain

\left(\prod\limits_{(i,j)\neq(1,1),(2,1)}\phi_{ij}\right)\left(\prod\limits_{(i,j)\neq(1,1),(1,2)}\alpha_{ij}\right)(h_{\epsilon_{1}-\delta_{1}}+n-r)(h_{\epsilon_{1}-\delta_{2}}+n-r-1).

Continue in this way, we obtain

\left(\prod\limits_{j\neq 1}\phi_{ij}\right)\left(\prod\limits_{i\neq 1}\alpha_{ij}\right)\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}(h_{\epsilon_{1}-\delta_{j}}+n-r-j+1).

Now we deal with the terms with roots of the form
\epsilon_{i}-\delta_{1}
for
1<i\leq r
, starting with
i=1
and moving up in
i
. Working in the same fashion as above, and we obtain

\left(\prod\limits_{i,j\neq 1}\phi_{ij}\right)\left(\prod\limits_{i,j\neq 1}\alpha_{ij}\right)\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}(h_{\epsilon_{1}-\delta_{j}}+n-r-j+1)\prod\limits_{i=2}^{r}(h_{\epsilon_{i}-\delta_{1}}+n-r+i-1).

Now we can conclude inductively to obtain:

\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq r\\
1\leq j\leq n\end{subarray}}(h_{\epsilon_{i}-\delta_{j}}+n-r+i-j).

Now set
t_{i}=\frac{1}{2}h_{\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{r+i}}
; in particular
(\epsilon_{i}-\epsilon_{r+i})(t_{i})=1
. Applying the antipode on
\mathfrak{a}
we have shown that (up to scalar):

HC(v_{\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}})=\prod\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq r\\
1\leq j\leq n\end{subarray}}(t_{i}-n+r-i+j).
