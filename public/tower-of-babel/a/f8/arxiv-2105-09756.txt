A Fully Adaptive Self-Stabilizing Transformer for LCL Problems
Shimon Bitton, Yuval Emek, Taisuke Izumi, Shay Kutten

Abstract
The first generic self-stabilizing transformer for local problems in a constrained bandwidth model is introduced. This transformer can be applied to a wide class of locally checkable labeling (LCL) problems, converting a given fault free synchronous algorithm that satisfies certain conditions into a self-stabilizing synchronous algorithm for the same problem. The resulting self-stabilizing algorithms are anonymous, size-uniform, and fully adaptive in the sense that their time complexity is bounded as a function of the number k k of nodes that suffered faults (possibly at different times) since the last legal configuration. Specifically, for graphs whose degrees are up-bounded by Δ \Delta , the algorithms produced by the transformer stabilize in time proportional to log ⁡ ( k + Δ ) \log(k+\Delta) in expectation, independently of the number of nodes in the graph. As such, the transformer is applicable also for infinite graphs (with degree bound Δ \Delta ). Another appealing feature of the transformer is its small message size overhead. The transformer is applied to known algorithms (or simple variants thereof) for some classic LCL problems, producing the first anonymous size-uniform self-stabilizing algorithms for these problems that are provably fully adaptive. From a technical point of view, the transformer’s key design feature is a novel probabilistic tool that allows different nodes to act in synchrony even though their clocks may have been adversarially manipulated.

Introduction

Distributed systems are notoriously subject to faults and much of the effort
in the design and implementation of distributed algorithms is devoted to fault
tolerance issues.
Introduced in the seminal paper of Dijkstra
[
Dij82
]
,

self-stabilization
is a fundamental approach to fault tolerance
requiring that the system recovers from any combination of transient faults and
stabilizes back to a legal configuration within finite time.
The strong guarantees of this natural approach attracted a lot of attention
over the years making self-stabilization an extensively studied research field
(see

[
Dol00
,
ADDP19
]

for textbooks).

The main performance measure for self-stabilizing algorithms is their
stabilization
run-time
.
With very few exceptions (discussed in Sec.
13
),
this is measured as a function of the size of the system:
the more processors are in the system, the longer is the time it takes for the
algorithm to stabilize.
However, while self-stabilizing systems are guaranteed to recover from

any
number of transient faults, in most systems, the common scenario is
that over a limited time interval, only
a few
faults occur.
Although a small number of faults can seriously hinder the operation of the
whole system, one may hope that the system recovers from them faster than the
recovery time from a larger number of faults, regardless of the total number
of processors.

With this hope in mind, we turn to the notion of
fully adaptive
run-time
that expresses the recovery time of a self-stabilizing algorithm as
a function of the number of faults (supporting also dynamic
topology
changes
), rather than the size of the graph
G
on which it runs.
Our main contribution is a generic transformer that can be applied to a wide
class of
locally checkable labeling (LCL)
problems

[
NS95
]
, converting a given fault free synchronous algorithm

\mathcal{A}
that satisfies certain properties into a self-stabilizing synchronous
algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
for the same
problem.

Our transformer ensures that
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}

the graph’s degree bound

Another appealing feature of our transformer is that the self-stabilizing
algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
is
anonymous

,
namely, the nodes do not need to have unique IDs, nor are they assumed to know
the size of
G

we do allow the nodes to know the degree bound

in cases that this is
necessary for the original fault free algorithm
\mathcal{A}

requirement of the transformer itself.
Combined with its fully adaptive run-time and with a (deterministic) bound
that we establish on the “propagation radius” of the faults, we conclude
that
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
can also work in an
infinite
(bounded

of transient faults as long as those can be partitioned into “spaced apart”
regions, each containing a finite number of faulty nodes (see Sec.

and

While generic transformers, that compile a fault free algorithm into a
self-stabilizing one, have been the topic of many works (see
Sec.
13
), to the best of our knowledge, we develop
the first local transformer that comes with appealing guarantees for both the
stabilization run-time and the message size overhead.
Our transformer is also the first one to ensure that the stabilization
run-time is fully adaptive without committing to huge messages that
essentially encode the whole history.
Moreover, we are unaware of any existing transformer that produces anonymous
size-uniform self-stabilizing algorithms (and it is far from being clear if
the transformers in the existing literature can be modified to provide such a
guarantee).
As such, the current paper presents the first transformer that is suitable
(out of the box) for infinite graphs as well.

Distributed Algorithms.

Consider a connected message passing communication network represented by a
graph

G\in\mathcal{U}_{\Delta}
.
The nodes of
G
are associated with identical (possibly randomized) state
machines and we refer to the vector encoding all node states as the network’s

.
These state machines operate in synchronous

so that in each round

,
every node

executes the following operations:

(1)

v
performs local computation and updates its state;

(2)

v
sends messages to (a subset of) its neighbors;
and

(3)

v
receives the messages sent to it by its neighbors in round
t
.

Throughout, we assume that round
t
occurs during the time interval

so that time
t
marks the beginning of the round.

We adhere to the conventions of the

graph algorithms:
Node

has a port

for each neighbor

such that from

u
is regarded as neighbor

.
The nodes do not have unique identifiers and they are not assumed to know the
graph size (if the graph is finite).
The only global information held by the nodes is the degree bound
\Delta
.

\mathcal{P}=\langle\mathcal{O},\mathcal{G},\{\mathcal{C}_{G}\}_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\rangle

.

,

.

(resp.,

.

Fault Free Algorithms.

Fix a positive integer
\Delta
.
Consider a family

\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\Delta}

of graphs with degree bound
\Delta
and a distributed node/edge
problem

\mathcal{P}=\langle\mathcal{O},\mathcal{G},\{\mathcal{C}_{G}\}_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\rangle
.
An algorithm for
\mathcal{P}
is said to be
fault free
if its
correctness
relies on the assumption that the execution commences from an initial global
state determined by the algorithm designer.
More formally, a fault free algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is associated with a local state

S_{d}
,

0\leq d\leq\Delta
,
in which the output registers holds
\bot
, assigned at the beginning of the
execution to each node of degree
d
.
It is guaranteed that if the graph is finite (and assuming that no faults
occur), then the execution reaches a legal configuration for
\mathcal{P}
in
finite time with probability
1
and that this configuration does not change
subsequently.

Phase-Based Algorithms.

In this paper, we focus on a class of fault free algorithms referred to as

phase-based
.
The execution of a phase-based algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for a distributed node (resp.,
edge) problem
\mathcal{P}
is divided into
phases
so that every phase
consists of
\phi
rounds, where

\phi\geq 2

is a parameter of
\mathcal{A}
.
The rounds within a phase are referred to as
steps
, indexed by

j=0,1,\dots,\phi-1
.
The execution of
\mathcal{A}
progresses by running the phases in succession so
that every node executes step

0\leq j\leq\phi-1

of phase

i=0,1,\dots

in round

t=i\phi+j
.

Consider the graph

G\in\mathcal{G}

on which the phase-based algorithm
\mathcal{A}
runs.
The operation of
\mathcal{A}
is fully specified by a
phase procedure
,
denoted by
\mathtt{Phase}
, that dictates the actions of a node

v\in V(G)

in each step

j=0,1,\dots,\phi-1

of phase
i
.
A key feature of the phase-based algorithm is that the code of

\mathtt{Phase}
for step
j
is oblivious to the phase number
i
.
As such,
\mathtt{Phase}
should be seen as a procedure that is invoked from
scratch at the beginning of every phase and runs for
\phi
rounds.
Moreover, at the beginning of each phase, node
v
resets all its registers
with the exception of the output register
\mathtt{out}_{v}
(resp., output
registers
\mathtt{out}_{v}(u)
for
u\in N_{G}(v)
), which means that the
only information passed from one application of
\mathtt{Phase}
to the next
is the node (resp., edge) configuration associated with
\mathcal{A}
.
In other words, all registers of
\mathcal{A}
other than the output registers are
temporary registers whose usage is confined to the scope of a single phase.

Among the registers that a node
v
resets at the beginning of phase
i
are
the registers that store the messages sent to
v
from its neighbors in the
last step of phase

i-1
.
This means, in particular, that
v
loses trace of its neighbors’ states,
including the content of their output registers, when a new phase begins.
To compensate for this loss, we require that the first step

j=0

of the phase, referred to as the
announcement step
, is a special step
in which each node

v\in V(G)

sends
\mathtt{out}_{v}
(resp.,
\mathtt{out}_{v}(u)
) to each one of
its neighbors

u\in N_{G}(u)
.
4
4

4

The announcement step is redundant in the context of fault free algorithms for
distributed edge problems as all edges are supposed to be port-consistent.
We add it nonetheless for the sake of compatibility.

The last step

j=\phi-1

of the phase, referred to as the
decision step
, has a special role as
well:
this is the only step in which the nodes are allowed to write into their
output registers.

Self-Stabilization and Fully Adaptive Run-Time.

An algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for

\mathcal{P}=\langle\mathcal{O},\mathcal{G},\{\mathcal{C}_{G}\}_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\rangle

is
self-stabilizing
if it is guaranteed to reach a legal configuration
in finite time with probability
1
from any initial global state.
In the current paper, this notion is captured by a malicious
adversary

that can modify the content of any register maintained by
\mathcal{A}
(essentially
modifying the nodes’ states),
including the registers that hold the incoming messages.
The adversary can also impose dynamic
topology changes
, including the
addition and removal of nodes and edges, as long as the resulting graph
remains in
\mathcal{G}
.
The only piece of information that the adversary cannot modify is the degree
bound
\Delta
, assumed to be hard-coded into the nodes.
To simplify the discussion, we assume that the adversarial manipulations that
occur in round

t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

take place towards the end of the round (say, at time

t+1-\epsilon
),
i.e., after the messages sent in round
t
have reached their destination and
before the local computation of round

t+1

begins (this assumption is without loss of generality).

Consider a graph

G\in\mathcal{G}

on which
\mathcal{A}
runs.
Node

v\in V(G)

is said to be
manipulated
by the adversary if either

(i)
the content of (any of)
v
’s registers is modified;

(ii)
the bijection between
v
’s ports and
N_{G}(v)
is modified;
or

(iii)

v
is added to the graph as a new node.

Notice that condition (ii) includes topological changes involving the edges
incident on
v
as well as “rewiring” of
v
’s ports.

Recall that
C_{t}
denotes the (node/edge) configuration of
\mathcal{A}
’s execution
on
G
at
time
t
.
Consider times

t^{*}_{a}<t^{*}_{b}

and an integer

k>0

and suppose that

(1)
configuration

C_{t^{*}_{a}-1}=C_{t^{*}_{a}}

is legal;

(2)
the adversary does not manipulate any node in round

t^{*}_{a}-1
;

(3)
the adversary manipulates
k
nodes during the round interval

[t^{*}_{a},t^{*}_{b}-1]
;
and

(4)
the adversary does not manipulate any node from round
t^{*}_{b}
onwards.

We say that
\mathcal{A}
has
fully adaptive run-time

T(\Delta,k)

for a function

T:\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\times\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{>0}

if there exists a time

t>t^{*}_{b}

such that

(i)

C_{t}
is a legal configuration for the graph resulting from the adversarial
manipulations, i.e., the graph at time
t^{*}_{b}
;

(ii)

C_{t^{\prime}}=C_{t}

for every

t^{\prime}\geq t
;
and

(iii)

t\leq t^{*}_{b}+T(\Delta,k)
.

If
t
and
C_{t}
are random variables determined by the coin tosses of

\mathcal{A}
, then we require that condition (iii) holds in expectation.
It is important to point out that the number
k
of manipulated nodes is
chosen by the adversary;
the self-stabilizing algorithm does not know
k
nor does it know any
approximation of
k
.

Locally Checkable Labelings.

A
locally checkable labeling (LCL)
over the output value set

\mathcal{O}
is a predicate
\ell
that is function

\ell:\mathcal{O}\times\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{O})\rightarrow\{\mathit{true},\mathit{false}\}
.

Fix an LCL

\ell

over the set
\mathcal{O}
of output values and consider a graph

G\in\mathcal{U}

and a node configuration

C:V(G)\rightarrow\mathcal{O}\cup\{\bot\}
.
A decided node

v\in D(C)

is said to be
content
under
C
(with respect to
\ell
) if

\ell(C(v),C[v])=\mathit{true}
;
otherwise,
v
is said to be
uncontent
.
Let
\Gamma(C)
be the set of nodes that are content under
C
and let

\Lambda(C)=D(C)-\Gamma(C)

be the set of nodes that are (decided yet) uncontent under
C
.
The node configuration
C
is said to be
strong
if

\Gamma(C)=D(C)
,
namely, if every decided node is also content.

The edges of the graph
G
can also be classified into content and uncontent
with respect to the LCL

\ell
.
Consider an edge configuration

C:E(G)\rightarrow\mathcal{O}\cup\{\bot\}
.
A decided edge

e\in D(C)

is said to be
content
under
C
(with respect to
\ell
) if
the node
e
is content under
C
with respect to
\ell
in the line
graph
L(G)
, recalling that the nodes of
L(G)
are identified with the edges
of
G
;
otherwise,
e
is said to be
uncontent
.
Let
\Gamma(C)
be the set of edges that are content under
C
and let

\Lambda(C)=D(C)-\Gamma(C)

be the set of edges that are (decided yet) uncontent under
C
.
The edge configuration
C
is said to be
strong
if

\Gamma(C)=D(C)
,
namely, if every decided edge is also content.
We emphasize that the distinction between content and uncontent nodes/edges
applies only to decided nodes/edges.

The notion of LCLs allows us to define an important class of distributed
problems.
An
LCL distributed node problem (node-LCL)
(resp.,
LCL
distributed edge problem (edge-LCL)
) is a
3
-tuple

\mathcal{P}=\langle\mathcal{O},\mathcal{G},\ell\rangle
,
where

\mathcal{O}
is a set of output values,

\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{U}

is a family of graphs,
and

\ell

is an LCL over the output value set
\mathcal{O}
.
The legal node (resp., edge) configurations for
\mathcal{P}
are defined as
follows:
Given a graph

G\in\mathcal{G}
,
a complete node (resp., edge) configuration

C:V(G)\rightarrow\mathcal{O}

(resp.,

C:E(G)\rightarrow\mathcal{O}
)
is legal for
\mathcal{P}
if and only if node
v
(resp., edge
e
) is content
under
C
with respect to
\ell
for every

v\in V(G)

(resp.,

e\in E(G)
).

Configured Graphs.

Consider a node-LCL (resp., edge-LCL)

\mathcal{P}=\langle\mathcal{O},\mathcal{G},\ell\rangle
.
A
configured graph
for
\mathcal{P}
is a pair

(G,C)
,
where

G\in\mathcal{G}

and

C\in\mathcal{O}^{V(G)}

(resp.,

C\in\mathcal{O}^{E(G)}
);
let
\mathcal{CG}(\mathcal{P})
denote the collection of all configured graphs for

\mathcal{P}
.
A configured graph

(G,C)\in\mathcal{CG}(\mathcal{P})

is called a
strongly configured graph
if
C
is strong for
G
(under

\mathcal{P}
);
let
\mathcal{SCG}(\mathcal{P})
denote the collection of all strongly
configured graphs for
\mathcal{P}
.

Technical Challenges and Intuition

Consider a distributed node (resp., edge) problem

\mathcal{P}=\langle\mathcal{O},\mathcal{G},\{\mathcal{C}_{G}\}_{G\in\mathcal{G}}\rangle

and a fault free algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for
\mathcal{P}
, running on a graph

G\in\mathcal{G}
.
In this section, we discuss the technical challenges that arise when one
wishes to transform
\mathcal{A}
into a self-stabilizing algorithm

\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
for
\mathcal{P}
.
We also present the requirements we impose on
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{A}
and
provide some intuition as to how these requirements allow us to overcome the
aforementioned challenges.

The first (and easiest) challenge is implementing a fault detection mechanism:
While the fault free execution of
\mathcal{A}
is guaranteed to reach a legal
configuration (and to remain in this configuration), when it comes to

\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
, we must be able to detect faults introduced by the
adversarial manipulations.
To this end, we require that
\mathcal{P}
is a node-LCL (resp., edge-LCL)

\mathcal{P}=\langle\mathcal{O},\mathcal{G},\ell\rangle
,
thus ensuring that illegal configurations can be detected locally (see
Prop.
4
and

11
).
Upon detecting an uncontent node

v\in V(G)

(resp., edge

\{u,v\}\in E(G)
),
the output register
\mathtt{out}_{v}
is reset to

\mathtt{out}_{v}\leftarrow\bot

(resp., output registers
\mathtt{out}_{u}(v)
and
\mathtt{out}_{v}(u)
are reset to

\mathtt{out}_{u}(v),\mathtt{out}_{v}(u)\leftarrow\bot
).

The next challenge is concerned with ensuring that a small number
k
of
manipulated nodes do not develop into a “reset avalanche”, leading to a
large number of undecided nodes (resp., edges);
this is crucial for bounding the stabilization time of the self-stabilizing
algorithm as a function of
k
, independently of the size of
G
.
Indeed, it is not difficult to devise LCL problems and legal configurations
for these problems in which the manipulation of a single node triggers an
arbitrarily long cascade of resets.
To overcome this difficulty, we require that
\ell
admits a bounded

influence number
, a parameter that allows us to control the distance to
which faults can propagate (see
Prop.
3
and

10
).

Perhaps the most interesting challenges are those involved with recovering
from an invalid global state.
First, by employing certain requirements imposed on
\mathcal{P}
(see
Prop.
2
and

9
) and
\mathcal{A}
(see Prop.
6
and

13
), we prove that starting from a
certain (deterministic) time

t_{s}>t^{*}_{b}
,
it is guaranteed that all configurations are strong, which means that resets
no longer occur.
From that time on, the task of converging to a valid global state reduces to
the task of augmenting an (arbitrary) incomplete strong configuration into a
complete one.

To accomplish this task, we require that
\mathcal{A}
is a phase-based algorithm
(see Prop.
5
and

12
).
One may hope that the robustness of phase-based algorithms alone ensures that
repeated invocations of the phase procedure
\mathtt{Phase}
eventually
“takes care” of all undecided nodes (resp., edges), leading to a complete
(legal) configuration.
Unfortunately, this (informal) argument hides a significant flaw:
The correctness of the phase-based algorithm
\mathcal{A}
relies on the assumption
that
\mathtt{Phase}
runs at all nodes in synchrony so that step

j=0,1,\dots,\phi-1

of phase

i=0,1,\dots

is executed by all nodes concurrently in round

t=i\phi+j
.
To implement that, the nodes should maintain a
modular clock
that keeps
track of

j=t\bmod\phi
.
While maintaining this modular clock in a fault free environment is a trivial
task, the situation becomes trickier in the self-stabilizing setting:
the adversary can modify the modular clocks, thus causing the corresponding
nodes to execute their phases “out of sync” indefinitely.

We cope with this difficulty by introducing a technique called

probabilistic phase synchronization
(see
Sec.
5
).
In high level terms, this technique replaces the (deterministic) modular clock
by an (algorithm independent) randomized module, referred to as
\mathtt{PPS}
, whose
implementation is based on a simple ergodic Markov chain, maintained
independently at each node.
The state space of the Markov chain is

\{\mathit{\hbar},0,1,\dots,\phi-1\}
,
where states

0,1,\dots,\phi-1

are identified with the
\phi
steps of
\mathtt{Phase}
and
\mathit{\hbar}

is an auxiliary state whose role is to ensure that the nodes initiate new
phases at random times.
This facilitates a probabilistic synchronization mechanism among the nodes:
A node

v\in V(G)

residing in state

j\in\{0,1,\dots,\phi-1\}

of its
\mathtt{PPS}
Markov chain simulates step
j
of
\mathtt{Phase}
in
conjunction with (and only with) its neighbors whose own
\mathtt{PPS}
Markov chains
also reside in state
j
.

By bounding the mixing time of the Markov chain underlying
\mathtt{PPS}
, we
guarantee that a node

v\in V(G)

has ample opportunities to execute
\mathtt{Phase}
in synchrony with each
neighbor

u\in N_{G}(v)
.
Combined with an additional requirement that
\mathcal{A}
admits a certain type of
potential function (see Prop.
7
and

14
), we can establish (probabilistically)
sufficient progress in each application of
\mathtt{Phase}
, ultimately
bounding the fully adaptive run-time of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
.

Care must be taken though that the decisions of a node

v\in V(G)

do not conflict with the decisions of neighboring nodes

u\in N_{G}(v)

that are not phase-synchronized with
v
.
To this end, we exploit the requirement that nodes may write into their output
registers only during the decision step of each phase, which means that during
its own decision step, node
v
is aware of the decisions of
u
for
every neighbor

u\in N_{G}(v)

that started a phase before
v
.

\mathcal{A}

\mathcal{P}
that we wish to transform

.

7

7

The current section is phrased for edge-LCL algorithms, however, it applies

Assume that
\mathcal{A}
satisfies the
\phi
-phase-based property and let

\mathtt{Phase}
be its corresponding phase procedure.
Recall that the execution of
\mathtt{Phase}
in each round

by the corresponding step number

\mathcal{A}

Under
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}

The
\mathtt{PPS}

Fix some graph

G\in\mathcal{G}

and consider a node

v\in V
.
The
\mathtt{PPS}
module can be viewed as an ergodic Markov chain over the state space

\{\mathit{\hbar},0,1,\dots,\phi-1\}

that

The current state of (
v

\mathtt{PPS}

\mathit{step}_{v}

Beside
\mathtt{Get\_Step\_PPS}

This procedure is denoted by
\mathtt{Step\_Counter\_\phi}

The fundamental properties of

Observation 5.1
.

For every time

and node

,
we have

•

\mathbb{P}(\mathit{step}_{v,t+1}=j\mid\mathit{step}_{v,t}=j-1)=1

•

\mathbb{P}(\mathit{step}_{v,t+1}=\mathit{\hbar}\mid\mathit{step}_{v,t}=\phi-1)=1

and

•

\mathbb{P}(\mathit{step}_{v,t+1}=\mathit{\hbar}\mid\mathit{step}_{v,t}=\mathit{\hbar})=\mathbb{P}(\mathit{step}_{v,t+1}=0\mid\mathit{step}_{v,t}=\mathit{\hbar})=1/2
.

This holds independently of any coin toss of
v
prior to time
t
and of
any
coin toss of all other nodes.

We now turn to bound the mixing time of the Markov chain in

The following lemma is established by showing that this ergodic Markov chain

\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
be the self-stabilizing algorithm produced from

\mathcal{A}
by the edge-LCL transformer presented in
Sec.

.

Our goal in this section is to establish the correctness and run-time

\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
, proving

.
The proof consists of two parts:
First, in Sec.

7.1
Fault Recovery

Recall that the adversarial manipulations may include the addition/removal of
nodes/edges, hence the graph may change during the round interval

[t^{*}_{a},t^{*}_{b}-1]
.
In what follows, we reserve
G
for the graph at time
t^{*}_{b}
, recalling
that this is also the graph at any time

t\geq t^{*}_{b}
.

Consider a graph

H\in\mathcal{G}

and an edge configuration

C:E(H)\rightarrow\mathcal{O}\cup\{\bot\}
.
We make an extensive use of the following operator:
given an edge subset

U\subseteq U(C)

and a function

f:U\rightarrow\mathcal{O}
,
let

[C\nwarrow f]

denote the edge configuration
C^{\prime}
defined by setting
(1)

C^{\prime}(e)=f(e)

for every

e\in U
;
and
(2)

C^{\prime}(e)=C(e)

for every

e\in E(H)-U
.

Recall that
C_{t}
denotes the edge configuration of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
at
time

t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
For a time

t\geq t^{*}_{b}
,
let

\hat{D}_{t}=U(C_{t})\cap D(C_{t+1})

be the subset of edges that become decided (as a result of the decision step
of
\mathtt{Phase}
) during round
t
.
Let

\hat{f}_{t}:\hat{D}_{t}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}

be the function that corresponds to the decisions taken by the edges in

\hat{D}_{t}
during round
t
, i.e.,

\hat{f}_{t}(e)=C_{t+1}(e)

for every

e\in\hat{D}_{t}
,
and let

\hat{C}_{t}=[C_{t}\nwarrow\hat{f}_{t}]
.
We are now ready to state the following two fundamental observations.

Observation
.

The following statements hold for every

t\geq t^{*}_{b}+1
:

(1)

D(\hat{C}_{t})=D(C_{t})\cup D(C_{t+1})
;

(2)

\hat{C}_{t}(e)=C_{t+1}(e)=\hat{C}_{t+1}(e)

for every edge

e\in D(C_{t+1})
;

(3)

\Lambda(C_{t})=D(C_{t})\cap U(C_{t+1})=D(\hat{C}_{t})\cap U(C_{t+1})
;
and

(4)

D(C_{t+1})=D(\hat{C}_{t})-\Lambda(C_{t})
.

Observation
.

For every time
t\geq t^{*}_{b}+2
, it holds that all the edges are
port-consistent.

The remainder of this section is dedicated to proving the following lemma.

Lemma 7.1
.

For every

t\geq t_{s}
,
it holds that

(G,C_{t})\in\mathcal{SCG}(\mathcal{P})
.
Moreover, if

e\in D(C_{t})
,
then

C_{t+1}(e)=C_{t}(e)
.

Lem.
7.1
provides the cornerstone
for the correctness of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
as it ensures that if
C_{t}
is
complete for some time

t\geq t_{s}
,
then
C_{t}
is also legal and

C_{t^{\prime}}=C_{t}

for every

t^{\prime}\geq t
.
The proof of
Lem.
7.1
relies on the following two
lemmas for which we define

t_{0}\,=\,t^{*}_{b}+\phi+1\,.

Lemma 7.2
.

For every
t\geq t_{0}
, it holds that

D(C_{t})\subseteq\Gamma(\hat{C}_{t-1})
.

Proof.

A node

v\in V(G)

that runs the decision step of
\mathtt{Phase}
in round

t-1

has

\mathit{step}_{v,t-1}=\phi-1
.
Taking

t^{\prime}=t-\phi
,
the design of
\mathtt{PPS}
ensures that

\mathit{step}_{v,t^{\prime}}=0
.
By the definition of the phase procedure, we know that
v
resets all the
registers maintained by
\mathtt{Phase}
in round
t^{\prime}
.
Since

t^{\prime}\geq t^{*}_{b}+1
,
it follows that the invocation of
\mathtt{Phase}
that starts in round

t^{\prime}

is completed by all involved nodes in a fault free manner.

Consider an edge

e=\{v,u\}\in D(C_{t})
.
We argue that

e\notin\Lambda(C_{t-1})
.
Indeed, assume by contradiction that

e\in\Lambda(C_{t-1})
.
Since
t-2>t^{*}_{b}
, in round
t-2
there are no manipulations and
it follows by the design of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
and the guarantee of

\mathtt{Detect}
that

\mathtt{Detect}_{v,t-1}(u)=\mathtt{Detect}_{u,t-1}(v)=\mathit{false}
.
Hence

\mathtt{out}_{v,t}(u)=\mathtt{out}_{u,t}=\bot
,
in contradiction to the assumption that

e\in D(C_{t})
.

If

e\in U(C_{t-1})
,
then
v
and
u
runs the decision step of
\mathtt{Phase}
in round

t-1
.
Otherwise
(
e\in D(C_{t-1})
),
we know that

e\in\Gamma(C_{t-1})
.
In both cases, Prop.
6
can be
applied to conclude that

e\in\Gamma(\hat{C}_{t-1})
.
∎

Lemma 7.3
.

Fix some time

t\geq t_{0}
.
If

e\in\Lambda(C_{t})
,
then the supportive digraph

D_{\ell}

admits a directed path

emerging from
C_{t}(e)
of length

.

Proof.

We establish the assertion by induction on

.
The base case of

admits a path of length

emerging from

for every

including

.

Assume that the assertion holds for

t-1

and consider an edge

e\in\Lambda(C_{t})
.
As

\Lambda(C_{t})\subseteq D(C_{t})
,
Lem.
7.2
ensures
that

e\in\Gamma(\hat{C}_{t-1})
;
let

be the output value of

under
\hat{C}_{t-1}
.
Since

e\in\Gamma(\hat{C}_{t-1})
,
it follows that

\hat{C}_{t-1}[e]\in\mathcal{T}(o)
.
Let

\hat{M}=\hat{M}(e,t)\in\mathcal{T}^{*}(o)

be a core for

such that

\hat{M}\subseteq\hat{C}_{t-1}[e]
.
We argue that

\hat{M}\neq\emptyset
.
Indeed, recalling that

\hat{C}_{t-1}[e]\supseteq C_{t}[e]
,
if

\hat{M}=\emptyset
,
then

e\in\Gamma(C_{t})
by the core coverage property
(Prop.
2
),
in contradiction to

e\in\Lambda(C_{t})
.

Let

\hat{B}=\hat{B}(e,t)\subseteq N_{G}(e)

be a subset of
e
’s neighbors whose decisions under

\hat{C}_{t-1}

realize
\hat{M}
.
For each

e^{\prime}\in\hat{B}
,
either
(1)

e^{\prime}\in D(C_{t})

and

C_{t}(e^{\prime})=\hat{C}_{t-1}(e^{\prime})
;
or
(2)

e^{\prime}\in U(C_{t})
.
Recalling that

\hat{C}_{t-1}[e]\supseteq C_{t}[e]
,
if

C_{t}(e^{\prime})=\hat{C}_{t-1}(e^{\prime})

for every

e^{\prime}\in\hat{B}
,
then

e\in\Gamma(C_{t})

by the core coverage property
(Prop.
2
), in contradiction to

e\in\Lambda(C_{t})
.

Therefore, there must exist an edge

e^{\prime}\in\hat{B}

such that

e^{\prime}\in U(C_{t})
.
Since

e^{\prime}\in D(\hat{C}_{t-1})
,
it follows that

e^{\prime}\in\Lambda(C_{t-1})
.
By the inductive hypothesis, the supportive digraph

D_{\ell}

admits a directed path
P^{\prime}
emerging from

of length

t-1-t_{0}
.
Recalling that

,
we conclude that

(o,o^{\prime})\in E(D_{\ell})
.
The assertion follows as

(o,o^{\prime})\circ P^{\prime}

is a directed path in
D_{\ell}
emerging from

of length

t-t_{0}
.
∎

We are now ready to establish
Lem.
7.1
.

proof of Lem.
7.1
.

The first part of the assertion follows from

7.3
since

and since
\mathcal{P}
satisfies that
\nu
-bounded influence property
(Prop.

).
The second part of the assertion follows from the design of

, recalling that if

and all the nodes in the set

,
then

\mathtt{Detect}_{v,t}(u)=\mathtt{Detect}_{u,t}(v)=\mathit{true}
.
∎

7.2
Fully Adaptive Run Time

Our goal in this section is to bound the fully adaptive run-time of

\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
, proving that in response to
k
node manipulations, the
algorithm stabilizes by time

t^{*}_{b}+T
,
where

\mathbb{E}(T)\,\leq\,O\left(\left(\phi^{5}/\beta\right)\cdot\left(\log(k)+(\nu+\phi)\log(\Delta)+\log(\sigma_{0})\right)\right)\,.

(1)

In passing, we also prove that the nodes whose distance from the manipulated
nodes is at least

\nu+\phi+1

retain their original output value (see
Cor.
7.8
), thus establishing
Thm.
4.3
.

To bound the stabilization time
T
of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
, we define the
random variable

T_{1}\,=\,\min\{z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\,:\,\pi(G,C_{t_{s}+z})=0\}\,\,.

Recalling that

t_{s}=t^{*}_{b}+\nu+\phi+2
,
we conclude that

T=O(\nu+\phi)+T_{1}
,
so it remains to bound
\mathbb{E}(T_{1})
.
We start with the following fundamental observation.

Observation
.

The potential function
\pi
satisfies

\pi(G,C_{t})\geq\pi(G,C_{t+1})

for every

t\geq t_{s}
.

Proof.

Follows from Lem.
7.1
by the
definition of a separable potential function.
∎

The following lemma bounds the number of undecided edges at time
t_{s}
.

Lemma 7.4
.

The number of undecided edges at time
t_{s}
satisfies

|U(C_{t_{s}})|\,\leq\,O\left(k\cdot\Delta^{2(\nu+\phi)+1}\right)\,.

To bound
\mathbb{E}(T_{1})
, observe that by combining the definition of a locally
separable potential function and
Lem.
7.4
, we conclude
that

\pi(G,C_{t_{s}})\,\leq\,O\left(\sigma_{0}\cdot k\cdot\Delta^{2(\nu+\phi)+1}\right)\,.

(2)

The desired bound is established by proving that this potential decreases
sufficiently fast in expectation.
This is stated formally in the following lemma (established in the
sequel), where

\tau=O(\phi^{3})

is the parameter promised in
Cor.
5.3
.

Lemma 7.5
.

Fix some time

t\geq t_{s}

and the global state at time
t
.
The design of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
ensures that

\mathbb{E}(\pi(G,C_{t+\tau+\phi}))\,\leq\,\left(1-\frac{\beta}{4\phi^{2}}\right)\cdot\pi(G,C_{t})\,.

Combining Lem.
7.5

with (
9
), we deduce by a standard
probabilistic argument that

\mathbb{E}(T_{1})\,\leq\,O\left(\left(\phi^{5}/\beta\right)\cdot\left(\log(k)+(\nu+\phi)\log(\Delta)+\log(\sigma_{0})\right)\right)\,.

(3)

The bound in (
1
) directly follows from
(
3
).

We now turn to establish
Lem.
7.5
.

.

Throughout the proof we denote

,

, and

by

, and

that their step variable is

at

.
For every

,
let

be the event that

u\in P_{\tau}
.
Notice that the design of the
\mathtt{PPS}

By Cor.
5.3
,
for every node

\mathbb{P}\left(\operatorname{A}_{u}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2\phi}\,.

(4)

Let

be an indicator function for the event

Let

and

(7)

We now turn to low-bound

G^{\tau}

.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\pi(G^{\tau},\mathtt{Phase}(G^{\tau},C^{\tau}))\right)=

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\pi(G^{\tau},\mathtt{Phase}(G^{\tau},C^{\tau}))\mid\pi(G^{\tau},C^{\tau})\right)\right)

Distributed Implementation of the Predicates.

8.2
Properties of the Fault Free Algorithm

Fix a node-LCL

\mathcal{P}=\langle\mathcal{O},\mathcal{G},\ell\rangle
,
where

\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\Delta}

for a degree bound
\Delta
and
\ell
,
and a fault free algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for
\mathcal{P}
.
In this section, we present
Prop.
12
–
14

that should be satisfied by
\mathcal{A}
as a necessary condition for its

Restricting the Phase Procedure.

Consider the graph

G\in\mathcal{G}

on which
\mathcal{A}
runs.
Recall that if
\mathcal{A}
is a phase-based algorithm, then it is fully specified
by a phase length parameter
\phi
and a phase procedure
\mathtt{Phase}

invoked (from scratch) at the beginning of every phase.
Moreover, each phase starts with an announcement step

j=0
,
in which the nodes announce the content of their output register, and ends
with a decision step

j=\phi-1
,
which is the only step in which the nodes may write into their output register.

For the node-LCL algorithm
\mathcal{A}
to be eligible, we add a few more
requirements on the structure of
\mathtt{Phase}
.
First, it is required that a decided node

v\in V(G)

keeps on sending the content of its output register
\mathtt{out}_{v}
to all its
neighbors throughout the phase.
9
9

9

This requirement is obviously redundant when
\mathtt{Phase}
operates under
the fault free algorithm
\mathcal{A}
.
As explained in the sequel though, it facilitates the construction of the
self-stabilizing algorithm.

Moreover, node
v
does not take any role in the operation of
\mathtt{Phase}

beyond informing its neighbors of its output value (in particular, the decided
nodes do not need to keep track of the step number
j
).

Let

U\subseteq V(G)

be the set of nodes that are undecided at the beginning of the phase and
recall that following the announcement step, each node

u\in U

is aware of its undecided neighbors

u^{\prime}\in N_{G}(u)\cap U
.
During steps

j=1,\dots,\phi-2
,
referred to as the phase’s
working stage
, an undecided node

u\in U

may send messages only to its undecided neighbors.
Moreover, during the working stage,
u
should completely “ignore” its
decided neighbors

v\in V(G)-U
,
including the content of their output registers
\mathtt{out}_{v}
.
More formally, the actions of
u
during the working stage may depend on the
number
\mathrm{d}_{G(U)}(u)
of undecided neighbors that
u
has and on the
(global) degree bound
\Delta
, but they should not depend on the decisions

\mathtt{out}_{v}
of its decided neighbors

v\in N_{G}(v)-U
,
nor should they depend on the number

\mathrm{d}_{G}(u)-\mathrm{d}_{G(U)}(u)

of decided neighbors that
u
has.

The only step of the phase in which an undecided node

u\in U

may take into account the content
\mathtt{out}_{v}
of the messages received from
its decided neighbors

v\in N_{G}(v)-U

is the decision step.
This means that the decision of
u
to write an output value

o\in\mathcal{O}

into its output register
\mathtt{out}_{u}
during the decision step may be
affected by the multiset

\{\mathtt{out}_{v}\mid v\in N_{G}(u)-U\}
.

Property 12

(
\phi
-phase-based
)
.

Algorithm
\mathcal{A}
satisfies the

-phase-based
property if it

.

Given a configuration

,
let
\eta
be the execution of the phase procedure
\mathtt{Phase}
on the
graph

whose output registers are set so that

\mathtt{out}_{v}=C(v)

for each

v\in V(G)

(recall that the initial content of all other registers is irrelevant as they
are reset by
\mathtt{Phase}
when
\eta

Let

\mathtt{Phase}(G,C)

be the configuration associated with
\mathtt{Phase}
when
\eta
halts,
i.e., after
\phi
rounds.
Notice that if
\mathtt{Phase}
is randomized, then

\mathtt{Phase}(G,C)

is a random variable that depends on the coin tosses of the nodes

v\in V(G)

during
\eta
.
The following key property can now be stated.

Property 13

(

)
.

\mathcal{A}
with phase procedure

satisfies the

,

;
and

(2)

.
10
10

10

The fact that the graph

belongs to the graph family
\mathcal{G}
is ensured by
Property

.

(Prop.
8
),

(Prop.

),

(Prop.

),
and
the
\mu(\Delta)
-detectability property
(Prop.

and

(II)

\mathcal{A}
satisfies

-phased-based property
(Prop.

),

(Prop.

and

-progress

The guarantees of our transformer are cast in the following theorem.

Theorem 8.1
.

Consider a node-LCL

\mathcal{P}=\langle\mathcal{O},\mathcal{G},\ell\rangle
,
where

\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\Delta}

for a degree bound
\Delta
,
and let
\mathcal{A}
be a fault free algorithm for
\mathcal{P}

\mu_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta)

\mathcal{A}
is

(\nu,\mu(\Delta),\phi,\beta,\sigma_{0})
-eligible,
then
\mathcal{P}
admits a randomized self-stabilizing algorithm

\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
that uses messages of size

O\left(\mu(\Delta)+\mu_{\mathcal{A}}(\Delta)+\log\phi\right)

whose fully adaptive run time in response to
k
node manipulations is

O\left(\left(\phi^{5}/\beta\right)\cdot\left(\log(k)+(\nu+\phi)\log(\Delta)+\log(\sigma_{0}+\rho_{0})\right)\right)\,.

Moreover, nodes whose distance from any manipulated node is at least

(with probability

).

Similarly to the edge-LCL transformer, here too we can allow the adversary to

as long as

can be partitioned into clusters

such that the following two conditions hold for every

(1)

for some

;
and

(2)
if

,
then

\delta_{G}(v,V(G)-C_{i})\geq\nu+\phi+1
.

The theorem then guarantees that the expected recovery time of
G(C_{i})
is
at most

O\left(\left(\phi^{5}/\beta\right)\cdot\left(\log(k_{i})+(\nu+\phi)\log(\Delta)+\log(\sigma_{0}+\rho_{0})\right)\right)

for each

i\geq 1
.

\mathcal{P}=\langle\mathcal{O},\mathcal{G},\ell\rangle

\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{\Delta}

,
and let

be a fault free algorithm for

(\nu,\mu(\Delta),\phi,\beta,\sigma_{0},\rho_{0})

Remark:

The transformer for node-LCL can be obtained without the
\mathtt{PPS}
module using
similar ideas to the ones presented here. We use the
\mathtt{PPS}

nonetheless, for the sake of simplicity.

The transformer composes the self-stabilizing algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}

promised in Thm.

from three components:

(1)
the detection procedure
\mathtt{Detect}
that realizes the

\mu(\Delta)
-detectability of
\mathcal{P}
;

(2)
the phase procedure
\mathtt{Phase}
associated with the
\phi
-phase-based
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
;
and

(3)
the
\mathtt{PPS}
module.

Any message
M
sent under
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
is partitioned into three
fields, one for each component, denoted by

M.\mathtt{detect}
,
M.\mathtt{phase}
, and
M.\mathtt{pps}
, respectively.

Consider the graph

G\in\mathcal{G}

on which
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
runs and some round

.
If a node

is decided at time

\mathtt{out}_{v,t}=o\in\mathcal{O}

then
v
starts round

by calling
\mathtt{Detect}
which is implemented over the

\mathtt{detect}
field of its messages.
If
\mathtt{Detect}
returns
\mathit{true}
, then
v
sends its output value

to all its
neighbors over the
\mathtt{phase}
field of its messages;
if
\mathtt{Detect}
returns
\mathit{false}
, then
v
resets

\mathtt{out}_{v}\leftarrow\bot

and turns on a flag denoted by

whose role is to prevent (the now

in both cases, round

The undecided nodes simulate the operation of
\mathtt{Phase}
.
To this end, we say that an undecided node

is

(in a phase) if

\mathit{step}_{u}\neq\mathit{\hbar}

and

.
Two engaged nodes

are said to be

\mathit{step}_{u}=\mathit{step}_{u^{\prime}}
.
Recalling that no adversarial manipulations occur after time

,
Obs.

u
and
u^{\prime}
are
phase-synchronized at time

t\geq t^{*}_{b}

with

\mathit{step}_{u,t}=\mathit{step}_{u^{\prime},t}=j

for

0\leq j\leq\phi-2
,
then
u
and
u^{\prime}
are
phase-synchronized at time

t+1

with

\mathit{step}_{u,t+1}=\mathit{step}_{u^{\prime},t+1}=j+1
.
By writing
\mathit{step}_{u}
into the
\mathtt{pps}
field of their outgoing messages,
the engaged node
u
can identify its phase-synchronized neighbors whenever

\mathit{step}_{u}>0
.
Recalling that step

j=0

of
\mathtt{Phase}
is the announcement step, in which the nodes do nothing
but sharing their output registers with their neighbors, this enables an
engaged node
u
to simulate an invocation of
\mathtt{Phase}
on the
subgraph induced by all nodes that are phase-synchronized with
u
.
Specifically, the invocation of
\mathtt{Phase}
runs for
\phi
consecutive
rounds, progressing as

\mathit{step}_{u}=0,1,\dots,\phi-1
.
The simulation of
\mathtt{Phase}
is implemented over the
\mathtt{phase}

fields of the messages.

Recall that the flag
wait_{v}
is turned on when a node

v\in V(G)

resets its output register.
Moreover, as long as

wait_{v}=\mathit{true}
,
node
v
is prevented from becoming engaged.
We design
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
so that
v
resets

wait_{v}\leftarrow\mathit{false}

when (and only when)

\mathit{step}_{v}=\mathit{\hbar}
.
This means that
v
may become engaged only after

\mathit{step}_{v}

moves from

\mathit{step}_{v}=\mathit{\hbar}

to

\mathit{step}_{v}=0

which is consistent with starting a phase from the beginning.

Consider a node-LCL

.

10.1

Consider a node

∎

Lemma 10.3

10.2
Fully Adaptive Run Time

Our goal in this section is to bound the fully adaptive run-time of

\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
, proving that in response to
k
node manipulations, the
algorithm stabilizes by time

t^{*}_{b}+T
,
where

\mathbb{E}(T)\,\leq\,O\left(\left(\phi^{5}/\beta\right)\cdot\left(\log(k)+(\nu+\phi)\log(\Delta)+\log(\sigma_{0}+\rho_{0})\right)\right)\,.

(8)

In passing, we also prove that the nodes whose distance from the manipulated
nodes is at least

\nu+\phi+1

retain their original output value (see
Cor.
10.8
), thus establishing
Thm.
8.3
.

To bound the stabilization time

of
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
, we define the
random variable

T_{1}\,=\,\min\{z\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\,:\,\pi(G,C_{t_{s}+z})=0\}\,\,.

Recalling that

t_{s}=t^{*}_{b}+\nu+\phi+2
,
we conclude that

,

.
We start with the following fundamental observation.

Observation
.

The potential function
\pi
satisfies

\pi(G,C_{t})\geq\pi(G,C_{t+1})

for every

t\geq t_{s}

Proof.

Follows from Lem.
10.1
by the
definition of a separable potential function.
∎

The following lemma bounds the number of undecided nodes at time

.

Lemma 10.4
.

The number of undecided nodes at time
t_{s}
satisfies

|U(C_{t_{s}})|\,\leq\,O\left(k\cdot\Delta^{\nu+\phi}\right)\,.

To bound

separable potential function and

\pi(G,C_{t_{s}})\,\leq\,O\left((\sigma_{0}+\rho_{0})\cdot k\cdot\Delta^{2(\nu+\phi)}\right)\,.

Lemma 10.5
.

Fix some time

and the global state at time

\mathbb{E}(\pi(G,C_{t+\tau+\phi}))\,\leq\,\left(1-\frac{\beta}{4\phi^{2}}\right)\cdot\pi(G,C_{t})\,.

Combining Lem.
10.5

with (
9

\mathbb{E}(T_{1})\,\leq\,O\left(\left(\phi^{5}/\beta\right)\cdot\left(\log(k)+(\nu+\phi)\log(\Delta)+\log(\sigma_{0}+\rho_{0})\right)\right)\,.

(10)

The bound in (
8

11
Simulating a Node-LCL Algorithm on the Line Graph

Simulating an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
for a distributed node problem on the line
graph
L(G)
of a graph
G
is a simple task in a fault free environment with
unique node IDs:
It suffices to appoint an endpoint

x(e)\in e

for each edge

e\in E(G)=V(L(G))

that takes on the responsibility for simulating the computation associated
with
e
throughout the execution (e.g., the endpoint with a smaller ID);
since

\delta_{G}(x(e),x(e^{\prime}))\leq 2

for every

e,e^{\prime}\in E(G)

such that

\{e,e^{\prime}\}\in E(L(G))
,
it suffices to simulate each round of
\mathcal{A}
on
L(G)
with
2
rounds in
G
,
e.g., allocating the even rounds for simulating the actions of
\mathcal{A}
on

L(G)
and the odd rounds for gathering the information from nodes that are

2
hops away.

Things become more involved when one wishes to simulate an anonymous
self-stabilizing algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
on
L(G)
.
First, we need to ensure that faulty nodes are safely detected.
Second, we no longer have a natural symmetry breaking rule that determines the
endpoint
x(e)
responsible for simulating the computation associated with an
edge

e\in E(G)
.
Third, in a self-stabilizing setting, the adversary can manipulate the nodes’
clocks, thus preventing them from partitioning the rounds into even and odd in
synchrony.

To overcome these difficulties, we shall design a self-stabilizing algorithm,
denoted by
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}^{\prime}
, that runs on
G
and simulates a run of

\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
on
L(G)
.
To this end, we exploit the structure of the self-stabilizing algorithms
developed in Sec.
9
, recalling
that
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
is composed of a detection procedure
\mathtt{Detect}
, a
phase procedure
\mathtt{Phase}
, and the
\mathtt{PPS}
module.
We design
\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}^{\prime}
by adapting each component of

\mathcal{A}_{\mathit{ST}}
separately, denoting the components that run on
G
and
simulate the operation of
\mathtt{Detect}
,
\mathtt{Phase}
, and
\mathtt{PPS}
on
L(G)

by
\mathtt{Detect}^{\prime}
,
\mathtt{Phase}^{\prime}
, and
\mathtt{PPS}^{\prime}
, respectively.

To simulate a run of
\mathtt{Detect}
on
L(G)
by the simulating procedure

\mathtt{Detect}^{\prime}
, consider an edge

e=\{u,v\}\in E(G)
.
The output register
\mathtt{out}_{e}
of
e
is represented under
\mathtt{Detect}^{\prime}
by the
two output registers
\mathtt{out}_{u}(v)
and
\mathtt{out}_{v}(u)
.
We augment the messages exchanged between
u
and
v
under
\mathtt{Detect}^{\prime}
with
the content of the simulated output register
\mathtt{out}_{e}
, thus allowing the
two nodes to verify that edge
e
is port-consistent.
Beyond that piece of information, nodes
u
and
v
simply share with each
other under
\mathtt{Detect}^{\prime}
the messages that
\mathtt{Detect}
sends when simulated on

L(G)
(typically, these messages simply contain the content of all their
output registers).
If a port-inconsistency is detected or if
\mathtt{Detect}
returns
\mathit{false}
, then
node
u
(resp.,
v
) resets

\mathtt{out}_{u}(v)\leftarrow\bot

(resp.,

\mathtt{out}_{v}(u)\leftarrow\bot
).

Next, we turn to explain how an invocation of the phase procedure

\mathtt{Phase}
on a subgraph of
L(G)
is simulated by procedure

\mathtt{Phase}^{\prime}
running on
G
.
Assuming that each phase of
\mathtt{Phase}
consists of
\phi
steps, a
phase of
\mathtt{Phase}^{\prime}
is stretch over

2\phi

steps.
Given an edge

e\in E(G)
,
module
\mathtt{PPS}^{\prime}
is responsible for (probabilistically) starting new phases of

\mathtt{Phase}^{\prime}
and for advancing the steps once a phase starts so that
both endpoints of
e
agree on the current step

j\in\{0,1,\dots,2\phi-1\}

of edge
e
.
Module
\mathtt{PPS}^{\prime}
is also responsible for breaking the symmetry between the
endpoints
x(e)
and
y(e)
of edge

e=\{x(e),y(e)\}

so that
x(e)
is responsible for simulating the execution of

\mathtt{Phase}
on
e
in the current phase and
y(e)
assists
x(e)
in
collecting information from nodes that are
2
hops away from
x(e)
.
Specifically, each step

j=0,1,\dots,\phi-1

of
\mathtt{Phase}
is simulated by steps

2j

and

2j+1

of
\mathtt{Phase}^{\prime}
, where in step

2j
,
node
y(e)
passes to
x(e)
the messages sent under
\mathtt{Phase}
from
edges

e^{\prime}\in E(G)

of the form

e^{\prime}=\{y(e)=y(e^{\prime}),x(e^{\prime})\}

to edge
e
, and in step

2j+1
,
node
x(e)
updates the registers of
\mathtt{Phase}
at
e
, maintained
under
\mathtt{Phase}^{\prime}
at
x(e)
.

The simulation of the decision step

j=\phi-1

of
\mathtt{Phase}
is slightly different, recalling that this is the only
step in which
\mathtt{Phase}
is allowed to write an output value

o\in\mathcal{O}

into the output register

\mathtt{out}_{e}
of (the undecided) edge

e\in E(G)
,
implemented under
\mathtt{Phase}^{\prime}
by writing
o
into the output registers

\mathtt{out}_{x(e)}(y(e))
and
\mathtt{out}_{y(e)}(x(e))
.
To ensure that the output value
o
is written into
\mathtt{out}_{x(e)}(y(e))
and

\mathtt{out}_{y(e)}(x(e))
concurrently, node
x(e)
employs step

2\phi-2

of
\mathtt{Phase}^{\prime}
to inform
y(e)
of
o
.
Then, in step

2\phi-1
,
serving as the decision step of
\mathtt{Phase}^{\prime}
,
nodes
x(e)
and
y(e)
set

\mathtt{out}_{x(e)}(y(e))\leftarrow o

and

\mathtt{out}_{y(e)}(x(e))\leftarrow o
.

The last action is conditioned on the output values of the decided edges

e^{\prime}\in N_{G}(e)

that are revealed to
x(e)
and
y(e)
at the beginning of step

2\phi-1
.
Here, we exploit the trivial (yet key) observation that if

e^{\prime}\in N_{G}(e)
,
then

|e^{\prime}\cap\{x(e),y(e)\}|=1
,
hence if
e^{\prime}
is already decided at the end of step

2\phi-2
,
then both
x(e)
and
y(e)
know about it at the beginning of step

2\phi-1
.

Finally, we explain how module
\mathtt{PPS}^{\prime}
simulates module
\mathtt{PPS}
(see
Sec.
5
) that runs in an
edge

e=\{u,v\}\in E(G)

and determines when a new phase of
\mathtt{Phase}^{\prime}
begins (as well as
advancing the steps within a phase).
Module
\mathtt{PPS}^{\prime}
is also responsible for breaking the symmetry between
u
and

v
, appointing one of them to
x(e)
and the other to
y(e)
, when a new
phase begins.

To implement module
\mathtt{PPS}^{\prime}
, nodes
u
and
v
maintain the register

\mathit{step}_{e}
in conjunction.
Specifically, node
u
(resp.,
v
) maintains a local copy of
\mathit{step}_{e}
,
denoted by
\mathit{step}_{u}(v)
(resp.,
\mathit{step}_{v}(u)
).
In each round, nodes
u
and
v
exchange the values of
\mathit{step}_{u}(v)
and

\mathit{step}_{v}(u)
;
if

\mathit{step}_{u}(v)\neq\mathit{step}_{v}(u)
,
then nodes
u
and
v
reset
\mathtt{PPS}^{\prime}
by setting

\mathit{step}_{u}(v)\leftarrow\mathit{\hbar}

and

\mathit{step}_{v}(u)\leftarrow\mathit{\hbar}
,
respectively.

Assuming that

\mathit{step}_{u}(v)=\mathit{step}_{v}(u)
,
nodes
u
and
v
update
\mathit{step}_{e}
in accordance with the policy of
\mathtt{PPS}

(see Sec.
5
).
If

\mathit{step}_{e}=j\in\{0,1,\dots,2\phi-1\}
,
then this update is deterministic, hence it can be performed by the two nodes
with no further interaction.
Otherwise
(
\mathit{step}_{e}=\mathit{\hbar}
),
nodes
u
and
v
toss an (unbiased) coin

to determine the next
state of
\mathit{step}_{e}
.
To this end, node
u
(resp.,
v
) tosses a coin denoted by

(resp.,

r_{v}(u)
) and shares its value with
v
(resp.,
u
).
The two nodes then set

and update
\mathit{step}_{e}
accordingly.

To assign the roles of
x(e)
and
y(e)
to the endpoints of edge
e
in a
given phase, we use a similar trick:
Whenever

\mathit{step}_{e}=\mathit{\hbar}
,
node
u
(resp.,
v
) tosses yet another (unbiased) coin, denoted by

\hat{r}_{u}(v)
(resp.,
\hat{r}_{v}(u)

v

(resp., (
u
).

advances in the subsequent round from

then edge

(resp.,

(resp.,

decreases by (no more than) a constant factor.
Recalling that a degree bound of
\Delta
for

for

, the following theorem is derived from

.

Consider a node-LCL

The eligible distributed algorithm

Let

Consider a node

, then

\mathtt{out}_{v}\leftarrow\mathit{OUT}

Otherwise, node

sets

of

Additional Related Work and Discussion

Transformers.

From the more theoretical point of view, a transformer is used to show the
reduction (or possibly the equivalence) of some source class of algorithms
(and/or problems) to some target class intended for problems that initially
seem “harder”. Some known such transformers in the context of distributed
computing are those that allow the execution of synchronous algorithms on
asynchronous networks and those that allow the use of algorithms for static
networks to be performed on one that may undergo (detectable) topological
changes
(
[
Awe85
,
AAG87
]
and many others). Transformers
can also ease the design of algorithms in the target class

[
Awe85
,
KP93
]
. When used in the context of faults
they may even reduce the amortized complexity per fault by avoiding
re-computation after fault
[
ACK90
]
.
Some of the transformers mentioned below are more general than the one
presented in the current paper in either transforming algorithms where nodes
may also start with some input other than the graph, or algorithms for non LCL
problems or
even algorithms that never terminate and interact with users outside the
network.

The first transformers, published as early as 1990

[
KP93
,
AKY90
]
, were rather inefficient. In
[
KP93
]
,
copies of the states of all the nodes were collected to some leader who
detected
illegal states and instructed the nodes to reset – restart from some legal
global state.
Some limitations were
(1)
assuming some incorruptible leader;
(2)
high overhead, including in time (that could be reduced to the diameter if the

\mathcal{LOCAL}

[
Pel00
]
model is assumed); and
(3)
assuming a directed spanning tree, otherwise, the reset could be invoked
many times
[
APSVD94
]
.

In
[
AKY97
]
, points (1) and (3) were addressed by introducing (an
inefficient, improved later) self stabilizing algorithm to elect a leader and
maintain a spanning tree. Point (2) was addressed by
suggesting the paradigm of local detection (also called local checking

[
APSV91
,
AV91
,
NS95
]
) that allowed some
nodes to detect incorrectness just by communicating with their neighbors,
rather than with the (possibly) remote leader.
It was shown that any problem could be revised to be locally checkable

[
AD02
,
KKP10
]
.

Transformers using these global reset protocols, e.g.,

[
AKM+93
,
APSVD94
]
, including on spanning tree, consume
time that is at least a diameter, since they reset the whole network to some
correct configuration. The “resynchronizer” of

[
AV91
]
, and the “superstabilizer”

[
DH95
,
DH97
]

are global in their stabilization time for a similar reason.
(The “superstabilizer” transformer may treat some faults faster in the
specific case that these faults are topological changes such that the endpoint
node of an inserted (or deleted) edge is notified by the environment about the
change.)

In
[
APSV91
]
, a class of distributed languages that are both
locally correctable and locally checkable is defined. Their local correction
algorithm nevertheless could suffer from at least a diameter time even for a
few faults.

The “rollback compiler” in
[
AV91
]

logs in each node, all the local history of the algorithm it transforms.
Moreover, each node exchanges its log with its neighbors in every round. That
way, the nodes can simulate the history repeatedly and verify that all the
actions were taken correctly, or correct them if they were not.
If the time complexity of a fault-free algorithm to be transformed is

t
, then the stabilization time of the transformed algorithm is also
t
.
Indeed they mention as applications local algorithms such as maximal
independent set and
\Delta^{2}
coloring.
However, this held only under the
\mathcal{LOCAL}
model, since their messages
were very
large. Moreover, this may not work well for randomized algorithms.
A more detailed description of how this transformer applies to local algorithms
appears in
[
LSW09
]
.

The above idea of exchanging logs was pushed further in

[
AD02
]
to not only exchange logs with neighbors, but also to
flood the network with the log of every node in every round of computation.
That allowed them
to address algorithms that never terminate and interact repeatedly with users
outside the system. Such interactive algorithms are adaptive in a different
sense than the one addressed in the current paper. That is, the stabilization
time may be proportional to the diameter.
However, the length of the duration of the interruption at each node is just
the diameter of the subgraph induced by the faulty nodes. It is plausible that
the kind of adaptivity addressed here can also be obtained. However, recall
that all this would hold
only under the
\mathcal{LOCAL}
model (because of the huge messages) and
possibly only for
deterministic algorithms. Another transformer for deterministic algorithms that
yielded time adaptive self stabilizing is
[
KPS97
]
. It also uses a
“brute force” method (and huge messages), thus time efficient only under the

\mathcal{LOCAL}
model.

Bounding the amount of memory used by transformers have been addressed
indirectly by addressing the memory requirements of the reset and the fault
detection module.
A bounded memory reset protocol was proposed in

[
KA98
]
, but only assuming a bounded number of
faults. Minimizing the memory used for fault detection has been the subject of
numerous studies, both in the general case and for specific tasks, see, e.g.,

[
BDDT98
,
KKP10
,
GS16
,
CHPP20
]
.

Locally Checkable Labelings.

The term LCL was coined in
[
NS95
]
. One of its motivations was the
above mentioned self stabilization transformers. Specifically, the checking of
local conditions and the detection of faults so that transformers could
reactivate the correction algorithm. Another motivations was to identify
algorithms where a fault
at a processor
p
could only affect processors in some bounded region around

p
. (The second motivation seems related to adaptivity, please see below).

Research on fault-free algorithms for LCL problems has been far too
extensive to survey here. Some very partial (and somewhat arbitrary) list
includes

[
KW85
,
II86
,
HKP01
,
BEPS16
,
Gha19
,
BBH+19
,
Suo20
,
GHK+20
,
CLP20
,
CP19
]
.
In the fault-free context, there has also been interest in automatic
conversion and generation of algorithms
[
Bra19
]
.

The transformer presented here addresses phase-based algorithms. We use the
fact that such algorithms are very common for LCLs, so the transformer
presented here is rather general. Moreover, even though every previous
algorithm was designed for some specific problem, many addressed issues of
phase synchronization under various environments that can be useful in
designing other phase-based algorithms
(e.g.,

[
ACS94
,
WW04
,
Tur19
]
).
The latter (
[
Tur19
]
), even designs two self-stabilizing
algorithms in

O(\log n)

time for the specific LCL problems of Maximal Matching and of
MIS by revising (manually) two fault-free and phase-base algorithms.
The algorithms presented in
[
Tur19
]
were not adaptive.
In is worth mentioning that the authors of
[
Tur19
]
stated that a
transformer for a wide class of phase-based algorithms is the ultimate goal.

The only other sublinear self stabilizing algorithms we are aware of for LCL
problems are the
O(\Delta+\log^{*}n)
stabilization time
(\Delta+1)
-node
coloring,
(2\Delta-1)
-edge-coloring, Maximal Independent
set and Maximal Matching of
[
BEG18
]
.
11
11

11

Many of the previous self-stabilizing algorithms were deterministic and
asynchronous.
Some also addressed related tasks that are not LCL.
A unique identity is
assumed and the algorithms are not shown to be adaptive.

Self-stabilizing LCL problems were extensively studied even before that.
In particular, there is a long line of works on distributed self-stabilizing
maximal matching, maximal independent set, and/or coloring algorithms, see
Table
1
in
[
ID19
]

and

[
HH92
,
Tel94
,
CLM+16
,

,
HJS01
,

,

,

,
XHGS03
,
CC13
,
HHJS03
,
HJS03
]
.
However, the stabilization time of all of them was
O(n)
(or more).

Adaptive Run-Time.

Adaptivity has gained a lot of interest outside distributed computing see,
e.g.,

[
Fre85
,
BGS15
,
BGS15
,
NS16
]
.
In the fault-free computing context, Lamport suggested that an
algorithm should benefit from the common case where the number of contending
processes is small
[
Lam87
]
.

In the context of fault tolerance, adaptivity in sublinear time was suggested
in
[
KP95b
]
for mending a maximal independent set that
was corrupted at some
k
locations.
The mending took
O(\log k)
time instead of
O(\log n)
for computing in
scratch.
That algorithm was not fully self-stabilizing.
Several other mending (or fixing) algorithms, again, not fully self
stabilizing, were suggested in
[
KW13
]
.
A graph theoretical definition of mendability was discussed in

[
BHM+21
]
and it was suggested that it can be
helpful for self stabilization in the
\mathcal{LOCAL}
model.

A self-stabilizing paradigm to handle a single fault, or multiple isolated
faults (“sufficiently far” from each other) was suggested in

[
GGHP96
,
GGHP07
]
.

Self-stabilizing algorithms whose time adaptivity was at least linear in the
number of faults for various tasks were suggested in

[
KP95a
,
KPS97
,
AKPS03
,
DANL04
,
BHKPS05
,
AZ06
,
KM07
]
.
We note that, while the above algorithms are adaptive since the time was
expressed as a function of the number
k
of faults, they are not always

fully
adaptive since all the faults are assumed to occur in a single
batch before the mending starts and no additional faults occur at least until
they are mended.
In contrast, the run-time analysis in the current paper is fully adaptive in
the sense that it allows the adversary to divide the
k
faults (and/or
topology changes) over multiple batches.

Adapting efficiently to detectable topological changes (that is, not self
stabilizing)
was addressed e.g., in
[
LPSR09
,
BKM18
,
CHDK+19
]
.
The algorithm in
[
BKM18
]
is adaptive (the time is not
expressed as a function of
k
) while in
[
CHDK+19
]
, the

amortized
(not the worst case) time complexity is optimized, assuming
that the graph is initially empty. Both papers rely on messages of
super-constant size.
