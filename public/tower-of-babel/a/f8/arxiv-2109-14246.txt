Localization for one-dimensional Anderson-Dirac models
Sylvain Zalczer

Abstract
. We prove spectral and dynamical localization for a one-dimensional Dirac operator to which is added an ergodic random potential, with a discussion on the different types of potential. We use scattering properties to prove the positivity of the Lyapunov exponent through Fürstenberg theorem. We get then the Hölder regularity of the integrated density of states through a new version of Thouless formula, and thus the Wegner estimate necessary for the multiscale analysis.

Primary 34L40;Secondary 81Q10, 37H15

1.
Introduction

The goal of this paper is to get localization for random Dirac operators in dimension 1, i.e. operators of the form

H_{\omega}=D_{0}+V_{per}+V_{\omega}\text{ on }L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{2}).

In this equation,
D_{0}=J\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}
, with
J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
, is the free Dirac operator.
The potential
V_{per}
is supposed to be 1-periodic and the random potential
V_{\omega}
is an Anderson ergodic potential:

V_{\omega}(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\lambda_{n}(\omega)u(x-n)

with random coupling constants
\lambda_{n}(\omega)
and an elementary potential
u
whose characteristics will be precised. As it is well-known, the Dirac operator has been defined to study relativistic matter
[
26
]
. On the other hand, the 2-dimensional Dirac operator can be used to model graphene
[
11
]
. It could thus be relevant to use, at least as a first step, a 1-dimensional Dirac operator to model a graphene nanoribbon.

Since the first works of Anderson
[
1
]
, random operators have been given a lot of interest. One-dimensional models, in particular, have been extensively studied
[
17
,
9
,
13
]
. Indeed, specific methods – involving Lyapunov exponents – have been developed for this setting. It has thus been possible to prove localization on the whole spectrum for these models, while in higher dimension results are known only for energies near band edges.

Most of these works are about random
Schrödinger
operators, i.e. with a kinetic energy given by a (discrete or continuous) Laplacian. Only few papers have addressed the question of localization for random
Dirac
operators. In
[
14
]
, Prado and de Oliveira consider a discrete Dirac operator with a random potential taking only
2 values. They prove, using a multiscale analysis, dynamical localization, on the whole spectrum for positive mass and outside a discrete critical set of energies for zero mass. In
[
21
]
, they give a deeper analysis of delocalization with the same kind of randomness, including the case of operators on the continuum. In
[
22
]
, together with Carvalho, they prove – among others – dynamical localization on the whole spectrum for a discrete model, in the case where the random potential has an absolutely continuous distribution. They use here a different method, called fractional moments method. In
[
3
]
, the author, together with Barbaroux and Cornean, proved localization near band edges in any dimension, in the case of absolutely continuous random variables. This paper has been followed by
[
28
]
, where Lipschitz continuity of the integrated density of states is proven.

The organization of the paper is the following. In section
2
, we present in detail the model and the results. In section
3
, we prove the positivity of the Lyapunov exponent of the group of transfer matrices, using properties of local uniqueness of Weyl-Titchmarsh functions. In section
4
, we get Hölder regularity for this exponent, using Grönwall-type estimates, and, in section
5
, we obtain the same result for the integrated density of states. This last point needs to prove a new Thouless formula, adapted to Dirac operators. Finally, in section
6
, we check all the hypotheses of the bootstrap multiscale analysis proposed by Germinet and Klein in
[
16
]
, which concludes the proof of our main result. In appendix
A
, we explain in detail the uniqueness properties for Weyl-Titchmarsh functions and, in appendix
B
, we prove that the density of states can be defined using Dirichlet boundary conditions.

2.
Model and results

As mentioned in the introduction, the operator studied in this paper will be

(2.1)

H_{\omega}=D_{0}+V_{per}+V_{\omega}\text{ on }L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{2}).

We recall that the free Dirac operator is
D_{0}=J\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}
, with
J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
.
We assume that
V_{per}
is a 1-periodic bounded function with values in the space of 2-by-2 real symmetric matrices.

In the context of Dirac operators, it is relevant to decompose the potentials according to the Pauli matrices:

\sigma_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix},\ \sigma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-{\rm i}\\
{\rm i}&0\end{pmatrix},\ \sigma_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

We write

V_{per}=v_{per}^{am}\sigma_{1}+v_{per}^{sc}\sigma_{3}+v_{per}^{el}I_{2},

where
v_{per}^{am}
is called
anomalous magnetic moment
,
v_{per}^{sc}
is called
scalar potential
and
v_{per}^{el}
is called
electrostatic potential
. Each of these functions is real-valued, measurable and bounded.
We do not include a potential multiple of
\sigma_{2}
, which would be called
magnetic moment
, since it can easily be proven that, if we denote by
D=D_{0}+V
a Dirac operator with a matrix-valued potential,
D+v_{mg}(x)\sigma_{2}
is unitarily equivalent to
D
(cf.
[
8
]
).

The random potential
V_{\omega}
is written as:

V_{\omega}(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\lambda_{n}(\omega)u(x-n).

The elementary potential
u
is supposed to have support in
[-1/2,1/2]
. It can be decomposed just as the periodic potential. Nevertheless, it is not possible to prove localization for all configurations. In particular, for any potential
V_{el}\in L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R})
, the operator
D_{0}+V_{el}I_{2}
is unitarily equivalent to
D_{0}
(this is the well-known Klein’s paradox). We will consider the two following cases:

(1)

u(x)=q_{am}(x)\sigma_{1}+q_{sc}(x)\sigma_{3},

(2)

u(x)=q_{el}(x)I_{2}
and
\mathrm{supp}\penalty\ q_{el}\cap\mathrm{supp}\penalty\ [(v_{per}^{am})^{2}+(v_{per}^{sc})^{2}]
has positive measure.

In both cases, the functions
q_{am}
,
q_{sc}
and
q_{el}
are measurable, bounded and real-valued. The way we deal with these different potentials is explained in Appendix
A
.

The coupling contants
\lambda_{n}
are independent, identically distributed real random variables on a complete probability space
\Omega
. We assume that their common distribution is bounded and non-trivial, in the sense that their support contains at least two points.
Note that, under these conditions, the operator has real coefficients.
With standard methods, we see that
H_{\omega}
is self-adjoint, with domain
H^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{2})
.

Under these conditions, the family
(H_{\omega})_{\omega\in\Omega}
is an ergodic random operator. As it is known from Pastur’s theorem (see for example
[
19
, Theorem 4.3]
), the spectrum of
H_{\omega}
is almost surely constant:
there exists
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}
such that
\sigma(H_{\omega})=\Sigma
with probability 1.
Similarly, the pure point, singular continuous and absolutely continuous parts of the spectrum are almost surely constant.

We want to prove Anderson localization for this operator.
Our first result will be the following.

Theorem 2.1

(Exponential localization)
.

With probability 1, the operator
H_{\omega}
has pure point spectrum, in the sense that it has no continuous spectrum. Moreover, all its eigenfunctions are exponentially decaying at
\pm\infty
.

We will be able to prove a stronger property, called
dynamical localization
.

Theorem 2.2

(Dynamical localization)
.

There exists a discret set
M\subset\mathbb{R}
such that, for every compact interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}\backslash M
, every compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}
, and every
p>0
,

(2.2)

{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sup_{t>0}\||X|^{p}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}tH_{\omega}}P_{I}(H_{\omega})\chi_{K}\|\right)<+\infty,

where
P_{I}
is the spectral projection on
I
.

The existence of a set
M
of critical energies for which it exists (dynamically) delocalized states is a well-known fact in one-dimensional disordered systems (cf.
[
13
]
and references therein). In the case of Dirac operators, quasi-ballistic behaviour at critical energies has been studied in
[
21
]
.
The critical set does not appear in Theorem
2.1
since it is discrete, and a discrete set cannot support continuous spectrum.

We will follow the classic methods of proof of localization for one-dimensional systems, as they are used for example in
[
13
]
for Schrödinger operators. The fundamental fact is that a non-zero potential induces a nontrivial scattering. Henceforth, we will get the hypotheses of Fürstenberg’s theorem which will give us the positivity of the Lyapunov exponent of the group of transfer matrices, which is called Fürstenberg’s group. With the help of Grönwall-type estimates, we will be able to get the Hölder continuity of this exponent.

The next step is to get the same regularity on the integrated density of states. In
[
28
]
, we proved that it was possible the define the density of states measure for random Dirac operators by, given a bounded measurable function
\phi
on
\mathbb{R}
,

(2.3)

\nu(\phi)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L}\mathrm{tr}(\chi_{L}\phi(H_{\omega})\chi_{L}),

where
\chi_{L}
is the characteristic function of
[-L/2,L/2]
(the result exists in any dimension, but we give here only the one-dimensional version).
By extension, given a Borel set
B
, we define the density of states measure by
\nu(B)=\nu(\chi_{B})
, where
\chi_{B}
is the characteristic function of
B
.
Since Dirac operators are not bounded from below, we cannot define the integrated density of states in the usual way as
N(E)=\nu((-\infty,E])
. Nevertheless, we prove a result of regularity.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
M
be the discrete set already mentioned in Theorem
2.2
and fix a compact interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}\backslash M
.
There exist
\alpha>0
and
C<\infty
such that, for all
E<E^{\prime}\in I
,

(2.4)

\nu([E,E^{\prime}])\leqslant C(E^{\prime}-E)^{\alpha}.

The relation between the Lyapunov exponent and the density of states is done through a result called Thouless formula. Note that, because of the asymptotic behaviour of the integrated density of states, we need to prove a new Thouless formula adapted to Dirac operators.

While in
[
28
]
we get such a regularity in a spectral gap of the unperturbed operator from a Wegner estimate previously proven in
[
3
]
to get Anderson localization, here, we use the regularity of the density of states to get the Wegner estimate and, then, Anderson localization on the whole real line. Our last section is devoted to the proof of the conditions to apply the multiscale analysis published by Germinet and Klein in
[
16
]
.

3.
Positivity of the Lyapunov exponent

3.1.
Floquet theory

Our preliminary step will be to study Floquet theory for the periodic operator
H_{0}=D_{0}+V_{\text{per}}
. We define some remarkable elements which will be used in the following, as they are introduced for example in the first two chapters of
[
15
]
. In that book, the considered system is a second-order scalar differential equation. Although our setting is different, we can work similarly since the quantity of interest in the second-order case is the vector
\begin{pmatrix}u\\
u^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}
. In our case, the function
u
is already a vector. As a consequence, we will be able to use similar techniques. In particular, by analogy, we will call
Dirichlet boundary conditions
on an interval
(a,b)
(for a solution
\begin{pmatrix}u^{\uparrow}\\
u^{\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}
) the condition
u^{\uparrow}(a)=u^{\uparrow}(b)=0
.

For
z\in\mathbb{C}
, we consider the equation

(3.1)

D_{0}u+V_{\text{per}}u=zu.

By analogy with the second-order case, we denote by
u_{N}(\cdot,z)
and
u_{D}(\cdot,z)
the solutions to (
3.1
) satisfying
u_{N}(-1/2,z)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}
and
u_{D}(-1/2,z)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}
.

We denote by
g_{0}(z)
the transfer matrix between -1/2 and 1/2, defined as the 2-by-2 matrix of which the columns are
u_{N}(1/2,z)
and
u_{D}(1/2,z)
. We see that this matrix is entire in
z
. Its eigenvalues satisfy the characteristic equation

(3.2)

\rho^{2}-D(z)\rho+1=0,

where
D(z)=\mathrm{tr}g_{0}(z)
.
We can then define 2 algebraic functions
\rho_{\pm}
(with singularities at points where
D(z)=\pm 2
) as the roots of this equation.

Let us consider a
stability interval
, i.e. a maximal real interval
(E_{-},E_{+})
such that
|D(E)|<2
for all
E\in(E_{-},E_{+})
. Since the equation has real coefficients, both
u_{N}(\cdot,E)
and
u_{D}(\cdot,E)
are real-valued for
E\in\mathbb{R}
and thus, for
E\in(E_{-},E_{+})
, one has that

(3.3)

\rho_{\pm}(E)=\frac{1}{2}(D(E)\pm{\rm i}\sqrt{4-D(E)^{2}}),

with
|\rho_{\pm}(E)|=1
and
\rho_{-}(E)=\overline{\rho_{+}(E)}
.

Let us now consider the strip

S=\{z=E+{\rm i}\eta,E\in(E_{-},E_{+}),\eta\in\mathbb{R}\}.

According to Theorem 3.1.1 of
[
15
]
, the eigenvalues of the problem with Dirichlet boundary conditions are not in the stability intervals.
Thus, for
z\in S
, we can define
v_{\pm}(z)
as the eigenvectors of
g_{0}(z)
corresponding to the eigenvalues
\rho_{\pm}(z)
with the first component normalized to one:

(3.4)

v_{\pm}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
c_{\pm}(z)\end{pmatrix}.

An explicit calculation gives that

c_{\pm}(z)=\frac{\rho_{\pm}(z)-u_{N}(1/2,z)}{u_{D}(1/2,z)},

where we know that
u_{D}(1/2,z)\neq 0
in
S
since the Dirichlet eigenvalues are real and outside the stability intervals. As a consequence,
c_{\pm}
and then
v_{\pm}
are analytic in
S
with at most algebraic singularities at
E_{-}
and
E_{+}
.

We will denote by
\phi_{\pm}
the
Floquet solutions
, i.e. the solutions satisfying

(3.5)

\phi_{\pm}(-1/2,z)=v_{\pm}(z)\penalty\ ;

these solutions are such that

\phi_{\pm}(x+1,z)=\rho_{\pm}(z)\phi_{\pm}(x,z)

for all
x
in
\mathbb{R}
. Since
\rho_{+}(z)\neq\rho_{-}(z)
on
S
, these two functions are a fundamental system of solutions to (
3.1
).
Moreover, for fixed
x
, the function
\phi_{\pm}(x,\cdot)
are both analytic in
S
, with at most algebraic singularities at
E_{-}
and
E_{+}
.

Last, we see that, if
\eta>0
,
\phi_{\pm}(\cdot,E+{\rm i}\eta)
decays exponentially near
\pm\infty
and if
\eta<0
,
\phi_{\pm}(\cdot,E+{\rm i}\eta)
decays exponentially near
\mp\infty
.
But we know, from the corollary given just after Theorem 6.8 in
[
27
]
, that, for any potential, Dirac operator is in the so-called “limit point case” at
\pm\infty
, which means that, for any
z\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\mathbb{R}
, there exists a unique (up to multiplication) solution to
(
3.1
)
, called
Weyl solution
, which is in
L^{2}
in a neighbourhood of
\pm\infty
. Here, we see that the Floquet solutions
\phi_{\pm}(\cdot,z)
are the Weyl solutions.

3.2.
Scattering

Let us now add a compactly supported potential
f\in L^{1}_{loc}
, such that
f\neq 0
. The goal of this paragraph is to prove that “the perturbation affects the wavefunction”, except maybe for a discrete set of energies. We assume that
f
satisfies the conditions for the elementary perturbation, which means that
\mathrm{supp}(f)\subset[-1/2,1/2]
and that we are in one of the following cases:

(1)

u(x)=q_{am}(x)\sigma_{1}+q_{sc}(x)\sigma_{3},

(2)

u(x)=q_{el}(x)I_{2}
and
\mathrm{supp}q_{el}\cap\mathrm{supp}(v_{per}^{am})^{2}+(v_{per}^{sc})^{2}
has positive measure.

For
\lambda
in the support of the coupling constants
\lambda(\omega)
, we consider the operator

H_{\lambda}=D_{0}+V_{per}+\lambda f

on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{2})
.
For
z\in S
, we define
u_{+}(\cdot,z)
as the unique solution to

(3.6)

H_{\lambda}u_{+}(\cdot,z)=zu_{+}(\cdot,z)

which coincides with
\phi_{+}
on
(-\infty,-1/2)
. Since the two functions
\phi_{\pm}(\cdot,z)
are a basis of the space of solutions to the unperturbed equation, we have that there exist some constants
a(z)
and
b(z)
such that, for
x\geqslant 1/2
,

(3.7)

u_{+}(x,z)=a(z)\phi_{+}(x,z)+b(z)\phi_{-}(x,z).

From the definition of the Floquet solutions and the fact that, for
E\in(E_{-},E_{+})
,
\rho_{+}(E)=\overline{\rho_{-}(E)}
, we get that
\phi_{-}(x,E)=\overline{\phi_{+}(x,E)}
. Thus, if we denote by
u_{-}
the solution to (
3.6
) which coincides with
\phi_{-}
on
(-\infty,-1/2)
, we have that, for
x\geqslant 1/2
and
E\in(E_{-},E_{+})
,

(3.8)

u_{-}(x,E)=\overline{a(E)}\phi_{-}(x,E)+\overline{b(E)}\phi_{-}(x,E).

The constancy of the Wronskian gives that

(3.9)

|a(E)|^{2}-|b(E)|^{2}=1.

We have the following important result about the coefficients
a
and
b
, the proof of which is similar to the one of Proposition 2.1 of
[
13
]
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The functions
a(\cdot)
and
b(\cdot)
, defined on
S
as above, are branches of multi-valued analytic functions with at most algebraic singularities at boundaries of stability intervals.

Our key lemma is the following; it states that, as soon as the perturbation is not identically 0, the reflection coefficient
b
cannot be identically 0 on a stability interval.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
b(E)=0
for all
E\in(E_{-},E_{+})
, then
\lambda=0
is identically 0.

Proof.

If we assume that
b(E)=0
for all
E\in(E_{-},E_{+})
, then, by analyticity, it is true for all
z\in S
. Then, for all
E\in(E_{-},E_{+})
and
\eta>0
,
u_{+}(\cdot,E+{\rm i}\eta)=a(E+{\rm i}\eta)\phi_{+}(\cdot,E+{\rm i}\eta)
is exponentially decaying at
+\infty
: it is thus the Weyl solution for the perturbed equation. The Weyl-Titchmarsh function at point -1/2 for the half-line
(-1/2,+\infty)
(see appendix) is then

m_{V_{per}+\lambda f}(E+{\rm i}\eta)=\frac{u_{+}^{\downarrow}(-1/2,E+{\rm i}\eta)}{u_{+}^{\uparrow}(-1/2,E+{\rm i}\eta)}=\frac{\phi_{+}^{\downarrow}(-1/2,E+{\rm i}\eta)}{\phi_{+}^{\uparrow}(-1/2,E+{\rm i}\eta)}=m_{V_{per}}(E+{\rm i}\eta).

Since both Weyl-Titchmarsh functions coincide in the upper part of
S
and are both analytic on
\mathbb{C}^{+}
, they coincide on
\mathbb{C}^{+}
.
Then, by appendix
A
, we get that
\lambda=0

∎

As a consequence, since we have assumed that
f
is not identically 0 and we know that
b
is analytic, we have that the set
\{E\in(E_{-},E_{+}):b(E)=0\}
is discrete.

Let us now consider a gap: let
\alpha<E_{-}<E_{+}
such that
(\alpha,E_{-})
is a maximal, non-trivial gap in the spectrum of
H_{0}
. Note that we always have
\alpha>-\infty
since the perturbation is bounded (cf.
[
7
]
, remark after Proposition 5.2). We consider a strip from which we have removed the spectrum:

S^{\prime}=\{z=E+{\rm i}\eta,\alpha<E<E_{+},\eta\in\mathbb{R}\}\backslash[E_{-},E_{+}).

Let
\rho_{i}(z)
(
i=1,2
) be the branches of (
3.2
), such that
|\rho_{1}(z)|<1
and
|\rho_{2}(z)|>1
in
S^{\prime}
. As before, these two functions are analytic in
S^{\prime}
with at worst algebraic singularities at
\alpha
,
E_{-}
and
E_{+}
. Moreover,
\rho_{1}=\rho_{+}
in the upper part of
S
and
\rho_{1}=\rho_{-}
in its lower part. For
z\in S^{\prime}
and
i=1,2
, we will denote by
v_{i}(z)
the eigenvector of
g_{0}(z)
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\rho_{i}(z)
; the
v_{i}
’s are analytic functions on
S^{\prime}
. We denote by
\phi_{i}(\cdot,z)
the solution to (
3.1
) satisfying
\phi_{i}(-1/2,z)=v_{i}(z)
. We define in this case
u_{i}(\cdot,z)
the solution to (
3.6
) which coincides with
\phi_{i}(\cdot,z)
on
(-\infty,-1/2)
. We have then that, for
x>1/2
,
u_{i}(x,z)=a_{i}(z)\phi_{i}(x,z)+b_{i}(z)\phi_{j}(x,z)
(
i\neq j
), where, for each of both
i
’s, the two functions
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
are analytic in
S^{\prime}
. Moreover, we see that, in the upper part (resp. lower part) of
S
,
a_{1}
and
b_{1}
(resp.
a_{2}
and
b_{2}
) coincide with the functions
a
and
b
defined in the first case. Hence, they are not identically zero: the set

\{E\in(\alpha,b):a_{1}(E)b_{1}(E)a_{2}(E)b_{2}(E)=0\}

is discrete.

3.3.
Transfer matrices and Lyapunov exponent

One of our main tool to get information on the nature of the spectrum of
H_{\omega}
is the Lyapunov exponent. For
E\in\mathbb{C}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\omega\in\Omega
, we denote by
g_{E}(n,\omega)
the transfer matrix from
n-1/2
to
n+1/2
for
H_{\omega}
, i.e. the matrix such that, for all
u
such that
H_{\omega}u=Eu
, we have

(3.10)

u(n+1/2)=g_{E}(n,\omega)u(n-1/2).

We define to the transfer matrix from
1/2
to
n+1/2
:

U_{E}(n,\omega)=g_{E}(n,\omega)...g_{E}(1,\omega).

We define the Lyapunov exponent by

(3.11)

\gamma(E)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}{\mathbb{E}}(\|U_{E}(n,\omega)\|).

The existence of the limit comes from the subadditive ergodic theorem (see for example
[
10
, Theorem IV.1.2]
).

Our goal here is to prove that
\gamma(E)>0
for all
E\in\mathbb{R}
, with the possible exception of a discrete set. To this purpose, we will use the theorem of Fürstenberg, of which we recall the statement.
We naturally define the action of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
on the projective space
P(\mathbb{R}^{2})
from a quotient of the usual action on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Theorem 3.3

(
[
4
]
, Theorem II.4.1 and Proposition II.4.3)
.

Let
(A_{\omega}^{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of random variables with values in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, with common distribution
\mu
. Let
G_{\mu}
be the smallest closed subgroup of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
containing the support of
\mu
. Let us assume that

(1)

G_{\mu}
is not compact.

(2)

\forall v\in P(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
\#\{gv:g\in G_{\mu}\}\geqslant 3
.

Then,
there exists a Lyapunov exponent
\gamma>0
such that, almost surely

(3.12)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log(\|A_{\omega}^{n}...A_{\omega}^{1}\|)=\gamma.

We will apply this theorem to the group spanned by transfer matrices, which will be called
Fürstenberg’s group
. More precisely, we will take, for all
E\in\mathbb{R}
:

(3.13)

G_{\mu}(E)=\overline{\langle\{g_{E}^{0}(\omega):\omega\in\Omega\}\rangle}.

We do not write the parameter
n
since the matrices are identically distributed.
The constancy of the Wronskian implies that
G_{\mu}
is included in
SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Note that the hypotheses of Theorem
3.3
are preserved if we take a bigger group: we can assume that
\mathrm{supp}\penalty\ \mu
has only two elements. We can assume, without loss of generality, that these two elements are 0 and 1 since, if they are 2 real numbers
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
, we can replace
V_{per}
by
V_{per}+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}q_{1}u(\cdot-n)
and
u
by
(q_{2}-q_{1})u
.

Proposition 3.4
.

There exists a discrete set
M\subset\mathbb{R}
such that, for all
E\in\mathbb{R}\backslash M
,
G_{\mu}(E)
satisfies the hypotheses of
3.3
. As a consequence,
\gamma(E)>0
for all
E\in\mathbb{R}\backslash M
.

We will treat separately the case where
E
is in a stability interval of
H_{0}
and the one where
E
is in a spectral gap of
H_{0}
.

Let us start by the case where
E
is in a stability interval. We have the following lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
E
be in a stability interval
(E_{-},E_{+})
of
H_{0}
. Then,

(3.14)

g_{1}(E)=C(E)\tilde{g}_{0}(E)s(E)C(E)^{-1}

where

(3.15)

C(E)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\mathrm{Re}[c(E)]&\mathrm{Im}[c(E)]\end{pmatrix}\text{ ; }\tilde{g}_{0}(E)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Re}[\rho(E)]&\mathrm{Im}[\rho(E)]\\
-\mathrm{Im}[\rho(E)]&\mathrm{Re}[\rho(E)]\end{pmatrix}

(3.16)

s(E)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Re}[a(E)+b(E)]&\mathrm{Im}[a(E)+b(E)]\\
-\mathrm{Im}[a(E)-b(E)]&\mathrm{Re}[a(E)-b(E)]\end{pmatrix}

with
c(E)=c_{+}(E)=\overline{c_{-}(E)}
,
\rho(E)=\rho_{+}(E)=\overline{\rho_{-}(E)}
,
a(E)
and
b(E)
are respectively defined by (
3.4
), (
3.3
) and (
3.7
).

Proof.

The proof is similar to the one of Lemma 2.4 of
[
13
]
and is a very simple matrix calculation. We omit the dependence in
E
to alleviate notations. First, expressing the solutions
u_{N/D}
in terms of the Weyl solutions

gives

g_{1}(E)=\begin{pmatrix}u_{+}^{\uparrow}(1/2)&u_{-}^{\uparrow}(1/2)\\
u_{+}^{\downarrow}(1/2)&u_{-}^{\downarrow}(1/2)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
c&\overline{c}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}.

Then, by (
3.7
) and (
3.8
),

\begin{pmatrix}u_{+}^{\uparrow}(1/2)&u_{-}^{\uparrow}(1/2)\\
u_{+}^{\downarrow}(1/2)&u_{-}^{\downarrow}(1/2)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{+}^{\uparrow}(1/2)&\phi_{-}^{\uparrow}(1/2)\\
\phi_{+}^{\downarrow}(1/2)&\phi_{-}^{\downarrow}(1/2)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&\overline{b}\\
b&\overline{a}\end{pmatrix}.

Finally, the definition of the Floquet solutions gives us that

\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{+}^{\uparrow}(1/2)&\phi_{-}^{\uparrow}(1/2)\\
\phi_{+}^{\downarrow}(1/2)&\phi_{-}^{\downarrow}(1/2)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
c&\overline{c}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\rho&0\\
0&\overline{\rho}\end{pmatrix}.

Putting all these equalities together, we get the result.
∎

The matrix
g_{0}
corresponding to trivial scattering (i.e. the case
a=1
,
b=0
), we have

(3.17)

g_{0}(E)=C(E)\tilde{g}_{0}(E)C(E)^{-1}.

We will consider the group
\tilde{G}(E)
defined as the subgroup of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
generated by
\tilde{g}_{0}(E)
and
s(E)
. It is easy to see that it is conjugate to
G(E)
, and thus that
G(E)
satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
3.3
if and only if
\tilde{G}(E)
satisfies them.

Given a stability interval
(E_{-},E_{+})
, let
\tilde{M}_{(E_{-},E_{+})}=\{E\in(E_{-},E_{+}):b(E)=0\}
and choose
E\in(E_{-},E_{+})\backslash\tilde{M}_{(E_{-},E_{+})}
. We set
a(E)=A{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\alpha}
and
b(E)=B{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\beta}
, with
A
and
B
real. We have thus

(3.18)

s(E)=A\begin{pmatrix}\cos\alpha&\sin\alpha\\
-\sin\alpha&\cos\alpha\end{pmatrix}+B\begin{pmatrix}\cos\beta&\sin\beta\\
\sin\beta&-\cos\beta\end{pmatrix}.

In order to prove that
G(E)
is not compact, we will exhibit a sequence of elements with an unbounded norm. An arbitrary element of
P(\mathbb{R}^{2})
can be represented by
v(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\\
\sin\theta\end{pmatrix}
, for some
\theta\in[0,\pi)
. We find then that

s(E)v(\theta)=Av(\theta-\alpha)+Bv(\beta-\theta).

In particular, if we take
\theta^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+\beta)
, we have that

s(E)v(\theta^{\prime})=(A+B)v((\beta-\alpha)/2).

Let us now consider the squared norm
R(\theta)=\|s(E)v(\theta)\|^{2}
. Using the fact that
A^{2}-B^{2}=1
(cf. (
3.9
)), we find that

(3.19)

R(\theta)-1=2B(B+A\cos(\alpha+\beta-2\theta)).

We have thus that
R(\theta)>1
if and only if
\cos(\alpha+\beta-2\theta)>-\frac{B}{A}
, which happens on an interval of measure higher than
\frac{\pi}{2}
.
Thus, there exists a compact interval
K
with measure larger than
\frac{\pi}{2}
such that
\|s(E)v(\theta)\|>c>1
for all
\theta
in
K
. In particular,
s(E)
applied to
v(\theta^{\prime})
produces a vector
v^{\prime}
with norm larger than one.

Concerning
g_{0}(E)
, as
E
is in a stability interval,
|\rho(E)|=1
and thus
g_{0}(E)
is a rotation. The angle of this rotation can be taken in
[-\pi/2,\pi/2)
and is not 0 since
\rho\neq\pm 1
.

The sequence is constructed in the following way.

(1)

Choose a
v(\theta_{0})
in
K
.

(2)

Apply
s(E)
to get a new vector
w
with larger norm.

(3)

If
\overline{w}
, the class of
w
in
P(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, is in
K
, then do (2) again.

(4)

If
\overline{w}\notin K
, then apply
g_{0}(E)
enough times to get a vector in
K
. It can be reached in a finite number of iterations since
g_{0}(E)
has an angle in
[-\pi/2,\pi/2)
and
K
is an interval of length larger than
\pi/2
. Then, apply (2) again.

To conclude the proof in the stability intervals, we will pick

M_{(E_{-},E_{+})}=\{E\in(E_{-},E_{+}):E\in\tilde{M}_{(E_{-},E_{+})}\text{ or }D(E)=0\}.

We clearly have that
M_{(E_{-},E_{+})}
is finite and, by the above,
\tilde{G}(E)
is not compact when we take
E\in(E_{-},E_{+})\backslash M_{(E_{-},E_{+})}
. Moreover, when
D(E)\neq 0
, the matrix
\tilde{g}_{0}(E)
is a rotation with an angle different from
\frac{\pi}{2}
.
Therefore, powers of this matrix produce at least three distinct elements in the projective space.

Let us now consider the case where
E
is in a spectral gap of
H_{0}
, which will be denoted
(E^{\prime},E_{-})
. Since in such an interval, we have
|D(E)|>2
,
g_{0}(E)
always have an eigenvalue with modulus larger than one. Hence, repeated iterations of
g_{0}(E)
on the associated eigenvector will produce an unbounded sequence of elements of
G(E)
, showing that this group is not compact.

To prove that condition (2) of Theorem
3.3
is satisfied in
(E^{\prime},E_{-})
(outside a discrete interval), let us introduce the set

M_{(E^{\prime},E_{-})}=\{E\in(E^{\prime},E_{-}):a_{1}(E)b_{1}(E)a_{2}(E)b_{2}(E)=0\},

which is discrete as we have seen in the last subsection, and take
E
in
(E^{\prime},E_{-})\backslash M_{(E^{\prime},E_{-})}
.

Let us denote by
\bar{v}_{1}
and
\bar{v}_{2}
the directions of the eigenvectors of
g_{0}(E)
. Since we have chosen
E
such that
a_{i}(E)
and
b_{i}(E)\neq 0
, we have that

s(E)\bar{v}_{i}\notin\{\bar{v}_{1},\bar{v}_{2}\}

for
i=1,2
.
Thus, if
\bar{v}\notin\{\bar{v}_{1},\bar{v}_{2}\}
, then
\#\{g_{0}(E)^{n}\bar{v},n\in\mathbb{Z}\}=\infty
. If
\bar{v}
is one the the eigenvectors, then an application of

followed by iterations of

gives an infinite orbit.
This proves the second condition for spectral gaps.

Finally, we conclude the proof by taking
M
as the union of, first, the
M_{(E_{-},E_{+})}
for all the stability intervals, second, all the
M_{(E^{\prime},E_{-})}
for all the maximal spectral gaps, and, third, all the endpoints of the stability intervals.

4.
Hölder regularity of the Lyapunov exponent

In this section, we will prove the Hölder regularity of the Lyapunov exponent on compact intervals. Fix
I
a compact interval of
\mathbb{R}\backslash M
, where
M
is the discrete set introduced in the previous section.

Proposition 4.1
.

The Lyapunov exponent defined by (
3.11
) is uniformly Hölder continuous on
I
, i.e. there exist
\alpha>0
and
C
such that, for all
E
,
E^{\prime}\in I
,

|\gamma(E)-\gamma(E^{\prime})|\leqslant C|E-E^{\prime}|^{\alpha}.

The proof will use the following theorem, whose statement has been written (in a more general setting) by Boumaza.

Theorem 4.2

(
[
5
]
, Theorem 1)
.

Let
(A_{\omega}^{n}(E))
a sequence of i.i.d. random matrices of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
depending on a real parameter
E
. Let
\mu_{E}
be the common distribution of the
(A_{\omega}^{n}(E))
,
G_{\mu_{E}}
the smallest closed subgroup of
SL_{2}(\mathbb{R})
containing the support of
\mu_{E}
and
\gamma(E)
the associated Lyapunov exponent. We fix a compact interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
and we assume that for all
E\in I
we have:

(1)

G_{\mu_{E}}
is non-compact and strongly irreducible.

(2)

There exist
C_{1}>0
,
C_{2}>0
, independent of
n
,
\omega
and
E
such that

(4.1)

\|A_{\omega}^{n}(E)\|^{2}\leqslant\exp(C_{1}+|E|+1)\leqslant C_{2}.

(3)

There exists
C_{3}>0
, independent of
n
,
\omega
and
E
such that for any
E
,
E^{\prime}\in I
:

(4.2)

\|A_{\omega}^{n}(E)-A_{\omega}^{n}(E^{\prime})\|\leqslant C_{3}|E-E^{\prime}|.

Then, there exist two real numbers
\alpha>0
and
C>0
such that

(4.3)

\forall E,E^{\prime}\in I;\quad|\gamma(E)-\gamma(E^{\prime})|\leqslant C|E-E^{\prime}|^{\alpha}.

We apply this theorem for
A_{\omega}^{n}(E)=g_{\omega}^{n}(E)
, the transfer matrices defined in the previous section.
This will directly give Proposition
4.1
; we only need to check the hypotheses of the theorem.
The first one has already been proven in the previous section.

Let us now prove (
4.1
).
We have the following lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{\uparrow}\\
\psi_{\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}
be a solution to
D_{0}\psi+V\psi=0
, with
V=q_{am}\sigma_{1}+q_{sc}\sigma_{3}+q_{el}I_{2}\in L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R},\mathcal{M}_{2}(\mathbb{R}))
. Then, for all
x
,
y\in\mathbb{R}
,

(4.4)

|\psi_{\uparrow}(x)|^{2}+|\psi_{\downarrow}(x)|^{2}\leqslant(|\psi_{\uparrow}(y)|^{2}+|\psi_{\downarrow}(y)|^{2})\exp\left(2\int_{\min(x,y)}^{\max(x,y)}|q_{am}(t)|+|q_{sc}(t)|{\rm d}t\right).

Proof.

If we write in coordinates the equation satisfied by
\psi
, we find:

-\psi_{\downarrow}^{\prime}+(q_{sc}+q_{el})\psi_{\uparrow}+q_{am}\psi_{\downarrow}=0

and

\psi_{\uparrow}^{\prime}+q_{am}\psi_{\uparrow}+(q_{el}-q_{sc})\psi_{\downarrow}=0.

We have then, denoting
R(t)=|\psi_{\uparrow}(t)|^{2}+|\psi_{\downarrow}(t)|^{2}
, for almost every
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle R^{\prime}(t)=

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{Re}(\psi_{\uparrow}^{\prime}\overline{\psi_{\uparrow}}+\psi_{\downarrow}^{\prime}\overline{\psi_{\downarrow}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{Re}(-q_{am}|\psi_{\uparrow}|^{2}-(q_{el}-q_{sc})\psi_{\downarrow}\overline{\psi_{\uparrow}}+(q_{sc}+q_{el})\psi_{\uparrow}\overline{\psi_{\downarrow}}+q_{am}|\psi_{\downarrow}|^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{Re}(q_{am}(|\psi_{\downarrow}|^{2}-|\psi_{\uparrow}|^{2})+2q_{sc}\psi_{\uparrow}\overline{\psi_{\downarrow}}).

Hence, we have
|R^{\prime}(t)|\leqslant 2(|q_{am}(t)|+|q_{sc}(t)|)|R(t)|
and we conclude with Grönwall’s lemma.
∎

We immediately get (
4.1
), taking
V=V_{per}+V_{\omega}-EI_{2}
; we even have something independent of
E
.

Let us now turn to the proof of (
4.2
). We use the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\psi_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1,\uparrow}\\
\psi_{1,\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}
and
\psi_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{2,\uparrow}\\
\psi_{2,\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}
be solutions to
D_{0}\psi_{i}+V_{i}\psi_{i}=0
, where, for
i=1,2
,
V_{i}
is a
L_{loc}^{1}
function with values in 2-by-2 matrices, and such that, for some
y
,
\psi_{1}(y)=\psi_{2}(y)
. Then, we have, for all
x
in
\mathbb{R}
,

(4.5)

|\psi_{1}(x)-\psi_{2}(x)|\leqslant|\psi_{2}(y)|\exp\left(\int_{\min(y,x)}^{\max(y,x)}|V_{1}(t)|+|V_{2}(t)|{\rm d}t\right)\times\int_{\min(y,x)}^{\max(y,x)}|V_{1}(s)-V_{2}(s)|{\rm d}s.

Proof.

We have

D_{0}(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})=V_{1}\psi_{1}-V_{2}\psi_{2}=V_{1}(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})+(V_{1}-V_{2})\psi_{2}.

Thus,

(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})^{\prime}=J^{-1}\left[V_{1}(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})+(V_{1}-V_{2})\psi_{2}\right]

and then, assuming without loss of generality that
y\leqslant x
,

(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})(x)=J^{-1}\left[\int_{y}^{x}V_{1}(t)(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})(t){\rm d}t+\int_{y}^{x}(V_{1}(t)-V_{2}(t))\psi_{2}(t){\rm d}t\right].

We get then that

|(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})(x)|\leqslant\left[\int_{y}^{x}|V_{1}(t)||\psi_{1}(t)-\psi_{2}(t)|{\rm d}t+\int_{y}^{x}|V_{1}(t)-V_{2}(t)||\psi_{2}(t)|{\rm d}t\right].

Grönwall’s lemma gives us that

|(\psi_{1}-\psi_{2})(x)|\leqslant\int_{y}^{x}|V_{1}(t)-V_{2}(t)||\psi_{2}(t)|{\rm d}t\exp\left(\int_{y}^{x}|V_{1}(s)|{\rm d}s\right).

Finally, the previous lemma gives us that for all
t\in[y,x]
,

|\psi_{2}(t)|\leqslant|\psi_{2}(y)|\exp\left(\int_{y}^{t}|V_{2}(s)|{\rm d}s\right),

which concludes the proof.
∎

We directly get (
4.2
) by taking
V_{1}=V_{per}+V_{\omega}-E
and
V_{2}=V_{per}+V_{\omega}-E^{\prime}
. We have thus satisfied all the hypotheses of Theorem

.

5.
Kotani theory and regularity of the integrated density of states

We prove here Theorem
2.3
, which is Hölder regularity of the density of states; we get this regularity from the one we got in the previous section for the Lyapunov exponent.

We recalled in the introduction that, in
[
28
]
, we had defined the density of states measure by (
2.3
).
Nevertheless, with this expression, it would be difficult to recover a Wegner estimate. As a consequence, we give here another definition. We restrict the operator on an interval with what we will call
Dirichlet boundary conditions
. Note that they are not the usual Dirichlet conditions, where
\psi=0
on the boundary, but we choose this name by analogy with the second-order case where we consider the vector
\begin{pmatrix}\psi\\
\psi^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}
.

Given
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
L>0
, we define the operator
H_{\omega,x,L}
, called the
restricted operator with Dirichlet boundary condition
to the interval
I=(x-L/2,x+L/2)
, as the operator acting as
H_{\omega}
on the domain

\mathrm{Dom}(H_{\omega,x,L})=\{\psi=\begin{pmatrix}\psi^{\uparrow}\\
\psi^{\downarrow}\end{pmatrix}\in H^{1}(I)\text{ such that }\psi^{\uparrow}(x-L/2)=\psi^{\uparrow}(x+L/2)=0\}.

We will use the notation
H_{\omega,L}
for
H_{\omega,0,L}
.

We define the density of states in the following way.

Definition 5.1
.

For all bounded measurable
\phi
, the density of states is

\nu(\phi)=\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L}{\mathbb{E}}(\mathrm{tr}(\phi(H_{\omega,L}))).

This definition is not the same as the one we have used in
[
28
]
, but we prove in appendix
B
that both are equivalent
.
It is easy to see that
\nu
is a positive linear functional on the bounded continuous functions and, thus comes from a measure on
\mathbb{R}
which will be denoted by
\nu
too.
Since our Hamiltonian is not bounded from below, we cannot define the integrated density of states in the usual way.
Instead of that, we take

(5.1)

N(E)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}-\nu((E,0])\text{ if }E<0\\
\nu((0,E])\text{ if }E\geqslant 0\end{array}\right.

We introduce too
N_{0}
, which is the integrated density of states for the free Dirac operator. An explicit calculation proves that the eigenvalues of this operator with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
[-L/2,L/2]
are the
(k\pi/L)_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
, and thus
N_{0}(E)=\frac{E}{\pi}
.

In order to get between the Lyapunov exponent and the integrated density of states, we prove the following result, called Thouless formula.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
N
and
N_{0}
be the integrated densities of states, respectively of the operator
H_{\omega}
and
D_{0}
, and
\gamma
be the Lyapunov exponent for the operator
H_{\omega}
. Then, there exists
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that, for every
E\in\mathbb{R}
,

(5.2)

\gamma(E)=-\alpha+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log\left|\frac{E-t}{t-{\rm i}}\right|{\rm d}(N-N_{0})(t).

The original formula for Schrödinger operators, in the discrete case, is given by
\gamma(E)=-\alpha+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log\left|{E-t}\right|{\rm d}N(t)
[
10
, Proposition VI.4.3]
. Nevertheless, it is easy to see that the integral is not convergent in our case. Several methods have been used in the case of continuous Schrödinger operators: one of them is to introduce the normalization term
t-{\rm i}

[
13
]
, another one is to consider the differences
N-N_{0}
and
\gamma-\gamma_{0}

[
2
]
. None of these two methods provides a convergent integral in our case, but a combination of both works. Note that, since the free Dirac operator has purely absolutely continuous spectrum on the whole real line, its Lyapunov exponent is identically zero (cf.
[
10
, Corollary VII.3.4]
). That is why, in most of the cases, we do not write
\gamma_{0}
in the formulas.
In all the following, the splitting between
V_{per}
and
V_{\omega}
will not be used. Hence, we define
W_{\omega}=V_{per}+V_{\omega}
.
We have the following lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

There exists a constant
C
, depending only on
\|W_{\omega}\|_{\infty}
, such that for all
E\in\mathbb{R}
, we have that

(5.3)

|N(E)-N_{0}(E)|\leqslant C.

Proof.

We will give a proof that uses the Hellmann-Feynman formula. Indeed, the restricted operator has discrete spectrum. We will give the proof for
E>0
, it is similar for
E<0
. For
\theta\in[0,1]
, let
H_{\omega,L,\theta}=D_{0}+\theta W_{\omega}
on
[-L/2,L/2]
with Dirichlet boundary conditions. Then, there exist continuous functions
E_{k}(\cdot)
, defined on
[0,1]
, such that the spectrum of
H_{\omega,L,\theta}
is the set
\{E_{k}(\theta),k\in\mathbb{Z}\}
for all
\theta
. Moreover, we have that each of these functions is differentiable and that, denoting by
\psi_{k}(\theta)
a
normalized
eigenfunction associated with
E_{k}(\theta)
,

\frac{{\rm d}E_{k}}{{\rm d}\theta}=\langle\psi_{k}(\theta),W_{\omega}\psi_{k}(\theta)\rangle.

Thus, the modulus of the derivative is smaller than
\|W_{\omega}\|_{\infty}
. Then, the number of eigenvalues of
H_{\omega,L,1}
in
[0,E]
cannot be larger than the number of eigenvalues of
H_{\omega,L,0}
in
[-\|W_{\omega}\|_{\infty},E+\|W_{\omega}\|_{\infty}]
, and thus

N(E)\leqslant N_{0}(E+\|W_{\omega}\|_{\infty})-N_{0}(-\|W_{\omega}\|_{\infty})=\frac{1}{\pi}(E+2\|W\|_{\infty}).

Symmetrically, we have that for all
E^{\prime}
,

N_{0}(E^{\prime})\leqslant N(E^{\prime}+\|W_{\omega}\|_{\infty})-N(-\|W_{\omega}\|_{\infty})

and thus

N(E)\geqslant N_{0}(E-\|W_{\omega}\|_{\infty})+N(-\|W_{\omega}\|_{\infty}).

∎

In the following, we need to have an
\mathbb{R}
-ergodic operator. In
[
18
]
, Kirsch proves that, given a
\mathbb{Z}
-ergodic operator
H_{\omega}
, we can construct an
\mathbb{R}
-ergodic operator
\overline{H}_{\overline{\omega}}
, on a wider probability space, with, for each
\overline{\omega}
,
\overline{H}_{\overline{\omega}}
is unitarily equivalent to
H_{\omega}
for some
\omega
.

Since we apply Kirsch’s suspension procedure, the expectation must here be understood as both expectation on
\Omega
and average value on
[0,1]
for the potentials. It will be denoted by
\overline{\mathbb{E}}
.

The next step towards the Thouless formula is to introduce a tool named Kotani
w
-function. We use it in the way it has been introduced by Sadel and Schulz-Baldes for Dirac operators in
[
23
]
.

For the operator we have introduced in the beginning, we denote by
m_{+}
the Weyl-Titchmarsh function on
(0,+\infty)
, as it is defined in Appendix
A
. If we can decompose
W_{\omega}=W_{\omega,am}\sigma_{1}+W_{\omega,sc}\sigma_{3}+W_{\omega,el}I_{1}
, we define the function

w(z)=-\overline{\mathbb{E}}(W_{\omega,am}+m_{+}(z)(W_{\omega,el}-W_{\omega,sc}-z)).

Similarly, we introduce the corresponding function for the free Dirac operator
D_{0}
:

w_{0}(z)=-zm_{0}(z)

where
m_{0}(z)={\rm i}
is the Weyl function associated with the free operator.

This function will provide us a link between the Lyapunov exponent and the integrated density of states through the Green’s function. We recall that the Green’s function
G(z,\cdot,\cdot)
is the integral kernel of the resolvent
(H_{\omega}-z)^{-1}
. We start by the following theorem of Sadel and Schulz-Baldes, which links the Green’s function and the Lyapunov exponent. Here
G(z)=G(z,x,x)
, and we drop the dependence in
x
since we take the expectation
\overline{\mathbb{E}}
.

Theorem 5.4

(
[
23
]
, Theorem 5)
.

Let
\mathrm{Im}(z)\neq 0
. Then,

(1)

\gamma(z)=-\mathrm{Re}(w(z))
.

(2)

\partial_{z}w(z)=\overline{\mathbb{E}}(\mathrm{tr}G(z))
.

These equalities are still valid when the potential is zero, i.e. for
w_{0}
,
G_{0}
and
\gamma_{0}
.

To get the link with the integrated density of states, we prove the following lemma:

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\mathbb{R}
. Then,

\overline{\mathbb{E}}(\mathrm{tr}(G(z)))=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{{\rm d}\nu(E)}{E-z}.

Proof.

We follow the proof given in
[
6
]
for Schrödinger operators. For
l>0
, we denote by
\chi_{l}
the characteristic function of
[-l/2,l/2]
.

\mathbb{R}
-ergodicity implies that,
for all
l>0
and all bounded borelian set
B
,

\nu(B)=\overline{\mathbb{E}}(\frac{1}{l}\mathrm{tr}(\chi_{l}E_{\omega}(B)\chi_{l})).

By taking the limit
l\to 0
, we get that

\nu(B)=\overline{\mathbb{E}}(\mathrm{tr}(\delta_{0}E_{\omega}(B)\delta_{0})).

The operator in the trace is well defined on
H^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{2})
since this space is included in
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{2})
.
Thus,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{{\rm d}\nu(E)}{E-z}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{E-z}{\rm d}\overline{\mathbb{E}}(\mathrm{tr}(\delta_{0}E_{\omega}\delta_{0}))(E)

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathbb{E}}(\mathrm{tr}(\delta_{0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{E-z}{\rm d}E_{\omega}(E)\delta_{0}))

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathbb{E}}(\mathrm{tr}(\delta_{0}(H_{\omega}-z)^{-1}\delta_{0}))

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathbb{E}}(\mathrm{tr}(G(z))).

∎

The second point of Theorem
5.4
and Lemma
5.5
can be combined to get a link between the imaginary part of the
w
functions and the integrated densities of states.

Lemma 5.6
.

There exists
c\in\mathbb{R}
such that, for all
E\in\mathbb{R}
, we have

(5.4)

\lim_{a\to 0^{+}}\mathrm{Im}\left(w(E+{\rm i}a)-w_{0}(E+{\rm i}a)\right)=\pi(N(E)-N_{0}(E))+c.

Proof.

According to the second point of Theorem
5.4
, we know that, for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
\mathrm{Im}(z)>0
,
w^{\prime}(z)=\overline{\mathbb{E}}(G(z))
. With the previous lemma, it gives that

w^{\prime}(z)-w_{0}^{\prime}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{{\rm d}(\nu-\nu_{0})(E^{\prime})}{E^{\prime}-z}.

With an integration by parts, in which we use the fact that
(N(E)-N_{0}(E))/(E-z)
tends to 0 in
\pm\infty
since the numerator is bounded, we get that

w^{\prime}(z)-w_{0}^{\prime}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{N(E^{\prime})-N_{0}(E^{\prime})}{(E^{\prime}-z)^{2}}{\rm d}E^{\prime}.

If we integrate, we find that

(5.5)

w(z)-w_{0}(z)=c+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+E^{\prime}z}{(E^{\prime}-z)(1+E^{\prime 2})}(N(E^{\prime})-N_{0}(E^{\prime})){\rm d}E^{\prime}.

Taking the imaginary part, for
z=E+{\rm i}a
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(w(E+{\rm i}a)-w_{0}(E+{\rm i}a))

\displaystyle=c^{\prime}+a\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{N(E^{\prime})-N_{0}(E^{\prime})}{(E-E^{\prime})^{2}+a^{2}}{\rm d}E^{\prime}

\displaystyle=c^{\prime}+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{N(E+au)-N_{0}(E+au)}{1+u^{2}}{\rm d}u.

In the last line, we have done the change of variable
u=(E^{\prime}-E)/a
.

Since
N
and
N_{0}
are right-continuous (as they are cumulative distribution functions), we get

\lim_{a\to 0+}\mathrm{Im}(w(E+{\rm i}a)-w_{0}(E+{\rm i}a))=(N(E)-N_{0}(E))\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{1+u^{2}}{\rm d}u=\pi(N(E)-N_{0}(E)).

∎

We can now conclude the proof of the Thouless formula using the first point of Theorem
5.4
.
We know that, for all
z
with
\mathrm{Im}(z)\neq 0
,
\gamma(z)=\gamma(z)-\gamma_{0}(z)=-\mathrm{Re}(w(z)-w_{0}(z)).

Thus, we get from (
5.5
) that

\gamma(z)=-c-\mathrm{Re}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+E^{\prime}z}{(E^{\prime}-z)(1+E^{\prime 2})}(N(E^{\prime})-N_{0}(E^{\prime})){\rm d}E^{\prime}\right).

If we integrate by parts, we find that

\gamma(z)=-c+\mathrm{Re}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log\left|\frac{t-z}{t-{\rm i}}\right|{\rm d}(\nu-\nu_{0})(t)\right).

We can get rid of the terms with
\lim_{t\to\pm\infty}\log\left|\frac{t-z}{t-{\rm i}}\right|(N(t)-N_{0}(t))
since the first factor goes to 0 in
\pm\infty
and the second one is bounded. We see that the integral is real.

It remains to take the limit when
z
becomes real. For the right-hand side, the pointwise convergence of the integrand is clear. If
z={\rm i}y
(for positive
y
), then the convergence as
y
goes to 0 is monotone. In the other cases, we use the fact that
\log\left|\frac{t-E-{\rm i}a}{t-{\rm i}}\right|=\log\left|\frac{t-E-{\rm i}a}{t-E-{\rm i}}\right|+\log\left|\frac{t-E-{\rm i}}{t-{\rm i}}\right|
to conclude.

The limit in the left-hand side is proven as in Proposition 8.2.14 of
[
6
]
.

With the Thouless formula, we can prove Hölder regularity of the integrated density of states with the same arguments as
[
13
]
. We give the proof here for the sake of completeness.

The tool is the Hilbert transformation defined, for a square-integrable function
\psi
, by

(T\psi)(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{|x-t|>\epsilon}\frac{\psi(t)}{x-t}{\rm d}t.

We use the two following results on this transformation, the proof of which being in
[
10
]
:

(1)

For all
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
,
T\psi
is a square-integrable function and
T^{2}\psi=-\psi
almost everywhere with respect to Lebesgue measure.

(2)

If a function
\psi
is Hölder-continuous on some interval
[x_{0}-a,x_{0}+a]
, then
T\psi
is Hölder-continuous on
[x_{0}-\frac{a}{2},x_{0}+\frac{a}{2}]
.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2.3
.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

The first point is that, since
t\mapsto\log|(E-t)/(t-{\rm i})|
is
{\rm d}(N-N_{0})
-integrable, then

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}\int_{E-\epsilon}^{E+\epsilon}|\log\left|\frac{t-E-{\rm i}a}{t-{\rm i}}\right||{\rm d}(N-N_{0})(t)=0,

and thus

(5.6)

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}|\log(\epsilon)|(N-N_{0})(E+\epsilon)-(N-N_{0})(E-\epsilon)=0.

Thus,
(N-N_{0})
is log-Hölder continuous and in particular continuous. Since
N_{0}
is continuous, we have the same property for
N
.

Let
E_{0}\in I
and
a>0
such that
J=[E_{0}-4a,E_{0}+4a]\subset\mathbb{R}\backslash M
, which implies that
\gamma
is Hölder-continuous in
J
by Proposition
4.1
. Taking
\psi(t)=(N-N_{0})(t)\chi_{J}(t)
, we note that
T^{2}\psi(t)=-(N-N_{0})(t)
for every
t
in
J
.

For
|E-E_{0}|<4a
, we can compute the Hilbert transform of
\psi
, using the Thouless formula to make
\gamma
appear and the previously proven Hölder continuity to get rid of the limit in
\epsilon
:

\displaystyle\pi(T\psi)(E)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma(E)+\alpha-\int_{[t-E_{0}|>4a}\log\left|\frac{E-t}{t-{\rm i}}\right|{\rm d}(N-N_{0})(t)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{E_{0}-4a}^{E_{0}+4a}\log(t^{2}+1){\rm d}(N-N_{0})(t)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\log(E_{0}-E+4a)(N-N_{0})(E_{0}+4a)+\log(E-E_{0}+4a)(N-N_{0})(E_{0}-4a).

We see then that
T\psi
is Hölder-continuous in
[E_{0}-2a,E_{0}+2a]
, which implies that
T^{2}\psi
is Hölder-continuous in
[E_{0}-a,E_{0}+a]
. But we know that
T^{2}\psi=N-N_{0}
almost everywhere in this interval and thus, since both are continuous, they are equal everywhere in the interval. We get thus that
N-N_{0}
is Hölder-continuous in
[E_{0}-a,E_{0}+a]
and
N
has this property too since
N_{0}
is Lipschitz.

By a compactness argument, we get that the Hölder continuity is uniform in any compact interval.
∎

6.
Multiscale analysis and localization

6.1.
The requirements of multiscale analysis

This section will be devoted to the proof of spectral and dynamical localization for our model. We use a method called
bootstrap multiscale analysis
, which has been developed by Germinet and Klein in
[
16
]
. Even if we do not need to write a proof, we have to state the hypotheses.

The first one is the possibility to use generalized eigenfunctions.

Let
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R},{\rm d}x;\mathbb{C}^{2})
. Given
\nu>1/4
, we put, for
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
\langle x\rangle=\sqrt{1+x^{2}}
and we define the weighted spaces
\mathcal{H}_{\pm}
as

\mathcal{H}_{\pm}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\langle x\rangle^{\pm 4\nu}{\rm d}x;\mathbb{C}^{2}).

The sesquilinear form

\langle\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{+},\mathcal{H}_{-}}=\int\bar{\phi_{1}}(x)\cdot\phi_{2}(x){\rm d}x

where
\phi_{1}\in\mathcal{H}_{+}
and
\phi_{2}\in\mathcal{H}_{-}
is the duality map.

We set
T
to be the self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}
given by multiplication by the function
\langle x\rangle^{2\nu}
; note that
T^{-1}
is bounded.

Property 6.1

(SGEE)
.

We say that an ergodic random operator
H_{\omega}
satisfies the strong property of generalized eigenfunction expansion (SGEE) in some open interval
I
if, for some
\nu>1/4
,

(1)

The set

\mathrm{Dom}^{\omega}_{+}=\{\phi\in\mathrm{Dom}(H_{\omega})\cap\mathcal{H}_{+};H_{\omega}\phi\in\mathcal{H}_{+}\}

is dense in
\mathcal{H}_{+}
and is an operator core for
H_{\omega}
with probability one.

(2)

There exists a bounded, continuous function
f
on
\mathbb{R}
, strictly positive on the spectrum of
H_{\omega}

such that

{\mathbb{E}}\left\{[\mathrm{tr}(T^{-1}f(H_{\omega})\Pi_{\omega}(I)T^{-1})]^{2}\right\}<\infty,

\Pi_{\omega}
being the spectral projection associated with
H_{\omega}
.

Definition 6.2
.

A measurable function
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
is said to be a generalized eigenfunction of
H_{\omega}
with generalized eigenvalue
E
if
\psi\in\mathcal{H}_{-}\backslash\{0\}

and

\langle H_{\omega}\phi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{+},\mathcal{H}_{-}}=E\langle\phi,\psi\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{+},\mathcal{H}_{-}},\ \mbox{ for all }\phi\in\mathrm{Dom}_{+}^{\omega}.

As explained in
[
20
]
, when (SGEE) holds, a generalized eigenfunction which is in
\mathcal{H}
is a bona fide eigenfunction.
Moreover, if
\mu_{\omega}
is the spectral measure for the restriction of
H_{\omega}
to the Hilbert space
E_{\omega}(I)\mathcal{H}
, then
\mu_{\omega}
-almost every
E
is a generalized eigenvalue of
H_{\omega}
.

The following properties are about finite volume operators
Given an interval
\Lambda_{L}(x)=[x-L/2,x+L/2]
, we recall that the localized operator
H_{\omega,x,L}
is the operator
H_{\omega}
restricted to
\Lambda_{L}(x)
with Dirichlet boundary condition, as defined in the previous section.

We can then define
R_{\omega,x,L}(z)=(H_{\omega,x,L}-z)^{-1}
the resolvent of

H_{\omega,x,L}
and
\Pi_{\omega,x,L}(\cdot)
its spectral projection.

Definition 6.3
.

We say that an ergodic random family of operators
H_{\omega}
is standard
[
20
]
if for each
x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
L\in\mathbb{N}
there is a measurable map
H_{\cdot,x,L}
from
\Omega
to self-adjoint operators
on

such that

U(y)H_{\omega,x,L}U(-y)=H_{\tau_{y}\omega,x+y,L}

where

and

define the ergodicity:

U(y)H_{\omega}U(y)^{*}=H_{\tau_{y}(\omega)}.

It is easy to see that the restriction of the operators to intervals with Dirichlet boundary conditions makes

a standard operator.

We now enumerate the properties which are needed for multiscale analysis to be performed, yielding thus various localization properties.

conditions on the finite volume operators

and
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|g(\frac{n\pi}{L})|^{2}<\infty
. Then,
H
is Hilbert-Schmidt and

(B.1)

\|H\|_{2}\leqslant\frac{C}{\sqrt{L}}\|f\|_{2}\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|g(\frac{n\pi}{L})|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Proof.

The first step is to diagonalize the operator
D_{0,L}^{(D)}
. With a standard calculation, we find that the eigenvalues are the
E_{k}=\frac{k\pi}{L}
for
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and the associated (normalized) eigenvectors are the
\frac{1}{\sqrt{L}}\begin{pmatrix}\sin(E_{k}x)\\
\cos(E_{k}x)\end{pmatrix}
. As a consequence, the operator
H
has a matrix-valued integral kernel given by

K(x,t)=\frac{1}{L}\begin{pmatrix}f(x)\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}g(E_{k})\sin(E_{k}t)\sin(E_{k}x)&0\\
0&f(x)\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}g(E_{k})\cos(E_{k}t)\cos(E_{k}x)\end{pmatrix}

According to Theorem 2.11 of
[
24
]
, the operator
H
is Hilbert-Schmidt if and only if its integral kernel is
L^{2}
and the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the operator is equal to the
L^{2}
norm of the integral kernel.
By standard trigonometric manipulations, we are left to bound the
L^{2}
norm of

f(x)\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}g(E_{k})\cos(E_{k}(t\pm x)).

This norm is clearly equal to

\|f\|_{2}\|\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}g(E_{k})\cos(E_{k}(\cdot))\|_{2}.

Evaluating the second factor gives the result.
∎
