Thermal Dark Matter with Low-Temperature Reheating
Nicolás Bernal, Kuldeep Deka, and Marta Losada

Abstract
We explore the production of thermal dark matter (DM) candidates (WIMPs, SIMPs, ELDERs and Cannibals) during cosmic reheating. Assuming a general parametrization for the scaling of the inflaton energy density and the standard model (SM) temperature, we study the requirements for kinetic and chemical DM freeze-out in a model-independent way. For each of the mechanisms, up to two solutions that fit the entire observed DM relic density exist, for a given reheating scenario and DM mass. As an example, we assume a simple particle physics model in which DM interacts with itself and with SM through contact interactions. We find that low-temperature reheating can accommodate a wider range of couplings and larger masses than those permitted in the usual instantaneous high-temperature reheating. This results in DM solutions for WIMPs reaching masses as high as 10 14 10^{14} GeV, whereas for SIMPs and ELDERs, we can reach masses of 10 13 10^{13} GeV. Interestingly, current experimental data already constrain the enlarged parameter space of these models with low-reheating temperatures. Next-generation experiments could further probe these scenarios.

Introduction

The evidence for the presence of nonbaryonic dark matter (DM) in the Universe is strongly supported by both astrophysical and cosmological data. For a DM candidate to be considered viable, it must meet several criteria: It must be electromagnetically neutral, cosmologically stable, and nonrelativistic at the time of Big Bang nucleosynthesis (BBN). WIMPs achieve thermal equilibrium with the SM thermal plasma and subsequently undergo chemical freeze-out, leading to the observed DM relic abundance. It is generally assumed that this freeze-out occurs well after reheating has ended, at a time when the Universe’s energy density is dominated by SM radiation. Low-Temperature Reheating

Cosmic reheating is the era in which the Universe transits from being dominated by inflaton to SM radiation energy density. During reheating, the inflaton
\phi
has an effective equation of state
\omega
, which implies that its energy density
\rho_{\phi}
scales as

\rho_{\phi}(a)\propto a^{-3\,(1+\omega)},

(1)

where
a
corresponds to the cosmic scale factor of the Universe. Particularly interesting cases correspond to
\omega=0
,
\omega=1/3
, and
\omega=1
, corresponding to an inflaton oscillating in a quadratic or quartic potential, and to kination, respectively. More generally, if the inflaton oscillates at the bottom of a monomial potential
V(\phi)\propto\phi^{n}
, its equation of state tends to be
\omega=(n-2)/(n+2)

[
102
]
.
4

4

Preheating effects due to possible self-interaction of the inflaton could result in a transition into a radiation-dominated regime within

104

During
Reheating

Although it is commonly assumed that cosmic reheating finishes at a very high temperature, it may not be the case. In this section, we study different thermal mechanisms in the case where they are effective during reheating. We emphasize that, by construction, the inflaton transmits its energy to the SM bath and not to the DM. This is typically a good assumption as long as its branching ratio to the dark sector is smaller than
\mathcal{O}(10^{-4})\times m/(100~\text{GeV})

[
70
,
77
]
.

4.1
WIMPs and SIMPs:
T_{\text{fo}}\gg T_{\text{rh}}

The impact on the final DM relic abundance produced through the WIMP and the SIMP mechanisms occurring during reheating is two-fold. On the one hand, chemical decoupling occurs earlier, increasing the DM yield; on the other hand, injection of entropy into the SM bath because of inflaton decays dilutes the DM abundance.

The dilution corresponds to the change in SM entropy
S
from a given moment until the end of the reheating and is given by

\frac{S(T)}{S(T_{\text{rh}})}=\frac{s(T)}{s(T_{\text{rh}})}\left(\frac{a(T)}{a(T_{\text{rh}})}\right)^{3}=\frac{g_{\star s}(T)}{g_{\star s}(T_{\text{rh}})}\left(\frac{T_{\text{rh}}}{T}\right)^{\frac{3\,(1-\alpha)}{\alpha}},

(20)

where Eqs. (
2
) and (
9
) were used. Given that the SM entropy is conserved after the end of reheating, the DM yield at present can be estimated by

Y_{0}\simeq Y_{\text{rh}}=Y_{\text{fo}}\,\frac{S(T_{\text{fo}})}{S(T_{\text{rh}})}=\frac{45}{4\pi^{4}}\,\frac{g}{g_{\star s}(T_{\text{rh}})}\,x_{\text{fo}}^{2}\,K_{2}(x_{\text{fo}})\left(\frac{x_{\text{fo}}}{x_{\text{rh}}}\right)^{\frac{3\,(1-\alpha)}{\alpha}},

(21)

where
Y_{\text{fo}}
is the same as the one computed in Eq. (
10
). Interestingly, Eq. (
21
) is valid as long as
T_{\text{fo}}>T_{\text{rh}}
, in the two cases
T_{\text{fo}}>T_{\text{rh}}>T_{\text{k}}
and
T_{\text{fo}}>T_{\text{k}}>T_{\text{rh}}
. Additionally, it can be approximated in the two limits

Y_{0}\simeq\begin{cases}\frac{45}{2\pi^{4}}\,\frac{g}{g_{\star s}(T_{\text{rh}})}\left(\frac{x_{\text{fo}}}{x_{\text{rh}}}\right)^{\frac{3\,(1-\alpha)}{\alpha}}&\text{ for }x_{\text{fo}}\ll x_{\text{fo}}^{\text{c}}\,,\\
\frac{45}{4\pi^{4}}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\,\frac{g}{g_{\star s}(T_{\text{rh}})}\,x_{\text{fo}}^{\frac{3}{2}}\,e^{-x_{\text{fo}}}\left(\frac{x_{\text{fo}}}{x_{\text{rh}}}\right)^{\frac{3\,(1-\alpha)}{\alpha}}&\text{ for }x_{\text{fo}}\gg x_{\text{fo}}^{\text{c}}\,,\end{cases}

(22)

where the critical value
x_{\text{fo}}^{\text{c}}
(after extremizing Eq. (
21
) w.r.t.
x_{\text{fo}}
) is implicitly defined by

\alpha\,x_{\text{fo}}^{\text{c}}\,K_{1}(x_{\text{fo}}^{\text{c}})+3\,(\alpha-1)\,K_{2}(x_{\text{fo}}^{\text{c}})=0\,.

(23)

Several comments are in order with respect to Eq. (
22
).

i)
To be realized, the first expression (corresponding to
x_{\text{fo}}\ll x_{\text{fo}}^{\text{c}}
) requires interaction rates with a higher temperature dependence than the Hubble expansion rate, as will be shown in Section
5
.
ii)
For the second expression (corresponding to
x_{\text{fo}}\gg x_{\text{fo}}^{\text{c}}
) one only has to require
x_{\text{fo}}<x_{\text{rh}}
. And
iii)
, with respect to
x_{\text{fo}}^{\text{c}}
, for a given
T_{\text{rh}}
, a critical value of the DM mass can be defined as the mass required to fit the entire abundance of DM if
x_{\text{fo}}=x_{\text{fo}}^{\text{c}}
. DM with a mass larger than that value overshoots the observations if it is produced during reheating.

4.2
Cannibals and ELDERs:
T_{\text{k}}\gg T_{\text{fo}}

This section is conveniently divided into two cases,

and

4.2.1
T_{\text{k}}\gg T_{\text{rh}}\gg T_{\text{fo}}

In this case where
T_{\text{k}}\gg T_{\text{rh}}\gg T_{\text{fo}}
, the DM yield at present is given by

\displaystyle Y_{0}

\displaystyle\simeq Y_{\text{fo}}=\frac{n_{\text{eq}}(T^{\prime}_{\text{fo}})}{s(T_{\text{fo}})}=\frac{n_{\text{eq}}(T^{\prime}_{\text{fo}})}{s(T_{\text{rh}})}\,\frac{s^{\prime}(T_{\text{rh}}^{\prime})}{s^{\prime}(T^{\prime}_{\text{fo}})}=\frac{n_{\text{eq}}(T^{\prime}_{\text{fo}})}{s(T_{\text{rh}})}\,\frac{s^{\prime}(T_{\text{k}})}{s^{\prime}(T^{\prime}_{\text{fo}})}\left(\frac{T_{\text{rh}}}{T_{\text{k}}}\right)^{\frac{3}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\frac{45}{4\pi^{4}}\,\frac{g}{g_{\star s}(T_{\text{rh}})}\,\frac{x_{\text{rh}}^{3}}{{x^{\prime}_{\text{fo}}}}\left(\frac{x_{\text{k}}}{x_{\text{rh}}}\right)^{\frac{3}{\alpha}}\frac{K_{2}({x^{\prime}_{\text{fo}}})\,K_{3}(x_{\text{k}})}{K_{3}({x^{\prime}_{\text{fo}}})},

(24)

where the separate conservation of entropies between the end of reheating and the DM freeze-out, and the scaling of the DM temperature during reheating were used.

In this case where
T_{\text{k}}\gg T_{\text{fo}}
, at the time of chemical freeze out the two sectors are not expected to share the same temperature. Using the conservation of the dark sector entropy between
a(T_{\text{k}})
and
a_{\text{fo}}
, the conservation of the SM entropy between
a_{\text{rh}}
and
a_{\text{fo}}
, and the entropy injection in Eq. (
20
), one gets that

x_{\text{fo}}=x_{\text{rh}}\left(\frac{x_{\text{k}}}{x_{\text{rh}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\left[\frac{g_{\star s}(T_{\text{fo}})}{g_{\star s}(T_{\text{rh}})}\,\frac{K_{3}(x_{\text{k}})}{K_{3}({x^{\prime}_{\text{fo}}})}\right]^{\frac{1}{3}}.

(25)

4.2.2
T_{\text{k}}\gg T_{\text{fo}}\gg T_{\text{rh}}

Alternatively, in the case where
T_{\text{k}}\gg T_{\text{fo}}\gg T_{\text{rh}}
, the DM yield at present is given by

\displaystyle Y_{0}

\displaystyle\simeq Y_{\text{rh}}=\frac{n_{\text{eq}}(T_{\text{rh}}^{\prime})}{s(T_{\text{rh}})}=\frac{n_{\text{eq}}(T^{\prime}_{\text{fo}})}{s(T_{\text{rh}})}\,\frac{s^{\prime}(T_{\text{rh}}^{\prime})}{s^{\prime}(T^{\prime}_{\text{fo}})}=\frac{n_{\text{eq}}(T^{\prime}_{\text{fo}})}{s(T_{\text{rh}})}\,\frac{s^{\prime}(T_{\text{k}})}{s^{\prime}(T^{\prime}_{\text{fo}})}\left(\frac{T_{\text{rh}}}{T_{\text{k}}}\right)^{\frac{3}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\frac{45}{4\pi^{4}}\,\frac{g}{g_{\star s}(T_{\text{rh}})}\,\frac{x_{\text{rh}}^{3}}{{x^{\prime}_{\text{fo}}}}\left(\frac{x_{\text{k}}}{x_{\text{rh}}}\right)^{\frac{3}{\alpha}}\frac{K_{2}({x^{\prime}_{\text{fo}}})\,K_{3}(x_{\text{k}})}{K_{3}({x^{\prime}_{\text{fo}}})}\,,

(26)

which, as expected, is equal to the expression in Eq. (
24
), and simply corresponds to Eq. (
16
) times the dilution factor between
T_{\text{k}}
and
T_{\text{rh}}
. However, even if the expressions for the DM yield are the same, the SM temperature at which the chemical freeze-out occurs is different. Using the conservation of the dark sector entropy between
a(T_{\text{k}})
and
a_{\text{fo}}
, and the entropy injection to the visible sector in Eq. (
20
), one gets that the SM temperature at chemical freeze-out is

x_{\text{fo}}=x_{\text{k}}\left[\frac{K_{3}(x_{\text{k}})}{K_{3}({x^{\prime}_{\text{fo}}})}\right]^{\frac{\alpha}{3}},

(27)

independently of the reheating temperature, and which only coincides with Eq. (
25
) in the limit
x_{\text{k}}=x_{\text{fo}}
.

5
Particle Physics Realization

In this section we give a simple example of a specific particle physics model that realizes the previous DM production mechanisms. In the case of contact interactions, the cross sections for the 2-to-2 annihilation of DM into SM particles
\langle\sigma v\rangle_{2\to 2}
and the elastic scattering of a DM particle with a SM particle
\langle\sigma v\rangle_{\text{el}}
are given by

\langle\sigma v\rangle_{\text{el}}\sim\langle\sigma v\rangle_{2\to 2}\sim\left[\frac{\epsilon_{\text{eff}}}{m}\,\frac{K_{1}(x)}{K_{2}(x)}\right]^{2}\simeq\begin{cases}\frac{\epsilon_{\text{eff}}^{2}}{4\,T^{2}}&\text{ for }x\ll 1\,,\\
\frac{\epsilon_{\text{eff}}^{2}}{m^{2}}&\text{ for }x\gg 1\,,\end{cases}

(30)

as a function of the dimensionless effective coupling
\epsilon_{\text{eff}}
. Additionally, 3-to-2 DM annihilations are characterized by the thermally averaged cross section

\langle\sigma v^{2}\rangle_{3\to 2}\sim\frac{y_{\text{eff}}^{3}}{m^{5}}

(31)

for
x\gg 1
, and depends on the the dimensionless effective coupling
y_{\text{eff}}
. Therefore, the corresponding interaction rates are

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{el}}

\displaystyle=n^{\text{sm}}_{\text{eq}}\,\langle\sigma v\rangle_{\text{el}}\,,

(32)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{2\to 2}

\displaystyle=n_{\text{eq}}\,\langle\sigma v\rangle_{2\to 2}\,,

(33)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{3\to 2}

\displaystyle=n_{\text{eq}}^{2}\,\langle\sigma v^{2}\rangle_{3\to 2}\,,

(34)

where
n_{\text{eq}}
is the DM number density in equilibrium given in Eq. (
8
), and

n^{\text{sm}}_{\text{eq}}(T)=\frac{\zeta(3)}{\pi^{2}}\,g_{\text{sm}}\,T^{3}

(35)

is the equilibrium number density for SM particles, where
g_{\text{sm}}(T)
is the number of relativistic SM degrees of freedom that interact with DM (each fermionic degree of freedom counts as 3/4 due to statistics), and
\zeta(3)\simeq 1.2
is the Riemann zeta function. Here we fix
g_{\text{sm}}=2
.

As 3-to-2 DM annihilations are effective, it is expected that elastic 2-to-2 DM self-scattering, with a cross section
\sigma_{\text{dm}}
in the non-relativistic limit given by

\sigma_{\text{dm}}\sim\frac{y_{\text{eff}}^{2}}{m^{2}},

(36)

is efficient as well. Here, for simplicity, we have assumed the same coupling
y_{\text{eff}}
for the 3-to-2 and 2-to-2 scatterings. The non-observation of an offset between the mass distributions of DM and hot baryonic gas in the Bullet cluster constrains the ratio of the self-scattering cross section over DM mass to be
\sigma_{\text{dm}}/m<1.25
cm
2
/g
[
128
,
129
,
130
]
. Further observations of other cluster collisions reinforce this bound to
\sigma_{\text{dm}}/m<0.2
-
0.5
cm
2
/g
[
131
,
132
,
133
]
.

6
Conclusions

Despite extensive experimental efforts during the last few decades, the nature of dark matter (DM) continues to be a mystery. Specifically, the hypothesis that DM consists of weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs) that achieve thermal equilibrium with the standard model (SM) degrees of freedom has garnered substantial theoretical and experimental interest. However, no definitive evidence has emerged to support the existence of WIMP DM. This lack of conclusive results has led to the exploration of other mechanisms. On the thermal side, alternative production mechanisms exist; DM could be a strongly interacting massive particle (SIMP), an ELastically DEcoupling Relic (ELDER) or a cannibal. In these new paradigms, self-interactions within the DM sector play a crucial role in determining the abundance of DM. The key differences lie in the timing of chemical and kinetic freeze-out. For SIMPs, chemical freeze-out occurs while kinetic equilibrium is maintained, similar to that of WIMPs. In contrast, for ELDERs and cannibals, chemical freeze-out occurs after kinetic equilibrium is broken, with the latter occurring while the DM is non-relativistic or relativistic, respectively.

It is crucial to understand that the current abundance of DM is influenced not just by particle-physics dynamics but also by the cosmological history of the Universe. Given that the early Universe’s evolution remains largely uncertain, the conventional assumption is a Universe primarily governed by SM radiation from the end of cosmological inflation up to matter-radiation equality, with a sudden cosmic reheating happening at a very high SM temperature. Here, this picture is not taken for granted. In fact, we allowed noninstantaneous reheating and analyzed its impact on the thermal genesis of the DM.

The focus of this study has been on understanding the viability and implications of these thermal DM candidates in low-reheating scenarios, with careful consideration of their kinetic and/or chemical decoupling. We have studied general reheating scenarios by parameterizing the equation of state of the inflaton during reheating and the dependence of the SM temperature with the scale factor of the Universe. For the standard case of thermal DM production after reheating, we obtained in a model-independent way the region of parameter space that fits the total observed DM abundance for each type of DM candidate. We have then shown how these regions are modified when DM production occurs during reheating and identified the regions in tension with cosmological and laboratory constraints. For the WIMP, SIMP and ELDER mechanisms and for a given cosmology, two solutions that fit the entire observed DM abundance can occur: one during reheating and the other during the radiation-dominated era, or even both during reheating, as shown in Fig.
5
.

We subsequently studied the implications for a simple particle-physics model, assuming contact operators for DM self-interactions and DM interactions with SM states, focusing on DM production both after and during reheating. In both cases, cannibal solutions are not viable as a result of strong temperature suppression when the DM is relativistic. Moreover, in this specific realization, WIMP, SIMP, and ELDER solutions become further constrained from BBN, and the possibility of having hot DM. At the same time, low reheating temperatures render viable larger mass values for the DM mass, up to the
10^{14}
GeV ballpark, well beyond the usual limit of
130
TeV for WIMPs and a few MeVs for SIMPs and ELDERs; see Fig.
11
. Additionally, broader ranges for coupling values are now feasible, expanding the parameter space compared to the DM genesis during radiation domination. We have also shown how current direct and indirect limits on DM constrain significant parts of the allowed parameter space even in low-temperature reheating scenarios. Finally, we showed that upcoming direct detection experiments will be able to probe a substantial part of the remaining parameter space currently allowed.

Appendix B
Bound on
T_{\text{max}}

During reheating, the energy densities of the inflaton and SM radiation can be written as

\displaystyle\rho_{\phi}(a)

\displaystyle\simeq 3\,M_{P}^{2}\,H_{I}^{2}\left(\frac{a_{I}}{a}\right)^{3(1+\omega)},

(44)

\displaystyle\rho_{R}(a)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{\pi^{2}}{30}\,g_{\star}\,T_{\text{rh}}^{4}\left(\frac{a_{\text{rh}}}{a}\right)^{4\alpha},

(45)

where
a_{I}
corresponds to the scale factor at the end of inflation and beginning of reheating, and
H_{I}\equiv H(a_{I})
is the inflationary scale. The BICEP/Keck bound on the tensor-to-scalar ratio implies that
H_{I}\leq 2.0\times 10^{-5}~M_{P}

[
157
]
. Taking into account that at the end of reheating
\rho_{\phi}(a_{\text{rh}})=\rho_{R}(a_{\text{rh}})
, the maximal energy density reached by the SM thermal bath near
a=a_{I}
can be estimated and therefore the maximal temperature
T_{\text{max}}
is

T_{\text{max}}\simeq T_{\text{rh}}\left[\frac{90}{\pi^{2}\,g_{\star}}\,\frac{H_{I}^{2}\,M_{P}^{2}}{T_{\text{rh}}^{4}}\right]^{\frac{\alpha}{3(1+\omega)}}.

(46)

This limit on the maximum temperature reached by the SM bath is particularly relevant, as a thermally produced DM particle has to satisfy
m<T_{\text{max}}
.
