The inviscid limit of third-order linear and nonlinear acoustic equations Thanks:
Barbara Kaltenbacher, Vanja Nikolić

Abstract
We analyze the behavior of third-order in time linear and nonlinear sound waves in thermally relaxing fluids and gases as the sound diffusivity vanishes. The nonlinear acoustic propagation is modeled by the Jordan–Moore–Gibson–Thompson equation both in its Westervelt and in its Kuznetsov-type forms, that is, including quadratic gradient nonlinearities. As it turns out, sufficiently smooth solutions of these equations converge in the energy norm to the solutions of the corresponding inviscid models at a linear rate. Numerical experiments illustrate our theoretical findings.

inviscid limit, relaxing media, nonlinear acoustics, convergence rates

Introduction

The present work focuses on the limiting behavior of linear and nonlinear equations that describe the motion of sound waves through thermally relaxing media, as the diffusivity of sound vanishes. In modeling, the need to combine thermal relaxation with the nonlinear and dissipative effects leads to third-order in time equations. They have the following general form:

\tau u_{ttt}+u_{tt}-(\delta+\tau c^{2})\Delta u_{t}-c^{2}\Delta u=f(u,u_{t},u_{tt},\nabla u,\nabla u_{t}),

(2)

where the function
u=u(x,t)
may denote the acoustic pressure or acoustic velocity potential. The parameter
\tau>0
denotes the thermal relaxation time,
c>0
the speed of sound, and the coefficient
\delta
is often referred to as the diffusivity of sound
[
13
, §3]
. The parameter
\delta
is relatively small in fluids and gases, which motivates our research into the behavior of solutions of (
2
) as
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
. We take into account two types of nonlinearities, which have a physical motivation:

\displaystyle f(u,u_{t},u_{tt})=\frac{1}{2}(ku^{2})_{tt}=kuu_{tt}+ku_{t}^{2},

(3)

and

\displaystyle f(u_{t},u_{tt},\nabla u,\nabla u_{t})=\frac{1}{2}(\kappa u_{t}^{2}+\sigma|\nabla u|^{2})_{t}=\kappa u_{t}u_{tt}+\sigma\nabla u\cdot\nabla u_{t}.

(4)

Third-order models of nonlinear acoustics in the form of (
2
) originate from using a general temperature law within the governing system of equations, which includes conservation laws and constitutive equations of the medium
[
15
]
. By the standard Fourier temperature law, a thermal disturbance at one point has an instantaneous effect elsewhere in the medium
[
27
,
12
]
, which may lead to an infinite speed of propagation paradox in waves. The Maxwell–Cattaneo law, on the other hand, introduces a time lag between the temperature gradient and the heat flux induced by it
[
23
,
39
]
via

\tau\mathbf{q}_{t}+\mathbf{q}=-K\nabla\mathbf{\theta}.

(5)

The heat then propagates in time via thermal waves, which is often referred to as the second-sound phenomenon in the literature
[
15
,
39
]
. Employing the Maxwell–Cattaneo temperature law within the governing equations of sound propagation leads to third-order in time models (
2
) that avoid the paradox of infinite speed of propagation and instead have the expected hyperbolic character.

Third-order wave motion may also originate from the presence of molecular relaxation when the pressure-density relation of the medium is not satisfied exactly, but up to a memory term; see
[
31
, §1.1]
and
[
37
, §4]
. Such relaxation mechanisms typically occur in media with “impurities”; these can be, for example, water with micro-bubbles, seawater, chemically reacting fluids, or a mixture of gases. As such, they arise in various applications. For instance, micro-bubbles are often used as a contrast agent in ultrasonic imaging
[
10
]
. They are also known to increase the speed and efficacy of the focused ultrasound treatments
[
40
]
.

In this work, we investigate the convergence of solutions to equations (
2
) as
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
. The analysis is performed for
\tau>0
fixed, and so the term “inviscid limit” should be understood in this context as the vanishing sound diffusivity limit in thermally relaxing fluids or gases. We consider the limiting behavior in the linearized versions of (
2
) on smooth bounded domains as well as in the nonlinear PDE, which is referred to as the Jordan–Moore–Gibson–Thompson equation in the literature.

Our main results pertain to the convergence of solutions to (
2
) with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions in the energy norm to their inviscid counterparts, as the sound diffusivity vanishes. It turns out that sufficiently smooth solutions of (
2
) converge to the solutions of the inviscid equation in the energy norm at a linear rate:

\|u^{(\delta)}-u\|_{\textup{E}}\lesssim\delta\qquad\text{as }\ \delta\rightarrow 0^{+};

(6)

see also (
29
) below. The smoothness requirements are naturally higher in the presence of quadratic gradient nonlinearity (
4
). We refer to upcoming Theorems
2
,
7
, and
11
for details.

We organize the remaining of our exposition as follows. In Section
2
, we provide more details about the mathematical modeling of ultrasonic waves in thermally relaxing fluids and give an overview of related results. Section
3
deals with a linear version of (
2
). There we derive a uniform bound with respect to
\delta
for its solutions and prove a limiting result as
\delta
vanishes; see Theorem
2
. Section
4
extends the investigation to the JMGT equation with the right-hand side nonlinearity given by (
3
) by employing Banach’s Fixed-point theorem; see Theorem
7
. In Section
5
, we return to a linearized problem to derive a higher-order energy bound that is uniform with respect to
\delta
. This is needed so that in Section
6
we can analyze the limiting behavior of the JMGT equation with the right-hand side given by (
4
); see Theorems
9
and
11
. Finally, in Section
7
we provide the results of numerical experiments, which illustrate our theory and the convergence rate
O(\delta)
.

2
Mathematical modeling and related work

The Jordan–Moore–Gibson–Thompson (JMGT) equation, given by

\tau\psi_{ttt}+\psi_{tt}-(\delta+\tau c^{2})\Delta\psi_{t}-c^{2}\Delta\psi=\frac{1}{2}(\kappa\psi_{t}^{2}+\sigma|\nabla\psi|^{2})_{t},

(7)

is a model of ultrasonic propagation that accounts for thermal relaxation, dissipation due to viscosity, and nonlinear effects of quadratic type. It is expressed in terms of the acoustic velocity potential
\psi=\psi(x,t)
; we refer to
[
15
]
for its derivation. The parameter
\tau>0
represents the relaxation time of the heat flux and the coefficient
c>0
stands for the speed of sound. The nonlinear parameters on the right-hand side are typically
\sigma=2
and
\kappa=\frac{B/A}{c^{2}}
, where
B/A
is the parameter of nonlinearity that arises from the pressure-density relation in a given medium. For the purposes of our analysis, it is sufficient to assume that
\sigma
,
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
.

Local nonlinear effects in sound propagation can often be neglected if the propagation distance is sufficiently large in terms of the number of wavelengths. In such cases, employing the approximation
|\nabla\psi|^{2}\approx(1/c^{2})\,\psi_{t}^{2}
in (
7
) leads to

\displaystyle\tau\psi_{ttt}+\psi_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\psi-(\delta+\tau c^{2})\Delta\psi_{t}=\frac{1}{c^{2}}\left(1+\frac{B}{2A}\right)(\psi_{t}^{2})_{t}.

(8)

This equation is frequently expressed in terms of the acoustic pressure. Formally differentiating (
8
) with respect to time and then using the relation
p=\varrho\psi_{t}
, where
\varrho
is the medium density, yields

\tau p_{ttt}+p_{tt}-(\delta+\tau c^{2})\Delta p_{t}-c^{2}\Delta p=\frac{1}{2}(kp^{2})_{tt},

(9)

where now
k=\frac{1}{\varrho c^{2}}\left(1+\frac{B}{2A}\right)
. Again, for our theoretical purposes, we can take
k\in\mathbb{R}
. Letting
\tau\rightarrow 0
in (
7
) and (
9
) leads to the classical second-order models of nonlinear acoustics – Kuznetsov’s
[
24
]
and Westervelt’s
[
52
]
equations, respectively. For this reason and to distinguish different third-order equations, we will henceforth refer to (
7
) and (
9
) as the JMGT–Kuznetsov and JMGT–Westervelt equation, respectively.

A linear version of these equations is referred to as the Moore–Gibson–Thompson (MGT) equation
[
30
,
43
]
:

\tau p_{ttt}+\alpha p_{tt}-(\delta+\tau c^{2})\Delta p_{t}-c^{2}\Delta p=0.

(10)

Early mathematical investigations of third-order in time linear PDEs include the studies on classical solutions and Cauchy problems by Varlamov in
[
49
,
44
,
46
]
, and Renno in
[
35
]
. Matkowsky and Reiss studied the asymptotic expansion of solutions to the linear problem as
\tau\rightarrow 0^{+}
in
[
29
]
. Further studies on the theory of singular perturbation can be found in
[
45
,
2
]
. In
[
50
]
, Varlamov and Nesterov analyzed the linear equation with spatially varying coefficients, establishing results on the existence and uniqueness of classical solutions and their asymptotic expansion as
\tau\rightarrow 0^{+}
. Nonlinear third-order propagation was seemingly first investigated by Varlamov in
[
48
,
47
]
in terms of existence and asymptotic behavior of classical solutions with a heuristically motivated right-hand side nonlinearity given by
\Delta(u^{2})
.

A more recent mathematical research on third-order ultrasonic waves was initiated in
[
16
]
with a semigroup approach employed in the well-posedness and stability analysis of the linear equation (
10
). As concluded in
[
16
]
, exponential stability of solutions requires that
\gamma:=\alpha-\frac{\tau c^{2}}{\delta+\tau c^{2}}>0.

The problem is unstable when
\gamma<0
and marginally stable when
\gamma=0
. We note that in this work the focus is on the short-time behavior, and therefore no assumptions on the sign of
\gamma
will be made.

Since the results of
[
16
]
, the interest in the qualitative behavior of third-order acoustic equations has flourished significantly, and these linear and nonlinear models represent by now a very active area of research. We thus provide the reader with a selection of relevant works here on the analysis of linear
[
33
,
5
,
9
,
7
,
32
,
28
]
and nonlinear third-order acoustic equations
[
17
,
34
,
6
]
. We also note that the limiting behavior of solutions for vanishing thermal relaxation time
\tau
(and fixed
\delta>0
) has been studied in, e.g.,
[
18
,
4
]
. Taking into account the effects of both thermal and molecular relaxation leads to third-order equations with memory, which have also been a topic of extensive research recently; see, for example,
[
25
,
8
,
1
]
and the references given therein. To the best of our knowledge, the present work is the first dealing with the convergence of solutions to third-order equations as the sound diffusivity
\delta
vanishes.

E[p](t)=\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}\|p_{tt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\tau^{2}c^{2}}{2}\|\nabla p_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{c^{2}}{2}\|\nabla p(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(26)

we see that on one hand, it dominates the physical energy for the auxiliary function
z=\tau p_{t}+p_{t}
:

\tilde{E}[z](t)=\frac{1}{2}\|z_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{c^{2}}{2}\|\nabla z(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

(27)

On the other hand, by (
16
), it satisfies an energy estimate of the form

E[p](t)\leq C(T,R,\tau)\,E[p](0)+\int_{0}^{t}\|f(s)\|_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s.

(28)

We will use
E[p]
to establish the convergence rate as
\delta\to 0^{+}
in the space

\displaystyle\textup{E}=W^{2,\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))\cap W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))

(29)

induced by this energy.

Theorem 2
.

Under the conditions of Proposition
1
, the family of solutions
\{p^{(\delta)}\}_{\delta>0}
to the generalized Moore–Gibson–Thompson equation converges in the topology induced by the energy norm for the wave equation to the solution
p
of the inviscid equation as
\delta\to 0^{+}
at a linear rate.

Proof.

Let
\delta
,
\delta^{\prime}\in[0,\overline{\delta}]
. Furthermore, let
p^{(\delta)}
and
p^{(\delta^{\prime})}
be the solutions of (
12
) with the sound diffusivity
\delta
and
\delta^{\prime}
, respectively. We follow the general strategy of
[
22
,
41
,
19
,
38
]
by proving that
\{\overline{p}^{\delta}\}
is a Cauchy sequence in suitable topology, where
\overline{p}=p^{(\delta)}-p^{(\delta^{\prime})}
. We note that
\overline{p}
solves the equation

\displaystyle\tau\overline{p}_{ttt}+\alpha\overline{p}_{tt}-(\delta+\tau c^{2})\Delta\overline{p}_{t}-c^{2}\Delta\overline{p}-\mu\overline{p}_{t}-\eta\overline{p}=(\delta-\delta^{\prime})\Delta p_{t}^{(\delta^{\prime})}

(30)

supplemented by zero initial conditions.
Applying estimate (
16
) in Proposition
1
with

directly yields

E[p](t)\leq C(\tau)\exp(K(\tau)(R^{2}+1)T)\,|\delta-\delta^{\prime}|^{2}\,\bar{R}^{2}

(31)

with energy

defined as in (

).
Here we have used the bound

\displaystyle\bar{R}^{2}=

\displaystyle C(\tau)e^{K(\tau)(R^{2}+1)T}\left(\|\nabla p_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta p_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta p_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla f\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}\right)

(32)

on

, resulting from (

). The stated rate is obtained by setting

to zero.

Remark 3
(Perturbation of the wave speed).

Regularizing perturbations of the second-order linear wave equations have been studied thoroughly in the literature; see, for example,
[
26
, § 8.5]
and
[
38
]
. In view of the fact that (
12a
) can be seen as a second-order wave equation for
z=\tau p_{t}+p
, we compare these known results to Theorem
2
above.
To this end, for simplicity, we set
\alpha=1
,
\mu=\eta=f=0
, which yields

z_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta z-\frac{\delta}{\tau}\Delta z+\frac{\delta}{\tau}\Delta p=0\,.

(33)

It thus becomes apparent that our results are not covered by those in
[
38
,
26
]
, which involve parabolic and non-singular perturbations; cf.
[
26
, Theorem 8.3]
and
[
38
, Propositions 7 and 9]
. In fact, equation (
33
) reveals that we do not deal with damping, but rather with a perturbation of the wave speed
c
.

Remark 4
(Heterogeneous media).

When besides
\alpha
,
\mu
,
\eta
, also
\tau
,
\delta
,
k
, and
c
are space (and possibly time) dependent coefficients, all statements in this section remain valid, as long as these coefficients are sufficiently smooth and
c
and

are bounded away from zero. In particular, an inspection of the proofs shows that it suffices to have an

sound speed satisfying

for reproducing the higher-order energy estimate (

, though.

4
Uniform bounds for the JMGT–Westervelt equation and the inviscid limit

We next wish to extend the study of the limiting behavior of the solutions to the nonlinear JMGT–Westervelt equation given by (
9
). To derive
\delta
-independent bounds on solutions to (
9
), we employ a fixed-point argument on the mapping
\mathcal{T}^{\textup{W}}:q\mapsto p,
which associates
q
with the solution of the linearized equation

\tau p_{ttt}+\alpha p_{tt}-(\delta+\tau c^{2})\Delta p_{t}-c^{2}\Delta p-kq_{t}p_{t}=0

(34)

with homogeneous Dirichlet data and initial conditions
(p,p_{t},p_{tt})|_{t=0}=(p_{0},p_{1},p_{2}).
Above, we have set
\alpha=1-kq
and taken

q\in B_{R}^{\textup{W}}:=\{q\in X^{\textup{W}}\,:\|q\|_{X^{\textup{W}}}\leq R\mbox{ and }q(0)=p_{0},\,q_{t}(0)=p_{1},\,q_{tt}(0)=p_{2}\},

(35)

with some appropriately chosen radius
R>0
; recall that
X^{\textup{W}}
is defined in (
14
). More precisely, crucial for our well-posedness proof will be the existence of
R>0
, such that the bounds

\displaystyle\sqrt{C(\tau)\exp(K(\tau)(R^{2}+1)T)}\ r\leq R

(36)

\displaystyle\theta=\left(C_{H^{2},L^{\infty}}+C_{H^{1},L^{4}}^{2}\right)C(\tau)\exp(K(\tau)(R^{2}+1)T)\,r|k|\sqrt{T}<1

(37)

hold. By showing that
\mathcal{T}^{\textup{W}}
is a self-mapping and contraction on
B_{R}^{\textup{W}}
, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 5
.

Let assumption
(A_{1})
hold and let
\tau
,
c>0
, and
k\in\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore, let
r
and
T
be such that assumptions (
36
) and (
37
) hold for some
R>0
. Then for any initial data
(p_{0},p_{1},p_{2})\in X^{\textup{W}}_{0}
satisfying

\|\nabla p_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta p_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta p_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq r^{2}

(38)

and any
\delta\in[0,\bar{\delta}]
, there exists a unique solution
p\in X^{\textup{W}}
of the corresponding initial boundary-value problem for the JMGT–Westervelt equation (
9
), where
X^{\textup{W}}
is defined in (
14
). The solution
p
satisfies the estimate

\displaystyle\|\nabla p_{tt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta p_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta p(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(39)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\tau)e^{K(\tau)(R^{2}+1)T}\left(\|\nabla p_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta p_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta p_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\right),

where the constants
C(\tau)
and
K(\tau)
tend to
+\infty
as
\tau\to 0^{+}
, but are independent of
\delta
.

Proof.

The proof follows by relying on the Banach Fixed-point theorem in combination with the linear result of Proposition
1
. Indeed, by employing estimates (
15
)
and (
23
)
with
\alpha=1-kq
,
\mu=kq_{t}
,
\eta=0
,
f=0
, and using
W^{1,\infty}(0,T;{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)})\subseteq X^{\textup{W}},
one immediately sees that
\mathcal{T}^{\textup{W}}
is a well-defined self-mapping on
B_{R}^{\textup{W}}
, provided (
36
) holds.

We next prove that
\mathcal{T}^{\textup{W}}
is strictly contractive with respect to the norm on the weaker space
E
, defined in (
29
). To this end, we take
q^{(1)}
and
q^{(2)}
in
B_{R}^{W}
and use the short-hand notation
p^{(1)}=\mathcal{T}^{\textup{W}}q^{(1)}
and
p^{(2)}=\mathcal{T}^{\textup{W}}q^{(2)}
. We also introduce the differences
\overline{p}=p^{(1)}-p^{(2)}
, and
\overline{q}=q^{(1)}-q^{(2)}.
Then we know that
\overline{p}
solves the linear equation

\tau\overline{p}_{ttt}+(1-kq^{(1)})\overline{p}_{tt}-c^{2}\Delta\overline{p}-b\Delta\overline{p}_{t}-kq_{t}^{(1)}\overline{p}_{t}=k\overline{q}_{t}p^{(2)}_{t}+k\overline{q}p^{(2)}_{tt}

(40)

and has zero initial conditions.
Estimate (
16
) with
\alpha=1-kq^{(1)}
,
\mu=kq_{t}^{(1)}
,
\eta=0
, and
f=k\overline{q}_{t}p^{(2)}_{t}+k\overline{q}p^{(2)}_{tt}
yields the bound

\displaystyle\|\overline{p}\|_{\textup{E}}\leq

\displaystyle\,\begin{multlined}\sqrt{C(\tau)\exp(K(\tau)(R^{2}+1)T)}|k|\Bigl(C_{H^{2},L^{\infty}}\|\Delta p^{(2)}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}\|\overline{q}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}\\
+C_{H^{1},L^{4}}^{2}\|\nabla p^{(2)}_{tt}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}\|\nabla\overline{q}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}\Bigr)\leq\theta\|\overline{q}\|_{\textup{E}}.\end{multlined}

Above, since
\overline{q}(0)=0
and
\overline{q}_{t}(0)=0
, we have exploited the bound

\|\overline{q}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}+\|\nabla\overline{q}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}\leq\sqrt{T}\|\overline{q}\|_{\textup{E}}.

(43)

We also know that

\|\Delta p^{(2)}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}+\|\nabla p^{(2)}_{tt}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}\leq\sqrt{C(\tau)\exp(K(\tau)(R^{2}+1)T)}\,r.

(44)

Thus we obtain strict contractivity provided condition (
37
) holds.

Note that the space
B_{R}^{\textup{W}}
with the metric induced by the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\textup{E}}
is a closed subset of a complete normed space. This follows from
B_{R}^{\textup{W}}
being a ball and thus weakly closed in
X^{\textup{W}}
. Therefore, any Cauchy sequence with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\textup{E}}
converges to some
y\in\textup{E}
and has a weakly-
\star
in
X^{\textup{W}}
convergent subsequence with limit
x\in B_{R}^{\textup{W}}
. Since the limits are unique, it must be
y=x\in B_{R}^{\textup{W}}
.

The claim then follows by Banach’s Fixed-point theorem, which at first yields a unique solution in
B_{R}^{\textup{W}}
. Uniqueness in
X^{\textup{W}}
follows by arguing by contradiction and employing the stability estimate on the resulting equation for the difference of two solutions; see, for example,
[
53
, Theorem 2.5.1]
for similar arguments.

Remark 6
(Small data or short time for the JMGT–Westervelt equation).

To satisfy conditions (
36
) and (
37
), we can either make the radius
r
of the initial data small or the final time
T
short. Let
C(\Omega,\tau,k)=(C_{H^{2},L^{\infty}}+C_{H^{1},L^{4}}^{2})C(\tau)|k|
. On the one hand, for given
T
, we can read these two conditions as

r\leq\frac{R}{\sqrt{C(\tau)\exp(K(\tau)(R^{2}+1)T)}},\quad r<\frac{1}{C(\Omega,\tau,k)\exp(K(\tau)(R^{2}+1)T)\sqrt{T}}

(45)

for some
R>0
. They can always be satisfied by, e.g., setting
R=1
and choosing
r>0
small enough. Note that this smallness condition can be weakened by maximizing the right-hand sides in (
45
) with respect to
R
.

On the other hand, for given
r>0
, conditions (
36
) and (
37
) can be satisfied by choosing short enough final time so that

\displaystyle T<\left(C(\Omega,\tau,k)\,r\right)^{-2},

(46)

\displaystyle T\leq\min\left\{1,\,\min\{\ln(R^{2}/(r^{2}C(\tau))),\,\ln(1/(rC(\Omega,\tau,k)))\}/(K(\tau(R^{2}+1))\right\}

for some
R>r\sqrt{C(\tau)}
. Again, the radius can simply be fixed to, for example,
R=r\sqrt{C(\tau)}+1.
A more sophisticated approach would be to optimize it to make the upper bounds in (
46
) as large as possible. The short-time setting might be more preferable having in mind applications of nonlinear ultrasonic waves, where the data are often smooth, but not necessarily small; see, for example,
[
21
, §14.6]
for the numerical modeling of high-intensity ultrasonic waves used in lithotripsy.

We are now ready to prove a convergence result for the nonlinear equation (
9
)
,
as the sound diffusivity vanishes.

Let

for the solutions of (

, as well as

, respectively, and prove that

5
The linearized JMGT–Kuznetsov equation

We continue by investigating a linearization of the JMGT–Kuznetsov equation given by

\tau\psi_{ttt}+\alpha\psi_{tt}-(\delta+\tau c^{2})\Delta\psi_{t}-c^{2}\Delta\psi=\,\sigma\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\psi_{t}

(49a)

with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions and initial conditions

\displaystyle(\psi,\psi_{t},\psi_{tt})|_{t=0}=(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2}),

(49b)

where now the coefficient in front of the second time derivative is given by
\alpha=1-\kappa\phi_{t}.

Since the JMGT–Kuznetsov equation has a quadratic gradient nonlinearity, we will need to obtain uniform bounds for
\|\nabla\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{\infty})}
and
\|\nabla\psi_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{\infty})}
in the course of the analysis. Our goal in this section is thus to derive a higher-order energy bound for the linearization (
49
) that is uniform with respect to
\delta
and will later allow us to derive the corresponding bound for the nonlinear equation. To this end, we strengthen our previous assumptions on the regularity of the coefficients and initial data.

(
\mathcal{A}_{2}
)

The coefficient
\phi
is sufficiently smooth so that

\phi\in L^{\infty}(0,T;{H_{\diamondsuit}^{3}(\Omega)})\cap W^{1,\infty}(0,T;{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)}),

(50)

and uniformly bounded so that
\|\phi\|_{W^{1,\infty}({H_{\diamondsuit}^{3}(\Omega)})},\,\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}({H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)})}\leq R
for some positive constant
R
, independent of
\delta
. This further implies

\alpha\in W^{1,\infty}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))\subseteq L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))\mbox{ with }\underline{\alpha}\leq\alpha\leq\overline{\alpha}

(51)

for
\underline{\alpha}=1-|\kappa|C_{H^{2},L^{\infty}}R
and
\overline{\alpha}=1+|\kappa|C_{H^{2},L^{\infty}}R
.

(
\mathcal{A}_{3}
)

The initial conditions (
49b
) satisfy

(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\in X^{\textup{K}}_{0}={H_{\diamondsuit}^{3}(\Omega)}\times{H_{\diamondsuit}^{3}(\Omega)}\times{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)}.

Note that also here we do not impose a non-degeneracy assumption on
\alpha
.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\delta\in[0,\overline{\delta}]
and
\tau
,
c>0
. Furthermore, let assumptions
(A_{1})
and
(
\mathcal{A}_{2}
)
–
(
\mathcal{A}_{3}
)
hold. Then problem (
49
) has a unique solution

\displaystyle\psi\in X^{\textup{K}}=W^{3,\infty}(0,T;H_{0}^{1}(\Omega))\cap W^{2,\infty}(0,T;{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)})\cap W^{1,\infty}(0,T;{H_{\diamondsuit}^{3}(\Omega)}),

(52)

which satisfies

\displaystyle\|\nabla\psi_{ttt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta\psi_{tt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(53)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(T,\tau,R)(\|\Delta\psi_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}),

where the constant
C(T,\tau,R)
tends to
+\infty
as
T\to+\infty
or
\tau\to 0^{+}
, but is independent of
\delta
.

Proof.

The proof can be carried out as before by employing smooth Faedo–Galerkin approximations in space, where we project the problem onto the span
V_{n}
of the first
n
eigenfunctions of the Dirichlet Laplacian pointwise in time. We will again focus our attention on deriving the crucial energy bound for the Galerkin approximations
\psi^{n}
. For ease of notation, we drop the superscript
n
below.

We test (
49a
) with
\Delta^{2}\psi_{tt}
and integrate in space. Since
\psi=\Delta\psi=0
on
\partial\Omega
for our Galerkin approximations, the following identities hold:

\displaystyle(\alpha\psi_{tt},\Delta^{2}\psi_{tt})_{L^{2}}=

\displaystyle(\Delta[\alpha\psi_{tt}],\Delta\psi_{tt})_{L^{2}}

(54)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\alpha\Delta\psi_{tt}+\psi_{tt}\Delta\alpha+2\nabla\alpha\cdot\nabla\psi_{tt},\Delta\psi_{tt})_{L^{2}},

\displaystyle-c^{2}(\Delta\psi,\Delta^{2}\psi_{tt})_{L^{2}}=

\displaystyle c^{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}(\nabla\Delta\psi,\nabla\Delta\psi_{t})_{L^{2}}-c^{2}\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}.

We thus arrive at the energy identity:

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\frac{1}{2}\tau\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\|\Delta\psi_{tt}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\delta+\tau c^{2})\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\end{multlined}

(55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}-(\alpha\Delta\psi_{tt}+\psi_{tt}\Delta\alpha+2\nabla\alpha\cdot\nabla\psi_{tt},\Delta\psi_{tt})_{L^{2}}-c^{2}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}(\nabla\Delta\psi,\nabla\Delta\psi_{t})_{L^{2}}\\
+c^{2}\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\sigma(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\psi_{t},\Delta^{2}\psi_{tt})_{L^{2}}.\end{multlined}

We next integrate in time and estimate the terms arising on the right-hand side. The
\alpha
terms can be estimated in the usual manner by utilizing Hölder’s inequality and the uniform boundedness of
\alpha
on account of assumption
(\mathcal{A}_{2})
. We further have

\displaystyle c^{2}(\nabla\Delta\psi(t),\nabla\Delta\psi_{t}(t))_{L^{2}}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}Tc^{4}\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(56)

where
\varepsilon\in(0,\tau c^{2}/2)
. Since
\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\psi_{t}=0
on
\partial\Omega
by the semi-discrete PDE, we have

\displaystyle(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\psi_{t},\Delta^{2}\psi_{tt})_{L^{2}}=

\displaystyle(\nabla\Delta\phi\cdot\nabla\psi_{t}+2D^{2}\phi:D^{2}\psi_{t}+\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\Delta\psi_{t},\Delta\psi_{tt})_{L^{2}}

(57)

where
D^{2}v=(\partial_{x_{i}}\partial_{x_{j}}v)_{i,j}
denotes the Hessian. By elliptic regularity, the Hessian satisfies
\|D^{2}v\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{\textup{H}}\|\Delta v\|_{L^{2}}

and so we can rely on the following bound:

\|D^{2}v\|_{L^{4}}\leq C_{H^{1},L^{4}}(\|D^{3}v\|_{L^{2}}+\|D^{2}v\|_{L^{2}})\leq C_{H^{1},L^{4}}C_{\textup{H}}(\|\nabla\Delta v\|_{L^{2}}+\|\Delta v\|_{L^{2}}).

(58)

Thus, together with assumption
(\mathcal{A}_{2})
on the uniform boundedness of
\phi
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}(\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\psi_{t},\Delta^{2}\psi_{tt})_{L^{2}}\,\textup{d}s\right|\leq\,\begin{multlined}\|\Delta\psi_{tt}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}R\left\{\vphantom{C_{H^{1},L^{4}}^{2}}\|\nabla\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{\infty})}+\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}\right.\\
\left.+4C_{H^{1},L^{4}}^{2}C^{2}_{\textup{H}}(\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}+\|\Delta\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})})\right\}.\end{multlined}

(59)

Employing the derived bounds within (
55
) after integration in time yields

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\frac{1}{2}\tau\|\Delta\psi_{tt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\tau c^{2}-\varepsilon)\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\end{multlined}

(60)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\|\Delta\psi_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+R^{2}(\|\psi_{tt}\|_{L^{2}(H^{2})}^{2}+\|\nabla\psi_{tt}\|_{L^{2}(L^{4})}^{2})\\
\hskip-14.22636pt+\|\Delta\psi_{tt}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}+\left.R^{2}\|\nabla\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{\infty})}^{2}+(R^{2}+T)\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}\right.+R^{2}\|\Delta\psi_{t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}.\end{multlined}

Note that by elliptic regularity
\|\nabla\psi_{tt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\psi_{tt}(t)\|_{H^{2}}\leq C\|\Delta\psi_{tt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}.

An application of Gronwall’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\|\Delta\psi_{tt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\,C(T,\tau,R)(\|\Delta\psi_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}).

(61)

We can obtain an additional uniform bound on
\|\nabla\psi_{ttt}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}
by testing with
-\Delta\psi_{ttt}
, and relying on the assumptions on
\phi
. Standard compactness arguments allow us to carry over the result to the solution of (
49
). Note that from
\psi\in X^{\textup{K}}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\psi\in\,C([0,T];{H_{\diamondsuit}^{3}(\Omega)}),\ \psi_{t}\in\,C_{w}([0,T];{H_{\diamondsuit}^{3}(\Omega)}),\ \psi_{tt}\in\,C_{w}([0,T];{H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)});

(62)

cf.
[
42
, Lemma 3.3]
. Thus
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are attained weakly.

For
f\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, we also briefly consider the linearization with a source term

\tau\psi_{ttt}+\alpha\psi_{tt}-(\delta+\tau c^{2})\Delta\psi_{t}-c^{2}\Delta\psi=\,\sigma\nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\psi_{t}+f,

(63)

under the same assumptions on
\phi
. Formally testing with
\psi_{tt}
and integrating over space and time, and employing standard computations leads to

\displaystyle\|\psi_{tt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\psi_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(64)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(T,\tau,R)(\|\psi_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|f\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}),

which we will rely on in the upcoming proof.

6
Uniform bounds for the JMGT–Kuznetsov equation and the inviscid limit

The goal of this section is to investigate the behavior of solutions to equation (
7
) as
\delta\rightarrow 0^{+}
. As before, our work plan is to derive uniform bounds for a linearization and then relate them to the nonlinear model via a fixed-point argument. We thus introduce the mapping

\mathcal{T}^{\textup{K}}:\phi\mapsto\psi,
where we take
\phi
from a suitably chosen ball in the space
X^{\textup{K}}
and
\psi
as the solution of the linear equation (
49a
) with initial data

(\psi(0),\psi_{t}(0),\psi_{tt})=(\phi(0),\phi_{t}(0),\phi_{tt}(0))=(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})

(65)

and
\alpha=1-\kappa\phi_{t}
. We next prove a small-data well-posedness result for (
7
).

Theorem 9
.

Let assumption
(A_{1})
hold and let
\tau
,
c>0
and
k\in\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore, let
T>0
be given. Then there exists
r>0
, such that for any initial data
(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\in X^{\textup{K}}_{0}
satisfying

\|\psi_{2}\|_{H^{2}}^{2}+\|\psi_{1}\|_{H^{3}}^{2}+\|\psi_{0}\|_{H^{3}}^{2}\leq r^{2},

(66)

and any
\delta\in[0,\bar{\delta}]
, there exists a unique solution
\psi\in X^{\textup{K}}
of the corresponding initial boundary-value problem for the JMGT–Kuznetsov equation (
7
). Furthermore, the solution fulfills the estimate

\displaystyle\|\nabla\psi_{ttt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\Delta\psi_{tt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(67)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(T,\tau)(\|\Delta\psi_{2}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{1}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\Delta\psi_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}),

where the constant
C(T,\tau)
tends to
+\infty
as
T\to\infty
or
\tau\to 0^{+}
, but is independent of
\delta
.

Proof.

Let
R>0
. Take
\phi\in B_{R}^{\textup{K}}
, where

\displaystyle B_{R}^{\textup{K}}=\{\ \phi\in X^{\textup{K}}:\ \|\phi\|_{X^{\textup{K}}}\leq R,\ (\phi(0),\phi_{t}(0),\phi_{tt}(0))=(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\,\}.

(68)

Then
\|\phi\|_{W^{1,\infty}({H_{\diamondsuit}^{3}(\Omega)})},\,\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}({H_{\diamondsuit}^{2}(\Omega)})}\leq R.

Thus on account of Proposition
8
, the mapping
\mathcal{T}^{\textup{K}}
is well-defined. Furthermore, by (
53
), it is a self-mapping provided
r
is chosen so that
0<r\leq R/\sqrt{C(T,\tau,R)}.

To show strict contractivity in the energy norm, we take
\phi^{(1)},\phi^{(2)}\in B_{R}^{\textup{K}}
and set
\psi^{(1)}=\mathcal{T}^{\textup{K}}\phi^{(1)}
and
\psi^{(2)}=\mathcal{T}^{\textup{K}}\phi^{(2)}
. Then
\overline{\psi}=\psi^{(1)}-\psi^{(2)}
solves

\displaystyle\tau\overline{\psi}_{ttt}+(1-\kappa\phi_{t}^{(1)})\overline{\psi}_{tt}-(\delta+\tau c^{2})\Delta\overline{\psi}_{t}-c^{2}\Delta\overline{\psi}-\sigma\nabla\phi^{(2)}\cdot\nabla\overline{\psi}_{t}

(69)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa\overline{\phi}_{t}\psi^{(2)}_{tt}+\sigma\nabla\overline{\phi}\cdot\nabla\psi^{(1)}_{t}

and satisfies zero initial conditions. This equation corresponds to (
63
) if we set

f=\kappa\overline{\phi}_{t}\psi^{(2)}_{tt}+\sigma\nabla\overline{\phi}\cdot\nabla\psi^{(1)}_{t}

(70)

together with
\alpha=1-\kappa\phi_{t}^{(1)}
. Using estimate (
64
) thus yields

\displaystyle\|\overline{\psi}_{tt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\overline{\psi}_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\nabla\overline{\psi}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C(T,\tau,R)\|f\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}

(71)

and then it remains to estimate the
f
term. By Hölder’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}\leq

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}|\kappa|\|\psi_{tt}^{(2)}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{4})}\|\overline{\phi}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{4})}+|\sigma|\|\nabla\psi_{t}^{(1)}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{\infty})}\|\nabla\overline{\phi}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}.\end{multlined}

(72)

Furthermore,

\|\psi_{tt}^{(2)}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{4})}\|\overline{\phi}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{4})}\leq\,C_{H^{1},L^{4}}^{2}\|\nabla\psi_{tt}^{(2)}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}T\|\nabla\overline{\phi}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}.

By additionally noting that
\|\nabla\overline{\phi}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}\leq T\|\nabla\overline{\phi}_{t}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}
, it further follows that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}\lesssim

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}2\kappa^{2}C_{H^{1},L^{4}}^{4}\|\nabla\psi_{tt}^{(2)}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}^{2}T^{2}\|\nabla\overline{\phi}_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}^{2}\\
\hskip 56.9055pt+2\sigma^{2}\|\nabla\psi_{t}^{(1)}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{\infty})}^{2}T^{2}\|\nabla\overline{\phi_{t}}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}.\end{multlined}

(73)

Employing this bound in (
71
) and relying on Gronwall’s inequality leads to

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\|\overline{\psi}_{tt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}+\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\|\nabla\overline{\psi}_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}

(74)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}C(T,R)(\|\nabla\psi_{tt}^{(2)}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}+\|\nabla\psi_{t}^{(1)}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{\infty})})T\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\|\nabla\overline{\phi}_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}.\end{multlined}

Note that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in(0,T)}\|\nabla\overline{\phi}_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\overline{\phi}\|_{\textup{E}}
. By (
53
), we know that

\|\nabla\psi_{tt}^{(2)}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}+\|\nabla\psi_{t}^{(1)}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{\infty})}\leq\sqrt{\tilde{C}(T,\tau,R)}\,r

(75)

for some
\tilde{C}(T,\tau,R)>0
, independent of
\delta
. Thus we can achieve strict contractivity of
\mathcal{T}^{\textup{K}}
in the energy norm by reducing
r
. We can reason as in the proof of Theorem
5
concerning
B_{R}^{\textup{K}}
being closed with respect to the metric induced by
\|\cdot\|_{\textup{E}}
and establish our claim by employing the Banach Fixed-point theorem.

Remark 10
(Small data or short time for the JMGT–Kuznetsov equation).

Like in the JMGT–Westervelt case (cf. Remark
6
), instead of choosing the maximal magnitude of the initial data
r
small enough for fixed final time
T
, we could have also achieved the self-mapping and contractivity properties needed for proving Theorem
9
by choosing
T
small enough, given initial data that is smooth but of arbitrary size.

We conclude our theoretical investigations by proving a convergence result for the JMGT–Kuznetsov equation (
7
). Note that as a by-product, we also obtain a convergence result for the JMGT–Westervelt equation in potential form (
8
) by setting
\kappa=\frac{1}{c^{2}}\left(1+\frac{B}{2A}\right)
and
\sigma=0
in (
7
) .

Theorem 11
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
9
hold. Then the family of solutions
\{\psi^{(\delta)}\}_{\delta>0}
to the JMGT–Kuznetsov equation (
7
) converges in the topology induced by the energy norm for the wave equation at a linear rate to the solution
\psi
of the inviscid JMGT–Kuznetsov equation as
\delta\to 0^{+}
.

Proof.

Let
\delta
,
\delta^{\prime}\in[0,\overline{\delta}]
. The difference equation for
\overline{\psi}=\psi^{(\delta)}-\psi^{(\delta^{\prime})}
is given by

\displaystyle\tau\overline{\psi}_{ttt}+(1-\kappa\psi_{t}^{(\delta)})\overline{\psi}_{tt}-(\delta+\tau c^{2})\Delta\overline{\psi}_{t}-c^{2}\Delta\overline{\psi}

(76)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\kappa\overline{\psi}_{t}\psi^{(\delta^{\prime})}_{tt}+(\delta-\delta^{\prime})\Delta\psi_{t}^{(\delta^{\prime})}+\sigma\nabla\overline{\psi}\cdot\nabla\psi_{t}^{(\delta)}+\sigma\nabla\psi^{(\delta^{\prime})}\cdot\nabla\overline{\psi}_{t}

with
(\overline{\psi},\overline{\psi}_{t},\overline{\psi}_{tt})|_{t=0}=(0,0,0)
. Testing this equation with
\overline{\psi}_{tt}
and integrating over space and time leads to

\displaystyle\frac{\tau}{2}\|\overline{\psi}_{tt}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+\|\sqrt{\alpha}\,\overline{\psi}_{tt}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}+\frac{\delta+\tau c^{2}}{2}\|\nabla\overline{\psi}_{t}(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

(77)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{multlined}\kappa\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\psi_{tt}^{(\delta^{\prime})}\overline{\psi}_{t}\overline{\psi}_{tt}\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}s+\sigma\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\overline{\psi}\cdot\nabla\psi_{t}^{(\delta)}+\nabla\psi^{(\delta^{\prime})}\cdot\nabla\overline{\psi}_{t})\overline{\psi}_{tt}\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}s\\
+(\delta-\delta^{\prime})\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\Delta\psi_{t}^{(\delta^{\prime})}\overline{\psi}_{tt}\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}s-c^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\nabla\overline{\psi}\cdot\nabla\overline{\psi}_{tt}\,\textup{d}x\textup{d}s,\end{multlined}

where now
\alpha=1-\kappa\psi_{t}^{(\delta)}
. We can proceed similarly to the proof of contractivity in Theorem
9
to arrive at the estimate

\sup_{t\in(0,T)}E[\overline{\psi}](t)\lesssim(\delta-\delta^{\prime})^{2}\|\Delta\psi_{t}^{(\delta^{\prime})}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}.

(78)

Setting
\delta^{\prime}
to zero yields the claimed convergence rate.

Remark 12
(Different boundary conditions).

It should be said that, to model ultrasonic propagation in realistic settings, acoustic equations are in practice often considered with Neumann excitation and possibly absorbing boundary conditions to avoid non-physical reflections; see, e.g.,
[
21
, §5]
. We expect our analysis to carry over in a relatively straightforward manner to problems with Neumann conditions, under suitable assumptions on the data. The case of having both Neumann and absorbing conditions on disjoint parts of the boundary is more delicate due to the fact that our energy analysis would imply only
H^{3/2+s}
regularity in space of the solution for
s\in[0,1/2)
. We refer the reader to
[
20
, §6]
, where the analysis of the JMGT–Westervelt equation is performed with Neumann and the lowest-order Engquist–Majda boundary conditions for
\delta>0
fixed and vanishing thermal relaxation time. We would thus expect that the arguments of
[
20
, §6]
can be adapted to the present setting of vanishing sound diffusivity for the generalized MGT and JMGT–Westervelt equations, whereas it does not seem immediately feasible to extend these considerations to the JMGT–Kuznetsov model.

Remark 13
(Propagation through tissue-like media).

In different biomedical applications, including lithotripsy, ultrasonic waves propagate through both fluidic and tissue-like (heterogeneous) media. It is well-known that in tissues , the attenuation of the wave obeys a power law with respect to the frequency; we refer to the book
[
14
]
for a detailed insight into these phenomena. These effects are typically modeled by space- or time-fractional wave equations; see, for example,
[
14
, §7, Eq. (7.8)]
. It is thus of clear interest to involve fractional propagation in the future analytical and numerical investigations.

Discussion and outlook

In this paper, we have investigated the limiting behavior of the third-order JMGT equations and their linearizations, as the sound diffusivity vanishes. The analysis also included well-posedness of the respective limiting equations as well as uniform
\delta
-independent energy estimates. From our estimates and even more clearly from our numerical experiments, it is apparent that there is a rather involved interplay among the diffusivity parameter
\delta
, the relaxation time
\tau
, and the nonlinearity (determined by the parameters
\kappa
and
\sigma
). Analytic and further numerical studies of this interaction will, therefore, be the subject of further research.
