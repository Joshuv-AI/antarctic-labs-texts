Computing Equilibria in Binary Networked Public Goods Games
Sixie Yu, Kai Zhou, P. Jeffrey Brantingham, Yevgeniy Vorobeychik

Abstract
Public goods games study the incentives of individuals to contribute to a public good and their behaviors in equilibria. In this paper, we examine a specific type of public goods game where players are networked and each has binary actions, and focus on the algorithmic aspects of such games. First, we show that checking the existence of a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium is NP-complete. We then identify tractable instances based on restrictions of either utility functions or of the underlying graphical structure. In certain cases, we also show that we can efficiently compute a socially optimal Nash equilibrium. Finally, we propose a heuristic approach for computing approximate equilibria in general binary networked public goods games, and experimentally demonstrate its effectiveness.

Introduction

Public goods games have been a major subject of inquiry as a natural way to model the tension between individual interest and social good
[
13
,
19
]
.
In a canonical version of such games, individuals choose the amount to invest in a public good, and the subsequent value of the good, which is determined by total investment of the community, is then shared equally by all.
A number of versions of this game have been studied, including variants where players are situated on a network, with individual payoffs determined solely by the actions of their network neighbors
[
2
]
.

An important variation of networked public goods games treats investment decisions as
binary

[
10
]
.
One motivating example is crime reporting, known to vary widely, with the general observation that crime is considerably under-reported
[
18
]
.
In a game theoretic abstraction of crime reporting, individuals choose whether or not to report crimes, and benefits accrue to the broader community, for example, causing a reduction in overall crime rate.
Another example of binary public goods games is vaccination. In this example, parents decide whether to vaccinate their children, with herd immunity becoming the public good.
To keep terminology general, we will refer to the binary decision whether or not to
invest
in a public good (thus, reporting a crime and vaccinating are forms of such investment).

A special case of binary public goods games (BNPGs), commonly known as
best-shot games
, has received some prior attention
[
6
,
10
,
14
,
16
]
.
However, best-shot games make a strong assumption about the structure of the player utility functions.
While
Levit et al. 2018
recently showed that equilibria for best-shot games can be computed in polynomial time by best response dynamics, these may fail to find social welfare-optimal equilibria, and nothing is known about BNPGs more generally.

We study the problem of computing pure strategy Nash equilibria in general binary public goods games on networks.
We provide examples to show that a pure strategy Nash equilibria is not guaranteed to exist for general BNPGs.
Furthermore, we show that even determining if a pure strategy Nash equilibrium exists is hard in general.
However, while pure strategy equilibria need not in general exist, and are hard to compute, we exhibit a number of positive results for important special cases.
One class of special cases pertains to binary public goods games for completely connected networks (communities), in which case an equilibrium can be found efficiently if it exists.
Similarly, we can efficiently find a pure strategy equilibrium in general BNPGs when the graph is a tree.
If we further restrict the externalities to have an identical impact on all players (we call this the
homogeneous
case), we can characterize
all
pure strategy equilibria, and efficiently compute a socially optimal equilibrium.
Moreover, if both investment costs and externalities are identical (termed the
fully homogeneous
case), we can characterize
all
pure strategy equilibria for arbitrary networks.
Finally, we present a heuristic approach for finding approximate equilibria to tackle general BNPGs, and experimentally demonstrate that it is highly effective.

Model

A Binary Networked Public Goods game (henceforth BNPG game) is characterized by a graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
; throughout this chapter we assume that
\mathcal{G}
is simple, undirected, and loop-free.
The players consist of the node set
\mathcal{V}=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
; the edge set
\mathcal{E}=\{(i,j)\mid i,j\in\mathcal{V}\}
represents the interdependencies among the players.
Let
x_{i}\in\{0,1\}
be player
i
’s action; we can think of
x_{i}=1
(resp.
x_{i}=0
) as the player decides to invest (resp. not invest) in some public goods.
The action profile of all players is represented by
\bm{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and the action profile of all players other than player
i
is
\bm{x}_{-i}
.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{i}=\{j\in\mathcal{V}\mid(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}\}
be the neighbors of
i
.
Define
n_{i}^{(\bm{x})}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{i}}x_{j}
as the number of
i
’s neighbors who choose to invest, e.g., in the crime reporting scenario
n_{i}^{(\bm{x})}
represents the number of
i
’s neighbors who report crimes.
Reporting crime is costly, e.g., time consuming or even dangerous.
We capture the cost of each player with a constant
c_{i}
.
Player
i
’s utility function is defined below

u_{i}(\bm{x})=u_{i}(x_{i},\bm{x}_{-i})=u_{i}(x_{i},n_{i}^{(\bm{x})})=g_{i}(x_{i}+n_{i}^{(\bm{x})})-c_{i}x_{i},

(1)

where
g_{i}(x_{i}+n_{i}^{(\bm{x})})
captures the positive externality that
i
experiences from her neighbors’ (and her own) investment.
As is standard in public goods literature, we assume that
g_{i}
is a
non-decreasing
function; in the case of crime reporting this means that higher reporting reduces crime rate.
Our definition of
g_{i}
generalizes the one given by
Bramoullé and Kranton 2007
; they assume that
g_{i}
is a twice differentiable strictly concave function, which may be violated in some scenarios.
For example, studies of incentives in P2P systems entail natural models in which
g_{i}
is an S-shaped function
[
3
]
; commonly considered best-shot games model
g_{i}
as a step function
[
10
]
;
g_{i}
may be convex as in models of social unrest
[
5
]
.
Our definition of
g_{i}
, on the other hand, is flexible enough to model the aforementioned scenarios.
Notice that each function
g_{i}
can be represented using
O(n)
values, so the entire BNPG game (including the graph structure) can be represented using
O(n^{2})
values.
A useful observation is that player
i
chooses to invest only if
u_{i}(1,n_{i}^{(\bm{x})})\geq u_{i}(0,n_{i}^{(\bm{x})})
,
which is rewritten as

\Delta g_{i}(n_{i}^{(\bm{x})}):=g_{i}(n_{i}^{(\bm{x})}+1)-g_{i}(n_{i}^{(\bm{x})})\geq c_{i}.

(2)

The finite difference
\Delta g_{i}(n_{i}^{(\bm{x})})
will be useful in later analysis.
In general, a BNPG is called
heterogeneous
if the players have different utility functions, i.e., different
c_{i}
and
g_{i}
.
As we discuss later, checking the existence of a Nash equilibrium in a heterogeneous BNPG is hard.
Due to the hardness result, we also study several special cases such that positive algorithmic results can be obtained; intuitively, we make different assumptions about the utility functions or the underlying graph.
For example, the two cases below restrict the externality functions or the cost parameters of the players.

Homogeneous BNPG:

The players share the same externality function, i.e.,
g_{1}=,\ldots,=g_{n}
.

Fully Homogeneous BNPG:

In addition to having the same externality function, the players share the same cost, i.e.,
c_{1}=,\ldots,=c_{n}
.

We use
\textsc{BNPG}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{U})
to represent an instance of BNPG with underlying graph
\mathcal{G}
; the set
\mathcal{U}
consists of the players’ utility functions, i.e.,
\mathcal{U}=\{u_{i}\mid i\in\mathcal{V}\}
, which contains all the information about
g_{i}
and
c_{i}
.
Given a BNPG instance, We seek
pure-strategy
Nash equilibria of the game.
A pure-strategy Nash equilibria (PSNE) is defined as follows:

Definition 2.1
.

Given
\textsc{BNPG}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{U})
,
a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium (PSNE) of the game is an action profile
\bm{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
such that for every player
u_{i}(x_{i},n_{i}^{(\bm{x})})\geq u_{i}(1-x_{i},n_{i}^{(\bm{x})})
.

We differentiate two types of PSNE: 1)
trivial
PSNEs, including
\bm{x}=\bm{1}
(i.e., all players invest) and
\bm{x}=\bm{0}
(i.e., no player invests) and 2)
non-trivial
PSNEs, i.e., any action profile
\bm{x}\notin\{\bm{0},\bm{1}\}
.
The formal definitions are as follows:

Definition 2.2
.

Given a BNPG, suppose at least one PSNE exists.
Let
\bm{x}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
be a PSNE.
If
\bm{x}\in\{\bm{0},\bm{1}\}
, it is a trivial PSNE; otherwise, it is a non-trivial PSNE.

Given a BNPG, suppose it has at least one non-trivial PSNE with
0<k<n
investing players.
We identify two types of players in the PSNE: 1) the players who
always
invest and 2) the players who
never
invest.
Intuitively, the two types of players either invest or do not invest, no matter what other players’ actions are.
The formal statement is as follows:

Proposition 2.1
.

Given a BNPG, suppose it has at least one non-trivial PSNE with
0<k<n
investing players.
Define the following two sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{+}(k):=\{i\in\mathcal{V}\mid c_{i}<\Delta g_{i}(k)\},

(3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{-}(k):=\{i\in\mathcal{V}\mid c_{i}>\Delta g_{i}(k-1)\}.

It follows that the set
\mathcal{I}_{+}(k)
includes the players who always invest while the set
\mathcal{I}_{-}(k)
the players who never invest; in addition, there is no
c_{i}
such that
\Delta g_{i}(k-1)<c_{i}<\Delta g_{i}(k)
.

Proof.

Suppose there is a player
j\in\mathcal{I}_{+}(k)
who may not invest; it follows that
u_{j}(0,k)\geq u_{j}(1,k)
and thus
c_{j}\geq\Delta g_{j}(k)
, which contradicts the definition of
\mathcal{I}_{+}(k)
.
Similarly, the set
\mathcal{I}_{-}(k)
includes the players who never invest.
The argument is similar: suppose a player
j\in\mathcal{I}_{-}(k)
who may invest; it follows that
u_{j}(1,k-1)\geq u_{j}(0,k-1)
and thus
\Delta g_{j}(k-1)\geq c_{i}
, a contradiction to the definition.
Since
\mathcal{I}_{+}(k)
is disjoint from
\mathcal{I}_{-}(k)
, we have that there is no
c_{i}
such that
\Delta g_{i}(k-1)<c_{i}<\Delta g_{i}(k)
.
∎

3
Heterogeneous BNPG

We first construct an example to show that a heterogeneous BNPG does not necessarily have a PSNE.
Moreover, we show that checking the existence of a PSNE is NP-complete.
Due to the hardness result, we restrict the game in several aspects in order to obtain positive algorithmic results.
In particular, we restrict the underlying graph
\mathcal{G}
to a complete graph or a tree, which leads to a polynomial-time algorithms to compute a PSNE (or conclude no one exists).
For general
\mathcal{G}
, we describe a heuristic algorithm to find an approximate PSNE.

We start by constructing a BNPG where no PSNE exists.
The game has two players
A
and
B
.
Let
u_{A}(x_{A},x_{B})=g_{A}(x_{A}+x_{B})-c_{A}x_{A}
and
u_{B}(x_{B},x_{A})=g_{B}(x_{B}+x_{A})-c_{B}x_{B}
be the utility functions of
A
and
B
, respectively.
Consider the setting where
g_{A}(0)=\epsilon
,
g_{A}(1)=c_{A}
,
g_{A}(2)=2c_{A}+\epsilon
, and
g_{B}(0)=\epsilon
,
g_{B}(1)=c_{B}+2\epsilon
,
g_{B}(2)=2c_{B}+\epsilon
, where
\epsilon\leq c_{A}
and
\epsilon\leq c_{B}
, ensuring that both
g_{A}
and
g_{B}
are non-decreasing.
By checking the four possible action profiles of the game, it is direct to verify that no PSNE exists.

Next, we show that checking if a heterogeneous BNPG has a PSNE is NP-complete.

Theorem 3.1
.

Given a heterogeneous BNPG, checking the existence of a PSNE is NP-complete.

Proof.

Given an action profile, it takes polynomial time to check if it is a PSNE, so the problem is in NP.
We construct a reduction from the

Given a graph

Consider the following best-response policy for the players:

\displaystyle i\in\mathcal{V}^{\prime}:x_{i}=\begin{cases}1,&n_{i}^{(\bm{x})}=0\\
0,&n_{i}^{(\bm{x})}\neq 0\end{cases}\text{ and }x_{t}=\begin{cases}1,&0<n_{t}^{(\bm{x})}<k\\

(4)

First, suppose we are given an independent set

Then node

connects
to every node in
\mathcal{G}
.
We check the remaining nodes in

We argue that all nodes in

For node

, it will not deviate since it has at least

Second, suppose we are given a PSNE

.
There must be some nodes choosing

it has no investing neighbor and it would prefer choosing

.
If the equilibrium is that all nodes choose

, we must have

; however, recall that

connects to all nodes in

, i.e.,

So the equilibrium cannot be that all nodes choose

Suppose in the equilibrium the nodes are divided into two sets

.
Further note that

cannot be in

. Because another node in

would prefer to choose

as it connects to

.
Thus, the nodes in

form an independent set.
For a node

in

.
Furthermore,

is an independent

Due to the hardness results presented in Theorem

3.1
Games on Complete Graphs

We assume that the underlying graph
\mathcal{G}
is complete.
For ease of exposition, we omit the parameterization of
\mathcal{U}
and an instance of BNPG is denoted by
\textsc{BNPG}(\mathcal{G})
.
We show that it takes polynomial time to find a PSNE or conclude no one exists.

First, we characterize the non-trivial PSNEs of a heterogeneous BNPG.

Proposition 3.2
.

Consider a heterogeneous
\textsc{BNPG}(\mathcal{G})
where
\mathcal{G}
is a complete graph.
The game has a non-trivial PSNE with
k\in(0,n)
investing players if and only if
1) there is no

\Delta g_{i}(k-1)<c_{i}<\Delta g_{i}(k)

|\mathcal{I}_{+}(k)|\leq k

|\mathcal{I}_{-}(k)|\leq n-k

|\{i\in\mathcal{V}\mid i\in\mathcal{V}\setminus(\mathcal{I}_{+}(k)\cup\mathcal{I}_{-}(k))\}|\geq k-|\mathcal{I}_{+}(k)|

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)

Suppose
\bm{x}
is a PSNE with
k\in(0,n)
investing players.
From Proposition
2.1
, for player
i
if
c_{i}<\Delta g_{i}(k)
the player must invest.
Thus, the number of players in
\mathcal{I}_{+}(k)
is at most
k
; otherwise, there would be more than
k
investing players.
Similarly, if
c_{i}>\Delta g_{i}(k-1)
the player never invests; thus, the number of players in the set
\mathcal{I}_{-}(k)
is at most
n-k
; otherwise, the number of players that can invest would be less than
k
.
From Proposition
2.1
, we know that there is no
c_{i}
such that
\Delta g_{i}(k-1)<c_{i}<\Delta g_{i}(k)
.
Let
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{V}\setminus(\mathcal{I}_{+}(k)\cup\mathcal{I}_{-}(k))
, i.e., the set of players who are indifferent between investing and not investing.
To have exactly
k
investing players, we need

\mathcal{T}
, which shows the lower bound on its cardinality.

(\Leftarrow)

We construct a PSNE with exactly
k
investing players.
First, let the players in
\mathcal{I}_{+}(k)
invest; they do not deviate as they always invest.
Then, we arbitrarily pick
k-|\mathcal{I}_{+}(k)|
players from
\mathcal{T}
and make them invest; they do not deviate as they are indifferent between investing and not investing.
Finally, let the remaining players not invest; they do not deviate as they are either indifferent between investing/not investing or never invest.
∎

Proposition
3.2
suggests the following algorithm to compute a PSNE or conclude that on one exists.
Given
\textsc{BNPG}(\mathcal{G})
, we first make sure there is not any
c_{i}
such that
\Delta g_{i}(k-1)<c_{i}<\Delta g_{i}(k)
; otherwise, we conclude that the game has no PSNE.
We then check if the game has any trivial PSNE.
Finally, by changing
k
from
1
to
n-1
, we check for each
k
if the game has a non-trivial PSNE with
k
investing players.
The algorithm runs in
O(n^{2})
time.

Thus, the range of
n^{\prime}_{k=0}
is
\{n^{1}_{k=0},n^{1}_{k=0}+1,\ldots,m-n^{0}_{k=0}\}
.
We obtain the range of
n^{\prime}_{k=1}
similarly.
Finally, we identify action profiles of
p
and
k
that are part of a PSNE, by substituting into (
5
) the four possibilities of
(x_{p},x_{k})
and the corresponding values of
n^{\prime}_{k}
conditioned on
x_{k}
.
If no
(x_{p},x_{k})
satisfies (
5
), we conclude that no PSNE exists.

Suppose node
k
is the root.
We compute the
best-response table
of
k
as follows.
Following the above procedure for an internal node, we compute
n^{\prime}_{k=0}
and
n^{\prime}_{k=1}
.
Then, we check if
x_{k}=0
(resp.
x_{k}=1
) is a best response by checking if
\Delta g_{k}(n^{\prime}_{k=0})\leq c_{k}
(resp.
\Delta g_{k}(n^{\prime}_{k=0})\geq c_{k}
) is satisfied.
Finally, we put
k
’s best responses into the table.
In summary, the downstream pass takes
O(d_{\text{max}}|\mathcal{V}|+|\mathcal{E}|)
time.

We show that the downstream pass does not miss any PSNE if there is one, by an inductive argument.
Suppose there is a PSNE that is missed by the downstream pass.
Then we must wrongly conclude that no PSNE exists at some node
k
as we move downward.
First, the node
k
cannot be a leaf node, since we exhaustively check the four possible action profiles of
k
and its parent.
Next, suppose the PSNE is not missed until an internal node
k
, in other words, we must wrongly exclude an action profile of
k
and its parent
p
.
However, we exhaustively check all combinations of
n^{\prime}_{k}
,
x_{k}
, and
x_{p}
, so it is not possible to miss such an action profile.
The same argument applies for the case where node
k
is the root.

In the upstream pass we traverse
\mathcal{G}
in a reversed depth-first order (start at the root and end the leaves).
We first select the action of the root.
If the root is indifferent between investing and not investing, we arbitrarily pick one.
Next, we sequentially determine each node’s action based on its parent’s action and the conditional best-response table passed to the parent (ties are broken arbitrarily).
This pass takes
O(|\mathcal{V}|)
time.
In conclusion, the total running time of
TreePSNE

is
O(d_{\text{max}}|\mathcal{V}|+|\mathcal{E}|)
.
∎

4
Homogeneous BNPG

In this section we study algorithmic issues of a
homogeneous
BNPG, i.e., all players share the same externality function
g
.
We construct an example to show that a PSNE may not exist.
In addition, we show that checking the existence of a non-trivial PSNE is NP-complete.
We then make further restrictions on either the underlying graph
\mathcal{G}
or the utility functions.
Specifically, when
\mathcal{G}
is a complete graph we show that it takes polynomial time to compute a socially optimal PSNE.
In addition, when the players share the same externality function
g
and the same cost
c
, the non-trivial PSNEs are characterized by
k
-cores of
\mathcal{G}
.
In the following discussion, we simplify
n_{i}^{(\bm{x})}
to
n_{i}
when the context is clear.

4.1
Existence of PSNE

We construct a game where a PSNE does not exist.
This game has three players
\{1,2,3\}
.
The underlying graph is a simple path with player
2
in the middle.
We set the costs as follows:
c_{1}=1
,
c_{2}=2
, and
c_{3}=3
.
The externality function satisfies that
\Delta g(0)=1.5
,
\Delta g(1)=3.5
, and
\Delta g(2)=0.5
; one way to realize such a function is
g(0)=4.5
,
g(1)=6
,
g(2)=9.5
, and
g(x)=10
for any
x\geq 3
, ensuring that
g
is non-decreasing.
By exhaustively checking the eight possible profiles, it is direct to verify that no PSNE exists.

Recall that a trivial PSNE is an action profile where either no player invests or all players invest.
Given a homogeneous BNPG, it is direct to check if the game has a trivial PSNE.
A non-trivial PSNE is an action profile where exactly
k
players invest with
0<k<n
.
The following theorem shows that checking the existence of a non-trivial PSNE is NP-complete, which is implied from Theorem
4.7
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Checking if a homogeneous BNPG has a non-trivial PSNE is NP-complete.

Next, we consider further restrictions that enable positive algorithmic results.
In particular, we consider two cases: 1) the underlying graph
\mathcal{G}
is a complete graph and 1) in addition to sharing the same externality function, the players share the same cost
c
.

4.2
Games on Complete Graphs

4.2.1
Computing a PSNE

Given an instance
\textsc{BNPG}(\mathcal{G})
where
\mathcal{G}
is a complete graph, we show that there always exists a PSNE and it can be computed in
O(n\log n)
time.
Recall from Proposition
3.2
, it takes
O(n^{2})
time to compute (or conclude no one exists) a PSNE of a heterogeneous
\textsc{BNPG}(\mathcal{G})
with a complete
\mathcal{G}
; the homogeneity improves the algorithm to
O(n\log n)
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Every homogeneous BNPG on a complete graph has a PSNE, which can be computed in
O(n\log n)
time.

Proof.

The proof is constructive.
Without loss of generality, suppose
c_{1}\leq c_{2}\leq\cdots\leq c_{n}
(we can start by sorting players in this order).
If
\Delta g(0)<c_{1}
, we immediately obtain a PSNE, i.e., no player invests; this is because for
i=1,\ldots,n
we have
u_{i}(0,n_{i})>u_{i}(1,n_{i})
where
n_{i}=0
.
If
\Delta g(n-1)>c_{n}
, the action profile that all players invest is a PSNE, since for
i=1,\ldots,n
we have
u_{i}(1,n_{i})>u_{i}(0,n_{i})
where
n_{i}=n-1
.
If neither of the two cases holds, we initialize
\bm{x}
to all-zeros. Since
c_{1}\leq\Delta g(0)
, we set
x_{1}=1
. For a subsequent player
i
, we set
x_{i}=1
if
c_{i}\leq\Delta g(n_{i})
. Note that at this point
n_{i}
is equal to the number of players with index smaller than
i
. We repeat this process for each player in ascending order of
c_{i}
until we found a certain player
m
such that
c_{m}>\Delta g(n_{m})
. We then set the decisions of player
m
and the subsequent players to
0
.
We show that the resulting action profile is a PSNE.

First, there are
m-1
investing players in the PSNE.
For player
m-1
, we have
c_{m-1}\leq\Delta g(n_{m-1})
by construction, so player
m-1
does not deviate from investing (also note that
n_{m-1}=m-2
).
For player
m
, we have
c_{m}>\Delta g(n_{m})
by construction, so player
m
keeps not investing (also note that
n_{m}=m-1
).
For any player
i
with
i<m-1
,
n_{i}
is equal to
(i-1)+(m-1-i)=m-2
.
The player does not deviate from investing since
c_{i}\leq c_{m-1}\leq\Delta g(n_{m-1})=\Delta g(m-2)=\Delta g(n_{i})
.
For any player
j
with
j>m
,
n_{j}
is equal to
m-1
.
The player keeps not investing since
c_{j}\geq c_{m}>\Delta g(n_{m})=\Delta g(m-1)=\Delta g(n_{j})
.
The time complexity of finding a PSNE is dominated by the
O(n\log n)
sorting of
c_{i}
.
∎

4.2.2
Computing Socially Optimal PSNE

Given an action profile
\bm{x}
, the social welfare (SW) is defined as the sum of all players’ utilities:

\text{SW}(\bm{x}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{u_{i}(x_{i},n_{i})}.

(6)

A common goal is to seek the PSNE that maximizes social welfare—a
socially optimal
PSNE.
Although the
SimpleSort
algorithm (i.e., Algorithm
4
) always finds a PSNE, it is not necessarily a socially optimal one.
We show in Theorem
4.3
that the socially optimal PSNE can be efficiently computed.

Theorem 4.3
.

Consider a homogeneous
\textsc{BNPG}(\mathcal{G})
where
\mathcal{G}
is a complete graph, it takes polynomial time to compute the socially optimal PSNE.

Proof.

It is direct to check if the game has any trivial PSNE and if so to compute the corresponding SW.
For a particular
0<k<n
, we call a PSNE with
k
investing players as a
k
-PSNE.
Given a
k
-PSNE, we know that
x_{i}+n_{i}=k
for all
i
since the underlying graph is complete.
The social welfare of a
k
-PSNE is re-written as follows

\text{SW}(\bm{x};k)=n\cdot g(k)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}x_{i}.

(7)

Next, we describe how to compute the socially optimal
k
-PSNE (if it exists):

•

Case 1: When
\Delta g(k)>\Delta g(k-1)
, if there is a
c_{i}
such that
\Delta g(k-1)<c_{i}<\Delta g(k)
then the
k
-PSNE does not exist.
Otherwise, let
\mathcal{I}_{+}(k)=\{i\mid c_{i}\leq\Delta g(k-1)\}
.
By Proposition
2.1
, the
k
-PSNE exists and is unique if and only if
|\mathcal{I}_{+}(k)|=k
.
If the
k
-PSNE exists, it is the socially optimal
k
-PSNE.

•

Case 2: When
\Delta g(k)\leq\Delta g(k-1)
, let
\mathcal{I}_{\times}(k):=\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{I}_{-}(k)
, i.e., the players that either always invest or are indifferent between investing and not investing.
The
k
-PSNE exists if and only if
|\mathcal{I}_{\times}(k)|\geq k
.
When the
k
-PSNE exists, the social welfare is maximized if we choose the
k
investing players as those with the smallest
c_{i}
, which can be identified by a simple sorting of
\mathcal{I}_{\times}(k)
with respect to
c_{i}
.

In summary, an algorithm to compute a socially optimal PSNE is as follows:
we compute the social welfare of each
k
-PSNE (if it exists) from
k=1
to
k=n-1
.
Then,if the game has any trivial PSNE, we compute the corresponding social welfare.
The socially optimal PSNE is obtained by comparing the above PSNEs with respect to their SW.
∎

4.3
Fully Homogeneous BNPG

A homogeneous BNPG is called fully homogeneous if the players share the same cost
c
.
Further, we restrict the function
g
to be strictly convex; as a result, a PSNE always exists and can be efficiently computed.
The convexity of
g
is essential for the positive results; without the convexity checking if a fully homogeneous BNPG has a non-trivial PSNE is NP-complete.

4.3.1
Strictly Convex
g

Given a fully homogeneous
\textsc{BNPG}(\mathcal{G})
, let
d_{\text{max}}
be the maximum degree of the underlying graph
\mathcal{G}
.
Since
g
is strictly convex, the first-order derivative is strictly increasing, i.e.,
\Delta g(0)<\Delta g(1)<\cdots<\Delta g(d_{\text{max}})
.
The following proposition characterizes the extreme case where a trivial PSNE is the only Nash equilibrium:

Proposition 4.4
.

If
c<\Delta g(0)
(resp.
c>\Delta g(d_{\text{max}})
), the action profile that all players invest (resp. no one invests) is the unique PSNE.

Proof.

Consider
c<\Delta g(0)
.
Suppose there is an action profile that some player
i
does not invest; this implies that
c\geq\Delta g(n_{i})
with
n_{i}\geq 0
; it follows that
c\geq\Delta g(n_{i})\geq\Delta g(0)
, which leads to a contradiction.
Next, consider
c>\Delta g(d_{\text{max}})
.
Suppose there is an action profile that some player
i
invests, which implies that
c\leq\Delta g(n_{i})
.
As
n_{i}
is at most
d_{\text{max}}
, it follows that
c\leq\Delta g(d_{\text{max}})
, which leads to a contradiction.
∎

Next, we connect non-trivial PSNEs of the game with
k
-cores of the underlying graph
\mathcal{G}
.
Specifically, a
k
-core of a graph is a
maximal
induced subgraph such that each node in the subgraph has degree at least
k
; we follow the definition as in
[
17
]
(Definition 4.1), which does not require a
k
-core to be connected.

Theorem 4.5
.

Given a fully homogeneous BNPG with strictly convex
g
.
Suppose
\bm{x}\notin\{\bm{0},\bm{1}\}
.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be the induced subgraph over the investing players.

\bm{x}
is a non-trivial PSNE if and only if
\mathcal{H}
is a
k
-core of the underlying graph with
k=\Delta g^{-1}(c)\geq 0
.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
)
Let
\mathcal{I}_{1}
be the (non-empty) set of investing players, i.e.,
\mathcal{I}_{1}=\{i\mid x_{i}=1\}
.
For any player
i\in\mathcal{I}_{1}
, it follows that
c\leq\Delta g(n_{i})
, or equivalently,
\Delta g^{-1}(c)\leq n_{i}
, where
\Delta g^{-1}(\cdot)
is the inverse function of
\Delta g
; it is well-defined due to the monotonicity of
\Delta g
.
As
\mathcal{H}
is the induced subgraph on the investing players, the degree of node
i\in\mathcal{H}
satisfies that
d_{\mathcal{H}}(i)=n_{i}\geq\Delta g^{-1}(c)
.
Let
k=\Delta g^{-1}(c)
.
We show that
k\geq 0
: suppose
k<0
, it follows that
\Delta g^{-1}(c)<0
and hence
c<\Delta g(0)
.
By Proposition
4.4
,
c<\Delta g(0)
implies that the only PSNE is a trivial one, which contradicts that
\bm{x}
is non-trivial.

(
\Leftarrow
)
Consider the action profile
\bm{x}
where the players in
\mathcal{H}
invest while the others do not invest.
We show that
\bm{x}
is a PSNE.
For any player
i\in\mathcal{H}
, its degree
d_{\mathcal{H}}(i)=n_{i}\geq\Delta g^{-1}(c)
; thus, we have
\Delta g(n_{i})\geq c
and the player does not deviate from investing.
For any player
j\notin\mathcal{H}
, its degree is less than
k
; otherwise it would be in
\mathcal{H}
; this implies that
n_{j}\leq d_{\mathcal{G}}(j)<k=\Delta g^{-1}(c)
; it follows that
c>\Delta g(n_{j})
, i.e., the player keeps not investing.
∎

As
\Delta g^{-1}(c)
is readily computable, we apply a simple pruning algorithm to find the
k
-core with
k=\Delta g^{-1}(c)
in polynomial time (or conclude no one exists); this implies that we can find a non-trivial PSNE (or conclude no one exists) in polynomial time.
The pruning algorithm iteratively removes the nodes with degree less than
k
until no more node can be removed.
If the remaining graph is not empty, it is the
k
-core and the node set consists of the investing players; otherwise, the
k
-core does not exist and the game does not have any non-trivial PSNE.
It is clearly that the pruning algorithm runs in polynomial time.

Corollary 4.6
.

Given a fully homogeneous BNPG with strictly convex
g
.
When
\Delta g(0)\leq c\leq\Delta g(d_{\text{max}})
, a trivial PSNE (i.e.,
\bm{x}=\bm{0}
) always exists. Besides, it takes polynomial time to find a non-trivial PSNE or conclude that no one exists.

Proof.

\bm{x}=\bm{0}
implies that
n_{i}=0
for all
i
; the action profile is a PSNE since
u_{i}(0,n_{i})\geq u_{i}(1,n_{i})
for all
i
.
The remaining statement is direct to show from the discussion above.
∎

Finally, Theorem
4.7
shows that the convexity of
g
is necessary to
efficiently checking the existence of a non-trivial PSNE; intuitively, this is because the convexity leads to the monotonicity of
\Delta g
, which is essential to characterize the non-trivial PSNE by the
k
-core.

Theorem 4.7

(
Yang and Wang 2020
)
.

Consider a fully homogeneous BNPG with general
g
, checking the existence of a non-trivial PSNE is NP-complete.

and
\gamma=1
.
In the former case, a relatively small fraction ultimately invest, whereas
\gamma=1
leads to a great deal of equilibrium investment, particularly for sufficiently large
\alpha
and
\beta
.
This is because convex
g_{i}
imply that as more players invest, the marginal benefits to investment increase, and therefore equilibrium may be seen as a cascade of investment decisions that ultimately captures most of the community.

6
Conclusion

We study binary networked public goods games from an algorithmic perspective.
We show that pure strategy equilibria may not exist even in highly restricted cases, and checking equilibrium existence is computationally hard in general.
However, we exhibit a number of important special cases in which such equilibria exist, can be efficiently computed, and even where socially optimal equilibria can also be efficiently found.
Finally, we presented a heuristic approach for approximately solving such games, shown to be highly effective in our experiments.
