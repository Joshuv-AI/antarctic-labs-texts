1.
Introduction

Groups of automorphisms of regular rooted trees are a rich source of examples with interesting properties, and they have been used to solve very important problems in Group Theory.
The first Grigorchuk group, defined by Grigorchuk
[
5
]
in 1980,
is one of the first instances of an infinite finitely generated periodic group, thus providing a negative solution to the General Burnside Problem.
It is also the first example of a group with intermediate growth
[
6
]
, hence solving the Milnor Problem, and the first group being amenable but not elementary amenable.
Many other groups of automorphisms of rooted trees have since been defined and studied.
Important examples are the Gupta-Sidki
p
-groups
[
7
]
, for
p
an odd prime, and the second Grigorchuk group
[
5
]
.
These are again finitely generated infinite periodic groups and they belong to the large family of the so-called
Grigorchuk-Gupta-Sidki
groups.

Let
{\mathcal{T}}_{d}
, or simply
{\mathcal{T}}
when it is clear from the context, be the
d
-adic tree (that is, a regular rooted tree with
d
descendants at every vertex), where
d\geq 2
. We denote with
\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
the set of all automorphisms of
{\mathcal{T}}
as a graph. An automorphism of
{\mathcal{T}}
fixes the root and preserves incidence. So, roughly speaking, it induces a permutation on each level of the tree.
The set of all automorphisms of
{\mathcal{T}}
form a group under composition; this is actually a profinite group.
Remarkable subgroups of
\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
have been studied in many different algebraic contexts, and also in dynamics and cryptography. Among others, for some classes of subgroups of
\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
it has been studied the existence of maximal subgroups of finite index, the decidability of some algorithmic problems, and the description of their Schreier graphs. Some research has been also done regarding verbal subgroups and the study of the lower central series of certain subgroups of
\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
, but in general very little is known.

We recall that if
w
is a group-word, and
G
is a group, then the verbal subgroup
w(G)
of
G
determined by
w
is the subgroup generated by the set of all values
w(g_{1},\dots,g_{n})
, where
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
run among the elements of
G
. It is well known that every verbal subgroup is fully invariant. Conversely, fully invariant subgroups need not be verbal.
The terms
\gamma_{i}(G)
of the lower central series are examples of verbal subgroups. More precisely,
\gamma_{i}(G)
is generated by the values in
G
of the commutator

[x_{1},\ldots,x_{i}]
of length
i
.
Other important examples of words related to commutators are the Engel word

e_{i}=[x,y,\overset{i}{\ldots},y]
, and the derived words
\delta_{i}
that define the terms of the derived series.

An automorphism
f
is said to be
finitary
if there exists a positive integer
m
such that the permutation induced by
f
is the identity for all levels
k\geq m
. The subgroup of
\Aut{\mathcal{T}}_{d}
of all finitary automorphisms of
{\mathcal{T}}_{d}
is denoted by
\mathcal{F}_{d}
.
By using some techniques regarding tabular representations of groups and polynomial expressions, Smetaniuk and Sushchansky proved in
[
10
]
that every verbal subgroup of
\mathcal{F}_{2}
coincides with some terms of the lower central series.
Further research has been done about the lower central series

\{\gamma_{i}(G)\}_{i\geq 1}
of specific subgroups
G
of
\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
, and more specifically of some particular Grigorchuk-Gupta-Sidki groups, but also in this case the knowledge is really scarce.
If
G
is the first Grigorchuk group then
|G:G^{\prime}|=8
, and Rozhkov
[
9
]

showed that
|\gamma_{i}(G):\gamma_{i+1}(G)|=2
or
4
for all
i\geq 2
.
On the other hand, Bartholdi
[
2
]
proved that

|\gamma_{i}(G):\gamma_{i+1}(G)|
is not bounded as
n\to\infty
when
G
is the Gupta-Sidki
3
-group. Bartholdi, Eick, and Hartung conjectured in
[
1
]
that

|\gamma_{i}(G):\gamma_{i+1}(G)|\leq p^{2}
for the generalized Fabrykowski-Gupta groups.
A group
G
in which
|\gamma_{i}(G):\gamma_{i+1}(G)|
is bounded for all
i\geq 1
is said to be of
finite width
.
Finally, Muntyan
[
8
]
described the verbal subgroups of the automorphism group of a rooted tree of a regular rooted tree
{\mathcal{T}}_{d}
, where
d>2
and odd. However this paper, which is available only in Russian, is difficult to comprehend since there are many results left unproven and some inaccuracies.

The goal of the present work is to fix this gap by providing an exhaustive and detailed version of the above results.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section 2 we recall some general definitions concerning the group of automorphisms acting on a rooted tree. In Section 3 we prove key preliminary results that we can be also used in a more general context, while in Section 4 we present the main description of the verbal subgroups of the whole group of automorphisms of
{\mathcal{T}}_{d}
for
d>2
and odd.

2.
Preliminaries

A connected graph with no cycles is called a
tree
. A tree is said to be
rooted
it has a designated vertex called the
root
and denoted by
\emptyset
. A rooted tree is
regular
if every non-root vertex has the same
valency
, defined as the number of edges passing through the vertex. If the valency of a regular rooted tree is
d+1
then the
degree

d
of the tree is the number of descendants of every non-root vertex. For every integer
d>1
there is an unique regular rooted tree of degree
d
, that we will call simply the
d
-adic tree
, which looks like this:

.

We denote this tree with
{\mathcal{T}}_{d}
. When the value of
d
is clear from the context, we will simply write
{\mathcal{T}}
. Let
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{d}\}
. A
word
of positive length
n
in
X
is an expression of the form
x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\dots x_{i_{n}}
, where
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{n}\in\{1,\dots,d\}
. Let
X^{n}
be the set of all words of length
n
and write
X^{*}=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}X^{n}
. Then
X^{*}
is the free monoid generated by
X
. Here the multiplication is defined by juxtaposition: if
u\in X^{n}
and
v\in X^{m}
, then
uv\in X^{n+m}
. There is an obvious bijection between
X^{*}
and
{\mathcal{T}}
: for all
n\geq 0
, the elements in
X^{n}
correspond to the
n
-th level of the tree. Chosen a vertex
u
, the subtree of
{\mathcal{T}}
hanging from
u
constitutes the set
uX^{*}
, which is a copy of
{\mathcal{T}}
. It is customary to choose the set
X=\{1,\dots,d\}
.

The set
\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
of all bijective maps of
X^{*}
which preserve incidence, i.e. automorphisms of
{\mathcal{T}}
, is a group with respect to the ordinary composition between functions, defined by
(fg)(u)=g(f(u))
. Let
\alpha\in\Sym(d)
, the symmetric group on
d
letters. Then the
rooted automorphism
corresponding to
\alpha
is the element of
\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
obtained by rigidly permuting the subtrees hanging from the vertices
1,\dots,d
using the permutation
\alpha
. On the other side, if
g\in\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
is an automorphism sending
u
to
v
then

\displaystyle u1

\displaystyle\longmapsto v\alpha(1)

\displaystyle u2

\displaystyle\longmapsto v\alpha(2)

\displaystyle\,\,\,\vdots

\displaystyle ud

\displaystyle\longmapsto v\alpha(d),

where
\alpha
is a suitable permutation. This element
\alpha\in\Sym(d)
is called the
label
of
g
at the vertex
u
and it is denoted with
g_{(u)}
. The
portrait
of
g
is the set of all labels of
g
.
Every
g\in\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
is uniquely determined by its portrait. Indeed, if
u=x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{n}\in X^{n}
, then we have

(1)

g(u)=g(x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{n})=(g_{\emptyset}(x_{1})g_{(x_{1})}(x_{2})g_{(x_{1}x_{2})}(x_{3})\dots g_{(x_{1}x_{2}\dots x_{n-1})}(x_{n})).

The
n
-th level stabilizer
of
\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
is

\St(n)=\{f\in\Aut{\mathcal{T}}\mid f(u)=u\ \forall u\in X^{n}\}.

Clearly
\St(n)
is a normal subgroup of
\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. It immediately follows from (
1
) that

\St(1)\geq\St(2)\geq\dots\geq\St(n)\geq\dots.

We also have

\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\St(n)=\{1\}.

Let
f\in\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
and
u\in X^{*}
, and suppose that
f(u)=u
. The
section
of
f
at
u
, denoted by
f_{u}
, is the restriction of
f
to the subtree hanging from
u
, which can be identified with
{\mathcal{T}}
. Thus
f_{u}
can be described by the rule
f(uv)=uf_{u}(v)
. For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we can define an isomorphism

\displaystyle\psi_{n}:\St(n)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\Aut{\mathcal{T}}\times\overset{d^{n}}{\dots}\times\Aut{\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle\longmapsto(g_{u})_{u\in X^{n}}.

Furthermore, straightforward computation shows that

\Aut{\mathcal{T}}=\Sym(d)\ltimes\St(1).

This, together with the fact that
\St(1)\cong\Aut{\mathcal{T}}\times\overset{d}{\dots}\times\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
, implies that every element
g\in\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
can be uniquely written in the form

(2)

g=v\sigma,\mbox{ where }v=\psi_{1}^{-1}(g_{1},\dots,g_{d})\in\St(1),\mbox{ and }\sigma\in\Sym(d).

Throughout the paper we will always write elements of
\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
in the form (
2
) making the abuse of notation of omitting
\psi_{1}
, that is
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{d})\sigma
.

Let
g=(g_{1},\dots,g_{d})\sigma
and
h=(h_{1},\dots,h_{d})\tau
be elements of
\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
, where
(g_{1},\dots,g_{d}),(h_{1},\dots,h_{d})\in\St(1)
and
\sigma,\tau\in\Sym(d)
. Then a straightforward calculation shows that the following formulas hold:

(3)

\displaystyle gh

\displaystyle=(g_{1}h_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\dots,g_{d}h_{\sigma^{-1}(d)})\sigma\tau,

(4)

\displaystyle g^{-1}

\displaystyle=(g_{\sigma(1)}^{-1},\dots,g_{\sigma(d)}^{-1})\sigma^{-1},

(5)

\displaystyle g^{\tau}

\displaystyle=(g_{\tau(1)},\dots,g_{\tau(d)})\sigma^{\tau},

(6)

\displaystyle g^{h}

\displaystyle=(h_{\tau(1)}^{-1}g_{\tau(1)}h_{(\sigma^{-1}\tau)(1))},\dots,h_{\tau(d)}^{-1}g_{\tau(d)}h_{(\sigma^{-1}\tau)(d))})\sigma^{\tau},

(7)

\displaystyle[g,h]

\displaystyle=(g_{\sigma(1)}^{-1}h_{(\tau\sigma)(1)}^{-1}g_{(\tau\sigma)(1)}h_{(\tau^{\sigma})(1)},\dots,g_{\sigma(d)}^{-1}h_{(\tau\sigma)(d)}^{-1}g_{(\tau\sigma)(d)}h_{(\tau^{\sigma})(d)})[\sigma,\tau].

Let now
g=v\sigma
, where
v\in\St(1)
and
\sigma\in\Sym(d)
. Then

\displaystyle g^{2}

\displaystyle=v\sigma v\sigma=vv^{\sigma^{-1}}\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle g^{3}

\displaystyle=vv^{\sigma^{-1}}\sigma^{2}v\sigma=vv^{\sigma^{-1}}v^{\sigma^{-2}}\sigma^{3}

and hence, for all positive integers
n
, we get

(8)

g^{n}=vv^{\sigma^{-1}}v^{\sigma^{-2}}\dots v^{\sigma^{-(n-1)}}\sigma^{n}.

A subgroup
G
of
\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
is said to be
spherically transitive
(or

) if it acts transitively on each level of the tree. That is, if for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

, there exists
g\in G
such that
g(u)=v
. Of course, the
whole group
\Aut{\mathcal{T}}
is spherically transitive.

Lemma 1

(

)

Every element of

In other words, for every

. Let

be the

Lemma 2

(

]
)

An automorphism

is spherically transitive if and only if it is conjugate to the adding machine.

Lemma 3
.

Let

, and assume that

, where both

are spherically transitive. Then

Proof.

By Lemma

, where

is the adding machine and

\displaystyle=uu^{\prime}=t^{x}t^{y}=((t^{-1})^{z})^{x}t^{y}=(t^{-1}(t^{y})^{(zx)^{-1}})^{zx}

\displaystyle=[t,y(zx)^{-1}]^{zx}=[t^{zx},(y(zx)^{-1})^{zx}]=[t^{zx},x^{-1}z^{-1}y],

Lemma 4
.

Let

Since each

\displaystyle x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{1})\alpha_{1},\qquad x_{k}=(x_{k+1},\dots,x_{k+1})\alpha_{k+1},\quad k\geq 1.

Then by (

\displaystyle a^{x}=(1,\dots,1,a_{1}^{x_{1}})\sigma,\qquad a_{k}^{x_{k}}=(1,\dots,1,a_{k+1}^{x_{k+1}})\sigma.

Since every element of

Let

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0}(g)=\begin{cases}0\quad&\text{if }g_{\emptyset}\in\Alt(d),\\

Theorem 5
.

The derived subgroup

By
