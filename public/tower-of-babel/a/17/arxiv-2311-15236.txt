Semilinear equations in bounded cylinders: Morse index and bifurcation from one-dimensional solutions
Danilo Gregorin Afonso

Abstract
. In this paper, we study semilinear elliptic equations in domains where there is a natural class of solutions, which depend only on one variable, and whose simple geometry reflects the geometry of the domain. We prove that under quite general assumptions, other types of solutions also exist. More precisely, we consider one-dimensional solutions in bounded cylinders and, combining a suitable separation of variables with the theory of ordinary differential equations, we show how to compute the Morse index of such solutions. The Morse index is then used to prove local and global bifurcation results.

35A02, 35A16, 35B06, 35B32, 35J60

1.
Introduction

An old and natural question in the qualitative theory of elliptic partial differential equations is to determine the conditions under which the solutions of differential problems inherit the geometric properties of the domain.

Concerning the radial symmetry, a fundamental step was the celebrated result of Gidas, Ni, and Nirenberg (
[
18
]
), which is based on the moving planes method and roughly states that positive solutions to semilinear Dirichlet problems in balls must be radial and radially decreasing. See also
[
19
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
for related results and developments of the method. Regarding the uniqueness/multiplicity of radial solutions, see
[
28
]
. On the other hand, there is also a vast literature on symmetry-breaking results. See, for example, the papers
[
22
,
20
,
21
,
23
,
11
]
, the monograph
[
13
]
, and the references therein.

Radial solutions can be seen as depending only on one variable, the distance to the central point. Our aim in this paper is to analyze another kind of “one-dimensional solution”, which is not related to invariance with respect to the action of any symmetry group but is nonetheless quite natural in the geometric setting we consider.

Let us denote a point in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
by
x=(x^{\prime},x_{N})
, where
x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
and
x_{N}\in\mathbb{R}
, and let
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
,
N\geq 2
, be a smooth bounded domain. We consider in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
the infinite half-cylinder spanned by
\omega
,

\Sigma_{\omega}\coloneqq\omega\times(0,+\infty)=\{x=(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\mathbb{R}^{N}\ :\ x^{\prime}\in\omega,\ x_{N}>0\},

and set

\Omega_{\omega}\coloneqq\{x=(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\Sigma_{\omega}\ :\ x_{N}<1\}

and

\Gamma_{0}\coloneqq\{x=(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\Sigma_{\omega}\ :\ x_{N}=1\}.

Finally, we denote
\Gamma\coloneqq\partial\Omega_{\omega}\setminus\overline{\Gamma}_{0}
.

The set
\Gamma_{0}
is usually said to be the relative boundary of the bounded cylinder
\Omega_{\omega}
with respect to
\Sigma_{\omega}
. We are interested in studying relative Dirichlet problems (i.e., with the Dirichlet condition imposed only on
\Gamma_{0}
) for semilinear elliptic equations in
\Omega_{\omega}
. More precisely, for
f\in C^{1,\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we consider the problem

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}-\Delta u&=&f(u)&\quad\text{ in }\Omega_{\omega}\\
u&=&0&\quad\text{ on }\Gamma_{0}\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}&=&0&\quad\text{ on }\Gamma\end{array}\right.,

where
\nu
denotes the outer unit normal vector to
\partial\Omega_{\omega}
. The Neumann condition is the natural boundary condition to impose on the boundary of
\Sigma_{\omega}
, for example, if one thinks that the PDE models the diffusion of some quantity
u
which should not leave
\Sigma_{\omega}
. Furthermore, under appropriate hypotheses on the nonlinearity
f
, this problem is analogous to pure Dirichlet problems in terms of the variational formulation.

We say that
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
is a weak solution of (
1.1
) if

\int_{\Omega_{\omega}}\nabla u\nabla v\ dx-\int_{\Omega_{\omega}}f(u)v\ dx=0\quad\forall v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma),

where
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
is the subspace of
H^{1}(\Omega_{\omega})
of functions whose trace vanishes on
\Gamma_{0}
. Whenever they exist, weak solutions are the critical points of the functional

(1.2)

J(v)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{\omega}}|\nabla v|^{2}\ dx-\int_{\Omega_{\omega}}F(v)\ dx,\quad v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma),

where
F(s)=\int_{0}^{s}f(r)\ dr
.

Our interest in relative problems in cylinders comes from the fact that, under certain points of view, the domain
\Omega_{\omega}
is analogous in
\Sigma_{\omega}
to balls in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(or, more in general, to spherical sectors in cones). One such aspect is that
\Gamma_{0}
is the only surface of constant mean curvature in a certain class (see
[
1
]
and the references therein).

Another analogy with balls in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
is that in
\Omega_{\omega}
there are special solutions of (
1.1
) that inherit the geometry of the cylinder, in the sense that they depend solely on the “height” variable
x_{N}
, and which could be analogous to radial solutions in balls. We call them one-dimensional solutions and denote them by
u_{\omega}
. They can be obtained by solving, for example by some standard variational method, the ordinary differential equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-u^{\prime\prime}=f(u)&\quad\text{ in }(0,1)\\
u^{\prime}(0)=u(1)=0&\end{array}\right.

and then setting

u_{\omega}(x^{\prime},x_{N})=\widetilde{u}(x_{N}),\quad(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\Omega_{\omega},

where
\widetilde{u}
is a solution of (
2.3
). Note that one-dimensional solutions satisfy the relative overdetermined condition
\frac{\partial u_{\omega}}{\partial\nu}=\text{constant}
on
\Gamma_{0}
, and we obtain a parallel with balls once again, in view of the celebrated result of Serrin (
[
36
]
).

A natural question is then to understand if there is an analogous of the famous result of Gidas, Ni, and Nirenberg,
[
18
]
, namely, if all positive solutions are one-dimensional. This is the main objective of the present paper, and we anticipate that the answer to this question is negative: there are other kinds of solutions, under very general assumptions on the data
f
and
\omega
, so the parallel between balls in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
\Omega_{\omega}
breaks down.

If instead one considers the analogous of Serrin’s problem, the parallel also breaks down: it is shown in
[
2
]
that, under certain conditions (both on
\omega
and
f
), one may expect that there exist domains in the cylinder
\Sigma_{\omega}
whose relative boundaries are not flat and which admit solutions to the relative overdetermined problem. When
f\equiv 1
and
\omega
is an interval, the existence of such domains was already contained in
[
16
]
. Recently, the existence of such domains was extended to general bases
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
(but always for
f\equiv 1
), see
[
30
]
.

One-dimensional solutions are not so much considered in the literature as their radial counterparts, and, to my knowledge, have always been studied in unbounded domains, since the pioneer works of Berestycki, Caffarelli, and Nirenberg (
[
6
,
5
]
) in the half-space
\mathbb{R}^{N-1}\times(0,+\infty)
, which lead to a famous conjecture later disproved by Sicbaldi in dimension
N\geq 3
(
[
37
]
, see also
[
34
,
35
]
). Always on the classification of one-dimensional solutions in half-spaces, see
[
12
,
17
]
, and also
[
15
]
on a conjecture of De Giorgi.

Mixed boundary value problems in (unbounded) cylindrical domains
\omega\times(0,\infty)
have been treated in
[
10
]
. In this work, strong rigidity results are found, in the sense that solutions to the differential problems under analysis are found to be one-dimensional under quite weak assumptions. The authors also obtain conditions for periodicity/monotonicity of the one-dimensional solutions.

In our bounded framework, the situation is quite the opposite. Roughly speaking, our main result is that, for a wide class of nonlinearities
f
, there exist solutions to (
1.1
) which are not one-dimensional, for “almost every shape” of the base
\omega
. This is achieved by means of bifurcation theory, as we show that solutions that are not one-dimensional bifurcate from one-dimensional solutions. One key aspect of our geometric setting is that the Morse index of one-dimensional solutions is easy to compute. We refer to Sections
3
and
4
for detailed statements and further comments.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
collects the main assumptions and recalls some preliminaries. In Section
3
we carry out a detailed study of one-dimensional solutions, showing how to compute the Morse index and that they are always nondegenerate in the space of one-dimensional functions. In the final Section
4
we prove our main results on bifurcation of other solutions from one-dimensional solutions.

2.
Notations, assumptions, and preliminaries

We assume that
f
is a
C^{1,\alpha}
nonlinearity such that

{(f_{1})}

the superlinear condition

(2.1)

f^{\prime}(s)>\frac{f(s)}{s}\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\};

{(f_{2})}

a solution of (
1.1
) exists;

{(f_{3})}

f
“preserves the sign”:

(2.2)

sf(s)>0\quad\forall s\neq 0.

It is widely known that there is a plethora of classes of nonlinearities
f
for which variational methods yield both positive and sign-changing solutions of (
1.1
) in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
(recall that
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
is the subspace of
H^{1}(\Omega_{\omega})
of functions whose trace vanishes on
\Gamma_{0}
). Of course, the same variational methods, when restricted to the space
H_{0,x_{N}}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
(which is the subspace of functions in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
which depend only on the
x_{N}
variable), yield the existence of one-dimensional solutions.

The archetype function satisfying

(

f
1

)

-

(

f
3

)

is the Lane-Emden nonlinearity

f(u)=|u|^{p-2}u,

for
p\in(1,p^{*})
, where
p^{*}=\frac{N+2}{N-2}
, if
N\geq 3
, or
p^{*}=\infty
if
N=2
, is the critical exponent for the Sobolev embedding
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega_{\omega})
. More in general, one could consider
f
satisfying

(

f
1

)

and, in addition:

•

subcritical growth: there exists
a\in L^{\frac{2N}{N+2}}(\Omega_{\omega})
and
b>0
such that

|f(s)|<a(x)+b|s|^{p}\quad\forall s\in\mathbb{R},

with
p\in(1,p^{*})
;

•

f(s)=o(s)
as
s\to 0
;

•

Ambrosetti-Rabinowitz condition: there exists
\theta>2
and
r>0
such that for

0<\theta F(s)\leq sf(s)\quad\forall|s|\geq r.

Then there exist positive solutions via either the Mountain Pass Theorem or minimization on the Nehari manifold. Moreover, it can be shown that such solutions have Morse index one (see the end of this section for a brief recall of the Morse index). We refer to
[
13
]
for the details.

For many more examples of classes of nonlinearities for which (
1.1
) has a solution, we refer the reader to
[
33
,
38
,
4
]
.

Remark 2.1
.

Since
f
is assumed to be of class
C^{1,\alpha}
, then every weak solution of (
1.1
) is also in
C^{2}(\overline{\Omega_{\omega}})
. This follows by standard regularity arguments, considering the boundary conditions and the fact that
\partial\Omega_{\omega}
is made by the union of
(N-1)
-dimensional smooth manifolds (with boundary) intersecting orthogonally (see
[
31
, Proposition 6.1]
).

Let
u_{\omega}
be a one-dimensional solution of (
1.1
). Recall that they can be found by solving the ODE

(2.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-u^{\prime\prime}=f(u)&\quad\text{ in }(0,1)\\
u^{\prime}(0)=u(1)=0&\end{array}\right.

and then setting

(2.4)

u_{\omega}(x^{\prime},x_{N})=\widetilde{u}(x_{N}),\quad(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\Omega_{\omega},

where
\widetilde{u}
is a solution of (
2.3
). Notice that reflecting
\widetilde{u}
we obtain a solution of the Dirichlet problem in
(-1,1)
. At the same time, every solution of this Dirichlet problem satisfies also (
2.3
), by the results of
[
18
]
, and thus the two problems are equivalent.

We will sometimes write
u_{\omega}
also for the solution of (
2.3
) in
(0,1)
. The meaning will be clear from the context.

The proofs of our main results are based on the theory of bifurcation, and our arguments rely heavily on the interplay between two eigenvalue problems. The first one is the eigenvalue problem for the linearized operator associated with the ODE (
2.3
):

(2.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-z^{\prime\prime}-f^{\prime}(u_{\omega})z=\alpha z&\quad\text{ in }(0,1)\\
z^{\prime}(0)=z(1)=0&\end{array}\right..

We denote its eigenvalues, which are all simple (see
[
24
]
), by
\alpha_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. The second one is for the Neumann-Laplacian in the
(N-1)
-dimensional domain
\omega
that spans the cylinder:

(2.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}\psi&=&\lambda\psi&\quad\text{ in }\omega\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\nu}&=&0&\quad\text{ on }\partial\omega\end{array}\right.,

where
-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}=-\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}^{2}}
is the Laplace operator in
\mathbb{R}^{N-1}
, that is, with respect to the variable
x^{\prime}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N-1})
. We denote its eigenvalues, counted with multiplicity, by

0=\lambda_{0}(\omega)<\lambda_{1}(\omega)\leq\lambda_{2}(\omega)\leq\ldots.

The Morse index
m(u)
of a weak solution
u
is given by the number of negative eigenvalues of the linearized operator

(2.7)

L_{u}=-\Delta-f^{\prime}(u)

in the space
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
. Note that if
u=u_{\omega}
is a one-dimensional solution, we can restrict our attention to the eigenvalues of
L_{u_{\omega}}
whose corresponding eigenfunctions are one-dimensional, which are precisely the eigenvalues of (
2.5
). We denote by
m^{x_{N}}(u_{\omega})
the Morse index of
u_{\omega}
in
H_{0,x_{N}}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
, which is the subspace of functions in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
which depend only on the
x_{N}
variable. A solution
u
is said to be degenerate if the linearized operator
L_{u}
admits zero as an eigenvalue, or, in other words, if there exists a nontrivial weak solution
\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
to the problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}-\Delta\varphi-f^{\prime}(u)\varphi&=&0&\quad\text{ in }\Omega_{\omega}\\
\varphi&=&0&\quad\text{ on }\Gamma_{0}\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\nu}&=&0&\quad\text{ on }\Gamma\end{array}\right..

Analogous definitions and statements hold in the subspace
H_{0,x_{N}}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
of one-dimensional functions and also for functions of one real variable in the space

\mathcal{H}^{1}(0,1)\coloneqq\{z\in H^{1}(0,1)\ :\ z(1)=0\}.

For more details on these matters, we refer the reader to
[
13
]
.

Let
u
be a weak solution of (
1.1
) (which is also a classical solution, in view of Remark
2.1
). We denote by
\mathcal{Z}(u)
the nodal set of
u
in
\Omega_{\omega}
:

\mathcal{Z}(u)=\{x\in\Omega_{\omega}\ :\ u(x)=0\}.

By a nodal domain, we mean a connected component of
\Omega_{\omega}\setminus\mathcal{Z}(u)
, that is, a region of
\Omega_{\omega}
where
u
does not change sign. We denote by
n(u)
the number of nodal domains of the solution
u
.

It is well-known that the superlinear condition (
2.1
) implies that the Morse index of a solution
u
is bounded from below by the number of nodal regions:

(2.8)

m(u)\geq n(u).

Indeed, the Morse index can be characterized as the maximal dimension of a subspace of
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
where the quadratic form associated with the linearized operator
L_{u}
is negative definite. If
u
satisfies (
1.1
), then multiplying the equation by
u
and integrating by parts yield

\int_{\Omega_{\omega}}|\nabla u|^{2}\ dx=\int_{\Omega_{\omega}}f(u)u\ dx.

On the other hand, the quadratic form associated to
L_{u}
is

L_{u}[v,v]=\int_{\Omega_{\omega}}|\nabla v|^{2}\ dx-\int_{\Omega_{\omega}}f^{\prime}(u)v^{2}\ dx,\quad v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma).

Hence (
2.1
) implies

\displaystyle L_{u}[u,u]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{\omega}}|\nabla u|^{2}\ dx-\int_{\Omega_{\omega}}f^{\prime}(u)u^{2}\ dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{\omega}}\left(\frac{f(u)}{u}-f^{\prime}(u)\right)u^{2}\ dx

\displaystyle<0.

This argument can be repeated using the restriction of
u
to a nodal region, in such a way that each nodal region yields a (linearly independent) direction where the second bilinear form corresponding to
L_{u}
is negative definite, which in turn yield (
2.8
).

For the bifurcation arguments, we will consider a fixed
(N-1)
-dimensional smooth bounded domain
\omega
, consider scalings
t\omega
, with
t>0
, and use
t
as the bifurcation parameter. We denote by
\Omega_{t\omega}
the bounded cylinder spanned by the scaled domain, and by
\Gamma_{0,t}
and
\Gamma_{t}
the parts of
\partial\Omega_{t\omega}
corresponding to
\Gamma_{0}
and
\Gamma
, respectively.

3.
One-dimensional solutions

3.1.
Decomposition of the spectrum of the linearized operator

Let
u_{\omega}
be a one-dimensional solution. Recall that we adopt the notation
u_{\omega}
also for the real function
\widetilde{u}
that solves (
2.3
) in
(0,1)
. To analyze the spectrum of
L_{u_{\omega}}
, it is convenient to consider the one-dimensional eigenvalue problem (
2.5
).

In what follows, we denote by
L_{u_{\omega}}^{x_{N}}
the linear operator defined by

L_{u_{\omega}}^{x_{N}}(z)=-z^{\prime\prime}-f^{\prime}(u_{\omega})z,\quad z\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(0,1).

It is immediately seen that the Morse index of
u_{\omega}
in
\mathcal{H}^{1}(0,1)
is precisely
m^{x_{N}}(u_{\omega})
in
H_{0,x_{N}}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
.

We have the following decomposition of the spectrum of
L_{u_{\omega}}
, obtained in
[
2
]
and which we report here for the convenience of the reader:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
u_{\omega}
be a one-dimensional solution of (
1.1
). The spectra of
L_{u_{\omega}}
,
L_{u_{\omega}}^{x_{N}}
and
-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}
are related by

\sigma(L_{u_{\omega}})=\sigma(L_{u_{\omega}}^{x_{N}})+\sigma(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}),

where
\sigma(\cdot)
denotes the spectrum of a linear operator.

Proof.

We begin by showing that
\sigma(L_{u_{\omega}})\subset\sigma(L_{u_{\omega}}^{x_{N}})+\sigma(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}})
. Let
\mu\in\sigma(L_{u_{\omega}})
and let
\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
be an associated eigenfunction, that is,
\varphi
is a weak solution of

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}-\Delta\varphi-f^{\prime}(u_{\omega})\varphi&=&\mu\varphi&\quad\text{ in }\Omega_{\omega}\\
\varphi&=&0&\quad\text{ on }\Gamma_{0}\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial\nu}&=&0&\quad\text{ on }\Gamma\end{array}\right..

As observed in Remark
2.1
, by the shape of
\Omega_{\omega}
and the boundary conditions, elliptic regularity theory yields that
\varphi
is a classical solution in
\overline{\Omega_{\omega}}
.

Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}
in
\omega
with homogeneous Neumann boundary condition and let
\psi
be a corresponding eigenfunction. Define

z(x_{N})=\int_{\omega}\varphi(x^{\prime},x_{N})\psi(x^{\prime})\ dx^{\prime}.

Differentiating twice with respect to
x_{N}
, using Green’s formula and the boundary conditions we obtain

\displaystyle-z^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\omega}-\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial x_{N}^{2}}\psi\ dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\omega}(-\Delta\varphi+\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}\varphi)\psi\ dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int_{\omega}f^{\prime}(u_{\omega})\varphi\psi\ dx^{\prime}+\int_{\omega}\lambda\varphi\psi\ dx^{\prime}+\int_{\omega}\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}\psi\varphi\ dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(u_{\omega})z+\mu z-\lambda\int_{\omega}\psi\varphi\ dx^{\prime}

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(u_{\omega})z+\mu z-\lambda z.

Thus
(\mu-\lambda)\in\sigma(L_{u_{\omega}}^{x_{N}})
and hence
\mu=(\mu-\lambda)+\lambda\in\sigma(L_{u_{\omega}}^{x_{N}})+\sigma(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}})
.

To show the reverse inclusion, let
\alpha\in\sigma(L_{u_{\omega}}^{x_{N}})
,
\lambda\in\sigma(-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}})
and let
z
,
\psi
be respective associated eigenfunctions. Setting

\varphi(x^{\prime},x_{N})=z(x_{N})\psi(x^{\prime}),\quad(x^{\prime},x_{N})\in\Omega_{\omega}

we have

\displaystyle-\Delta\varphi

\displaystyle=-z^{\prime\prime}\psi-\Delta_{\mathbb{R}^{N-1}}\psi z

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(u_{\omega})z\psi+\alpha z\psi+\lambda z\psi

\displaystyle=f^{\prime}(u_{\omega})\varphi+(\alpha+\lambda)\varphi.

As a consequence,
\alpha+\lambda\in\sigma(L_{u_{\omega}})
, and the proof is complete.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
u_{\omega}
be a one-dimensional solution of (
1.1
). Then
u_{\omega}
is degenerate if and only if there exist
i\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\alpha_{i}+\lambda_{j}=0.

3.2.
Morse index computation

Our objective in this section is to understand the one-dimensional Morse index
m^{x_{N}}(u_{\omega})
. To this aim, we analyze the eigenvalue problem corresponding to the linearized operator associated with the ODE (
2.3
):

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-z^{\prime\prime}-f^{\prime}(u_{\omega})z=\alpha z&\quad\text{ in }(0,1)\\
z^{\prime}(0)=z(1)=0&\end{array}\right..

The spectral theory for this kind of problem is well-established in the literature (see, e.g.,
[
24
, Chapter XI]
). It is known that there exists a sequence of real eigenvalues

\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\ldots<\alpha_{i}<\ldots,

with
\alpha_{i}\to+\infty
as
i\to\infty
; corresponding to each eigenvalue
\alpha_{i}
, there exists a unique (up to multiplicative constants) eigenfunction
z_{i}
; for each
i\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, the eigenfunction
z_{i}
has precisely
i-1
zeros in
(0,1)
.

Our first result relates the one-dimensional Morse index
m^{x_{N}}
with the number of nodal regions.

Proposition 3.3
.

Assume that

(

f
1

)

-

(

f
3

)

hold. Let
u_{\omega}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
be a one-dimensional solution of (
1.1
) with
n(u_{\omega})=n\geq 1
nodal domains. Then

m^{x_{N}}(u_{\omega})=n.

Proof.

It is well-known (see Section
2
) that
m^{x_{N}}(u_{\omega})\geq n
. Since
m^{x_{N}}(u_{\omega})
is the number of negative eigenvalues of (
2.5
), we have that

\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\ldots<\alpha_{n}<0.

Our aim is then to show that
\alpha_{n+1}\geq 0
.

Since
u_{\omega}
satisfies the equation
-u_{\omega}^{\prime\prime}=f(u_{\omega})
in the interval
(0,1)
, taking the derivative with respect to
x_{N}
and rearranging terms we obtain

(u_{\omega}^{\prime})^{\prime\prime}+f^{\prime}(u_{\omega})u_{\omega}^{\prime}=0\quad\text{ in }(0,1)

Moreover, since
u_{\omega}
has
n
nodal regions, then we known that
u_{\omega}^{\prime}
has
n-1
zeros in
(0,1)
(see Remark
3.4
).

On the other hand, from the spectral theory for (
2.5
), we known that the eigenfunction
z_{n+1}
has
n
zeros in
(0,1)
(and another zero at
x_{N}=1
) and satisfies

z_{n+1}^{\prime\prime}+(f^{\prime}(u_{\omega})+\alpha_{n+1})z_{n+1}=0\quad\text{ in }(0,1)

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that
\alpha_{n+1}<0
. Should this be the case, we would have
f^{\prime}(u_{\omega})+\alpha_{n+1}<f^{\prime}(u_{\omega})
and therefore the Sturm-Picone comparison theorem would imply that
u_{\omega}^{\prime}
has at least one zero between each consecutive zero of
z_{n+1}
. Since
z_{n+1}
has
n+1
zeros in
[0,1]
, then it would follow that
u_{\omega}^{\prime}
has at least
n
zeros in
(0,1)
, which is a contradiction with the assumption on the number of nodal regions of
u_{\omega}
. Then
\alpha_{n+1}
cannot be negative, and the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 3.4

3.3.
Nondegeneracy in the space

Proposition 3.8
.

Assume that

(

f
1

)

-

(

f
3

)

hold. Let

be a one-dimensional solution of (

). Then

is nondegenerate in

Proof.

Let

be the number of nodal regions of

. By Proposition

, we have that

and therefore

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-z_{n+1}^{\prime\prime}-f^{\prime}(u_{\omega})z_{n+1}=0&\quad\text{ in }(0,1)\\
z_{n+1}^{\prime}(0)=z_{n+1}(1)=0&\end{array}\right.,

has

Let us choose the sign of

so that

has one more nodal region than

, we have that

(strict inequality is due to Hopf’s Lemma). Now, observe that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle>u_{\omega}^{\prime}(1)z_{n+1}^{\prime}(1)

\displaystyle=u_{\omega}^{\prime}(1)z_{n+1}^{\prime}(1)-\underbrace{u_{\omega}^{\prime}(0)z_{n+1}^{\prime}(0)}_{=0}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}(u_{\omega}^{\prime}z_{n+1}^{\prime})^{\prime}\ dx_{N}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}u_{\omega}^{\prime\prime}z_{n+1}^{\prime}\ dx_{N}+\int_{0}^{1}u_{\omega}^{\prime}z_{n+1}^{\prime\prime}\ dx_{N}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}f(u_{\omega})z_{n+1}^{\prime}\ dx_{N}-\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(u_{\omega})u_{\omega}^{\prime}z_{n+1}\ dx_{N}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}(f(u_{\omega})z_{n+1})^{\prime}\ dx_{N}

\displaystyle=f(u_{\omega}(0))z_{n+1}(0)-f(u_{\omega}(1))z_{n+1}(1)

\displaystyle=f(u_{\omega}(0))z_{n+1}(0)

(3.3)

\displaystyle>0,

where the last inequality follows from our choice on the sign of

and (

∎

4.
Bifurcation results

In this section, we prove the existence of solutions to (
1.1
) which are not one-dimensional. We will show that such solutions bifurcate from one-dimensional ones when the domain
\omega
which spans the cylinder is scaled. In addition to local bifurcation results, we study the structure of the bifurcating solutions, including a global bifurcation result in the spirit of Rabinowitz (
[
32
]
). See Theorem
4.6
.

We fix
\omega
, consider the scalings
t\omega
, with
t>0
, and use
t
as the bifurcation parameter. We denote by
\Omega_{t\omega}
the bounded cylinder spanned by the scaled domain, and by
\Gamma_{t}
the part of the boundary corresponding to
\Gamma
.

Of course, when we scale the domain
\omega
, the PDE changes, since the domain
\Omega_{t\omega}
and the functional space
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{t\omega}\cup\Gamma_{t})
are different. However, it is more convenient to develop the bifurcation argument in a fixed functional setting, independent of the scaling. We choose to work in the Banach space (endowed with the
C^{1,\alpha}
norm)

\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)\coloneqq\left\{u\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega_{\omega}})\ :\ u|_{\Gamma_{0}}=0,\ \left.\frac{\partial u}{\partial\nu}\right|_{\Gamma}=0\right\},

and we “bring back” to this space the PDE problems considered in the scaled domains
\Omega_{t\omega}
via suitable diffeomorphisms.

To be more precise, denote by
x=(x^{\prime},x_{N})
the points in the original cylinder
\Omega_{\omega}
, and by
y=(y^{\prime},x_{N})
the points in the scaled cylinder
\Omega_{t\omega}
. Clearly,
y^{\prime}=tx^{\prime}
. We consider the diffeomorphism
h_{t}:\overline{\Omega_{t\omega}}\to\overline{\Omega_{\omega}}
given by

h_{t}(y^{\prime},x_{N})=\left(\frac{y^{\prime}}{t},x_{N}\right).

Naturally, functions defined in the scaled domain correspond to functions in
\Omega_{\omega}
, via a scaling of the
x^{\prime}
argument. More precisely, the diffeomorphism
h_{t}
induces the linear isomorphism
h_{t}^{*}:\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{t\omega}\cup\Gamma_{t})\to\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
given by

h_{t}^{*}(v)(x^{\prime},x_{N})=v(h_{t}^{-1}(x^{\prime},x_{N}))=v(tx^{\prime},x_{N}).

Let us now describe how to bring back the PDE from
\Omega_{t\omega}
to
\Omega_{\omega}
. Let
u_{t}\in\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{t\omega}\cup\Gamma_{t})
be any solution of (
1.1
) in
\Omega_{t\omega}
. Then, the function

u_{t}^{*}\coloneqq h_{t}^{*}(u_{t})=u_{t}\circ h_{t}^{-1}\in\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)

satisfies

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}D_{t}(u_{t}^{*})&=&f(u_{t}^{*})&\quad\text{ in }\Omega_{\omega}\\
u_{t}^{*}&=&0&\quad\text{ on }\Gamma_{0}\\
\displaystyle\frac{\partial u_{t}^{*}}{\partial\nu}&=&0&\quad\text{ on }\Gamma\end{array}\right.,

where the operator
D_{t}
is defined as

D_{t}(u)=h_{t}^{*}\left(-\Delta\left((h_{t}^{*})^{-1}(u)\right)\right).

Indeed, note that
u_{t}=u_{t}^{*}\circ h_{t}=(h_{t}^{*})^{-1}(u_{t}^{*})
, and thus, since
-\Delta u_{t}=f(u_{t})
in
\Omega_{t\omega}
, we have

-\Delta\left((h_{t}^{*})^{-1}(u_{t}^{*})\right)(y)=f\left((h_{t}^{*})^{-1}(u_{t}^{*})\right)(y)\quad\text{ in }\Omega_{t\omega}.

Then, writing
y=h_{t}^{-1}(x)
, for
x\in\overline{\Omega_{\omega}}
,
D_{t}(u_{t}^{*})=f(u_{t}^{*})
immediatelly follows. Moreover, the boundary conditions in (
4.1
) are clearly satisfied.

We can further rewrite the equation
D_{t}(u)=f(u)
as
u=T_{t}(u)
, where

T_{t}:\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)\to\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)

is the operator defined as

T_{t}(u)=D_{t}^{-1}(f(u)),\quad u\in\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma),

which can also be written as

(4.2)

T_{t}(u)=h_{t}^{*}\left((-\Delta)^{-1}\left((h_{t}^{*})^{-1}(f(u))\right)\right).

Due to classical regularity results, this operator is well-defined and compact, and therefore, for any open bounded set
U\subset\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
such that
I-T_{t}\neq 0
on
\partial U
, the Leray-Schauder degree
\deg(I-T_{t},U,0)
is well-defined.

Finally, note that if
u_{\omega}
is a one-dimensional solution in
\Omega_{\omega}
, then

(u_{t\omega})^{*}=h_{t}^{*}(u_{t\omega})=u_{\omega}.

We can now define what we mean by bifurcating solutions in the scaled domains:

Definition 4.1
.

Let
u_{\omega}
be a one-dimensional solution of (
1.1
) in
\Omega_{\omega}
, and let
\bar{t}>0
. We say that a bifurcation occurs at
(\bar{t},u_{\bar{t}\omega})
if in any neighborhood of
(\bar{t},u_{\omega})
in
(0,+\infty)\times\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
there exists a solution of (
4.1
). Such other solutions will be referred to as bifurcating solutions.

Remark 4.2
.

Solutions of (
4.1
) in
\Omega_{\omega}
give rise to solutions of the original PDE in
\Omega_{t\omega}
via the action of
(h_{t}^{*})^{-1}
. Then Definition
4.1
says that a bifurcation happens if, for
t
arbitrarily close to
\bar{t}
, there exists a solution of (
1.1
) in
\Omega_{t\omega}
close to
u_{t\omega}
. Moreover, since one-dimensional solutions are never degenerate in the space of one-dimensional functions (Proposition
3.8
), such new solutions cannot be one-dimensional.

So far in this section, we have seen how to transport the PDE from the dilated domain
\Omega_{t\omega}
back to
\Omega_{\omega}
, to obtain an appropriate functional framework for the bifurcation. Our arguments will rely heavily on the behavior of the Leray-Schauder degree of
I-T_{t}
in the space
\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
when
t
changes. The key idea is that due to our next result, Lemma
4.5
, we can detect changes on
\lsd(I-T_{t},U,0)
due to the change in the Morse index of
u_{t\omega}
, which in turn is a phenomenon happening in the dilated domains/respective functional spaces.

Lemma
4.5
allows us to “transport” the calculation of the degree to the dilating domains. Its proof is an application of the following result of Nussbaum:

Proposition 4.3

(
[
29
, Section D, Proposition 2]
)
.

Let
V
be an open subset of a Banach space
E_{1}
. Let
C:E_{1}\to E_{2}
be a compact map into a Banach space
E_{2}
. Let
\beta:\overline{V}\to E_{2}
be a homeomorphism. Assume that
\beta(x)-C(x)\neq 0
for
x\in\partial V
. Then

(4.3)

\lsd(I-(C\circ\beta^{-1}),\beta(V),0)=\lsd(I-(\beta^{-1}\circ C),C^{-1}(\beta(V)),0).

Remark 4.4
.

It is to be understood as part of the statement that the Leray-Schauder degrees in (
4.3
) are well-defined.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
t>0
. Let
P_{t}:\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{t\omega}\cup\Gamma_{t})\to\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{t\omega}\cup\Gamma_{t})
be the operator given by

(4.4)

P_{t}(v)=(-\Delta)^{-1}(f(v)),\quad v\in\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{t\omega}\cup\Gamma_{t}).

Let
U\subset\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
be an open bounded set such that
I-T_{t}\neq 0
on
\partial U
. Then

(4.5)

\lsd(I-T_{t},U,0)=\lsd(I-P_{t},(h_{t}^{*})^{-1}(U),0).

Proof.

That the operator
P_{t}
is compact is standard. Let us now consider the operator
C_{t}:\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{t\omega}\cup\Gamma_{t})\to\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma)
given by

C_{t}(v)=h_{t}^{*}(P_{t}(v))=h_{t}^{*}\left((-\Delta)^{-1}(f(v))\right),\quad v\in\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{t\omega}\cup\Gamma_{t}).

It is standard to show that
C_{t}
is compact as well. Moreover,
P_{t}=(h_{t}^{*})^{-1}\circ C_{t}
. Furthermore, recalling that

T_{t}(u)=h_{t}^{*}\left((-\Delta)^{-1}\left((h_{t}^{*})^{-1}(f(u))\right)\right),\quad u\in\mathcal{X}(\Omega_{\omega}\cup\Gamma),

we see that
T_{t}=C_{t}\circ(h_{t}^{*})^{-1}
.

Invoking Proposition
4.3
, with
C=C_{t}
,
\beta=h_{t}^{*}
and
V=(h_{t}^{*})^{-1}(U)
, we have

\displaystyle\lsd(I-T_{t},U,0)

\displaystyle=\lsd\left(I-P_{t},C_{t}^{-1}\left((h_{t}^{*})\left((h_{t}^{*})^{-1}(U)\right)\right),0\right)

\displaystyle=\lsd(I-P_{t},C_{t}^{-1}(U),0)

\displaystyle=\lsd(I-P_{t},(h_{t}^{*})^{-1}(U),0),

since
C_{t}^{-1}(U)\subset(h_{t}^{*})^{-1}(U)
and
(I-P_{t})(v)\neq 0
for all
v\in(h_{t}^{*})^{-1}(U)\setminus C_{t}^{-1}(U)
. Indeed, since
C_{t}(V)=T_{t}(U)
, we have

\displaystyle(C_{t}(V)\cap U)=(T_{t}(U)\cap U)\subset T_{t}(U),

which in turn implies that

C_{t}^{-1}(U)\subset(h_{t}^{*})^{-1}\left(T_{t}^{-1}(T_{t}(U))\right)=(h_{t}^{*})^{-1}(U),

and
v=P_{t}(v)
if, and only if,
h_{t}^{*}(v)=C_{t}(v)
.
∎

.

Let

(

{(i)}

Our aim is to show that for every eigenvalue of

[

Let

be an eigenfunction of

corresponding to the eigenvalue
\gamma_{k}
, beginning from the largest:

\gamma_{1}>\gamma_{2}\geq\ldots>0.

As
P_{t}^{\prime}(u_{t\omega})\xi_{k}=\gamma_{k}\xi_{k}
, we have

-\Delta\xi_{k}=\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}}f^{\prime}(u_{t\omega})\xi_{k}

Multiplying by
\xi_{k}
and integrating by parts we obtain

\int_{\Omega_{t\omega}}|\nabla\xi_{k}|^{2}\ dx=\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}}\int_{\Omega_{t\omega}}f^{\prime}(u_{t\omega})\xi_{k}^{2}\ dx.

Let us denote by
\mu_{i}
,

the eigenvalues of

in

. Then, by the variational characterization of the

, we have:

\displaystyle\mu_{1}

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varphi\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{t\omega}\cup\Gamma_{t})\\
\varphi\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\int_{\Omega_{t\omega}}|\nabla\varphi|^{2}\ dx-\int_{\Omega_{t\omega}}f^{\prime}(u_{t\omega})\varphi^{2}\ dx}{\|\varphi\|_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\int_{\Omega_{t\omega}}|\nabla\xi_{1}|^{2}\ dx-\int_{\Omega_{t\omega}}f^{\prime}(u_{t\omega})\xi_{1}^{2}\ dx}{\|\xi_{1}\|_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\left(\frac{1}{\gamma_{1}}-1\right)\int_{\Omega_{t\omega}}f^{\prime}(u_{t\omega})\xi_{1}^{2}\ dx}{\|\xi_{1}\|_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle<0.

Now, letting
\mathbf{H}_{i}
denote an
i
-dimensional subspace of
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{t\omega}\cup\Gamma_{t})
, we have:

\displaystyle\mu_{i}

Remark 4.8

Observe that the assumption on the simplicity of
