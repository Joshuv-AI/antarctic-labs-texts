Proof of the bounded conformal conjecture
Sameer Kumar

Abstract
. Given any asymptotically flat 3-manifold ( M , g ) (M,g) with smooth, non-empty, compact boundary Σ \Sigma , the conformal conjecture states that for every δ > 0 \delta>0 , there exists a metric g ′ = u 4 ​ g g^{\prime}=u^{4}g , with u u a harmonic function, such that the area of outermost minimal area enclosure Σ ~ g ′ \tilde{\Sigma}_{g^{\prime}} of Σ \Sigma with respect to g ′ g^{\prime} is less than δ \delta . Recently, the conjecture was used to prove the Riemannian Penrose inequality for black holes with zero horizon area, and was proven to be true under the assumption of existence of only a finite number of minimal area enclosures of boundary Σ \Sigma , and boundedness of harmonic function u u . We prove the conjecture assuming only the boundedness of u u .



1.
Introduction

The definition of mass of objects in general relativity has been a fascinating topic for both mathematicians and physicists alike since decades. The ADM formalism
[
1
]
, named after its authors and describing the mass of an asymptotically flat manifold
(M,g)
, is a widely studied topic in mathematical general relativity. It describes the mass of an asymptotically flat end of a manifold
(M,g)
as a flux integral of the gravitational field over a large celestial sphere. A end of manifold
(M,g)
is said be asymptotically flat if it is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash K
, where
K
is a compact set, and the metric
g_{ij}
satisfies the following conditions
[
8
]
:

|g_{ij}-\delta_{ij}|\leq\frac{C}{|x|},\;\;|g_{ij;k}|\leq\frac{C}{|x|^{2}},\;\;Rc\geq-\frac{Cg}{|x|^{2}}

as
|x|\rightarrow\infty
, and where the derivatives in
g_{ij;k}
are taken with respect to the flat metric on
\mathbb{R}^{3}\backslash K
.

The ADM formalism defines the mass of an asymptotically flat spacetime
(M,g)
as -

m_{ADM}=\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{16\pi}\int\limits_{\partial B_{r}}(g_{ij;i}-g_{ii;j})\nu^{j}d\mu_{\delta}

where
\nu^{j}
is the normal vector in j-th direction, and
d\mu_{\delta}
represents the flat metric.

By Stokes’ theorem, the above expression is simply the flux integral of the field form generated by the value under the integral through a large celestial sphere. This is similar to “description of charge" in classical electrodynamics, and we can call the mass of an object as the
“gravitational charge"
generating its gravitational field.

In this paper, by a
black hole
we mean a connected component of the outermost compact minimal surface in
(M,g)
. We will give a more precise description of outermost minimal surfaces in the next section. Penrose
[
10
]
conjectured that the total mass contributed by a black hole should be at least
\sqrt{A/16\pi}
, where
A
is the area of the black hole. Penrose made this argument using physical principles; showing that if the condition was not satisfied, the manifold would admit Cauchy data for the Einstein’s equation that would lead to naked singularities - a contradiction to the so called “cosmic censorship principle", which states that singularities are always encapsulated by a black hole. This inequality is known as the
Penrose inequality
. Schoen and Yau
[
11
,
12
]
proved the following result known as the
Positive mass theorem
.

Theorem 1
.

(Positive mass theorem)
Let
(M,g)
be an asymptotically flat Riemannian 3-manifold with nonnegative scalar curvature. Then
m_{ADM}\geq 0
, with equality in the case when M is isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Although less sharper than originally conjectured by Penrose, the paper was the first prominent step in the direction of the Penrose inequality. In
[
16
]
another proof of was presented by Witten, with techniques involving spinors. The paper also lay forward the idea of a connection of supergravity-like compactifications occuring due to possible instability of Minkowski space in higher dimensions, due to a possibly negative gravitational (ADM) mass. While it was unknown at that time, it is now known that the positive mass theorem holds for all dimensions
[
13
]
, rendering Minkowski space as a stable vaccum solution to Einstein equations in all dimensions. Following the developments of Shoen-Yau and Witten, Huisken and Ilmanen
[
8
]
proved a sharper bound by considering the area of only a single connected component of
\Sigma=\partial M
(equivalent to considering the “largest blackhole" in the manifold) using inverse mean curvature flow. Almost simultaneously, Bray
[
3
]
proved the general case of multiple connected components of boundary
\partial M
(i.e., multiple black hole horizons) using the positive mass theorem.

Theorem 2
.

(Riemannian Penrose inequality)
Let
(M,g)
be an asymptotically flat 3-manifold with nonnegative scalar curvature such that
\partial M
is smooth, compact and non empty. Then
m_{ADM}\geq\sqrt{\frac{A}{16\pi}}
, with the equality taking place when
(M,g)
is isometric to Shwarzchild manifold with mass
m_{ADM}
.

Here,
A
denotes the area of a single connected component of
\partial M
in the theorem of Huisken and Ilmanen
[
8
]
, and the area of
\partial M
in the theorem of Bray
[
3
]
. For a thorough review of the above-said developments, see
[
4
]
.

The current work is connected to a progression of the work stated above, for manifolds where the metric has a so-called
zero area singularity
. Zero area singularities (ZAS) are horizons (i.e. components of the boundary
\partial M
) where the metric trivializes, and hence the area of the horizon tends to zero in a weak surface convergence sense. This is the case, for example, of a Shwarzchild metric with a black hole with “negative mass". Mass bounds for zero area singularities concerns the stability of the existence of such black holes. We will discuss the technical details regarding the convergence of surfaces, and the definition of ZAS in section
2
.

The conformal conjecture was introduced by Bray in
[
5
]
to prove the
Riemannian ZAS inequality
. Jauregui
[
9
]
introduced a weaker statement of the above conjecture, equivalent to the original.

Conjecture 1
.

(Conformal conjecture)
Given
\delta>0
, there exists
g^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{H}(g)}
with outermost minimal area enclosure
\tilde{\Sigma}_{g^{\prime}}
such that :

(1)

\tilde{\Sigma}_{g^{\prime}}
is disjoint from
\Sigma
, and

(2)

||\tilde{\Sigma}_{g^{\prime}}|_{g^{\prime}}-|\Sigma|_{g^{\prime}}|<\delta

In this paper, we prove the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

(Main theorem)
Conformal conjecture is true under the following additional assumption:

(1)

the maximizer
g^{\prime}=u^{4}g
of Theorem 3 below satisfies
u\leq C
for a constant
C>0

Here,
\overline{\mathcal{H}(g)}
is the generalised harmonic conformal class (discussed further in section
2
). The above theorem is an improvement of the following previously known result, and our approach is a generalisation of the approach developed in
[
9
]
.

Theorem 4
.

(Theorem 29
[
9
]
)
Conformal conjecture is true under the following additional assumptions:

(1)

the maximizer
g^{\prime}=u^{4}g
of Theorem 3 below satisfies
u\leq C
for a constant
C>0
, and

(2)

the number of minimal area enclosures of
\Sigma
with respect to
g^{\prime}
is finite.

This paper is structured as follows: section
2
contains the background concepts to be used later, section
3
contains a heuristic proof of the main theorem, section
4
contains the preliminary lemmas leading to the proof of the main theorem, section
5
contains our proof of the main theorem, and section
6
contains a short discussion on some open problems related to this paper, and possible approaches for solving them.

2.1.
Surfaces and minimal area enclosures

Surfaces can be defined in many ways in geometry. One useful way to define surfaces is as functionals acting on forms over them, as followed in
[
9
]
(see also
[
6
]
,
[
14
]
). We call this object a current.

Definition 1
.

(Currents) Let M be a manifold of dimension
m\geq n
. An n-current in
M
is a linear functional on the space
C_{o}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{n}M)
of compactly supported, smooth, differential n-forms.

Submanifolds define current in a natural way - let
U\subset M
be a k-submanifold (
k\geq n
) of M. Then an n-current associated to
U
is defined as

U(\phi)=\int\limits_{U}\phi

for
\phi\in C_{o}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{n}U)
.

A
boundary current
is made to conform to Stokes’ theorem:
\partial S(\phi)=S(\partial\phi)
. An
integral n-current
is a current whose boundary is an n-1 current. We can also define the support of a current in a natural way.

Definition 2
.

The support of an n-current
S
(supp
\phi
) is defined as the intersection of all closed sets
C
such that

\text{supp }\phi\cap C=\emptyset\Rightarrow S(\phi)=0

for all
\phi\in C_{o}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{n}S)
, where
\text{supp }\phi
is the support of
\phi
.

Next, we define the
mass norm
of a current
S
in
M
.

Definition 3
.

The mass norm
|S|
of an n-current
S\subset M
is defined as

|S|=\sup\limits_{\phi}\{S(\phi),\;\phi\in C_{o}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{n}S):||\phi||\leq 1\}

where
||\cdot||
is the max norm for differential n-form
\phi
.

A surface in
M
is hence realised as a 2-current in
M
. We can also define the convergence of surfaces in terms of currents as follows.

Definition 4
.

A sequence of n-currents
\{S_{i}\}
is said to converge (weakly) to an n-current
S
if

S_{i}(\phi)\rightarrow S(\phi)

for all
\phi\in C_{o}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{R}^{n+k})
, where
k\geq 0
.

The above definitions get a more geometric meaning once we define a particular choice of measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n+k}
.

Definition 5
.

The Hausdorff measure of a set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+k}
is defined as:

\mathscr{H}^{n}(S)=\lim\limits_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\inf\limits_{\{C_{j}\}}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{\infty}\omega_{n}\Big(\frac{\text{diam }C_{j}}{2}\Big)

where
\omega_{n}
is the volume of a unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and the infimum is taken over all countable collections
\{C_{j}\}
covering
S
, such that
\text{diam }C_{j}<\delta
for each j.

Then the current of a
\mathscr{H}^{n}
measurable set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be defined as a functional acting on
\phi\in C_{o}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{R}^{n})

S(\phi)=\int\limits_{\Omega}\rho d\mathscr{H}^{n}

since any
\phi\in C_{o}^{\infty}(\Lambda^{n}\mathbb{R}^{n+k})
can be decomposed as
\phi=\rho\omega
, with
\omega
being the volume form for
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This, in particular, allows us to equate the mass norm to the Hausdorff measure of the set:
|S|=\mathscr{H}^{n}(S)
.

We denote the Hausdorff k-measure on
M
with respect to the metric
g
as
\mathscr{H}^{k}_{g}
. Let
\Omega\subset M
be a
\mathscr{H}^{3}_{g}
-measurable set. We will not distinguish notationally between
\Omega
being viewed as a set or a current and use the symbol
\Omega
for both purposes. We let
\Sigma=-\partial M
(the normal at the boundary points into the manifold). We can now define an enclosing surface as follows.

Definition 6
.

Let
\Omega\subset M
be an
\mathscr{H}^{3}_{g}
-measurable, compact set that defines an integral 3-current (of multiplicity one). A surface enclosing
\Sigma
is a 2-current S of the form

S=\Sigma+\partial\Omega

We say that
S
encloses
\Sigma
properly if
S
does not intersect
\Sigma
. Also, we say that
S
is
C^{k,a}
if the supp
S
is
C^{k,a}
embedded submanifold of M. If
S_{i}=\Sigma+\partial\Omega_{i}
,
i=1,2
, then we further say
S_{1}
encloses
S_{2}
if supp
S_{2}\subset
supp
S_{1}
.

By the statement
p\in S
, we mean that
p\in\text{supp }S
,
S\cap\Sigma
means the restriction of current
S
to the set
\Sigma
(defined in the integral sense as the integral being evaluated on the intersection
S\cap\Sigma
, also denoted as
S\llcorner\Sigma
), and
S\backslash\Sigma
as the restriction of
S
to the set
M\backslash\Sigma
.

Definition 7
.

A surface
S
enclosing
\Sigma
is called a minimal area enclosure of
\Sigma
with respect to
g
, if for all surfaces
T
enclosing
\Sigma

|S|_{g}\leq|T|_{g}

where
|\cdot|_{g}
is the mass measure with respect to the metric
g
.

As is evident from the definition, multiple (possibly infinitely many) minimal area enclosures of
\Sigma
can exist and all of them will have the same area. An
outermost minimal area enclosure
of
\Sigma
with respect to
g
, denoted
\tilde{\Sigma}_{g}
, is a minimal area enclosure of
\Sigma
with respect to
g
that encloses every other minimal area enclosures of
\Sigma
with respect to
g
. The following result about the existence of outermost minimal area enclosures is well known
[
3
,
8
]
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
(M,g)
be an asymptotically flat 3-manifold with smooth, non-empty, compact boundary. Let
g
extend smoothly to the boundary. Then, there exists a unique surface
\tilde{\Sigma}
enclosing
\Sigma
with the following properties:

(1)

\tilde{\Sigma}
is a minimal area enclosure of
\Sigma
with respect to
g
.

(2)

If S is any other minimal area enclosure of
\Sigma
with repsect to
g
, then
\tilde{\Sigma}
encloses
S
.

Moreover,
\tilde{\Sigma}
is an embedded
C^{1,1}
surface with non-negative mean curvature, and
\tilde{\Sigma}\backslash\Sigma
, if non-empty, is
C^{\infty}
with zero mean curvature.

It was proved in
[
9
]
, that the set
\tilde{\Sigma}\cap\Sigma
has zero g-measure (c.f. Proposition 36). Since our proof of Theorem
3
and its supporting lemmas relies on the local geometry of the manifold near points in the intersection of
\tilde{\Sigma}
and
\Sigma
, it is necessary to consider the following construction of a diffeomorphism between the neighbourhood of an arbitrary point
p\in\tilde{\Sigma}\cap\Sigma
and the upper half space.
[
9
]

Let
p\in\tilde{\Sigma}\cap\Sigma
, where
\tilde{\Sigma}
denotes the outermost minimal area enclosure of
\Sigma
with respect to the metric
g^{\prime}=u^{4}g
. Choosing
\rho>0
sufficiently small, we construct a diffeomorphism
\Phi
between the sets
\overline{U}=\overline{B(p,\rho)}
and
\overline{B}^{+}=\{z\geq 0\}\cap\overline{B(0,1)}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that

(1)

\Phi(p)=(0,0,0)

(2)

\Phi(\overline{B(p,\rho)}\cap\Sigma)=\{z=0\}

(3)

d\Phi_{p}:T_{p}M\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an isometry.

The third property makes the coordinate frame
\{\partial_{x},\partial_{y},\partial_{z}\}
orthonormal at
p
. We identify the coordinates on the sets
\overline{B(p,\rho)}
and
\overline{B}^{+}
and denote them by
(x,y,z)
.

For
\alpha>0
, define

C_{\alpha}=\{(x,y,z)\in\overline{U}:\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}\leq\alpha z\}.

C_{\alpha}
defines a region in
\overline{U}
above a cone with opening parameter
\alpha
and vertex at
p
. The cone closes as
\alpha\rightarrow 0
and approaches a place as
\alpha\rightarrow+\infty
. We define
lower partial density
as follows.

Definition 8
.

Let
S\subset M
be surface and let
p\in S\cap\Sigma
. We define the the lower partial density, denoted
\Theta_{\alpha}(S,p)
of
S
at point
p
as:

\Theta_{\alpha}(S,p)=\lim\limits_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{|S\llcorner(B(p,r)\cap C_{\alpha})|_{g}}{\pi r^{2}}

Lower partial density is a measure of density of
S
at
p
with contributions coming only from parts within the cone
C_{\alpha}
. For contrast, the
lower density
of
S
at
p
is defined as the quantity:

\Theta(S,p)=\lim\limits_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{|S\llcorner B(p,r)|_{g}}{\pi r^{2}}

We can also define the
partial mass function
as the numerator of the partial density function:

m_{\alpha,r}(S,p)=|S\llcorner(B(p,r)\cap C_{\alpha})|_{g}

2.2.
The generalised harmonic conformal class and maximizers

Assume that
(M,g)
is a smooth, complete, asymptotically flat 3-manifold with non-empty compact smooth boundary
\partial M=\Sigma
. Consider the following equivalence relation:

g^{\prime}\equiv g\Leftrightarrow\;g^{\prime}=u^{4}g,\,\Delta_{g}u=0,\,u>0,\,u\rightarrow 1\text{ at infinity },\,u\text{ is smooth }

In particular, we identify metrics related to each other by well behaved harmonic functions
u
. The classes of equivalence under this identification are called the
harmonic conformal classes
of
M

[
3
]
, and the harmonic conformal class to which
g
belongs will be denoted by
\mathcal{H}(g)
. Every element of
\mathcal{H}(g)
is an asymptotically flat metric, since the harmonic function
u
expands as
[
2
]

u(x)=1+\frac{a}{|x|}+O(\frac{1}{x^{2}})

Hence, all elements of
\mathcal{H}(g)
have the same asymptotic behaviour. The asymptotic normalisation (
u\rightarrow 1
) in the above definition is for convinience. Having defined the conformal class, we can further generalise the above construction to define the
generalised conformal class

[
9
]
of the metric
g
. We do this to generalise the set
\mathcal{H}(g)
to some larger compact set
\overline{\mathcal{H}}(g)\supset\mathcal{H}(g)
, in order to take maximums over it (used in the definition of maximizer below).

Let
f\geq 0
belong to class
L^{4}(\Sigma)
with respect to area measure
dA_{g}
induces by
g
. For
x\in\text{int }M
define

u(x)=\varphi(x)+\int\limits_{\Sigma}K(x,y)f(y)dA_{g}(y)

where
\varphi(x)
is the unique g-harmonic function that vanishes on
\Sigma
and tends to one at infinity, and
K(x,y)
is the Poisson kernel for
(M,g)
with
x\in M,\,y\in\Sigma,\,x\neq y
. Hence, by definition,
u
is g-harmonic in the interior of
M
and tends to one at infinity. We will call
u
as the harmonic function associated to
f
. Since
f
is determined uniquely by
u
(under almost everywhere equivalence), we will call
f
to be the function in
L^{4}(\Sigma)
determined by
u
. Since
u
need not extend smoothly onto the boundary
\Sigma
,
u^{4}g
is a smooth metric on
M\backslash\Sigma
.

Definition 9
.

The
generalised harmonic conformal class
of
g
is the set
\overline{\mathcal{H}(g)}
of all Riemannian metrics
u^{4}g
on
M\backslash\Sigma
, where
u
is the harmonic function associated to some non-negative
f\in L^{4}(\Sigma)
.

Having defined the generalised harmonic conformal class, we can define the area of surfaces
S
enclosing the boundary
\Sigma
with respect to
g^{\prime}=u^{4}g\in\overline{\mathcal{H}(g)}
as

|S|_{g^{\prime}}=|S|_{u^{4}g}=\int\limits_{S\cap\Sigma}f^{4}d\mathscr{H}^{2}_{g}+\int\limits_{S\backslash\Sigma}u^{4}d\mathscr{H}^{2}_{g}

For our purposes, it is necessary to define the
convergence of metrics
in
\overline{\mathcal{H}(g)}
.

Definition 10
.

Let
\{u_{n}^{4}g\}
be a sequence of metrics in
\overline{\mathcal{H}(g)}
associated with
\{f_{n}\}
of functions in
L^{4}(\Sigma)
, and let
u^{4}g\in\overline{\mathcal{H}(g)}
be associated to
f\in L^{4}(\Sigma)
. Then the sequence
\{u_{n}^{4}g\}
is said to
converge weakly
to the metric
u^{4}g
if
\{f_{n}\}
converges weakly to
f
in
L^{4}(\Sigma)
.

Preliminary results regarding the topology of
\overline{\mathcal{H}(g)}
were proved in
[
9
]
. We state here a result that will be helpful to prove the results in section
4.1
.

Lemma 1
.

(Lemma 15 of
[
9
]
)
Suppose a sequence of metrics
\{u_{n}^{4}g\}
in
\overline{\mathcal{H}(g)}
converges weakly to
u^{4}g\in\overline{\mathcal{H}(g)}
. Then
u_{n}
converges pointwise to
u
in the interior of
M
.

The
g
-area of a minimal area enclosure (if it exists) of
\Sigma
with respect to
g
is equal to the
minimal enclosing area
of
\Sigma
with respect to
g
. More formally,

Definition 11
.

For
g^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{H}(g)}
, the
minimal enclosing area
of
\Sigma
with respect to
g^{\prime}
, denoted min
(\Sigma,g^{\prime})
, is

\text{min}(\Sigma,g^{\prime})=\inf\limits_{g^{\prime}}\{|S|_{g^{\prime}}:\text{ $S$ encloses $\Sigma$}\}.

Minimal area enclosure
and
outermost minimal area enclosure
of
\Sigma
are defined as in the last section. At last, we can define the
maximizer
.

Definition 12
.

For
A>0
, we define

\alpha(A)=\sup\limits_{g^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathcal{H}(g)}}\{\text{min}(\Sigma,g^{\prime}):|\Sigma|_{g^{\prime}}\leq A\}.

The metric
g^{\prime}
attaining the supremum in the above definition is called the
maximizer
for
\alpha(A)

The proof of the existence of the maximizer
g^{\prime}
above was presented in
[
9
]
(Theorem 32).

2.3.
Convergence of surfaces and ZAS

For other purposes and for defining zero area singularities (ZAS), we need to define the convergence of surfaces. Preliminary definition of convergence of surfaces, viewed as currents in
M
, was presented in section
2.1
. In this section, we present a definition based on convergence of area measures.

Definition 13
.

A sequence of surfaces
\{S_{i}=\Sigma+\Omega_{i}\}
is said to
converge
to a surface
S=\Sigma+\Omega
if the sequence
\{\Omega_{i}-\Omega\}
converges to zero in mass measure:

|\Omega_{i}-\Omega|_{g}\rightarrow 0

Stronger
C^{k}
modes of convergence of
C^{k}
-surfaces were presented in
[
5
]
, mass convergence is implied by these
C^{k}
convergence modes. This approach considers a convergence of surfaces to a surface
\Sigma^{0}\subset M
as a convergence of “graph" of surfaces near
\Sigma^{0}
as follows: let
U\subset M
be a neighbourhood of
\Sigma^{0}
diffeomorphic to
\Sigma^{0}\times[0,a)
for some
a>0
. Let
(x,s)
be coordinates on
U
with
x\in\Sigma^{0}
and
s\in[0,a)
. We say that a surface
S\subset U
is a
graph
over
\Sigma^{0}
if it can be parametrised as
s=s(x)
for
x\in\Sigma^{0}
. Then the
C^{k}

convergence
of surfaces in
U
to
\Sigma^{0}
is defined as follows:

Definition 14
.

A sequence of
C^{k}
surfaces
\{S_{i}\}
that are graphs over
\Sigma^{0}
is said to
converge
to
\Sigma^{0}
in
C^{k}
if the associated coordinate functions (see above)
s_{n}:\Sigma^{0}\rightarrow[0,a)
converges to zero in
C^{k}
.

In
[
5
]
, authors have considered the surfaces to be
C^{\infty}
, although the definition stated above is equivalent. We can now define
zero area singularities
as follows.

Definition 15
.

Let
(M,g)
be a 3-manifold with smooth, compact, nonempty boundary
\Sigma
. Assume that
g
is smooth on
M\backslash\Sigma
. A connected component
\Sigma^{0}
of
\Sigma
is called a
zero area singularity (ZAS)
of
g
if for every sequence of smooth surfaces
\{S_{i}\}
properly enclosing
\Sigma^{0}
and converging in
C^{1}
to
\Sigma^{0}
, the sequence of areas
\{|S_{n}|_{g}\}
converges to zero.

3.
Heuristic argument of the proof

Our proof of theorem
3
can be conveniently split into two parts. It is a metric-variation argument over all surfaces
S
enclosing the boundary component
\Sigma
. Our proof is same as of Theorem 29 in
[
9
]
, except that we “kick out" the collection of surfaces enclosing
\Sigma
with
zero partial density
from the main variational argument. We justify this removal of surfaces with zero lower partial density by proving that the outermost minimal area enclosure of
\Sigma
cannot have zero lower partial density. This is achieved in two steps. Firstly, let
p\in\tilde{\Sigma}\cap S
, then, in section
8
we prove that the lower partial density
\Theta_{\alpha}(S,p)
of a surface
S
is positive under the assumption that its numerator (i.e., the
partial mass function
) is not trivially zero. The argument for proving this is based on Lemma 25 of
[
9
]
, with introduction of new techniques for defining
partial slices of surfaces
, and their properties thereby. Secondly, in section
9
we prove that the partial mass function
m_{\alpha,r}(\tilde{\Sigma},p)
(defined as the numerator of
\Theta_{\alpha}(\tilde{\Sigma},p)
) of an outermost minimal area enclosure
\tilde{\Sigma}
of
\Sigma
cannot be trivially zero by using a coarea argument. These two arguments imply that that lower partial density of an outermost minimal area enclosure
\tilde{\Sigma}
of
\Sigma

At last, let us discuss the metric-variation argument of the main theorem more explicitly. The idea was presented originally in Theorem 29 of
