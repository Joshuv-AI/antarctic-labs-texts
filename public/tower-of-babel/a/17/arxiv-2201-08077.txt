Spectral properties of the Neumann-Poincaré operator on rotationally symmetric domains in two dimensions Thanks: This work was partially supported by NRF (of S. Korea) grant No. 2019R1A2B5B01069967.
Yong-Gwan Ji, Hyeonbae Kang

Abstract
This paper concerns the spectral properties of the Neumann-Poincaré operator on m m -fold rotationally symmetric planar domains. An m m -fold rotationally symmetric simply connected domain D D is realized as the m m th-root transform of a certain domain, say Ω \Omega . We prove that the domain of definition of the Neumann-Poincaré operator on D D is decomposed into invariant subspaces and the spectrum on one of them is the exact copy of the spectrum on Ω \Omega . It implies in particular that the spectrum on the transformed domain D D contains the spectrum on the original domain Ω \Omega counting multiplicities.

Introduction

The purpose of the present paper is to investigate the spectral properties of the NP operator (NP is the acronym of Neumann-Poincaré) on
m
-fold rotationally symmetric planar domains, which are domains invariant under the rotation by the angle
2\pi/m
. An
m
-fold rotationally symmetric domain, if it is simply connected, is realized as the
m
th-root transform of a certain domain (see below). We are particularly interested in how the spectrum of the original domain is inherited by the
m
th-root transform.

The
m
th-root transform is defined as follows. Let
\Omega
be a simply connected bounded domain containing
0
in its interior. By the Riemann mapping theorem there exist a positive constant
R
and a univalent function
\Psi
from
\{|z|>R\}
onto
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
which admits a Laurent series expansion of the form

\Psi(z)=z+a_{0}+\frac{a_{1}}{z}+\frac{a_{2}}{z^{2}}+\cdots.

(1.1)

For a positive integer
m
, the
m
th-root transform of
\Psi
is defined to be

\Psi_{m}(z):=\Psi(z^{m})^{1/m}

(1.2)

for
|z|>R^{1/m}
. It is known (see
[
6
, p. 28]
) that
\Psi_{m}
is univalent.

Let
\Omega_{m}
be the bounded domain defined by
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\Omega_{m}}:=\Psi_{m}(\{|z|>R^{1/m}\})
. The domain
\Omega_{m}
is called the
m
th-root transform of
\Omega
. It is easy to see that
\Omega_{m}
is
m
-fold rotationally symmetric. It is also easy to prove (actually it is left as an exercise in
[
6
]
) that any simply connected
m
-fold rotationally symmetric domain is an
m
th-root transform of a certain domain.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain with the Lipschitz continuous boundary. The NP operator (NP is the acronym of Neumann-Poincaré), denoted by
\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega}
, is the boundary integral operator on
\partial\Omega
defined by

\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega}[\varphi](x):=\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu_{y}}\Gamma(x-y)\,\varphi(y)\,d\sigma(y),\quad x\in\partial\Omega,

(1.3)

where
\partial_{\nu_{y}}
denotes the outward normal derivative at the point
y\in\partial\Omega
and
\Gamma(x-y)
is the fundamental solution to the Laplacian. In two dimensions which this paper is concerned with,
\Gamma(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln|x|
. The integral above is understood as the Cauchy principal value if
\partial\Omega
is merely Lipschitz continuous.

The NP operator appears naturally when solving the classical boundary value problem on
\Omega
in terms of layer potential, which was initiated by Neumann and Poincaré as the name of the operator suggests. Recently there is rapidly growing interest in the spectral properties of the NP operator in relation to the plasmonic resonance on meta material and significant new results are being produced. We refer to recent surveys
[
3
,
12
]
for historical account and recent development on the operator. In relation to the subject of this paper, we mention that
\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega}
can be realized as a self-adjoint operator on
\mathcal{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
(
\mathcal{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
is the Sobolev space of order
1/2
on the curve
\partial\Omega
) by introducing a new inner product
[
13
]
. If
\partial\Omega
is
C^{1,\alpha}
-smooth for some
\alpha>0
, then
\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega}
is compact on
\mathcal{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
and hence its spectrum consists of eigenvalues and their limit point
0
; If it has a corner, then
\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega}
admits nontrivial essential spectrum (see, for example,
[
15
]
).

The following is the main result of this paper. Here and throughout this paper,
\sigma(\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega},\mathcal{H})
denotes the spectrum of
\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega}
on
\mathcal{H}
for a subspace
\mathcal{H}
of
\mathcal{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
invariant under
\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega}
. When
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, we denote it by
\sigma(\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega})
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a simply connected domain with
C^{1,\alpha}
boundary for some
\alpha>0
. Suppose
0\in\Omega
and let
\Omega_{m}
be the
m
th-root transform of
\Omega
. Then, there are subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{0},\ldots,\mathcal{H}_{m_{*}}
(
m_{*}=m/2
if
m
is even and
m_{*}=(m-1)/2
if
m
is odd) of
\mathcal{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega_{m})
invariant under
\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega_{m}}
such that

\mathcal{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega_{m})=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{m_{*}}\mathcal{H}_{j}

(1.4)

and

\sigma(\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega})=\bigcup_{j=0}^{m_{*}}\sigma(\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega},\mathcal{H}_{j}).

(1.5)

Moreover, there is a unitary transform
U
from
\mathcal{H}_{0}
onto
\mathcal{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
such that

\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega_{m}}|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}=U^{-1}\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega}U,

(1.6)

where
\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega_{m}}|_{\mathcal{H}_{0}}
denotes the
\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega_{m}}
restricted to
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, and hence

\sigma(\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega},\mathcal{H}_{0})=\sigma(\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega}),

(1.7)

counting multiplicities.

Above theorem says in particular that

\sigma(\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega})\subset\sigma(\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega_{m}}).

If
m\geq 2
, then the inclusion is proper counting multiplicities since
\mathcal{H}_{j}
(
j\geq 1
) is not empty. There are domains for which the inclusion is proper as sets, namely, there is an eigenvalue on
\partial\Omega_{m}
which is not an eigenvalue on
\partial\Omega
. For example, let
\Omega
be a disk centered at a point other than the origin. Then spectrum
\sigma(\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega})
consists of
0
and
1/2
, where
0
is an eigenvalue of infinite multiplicities and
1/2
is a simple eigenvalue. The
m
th-root transform
\Omega_{m}
of
\Omega
is the
m
-leaf symmetric lemniscate and
\sigma(\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega_{m}})
contains infinitely many nonzero eigenvalues in addition to the eigenvalue
0
of infinite multiplicities since it is known that if the NP operator on a bounded planar domain is of finite rank, then the domain must be a disk
[
18
, Theorem 7.6]
.

There are not many classes of domains where NP spectra (spectra of the NP operators) are known. Ellipses are among them (see, for example,
[
13
]
). Since the NP spectrum is invariant under Möbius transform
[
17
]
(see also
[
10
]
), the NP spectrum on the limaçon of Pascal can be computed. The limaçon of Pascal is the image of the unit disc under the map
w=z+Az^{2}
for some constant
A
and can be realized as the Möbius transform of an ellipse (see
[
2
]
). On the other hand, it is proved in
[
13
]
that lemniscates has
0
as an NP eigenvalue and its multiplicity is infinite. Lemniscates have NP eigenvalues other than
0
even though we don’t know what they are. Theorem
1.1
yields a way to construct domains, via
m
th-root transforms, whose partial NP eigenvalues can be computed.

Theorem
1.1
is proved using the representation of the NP operator in terms of the Grunsky coefficients which is obtained in
[
11
]
. In the next section we briefly review the Grunsky coefficients and present a simple alternative proof of the representation based on Cauchy’s theorem. Using the representation we also give an alternative proof of the fact that the NP operator on lemniscates have the infinite dimensional kernel which was proved in
[
13
, Theorem 9]
as mentioned before. The proof of Theorem
1.1
is presented in section
3
. In the section to follow we discuss the matrix representation of the NP operator on
m
-fold rotationally symmetric planar domains. In the last section we discuss two examples:
m
-star shaped domains and the Cassini oval. It is interesting, but seems quite difficult (if possible), to compute the spectrum on other subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{H}_{m_{*}}
. We discuss possibility of computing them on the Cassini oval, which seems the simplest possible case: The Cassini oval is the 2nd-root transform of a disk.

Throughout this paper, we use notation
z
to denote a point in
\mathbb{C}
or a point in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, namely,
z=(z_{1},z_{2})
.

The NP operator and Grunsky coefficients, lemniscates

2.1
Double layer potential and Cauchy transform

Here and afterwards, we assume that
\Omega
be a simply connected bounded planar domain with
C^{1,\alpha}
boundary for some
\alpha>0
.
Note that the NP operator
\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega}[\varphi](z)
is defined only for
z\in\partial\Omega
, in other words,
\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega}
is an operator on
\partial\Omega
. If we define the integral for
z
outside
\partial\Omega
, it is called the double layer potential, that is,

\mathcal{D}_{\partial\Omega}[\varphi](z):=\int_{\partial\Omega}\partial_{\nu_{w}}\Gamma(w-z)\,\varphi(w)\,d\sigma(w),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\partial\Omega.

(2.1)

The NP operator and the double layer potential enjoy the following jump relation: for
z\in\partial\Omega

\mathcal{D}_{\partial\Omega}[\varphi]|_{\pm}(z):=\lim_{t\to+0}\mathcal{D}_{\partial\Omega}[\varphi](z\pm t\nu_{z})=\left(\mp\frac{1}{2}+\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega}\right)[\varphi](z),

(2.2)

where
\nu_{z}
denotes the (complexified) outward unit normal vector at
z
(see, for example,
[
1
]
).

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\partial\Omega}
be the Cauchy transform, that is,

\mathcal{C}_{\partial\Omega}[\varphi](z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\varphi(w)}{w-z}\,dw,\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\partial\Omega.

(2.3)

The following relation holds (see
[
18
, p. 67]
):

\mathcal{D}_{\partial\Omega}[\varphi](z)=\frac{\mathcal{C}_{\partial\Omega}[\varphi](z)+\overline{\mathcal{C}_{\partial\Omega}[\overline{\varphi}](z)}}{2}.

(2.4)

In fact, it can be proved using the relations

\partial_{\nu_{z}}=\Re{\left[(\nu_{1}\partial_{x}+\nu_{2}\partial_{y})+i(-\nu_{1}\partial_{y}+\nu_{2}\partial_{x})\right]}=2\Re{\left(\nu_{z}\partial_{z}\right)}

and
\tau_{w}d\sigma(w)=dw
, where
\tau_{w}
is the unit tangential vector (with the positive orientation) at
w\in\partial\Omega
. Here
z=x+iy
and
\partial_{z}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{x}-i\partial_{y})
.

2.2
The NP operator and Grunsky coefficients

From now on we denote the set
\{|z|>R\}
by
B_{R}
. Let
\mathcal{H}^{1/2}(\partial B_{R})
be the Sobolev space of order
1/2
and let
\mathcal{H}^{1/2}_{0}(\partial B_{R})
be the subspace of functions of mean value zero, i.e., the collection of
f\in\mathcal{H}^{1/2}(\partial B_{R})
of the form
f(z)=\sum_{n\neq 0}a_{n}(z/R)^{n}
. For such a function, the homogeneous
\mathcal{H}^{1/2}
norm is defined by

\|f\|^{2}=\sum_{n\neq 0}|n||a_{n}|^{2},

(2.5)

and it is equivalent to the usual
\mathcal{H}^{1/2}
norm. For an integer
n\neq 0
, let

f_{n}(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|n|}}\left(\frac{z}{R}\right)^{n},\quad z\in\partial B_{R}.

(2.6)

The functions
f_{n}
form an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}^{1/2}_{0}(\partial B_{R})
.

Let
\Psi
be the Riemann mapping from
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{B_{R}}
onto
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
of the form (
1.1
). Let
\mathcal{H}^{1/2}_{0}(\partial\Omega)
be the collection of functions
g
such that
g\circ\Psi\in\mathcal{H}^{1/2}_{0}(\partial B_{R})
, and define the norm on
\mathcal{H}^{1/2}_{0}(\partial\Omega)
by

\|g\|_{*}:=\|g\circ\Psi\|.

(2.7)

Since
\partial\Omega
is assumed to be
C^{1,\alpha}
,
\Psi
is
C^{1,\alpha}
up to
\partial\Omega

[
16
, Theorem 3.6]
. Thus, the norm
\|\ \|_{*}
is equivalent to the usual
\mathcal{H}^{1/2}
norm on
\mathcal{H}^{1/2}_{0}(\partial\Omega)
.

For each integer
n\neq 0
, define
g_{n}
by

g_{n}(w):=(f_{n}\circ\Psi^{-1})(w),\quad w\in\partial\Omega.

(2.8)

Then,
g_{n}
,
n\neq 0
, form an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
with respect to the inner product induced by the norm
\|\ \|_{*}
. We note that since
\overline{f_{n}}=f_{-n}
,

\overline{g_{n}}=g_{-n}.

(2.9)

If
n>0
,
\left(\Psi^{-1}(w)\right)^{n}
can be uniquely decomposed as

\left(\Psi^{-1}(w)\right)^{n}=F_{n}(w)+\widehat{F}_{n}(w),

(2.10)

where
F_{n}(w)
is a polynomial and
\widehat{F}_{n}(w)
is an analytic function in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
such that
\widehat{F}_{n}(w)\to 0
as
|w|\to\infty
. The function
F_{n}(w)
is called the Faber polynomial of degree
n
generated by
\Psi^{-1}
. Let the Laurent series expansion of
\widehat{F}_{n}(\Psi(z))
be given by

\widehat{F}_{n}(\Psi(z))=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{n,k}}{z^{k}},

(2.11)

so that

\left(\Psi^{-1}(w)\right)^{n}=F_{n}(w)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{n,k}}{(\Psi^{-1}(w))^{k}}

(2.12)

The coefficients
c_{n,k}
are called Grunsky coefficients. See
[
5
]
for the Faber polynomials and the Grunsky coefficients.

The following theorem is obtained in
[
11
]
. We include an alternative proof based on the Cauchy integral formula.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
11
]
)
.

Let

\mu_{n,k}=\frac{\sqrt{k}}{2\sqrt{n}}\frac{c_{n,k}}{R^{n+k}},\quad n,k=1,2,\ldots.

(2.13)

It holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega}[g_{n}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu_{n,k}\,g_{-k},

(2.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega}[g_{-n}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\overline{\mu_{n,k}}\,g_{k},

(2.15)

for all
n=1,2,\cdots
.

Proof.

We assume
R=1
for simplicity. Since
F_{n}
is analytic in
\Omega
,
\widehat{F}_{n}
is analytic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
and satisfies
\widehat{F}_{n}(w)\to 0
as
|w|\to\infty
, and both of them are continuous up to the boundaries, the Cauchy integral formula and Cauchy’s theorem yield that
\mathcal{C}_{\partial\Omega}[g_{n}](w)=F_{n}(w)/|n|^{1/2}
if
w\in\Omega
. Since
\Psi^{-1}(w)^{-n}\to 0
as
|w|\to\infty
, we see that
\mathcal{C}_{\partial\Omega}[g_{-n}](w)=0
if
w\in\Omega
. It then follows from (
2.4
) and (
2.9
) that
\mathcal{D}_{\partial\Omega}[g_{n}](w)=F_{n}(w)/(2|n|^{1/2})
if
w\in\Omega
. We then infer from the jump relation (
2.2
) that

\mathcal{K}_{\partial\Omega}[g_{n}]=\mathcal{D}_{\partial\Omega}[g_{n}]|_{-}-\frac{1}{2}g_{n}=-\frac{\widehat{F}_{n}}{2\sqrt{|n|}}.

Now, (
2.14
) follows from (
2.11
) and the definition (
2.8
) of
g_{n}
. (
2.15
) follows from (
2.9
) and (
2.14
).
∎

shows that if
g=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(a_{n}g_{n}+b_{n}g_{-n})\in\mathcal{H}_{0}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)

(

]

where

The following theorem is proved in

Theorem 2.2

(
[
13
]
)
.

If

Here we give an alternative proof of this theorem using the representation of

If

Proof of Theorem
1.1

Since

Let

\left(\Psi_{D}^{-1}(w)\right)^{n}=F_{n}^{D}(w)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{n,k}^{D}}{\Psi_{D}^{-1}(w)^{k}}.

(3.1)

Let

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
D
be a simply connected
m
-fold rotationally symmetric (with respect to the point 0) domain. Then, for

\mu_{n,k}^{D}=0\;\;\text{if}\;\;n+k\not\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;\;m).

(3.2)

Let
\zeta_{m}=e^{2\pi i/m}

On the other hands,

\left(\Psi_{D}^{-1}(\zeta_{m}w)\right)^{n}=F_{n}^{D}(\zeta_{m}w)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{n,k}^{D}}{\zeta_{m}^{k}\left(\Psi_{D}^{-1}(w)\right)^{k}}.

By equating two identities above, we obtain

c_{n,k}^{D}\left(\zeta_{m}^{n+k}-1\right)=0,

and hence

if
n+k\not\equiv 0\;(\text{mod}\;\;m)
. Thus, (
3.2
) follows.

We thus have

\left(\Psi_{D}^{-1}(w)\right)^{mn}=\left(\Psi_{\Omega}^{-1}(w^{m})\right)^{n}=F_{n}^{\Omega}(w^{m})-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{n,k}^{\Omega}}{\left(\Psi_{\Omega}^{-1}(w^{m})\right)^{k}}.

On the other hands, we have from (
3.2
) that

\displaystyle\left(\Psi_{D}^{-1}(w)\right)^{mn}

\displaystyle=F_{mn}^{D}(w)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{{mn},k}^{D}}{\left(\Psi_{D}^{-1}(w)\right)^{k}}

\displaystyle=F_{mn}^{D}(w)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{{mn},mk}^{D}}{\left(\Psi_{D}^{-1}(w)\right)^{mk}}

\displaystyle=F_{mn}^{D}(w)-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{c_{{mn},mk}^{D}}{\left(\Psi_{\Omega}^{-1}(w^{m})\right)^{k}}.

Therefore we have
c_{{mn},mk}^{D}=c_{{n},k}^{\Omega}
and (
3.5
) follows.
∎

4
Matrix representations

Let
D
be a simply connected
m
-fold rotationally symmetric domain and let
\Omega
be the domain such that
D=\Omega_{m}
. The matrix representations of
\mathcal{K}_{\partial D}
would yield a clear picture on the spectral properties.

As in (
2.16
),
\mathcal{K}_{\partial D}
is represented by the operator
[\mathcal{K}_{\partial D}]
on
\ell^{2}\times\ell^{2}
defined by

\left[\mathcal{K}_{\partial D}\right](a,b)=(\overline{\mathcal{M}_{\partial D}}b,\mathcal{M}_{\partial D}a),

where
\mathcal{M}_{\partial D}=(\mu_{n,k}^{D})_{n,k\geq 1}^{t}
. According to (
3.2
),

\mathcal{M}_{\partial D}
takes the form

\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc|c|ccc|c|c}0&&\mu_{1,m-1}^{D}&&0&&\mu_{1,2m-1}^{D}&&\\
&\iddots&&0&&\iddots&&0&\cdots\\
\mu_{m-1,1}^{D}&&0&&\mu_{m-1,m+1}^{D}&&0&&\\
\hline\cr&0&&\mu_{m,m}^{D}&&0&&\mu_{m,2m}^{D}&\cdots\\
\hline\cr 0&&\mu_{m+1,m-1}^{D}&&0&&\mu_{m+1,2m-1}^{D}&&\\
&\iddots&&0&&\iddots&&0&\cdots\\
\mu_{2m-1,1}^{D}&&0&&\mu_{2m-1,m+1}^{D}&&0&&\\
\hline\cr&0&&\mu_{2m,m}^{D}&&0&&\mu_{2m,2m}^{D}&\cdots\\
\hline\cr&\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots&\ddots\end{array}\right].

Thus
\mathcal{M}_{j}
represneting
\mathcal{K}_{\partial D}|_{\mathcal{H}_{j}}
as in (
3.11
) is given as follow:

(i)

If
j=0
or
j=m/2
(
m
is even), then

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{j}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\mu_{m-j,m-j}^{D}&\mu_{m-j,2m-j}^{D}&\mu_{m-j,3m-j}^{D}&\cdots\\
\mu_{2m-j,m-j}^{D}&\mu_{2m-j,2m-j}^{D}&\mu_{2m-j,3m-j}^{D}&\cdots\\
\mu_{3m-j,m-j}^{D}&\mu_{3m-j,2m-j}^{D}&\mu_{3m-j,3m-j}^{D}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{array}\right].

(ii)

Otherwise,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{j}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc|cc|c}0&\mu^{D}_{j,m-j}&0&\mu^{D}_{j,2m-j}&\cdots\\
\mu^{D}_{m-j,j}&0&\mu^{D}_{m-j,m+j}&0&\cdots\\
\hline\cr 0&\mu^{D}_{m+j,m-j}&0&\mu^{D}_{m+j,2m-j}&\cdots\\
\mu^{D}_{2m-j,j}&0&\mu^{D}_{2m-j,m+j}&0&\cdots\\
\hline\cr\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{array}\right].

Note that

\mathcal{M}_{0}=\mathcal{M}_{\partial\Omega},

(4.10)

which is due to (
3.5
).

We note that
\mathcal{M}_{j}^{t}=\mathcal{M}_{j}
. So, if
\mathcal{M}_{j}
is real, then it admits the diagonalization, and its eigenvalues completely determines those of
\mathcal{K}_{\partial D}|_{\mathcal{H}_{j}}
. In fact, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{M}_{j}
is real. If
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{K}_{\partial D}|_{\mathcal{H}_{j}}
, then either
\lambda
or
-\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{M}_{j}
. Conversely, if
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{M}_{j}
, then both
\lambda
and
-\lambda
are eigenvalues of
\mathcal{K}_{\partial D}|_{\mathcal{H}_{j}}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{K}_{\partial D}|_{\mathcal{H}_{j}}
. According to (
3.11
), there is
a,b\in\ell^{2}
(not both zero) such that

\mathcal{M}_{j}b=\lambda a,\quad\mathcal{M}_{j}a=\lambda b.

Thus we have

\mathcal{M}_{j}(a+b)=\lambda(a+b),\quad\mathcal{M}_{j}(a-b)=-\lambda(a-b).

Since either
a+b
or
a-b
is nonzero, we infer that
\lambda
or
-\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{M}_{j}
.

If
\lambda
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{M}_{j}
, then there is nonzero
a\in\ell^{2}
such that

\mathcal{M}_{j}a=\lambda a.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{K}_{\partial D}|_{\mathcal{H}_{j}}\right](a,a)=(\mathcal{M}_{j}a,\mathcal{M}_{j}a)=\lambda(a,a),

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{K}_{\partial D}|_{\mathcal{H}_{j}}\right](a,-a)=(-\mathcal{M}_{j}a,\mathcal{M}_{j}a)=-\lambda(a,-a).

We conclude that both
\lambda
and
-\lambda
are eigenvalues of
\mathcal{K}_{\partial D}|_{\mathcal{H}_{j}}
.
∎
