Fast and Provable Simultaneous Blind Super-Resolution and Demixing for Point Source Signals: Scaled Gradient Descent without Regularization
Jinchi Chen

Abstract
We address the problem of simultaneously recovering a sequence of point source signals from observations limited to the low-frequency end of the spectrum of their summed convolution, where the point spread functions (PSFs) are unknown. By exploiting the low-dimensional structures of the signals and PSFs, we formulate this as a low-rank matrix demixing problem. To solve this, we develop a scaled gradient descent method without balancing regularization. We establish theoretical guarantees under mild conditions, demonstrating that our method, with spectral initialization, converges to the ground truth at a linear rate, independent of the condition number of the underlying data matrices. Numerical experiments indicate that our approach is competitive with existing convex methods in terms of both recovery accuracy and computational efficiency.

Introduction

I-A

Problem Formulation

The problem of blind super-resolution is about estimating high-resolution information of a signal from its measurements at the lower end of spectrum when the point spread functions are unknown. This technique is widely used in various fields, including seismic data analysis
[
1
]
, nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy
[
2
]
, and 3D single-molecule microscopy
[
3
]
. In this paper, we consider a more general and difficult problem in which we have to extract multiple point sources from one low-frequency observation. Such problem arises in a range of applications such as integrated sensing and communications
[
4
,
5
]
, over the air computation
[
6
,
7
]
and internet-of-things
[
8
]
.

More specifically, consider a series of point source signal of the form:

\displaystyle x_{k}(t)=\sum_{p=1}^{r}d_{k,p}\delta(t-\tau_{k,p}),k=1,\cdots,K,

where
K
means the number of signals or users,
\delta(\cdot)
represents the Dirac function,
r
indicates the number of spikes of the
k
-th signal, and
\{\tau_{k,p}\}_{p=1}^{r}\subset[0,1)
and
\{d_{k,p}\}_{p=1}^{r}\subset\mathbb{C}
represent the locations and amplitudes of the point source signals, respectively.

Let
y(t)
be the summation of point source signals convolved with unknown point spread functions, given by

\displaystyle y(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\sum_{p=1}^{r}d_{k,p}\delta(t-\tau_{k,p})\ast g_{k,p}(t)\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{p=1}^{r}d_{k,p}g_{k,p}(t-\tau_{k,p}).

(I.1)

By taking the Fourier transform of (
I.1
)

\displaystyle\hat{y}(f)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{p=1}^{r}d_{k,p}\int g_{k,p}(t-\tau_{k,p})e^{-2\imath\pi ft}dt=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{p=1}^{r}d_{k,p}\hat{g}_{k,p}(f)e^{-2\imath\pi f\tau_{k,p}}dt,

where
\hat{g}_{k,p}(f)
is the Fourier transform of
g_{k,p}(t)
. Subsequently sampling, we obtain that

\displaystyle\hat{y}[j]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{p=1}^{r}d_{k,p}e^{-\imath 2\pi(j-1)\tau_{k,p}}\hat{g}_{k,p}[j],\quad j=0,\cdots,n-1.

(I.2)

where
\bm{g}_{k,p}:=\begin{bmatrix}\hat{g}_{k,p}[0]&\cdots&\hat{g}_{k,p}[n-1]\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}

are unknown. The goal is to jointly recover both
\{d_{k,p},\tau_{k,p}\}
and
\{\bm{g}_{k,p}\}
from (
I.2
). We refer to this issue as simultaneous blind super-resolution and demixing. Specifically, when there is only one user (
K=1
), the measurement model in (
I.2
) simplifies to the problem of blind super-resolution, as discussed in
[
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
. Furthermore, if there are two types of signals (
K=2
), with one related to communication and the other associated with radar, the problem described in (
I.2
) can be considered a case of dual-blind deconvolution, as examined in
[
13
]
.

Since the number of unknowns
{\cal O}(Knr)
in (
I.2
) is larger than the number of samples, simultaneous blind super-resolution and demixing is an ill-posed problem. To alleviate this issue, we follow the same route as that in
[
14
,
9
,
10
,
12
]
and assume that the set of vectors
\{\bm{g}_{k,p}\}_{p=1}^{r}
associated with the
k
-th unknown point spread function belong to a known low dimensional subspace spanned by the columns of
\bm{B}_{k}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times s}
with
s<n
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{k,p}=\bm{B}_{k}\bm{h}_{k,p},\quad p=1,\cdots,r,

where
\bm{h}_{k,p}\in\mathbb{C}^{s}
denotes an unknown coefficient vector. Then the measurements can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle y[j]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle\bm{b}_{k,j}\bm{e}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}},\sum_{p=1}^{r}d_{k,p}\bm{h}_{k,p}\bm{a}_{\tau_{k,p}}^{\mathsf{T}}\right\rangle:=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\langle\bm{b}_{k,j}\bm{e}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}},\bm{X}_{k,\natural}\right\rangle,\quad j=0,\cdots,n-1,

(I.3)

where the inner product of two matrices
\bm{A},\bm{B}
is defined as
\left\langle\bm{A},\bm{B}\right\rangle=\trace(\bm{A}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{B})
,
\bm{e}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
represents the
j
-th standard basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\bm{b}_{k,j}\in\mathbb{C}^{s}
denotes the
j
-th column of
\bm{B}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}
, and
\bm{a}_{\tau}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
stands as the steering vector, defined as

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&e^{-\imath 2\pi\cdot 1\cdot\tau}&\cdots&e^{-\imath 2\pi\cdot(n-1)\cdot\tau}\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.

Let
{\cal A}_{k}:\mathbb{C}^{s\times n}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a linear operator such that

\displaystyle{\cal A}_{k}(\bm{X})[j]=\left\langle\bm{b}_{k,j}\bm{e}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}},\bm{X}\right\rangle.

(I.4)

Then the measurement model (
I.3
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\bm{y}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\cal A}_{k}(\bm{X}_{k,\natural}),

(I.5)

where
\bm{y}=\begin{bmatrix}y[0]&\cdots&y[n-1]\end{bmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}
.

Based on the aforementioned reformulation of simultaneous blind super-resolution and demixing under the subspace assumption, it is evident that the key is to recover
{\bm{X}_{k,\natural}}
from the linear measurement vector
\bm{y}
. Once the data matrix
\bm{X}_{k,\natural}
is reconstructed, the locations
\{\tau_{k,p}\}
of the
k
-th signal can be determined using spatial smoothing MUSIC
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
12
]
. With all the location information available,
\{d_{k,p}\}
and
\{\bm{h}_{k,p}\}
can be recovered by solving an overdetermined linear system. In the following sections, we focus on the problem of jointly recovering
\{\bm{X}_{k,\natural}\}
from their mixed linear measurements.

I-B

Related Work and Contributions

The convex optimization method for addressing the simultaneous blind super-resolution and demixing problem has been extensively studied in recent years. In
[
13
,
18
]
, the authors build upon the approach initially developed in
[
10
]
for blind super-resolution and introduce an atomic norm minimization (ANM) method for the simultaneous blind super-resolution and demixing problem specifically with
K=2
. More recently,
[
19
]
extends the ANM approach to handle arbitrary
K
values, providing theoretical guarantees.
Apart from ANM, another line of research exploits the low-rank structure of the target matrix associated with the signals. Specifically, leveraging the vectorized Hankel lift (VHL) technique introduced in
[
12
]
,
[
20
]
proposes a nuclear norm minimization approach for dual-blind deconvolution with
K=2
, which is subsequently generalized to arbitrary
K
in
[
21
]
.
However, these convex approaches struggle with large-scale problems. This challenge motivates us to design efficient and provable algorithms for the simultaneous blind super-resolution and demixing problem.

When
K=1
, the simultaneous blind super-resolution and demixing problem simplifies to the case of blind super-resolution. Previous approaches, including atomic norm minimization
[
9
,
11
]
and nuclear norm minimization
[
12
]
, have been thoroughly studied for addressing blind super-resolution. Recently, several studies have focused on developing and analyzing efficient algorithms with provable guarantees for blind super-resolution
[
22
,
23
]
. Our research extends the concept of blind super-resolution (with
K=1
) to a multi-user framework (with
K>1
). However, our approach differs significantly from existing methods. The PGD-VHL method proposed in
[
22
]
relies on additional projections to maintain incoherence properties and employs explicit regularization to balance the norms of two low-rank factors. In contrast, our method eliminates the need for such projections or balancing terms.
Concurrently, a scaled gradient descent method for blind super-resolution has been proposed with theoretical guarantees (Jinsheng Li, Wei Cui, and Xu Zhang, personal communication). In contrast, our study addresses a more challenging problem, focusing on the simultaneous blind super-resolution and demixing problem. The key to our approach lies in constructing a suitable basin of attraction and understanding the properties of the linear sensing operator when constrained to a block-diagonal subspace. Consequently, our findings are unique and not covered by existing research.

This work is also related to the problem of low-rank matrix demixing, which involves the joint recovery of multiple low rank matrices from a single measurement vector. Specifically, McCoy and Tropp
[
24
]
study the compressive demixing model represented by
\bm{y}={\cal A}(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{X}_{k})
and propose a nuclear norm minimization approach to recover
\{\bm{X}_{k}\}
. Strohmer and Wei
[
25
]
introduce a provable non-convex algorithm for the demixing problem based on the Gaussian measurement model. Additionally, both convex
[
26
,
27
]
and non-convex
[
28
,
29
]
optimization methods have been examined for rank-one matrix demixing from rank-one observations. However, due to the distinct structures of the target matrices and sensing operators in our work, the theoretical guarantees from these studies cannot be directly extended to our model.

In recent years, substantial advancements have been made in the development of scaled or preconditioned algorithms for low-rank matrix recovery. Essentially, these scaled algorithms are designed to function similarly to a (Riemannian) gradient descent method, incorporating appropriately chosen preconditioning techniques. Empirical and theoretical studies have shown that such scaled methods are more robust to ill-conditioned low-rank matrix recovery problems while maintaining a comparable computational cost to gradient descent methods. Furthermore, scaled gradient descent benefits from an implicit regularization property, eliminating the need to explicitly balance the norm of low-rank factors, as required in the traditional literature (see
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
and references therein). The empirical performance of scaled (alternating) gradient descent for the low-rank matrix completion problem has been illustrated in
[
33
,
34
,
35
]
. Recently, theoretical convergence guarantees have also been established. Tong et al.
[
36
]
demonstrated that the scaled gradient descent method for the low-rank matrix sensing problem achieves a linear convergence rate given spectral initialization. Zhang et al.
[
37
]
extended the results of
[
36
]
to the over-parameterized case. The convergence guarantee for scaled gradient descent with small random initialization is provided by Xu et al.
[
38
]
, while Cheng and Zhao further generalized the findings of Zhang et al. to the asymmetric case. Despite some general similarities, there is minimal overlap between the aforementioned studies and our framework.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

•

We address the simultaneous blind super-resolution and demixing problem by formulating it as a low-rank matrix demixing problem. Leveraging low-rank structures, we propose an implementable algorithm named the Scaled Gradient Descent method (Scaled-GD), which operates without explicit balancing regularization. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first provable and computationally efficient algorithm for simultaneous blind super-resolution and demixing.

•

We establish the convergence rate of Scaled-GD, demonstrating that it linearly converges to the ground truth from spectral initialization, provided there is no noise and the sample complexity is of the order
{\cal O}(K^{2}s^{2}r^{2}\kappa^{2}\mu_{1}\mu_{0}\log^{2}(sn))
. Additionally, the convergence rate of Scaled-GD is independent of the condition number of the ground truth, highlighting the efficiency of the proposed method.

•

We empirically validate the recovery performance of Scaled-GD. Numerical experiments show that Scaled-GD is competitive with convex recovery methods such as ANM and VHL in terms of recovery ability, but is much more efficient.

Main Results

In this section, we present a theoretical guarantee for the proposed approach. To achieve this, we introduce two standard assumptions.

Assumption III.1

(

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq p\leq s}|\bm{b}[p]|

,

,

,

obeys the following conditions:

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq j\leq n_{1}-1}\left\|\bm{U}_{k,\natural,j}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{2}\leq\frac{\mu_{1}r}{n}\text{ and }\max_{0\leq\ell\leq n_{2}-1}\left\|\bm{e}_{\ell}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{V}_{k,\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}\leq\frac{\mu_{1}r}{n}

for some positive constant
\mu_{1}
.

Remark III.2
.

Assumption
III.2
is the same as the incoherence property made in low rank matrix completion
[
40
]
and has been used in blind super-resolution
[
12
,
22
]
. This assumption is satisfied when the minimum wrap-up distance between the locations of point sources is larger than
\frac{2}{n}

[
41
]
.

Now we present our main result, whose proof is deferred to Section
V-A
.

Theorem III.1
.

Suppose that Assumption
III.2
and Assumption
III.1
hold. Let
\eta_{t}\leq\frac{1}{20}
. If the number of measurements satisfies that
n\geq C_{\gamma}K^{2}s^{2}r^{2}\kappa^{2}\mu_{0}\mu_{1}\log^{2}(sn)
, then with probability at least
1-(sn)^{-\gamma}
, the sequence
\{\bm{L}_{k,t},\bm{R}_{k,t}\}_{k=1,\cdots,K}
returned by Algorithm
1
obeys that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|\bm{L}_{k,t}\bm{R}_{k,t}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{k,\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\frac{\eta_{t}}{90}\right)^{t}\cdot\frac{\delta^{2}\sigma_{0}^{2}}{K},

(III.1)

where
\sigma_{0}=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma_{r}^{2}(\bm{Z}_{k,\natural})}
and
\kappa=\frac{\max_{k}\sigma_{1}(\bm{Z}_{k,\natural})}{\min_{k}\sigma_{r}(\bm{Z}_{k,\natural})}
.

Remark III.3
.

Theorem
III.1
implies that the sample complexity depends quadratically on
s,r
and
K
. The
r^{2}
dependence arises from the initialization process. The convergence rate is established based on the Frobenius norm, whereas the initial error is bounded by the spectral norm. An additional
r
factor is introduced to establish the relationship between these two norms. More details can be found in Lemma
V.6
. The quadratic dependence of
s
and
K
is due to the construction of the initial guess, which is designed to ensure that the initial error is of the order
1/\sqrt{K\mu_{0}s}
(see Lemma
V.2
and Lemma
V.3
). This careful construction is essential for achieving the desired error bounds. However, we empirically observe a linear relationship between
n
and
K
. Therefore, we acknowledge that the quadratic sample complexity is likely an artifact of our proof.

Remark III.4
.

The sample complexities of the nuclear norm minimization method
[
21
]
and the ANM method
[
19
]
are approximately
{\cal O}(K^{2}sr\log^{4}(sn))
and
{\cal O}(Ksr\log^{2}(ns))
, respectively. Compared to these results, our sample complexity is sub-optimal with respect to

s,r
and
K
. However, as previously mentioned, our method is more efficient than these convex relaxation methods. Moreover, it is worth noting that our results are established based on slightly milder assumptions than those in
[
19
]
. The theoretical performance guarantee in
[
19
]
relies on an additional assumption requiring the randomness of the coefficient vectors
\bm{h}_{k,p}
. In contrast, the theoretical performance of Scaled-GD is independent of such assumptions.

Remark III.5
.

Theorem
V.6
establishes that Scaled-GD converges linearly to the sequence
{\bm{Z}_{k,\natural}}
. To achieve an accuracy of
\varepsilon
, defined as
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|\bm{L}_{k,t}\bm{R}_{k,t}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{k,\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{2}\leq\varepsilon\sigma_{0}^{2}
, Scaled-GD requires at most
T=\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\varepsilon))
iterations, which is notable for its independence from the condition number
\kappa
. Given the iterates
{\bm{L}_{k,T},\bm{R}_{k,T}}
, the estimate of
\bm{X}_{k,\natural}
can be obtained by applying
{\cal D}^{-1}{\cal G}^{\ast}(\bm{L}_{k,T}\bm{R}_{k,T}^{\ast})
.

The proof of Theorem
III.1
follows the primary ideas outlined in
[
37
]
and is deferred to Section
V
. Fundamentally, we establish the Lipschitz smoothness and the Polyak-Lojasiewicz inequality for the objective function in (
II.2
) with respect to a scaled norm. This framework enables us to demonstrate a linear convergence rate through induction. However, the specifics of the proof are intricate and notably distinct, preventing us from addressing them using existing methodologies. Our work diverges from the closely related study by
[
37
]
in two significant aspects:

•

We consider a more complex problem where
K
structured matrices must be jointly recovered from a single measurement vector. To tackle this issue, we formulate both the target matrices and the sensing operator as block diagonal matrices. Furthermore, the measurement matrices are rank-
1
matrices, which leads to the failure of the restricted isometry property (RIP) for the sensing operator. The primary challenge is to understand the properties of the sensing operator when restricted to block diagonal matrices. As a result, the convergence analysis presented in
[
37
]
cannot be easily generalized to our model.

•

The construction of the basin of contraction is crucial for understanding non-convex optimization and varies across different problems. As a result, the initial guess slightly differs from that in
[
37
]
. Moreover, to ensure the initialization is close to the ground truth, we must carefully address the effect of inter-coherence between different target matrices. Consequently, additional effort is required to achieve the desired guarantee.

To establish our main results, we first reformulate (

{\cal A}
be a operator which maps a

block diagonal matrix into a

\displaystyle{\cal A}(\bm{X}):=\sum_{k=1}^{K}{\cal A}_{k}(\bm{X}_{k}),

where

is defined in (

) and
\bm{X}=\blkdiag(\bm{X}_{1},\cdots,\bm{X}_{K})
.
The adjoint operator of

, denoted by

is given as

\displaystyle{\cal A}^{\ast}(\bm{y})=\blkdiag({\cal A}_{1}^{\ast}(\bm{y}),\cdots{\cal A}^{\ast}_{K}(\bm{y}))\in\mathbb{C}^{Ks\times Kn}

for any vector
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Define
\widetilde{{\cal G}}:\mathbb{C}^{Ks\times Kn}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{Ksn_{1}\times Kn_{2}}
as

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\cal G}}(\bm{X})=\blkdiag({\cal G}(\bm{X}_{1}),\cdots{\cal G}(\bm{X}_{K}))\in\mathbb{C}^{Ksn_{1}\times Kn_{2}}.

The adjoint operator
\widetilde{{\cal G}}^{\ast}
is naturally given as

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\cal G}}^{\ast}(\bm{Z})=\blkdiag({\cal G}^{\ast}(\bm{Z}_{1}),\cdots,{\cal G}^{\ast}(\bm{Z}_{K})).

It can be seen that
\widetilde{{\cal G}}^{\ast}\widetilde{{\cal G}}={\cal I}
, where
{\cal I}:\mathbb{C}^{Ks\times Kn}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{Ks\times Kn}
is the identity operator. Throughout this work, we abuse the operator

to denote the identity operator in
\mathbb{C}^{Ks\times Kn}
or
\mathbb{C}^{s\times n}
, whenever the context is clear.
Using this notation, the optimization problem (

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{L},\bm{R}}\left\{f(\bm{L},\bm{R}):=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\cal A}\widetilde{{\cal G}}^{\ast}(\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{2}}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\left\|({\cal I}-\widetilde{{\cal G}}\widetilde{{\cal G}}^{\ast})(\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural})\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{2}\right\},

(V.1)

where

is the ground truth matrix obeying that

and

\displaystyle=\bm{R}_{t}-\eta_{t}\nabla f(\bm{R}_{t})(\bm{L}_{t}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{L}_{t})^{-1},

where

and

are given by

\displaystyle\nabla f(\bm{L})

\displaystyle=(\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural})\bm{R}+\left(\widetilde{{\cal G}}\left({\cal A}^{\ast}{\cal A}-{\cal I}\right)\widetilde{{\cal G}}^{\ast}(\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural})\right)\bm{R},

(V.2)

\displaystyle\nabla f(\bm{R})

\displaystyle=(\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural})^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{L}+\left(\widetilde{{\cal G}}\left({\cal A}^{\ast}{\cal A}-{\cal I}\right)\widetilde{{\cal G}}^{\ast}(\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural})^{\mathsf{H}}\right)\bm{L}.

Now, we are in the position to prove our main results by induction. Firstly, we show (

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\eta_{t}}{90}\right)^{t}\frac{\delta^{2}\sigma_{0}^{2}}{K}

\displaystyle\geq\max_{k}\frac{\left\|\bm{L}_{k,t}\bm{R}_{k,t}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{k,\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{2}}{\sigma_{r}^{2}(\bm{Z}_{k,\natural})}\cdot\sigma^{2}_{0},

which implies that

Therefore, Lemma

implies that

which completes the proof.

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we investigate the simultaneous blind super-resolution and demixing problem, formulating it as a low-rank matrix recovery problem. We introduce a novel non-convex algorithm, termed scaled gradient descent without balance, specifically designed to address this problem. We establish the sample complexity and provide a linear convergence guarantee for the proposed algorithm, notably demonstrating that the convergence rate is independent of the condition number of the target matrices. The empirical performance of our algorithm has been validated through extensive experiments.

. Then one has

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Upsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6\kappa\delta}{\sqrt{K}}\left\|\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}},

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{6\kappa\delta}{\sqrt{K}}\right)\left\|\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\bm{L}_{\natural}\bm{\Upsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}+{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{R}_{\natural}^{\mathsf{H}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}\leq\left(1+\frac{6\kappa\delta}{\sqrt{K}}\right)\left\|\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}.

Proof.

Firstly, a direct computation yields that

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Upsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}=\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\Upsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}\leq\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|\left\|\bm{\Upsilon}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\frac{2\kappa\delta}{\sqrt{K}}\left\|\bm{\Upsilon}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}},

where step (a) follows from Lemma
B.6
. On the other hand, one can also obtain that

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Upsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}=\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\Upsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}\leq\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}\left\|\bm{\Upsilon}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\frac{2\kappa\delta}{\sqrt{K}}\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}},

where last line is due to Lemma
B.6
. Combining together, one has

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Upsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\kappa\delta}{\sqrt{K}}\left(\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}+\left\|\bm{\Upsilon}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}\kappa\delta}{\sqrt{K}}\left(\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{2}+\left\|\bm{\Upsilon}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\sqrt{2}\kappa\delta}{\sqrt{K}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left(\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}_{k}\bm{\Sigma}_{k,\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{2}+\left\|\bm{\Upsilon}_{k}\bm{\Sigma}_{k,\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}^{2}\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\sqrt{2}\kappa\delta}{\sqrt{K}}\left(\sqrt{2}+1\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left\|\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6\kappa\delta}{\sqrt{K}}\left\|\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}},

where the fourth line is due to Lemma
C.3
.
Secondly, notice that

\displaystyle\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}

\displaystyle=(\bm{L}_{\natural}+{\bm{\Delta}})(\bm{R}_{\natural}+\bm{\Upsilon})^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}=\bm{L}_{\natural}\bm{\Upsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}+{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{R}_{\natural}^{\mathsf{H}}+{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Upsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}.

Thus a simple triangular inequality yields that

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{L}_{\natural}\bm{\Upsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}+{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{R}_{\natural}^{\mathsf{H}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}-{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Upsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}+\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Upsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{6\kappa\delta}{\sqrt{K}}\right)\left\|\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}.

On the other hand, one has

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{L}_{\natural}\bm{\Upsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}+{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{R}_{\natural}^{\mathsf{H}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}

\displaystyle\geq\left\|\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}-\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Upsilon}^{\mathsf{H}}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{6\kappa\delta}{\sqrt{K}}\right)\left\|\bm{L}\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}.

Thus we complete the proof.
∎

Lemma B.8
.

Denote
\kappa=\frac{\max_{k}\sigma_{1}(\bm{Z}_{k,\natural})}{\min_{k}\sigma_{r}(\bm{Z}_{k,\natural})}
. Suppose
\left\|\bm{L}_{k}\bm{R}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}-\bm{Z}_{k,\natural}\right\|_{{\footnotesize{\mathsf{F}}}}\leq\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{K}}\sigma_{r}(\bm{Z}_{k,\natural})
for any
k\in[K]
, where
\delta
is a constant such that
\delta<\frac{1}{2\kappa}
. Then one has

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{L}_{\natural}(\bm{L}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{L})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-2\kappa\delta},

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{R}_{\natural}(\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{R})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-2\kappa\delta},

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}(\bm{L}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{L})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\frac{2\kappa\delta}{1-2\kappa\delta},

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\Upsilon}(\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{R})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\frac{2\kappa\delta}{1-2\kappa\delta}

Proof.

Recall that
\bm{L}_{\natural}=\bm{U}_{\natural}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}=\blkdiag(\bm{U}_{1,\natural}\bm{\Sigma}_{1,\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}},\cdots,\bm{U}_{K,\natural}\bm{\Sigma}_{K,\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}})
and
\bm{L}=\blkdiag(\bm{L}_{1},\cdots,\bm{L}_{K})
. A direct computation yields that

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{L}_{\natural}(\bm{L}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{L})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{L}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{L})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|=\max_{k}\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{k,\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{L}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{L}_{k})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|=\max_{k}\left\|\bm{L}_{k}(\bm{L}_{k}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{L}_{k})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{k,\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\|

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\max_{k}\frac{1}{1-\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}_{k}\bm{\Sigma}_{k,\natural}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|}=\frac{1}{1-\max_{k}\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}_{k}\bm{\Sigma}_{k,\natural}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|}=\frac{1}{1-\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{1-\frac{2\kappa\delta}{\sqrt{K}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-2\kappa\delta},

where step (a) follows from Lemma
C.4
and step (b) is due to Lemma
B.6
. Moreover, one has

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}(\bm{L}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{L})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{L}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{L})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\bm{\Delta}}\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|\cdot\left\|\bm{\Sigma}_{\natural}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{L}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{L})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\kappa\delta}{\sqrt{K}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-2\kappa\delta},

where the last line is due to Lemma
B.6
. Using the same argument, one can show that

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{R}_{\natural}(\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{R})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-2\kappa\delta},

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{\Upsilon}(\bm{R}^{\mathsf{H}}\bm{R})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\frac{2\kappa\delta}{1-2\kappa\delta}.

∎
