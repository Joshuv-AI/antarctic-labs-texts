Integral Points Close to Smooth Plane Curves
ZiAn Zhao (Cody)

Abstract
This is an exposition of a class of problems and results on the number of integral points close to plane curves. We give a detailed proof of a theorem of Huxley and Sargos, following the account of Bordellès. Along the way we correct an oversight in the proof, changing some of the explicit values of the constants in the theorem.

Introduction

The topic of this essay is the methods used to estimate the number of integer points around smooth curves in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. In the second chapter, we will look at some relatively weaker statements and eventually build up to a theorem by Huxley and Sargos
[
11
]

[
1

of the main theorem given by Huxley and Sargos has some issues. In Lemma

[

suggest that if a point is outside the proper major arc, then the Lagrange polynomial won’t intersect it, but this is not the case. If we only fix the erroneous lemma, the proof of the main theorem will have to assume that two major arcs do not intersect for it to work, which is also not clear. However, we have developed some techniques to go around this oversight by proving that two proper majors do not intersect, which fortunately is enough to prove the most important part of the main theorem that the asymptotic inequality still holds. In the third chapter, we will introduce some improvements given by Huxley, Braton, and Tritonov to the main theorem and discuss why they work. In the final chapter, we will introduce two applications of the methods—the square-free number problems and estimating solutions for diophantine inequalities. This essay aims to introduce and understand this class of problems and methods in detail and illustrate them in a way that best fits my understanding. Hopefully, in the continuation of my studies, I will be able to use these interesting methods to solve problems in related subjects. The main source of this essay comes from the book Arithmetic Tales

2
The Theorem of Huxley and Sargos

One of the goals of this essay is to discuss a certain class of problems in regards of estimating the number of integral points around a smooth curve. At the start, we will take a look at some less generalized methods with strict conditions, their appearances follow directly from the classical mean value theorem and the divided differences. The problem is then complicated by the appearance of major arcs which allows us to bound the integers in a different way, the goal of the first chapter is to use tools and definitions we will introduce to prove a finer version of the theorem comes from Huxley and Sargos involving major arcs
[
11
]
, which is considered to be a more generalized method.
In this chapter, we may assume that
N\geq 4
is an integer and
\delta
and
c_{0}
are small positive real numbers such that
0<\delta<\frac{1}{4}
. We introduce the following notations

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)=\{n\in[N,2N]\cap Z:\parallel f(n)\parallel<\delta)\}.

\parallel f(n)\parallel<\delta
means the nearest integer point has distance less than
\delta
to
f(n)
i.e.
(n,y)
is the nearest integral point to
(n,f(n))
where
y
is an integer such that
|f(n)-y|<\delta
. Also

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)=|\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)|.

Notice the trivial bound
\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\leq N+1
.

The theorem of Huxley and Sargos will be stated here as a motivation, the proof of the theorem will come in at the end of this chapter when we are in a position to do so. The theorem below
is stated differently from the book Arithmetic Tales
[
1
, p. 373–374 Theorem 5.5]
and the original paper by Huxley and Sargos
[

]

Theorem 2.1

(Theorem of Huxley and Sargos)
.

Let
k\geq 3
be an integer and
f\in C^{k}[N,2N]
such that there exist
\lambda_{k}>0
and
c_{k}\geq 1
such that, for all
x\in[N,2N]
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\leq|f^{(k)}(x)|\leq c_{k}\lambda_{k}.

(1)

Let
0<\delta<\frac{1}{4}
be a real number. Then

•

If the set of points in each proper major arc with denominator
\leq(a_{k}\delta)^{-1}
can be covered with interval of length
L_{j}(a_{k}q_{j}\delta)^{-1/k}
which is disjoint from all such covers for other major arcs, where
L_{j}
and
q_{j}
are the length and denominator associated with their proper major arcs:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\leq\alpha_{k}N\lambda_{k}^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}+\beta_{k}N\delta^{\frac{2}{k(k-1)}}+8k^{3}(\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{k}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{k}}+2k^{2}(5e^{3}+1);

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}=2k^{2}c_{k}^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{k}=4k^{2}(5e^{3}c_{k}^{\frac{2}{k(k-1)}}+1);

•

otherwise:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\leq

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}N\lambda_{k}^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}+\beta_{k}N\delta^{\frac{2}{k(k-1)}}+16k^{3}(\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{k}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{k}}+2k^{2}(5e^{3}+1);

where

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}=2k^{2}c_{k}^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{k}=30e^{3}k^{2}c_{k}^{\frac{2}{k(k-1)}}+4k^{2};

or alternatively we could write and use the above results as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\ll N\lambda_{k}^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}+N\delta^{\frac{2}{k(k-1)}}+\big(\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{k}}\big)^{\frac{1}{k}}+1.

Using Vinogradov notation,
f(x)\ll g(x)
is equivalent to
f(x)=O(g(x)
, also the implied constant above depends only on
k
and
c_{k}
.
We will have an in depth look on how we can use this method, if there exists any weaker version of it and could we improve it. At the end of this section, we will try and prove Theorem
2.1
.

2.1
Lagrange Interpolation Polynomial

First, we will introduce an interpolation method. That is, for a finite set of data points, we will try to construct a function which must go exactly through the values at these data points. The reason why this method will be useful is, for the following sections, we will try to bound the number of certain integer points around a smooth curve. To avoid a trivial bound we could always assume there are more than
k
such points. By taking multiple intervals which each contain
k
points, we could reduce the question to bounding the length of the interval by using the classical mean value theorem or a generalization of the mean value theorem which involves using Lagrange polynomials. This would be a common trick to give a bound through out the essay.

Here is a simple construction of such a polynomial. Let’s say it interpolates at the points
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\dots,x_{k}
of some function
f
. Consider a sequence of functions such that each function equals 0 at all the points except at one point it equals
f(x_{i})
. By adding these functions together, we get a polynomial passing through exactly these
k+1
points. Following some straightforward computations we get

\displaystyle f_{i}(x)=\begin{cases}1,&x=x_{i}\\
0,&x=x_{j},i\neq j\end{cases}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow f_{i}(x)=\prod_{j=0,i\neq j}^{k}\frac{x-x_{j}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}f(x_{i})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathcal{P}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}\Big(\prod_{j=0,i\neq j}^{k}\frac{x-x_{j}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}f(x_{i})\Big).

(2)

The leading coefficient of
\mathcal{P}(x)
,
b_{k}
, is called the divided difference of
f
at the points
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\dots,x_{k}
and it is denoted by
f[x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{k}]
. With some brute force calculation, we can show that

\displaystyle b_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{f(x_{j})}{\prod_{0\leq i\leq k,i\neq j}(x_{j}-x_{i})}=\frac{A}{\prod_{0\leq i<j\leq k}(x_{j}-x_{i})},

(3)

with

\displaystyle A=\begin{vmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
x_{0}&x_{1}&\cdots&x_{k}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
x_{0}^{k-1}&x_{1}^{k-1}&\cdots&x_{k}^{k-1}\\
f(x_{0})&f(x_{1})&\cdots&f(x_{k})\end{vmatrix}\begin{matrix}\vphantom{x}\\
\vphantom{y}\\
\vphantom{z}\\
\vphantom{s}\\
\vphantom{f}.\end{matrix}

(4)

It might seem like the degree of the Lagrange interpolating polynomial is directly related to the number of data points it is fitting, specifically, if you have
n
data points, the Lagrange interpolating polynomial should be of degree
n-1
. But this is simply not the case, we will give a somewhat trivial example here which both showcase the power of this new tool and to prove the point we are making here. Let
f(x)=x
and let the points we will try to interpolate be
x_{i}=i
for
i\in\{1,2,3,4\}
. Following the way we construct the polynomial (
2
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{(x-2)(x-3)(x-4)}{-6}+\frac{(x-1)(x-3)(x-4)}{-2}2+\frac{(x-1)(x-2)(x-4)}{-2}3

\displaystyle+\frac{(x-1)(x-2)(x-3)}{6}4=x.

To amend our statement, we could say if you have
n
data points, the Lagrange interpolating polynomial should be of degree
\leq n-1
. The next thing we will do is to justify that Lagrange interpolation polynomial is well defined by proving its existence and uniqueness
[
13
, p. 271–287]
.

Definition 2.2
.

\mathbb{P}_{n}[x]
is a set of all polynomials of degree
n
and smaller.

Theorem 2.3

(Existence and uniqueness)
.

Given
n+1
distinct points
(x_{i})_{i=0}^{n}\in[a,b]
and
n+1
real numbers
(f_{i})_{i=0}^{n}
, there is exactly one polynomial
p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}[x]
, namely that given by (
2
), such that
p(x_{i})=f_{i}
for all
i
.

Proof.

First we define the Lagrange cardinal polynomials for the points
x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
as follows

\displaystyle l_{k}(x)=\prod_{i=0,i\neq k}^{n}\frac{x-x_{i}}{x_{k}-x_{i}},\quad k=0,1,\dots,n.

Each
l_{k}
is the product of
n
linear factors, hence
l_{k}\in\mathbb{P}_{n}[x]
, and from (
2
),
p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}[x]
. We have shown by construction before such
l_{k}(x_{k})=1
and
l_{k}(x_{j})=0
for
j\neq k
. Hence

\displaystyle p(x_{j})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}f_{k}l_{k}(x_{j})=f_{j},\quad j=0,\dots,n,

thus
p
is a polynomial interpolate at our given data points.
For the uniqueness part, let’s suppose both
p\in\mathbb{P}_{n}[x]
and
q\in\mathbb{P}_{n}[x]
interpolate same
n+1
data points. The polynomial
r=p-q
is of degree at most n and equals to 0 at
n+1
distinct points. Such a polynomial can only be the zero polynomial, therefore
r\equiv 0
and thus the interpolating polynomial is unique.
∎

Notice that the result for uniqueness is only in one direction, for
n+1
distinct points, you will get an unique Lagrange polynomial associated to it. But for a given Lagrange polynomial, it could interpolate a different set of points at the same time. The example we discussed before is a good illustration of this.

The Lagrange interpolation polynomials are often the appropriate forms to use when we wish to manipulate the interpolation polynomial as part of a larger mathematical expression. However, they are not ideal for numerical evaluation, both because of the speed of calculation and because of the accumulation of rounding error. But it is good enough to give us a practical bound in the following theorems.

The First, Second and
k

The initial aim of theorem
2.1
was to improve a restriction on the
k

k

Definition 2.4
.

[x]
is the nearest integer below
x

x-1<[x]\leq x

Theorem 2.5

(First derivative test)
.

Let
f\in C^{1}[N,2N]
such that there exists
\lambda_{1}>0
and
c_{1}\geq 1
such that for all
x\in[N,2N]
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\leq|f^{\prime}(x)|\leq c_{1}\lambda_{1},

(5)

then

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\leq 2c_{1}N\lambda_{1}+4c_{1}N\delta+\frac{2\delta}{\lambda_{1}}+1.

In practice, using Titchmarsh-Vinogradov notation, the result can be stated as follows. If

\displaystyle|f^{\prime}(x)|\asymp\lambda_{1},

then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\ll N\lambda_{1}+N\delta+\frac{\delta}{\lambda}+1.

f\asymp g
is equivalent to
f\ll g
and
g\ll f
.

The result above follows naturally from the classical mean-value theorem. It’s rather restrictive and becomes useless when
\lambda_{1}
is big, but it is a starting point of more elaborate estimates.

Also the statement itself could be a bit more general, since the proof only invokes the property of
f
being continuous and differentiable, the derivative
f^{\prime}
is not necessarily needed to be continuous.

Proof.

First we would like to get rid of some trivial cases. If
4c_{1}\delta\geq 1
, then
4c_{1}\delta+1\geq N+1\geq\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)
, similar thing can be said for
2c_{1}\lambda_{1}\geq 1
. So we may as well suppose that max
(4c_{1}\delta,2c_{1}\lambda_{1})<1
.
We can also assume that there is more than one point in the set otherwise the inequality is trivial. Let
n
and
n+a
be any integers in
\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)
(not necessarily consecutive), using the mean value theorem we could show that either

\displaystyle a>\frac{1}{2c_{1}\lambda_{1}}=a_{1};

(6)

or

\displaystyle a<\frac{2\delta}{\lambda_{1}}=a_{2};

(7)

we assume the above is true for now and continue, will come back to that later. Also notice that max(
4c_{1}\delta
,
2c_{1}\lambda_{1}
)
<1
implies

\displaystyle a_{1}=\frac{1}{2c_{1}\lambda_{1}}>max(\frac{2\delta}{\lambda_{1}},1)=max(a_{2},1).

More specifically,
a_{1}>1
, therefore we can sub-divide the interval
[N,2N]
into
s=[\frac{N}{a_{1}}]+1
sub-intervals
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{s}
where each interval has length
\leq a_{1}
. Which means if there is more than one element from
\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)
inside the interval
\mathcal{I}_{j}
, we would have a distance between any of the two elements
\leq a_{1}
(not necessarily
2
consecutive elements) and by (
6
) and (
7
), we have their distance
\leq a_{2}
. For any two elements inside the interval, choose the pair with the largest distance, we would still have the previous result. Let
n
and
n+a
be such a pair with
a\leq a_{2}
. Then if there are more elements lie in the interval, they must lie in between
n
and
n+a
. Thus, we could infer the following to be true,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)\cap\mathcal{I}_{j}|\leq a_{2}+1,

which still holds if there is only one element inside the interval. From here we could easily deduce the result.

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\leq\Big(\frac{N}{a_{1}}+1\Big)(a_{2}+1)=2c_{1}N\lambda_{1}+4c_{1}N\delta+\frac{2\delta}{\lambda_{1}}+1.

Now we will try to end the argument by deducing (
7
) and (
6
) to be true by using the mean value theorem. For any two elements
n
and
n+a
in
\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)
, there exist
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
integers and
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
real numbers such that
f(n)=m_{1}+\delta_{1}
and
f(n+a)=m_{2}+\delta_{2}
with
|\delta_{i}|<\delta
for
i\in\{1,2\}
. Which means there exists
m\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\delta_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
|\delta_{3}|<2\delta
and

\displaystyle f(n+a)-f(n)=m+\delta_{3}.

Using the mean value theorem on
f
, we get for
t\in[N,2N]
the following

\displaystyle f(n+a)-f(n)=af^{\prime}(t)\quad\text{and}\quad af^{\prime}(t)=m+\delta_{3}.

If
m\neq 0
, since
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have
|m|\geq 1
. From (
5
) we could deduce

\displaystyle ac_{1}\lambda_{1}\geq a|f^{\prime}(t)|=|m+\delta_{3}|\geq|m|-|\delta_{3}|>1-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{2},

which gives us (
6
). If
m=0
, by (

) we have

\displaystyle a\lambda_{1}\leq a|f^{\prime}(t)|=|\delta_{3}|<2\delta,

which gives (
7
).
∎

For the next result, we will assume
0<\delta<\frac{1}{8}
. First we will introduce the following lemma.

Lemma 2.6

(Reduction principle)
.

Let
f:[N,2N]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
be any map,
A
be a real number satisfying
1\leq A\leq N
and, for all integers
a\in[1,A]
, we define on
[N,2N-a]
the function
\Delta_{a}f
by

\displaystyle\Delta_{a}f(x)=f(x+a)-f(x).

Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\leq\frac{N}{A}+\sum_{a\leq A}\mathcal{R}(\Delta_{a}f,N,2\delta)+1.

Proof.

For all
a\in\mathbb{N}
, define

\displaystyle S(a)=\{n\in[N,2N]\cap\mathbb{Z}:n\text{ and }n+a\text{ are consecutive in }\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)\}.

As before, assume more than one point in the set
\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)
, otherwise the statement is trivial. We observe that for any integer inside
\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)
, except the largest one, has a successive element and lies in only one subset
\mathcal{S}(a)
, so we could easily deduce the following,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)=\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}|\mathcal{S}|+1=\sum_{a\leq A}|\mathcal{S}(a)|+\sum_{a>A}|\mathcal{S}(a)|+1.

(8)

We write the set as
\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)=\{n_{1}\leq n_{2}\leq\cdots\leq n_{k}\}
and define

for

as

\displaystyle d_{1}=n_{2}-n_{1}\text{, }d_{2}=n_{3}-n_{2}\text{,\ldots,}d_{k-1}=n_{k}-n_{k-1}.

By definition we have
|\mathcal{S}(a)|
counts the number of elements with distance a, that is the number of indexes

such that

, so clearly

\displaystyle\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}a|\mathcal{S}(a)|=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}d_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}(n_{j+1}-n_{j})=n_{k}-n_{1}\leq N.

Therefore we can trivially bound the second term of (
8

\displaystyle N\geq\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}a|\mathcal{S}(a)|\geq\sum_{a>A}a|\mathcal{S}(a)|\geq A\sum_{a>A}|\mathcal{S}(a)|\Rightarrow\sum_{a>A}|\mathcal{S}(a)|\leq\frac{N}{A}.

Let
n\in\mathcal{S}(a)
, which means
n
and
n+a
are consecutive in
\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)
, so that

\displaystyle||\Delta_{a}f(n)||=||f(n+a)-f(n)||\leq||f(n+a)||+||f(n)||<2\delta.

To justify the first inequality above to be true, simply take

||(f(n+a)\text{ mod }1)-(f(n)\text{ mod }1)||
, it gives the same value as
||f(n+a)-f(n)||
. So we have

, that is

\displaystyle|\mathcal{S}(a)|\leq\mathcal{R}(\Delta_{a}f,N,2\delta),

which gives a bound for first term of (
8
) and the result.
∎

Theorem 2.7

(Second derivative test)
.

Let

such that there exist
\lambda_{2}>0
and

such that, for all

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}\leq|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|\leq c_{2}\lambda_{2},

(9)

\displaystyle N\lambda_{2}\geq c_{2}^{-1}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\leq 6\{(3c_{2})^{\frac{1}{3}}N\lambda_{2}^{\frac{1}{3}}+(12c_{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}N\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}+1\},

\displaystyle|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|\asymp\lambda_{2}\text{ and }N\lambda_{2}\gg 1,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\ll N\lambda_{2}^{\frac{1}{3}}+N\delta^{\frac{1}{2}}+1.

\displaystyle\min_{H_{1}\leq H\leq H_{2}}E(H)\leq(m+n)\Big\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(A_{i}^{b_{j}}B_{j}^{a_{i}})^{\frac{1}{a_{i}+b_{j}}}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}A_{i}H_{1}^{a_{i}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}B_{j}H_{2}^{-b_{j}}\Big\}.

Now we let
n=1,m=2,b_{1}=1,a_{1}=1,a_{2}=2,B_{1}=N,A_{1}=12c_{2}N\delta,A_{2}=3c_{2}N\lambda_{2},H=A

\displaystyle\min_{1\leq A\leq N}\Big(

\displaystyle NA^{-1}+3c_{2}N\lambda_{2}A^{2}+12c_{2}NA\delta\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 3\Big\{(A_{1}N^{a_{1}})^{\frac{1}{a_{1}+1}}+(A_{2}N^{a_{2}})^{\frac{1}{a_{2}+1}}+A_{1}H_{1}^{a_{1}}+A_{2}H_{1}^{a_{2}}+B_{1}H_{2}^{-b_{1}}\Big\}

\displaystyle=3N\Big\{(3c_{2}\lambda_{2})^{\frac{1}{3}}+3c_{2}\lambda_{2}+(12c_{2}\delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}+12c_{2}\delta\Big\}+3.

(16)

Now (
12
) implies that

\displaystyle 0<3c_{2}\lambda_{2}<1\text{ and }0<12c_{2}\delta<1,

which means
(3c_{2}\lambda_{2})^{\frac{1}{3}}>3c_{2}\lambda_{2}
and
(12c_{2}\delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}>12c_{2}\delta
, with all that said, bound in (
15
) can be optimized as the following,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)

\displaystyle\leq 3N\Big\{(3c_{2}\lambda_{2})^{\frac{1}{3}}+3c_{2}\lambda_{2}+(12c_{2}\delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}+12c_{2}\delta\Big\}+4

\displaystyle\leq 6\Big\{(3c_{2}\lambda_{2})^{\frac{1}{3}}N+(12c_{2}\delta)^{\frac{1}{2}}N+1\Big\},

which is the result.
∎

The natural question to ask now is if the previous two results could be generalized in some way. In order to do so, we should first consider a more generalized version of the mean value theorem which put higher derivatives into play. It is stated as follows
[
1
, p.380–381 Theorem 5.2]
.

Theorem 2.9

(Divided differences)
.

Let
k
be a positive integer,
x_{0}<x_{1}<\cdots<x_{k}
be real numbers and
f\in C^{k}[x_{0},x_{k}]
. Set
\mathcal{P}(x)=b_{k}x^{k}+\cdots+b_{0}
the unique polynomial of degree
\leq k
such that
\mathcal{P}(x_{i})=f(x_{i})
for
i=0,\dots,k
. Then there exists a real number
t\in[x_{0},x_{k}]
such that

\displaystyle b_{k}=\frac{f^{(k)}(t)}{k!}.

The proof of the theorem is rather straightforward. By letting
F(x)=f(x)-\mathcal{P}(x)
, we can use induction to prove that for any
F(x)\in C^{k}[x_{0},x_{k}]
such that
F(x_{0})=F(x_{1})=\cdots=F(x_{k})
. Then there exists a real number
t\in[x_{0},x_{k}]
such that
F^{(k)}(t)=0
. Notice that
\mathcal{P}^{(k)}(x)=k!b_{k}
.

The polynomial
\mathcal{P}
mentioned above is a Lagrange polynomial. We can see from (
4
) that
A\in\mathbb{Z}
if
x_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
f(x_{j})\in\mathbb{Z}
. When
k=1
, by (
3
), we can find
b_{k}=\frac{f(x_{1})-f(x_{0})}{x_{1}-x_{0}}
, which shows that Theorem
2.9
generalizes the mean value theorem. With that in mind, now we can go back to the generalization of the first and second derivative tests. The
k
th derivative test
[
1
, p. 387–372 Proposition 5.1]
is stated as follows

Theorem 2.10

(
k
th derivative test)
.

Let
k\geq 1
be an integer and
f\in C^{k}[N,2N]
such that there exist
\lambda_{k}>0
and
c_{k}\geq 1
such that, for all
x\in[N,2N]
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}\leq|f^{(k)}(x)|\leq c_{k}\lambda_{k}.

(17)

Assume also that

\displaystyle(k+1)!\delta<\lambda_{k}.

(18)

If
\alpha_{k}=2k(2c_{k})^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\leq\alpha_{k}N\lambda_{k}^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}+4k.

To state the theorem in a more practical sense, we can say if

\displaystyle|f^{(k)}(x)|\asymp\lambda_{k}\text{ and }\delta\ll\lambda_{k},

then

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\ll N\lambda_{k}^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}+1.

Proof.

As usual, we first get rid of the trivial case, if
\lambda_{k}\geq\frac{1}{2}
, then

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}N\lambda_{k}^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}+1\geq N+1\geq\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta).

Since
k\geq 1
and
c_{k}\geq 1
. So we can assume that
\lambda_{k}<\frac{1}{2}
and that the size of
\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)
is more than
4k
. The way we try to generalize the first derivative test here is by taking
k+1
consecutive points
n<n+a_{1}<n+a_{2}<\cdots<n+a_{k}
in
\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)
and say the distance between the first and the last elements
a_{k}
satisfies

\displaystyle a_{k}\geq 2k\alpha_{k}^{-1}\lambda_{k}^{-\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}.

(19)

As we did in the proof of the first derivative test, we will assume (
19
) to be true for now and will come back and prove it later. Let’s write the set in the form
\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)=\{n<n+a_{1}<\cdots<n+a_{k}<\cdots<n+a_{z}\}
where
z>4k
. Let
T
be a subset of
\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)
such that it contains each
(k+1)
th elements of
\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)
, that is

\displaystyle T=\{n+a_{mk+(m-1)}\in\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta):m\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}\text{ and }mk+(m-1)\leq z\}.

We can see by (
19
), for any two elements in the set
T
, they differ by more than
d_{k}=2k\alpha_{k}^{-1}\lambda_{k}^{-\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}
and
k\geq 1
, therefore we can deduce the following, which is essentially the result

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\leq(k+1)(|T|+1)\leq 2k\Big(\frac{N}{d_{k}}+2\Big).

Now our job is to show that the inequality (
19
) is indeed true and then we are done. We may abuse the notations a little bit and let
n,n+a_{1},\dots,n+a_{k}
be any
k+1
consecutive elements in
S(f,N,\delta)
. By definition, there exist integers
m_{0},\dots,m_{k}
and real numbers
\delta_{0},\dots,\delta_{k}
such that
|\delta_{j}|<\delta
for all
j\in\{0,\dots,k\}
and
f(n+a_{j})=m_{j}+\delta_{j}
where
a_{0}=0
. Using Theorem
2.9
and (
3
), let
\mathcal{P}(x)=b_{k}x^{k}+\cdots+b_{0}
be the Lagrange polynomial interpolating the points
(n+a_{j},m_{j}+\delta_{j})
. We know there exist
t\in[n,n+a_{k}]
such that

\displaystyle b_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{m_{j}+\delta_{j}}{\prod_{0\leq i\leq k,i\neq j}(a_{j}-a_{i})}=\frac{f^{(k)}(t)}{k!};

(20)

let
P=b^{\prime}_{k}X^{k}+\cdots+b^{\prime}_{0}
be the polynomial interpolating the points
(n+a_{j},m_{j})
, then we also have

\displaystyle b^{\prime}_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{m_{j}}{\prod_{0\leq i\leq k,i\neq j}(a_{j}-a_{i})}=\frac{A_{k}}{D_{k}};

where we can see
A_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
D_{k}=\prod_{0\leq i<j\leq k}(a_{j}-a_{i})>0
, and we can get

\displaystyle b^{\prime}_{k}=b_{k}-\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\delta_{j}}{\prod_{0\leq i\leq k,i\neq j}(a_{j}-a_{i})};

and then

\displaystyle k!A_{k}=k!D_{k}b^{\prime}_{k}=D_{k}f^{(k)}(t)-k!D_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\delta_{j}}{\prod_{0\leq i\leq k,i\neq j}(a_{j}-a_{i})}=x+y.

Since
|\delta_{j}|<\delta
and
|a_{j}-a_{i}|\geq 1
for all
i\neq j
, we have

\displaystyle|y|<k!D_{k}\delta\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{1}{\prod_{0\leq i\leq k,i\neq j}|a_{j}-a_{i}|}\leq(k+1)!D_{k}\delta.

Now from (
17
), (
18
) and (
20
), the above becomes

\displaystyle|y|<\lambda_{k}D_{k}\leq D_{k}|f^{(k)}(t)|=|x|.

It is easy to see that
|x|>\frac{1}{2}
since
x+y=k!A_{k}
is an integer, because
|x|>|y|
which means
x+y\neq 0
and
1\leq|x+y|\leq|x|+|y|<2|x|
. Also we can see
a_{0}<a_{1}<\cdots<a_{k}
, so

\displaystyle D_{k}=\prod_{0\leq i<j\leq k}(a_{j}-a_{i})\leq\prod_{0\leq i<j\leq k}a_{j}\leq\prod_{0\leq i<j\leq k}a_{k}\leq a_{k}^{\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}.

With (
17
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}<D_{k}|f^{(k)}(x)|\leq a_{k}^{\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}c_{k}\lambda_{k}=\frac{\lambda_{k}}{2}((2k)^{-1}a_{k}\alpha_{k})^{\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}

which implies (

), and concludes the proof.
∎

The aim of this section is to discuss the techniques and tools that we will be using in the proof of Theorem

k\geq 3
, which are stated in the Theorem. The first tool we will introduce is an easy enumeration principle

Lemma 2.11
.

Let
S
be a finite set of integers,
S=\{a_{1}<a_{2}\cdots<a_{n}\}
such that
|a_{n}-a_{1}|\leq N
. If one can cover
S
by pairwise distinct intervals
\mathcal{I}
and if
L(\mathcal{I})
is the length of
\mathcal{I}
, then

\displaystyle|S|\leq N\max_{\mathcal{I}}\Big(\frac{|S\cap\mathcal{I}|}{L(\mathcal{I})}\Big)+2\max_{\mathcal{I}}|S\cap\mathcal{I}|.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{I}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{I}_{J}
be such a covering of
S
, pairwise distinct here simply means if
h\neq j
, then
\mathcal{I}_{h}\cap\mathcal{I}_{j}=\emptyset
and if
2\leq j\leq J-1
, then
\mathcal{I}_{j}\subseteq[a_{1},a_{n}]
, and what follows next is very easy to see,

\displaystyle|S|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{J}|S\cap\mathcal{I}_{j}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{J-1}\Big\{L(\mathcal{I}_{j})\frac{|S\cap\mathcal{I}_{j}|}{L(\mathcal{I}_{j})}\Big\}+|S\cap\mathcal{I}_{1}|+|S\cap\mathcal{I}_{J}|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq j\leq J}\Big(\frac{|S\cap\mathcal{I}_{j}|}{L(\mathcal{I}_{j})}\Big)\sum_{j=2}^{J-1}L(\mathcal{I}_{j})+2\max_{1\leq j\leq J}|S\cap\mathcal{I}_{j}|

\displaystyle\leq N\max_{1\leq j\leq J}\Big(\frac{|S\cap\mathcal{I}_{j}|}{L(\mathcal{I}_{j})}\Big)+2\max_{1\leq j\leq J}|S\cap\mathcal{I}_{j}|.

∎

The next tool we will introduce is a certain class of inequalities which exist to bound a derivatives of some function if we know the bound of its highest order of derivative and they were the lowest. They are called the Landau-Hadamard-Kolmogorov inequalities. It was first developed by E. Landau
[
21
]
in 1913 with rather restricted conditions, explicit statements of the following three theorems can also be found in
[
1
, p. 374]
.

Theorem 2.12

(E. Landau)
.

If
I
is an interval with length
\geq 2
and
f\in C^{2}(I)
satisfies the conditions
|f(x)|\leq 1
and
|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|\leq 1
on
I
, then
|f^{\prime}(x)|\leq 2
and the constant 2 is the best possible.

This was generalized by Hadamard
[
22
]
, without the explicit restrictions on the bound of

and
|f^{\prime\prime}|

Theorem 2.13

(Hadamard)
.

If
f\in C^{2}([a,a+L])
and

,
L>0
, then

\displaystyle\sup_{a\leq x\leq a+L}|f^{\prime}(x)|\leq\frac{2}{L}\sup_{a\leq x\leq a+L}|f(x)|+\frac{L}{2}\sup_{a\leq x\leq a+L}|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|.

As with the development of the derivative tests we have introduced, the next natural step was to generalize it for higher order of derivatives
[
23
]

Theorem 2.14

(L. Neder)

If

,

\displaystyle\sup_{a\leq x\leq a+L}|f^{(j)}(x)|\leq\frac{(2k)^{2k}}{L^{j}}\sup_{a\leq x\leq a+L}|f(x)|+L^{k-j}\sup_{a\leq x\leq a+L}|f^{(k)}(x)|.

(21)

Lemma 2.15

(Gorny)
.

Let

be an integer,

,

and

\displaystyle|f(x)|\leq M_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad|f^{(k)}(x)|\leq M_{k}

In practice, we will use this result in the following form

\displaystyle|f^{(j)}(x)|<4e(ek/j)^{j}\big\{k^{j+1}M_{0}L^{-j}+e^{j-1}M_{0}^{1-j/k}M_{k}^{j/k}\big\}.

(22)

The next technique
[
1
, Lemma 5.11]
will be used to calculate the implied constant appeared in Theorem
2.1
.

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
k\geq 3
be an integer and
a>e(k-1)
be a real number. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\Big(\frac{a}{j}\Big)^{2j}\leq e^{2}k\Big(\frac{a}{k}\Big)^{2k-2}.

Proof.

By some brute force calculation, we can find that the derivative of
\Big(\frac{e(k-1)}{x}\Big)^{2x}
equals
0
only when
x=k+1
, and it is a local maximum. Since
a>e(k-1)
, we now can say that the function
x\mapsto(a/x)^{2x}
is increasing as soon as
1\leq x\leq k-1
and so

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\Big(\frac{a}{j}\Big)^{2j}\leq(k-1)\Big(\frac{a}{k-1}\Big)^{2k-2}

and further more

\displaystyle\exp\Big\{\log\Big(\frac{k-1}{k}\Big(\frac{a}{k-1}\times\frac{k}{a}\Big)^{2k-2}\Big)\Big\}=\exp\Big\{(2k-1)\log\Big(1+\frac{1}{k-1}\Big)\Big\}.

(23)

Notice that if we let
\frac{1}{k-1}-\log(1+\frac{1}{k-1})=b
, we will find
-b=\log\Big(\frac{1+\frac{1}{k-1}}{e^{\frac{1}{k-1}}}\Big)
, and we know
1+x\leq e^{x}
to be true by the fact that

is tangent to
e^{x}
at
x=0
and
e^{x}
is convex. So
b\geq 0
, and to continue from (
23
), we have

\displaystyle\exp\Big\{(2k-1)\log\Big(1+\frac{1}{k-1}\Big)\Big\}\leq\exp\Big(\frac{2k-3}{k-1}\Big)\leq e^{2}.

Rearrange things a bit, we will get the result.
∎

.

(Major arc)

.

The set

Proof.

First, we expand the region where

is defined. Let

\displaystyle\tilde{f}^{(k)}=f^{(k)}(N)\text{ if }x\leq N\text{ and }\tilde{f}^{(k)}(x)=f^{(k)}(2N)\text{ if }x\geq 2N,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{P^{(k)}(t_{1})}{k!}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{P(\alpha_{j})}{\prod_{0\leq i\leq k,i\neq j}(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i})}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{P(\alpha_{j})\pm\delta}{\prod_{0\leq i\leq k,i\neq j}(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i})}

Set

, for some

\displaystyle\frac{f^{(k)}(t)}{k!}=\frac{g^{(k)}(t)}{k!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{g(\alpha_{j})}{\prod_{0\leq j\leq k,i\neq j}(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i})}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{k}{L}\Big)^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{g(\alpha_{j})}{\prod_{0\leq i\leq k,i\neq j}(j-i)}.

We can see by the way we defined

, the above equation can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{f^{(k)}(t)}{k!}\leq\delta\Big(\frac{k}{L}\Big)^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{1}{\prod_{0\leq i\leq k,i\neq j}(j-i)}\leq\delta\Big(\frac{k}{L}\Big)^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{1}{(k-j)!j!}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{k!}\Big(\frac{k}{L}\Big)^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}=\frac{2^{k}\delta}{k!}\Big(\frac{k}{L}\Big)^{k}.

A further note on the third inequality above, we can imagine the product

as multiplying the differences between

. Also it still might be worth mentioning that

, which gives us the final part above. By rearranging the above equation, we will obtain the required
bound.

2.

Let

\displaystyle P(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Big(\prod_{i=1,i\neq j}^{k}\frac{x-n_{i}}{n_{j}-n_{i}}\Big)\lfloor f(n_{i})\rceil

and

\displaystyle b_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\lfloor f(x_{j})\rceil}{\prod_{1\leq i\leq k,i\neq j}(n_{j}-n_{i})}=\frac{A}{\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(n_{j}-n_{i})}.

Where

and A is an integer since all the data points we are interpolating here are integral points. If we expand (

as denominator, so

since it is the smallest positive integer such that

. Hence, we have

\displaystyle q\leq\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(n_{j}-n_{i})\leq\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}(n_{k}-n_{1})=(n_{k}-n_{1})^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}.

so that

\displaystyle L\geq n_{k}-n_{1}\geq q^{\frac{2}{k(k-1)}}.

Which implies that for any such

. So it follows that there can only be at most

\displaystyle|\bar{A}|\leq k(\frac{L}{n_{k}-n_{1}}+1)\leq 2kLq^{-\frac{2}{k(k-1)}}.

For the third part of the proof, let

\displaystyle\geq|P(n)-m|-|f(n)-m|-\delta\geq\frac{1}{3q}+2\delta-\delta-\delta

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3q}.

(25)

Since

for some

2.4
The Proof of the Theorem of Huxley and Sargos

From the end of the previous section, we get an estimation of the contribution of points of
\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)
which come from major arcs. The next natural step to progress our proof of the theorem of Huxley and Sargos is to estimate the contribution of those points which do not come from major arcs. The next Lemma
[
1
, Lemma 5.15]
we will state provides such an estimate, and its proof is similar to the proof of the
k
th derivative test. Also, again to avoid the trivial case, we may assume
\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\geq k+1
.

Lemma 2.22
.

Let
N\leq n_{0}<\cdots<n_{k}\leq 2N
be
k+1
points of
\mathcal{S}(f,N,\delta)
, which do not lie on the same algebraic curve of degree
<k
. Then

\displaystyle n_{k}-n_{0}>\min\Big((c_{k}\lambda_{k})^{-\frac{2}{k(k+1)}},2^{-1}\delta^{-\frac{2}{k(k-1)}}\Big).

Proof.

By definition, there exist integers
m_{0},\dots,m_{k}
and real numbers
\delta_{0},\dots,\delta_{k}
such that

\displaystyle f(n_{j})=m_{j}+\delta_{j}

with
|\delta_{j}|<\delta
for all
j\in\{0,\dots,k\}
. We define
D_{k}
as follows

\displaystyle D_{k}=\prod_{0\leq h<i\leq k}(n_{i}-n_{h})>0.

and let
P=b_{k}X^{k}+\cdots+b_{0}
be the Lagrange polynomial interpolating the points
(n_{j},m_{j})
. Then we have

\displaystyle b_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{m_{j}}{\prod_{0\leq i\leq k,i\neq j}(n_{j}-n_{i})}=\frac{A_{k}}{D_{k}}

where
A_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}
, since the points we are interpolating are integral points. Reasoning exactly in the same way as in the proof of Theorem
2.10
, we get

\displaystyle k!A_{k}=D_{k}f^{(k)}(t)-k!D_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{\delta_{j}}{\prod_{0\leq i\leq k,i\neq j}(n_{j}-n_{i})}.

Since the points we are interpolating do not lie on the same algebraic curve of degree
<k
, we have
b_{k}\neq 0
. Since
A_{k}
is an integer, we have
|A_{k}|\geq 1
, and using
|\delta_{j}|<\delta
and condition (
1
) we get

\displaystyle k!\leq k!|A_{k}|

\displaystyle<c_{k}\lambda_{k}D_{k}+k!\delta D_{k}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\frac{1}{\prod_{0\leq i\leq k,i\neq j}|n_{j}-n_{i}|}

\displaystyle=c_{k}\lambda_{k}D_{k}+k!\delta\sum_{j=0}^{k}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq h<i\leq k\\
h\neq j,i\neq j\end{subarray}}(n_{i}-n_{h})

\displaystyle\leq c_{k}\lambda_{k}(n_{k}-n_{0})^{\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}+(k+1)!\delta(n_{k}-n_{0})^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}.

which suggests that either

\displaystyle k!\leq 2c_{k}\lambda_{k}(n_{k}-n_{0})^{\frac{k(k+1)}{2}}\quad\text{or}\quad k!\leq 2(k+1)!\delta(n_{k}-n_{0})^{\frac{k(k-1)}{2}}

and this implies

\displaystyle n_{k}-n_{0}>\min\Big(\Big(\frac{k!}{2}\Big)^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}(c_{k}\lambda_{k})^{-\frac{2}{k(k+1)}},(2k+2)^{-\frac{2}{k(k-1)}}\delta^{-\frac{2}{k(k-1)}}\Big)\quad\qed

Proof.

or depending on the condition we discussed before

\displaystyle|S_{0}|=R_{0}\leq\frac{3}{2}b_{k}N\delta^{\frac{2}{k(k-1)}}+16k^{3}\Big(\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{k}}\Big)^{1/k}+10e^{3}k^{2}

where

T_{0}
, again here we assume the size of
T_{0}
is larger than
k^{2}+1
, otherwise the result is trivial. Since
G
is not contained in any major arc, so we can find an integer
j\in\{k,\dots,k^{2}\}
such that
j+1
points
(n_{i},m_{i})
do not lie on the same algebraic curve of degree
<k
. By Lemma
2.22
, we have

\displaystyle n_{k^{2}}-n_{0}\geq n_{j}-n_{0}\geq n_{k}-n_{0}>\min\Big((c_{k}\lambda_{k})^{-\frac{2}{k(k+1)}},2^{-1}\delta^{-\frac{2}{k(k-1)}}\Big)

which means

\displaystyle|T_{0}|\leq 2k^{2}\Big(N(c_{k}\lambda_{k})^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}+2N\delta^{\frac{2}{k(k-1)}}+1\Big)

and the proof is completed with the fact that
\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\leq|T_{0}|+|S_{0}|
.
∎

3
Optimality of The Methods

3.1
Integral Points Around Polynomials

In this chapter, we will discuss the optimality of Theorem
2.1
, that is whether the bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\ll N\lambda_{k}^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}+N\delta^{\frac{2}{k(k-1)}}+\big(\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{k}}\big)^{\frac{1}{k}}+1

(29)

could be improved with some extra conditions or if the bound is false without certain conditions. A good place to start will be putting the theorem to the test where polynomials are concerned. First, let’s discuss some general scenario where a better bound can be obtained.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\delta\in[0,\frac{1}{4}]
,
N,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
and

. If there exist

and

such that the greatest common divisor
(a,q)=1
and

, then for all
\epsilon>0

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(P,N,\delta)\ll Nq^{-1/k}+N\delta^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}+N^{\epsilon}

the term
N^{\epsilon}
being replaced by
1
if
k=1
.

If
\alpha_{k}\in(0,1]
, then we can take
a=1
and
q=\lfloor\alpha_{k}^{-1}\rfloor
. So if we further assume that

, then we derive

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(P,N,\delta)\ll N\alpha_{k}^{1/k}+N\delta^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}+N^{\epsilon}.

(30)

If
\alpha_{k}>1
, then the bound (
30
) is trivial provided that
\alpha_{k}\geq N^{-k}
. Now if
\alpha_{k}<N^{-k}
, then
N<\alpha^{-1/k}
, and thus we can derive the following corollary.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\delta\in[0,\frac{1}{4}]
,

and

with

. Then, for all

,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(P,N,\delta)\ll N\alpha_{k}^{1/k}+N\delta^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}+\alpha_{k}^{-1/k}+N^{\epsilon},

the term
N^{\epsilon}
being replaced by
1
if
k=1
.

Next, we can see from the proof of Theorem
2.1
that the bound
\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\leq|T_{0}|+|S_{0}|
has two parts. If the function
f
is already in the form of a Lagrange polynomial with rational coefficient, then the contribution of the points in the set

will only come from major arcs, that is

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\leq b_{k}N\delta^{\frac{2}{k(k-1)}}+8k^{3}\Big(\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{k}}\Big)^{1/k}+10e^{3}k^{2}

or

Further Refinements

The refinements of Theorem

]
. We will show the following version which is stated explicitly in

Theorem 3.3

(Branton-Sargos)
.

Let

such that there exists
\lambda_{2}>0
such that, for all

\displaystyle|f^{\prime\prime}(x)|\asymp\lambda_{2}.

Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\ll N\lambda_{2}^{1/3}+N\delta+\Big(\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{2}}\Big)^{1/2}+1.

If in addition there exists
\lambda_{1}>0
such that

for all

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\ll N\lambda_{2}^{1/3}+N\delta+\lambda_{1}\Big(\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{2}}\Big)^{1/2}+\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{1}}+1.

From Theorem

,

is the main term of the equality. It’s very difficult to improve on the main term. The others are the secondary terms and the quantity

is quasi-optimal. So we turn our eyes on the term

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
k\geq 3
be an integer and

such that there exist
\lambda_{k-1}>0
and
\lambda_{k}>0
such that, for all

\displaystyle|f^{(k-1)}(x)|\asymp\lambda_{k-1},\quad|f^{(k)}(x)|\asymp\lambda_{k}\quad\text{and}\quad\lambda_{k-1}=N\lambda_{k}.

(31)

Then the following upper bounds hold.

•

For all

, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\ll N\lambda_{k}^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}+N\delta^{\frac{2}{(k-1)(k-2)}}+N(\delta\lambda_{k-1})^{\frac{2}{k^{2}-k+2}}+\Big(\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{k-1}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}+1.

•

For

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\ll N\lambda_{3}^{1/6}+N\delta^{2/3}+N(\delta^{3}\lambda_{3})^{1/12}+\Big(\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{2}}\Big)^{1/2}+1.

•

For all
k\geq 4
and

, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\ll\Big\{

\displaystyle N\lambda_{k}^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}+N(\delta\lambda_{k-1})^{\frac{2}{k^{2}-k+2}}+N\delta^{\frac{4}{k^{2}-3k+6}}

\displaystyle+N(\delta^{2}N^{-1}\lambda_{k-1}^{-1})^{\frac{2}{k^{2}-3k+4}}\Big\}N^{\epsilon}+\Big(\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{k-1}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}+1.

•

For all
k\geq 5
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\ll N\lambda_{k}^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}+N\delta^{\frac{2}{(k-1)(k-2)}}+\Big(\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{k-1}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{k-1}}+1.

We can see easily that Theorem
2.1
implies the
k
th derivative test, because the condition
\delta\ll\lambda_{k}
means the main term dominates all the others. The following result is an estimate analogous to the
k
th derivative test but with a more flexible condition than (

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
f\in C^{\infty}[N,2N]
such that there exists
T\geq 1
such that, for all
x\in[N,2N]
and all
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, we have

\displaystyle|f^{(j)}(x)|\asymp\frac{T}{N^{j}},

(32)

and

\displaystyle N\delta\leq T\leq\delta^{-1}.

(33)

Then for all
k\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\ll T^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}N^{\frac{k-1}{k+1}}.

Note that, using the notion of (
32
), for
k\geq 2
, Huxley and Sargos’s result may be stated as follow

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\ll T^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}N^{\frac{k-1}{k+1}}+N\delta^{\frac{2}{k(k-1)}}+N(\delta T^{-1})^{1/k},

(34)

and the term
N(\delta T^{-1})^{1/k}
is dominated by the term
N\delta^{\frac{2}{k(k-1)}}
for
k\geq 3
. So if Proposition
3.5
is true, it tells us that the condition (
33
) is sufficient to remove both these two terms mentioned.

Proof.

We will be using induction to prove the proposition. For
k=1
, by the first derivative test we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)

\displaystyle\ll N\lambda_{1}+N\delta+\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{1}}+1=N\frac{T}{N}+N\delta+\frac{\delta}{T/N}+1

\displaystyle=T+N\delta+\delta\frac{N}{T}+1\ll T\qquad\text{by (\ref{33})}.

Now suppose the claim to be true for some
k\geq 1
, that is
\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\ll T^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}N^{\frac{k-1}{k+1}}
. Now by Huxley and Sargos’s result (
34
), what we have for
k+1
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\ll T^{\frac{2}{(k+1)(k+2)}}N^{\frac{k}{k+2}}+N\delta^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}+N(\delta T^{-1})^{\frac{1}{k+1}}.

Let

\displaystyle E=\max\Big(T^{\frac{2}{(k+1)(k+2)}}N^{\frac{k}{k+2}},N\delta^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}},N(\delta T^{-1})^{\frac{1}{k+1}}\Big)=\max(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}).

We will get

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\ll\min\Big(E,T^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}N^{\frac{k-1}{k+1}}\Big).

The result follows if
E=e_{1}
. Now to discuss the cases where
E=e_{2}
and
E=e_{3}
separately we will need the following obvious inequality. If
x,y\geq 0
and
0\leq a\leq 1
, then
\min(x,y)\leq x^{a}y^{1-a}
.

•

Case
E=e_{2}
, we choose
a=\frac{1}{k+2}
. By the inequality we have deduced and (
33
)

\displaystyle\min\Big(e_{2},T^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}N^{\frac{k-1}{k+1}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq T^{\frac{2}{(k+1)(k+2)}}N^{\frac{k}{k+2}}(T\delta)^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)(k+2)}}

\displaystyle\leq T^{\frac{2}{(k+1)(k+2)}}N^{\frac{k}{k+2}}.

•

Case
E=e_{3}
, we choose
a=\frac{2}{k+2}
, similar as in the previous case, we get

\displaystyle\min\Big(e_{3},T^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}N^{\frac{k-1}{k+1}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq T^{\frac{2}{(k+1)(k+2)}}N^{\frac{k}{k+2}}(N\delta T^{-1})^{\frac{2}{(k+1)(k+2)}}

\displaystyle\leq T^{\frac{2}{(k+1)(k+2)}}N^{\frac{k}{k+2}}.

Which completes the proof.  ∎

The main term in the case

3.3
Smooth Curves

Simply by using the first derivative test and letting
\delta\rightarrow 0
, we get deduce a bound of the number of integer points lying on the arc of the curve
y=f(x)
with
N<x\leq 2N
, which is
\ll N\lambda_{k}^{\frac{2}{k(k+1)}}+1
. Historically, this number was first investigated by Jarník
[
25
]
who proved that a strictly convex arc
y=f(x)
with length
L
has at most
\leq\frac{3}{(2\pi)^{1/3}}L^{2/3}+O(L^{1/3})
integer points on the arc of the curve, this is a nearly best possible result under the sole condition of convexity.

However, Swinnerton Dyer
[
26
]
and Schmidt
[
27
]
proved independently that if
f\in C^{3}[0,N]
is such that
|f(x)|\leq N
and
f^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in[0,N]
, then the number of integer points on the arc
y=f(x)
with
0\leq x\leq N
is
\ll N^{3/5+\epsilon}
. This result was then generalized by Bombieri and Pila
[
28
]
as follows
[
1
, Proposition 5.3]
.

Proposition 3.8

(Bombieri-Pila)
.

Let
N\geq 1
,
k\geq 4
be integers and set
K=\binom{k+2}{2}
. Let
\mathcal{I}
be an interval with length
N
and
f\in C^{k}(\mathcal{I})
satisfying
|f^{\prime}(x)|\leq 1
,
f^{\prime\prime}(x)>0
and such that the number of solutions of the equation
f^{(K)}(x)=0
is
\leq m
. Then there exists a constant
c_{0}=c_{0}(k)>0
such that the number of integer points on the arc
y=f(x)
with
x\in\mathcal{I}
is

\displaystyle\leq c_{0}(m+1)N^{1/2+3/(k+3)}

The ideas of Bombieri and Pila have been extended by Huxley
[
7
]
to apply on the problems involving counting the number of integer points which are very close to regular curves, an explicit statement can also be found in
[
1
, Proposition 5.4]
. The function is supposed to be
C^{5}
and along with the usual non-vanishing conditions of the derivatives on
[N,2N]
, the proof also requires lower bounds of the following determinants:

\displaystyle D_{1}(f;x)=\begin{vmatrix}f^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)&3f^{\prime\prime}(x)\\
f^{(4)}(x)&4f^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)\\
\end{vmatrix}\begin{matrix}\vphantom{x}\\
\vphantom{y}.\end{matrix}

\displaystyle D_{2}(f;x)=\frac{1}{2f^{\prime\prime}(x)}\begin{vmatrix}f^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)&3f^{\prime\prime}(x)&0\\
f^{(4)}(x)&4f^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)&6f^{\prime\prime}(x)^{2}\\
f^{(5)}(x)&5f^{(4)}(x)&20f^{\prime\prime}(x)f^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)\\
\end{vmatrix}\begin{matrix}\vphantom{x}\\
\vphantom{y}\\
\vphantom{z}.\end{matrix}

Proposition 3.9

(Huxley)
.

Assume that
f\in C^{5}[N,2N]
such that there exist real numbers
C,T\geq 1
such that

\displaystyle|f^{(j)}(x)|\leq C^{j+1}j!\times\frac{T}{N^{j}}\quad(j=1,\cdots,5)

\displaystyle|f^{(j)}(x)|\geq\frac{j!}{C^{j+1}}\times\frac{T}{N^{j}}\quad(j=2,3)

\displaystyle|D_{1}(f;x)|\geq 144C^{-8}\times\frac{T^{2}}{N^{6}},\quad|D_{2}(f;x)|\geq 4320C^{-12}\times\frac{T^{3}}{N^{9}}.

Let

be a real number. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(f,N,\delta)\ll(NT)^{4/15}+N(\delta^{11}T^{9})^{1/75}.

The implied constant depends only on

.

The main term of this result is very good, since in the second derivative test, the main term only yields
(NT)^{1/3}
and Theorem
3.6
can only give

On the other hand, the secondary term of this result is too large, and thus useless in many applications. But this may be improved subject to some additional non-vanishing conditions of certain quite complicated determinants

4
Applications

4.1
Diophantine Inequality

One interesting application arises naturally from the methods we have been discussing is to estimate the number of solutions of a given Diophantine inequality. Consider the following Diophantine inequality

\displaystyle|\alpha n^{2}+\beta m^{2}-z|\leq\delta.

where
\alpha,\beta,z\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta
small. The goal is to estimate the number of non-trivial solutions as
(n,m)
range over a closed interval, The inequality can be then reformulated as a problem of finding integer points near the planar curve
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
given by

\displaystyle\alpha n^{2}+\beta m^{2}-z=0.

Similar ideas have also been discussed by Damaris Schindler
[
17
]
where Diophantine inequalities for generic ternary diagonal forms are in concern. Fix some degree
k\geq 2
and let
\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, Let
\theta>0
and consider the inequality

\displaystyle|x_{1}^{k}-\alpha_{2}x_{2}^{k}-\alpha_{3}x_{3}^{k}|<\theta.

(38)

The goal here is to understand when this inequality has a non-trivial solution if the variables
x_{i}
are allowed to range over a box of size
P
. With some techniques rather complicated, we would eventually be able to reformulate the inequality (
38
) as a problem of finding rational points near the planar curve
\mathcal{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
given by
1-\alpha_{2}y_{2}^{k}-\alpha_{3}y_{3}^{k}=0
. Finding solutions to (
38
) would be translated into studying a counting function of the type with
\delta=\frac{\theta}{P^{k-1}}

\displaystyle N_{\mathcal{C}}(P,\delta)=\sharp\Big\{\frac{p}{q}\in\mathbb{Q}^{2}:1\leq q\leq P,\operatorname{dist}\Big(\frac{p}{q},\mathcal{C}\Big)\ll\frac{\delta}{q}\Big\}.

4.2
The Squarefree Number Problem

Huxley and Sargos’s Theorem also can be applied on the squarefree number problem. Without using the theorem, we could still get the basic result as follows
[
1
, Lemma 5.3]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
x,y
satisfy
16\leq y<\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{x}
and
2\sqrt{y}\leq A<B\leq 2\sqrt{x}
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{x<n\leq x+y}\mu_{2}(n)=\frac{y}{\zeta(2)}+O\Big((R_{1}+R_{2})\log x+A\Big),

where
R_{1}=R_{1}(A,B)
and
R_{2}=R_{2}(B)
are defined by

\displaystyle R_{1}=\underset{A<N\leq B}{\max}\mathcal{R}\Big(\frac{x}{n^{2}},N,\frac{y}{N^{2}}\Big)\quad\text{and}\quad R_{2}=\underset{N\leq 2x/B^{2}}{\max}\mathcal{R}\Big(\sqrt{\frac{x}{n}},N,\frac{y}{\sqrt{Nx}}\Big).

Now suppose first that
y\leq x^{4/9}
, if we use the above lemma with
A=2x^{2/9}
and
B=x^{1/3}
, by the restriction stated in the lemma, we have
A>2y^{1/2}
and hence

\displaystyle\sum_{x<n\leq x+y}\mu_{2}(n)=\frac{y}{\zeta(2)}+O\Big(R_{1}(2x^{2/9},x^{1/3})\log x+R_{2}(x^{1/3})\log x+x^{2/9}\Big).

Notice that. The restriction on the second derivative test i.e. Theorem
2.7
is satisfied by the function in its respective range of summation. So using Theorem
2.7
we can get

\displaystyle R_{2}\Big(x^{1/3}\Big)\ll\underset{N\leq 2x^{1/3}}{\max}\Big((Nx)^{1/6}+y^{1/2}(N^{3}x^{-1})^{1/4}\Big)\ll x^{2/9}+yx^{-2/9}.

Furthermore, if we use the Theorem of Huxley and Sargos with
k=3
for
R_{1}(2x^{2/9},x^{1/3})
, we will have

\displaystyle R_{1}(2x^{2/9,x^{1/3}})

\displaystyle\ll\underset{2x^{2/9}<N\leq x^{1/3}}{\max}\Big\{(Nx)^{1/6}+(Ny)^{1/3}+N(yx^{-1})^{1/3}\Big\}

\displaystyle\ll x^{2/9}+x^{1/9}y^{1/3}.

Thus, if
y\leq x^{1/3}
, we will have

\displaystyle\sum_{x<n\leq x+y}\mu_{2}(n)=\frac{y}{\zeta(2)}+O\Big(x^{2/9}\log x\Big).

Which means that there exists a constant
c_{0}>0
such that if
c_{0}x^{2/9}\log x\leq y<\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{x}
, the interval
[x,x+y]
contains a squarefree number is since by the condition we already have
y\geq 16
, to guarantee that the RHS non-zero, we only need to make sure
\frac{y}{\zeta(2)}
is bigger than the approximated term
O(x^{2/9}\log x)
for some such
c_{0}
.
