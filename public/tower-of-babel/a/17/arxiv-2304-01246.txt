Safety Analysis in the Era of Large Language Models: A Case Study of STPA using ChatGPT
Yi Qi 1 , Xingyu Zhao 1,2 , Siddartha Khastgir 2 , Xiaowei Huang 1

Abstract
Can safety analysis make use of Large Language Models (LLMs)? A case study explores the application of Systems Theoretic Process Analysis (STPA) to Automatic Emergency Brake (AEB) and Electricity Demand Side Management (DSM) systems, using ChatGPT. We investigate how collaboration schemes, input semantic complexity, and prompt engineering influence STPA results. Comparative results show that using ChatGPT without human intervention may be inadequate due to reliability related issues, but with careful design, it may outperform human experts. No statistically significant differences are found when varying the input semantic complexity or using domain-agnostic prompt guidelines. While STPA-specific prompt engineering shows statistical significance of producing more pertinent results, ChatGPT tends to produce more conservative and less comprehensive outcomes. We also highlight future challenges, including concerns about LLM trustworthiness and the necessity for standardisation and regulation in this domain. All our experimental data are publicly accessible.



Keywords
: STPA, safety-critical systems, ChatGPT, large language models, safe AI, automatic emergency brake systems, human machine interaction, hazards identification, safety assurance.

According to both qualitative comparison and statistical analysis, some key findings emerge:

•

Applying ChatGPT without human experts’ intervention is inadequate due to the problematic reliability of LLMs in general.

•

More co-analysis activities and human-ChatGPT interactions in STPA may outperform human experts alone.

•

Neither input complexity nor the use of domain-agnostic prompts had a statistically significant impact on ChatGPT’s outputs.

•

While STPA-specific prompt engineering shows statistical significance of producing more pertinent results, ChatGPT tends to produce more conservative and less comprehensive outcomes.

2.2
Systems Theoretic Process Analysis

STPA
is a widely used safety analysis method that is based on the
STAMP
model. The main focus of
STPA
is on the overall control loop and process analysis of a system, with an emphasis on identifying unsafe control actions and causal factors within the control structure. This method has found applications in various domains, including railway safety assurances
[
50
]
, cyber safety and security
[
24
]
, collaborative robots
[
3
]
, and autonomous vehicles
[
28
,
8
]
. Readers are refereed to
[
18
,
41
]
.

The workflow of STPA, as outlined by N. Leveson
[
34
]
, encompasses five critical steps:
1) Define Accidents/Hazards. 2) Model Control Structure. 3) Identify Unsafe Control Actions. 4) Identify Causal Scenarios. 5) Derive Safety Requirements.
Specifically, the initial step involves defining potential accidents or hazards at a high level, typically encompassing significant negative outcomes for personnel or equipment that can be anticipated
[
21
]
. The subsequent step involves creating a system control structure that segregates the control loop from its individual components, such as the starting point, intermediate processes (like mechanical and software structures), and completion components.
Following this,
a standard
Unsafe Control Actions (UCAs)
consist of the following parts:

Source+Type+Control\;Action+Context+Link\;to\;Hazards

(1)

This structure of UCAs
embodies a comprehensive framework that necessitates meticulous consideration of each constituent element
1
1

1

The first element comprises the controller responsible for delivering the control action. Following this, the second element categorises the nature of the unsafe control action, which may manifest as either provided, not provided, too early or too late, stopped too soon or applied too long. The third element pertains to the specific control action or directive, as defined within the control structure. The fourth element addresses the previously mentioned context, while the final element establishes a connection to hazards or sub-hazards. Although UCAs are typically documented in the order outlined above, alternative sequencing might be more coherent or intuitive in certain instances. The sequence itself is not of paramount importance; rather, the inclusion of all five components is crucial.
.
After analysing the
UCAs
to identify potential hazards or risks, the
STPA
proceeds to determine potential causes and scenarios. The final step involves generating safety requirements for each identified risk or hazard.

Expert knowledge is essential in order to conduct all five steps described above. The first step largely depends on the safety-critical application and its environments, while the second step rely on the system’s internal structure. The third step is highly dependent on expertise of the safety analyst, as it requires an in-depth understanding of the system or process under analysis, as well as prior knowledge of relevant safety regulations and standards. The fourth and fifth steps also require the expert knowledge, as the expert team is able to identify and evaluate potential failure modes and develop reasonable safety requirements.
Despite its popularity, conducting STPA is widely acknowledged to be challenging.
The complexity arises from the increasingly complex systems being analysed (including recent learning-enabled systems
[
41
]
), while subjectivity is due to the limited knowledge and expert judgement of individuals
[
11
]
. These motivate us to investigate if and how LLMs can address the two challenges.

3
Methodology

Our case study comprises a series of experiments, conducting both qualitative and quantitative analyses on the output generated by ChatGPT in STPA. Two independent human expert teams were engaged to participate in and independently validate our experimental results.

3.1
Research Questions

We first define three levels of abstraction, ranging from coarse to fine-grained, that represents how human experts may interact with ChatGPT:

•

Workflow Level
: At this most coarse abstraction level, we examine the varying frequencies, directions, and timing of expert interactions with ChatGPT throughout the STPA process.

•

Semantics Level
:
Given a workflow, the system under analysis can be modelled with varying amount of semantic information, i.e., level of system details in the control loop structures. We consider such differences in input complexity and study the impact on outputs.

•

Syntax Level
:
While preserving the main semantic information, the order of words, sentence structures and requirements on output formats may vary in individual prompts, with or without prompt engineering. We consider such syntactic differences at this level.

To make remarks and draw conclusions on the optimal ways of using ChatGPT for STPA, we frame the following research questions (RQs), corresponding to the three levels of collaboration, respectively:

•

RQ1 (Collaboration Scheme): How do various collaboration schemes of integrating ChatGPT into STPA affect the effectiveness and usability of STPA?

•

RQ2 (Control Loop Complexity): To what extent do variations in semantic complexity of individual input questions to ChatGPT affect the comprehensiveness and pertinence of STPA results?

•

RQ3 (Prompt Engieering): Does the utilisation of domain-agnostic and STPA-specific prompt engineering affect the comprehensiveness and pertinence of STPA results?

The first case shown in Fig.

Intuitively, this case represents the scenario in which the human experts fully trust ChatGPT and only need to specify their high-level inputs (i.e., conducting STPA on the AEB systems) to get the final STPA results.

The “Recurring simplex collaboration” is illustrated in Fig.
2

In this case, the expert team begins with providing “AEB System” and “STPA Method” to ChatGPT, and then proceeds to perform each of the five steps of STPA. At each step, the experts initiate the analysis by sending clear instructions containing keywords for that step, but without questioning and analysing the answers from the last step. Finally, ChatGPT outputs the overall result of the STPA analysis.

As the name “Recurring duplex collaboration” suggests, the third way of integrating ChatGPT in STPA involves multiple bi-directional interactions. As shown in Fig.
2
(c), the human safety experts sending “AEB System” and “STPA Method” to ChatGPT.
However, unlike “Recurring simplex collaboration” in Fig.
2
(b), they collect each output of ChatGPT, analyse the information by incorporating domain knowledge, and then query ChatGPT in turn based on the human processed question. The expert team is responsible for “Sending Messages”, “Reserving Messages”, and “Analysing Messages” in each step of STPA.

Since we will collect 3 groups (representing 3 control loop structures with different levels of complexity) of statistics on the two metrics—number and proportion of correct UCAs for each control action in Fig.

1

RQ3 Experiment and Evaluation Design

Experiment Design

We aim to study the impact of using prompt guidelines in RQ3. Prompt guidelines is the skill of creating precise questions or cues that steer ChatGPT toward generating the answers. It serves as a critical link between what the user is looking for and what the model understands. Good prompts can substantially boost the quality and pertinence of ChatGPT’s replies, whereas inadequate ones may yield incorrect or irrelevant outputs
[
14
]
. Normally, the standard prompt guide is as follows:

Roles+Tasks+Steps+Limitation\;description+Output\;request

(2)

For example, when “
Could you help me output a result using the STPA safety analysis method on an AEB system?
” is an intended question for ChatGPT, the structured question using a prompt guide becomes: “
You are a safety expert, could you help me output a result using the STPA safety analysis method on an AEB system? Please provide more specific answers based on your previous responses, and avoid generating redundant answers.
”

As illustrated in the example, the provided template represents a general approach for posing questions to ChatGPT
[
17
]
. However, its application in the domain of safety analysis, particularly concerning the characteristic of STPA, necessitates a specific focus. Thus, we design the the following STPA-specific prompt, which encapsulates the fundamental elements of UCAs (cf. expression (
1
)):

\begin{split}&Using\;STPA\;analysis,\;from<Source>send/recieve\;<ControlAction>to<Controlled\;\\
&Component>in\;the<System>,list\;potential\;unsafe\;control\;actions,which\;includes\;all\;\\
&<Type>,<Context>and<Link\;to\;Hazards>\end{split}

(3)

For example, “
Using STPA analysis, from Didtribution Network Operator (DNO) send the request to the Smart Grid, list the potential unsafe control actions, which includes all type (provided, not provided, too early or too late, stopped too soon or applied too long), context and link to the Hazards.
” is a question we built to query ChatGPT using the STPA-specific prompt guide.

In our later experiments for RQ3, the input prompts will be constructed in alignment with the aforementioned prompt guidelines. Each unique control action enumerated in Baseline II will be queried thrice—without the prompt guideline, with domain-agnostic prompt (
2
), and with STPA-specific prompt (
3
).
Similarly to RQ2, we opted for the third collaboration schemes and the third step of STPA, which involves identifying UCAs for each control action.

Regarding the comprehensiveness of correct UCAs identified, we only observe that using domain-agnostic prompts can significantly identify more UCAs than using STPA-specific prompts. On the other hand, using STPA-specific prompts can significantly enhance the pertinence of ChatGPT’s answers compared to using either domain-agnostic prompts or no prompts at all. 4.4
Threats to Validity

Construct Validity

“Construct validity” pertains to the degree to which the investigative goals and outcomes accurately measure their targeted constructs. It assesses whether a test or measurement tool truly measures what it claims to measure.
In our study, we collect data regarding the correct UCAs to do both qualitative and quantitative analyses. But the determination of “correctness” of a UCA is prone to bias, as it depends on the specific background and expertise of the human experts involved. To mitigate, we engaged two independent teams of experts to validate the case study results. We also make all intermediate results publicly accessible on our project website for feedbacks to further mitigate this threat.
In RQ3, we aim to examine the effect of using prompt guidelines, for this purpose, we employed both a domain-agnostic guide and a STPA-specific prompt guide. However, only one STPA-specific prompt guide was used, which poses a potential threat. We recognise that prompt engineering is a rapidly evolving field, potentially leading to new methods for crafting more effective domain-specific prompts. In future, we plan to diversify the STPA-specific prompt guidelines in our study to mitigate this threat further.

Internal Validity

Threats may correspond to bias in establishing cause-effect relationships in our case study. In RQ2 and RQ3, we conducted well established statistical testing techniques to draw the causal-effect conclusions. Confounding variables, i.e., factors other than the control loop structure complexity and prompt guideline (e.g., tone and politeness) may have influenced outcomes of correct UCAs. To mitigate such threat, we plan to do more Randomised Controlled Trials in the future by carefully considering and addressing confounding variables in the design and analysis. Moreover, when making claims on the effectiveness of using ChatGPT in STPA, our focus has been on experimenting with the step of finding UCAs in STPA. Although it is arguably the key step of STPA, this threat can be mitigated by completing the experiments on other STPA steps in future.

External Validity

Factors limiting generalisability threaten external validity. In this regard, we identify three potential threats: (1) the relatively small sample size in RQ2 and RQ3; (2) only two baseline systems (published in peer-reviewed works) were selected across the 3 RQs; (3) only one ChatGPT version was studied. To mitigate them, more sample needs to be collect with diversified data representing more systems under study and LLMs. In this preliminary study, we have explicitly discussed the limitations related to our sample’s representatives.

Discussion and Conclusion

5.1
Expanding the Research Horizon

We pose the following questions to initiate the discussion of future research in this direction.

What is the top concern of using LLMs in safety analysis, and how can it be addressed?

LLMs require a large amount of high-quality data for effective training, however, safety-critical industries may not have enough data available for this purpose, which may lead to LLMs not being able to output specific details of the safety analysis domain but only general information. There is currently no widely accepted standard for evaluating the accuracy and robustness of LLMs’ results. Additionally, the system may be vulnerable to unauthorised data disclosure and privacy violations, as well as backdoor attacks that bypass normal authentication
[
29
,
20
]
. Having said all that, we believe the top concern of using LLMs for safety analysis is the trustworthiness issues.

To make the safety analysis results trustworthy, a range of LLMs’ properties need to be considered, such as accuracy, reliability, generalisation, robustness, interpretability, and fairness

[
39
,
56
]
. With advancements in LLMs technology and increased research efforts in the field, issues surrounding the trustworthiness of LLMs may be resolved to a certain extent, such as the recent efforts
[
49
,
40
]
.

How does the rapid and constant updating of LLMs impact safety analysis?

The field of LLMs is currently undergoing rapid development, and the frequent updates and iterations of models like GPT-4 (published in March 2023) can contribute to lowering the blue bar in Fig.
1
, indicating an improvement in the generlisability of LLMs and a broader range of risks that can be addressed. Traditional safety assurance processes are static and carried out prior to the system’s deployment
[
7
]
, but the emergence of
dynamic assurance
presents an ongoing metric for continuously assessing safety assurance to ensure ongoing safety
[
4
,
7
]
.

The dynamic and constantly evolving nature of LLMs is well-suited for the dynamic assurance concept in safety analysis, while how to integrate them in a principled way require more investigation
.

What about other types of safety analyses?

In this paper, we have opted to utilise the STPA method because of its popularity
[
18
,
34
]
. For future research,
it is worthwhile to investigate other safety analysis methods, e.g. HAZOP, FTA and FMEA, as well, and determine which safety analysis method can be more effectively combined with LLMs
, and how to do so.

How to facilitate the practical and large-scale adoption of LLMs in safety analysis?

We believe the key step is standardisation, which is a pressing need in this emerging approach to safety analysis. All aspects of integrating LLMs into the safety analysis process (e.g., terminologies, actives, evaluations metrics, etc.) should be agreed upon, formalised, documented in safety standards, and subject to regulatory oversight.

5.2
Conclusion

Impressed by the recent successful stories of ChatGPT in many domains, we pose the question: “if safety analysis can actually make use of LLMs?”. To answer, we conducted a case study of applying ChatGPT in the STPA for a AEB system and a DSM system, which is the first attempt of using LLMs in safety analysis, to the best of our knowledge.

Our findings centre around the effectiveness, efficiency, and relevance of STPA when using various collaboration approaches with ChatGPT. These claims are supported by both comparative studies and statistical analyses, highlighting the potential of LLMs in addressing the inherent challenges of high complexity and subjectivity in STPA. Our work contributes by offering preliminary yet valuable insights toward the development of a more intelligent safety analysis framework that leverages the capabilities of LLMs.

(a) False Positives: AEB systems may mistake harmless obstacles or situations for potential collisions and trigger the brakes unnecessarily, causing the vehicle to stop suddenly and unexpectedly. This can result in rear-end collisions or accidents with other vehicles or pedestrians.

(b) False Negatives: AEB systems may fail to detect potential collisions due to sensor malfunctions, environmental factors (e.g., adverse weather conditions), or human error (e.g., the driver’s inattention or distraction). (c) Over-reliance on AEB: Drivers may become overly reliant on AEB systems, leading to a false sense of security and a decrease in their situational awareness. (d) Insufficient AEB Performance: AEB systems may not perform as intended in certain situations, such as high-speed or low-light conditions. This can lead to accidents if the system fails to brake in time or if the braking distance is longer than expected.

(e) Malfunctioning AEB: AEB systems may malfunction due to software errors, hardware failures, or maintenance issues. This can cause the system to either trigger false positives or false negatives, or to fail altogether, leading to accidents.

Q:

Could you help me define the accidents/hazards of the Electricity Demand Side Management (DSM) system as the first step of STPA?

The Systems-Theoretic Process Analysis (STPA) is a safety engineering methodology that focuses on identifying potential hazards and unsafe control actions within complex systems. The first step in STPA usually involves defining the system’s purpose and the hazards or unsafe states that could lead to accidents. Once these are defined, the next steps look at control loops and unsafe control actions that could contribute to the system reaching one of these hazardous states.

In the context of Electricity Demand Side Management (DSM), which aims to manage and optimize energy consumption on the consumer side, hazards could arise from various factors including but not limited to:

Accidents/Hazards:
(a) Power Surges or Spikes:
Unplanned changes in electricity demand can lead to power surges, affecting both the grid and consumer appliances.

(b) System Overload:
Improper management of energy demand could overwhelm the system, potentially causing failure in the grid.

(c) Data Security Incidents:
DSM often relies on real-time data. Unauthorized access could compromise the stability of the system.

(d) Inaccurate Predictive Analysis:
Inaccurate demand forecasting could result in inefficient load distribution.

(e) Uncontrolled Shutdowns:
Sudden, unplanned disconnection from the grid could lead to both minor inconveniences and major disruptions.

(f) Rate Instabilities:
Improper demand-side management could lead to fluctuating electricity rates, impacting consumers.

(g) Resource Depletion:
Poor DSM can lead to inefficient use of electricity, which can be especially problematic in areas where electricity generation relies on non-renewable resources.

(h) Reduced Power Quality:
If DSM systems cannot adapt to variations in energy production (such as from renewable sources), the quality of the delivered power may be compromised.

(i) Economic Loss:
Inefficient demand-side management can have economic consequences, such as increased operational costs.

(j) Environment Hazards:
Poorly managed systems may lead to increased emissions from electricity generation, affecting local and global environments.

(k) Human Errors:
Misconfiguration or lack of understanding of the DSM system by operators or consumers can lead to hazardous conditions.

(l) Communication Failures:
Failures in the communication system can lead to incorrect or delayed commands, causing system instability.

Q:

(a) Sensors: The sensors in the AEB system include cameras, radar, lidar, and other sensors that detect potential collisions. These sensors send signals to the controller indicating the location and speed of nearby objects.

(b) Controller: The controller in the AEB system receives the signals from the sensors and calculates the appropriate response to avoid or mitigate a collision. The controller determines whether to trigger the brakes, how much braking force to apply, and for how long.

(c) Actuator: The actuator in the AEB system is responsible for physically applying the brakes. When the controller determines that braking is necessary, it sends a signal to the actuator, which applies the brakes to slow down or stop the vehicle.

(d) Plant: The plant in the AEB system is the vehicle itself, including the braking system and other components that affect the behavior of the vehicle. The plant responds to the actions of the actuator by slowing down or stopping the vehicle.
