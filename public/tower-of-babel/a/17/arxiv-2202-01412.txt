Abstract
. Tarski’s Circle Squaring Problem from 1925 asks whether it is possible to partition a disk in the plane into finitely many pieces and reassemble them via isometries to yield a partition of a square of the same area. It was finally resolved by Laczkovich in 1990 in the affirmative. Recently, several new proofs have emerged which achieve circle squaring with better structured pieces: namely, pieces which are Lebesgue measurable and have the property of Baire (Grabowski–Máthé–Pikhurko) or even are Borel (Marks–Unger). In this paper, we show that circle squaring is possible with Borel pieces of positive Lebesgue measure whose boundaries have upper Minkowski dimension less than 2 (in particular, each piece is Jordan measurable). We also improve the Borel complexity of the pieces: namely, we show that each piece can be taken to be a Boolean combination of F σ F_{\sigma} sets. This is a consequence of our more general result that applies to any two bounded subsets of ℝ k {\mathbbm{R}}^{k} , k ⩾ 1 k\geqslant 1 , of equal positive measure whose boundaries have upper Minkowski dimension smaller than k k .



1.
Introduction

Tarski’s Circle Squaring Problem
[
39
]
from 1925 asks if a circle (i.e., a circular disk) and a square of the same area in
\mathbbm{R}^{2}

are
equidecomposable
, that is, whether we can partition the circle into finitely many pieces and apply some isometry to each piece to get a partition of the square.
This question was answered affirmatively some 65 years later by Laczkovich who showed in a deep and groundbreaking paper
[
27
]
that, in fact, it is possible to square a circle using translations only.

The Axiom of Choice plays a crucial role in his proof and, consequently, the pieces of his circle squaring could not be guaranteed to have any discernible regularity properties. A notable problem (mentioned by
e.g., Wagon
[
43
]
*Appendix C or Laczkovich
[
27
]
*Section 10) has been to determine whether there exist circle squarings using “better structured” pieces. Recently, Grabowski, Máthé and Pikhurko
[
18
]
proved that the pieces of a circle squaring can simultaneously be Lebesgue measurable and have the property of Baire. Then, Marks and Unger
[
33
]
proved that the pieces can be made Borel. (Let us assume in this paper that the disk and the square are closed and thus Borel sets.)
In fact, Marks and Unger
[
33
]
*Section 7 showed that the pieces of a circle squaring can be chosen to be in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{4}^{0})
, where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{i}^{0}
is the
i
-th additive class of the standard Borel hierarchy (see e.g.,
[
24
]
*Section 11.B) and
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{F}})
denotes the algebra generated by
{\mathcal{F}}
(that is, the family of all Boolean combinations of elements from
{\mathcal{F}}
). For some generalisations and simplifications of the above results, we refer the reader to Cieśla and Sabok
[
5
]
and Bowen, Kun and Sabok
[
3
]
.

Shortly after his circle-squaring paper, Laczkovich
[
29
,
28
]
proved a far-reaching generalisation. Before stating it, let us set up some notation.
Fix
k\geqslant 1
. Let
\lambda
denote the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbbm{R}}^{k}
and let
\partial X
denote the (topological) boundary of
X\subseteq{\mathbbm{R}}^{k}
. Recall that the
upper Minkowski dimension
, sometimes called
box
or
grid dimension
, of
X\subseteq\mathbbm{R}^{k}
is

(1.1)

\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(X):=\limsup_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\frac{\log(N_{\delta}(X))}{\log(\delta^{-1})},

where
N_{\delta}(X)
is the number of boxes from the regular grid in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{k}
of side-length
\delta
that intersect
X
.

Theorem 1.1

(Laczkovich
[
29
,
28
]
)
.

If
k\geqslant 1
and
A,B\subseteq\mathbbm{R}^{k}
are bounded sets such that
\lambda(A)=\lambda(B)>0
,
\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial A)<k
and
\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial B)<k
, then
A
and
B
are equidecomposable by translations.

The subsequent papers
[
18
,
33
]
on circle squaring in fact prove appropriate “constructive” versions of Theorem
1.1
and derive the corresponding circle squaring results as special cases. The aim of this paper is to extend this line of research.
In the context of circle squaring, we prove the following result which decreases the Borel complexity of the pieces by two hierarchy levels and ensures that the boundary of each piece is “small” in a strong sense.

Theorem 1.2
.

In
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}
, a closed disk and square of the same area can be equidecomposed using translations so that every piece has
boundary of upper Minkowski dimension at most
1.987
, belongs to
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}^{0})
(i.e., is a Boolean combination of
F_{\sigma}
sets), and has positive Lebesgue measure.

Recall that a subset
X\subseteq\mathbbm{R}^{k}
is
Jordan measurable
if its indicator function is Riemann integrable. An equivalent definition is that
X
is bounded and
\lambda(\partial X)=0
. It easily follows that any bounded set
X\subseteq\mathbbm{R}^{k}
with
\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial X)<k
is Jordan measurable. Therefore, Theorem
1.2
implies that circle squaring is possible with Jordan measurable pieces, which addresses questions by Laczkovich
[
32
]
and Máthé
[
34
]
*Question 6.2. An advantage of a Jordan measurable circle squaring is that an arbitrarily large portion of it can be described in an error-free way with finitely many bits of information. Namely, for every
\varepsilon>0
, if
n
is large enough, then at most
\varepsilon n^{2}
boxes of the regular
n\times n
grid on the equidecomposed unit square can intersect more than one piece and thus
O(n^{2})
bits are enough to describe our equidecompostion up to a set of measure at most
\varepsilon
. (Furthermore, the dimension estimate of Theorem
1.2
shows that
\varepsilon
, as a function of
n\to\infty
, can be taken to be
n^{-0.013+o(1)}
.)

We will obtain Theorem
1.2
as a special case of the following general result. For a set
A\subseteq{\mathbbm{R}}^{k}
, let
{\mathcal{T}}_{A}:=\{A+\boldsymbol{t}:\boldsymbol{t}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{k}\}
consist of all its translations. For a family
\mathcal{F}
of sets, let
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\mathcal{F})
be the collection of all countable unions of sets in
\mathcal{F}
. Also, recall that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{0}
stands for the collection of open sets in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{k}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

If
k\geqslant 1
and
A,B\subseteq\mathbbm{R}^{k}
are bounded sets such that
\lambda(A)=\lambda(B)>0
,
\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial A)<k
and
\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial B)<k
, then
A
and
B
are equidecomposable by translations so that all the following statements hold simultaneously:

(1.1)

for some
\zeta>0
the topological boundary of each piece has upper Minkowski dimension at most

,

(1.1)

each piece belongs to
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1}^{0}\cup{\mathcal{T}}_{A}\cup{\mathcal{T}}_{B})))
,

(1.1)

if

(1.2)

\lambda\left(\left\{\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbbm{R}^{k}:(A+\boldsymbol{t})\cap B\neq\emptyset\text{ and }\lambda\left((A+\boldsymbol{t})\cap B\right)=0\right\}\right)=0

(that is, the set of vectors
\boldsymbol{t}\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{k}
such that

is non-empty and Lebesgue-null has measure 0), then each piece has positive Lebesgue measure.

In fact, the parameter
\zeta
in Part

can be chosen to depend only on

,
\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial A)
and
\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial B)
, with an explicit expression given in (
7.11
). For circle squaring, the bound in (

) states that any

suffices. Interestingly, by Part
1.1
, each non-null piece of the equidecomposition individually satisfies the hypothesis of the theorem and is therefore equidecomposable to a ball or a cube of the same measure. Moreover, each non-null piece automatically has non-empty interior. (Note that a Jordan measurable subset of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{k}
is null if and only if it has empty interior.)

If the sets
A
and
B
in Theorem
1.3
are Borel, then, by Part
1.1
, all pieces of the equidecomposition can be taken to be Borel with a good control over their Borel complexity (that improves upon the analogous result in
[
33
]
by two hierarchy levels). For example, if each of
A
and
B
is simultaneously a
G_{\delta}
set and an
F_{\sigma}
set (in particular, if each of
A
and
B
is open or closed), then every piece in the equidecomposition is a Boolean combination of
F_{\sigma}
sets, which follows from

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}^{0}\cap\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{0})))=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}^{0}\cap\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{0}))\subseteq\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}^{0}))=\boldsymbol{\mathcal{B}}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}^{0}),

where we used the easy facts that
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}^{0}\cap\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{2}^{0}
is closed under Boolean combinations and
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2}^{0}
is closed under countable unions.

Regarding Part
1.1
of Theorem
1.3
, note that it is impossible to guarantee that all pieces in Theorem
1.3
have positive measure in general. For example, if
A
contains a point
\boldsymbol{x}
such that the distance from
\boldsymbol{x}
to
A\setminus\{\boldsymbol{x}\}
is greater than the diameter of
B
, then every equidecomposition of
A
to
B
must include the set
\{\boldsymbol{x}\}
as a single piece. However, the extra assumption of Part
1.1
applies in many natural cases: for example, it holds for a ball and a cube in any dimension (with arbitrary subsets of their boundaries removed), or for any open sets
A
and
B
.

It seems difficult to weaken the assumptions of Theorem
1.3
in some substantial way. Laczkovich
[
30
]
*Corollary 3.5 showed that, for every
k\geqslant 1
, there is a bounded subset
A\subseteq{\mathbbm{R}}^{k}
which is a countable union of pairwise disjoint convergent cubes but which is not equidecomposable to a single cube by translations. In particular, even in Theorem
1.1
, one cannot replace the assumption that
\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial A),\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial B)<k
by requiring that, for example, the Hausdorff dimension of the boundaries is at most
k-1
. Another family that refutes various extensions of Theorem
1.1
(and thus of Theorem
1.3
) comes from the work of Laczkovich
[
31
]
*Theorem 3 who showed that for every
k\geqslant 2
there are countinuum many Jordan domains, each of volume 1 with everywhere differentiable boundary, so that no two are equidecomposable using any amenable subgroup of isometries. (In particular, this applies to the group
{\mathbbm{R}}^{k}
of translations for any
k
and the full group of isometries of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{2}
, which are amenable.)

On the other hand, if
k\geqslant 3
and one allows all orientation-preserving isometries of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{k}
, then the obvious necessary conditions for a set to be equidecomposable to a cube using Lebesgue (resp. Baire) measurable pieces turn out to be sufficient, see Grabowski, Máthé and Pikhurko
[
19
]
*Corollary 1.10. However, nothing like this is known for Borel and Jordan measurable equidecompositions.

1.1.
Historical Background

Tarski’s Circle Squaring Problem has its roots in the theory of paradoxical decompositions. The most famous result in this area is undoubtedly the theorem of Banach and Tarski
[
1
]
that, if
k\geqslant 3
and
A,B\subseteq\mathbbm{R}^{k}
are bounded and have non-empty interior, then
A
and
B
are equidecomposable. As a special case, one obtains the striking Banach–Tarski Paradox that a solid ball in
\mathbbm{R}^{3}
admits an equidecomposition to two disjoint copies of itself.

The assumption
k\geqslant 3
is necessary in the Banach–Tarski Theorem, as Banach
[
2
]
proved that the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbbm{R}
or
\mathbbm{R}^{2}
can be extended to a finitely additive measure on all subsets which is invariant under isometries.
Thus, in
\mathbbm{R}^{2}
, there cannot exist equidecomposable sets of different Lebesgue measure. The theory of amenable groups, pioneered by von Neumann
[
35
]
, originated as an attempt to obtain a deeper group-theoretic understanding of the change in behaviour between dimensions two and three; the key difference turns out to be that the group of isometries of
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
is amenable for
k\in\{1,2\}
but not for
k\geqslant 3
. Generally, if
A
and
B
are Lebesgue measurable subsets of
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
which are equidecomposable using an amenable subgroup of isometries, then they must have the same measure. Note that the group of translations of
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
is amenable for all
k\geqslant 1
, and so the condition that
\lambda(A)=\lambda(B)
is necessary in Theorem
1.1
in general.

Decades before Laczkovich squared the circle, Dubins, Hirsch and Karush
[
11
]
proved that a disk is not
scissor-congruent
to a square, meaning that there does not exist a squaring of the circle with pieces that are interior-disjoint Jordan domains, even if boundaries are ignored. This is in strong contrast to the case of polygons: the classical Wallace–Bolyai–Gerwein Theorem states that it is possible to dissect a polygon into finitely many pieces using straight lines and, ignoring boundaries, reassemble them to form any polygon of the same area; see e.g.,
[
41
]
*pp. 34–35. Another notable negative result of Gardner
[
16
]
asserts that there is no solution to the Circle Squaring Problem using isometries from a locally discrete subgroup of isometries.

For more background, we refer the reader to the monograph of Tomkowicz and Wagon
[
41
]
, whose Chapter 9 is dedicated to circle squaring.

1.2.
Some Ideas Behind the Proof of Theorem
1.3

Let us give a very high-level outline of the proof of Theorem
1.3
; all formal definitions will appear later in the paper. A more detailed sketch in the special case of Jordan measurable pieces is given in Section
3
.

Like in the previous work, we assume that
A
and
B
are subsets of the torus
{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}:={\mathbbm{R}}^{k}/{\mathbbm{Z}}^{k}
and do all translations modulo
1
. We pick a suitable (somewhat large) integer
d
and vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{x}_{d}\in{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
satisfying certain conditions (that are satisfied with positive probability by random vectors). Let
G_{d}
be the graph on
{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
where we connect
\boldsymbol{u}
to
\boldsymbol{u}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
for each non-zero
(n_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,n_{d})\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}
. Assuming that
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{x}_{d}
do not satisfy any linear dependencies with rational coefficients, each component of
G_{d}
is a
(3^{d}-1)
-regular graph on a copy of
{\mathbbm{Z}}^{d}
.

Our aim is to “construct” a bijection from
A
to
B
such that, for some constant
r
, each element of
A
is moved by the bijection by distance at most
r
within the graph
G_{d}
. Such a bijection naturally gives an equidecomposition between
A
and
B
that uses at most
(2r+1)^{d}
pieces. As was observed by Marks and Unger
[
33
]
, the problem of finding such a bijection can be reduced to finding a uniformly bounded integer-valued flow within the graph
G_{d}
, where the demand is 1 on
A
,
-1
on
B
and 0 elsewhere (see Lemma
2.16
here).

As one of the first steps of their proof, Marks and Unger
[
33
]
constructed a real-valued (i.e. not necessarily integer-valued) flow
f_{\infty}
which satisfies these demands; see Lemma
3.1
. The flow
f_{\infty}
is defined to be a pointwise limit of a sequence of flows
f_{m}
that are locally constructed from
A
and
B
. Since the collection of bounded subsets of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{k}
with boundary of upper Minkowski dimension at most
k-\zeta
is not a
\sigma
-algebra, we should not use the values of
f_{\infty}
if we want to produce pieces with this structure. Instead, we work with the locally defined approximations
f_{m}
.

For flow rounding (that is, making all flow values integer), we construct Jordan measurable subsets
J_{1},J_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
of
{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
such that their union
\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}J_{i}
is co-null in
{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
and
(J_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
is a
toast sequence
(see Definition
2.14
), roughly meaning that each
J_{i}
induces only finite (in fact, uniformly bounded) components in
G_{d}
and the graph boundaries of all components arising this way are well separated from each other. In fact, each set
J_{i}
is a finite union of “strips,” i.e., sets of the form
[a,b)\times[0,1)^{k-1}
; in particular, it is Borel and has a
(k-1)
-dimensional boundary. The idea of using toast sequences to construct satisfying assignments was previously applied to many problems in descriptive combinatorics (with the exact definition of “toast sequence” often being problem-specific). For a systematic treatment of this idea for general actions of
{\mathbbm{Z}}^{d}
, we refer the reader to Grebík and Rozhoň
[
20
]
.

We can view the toast sequence
(J_{1},J_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt)
as a process where, at time
i
, vertices of the set
J_{i}
arrive and our algorithm has to decide the value of the final integer flow
f
on every edge with at least one vertex in this set. We are not allowed to look into the future nor modify any already defined values of the flow
f
. We prove that, if all things are set up carefully, then this is indeed possible to do and, in fact, there are some constants
m_{i}
and
R_{i}
such that the value of
f
on any edge
xy\in E(G_{d})
intersecting
J_{i}
can be computed only from the current picture in the
R_{i}
-neighbourhood of
\{x,y\}
in
G_{d}
and the values of the approximation
f_{m_{i}}
of
f_{\infty}
there.
Here, a key challenge is that, when we round the flow on
J_{i}
, we have only incomplete information (namely, the flow
f_{m_{i}}
which meets the demands only within some small error). The idea that allows us to overcome this difficulty is that, if the cumulative error of
f_{m_{i}}
on each component of
J_{i}
is small, then whenever our algorithm encounters some inconsistency, it can round it to the nearest integer and produce values that are in fact perfectly compatible with all past and future choices of the algorithm.

The proof coming from the above arguments, with a careful choice of how the size of the components of
J_{i}
can grow with
i
, produces a partial equidecomposition between
A
and
B
so that the topological boundaries of the pieces as well as the unmatched part of
A
and of
B
have upper Minkowski dimension less than
k
. Thus if we can extend this equidecomposition to all of
A
and
B
(even by using the Axiom of Choice), then we can achieve Part
1.1
of Theorem
1.3
.

However, these ideas do not seem to be enough to yield pieces which satisfy Parts
1.1
and
1.1
of the theorem simultaneously. If
A
and
B
are Borel, then all pieces in the partial equidecomposition are Borel, but we did not see a way to match the remaining parts of
A
and
B
in a Borel way by using the results of Marks and Unger
[
33
]
as a “black box”.
So, in order to achieve Part
1.1
of Theorem
1.3
, we essentially run the proof from
[
33
]
on the complement of
\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}J_{i}
, making sure that when we define the sets
J_{i}
in the first place, we leave enough remaining “wiggle” space. Running the Borel proof on the remaining set
is probably the most technical part of this paper. This extra work is well worth the effort though since modifying both the construction and analysis of Marks and Unger
[
33
]
also allows us to reduce the Borel complexity of the obtained pieces by two hierarchy levels.

For Part
1.1
, we choose
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{x}_{d}\in{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}

so that, additionally, for every vector
\boldsymbol{t}
which is an integer linear combination of them, the intersection
(A+\boldsymbol{t})\cap B
is null only if it is empty.
Recall that our equidecomposition translates each

\boldsymbol{u}\in A
by a vector of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
where
n_{1},n_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,n_{d}
are integers between
-r
and
r
, for some
r
. Denote by
\boldsymbol{t}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{t}_{N}
the vectors
\boldsymbol{t}
of this form for which
(A+\boldsymbol{t})\cap B
is non-empty (and thus non-null).
To achieve Part
1.1
, for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant N
, we pre-select a small non-null subset
A_{i}
of
(B-\boldsymbol{t}_{i})\cap A
to be part of the piece of the equidecomposition that is translated according to
\boldsymbol{t}_{i}
. This is done in such a way that the sets
A_{1}+\boldsymbol{t}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,A_{N}+\boldsymbol{t}_{N}
are pairwise disjoint and our proof of Parts
1.1
and
1.1
still applies to the remaining sets
A\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}A_{i}
and
B\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}(A_{i}+\boldsymbol{t}_{i})
, using the same translation vectors
\boldsymbol{t}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{t}_{N}
. Thus, all pieces are non-null by construction.

2.
Preliminaries

We denote by
{\mathbbm{R}}
and
{\mathbbm{Z}}
the sets of reals and integers, respectively. For
x\in{\mathbbm{R}}
, we define its
nearest integer

[x]
to be equal to
\lfloor x\rfloor
if
x-\lfloor x\rfloor<1/2
and
\lceil x\rceil
otherwise. The set of all values assumed by a function
f
is denoted by
\mathrm{range}(f)
. When we write e.g.,
(a_{i},b_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
, we mean the sequence
(a_{1},b_{1},a_{2},b_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt)
. The indicator function of a set

is denoted by
\mathbbm{1}_{X}
.

2.1.
Discrepancy Bounds for the Torus

The
k
-torus

\mathbbm{T}^{k}
is the quotient group of
(\mathbbm{R}^{k},+)
modulo
\mathbbm{Z}^{k}
.
When we write the sum of
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
, we take it modulo 1 and thus
\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{v}
is an element of
{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
. In particular, the translation of
Y\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{k}
by a vector
\boldsymbol{t}\in{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
,

Y+\boldsymbol{t}:=\left\{\boldsymbol{y}+\boldsymbol{t}:\boldsymbol{y}\in Y\right\}\subseteq{\mathbbm{T}}^{k},

is done modulo 1. Similarly, scalar multiples of vectors in
{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
are also taken modulo 1.

For notational convenience, we may occasionally identify
\mathbb{T}^{k}
with
[0,1)^{k}
. While this identification is not a topological homeomorphism, it does preserve the set families that are of interest to us, namely, sets that are Borel, are Jordan measurable, have boundary of upper Minkowski dimension at most
k-\zeta
(when
\zeta<1
), etc.
The restriction of the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
to the torus will be denoted by the same symbol
\lambda
. We have
\lambda({\mathbbm{T}}^{k})=1
.

Given a finite set
F\subseteq\mathbbm{T}^{k}
and a Lebesgue measurable set
X\subseteq\mathbbm{T}^{k}
, the
discrepancy of
F
relative to
X
is defined to be

(2.1)

D(F,X):=\big||F\cap X|-|F|\cdot\lambda(X)\big|.

In other words,
D(F,X)
is the deviation between
|F\cap X|
and the expected size of this intersection if
F
were a uniformly random subset of
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
of cardinality
|F|
.

A key tool used in this paper, as well as in
[
18
,
33
]
, is the following discrepancy lemma of Laczkovich
[
28
]
, whose proof sketch can be found in
Appendix
A
.

Lemma 2.1

(Laczkovich
[
28
]
*Proof of Theorem 3; see also
[
18
]
*Lemma 6)
.

Let
X
be a measurable subset of
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
such that
\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial X)<k
, let
d
be a positive integer such that

d>\frac{k}{k-\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial X)}

and let
\varepsilon\in\mathbbm{R}
be such that

0<\varepsilon<\frac{d(k-\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial X))-k}{k}.

If
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{x}_{d}
are chosen uniformly at random from
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
, independently of one another, then, with probability
1
, there exists
c>0
such that, for every
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}
and integer
n\geqslant 0
, we have

D\left(N_{n}^{+}[\boldsymbol{u}],X\right)\leqslant c\cdot(n+1)^{d-1-\varepsilon},

where we define

(2.2)

N_{n}^{+}[\boldsymbol{u}]:=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}:\vec{n}\in\{0,\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,n\}^{d}\right\},

calling it a
discrete
(n+1)
-cube
(or just a
discrete cube
).

Essentially, this lemma says that, if a set
X\subseteq\mathbbm{T}^{k}
has “small” topological boundary, then, for
d
sufficiently large, the discrepancy of any large discrete cube given by typical vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{x}_{d}\in\mathbb{T}^{k}
with respect to
X
is significantly smaller than the number of points on the combinatorial boundary of the cube.

2.2.
Graph-Theoretic Definitions

A (simple undirected)
graph
is a pair
G=(V,E)
where the elements of
V
are called
vertices
and
E
is a collection of unordered pairs
\{u,v\}
of vertices called
edges
. For brevity, an edge
\{u,v\}\in E
is written
uv
or, equivalently,
vu
. The vertex set and edge set of a graph
G
are denoted by
V(G)
and
E(G)
, respectively.

A vertex
u
is said to be
adjacent to
(or
a neighbour of
) a vertex
v
if
uv\in E(G)
. Given a set
S\subseteq V(G)
, the
subgraph of
G
induced by
S
, denoted
G\upharpoonright S
, is the graph with vertex set
S
and edge set
\{uv\in E(G):u,v\in S\}
.

Given a graph
G
and
u,v\in V(G)
, we let
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v)
denote the
graph distance from
u
to
v
in
G
, i.e., the fewest number of edges in a path from
u
to
v
in
G
. If no such path exists, then
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v):=\infty
. Also, note that
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,u)=0
for every vertex
u
. For sets
S,T\subseteq V(G)
, we let

\operatorname{dist}_{G}(S,T):=\min\{\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v):u\in S\text{ and }v\in T\}

and, for
w\in V(G)
, we write
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(w,T)
to mean
\operatorname{dist}_{G}(\{w\},T)
.
Given
u\in V(G)
, the
connected component of
G
containing
u
is the set

[u]_{G}:=\{v\in V(G):\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v)<\infty\}.

We say that
G
is
connected
if
[u]_{G}=V(G)
for every (equivalently, some) vertex
u\in V(G)
.

Given a graph
G
and
u\in V(G)
, let

N_{G}[u]:=\{v\in V(G):\operatorname{dist}_{G}(u,v)\leqslant 1\}

denote the
(closed) neighbourhood of
u
in
G
. Also, for a set
S\subseteq V(G)
, let
N_{G}[S]:=\bigcup_{u\in S}N_{G}[u]
be the
(closed) neighbourhood of
S
in
G
. The
degree
of a vertex
u\in V(G)
is defined to be

\operatorname{deg}_{G}(u):=\left|N_{G}[u]\setminus\{u\}\right|.

We say that
G
is
locally finite
if
\operatorname{deg}_{G}(u)
is finite for every
u\in V(G)
.
For
d\in\mathbbm{N}
, we say that
G
is
d
-regular
if
\operatorname{deg}_{G}(u)=d
for all
u\in V(G)
.

The
edge boundary
of
S\subseteq V(G)
in
G
is the set

(2.3)

\partial_{E}S:=\{uv\in E(G):u\in S\text{ and }v\in V(G)\setminus S\}.

2.3.
Network Flows

A
flow
in a graph
G
is a function
f:V(G)\times V(G)\to\mathbbm{R}
such that

f(u,v)=-f(v,u)\text{ for all }u,v\in V(G),\text{ and}

f(u,v)=0\text{ if }uv\notin E(G).

The quantity
f(u,v)
is called the
flow from
u
to
v
under
f
. Given a finite set
S\subseteq V(G)
, the
flow out of
S
under
f
is defined to be

f^{\operatorname{out}}(S):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in S\\
v\in V(G)\setminus S\end{subarray}}f(u,v).

Note that, in this paper, we will deal with locally finite graphs only, and so the flow out of a finite set will always be well-defined. For a vertex
u\in V(G)
, the
flow out of
u
under
f
is
f^{\operatorname{out}}(u):=f^{\operatorname{out}}(\{u\})
. The following is an easy consequence of the definition of a flow.

Observation 2.2
.

Given a locally finite graph
G
, a flow
f
in
G
and a finite set
S\subseteq V(G)
, it holds that

f^{\operatorname{out}}(S)=\sum_{u\in S}f^{\operatorname{out}}(u)
.

Given a function
\chi:V(G)\to\mathbbm{R}
, which we call a
demand function
, a
\chi
-flow in
G
is a flow
f
such that
f^{\operatorname{out}}(u)=\chi(u)
for every
u\in V(G)
. For
S,T\subseteq V(G)
, a
flow from
S
to
T
in
G
is a
\left(\mathbbm{1}_{S}-\mathbbm{1}_{T}\right)
-flow in
G
.

It is well-known that, if
\chi
is an integer-valued demand function for a finite graph
G
, then a real-valued
\chi
-flow in
G
can be converted into an integer-valued
\chi
-flow in
G
by changing the flow along each edge by less than
1
. This is known as the Integral Flow Theorem. It seems to have been first observed by Dantzig and Fulkerson
[
7
]
using ideas of Dantzig
[
8
]
; see
[
38
]
*Corollary 10.3a and the discussion in
[
38
]
*p. 64. The following version can be derived from the finite case via a standard compactness (i.e., Axiom of Choice) argument; for a proof see e.g.,
[
33
]
*Corollary 5.2.

Theorem 2.3

(Integral Flow Theorem)
.

Let
G
be a locally finite graph and let
\chi:V(G)\to\mathbbm{Z}
be an integer-valued demand function. If there exists a
\chi
-flow
g
in
G
, then there exists an integer-valued
\chi
-flow
f
in
G
with
|f(u,v)-g(u,v)|<1
for all
u,v\in V(G)
.

2.4.
The Setting

At this point, we are ready to make some key definitions and assumptions that will apply throughout the rest of the paper.

Let
k\geqslant 1
be integer, and let
A
and
B
be sets that satisfy the assumptions of Theorem
1.3
. Thus
A,B\subseteq\mathbbm{R}^{k}
are bounded sets that have the same positive Lebesgue measure (i.e.,
\lambda(A)=\lambda(B)>0
) and boundary of the upper Minkowski dimension less than
k
(i.e.,
\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial A)<k
and
\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial B)<k
).

By scaling
A
and
B
by the same factor, we
can assume that each of them has diameter less than
1/2
in the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm. Furthermore, by translating them, we can assume that
A
and
B
are disjoint subsets of
[0,1)^{k}
. If
A
and
B
are equidecomposable using translations inside
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
, then they are also equidecomposable using translations in
\mathbbm{R}^{k}
with exactly the same pieces (by the diameter assumption). From now on, we always assume that we are working in the setting of the
k
-torus.

In order to satisfy Lemma
2.1
, and to optimise the bound on the upper Minkowski dimension of the boundaries of the final pieces, we make the following assignments. First, fix
\epsilon
so that

(2.4)

0<\epsilon<k-\max\left\{\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial A),\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial B)\right\},

where we should choose
\epsilon
close to the upper bound for optimality. Then
we set

(2.5)

d:=\left\lfloor k/\epsilon\right\rfloor+1,

that is,
d
is the smallest integer greater than
k/\epsilon
. We also define

(2.6)

\varepsilon:=(d\epsilon-k)/k.

Note that, if the boundaries of
A
and
B
have upper Minkowski dimension
k-1
, as is the case for most simple “geometric” sets like balls and cubes, then we can take
d=k+1
and let
\epsilon
and
\varepsilon
be close to
1
and
1/k
, respectively.

For each integer
i\geqslant 0
, define

(2.7)

r_{i}^{\prime}:=100^{2^{i+1}-1}

and, for each integer
i\geqslant 1
, define

(2.8)

r_{i}:=100^{2^{i+1}-2}.

Next, we recursively define
q_{0}^{\prime}<q_{1}<q_{1}^{\prime}<q_{2}<\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
and
t_{1}<t_{1}^{\prime}<t_{2}<t_{2}^{\prime}<\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
by setting
q_{0}^{\prime}:=0
and, for each
i\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle t_{i}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle 2r_{i}+4q^{\prime}_{i-1}+4,

\displaystyle q_{i}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle t_{i}+2q^{\prime}_{i-1}+4,

\displaystyle t^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lfloor 4r^{\prime}_{i}/5\rfloor+2q_{i},

(2.9)

\displaystyle q^{\prime}_{i}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle t^{\prime}_{i}+2q_{i}+4.

We fix, for the rest of the paper, some vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{x}_{d}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}
that satisfy all of the following properties:

(2.10)

the conclusion of Lemma
2.1
, with respect to the above
d
and
\varepsilon
, holds for some
c>0
(which is also fixed
throughout the paper) for both
X:=A
and
X:=B
;

(2.11)

the projections of
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{x}_{d}
on the first coordinate are linearly independent over the rationals;

(2.12)

if the extra assumption of Part
1.1
of Theorem
1.3
(that is, (
1.2
)) holds, then no integer combination of
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{x}_{d}
belongs to the “bad” set

\{\boldsymbol{t}\in{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}:(A+\boldsymbol{t})\cap B\mbox{ is non-empty and $\lambda$-null}\};

(2.13)

the conclusion of Lemma
7.4

(to be stated in Section
7.1
) holds.

This is possible since, if
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{x}_{d}
are chosen uniformly at random from

independently of one another, then each of Properties (
2.10
)–(

, while Lemma

is satisfied with positive probability. For example, Property (

since, for each non-zero
(n_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,n_{d})\in{\mathbbm{Z}}^{d}
, the vector
\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is a uniform element of
{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
while the “bad” set has measure 0 by (

);
also, the zero vector is “good”
by our assumption that
A\cap B=\emptyset
.

Remark 2.4
.

If the reader is interested only in a version of Theorem
1.3
where the conclusion of Part
1.1
is weakened to requiring that the pieces are just Jordan measurable, then we could have chosen an arbitrary sufficiently fast growing sequence
r_{0}^{\prime}\ll r_{1}\ll r_{1}^{\prime}\ll r_{2}\ll\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
and ignored Property (

), which would not be needed. This is why the (somewhat technical) statement of Lemma

is postponed to Section

, where the dimension of the boundaries of the obtained pieces is analysed.

Next, we repeat the definition of the graph
G_{d}
from Subsection
1.2
. We denote elements of
\mathbbm{Z}^{d}
as vectors accented by an arrow, e.g.,
\vec{n}
, to distinguish them from vectors in
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
which are typeset in boldface. For
\vec{n}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{d}
and
1\leqslant i\leqslant d
, let
n_{i}
denote the
i
-th coordinate of
\vec{n}
. Consider the action
a:\mathbbm{Z}^{d}{\curvearrowright}\mathbbm{T}^{k}
defined by

\vec{n}\cdot_{a}\boldsymbol{u}:=\boldsymbol{u}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\quad\mbox{for $\vec{n}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{d}$ and $\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}$.}

This action is continuous and preserves the measure
\lambda
.

We let
G_{d}
denote the
(3^{d}-1)
-regular Schreier graph associated to the action
a
with respect to the symmetric set
\{\vec{n}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{d}:\|\vec{n}\|_{\infty}=1\}
. Thus the vertex set of
G_{d}
is
\mathbbm{T}^{k}

and the neighbours of
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}
are exactly vectors
\boldsymbol{u}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}

for non-zero
\vec{n}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}
.
The edge set of
G_{d}
, when viewed as a symmetric binary relation on the (standard Borel) space
\mathbb{T}^{k}
, is a closed and thus Borel set. This means that
G_{d}
is a
Borel graph
, a fundamental object of study in
descriptive graph combinatorics
(see e.g., the survey of this field by Kechris and Marks
[
21
]
).
Note that the action
a
is free
by our choice of the vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
, namely by (
2.11
). Therefore, the subgraph of
G_{d}
induced by any component of
G_{d}
is nothing more than a copy of
\mathbbm{Z}^{d}
in which two elements are adjacent if they are at
\ell_{\infty}
-distance
1
.

We pause to remind the reader of the role of the graph
G_{d}
in the equidecomposition.

Remark 2.5
.

The translation vectors used in the constructed equidecomposition will be of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
where
\vec{n}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{d}
and
\|\vec{n}\|_{\infty}
is bounded by a large constant depending only on
A
and
B
(which we do not attempt to estimate). Assuming that the translation vectors will have this form, we see that constructing the equidecomposition becomes equivalent to finding a consistent way of “matching” each element of
A
with an element of
B
at bounded distance from it in
G_{d}
. Given such a matching, each piece of the partition is indexed by a vector
\vec{n}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{d}
of bounded
\ell_{\infty}
-norm, where the piece corresponding to
\vec{n}
is the set of all
\boldsymbol{u}\in A
such that
\boldsymbol{u}
is matched to
\boldsymbol{u}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in B
.

2.5.
Some Auxiliary Structures in
G_{d}

Here we present some useful building blocks and definitions.
Note that, once
d
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{x}_{d}
are fixed,
the definitions and constructions in this section do not depend on the sets
A
and
B
.

At certain points during the construction, we are forced to make arbitrary “tie-breaking” choices in a consistent manner. In doing so, it is useful to endow
{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
with a partial order in which each component of
G_{d}
is totally ordered. We will use the
lexicographic ordering
where, for distinct
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}
, we write
\boldsymbol{u}\prec_{\operatorname{lex}}\boldsymbol{v}
to mean
[\boldsymbol{u}]_{G_{d}}=[\boldsymbol{v}]_{G_{d}}
and the first non-zero entry of
\vec{n}
is positive, where
\vec{n}
is the (unique) element of
{\mathbbm{Z}}^{d}
with

\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{u}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}.

This naturally extends to a partial order on
\left(\mathbbm{T}^{k}\right)^{t}
for any
t\geqslant 1
where
(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{u}_{t})\prec_{\operatorname{lex}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{v}_{t})

1\leqslant i\leqslant t
such that
\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\prec_{\operatorname{lex}}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{j}=\boldsymbol{v}_{j}
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant i-1
.

Given
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}
, we write
N_{G_{d}}[\boldsymbol{u}]
simply as

. For

, define the
n

of

to be

N_{n}[\boldsymbol{u}]:=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}:\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\leqslant n\right\}.

Note that
N_{n}[\boldsymbol{u}]=N_{2n}^{+}[\boldsymbol{v}]
, where
\boldsymbol{v}:=\boldsymbol{u}-n(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}+\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt+\boldsymbol{x}_{d})
and

is defined as in (
2.2
).
Thus,
N_{n}[\boldsymbol{u}]
(resp.

) is the set of vertices which can be reached from
\boldsymbol{u}
by taking at most
n
steps in any (resp. “completely non-negative”) directions in
G_{d}
.
Given a set
S\subseteq\mathbbm{T}^{k}
and an integer

, let

N_{n}[S]:=\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{u}\in S}N_{n}[\boldsymbol{u}]\quad\mbox{and}\quad N_{n}^{+}[S]:=\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{u}\in S}N_{n}^{+}[\boldsymbol{u}].

We say that a set

on (or is an

-local function

. This is equivalent to

being some Boolean combination of the sets of form

for

and
\vec{n}\in{\mathbbm{Z}}^{d}
with

. When the specific value of

locally depends
on (or is a

of)

. Here is a trivial but very useful observation.

Observation 2.6
.

If
Y
locally depends on some finite sequence of sets from an algebra

on
\mathbb{T}^{k}
which is

under the action

Note that the families of subsets of

that are Jordan measurable, or have boundary of upper Minkowski dimension at most some given constant, are both examples of

If

, then for

Observation 2.7
.

Given a flow
f
in
G_{d}
and
\vec{\gamma}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{d}
with
\|\vec{\gamma}\|_{\infty}=1
, the sets

\mathbbm{T}^{k}
. If the flow

for all possible choices of

and

. Thus we will say that a flow

is an

-local function
of

to mean that each set

is an

-local function of

. In the other direction,

Definition 2.8
.

Define a
strip
to be a subset of
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
of the form
[a,b)\times[0,1)^{k-1}
for some

. The

.

Note that any translation of a strip by a vector can be written as a union of at most two strips and that any Boolean combination of strips can be written as a union of finitely many disjoint strips. For these reasons, strips are particularly convenient to work with.

Definition 2.9
.

A set

is said to be

)
if
\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})>r

\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in X
and
maximally

if it is maximal under set inclusion with respect to this property.

Of course, the above properties are not affected when we replace

Observation 2.10

For any

We will also need the following lemma (whose main proof idea goes back to Kechris, Solecki and Todorcevic

Lemma 2.11

For

be subsets of
\mathbbm{T}^{k}

-discrete in

and
\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}C_{i}=\mathbbm{T}^{k}
. Then there exists a maximally
r
-discrete set

r(m-1)
-local function of
C_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,C_{m}

Proof.

Let
C_{1}^{\prime}:=C_{1}
and, for

C_{i}^{\prime}:=C_{i}\setminus N_{r}\left[\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}C_{j}^{\prime}\right].

Let

. By construction, and because the sets

are
r
-discrete in

, the set

is also
r
-discrete in
G_{d}
. Since the sets

cover

, it follows that

is maximally

-discrete in

.

Since

-local function of

is a Boolean combination of

is an

-local function

Definition 2.13

The following definition describes the types of structures that we will use to round a sequence of real-valued flows to an integer-valued one. We use the word “toast” which seems now to be the standard term for structures of this type.

Definition 2.14
.

We say that a sequence

(2.16)

any two distinct elements of

Let us make a brief contextual remark. An idea which is ubiquitous in descriptive graph combinatorics is the notion of “hyperfiniteness”. Namely, a Borel graph

can be written as an increasing union of edge sets of Borel graphs with finite components; see, e.g.,
[

]
*Section 7.2 or

*Section II.6.
In this context, a Borel toast sequence

(or, more precisely, the corresponding sequence of the induced edge sets)
can be viewed as a specific type of a hyperfiniteness certificate for the graph

.

Note that that our definition of a toast sequence allows

2.6.
The Setting (Continuation)

Here we define two sequences of sets, with these definitions applying throughout the paper. Namely, for each

and

For Part

\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(\partial Y_{i},\partial Y_{j})=\infty,\quad\mbox{for all $i\neq j$},

2.7.
Equidecompositions from Integer-Valued Flows

We will need the following lemma, whose main idea is inspired by the proof sketch in
[
33
]
*Remark 6.2. In the context of Theorem
1.3
, this lemma shows that it is enough to find a bounded integer-valued flow
f
from
A
to
B
with the desired regularity properties.

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
f
be a bounded integer-valued flow from
A
to
B
in
G_{d}
. Then there is an integer
R
and an equidecomposition
between
A
and
B
such that each piece is an
R
-local function of
f
,
A
,
B
and finitely many strips. Moreover, if (
1.2
) (that is, the extra assumption of Part
1.1
of Theorem
1.3
) holds, then we can additionally require that each piece contains a Lebesgue measurable subset of positive measure.

Proof.

Using Corollary
2.12
, let
W_{r}
, for each integer
r\geqslant 1
, be a maximally
r
-discrete subset which is a finite union of strips.
For each
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}
, let
\eta_{r}(\boldsymbol{v})
be the vertex
\boldsymbol{u}\in W_{r}
such that
\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u})
is minimised and, among all vertices of
W_{r}
at minimum distance from
\boldsymbol{v}
, the vertex
\boldsymbol{u}
comes earliest under
\prec_{\operatorname{lex}}
. For each
r\geqslant 1
and
\boldsymbol{u}\in W_{r}
, let

V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}:\eta_{r}(\boldsymbol{v})=\boldsymbol{u}\}.

The sets
V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})
for
\boldsymbol{u}\in W_{r}
clearly partition
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
. They can be thought of as “Voronoi cells” generated by
W_{r}
with respect to the graph distance in
G_{d}
, where ties are broken using
\prec_{\operatorname{lex}}
. Since
W_{r}
is a maximal
r
-discrete set, every element of
V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})
is at distance at most
r
from
\boldsymbol{u}\in W_{r}
and thus the diameter of every Voronoi cell is at most
2r
.

Since
W_{r}
is
r
-discrete, we have

N_{\lfloor r/2\rfloor}[\boldsymbol{u}]\subseteq V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})

for every
\boldsymbol{u}\in W_{r}
. Combining this with Lemma
2.1
, we see that

\min\left\{\,|V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})\cap A|,|V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})\cap B|\,\right\}=\Omega(r^{d})

as
r\to\infty
. It is not hard to argue (see, e.g., the proof of Lemma
5.13
) that
\left|\partial_{E}V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})\right|=O(r^{d-1})
as
r\to\infty
where the implicit constant depends on
d
only. (Recall that
\partial_{E}
denotes the edge boundary of a set, as defined in (
2.3
).) Thus, if
r
is sufficiently large with respect to
\|f\|_{\infty}
, then

(2.19)

\min\left\{\,|V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})\cap A|,|V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})\cap B|\,\right\}\geqslant\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{w}\in\partial_{E}V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})}|f(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})|

for every
\boldsymbol{u}\in W_{r}
. We fix
r
large enough so that (
2.19
) holds for every
\boldsymbol{u}\in W_{r}
; to achieve the “moreover” part of the lemma, we will need the slightly stronger inequality (
2.20
) stated later.

For each pair
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in W_{r}
, define

F(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{v}\in V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})\\
\boldsymbol{w}\in V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})\end{subarray}}f(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}),

that is,
F(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})
is the total flow sent by
f
from the Voronoi cell of
\boldsymbol{u}
to that of
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}
.
Given
\boldsymbol{u}\in W_{r}
, there are finitely many
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in W_{r}
for which
F(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})\neq 0
. For every such
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}
, one by one in order prescribed by
\prec_{\operatorname{lex}}
, we define
A(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})
to be the set of those
\max\left\{0,F(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})\right\}
elements of
V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})\cap A
that have not already been assigned to
A(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime\prime})
for some
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime\prime}\prec_{\operatorname{lex}}\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}
and, subject to that, are minimal under
\prec_{\operatorname{lex}}
. Similarly, we define
B(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})
to be the set of those
\max\left\{0,-F(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})\right\}

\prec_{\operatorname{lex}}
-minimal elements of
V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})\cap B
that have not already been assigned to
B(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime\prime})
for some
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime\prime}\prec_{\operatorname{lex}}\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}
. Note that, by (
2.19
), this construction is well defined. Finally, for each
\boldsymbol{u}\in W_{r}
, define
A(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})
to be the set of those vertices in
V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})\cap A
that have not been assigned to
A(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})
for any
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in W_{r}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{u}\}
and define
B(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u})
similarly. We have by construction that

|A(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})|=|B(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{u})|,\quad\mbox{for all distinct $\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in W_{r}$,}

and this holds also for
\boldsymbol{u}=\boldsymbol{u^{\prime}}
since
f
is a flow from
A
to
B
.
The final equidecomposition assigns the vertices of
A(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})
to those of
B(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{u})
in order prescribed by
\prec_{\operatorname{lex}}
for all
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in W_{r}
; see Figure
1
for an illustration.

Figure 1.
An illustration of the construction of the equidecomposition from an integer-valued flow
f
in Lemma
2.16
in the simplified setting
d=2
. White nodes are elements of a maximally 19-discrete set and the boundaries of the induced Voronoi cells are in bold. Elements of
A
and

to the neighbouring cells, the two lexicographically latest elements of

and

in the central cell are mapped to one another.

Since each cell has diameter at most
2r
, this yields an equidecomposition by translations in which the translation vectors are of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
with
\|\vec{n}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 4r+1
. Thus the total number of pieces is finite. Furthermore, the vector by which we translate any
\boldsymbol{v}\in V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})\cap A
depends only on the situation inside
V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})
, its adjacent Voronoi cells and their adjacent Voronoi cells, and the values of
f
at boundary edges. All these cells are contained entirely inside
N_{6r+2}[\boldsymbol{v}]
, and so the statement holds with
R:=6r+2
.

The “moreover” part of the lemma is only needed for Part
1.1
of Theorem
1.3
. Its proof may be skipped by the reader interested only in the other parts of Theorem
1.3
. In brief, the new idea is to arrange that the piece of the equidecomposition corresponding to each translation vector
\boldsymbol{t}\in{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
that is used in the equidecomposition is pre-assigned a small non-null subset
A_{\boldsymbol{t}}\subseteq A
. Of course, this is possible only if
(A+\boldsymbol{t})\cap B
has positive measure, so we have to exclude all vectors violating this.

Let us provide the details. For each integer
r\geqslant 1
, let
W_{r}
,
\eta_{r}
and
V_{r}
be as in the proof of the first part. Now, fix an integer
r
such that, for every
\boldsymbol{u}\in W_{r}
, it holds that

(2.20)

\min\left\{\,\left|V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})\cap A\right|,\left|V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})\cap B\right|\,\right\}\geqslant\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{w}\in\partial_{E}V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})}\left(|f(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})|+1\right),

that is, we require a slightly stronger bound than the one in (
2.19
).

Let
T
be the set of all vectors of the form
\boldsymbol{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
such that
\vec{n}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{d}
with
\|\vec{n}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 10r
and
(A+\boldsymbol{t})\cap B\neq\emptyset
. Since (
1.2
) holds, we have by (
2.12
) that, for every
\boldsymbol{t}\in T
, the set
(A+\boldsymbol{t})\cap B
has positive measure. (Recall that the sets
A
and
B
satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
1.3
are necessarily Lebesgue measurable.)

Claim 2.16.1
.

There exists a sequence
(A_{\boldsymbol{t}})_{\boldsymbol{t}\in T}
of pairwise disjoint measurable non-null subsets of
A
such that the union
\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in T}A_{\boldsymbol{t}}
is
(100r)
-discrete in
G_{d}
and, for all
\boldsymbol{t}\in T
,
A_{\boldsymbol{t}}+\boldsymbol{t}\subseteq B
.

Proof.

We present a simple greedy argument. By Observation
2.10
, we can choose
\gamma
sufficiently small so that every strip of width at most
\gamma
is
(100r)
-discrete. Assume further that
\gamma
is chosen small enough so that

\gamma(200r+1)^{d}|T|<\lambda\left((A+\boldsymbol{t})\cap B\right)=\lambda\left(A\cap(B-\boldsymbol{t})\right)

for every
\boldsymbol{t}\in T
. Label the elements of
T
by
\boldsymbol{t}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{t}_{|T|}
in an arbitrary fashion.

Start by taking
S_{\boldsymbol{t}_{1}}
to be a strip of width
\gamma
such that the intersection
A_{\boldsymbol{t}_{1}}:=A\cap(B-\boldsymbol{t}_{1})\cap S_{\boldsymbol{t}_{1}}
is non-null. Clearly, the measure of
A_{\boldsymbol{t}_{1}}
is at most the measure of
S_{\boldsymbol{t}_{1}}
, which is
\gamma
.

Now, let
2\leqslant i\leqslant|T|
and assume that each of the sets
A_{\boldsymbol{t}_{1}},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,A_{\boldsymbol{t}_{i-1}}
has measure at most
\gamma
. Then, by our choice of
\gamma
, the set

(2.21)

\left(A\cap(B-\boldsymbol{t}_{i})\right)\setminus\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}N_{100r}\left[A_{\boldsymbol{t}_{j}}\right]\right)

has positive measure. Take any strip
S_{\boldsymbol{t}_{i}}
of width
\gamma
that has non-null intersection with the above set and let
A_{\boldsymbol{t}_{i}}
be this intersection.

The collection
\{A_{\boldsymbol{t}_{i}}:1\leqslant i\leqslant|T|\}
has all of the desired properties, simply by construction, finishing the proof of the claim.

Let
(A_{\boldsymbol{t}})_{\boldsymbol{t}\in T}
be the sequence returned by the claim. Observe that, for any distinct
\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}\in T
, the sets
A_{\boldsymbol{t}}+\boldsymbol{t}
and
A_{\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}}+\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}
are disjoint; otherwise, by definition of
T
, the sets
A_{\boldsymbol{t}}
and
A_{\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}}
would be at distance at most
20r
in
G_{d}
, contradicting the fact that
\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in T}A_{\boldsymbol{t}}
is
(100r)
-discrete in
G_{d}
. Thus, the sets
A_{\boldsymbol{t}}
for
\boldsymbol{t}\in T
form an equidecomposition of the subset

A^{*}:=\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in T}A_{\boldsymbol{t}}

of
A
to the subset

B^{*}:=\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in T}\left(A_{\boldsymbol{t}}+\boldsymbol{t}\right)

of
B
using translations in
T
.

Now, for each
\boldsymbol{t}\in T
and each vertex
\boldsymbol{v}\in A_{\boldsymbol{t}}
, take a shortest path from
\boldsymbol{v}
to
\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{t}
in
G_{d}
, chosen to be
\prec_{\operatorname{lex}}
-minimal among all such paths, and let
f_{\boldsymbol{v}}
be the (unique)
\{0,\pm 1\}
-valued flow in
G_{d}
from
\{\boldsymbol{v}\}
to
\{\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{t}\}
supported on the pairs which form edges of this path. Since the set
\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in T}A_{\boldsymbol{t}}
is
(100r)
-discrete, any two such paths are edge disjoint and so the flow

f^{*}:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{t}\in T}A_{\boldsymbol{t}}}f_{\boldsymbol{v}}

satisfies
\|f^{*}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1
. So, letting
f^{\prime}:=f-f^{*}
, we see that (
2.20
) implies that, for every
\boldsymbol{u}\in W_{r}
,

\min\left\{\,|V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})\cap A|,|V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})\cap B|\,\right\}\geqslant\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\boldsymbol{w}\in\partial_{E}V_{r}(\boldsymbol{u})}|f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w})|.

Clearly,
f^{\prime}
is a flow from
A\setminus A^{*}
to
B\setminus B^{*}
in
G_{d}
. By the proof of the first part of the lemma with
f^{\prime}
in the place of
f
, we see that there is an equidecomposition from
A\setminus A^{*}
to
B\setminus B^{*}
in which each
\boldsymbol{v}\in A\setminus A^{*}
is associated to a point in
B\setminus B^{*}
which is either in the same Voronoi cell as
\boldsymbol{v}
or one which neighbours it. In particular, all of the translation vectors used in the equidecomposition are contained in
T
. Moreover, by making the
\prec_{\operatorname{lex}}
-smallest choices inside each Voronoi cell, we can assume
that each piece is an
O(r)
-local function of
W_{r},A,B,A_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,A_{|T|}
, relying here on the particular way the sets
A_{t}
were defined at (
2.21
).

Taking, for each
\boldsymbol{t}\in T
, the union of
A_{\boldsymbol{t}}
and the (possibly empty) piece of the equidecomposition from
A\setminus A^{*}
to
B\setminus B^{*}
corresponding to
\boldsymbol{t}
yields an equidecomposition of
A
to
B
using translation vectors in
T
such that every piece is non-null, as required.

3.
Proof Outline

With some preliminaries covered by Section
2
, we can now
provide a more detailed outline of the proof of Theorem
1.3
than the one given in Section
1.2
.
In order to illustrate how the various arguments fit together, we will first sketch the construction of an equidecomposition with Jordan measurable pieces (which are not necessarily Borel) and then outline how to build upon it to get the full result.
Our approach mostly follows the general recipe established by Marks and Unger
[
33
]
, some aspects of which can be traced back to the ideas of Laczkovich
[
27
,
29
,
28
]
and Grabowski, Máthé and Pikhurko
[
18
]
. A major challenge here is that the family of Jordan measurable subsets of
\mathbb{T}^{k}
is not a
\sigma
-algebra (although it is an algebra).
Therefore, there are a few key differences in our implementation and analysis of this strategy which are necessary to achieve Jordan measurable pieces.

3.1.
Real-Valued Flows

Following Marks and Unger
[
33
]
*Section 4, we construct a sequence
f_{1},f_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
of bounded real-valued flows in
G_{d}
which converge uniformly to a bounded real-valued flow
f_{\infty}
from
A
to
B
.
The following lemma
summarises the key properties of
f_{1},f_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
that we will need.
Recall that
c
and
\varepsilon
are fixed quantities which were defined in Section
2.1
.

Lemma 3.1

(Marks and Unger
[
33
]
)
.

There exist flows
f_{1},f_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
in
G_{d}
such that for all

the following statements hold with
f_{0}:=0
being the flow which is identically zero:

(3.1)

2^{2dm}f_{m}
is integer-valued,

(3.2)

\|f_{m}^{\operatorname{out}}-\mathbbm{1}_{A}+\mathbbm{1}_{B}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{2c}{2^{m(1+\varepsilon)}}
,

(3.3)

\|f_{m}-f_{m-1}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{2c}{2^{d+\varepsilon(m-1)}}
, and

(3.4)

f_{m}
is a
(2^{m}-1)
-local function of
A
and
B
.

The proof of the lemma follows that of a similar result in
[
33
]
*Section 4; we include the proof in Section
4
for completeness. The rough idea is as follows. Suppose that we have fixed a partition of
[\boldsymbol{u}]_{G_{d}}\cong{\mathbbm{Z}}^{d}
into discrete
2^{m}
-cubes. Within each cube
Q
, the flow
f_{m}
cancels as much as possible between the positive demand
\mathbbm{1}_{A}
and the negative demand
-\mathbbm{1}_{B}
, and spreads the rest uniformly over
Q
. A bit more formally, the restriction of
f_{m}
to
Q
is an
(\mathbbm{1}_{A}-\mathbbm{1}_{B}+\xi(Q))
-flow, where
\xi(Q):=(|A\cap Q|-|B\cap Q|)/|Q|
. Also,
f_{m}
is zero on all edges between distinct cubes of the partition. Assuming that the fixed dyadic partitions are aligned for different values of
m
, we can construct such
f_{m}
incrementally from
f_{m-1}
. As long as we do this increment in a “reasonable” way, the discrepancy bound of Lemma
2.1
gives a good upper bound on
\|f_{m}-f_{m-1}\|_{\infty}
. Unfortunately, one cannot take a perfect dyadic decomposition of each component of
G_{d}
in a constructive way for generic choice of
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{x}_{d}
. However, the convenience of working with flows (versus graph matchings) is that we can always take their convex combinations. So, in order to make the construction of each
f_{m}
local, one can simply take the average over all
2^{dm}
possible partitions of each component (a copy of
{\mathbbm{Z}}^{d}
) into a grid of discrete
2^{m}
-cubes.

Each flow
f_{m}
assumes finitely many values by (
3.1
) and is a local function of the Jordan measurable sets
A
and
B
.
Since the right hand side of (
3.3
) is summable, the sequence
f_{1},f_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
converges uniformly to a bounded flow in
G_{d}
, which we denote by
f_{\infty}
. By (
3.2
), the limit
f_{\infty}
is a flow
from
A
to
B
, exactly without any errors.

With Lemma
3.1
in hand, the goal of the next two steps is to transform the sequence
f_{1},f_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
into a bounded integer-valued flow
f
from
A
to
B
in
G_{d}
. Notice that, if we were indifferent about the structure of the pieces of the final equidecomposition, then we could just apply the Integral Flow Theorem to
f_{\infty}
to obtain such a flow
f
and then feed it into Lemma
2.16
. In particular, this approach is sufficient for proving Theorem
1.1
. However, this would use the Axiom of Choice and yield virtually no structural guarantees on the pieces of the equidecomposition. The main purpose of the next two steps, therefore, is to obtain an integer-valued flow from
A
to
B
in a more careful manner which allows us to analyse the pieces of the equidecomposition.

3.2.
Toast Sequences

Recall that the notion of a toast sequence was defined in Definition
2.14
. Informally, if
(J_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
is a toast sequence, then one can view
J_{i}
as a collection of bounded and well separated connected subgraphs of
G_{d}
that arrive at time
i
so that every component
S
of vertices that arrived in an earlier stage is either entirely inside
J_{i}
or entirely outside
J_{i}
, including some “padding”.

The following lemma provides a simple construction of a toast sequence which covers
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
up to a null set. (This will be sufficient for our sketch of the construction of an equidecomposition with Jordan measurable pieces.)

Lemma 3.2
.

There exists a toast sequence
(J_{1},J_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt)
in
G_{d}
such that
\lambda\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}J_{i}\right)=1
and, for each
i\geqslant 1
, the set
J_{i}
is a union of finitely many disjoint strips.

Proof Sketch.

The construction is slightly more complicated than is necessary, in order for the sets
J_{i}
to be defined in the same way as they will be in the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Recall that
r_{0}^{\prime}<r_{1}<r_{1}^{\prime}<r_{2}<r_{2}^{\prime}<\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
is a rapidly increasing sequence of integers and
X_{i}\subseteq{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
is a maximally
r_{i}
-discrete
set which is a union of finitely many strips.
Define
I_{i}
to be the set of all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}
for which there exists
\boldsymbol{u}\in X_{i}
such that

\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})\geqslant\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u})+5r^{\prime}_{i-1},\quad\mbox{for every $\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in X_{i}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{u}\}$.}

Informally speaking, the components of
I_{i}
are the Voronoi cells of
X_{i}\subseteq V(G_{d})
, as defined in the proof of Lemma
2.16
, except we retract somewhat from their graph boundaries. It is not hard to show that the diameter of each component of
I_{i}
is at most
2r_{i}
and every two components are at distance at least
5r_{i-1}^{\prime}
(see Lemma
5.4
).

We use induction on
i
to define
J_{i}
. Fix
i\geqslant 1
and assume that the sets
J_{j}
,
j<i
, have already been defined. We obtain
J_{i}
as the result of the following procedure. Initialise
J_{i}:=I_{i}
.
Then, while there exist
j<i
and a component
S
of
J_{j}
such that
\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(S,J_{i})\leqslant q_{j-1}^{\prime}+4

and
N_{q_{j-1}^{\prime}+4}[S]\not\subseteq J_{i}
, we add to
J_{i}
all elements of
N_{q_{j-1}^{\prime}+4}[S]
. (Recall that
q_{i}^{\prime}
was defined by (
2.9
), being slightly larger than
4r_{i}^{\prime}/5
.)

Since the sequence
r_{0}^{\prime}<r_{1}<r_{1}^{\prime}<\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
increases sufficiently rapidly, this procedure eventually terminates and in fact stays within the
q^{\prime}_{i-1}
-neighbourhood of
I_{i}
(Lemma
5.6
). Thus, when we construct
J_{i}
from
I_{i}
, the original components of
I_{i}
do not merge and stay well separated, being at distance at least
5r_{i-1}^{\prime}-2q^{\prime}_{i-1}
from each other.
(This stronger separation property is not needed here, but will be useful in the general proof.)
The other properties of a toast sequence from Definition
2.14
hold because, whenever some earlier component
S^{\prime}
(that is,
S^{\prime}\in\operatorname{comp}(J_{j})
with
j<i
)
comes too close to a currently defined component
S
of
J_{i}
, we add
S^{\prime}
with some padding to
S
(with the new enlarged set
S
still being connected).

We trivially have
\lambda(J_{i})\geqslant\lambda(I_{i})
. On the other hand, the measure of
I_{i}
can be shown to be least
1-O(r_{i-1}^{\prime}/r_{i})
(see Lemma
5.13
), which approaches 1 as
i\to\infty
.

Whether or not a vertex
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}
is contained in
J_{i}
can be determined by the structure of the sets
X_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,X_{i}
at bounded distance (say,
5r_{i}
) from
\boldsymbol{u}
in
G_{d}
; thus,
J_{i}
is a local function of
X_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,X_{i}
. So, it can be written as a union of finitely many disjoint strips, finishing our proof sketch.

3.3.
Integer-Valued Flow

The last remaining step is to use the flows
(f_{m})_{m=1}^{\infty}
from Lemma
3.1
and
the toast sequence
(J_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
from Lemma
3.2
to construct an integer-valued bounded flow
f
from
A
to
B
that will be used as input to Lemma
2.16
to equidecompose
A
and
B
. Unfortunately, the construction of
f
is somewhat involved, even in the context of Jordan measurable pieces.

Although this is not strictly necessary, we keep the perspective
that a new set of vertices
J_{i}
arrives at time
i
and we have to fix for good the value of
f
on every new edge intersecting
J_{i}
, being compatible with all previous choices. It is enough to concentrate on constructing the final values of
f
on the edges in
\partial_{E}J_{i}
, that is, on all new boundary edges. Indeed, since every component
S
of
J_{i}
is finite, there are only finitely many possible extensions of
f
to a uniformly bounded integer flow inside
S
and, if at least one exists, then the lexicographically smallest extension is a local function of the boundary flow values,
J_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,J_{i},A
and
B
whose radius is at most the diameter of
S
. A bit later, we will address the problem of certifying the existence of such an extension.

So, take any
S\in\operatorname{comp}(J_{i})
, i.e., a component of
G_{d}\upharpoonright J_{i}
which must be finite by the definition of a toast sequence. Initially let
f
be
f_{m_{i}}
on
\partial_{E}S
, where
m_{i}
is sufficiently large integer depending on the maximum diameter of the components of
J_{i}
.
We repeat the following for every “connected” part
P\subseteq\partial_{E}S
of the edge boundary of
S
. Let
S^{\prime}
consist of the vertices “enclosed” by
P
; thus,
S^{\prime}
is either a hole of
S
or the set
S
with all its holes filled.
By adjusting the value of
f
on one edge of
P
, we make the total flow out of
S^{\prime}
to be
|A\cap S^{\prime}|-|B\cap S^{\prime}|
.
Since
f_{m_{i}}^{\operatorname{out}}
is a very good approximation to
\mathbbm{1}_{A}-\mathbbm{1}_{B}
,
we need to adjust the flow by less than
1/2
.
Using Lemma
6.4
, we find a sequence
(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{u}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{v}_{t-1})
in
P
so that each edge in
P
appears at least once but at most constant number of times while
\boldsymbol{u}_{j}\boldsymbol{v}_{j}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{j+1}\boldsymbol{v}_{j+1}
are in a triangle in
G_{d}
for each
1\leqslant j\leqslant t-2
.
Make
f
to be integer on
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
by adding a
0
-flow (i.e., a flow
\phi
such that
\phi^{\operatorname{out}}:V(G_{d})\to{\mathbbm{R}}
is the zero function) on the unique triangle containing both
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
. (Note
that the third edge of this triangle is not in
\partial_{E}S

and thus we do not care about the flow through it being integral at this stage.) By adding a
0
-flow along the triangle containing both

\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
and
\boldsymbol{u}_{3}\boldsymbol{v}_{3}
,
we make the flow value on
\boldsymbol{u}_{2}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}
integer, and so on. We repeat this procedure
t-2
times in total, ensuring that the final flow
f
is integer-valued on all edges of
P
except perhaps
\boldsymbol{u}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{v}_{t-1}
. Note that this part of the process does not change the flow out of any vertex under
f
. So
f^{\operatorname{out}}(S^{\prime})
keeps its (integer) value and thus the final flow has to be integer also on
\boldsymbol{u}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{v}_{t-1}
.
Clearly, the definition of
f
can be made local by fixing a consistent rule for choosing the adjustment, the edge sequence and the values of the added
0
-flows.

If we had used
f_{\infty}
as the initial values of
f
on
P
, then the initial adjustment step would not be necessary, and the obtained rounding algorithm would be essentially the same as used by Marks and Unger
[
33
]
. Unfortunately, if we wish to have Jordan measurable pieces, then we should (and, in fact, we do) avoid using the values of
f_{\infty}
(which is the pointwise limit of
f_{1},f_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
) when defining
f
on
\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\partial_{E}J_{i}
.
However, we can freely use
f_{\infty}
to define, in a way that parallels the construction of
f
from
f_{m_{i}}
, a real-valued flow which certifies via the
Integral Flow Theorem (Theorem
2.3
) that the constructed integer flow values on
\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\partial_{E}J_{i}
, can be extended to a bounded integer
(\mathbbm{1}_{A}-\mathbbm{1}_{B})
-flow on all edges of
G_{d}

(see Lemma
6.9
for details). As mentioned above, given that such an extension exists, we can choose the lexicographically minimal one on each finite component of the remaining graph; this includes all edges inside
\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}J_{i}
. Here, crucially, the choice of the extension is independent of
f_{\infty}
.

Now, during the construction, each of the (finitely many) sets
Z^{f}_{\vec{\gamma},\ell}\cap\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{i}J_{j}\right)
that describe the flow
f
on edges incident to the vertices which have arrived by time
i
, where
Z^{f}_{\vec{\gamma},\ell}
is as in (
2.14
), only grows in time, since the values of
f
fixed at time
j
are never overwritten later. These sets, after we have run through all values of
i
, cover the co-null set
\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}J_{i}
. Thus each of them is Jordan measurable by the following lemma (applied with
Z:={\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
).

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
Z_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,Z_{N}
be disjoint subsets of a Jordan measurable set
Z\subseteq\mathbbm{T}^{k}
.
If, for every real
\varepsilon>0
, there are Jordan measurable subsets
Z_{j}^{\prime}\subseteq Z_{j}
such that
\lambda(Z\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}Z_{j}^{\prime})<\varepsilon
, then all of the sets
Z_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,Z_{N}
are Jordan measurable.

Proof.

By adding
\mathbbm{T}^{k}\setminus Z
as an extra set and increasing
N
by 1, we can assume that
Z=\mathbbm{T}^{k}
. For
1\leqslant j\leqslant N
and each integer
i\geqslant 1
, let
Z_{j,i}
be a Jordan measurable subset of
Z_{j}
with
\lambda(Z_{j}\setminus Z_{j,i})<1/i
and let
U_{j,i}
be the interior of
Z_{j,i}
. Define
U_{j}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}U_{j,i}
. Since
Z_{j,i}
is Jordan measurable, we have that
U_{j,i}
has the same Lebesgue measure as
Z_{j,i}
. Thus, the sets
U_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,U_{N}
are pairwise disjoint, open and cover
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
up to a null set. For any
1\leqslant j\leqslant N
, since
U_{j}
is open and contained in
Z_{j}
, the boundary of
Z_{j}
is disjoint from
U_{j}
. Also, for any
j^{\prime}\neq j
, since the set
U_{j^{\prime}}
is open and disjoint from
Z_{j}
, we see that the boundary of
Z_{j}
is also disjoint from
U_{j^{\prime}}
. Therefore, for each
1\leqslant j\leqslant N
, the boundary of
Z_{j}
is contained in the complement of
\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}U_{j}
and therefore has measure zero. Thus the set
Z_{j}
is Jordan measurable.

It also follows from the above lemma that the remaining set
{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}J_{i}
has null closure. Thus, without destroying Jordan measurability, we can use the Axiom of Choice to define a suitable integer-valued flow on all edges inside this set, using
f_{\infty}
in the same way as above to certify the existence via the Integral Flow Theorem.

Finally, we apply Lemma
2.16
to
f
to get an equidecomposition between
A
and
B
as an
R
-local function of
f
(that is, the sets
Z^{f}_{\vec{\gamma},\ell}
) and finitely many strips; thus, the pieces are Jordan measurable.

Remark 3.4
.

We could have slightly restructured the proof so that whenever the partial integer-valued flow
f
is defined in the whole
R
-neighbourhood of some
\boldsymbol{u}\in A
, where
R
is the constant returned by Lemma
2.16
, then we assign
\boldsymbol{u}
to the appropriate part of the final equidecomposition. Then the obtained Jordan measurable pieces will be incrementally growing as we process
J_{1},J_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
one by one, exhausting the set
A
up to measure zero. Thus, we could have applied Lemma
3.3
directly to the pieces (with
Z:=A
).

3.4.
Making the Pieces Borel

Let us now discuss how the approach to obtaining a Jordan measurable equidecomposition described above can be built upon to obtain the stronger conditions of Theorem
1.3
.

The main issue to address is the use of the Axiom of Choice in the final step, which must be avoided in order to yield Part
1.1
of the theorem. If
A
and
B
are Borel, then a natural idea for obtaining simultaneously Jordan and Borel pieces is to try to use the existence of a Jordan measurable equidecomposition together with the equidecomposition of Marks and Unger
[
33
]
; however, we did not see a way of doing this directly. Instead, we follow essentially
the same proof as outlined above (with all locally defined structures being Borel if the sets
A
and
B
are) except that we use a version of Marks and Unger’s proof in
[
33
]
to round the flow values inside
\mathbbm{T}^{k}\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}J_{i}
in a way that preserves Borel structure. The Jordan measurability of the final pieces is preserved since, in this extra stage, we do not change any flow value on any edge intersecting
\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}J_{i}
.

Our extra steps can be summarised as follows.
With sets
J_{i}
defined as above and fixed for good (where the role of the parameter
r_{i-1}^{\prime}
is to provide enough “wiggle space” between the components of
J_{i}
), we interleave the sequence
(J_{1},J_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt)
with sets
K_{1},K_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
to create an augmented toast sequence
(J_{i},K_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
. (Recall that
(J_{i},K_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
is a shorthand for
(J_{1},K_{1},J_{2},K_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt)
.) Simultaneously, we will construct sets
L_{1},L_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
so that
(K_{i},L_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
is also a toast sequence. The purpose of the sets
L_{1},L_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
will be to cover all of the points of
{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
that are “left behind” by the construction of
J_{1},J_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
in a Borel way, while the purpose of
K_{i}
is to provide some extra “cushioning” around
J_{i}
to protect the flow values on edges intersecting
J_{i}
from being influenced by
L_{i}
. The sequence
(K_{1},K_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt)
can be seen as a mediator between the competing goals of
J_{1},J_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
(to keep the boundaries of the final pieces small) and
L_{1},L_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
(to cover all of
{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
in a Borel way).

We build
K_{i}
by initialising
K_{i}:=N_{2}[J_{i}]
and then iteratively adding (with an appropriate padding) all components of
J_{j}
,
K_{j}
or
L_{j}
with
j<i
that come too close. As before, this process when started at any
S\in\operatorname{comp}(J_{i})
goes only a small distance away from
S
and the obtained component
S^{\prime}
of
K_{i}
satisfies
S^{\prime}\subseteq N_{q_{i-1}^{\prime}+2}[S]
(Lemma
5.7
) while, trivially,
S^{\prime}\supseteq N_{2}[S]
. Thus every component
S
of
J_{i}
is “mimicked” by a component
S^{\prime}
of
K_{i}
.
When we construct
L_{i}
, we start with
L_{i}:=N_{\lfloor 2r^{\prime}/5\rfloor}[Y_{i}]
(where
Y_{i}
is maximally
r_{i}^{\prime}
-discrete) and keep adding (with some padding) components of
K_{j}
for
j\leqslant i
and
L_{j}
for
j<i
that come too close. Note that each component
S
of
J_{j}
with
j\leqslant i
is “protected” in this process by the component of
K_{j}
that mimics it. What we have achieved is that the toast sequence
(K_{i},L_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
covers all vertices in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}L_{i}\supseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}N_{\lfloor 2r^{\prime}/5\rfloor}[Y_{i}]
. Using a compactness argument, we will show that, by applying this construction with a modified version of
Y_{i}
, we can ensure that all vertices of
{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
are covered; see the discussion following Lemma
5.14
.

Unfortunately,
(J_{i},K_{i},L_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
is not, in general, a toast sequence. Also, we do not want any
S\in\operatorname{comp}(K_{j})\cup\operatorname{comp}(L_{j})
to “interfere” with what happens inside
J_{i}
for some
i>j
.
Our solution is, essentially, to remove all such conflicting components
S
, obtaining sets
\widetilde{K}_{j}
and
\widetilde{L}_{j}
. The new sequence
(J_{i},\widetilde{K}_{i},\widetilde{L}_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
is then a toast sequence that does not break up any finite components coming from
the toast sequence

(J_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
. Also,
it still covers every vertex of
\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}L_{i}
. The proofs of these claims rely on the fact that the components of
K_{j}
mimic the components of
J_{j}
(see Lemma
5.11
).

We define the desired integer-valued Borel flow
f
by using the same construction as in Section
3.3
, except that we replace the toast sequence
(J_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
by
(J_{i},\widetilde{K}_{i},\widetilde{L}_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
.

3.5.
Reducing/Analysing the Complexity of the Pieces

The idea to use
f_{m_{i}}
instead of
f_{\infty}
in the construction of the integer-valued flow
f
is, essentially by itself, enough to save one level of the Borel hierarchy when compared to the proof of Marks and Unger
[
33
]
.
There are a couple of places where we changed their construction or its analysis in order to drop down by another level of Borel complexity. These are incorporated in the main construction while the Borel complexity analysis is postponed until Section
7.2
for the clarity of presentation.

Our approach to bounding the upper Minkowski dimension (defined in (
1.1
)) of the boundaries of the pieces is, for given
\delta>0
, to choose some index
i=i(\delta)
, run our algorithm to process
J_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,J_{i}
, and estimate the number of boxes of a regular grid with side length
\delta
that intersect the currently unassigned part of
A
or the boundary of the current pieces
A_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,A_{N}
. (Thus, our estimates are insensitive to what
K_{1},L_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
are.) Recall by Lemma
3.2
that each set
J_{j}
is a finite union of strips, whose number we can estimate if the sets
X_{i}
are carefully chosen (namely, as in Lemma
7.5
). Also,
we can estimate a radius
R
so that the current pieces
A_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,A_{N}
can be
R
-locally determined from
A
,
B
and
J_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,J_{i}
. Thus each
A_{j}
is a Boolean combination of
(2R+1)^{d}
translates of each of these sets, and the number of boxes that intersect its boundary
\partial A_{j}
can be bounded above by the number of boxes intersecting the boundary of at least one constituent. Furthermore, every other box that may potentially intersect the boundary of a final piece has to be a subset of
A^{\prime}:=A\setminus(\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}A_{j})
and their number can be upper bounded by
\lambda(A^{\prime})/\delta^{k}
by the trivial volume argument. Thus we have to control our parameters carefully to get a good balance between the minimal distance between the components of
J_{i}
(in order to control the measure of the leftover part
A^{\prime}
of
A
) and
their maximum diameter (as our local rule processing
J_{i}
has to use radius at least as large as this diameter).

4.
Proof of Lemma

Our goal in this section is to present, for the sake of completeness, the (somewhat rephrased) construction of Marks and Unger
[
33
]
of a sequence

which converge uniformly to a bounded flow

from

to

in

.

First, we need to introduce a few definitions (that apply only in this section).
Given a discrete cube
Q
in
G_{d}
(as defined in (
2.2
)), define

\xi(Q):=\frac{|Q\cap A|-|Q\cap B|}{|Q|}.

In other words,
\xi(Q)
measures the difference between the number of vertices of
A
and
B
in
Q
normalised by the number of vertices in
Q
. In particular, if
Q
is a discrete
1
-cube, i.e.,
Q=\{\boldsymbol{u}\}
for some
\boldsymbol{u}\in{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
, then
\xi(Q)={\mathbbm{1}}_{A}(\boldsymbol{u})-{\mathbbm{1}}_{B}(\boldsymbol{u})
.
Given
\boldsymbol{u}\in{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
,
\vec{\gamma}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}
and
n\in{\mathbbm{Z}}
, let
\phi_{\boldsymbol{u},n\vec{\gamma}}
be the unique
\{-1,0,1\}
-valued
\left({\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\boldsymbol{u}\}}-{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{(n\vec{\gamma})\cdot\boldsymbol{u}\}}\right)
-flow in

supported on the edges of the path

\boldsymbol{u},\,\vec{\gamma}\cdot\boldsymbol{u},\,(2\vec{\gamma})\cdot\boldsymbol{u},\,\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\,(n\vec{\gamma})\cdot\boldsymbol{u}.

In other words,
\phi_{\boldsymbol{u},n\vec{\gamma}}
sends a unit of flow from
\boldsymbol{u}
to
(n\vec{\gamma})\cdot\boldsymbol{u}
along the straight-line path (whose direction is
\vec{\gamma}
and graph length is
n
).
In particular, if
n\vec{\gamma}
is the zero vector, then the flow
\phi_{\boldsymbol{u},n\vec{\gamma}}
is identically zero. Also, for an integer
m\geqslant 0
and a discrete
2^{m+1}
-cube
Q
containing
\boldsymbol{u}\in{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
, let

\phi_{\boldsymbol{u},Q}:=\frac{1}{2^{d}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\vec{\gamma}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}\\
(2^{m}\vec{\gamma})\cdot\boldsymbol{u}\in Q\end{subarray}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{u},2^{m}\vec{\gamma}}.

Note that the above sum contains exactly
2^{d}
terms. Informally speaking,
\phi_{\boldsymbol{u},Q}
spreads one unit of demand from
\boldsymbol{u}
uniformly among
Q\cap((2^{m}{\mathbbm{Z}}^{d})\cdot_{a}\boldsymbol{u})
, the set of
all
2^{d}
points of
Q

that, when we view
Q
as a subset of
{\mathbbm{Z}}^{d}
, are congruent to
\boldsymbol{u}
modulo
2^{m}
.
For every discrete
2^{m+1}
-cube
Q
, let
\mathcal{P}(Q)
be the unique partition of
Q
into
2^{d}
discrete
2^{m}
-cubes. Given
\boldsymbol{u}\in{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
, let
\mathcal{Q}_{n}(\boldsymbol{u})
denote the set of all discrete
n
-cubes that contain
\boldsymbol{u}
.

We are now ready to define the flows
f_{m}
. We use induction on
m
and
start by defining
f_{0}:=0
to be the identically zero flow. For
m\geqslant 1
, we define
f_{m}:=f_{m-1}+\theta_{m}
, where

(4.1)

\theta_{m}:=\frac{1}{2^{dm}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{Q}_{2^{m-1}}(\boldsymbol{u})}\xi(C)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mbox{\scriptsize discrete $2^{m}$-cube $Q$}\\
\mathcal{P}(Q)\ni C\end{subarray}}\phi_{\boldsymbol{u},Q}.

Let us provide a probabilistic interpretation that motivates the definition in (
4.1
) and relates it to the proof outline of Section
3.1
. Suppose that we wish to compute
\theta_{m}
and
f_{m}
inside the component
[\boldsymbol{v}]_{G_{d}}
of some vertex
\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}
. Take a random partition
{\mathcal{G}}_{m}
of the orbit
[\boldsymbol{v}]_{G_{d}}
, which is a copy of
{\mathbbm{Z}}^{d}
, into a
2^{m}
-grid

{\mathcal{G}}_{m}
(that is, a regular grid of discrete

2^{m}
-cubes), where all
2^{dm}
choices of
{\mathcal{G}}_{m}
are equally likely. By defining

{\mathcal{G}}_{i}:=\{\mathcal{P}(Q):Q\in{\mathcal{G}}_{i+1}\}

inductively for

, we get the dyadic refinements of
{\mathcal{G}}_{m}
down to the partition
{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
into discrete
1
-cubes (i.e., singletons). Note that we work inside one component only since, for generic
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{x}_{d}

2^{m}
-grid inside each orbit by ergodicity considerations.

Starting with
f_{0,{\mathcal{G}}_{0}}:=0
being the zero flow, obtain inductively for
i=1,2,\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,m
, the flow
f_{i,{\mathcal{G}}_{i}}
from

by adding

(4.2)

\theta_{i,{\mathcal{G}}_{i}}:=\sum_{Q\in{\mathcal{G}}_{i}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in Q}\left({\mathbbm{1}}_{A}(\boldsymbol{u})-{\mathbbm{1}}_{B}(\boldsymbol{u})-f_{i-1,{\mathcal{G}}_{i-1}}^{\operatorname{out}}(\boldsymbol{u})\right)\phi_{\boldsymbol{u},Q}.

Note that, inside each
2^{i}
-cube

spreads the current demand error
{\mathbbm{1}}_{A}-{\mathbbm{1}}_{B}-f_{i-1,{\mathcal{G}}_{i-1}}^{\operatorname{out}}
uniformly inside each congruence class modulo

. Of course, if the error

then

spreads this error evenly inside

. Thus an easy induction on

shows that
{\mathbbm{1}}_{A}-{\mathbbm{1}}_{B}-f_{i,{\mathcal{G}}_{i}}^{\operatorname{out}}
is constant on every cube

. Moreover, since

is zero on the edge boundary of

(and thus sends no flow out of

, that is,

(4.3)

{\mathbbm{1}}_{A}(\boldsymbol{u})-{\mathbbm{1}}_{B}(\boldsymbol{u})-f_{i,{\mathcal{G}}_{i}}^{\operatorname{out}}(\boldsymbol{u})=\xi(Q),\quad\mbox{for all
$Q\in{\mathcal{G}}_{i}$ and $\boldsymbol{u}\in Q$}.

Thus we have

(4.4)

\theta_{i,{\mathcal{G}}_{i}}:=\sum_{Q\in{\mathcal{G}}_{i}}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{P}(Q)}\xi(C)\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in C}\phi_{\boldsymbol{u},Q},\quad\mbox{for every $1\leqslant i\leqslant m$}.

It follows from (

) that, inside the component

,
\theta_{i}
for each

is the expectation of
\theta_{i,{\mathcal{G}}_{i}}
over a uniformly random
2^{m}
-grid
{\mathcal{G}}_{m}
, since
{\mathcal{G}}_{i}=\mathcal{P}^{m-i}({\mathcal{G}}_{m})
is a uniformly random
2^{i}
-grid. By the linearity of expectation,
f_{m}
inside

is the expectation of
f_{m,{\mathcal{G}}_{m}}
.

Now we are ready to verify all four conclusions, (
3.1
)–(
3.4
), of Lemma
3.1
.

Conclusion (

) states that

is integer-valued. Indeed, the only non-integer factors in (

) in addition to

are

(from the definition of
\xi(C)
) and
2^{-d}
(from the definition of

), giving the required.

Next, let estimate how close

is from the desired demand
\mathbbm{1}_{A}-\mathbbm{1}_{B}
.
By taking the expectation over
{\mathcal{G}}_{m}
of (
4.3
) for
i:=m
, we get that

(4.5)

{\mathbbm{1}}_{A}(\boldsymbol{u})-{\mathbbm{1}}_{B}(\boldsymbol{u})-f_{m}^{\operatorname{out}}(\boldsymbol{u})=\frac{1}{2^{dm}}\sum_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}_{2^{m}}(\boldsymbol{u})}\xi(Q),

for all

Now, (

3.2

For (

) using only straight-line paths:

(4.6)

\theta_{m}=\frac{1}{2^{dm}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{u}\in{\mathbbm{T}}^{k}}\sum_{C\in\mathcal{Q}_{2^{m-1}}(\boldsymbol{u})}\xi(C)\sum_{\vec{\gamma}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}}2^{|\{i:\gamma_{i}=0\}|}\,\frac{\phi_{\boldsymbol{u},2^{m-1}\vec{\gamma}}}{2^{d}}.

5.1.
Some Preliminaries

Let us say that a set
A

cuts

if both

and

are non-empty.
The following definition is key to describing our constructions.

Definition 5.1
.

For
i\geqslant 1
, let

be a sequence of subsets of
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
and let

. Define
\mathcal{C}_{b}(D)\subseteq\mathbbm{T}^{k}
to be the set constructed as follows:

(5.1)

initialise
\mathcal{C}_{b}(D):=D_{i}
,

(5.2)

while there exist
1\leqslant j\leqslant i-1
and
S\in\operatorname{comp}(D_{j})
such that
\mathcal{C}_{b}(D)
cuts
N_{b}[S]
,
add all vertices of
N_{b}[S]
to
\mathcal{C}_{b}(D)
.

Note that the order in which we perform the operations in (
5.2
) does not affect the final set
\mathcal{C}_{b}(D)
.
Intuitively, Definition
5.1
is designed to take an infinite sequence
D_{1},D_{2},D_{3},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
of subsets of
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
and transform it into a sequence
\mathcal{C}_{2}(D_{1}),\mathcal{C}_{2}(D_{1},D_{2}),\mathcal{C}_{2}(D_{1},D_{2},D_{3}),\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
which satisfies property (
2.17
) of Definition
2.14
(that the component boundaries are appropriately separated from each other). The next lemma demonstrates that if, for every

, the diameter of each component of

is not too large and the distance between them is not too small, then the resulting sequence satisfies (
2.15
) and (
2.16
) as well.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
D=(D_{1},D_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,D_{i})
be a sequence of subsets of
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
, let
b\geqslant 0
and let
(a_{1},a_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,a_{i-1})
and
(b_{0},b_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,b_{i-1})
be sequences of integers such that
b_{0}\geqslant 0
and

(5.3)

b_{j}\geqslant a_{j}+2b_{j-1}+2b,\quad\mbox{for all $1\leqslant j\leqslant i-1$}.

If, for every
1\leqslant j\leqslant i-1
, every component of
G_{d}\upharpoonright D_{j}
has diameter at most

in
G_{d}
and the distance in

between any two components of

\mathcal{C}_{b}(D)\subseteq N_{b_{i-1}}[D_{i}]
.

Proof.

Take any vertex
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{C}_{b}(D)\setminus D_{i}
.
By definition of
\mathcal{C}_{b}(D)
, there exists a sequence
S_{1},S_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,S_{n}
of distinct subsets of
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
such that

(5.4)

for each
1\leqslant\ell\leqslant n
, there exists
1\leqslant j\leqslant i-1
such that
S_{\ell}
is a component of
G_{d}\upharpoonright D_{j}
,

(5.5)

\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(S_{1},D_{i})\leqslant b
,

(5.6)

\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(S_{\ell},S_{\ell+1})\leqslant 2b
for

, and

(5.7)

\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(S_{n},\boldsymbol{w})\leqslant b
.

Given such a vertex
\boldsymbol{w}
and

, let

be such that

. Our aim is to prove, by induction on

\displaystyle\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w})

\displaystyle\leqslant

We will use the global parameters

It is tedious, but not hard, to verify that, with the specific definitions in (

Recall that, for each

The set

can be viewed as a union of “partial Voronoi cells” around the elements of

. Now, for
i\geqslant 1
, define

(5.11)

\displaystyle J_{i}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}(N_{q^{\prime}_{0}+2}[J_{1}],\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,N_{q^{\prime}_{i-2}+2}[J_{i-1}],I_{i}),

(5.12)

\displaystyle K_{i}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}(K_{1},L_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,K_{i-1},L_{i-1},N_{2}[J_{i}]),

(5.13)

\displaystyle L_{i}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}(K_{1},L_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,K_{i-1},L_{i-1},K_{i},N_{\lfloor 2r^{\prime}_{i}/5\rfloor}[Y_{i}]).

We will show that
(J_{1},K_{1},J_{2},K_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt)
and
(K_{1},L_{1},K_{2},L_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt)
are toast sequences. In particular, this implies that

is a toast sequence.
Along the way, we will also prove some key properties of these sequences of sets which will be applied later in the paper to prove Theorem

.

5.3.
Properties of the Constructed Toast Sequences

We begin with the following statement, which simply follows by construction.

Lemma 5.3
.

For every
i\geqslant 1
, we have
I_{i}\subseteq J_{i}
,
N_{2}[J_{i}]\subseteq K_{i}
and
N_{\lfloor 2r^{\prime}_{i}/5\rfloor}[Y_{i}]\subseteq L_{i}
.

Next, we prove a lemma regarding the structure of
I_{i}
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Every component of
G_{d}\upharpoonright I_{i}
has diameter at most
2r_{i}
and the distance in
G_{d}
between any two such components is at least
5r^{\prime}_{i-1}
.

Proof.

Note that, by (
5.8
) and the definition of
I_{i}
, we have
X_{i}\subseteq I_{i}
. Given
\boldsymbol{v}\in I_{i}
, if
\boldsymbol{u}\in X_{i}
is such that
\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u})
is minimum, then every vertex on any shortest path from
\boldsymbol{v}
to
\boldsymbol{u}
in
G_{d}
is contained in
I_{i}
. In particular, this implies that every component of
G_{d}\upharpoonright I_{i}
contains at least one vertex of
X_{i}
. Now, for distinct
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in X_{i}
in the same component of
G_{d}
, let
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\boldsymbol{v}_{1}\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt\boldsymbol{v}_{n}
be the shortest path in
G_{d}
from the component of
G_{d}\upharpoonright I_{i}
containing
\boldsymbol{u}
to the component of
G_{d}\upharpoonright I_{i}
containing
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}
. Then, by definition of
I_{i}
, we have

\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}\left(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\boldsymbol{u}\right)+2n\geqslant\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{n},\boldsymbol{u})+n\geqslant\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{n},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})+5r^{\prime}_{i-1}+n

\geqslant\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})+5r^{\prime}_{i-1}\geqslant\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}\left(\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\boldsymbol{u}\right)+10r^{\prime}_{i-1}

and so
n\geqslant 5r^{\prime}_{i-1}
. So, we know that each component of
G_{d}\upharpoonright I_{i}
contains a unique element of
X_{i}
and that the distance in
G_{d}
between any two components at least
5r^{\prime}_{i-1}
. Now, let
\boldsymbol{u}\in X_{i}
and let
\boldsymbol{v}
be in the same component of
G_{d}\upharpoonright I_{i}
as
\boldsymbol{u}
. Since
X_{i}
is maximally
r_{i}
-discrete and
\boldsymbol{u}
is the element of
X_{i}
at minimum distance from
\boldsymbol{v}
, we have
\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\leqslant r_{i}
. Thus, the diameter of each component is at most
2r_{i}
.

The following lemma holds because
Y_{i}
is maximally
r^{\prime}_{i}
-discrete; we omit its easy proof.

Lemma 5.5
.

Every component of
G_{d}\upharpoonright N_{\lfloor 2r^{\prime}_{i}/5\rfloor}[Y_{i}]
has diameter at most
4r_{i}^{\prime}/5
and the distance in
G_{d}
between any two such components is at least
r_{i}^{\prime}/5
.

The proofs of the next two lemmas are the main applications of Lemma
5.2
.

Lemma 5.6
.

For
i\geqslant 1
, we have
J_{i}\subseteq N_{q^{\prime}_{i-1}}[I_{i}]
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
i
. The base case
i=1
is trivial as
J_{1}=I_{1}
. Now, for
i\geqslant 2
, our aim is to apply the inductive hypothesis and Lemma
5.2
with
b:=2
to the sequences
(N_{q^{\prime}_{0}+2}[J_{1}],\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,N_{q^{\prime}_{i-2}+2}[J_{i-1}],I_{i})
,
(t^{\prime}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,t^{\prime}_{i-1})
and
(q^{\prime}_{0},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,q^{\prime}_{i-1})
.

By induction, for every
1\leqslant j\leqslant i-1
, the set
J_{j}
is contained within
N_{q^{\prime}_{j-1}}[I_{j}]
. This implies that
N_{q^{\prime}_{j-1}+2}[J_{j}]\subseteq N_{2q^{\prime}_{j-1}+2}[I_{j}]
. Thus, by Lemma
5.4
, every component of
G_{d}\upharpoonright N_{q^{\prime}_{j-1}+2}[J_{j}]
has diameter at most
2r_{j}+4q^{\prime}_{j-1}+4=t_{j}<t_{j}^{\prime}
and the distance in
G_{d}
between any two such components is at least
5r^{\prime}_{j-1}-4q^{\prime}_{j-1}-4
, which is at least
q^{\prime}_{j-1}+5
by (
5.9
). So, the result follows from Lemma
5.2
since
q^{\prime}_{j}=t^{\prime}_{j}+2q_{j}+4>t^{\prime}_{j}+2q^{\prime}_{j-1}+4
for all
j\geqslant 1
.

Lemma 5.7
.

For
i\geqslant 1
, we have
K_{i}\subseteq N_{q^{\prime}_{i-1}+2}[J_{i}]
and
L_{i}\subseteq N_{\lfloor 2r^{\prime}_{i}/5\rfloor+q_{i}}[Y_{i}]
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction, where the (trivial) inequality
K_{1}\subseteq N_{q_{0}^{\prime}+2}[J_{1}]
forms the base case. For
i\geqslant 2
, in the
i
-th step of the induction, we prove that

•

L_{i-1}\subseteq N_{\lfloor 2r^{\prime}_{i-1}/5\rfloor+q_{i-1}}[Y_{i-1}]
, and

•

K_{i}\subseteq N_{q^{\prime}_{i-1}+2}[J_{i}]
.

Analogous to the proof of Lemma
5.6
, we will apply the inductive hypothesis and Lemma
5.2
to respectively

•

(K_{1},L_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,K_{i-1},N_{\lfloor 2r^{\prime}_{i-1}/5\rfloor}[Y_{i-1}])
,
(t_{1},t_{1}^{\prime},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,t^{\prime}_{i-2},t_{i-1})
and
(q_{0}^{\prime},q_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,q^{\prime}_{i-2},q_{i-1})
,

•

(K_{1},L_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,L_{i-1},N_{2}[J_{i}])
,
(t_{1},t_{1}^{\prime},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,t_{i-1},t^{\prime}_{i-1})
and
(q_{0}^{\prime},q_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,q_{i-1},q^{\prime}_{i-1})
.

By induction, for every
1\leqslant j\leqslant i-1
, we have
K_{j}\subseteq N_{q^{\prime}_{j-1}+2}[J_{j}]
. Also, for every
2\leqslant j\leqslant i-1
, we have
L_{j-1}\subseteq N_{\lfloor 2r^{\prime}_{j-1}/5\rfloor+q_{j-1}}[Y_{j-1}]
. Thus, by Lemmas
5.4
and
5.6
, every component of
G_{d}\upharpoonright K_{j}
has diameter at most
2r_{j}+4q^{\prime}_{j-1}+4=t_{j}
and the distance in
G_{d}
between any two such components is at least
5r^{\prime}_{j-1}-4q^{\prime}_{j-1}-4
, which is at least
q^{\prime}_{j-1}+5
by (
5.9
). Similarly, for
2\leqslant j\leqslant i-1
, by Lemma
5.5
, every component of
G_{d}\upharpoonright L_{j-1}
has diameter at most
\lfloor 4r^{\prime}_{j-1}/5\rfloor+2q_{j-1}=t^{\prime}_{j-1}
and the distance in
G_{d}
between any two such components is at least
r^{\prime}_{j-1}/5-2q_{j-1}
, which is at least
q_{j-1}+5
by (
5.10
). So, the lemma follows from Lemma
5.2
.

Let us collect together the bounds on the diameter of the obtained components that are direct consequences of the above results.

Lemma 5.8
.

For each
i\geqslant 1
, every component of
G_{d}\upharpoonright J_{i}
,
G_{d}\upharpoonright K_{i}
and
G_{d}\upharpoonright L_{i}
has diameter at most
2r_{i}+2q_{i-1}^{\prime}
,
2r_{i}+4q_{i-1}^{\prime}+4
and
4r^{\prime}_{i}/5+2q_{i}
respectively.

Also, we need to refer a few times to the following, not entirely trivial result so we state it as a separate lemma.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
i>j\geqslant 1
.
If
S\in\operatorname{comp}(K_{j})
and
T\in\operatorname{comp}(J_{i})
are at distance at most
2
in

N_{2}[S]\subseteq T
.

Proof.

Observe that, by Lemma

, we have
S\subseteq K_{j}\subseteq N_{q^{\prime}_{j-1}+2}[J_{j}]
.
By the definition of
J_{i}
, every component of
N_{q^{\prime}_{j-1}+2}[J_{j}]
that is within distance
2
of

is added with its 2-neighbourhood into

. Thus

contains

, as desired.

We are now in position to prove the following lemma.

Lemma 5.10
.

Both of
(J_{1},K_{1},J_{2},K_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt)
and
(K_{1},L_{1},K_{2},L_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt)

Proof.

The fact that the components of the subgraphs of
G_{d}
induced by
J_{i}
,

and

are of uniformly bounded cardinality and are pairwise separated by distance at least three follows from Lemmas

,
5.5
,
5.6
and
5.7
and Inequalities (

) and (

) and (

) of Definition

Let us check Property (
2.17
) for the sequence
(J_{i},K_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
. First, take any

. Suppose on the contrary to (

) that

is at distance at most 2 from

for

or
K_{j}
for

is a not a subset of

For each

from

. By Lemma

\widetilde{K}_{i}=K_{i}\setminus\left(\bigcup_{j=i+1}^{\infty}J_{j}\right),

that is, we could have equivalently defined

’s form a toast sequence without loosing any single vertex. (Recall that

for every

Lemma 5.11
.

The following properties hold.

(5.16)

For every

, we have
\operatorname{comp}(\widetilde{K}_{i})\subseteq\operatorname{comp}(K_{i})
and

\operatorname{comp}(\widetilde{L}_{i})\subseteq\operatorname{comp}(L_{i})

(5.17)

\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}(J_{i}\cup\widetilde{K}_{i}\cup\widetilde{L}_{i})\supseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}L_{i}

(5.18)

If

and

are at distance at most

, then

and

.

(5.19)

Proof.

and
\widetilde{L}_{i}
.

For (

\boldsymbol{u}\in L_{i}\setminus\widetilde{L}_{i}

\boldsymbol{u}\in\bigcup_{j=1}^{\infty}(J_{j}\cup\widetilde{K}_{j})

with

lies

For (

i\geqslant j

For (

5.4.
Some Structure of the Constructed Sets

The next two lemmas will be useful in analysing the structure of the pieces in the final equidecomposition.

Lemma 5.12
.

Each of the sets
J_{i},K_{i}
and
L_{i}
for
i\geqslant 1
can be expressed as a union of finitely many disjoint strips.

Proof.

Observe that each of the sets
J_{i},K_{i}
and
L_{i}
is a local function of
X_{1},Y_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,X_{i},Y_{i}
, simply by their definitions and, for example, the uniform diameter bounds of Lemma
5.8
.
Since every set
X_{j}
and
Y_{j}
is in turn a finite union of strips (by our choices in Section
2.6
), the lemma holds.

Let us remark that the sets
\widetilde{K}_{i}
and
\widetilde{L}_{i}
need not satisfy Lemma
5.12
, that is, they are not in general finite unions of strips.

Lemma 5.13
.

We have

\lambda(I_{i})=1-O\left(r^{\prime}_{i-1}/r_{i}\right)

as
i\to\infty
, where the constant factor is bounded by a function of
d
only.

Proof.

Note that
I_{i}
is a union of finitely many disjoint strips and, therefore, it is measurable. For any pair of distinct vertices
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in X_{i}
, let
W_{i}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})
be the set of all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}
such that

\max\left\{\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}),\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{v})\right\}\leqslant r_{i}+5r^{\prime}_{i-1}\text{ and}

\left|\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})-\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{v})\right|\leqslant 5r^{\prime}_{i-1}.

Define
W_{i}
to be the union of
W_{i}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})
over all pairs of distinct vertices
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in X_{i}
. The set
W_{i}
can be written as a Boolean combination of translates of
X_{i}
, and so it is measurable. By definition of
I_{i}
and the fact that
X_{i}
is maximally
r_{i}
-discrete, we have that
\mathbbm{T}^{k}\setminus I_{i}\subseteq W_{i}
. So, it suffices to prove that
\lambda(W_{i})=O(r^{\prime}_{i-1}/r_{i})
.

First, for each
\boldsymbol{u}\in X_{i}
, let us bound the number of
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in X_{i}
such that
W_{i}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})
is non-empty. Given any such
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}
, we have

\operatorname{dist}_{G_{d}}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})\leqslant 2r_{i}+10r^{\prime}_{i-1}.

Therefore,

N_{\lfloor r_{i}/2\rfloor}[\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}]\subseteq N_{2r_{i}+\lfloor r_{i}/2\rfloor+10r^{\prime}_{i-1}}[\boldsymbol{u}].

Since the set
X_{i}
is
r_{i}
-discrete, the sets
N_{\lfloor r_{i}/2\rfloor}[\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}]
are disjoint for different
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in X_{i}
. So, by the Pigeonhole Principle, the number of such
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}
is at most

\frac{\left(4r_{i}+2\lfloor r_{i}/2\rfloor+20r^{\prime}_{i-1}+1\right)^{d}}{\left(2\lfloor r_{i}/2\rfloor+1\right)^{d}}

which is bounded above by a constant (that depends on
d
,
r_{i}
and
r_{i-1}^{\prime}
only).

Now, given a pair of distinct
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\in X_{i}
in the same component of
G_{d}
, let
\vec{n}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{d}
be such that
\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}=\vec{n}\cdot_{a}\boldsymbol{u}
. Note that
\|\vec{n}\|_{\infty}\geqslant r_{i}+1
since
X_{i}
is
r_{i}
-discrete. Then
W_{i}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})
is the set of all
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}
of the form
\boldsymbol{v}=\vec{m}\cdot_{a}\boldsymbol{u}
where

•

\|\vec{m}\|_{\infty}\leqslant r_{i}+5r^{\prime}_{i-1}
,

•

\|\vec{n}-\vec{m}\|_{\infty}\leqslant r_{i}+5r^{\prime}_{i-1}
and

•

\big|\|\vec{m}\|_{\infty}-\|\vec{n}-\vec{m}\|_{\infty}\big|\leqslant 5r^{\prime}_{i-1}
.

For fixed
d
, the number of vectors
\vec{m}
of this type is
O\left(r_{i}^{d-1}r_{i-1}^{\prime}\right)
. Indeed, there are
d^{2}
ways to choose the indices
j_{1},j_{2}\in\{1,\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,d\}
such that
\left|m_{j_{1}}\right|
and
\left|n_{j_{2}}-m_{j_{2}}\right|
are maximum. In the case that
j_{1}=j_{2}
, there are
O(r_{i-1}^{\prime})
choices for
m_{j_{1}}
and
O(r_{i})
choices for
m_{j}
for each
j\in\{1,\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,d\}\setminus\{j_{1}\}
. On the other hand, if
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
, then the number of choices of
m_{j_{1}}
is
O(r_{i})
and, given this choice, the number of choices for
m_{j_{2}}
is
O(r_{i-1}^{\prime})
and, again, there are
O(r_{i})
choices for
m_{j}
for each
j\in\{1,\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,d\}\setminus\{j_{1},j_{2}\}
.

Suppose now that we split
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
into Voronoi cells with the points in

6.
Using Toast Sequences to Round Flows

\{\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\}
is a triangle in
G_{d}
, there is a unique component
T
of
G_{d}\upharpoonright\left(\mathbbm{T}^{k}\setminus S\right)
containing
\boldsymbol{w}
. All three edges of this triangle change their flow value at most once when rounding
\phi
along the boundary of
T
(in the case that
T
is finite) or the union of
S
and all of its holes (in the case that
T
is infinite). Thus, each triangle containing
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
contributes at most
1/2
to the amount that
\phi(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})
changes; there are at most
3^{d}-2
such triangles, and so the proof is complete.

Next, given a flow
\phi
in
G_{d}
and a subset
S
of
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
such that
G_{d}\upharpoonright S
is connected, the operation of
completing
\phi
within
S
is defined as follows. Consider all integer-valued flows
\phi^{\prime}
in
G_{d}\upharpoonright S
such that

(6.2)

\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in S}\phi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+\sum_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}\setminus S}\phi(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})=\mathbbm{1}_{A}(\boldsymbol{u})-\mathbbm{1}_{B}(\boldsymbol{u}),\quad\mbox{for every $\boldsymbol{u}\in S$,}

and, given this,
\|\phi^{\prime}\|_{\infty}
is as small as possible.
(It will be the case that, whenever we apply this operation, at least one such
\phi^{\prime}
exists.)
If
S
is finite then we choose
\phi^{\prime}
so that the sequence
(\phi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}):(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\in S^{2})
is lexicographically minimised, where the pairs in
S
are viewed as being ordered according to
\prec_{\operatorname{lex}}
. If
S
is infinite then we choose
\phi^{\prime}
arbitrarily, using Theorem
2.3
(i.e., using the Axiom of Choice).
Change
\phi(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})
to be equal to
\phi^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})
for all
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in S
.

6.2.
Rounding a Sequence of Flows on a Toast Sequence

The following key definition will allow us to round flows.

Definition 6.8
.

The
rounding
of a sequence
(g_{1},g_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt)
of real-valued flows in
G_{d}
on a toast sequence
(D_{1},D_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt)
is the flow
f
obtained via the following steps.

(6.2)

Initially, let
f
be the identically zero flow except for each
i\geqslant 1
,
S\in\operatorname{comp}(D_{i})
, and
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})\in\partial_{E}S
we define
f(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}):=g_{i}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})
and
f(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{u}):=g_{i}(\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{v})
(that is, we copy the values of
g_{i}
on
\partial_{E}D_{i}
).

(6.2)

For each
i\geqslant 1
, round
f
along the boundary of
D_{i}
.

(6.2)

For each component
S
of the graph
G^{\prime}:=({\mathbbm{T}}^{k},E(G_{d})\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}\partial_{E}D_{i})
, which is obtained from
G_{d}
by removing the edge boundaries of all sets
D_{i}
, complete
f
within
S
, as specified after (
6.2
).
(If at least one completion step fails, then the whole procedure fails and
f
is undefined.)

Observe that, since
(D_{1},D_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt)
is a toast sequence, the first two steps in the above definition are well-defined; also, the second step does not depend on the order in which we round the boundaries.

Lemma 6.9
.

In the notation of Definition
6.8
, suppose that there are a constant
C
such that
\|g_{1}\|_{\infty}\leqslant C
and a sequence
d_{1}\leqslant d_{2}\leqslant\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
of integers such that, for each
i\geqslant 1
, every component of
G_{d}\upharpoonright D_{i}
has diameter at most
d_{i}
and

(6.3)

(3^{d}-1)\cdot(d_{i}+1)^{d}\cdot\sum_{j=i}^{\infty}\|g_{j+1}-g_{j}\|_{\infty}<1/2.

Then the following statements hold:

(6.4)

all completion steps succeed,

(6.5)

the obtained function
f
is an integer-valued flow from
A
to
B
,

(6.6)

\|f\|_{\infty}\leqslant C+(3^{d}+2)/2
,

(6.7)

the restriction of
f
to the edges intersecting
D_{i}
is a
(d_{i}+1)
-local function of
g_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,g_{i}
and
D_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,D_{i}
.

Proof.

Combining the fact that
\|g_{1}\|_{\infty}\leqslant C
with the bound in (
6.3
) in the case

implies that the sequence
g_{1},g_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
converges uniformly. Let
f_{\infty}
denote the pointwise limit of this sequence.

Let us show (
6.4
), that is, that all completion steps succeed.
For this, we introduce one more operation. Given two flows
\phi
and
\psi
in
G_{d}
and a finite subset
S
of
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
with no holes such that
G_{d}\upharpoonright S
is connected, the operation of
equalising
\phi
to
\psi
along the boundary of
S
is defined as follows. Let
(\boldsymbol{u}_{1}\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\boldsymbol{v}_{t})
with
\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{u}_{t}\in S
be the lexicographically minimal Eulerian circuit in
\triangle_{\partial S}
(which exists by Lemma
6.5
).
For each
s=1,\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,t-2
, one by one, we let
\boldsymbol{w}_{s}\in\{\boldsymbol{u}_{s+1},\boldsymbol{v}_{s+1}\}
be such that
\{\boldsymbol{u}_{s},\boldsymbol{v}_{s},\boldsymbol{w}_{s}\}
is a triangle in
G_{d}
and redefine
\phi
to be

(6.8)

\phi:=\phi+\left(\psi(\boldsymbol{u}_{s},\boldsymbol{v}_{s})-\phi(\boldsymbol{u}_{s},\boldsymbol{v}_{s})\right)\circlearrowleft_{\boldsymbol{u}_{s},\boldsymbol{v}_{s},\boldsymbol{w}_{s}}.

Also, we define
{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\phi,\psi}_{S}
(where
\phi
stands for its initial value) as the difference between the final and initial flows
\phi
during this process, that is,
{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\phi,\psi}_{S}
is the sum of all

increments in (

). Thus
{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\phi,\psi}_{S}
is a
0
-flow such that
\phi+{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\phi,\psi}_{S}
is equal to
\psi
on every pair in
\partial S
except possibly the pair
\boldsymbol{u}_{t-1}\boldsymbol{v}_{t-1}
.
More generally, for a set
D\subseteq\mathbbm{T}^{k}
such that every component of

is finite,
equalising
\phi
to
\psi
along the boundary of

is defined as follows. First, for each component
S
of

and hole

of

, we equalise
\phi
to
\psi
along the boundary of

. Then, for every component
S
of
G_{d}\upharpoonright D
, we equalise
\phi
to
\psi
along the boundary of the union of
S
and all of its holes. Also, define

{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\phi,\psi}_{D}:=\sum_{S\in\operatorname{comp}(D)}\sum_{S^{\prime}}{{\mathcal{E}}}^{\phi,\psi}_{S^{\prime}},

where

in the inner sum is a hole of
S
, or
S
with all holes filled.

The proof of the following claim follows similar lines as that of Lemma
6.7
, so we omit it.

Claim 6.9.1
.

Let
\phi
and
\psi
be flows in

,

be a non-negative real number and

be a subset of

such that

for every component

. Then

.

This claim
gives that

\|{{\mathcal{E}}}^{f_{\infty},g_{i}}_{S}\|_{\infty}\leqslant 1/2,\quad\mbox{for every $S\in\operatorname{comp}(D_{i})$}.

Indeed,
|S|\leqslant(d_{i}+1)^{d}

Now, consider the real-valued flow

to

Take any

. By Lemma

, the graph

has an Eulerian circuit; let

is a flow from

to

since it is obtained from the

\Big|\,g_{i}^{\operatorname{out}}(S)-|A\cap S|+|B\cap S|\,\Big|\leqslant|\partial_{E}S|\cdot\|f_{\infty}-g_{i}\|_{\infty}<1/2,

(for every

Conclusion (

from

.

For (

), recall that the Integral Flow Theorem (Theorem

) guarantees that the produced integer-valued flow differs from the given real-valued flow by at most 1 on every edge. Thus, by Lemma

For (

Lemma 7.4

There exist positive constants

Z^{f_{J\widetilde{K}^{\vec{p}}\widetilde{L}^{\vec{p}}}}_{\vec{\gamma},\ell}
.

Lemma 7.9
.

There exists a collection
\mathcal{W}
of subsets of
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
such that

(7.19)

every set in
\mathcal{W}
is a countable union of Boolean combinations of finitely many strips and translates of
A
and
B
;

(7.20)

for any
\vec{\gamma}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}
,
\ell\in\mathrm{range}(f)
and
\vec{p}\in\{0,\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,5\}^{d}
, the set
Z_{\vec{\gamma},\ell}^{f_{J\widetilde{K}^{\vec{p}}\widetilde{L}^{\vec{p}}}}
can be written as a Boolean combination of some sets in

.

Proof.

Recall that the flow
f_{J}
is obtained by rounding
(f_{m_{i}})_{i=1}^{\infty}
on
(J_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
. For the proof, we need to define two further flows:

•

f_{JK^{\vec{p}}}
is the rounding of
(f_{m_{i}},f_{m_{i}})_{i=1}^{\infty}
on the toast sequence
(J_{i},K_{i}^{\vec{p}})_{i=1}^{\infty}
,
and

•

f_{K^{\vec{p}}L^{\vec{p}}}
is the rounding of
(f_{m_{i}},f_{m_{i}^{\prime}})_{i=1}^{\infty}
on the toast sequence
(K_{i}^{\vec{p}},L_{i}^{\vec{p}})_{i=1}^{\infty}
.

Lemma
7.2
applies to each of these two flows
(with the used toast sequence
(J_{i}^{\prime},K_{i}^{\prime},L_{i}^{\prime})_{i=1}^{\infty}
being respectively
(J_{i},K_{i}^{\vec{p}},\emptyset)_{i=1}^{\infty}
and
(\emptyset,K_{i}^{\vec{p}},L_{i}^{\vec{p}})_{i=1}^{\infty}
).

For every
\vec{\gamma}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{d}
,
\ell\in\mathrm{range}(f)
,
\vec{p}\in\{0,\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,5\}^{d}
and
i\geqslant 1
, define

\displaystyle W^{J}_{\vec{\gamma},\ell,i}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{\boldsymbol{u}:f_{J}\left(\boldsymbol{u},\vec{\gamma}\cdot_{a}\boldsymbol{u}\right)=\ell\mbox{ and }\{\boldsymbol{u},\vec{\gamma}\cdot_{a}\boldsymbol{u}\}\cap\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{i}J_{i}\right)\neq\emptyset\right\},

\displaystyle W^{JK^{\vec{p}}}_{\vec{\gamma},\ell,i}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{\boldsymbol{u}:f_{JK^{\vec{p}}}\left(\boldsymbol{u},\vec{\gamma}\cdot_{a}\boldsymbol{u}\right)=\ell\mbox{ and }\{\boldsymbol{u},\vec{\gamma}\cdot_{a}\boldsymbol{u}\}\cap\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{i}K_{i}^{\vec{p}}\right)\neq\emptyset\right\},

\displaystyle W^{K^{\vec{p}}L^{\vec{p}}}_{\vec{\gamma},\ell,i}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{\boldsymbol{u}:f_{K^{\vec{p}}L^{\vec{p}}}\left(\boldsymbol{u},\vec{\gamma}\cdot_{a}\boldsymbol{u}\right)=\ell\mbox{ and }\{\boldsymbol{u},\vec{\gamma}\cdot_{a}\boldsymbol{u}\}\cap\left(\bigcup_{j=1}^{i}(K_{i}^{\vec{p}}\cup L_{i}^{\vec{p}})\right)\neq\emptyset\right\}.

,

and

and

and encoding

for every

.)

, each of these newly defined sets is a local function of

,

while, in turn, each of the sets

is a finite union of strips by Lemma

. So each set defined above is a Boolean combination of finitely many strips and translates of
A
and
B
.

Now, define

W^{J}_{\vec{\gamma},\ell}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}W^{J}_{\vec{\gamma},\ell,i}\quad\mbox{and}\quad W^{J}_{\vec{\gamma},\neg\ell}:=\bigcup_{s\in\mathrm{range}(f_{J})\setminus\{\ell\}}W^{J}_{\vec{\gamma},s},

W^{JK^{\vec{p}}}_{\vec{\gamma},\ell}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}W^{JK^{\vec{p}}}_{\vec{\gamma},\ell,i}\quad\mbox{and}\quad W^{JK^{\vec{p}}}_{\vec{\gamma},\neg\ell}:=\bigcup_{s\in\mathrm{range}(f_{JK^{\vec{p}}})\setminus\{\ell\}}W^{JK^{\vec{p}}}_{\vec{\gamma},s}

W^{K^{\vec{p}}L^{\vec{p}}}_{\vec{\gamma},\ell}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}W^{K^{\vec{p}}L^{\vec{p}}}_{\vec{\gamma},\ell,i}\quad\mbox{and}\quad W^{K^{\vec{p}}L^{\vec{p}}}_{\vec{\gamma},\neg\ell}:=\bigcup_{s\in\mathrm{range}(f_{JK^{\vec{p}}L^{\vec{p}}})\setminus\{\ell\}}W^{K^{\vec{p}}L^{\vec{p}}}_{\vec{\gamma},s}.

\mathcal{W}
to be the collection of all sets of the form

,

W^{J}_{\vec{\gamma},\neg\ell}
,

W^{JK^{\vec{p}}}_{\vec{\gamma},\ell}
,
W^{JK^{\vec{p}}}_{\vec{\gamma},\neg\ell}
,

or

for any
\vec{\gamma},\ell
and

. Since each flow is integer-valued, all unions above are over a countable index set. Thus the family

satisfies (

).

By construction (or by

), the flows

and

coincide on every pair intersecting
\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}J_{i}
.
Also, if a pair
\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}
intersects

, then the value of
f
on this pair equals the value of
f_{JK^{\vec{p}}}
unless there exists
i>j
such that
\boldsymbol{u}\boldsymbol{v}
intersects
J_{i}
and the values of
f_{J}
and
f_{JK^{\vec{p}}}
on this pair differ.
Thus, informally speaking, the value under

is retained by the final flow, unless it is overwritten due to a later set

.
Similarly, if an edge intersects

,
then the value of

on this edge is retained by

,
unless it is overwritten due to

with

Therefore,

Appendix A
Laczkovich’s Discrepancy Bound

The purpose of this appendix is to sketch a proof of Lemma
2.1
. This can be seen as an expanded version of the sketch given in
[
18
]
*pp. 677–678. Let
X\subseteq\mathbbm{T}^{k}
be a measurable set such that
\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial X)<k
. The first step is to use the upper Minkowski dimension of the boundary to reduce the proof to bounding discrepancy of
N_{r}^{+}[\boldsymbol{u}]
relative to boxes in
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
. This argument is due to Niederreiter and Wills
[
36
]
*Kollorar 4. Let
d
be an integer such that
d>\frac{k}{k-\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial X)}
and let
0<\varepsilon<\frac{d(k-\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial X))-k}{k}
. Define

\alpha:=\frac{(1+\varepsilon)k}{d}.

By the definition of upper Minkowski dimension and the fact that
\alpha<k-\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial X)
, there exists
\delta_{0}\in(0,1)
such that

(A.1)

\lambda\left(\left\{\boldsymbol{x}:\operatorname{dist}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{x},\partial X)\leqslant\delta\right\}\right)\leqslant\delta^{\alpha}

for all
0<\delta<\delta_{0}
. Now, for
r\in\mathbbm{N}
, choose
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
small with respect to
r
, where the dependence is clarified below. For convenience, let us assume that
\delta^{-1}
is an integer.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the partition of
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
into a grid of
\delta^{-k}
boxes, each with side-length
\delta
. Let
\mathcal{B}
be the elements of
\mathcal{P}
which intersect
\partial X
. We have

\delta^{k}|\mathcal{B}|\leqslant\lambda\left(\left\{\boldsymbol{x}:\operatorname{dist}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{x},\partial X)\leqslant\delta\right\}\right)\leqslant\delta^{\alpha}

by (
A.1
) and so
|\mathcal{B}|\leqslant\delta^{-k+\alpha}
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be the elements of
\mathcal{P}
contained in the interior of
X
. We then let
\mathcal{I}^{*}
be the collection of boxes obtained by starting with
\mathcal{I}
and iteratively merging two boxes if they have the same projection onto the first
k-1
coordinates and their closures share a
(k-1)
-dimensional face. For any two distinct boxes in
\mathcal{I}^{*}
which have the same projection onto the first
k-1
coordinates, there must be at least one element in
\mathcal{B}
“between” them which prevents them from merging. Conversely, each element of
\mathcal{B}
prevents at most one potential merging. Therefore,

|\mathcal{I}^{*}|\leqslant\delta^{-k+1}+|\mathcal{B}|\leqslant\delta^{-k+1}+\delta^{-k+\alpha}<2\delta^{-k+\alpha}

since
\alpha<1
. Thus,
\mathcal{I}^{*}\cup\mathcal{B}
is a covering of
X
with at most
3\delta^{-k+\alpha}
boxes such that each of the boxes in
\mathcal{B}
has measure at most
\delta^{k}
.

Now, given any finite set
F\subseteq\mathbbm{T}^{k}
, by the triangle inequality, the discrepancy of
F
relative to
X
can be bounded as follows:

(A.2)

D(F,X)\leqslant\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}^{*}}D(F,I)+\sum_{I\in\mathcal{B}}D(F,I\cap X)

To prove Lemma
2.1
, we want to upper bound the right side of (
A.2
) in the case that
F=N_{r}^{+}[\boldsymbol{u}]
for some
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}
. The key to this is the following lemma which bounds the discrepancy of
N_{r}^{+}[\boldsymbol{u}]
relative to any box
I
in
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
.

Lemma A.1

(Laczkovich
[
28
]
*Lemma 2; see also Schmidt
[
37
]
*p. 517)
.

For almost every choice of
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{x}_{d}
in
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
and for every
t>0
there exists
C>0
such that

D\left(N_{r}^{+}[\boldsymbol{u}],I\right)\leqslant C\left(\log(r)\right)^{k+d+t}

for every
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}
and box
I
in
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
.

Let us first show how this lemma is used to complete the proof of Lemma
2.1
before sketching the proof of the lemma itself. Applying Lemma
A.1
to the first summation on the right side of (
A.2
), we get

\sum_{I\in\mathcal{I}^{*}}D\left(N_{r}^{+}[\boldsymbol{u}],I\right)\leqslant C\left(\log(r)\right)^{k+d+t}|\mathcal{I}^{*}|\leqslant 2C\left(\log(r)\right)^{k+d+t}\delta^{-k+\alpha}.

The second summation is different because the set
I\cap X
is not necessarily a box when
I\in\mathcal{B}
. The key here is that
I\cap X
has measure at most
\delta^{k}
and contains at most as many points of
N_{r}[\boldsymbol{u}]
as
I
does; therefore,

\sum_{I\in\mathcal{B}}D\left(N_{r}^{+}[\boldsymbol{u}],I\right)\leqslant\left((r+1)^{d}\delta^{k}+C\left(\log(r)\right)^{k+d+t}\right)|\mathcal{B}|

\leqslant\left((r+1)^{d}\delta^{k}+C\left(\log(r)\right)^{k+d+t}\right)\delta^{-k+\alpha}.

So, if we set
\delta\leqslant(r+1)^{-d/k}
, then we get

D\left(N_{r}^{+}[\boldsymbol{u}],X\right)\leqslant(r+1)^{d-\alpha d/k+o(1)}=(r+1)^{d-1-\varepsilon+o(1)}.

The extra
o(1)
term in the exponent can clearly be taken care of by choosing
\varepsilon
slightly closer to
\frac{d(k-\operatorname{dim}_{\square}(\partial X))-k}{k}
in the beginning. Thus, Lemma
2.1
reduces to Lemma
A.1
.

So, all that remains is to estimate the discrepancy of
N_{r}^{+}[\boldsymbol{u}]
relative to an arbitrary box
I
. Lemma
A.1
can be viewed as saying that the set
N_{r}^{+}[\boldsymbol{u}]
is “uniformly spread” in the sense that it has low discrepancy on every box. There is a long and fascinating history of results of this type. As a starting point, let us think about the case
k=1
. We say that a sequence
x_{1},x_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
in
[0,1)
to be
uniformly spread
in
[0,1)
if, for any
0\leqslant a<b\leqslant 1
, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\left|\{k:1\leqslant k\leqslant n\text{ and }x_{n}\in[a,b)\}\right|}{n}=b-a.

If we view
\{x_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,x_{n}\}
as a multiset and consider the natural extension of the discrepancy
D(F,X)
, defined in (
2.1
), to multisets
F
, the sequence
x_{1},x_{2},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt
is uniformly spread if and only if
D(\{x_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,x_{n}\},[a,b])=o(n)
as
n\to\infty
. The study of uniformly spread sequences can be traced back to the well-known Kronecker Approximation Theorem
[
26
]
which says that, for any irrational
\alpha>0
, the set
\{n\alpha-\lfloor n\alpha\rfloor:n\in\mathbbm{N}\}
is dense in
[0,1)
; we refer the interested reader to
[
10
]
*Chapter 1 for more history and background. The famous Weyl Criterion
[
44
]
characterises uniformly spread sequences in
[0,1)
as those for which
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}e^{2\pi ihx_{k}}=0
for every
h\in\mathbbm{N}
. The Erdős–Turán Inequality
[
12
,
13
]
provides a quantitative version of this statement (see Corollary
A.3
below).

Theorem A.2

(The Erdős–Turán Inequality
[
12
,
13
]
)
.

There exists
C>0
such that for every probability measure
\mu
on
[0,1)
, natural number
n_{0}
and
0\leqslant a\leqslant b<1
,

\left|\mu([a,b))-(b-a)\right|\leqslant C\left(\frac{1}{n_{0}}+\sum_{h=1}^{n_{0}}\frac{|\hat{\mu}(h)|}{h}\right)

where

\hat{\mu}(h):=\int_{0}^{1}e^{2\pi ih\theta}d\mu(\theta).

Corollary A.3
.

There exists
C>0
such that for every finite multiset
F
of points in
[0,1)
, natural number
n_{0}
and
0\leqslant a\leqslant b<1
,

|D(F,[a,b))|\leqslant C\left(\frac{|F|}{n_{0}}+\sum_{h=1}^{n_{0}}\frac{1}{h}\left|\sum_{x\in F}e^{2\pi ihx}\right|\right).

Proof.

Let
\mu_{F}
be the measure such that
\mu_{F}(S):=\frac{|F\cap S|}{|F|}
for each
S\subseteq[0,1)
. Then
\mu_{S}
is a probability measure and so, by the Erdős–Turán Inequality,

\frac{|D(F,[a,b))|}{|F|}=|\mu_{F}([a,b))-(b-a)|\leqslant C\left(\frac{1}{n_{0}}+\sum_{h=1}^{n_{0}}\frac{|\hat{\mu_{F}}(h)|}{h}\right).

Note that

\hat{\mu_{F}}(h)=\int_{0}^{1}e^{2\pi ih\theta}d\mu_{F}(\theta)=\frac{1}{|F|}\sum_{x\in F}e^{2\pi ihx}

and so the proof is complete.

The notion of a uniformly spread sequence naturally generalises to the multidimensional setting of
\mathbbm{T}^{k}
. The extension of Corollary
A.3
to higher dimensions was proved by Koksma
[
25
]
and is known as the Erdős–Turán–Koksma Inequality. The version below was proved by Grabner
[
17
]
using a method of Vaaler
[
42
]
; see
[
10
]
*Theorem 1.21.

Theorem A.4

(The Erdős–Turán–Koksma Inequality
[
12
,
13
,
25
]
)
.

For every finite multiset
F
of points in
[0,1)^{k}
, natural number
n_{0}
and box
I\subseteq[0,1)^{k}
,

|D(F,I)|\leqslant\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{k}\left(\frac{2|F|}{n_{0}+1}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{k}\\
0<\|\boldsymbol{h}\|_{\infty}\leqslant n_{0}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{r(\boldsymbol{h})}\left|\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}\in F}e^{2\pi i\langle\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle}\right|\right)

where
r(\boldsymbol{h}):=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\max\{1,|h_{i}|\}
for
\boldsymbol{h}=(h_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,h_{k})\in\mathbbm{Z}^{k}

It is not hard to see the potential utility of Theorem

Proof Sketch of Lemma

.

By the Erdős–Turán–Koksma Inequality, for every

,
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}

\left|D(N_{r}^{+}[\boldsymbol{u}],I)\right|\leqslant\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{k}\left(\frac{2(r+1)^{d}}{n_{0}+1}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{k}\\

If we let

, then the first term inside of the large parentheses is less than
1/2
. So, it suffices to prove that, for almost every choice of
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,\boldsymbol{x}_{d}

(A.3)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbbm{Z}^{k}\\

for every
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbbm{T}^{k}
. By writing
\langle\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle=\langle\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{u}\rangle+\langle\boldsymbol{h},\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{u}\rangle
and factoring out

\boldsymbol{u}=(0,0,\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt,0)
. We omit the remaining details but briefly remark that statements similar to the following identity are useful (cf.
[
41
]
*p. 152):

\left|\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{2\pi in\beta}\right|=\left|\frac{\sin(\pi\beta N)}{\sin(\pi\beta)}\right|\leqslant\left|\frac{1}{\sin(\pi\beta)}\right|\leqslant\frac{1}{4\min\{\,|\beta-\lfloor\beta\rfloor|,|\beta-\lceil\beta\rceil|\,\}\}}

for every
\beta\in\mathbbm{R}\setminus\mathbbm{Z}
.
