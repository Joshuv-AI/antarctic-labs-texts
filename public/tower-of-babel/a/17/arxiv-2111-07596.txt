Photon superbunching in cathodoluminescence of excitons in WS 2 \text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} monolayer
Saskia Fiedler, Sergii Morozov, Leonid Iliushyn, Sergejs Boroviks, Martin Thomaschewski, Jianfang Wang, Timothy J. Booth, Nicolas Stenger, Christian Wolff, N. Asger Mortensen

Abstract
ABSTRACT Cathodoluminescence spectroscopy in conjunction with second-order auto-correlation measurements of g 2 ​ ( τ ) g_{2}(\tau) allows to extensively study the synchronization of photon emitters in low-dimensional structures. Co-existing excitons in two-dimensional transition metal dichalcogenide monolayers provide a great source of identical photon emitters which can be simultaneously excited by an electron. Here, we demonstrate large photon bunching with g 2 ​ ( 0 ) g_{2}(0) up to 156 ± 16 156\pm 16 of a tungsten disulfide monolayer ( WS 2 \text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} ), exhibiting a strong dependence on the electron-beam current. To further improve the excitation synchronization and the electron-emitter interaction, we show exemplary that the careful selection of a simple and compact geometry – a thin, monocrystalline gold nanodisk – can be used to realize a record-high bunching g 2 ​ ( 0 ) g_{2}(0) of up to 2152 ± 236 2152\pm 236 . This approach to control the electron excitation of excitons in a WS 2 \text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}} monolayer allows for the synchronization of photon emitters in an ensemble, which is important to further advance light information and computing technologies. Keywords : photon superbunching, cathodoluminescence, electron-beam excitation, transition metal dichalcogenide monolayer, van der Waals heterostructures, hexagonal boron nitride



I
Introduction

The recent development in the synthesis and fabrication of two-dimensional (2D) transition metal dichalcogenide (TMDC) monolayers has sparked intensive research of their implementation in applications in the emerging field of quantum information and computing technologies
Aharonovich:2016
;
Reserbat-Plantey2021
.
The family of TMDC monolayers with a thickness of a few atoms provides an attractive combination of optoelectronic properties, including a direct bandgap, a strong spin-orbit coupling and splitting, an optical access to spin and valley degrees of freedom
Manzeli2017
.
An isolated monolayer is a robust material platform for photon generation, where the co-existence of multiple excitons is a resource that represents an ensemble of identical photon sources.

Photon statistics of an ensemble of emitters localized within a sub-wavelength volume, strongly depends on the excitation synchronization and can exhibit photon bunching
Temnov:2009
;
Meuret:2015
. Photoexcitation does not provide the required synchronization of an ensemble as the photon arrival statistics is Poissonian in nature.
Moderate photon bunching – with a
g_{2}(0)<2
second-order auto-correlation function – is characteristic for thermal light, for example the solar radiation of the Sun was measured to be
g_{2}(0)\simeq 1.6

Tan2014
.
The generation of superthermal light with
g_{2}(0)>2
has been observed under the electron-beam excitation, namely in cathodoluminescence (CL)
Meuret:2015
;
Fiedler:2023
, which was theoretically described by the quantum master equation method
Yuge2022
.
The temporal correlation of CL photons with a Hanbury Brown and Twiss (HBT) interferometer gives an insight into coherent and incoherent processes in electron-matter interaction
Scheucher2022
.
The width of the bunching peak provides information about the radiative lifetime of the emitters, while its amplitude indicates the probability of the excitation to interact with an ensemble of emitters
Meuret2020
.

In the limit of high electron-beam current, the electron arrival approaches Poissonian statistics, thus resulting in a flat second-order auto-correlation function
g_{2}(\tau)\to 1
as in the case of photoexcitation.
However, reducing the electron-beam current to sub-few hundred pA is allowing for a single electron excitation, which generates multiple electron-hole pairs capable of synchronized excitation of an ensemble.
The synchronized radiative recombination does not necessarily imply phase locking, thus can be considered as simultaneous emission of emitters in an ensemble triggered by the same electron.
Exploiting the low currents, ensembles in various solid state systems – such as indium gallium nitride (
InGaN
) quantum wells and color centers in nano-diamonds – have been shown to generate bunched light with
g_{2}(0)
up to
80

Meuret2018
;
Feldman:2018
.
A decrease of the electron-beam current to sub-pA is expected to further increase the
g_{2}(0)
-values, however such low currents are unattainable by common commercial scanning electron microscopes (SEMs).
Therefore, to further boost the photon bunching, ensembles consisting of identical and densely localized emitters within a sub-wavelength volume are needed
Temnov:2009
;
Yuge2022
, while a geometry increasing the probability of electron-emitter interaction would increase the brightness of the source.

In this letter, we study luminescence properties and photon statistics of a tungsten disulfide (
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
) monolayer encapsulated in hexagonal boron nitride (
hBN
) layers.
We report the first observation of photon bunching in a
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
monolayer under electron-beam excitation, which reaches
g_{2}(0)=156\pm 16
at the lowest attainable current by our instrument.
We further explore methods for improving the excitation synchronization and electron-emitter interaction via adding a monocrystalline gold nanodisk.
In such a geometry, a gold disk can scatter and redirect incoming electrons to large angles, even allowing for excitation of in-plane momentum components.
This electron excitation is increasing the probability of electron interaction with the sample, which allows us to achieve record-high photon bunching of
g_{2}(0)=2152\pm 236
, exceeding the previously reported values by over an order of magnitude.
Our simple geometry offers a robust and compact platform for efficient electron beam excitation and generation of light with photon superbunching statistics, which has important applications in light-based information and computing technologies.

II
Results and discussion

II.1
Sample and experimental setup

We perform photoluminescence (PL) and cathodoluminescence (CL) experiments of a
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
monolayer sandwiched between two thin
hBN
flakes (top and bottom flakes are ca. 35 and 105 nm thick, respectively, see also SI Fig. S1) on a
50\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
-thin silicon nitride (SiN) membrane.
The hBN encapsulation on both sides of the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
monolayer serves three purposes: (i) protecting the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
from electron-beam damage and contamination, (ii) increasing the effective interaction volume of incoming electrons with the monolayer, and (iii) providing layers for the generation of additional charge carriers which can subsequently diffuse into the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
and radiatively recombine, thereby significantly enhancing the CL intensity
Zheng:2017
.
We note that even smallest contamination during the assembly of
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
–
hBN
heterostructure can cause quick and irreversible degradation of the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
monolayer under the electron beam.
Fig.
1
a schematically presents the experimental setup for the electron-beam excitation of the investigated sample and for collection of generated CL.
Here, the electron beam of
10\text{\,}\mathrm{kV}
and
30\text{\,}\mathrm{kV}
is focused onto the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
–
hBN
heterostructure, and generated CL is subsequently collimated by a parabolic mirror and focused onto a spectrometer and an HBT interferometer (see details in SI).

II.2
Photo and electron-beam excitation

Photo and electron-beam excitation are essentially different mechanisms of exciton generation in a
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
monolayer.
An incoming photon typically creates one electron-hole pair in the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
monolayer, while an incoming electron can excite many electron-hole pairs in the sample (Fig.
1
b), predominantly in the thicker
hBN
layers due to a large interaction volume
Yacobi:1990
.
The latter process can be described by a scattered incoming electron which excites high-energy interband transitions in the sample, also referred to as bulk plasmons in electron spectroscopy literature
Egerton:2011
;
Meuret:2015
.
Such bulk plasmons (
\sim 30
eV) are comprised of a coherent sum of interband transitions contrary to the collective intraband oscillations of free charges, e.g., manifesting in surface plasmons (
<5
eV).
Electron induced bulk plasmons subsequently decay into multiple electron-hole pairs which, after a typical excitonic lifetime, exhibit synchronized radiative recombination.
The photons appear to arrive as a ”packet” and can be detected with the HBT interferometer as photon bunching (
g_{2}(0)>1
).

In Fig.
2
a, we compare spectral emission properties of the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
–
hBN
heterostructure under photo and electron-beam excitation.
The PL spectrum is centered around
621\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
and has a red-shifted satellite peak at
635\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
(green line in Fig.
2
a).
These emission lines are characteristic for the neutral and red-shifted charged exciton (trion) in
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
monolayers at room temperature
Morozov2021
.
The CL spectrum in Fig.
2
a is dominated by the neutral exciton peak at
626\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
which is red-shifted with respect to the PL spectrum.
In general, we observe a sub-
10\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
spectral wandering of the excitonic peak in both PL and CL measurements which is possibly due to local strain within the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
–
hBN
heterostructure
Li2020
.
Fig.
2
b presents a CL intensity map obtained by scanning the electron beam over the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
–
hBN
heterostructure. The spatial CL distribution reveals areas of lower intensity, which are due to cracks in the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
monolayer. The round dark patches apparent in the CL map can be attributed to bubbles in between the three layers, as the lack of adherence in the stacked sample has been shown to result in quenched luminescence
Nayak:2019
.
Fig.
2
d displays a corresponding PL confocal map obtained with laser excitation at
404\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
. We find a similar intensity distribution as in the CL map in Fig.
2
b, although characterized by a lower spatial resolution than with an electron beam.

The photon statistics of emission generated from the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
–
hBN
heterostructure drastically depend on the type of excitation.
Fig.
2
c presents photon correlation histograms obtained under photo and electron-beam excitation.
Electron-beam excitation results in a pronounced photon bunching peak at zero correlation time (blue diamonds in Fig.
2
c), while the photon correlation histogram obtained under photoexcitation is flat with
g_{2}(\tau)=1
(green circles in Fig.
2
c).
Moreover, the bunching amplitude in CL can be tuned by the electron-beam current, unlike the flat photon correlation histogram in PL
Meuret:2015
.

II.3
CL response to electron-beam current

The CL intensity and the amplitude of photon bunching can be controlled by the electron-beam current
I
.
As the current is increased, a higher CL intensity is observed due to the larger amount of incoming electrons, generating more electron-hole pairs in the sample, which can subsequently recombine radiatively in the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
monolayer (Fig.
3
a).
In contrast, the amplitude of the photon bunching peak follows the opposite trend: with increasing
I
, the contribution of uncorrelated emission is increased, resulting in a lower photon bunching
(SI Fig. S2)
.
Fig.
3
b shows the bunching histograms collected at low and high electron-beam currents, which we fit to extract the values of
g_{2}(0)
(see details in SI).
At the lowest electron-beam current of
3\text{\,}\mathrm{pA}
, we achieve a giant bunching factor
156\pm 16
, indicating the high quality of the investigated sample.
In contrast, the bunching factor is greatly reduced to approximately
4.7\pm 0.2
for the highest electron-beam current of
211\text{\,}\mathrm{pA}
, which is summarized in Fig.
3
c.
We fit the examined dynamics with

g_{2}(0,I)=1-\frac{I_{0}}{P_{e}\times I}

(1)

as the bunching factor has been observed to be inversely dependent on the applied electron-beam current
Meuret:2015
;
Meuret:2020
.
Here
I_{0}
relates to the current exciting a bulk plasmon and
P_{e}
is a probability of incoming electrons to interact with emitters.

The physical reasons behind the photon bunching can be attributed to the synchronization of emission from emitters, where each incoming electron creates a photon packet.
With increasing electron-beam current, the electrons arrive closer in time and the photon packets become increasingly indistinguishable, thereby reducing the bunching factor until the Poissonian distribution
g_{2}(0,{I\rightarrow}\infty)=1
is reached.
In the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
–
hBN
heterostructure, the inverse
I
-dependence is confirmed in Fig.
3
c, although the Poissonian distribution cannot be reached due to sample degradation at high electron-beam currents
I>

200\text{\,}\mathrm{pA}

(SI Fig. S3)
.

II.4
Boosting bunching through geometry

A further increase of the photon bunching factor by reducing the applied electron-beam current is limited by the instrument.
Decreasing the acceleration voltage potentially allows to reduce the current to sub-
\mathrm{pA}
, however in practice, it causes severe sample charging already at
5\text{\,}\mathrm{kV}
.
To overcome this limit, we suggest to modify the geometry by adding a thin gold nanodisk to induce a local change of electron-beam excitation parameters.
We drop-cast monocrystalline gold nanodisks with a diameter ranging from
80\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
to
230\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
on top of the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
–
hBN
heterostructure.
Gold nanodisks are well separated from each other across the heterostructure, allowing for a comparison of electron-beam excitation of bare heterostructure and through a gold nanodisk (SI Fig. S1).

For further CL studies, we chose a gold nanodisk with an approximate thickness of
30\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
and a diameter of
120\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
, as shown in the inset of Fig.
4
a.
The reasoning behind this selection is that the (broad) plasmonic dipole mode spectrally overlaps with the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
-exciton, in principle allowing for plasmonic interaction
Fiedler:2020
.
Fig.
4
a presents the CL spectra of the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
–
hBN
heterostructure excited through the gold nanodisk with increasing electron-beam current. We observe an overall CL brightening with increasing
I
, similar to the bare
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
–
hBN
heterostructure. However, the CL spectra do not reveal significant emission enhancement which would indicate a plasmon-assisted Purcell effect, nor any evidence of strong coupling (Fig.
4
a). This observation is in agreement with the PL measurements, where the position of the gold nanodisks distributed over the entire
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
–
hBN
heterostructure cannot be made out.
We ascribe this lack of luminescence enhancement to the large separation of about
35\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
between the gold nanodisk and the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
monolayer, which does not allow for efficient near-field coupling.

Remarkably, the addition of gold nanodisks improves the emission synchronization in the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, which manifests in the increase of photon bunching factor.
The gold nanodisks themselves – on
hBN
only – exhibit dim CL emission
(see SI Fig. S3-S4)
, which allowed us to quantify the bunching factor
g_{2}(0)=43.1\pm 4.9
of a similarly sized
120\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
nanodisk (orange in Fig.
4
b). Fig.
4
b further compares the photon correlation histograms measured at identical excitation parameters (
8\text{\,}\mathrm{pA}
,
30\text{\,}\mathrm{kV}
) from the bare
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
–
hBN
heterostructure (blue), and a system of a gold nanodisk on
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
–
hBN
heterostructure (dark red).
The fit of the histograms provides information about the lifetime of the generated emission
\tau
and the bunching factor
g_{2}(0)
.
We observe a minor change in radiative lifetime of
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
after the addition of the gold nanodisk, extracting values of
\tau_{{}\mathrm{WS}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}}=365\pm 18

\mathrm{ps}
and
\tau_{{}\mathrm{WS}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}+{}\mathrm{gold}}=416\pm 10

\mathrm{ps}

The extracted values of radiative lifetime agree with one obtained via time-correlated single-photon counting (TCSPC) methods using a pulsed laser (

II.5
The role of gold nanodisk

We perform Monte Carlo simulations
Drouin:2007
to shed light on the change in electron interaction probability and photon superbunching caused by the addition of a gold nanodisk.
Fig.
5
a,c present results for electron trajectory simulations of the investigated geometries using a
10\text{\,}\mathrm{kV}
-electron beam with a spot size of
5\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
.
In case of the bare
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
–
hBN
heterostructure, the hBN interface barely affects the direction of incoming electrons, which is visualized using 10 simulated electron trajectories in Fig.
5
a (blue lines). We further accumulate 1000 trajectories to calculate the electron angular divergence histogram (Fig.
5
b). Evidently, it reveals the principal direction of electron propagation normal to the sample plane with a small divergence of
\Theta_{1/2}^{{}\mathrm{WS}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}}=45\degree
(the full width at half power).
The addition of a 30 nm thick gold nanodisk in Fig.
5
c drastically changes the direction of electron propagation within the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
–
hBN
heterostructure as it is shown in the corresponding angular divergence histogram in Fig.
5
d.
The incoming electrons are widely scattered by the gold nanodisk so the principal direction of electron propagation cannot be identified, while electrons propagate omnidirectionally with divergence
\Theta_{1/2}^{{}\mathrm{WS}{\vphantom{\mathrm{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\mathrm{2}}}+{}\mathrm{gold}}=155\degree
.
We summarize the simulation results in Fig.
5
e, which presents the electron divergence for a range of gold thickness and acceleration voltage.

The divergence of the electron beam serves to (on average) provide impinging electrons with a significant in-plane momentum which further enhances coupling to the in-plane dipole moment of the excitons.
Moreover, the redirection of electrons by a gold nonodisk from the normal-to-sample plane extends the geometrical path of the electrons in the
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
–
hBN
heterostructure. This increases the probability of electrons
P_{e}
to interact with the thin sub-100 nm sample and to excite bulk plasmons in it. As the interaction length and the probability of bulk plasmon excitation are both increased, one would expect to observe an increase in CL emission intensity while preserving the degree of bunching in the experiment.
Fig.
5
f presents the results of such an experiment, where we collect both the
g_{2}(0)
and the corresponding CL intensity from the bare heterostructure and that with an additional gold nanodisk.
We average the
g_{2}(0)
-intensity data sets in Fig.
5
f and fit the result with an intensity inverse function since the CL intensity is proportional to the electron-beam current. The fit results clearly demonstrate the brightening of CL intensity after adding a gold nanodisk, which is due to the increase in probability of electrons to interact with the sample. Such a change in excitation efficiency improves the synchronization of an ensemble, which results in increase of the degree of photon bunching according to Eq.
1

Meuret:2015
;
Yuge2022
, which we observe in Fig.
4
c.

III
Conclusion and Outlook

In conclusion, we demonstrated the importance of electron-beam excitation for synchronization of large emitter ensembles, and for achieving high photon bunching factors. We reported the first experimental observation of photon bunching
g_{2}(0)=156\pm 16
in cathodoluminescence of excitons in
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
monolayer.
We further suggested that an appropriate geometry can locally facilitate the electron-beam interaction with the sample, which is allowing to increase the CL intensity signal as well as to boost the photon bunching dynamics far beyond the instrumentation-limited currents. We utilized a system of a thin gold nanodisk on a
\text{WS}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
–
hBN
heterostructure to demonstrate a dramatic increase in photon bunching to
g_{2}(0)=2152\pm 236
, which is over an order of magnitude larger than previously reported values.
Employing Monte Carlo simulations in combination with CL-
g_{2}(\tau)
-measurements, we showed that a gold nanodisk can redirect incoming electrons in the sample increasing the interaction length and affecting the bulk plasmon excitation probability, which is another parameter allowing to manipulate the photon bunching dynamics beside the electron-beam current.
We presented a simple way to drive the values of
g_{2}(0)
to extremes, which offers a photon source with super-Poissonian emission statistics for applications in emerging light information and computing technologies.
