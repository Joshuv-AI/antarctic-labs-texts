Abstract
In this paper we study a stationary double-diffusive natural convection problem in porous media given by a Navier-Stokes/Darcy type system, for describing the velocity and the pressure, coupled to a vector advection-diffusion equation describing the heat and substance concentration, of a viscous fluid in a porous media with physical boundary conditions. The model problem is rewritten in terms of a first-order system, without the pressure, based on the introduction of the strain tensor and a nonlinear pseudo-stress tensor in the fluid equations. After a variational approach, the resulting weak model is then augmented using appropriate redundant penalization terms for the fluid equations along with a standard primal formulation for the heat and substance concentration. Then, it is rewritten as an equivalent fixed-point problem. Well-posedness and uniqueness results for both the continuous and the discrete schemes are stated, as well as the respective convergence result under certain regularity assumptions combined with the Lax-Milgram theorem, and the Banach and Brouwer fixed-point theorems. In particular, Raviart-Thomas elements of order k k are used for approximating the pseudo-stress tensor, piecewise polynomials of degree ≤ k \leq k and ≤ k + 1 \leq k+1 are utilized for approximating the strain tensor and the velocity, respectively, and the heat and substance concentration are approximated by means of Lagrange finite elements of order ≤ k + 1 \leq k+1 . Optimal a priori error estimates are derived and confirmed through some numerical examples that illustrate the performance of the proposed semi-augmented mixed-primal scheme.

augmented formulation, mixed-primal finite element method,
fixed point theory, a priori error analysis.

Introduction

In nature and several technological applications, transport phenomena widely occur as a result of a combined heat and mass transfer that are driven by buoyancy effects due to both temperature and concentration variations (see, e.g.,
[
15
,
37
,
39
]
). Such processes, also known as thermosolutal or double-diffusive natural convection, involving fluid circulation in a porous media, are frequently found in astrophysics, oceanology, metallurgy, electrophysics and geophysics, but also appear in several engineering applications such as filtration processes, geothermal energy exploitation, spreading on porous substrates, bio-film growth, gasification of biomass, to name a few.

From the mathematical point of view the Darcy-Oberbeck-Boussinesq model allows to adequately describe and quantify this complex flow by means of a nonlinear partial differential equations system. More precisely, the momentum and conservation of fluid mass give rise to a Navier-Stokes/Darcy type system for describing the fluid flow in the porous media which, in turn, is coupled via buoyancy forces and convective mass and heat transfer to a vector advection-diffusion equation for describing the substance concentration and the temperature, as a result of an energy and mass transfer balance (see, e.g.,
[
37
,
39
]
).

Numerical simulations in two and three dimensions illustrate the accuracy and performance of this technique. However, the theoretical analyses of the associate continuous and the discrete variational problems as well as the convergence of the method are not carried out there, and the method only allows to carry out low-order approximations of the main unknowns. On the other hand, in
[

Focusing in double-diffusive viscous flow in porous media,
[
17
]
proposes a technique consisting of a projection-based stabilization method in the unsteady state. There, the convergence of the velocity, temperature and concentration in the semi-discrete case are derived. In addition, some numerical experiments are reported to confirm optimal order error estimates and to compare their results with previous ones. In
[
10
]
the authors construct a divergence-conforming primal scheme and establish the existence and uniqueness results for the continuous problem and the discrete scheme as well as convergence properties. On the other hand, in
[
18
]
the authors propose a high-order fully-mixed method based on the introduction of the velocity gradient, the temperature gradient and the concentration gradient as new unknowns into the problem. The resulting formulation has a saddle-point structure on reflexive Banach spaces for both the Navier-Stokes/Darcy and the thermal energy conservation equations. There, it is particularly shown that the discrete scheme is well-posed and an a priori error estimate for the Galerkin scheme is also derived under sufficiently small data. However, feasible finite element subspaces must be constructed over meshes with a macro-element structure in order to satisfy an inf-sup compatibility condition, which in turn significantly increased the computational cost, especially in the three-dimensional case.

According to the above discussion and in order to contribute with the design and analysis of new mixed finite schemes for simulating double-diffusive convection in porous media. The main goal of this work is precisely to propose a new semi-augmented mixed finite element method in which the strain tensor and a pseudo-stress tensor are introduced as primary unknowns of interest in the fluid equations and the pressure is eliminated from the system by its own definition. To avoid any inf-sup restriction, to guarantee greater flexibility regarding finite element spaces and lower computational cost than
[
18
]
, the respective variational formulation is augmented by using appropriate redundant penalization Galerkin type-terms based on the constitutive and equilibrium equations combined with a primal formulation for the heat and substance concentration equations in standard Hilbert spaces. In this way, the aforementioned strain tensor, the nonlinear pseudostress, the velocity and a vector field whose components are the temperature of the fluid and the substance concentration are the main unknowns of the resulting coupled system. Moreover, physical boundary conditions are considered. Indeed, a no-slip condition (that is, homogeneous Dirichlet condition) for the fluid velocity, a prescribed temperature and substance concentration on a Dirichlet boundary and no heat/mass flow across an isolated surface/homogeneous Neumann condition.

Concerning the solvability analysis, we proceed similarly to
[
4
,
19
]
by introducing an equivalent fixed-point setting. According to this, combining the Lax-Milgram theorem with the classical Banach and Brouwer fixed-point theorems, we establish the respective solvability of the continuous problem and the associated Galerkin scheme, under suitable regularity assumptions (see
[
7
]
, for further details), a feasible choice of parameters and, in the discrete case, for any family of finite element subspaces. To handle the non-homogeneous Dirichlet condition for the temperature and concentration, we carry out a rigorous treatment of the boundary data throughout the analysis by means of appropriate extensions involving the Scott-Zhang interpolator (in the discrete case), which allows us to establish the well-posedness of our scheme, along with its convergence result and the respective a priori error bounds.
Up to the best of our knowledge, because of the difficulties that can arise in the analysis, the physically relevant non-homogeneous Dirichlet condition case is usually either omitted or not considered; this is what motivates us to contribute in this direction as well.

A Strang-type lemma, enables us to derive the corresponding Céa estimate and to provide optimal a priori error bounds for the Galerkin solution. In turn, the pressure can be recovered by a post-processed of the discrete solutions, preserving the same rate of convergence. Finally, some numerical experiments are presented to illustrate the performance of the technique, including the unsteady case with unknown solution, to confirm the expected orders.

2
The model problem

This section introduces the mathematical model we address in the present work as well as the auxiliary unknowns that are introduced and considered in the subsequent variational formulation. Under the Oberbeck-Boussinesq approximation framework, double-diffusive natural convection phenomenon in a porous media is described in terms of a Navier-Stokes-Brinkman model coupled to a system of diffusion-advection equations. In the stationary state, the problem consists of finding the velocity
\boldsymbol{u}
, the pressure
p
, and the vector
\boldsymbol{\varphi}=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2})^{\mathrm{t}}
when
n=2
and
\boldsymbol{\varphi}=(\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2},0)^{\mathrm{t}}
when
n=3
, where
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are the temperature and the concentration fields, respectively, of a fluid in a confined porous enclosure
\Omega
, satisfying the set of equations:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\gamma\,\boldsymbol{u}-2\,\mathbf{div}\left(\nu(\boldsymbol{\varphi})\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{u})\right)+\left(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\right)\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla\,p&=&\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}\right){\boldsymbol{g}}{\quad\hbox{in}\quad}\Omega\,,\\[4.30554pt]
\mathrm{div}\,\boldsymbol{u}&=&0{\quad\hbox{in}\quad}\Omega\,,\\[4.30554pt]
-{\mathbf{div}}(\mathbb{K}\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi})\,+\,(\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi})\boldsymbol{u}&=&\mathbf{0}{\quad\hbox{in}\quad}\Omega\,,\end{array}

(2.1)

where
\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{u})
stands for the symmetric part of the velocity gradient, i.e.,
\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+(\nabla\boldsymbol{u})^{\mathrm{t}})
. The data are the gravitational force
\boldsymbol{g}
, the positive constant
\gamma
corresponding to the reciprocal of the Darcy number, the (thermal and solute) expansion coefficients vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
, and the diagonal matrix of (thermal and mass) diffusion constants
\mathbb{K}=\mathrm{diag}(k_{i})_{1\leq i\leq n}\in\mathbb{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, with
k_{i}=0
when
i=3
, which is assumed to be a uniformly positive definite tensor, which means that there exists a positive constant
k_{0}
such that

(\mathbb{K}\,\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\boldsymbol{x}\,\geq\,k_{0}\,|\boldsymbol{x}|^{2}\,\qquad\forall\,\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathrm{R}^{n}\,,\quad\mbox{with}\quad k_{0}=\min\{k_{1},k_{2},\dots,k_{n}\}\,.

(2.2)

In turn, the kinematic viscosity
\nu:\mathrm{R}^{+}\times\mathrm{R}^{+}\longrightarrow\mathrm{R}^{+}
is assumed to be a bounded and Lipschitz continuous function that might depend on both the temperature and the mass concentration. That is, we assume the existence of positive constants
\nu_{1},\,\nu_{2},
and
L_{\nu}
that satisfy

\nu_{1}\,\leq\,\nu(\boldsymbol{\phi})\,\leq\,\nu_{2}{\quad\hbox{and}\quad}|\nu(\boldsymbol{\phi})-\nu(\boldsymbol{\psi})|\,\leq\,L_{\nu}\,|\boldsymbol{\phi}-\boldsymbol{\psi}|\qquad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\phi},\,\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathrm{R}\times\mathrm{R}^{+}\,.

(2.3)

The system (
2.1
) is finally completed with a non-slip condition on the whole boundary for the velocity and physical boundary conditions for both the temperature and the concentration fields, that is

\boldsymbol{u}\,=\,\mathbf{0}\,\hskip 5.69046pt\mbox{on $\Gamma$\,,}\qquad\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varphi}\,=\,\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{{\mathrm{D}}}\,\,\,\mbox{on ${\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}$}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\displaystyle(\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi})\,{\boldsymbol{n}}=\mathbf{0}\,\,\,\mbox{on}\,\,{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}}\,,

(2.4)

where
\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\mathrm{D}}\in\mathbf{H}^{1/2}(\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}})
is a known trace function on
{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}.

Next, proceeding similarly to
[
13
]
, we introduce the strain tensor
\boldsymbol{t}:=\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{u})
as an auxiliary variable and then define

\boldsymbol{\sigma}\ :=\ 2\nu(\boldsymbol{\varphi})\boldsymbol{t}-(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{u})-\big(p+c_{\boldsymbol{u}}\big)\mathbb{I}\quad\mathrm{in}\quad\Omega\,,

(2.5)

as an additional tensorial unknown, where
c_{\boldsymbol{u}}
is a constant to be suitably defined next (see equation (
2.7
) below). Thus, noting that the incompressibility condition of the fluid
({\mathrm{div}}\,\boldsymbol{u}=0)
implies that
{\mathbf{div}}(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{u})=(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla)\boldsymbol{u}
, we get from the first relation of (
2.1
) the equilibrium equation

\gamma\boldsymbol{u}-{\mathbf{div}}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\ =\ (\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi})\,{\boldsymbol{g}}\quad\mathrm{in}\quad\Omega\,,

and taking deviatoric part in (
2.5
), we find that the constitutive relation defined by (
2.5
) can be written as

\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{d}}\ =\ 2\nu(\boldsymbol{\varphi})\boldsymbol{t}-(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{u})^{\mathrm{d}}\,{\quad\hbox{in}\quad}\,\,\Omega\,

where
\boldsymbol{t}^{\mathrm{d}}=\boldsymbol{t}
since
{\mathrm{div}}\,\boldsymbol{u}=0.
Thus, the pressure is eliminated from the system, however taking trace in (
2.5
), we find that it can be easily recovered according to the formula

p\,=\,-\dfrac{1}{n}\Big[\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}+(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{u}))\Big]\,-\,c_{\boldsymbol{u}}\,,\qquad\mbox{in}\qquad\Omega\,.

(2.6)

Now, since
p\in\mathrm{L}^{2}_{0}(\Omega)
(which as usual is clearly required for uniqueness of an eventual pressure solution to (
2.1
)), the equation (
2.6
) suggests to define

c_{\boldsymbol{u}}\,=\,-\dfrac{1}{n|\Omega|}\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{tr}(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{u})\,=\,-\dfrac{1}{n|\Omega|}\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}\,,

(2.7)

so as to get the equivalence

\int_{\Omega}p\,=\,0\qquad\mbox{if, and only if,}\qquad\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{tr}\,{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\,=0\,.

(2.8)

According to the above discusion, the system (
2.1
) and (
2.4
) equivalently reads: Find
\boldsymbol{t}
,
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
and
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
such that

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle 2\nu(\boldsymbol{\varphi})\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{d}}-(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{u})^{\mathrm{d}}\ =\ \mathbf{0}{\quad\hbox{in}\quad}\Omega\,,\quad\displaystyle\gamma\boldsymbol{u}-{\mathbf{div}}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\ =\ (\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi})\,{\boldsymbol{g}}{\quad\hbox{in}\quad}\Omega\,,\\[8.61108pt]
\boldsymbol{t}+\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{u})\ =\ \nabla\boldsymbol{u}{\quad\hbox{in}\quad}\Omega\,,\quad-{\mathbf{div}}(\mathbb{K}\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi})\,+\,(\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi})\boldsymbol{u}\ =\ \mathbf{0}{\quad\hbox{in}\quad}\Omega\,,\\[8.61108pt]
\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\ =\ \boldsymbol{0}{\quad\hbox{on}\quad}\Gamma\,,\quad\boldsymbol{\varphi}\ =\ \boldsymbol{\varphi}_{{\mathrm{D}}}{\quad\hbox{on}\quad}\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}\,,\quad(\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi})\,{\boldsymbol{n}}\ =\ \mathbf{0}{\quad\hbox{on}\quad}\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}\,,{\quad\hbox{and}\quad}\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{tr}\,{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\ =\ 0\,,\end{array}

(2.9)

where
\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}-(\nabla\boldsymbol{u})^{\mathrm{t}})
is the skew-symmetric part of the velocity gradient. We emphasize that the introduction of the variables
\boldsymbol{t}
and
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
as new unknowns in the system allows us to equivalently rewrite the Navier-Stokes-Brinkman model (first row of (
2.1
)) in terms of a first-order set of equations. Also, observe that according to (
2.6
)-(
2.8
), the zero mean value restriction of the pressure on the domain is imposed via the respective restriction on
\mathrm{tr}\,{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
in the last equation of (
2.9
). Note further that the incompressibility condition of the fluid is implicitly present via the equilibrium relation defined by
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
according to the second equation in the first row of (
2.9
).

3.1
The semi-augmented mixed-primal variational formulation

To begin with, the fact that the trace of the tensor solution
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
of system (
2.9
) has zero mean value in
\Omega
(see last equation of (
2.9
)) suggests to introduce the space

\mathbb{H}_{0}(\mathbf{div};\Omega)\ :=\ \left\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}(\mathbf{div};\Omega):\quad\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{tr}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}=0\right\}.

In addition, by their own definitions, we introduce the following space for the strain tensor
\boldsymbol{t}
, as

\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2}(\Omega)\ :=\ \Big\{\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega):\quad\boldsymbol{r}^{\mathrm{t}}=\boldsymbol{r}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\mathrm{tr}\,\boldsymbol{r}=0\Big\}.

In turn, because of the boundary conditions for the temperature and concentrations (see second and third equations of last row in (
2.9
)) we consider the closed subspace

\mathbf{H}^{1}_{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}}(\Omega)\ :=\ \Big\{\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega):\quad\boldsymbol{\psi}\big|_{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}}=\mathbf{0}\,\Big\}\,,

for which, from the generalized Poincaré inequality, we know that there exists
c_{\text{gp}}>0
, depending only on
\Omega
and
{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}
, such that

\|\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{1,\Omega}\;\leq\;c_{\text{gp}}|\boldsymbol{\psi}|_{1,\Omega}\qquad\forall\ \boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}}(\Omega)\,.

(3.1)

Now, testing the first equation from first row in (
2.9
) with
\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2}(\Omega)
, we obtain

2\int_{\Omega}\nu(\boldsymbol{\varphi})\boldsymbol{t}:\boldsymbol{r}-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{d}}:\boldsymbol{r}-\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{u})^{\mathrm{d}}:\boldsymbol{r}\ =\ 0\qquad\forall\ \boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2}(\Omega)\,.

(3.2)

At this point, we readily note that in order to bound the third term on the left hand side of (
3.2
), and thanks to the continuous injection
\boldsymbol{i}:\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbf{L}^{4}(\Omega)
(see e.g.
[
2
]
or
[
41
]
), we requiere the unknown
\boldsymbol{u}
to live in
\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega):=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega):\;\boldsymbol{v}|_{\Gamma}=\bf{0}\}
. Indeed, by applying Cauchy-Schwarz and Hölder inequalities, we deduce that there exists a positive constant
c_{1}(\Omega):=\|\boldsymbol{i}\|^{2}
, such that

\left|\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{w})^{\mathrm{d}}:\boldsymbol{r}\right|\,\leq c_{1}(\Omega)\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1,\Omega}\|\boldsymbol{r}\|_{0,\Omega}\,\quad\forall\,\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\qquad\forall\,\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{L}^{2}(\Omega)\,.

(3.3)

Next, multiplying the first equation from second row in (
2.9
) by a test function
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}(\mathbf{div};\Omega)
, integrating by parts, using the Dirichlet condition for
\boldsymbol{u}
, and the identity
\boldsymbol{t}:\boldsymbol{\tau}=\boldsymbol{t}:\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\mathrm{d}}
(since
\boldsymbol{t}
is trace-free), we get

\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{t}:\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\mathrm{d}}+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{u}):\boldsymbol{\tau}+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\ =\ 0\qquad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}(\mathbf{div};\Omega).

Likewise, the equilibrium relation associated to
{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
(second equation from first row in (
2.9
)) is written as

\gamma\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\ =\ \int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}){\boldsymbol{g}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\qquad\forall\ \boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\,,

whereas, the symmetry of the pseudo-stress tensor is imposed in an ultra-weak sense (see e.g
[
8
]
) through the identity

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{v})\ =\ 0\,\qquad\forall\,\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\,.

As for the equation associated to the temperature and concentration (second equation from second row in (
2.9
)), we simply multiply it by a test function
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}}(\Omega)
and, after integrating by parts, and employing the Neumann boundary contidions on
{\Gamma_{\mathrm{N}}}
, we find

\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{K}\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\psi}\ +\ \int_{\Omega}(\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi})\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}\ =\ 0\qquad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}}(\Omega)\,.

In this way, a preliminary weak formulation for the coupled problem (
2.9
) takes the form: Find
(\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\varphi})\in\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathbb{H}_{0}(\mathbf{div};\Omega)\times\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)
, with
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\big|_{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\mathrm{D}}
, such that

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\displaystyle{2\int_{\Omega}\nu(\boldsymbol{\varphi})\boldsymbol{t}:\boldsymbol{r}-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{d}}:\boldsymbol{r}-\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{u})^{\mathrm{d}}:\boldsymbol{r}}&=&0&\forall\,\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2}(\Omega)\,,\\[12.91663pt]
\displaystyle{\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{t}:\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\mathrm{d}}+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{u}):\boldsymbol{\tau}+\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}}&=&0&\forall\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}(\mathbf{div};\Omega)\,,\\[12.91663pt]
\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{v}):\boldsymbol{\sigma}+\gamma\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}&=&\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}){\boldsymbol{g}}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}&\forall\,\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\,,\\[12.91663pt]
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{K}\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\psi}+\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\boldsymbol{\varphi})\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}&=&0&\forall\,\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}}(\Omega)\,.\end{array}

(3.4)

In order to analyze the variational formulation (
3.4
), and similarly as in
[
13
, Section 2]
(see also
[
4
,
8
,
21
]
), we additionally augment (
3.4
) by incorporating the following residual Galerkin type-terms
coming from the constitutive and equilibrium equations for the fluid,

\begin{array}[]{rcll}\displaystyle\kappa_{1}\int_{\Omega}(\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{u})-\boldsymbol{t}):\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{v})&=&0&\forall\,\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\,,\\[17.22217pt]
\displaystyle-\kappa_{2}\gamma\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}+\kappa_{2}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}&=&\displaystyle-\kappa_{2}\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\boldsymbol{\varphi}){\boldsymbol{g}}\cdot\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}&\forall\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}(\mathbf{div};\Omega)\,,\\[17.22217pt]
\displaystyle\quad-\kappa_{3}\int_{\Omega}\left\{2\nu(\boldsymbol{\varphi})\boldsymbol{t}-\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{d}}-(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{u})^{\mathrm{d}}\right\}:\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\mathrm{d}}&=&0&\forall\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathbb{H}_{0}(\mathbf{div};\Omega)\,,\end{array}

(3.5)

where
(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2},\kappa_{3})
is a vector of positive parameters to be specified later in Section
3.3
.

Hence, letting

\vec{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\ :=\ (\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{u})\in\mathbb{H}:=\mathbb{L}_{\mathrm{tr}}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathbb{H}_{0}(\mathbf{div};\Omega)\times\mathbf{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

where
\mathbb{H}
is endowed with the natural norm

\|\vec{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\|_{\mathbb{H}}:=\Big\{\|\boldsymbol{r}\|_{0,\Omega}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\tau}\|_{\mathbf{div},\Omega}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{1,\Omega}^{2}\Big\}^{1/2},\quad\forall\,\vec{\boldsymbol{\eta}}:=(\boldsymbol{r},\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{v})\in\mathbb{H}\,,

and adding up (
3.4
) with (
3.5
), we arrive at the following semi-augmented mixed-primal formulation for the double-diffusive natural convection problem in porous media: Find
(\vec{\boldsymbol{\xi}},\boldsymbol{\varphi})\in\mathbb{H}\times\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)
, with
\boldsymbol{\varphi}\big|_{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{\mathrm{D}}
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\xi}},\vec{\boldsymbol{\eta}})+\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{u}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\xi}},\vec{\boldsymbol{\eta}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\eta}})\qquad\quad\forall\,\vec{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathbb{H},

(3.6a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\psi})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G_{\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{\psi})\qquad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}}(\Omega),

(3.6b)

where, given
\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)
and
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbf{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
,
\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
,
\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{w}}
and
\mathbf{C}
are the bilinear forms defined, respectively, as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\xi}},\vec{\boldsymbol{\eta}})&:=&\displaystyle 2\int_{\Omega}\nu(\boldsymbol{\phi})\boldsymbol{t}:\big\{\,\boldsymbol{r}-\kappa_{3}\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\mathrm{d}}\,\big\}\,+\,\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{t}:\{\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\mathrm{d}}-\kappa_{1}\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{v})\}\,-\,\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\mathrm{d}}:\big\{\boldsymbol{r}-\kappa_{3}\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\mathrm{d}}\big\}\\[12.91663pt]
&&\displaystyle+\ (1-\kappa_{2}\gamma)\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot{\mathbf{div}}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\,-\,\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\,+\,\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{u}):\boldsymbol{\tau}-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\sigma}:\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{v})\\[12.91663pt]
&&\displaystyle+\ \gamma\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,+\,\kappa_{2}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}\cdot\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\,+\,\kappa_{1}\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{u}):\mathbf{e}(\boldsymbol{v})\,,\end{array}

(3.7)

and

\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{w}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\xi}},\vec{\boldsymbol{\eta}})\ :=\ \int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{u}\otimes\boldsymbol{w})^{\mathrm{d}}:\big\{\kappa_{3}\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\mathrm{d}}-\boldsymbol{r}\big\}\,,

(3.8)

for all
\vec{\boldsymbol{\xi}},\vec{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathbb{H}
and

\mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{\chi},\boldsymbol{\psi})\ :\ =\,\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{K}\nabla\boldsymbol{\chi}:\nabla\boldsymbol{\psi}\qquad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\chi},\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)\,.

(3.9)

In turn,
F_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
and
G_{\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\phi}}
are linear functionals defined as

F_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\eta}})\ :=\ \int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}){\boldsymbol{g}}\cdot\{\boldsymbol{v}-\kappa_{2}\mathbf{div}\,\boldsymbol{\tau}\big\}\,\qquad\forall\,\vec{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathbb{H}\,,

(3.10)

and

G_{\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{\psi})\ :=\ -\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\boldsymbol{\phi})\boldsymbol{w}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}\,\qquad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}}(\Omega)\,.

(3.11)

3.2
The fixed point approach

In this Section we describe a fixed point strategy that allow us to solve the coupled problem given by (
3.6
). We start by denoting
\mathbf{H}:=\mathbf{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\times\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)
, and defining the operator
\text{\Large$\mathpzc{A}$}:\mathbf{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}
by

\text{\Large$\mathpzc{A}$}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\phi})\,=\,(\text{\Large$\mathpzc{A}$}_{1}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\phi}),\,\text{\Large$\mathpzc{A}$}_{2}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\phi}),\,\text{\Large$\mathpzc{A}$}_{3}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\phi}))\,:=\,\vec{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\quad\quad\forall\,(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\phi})\in\mathbf{H}

where
\vec{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=(\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{u})\in\mathbb{H}
is the unique solution of the augmented mixed formulation given by (
3.6a
), with
(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\phi})
instead
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\varphi})
, that is:

\begin{array}[]{rlll}\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\xi}},\vec{\boldsymbol{\eta}})\,+\,\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{w}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\xi}},\vec{\boldsymbol{\eta}})&=&F_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\vec{\boldsymbol{\eta}})&\quad\forall\,\vec{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\in\mathbb{H}\,,\end{array}

(3.12)

where the bilinear forms
\mathbf{A}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
,
\mathbf{B}_{\boldsymbol{w}}
and the functional
F_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}
are defined exactly as in (
3.7
), (
3.8
), and (
3.10
), respectively.
In addition, we also introduce the operator
\text{\Large$\mathpzc{B}$}:\mathbf{H}\rightarrow\mathbf{H}^{1}(\Omega)
defined as

\text{\Large$\mathpzc{B}$}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\phi})\,:=\,\boldsymbol{\varphi}\quad\quad\forall\,(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\phi})\in\mathbf{H},

where
\boldsymbol{\varphi}
is the unique solution of the problem (
3.6b
),
with
(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\phi})
instead
(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\varphi})
, that is:

\mathbf{C}(\boldsymbol{\varphi},\boldsymbol{\psi})\ =\ G_{\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\boldsymbol{\psi})\qquad\forall\,\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathbf{H}^{1}_{{\Gamma_{\mathrm{D}}}}(\Omega),

(3.13)

where the bilinear form
\mathbf{C}
, and the functional
G_{\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\phi}}
are defined by
(
3.9
) and (
3.11
), respectively.

In this way, by introducing the operator
\text{\large$\mathscr{L}$}:\mathbf{H}\rightarrow\mathbf{H}
as

\text{\large$\mathscr{L}$}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\phi})\,:=\,(\text{\Large$\mathpzc{A}$}_{3}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\phi}),\,\text{\Large$\mathpzc{B}$}(\text{\Large$\mathpzc{A}$}_{3}(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\phi}),\boldsymbol{\phi}))\quad\quad\forall\,(\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{\phi})\in\mathbf{H}

we readily realize that solving (
3.6
) is equivalent to seeking a fixed point of

, that is: Find

(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\varphi})\in\mathbf{H}
such that

\text{\large$\mathscr{L}$}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\varphi})\,=\,(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{\varphi})\,.

(3.15)
