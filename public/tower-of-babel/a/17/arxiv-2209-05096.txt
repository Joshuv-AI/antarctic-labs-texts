Interface roughening in nonequilibrium phase-separated systems
M. Besse, G. Fausti, M.E. Cates, B. Delamotte, C. Nardini

Abstract
Interfaces of phase-separated systems roughen in time due to capillary waves. Because of fluxes in the bulk, their dynamics is nonlocal in real space and is not described by the Edwards-Wilkinson or Kardar-Parisi-Zhang (KPZ) equations, nor their conserved counterparts. We show that in the absence of detailed balance, the phase-separated interface is described by a new universality class which we term | 𝐪 | \mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert} KPZ. We compute the associated scaling exponents via one-loop renormalization group, and corroborate the results by numerical integration of the | 𝐪 | \mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert} KPZ equation. Deriving the effective interface dynamics from a minimal field theory of active phase separation, we finally argue that the | 𝐪 | \mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert} KPZ universality class generically describes liquid-vapor interfaces in two- and three-dimensional active systems.

The roughening of interfaces is among the best-studied problems in statistical mechanics
Krug 1997
;
Barabási et al. 1995
. Early theoretical investigations
Peters et al. 1979
;
Plischke and Rácz 1984
;
Jullien and Botet 1985
were concerned with the Eden model
Eden 1958
, proposed to describe the shape of cell colonies, and with the ballistic deposition model
Family and Vicsek 1985
. Kardar, Parisi and Zhang (KPZ)
Kardar et al. 1986
discovered an important universality class for growing rough interfaces, by adding the lowest order nonlinearity to the continuum Edwards-Wilkinson (EW) model in which height fluctuations are driven by nonconserved noise and relax diffusively
Edwards and Wilkinson 1982
. Despite its fame, KPZ does not describe all roughening interfaces: in the presence of conservation laws, the KPZ nonlinearity can be forbidden and novel universality classes arise
Krug 1997
;
Sun et al. 1989
;
Caballero et al. 2018
.

A notable category of interfaces, that stands out from all these well-known universality classes, are those arising in phase-separated systems, which roughen due to thermally activated capillary waves
v. Smoluchowski 1908
;
Rowlinson and Widom 1982
;
Evans 1979
;
Wertheim 1976
;
Weeks 1977
. There, because of fluxes in the bulk phases, liquid-vapor or liquid-liquid interfaces have nonlocal dynamics in real space
Bray et al. 2001
;
Shinozaki 1993
. In the absence of fluid motion, small amplitude capillary waves with wavenumber
{\mathbf{q}}
relax by diffusion at a rate
\tau^{-1}=\sigma\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}^{3}
, with
\sigma
proportional to the interfacial tension
Bray et al. 2001
. We consider in the following phase separation in
d+1
dimensions and
we denote by
\hat{h}(\mathbf{x},t)
the height of the resulting interface above a

d
-dimensional plane. (This notation is standard for interface problems
such as KPZ.) The dynamics becomes, in Fourier variables
h({\mathbf{q}},t)
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h({\mathbf{q}},t)=-\sigma\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}^{3}h({\mathbf{q}},t)+\sqrt{2D\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}}\eta({\mathbf{q}},t),

(1)

where
\eta
is a Gaussian white noise such that

\displaystyle\langle\eta({\mathbf{q}}_{1},t_{2})\eta({\mathbf{q}}_{2},t_{2})\rangle=\delta({\mathbf{q}}_{1}+{\mathbf{q}}_{2})\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})\,.

(2)

The linear evolution equation (
1
) can be written in real space as

\partial_{t}\hat{h}=G\star\nabla^{2}\hat{h}+\eta_{R}

where
\star
stands for the spatial convolution,
\langle\eta_{R}(\mathbf{x}_{1},t_{1})\eta_{R}(\mathbf{x}_{2},t_{2})\rangle\sim 2DG(\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{2})\delta(t_{1}-t_{2})
and the kernel
G
is long ranged:
G(\mathbf{x})\sim|\mathbf{x}|^{-d-1}
at large distances.

The interface in passive phase-separated fluids must be described, at least in the stationary state, by an equation that respects detailed balance.
In this case the interface is a subset of degrees of freedom within a thermally equilibrated state of the full, phase-separated, system and hence is itself in equilibrium. The fluctuation-dissipation theorem then means that any nonlinearity that enters Eq. (
1
) is of the form
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}\,\delta\mathscr{F}_{I}/\delta h_{{\mathbf{q}}}
for some free energy functional
\mathscr{F}_{I}[h]
. A simple dimensional analysis argument then shows that there exists no nonlinearity correcting Eq. (
1
) that is relevant in the renormalization group (RG) sense for interfaces of dimension
d=1,2
. Hence the stationary dynamics of diffusive (
i.e.
, without momentum conservation) interfaces between phase-separated passive liquids is described by mean-field scaling exponents. Dimensional analysis
Täuber 2014
of Eq. (
1
) then gives
z=3
and
\chi=(z-d-1)/2
, in terms of which spatial and temporal correlations scale as
\langle\hat{h}(\mathbf{x},t)\hat{h}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t)\rangle\sim|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|^{2\chi}
and
\langle\hat{h}(\mathbf{x},t)\hat{h}(\mathbf{x},t^{\prime})\rangle\sim|t-t^{\prime}|^{2\chi/z}
, while the static structure factor
S=\langle|h({{\mathbf{q}}},t)|^{2}\rangle
scales as
S\sim|{\mathbf{q}}|^{-d-2\chi}
for small
|{\mathbf{q}}|
.

We shall show in the following that this conclusion changes in phase-separated systems that lack detailed balance. Important examples are found in active matter, where elemental units such as self-propelled colloids, bacteria, or cells extract nonthermal energy from the environment and dissipate it to self-propel
Marchetti et al. 2013
. Active liquid-vapor systems are known to display a phenomenology impossible in equilibrium
Gompper et al. 2020
; for example, phase separation arises even in the absence of any attraction among particles
Tailleur and Cates 2008
;
Fily and Marchetti 2012
;
Stenhammar et al. 2014
;
Cates and Tailleur 2015
. Moreover, steady-state currents can be present
Tjhung et al. 2018
, and phase-separated states can be sustained even when the interfacial tension is negative
Tjhung et al. 2018
;
Fausti et al. 2021
;
Bialké et al. 2015
;
Caballero and Marchetti 2022
.
In the simplest cases their large-scale interfacial properties are nonetheless similar to those in passive fluids
Cates and Tailleur 2015
;
Wittkowski et al. 2014
;
Nardini et al. 2017
;
Partridge and Lee 2019
;
Solon et al. 2018
;
Gnan and Maggi 2022
. In this situation, it was recently assessed on the basis of numerical simulations of particle models
Lee 2017
;
Sussman et al. 2018
and field-theoretical analysis
Fausti et al. 2021
that capillary wave theory holds. Indeed, some of us have recently shown that small amplitude, long wavelength capillary waves obey Eq. (
1
), although activity changes the capillary interfacial tension (and can make it negative in some parameter regimes, not considered here)
Fausti et al. 2021
.

In this Letter we show that when the underlying dynamics breaks detailed balance, as happens generically in active systems, a new universality class describes the roughening of the liquid-vapor interface. This is because a new nonlinearity, which is RGrelevant for interfacial dimension
d\leq 2
, can appear in Eq. (
1
). We study the ensuing equation, which we term
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}
KPZ, by one-loop RG analysis perturbatively for small
\varepsilon=2-d
. We compute the scaling exponents and confirm our conclusions by numerical simulations of the
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}
KPZ equation in
d=1
. We further argue that
the
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}
KPZ universality class includes the liquid-vapor interface of active systems undergoing bulk phase separation. We do so by considering Active Model B+ (AMB+)
Tjhung et al. 2018
, a minimal continuum description of active systems undergoing phase separation, and showing how
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}
KPZ emerges as the associated equation for the liquid-vapor interface. We thus predict the emergence of a new universality class for the roughening of liquid-vapor interface in two-dimensional active systems (
d+1=2
), the setup in which they are most commonly studied both theoretically
Cates and Tailleur 2015
and experimentally
Buttinoni et al. 2013
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Van Der Linden et al. 2019
.

To assess which nonlinearities might modify Eq. (
1
), let us first recall the symmetries that have to be respected. First, we should impose invariance under rotations and translations, as well as under a shift in the origin of the reference frame, which translates to
h({\mathbf{q}},t)\to h({\mathbf{q}},t)+(2\pi)^{d}\delta({\mathbf{q}})C
in Fourier space, for any
C\in\mathbb{R}
. Second, the total amounts of liquid and vapor do not change during roughening, implying that the “total height”
\int_{\mathbf{x}}\penalty\ \hat{h}
is constant. We further assume that chiral symmetry (invariance under
\mathbf{x}\to-\mathbf{x}
) is not broken. Under these symmetries, the KPZ nonlinearity is forbidden, as it does not conserve the total height. Moreover, the nonlinearities captured in existing models of conserved surface growth, such as cKPZ
Sun et al. 1989
;
Krug 1997
and cKPZ+
Caballero et al. 2018
are found to be RG irrelevant by dimensional analysis.

Inspecting Eq. (
1
) it appears that
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}
plays the role of a mobility in the linear description of capillary waves. This thus suggests to consider nonlinearities in the form

\displaystyle\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}\int_{\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{q}}_{i}={\mathbf{q}}}g({\mathbf{q}}|{\mathbf{q}}_{1},...,{\mathbf{q}}_{n})h({\mathbf{q}}_{1})...h({\mathbf{q}}_{n}),

(3)

where the integral is over
{\mathbf{q}}_{1},...,{\mathbf{q}}_{n}
with the constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{q}}_{i}={\mathbf{q}}
(nonlinearities depending on the frequencies
\omega,\omega_{1},...,\omega_{n}
could be considered as well without changing any conclusion). Note that the prefactor
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}
ensures that the total height of the interface is conserved. Assuming that
g
is analytic in all its arguments, and imposing the symmetries mentioned above, the most relevant nonlinearity that can modify Eq. (
1
) is
g({\mathbf{q}}|{\mathbf{q}}_{1},{\mathbf{q}}_{2})=\lambda_{1}i{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\cdot i{\mathbf{q}}_{2}
. This term is relevant for
d<d_{c}=2
. We are thus led to investigate the following equation, which we term
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}
KPZ:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h=-\sigma\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}^{3}h+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}\mathcal{F}[|\nabla\hat{h}|^{2}]+\sqrt{2D\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}}\eta,

(4)

where
\mathcal{F}[\cdot]
stands for the Fourier transform. It is worth noting that Eq. (
4
) differs from the KPZ equation endowed with long-range interactions
Medina et al. 1989
;
Chattopadhyay 1999
.

We next study the
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}
KPZ equation by RG analysis to one loop, perturbatively in
\varepsilon=2-d>0
. Within the Martin-Siggia-Rose formalism
Täuber 2014
, the action
S
associated with Eq. (
4
) reads

\displaystyle S=\int_{Q}\tilde{h}(-Q)\left(G_{0}^{-1}(Q)h(Q)+A(Q)-D\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}\tilde{h}(Q)\right)

(5)

where
\tilde{h}
is a response field,
Q=(\omega,{\mathbf{q}})
,
G_{0}(Q)^{-1}=-i\omega+\sigma\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}^{3}
and
A(Q)=(\lambda_{1}/2)\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}\int_{Q_{1}+Q_{2}=Q}{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{q}}_{2}h(Q_{1})h(Q_{2})
.

We thereby obtain in
sup
the following RG flow for the reduced coupling constant
\mathdutchcal{g}=D\lambda_{1}^{2}/\sigma^{3}

\displaystyle\Lambda\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathdutchcal{g}}{\mathrm{d}\Lambda}=\varepsilon\mathdutchcal{g}-\frac{3}{8}K_{2}\mathdutchcal{g}^{2},\qquad\varepsilon=2-d.

(6)

where
\Lambda
is the momentum scale,
K_{d}=S_{d}/(2\pi)^{d}
and
S_{d}=2\pi^{d/2}/\Gamma(d/2)
.
As expected from dimensional analysis, the Gaussian fixed point (
\mathdutchcal{g}^{\star}=0
)
is the only one of the RG flow for
d\geq 2
: here the mean-field exponents exactly describe the interface properties at large scales. For
d<2
, a new attractive fixed point emerges at
\mathdutchcal{g}^{\star}=8\varepsilon/(3K_{2})
. At this fixed point, the scaling exponents are

\displaystyle z=3-\frac{\varepsilon}{3},\qquad\qquad\chi=\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\,.

(7)

These exponents describe the new
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}
KPZ universality class to one loop, that is, to first order in
\varepsilon
.

To test these predictions, we numerically integrate the
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}
KPZ in
d=1

2/3

\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}

L

. The results presented were obtained with spatial discretization
\Delta x=1

\Delta t=10^{-2}

We quantified the interfacial width via

\displaystyle W^{2}(t,L)=\frac{1}{L}\int_{\mathbf{x}}\hat{h}^{2}(\mathbf{x},t),

(8)

Our final task is to evidence that the
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}
KPZ nonlinearity is indeed generically present for the dynamics of interfaces in phase-separated active systems. To do this, we consider continuum scalar field theories for active phase separation
Wittkowski et al. 2014
;
Tjhung et al. 2018
;
Thomsen et al. 2021
. These are generalizations of Model B
Hohenberg and Halperin 1977
;
Chaikin and Lubensky 2000
;
Bray et al. 2001
, the standard large-scale description of phase separation in diffusive passive fluids (without momentum conservation). Like Model B they address the dynamics of a conserved scalar density field
\phi
in
d+1
dimensions which switches steeply from positive to negative values on crossing the interface from liquid to vapor. Unlike Model B, the active versions take into account that detailed balance is broken at the microscale. The ensuing minimal theory is called AMB+
Nardini et al. 2017
;
Tjhung et al. 2018
, and includes all terms that break detailed balance up to order
\mathcal{O}(\nabla^{4},\phi^{2})

Tjhung et al. 2018
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{J}+\sqrt{2DM}\bm{\Lambda}\right),

(9)

\displaystyle{\bf J}/M

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nabla\mu_{\lambda}+\zeta(\nabla^{2}\phi)\nabla\phi,

(10)

\displaystyle\mu_{\lambda}[\phi]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathscr{F}}{\delta\phi}+\lambda|\nabla\phi|^{2}.

(11)

Here
\mathscr{F}=\int d\mathbf{r}\,\left[f(\phi)+\frac{K(\phi)}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}\right]
,
f(\phi)
is a double-well local free energy,
and
\bm{\Lambda}
is a vector of zero-mean, unit-variance, Gaussian white noises. Standard Model B is recovered at vanishing activity
(
\lambda=\zeta=0
), unit mobility
M=1
and constant noise level
D

Hohenberg and Halperin 1977
. Here we shall retain the choice
M=1
, constant
D
and further assume for simplicity that
K
is also constant. Our analysis below assumes that the interfacial tensions determining the Ostwald process
Tjhung et al. 2018
and the relaxation of capillary waves
Fausti et al. 2021
, which in active systems can differ, are both positive so that the system undergoes bulk phase separation rather than microphase separation.

The linear description of capillary waves, Eq. (
1
), has been classically derived for passive fluids starting from Model B
Bray et al. 2001
and, more recently, for active fluids starting from AMB+
Fausti et al. 2021
. This is done by assuming that
\phi
evolves quasistatically with respect to fluctuations of the interfacial height, equivalent to an
ansatz

\phi(\mathbf{x},y,t)=\varphi(y-\hat{h}(\mathbf{x},t))
, which is exact at leading order in
h
and
{\mathbf{q}}

Fausti et al. 2021
. As detailed in
sup
, we extend this procedure to obtain the nonlinear terms that correct Eq. (
1
):

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\int_{{\mathbf{q}}_{1},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{1}}(-1)^{n}\frac{(\hat{h}(\mathbf{x})-\hat{h}(\mathbf{x}_{1}))^{n}}{2n!}\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\right\rvert}^{n}\left\{A_{n}({\mathbf{q}}_{1})\partial_{t}\hat{h}(\mathbf{x})+\zeta D_{n}({\mathbf{q}}_{1})\mathbf{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}\hat{h}\right\}e^{-i{\mathbf{q}}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{1}-i{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{x}_{1}+i{\mathbf{q}}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{x}}=-\sigma_{\lambda}{\mathbf{q}}^{2}h_{\mathbf{q}}+\chi_{\mathbf{q}},

(12)

where the Stratonovich convention is used, expressions for the
h
-independent factors
A_{n}({\mathbf{q}}),D_{n}({\mathbf{q}}),\sigma_{\lambda}
are given in
sup
, and
\chi_{\mathbf{q}}
can be found as a sum of Gaussian noises
sup
.
A_{n}({\mathbf{q}})
and
D_{n}({\mathbf{q}})
have been defined to make clear the reading of the dimension of the nonlinearities, given the fact that the dimension of
A_{n}({\mathbf{q}})\partial_{t}\hat{h}(\mathbf{x})
and
D_{n}({\mathbf{q}})\mathbf{\nabla}_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}\hat{h}
is at least
\mathcal{O}(\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}^{2}\hat{h})
. To leading order in
h
and
{\mathbf{q}}
, Eq. (
12
) reduces to Eq. (
1
), where the interfacial tension
\sigma
is proportional to the capillary waves interfacial tension
\sigma_{\rm cw}

Fausti et al. 2021
.

Notably, the
\lambda_{1}
-term of Eq. (
4
) is not present at bare level in Eq. (
12
). Furthermore, Eq. (
12
) contains nonlinearities in the form of Eq. (
3
) with
g
singular. In order to perform the RG analysis of Eq. (
12
), we transform it to the Ito convention in
sup
, following a standard procedure
Gardiner et al. 1985
;
Lau and Lubensky 2007
;
Cates et al. 2022
. We then find the canonical dimension of the nonlinear terms and show that the singular nonlinearities in Eq. (
12
) are all RG irrelevant
sup
. Moreover, by computing one-loop diagrams we further show in
sup
that
\lambda_{1}\neq 0
is generated under RG from the nonlinearities of Eq. (
12
). These results strongly suggest that the liquid-vapor interface of AMB+ belongs to the
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}
KPZ universality class. However a complete proof would require us to derive the analog of Eq. (
12
) allowing for a dependence of
\phi
on interfacial curvature, and then show that no relevant singular nonlinearity is generated by the associated RG flow. This goes beyond the scope of this Letter. Preliminary numerical simulations of AMB+, which will be presented elsewhere, do however indicate that the interface roughens accordingly to
W^{2}(t)\sim t^{0.28}
as expected from the
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}
KPZ universality class.

In conclusion, we have introduced a minimal field theory, termed the
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}
KPZ equation, to describe the roughening of interfaces in nonequilibrium phase-separated systems lacking momentum conservation. The
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}
KPZ equation differs from the standard description of roughening interfaces (either EW, KPZ or their conserved counterparts) because diffusive fluxes in the bulk cause the interfacial dynamics to be nonlocal in real space. We discovered a nontrivial fixed point of the RG flow for interfacial dimension
d<2
. This should control active, phase-separated interfaces in
d+1=2
bulk dimensions. We characterized this new universality class, computing its scaling exponents by one-loop RG and numerical simulations.
We finally gave evidence that the
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}
KPZ class includes interfacial roughening in phase-separated active systems, by explicitly deriving the effective interface equation from a scalar active field theory for the particle density in
d+1
bulk dimensions, and showing that the
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert{\mathbf{q}}\right\rvert}
KPZ nonlinearity thereby emerges.

Previous studies of roughening of the liquid-vapor interface in active particle models concluded that
z\sim 2
as in the Edwards-Wilkinson universality class
Wysocki et al. 2016
;
Lee 2017
;
Patch et al. 2018
. We speculate that such disagreement might arise if these particle models undergo bubbly phase separation
Tjhung et al. 2018
instead of bulk phase separation as addressed here. (Notably, in
Patch et al. 2018
, vapor bubbles are indeed visible in the liquid phase). Local events in which a vapor bubble “pops” through the interface do not conserve the total height
\int_{\mathbf{x}}\penalty\ \hat{h}
, and also create overhangs, evading our description – at least on scales smaller than the largest bubbles present. More work is needed to clarify this aspect. Beyond active systems, our results might also describe the roughening of interfaces in other nonequilibrium phase-separating systems, such as granular materials
Oyarte et al. 2013
.
