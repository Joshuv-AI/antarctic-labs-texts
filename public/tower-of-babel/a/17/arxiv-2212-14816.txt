Statistics of small prime quadratic non-residues
Efthymios Sofos

Abstract
. We prove that the average of the k k -th smallest prime quadratic non-residue modulo a prime approximates the 2 ​ k 2k -th smallest prime.

1.
Introduction

What is the expected size of the
k
-th smallest quadratic non-residue modulo
p
?
This question is interesting only when we exclude certain obvious choices. For example,
all small integer multiples of the first quadratic non-residue
are quadratic non-residues.
For this reason we define
n_{k}(p)
to be the
k
-th smallest
prime

quadratic non-residue modulo
p
.
A well-known
notorious problem of Vinogradov
[
10
]

regards upper bounds for
n_{k}(p)
:
he conjectured that
n_{k}(p)=O_{k}(p^{\varepsilon})
for all
\varepsilon>0
.
For
k=1
this is known conditionally on the validity of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis,
see the work of Lamzouri–Li–Soundararajan
[
8
]
.
Unconditionally, the problem is wide open. The best result in this direction is due to Burgess
[
4
]
,
who proved deep bounds for short character sums to deduce that
n_{1}(p)=O(p^{\varepsilon})
holds for all
\varepsilon>\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}}
.
His work was extended to all
k
by
Banks and Guo
[
2
]
; remarkably, they achieve a bound of the same quality for all
k\geqslant 1
.
Another related work is due to Bourgain and Lindenstraus
[
3
, Theorem 5.1]
,
who produced many prime quadratic non-residues below
p

by exploiting connections to Quantum Unique Ergodicity.

In this work we investigate the average value of
n_{k}
. Erdős
[
5
]
proved that
n_{1}
has constant average;
we extend this to all
n_{k}
. Let
p_{n}
be the
n
-th smallest prime
and
\pi(x)
denote the number of primes
p\leqslant x
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{\pi(x)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{ prime }p\\
p\leqslant x\end{subarray}}n_{k}(p)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N},m\geqslant k}\frac{p_{m}}{2^{m}}\frac{(m-1)!}{(m-k)!(k-1)!}.

Furthermore,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{m\geqslant k}\frac{p_{m}}{2^{m}}\frac{(m-1)!}{(m-k)!(k-1)!}}{p_{2k}}=1.

Remark 1.2
.

When combined the two statements say that, on average,
the
k
-th smallest prime quadratic non-residue approximates the
2k
-th prime.

Theorem
1.1
will follow from a more general statement (Theorem
3.1
), which
shows that for any function
f:\mathbb{N}^{k}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying only
a growth condition,

f(n_{1}(p),\ldots,n_{k}(p))

has finite average. This allows us to study the joint distribution of
n_{1}(p)
and
n_{2}(p)
which is relevant
to upcoming work of
Languasco and Moree
on quadratic residue bias of the divisor function.

Theorem 1.3
.

For every
z>1

we have

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\sharp\{\text{prime }p\leqslant x:n_{2}(p)>zn_{1}(p)\}}{\pi(x)}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}2^{-n(m,z)},

where
n(m,z)
is defined as the largest integer
n
for which
p_{n}\leqslant zp_{m}
.

Remark 1.4
.

The inequality
n(m,z)\geqslant m
shows that the sum over
m
in Theorem
1.3

is rapidly convergent and it allows a fast numerical approximation. For
z=3/2
the first
13
terms
give the first
3
correct digits. Precisely,

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\sharp\left\{\text{prime }p\leqslant x:n_{2}(p)\leqslant\frac{3}{2}n_{1}(p)\right\}}{\pi(x)}=0.350\ldots\ .

The function
M(p)=\min\{n_{1}(p),n_{2}(p)-n_{1}(p)\}
plays a special rôle in the
upcoming work of
Languasco and Moree.
We study its average and largest value.

Theorem 1.5
.

We have

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{\pi(x)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{ prime }p\\
p\leqslant x\end{subarray}}M(p)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}\frac{\min\{p_{m},p_{k}-p_{m}\}}{2^{k}}=2.504\ldots\ .

Remark 1.6
.

By definition one always has
M(p)\geqslant 2
and it is
somewhat surprising that the average of
M(p)
is so close to its minimum.
This may be explained by
combining two facts: first that for the majority of primes,
M(p)
equals
n_{1}

and, secondly, that the average of
n_{1}
is smaller than the average of
n_{2}-n_{1}
.
To see the first point we use the case
z=2
of Theorem
1.3
together with Remark
1.4
to see that

M(p)=\begin{cases}n_{1}(p),&\text{with probability }0.540\ldots\ ,\\
n_{2}(p)-n_{1}(p),&\text{with probability }0.459\ldots\ .\end{cases}

For the second point, we use the cases
k=1
and
k=2
of Theorem
1.1
.
They show that the average of
n_{1}
and
n_{2}-n_{1}
is

\sum_{m\geqslant 1}\frac{p_{m}}{2^{m}}=3.674\ldots\ \ \text{ and }\ \ -1+\sum_{m\geqslant 2}\frac{p_{m}}{2^{m}}(m-2)=4.352\ldots

respectively.

By Theorem
1.1
with
k=1,2
we know that
M(p)
has finite average, hence, by Markov’s inequality
we see that for any fixed
c>0
one has

\frac{\sharp\{\text{prime }p\leqslant x:M(p)>c\log p\}}{\pi(x)}\to 0,\ \ \textrm{ as }x\to\infty.

In other words,
M(p)>c\log p
with
0
probability.
Our next result shows that there are infinitely many exceptions:

Theorem 1.7
.

Fix any
0<c<1/10
. Then the inequality

M(p)>c\log p

holds for infinitely many primes
p
.
Conditionally on the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis there exists
c_{0}>0

such that

M(p)>c_{0}(\log p)(\log\log p)

holds for infinitely many primes
p
.

To prove the conditional bound we follow the proof of Montgomery
[
9
, Theorem 13.5]
quite closely; his proof
regards the inequality

n_{1}(p)\geqslant c_{0}(\log p)(\log\log p)
; our results includes his.

Although we shall not prove it,
the logarithm lower bounds are tight under GRH:
Ankeny
[
1
]

showed that
n_{1}(p)=O((\log p)^{2})
under GRH
and his proof can be modified to show
n_{k}(p)=O((\log p)^{2})
for all fixed
k
.
This would show that under GRH one has

M(p)=O((\log p)^{2}).

2.
Preparatory lemmas

Lemma 2.1

(Using Siegel–Walfisz’s theorem)
.

Assume that for each prime
p_{k}
we are given
\varepsilon_{k}\in\{1,-1\}
.
Fix any constant
A>0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}.

Then for all
x\geqslant 2
we have

\sharp\left\{\textrm{prime }p\leqslant x:\forall 1\leqslant k\leqslant n\Rightarrow\left(\frac{p_{k}}{p}\right)=\varepsilon_{k}\right\}=\frac{\pi(x)}{2^{n}}+O_{A}\left(\frac{p_{1}\cdots p_{n}x}{(\log x)^{A}}\right),

where the implied constant depends at most on
A
.

Proof.

Let
q=8\prod_{j=2}^{n}p_{j}
. We will show that there exists
\mathscr{S}\subset(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{*}
of cardinality
\varphi(q)2^{-n}
,
such that a prime
p>p_{n}
satisfies the
\varepsilon_{k}
-conditions
if and only if

p\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)\in\mathscr{S}
.
If
p\equiv 1\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right)
then by quadratic reciprocity the conditions are equivalent to

\left(\frac{2}{p}\right)=\varepsilon_{1},1<i\leqslant n\Rightarrow\left(\frac{p}{p_{i}}\right)=\varepsilon_{i}.

Clearly the solubility for
p
is periodic modulo
q
.
Furthermore, there are exactly exactly
\prod_{j=2}^{n}\frac{p_{j}-1}{2}
such solutions
\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)
,
since for each odd prime
p_{i}
exactly half elements of
\mathbb{F}_{p_{i}}^{*}
are squares.
In the remaining case
p\equiv 3\left(\textnormal{mod}\ 4\right)
one ends up with the conditions

\left(\frac{2}{p}\right)=\varepsilon_{1},1<i\leqslant n\Rightarrow\left(\frac{p}{p_{i}}\right)=(-1)^{\frac{p_{i}-1}{2}}\varepsilon_{i}

and the same considerations apply. The total number of solutions in
(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{*}
is

2\prod_{j=2}^{n}\frac{p_{j}-1}{2}=\varphi(q)2^{-n}.

Therefore,

(2.1)

\sharp\left\{\textrm{prime }p\leqslant x:\forall 1\leqslant k\leqslant n\Rightarrow\left(\frac{p_{k}}{p}\right)=\varepsilon_{k}\right\}=\sum_{t\in\mathscr{S}}\sharp\{p\leqslant x:p\equiv t\left(\textnormal{mod}\ q\right)\}.

Using the Siegel–Walfisz in the form
[
7
, Eq. (5.77)]

we obtain

\sum_{t\in\mathscr{S}}\left(\frac{\pi(x)}{\varphi(q)}+O_{A}\left(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{A}}\right)\right)

and the proof concludes by using
\sharp\mathscr{S}=\varphi(q)2^{-n}
. ∎

Lemma 2.2

(Using Brun–Titchmarsch’s inequality)
.

Assume that for each prime
p_{k}
we are given
\varepsilon_{k}\in\{1,-1\}
.
Then for all
x\geqslant 2
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
with
p_{1}\ldots p_{n}\leqslant x^{9/10}

we have

\sharp\left\{\textrm{prime }p\leqslant x:\forall 1\leqslant k\leqslant n\Rightarrow\left(\frac{p_{k}}{p}\right)=\varepsilon_{k}\right\}=O\left(\frac{\pi(x)}{2^{n}}\right),

where the implied constant is absolute.

Proof.

Let Let
q=8\prod_{j=2}^{n}p_{j}
.
The condition

p_{1}\ldots p_{n}\leqslant x^{9/10}

ensures that
q\leqslant x^{99/100}
, hence, injecting
[
7
, Eq. (6.95)]

into (
2.1
)
yields

\ll\sum_{t\in\mathscr{S}}\frac{\pi(x)}{\varphi(q)},

with an absolute implied constant.
The proof concludes by using that
\sharp\mathscr{S}=\varphi(q)2^{-n}
.
∎

Lemma 2.3

(Using Linnik’s large sieve)
.

Let
x\geqslant z\geqslant 2
and
q\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then the number of primes
p\leqslant x
for which
\left(\frac{\ell}{p}\right)=1
for all primes
\ell
except those dividing
q

is

\ll\frac{\sharp\{1\leqslant n\leqslant x^{2}:\gcd(n,q)=1\}}{\sharp\{1\leqslant n\leqslant x^{2}:\gcd(n,q)=1,p\mid n\Rightarrow p\leqslant z\}},

where the implied constant is absolute.

Proof.

This is a variation of the proof given in
[
7
, Th. 7.16]
. We let

\mathscr{M}=\left\{1\leqslant n\leqslant x^{2}:\gcd(n,q)=1\right\},\mathscr{P}=\left\{p\leqslant x:\ell\leqslant z,\ell\nmid q\Rightarrow\left(\frac{\ell}{p}\right)=1\right\},Q=x,

where
\ell
denotes a prime.
Then the argument in
[
7
, Th. 7.16]
works in our setting and yields the upper bound
\ll\sharp\mathscr{M}/\sharp\mathscr{P}

for the number of
n\in\mathscr{M}
that are quadratic residues modulo every
p\in\mathscr{P}
.
To conclude the proof note that if an integer
n\leqslant x^{2}
is coprime to
q
and has all its prime divisors in the interval
[1,z]
,
then for every
p\in\mathscr{P}
one has
(\frac{n}{p})=1
by the multiplicativity of the quadratic symbol.
∎

Lemma 2.4

(Smooth numbers; Hildebrand
[
6
]
)
.

Fix any
\beta>1
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then for all
x\geqslant 2
and
all
q\in\mathbb{N}
with at most
k
distinct prime divisors we have

\sharp\left\{n\leqslant x:\gcd(n,q)=1,p\mid n\Rightarrow p\leqslant(\log x)^{\beta}\right\}\gg x^{1-\frac{2}{\beta}},

where the implied constant depends at most on
\beta
and
k
.

Proof.

The special
case
q=1
is due to Hildebrand
[
6
]
and one can deduce from this the general case as follows:
letting
Q
be the square-free number composed of all prime divisors of
q
that are
\leqslant(\log x)^{\beta}

we can write the quantity in the lemma as

\sum_{d\mid Q}\mu(d)\sharp\{1\leqslant n\leqslant x/d:p\mid n\Rightarrow p\leqslant(\log x)^{\beta}\}.

Note that
x/(\log x)^{\beta}<x/d\leqslant x
since
d\leqslant Q\leqslant(\log x)^{k\beta}
. Hence, we obtain

\sum_{d\mid Q}\mu(d)\left(\frac{x}{d}\right)^{1-1/\beta+o(1)}=x^{1-1/\beta+o(1)}\prod_{p\mid q,p\leqslant(\log x)^{\beta}}(1-p^{-1+1/\beta+o(1)}),

which is
\geqslant x^{1-2/\beta}2^{-k}\gg_{k}x^{1-2/\beta}.

This is sufficient. ∎

Lemma 2.5

(Bounding
n_{1}(p)
; Burgess
[
4
]
)
.

For any fixed constant
c>\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}}

we have

n_{1}(p)=O(p^{c})
.

Lemma 2.6

(Burgess bound for
n_{k}(p)
; Banks–Guo
[
2
]
)
.

For any fixed
k\geqslant 2
and any fixed constant
c>\frac{1}{4\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}}

we have

n_{k}(p)=O_{k}(p^{c})
.

Lemma 2.7
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N},n\geqslant k}{n\choose k}2^{-n}=2.

Furthermore,

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N},n>3k}n{n\choose k}2^{-n}\ll\left(\frac{29}{32}\right)^{k}

with an absolute implied constant.

Proof.

The first equation can be obtained by letting
x=1/2
in

\frac{x^{k}}{(1-x)^{k+1}}=\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}{n\choose k}x^{n}

that can be proved by differentiating
k+1
times the power series for
1/(1-x)
around
x=0
.
For the second statement we note that if
n>3k
then
n\geqslant 4
, hence,

\frac{(n+1){n+1\choose k}2^{-n-1}}{n{n\choose k}2^{-n}}=\frac{1+\frac{1}{n}}{2(1-\frac{k}{n+1})}\leqslant\frac{1+\frac{1}{n}}{2(1-\frac{1}{3})}=\frac{3}{4}\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)\leqslant\frac{3}{4}\left(1+\frac{1}{4}\right)=\frac{15}{16}.

By induction we can then obtain the following for
n>3k
,

n{n\choose k}2^{-n}\leqslant\frac{15}{16}(n-1){n-1\choose k}2^{-(n-1)}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\left(\frac{15}{16}\right)^{n-3k-1}(3k+1){3k+1\choose k}2^{-3k-1}.

By Stirling’s approximation for the factorial we obtain

(3k+1){3k+1\choose k}=(3k+1)\frac{(3k+1)!}{k!(2k+1)!}\ll\frac{(3k+1)^{3k}\sqrt{k}}{k^{k}(2k+1)^{2k}}=\frac{3^{3k}(1+1/3k)^{3k}\sqrt{k}}{2^{2k}(1+1/2k)^{2k}}\ll\frac{3^{3k}\sqrt{k}}{2^{2k}}.

Hence for
n>3k
we have

n{n\choose k}2^{-n}\ll\left(\frac{15}{16}\right)^{n-3k-1}\frac{3^{3k}\sqrt{k}}{2^{2k}}2^{-3k}\ll\left(\frac{15}{16}\right)^{n-3k-1}\left(\frac{29}{32}\right)^{k}

with an absolute implied constant.
Therefore,

\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N},n>3k}n{n\choose k}2^{-n}\ll\sum_{n\geqslant 3k+1}\left(\frac{15}{16}\right)^{n-3k-1}\left(\frac{29}{32}\right)^{k}\leqslant\left(\frac{29}{32}\right)^{k}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{15}{16}\right)^{t}\ll\left(\frac{29}{32}\right)^{k}.

This is sufficient. ∎

Lemma 2.8
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
c>0
and assume that

f:\mathbb{N}^{k}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfies

\max_{t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\leqslant x}|f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})|=O\left(x^{c}\right)

for all
x\geqslant 1
. Then

(2.2)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\\
1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\leqslant M\end{subarray}}|f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})|=O((\log M)^{c}M^{c+k})

and

(2.3)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\\
1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\\
m_{k}>M\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})|}{2^{m_{k}}}=O((3/4)^{M})

hold for all
M>1

with the implied constants depending at most on
c
and
k
.

Proof.

First we note that
|f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})|=O(p_{m_{k}}^{c})
, hence, letting
m:=m_{k}
, we obtain the bound

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\\
1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\leqslant M\end{subarray}}|f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})|\ll\sum_{1\leqslant m\leqslant M}p_{m}^{c}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{k-1})\in\mathbb{N}^{k-1}\\
1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k-1}<m\end{subarray}}1\leqslant\sum_{1\leqslant m\leqslant M}p_{m}^{c}m^{k-1}.

By the Prime Number Theorem we have
p_{m}\ll m\log m
, hence, this is

\ll\sum_{m\leqslant M}m^{c+k-1}(\log m)^{c}\ll(\log M)^{c}M^{c+k}.

This proves the first assertion. To prove the second a similar argument yields

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\\
1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\\
m_{k}>M\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})|}{2^{m_{k}}}\ll\sum_{m>M}\frac{m^{c+k-1}(\log m)^{c}}{2^{m}}\ll\sum_{m>M}\frac{m^{c+k}}{2^{m}}.

Using
m^{c+k}=O((3/2)^{m})
shows that
the right-hand side is
O((3/4)^{M})
.
∎

3.
The main theorem

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
assume that we are given any function
f:\mathbb{N}^{k}\to\mathbb{C}
such that

(3.1)

\max_{1\leqslant t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\leqslant x}|f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})|=O\left(x^{4\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}-\varepsilon}\right)

for some constant
\varepsilon>0
. The exponent
4\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}
allows to control the size of
f
at the first prime quadratic non-residues.
Any improvement on the exponent in Lemma
2.5

will allow to relax assumption (
3.1
) in what follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume that
f:\mathbb{N}^{k}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfies (
3.1
).
We have

\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{\pi(x)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\textrm{prime }p\\
p\leqslant x\end{subarray}}f(n_{1}(p),\ldots,n_{k}(p))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\\
1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\end{subarray}}\frac{f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})}{2^{m_{k}}}.

Proof.

We write the sum over
p

in the theorem as

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\\
1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\end{subarray}}f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})\sharp\{p\leqslant x:1\leqslant i\leqslant k\Rightarrow n_{i}(p)=p_{m_{i}}\}

and we split the sum according to the size of the largest prime as follows:

(1)

p_{m_{k}}\leqslant A\log\log x
,

(2)

A\log\log x<p_{m_{k}}\leqslant\frac{\log x}{5},

(3)

\frac{\log x}{5}<p_{m_{k}}\leqslant(\log x)^{\beta},

(4)

p_{m_{k}}>(\log x)^{\beta}
,

where
A
and
\beta
are constants that both strictly
exceed
1
and that will be specified later.
Letting
n
be the largest integer with
p_{n}\leqslant A\log\log x
and using Lemma
2.1
,
the contribution of the first case is

\sum_{1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\leqslant n}f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})\left(\frac{\pi(x)}{2^{m_{k}}}+O\left(\frac{\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m_{k}}p_{j}}{(\log x)^{A\log 3}}\cdot\frac{x}{(\log x)^{2A}}\right)\right).

The prime number theorem shows that
p_{1}\cdots p_{t}\leqslant 3^{p_{t}}
holds for all large
t
, hence,

\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m_{k}}p_{j}\leqslant p_{1}\cdots p_{n}\leqslant 3^{p_{n}}\leqslant 3^{A\log\log x}=(\log x)^{A\log 3}.

The contribution then becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\leqslant n}f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})\left(\frac{\pi(x)}{2^{m_{k}}}+O\left(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{2A}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\pi(x)

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\leqslant n}\frac{f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})}{2^{m_{k}}}+O\left(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{2A}}\sum_{1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\leqslant n}|f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})|\right).

By (
2.2
) one gets

\sum_{1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\leqslant n}|f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})|\ll(\log n)^{4\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}}n^{4\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}+k}\ll n^{12+k}\ll(\log\log x)^{12+k}\ll(\log x)^{A}.

Therefore, the first case contributes

\displaystyle\pi(x)\sum_{1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\leqslant n}\frac{f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})}{2^{m_{k}}}+O\left(\frac{x}{(\log x)^{A}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\leqslant n}\frac{f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})}{2^{m_{k}}}\right)\pi(x)+o(\pi(x))

due to
\pi(x)\sim x/\log x
and our assumption
A>1
. By (
2.3
) the sum over
m_{i}
converges absolutely, hence,
the restriction
m_{k}\leqslant n
can be removed at the cost of an admissible error.
In particular, the first case contributes

\left(\sum_{1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}}\frac{f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})}{2^{m_{k}}}\right)\pi(x)(1+o(1)).

It now remains to show that all the other cases contribute
o(\pi(x))
.

Let us next deal with Case
(2)
. If
p_{t}\leqslant\frac{\log x}{5}
we obtain
p_{1}\cdots p_{t}\leqslant 3^{p_{t}}\leqslant x^{99/100}
, hence,
Lemma
2.2
yields

(3.2)

\sharp\{p\leqslant x:1\leqslant i\leqslant k\Rightarrow n_{i}(p)=p_{m_{i}}\}\ll\frac{\pi(x)}{2^{m_{k}}}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\\
A\log\log x<p_{m_{k}}\leqslant\frac{\log x}{5}\end{subarray}}f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})\sharp\{p\leqslant x:1\leqslant i\leqslant k\Rightarrow n_{i}(p)=p_{m_{i}}\}

\displaystyle\ll\pi(x)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\\
A\log\log x<p_{m_{k}}\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})|}{2^{m_{k}}}.

This is
o(\pi(x))
as the sum over
m_{k}
is the tail of an absolutely convergent series due to (
2.3
).

Let us now move to Case
(3)
.
Every prime
\ell\leqslant\frac{\log x}{5}
that is not in the set
\{p_{m_{1}},p_{m_{2}},\ldots,p_{m_{k-1}}\}

must be a quadratic-residue modulo
p
for each
p
in Case
(3)
. Hence,
denoting by
r
the largest integer such that
p_{r}\leqslant\frac{\log x}{5}
, this means that

j\in\{1,2\ldots,r\}\setminus\{m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{k-1}\}\Rightarrow\left(\frac{p_{j}}{p}\right)=1.

Thus, applying Lemma
2.2

we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\\
\frac{\log x}{5}<p_{m_{k}}\leqslant(\log x)^{\beta}\end{subarray}}|f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})|\sharp\{p\leqslant x:1\leqslant i\leqslant k\Rightarrow n_{i}(p)=p_{m_{i}}\}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\frac{\pi(x)}{2^{r-k}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\\
\frac{\log x}{5}<p_{m_{k}}\leqslant(\log x)^{\beta}\end{subarray}}|f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})|\ll\frac{\pi(x)}{2^{r}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\\
m_{k}\leqslant(\log x)^{\beta}\end{subarray}}|f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})|,

with an implied constant depending at most on
k
. By (
2.2
)
the sum over
m_{i}
is

\ll(\log\log x)^{4\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}}(\log x)^{\beta(4\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}+k)}\ll(\log x)^{\beta(7+k)}.

Using the fact that
p_{r}\sim r\log r

and
p_{r}\sim\frac{\log x}{5}
we infer that
r\sim\frac{\log x}{5\log\log x}
.
In particular,
(\log x)^{\beta(7+k)}\leqslant 2^{r/2}
for all large
x\geqslant 2
, hence,

\frac{\pi(x)}{2^{r}}(\log x)^{\beta(8+k)}\ll\frac{\pi(x)}{2^{r/2}}=o(\pi(x)).

To deal with Case
(4)
we use Lemmas
2.5
-
2.6
as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k}\\
p_{m_{k}}>(\log x)^{\beta}\end{subarray}}|f(p_{m_{1}},\ldots,p_{m_{k}})|\sharp\{p\leqslant x:1\leqslant i\leqslant k\Rightarrow n_{i}(p)=p_{m_{i}}\}

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\max\left\{|f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})|:1\leqslant t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}\leqslant x^{\frac{(1+\varepsilon)}{4\sqrt{\mathrm{e}}}}\right\}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant m_{1}<\ldots<m_{k-1}\in\mathbb{N}}\sharp\{p\leqslant x:n_{k}(p)>(\log x)^{\beta},n_{i}(p)=p_{m_{i}}\forall 1\leqslant i\leqslant k-1\}.

By assumption (
3.1
) the maximum is
O(x^{1-\varepsilon^{2}})
, whereas
Lemma
2.3
with
q:=p_{m_{1}}\cdots p_{m_{k-1}}
shows that
the overall
contribution is

\ll\frac{x^{3-\varepsilon^{2}}}{\sharp\{n\leqslant x^{2}:\gcd(n,q)=1,p\mid n\Rightarrow p\leqslant(\log z)^{\beta}\}}.

Alluding to Lemma
2.4

shows that this is
\ll x^{1-\varepsilon^{2}+10/\beta}
. Finally, the proof concludes by taking
\beta=20/\varepsilon^{2}
.

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Taking
f(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})=t_{k}
in Theorem

proves that as

\frac{1}{\pi(x)}\sum_{p\leqslant x}n_{k}(p)\ \to\mu_{k}:=\sum_{n\geqslant k}\frac{p_{n}}{2^{n}}\sharp\{\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{N}^{k-1}:1\leqslant t_{1}<t_{2}<\ldots<t_{k-1}\leqslant n-1\}.

We can simplify this to

\mu_{k}=\sum_{n\geqslant k}\frac{p_{n}}{2^{n}}{n-1\choose k-1}=k\sum_{n\geqslant k}\frac{p_{n}}{n}{n\choose k}2^{-n}.

It now
remains to
prove the asymptotic
\mu_{k}\sim p_{2k}
, which is equivalent to

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\mu_{k}}{k\log k}=2

by the Prime Number Theorem. By the Prime Number Theorem we have

p_{n}\sim n\log n\leqslant n^{2}
for all large
n
, hence,

\sum_{n>3k}\frac{p_{n}}{n(\log k)}{n\choose k}2^{-n}\leqslant\sum_{n>3k}n{n\choose k}2^{-n}\ll\left(\frac{29}{32}\right)^{k}=o(1)

by Lemma
2.8
. We deduce

(4.1)

\frac{\mu_{k}}{k(\log k)}=\sum_{n\in[k,3k]}\frac{p_{n}}{n(\log k)}{n\choose k}2^{-n}+o(1).

Let us
fix an arbitrary

\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
.
Since
p_{n}\sim n\log n
we know that
for all sufficiently large
k
and all
n\in[k,3k]

one has

1-\varepsilon\leqslant\frac{p_{n}}{n(\log k)}\leqslant 1+\varepsilon.

Therefore,

(1-\varepsilon)\sum_{n\in[k,3k]}{n\choose k}2^{-n}\leqslant\frac{1}{\log k}\sum_{n\in[k,3k]}\frac{p_{n}}{n}{n\choose k}2^{-n}\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{n\in[k,3k]}{n\choose k}2^{-n}.

Combining the two statements in Lemma
2.8

shows that as
k\to\infty
one has

\sum_{n\in[k,3k]}{n\choose k}2^{-n}=2+o(1).

This implies that for all sufficiently large
k
one has

2(1-2\varepsilon)\leqslant\frac{1}{\log k}\sum_{n\in[k,3k]}\frac{p_{n}}{n}{n\choose k}2^{-n}\leqslant 2(1+2\varepsilon).

By (
4.1
) this means that

\mu_{k}\sim 2k\log k
, which is sufficient.

4.4.
Proof of Theorem
1.7

We first prove the unconditional lower bound.
For
y\geqslant 2
we let
p_{m}
be the largest prime with

and
p_{n}

\gcd(t,q)=1

2.9
