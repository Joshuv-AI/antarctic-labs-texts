Schur rings over Free Abelian Group of Rank Two


Abstract
. Schur rings are a type of subrings of group rings afforded by a partition of the underlined group. In this paper, Schur rings over free abelian group of rank two are classified under the assumption that one of the direct factor is a union of some basic sets. There are eight different types, and all but one type of which are traditional. Keywords: Schur rings; traditional Schur rings; wedge products.

1.
Introduction

The concept of Schur ring was first introduced by Schur in
[
9
]
and later studied by Wielandt in
[
10
]
in their work concerning permutation groups. Afterwards, of particular importance is the fact that Schur rings have been found to have many applications in algebraic combinatorics. For the applications of Schur rings to association schemes, see for example, subsections 2.4 and 4.4 of
[
2
]
.

Classification of Schur rings over a given group
G
has been a subject of much research interest.
Most papers have studied Schur rings over finite groups.
Leung and Man have classified Schur rings over finite cyclic groups in
[
6
]
and
[
7
]
. According to their results, there are four types of Schur rings over finite cyclic groups which are: partitions induced by direct product decomposition of groups (
Schur rings of tensor produt type
); partitions induced by orbits of automorphism subgroups (
orbit Schur rings
); partitions induced by cosets of normal subgroups (
Schur rings of wedge product type
);
trivial Schur ring
. We say that a Schur ring over a group is a
traditional Schur ring
if it is one of these four types.

This paper is a continuation of
[
1
]
. The authors of the current paper introduced a method for constructing all Schur rings over
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}_{3}
in
[
3
]
and over infinite dihedral groups in
[
4
]
. The idea involved is extending a Schur ring over a subgroup to one over the whole group. In this paper, we shall look at another group, that is, the free abelian group
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}
of rank two. The structure of any Schur ring
\mathcal{A}
over
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}
which satisfies one of the direct factor
\mathcal{Z}
is an
\mathcal{A}
-subgroup can be completely characterized. There are eight different types, and all but one type of which are traditional.

Throughout, let
G
denote an arbitrary group with identity element
1
, and
\mathbb{F}
an arbitrary field of characteristic
0
and we assume that it contains
\mathbb{Z}
as a subring. The group algebra over
G
with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}
is denoted
\mathbb{F}[G]
. Let
\mathbb{Z}^{\#}
denote the set of nonzero integers. For an abelian group
G
, it has an automorphism sending any element to its inverse and the corresponding Schur ring is denoted
\mathbb{F}[G]^{\pm}
.

Let
\mathcal{Z}
denote an infinite cyclic group, written multiplicative, and let
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}=\langle a\rangle\times\langle b\rangle
denote a free abelian group of rank two. The following is the main result of the current paper.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a Schur ring over
\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}
. If there exists
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{\#}
such that
\langle a^{k}\rangle
is an
\mathcal{A}
-subgroup.
Then one the following holds:

(i)

\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}]~;

(ii)

\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{F}{[\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}]}^{\pm}~;

(iii)

\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{Z}]\otimes\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{Z}]^{\pm}
;

(iv)

\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{Z}]^{\pm}\otimes\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{Z}]^{\pm}
;

(v)

\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{F}}\left\{{\underline{D}}:D\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})\}\right\}
, where
{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})=\{\{a^{nj+i}b^{-j},a^{i}b^{j}\}:i,j\in\mathbb{Z}\}
for some fixed
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{\#}
;

(vi)

\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{Span}{{}_{\mathbb{F}}}\left\{{\underline{D}}:D\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})\}\right\}
, where
{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})=\{\{a^{nj-i}b^{j},a^{i}b^{j}\}:i,j\in\mathbb{Z}\}
for some fixed
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{\#}
;

(vii)

\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{F}}\left\{\>{\underline{D}\>:\>D\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})\>}\right\}
, where
{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})=\{\{a^{i}b^{j},a^{nj-i}b^{j},a^{-i}b^{-j},a^{i-nj}b^{-j}\}:\>i,j\in\mathbb{Z}\>\}
for some
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{\#}
;

(viii)

\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{Span}_{\mathbb{F}}\left\{\>{\underline{D}\>:\>D\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})\>}\right\}
,
where
{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})=\{\{a^{i},a^{-i}\}:i\in\mathbb{Z}\}\cup\{\{a^{nj+i}b^{-j},a^{i}b^{j}\}:\>i,j\in\mathbb{Z},j\neq 0\>\}
for some fixed
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{\#}
.

Furthermore, all but the last type is traditional.

2.
Preliminary

For any finite non-empty subset
D\subseteq G
, denote
\underline{D}=\sum_{g\in C}g
and call
\underline{D}
a
simple quantity
. In addition, we define
D^{*}=\{g^{-1}:g\in D\}
. A partition
{\mathcal{D}}
of
G
is said to be
finite support
provided any
D\in{\mathcal{D}}
is a finite subset of
G
. Our notation on Schur rings is taken from
[
8
]
.

2.1.
Schur Rings

Definition 2.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{D}}
be a finite support partition of
G
and
\mathcal{A}
a subspace of
\mathbb{F}[G]
spanned by
\{\underline{D}:D\in{\mathcal{D}}\}
. We say that
\mathcal{A}
is a
Schur ring
(or
S
-ring) over
G
if

(1)

\{1\}\in{\mathcal{D}}
,

(2)

for any
D\in{\mathcal{D}}
,
D^{*}\in{\mathcal{D}}
,

(3)

for all
D_{1},D_{2}\in{\mathcal{D}}
,
\underline{D_{1}}\cdot\underline{D_{2}}=\sum_{D\in{\mathcal{D}}}\lambda_{D_{1}D_{2}D}\underline{D}
, where all except finitely many
\lambda_{D_{1}D_{2}D}
are equal to
0
.

For a Schur ring
\mathcal{A}
over
G
, the associated partition
{\mathcal{D}}
is denoted
{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})
and each element in
{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})
is called a
basic set

of
\mathcal{A}
. Each
\lambda_{D_{1}D_{2}D}
is called a
structure constant
. We say that a subset
D
of
G
is an
\mathcal{A}
-set
if
D
is a union of some basic sets of
\mathcal{A}
. An
\mathcal{A}
-subgroup
is simultaneously an
\mathcal{A}
-set and a subgroup.

Suppose
\alpha=\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}g\in\mathbb{F}[G]
and
\beta=\sum_{g\in G}\beta_{g}g\in\mathbb{F}[G]
, define

\alpha^{*}=\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}g^{-1}~\mathrm{and}~\supp(\alpha)=\{g\in G:\alpha_{g}\neq 0\}.

Additionally, the
Hadamard product
of
\alpha
and
\beta
is defined as

\alpha\circ\beta=\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}\beta_{g}g.

The following lemma is well-known, which is
[
1
, Lemma 2.15]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a Schur ring over a group
G
. Suppose
C=\{g\}
is a basic set. Then for any basic set
D
,
gD
and
Dg
are basic sets.

The following theorem was proved by Wielandt in
[
11
]
when
G
is finite. The general case appeared as
[
1
, Corollary 2.12]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
G
be a group and
\mathcal{A}
a subring of
\mathbb{F}[G]
which is spanned by
\{\underline{D}:D\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})\}
as an
\mathbb{F}
-vector space. Then
\mathcal{A}
is a Schur ring over
G
with basic sets from
{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})
if and only if
\mathcal{A}
is closed under
\circ
and
*
,
1\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})
, and for all
g\in G
there exists some
D\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})
such that
g\in D
.

Let
f:\mathbb{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}
be a function such that
f(0)=0
. For any
\alpha=\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}g
, we set

f(\alpha)=\sum_{g\in G}f(\alpha_{g})g.

The following proposition was proved by Wielandt in
[
11
, Proposition 22.3]
. The general case appeared as
[
1
, Propostion 2.4]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a Schur ring over a group
G
and the function be as above. Then

f(\alpha)\in\mathcal{A}
whenever
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
.

Let
f
take value
1
at one non-zero number and
0
at other numbers. We get the following statement, which is known as the Schur-Wielandt principle; see
[
8
, Corollary 1.10]
.

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a Schur ring over
G
. For any
\alpha=\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}g\in\mathcal{A}
, suppose
g\in\supp(\alpha)
with
\alpha_{g}=c\neq 0
. Then the set

\{g\in G:\alpha_{g}=c\}

is an
\mathcal{A}
-set.

The following proposition, which was first proved by Wielandt in
[
11
]
and was generalized in
[
1
]
, tells us how to generate
\mathcal{A}
-subgroups in a Schur ring
\mathcal{A}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a Schur ring over
G
. Let
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
and
H=\langle\supp(\alpha)\rangle
. Then
H
is an
\mathcal{A}
-subgroup.

Suppose that
H
is an
\mathcal{A}
-subgroup, then
\{D:D\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}),D\subseteq H\}

consists of a basic set of a Schur ring over
H
. It is denoted
\mathcal{A}_{H}
.

When we have normal
\mathcal{A}
-subgroup, we can construct Schur ring over the factor group as shown in the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

(
[
5
, Lemma 1.2]
) Let
\varphi:G\rightarrow H
be a group homomorphism with kernel
K
and
\mathcal{A}
a Schur ring over
G
. Suppose that
K
is an
\mathcal{A}
-subgroup. Then the image
\varphi(\mathcal{A})
is a Schur ring over
\varphi(G)
, where
{\mathcal{D}}(\varphi(\mathcal{A}))=\{\varphi(C):C\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})\}
.

In particular, if
H=G/K
, the factor group of
G
over
K
, and
\varphi
is the natural homomorphism, the corresponding Schur ring is denoted
\mathcal{A}_{G/K}
.

2.2.
Traditional Schur rings

Discrete and trivial Schur rings
.
The group ring
\mathbb{F}[G]
itself forms a Schur ring over
G
, which is called
the
discrete Schur ring
over
G
. When
G
is a finite group, the trivial partition
\{1,G\setminus 1\}
produces
a Schur ring which is known as the
trivial Schur ring
over
G
.

Tensor products
. Assume that
G=G_{1}\times G_{2}
,
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
are Schur rings over
{G_{1}}
and
{G_{2}}
, respectively. Then the
set

\{CD:C\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}_{1}),D\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A}_{2})\}

consists of the basic sets of a Schur ring over
G
, which was first proved by Wielandt in
[
10
]
when group
G
is a finite group. If
G
is an infinite group, it is easy to see that this conclusion also
holds. The corresponding Schur ring is called the
tensor product
of
\mathcal{A}_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}
, denoted
\mathcal{A}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{A}_{2}
.

Orbit Schur rings
. If
{\mathcal{K}}
is a finite subgroup of
\aut(G)
, the set of elements of
\mathbb{F}[G]
fixed by
{\mathcal{K}}
is a Schur ring over
G
, denoted
\mathbb{F}[G]^{{\mathcal{K}}}
and called the
orbit Schur ring
associated with
{\mathcal{K}}
. Obviously, the group ring
\mathbb{F}[G]
itself also forms an orbit Schur ring associated with the trivial automorphism group.

Wedge products
. A Schur ring
\mathcal{A}
over
G
is a
wedge product
if there exist nontrivial proper
\mathcal{A}
-subgroups
H,K
such that
K\leq H,K\unlhd G
, and every basic set outside
H
is a union of
K
-cosets. In this case, the series

1<K\leq H<G

is called a wedge-decomposition of
\mathcal{A}
. Furthermore, we write
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}_{1}\wedge\mathcal{A}_{2}
, where
\mathcal{A}_{1}=\mathcal{A}_{H}
and
\mathcal{A}_{2}=\mathcal{A}_{G/K}
.

A Schur ring over a group
G
is called
traditional
if it is either a trivial Schur ring (when
G
is finite), or an orbit Schur ring, or a tensor product of Schur rings over proper subgroups, or a wedge product. A group
G
is said to be
traditional
if each Schur ring over it is traditional.

Theorem 2.2
.

(
[
1
, Theorem 3.3]
) The only Schur rings over the infinite cyclic group
\mathcal{Z}:=\langle z\rangle
are either discrete or symmetric Schur ring. Here each basic set of the discrete Schur ring is a singleton set and each basic set of the the symmetric Schur rings has the form
\{z^{i},z^{-i}\}
for some nonnegative integer
i
. Note that the latter Schur ring is
\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{Z}]^{\pm}
.

3.
Proof of the main Results

For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, define the
n
th Frobenius map of
\mathbb{F}[G]
as

\alpha=\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}g\mapsto\alpha^{(n)}=\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}g^{n},

for any
\alpha=\sum_{g\in G}\alpha_{g}g\in\mathbb{F}[G]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

(
[
1
, Theorem 2.20]
) Suppose that
\mathcal{A}
is a Schur ring over
G
where
G
is an abelian group. Let
m
be an integer coprime to the orders of all torsion elements of
G
. Then, for all
\alpha\in\mathcal{A}
we have
\alpha^{(m)}\in\mathcal{A}
. In particular, if
G
is torsion-free, then, all Schur rings over
G
are closed under all Frobenius maps.

Lemma 3.2
.

(
[
1
, Lemma 2.21]
) Let
G
be an abelian group such that the torsion subgroup
T(G)
has finite exponent. Let
\mathcal{A}
be a Schur ring over
G
. Then,
T(G)
is an
\mathcal{A}
-subgroup.

Proof of Theorem
1.1

In the sequel, we fix the following notation:

G=\mathcal{Z}\times\mathcal{Z}=\langle a\rangle\times\langle b\rangle.

Let

\varphi:G\rightarrow G/\langle a^{k}\rangle

be the natural
homomorphism, and set

\mathcal{T}:=\varphi(\mathcal{A}).

Then
\mathcal{T}
is a Schur ring over
G/\langle a^{k}\rangle
by Lemma
2.2
. Moreover, according to Lemma
3.2
,

T(\varphi(G))=\langle\varphi(a)\rangle

is a
\mathcal{T}
-subgroup. It follows that

\langle a\rangle=\varphi^{-1}(\langle\varphi(a)\rangle)

is an
\mathcal{A}
-subgroup.
Therefore we may assume that
\mathcal{Z}=\langle a\rangle
is an
\mathcal{A}
-subgroup in the following.

Choose a basic set
C
such that
a\in C
. Then

C=\{a\}~\mathrm{or}~C=\{a,a^{-1}\}

by Theorem
2.2
. Hence
\{a^{i},a^{-i}\}
are
\mathcal{A}
-sets for all
i\in\mathbb{Z}
. In addition, as
G/\langle a\rangle
is cyclic, the basic set containing
\varphi(b)
is equal to

\{\varphi(b)\}~\mathrm{or}~\{\varphi(b),\varphi(b^{-1})\}

by Theorem
2.2
.

Theorem 3.1
.

All conditions are the same as in Theorem 1.1, and let
D\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})
with
b\in D
. Then one of the following holds:

(i)

D=\{b\}
;

(ii)

D=\{b,b^{-1}\}
;

(iii)

D=\{ba^{i_{0}},b\}
for some
i_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\#}
;

(iv)

D=\{b^{-1}a^{i_{1}},b\}
for some
i_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\#}
;

(v)

D=\{b,ba^{i_{2}},b^{-1},b^{-1}a^{-i_{2}}\}
for some
i_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\#}
.

Proof
. Observe that

D\subseteq\varphi^{-1}(\{\varphi(b),\varphi(b^{-1})\})=b\langle a\rangle\cup b^{-1}\langle a\rangle.

To prove this theorem, we next consider the following two cases.

Case 1.
Suppose

D\subseteq b\langle a\rangle.

Set
D:=b\{a^{i_{1}},\ldots,a^{i_{k}}\}
, where
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}
and one of the
i_{j}
is zero.

Then either
D=\{b\}
or
k
is greater than or equal to
2
. If
k>2
, then

\{a^{i_{1}}b,a^{i_{k}}b\}=D\cap\{a^{i_{k}-i_{1}},a^{i_{1}-i_{k}}\}D

(3.1)

is an
\mathcal{A}
-set, a contradiction. We conclude that
k=2
and
D=\{a^{i_{0}}b,b\}
for some
i_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\#}
in this case.

Case 2.
Now suppose

D=b\{a^{i_{1}},\ldots,a^{i_{k}}\}\cup b^{-1}\{a^{j_{1}},\ldots,a^{j_{t}}\},

where
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}
and
j_{1}<\cdots<j_{t}
.

Claim 1.

We have
k\leq 2
and
t\leq 2
.

Proof
. If the claim is false, assume on the contrary that
k>2
. Then similar to equality (
3.1
), one can see that
D\cap\{a^{i_{k}-i_{1}},a^{i_{1}-i_{k}}\}D
contains
\{ba^{i_{1}},ba^{i_{k}}\}
but not
ba^{i_{3}}
, which is a contradiction. Thus,
k\leq 2
and similar argument shows that
t\leq 2
. The claim follows.

\square

Claim 2.

We have
k=t
.

Proof
. If the claim is false, we first assume that
k=1,t=2
. Let

D=\{b\}\cup b^{-1}\{a^{j_{1}},a^{j_{2}}\},

where
j_{1}<j_{2}
.

Then
\mathcal{A}
contains the following element:

c:=\underline{D}(a^{-j_{1}}+a^{j_{1}})=b(a^{-j_{1}}+a^{j_{1}})+b^{-1}(1+a^{j_{2}-j_{1}}+a^{2j_{1}}+a^{j_{1}+j_{2}}).

(3.2)

Observe that

D^{*}=\{b^{-1},ba^{-j_{1}},ba^{-j_{2}}\}.

Thus,
D^{*}
is contained in
\supp(c)
and this fact forces
j_{1}=-j_{2}
. It then follows that

c:=b(a^{-j_{1}}+a^{j_{1}})+2b^{-1}+b^{-1}(a^{-2j_{1}}+a^{2j_{1}}).

Thus,
c=2\underline{D^{*}}
, which is absurd.

Now assume that
k=2
and
t=1
. Let

D=\{b,ba^{i_{1}},b^{-1}a^{j_{1}}\}.

Then

c^{\prime}:=\underline{D}(a^{-j_{1}}+a^{j_{1}})=ba^{-j_{1}}+ba^{j_{1}}+ba^{i_{1}-j_{1}}+ba^{i_{1}+j_{1}}+b^{-1}+b^{-1}a^{2j_{1}}\in\mathcal{A}.

Note that

D^{*}=\{b^{-1},b^{-1}a^{-i_{1}},ba^{-j_{1}}\}.

One can see that
D^{*}\subseteq\supp(c^{\prime})
. This implies that
i_{1}=-2j_{1}
. It then follows that

c^{\prime}=2ba^{-j_{1}}+b^{-1}+b^{-1}a^{2j_{1}}+ba^{j_{1}}+ba^{-3j_{1}}.

This yields that
c^{\prime}=2\underline{D^{*}}
, which is impossible.

\square

Now assume that
k=t=2
. Let

D=\{b,ba^{i_{2}},b^{-1}a^{j_{1}},b^{-1}a^{j_{2}}\},

where
i_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\#}
and
j_{1}<j_{2}
.
Then
\mathcal{A}
contains the following element:

\begin{split}&\underline{D}(a^{i_{2}}+a^{-i_{2}})=b+ba^{i_{2}}+ba^{-i_{2}}+ba^{2i_{2}}\\
+&b^{-1}a^{j_{1}+i_{2}}+b^{-1}a^{j_{1}-i_{2}}+b^{-1}b^{j_{2}+i_{2}}+b^{-1}a^{j_{2}-i_{2}}.\end{split}

One can see that
j_{2}+i_{2}=j_{1}
or
j_{1}+i_{2}=j_{2}
.

Consequently, there exist integers
i_{2}\neq 0
and
j_{1}
such that

D=\{b,ba^{i_{2}},b^{-1}a^{j_{1}},b^{-1}a^{i_{2}+j_{1}}\}.

Then

D^{*}=\{b^{-1},b^{-1}a^{-i_{2}},ba^{-j_{1}},ba^{-i_{2}-j_{1}}\}

is also a basic set. Note that

\begin{split}d:=&(a^{j_{1}}+a^{-j_{1}})\underline{D}\\
=&ba^{j_{1}}+ba^{-j_{1}}+ba^{i_{2}+j_{1}}+ba^{i_{2}-j_{1}}+\\
&b^{-1}a^{2j_{1}}+b^{-1}+b^{-1}a^{i_{2}+2j_{1}}+b^{-1}a^{i_{2}}\in\mathcal{A}.\end{split}

Thus,
D^{*}\subseteq\supp(d)
. Hence

2j_{1}=-i_{2},\,\,{\rm or}\,\,j_{1}=-i_{2}.

Claim 3.

The case where
2j_{1}=-i_{2}
can not happen.

Proof
. Towards a contradiction, assume that
2j_{1}=-i_{2}
. Then

D=\{b,ba^{-2j_{1}},b^{-1}a^{j_{1}},b^{-1}a^{-j_{1}}\},\quad\text{and}\quad D^{*}=\{b^{-1},b^{-1}a^{2j_{1}},ba^{-j_{1}},ba^{j_{1}}\}.

In addition,

\begin{split}f:=&(a^{j_{1}}+a^{-j_{1}})\underline{D}\\
=&2(b^{-1}+ba^{-j_{1}})+ba^{j_{1}}+ba^{-3j_{1}}+b^{-1}a^{2j_{1}}+b^{-1}a^{-2j_{1}}\in\mathcal{A}.\end{split}

Thus,
f=2\underline{D^{*}}
, which forces
j_{1}=0
and thus
i_{2}=0
, a contradiction.

Therefore,
j_{1}=-i_{2}
. It then follows that

D=\{b,ba^{i_{2}},b^{-1},b^{-1}a^{-i_{2}}\}

for some
i_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}^{\#}
.

Finally, if
k=t=1
, then statement
(iv)
in the theorem happens. The proof of this theorem is complete.

\square

Corollary 3.1
.

All conditions are the same as in Theorem 1.1, and let
D\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})
with
b\in D
. If there exists
i_{0},j_{0}\in\mathbb{Z},j_{0}\neq 0
such that
\{a^{i_{0}}b^{j_{0}}\}\in{\mathcal{D}}(\mathcal{A})
, then one of the following holds:

(i)

D=\{b\}
;

(ii)

D=\{a^{n_{0}}b,b\}
, where the integer
n_{0}
satisfies
2i_{0}=n_{0}j_{0}
;

(iii)

D=\{a^{n_{1}}b,a^{-n_{1}}b^{-1},b,b^{-1}\}

Proof
