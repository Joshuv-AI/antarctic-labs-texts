Toward NeuroDM: Where Computational Neuroscience Meets Data Mining
Xin Li, Bin Liu, Shuo Wang

Abstract
At the intersection of computational neuroscience (CN) and data mining (DM), we advocate a holistic view toward their rich connections. On the one hand, fundamental concepts in neuroscience such as saliency, memory, and emotion can find novel applications in data mining. On the other hand, multimodal imaging has opened the door for data mining to facilitate the extraction of important cognitive and behavioral information from multimodal neural data. By NeuroDM, we advocate for more collaboration between CN and DM to expedite the advances in two well-established fields. The analogy between the over-parameterization of biological and artificial neural networks might suggest a unifying perspective of advancing both fields.



1
What is NeuroDM?

A Wikipedia definition of data mining (DM) is “the process of extracting and discovering patterns in large data sets”. Few people realize that human brains offer a nearly optimal solution to the problem of DM - the sensory data perceived and processed by our brain are large and complex. From attention and saliency to memory and novelty detection, computational modeling of the human brain offers great inspiration for biologically inspired data mining research. Meanwhile, to investigate the mechanism of cortical processing, a large amount of multimodal neuroimaging data
[
1
]
has been collected. New data related to the behavior and pathology of neurological diseases increase at an unprecedented pace. The analysis and mining of these datasets of astronomical size has presented numerous technical challenges to the DM community.

At the intersection of computational neuroscience (CN) and DM, we argue that the timing is right for connecting these two fields and for the establishment of a new research field, which we call NeuroDM. We envision NeuroDM as a baby step toward more ambitious NeuroAI
[
2
]
aiming at artificial agents with capabilities that can match those of humans (e.g., the latest GPT-3 large language model
[
3
]
). The premise underlying NeuroDM is that over-parameterized models, which unify both biological and artificial neural networks, could unlock the secret to Mountcastle’s universal cortical processing
[
4
]
in CN and pattern discovery
[
5
]
in DM. A closer collaboration between the two well-established communities can accelerate the advances in both fields.

2
Why is It a Blue Sky Idea?

To jumpstart the emerging field of NeuroDM, we propose a few motivating scenarios that can potentially benefit from closer collaboration between CN and DM communities. The first two exemplify how the former can influence the latter; the last two vice versa.

Biologically-Inspired Visual Data Mining
. More than 90% of the information processed by the human brain have been reported to be visual
[
6
]
; indeed, video serves as the primary stimuli for most of us who interact with the physical world. In particular, rapid advances in sensing and communication technologies have led to the dominance of video traffic on the Internet; as of 2022, video accounts for 82% according to the latest Cisco report. There is an interesting analogy between ventral stream processing in the visual cortex and visual DM of Internet video. Sparse coding has been widely recognized as a principle of sensory neurons through both evolutionary and developmental processes
[
7
]
. To analyze a large amount of visual data received by the visual cortex, the human vision system (HVS) relies on the attention mechanism to detect saliency from stimuli. By analogy, we might ask why some video becomes viral or salient, which attracts more people’s attention.

Do Complex Systems Have a Long-term Memory?
In cognitive science, long-term memory, such as the Atkinson–Shiffrin model
[
8
]
plays an important role in the organization of human knowledge of the physical world. Unlike short-term memory (the magical number of seven
[
9
]
), long-term memory capacity varies from person to person. By analogy, we may ask about the historical prices of some items when we do Christmas shopping; similarly, financial analysts are interested in predicting the trend of stock prices based on their long past
[
10
]
. The fundamental question of climate change deals with the dynamic modeling of the atmospheric system at an extreme timescale much longer than the lifespan of humans. All these questions boil down to the collection, storage, and retrieval of temporal data for a complex system.

Change Detection in NeuroImaging Datasets
. Connectomics - the study of a comprehensive map of neural connections in the brain - has generated a huge amount of neuroimaging data. Even for the cortex of a small mammal, data can reach several terabytes in size
[
11
]
. Existing data mining tools often fail to capture large-scale datasets due to their size and complexity. Some seemingly easy problems in other domains - e.g., tumor detection from a CT image - become notoriously difficult because it is often challenging, if not impossible, to define the change associated with brain diseases (e.g., Alzheimer’s Disease or Parkinson’s Disease) in advance. Developing scalable and efficient DM tools for connectomics is likely to accelerate our understanding of healthy and pathological brains.

Data Mining for Early Diagnosis of Autism
. The latest statistics for 2021 show that the prevalence rate of autism is 1 in 44 children in the US. The average age of diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is four years
[
12
]
. Despite the widely documented benefits of early intervention, early diagnosis of ASD in young children (e.g., 18–48 months) has remained an open problem, known as the “detection gap”
[
13
]
, due to limited resources and the high cost associated with the gold standard Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS)
[
14
]
. Can we reduce the detection gap by automating ADOS video analysis or developing an alternative home video-based screening protocol? These technical challenges require the data mining community to work closely with autism professionals and researchers to address one of the biggest challenges in our healthcare business.

3
Comput. Neuroscience for Data Mining

There are two sets of wicked data mining problems that we believe can benefit from a biologically inspired approach. One is the mining of non-visual patterns and the other is the mining of complex patterns. For the former, we advocate the data visualization approach to facilitate the match with visual data mining; for the latter, we propose to make an analogy with human learning advanced skills (e.g., sport, music, or language).

3.1
Manifold Unfolding for Visual Data Mining

More than 90% of the information processed by the human brain have been reported to be visual
[
6
]
; in fact, video serves as the main stimulus for most of us to interact with the physical world. The visual cortex is probably the most well studied area in the neocortex, including how we recognize a face or a car. Meanwhile, rapid advances in sensing and communication technologies have led to the dominance of video on the Internet. As of 2022, video accounts for 82% based on the latest Cisco report. Both scientifically and technologically, visual data mining is likely to play an increasingly important role in the future. In the literature, video DM
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
has been widely studied by the technical community. We summarize our bold ideas about future directions in two lines of research:
biologically inspired visual DM through manifold untangling
and
scientific data visualization for non-visual DM
.

In the visual cortex, ventral stream processing is mainly responsible for the recognition of complex visual patterns such as different objects. The unfolding of the perceptual manifold
[
18
]
is an influential hypothesis that states that each layer of the visual cortex implements a local subspace untangling algorithm to flatten the object manifold. Such a conceptually simple but powerful idea inspires us to tackle visual data mining from the manifold untangling perspective. If any visual data (image or video) can be characterized by a low-dimensional manifold in high-dimensional space, discovering their patterns can be universally solved by two principled untangling techniques (embedding and flattening). Manifold embedding and flattening jointly strike an improved trade-off between selectivity (discrimination) and tolerance (invariance).

For non-visual data, translating them into visual representation is not new. Speech data is often translated into a spectrogram - a redundant time-frequency representation; thermal/temperature data is often assigned pseudo-color to facilitate their visual inspection. What seems to have been underresearched is the comparison and optimization of ideal data representation. For example, it is widely known that color images admit several representations in different color spaces (e.g., YUV vs. HSV). Optimizing the transformation into color space for a specific mining task has remained an open problem. Similarly, speech signals admit the representation of the cepstral coefficient and Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs). They have been found to be suitable for speech and speech recognition, respectively. An optimal data representation in terms of style-content separation might be obtained from manifold untangling.

3.2
Association Mechanism for Mining Complex Patterns

Through a long period of evolution and development, the neocortex has discovered a universal processing algorithm regardless of sensory or motor modalities
[
4
]
. At the cellular level, pyramid cells have been hypothesized to play a fundamental role in associating aspects of perceived experience with an internal representation of the world and making predictions about the future
[
19
]
. According to Mountcastle, no matter how we learn to play Mozart, shoot basketball or speak a second language, a universal abstraction of intelligent behaviors boils down to the formation of complex sensorimotor patterns in the neocortex. From this perspective, the internal representation learned by Helen Keller is not less sophisticated than that of normal people
[
20
]
despite the deprivation of visual and hearing capabilities.

By analogy, we can imagine that human-centered computing might benefit from such a biologically inspired approach. With multimodal sensing, one can imagine an enriched learning experience in the future, for example, how to train athletes to play professional soccer? Instead of working with a human coach, multimodal data collected by specially designed sensors can convey a rich description about a player’s physical state and skills. Such “internal representation” can provide useful tips about training areas or strategies, which is analogous to the action taken by the neocortex. The trajectory or prediction errors made by the multimodal sensory data will serve as the memory trace of this data-driven learning practice. From sport and music to language and surgery, this closed-loop paradigm could have a profound impact on educational data mining
[
21
]
.

At the next level, we can think that the most complex patterns of the physical world - no matter whether they are related to climate change or the global economy - can be comprehended by collective intelligence
[
22
]
, the social extension of the individual brain. Despite the fact that mining weather or financial data at the macroscopic scale is still out of the reach of current computing resources, we can imagine a theoretically appealing approach to understand the large-scale patterns generated by complex systems. That is, if high performance computing (HPC) can associate the big data collected by sensor networks with a virtual representation (corresponding to internal representation) of the complex system, such a machine with collective intelligence might be endowed with the capability of learning to make predictions about the future (e.g., more accurate weather or stock market forecasting).

Data Mining for Comput. Neuroscience

Rapid advances in neuroimaging have generated a huge amount of data covering various modalities, from serial-section electron microscopy (ssEM)
[
23
]
and calcium imaging
[
24
]
to diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (dMRI)
[
25
]
and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)
[
26
]
. Regardless of the anatomical (a.k.a. structural) or functional connectome, we face the challenge of big data: even for the cortex of a small mammal, the output of connectome graphs can reach several terabytes in size
[
11
]
, which calls for efficient mining tools.

4.1
Structural Data Mining for Anatomical Connectomics

Great progress has been made toward the reconstruction of brain maps for insects
[
27
]
, zebrafish
[
23
]
, and humans
[
28
]
. However, it often takes dozens or even hundreds of people
[
29
]
to collaborate on the FlyEM project of a small organ such as the central brain of Drosophila. From automated segmentation to prediction of synapses, many computational tools in anatomical connectomics call for streamlined processing and optimization. We suggest that the following issues call for closer collaboration between neuroscientists and computer scientists.

Automated Segmentation
. Auto-segmentation
[
30
]
has experienced an evolution from first generation (low level) to fourth generation (based on deep learning). In view of the increasing impact of pre-trained models
[
31
]
on other high-level vision tasks, we believe that the field of auto-segmentation can also benefit from this line of research. To address the data requirement for pre-trained models, we propose two possible attacks as follows. The first is to consolidate the existing effort in the biomedical image segmentation datasets. This is based on the assumption that a common principle is shared by different subjects/species and imaging modalities. The second is to construct deep generative models (e.g., generative adversarial networks and diffusion models) with controllable ground-truth of segmentation.

Bayesian/Transfer Reconstruction
. The basic idea is to take advantage of experience. With the achievement of constructing the
first
brain map for fly or zebrafish, we believe that it will take much less effort to construct the second, the third, and so on. Since the entire genus of Drosophila contains more than 1,500 species, we imagine that the transfer of brain map reconstruction from one species to another can save time and effort. A principled solution to implement such acceleration will be through either Bayesian reconstruction (incorporate the first-time experience as the prior) or transfer learning (treat old and new specifies as source and destination domains).

4.2
Functional Data Mining for Decoding Brain Activities

A dogma in biology is that structure determines function. Using neuroimaging data to study the functional property of the human brain is called the functional connectome
[
32
]
. Unlike the anatomical connectome, the functional connectome requires in vivo study (e.g., from dMRI to fMRI). The amount of neuroimaging data in the functional connectome is often orders of magnitude higher than that in the anatomical connectome. We will address two issues that we deem to benefit from closer collaboration between neuroscientists and computer scientists.

Comparative Connectomics
. Comparative connectomics focuses on the study of structural / functional differences between different brains. There are two main sources of difference: individual differences in the behavior of normal brains
[
33
]
and temporal differences arising from brain disorders
[
34
]
. The former can help us better understand how we think or behave differently by modeling the intraclass variation of the healthy population. The latter is fundamental to the diagnosis of pathological conditions by modeling interclass variations. In addition to neuroimaging, behavioral imaging often provides valuable (sometimes even dominant) information for the diagnosis of neurological developmental diseases (e.g., autism spectrum disorder) and mental disorders (e.g., depression).

Multimodal Data Fusion
. As mentioned above, probing the mental state
of human brains often requires multimodal data acquired from different imaging modalities (e.g. EEG vs. fMRI vs. implantable electrode)
[
35
]
. Multimodal neuroimaging is particularly attractive because each individual modality is constrained by the trade-off between spatial and temporal resolutions
[
36
]
. It is possible to achieve finer spatio-temporal resolution by combining the complementary information contained in multiple sources. After multimodal data fusion, we can model the human brain as a complex network
[
37
]
with a structural description of the network
architecture. This network model offers the first step towards understanding how the connectome can provide new insights into the organization of functional connections and the dynamics of the brain.

5
Challenges and Opportunities

5.1
Data Collection and Augmentation

Collecting data in the physical world is not always easy. In multimodal neuroimaging, the ability to simultaneously record neural data in different modalities is often limited by hardware constraints. For example, it is possible to record EEG and fMRI in sync
[
38
]
but simultaneous recording of fMRI and single neurons is challenging, if not impossible, due to their physical barriers. Even in the situation of relatively technology mature (e.g., the acquisition of home video to support clinical diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder), behavioral imaging still faces nontechnical challenges such as lack of acquisition protocol, controllable environment (e.g., children’s motion), and storage constraint (i.e., capturing anomalies in social behavior might require persistent recording). It also takes a lot of more effort to preprocess neuroimaging data (e.g., spike sorting of single-neuron recording and motion correction of fMRI) than common image and video data.
Meanwhile, it is often expensive and time-consuming to collect neural data, which calls for virtual acquisition (a.k.a., data augmentation). Deep generative models, such as generative adversarial networks (GAN), and diffusion models, have been widely studied for the synthesis of natural images by the computer vision community. Can we leverage those tools for the virtual generation of neuroimaging data to accelerate the evolution of large pre-trained models such as GPT-3
[
3
]
?

5.2
Data Compression and Fusion

Conventional wisdom of data compression is based on Shannon’s information theory, ignoring data’s semantic information. Early work on video summarization
[
39
]
and text summarization
[
40
]
can be interpreted as semantic data compression that achieves the objective of data reduction through high-level understanding. For example, an effective strategy for summarizing baseball video from audio data is based on the observation that home runs in a baseball game are inevitably followed by the audience’s vocal response. Under the framework of NeuroDM, we advocate a systematic study of such semantic data compression because rapid advances in large pre-trained models have made it possible to extract semantically important information from visual data. Given the rapid growth of video data (e.g., surveillance and social media), it makes sense to strategically select novel information to save (analogous to the hippocampus’s novelty detection). Similarly, we believe that it is important to combine the existing joint multimodal data embedding with task-specific multimodal data fusion into a closed loop. Such a joint optimization of low-level embedding and high-level fusion will facilitate robust NeuroDM algorithms capable of handling missing or noise data.

5.3
Data Representation and Visualization

Depending on the ultimate objective (clustering vs. classification vs. regression), one might construct different model representations or learning networks. The direct fit model
[
41
]
inspired by the analogy between evolution and learning offers a refreshing perspective. That is, “similar to evolutionary processes, over-parameterized models can be simple and parsimonious, as they provide a versatile and robust solution for learning a diverse set of functions”. Apparently, nature has discovered that overparameterized models, such as the neocortex, can be equipped with a universal cortical processing algorithm to pass the embodied Turing test
[
2
]
. How can we mirror such an achievement using a computational proxy model? We conjecture that an IDR might jointly exploit the blessing of dimensionality
[
42
]
and the principle of association
[
19
]
regardless of the physical origin of the data. If either connectome or its proxy models can be better visualized, we can have a better chance of closing the gap between the structure and functionality of over-parameterized models.
