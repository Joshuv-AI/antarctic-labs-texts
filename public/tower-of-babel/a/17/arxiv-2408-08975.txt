Quantum paving: When sphere packings meet Gabor frames
Markus Faulhuber, Thomas Strohmer

Abstract
. We introduce the new problems of quantum packing, quantum covering, and quantum paving. These problems arise naturally when considering an algebra of non-commutative operators that is deeply rooted in quantum physics as well as in Gabor analysis. Quantum packing and quantum covering show similarities with energy minimization and the dual problem of polarization. Quantum paving, in turn, aims to simultaneously optimize both quantum packing and quantum covering. Classical sphere packing and covering hint the optimal configurations for our new problems. We present solutions in certain cases, state several conjectures related to quantum paving and discuss some applications.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
42C15, 81S30

1.
Introduction

The sphere packing problem is an ancient mathematical problem and has fascinated and attracted researchers for centuries
[
12
]
. The goal is to arrange non-overlapping spheres of same size in the most economical way, i.e., such that their interiors use up the largest possible portion of Euclidean space. In dimension three, the problem became (in)famous due to the Kepler conjecture which, with great effort, was proven to be correct by T. Hales
[
28
]
about 20 years ago, and four centuries after its statement.

M. Viazovska’s tremendous breakthrough in solving the sphere packing problem in dimension 8
[
62
]
and, in joint work with H. Cohn, A. Kumar, S. Miller and D. Radchenko in dimension 24
[
9
]
is among the greatest recent developments in mathematics.

The cousin of the sphere packing problem is the sphere covering problem, which has gained less attention
[
12
]
. The goal is to entirely cover Euclidean space by equally sized spheres, such that the overlap of their interiors is as small as possible. In a way, the two problems are two sides of the same medal. Nonetheless, they are usually treated separately and tools and solutions may be quite different.

We introduce the new notions of
quantum packing
and
quantum covering
, which are inspired by viewing the construction of optimal Gaussian Gabor frames through the lens of classical sphere packing and covering. This is not the first time these two fields meet: Gabor systems were used in the article by Manin and Marcolli
[
45
]
to construct Cohn-Elkies functions which come arbitrarily close to the linear programming bound for the sphere packing problem derived by Cohn and Elkies
[
7
]
. Here, we go in the other directions and seek to use knowledge on sphere packings and coverings to find candidates which solve our newly introduced problems.
They can also be formulated by a two-sided inequality, involving non-commuting unitary operators, arising from the Schrödinger representation of the Heisenberg group.

Just as its classical pendant, the quantum packing and covering problem is simple in its statement (though certainly more involved than the classical case), but notoriously difficult to solve. In fact, there are additional issues popping up, which do not arise in the classical questions, e.g., it is non-obvious and perhaps not true that the solutions are invariant under scaling (i.e, changing the density of a lattice).

Lastly, we present the notion of
quantum paving
, which aims (in a sense that we will make precise) to simultaneously optimize both quantum packing and quantum covering. As such it is related to finding the packing-covering constant
[
51
]
.

The problems we discuss have applications in frame theory, time-frequency analysis, wireless communications, and potentially also in quantum error correction.

2.
Formulation of the problems

We start with introducing the problems, using mainly notation from the textbooks of G. Folland
[
22
]
and K. Gröchening
[
23
]
. The details are then given in subsequent sections.

Let
\varphi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be the normalized standard Gaussian function, i.e.,

(1)

\varphi(t)=2^{d/4}e^{-\pi|t|^{2}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\qquad\text{ so }\quad\left\lVert\varphi\right\rVert_{L^{2}}=1.

Let
z=(x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, so
(x,\omega;\tau)
is an element of the Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}
, and let
\rho(z)=\rho(x,\omega;0)
denote the unitary operator coming from the Schrödinger representation of the element
(x,\omega;0)
from the Heisenberg group. In particular, up to a phase factor
c\in\mathbb{C}
,
|c|=1
, we have
\rho(z)=c\ \pi(z)
, where
\pi(z)
is the classical time-frequency shift, i.e., the composition of the translation operator
T_{x}
and the modulation operator
M_{\omega}
, formally defined in (
8
).

Now, for a lattice
\Lambda
in phase space
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, find the optimal constants
A_{\Lambda}
and
B_{\Lambda}
such that

(2)

A_{\Lambda}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|\langle f,\rho(\lambda)\varphi\rangle|^{2}\leq B_{\Lambda}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2},\qquad\forall f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

The reader familiar with Gabor analysis will identify (
2
) as the frame inequality of the Gabor system
\mathcal{G}\{c\,\pi(\lambda)\varphi\mid\lambda\in\Lambda\}
. We can now introduce the problems of quantum packing, covering, and paving.

Problem 1

(Quantum packing)
.

For any given fixed density, find the configuration
\Lambda_{B}
such that
B_{\Lambda_{B}}\leq B_{\Lambda}
for all
\Lambda
of the same density.

Problem 2

(Quantum covering)
.

For any given fixed density, find the configuration
\Lambda_{A}
such that
A_{\Lambda_{A}}\leq A_{\Lambda}
for all
\Lambda
of the same density.

Problem 3

(Quantum paving)
.

For any given fixed density, find the configuration
\Lambda_{B/A}
such that
B_{\Lambda_{B/A}}/A_{\Lambda_{B/A}}\leq B_{\Lambda}/A_{\Lambda}
for all
\Lambda
of the same density.

The “quantum” aspect in the aforementioned problems can be traced back along different routes to a non-commuting operator algebra that plays a foundational role in quantum physics. Each of these different routes highlights a different facet of these problems.
In one route, taken in Sections
3.1
and
4
we will encounter
coherent states
, i.e., states of minimum uncertainty, which form a fundamental concept in quantum mechanics. Another route will take us to “paving” the phase space, see Section
6
.
Yet another route revolves around the
quantum torus
, discussed briefly in the next subsection.

2.1.
The quantum torus

For a pair of non-commuting, unitary operators
(U_{1}
,
U_{2})
on a Hilbert space and
r\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, the quantum torus
\mathcal{T}
, also called non-commutative torus
[
11
]
, is an operator algebra with elements being the formal sums of the form (cf.
[
11
, pp. 217, Ex. 2.
\beta
]
)

(3)

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}c_{\lambda}\ U(\lambda),\quad\text{ where }\quad U(\lambda)=U_{2}(\lambda_{2})U_{1}(\lambda_{1}),\quad U_{2}U_{1}=e^{2\pi ir}U_{1}U_{2},\quad\text{ and }\quad\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

In our situation, we use the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of square-integrable functions and our non-commuting operators are the translation operator
T_{x}
and the modulation operator
M_{\omega}
. Their combination leads to time-frequency shifts
\pi(z)
, serving as coordinates for the quantum torus (see (
8
), (
9
) and (
10
) below). So, its elements are formally given by

(4)

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}c_{\lambda}\ \pi(\lambda),\quad\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d},

where
c=(c_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
is a sequence on the lattice
\Lambda
. The properties of the sequence induce an additional structure on the quantum torus
\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}
. For example, denoting the bounded operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by
\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
, if
c\in\mathcal{S}(\Lambda)
is a sequence of rapid decay, i.e., it decreases faster than the inverse of any polynomial, then we obtain the
smooth
structure:

(5)

\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}^{\infty}=\{T\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\mid T=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}c_{\lambda}\ \pi(\lambda),\,(c_{\lambda})\in\mathcal{S}(\Lambda)\}.

By assuming the sequence of coefficients to be absolutely summable we obtain:

(6)

\mathcal{T}_{\Lambda}^{1}=\{T\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))\mid T=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}c_{\lambda}\ \pi(\lambda),\,(c_{\lambda})\in\ell^{1}(\Lambda)\}.

Characterizations of these and more structures, also in terms of Gabor frames, may be found in the work of Luef
[
38
]
,
[
39
]

[
40
]
. For a more general framework, we refer to the article of Manin
[
44
]
and for our specific setup we refer to
[
41
, Sec. 5]
.

For us, (
4
) provides one way of motivating quantum packing and covering, as they concern continuity and invertibility properties of elements of the form (
4
) which come from a quantum torus. The discipline which studies these properties is
quantum-harmonic analysis
(QHA), introduced by Werner
[
63
]
. QHA aims to formulate classical results from harmonic analysis for operators of the form (
4
) and has recently also gained new attraction in the context of Gabor analysis
[
42
]
,
[
55
]
,
[
56
]
. Depending on the (decay) properties of the sequence
(c_{\lambda})_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
, we have different kinds of bounded operators forming a quantum torus (see
[
37
, Sec. 3]
), similar to various function spaces on the torus. Finding conditions on the invertibility of the series in (
4
) is a pendant to Wiener’s lemma for Fourier series and falls into the scope of quantum covering. Questions of convergence fall into the scope of quantum packing. The mentioned properties may be studied by means of
quantum thetas

\Theta_{\Lambda}
.
These are operator pendants to classical
theta functions
(see
[
41
]
,
[
44
]
) and act on functions
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by the rule

(7)

\Theta_{\Lambda}f=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\langle\varphi,\pi(\lambda)\varphi\rangle\pi(\lambda)f.

Compared to classical thateta functions, the inner product is the replacement for the Gaussian function,
\pi(\lambda)
replaces the complex units and
f
the point at which we evaluate.
By the
symplectic Poisson summation formula
(see Section
3.2
) we will see that a quantum theta is just the frame operator in disguise: using the adjoint lattice
\Lambda^{\circ}
(
21
),
\Theta_{\Lambda^{\circ}}
is the
Janssen representation
of the frame operator (see
[
23
, Chap. 9.4]
).

If we take the inner product of
\Theta_{\Lambda}f
with
f
we arrive at

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|\langle f,\rho(\lambda)\varphi\rangle|^{2}
, which is the term we try to bound in (
2
).
Lower bounds of (
2
) are related to the invertibility of the Gabor frame operator, while upper bounds of (
2
) ensure continuity of the Gabor frame operator, see Section
3.1
.

3.
Notation and tools

We denote the Hilbert space of square integrable (complex-valued) functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The unitary operators
T_{x}
of translation by
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
M_{\omega}
of modulation by
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
act on functions by the rules

(8)

T_{x}f(t)=f(t-x)\quad\text{ and }\quad M_{\omega}f(t)=e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot t}f(t).

The usual positive-semidefinite order between operators or matrices is denoted by
\preccurlyeq
.

The dot
\cdot
denotes the Euclidean inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which we see as a space of column vectors. The composition of
T_{x}
and
M_{\omega}
is called a time-frequency shift and is denoted by

(9)

\pi(z)=M_{\omega}T_{x},\quad z=(x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2d}.

We say
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is the
time-frequency plane
and also call it
phase space
in our setting. The operators
T_{x}
and
M_{\omega}
do not commute in general but satisfy the following commutation relations:

(10)

M_{\omega}T_{x}=e^{2\pi i\omega\cdot x}\,T_{x}M_{\omega}.

Hence, the set of time-frequency shifts is not closed under composition. Indeed, we have

(11)

\pi(z)\pi(z^{\prime})=e^{-2\pi ix\cdot\omega^{\prime}}\,\pi(z+z^{\prime}),\qquad z=(x,\omega),\ z^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},\omega^{\prime}).

We use the unitary Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
as usual in harmonic analysis
[
22
]
and time-frequency analysis
[
23
]
:

(12)

\mathcal{F}f(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t)e^{-2\pi i\omega\cdot t}\,dt.

This formula is valid for a suitable dense subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mathcal{F}
extends to a unitary operator on all of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Fixing a function
g\neq 0
, called window function, the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) of a function
f
with respect to
g
is given by

(13)

V_{g}f(x,\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(t)\overline{g(t-x)}e^{-2\pi it\cdot\omega}dt=\langle f,M_{\omega}T_{x}g\rangle,\quad\text{for $x,\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$.}

It plays a central role in the formulation of quantum paving as we will outline in the upcoming subsection.

A lattice (which we always assume to be full-rank) is a discrete co-compact subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We can also write it as
\mathbb{Z}
-module of a basis
M=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{d}\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
;

(14)

\Lambda=M\mathbb{Z}^{d}=\left\{\sum_{l=1}^{d}k_{l}v_{l}\mid k_{l}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\},\quad M\in GL(d,\mathbb{R}).

The dual lattice of
\Lambda
is denoted by
\Lambda^{\perp}
and characterized by

(15)

\Lambda^{\perp}=\{\lambda^{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mid\lambda\cdot\lambda^{\perp}\in\mathbb{Z},\ \forall\lambda\in\Lambda\}.

With this notation, the
Poisson summation formula
reads

(16)

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}f(\lambda+x)=\frac{1}{\textnormal{vol}(\mathbb{R}^{d}/\Lambda)}\sum_{\lambda^{\perp}\in\Lambda^{\perp}}\mathcal{F}f(\lambda^{\perp})e^{2\pi iz\cdot\lambda^{\perp}}.

Note that
\Lambda^{\perp}
is indeed a lattice and we have
\Lambda^{\perp}=M^{-T}\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, where
M^{-T}
is the inverse transpose of
M
and
\Lambda=M\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. The density of
\Lambda
is given by
\delta(\Lambda)=1/|\det(M)|=1/\textnormal{vol}(\mathbb{R}^{d}/\Lambda)
.

3.1.
Gabor systems and frames

Gabor frames are a key concept of time-frequency analysis which enjoy a wide range of applications in mathematics, engineering, and signal processing. We refer to
[
23
]
for an excellent introduction to Gabor analysis. Classically, Gabor frames have been defined only for rectangular time-frequency lattices. But for obvious reasons, in this paper we prefer the flexibility that comes with general lattices in the time-frequency domain. Hence,
given a non-zero window function
g\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and a lattice
\Lambda
we define a Gabor system
\mathcal{G}(g,\Lambda)=\{g_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
via

g_{\lambda}=\pi(\lambda)g,\qquad\lambda\in\Lambda.

If there exist finite constants
0<A\leq B
such that for all
f\in L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

(17)

A\|f\|_{2}\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|\langle f,g_{\lambda}\rangle|^{2}\leq B\|f\|_{2},

then
\{g_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
is called a Gabor frame for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. The smallest constant
A
and the largest constant
B
in (
17
) are called the frame bounds.
The associated frame operator
S
is defined by

Sf=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\langle f,g_{\lambda}\rangle g_{\lambda}.

It is easy to see that this positive-definite self-adjoint operator satisfies
IA\preccurlyeq S\preccurlyeq IB
, where
I
is the identity operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. If in addition to (
17
) it holds that the elements are linearly independent then
\{g_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
is called a Gabor Riesz basis.

For a given Gabor frame
\{g_{\lambda},\lambda\in\Lambda\}
with frame bounds
A,B>0
,
\{S^{-1}g_{\lambda},\lambda\in\Lambda\}
is a frame with frame bounds
B^{-1},A^{-1}>0
, the so-called canonical dual Gabor frame. Since the frame operator commutes with time-frequency shift
\pi(\lambda)
for
\lambda\in\Lambda
, we have

S^{-1}g_{\lambda}=\pi(\lambda)S^{-1}g
, where the function
S^{-1}g
is also referred to as the canonical dual window.

Also, for a Gabor system

\{g_{\lambda},\lambda\in\Lambda\}
(no matter if it is a frame or not), we define the associated Gram matrix
G
by
G_{\lambda,\nu}=\langle\pi(\nu)g,\pi(\lambda)g\rangle
for
\lambda,\nu\in\Lambda
. The structure of the Gram matrix
G
was exploited by Janssen
[
31
]
to give first formulas for exact frame bounds for several window functions, including the Gaussian
\varphi
, under the assumption that the lattice is rectangular and its density is an integer. The integer density assumption is due to technical issues caused by the non-commutativity of time-frequency shifts.

An important quantity of the frame operator, both from a theoretical viewpoint as well as for numerical and practical purposes, is the condition number of
S
, given by the ratio
\frac{B}{A}
. Like for matrices, the motto “smaller is better” is in general true for frame operator condition numbers. In particular, if
A=B
, we have a
tight frame
and
S
is just a multiple of the identity operator, making its inversion trivial. In that case the frame and its dual coincide up to normalization.

Throughout this paper we are only interested in Gabor frames where the window function is a Gaussian, i.e.,
g=\varphi
, where
\varphi
is defined in (
1
). Gaussian functions play a special role in quantum physics (as well as in many other areas), since they minimize the uncertainty principle
[
23
]
. In quantum
mechanics and in quantum optics, time-frequency shifts of a Gaussian, play an important role, where they are usually referred to by the name
coherent states

[
33
,
13
]
.

It is a rather deep result that
\{\varphi_{\lambda},\lambda\in\Lambda\}
is a frame for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
if and only if
\delta(\Lambda)>1
, see
[
23
]
. If we consider Gabor systems for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
d>1
, then
\delta(\Lambda)>1
is a necessary density condition, but provably not always sufficient. A characterization of multivariate (Gaussian) Gabor frames is still missing.

3.2.
Symplectic methods

We will exploit the symplectic structure of phase space and introduce the
standard symplectic matrix
as

(18)

\mathcal{J}=\begin{pmatrix}[r]\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{I}\\
-\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},

where the blocks are of size
d\times d
and denote the identity matrix
\mathbf{I}
and the zero matrix
\mathbf{0}
. For
z,z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, the
standard symplectic form
is given by

(19)

\sigma(z,z^{\prime})=z\cdot\mathcal{J}z^{\prime}.

A matrix
S\in SL(2d,\mathbb{R})
belongs to the symplectic group
Sp(d)
if and only if

(20)

\sigma(z,z^{\prime})=\sigma(Sz,Sz^{\prime})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad S^{T}\mathcal{J}S=\mathcal{J}.

We recall that the symplectic group
Sp(d)
is in general a proper subgroup of
SL(2d,\mathbb{R})
. Only in the case
d=1
, we have
Sp(1)=SL(2,\mathbb{R})
.

As we are only interested in even-dimensional lattices we also wish to assign them a
symplectic dual
or
adjoint
lattice
\Lambda^{\circ}
. This may be characterized by commutations of time-frequency shifts or, equivalently, by using the standard symplectic form
\sigma(.\,,\,.)
:

(21)

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\circ}

\displaystyle=\{\lambda^{\circ}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\mid\pi(\lambda)\pi(\lambda^{\circ})=\pi(\lambda^{\circ})\pi(\lambda),\,\forall\lambda\in\Lambda\}

(22)

\displaystyle=\{\lambda^{\circ}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\mid\sigma(\lambda,\lambda^{\circ})\in\mathbb{Z},\,\forall\lambda\in\Lambda\}.

Note the similarity between the second characterization of the symplectic dual lattice
\Lambda^{\circ}
to the definition of the classical dual lattice
\Lambda^{\perp}
.

A
symplectic lattice
in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is a lattice
\Lambda=S\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
with
S\in Sp(d)
. More generally, we will also call scaled versions
\alpha\Lambda=\alpha S\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
,
\alpha>0
, symplectic if the generating matrix
S
is symplectic. It follows from (
20
) that, for
S\in Sp(d)
,

(23)

S=\mathcal{J}^{-1}S^{-T}\mathcal{J}\quad\text{ and }\quad\Lambda=S\mathbb{Z}^{2d}=\mathcal{J}^{-1}S^{-T}\mathcal{J}\mathbb{Z}^{2d}=\mathcal{J}^{-1}S^{-T}\mathbb{Z}^{2d}=\mathcal{J}^{-1}\Lambda^{\perp}=\mathcal{J}\Lambda^{\perp},

as
\mathcal{J}^{-1}=-\mathcal{J}
and
\Lambda^{\perp}=-\Lambda^{\perp}
. We also demand a
symplectic Fourier transform
, which for a suitable function
F
on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is given by

(24)

\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}F(z)=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}F(z^{\prime})e^{-2\pi i\sigma(z^{\prime},z)}\,dz^{\prime}=\mathcal{F}F(\mathcal{J}z).

Hence,
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
inherits properties from the usual Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}
, e.g., it extends to a unitary operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})
. In addition, it is involutive, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}^{-1}=\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
. The symplectic Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
and the symplectic dual lattice together provide us with a
symplectic Poisson summation formula
(cf.
[
15
, Sec. 2.2]
):

(25)

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}F(\lambda+z)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}F(\lambda)e^{2\pi i\sigma(z,\lambda)}.

We refer to the textbooks by Folland
[
22
]
and Gröchenig
[
26
]
as well as the articles
[
3
, Sec. 1.1]
,
[
4
, App. B]
,
[
15
]
for more details on symplectic methods.

As an application of the symplectic Poisson summation formula, we derive the Janssen representation of the Gaussian Gabor frame operator, which is given by

(26)

S_{\Lambda}=\textnormal{vol}(\Lambda)^{-1}\sum_{\lambda^{\circ}\in\Lambda^{\circ}}\langle\varphi,\pi(\lambda^{\circ})\varphi\rangle\,\pi(\lambda^{\circ}).

We see that, by the Janssen representation (
26
), the scaled frame operator
\textnormal{vol}(\Lambda)S_{\Lambda}
is actually the quantum theta
\Theta_{\Lambda^{\circ}}
defined by (
7
). If the lattice
\Lambda
is symplectic (up to scaling by
\alpha>0
), then the adjoint lattice
\Lambda^{\circ}
can be replaced by the lattice
\Lambda
(appropriately scaled by
1/\alpha
).

3.3.
The Fundamental Identity of Gabor Analysis

In what follows, it is convenient for us to introduce the
modulation spaces

M^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, in particular the modulation space
M^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which is also known as Feichtinger’s algebra
S_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Definition 3.1

(Modulation space)
.

The modulation space
M^{p}(\mathbb{R})
consists of all tempered distributions
f\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
such that

(27)

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{M^{p}(\mathbb{R})}=\left\lVert V_{\varphi}f\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}<\infty.

Note that
V_{g}g\in M^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
if (and only if)
g\in M^{1}(\mathbb{R})

[
30
, Cor. 5.5]
. Hence, Feichtinger’s algebra is a convenient setting for us, as it contains
\varphi
. Therefore, all manipulations in the sequel are justified and we do not run into any convergence issues (all series we encounter converge at least unconditionally).

The following (auxiliary) results we are going to present are well-known in Gabor analysis, but for the sake of completeness we provide the proof. We start with a result which is known as the
Fundamental Identity of Gabor Analysis
(FIGA). It was first obtained by Janssen
[
32
]
for rectangular lattices and for general lattices it was shown by Feichtinger and Luef
[
21
]
. We also refer to the work of Gröchenig and Koppensteiner
[
24
]
, where all technical details are clarified. The proof uses the symplectic Poisson summation formula (
16
) and the following orthogonality relations:

(28)

\langle V_{g_{1}}f_{1},V_{g_{2}}f_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}=\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\overline{\langle g_{1},g_{2}\rangle}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}.

The proof of (
28
) is by straight forward computation and the interested reader is referred to the textbook of Gröchenig
[
23
, Chap. 3.2]
. Formula (
28
) is the STFT-version of Moyal’s identity (see (
60
) below) for the Wigner distribution introduced in
[
48
]
(see also
[
27
, Chap. 6]
,
[
23
, Chap. 4.3]
).

Proposition 3.2

(Fundamental Identity of Gabor Analysis)
.

Assume we have functions
f_{1},f_{2},g_{1},g_{2}\in M^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then the following identity holds

(FIGA)

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}V_{g_{1}}f_{1}(\lambda)\,\overline{V_{g_{2}}f_{2}(\lambda)}=\frac{1}{\textnormal{vol}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}/\Lambda)}\sum_{\lambda^{\circ}\in\Lambda^{\circ}}V_{g_{1}}g_{2}(\lambda^{\circ})\,\overline{V_{f_{1}}f_{2}(\lambda^{\circ})}.

Proof.

As we assume all functions to be in the modulation space
M^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, all of the following manipulations are justified. We observe that by the commutation relation (
10
) we have

(29)

6.

The presented problems can also be formulated in phase space. For this, we introduce the (cross-)Wigner distribution which for functions

Applying

The function

and

(56)

(64)

\displaystyle A_{\Lambda}\left\lVert W(f,\varphi)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\langle W(f,\varphi),\,W(\rho(\lambda)\varphi,\varphi)\rangle\right|^{2}\leq B_{\Lambda}\left\lVert W(f,\varphi)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}^{2}

(65)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle A_{\Lambda}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\langle f,\rho(\lambda)\varphi)\rangle\right|^{2}\leq B_{\Lambda}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2},

with same optimal constants
A_{\Lambda}
and
B_{\Lambda}
. Hence, problems
1
,
2
,
3
have an equivalent formulation using phase space formalism. Since
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}
is a unitary operator, we have Parseval’s identity and Plancherel’s formula at hand and also have the following equivalent two-sided inequalities:

(66)

\displaystyle A_{\Lambda}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\langle f,\rho(\lambda)\varphi)\rangle\right|^{2}\leq B_{\Lambda}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}

(67)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle A_{\Lambda}\left\lVert A(f,\varphi)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\left|\langle A(f,\varphi),\,A(\rho(\lambda)\varphi,\varphi)\rangle\right|^{2}\leq B_{\Lambda}\left\lVert A(f,\varphi)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2d})}^{2}

The same holds true if
A(f,\varphi)
is replaced by the STFT
V_{\varphi}f
or by the Rihacek distribution, which is the symplectic Fourier transform of the STFT.

7.
Existing (auxiliary) results

In this section we summarize some results on energy minimization and polarization which can be used to solve quantum packing, quantum covering and quantum paving in (quite) specific situations. Quite generally, energy minimization for lattices of a fixed density considers the task

(68)

\text{solve:}\quad\min_{\Lambda}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}p(|\lambda|),

where
p(r)=f(r^{2})
and
f
is completely monotone. The dual problem of polarization for lattices is

(69)

\text{solve:}\quad\max_{\Lambda}\min_{y}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}p(|\lambda+y|),

where
p
is as above. In both cases Gaussians are of special interest due to the Bernstein-Widder theorem.

Theorem

(Bernstein-Widder)
.

Let
f
be a completely monotone function, then there exists a non-negative finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
with distribution function
\mu_{f}
such that

(70)

f(r)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}e^{-\alpha r}d\mu_{f}(\alpha).

Hence, completely monotone functions of squared distance, i.e.,
p(r)=f(r^{2})
,
f
completely monotone, are in the span of the Gaussians
g(\alpha r)
,
\alpha>0
and the constant function (cf.
[
10
]
). For further reading we refer to the article by Cohn and Kumar
[
8
]
or to
[
10
,
18
,
20
]
.

The first result we present is due to Montgomery
[
47
]
and concerns energy minimization in dimension 2.

Theorem

(Montgomery, 1988)
.

Among all lattices of any fixed density and for any fixed
\alpha>0
, the hexagonal lattice
\Lambda_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the unique solution the following problem.

(71)

\text{Solve:}\quad\min_{\Lambda}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}e^{-\pi\alpha|\lambda|^{2}}.

The next result is from
[
4
]
and can be regarded dual to the theorem of Montgomery. It solves, e.g., the polarization problem in dimension 2 as conjectured in
[
29
]
.

Theorem

(Bétermin, Faulhuber, Steinerberger, 2021)
.

Among all lattices of any fixed density and for any fixed
\alpha>0
, the hexagonal lattice
\Lambda_{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the unique solution the following problem.

(72)

\text{Solve:}\quad\max_{\Lambda}\min_{y}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}e^{-\pi\alpha|\lambda+y|^{2}}.

The last result we present certifies the so-called universal optimality of the
\mathsf{E}_{8}
and Leech lattice in dimension 8 and 24, respectively.

Theorem

(Cohn, Kumar, Miller, Radchenko, Viazovska, 2022)
.

Among all lattices of any fixed density and for any fixed
\alpha>0
, the
\mathsf{E}_{8}
and Leech lattice give the unique solution to the following problem in their respective dimension.

(73)

\text{Solve:}\quad\min_{\Lambda}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}e^{-\pi\alpha|\lambda|^{2}}.

We remark that the result hols for periodic point configurations and not only lattices, in contrast to the presented results in dimension 2. These, however, are also expected to hold for general point configurations.

Due to the appearance of the Gaussian function in all of the above results and the fact that

(74)

|\pi(z)\varphi,\pi(\lambda)\varphi|^{2}=e^{-\pi|\lambda-z|^{2}},

we can make take advantages of these results to solve quantum packing, covering and paving in phase space dimension 2 under the assumption that the lattice density is even.

8.
Known solutions and some conjectures

The only case where we know solutions to the quantum packing, covering, and paving problems is for the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
(i.e.,
d=1
and phase space dimension is 2) and only if we consider solely lattices where the density is an even integer. This is a consequence of combining the polarization result from
[
4
]
, the energy minimization result from
[
47
]
(see also
[
14
]
) and a result of Janssen on Gabor frame bounds
[
31
]
.

Theorem

(Bétermin, Faulhuber, Steinerberger, 2021)
.

Let
d=1
, so phase space is
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Consider the quantum problems
1
,
2
,
3
above for lattices of even integer density. Then, for fixed density and within this class of lattices, the hexagonal lattice
\Lambda_{2}
solves all of the above quantum problems simultaneously.

Proof.

We give the sketch of the proof and start with defining the (theta-like) functions in phase space:

(75)

F_{\Lambda}(z)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}e^{-\pi|\lambda+z|^{2}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\widehat{F}_{\Lambda}(z)=\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}e^{-\pi|\lambda|^{2}}e^{2\pi i\sigma(\lambda,z)}.

For a lattice
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the results of Janssen
[
31
]
on Gaussian Gabor systems (see
[
15
]
for the concrete formula we use here) shows that

(76)

A_{\Lambda}=\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\textnormal{vol}(\Lambda)^{-1}\,\widehat{F}_{\Lambda^{\circ}}(z)\quad\text{ and }\quad B_{\Lambda}=\max_{z\in\mathbb{R}}\textnormal{vol}(\Lambda)^{-1}\,\widehat{F}_{\Lambda^{\circ}}(z).

A simple application of the triangle inequality yields

(77)

\displaystyle\left|\textnormal{vol}(\Lambda)^{-1}\sum_{\lambda^{\circ}\in\Lambda^{\circ}}e^{-\pi|\lambda^{\circ}|^{2}}e^{2\pi i\sigma(\lambda^{\circ},z)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{vol}(\Lambda)^{-1}\sum_{\lambda^{\circ}\in\Lambda^{\circ}}\left|e^{-\pi|\lambda^{\circ}|^{2}}\right|\,\left|e^{2\pi i\sigma(\lambda^{\circ},z)}\right|

(78)

\displaystyle=\textnormal{vol}(\Lambda)^{-1}\sum_{\lambda^{\circ}\in\Lambda^{\circ}}e^{-\pi|\lambda^{\circ}|^{2}},

which shows that the maximum is always taken at a lattice point
\lambda\in\Lambda
(in particular at the origin). Therefore, Montgomery’s result
[
47
]
implies that
B_{\Lambda}
is minimal if and only if
\Lambda
is the hexagonal lattice
\Lambda_{2}
. The polarization theorem in
[
4
]
implies that
A_{\Lambda}
is maximal if and only if the lattice is hexagonal and, hence, the quantum paving problem is uniquely solved by the hexagonal lattice under the assumptions of the theorem.
∎

Note that finding the minimum of the function
F_{\Lambda}(z)
or, equivalently
\textnormal{vol}(\Lambda)^{-1}\widehat{F}_{\Lambda^{\circ}}(z)
is a difficult task. There are only few rigorous results and generally the minimizer does not only depend on the geometry but also on the density of the lattice. In some exceptional cases, however, the minimizer is static: for
\mathbb{Z}^{2d}
the minimizer of
f_{\mathbb{Z}^{2d}}
is always taken at
(\mathbb{Z}+1/2)^{2d}
and if
\Delta
is a full-rank diagonal matrices, then the minimizer of
f_{\Delta\mathbb{Z}^{2d}}
is found at
\Delta(\mathbb{Z}+1/2)^{2d}
(see
[
31
]
,
[
18
]
). To the best of our knowledge, the only other lattice for which we know the minimizer of
f_{\Lambda}(z)
is the hexagonal lattice
\Lambda_{2}
, where the minimum is taken at deep holes
[
2
]
.

It is quite remarkable that the recent result of L. Bétermin, M. Faulhuber, and S. Steinerberger
[
4
]
shows that
\Lambda_{2}
maximizes the minimum among all
F_{\Lambda}(z)
for fixed density of
\Lambda
, without knowing the minimizer of
F_{\Lambda}(z)
in general.

Conjecture 8.1
.

Due to the blatant lack of counter examples, we conjecture that the hexagonal lattice solves all quantum problems simultaneously, among periodic configurations of arbitrary fixed density.

In higher dimensions, the lack of knowledge on the characterization of Gabor frames makes it hard to get results on quantum covering and paving. However, for quantum packing we may use Lemma
3.4
and the frame inequality (
2
) to obtain the following estimates on
B_{\Lambda}
.

(79)

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda}|V_{\varphi}\varphi(\lambda)|^{2}\leq B_{\Lambda}\leq\frac{1}{\textnormal{vol}(\mathbb{R}^{2d}/\Lambda)}\sum_{\lambda^{\circ}\in\Lambda^{\circ}}|V_{\varphi}\varphi(\lambda^{\circ})|=\widetilde{B}_{\Lambda}

We conclude with a first partial result in higher dimensions and some more open problems.

Theorem 8.2
.

Let
d\in\{4,12\}
, so the dimension of phase space is 8 or 24. Then the
\mathsf{E}_{8}
lattice and the Leech lattice minimize
\widetilde{B}_{\Lambda}
in their respective dimensions.

Proof.

This follows as a consequence of the universal optimality of the
\mathsf{E}_{8}
and Leech lattice
[
10
]
.
∎

The following conjecture is based on what Robert Calderbank calls “Proof by Intimitation”, which was “employed” by one of the authors in
[
16
]
for Conjecture
8.1
, namely
’what else could it be?’
.

Conjecture 8.3
.

We conjecture that a Leech lattice solves the quantum paving problem in phase space of dimension 24 for arbitrary fixed density greater than 1.

Some arguments in favor of Conjecture
8.3
are the optimality of the Leech lattice for sphere packing
[
9
]
and its universal optimality for energy minimization
[
10
]
combined with the fact that it is still the best known solution to the sphere covering problem and locally optimal among lattice covering
[
52
]
. An issue, however, which needs to be considered is that quantum paving is highly sensitive to permutations of the base vectors and rotations of the lattice (expect for phase space dimension 2). We refer, e.g., to
[
50
]
where some aspects of this problem are discussed. Hence, it is important to note that we conjecture that there is
a
(not
the
) Leech lattice which solves quantum paving, leaving the option that a permutation of the base vectors or a rotation of the classical Leech lattice is needed and that other versions may lead to a non-optimal solution.

To better understand the difficulty, consider the following example. Take
\mathbb{Z}^{4}
with its canonical base in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Let
\alpha,\beta>0
and set, only for the moment,

(80)

\Lambda_{1}(\alpha)=\alpha\begin{pmatrix}\beta&0&0&0\\
0&\beta&0&0\\
0&0&\frac{1}{\beta}&0\\
0&0&0&\frac{1}{\beta}\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{4}\quad\text{ and }\quad\Lambda_{2}(\alpha)=\alpha\begin{pmatrix}\beta&0&0&0\\
0&\frac{1}{\beta}&0&0\\
0&0&\beta&0\\
0&0&0&\frac{1}{\beta}\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{4}.

As lattices,
\Lambda_{1}(\alpha)
and
\Lambda_{2}(\alpha)
would usually be identified. One is obtained from the other by re-labeling the co-ordinates (so they are equivalent by a permutation matrix). However, the matrix generating
\Lambda_{1}(\alpha)
is symplectic, whereas
\Lambda_{2}(\alpha)
is not a symplectic lattice (unless
\beta=1
). A consequence for Gabor systems in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with 2-dimensional Gaussian function
\varphi(t_{1},t_{2})=2^{1/2}e^{-\pi(t_{1}^{2}+t_{2}^{2})}
is

(81)

\mathcal{G}(\varphi,\Lambda_{1}(\alpha))\text{ is a frame}\Longleftrightarrow\alpha<1,\quad\text{ whereas }\quad\mathcal{G}(\varphi,\Lambda_{2}(\alpha))\text{ is a frame}\Longleftrightarrow\alpha\beta<1\,\wedge\,\alpha/\beta<1.

This follows from the characterization results on univariate Gaussian Gabor frames
[
43
]
,
[
53
]
,
[
54
]
in combination with results on tensor Gabor systems
[
6
]
(see also
[
50
, Prop. 3]
).

In phase space dimension 8 it is even more delicate to come up with a first guess. While the
\mathsf{E}_{8}
lattice yields the best sphere packing in dimension 8
[
62
]
and is universally optimal for energy minimization
[
10
]
, it provably fails to give the optimal sphere covering solution: it has worse covering density than the
\mathsf{A}_{8}^{*}
lattice
[
12
]
and is not even locally optimal
[
52
]
. We pose the following question.

Question 1
.

Does an
\mathsf{E}_{8}
lattice solve the quantum paving problem in phase space of dimension 8 for arbitrary fixed density greater than 1?
Indeed, we expect the answer to be ‘
no
’ due to results and an argument (eq. (1.1)) given in
[
17
]
, and the non-optimality of
\mathsf{E}_{8}
regarding the sphere covering problem.

Question 2
.

Is it possible that in some dimension we may have different solutions for different lattice densities for the quantum packing, covering, or paving problem?
