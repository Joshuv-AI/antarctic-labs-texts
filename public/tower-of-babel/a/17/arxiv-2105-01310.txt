Waiting Times for Ties in Random Competitions
Ivan Matic

Abstract
. Multiple teams participate in a random competition. In each round the winner receives one point. We study the times until ties occur among teams. The martingales and supermartingales that vanish at the relevant stopping times do not satisfy the boundedness conditions which are necessary for applying the traditional optional stopping theorems. We use the positivity of the processes to prove inequalities that allow us to avoid the restrictions imposed by optional stopping theorems. The problems studied in this paper are motivated by their applications to databases and their storage engines that are based on augmented balanced search trees. The ties in the competitions are related to the re-balancing operations that have to be executed on the database.

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=

Theorem 1.4

If

The problem that we study can also be placed in a context of

-valued random walks. The available jumps belong to a relatively small set. Consequently, the probability distribution function assigns
0

corresponds to the exit time of a very special region in the space whose boundary is defined as hyperplanes where two of the coordinates are the same.

There are several results related to large deviations and asymptotic behavior of the exit time from cones. The Martin boundary for random walk killed when exiting the first quadrant in

[

. When the random walks are assumed to take jumps whose values are in

the results from
[

]

[
14
]
establish the properties of large deviation events and exponential rates of decay of probabilities that the exit times are large. The asymptotics is studied using harmonic functions
[
10

13
]
. The calculation of the number of random walks in quadrant is an important combinatorial problem. The Tutte’s invariant method can be used to count the number of such walks
[
7

Discrete multidimensional random walks are known to have numerous surprising properties. On the first sight, the most fundamental models would be the walks that occur on hypercubes and related regular graphs. The behaviors of the limiting distributions of the distances are established in
[
6
]
. The times to reach stationary distributions were evaluated using Fourier analysis
[
9
]
.
The next class of finite graphs includes the lattice torus in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. The random walk will cover the entire torus and the properties of the cover times of the late points are obtained in
[
8
]

The random walks in which certain steps are forbidden are analyzed in
[
19
]
. The behavior for the Laplace transform of the expected mean-square displacement was quite different in dimensions 2 and 3.

A future research could analyze the convex minorants of the underlying process
[
1

Balanced binary search trees are the fundamental data structures that enable us to have logarithmic complexity for insertion, deletion, and retrieval of data. The first such tree was constructed by Adelson-Velskii and Landis
[
2
]
. Future generalization of the model to trees in which each parent can have multiple children were obtained by Rudolf Bayer
[
4
]
. Every time we insert or delete data, the tree may need a re-balancing procedure. If several such operations are performed at the same time, a special care needs to be made to maintain the integrity of data. The reduction of re-balancing operations is a very effective way to speed up the operations. Different modifications of the fundamental data structures are made. It is common to introduce the randomness in the construction, as was done in
[
24
]
.

Most of the results in this paper are proved by applying the optional stopping theorem to certain martingales and supermartingales
[
11
,
25
]
. However, we cannot use the standard version of the theorem. The martingales that we construct are not bounded and do not have increments with bounded conditional expectations. In addition, the stopping time could not be easily controlled. In the end it will turn out that the stopping times could have

Another example of a situation where unbounded functions require modifications of the optional stopping theorem occurs in
[
22
]
. There are situations where the theorem has to be avoided completely

,
20
]
.
For additional generalizations of the optional stopping theorem, the reader is referred to

]

Case
m=3

Let us use
A(n)
and
B(n)
instead of
A_{1}(n)
and
A_{2}(n)
. Furthermore, let us denote
a=A(0)
and

. Let
\mathcal{F}_{n}

Theorem 2.1
.

Define the process
\left(G(n)\right)_{n=0}^{\infty}
with

G(n)=A(n)B(n)+\frac{n}{3}.

The process
\left(G(n)\right)_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a martingale with respect to
\left(\mathcal{F}_{n}\right)_{n=0}^{\infty}
.

Proof.

Denote
\alpha(n+1)=A(n+1)-A(n)
and
\beta(n+1)=B(n+1)-B(n)
. Then

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha(n+1)\\
\beta(n+1)\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle\in

\displaystyle\left\{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}-1\\
0\end{array}\right],\left[\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
-1\end{array}\right],\left[\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
1\end{array}\right]\right\}.

The conditional expectation of
G(n+1)
with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.G(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n+1}{3}+\mathbb{E}\left[\left.A(n+1)B(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n+1}{3}+\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\left(A(n)+\alpha(n+1)\right)\left(B(n)+\beta(n+1)\right)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n+1}{3}+A(n)B(n)+A(n)\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\alpha(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle+B(n)\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\beta(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\alpha(n+1)\beta(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right].

Observe that
\alpha(n+1)
and
\beta(n+1)
are independent of
\mathcal{F}_{n}
and that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha(n+1)\right]=0,\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\beta(n+1)\right]=0,\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha(n+1)\beta(n+1)\right]=-\frac{1}{3}.

Therefore
\mathbb{E}\left[\left.G(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]=G(n)
, and
\left(G(n)\right)_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a martingale with respect to
\left(\mathcal{F}_{n}\right)_{n=0}^{\infty}
.
\Box

Theorem 2.2

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A^{2}(n)B(n)\quad\mbox{ and}

\displaystyle G^{B}_{2,1}(n)

\displaystyle=

The conditional expectation of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.G^{A}_{2,1}(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.A^{2}(n+1)B(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\left(A(n)+\alpha(n+1)\right)^{2}\left(B(n)+\beta(n+1)\right)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\left(A^{2}(n)+2\alpha(n+1)A(n)+\alpha^{2}(n+1)\right)\left(B(n)+\beta(n+1)\right)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A^{2}(n)B(n)+2A(n)\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\alpha(n+1)\beta(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]+B(n)\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\alpha^{2}(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\alpha^{2}(n+1)\beta(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A^{2}(n)B(n)-\frac{2}{3}A(n)+\frac{2}{3}B(n)-\frac{1}{3}.

The conditional expectation of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.G^{B}_{2,1}(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\left(B^{2}(n)+2\beta(n+1)B(n)+\beta^{2}(n+1)\right)\left(A(n)+\alpha(n+1)\right)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B^{2}(n)A(n)+2B(n)\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\alpha(n+1)\beta(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]+A(n)\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\beta^{2}(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\beta^{2}(n+1)\alpha(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B^{2}(n)A(n)-\frac{2}{3}B(n)+\frac{2}{3}A(n)+\frac{1}{3}.

It remains to add (
8
) and (

) to conclude that

Theorem 3.2

\displaystyle T_{i,i+1}

\displaystyle\inf\left\{n:A_{i}(n)A_{i+1}(n)=0\right\}.

Theorem 3.3
.

The expected value of
T_{i,i+1}
satisfies

(22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[T_{i,i+1}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ma_{i}a_{i+1}.

Proof.
The proof is almost the same as the proof of Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Assume that
i\in\{1,2,\dots,m-2\}
is fixed. Assume that
a_{i}
and
a_{i+1}
are two fixed positive real numbers. We will prove that for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an integer
N_{0}
such that if
a_{j}\geq N_{0}
for
j\in\{1,2,\dots,m-1\}\setminus\{i,i+1\}
, then

(23)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[T\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle ma_{i}a_{i+1}-\varepsilon.

Let
n_{1}
,
n_{2}
,
\dots
, be the sequence of times for which

\left(A_{i}(n_{k}-1),A_{i+1}(n_{k}-1)\right)\neq\left(A_{i}(n_{k}),A_{i+1}(n_{k})\right).

The quantities
n_{1}
,
n_{2}
,
\dots
are random variables.
Clearly,
A_{i}(n)
and
A_{i+1}(n)
can become
0
only for
n\in\{n_{1},n_{2},\dots\}
.
Define
T^{\prime}
as

T^{\prime}=\min\left\{k:A_{i}(n_{k})A_{i+1}(n_{k})=0\right\}.

For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, let
T^{\prime}_{N}=\min\{T^{\prime},N\}
. Theorem
1.1
implies that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[T^{\prime}_{N}\right]=3a_{i}a_{i+1}.

Therefore, for our fixed
\varepsilon
there exists
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

(24)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[T^{\prime}_{N}\right]\geq 3a_{i}a_{i+1}-\frac{3}{2m}\varepsilon.

Let us denote by
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
,
\dots
, the gaps in the sequence
n_{1}
,
n_{2}
,
\dots
. More precisely,
G_{k}
is defined as

G_{k}=n_{k}-n_{k-1}.

In the last equation we assume that
n_{0}=0
.

Each of the variables
G_{k}
has geometric distribution with parameter
\frac{3}{m}
. Therefore,
\mathbb{E}\left[G_{k}\right]=\frac{m}{3}
for every
k
.
This implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[G_{1}+G_{2}+\cdots+G_{T_{N}^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.G_{1}+G_{2}+\cdots+G_{T_{N}^{\prime}}\right|T_{N}^{\prime}=p\right]\mathbb{P}\left(T_{N}^{\prime}=p\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\left.G_{1}+G_{2}+\cdots+G_{p}\right|T_{N}^{\prime}=p\right]\mathbb{P}\left(T_{N}^{\prime}=p\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p=1}^{N}\frac{mp}{3}\mathbb{P}\left(T_{N}^{\prime}=p\right)=\frac{m}{3}\sum_{p=1}^{N}p\mathbb{P}\left(T_{N}^{\prime}=p\right)=\frac{m}{3}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[T_{N}^{\prime}\right].

We can now use inequality (
24
) to obtain

(25)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[G_{1}+G_{2}+\cdots+G_{T_{N}^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{m}{3}\left(3a_{i}a_{i+1}-\frac{3}{2m}\varepsilon\right)=ma_{i}a_{i+1}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

It suffices to prove that there exists
N_{0}
such that if
a_{j}\geq N_{0}
for every
j\not\in\{i,i+1\}
, then

(26)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[T\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[G_{1}+G_{2}+\cdots+G_{T_{N}^{\prime}}\right]-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Then, inequalities (
25
) and (
26
) would imply (
23
).

So far, we proved that for every choice of
\left(a_{i},a_{i+1}\right)
and every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
N
such that (
24
) holds. The inequality does not depend on values
a_{j}
for
j\not\in\{i,i+1\}
. This is not too surprising since the random variables
T_{N}^{\prime}
,
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
,
\dots
do not depend on
a_{j}
for
j\not\in\{i,i+1\}
.
Recall that
T_{n}=\min\{T,n\}
is the truncation of the stopping time
T
. The stopping times
T
and
T_{n}
depend on all
a_{j}
, for
j\in\{1,2,\dots,m-1\}

\hat{G}_{N}=G_{1}+G_{2}+\dots+G_{T^{\prime}_{N}}
and let

be the event defined as

Q_{n}=\{T_{n}\geq\hat{G}_{N}\}.

For every
n
and every choice of
a_{j}
with
j\not\in\{i,i+1\}
we have

(27)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[T\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}\cdot 1_{Q_{n}}\right]\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{G}_{N}\cdot 1_{Q_{n}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{G}_{N}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{G}_{N}\cdot 1_{Q_{n}^{C}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[G_{1}+G_{2}+\cdots+G_{T_{N}^{\prime}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{G}_{N}\cdot 1_{Q_{n}^{C}}\right].

We will now prove that there exists
N_{0}
such that whenever
a_{j}\geq N_{0}
for
j\not\in\{i,i+1\}
, the following inequality holds:

(28)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{G}_{N}\cdot 1_{Q_{n}^{C}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

Then, the inequalities (
27
) and (
28
) would imply that (
26
) holds whenever
a_{j}\geq N_{0}
for
j\not\in\{i,i+1\}
.
Observe that
\hat{G}_{N}=G_{1}+G_{2}+\cdots+G_{T^{\prime}_{N}}\leq G_{1}+G_{2}+\cdots+G_{N}
and that
G_{1}+\cdots+G_{N}
has negative binomial distribution with parameters
N
and
\frac{m-3}{m}
. Here we use the following convention: A negative binomial random variable with parameters
r
and
p
is the total number of Bernoulli trials until
r
failures, where the probability of success in each trial is
p
. The second moment of negative binomial random variable

is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(G_{1}+\cdots+G_{N}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{r(r+p)}{(1-p)^{2}}=\frac{N\left(N+\frac{m-3}{m}\right)}{\frac{9}{m^{2}}}=\frac{mN\left(mN+m-3\right)}{9}.

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality implies

(29)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{G}_{N}\cdot 1_{Q_{n}^{C}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{G}_{N}^{2}\right]}\cdot\sqrt{\mathbb{P}\left(Q_{n}^{C}\right)}=\sqrt{\frac{mN\left(mN+m-3\right)}{9}}\sqrt{\mathbb{P}\left(Q_{n}^{c}\right)}.

Let us now analyze the event
Q_{n}^{C}
.

\displaystyle Q_{n}^{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{T_{n}<\hat{G}_{N}\right\}\subseteq\left\{T_{n}<G_{1}+G_{2}+\cdots+G_{T^{\prime}}\right\}.

Observe that
G_{1}+G_{2}+\cdots+G_{T^{\prime}}=T_{i,i+1}
where
T_{i,i+1}
is defined by (
21
) and is the smallest
k
for which
A_{i}(k)A_{i+1}(k)=0
. Therefore

(30)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Q_{n}^{C}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(T_{n}<T_{i,i+1}\right).

From Theorem
3.3
we know that
\mathbb{E}\left[T_{i,i+1}\right]=ma_{i}a_{i+1}<+\infty
. Therefore, we must have

\lim_{w\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(T_{i,i+1}>w\right)=0.

This means that for given
\varepsilon>0
there exists
N_{0}
such that
n\geq N_{0}
implies

(31)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(T_{i,i+1}>n\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4\cdot\frac{mN\left(mN+m-3\right)}{9}}.

We will now prove that if
a_{j}>N_{0}
for all
j\in\{1,2,\dots,m-1\}\setminus\{i,i+1\}
, then

(32)

\displaystyle\left\{T_{i,i+1}>T_{N_{0}}\right\}\subseteq\left\{T_{i,i+1}>N_{0}\right\}.

Indeed, if all
a_{j}
for
j\not\in\{i,i+1\}
are bigger than
N_{0}
, then in the first
N_{0}
steps none of the random variables
A_{j}
can reach
0
. Therefore, on the event
\left\{T\leq N_{0}\right\}
, the random variable
T
is equal to
T_{i,i+1}
. The event
\{T_{i,i+1}>T_{N_{0}}\}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left\{T_{i,i+1}>T_{N_{0}}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{T_{i,i+1}>T_{N_{0}},T<N_{0}\right\}\cup\left\{T_{i,i+1}>T_{N_{0}},T\geq N_{0}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{T_{i,i+1}>T,T<N_{0}\right\}\cup\left\{T_{i,i+1}>{N_{0}},T\geq N_{0}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\emptyset\cup\left\{T_{i,i+1}>{N_{0}},T\geq N_{0}\right\}.

The last equality clearly implies (
32
). We now use (
30
), (
31
), and (
32
) with
n=N_{0}
to derive

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Q_{N_{0}}^{C}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(T_{N_{0}}<T_{i,i+1}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(N_{0}<T_{i,i+1}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4\cdot\frac{mN\left(mN+m-3\right)}{9}}.

The last inequality together with (
29
) implies (
28
). As discussed earlier, (
28
) immediately implies (
26
) under the conditions
a_{j}\geq N_{0}
for
j\not\in\{i,i+1\}
. Together with (
25
), the inequality (
26
) implies (
23
), which completes the proof of the theorem.

\Box

4.
Improved bound for
m=4

The vector-valued random process
\overrightarrow{A}(n)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}A(n)\\
B(n)\\
C(n)\end{array}\right]

starts from
\overrightarrow{A}(0)=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}a\\
b\\
c\end{array}\right]
for some
a
,
b
,
c\in\mathbb{N}
.
In each step we have
\overrightarrow{A}(n+1)=\overrightarrow{A}(n)+\overrightarrow{\xi}(n+1)
, where
\overrightarrow{\xi}(1)
,
\overrightarrow{\xi}(2)
,
\dots
are independent random vectors with uniform distribution on the set

V=\left\{\left[\begin{array}[]{c}-1\\
0\\
0\end{array}\right],\left[\begin{array}[]{c}1\\
-1\\
0\end{array}\right],\left[\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
1\\
-1\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
0\\
1\end{array}\right]\right\}.

Let
\varphi:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
be the function defined in the following way:

\displaystyle\varphi(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\max\{x,y\},&\mbox{if }x\neq y,\\
\frac{2x^{2}}{2x-1},&\mbox{if }x=y.\end{array}\right.

Let us define the following process:

(36)

\displaystyle M(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{A(n)B(n)C(n)}{\varphi\left(A(n),C(n)\right)}+\frac{n}{4}.

Theorem 4.1
.

The processes
\left(M(n)\right)_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a supermartingale with respect to the filtration
\left(\mathcal{F}_{n}\right)_{n=0}^{\infty}
of sigma algebras generated by the processes
A(n)
,
B(n)
, and
C(n)
. In other words,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left.M(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]\leq M(n).

Proof.

The conditional expectation of
M(n+1)
with respect to the sigma algebra
\mathcal{F}_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.M(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n+1}{4}+\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\frac{A(n+1)B(n+1)C(n+1)}{\varphi\left(A(n+1),C(n+1)\right)}\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n+1}{4}+\frac{\left(A(n)-1\right)B(n)C(n)}{4\varphi(A(n)-1,C(n))}+\frac{\left(A(n)+1\right)\left(B(n)-1\right)C(n)}{4\varphi(A(n)+1,C(n))}

\displaystyle+\frac{A(n)\left(B(n)+1\right)\left(C(n)-1\right)}{4\varphi(A(n),C(n)-1)}+\frac{A(n)B(n)\left(C(n)+1\right)}{4\varphi(A(n),C(n)+1)}.

In order to prove that
\left(M(n)\right)_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a supermartingale it suffices to show that for all
x
,
y
,
z\in\mathbb{N}
the following inequality holds

(37)

\displaystyle\frac{4xyz}{\varphi(x,z)}-1

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{(x-1)yz}{\varphi(x-1,z)}+\frac{(x+1)(y-1)z}{\varphi(x+1,z)}+\frac{x(y+1)(z-1)}{\varphi(x,z-1)}+\frac{xy(z+1)}{\varphi(x,z+1)}.

We will distinguish the following
5
cases.

1^{\circ}

x=z
;

2^{\circ}

x=z+1
;

3^{\circ}

x=z-1
;

4^{\circ}

x\geq z+2
;

5^{\circ}

x\leq z-2
.

In case
1^{\circ}
the inequality (
37
) turns into an equality. The following holds

(38)

\displaystyle\frac{4x^{2}y}{\frac{2x^{2}}{2x-1}}-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(x-1)yx}{x}+\frac{(x+1)(y-1)x}{(x+1)}+\frac{x(y+1)(x-1)}{x}+\frac{xy(x+1)}{(x+1)}.

The left-hand side is equal to
L_{1}(x,y)=2y(2x-1)-1
and the right-hand side
is
R_{1}(x,y)=(x-1)y+x(y-1)+(x-1)(y+1)+xy
. After simple algebraic transformations one can see that the polynomials
L_{1}(x,y)
and
R_{2}(x,y)
are equal. We conclude that (
38
) is true in Case
1^{\circ}
.

In case
2^{\circ}
the inequality (
37
) transforms into

(39)

\displaystyle\frac{4xy(x-1)}{x}-1

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{(x-1)y(x-1)}{\frac{2(x-1)^{2}}{2(x-1)-1}}+\frac{(x+1)(y-1)(x-1)}{(x+1)}+\frac{x(y+1)(x-2)}{x}+\frac{xyx}{\frac{2x^{2}}{2x-1}}.

After canceling the fractions we obtain that the left-hand side of (
39
) is
L_{2}(x,y)=4y(x-1)-1
and the right-hand side is
R_{2}(x,y)=y(x-\frac{3}{2})+(y-1)(x-1)+(y+1)(x-2)+y\left(x-\frac{1}{2}\right)
. The difference between the left-hand side and the right-hand side is
y
, which is a non-negative integer. Therefore (
37
) holds in Case
2^{\circ}
.

Let us now consider the case
3^{\circ}
.
The inequality (
37
) is equivalent to

(40)

\displaystyle\frac{4xy(x+1)}{(x+1)}-1

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{(x-1)y(x+1)}{(x+1)}+\frac{(x+1)(y-1)(x+1)}{\frac{2(x+1)^{2}}{2(x+1)-1}}+\frac{x(y+1)x}{\frac{2x^{2}}{2x-1}}+\frac{xy(x+2)}{x+2}.

The left-hand side is
L_{3}(x,y)=4xy-1
and the right-hand side is
R_{3}(x,y)=(x-1)y+(y-1)\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)+(y+1)\left(x-\frac{1}{2}\right)+xy
. The difference between the two polynomials becomes
L_{3}(x,y)-R_{3}(x,y)=y
. This is always a non-negative number, hence (
37
) holds in Case
3^{\circ}
.

In each of the cases
4^{\circ}
and
5^{\circ}
the inequality (
37
) turns into equality.
In case
4^{\circ}
we have

(41)

\displaystyle\frac{4xyz}{x}-1

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{(x-1)yz}{x-1}+\frac{(x+1)(y-1)z}{x+1}+\frac{x(y+1)(z-1)}{x}+\frac{xy(z+1)}{x}.

The left-hand side is the polynomial
L_{4}(y,z)=4yz-1
, while the right-hand side is the polynomial

R_{4}(y,z)=yz+(y-1)z+(y+1)(z-1)+y(z+1)=4yz-z+z-y-1+y=4yz-1.

It is easy to verify that
L_{4}(y,z)=R_{4}(y,z)
.
In case
5^{\circ}
we obtain that the left-hand side of (
37
) is

L_{5}(x,y)=4xy-1
while the right-hand side is

R_{5}(x,y)=(x-1)y+(x+1)(y-1)+x(y+1)+xy=4xy-y+y-x+x-1=4xy-1.

The polynomials
L_{5}(x,y)
and
R_{5}(x,y)
are equal.
This completes the casework. Thus, the process
\left(M(n)\right)_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a supermartingale.
\Box

5.
Second moments

Lemma 5.1
.

For fixed
i\in\{1,2,\dots,m-2\}
, let us define the process
\left(H_{i,i+1}(n)\right)_{n=0}^{\infty}
as

(42)

\displaystyle H_{i,i+1}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{i}^{2}(n)A_{i+1}^{2}(n)+\frac{2}{3}A_{i}(n)A_{i+1}(n)\left(A_{i}^{2}(n)+A_{i+1}^{2}(n)\right).

The process

satisfies

(43)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.H_{i,i+1}(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H_{i,i+1}(n)+\frac{4}{m}A_{i}(n)A_{i+1}(n)-\frac{1}{3m}.

Proof.

We will first establish the following identities.

(44)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.A_{i}^{2}(n+1)A_{i+1}^{2}(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{i}^{2}(n)A_{i+1}^{2}(n)+\frac{1}{m}\left(2A_{i}^{2}(n)+2A_{i+1}^{2}(n)\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{m}\left(2A_{i}(n)-2A_{i+1}(n)-4A_{i}(n)A_{i+1}(n)+1\right),

(45)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.A_{i}^{3}(n+1)A_{i+1}(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{i}^{3}(n)A_{i+1}(n)+\frac{1}{m}\cdot 6A_{i}(n)A_{i+1}(n)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{m}\left(-3A_{i}^{2}(n)-3A_{i}(n)-1\right),

(46)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.A_{i}(n+1)A_{i+1}^{3}(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A_{i}(n)A_{i+1}^{3}(n)+\frac{1}{m}\cdot 6A_{i}(n)A_{i+1}(n)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\left(-3A_{i+1}^{2}(n)+3A_{i+1}(n)-1\right).

The proof of each of equalities (
44
), (
45
), and (
46
) is based on the observation that

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}A_{i}(n+1)\\
A_{i+1}(n+1)\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}A_{i}(n)\\
A_{i+1}(n)\end{array}\right]

holds with probability
\frac{m-3}{m}
. The vector
\left[\begin{array}[]{c}A_{i}(n+1)\\
A_{i+1}(n+1)\end{array}\right]
is equal to one of the vectors from the set

A_{i+1}(n)\end{array}\right],\left[\begin{array}[]{c}A_{i}(n)+1\\
A_{i+1}(n)-1\end{array}\right],\left[\begin{array}[]{c}A_{i}(n)\\
A_{i+1}(n)-1\end{array}\right]\right\}.

Each of the last vectors from
V_{i,i+1}(n)
occurs with probability
\frac{1}{m}
.
Now, the left-hand side of equality (
44
) can be transformed into

(47)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.A_{i}^{2}(n+1)A_{i+1}^{2}(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m-3}{m}A_{i}^{2}(n)A_{i+1}^{2}(n)+\frac{1}{m}\left(A_{i}(n)-1\right)^{2}A_{i+1}^{2}(n)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{m}\left(A_{i}(n)+1\right)^{2}\left(A_{i+1}-1\right)^{2}(n)+\frac{1}{m}A_{i}^{2}(n)\left(A_{i+1}(n)+1\right)^{2}.

Elementary algebraic transformations can be now used to turn the equality (
47
) into (
44
).
We will now prove the equality (
45
). To make notation shorter we will omit the argument of
A_{i}
and
A_{i+1}
, if it is equal to
n
. We will write
A_{i}
and
A_{i+1}
instead of
A_{i}(n)
and
A_{i+1}(n)
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.A_{i}^{3}(n+1)A_{i+1}(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m-3}{m}A_{i}^{3}A_{i+1}+\frac{1}{m}\left(A_{i}^{3}-3A_{i}^{2}+3A_{i}-1\right)A_{i+1}

Theorem 5.1
.

Define the process

(49)

\displaystyle R(n)=H_{i,i+1}(n)-\frac{4n}{m}A_{i}(n)A_{i+1}(n).

The random variable
M_{i,i+1}(n)
can be written in terms of
R(n)
in the following way

(50)

\displaystyle M_{i,i+1}(n)=R(n)-\frac{2}{m^{2}}n^{2}+\left(\frac{1}{3m}-\frac{2}{m^{2}}\right)n.

We can use (
19
) and (
43
) to calculate the conditional expectation of

. Using (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left.R(n+1)\right|\mathcal{F}_{n}\right]

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

We will use the method of contradiction to prove that
\mathbb{E}\left[T^{2}\right]=+\infty
. Assume the contrary, that
\mathbb{E}\left[T^{2}\right]<+\infty
. Let
T_{n}=\min\{T,n\}
. Lebesgue monotone convergence theorem implies that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[T^{2}\right].

We will write
A(n)
and
B(n)
instead of
A_{1}(n)
and
A_{2}(n)
. We will also use the notation
a=A(0)
and
b=B(0)
.
Let us define the random variables
M_{1,2}(n)
for
n\in\{0,1,2\dots\}
in the following way

\displaystyle M_{1,2}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A^{2}(n)B^{2}(n)+\frac{2}{3}A(n)B(n)\left(A^{2}(n)+B^{2}(n)\right)-\frac{4}{3}nA(n)B(n)-\frac{2n^{2}}{3}-\frac{1}{9}n.

According to Theorem
5.1
, the process
\left(M_{1,2}(n)\right)_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a martingale with respect to
\left(\mathcal{F}_{n}\right)_{n=0}^{\infty}
. The optional stopping theorem implies

(52)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[A^{2}(T_{n})B^{2}(T_{n})+\frac{2}{3}A(T_{n})B(T_{n})\left(A^{2}(T_{n})+B^{2}(T_{n})\right)-\frac{4}{3}T_{n}A(T_{n})B(T_{n})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{2}b^{2}+\frac{2}{3}ab\left(a^{2}+b^{2}\right)+\frac{2}{3}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{9}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}\right].

If we were able to justify passing to the limit as
n\to\infty
in (
52
) we would get an immediate contradiction. The left-hand side would be equal to
0
and the right-hand side is obviously positive. However, it is not clear how to justify the change of order of integral and limit. We will build an argument that uses Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Let us denote by
L(n)
and
R(n)
the left-hand side and right-hand side of (
52
). Formally,

\displaystyle L(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[A^{2}(T_{n})B^{2}(T_{n})+\frac{2}{3}A(T_{n})B(T_{n})\left(A^{2}(T_{n})+B^{2}(T_{n})\right)-\frac{4}{3}T_{n}A(T_{n})B(T_{n})\right],

\displaystyle R(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{2}b^{2}+\frac{2}{3}ab\left(a^{2}+b^{2}\right)+\frac{2}{3}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{9}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}\right].

Let us add
\frac{4}{9}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}^{2}\right]
to both left and right side of (
52
). We obtain the equation

(53)

\displaystyle\hat{L}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{R}(n),\quad\mbox{where}

\displaystyle\hat{L}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[A^{2}(T_{n})B^{2}(T_{n})-\frac{4}{3}T_{n}A(T_{n})B(T_{n})+\frac{4}{9}T_{n}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2}{3}A(T_{n})B(T_{n})\left(A^{2}(T_{n})+B^{2}(T_{n})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\hat{R}(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{2}b^{2}+\frac{2}{3}ab\left(a^{2}+b^{2}\right)+\frac{10}{9}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{9}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}\right].

Consider the first expectation in the definition of
\hat{L}(n)
and denote it by
\lambda
, i.e.

\lambda=\mathbb{E}\left[A^{2}(T_{n})B^{2}(T_{n})-\frac{4}{3}T_{n}A(T_{n})B(T_{n})+\frac{4}{9}T_{n}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(A(T_{n})B(T_{n})-\frac{2}{9}T_{n}\right)^{2}\right].

Clearly
\lambda\geq 0
and (

(54)

\displaystyle a^{2}b^{2}+\frac{2}{3}ab\left(a^{2}+b^{2}\right)+\frac{10}{9}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}^{2}\right]+\frac{1}{9}\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{2}{3}A(T_{n})B(T_{n})\left(A^{2}(T_{n})+B^{2}(T_{n})\right)\right].

Let
g(n)
be the function defined as

(55)

\displaystyle g(n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle A(T_{n})B(T_{n})\left(A^{2}(T_{n})+B^{2}(T_{n})\right).

Due to our assumption that
\mathbb{E}\left[T_{n}^{2}\right]
is finite, we have that the left-hand side of (
54
) is convergent and, therefore, bounded. Hence, there exist a positive real number
D
such that the inequality

(56)

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[g(n)\right]

holds for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Using (
11
) and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we obtain that the following inequality also holds for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

(57)

\displaystyle ab(a+b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sqrt{A\left(T_{n}\right)B\left(T_{n}\right)}\cdot\sqrt{A\left(T_{n}\right)B\left(T_{n}\right)}\left(A\left(T_{n}\right)+B\left(T_{n}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[A\left(T_{n}\right)B\left(T_{n}\right)\right]}\cdot\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[g(n)\right]}.

From (
13
), the first factor
\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[A\left(T_{n}\right)B\left(T_{n}\right)\right]}
on the right-hand side of (

) converges to

as

. From (
56
) we have that the factor
\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[g(n)\right]}
is bounded by

. Therefore, as
n\to\infty
, the right-hand side of (

) converges to

. The left-hand side of (

. This is a contradiction that proves that

6.
Further directions

The expected values of the stopping times
T
are difficult to calculate when
m\geq 4
and we don’t yet have the exact formulas. We have seen that the
L^{1}
norms are finite. In order to use the optimal stopping theorem, we need to first construct functions that generate convenient martingales. The function
H:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
will be called
perfect time martingale generator
if for some constant
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
it satisfies

(58)

\displaystyle H\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\right)-\gamma

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k+1}\left(H\left(x_{1}-1,\dots,x_{k}\right)\right.

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}H\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{i}+1,x_{i+1}-1,\dots,x_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\left.+H\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1},x_{k}+1\right)\right),\quad\mbox{and}

(59)

\displaystyle H\left(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,\quad\mbox{if }\;x_{1}x_{2}\cdots x_{k}=0.

If
H
is a perfect time martingale generator, then
H\left(\overrightarrow{A}(n)\right)+\gamma n
is a martingale.

Problem 6.1
.

Find functions
H
that satisfy (
58
) and (
59
).

The process
H\left(\overrightarrow{A}(n)\right)+\gamma n
is a martingale even if just (
58
) holds. However, unless the condition (
59
) holds as well, the martingale is not of a big use in calculating
\mathbb{E}[T]
. There are quite a few functions that satisfy (
58
) but not (
59
). Examples are:
x_{i}^{2}
and
x_{i}x_{i+1}
. We used these functions to construct supermartingales.

It is clear that there are functions that satisfy (
58
) and (
59
) at least for integers. We can simply define them as

H\left(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{k}\right)=\tau\left(z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\right).

One would expect that they could be extended from
\mathbb{Z}^{k}
to
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.
However, the search for functions that satisfy (
58
) and (
59
) is not easy even numerically.

To guarantee that
H
satisfies (
58
) and (
59
), we make a substitution

(60)

\displaystyle H\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\right)=x_{1}\cdots x_{k}F\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\right).

If
H
and
F
satisfy the relation (
60
) then the equation (
58
) implies that for positive real numbers
x_{1}
,
\dots
,
x_{k}
, the following equality holds:

(61)

\displaystyle F(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})-\frac{\gamma}{x_{1}\cdots x_{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k+1}\left(\frac{x_{1}-1}{x_{1}}F\left(x_{1}-1,\dots,x_{k}\right)\right.

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{1+x_{i}}{x_{i}}\cdot\frac{1-x_{i+1}}{x_{i+1}}F\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{i}+1,x_{i+1}-1,\dots,x_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{1+x_{k}}{x_{k}}F\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{k-1},x_{k}+1\right)\right).

Let us denote
u_{i}=\frac{1}{x_{i}}
and

f(u_{1},u_{2},\dots,u_{k})=F\left(\frac{1}{u_{1}},\dots,\frac{1}{u_{k}}\right).

The equation (
61
) can be written in terms of variables
u_{1}
,
\dots
,
u_{k}
as

(62)

\displaystyle f(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})-\gamma u_{1}\cdots u_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k+1}\left(\left(1-u_{1}\right)f\left(\frac{u_{1}}{1-u_{1}},\dots,u_{k}\right)\right.

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\left(1+u_{i}\right)\left(1-u_{i+1}\right)f\left(u_{1},\dots,\frac{u_{i}}{1+u_{i}},\frac{u_{i+1}}{1-u_{i+1}},\dots,u_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\left.+\left(1+u_{k}\right)f\left(u_{1},\dots,u_{k-1},\frac{u_{k}}{1+u_{k}}\right)\right).

We will rewrite the equation (
62
) using generalized convolution operators.

6.1.
Generalized convolution

Let
I
be a finite set. The set
I
will be considered to be a set of indices. A multidimensional sequence
\alpha
is a function whose domain is
\mathbb{N}^{I}
and codomain
\mathbb{R}
. The elements of
\mathbb{N}^{I}
are functions from
I
to
\mathbb{N}
. There are two ways to characterize
\alpha
as a function. The first way is to write
\alpha:\mathbb{N}^{I}\to\mathbb{R}
, and the second way is
\alpha:\left(I\to\mathbb{N}\right)\to\mathbb{R}
.

For fixed
i\geq 0
, let us consider the functions
\rho^{\pm}_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{(1\pm x)^{i}}
. Let us denote by
\left(\varphi^{\pm}(i,k)\right)_{k=0}^{\infty}
the sequence for which

(63)

\displaystyle\rho^{\pm}_{i}(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\varphi^{\pm}(i,k)x^{k}.

Let
M=\{1,2,\dots,m\}
. Denote by
\mathcal{A}_{m}
the set of functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
that have power series representations of the form

f\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\right)=\sum_{\theta\in\mathbb{N}^{M}}\alpha(\theta)x_{1}^{\theta(1)}x_{2}^{\theta(2)}\cdots x_{m}^{\theta(m)},

for some
|M|
-dimensional sequence
\alpha:\mathbb{N}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}
.

Let us define the operators
L_{k}^{\pm}:\mathcal{A}_{m}\to\mathcal{A}_{m}
in the following way. For given
f\in\mathcal{A}_{m}
we define
L_{k}^{\pm}(f)
to be the function that satisfies

(64)

\displaystyle L_{k}^{\pm}(f)\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f\left(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k-1},\frac{x_{k}}{1\pm x_{k}},x_{k+1},\dots,x_{m}\right).

Denote by
\alpha:\mathbb{N}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}
the generating sequence of
f
. Now we will determine the generating sequence of
L_{k}^{\pm}(f)
.

\displaystyle L_{k}^{\pm}(f)\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\theta\in\mathbb{N}^{M}}\alpha(\theta)x_{1}^{\theta(1)}\cdots x_{m}^{\theta(m)}\cdot\rho^{\pm}_{\theta(k)}(x_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\theta\in\mathbb{N}^{M}}\sum_{l}\alpha(\theta)x_{1}^{\theta(1)}\cdots x_{m}^{\theta(m)}\varphi^{\pm}(\theta(k),l)x_{k}^{l}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\theta\in\mathbb{N}^{M}}\sum_{l}\alpha(\theta)\varphi^{\pm}(\theta(k),l)x_{1}^{\theta(1)}\cdots x_{k}^{\theta(k)+l}\cdots x_{m}^{\theta(m)}.

The generating sequence
\beta:\mathbb{N}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}
for
L_{k}^{\pm}(f)
satisfies

\displaystyle\beta(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum\Big\{\alpha(\mu)\varphi^{\pm}(\nu):\mu\in\mathbb{N}^{M},\nu\in\mathbb{N}^{2},P_{\{k\}^{C}}\left(\mu\right)=P_{\{k\}^{C}}\left(\theta\right),

\displaystyle P_{\{k\}}(\mu)=P_{\{1\}}(\nu),P_{\{k\}}(\mu)+P_{\{2\}}(\nu)=P_{\{k\}}(\theta)\Big\},

where for
\eta\in\mathbb{N}^{Q}
and
R\subseteq Q
, the projection
P_{R}(\eta)
of
\eta
to
R
defined as
P_{R}(\eta)(r)=\eta(r)
for all
r\in R
.

The operators
L_{k}^{\pm}
on the set
\mathcal{A}_{m}
naturally generate the operators
S_{k}^{\pm}
on the set of sequences
\mathbb{N}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}
. If for
\alpha:\mathbb{N}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}
there is a generating function
f\in A_{m}
, then we define
S_{k}^{\pm}(\alpha)
to be the generating sequence for the function
L_{k}^{\pm}(f)
.

We will now prove that for
k_{1}\neq k_{2}
, the following equalities hold:

S_{k_{1}}^{\pm}\circ S_{k_{2}}^{\pm}=S_{k_{2}}^{\pm}\circ S_{k_{1}}^{\pm}.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{M}\to\mathbb{R}
. Then
S_{k_{1}}^{\pm}(\alpha)
satisfies

\displaystyle S_{k_{1}}^{\pm}(\alpha)(\theta)=\sum_{i=0}^{\theta(k_{1})}\alpha\left(\theta(1),\dots,\theta({k_{1}}-1),i,\theta({k_{1}}+1),\dots,m\right)\varphi^{\pm}(i,\theta(k_{1})-i).

From here we obtain that
S_{k_{2}}^{\pm}\left(S_{k_{1}}^{\pm}(\alpha)\right)(\theta)
satisfies

(65)

\displaystyle S_{k_{2}}^{\pm}\left(S_{k_{1}}^{\pm}(\alpha)\right)(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\theta(k_{2})}S_{k_{1}}^{\pm}(\alpha)\left(\theta(1),\dots,\theta({k_{2}}-1),j,\theta({k_{2}}+1),\dots,m\right)\varphi^{\pm}(j,\theta(k_{2})-j)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\theta(k_{2})}\varphi^{\pm}(j,\theta(k_{2})-j)\Big(\sum_{i=0}^{\theta(k_{1})}\alpha\Big(\theta(1),\dots,\theta({k_{1}}-1),i,\theta(k_{1}+1),

\displaystyle\quad\quad\dots,\theta({k_{2}}-1),j,\theta({k_{2}}+1),\dots,m\Big)\varphi^{\pm}(i,\theta(k_{1})-i)\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{\theta(k_{1})}\sum_{j=0}^{\theta(k_{2})}\alpha\Big(\theta(1),\dots,\theta({k_{1}}-1),i,\theta(k_{1}+1),

\displaystyle\quad\quad\dots,\theta({k_{2}}-1),j,\theta({k_{2}}+1),\dots,m\Big)\times

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\times\varphi^{\pm}(i,\theta(k_{1})-i)\varphi^{\pm}(j,\theta(k_{2})-j).

In a similar way we prove that
S_{k_{2}}^{\pm}\left(S_{k_{1}}^{\pm}(\alpha)\right)(\theta)
is equal to the double summation on the right-hand side of equation (
65
).

6.2.
Searching for solutions

The definition of sequences
\varphi^{\pm}:\mathbb{N}^{\{1,2\}}\to\mathbb{R}
from (
63
) and the definition of the operators
L_{k}^{\pm}
from (
64
) can be now used to re-write the equation (
62
) as

(66)

\displaystyle f\left(u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\right)-\gamma u_{1}\cdots u_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k+1}\left(\left(1-u_{1}\right)L_{1}^{-}f\left(u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\right)\right.

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}\left(1+u_{i}\right)\left(1-u_{i+1}\right)L_{i}^{+}\circ L_{i+1}^{-}f\left(u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\right)

\displaystyle\left.+\left(1+u_{k}\right)L_{k}^{+}f\left(u_{1},\dots,u_{k}\right)\right).

For
k=2
, one solution that works is
f(u_{1},u_{2})=1
. For
k\geq 2
, the above equation has undesireable solutions. For example, for
k=3
, the functions
f_{1}(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})=u_{1}
and
f_{3}(u_{1},u_{2},u_{3})=u_{3}
are the solutions. However, since the variables
u_{i}
are reciprocals of
x_{i}
, the corresponding functions
H
would not satisfy (
59
). If we put the additional requirement that
f(0,0)\neq 0
, then (
66
) gives us unsolvable system of equations for the coefficients of generating sequence for
f
.

Another approach in the case
k=3
is to consider the function
F
of the form

(67)

\displaystyle F(x,y,z)=\frac{G(x,y,z)}{x+z},

where
G
is a bounded function. Here is the intuition behind the idea to search for functions
F
of the above form. As
x\to\infty
the function
H(x,y,z)
converges to
3yz
. Similarly, as
z\to\infty
, the function
H(x,y,z)
converges to
3xy
.

We can re-write the equation (
61
) as

(68)

\displaystyle\frac{G(x,y,z)}{x+z}-\frac{\gamma}{xyz}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{x-1}{x}\cdot\frac{G(x-1,y,z)}{x+z-1}+\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{x+1}{x}\cdot\frac{y-1}{y}\cdot\frac{G(x+1,y-1,z)}{x+z+1}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{y+1}{y}\cdot\frac{z-1}{z}\cdot\frac{G(x,y+1,z-1)}{x+z-1}+\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{z+1}{z}\cdot\frac{G(x,y,z+1)}{x+z+1}.\quad\quad

Let us introduce the substitutions
u=\frac{1}{x}
,
v=\frac{1}{y}
,
z=\frac{1}{w}
, and

g(u,v,w)=G\left(\frac{1}{u},\frac{1}{v},\frac{1}{w}\right)=G\left(x,y,z\right).

The equation (
68
) becomes

(69)

\displaystyle\frac{g(u,v,w)}{u+w}-\gamma v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\cdot(1-u)\cdot\frac{g\left(\frac{u}{1-u},v,w\right)}{u+w-uw}+\frac{1}{4}\cdot(1+u)\cdot(1-v)\cdot\frac{g\left(\frac{u}{1+u},\frac{v}{1-v},w\right)}{u+w+uw}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\cdot(1+v)\cdot(1-w)\cdot\frac{g\left(u,\frac{v}{1+v},\frac{w}{1-w}\right)}{u+w-uw}+\frac{1}{4}\cdot(1+w)\cdot\frac{g\left(u,v,\frac{w}{1+w}\right)}{u+w+uw}.\quad\quad

Let us multiply both sides by
4(u+w)(u+w+uw)(u+w-uw)
and introduce the functions
\Gamma
