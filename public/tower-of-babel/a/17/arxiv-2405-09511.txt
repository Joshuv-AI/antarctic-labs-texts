Stability via resampling: statistical problems beyond the real line
Jake A. Soloff, Rina Foygel Barber, Rebecca Willett

Abstract
Model averaging techniques based on resampling methods (such as bootstrapping or subsampling) have been utilized across many areas of statistics, often with the explicit goal of promoting stability in the resulting output. We provide a general, finite-sample theoretical result guaranteeing the stability of bagging when applied to algorithms that return outputs in a general space, so that the output is not necessarily a real-valued—for example, an algorithm that estimates a vector of weights or a density function. We empirically assess the stability of bagging on synthetic and real-world data for a range of problem settings, including causal inference, nonparametric regression, and Bayesian model selection.



1
Introduction

Statistical stability holds when our conclusions, estimates, fitted models, predictions, or decisions are insensitive to small perturbations of data. Stability is a pillar of modern data science
(
Yu and Kumbier, 2020
)
. Nonparametric tools for statistical inference—e.g. the bootstrap, subsampling, the Jackknife—perturb data to assess variability and can thus be viewed as aspects of stability analysis
(
Yu, 2013
)
.
While assessing stability is in general statistically intractable without placing assumptions on the data
(
Kim and Barber, 2023
)
,
these resampling-based approaches can also be used to reduce the issue of instability:
for instance,
Breiman (1996a)
;
Breiman (1996b)
originally proposed bagging—which applies bootstrap resampling to construct an ensemble of machine learning models—as an off-the-shelf stabilizer for regression and classification.
In this paper, we show how bagging can be used to stabilize statistical methods far beyond its origins in supervised learning problems.

1.1
Problem settings

We begin by highlighting some specific statistical problems where provably stable methods are desirable.

Problem setting 1 (background): the supervised learning setting

In the supervised learning setting, we are given a labeled data set
(X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n},Y_{n})
and are tasked with constructing a regression function
\hat{f}
to predict
Y
given
X
for new data points. Many modern methods from the recent machine learning literature offer powerful predictive performance, but suffer from high sensitivity to the training data, where a prediction
\hat{f}(X)
(at a new test point
X
) may vary immensely if the training data is perturbed slightly. Interestingly, both high accuracy and poor stability can often be a consequence of working with highly overparameterized models.

Suppose that the response
Y
is real-valued, so that our regression functions take values in
\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,
\hat{f}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
. Recent work by
Soloff et al. (2024)
establishes that bagging the predictive model—that is, re-fitting
\hat{f}
on bootstrapped training sets, and then returning the averaged prediction—yields an assumption-free guarantee on the stability of the prediction
\hat{f}(X)\in\mathbb{R}
.

While
Soloff et al. (2024)
’s work is presented as a result for supervised learning, it can be viewed more generally as a guarantee of stability for
any
algorithm that is trained on data and returns a real-valued output. But even with this generalization,
this result only covers a very specific statistical scenario. For many statistical problems, we might be interested in the stability of a procedure that returns a more complex object, such as a function or a vector of weights, rather than a real-valued output.

Problem setting 2: reproducible Bayesian inference

In Bayesian data analysis, given a data set
\mathcal{D}
containing
n
observations drawn from some distribution
p_{\theta}
, we are interested in the posterior distribution of the parameter
\theta
. For instance,
\theta
might represent a particular model from a finite set of possibilities, in which case the posterior distribution on
\theta
is simply a vector of weights on this finite set. Alternatively,
\theta
might take values in a continuous space such as
[0,1]
(e.g., if
\theta
represents a probability) or
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(e.g., coefficients for some regression model), in which case the posterior distribution is a distribution over
[0,1]
or over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
—a more complex object.

Bayesian inference may sometimes lead to answers that are highly sensitive with respect to perturbations of the data. In particular, this can occur in settings where the model is misspecified (but nonetheless, is a reasonably good approximation to the observed data, so that computing the posterior is still a reasonable goal). To alleviate this instability, the
BayesBag
approach
(
Bühlmann, 2014
)
averages posterior distributions over different resamplings of the data
\mathcal{D}
.
Huggins and Miller (2023)
analyzes the accuracy and stability properties of the BayesBag approach for the specific problem of Bayesian model selection, where
\theta
lies in a finite set of models
\mathfrak{M}=\{\mathfrak{m}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{m}_{K}\}

(and so the posterior on
\theta
is given by a vector of weights of length
K
, i.e., with the
k
th weight indicating the posterior probability
\mathbb{P}\{\theta=\mathfrak{m}_{k}\mid\mathcal{D}\}
).
Their work establishes BayesBag as an empirically effective method with asymptotic guarantees, but has not yielded finite-sample guarantees even in the finite model setting.
Of course, we can also ask this question even more generally: will BayesBag lead to greater stability in a general setting, where
\theta
may lie in an arbitrary space (e.g., if the posterior is a distribution on
[0,1]
, or on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, etc.)?

Problem setting 3: synthetic controls for causal inference

The synthetic control method (SCM) is a popular approach for estimating causal effects with panel data
(
Abadie and Gardeazabal, 2003
;
Abadie et al., 2010
;
Abadie et al., 2015
;
Abadie, 2021
)
. The method is particularly useful in settings with a single treated unit and a relatively small number of control units. A synthetic control is a weighted combination of control units, where the weights are chosen to match the treated unit’s pre-treatment outcomes and other covariates. SCM estimates the counterfactual—what outcomes would we expect for the treated unit had the policy not been adopted?—using the synthetic control’s post-treatment outcomes. For example,
Abadie et al. (2015)
applies SCM to the 1990
German reunification and its impact on per capita GDP in West Germany. Per capita GDP
was increasing prior to reunification and continued to increase in subsequent years, for this was a period of sustained growth across industrialized countries. To assess the counterfactual, a ‘synthetic West Germany’ is constructed as follows: we find the convex combination of other industrialized countries such that the pre-1990 economic trend most closely resembles that of West Germany.
Abadie et al. (2015)
finds that West Germany’s economy did not grow as much as it would have without reunification.

Often SCM’s weights are of direct interest, as they say something about which peer countries are most relevant in this counterfactual assessment.
However, in order to reliably interpret the weights, we should first ensure they are stable
(
Murdoch et al., 2019
)
. For instance, if we remove
Denmark
from the analysis
(a country which receives zero weight in the synthetic West Germany) then there is no longer a positive weight on the United States, which previously received
22\%
of the weight. This sensitivity appears to betray our intuitive interpretation of the meaning of SCM’s weights.
Can a resampling-type approach to SCM alleviate these issues?

Our contributions

Algorithmic stability is desirable for statistical methods far beyond its origins in statistical learning theory
(
Devroye and Wagner, 1979a
;
Devroye and Wagner, 1979b
;
Bousquet and Elisseeff, 2002
)
. Broadly, stability is considered a ‘prerequisite for trustworthy interpretations’
(
Murdoch et al., 2019
)
. The goal of this paper is to establish quantitative guarantees on the stability of bagging as a general-purpose tool across a wide range of problems where the output—the output being estimated from the data—might not be real-valued and might even be infinite-dimensional.

Framework: algorithmic stability in a metric space

We define a
randomized algorithm

\mathcal{A}
as function that takes in a finite sequence of data
\mathcal{D}=(Z_{i})_{i\in[n]}
, as well as some independent randomness
\xi\sim\text{Uniform}([0,1])
, and returns an output
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D};\xi)
.
The term
\xi
, which we refer to as the
random seed
, determines all randomness used by the algorithm—for instance, a random initialization or a random split of the data into batches.
The algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is deterministic if it does not depend on the seed
\xi
.

This notion of a (deterministic or randomized) algorithm covers many disparate problems in statistical learning, such as the problem settings introduced in
Section

1.1
.

•

Consider a supervised learning setting (Problem Setting 1) with data points
Z_{i}=(X_{i},Y_{i})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}
. If we are interested in predicting
Y
given a new feature
X=x
, our algorithm
\mathcal{A}
can return
\hat{f}(x)\in\mathbb{R}
, the predicted value at
X=x
, as its output. (Alternatively, if we are interested in building a model for
Y
given
X
, our algorithm
\mathcal{A}
can return an entire fitted regression function
\hat{f}
—a more complex output.)

•

In Bayesian inference (Problem Setting 2), we observe data points
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
drawn from some distribution
p_{\theta}
, where
\theta\in\Theta
is the unknown parameter. To perform Bayesian inference on
\theta
, our algorithm
\mathcal{A}
would then return the posterior distribution over
\theta\in\Theta
.

•

For the problem of synthetic controls (Problem Setting 3), our data points
Z_{i}
are the feature vectors for each control unit
i=1,\dots,n
, and the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
returns a weight vector of dimension
n
, specifying the convex combination of control units that define the synthetic control.

In some settings it may be common to use a randomized, rather than deterministic, algorithm. For example, in Bayesian inference,
in a high-dimensional setting we might choose to approximate the posterior through (random) sampling rather than computing the density explicitly.

Formally, we write

\mathcal{A}:\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{Z}^{n}\times[0,1]\to\mathcal{W},

where the output takes values in some space
\mathcal{W}
equipped with a metric
\mathsf{d}
—see
Table

1
for examples. From this point on, we write
\hat{w}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D};\xi)
to denote the output, regardless of the nature of the output (e.g., a finite-dimensional vector of weights for the synthetic control problem, or a posterior density function for the Bayesian inference setting, etc.).
1
1

1

We assume throughout that
t\mapsto\mathcal{A}((Z_{i})_{i\in[n]},t)
, which is a function from
[0,1]
to
\mathcal{W}
, is measurable for any fixed
(Z_{i})_{i\in[n]}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
. Measurability is defined with respect to the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
(\mathcal{W},\mathsf{d})
.

2.1
Defining stability

There are many related definitions of algorithmic stability in the literature. We focus on stability of the algorithm’s output with respect to dropping a data point: will the output
\hat{w}
of the algorithm be similar if one data point is removed at random from the training data set?

Definition 1
.

An algorithm
\mathcal{A}
has
mean-square stability

\beta^{2}
with respect to a metric
\mathsf{d}
if, for all data sets
\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
of size
n
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\mathsf{d}^{2}\left(\hat{w},\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\right)\right]\leq\beta^{2}.

where
\hat{w}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D};\xi)
is the output of the algorithm run on the full data set
\mathcal{D}
,
\hat{w}^{\setminus i}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}^{\setminus i};\xi)
is the output of the algorithm run on the leave-one-out data set
\mathcal{D}^{\setminus i}=(Z_{j})_{j\neq i}
, and the expectation averages over
\xi\sim\text{Uniform}[0,1]
.

This first definition captures the average perturbation in
\hat{w}
when one data point is removed at random from some data set
\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
—that is,
\mathsf{d}^{2}\left(\hat{w},\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\right)
, averaged over which data point
i
gets removed.
An alternative way to define stability is to control the tails of these perturbations
\mathsf{d}\left(\hat{w},\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\right)
, ensuring that not too many of them can be large.

Definition 2
.

An algorithm
\mathcal{A}
has
tail stability

(\varepsilon,\delta)
with respect to a metric
\mathsf{d}
if, for all data sets
\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
of size
n
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}_{\xi}\left\{\mathsf{d}(\hat{w},\hat{w}^{\setminus i})\geq\varepsilon\right\}\leq\delta,

where
\hat{w}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D};\xi)
and
\hat{w}^{\setminus i}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}^{\setminus i};\xi)
as in
Definition

1
.

The terminology ‘
\mathcal{A}
has mean-square stability
\beta^{2}
’ in
Definition

1
, and ‘
\mathcal{A}
has tail stability
(\varepsilon,\delta)
’
in
Definition

2
,
suppresses the dependence on the sample size
n
. Since we perform a non-asymptotic stability
analysis, we treat
n
as a fixed positive integer throughout.

These two notions of stability are clearly very similar in flavor, and the following simple result shows that each implies the other.

Proposition 3
.

If
\mathcal{A}
has mean-square stability
\beta^{2}
with respect to
\mathsf{d}
, then
\mathcal{A}
has tail stability
(\varepsilon,\delta)
with respect to
\mathsf{d}
, for any
\varepsilon,\delta\geq 0
satisfying
\delta\varepsilon^{2}\geq\beta^{2}
. Conversely, if
\mathcal{A}
has tail stability
(\varepsilon,\delta)
with respect to
\mathsf{d}
, then
\mathcal{A}
has mean-square stability
\beta^{2}=\varepsilon^{2}+\delta\,\sup_{w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathsf{d}^{2}\left(w,w^{\prime}\right)
with respect to
\mathsf{d}
.

Proof.

The first claim follows by Markov’s inequality, since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}_{\xi}\left\{\mathsf{d}\left(\hat{w},\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\right)\geq\varepsilon\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\left\{\mathsf{d}^{2}\left(\hat{w},\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\right)\geq\varepsilon^{2}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{-2}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathsf{d}^{2}\left(\hat{w},\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\right)\right]\leq\varepsilon^{-2}\beta^{2}.

For the second claim, since
\mathsf{d}^{2}\left(\hat{w},\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\right)\leq\varepsilon^{2}+\mathbf{1}\left\{\mathsf{d}\left(\hat{w},\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\right)\geq\varepsilon\right\}\cdot\sup_{w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathsf{d}^{2}\left(w,w^{\prime}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\mathsf{d}^{2}\left(\hat{w},\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}_{\xi}\left\{\mathsf{d}\left(\hat{w},\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\right)\geq\varepsilon\right\}\cdot\sup_{w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathsf{d}^{2}\left(w,w^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2}+\delta\cdot\sup_{w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}}\mathsf{d}^{2}\left(w,w^{\prime}\right),

completing the proof.
∎

2.2
Related notions of stability

Our definitions of stability are stronger than many common definitions in the literature. For instance, we require the algorithm to be stable for
any
fixed data set
\mathcal{D}
to certify the stability of the method without looking at the specific data set.
Definition

2
implies in particular that the algorithm is stable when the data
(Z_{i})_{i\in[n]}
are i.i.d. from some distribution
P
, but our definition does not require any stochastic model.

In many cases, practitioners care specifically about the stability of the algorithm’s output
\hat{w}
, e.g., if we want to interpret the values of some learned weights, cluster assignments, etc. However, in other cases, we may be more concerned with the stability of some downstream decisions or outcomes based on an analysis. For instance, in supervised learning (Problem Setting 1), we may desire stability of a loss incurred by the output
\hat{w}
—e.g., a predictive error
\ell(\hat{w},(x,y))=|y-\hat{w}(x)|
.
These types of loss-based stability properties can often be inferred from stability of the output
\hat{w}
(see
Soloff et al. (2024)
for further discussion). In this paper, we only consider the stability of
\hat{w}
itself.

3
Constructing a bagged algorithm via resampling

In this section, we formally define the construction of a bagged algorithm.

Stability is about (in)sensitivity to perturbed data sets. Resampling methods deliberately perturb the data, so they are naturally a useful tool for stabilizing algorithms: given a data set
\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
containing
n
data points, we might retrain our algorithm repeatedly on resampled data sets,
and then average the resulting outputs.
There are several common schemes for constructing these resampled data sets.
Here, we mention two of the most common methods:

•

Bootstrapping

constructs each resampled data set by sampling
m
indices
i_{1},\dots,i_{m}
uniformly with replacement from
[n]
(the term “bootstrap” commonly refers to this method with the choice
m=n
, so that the resampled data sets are of the same size as the original
\mathcal{D}
).

•

Alternatively, we may instead sample data points without replacement, so that each contains a uniformly drawn subset containing
m
of the original
n
data points (e.g., with
m=n/2
). This type of approach is known as
subbagging
.

We now define some notation that unifies these two variants (as well as allowing for other resampling schemes, as we discuss below).

Following the framework and notation of
Soloff et al. (2024)
, we define the set of finite sequences of indices in
[n]
:

\textnormal{seq}_{[n]}:=\{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}):k\geq 0,\,i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in[n]\}.

A sequence
r=(i_{1},\dots,i_{m})\in\textnormal{seq}_{[n]}
is commonly referred to as a
bag
in the context of data set resampling. For any
r\in\textnormal{seq}_{[n]}
,
define
\mathcal{D}^{r}=(Z_{i})_{i\in r}\in\mathcal{Z}^{m}
, the corresponding data set.
Note that if the bag
r
contains repeated indices (i.e.,
i_{k}=i_{\ell}
for some
k\neq\ell
), then the same data point from the original data set
\mathcal{D}
will appear multiple times in
\mathcal{D}^{r}
.

We are now ready to define the bagged version of any base algorithm
\mathcal{A}
, using a particular resampling distribution
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
.
2
2

2

While the term “bagging” is sometimes used as a synonym for bootstrap averaging (i.e., averaging outputs after sampling from the data set without replacement) in the literature, in this paper we will use “bagging” more broadly, allowing for any resampling distribution
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
.
From this point on, we assume that
\mathcal{W}
is a closed and compact subset of a separable Banach space—this technical condition ensures that taking an average or an expected value of outputs in
\mathcal{W}
is well-defined.

Definition 4

(Bagging for an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
)
.

Given a data set
\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
and a distribution
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
on
\textnormal{seq}_{[n]}
,
return the output

\hat{w}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}^{r_{b}};\xi_{b}),

where
(r_{b},\xi_{b})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{Q}_{n}\times\textnormal{Uniform}[0,1]
.

For example, bootstrapping corresponds to choosing
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
as the uniform distribution over all sequences of length
m
, while subbagging takes
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
as the uniform distribution over sequences of
m

distinct
elements.

For our theoretical analysis, it will be useful to consider taking
B\to\infty
, so as to “derandomize” the procedure.

Definition 5

(Derandomized bagging for an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
)
.

Given a data set
\mathcal{D}\in\mathcal{Z}^{n}
and a distribution
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
on
\textnormal{seq}_{[n]}
,
return the output

\hat{w}=\mathbb{E}_{r,\xi}[\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}^{r};\xi)],

where the expected value is computed with respect to
(r,\xi)\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}\times\text{Uniform}[0,1]
.

3.1
A general framework for bagging

The two variants discussed above, bagging and subbagging, are special cases of a broader framework, allowing for more flexibility in the choice of the resampling distribution
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
.
(See
Soloff et al. (2024)
for a more complete discussion of this framework, and for examples of resampling methods beyond bagging and subbagging.)
Here we formalize the conditions that
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
needs to satisfy, in order for our main results to be applied.

Assumption 1
.

Fix a sample size
n\geq 1
. The following conditions must be satisfied by the resampling distribution
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
.

•

Symmetry:
for all
m\geq 1
,
i_{1},\ldots,i_{m}\in[n]
, and permutations
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
,

\mathcal{Q}_{n}\{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{m})\}=\mathcal{Q}_{n}\{(\sigma(i_{1}),\ldots,\sigma(i_{m}))\}.

•

Nontrivial subsampling:
it holds that
p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}<1
, where we define

p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}=\mathbb{E}_{r\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{i\in r\}\right],

the expected fraction of data points appearing in a bag
r
sampled from
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
.

•

Nonpositive covariance:

\textnormal{Cov}_{r\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}}(\mathbf{1}_{i\in r},\mathbf{1}_{j\in r})\leq 0\textnormal{ for any $i\neq j\in[n]$}.

•

Compatibility with the leave-one-out distribution:
the corresponding distribution
\mathcal{Q}_{n-1}
on
\textnormal{seq}_{[n-1]}
must equal the distribution of
r\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}
conditional on the event
n\not\in r
.

Before proceeding, we comment on the last part of the assumption: why does
\mathcal{Q}_{n-1}
appear in our assumptions on
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
? To study the stability of the bagged algorithm (
Definition

4
), it is not sufficient to consider only
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
, the distribution on
\textnormal{seq}_{[n]}
—when we compare to the result of the algorithm on the leave-one-out data set
\mathcal{D}^{\setminus i}
, we now have sample size
n-1
(instead of
n
), and consequently we need to specify a distribution
\mathcal{Q}_{n-1}
on
\textnormal{seq}_{[n-1]}
, as well.

The four conditions of
Assumption

2
are all satisfied by the standard resampling schemes described above, with

p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}=1-\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)^{m}
for bootstrapping (sampling
m
out of
n
points uniformly
with
replacement),
and

p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}=\frac{m}{n}
for subbagging (sampling
m
out of
n
points uniformly
without
replacement).

4
Main results for a Hilbert space

We are now ready to present our stability guarantees for bagging.
Our first main result establishes that derandomized bagging automatically provides mean-square stability for any algorithm with outputs in a bounded subset of a Hilbert space.
In this section, we work with stability with respect to the Hilbert norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
.
For instance,

•

If
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then the Hilbert norm may be chosen to be the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
, or more generally, a Mahalanobis distance
\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\sqrt{w^{\top}Sw}
for a fixed positive definite matrix
S
.

•

If
\mathcal{W}\subset L_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, the space of square-integrable functions
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, then the Hilbert norm is given by the
L_{2}
norm on functions,
\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}}=(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}w(x)^{2}\;\textnormal{d}x)^{1/2}
. Alternatively, we can take a Sobolev space of functions
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
with smoothness parameter
s
with the Hilbert norm being the Sobolev norm
\|\cdot\|_{s,2}
(defined formally later on in (
3
)—see Experiment 3 in
Section

6.1
for details).

Theorem 6
.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}
takes values in a convex and closed subset
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathcal{H}
, where
\mathcal{H}
is a separable Hilbert space, and define

\mathsf{rad}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{W})=\inf_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\sup_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}}\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{H}}

as the radius of the set
\mathcal{W}
. Fix a resampling distribution
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
on
\textnormal{seq}_{[n]}
, which satisfies
Assumption

1
.
Then derandomized bagging, run with base algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and resampling distribution
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
, has mean-square stability
\beta^{2}
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
, with

\displaystyle\beta^{2}:=\frac{\mathsf{rad}^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{W})}{n-1}\cdot\frac{p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}.

(1)

Combining this result with
Proposition

3
immediately yields a guarantee on
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-stability.

Corollary 7
.

Under the notation and assumptions of
Theorem

6
,
derandomized bagging has tail stability
(\varepsilon,\delta)
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
for any
\varepsilon,\delta\geq 0
satisfying

\displaystyle\delta\varepsilon^{2}\geq\frac{\mathsf{rad}_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}(\mathcal{W})}{n-1}\cdot\frac{p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}.

(2)

Comparing to prior bounds

The main result of
(
Soloff et al., 2024
, Theorem 8)
, ensuring the stability of algorithms that return a real-valued output, can be recovered as a special case of
Corollary

7
above, by taking
\mathcal{W}=[0,1]\subseteq\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}
(note that we then have
\mathsf{rad}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{W})=\frac{1}{2}
). But remarkably, the result of
Theorem

6
shows that there is no price to pay for high-dimensionality (or even for infinite dimensionality): the stability guarantee is
exactly the same
for any Hilbert space, regardless of dimension, as long as the radius of the output space
\mathcal{W}
is bounded.

4.1
Results for a finite number of bags

The results above apply to the derandomized bagged version of
\mathcal{A}
, as constructed in
Definition

5
.
In practice, of course, it is not feasible to calculate this output
\hat{w}
since we would need to average over all possible bags
r
, so we would instead use the bagged estimator
\hat{w}
constructed in
Definition

4
for some finite number of bags
B
. Since we are simply taking a Monte Carlo approximation to the derandomized bagged estimator of
Definition

5
, we would expect to incur error on the order of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{B}}
.
To formalize this, we first recall a generalization of the
Azuma-Hoeffding inequality:

Theorem 8

(
(
Hayes, 2005
)
)
.

Let
\hat{w}_{1},\ldots,\hat{w}_{B}\in\mathcal{H}
be independent random variables in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
such that
\|\hat{w}_{b}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{w}_{b}]\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C
almost surely for
b=1,\ldots,B
. Then for any
\delta>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left\{\bigg\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}(\hat{w}_{b}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{w}_{b}])\bigg\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq C\sqrt{\frac{1}{2B}\log\left(\frac{2e^{2}}{\delta}\right)}\right\}\geq 1-\delta.

In particular,

\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}(\hat{w}_{b}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{w}_{b}])\bigg\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right]\leq\frac{C^{2}e^{2}}{B}.

Note that, aside from the additional factor
e^{2}
, this result is the same as what we would obtain for the case
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}
. Combining this result with
Theorem

6
yields the following stability guarantee for bagged algorithms with a finite number of bags
B
.

Corollary 9
.

Under the notation and assumptions of
Theorem

6
, let
B\geq 1
be fixed.
Then bagging, run with base algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and resampling distribution
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
and with
B
bags, has mean-square stability
\beta^{2}
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
, with

\beta^{2}:=\mathsf{rad}^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{W})\left(\frac{1}{n-1}\cdot\frac{p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}+\frac{16e^{2}}{B}\right).

Of course, tail stability follows as an immediate consequence of
Proposition

3
.

5
Extension: results outside the Hilbert space setting

We now turn to the question of stability in a more general setting, where the output space is not necessarily a Hilbert space. We assume that
\mathcal{A}
returns outputs lying in
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathcal{B}
, where
\mathcal{B}
is a separable Banach space equipped with norm
\|\cdot\|
, and we now aim to establish stability with respect to this norm
\|\cdot\|
directly.

As a motivating example, consider a setting where
\mathcal{A}
returns an output
\hat{w}
that specifies a distribution—for instance, this may arise in Bayesian inference (Problem Setting 2 from
Section

1.1
), where
\mathcal{A}
returns a posterior distribution over some parameter of interest.
In this type of setting,
since
\hat{w}
represents a distribution, it is natural to study its stability with respect to
\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{TV}}
, the total variation distance.
While
\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{TV}}
defines a valid norm, this is not a Hilbert norm and so the theoretical guarantees of
Section

4
cannot be directly applied.

To allow for meaningful stability guarantees in a broader range of settings, then, we now consider stability in this more general context, where
\|\cdot\|
is not necessarily a Hilbert norm.

5.1
Theoretical guarantee

Unlike the special case of a Hilbert space, in this more general setting we need an additional assumption to ensure that stability will hold.

Assumption 2
.

There exists a constant
R>0
such that
\sup_{w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}}\|w-w^{\prime}\|\leq R
, and moreover,
there exist some fixed elements
v_{1},\dots,v_{K}\in\mathcal{B}
with unit norm,
\|v_{1}\|=\dots=\|v_{K}\|=1
, and a constant
\rho>0
such that for any
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}
, the difference
w-w^{\prime}
can be approximated by a linear combination of the
v_{j}
’s,

\Big\|(w-w^{\prime})-\sum_{i=1}^{K}t_{i}v_{i}\Big\|\leq\rho

for some
t=(t_{1},\dots,t_{K})\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
with
\|t\|_{1}\leq R
.

Here
R
is playing a role analogous to the radius,
\mathsf{rad}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{W})
, in the Hilbert space setting. For instance, returning to our earlier example, if
\mathcal{W}=\Delta_{K-1}
(and the norm is given by
\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{TV}}
) then we can take
v_{j}
to be the
j
th canonical basis vector. Then
Assumption

2
holds, with this same value of
K
, and with
R=1
and
\rho=0
.

Theorem 10
.

Suppose
\mathcal{A}
takes values in a convex and closed subset
\mathcal{W}\subset\mathcal{B}
, where
\mathcal{B}
is a separable Banach space equipped with norm
\|\cdot\|
, and where
\mathcal{W}
satisfies
Assumption

2
for some integer
K\geq 1
and some
R,\rho\geq 0
. Fix a resampling distribution
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
on
\textnormal{seq}_{[n]}
, which satisfies
Assumption

1
.
Then derandomized bagging, run with base algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and resampling distribution
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
, has mean-square stability
\beta^{2}
with respect to
\|\cdot\|
, with

\beta^{2}:=1.6\left[R\sqrt{\frac{2(1+\eta_{n})\log(2K)}{n}\left(\frac{p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}+\frac{4\eta_{n}}{(1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}})^{2}}\right)}+R\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}}+2\rho\cdot p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\right]^{2},

where
\eta_{n}=\frac{H_{n}-1}{n-H_{n}}\asymp\frac{\log n}{n}
, for
H_{n}=1+\frac{1}{2}+\dots+\frac{1}{n}
denoting the
n
th harmonic number.

As in the Hilbert space setting, we can also extend this theorem to verify tail stability and to prove a stability result for bagging with a finite
B
—see
Appendix

B
for details.

5.2
Examples

Now we return to Problem Setting 2, as discussed in the motivation for the extension to Banach spaces, where
\mathcal{A}
returns a distribution (e.g., a posterior), to see what the new theorem implies in this setting.
To make this more concrete, we can consider two specific scenarios: a discrete setting and a continuous setting. We will see how, in each scenario, the new result of
Theorem

10
gives a much stronger guarantee than what can be derived from the results of
Section

4
. For intuition, in this discussion, we treat
\frac{p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}
as a constant.

Problem Setting 2(a): the discrete case

First, consider a finite setting, where
\mathcal{A}
returns a distribution on
K
many values—for instance, if the output of
\mathcal{A}
specifies a Bayesian posterior over
K
many possible models. In this scenario,
\hat{w}
takes values in the simplex
\Delta_{K-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{K}
, and so the total variation distance is given by
\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{TV}}(w,w^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{1}
.

Of course, since
\mathbb{R}^{K}
is itself a Hilbert space (with norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\|\cdot\|_{2}
), we can apply our results from
Section

4
—but this will not give a very useful bound for the stability of derandomized bagging. Specifically,
Theorem

6
tells us that mean-square stability with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{2}
holds, for
\beta^{2}\propto 1/n
. Since
\|\cdot\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{K}\|\cdot\|_{2}
(the usual inequality relating
\ell_{1}
norm to the
\ell_{2}
norm in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
), this immediately implies that

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{TV}}^{2}\left(\hat{w},\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\right)\right]\leq\frac{K}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\|^{2}_{2}]\lesssim K/n,

or in other words, mean-square stability with respect to
\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{TV}}
holds, for
\beta^{2}\propto K/n
. But if the dimension
K
is large relative to
n
, then this is not a very useful guarantee.

In contrast, let us now see what can be guaranteed via
Theorem

10
. As mentioned above,
Assumption

2
holds with
K
equal to the dimension, and with
R=1
and
\rho=0
. Then
Theorem

10
tells us that mean-square stability with respect to
\|\cdot\|
holds for
\beta^{2}\propto\frac{\log K}{n}
—a far better bound.

Problem Setting 2(b): the continuous case

Next, consider a continuous setting, where
\mathcal{A}
returns a density on
[0,1]
, given by some function
\hat{w}:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,\infty)
satisfying
\int_{t=0}^{1}\hat{w}(t)\;\textnormal{d}t=1
.
In this case we have
\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{TV}}(w,w^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{t=0}^{1}|w(t)-w^{\prime}(t)|\;\textnormal{d}t
.

To formalize this setting, consider the Banach space

\mathcal{B}=L_{1}([0,1]):=\left\{f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}:\int_{t=0}^{1}|f(t)|\;\textnormal{d}t<\infty\right\},

equipped with the norm
\|f\|=\frac{1}{2}\int_{t=0}^{1}|f(t)|\;\textnormal{d}t
(note that this differs from the usual
\ell_{1}
norm by the factor
\frac{1}{2}
, in order to agree with total variation distance). Suppose that our algorithm returns densities that are further constrained to be
L
-Lipschitz, so that we have

\mathcal{W}=\left\{w:[0,1]\to[0,\infty):\int_{t=0}^{1}w(t)=1,\ \sup_{t\neq t^{\prime}}\frac{|w(t)-w(t^{\prime})|}{|t-t^{\prime}|}\leq L\right\}.

As for Problem Setting 2(a), we again begin by asking whether the Hilbert space result of
Theorem

6
might already be sufficient in this setting. Indeed, while
\mathcal{B}=L_{1}([0,1])
is not a Hilbert space, the Lipschitz constraint on
\mathcal{W}
means that
\int_{t=0}^{1}|w(t)|^{2}\;\textnormal{d}t<\infty
for all
w\in\mathcal{W}
, meaning that
\mathcal{W}
can be viewed as a subset of the Hilbert space
L_{2}([0,1])
, equipped with norm
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}=\|f\|_{L_{2}}=(\int_{t=0}^{1}|f(t)|^{2}\;\textnormal{d}t)^{1/2}
.
A straightforward calculation verifies that
\mathsf{rad}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{W})\propto L^{1/4}
,
meaning that by
Theorem

6
, derandomized bagging satisfies mean-square stability with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}}
for
\beta^{2}\propto L^{1/2}/n
. Since for this function space we have
\|\cdot\|_{L_{1}}\leq\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}}
, this immediately implies that mean-square stability with respect to
\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{TV}}
also holds with parameter
\beta^{2}\propto L^{1/2}/n
. However, if the Lipschitz constant
L
is large, this may not be a satisfying result.

Now we turn to our new result,
Theorem

10
, to see if the stability guarantee can be improved. First, observe that
Assumption

2
holds for any
K\geq 1
if we take
R=1
and
\rho\propto L/K
. This holds because we can take
v_{j}
to be the function
v_{j}(t)=K\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\frac{j-1}{K}\leq t\leq\frac{j}{K}\}
, for each
j\in[K]
—then for any
L
-Lipschitz densities
w,w^{\prime}
, the
2L
-Lipschitz difference
w-w^{\prime}
can be approximated with a piecewise constant function, i.e., a linear combination of these
v_{j}
’s. Choosing
K\propto Ln
,
we apply
Theorem

10
, which then leads to a guarantee on the mean-square stability with respect to
\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{TV}}
, with
\beta^{2}\propto\frac{\log(Ln)}{n}
. Again, as for the discrete case, if
L
is large (i.e.,
L\gg\log^{2}n
) then this is a much stronger bound than the one we can obtain via the Hilbert space result.

Remark 11

(On the necessity of
Assumption

2
)
.

Our stability guarantee for general Banach spaces essentially requires that
\mathcal{W}
lies (approximately) in a convex hull of
2K
many points—namely,
Rv_{1},-Rv_{1}
, …,
Rv_{K},-Rv_{K}
. In particular, this implies that
\mathcal{W}
is (approximately) contained in a
finite-dimensional
subspace of
\mathcal{B}
.
In contrast, our Hilbert space results apply regardless of dimensionality—while we do require
\mathcal{W}
to be bounded via its radius,
\mathsf{rad}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{W})
, there is no implicit cost to the dimension of
\mathcal{H}
in the results of
Theorem

6
.
It turns out that some assumption of this form is actually required for the Banach case—see
Proposition

13
(in
Section

B.2
) for an explicit counterexample verifying stability fails without this type of assumption.

6.1
Data and Methods

We start by presenting an overview of our four experiments. Experiments 1, 2, and 3 each examine stability in a Hilbert space, with respect to the appropriate Hilbert norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}
(as studied in
Section

4
). In contrast, the setting of Experiment 4 is a Banach space (as in
Section

5
).

Experiment 1 (Estimating a regression function using bagged decision trees)

We draw data from the following data-generating process:

\displaystyle(X_{i},\alpha_{i},\gamma_{i})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\textnormal{Uniform}([0,1]^{d})\times\textnormal{Uniform}([-.25,.25])\times\textnormal{Uniform}([0,1]),

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sin\left(\frac{X_{ij}}{j}\right)+\alpha_{i}\mathbf{1}\left\{i=1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 3)\right\}+\gamma_{i}\mathbf{1}\left\{i=1\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 4)\right\},

with
n=500
and
d=40
. We then set
Y_{i}=\frac{\tilde{Y}_{i}-\min_{j}\tilde{Y}_{j}}{\max_{j}\tilde{Y}_{j}-\min_{j}\tilde{Y}_{j}}
so that each response
Y_{i}
is in the unit interval
[0,1]
. Note that the algorithm has access to the observed data
\mathcal{D}=(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i\in[n]}
, i.e.,
\alpha_{i}
and
\gamma_{i}
are latent variables used only to generate the data
\mathcal{D}
. We apply
sklearn.tree.DecisionTreeRegressor
to train the regression trees, setting
max_depth=50
and leaving all other parameters at their default values. We evaluate the stability of the learned function in the
L_{2}
norm
\|\hat{w}\|_{L_{2}}=(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\hat{w}(x)^{2}\textnormal{d}x)^{1/2}
.

Experiment 2 (Synthetic controls real data example)

For this experiment, we reproduce
Abadie et al. (2015)
’s SCM analysis on the 1990 reunification of Germany, as discussed above in
Section

1.1
(see Problem Setting 3).
The data contains
n=16
countries (aside from Germany), with
6
predictors measured for each country (pre-treatment averages of various macroeconomic factors such as inflation and trade openness—see Table 1 of
Abadie et al. (2015)
for more details). SCM then estimates a weighted combination of these
n
countries that represents a ‘synthetic West Germany’, meaning that the output of the algorithm is a vector
\hat{w}\in\Delta_{n-1}
. When we run SCM on a subset of the
n=16
countries, we assign a weight of
0
to each omitted country. We run SCM using the Python package
pysyncon
and make extensive use of their reproduction of the original study
Abadie et al. (2015)
. We evaluate the stability of these weights in the Euclidean norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
.

Experiment 3 (Least-squares spectrum analysis)

We study function estimation in a Sobolev space, corresponding to the Hilbert space
W^{s,2}(\mathbb{R})
with smoothness parameter
s=2
,
with the corresponding norm

\displaystyle\|f\|_{s,2}:=\sqrt{\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}(1+k^{2})^{s}\cdot|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}},

(3)

where
\hat{f}(k)
denotes the
k
th Fourier coefficient, indexed by
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. We wish to recover
f
, which is equivalent to estimating the Fourier coefficients. We restrict our estimation problem to
even functions
, so that we may constrain the Fourier coefficients to be real valued.

Least squares spectral analysis (LSSA) is one method for estimating the Fourier coefficients of a function from irregularly spaced samples
Vaníček (1969)
. Given a data set
\mathcal{D}=(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i\in[n]}
, we first construct a countable set of features
\Psi_{ik}=\exp\left(jkX_{i}\right)
, where
j=\sqrt{-1}
,
i\in[n]
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
. The base algorithm
\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D})
returns a function
\hat{w}:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
, given by

\hat{w}(x)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{a}_{k}\exp(jkx),\qquad\textnormal{with}\qquad\hat{a}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{a\in\mathcal{C}_{n}(R)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Y_{i}-\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi_{ik}a_{k}\right)^{2},

and where the constraint set is given by

\mathcal{C}_{n}(R):=\left\{(\ldots,a_{-1},a_{0},a_{1},\ldots):a_{k}\in\mathbb{R},a_{k}=0\,\forall\,|k|>\lceil{n/2}\rceil,\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}(1+k^{2})^{2}a_{k}^{2}\leq R^{2}\right\}.

For our simulation, we sample
n=200
random variables
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\in[-1,1]
iid from a non-uniform density
g_{X}(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\log|x|
. Then, the corresponding responses are given by
Y_{i}=f^{*}(X_{i})+\zeta_{i}
, where
\zeta_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{iid}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,.01)
and
f^{*}(x)=\sin\left(\frac{2}{1-x}+\frac{2}{1+x}\right)
. We run the base algorithm above using the Python package
cvxpy
, setting
R=10
. We evaluate the stability of the learned function in the Sobolev norm
\|\cdot\|_{2,2}
(see
Equation

3
above).

Experiment 4 (Stability of the softmax function)

n\times d

X\in\{0,1\}^{n\times d}

7
Conclusion

In supervised learning, algorithmic stability plays a fundamental role in generalization theory
(
Bousquet and Elisseeff, 2002
;
Mukherjee et al., 2006
;
Shalev-Shwartz et al., 2010
)
. More broadly, various forms of stability are fundamental across applied statistics, in part for their role in establishing reproducibility and interpretability
(
Yu and Kumbier, 2020
;
Yu and Barter, 2024
)
. This work provides general, finite sample theoretical guarantees supporting the use of bagging and related techniques as general-purpose stabilizers. Our stability guarantees represent a major generalization of
Soloff et al. (2024)
—which studied algorithms with outputs on the real line—as our results apply to infinite dimensional settings.

An important remaining open question is how to define and guarantee stability for algorithms with discrete outputs.
In the synthetic controls setting, for example, our theory shows that bagging can stabilize the SCM
weights
.
However, our results do not imply stability in the resulting
support
—that is, the set of control units that receive nonzero support.
In a sense, the goal of sparsity is incompatible with stability
(
Xu et al., 2011
)
, so we need a new framework to reconcile these two desiderata. We leave these questions for future work.

Proof of
Theorem

.

For each
r\in\textnormal{seq}_{[n]}
, define

\hat{w}^{r}=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}^{r};\xi)]
,
the expected output of the randomized algorithm for the data set given by
\mathcal{D}^{r}
. From this point on, we write
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
to denote expectation with respect to
r\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}
. We then have
\mathbb{E}[\hat{w}^{r}]=\hat{w}
and

\hat{w}^{\setminus i}=\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{w}^{r}\mid i\not\in r\right]=\frac{1}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{w}^{r}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{i\not\in r\}\right]

by construction,
and therefore, a straightforward calculation shows that

\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i}=\frac{1}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{w}^{r}-\hat{w})\cdot\left(\mathbf{1}\{i\in r\}-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\right)\right].

(4)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[n]}\|\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[n]}\left\langle\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i},\frac{1}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{w}^{r}-\hat{w})\cdot\left(\mathbf{1}\{i\in r\}-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\right)\right]\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\sum_{i\in[n]}(\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i})\cdot\left(\mathbf{1}\{i\in r\}-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\right),\hat{w}^{r}-\hat{w}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{i\in[n]}(\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i})\cdot\left(\mathbf{1}\{i\in r\}-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right]^{1/2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\hat{w}^{r}-\hat{w}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathsf{rad}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{W})}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{i\in[n]}(\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i})\cdot\left(\mathbf{1}\{i\in r\}-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right]^{1/2}.

The last step holds because we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{w}^{r}-\hat{w}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right]=\inf_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{w}^{r}-w\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right]\leq\inf_{w\in\mathcal{W}}\sup_{w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}}\|w^{\prime}-w\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}=\mathsf{rad}^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{W}),

where the first equality holds since
\hat{w}=\mathbb{E}[\hat{w}^{r}]
minimizes mean-square error.

To calculate the last remaining expected value, we first note that since we have assumed symmetry and nonpositive covariance for
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
(see
Assumption

1
), we have
\textnormal{Cov}\left(\mathbf{1}\{i\in r\},\mathbf{1}\{j\in r\}\right)=-q
for all
i\neq j
, for some
q\geq 0
. Moreover, since the covariance matrix of the random vector
(\mathbf{1}\{i\in r\})_{i\in[n]}
must be positive semidefinite, we must have
q\leq\frac{p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}(1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}})}{n-1}
.
We then have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{i\in[n]}(\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i})\cdot\left(\mathbf{1}\{i\in r\}-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[n]}\textnormal{Var}(\mathbf{1}\{i\in r\})\cdot\|\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}+\sum_{i\neq j\in[n]}\textnormal{Cov}\left(\mathbf{1}\{i\in r\},\mathbf{1}\{j\in r\}\right)\cdot\langle\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i},\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[n]}p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}(1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}})\cdot\|\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}+\sum_{i\neq j\in[n]}(-q)\cdot\langle\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i},\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}(1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}})+q\right)\cdot\sum_{i\in[n]}\|\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}-q\cdot\left\|\sum_{i\in[n]}\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\right\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n}{n-1}\cdot p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}(1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}})\cdot\sum_{i\in[n]}\|\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}.

Combining everything, then, we have

\sum_{i\in[n]}\|\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\frac{\mathsf{rad}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{W})}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}\left(\frac{n}{n-1}\cdot p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}(1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}})\cdot\sum_{i\in[n]}\|\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\right)^{1/2},

which yields

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\|\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\frac{\mathsf{rad}^{2}_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{W})}{n-1}\cdot\frac{p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}

after rearranging terms.
∎

Proof of

Lemma 12
.

Let
B\in\{0,1\}^{n}
be a uniformly random permutation of
(\mathbf{1}_{k},\mathbf{0}_{n-k})

p=k/n
. Let

\max_{j\in[K]}\|c^{(j)}\|_{2}\leq C_{2}

\max_{j\in[K]}\|c^{(j)}\|_{\infty}\leq C_{\infty}

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{j\in[K]}c^{(j)\top}(B-p\mathbf{1}_{n})\right]\leq C_{2}\sqrt{2(1+\eta_{n})(p(1-p)+4\eta_{n})\log K}+\frac{2C_{\infty}}{3}(1+\eta_{n})\log K,

Proof.

Applying

(
Barber, 2024

j

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{\lambda c^{(j)\top}(B-p\mathbf{1}_{n})\right\}\right]\leq\exp\left\{\frac{\lambda^{2}\|c^{(j)}\|^{2}_{2}(1+\eta_{n})}{2(1-\frac{2\lambda}{3}\|c^{(j)}\|_{\infty}(1+\eta_{n}))}\cdot\left(p(1-p)+4\eta_{n}\right)\right\}.

Therefore, from the definition of
C_{2}
and
C_{\infty}

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{\lambda c^{(j)\top}(B-p\mathbf{1}_{n})\right\}\right]\leq\exp\left\{\frac{\lambda^{2}C_{2}^{2}(1+\eta_{n})}{2(1-\frac{2\lambda}{3}(1+\eta_{n})C_{\infty})}\cdot\left(p(1-p)+4\eta_{n}\right)\right\}

for each
j

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{j\in[K]}c^{(j)\top}(B-p\mathbf{1}_{n})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{-1}\log\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left\{\lambda\max_{j\in[K]}c^{(j)\top}(B-p\mathbf{1}_{n})\right\}\right]\right)\textnormal{ by Jensen's inequality}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda^{-1}\log\left(K\cdot\exp\left\{\frac{\lambda^{2}C_{2}^{2}(1+\eta_{n})}{2(1-\frac{2\lambda}{3}(1+\eta_{n})C_{\infty})}\cdot\left(p(1-p)+4\eta_{n}\right)\right\}\right)

B.2
Is

2

We return to the role of
Assumption

2

11

\mathcal{W}
is approximately contained in a

\mathcal{B}
.

We will now see that no uniform stability guarantee can hold without this type of condition. A counterexample can be found with a standard infinite-dimensional Banach space:
\mathcal{B}=L_{1}(\mathbb{N})=\{(x_{1},x_{2},\dots):\sum_{j\geq 1}|x_{j}|<\infty\}
. We will equip
\mathcal{B}
with the norm induced by the
\mathsf{d}_{\mathsf{TV}}
metric, given by
\|x\|=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\geq 1}|x_{j}|
.
This is an infinite-dimensional and separable Banach space.

Next, define
\mathcal{W}=\Delta_{\infty}=\{x\in\mathcal{B}:x_{i}\geq 0\ \forall i,\ \|x\|_{1}=1\}
, the infinite-dimensional probability simplex.
To relate this to our earlier examples, this can be viewed as an infinite-dimensional version of Problem Setting 2(b)—
\mathcal{W}
is the space of distributions over
\mathbb{N}
, so for instance, we might have an algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that returns a posterior distribution over
countably infinitely
many possible models.
Although
\mathcal{W}
is bounded in the norm
\|\cdot\|
, it cannot be approximated by any finite-dimensional subspace in
\mathcal{B}
. The following result shows stability can fail when bagging algorithms with output in
\mathcal{W}
.

in the
j
th location and
0
’s elsewhere.

Now consider data set
\mathcal{D}=(1,\dots,n)
. Let
\hat{w}
and
\hat{w}^{\setminus i}
denote the derandomized bagged estimators, as before, for data sets
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\setminus i}
, respectively.
By construction of
\mathcal{A}
, we can verify that

\hat{w}_{j}=\mathbb{P}_{r\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\{\psi(S(\mathcal{D}^{r}))=j\}

and similarly

\hat{w}^{\setminus i}_{j}=\mathbb{P}_{r\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\{\psi(S(\mathcal{D}^{r}))=j\mid i\not\in r\}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\left|\hat{w}_{j}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i}_{j}\right|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\left|\mathbb{P}_{r\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\{\psi(S(\mathcal{D}^{r}))=j\}-\mathbb{P}_{r\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\{\psi(S(\mathcal{D}^{r}))=j\mid i\not\in r\}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\left|\mathbb{P}_{r\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\{\psi(S(\mathcal{D}^{r}))=j\}-\frac{\mathbb{P}_{r\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\{\psi(S(\mathcal{D}^{r}))=j\}}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{i\not\in\psi^{-1}(j)\}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}_{r\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\{\psi(S(\mathcal{D}^{r}))=j\}\cdot\left|1-\frac{\mathbf{1}\{i\not\in\psi^{-1}(j)\}}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}_{r\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\{\psi(S(\mathcal{D}^{r}))=j\}\cdot\frac{(1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}})\cdot\mathbf{1}\{i\in S(\mathcal{D}^{r})\}+p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\cdot\mathbf{1}\{i\not\in S(\mathcal{D}^{r})\}}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}\left((1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}})\cdot\mathbb{P}_{r\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\{i\in S(\mathcal{D}^{r})\}+p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{r\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\{i\not\in S(\mathcal{D}^{r})\}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}},

since
\mathbb{P}_{r\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\{i\in S(\mathcal{D}^{r})\}=\mathbb{P}_{r\sim\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\{i\in r\}=p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}
.
Since this holds for every
i
, we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\|\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\backslash i}\|^{2}=p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}^{2}
,
which completes the proof.
∎

B.2.1
Remark on dimensionality

To relate the construction in this proof back to
Assumption

2
and
Theorem

10
, note that in the construction of
\mathcal{A}
, at each sample size
n
we are essentially working in a
K
-dimensional space for
K=2^{n}
—that is, even though
\mathcal{W}=\Delta_{\infty}
is the space of all infinite sequences, the algorithm
\mathcal{A}
only ever returns sequences
\hat{w}
with nonzero values in the first
K=2^{n}
entries (and in particular,
\hat{w}
will always lie in the convex hull of
\mathbf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{e}_{K}
).

Compare this to the result of
Theorem

10
, which essentially suggests that mean-square stability vanishes at the rate
\beta^{2}\propto\frac{\log K}{n}
if
\mathcal{W}
can be approximated as a convex hull of
\mathcal{O}(K)
many points. Since we have taken
\frac{\log K}{n}
to be nonvanishing in this example (due to
K=2^{n}
being the “effective dimension”, as described above), stability is no longer ensured as
n\to\infty
.

B.3
Additional results

First, we state a result for tail stability in the Banach space setting.

Corollary 14
.

Under the notation and assumptions of
Theorem

10
, derandomized bagging has tail stability
(\varepsilon,\delta)
with respect to
\|\cdot\|
for any
\varepsilon,\delta\geq 0
satisfying

\delta\varepsilon^{2}\geq 1.6\left[R\sqrt{\frac{2(1+\eta_{n})\log(2K)}{n}\left(\frac{p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}+\frac{4\eta_{n}}{(1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}})^{2}}\right)}+R\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}}+2\rho\cdot p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\right]^{2}.

Proof.

This result follows immediately from
Theorem

10
together with
Proposition

3
.
∎

Finally, we state a finite-
B
result for the Banach space setting.

Corollary 15
.

Under the notation and assumptions of
Theorem

10
, let
B\geq 1
be fixed. Then bagging, run with base algorithm
\mathcal{A}
and resampling distribution
\mathcal{Q}_{n}
and with
B
bags, has mean-square stability
\beta^{2}
with respect to
\|\cdot\|
, with

\beta^{2}:=\inf_{\varepsilon>0}\Bigg\{(1+\varepsilon)\cdot 1.6\bigg[R\sqrt{\frac{2(1+\eta_{n})\log(2K)}{n}\left(\frac{p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}+\frac{4\eta_{n}}{(1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}})^{2}}\right)}+R\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}{1-p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}}}\\
{}+2\rho\cdot p_{\mathcal{Q}_{n}}\bigg]^{2}{}+(1+\varepsilon^{-1})\cdot\left(\frac{8KR^{2}}{B}+8\rho^{2}\right)\Bigg\}

Proof.

Let
\hat{w}
and
\hat{w}^{\setminus i}
be the output of the bagged algorithm (as in
Definition

4
), and let
\tilde{w}
and
\tilde{w}^{\setminus i}
be the corresponding derandomized bagged estimators (as in
Definition

5
). We have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\setminus i}\|^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[\|(\hat{w}-\tilde{w})-(\hat{w}^{\setminus i}-\tilde{w}^{\setminus i})+(\tilde{w}-\tilde{w}^{\setminus i})\|^{2}\right]\\
\leq\frac{1+\varepsilon}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\|\tilde{w}-\tilde{w}^{\setminus i}\|^{2}+\frac{2(1+\varepsilon^{-1})}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{w}-\tilde{w}\|^{2}\right]+\frac{2(1+\varepsilon^{-1})}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\hat{w}^{\setminus i}-\tilde{w}^{\setminus i}\|^{2}\right],

since
\|a+b+c\|^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|a\|^{2}+(1+\varepsilon^{-1})\|b+c\|^{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|a\|^{2}+2(1+\varepsilon^{-1})(\|b\|^{2}+\|c\|^{2})
, for any
a,b,c\in\mathcal{B}
(because
\|\cdot\|
satisfies the triangle inequality).
The first term in the upper bound is bounded by
Theorem

10
. Now we bound the remaining terms.

First, let
\hat{w}^{\prime}
be an i.i.d. copy of
\hat{w}
. Under the same notation as in the proof of
Theorem

10
, let
\|\cdot\|_{*}
denote the dual norm. Then

\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{w}-\tilde{w}\|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f:\|f\|_{*}\leq 1}f(\hat{w}-\tilde{w})^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f:\|f\|_{*}\leq 1}f\left(\hat{w}-\mathbb{E}[\hat{w}^{\prime}\mid\hat{w}]\right)^{2}\right]\\
=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f:\|f\|_{*}\leq 1}\mathbb{E}\left[f\left(\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\prime}\right)\mid\hat{w}\right]^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f:\|f\|_{*}\leq 1}f\left(\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\prime}\right)^{2}\mid\hat{w}\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\prime}\|^{2}],

where the second step holds since
\hat{w}\perp\!\!\!\perp\hat{w}^{\prime}
and
\mathbb{E}[\hat{w}^{\prime}]=\mathbb{E}[\hat{w}]=\tilde{w}
.
Now let

\hat{w}^{(b)}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}^{r_{b}};\xi_{b})
,
for each
b\in[B]
, so that we have
\hat{w}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\hat{w}^{(b)}
, and similarly write

\hat{w}^{\prime}{}^{(b)}=\mathcal{A}(\mathcal{D}^{r_{b}^{\prime}};\xi_{b}^{\prime})
,
where the
r_{b}^{\prime}
and
\xi^{\prime}_{b}
samples are independently drawn, so that
\hat{w}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\hat{w}^{\prime}{}^{(b)}
.
Then
\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\prime}
is equal in distribution to

\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\zeta_{b}\left(\hat{w}^{(b)}-\hat{w}^{\prime}{}^{(b)}\right)

where the
\zeta_{b}
’s are i.i.d. random signs.

Now we will bound
\mathbb{E}[\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\zeta_{b}(w_{b}-w^{\prime}_{b})\|^{2}]
for any
fixed

w_{1},\dots,w_{B},w^{\prime}_{1},\dots,w^{\prime}_{B}\in\mathcal{W}
.
For each
b
we can write

w_{b}-w^{\prime}_{b}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}t^{b}_{j}v_{j}+\Delta_{b}

with
\|t^{b}\|_{1}\leq R
and
\|\Delta_{b}\|\leq\rho
, by
Assumption

2
. Then

\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\zeta_{b}(w_{b}-w^{\prime}_{b})\right\|=\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\zeta_{b}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}t^{b}_{j}v_{j}+\Delta_{b}\right)\right\|\\
\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\zeta_{b}t^{b}_{j}v_{j}\right\|+\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\zeta_{b}\Delta_{b}\right\|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\zeta_{b}t^{b}_{j}\right|+\rho,

since
\|v_{j}\|=1
for all
j
, and
\|\Delta_{b}\|\leq\rho
for all
b
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\zeta_{b}(w_{b}-w^{\prime}_{b})\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{j=1}^{K}\left|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\zeta_{b}t^{b}_{j}\right|+\rho\right)^{2}\right]\leq 2K\sum_{j=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\zeta_{b}t^{b}_{j}\right|^{2}\right]+2\rho^{2}\\
\leq\frac{2K}{B^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\sum_{b=1}^{B}(t_{j}^{b})^{2}+2\rho^{2}=\frac{2K}{B^{2}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\|t^{b}\|^{2}_{2}+2\rho^{2}\leq\frac{2K}{B^{2}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\|t^{b}\|^{2}_{1}+2\rho^{2}\leq\frac{2KR^{2}}{B}+2\rho^{2}.

Combining all our work we then have

\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{w}-\tilde{w}\|^{2}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{w}-\hat{w}^{\prime}\|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\zeta_{b}(\hat{w}^{b}-\hat{w}^{\prime}{}^{b})\right\|^{2}\right]\\
=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\zeta_{b}(\hat{w}^{b}-\hat{w}^{\prime}{}^{b})\right\|^{2}\ \middle|\ \hat{w}^{1},\dots,\hat{w}^{B},\hat{w}^{\prime}{}^{1},\dots,\hat{w}^{\prime}{}^{B}\right]\right]\leq\frac{2KR^{2}}{B}+2\rho^{2}.

The same bound holds for
\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{w}^{\setminus i}-\tilde{w}^{\setminus i}\|^{2}]
. Combining all our calculations establishes the result.
∎
