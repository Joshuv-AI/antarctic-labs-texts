Helicoidal surfaces of prescribed mean curvature in
ℝ

Abstract




Abstract

Given a function
\mathcal{H}\in C^{1}(\mbox{${\mathbb{S}}$}^{2})
, an
\mathcal{H}
-surface
\Sigma
is a surface in the Euclidean space
\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}^{3}
whose mean curvature
H_{\Sigma}
satisfies
H_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{H}\circ\eta
, where
\eta
is the Gauss map of
\Sigma
. The purpose of this paper is to use a phase space analysis to give some classification results for helicoidal
\mathcal{H}
-surfaces, when
\mathcal{H}
is rotationally symmetric, that is,
\mathcal{H}\circ\eta=\mathfrak{h}\circ\nu
, for some
\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}\in C^{1}([-1,1])
, where
\nu
is the angle function of the surface. We prove a classification theorem for the case where
\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}(t)
is even and increasing for
t\in[0,1]
. Finally, we provide examples of helicoidal
\mathcal{H}
-surfaces in cases where
\mathfrak{h}
vanishes at some point.

Mathematics Subject Classification:

53A10, 53C42, 34C05, 34C40.

Keywords:

helicoidal surfaces, prescribed mean curvature.

1.  Introduction

In this article, we study the existence and classification of helicoidal surfaces
\Sigma
of
\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}^{3}
whose mean curvature
H_{\Sigma}
is given as a prescribed function of its Gauss map
\eta:\Sigma\to\mbox{${\mathbb{S}}$}^{2}
. Specifically, given
\mathcal{H}\in C^{1}(\mbox{${\mathbb{S}}$}^{2})
, we are interested in finding surfaces
\Sigma
that satisfy

H_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{H}\circ\eta.

Any such
\Sigma
will be called a surface of
prescribed mean curvature

\mathcal{H}
, or simply, an
\mathcal{H}
-surface
.

The study of
\mathcal{H}
-hypersurfaces in
\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}^{n+1}
is motivated by the classical contributions of Alexandrov and Pogorelov in the 1950s (see
[
1
,
7
]
). They started to investigate the existence and uniqueness of ovaloids in
\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}^{n+1}
determined by a prescribed curvature function through its Gauss map.

When specific functions
\mathcal{H}
are chosen as prescribed, some geometric theories receive deeply research. The following examples are emphasized:

(1)

Surfaces of constant mean curvature, or simply, CMC surfaces. In this case,
\mathcal{H}\equiv H_{0}\in\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}
is a constant. When
H_{0}=0
, they are minimal surfaces;

(2)

\lambda
-translators, which corresponds to the case
\mathcal{H}(x)=\langle x,v\rangle+\lambda
, where
v\in\mbox{${\mathbb{S}}$}^{n}
and
\lambda\in\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}
. The vector
v
is called the density vector. When
\lambda=0
, we have translating solitons of the mean curvature flow.

The theory of complete, non-compact
\mathcal{H}
-surfaces for general choice of
\mathcal{H}
has been recently developed by A. Bueno, J. Gálvez and P. Mira
[
2
,
3
]
, taking as starting point the theories of CMC surfaces and translating solitons. In the first article, the authors started the development of the global theory of complete
\mathcal{H}
-hypersurfaces in
\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}^{n+1}
, demonstrating that, under mild symmetry and regularity assumptions on the function
\mathcal{H}
, the theory of
\mathcal{H}
-hypersurfaces sometimes admits a uniform treatment that resembles the constant mean curvature case.

In
[
3
]
, focusing on the study of rotational
\mathcal{H}
-hypersurfaces in
\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}^{n+1}
, in case that the function
\mathcal{H}\in C^{1}(\mbox{${\mathbb{S}}$}^{n})
is rotationally symmetric, i.e.
\mathcal{H}(x)=\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}(\langle x,e_{n+1}\rangle)
for some
\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}\in C^{1}([-1,1])
, the same authors treated the resulting ordinary differential equation as a nonlinear autonomous system and conducted a qualitative study of its solutions through a phase space analysis. The aim was to provide classification results for these hypersurfaces.

Motivated by these ideas, our objective in this article is to use a similar phase space analysis to study, classify and exhibit some helicoidal surfaces of prescribed mean curvature in
\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}^{3}
.

First, in Section
2
, we define a helicoidal surface
\Sigma
in
\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}^{3}
(Def.
2.1
) and explicitly calculate in (
2
) the mean curvature of
\Sigma
in terms of its profile curve. Since the mean curvature of a helicoidal surface does not depend on its rotational parameter, we focus on the case where
\mathcal{H}\in C^{1}(\mbox{${\mathbb{S}}$}^{2})
is rotationally symmetric, that is, up to Euclidian change of coordinates, there is a function
\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}\in C^{1}([-1,1])
such that
\mathcal{H}(x)=\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}(\langle x,e_{3}\rangle)
. Then, the mean curvature of a helicoidal
\mathcal{H}
-surface
\Sigma
is given by

H_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{H}\circ\eta=\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}\circ\nu.

In terms of the profile curve of
\Sigma
, we treat next the resulting ODE as the nonlinear autonomous system (
2.12
), carrying out a qualitative study of its solutions through a phase space analysis. We also prove in Proposition
2.4
that, up to rotations around the
e_{3}
-axis, there exists at most a unique helicoidal
\mathcal{H}
-surface in
\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}^{3}
that intersects its rotation axis.

Building on the ideas of Theorem 4.1 of
[
3
]
, we study in Section
3
the helicoidal
\mathcal{H}
-surfaces in cases where
\mathcal{H}\in C^{1}(\mbox{${\mathbb{S}}$}^{2})
is positive, rotationally invariant and even, i.e.
\mathcal{H}(-x)=\mathcal{H}(x)>0
for all
x\in\mbox{${\mathbb{S}}$}^{2}
. Different from the rotational case, we also need to suppose that the function
\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}(t)
related to
\mathcal{H}
is increasing for
t\in[0,1]
. Then, we arrive at the classification Theorem
3.3
.

Finally, in Section
4
, we provide examples of helicoidal
\mathcal{H}
-surfaces in cases where
\mathcal{H}
vanishes at some point. The biggest problem to extend the orbits behavior of the examples to general choices of
\mathcal{H}
is not knowing exactly how the phase space looks like. It is important to observe that, even though these examples are specific, similar functions
\mathcal{H}
can lead to similar behaviors of the resulting helicoidal
\mathcal{H}
-surfaces. Thus, attempting to generalize these models to various choices of
\mathcal{H}
could inspire future research in this area.

2.  Phase space analysis of helicoidal
\mathcal{H}
-surfaces

Let
\Sigma
be an immersed, oriented surface in
\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}^{3}
and
\eta:\Sigma\to\mbox{${\mathbb{S}}$}^{2}
its Gauss map. Given a function
\mathcal{H}\in C^{1}(\mbox{${\mathbb{S}}$}^{2})
, we will say that
\Sigma
is a
surface of prescribed mean curvature

\mathcal{H}
, or simply,
\mathcal{H}
-surface
, if its mean curvature
H_{\Sigma}
is given by

(2.1)

H_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{H}\circ\eta.

When
\Sigma
is given as a graph
x_{3}=u(x_{1},x_{2})
, then (
2.1
) is written as the elliptic, second order quasilinear PDE

(2.2)

\mathrm{div}\left(\frac{\mathrm{D}u}{\sqrt{1+|\mathrm{D}u|^{2}}}\right)=2\mathcal{H}(\mathrm{Z}_{u}),\quad\mathrm{Z}_{u}:=\frac{(-\mathrm{D}u,1)}{\sqrt{1+|\mathrm{D}u|^{2}}},

where
\mathrm{div}
and
\mathrm{D}
denote respectively the divergence and gradient operators on
\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}^{2}
and
\mathrm{Z}_{u}
is the unit normal of the graph.

Definition 2.1
.

A helicoidal surface
\Sigma
in
\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}^{3}
is an immersed, oriented surface obtained as the orbit of a regular planar curve parametrized by arc-length

\alpha(s)=(x(s),0,z(s)):I\subset\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}\to\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}^{3}

under a helicoidal motion, that is, up to isometries of
\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}^{3}
, there exists a constant
c_{0}\neq 0
such that

(2.3)

\Psi(s,\theta)=\left(x(s)\cos{\theta},x(s)\sin{\theta},z(s)+c_{0}\theta\right):I\times\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}\to\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}^{3}

is a parametrization of
\Sigma
.

Up to a change of orientation, the Gauss map of a helicoidal surface
\Sigma
is given by

(2.4)

\eta(s,\theta)=\frac{\left(c_{0}\sin{\theta}x^{\prime}(s)-\cos{\theta}z^{\prime}(s)x(s),-\sin{\theta}x(s)z^{\prime}(s)-c_{0}\cos{\theta}x^{\prime}(s),x(s)x^{\prime}(s)\right)}{\sqrt{c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime}(s)^{2}+x(s)^{2}}}.

Therefore, the
angle function

\nu:=\langle\eta,e_{3}\rangle
of
\Sigma
is given by

(2.5)

\nu(s)=\nu(s,\theta)=\frac{x(s)x^{\prime}(s)}{\sqrt{c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime}(s)^{2}+x(s)^{2}}}.

Note that
\|\Psi_{s}(s,\theta)\wedge\Psi_{\theta}(s,\theta)\|=0
if, and only if,
x(s)=x^{\prime}(s)=0
. So, we assume that
x^{\prime}(s)\neq 0
for
s\in I
such that
x(s)=0
to
\Sigma
be a regular surface.

Remark 2.2
.

In a helicoidal surface
\Sigma
, its angle function satisfies
|\nu|<1
. Indeed, for
s\in I
such that
x^{\prime}(s)=0
, we directly obtain
\nu(s)=0<1
. For
s\in I
such that
x^{\prime}(s)\neq 0
, since
|x^{\prime}(s)|\leq 1
and
c_{0}\neq 0
, we have

|\nu(s)|=\frac{|x(s)||x^{\prime}(s)|}{\sqrt{c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime}(s)^{2}+x(s)^{2}}}\leq\frac{|x(s)|}{\sqrt{c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime}(s)^{2}+x(s)^{2}}}<1.

∎

The coefficients of the first fundamental form of
\Sigma
are

\displaystyle E

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi_{s},\Psi_{s}\rangle=\cos^{2}(\theta)x^{\prime}(s)^{2}+\sin^{2}(\theta)x^{\prime}(s)^{2}+z^{\prime}(s)^{2}=1,

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi_{s},\Psi_{\theta}\rangle=-\sin\theta\cos\theta x(s)x^{\prime}(s)+\sin\theta\cos\theta x(s)x^{\prime}(s)+c_{0}z^{\prime}(s)=c_{0}z^{\prime}(s),

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi_{\theta},\Psi_{\theta}\rangle=\sin^{2}(\theta)x^{2}(s)+\cos^{2}(\theta)x^{2}(s)+c_{0}^{2}=x(s)^{2}+c_{0}^{2},

and its coefficients of the second fundamental form are

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi_{ss},\eta\rangle=\frac{x(s)\left(z^{\prime\prime}(s)x^{\prime}(s)-z^{\prime}(s)x^{\prime\prime}(s)\right)}{\sqrt{c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime}(s)^{2}+x(s)^{2}}},

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi_{s\theta},\eta\rangle=-\frac{c_{0}x^{\prime}(s)^{2}}{\sqrt{c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime}(s)^{2}+x(s)^{2}}},

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi_{\theta\theta},\eta\rangle=\frac{x(s)^{2}z^{\prime}(s)}{\sqrt{c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime}(s)^{2}+x(s)^{2}}}.

Therefore, the mean curvature of a helicoidal surface
\Sigma
is given by

\displaystyle H_{\Sigma}

\displaystyle=\frac{eG-2fF+gE}{2(EG-F^{2})}=\frac{x(z^{\prime\prime}x^{\prime}-z^{\prime}x^{\prime\prime})(x^{2}+c_{0}^{2})+2c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime 2}z^{\prime}+x^{2}z^{\prime}}{2\sqrt{c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2}}\left(x^{2}+c_{0}^{2}-c_{0}^{2}z^{\prime 2}\right)}

(2.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{(x^{\prime}z^{\prime\prime}-z^{\prime}x^{\prime\prime})(x^{3}+c_{0}^{2}x)+z^{\prime}(2c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2})}{2(c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

Since the mean curvature of a helicoidal surface does not depend on the rotational parameter
\theta
, let
\mathcal{H}\in C^{1}(\mbox{${\mathbb{S}}$}^{n})
be a rotationally symmetric function, that is,
\mathcal{H}(x)=\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}(\langle x,v\rangle)
, for some
v\in\mbox{${\mathbb{S}}$}^{2}
and
\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}\in C^{1}([-1,1])
. Up to an Euclidean change of coordinates, we can assume that
v=e_{3}
, and then
\mathcal{H}(x)=\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}(\langle x,e_{3}\rangle)
.

Let now
\Sigma
be a helicoidal
\mathcal{H}
-surface in
\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}^{3}
. From (
2.1
), the mean curvature of
\Sigma
is given by

(2.7)

H_{\Sigma}=\mathcal{H}\circ\eta=\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}\circ\nu.

Therefore, from (
2
), the profile curve
\alpha(s)
of
\Sigma
satisfies

(2.8)

2\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}\left(\frac{xx^{\prime}}{\sqrt{c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2}}}\right)=\frac{(x^{\prime}z^{\prime\prime}-z^{\prime}x^{\prime\prime})(x^{3}+c_{0}^{2}x)+z^{\prime}(2c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2})}{(c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

Taking into account that
x^{\prime 2}+z^{\prime 2}=1
, we can denote
z^{\prime}=\varepsilon\sqrt{1-x^{\prime 2}}
, where
\varepsilon=\sign(z^{\prime})
. Thus,

(2.9)

z^{\prime\prime}=-\varepsilon\frac{x^{\prime}x^{\prime\prime}}{\sqrt{1-x^{\prime 2}}},

on every subinterval
J\subset I
where
z^{\prime}(s)\neq 0
, for all
s\in J
. So, from (
2.8
), we obtain that
x(s)
is a solution to the autonomous second
order ordinary differential equation

\displaystyle 2(c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}\left(\frac{xx^{\prime}}{\sqrt{c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon x^{\prime\prime}\left(\frac{x^{\prime 2}}{\sqrt{1-x^{\prime 2}}}+\sqrt{1-x^{\prime 2}}\right)(x^{3}+c_{0}^{2}x)

\displaystyle\phantom{==}+\varepsilon\sqrt{1-x^{\prime 2}}(2c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2})

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\left(\frac{x^{\prime\prime}}{\sqrt{1-x^{\prime 2}}}(x^{3}+c_{0}^{2}x)-\sqrt{1-x^{\prime 2}}(2c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2})\right)

(2.10)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\left(\frac{x^{\prime\prime}(x^{3}+c_{0}^{2}x)-(1-x^{\prime 2})(2c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2})}{\sqrt{1-x^{\prime 2}}}\right),

that is, when
x\neq 0
,

(2.11)

x^{\prime\prime}=\frac{(1-x^{\prime 2})(x^{2}+2c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime 2})-2\varepsilon\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}\left(\frac{xx^{\prime}}{\sqrt{c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2}}}\right)\sqrt{1-x^{\prime 2}}(c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime 2}+x^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}{x^{3}+c_{0}^{2}x}.

Denoting
y=x^{\prime}
, the ODE (
2.11
) transforms into the first order autonomous system

(2.12)

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
y\end{array}\right)^{\prime}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}y\\
\frac{(1-y^{2})(x^{2}+2c_{0}^{2}y^{2})-2\varepsilon\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}\left(\frac{xy}{\sqrt{c_{0}^{2}y^{2}+x^{2}}}\right)\sqrt{1-y^{2}}(c_{0}^{2}y^{2}+x^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}{x^{3}+c_{0}^{2}x}\end{array}\right)=:F(x,y).

The phase space of the system is

\Theta_{\varepsilon}:=\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}\smallsetminus\{0\}\times(-1,1)

with coordinates
(x,y)
denoting, respectively, the distance to the rotation axis and its variation.

If the profile curve
\alpha(s)=(x(s),0,z(s))
intersects the
e_{3}
-axis, that is,
x(s)=0
for some
s\in I
, we obtain from (
2.10
) that

\varepsilon\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}(0)=\frac{2(1-x^{\prime}(s)^{2})c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime}(s)^{2}}{2\sqrt{1-x^{\prime}(s)^{2}}|c_{0}|^{3}|x^{\prime}(s)|^{3}}=\frac{\sqrt{1-x^{\prime}(s)^{2}}}{|c_{0}||x^{\prime}(s)|}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}(0)^{2}=\frac{1-x^{\prime}(s)^{2}}{c_{0}^{2}x^{\prime}(s)^{2}},

that is,
\varepsilon\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}(0)\geq 0
and
\alpha(s)
satisfies

(2.13)

x^{\prime}(s)=\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+c_{0}^{2}\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}(0)^{2}}}.

In particular, its associated orbit in
\Theta_{\varepsilon}
, such that
\varepsilon\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}(0)\geq 0
, must intersect the
x=0
axis at the points

p_{\varepsilon}:=\left(0,\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+c_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{h}(0)^{2}}}\right)\quad\text{or}\quad-p_{\varepsilon}=\left(0,-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+c_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{h}(0)^{2}}}\right).

Moreover, observe that no orbit in
\Theta_{\varepsilon}
can have a limit point of the form
(0,y)
, with
y\neq\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+c_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{h}(0)^{2}}}
. Indeed, since
\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}\in C^{1}([-1,1])
and the equation (
2.10
) is continuous around
\Theta_{\varepsilon}
, we can take
x(s)\to 0
and obtain
y(s)\to\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+c_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{h}(0)^{2}}}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Let
\gamma_{1}(s)=(x(s),y(s))
be any solution to (
2.12
). Observe that
\gamma_{2}(s)=(-x(s),-y(s))
is also a solution to the system. This means that the phase space
\Theta_{\varepsilon}
is symmetric with respect to the origin. Thus, without loss of generality, it is enough to analyse the case
x(s)>0
and consider the phase space

\Theta_{\varepsilon}=(0,\infty)\times(-1,1).

If the profile curve intersects the
e_{3}
-axis, its associated orbit
(x(s),y(s))
in
\Theta_{\varepsilon}
must pass through the point
\pm p_{\varepsilon}
at some
s_{0}\in I
(
x(s_{0})=0
). Since
x^{\prime}(s_{0})\neq 0
, let
\delta>0
such that, for
s\in(s_{0},s_{0}+\delta)
or
s\in(s_{0}-\delta,s_{0})
, we would have
x(s)<0
. Note that we can analyse the orbit behavior from
\mp p_{\varepsilon}
, considering the symmetry, and obtain
x(s)>0
for
s\in(s_{0}-\delta,s_{0}+\delta)\smallsetminus\{s_{0}\}
.

Moreover, let
\alpha_{i}(s)=(x_{i}(s),0,z_{i}(s))
,
i=1,2
, be the profile curve of the helicoidal
\mathcal{H}
-surface
\Sigma_{i}
associated to
\gamma_{i}(s)
and let
\Psi_{i}(s,\theta)
be the parametrization of
\Sigma_{i}
, as in (
2.3
). Letting
z_{0}:=z_{1}(0)
and considering, up to vertical translations, that
z_{2}(0)=z_{0}+c_{0}\pi
, we obtain that

\Psi_{2}(s,\theta)=\Psi_{1}(s,\theta+\pi),

that is, up to a change of coordinates that corresponds to a rotation by
\pi
around the
e_{3}
-axis,
\Sigma_{1}=\Sigma_{2}
.

Indeed, note that
x_{2}(s)=-x_{1}(s)=-x(s)
and

z_{2}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\varepsilon\sqrt{1-(-y(t))^{2}}dt+z_{2}(0)=\int_{0}^{s}\varepsilon\sqrt{1-(y(t))^{2}}dt+z_{0}+c_{0}\pi=z_{1}(s)+c_{0}\pi.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}(s,\theta)

\displaystyle=\left(x_{2}(s)\cos{\theta},\,x_{2}(s)\sin{\theta},\,z_{2}(s)+c_{0}\theta\right)

\displaystyle=\left(-x(s)\cos{\theta},\,-x(s)\sin{\theta},\,z_{1}(s)+c_{0}\pi+c_{0}\theta\right)

\displaystyle=\left(x(s)\cos{(\theta+\pi)},\,x(s)\sin{(\theta+\pi)},\,z_{1}(s)+c_{0}(\pi+\theta)\right)

\displaystyle=\Psi_{1}(s,\theta+\pi).

∎

Proposition 2.4
.

There exists at most a unique (up to rotations around the
e_{3}
-axis) helicoidal
\mathcal{H}
-surface in
\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}^{3}
that intersects its rotation axis.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
be helicoidal
\mathcal{H}
-surfaces that meet the
e_{3}
-axis. Then, this axis is contained in both surfaces. Let
\Psi_{1}(s,\theta)
and
\Psi_{2}(s,\theta)
be the parametrization of
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
, respectively, as in (
2.3
), where, up to rotations around the
e_{3}
-axis and a change of coordinates,
\alpha_{1}(s)=(x_{1}(s),0,z_{1}(s))
and
\alpha_{2}(s)=(x_{2}(s),0,z_{2}(s))
are the profile curves of
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
, respectively, in the plane
\Pi=\{y=0\}
, such that
\Psi_{i}(s,0)=\alpha_{i}(s)
,
i=1,2
, and
\alpha_{1}(0)=\alpha_{2}(0)=p_{0}
, for some
p_{0}=(0,0,z_{0})\in\Sigma_{1}\cap\Sigma_{2}
.

Consider
\delta_{i}:=\sign(x_{i}^{\prime}(0))
,
i=1,2
. By (
2.4
), we obtain that the Gauss map of
\Sigma_{i}
in
p_{0}
is given by

\eta(0,0)=\frac{\left(c_{0}\sin(0)x_{i}^{\prime}(0),-c_{0}\cos(0)x_{i}^{\prime}(0),0\right)}{|c_{0}||x_{i}^{\prime}(0)|}=-\sign(c_{0})\left(0,\delta_{i},0\right).

Let
\varepsilon\in\{-1,1\}
such that
\varepsilon\mbox{$\mathfrak{h}$}(0)\geq 0
, where
\mathfrak{h}
is given by (
2.7
) in terms of
\mathcal{H}
. By (
2.13
), we have

|x_{1}^{\prime}(0)|=|x_{2}^{\prime}(0)|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+c_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{h}(0)^{2}}},

that is, the orbit associated to the profile curve of
\Sigma_{i}
passes through the point
\delta_{i}p_{\varepsilon}\in\Theta_{\varepsilon}
. If
\delta_{1}\neq\delta_{2}
, we can rotate
\Sigma_{2}
by
\pi
around the
e_{3}
-axis and, then, by Remark
2.3
, obtain that
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
have the same unit normal in
p_{0}
.

On a sufficiently small ball
B_{\delta}(p_{0})\subset\Pi
, we can see
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
as graphs of differential functions
u_{1}(x,z)
and
u_{2}(x,z)
, respectively, with

u_{1}(0,z)=u_{2}(0,z)=0,\quad\forall z\in(z_{0}-\delta,z_{0}+\delta),

where the difference
v:=u_{1}-u_{2}
is a pseudoanalytic function, since
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
are solutions to the same elliptic PDE (
2.2
) (
[
4
, p. 469]
).

If there exists some
(x_{0},z_{0})\in B_{\delta}(p_{0})
, with
x_{0}\neq 0
, such that
u_{1}(x_{0},z_{0})=u_{2}(x_{0},z_{0})
, consider
s_{0},\theta_{0}\in\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}
such that
\Psi_{1}(s_{0},\theta_{0})=u_{1}(x_{0},z_{0})=\Psi_{2}(s_{0},\theta_{0})
. This means that
\Psi_{1}(s_{0},\theta)=\Psi_{2}(s_{0},\theta)
, for all
\theta\in\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}
, that is,
u_{1}=u_{2}
along the curve
\gamma_{s_{0}}(\theta):=\Psi_{1}(s_{0},\theta)
in
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
. In case there exists a set of accumulated curves
\gamma_{s_{0}}(\theta)
, we obtain that
u_{1}\equiv u_{2}
by pseudoanalyticity, i.e.
\Sigma_{1}=\Sigma_{2}
. Therefore, the curves
\gamma_{s_{0}}
are isolated and we can get a sufficiently small ball
B_{\epsilon}(p_{0})
such that
u_{1}\leq u_{2}
in

B_{\epsilon}(p_{0})\cap\{x\leq 0\}.

By (
2.2
), the functions
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
satisfy the same absolutely elliptic partial differential equation. It follows from
[
5
, Corollary 4.6]
that
u_{1}\equiv u_{2}
and, therefore, up to rotations around the
e_{3}
-axis,

e_{0}=\left(\frac{1}{2\mathfrak{h}(0)},0\right)\in\Theta_{1}
corresponds to the cylinder
C_{\mathcal{H}}=\mathbb{S}^{1}\left(\frac{1}{2\mathfrak{h}(0)}\right)\times\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}
.

The linearized system at
e_{0}
associated to the nonlinear system (
2.12
) for
\varepsilon=1
is

(3.5)

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u\\
v\end{array}\right)^{\prime}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-\frac{4\mathfrak{h}(0)^{2}}{1+4c_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{h}(0)^{2}}&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u\\
v\end{array}\right),

whose orbits are ellipses around the origin. By classical theory of nonlinear autonomous systems, since the eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix in (
3.5
) are pure complex numbers
\pm\frac{2\mathfrak{h}(0)}{\sqrt{1+4\mathfrak{h}(0)^{2}}}i
, we get that the equilibrium
e_{0}
has a center structure. This means that all orbits around
e_{0}
are closed curves, since
\Theta_{1}
is symmetric with respect to the
y=0
axis. In particular, we deduce that all orbits of
\Theta_{1}
stay at a positive distance from
e_{0}
.

By Proposition
2.4
, there exists an unique orbit
\gamma_{0}
in the phase space
\Theta_{1}
that passes through
p_{1}=\left(0,\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+c_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{h}(0)^{2}}}\right)
. By monotonicity properties,
\gamma_{0}
lies near
p_{1}
inside the monotonicity region
\Lambda_{2}
and it cannot stay forever in that region, by a same previous argument using that
\mathcal{H}\geq H_{0}>0
. Moreover,
\gamma_{0}
stays at a positive distance from
e_{0}
and it does not intersect
\Gamma_{1}
. Thus,
\gamma_{0}
must intersect the
y=0
axis at some point
(x_{0},0)
with
x_{0}>\frac{1}{2\mathfrak{h}(0)}
.

By symmetry of the phase space, we deduce that
\gamma_{0}
can be extended to be an orbit in
\Theta_{1}
that joins the limit points
p_{1}
and
-p_{1}
, and that lies in the region
\Lambda_{2}\cup\Lambda_{3}\cup\{y=0\}
. Taking into account that we can extend this orbit to
x(s)<0
, using that the phase space is symmetric by Remark
2.3
, the helicoidal
\mathcal{H}
-surface associated to the orbit
\gamma_{0}
is given by the orbit of a curve
\alpha(s)=(x(s),0,z(s))
under a helicoidal motion, with
x(s)
periodic and
x(s_{0}+kT)=0
, for some
s_{0},T\in\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}
and for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
z^{\prime}(s)>0
, for all
s\in\mbox{${\mathbb{R}}$}
. See Figure
3.6
.

Figure 3.6.

\gamma_{0}
in

-surface.

The orbit

divides
\Theta_{1}
into two connected components. The first one containing the equilibrium

, which we will denote by

is a symmetric horizontal graph

with

for some

-surface

By (

\Sigma
is constant along the curves

\beta_{0}:=\left\{(x,y)\in\Theta_{\varepsilon}:\frac{xy}{\sqrt{x^{2}+c_{0}^{2}y^{2}}}=t_{0}\right\}

in each

\Theta_{\varepsilon}

By letting

converges to the straight line
