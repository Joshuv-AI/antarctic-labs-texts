Field Theory of Free Run and Tumble Particles in d d Dimensions
Ziluo Zhang, Gunnar Pruessner

Abstract
In this paper, Doi-Peliti field theory is used to describe the motion of free Run and Tumble particles in arbitrary dimensions. After deriving action and propagators, the mean square displacement and the corresponding entropy production at stationarity are calculated in this framework. We further derive the field theory of free Active Brownian Particles in two dimensions for comparison.



1
Introduction

The Run and Tumble (RnT) process is a random walk used to describe the motion of several biological species, such as
Escherichia coli
, also known as
E. coli

[
1
]
. Due to the lack of sensing organs, an
E. coli
performs a run with nearly constant speed
v
in an almost straight line to probe the environment, until, with the Poissonian rate
\alpha
, it parks for some time to choose a random direction from a uniform distribution to continue a ballistic motion
[
2
]
.
Any particle that follows an RnT process is called an RnT particle, also known as persistent Brownian motion
[
3
]
.

Doi-Peliti field theory
[
4
,
5
]
is a class of field theories to deal with non-equilibrium statistical mechanics
[
6
]
. Observables in this field theory are calculated from the master or Fokker-Planck equation
[
7
,
8
]
, applying second quantisation and the path integral, in a straight forward manner. The aim in the following is to apply Doi-Peliti field theory to the one and higher dimensional RnT process, to calculate the particle density subject to initial conditions, the propagators and to use it to determine the mean squared displacement (MSD) as well as the entropy production. A free RnT particle can take two directions in one dimension, so that there is always one direction available to be picked, which is inverse to the current direction. In one dimension, particles can be assigned two distinct species, depending on their drift direction. However, because of the infinite choices of directions in two and higher dimensions, this option is no longer available as the range of species is continuous and periodic.

There are several questions we are motivated by. Firstly, we want to establish the field-theoretic framework to capture RnT motion in arbitrary dimensions exactly. While this is a mere exercise in one dimension, the two-dimensional problem is significantly richer. At this stage, we consider only the free case,
i.e.
RnT particle without external potential. Secondly, we want to compare RnT motion to diffusive processes. While it is clear that the MSD is asymptotically identical to that of diffusion, the time and length scale below which this precisely breaks down is relevant in theories and applications.
Thirdly, we want to determine the entropy production of free RnT particles in dimensions beyond one to see how previous results generalise.

In the following, we will derive the action and the propagator of one, two and higher dimensional RnT processes in Section
2
, determine the MSD and the entropy production in Section
3
, and the MSD of active Brownian particles in Section
4
. We discuss the results in Section
5
.

2
Field theory of RnT particles

The motion of RnT particles can be divided into two parts: ‘run’ and ‘tumble’. During a run, particles drift
in the direction of a director
with constant speed
v
deviating from a straight line only due to diffusion with constant
D
, until, during a ’tumble’, they change direction instantaneously and spontaneously as the effect of an underlying Poisson process with rate
\alpha
. In other words, particles tumble with rate
\alpha
and at tumbling, they immediately choose a new, random, uniformly distributed direction. In one dimension the director can take only two values, which is conveniently encoded by different particle species.
In two dimensions the director is parameterised by the angle
\theta
relative to the
x
-axis and is, at tumbling, drawn uniformly from the interval
[0,2\pi)
. Figure
1
shows a sample path of a two-dimensional RnT particle.

Figure 1:
An example of a 2D RnT particle’s motion. The straight lines show the trajectory of the motion, the wriggly line is the ’tail’ of the particle, which points the opposite direction of the velocity. The arrow from the dashed line to the straight line shows a change of the velocity during a tumble.

In the following, we will derive the field theory of RnT particles. To illustrate the procedure, we will initially focus on the one-dimensional case. (4)

2.1
One dimension

In one dimension, particles have only two velocities to drift, leftwards
-v
and rightwards
v
and therefore particles are in the Markovian sense fully characterised by their position
x
and direction of the drift. The tumbling may be interpreted as a transmutation of a species
drifting with velocity
v
to another one with velocity
-v
and vice versa. Because tumbling results in a randomly chosen direction and transmutation is a reversal of the director, the latter takes place with a rate half that of the former. The coupled Fokker-Planck equations of this process read

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P_{L}(x,t)

\displaystyle=D\partial_{x}^{2}P_{L}(x,t)+v\partial_{x}P_{L}(x,t)-\frac{\alpha}{2}P_{L}(x,t)+\frac{\alpha}{2}P_{R}(x,t),

(5a)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P_{R}(x,t)

\displaystyle=D\partial_{x}^{2}P_{R}(x,t)-v\partial_{x}P_{R}(x,t)-\frac{\alpha}{2}P_{R}(x,t)+\frac{\alpha}{2}P_{L}(x,t),

(5b)

where
P_{L}(x,t)
and
P_{R}(x,t)
are the probability densities of leftward and rightward particle at position
x
and time
t
respectively. To pave the way for the two-dimensional case, we may write Eq. (
5
) in the form

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P(x,\theta_{i},t)

\displaystyle=D\partial_{x}^{2}P(x,\theta_{i},t)-v_{i}\partial_{x}P(x,\theta_{i},t)-\alpha P(x,\theta_{i},t)+\frac{\alpha}{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}P(x,\theta_{j},t)

(6)

for a density
P(x,\theta_{i},t)
of finding a particle at time
t
at
x
with director
\theta_{i}

where
\theta_{i}
with
i\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
refers to the
M=2
directions particles can be moving in with velocity
v_{1}=v
and
v_{2}=-v
. This probability density is in fact the full propagator Eq. (
2

\mathcal{G}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{0},\theta_{i},\theta_{j},t-t_{0})=\left\langle\phi(\mathbf{x},\theta_{i},t)\tilde{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{0},\theta_{j},t_{0})\right\rangle=P(\mathbf{x},\theta_{i},t)

(7)

with the initial condition stated explicitly. The field theory provides a perturbative approach to solving the Fokker-Planck equation.

With drift, diffusion and transmutation well established

[
7

9
]

\mathcal{A}\big([\phi(x,\theta_{i},t),\tilde{\phi}(x,\theta_{i},t)]\big)=\mathcal{A}_{0}\big([\phi(x,\theta_{i},t),\tilde{\phi}(x,\theta_{i},t)]\big)+\mathcal{A}_{\text{\tiny pert}}\big([\phi(x,\theta_{i},t),\tilde{\phi}(x,\theta_{i},t)]\big)

(8)

\mathcal{A}_{0}

\mathcal{A}_{\text{\tiny pert}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{0}\big([\phi(x,\theta_{i},t),\tilde{\phi}(x,\theta_{i},t)]\big)

\displaystyle=\int\mathchoice{\!\mathrm{d}x\,}{\!\mathrm{d}x\,}{\!\mathrm{d}x\,}{\!\mathrm{d}x\,}\mathchoice{\!\mathrm{d}t\,}{\!\mathrm{d}t\,}{\!\mathrm{d}t\,}{\!\mathrm{d}t\,}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\tilde{\phi}(x,\theta_{i},t)\big[\partial_{t}+v_{i}\partial_{x}-D\partial_{x}^{2}+\alpha+r\big]\phi(x,\theta_{i},t)

(9a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\text{\tiny pert}}\big([\phi(x,\theta_{i},t),\tilde{\phi}(x,\theta_{i},t)]\big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\alpha}{M}\int\mathchoice{\!\mathrm{d}x\,}{\!\mathrm{d}x\,}{\!\mathrm{d}x\,}{\!\mathrm{d}x\,}\mathchoice{\!\mathrm{d}t\,}{\!\mathrm{d}t\,}{\!\mathrm{d}t\,}{\!\mathrm{d}t\,}\sum_{i,j=1}^{M}\tilde{\phi}(x,\theta_{j},t)\phi(x,\theta_{i},t)\ ,

\phi

\left\langle\phi(k,\theta_{2},\omega)\tilde{\phi}(k^{\prime},\theta_{1},\omega^{\prime})\right\rangle&\left\langle\phi(k,\theta_{2},\omega)\tilde{\phi}(k^{\prime},\theta_{2},\omega^{\prime})\right\rangle\end{pmatrix}=\delta\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22(k+k^{\prime})\delta\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22(\omega+\omega^{\prime})(\underline{\underline{G}}+\underline{\underline{G}}\underline{\underline{T}}\underline{\underline{G}}+\ldots)\\
=\delta\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22(k+k^{\prime})\delta\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22(\omega+\omega^{\prime})\underline{\underline{G}}(\mathds{1}-\underline{\underline{T}}\underline{\underline{G}})^{-1}=\delta\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22(k+k^{\prime})\delta\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22(\omega+\omega^{\prime})(\underline{\underline{G}}^{-1}-\underline{\underline{T}})^{-1}\ ,

(17)

reproducing Eq. (
16
) as

\underline{\underline{G}}^{-1}-\underline{\underline{T}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-\mathring{\imath}\omega+\mathring{\imath}v_{1}k+Dk^{2}+\alpha+r-\frac{\alpha}{M}&-\frac{\alpha}{M}\\
-\frac{\alpha}{M}&-\mathring{\imath}\omega+\mathring{\imath}v_{2}k+Dk^{2}+\alpha+r-\frac{\alpha}{M}\end{array}\right)=\underline{\underline{A}}\ ,

(18)

identical to the matrix used in Eq. (
15
).

The pattern we follow below
to calculate the propagator in higher dimensions, in particular Eqs. (
32
) and (
35
),
corresponds in one dimension to writing
the matrix of propagators Eq. (
17
) in the form

\underline{\underline{G}}+\underline{\underline{G}}\underline{\underline{T}}\underline{\underline{G}}+\ldots=\underline{\underline{G}}+\underline{\underline{G}}\underline{\underline{T}}\underline{\underline{G}}(\mathds{1}-\underline{\underline{T}}\underline{\underline{G}})^{-1}
, which corresponds to
separating propagation without tumbling from any propagation with tumbling.
We therefore calculate explicitly

\underline{\underline{G}}\underline{\underline{T}}\underline{\underline{G}}=\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}G(k,\theta_{1},\omega)G(k,\theta_{1},\omega)&G(k,\theta_{1},\omega)G(k,\theta_{2},\omega)\\
G(k,\theta_{2},\omega)G(k,\theta_{1},\omega)&G(k,\theta_{2},\omega)G(k,\theta_{2},\omega)\end{array}\right)\ .

(19)

and

\underline{\underline{G}}\underline{\underline{T}}\underline{\underline{G}}(\mathds{1}-\underline{\underline{T}}\underline{\underline{G}})^{-1}=\frac{1}{1-\frac{\alpha}{2}(G(k,\theta_{1},\omega)+G(k,\theta_{2},\omega))}\underline{\underline{G}}\underline{\underline{T}}\underline{\underline{G}}\ ,

(20)

so that in fact the full propagators according to Eq. (
17
) are

\mathcal{G}(k,\theta_{i},\theta_{j},\omega)\delta\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22(k+k^{\prime})\delta\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22(\omega+\omega^{\prime}):=\left\langle\phi(k,\theta_{i},\omega)\tilde{\phi}(k^{\prime},\theta_{j},\omega^{\prime})\right\rangle\\
=G(k,\theta_{i},\omega)\delta\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22(k+k^{\prime})\delta\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22(\omega+\omega^{\prime})\Big(\delta_{i,j}+\frac{\alpha}{2}G(k,\theta_{j},\omega)\frac{1}{1-\mathcal{T}_{1}(k,\omega)}\Big)\ ,

(21)

with

\mathcal{T}_{1}(k,\omega):=\frac{\alpha}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{2}G(k,\theta_{\ell},\omega)\ .

(22)

This concludes the derivation of the full propagator of RnT particles in one dimension.

In the following, we calculate first the mean squared displacement of RnT particles in one and two dimensions, followed by their entropy production in various settings.

3.1
Mean squared displacement

The MSD is most conveniently calculated on the basis of Eq. (
4
) and the general expression for the propagator integrated over the director, Eq. (
43
). Taking the Laplacian inside the integral requires the Laplacian of
\mathcal{G}_{d}
, which seems rather difficult to generalise, given that a general expression for
\mathcal{T}_{d}
is not available. However, derivatives and integration commute for
\mathcal{T}_{d}
, Eq. (
41
), at this stage restricting the calculation to
d\geq 2
. As
\mathcal{T}_{d}(\mathbf{k},\omega)
is even in
\mathbf{k}
, the MSD has the simple form

\overline{\mathbf{x}^{2}}(t)=-\int\mathchoice{\!\mathrm{d}\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22\omega\,}{\!\mathrm{d}\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22\omega\,}{\!\mathrm{d}\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22\omega\,}{\!\mathrm{d}\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22\omega\,}\mathchoice{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathring{\imath}\omega t}}{\operatorname{exp}\left(-\mathring{\imath}\omega t\right)}{\operatorname{exp}\left(-\mathring{\imath}\omega t\right)}{\operatorname{exp}\left(-\mathring{\imath}\omega t\right)}\left(\frac{\left.\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\right|_{\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{0}}G_{d}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{v}^{\prime},\omega)}{1-\mathcal{T}_{d}(0,\omega)}+\frac{G_{d}(0,\mathbf{v}^{\prime},\omega)\left.\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\right|_{\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{0}}\mathcal{T}_{d}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{v}^{\prime})}{(1-\mathcal{T}_{d}(0,\omega))^{2}}\right)

(44)

Both derivatives are easy to determine, as Eq. (
39
) gives

\left.\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\right|_{\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{0}}G_{d}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{v}^{\prime},\omega)=-\frac{2Dd}{(-\mathring{\imath}\omega+\alpha+r)^{2}}-\frac{2v^{2}}{(-\mathring{\imath}\omega+\alpha+r)^{3}}

(45)

and by taking this very same derivative in the first integral of Eq. (
41
),

\left.\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\right|_{\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{0}}\mathcal{T}_{d}(\mathbf{k},\omega)=\alpha\left.\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}^{2}\right|_{\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{0}}G_{d}(\mathbf{k},\mathbf{v}^{\prime},\omega)\ ,

(46)

as the integrand becomes independent of
\mathbf{v}
at
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{0}
. Similarly
\mathcal{T}_{d}(0,\omega)=\alpha G_{d}(0,\mathbf{v},\omega)=\alpha(-\mathring{\imath}\omega+\alpha+r)^{-1}
. In particular

\frac{1}{1-\mathcal{T}_{d}(0,\omega)}=\frac{G_{d}^{-1}(0,\mathbf{v},\omega)}{-\mathring{\imath}\omega+r}

(47)

so that after some algebra for
d\geq 2

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{x}^{2}}(t)

\displaystyle=\int\mathchoice{\!\mathrm{d}\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22\omega\,}{\!\mathrm{d}\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22\omega\,}{\!\mathrm{d}\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22\omega\,}{\!\mathrm{d}\mkern-6.0mu\mathchar 22\omega\,}\mathchoice{\mathrm{e}^{-\mathring{\imath}\omega t}}{\operatorname{exp}\left(-\mathring{\imath}\omega t\right)}{\operatorname{exp}\left(-\mathring{\imath}\omega t\right)}{\operatorname{exp}\left(-\mathring{\imath}\omega t\right)}\left(\frac{1}{-\mathring{\imath}\omega+r}+\frac{\alpha}{(-\mathring{\imath}\omega+r)^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{2Dd}{-\mathring{\imath}\omega+\alpha+r}+\frac{2v^{2}}{(-\mathring{\imath}\omega+\alpha+r)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=2Ddt+2\frac{v^{2}}{\alpha^{2}}\left(\mathchoice{\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha t}}{\operatorname{exp}\left(-\alpha t\right)}{\operatorname{exp}\left(-\alpha t\right)}{\operatorname{exp}\left(-\alpha t\right)}-1+\alpha t\right)\ .

(48)

By direct calculation from Eqs. (
22
) and (
24
) this expression turns out to hold also for
d=1
. The MSD is thus identical to that of a diffusive particle at times

with effective diffusion constant
D+v^{2}/(d\alpha)

[
12
]
, which is one of the key results of this work. As the plain diffusion with constant
D
is modelled to equally take place in all spatial directions, the diffusion constant enters into the MSD linearly in the dimensionality of space

. The ballistic MSD
v^{2}t^{2}
is indeed visible as
\mathchoice{\mathrm{e}^{-\alpha t}}{\operatorname{exp}\left(-\alpha t\right)}{\operatorname{exp}\left(-\alpha t\right)}{\operatorname{exp}\left(-\alpha t\right)}-1+\alpha t=(\alpha t)^{2}/2+\mathcal{O}(t^{3})
at short times, provided
Dd
is small enough, as diffusion otherwise dominates at the short timescale. That
D
can be chosen to vanish is consistent with the perturbation theory, which does not rely on non-vanishing
D

The internal entropy production rate for RnT particles in

dimensions can be calculated via
[

,

,

\times P(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},t)\mathring{W}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}\rightarrow\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v}^{\prime};\tau)\ln\left(\frac{P(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v},t)W(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}\rightarrow\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v}^{\prime};\tau)}{P(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v}^{\prime},t)W(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v};\tau)}\right)\ ,

where

\mathbf{x}

\mathring{S}(t)=v^{2}/D

[

4
Active Brownian Particles

The active Brownian particle (ABP) model
[
12
,
17
,
18
,

]
describes the motion of a particle in

dimensions that moves with constant speed
|\mathbf{v}|=v
but whose director undergoes diffusion with constant
D_{r}
on a
d

D
. What makes the ABP model different from RnT is the continuous evolution of the director. In one dimension this is no longer possible and the ABP model may be thought of degenerating into RnT.

In particular, an ABP in two dimensions moves with velocity
\mathbf{v}_{\theta}=v[\cos\theta,\sin\theta]^{T}
, parameterised by an angle
\theta
that undergoes rotational diffusion with
D_{r}
.
In the following, we will derive the action, the propagator and the MSD of ABP in two dimensions, via the field theory, demonstrating the equivalence between RnT and ABP as far as the MSD is concerned, both on the large and the small time scale.

The Fokker-Planck equation of ABP reads
[
17
]

\partial_{t}P(\mathbf{x},\theta,t)=\bigg[D\nabla^{2}-\mathbf{v}_{\theta}\nabla+D_{r}\partial^{2}_{\theta}\bigg]P(\mathbf{x},\theta,t)\ ,

[

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\bigg([\phi(\mathbf{k},\theta,\omega),\tilde{\phi}(\mathbf{k},\theta,\omega)]\bigg)

(65)

5
Discussion and Conclusion

In this work, the Doi-Peliti framework
[
4
,
5
]
has been used to describe Run and Tumble (RnT) particles in arbitrary dimensions.
We have determined the action for RnT particles in one dimension, Eqs. (
11a
), (
15
), two Eq. (
26
) and higher dimensions Eq. (
38
).
The full propagators of an RnT particle in one Eq. (
21
), two Eq. (
35
) and arbitrarily higher dimensions Eq. (
42
) have been derived, and the MSD with averaged final velocity for arbitrary dimensions Eq. (
48
) has been determined. The MSD implies that the behavior of a free RnT particle is equivalent to a diffusive particle on large time scales, with an effective diffusion coefficient
D_{e}=D+v^{2}/(d\alpha)
. On short time scales, however, the ballistic motion
v^{2}t^{2}
is visible, although competing with the diffusive contribution
2Ddt
.

It is this competition that drives the rich phenomenology of RnT particles in a harmonic potential with spring-constant
k

[
8
]
, as the deviation from the equilibrium behaviour vanishes with
v/(Dk)
.

For comparison, we have also determined the action Eq. (
65
) and subsequently the propagator for a two dimensional Active Brownian Particle (ABP) Eq. (
79
).
This required the use of Mathieu functions, which would probably need to be replaced by the spherical harmonics in higher dimensions, where the diffusion on the director becomes considerably more complicated, given the curved space. A perturbative approach might then become more feasible, possibly over RnT motion. The MSD of ABPs Eq. (
84
) is identical to that of RnT particles on all time scale if one identifies the tumble rate with the diffusion constant of the director of the ABPs.

In Section
3.2
we have calculated the stationary internal entropy production rate
\dot{S}=v^{2}/D
, Eq. (
61
), which is valid in all dimensions. In the derivation it becomes clear that one dimension has some idiosynchracies, but the entropy production in all dimensions is essentially the velocity
\mathbf{v}
times the Stokian viscous force
\mathbf{v}k_{B}T/D
acting on the particle, at temperature
T
and with Boltzmann constant
k_{B}
. This is the power output of a particle that self-propels with velocity
\mathbf{v}
.

The present work provides the basis to derive characteristics of interacting RnT particles and those subject to external potentials on the basis of field theories. Using this framework provides a flexible, extensible, systmatic and perturbative approach to active matter. It comes equipped with the powerful tool of the renormalisation group and a diagrammatic that has an immediate physical interpretation, as we have attempted to illustrate above. We believe that field theory, which has been so overwhelmingly successful in the characterisation of collective phenomena
[
22
]
, is ideally suited for the challenges that lie ahead in the research of active matter.
