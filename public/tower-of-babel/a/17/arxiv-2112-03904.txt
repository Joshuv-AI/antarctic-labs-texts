Hypergraph Co-Optimal Transport: Metric and Categorical Properties
Samir Chowdhury, Tom Needham, Ethan Semrad, Bei Wang, Youjia Zhou

Abstract
Hypergraphs capture multi-way relationships in data, and they have consequently seen a number of applications in higher-order network analysis, computer vision, geometry processing, and machine learning. In this paper, we develop theoretical foundations for studying the space of hypergraphs using ingredients from optimal transport. By enriching a hypergraph with probability measures on its nodes and hyperedges, as well as relational information capturing local and global structures, we obtain a general and robust framework for studying the collection of all hypergraphs. First, we introduce a hypergraph distance based on the co-optimal transport framework of Redko et al. and study its theoretical properties. Second, we formalize common methods for transforming a hypergraph into a graph as maps between the space of hypergraphs and the space of graphs, and study their functorial properties and Lipschitz bounds. Finally, we demonstrate the versatility of our Hypergraph Co-Optimal Transport (HyperCOT) framework through various examples.

Introduction

The study of hypergraphs is motivated by higher-order network analysis.
Graphs are commonly used to encode data from complex systems in cybersecurity, biology, sociology, and physical infrastructure, where nodes represent entities and edges represent
pairwise
relations between entities.
However, real-world data contain abundant
multi-way
relationships. For example, Patania
et al.

Patania
et al. 2017
studied coauthorship of scientific papers from arXiv, where a paper represents a multi-way relationship between several authors; Cencetti
et al.

Cencetti
et al. 2021
found that higher-order interactions are ubiquitous in human face-to-face communications characterized by heterogeneous dynamics.

These multi-way relationships are better captured by hypergraphs.
A
hypergraph
consists of a set of
nodes
and a set of subsets of the node set, whose elements are called
hyperedges
.
A hypergraph generalizes the notion of a graph, which is simply a hypergraph whose hyperedges each contain exactly two nodes. For example, a hypergraph can be used to represent molecular structure, where nodes are individual atoms, hyperedges represent either covalent bonds between pairs of atoms or polycentric bonds between multiple atoms (see
(
Bretto 2013
, Chapter 7.1)
and
Konstantinova and
Skorobogatov 2001
).
Hypergraphs can also model cellular networks
Klamt et al. 2009
, where protein complexes are hyperedges consisting of multiple proteins.
Lotito
et al.
demonstrated the informative power of higher-order motifs among a number of hypergraph datasets
Lotito
et al. 2022
in sociology, biology, and technology.

Hypergraphs also arise from applications in computer vision, geometry processing, pattern recognition, and machine learning, where establishing correspondences between two feature sets is a fundamental issue.
The correspondence problem is traditionally formulated as a
graph matching
problem
Umeyama 1988
;
Gold and
Rangarajan 1996
: each graph is constructed with nodes representing features and edges representing
pairwise
relationships between features.
For example, in geometry processing, absolute locations of features are not as relevant as their pairwise relations, e.g., when imposing invariance over translations and rotations.
However, pairwise relations are not sufficient to incorporate the higher-order relations among features in establishing correspondences – this can be addressed by a
hypergraph matching
problem
Chertok and Keller 2010
;
Lee et al. 2011
.

Recent years have seen the extension of the Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) framework – originally developed as a tool for comparing metric measure spaces
Mémoli 2007
;
Mémoli 2011a
– to probabilistic graph matching tasks
Solomon et al. 2016
;
Hendrikson 2016
;
Xu et al. 2019b
;
Xu et al. 2019a
;
Chowdhury and
Mémoli 2019
;
Vayer et al. 2019
;
Chowdhury
et al. 2021
. Details on this framework are provided in
Section 2.1
, but the rough idea is as follows. Optimal transport metrics are typically used to compare probability distributions on a common metric space, and are widely perceived as a powerful framework for quantifying uncertainty within geometric measurements
Peyré
et al. 2019
;
Solomon 2018
. GW distances, in contrast, are used to obtain correspondences across different spaces, which in turn permits comparison between spaces that are not comparable a priori
Mémoli 2007
;
Mémoli 2011a
. GW distances are based on finding probabilistic alignments between points in two metric spaces which minimize an overall metric distortion objective. The numerous benefits of this approach include computability via gradient descent
Peyré et al. 2016
;
Flamary and Courty 2017
or backpropagation
Xu 2020
, state-of-the-art performance in tasks such as graph partitioning
Xu et al. 2019a
;
Chowdhury and
Needham 2021
, and an underlying Riemannian theoretical framework
Sturm 2012
;
Chowdhury and
Needham 2020
.
These successes motivate the development of a GW framework for hypergraphs, which is the goal of this paper. Our contributions include:

•

We extend the co-optimal transport framework of Redko
et al.

Redko et al. 2020
to define an optimal transport-based distance between hypergraphs. To put this on firm theoretical footing, we introduce a generalized notion of a hypergraph, called a
measure hypernetwork
, see
Section 2.2
. We then establish fundamental properties of our hypergraph distance; in particular, we show that it is a pseudometric on the space of measure hypernetworks which induces a complete, geodesic metric on the space of measure networks modulo the distance-0 equivalence relation (
Theorem 1
).

•

We introduce categorical structures on the space of measure (hyper)networks. Through this lens, we study common transformations taking hypergraphs to graphs (e.g., incidence graphs, clique expansions, and line graphs) as functors from the category of hypernetworks to the category of networks. We also study their Lipschitz properties with respect to our hypernetwork distance and the GW distance on the space of networks. We show that the incidence graph map is a functorial isometry of our distance to a novel variant of the GW distance (
Theorem 10
). We then introduce 1-parameter families of clique expansion and line graph maps and prove that they are Lipschitz functors (
Theorem 11
).

•

We obtain some structural results on our categories of measure (hyper)networks. Proposition
17
shows the equivalence of certain metric notions of isomorphism from the literature and isomorphisms in the category-theoretic sense. The (non)existence of various limit constructions in our categories is established in Propositions
21
and
22
.

1.1
Related Work

Hypergraph metrics, similarity and dissimilarity measures.

Although there is an abundant amount of data to be modeled as hypergraphs, metrics between hypergraphs have not been explored extensively in the literature.
Karonski and Palka
Karoński and
Palka 1977
considered hypergraphs defined over the same set of nodes as clusterings and defined the Marczewski–Steinhaus (MS) distance between the clusterings.
The MS distance is a modification from a generalization of the Hausdorff metric for sets
Ulam 1972
.
Karonski and Palka also discussed a distance between hypergraphs generated by arborescences with the same set of terminal vertices.
In comparison with the MS distance, our hypergraph distance does not require hypergraphs to be defined on the same set of nodes; and more importantly, our distance comes with nice metric properties, encodes explicitly structural relations between nodes and hyperedges, and naturally gives rise to meaningful hypergraph matching. Lee
et al.

Lee et al. 2011
generalized the edit distance from graphs to hypergraphs.
However, such a distance is impractical as the graph edit distance is NP-hard to compute
Zeng et al. 2009
and APX-hard to approximate
Lin 1994
.
Smaniotto and Pelillo
Smaniotto and
Pelillo 2021
recently defined distances between attributed hypergraphs using their maximal common subhypergraph; however, computing the maximum similarity subhypergraph isomorphism between the two hypergraphs is NP-complete.
In comparison, our hypergraph distance may be approximated efficiently by following the optimization routine of co-optimal transport. Another approach to hypergraph comparison is taken in
Surana et al. 2021
, which transforms a hypergraph into a graph and applies standard graph similarity or dissimilarity measures.
Hypergraphs can also be encoded as tensors
Banerjee
et al. 2017
, so that spectral comparisons between hypergraphs can be made through their tensor representations.
The work in
Duchenne et al. 2011
describes a principled framework for hypergraph matching and proposed to use this framework for going beyond isometry-invariant shape matching and obtaining invariance under similarity, affine, and projective transformations. However, the framework in
Duchenne et al. 2011
constructs hypergraphs with a fixed size for each hyperedge, and is not suited for cases where the input data is an arbitrary hypergraph. In contrast, our hypergraph distance can accept any pair of arbitrary hypergraphs as input.

Optimal transport and Gromov-Wasserstein distances.

Gromov-Wasserstein (GW) distances, as described above, essentially require only square matrices encoding pairwise relational information in the spaces to be compared
Chowdhury and
Mémoli 2019
, and are thus well-suited for comparing graphs via adjacency, shortest path distance, or spectral representations
Xu et al. 2019b
;
Vayer et al. 2019
;
Chowdhury and
Needham 2021
. However, hypergraphs typically encode more than pairwise relations; our strategy for extending the GW framework to hypergraphs is to work directly with multi-way relations (modeled as rectangular matrices encoding node-hyperedge relations in finite settings) and to leverage the recently-developed co-optimal transport framework of
Redko et al. 2020
. Using this framework, we are able to simultaneously infer node-node and hyperedge-hyperedge correspondences when comparing hypergraphs.

Hypernetworks: Theoretical Formulations

This section introduces the basic concepts of measured hypernetworks and distances between them.

2.1
Background: Measure Networks

A
graph
is a pair
(V,E)
, where
V
is a set of
nodes
and
E
is a set of 2-element subsets of
V
, each of which is called an
edge
. The following gives a far-reaching generalization of the notion of a graph.

Definition 1

(
Chowdhury and
Mémoli 2019
)
.

A
measure network
is a triple
N=(X,\mu,\omega)
, where
X
is a Polish space,
\mu
is a Borel probability measure on
X
and
\omega:X\times X\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a measurable, bounded
network function
. We take the simplifying conventions that
\mu
is fully supported and
\omega
takes non-negative values.
Let
\mathcal{N}
denote the collection of all measure networks.

Recall that the support of a Borel probability measure
\mu
on a Polish space
X
is the set
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
of
x\in X
such that
\mu(U)>0
for every open neighborhood of
x
(this is equivalent to
(
Mémoli 2011a
, Definition 2.2)
). Then a measure network is assumed to have
\mathrm{supp}(\mu)=X
.

A graph
(V,E)
with a probability distribution
\mu
on
V
defines a measure network by, for example, taking
\omega
to be a binary adjacency function. However, this definition encompasses a huge variety of other structures; any metric measure space (metric space endowed with a Borel probability measure) is, in particular, a measure network. As was also observed in, e.g.,
Sturm 2012
;
Peyré et al. 2016
, measure networks give a general setting for defining GW distances (which were originally defined only for metric measure spaces
Mémoli 2007
).

In the following, we use tools from measure theory. Given a measurable map
f:X\to Y
from a Borel measure space
(X,\mu)
to a Borel space
Y
, the
pushforward of
\mu
is the measure
f_{\#}\mu
on
Y
defined by
f_{\#}\mu(U)=\mu(f^{-1}(U))
for all Borel sets
U\subset Y
. For a measurable function
f:X\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
p\in[1,\infty)
, the
L^{p}
norm of
f
is

\|f\|_{L^{p}(\mu)}=\left(\int_{X}\lvert f(x)\rvert^{p}\;\mu(dx)\right)^{1/p},

provided this integral converges. For
p=\infty
,
\|\cdot\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}
is the
essential supremum
,

\|f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mu)}=\inf\{c\geq 0\mid\lvert f(x)\rvert\leq c\mbox{ for $\mu$-almost every $x\in X$}\},

provided this is finite. Given two Borel measure spaces
(X,\mu)
and
(Y,\nu)
, the
product measure

\mu\otimes\nu
on
X\times Y
satisfies
\mu\otimes\nu(U\times V)=\mu(U)\cdot\nu(V)
for all Borel
U\subset X
and
V\subset Y
.

Definition 2
.

Given two probability spaces
(X,\mu)
and
(Y,\nu)
, a
coupling

\pi
is a probability measure on
X\times Y
satisfying
(p_{X})_{\#}\pi=\mu
and
(p_{Y})_{\#}\pi=\nu
, where
p_{X}:X\times Y\to X
and
p_{Y}:X\times Y\to Y
are coordinate projections. That is, the marginals of
\pi
are
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively. The collection of couplings between
\mu,\nu
is denoted
\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)
.

Definition 3

(Gromov-Wasserstein Distance)
.

For measure networks
N=(X,\mu,\omega)
and
N^{\prime}=(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})
and
p\in[1,\infty]
, the
p
-distortion
functional is

\mathrm{dis}^{\mathcal{N}}_{p}=\mathrm{dis}^{\mathcal{N}}_{N,N^{\prime},p}:\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\to{\mathbb{R}}

defined by

\mathrm{dis}^{\mathcal{N}}_{p}(\pi)=\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\pi\otimes\pi)},

(1)

where
\omega-\omega^{\prime}
is understood as the function
X\times X^{\prime}\times X\times X^{\prime}\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by
(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})\mapsto\omega(x,y)-\omega^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.
Equivalently, for
p<\infty
, (
1
) may be expanded as,

\mathrm{dis}^{\mathcal{N}}_{p}(\pi)=\left(\int_{X\times X^{\prime}}\int_{X\times X^{\prime}}\mid\omega(x,y)-\omega^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\mid^{p}\pi(dx\times dx^{\prime})\pi(dy\times dy^{\prime})\right)^{1/p}.

The
Gromov-Wasserstein (GW)
p
-distance
is then defined to be

d_{\mathcal{N},p}(N,N^{\prime}):=\inf_{\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\mathrm{dis}^{\mathcal{N}}_{p}(\pi).

(2)

An
optimal coupling
—i.e. a coupling
\pi
realizing (
2
)—always exists
(
Chowdhury and
Mémoli 2019
, Theorem 2.2)
. Intuitively, an optimal coupling provides a
soft matching
between the points in the measure networks which minimally distorts their network functions.

GW
p
-distance is known to define a pseudometric on
\mathcal{N}

(
Chowdhury and
Mémoli 2019
, Theorem 2.3)
and the distance-0 equivalence classes are well-understood. We define measure networks
N,N^{\prime}
to be
weakly isomorphic
if
d_{\mathcal{N},p}(N,N^{\prime})=0
. It follows from
(
Chowdhury and
Mémoli 2019
, Theorem 2.4)
that
N=(X,\mu,\omega),N^{\prime}=(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})
are weakly isomorphic if and only if there exists a measure network
\overline{N}=(\overline{X},\overline{\mu},\overline{\omega})
and measure-preserving maps
\phi:\overline{X}\to X
and
\phi^{\prime}:\overline{X}\to X^{\prime}
such that
\omega(\phi(x),\phi(y))=\omega^{\prime}(\phi^{\prime}(x),\phi^{\prime}(y))=\overline{\omega}(x,y)
for
\overline{\mu}\otimes\overline{\mu}
-almost every
(x,y)\in\overline{X}\times\overline{X}
.

Remark 1
.

The notion of weak isomorphism is introduced because it is a relaxation of the more obvious notion of a strong isomorphism. Following
Chowdhury and
Mémoli 2019
, a
strong isomorphism
from
N
to
N^{\prime}
is defined to be a bijective measure-preserving map
\phi:X\to X^{\prime}
with measure-preserving inverse such that for all
x,y\in X
,
\omega(x,y)=\omega^{\prime}(\phi(x),\phi(y))
. If a strong isomorphism exists, then
N
and
N^{\prime}
are weakly isomorphic, but the converse to this statement does not hold; see
(
Chowdhury and
Mémoli 2019
, Example 2.1)
.

2.2
Measure Hypernetworks and Hypernetwork Distance

A
hypergraph
is a pair
(X,Y)
, where
X
is a set of
nodes
and
Y
is a collection of subsets of
X
, each of which is called a
hyperedge
.
Figure 1
shows some simple hypergraphs and different hypergraph visualization techniques.

Figure 1:
(a) A hypergraph visualized as a Venn diagram; nodes are black, and hyperedges are colored convex hulls. (b) Replacing each hyperedge in the Venn diagram with its own node representation, we visualize the same hypergraph as an
incidence graph
(see
Section 3
for theoretical properties of this representation). (c) Hybrid Venn diagram and incidence graph visualization. (d)-(e) An example of a
morphism
between hypergraphs—categorical structures on the space of hypergraphs will be studied in detail in
Section 3
.

The notion of a hypergraph can be formalized
Dörfler and
Waller 1980
as a triple
(X,Y,\iota)
, where
X
and
Y
are sets and
\iota:Y\to\mathcal{P}(X)
is a map from
Y
to the power set of
X
, assigning a subset of
X
to each point in
Y
. One could equivalently consider a triple
(X,Y,\omega)
, where
\omega:X\times Y\to\{0,1\}
is an indicator function encoding containment of a point
x\in X
in
y\in Y
by
\omega(x,y)=1
. Inspired by the definition of a measure network given above, we now give a generalization of the notion of a hypergraph, which introduces probability measures on
X
and
Y
and allows for more general functions
\omega
.

Definition 4
.

A
measure hypernetwork
is a quintuple

H=(X,\mu,Y,\nu,\omega)
,
where
(X,\mu)
and
(Y,\nu)
are Polish spaces endowed with Borel probability measures and

\omega:X\times Y\to{\mathbb{R}}

is a measurable, bounded
hypernetwork function
. We take the simplifying conventions that
\mu
and
\nu
are fully supported and that
\omega
takes non-negative values. Let
\mathcal{H}
denote the collection of all hypernetworks.

Example 1

(Hypergraphs)
.

Our motivating examples of hypernetworks arise from classical hypergraphs. To emphasize the distinction between the classical setting and our generalized measure hypernetworks, we refer to a pair
(X,Y)
where
X
is a finite set of nodes and
Y\subset{\mathcal{P}}(X)
is a set of hyperedges as a
combinatorial hypergraph
.

A combinatorial hypergraph
(X,Y)
gives rise to a number of representations as a measure hypernetwork. For example, we may endow
X
and
Y
with uniform measures
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively; i.e.,
\mu(x)={1}/{\lvert X\rvert}
for any
x\in X
and
\mu(y)=1/{\lvert Y\rvert}
for any
y\in Y
.
We may define a hypernetwork function
\omega:X\times Y\to{\mathbb{R}}
by the
incidence relation
:

\omega(x,y):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1&\mbox{if $x\in y$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Alternatively,
\omega
may be defined by a
shortest path relation
: (a) if
x\in y
, then
\omega(x,y)=0
; (b) if
x\notin y
, then
\omega(x,y)
is the length of the shortest
hyperedge path
—a sequence of hyperedges with nontrivial sequential pairwise overlap, with length equal to the sum of the size of the overlaps—from any hyperedge
y^{\prime}
containing
x
to hyperedge
y
. This method for transforming a hypergraph into a measure hypernetwork assumes that the hypergraph is
connected
: any two hyperedges are joined by a hyperedge path. For the example hypergraph shown in
Figure 1
c, we have

X=\{1,2,3,4,5\}
,
Y=\{a,b,c,d\}
,
\mu(x)=1/5
for each
x\in X
, and
\nu(y)=1/4
for each
y\in Y
.
If
\omega
encodes the incidence relation or the shortest path relation, we have, respectively,

\omega=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&0&0\\
1&0&1&0\\
0&1&0&1\\
0&1&1&1\\
0&0&1&0\\
\end{bmatrix},\text{ or }\omega=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&1&2\\
0&1&0&1\\
1&0&1&0\\
1&0&0&0\\
1&1&0&1\\
\end{bmatrix},

where the matrices encode the function
\omega
by indexing rows by
X
and columns by
Y
.

Remark 2
.

The constructions in Example
1
involve several choices: a measure on the nodes, a measure on the hyperedges and a hypernetwork function. There are many more ways to model a hypergraph as a measure hypernetwork; some are described in detail in
Section 5
. For instance, one may encode the degree information of a hypergraph in constructing its corresponding hypernetwork. In general, we consider the design of a measure hypernetwork representation of a hypergraph as treating the assignment of node and hyperedge weights
\mu
and
\nu
, respectively, and hypernetwork function
\omega
independently. Intuitively,
\mu
and
\nu
are treated as measuring the “importance” of each node and hyperedge in the model (e.g., uniform weights mean equal importance in the analysis), while
\omega
encodes interactions between nodes and hyperedges in a measure-independent manner.

Example 2

(Data Matrices)
.

The main motivation for introducing the co-optimal transport framework in
Redko et al. 2020
was to compare
data matrices
; i.e. rectangular matrices used to store vector-valued datasets. Such an object is represented in our formalism by taking
X
to be a set of samples,
Y
to be a set of features (in the machine learning sense),
\mu
and
\nu
to be some data-dependent distributions and defining
\omega(x,y)
to be the value of feature
y
on sample
x
.

Example 3

(Continuous Examples)
.

Hypergraphs and data matrices involve finite sets, whereas we are working in a generality that allows infinite sets. A theoretical motivation for allowing infinite sets is that, as we will see in
Theorem 1
, this is necessary to guarantee completeness for the metric on hypernetworks defined below. More concretely, there are many interesting instances of infinite measure hypernetworks. For example, a hypernetwork
H=(X,\mu,Y,\nu,\omega)
can encode a
machine learning problem
, where
X
is a
data space
(say,
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
),
\mu
is a distribution of data in
X
,
Y
is a
parameter space
(say,
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
),
\nu
is a distribution representing constraints on the parameters, and
\omega:X\times Y\to{\mathbb{R}}
is considered as a parametric family of machine learning models; model training involves considering
\mu
as a distribution of training data and optimizing an objective function of
\omega
over parameters
Y
. As another example, suppose that
(X,\mu)
and
(Y,\nu)
are bounded, measured subsets of a common ambient metric space
(Z,d_{Z})
and define
\omega:X\times Y\to Z
by
\omega(x,y)=d_{Z}(x,y)
; this is the sort of setup that arises in a metric construction of Sturm, related to the usual embedding formulation of Gromov-Hausdorff distance
Sturm 2006
. See
Mutlu and Gürdal 2016
for other examples along these lines.

We are now prepared to define our distance between measure hypernetworks, which is a direct extension of the co-optimal transport framework of Redko
et al.
introduced in
Redko et al. 2020
.

Definition 5
.

Let
H=(X,\mu,Y,\nu,\omega),H^{\prime}=(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime},Y^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})\in\mathcal{H}
. For
p\in[1,\infty]
, the
p
-th co-optimal transport distortion
is the functional

\mathrm{dis}_{p}^{\mathcal{H}}=\mathrm{dis}_{H,H^{\prime},p}^{\mathcal{H}}:\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\times\mathcal{C}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\to{\mathbb{R}}

defined by

\mathrm{dis}_{p}^{\mathcal{H}}(\pi,\xi)=\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\pi\otimes\xi)},

(3)

where
\omega-\omega^{\prime}
is understood as the function
X\times X^{\prime}\times Y\times Y^{\prime}\to{\mathbb{R}}
taking
(x,x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})
to
\omega(x,y)-\omega^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
. For
p<\infty
, (
3
) reads

\mathrm{dis}_{p}^{\mathcal{H}}(\pi,\xi)=\left(\int_{Y\times Y^{\prime}}\int_{X\times X^{\prime}}\lvert\omega(x,y)-\omega^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\rvert^{p}\pi(dx\times dx^{\prime})\xi(dy\times dy^{\prime})\right)^{1/p}.

The
hypernetwork
p
-distance
is then defined to be

d_{\mathcal{H},p}(H,H^{\prime})=\inf_{\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})}\inf_{\xi\in\mathcal{C}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})}\mathrm{dis}_{p}^{\mathcal{H}}(\pi,\xi).

(4)

Similar to the GW setting, an important feature of this metric is that its practical computation involves determining optimal couplings
\pi
and
\xi
of both
X,X^{\prime}
and
Y,Y^{\prime}
, respectively. In the hypergraph setting, these couplings correspond to soft matchings of both node sets
and
hyperedge sets. Computational examples are provided in
Section 5.2
.

We emphasize that
d_{\mathcal{H},p}
is a straightforward extension of the co-optimal transport distance defined in
Redko et al. 2020
; there the authors restricted their attention to measure hypernetworks whose underlying sets are finite, specifically focusing on data matrices. Our extension of
Redko et al. 2020
encompasses the infinite examples described above, and the generalization to infinite spaces is necessary in order to get a complete metric space—see
Theorem 1
below—but practical computations will always reduce back to the finite setting.

2.3
Properties of Hypernetwork Distance

We now describe some basic theoretical properties of the hypernetwork distance
d_{\mathcal{H},p}
. Our first result shows that
d_{\mathcal{H},p}
is a peudometric on the space of measure hypernetworks. We say that
H
and
H^{\prime}
are
weakly isomorphic
if
d_{\mathcal{H},p}(H,H^{\prime})=0
—a more geometric characterization of weak isomorphism, along the lines of the characterization for
d_{\mathcal{N},p}
in
Section 2.1
, is given below in Proposition
2
.

Theorem 1
.

The hypernetwork
p
-distance
d_{\mathcal{H},p}
is a pseudometric on
\mathcal{H}
. The induced metric on the space of hypernetworks considered up to weak isomorphism is complete and geodesic.

The proof of
Theorem 1
adapts techniques of
Chowdhury and
Mémoli 2019
and
Sturm 2012
in the GW setting. The main idea is that GW distance involves computing an integral over a product of a coupling with itself
\pi\otimes\pi
; the proofs can be adapted to handle the co-optimal transport distance, which integrates over a product of distinct couplings
\pi\otimes\xi
. For completeness, we have included the details of the proof in the Appendix
A.1
. In particular, we construct an explicit geodesic between the equivalence classes of
H
and
H^{\prime}
of the form

\gamma(t)=\left(\mathrm{supp}(\pi),\pi,\mathrm{supp}(\xi),\xi,(1-t)\omega+t\omega^{\prime}\right),

where
\pi
and
\xi
are optimal couplings of the hypernetworks.

Next, we give a characterization of weak isomorphism in the measure hypernetwork setting.

Definition 6
.

A
basic weak isomorphism
of measure hypernetworks
H
and
H^{\prime}
is a pair of measure-preserving maps
\phi:X\to X^{\prime}
and
\psi:Y\to Y^{\prime}
such that
\omega(x,y)=\omega^{\prime}(\phi(x),\psi(y))
for
\mu\otimes\nu
-almost every
(x,y)\in X\times Y
.

Similar to the measure network setting, we define a
strong isomorphism
between measure hypernetworks to be a basic weak isomorphism
(\phi,\psi)
such that equality of hypernetwork functions holds for all points
(x,y)\in X\times Y
and both maps are bijective with measure-preserving inverses.

Proposition 2
.

A pair of measure hypernetworks
H=(X,\mu,Y,\nu,\omega)
and
H^{\prime}=(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime},Y^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})
are weakly isomorphic if and only if there exists a measure hypernetwork
\overline{H}=(\overline{X},\overline{\mu},\overline{Y},\overline{\nu},\overline{\omega})
and basic weak isomorphisms
(\phi,\psi)
from
\overline{H}
to
H
and
(\phi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime})
from
\overline{H}
to
H^{\prime}
.

The proof is similar to the one in the GW setting, but it involves some useful techniques, so we will include it here.

Proof.

For the forward direction, if
H
and
H^{\prime}
are weakly isomorphic with coupling
(\pi,\xi)\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\times\mathcal{C}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})
realizing
d_{\mathcal{H},p}(H,H^{\prime})=0
(such a pair always exists; see Lemma
24
in the Appendix), for
\overline{H}
define the following:
\overline{X}:=\mathrm{supp}(\pi)
,
\overline{\mu}:=\pi
,
\overline{Y}:=\mathrm{supp}(\xi)
and
\overline{\nu}:=\xi
. Let
\phi:\overline{X}\rightarrow X
,
\phi^{\prime}:\overline{X}\rightarrow X^{\prime}
,
\psi:\overline{Y}\rightarrow Y
, and
\psi^{\prime}:\overline{Y}\rightarrow Y^{\prime}
be coordinate projection maps. Each of these maps is measure-preserving by the marginal conditions on
\pi
and
\xi
. We define
\overline{\omega}
as the pullback function of the projection map; i.e.,
\overline{\omega}((x,x^{\prime}),(y,y^{\prime}))=\omega(\phi(x),\psi(y))
. Then
d_{\mathcal{H},p}(H,H^{\prime})=0
being realized by
\pi
and
\xi
implies that
\overline{\omega}(\overline{x},\overline{y})=\omega(\phi(\overline{x}),\psi(\overline{y}))=\omega^{\prime}(\phi^{\prime}(\overline{x}),\psi^{\prime}(\overline{y}))
for almost every
(\overline{x},\overline{y})\in\overline{X}\times\overline{Y}
.

For the reverse direction, we define couplings
\pi:=(\phi\times\phi^{\prime})_{\#}\overline{\mu}
and
\xi:=(\psi\times\psi^{\prime})_{\#}\overline{\nu}
, which satisfy

\displaystyle\mathrm{dis}_{p}^{\mathcal{H}}(\pi,\xi)

\displaystyle=\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\pi\otimes\xi)}

\displaystyle=\|\omega\circ(\phi\times\psi)-\omega^{\prime}\circ(\phi^{\prime}\times\psi^{\prime})\|_{L^{p}(\overline{\mu}\otimes\overline{\nu})}

\displaystyle\leq\|\omega-\overline{\omega}\|_{L^{p}(\overline{\mu}\otimes\overline{\nu})}+\|\overline{\omega}-\omega^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\overline{\mu}\otimes\overline{\nu})}=0,

where the second line follows by the change-of-variables formula.
∎

We point out in the following example that the notion of basic weak isomorphism provides a formalism for describing a procedure used in hypergraph simplification, referred to as node and hyperedge collapses
Zhou et al. 2022
.

Example 4

(Node and Hyperedge Collapses as Basic Weak Isomorphisms)
.

In

and

As illustrated in

This operation can be defined in generality for finite hypernetworks.

Let

be a finite (i.e.

,

3
Graphification

A common technique in hypergraph analysis and machine learning is to transform a hypergraph into a traditional graph, which has a more tractable structure (e.g.,
Agarwal et al. 2005
;
Pu and Faltings 2012
;
Surana et al. 2021
;
Xia et al. 2021
).
In this section, we formalize
graphification
as the study of functors from the category of measure hypernetworks to the category of measure networks.

3.1
Transformations of Hypergraphs to Graphs

Let
H=(X,Y)
be a (combinatorial) hypergraph with nodes
X=\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}
and hyperedges
Y=\{y_{1},\cdots,y_{m}\}
, where each
y_{i}\subseteq X
.
There are several transformations from a hypergraph to a graph: we focus on the
bipartite incidence graph
(also called the
star expansion
)
Sun et al. 2008
, the
line graph

Bermond
et al. 1977
, and the
clique expansion

Zien et al. 1999
, shown in
Figure 3
.

Figure 3:
A hypergraph
H
(a) with its bipartite incidence graph
\mathsf{B}(H)
(b), line graph
\mathsf{L}(H)
(c), dual hypergraph
H_{*}
(d), and clique expansion
\mathsf{Q}(H)
(e).

A hypergraph
H
may be represented by a bipartite incidence graph
\mathsf{B}(H)
as follows:
X\cup Y
forms a partition of the node set of
\mathsf{B}(H)
, and
x\in X
and
y\in Y
are connected by an edge if and only if node
x
is contained in hyperedge
y
in
H
.
The line graph
\mathsf{L}(H)
of
H
is a graph whose node set corresponds to the set of hyperedges of
H
; two nodes are adjacent in
\mathsf{L}(H)
if their corresponding hyperedges have a nonempty intersection in
H
.
The clique expansion
\mathsf{Q}(H)
of
H
consists of node set
X
, and there is an edge
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}
in
\mathsf{Q}(H)
if there exists some hyperedge
y\in Y
such that
x_{i},x_{j}\in y
.
That is,
\mathsf{Q}(H)
is a graph constructed from
H
by replacing each hyperedge with a clique among its nodes.

The line graph and clique expansion are related through the concept of a
dual hypergraph

H_{*}
, which swaps the roles of nodes and hyperedges by considering the transpose of the incidence matrix of
H
: the clique expansion is the line graph of the dual,
\mathsf{Q}(H)=\mathsf{L}(H_{*})
.

Finally, the edges in
\mathsf{B}(H)
,
\mathsf{L}(H)
, and
\mathsf{Q}(H)
may be weighted based on the similarities or dissimilarities between nodes and hyperedges of
H
. The particular weighting scheme is typically application-dependent or ad hoc—for example, a common practice
Rodri´ guez 2002
is to weight an edge
\{x_{i},x_{j}\}
of
\mathsf{Q}(H)
by the number of hyperedges in which
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
are commonly contained. In the following subsection, we develop principled models for these various transformations in the setting of measure hypernetworks.

In what follows, we take a categorical perspective on transformations from hypernetworks to networks, as described above. A function
\mathsf{F}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{N}
is called a
graphification
. Given a graphification
\mathsf{F}
, the questions that we aim to address are:

•

Is
\mathsf{F}
functorial (with respect to categorical structures defined below)?

•

Is
\mathsf{F}
Lipschitz with respect to
d_{\mathcal{H},p}
and
d_{\mathcal{N},p}
?

Intuitively, functoriality tells us that the graphification process preserves relational information between hypergraphs—i.e., that it is
structure-preserving
. Functoriality of the bipartite incidence, clique expansion and line graph maps for combinatorial hypergraphs was shown by Dörfler and Waller
Dörfler and
Waller 1980
; we aim to extend these results to our more general categories. The Lipschitz condition says that graphification is
stable
in the sense that similar hypernetworks must be sent to similar networks.

Remark 3
.

Our current category-theoretic approach was inspired by a line of work on the categorical aspects of (hierarchical) metric space and network partitioning schemes
Carlsson
et al. 2018
;
Carlsson et al. 2013
;
Carlsson et al. 2021
;
Carlsson and
Mémoli 2010
;
Carlsson and
Mémoli 2013
;
Carlsson and
Mémoli 2008
and more general clustering schemes allowing overlapping clusters
Culbertson
et al. 2016
;
Culbertson
et al. 2018
. A clustering algorithm can be interpreted as a function
\mathcal{N}\to\mathcal{H}
(i.e., a
hypergraphification
), and one could likewise ask about functoriality or Lipschitzness of various hypergraphifications. As an obvious example, consider the map which takes a measure network
(X,\mu,\omega)
to the measure hypernetwork
(X,\mu,X,\mu,\omega)
; with the categorical structures defined in
Section 3.2
, this map is functorial and it is clearly 1-Lipschitz (in fact, it is an isometric embedding when restricted to a certain subcategory of measure networks; see
(
Redko et al. 2020
, Proposition 3)
). To keep things focused, we only consider graphifications in the present paper. We plan to explore theoretical properties of hypergraphifications in future work.

3.2
Categories of Networks

We now proceed with describing categorical structures on our spaces
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{N}
, starting with the network setting.

Definition 7

(Category of Measure Networks with Expansive Maps)
.

Let
N,N^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
be measure networks. A function
\phi:X\to X^{\prime}
between their underlying sets is called an
expansive map
if
\omega(x,y)\leq\omega^{\prime}(\phi(x),\phi(y))
for every
(x,y)\in X\times X
. We will sometimes use the notation
N\xrightarrow{\phi}N^{\prime}
for an expansive map to emphasize its dependence on the network functions. Let
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
denote the category whose objects are elements of
\mathcal{N}
and whose morphisms are expansive maps. Composition of morphisms is function composition and the identity morphism is the identity map.

In the (combinatorial) graph theory literature, a common convention is to consider maps which preserve edge relations
Hell and Nesetril 2004
; that is, a morphism between combinatorial graphs
(V,E)
and
(V^{\prime},E^{\prime})
is a function
\phi:V\to V^{\prime}
such that
\{v,w\}\in E
implies
\{\phi(v),\phi(w)\}\in E^{\prime}
. This property is generalized by expansive maps in the measure hypernetwork setting—indeed, if
N,N^{\prime}
are finite measure network representations of graphs, with
\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}
binary adjacency functions, then the expansive condition says that
\phi
takes edges to edges (if there is an edge joining vertices
x
and
y
in a network, then
\omega(x,y)=1
, which forces
\omega(\phi(x),\phi(y))=1
, hence there is an edge joining
\phi(x)
and
\phi(y)
). Thus, the category of combinatorial graphs embeds into
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
as a full subcategory.

Remark 4
.

One could similarly define a
non-expansive map
from
N
to
N^{\prime}
to be a function
\phi:X\to X^{\prime}
with
\omega(x,y)\geq\omega^{\prime}(\phi(x),\phi(y))
for all
x,y\in X
. Non-expansive maps give the classical categorical structure for metric spaces, going back to Isbell
Isbell 1964
—in the metric space setting, this is the same as specifying that morphisms are 1-Lipschitz. This convention has been adopted in more general settings of networks (e.g.,
Culbertson
et al. 2018
;
Carlsson et al. 2021
). The appropriateness of the choice of either categorical structure depends on context: if one is considering measure networks as generalized metric measure spaces, then it makes more sense to consider the non-expansive (1-Lipschitz) category, whereas if one is considering measure networks as generalizing weighted adjacency structures, then the non-expansive category is more relevant. Since we are primarily interested in graphification functors, which are typically defined in terms of adjacency information, we focus on the category of expansive maps in this paper.

It is natural to consider network morphisms which more strongly encode information about the measures. A first attempt would be to consider expansive maps which are also measure-preserving. This is too restrictive—for finite measure spaces, the set of measure-preserving maps between two spaces is frequently empty. We give the following alternative, which defines a measured version of the Schmidt-Ströhlein categorical structure on graphs
(
Schmidt and
Ströhlein 2012
, Chapter 7)
, which consider morphisms between graphs given by relations between their node sets; we instead use couplings, which can be seen intuitively as weighted relations. The idea of defining morphisms between probability spaces as couplings has appeared previously in the literature, e.g.,
Lawvere 1962
;
Perrone 2021
(see Remark
5
). Our notion of
expansive coupling
defined below is, to our knowledge, novel.

Definition 8
.

Let
N,N^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
be measure networks. An
expansive coupling
is a coupling
\pi
of
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
such that for
\pi\otimes\pi
-almost every pair
(x,x^{\prime}),(y,y^{\prime})\in X\times X^{\prime}
, we have
\omega(x,y)\leq\omega^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.

Expansive couplings can be composed via a gluing operation, which takes some care to describe. We begin with a lemma, which is well-known in the optimal transport literature; see, e.g.,
(
Sturm 2012
, Lemma 1.4)
or
(
Villani 2003
, Lemma 11.8.3)
.

Lemma 3

(Disintegration of Measures and the Gluing Lemma)
.

Let
(X,\mu),(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime}),(X^{\prime\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime})
be Polish spaces endowed with Borel probability measures and let
\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(\mu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime})
.

1.

For each
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
, there exists a probability measure
\pi_{x^{\prime}}
on
X
such that
\pi_{x^{\prime}}(dx)=\pi(dx\times dx^{\prime})
. The family
\{\pi_{x^{\prime}}\}_{x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}}
is called a
disintegration kernel for
\pi
with respect to
X^{\prime}
and the
\pi_{x^{\prime}}
are uniquely determined for
\mu^{\prime}
-almost every
x^{\prime}
. Likewise, there exists a disintegration kernel
\{\pi_{x}\}_{x\in X}
with respect to
X
, consisting of probability measures on
X^{\prime}
with analogous properties.

2.

There exists a unique probability measure
\pi^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi
on
X\times X^{\prime}\times X^{\prime\prime}
such that

(p_{X}\times p_{X^{\prime}})_{\#}(\pi^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi)=\pi\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad(p_{X^{\prime}}\times p_{X^{\prime\prime}})_{\#}(\pi^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi)=\pi^{\prime}.

This is called the
gluing of
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
. The gluing is characterized by the formula

\pi^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi(dx\times dx^{\prime}\times dx^{\prime\prime})=\pi^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}}(dx^{\prime\prime})\pi_{x^{\prime}}(dx)\mu^{\prime}(dx^{\prime}).

Definition 9
.

Let
N,N^{\prime},N^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
and let
\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(\mu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime})
. We define the
composition of
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
to be

\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi:=(p_{X}\times p_{X^{\prime\prime}})_{\#}(\pi^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi).

When working with expansive couplings, technical considerations require us to work with measure networks whose underlying topological spaces are compact. An element
N\in\mathcal{N}
which has this property will be referred to as a
compact measure network
. The need for this restriction is to get a well-defined composition operation for expansive couplings.

Proposition 4
.

Let
N,N^{\prime},N^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
be compact measure networks and let
\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(\mu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime})
be expansive couplings. Then the composition
\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi
is an expansive coupling of
N
and
N^{\prime\prime}
.

The proof will use the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

Let
X,X^{\prime},X^{\prime\prime}
be compact Polish spaces endowed with Borel probability measures
\mu,\mu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime}
and let
\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(\mu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime})
.

1.

If
(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)
, then
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
and
x^{\prime}\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu^{\prime})
.

2.

If
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
, then there exists
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
such that
(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)
.

3.

If
(x,x^{\prime\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi)
then there exists
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
such that
(x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi)
.

Proof.

We first prove Part 1. Suppose
(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)
, so that every open neighborhood of
(x,x^{\prime})
has positive measure with respect to
\pi
. Then for any open neighborhood
U
of
x\in X
, we have that
U\times X^{\prime}
is open in
X\times X^{\prime}
, so that
\mu(U)=\pi(U\times X^{\prime})>0
. It follows that
x\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu)
. Similarly,
x^{\prime}\in\mathrm{supp}(\mu^{\prime})
.

Part 2 follows from
(
Mémoli 2011a
, Lemma 2.2)
and implies Part 3. Indeed, consider the map
X\times X^{\prime}\times X^{\prime\prime}\to X\times X^{\prime\prime}\times X^{\prime}
given by rearranging coordinates. This map pushes the measure
\pi^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi
forward to a measure
\xi\in\mathcal{C}(\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi,\mu^{\prime})
. By Part 2, there is
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
such that
(x,x^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\xi)
. The inverse of the coordinate rearrangement map pushes
\xi
back to
\pi^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi
and it follows that
(x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi)
.∎

Proof of Proposition
4
.

For
(x,x^{\prime\prime}),(y,y^{\prime\prime})
in the support of
\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi
, by Part 3 of Lemma
5
, we have that there exists some
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in N^{\prime}
such that
(x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})
and
(y,y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime})
are in the support of
\pi^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi
. Then by Part 1 of Lemma
5
, we get that
(x,x^{\prime})
and
(y,y^{\prime})
are in the support of
\pi
and
(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})
and
(y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime})
are in the support of
\pi^{\prime}
. As
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
are expansive couplings, we get that

\omega(x,y)\leq\omega^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq\omega^{\prime\prime}(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime}).

So
\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi
is an expansive coupling of
N
and
N^{\prime\prime}
.

Example 5
.

We now observe that the restriction to compact measure networks was not only a matter of convenience, but in fact a necessity to get well-defined compositions.

Consider measure networks
N,N^{\prime},N^{\prime\prime}
defined as follows. Let
N=(X,\mu,\omega)
, where
X=\{\frac{1}{n}\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\}\cup\{0\}
,

\mu(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}c\cdot\frac{1}{n^{2}}&\mbox{if $x=\frac{1}{n}$}\\
0&\mbox{if $x=0$,}\end{array}\right.\quad\mbox{and}\quad\omega(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}1&\mbox{if $x=y=0$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

with
c=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}
chosen to normalize
\mu_{X}
to be a probability measure. Observe that the set
X
is Polish when topologized as a subspace of
\mathbb{R}
and that
\mu
is fully supported. Next, we define
N^{\prime}=(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})
with
X^{\prime}=\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
(
X^{\prime}
is a Polish space, as a subspace of
\mathbb{R}
)
\mu^{\prime}(n)=c\cdot\frac{1}{n^{2}}
(a fully supported measure) and
\omega^{\prime}(m,n)=1
for all
m,n\in X^{\prime}
. Finally, let
N^{\prime\prime}=(X^{\prime\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime})
be defined by setting
X^{\prime\prime}=X
,
\mu^{\prime\prime}=\mu
, and
\omega^{\prime\prime}(x,y)=1-\omega(x,y)
.

We now construct couplings to compose. Let
\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
be defined by

\pi(x,x^{\prime})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}c\cdot\frac{1}{n^{2}}&\mbox{if $x=\frac{1}{n}$ and $x^{\prime}=n$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

and define
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(\mu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime})
by
\pi^{\prime}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})=\pi(x^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime})
. Then

\mathrm{supp}(\pi)=\{(1/n,n)\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\prime})=\{(n,1/n)\mid n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\}.

It follows by construction that for all pairs
(\frac{1}{n},n)
and
(\frac{1}{m},m)
in the support of
\pi
,

\omega\left(1/n,1/m\right)\leq\omega^{\prime}(n,m)
,
so that
\pi
is expansive. Likewise,
\pi^{\prime}
is expansive. However,
(0,0)\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi)
and we have

1=\omega(0,0)>\omega^{\prime\prime}(0,0)=0,

so that the composition
\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi
is not expansive.

The identity morphism for
N=(X,\mu,\omega)
in the context of expansive couplings will be the
diagonal coupling

\mathbbm{1}_{N}:=(\mathrm{id}_{X}\times\mathrm{id}_{X})_{\#}\mu
.
This coupling acts as an identity follows from
(
Perrone 2021
, Proposition 4.6)
; the idea is that the disintegration kernel for
\mathbbm{1}_{N}
at
x\in X
is
(\mathbbm{1}_{N})_{x}(dy)=\delta_{x,y}\cdot\mu(dx)
, with
\delta_{x,y}
the Dirac indicator function for the condition
x=y
, so it follows that
\pi\bullet\mathbbm{1}_{N}=\pi=\mathbbm{1}_{N^{\prime}}\bullet\pi.

Associativity of composition of couplings is also shown in
(
Perrone 2021
, Proposition 4.6)
. With these ingredients, we are now able to define a new category structure for measure networks.

Definition 10

(Category of Measure Networks with Expansive Couplings)
.

The
category of measure networks with expansive couplings

\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
is the category whose objects are
compact
measure networks and whose morphisms are expansive couplings. Morphisms are composed by the gluing operation and the identity morphism of
N
is the diagonal coupling
\mathbbm{1}_{N}
. We sometimes denote an expansive coupling between
N
and
N^{\prime}
as
N\xrightarrow{\pi}N^{\prime}
.

We will see below (
Theorem 11
and Corollary
16
) that this categorical structure allows for a richer variety of graphification functors than the expansive maps category. Moreover, this category allows us to capture the notion of strong isomorphism between measure networks in categorical formalism; see Section
4.1
and Proposition
17
.

Remark 5
.

Similar constructions of categorical structures on probability spaces are explored in the lecture notes of Lawvere
(
Lawvere 1962
)
and more recently by Perrone
(
Perrone 2021
)
. The former describes a category where the objects are arbitrary measurable spaces and the morphisms are Markov kernels. The author also adds a deterministic map which defines a subcategory that utilizes Dirac-delta probability measures. The latter defines a weighted category where the objects are probability measures on a Polish space
X
paired with a measurable (pseudo-quasi-)metric and morphisms are couplings with a weight given by a cost function that mimics the
p
-distortion functional we have in Equation (
1
).

We end this subsection by pinning down the precise categorical relationship between measure-preserving expansive maps and expansive couplings. Let
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{mp}}
denote the category of compact measure networks whose morphisms are expansive measure-preserving maps. Consider the map
\mathsf{P}:\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{mp}}\to\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
which is the identity on objects and which takes a measure-preserving expansive map
N\xrightarrow{\phi}N^{\prime}
to the
induced coupling

(\mathrm{id}_{X}\times\phi)_{\#}\mu
.

Proposition 6
.

The map
\mathsf{P}:\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{mp}}\to\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
is a faithful functor.

Proof.

We first observe that
\mathsf{P}
is well defined in the sense that if
N\xrightarrow{\phi}N^{\prime}
is an expansive measure-preserving map, then
\pi_{\phi}:=(\mathrm{id}_{X}\times\phi)_{\#}\mu
is an expansive coupling. Indeed, the support of
\pi_{\phi}
is
\{(x,\phi(x))\mid x\in X\}
, so expansiveness of
\pi_{\phi}
follows immediately from expansiveness of
\phi
.

We need to show that
\mathsf{P}
respects compositions. Let
N\xrightarrow{\phi}N^{\prime}
and
N^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\phi^{\prime}}N^{\prime\prime}
be expansive measure-preserving maps and let
\pi_{\phi}=\mathsf{P}(\phi)
and
\pi_{\phi^{\prime}}=\mathsf{P}(\phi^{\prime})
. We first observe that the disintegration kernel for
\pi_{\phi}
at
x\in X
is the Dirac measure
\delta_{\phi(x)}(dx^{\prime})
, so that
\pi_{\phi}(dx\times dx^{\prime})=\delta_{\phi(x)}(dx^{\prime})\mu(dx)
. Likewise,
\pi_{\phi^{\prime}}(dx^{\prime}\times dx^{\prime\prime})=\delta_{\phi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}(dx^{\prime\prime})\mu^{\prime}(dx^{\prime})
.

We claim that the gluing of
\pi^{\prime}
and
\pi
is given by the formula

\pi^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi(dx\times dx^{\prime}\times dx^{\prime\prime})=\delta_{\phi(x)}(dx^{\prime})\delta_{\phi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}(dx^{\prime\prime})\mu(dx)

(note that this is not our usual formula because we disintegrated in a different order for the sake of explicitness). This is verified by checking the
X\times X^{\prime}
and
X^{\prime}\times X^{\prime\prime}
marginals and appealing to the uniqueness part of Lemma
3
: respectively, we have,

\displaystyle\int_{X^{\prime\prime}}\delta_{\phi(x)}(dx^{\prime})\delta_{\phi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}(dx^{\prime\prime})\mu(dx)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\phi(x)}(dx^{\prime})\mu(dx)\int_{X^{\prime\prime}}\delta_{\phi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}(dx^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\delta_{\phi(x)}(dx^{\prime})\mu(dx)=\pi_{\phi}(dx\times dx^{\prime})

and

\displaystyle\int_{X}\delta_{\phi(x)}(dx^{\prime})\delta_{\phi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}(dx^{\prime\prime})\mu(dx)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\phi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}(dx^{\prime\prime})\int_{X}\delta_{\phi(x)}(dx^{\prime})\mu(dx)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\phi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}(dx^{\prime\prime})\int_{\{x\in X\mid\phi(x)=x^{\prime}\}}\mu(dx)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\phi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}(dx^{\prime\prime})\mu^{\prime}(dx^{\prime})=\pi_{\phi^{\prime}}(dx^{\prime}\times dx^{\prime\prime}).

We obtain
\pi_{\phi^{\prime}}\bullet\pi_{\phi}
by taking the
X\times X^{\prime\prime}
marginal of
\pi^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi
, which yields

\displaystyle\int_{X^{\prime}}\delta_{\phi(x)}(dx^{\prime})\delta_{\phi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}(dx^{\prime\prime})\mu(dx)

\displaystyle=\mu(dx)\int_{X^{\prime}}\delta_{\phi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})}(dx^{\prime\prime})\delta_{\phi(x)}(dx^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mu(dx)\int_{\{x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}\mid\phi^{\prime}(x^{\prime})=x^{\prime\prime}\}}\delta_{\phi(x)}(dx^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\delta_{\phi^{\prime}\circ\phi(x)}(dx^{\prime\prime})\mu(dx)=\pi_{\phi^{\prime}\circ\phi}(dx\times dx^{\prime\prime}).

Therefore,
\mathsf{P}
respects compositions.

Faithfulness of
\mathsf{P}
follows by checking supports. Suppose that
\phi
and
\psi
are measure-preserving expansive maps from
N
to
N^{\prime}
. Then

\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\phi})=\{(x,\phi(x))\mid x\in X^{\prime}\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathrm{supp}(\pi_{\psi})=\{(x,\psi(x))\mid x\in X^{\prime}\},

so that
\pi_{\phi}=\pi_{\psi}
if and only if
\phi=\psi
.
∎

3.3
Categories of Hypernetworks

In the setting of combinatorial hypergraphs, an obvious generalization of a graph morphism is to consider a morphism from
(X,Y)
to
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
to be a pair of maps
(\phi,\psi)
with
\phi:X\to X^{\prime}
and
\psi:Y\to Y^{\prime}
such that
x\in y
implies
\phi(x)\in\psi(y)
—this is the Dörfler-Waller hypergraph category
Dörfler and
Waller 1980
. This directly generalizes to the measure hypernetwork setting as an
expansive map
. Similar to the measure network setting, we can involve measures in the morphism structure through
expansive couplings
, generalizing the relation-based Schmidt-Ströhlein categorical structure on hypergraphs
(
Schmidt and
Ströhlein 2012
, Section 7.2)
.

Definition 11
.

Let
H,H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
be measure hypernetworks. A pair of functions
(\phi,\psi)
,
\phi:X\to X^{\prime}
and
\psi:Y\to Y^{\prime}
, is called an
expansive map
if
\omega(x,y)\leq\omega^{\prime}(\phi(x),\psi(y))
for all
(x,y)\in X\times Y
. An
expansive coupling
is a pair
(\pi,\xi)
, where
\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
,
\xi\in\mathcal{C}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})
and for
\pi\otimes\xi
-almost every pair
(x,x^{\prime})\in X\times X^{\prime}
and
(y,y^{\prime})\in Y\times Y^{\prime}
,
\omega(x,y)\leq\omega^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
.

We use
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}
to denote the category whose objects are measure hypernetworks and whose morphisms are expansive maps. We use
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{c}}
to denote the category whose objects are
compact measure hypernetworks
(measure hypernetworks whose underlying topological spaces are both compact) and whose morphisms are expansive couplings. Expansive couplings are composed as
(\pi^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})\bullet(\pi,\xi):=(\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi,\xi^{\prime}\bullet\xi)
and the identity morphism on
H
is
\mathbbm{1}_{H}=((\mathrm{id}_{X}\times\mathrm{id}_{X})_{\#}\mu,(\mathrm{id}_{Y}\times\mathrm{id}_{Y})_{\#}\nu)
.

The results of the previous subsection extend straightforwardly to show that the category
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{c}}
is well-defined.

The next few subsections address the main problem above: are the transformations described in
Section 3.1
Lipschitz (with respect to
d_{\mathcal{N}}
and
d_{\mathcal{H}}
) and functorial (with respect to the category structures defined above).

3.4
Dualization Functor

Generalizing the discussion in
Section 3.1
, a measure hypernetwork
H=(X,\mu,Y,\nu,\omega)
has a
dual hypernetwork

H_{\ast}=(Y,\nu,X,\mu,\omega_{\ast})
, where

\omega_{\ast}(y,x):=\omega(x,y).

As a first application of the categorical perspective, we show that the dualization map is a functorial isometry.

Proposition 7
.

The dualization map
H\mapsto H_{\ast}
is a covariant endofunctor for both categories
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{c}}
and is an isometry of the pseudometric space
({\mathcal{H}},d_{{\mathcal{H}},p})
.

Proof.

Given measure hypernetworks
H,H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
and an expansive map
(\phi,\psi)
with
\phi:X\to X^{\prime}
and
\psi:Y\to Y^{\prime}
, define
\phi_{\ast}=\psi
and
\psi_{\ast}=\phi
, we have

\omega_{\ast}(y,x)=\omega(x,y)\leq\omega^{\prime}(\phi(x),\psi(y))=\omega_{\ast}(\psi_{\ast}(y),\phi_{\ast}(x)),

for every
(y,x)
, so that
(\psi_{\ast},\phi_{\ast})
is an expansive map for
H_{\ast}
and
H^{\prime}_{\ast}
. The dualization map respects composition: given also
H^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
and
\phi^{\prime}:X^{\prime}\to X^{\prime\prime}
,
\psi^{\prime}:Y^{\prime}\to Y^{\prime\prime}
,
(\phi^{\prime}\circ\phi)_{\ast}=\psi^{\prime}\circ\psi=\phi^{\prime}_{\ast}\circ\phi_{\ast}
and
(\psi^{\prime}\circ\psi)_{\ast}=\phi^{\prime}\circ\phi=\psi^{\prime}_{\ast}\circ\psi_{\ast}
. The arguments in the category of expansive couplings are similar; given an expansive coupling
(\pi,\xi)
of
H
and
H^{\prime}
,
(\xi,\pi)
is an expansive coupling of
H_{\ast}
and
H_{\ast}^{\prime}
, and compositions are respected.

The isometry claim follows from the observation that, for any couplings
\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and
\xi\in\mathcal{C}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})
for
H
and
H^{\prime}
, we have

\mathrm{dis}_{H,H^{\prime},p}(\pi,\xi)=\mathrm{dis}_{H_{\ast},H_{\ast}^{\prime},p}(\xi,\pi).

∎

3.5
The Bipartite Incidence Map

For the rest of this section, we consider graphifications
\mathsf{F}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{N}
inspired by the transformations described in
Section 3.1
.

As a first example of a graphification, we define a map
\mathsf{B}:{\mathcal{H}}\to\mathcal{N}
by
\mathsf{B}(X,\mu,Y,\nu,\omega)=(Z,\mu_{\mathsf{B}},\omega_{\mathsf{B}})
, where
Z=X\cup Y
is endowed with the disjoint union topology,

\omega_{\mathsf{B}}(w,z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\omega(w,z)&\mbox{if $w\in X$ and $z\in Y$}\\
\omega_{\ast}(w,z)&\mbox{if $z\in X$ and $w\in Y$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

and
\mu_{\mathsf{B}}
is defined as

\mu_{\mathsf{B}}=\frac{1}{2}((i_{X})_{\#}\mu+(i_{Y})_{\#}\nu),

(5)

where
i_{X}:X\to Z
and
i_{Y}:Y\to Z
are inclusions.

The map
\mathsf{B}
generalizes the transformation from a hypergraph to its bipartite incidence (or star expansion) graph (
Section 3.1
). As such, we refer to
\mathsf{B}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{N}
as the
bipartite incidence map
. In the statement below, we abuse notation and write
\mathsf{B}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}\to\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
and
\mathsf{B}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{c}}\to\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
for functors defined on objects by
\mathsf{B}
(or the restiction of
\mathsf{B}
to the set of compact hypernetworks in the expansive coupling category).

Proposition 8
.

The bipartite incidence map induces functors
\mathsf{B}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}\to\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
and
\mathsf{B}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{c}}\to\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
and is
\frac{1}{2^{1/p}}
-Lipschitz with respect to
d_{\mathcal{H},p}
and
d_{\mathcal{N},p}
for all
p\in[1,\infty)
and
1
-Lipschitz for
p=\infty
.

The proof will use an elementary lemma describing distributive properties of various probabilistic constructions.

Lemma 9
.

Let
(X,\mu)
,
(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
and
(X^{\prime\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime})
be Polish, Borel probability spaces,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and
\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(\mu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime})
. Also let
\pi=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha+\beta)\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and
\pi^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha^{\prime}+\beta^{\prime})\in\mathcal{C}(\mu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime})
. Then we have the following distributive laws:

1.

for
\mu^{\prime}
-almost all
x^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
,
\pi_{x^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha_{x^{\prime}}+\beta_{x^{\prime}})
;

2.

\alpha^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha^{\prime}\boxtimes\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}\boxtimes\beta)
, and likewise
\pi^{\prime}\boxtimes\alpha=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha^{\prime}\boxtimes\alpha+\beta^{\prime}\boxtimes\alpha)
;

3.

\alpha^{\prime}\bullet\pi=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha^{\prime}\bullet\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}\bullet\beta)
, and likewise
\pi^{\prime}\bullet\alpha=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha^{\prime}\bullet\alpha+\beta^{\prime}\bullet\alpha)
.

Proof.

Part 1 follows from

\displaystyle(\alpha_{x^{\prime}}+\beta_{x^{\prime}})(dx)\mu^{\prime}(dx^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\alpha_{x^{\prime}}(dx)\mu^{\prime}(dx^{\prime})+\beta_{x^{\prime}}(dx)\mu^{\prime}(dx^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\alpha(dx\times dx^{\prime})+\beta(dx\times dx^{\prime})=2\cdot\pi(dx\times dx^{\prime}).

Part 2 holds because

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi(dx\times dx^{\prime}\times dx^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}}(dx^{\prime\prime})\pi_{x^{\prime}}(dx)\mu^{\prime}(dx^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\alpha^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}}(dx^{\prime\prime})\cdot\frac{1}{2}(\alpha_{x^{\prime}}(dx)+\beta_{x^{\prime}}(dx))\cdot\mu^{\prime}(dx^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}}(dx^{\prime\prime})\alpha_{x^{\prime}}(dx)\mu^{\prime}(dx^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}}(dx^{\prime\prime})\beta_{x^{\prime}}(dx^{\prime})\mu^{\prime}(dx^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha^{\prime}\boxtimes\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}\boxtimes\beta)(dx\times dx^{\prime}\times dx^{\prime\prime}).

Finally, Part 3 follows easily:

\displaystyle\alpha^{\prime}\bullet\pi

\displaystyle=(p_{X\times X^{\prime\prime}})_{\#}\alpha^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi

\displaystyle=(p_{X\times X^{\prime\prime}})_{\#}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\alpha^{\prime}\boxtimes\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}\boxtimes\beta)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left((p_{X\times X^{\prime\prime}})_{\#}\alpha^{\prime}\boxtimes\alpha+(p_{X\times X^{\prime\prime}})_{\#}\alpha^{\prime}\boxtimes\beta\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha^{\prime}\bullet\alpha+\alpha^{\prime}\bullet\beta).

∎

Proof of Proposition
8
.

Throughout the proof, let
H,H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
be arbitrary measure hypernetworks.

Functorial.
We first establish functoriality with respect to expansive maps. Let
(\phi,\psi)
be an expansive map for measure hypernetworks
H
and
H^{\prime}
and define
\mathsf{B}(\phi,\psi):\mathsf{B}(H)\to\mathsf{B}(H^{\prime})
by

\mathsf{B}(\phi,\psi)(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\phi(z)&\mbox{if $z\in X$}\\
\psi(z)&\mbox{if $z\in Y$}.\end{array}\right.

Then for every
(w,z)\in Z\times Z
,

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathsf{B}}(w,z)

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\omega(w,z)&\mbox{if $w\in X$ and $z\in Y$}\\
\omega_{\ast}(w,z)=\omega(z,w)&\mbox{if $z\in X$ and $w\in Y$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\omega^{\prime}(\phi(w),\psi(z))&\mbox{if $w\in X$ and $z\in Y$}\\
\omega^{\prime}(\phi(z),\psi(w))=\omega_{\ast}^{\prime}(\psi(w),\phi(z))&\mbox{if $z\in X$ and $w\in Y$}\\

\displaystyle=\omega_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}(\mathsf{B}(\phi,\psi)(w),\mathsf{B}(\phi,\psi)(z)),

so that
\mathsf{B}(\phi,\psi)

from

(\phi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime})

\mathsf{B}(\phi^{\prime},\psi^{\prime})(\psi(z))&\mbox{if $z\in Y$.}\end{array}\right.

Functoriality for expansive couplings is similar. Now assume that
H,H^{\prime},H^{\prime\prime}

(\pi,\xi)

H

H^{\prime}

X^{\prime}\times X^{\prime\prime}
, and from the calculation

\mu^{\prime}_{\mathsf{B}}(dx^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}((i_{X^{\prime}})_{\#}\mu^{\prime}(dx^{\prime})+(i_{Y^{\prime}})_{\#}\nu^{\prime}(dx^{\prime}))=\frac{1}{2}\mu^{\prime}(dx^{\prime}).

Lipschitz.
It remains to show that
\mathsf{B}
is Lipschitz. Let
H,H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
and let
\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and
\xi\in\mathcal{C}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})
and consider the coupling
\rho=\mathsf{B}(\pi,\xi)\in\mathcal{C}(\mu_{\mathsf{B}},\mu_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime})
from (
6
).

For
p\in[1,\infty)
, consider the
p
-network distortion for
\mathsf{B}(H)
and
\mathsf{B}(H^{\prime})
,

\mathrm{dis}_{p}^{\mathcal{N}}(\rho)^{p}=\int_{Z\times Z^{\prime}}\int_{Z\times Z^{\prime}}\lvert\omega_{\mathsf{B}}(w,z)-\omega_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}(w^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rvert^{p}\rho(dw\times dw^{\prime})\rho(dz\times dz^{\prime}).

(11)

The measure being integrated against can be written as

\displaystyle\rho\otimes\rho=\frac{1}{4}\left((i_{X\times X^{\prime}})_{\#}\pi\otimes(i_{X\times X^{\prime}})_{\#}\pi\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+2(i_{X\times X^{\prime}})_{\#}\pi\otimes(i_{Y\times Y^{\prime}})_{\#}\xi+(i_{Y\times Y^{\prime}})_{\#}\xi\otimes(i_{Y\times Y^{\prime}})_{\#}\xi\right),

so that (
11
) is a sum of integrals with respect to each term. The integral over the first term is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\int_{Z\times Z^{\prime}}\int_{Z\times Z^{\prime}}\lvert\omega_{\mathsf{B}}(w,z)-\omega_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}(w^{\prime},z^{\prime})\rvert^{p}(i_{X\times X^{\prime}})_{\#}\pi(dw\times dw^{\prime})(i_{X\times X^{\prime}})_{\#}\pi(dz\times dz^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{4}\int_{X\times X^{\prime}}\int_{X\times X^{\prime}}\lvert\omega_{\mathsf{B}}(x_{1},x_{2})-\omega_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime})\rvert^{p}\pi(dx_{1}\times dx_{1}^{\prime})\pi(dx_{2}\times dx_{2}^{\prime})=0,

by the definition of
\omega_{\mathsf{B}}
. Likewise, the integral with respect to the
(i_{Y\times Y^{\prime}})_{\#}\xi\otimes(i_{Y\times Y^{\prime}})_{\#}\xi
-term vanishes. Finally, the integral with respect to the cross term becomes

\frac{1}{2}\int_{X\times X^{\prime}}\int_{Y\times Y^{\prime}}\lvert\omega(x,y)-\omega^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\rvert^{p}\pi(dx\times dx^{\prime})\xi(dy\times dy^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{dis}^{\mathcal{H}}_{p}(\pi,\xi)^{p}.

Therefore

\mathrm{dis}_{p}^{\mathcal{N}}(\rho)=\frac{1}{2^{1/p}}\mathrm{dis}_{p}^{\mathcal{H}}(\pi,\xi),

and by a limiting argument we see that

\mathrm{dis}_{\infty}^{\mathcal{N}}(\rho)=\mathrm{dis}_{\infty}^{\mathcal{H}}(\pi,\xi).

Since
\pi
and
\xi
were arbitrary, the Lipschitz claim follows.

∎

In general, one can construct non-weakly isomorphic hypernetworks
H,H^{\prime}
such that
d_{\mathcal{N},p}(\mathsf{B}(H),\mathsf{B}(H^{\prime}))=0
(e.g., take
H^{\prime}=H_{\ast}
for any
H
not weakly isomorphic to its dual), indicating that
\mathsf{B}
does not define a bi-Lipschitz equivalence. However, the proof of Proposition
8
suggests an alternative characterization of
d_{\mathcal{H},p}
, which we will now explain.

Definition 12
.

A measure network
N=(Z,\mu,\omega)
is called
bipartite
if there exists a topological disconnection
Z=X\cup Y
such that
\mu(X)=\mu(Y)=\frac{1}{2}
and for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
and
y_{1},y_{2}\in Y
we have
\omega(x_{1},x_{2})=\omega(y_{1},y_{2})=0
and
\omega(x_{1},y_{1})=\omega(y_{1},x_{1})
. A
labeled bipartite network
is a bipartite network with a fixed decomposition, written as
N=(X,Y,\mu,\omega)
. The collection of labeled bipartite measure networks is denoted
\mathcal{B}
. One defines expansive maps and expansive couplings between labeled bipartite measure networks in the obvious way. Let
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{m}}
denote the category of labeled bipartite networks and expansive maps as morphisms, and let
\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{c}}
denote the category of compact labeled bipartite networks with expansive couplings as morphisms.

We now define a variant of the network GW distance for labeled bipartite networks
N=(X,Y,\mu,\omega)
and
N^{\prime}=(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime},\mu^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})
which respects labels. A
labeled coupling
of
\mu
and
\mu^{\prime}
is a coupling
\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
such that

\mathrm{supp}(\pi)\subset(X\times X^{\prime})\cup(Y\times Y^{\prime}).

Let
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{B}}((X,Y,\mu),(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime},\mu^{\prime}))
denote the set of all labeled couplings.

Definition 13
.

The
labeled Gromov-Wasserstein
p
-distance

d_{\mathcal{B},p}:\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is

d_{\mathcal{B},p}(N,N^{\prime})=\inf_{\pi\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{B}}((X,Y,\mu),(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime},\mu^{\prime}))}\mathrm{dis}_{p}^{\mathcal{N}}(\pi).

Theorem 10
.

The bipartite incidence map induces functors
\mathsf{B}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}\to\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{m}}
and
\mathsf{B}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{c}}\to\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{c}}
, and is an isometry from
(\mathcal{H},d_{\mathcal{H},p})
to
(\mathcal{B},2^{1/p}\cdot d_{\mathcal{B},p})
for
p\in[1,\infty)
and to
(\mathcal{B},d_{\mathcal{B},p})
for
p=\infty
.

Proof.

Functoriality follows from Proposition
8
. It remains to show that
\mathsf{B}
is an isometry onto
\mathcal{B}
.

The bipartite incidence functor is a bijection
\mathsf{B}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{B}
, with inverse

(X,Y,\mu,\omega)\to(X,2\cdot\mu\mid_{X},Y,2\cdot\mu\mid_{Y},\omega\mid_{X\times Y}),

where
\mu\mid_{X}
is the measure on
X
defined by

\mu\mid_{X}(U)=\mu(U)

for any Borel
U\subset X\subset X\cup Y
,
\mu\mid_{Y}
is defined similarly and
\omega\mid_{X\times Y}
is the restriction of
\omega
to pairs in
X\times Y\subset(X\cup Y)\times(X\cup Y)
.

Now let
H,H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
. We consider
p\in[1,\infty)
, with the
p=\infty
case following similarly. Proposition
8
shows that
2^{1/p}d_{\mathcal{B},p}(\mathsf{B}(H),\mathsf{B}(H^{\prime}))\leq d_{\mathcal{H},p}(H,H^{\prime})
: given
\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and
\xi\in\mathcal{C}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})
, the coupling
\mathsf{B}(\pi,\xi)
defined in (
6
) is an element of
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{B}}((X,Y,\mu_{\mathsf{B}}),(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime},\mu_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}))
(
\mu_{\mathsf{B}}
and
\mu_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}
defined as in (
6
)), so the proof of Lipschitzness goes through. To prove the reverse inequality, let
\rho
be an arbitrary coupling in
\mathcal{C}_{\mathcal{B}}((X,Y,\mu_{\mathsf{B}}),(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime},\mu_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}))
and define
\pi
and
\xi
on Borel sets
U,V
by

\pi(U)=2\cdot\rho(U\cap(X\times X^{\prime}))\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\xi(V)=2\cdot\rho(V\cap(Y\times Y^{\prime})).

Then
\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and
\xi\in\mathcal{C}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})
and computations similar to those in the proof of Proposition
8
can be used to show that

\displaystyle\mathrm{dis}_{\mathsf{B}(H),\mathsf{B}(H^{\prime}),p}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{1/p}}\mathrm{dis}_{H,H^{\prime},p}(\pi,\xi).

It follows that
2^{1/p}d_{\mathcal{B},p}(\mathsf{B}(H),\mathsf{B}(H^{\prime}))\geq d_{\mathcal{H},p}(H,H^{\prime})
.
∎

.

.

be a measure hypernetwork. For

of

\mathsf{Q}_{q}(H)=(X,\mu,\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{q}})

\mathsf{L}_{q}(H)=(Y,\nu,\omega_{\mathsf{L}_{q}})

.

Remark 6
.

Suppose that
(X,Y)
is a (combinatorial) hypergraph, represented as a measure hypernetwork
H=(X,\mu,Y,\nu,\omega)
with
\mu
and
\nu
uniform and
\omega
encoding hyperedge incidence (
\omega(x,y)=1
if

and zero otherwise). Then

captures the classical clique expansion construction, where
\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{\infty}}
is the adjacency function. Indeed, for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
,
\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{\infty}}(x_{1},x_{2})=1
if and only if there exists

such that
x_{1},x_{2}\in y
, and is equal to zero otherwise. Similarly,
Q_{1}(H)
returns the clique expansion with edge
\{x_{1},x_{2}\}
weighted by the proportion of hyperedges
y
containing both
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, another typical convention.

Remark 7
.

It is clear, by definition, that
\mathsf{L}_{q}(H)=\mathsf{Q}_{q}(H_{\ast})
, where
H_{\ast}
is the dual of
H
. Then
\mathsf{L}_{q}(H)
captures the line graph transformation from
Section 3.1
(particularly, in the

and
q=\infty
cases).

Theorem 11
.

The
q
-clique expansion and
q
-line graph maps
\mathsf{Q}_{q},\mathsf{L}_{q}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{N}
:

•

are Lipschitz with respect to
d_{\mathcal{H},p}
and
d_{\mathcal{N},p}
when
1\leq q\leq p\leq\infty
, and

•

induce functors
\mathsf{Q}_{q},\mathsf{L}_{q}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{c}}\to\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
for all
q\in[1,\infty]
and
\mathsf{Q}_{\infty},\mathsf{L}_{\infty}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}\to\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
.

The proof will use some technical lemmas. Their proofs will be delayed to Appendix
A.2
.

The first lemma says that Gromov-Wasserstein and co-optimal transport distances can always be realized as
L^{p}
distances between functions over a common space, by “lifting” to a weak isomorphism representative. We remark that the idea of the lemma is implicitly implemented in
Chowdhury and
Needham 2020
in the setting of GW distance for the purpose of computation of Fréchet means of ensembles of networks (see also
Li et al. 2021
).

Lemma 12
.

Let
H,H^{\prime}
be measure hypernetworks. There exist measure hypernetworks
\overline{H}=(\overline{X},\overline{\mu},\overline{Y},\overline{\nu},\overline{\omega})
and
\overline{H^{\prime}}=(\overline{X},\overline{\mu},\overline{Y},\overline{\nu},\overline{\omega}^{\prime})
(i.e., with the same underlying measure spaces, but different hypernetwork functions) such that
\overline{\mu}
and
\overline{\nu}
are fully supported,
H
is weakly isomorphic to
\overline{H}
,
H^{\prime}
is weakly isomorphic to
\overline{H^{\prime}}
, and for all
p\in[1,\infty]

d_{\mathcal{H},p}(H,H^{\prime})=d_{\mathcal{H},p}(\overline{H},\overline{H^{\prime}})=\|\overline{\omega}-\overline{\omega}^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\overline{\mu}\otimes\overline{\nu})},

(12)

where, in this setting,
\overline{\omega}-\overline{\omega}^{\prime}
is understood as the function
X\times Y\to{\mathbb{R}}
taking
(x,y)
to
\overline{\omega}(x,y)-\overline{\omega}^{\prime}(x,y)
.

Similarly, for measure networks
N,N^{\prime}
, there exist measure networks
\overline{N}=(\overline{X},\overline{\mu},\overline{\omega})
and
\overline{N^{\prime}}=(\overline{X},\overline{\mu},\overline{\omega}^{\prime})
such that
\overline{\mu}
is fully supported,
N
is weakly isomorphic to
\overline{N}
,
N^{\prime}
is weakly isomorphic to
\overline{N^{\prime}}
, and for all
p\in[1,\infty]
,

d_{\mathcal{N},p}(N,N^{\prime})=d_{\mathcal{N},p}(\overline{N},\overline{N^{\prime}})=\|\overline{\omega}-\overline{\omega}^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\overline{\mu}\otimes\overline{\mu})}.

Lemma 13
.

If
H
and
H^{\prime}
are weakly isomorphic measure hypernetworks, then
\mathsf{Q}_{q}(H)
and
\mathsf{Q}_{q}(H^{\prime})
are weakly isomorphic measure networks for all
q\in[1,\infty]
.

Lemma 14

(
Mémoli 2011a
, Theorem 5.1(d))
.

Let
N=(X,\mu,\omega),N^{\prime}=(X,\mu,\omega^{\prime})
be measure networks over the same measure space. Then, for
p\in[1,\infty]
,

d_{\mathcal{N},p}(N,N^{\prime})\leq\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\mu)}.

Here, we are considering
\omega-\omega^{\prime}
as the function
X\times X\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined by
(x,x^{\prime})\mapsto\omega(x,x^{\prime})-\omega^{\prime}(x,x^{\prime})
.

Proof of Theorem
11
.

Since properties of the clique expansion transfer to those of the line graph via dualization (Proposition
7
), we will focus on the map
\mathsf{Q}_{q}
.

Lipschitz.
First, we will prove that
\mathsf{Q}_{q}
is Lipschitz when
1\leq q\leq p\leq\infty
. We will proceed by establishing some estimates, then combining them at the end to get the desired inequality.

Let
f:X\times X\times Y\to{\mathbb{R}}
be the function
f(x_{1},x_{2},y)=\min_{j}\omega(x_{j},y)
and, likewise, let
f^{\prime}(x_{1},x_{2},y)=\min_{j}\omega^{\prime}(x_{j},y)
. We claim that

\|\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{q}}-\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{q}}^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\mu)}\leq\|f-f^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\mu\otimes\nu)}.

(13)

Indeed, when
p<\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\|\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{q}}-\omega^{\prime}_{\mathsf{Q}_{q}}\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\mu)}^{p}

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{X}\int_{X}\lvert\|\min_{j}\{\omega(x_{j},\cdot)\}\|_{L^{q}(\nu)}-\|\min_{j}\{\omega^{\prime}(x_{j},\cdot)\}\|_{L^{q}(\nu)}\rvert^{p}\;\mu(dx_{1})\mu(dx_{2})

\displaystyle\quad\leq\int_{X}\int_{X}\|\min_{j}\{\omega(x_{j},\cdot)\}-\min_{j}\{\omega^{\prime}(x_{j},\cdot)\}\|_{L^{q}(\nu)}^{p}\;\mu(dx_{1})\mu(dx_{2})

(14)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\int_{X}\int_{X}\|\min_{j}\{\omega(x_{j},\cdot)\}-\min_{j}\{\omega^{\prime}(x_{j},\cdot)\}\|_{L^{p}(\nu)}^{p}\;\mu(dx_{1})\mu(dx_{2})

(15)

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{X}\int_{X}\int_{Y}\lvert\min_{j}\{\omega(x_{j},y)\}-\min_{j}\{\omega^{\prime}(x_{j},y)\}\rvert^{p}\;\nu(dy)\mu(dx_{1})\mu(dx_{2})

\displaystyle\quad=\|f-f^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\mu\otimes\nu)}^{p},

where (
14
) follows by the reverse triangle inequality form of Minkowski’s inequality, (
15
) follows from Hölder’s inequality. The
p=\infty
case follows by a limiting argument.

Now define three new functions:

\displaystyle f_{j}:X\times X\times Y\to{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle:(x_{1},x_{2},y)\mapsto\omega(x_{j},y),\quad\mbox{for $j\in\{1,2\}$, and}

\displaystyle g:X\times X\times Y\to{\mathbb{R}}

\displaystyle:(x_{1},x_{2},y)\mapsto\lvert\omega(x_{1},y)-\omega(x_{2},y)\rvert.

Analogously define
f_{1}^{\prime},f_{2}^{\prime},g^{\prime}
by replacing all instances of
\omega
with
\omega^{\prime}
. Our next goal is to establish the following:

\displaystyle\|f_{j}-f_{j}^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\mu\otimes\nu)}

\displaystyle=\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\nu)}

(16)

\displaystyle\|g^{\prime}-g\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\mu\otimes\nu)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\nu)}.

(17)

The equality (
16
) (for, say,
j=1
) holds because the functions
f_{1}
and
f_{1}^{\prime}
do not depend on
x_{2}
, so we marginalize over the second copy of
\mu
. To see (
17
) for
p<\infty
,

\displaystyle\|g^{\prime}-g\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\mu\otimes\nu)}^{p}

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{Y}\int_{X}\int_{X}\lvert\lvert\omega^{\prime}(x_{1},y)-\omega^{\prime}(x_{2},y)\rvert-\lvert\omega(x_{1},y)-\omega(x_{2},y)\rvert\rvert^{p}\mu(dx_{1})\mu(dx_{2})\nu(dy)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\int_{Y}\int_{X}\int_{X}\lvert(\omega^{\prime}(x_{1},y)-\omega(x_{1},y))+(\omega(x_{2},y)-\omega^{\prime}(x_{2},y))\rvert^{p}\mu(dx_{1})\mu(dx_{2})\nu(dy),

(18)

where we applied the reverse triangle inequality
\lvert\lvert a\rvert-\lvert b\rvert\rvert\leq\lvert a-b\rvert
for real numbers
a,b
and rearranged terms. Applying the triangle inequality for the
L^{p}
norm and then marginalizing, we obtain

\displaystyle\|g^{\prime}-g\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\mu\otimes\nu)}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left(\int_{Y}\int_{X}\lvert\omega^{\prime}(x_{1},y)-\omega(x_{1},y)\rvert^{p}\mu(x_{1})\nu(dy)\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\left(\int_{Y}\int_{X}\lvert\omega(x_{2},y)-\omega^{\prime}(x_{2},y)\rvert^{p}\mu(x_{2})\nu(dy)\right)^{1/p}

\displaystyle\quad=\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\nu)}+\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\nu)}.

(19)

The
p=\infty
case follows similarly.

Recall the general formula
\min\{a,b\}=\frac{1}{2}(a+b-\lvert a-b\rvert)
for real numbers
a
and
b
. By the definitions of our functions, we therefore have
f=\frac{1}{2}(f_{1}+f_{2}-g)
and
f^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}(f_{1}^{\prime}+f_{2}^{\prime}-g^{\prime})
. We are now prepared to complete the proof:

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{N},p}(\mathsf{Q}_{q}(H),\mathsf{Q}_{q}(H^{\prime}))

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{q}}-\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{q}}^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\mu)}

(20)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\|f-f^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\mu\otimes\nu)}

(21)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{2}\|f_{1}+f_{2}-g-f_{1}^{\prime}-f_{2}^{\prime}+g^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\mu\otimes\nu)}

(22)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(\|f_{1}-f_{1}^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\mu\otimes\nu)}+\|f_{2}-f_{2}^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\mu\otimes\nu)}+\|g^{\prime}-g\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\mu\otimes\nu)}\right)

(23)

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\|\omega-\omega^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\mu\otimes\nu)}

(24)

\displaystyle\quad=2d_{\mathcal{H},p}(H,H^{\prime}),

where the steps are justified as follows. Inequality (
20
) is Lemma
14
, (
21
) comes from our estimate (
13
), (
22
) follows from the formula for
\min\{a,b\}
discussed above, (
23
) is the triangle inequality for the
L^{p}
norm, and (
24
) follows from our estimates (
16
) and (
17
). This shows that
\mathsf{Q}_{q}
is 2-Lipschitz.

Functorial.
We now demonstrate functoriality, beginning with expansive maps in the
q=\infty
case. Let
(\phi,\psi)
be an expansive map from
H=(X,\mu,Y,\nu,\omega)
to
H^{\prime}=(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime},Y^{\prime},\nu^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})
. We will show that
\phi:X\to X^{\prime}
defines an expansive map from
\mathsf{Q}_{\infty}(N)
to
\mathsf{Q}_{\infty}(N^{\prime})
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{\infty}}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\|\min_{j}\omega(x_{j},\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\nu)}\leq\|\min_{j}\omega(\phi(x_{j}),\psi(\cdot))\|_{L^{\infty}(\nu)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\min_{j}\omega(\phi(x_{j}),\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(\nu^{\prime})}=\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{\infty}}^{\prime}(\phi(x_{1}),\phi(x_{2})),

where the second inequality follows because the essential supremum over
\nu^{\prime}
is greater than or equal to the essential supremum over the pushforward of
\nu
by
\psi
, which may not have full support.

Finally, we show functoriality for
q\in[1,\infty]
with respect to expansive couplings. Assume that
H,H^{\prime}
are compact measure hypernetworks and let
\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and
\xi\in\mathcal{C}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})
be expansive couplings of
H
and
H^{\prime}
. We will show that
\pi
is an expansive coupling for
\mathsf{Q}_{q}(H)
and
\mathsf{Q}_{q}(H^{\prime})
; that is,
\mathsf{Q}_{q}(\pi,\xi)=\pi
. Before doing so, we easily observe that
\mathsf{Q}_{q}
respects compositions, due to its forgetful structure: for
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(\mu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime})
and
\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(\nu^{\prime},\nu^{\prime\prime})
,

\mathsf{Q}_{q}(\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi,\xi^{\prime}\bullet\xi)=\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi=\mathsf{Q}_{q}(\pi^{\prime},\xi^{\prime})\bullet\mathsf{Q}_{q}(\pi,\xi).

For
\pi\otimes\pi\otimes\xi
-almost every
(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2},x_{2}^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})
, it holds that
\omega(x_{j},y)\leq\omega^{\prime}(x_{j}^{\prime},y^{\prime})
for both
j=1,2
, and it follows that
\min_{j}\omega(x_{j},y)\leq\min_{j}\omega^{\prime}(x_{j}^{\prime},y^{\prime})
holds
\pi\otimes\pi\otimes\xi
-almost everywhere. Therefore, for
\pi\otimes\pi
-almost every pair
(x_{1},x_{1}^{\prime})
and
(x_{2},x_{2}^{\prime})
,

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{q}}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\|\min_{j}\omega(x_{j},\cdot)\|_{L^{q}(\nu)}=\|\min_{j}\omega(x_{j},\cdot)\|_{L^{q}(\xi)}

(25)

\displaystyle\leq\|\min_{j}\omega^{\prime}(x_{j}^{\prime},\cdot)\|_{L^{q}(\xi)}=\|\min_{j}\omega^{\prime}(x_{j}^{\prime},\cdot)\|_{L^{q}(\nu)}=\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{q}}^{\prime}(x_{1}^{\prime},x_{2}^{\prime}).

In (
25
), we consider the argument of the norm on the right as a function
Y\times Y^{\prime}\to{\mathbb{R}}
given by
(y,y^{\prime})\mapsto\min_{j}\omega(x_{j},y)
. In particular, this function does not depend on
y^{\prime}
, so the second equality follows by marginalizing. The remaining equalities follow by similar arguments, and this completes the proof.
∎

Example 6
.

(Matrix Formulation of Graphifications)
Let
(X,Y)
be a (combinatorial) hypergraph. In the finite setting, we can represent the binary incidence function as a matrix
\omega
of size
\lvert X\rvert\times\lvert Y\rvert
, valued in
\{0,1\}
. One may observe that the
1
-clique expansion (respectively,
1
-line graph) considered above is (up to a constant factor, when all node and hyperedge probability distributions are uniform) equal to the matrix product
\omega\omega^{T}
(respectively,
\omega^{T}\omega
), with the result being interpreted as the weighted adjacency matrix of a graph. Here, superscript
T
denotes matrix transpose. For these maps to be well-defined in general on weak isomorphism classes (as in Lemma
13
), one must involve probability measures. For example, we define the
matrix product line graph map
to be
H=(X,\mu,Y,\nu,\omega)\mapsto\mathsf{L}_{\mathrm{mp}}(H):=(Y,\nu,\omega^{T}\mathrm{diag}(\mu)\omega)
, where
\mathrm{diag}(\mu)
is the diagonal matrix with entries coming from
\mu
.

The matrix formulations described above are computationally convenient, and have some nice properties. For example, one can check that the matrix product line graph map is indeed well-defined on weak isomorphism classes of finite measure hypernetworks. Moreover, it is functorial with respect to expansive couplings. However, the matrix formulations are no longer Lipschitz when extended to general hypernetwork functions. This is illustrated by the following example.

For
\alpha\geq 1
, set
H_{\alpha}=(X,\mu,Y,\nu,\omega_{\alpha})
, where
\lvert X\rvert=\lvert Y\rvert=2
,
\mu,\nu
are uniform and
\omega_{\alpha}
is described by the matrix
\omega_{\alpha}=\mathrm{diag}(\alpha,\alpha)
. The matrix product line graph map takes
H_{\alpha}
to
\mathsf{L}_{\mathrm{mp}}(H_{\alpha})=(Y,\nu,\mathrm{diag}(\alpha^{2}/2,\alpha^{2}/2))
. Now consider
d_{\mathcal{H},p}(H_{\alpha},H_{1})
and
d_{\mathcal{N},p}(\mathsf{L}_{\mathrm{mp}}(H_{\alpha}),\mathsf{L}_{\mathrm{mp}}(H_{1}))
for
\alpha\geq 1
and
p\in[1,\infty)
. One can show that
d_{\mathcal{H},p}(H_{\alpha},H_{1})
is realized by optimal couplings
\pi=\xi=\mathrm{diag}(1/2,1/2)
and that this coupling is also optimal for
d_{\mathcal{N},p}(\mathsf{L}_{\mathrm{mp}}(H_{\alpha}),\mathsf{L}_{\mathrm{mp}}(H_{1}))
. It follows by direct computation that

d_{\mathcal{H},p}(H_{\alpha},H_{1})=\frac{1}{2^{1/p}}\left(\alpha-1\right)\mbox{ and }d_{\mathcal{N},p}(\mathsf{L}_{\mathrm{mp}}(H_{\alpha}),\mathsf{L}_{\mathrm{mp}}(H_{1}))=\frac{1}{2^{1/p}}\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\right).

Taking
\alpha
arbitrarily large shows that the matrix product line graph map
\mathsf{L}_{\mathrm{mp}}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{N}
is not Lipschitz.

This example shows that one must take care when designing graphifications in order to ensure the desired properties.

We end this section with a structural result on graphifications showing (roughly) that
\mathsf{Q}_{\infty}
is minimal among graphifications satisfying mild assumptions. A graphification
\mathsf{F}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{N}
is called:

•

a
node-type functor
if it is of the form
\mathsf{F}(X,\mu,Y,\nu,\omega)=(X,\mu,\omega_{\mathsf{F}})
and is functorial with respect to expansive maps, i.e.,
\mathsf{F}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}\to\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
;

•

non-trivial
if there exists
H\in\mathcal{H}
such that the network function of
\mathsf{F}(H)
is not identically zero on a full measure set;

•

well-defined on weak isomorphism classes
if whenever
H
and
H^{\prime}
are weakly isomorphic as measure hypernetworks,
\mathsf{F}(H)
and
\mathsf{F}(H^{\prime})
are weakly isomorphic as measure networks; and

•

scale-equivariant
if it satisfies the following condition for all
H=(X,\mu,Y,\nu,\omega)
. Let
\beta\geq 0
and define
\beta\cdot H=(X,\mu,Y,\nu,\beta\cdot\omega)
; i.e., a rescaling of the hypernetwork function of
H
by
\beta
. Similarly, for
N\in\mathcal{N}
, let
\beta\cdot N
denote a rescaling of the network function of
N
by
\beta
. Scale-equivariance then requires
\mathsf{F}(\beta\cdot H)=\beta\cdot\mathsf{F}(H)
.

Proposition 15
.

If
\mathsf{F}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}\to\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
is a node-type functor which is non-trivial, well-defined on weak isomorphism classes and scale-equivariant, then there exists a constant
c>0
such that for all
H=(X,\mu,Y,\nu,\omega)\in\mathcal{H}
and for every
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
,

\omega_{\mathsf{F}}(x_{1},x_{2})\geq c\cdot\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{\infty}}(x_{1},x_{2}).

(26)

Remark 8
.

The Proposition can be stated in a more categorical language. First, various conditions on
\mathsf{F}
can be stated diagramatically. Let
\mathcal{M}
denote the category whose objects are pairs
(X,\mu)
consisting of a Polish space
X
and a Borel probability measure
\mu
, whose morphisms are functions between underlying sets. Let
\mathsf{M}_{\mathcal{N}}:\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}\to\mathcal{M}
be the forgetful functor
(X,\mu,\omega)\mapsto(X,\mu)
; likewise, let
\mathsf{M}_{\mathcal{H}}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}\to\mathcal{M}
be the forgetful functor
(X,\mu,Y,\nu,\omega)\mapsto(X,\mu)
(taking an expansive map
(\phi,\psi)
to the function
\phi
). The property of being a node-type functor is that the following diagram commutes.

\begin{CD}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}@>{\mathsf{F}}>{}>\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}\\
@V{\mathsf{M}_{\mathcal{H}}}V{}V@V{}V{\mathsf{M}_{\mathcal{N}}}V\\
\mathcal{M}@>{=}>{}>\mathcal{M}\end{CD}

The property of being well-defined on weak isomorphism classes says that
\mathsf{F}
is a functor between categories with weak equivalences; see
Section 4.2
below. To restate scale-equivariance more categorically, we introduce the rescaling functor
\mathsf{T}_{\mathcal{N}}^{\beta}:\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}\to\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
,
\beta\geq 0
, defined on objects by
\mathsf{T}_{\mathcal{N}}^{\beta}(N)=\beta\cdot N
(as above) and defined to be the identity on morphisms. We similarly define a rescaling functor for hypernetworks
\mathsf{T}_{\mathcal{H}}^{\beta}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}\to\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}
. Then the scale equivariance property of
\mathsf{F}
says that the following diagram commutes for all
\beta\geq 0
.

\begin{CD}\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}@>{\mathsf{F}}>{}>\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}\\
@V{\mathsf{T}_{\mathcal{H}}^{\beta}}V{}V@V{}V{\mathsf{T}_{\mathcal{N}}^{\beta}}V\\
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}@>{\mathsf{F}}>{}>\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}\end{CD}

The nontriviality of
\mathsf{F}
says that
\mathsf{F}
does not factor as
\mathsf{T}_{\mathcal{N}}^{0}\circ\mathsf{G}
for some functor
\mathsf{G}
. Finally, condition (
26
) says that there is a natural transformation
\mathsf{T}^{c}_{\mathcal{N}}\circ\mathsf{Q}_{\infty}\Rightarrow\mathsf{F}
whose components are identity morphisms.

Proof of Proposition
15
.

For
\alpha\geq 0
, let
H_{\alpha}
be the measure hypernetwork with one node
x^{\alpha}
, one hyperedge
y^{\alpha}
and with hypernetwork function
\omega_{\alpha}(x^{\alpha},y^{\alpha})=\alpha
. Let
(\omega_{1})_{\mathsf{F}}(x^{1},x^{1})=c
for some
c\geq 0
. Our first goal is to show that
c>0
.

By scale equivariance, we have
(\omega_{\alpha})_{\mathsf{F}}(x^{\alpha},x^{\alpha})=c\cdot\alpha
. Now let
H=(X,\mu,Y,\nu,\omega)\in\mathcal{H}
be arbitrary. Since
\omega
is assumed to be bounded, there exists
\alpha\geq 0
such that
\omega(x,y)<\alpha
for every
x,y
. The unique functions
X\to\{x^{\alpha}\}
and
Y\to\{y^{\alpha}\}
define an expansive map from
H
to
H_{\alpha}
, so functoriality implies that there is an expansive map from
\mathsf{F}(H)
to
\mathsf{F}(H_{\alpha})
. In particular,
\omega_{\mathsf{F}}(x_{1},x_{2})\leq(\omega_{\alpha})_{\mathsf{F}}(x^{\alpha},x^{\alpha})=c\cdot\alpha
for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
. It follows that
c>0
, by non-triviality of
\mathsf{F}
.

Now let
H
be an arbitrary measure hypernetwork, let
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
,
y\in Y
, and set
\beta=\min_{j}\omega(x_{j},y)
. Let
\overline{H}_{\beta}
denote the measure hypernetwork with nodes
\{x_{1}^{\beta},x_{2}^{\beta}\}
, hyperedge set
\{y^{\beta}\}
, measures uniform and
\overline{\omega}_{\beta}(x^{\beta}_{j},y^{\beta})=\beta
for
j=1,2
. Observe that
\overline{H}_{\beta}
is weakly isomorphic to
H_{\beta}
. There is an expansive map from
\overline{H}_{\beta}
to
H
given by
x_{j}^{\beta}\mapsto x_{j}
and
y^{\beta}\mapsto y
. This induces an expansive map from
\mathsf{F}(\overline{H}_{\beta})
to
\mathsf{F}(H)
so that for every choice of
x_{1},x_{2}
we have

\omega_{\mathsf{F}}(x_{1},x_{2})\geq(\overline{\omega}_{\beta})_{\mathsf{F}}(x_{1}^{\beta},x_{2}^{\beta})=(\omega_{\beta})_{\mathsf{F}}(x^{\beta},x^{\beta})=c\cdot\beta=c\cdot\min_{j}\omega(x_{j},y).

Since this holds for any
y\in Y
, we have established (
26
).
∎

Since
q
-clique expansions are non-trivial, well-defined on weak isomorphism classes and scale-equivariant, and since it is possible to construct
H
containing nodes
x_{1},x_{2}
such that
\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{q}}(x_{1},x_{2})<\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{\infty}}(x_{1},x_{2})
, with an arbitrarily large gap, we have the following immediate corollary.

Corollary 16
.

If
q<\infty
, then
\mathsf{Q}_{q}
is not functorial with respect to expansive maps.

4
Further Category-Theoretic Results

This section collects some additional results on the categories of measure networks and hypernetworks, some of which involve more technical category-theoretic terminology and fall outside the discussion of graphification above. Our main reference for this terminology is
Borceux 1994
.

4.1
Categorical and Metric Isomorphisms

Our categorical constructions for (hyper)networks raise a natural question: do isomorphisms in our categories correspond to metric notions of isomorphism introduced in Section
2
? We address this question in this subsection.

Borrowing terminology from metric geometry, we say that two measure measure networks
N,N^{\prime}
are
isometric
if there exists a (not necessarily measure-preserving) bijection
\phi:X\to X^{\prime}
such that
\omega^{\prime}(\phi(x),\phi(y))=\omega(x,y)
for every
x,y\in X
. We call such a map an
isometry
. Similarly, two measure hypernetworks
H,H^{\prime}
are
isometric
if there exist
\phi:X\to X^{\prime}
and
\psi:Y\to Y^{\prime}
such that
\omega^{\prime}(\phi(x),\psi(y))=\omega(x,y)
for all
x\in X
and
y\in Y
.

In a category
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
or
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
, we say that two objects
N,N^{\prime}
are
categorically isomorphic
if there exist morphisms
N\to N^{\prime}
and
N^{\prime}\to N
such that each composition is equal to the identity. We define hypernetworks
H,H^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}
or
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{c}}
to be
categorically isomorphic
in a similar fashion. The following proposition relates categorical isomorphisms with our notions of metric isomorphisms.

Proposition 17
.

We have the following equivalences.

1.

Measure networks
N,N^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
are categorically isomorphic if and only if they are isometric. Likewise, measure hypernetworks
H,H^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}
are categorically isomorphic if and only if they are isometric.

2.

Measure networks
N,N^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
are categorically isomorphic if and only if they are strongly isomorphic. In particular, any categorical isomorphism
\pi
from
N
to
N^{\prime}
is of the form
\pi=(\mathrm{id}_{X}\times\phi)_{\#}\mu
for some strong isomorphism
\phi
. Likewise, any categorical isomorphism
(\pi,\xi)
from
H
to
H^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{c}}
is induced by a strong isomorphism
(\phi,\psi)
.

Proof.

We prove the statements in the setting of measure networks, with the proofs for measure hypernetworks following by similar arguments.

For Part 1, suppose that
N\xrightarrow{\phi}N^{\prime}
and
N^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\phi^{\prime}}N
are categorical isomorphisms with
\phi^{\prime}\circ\phi=\mathrm{id}_{X}
and
\phi\circ\phi^{\prime}=\mathrm{id}_{X^{\prime}}
. Then
\phi^{\prime}=\phi^{-1}
at a set level, and
\phi
must be a bijection. Moreover, for every
x,y\in X
,

\omega(x,y)\leq\omega^{\prime}(\phi(x),\phi(y))\leq\omega(\phi^{\prime}(\phi(x)),\phi^{\prime}(\phi(y)))=\omega(x,y),

(27)

so that all inequalities are forced to be equalities. This shows that
\phi
is an isometry, and the converse statement is trivial.

To prove Part 2, first suppose that
N\xrightarrow{\pi}N^{\prime}
and
N^{\prime}\xrightarrow{\pi^{\prime}}N
are categorical isomorphisms with
\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi=\mathbbm{1}_{N}
and
\pi\bullet\pi^{\prime}=\mathbbm{1}_{N^{\prime}}
. We first observe that

(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)\mbox{ and }(x^{\prime},y)\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\prime})\quad\mbox{implies}\quad(x,x^{\prime},y)\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi);

(28)

this follows by the defining equation

\pi^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi(dx\times dx^{\prime}\times dy)=\pi^{\prime}_{x^{\prime}}(dx)\pi_{x^{\prime}}(dy)\mu^{\prime}(dx^{\prime}).

From this, we deduce that if
(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)
, then
(x^{\prime},x)\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\prime})
. Indeed, (
28
) applied to
(x,x^{\prime}),(x^{\prime},x)
tells us that
(x,x^{\prime},x)\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi)
, so that
(x^{\prime},x)\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\prime})
, by Part 1 of Lemma
5
.

Next, we show that
\pi
is supported on the graph of a measure-preserving map. To this end, suppose that
(x,x^{\prime}),(x,y^{\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)
. Then
(x^{\prime},x)\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\prime})
and an application of (
28
) (with the roles of
\pi^{\prime}
and
\pi
reversed) tells us that
(x^{\prime},y,y^{\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi\boxtimes\pi^{\prime})
, hence that
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi\bullet\pi^{\prime})
. Since
\pi\bullet\pi^{\prime}=\mathbbm{1}_{N^{\prime}}
, it follows that
x^{\prime}=y^{\prime}
, so that
\pi
is supported on a graph of some measure preserving map
\phi:X\to X^{\prime}
. Applying the above arguments to
\pi^{\prime}
, we deduce that
\pi^{\prime}=(\mathrm{id}_{X^{\prime}}\times\phi^{\prime})_{\#}\mu^{\prime}
for a measure-preserving expansive map
\phi^{\prime}:X^{\prime}\to X
. It then follows from Proposition
6
that
\phi^{\prime}=\phi^{-1}
; indeed, using the functor
\mathsf{P}
from the proposition, we have

\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{id}_{X})=\mathbbm{1}_{N}=\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi=\mathsf{P}(\phi^{\prime}\circ\phi),

so faithfulness implies
\phi^{\prime}\circ\phi=\mathrm{id_{X}}
, and we similarly have
\phi\circ\phi^{\prime}=\mathrm{id}_{X^{\prime}}
. In particular,
\phi
is a measure-preserving bijection with measure-preserving inverse. Finally,
\phi
must preserve
\omega
by an argument similar to the one used in (
27
). The converse statement for Part 2 is straightforward, so this completes the proof.
∎

4.2
Distortion Zero Couplings as Weak Equivalences

The notion of weak isomorphism of measure (hyper)networks also has a natural categorical interpretation. Recall that a
category with weak equivalences
is a pair
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{W})
consisting of a category
\mathcal{C}
and a subcategory
\mathcal{W}
such that
\mathcal{W}
contains all (categorical) isomorphisms and satisfies the
two-out-of-three property
, meaning that for any morphisms
f,g
and
g\circ f
in
\mathcal{C}
, if two members of the set
\{f,g,g\circ f\}
are morphisms of
\mathcal{W}
, then the third must be as well. Morphisms of
\mathcal{W}
are called
weak equivalences
.

Let
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{w}}
denote the wide subcategory of
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
whose morphisms are expansive couplings
\pi
with
\mathrm{dis}_{\infty}^{\mathcal{N}}(\pi)=0
. This does define a subcategory, since any two morphisms
\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(\mu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime})
with zero distortion must compose to a coupling with zero distortion—see Remark
12
in the Appendix. Similarly, let
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{w}}
be the wide subcategory of
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{c}}
whose morphisms
(\pi,\xi)
satisfy
\mathrm{dis}_{\infty}^{\mathcal{H}}(\pi,\xi)=0
.

Proposition 18
.

The pairs
(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}},\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{w}})
and
(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{c}},\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{w}})
are categories with weak equivalences.

Proof.

We only include details for
(\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}},\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{w}})
, as the measure hypernetworks case is conceptually the same, but requires more complicated notation.

By Proposition
17
,
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{w}}
contains all (categorical) isomorphisms: a categorical isomorphism is induced by a strong isomorphism, and therefore has zero distortion. It remains to show that the two-out-of-three property holds. Let
\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(\mu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime})
. If
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
are morphisms of
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{w}}
, then so is
\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi
, by virtue of the inequality

\mathrm{dis}_{\infty}^{\mathcal{N}}(\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi)\leq\mathrm{dis}_{\infty}^{\mathcal{N}}(\pi^{\prime})+\mathrm{dis}_{\infty}^{\mathcal{N}}(\pi)

(see Remark
12
). Suppose that
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi
are morphisms of
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{w}}
. We wish to show that
\pi^{\prime}
has zero distortion, which is to say that

\omega(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\omega(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})

for all
(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}),(y^{\prime},y^{\prime\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\prime})
. Assuming
(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\prime})
, there exists
x\in X
such that
(x,x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\prime}\boxtimes\pi)
, by Lemma
5
, Part 2. It follows that
(x,x^{\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)
and
(x,x^{\prime\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi)
, by Part 1 of Lemma
5
. We can likewise choose
y\in Y
such that
(y,y^{\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)
and
(y,y^{\prime\prime})\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi)
. By our assumption that
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi
both have zero distortion, it follows that

\omega(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\omega(x,y)=\omega(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime}).

The remaining case (
\pi^{\prime}
and
\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi
have zero distortion implies
\pi
does as well) follows similarly.
∎

Let
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{W})
and
(\mathcal{C}^{\prime},\mathcal{W}^{\prime})
be categories with weak equivalences. We say that a functor
\mathsf{F}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}^{\prime}

respects weak equivalences
if for any morphism
\phi
of
\mathcal{W}
,
\mathsf{F}(\phi)
is a morphism of
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
.

Corollary 19
.

The functors
\mathsf{B},\mathsf{Q}_{q},\mathsf{L}_{q}:\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{c}}\to\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
respect weak equivalences
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{w}}
and
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{w}}
.

Proof.

This follows since the functors were all shown to be Lipschitz. In particular, the zero distortion condition is preserved.
∎

4.3
Limit Constructions

We end this section by studying the (non)existence of a few standard limit constructions in our categories of (hyper)networks. In particular, we study whether our categories have any of the following so-called
basic limit constructions
: initial object, terminal object, products, coproducts, pullbacks and/or pushouts.

First, we will show that limit constructions in the category
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
of measure networks with expansive maps are closely related to limit constructions in the category of sets. Let
\mathcal{S}
denote the category of sets and functions and let
\mathsf{F}:\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}\to\mathcal{S}
denote the forgetful functor, taking
N=(X,\mu,\omega)
to
X
and an expansive map to its underlying function. For a fixed measure network
N_{0}\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
, let
\mathsf{H}_{N_{0}}:\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}\to\mathcal{S}
denote the
hom functor
taking
N
to
\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathrm{m}}(N_{0},N)
, the set of expansive morphisms from
N_{0}
to
N
. For an expansive map
N\xrightarrow{\phi}N^{\prime}
,
\mathsf{H}_{N_{0}}(\phi):\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathrm{m}}(N_{0},N)\to\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathrm{m}}(N_{0},N^{\prime})
is defined by
\mathsf{H}_{N_{0}}(\phi)(\psi)=\phi\circ\psi
.

Proposition 20
.

The forgetful functor
\mathsf{F}:\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}\to\mathcal{S}
is representable. It follows that
\mathsf{F}
preserves limits.

Proof.

We need to show that there is a natural isomorphism of
\mathsf{F}
with
\mathsf{H}_{N_{0}}
for some measure network
N_{0}
. Let
N_{0}=(X_{0},\mu_{0},\omega_{0})
, where
X_{0}=\{x_{0}\}
,
\mu_{0}(x_{0})=1
and
\omega_{0}(x_{0},x_{0})=0
. Let
N\xrightarrow{\phi}N^{\prime}
be an expansive map. We define a natural transformation with component
\eta_{N}:\mathsf{F}(N)\to\mathsf{H}_{N_{0}}(N)
given by
\eta_{N}(x)=\phi_{x}
, where
\phi_{x}:X_{0}\to X
is defined by
\phi_{x}(x_{0})=x
; by our convention that network functions
\omega
are nonnegative-valued, this is an expansive map. One can check that the requisite diagram

\begin{CD}X@>{\eta_{N}}>{}>\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathrm{m}}(N_{0},N)\\
@V{\phi}V{}V@V{}V{\mathsf{H}_{N_{0}}(\phi)}V\\
X^{\prime}@>{\eta_{N^{\prime}}}>{}>\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathrm{m}}(N_{0},N^{\prime})\end{CD}

commutes. Moreover, the component maps are isomorphisms, so this proves that
\mathsf{H}_{N_{0}}
is representable. By
(
Borceux 1994
, Proposition 2.9.4)
,
\mathsf{F}
preserves limits.
∎

Proposition 21
.

The categories
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}
have countable nonempty products and coproducts, and pullbacks. The full subcategories
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}^{R}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}^{R}
containing measure (hyper)networks whose (hyper)network functions are uniformly bounded above by
R\geq 0
have all of the above, as well as a terminal object.

Proof.

We will first focus on the categories
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
and
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}^{R}
, as the hypernetwork constructions are conceptually the same, but with more complicated notation. We summarize the constructions for hypernetworks at the end of the proof.

Let
\{N_{j}=(X_{j},\mu_{j},\omega_{j})\}_{j}
be a countable collection of measure networks in
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
. A product
\Pi_{j}N_{j}
must have underlying set
\Pi_{j}X_{j}
(i.e., the product space), which is still a Polish space (with the product topology). Take the measure on the product to be any Borel probability measure (e.g., the product measure) and the network function
\omega_{\Pi_{j}N_{j}}
to be defined by

\omega_{\Pi_{j}N_{j}}((x_{1},x_{2},\ldots),(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots)):=\inf_{j}\omega_{j}(x_{j},y_{j}).

Then
\omega_{\Pi_{j}N_{j}}
is measurable: the map
((x_{i})_{i},(y_{i})_{i})\mapsto\omega_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})
is measurable for each
j
, so the infimum is measurable by
(
Folland 1999
, Proposition 2.7)
. We define maps
\Pi_{j}N_{j}\xrightarrow{\phi_{j}}N_{j}
to be coordinate projections; by construction of
\omega_{\Pi_{j}N_{j}}
, these are expansive maps. One can easily check that this construction has the universal property of a product.

To construct the coproduct
\bigsqcup_{j}N_{j}
, we take the underlying space to be the disjoint union
\bigsqcup_{j}X_{j}
, endowed with the disjoint union topology (which is Polish). The measure on the union is chosen to be an arbitrary Borel probability measure and the network function
\omega_{\sqcup_{j}N_{j}}
is defined by

\omega_{\sqcup_{j}N_{j}}(z,z^{\prime})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\omega_{\ell}(z,z^{\prime})&\mbox{if $z,z^{\prime}\in X_{\ell}$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

It is straightforward to check that
\omega_{\sqcup_{j}N_{j}}
is measurable and that the inclusion maps
i_{\ell}:X_{\ell}\to\bigsqcup_{j}X_{j}
are expansive. Once again, it is easy to check that this construction has the required universal property.

A pullback of a diagram
N_{1}\xrightarrow{\psi_{1}}N\xleftarrow{\psi_{2}}N_{2}
in
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
is given by the measure network
\overline{N}
with underlying set
\overline{X}:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in X_{1}\times X_{2}\mid\psi_{1}(x_{1})=\psi_{2}(x_{2})\}
, endowed with the natural topology, an arbitrary choice of Borel probability measure
\overline{\mu}
, and network function
\overline{\omega}
defined as in the product construction; that is,

\overline{\omega}\left((x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\right)=\min_{i}\omega_{i}(x_{i},y_{i}).

We define maps
\phi_{i}:\overline{X}\to X_{i}
to be coordinate projections. These maps are expansive, by construction, and it is straightforward to show that the universal property for pullbacks holds.

Observe that
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
has no terminal object: by Proposition
20
, a terminal object must have a singleton as its underlying set. Let
N
be such a measure network and suppose that
\omega(x,x)=R\geq 0
(with
x
the unique element in the underlying set). Then there is no expansive map from any network whose network function is not bounded above by
R
into
N
, so
N
cannot be a terminal object. On the other hand,
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}^{R}
has terminal object given by
(\{x\},\delta_{x},\omega_{R})
, where
\delta_{x}
is the Dirac measure and
\omega_{R}(x,x)=R
.

We now explain how to modify the constructions above to apply to measure hypernetworks. For a countable collection
\{H_{j}=(X_{j},\mu_{j},Y_{j},\nu_{j},\omega_{j})\}_{j}
of measure hypernetworks in
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{m}}
, a product is given by

\left(\Pi_{j}X_{j},\mu,\Pi_{j}Y_{j},\nu,\omega_{\Pi_{j}H_{j}}\right),

where
\Pi_{j}X_{j}
and
\Pi_{j}Y_{j}
are product spaces,
\mu
and
\nu
are arbitrary Borel probability measures and
\omega_{\Pi_{j}H_{j}}((x_{1},x_{2},\ldots),(y_{1},y_{2},\ldots))=\inf_{j}\omega_{j}(x_{j},y_{j})
. A coproduct is given by

\left(\bigsqcup_{j}X_{j},\mu,\bigsqcup_{j}Y_{j},\nu,\omega_{\sqcup_{j}H_{j}}\right),

where
\bigsqcup_{j}X_{j}
and
\bigsqcup_{j}Y_{j}
are disjoint unions endowed with compact Polish topologies,
\mu
and
\nu
are arbitrary Borel probability measures and

\omega_{\sqcup_{j}H_{j}}(w,z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\omega_{\ell}(w,z)&\mbox{if $w\in X_{\ell}$ and $z\in Y_{\ell}$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

The remaining constructions extend similarly.
∎

Remark 9
.

Suppose that
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
are measure network representations of combinatorial graphs, where the network functions are adjacency functions. The product network function then satisfies

\omega_{\Pi_{j}N_{j}}((x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2}))=1\Leftrightarrow\omega_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})=\omega_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})=1;

that is, the product has an edge if and only if there is an edge between each of the nodes in the corresponding graphs. This means that the categorical product in
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
is a generalization of the usual tensor product (or Kronecker product) of combinatorial graphs. Similarly, the categorical coproduct in
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
is a generalization of the disjoint union of two graphs.

Remark 10
.

By Proposition
20
, the underlying sets of limit constructions should agree with the usual constructions in
\mathcal{S}
. Since the categories considered in Proposition
21
do not contain objects whose underlying sets are empty, none of the categories has an initial object.

Remark 11
.

The question of whether the categories in Proposition
21
admit pushouts is not resolved. By Proposition
20
, if a pushout of a diagram
N_{1}\xleftarrow{\psi_{1}}N\xrightarrow{\psi_{2}}N_{2}
in
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
was given by a measure network
\widetilde{N}
, then the underlying set would have to be
X_{1}\sqcup X_{2}/\sim
, where
\sim
is the equivalence relation generated by
\psi_{1}(z)\sim\psi_{2}(z)
. However, the choice of topology is not clear: one can construct a diagram
N_{1}\xleftarrow{\psi_{1}}N\xrightarrow{\psi_{2}}N_{2}
in
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{m}}
such that the quotient topology on
X_{1}\sqcup X_{2}/\sim
is not Hausdorff, so cannot be Polish. Assuming for the moment that the quotient topology is Polish, we can proceed with the construction by choosing an arbitrary probability measure
\widetilde{\mu}
and defining the network function
\widetilde{\omega}
by

\widetilde{\omega}([x],[y])=\max\{\omega_{\ell}(x_{\ell},y_{\ell})\mid\exists\,x_{\ell}\in X_{\ell}\cap[x]\mbox{ and }y_{\ell}\in Y_{\ell}\cap[y],\,\ell=1,2\},

where we take the convention that
\max\emptyset=0
. The pushout maps
\phi_{i}:X_{i}\to\widetilde{X}
are given by
\phi_{i}(x)=[x]
.

Generally, it appears that pushouts exist in the case that
X_{1}\sqcup X_{2}/\sim
admits a Polish topology such that the function
\widetilde{\omega}
is Borel measurable. This is the case, for example, when all networks in the diagram have finite underlying sets. On the other hand, we were not able to produce any explicit counterexample, so the existence of pushouts is an open question.

On the other hand, the category
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
of measure networks with expansive couplings has fewer limit constructions, due to measure-theoretic issues.

Proposition 22
.

The categories
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{c}}
have initial objects. The full subcategories
\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}^{R}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{c}}^{R}
of compact measure (hyper)networks with (hyper)network functions uniformly bounded by

additionally have terminal objects. However, none of these categories admit products, coproducts, pullbacks or pushouts.

We will use a technical lemma.

Lemma 23
.

Let
\overline{N}\xrightarrow{\pi}N
be an expansive coupling of measure networks
\overline{N},N\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{c}}
such that there exists an expansive coupling
N\xrightarrow{\xi}\overline{N}
with
\pi\bullet\xi=\mathbbm{1}_{N}
. Then
\pi
is induced by a map
\phi:\overline{X}\to X
(i.e.
\pi=(\mathrm{id}_{\overline{X}}\times\phi)_{\#}\overline{\mu}
) and

\mathrm{supp}(\xi)=\{(\phi(z),z)\mid z\in\overline{X}\}.

Proof.

For the first claim, it suffices to show that for all
z\in\overline{X}
there is a unique
x\in X
such that
(z,x)\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi)
, and this follows by a similar argument to the one used in Part 2 of Proposition
17
. Let
\phi
denote the map which induces
\pi
and note that
\mathrm{supp}(\pi)=\{(z,\phi(z))\mid z\in\overline{X}\}
.

We now characterize the support of
\xi
. First, it must contain all points of the form
(\phi(z),z)
: if there exists
z\in\overline{X}
such that
(\phi(z),z)\not\in\mathrm{supp}(\xi)
, then
(\phi(z),\phi(z))
does not lie in the support of

, which is a contradiction. Conversely, suppose that
(x,z)\in\mathrm{supp}(\xi)

(x,\phi(z))\in\mathrm{supp}(\pi\bullet\xi)=\mathrm{supp}(\mathbbm{1}_{N})

.
∎

Proof of Proposition
22
.

Similar to proof of Proposition
21
, we focus on the categories of measure networks. All of the arguments and constructions generalize straightforwardly to the categories of measure hypernetworks.

Let
N_{R}=(\{x\},\delta_{x},\omega_{R})
, for

, be the measure network with
\delta_{x}
the Dirac measure and
\omega_{R}(x,x)=R

(Proposition

is a terminal object for

Next, we consider the case of products and show, in particular, that there is no product of a measure network

with itself, provided the cardinality of its underlying set is at least
2
. Suppose that there is such a product; we denote the product as
\overline{N}
and the projection maps onto the left and right factors as

for

, we deduce that the projections
\pi_{1}
and

\xi^{\prime}
of

and
\overline{\mu}
such that

∎

In summary, the similarity in structure between

and the category of sets

means that it enjoys many basic limit constructions. However, the ambivalence of expansive maps to topological and measure structure may be seen as undesirable; for example, this causes the issue with functoriality for the

leads to obstructions to many limit constructions. An interesting future direction of research will be to explore explore alternative category structures on measure networks which balance structure preservation and richness.

Soft Matching Between Hypergraphs

We first expand on Example
1
and give a refined method for generating a measure hypernetwork from a combinatorial hypergraph.

Before we begin, we recall various choices available when modeling a hypergraph as a hypernetwork.
Here we suppose that we are given a hypergraph
(X,Y)
and are tasked with obtaining a hypernetwork
(X,\mu,Y,\nu,\omega)
.

First we consider
\mu
. Let
x\in X
. Two simple choices for
\mu(x)
are as follows:

•

Uniform:
\mu(x):={1}/{\lvert X\rvert}.

•

Normalized node degree:
\mu(x):={\mathrm{deg}(x)}/{\sum_{x^{\prime}\in X}\mathrm{deg}(x^{\prime})}.

Next we consider
\nu
. Let
y\in Y
. Two simple choices for
\nu(y)
are as follows:

•

Uniform:
\nu(y):={1}/{\lvert Y\rvert}.

•

Normalized sum of node degree:

\nu(y):={\widehat{\nu}(y)}/{\sum_{y^{\prime}\in Y}\widehat{\nu}(y^{\prime})}
, where
\widehat{\nu}(y):=\sum_{x\in y}\mathrm{deg}(x).

The simplest choice for
\omega
is to use the incidence function
\omega(x,y)=1
if
x\in y
and
0
otherwise. Describing more interesting choices for
\omega
requires some setup. Recall the line graph construction in
Section 3.1
: a line graph
\mathsf{L}(H)
(in this section,
\mathsf{L}(H)
will be used generically as a stand in for several specific line graph constructions) is constructed from a combinatorial hypergraph
H=(X,Y)
by taking the node set of
\mathsf{L}(H)
to be
Y
, and adding an edge
\{y,y^{\prime}\}
whenever
y\cap y^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
—this coincides with the
\mathsf{L}_{\infty}
line graph map defined in
Section 3.6
. Moreover,
\mathsf{L}(H)
may be weighted—our line graph maps
\mathsf{L}_{q}

In the hypergraph modeling task discussed here, the line graph map is only an intermediate step and we have found empirically that different weighting schemes are more useful. To assign a hypernetwork function to

, we will use a metric structure on the line graph, where it is more useful to intuitively think of edge weights as conductances rather than impedances—that is, hyperedges that share more nodes are considered as more similar and should thus receive a smaller edge weight. With this in mind, we use edge weights corresponding to
intersection size

{1}/{\lvert y\cap y^{\prime}\rvert}
(if
y\cap y^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
) or

{\lvert y\cup y^{\prime}\rvert}/{\lvert y\cap y^{\prime}\rvert}
. Then, we may define the hypernetwork function as
\omega(x,y):=\min\{d_{\mathsf{L}(H)}(y^{\prime},y):x\in y^{\prime}\}
, where

denotes the shortest path metric with respect to the chosen edge weights. In summary, we consider three choices for
\omega
:

•

Intersection size:
\omega
is the shortest path distance based on the intersection size-weighted line graph.

•

Jaccard index:
\omega
is the shortest path distance based on the reciprocal Jaccard index-weighted line graph.

•

Incidence:
\omega(x,y)=1
if

and
0
otherwise.

5.3
Multiscale Graph Matching

In this section, we give a use case of hypergraph matching for multiscale graph matching. Multiscale structure has implicitly been shown to be instrumental in providing efficient proxies for Gromov-Wasserstein matching through low-rank approximations
Scetbon et al. 2022
, quantization
Chowdhury
et al. 2021
, and iterated clustering approaches
Xu et al. 2019a
;
Blumberg et al. 2020
. While these aforementioned works have focused on demonstrating performance benchmarks against a well-defined problem of matching point clouds or graphs, we focus on demonstrating a new use case toward
summarized
graph matching.

Our method proceeds by first constructing multiple levels of simplification for two graphs, and then performing graph matching within each level of simplification
while maintaining compatibility across levels of simplification
. Note that unless explicitly enforced, compatibility may be broken by the symmetries of the problem (e.g. a left limb being matched to a left limb at one scale and to a right limb at another scale). Hypergraph matching is specifically used to maintain the compatibility condition. Informally, we are summarizing our graph matching at multiple levels of granularity while maintaining consistency across summaries.

The first stage of our method is inspired by a construction known as the
multiscale mapper

Dey et al. 2016
. We begin by recalling some concepts related to the
mapper

Singh et al. 2007
construction. Given a topological space
X
and a finite open cover
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
X
, that is
X\subseteq\bigcup_{i\in I}U_{i}
, the
nerve
of
\mathcal{U}
is the simplicial complex
\mathsf{N}(\mathcal{U})
with vertices given by the index set
I
and subsets
\{i_{0},\ldots,i_{k}\}\subseteq I
forming
k
-simplices whenever
U_{i_{0}}\cap\ldots\cap U_{i_{k}}\neq\emptyset
, for any
k\geq 1
. The
nerve graph
is simply the 1-skeleton of this simplicial complex, and it is easier to compute in practice than the nerve complex because it involves checking only pairwise intersections. Next, given topological spaces
X
and
Y
, a finite open cover
\mathcal{V}
of
Y
, and a continuous map
f:X\to Y
, consider a pullback cover of
X
, defined as
f^{-1}(\mathcal{V})
. The mapper construction is simply the nerve of such a pullback cover, denoted as
\mathsf{N}(f^{-1}(\mathcal{V}))
. In practice, one often considers only the nerve graph, and refers to the simplified construction as a
mapper graph
.

The mapper graph provides a representation of data at a
single
scale controlled by the scale of the cover
\mathcal{V}
. The multiscale mapper was devised as a method for relating mapper graphs constructed at different scales. In this setup, the key idea is that of a map of covers. Let
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I},\mathcal{V}=\{V_{j}\}_{j\in J}
be two covers of a topological space
Z
. Then a set map
\xi:I\to J
is a
map of covers
if
U_{i}\subseteq V_{\xi(i)}
for all
i\in I
. The multiscale mapper construction utilizes the observation
Dey et al. 2016
that a map of covers induces a map of pullback covers, and thus induces a map between mapper graphs at different scales.

Step 1: multiscale reduction

With the mapper context in place, we now present the first stage of our multiscale graph matching method. Given a graph
G=(V,E)
, we first use a data-driven method (described below) to compute an overlapping cover
\mathcal{U}=\{U_{i}\}_{i\in I}
of
V
and its nerve graph
G_{1}
. We then repeat the process on the new nerve graph
G_{1}
, obtaining a reduced graph at each step of the iteration,
G_{1},G_{2},\dots
, etc. We terminate the reduction process after the number of nodes in the graph falls below a threshold
n_{\alpha}
. For a multiscale graph matching problem, we compute these reductions for each graph and pass the collections of nerve graphs into the second stage of the method.

To compute a data-driven cover, we use a procedure involving the graph heat kernel. The heat kernel and related spectral constructions are well-known for capturing shape signatures
Reuter et al. 2006
;
Mémoli 2011b
, and thus help produce semantically meaningful cover elements. Moreover, heat kernels have been shown to produce superior performance in GW graph matching applications
Chowdhury and
Needham 2021
, and thus we are able to recycle computational elements across both stages of our multiscale graph matching method. Our exact procedure is described next.

Fix a graph
G
with node set
V
. Initialize a set of visited nodes
U=\emptyset
as well as a cover
Y=\emptyset
. Also fix a node
x\in V\setminus U
. Let
L
denote the normalized graph Laplacian of
G
. We first compute its eigendecomposition
\Phi\Lambda\Phi^{T}
. Computing a full eigendecomposition is an
O(n^{3})
operation, so for large graphs we limit runtime by using Lanczos iterations to obtain extremal eigenpairs and using these to construct a low-rank factorization of
L
. In our experiments, we have found the top 300 largest eigenvalues and their eigenvectors to provide good results while keeping runtimes low (e.g. below 1 minute for a graph having over 15K nodes). Next, note that for a given
t>0
, we may compute the graph heat kernel
K^{t}:=\operatorname{exp}(-tL)
as
K^{t}=\Phi\operatorname{exp}(-t\Lambda)\Phi^{T}
. Given a Dirac delta vector
\delta_{x}
, the matrix-vector product
v:=K^{t}\delta_{x}
can be interpreted as the diffusion of a unit mass of heat out from
x
within time
t
, and essentially has the form of a Gaussian centered at
x
. Following the idea of full width at half maximum (FWHM), we mark the set
\{v\geq\max(v)/2\}
as visited, and set
U\leftarrow U\cup\{v\geq\max(v)/2\}
. Additionally we set
Y\leftarrow Y\cup\{\{v\geq\max(v)/4\}\}.
We then select another
x\in V\setminus U
and iterate the procedure until
U=V
. Note that the use of
U
prevents us from sampling graph nodes too densely, and the extra
1/2
multiplicative factor in
Y
allows us to enforce overlaps between cover elements. Also note that the choice of
t
is difficult to prescribe
a priori
. In our experiments, we found
t:=\log_{10}(|V|)
to produce good results. Note from this choice that we utilize different values of
t
at each reduction step.

In summary, the multiscale reduction step iterates a particular technique of reducing a graph that factors through a process of computing covers and taking nerves, as illustrated in

Step 2: Hypergraph matching

We now describe the graph matching stage of our method.
A cover

Consequently, the iterated nerve construction described above provides a sequence of hypergraphs where the hyperedges of one hypergraph comprise the nodes of the next. We will first describe our method for graph matching that utilizes these hypergraphs, and then provide some comparison to alternative methods.

Fix a method for converting a hypergraph to a hypernetwork. Let

be two graphs. We first apply the iterated nerve construction described above for obtaining sequences of hypergraphs, and then the method for passing from hypergraphs to hypernetworks. Let

\pi_{i}
between
\mu_{i},\mu^{\prime}_{i}

\xi_{i}

2k

We now describe some design considerations that went into our method. An alternative form of multiscale graph matching could be carried out by: (1) iteratively applying any black-box graph simplification method, and (2) applying any black-box graph matching method along each level of simplification. The second step of such a method benefits from being embarrassingly parallel, as the graph matchings across different levels of simplification are independent. As a consequence of this independence, however, such a method is unable to enforce consistency of correspondences across different levels of simplification. In contrast, our method utilizes a careful choice of a graph simplification method that immediately suggests an application of hypergraph matching. While hypergraph matching across different levels of simplification could be solved in a parallel, independent manner, we choose to create dependence by enforcing the
\pi_{i+1}=\xi_{i}
condition. Thus we trade off performance (via parallelization) for consistency of matching across scales.

6
Conclusion

We view this work to be the first in a landscape of new research directions in hypergraph data analysis. In analogy with the measure network setting, we expect that future work could further develop the geometry of hypergraph space, and utilize this study to obtain methods for carrying out geometric statistics (e.g. Fréchet means) on this space.
From the applied perspective, we expect that future work could focus on applications to machine learning problems as well as scalability and deployment into deep learning pipelines. Integrating GW distance computation as part of the hypergraph visual analytic tool
Zhou et al. 2022
would be of interest as well.
Finally, the study of morphisms between network and hypernetwork categories initiated in this work suggests new questions about obtaining families of Lipschitz maps and further understanding existing graphification methods from a categorical perspective. For example, a long term project would be to classify all Lipschitz, functorial graphifications satisfying reasonable axioms—Proposition
15
gives a first step in this direction. It will also be interesting to consider metric and categorical aspects of maps from networks to hypernetworks, or multiscale versions thereof, building on the already substantial literature on the category theory of data partitioning.

and
[H^{\prime}]
, following the main idea of the proof of
(
Sturm 2012
, Theorem 3.1)
. Let
\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and
\xi\in\mathcal{C}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})
be optimal couplings (which exist, by Lemma
24
). Consider the path
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{H}
defined by

\gamma(t)=\left(\mathrm{supp}(\pi),\pi,\mathrm{supp}(\xi),\xi,\omega_{t}\right),

(32)

where

\omega_{t}((x,x^{\prime}),(y,y^{\prime}))=(1-t)\omega(x,y)+t\omega^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}).

Observe that
\gamma(0)\in[H]
and
\gamma(1)\in[H^{\prime}]
; indeed, coordinate projection maps
\phi:X\times X^{\prime}\to X
and
\psi:Y\times Y^{\prime}\to Y
define a basic weak isomorphism
\gamma(0)\to H
and a similar construction works for
\gamma(1)\to H^{\prime}
. To show that
\gamma
defines a geodesic in
[\mathcal{H}]
, it suffices to show that for all
0\leq s\leq t\leq 1
,

d_{\mathcal{H},p}(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))\leq(t-s)d_{\mathcal{H},p}(H,H^{\prime})

(33)

(see, e.g.,
(
Chowdhury and
Mémoli 2018
, Lemma 1.3)
). One can show by an elementary computation that

\mathrm{dis}_{\gamma(s),\gamma(t),p}^{\mathcal{H}}(\pi,\xi)^{p}\leq(t-s)^{p}\mathrm{dis}_{H,H^{\prime},p}^{\mathcal{H}}(\pi,\xi)^{p}

for any pair of couplings
(\pi,\xi)
—indeed, this holds because linear interpolations are geodesics in
L^{p}
spaces—and (
33
) follows.

∎

Remark 12
.

The proof of the triangle inequality above uses the notion of gluing, which appears in our category theoretic framework in
Section 3
. The coupling
\pi_{13}
constructed above is written as
\pi_{13}=\pi_{23}\bullet\pi_{13}
in the notation from
Section 3
. A byproduct of our proof is the following useful fact: for measure networks
N,N^{\prime},N^{\prime\prime}
and couplings
\pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})
and
\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(\mu^{\prime},\mu^{\prime\prime})
, we have

\mathrm{dis}_{p}^{\mathcal{N}}(\pi^{\prime}\bullet\pi)\leq\mathrm{dis}_{p}^{\mathcal{N}}(\pi^{\prime})+\mathrm{dis}_{p}^{\mathcal{N}}(\pi).

A similar statement holds in the measure hypernetwork setting.

Proof of Lemma
12
.

We will prove the statement for hypernetworks, as the network case is similar. Let
\pi
and
\xi
be couplings realizing
d_{\mathcal{H},p}(H,H^{\prime})
(such couplings always exist, see Lemma
24
in the appendix). Define

\overline{X}=\mathrm{supp}(\pi)\subset X\times X^{\prime},\quad\overline{\mu}=\pi\mid_{\overline{X}},\quad\overline{Y}=\mathrm{supp}(\xi)\subset Y\times Y^{\prime},\quad\overline{\nu}=\xi\mid_{\overline{Y}}

and define hypernetwork functions
\overline{\omega},\overline{\omega}^{\prime}:\overline{X}\times\overline{Y}\to{\mathbb{R}}
as follows. Let
p_{Z}:X\times X^{\prime}\to Z
be coordinate projection for
Z\in\{X,X^{\prime}\}
and let
p_{Z}:Y\times Y^{\prime}\to Z
be coordinate projection for
Z\in\{Y,Y^{\prime}\}
. The hypernetwork functions are then given by

\overline{\omega}=\omega\circ(p_{X}\times p_{Y})\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\overline{\omega}^{\prime}=\omega^{\prime}\circ(p_{X^{\prime}}\times p_{Y^{\prime}}).

The coordinate projections are measure-preserving, due to the marginal constraints of
\pi
and
\xi
, and the pair
(p_{X},p_{Y})
(respectively,
(p_{X^{\prime}},p_{Y^{\prime}})
) therefore defines a basic weak isomorphism (Definition
6
) from
\overline{H}
to
H
(respectively,
\overline{H^{\prime}}
to
H^{\prime}
).

To see (
12
), observe first that
d_{\mathcal{H},p}(H,H^{\prime})=d_{\mathcal{H},p}(\overline{H},\overline{H^{\prime}})
follows by weak isomorphism. To see the other equality for
p\in[1,\infty)
, we have

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{H},p}(H,H^{\prime})^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{Y\times Y^{\prime}}\int_{X\times X^{\prime}}\lvert\omega(x,y)-\omega^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\rvert^{p}\;\pi(dx\times dx^{\prime})\xi(dy\times dy^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{\overline{Y}}\int_{\overline{X}}\lvert\omega(x,y)-\omega^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\rvert^{p}\;\overline{\mu}(dx\times dx^{\prime})\overline{\nu}(dy\times dy^{\prime}),

since the value of the integral isn’t changed by replacing the full spaces with supports of the measures. Using the change of variables
\overline{x}=(x,x^{\prime})\in\overline{X}
and
\overline{y}=(y,y^{\prime})\in\overline{Y}
, the above is equal to

\int_{\overline{Y}}\int_{\overline{X}}\lvert\overline{\omega}(\overline{x},\overline{y})-\overline{\omega}^{\prime}(\overline{x},\overline{y})\rvert^{p}\;\overline{\mu}(d\overline{x})\overline{\nu}(d\overline{y})=\|\overline{\omega}-\overline{\omega}^{\prime}\|_{L^{p}(\overline{\mu}\otimes\overline{\nu})}^{p}.

For the
p=\infty
case, a similar change of variables gives

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{H},\infty}(H,H^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\inf\{c\mid\lvert\omega(x,y)-\omega^{\prime}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\rvert\leq c\mbox{ for $\pi\otimes\xi$-almost every $(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})$}\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{c\mid\lvert\overline{\omega}(\overline{x},\overline{y})-\overline{\omega}^{\prime}(\overline{x},\overline{y})\rvert\leq c\mbox{ for $\overline{\mu}\otimes\overline{\xi}$-almost every $(\overline{x},\overline{y})$}\}

\displaystyle=\|\overline{\omega}-\overline{\omega}^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\overline{\mu}\times\overline{\nu})}.

∎

Proof of Lemma

.

By Proposition
2
, it suffices to show that if there is a basic weak isomorphism
(\phi,\psi)
from
H
to
H^{\prime}
, then
\phi
defines a basic weak isomorphism of
N:=\mathsf{Q}_{q}(H)=(X,\mu,\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{q}})
and
N^{\prime}:=\mathsf{Q}_{q}(H^{\prime})=(X^{\prime},\mu^{\prime},\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{q}}^{\prime})
. By an argument similar to the one used to prove functoriality in Theorem
11
, the assumption that
(\phi,\psi)
is a basic weak isomorphism implies that for
\mu\otimes\mu\otimes\nu
-almost every
(x_{1},x_{2},y)\in X\times X\times Y
,
\min_{j}\omega(x_{j},y)=\min_{j}\omega^{\prime}(\phi(x_{j}),\psi(y))
. In the
q<\infty
case, we deduce that for
\mu\otimes\mu
almost every
(x_{1},x_{2})
,

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathsf{Q}_{q}}^{\prime}(\phi(x_{1}),\phi(x_{2}))^{p}

\displaystyle=\int_{Y^{\prime}}\min_{j}\omega^{\prime}(\phi(x_{j}),y^{\prime})^{p}\;\nu^{\prime}(dy^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{Y}\min_{j}\omega^{\prime}(\phi(x_{j}),\psi(y))^{p}\;\nu(dy)

where (

) follows by the change-of-variables formula (using that
