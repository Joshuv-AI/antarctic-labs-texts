Chaos for generalized Black-Scholes equations
Anna Maria Candela Gisèle Ruiz Goldstein, Jerome A. Goldstein Silvia Romanelli

Abstract
The Nobel Prize winning Black-Scholes equation for stock options and the heat equation can both be written in the form ∂ u ∂ t = P 2 ​ ( A ) ​ u , \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=P_{2}(A)u, where P 2 ​ ( z ) = α ​ z 2 + β ​ z + γ P_{2}(z)=\alpha z^{2}+\beta z+\gamma is a quadratic polynomial with α > 0 \alpha>0 . In fact, taking A = x ​ ∂ ∂ x A=x\frac{\partial}{\partial x} on functions on [ 0 , ∞ ) × [ 0 , ∞ ) [0,\infty)\times[0,\infty) the previous equality reduces to the Black-Scholes equation, while taking A = ∂ ∂ x A=\frac{\partial}{\partial x} for functions on ℝ × [ 0 , ∞ ) \mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty) it becomes the heat equation. Here, we “connect” the two previous problems by considering the generalized operator A = x a ​ ∂ ∂ x A=x^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial x} for functions on [ 0 , ∞ ) × [ 0 , ∞ ) [0,\infty)\times[0,\infty) with 0 < a < 1 0<a<1 , and our main result is that the corresponding degenerate parabolic equation is governed by a semigroup of operators which is chaotic on a class of Banach spaces. The relevant Banach spaces are weighted supremum norm spaces of continuous functions on [ 0 , ∞ ) [0,\infty) . This paper unifies, simplifies and significantly extends earlier results obtained for the Black-Scholes equation ( a = 1 a=1 ) in [ 3 ] and the heat equation ( a = 0 a=0 ) in [ 4 ] .

Introduction

In 2012 H. Emamirad, G. R. Goldstein and J. A. Goldstein in
[
3
]

proved that the Nobel Prize winning stock options research of the 1970s
led to a second order linear parabolic partial differential equation that is
governed by a one parameter semigroup of bounded linear operators which is
chaotic on certain weighted supremum norm Banach spaces.
The equation, usually called the Black-Scholes equation,
originally introduced
by F. Black, R. C. Merton and M. Scholes in
[
1
,
10
]
, is

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}x^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}+rx\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}-ru,

(1.1)

for
u(x,t)
, a function on
[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)
.
This function
u(x,t)
is the price
of the stock option when the price of the stock is
x

and
t
represents time.
The relevant initial condition in Economics
for (
1.1
) is

u(x,t)=(x-p)_{+}

where
p>0
is the strike price. Note that this initial condition forces us to focus on unbounded functions.

In
[
9
]
the authors gave a simple explicit representation
of the solutions of an abstract Cauchy problem associated with (
1.1
)

In
[
3
]
the authors noted that (
1.1
) can be written as

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=P_{2}(A)u

(1.2)

where
P_{2}(z)
is the real quadratic polynomial

P_{2}(z)=\alpha z^{2}+\beta z+\gamma
,
\alpha>0
,
and with
A=x\frac{\partial}{\partial x}

for functions on
[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)

and appropriate choices of
\alpha
,
\beta
and
\gamma

which are related to the volatility and the interest rate.

After the first systematic study of the notion of chaotic
(C_{0})
semigroup
in
[
2
]
, in
[
4
]
Emamirad, G. Goldstein and J. Goldstein proved that the heat
equation was chaotic on certain weighted sup norm spaces
by taking
A=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
in (
1.2
)
for functions on
\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty)
.

More recently in
[
6
]
,
under suitable assumptions,
the property of chaos was also faced in the
framework of a nonautonomous generalized version of (
1.1
).

Here we extend the autonomous theory to

A=\nu x^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}

for functions on
[0,\infty)\times[0,\infty)

with
0<a<1
,
\nu\neq 0
.
In this case the corresponding partial differential
equation (
1.2
) becomes

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\ =\nu^{2}x^{2a}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}+\nu^{2}ax^{2a-1}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\beta\nu x^{a}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\gamma u,\quad x>0,\,t\geq 0,

with
\alpha=1
and
\beta
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
.
To keep the connection with the Black-Scholes problem (corresponding to
a=1
),
we work on spaces of unbounded functions.

By understanding all underlying principles more clearly,
we can simplify and clarify the earlier methods.
Still, our proof is quite complicated and based
on a lot of heavy machinery developed by others.
Thus we hope our new and relatively
selfcontained approach will attract mathematicians
to use these new tools and advance rigorous mathematical finance even further.

2
Properties of the Underlying Group and Operator

Let
X
be a complex Banach space equipped with the supremum norm
\|\cdot\|
,

\mathbb{R}^{+}=[0,\infty)
and let
S(t)\in\mathcal{L}(X)

(the Banach space of all bounded linear operators on
X
) satisfy

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}S(t+s)=S(t)S(s)&\hbox{for all $t,s\in\mathbb{R}^{+}$},\\
S(0)=I.\\
\end{array}\right.

(2.1)

Then
S=\{S(t):t\geq 0\}
is an (operator) semigroup.
A semigroup is
strongly continuous

(called a
(C_{0})
semigroup
)
if
S(t)f
is a continuous function of
t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
for all
f\in X
.
If (
2.1
) and strong continuity hold
for all
t,s\in\mathbb{R}
, then
S
is a
(C_{0})
group
.

For the remainder of the paper,
\nu>0
and
0<a<1
are fixed.

We begin by studying the first order Cauchy problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\nu x^{a}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}&\hbox{if $x>0,\;t\in\mathbb{R}$,}\\
u(x,0)=f(x)&\hbox{if $x>0$.}\end{array}\right.

(2.2)

If we define the operator
A
formally by

A\ =\ \nu x^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial x},

(2.3)

then the initial value problem (
2.2
) can be written in abstract form as

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=Au\\
u(0)=f\end{array}\right.

where
f
is a
C^{1}
function on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
.

Define

S(t)f(x)\ :=\ f(|x^{1-a}+\nu(1-a)t|^{\frac{1}{1-a}}).

(2.4)

The next lemma proves that
S=\{S(t):t\in\mathbb{R}\}
defined via
(
2.4
) is a group.

Lemma 2.1
.

The unique solution of (
2.2
) is

u(x,t)=f(|x^{1-a}+\nu(1-a)t|^{\frac{1}{1-a}})=S(t)f(x).

(2.5)

Proof.

Let
x>0
and, taking for simplicity
y=x^{1-a}+\nu(1-a)t
,
from (
2.5
) and direct computations we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu f^{\prime}(|y|^{\frac{1}{1-a}})|y|^{\frac{a}{1-a}}sgn(y),

(2.6)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(|y|^{\frac{1}{1-a}})|y|^{\frac{a}{1-a}}sgn(y)x^{-a},

whence

\nu x^{a}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}=\nu f^{\prime}(|y|^{\frac{1}{1-a}})|y|^{\frac{a}{1-a}}sgn(y).

(2.7)

Thus,
\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\nu x^{a}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}

holds for all
x
,
t
such that
y\neq 0
.
Furthermore, by (
2.6
),

\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=0

because
f^{\prime}(0)
exists and
|y|^{\frac{a}{1-a}}\to 0

as
y\to 0
since
0<a<1
. Similarly, by (
2.7
),

\lim_{y\to 0^{+}}\left(\nu x^{a}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\right)=0

and the same limits equal to 0 hold also
if
y\to 0^{-}
.
Thus
y=0
is a removable singularity for

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}-\nu x^{a}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}
,
so this function can be viewed as a continuous function of
y

for
y\in\mathbb{R}
, equal to 0 if
y=0
,
and so also a continuous function of
(x,t)
in
\mathbb{R}^{*}\times\mathbb{R}
.
Uniqueness of the initial value problem gives us that
S
is a group.
∎

We now introduce the relevant spaces for our study.
For any
s\geq 0
, define

Y_{s}\ =\ \left\{f\in C(\mathbb{R}^{+}):\,\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{|f(x)|}{(1+{\rm e}^{sx^{1-a}})(1+{\rm e}^{-sx^{1-a}})}\ =\ 0\right\}

equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{s}\ =\ \sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}}\frac{|f(x)|}{(1+{\rm e}^{sx^{1-a}})(1+{\rm e}^{-sx^{1-a}})}\ <\ \infty.

Lemma 2.2
.

The operators
S=\{S(t):t\in\mathbb{R}\}
defined by (
2.4
)
form a one parameter quasicontractive group on
Y_{s}
, with

\|S(t)f\|_{s}\ \leq\ {\rm e}^{\omega|t|}\ \|f\|_{s}

(2.8)

for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
f\in Y_{s}
,
where
\omega:=s\nu(1-a)
.

Proof.

First we note that infinitesimal generator of
S
is
formally
A=\nu x^{a}\frac{d}{dx}
since

\begin{split}Af(x)&=\frac{d}{dt}S(t)f(x)|_{t=0}\\
&=\nu x^{a}f^{\prime}(x),\end{split}

Clearly
f\in\mathcal{D}(A)
requires that both
f
and
x^{a}f^{\prime}

are in
Y_{s}
; we will characterize the domain later. That
S
is group follows
from Lemma
2.1
.

To prove the quasicontractivity, we look at

\|S(t)f\|_{s}=\sup_{x>0}\frac{|(S(t)f)(x)|}{(1+{\rm e}^{sx^{1-a}})\ (1+{\rm e}^{-sx^{1-a}})}=\sup_{x>0}\ \frac{|f(|x^{1-a}+\nu(1-a)t|^{\frac{1}{1-a}})|}{(1+{\rm e}^{sx^{1-a}})\ (1+{\rm e}^{-sx^{1-a}})}.

Set

y\ =\ |x^{1-a}+\nu(1-a)t|^{\frac{1}{1-a}}.

We have three cases to consider.

Case 1.

Let
t\geq 0
.
Then

y\ =\ (x^{1-a}+\nu(1-a)t)^{\frac{1}{1-a}},

(2.9)

and so

{\rm e}^{sx^{1-a}}={\rm e}^{sy^{(1-a)}}e^{-s\nu(1-a)t}

and
t\nu>0
.
Then

\begin{split}\|S(t)f\|_{s}\ &=\ \sup_{y>0}\frac{|f(y)|}{(1+{\rm e}^{sy^{1-a}}\ {\rm e}^{-\omega t})\ (1+{\rm e}^{-sy^{1-a}}\ {\rm e}^{\omega t})}\\
&\leq\ \sup_{y>0}\frac{|f(y)|}{{\rm e}^{-\omega t}({\rm e}^{\omega t}+{\rm e}^{sy^{1-a}})\ (1+{\rm e}^{-sy^{1-a}})}\ \leq\ \sup_{y>0}{\rm e}^{\omega t}\ \frac{|f(y)|}{(1+{\rm e}^{sy^{1-a}})\ (1+{\rm e}^{-sy^{1-a}})}\\
&\leq\ {\rm e}^{\omega t}\ \|f\|_{s}\end{split}

by our choice of
\omega
, and so the estimate (
2.8
) holds.

Case 2.
Let
t<0
but
x^{1-a}+\nu(1-a)t\geq 0
.
In this case,
y
is still given by (
2.9
) but
t\nu<0
.
Then we have

\begin{split}\|S(t)f\|_{s}\ &\leq\ \sup_{y>0}\frac{|f(y)|}{{\rm e}^{\omega t}(1+{\rm e}^{sy^{1-a}})\ ({\rm e}^{-\omega t}+{\rm e}^{-sy^{1-a}})}\ \leq\ \sup_{y>0}{\rm e}^{-\omega t}\ \frac{|f(y)|}{(1+{\rm e}^{sy^{1-a}})\ (1+{\rm e}^{-sy^{1-a}})}\\
&\leq\ {\rm e}^{\omega|t|}\ \|f\|_{s}.\end{split}

and so (
2.8
) holds.

Case 3.
Let
t<0
and
x^{1-a}+\nu(1-a)t\leq 0
.
In this case
t\nu<0
and
y
is given by

y^{1-a}=-x^{1-a}-\nu(1-a)t,

and so

x^{1-a}=-y^{1-a}-\nu(1-a)t.

Then

\begin{split}\|S(t)f\|_{s}&\leq\ \sup_{y>0}\frac{|f(y)|}{{\rm e}^{\omega t}(1+{\rm e}^{-sy^{1-a}})\ ({\rm e}^{-\omega t}+{\rm e}^{sy^{1-a}})}\ \leq\ \sup_{y>0}{\rm e}^{-\omega t}\ \frac{|f(y)|}{(1+{\rm e}^{-sy^{1-a}})\ (1+{\rm e}^{sy^{1-a}})}\\
&\leq\ {\rm e}^{\omega|t|}\ \|f\|_{s}.\end{split}

which is again (
2.8
).
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

S=\{S(t):t\in\mathbb{R}\}
is a
(C_{0})
group on
Y_{s}
,
that is for any
f\in Y_{s}
,

\|S(t)f-f\|_{s}\ \to\ 0\quad\hbox{as $t\to 0$.}

(2.10)

Proof.

Because
C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
, the space of continuously differentiable
functions with compact support on
\mathbb{R}^{+}
, is dense in
Y_{s}
,
it is sufficient to prove the statement on
C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
.
Note that functions in
C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
need not vanish
at the origin.
Thus, taking
f\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
, we have that

{\rm supp}(f)\subseteq[0,M]
for some
M<\infty
.
We see that

\begin{split}\|S(t)f-f\|_{s}\ &=\sup_{x\in[0,M]}\ \frac{|f(|x^{1-a}+\nu(1-a)t|^{\frac{1}{1-a}})-f(x)|}{(1+{\rm e}^{sx^{1-a}})\ (1+{\rm e}^{-sx^{1-a}})}\\
&\leq\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\sup_{x\in[0,M]}\ \frac{||x^{1-a}+\nu(1-a)t|^{\frac{1}{1-a}}-x|}{(1+{\rm e}^{sx^{1-a}})\ (1+{\rm e}^{-sx^{1-a}})}\\
&\leq C\|f^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\sup_{x\in[0,M]}\ \frac{||x^{1-a}+\nu(1-a)t|^{{\frac{1}{1-a}}}-x|}{1+{\rm e}^{sx^{1-a}}}.\end{split}

But the function
|x^{1-a}+\nu(1-a)t|^{{\frac{1}{1-a}}}

is uniformly continuous on
[0,M]\times[0,1]
,
so as
t
approaches
0
,

||x^{1-a}+\nu(1-a)t|^{\frac{1}{1-a}}-x|\rightarrow 0
uniformly
in
t
for
x\in[0,M]
. Hence, (
2.10
) holds.
∎

(2.11)

3
The Chaotic Semigroup on the Space
Y_{s}

In this section we prove that the semigroup associated with
the operator
A
is chaotic. We begin by defining the notion
of chaos for semigroups and then introduce
an essential tool, the Godefroy-Shapiro Criterion
[
5
]
,
for proving chaos in our context.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
T=\{T(t):t\geq 0\}
be a
(C_{0})
semigroup
on a separable infinite dimensional Banach space
X
.
The semigroup
T
is called
hypercyclic

if there exists some
f\in X
whose orbit
\{T(t)f:\,t\geq 0\}
is dense in
X
.

Definition 3.2
.

A semigroup
T

is called
chaotic
if it is hypercyclic and
has a dense set of periodic orbits, that is,

P:=\{f\in X:\,\hbox{some $t_{0}>0$ exists so that}\,T(t_{0})f=f\}

is dense in
X
.

Theorem 3.3
.

(Godefroy-Shapiro Criterion
[
5
]
)
Let
L
be a bounded linear operator on a separable infinite dimensional Banach space
X
.
Let
Q_{1}
,
Q_{2}
be dense subspaces of
X

and let
Z:Q_{1}\to Q_{2}
be so that the following
properties hold:

(i)

\;LZy=y
for all
y\in Q_{1}
,

(ii)

\;\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}Z^{n}y=0

for all
y\in Q_{1}
,

(iii)

\;\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}L^{n}x=0
for all
x\in Q_{2}
.

Then
L
is hypercyclic, that is,

\{L^{n}x:\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is dense in
X
for some
x\in X
.

The following lemma holds by the argument in

[
3
, Lemma 2.2]
with trivial modifications.

Lemma 3.4
.

The dual space
(Y_{s})^{*}
is

(Y_{s})^{*}=\{\psi\in\mathcal{M}_{loc}[0,\infty):\eta(dx)=(1+{\rm e}^{sx^{1-a}})^{-1}(1+{\rm e}^{-sx^{1-a}})^{-1}\psi(dx)\in\mathcal{M}[0,\infty)\}

where
\mathcal{M}[0,\infty)
is the set of all finite complex Borel measures on
[0,\infty)
.

We are now ready to apply these tools to our setting.

Denote by

\mathscr{S}_{s}\ =\ \{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\,0<Re(\lambda)<s(1-a)\nu\}

the open strip in
\mathbb{C}
and take

\varphi_{\lambda}(x)\ =\ {\rm e}^{\frac{\lambda x^{1-a}}{\nu(1-a)}},\quad x\geq 0.

Then
\varphi_{\lambda}
is well defined
and in
C(\mathbb{R}^{+})
for any
\lambda\in\mathscr{S}_{s}

(in fact for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
).

Lemma 3.5
.

The mapping
\lambda\mapsto\varphi_{\lambda}
is analytic
from the strip
\mathscr{S}_{s}

to
Y_{s}
.

Proof.

Since weak analyticity is equivalent to analyticity,
it suffices to show weak analyticity, i.e., to show that

\lambda\ \mapsto\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\varphi_{\lambda}(x)\ \psi(dx)

is analytic for any
\psi\in F\subseteq\mathcal{M}[0,\infty)
,
where
F
is any norm determining subset of the
dual space
(Y_{s})^{*}
. Here we use

F\ :=\ \{c\delta_{x}:\,c\in\mathbb{C},\,x\in[0,\infty)\},

where
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac point mass measure at
x
.
Note that

\|f\|_{s}\ =\ \sup\big\{|cf(x)|=|\langle f,\psi\rangle|:\,\psi=c\delta_{x},\,c\in\mathbb{C},\,x\in[0,\infty),\,\|\psi\|_{(Y_{s})^{*}}=1\big\}.

A choice of
c
that works is

c\ =\ \frac{1}{(1+{\rm e}^{sx_{*}^{1-a}})\ (1+{\rm e}^{-sx_{*}^{1-a}})},

where
x_{*}
is a supremum point for
f
, i.e.,

\|f\|_{s}\ =\ |cf(x_{*})|.

Since

\varphi_{\lambda}(x)=\langle\varphi_{\lambda},\delta_{x}\rangle

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, we have that

\lambda\ \mapsto\ {\rm e}^{\frac{\lambda}{\nu(1-a)}x^{1-a}}\ =\ \langle\varphi_{\lambda},\delta_{x}\rangle

is an entire function of
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
for all
x\geq 0
.
For
\lambda\in\mathscr{S}_{s}
, we have that

\begin{split}\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{|\varphi_{\lambda}(x)|}{(1+{\rm e}^{sx^{1-a}})\ (1+{\rm e}^{-sx^{1-a}})}\ &=\ \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{{\rm e}^{\frac{Re(\lambda)}{\nu(1-a)}x^{1-a}}}{(1+{\rm e}^{sx^{1-a}})\ (1+{\rm e}^{-sx^{1-a}})}\\
&=\ \lim_{x\to\infty}{\rm e}^{(\frac{Re(\lambda)}{\nu(1-a)}-s)x^{1-a}}\ =\ 0\end{split}

since
Re(\lambda)<s(1-a)\nu
.
Hence
\varphi_{\lambda}\in Y_{s}
.
Here we used the obvious fact that if
\varphi_{\lambda}\in Y_{s}
,
then
\varphi_{\lambda}\in Y_{\tau}
for all
\tau\geq s
.
∎

Consider the polynomial

p(z)=z^{2}+\beta z+\gamma

(3.1)

for any given
\beta
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
.
The operator
A

generates
S={\{S(t):t\in\mathbb{R}}\}
, a
(C_{0})

quasicontractive group on
Y_{s}
. We define
the operator

B:=p(A)=A^{2}+\beta A+\gamma I.

By Theorem
2.5
,
B
generates
an analytic semigroup of angle
\frac{\pi}{2}
on
Y_{s}
.
The semigroup
T=\{T(t):t\in\mathbb{C},Re(t)>0\}\cup\{0\}

generated by
B
is given by

T(t)\ :=\ h_{t}(A),

where

h_{t}(z)={\rm e}^{tp(z)},\quad z\in\mathbb{C},\;Re(t)\geq 0,

(3.2)

with
p(z)
as in (
3.1
).

Our goal is to prove
T
is chaotic on
Y_{s}
for some
s>0
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Suppose that there exists an open connected set

\Omega\subset\mathscr{S}_{s}

which has an accumulation point in
\mathscr{S}_{s}
.
Then the subspace

Q\ =\ {\rm span}\{\varphi_{\lambda}:\ \lambda\in\Omega\}

is dense in
Y_{s}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\psi\in Q^{\perp}
.
Since
\varphi_{\lambda}\in Y_{s}
and
\psi\in(Y_{s})^{*}
,
it follows that

q(\lambda)\ =\ \langle\varphi_{\lambda},\psi\rangle

is well defined and analytic in
\mathscr{S}_{s}
.
But
q(\lambda)=0
for any
\lambda\in\Omega

and
\Omega
has an accumulation point of such points
\lambda
in
\mathscr{S}_{s}
.
Hence,
q(\lambda)=0
in all of
\mathscr{S}_{s}

by the analytic continuation theorem, and so
\psi\equiv 0
.
This proves the assertion.
∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\mathbb{T}
be the unit circle in
\mathbb{C}
and consider
the polynomial
p(z)
in (
3.1
) with
\beta
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
and
h_{t}(z)
as in (
3.2
) for any fixed
t>0
.
Then

h_{t}(\mathscr{S}_{s})\cap\mathbb{T}\neq\emptyset

(3.3)

and
h_{t}(\mathscr{S}_{s})\cap\mathbb{T}

has infinitely many accumulation points in
\mathscr{S}_{s}
for
s\ >\ s^{*}
where

\quad s^{*}\ :=\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{-\beta+\sqrt{\beta^{2}-4\gamma}}{2\nu(1-a)}&\hbox{if $\gamma<\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}$ and
$\ \sqrt{\beta^{2}-4\gamma}\geq\beta$,}\\
0&\hbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(3.4)

Proof.

Showing (
3.3
) is equivalent to finding

z\in\mathscr{S}_{s}
with

|{\rm e}^{t\ p(z)}|=1
,
that is, finding
z\in\mathscr{S}_{s}
such that

Re(p(z))=0
. If
z=x+iy
with
x=Re(z)
and
y=Im(z)
,
from (
3.3
) we need

0=\ Re(p(z))\ =\ Re(x^{2}-y^{2}+2ixy+\beta(x+iy)+\gamma)\ =\ x^{2}-y^{2}+\beta x+\gamma.

So to prove (
3.3
) we need to find a
(x,y_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

such that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}{y_{0}}^{2}=x^{2}+\beta x+\gamma\\
$with$\ 0<x<s(1-a)\nu,\end{array}\right.

in other words, we must find a pair
(x,y)
with
0<x<s\nu(1-a)
and
y>0
.
To that end we must investigate when the
graph of parabola

y=x^{2}+\beta x+\gamma

intersects the portion of the strip

\mathscr{S}_{s}
above the real axis in
\mathbb{C}
.

Since the parabola opens upward, if
\beta^{2}-4\gamma<0
,
the parabola never crosses the real axis and so the
parabola will always have a segment that intersects

\mathscr{S}_{s}
above the real axis.

If
\beta^{2}=4\gamma
, the vertex of the parabola
lies on the real axis and hence a portion of the parabola must intersect our strip in the positive half-plane.

In the case
\beta^{2}-4\gamma>0
, the parabola intersects
the real axis at

x_{\pm}\ :=\ \frac{-\beta\pm\sqrt{\beta^{2}-4\gamma}}{2}.

Clearly
x_{-}\leq x_{+}
.

If
x_{-}>0
, there is a portion intersecting
\mathscr{S}_{s}
in the upper half plane.
If
x_{-}\leq 0
, we must choose
s
so large
that

x_{+}<s(1-a)\nu.

that is, choose

s\ >\ \frac{-\beta+\sqrt{\beta^{2}-4\gamma}}{2\nu(1-a)}.

With that choice of
s
, a portion of the parabola intersects
\mathscr{S}_{s}

in the upper half plane.

The proof of the lemma is now complete by noting once a portion of the parabola
is in
\mathscr{S}_{s}\cap\mathbb{T}
,
h_{t}(\mathscr{S}_{s})\cap\mathbb{T}
has
infinitely many accumulation points.
∎

We are now ready to state and prove our main result.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
\beta
,
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
,
B=A^{2}+\beta A+\gamma I
where
A=\nu x^{a}\frac{d}{dx}.

Then the semigroup generated by to the operator
B
,

T=\{T(t):t\geq 0\}
, governing the problem

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\ =\nu^{2}x^{2a}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}+\nu^{2}ax^{2a-1}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\beta\nu x^{a}\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+\gamma u,\quad x>0,\,t\geq 0,

is chaotic on
Y_{s}
if
s>s^{*}
, with
s^{*}
as in (
3.4
).

Proof.

Recall that

T(t)=h_{t}(A)={\rm e}^{tp(A)}

where
p(z)
is the polynomial in (
3.1
).

The proof is organized in two steps.

Step 1:
T
is hypercyclic.
Let
s>s^{*}
and fix any
t>0
. Define the sets

\begin{split}\Omega_{1}&=\ \{\lambda\in\mathscr{S}_{s}:\,|h_{t}(\lambda)|>1\},\\
\Omega_{2}&=\ \{\lambda\in\mathscr{S}_{s}:\,|h_{t}(\lambda)|<1\}.\end{split}

Consider the set

Q_{j}\ =\ {\rm span}\{\varphi_{\lambda}\in Y_{s}:\,\lambda\in\Omega_{j}\},\quad j\in\{1,2\}.

Let
z_{0}\in h_{t}(\mathscr{S}_{s})\cap\mathbb{T}
.
Since
h_{t}
is analytic and nonconstant,
h_{t}(\mathscr{S}_{s})
is an open set.
Then

\begin{split}\Omega_{1}&=\ h_{t}(\mathscr{S}_{s})\cap\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,|z|>1\},\\
\Omega_{2}&=\ h_{t}(\mathscr{S}_{s})\cap\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\,|z|<1\}\end{split}

are both open connected sets also. Thus any point in
\Omega_{j}
is an accumulation point for
j=1,2.

By Lemma
3.6
it follows that
Q_{j}
for
j=1,2
is dense.

We want to apply the Godefroy-Shapiro Criterion (here Theorem
3.3
)
with
L=h_{t}(A)
.
Define

Z=h_{t}(A)^{-1}

on
Q_{1}
.
Then

Z(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\lambda_{j}})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}(h_{t}(\lambda_{j}))^{-1}\varphi_{\lambda_{j}},

for
\lambda_{j}\in\Omega_{j}
,
\alpha_{j}\in\mathbb{C}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}.

It is clear that for any

y=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\lambda_{j}}\in Q_{1}
, we have

LZy=y
. Furthermore, for

\lambda_{j}\in\Omega_{1}
,
|h_{t}(\lambda_{j})|>1
and so

\lim_{n\to\infty}Z^{n}y=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}(h_{t}(\lambda_{j}))^{-n}\varphi_{\lambda_{j}}=0.

For
y=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\lambda_{j}}\in Q_{2}
, we have
|h_{t}(\lambda_{j})|<1
for each
j
and so

\lim_{n\to\infty}L^{n}y=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}(h_{t}(\lambda_{j}))^{n}\varphi_{\lambda_{j}}=0.

The hypotheses of Godefroy-Shapiro are then satisfied and so
L
, that is,
T(t)

for each
t
with
Re(t)>0
, is hypercyclic.

Step 2:
T
is chaotic.

To see that
L=h_{t}(A)
is chaotic (and of course recalling we fix any
t>0
),
set

\Omega_{3}=\{\lambda\in\mathscr{S}_{s}:\,h_{t}(\lambda)\in e^{2\pi i\mathbb{Q}}\},

and

Q_{3}=span\{\varphi_{\lambda}:\,\lambda\in\Omega_{3}\}.

Then
Q_{3}
is contained in the span of all periodic orbits of
L
with
period a rational multiple of
2\pi
. Let
\lambda_{j}\in Q_{3}
; suppose

h_{t}(\lambda_{j})=e^{2\pi i\frac{n_{j}}{m_{j}}}.

Then for
y=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\lambda_{j}}
,
and
m=\Pi_{j=1}^{N}m_{j},
we clearly have

h_{t}(A)^{m}y=\sum_{j=1}^{N}[e^{2\pi in_{j}\widehat{m}_{j}}]\alpha_{j}\varphi_{\lambda_{j}}=y

(where
\widehat{m}_{j}=(\Pi_{k\neq j}m_{k})
).
So the set of all periodic orbits of
h_{t}(A)
is dense. Since
t>0
was fixed
but otherwise arbitrary, we have shown the semigroup
T
is chaotic.
∎
