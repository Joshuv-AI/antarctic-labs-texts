The minimum neighborliness of a random polytope
Brett Leroux

Abstract
Let μ \mu be a probability distribution on ℝ d \operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d} which assigns measure zero to every hyperplane and S S a set of points sampled independently from μ \mu . What can be said about the expected combinatorial structure of the convex hull of S S ? These polytopes are simplicial with probability one, but not much else is known except when more restrictive assumptions are imposed on μ \mu . In this paper we show that, with probability close to one, the convex hull of S S has a high degree of neighborliness no matter the underlying distribution μ \mu as long as n n is not much bigger than d d . As a concrete example, our result implies that if for each d d in ℕ \mathbb{N} we choose a probability distribution μ d \mu_{d} on ℝ d \operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d} which assigns measure zero to every hyperplane and then set P n P_{n} to be the convex hull of an i.i.d. sample of n ≤ 5 ​ d / 4 n\leq 5d/4 random points from μ d \mu_{d} , the probability that P n P_{n} is k k -neighborly approaches one as d → ∞ d\to\infty for all k ≤ d / 20 k\leq d/20 . We also give a simple example of a family of distributions which essentially attain our lower bound on the k k -neighborliness of a random polytope.

Introduction

The most well-studied models of random polytopes are those where a random polytope is defined as the convex hull of a set of independent and identically distributed points from some probability distribution on the space
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. These objects have been studied for several reasons. One reason is that random polytopes can sometimes give us some insight into the possible metric or combinatorial properties of deterministic convex polytopes with a given dimension and number of vertices. Another is that convex polytopes have a wide range of applications in geometric algorithms including the simplex method
[
7
]
and Wolfe’s method
[
20
]
. For many such algorithms, the input data defines a convex polytope and it is useful to understand combinatorial and metric properties of that polytope in order to understand the complexity of the algorithm. Since algorithmic applications often assume that the input data is random according to some predetermined distribution, random polytopes are particularly relevant.

Gaussian random polytopes, i.e. random polytopes where the underlying distribution is the standard Gaussian distribution on
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
have received much attention
[
3
,
5
,
15
,
16
,
21
,
22
]
. Perhaps the main reason for this focus on Gaussian random polytopes is that they coincide in distribution with uniform random projections of a simplex to some lower dimensional space
[
6
]
. Another reason is that the Gaussian distribution has many nice properties which often make calculations simpler and behavior of combinatorial properties easier to determine. Other commonly studied families of random polytopes are those for which the underlying distribution is the uniform distribution on a convex body or the boundary of a convex body. Yet another important example is
random
0/1
polytopes

[
17
]
where the vertices are a random subset of the vertices of the
d
-dimensional cube. Some papers prove results which assume only that the underlying distribution has some property such as being log-concave
[
8
]
or subgaussian
[
19
]
.

In all of these examples, either the distribution is specified, or it satisfies some restrictive condition such as being subgaussian. Our main result in contrast assumes only that the distribution assigns measure zero to every (affine) hyperplane. With this assumption only, we consider random polytopes where the number of random points is proportional to the dimension and the dimension approaches infinity. The main property of convex polytopes we are interested in is
k
-neighborliness (defined in
Section

1.2
). One of our main results shows that if the constant of proportionality is less than two, then there exists some constant
\beta>0
(depending on the constant of proportionality), such that the probability that the random polytope is at least
\lfloor\beta d\rfloor
-neighborly approaches one as the dimension approaches infinity (
Theorem

3
).

To put this result in context, we need to review what has previously been known about the neighborliness of random polytopes. First we collect some notation.

1.3
Our results

Our two main results are similar to the two results explained in the previous section, the first due to Donoho and Tanner, and the second due to Vershik and Sporyshev. The main difference is that our results apply to any random polytope whose vertices are i.i.d. according to a probability distribution on
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
which assigns measure zero to every hyperplane. This is a very weak assumption on the distribution. In particular, it is the minimal assumption which guarantees that the random polytopes under consideration are simplicial with probability one. Not surprisingly, because of the generality of the distributions we consider, our result guarantees a lower degree of neighborliness than in the case where the distribution is Gaussian.

2.1
An upper bound on the probability that a point is in the convex hull

Wendel’s theorem (
[
28
]
,
[
24
, Theorem 8.2.1]
) is a classic result in geometric probability which says that the probability that a set of
n
i.i.d. random points from a distribution on
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
which is symmetric about
o
and which assigns measure zero to every linear hyperplane is equal to
\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-1}\binom{n-1}{i}}{2^{n-1}}
. More recently, Wendel’s theorem has been generalized by Wagner and Welzl in the following way. An
absolutely continuous
probability distribution on
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is a probability distribution which has a density function with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. A measure
\mu
is
balanced about a point
p
if every hyperplane through
p
divides
\mu
into two equal halves. Wagner and Welzl showed the following

Theorem 5

(
[
27
, Corollary 3.7]
)
.

Let
\mu
be an absolutely continuous probability measure on
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Let
S
be a set of
n
independent and identically distributed points from
\mu
. Then the probability that
\operatorname{conv}S
contains the origin is at most

\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-1}\binom{n-1}{i}}{2^{n-1}}=1-\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\binom{n-1}{i}}{2^{n-1}}.

and this bound is attained if and only if
\mu
is balanced about the origin.

Why is this sort of result useful for our purposes? In order to prove
Theorem

3
, we need to show that certain random polytopes are
k
-neighborly with probability close to one. Therefore, we need an upper bound on the probability that the polytope is not
k
-neighborly. By the union bound, this probability is upper bounded by
\binom{n}{k}
times the probability that a subset
K
of the vertices of size
k
is not a face of the polytope. A standard fact about polytopes is that
\operatorname{conv}K
is not a face of the polytope if and only if the affine hull of
K
intersects the convex hull of the remaining vertices of the polytope (
Proposition

8
). Therefore, letting
V
denote the set of vertices of the polytope, if we project
V
to the subspace which is orthogonal to the affine hull of
K
, then the event that
\operatorname{conv}K
is not a face of the polytope is equivalent to the event that the convex hull of
V\setminus K
contains the projection of the affine hull of
K
which is a point in the image space of the projection.
Theorem

5
can then be used to given an upper bound for the probability of this event.

Rather than using
Theorem

5
, we will prove another version of this result which applies not just to absolutely continuous distribution but to any distribution which assigns measure zero to every hyperplane. We also give a proof of this result which is very different from the proof of
Theorem

5
given in
[
27
]
. However the following result is not entirely new; it was mentioned in
[
27
]
that such a proof works but no details were given.

Theorem 6
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
that assigns measure zero to every hyperplane. Let
S
be a set of
n
independent and identically distributed points from
\mu
. Then the probability that
\operatorname{conv}S
contains the origin is at most

\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-1}\binom{n-1}{i}}{2^{n-1}}.

Proof.

The quantity of interest is

\mathbb{P}(o\in\operatorname{conv}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n})).

Note that since
\mu
assigns measure zero to ever hyperplane (and, in particular, every hyperplane through the origin), the probability that some
\ell
-flat through the origin contains more than
\ell
points is zero. This means that the probability that
\operatorname{conv}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n})
contains the origin on its boundary is zero and so the above probability is equal to

p:=\mathbb{P}(o\in\operatorname{int}\operatorname{conv}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n})).

Let
X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}
be
N
i.i.d. points distributed according to
\mu
where
N>n
is some integer. Now we consider the
Gale transform

[
14
, Section 5.4]
of the set of points
\{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\}
. The Gale transform of
\{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\}
is a set
\bar{X}_{N}
of
N
points in
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{N-d-1}
and by
[
14
, 5.4.1]
there is a one-to-one correspondence between subsets of
\{X_{1},\dots,X_{N}\}
of size
n
which contain the origin in their interior and subsets of
\bar{X}_{N}
of size
N-n
which are faces of the polytope
\operatorname{conv}\bar{X}_{N}
. We note that since no hyperplane through the origin contains more than
d-1
points of
\{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\}
with probability one, the polytope
\operatorname{conv}\bar{X}_{N}
is simplicial with probability one
[
14
, Section 5.4]
.

By the Upper Bound Theorem for convex polytopes
[
18
]
, and the formula for the number of faces of each dimension of a cyclic polytope, see for example
[
13
, Theorem 17.3.4]
, the number of subsets of
\bar{X}_{N}
of size
N-n
which are faces of the polytope
\operatorname{conv}\bar{X}_{N}
is at most

\displaystyle C(N,n,d):=\frac{N-\delta(n-1)}{n}\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor\frac{N-d-1}{2}\rfloor}\binom{N-1-j}{n-1}\binom{n}{N-2j+\delta}

(1)

where
\delta=N-d-1-2\lfloor(N-d-1)/2\rfloor
. Therefore, we know that the expected number of subsets of
\{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{N}\}
of size
n
which contain the origin in the interior of their convex hull is at most
C(N,n,d)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle p\cdot\binom{N}{n}\leq C(N,n,d).

Therefore, in order to prove the desired bound on
p
, it will suffice to show that

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{C(N,n,d)}{\binom{N}{n}}=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-1}\binom{n-1}{i}}{2^{n-1}}.

(2)

To make the calculation simpler, we will restrict our attention to values of
N
such that
N-d-1
is even, i.e. the parity of
N
is the opposite of the parity of
d
. This means that
\delta=0
.
For the terms in
Eq.

1
to be non-zero, we need
N-2j+\delta\leq n
and so
2j\geq N+\delta-n
. Therefore,
Eq.

1
equals

\frac{N}{n}\sum_{j=\lceil\frac{N-n}{2}\rceil}^{\frac{N-d-1}{2}}\binom{N-1-j}{n-1}\binom{n}{N-2j}=\frac{N}{n}\sum_{j=\lceil\frac{N-n}{2}\rceil}^{\frac{N-d-1}{2}}\binom{N-1-j}{n-1}\bigg(\binom{n-1}{N-2j-1}+\binom{n-1}{N-2j}\bigg)

Letting
m=(N-d-1)/2-j
and using that
(N-d-1)/2-\lceil\frac{N-n}{2}\rceil=\lfloor\frac{n-d-1}{2}\rfloor
, the above is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{N}{n}\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n-d-1}{2}\rfloor}\binom{N-1-(N-d-1)/2-m}{n-1}\bigg(\binom{n-1}{d+1+2m-1}+\binom{n-1}{d+1+2m}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{n}\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n-d-1}{2}\rfloor}\binom{N/2+(d+1)/2-1-m}{n-1}\bigg(\binom{n-1}{d+1+2m-1}+\binom{n-1}{d+1+2m}\bigg)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N\to\infty}}{{\sim}}\frac{N^{n}}{n!}\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}\sum_{m=0}^{\lfloor\frac{n-d-1}{2}\rfloor}\bigg(\binom{n-1}{d+1+2m-1}+\binom{n-1}{d+1+2m}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{n}}{n!}\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-1}\binom{n-1}{i}.

Since
\binom{N}{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle N\to\infty}}{{\sim}}\frac{N^{n}}{n!}
,
Eq.

2
follows.
∎

Corollary 7
.

Let
\mu

\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
that assigns measure zero to every hyperplane. Let

\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-(d-\ell)-1}\binom{n-1}{i}}{2^{n-1}}.

Proof.

Note that any affine coordinate transformation of
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
preserves the fact that
\mu
assigns measure zero to every hyperplane. Therefore, after applying an affine coordinate transformation to
\mu
, we may assume that
L
is the span of the first

\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d-\ell}
with
\{0,\dotsc,0\}\times\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d-\ell}\subset\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

\pi_{d-\ell}
denote the orthogonal projection onto
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d-\ell}
.

The probability that
L\cap\operatorname{conv}S\neq\emptyset
is equal to the probability that
\operatorname{conv}(\tilde{X_{1}},\dotsc,\tilde{X_{n}})
contains the origin where the
\tilde{X_{i}}
are independent and distributed according to the projection of
\mu
by
\pi_{d-\ell}
. The corollary now follows from
Theorem

6
.
∎

The above corollary is useful for our purposes due to the following well-known fact:

Proposition 8

(
[
14
, Exercise 3.1.1]
)
.

Let
P
be a polytope and
A\subset V(P)
. Then
\operatorname{conv}A
is a face of
P
if and only if
\operatorname{aff}A\cap\operatorname{conv}(V(P)\setminus A)=\emptyset
.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
that assigns measure zero to every hyperplane. Let
S_{n}
be a set of
n
independent and identically distributed points from
\mu
. Then for any subset
K
of
S_{n}
of size
k
, the probability that
\operatorname{conv}K
is not a face of
\operatorname{conv}S_{n}
is at most

\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-2}\binom{n-k-1}{i}}{2^{n-k-1}}.

Proof.

Let
S_{n}:=\{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n}\}
. Define

f(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n}):=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\operatorname{conv}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n})\text{ is not a face of }\operatorname{conv}S_{n}\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

Since all the
X_{i}
are independent and identically distributed, the probability that
\operatorname{conv}(X_{i_{1}},\dotsc,X_{i_{k}})
is not a face of
\operatorname{conv}S_{n}
is independent of the choice of subscripts. The probability that
\operatorname{conv}(X_{n-k+1},\dotsc,X_{n})
is not a face of
\operatorname{conv}S_{n}
is equal to

\int_{\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\dotsc\int_{\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x_{n-k+1},\dotsc,x_{n})\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1})\dotsc\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{n}).

(3)

For any fixed choice of points
x_{n-k+1},\dotsc,x_{n}
, the inner integral

\int_{\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\dotsc\int_{\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x_{n-k+1},\dotsc,x_{n})\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1})\dotsc\mu(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{n-k})

is equal (by
Proposition

8
) to the probability that
\operatorname{aff}(x_{n-k+1},\dotsc,x_{n})\cap\operatorname{conv}(S_{n}\setminus\{x_{n-k+1},\dotsc,x_{n}\})\neq\emptyset
. If
\{x_{n-k+1},\dotsc,x_{n}\}
are in general position, that is, they are not contained in any affine
(k-2)
-flat, then by
Corollary

7
, this probability is at most

\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-2}\binom{n-k-1}{i}}{2^{n-k-1}}.

(4)

Since
\mu
assigns measure zero to every hyperplane, the measure of the set of
\{x_{n-k+1},\dotsc,x_{n}\}
which are contained in some affine
(k-2)
-flat is zero. Therefore,
Eq.

3
is the integral of a function that is bounded by
Eq.

4
except possibly on a set of measure zero, so statement of the theorem follows.
∎

We can now prove the main results.

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

Let
S_{n}:=\{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n}\}
. By
Theorem

9
and the union bound, the probability that
\operatorname{conv}S_{n}
is not
k
-neighborly is at most

\displaystyle\binom{n}{k}\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-2}\binom{n-k-1}{i}}{2^{n-k-1}}.

So we just need to prove that this quantity goes to zero as
d\to\infty
. The assumptions on
n,k,d
imply that for
d
sufficiently large,
n-d-2<\frac{n-k-1}{2}
. Therefore, by the unimodality of the binomial coefficients and using that
\binom{n}{k}\leq 2^{nH(k/n)}
, for
d
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\binom{n}{k}\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-2}\binom{n-k-1}{i}}{2^{n-k-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\binom{n}{k}\cdot n\cdot\binom{n-k-1}{n-d-2}}{2^{n-k-1}}

\displaystyle\leq n\frac{2^{nH(k/n)}2^{(n-k-1)H((n-d-2)/(n-k-1))}}{2^{n-k-1}}

\displaystyle=n2^{nH(k/n)}2^{(n-k-1)\big(H((n-d-2)/(n-k-1))-1\big)}.

Since
n\sim\alpha d
and
k\sim\beta d
we have that
n-d-2\sim(\alpha-1)d
and
n-k-1\sim(\alpha-\beta)d
. So for
d
sufficiently large, the above is at most

2\alpha d2^{\alpha dH(\beta/\alpha)+(\alpha-\beta)d\big(H(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha-\beta})-1\big)}.

This quantity goes to zero as
d\to\infty
as long as
\alpha H(\beta/\alpha)+(\alpha-\beta)(H(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha-\beta})-1)<0
which is the assumption on
\beta
.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

4
.

Let
S_{n}:=\{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n}\}
. The expected
\ell
-face density of
\operatorname{conv}S_{n}
is equal to the probability that
\operatorname{conv}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{\ell+1})
is a face of
\operatorname{conv}S_{n}
. So we just need to show that the probability that
\operatorname{conv}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{\ell+1})
is not a face of
\operatorname{conv}S_{n}
is
o(1)
as
d\to\infty
. By
Theorem

9
, the probability that
\operatorname{conv}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{\ell+1})
is not a face of
\operatorname{conv}S_{n}
is at most

\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-2}\binom{n-\ell-2}{i}}{2^{n-\ell-2}}

Since
n\sim\alpha d
and
\ell\sim\beta d
, we know that

\frac{n-d-2}{n-\ell-2}\sim\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha-\beta}.

By the assumption that
\beta<2-\alpha
, we have that
\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha-\beta}<1/2
. Therefore, there exists
\epsilon>0
such that for
d
sufficiently large,
\frac{n-d-2}{n-\ell-2}<1/2-\epsilon
. By the unimodality of the binomial coefficients, the fact that
\frac{n-d-2}{n-\ell-2}<1/2-\epsilon
implies that

\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-2}\binom{n-\ell-2}{i}}{2^{n-\ell-2}}\leq\frac{n\binom{n-\ell-2}{n-d-2}}{2^{n-\ell-2}}

Again using that
\binom{n}{k}\leq 2^{nH(k/n)}
, we have that for
d
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-2}\binom{n-\ell-2}{i}}{2^{n-\ell-2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n\binom{n-\ell-2}{n-d-2}}{2^{n-\ell-2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n2^{(n-\ell-2)H(\frac{n-d-2}{n-\ell-2})}}{2^{n-\ell-2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\alpha d2^{d(\alpha-\beta)H(1/2-\epsilon)}}{2^{d(\alpha-\beta)}}.

And the above quantity goes to zero as
d\to\infty
because
H(r)<1
as long as
r\neq 1/2
.
∎

The least neighborly distributions

As previously mentioned, we will construct a family of distributions
\{\mu_{d}\}_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
which show that
Theorems

3
and
4
are in some sense best possible. Before giving the construction, we need to establish the following proposition, which gives the reverse of the bound given by
Theorem

5
in
Section

2.1
.

3.1
A lower bound on the probability that a point is in the convex hull

Definition 10
.

Let
\mu
be a probability distribution on
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
p
a point in
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. The
depth of
p
in
\mu
is defined to be

\min\{\mu(H^{+})\mathrel{:}H^{+}\text{ is a closed halfspace containing }p\}.

Proposition 11
.

Let
\mu
be an absolutely continuous probability distribution on
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Let
S
be a set of
n
independent and identically distributed points from
\mu
. Let
p\in\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a point and assume that the depth of
p
in
\mu
is greater than or equal to
a
. Then the probability that
\operatorname{conv}S
contains
p
is greater than or equal to

\displaystyle(d+1)\binom{n}{d+1}\int_{0}^{a}(y^{n-d-1}+(1-y)^{n-d-1})y^{d}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-1}\binom{n}{i}a^{i}(1-a)^{n-i}+a^{n}\binom{n-1}{d}.

Proof.

Note that it suffices to prove the statement when
p
is the origin because otherwise we could translate
p
and
\mu
to reduce to this case. Let
p_{n,\mu}
denote the probability that
\operatorname{conv}S
contains the origin, where
S
is a set of
n
independent and identically distributed points from
\mu
. It is shown in
[
27
]
that there exists a function
h(y)
(which depends only on
\mu
) such that

p_{n,\mu}=2\binom{n}{d+1}\int_{0}^{1}y^{n-d-1}h(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

For completeness, we give the definition of
h(y)
from
[
27
]
: As in
[
27
, Section 2.2]
, we choose some absolutely continuous probability distribution
\tilde{\mu}
on
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
such that the orthogonal projection of
\tilde{\mu}
to the first
d
coordinates is the distribution
\mu
and we let
\tilde{\ell}
be the
x_{d+1}
axis in
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}
. Then as in
[
27
, Section 1.2]
, we let
\sigma
denote a
\tilde{\mu}
-random oriented simplex
, i.e. a
d
-simplex whose
d+1
vertices are i.i.d. points from
\tilde{\mu}
and one side of
\sigma
is chosen as the “positive” side which is denoted
H^{+}(\sigma)
. Furthermore, we say that a directed line
\tilde{\ell}

enters
an oriented simplex
\sigma
if it intersects the relative interior of
\sigma
and is directed from the positive to the negative side of
\sigma
. With this we define

H_{\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\ell}}:=\mathbb{P}(\tilde{\ell}\text{ enters }\sigma\text{ and }\tilde{\mu}(H^{+}(\sigma))\leq y).

We then define

h(y):=h_{\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\ell}}(y):=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}H_{\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\ell}}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y}.

This completes the definition of
h(y)
, see
[
27
]
for more details.

Now by
[
27
, Theorem 2.6]
,
h(y)=h(1-y)
and so

2\binom{n}{d+1}\int_{0}^{1}y^{n-d-1}h\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=2\binom{n}{d+1}\int_{0}^{1/2}(y^{n-d-1}+(1-y)^{n-d-1})h\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

By
[
27
, Theorem 3.6]
and the remarks following the proof of that theorem, because of the assumption that the depth of
o
in
\mu
is greater than or equal to
a
, the function
h
satisfies
h=\frac{(d+1)}{2}\min(y,1-y)^{d}
for
y\leq a
and
y\geq 1-a
. Alternatively, see
[
26
, Lemma 4.33]
for a rigorously stated proof of this claim.
Therefore,

\displaystyle p_{n,\mu}

\displaystyle\geq(d+1)\binom{n}{d+1}\int_{0}^{a}(y^{n-d-1}+(1-y)^{n-d-1})y^{d}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

We use
[
1
, 6.6.4]
to get

\int_{0}^{a}(1-y)^{n-d-1}y^{d}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=\frac{(n-d-1)!d!}{n!}\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-1}\binom{n}{i}a^{i}(1-a)^{n-i}.

And since
\int_{0}^{a}y^{n-1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=\frac{a^{n}}{n}
and
\frac{a^{n}}{n}(d+1)\binom{n}{d+1}=a^{n}\binom{n-1}{d}
,

p_{n,\mu}\geq\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-1}\binom{n}{i}a^{i}(1-a)^{n-i}+a^{n}\binom{n-1}{d}.

∎

We remark that a calculation similar to the one in the proof of

11
was done in

. However, there is a mistake in that proof; the summation formula they get for the integral is incorrect.

3.2
A family of distributions essentially attaining the lower bound

Here is the definition of the family of distributions: Let
\epsilon_{d}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}
(This choice is somewhat arbitrary). Let

f(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{d}):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}e^{-(1/2)(x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{d}^{2})}

be the probability density function of the standard (mean zero and identity covariance matrix) Gaussian distribution on
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Let
\mu_{d}
be the distribution on
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with density

\big(1-\epsilon_{d}\big)f(x)+\epsilon_{d}\frac{1_{\{\|x\|_{2}\leq\epsilon_{d}\}}}{V_{d}}

where
V_{d}
is the volume of the
d
-ball with radius
\epsilon_{d}
. In other words,
\mu_{d}
is the combination of a Gaussian distribution having
1-\epsilon_{d}
of the mass (we call it the “Gaussian part”) and the uniform distribution on the
d
-ball of radius
\epsilon_{d}
(called the “ball part”) having the remaining mass. Note that each distribution
\mu_{d}
is absolutely continuous.
The following proposition shows that
Theorem

4
is in some sense best possible.

Proposition 12
.

Let
\alpha>1
and assume that
\beta>0
satisfies
\beta>2-\alpha
. Let
\{\mu_{d}\}_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
be the family of probability distributions defined at the start of this section. If for each
d\in\mathbb{N}
we let
n:=n(d)=\lfloor\alpha d\rfloor
and let
S_{n}=\{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n}\}
be a set of
n
iid random points from
\mu_{d}
, then for any sequence
\ell:=\ell(d)
with
\ell\sim\beta d
, the expected
\ell
-face density of
\operatorname{conv}S_{n}
is
o(1)
as
d\to\infty
.

Proof.

By definition, the expected
\ell
-face density of
\operatorname{conv}S_{n}
is equal to the probability that
\operatorname{conv}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{\ell+1})
is a face of
\operatorname{conv}S_{n}
. Therefore, in order to show that the expected
\ell
-face density is
o(1)
, it will suffice to show that the probability that
\operatorname{conv}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{\ell+1})
is not a face of
\operatorname{conv}S_{n}
is
1-o(1)
.

First we will show that we can assume that at least one of the points
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in[\ell+1]}
is sampled from the ball part of the distribution. Let
B
be the event that at least one of the points
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in[\ell+1]}
is sampled from the ball part of the distribution and
1_{B}
the indicator function of the event
B
. Using that
\big(1+x/y\big)^{y}<e^{x}
, we have that

\mathbb{P}(\neg B)=\big(1-\epsilon_{d}\big)^{\ell+1}\leq e^{-\epsilon_{d}(\ell+1)}=o(1).

and so
\mathbb{P}(B)=1-o(1)
. This means that we ignore the case when event
B
is not satisfied. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{conv}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{\ell+1})\text{ is not a face})

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{conv}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{\ell+1})\text{ is not a face}\cap B)

and so it suffices to show that
\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{conv}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{\ell+1})\text{ is not a face}\cap B)=1-o(1)
.

Define

f(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\ell+1}):=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\operatorname{conv}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\ell+1})\text{ is not a face}\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

Then we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{conv}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{\ell+1})\text{ is not a face}\cap B)

\displaystyle=\int_{\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\dotsc\int_{\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}1_{B}f(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\ell+1})\mu_{d}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{n})\dotsc\mu_{d}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}).

Letting
B^{\prime}\subset\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d(\ell+1)}
be the set of point sets satisfying
1_{B}=1
, we can rewrite the above as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{conv}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{\ell+1})\text{ is not a face}\cap B)

\displaystyle=\int_{B^{\prime}}\int_{\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\dotsc\int_{\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\ell+1})\mu_{d}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{n})\dotsc\mu_{d}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}).

(5)

For any fixed choice of points
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\ell+1}
, the inner integral

\displaystyle\int_{\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\dotsc\int_{\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\ell+1})\mu_{d}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{n})\dotsc\mu_{d}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{\ell+2})

(6)

is equal to the probability that
\operatorname{conv}(\{X_{i}\}_{i\in[\ell+2,n]})\cap L\neq\emptyset
where
L:=\operatorname{aff}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\ell+1})
. Under the assumption that the points
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\ell+1}
satisfy
1_{B}=1
, we will show that this probability is
1-o(1)
and therefore that
Eq.

6
is
1-o(1)
for any choice of
x_{1},\dotsc x_{\ell+1}
such that
1_{B}=1
. Let
\pi_{L}
be the orthogonal projection of
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
to the subspace
L^{\perp}
of dimension
d-\ell
that is orthogonal to
L
. Let
\pi\mu_{d}
denote the measure on
L^{\perp}
which is the projection of
\mu_{d}
. The probability that
\operatorname{conv}(\{X_{i}\}_{i\in[\ell+2,n]})\cap L\neq\emptyset
is equal to the probability that
\operatorname{conv}(\pi_{L}X_{\ell+2},\dotsc,\pi_{L}X_{n})
contains
\pi L
.
Note that since at least one of the
x_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq\ell+1
is sampled from the ball part of the distribution, the distance from
L
to the origin is at most
\epsilon_{d}
. We claim that this means that the depth of
\pi L
in
\pi\mu_{d}
is at least
(1-\epsilon_{d})(1/2-\epsilon_{d})
. In order to show this, we need to show that every hyperplane in
\pi_{L}\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
through
\pi L
has at least
(1-\epsilon_{d})(1/2-\epsilon_{d})
of the mass of
\pi\mu_{d}
on each side. We will actually prove the stronger statement that every hyperplane in
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
which contains
L
has has at least
(1-\epsilon_{d})(1/2-\epsilon_{d})
of the mass of
\mu_{d}
on each side. The Gaussian measure of halfspace determined by a hyperplane at distance less than
\epsilon_{d}
from the origin is greater than

1/2-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\epsilon_{d}}e^{-x^{2}/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq 1/2-\epsilon_{d}.

And
1-\epsilon_{d}
of the mass of
\mu_{d}
is the standard Gaussian measure. So the claim that the depth of
\pi L
in
\pi\mu_{d}
is at least
(1-\epsilon_{d})(1/2-\epsilon_{d})\geq 1/2-2\epsilon_{d}
follows.

Now by
Proposition

11
, the probability that
\operatorname{conv}(\pi_{L}X_{\ell+2},\dotsc,\pi_{L}X_{n})
contains
\pi L
is at least

(d-\ell+1)\binom{n-\ell-1}{d-\ell+1}\int_{0}^{1/2-2\epsilon_{d}}(y^{n-d-2}+(1-y)^{n-d-2})y^{d-\ell}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

Using the formula in
Proposition

11
along with the fact that
\binom{n}{i}=\binom{n-1}{i-1}+\binom{n-1}{i}
one can show that if the range of integration is extended from
(0,1/2-\epsilon_{d})
to
(0,1/2)
, then

(d-\ell+1)\binom{n-\ell-1}{d-\ell+1}\int_{0}^{1/2}(y^{n-d-2}+(1-y)^{n-d-2})y^{d-\ell}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-2}\binom{n-\ell-2}{i}}{2^{n-\ell-2}}.

Therefore, using that
\big(1+x/y\big)^{y}<e^{x}
, we have that

\displaystyle(d-\ell+1)\binom{n-\ell-1}{d-\ell+1}\int_{0}^{1/2-2\epsilon_{d}}(y^{n-d-2}+(1-y)^{n-d-2})y^{d-\ell}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-2}\binom{n-\ell-2}{i}}{2^{n-\ell-2}}-(d-\ell+1)\binom{n-\ell-1}{d-\ell+1}\int_{1/2-2\epsilon_{d}}^{1/2}(y^{n-d-2}+(1-y)^{n-d-2})y^{d-\ell}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-2}\binom{n-\ell-2}{i}}{2^{n-\ell-2}}-2\epsilon_{d}(d-\ell+1)\binom{n-\ell-1}{d-\ell+1}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+2\epsilon_{d}\bigg)^{n-\ell-2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-2}\binom{n-\ell-2}{i}}{2^{n-\ell-2}}-2\epsilon_{d}(d-\ell+1)\binom{n-\ell-1}{d-\ell+1}\frac{1}{2^{n-\ell-2}}\big(1+4\epsilon_{d}\big)^{n-\ell-2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-2}\binom{n-\ell-2}{i}}{2^{n-\ell-2}}-2\epsilon_{d}(d-\ell+1)\binom{n-\ell-1}{d-\ell+1}\frac{1}{2^{n-\ell-2}}e^{4\epsilon_{d}(n-\ell-2)}.

First we claim that the second term above is
o(1)
. To show this, note that since
\ell\sim\beta d
and
n\sim\alpha d
,
\frac{d-\ell-1}{n-\ell-2}\sim\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha-\beta}
. And by the assumption that
\beta>2-\alpha
, we have that
\frac{1-\beta}{\alpha-\beta}<\frac{1-\beta}{2-2\beta}=1/2
and so there exists
\epsilon>0
such that for
d
sufficiently large,
\frac{d-\ell-1}{n-\ell-2}<1/2-\epsilon
. Now using that
\binom{n}{k}\leq 2^{nH(k/n)}
, we have that for
d
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle 2\epsilon_{d}(d-\ell+1)\binom{n-\ell-1}{d-\ell+1}\frac{1}{2^{n-\ell-2}}e^{4\epsilon_{d}(n-\ell-2)}

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon_{d}(d-\ell+1)\frac{2^{(n-\ell-1)H(1/2-\epsilon)}}{2^{n-\ell-2}}e^{8\sqrt{d}}

\displaystyle=o(1)

because
H(1/2-\epsilon)<1
. This means that it suffices to show that
2^{\ell+2-n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-d-2}\binom{n-\ell-2}{i}=1-o(1)
, or equivalently, that
2^{\ell+2-n}\sum_{i=n-d-1}^{n-\ell-2}\binom{n-\ell-2}{i}=2^{\ell+2-n}\sum_{j=0}^{d-\ell-1}\binom{n-\ell-2}{j}=o(1)
. So by unimodality of the binomial coefficients, and again using that
\binom{n}{k}\leq 2^{nH(k/n)}
,

\displaystyle 2^{\ell+2-n}\sum_{j=0}^{d-\ell-1}\binom{n-\ell-2}{j}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\ell+2-n}(d-\ell)\binom{n-\ell-2}{d-\ell-1}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\ell+2-n}(d-\ell)2^{(n-\ell-2)H(\frac{d-\ell-1}{n-\ell-2})}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\ell+2-n}(d-\ell)2^{(n-\ell-2)H(1/2-\epsilon)}.

And the above quantity is
o(1)
because
H(1/2-\epsilon)<1
.

This shows that
Eq.

5
is the integral over
B^{\prime}
of a function which is uniformly bounded from below by a function which is equal to
1-o(1)
. Since the measure of
B^{\prime}
is equal to
\mathbb{P}(B)=1-o(1)
, this shows that
Eq.

5
is equal to
1-o(1)
as desired.
∎

We can also prove a similar result for
k
-neighborliness of the distributions
\mu_{d}
. (The distributions
\mu_{d}\}_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
in the following proposition are the distributions defined at the beginning of this section.)

Proposition 13
.

Let
\alpha>1
and assume that
\beta>0
satisfies
\alpha H(\beta/\alpha)+(\alpha-\beta)(H(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha-\beta})-1)>0
, or equivalently,
\frac{\alpha^{\alpha}}{(\alpha-1)^{\alpha-1}2^{\alpha}}>\frac{\beta^{\beta}(1-\beta)^{1-\beta}}{2^{\beta}}
. Let
\{\mu_{d}\}_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
be the family of probability distributions defined at the start of this section. If for each
d
we let
n:=n(d)=\lfloor\alpha d\rfloor
and let
S_{n}=\{X_{1},\dotsc,X_{n}\}
be a set of
n
iid random points from
\mu_{d}
, then for any sequence
k:=k(d)
with
k\sim\beta d
, the expected number of subsets of
S_{n}
of size
k
which are not faces of
\operatorname{conv}S_{n}
goes to infinity as
d\to\infty
.

Proof.

Let
\{\mu_{d}\}_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
be the distributions defined at the start of this section.

Let
B
be the event that at least one of the points
\{X_{i}\}_{i\in[k]}
is sampled from the ball part of the distribution and
1_{B}
the indicator function of the event
B
. The same argument as in the proof of
Proposition

12
shows that
\mathbb{P}(B)=1-o(1)
. We want to show that the expected number of subsets of size
k
which are not faces goes to infinity. It will suffice to show that the expected number of subsets of size
k
which contain at least one point sampled from the ball part and which are not faces goes to infinity. That is, it suffices to show that

\binom{n}{k}\cdot\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{conv}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{k})\text{ is not a face}\cap B)\to\infty.

Define

f(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\ell+1}):=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }\operatorname{conv}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{k})\text{ is not a face}\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}

Then we have that

\displaystyle\binom{n}{k}\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{conv}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{\ell+1})\text{ is not a face}\cap B)

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{k}\int_{\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\dotsc\int_{\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}1_{B}f(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{\ell+1})\mu_{d}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{n})\dotsc\mu_{d}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}).

Letting
B^{\prime}\subset\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d(\ell+1)}
be the set of point sets satisfying
1_{B}=1
, we can rewrite the above as

\displaystyle\binom{n}{k}\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{conv}(X_{1},\dotsc,X_{k})\text{ is not a face}\cap B)

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{k}\int_{B^{\prime}}\int_{\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\dotsc\int_{\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{k})\mu_{d}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{n})\dotsc\mu_{d}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{1}).

(7)

For any fixed choice of points
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{k}
, the inner integral

\displaystyle\int_{\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\dotsc\int_{\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{k})\mu_{d}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{n})\dotsc\mu_{d}(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{k+1})

(8)

is equal to the probability that
\operatorname{conv}(\{X_{i}\}_{i\in[k+1,n]})\cap L\neq\emptyset
where
L:=\operatorname{aff}(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{k})
.

Under the assumption that the points
x_{1},\dotsc,x_{k}
satisfy
1_{B}=1
, we will show that
\binom{n}{k}
times this probability approaches infinity. Let
\pi_{L}
be the orthogonal projection of
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
to the subspace
L^{\perp}
of dimension
d-k+1
that is orthogonal to
L
. Let
\pi\mu_{d}
denote the measure on
L^{\perp}
which is the projection of
\mu_{d}
. The probability that
\operatorname{conv}(\{X_{i}\}_{i\in[k+1,n]})\cap L\neq\emptyset
is equal to the probability that
\operatorname{conv}(\pi_{L}X_{k+1},\dotsc,\pi_{L}X_{n})
contains
\pi L
.
Note that since at least one of the
x_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq k
is sampled from the ball part of the distribution, the distance from
L
to the origin is at most
\epsilon_{d}
. We claim that this means that the depth of
\pi L
in
\pi\mu_{d}
is at least
(1-\epsilon_{d})(1/2-\epsilon_{d})
. In order to show this, we need to show that every hyperplane in
\pi_{L}\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
through
\pi L
has at least
(1-\epsilon_{d})(1/2-\epsilon_{d})
of the mass of
\pi\mu_{d}
on each side. We will actually prove the stronger statement that every hyperplane in
\operatorname{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
which contains
L
has has at least
(1-\epsilon_{d})(1/2-\epsilon_{d})
of the mass of
\mu_{d}
on each side. The Gaussian measure of halfspace determined by a hyperplane at distance less than
\epsilon_{d}
from the origin is greater than

1/2-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{0}^{\epsilon_{d}}e^{-x^{2}/2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\geq 1/2-\epsilon_{d}.

And
1-\epsilon_{d}
of the mass of
\mu_{d}
is the standard Gaussian measure. So the claim that the depth of
\pi L
in
\pi\mu_{d}
is at least
(1-\epsilon_{d})(1/2-\epsilon_{d})\geq 1/2-2\epsilon_{d}
follows.

Now by
Proposition

11
, the probability that
\operatorname{conv}(\pi_{L}X_{k+1},\dotsc,\pi_{L}X_{n})
contains
\pi L
is at least

(d-k+2)\binom{n-k}{d-k+2}\int_{0}^{1/2-2\epsilon_{d}}(y^{n-d-2}+(1-y)^{n-d-2})y^{d-k+1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y.

Now using the fact that
\big(1+x/y\big)^{y}>e^{xy/(x+y)}
, and
\binom{n}{k}=\binom{n}{n-k}
,

\displaystyle\binom{n}{k}(d-k+2)\binom{n-k}{d-k+2}\int_{0}^{1/2-2\epsilon_{d}}(y^{n-d-2}+(1-y)^{n-d-2})y^{d-k+1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geq\binom{n}{k}(d-k+2)\binom{n-k}{d-k+2}\int_{1/2-3\epsilon_{d}}^{1/2-2\epsilon_{d}}(y^{n-d-2}+(1-y)^{n-d-2})y^{d-k+1}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geq\binom{n}{k}(d-k+2)\binom{n-k}{d-k+2}\epsilon_{d}\Bigg(\frac{1}{2}-3\epsilon_{d}\Bigg)^{n-k-1}

\displaystyle=\binom{n}{k}(d-k+2)\epsilon_{d}\binom{n-k}{n-d-2}\frac{1}{2^{n-k-1}}\bigg(1-6\epsilon_{d}\bigg)^{n-k-1}

\displaystyle\geq\binom{n}{k}(d-k+2)\epsilon_{d}\binom{n-k}{n-d-2}\frac{1}{2^{n-k-1}}e^{-6\epsilon_{d}(n-k-1)/(6\epsilon_{d}+1)}.

Now since
n\sim\alpha d
and
k\sim\beta d
, we have that
\frac{n-d-2}{n-k}\sim\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha-\beta}
. And using the fact that
\binom{n}{k}\geq(1/(n+1))2^{nH(k/n)}
, for
d
sufficiently large the above is lower bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{2^{\alpha dH(\beta/\alpha)}2^{d(\alpha-\beta)H(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha-\beta})}}{2^{(\alpha-\beta)d}}\cdot\frac{(d-k+2)\epsilon_{d}e^{-6\epsilon_{d}(n-k-1)/(6\epsilon_{d}+1)}}{(n+1)(n-k+1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot 2^{d\big(\alpha H(\beta/\alpha)+(\alpha-\beta)(H(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha-\beta})-1)\big)}\cdot\frac{(d-k+2)\epsilon_{d}e^{-6\epsilon_{d}(n-k-1)/(6\epsilon_{d}+1)}}{(n+1)(n-k+1)}.

And the above quantity goes to infinity as
d\to\infty
because
\alpha H(\beta/\alpha)+(\alpha-\beta)(H(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha-\beta})-1)>0
and the term
e^{-6\epsilon_{d}(n-k-1)/(6\epsilon_{d}+1)}
is
\Omega(1/2^{\delta d})
for all
\delta>0
.

We have shown that
Section

3.2
is the integral over a set of measure
1-o(1)
of a function which is uniformly bounded from below by a function which approaches infinity as
d\to\infty
. This shows that the quantity in
Section

3.2
approaches infinity.
∎
