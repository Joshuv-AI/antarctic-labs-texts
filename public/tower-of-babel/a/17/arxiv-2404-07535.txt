Free boundary regularity for the inhomogeneous one-phase Stefan problem
Fausto Ferrari, Nicolò Forcillo, Davide Giovagnoli, David Jesus

Abstract




Abstract.
In this paper, we prove that flat free boundaries of solutions of the inhomogeneous one-phase Stefan problem are
C^{1,\alpha}
. The method consists of employing a hodograph transform and deriving the regularity via a linearization technique, following the approach introduced by De Silva, Forcillo, and Savin in
[
7
]
.

1
Introduction

The goal of this paper is to introduce new results concerning the regularity of flat free boundaries in a one-phase Stefan problem governed by an inhomogeneous equation. More precisely, we deal with nonnegative viscosity solutions
u:\Omega\times(0,T]\mapsto\mathbb{R}
of the free boundary problem,

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-\Delta u=f&\text{in}\,\,\Omega^{+}(u):=\left(\Omega\times(0,T]\right)\cap\{u>0\},\\
\partial_{t}u=\left\lvert\nabla u\right\rvert^{2}&\text{on}\,\,\mathcal{F}(u):=\left(\Omega\times(0,T]\right)\cap\partial\{u>0\},\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
\Omega
is a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
T>0
,
f:\Omega\times[0,T]\mapsto\mathbb{R},\,f\in C(\Omega\times[0,T])\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega\times[0,T])
.
\mathcal{F}(u)
is the so-called
free boundary
of the solution.

The Stefan problem consistently attracts attention since it models fundamental physical phenomena, especially the processes of ice melting or liquid water freezing. This topic plays an important role within the context of free boundary problems.
In literature, the Stefan problem has been treated in several ways, so, we refer the interested reader to
[
28
,
20
,
21
,
5
]
for a detailed discussion on the various formulations and results.

We are interested in the free boundary regularity of viscosity solutions of the problem, as in
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
7
]
. This topic had been treated in the trilogy
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, which collects important contributions to the free boundary regularity in the homogeneous Stefan problem. In these papers, the authors obtained sharp results in the two-phase setting.
Further achievements regarding the existence of analytic solutions can be found in
[
27
]
. We refer to
[
6
]
for the relationship between the solutions’ smoothness and the initial data regularity. In addition,
[
22
]
focused on the existence of a solution, while, in
[
19
]
, operators with variable coefficients have been studied, exploiting the same techniques of
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.

Recently, in
[
7
]
, a new approach for investigating the free boundary regularity has been introduced. Specifically, the authors addressed the regularity for the homogeneous one-phase Stefan problem by employing the
hodograph transform
jointly with the viscosity theory, constructing a notion of flat free boundary within an evolving framework. This approach follows the idea introduced in
[
8
]
in the elliptic scenario, and then widely applied in different cases, see, for instance,
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
and
[
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
for recent results in the elliptic degenerate nonlinear setting, see also
[
14
,
12
]
for comprehensive surveys.

In the parabolic setting, fully nonlinear operators in the homogeneous framework have been recently investigated in
[
29
]
. We also mention
[
25
]
, where a parabolic homogeneous two-phase problem with a transmission-type free boundary condition has been considered. In that work, the free boundary condition involves only spatial derivatives, in contrast to the one appearing in the Stefan problem.

In our paper, we deal with the inhomogeneous one-phase case (
1.1
), which models the melting of the ice in the presence of an external source or a sink of heat, following the approach introduced in
[
7
]
.
More precisely, we are interested in the regularity of the free boundary starting from a geometric condition, which we call
\varepsilon
-flatness and introduce in the next definition.

Definition 1.1
.

For
K>1
,
0<\lambda\leq 1
, and
\varepsilon>0
, we say that
u
is
\varepsilon
-flat in
B_{2\lambda}\times(-2K^{-1}\lambda,0]
if there exist two smooth functions
a_{n},b:\R\to\R
such that

a_{n}(t)\,\left(x_{n}-b(t)-\varepsilon\lambda\right)^{+}\leq u(x,t)\leq a_{n}(t)\,\left(x_{n}-b(t)+\varepsilon\lambda\right)^{+},\quad(\varepsilon\text{-flatness})

(1.2)

with

c(1+\|f^{-}\|_{\infty})\leq a_{n}\leq C,\quad\quad|a_{n}^{\prime}(t)|\leq\lambda^{-2},\quad\quad b^{\prime}(t)=-a_{n}(t),\quad\quad\|f\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\varepsilon^{2},

(1.3)

where
c,\,C>0
depend only on
K
and
n
. Here,
a_{n}^{\prime}
and
b^{\prime}
denote the derivatives of
a_{n}
and
b
, respectively.

We remark that the assumption
b^{\prime}(t)=-a_{n}(t)
in Definition
1.1
implies that the approximating function
a_{n}(t)(x_{n}-b(t))^{+}
satisfies the free boundary condition. Moreover, we emphasize that Definition
1.1
differs from the more common notion of
\varepsilon_{0}
-flatness of the free boundary, which we recall below.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
\partial_{x}\{u(\cdot,t)>0\}
denote the spatial boundary in
\R^{n}
of
\{u(\cdot,t)>0\}
. We say that
\partial_{x}\{u>0\}
is
\varepsilon_{0}
-flat in
B_{\lambda}
if, for every time
t
, there exists a direction
\nu
such that

\partial_{x}\{u(\cdot,t)>0\}\cap B_{\lambda}\subset\{|\left<x,\nu\right>|\leq\varepsilon_{0}\lambda\}

and

\displaystyle u=0\quad

\displaystyle\textit{ in }\quad\{\left<x,\nu\right><-\varepsilon_{0}\lambda\},

\displaystyle u>0\quad

\displaystyle\textit{ in }\quad\{\left<x,\nu\right>>\varepsilon_{0}\lambda\}.

We observe that a key difference from the elliptic framework is the definition of flatness of the free boundary, due to its evolution in time.

When
f\equiv 0
, Definition
1.1
reduces to the assumption of Theorem 1.2 in
[
7
]
. For the sake of clarity, in the sequel, we always refer to Definition
1.1
when we mention the
\varepsilon
-flatness of
u
.

In
[
7
, Lemma 2.1]
, the authors show that the geometric notion of flatness for the free boundary, stated in Definition
1.2
, combined with a nondegeneracy condition, implies the
\varepsilon
-flatness of the solution near the free boundary.
In our inhomogeneous case, the proof of this fact is provided in
[
13
, Theorem 1.1]
.

The main result of this paper is the following.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
K>1
. There exists
0<\lambda\leq 1
such that if
u
is a viscosity solution of the one-phase Stefan problem (
1.1
) in
B_{2\lambda}\times(-2K^{-1}\lambda,0]
,
0\in\mathcal{F}(u),
and
u
is
\varepsilon
-flat in the sense of Definition
1.1
for some small
\varepsilon
only depending on
K
and
n
, then the free boundary
\mathcal{F}(u)
is a
C^{1,\alpha}
-graph in the
x_{n}
-direction in
B_{\lambda}\times[-K^{-1}\lambda,0]
.

In other words, we prove the
C^{1,\alpha}
-regularity of flat free boundaries for solutions to (
1.1
).

We point out that it is enough to prove Theorem
1.3
under the assumptions

\lambda\leq\lambda_{0}\quad\text{and}\quad|a_{n}^{\prime}(t)|\leq c_{0}\lambda^{-2},

(1.4)

where
\lambda_{0}
and
c_{0}
are sufficiently small constants depending on
K
and
n
. This reduction can be achieved by considering the problem in a domain of size
\tau\lambda
for
\tau
small enough, and by relabeling
\tau\lambda
and
\varepsilon\tau^{-1}
as
\lambda
and
\varepsilon
, respectively.

The proof of Theorem
1.3
follows the approach in
[
7
]
. After applying the hodograph transform to solutions of (
1.1
), the problem formally reduces to studying viscosity solutions of

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u-\tr(A(\nabla u)D^{2}u)+(\partial_{x_{n}}u)\,f(x^{\prime},u,t)=0&\text{in}\,\,\{x_{n}>0\},\\
\partial_{t}u=g(\nabla u)&\text{on}\,\,\{x_{n}=0\},\end{cases}

(1.5)

where
A
and
g
are defined in (
3.2
) and (
3.3
).

The hodograph transform plays an important role in our analysis. Since it transforms the free boundary into a flat plane, it removes the main challenge in the study of free boundary problems. However, this difficulty is transferred into the solution itself since, in a small neighborhood of this flat interface, it might be multi-valued, thus failing to be a well-defined function. To address this subtle and important issue, we need to deal with the very weak notion of multi-valued viscosity solution, made precise in Definition
3.2
. Therefore, the problem of studying the free boundary regularity of (
1.1
) turns out to follow from the regularity up to the
fixed
boundary of solutions to (
1.5
). The proof of this last property relies on an improvement of flatness result, see Proposition
3.6
.

The hodograph transform introduces some extra difficulties in a small neighborhood of the free boundary, as already faced in
[
7
]
. The problem (
1.5
) is different from the one in
[
7
]
due to the presence of the gradient term
(\partial_{x_{n}}u)\,f(x^{\prime},u,t)
.
This fact introduces a further challenge in recovering the Harnack inequality, as in the homogeneous case it follows from the usual parabolic Harnack inequality. Specifically, we apply a more sophisticated Harnack inequality for the following extremal equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\mathcal{M}^{\pm}(D^{2}u)\mp\Lambda|\nabla u|+f=0,

which can be found in
[
24
]
.

Even though the equation in (
1.5
) does not fit the homogeneous framework, we prove that the achieved limiting profile is the same as the one obtained in the homogeneous case.
Consequently, we can exploit the Schauder-type estimates, obtained in
[
7
]
, with respect to an appropriate distance, which encodes features of both the parabolic and hyperbolic rescaling of the problem. The improved regularity for the linearized problem is then transferred to (
1.5
) by a comparison principle.

Let us mention that the argument developed in this paper can be combined with the one introduced in
[
29
]
to consider a fully nonlinear PDE in (
1.1
) governing the Stefan problem.

Now, we describe the structure of the paper. In Section
2
, we provide the necessary notation, definitions, and further remarks on the lack of scaling of the problem. In Section
3
, we apply the hodograph transform to change equation (
1.1
) into (
1.5
) and discuss how the initial assumptions translate into this new context. We end this section by stating the main improvement of flatness result. We devote Section
4
to study properties of the error function, which is defined as
u-\ell,
where
\ell
is the affine approximation of
u
, for each time
t
. In Section
5
, the proofs of the improvement of flatness and Theorem
1.3
are presented.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Notation and definitions

In this subsection, we introduce our main notation. As usual, given
(x,t)\in\R^{n+1}
, we call
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the space variable and
t\in\R
the time variable. A point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
will sometimes be written as
x=(x^{\prime},x_{n})
, where
x^{\prime}\in\R^{n-1}
and
x_{n}\in\R
.
For a function
u(x,t)
, we denote by
\nabla u
the gradient of
u
with respect to
x
,
D^{2}u
the Hessian with respect to
x
, and
\nabla_{x^{\prime}}u
the gradient with respect to
x^{\prime}
.
A
parabolic cylinder

\mathcal{P}
is any set of the form

\mathcal{P}=U\times(t_{1},t_{2}],

where
U
is a smooth bounded open domain in
\R^{n}
and
t_{1}<t_{2}
. We denote by
\partial_{p}\mathcal{P}
the
parabolic boundary
of
\mathcal{P}
, i.e.,
\partial_{p}{\mathcal{P}}=\left(U\times\{t_{1}\}\right)\cup\left(\partial U\times[t_{1},t_{2}]\right)
.

Given
(x,t),(y,s)\in\R^{n+1}
, we define the
parabolic distance
as

d_{p}((x,t),(y,s)):=\big(|x-y|^{2}+|t-s|\big)^{1/2}.

(2.1)

Let
C^{0}(\mathcal{P})
be the set of continuous functions in
{\mathcal{P}}
. For
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
u\in C^{0}(\mathcal{P})
, we define the parabolic Hölder semi-norm as

[u]_{C^{\alpha}(\mathcal{P})}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,t)\neq(y,s)\end{subarray}}\frac{\left\lvert u(x,t)-u(y,s)\right\rvert}{d_{p}((x,t),(y,s))^{\alpha}},

and the parabolic Hölder norm as

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}(\mathcal{P})}:=\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{P})}+[u]_{C^{\alpha}({\mathcal{P}})}.

Furthermore, we define the Hölder semi-norm in time to be

[u]_{C_{t}^{\alpha}({\mathcal{P}})}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}(x,t_{1})\neq(x,t_{2})\end{subarray}}\frac{|u(x,t_{1})-u(x,t_{2})|}{|t_{1}-t_{2}|^{\alpha}}.

This is necessary to introduce the
C^{1,\alpha}
-norm in
{\mathcal{P}}

\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}({\mathcal{P}})}:=\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{P}})}+\left\lVert\nabla u\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}({\mathcal{P}})}+[\nabla u]_{C^{\alpha}({\mathcal{P}})}+[u]_{C_{t}^{(1+\alpha)/2}({\mathcal{P}})}.

We denote by
C^{\alpha}({\mathcal{P}})
(resp.
C^{1,\alpha}({\mathcal{P}})
) the space of continuous (resp. differentiable in space) functions in
\mathcal{P}
, which are bounded in the
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{C^{\alpha}({\mathcal{P}})}
(resp.
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{C^{1,\alpha}({\mathcal{P}})}
) norm.

In the following, for simplicity, we consider parabolic cylinders of the form

\mathcal{P}_{r}(x_{0},t_{0}):=Q_{r}(x_{0})\times(t_{0}-r^{2},t_{0}],

(2.2)

where
n\geq 2
, given
r>0
, we set

Q_{r}:=(-r,r)^{n},\quad Q_{r}^{+}:=Q_{r}\cap\{x_{n}\geq 0\},\quad Q_{r}(x_{0}):=x_{0}+Q_{r}.

In addition, we denote by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{r}:=(Q_{r}\cap\{x_{n}>0\})\times(-r,0],

(2.3)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{r}:=\{(x,t)|\,x\in Q_{r}\cap\{x_{n}=0\},\,t\in(-r,0]\}.

It will be useful to define the
Dirichlet boundary
of
\mathcal{C}_{1}
as

\partial_{D}\mathcal{C}_{1}:=\partial\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap\left(\{t=-1\}\cup\{x_{n}=1\}\cup_{i=1}^{n-1}\{\left\lvert x_{i}\right\rvert=1\}\right),

which, together with
\mathcal{F}_{1},
represents the parabolic boundary of
\mathcal{C}_{1}
.

Lastly, for
K>1
, we recall the definition of extremal Pucci operators with ellipticity constants
K^{-1}
and
K

\mathcal{M}_{K}^{+}(N)=\max_{K^{-1}I\leq A\leq KI}\quad\tr(AN),\quad\quad\quad\mathcal{M}_{K}^{-}(N)=\min_{K^{-1}I\leq A\leq KI}\quad\tr(AN).

We now provide the definition of viscosity solution of (
1.1
).

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a continuous function
u
is a viscosity solution of (
1.1
) in
\Omega\times(0,T]
if it is nonnegative and its graph cannot be touched by above (resp. below) at a point
(x_{0},t_{0})\in,
in a parabolic cylinder
\mathcal{P}_{r}(x_{0},t_{0})\subset\Omega\times(0,T],

by the graph of a classical strict supersolution
\varphi^{+}
(resp. subsolution).
By a classical strict supersolution in
\mathcal{P}
, we mean that
\varphi(x,t)\in C^{2}(\mathcal{P}_{r}(x_{0},t_{0}))
,
\nabla\varphi\neq 0,
and it solves

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\varphi-\Delta\varphi>f&\text{in}\,\,\mathcal{P}_{r}(x_{0},t_{0})\cap\{\varphi>0\},\\
\partial_{t}\varphi>\left\lvert\nabla\varphi\right\rvert^{2}&\text{on}\,\,\mathcal{P}_{r}(x_{0},t_{0})\cap\partial\{\varphi>0\}.\end{cases}

Similarly, we can define a strict classical subsolution.

We want to relate our inhomogeneous equation to the homogeneous one studied in
[
7
]
. Hence, we start by performing a rescaling to ensure that we can assume, without loss of generality, that the source term
f
is arbitrarily small. Specifically, if
u
is a viscosity solution of (
1.1
) in
\mathcal{P}_{1}(0,0)
, see (
2.2
), calling

\Lambda:=\frac{\|f\|^{\frac{1}{2}}_{L^{\infty}}}{\varepsilon_{1}},

then the function

\bar{u}(x,t)=u(\Lambda^{-1}x,\Lambda^{-2}t),\quad(x,t)\in\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(0,0),

is a solution of

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\bar{u}-\Delta\bar{u}=\bar{f}&\text{in}\,\,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(0,0)\cap\{\bar{u}>0\},\\
\partial_{t}\bar{u}=\left\lvert\nabla\bar{u}\right\rvert^{2}&\text{on}\,\,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}(0,0)\cap\partial\{\bar{u}>0\},\end{cases}

with
\|\bar{f}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq\varepsilon_{1}^{2}
. For ease of notation, we will refer to this function as
u
.

2.2
Some remarks about the scalings of the problem

We recall that, when dealing with a problem such as (
1.1
), a dichotomy often arises in the application of the rescaling argument, due to the differing scaling behaviors of the PDE and the free boundary condition, as already noted in
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.
Indeed, the Stefan problem exhibits a natural mixed scaling: parabolic in the interior and hyperbolic near the free boundary.

If we apply the hyperbolic rescaling, for instance, centered at
(0,0)\in\mathcal{F}(u)
, given by

(x,t)\mapsto u_{\lambda}^{H}(x,t)\coloneqq\frac{u(\lambda x,\lambda t)}{\lambda},\quad(x,t)\in B_{1}\times(-1,0],

we obtain that
u_{\lambda}^{H}
solves a Stefan problem with a rescaled invariant free boundary condition, but with a possibly large diffusion coefficient
\lambda^{-1}
in the equation, that is,

\begin{cases}\lambda\partial_{t}u_{\lambda}^{H}-\Delta u_{\lambda}^{H}=\lambda f(\lambda x,\lambda t)&\text{in}\,\,\left(B_{1}\times(-1,0]\right)\cap\{u_{\lambda}^{H}>0\},\\
\partial_{t}u_{\lambda}^{H}=\left\lvert\nabla u_{\lambda}^{H}\right\rvert^{2}&\text{on}\,\,\left(B_{1}\times(-1,0]\right)\cap\partial\{u_{\lambda}^{H}>0\}.\end{cases}

(2.4)

Note that, letting
\lambda\to 0^{+}
, a solution of (
2.4
) formally solves the Hele-Shaw equation for which information about time regularity is lost. We point out that for this problem, recently, in
[
23
]
, it has been shown that flat free boundaries are
C^{1,\alpha}
at each time possibly admitting the presence of a nonnegative source and a drift term.

On the other hand, if we consider the parabolic rescaling,

(x,t)\mapsto u_{\lambda}^{P}(x,t)\coloneqq\frac{u(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t)}{\lambda},\quad(x,t)\in B_{1}\times(-\lambda^{-1},0],

then we get an equation of the same form, but a different free boundary condition depending on the coefficient
\lambda
appears. More precisely, it holds

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u_{\lambda}^{P}-\Delta u_{\lambda}^{P}=\lambda f(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t)&\text{in}\,\,\left(B_{1}\times(-\lambda^{-1},0]\right)\cap\{u_{\lambda}^{P}>0\},\\
\partial_{t}u_{\lambda}^{P}=\lambda\left\lvert\nabla u_{\lambda}^{P}\right\rvert^{2}&\text{on}\,\,\left(B_{1}\times(-\lambda^{-1},0]\right)\cap\partial\{u_{\lambda}^{P}>0\}.\end{cases}

Thus, the limiting problem leads to no motion of the free boundary, and therefore, no regularization is expected in this case.
These two alternative representations of the problem highlight the main difference with respect to the elliptic situation.

3
The nonlinear problem arising from the hodograph transform

In this section, we discuss the application of the hodograph transform introduced in
[
7
]
to our problem.
To avoid confusion, we will use
u_{S}
to denote a solution of the Stefan problem (
1.1
) and
u_{H}
for its hodograph transform.

First, we look at the graph of
u_{S}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}

\Gamma:=\{(x,x_{n+1},t)|\;x_{n+1}=u_{S}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n},t)\}

(3.1)

as the graph of a possibly multi-valued function
u_{H}
with respect to the
x_{n}
-coordinate

\Gamma:=\{(x,x_{n+1},t)|\;x_{n}=u_{H}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n-1},x_{n+1},t)\}.

We say that
u_{H}
is
graphical
with respect to the direction
e_{n+1}
since its graph can be expressed by (
3.1
) where
u_{S}
is a single-valued function.
If we denote the coordinates
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n-1},x_{n+1})
by
(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
, then we can have a formal expression of
\nabla u_{S}
in terms of
\nabla u_{H}
for a point on the graph
\Gamma
.
Precisely, we can rewrite

x_{n+1}=u_{S}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{n},t)=u_{S}(y^{\prime},u_{H}(y,t),t).

Hence, differentiation in
x_{n+1}
gives

1=\partial_{x_{n+1}}u_{S}=\partial_{x_{n}}u_{S}\cdot\partial_{x_{n+1}}u_{H},

which implies

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{n}}u_{S}=\frac{1}{\partial_{x_{n+1}}u_{H}}.

On the other hand, for
x_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,n-1
, and
t
, we have, using the previous equality,

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{x_{j}}u_{S}+\partial_{x_{n}}u_{S}\cdot\partial_{x_{j}}u_{H}\Longrightarrow\partial_{x_{j}}u_{S}=-\frac{\partial_{x_{j}}u_{H}}{\partial_{x_{n+1}}u_{H}},

\displaystyle{0=\partial_{t}u_{S}+\partial_{x_{n}}u_{S}\cdot\partial_{t}u_{H}\Longrightarrow\partial_{t}u_{S}=-\frac{\partial_{t}u_{H}}{\partial_{x_{n+1}}u_{H}},}

and thus

\displaystyle\nabla u_{S}=-\frac{1}{\partial_{y_{n}}u_{H}}\left(\nabla_{y^{\prime}}u_{H},-1\right),\quad\partial_{t}u_{S}=-\frac{\partial_{t}u_{H}}{\partial_{y_{n}}u_{H}},

\displaystyle D^{2}u_{S}=-\frac{1}{\partial_{y_{n}}u_{H}}{(A(\nabla u_{H}))^{T}}\,D^{2}u_{H}\,A(\nabla u_{H}),

where
A(\nabla u_{H})
is a square matrix which coincides with the identity except for the
n
-th row in which the entries are given by the right hand side of
\nabla u_{S}.
Then, we can transform the Stefan problem (
1.1
) to a quasilinear parabolic equation with oblique derivative boundary condition of the type

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u_{H}-\tr(\bar{A}(\nabla u_{H})D^{2}u_{H})+\partial_{y_{n}}u_{H}\,f(y^{\prime},u_{H},t)=0&\text{in}\,\,\{y_{n}>0\},\\
\partial_{t}u_{H}=g(\nabla u_{H})&\text{on}\,\,\,\,\{y_{n}=0\},\end{cases}

(3.2)

where, taking
p=(p^{\prime},p_{n})\in\R^{n},

g(p)=-\frac{1}{p_{n}}\left(1+\left\lvert p^{\prime}\right\rvert^{2}\right)

(3.3)

and

\bar{A}(p)=\begin{pNiceArray},

with eigenvalues

\spec(\bar{A})=\left\{1,\frac{1+\left\lvert p\right\rvert^{2}-\sqrt{\left(1+\left\lvert p\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{2}-4p_{n}^{2}}}{2p_{n}^{2}},\frac{1+\left\lvert p\right\rvert^{2}+\sqrt{\left(1+\left\lvert p\right\rvert^{2}\right)^{2}-4p_{n}^{2}}}{2p_{n}^{2}}\right\}.

The matrix
\bar{A}
is positive definite and
\partial_{p_{n}}g(p)>0
as long as
p_{n}\neq 0
. Define

\mathcal{R}_{K}:=B_{K}(0)\cap\{p_{n}\geq K^{-1}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

and notice that if
p\in\mathcal{R}_{K}
, then
\partial_{{p_{n}}}g(p)>0
.
If
K>0
is chosen large enough, then we may assume that
\tr(\bar{A}(p)M)
is uniformly elliptic in
M
with ellipticity constants
K
and
K^{-1}
in
\mathcal{R}_{K}
.
Furthermore, in this set, we can suppose that

\left\lvert\nabla_{p}\tr(\bar{A}(p)M)\right\rvert\leq K\left\lvert M\right\rvert,\quad\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{C^{1}}\leq K,\quad\partial_{p_{n}}g(p)\geq K^{-1}.

(3.4)

The hodograph transform is very useful because it flattens the free boundary, and thus, the problem is transformed from a free boundary problem into one with a fixed boundary but with possible multi-valued solutions. This issue of multi-valued functions becomes the main subtlety with the transformed equation (
3.2
).
Indeed, one must adapt the definition of viscosity solution to include possibly multi-valued functions as solutions of the following fully nonlinear problem, recall (
2.3
),

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u=F(x,t,u,\nabla u,D^{2}u)&\text{in $\mathcal{C}_{\lambda}$ }\\
\partial_{t}u=g(\nabla u)&\text{on $\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}$.}\end{cases}

(3.5)

Definition 3.1
.

We say that a continuous function

\displaystyle\leq\tr(A(a(\lambda t+\varepsilon_{1}\nabla v))D^{2}v)+K\varepsilon_{1}M^{2}-C\delta\leq\tr(A(a(\lambda t+\varepsilon_{1}\nabla v))D^{2}v)+K\varepsilon_{1}M^{2}-C\delta

\displaystyle-

.
Then,

\displaystyle|p_{k}-q_{k}|\leq\operatorname{osc}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{B}_{\lambda,{d_{p}((x_{k},t_{k}),(z_{k},r_{k}))}}(x_{k},t_{k})\end{subarray}}w_{k}+\operatorname{osc}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{B}_{\lambda,{d_{p}((z_{k},r_{k}),(y_{k},s_{k}))}}(y_{k},s_{k})\end{subarray}}w_{k}\leq 2\omega(3\delta_{k}){.}

Letting
k\to\infty
, we again reach a contradiction.

Observe that for any
(x,t)\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{1/2}}
, there is some
p\in[-1,1]
such that
(x,t,p)\in K
. Indeed, let
(x,t)\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{1/2}}
and consider
(x,t,w_{k}(x,t))\subset G_{k}
. By (
4.6
), for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
{(k_{0})_{\varepsilon}}\geq 1
such that
{d((x,t,w_{k}(x,t)),K)}\leq\varepsilon
for all
k\geq{(k_{0})_{\varepsilon}}
. Taking
\varepsilon=1/j
, by a diagonalization argument, we obtain that there is a subsequence of
\{(x,t,w_{k}(x,t))\}_{k\geq 1}
converging to
(x,t,p)\in K
, where
|p|\leq 1
by
(i)
.

We define the function
\phi:\overline{\mathcal{C}_{1/2}}\to[-1,1]
as
\phi(x,t):=p
, if
x_{n}>0
.
Let
(x,t){\neq}(y,s)\in\overline{\mathcal{C}_{1/2}}
. Then,

|\phi(x,t)-\phi(y,s)|\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}\operatorname{osc}_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathcal{B}_{\lambda,\delta}(x,t)\end{subarray}}w_{k}\leq\omega({\delta}),

where we used that
d_{p}((x,t),(y,s))=:\delta\geq\delta_{k}>0
for
k
large enough. This proves that
\phi
is uniformly continuous with a modulus
\omega.

To also include the case
x_{n}=0
, we point out that
w_{k}(x^{\prime},\varepsilon,t)\rightarrow\phi(x^{\prime},\varepsilon,t)
as
k\rightarrow\infty
for every
\varepsilon>0
. Being
\phi
bounded in
\mathcal{C}_{1/2}
by definition, we define
\phi(x^{\prime},0,t):=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\phi(x^{\prime},\varepsilon,t)
, where the limit exists up to a subsequence.

\square

Proof of Lemma
4.9
.

We argue by contradiction by assuming that there exist sequences of
\delta_{k}\rightarrow 0
and
w_{k}
satisfying the assumption with
\delta_{k}
instead of
\bar{\delta}
. Therefore, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that

\left\lVert w_{k}-\phi\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{C}_{1/2})}>\varepsilon_{0}

holds for every
\phi\in C_{d_{\lambda}}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{C}_{1/2})
.
However, this immediately leads to a contradiction by applying Lemma
4.10
.

\square

5
Proofs of the main results

Proof of Proposition
3.6
.

Since the limiting problem, (
3.17
), coincides with the one in the homogeneous case, this proof is identical to the one presented in
[
7
]
, having obtained the analogous results for the error function
w
in the inhomogeneous case, see Subsection
4.3
. Anyway, for the sake of completeness, we include the proof. We proceed in two steps.

Step 1.

First, we prove that there exists a function
v
solving (
3.17
), which approximates well
w
in
\mathcal{C}_{1/2}
, that is,

\left\lvert v-w\right\rvert\leq C\delta\quad\text{in }\mathcal{C}_{1/2},

(5.1)

as long as
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{1}(\delta)
.

To this end, we want to apply Lemma
4.9
to
w
with
\bar{\varepsilon}=\delta
. We thus take
\varepsilon
so small that
\varepsilon^{1/2}\leq\bar{\delta}
, only depending on
\delta
and universal constants, with
\bar{\delta}
given in Lemma
4.9
. We remark that our assumptions imply that we are in the conditions of the lemma, which guarantees the existence of a (single-valued) function
\phi\in C_{d_{\lambda}}^{\alpha}({\overline{\mathcal{C}_{1/2}}})
such that

\left\lvert w-\phi\right\rvert\leq\delta,\qquad\left\lVert\phi\right\rVert_{C_{d_{\lambda}}^{\alpha}({\overline{\mathcal{C}_{1/2}}})}\leq C.

(5.2)

In view of Proposition
4.2
, let
v
be the solution of (
3.17
) in
\mathcal{C}_{1/2}
with
v=\phi
on
\partial_{D}\mathcal{C}_{1/2}
, satisfying

\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{C_{d_{\lambda}}^{\alpha}(\overline{\mathcal{C}_{1/2}})}\leq C.

(5.3)

From this, together with (
5.2
), if
d_{\lambda}((x,t),\partial_{D}\mathcal{C}_{1/2})\leq\delta^{1/\alpha}
, then there exists
(y,s)\in\partial_{D}\mathcal{C}_{1/2}
for which

\displaystyle\left\lvert v(x,t)-\phi(y,s)\right\rvert\leq C_{1}\delta,

\displaystyle\left\lvert w(x,t)-\phi(y,s)\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert w(x,t)-\phi(x,t)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\phi(x,t)-\phi(y,s)\right\rvert\leq\delta+Cd_{\lambda}((x,t),(y,s))^{\alpha}\leq C_{2}\delta.

Therefore, we have

|v-w|\leq C\delta\quad\text{in }\mathcal{C}_{1/2}\cap\{(x,t):\,\,d_{\lambda}((x,t),\partial_{D}\mathcal{C}_{1/2})\leq\delta^{1/\alpha}\}.

(5.4)

In particular, for
\Omega:=\mathcal{C}_{1/2}\cap\{d_{\lambda}((x,t)),\partial_{D}\mathcal{C}_{1/2})>\delta^{1/\alpha}\}
, it holds

|v-w|\leq C\delta\quad\textit{ on }\,{\partial_{D}\Omega}.

Using the estimates of Proposition
4.2
, we obtain
\left\lvert\nabla v\right\rvert,\left\lvert D^{2}v\right\rvert\leq C(\delta)
in
\Omega
, recalling (
5.3
). Consequently, by Corollary
4.4
, it holds

\left\lvert v-w\right\rvert\leq C\delta\quad\textit{in }\Omega,

which proves (
5.1
), together with (
5.4
).

Step 2.
Now, applying Proposition
4.1
to the solution
v
above, we achieve

\left\lvert w-\ell_{\bar{a},\bar{b}}\right\rvert\leq\left\lvert w-v\right\rvert+\left\lvert v-\ell_{\bar{a},\bar{b}}\right\rvert\leq C\delta+\bar{C}\rho^{1+\alpha}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{C}_{\rho},\quad{0<\rho\leq\frac{1}{2}}.

We then choose
\tau
small and universal in such a way that if
\rho=\tau
and
\delta=\tau^{1+\alpha/2}
, we have
\delta\leq c^{\prime}
, with
c^{\prime}
given by Proposition
4.8
, and

\left\lvert w-\ell_{\bar{a},\bar{b}}\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{4}\tau\quad\text{in }\mathcal{C}_{\tau},\quad\left\lvert\bar{a}_{n}^{\prime}\right\rvert\leq\frac{1}{4}\tau^{-2}\delta\lambda^{-1}.

Going back to the original function
u
, see (
3.15
), we get

\left\lvert(u-\ell_{a,b})(\lambda x,\lambda t)-\varepsilon\lambda\ell_{\bar{a},\bar{b}}(x,t)\right\rvert\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\tau\lambda\quad\text{in }\mathcal{C}_{\tau},

which gives

\left\lvert(u-\ell_{a,b})(x,t)-\varepsilon\lambda\ell_{\bar{a},\bar{b}}\bigg(\frac{x}{\lambda},\frac{t}{\lambda}\bigg)\right\rvert\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\tau\lambda\quad\text{in }\mathcal{C}_{\tau\lambda}.

Setting

\tilde{a}(t):=a(t)+\varepsilon\bar{a}\left(\frac{t}{\lambda}\right),\quad\hat{b}(t):=b(t)+\varepsilon\lambda\bar{b}\left(\frac{t}{\lambda}\right),

we can rewrite the previous inequality as

\left\lvert u-\ell_{\tilde{a},\hat{b}}\right\rvert\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\tau\lambda\quad\text{in }\mathcal{C}_{\tau\lambda},\quad\left\lvert\tilde{a}_{n}^{\prime}\right\rvert\leq\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{\lambda^{2}}\left(1+\frac{1}{4\tau^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon\delta}{2(\tau\lambda)^{2}}.

To have a control on the behavior of
b
, we define
\tilde{b}
as the solution of the ODE

\tilde{b}^{\prime}=g(\tilde{a}),\quad\tilde{b}(0)=\hat{b}(0),

for which we have

\hat{b}^{\prime}(t)=b^{\prime}(t)+\varepsilon\bar{b}^{\prime}\left(\frac{t}{\lambda}\right)=g(a(t))+\varepsilon\nabla g(a(t))\cdot\bar{a}\left(\frac{t}{\lambda}\right)=g(\tilde{a}(t))+O(\varepsilon^{2})=\tilde{b}^{\prime}(t)+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

Therefore, if
t\in[-\tau\lambda,0],
then

|(\tilde{b}-\hat{b})(t)|\leq C\varepsilon^{2}|t|\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\tau\lambda.

This allows us to estimate

\displaystyle\left\lvert u-\ell_{\tilde{a},\tilde{b}}\right\rvert

\displaystyle=\left\lvert u-\tilde{a}(t)\cdot x-\tilde{b}(t)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\,\left\lvert u-\tilde{a}(t)\cdot x-\hat{b}(t))\right\rvert+\left\lvert\tilde{b}(t)-\hat{b}(t)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\,\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\tau\lambda+C\varepsilon^{2}\tau\lambda

\displaystyle\leq\,\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\tau\lambda

for
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{1}
small enough, as intended, concluding the proof.

\square

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

As already pointed out in Remark
3.4
, Theorem
1.3
is equivalent to obtaining
C^{1,\alpha}
-estimates up to the boundary
\{x_{n}=0\}
for the hodograph transform. In the same remark, we highlighted how the hypotheses of Theorem
1.3
translate into Proposition
3.6
. In particular, we can take
\lambda\leq\min\{\delta\varepsilon_{1},\lambda_{1}\}
and assume that
a_{0}(t)=(0,\ldots,0,(a_{0})_{n}(t))\in\mathcal{R}_{K/2}
.

We can thus iterate Proposition indefinitely
3.6
in cylinders
\mathcal{C}_{\lambda_{k}}
with
\lambda_{k}:=\lambda\tau^{k}
and
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{k}:=\varepsilon_{1}2^{-k}.
If we write
k=(\log_{\tau}\lambda_{k}-\log_{\tau}\lambda),
then

{\varepsilon_{k}=\varepsilon_{1}\left(\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda}\right)^{-\log_{\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda}}2\,\cdot\,\log_{\tau}\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda}}}=\varepsilon_{1}\left(\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda}\right)^{-\frac{1}{\log_{2}\tau}}=\varepsilon_{1}\lambda^{\frac{1}{\log_{2}\tau}}\lambda_{k}^{-\frac{1}{\log_{2}\tau}}=:C(\lambda)\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}.

Furthermore, the given coefficients
a_{k}(t)
are still in
\mathcal{R}_{K/2}
, for every
k
, since it holds

\left\lvert a_{k}(t)-a_{0}(t)\right\rvert\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left\lvert a_{j}(t)-a_{j-1}(t)\right\rvert\leq C\sum_{j=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{{j-1}}\leq C\varepsilon_{1}.

In addition,

|u-\ell_{a_{k},b_{k}}|\leq\varepsilon_{k}\lambda_{k}=C(\lambda)\lambda_{k}^{1+\alpha}\quad\text{in }\mathcal{C}_{\lambda_{k}}

(5.5)

for all
k\geq 0
, which implies pointwise
C^{1,\alpha}
-regularity in space at
0
.

We also have, recalling the definition of
\lambda_{k}
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert a_{k+n}{(t)}-a_{k}{(t)}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\lvert a_{k+j}{(t)}-a_{k+j-1}{(t)}\right\rvert\leq C\lambda^{\alpha}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\tau^{({k+j})\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda^{\alpha}\tau^{k\alpha}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\tau^{j\alpha}=C\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\frac{1}{1-\tau^{\alpha}}\leq C\lambda_{k}^{\alpha},

for
\tau\leq 2^{-\alpha}
.
So,
a_{k}{(t)}
is a Cauchy sequence, and for
k\rightarrow\infty
, it holds
a_{k}(t)\rightarrow a_{\infty}{(t)}
with
a_{\infty}{(t)}=\nabla u(0,t)
. Then,

\left\lvert\nabla u(0,t)-a_{k}(t)\right\rvert\leq\sum_{j={k}}^{\infty}\left\lvert a_{j}(t)-a_{j+1}(t)\right\rvert\leq C\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}.

Now, if
t,s
are such that
\left\lvert t-s\right\rvert=\lambda_{k}^{2}
, we get, from (
3.14
),

\displaystyle\left\lvert\nabla u(0,t)-\nabla u(0,s)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\nabla u(0,t)-a_{k}(s)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\nabla u(0,s)-a_{k}(s)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}+2\varepsilon_{1}\lambda_{k}=C_{1}\left\lvert t-s\right\rvert^{\alpha/2}.

Thus, for any
t,s\in[-\lambda_{k},0]
,

\displaystyle\left\lvert a_{k}(t)-a_{k}(s)\right\rvert\leq

\displaystyle\left\lvert a_{k}(t)-\nabla u(0,t)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\nabla u(0,t)-\nabla u(0,s)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\nabla u(0,s)-a_{k}(s)\right\rvert

(5.6)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}+{C_{1}}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha/2}+C\lambda_{k}^{\alpha}\leq{C_{2}}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha/2}.

Being
b_{k}^{\prime}{(t)}=g(a_{k}{(t)})\in C^{1}
, by
(
3.4
) and (
3.13
), we have

\left\lvert b^{\prime\prime}(t)\right\rvert\leq\bar{C}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha-2},

which yields, with
t\in(t_{0}-\lambda_{k}^{2},t_{0}+\lambda_{k}^{2})
, we get

\ \left\lvert b_{k}(t)-b_{k}(0)-b_{k}^{\prime}(0)t\right\rvert\leq C_{3}\lambda_{k}^{\alpha+2}.

Finally, exploiting this and (
5.6
), we reach, according to (
5.5
),

\displaystyle\left\lvert u-(a_{k}(0)\cdot x+b_{k}^{\prime}(0)t+b_{k}(0))\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert u-\ell_{a_{k},b_{k}}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\ell_{a_{k},b_{k}}-\ell_{a_{k},b_{k}}{(x,0)}-b_{k}^{\prime}(0)t\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda_{k}^{1+\alpha}+\left\lvert(a_{k}(t)-a_{k}(0))\cdot x\right\rvert+\left\lvert b_{k}(t)-b_{k}(0)-b_{k}^{\prime}(0)t\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda_{k}^{1+\alpha}+{C_{2}}\lambda_{k}^{1+\alpha/2}+{C_{3}}\lambda_{k}^{1+\alpha/2}\leq C(\lambda)\lambda_{k}^{1+\alpha/2}\quad\text{in }C_{\lambda_{k}}.

\square
