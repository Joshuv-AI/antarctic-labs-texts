Characterizations of Urysohn universal ultrametric spaces
Yoshito Ishiki

Abstract
. In this paper, using the existence of infinite equidistant subsets of closed balls, we first characterize the injectivity of ultrametric spaces for finite ultrametric spaces. This method also gives characterizations of the Urysohn universal ultrametric spaces. As an application, we find that the operations of the Cartesian product and the hyperspaces preserve the structures of the Urysohn universal ultrametric spaces. Namely, let ( X , d ) (X,d) be the Urysohn universal ultrametric space. Then we show that ( X × X , d × d ) (X\times X,d\times d) is isometric to ( X , d ) (X,d) , and show that the hyperspace consisting of all non-empty compact subsets of ( X , d ) (X,d) and symmetric products of ( X , d ) (X,d) are isometric to ( X , d ) (X,d) . We also establish that every complete ultrametric space injective for finite ultrametric space contains a subspace isometric to ( X , d ) (X,d) .

Backgrounds

For a class

-injective

For a cardinal

,

Definition 1.3
.

Let

and

be a range set.
An ultrametric space

is

and for every

,

-equidistant subset

.

Definition 1.4
.

Let

\kappa\leq\omega_{0}

and

R

be a range set.
An ultrametric space

(X,d)

is

(R,\kappa)
-avoidant

if
for every

a\in X
,
every

r\in R\setminus\{0\}
,
and for every subset

of

\mathrm{B}(a,r)

p\in\mathrm{B}(a,r)

.

The following theorem is our first main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let

R

be a range set.
Let

(X,d)

be an

R
-valued ultrametric space.
Then,

,

(A1)

The space

(X,d)

is

(R,n)
-haloed.

(A2)

The space

(X,d)

(R,n)

(A3)

The space

(X,d)

-injective.

(B1)

The space

(X,d)

is

(R,\omega_{0})
-haloed.

(B2)

The space

(X,d)

is

(R,\omega_{0})
-avoidant.

(B3)

(X,d)

\mathscr{N}(R,\omega_{0})

Remark 1.1
.

The proof of Key Lemma in

In

-injective

,

-equidistant subset

For metric spaces

.

-injective.

For a metric space

(X,d)

and for a subset of

,

Lemma 2.1
.

Let

(X,d)

be an ultrametric space.
Then the following are true:

(1)

For every triple

x,y,z\in X

the inequality

implies

.

(2)

For every

a\in X
,
for
every

r\in(0,\infty)
,
and for every

we have

\mathrm{B}(a,r)=\mathrm{B}(q,r)

(3)

For every pair

and for every pair

,

then

\mathrm{B}(a,r)\subseteq\mathrm{B}(b,l)

or

\mathrm{B}(b,l)\subseteq\mathrm{B}(a,r)
.

2.2.
Urysohn universal ultrametric spaces

A subset

E

of

[0,\infty)

is said to be

semi-sporadic

if
there exists a strictly decreasing sequence

\{a_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}

in

(0,\infty)

such that

\lim_{i\to\infty}a_{i}=0

and

E=\{0\}\cup\{\,a_{i}\mid i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\,\}
.
A subset of

[0,\infty)

is
said to be

tenuous

if it is finite or semi-sporadic
(see

[
13
]
).
For a range set

R
,
we denote by

\mathbf{TEN}(R)

the set of all tenuous
range subsets of

R
.
For a metric space

(X,d)
,
and for a subset

A

of

X
,
we write

d(x,A)=\inf_{a\in A}d(x,a)
.
In this paper,
we often represent a restricted metric
as the same symbol to the ambient one.
We now provide the definition of
the petaloid ultrametric space that is introduced in

[
14
]
by the author.

Definition 2.1
.

Let

R

be an uncountable range set.
We say that a metric space

(X,d)

is

R
-petaloid

if
it is an

R
-valued ultrametric space
and
there exists a family

\{\Pi(X,S)\}_{S\in\mathbf{TEN}(R)}

of subspaces of

X

satisfying the following properties:

(P1)

If

S\in\mathbf{TEN}(R)

satisfies

S\neq\{0\}
,
then

(\Pi(X,S),d)

is
isometric to the

S
-Urysohn
universal ultrametric space.
Namely,

(\Pi(X,S),d)

is a
separable complete

\mathscr{N}(S)
-injective

S
-valued ultrametric space.

(P2)

We have

\bigcup_{S\in\mathbf{TEN}(R)}\Pi(X,S)=X
.

(P3)

If

S,T\in\mathbf{TEN}(R)
,
then

\Pi(X,S)\cap\Pi(X,T)=\Pi(X,S\cap T)
.

(P4)

If

S,T\in\mathbf{TEN}(R)

and

x\in\Pi(X,T)
,
then

belongs to

\{0\}\cup(T\setminus S)
.

We call the family

\{\Pi(X,S)\}_{S\in\mathbf{TEN}(R)}

an

R
-petal of

,
and call

\Pi(X,S)

the

S
-piece
of the

R
-petal

\{\Pi(X,S)\}_{S\in\mathbf{TEN}(R)}
.
We simply write

\Pi(S)=\Pi(X,S)

when the whole space is clear by the context.

Remark 2.1
.

For every uncountable range set

R
,
and for every

R
-petaloid space

(X,d)
,
the petal

\Pi(X,\{0\})

Let

-petaloid ultrametric space.

,

Remark 2.2

Characterizations of injectivity

In this section,

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

R

be a range set.
Then the following statements are true:

(1)

An ultrametric space

(X,d)

is

(R,\omega_{0})
-haloed
if and only if

(X,d)

is

(R,n)
-haloed
for all

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
.

(2)

An ultrametric space

(X,d)

is

(R,\omega_{0})
-avoidant
if and only if

(X,d)

is

(R,n)
-avoidant
for all

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
.

(3)

An
ultrametric space

(X,d)

is

\mathscr{N}(R,\omega_{0})
-injective
if and only if

(X,d)

is

\mathscr{N}(R,n)
-injective
for all

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
.

Proof.

The statements

(2)

and

(3)

follow from
Definitions

1.3

and

1.4
.
Next we prove

(1)
.
We only need to show that
if

(X,d)

is

(R,n)
-haloed
for all

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
,
then it is

\mathscr{N}(R,\omega_{0})
.
Take

a\in X

and

r\in R\setminus\{0\}
.
We define a relation

\mathbin{\sim_{r}}

on

\mathrm{B}(a,r)

by declaring that

x\mathbin{\sim_{r}}y

means

d(x,y)<r
.
From the strong triangle inequality,
it follows that

\mathbin{\sim_{r}}

is an equivalence relation
on

\mathrm{B}(a,r)
.
Let

denote the quotient set of

\mathrm{B}(a,r)

by

\mathbin{\sim_{r}}
.
Since

(X,d)

is

(R,n)
-haloed
for all

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
,
we notice that

n\leq\card(Q)

for all

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
.
Therefore

Q

is infinite.
Take a complete system

W

of representatives of

Q
.
Then

W

becomes an infinite

r
-equidistant subset of

\mathrm{B}(a,r)
.
This proves that

(X,d)

is

\mathscr{N}(R,\omega_{0})
-injective.
∎

The next three lemmas play key roles to
prove our first main result.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

R

be a range set and

.
If an ultrametric space

(R,n)
-haloed,

(R,n)
-avoidant.

To show that

is

(R,n)
-avoidant,
take

,

and take

of

\mathrm{B}(a,r)

is

(R,n)

-equidistant subset

is an ultrametric,

Lemma 3.3
.

Let

R

be a range set and

n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
.
If an ultrametric space

is

(R,n)
-avoidant,

\mathscr{N}(R,n+1)
-injective.

Proof.

Take an arbitrary

R
-valued ultrametric space

in

\mathscr{N}(R,n+1)

.
Notice that

\card(Y\sqcup\{\theta\})<n+1

To show that

(X,d)
is

\mathscr{N}(R,n+1)
-injective,
we only need to verify that

\Phi\colon Y\sqcup\{\theta\}\to X

with

\Phi|_{Y}=\phi

Put

r=\min_{y\in Y}e(y,\theta)

(i)

In this situation, we have

r\in R

Put

Notice that

Since

(R,n)

d(a,t)=r

We divide the

From

Case 2. [

Using

The inequality

Next we define a map

\mathscr{N}(R,n+1)

The proof of Theorem

.

Take

Of course,

We now consider a one-point extension of

Since each

and

\rho\times_{p}\rho

on

and since

the function

is not

-injective,
we shall show that we can take a point

\mathbf{r}\in[2^{-1},1]^{4}

such that
there is no point

(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{U}\times\mathbb{U}

satisfying that:

(I)

e_{\mathbf{r}}((s_{i},s_{j}),\theta)=(\rho\times_{p}\rho)((s_{i},s_{j}),(\alpha,\beta))

for all

.

\mathbf{r}\in[2^{-1},1]^{4}

.

,

,

,

Then the next two statements are true:

(1)

If

(X,d)

is separable, then
so is

(\mathscr{K}_{m,l}(X),\mathcal{HD}_{d})
.

(2)

If

(X,d)

is complete,
then
so is

(\mathscr{K}_{m,l}(X),\mathcal{HD}_{d})
.

Proof.

To prove

(1)
,
take a countable dense subset

of

.
Then the set of all finite non-empty subsets of

is dense in

(\mathscr{K}(X),\mathcal{HD}_{d})
.
Thus, it is separable.
Since

\mathscr{K}_{m,l}(X)

is a metric subspace of

\mathscr{K}(X)
,
the space

(\mathscr{K}_{m,l}(X),\mathcal{HD}_{d})

is separable.

Next we show

(2)
.
The completeness of

(\mathscr{K}(X),\mathcal{HD}_{d})

can be proven in
a
similar way to

[

, Proposition 7.3.7]
.
Thus
it suffices to verify that

\mathscr{K}_{m,l}(X)

is
closed in

(\mathscr{K}(X),\mathcal{HD}_{d})

for all

m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}

and

.
Take a sequence

\{K_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}

in

\mathscr{K}_{m,l}(X)

and assume that

a set

.
Since the map

defined by

is continuous
(in fact,
it is
2
-Lipschitz),
we obtain

\yodiam L\leq l
.
We next

\card(L)\leq m
.
For the sake of contradiction,
suppose that

m<\card(L)
.
Then there exists a subset

E

of

with

\card(E)=m+1
.
Put

,
and
take a sufficiently large

so that

\mathcal{HD}_{d}(L,K_{n})<r/3
.
In this case,

implies that
for every

,
there exists

such that

.
By the definition of

,
we have

y_{x}\neq y_{x^{\prime}}

for all
distinct

.
Thus the set

F=\{\,y_{x}\in K_{n}\mid x\in E\,\}

has

.
This is impossible due to

\card(K_{n})\leq m
.
Therefore

\card(L)\leq m
.
Subsequently,
we obtain

.
This finishes the proof.

Let

(X,d)

be an ultrametric space,
and

r\in(0,\infty)
.
For

E\in\mathscr{K}(X)

we denote by

\langle E\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(r)}

the set

\{\,\mathrm{B}(a,r)\mid a\in E\,\}
.
For
convenience,
we also define

\langle E\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(0)}=\{\,\{a\}\mid a\in E\,\}
,
which is the case of

r=0
.
By

(3)
in
Lemma

2.1

and the compactness of

,
the set

\langle E\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(r)}

is finite for all

.
The next
proposition
is an analogue of

[
19
, Theorem 5.1]

and

[
13
, Corollary 2.30]

for the Hausdorff distance.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let

(X,d)

be an ultrametric space.
Then for
every
pair of subsets

E
and

F
of

X
,
the value

\mathcal{HD}_{d}(E,F)

is equal to the minimum

r\in R

such that

\langle E\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(r)}=\langle F\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(r)}
.

Proof.

Let

W

denote the set of all

r\in R

such that

\langle E\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(r)}=\langle F\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(r)}
.
Put

u=\inf W
.
First we shall prove that

u

is equal to
the
Hausdorff distance between

E

and

F
.
For every

r\in W
,
the equality

\langle E\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(r)}=\langle F\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(r)}

is true.
This result means that

\{\,\mathrm{B}(a,r)\mid a\in E\,\}=\{\,\mathrm{B}(b,r)\mid b\in F\,\}
.
This equality
yields

E\subseteq\bigcup_{b\in F}\mathrm{B}(b,r)

and

F\subseteq\bigcup_{a\in E}\mathrm{B}(a,r)
.
Thus
Lemma

4.1

implies that

\mathcal{HD}_{d}(A,B)\leq u
.
To obtain the opposite inequality,
take an arbitrary number

l

with

\mathcal{HD}_{d}(A,B)<l
.
Then

E\subseteq\bigcup_{b\in F}\mathrm{B}(b,l)

and

F\subseteq\bigcup_{a\in E}\mathrm{B}(a,l)
.
To show

\langle E\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(l)}\subseteq\langle F\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(l)}
,
take

\mathrm{B}(s,l)\in\langle E\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(l)}
.
From

E\subseteq\bigcup_{b\in F}\mathrm{B}(b,l)
,
it follows that
there exists
b\in F
such that

s\in\mathrm{B}(b,l)
.
Thus
the statement

(3)

in Lemma

2.1

yields

\mathrm{B}(s,l)=\mathrm{B}(b,l)
.
Namely,

\mathrm{B}(s,l)\in\langle F\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(l)}
,
and hence

\langle E\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(l)}\subseteq\langle F\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(l)}
.
Replacing the role of

\langle E\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(l)}

with that of

\langle F\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(l)}
,
we also obtain

\langle F\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(l)}\subseteq\langle E\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(l)}
.
As a result, we have

\langle E\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(l)}=\langle F\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(l)}
.
Therefore

u\leq\mathcal{HD}_{d}(E,F)
,
and we then obtain

u=\mathcal{HD}_{d}(E,F)
.

Next we will show that

u=\min W
.
If

u=0
,
then

u\in R

and

E=F
.
Thus

\langle E\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(0)}=\langle F\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(0)}
.
Namely,

u=\min W
.
In what follows,
we may assume that

u>0
.

Now we show that

u\in R
.
Put

S=d(E^{2})\cup d(F^{2})
.
Then

S

is tenuous
(see

[
13
, Corollary 2.28]
)
and

S\subseteq R
.
To obtain a contradiction,
suppose that

u\not\in S
.
Then,
since

S
is tenuous,
we can take

s,t\in S\setminus\{0\}

such that

u\in(s,t)

and

(s,t)\cap S=\emptyset
.
In this setting,
we obtain the following statement:

(B)

For every

x\in E\cup F

and for every

r\in(s,t)
,
we have

\mathrm{B}(x,r)=\mathrm{B}(x,s)
.

Since

u=\inf W
,
there exists

v\in W

such that

v\in[u,t)
.
Due to

v\in W
,
we have

\langle E\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(v)}=\langle F\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(v)}
.
This equality and the statement

(B)

show that

\langle E\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(s)}=\langle F\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(s)}
,
and hence

s\in W
,
which is a contradiction to

u=\inf W

and

s<u
.
Therefore

u\in S
,
and hence

u\in R
.

To verify that

u

attains the minimum,
we will show

u\in W
.
Take a sequence

\{r_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}}

in

W

such that

u\leq r_{i}

for all

i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
and

\lim_{i\to\infty}r_{i}=u
.
If

r_{i}=u

for some

i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
we have

u\in W
.
We may assume that

r_{i}\neq u

for any

i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.
Since

S

is tenuous and

u\in S\setminus\{0\}
,
we can take

q\in S

such that

(u,q)\cap S=\emptyset
.
For a sufficiently large

i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
the value

r_{i}

belongs to

(u,q)
.
Using the same argument as the proof
of

u\in R
,
similarly to the statement

(B)
,
we notice that every

x\in E\cup F

satisfies

\mathrm{B}(x,r_{i})=\mathrm{B}(x,u)
.
Consequently,
we obtain

\langle E\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(u)}=\langle F\rangle_{\mathfrak{c}(u)}
,
which means that

u\in W
.
As a result, we conclude that

u

is minimal in

W
.
This completes the proof.
∎

For the uncountable case,

Lemma 4.7
.

Let

R

be an uncountable range set,
and

(X,d)

be the

R

,
and for every finite subset

of

\yodiam(M)\leq\yodiam(A)

Proof.

The lemma follows from
Lemma

2.2

and

stating that every proximal subset of an
(generalized) ultrametric space is a

-Lipschitz
retract of the whole space.
For the sake of self-containedness,
we briefly explain a construction of

based on the proof of

[
2
, Theorem 4.6]
.
For each

a\in A
,
we put

l_{a}=d(a,\Pi(X,S))

and
we denote by

E[a]

the set of all

such that

.
For each set

,
take a point

.
Notice that if

,
then

implies that

for any

.
Define

An important part of the construction of

is

so that

rather than the point

.
Since

The property

(2)

follows
from the definition of

Next we verify

Thus

.

Before proving the theorem,
we explain the plan of the proof.
To obtain a subspace

F

stated in Theorem
1.5
,
we shall construct a petal structure
consisting of subsets of

X
.
For this purpose,
we use the idea of a family tree,
or
a genealogical tree.
This strategy is inspired by the fact that
every ultrametric space can be seen as
the end space
of a tree.
We fix a point

\varpi\in X

and
we regard

\varpi

as an original ancestor,
or a
protobiont.
For every

S\in\mathbf{TEN}(R)
,
we also define

S
-heirs
of

\varpi

by sequences beginning from

\varpi
,
which also can be regarded as descendants of

\varpi

passing through

S
.
Considering the completion

\mathbb{E}(S)

of
the set

\mathbb{A}(S)

of all

S
-heirs
of

\varpi
,
we can observe that
the family

\{\mathbb{E}(S)\}_{S\in\mathbf{TEN}(R)}

becomes a petal structure contained in

X
.
Then
the space

F=\bigcup_{S\in\mathbf{TEN}(R)}\mathbb{E}(S)

is
as desired.

We begin with a concept that is
a consequence of Theorem

1.1
.

Definition 5.1
.

We say that a family

\{\Omega[a,r]\}_{a\in X,r\in R\setminus\{0\}}

of
subsets of

X

is
an

R
-seed of
X

if
the following conditions are satisfied
for every

a\in X
,
and
for every

r\in R\setminus\{0\}
:

(1)

we have

a\in\Omega[a,r]
;

(2)

the set

\Omega[a,r]

is

r
-equidistant;

(3)

we have

\card(\Omega[a,r])=\aleph_{0}
.

Notice that since

(X,d)

is

(R,\omega_{0})
-haloed
(see Theorem

1.1
),
there
exists an

R
-seed,
and
notice
that
each

\Omega[a,r]

is not necessarily maximal with respect to
inclusion

\subseteq
.

Definition 5.2
.

Fix an

R
-seed

\{\Omega[a,r]\}_{a\in X,r\in R\setminus\{0\}}

and
a point

\varpi\in X
.
For

S\in\mathbf{TEN}(R)
,
we say that a point

x\in X

is
an

S
-heir
of

\varpi

if
there exists sequences

\{v_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m}

in

X

and

\{r_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m-1}

in

S

such that:

(S1)

v_{0}=\varpi
;

(S2)

v_{m}=x
;

(S3)

v_{i+1}\in\Omega[v_{i},r_{i}]

for all

i\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
;

(S4)

v_{i}\neq v_{i+1}

for
all

i\in\{0,\dots,m-1\}
;

(S5)

if

2\leq m
,
then

r_{i+1}<r_{i}
for all

i\in\{0,\dots,m-2\}
.

In this case,
the pair of

\{v_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m}

and

\{r\}_{i=0}^{m-1}

is called
an

S
-inheritance
of

x

from

\varpi

with length

m
.
If

m=0
,
then we consider

\{r_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m-1}

as
the empty sequence.

Definition 5.3
.

For an

R
-seed

\{\Omega[a,r]\}_{a\in X,r\in R\setminus\{0\}}
,
and a point

\varpi\in X
,
we denote by

\mathbb{A}(S)

the set of all

S
-heirs
of

\varpi
.
We also denote by

\mathbb{E}(S)

the closure of

\mathbb{A}(S)

in

X
.

Notice that since

(X,d)

is complete,
the
set

\mathbb{E}(S)

is isometric to
the
completion of

\mathbb{A}(S)

for all

S\in\mathbf{TEN}(R)
.

Remark 5.1
.

The point

\varpi

is
the unique

S
-heir
from

\varpi

that has
an

S
-inheritance
with
length

0
for all

S\in\mathbf{TEN}(R)
.
Thus,
we have

\mathbb{A}(\{0\})=\{\varpi\}

and

\mathbb{E}(\{0\})=\{\varpi\}

In the next proposition,
we observe that
inheritances of two points
determine a distance between these two points.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let

S,T\in\mathbf{TEN}(R)
,
and

x

and

y

be

S
-heir
and

T
-heir
of

\varpi
,
respectively.
Let

(\{v_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m},\{r_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m-1})

and

(\{w_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n},\{l_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n-1})

be

S
-inheritance
and

T
-inheritance
of

x

and

y
,
respectively.
In this situation,
the next three statements are true:

(1)

If

k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

satisfies
that

k\leq\min\{m,n\}

and

v_{i}=w_{i}

for all

i\leq k
,
then

r_{i}=l_{i}

for all

i<k
.

(2)

If

k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}

satisfies that:

(a)

v_{i}=w_{i}

for all

i\leq k
;

(b)

v_{k+1}\neq w_{k+1}
,

then
we have

\max\{r_{k},l_{k}\}=d(x,y)
.

(3)

If

m<n

and

v_{i}=w_{i}

for all

i\leq m
,
then we have

d(x,y)=l_{m}
.

Proof.

Since

r_{i}=d(v_{i},v_{i+1})

and

l_{i}=d(w_{i},w_{i+1})

(see the condition

(S3)

in Definition

5.2
),
under the assumption of

(1)

we see that

r_{i}=l_{i}

for all

i<k
.
This finishes the proof of

(1)
.

We next prove
(2)
.
Put

z=v_{k}
.
Then

z=w_{k}

by the assumption.
By the definition of heirs,
the strong triangle inequality,
and by the conditions
(S2)

and

(S5)

in
Definition

5.2
,
we have

\displaystyle d(v_{k+1},x)

\displaystyle\leq d(v_{k},v_{k+1})\lor\dots\lor d(v_{m-1},v_{m})

\displaystyle=\max\{\,r_{i}\mid i\in\{k+1,\dots,m-1\}\,\}<r_{k},

and hence

d(v_{k+1},x)<r_{k}
.
Similarly,
we also have

d(w_{k+1},y)<l_{k}
.
Since

d(v_{k},v_{k+1})=r_{k}

and

d(w_{k},w_{k+1})=l_{k}
,
the statement

(1)

in
Lemma

2.1

yields

d(z,x)=d(v_{k},x)=r_{k}

and

d(z,y)=d(w_{k},y)=l_{k}
.
If

r_{k}\neq l_{k}
,
then
we obtain

d(x,y)=\max\{r_{k},l_{k}\}

using the statement

(1)

in
Lemma

2.1

again.
If

r_{k}=l_{k}
,
then

v_{k+1},w_{k+1}\in\Omega[z,r_{k}]

(see

(S3)
in
Definition

5.2
).
Thus,
due to

v_{k+1}\neq w_{k+1}
,
we have

d(v_{k+1},w_{k+1})=r_{k}(=l_{k})
.
Using the statement

(1)

in
Lemma

2.1

once again,
and by

d(v_{k+1},x)<r_{k}

and

d(w_{k+1},y)<r_{k}
,
we obtain

d(x,y)=r_{k}(=l_{k})
.
This proves the statement

(2)
.

Under the assumption of

(3)
,
we obtain

d(w_{m},w_{m+1})=l_{m}

and

d(w_{m+1},y)<l_{m}
.
The condition

(S2)

states that

w_{m}=x
.
Thus,
the statement

(1)

in
Lemma

2.1

yields

d(x,y)=d(w_{m},y)=l_{m}
.
The proof is finished.
∎

Lemma 5.3
.

For every

Proof.

Since

From

-valued.
We now

(\mathbb{E}(S),d)

is

(S,\omega_{0})
-haloed.
It suffices to show that for
every

a\in\mathbb{A}(S)

and for
every

r\in S\setminus\{0\}
,

r
-equidistant

\mathrm{B}(a,r)

Let

(\{v_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m},\{r_{i}\}_{i=0}^{m-1})

-inheritance

We divide the proof into three cases.

Case 1. [

In this case,

\Omega[\varpi,r]

.
Indeed, for every

,

-inheritance
of

from

By the statement

(2)
in

5.1
,

\Omega[\varpi,r]

Case 2. [There exists

To prove

for each

-inheritance
of

from

\varpi
,
and hence

\Omega[v_{k},r]\subseteq\mathbb{E}(S)
.
By the statement

(2)

in
Proposition

5.1
,

Thus

\Omega[v_{k},r]

an
infinite

-equidistant

Case 3. [

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

It suffices to show the latter part
of Theorem

1.5
.
Choose a point

\varpi

such that

\varpi\in K
and
choose an

R
-seed

,
