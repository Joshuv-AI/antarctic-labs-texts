I
Introduction

Dimensionful constants appearing in a theory enable one to define dimensionful attributes of a system that are of special significance in the dynamics of that system. In quantum mechanics, Planck’s
\hbar
defines the de Broglie wavelength of a particle, given its momentum, and in relativistic quantum mechanics,
\hbar
(with
c
) defines the Compton wavelength of a particle given its mass. In relativistic gravity,
G
and
c
define the Schwarzschild radius of a body, given its mass. All these quantities are of obvious and proven usefulness.

MOND
Milgrom 1983
– which strives to account for the mass discrepancies in The Universe without dark matter – introduces a new constant,
a_{0}
, with the dimensions of acceleration (for reviews of MOND see Refs.
Famaey and McGaugh 2012
;
Milgrom 2014
;
Milgrom 2020
). Two system-specific attributes of proven usefulness that MOND defines with the use of
a_{0}
(and
G
) are the “MOND radius” and the “MOND speed” of a given mass.

a_{0}
, together with
c
and
G
, also define universal, not-system-specific, quantities, the MOND length,
\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\equiv c^{2}/a_{0}
, the MOND time
t_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\equiv c/a_{0}
, and the MOND mass,
M_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\equiv c^{4}/Ga_{0}
. Their values are of cosmological magnitudes, which perhaps points to the fundamental basis of MOND (e.g., Ref,
Milgrom 2020a
).
They are analogous to the universal Planck length, time, and mass, which
\hbar
defines, together with
c
and
G
.

Here, I point out that MOND also defines an important fiducial value of the specific angular momentum (hereafter, SAM), and I elaborate on the ways in which it enters the dynamics of galaxies and their evolution.

Correlations are observed between global properties of the baryonic body of disc galaxies – such as their total mass, disc mass, rotational speed, etc. – and their angular momentum (hereafter, AM) (see, e.g., the recent Refs.
Obreschkow Glazebrook 2014
;
Jadhav and Banerjee 2019
;
Posti et al. 2018
;
Fall and Romanowski 2018
;
Piña et al. 2021
for but a few examples). These have given rise to much discussion as to their significance and possible reasons for their emergence in the dark-matter paradigm (e.g., Ref.
Dalcanton et al. 1997
and Refs.
Obreschkow Glazebrook 2014
;
Posti et al. 2018
;
Fall and Romanowski 2018
;
Jadhav and Banerjee 2019
;
Piña et al. 2021
). Reference
Lelli et al. 2019
discusses
AM-mass relations vis a vis the baryonic Tully-Fisher relation.

Correlations involving the AM require, in the dark-matter paradigm, separate explanations, because they hinge on mutual effects of the baryonic and the putative dark-matter halo; e.g., the exchange of AM between the components.
The correlations I specifically discuss here can perhaps be worked by hand into the results of dark-matter simulations, as is usually done in the context of this paradigm. In MOND, however, such correlations follow naturally and without recourse to complicated galaxy formation and evolution scenarios, as MOND is much more constrained than the dark-matter paradigm.

Inasmuch as MOND predicts correctly, as it does, the rotation curves of individual disc galaxies from their baryonic mass distribution, it, perforce, also predicts correctly their AM from the mass distribution. However, this does not directly imply correlations between global properties and the AM. It is thus interesting to inquire how such correlations arise in MOND.

In Sec.
II
, I define the MOND fiducial SAM. In Sec.
II.1
, I show how it underlies galaxy correlations involving their AM, and, in Sec.
II.2
, how it is an important predictor of the type of galaxy a protogalaxy will settle to, explaining why some galaxies end up as low-surface-density, pure discs, and some as high-surface-density, disc-plus-bulge ones. In Sec.
III
, I compare with some relevant observations. Section
IV
is a discussion of further points.

II
The MOND specific angular momentum of a galaxy

For a body of (“baryonic”) mass
\mathcal{M}
(a “galaxy” hereafter), the MOND constant,
a_{0}
, with
G
, defines
the “MOND radius”

r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\equiv\left(\frac{\mathcal{M}G}{a_{0}}\right)^{1/2}=\left(\frac{r_{\scriptscriptstyle s}\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle M}}{2}\right)^{1/2},

(1)

where
r_{\scriptscriptstyle s}
is the Schwarzschild radius of
\mathcal{M}
, and
\ell_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
the MOND length, which is comparable with the Hubble distance.
It also defines the “MOND velocity”

V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}\equiv(\mathcal{M}Ga_{0})^{1/4}.

(2)

MOND also defines a universal (i.e., mass independent) fiducial surface density

\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\equiv\frac{a_{0}}{2\pi G}.

(3)

Their special roles in galaxy dynamics have been widely discussed (see below, and the MOND reviews
Famaey and McGaugh 2012
;
Milgrom 2014
;
Milgrom 2020
).

I note here that
a_{0}
(with
G
) also defines an important fiducial value of the SAM for a body of mass
\mathcal{M}
:

j_{\scriptscriptstyle M}(\mathcal{M})\equiv r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}=\mathcal{M}^{3/4}(G^{3}/a_{0})^{1/4},

(4)

and in terms of
V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}

j_{\scriptscriptstyle M}(\mathcal{M})=V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}^{3}/a_{0}.

(5)

For the canonical value,
a_{0}=1.2~10^{-8}{\rm cm~s^{-2}}

Famaey and McGaugh 2012
;
Milgrom 2014
;
Milgrom 2020

j_{\scriptscriptstyle M}(\mathcal{M})=383(\mathcal{M}/10^{10}M_{\odot})^{3/4}~{\rm kpc}~{\rm km~s^{-1}}

II.1
Role of
j_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
in scaling relations

To better understand the role of
j_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
we need to recapitulate the special roles of
r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
and
V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}
.
The MOND radius,
r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
, plays an important role in defining the transition radius from the Newtonian to the deep-MOND regime. Bodies whose effective size is much larger than their
r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
are governed by deep-MOND, scale-invariant dynamics, while bodies contained well within their
r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
are, by and large, governed by standard dynamics (Newtonian or general relativistic).

While there are objects (such as stars) that are much smaller than their MOND radius,
galaxies of all types are not found to be appreciably smaller than their MOND radius. This is the expression in MOND of early observations dubbed the “Freeman law” (for disc galaxies) and “Fish law” (for elliptical galaxies), which state that the distribution of mean surface densities,
\langle\Sigma\rangle
, of galaxies is cut off above a certain value,
\langle\Sigma\rangle_{0}
(see, e.g. Ref.
McGaugh et al. 1995
, in particular, their Fig. 3).

The original statements where that
\langle\Sigma\rangle
was narrowly distributed around some
\langle\Sigma\rangle_{0}
. We now understand that this was due to selection against galaxies with
\langle\Sigma\rangle<\langle\Sigma\rangle_{0}
, of which there are, indeed, many.
With the advent of MOND it was pointed out that this value
\langle\Sigma\rangle_{0}
corresponds to a mean acceleration (
G\langle\Sigma\rangle_{0}
) of
\approx a_{0}
. Moreover, there are explanations for the existence of such a cutoff in the framework of MOND (e.g., Refs.
Milgrom 1983b

Milgrom 1989

V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}
is of special significance in that MOND predicts that the rotational speed on circular orbits around a mass
\mathcal{M}
should become independent of orbital radius at asymptotically large radii, leveling at the value
V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\mathcal{M})
.
But,
V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}
features not only on the asymptote, it also sets the velocity scale for the whole galaxy. For example, for self-gravitating bodies that are deep in the MOND regime (i.e., of mean accelerations
\ll a_{0}
) MOND predicts that the mass-weighted root-mean-square velocity is
\langle V^{2}\rangle^{1/2}\approx 0.8V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}
(see, e.g., Refs.

At the other end, for high-surface-density galaxies, barring some extreme examples, the rotational speed remains roughly constant at
V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}
down to small radii. This is understood in MOND as tantamount to the existence of the Freeman cutoff:
Without further knowledge, and based only on AM argumentation, a galaxy can, in principle, be envisaged that has
\langle V\rangle_{j}\gg V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\mathcal{M})
. But this would mean that the bulk of the galaxy is very deep in the Newtonian regime. This however, is largely excluded in MOND by the arguments leading to the Freeman and Fish limits, as indeed is observed in real galaxies. Galaxies with even the maximum rotation speed exceeding
1.5V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}
are very rare.

More quantitatively,
the relevant mean of the rotational speeds in the disc of surface density
\Sigma(r)
is

\langle V\rangle_{j}\equiv\frac{\int\Sigma(r)r^{2}V(r)dr}{\int\Sigma(r)r^{2}dr},

(7)

where
V(r)
is weighted in the way it enters the SAM of the disc:

j_{\scriptscriptstyle D}=\frac{\int\Sigma(r)r^{2}V(r)dr}{\int\Sigma(r)r~dr}=\langle r\rangle\langle V\rangle_{j},

(8)

where the mean disc radius is

\langle r\rangle\equiv\frac{\int\Sigma(r)r^{2}dr}{\int\Sigma(r)r~dr}.

(9)

The arguments above state that for discs of all types we have in MOND
\langle V\rangle_{j}\approx V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}
.
Even more specifically, I calculated

\langle r\rangle=2h

\xi=r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}/h

\langle V\rangle_{j}/V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}=0.95
. For
\xi=1,~2,~4

\langle V\rangle_{j}/V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}=1.03,~1.12,~1.29
[with the MOND interpolating function

of the disc component
in disc galaxies.

j_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\approx\frac{\langle r\rangle}{r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}}j_{\scriptscriptstyle M}(\mathcal{M})=\left(\frac{\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}}{\langle\Sigma\rangle}\right)^{1/2}j_{\scriptscriptstyle M}(\mathcal{M}),

(10)

where
\mathcal{M}
is the total baryonic mass of the galaxy (including all components, such as a bulge),
\langle\Sigma\rangle=\mathcal{M}/2\pi\langle r\rangle^{2}
is some mean surface density of the galaxy, and
\Sigma_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
is the MOND surface density defined in Eq. (
3
).

I adopt for concreteness sake a simplified picture of a disc galaxy of total mass
\mathcal{M}
, with one rotationally supported disc component of mass
\mathcal{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
, and a nonrotating bulge of mass
\mathcal{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}=\mathcal{M}-\mathcal{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
, with
J
– the total AM of baryons, wholly in the disc. Thus,
j\equiv J/\mathcal{M}
, and
j_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\equiv J/\mathcal{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
.

Relation (
10
) establishes
j_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
as an underlying cornerstone defining correlations between the AM or SAM of galaxies and other global properties. It does so in ways that depend only little on details of the formation and evolution of these galaxies – in contradistinction with what happens in the dark-matter paradigm as follows:

High-surface-density galaxies, with few exceptions, have (as per the “Freeman law”)
\langle r\rangle\approx r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}(\mathcal{M})
. Relation (
10
) then tells us that their discs should lie on the fiducial relation
j_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\approx j_{\scriptscriptstyle M}(\mathcal{M})
. Thus, their global SAM should satisfy

j\approx j_{\scriptscriptstyle M}(\mathcal{M})(\mathcal{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}/\mathcal{M})
. In other words, to the extent that high-surface galaxy does not have a dominant, nonrotating bulge it should lie on the
j_{\scriptscriptstyle M}(\mathcal{M})
relation in the
j-\mathcal{M}
plane. If it has a substantial bulge it should lie below the relation.

As to low-surface-density galaxies, which are deep in the MOND regime; they are generically of subdominant bulge, and relation (
10
) tells us that they should lie above the
j_{\scriptscriptstyle M}(\mathcal{M})
relation; the more so the deeper they are in the MOND regime (i.e. the larger their
\langle r\rangle/r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
is).

II.2
Role of
j_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
in galaxy evolution

Based on the arguments in the previous sections, MOND can explain why some galaxies become low-surface-density virialized disc galaxy, mostly of negligible bulge, while others virialize into disc galaxies with mean surface densities near the Freeman limit, mostly possessing bulges.
3
3

3

By “bulge” here and in what follows I mean any component
of the galaxy – possibly also a halo – that takes up mass but little angular momentum, and
is used solely as a component to be reckoned with in the mass and AM balance. I am not referring to any specific type of bulge or to the possibly complicated way in which it forms, as long as it is a result of a secular evolution, not e.g. of mergers.

Envisage a protogalactic body
4
4

4

A “protogalaxy” here means the state of the would-be-disc-galaxy when it starts to evolve in isolation. It can be a primordial mass that will develop in isolation, or a pair or a group of galaxies that will eventually merge and evolve into one galaxy whose virialized state we want to determine.
of total mass
\mathcal{M}
and total AM
J
(with SAM
j=J/M
). Suppose that it decouples from its surrounding, and evolves into a virialized state in isolation, and without disintegrating, exchanging mass or AM with neither external entities, nor – as MOND posits – with a dark-matter halo.

We saw in Sec.
II.1
that MOND dictates that in the virialized state, the disc has
\langle V\rangle_{j}\approx V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\mathcal{M})
, where
\mathcal{M}
is the total mass of the galaxy.
Consider first the case with

\zeta\equiv j/j_{\scriptscriptstyle M}(\mathcal{M})\gg 1.

(11)

If the virialized galaxy possesses a (nonrotating) bulge of mass
\mathcal{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
, the disc, of mass
\mathcal{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}=\mathcal{M}-\mathcal{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}
is left with SAM

j_{\scriptscriptstyle D}=j\mathcal{M}/\mathcal{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}
, which has to equal
\langle r\rangle\langle V\rangle_{j}\approx\langle r\rangle V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\mathcal{M})
. To accommodate this the disc must adjust its mean radius to be

\frac{\langle r\rangle}{r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}(\mathcal{M})}\approx\zeta\frac{\mathcal{M}}{\mathcal{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}}\gg 1.

(12)

The minimal value of
\langle r\rangle
is
\zeta r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}(\mathcal{M})
, when no bulge is formed. This state of things describes a low-surface-density disc, as concretely defined in the context of MOND: a disc whose mean radius is much larger than the MOND radius of the galaxy, or equivalently, the mean acceleration in which,
\langle a\rangle=\mathcal{M}G/\langle r\rangle^{2}\ll a_{0}
.

Reference
Wittenburg et al. 2020
shows results of MOND simulations of collapsing gas clouds (finding that they form virialized disc galaxies with exponential discs). Based on only a handful of simulations they observed that: “…This suggests that low surface brightness galaxies result from collapsing gas clouds with high specific angular momentum.” This is in line with the above general result. It would be interesting to see if this result and the ones below are supported by more quantitative and systematic MOND simulations.

If, on the other hand, the protogalaxy starts with
\zeta\ll 1
, how can this be accommodated by the disc of the virialized galaxy, given that it has to have
\langle V\rangle_{j}\approx V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\mathcal{M})
?
In principle, considering only conservation of mass and AM, the galaxy can end up as a pure disc. But it then has to attain a small enough
\langle r\rangle
to make
j_{\scriptscriptstyle D}=\langle r\rangle\langle V\rangle_{j}\approx\langle r\rangle V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\mathcal{M})
. This would imply
\langle r\rangle/r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}\approx\zeta\ll 1
. So the disc would be deep in the Newtonian regime, and would greatly exceed the Freeman limit. MOND does not robustly predict that the Freeman limit must be obeyed, but it points to it as the more probable outcome.

If there are processes that limit
\langle\Sigma\rangle
roughly to the Freeman value, the only route the galaxy can take is to put enough of its mass into a nonrotating bulge and attain the minimal
\langle r\rangle
compatible with the Freeman limit
\langle r\rangle\approx r_{\scriptscriptstyle M}(\mathcal{M})
. The disc then sits on the fiducial MOND SAM

j_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\approx j_{\scriptscriptstyle M}(\mathcal{M})
, which, in turn, dictates that its mass is
\mathcal{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle D}\approx\zeta\mathcal{M}
, with the bulge mass
\mathcal{M}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\approx(1-\zeta)\mathcal{M}
.
Such an enforced formation of a bulge is also in line with the arguments, and compatible with the expected dynamics, leading to a Freeman limit in MOND: When the inner surface density of the disc exceeds the Freeman limit dynamics become Newtonian, hence the inner disc becomes unstable, not being protected by the stabilizing MOND effects
Milgrom 1983b
;
Milgrom 1989
;
Brada and Milgrom 1999
, hence is amenable to churning up a bulge, leaving the galaxy at the Freeman limit.

IV
Discussion

I have explained how the fiducial MOND SAM,
j_{\scriptscriptstyle M}
plays a central role in defining the properties of virialized disc galaxies, and how
it defines the borderline in the mass-AM plane of protogalaxies between those that will become bulgeless low-surface-density discs, and those that will possess a bulge and have mean surface densities near the Freeman limit.

The former of the two roles does not depend much on formation scenarios, as it concerns, and is based on, MOND dynamics of settled galaxies that follow from only the basic tenets of MOND. Its application requires, though, considerations of several caveats. For example, we used simplistic picture of a disc galaxy as made of a single component disc, and a nonrotating bulge. Galactic discs generally have a stellar and a gas component with different masses, and different surface densities, hence different SAM. (They both have, however, roughly the same
\langle V\rangle_{j}\approx V_{\scriptscriptstyle\infty}(\mathcal{M})
.)
This is not a concern at the high-mass end, where the gas disc is by far subdominant generically. Generically the gas fraction increases with decreasing surface density (and mass) (see, e.g., Fig. 4 of Ref.
Lelli et al. 2016
, which, however use the surface brightness, not the total surface densities – including gas – which are relevant here) and at the low end we may thus be dealing again with a single dominant gas component, which generically has a different
\Sigma(r)
than the stars (as in the galaxies studied in Ref.
Sanders 2021
). One should then be careful not to use
\langle r\rangle
and
\langle\Sigma\rangle
values based on the stellar distribution alone.
