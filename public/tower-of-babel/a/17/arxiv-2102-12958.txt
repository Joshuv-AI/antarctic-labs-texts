Analyticity of resolvents of elliptic operators on quantum graphs with small edges
D.I. Borisov

Abstract
We consider an arbitrary metric graph, to which we glue another graph with edges of lengths proportional to ε \varepsilon , where ε \varepsilon is a small positive parameter. On such graph, we consider a general self-adjoint second order differential operator ℋ ε \mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon} with varying coefficients subject to general vertex conditions; all coefficients in differential expression and vertex conditions are supposed to be analytic in ε \varepsilon . We introduce a special operator on a certain graph obtained by rescaling the aforementioned small edges and assume that it has no embedded eigenvalues at the threshold of its essential spectrum. Under such assumption, we show that certain parts of the resolvent of ℋ ε \mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon} are analytic in ε \varepsilon . This allows us to represent the resolvent of ℋ ε \mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon} by an uniformly converging Taylor-like series and its partial sums can be used for approximating the resolvent up to an arbitrary power of ε \varepsilon . In particular, the zero-order approximation reproduces recent convergence results by G. Berkolaiko, Yu. Latushkin, S. Sukhtaiev and by C. Cacciapuoti, but we additionally show that next-to-leading terms in ε \varepsilon -expansions of the coefficients in the differential expression and vertex conditions can contribute to the limiting operator producing the Robin part at the vertices, to which small edges are incident. We also discuss possible generalizations of our model including both the cases of a more general geometry of the small parts of the graph and a non-analytic ε \varepsilon -dependence of the coefficients in the differential expression and vertex conditions. Keywords: graph, small edge, resolvent, analyticity, Taylor series, approximation

Introduction

The theory of quantum graphs or, in other words, the spectral theory of differential operators on graphs is actively developing nowadays and a rather new direction is devoted to studying quantum graphs with small edges. One of early studied problems came from the homogenization theory and it was devoted to models of woven membrane kind, see
[
9
]
,
[
13
]
. The well-known result states that an elliptic operator on a graph looking as a net with small edges converge to a two-dimensional operator on an Euclidean domain approximated in a natural sense by such graph.

Another situation, which attracted quite a lot of attention, concerned the graphs with finitely many small edges. As a rather early publication on this subject we mention paper
[
8
]
, in which it was shown that a general vertex condition in a graph can be approximated in the norm resolvent sense via ornamenting by small edges supporting a magnetic field and with delta-coupling at the vertices.

The case of a general graph with finitely small edges was addressed in
[
1
]
. On such graph, a Schrödinger operator subject to general vertex conditions was considered. The main result stated that under a certain non-resonance condition, see Condition 3.2 in
[
1
]
, the considered operator converged in the norm resolvent sense to a Schrödinger operator on the limiting graph without small edges, which were replaced by certain conditions at the limiting vertices. The vertex conditions in
[
1
]
were described in terms of sympletic geometry and Lagrangian planes; the limiting vertex conditions were also formultated in the same terms. A similar study was made in
[
7
]
, where there was considered a star graph, in which the vertex was replaced by a small rescaled finite graph. The operator on this perturbed graph was designed so that after the rescaling the small graph back to a finite size, the differential expression and vertex conditions turned out to be independent of a small parameter characterizing the size of the small graph. The main result of
[
7
]
provided the leading terms in the asymptotics for the perturbed resolvent and the estimates for the error term. The limiting condition at the vertex of the limiting star graph contained only the Neumann and Dirichlet parts.

The above described results motivated further studies of the graphs with small edges
aimed on better understanding and describing the behavior of the resolvents. A natural question, apart of determining the limiting operator, is to find several next terms in the asymptotics for the resolvent or even its complete asymptotic expansion. This question was addressed in few recent papers
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
for some toy models represented by very simple graphs. A main feature of the considered models was the possibility of singular dependence of the coefficients in the differential expression on a small parameter governing the lengths of small edges. The main results obtained for these models stated that the resolvents were analytic
in certain sense with respect to the small parameter and in the limit, the condition at the vertex to which the small edges shrank, could involve a Robin part. This Robin part was generated by the next-to-leading terms in the expansions of the coefficients of the differential expression with respect to the small parameter. For the resolvents, several first terms in their asymptotic expansions were found. The analytic
dependence of the resolvent on the small parameter was in some sense surprising since small edges is an example of a singular perturbation and usually, while dealing with singular perturbation, one can hope only to find an asymptotic expansion for the resolvent but not to prove that this expansion is a converging Taylor series.

Problem and main results

2.1
Problem

Let
\Gamma
be a finite metric graph having no isolated vertices. The lengths of its edges can be both finite or infinite. We fix arbitrarily a vertex in the graph
\Gamma
and denote it by
M_{0}
. The degree of this vertex is finite, that is, there exist finitely many edges
\mathrm{e}_{i}
,

, while each edge

.

We chose arbitrary vertices

,

, in the graph

and then partition the aforementioned edges

,

, in the graph

non-empty groups

,
j=1,\ldots,n
, where

are some non-empty disjoint sets of indices and
\bigcup\limits_{j=1}^{n}J_{j}=\{1,\ldots,d_{0}\}
,
1\leqslant n\leqslant d_{0}
. Then
we replace the vertex

in the graph

by its
n
copies, one copy for each group
\{\mathrm{e}_{i}\}_{i\in J_{j}}
. Finally, we introduce a graph, which will be the main object of our study, as the union of the graphs
\Gamma
and
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
, where the vertices
M_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
, are identified with the aforementioned copies of the vertex

in the graph

The main object of our study is an unbounded operator
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
in

with a differential expression
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(\varepsilon)
, the action of which is defined as

\hat{\mathcal{H}}(\varepsilon)u:=-\frac{d\ }{dx}p_{\varepsilon}\frac{du}{dx}+\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{d\ }{dx}(q_{\varepsilon}u)+q_{\varepsilon}\frac{du}{dx}\right)+V_{\varepsilon}u,

(2.1)

where
\mathrm{i}
is the imaginary unit and the coefficients are

\begin{gathered}p_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{\begin{aligned} &p_{\Gamma}(\,\cdot\,,\varepsilon)&&\text{on}\quad\Gamma,\\
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}&p_{\gamma}(\,\cdot\,,\varepsilon)&&\text{on}\quad\gamma_{\varepsilon},\end{aligned}\right.\qquad q_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{\begin{aligned} &q_{\Gamma}(\,\cdot\,,\varepsilon)&&\text{on}\quad\Gamma,\\
\varepsilon^{-1}&\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}q_{\gamma}(\,\cdot\,,\varepsilon)\quad&&\text{on}\quad\gamma_{\varepsilon},\end{aligned}\right.\\
V_{\varepsilon}:=\left\{\begin{aligned} &V_{\Gamma}(\,\cdot\,,\varepsilon)&&\text{on}\quad\Gamma,\\
\varepsilon^{-2}&\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}V_{\gamma}(\,\cdot\,,\varepsilon)&&\text{on}\quad\gamma_{\varepsilon}.\end{aligned}\right.\end{gathered}

(2.2)

Here
p_{\Gamma}=p_{\Gamma}(\,\cdot\,,\varepsilon)\in W_{\infty}^{1}(\Gamma)
,
q_{\Gamma}=q_{\Gamma}(\,\cdot\,,\varepsilon)\in W_{\infty}^{1}(\Gamma)
,
V_{\Gamma}=V_{\Gamma}(\,\cdot\,,\varepsilon)\in L_{2}(\Gamma)
and
p_{\gamma}=p_{\gamma}(\,\cdot\,,\varepsilon)\in W_{\infty}^{1}(\gamma)
,
q_{\gamma}=q_{\gamma}(\,\cdot\,,\varepsilon)\in W_{\infty}^{1}(\gamma)
,

are some real functions defined on the graphs
\Gamma
and

V_{\gamma}
and these functions are analytic
in
\varepsilon
in the sense of the norms in the mentioned spaces.
The symbol
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}
stands for a linear operator mapping

onto

by the rule

(\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}u)(x):=u\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}\right)\quad\text{as}\quad x\in\mathrm{e}_{\varepsilon}

on each edge
\mathrm{e}_{\varepsilon}
in the graph
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
.
We also assume an uniform ellipticity condition for the expression

, namely, the existence of a fixed constant

,

p_{\Gamma}(x,0)\geqslant c_{\mathcal{H}}\quad\text{a.e. on}\quad\Gamma,\qquad p_{\gamma}(\xi,0)\geqslant c_{\mathcal{H}}\quad\text{a.e. on}\quad\gamma

Thanks to the assumed analyticity

\mathcal{U}_{M}(u):=\begin{pmatrix}u_{1}(M)\\
\vdots\\

\frac{du_{d(M)}}{dx_{d(M)}}(M)\end{pmatrix},

where

The matrices

The domain of the operator

consists of the functions in

,

. The action of the operator

).

We want the introduced operator

\mathrm{A}_{M}(\varepsilon)\Pi_{M}^{-1}(\varepsilon)\mathrm{B}_{M}^{*}(\varepsilon)+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{B}_{M}(\varepsilon)\Pi_{M}^{-1}(\varepsilon)\Theta_{M}(\varepsilon)\Pi_{M}^{-1}(\varepsilon)\mathrm{B}_{M}^{*}(\varepsilon)

(2.5)

is self-adjoint, where

\displaystyle\Pi_{M}(\varepsilon):=\diag\big\{\nu_{i}(M)p_{\varepsilon}\big|_{\mathrm{e}_{i}(M)}(M)\big\}_{i=1,\ldots,d(M)},

(2.6)

\displaystyle\Theta_{M}(\varepsilon):=\diag\big\{\nu_{i}(M)q_{\varepsilon}\big|_{\mathrm{e}_{i}(M)}(M)\big\}_{i=1,\ldots,d(M)},

and
\mathrm{e}_{i}(M)
are the edges incident to the vertex
M
, while

if the inward direction on the edge
\mathrm{e}_{i}(M)

The second assumption is the rank condition

\rank\big(\mathrm{A}_{M}(0)\ \;\mathrm{B}_{M}(0)\big)=d(M)
imposed at each vertex

. In view of the above conditions for the structure of matrices
\mathrm{A}_{M}
and
\mathrm{B}_{M}
, this condition
is obviously equivalent to

\rank\big(\mathrm{A}_{M}(\varepsilon)\ \;\mathrm{B}_{M}(\varepsilon)\big)=d(M)

(2.7)

for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon

2.2
Notation and assumption

To formulate our main results, we need some additional notations. By
\gamma_{\infty}
we denote a graph obtained by attaching leads (edges of infinite lengths)
\mathrm{e}_{i}^{\infty}
,
i\in J_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
, to each vertex
M_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
, in the graph
\gamma
; the vertex
M_{j}
serves as the origin for the attached edges
\mathrm{e}_{i}
. The variable on the graph
\gamma_{\infty}
is denoted by
\xi
.

The introduced graph appears in a very natural way. Namely, we consider the subgraph
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
in
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
with the edges
\mathrm{e}_{i}
incident to the vertices
M_{j}
. Then we rescale this subgraph and the edges by means of
\varepsilon
so that the graph
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
turns into the graph
\gamma
. Under such rescaling, the edges
\mathrm{e}_{i}
become ones with lengths of order
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
and in the limit
\varepsilon\to+0
, we just replace them by the leads
\mathrm{e}_{i}^{\infty}
.

The described scaling corresponds to the change of the variables
\xi=x\varepsilon^{-1}
and we need to see how the part of the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
corresponding to
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
is transformed under such change. Namely, we first
replace the coefficients of the differential expression
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\varepsilon}
on the edges
\mathrm{e}_{i}
by their values at
M_{0}
. Then we multiply the obtained differential expression by
\varepsilon^{2}
, pass to the variable
\xi
and
\varepsilon=0
. This gives rise to the differential expression

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\infty}u:=-\frac{d\ }{d\xi}p_{\gamma}(\,\cdot\,,0)\frac{du}{d\xi}+\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{d\ }{d\xi}(q_{\gamma}(\,\cdot\,,0)u)+q_{\gamma}(\,\cdot\,,0)\frac{du}{d\xi}\right)+V_{\gamma}(\,\cdot\,,0)u\quad\text{on}\quad\gamma,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\infty}u:=-\mathsf{p}_{i}(0)\frac{d^{2}u}{d\xi^{2}}\quad\text{on}\quad\mathrm{e}_{i}^{\infty},\quad i\in J_{j},\quad j=1,\ldots,n,\qquad\mathsf{p}_{i}(\varepsilon):=p_{\Gamma}\big|_{\mathrm{e}_{i}}(M_{0},\varepsilon).

Then we do a similar rescaling in the vertex conditions, but we multiply them by

\tilde{\mathrm{E}}_{M}(\varepsilon):=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{I}_{r(M)}&0\\
0&\varepsilon^{-1}\mathrm{I}_{d(M)-r(M)}\end{pmatrix}.

We see that

\displaystyle\varepsilon\tilde{\mathrm{E}}_{M}(\varepsilon)\mathrm{A}_{M}(\varepsilon)=\mathrm{A}_{M}^{(0)}+O(\varepsilon),\qquad\tilde{\mathrm{E}}_{M}(\varepsilon)\mathrm{B}_{M}(\varepsilon)=\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(0)}+O(\varepsilon),\quad

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathrm{A}_{M}^{(0)}:=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\mathrm{A}_{M}^{-}(0)\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(0)}:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{B}_{M}^{+}(0)\\
\frac{d\mathrm{B}_{M}^{-}}{d\varepsilon}(0)\end{pmatrix}

(2.10)

and
\mathrm{A}_{M}^{-}(\cdot)
and
\mathrm{B}_{M}^{-}(\cdot)
are the matrices formed by last
d(M)-r(M)
rows of respectively the matrices
\mathrm{A}_{M}(\cdot)
and
\mathrm{B}_{M}(\cdot)
, while a matrix
\mathrm{B}_{M}^{+}(\cdot)
is formed by first
r(M)
rows of the matrix
\mathrm{B}_{M}
.
Passing then to the limit as
\varepsilon\to+0
, we end up with the following vertex conditions:

\mathrm{A}_{M}^{(0)}\mathcal{U}_{M}(u)+\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(0)}\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\prime}(u)=0\quad\text{at each}\quad M\in\gamma_{\infty},

(2.11)

where the vectors
\mathcal{U}_{M}(u)
and
\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\prime}(u)
are introduced as in (
2.3
) with the derivatives
\frac{du_{i}}{dx_{i}}
substituted by
\frac{du_{i}}{d\xi_{i}}

The described procedure gives rise to an auxiliary operator
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
on the graph
\gamma_{\infty}
with differential expression (
2.8
) subject to the vertex conditions (
2.11

The domain of the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
consists of the functions in
\dot{W}_{2}^{2}(\gamma)

Since the graph

are the leads

The operator

Again in view of the definition of the differential expression

\mathcal{U}_{\gamma}(u):=\begin{pmatrix}u\big|_{\mathrm{e}_{i}^{\infty}}(M_{j})\end{pmatrix}_{i\in J_{j},\ j=1,\ldots,n}

In view of the said above, Condition

2.13
). It is clear that
k\leqslant d_{0}
since once
k>d_{0}
it is possible to find a linear combination of the functions
\psi^{(j)}
, for which condition (
2.13
) is violated. If the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
has no virtual level at the bottom of its essential spectrum, we let
k:=0
.

We denote:

\varPsi^{(j)}:=\mathcal{U}_{\gamma}(\psi^{(j)})
,

j=1,\ldots,k
.
We choose the functions
\psi^{(j)}
so that the associated vectors
\varPsi^{(j)}
are orthonormalized in
\mathds{C}^{d_{0}}
. If
k<d_{0}
, we choose arbitrary vectors
\varPsi^{(j)}\in\mathds{C}^{d_{0}}
,
j=k+1,\ldots,d_{0}
, so that the vectors
\varPsi^{(j)}\in\mathds{C}^{d_{0}}
,
j=1,\ldots,d_{0}
, form an orthonormalized basis in
\mathds{C}^{d_{0}}
. Hence, the matrix

\Psi:=\begin{pmatrix}\varPsi^{(1)}&\ldots&\varPsi^{(k)}&\varPsi^{(k+1)}&\ldots&\varPsi^{(d_{0})}\end{pmatrix}

is unitary.

2.3
Parts of the resolvent and main result

Our main result concerns certain operators, which are naturally regarded as some parts of the resolvent
(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}
. In this subsection we introduce these operators and their limits as
\varepsilon\to+0
and we formulate our main result.

Let
\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}:L_{2}(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})\to L_{2}(\Gamma)
and
\mathcal{P}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}:L_{2}(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})\to L_{2}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})
be the operators of restriction to the graphs
\Gamma
and
\gamma_{\varepsilon}

they act as follows:

\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}f:=f\big|_{\Gamma}

\mathcal{P}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}f:=f\big|_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}

these operators satisfy the identity

\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}\oplus\mathcal{P}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}=\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}},

(2.14)

where
\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}
is the identity mapping in
L_{2}(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})
.

For each
\lambda\in\mathds{C}\setminus\mathds{R}
the resolvent
(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}
is well-defined.
The operators

\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda):=\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}(\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}),\qquad\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda):=\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}(\mathcal{I}_{\Gamma}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon})

are also well-defined, linear and are bounded as acting from
L_{2}(\Gamma)\oplus L_{2}(\gamma)
into
\dot{W}_{2}^{2}(\Gamma)
and
\dot{W}_{2}^{2}(\gamma)
; here the direct sum is understood in the sense of identity (
2.14
).

Let us clarify the action of the operators
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}
.
Given
f\in L_{2}(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})
and
u_{\varepsilon}:=(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}f
, we consider the restrictions of these functions to the subgraphs
\Gamma
and
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
. The restrictions to
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
are additionally rescaled by means of the operator
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}

defined on

\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}f

\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}u_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}f

\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}

\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
and
\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
map a pair
(\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}f,\,\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}f)

\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}u_{\varepsilon}

\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}

We also observe an obvious identity implied immediately by the definition of the operators
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda)

\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda)

(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}=\big(\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda)\oplus\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda)\big)(\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}).

(2.15)

Now we introduce an operator, which will serve as a limiting one for
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda)

\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
represents the action of the resolvent
(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}
on the subgraph
\Gamma

(\mathcal{H}_{0}-\lambda)^{-1}

\mathcal{H}_{0}
is an operator in
L_{2}(\Gamma)

with the differential expression
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(0)

subject to the vertex conditions

For the vertices

\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(0)}:=\mathrm{B}_{M}(0)

(2.21)

,

The corresponding matrices

3
Discussion and generalizations

3.1
Discussion of main results

In this subsection we discuss the main features of our problem and the main result. First of all we stress that our operator is very general. Namely, its differential expression involves not only the potential, but also the first order terms and a varying coefficient at a higher derivatives, see (
2.1
). On the small edges, the coefficients can depend singularly on
\varepsilon
because of the presence of the negative powers of
\varepsilon
in (
2.2
). The vertex conditions are also of general form, see (
2.4
). The self-adjointness of the matrix in (
2.5
) and rank condition (
2.7
) are very natural and in fact, they are a criterion ensuring the self-adjointness of the considered operator.

Our main result, Theorem
2.1
, states that under the assumptions made for the coefficients in the differential expression and the vertex conditions and under Condition
(A)
, the resolvent of the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
is analytic
in
\varepsilon
in certain sense. Namely, the operators
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
and
\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
are analytic
in
\varepsilon
. Since the resolvent
(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}
can be recovered from the operators
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
and
\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
via formula (
2.15
), Theorem
2.1
in fact states the analyticity
of the parts of the resolvent
(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}
corresponding to subgraphs
\Gamma
and
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
. As we have already mentioned, the presence of small edges is a singular perturbation, which can not be treated by the methods from the regular perturbation theory
[
11
]
. Usually, for singularly perturbed problems one can expect only the convergence result and a chance to construct some asymptotic series. For instance, this is a standard situation for elliptic operators on Euclidean domains or manifolds with singular localized perturbation
[
10
]
,
[
12
]
. A classical example is an elliptic problem in a domain with a small hole, see
[
10
, Ch. I​I​I]
,
[
12
, Ch. I​I, Sec. 2.2]
. It is possible to construct complete asymptotic expansions for the corresponding resolvent provided it acts on a sufficiently smooth function, but already simple explicitly solvable examples of such problems show that these asymptotic series are not analytic in the small parameter. From this point of view, our result on analyticity is of a special nature: despite of the presence of a singular perturbation, the parts of the resolvent
(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}
are analytic in
\varepsilon
in the operator norms and these norms are strongest possible.

Once we know that the operators
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
and
\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda)
are analytic
in
\varepsilon
, they are represented by their converging Taylor series. Then we can recover the resolvent
(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}
via formula (
2.15
) and obtain a converging Taylor-like series for the resolvent. Namely, given the Taylor series

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda)(f_{\Gamma},f_{\gamma})=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}u_{l}^{\Gamma},

\displaystyle u_{0}^{\Gamma}:=(\mathcal{H}_{0}-\lambda)^{-1}f_{\Gamma},

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}(\varepsilon,\lambda)(f_{\Gamma},f_{\gamma})=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}u_{l}^{\gamma},\quad

\displaystyle u_{0}^{\gamma}:=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}(f_{\Gamma})\psi^{(i)},

converging respectively in
\dot{W}_{2}^{2}(\Gamma)\cap\mathfrak{C}^{2}(\Gamma)
and
\dot{W}_{2}^{2}(\gamma)\cap\mathfrak{C}^{2}(\gamma)
, we immediately obtain

(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}f=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{l}u_{l}^{\Gamma}\oplus\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}u_{l}^{\gamma},\qquad f_{\Gamma}:=\mathcal{P}_{\Gamma}f,\qquad f_{\gamma}:=\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}_{\gamma_{\varepsilon}}f,

(3.2)

where the series converges in
\dot{W}_{2}^{2}(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})\cap\mathfrak{C}^{2}(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})
. The remainder in this series can be estimated by means of standard estimates for the remainders of the Taylor series in (
3.1
); one just should take into consideration the following obvious relations:

\big\|f_{\Gamma}\oplus f_{\gamma}\big\|_{L_{2}(\Gamma)\oplus L_{2}(\gamma)}=\left(\|f\|_{L_{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}+\varepsilon^{-1}\|f\|_{L_{2}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\leqslant\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|_{L_{2}(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})}.

In particular, in this way we obtain:

\Big\|(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}f-(\mathcal{H}_{0}-\lambda)^{-1}f\Big\|_{\dot{W}_{2}^{2}(\Gamma)\cap\mathfrak{C}^{2}(\Gamma)}\leqslant C\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|_{L_{2}(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})};

(3.3)

hereinafter the symbol
C
denotes various inessential constants independent of
f
,
f_{\Gamma}
,
f_{\gamma}
and
\varepsilon
.
By straightforward calculations it is easy to confirm that

\|\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}^{-1}u_{0}^{\gamma}\|_{L_{2}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})}^{2}=\varepsilon\|u_{0}^{\gamma}\|_{L_{2}(\gamma)}^{2}\leqslant C\varepsilon\|f_{\Gamma}\|_{L_{2}(\Gamma)}^{2}\leqslant C\varepsilon\|f\|_{L_{2}(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})}^{2},

and then it follows from (
3.2
)
that

\big\|(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}f\big\|_{L_{2}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})}\leqslant C\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|_{L_{2}(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})}.

The latter estimate and (
3.3
) are exactly a convergence result similar to ones established in papers
[
1
]
and
[
7
]
. However, in these papers the difference of the resolvents
(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}f
and
(\mathcal{H}_{0}-\lambda)^{-1}f
was estimated in
L_{2}
-norm only, while our inequality (
3.3
) provides the bounds in stronger
\dot{W}_{2}^{2}(\Gamma)
-norm and
\mathfrak{C}^{2}(\Gamma)
-norm. Moreover, we have one more estimate

\bigg\|(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}f-\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}u_{0}^{\gamma}\bigg\|_{\mathfrak{C}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})}\leqslant C\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|_{L_{2}(\Gamma_{\varepsilon})}.

This estimate shows how to approximate the resolvent
(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}f
on
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
to get a small error term. In particular, in view of formula for
u_{0}^{\gamma}
in (
3.1
), we see that the resolvent
(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}f
is approximated by
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}(f_{\Gamma})\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}\psi^{(i)}
in
\mathfrak{C}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})
-norm with an error of order
\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

In comparison with
[
1
]
and
[
7
]
, our convergence result has one more important feature. This feature is related with the limiting condition at the vertex
M_{0}
for the operator
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, namely, with the definition of the matrices
\mathrm{A}_{M_{0}}^{(0)}
and
\mathrm{B}_{M_{0}}^{(0)}
. As formulae (
2.22
) show, if the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
has a virtual level at the bottom of its essential spectrum, the matrix
\mathrm{A}_{M_{0}}^{(0)}
involves the matrix
\mathrm{Q}
. The presence of the latter produces a Robin part in the vertex condition at the vertex
M_{0}
. According to formulae (
2.23
), (
2.24
), (
2.25
), (
2.26
), the matrix
\mathrm{Q}
is determined by the functions
\frac{dV_{\gamma}^{(i)}}{d\varepsilon}(\,\cdot\,,0)
and by the matrices
\mathrm{A}_{M}^{(1)}
,
\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(1)}
,
M\in\gamma
.

We also observe that the functions
\frac{dV_{\gamma}^{(i)}}{d\varepsilon}(\,\cdot\,,0)
and the matrices
\mathrm{A}_{M}^{(1)}
,
\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(1)}
are in fact
next-to-leading terms
in the above differential expression and vertex conditions. Hence, we conclude that the Robin part in the limiting condition at the vertex
M_{0}
is generated
solely by the above next-to-leading terms
. Once such terms vanish, only the Dirichlet and Neumann parts in the vertex condition at
M_{0}
can be present. In particular, this explains why only the Dirichlet and Neumann parts appeared in the limit in
[
7
]
; the next-to-leading terms were apriori assumed to be zero. The results of
[
1
]
allowed a Robin part in the limiting vertex condition, but in terms of our notations this was because the matrices
\mathrm{A}_{M}
and
\mathrm{B}_{M}
in (
2.9
)
were assumed to be independent of
\varepsilon
and this is why the matrices
\mathrm{A}_{M}^{(1)}
were in general non-zero. However, the issue when and why the Robin part appears in the limiting condition at
M_{0}
was not addressed in
[
1
]
and moreover, since the main result in
[
1
]
was formulated in terms of Lagrangian planes, the presence and the origination of the Robin part in the limiting vertex condition was not so explicit as in our case. We also stress that our result shows how the Robin condition can be generated in the general case by the coefficients both in the differential expression and vertex conditions of
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
.

In order to demonstrate the difference between our convergence result and those in
[
1
]
,
[
7
]
, we consider a simple model of a star graph with two fixed edges and one small edge of the length
\varepsilon
, which was studied in
[
6
]
, see Figure
3
. On the fixed edges we consider a Schrödinger operator
-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+V_{\pm}
with some continuous potentials, while on the small edge the operator reads as
-\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+\varepsilon^{-1}V_{0}(\,\cdot\,\varepsilon)
, where
V_{0}\in C[0,1]
is some given function. At the vertex
M_{0}
the Kirchhoff condition is imposed, while the vertex
M_{\varepsilon}
is subject to the Dirichlet or Neumann condition. The limiting operator then is just the same operator on finite edges, while at the vertex
M_{0}
we have the Dirichlet condition if the same is imposed at
M_{\varepsilon}
. But if the vertex
M_{\varepsilon}
is subject to the Neumann condition, then in the limit at
M_{0}
we have a delta-interaction, that is, the Kirchhoff condition with a coupling:

u\big|_{\mathrm{e}_{-}}(M_{0})=u\big|_{\mathrm{e}_{-}}(M_{0})=:u(M_{0}),\qquad u\big|_{\mathrm{e}_{-}}(M_{0})+u\big|_{\mathrm{e}_{-}}(M_{0})=cu(M_{0}),\qquad c:=\int\limits_{0}^{1}V_{0}(t)\,dt.

As we see, the coupling constant
c
is determined solely by the potential
V_{0}
.

It is also interesting to compare our Condition
(A)
with similar conditions in
[
1
]
and
[
7
]
. All these conditions are close but there are some differences. In our terms, roughly speaking, Non-resonance condition 3.2 in
[
1
]
says that there are no embedded eigenvalues at the bottom of the essential spectrum for all
\varepsilon>0
. Namely,
we should repeat the rescaling we employed while obtaining the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
in (
2.8
), (
2.11
) but without letting
\varepsilon=0
. This gives an operator depending on
\varepsilon
and coinciding with
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
as
\varepsilon=0
. Such operator can not have an embedded eigenvalue at the bottom of its essential spectrum for
\varepsilon>0
. This does not imply that the same holds for
\varepsilon=0
and this is the main difference between our Condition
(A)
and Non-resonance condition in
[
1
]
.
Once we make the aforementioned rescaling of small edges for the model studied in
[
7
]
, we arrive at an operator on the graph
\gamma_{\infty}
, which is independent of
\varepsilon
and coincides with our operator
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
.
In this specific situation, in
[
7
]
, there were considered two cases: generic and non-generic ones. The generic case corresponds to the absence of embedded eigenvalues. In the non-generic case, there can be both virtual level and embedded eigenvalues at the bottom of the essential spectrum. But since the model in
[
7
]
was quite specific, it was possible to study the the convergence of the resolvent in the non-generic case as well.

In the present work we do not study the case when Condition
(A)
is violated and the operator has an embedded eigenvalue at the bottom of the essential spectrum. The reason is that although our technique is sufficient to treat such case, it involves more technical details. The nature of the expected results also suggests that this case deserves an independent study, which we postpone for the next work.

3.2
Generalizations of model

Here we discuss
possible ways of extending our results to more general models.

The first
possibility concerns a case, when instead of one graph
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
, several similar small rescaled graphs
\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{(j)}
are glued at
different vertices of the graph
\Gamma
, see Figure
4
. Each of the small graphs
\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{(j)}
can be introduced via rescaling by means of an associated small parameter
\varepsilon^{(i)}
; then coefficients
p_{\varepsilon}^{(j)}
,
q_{\varepsilon}^{(j)}
,
V_{\varepsilon}^{(j)}
of the differential expression on each such graph
\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{(j)}
are still defined by formula (
2.2
), but now
on each graph
\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{(j)}
the rescaling is made by means of a corresponding operator
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}^{(j)}
. We can suppose first that all parameters
\varepsilon^{(j)}
are analytic
functions in a single small parameter
\varepsilon
. In this situation, all our results remain valid with obvious minor changes; in particular, the form of the limiting vertex condition at a vertex
M_{0}^{(j)}
, to which the graph
\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{(j)}
is attached, is determined only by the graph
\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{(j)}
and there is no influence by the neighbouring ones.

Figure 3:
Star graph with a small edge and the limiting graph without the small edge.

A more general situation is when in the same setting we assume that the parameters
\varepsilon^{(j)}
are independent and all coefficients in the differential expression and vertex conditions of the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
depend analytically in all
\varepsilon^{(j)}
. In such case, we first of all should assume additionally that for each attached graph
\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{(j)}
and each vertex
M\in\gamma_{\infty}^{(j)}
, identities similar to (
2.9
) hold:

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{(j)}\tilde{\mathrm{E}}_{M}(\varepsilon^{(j)})\mathrm{A}_{M}(\varepsilon)=\big(\varepsilon^{(j)}\big)^{\mu}\big(\mathrm{A}_{M}^{(0)}+\varepsilon^{(j)}\mathrm{A}_{M}^{(1)}\big)+O(|\varepsilon|^{2+\mu}),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{E}}_{M}(\varepsilon^{(j)})\mathrm{B}_{M}(\varepsilon)=\big(\varepsilon^{(j)}\big)^{\mu}\big(\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(0)}+\varepsilon^{(j)}\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(1)}\big)+O(|\varepsilon|^{2+\mu}),

where
\mu=0

if
\mathrm{B}_{M}(0)\neq 0
and
\mu=1

if
\mathrm{B}_{M}(0)=0
and
\varepsilon=(\varepsilon^{(1)},\varepsilon^{(2)},\ldots)
. For the coefficients of the differential expression we should assume that

p_{\gamma^{(j)}}(\,\cdot\,,\varepsilon)=p_{\gamma^{(j)}}(\,\cdot\,,0)+\varepsilon^{(j)}\frac{dp_{\gamma^{(j)}}}{d\varepsilon^{(j)}}(\,\cdot\,,0)+O(|\varepsilon|^{2}),

(3.4)

and the same should hold for the other coefficients.
Then the corresponding matrices
Q^{(j)}
are to be calculated for each graph
\gamma^{(j)}
by formulae (
2.23
), (
2.24
), (
2.25
), (
2.26
). If for some
j
the matrix
Q^{(j)}
turns out to have a zero eigenvalue, then assumptions (
2.9
), (
3.4
)
should be replaced by stricter ones:

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{(j)}\tilde{\mathrm{E}}_{M}(\varepsilon^{(j)})\mathrm{A}_{M}(\varepsilon)=\big(\varepsilon^{(j)}\big)^{\mu}\big(\mathrm{A}_{M}^{(0)}+\varepsilon^{(j)}\mathrm{A}_{M}^{(1)}\big)+O(|\varepsilon|^{2+\mu}),

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{E}}_{M}(\varepsilon^{(j)})\mathrm{B}_{M}(\varepsilon)=\big(\varepsilon^{(j)}\big)^{\mu}\big(\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(0)}+\varepsilon^{(j)}\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(1)}\big)+O(|\varepsilon|^{2+\mu}),

\displaystyle p_{\gamma^{(j)}}(\,\cdot\,,\varepsilon)=p_{\gamma^{(j)}}(\,\cdot\,,0)+\varepsilon^{(j)}\frac{dp_{\gamma^{(j)}}}{d\varepsilon^{(j)}}(\,\cdot\,,0)+\frac{\big(\varepsilon^{(j)}\big)^{2}}{2}\frac{d^{2}p_{\gamma^{(j)}}}{d(\varepsilon^{(j)})^{2}}(\,\cdot\,,0)+O(|\varepsilon|^{3}).

The above conditions mean that the coefficients in the differential expression and vertex conditions can depend on all small parameters, but the leading terms in their Taylor should be as specified above. Under such assumptions, all our results remain true and now the analyticity holds with respect to all parameters
\varepsilon^{(i)}
. Once these assumptions fail, in the general situation, the result on analyticity
is no longer valid. The reason for this situation is in fact a well-known phenomenon in the multi-parametric perturbation theory, which says that the eigenvalues of a matrix analytic with respect to more than one small parameter are not necessary analytic, see

[
11
, Ch. I​I, Sect. 5.7]
. The same also concerns the case, when several graphs
\gamma^{(j)}
are rescaled by means of different independent small parameters and are glued to the same vertex in the graph
\Gamma
. In such general situation one can expect only a convergence result as one proved in
[
1
]
. Of course, this does not exclude the situations, when for some particular graphs and specific choices of small edges, differential expression and vertex conditions, the resolvent has the analyticity property, but this can happen only due to some specific features of the considered models.

Figure 4:
Graph
\Gamma
with several glued small graphs

One more important way of extending our model is to assume that all coefficients in the differential expression and the vertex conditions are not analytic in
\varepsilon
but either infinite differentiable or just possess power asymptotic expansions in
\varepsilon
. In these cases all our results, including ones just discussed above for several glued small graphs, remain true with appropriate modifications. Namely, if the mentioned coefficients are infinite differentiable in
\varepsilon
, then the statement on the analyticity property is to be replaced by the infinite differentiability in
\varepsilon
for the operators
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}
. If the coefficients in the differential expression and the vertex conditions just possess power asymptotic expansions in
\varepsilon
, then the operators
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}
also have power asymptotic expansions in
\varepsilon
. The way of proving such results is very simple and is based on the analyticity case treated above. Namely, we represent the matrices in the vertex conditions as

\mathrm{A}_{M}(\varepsilon)=\mathrm{A}_{M}^{(0)}+\varepsilon\mathrm{A}_{M}^{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}\mathrm{A}_{M}^{(2)}+\varepsilon^{3}\breve{\mathrm{A}}_{M}(\varepsilon),\qquad\mathrm{B}_{M}(\varepsilon)=\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(0)}+\varepsilon\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(1)}+\varepsilon^{2}\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(2)}+\varepsilon^{3}\breve{\mathrm{B}}_{M}(\varepsilon),

(3.5)

where the matrices
\breve{\mathrm{A}}_{M}(\varepsilon)
and
\breve{\mathrm{B}}_{M}(\varepsilon)
are either infinitely differentiable in
\varepsilon
or have power in
\varepsilon
asymptotic expansions. For the coefficients of the differentiable expression, a similar representation is to be employed. The terms
\breve{\mathrm{A}}_{M}
and
\breve{\mathrm{B}}_{M}
are to be treated as frozen coefficients independent of
\varepsilon
and the matrices in (
3.5
) should be treated as second order polynomials in
\varepsilon
. Then it is possible to track the dependence on
\breve{\mathrm{A}}_{M}
and
\breve{\mathrm{B}}_{M}(\varepsilon)
and on similar terms for the coefficients in the differential expression in the proof of Theorem
2.1
. As a result, then one can see that the operators
\mathcal{R}_{\Gamma}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\gamma}
as well as the resolvent
(\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}-\lambda)^{-1}
are represented by convergent series (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), but now with coefficients depending on
\varepsilon
. These coefficients are either infinitely differentiable in
\varepsilon
or possess power in
\varepsilon
asymptotic expansions. In the first case we conclude immediately that all the operators in question are infinitely differentiable in
\varepsilon
, while in the other case we can easily find the asymptotic expansions for these operators from their series.

4
Self-adjointness

Our main result includes a statement on the self-adjointness of both perturbed and limiting operators and in the proofs, we shall also use the same property for certain auxiliary operators.
In this section we first establish a criterion of the self-adjointness for a general operator. Then we shall employ it to prove the same property for the operators
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\gamma}
.

4.1
Criterion of self-adjointness

Let
\Xi
be a finite metric graph having no isolated vertices; the lengths of its edges can be both finite or infinite. On each edge we fix arbitrarily an orientation and as above, by
d(M)
we denote the degree of a vertex
M\in\Xi
.

We consider an unbounded operator
\mathcal{H}
in
L_{2}(\Xi)
with the differential expression

\hat{\mathcal{H}}u:=-\frac{d\ }{dx}p\frac{du}{dx}+\mathrm{i}\left(\frac{d\ }{dx}(qu)+q\frac{du}{dx}\right)+Vu

subject to the vertex conditions

\mathrm{A}_{M}\mathcal{U}_{M}(u)+\mathrm{B}_{M}\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\prime}(u)=0\quad\text{at each vertex $M$}.

(4.1)

Here
p,\,q\in W_{\infty}^{1}(\Xi)
,
V\in L_{2}(\Xi)
are real-valued functions, while
\mathrm{A}_{M}
and
\mathrm{B}_{M}
are some matrices of size
d(M)\times d(M)
. The ellipticity condition is assumed:

p\geqslant c>0
uniformly on
\Xi
.
The domain of the operator
\mathcal{H}
consists of the functions in
\dot{W}_{2}^{2}(\Xi)
obeying vertex condition (
4.1
).

By
\Pi_{M}
,
\Theta_{M}
we denote the following diagonal matrices with real entries:

\Pi_{M}:=\diag\big\{\nu_{i}(M)p\big|_{\mathrm{e}_{i}(M)}(M,\varepsilon)\big\}_{i=1,\ldots,d(M)},\qquad\Theta_{M}:=\diag\big\{\nu_{i}(M)q\big|_{\mathrm{e}_{i}(M)}(M,\varepsilon)\big\}_{i=1,\ldots,d(M)},

where
\mathrm{e}_{i}(M)
are the edges incident to the vertex
M
and the numbers
\nu_{i}(M)
are defined in the same way as in (
2.6
).

The main statement of this subsection is the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

The operator
\mathcal{H}
is self-adjoint if and only if for each vertex
M\in\Xi
the matrices
\mathrm{A}_{M}
and
\mathrm{B}_{M}
satisfy the rank condition

\rank(\mathrm{A}_{M}\ \mathrm{B}_{M})=d(M)

(4.2)

and the matrix

\mathrm{A}_{M}\Pi_{M}^{-1}\mathrm{B}_{M}^{*}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{B}_{M}\Pi_{M}^{-1}\Theta_{M}\Pi_{M}^{-1}\mathrm{B}_{M}^{*}

(4.3)

is self-adjoint. Under these restrictions, vertex condition (
4.1
) can be equivalently rewritten as

\mathrm{i}(\mathrm{U}_{M}-\mathrm{I}_{d(M)})\mathcal{U}_{M}(u)+(\mathrm{U}_{M}+\mathrm{I}_{d(M)})\big(\Pi_{M}\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\prime}(u)-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{M}\mathcal{U}_{M}(u)\big)=0,

(4.4)

where the matrix

\mathrm{U}_{M}:=-\big(\mathrm{A}_{M}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{B}_{M}\Pi_{M}^{-1}(\Theta_{M}-\mathrm{I}_{d(M)})\big)^{-1}\big(\mathrm{A}_{M}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{B}_{M}\Pi_{M}^{-1}(\Theta_{M}+\mathrm{I}_{d(M)})\big)

is well-defined and is unitary.

Proof.

In a particular case
p\equiv 1
,
q\equiv 0
,
V\equiv 0
, the lemma states a well-known result, see Theorem 1.1.4 in
[
2
, Ch. 1, Sect. 1.4.]
. In fact, the proof of this theorem can be adapted also for our more general case up to some minor changes. Below we describe how to do this.

First of all we observe that vertex condition (
4.1
) can be equivalently rewritten as

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{A}}_{M}\mathcal{U}_{M}(u)+\tilde{\mathrm{B}}_{M}\big(\Pi_{M}\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\prime}(u)-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{M}\mathcal{U}_{M}(u)\big)=0,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathrm{A}}_{M}:=\mathrm{A}_{M}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{B}_{M}\Pi_{M}^{-1}\Theta_{M},\qquad\tilde{\mathrm{B}}_{M}:=\mathrm{B}_{M}\Pi_{M}^{-1}.

(4.6)

Let
u
be an arbitrary function from the domain of the operator
\mathcal{H}
and
v
be an arbitrary function from
\bigoplus\limits_{\mathrm{e}\in\Xi}C^{\infty}(\mathrm{e})
vanishing outside a small neighbourhood of an arbitrarily fixed vertex
M\in\Xi
. Integrating twice by parts, we get:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{H}u,v)_{L_{2}(\Xi)}=

\displaystyle\big(\Pi_{M}\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\prime}(u),\mathcal{U}_{M}(v)\big)_{\mathds{C}^{d(M)}}-\big(\mathcal{U}_{M}(u),\Pi_{M}\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\prime}(v)\big)_{\mathds{C}^{d(M)}}+(u,\hat{\mathcal{H}}v)_{L_{2}(\Xi)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathsf{F}_{M}^{\prime},\mathsf{G}_{M})_{\mathds{C}^{d(M)}}-(\mathsf{F}_{M},\mathsf{G}_{M}^{\prime})_{\mathds{C}^{d(M)}}+(u,\hat{\mathcal{H}}v)_{L_{2}(\Xi)},

where

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{M}^{\prime}:=\Pi_{M}\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\prime}(u)-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{M}\mathcal{U}_{M}(u),\qquad\mathsf{G}_{M}:=\mathcal{U}_{M}(v),

\displaystyle\mathsf{F}_{M}:=\mathcal{U}_{M}(u),\qquad\mathsf{G}_{M}^{\prime}:=\Pi_{M}\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\prime}(v)-\mathrm{i}\Theta_{M}\mathcal{U}_{M}(v).

Hence, the function
v
is in the domain of the adjoint operator
\mathcal{H}^{*}
if

(\mathsf{F}_{M}^{\prime},\mathsf{G}_{M})_{\mathds{C}^{d(M)}}-(\mathsf{F}_{M},\mathsf{G}_{M}^{\prime})_{\mathds{C}^{d(M)}}=0.

The latter identity is exactly equation (1.4.16) in the proof of Theorem 1.1.4 in
[
2
, Ch. 1, Sect. 1.4]
but with different
\mathsf{F}_{M}
,
\mathsf{F}_{M}^{\prime}
,
\mathsf{G}_{M}
,
\mathsf{G}_{M}^{\prime}
. However, the specific form of these quantities were not important in that proof in
[
2
]
. This is why the arguing from
[
2
]
can be reproduced literally with above introduced
\mathsf{F}_{M}
,
\mathsf{F}_{M}^{\prime}
,
\mathsf{G}_{M}
,
\mathsf{G}_{M}^{\prime}
; as the matrices
A
and
B
used in that proof, our matrices
\tilde{\mathrm{A}}_{M}
and
\tilde{\mathrm{B}}_{M}
serve. Rank condition
(
4.2
) is equivalent to similar condition (1.4.10) in
[
2
, Ch. 1, Sect. 1.4]
since thanks to the non-degeneracy of the matrix
\Pi_{M}^{-1}
we have

\displaystyle\rank(\tilde{\mathrm{A}}_{M}\ \;\tilde{\mathrm{B}}_{M})=

\displaystyle\rank\big(\mathrm{A}_{M}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{B}_{M}\Pi_{M}^{-1}\Theta_{M}\ \;\mathrm{B}_{M}\Pi_{M}^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rank\big(\mathrm{A}_{M}\ \;\mathrm{B}_{M}\Pi_{M}^{-1}\big)=\rank\big(\mathrm{A}_{M}\ \;\mathrm{B}_{M}\big)=d(M).

Condition (1.4.11) for the matrices
A
and
B
from Theorem 1.1.4 in
[
2
, Ch. 1, Sect. 1.4.]
is exactly our condition (
4.3
), while equivalent formulation
(
4.4
)
of the vertex condition are implied immediately by similar formulations (1.4.13)
in
[
2
]
. The proof is complete.
∎

4.2
Self-adjointness of operators
\mathcal{H}_{0}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}

In this subsection we prove that under the assumptions of

2.1

\mathcal{H}_{0}

Lemma 4.2

The matrix
\mathrm{Q}

Let us show that the operators
\mathcal{H}_{0}

The matrix in (
2.5

\varepsilon=0
for each vertex
M\in\Gamma
,
M\neq M_{0}
. Hence, the matrices
\mathrm{A}_{M}^{(0)}
,
\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(0)}
satisfy condition (
4.2
), (
4.3
) for each vertex
M\in\Gamma
,
M\neq M_{0}

\mathrm{Q}
stated in Lemma
4.2
, the self-adjointness of the matrix in (

) corresponding to the matrices
\mathrm{A}_{M_{0}}^{(0)}
and
\mathrm{B}_{M_{0}}^{(0)}
is confirmed by simple straightforward calculations. In view of the non-degeneracy of the matrix
\Pi_{\Gamma,M_{0}}(0)

4.2

\displaystyle\rank\big(\mathrm{A}_{M_{0}}^{(0)}\ \mathrm{B}_{M_{0}}^{(0)}\big)=

\displaystyle\rank\Big(\mathrm{A}_{M_{0}}^{(0)}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{B}_{M_{0}}^{(0)}\Pi_{\Gamma,M_{0}}^{-1}(0)\Theta_{\Gamma,M_{0}}(0)\Psi\quad-\mathrm{B}_{M_{0}}^{(0)}\Pi_{\Gamma,M_{0}}^{-1}(0)\Psi\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rank\left(\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{Q}&0\\
0&\mathrm{I}_{d_{0}-k}\end{pmatrix}\quad\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{I}_{k}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\right)=d_{0}.

Hence, we can apply Lemma
4.1
and we see that the operator
\mathcal{H}_{0}

We proceed to proving the self-adjointness of the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
. In view of the assumption on the matrices
\mathrm{A}_{M}(0)
and
\mathrm{B}_{M}(0)
made in Subsection

, see the explanation after (

\mathrm{A}_{M}^{(0)}
and
\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(0)}
defined in (

Then we multiply the matrix in (

and then by
\tilde{\mathrm{E}}_{M}(\varepsilon)
from left and right keeping its self-adjointness and then we pass to the limit as

\mathrm{A}_{M}^{(0)}\Pi_{\gamma,M}^{-1}(0)\big(\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(0)}\big)^{*}+\mathrm{i}\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(0)}\Pi_{\gamma,M}^{-1}(0)\Theta_{\gamma,M}(0)\Pi_{\gamma,M}^{-1}(0)\big(\mathrm{B}_{M}^{(0)}\big)^{*}.

Applying now Lemma
4.1
, we conclude that the operator

4.3
Self-adjointness of
\mathrm{Q}

In this section we prove Lemma
4.2
. The proof is based on obtaining certain representations for the operators
\mathcal{L}_{M}
and
\mathcal{E}_{M}
used in (

), (

), (

), which will imply then the desired self-adjointness. These representations are based on an appropriate transformation of vertex conditions (

Given a vertex
M\in\gamma_{\varepsilon}
, including vertices
M=M_{j}
,

, we rescale the variables as
\xi=x\varepsilon^{-1}
passing in fact to the graph
\gamma_{\infty}
. Then vertex condition (
2.4
) becomes

\varepsilon\mathrm{A}_{M}(\varepsilon)\mathcal{U}_{M}(u)+\mathrm{B}_{M}(\varepsilon)\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\prime}(u)=0,

(4.7)

where the vectors
\mathcal{U}_{M}(u)
and
\mathcal{U}_{M}^{\prime}(u)
are introduced as above but are treated in terms of the variable
\xi
. According Lemma
4.1
, at each vertex

The proof of Lemma

5.1
Meromoprhic dependence on
\varepsilon

In this section we begin proving Lemma
5.1
and we shall show that the resolvent
(\mathcal{H}_{ex}(\varepsilon)-\varepsilon^{2}\lambda)^{-1}
is meromoprhic in
\varepsilon
and has a pole at
\varepsilon=0
of at most second order.

Since the graph
\gamma_{ex}
is finite, the resolvent of the operator
\mathcal{H}_{ex}(\varepsilon)
is compact and hence, this operator has a discrete spectrum. For small
\varepsilon
the number
\varepsilon^{2}\lambda
is also small and this is why we should first check whether the operator
\mathcal{H}_{ex}(\varepsilon)
has small eigenvalues and if so, how they behave.
In order to understand this, we consider the operator
\mathcal{H}_{ex}(0)
. We restrict the functions
\tilde{\psi}^{(j)}
to the graph
\gamma_{ex}
and we see immediately that these restrictions, still denoted by
\tilde{\psi}^{(j)}
, are the eigenfunctions of the operator
\mathcal{H}_{ex}(0)
associated with a zero eigenvalue. And vice versa, if
\psi
is an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{H}_{ex}(0)
associated with the zero eigenvalue, then in view of the definition of the differential expression
\mathcal{H}_{ex}(0)
on the edges
\mathrm{e}_{i}^{ex}
and the Neumann condition at their end-points
M_{i}^{ex}
, the function
\psi
is necessary constant on each of these edges. Then we replace the edges
\mathrm{e}_{i}^{ex}
by infinite edges
\mathrm{e}_{i}^{\infty}
passing in this way to the graph
\gamma_{\infty}
and we continue then the function
\psi
by the aforementioned constants on entire edges
\mathrm{e}_{i}^{\infty}
. The resulting function solves problem (
2.12
). Therefore, zero is an eigenvalue of the operator
\mathcal{H}_{ex}(0)
if and only if the operator
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
has a virtual level at the bottom of its essential spectrum. The eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{H}_{ex}(0)
associated with the zero eigenvalues are the aforementioned restrictions
\tilde{\psi}^{(j)}
to
\gamma_{ex}
.

The resolvent
(\mathcal{H}_{ex}(\varepsilon)-z)^{-1}
depends analytically in small
\varepsilon
for

separated from the spectrum of the operator
\mathcal{H}_{ex}(0)
.
In a particular case when the operator
\mathcal{H}_{ex}(\varepsilon)
is just a Schrödinger operator with a fixed potential independent of
\varepsilon
, this is a corollary of a more general result presented in Theorem 3.5 in
[
3
]
. For the case of the differential expression with general varying and
\varepsilon
-dependent coefficients the technique used in
[
3
]
still works as it is said in
[
3
]
after Equation (2). Hence, due to standard results in the analytic perturbation theory, the operator
\mathcal{H}_{ex}(\varepsilon)
has exactly
k
eigenvalues, counting their multiplicity, which converge to zero as
\varepsilon\to+0
. We denote these eigenvalues by
\Lambda_{j}^{(\varepsilon)}
,
j=1,\ldots,k
, while the associated eigenfunctions orthonormalized in
L_{2}(\gamma_{ex})

are denoted by
\varphi_{j}^{(\varepsilon)}
. According general Theorems 3.8, 3.9 in
[
3
]
, the eigenvalues
\Lambda_{j}(\varepsilon)
are analytic in
\varepsilon
and the eigenfunctions
\varphi_{j}
can be also chosen being analytic in
\varepsilon
.

Standard representation for the resolvent of a self-adjoint operator, see
[
11
, Ch. V, Sect. 3.5]
, then gives:

\big(\mathcal{H}_{ex}(\varepsilon)-\varepsilon^{2}\lambda\big)^{-1}f=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\big(f,\varphi_{j}^{(\varepsilon)}\big)_{L_{2}(\gamma_{ex})}}{\Lambda_{j}^{(\varepsilon)}-\varepsilon^{2}\lambda}\varphi_{j}^{(\varepsilon)}+\mathcal{A}_{1}(\varepsilon,\lambda),

(5.9)

and
\mathcal{A}_{1}
is the reduced resolvent, which can be represented as

\mathcal{A}_{1}(\varepsilon,\lambda)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int\limits_{|z|=\delta}(z-\varepsilon^{2}\lambda)^{-1}\big(\mathcal{H}_{ex}(\varepsilon)-z\big)^{-1}\,dz,

where
\delta
is a sufficiently small fixed positive constant such that the ball
\{z\in\mathds{C}:\,|z|\leqslant\delta\}
contains only zero eigenvalue of the operator
\mathcal{H}_{ex}(0)
and is separated from the other eigenvalues. By the aforementioned analyticity of the resolvent
(\mathcal{H}_{ex}(\varepsilon)-z)^{-1}
in
\varepsilon
and the known analyticity in

\varepsilon
for each fixed

Since the eigenvalues

,

\varepsilon

\varphi_{j}^{(0)}
are eigenfunctions of the operators

associated with the zero eigenvalue and hence, they are some linear combinations of the functions
\psi^{(j)}

We substitute representations (

and it reads as:
