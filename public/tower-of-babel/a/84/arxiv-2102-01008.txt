Quantum scrambling with classical shadows
Roy J. Garcia, You Zhou, Arthur Jaffe

Abstract
Quantum dynamics is of fundamental interest and has implications in quantum information processing. The four-point out-of-time-ordered correlator (OTOC) is traditionally used to quantify quantum information scrambling under many-body dynamics. Due to the OTOC’s unusual time ordering, its measurement is challenging. We propose higher-point OTOCs to reveal early-time scrambling behavior, and present protocols to measure any higher-point OTOC using the shadow estimation method. The protocols circumvent the need for time reversal evolution and ancillary control. They can be implemented in near-term quantum devices with single-qubit readout.



I
Introduction

Quantum scrambling describes the delocalization of quantum information in quantum chaotic systems
Lewis-Swan et al. 2019a
;
Shenker and Stanford 2014a
. Much of the interest in scrambling derives from the study of black holes—the fastest scramblers in nature
Hayden and Preskill 2007
;
Sekino and Susskind 2008
;
Shenker and Stanford 2014a
;
Maldacena et al. 2016
. The holographic duality permits the investigation of black hole scrambling via the probing of certain models in condensed matter physics, like the Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev model
Sachdev and Ye 1993
;
Kitaev 2015
. Scrambling can be studied by probing the four-point out-of-time-ordered correlator (OTOC)
Roberts and Stanford 2015
;
Swingle et al. 2016
;
Swingle and Chowdhury 2017a
;
Chowdhury and Swingle 2017
. These correlators can be used to quantify chaos in many-body systems ranging from a non-integrable Ising model
Bañuls et al. 2011
to the Dicke model
Dicke 1954
;
Alavirad and Lavasani 2019
;
Lewis-Swan et al. 2019b
.
For fast scrambling systems
Sachdev and Ye 1993
;
Kitaev 2015
;
Bentsen et al. 2019
;
Belyansky et al. 2020
, this correlator decays exponentially within the scrambling time, according to a Lyapunov exponent. Measuring OTOCs in this regime can be used to investigate models with holographic duals. OTOCs can also be used to describe slow scrambling due to a small Lyapunov exponent, by probing many-body localized systems
Huang et al. 2016
;
Fan et al. 2017
;
Chen 2016
;
Chen et al. 2016
;
He and Lu 2017
;
Swingle and Chowdhury 2017b
.

The time ordering of the OTOC makes its measurement tedious. Nevertheless, there are experimental protocols to measure the four-point OTOC. Protocols based on time reversal evolution have been demonstrated
Gärttner et al. 2017
;
Wei et al. 2018
. The OTOC has also been measured using a nuclear magnetic resonance quantum simulator
Li et al. 2017
. Recent investigations of scrambling have searched for measurement protocols that circumvent the need for time reversal. To distinguish between scrambling and decoherence, a teleportation protocol that measures the OTOC in the large-time limit has been developed in Ref.
Yoshida and Yao 2019
and demonstrated in Ref.
Landsman et al. 2019
. A method based on statistical correlations computes the four-point OTOC in terms of experimentally-friendly correlators
Vermersch et al. 2019
; it has been demonstrated in Ref.
Joshi et al. 2020
. Although quantum chaos is often studied through the four-point OTOC, it is suspected that scrambling is sensitive to higher-point correlators
Shenker and Stanford 2014b
;
Roberts and Yoshida 2017
;
Landsman et al. 2019
. Thus, one desires a protocol to measure higher-point OTOCs, especially one without time reversal or ancillary control operations.

We present protocols to measure any higher-point out-of-time-ordered correlator using classical shadows
Aaronson 2018
;
Huang et al. 2020
, which is an efficient scheme recently proposed to predict functions of quantum states. While nonlinear functions are often computed by preparing multiple copies of a state
Ekert et al. 2002
;
Daley et al. 2012
;
Abanin and Demler 2012
;
Islam et al. 2015
;
Kaufman et al. 2016
, classical shadows allow us to bypass this preparation by measuring a single copy of the state. Our protocols avoid time reversal and can probe OTOCs at any time. While ancillary qubits are integrated, we do not require they exert an interaction on the system
Yao et al. 2016
. We find that the eight-point OTOC reveals early-time information delocalization not present in the four-point OTOC, making it a promising candidate to probe scrambling dynamics.

We provide a statistical error analysis to show that our protocols are more efficient than brute force tomography. Since our protocols express OTOCs in terms of nonlinear functions of a state, we give a refined variance analysis on nonlinear functions which rely on prior knowledge of the target state, establishing a tighter bound than previous works
Huang et al. 2020
;
Elben et al. 2020a
–a result which is of independent interest. We also numerically simulate our protocols in a non-integrable, mixed-field Ising model and show that they can be implemented by current experimental platforms containing a moderate number of qubits.

I.1
Higher-point correlators

Scrambling is often quantified through operator spreading, described as follows. Consider a quantum many-body system consisting of
N
qubits that evolves by a chaotic Hamiltonian,
H
. Let
W
and
V
be local, unitary operators on Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{d}
of dimension
d=2^{N}
. ‘Local’ refers to unitaries which act on different qubits. Assume
W
and
V
commute at time
t=0
. The Heisenberg operator

W(t)\equiv U_{H}^{\dagger}(t)WU_{H}(t)

(1)

evolves with
U_{H}(t)=e^{-iHt}
(
\hbar=1
). The quantum butterfly effect
Maldacena et al. 2016
;
Karkuszewski et al. 2002
states that
[W(t),V]
grows as
W(t)
delocalizes, i.e. spreads throughout the system. Intuitively,
W(t)
eventually acts non-trivially at the location of
V
, at which point the commutator no longer vanishes. The size of the commutator can therefore be used to quantify the spreading of
W(t)
and hence measure scrambling.

To measure the growth of the commutator, it is common to take its norm
Lieb and Robinson 1972
. The Hilbert–Schmidt norm is most often employed
Swingle et al. 2016
;
Swingle and Chowdhury 2017a
;
Chowdhury and Swingle 2017
,

||[W(t),V]||_{\mathrm{HS}}=[\mathrm{Tr}\{|[W(t),V]|^{2}\}]^{1/2}.

(2)

To measure this quantity, we interpret the trace as (up to a normalization) the expectation value of
|[W(t),V]|^{2}
over the infinite temperature thermal state
\rho_{\infty}=\frac{1}{d}I_{N}
. By adopting the notation
\langle\bm{\cdot}\rangle\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{\infty}\bm{\cdot}\}
, the expectation value is

\langle|[W(t),V]|^{2}\rangle=2(1-Re\{C_{4}(t)\}),

(3)

where the four-point out-of-time-ordered correlator is defined as

C_{4}(t)=\langle W^{\dagger}(t)V^{\dagger}W(t)V\rangle.

(4)

Scrambling causes this correlator to decay to near zero. Although
C_{4}(t)
has been analyzed extensively in the literature, it does not describe the complete evolution of the commutator. Higher-point correlators are necessary to reveal new, early-time scrambling.

To extract higher-point correlators, we measure the commutator growth using the Schatten
2n
-norm for positive integer
n

Bhattacharya et al. 2018
,

||[W(t),V]||_{2n}=[\mathrm{Tr}\{|[W(t),V]|^{2n}\}]^{1/2n}.

(5)

This is computed by measuring
\langle|[W(t),V]|^{2n}\rangle=\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}\{|[W(t),V]|^{2n}\}
. By expanding out
|[W(t),V]|^{2n}
, the Schatten
2n
-norm can be expressed in terms of a linear combination of higher-point correlators

||[W(t),V]||_{2n}=\Bigg[d\sum_{k=0}^{n}b_{k}(n)Re\{C_{4k}(t)\}\Bigg]^{1/2n},

(6)

for some coefficients
b_{k}(n)
. The
4k
-point OTOC, for a non-negative integer
k
, is defined as

C_{4k}(t)\equiv\langle(W^{\dagger}(t)V^{\dagger}W(t)V)^{k}\rangle.

(7)

We propose a physical interpretation of these correlators in Appendix
A
.

In this work, we develop measurement protocols to estimate
C_{4k}(t)
by using randomized measurements
van Enk and Beenakker 2012
;
Elben et al. 2019a
via the classical shadow framework
Huang et al. 2020
. The applications of randomized measurements range from quantum many-body physics, such as the detection of topological order
Elben et al. 2020b
;
Cian et al. 2020
and entanglement entropy
Elben et al. 2018
;
Brydges et al. 2019
, to quantum information and quantum foundations, such as the extraction of entanglement negativity
Elben et al. 2020a
;
Zhou et al. 2020
, quantum benchmarking
Elben et al. 2020c
;
Zhang et al. 2020
, and entanglement detection without reference frames
Tran et al. 2015
;
Tran et al. 2016
;
Ketterer et al. 2019
;
Ketterer et al. 2020
;
Knips et al. 2020
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we adopt a global random ensemble to measure the eight-point correlator
C_{8}(t)
, which reveals scrambling earlier than
C_{4}(t)
. In Sec.
III
, we propose three complementary protocols based on shadow tomography, only requiring random Pauli measurements on individual qubits. We give analytical variance upper bounds for a sample protocol in Sec.
IV
and show the protocol is more efficient than direct tomography. In Sec.
V
we present numerical simulations for the predicted and estimated OTOC values. We give conclusions and provide an outlook in Sec.
VI
.

II
Global protocol for eight-point OTOC

We motivate our search for a novel protocol to measure higher-point correlators by first extending the four-point OTOC protocol developed in
Vermersch et al. 2019
to evaluate
C_{8}(t)
. The protocol implements global random unitaries and demonstrates new scrambling dynamics in
C_{8}(t)
.

Assume
W
and
V
are unitary, traceless, and Hermitian operators on
\mathcal{H}_{d}
. Defining operators
A_{1}=W(t)
and
A_{2}=VW(t)V
, the four-point and eight-point OTOCs are

C_{4}(t)=\langle A_{1}A_{2}\rangle,\quad C_{8}(t)=\langle A_{1}A_{2}A_{1}A_{2}\rangle.

(8)

A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are also unitary, traceless, and Hermitian operators. Let
U
be a unitary on
\mathcal{H}_{d}
randomly sampled from the Haar measure on the unitary group. Define the notation for the integral over the Haar measure as
\overline{(\cdots)}=\int_{\mathrm{Haar}}dU(\cdots)
. Define the expectation value over the pure state
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{H}_{d}
as
\langle\bm{\cdot}\rangle_{\rho_{0}}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{0}\bm{\cdot}\}
.
One can prove that (see Appendix
D
)

\langle A_{1}A_{2}\rangle=(d+1)\overline{\langle U^{\dagger}A_{1}U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}\langle U^{\dagger}A_{2}U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}},

(9)

\langle A_{1}A_{2}A_{1}A_{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(d+1)(d+2)(d+3)\overline{\langle U^{\dagger}A_{1}U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}^{2}\langle U^{\dagger}A_{2}U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}^{2}}-d\langle A_{1}A_{2}\rangle^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(d+4),

(10)

for any pure state
\rho_{0}
, even, for example,
\ket{0}^{\otimes N}
.
In terms of
W(t)
and
V
, the OTOCs are

C_{4}(t)=(d+1)\overline{\langle U^{\dagger}W(t)U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}\langle U^{\dagger}V^{\dagger}W(t)VU\rangle_{\rho_{0}}},

(11)

C_{8}(t)=\frac{1}{2}(d+1)(d+2)(d+3)\overline{\langle U^{\dagger}W(t)U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}^{2}\langle U^{\dagger}V^{\dagger}W(t)VU\rangle_{\rho_{0}}^{2}}-dC^{2}_{4}(t)-\frac{1}{2}(d+4).

(12)

Eq. (
12
) reveals
C_{8}(t)
contains dynamics not captured by
C_{4}(t)
. Its third term is constant and does not affect the dynamics. The second term depends only on
C_{4}(t)
. The ‘hidden’ dynamics arise due to the new term
\overline{\langle U^{\dagger}W(t)U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}^{2}\langle U^{\dagger}V^{\dagger}W(t)VU\rangle_{\rho_{0}}^{2}}
.

To evaluate the OTOCs, we measure
\langle U^{\dagger}W(t)U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}
and
\langle U^{\dagger}V^{\dagger}W(t)VU\rangle_{\rho_{0}}
with global random unitaries as follows.

1.

Randomly sample a unitary
U
from the Haar measure on the unitary group.

2.

Prepare pure state
\rho_{0}
and evolve with
U
. Then evolve with
U_{H}(t)
. Measure
W
.

3.

Repeat step 2 many times to compute the expectation value
\langle U^{\dagger}W(t)U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}
.

4.

Prepare
\rho_{0}
and evolve with
U
. Apply
V
, then evolve with
U_{H}(t)
. Measure
W
.

5.

Repeat step 4 many times to compute the expectation value
\langle U^{\dagger}V^{\dagger}W(t)VU\rangle_{\rho_{0}}
.

6.

Repeat steps 1-5 with many random unitaries.

We compute
\overline{\langle U^{\dagger}W(t)U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}^{2}\langle U^{\dagger}V^{\dagger}W(t)VU\rangle_{\rho_{0}}^{2}}
by calculating
\langle U^{\dagger}W(t)U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}^{2}\langle U^{\dagger}V^{\dagger}W(t)VU\rangle_{\rho_{0}}^{2}
for each
U
, then averaging.

Although a sub-ensemble can be substituted in for the Haar measure on the unitary group via unitary t-design
Gross et al. 2007
;
Roberts and Yoshida 2017
, an ensemble forming a 4-design is needed to measure
C_{8}(t)
. That is, one must apply a more random (chaotic) unitary ensemble to access higher-point scrambling features. Note that the Clifford group forms a 3-design
Webb 2015
;
Zhu et al. 2016
;
Kueng and Gross 2015
, but not a 4-design
Zhu et al. 2016
. One can generate an approximate t-design through a random local circuit
Brando et al. 2016
, in particular, by inserting few
T
gates into Clifford circuits
Haferkamp et al. 2020
. Since generating global random unitaries is experimentally challenging, Ref.
Vermersch et al. 2019
adapts its global protocol to local unitaries, greatly simplifying the measurement procedure. However, higher-point correlators are difficult to evaluate using this local protocol, due to the lack of degrees of freedom needed to construct larger permutation operators. This kind of no-go result is also observed in the measurement of the higher moments of the density matrix
Zhou et al. 2020
.

The global protocol serves as a proof of principle that higher-point OTOCs contain new scrambling dynamics not present in
C_{4}(t)
. This motivates the development of protocols based on classical shadows
Huang et al. 2020
in the following section, which only utilize single-qubit, random Clifford unitaries.

III
Classical Shadow protocols

We present three protocols to estimate
C_{4k}(t)
using classical shadows generated through quantum shadow tomography
Aaronson 2018
;
Huang et al. 2020
. We summarize the essentials of shadow tomography, but refer to
Huang et al. 2020
for further details.

III.1
Shadow Tomography

The classical shadow protocols rely on the prediction of functions of an
N
-qubit state
\rho
. First, define a random unitary as a tensor product of local unitaries,

U=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}u_{i}.

(13)

Each single-qubit unitary
u_{i}
is drawn randomly and independently from the Clifford group. Evolve
\rho
to
U\rho U^{\dagger}
. Upon measurement in the computational basis, the state collapses to
\ket{\hat{b}}\bra{\hat{b}}
by Born’s rule, where
\ket{\hat{b}}\in\{0,1\}^{\otimes N}
is an
N
-bit random variable. That is, the state is randomly measured in the Pauli basis, similar to traditional tomography. In shadow tomography, however, one is interested in estimating the properties of the state, not in reconstructing it. After measurement, the outcome is stored and processed classically. Then, we classically compute
U^{\dagger}\ket{\hat{b}}\bra{\hat{b}}U
and apply the inverted channel
\mathcal{M}^{-1}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{M}^{-1}_{i}
, where
\mathcal{M}^{-1}_{i}(\bm{\cdot})=3(\bm{\cdot})-\mathrm{Tr}(\cdot)I
and
I
is the identity on a single qubit. The result is a classical snapshot of
\rho
,

\hat{\rho}(U;\hat{b})=\mathcal{M}^{-1}(U^{\dagger}\ket{\hat{b}}\bra{\hat{b}}U).

(14)

The classical snapshot satisfies
\mathbb{E}(\hat{\rho}(U;\hat{b}))=\rho
, where
\mathbb{E}
is the average of the outcomes and of the unitaries over the local Clifford group. More formally,

\mathbb{E}(\hat{\rho}(U;\hat{b}))=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathrm{Cl(2)}^{\otimes N}}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{N}}\bra{b}U\rho U^{\dagger}\ket{b}\mathcal{M}^{-1}(U^{\dagger}\ket{b}\bra{b}U).

(15)

As a result, an observable
O
can be estimated using the snapshot through
\mathrm{Tr}\{O\hat{\rho}\}
with
\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{Tr}\{O\hat{\rho}\})=\mathrm{Tr}\{O\mathbb{E}(\hat{\rho})\}=\mathrm{Tr}\{O\rho\}
. To reduce the statistical error of the estimation, one can repeat this process to generate
K
independent classical snapshots. The
classical shadow
of
\rho
is defined as the set of these snapshots

S(\rho,K)=\Big\{\hat{\rho}^{(i)}(U_{i};\hat{b}_{i})\Big\}_{i=1}^{K}.

(16)

Each snapshot corresponds to a new measurement.
K
is referred to as the size of the shadow. The shadow can also be used to estimate nonlinear functions of
\rho
, which are encountered in our measurement protocols. For example, a second-order function
f_{2}
can be written as
f_{2}(\rho)=\mathrm{Tr}\{O^{\prime}\rho^{\otimes 2}\}
for some observable
O^{\prime}
on
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\otimes 2}
. This can be estimated using two distinct snapshots:
\mathrm{Tr}\{O^{\prime}\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\rho^{\prime}}\}
.

C_{4k}(t)
by preparing multiple Bell states. Since
C_{4k}(t)
is a function of the evolution unitary
U_{H}(t)

U_{H}(t)

Hosur et al. 2016
;
Jaffe et al. 2018

2N

III.3
Mixed state protocol

We introduce a protocol to estimate
C_{4k}(t)
requiring a measurement on only
N
qubits, by evolving the operator
V
with some corresponding initial state. Using the cyclic property of the trace, the correlator can be written as

C_{4k}(t)=\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}\left\{\left(U_{H}(t)VU_{H}^{\dagger}(t)WU_{H}(t)VU_{H}^{\dagger}(t)W\right)^{k}\right\}.

(32)

Set
W
and
V
, for example, to

W=Z\otimes I^{\otimes N-1},\quad V=I^{\otimes N-1}\otimes Z.

(33)

Writing
Z=2\ket{0}\bra{0}-I
and introducing the initial state

\rho_{in}=\frac{1}{2^{N-1}}I^{\otimes N-1}\otimes\ket{0}\bra{0},

(34)

such that
V=d\rho_{in}-I^{\otimes N}
. Defining the time-evolved state

\rho_{V}=U_{H}(t)\rho_{in}U_{H}^{\dagger}(t)

(35)

and using the expression for
V
, the correlator becomes

C_{4k}(t)=\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}\left\{\left(d^{2}\rho_{V}W\rho_{V}W-d\rho_{V}-dW\rho_{V}W+I^{\otimes N}\right)^{k}\right\}.

(36)

Shadow tomography can be used to construct an estimator for
C_{4k}(t)
for any
k
. For demonstration, we estimate the four-point and eight-point OTOCs. Evaluating Eq. (
36
) at
k=1
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}C_{4}(t)&=d\ \mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{V}W\rho_{V}W\}-1\\
&=d\ \mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{V}^{\otimes 2}W^{\otimes 2}T_{(1,2)}\}-1.\end{split}

(37)

By creating a shadow of size
K\geq 2
for state
\rho_{V}
,

S_{V}(\rho_{V},K)=\Big\{\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(i)}(U_{i};\hat{b}_{i})\Big\}_{i=1}^{K},

(38)

an unbiased estimator of
C_{4}(t)
can be constructed through the U-statistic

\hat{C}_{4}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\binom{K}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}}d\ \mathrm{Tr}\Big\{\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(i_{1})}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(i_{2})}W^{\otimes 2}T_{(1,2)}\Big\}-1.

(39)

The sum is taken over all size 2 permutations of snapshots in
S_{V}
.

Eq. (
36

k=2

C_{8}(t)=d^{3}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{V}W\rho_{V}W\rho_{V}W\rho_{V}W\}-4d^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{V}W\rho_{V}W\rho_{V}\}+4d\ \mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{V}W\rho_{V}W\}+1.

(40)

Noting
\rho_{V}^{2}=\frac{2}{d}\rho_{V}

C_{8}(t)=L_{8}(t)-4C_{4}(t)-3,

(41)

with the leading-order term

L_{8}(t)=d^{3}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{V}W\rho_{V}W\rho_{V}W\rho_{V}W\}

(42)

which determines the scrambling dynamics not captured by the four-point correlator. Using shadow
S_{V}
of size
K\geq 4
, the unbiased estimator for the leading-order term is

\hat{L}_{8}(t)=\frac{1}{4!}\binom{K}{4}^{-1}\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}\neq i_{3}\neq i_{4}}d^{3}\mathrm{Tr}\Big\{\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(i_{1})}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(i_{2})}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(i_{3})}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(i_{4})}W^{\otimes 4}T_{(1,2,3,4)}\Big\}.

(43)

The sum is over all size 4 permutations of snapshots in
S_{V}

\hat{C}_{8}(t)=\hat{L}_{8}(t)-4\hat{C}_{4}(t)-3,

(44)

where
\hat{C}_{4}(t)
is given by Eq. (
39

IV
Statistical error analysis

We analyze the statistical error for estimators due to the finite shadow size
K
. We focus on the mixed state protocol from Sec.
III.3
for its experimental practicality. We state the following results on the variance in shadow tomography:

Fact 1
.

(Proposition 3 in
Huang et al. 2020
). For a state
\rho\in\mathcal{H}_{d}
and a linear function
\mathrm{Tr}(O\rho)
, the single-shot variance
of the function obeys

\mathrm{Var}[\mathrm{Tr}\{O\hat{\rho}\}]\leq d\mathrm{Tr}(O^{2}),

(54)

where
\hat{\rho}
is a snapshot of
\rho
.

Lemma 1
.

For a state
\rho^{\otimes 2}\in\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\otimes 2}
and a nonlinear function
\mathrm{Tr}\left\{O_{2}\rho^{\otimes 2}\right\}
with observable
O_{2}=T_{(1,2)}W^{\otimes 2}
, the single-shot variance
of the function obeys

\mathrm{Var}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left\{O_{2}\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\rho}^{\prime}\right\}\right]\leq d^{3},

(55)

where
W
is any Pauli operator and
\hat{\rho},\hat{\rho}^{\prime}
are two distinct snapshots of
\rho
.

The proof of Lemma
1
is left in Appendix
F.1
. The lemma shows a significant enhancement when compared with using Fact
1
on the doubled Hilbert space, which yields
d^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\{O_{2}^{2}\}=d^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\{T_{(1,2)}^{2}\}=d^{4}
. Lemma
1
is also suitable for
O_{2}=T_{(1,2)}
, which can tighten the variance for the purity measurement performed in
Huang et al. 2020
;
Elben et al. 2020a
. The improvement relies on the prior knowledge that the target state here is in the tensor product form
\rho\otimes\rho
. We believe a modification of Lemma
1
with an appropriate
O
on a few-copy state can enhance shadow tomography in other scenarios.

IV.1
Variance of four-point OTOC

We compute the variance of
\hat{C}_{4}(t)
in Eq. (
39
). The summation index constraint
i_{1}\neq i_{2}
in
\hat{C}_{4}(t)
can be changed to
i_{1}<i_{2}
by symmetrizing the observable as follows

\displaystyle\hat{C}_{4}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{2}\binom{K}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i_{1}\neq i_{2}}\ \mathrm{Tr}\Big\{\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(i_{1})}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(i_{2})}W^{\otimes 2}T_{(1,2)}\Big\}-1

(56)

\displaystyle=d\binom{K}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}}\ \mathrm{Tr}\left\{\frac{W^{\otimes 2}T_{(1,2)}+T_{(1,2)}W^{\otimes 2}}{2}\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(i_{1})}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(i_{2})}\right\}-1

\displaystyle=d\binom{K}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}}\ \mathrm{Tr}\left\{T_{(1,2)}W^{\otimes 2}\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(i_{1})}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(i_{2})}\right\}-1

\displaystyle=d\binom{K}{2}^{-1}\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}}\hat{D}_{4}(i_{1},i_{2})-1.

Define
\hat{D}_{4}(i_{1},i_{2})=\mathrm{Tr}\left\{T_{(1,2)}W^{\otimes 2}\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(i_{1})}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(i_{2})}\right\}
. The third line is due to
T_{(1,2)}
commuting with
W^{\otimes 2}
.
To simplify notation, we suppress all time dependence in this section. The variance of
\hat{C}_{4}
satisfies

\mathrm{Var}(\hat{C}_{4})=\left[d\binom{K}{2}^{-1}\right]^{2}\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}}\hat{D}_{4}(i_{1},i_{2})\right),

(57)

with

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left(\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}}\hat{D}_{4}(i_{1},i_{2})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}}\hat{D}_{4}(i_{1},i_{2})\right)^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}}\hat{D}_{4}(i_{1},i_{2})\right]^{2}

(58)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}<i_{2}\\
j_{1}<j_{2}\end{subarray}}\left\{\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{D}_{4}(i_{1},i_{2})\hat{D}_{4}(j_{1},j_{2})\right]-D_{4}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}<i_{2}\\
j_{1}<j_{2}\end{subarray}}\left[V_{4}(i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2})-D_{4}^{2}\right].

Define
D_{4}=\mathbb{E}[\hat{D}_{4}(i_{1},i_{2})]
for any
i_{1},i_{2}
and
V_{4}(i_{1},i_{2},j_{1},j_{2})=\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{D}_{4}(i_{1},i_{2})\hat{D}_{4}(j_{1},j_{2})\right]
.
V_{4}
depends on the coincidences between indices
i_{1},i_{2}
and
j_{1},j_{2}
, respectively. A coincidence indicates that the two
\hat{D}_{4}
in the second line of Eq. (
58
) share the same snapshot, and thus are not independent. There are three possible coincidence cases of the indices discussed as follows:

•

No coincidence:
V_{4}=\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{D}_{4}(i_{1},i_{2})\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{D}_{4}(j_{1},j_{2})\right]=D_{4}^{2}
since all snapshots are independent.

•

One coincidence: take for example
i_{1}\neq j_{1}
and
i_{2}=j_{2}
. There are a total of
2\binom{K}{1}\binom{K-1}{2}
such terms. We simplify

\displaystyle V_{4}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left\{T_{(1,2)}W^{\otimes 2}\hat{\rho}^{(i_{1})}_{V}\otimes\hat{\rho}^{(i_{2})}_{V}\right\}\mathrm{Tr}\left\{T_{(1,2)}W^{\otimes 2}\hat{\rho}^{(j_{1})}_{V}\otimes\hat{\rho}^{(i_{2})}_{V}\right\}\right]

(59)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left\{T_{(1,2)}W^{\otimes 2}\rho_{V}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{V}\right\}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left\{W\rho_{V}W\hat{\rho}_{V}\right\}^{2}\right].

In the second line, we take the expectation of
\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(i_{1})}
and
\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(j_{1})}
, then denote
\hat{\rho}_{V}^{(i_{2})}
as
\hat{\rho}_{V}
without ambiguity.
Set
O_{1}=W\rho_{V}W
so that

VI
Conclusion

We present a definition of higher-point out-of-time-ordered correlators to describe the dynamics of quantum scrambling in chaotic systems. In the early-time limit, higher-point correlators exhibit faster decay and reveal information delocalization earlier than the four-point OTOC. We present protocols using classical shadows to estimate these correlators and show they can outperform full quantum state tomography. For sufficiently many measurements, good agreement between the predicted and estimated values can be achieved. The protocols avoid time reversal and can probe the dynamics of OTOCs at any time. They can be implemented using single-qubit Pauli measurements, making them ideal for experiments with single-qubit control. The protocols here extract nonlinear functions by measuring only a single-copy of a target state, thus avoiding the preparation of multiple identical copies and the joint control and readout on them. In addition, the same classical shadow can be used to compute multiple correlators by classical post-processing. Furthermore, the protocols can be used to construct estimators for general correlators over an arbitrary initial state
\langle(W(t)VW(t)V)^{k}\rangle_{\rho}
, allowing for the study of OTOCs beyond the thermal state background.

There are a few interesting points which merit further investigation. First, our protocols can be directly extended to the noisy evolution scenario, where the system dynamics are described by general quantum channels. Adopting noise mitigation methods
Chen et al. 2020
;
Koh and Grewal 2020
may be an intriguing approach to distinguish quantum scrambling from classical decoherence effects
Swingle and Yunger Halpern 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Kudler-Flam et al. 2020
. Second, the enhancement of the variance analysis based on prior knowledge of the state’s tensor structure can be generalized to other scenarios, which can further improve the practicality of shadow tomography for nonlinear functions.
Third, it is possible to extend the protocols to measure other quantities in quantum scrambling, such as the operator weight distribution
Roberts et al. 2018
;
Qi et al. 2019
. Finally, it is important to explore the operational and physical implications of higher-point OTOCs, and demonstrate our protocols on near-term quantum platforms.

Appendix A
Interpretation of higher-point OTOCs

We propose a physical interpretation of higher-point OTOCs. For simplicity, we approximate the expectation value over the maximally mixed state as one over a Haar random state
\ket{\psi_{h}}

Luitz and Bar Lev 2017
;
Dağ et al. 2020
. The approximate four-point OTOC is

\tilde{C}_{4}(t)=\bra{\psi_{h}}W^{\dagger}(t)V^{\dagger}W(t)V\ket{\psi_{h}}=\bra{\psi_{h}}U_{H}^{\dagger}W^{\dagger}U_{H}V^{\dagger}U^{\dagger}_{H}WU_{H}V\ket{\psi_{h}}.

(67)

In analogy to the Loschmidt echo
Gorin et al. 2006
;
Yan et al. 2020
, define the perturbed evolution unitary as
U_{p}(t)=U_{H}(t)V
. The state
U_{p}(t)\ket{\psi_{h}}
represents a small ‘kick’,
V
, perturbing
\ket{\psi_{h}}
before evolution by
U_{H}(t)
. The approximate OTOC in terms of
U_{p}(t)
is

\tilde{C}_{4}(t)=\bra{\psi_{h}}U_{H}^{\dagger}W^{\dagger}U_{H}U^{\dagger}_{p}WU_{p}\ket{\psi_{h}}.

(68)

The state
\ket{\psi_{1}}=U_{H}^{\dagger}W^{\dagger}U_{H}U^{\dagger}_{p}WU_{p}\ket{\psi_{h}}
is interpreted through following perturbation procedure (see Fig.
7
). Evolve
\ket{\psi_{h}}
by
U_{p}(t)
, then apply
W
. Evolve the state using
U_{p}^{\dagger}(t)
, which corresponds to backwards time evolution by
U^{\dagger}_{H}(t)=U_{H}(-t)
followed by
V^{\dagger}
. Evolve with
U_{H}(t)
, apply
W^{\dagger}
, and finally evolve backwards in time using
U^{\dagger}_{H}(t)
. The correlator is the overlap between the initial state
\ket{\psi_{h}}
and the evolved state
\ket{\psi_{1}}
. The role of
W
is to perturb the state and ensure
U_{p}(t)
is not undone by
U_{p}^{\dagger}(t)
.
V
probes how chaotic
U_{H}(t)
is. In the case where
V
is the identity,
U_{p}(t)=U_{H}(t)
and the evolution procedure evolves
\ket{\psi_{h}}
away from and back to itself. No chaos is detected. For a nontrivial
V
, the perturbation procedure evolves the state away from
\ket{\psi_{h}}
.
\tilde{C}_{4}(t)
indicates the sensitivity of the evolution of
U_{H}(t)
to perturbations.

Figure 7:
Schematic of a single perturbed evolution procedure evolving
\ket{\psi_{h}}
to
\ket{\psi_{1}}
.

Consider the approximate
4k
-point OTOC,

\tilde{C}_{4k}(t)=\bra{\psi_{h}}(U_{H}^{\dagger}W^{\dagger}U_{H}U^{\dagger}_{p}WU_{p})^{k}\ket{\psi_{h}}.

(69)

This correlator gives the overlap of
\ket{\psi_{h}}
with the state resulting from
k
applications of the perturbation procedure to
\ket{\psi_{h}}
. Each application evolves the state successively further from
\ket{\psi_{h}}
. The higher-point correlators correspond to a larger number of perturbations to the initial state, which probe the chaos induced by
U_{H}(t)
in finer detail.

We return to the discussion of exact OTOCs. A higher-point OTOC
C_{4k}(t)
reveals the finer scrambling dynamics due to
k
perturbations by
V
. After each perturbation, evolution by
U_{H}(t)
further delocalizes the quantum information of the initially local operator
W
. Higher-point correlators therefore scramble information more quickly, resulting in a faster OTOC decay. In a different context, an interpretation of higher-point correlators is given in terms of multiple shock waves
Shenker and Stanford 2014b
.

Appendix C
Background on random matrix theory

This section relates permutation operators to random matrices, which allow for the computation of OTOCs through random measurements. For a more complete discussion, see
Roberts and Yoshida 2017
;
Collins and Śniady 2006
. Let
U
be a unitary on Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{d}
. Define the operator

A=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{k}A_{i},

(82)

where
A_{i}
is an operator on
\mathcal{H}_{d}
. By the Schur-Weyl duality, if
[A,U^{\otimes k}]=0
for all unitaries
U
, then
A
can be written as a linear combination of permutations operators
T_{\pi}
,

A=\sum_{\pi\in S_{k}}c_{\pi}T_{\pi}.

(83)

The sum is carried out over
S_{k}
, the set of all permutations of the set
\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
. Define the
k
-fold twirling channel of
A
as

\Phi_{\mathrm{Haar}}^{(k)}(A)=\int_{\mathrm{Haar}}(U^{\dagger\otimes k})AU^{\otimes k}dU.

(84)

The integral is over the Haar measure
dU
on the unitary group. As the Haar measure is invariant under the action of the group, we write the twirling channel as a superposition of permutation operators

\Phi_{\mathrm{Haar}}^{(k)}(A)=\sum_{\pi,\sigma\in S_{k}}C_{\pi,\sigma}T_{\pi}\mathrm{Tr}\{T_{\sigma}A\},

(85)

where the coefficients
C_{\pi,\sigma}
are known as the Weingarten matrix. They satisfy

(C^{-1})_{\pi,\sigma}=d^{f(\pi\circ\sigma)}.

(86)

where
f(\pi\circ\sigma)
is the number of cycles of the composition of permutations
\pi
and
\sigma
.

Fact 2
.

Let
\{A_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
be a set of traceless operators
A_{i}
on Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{d}
of dimension
d
. Let
T_{\sigma}
be a permutation operator on
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\otimes k}
and let
U
be a Haar random unitary on
\mathcal{H}_{d}
. Then

\sum_{\sigma\in D_{k}}\mathrm{Tr}\{T_{\sigma}A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k}\}=\frac{(d-1+k)!}{(d-1)!}\overline{\langle U^{\dagger}A_{1}U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}\cdots\langle U^{\dagger}A_{k}U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}}.

(87)

The sum is carried out over
D_{k}
, the set of derangements (permutations with no fixed points) in
S_{k}
. The notation

\langle\bm{\cdot}\rangle_{\rho_{0}}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{0}\ \bm{\cdot}\}
denotes the expectation value over a pure state
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{H}_{d}
. The notation
\overline{(\cdots)}
denotes an integral over the Haar measure on the unitary group:
\overline{(\cdots)}=\int_{\mathrm{Haar}}(\cdots)dU
.

Proof.

Consider the k-fold twirling channel on
\otimes_{i=1}^{k}A_{i}
,

\displaystyle\Phi^{(k)}_{\mathrm{Haar}}(A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k})=\int_{\mathrm{Haar}}dU(U^{\dagger\otimes k})(A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k})(U^{\otimes k}).

(88)

Introducing pure state
\rho_{0}
, we compute the following trace

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{0}^{\otimes k}\Phi^{(k)}_{\mathrm{Haar}}(A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k})\}=\overline{\langle U^{\dagger}A_{1}U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}\cdots\langle U^{\dagger}A_{k}U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}}.

(89)

Using Eq. (
85
),

\Phi^{(k)}_{\mathrm{Haar}}(A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k})=\sum_{\pi,\sigma\in S_{k}}C_{\pi,\sigma}T_{\pi}\mathrm{Tr}\{T_{\sigma}A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k}\},

(90)

so

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{0}^{\otimes k}\Phi^{(k)}_{\mathrm{Haar}}(A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k})\}=\sum_{\pi,\sigma\in S_{k}}C_{\pi,\sigma}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{0}^{\otimes k}T_{\pi}\}\mathrm{Tr}\{T_{\sigma}A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k}\}.

(91)

For any
\pi
, we can write

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{0}^{\otimes k}T_{\pi}\}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{0}^{k}\}^{n_{k}}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{0}^{k-1}\}^{n_{k-1}}\cdots\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{0}\}^{n_{1}},

(92)

where
n_{1},...,n_{k}
are some integers. Since
\rho_{0}
is a pure state,
\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{0}^{m}\}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{0}\}=1
for any positive integer
m
. This yields
\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{0}^{\otimes k}T_{\pi}\}=1
.
Our expression for the trace then becomes

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{0}^{\otimes k}\Phi^{(k)}_{\mathrm{Haar}}(A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k})\}=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}\mathrm{Tr}\{T_{\sigma}A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k}\}\sum_{\pi\in S_{k}}C_{\pi,\sigma}.

(93)

It can be shown that the Weingarten matrix satisfies

\sum_{\pi\in S_{k}}C_{\pi,\sigma}=\frac{(d-1)!}{(d-1+k)!}.

(94)

The trace then becomes

\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{0}^{\otimes k}\Phi^{(k)}_{\mathrm{Haar}}(A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k})\}=\frac{(d-1)!}{(d-1+k)!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}\mathrm{Tr}\{T_{\sigma}A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k}\}.

(95)

We now have two expressions for
\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{0}^{\otimes k}\Phi^{(k)}_{\mathrm{Haar}}(A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k})\}
. Together, they yield

\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}\mathrm{Tr}\{T_{\sigma}A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k}\}=\frac{(d-1+k)!}{(d-1)!}\overline{\langle U^{\dagger}A_{1}U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}\cdots\langle U^{\dagger}A_{k}U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}}.

(96)

We have not yet used the traceless property of
A_{i}
, so the above equation is valid even for an
A_{i}
with a non-vanishing trace.
We can split the sum over
S_{k}
into two sums

\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}\mathrm{Tr}\{T_{\sigma}A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k}\}=\sum_{\sigma\in D_{k}}\mathrm{Tr}\{T_{\sigma}A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k}\}+\sum_{\sigma\in F_{k}}\mathrm{Tr}\{T_{\sigma}A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k}\}.

(97)

D_{k}
is the set of derangements and
F_{k}
is the set of permutations in
S_{k}
containing at least one fixed point. For
\sigma\in F_{k}
, the trace
\mathrm{Tr}\{T_{\sigma}A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k}\}
introduces at least one trace of the form
\mathrm{Tr}\{A_{i}\}
, which vanishes since all
A_{i}
are traceless. The sum over
F_{k}
then vanishes and we may write

\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}\mathrm{Tr}\{T_{\sigma}A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k}\}=\sum_{\sigma\in D_{k}}\mathrm{Tr}\{T_{\sigma}A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k}\}.

(98)

This yields

\sum_{\sigma\in D_{k}}\mathrm{Tr}\{T_{\sigma}A_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes A_{k}\}=\frac{(d-1+k)!}{(d-1)!}\overline{\langle U^{\dagger}A_{1}U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}\cdots\langle U^{\dagger}A_{k}U\rangle_{\rho_{0}}},

(99)

proving the fact.
∎

and
\langle A_{1}A_{2}\rangle
(where all

are traceless, unitary, and Hermitian) in terms of simpler correlators that can be measured experimentally. This is done by introducing random unitaries.

Appendix E
OTOCs at late times

We calculate the average OTOC values where the evolution unitary is drawn randomly from the Haar measure on the unitary group. Since the Haar random unitary ensemble can be used in place of large-time chaotic evolution, these values serve as a benchmark for the evolution due to physical, chaotic
Hamiltonians. Consider the
4k
-point OTOC

C_{4k}(t)=\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}\{(W^{\dagger}(t)V^{\dagger}W(t)V)^{k}\},

(112)

and take
W
and
V
as Pauli operators. For instance,
W=Z_{1}X_{2}
and
V=Z_{N-1}Z_{N}
. Due to the random feature of the Haar measure, the specific choice of Pauli operators does not affect the result. Based on the Hermitian property of
W
and
V
, the average OTOC can be written as

\displaystyle\overline{C_{4k}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathrm{Haar}}\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}\{[U^{{\dagger}}WUV]^{2k}\}dU

(113)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}\Big\{\int_{\mathrm{Haar}}[U^{{\dagger}}WU]^{\otimes 2k}dU\ V^{\otimes 2k}T_{\sigma_{0}}\Big\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}\left\{\Phi^{(2k)}_{\mathrm{Haar}}(W^{\otimes 2k})V^{\otimes 2k}T_{\sigma_{0}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{\pi,\sigma\in S_{2k}}C_{\pi,\sigma}\mathrm{Tr}\left\{T_{\pi}W^{\otimes 2k}\right\}\mathrm{Tr}\left\{V^{\otimes 2k}T_{\sigma^{\prime}}\right\},

where in the second line we write the equation over
2k
copies of the original Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{d}
and move the integral inside the trace. In the final line we apply the Weingarten formula. We also use the permutations
\sigma_{0}=(1,2,\ldots,2k)
and
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma_{0}\circ\sigma
. Since any Pauli operator
W
satisfies
W^{2}=I^{\otimes N}
and
\mathrm{Tr}\left\{W\right\}=0
, the
term
\mathrm{Tr}\left\{T_{\pi}W^{\otimes 2k}\right\}
simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\left\{T_{\pi}W^{\otimes 2k}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\ \ \ \mathrm{,\ if\ }\pi\ \mathrm{has\ an\ odd\ cycle}

(114)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d^{f(\pi)}\ \mathrm{,\ if\ }\pi\ \mathrm{has\ no\ odd\ cycles}

where
f(\pi)
is the number of cycles of the permutation
\pi
. For example,
(12)(34)
and
(123)(4)
both contain two cycles, but both cycles are odd in the latter case.
This relation also holds for
\mathrm{Tr}\left\{V^{\otimes 2k}T_{\sigma^{\prime}}\right\}
. As a result, Eq. (
113
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\overline{C_{4k}}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\pi,\sigma\in S_{2k},\\
\pi,\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma_{0}\circ\sigma\mathrm{\ even\ cycles}\end{subarray}}C_{\pi,\sigma}d^{f(\pi)+f(\sigma^{\prime})}.

(115)

A more general formula is derived in Ref.
Roberts and Yoshida 2017
.

E.1
Four-point OTOC

We first compute
\overline{C}_{4}
where
k=1
. Noting
S_{2}=\{(1)(2),(1,2)\}
, the Weingarten matrix in this case is

C_{\pi,\sigma}=\frac{1}{d^{2}-1}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&-\frac{1}{d}\\
-\frac{1}{d}&1\end{array}\right).

(116)

We take
\sigma_{0}=(1,2)
. The sum in Eq. (
115
), only allows
\pi=(1,2)
and
\sigma=(1)(2)
. As a result, the four-point OTOC in the large-time limit satisfies

\begin{aligned} \overline{C_{4}}=\frac{1}{d}\frac{1}{d^{2}-1}\Big(-\frac{1}{d}\Big)d^{2}=-\frac{1}{d^{2}-1}\end{aligned}.

(117)

E.2
Eight-point OTOC and (non)commutator types

Reference
Roberts and Yoshida 2017
has found that there are two general definitions of eight-point OTOCs that display distinct large-time behavior. For operators
A,B,C,D
, one can define the commutator type correlator as
C_{ct}=\langle\tilde{A}B\tilde{C}D\tilde{A}D\tilde{C}B\rangle
and the non-commutator type correlator as
C_{nc}=\langle\tilde{A}B\tilde{C}D\tilde{A}B\tilde{C}D\rangle
. Define
\tilde{A}=U^{{\dagger}}AU
, where
U
is a Haar random unitary. The non-commutator type correlator attains a large-time value which scales as
\sim\frac{1}{d^{2}}
. This occurs even if
U
is sampled from the Clifford ensemble. The four-point OTOC also scales as
\sim\frac{1}{d^{2}}
, indicating that non-commutator type correlators saturate the same floor value as the four-point correlator and therefore cannot reveal any new scrambling information in the large-time limit. The correlator
C_{8}(t)=\langle W(t)VW(t)VW(t)VW(t)V\rangle
defined in our work is a non-commutator type correlator. Thus, it can only be used to study early-time scrambling dynamics.

The commutator type correlator scales as
\sim\frac{1}{d^{4}}
at long times, allowing us to probe systems which display higher-point scrambling in the large-time limit. By taking
A=B
and
C=D
, the commutator type correlator can be written as

\begin{aligned} C_{ct}=\langle\tilde{A}A\tilde{C}C\tilde{A}C\tilde{C}A\rangle\end{aligned}.

(118)

We can adapt our measurement protocols to estimate this correlator.

F.1
Proof of Lemma
1

Proof.

We begin the proof by first taking
O_{2}=T_{(1,2)}
, and later show that it can be used to bound the original case
O_{2}=T_{(1,2)}W^{\otimes 2}
. The swap operator
T_{(1,2)}
on
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\otimes 2}
can be decomposed in the Pauli basis

T_{(1,2)}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i}P_{i}\otimes P_{i},

(127)

where
P_{i}
is a Pauli operator and the sum is carried out over the
N
-qubit Pauli group. Following the variance analysis for the shadow norm (see Eq. (S53) in Ref.
Huang et al. 2020
), the variance can be upper bounded by

\displaystyle\max_{\sigma=\rho^{\otimes 2}}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathrm{Cl}(2)^{\otimes{2N}}}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{2N}}\bra{b}U\sigma U^{{\dagger}}\ket{b}\bra{b}U(\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{\frac{1}{3}})^{\otimes 2N}(T_{(1,2)})U^{{\dagger}}\ket{b}^{2}

(128)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\sigma=\rho^{\otimes 2}}\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathrm{Cl}(2)^{\otimes{2N}}}\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^{2N}}\bra{b}U\sigma U^{{\dagger}}\ket{b}\bra{b}U(\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{\frac{1}{3}})^{\otimes 2N}(P_{i}^{\otimes 2})U^{{\dagger}}\ket{b}\bra{b}U(\mathcal{D}^{-1}_{\frac{1}{3}})^{\otimes 2N}(P_{j}^{\otimes 2})U^{{\dagger}}\ket{b}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\sigma=\rho^{\otimes 2}}\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i,j}f(i,j)^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\{\sigma P_{i}^{\otimes 2}P_{j}^{\otimes 2}\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\rho}\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i,j}f(i,j)^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho P_{i}P_{j}\}^{2},

where in the second line we insert the decomposition of the swap operator, and
f(i,j)
is a function for two Pauli operators defined in Lemma 4 in Ref.
Huang et al. 2020
.
f(i,j)=0
if there exists a qubit index
k
such that the k-th qubit operators
P_{i}^{k}\neq P_{j}^{k}
and
P_{i}^{k},P_{j}^{k}\neq I
. Otherwise,
f(i,j)=3^{s}
, where s is the number of non-identity Pauli indices that match.
We remark that, different than the shadow norm, our maximization is on the subset of the states in
\mathcal{H}_{d}^{\otimes 2}
with a 2-copy structure, i.e. states of the form
\sigma=\rho^{\otimes 2}
. This tensor structure leads to the tighter bound for the variance.

We introduce three functions denoted by
a,b,c
for the two
N
-qubit Pauli operators
P_{i},P_{j}
indexed by
i,j
.
a(i,j)
denotes the qubit positions for which
P_{i},P_{j}
share the same single-qubit Pauli operator;
b(i,j)
denotes the positions where
P_{i}
has a single-qubit Pauli operator and
P_{j}
has
I
(or vice versa);
c(i,j)
denotes the positions where
P_{i},P_{j}
both have
I
.
For example, for a 6-qubit system with
P_{i}=X_{1}Y_{2}X_{3}I_{4}I_{5}I_{6}
and
P_{j}=X_{1}Y_{2}I_{3}Z_{4}I_{5}I_{6}
, the functions are:
a(i,j)=\{1,2\}
,
b(i,j)=\{3,4\}
and
c(i,j)=\{5,6\}
. We use
|a(i,j)|
to denote the number of elements in
a(i,j)
and omit the
i,j
indices when there is no ambiguity.
P^{a}_{i}
is the
|a|
-qubit operator restricted on subsystem
a
from
P_{i}
.

One needs not to consider the
P_{i},P_{j}
pair with different Pauli operators acting on the same qubit, since they return
f(i,j)=0
. As a result, the summation in Eq. (
128
) can be transformed to the summation on all possible
a,b,c
with
|a|+|b|+|c|=n
. We use
(i,j)\vdash(a,b,c)
to denote that
P_{i},P_{j}
satisfy the operator constraints on the given
a,b,c
subsystems. We write

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}f(i,j)^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho P_{i}P_{j}\}^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{a,b,c}3^{2|a|}\sum_{(i,j)\vdash(a,b,c)}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho I_{a}\otimes P_{i}^{b}P_{j}^{b}\otimes I_{c}\}^{2}

(129)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a,b,c}3^{2|a|}\sum_{(i,j)\vdash(a,b,c)}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{b}P_{i}^{b}P_{j}^{b}\}^{2}.

Here
3^{2|a|}
accounts for the function
f(i,j)^{2}
, and we use the fact that the qubit Pauli operators of
P_{i},P_{j}
are the same in
a
and they both have
I
on
c
.

There are
3^{|a|}
possible choices of Paulis
\{X,Y,Z\}^{|a|}
on subsystem
a
.
P^{i}_{b}P^{j}_{b}
can take all Pauli operators
\{X,Y,Z\}^{|b|}
on
b
by the property of the
b
function. For each choice of operators on
a
, we can sum the result of all of these possible Paulis on
b
. One thus has

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\vdash(a,b,c)}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{b}P^{i}_{b}P^{j}_{b}\}^{2}

\displaystyle=3^{|a|}2^{|b|}\sum_{\tilde{P_{b}}\in\{X,Y,Z\}^{|b|}}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{b}\tilde{P_{b}}\}^{2}

(130)

\displaystyle\leq 3^{|a|}2^{|b|}\sum_{\tilde{P_{b}}\in\{\mathbb{I},X,Y,Z\}^{|b|}}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{b}\tilde{P_{b}}\}^{2}

\displaystyle=3^{|a|}2^{|b|}2^{|b|}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{b}^{2}\}

\displaystyle\leq 3^{|a|}2^{2|b|}.

The
2^{|b|}
in the first line is due to the two possibilities for
P_{i}
or
P_{j}
taking a Pauli or the identity on the qubit in
b
. The last inequality is due to the purity
\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{b}^{2}\}\leq 1
.
Inserting Eq. (
130
) into Eq. (
129
), we get the upper bound for the variance

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i,j}f(i,j)^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho P_{i}P_{j}\}^{2}

(131)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{a,b,c}3^{2|a|}\sum_{(i,j)\vdash(a,b,c)}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho_{b}P^{i}_{b}P^{j}_{b}\}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{|a|+|b|+|c|=N}\binom{N}{|a|}\binom{N-|a|}{|b|}\binom{N-|a|-|b|}{|c|}3^{3|a|}2^{2|b|}=\frac{(3^{3}+2^{2}+1)^{N}}{d^{2}}=d^{3}.

For the case
O_{2}=T_{(1,2)}W^{\otimes 2}
with
W
a Pauli operator, as in Eq. (
129
) one has that the variance is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\left[\mathrm{Tr}\left\{O_{2}\hat{\rho}\otimes\hat{\rho}^{\prime}\right\}\right]\leq

\displaystyle\max_{\sigma=\rho^{\otimes 2}}\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i,j}f(i,j)^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\{\sigma P_{i}^{\otimes 2}W^{\otimes 2}P_{j}^{\otimes 2}W^{\otimes 2}\}

(132)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\rho}\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i,j}f(i,j)^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho P_{i}WP_{j}W\}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{\rho}\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i,j}f(i,j)^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho P_{i}P_{j}\}^{2}\leq d^{3},

where we use the fact
\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho P_{i}WP_{j}W\}^{2}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\rho P_{i}P_{j}\}^{2}
for any Pauli operator.

∎
