A strong composition theorem for junta complexity and the boosting of property testers
Guy Blanc, Caleb Koch, Carmen Strassle, Li-Yang Tan

Abstract
We prove a strong composition theorem for junta complexity and show how such theorems can be used to generically boost the performance of property testers. The ε \varepsilon -approximate junta complexity of a function f f is the smallest integer r r such that f f is ε \varepsilon -close to a function that depends only on r r variables. A strong composition theorem states that if f f has large ε \varepsilon -approximate junta complexity, then g ∘ f g\circ f has even larger ε ​ ’ \varepsilon’ -approximate junta complexity, even for ε ​ ’ ≫ ε \varepsilon’\gg\varepsilon . We develop a fairly complete understanding of this behavior, proving that the junta complexity of g ∘ f g\circ f is characterized by that of f f along with the multivariate noise sensitivity of g g . For the important case of symmetric functions g g , we relate their multivariate noise sensitivity to the simpler and well-studied case of univariate noise sensitivity. We then show how strong composition theorems yield boosting algorithms for property testers: with a strong composition theorem for any class of functions, a large-distance tester for that class is immediately upgraded into one for small distances. Combining our contributions yields a booster for junta testers, and with it new implications for junta testing. This is the first boosting-type result in property testing, and we hope that the connection to composition theorems adds compelling motivation to the study of both topics.

Our results and techniques

2.1
First main result: A strong composition theorem for junta complexity

Composition theorems are statements about
hardness amplification
: the goal is to understand the extent to which the disjoint composition
(g\circ f)(x)\coloneqq g(f(x^{(1)}),\ldots,f(x^{(k)}))
is more complex than
f
itself, and how this depends on intrinsic properties of the combining function
g
. For approximate measures such has junta complexity, we are furthermore interested in
strong
composition theorems, statements of the form:

J_{\mathcal{D}^{k}}(g\circ f,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{large}})\gg J_{\mathcal{D}}(f,\varepsilon_{\mathrm{small}})\ \ \text{even for}\ \ \varepsilon_{\mathrm{large}}\gg\varepsilon_{\mathrm{small}}.

(
\diamondsuit
)

In words, the composed function requires much more resources—in our case, much larger junta approximators—even if one only seeks a much coarser approximation. Strong composition theorems stand in contrast to weak ones that only amplify hardness with respect to one of the two parameters, either resources or approximation quality only. The canonical example in this context is Yao’s XOR lemma
[
Yao82
]
, which says that if
f
is mildly hard to approximate with size-
s
circuits, then
\mathrm{XOR}\circ f
is extremely hard to approximate with size-
s’
circuits. A long-recognized downside of this important result, inherent to all known proofs of it
[
Lev85
,
GNW11
,
Imp95
,
IW97
]
and its generalizations to arbitrary combining functions
[
O’D02
]
, is the fact that it is only known to hold for
s’\ll s
, whereas intuitively it should hold even for
s’\gg s
.

Composition theorems, both weak and strong, have been studied for a variety of complexity measures
but appear to have been underexplored for junta complexity. One reason may be that the question appears deceptively simple. Indeed, things
are
completely straightforward in the zero-error setting, where we have the intuitive identity
J(g\circ f,0)=J(g,0)\cdot J(f,0)
. However, we show that the question becomes surprisingly intricate once error is allowed.

2.1.1
Context and motivation: Counterexamples to natural composition theorems

The question proves to be tricky even in the special case where the combining function
g
is symmetric. We now state a sequence of three seemingly intuitive conjectures for this special case. While false, these conjectures and their counterexamples will motivate and lead us to the statement of our actual composition theorem. (Details and proofs of the counterexamples discussed in this section are given in
Appendix

A
.)

The following notation will be useful for us throughout this paper:

Notation.

For a function
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
, distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, and integer
r
, we write
\tilde{f}_{\mathcal{D},r}
to denote the best
r
-junta approximator of
f
with respect to
\mathcal{D}
. When
\mathcal{D}
is clear from context, we simply write
\tilde{f}_{r}
.

Conjecture 1.

It will be convenient for us to consider composition theorems in their contrapositive form. Suppose we would like to approximate
g\circ f
with an
R
-junta, say with respect to the uniform distribution. If
g
is a
k
-variable symmetric function, how would we go about constructing an approximator that achieves the highest accuracy possible? Since
g
is symmetric, one may be inclined to divide the “junta budget” of
R
evenly among the
k
inner functions and conjecture that

g\circ\tilde{f}_{R/k}=g(\tilde{f}_{R/k},\ldots,\tilde{f}_{R/k})

achieves the best, or close to the best, accuracy among all
R
-junta approximators.

However, this is badly false. Let
g
be the
k
-variable Majority function and
f
the
n
-variable Parity function. For any choice of
R
satisfying
R/k<n
(i.e. each inner Parity receiving a budget that falls short of its arity), we have
\mathrm{Pr}[g\circ\tilde{f}_{R/k}\neq g\circ f]=\frac{1}{2}.
This is because it is “all or nothing” when it comes to approximating Parity: no (
n-1
)-junta can achieve accuracy better than that of a constant approximator. The best strategy is therefore to allocate a full budget of
n
to as many of the inner Parities as possible (i.e.
R/n
many of them), and a budget of zero to the others. This shows a gap of
\frac{1}{2}
versus
1-o(1)
in the accuracies of the “divide budget equally” strategy and the optimal one.

Conjecture 2.

In light of this counterexample, one may then conjecture that the best strategy is to partition the junta budget optimally among the
k
inner functions and feed the respective approximators of
f
into
g
. That is, the conjecture is that the best approximator is of the form:

g(\tilde{f}_{r_{1}},\ldots,\tilde{f}_{r_{k}})\text{ where }\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}=R.

While this is true for our example above, it is again badly false in general. In fact, the error of such an approximator can be close to
1
, even worse than the trivial bound of
\leq\frac{1}{2}
achievable with a constant approximator.

Our counterexample reveals another counterintuitive aspect of the overall problem. Consider an approximator for
g\circ f
of the form
g(\tilde{f}_{r_{1}},\ldots,\tilde{f}_{r_{k}}).
We show its approximation accuracy can
increase
if we replace one of the inner approximators for
f
with a
worse
one: e.g. if we replace
\tilde{f}_{r_{1}}
with
\tilde{f}_{{r_{1}}’}
where
{r_{1}}’<r_{1}
. In more technical terms that we will soon define: while the noise stability of a function is, as one would expect, monotone in the noise rate, we show that the natural generalization of it where the corruption probabilities of
0
’s and
1
’s are decoupled (defined in
Section

6.1
) is
not
monotone.

Conjecture 3.

Finally, we consider a conjecture that is far laxer than either of the previous ones. It simply states that the optimal approximator for the composed function
g\circ f
is one of
composed form
:

h(q^{(1)},\ldots,q^{(k)})\text{ for some $h:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}$ and $q^{(1)},\ldots,q^{(k)}:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}$,}

where the relevant variables of
q^{(i)}
fall within the
i
th block of variables.
We show (to our own surprise) that this conjecture is
still
false: there are composed functions for which the optimal approximator is not of composed form. However, unlike the first two conjectures, our work shows that this conjecture is morally true in a precise sense.

2.1.2
Our Strong Composition Theorem

Our strong composition theorem implies a close quantitative relationship between the error of the optimal approximator and that of the optimal composed form approximator, and indeed one with a specific structure that we call
canonical
:

Definition 1

(Canonical composed form approximators)
.

We say that a composed form approximator for
g\circ f
is
canonical
if it is of the form:

h(\tilde{f}_{r_{1}},\ldots,\tilde{f}_{r_{k}}),

where
h:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
is the function:

h(y)=\mathrm{sign}\bigg(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}^{k}}\Big[(g\circ f)(\bm{x})\mid\text{$y_{i}=\tilde{f}_{r_{i}}(\bm{x}^{(i)})$ for all $i\in[k]$}\Big]\bigg).

For intuition regarding the choice of
h
, we note that for the fixed
k
-tuple of functions
\tilde{f}_{r_{1}},\ldots,\tilde{f}_{r_{k}}
, it is the combining function that minimizes error with respect to
g\circ f
.
Canonical composed form approximators are therefore ones whose individual components are “locally” optimal: each
\tilde{f}_{r_{i}}
is the optimal
r_{i}
-junta approximator for
f
, and
h
the optimal way of combining the
f_{r_{i}}
’s. Our strong composition theorem will say that we can get very close to the
globally
optimal approximator this way.

The notion of
noise stability
is central to our work:

Definition 2

(Multivariate noise stability)
.

For any
\mu\in(-1,1)
and vector
\vec{\rho}\in[0,1]^{k}
, we define the multivariate noise stability of
g
as

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(g)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[g(\bm{y})g(\bm{z})]

where independently for each
i\in[k]
, we draw
(\bm{y}_{i}
,
\bm{z}_{i})
as follows: Using
\pi_{\mu}
to denote the unique distribution supported on
\{\pm 1\}
with mean
\mu
,
\bm{y}_{i}\sim\pi_{\mu}
, and

\bm{z}_{i}=\begin{cases}\bm{y}_{i}&\text{w.p.~}\vec{\rho}_{i}\\
\text{Independent draw from $\pi_{\mu}$}&\text{w.p.~}1-\vec{\rho}_{i}.\end{cases}

When
\mu=0
we simply write
\mathrm{Stab}_{\vec{\rho}}(g)
.

This definition allows for a different noise rate for each coordinate, generalizing the more commonly studied definition where the noise rates are the same for every coordinate (see e.g. Chapter 2 of
[
O’D14
]
). We use the terms
multivariate
noise stability and
univariate
noise stability to distinguish these definitions. Even in the case of symmetric combining functions
g
, our strong composition theorem will naturally involve its multivariate noise stability (necessarily so, as already suggested by the counterexample to Conjecture 1).

We present our strong composition theorem as a sequence of two parts that each carries a standalone message, the first of which formalizes the fact that the optimal canonical composed form approximator is a good proxy for the actual optimal approximator. It will be more convenient for us to state our results in terms of advantage instead of error, the two quantities being related via the identity
\text{advantage}=1-2\cdot\text{error}
. Also, for notational clarity we only state here the special case where
f
is balanced (i.e.
\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{\mathcal{D}}[f]=0
).

Theorem 1

(Part I: Canonical composed form approximators are near optimal)
.

Let
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
and
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
be arbitrary functions and
\mathcal{D}
be any distribution over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
. Assume that
\operatorname{{\mathds{E}}}_{\mathcal{D}}[f]=0
. For the task of approximating
g\circ f
under
\mathcal{D}^{k}
with an
R
-junta, there is a correlation vector
\vec{\rho}\in[0,1]^{k}
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Stab}_{\vec{\rho}}(g)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{Advantage of optimal canonical composed form approximator}

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{Advantage of optimal approximator}\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{Stab}_{\vec{\rho}}(g)}.

For most applications of composition theorems, including those in this paper, the parameters of interest are such that the quartic gap between the upper and lower bounds above are inconsequential. (In particular, if the advantage of the optimal canonical composed form approximator diminishes to
0
as
k
grows, our bounds imply that the same is true for the actual optimal approximator. Indeed, the two rates of convergence are the same up to a polynomial factor.)

Part II of
Theorem

1
elaborates on the correlation vector
\vec{\rho}
, showing how it is is determined by the junta complexity of
f
and the noise stability of
g
:

Theorem 1
(Part II: Explicit description of
\vec{\rho}
)
.

The correlation vector
\vec{\rho}\in[0,1]^{k}
in Part I is the vector that maximizes
\mathrm{Stab}_{\vec{\rho}}(g)
, subject to the constraint:

\vec{\rho}_{i}=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\mathcal{D}}\big[f\cdot\tilde{f}_{r_{i}}\big]\text{ for all $i\in[k]$ where }\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}=R.

Taken together, the two parts of
Theorem

1
show that the junta complexity of
g\circ f
is tightly characterized by the junta complexity of
f
and the multivariate noise stability of
g
. It furthermore gives a simple and explicit strategy for constructing a near-optimal approximator: first partition the junta budget optimally among the
k
inner functions; next approximate each inner function optimally with its allocated budget; and finally combine these approximators in the optimal way.

Naturally, it would be preferable to understand the strategy for constructing the actual optimal approximator, but our counterexamples suggest that it defies a clean and interpretable description even for symmetric
g
(indeed, even for
g
being the And function).

(Stability under partial noise)

The

In words,

\frac{1}{2}(1-\sqrt{\tau})\eqqcolon\varepsilon_{\mathrm{large}}

The

The multivariate noise sensitivity of symmetric functions.

For a symmetric function
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
one intuits that its multivariate noise stability at a vector
\vec{\rho}\in[0,1]^{k}
should be related to its univariate noise stability at a value
\rho^{\star}\in[0,1]
that is an “average” of the coordinates of
\vec{\rho}
. (This is certainly not true for general functions; consider for example the dictator function.) Using techniques from the study of negative association, we formalize this intuition and prove that indeed it is sandwiched by the arithmetic and geometric means of the coordinates of
\vec{\rho}
:

Lemma 2.2

(Multivariate and univariate noise stabilities of symmetric functions)
.

Let
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
be a symmetric function,
\mu\in(-1,1)
, and
\vec{\rho}\in[0,1]^{k}
. Define

\rho^{(\mathrm{GM})}\coloneqq\Bigg(\prod_{i\in[k]}\vec{\rho}_{i}\Bigg)^{1/k}\quad\text{and}\quad\ \ \rho^{(\mathrm{AM})}\coloneqq\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i\in[k]}\vec{\rho}_{i}.

Then

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\rho^{(\mathrm{GM})}}(g)\leq\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(g)\leq\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\rho^{(\mathrm{AM})}}(g).

Furthermore, the lower bound holds under the weaker assumption that
g
is transitive.

The more “reasonable”
\vec{\rho}
is, the closer the upper and lower bounds of
Lemma

2.2
are. In particular, we get the following bound on the
(\delta,\varepsilon)
-noise stability of symmetric functions:

Corollary 2.3

(The
(\delta,\varepsilon)
-noise stability of symmetric functions; informal)
.

For any symmetric function
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
,
\delta\in(0,1)
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2)
, the
(\delta,\varepsilon)
-noise stability of

is equal to
\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\rho^{\star}}(g)
for some
\rho^{\star}\in[0,1]
satisfying

1-2\delta\varepsilon-O(\varepsilon^{2})\leq\rho^{\star}\leq 1-2\delta\varepsilon.

Recall that
\varepsilon

in

, and so the additive gap of

between the upper and lower bounds is indeed small for our intended application.

2.2
Second main result: Composition theorems and boosting of property testers

Composition theorems are most naturally thought of as statements about hardness amplification, and indeed that is how they are most commonly used. As our second main contribution, we show how they can be used fruitfully in their contrapositive form as meta-algorithms. In more detail, we show how they can be used to generically boost the performance guarantees of
property testers
. While boosting is a story of success in both the theory and practice of machine learning, to our knowledge the analogous concept in property testing has not yet been considered. The connection that we draw can be instantiated with either strong or weak composition theorems, but as we now see, the parameters are qualitatively better in case of strong composition theorems.

Within property testing, a major strand of research, initiated by Parnas, Ron, and Samorodnitsky
[
PRS02
]
, concerns testing whether an unknown function has a
concise representation
. Consider any parameterized property
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathcal{P}_{s}\}_{s\in\mathbb{N}}
of boolean functions: size-
s
parities, size-
s
juntas, size-
s
decision trees,
s
-sparse polynomials over various fields, and so on. The task is as follows:

Definition 4

(
(\varepsilon,s,s^{\prime})
-testing of
\mathcal{P}
under
\mathcal{D}
)
.

Given queries to an unknown function
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
, access to i.i.d. draws from a distribution
\mathcal{D}
, and parameters
s,s^{\prime}\in\mathds{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
, distinguish between:

\circ

Yes:
f\in\mathcal{P}_{s}

\circ

No:
f
is
\varepsilon
-far under
\mathcal{D}
from every function in
\mathcal{P}_{s^{\prime}}
.

Note that the task is more challenging as
\varepsilon
gets smaller, and as the gap between
s
and
s^{\prime}
gets smaller. We show how a composition theorem for
\mathcal{P}
allows one to trade off these two parameters: a tester for large
\varepsilon
can be upgraded into one for small
\varepsilon
, at the price of larger gap between
s
and
s^{\prime}
. The stronger the composition theorem, the more favorable this tradeoff is, and with an optimally strong composition theorem one is able to improve the
\varepsilon
-dependence without any associated price in the multiplicative gap between
s
and
s^{\prime}
:

Theorem 2

(Composition theorems yield boosters for testers; informal)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathcal{P}_{s}\}_{s\in\mathds{N}}
be a property and
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
be such that
\mathcal{P}
behaves linearly w.r.t.
g
. Suppose that
\mathcal{P}
admits an
(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{small}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{large}},\lambda)
-composition theorem w.r.t.
g
. Then any
(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{large}},ks,\lambda ks^{\prime})
-tester for
\mathcal{P}
can be converted in to an
(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{small}},s,s^{\prime})
-tester for
\mathcal{P}
.

We defer the precise definitions of the terms “
(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{small}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{large}},\lambda)
-composition theorem” and “behaves linearly” to the body of the paper, mentioning for now that
\lambda\in[0,1]
measures the strength of the composition theorem: such a theorem says that the composed function requires
\lambda k
more resources to achieve
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{large}}
error than original function to achieve
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{small}}
error. Therefore
\lambda=\frac{1}{k}
can be viewed as the threshold separating weak and strong composition theorems, with
\lambda=1
corresponding to an optimally strong one. (
Corollary

2.1
, for example, achieves
\lambda=0.99
.) Note that if
\lambda=1
in
Theorem

2
, then an
(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{large}},s,s)
-tester for all
s
yields an
(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{small}},s,s)
-tester for all
s
.

The formal version of
Theorem

2
will also show that it upgrades uniform-distribution testers to strong uniform-distribution testers, and distribution-free testers to strong distribution-free testers. This stands in contrast to standard boosting in learning which can only upgrade distribution-free learners.

2.2.1
Example applications of
Theorem

2
: New implications for junta testing

As mentioned in the introduction, juntas are among the most basic and intensively-studied function classes in property testing. Owing to two decades of research, the complexity of testing juntas in the
non-tolerant
setting is now fairly well-understood: we have highly-efficient adaptive
[
Bla09
]
, non-adaptive
[
Bla08
]
, and distribution-free testers
[
LCS+18
,
Bsh19
]
, all of them achieving query complexities that are essentially optimal
[
CG04
,
Bla08
,
STW15
,
CST+18
]
.

The picture is much less clear in the more challenging
tolerant
setting. For the uniform distribution, the best known testers require exponentially many queries
[
BCE+18
,
ITW21
]
, and there are no known distribution-free testers. By generalization
Theorem

2
to the tolerant setting and instantiating it with our strong composition theorem for juntas, we obtain new implications, both positive and negative, that help clarify this picture.

Positive implication: boosting of tolerant junta testers.

First, any tolerant junta tester for large distance parameter can now be converted into one for small distance parameters, at the price of a slight gap in the junta sizes of the Yes and No cases. For example, for both the uniform and distribution-free settings we get:

Corollary 2.4

(Boosting of tolerant junta testers; special case)
.

Suppose we have a
\mathrm{poly}(r)
-query tester that distinguishes between

\circ

Yes:
f
is
\frac{1}{4}
-close to an
r
-junta

\circ

No:
f
is
\frac{1}{3}
-far from every
r
-junta.

Then for every
\varepsilon>0
we have a
\mathrm{poly}(r/\varepsilon)
-query tester that distinguishes between

\circ

Yes:
f
is
\varepsilon
-close to an
r
-junta

\circ

No:
f
is
\Omega(\varepsilon)
-far from every
1.001r
-junta.

The resulting gap between the junta sizes of the Yes and No cases, while mild, is admittedly not ideal. As alluded to above, this stems from the fact that the “strength parameter” of
Corollary

2.1
is
\lambda=0.99
and not
\lambda=1
. Designing boosters that do not incur this gap, either via an optimally strong composition theorem or otherwise, is a natural avenue for future work.

On the other hand, we now show that even with this gap,
Corollary

2.4
already carries with it an interesting consequence. This consequence crucially relies on our composition theorem for juntas being strong; the proof would not have gone through had the strength parameter of
Corollary

2.1
only been
\lambda=\frac{1}{k}
.

Negative implication: NP-hardness in the distribution-free setting.

This implication concerns the
time
rather than query complexity of testers. The same proof of
Corollary

2.4
also converts a
\mathrm{poly}(r,n)
-time tester into a
\mathrm{poly}(r,1/\varepsilon,n)
-time tester. Implicit in the work of Hancock, Jiang, Li, and Tromp
[
HJLT96
]
is an NP-hardness result for tolerantly testing juntas in the distribution-free setting. One downside of their result is that it only holds in the regime of
\varepsilon=1/\mathrm{poly}(n)
. Applying the time-analogue of
Corollary

2.4
, we lift this hardness up to the standard regime of constant
\varepsilon
:

Corollary 2.5

(NP-hardness in the distribution-free testing)
.

The following task is NP-hard under randomized reductions. Given queries to a function
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
, access to i.i.d. draws from a distribution
\mathcal{D}
, and parameters
r\in\mathds{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
, distinguish between:

\circ

Yes:
f
is
\frac{1}{4}
-close under
\mathcal{D}
to an
r
-junta;

\circ

No:
f
is
\frac{1}{3}
-far under
\mathcal{D}
from every
r
-junta.

This implies a fairly dramatic separation between the non-tolerant versus tolerant versions of the problem. The recent
\mathrm{poly}(r)
-query non-tolerant testers
[
LCS+18
,
Bsh19
]
are also time efficient, running in
\mathrm{poly}(r,n)
time.
Corollary

2.5
shows that any tolerant tester, regardless of query efficiency, must have time complexity that is as bad as that of SAT: e.g. if SAT requires randomized exponential time, then so does any tolerant tester.

In fact, our actual result is stronger than as stated in
Corollary

2.5
: we prove that the task is NP-hard even if the Yes case states that
f
is
0-close
under
\mathcal{D}
to an
r
-junta. We therefore show that the testers of
[

,
Bsh19
]
are quite fragile in the sense that they break if the Yes case in the definition of non-tolerant testing is changed from “

is an

-junta” to “
f
is

-close under
\mathcal{D}
to an
r
-junta”.

3
Other related work

O’Donnell’s generalization of Yao’s XOR lemma.

Yao’s XOR lemma states that if
f
is
\varepsilon
-hard against circuits of size
s
, meaning every size-
s
circuit differs from
f
on at least an
\varepsilon
-fraction of inputs, then
\mathrm{XOR}_{k}\circ f
is
(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}(1-2\varepsilon)^{k}+\delta)
-hard against circuits of size
s^{\prime}
where

s^{\prime}=\Theta\bigg(\frac{\delta^{2}}{\log(1/\varepsilon)}\bigg)\cdot s.

The
(1-2\varepsilon)^{k}
term in the resulting inapproximability factor agrees precisely with the (univariate) noise stability of
\mathrm{XOR}_{k}
at
\rho=1-2\varepsilon
. In
[
O’D02
]
O’Donnell showed that this is no coincidence. He proved a far-reaching generalization of Yao’s XOR lemma that allows for an arbitrary combining function
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
instead of XOR, and showed that the resulting inapproximability of
g\circ f
is given by the “expected bias” of
g
, a quantity that is closely related to the (univariate) noise stability of
g
.

Like Yao’s XOR lemma,
[
O’D02
]
’s composition theorem is weak in the sense that the hardness of
g\circ f
only holds against size
s^{\prime}
circuits where
s^{\prime}\ll s
. (In fact,
[
O’D02
]
incurs an additional multiplicative loss of
k
in the resulting circuit size.) Our composition theorem concerns a different resource, juntas instead of circuits, and as emphasized in the introduction, our main focus is on proving a composition theorem that is strong in the sense of amplifying both the amount of resource required and the inapproximability factor.

Both our work and
[
O’D02
]
utilize Fourier analysis in our proofs, which is to be expected given the centrality of noise stability to both works. That aside, our overall approach and techniques are entirely different from
[
O’D02
]
’s—necessarily so, as we elaborate next.

Hardness amplification via boosting.

In
[
KS03
]
Klivans and Servedio observed that most known hardness amplification results are proved via a boosting-type argument. For example, for Yao’s XOR lemma and
[
O’D02
]
’s generalization of it, one proceeds by contradiction: one assumes that
\mathrm{XOR}_{k}\circ f
can be mildly approximated by a size-
s^{\prime}
circuit
C
(in the language of boosting,
C
is a weak hypothesis for
\mathrm{XOR}_{k}\circ f
), and one constructs a larger circuit
C^{\star}
of size
s
that well-approximates
f
(i.e.
C^{\star}
is a strong hypothesis for
f
). In boosting, the strong hypothesis is built out of many weak hypotheses; likewise, in Yao’s XOR lemma the size-
s
circuit
C^{\star}
is built out of many size-
s^{\prime}
circuits that are like
C
. The work of
[
KS03
]
formalizes this connection.

From this perspective, it becomes clear why such approaches are fundamentally limited to weak composition theorems where
s^{\prime}\ll s
. Strong composition theorems therefore necessitate a different tack, and indeed our proof proceeds via the forward implication instead of the contrapositive: we reason directly about the inapproximability of
g\circ f
under the assumption about the inapproximability of
f
. Somewhat ironically, our second main contribution is then an application
of
strong composition theorems
to
the boosting of property testers, which goes in the opposite direction to
[
KS03
]
’s “Boosting
\Rightarrow
Hardness Amplification” observation above.

Independent work of Chen and Patel
[
CP23
]
.

A recent work of Chen and Patel also gives new lower bounds for tolerant junta testing. For the problem of testing whether an unknown function is
\varepsilon_{1}
-close to or
\varepsilon_{2}
-far from a
k
-junta under the
uniform distribution
, they prove a
query
lower bound of
k^{\Omega(\log(1/(\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1})))}
, which is superpolynomial when the gap
\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}
is
subconstant
. This yields the first superpolynomial query complexity separation between tolerant and non-tolerant testing for a natural property of boolean functions.

Their result is incomparable to
Corollary

2.5
in several respects. We give a
time
lower bound when the gap
\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}
is a
fixed constant
in the
distribution-free
setting. Being an NP-hardness result, our lower bound is conditional whereas theirs is unconditional.

4
Discussion and future work

Complexity measures can behave in highly counterintuitive ways under composition, which makes composition theorems, and strong composition theorems in particular, tricky to prove.
A motivating goal of this work is to develop an understanding of strong composition theorems from first principles, and hence our focus on junta complexity, perhaps the most basic complexity measure of a function. We are optimistic that our techniques can apply to other measures, though we believe that as in this work, much of the challenge will lie in first figuring out the right statement to prove.

Consider for example decision tree complexity, a natural next step from junta complexity. There are existing strong XOR lemmas for decision tree complexity, but they come with limitations and do not appear to be the final word. (Briefly, the XOR lemma of
[
Dru12
]
is only strong when the initial inapproximability factor
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{small}}
is at least a constant, and the strong XOR lemma of
[
BB19
,
BKLS20
]
only holds for decision trees that are allowed to “abort”.) Indeed, Shaltiel
[
Sha04
]
has shown that certain hoped-for strong XOR lemmas for decision tree complexity are false, though as he remarked, his counterexample “seems to exploit defects in the formation of the problem rather than show that our general intuition for direct product assertions is false”. We hope that our results, and specifically the new connections to various notions of noise stability, can serve as a guide to the right statement for decision tree complexity and other measures.

As for our second main result, the general connection between strong composition theorems and the boosting of property testers, we believe that it adds compelling algorithmic motivation to the study of composition theorems, a topic traditionally considered to be mostly of complexity-theoretic interest. Likewise, we hope that our work spurs future research on this new notion of boosting for property testers, a notion that we believe is of interest independent of the connections to composition theorems. For example, an ambitious goal for future work is to broadly understand when and how a tester for constant distance parameter
\varepsilon
can be automatically upgraded into one with the optimal
\varepsilon
-dependence, as well as the associated costs of such a transformation.

5.1
Fourier Analysis

Our proof of
Theorem

1
will make heavy use of Fourier analysis over the
\mu
-biased hypercube,
(\pi_{\mu})^{k}
. In this section, we will review relevant definitions and facts. A more complete exposition is given in
[
O’D14
]
.

For any
\mu\in(-1,1)
, we define
\phi_{\mu}(x)\coloneqq\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}
where
\sigma\coloneqq\sqrt{1-\mu^{2}}
. Every
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\mathds{R}
can be uniquely decomposed as

g(y)=\sum_{S\subseteq[k]}\hat{g}_{\mu}(S)\prod_{i\in S}\phi_{\mu}(y_{i})\quad\quad\text{where}\quad\quad\hat{g}_{\mu}(S)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim(\pi_{\mu})^{k}}\left[g(\bm{y})\prod_{i\in S}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{y}_{i})\right].

This decomposition has a number of useful properties stemming from the fact that transforming
g
from its representation as a truth table to its Fourier coefficients
\hat{g}_{\mu}(S)
is an orthonormal transformation.

Fact 5.1

(Basic facts about the Fourier decomposition)
.

1.

Plancherel’s theorem:
For any
g,h:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\mathds{R}
and
\mu\in(-1,1)
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim(\pi_{\mu})^{k}}[g(\bm{y})h(\bm{y})]=\sum_{S\subseteq[k]}\hat{g}_{\mu}(S)\hat{h}_{\mu}(S).

2.

Parseval’s theorem:
For any
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\mathds{R}
and
\mu\in(-1,1)
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim(\pi_{\mu})^{k}}[g(\bm{y})^{2}]=\sum_{S\subseteq[k]}\hat{g}_{\mu}(S)^{2}.

In particular, when
g
has a range of
\{\pm 1\}
, Parseval’s theorem guarantees that the sum of its squared Fourier coefficients is
1
. As a result, the following distribution is well defined.

Definition 7

(Spectral sample)
.

For any
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
and bias
\mu\in(-1,1)
, the
spectral sample
of
g
, denoted
\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(g)
, is the probably distribution over subsets of
[k]
in which the set
S
has probability
\hat{g}_{\mu}(S)^{2}
.

The Fourier decomposition gives a concise way to represent important quantities, as in the following results.

Proposition 5.2

(Multivariate noise stability from the Fourier spectrum.)
.

For any
\mu\in(-1,1)
and
\vec{\rho}\in[0,1]^{k}
,
\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}
can be related to
g
’s
\mu
-biased Fourier decomposition as,

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(g)=\sum_{S\subseteq[k]}\hat{g}(S)^{2}\vec{\rho}^{S}=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(g)}[(\vec{\rho})^{\bm{S}}].

Proof.

We define
g^{(\vec{\rho})}(y)\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{z}\overset{\vec{\rho}}{\sim}y}[g(\bm{z})]
. Then, by Plancherel’s theorem,

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(g)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim(\pi_{\mu})^{k}}[g(\bm{y})g^{(\vec{\rho})}(\bm{y})]=\sum_{S\subseteq[k]}\widehat{g}_{\mu}(S)\widehat{g^{(\vec{\rho})}}_{\mu}(S).

Next, we compute the Fourier decomposition of
g^{(\vec{\rho})}
.

\displaystyle\widehat{g^{(\vec{\rho})}}_{\mu}(S)

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim(\pi_{\mu})^{k}}\left[g^{(\vec{\rho})}(\bm{y})\prod_{i\in S}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{y}_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim(\pi_{\mu})^{k},\bm{z}\overset{\vec{\rho}}{\sim}\bm{x}}\left[g(\bm{z})\prod_{i\in S}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{y}_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim(\pi_{\mu})^{k},\bm{z}\overset{\vec{\rho}}{\sim}\bm{x}}\left[g(\bm{y})\prod_{i\in S}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{z}_{i})\right]

(
(\bm{y},\bm{z})
distributed identically to
(\bm{z},\bm{y})
)

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim(\pi_{\mu})^{k}}\left[g(\bm{y})\cdot\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{z}\overset{\vec{\rho}}{\sim}\bm{y}}\left[\prod_{i\in S}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{z}_{i})\right]\right].

Applying the independence of
\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{k}
conditioned on
\bm{y}
and that
\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\bm{z}_{i})]=\vec{\rho}_{i}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{y}_{i})
,

\displaystyle\widehat{g^{(\vec{\rho})}}_{\mu}(S)

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim(\pi_{\mu})^{k}}\left[g(\bm{y})\cdot\prod_{i\in S}\vec{\rho}_{i}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{y}_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=(\vec{\rho})^{S}\cdot\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim(\pi_{\mu})^{k}}\left[g(\bm{y})\cdot\prod_{i\in S}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{y}_{i})\right]=(\vec{\rho})^{S}\widehat{g}_{\mu}(S).

Putting the above together,

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(g)=\sum_{S\subseteq[k]}\hat{g}_{\mu}(S)^{2}(\vec{\rho})^{S}.\qed

One immediate corollary of the above is that multivariate noise stability is monotone.

Corollary 5.3

(Multivariate noise stability is monotone)
.

For any
\mu\in(-1,1)
,
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
, and
\vec{\rho},\vec{\rho^{\prime}}\in[0,1]^{k}
satisfying
\vec{\rho}_{i}\leq\vec{\rho^{\prime}}_{i}
for all
i\in[k]
,

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(g)\leq\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho^{\prime}}}(g).

Recall that for any
\nu\in[-1,1]^{k}
, the distribution
\pi_{\nu}
is the unique product distribution supported on
\{\pm 1\}^{k}
with mean
\nu
. The Fourier decomposition of
g
also gives a useful way to compute
\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\pi_{\nu}}[g(\bm{y})]
.

Fact 5.4
.

For any
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\mathds{R}
,
\mu\in(-1,1)
, and
\nu\in[-1,1]^{k}
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\pi_{\nu}}[g(\bm{y})]=\sum_{S\subseteq[k]}\hat{g}_{\mu}(S)\prod_{i\in S}\phi_{\mu}(\nu_{i}).

Proof.

We expand
g
into it’s Fourier decomposition

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[g(\bm{y})]

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq[k]}\hat{g}_{\mu}(S)\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\prod_{i\in S}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{y}_{i})\right]

(Linearity of expectation)

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq[k]}\hat{g}_{\mu}(S)\prod_{i\in S}\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\phi_{\mu}(\bm{y}_{i})\right]

(

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq[k]}\hat{g}_{\mu}(S)\prod_{i\in S}\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\frac{\bm{y}_{i}-\mu}{\sigma}\right]

(Definition of

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq[k]}\hat{g}_{\mu}(S)\prod_{i\in S}\phi_{\mu}(\nu_{i}).

(Linearity of expectation)

A strong composition theorem for juntas

In this section, we characterize the junta size required to approximate
g\circ f
in terms of the
multivariate noise stability
of
g
, and the junta size required to approximate
f
.

Theorem 3

(A strong composition theorem for junta complexity, generalization of
Theorem

1
)
.

For any
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
,
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
and base distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, let
\mu=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}[f(\bm{x})]
.

1.

Lower bound on advantage:
For any approximators
q^{(1)},\ldots,q^{(k)}:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
, define the
lower normalized correlations
, for each
i\in[k]
as

\alpha_{i}\coloneqq\max\left(0,\frac{\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathcal{D}}(f,q^{(i)})^{2}-\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}\right).

Then, there is an
h:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
for which

\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathcal{D}^{k}}(g\circ f,h(q^{(1)},\ldots,q^{(k)}))\geq\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\alpha}(g).

2.

Upper bound on advantage:
For any
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
, define the
upper normalized correlation
as

\beta_{i}\coloneqq\max\left(0,\frac{\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathcal{D}}(f,S_{i})-\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}\right),

construct
S\subseteq[n]\times[k]
by taking
S_{1}
from the first block,
S_{2}
from the second block, and so on (formally
S\coloneqq\cup_{i\in[k],j\in S_{i}}\{(j,i)\}
). Then,

\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathcal{D}^{k}}(g\circ f,S)\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\beta}(g)}.

Our goal is to understand the error of the best
R
-junta approximating
g\circ f
.
Theorem

3
says that for
any
way to partition
R=r_{1}+\cdots r_{k}
, the approximator
h(\tilde{f}_{r_{1}},\ldots,\tilde{f}_{r_{k}})
achieves nearly optimal advantage across all
R
-juntas that partition their budget this way. Of course, by maximizing both sides across all partitions, we can conclude that there is some partitioning and function
h
for which
h(\tilde{f}_{r_{1}},\ldots,\tilde{f}_{r_{k}})
has nearly optimal advantage among all
R
-juntas. Indeed, as a simple corollary of
Theorem

3
, we can show that the
error
of the optimal canonical composed form approximator is within a factor of
4
of the optimal approximator. Recall that
\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}}(q_{1},q_{2})=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}[q_{1}(\bm{x})\neq q_{2}(\bm{x})]
and is related to advantage via the equality
\mathrm{Adv}=1-2\cdot\mathrm{error}
.

Corollary 6.1

(Canonical composed form achieves nearly optimal error)
.

For any
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\},f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
, junta budget
R
, and base distribution
\mathcal{D}
, there is an
h:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
and partition of the budget
r_{1}+\cdots+r_{k}=R
for which,.

\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}^{k}}(g\circ f,h(\tilde{f}_{r_{1}},\ldots,\tilde{f}_{r_{k}}))\leq 4\cdot\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}^{k}}(g\circ f,R).

When
\mu=0
, the guarantee of
Theorem

3
can further be given in the concise form of
Theorem

1
: For an appropriately chosen
\vec{\rho}\in[0,1]^{k}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Stab}_{\vec{\rho}}(g)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{Advantage of optimal canonical composed form approximator}

\displaystyle\leq\textnormal{Advantage of optimal approximator}\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{Stab}_{\vec{\rho}}(g)}.

We include the proofs of
Corollary

6.1
and
Theorem

1
in
Section

6.3
.

6.1
Proof of the lower bound on advantage

In this subsection, we show that
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\to h(\tilde{f}_{r_{1}}(x_{1}),\ldots,\tilde{f}_{r_{k}}(x_{k}))
is close to the best
R
-junta approximator for
g\circ f
. Here, the function
h
can be different than
g
, and this is necessary as shown in the counterexample to conjecture 2 in
Section

2.1.1
.

Lemma 6.2

(Part 1 of
Theorem

3
: Lower bound on advantage)
.

For any
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
,
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
, and approximators
q^{(1)},\ldots,q^{(k)}
, there is some
h:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
for which

\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathcal{D}^{k}}(g\circ f,h\circ(q^{(1)},\ldots,q^{(k)}))\geq\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\alpha}(g),

where
\mu=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}[f(\bm{x})]
and for each
i\in[k]
,

\alpha_{i}\coloneqq\max\left(0,\frac{\mathrm{Adv}(f,q^{(i)})^{2}-\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}\right).

Note
\alpha_{i}
naturally interpolates between
0
and
1
. Setting
q^{(i)}
to the better of the constant
-1
or the constant
+1
function will lead to
\alpha_{i}=0
, while setting
q^{(i)}=f
gives
\alpha_{i}=1
.

6.1.1
Characterizing the advantage of composed form approximators

To ease notation, we begin with a simpler setting. Suppose we use the same budget,
r\coloneqq R/k
, in each of the
k
pieces. Our goal is to understand

\max_{h:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}}\mathrm{Adv}(g\circ f,h\circ\tilde{f}_{r})

in terms of the noise sensitivity of
g
and
\mathrm{Adv}(f,\tilde{f}_{r})
. To do so, we will consider
unbalanced
noise stability.

Definition 8

(Unbalanced noise stability)
.

For any
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{k}
, we use the notation
\bm{y}\overset{a,b}{\sim}x
to denote that for each
i\in[k]
,
\bm{y}_{i}
is independently drawn as

1.

If
x_{i}=-1
, with probability
a
, we set
\bm{y}_{i}=x_{i}
and otherwise set
\bm{y}_{i}=-x_{i}

2.

If
x_{i}=1
, with probability
b
, we set
\bm{y}_{i}=x_{i}
and otherwise set
\bm{y}_{i}=-x_{i}
.

For any
g,h:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
,
\mu\in[-1,1]
and
a,b\in[0,1]
, we define the
unbalanced
noise stability as

\mathrm{UnbalStab}_{\mu,(a,b)}(g,h)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim(\pi_{\mu})^{k},\bm{y}\overset{a,b}{\sim}\bm{x}}[g(\bm{x})h(\bm{y})].

We refer to the above notion as
unbalanced
because when drawing
\bm{y}\overset{a,b}{\sim}x
, the probability of the
i^{\text{th}}
coordinate flipping from
-1
to
1
and from
1
to
-1
may differ. Unbalanced noise stability is useful in our setting due to the following proposition.

Proposition 6.3
.

For any
f,\tilde{f}:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
and
g,h:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}^{k}}[(g\circ f)(\bm{x})\cdot(h\circ\tilde{f})(\bm{x})]=\mathrm{UnbalStab}_{\mu,(a,b)}(g,h),

where

\mu\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}[f(\bm{x})],\quad\quad a\coloneqq\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}[\tilde{f}(\bm{x})=-1\mid f(\bm{x})=-1],\quad\quad b\coloneqq\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}[\tilde{f}(\bm{x})=1\mid f(\bm{x})=1].

Proof.

Draw
\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}^{k}
and then define
\bm{y}\coloneqq f^{\otimes k}(\bm{x}),\tilde{\bm{y}}\coloneqq\tilde{f}^{\otimes k}(\bm{x})
. Clearly,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}^{k}}[(g\circ f)(\bm{x})\cdot(h\circ\tilde{f})(\bm{x})]=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[g(\bm{y})h(\tilde{\bm{y}})].

Furthermore, the distribution of
\bm{y},\tilde{\bm{y}}
is equivalent to if we drew
\bm{y}\sim(\pi_{\mu})^{k},\tilde{\bm{y}}\overset{a,b}{\sim}\bm{y}
. The above quantity therefore matches the definition of
\mathrm{UnbalStab}_{\mu,(a,b)}(g,h)
.
∎

6.1.2
Unbalanced noise stability behaves strangely

The most basic requirement of our approximation for
g\circ f
is that it have advantage at least
0
, as either the constant
-1
or the constant
+1
function is guaranteed to have such an advantage. Indeed, in the balanced case, it is well known that the approximation will satisfy this basic requirement even if we take
h=g
.

Fact 6.4
.

For any
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
and
a\in[0,1/2]
,

\mathrm{UnbalStab}_{0,(a,a)}(g,g)\geq 0.

However, in the unbalanced case, this basic requirement no longer holds.

Proposition 6.5
.

For any
k\geq 0
, and
a,b\in[0,1]
for which
|a-b|\geq 0.01
, there is a function
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
for which

\mathrm{UnbalStab}_{0,(a,b)}(g,g)\leq-(1-2^{-\Omega(k)}).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume
b\geq a+0.01
. We define

g(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\sum_{i\in[k]}x_{i}\geq 0.005k,\\
-1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Draw
\bm{x}\sim(\pi_{\mu})^{k},\bm{y}\overset{a,b}{\sim}\bm{x}
. Then,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\sum_{i\in[k]}\bm{x}_{i}\right]=0,\quad\quad\quad\quad\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\sum_{i\in[k]}\bm{y}_{i}\right]=k(b-a).

Furthermore, a standard application of Hoeffding’s inequality implies that

\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}[g(\bm{x})=1]\leq 2^{-\Omega(k)},\quad\quad\quad\quad\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}[g(\bm{y})=-1]\leq 2^{-\Omega(k)}.

By union bound, with probability at least
2^{-\Omega(k)}
, we have that both
g(\bm{x})=-1
and
g(\bm{y})=1
. This implies the desired result.
∎

6.1.3
Unbalanced noise stability behaves well if we use the best
h

Surprisingly, we show that if we use the best
h
, our approximation does meet this most basic requirement. Furthermore, we can relate it to the classical notion of
balanced
noise stability. The below Lemma directly implies
Lemma

6.2
.

Lemma 6.6
.

For any
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
and distribution over
\bm{x},\bm{y}
each in
\{\pm 1\}^{k}
satisfying,

1.

The pairs
(\bm{x}_{1},\bm{y}_{1}),\ldots,(\bm{x}_{k},\bm{y}_{k})
are independent of one another.

2.

The means satisfy
\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\bm{x}_{1}]=\cdots=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\bm{x}_{k}]=\mu
.

Define the correlations
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
as

\alpha_{i}\coloneqq\max\left(0,\frac{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\bm{x}_{i}\bm{y}_{i}]^{2}-\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}\right).

Then, there is an
h:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
for which

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[g(\bm{x})h(\bm{y})]\geq\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\alpha}(g).

Comparing to
Proposition

6.5
, if
\mu=0
, then
\alpha_{i}=\max(0,1-a-b)
for all
i\in[k]
. Since
\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\alpha}(g)\geq 0
whenever
\alpha\geq 0
,
Lemma

6.6
shows that the phenomenon in
Proposition

6.5
cannot occur if we use the best approximator
h
.

The following Lemma will be useful in the proof of
Lemma

6.6
.

Lemma 6.7
.

For any function
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
, let
\bm{\nu}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\nu}_{k}
be independent random variables each with mean
\mu
and supported on
[-1,1]
. Then,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{\nu}}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\pi_{\bm{\nu}}}[g(\bm{y})]^{2}\right]=\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\nu}_{1})^{2}],\ldots,\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\nu}_{k})^{2}])}(g).

Proof.

We’ll use the
\mu
-biased Fourier expansion of
g
. Applying
5.4
,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{\nu}}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\pi_{\bm{\nu}}}[g(\bm{y})]^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{\nu}}\left[\left(\sum_{S\subseteq[k]}\hat{g}(S)\prod_{i\in S}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\nu}_{i})\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq[k]}\hat{g}(S_{1})\hat{g}(S_{2})\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\prod_{i\in S_{1}}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\nu}_{i})\prod_{i\in S_{2}}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\nu}_{i})\right].

We claim that, in the above sum, any term in which
S_{1}\neq S_{2}
is equal to
0
. Let
S_{1}\triangle S_{2}
denote the symmetric difference of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
. Then, due to the independence of
\bm{\nu}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\nu}_{k}
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\prod_{i\in S_{1}}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\nu}_{i})\prod_{i\in S_{2}}\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\nu}_{i})\right]=\prod_{i\in S_{1}\cap S_{2}}\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\nu}_{i})^{2}]\prod_{i\in S_{1}\triangle S_{2}}\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\nu}_{i})].

Since the mean of
\bm{\nu}_{i}
is
\mu
,
\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\nu}_{i})]=\phi_{\mu}(\mu)=0
. If
S_{1}\neq S_{2}
, there is at least one element in
S_{1}\triangle S_{2}
, and so the term is
0
. We are therefore left with,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{\nu}}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\mathcal{D}(\bm{\nu})}[g(\bm{y})]^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq[k]}\hat{g}(S)^{2}\prod_{i\in S}\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\nu}_{i})^{2}\right].

This is exactly the Fourier expansion for the claimed result.
∎

We’ll also use the following proposition.

Proposition 6.8
.

For any random variable
\bm{\nu}
bounded on
[-1,1]
almost surely and with mean
\mu
,

\max\left(0,\frac{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[|\bm{\nu}|]^{2}-\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}\right)\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\nu})^{2}]\leq\frac{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[|\bm{\nu}|]-\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}.

Proof.

We expand, using linearity of expectation,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\nu})^{2}]=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\frac{(\bm{\nu}-\mu)^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}\right]=\frac{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\rho^{2}]-2\mu\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\bm{\nu}]+\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}.

Since
\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\bm{\nu}]=\mu
, we have that
\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\nu})^{2}]=\frac{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\bm{\nu}^{2}]-\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}
. Therefore, by Jensen’s inequality,

\frac{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[|\bm{\nu}|]^{2}-\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\nu})^{2}].

Furthermore, since
\bm{\nu}^{2}\leq|\bm{\nu}|
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\nu})^{2}]\leq\frac{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[|\bm{\nu}|]-\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}.

Lastly,
\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\bm{\nu})^{2}]\geq 0
follows from non-negativity.
∎

Finally, we are ready to prove
Lemma

6.6
.

Proof of
Lemma

6.6
.

For any
y\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, we define

g_{\mathop{\mathrm{avg}}}(y)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[g(\bm{x})\mid\bm{y}=y].

Then, setting
h(y)\coloneqq\mathrm{sign}(g_{\mathop{\mathrm{avg}}}(y))
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[g(\bm{x})h(\bm{y})]=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}}\left[\left|g_{\mathop{\mathrm{avg}}}(\bm{y})\right|\right]\geq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}}\left[\left(g_{\mathop{\mathrm{avg}}}(\bm{y})\right)^{2}\right].

Note that, conditioning on
\bm{y}=y
, the distribution of
\bm{x}
is still product. Let
\nu(y)
be the mean of this distribution, so that

g_{\mathop{\mathrm{avg}}}(y)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\pi_{\nu(y)}}\left[g(\bm{z})\right].

By
Lemma

6.7
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}}\left[\left(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\pi_{\nu(\bm{y})}}\left[g(\bm{z})\right]\right)^{2}\right]=\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\nu(\bm{y})_{1})^{2}],\ldots,\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\nu(\bm{y})_{k})^{2}]}(g).

For each
i\in[k]
,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\nu(\bm{y})_{i})^{2}]

\displaystyle\geq\max\left(0,\frac{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}}[|\nu(\bm{y})_{i}|]^{2}-\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}\right)

(
Proposition

6.8
)

\displaystyle\geq\max\left(0,\frac{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}}[\bm{y}_{i}\nu(\bm{y})_{i}]^{2}-\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}\right)

(
|x|\geq cx
when
c\in\{\pm 1\}
)

\displaystyle=\max\left(0,\frac{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}[\bm{y}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}]^{2}-\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}\right)

(Definition of
\nu(y)
)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{i}.

Putting all of the above together,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[g(\bm{x})h(\bm{y})]

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\nu(\bm{y})_{1})^{2}],\ldots,\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\nu(\bm{y})_{k})^{2}]}(g)

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\rho}(g),

where the final inequality follows from the monotonicity of noise stability.
∎

6.2
Proof of the upper bound on advantage

In this section, we prove the following.

Lemma 6.9

(Part 2 of
Theorem

3
: Upper bound on advantage)
.

For any
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
,
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
,
\mu\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}[f(\bm{x})]
, and
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
, define the
upper normalized correlation
as

\beta_{i}\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathcal{D}}(f,S_{i})-\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}.

For
S\subseteq[n]\times[k]
constructed by taking
S_{1}
from the first block,
S_{2}
from the second block, and so on (formally
S\coloneqq\cup_{i\in[k],j\in S_{i}}\{(j,i)\}
).. Then,

\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathcal{D}^{k}}(g\circ f,S)\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\beta}(g)}.

To begin with, we rewrite advantage in the following form.

Proposition 6.10
.

For any function
q:\{\pm 1\}^{m}\to\{\pm 1\}
, distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\{\pm 1\}^{m}
, and
S\subseteq[m]
, define

q_{S,\mathcal{D}}^{\mathop{\mathrm{avg}}}(x)\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\mathcal{D}}[q(\bm{y})\mid\bm{y}_{S}=x_{S}],

(1)

where
y_{S}=x_{S}
is shorthand for
x_{i}=y_{i}
for all
i\in S
. Then,

\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathcal{D}}(q,S)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\left|q_{S,\mathcal{D}}^{\mathop{\mathrm{avg}}}(\bm{x})\right|\right].

Proof.

Consider any
S
-junta
h
. Then,

\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathcal{D}}(q,h)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\mathcal{D}}[q(\bm{y})h(\bm{y})]=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\mathcal{D}}[q(\bm{y})h(\bm{y})\mid\bm{x}_{S}=\bm{y}_{S}]\right].

Since
h
is an
S
-junta, it must classify
x
and
y
the same whenever
x_{S}=y_{S}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Adv}(q,h)

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[h(\bm{x})\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\mathcal{D}}[q(\bm{y})\mid\bm{x}_{S}=\bm{y}_{S}]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[h(\bm{x})q^{\mathop{\mathrm{avg}}}_{S,\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x})\right].

to maximize the above advantage among all
h
, we set
h(x)=\mathrm{sign}(q^{\mathop{\mathrm{avg}}}_{S,\mathcal{D}}(x))
, in which case

\mathrm{Adv}(q,h)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\left|q^{\mathop{\mathrm{avg}}}_{S,\mathcal{D}}(\bm{x})\right|\right].\qed

Given
Proposition

6.10
, to compute
\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathcal{D}^{k}}(g\circ f,S)
, it suffices to understand the function
(g\circ f)^{\mathop{\mathrm{avg}}}_{S,\mathcal{D}}
. We proceed to transform that function into a form which is easier to understand.

Proposition 6.11
.

In the setting of
Lemma

6.9
, for any
x\in(\{\pm 1\}^{n})^{k}
, let
\nu(x)\in[-1,1]^{k}
be the vector where

\nu(x)_{i}\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\mathcal{D}^{k}}[f(\bm{y})\mid x^{(i)}_{S_{i}}=\bm{y}_{S_{i}}].

Then,

(g\circ f)^{\mathop{\mathrm{avg}}}_{S,\mathcal{D}^{k}}(x)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\pi_{\nu(x)}}[g(\bm{z})].

Proof.

Consider drawing
\bm{y}\sim(\{\pm 1\}^{n})^{k}
conditioned on
\bm{y}_{S}=x_{S}
. Let
\bm{z}=f^{\otimes k}(\bm{y})
. By definition,

(g\circ f)^{\mathop{\mathrm{avg}}}_{S,\mathcal{D}^{k}}(x)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[g(\bm{z})].

Therefore, we merely need to show that the distribution of
\bm{z}
is that of
\pi_{\nu(x)}
. For this it is sufficient that,

1.

Each
\bm{z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{z}_{k}
is independent. This follows from the fact
\bm{y}_{1},\ldots,\bm{y}_{k}
are independent, and that the restriction that
\bm{y}_{S}=x_{S}
is a disjoint restriction for each of the
k
components.

2.

For each
i\in[k]
, that
\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\bm{z}_{i}]=\nu(x)_{i}
. This follows from the definition of
\nu(x)_{i}
.

The desired result follows from the fact that
\pi_{\nu(x)}
is the unique product distribution over
\{\pm 1\}^{k}
with mean
\nu(x)
.
∎

We now prove the upper bound.

Proof of
Lemma

6.9
.

Let
\nu
be as defined in
Proposition

6.11
. Applying it and
Proposition

6.10
,

\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathcal{D}^{k}}(g\circ f,S)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}^{k}}\left[\left|\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\pi_{\nu(\bm{x})}}[g(\bm{z})]\right|\right]\leq\sqrt{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}^{k}}\left[\left(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\pi_{\nu(\bm{x})}}[g(\bm{z})]\right)^{2}\right]}.

The inequality above is Jensen’s. Consider the random variables
\nu(\bm{x})_{1},\ldots,\nu(\bm{x})_{k}
. The have the following two properties.

1.

They are independent. This is because the value of
\nu(\bm{x})_{i}
depends on only the value of
\bm{x}_{i}
, which is independent of the other
\bm{x}_{j}
for
j\neq i
.

2.

They each have mean
\mu
. This is because,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\nu(\bm{x})_{i}]=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\mathcal{D}}[f(\bm{y})\mid(\bm{x}^{(i)})_{S_{i}}=y_{S_{i}}]\right]=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim\mathcal{D}}[f(\bm{y})]=\mu.

Therefore, we can use
Lemma

6.7
:

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\mathcal{D}^{k}}\left[\left(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{z}\sim\pi_{\nu(\bm{x})}}[g(\bm{z})]\right)^{2}\right]=\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\nu(\bm{x})_{1})^{2}],\ldots,\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\nu(\bm{x})_{k})^{2}])}(g).

We can further upper bound,

\displaystyle\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\phi_{\mu}(\nu(\bm{x})_{i})^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[|\nu(\bm{x})_{i}|]-\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}

(
Proposition

6.8
)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{Adv}(f,S_{i})-\mu^{2}}{1-\mu^{2}}

(
Proposition

6.10
)

\displaystyle=\beta_{i}.

Putting the above together, we have that

\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathcal{D}^{k}}(g\circ f,S)\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\beta}(g)}.\qed

Our proof of

Proposition 6.12
.

For any

Proof.

We consider the vector

satisfying

Note that
\beta^{\prime}_{i}\geq\beta_{i}
, which means that

1-\prod_{i\in[m]}\beta^{\prime}_{i}\leq 1-\prod_{i\in[m]}\beta_{i}.

Now, consider the function

defined as

A quick calculation confirms that the second derivative of

is concave. Furthermore, it satisfies,

\displaystyle q(0)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle q(1)

\displaystyle=1-\prod_{i\in[m]}\alpha_{i},

\displaystyle q(1/2)

\displaystyle=1-\prod_{i\in[m]}\beta^{\prime}_{i}.

We conclude,

1-\prod_{i\in[m]}\alpha_{i}\overset{\text{concavity of $q$}}{\leq}2\left(1-\prod_{i\in[m]}\beta^{\prime}_{i}\right)\leq\left(1-\prod_{i\in[m]}\beta_{i}\right).\qed

Multivariate noise stability of symmetric functions

In this section, we prove
Lemma

2.2
and
Corollary

2.3
, connecting the multivariate noise stability of symmetric functions to their univariate noise stability.

Definition 9

(Symmetric and transitive functions)
.

For any function
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
, a permutation
\sigma:[k]\to[k]
is an automorphism of
g
if for all inputs
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{k}
,

g(x)=g(x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(k)}).

We say
g
is
symmetric
if every permutation of
[k]
is an automorphism of
g
. Similarly,
g
is
transitive
if for all
i,j\in[k]
, there is an automorphism of
g
sending
i
to
j
.

7.1
The upper bound on the multivariate noise stability of symmetric functions

Lemma 7.1

(Upper bound of
Lemma

2.2
)
.

For any symmetric
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
,
\mu\in(-1,1)
, and
\vec{\rho}\in[0,1]^{k}
, let
\rho^{(\mathrm{AM})}\coloneqq 1/k\cdot\sum_{i\in[k]}\vec{\rho}_{i}
. Then,

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(g)\leq\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\rho^{(\mathrm{AM})}}(g).

Our proof of
Lemma

7.1
will use make heavy use of the
negative association
of random variables.

Definition 10

(Negative association
[
JDP83
]
)
.

A set of random variables
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{m}
supported on
\mathds{R}
are
negatively associated
if for all
disjoint
subsets
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq[m]
and
S_{1}
-juntas
f_{1}:\mathds{R}^{m}\to\mathds{R}
,
S_{2}
-juntas
f_{2}:\mathds{R}^{m}\to\mathds{R}
both monotonically nondecreasing,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[f_{1}(\bm{x})f_{2}(\bm{x})]\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[f_{1}(\bm{x})]\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[f_{2}(\bm{x})].

For our purposes, we will only need a few useful facts about negatively associated random variables given in
[
JDP83
]
(see also
[
Waj17
]
for a useful overview).

Fact 7.2

(Permutation distributions are negatively associated,
[
JDP83
]
)
.

For any
z_{1},\ldots,z_{m}\in\mathds{R}
, draw a uniformly random permutation
\bm{\sigma}:[m]\to[m]
and set
\bm{x}_{i}=z_{\bm{\sigma}(i)}
for each
i\in[k]
. Then,
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{m}
are negatively associated.

Fact 7.3

(Subsets of negatively associated random variables are negatively associated)
.

For any
2\leq m^{\prime}\leq m
, if
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{m}
are negatively associated, then
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{m^{\prime}}
are also negatively associated.

Fact 7.4

(Product consequence of negative association)
.

For any negatively associated
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{m}
and nondecreasing
f:\mathds{R}\to\mathds{R}_{\geq 0}
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\prod_{i\in[m]}f(\bm{x}_{i})\right]\leq\prod_{i\in[m]}\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[f(\bm{x}_{i})\right].

Given the above, facts about negative associated random variables, we can now prove
Lemma

7.1
.

Proof of
Lemma

7.1
.

We expand
\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(g)
using the Fourier spectrum of
g
(
Proposition

5.2
),

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(g)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(g)}[(\vec{\rho})^{\bm{S}}].

Let
\bm{\ell}
be the distributed the same as
|\bm{S}|
for
\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(g)
. Then,

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(g)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{\ell}}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(g)}[(\vec{\rho})^{\bm{S}}\mid|\bm{S}|=\ell]\right].

Since
g
is symmetric, for any
|S_{1}|=|S_{2}|
,
\hat{g}(S_{1})=\hat{g}(S_{2})
. As a result the distribution of
\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(g)
conditioned on
|\bm{S}|=\ell
is simply a uniformly random size-
\ell
subset of
[k]
. Formally,

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(g)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{\ell}}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\binom{[k]}{\bm{\ell}}}[(\vec{\rho})^{\bm{S}}]\right].

Let
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{k}
be a uniform random permutation of
\vec{\rho}_{1},\ldots,\vec{\rho}_{k}
. Then, the distribution of
(\vec{\rho})^{\bm{S}}
for
\bm{S}\sim\binom{[k]}{\ell}
is identical to that of
\prod_{i\in[\ell]}\bm{x}_{i}
. By
7.2
and
7.3
,
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{\ell}
are negatively associated, and so,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\binom{[k]}{\ell}}[(\vec{\rho})^{\bm{S}}]=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\prod_{i\in[\ell]}\bm{x}_{i}\right]\overset{\mathrm{(\lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{fact:NA-prod})}}{\leq}\prod_{i\in[\ell]}\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}[\bm{x}_{i}]=\left(\rho^{(\mathrm{AM})}\right)^{\ell}.

Therefore,

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(g)\leq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{\ell}}\left[\left(\rho^{(\mathrm{AM})}\right)^{\bm{\ell}}\right]=\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\rho^{(\mathrm{AM})}}(g).\qed

7.2
The lower bound on the multivariate noise stability of symmetric functions

Lemma 7.5

(Lower bound of
Lemma

2.2
)
.

For any transitive
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
,
\mu\in(-1,1)
, and
\vec{\rho}\in[0,1]^{k}
, let
\rho^{(\mathrm{GM})}\coloneqq\left(\prod_{i\in[k]}\vec{\rho}_{i}\right)^{1/k}
. Then,

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(g)\geq\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\rho^{(\mathrm{GM})}}(g).

Note that every transitive
g
is also symmetric, but the reverse does not hold.

Proof.

Similarly to the proof of
Lemma

7.1
, let
\bm{\ell}
be the distribution of
|\bm{S}|
when
\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(g)
. Then,

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(g)=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{\ell}}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(g)}[(\vec{\rho})^{\bm{S}}\mid|\bm{S}|=\bm{\ell}]\right].

For each
S\subseteq[k]
, we’ll use
\chi(S)\in\{0,1\}^{k}
to denote the characteristic vector of
S
, meaning
\chi(S)_{i}\coloneqq\mathds{1}[i\in S]
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(g)

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{\ell}}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(g)}\left[\prod_{i\in[k]}(\vec{\rho}_{i})^{\chi(\bm{S})_{i}}\,\bigg|\,|\bm{S}|=\bm{\ell}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{\ell}}\left[\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(g)}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i\in[k]}\chi(\bm{S})_{i}\log(\vec{\rho}_{i})\right)\,\bigg|\,|\bm{S}|=\bm{\ell}\right]\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{\ell}}\left[\exp\left(\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(g)}\left[\sum_{i\in[k]}\chi(\bm{S})_{i}\log(\vec{\rho}_{i})\,\bigg|\,|\bm{S}|=\bm{\ell}\right]\right)\right]

(Jensen’s inequality)

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{\ell}}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i\in[k]}\log(\vec{\rho}_{i})\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(g)}\left[i\in\bm{S}\mid|\bm{S}|=\bm{\ell}\right]\right)\right].

(Linearity of expectation)

Fix any
i_{1},i_{2}\in[k]
and level
\ell\in[0,k]
. Since
g
is transitive, there is an automorphism,
\sigma
, of
g
sending
i_{1}
to
i_{2}
. Since
\sigma
is an automorphism of
g
, for any
S\subseteq[k]

\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{S}=S]=\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{S}=\sigma(S)]
. As a result

\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(g)}\left[i_{1}\in\bm{S}\mid|\bm{S}|=\ell\right]=\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(g)}\left[i_{2}\in\bm{S}\mid|\bm{S}|=\ell\right],

and so
\mathop{{\operatorname{{Pr}}}\/}_{\bm{S}\sim\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(g)}\left[i\in\bm{S}\mid|\bm{S}|=\ell\right]
must be the same for all
i\in[k]
. The sum of these probabilities is
\ell
, meaning each is
\frac{\ell}{k}
. This allows us to bound,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(g)

\displaystyle\geq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{\ell}}\left[\exp\left(\sum_{i\in[k]}\log(\vec{\rho}_{i})\cdot\frac{\bm{\ell}}{k}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{\ell}}\left[\prod_{i\in[k]}\left(\vec{\rho}_{i}\right)^{\frac{\bm{\ell}}{k}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{\ell}}\left[(\rho^{(\mathrm{GM})})^{\bm{\ell}}\right]=\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\rho^{(\mathrm{GM})}}(g).\qed

Composition theorems yield boosters for property testing

8.1
A general boosting framework

Let
\mathcal{P}=\left\{\mathcal{P}_{s}\right\}_{s\in\mathds{N}}
be a parametrized property of Boolean functions. For a function
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
and distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, we write

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{D}}(f,\mathcal{P}_{s})\coloneqq\min_{h\in\mathcal{P}_{s}}\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}}(f,h)

to denote
f
’s distance to
\mathcal{P}_{s}
over
\mathcal{D}
. We are interested in the
relaxed
testing regime for size parameters
s>s^{\prime}
where we want to decide whether an unknown target function
f
belongs to
\mathcal{P}_{s}
or is
\varepsilon
-far from
\mathcal{P}_{s^{\prime}}
under
\mathcal{D}
:
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{D}}(f,\mathcal{P}_{s^{\prime}})>\varepsilon
(recall
Definition

4
). We say that
\mathcal{P}
is
(\varepsilon,s,s^{\prime})
-testable if there exists an algorithm for
(\varepsilon,s,s^{\prime})
-testing
\mathcal{P}
for every distribution
\mathcal{D}
. As
\varepsilon\to 0
, the gap between the Yes and No cases becomes smaller and
(\varepsilon,s,s^{\prime})
-testing becomes more difficult. The main result of this section is that if
\mathcal{P}
“behaves well” under function composition, then testers for large
\varepsilon
can be boosted to testers for the more challenging regime of small
\varepsilon
. We will specialize our attention to properties which behave
linearly
with respect to function composition.

Definition 11

(Linear with respect to composition)
.

A parametrized property
\mathcal{P}=\left\{\mathcal{P}_{s}\right\}_{s\in\mathds{N}}
behaves linearly (with respect to function composition) if

f\in\mathcal{P}_{s}\quad\Rightarrow\quad g\circ f\in\mathcal{P}_{k\cdot s}

for all
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
,
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
, and
s\in\mathds{N}
.

Examples.

Being an
s
-junta, depth-
s
decision tree, depth-
s
formula, or degree-
s
polynomial are all properties of Boolean functions which behave linearly with respect to composition. As is often the case, it is straightforward to show from their definitions that these properties behave linearly. Many properties which do not
a priori
behave linearly can be converted into ones that do by applying an appropriate transformation to their size. For example, the property
\mathcal{P}_{s}=\{\text{size-exp}(s)\text{ decision trees}\}
behaves linearly.

Strong composition theorems for properties.

A property
\mathcal{P}
which behaves linearly with respect to function composition is said to admit a
strong composition theorem
if the upper bound from
Definition

11
can be shown to be nearly tight. This definition generalizes the relation

♢

‣
2.1
.

Definition 12

(
(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{small}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{large}},\lambda)
-composition theorem)
.

A parametrized property
\mathcal{P}=\left\{\mathcal{P}_{s}\right\}_{s\in\mathds{N}}
admits an
(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{small}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{large}},\lambda)
-composition theorem with respect to
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
for
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{small}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{large}}\in(0,1)
and a constant
\lambda>0
if

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{D}}(f,\mathcal{P}_{s})>\varepsilon_{\mathrm{small}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathrm{dist}_{\mathcal{D}^{k}}(g\circ f,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda ks})>\varepsilon_{\mathrm{large}}

for all
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
and distributions
\mathcal{D}
over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.

Strong composition theorems depend on the combining function
g
. For example, if
g
is a constant function then one would not expect the upper bound from
Definition

11
to be tight. For this reason, the dependence on
g
is made explicit in the definition of strong composition theorem.
Roughly speaking, the definition says that if a property
\mathcal{P}
behaves linearly and admits a strong composition theorem with respect to
g
, then composing with
g
turns a function in
\mathcal{P}_{s}
into one in
\mathcal{P}_{sk}
and turns a function slightly far from
\mathcal{P}_{s}
into one very far from
\mathcal{P}_{\Theta(sk)}
. For a fixed
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{large}}
, having an
(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{small}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{large}},\lambda)
-composition theorem with respect to
g
becomes stronger as
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{small}}
approaches
0
. In general, we are interested in
(\varepsilon_{\mathrm{small}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{large}},\lambda)
-composition theorems when
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{large}}\gg\varepsilon_{\mathrm{small}}
. The parameter
\lambda
is built into the definition to tolerate a small amount of slack between the upper and lower bounds on
g\circ f
. For many applications, this constant factor is necessary. We are now equipped to state our main boosting theorem.

Theorem 4

(Boosting property testers, formal version of

\mathcal{D}

Lemma 8.1

Our proof of

Proposition 8.2

(The multivariate noise stability of parity)
.

For any

,

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(\mathrm{XOR}_{k})=\prod_{i\in[k]}\left(\vec{\rho}_{i}+(1-\vec{\rho}_{i})\cdot\mu^{2}\right)=\prod_{i\in[k]}\left(1-(1-\vec{\rho}_{i})(1-\mu^{2})\right).

Proof.

Note that

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(\mathrm{XOR}_{k})=\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{y}\sim(\pi_{\mu})^{k},\bm{z}\overset{\vec{\rho}}{\sim}\bm{y}}\left[\prod_{i\in[k]}\bm{y}_{i}\bm{z}_{i}\right].

Each pair

are independent of another, so

\mathrm{Stab}_{\mu,\vec{\rho}}(\mathrm{XOR}_{k})=\prod_{i\in[k]}\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\bm{y}_{i}\bm{z}_{i}\right].

The distribution of

can be succinctly described: With probability

\bm{z}_{i}=\bm{y}_{i}
. Otherwise, they are each independent draws from

. Therefore,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}\left[\bm{y}_{i}\bm{z}_{i}\right]=\vec{\rho}_{i}+(1-\vec{\rho}_{i})\cdot\mu^{2}.

The desired result follows from combining the above equations

. Then, for any choice of

\operatorname{{Pr}}\left[\sum_{i\in[k-1]}\bm{y}_{i}=c\right]\leq O\left(1/\sqrt{k}\right).

We now give the junta achieving low error.

Proposition A.3

(
Lemma

A.1
, there is a low error junta)
.

Let

. Then,

1.

h
is an
((k-1)n\leq R)
-junta.

2.

h
achieves,

\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}^{k}}(g\circ f,h)\leq O(1/\sqrt{k}).

Proof.

Clearly
h
depends on only the first

bits of its inputs, so it is an
R
-junta as long as
(k-1)n\leq(n-1)k
, which is guaranteed by the assumption

in

A.1
. We compute
h

\mathrm{error}_{\mathcal{D}^{k}}(g\circ f,h)=\operatorname{{Pr}}_{\bm{y}\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}[\textsc{Maj}_{k}(\bm{y})\neq\textsc{Maj}_{k-1}(\bm{y})].

In order for
\textsc{Maj}_{k}(\bm{y})\neq\textsc{Maj}_{k-1}(\bm{y})
, it must be the case that the

0

A.2
.
∎

Proposition A.4
.

Counterexample to Conjecture 2

Lemma A.7

Lemma

-1

Proposition A.8
.

For any
n\geq 10
, there is an
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
for which
\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}[f(\bm{x})]\leq 0.5
but, for all
|S|\leq n/2
and
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
,

\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}[f(\bm{x})\mid\bm{x}=x]>0.

Proof.

Consider a random function
{\bm{f}}
where, for each
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
,
{\bm{f}}(x)\sim\pi_{0.25}
. We’ll show that
{\bm{f}}
meets the desired criteria with a strictly positive probability, proving the existence of at least one such
f
.

Let
\mu({\bm{f}})\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}[{\bm{f}}(\bm{x})]
. Then
\mu({\bm{f}})
is the average of
2^{n}
independent samples of
\pi_{0.25}
. Applying Hoeffding’s inequality,

\operatorname{{Pr}}[\mu({\bm{f}})>0.5]\leq\exp(-2\cdot(0.25)^{2}\cdot 2^{n})=\exp(-2^{n}/2).

Similarly, for any
|S|\leq n/2
and
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, let
\mu({\bm{f}},S,x)\coloneqq\mathop{{\mathds{E}}\/}_{\bm{x}\sim\{\pm 1\}^{n}}[{\bm{f}}(\bm{x})\mid\bm{x}=x]
.
\mu({\bm{f}},S,x)
the average of at least
2^{n/2}
independent samples of
\pi_{0.25}
. Once again, by Hoeffding’s inequality,

\operatorname{{Pr}}[\mu({\bm{f}},S,x)\leq 0]\leq\exp(-2\cdot(0.25)^{2}\cdot 2^{n/2})=\exp(-2^{n/2}/2).

Union bounding over all
2^{n}
choices of
S
and
2^{n}
choices for
x
, we have that
{\bm{f}}
meets the desired criteria with probability at least

1-\exp(-2^{n}/2)-2^{2n}\exp(-2^{n/2}/2).

When
n\geq 10
, the above probability is strictly positive, so such an
f
must exist.
∎

A.3
Counterexample to Conjecture 3

Lemma A.9
.

There is
g:\{\pm 1\}^{k}\to\{\pm 1\}
,
f:\{\pm 1\}^{n}\to\{\pm 1\}
, distribution
\mathcal{D}
over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, and budget
R
for which no
R
-junta of composed form achieves optimal error among all
R
-Juntas for
g\circ f
with respect to
\mathcal{D}^{k}
.

Proof.

We’ll set
k=2
,
g=\textsc{And}_{2}
. Let
p:\{\pm 1\}^{2}\to[0,1]
be defined as

p(x)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x_{1}=x_{2}=1,\\
3/4&\text{if }x_{1}\neq x_{2},\\
3/5&\text{if }x_{1}=x_{2}=-1.\end{cases}

We begin by describing a probabilistic construction: Given the input
x
, the value of
{\bm{f}}(x)
will still be a random variable. In particular, we set
n=2
, and
{\bm{f}}(x)
is set to
+1
with probability
p(x)
and
-1
otherwise. This probabilistic construction will later be derandomized. We allow a junta budget of
R=4
.

Next, we construct an optimal approximator for
g\circ{\bm{f}}
. Given an input
x^{(1)},x^{(2)}
, let
\bm{y}_{1}={\bm{f}}(x^{(1)})
and
\bm{y}_{2}={\bm{f}}(x^{(2)})
. For succinctness, we’ll use
p_{i}
to refer to the
\operatorname{{Pr}}[\bm{y}_{i}=1]
. Then, since
g=\textsc{And}_{2}
, the optimal approximator will return
1
iff
p_{1}p_{2}\geq 1/2
. For our particular
{\bm{f}}
the only choices for
p_{i}
are
3/5,3/4,1
. As a result,

h^{(\mathrm{opt})}(p_{1},p_{2})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }p_{1}=1\text{ or }p_{2}=1,\\
1&\text{if }p_{1}=p_{2}=3/4,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

However, no composed form can achieve the above optimal approximator. Recall that composed form approximators are of the form
h(q_{1},q_{2})
, where each
q_{i}
has range
\{\pm 1\}
. The fact that the size of this range is
2
, but there are three possible choices
(3/5,3/4,1)
for
p_{i}
, is the crux of the issue.

In more detail, of the three choices
(3/5,3/4,1)
for
p_{i}
,
q_{1}
must classify at least two of them the same way. This gives three cases.

1.

If
q_{1}
classifies
3/4
and
1
the same way,
h(q_{1},q_{2})
cannot distinguish between
p_{1}=3/4,p_{2}=3/5
and
p_{1}=1,p_{2}=3/5
, and so cannot be optimal.

2.

If
q_{1}
classifies
3/5
and
3/4
the same way,
h(q_{1},q_{2})
cannot distinguish between
p_{1}=3/4,p_{2}=3/4
and
p_{1}=3/5,p_{2}=3/4
, and so cannot be optimal.

3.

If
q_{1}
classifies
3/5
and
1
the same way,
h(q_{1},q_{2})
cannot distinguish between
p_{1}=3/5,p_{2}=3/4
and
p_{1}=1,p_{2}=3/4
, and so cannot be optimal.

In all three cases composed form cannot achieve optimal error. It will always be off by some constant.

To derandomize this construction, we set
n\gg 2
sufficiently large. For each
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
, we sample the value
f(x)
to be
+1
with probability
p(x_{1},x_{2})
and
-1
otherwise. Note that after randomly selecting the value of
f
on each input
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
,
f
is now a deterministic function. Following the same arguments as in
Proposition

A.8
, with high probability over the random choices in defining
f
, the error of the optimal
4
-junta and of the optimal composed form
4
-junta for
g\circ f
are within
\pm\varepsilon(n)
of what they are for
g\circ{\bm{f}}
, where
\varepsilon(n)
goes to
0
as
n\to\infty
. Therefore, for sufficiently large
n
, there exists an
f
meeting the desired criteria.
∎
