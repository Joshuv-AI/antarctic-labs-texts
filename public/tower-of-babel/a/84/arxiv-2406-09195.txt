On the statistical analysis of grouped data: when Pearson χ 2 \chi^{2} and other divisible statistics are not goodness-of-fit tests
Sara Algeri 1 and Estate V. Khmaladze 2

Abstract
Thousands of experiments are analyzed and papers are published each year involving the statistical analysis of grouped data. While this area of statistics is often perceived – somewhat naively – as saturated, several misconceptions still affect everyday practice, and new frontiers have so far remained unexplored. Researchers must be aware of the limitations affecting their analyses and what are the new possibilities in their hands. Motivated by this need, the article introduces a unifying approach to the analysis of grouped data, which allows us to study the class of divisible statistics – that includes Pearson’s χ 2 \chi^{2} , the likelihood ratio as special cases – with a fresh perspective. The contributions collected in this manuscript span from modeling and estimation to distribution-free goodness-of-fit tests. Perhaps the most surprising result presented here is that, in a sparse regime, all tests proposed in the literature are dominated by members of the class of weighted linear statistics.



keywords
Goodness-of-fit, Divisible statistics, Grouped data, Counting experiments, small groups, Poisson statistics, Empirical processes, Distribution-free tests

1
Introduction

The rise of goodness-of-fit in statistics can be traced back to a correspondence between Edgeworth and Pearson at the end of the 19th century
(
Stigler 1990
, cf.)
on testing regarding the multinomial distribution of the 37 numbers on the roulette wheel, which led to Pearson’s seminal paper on the
\chi^{2}
-test
(
Pearson 1900
)
. The latter can be considered one of the pillars of data analysis and, similarly to other foundational tools, it is still dominant in everyday practice.

Given
K
mutually exclusive groups or cells, labeled by
x_{k}
, let
\nu(x_{k})
be the frequencies observed in each group.
Pearson’s
\chi^{2}
aims to test the hypothesis

H_{0}:E[\nu(x_{k})]=m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})\quad\text{ for all $k\in\{1,\dots,K\}$}

(1)

where the expected frequencies,
m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})
, reflect our understanding of the phenomenon under study and may depend on a – possibly unknown – parameter
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The prescribed test statistic is

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\bigl(\nu(x_{k})-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})\bigl)^{2}}{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})},

and it is known to be
\chi^{2}
distributed under
H_{0}
if
\min_{x_{k}}\{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})\}\rightarrow\infty
.

Following Pearson’s result, many different statistics have been introduced in the literature as substitutes to the
\chi^{2}
-test or supplementary to it
(
Cochran 1952
, cf.,)
.
They can be classified into two main kinds: those requiring the data to be in a grouped form and those that apply to continuous data.

Theoretical unification in the analysis of continuous data.
An important feature of many goodness-of-fit statistics for continuous data is that they can be specified as functionals of the
empirical process
.
Letting
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
be i.i.d. realizations of a real-valued random variable
Y
, distributed according to the distribution
F
, the empirical process
w_{n}(y)
is such that

w_{n}(y)=\sqrt{n}[F_{n}(y)-F(y)],

with
F_{n}(y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}_{\{y_{i}\leq y\}}
being the empirical distribution function. A prominent example of a goodness-of-fit test based on
w_{n}(y)
is the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic
(
Kolmogorov 1933
;
Smirnov 1939
)

\sup_{y}|w_{n}(y)|.

Also very prominent are the Cramer-von Mises
(
Smirnov 1937
)
and Anderson-Darling
(
Anderson and Darling 1954
)
statistics given by

\int w^{2}_{n}(y)dF(y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathop{\text{\Large$\int$}}\nolimits\hskip-2.84544pt\frac{w^{2}_{n}(y)}{F(y)(1-F(y))}dF(y),

respectively. Several other constructs used in the analysis of continuous data can be expressed as linear functionals of
w_{n}(y)

(
Wellner 1992
, e.g.,)
. Such unification has led to notable advancements in different areas of statistics and has enabled developments in the theory and practice of goodness-of-fit, which continue at present

(
Henze and Meintanis 2005
;
Khmaladze 2016
;
Moscovich et al. 2016
;
Durio and Nikitin 2016
;
Algeri 2022
;
Dümbgen and Wellner 2023
;
Roberts et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
.

An attempt of unification in the analysis of grouped data.
Alternatives to Pearson in the analysis of grouped data include the likelihood ratio

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nu(x_{k})\ln\frac{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})}{\nu(x_{k})},

the linear statistic

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bigl[\nu(x_{k})-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})\bigl],

and the (cumulative) spectral statistic

\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathds{1}_{\{\nu(x_{k})\leq q\}}\quad\text{with $q\in\mathbb{N}$,}

which plays a central role in occupancy and diversity problems
(
Magurran 1988
;
Mirakhmedov 2007
;
Barbour and Gnedin 2009
;
Khmaladze 2011
, e.g.,)
.
The commonality of these and many other tests is that they can all be expressed as the sum of a function of the observed and expected frequencies. They have been referred to in the literature
as ‘divisible statistics’ (or sometimes ‘separable statistics’)
(
Medvedev 1970
, cf.)
.

Given a function
g(z,t)
, usually differentiable in
t
, a divisible statistic based on such a function has the form

\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}g\bigl(\nu(x_{k}),m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})\bigl).

(2)

The study of the class of divisible statistics as such began in the last quarter of the 20th century

(
Ivchenko and Medvedev 1979
;
Ivchenko and Medvedev 1981
;
Khmaladze 1984
;
Mnatsakanov 1986
;
Mnatsakanov 1988
, e.g.,)
.
Of particular interest is their behavior in the high-dimensional regime, also explored in recent literature on minimax optimal tests
(
Balakrishnan and Wasserman 2018
;
Balakrishnan and Wasserman 2019
;
Cai et al. 2024
, e.g.,)
, in which the number of cells is not fixed and grows with the size of the sample.

Formally, letting
T=\sum_{k=1}^{K}m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})
be the expected sample size, we assume that
K
and
T
increase simultaneously so that

\frac{T}{K}\to c\in(0,\infty),\quad\text{ as $T\rightarrow\infty$ and $K\rightarrow\infty$,}

(3)

and
c
can be interpreted as the average number of expected events per cell.
Note that, differently from the case in which
T
grows at a faster rate than
K
, by assuming
T
and
K
have the same order of magnitude, we allow the frequencies to be potentially small and, thus, not necessarily Gaussian in the limit. The condition in (
3
) is therefore necessary
to describe many commonly encountered phenomena in which replicate observations are available, but only in a limited number. For example,
in Edgeworth and Pearson’s epistolary communication, the roulette test relied on the normal approximation of the frequencies. This is sensible since the underlying distribution has 36 dimensions. But if we were to ask the same question today considering, instead of a roulette wheel, a poker machine with about 20 images on 15 wheels, this would give us
20^{15}\sim 10^{19}
dimensions or cells, which could not be easily filled in any real experiment.
In the statistical analysis of large texts, the asymptotic described by (
3
) is practically unavoidable. In a text composed of several hundred thousand words, more than
90\%
of the words that constitute its vocabulary are used no more than
10
times each, and about
50\%
of the words are used only once
(
Baayen 2001
, e.g.,)
. In sociological surveys, the diversity of opinions is studied through questionnaires, and the situation is similar: in a questionnaire of, say,
15
binary questions, the number of different possible opinions, or groups, is
2^{15}\sim 33,000
. In a sample of
100,000
respondents, about
78\%
of different opinions will be expressed no more than
10
times; in balanced questionnaires, the probability of the answer ‘yes’ is close to
1/2
and
c
will be very close to
3
or
4

(
Khmaladze 2011
, e.g.,)
. The asymptotics in (
3
) are also typical in occupation problems in environmental ecology. In this field, most models involve a large number of different species populating a certain region. When an observational experiment is conducted over a given period of time, the number of different species observed is also large, but individuals from the same species may only be observed a handful of times
(
Magurran 1988
, e.g.,)
.
As discussed in detail below, the assumption in (
3
) is also relevant in counting experiments in physics and astronomy.
In these and many other instances, researchers have so far been forced to group their data in large groups to use classical asymptotic theory in which
c
increases. This manuscript demonstrates that this grouping is not necessary. An asymptotic theory under (
3
) very much exists, although it is not the same theory as in the case in which the frequencies are accumulated.

The existing literature on the class of divisible statistics focuses on goodness-of-fit for simple hypotheses. One of the most notable results under (
3
) is that, if
\theta
is known, no divisible statistic can detect all local departures from the null, somewhat invalidating their adequacy for goodness-of-fit. However, power can be restored by considering partial sums
(
Khmaladze 1984
, cf.)
. The effect of parameter estimation, on the other hand, has only been investigated on a case-by-case basis
(
Cressie and Read 1989
, cf.,)
or for sub-classes such as that of power-divergence statistics
(
Cressie and Read 1984
;
Müller and Osius 2003
)
.
No parametric result on the class of divisible statistics as a whole has yet been introduced, nor has an analog to the empirical process been proposed for the analysis of grouped data.

Divisible statistics anew: statistical motivation and relevance to the physical sciences.
The primary theoretical motivation of this manuscript is the need to unify the theory of statistical inference for grouped data to an extent comparable to what is enabled by the theory of empirical processes in the continuous regime.
In Section
3
, we show that it is possible to express different statistics involved in the analysis of grouped data as linear functionals based on the same random measure. This construction not only brings the desired unification but also unveils new phenomena, not visible in the analysis of i.i.d. observations. For example, a somewhat unanticipated result is presented in Section
6.4
: in a sparse regime, all tests based on divisible statistics are dominated by weighted linear statistics.

In what follows, we assume the observed frequencies are independent Poisson random variables and (
3
) holds – that is, asymptotically, the frequencies remain Poisson and do not reach the Gaussian limit. We also assume that, given a compact subset of
\mathcal{X}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with Lebesgue measure
|\mathcal{X}|
, the grouping leads to an exhaustive partition of
\mathcal{X}
into bins.

Figure 1:
X
-ray source-free spectrum from a Chandra observation and discretized in
350
bins. The blue solid and red dashed lines are, respectively, the best fits obtained for constant, linear, and piecewise linear mean functions.

These assumptions are especially relevant in the physical sciences.
Specifically, in physics and astronomy, the detector mechanism often leads to a discretization of the area under study. As a result, the data consists of event counts observed in the bins – be it channels, pixels, voxels, or other segments – of the discretized space. Moreover, even when the detector resolution is so high that it leads to a nearly continuous data stream, the statistical analysis is often conducted by partitioning the data into bins. This choice may be dictated by the need for fast computations and numerical stability when fitting the models under study, to improve the signal-to-noise ratio, and/or to simplify the model assessment stage.
In
X
-ray astronomy, for example, the images and the spectra collected by the Chandra
X
-ray Observatory
(
Swartz et al. 2010
)
are used to study the properties of celestial objects. In high-energy physics, the energy spectra produced by the Large Hadron Collider are at the core of
searches for new physics beyond the Standard Model; of particular interest are thinly populated high-energy bins in which the new phenomena are often expected to appear
(
Belfonte et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
. In astroparticle physics, the images and the spectra generated by the Fermi Large Area Telescope
(
Atwood et al. 2009
)
enable the study of subatomic particles. In these settings, the data consists of Poisson-distributed photon counts observed over many bins.
Hence, the goal is to estimate and validate the hypothesized mean function of a Poisson process defined over a discretized space.

Extensions to the multinomial scheme or situations in which the cells are defined by categorical variables are also possible in the proposed setup.

1.1
An analysis of data from the Chandra
X
-ray observatory

Throughout this manuscript, we consider data from the Chandra
X
-ray observatory to demonstrate the practical implications of our findings in scientific research and illustrate their implementation.

The Chandra
X
-ray Observatory is an
X
-ray telescope orbiting the Earth since its launch in 1999. It captures and probes
X
-rays from astronomical sources over a wide wavelength range. The analysis of
X
-ray spectra obtained with Chandra provides crucial information about the chemical composition, temperature, distance from the Earth, and other features of astronomical sources.
In this manuscript, we consider an
X
-ray spectrum relevant to the study of RT Cru, a star belonging to a rare class of
X
-ray-emitting symbiotic systems. The origin of these systems remains poorly understood, making RT Cru a target of significant astronomical interest.

While previous studies made important contributions in the study of RT Cru
(
Luna and Sokoloski 2007
;
Danehkar et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
, many questions have remained unaddressed due to substantial background contamination in the
15-20\r{A}
(Angstroms) range. In astronomy, the term ‘background’ refers to the combined signal of all astrophysical and cosmic sources, which are not those we aim to study.
When analyzing data with significant background, accurate modeling of its distribution is crucial. For RT Cru,
Zhang et al. 2023
conducted a comprehensive statistical analysis to validate and recalibrate proposed background models using ‘source-free spectra’ – that is, data collected in an off-source region of the detector. Our present analysis examines a subset of the data used in their study; it consists of a source-free spectrum in the
14.6-17.4\r{A}
range. In this region, the hypothesized uniform background model was rejected, and a linear correction for it was recommended.
The analysis in this manuscript demonstrates the efficacy of the proposed methods in validating and complementing such findings.

Given the high resolution of the detector, the original dataset is unbinned; thus, in principle, one could attempt to perform an unbinned data analysis. However, the study of high-resolution
X
-ray spectra is typically conducted by binning the data
(
Luna and Sokoloski 2007
;
Karovska et al. 2010
;
Bonamente 2020
, e.g.,)
. Moreover, when analyzing low-resolution
X
-ray spectra, the data observed are naturally binned, with at least some of the bin counts being small.
Keeping these aspects in mind, we choose to discretize the spectrum considered in 750 bins. As shown in Fig.
1
, the bins average approximately seven counts, with many containing fewer than five counts. Such a choice allows us to demonstrate how the methods proposed in this manuscript can be used to test models for both low-resolution and sparsely binned high-resolution spectra while warning practitioners about their limitations.

2
Modeling framework

To formally define the quantities introduced in Section
1
, consider a Poisson process
N_{{}_{T}}{\hskip-1.42271pt}(A),A\subseteq\mathcal{X},
with mean

E[N_{{}_{T}}{\hskip-1.42271pt}(A)]=T\Lambda_{\beta}(A)=T\int_{A}\lambda_{\beta}(x)dx,

(4)

where
\lambda_{\beta}(x)
is the function describing the spatial spread of events over
\mathcal{X}
which depends on
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
. The parameter
T
determines the size of the sample – that is, in our context, ‘large samples’ means
T\rightarrow\infty
.
It is reasonable to assume that
\lambda_{\beta}(x)
is independent of
T
and

\int_{\mathcal{X}}\lambda_{\beta}(x)dx=1\quad\text{for all }\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}.

We also assume that
\lambda_{\beta}
is piece-wise continuous. Without loss of generality, let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite union of
d
-dimensional hypercubes. When the region of interest is not rectangular but merely a bounded set in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we can cover it with a finite union of cubes,
\mathcal{R}
, and assume
\lambda_{\beta}(x)=0
for all
x\in\mathcal{R}\setminus\mathcal{X}
.
In other words, we can construct a grid on
\mathcal{X}
composed of
K
disjoint squares, or bins, denoted by
\{\Delta[x_{k}]\}_{k=1}^{K}
and centered at points
x_{k}
. Assume all the bins have the same volume

\delta=\frac{|\mathcal{X}|}{K}.

The observed frequencies correspond to the increments of the process
N_{{}_{T}}{\hskip-1.42271pt}(A)
over each bin, i.e.,

\nu(x_{k})=N_{{}_{T}}{\hskip-1.42271pt}(\Delta[x_{k}]),\quad\text{for $k=1,\dots,K$},

where the dependence of
\nu(x_{k})
on
T
is dropped to simplify the notation. Their expectation is

m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})=T\int_{\Delta[x_{k}]}\hskip-7.11317pt\lambda_{\beta}(x)dx\sim T\delta\lambda_{\beta}(x_{k})\sim c|\mathcal{X}|\lambda_{\beta}(x_{k}),

(5)

in which the first equivalence follows from the Lebesgue differentiation theorem
(
Rudin 1987
)
and the second follows from (
3
).
The collection of the frequencies
\{\nu(x_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}
, which, from some point of view, one would
like to call a Poisson brush, is an unusual object. Indeed,
\nu(x_{k})
counts the number of jump
points of
N_{T}
in the bin
\Delta[x_{k}]
; yet, as the bin shrinks, these points do not vanish because
T
increases at the same time; but where do the limiting jump points live? The answer is, we believe, associated with the notion of fold-up derivatives of shrinking sets and the local Poisson processes (cf.

Khmaladze 2007
,
Khmaladze and Weil 2008
). However, we will not digress in this direction here.

In practice,
\beta
is often unknown or unspecified. Moreover, from a technical standpoint, it is convenient to treat
T
as an unknown parameter to be estimated, along with
\beta
. Since
T
may grow without limit, we estimate it in relative terms by considering
c\sim\frac{T}{K}
. Therefore,
\theta=(c,\beta)^{\sf T}\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the focus of estimation.

3
Divisible statistics as linear functionals based on the same random measure

As described in Section
1
, classical goodness-of-fit statistics for testing (
1
) fall under the class of divisible statistics defined as in (
2
). This definition, however, does not include other important statistics encountered in the analysis of binned data, especially in the context of estimation.

To bring unification to the theory and broaden the class of divisible statistics, we propose a revision of its traditional definition to include any statistic that can be expressed as a linear functional of the random measure:

v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(A,z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathds{1}_{\{x_{k}\in A\}}\hskip-1.42271pt\Bigl[\mathds{1}_{\{\nu(x_{k})\leq z\}}-P(z|m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k}))\Bigl],\quad A\subseteq\mathcal{X},z\in\mathbb{N}.

(6)

in which
P(z|t)
denotes the Poisson distribution function with mean
t
.

Let
m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
be a step-wise constant function defined as

m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)=m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})\quad\text{for all $x\in\Delta[x_{k}]$}

and denote with
E_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt[\cdot]
the expectation taken with respect to a Poisson random variable,
\nu(x)
, with mean
m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
.
From (
5
) it follows that
m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
converges to
c|\mathcal{X}|\lambda_{\beta}(x)
.
Consider now a more general class of divisible statistics with summands in
(
2
) of the form

g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k},\nu(x_{k}))=g(x_{k},\nu(x_{k}),m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k}))
–
that is, each term explicitly depend on the variable
x_{k}
. Hence,
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)
constitutes the ‘inhomogenous’ version of
g(z,m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x))
. For example,
when different weights are assigned to different bins, we may choose

g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)=\omega(x)g(z,m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)),

(7)

leading back to the ‘homogeneous’ divisible statistics in (
2
) when
\omega(x)=1
.
A divisible statistic defined by a function
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)
is

v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)=\int\hskip-5.69046pt\int\hskip-1.42271ptg_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(dx,dz)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Bigl[g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\bigl(x_{k},\nu(x_{k})\bigl)-E_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\bigl[g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k},\nu(x_{k}))\bigl]\Bigl],

(8)

where the integral is a random Stieltjes integral and
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
is a direct analog of the function-parametric empirical process
(e.g.,
van der Vaart 2000
, Ch 19)
.
For instance, estimating equations are divisible statistics of the form in (
8
) equated to zero, and
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
can be chosen as in (
7
), with
\omega(x)
depending on
\theta
.
Partial sums can be constructed similarly by choosing
\omega(x)=\mathds{1}_{\{x\in A\}}
.
For fixed
A
and
z
,
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(A,z)
is itself a divisible statistic, the localised (and centered) cumulative spectral statistic. It counts the number of bins in
A
with frequencies
\nu(x_{k})
less than or equal to
z
while centering the random terms,
\mathds{1}_{\{\nu(x_{k})\leq z\}}
, by their expectation.

The stochastic representation in (
8
) unfolds the intrinsic nature of all divisible statistics. Even those statistics that may at first appear highly non-linear – such as Pearson’s
\chi^{2}
– are, unavoidably, linear functionals of the random measure
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt
. As demonstrated in Section
6
, this inherent linear structure inhibits divisible statistics from performing goodness-of-fit testing.

To better understand the connection between the random measure
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt
and the classical empirical process for i.i.d. observations, rewrite (
6
) as

v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(A,z)=\sqrt{K}\biggl[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathds{1}_{\{x_{k}\in A\}}\mathds{1}_{\{\nu(x_{k})\leq z\}}-\mu_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(A,z)\biggl]

(9)

with

\mu_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(A,z)=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathds{1}_{\{x_{k}\in A\}}P(z|m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k}))=\frac{\delta}{|\mathcal{X}|}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathds{1}_{\{x_{k}\in A\}}P(z|m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})).

In (
9
), the first average in
k
serves a purpose similar to that of the empirical distribution function.
The expected value of such empirical distribution, given the collection of
x_{k}
, is the measure
\mu_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(A,z)
. The latter assigns to each bin in
A
the Poisson probability
P(z|m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k}))
.
As
K\rightarrow\infty
, the marginal
\mu_{{}_{K}}\hskip-1.42271pt(A)=\mu_{{}_{K}}\hskip-1.42271pt(A,\infty)
converges to
\mu(A)=|A|/|\mathcal{X}|
, the normalized Lebesgue measure, or uniform distribution, on
\mathcal{X}
and
\mu_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(A,z)
converges weakly to

\mu_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(A,z)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}|}\int_{A}P(z|m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x))dx.

For example,
as
K\to\infty
, we have

\displaystyle\int\hskip-5.69046pt\int\hskip-2.84544ptg_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)\mu_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(dx,dz)=\hskip-2.84544pt\int\hskip-2.84544ptE_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\bigl[g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,\nu(x))\bigl]\mu_{{}_{K}}\hskip-1.42271pt(dx)\to\hskip-2.84544pt\int\hskip-5.69046pt\int\hskip-1.42271ptg_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)\mu_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(dx,dz)=\hskip-2.84544pt\int\hskip-2.84544ptE_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\bigl[g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,\nu(x))\bigl]\mu(dx).

The stochastic representation in (
8
) implies that – similarly to the i.i.d. setting in which classes of statistics can be expressed as values of function-parametric empirical processes
(cf.
van der Vaart 2000
, Ch.19)
–
the class of divisible statistics becomes the function-parametric process based on
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt
in (
6
). The natural range for the functions
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
indexing such a process is the Hilbert space

{\mathcal{L}}_{2}(\mu_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt)=\{g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z):\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,1\rangle=0,\|g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\|<\infty\}

with inner product
\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\prime}\rangle=\int\hskip-5.69046pt\int\hskip-1.42271ptg_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\prime}(x,z){\mu_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt}(dx,dz)
and norm
\|g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\|=\sqrt{\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\rangle}
. The orthogonality condition
\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,1\rangle=0
indicates that we can restrict our attention to functions
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
that are centered. Moreover, the representation in (
8
) and the central limit theorem imply that any divisible statistic is approximately Gaussian with variance
E_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt[g^{2}_{{}_{\theta}}]=||g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt||^{2}
.
Seemingly different statistics, such as Pearson’s
\chi^{2}
statistic and the spectral statistic, can be expressed as the values of the same function-parametric process
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
. They are, therefore, not so different and are subject to the same treatment.

4
Divisible statistics with estimated parameters

When testing the validity of a given model
m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
without a prescribed value of
\theta
, one would need to replace the latter with an estimator. Therefore, it is useful to study how parameter estimation affects the structure of divisible statistics.

Let
b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
be a
p
-dimensional vector function with linearly independent components in
{\mathcal{L}}_{2}(\mu_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
and consider the system of estimating equations

v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(b_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt)=0.

(10)

For example, when using Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE),
b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
is equal to the score function

\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\ln p(z|m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x))=\frac{\dot{m}_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}(z-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)),

with
p(z|t)
denoting the Poisson probability mass function with rate
t
and
\dot{m}_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
. When estimating
\theta
via least squares, the minimization

\widehat{\theta}=\arg\min_{\theta}\sum_{k=1}^{K}(\nu(x_{k})-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k}))^{2},

leads to

b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)=-2\dot{m}_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)(z-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x))
.

Under mild regularity conditions
(e.g.,
van der Vaart 2000
, Chapter 5)
, there exists a consistent root
\widehat{\theta}
of (
10
) and for this estimator the Taylor expansion

v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt)=v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)+\sqrt{K}(\widehat{\theta}-\theta)^{\sf T}\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\int\hskip-5.69046pt\int g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(dx,dz)+o_{{}_{P}}(1)

is valid for
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\in{\mathcal{L}}_{2}(\mu_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
. Also, assume that the differentiation in
\theta
can be moved under the integral sign and the coordinates of

\dot{g}_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)
belong to
{\mathcal{L}}_{2}(\mu_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
, then

\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\int\hskip-5.69046pt\int g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(dx,dz)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\dot{g}_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)-\int\hskip-5.69046pt\int g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\mu_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(dx,dz)

in which the first term on the right-hand side is
o_{p}(1)
and the second term is

\begin{split}\int\hskip-5.69046pt\int g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}P(dz|m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x))\mu_{{}_{K}}\hskip-1.42271pt(dx)&=\int E_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\bigl[g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,\nu(x))\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,\nu(x))\bigl]\mu_{{}_{K}}\hskip-1.42271pt(dx)=\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\rangle.\end{split}

Therefore, the Taylor expansion above can be rewritten as

v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt)=v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)-\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle\sqrt{K}(\widehat{\theta}-\theta)+o_{{}_{P}}(1).

(11)

The components of
b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
live in
{\mathcal{L}}_{2}(\mu_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
, thus, the asymptotic representation in (
11
) can also be applied to the left-hand side of the estimating equations in (
10
). By equating the resulting expansion to zero, we obtain,

\begin{split}\sqrt{K}(\widehat{\theta}-\theta)=\langle b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle^{-1}v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)+o_{{}_{P}}\hskip-1.42271pt(1)\end{split}

(12)

and substituting the right-hand side of this equality into (
11
) gives

\begin{split}v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt)&=v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)-\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle\langle b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle^{-1}v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)+o_{{}_{P}}(1).\\
\end{split}

(13)

The leading term of the right-hand side involves values of
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt
at two functions,
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
and
b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
. However, it is more natural and fruitful to view it as the value of
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt
at one function,
\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
, so that

v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt\bigl(\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\bigl)=v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)-\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle\langle b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle^{-1}v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)

where

\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)=g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)-\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle\langle b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle^{-1}b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z).

(14)

According to Proposition
1
,
\Pi
has a specific nature – it is a projector operator.

Proposition 1

The linear transformation in (
14
)
is a projection of
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
parallel to the function
b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
, which defines the estimating equations, and orthogonal to the score function
\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
, i.e.,

\langle\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\rangle=0\quad\text{and}\quad\Pi b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)=0.

(15)

The projector
\Pi
is orthogonal when
b_{{}_{\theta}}=\psi_{{}_{\theta}}
.

One can show that
\Pi
is a projection operator by verifying that

\Pi\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt=\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
.
The second equality in (
15
) tells us that
b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
belongs to the kernel of the operator
\Pi
. When
b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\neq\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
, however, the kernel of
\Pi
does not coincide with the orthogonal complement of its image. Hence, in general, the projection is not orthogonal, but, as noted in Proposition
1
, it is when the parameters are estimated via MLE. For the latter, the leading term on the right-hand side of (
13
) can be expressed as

v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)=v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,s_{j}\rangle v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(s_{j}),

in which
s_{j}\hskip-0.56917pt
denotes the
j
-th coordinate of the orthonormalized score function

s(x,z)=\langle\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle^{-1/2}\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)

and
\langle\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle^{-1/2}
is the inverse square root of the Fisher information matrix.

The existence of the projection operator
\Pi
, characterizing divisible statistics with estimated parameters, has implications of both practical and theoretical interest.
For instance, as formalized in Proposition
6.3
in Section
6.1
, it suggests that the random variable
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
should be stochastically smaller than
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
, and thus,
substituting
\widehat{\theta}
in
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt
changes the entire class of divisible statistics, making them stochastically smaller.
It is also the key fact that enables the construction of distribution-free tests, with known asymptotic distribution, as described in Section
7.3
.
Moreover,
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
belongs to the class of divisible statistics defined by (
8
); hence,
v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt)
is approximately Gaussian with mean zero and variance
\|\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\|^{2}
.

Let us now choose the function
b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
defining the estimating equations in (
10
) equal to the right-hand side of (
7
) with the weighting function
\omega_{{}_{\theta}}
depending on
\theta
. That is, for a given choice of
g
, we consider
the class of estimators defined by

v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\omega_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}g)=0.

(16)

For instance, when
g
gives the linear statistic, two members of such a class are the MLE and the least squares estimator with
\omega_{{}_{\theta}}(x)=\frac{\dot{m}_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}
and
\omega_{{}_{\theta}}(x)=-2\dot{m}_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
, respectively.

The estimator with the minimum variance among those given by (
16

\omega_{{}_{\theta}}(x)
equal to

\gamma_{{}_{\theta}}(x)=\frac{E_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\bigl[g(\nu(x),m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x))\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,\nu(x))\bigl]}{E_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt[g^{2}(\nu(x),m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x))]}.

(17)

This result is formalized in Proposition
2
.

Proposition 2

Let
\widehat{\theta}
be a consistent root of the estimating equations defined in (
16
)
and let
u
be a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. For a given choice of
g
, the asymptotic variance
of
u^{\sf T}\sqrt{K}\bigl(\widehat{\theta}-\theta\bigr)
is the smallest when
\omega_{{}_{\theta}}=\gamma_{{}_{\theta}}
.

Proof 4.1
.

By the definition of
\gamma_{{}_{\theta}}
we have that

\langle\omega_{{}_{\theta}}g,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle=\langle\omega_{{}_{\theta}}g,g\gamma_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle
. Therefore, the asymptotic representation of
\widehat{\theta}
is

\sqrt{K}(\widehat{\theta}-\theta)\sim\langle\omega_{{}_{\theta}}g,g\gamma_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle^{-1}v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\omega_{{}_{\theta}}g).

(18)

Let us now consider the random variables

\displaystyle V=u^{\sf T}\langle\omega_{{}_{\theta}}g,g\gamma_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle^{-1}v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\omega_{{}_{\theta}}g)\quad\text{and}\quad W=u^{\sf T}\langle\gamma_{{}_{\theta}}g,g\gamma_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle^{-1}v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\gamma_{{}_{\theta}}g).

After some algebra, we obtain that their covariance is

E_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt[VW]=E_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt[W^{2}]
.
Hence,

0\leq E_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\bigl[(V-W)^{2}\bigl]=E_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt[V^{2}]-E_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt[W^{2}];

thus, the variance of
W
is always smaller or equal to that of
V
. ∎

When
g
defines the linear statistic, we have that
\gamma_{{}_{\theta}}g=\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
. When
g
is not linear, for each
x
fixed,
\gamma_{{}_{\theta}}g
gives the best mean-square approximation of
\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
given
g
.
For example, when
g
defines the centred Pearson’s
\chi^{2}
statistic,
\gamma_{{}_{\theta}}g
specifies as

\frac{\dot{m}_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}{2m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)+1}\biggl[\frac{(z-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x))^{2}}{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}-1\biggl],

and for the centred spectral statistic,
\gamma_{{}_{\theta}}g
is

\frac{q-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}{1-p(q|m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x))}\frac{\dot{m}_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}\bigl[\mathds{1}_{\{z\leq q\}}-P(q|m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x))\bigl].

In real-data analyses, statisticians would typically choose a function
g
for goodness-of-fit testing, and, in some instances, they may also want to, or have to, use the same function to estimate the unknown parameters. For instance, in the so-called problem of empty boxes,
q=0
, one has only information of which boxes are empty; the frequencies
\{\nu(x_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}
are not available
(
Gnedin et al. 2007
;
Domanski 2012
, see, e.g.,)
. Yet, an estimation of the parameters is needed. What should be the form of the estimating equations? – Proposition
2
provides the answer.
Nonetheless, as shown in Section
6.1
, when applicable, the MLE is preferable for goodness-of-fit.

4.1
Testing background uniformity in sparse
X
-ray spectra via Pearson’s
\chi^{2}
test

In the analysis of source-free
X
-ray spectra, the mean function is often assumed to be constant
(
Luna and Sokoloski 2007
;
Bonamente 2020
, e.g.,)
, that is,
m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
is identically equal to
c
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. In this setting, the score function is
\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)=\frac{1}{c}(z-c)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
; thus, when
c
is estimated via MLE, we obtain
\widehat{c}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nu(x_{k})}{K}
.

The divisible statistic
v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt)
chosen to test the hypothesis of uniformity of the background is the centered Pearson’s statistic, i.e.,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\biggl[\frac{(\nu(x_{k})-\widehat{c})^{2}}{\widehat{c}}-1\bigg];

hence,
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)=\frac{(z-c)^{2}}{c}-1
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Moreover,

||\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt||^{2}=\frac{1}{c},\quad s(x,z)=\frac{(z-c)}{\sqrt{c}},\quad\|g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\|^{2}=2+\frac{1}{c},\quad\text{and}\quad\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,s\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}.

The theoretical results in Section
4
imply that when the number of bins considered is sufficiently large,
the asymptotic null distribution of Pearson’s statistic is Gaussian with zero mean and variance
\|g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\|^{2}-\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,s\rangle^{2}=2.

Let us now test the hypothesized uniformity of the background density using the source-free spectrum from Chandra in Fig.
1
. For these data,
\widehat{c}=6.947
, and the value of the Pearson’s
\chi^{2}
statistic observed is
-0.852
.
The p-value based on the Gaussian approximation is
0.547
.
Thus, the test completely fails to reject the constant background model.
This result is in contrast with the recommendation provided by
Zhang et al. 2023
. In their study, the authors tested the same hypothesis by means of smooth tests
(cf.
Lehmann and Romano 1986
, Chapter 16)
. Their approach resulted in a rejection of the uniform assumption and proposed the introduction of an additional linear term to suitably model the mean function of the spectrum. The divergence in conclusions, however, is not surprising and, as we shall see in Section
6.3
, can be formally explained from a theoretical standpoint: Pearson’s
\chi^{2}
and many other divisible statistics have no power against weak departures from uniformity.

5
Contiguous alternatives and adequacy for goodness-of-fit

In the context of goodness-of-fit, the usual class of alternatives considered for such a study is that of
contiguous alternatives
. The notion of contiguity, introduced by
Le Cam 1960
, allows us to define sequences of alternatives that converge to the null hypothesis as the sample size increases, thereby describing local deviations from
m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
due to fainter and fainter signals. Yet, these alternatives remain distinguishable from the null hypothesis.

Consider the distribution function

\Lambda_{\beta}(A)=\int_{A}\lambda_{\beta}(x)dx
.
When
\theta
is known, we say that

\widetilde{m}_{{}_{\theta,T}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)=m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)\biggl[1+\frac{h_{{}_{T}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}{\sqrt{T}}\biggl]

(19)

defines a sequence of
contiguous alternatives
to
m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
if,
for a given sequence of functions,
h_{{}_{T}}\hskip-1.42271pt\in L_{2}(\Lambda_{\beta})
, we have

\limsup_{T\to\infty}\|h_{{}_{T}}\|^{2}_{\Lambda_{\beta}}=\limsup_{T\to\infty}\int h^{2}_{{}_{T}}(x)\Lambda_{\beta}(dx)<\infty.

(20)

Moreover, if

\|h_{{}_{T}}-h\|^{2}_{\Lambda_{\beta}}\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{as $T\rightarrow\infty$,}

(21)

for some
h\in L_{2}({\Lambda_{\beta}})
, we say that
\widetilde{m}_{{}_{\theta,T}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
defines a sequence of
converging contiguous alternatives
to
m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
.

The function
h
defining a given sequence of alternatives can be interpreted as the functional direction from which
\widetilde{m}_{{}_{\theta,T}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
approaches
m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
and
\|h\|^{2}_{\Lambda_{\beta}}
can be taken equal to one without loss of essential content. Sequences of
\widetilde{m}_{{}_{\theta,T}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
approaching
m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
from the same direction can be considered equivalent and can be labeled by functions
h
in the unit ball in
L_{2}(\Lambda_{\beta})
.

Figure 2:
Mean functions under the null (black solid lines), alternatives (blue dashed lines), and their component detectable by parametric tests obtained by replacing
h
in (
19
) with
\widehat{h}
in (
23
) (red chained lines) for Example I (left panel), Example II (central panel), and Example III (right panel).

When testing parametric hypotheses, the class of converging contiguous alternatives is much wider compared to the case of simple hypotheses. Such a class may seem to include all sequences of alternatives indexed by functions
h
in the unit ball in any of the spaces
L_{2}(\Lambda_{\beta})
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
. However, this is not quite true. Infinitesimal changes along the tangent spaces of the parametric family
\{\Lambda_{\beta},\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}\}
are now not relevant. As the projection argument of Proposition
1
and Proposition
6.3
in Section
6.1
show, such changes are not detectable by statistics with estimated parameters.
More specifically, we will consider alternatives satisfying the conditions

\begin{split}\int h(x)\Lambda_{\beta}(dx)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\int h(x)\frac{\dot{\lambda}_{\beta}(x)}{\lambda_{\beta}(x)}\Lambda_{\beta}(dx)=0,\end{split}

(22)

with
\dot{\lambda}_{\beta}(x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\lambda_{\beta}(x)
.
The first condition excludes sequences of alternatives that differ from
\lambda_{\beta}(x)
only by a scaling factor and can be accounted for through the estimation of
c
. In other words, we consider alternatives that only affect the spatial spread of the data over
\mathcal{X}
; as a result, the product of
\lambda_{\beta}(x)
and the term in the square brackets in (
19
) integrates to one.
The second condition in (
22
) excludes alternatives indexed by functions
h\in L_{2}(\Lambda_{\beta})
which are linear combinations of the coordinates of
\frac{\dot{\lambda}_{\beta}(x)}{\lambda_{\beta}(x)}
. The latter describes small changes in
\lambda_{\beta}(x)
in
\beta

(
Amari 1985
;
Kass and Vos 2011
, cf.)
.
Such deviations do not describe departures from the parametric family as such and are incorporated into the null through the estimation of
\beta
. Alternatives for which both conditions in (
22
) hold are of the form

\widehat{h}(x)=h(x)-{\frac{\dot{m}_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}}^{\sf T}\Gamma^{-1}_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\int{\frac{\dot{m}_{{}_{\theta}}(y)}{m_{{}_{\theta}}(y)}}h(y)\Lambda_{\beta}(dy),

(23)

with
\Gamma_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt=\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int$}}\nolimits\frac{\dot{m}_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}{\frac{\dot{m}_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}}^{\sf T}\Lambda_{\beta}(dx)=\frac{1}{c}\langle\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle

. In physics and astronomy, when a reliable description of the background is available, a common problem is the detection of a faint ‘bump-like’ signal on top of it. Assume the background density,

\frac{\dot{\lambda}_{\beta}(x)}{\lambda_{\beta}(x)}=\phi(\beta)-\ln x

where

In the literature, the use of contiguous alternatives typically requires the validity of (
21
). This condition is sufficient for contiguity but not necessary. On the contrary, (
20
) is both sufficient and necessary
(
Oosterhoff and Van Zwet 1979
, cf.)
. This implies that there exists a sub-class of alternatives that are contiguous even without (
21
). In this case, it is not possible to identify a direction from which
\widetilde{m}_{{}_{\theta,T}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
approaches
m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
, and thus, the sequence of alternatives diverges or oscillates without limit around the null. These alternatives, interesting in themselves, cannot be detected via goodness-of-fit, neither in the continuous nor in the grouped data regime, but they remain discernible from the null through specifically adjusted empirical processes. For this reason, they have been called
chimeric alternatives

(
Khmaladze 1998
, cf.)
.

Therefore, in the attempt to formally define the adequacy of a statistical test for goodness-of-fit, we rely on the class of converging contiguous alternatives.

Definition 5.1
.

(Adequacy for goodness-of-fit)

We say that a statistical test is adequate for goodness-of-fit if its power exceeds the significance level for all converging contiguous alternatives.

While the expression ‘all converging contiguous alternatives’ is formally correct, given how these alternatives are defined, it may seem an exaggeration. Indeed, all the alternatives considered here preserve the assumption that the observed process is Poisson, i.e., a point process with independent increments. Thus, the statements under the null and the alternative only apply to the intensity of this process.

When testing simple hypotheses in a sparse regime, tests based on a single divisible statistic, including Pearson’s
\chi^{2}
, are known not to satisfy Definition
5.1

(
Khmaladze 1984
, cf.)
. As shown in the sections that follow, the problem persists when testing parametric hypotheses.

In the remainder of the manuscript, we will only consider alternatives within the class of converging contiguous alternatives; thus, we will simply refer to them as ‘alternatives’.

6
On the behavior of divisible statistics under contiguous alternatives

6.1
Distribution of divisible statistics under converging contiguous alternatives

The stochastic representation of divisible statistics in (

v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
are asymptotically Gaussian with zero mean. The limits of their first two moments under the converging contiguous alternatives are given in Proposition

Proposition 6.1
.

Denote with
\widetilde{E}_{{}_{\theta,T}}[\cdot]
and
\widetilde{V}_{{}_{\theta,T}}[\cdot]

\nu(x_{k})
has Poisson distribution with expected value
\widetilde{m}_{{}_{\theta,T}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})
. Let
h
be the functional direction defining the alternatives as in (
19

Then,

\widetilde{E}_{{}_{\theta,T}}[v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)]\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\int C(x;g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)h(x)\mu(dx),

(25)

where

C(x;g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)=E_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt[g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,\nu(x))(\nu(x)-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x))].

(26)

Moreover,
\widetilde{V}_{{}_{\theta,T}}[v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)]\sim\|g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\|^{2}
.

Proof 6.2
.

Recall that
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
is assumed to be centered under the null. The expected value under the alternative is

\widetilde{E}_{{}_{\theta,T}}[v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)]=\sqrt{K}\int\sum_{z=0}^{\infty}g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)\left[p(z|\widetilde{m}_{{}_{\theta,T}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x))-p(z|m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x))\right]\mu_{{}_{K}}\hskip-1.42271pt(dx)

where the difference in the square bracket is asymptotically small because
\widetilde{m}_{{}_{\theta,T}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
is asymptotically small, while the Poisson probabilities
p(z|t)
are regular in
t
. Therefore, one can approximate it using

\displaystyle\sqrt{K}\frac{\partial p(z|m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k}))}{\partial m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})}(\widetilde{m}_{{}_{\theta,T}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})

\displaystyle-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k}))=(z-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k}))p(z|m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k}))\sqrt{K}\frac{\widetilde{m}_{{}_{\theta,T}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})}{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})}

\displaystyle=(z-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k}))p(z|m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k}))\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}h_{{}_{T}}(x_{k}).

(27)

The substitution of this approximation in the expression for

leads to the result. (A formal justification of the approximation is given in the Supplementary Material.) As to the variance, the convergence
p(z|\widetilde{m}_{{}_{\theta,T}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x))\to p(z|m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x))
implies

\widetilde{V}_{{}_{\theta,T}}[v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)]=\int\widetilde{E}_{{}_{\theta,T}}\bigl[g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,\nu(x))-\widetilde{E}_{{}_{\theta,T}}[g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,\nu(x))]\bigl]^{2}\mu_{{}_{K}}\hskip-1.42271pt(dx)\to\int E_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt[g^{2}_{{}_{\theta}}(x,\nu(x))]\mu(dx)\sim\|g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\|^{2}.

∎

Since the asymptotic distribution of

under the alternatives differs from the null only in the mean, to assess the adequacy of divisible statistics for goodness-of-fit, we focus on their shift.

Assume that the representation in (

\tilde{E}_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt[v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)]\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\int C(x;\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)h(x)\mu(dx).

The analysis of when the right-hand side can be zero – and, therefore, of when tests based on

Proposition 6.3
.

(i)

The limiting shift of

\widetilde{E}_{{}_{\theta,T}}[v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)]\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\int C(x;\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)\widehat{h}(x)\mu(dx),

(ii)

If
\theta

\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\int C(x;g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)\widehat{h}(x)\mu(dx).

(30)

Moreover, if the asymptotic equality in (

\widehat{h}
, the limiting power of

v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)

Proof 6.4
.

The projection nature of
\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt

\begin{split}C(x;\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)&=C(x;g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)-\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle\langle b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle^{-1}C(x;b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)\\
\text{where}\quad&\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle=\int C(x,g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)\frac{\dot{m}_{\theta}^{\sf T}(x)}{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}\mu_{{}_{K}}\hskip-1.42271pt(dx),\\
&\langle b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle=\int C(x;b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)\frac{\dot{m}_{\theta}^{\sf T}(x)}{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}\mu_{{}_{K}}\hskip-1.42271pt(dx),\end{split}

(31)

and

\int C(x;\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)\frac{\dot{m}_{\theta}(x)}{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}\mu_{{}_{K}}\hskip-1.42271pt(dx)=0.

At the same time, from (

is a linear combination of the coordinates of
\frac{\dot{m}_{{}_{\theta}}}{m_{{}_{\theta}}}

When

\int C(x;\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)\widehat{h}(x)\mu(dx)\propto\int\frac{\dot{m}_{{}_{\theta}}(x)}{m_{{}_{\theta}}(x)}\widehat{h}(x)\Lambda_{\beta}(dx)=0.

Hence, for sequences of alternatives defined by a function
\widehat{h}
, the limit of both

\widetilde{E}_{{}_{\theta,T}}[v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)]

Under the null hypothesis, the variance of

\|\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\|^{2}=\|g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\|^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,s_{j}\rangle^{2}\leq\|g\|^{2}

.

6.2
No single divisible statistic is adequate for goodness-of-fit

The limiting shifts in (
25
) and (
28
) are linear functionals in
L_{2}(\mu)
; hence, for any
h
lying in the subspaces of
L_{2}(\mu)
annihilated by these functionals, the limiting power will be equal to the significance level.

For instance, for the (centered) Pearson’s
\chi^{2}
statistic :

\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{(\nu(x_{k})-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k}))^{2}}{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})}-1,

(32)

we have that
C(x;g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)=1
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
. Hence, when
\theta
is estimated via MLE, the shift in (
30
) reduces to

\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\int\widehat{h}(x)\mu(dx).

(33)

Therefore, Pearson’s
\chi^{2}
has no power against alternatives approaching the null from a direction
\widehat{h}
orthogonal to one with respect to the Lebesgue measure. Equation (
33
) also implies that no departures from uniformity can be detected by Pearson’s statistic, a problem originally pointed out by
Chibisov 1965
. Unfortunately, this fallacy is not limited to the case of Pearson’s
\chi^{2}
but, as demonstrated in Section
6.3
, it affects an entire subclass of divisible statistics.

Examples I-III (continued).
When
c
and
\beta
are estimated via MLE, for all three examples, the power of Pearson’s
\chi^{2}
, obtained by means of
100,000
Monte Carlo replicates, is approximately equal to the significance level, here chosen to be
5\%
.
While unpleasant, this result has a clear theoretical explanation. As noted above, when the parameters are estimated, only the alternatives defined by the functions
\widehat{h}_{j}
,
j=1,2,3
(red chained lines in Fig.
2
) are detectable by divisible statistics. In Example III, the effect of
\widehat{h}_{3}
is rather faint. Thus, regardless of the choice of
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
, no divisible statistic is likely to detect such a deviation. On the other hand, both alternatives defined by
\widehat{h}_{1}
and
\widehat{h}_{2}
in Examples I and II are distinguishable from the null. When using Pearson’s
\chi^{2}
, however, the limiting shift is approximately equal to
-0.014
in Example I and -0.019 in Example II. This tells that, despite
\widehat{h}_{j}
,
j=1,2
being not so small, their integrals are. Thereby leading to a loss of power.

Note that, although divisible statistics do not satisfy Definition
5.1
, there exist situations in which scientists may be willing to sacrifice adequacy for goodness-of-fit to ensure high power against at least some classes of alternatives relevant to a given application. In the construction of such tests, the structure of the shift in (
28
) can be used to verify that, for a given choice of
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
, power is preserved in the directions defining such alternatives.

6.3
On the impossibility of testing uniformity via space-homogeneous divisible statistics

In the previous sections, we saw some implications of the fact that the shift of
v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt)
is a linear functional from
\widehat{h}
. In particular, from Proposition
6.3
(i), we have that this linear functional is not defined by the function
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
but by
C(x;\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
. This leads to a phenomenon that does not have an analogy in the classical theory of empirical processes. What can now occur is the following: a non-zero function
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
produces a non-zero divisible statistic, but if
C(x;\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)=0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, then the shift of this statistic is zero for all alternatives.

One situation in which this phenomenon arises is when
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
belongs to the class of
space-homogeneous
divisible statistics defined as follows.

Definition 6.5
.

We say that
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
is a space-homogeneous divisible statistic, if the function
C(x;g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
in (
26
) is constant.

For example, regardless of the model being tested, both the (centered) Pearson’s statistic
and the statistic

\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left[\frac{\nu(x_{k})}{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})}-1\right]

are space-homogeneous.

When
\lambda_{\beta}
is uniform, the class of space-homogeneous statistics is rather broad. In this case, the frequencies
\{\nu(x_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}
are all identically distributed Poisson random variables with mean
m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})=c
for all
k=1,\dots,K
. Therefore, if
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\nu(x_{k}),m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k}))=g(\nu(x_{k}),c)
does not depend on
x_{k}
explicitly, then these random variables are also identically distributed for all
k=1,\dots,K
and thus
C(x;g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
is constant.
This brings us to the following statement:

Proposition 6.6
.

Suppose
\lambda_{\beta}
is the uniform density on
\mathcal{X}
and
c
is estimated with MLE. Then, for any space-homogeneous divisible statistics,

\widetilde{E}_{{}_{\theta,T}}[v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)]\sim 0,

that is, no such statistic has any asymptotic power for any
h
.

Proof 6.7
.

Since
C(x;g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
is constant and
\lambda_{\beta}(x)=|\mathcal{X}|^{-1}
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, we have

C(x;\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)=C(x;g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)-\int C(x;g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)\mu_{{}_{K}}\hskip-1.42271pt(dy)=0.

It follows that, for any
h
, the limiting shift in (
28
) is zero.
∎

Proposition
6.6
tells us that, although uniformity is a relevant null hypothesis in many practical settings, no space-homogeneous statistic is useful for detecting departures from it.

6.3.1
Space-homogeneous statistics are unsuitable to test constant background in
X
-ray spectra

When analyzing the source-free
X
-ray spectrum from Chandra introduced in Section
4.1
, Pearson’s statistic failed to reject the hypothesis of a constant background model. As a follow-up study, consider the weighted linear statistic

\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\nu(x_{k})-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})}{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})};

(34)

and the (centered) likelihood ratio – known in astronomy as ‘Cash statistic’
(
Cash 1979
)
– defined by the function

g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)=z\ln z-E_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt[\nu(x)\ln\nu(x)]-(z-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x))(1+\ln m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)).

The importance of (
34
) will be discussed in the next section.
When testing the hypothesis
H_{0}:m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)=c
, the p-values obtained by means of a parametric bootstrap simulation involving 100,000 replicates are
0.145
for the weighed linear statistic and
0.987
for the likelihood ratio. As expected, neither statistic rejected the constant background model. We argue that, in this specific application, the deviations of the true background model from uniformity are indeed contiguous and, therefore, undetectable by space-homogeneous divisible statistics. Nevertheless, as shown in Section
7.1
, they can be detected when relying on omnibus functionals of the process involving their partial sums.

6.4
Every divisible statistic is dominated by a weighted linear statistic.

The situation in which
C(x;\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
is identically equal to zero for non-zero choices of
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
can be retrieved in most settings, not only in the case of space-homogeneous statistics.

To see how this can happen, consider the structure of the projection operator in (
14
). At first glance, choosing
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
orthogonal to the score function – i.e., such that
\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\rangle=0
– may seem advantageous: for all such
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
,
\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt=g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
, thus, statistics
v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt)
will be asymptotically equivalent to
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
and, therefore, their asymptotic behaviour will not depend on whether we estimate
\theta
or not.
However, the condition
\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\rangle=0
is achieved as soon as
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k},\nu(x_{k}))
and
\nu(x_{k})
are uncorrelated for every given
x_{k}
, i.e.,

C(x_{k},g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)=E_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\bigl[g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k},\nu(x_{k}))(\nu(x_{k})-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k}))\bigl]=0,

which then leads to a zero limiting shift of the statistic
v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt)
, making it useless.
Let us understand the situation better.
In particular, let us decompose a given
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
into the parts orthogonal to and collinear with
\nu(x)-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)
:

g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\perp}(x,z)=g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)-\frac{C(x,g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)}{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}(\nu(x)-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)),\quad g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}(x,z)=\frac{C(x,g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)}{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}(\nu(x)-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)).

(35)

Since
C(x,g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\perp})=0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, unbinding
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
from the extra randomness due to
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\perp})
allows us to preserve the asymptotic shift while simultaneously reducing the variance. As formalized in Proposition
6.8
, the same holds true when
\theta
is estimated via MLE.

Proposition 6.8
.

Assume that the asymptotic representation (
13
) is valid and
\theta
is estimated via MLE. Then, for any divisible statistic
v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt)
, there exists another divisible statistic,
v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}})
, whose limiting power is greater than or equal to that of
v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt)
.

Proof 6.9
.

Recall that
v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt)
is asymptotically equivalent to
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
and consider the expansion

v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)=v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi g^{\perp}_{{}_{\theta}})+v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}).

From (
31
) it follows that, since
C(x_{k};g^{\perp}_{{}_{\theta}})=0
for all
x_{k}
, we obtain
C(x_{k},\Pi g_{\theta}^{\perp})=0
for all
x_{k}
, regardless of the choice of
b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
defining the estimating equations in (

). This fact and Proposition

imply that
\widetilde{E}_{{}_{\theta,T}}\bigl[v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi g^{\perp}_{{}_{\theta}})\bigl]\sim 0
for all alternatives. Therefore,
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi g^{\perp}_{{}_{\theta}})
will not be useful for testing purposes and
\widetilde{E}_{{}_{\theta,T}}\bigl[v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)\bigl]
and
\widetilde{E}_{{}_{\theta,T}}\bigl[v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}})\bigl]
have the same limit, i.e.,

\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\int C(x;\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}})\widehat{h}(x)\mu(dx)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\int C(x;g^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}_{\theta})\widehat{h}(x)\mu(dx),

where the equality follows from Proposition

(ii).
At the same time, from the equality in (
14
) we have
\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\perp}=g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\perp}
and if
\theta
is estimated via MLE,
b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt=\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
and
\Pi
is orthogonal, i.e., it is a self-adjoint projector. Therefore,

\langle\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\perp},\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}\rangle=\langle\Pi^{2}g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\perp},g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}\rangle=\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\perp},g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}\rangle=0.

and
\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}
are orthogonal, the limiting variance of

always exceeds or is equal to that of
v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt)
, with equality holding when
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt=g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\perp}
or
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt=g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}
. ∎

Goodness-of-fit via divisible statistics

7.1
On the construction of goodness-of-fit tests based on partial sums

Despite the advantages given by parameter estimation against alternatives of the form in (
23
) and the use of
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}
in (
35
) to increase the power of single divisible statistics, neither of these phenomena guarantees their adequacy for goodness-of-fit.
Nevertheless, power can be restored by relying on a family of divisible statistics.

As shown by
Khmaladze 1984
for the case of simple hypotheses, one possible choice is the process of partial sums

v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\mathds{1}_{A})=\int_{A}\hskip-1.42271pt\int g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(dx,dz)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{x_{k}\in A}g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\bigl(x_{k},\nu(x_{k})\bigl),

(38)

in which
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\mathds{1}_{A}
denotes functions of the form
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)\mathds{1}_{\{x\in A\}}
.
When testing parametric hypotheses, the process of interest is

v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt\mathds{1}_{A})=v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt\bigl(\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\mathds{1}_{A}\bigl)+o_{{}_{P}}(1)

(39)

in which the functions
\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\mathds{1}_{A}
can be written explicitly as

\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)\mathds{1}_{\{x\in A\}}=g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z)\mathds{1}_{\{x\in A\}}-\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\mathds{1}_{A},\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle\langle b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt,\psi_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\sf T}\rangle^{-1}b_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x,z).

In this paper, we choose the sets
A
to be members of the
scanning family

(
Khmaladze 1993
, cf.)
, i.e., an increasing sequence of subsets,
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{t}\}_{0\leq t\leq 1}
, of
\mathcal{X}
such that

(a)

A_{s}\subseteq A_{t}\text{ if }s\leq t
;

(b)

|A_{0}|=0,|A_{1}|=|\mathcal{X}|
;

(c)

|A_{t}|
is absolutely continuous in
t
.

It follows that one can label the functions in
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mu_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
indexing the processes in (
38
)-(
39
) using the one-dimensional time parameter
t\in[0,1]
. As shown in Section
7.3
, this also simplifies the construction of asymptotically distribution-free goodness-of-fit tests.

The process
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\mathds{1}_{A})
consists of partial sums of independent random variables; hence, it converges weakly to a Brownian motion
(
Pyke 1983
;
Kenneth and Pyke 1986
, cf.)
. The projection

v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt\bigl(\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\mathds{1}_{A}\bigl)
, on the other hand, converges weakly to a projected Brownian motion.
Under the null, the mean function of such processes is identically equal to zero for all
A\in\mathcal{A}
; whereas their shift under the alternatives can be derived by replacing
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
with
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt\mathds{1}_{A}
in (
25
) and (
28
). For instance,
(
25
) becomes

\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\int_{A}C(x;g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)h(x)\mu(dx).

Since the integral is taken over all
A\in\mathcal{A}
, it is identically equal to zero only if
C(x;g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt)=0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
or if the function
h
satisfies rather exotic conditions. Therefore, in practice, any choice of
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt
that differs from
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\perp}
(cf. Section
6.4
) would give a non-zero shift over at least one set
A\in\mathcal{A}
.
Consequently, goodness-of-fit tests satisfying Definition
5.1
can be constructed by considering omnibus functionals of the partial sum processes in (
38
)-(
39
) as test statistics.

For example, let us start with a collection of hyperrectangles

(-\infty,y_{t}]=(-\infty,y_{1t}]\times\dots\times(-\infty,y_{dt}]

with
y_{t}=(y_{1t},\dots,y_{dt})\in\mathcal{X}
such that, if
s\leq t
,
y_{is}\leq y_{it}
, for all
i=1,\dots,d
. Then choose
A_{t}
to be

. A possible test statistic for parametric goodness-of-fit is

\begin{split}\widehat{\text{KS}}=\max_{t}\bigl|v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}\mathds{1}_{t})\bigl|\end{split}

(40)

in which
\mathds{1}_{t}
denotes the indicator function
\mathds{1}_{\{x\in(-\infty,y_{t}]\cap\mathcal{X}\}}
and
g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}
is constructed as in (
35
).
The statistic in (
40
) is the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic for testing parametric hypotheses in the binned data regime.

\theta
obtained via MLE on the set of data collected by the experiment. We are interested in simulating the null distribution of the process

v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}\mathds{1}_{A})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{K}}\sum_{x_{k}\in A}\frac{C(x_{k};g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt)}{m_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k})}(\nu(x_{k})-m_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x_{k}))

(42)

and its functionals.
To account for the randomness associated with
\widehat{\theta}
, the classical parametric bootstrap entails maximizing the log-likelihood on each bootstrap sample and re-evaluating
v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}\mathds{1}_{A})
at each bootstrap estimate of
\theta
.

Let us now consider the projected process of partial sums

v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}\mathds{1}_{A})=v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}\mathds{1}_{A})-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}\mathds{1}_{A},s_{j}\rangle v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(s_{j}).

Such a process is asymptotically equal to

v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}\mathds{1}_{A})
(cf. Section
4
), however, from a computational standpoint, simulating the null distribution of
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}\mathds{1}_{A})
is more advantageous than simulating that of
v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(g_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}}}\hskip-0.56917pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}\mathds{1}_{A})
. That is because, in the former case, one needs not to re-estimate
\theta
on each bootstrap sample. Instead, the randomness induced by parameter estimation is accounted for through the second term on the right-hand side of the above expression in which the inner products
\langle g_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt^{\scalebox{0.5}{$/\penalty\!/\penalty$}}\mathds{1}_{A},s_{j}\rangle
need only to be computed once at
\theta=\widehat{\theta}_{\text{obs}}
.

Algeri 2022
.

(43)

where

labels the sets

(cf. Section

) and

7.3
Asymptotically distribution-free goodness-of-fit tests via unitary operators

The projected bootstrap can reduce the CPU time needed to simulate the null distribution of goodness-of-fit statistics.
At the same time, it is also possible to construct goodness-of-fit tests based on the asymptotically distribution-free transformation of empirical processes introduced in
Khmaladze 2016
. If we do this, the limiting null distribution of the test statistics based on the transformed process will be known analytically and will not depend on the model we test.
Such a feature is convenient for testing many different models simultaneously or when analyzing a large amount of data. For example,
X
-ray astronomical missions survey millions of astronomical sources. When analyzing the images and spectra arising from such observations, a common problem is identifying those in which features other than the background are present. In statistical terms, this problem involves testing spectral models on several millions of datasets, which would be unfeasible through case-by-case numerical simulations.

Given the prominence of the linear statistics, we consider the functions

\displaystyle\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt(x,z)=\frac{z-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}{\sqrt{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}}\mathds{1}_{\{x\in A_{t}\}}

in which the sets
A_{t}
are members of the scanning family
\mathcal{A}
and are chosen so that

\mu(A_{t})=t.

(44)

This condition is not necessary but simplifies the notation in what follows.
Under (
3
) and if
\theta
is known, the null distribution of the process
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt)
converges weakly to that of a standard Brownian motion. When
\theta
is estimated via MLE,
v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt)
is asymptotically equivalent to
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt)
and its limiting null distribution is that of a projected Brownian motion orthogonal to the score function
s
of the model we wish to test. Thus, the null distribution of test statistics based on
v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt)
will be different for different models and for different values of
\theta
within the same model.

However, it is possible to construct a linear operator in
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mu_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
, denoted by
U_{p}
, which maps the process
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt)
into a process in
t
with standard distribution, unconnected with the model we are testing: the operator
U_{p}
will depend on the model, but the distribution of the resulting process will be free from it. In its spirit, the situation is not unlike the one encountered in the i.i.d. data regime in which the classical time-transformation
t=F(x)
depends on the hypothetical
F
but maps
F
-empirical processes into the uniform empirical process, which is free from
F
.

The idea behind the choice of the operator
U_{p}
is geometrically clear: many different parametric models with a
p
-dimensional estimated parameter will asymptotically lead to projected Brownian motions parallel to the corresponding score functions. Therefore, the kernels of these projections, albeit different for different models, will all have the same dimension
p
. But then one can choose one such projection orthogonal to a fixed collection of
p
orthonormal functions and map particular projections corresponding to particular models into this chosen standard projection.

Let
r=\{r_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
form a fixed orthonormal system in
\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mu_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
. Unlike the coordinates of the orthonormalized score function
s=\{s_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
, this new system will stay the same for many different models.
Consider the projection of
\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt
orthogonal to
r
:

\Pi_{r}\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt(x,z)=\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt(x,z)-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\langle\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt,r_{j}\rangle r_{j}(x,z).

Choose
U_{p}
to be the unitary operator that maps
r
into
s
and, therefore, functions orthogonal to
r
into functions orthogonal to
s
. Then, the process
v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(U_{p}\Pi_{r}\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt)
serves our purpose since

v_{{}_{\widehat{\theta}\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(U_{p}\Pi_{r}\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt)\sim v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(\Pi U_{p}\Pi_{r}\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt)=v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(U_{p}\Pi_{r}\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt)

and the variance of the process on the right-hand side – and, therefore, its covariance – depends on
r
but does not involve
s
:

E_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt[v^{2}_{{}_{\theta,K}}(U_{p}\Pi_{r}\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt)]=\|U_{p}\Pi_{r}\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt\|^{2}=\|\Pi_{r}\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt\|^{2}=\|\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt\|^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\langle\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt,r_{j}\rangle^{2}.

(45)

Thus, the limiting distribution of statistics based on the transformed process
v_{{}_{\theta,K}}(U_{p}\Pi_{r}\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt)
will need to be found only once and then used for testing many different models.

Furthermore, we can suggest a particular choice of
r
that allows us to derive the asymptotic null distribution of goodness-of-fit statistics in closed form. For this, choose a sequence
\{A_{t_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{p}
of sets within the scanning family
\mathcal{A}
such that
\mu(A_{t_{j}})=j/p
. Denote with
\{B_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
the collection of increments

B_{j}=A_{t_{j}}\setminus A_{t_{j-1}},

with
B_{1}=\mathcal{X}
if
p=1
; for any
p
,
\{B_{j}\}_{j=1}^{p}
provides an exhaustive partition of
\mathcal{X}
.
Choose

r_{j}\hskip-0.56917pt(x,z)=\frac{z-m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}{\sqrt{m_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt(x)}}\sqrt{p}\mathds{1}_{\{x\in B_{j}\}}.

(46)

We will see that, for this choice of
r
, the process
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(U_{p}\Pi_{r}\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt)
is asymptotically a sequence of independent standard Brownian bridges.

One can split
\Pi_{r}\ell_{t}
as the sum

\Pi_{r}\ell_{t}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\ell_{t}{\mathds{1}}_{\{x\in B_{j}\}}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\langle r_{j},\ell_{t}\rangle r_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left[\ell_{t}{\mathds{1}}_{\{x\in B_{j}\}}-\langle r_{j},\ell_{t}\rangle r_{j}\right].

In this sum, only the summand for which
t_{j-1}<t\leq t_{j}
is non-zero. For this summand

\|\ell_{t}{\mathds{1}}_{\{x\in B_{j}\}}\|^{2}\to t-t_{j-1},\;\;\text{while}\;\;\;\langle r_{j},\ell_{t}\rangle^{2}\to p(t-t_{j-1})^{2}

and eventually, we obtain

E_{{}_{\theta}}\hskip-1.42271pt[v^{2}_{{}_{\theta,K}}(U_{p}\Pi_{r}\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt)]\to t-t_{j-1}-p(t-t_{j-1})^{2}.

Simulated null distributions of the test statistic
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\text{KS}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu
in (

) (top panels)
and its transformed counterpart
\text{KS}^{*}
in (

) shifted by

When comparing the last expression with the variance of the standard Brownian bridge
u(t),t\in[0,1]
, rescaled on the set
B_{j}
, i.e.,

u_{j}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}u(p(t-t_{j-1})),\;\;t_{j-1}<t\leq t_{j},

(47)

we find that they are the same. Therefore, asymptotically, the process
v_{{}_{\theta,K}}(U_{p}\Pi_{r}\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt)
is indeed a succession of rescaled independent standard Brownian bridges. For illustration purposes, one realization of such a process when
p=4
is provided on the right panel of Fig.
3
.

The asymptotic null distribution of many goodness-of-fit statistics from such a process is analytically known. For example, consider

\text{KS}^{*}=\max_{t}\bigl|v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(U_{p}\Pi_{r}\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt)\bigl|.

(48)

Since the maximum of a standard Brownian bridge is known to follow the Kolmogorov distribution
(
Kolmogorov 1933
)
, here denoted with
\mathcal{K}(\cdot)
, we have that, as
K\rightarrow\infty
,

P\bigl(\text{KS}^{*}\leq y|H_{0}\bigl)\rightarrow P\Bigl(\max_{i}\bigl\{\max_{t}\bigl|\sqrt{p}u_{i}(t)\bigl|\bigl\}\leq\sqrt{p}y\Bigl)=\Bigl[\mathcal{K}(\sqrt{p}y)\Bigl]^{p}.

(49)

The partial sums process, however, is observed over discrete times; hence, its maximum is always smaller than or equal to the maximum of the Brownian bridge, leading to a shift in the distribution. Nevertheless, the application of the continuity correction

\Bigl[\mathcal{K}\bigl(\sqrt{p}(y+{\textstyle\frac{0.6}{\sqrt{K}}})\bigl)\Bigl]^{p}.

agrees with the finite-
K
distribution even for
K\sim 10
. A similar result can be obtained for situations in which the number of sets over which the partial sums are taken does not coincide with the number of bins.

It remains to address how to construct the unitary operator
U_{p}
.
Consider the unitary operator
U_{a,b}
defined as

U_{a,b}=I-\frac{\langle\cdot,a-b\rangle}{1-\langle a,b\rangle}[a(x,z)-b(x,z)]

where
a,b\in\mathcal{L}_{2}(\mu_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt)
and
\|a\|=\|b\|=1
.
U_{a,b}
maps
a
into
b
,
b
into
a
, and leaves functions orthogonal to both
a
and
b
unchanged.
Proceed by constructing a set of auxiliary functions
\widetilde{r}_{j}\hskip-0.56917pt
such that
\widetilde{r}_{1}={r}_{1}
and, for
j>1
, set

\widetilde{r}_{j}\hskip-0.56917pt(x,z)=U_{j-1}r_{j}(x,z)\quad\text{with}\quad U_{j}=U_{\widetilde{r}_{j},s_{j}}\dots U_{\widetilde{r}_{1},s_{1}}.

(50)

For example,

\widetilde{r}_{2}\hskip-0.56917pt(x,z)=U_{\widetilde{r}_{1},s_{1}}r_{2}\hskip-0.56917pt(x,z),\quad\widetilde{r}_{3}\hskip-0.56917pt(x,z)=U_{\widetilde{r}_{2},s_{2}}U_{\widetilde{r}_{1},s_{1}}r_{3}\hskip-0.56917pt(x,z),\quad\text{etc.}

Each function
\widetilde{r}_{j}\hskip-0.56917pt
is orthogonal to all
s_{i}
with
i<j
.

The operator
U_{p}
, constructed as in (
50
),
is a product of unitary operators; hence, it is unitary, and it can be shown that
U_{p}r_{j}=s_{j}
for all
j=1,\dots,p
.
Furthermore, given that
\Pi_{r}
and
U_{p}
are linear operators,
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(U_{p}\Pi_{r}\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt)
is a function-parametric process with values given by weighted linear statistics. Thus, the corresponding
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(U_{p}\Pi_{r}\ell_{t}\hskip-0.56917pt^{\perp})
component (cf. Section
6.4
) is always zero.

8
Summary of the main results and final remarks

This article proposes a revision of the concept of divisible statistics that includes several forms of statistics routinely used in the analyses of grouped data and enables their representation as linear functionals of the random measure
v_{{}_{\theta\hskip-0.85355pt,\hskip-1.13791ptK}}\hskip-1.42271pt(A,z)
.
By studying the latter, researchers can acquire a comprehensive perspective on the behavior of the class of divisible statistics as a whole.

This study has revealed that, in a sparse regime, no single divisible statistic is adequate for goodness-of-fit; however, tests based on functionals of the partial sum process are. Moreover, parameter estimation can lead to higher power compared to testing for simple hypotheses. The most unexpected finding (for us) is that a class of weighted linear statistics dominates all divisible statistics.

We show that to conduct goodness-of-fit, it is beneficial to rely on omnibus functionals of the specific empirical process given in (
42
). Their null distribution can be efficiently derived using the projected parametric bootstrap. It is also possible to transform the empirical process to construct test statistics with an asymptotic null distribution free from the model and known in closed form.

The theoretical framework introduced in this manuscript is illustrated through the lenses of a Poisson-binned data analysis, it immediately applies to Poisson regression problems, and it naturally lends itself to any other situation in which non-identically distributed data are encountered. It would be interesting to explore extensions of the proposed setup to high-dimensional regression in which the size of parameter space is much larger than the sample size. Such a development could broaden further the set of inferential tools currently available in this context
(
Chen and Lockhart 2001
;
van de Geer et al. 2014
;
Janková et al. 2020
, e.g.,)
.

The application on data from the Chandra
X
-ray Observatory allowed us to detect departures from uniformity in
X
-ray source-free spectra. It also suggested that a revision of the linear model proposed by
Zhang et al. 2023
may be needed to suitably describe the background distribution in the 14.6-17.4 Angrstom range.
