On the Statistics of the Number of Fixed-Dimensional Subcubes in a Random Subset of the n-Dimensional Discrete Unit Cube Thanks: Accompanied by Maple package SMCboole.txt, available from http://www.math.rutgers.edu/ ∼ \sim zeilberg/mamarim/mamarimhtml/subcubes.html .
Svante Janson, Blair Seidler, Doron ZEILBERGER

Abstract
. This paper consists of two independent, but related parts. In the first part we show how to use symbolic computation to derive explicit expressions for the first few moments of the number of implicants that a random Boolean function has, or equivalently the number of fixed-dimensional subcubes contained in a random subset of the n n -dimensional cube. These explicit expressions suggest, but do not prove, that these random variables are always asymptotically normal. The second part presents a full, human-generated proof, of this asymptotic normality, first proved by Urszula Konieczna.

Introduction

1.1.
Motivation

Recall that an
implicant
of a Boolean function in
n
variables,
f(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is a pure disjunction

\displaystyle x_{i_{1}}^{a_{1}}\,\wedge\,x_{i_{2}}^{a_{2}}\wedge\dots\wedge x_{i_{r}}^{a_{r}},

(1.1)

that implies it.
Here
1\leq i_{1}<\dots i_{r}\leq n
,
a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\in\{0,1\}
,
z^{1}=z
, and
z^{0}=\bar{z}
(the negation of

).

Fix
r
and let
n
vary. We are interested in the statistical distribution of the random variable
number of implicants of length
n-r
in a uniformly-at-random Boolean function
of
n
variables. Clearly, when
r=0
it is nothing but the good old (fair) binomial distribution with
2^{n}

fair coin-tosses
,
B(\frac{1}{2},2^{n})
.

Equivalently, for a random subset of the
n
-dimensional cube, we are interested in the statistical distribution of the number of
r
-dimensional subcubes properly contained in it.

We would like to have
explicit expressions
, in
n
, for the
k^{th}
moment of this random variable, for as many as possible

and

. This turns out to be
a challenging
symbolic-computational
problem that we will address in the first part of this paper.

In the second part we consider asymptotics as
{n\to\infty}
.
It was proved by Urszula Konieczna
[
3
]
that

, this distribution is

.
We will reprove this by a somewhat different method which leads to a simple
proof.

1.2.
Our Random Variables

The

is the set of subsets of
\{0,1\}^{n}
, that has cardinality

.

Let’s define our random variables formally.
For a (uniformly-at-) random subset

For example, if

(1.3)

\displaystyle X_{0}(S)=6,\quad X_{1}(S)=6,\quad X_{2}(S)=1,\quad X_{3}(S)=0.

(1.4)

We would like to get, for as many pairs
(k,r)
as possible,

, for the

, i.e. for

\displaystyle f_{kr}(n):=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[{X_{r}}^{k}](n).

(1.5)

2.
Explicit Expressions for Moments of the Number of Low-Dimensional Subcubes in a Random Subest of the
n
-dimensional cube

2.1.
The Expectation and Variance

The
first
moment, aka
expectation
, aka
mean
, aka
average
, is easy, using
linearity of expectation
.

For any specific subcube
C
of
\{0,1\}^{n}
, define the
atomic
random variable,
X_{C}
, on subsets,
S
, of
\{0,1\}^{n}
as follows.

\displaystyle X_{C}(S)\,=\,\begin{cases}1,&\text{if}\quad C\subset S;\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2.1)

Let
\mathcal{C}(n,r)
be the set of all
{\binom{n}{r}}2^{n-r}

r
-dimensional subcubes of
\{0,1\}^{n}
. Then, of course,

\displaystyle X_{r}(S)\,=\,\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(n,r)}X_{C}(S).

(2.2)

Applying the
expectation
functional and using the
linearity of expectation
, we get that the average, let’s call it

\mu_{r}(n)
, is

\displaystyle\mu_{r}(n)=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[X_{r}]\,=\,\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}\left[\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(n,r)}X_{C}\right]\,=\,\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(n,r)}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[X_{C}].

(2.3)

Now the probability that a random subset of
\{0,1\}^{n}
contains an
r
-dimensional subcube
C
is

\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2^{r}}
, since for each of its vertices, the chance of it belonging to
S
is
\frac{1}{2}
,
and by
independence
the probability that all its
2^{r}
vertices belong to
S
is indeed
\frac{1}{2^{2^{r}}}
. The probability that
X_{C}(S)=0
is of course
1-\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2^{r}}
, hence

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[X_{C}]=1\cdot\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2^{r}}\,+\,0\cdot\left(1-\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2^{r}}\right)\,=\,\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2^{r}}.

(2.4)

Going back to equation (
2.3
) we have

\displaystyle\mu_{r}(n)\,=\,\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(n,r)}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[X_{C}]=\,\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(n,r)}\frac{1}{2^{2^{r}}}=|\mathcal{C}(n,r)|\cdot\frac{1}{2^{2^{r}}}=\frac{{\binom{n}{r}}2^{n-r}}{2^{2^{r}}}.

(2.5)

In a beautiful paper, Thanatipanonda
[
5
]
derived an explicit expression for the general second moment, for every
r
-dimensional cube.

Thanatipanoda’s General Formula for the Second Moment
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[X_{r}^{2}]\,=\,\sum_{i=0}^{r}\frac{n!2^{n-i}}{i!(r-i)!^{2}(n-2r+i)!2^{2^{r+1}}}\cdot(2^{2^{i}}-1)\,+\,\frac{[{\binom{n}{r}}2^{n-r}]^{2}}{2^{2^{r+1}}},

(2.6)

from which immediately follows, using
[\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}(X_{r}-\mu_{r}(n))^{2}]=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[X_{r}^{2}]-\mu_{r}(n)^{2}
, the following formula.

Thanatipanoda’s General Formula for the Variance
:

\displaystyle\text{Var}(X_{r})=\sum_{i=0}^{r}\frac{n!2^{n-i}}{i!(r-i)!^{2}(n-2r+i)!2^{2^{r+1}}}\cdot(2^{2^{i}}-1).

(2.7)

Note that the variance is a polynomial in
(n,2^{n})
of degree
2r
in
n
and degree
1
in
2^{n}
.

2.3.
Our Method

Obviously we did not derive these formulas by hand. We had to teach our computer how to find them. It also uses
linearity of expectation
, but
with higher moments things get very complicated.
Recall that

\displaystyle X_{r}(S)\,=\,\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(n,r)}X_{C}(S).

(2.15)

Hence, the
k
-th moment is

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}\left[(X_{r})^{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}\Big[\Big(\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}(n,r)}X_{C}(S)\Big)^{k}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{[C_{1},\dots,C_{k}]\in\mathcal{C}(n,r)^{k}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[X_{C_{1}}X_{C_{2}}\cdots X_{C_{k}}].

(2.16)

So we sum over all
({\binom{n}{r}}2^{n-r})^{k}
members of
\mathcal{C}(n,r)^{k}
.
Since the product
X_{C_{1}}(S)X_{C_{2}}(S)\cdots X_{C_{k}}(S)=1
if
each
of
C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{k}
is properly included in
S
(i.e. if
each vertex in
C_{1}\cup C_{2}\cup\dots C_{k}
belongs to
S
), and
0
otherwise,
the contribution,
or weight,
due to each such term is

\displaystyle\text{Weight}([C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{k}])=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}\left[X_{C_{1}}X_{C_{2}}\cdots X_{C_{k}}\right]=\frac{1}{2^{|C_{1}\cup C_{2}\cup\dots C_{k}|}}.

(2.17)

2.3.1.
Data Structure

Every
r
-dimensional subcube of
\{0,1\}^{n}
has the form

\displaystyle C=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}\,|\,x_{i_{1}}=\alpha_{i_{1}},\dots,x_{i_{n-r}}=\alpha_{i_{n-r}}\},

(2.18)

for some
1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{n-r}\leq n
and
(\alpha_{i_{1}},\dots\alpha_{i_{n-r}})\in\{0,1\}^{n-r}
.
A good way to represent it on a computer is as a row-vector of length
n
, in the
alphabet

\{0,1,*\}
,
where the entries corresponding to
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{n-r}
have
\alpha_{i_{1}},\dots.\alpha_{i_{n-r}}
respectively
and the remaining
r
entries are filled with
wild cards
, denoted by
*
.

For example, if
n=7
and
r=3
, the
3
-dimensional cube

\displaystyle\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{7})\in\{0,1\}^{7}\,|\,x_{2}=1,x_{4}=1,x_{5}=0,x_{7}=1\},

(2.19)

is represented by

\displaystyle*1*10*1.

(2.20)

We are trying to find a weighted count of
ordered

k
-tuples of
r
-dimensional subcubes. The natural data structure for these is the set of
k
by
n
matrices in the
‘alphabet’
\{0,1,*\}
where every row has exactly
r
‘wildcards’, *.

Let’s call this set of matrices, that correspond to
\mathcal{C}(n,r)^{k}
,
\mathcal{C}(n,r,k)
.

For any
specific
, numeric
n
, there are ‘only’
(2^{n-r}{\binom{n}{r}})^{k}
of these matrices, and for each and every one of them one can find
the cardinality of the union of the corresponding subcubes, let’s call it
v
, and add to the running sum
\frac{1}{2^{v}}
. But we want to do it for

symbolic

n
, i.e. for ‘all’
n
.
We will soon see how, for each
specific
(numeric)
r
and
k
this can be done
in principle
, but only for relatively small
r
and
k

in practice
.
But let’s try and push it as far we can.
An interesting consequence of our algorithm is the precise degree in
n
and
2^{n}
of the expression for
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[X_{r}^{k}](n)
.

2.3.2.
The Kernel

A key object in our approach is the
kernel
.
Given a
k\times n
matrix
M
in the alphabet
\{0,1,*\}
let’s call a column
active
if it contains
at least one ‘
*
’. Note that the matrix has exactly
k\cdot r
‘
*
’s, hence the number of
active columns
, let’s call it
a
,
is between
r
and
k\cdot r
.
1
1

1

More generally, if we want to find an expression for the
mixed moment

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[X_{r_{1}}\cdots X_{r_{k}}]

the number of active columns is between
\max(r_{1},\dots,r_{k})
and
r_{1}+\dots+r_{k}
.
The
kernel
of
M
is
the submatrix of
M
consisting of its active columns.

Let
\mathcal{C}_{a}(n,r,k)
be the subset of
\mathcal{C}(n,r,k)
matrices with exactly
a
active columns. We will call such a matrix in
canonical form
if
the active columns are occupied by the
a
leftmost columns (i.e. its kernel is contiguous starting in the first column).
Let’s denote by
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{a}(n,r,k)
the set of such matrices in canonical form.
Obviously, there are
{\binom{n}{a}}
ways to choose which of the
n
columns are active and hence

\displaystyle\text{Weight}(\mathcal{C}(n,r,k))\,=

\displaystyle\,\sum_{a=r}^{rk}\text{Weight}(\mathcal{C}_{a}(n,r,k))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a=r}^{rk}{\binom{n}{a}}\,\text{Weight}(\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{a}(n,r,k)).

(2.21)

For any set,
S
,
\text{Weight}(S)
is the sum of the weights of its members. Note that this has degree
rk
in
n
.

It remains to do a
weighted-count
, where every matrix gets ‘credit’
1/2^{v}
, where
v
is the cardinality of the union of the corresponding subcubes represented by the
k
rows,
for the set
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{a}(n,r,k)
, of matrices in canonical form. Note that there are only
finitely many
choices for the
a
leftmost columns, i.e. the set of

k\times a
matrices in the alphabet
\{0,1,*\}
with the property that every column has at least one ‘
*
’, and every row has exactly
r
‘
*
’s.
These can be divided into
equivalence classes
obtained by permuting rows and columns and transposing
0
and
1
in any given column. Once these are sorted into equivalence classes, one needs only
examine one representative, and then multiply the weight by the cardinality of the class.

But what about the
n-a
rightmost columns? There are
2^{k(n-a)}
possible submatrices; the alphabet here is
\{0,1\}
.
Almost all of these have distinct rows, more precisely,

\displaystyle{\binom{2^{n-a}}{k}}k!

(2.22)

of them, and these will produce the smallest possible weight in conjunction with any kernel. The other extreme is that all the rows of the submatrix consisting of the
n-a
rightmost columns are identical, and then there are only
2^{n-a}
choices to fill them in.

In general, every such member of
\overline{\mathcal{C}}_{a}(n,r,k)
, determines a
set partition
of the set of rows
\{1,\dots,k\}
, where two rows are
roomates
if they have the same last
n-a
entries.
If that set-partition has
m
members

1\leq m\leq k
, then the number of choices of assigning
different

0-1
vectors of length
n-a
to each of the parts of the set-partition is

\displaystyle{\binom{2^{n-a}}{m}}m!.

(2.23)

Now for each
a
and for each set-partition, we let the computer generate the
finite
set of
k\times a
matrices in the alphabet
\{0,1,*\}
. Each of the
members of the set partition has its own submatrix, and we ask our computer to kindly find the number of vertices in the corresponding union of
subcubes corresponding to each member of the examined set partition. Since they are disjoint, we add them up, getting
v
for that particular pair (matrix, set-partition), giving credit
1/2^{v}
.

2.3.4.
Consequence of the algorithm

: The
k
-th moment of
X_{r}
is a bivariate polynomial in
(n,2^{n})
of degree
k\,r
in
n
and degree
k
in
2^{n}
.

This raises the
theoretical
possibility (in God’s computer) of finding these expressions by
pure brute force
. The generic polynomial in
(n,2^{n})
of degree
kr
in
n
and degree
k
in
2^{n}

has
(1+kr)(1+k)
‘degrees of freedom’. So using
undetermined coefficients
we need to generate a table of
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[X_{r}^{k}](n)
for
1\leq n\leq(1+kr)\cdot(1+k)
.
After gathering the data, we use linear algebra to solve a system of
(1+kr)\cdot(1+k)
equations with that many unknowns.
For each specific

n=n_{1}
there are ‘only’
2^{2^{n_{1}}}
subsets, and for each of them we can ask how many
r
-dimensional subcubes do they contain, raise it to the
k
-th power and take the average.
Alas,
2^{2^{5}}
is already big enough, so only God’s computer, with practically infinite time and space, can carry this brute force approach.

3.
Asymptotic Normality

As said in the introduction,
asymptotic normality of
X_{r}
was proved by Urszula Konieczna
[
3
]
.
The random variable
X_{r}
depends on
n
, and for emphasis, we denote it in
the sequel by
X_{n,r}
.
We may then state the result as follows,
where
N(0,1)
denotes
the standard normal distribution.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
3
]
)
.

For every fixed
r\geq 0
, we have as
{n\to\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{X_{n,r}-\mu_{r}(n)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X_{n,r})}}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}N(0,1)

(3.1)

with convergence in distribution
and of all moments.

As already noted at the very beginning of this paper, the case
r=0
is
well known, since then the variables
X_{C}
are
independent and this becomes an instance of the central limit theorem for
binomial variables, known since a pamphlet by de Moivre in 1733.
(This case can be used as a sanity check in the arguments below.)

The proof in
[
3
]
is based on the method of moments.
We will in this section survey how a variation of this method, based on

cumulants
and
dependency graphs
leads to a quick proof with
almost no calculations.

Remark 3.2
.

Urszula Konieczna
[
3
]

considered a more general case where each subcube appears with
probability
p\in(0,1)
, where
p=p(n)
may depend on
n
, and showed
asymptotic normality for a large range of
p(n)
.
A special case, not including the case
p=\frac{1}{2}
treated here, was earlier
shown by Karl Weber
[
6
]
.
This extension can also be treated by the method below, but for simplicity
we continue to consider only the case
p=\frac{1}{2}
discussed in Section
2
.

The aim is thus to show
that the leading terms in the expressions for the
central moments

\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[(X_{n,r}-\mu_{r}(n))^{k}]
are what we would expect from a normal distribution, or,
more formally, that the scaled central moments

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}\left[\left(\frac{X_{n,r}-\mu_{r}(n)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X_{n,r})}}\right)^{k}\right]

(3.2)

converge as
{n\to\infty}

to the corresponding moment
\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[Z^{k}]
of a standard normal random
variable
Z
, for every fixed
r\geq 0
and
k\geq 1
.
Then, the random variables
X_{n,r}
are asymptotically normal by the method of
moments.

We thus want to find
the leading term of the moment (
3.2
). In Section
2
, we computed the
central moments from the pure moments. This involves massive cancellation of
high-order terms, and is less suitable for a (human) proof for general
k
.

As a first step, we may modify (
2.3
) and instead expand the central
moment as

\displaystyle\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}\bigl[(X_{n,r}-\mu_{r}(n))^{k}\bigr]

\displaystyle=\sum_{[C_{1},\dots,C_{k}]\in\mathcal{C}(n,r)^{k}}\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}\bigl[(X_{C_{1}}-\mu)\cdots(X_{C_{k}}-\mu)\bigr],

(3.3)

where
\mu=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[X_{C}]
is given by (
2.4
).
Note that two variables
X_{C_{1}}
and
X_{C_{2}}
are independent if

C_{1}\cap C_{2}=\emptyset
. In particular, for
k=2
, all such terms in the sum
in (
3.3
) vanish; this leaves only

\Theta(n^{2r}2^{n})
terms of the
\Theta\bigl(n^{2r}2^{2n}\bigr)
terms in the
full sum, or in (
2.3
), which is reflected in the leading terms in
(
2.6
) and (
2.7
).

For higher moments, we can obtain a further reduction by considering

cumulants
(also called
semiinvariants
) instead of moments.
We describe this briefly, referring to e.g.
[
4
]
or
[
2
, pp. 145–149]
for details.
In general, if
Y
is a random variable with moments
m_{k}:=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[Y^{k}]
and
moment generating function
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}m_{k}t^{k}/k!
, then the cumulants

\varkappa_{k}=\varkappa_{k}(Y)
are the coefficients of the generating function

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\varkappa_{k}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}:=\log\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}m_{k}\frac{t^{k}}{k!}\right).

(3.4)

(The generating functions can be regarded as formal power series.)
Moreover, this generalizes to mixed cumulants
\varkappa_{k}(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k})
of
several random variables.
This means that there are algebraic relations: each cumulant

\varkappa_{k}
is a polynomial
in the moments
m_{\ell}
of order
\ell\leq k
, and conversely, and this
generalizes to mixed cumulants and moments.
For example,
\varkappa_{1}=m_{1}=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}Y
, and
\varkappa_{2}=m_{2}-m_{1}^{2}=\operatorname{Var}(Y)
, and the mixed
cumulant
\varkappa(Y_{1},Y_{2})=\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[Y_{1}Y_{2}]-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[Y_{1}]\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}[Y_{2}]
, the covariance of
Y_{1}

and
Y_{2}
.

A convenient property of cumulants is that a normal distribution

N(\mu,\sigma^{2})
has moment generating function

\exp\bigl(\mu t+\tfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}t^{2}\bigr)
, and thus by (
3.4
) cumulants

\varkappa_{k}=0
for
k\geq 3
. Consequently, to show that a normalized sequence of
random variables is asymptotically normal, it suffice to show that each
cumulant
\varkappa_{k}
with
k\geq 3
converges to 0.

The mixed cumulants are multilinear, and thus we have an analog of
(
2.3
) and (
3.3
):

\displaystyle\varkappa_{k}\bigl(X_{n,r}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\varkappa\bigl(X_{n,r},\dots,X_{n,r}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{[C_{1},\dots,C_{k}]\in\mathcal{C}(n,r)^{k}}\varkappa\bigl(X_{C_{1}},\dots,X_{C_{k}}\bigr).

(3.5)

We can here reduce the number of terms in the sum by a general
property of cumulants: If there is a partition
\{1,\dots,k\}=I\cup J

into two non-empty sets
I
and
J
such the two families of random
variables

(Y_{i}\mid i\in I)
and
(Y_{j}\mid j\in J)
are independent of each other,
then the mixed cumulant
\varkappa(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{k})=0
.
(Informally, this means that the
k
th cumulant
\varkappa_{k}(X_{n,r})
can be seen
as some kind of measure of
k
th order dependencies in the sum (
2.2
),
with lower order dependencies removed.)

As noticed above, each of our variables
X_{C}
is independent of most of the
others. We need to keep track of not just pairwise independence, but also
independence between families of such variables; it is then convenient to
use the notion of
dependency graphs
.

Definition
.

Let
(Y_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}
be a family of random variables, with some
arbitrary index set
\mathcal{A}
.
A
dependency graph
for this family is a graph
\Gamma
with vertex set

\mathcal{A}
, such that if
I
and
J
are two disjoint subsets of
\mathcal{A}
such that
there is no edge in
\Gamma
with one endpoint in
I
and the other in
J
,
then the
two families
(Y_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in I)
and
(Y_{\beta}\mid\beta\in J)

are independent of each other.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\Gamma(n,r)
be the graph with vertex set
\mathcal{C}(n,r)
and an edge between
two distinct cubes
C,C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}(n,r)
iff
C\cap C^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
.
Then
\Gamma(n,r)
is a dependency graph for the family
(X_{C}\mid C\in\mathcal{C}(n,r))
.

Proof.

Let
I
and
J
be disjoint subsets of
\mathcal{C}(n,r)
with no edge between
I

and
J
.
Then
V_{I}:=\bigcup_{C\in I}C
and
V_{J}:=\bigcup_{C\in J}C
are two disjoint
subsets of
\{0,1\}^{n}
. Thus the two random sets
S\cap V_{I}
and
S\cap V_{J}

are independent. Since the random variables
(X_{C}\mid C\in I)
only
depend on
S\cap V_{I}
, and similarly for
J
,
it follows that the families

(X_{C}\mid C\in I)
and
(X_{C}\mid C\in J)
are independent.
∎

We can now use the dependency graphs
\Gamma(n,r)
and the machinery above to
estimate cumulants of
X_{n,r}
and show that

\varkappa_{k}\bigl(X_{n,r}\bigr)=o\bigl((\operatorname{Var}X_{n,r})^{k/2}\bigr)
as
{n\to\infty}
for every
fixed
r\geq 0
and
k\geq 3
. However, we do not have to do these calculations,
since they already have been done under general hypotheses, leading to
convergence theorems that are easy to apply in situations like ours where
there is a rather sparse dependency graph.
We use here the following theorem,
taken (with minor changes in notation) from
[
1
, Theorem 2, with moment convergence by its proof and Theorem 1]
.
See e.g.
[
2
, Theorems 6.18 and 6.20]
for some related theorems
(proved by the same cumulant method) that also can be used to show Theorem
3.1
,
and
[
2
, Theorem 6.33]
for another related theorem (proved by a
different method) that also yields the asymptotic normality in Theorem
3.1

(but not immediately moment convergence).

Theorem 3.4

(
[
1
]
)
.

Suppose that, for each
n
,

(Y_{n,\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\mathcal{A}_{n})
is a family of bounded random variables:

|Y_{n,\alpha}|\leq A_{n}
. Let
N_{n}:=|\mathcal{A}_{n}|
.
Suppose further that
\Gamma_{n}
is a dependency graph for this family, and
let
M_{n}
be the maximal degree of
\Gamma_{n}
(assuming this is not
0
, in which
case we let
M_{n}:=1
). Let
W_{n}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{i}}Y_{n,i}
and

\sigma^{2}_{n}:=\operatorname{Var}(W_{n})
.
If there exists a positive integer
m
such that

\displaystyle(N_{n}/M_{n})^{1/m}M_{n}A_{n}/\sigma_{n}\to 0\qquad\text{as ${n\to\infty}$},

(3.6)

then

\displaystyle\bigl(W_{n}-\operatorname{\mathbb{E}{}}W_{n}\bigr)/\sigma_{n}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\longrightarrow}N(0,1)\qquad\text{as ${n\to\infty}$},

(3.7)

with convergence in distribution and with all moments.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Fix
r\geq 0
. We let
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}
denote
unimportant positive constants that may
depend on
r
, but not on
n
.

We use Theorem
3.4
, with

Y_{n,C}:=X_{C}
for

C\in\mathcal{A}_{n}:=\mathcal{C}(n,r)
, so
W_{n}=X_{n,r}
.
We use Lemma
3.3
and take the dependency graph
\Gamma_{n}=\Gamma(n,r)
.
Then

\displaystyle N_{n}=|\mathcal{C}(n,r)|=\binom{n}{r}2^{n-r}\leq n^{r}2^{n},

(3.8)

and, by (
2.7
) (where
i=0
yields the leading term),

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{n}:=\operatorname{Var}(X_{n,r})\geq c_{1}n^{2r}2^{n}.

(3.9)

Furthermore, we may take

\displaystyle A_{n}:=1.

(3.10)

Moreover, from the definition of the graph
\Gamma(n,r)
follows that it is
regular, with degree

\displaystyle M_{n}\leq 2^{r}\binom{n}{r}\leq c_{2}n^{r}.

(3.11)

We choose
m=3
in Theorem
3.4
. (This is the standard case, which corresponds
to the method of moment without extra arguments, see the proof in

[
1
]
.)
Then (
3.8
)–(
3.11
) yield

\displaystyle(N_{n}/M_{n})^{1/m}M_{n}A_{n}/\sigma_{n}=N_{n}^{1/3}M_{n}^{2/3}A_{n}/\sigma_{n}\leq c_{3}\frac{n^{r}2^{n/3}}{n^{r}2^{n/2}}\to 0

(3.12)

as
{n\to\infty}
, so (
3.6
) holds. Consequently, Theorem
3.4
applies and shows
Theorem
3.1
.
∎

4.
Conclusion

In the first part, we demonstrated the power of computers to
automatically
derive complicated explicit expressions, all polynomials in
n
and
2^{n}
, for low moments of the
random variable
number of fixed-size implicants
of a Boolean function of
n
variable, or equivalently, the number of fixed-dimensional subcubes of
a random subset of the
n
-dimensional unit cube. These implied that the scaled first few moments for low-dimensional subcubes
tend, as
n
goes to infinity, to those of the Normal Distribution, suggesting, but by no means proving, that for each fixed dimension, this random variable
is
asymptotically normal
.

In the second part, we demonstrated the power of
human ingenuity
, and
the traditional lore of probability and statistics, by presenting a simple proof
of asymptotic normality, first proved in
[
3
]
.
