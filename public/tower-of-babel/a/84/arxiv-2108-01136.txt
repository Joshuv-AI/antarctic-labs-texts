Dirac operators for matrix algebras converging to coadjoint orbits
Marc A. Rieffel

Abstract
. In the high-energy physics literature one finds statements such as “matrix algebras converge to the sphere”. Earlier I provided a general precise setting for understanding such statements, in which the matrix algebras are viewed as quantum metric spaces, and convergence is with respect to a quantum Gromov-Hausdorff-type distance. But physicists want even more to treat structures on spheres (and other spaces), such as vector bundles, Yang-Mills functionals, Dirac operators, etc., and they want to approximate these by corresponding structures on matrix algebras. In the present paper we provide a somewhat unified construction of Dirac operators on coadjoint orbits and on the matrix algebras that converge to them. This enables us to prove our main theorem, whose content is that, for the quantum metric-space structures determined by the Dirac operators that we construct, the matrix algebras do indeed converge to the coadjoint orbits, for a quite strong version of quantum Gromov-Hausdorff distance.

Introduction

In the literature of theoretical high-energy physics one finds statements along the lines
of “this sequence of matrix algebras converges to the sphere”
and “here are the Dirac operators on the
matrix algebras that correspond to the Dirac operator on the sphere”. But one also finds
that at least three inequivalent definitions of Dirac operators have been proposed in this context.
See, for example,
[
4
,
5
,
7
,
15
,

,

,

,
86
]
and the references
they contain.
In
[
69
,
70
,
73
]
I provided definitions and theorems that give a precise meaning to
the idea of
the convergence of matrix algebras to spheres. This involved equipping the matrix
algebras in a natural way with the structure of a non-commutative (or “quantum”)
compact metric space (motivated by the “spectral triples”
of Connes
[
20
,
18
,
19
,
21
]
),
and developing non-commutative (or “quantum”) versions
of the usual Gromov-Hausdorff distance between
compact metric spaces.
These results were developed in the general context of coadjoint orbits of compact Lie groups,
which is the appropriate context for this topic, as is clear from the physics literature. (In
[
48
]
it is shown that the matrix algebras form a
strict quantization of the coadjoint orbits. A much-simplified proof of
much of that
fact, in the more general context of compact quantum groups, is given
in proposition 4.13 of
[
77
]
.)

The purpose of the present paper is to provide a somewhat unified construction
of Dirac operators (always
G
-invariant) that handles both the
matrix algebras and the corresponding coadjoint orbits.
This enables us to prove the main theorem of this paper, which states that
for the quantum metrics determined by the Dirac operators
on the matrix algebras and on the coadjoint orbits,
the matrix algebras do indeed converge to the coadjoint orbits, in fact for a stronger
version of quantum Gromov-Hausdorff distance than I had used earlier.

Roughly speaking, physicists who try to develop quantum field
theory on spaces like the sphere,
have found attractive the idea of approximating the spaces by
means of matrix algebras because
if they try “lattice approximations” by a collections of
points, they lose the action of the symmetry group,
whereas the matrix algebras can be viewed as
quantum finite sets, on which the symmetry group still acts.
It is my hope that when results of the kind contained in this paper are extended to further
types of structure of interest to quantum physicists, such as Yang-Mills functionals
[
22
,
66
]

and other action functionals, then the fact that we have quantified the idea of distance
between matrix algebras and spaces will help in quantifying the size of the error made
by approximating
quantities in quantum field theory on the spaces by corresponding quantities obtained
for quantum field theory on the approximating matrix algebras. (Another type of structure
for which this kind of approximation theory has already been worked out consists of vector
bundles
[
75
,
51
]
.)

Here is a brief imprecise sketch of what our construction of Dirac operators looks like
for the case of the matrix algebras

For each positive integer

{\mathfrak{g}}=su(2)

Let

\Omega^{n}={\mathcal{B}}^{n}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}

{\mathcal{B}}^{n}

We let
\alpha

infinitesimal action of
{\mathfrak{g}}
on
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}

for each

its total differential,

of

defined by

for all

As inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}
we use the negative of the

{\mathfrak{g}}

of the inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}

{\mathcal{B}}^{n}
, viewed as a

S,T\in{\mathcal{B}}^{n}
and
\nu,\mu\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}

{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})

be the complex Clifford algebra corresponding to the inner product

The spinor bundle is then

Let
\{E_{j}\}_{j=1}^{3}

{\mathfrak{g}}

{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}

(0.1)

This Dirac operator has many attractive properties, which
we will describe later. In particular, we will see that it is closely
related to the Casimir element for the chosen inner product

, and that this gives an attractive way of calculating

. When the main theorem of this paper,

is applied to the case of the sphere, it tells us that when the sphere
is equipped with its usual metric (which corresponds to its Dirac operator),

Our construction of Dirac operators on matrix algebras sketched above
is closely related to some of the proposals in the physics literature.
At the end of Section
9
we give a substantial discussion of the
relationship between our construction and various proposals,
for the case of the sphere.
In section
19
we further discuss how our construction relates
to proposals in the physics literature for Dirac operators on
other coadjoint orbits,
such as projective spaces.

Our general construction has some deficiencies. We discuss these deficiencies
in some detail in Section
18
. But briefly,
Dirac operators on Riemannian manifolds are usually constructed using the
the Levi–Civita connection, which is the unique torsion-free connection
compatible with the Riemannian metric. But our construction
in essence uses the “canonical connection”, and as is well-known,
and explained in section 6 of
[
71
]
, for homogeneous spaces
the Levi–Civita connection agrees with the canonical connection only for
symmetric spaces.
The consequence is that our construction is very satisfactory for the case in which
the coadjoint orbit is a symmetric space,
such as the sphere or a projective space,
but somewhat less satisfactory otherwise, though we will see that
the metric from the canonical connection agrees with the metric
from the Levi–Civita connection, so our
sequence of matrix algebras does converge to the coadjoint orbit
for the Dirac operator for its Levi–Civita connection.

A related deficiency, discussed further in Section
18
, comes from
the fact that each highest weight determines
a
G
-invariant Kähler structure on its coadjoint orbit,
which includes not just a Riemannian structure,
but also a closely related complex structure and symplectic structure.
But these Riemannian
metrics are not related to the Killing form unless
the coadjoint orbit is a symmetric space.
When that is not the case, our sequence of matrix algebras will not converge
to the coadjoint orbit equipped with the Riemannian metric from the Kähler structure,
because it converges
to the coadjoint orbit equipped with the Riemannian metric related to the Killing form.

This paper concludes with a final section that discusses how
the main results of this paper are related to the very interesting
“spectral propinquity” of Latrémolière
[
54
]
, which is a metric on
equivalent classes of spectral triples. The conclusion that is reached is that the spectral propinquity is too strong, in the sense that the spectral triples
that we construct for matrix algebras do not converge to the spectral triples of coadjoint orbits for the spectral propinquity. On the other hand, the present paper says nothing about convergence of these spectral triples. It only
shows that for the C*-metrics coming from the spectral triples, the matrix
algebras do converge to the coadjoint orbits for Latrémolière’s Gromov-Hausdorff
propinquity
[
50
]
. It would be very desirable to
have a weaker form of convergence for spectral triples than the spectral
propinquity, for which our spectral triples for the matrix
algebras do converge to the spectral triples for the coadjoint orbits.
Perhaps there might be something roughly along the lines that Lott used for ordinary Dirac operators
[
59
,
58
]
.

There is a substantial relationship between the Dirac operators on matrix algebras
that we construct here and the more general “matrix models” that are being
intensively explored in the literature of theoretical high-energy physics.
See
[
80
]
(some of whose examples are “fuzzy” spaces of the kind
studied in the present paper) and its many references. It
would be very interesting to explore how some of the ideas in the present paper
might be extended to various matrix models, such as those
in
[

,
62
,
63
,
64
,
80
,

,

.

1.
Ergodic actions and Dirac operators

In this section we construct a rough approximation to the Dirac
operators that we seek. We do this in the general setting of ergodic
actions of compact Lie groups on unital C*-algebras. This rough approximation
will be crucial for the proofs of some of our main results.

Let
G
be a connected compact Lie group. In this paper we are concerned
with two types of actions of
G
on unital C*-algebras. One type consists of
actions by translation on the C*-algebras
C(G/K)
of continuous
complex-valued functions on homogenous spaces
G/K

is a
closed subgroup of
G
. The other type consists of actions on the C*-algebra

{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
consisting of all the operators on the Hilbert space

of
an irrep of
G
, where the action of
G
on
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})

is by conjugation by the representation. In both cases the action is
ergodic in the sense that the only elements of the algebra that are
invariant under the action of
G
are scaler multiples of the identity element.
Consequently it is natural to begin by considering general ergodic actions
on unital C*-algebras.

Thus let
\alpha
be an ergodic action of
G
on a unital C*-algebra

.
Using the fact that

is a Lie group, we can define the subalgebra

of smooth elements of

such that the function

to

is infinitely differentiable for the norm on
{\mathcal{A}}
.
This means that for any
X\in{\mathfrak{g}}
, where

is the Lie algebra of
G
,
there is an element,
\alpha_{X}(a)\in{\mathcal{A}}
, such that

,
(where

It is a standard fact that

is a Lie algebra homomorphism from

into the Lie algebra of
*

{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}

.
(See Sections 3 and 4 of chapter III of
[

It is easily verified that

Let

In section 4 of
[

we briefly constructed some Dirac-type operators

{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}

recall the construction, slightly reformulated in a way that will be more

29

are defined in terms of a Riemannian metric on the tangent bundle of

To aid the reader’s intuition, in this

is a

To construct a Dirac operator in our general framework, we
need a “Riemannian metric”

. For this purpose we choose an inner product

{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
that is
\mathrm{Cad}
-invariant. (Because

{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
using the

We fix such an inner product, and denote it just by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. We view

\Omega_{o}
as a right
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-module, and our Riemannian metric on
\Omega_{o}
is the

{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-valued inner product on

that is given on elementary tensors (necessarily over

) by

\langle a\otimes\mu,b\otimes\nu\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}=a^{*}b\langle\mu,\nu\rangle

,

This “bundle metric” is easily checked to respect the action

For our chosen inner product on

Much as in

.
It is the complexification

with our chosen inner product.
We follow the convention that the defining relation is

\mu\nu+\nu\mu=-2\langle\mu,\nu\rangle 1

We include the minus sign for consistency with

Thus if one wants to apply the results of the first pages of

is
\alpha
-invariant, the action

\alpha
is unitary with respect to the inner product that
\tau
determines on

{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
, and so it extends to a unitary representation on
L^{2}({\mathcal{A}},\tau)
.
Because
G
is compact, the irreducible
\alpha
-invariant subspaces of

{\mathcal{A}}
are finite-dimensional, and so are contained in
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
, and the
span of all of them together is dense in
{\mathcal{A}}
. It follows that
L^{2}({\mathcal{A}},\tau)

decomposes into a (Hilbert space)-direct sum of orthogonal
finite-dimensional
\alpha
-invariant subspaces of
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
. Consequently, since

{\mathcal{S}}
is finite-dimensional,

L^{2}({\mathcal{A}},\tau)\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
decomposes into a direct sum of orthogonal
finite-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
that are each invariant
under the action
\alpha\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}}
of
G
. From the definition of

D_{o}
it is evident that
D_{o}
carries each of these finite-dimensional
subspaces into itself. From this and the fact that
D_{o}
is symmetric,
it is easy to obtain:

Proposition 1.4
.

As an operator on
L^{2}({\mathcal{A}},\tau)\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
with dense domain

{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
, the operator
D_{o}
is essentially self-adjoint,
and there is an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}({\mathcal{A}},\tau)\otimes{\mathcal{S}}

consisting of elements of

{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
that are eigenvectors of
D_{o}
.

What is not so easy to see is that when
{\mathcal{A}}
is infinite-dimensional
the eigenvalues of
D_{o}
, counted with multiplicity,
converge in absolute value to
\infty
(i.e.
D_{o}

has “compact resolvent”). A somewhat indirect
proof of this fact is given in theorem 5.5 of
[
29
]
.

For
a\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
let
M_{a}
denote the operator on

{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
corresponding to the left regular
representation of
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
on itself. As seen in
[
67
]
,
a simple calculation shows that
for any

and
\psi\in{\mathcal{S}}

[D_{o},M_{a}](b\otimes\psi)=\sum(M_{\alpha_{E_{j}}(a)}\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}})(b\otimes\psi).

To simplify notation we will usually write
\alpha_{E_{j}}(a)
instead of

M_{\alpha_{E_{j}}(a)}
from now on. Then we see that we obtain:

Proposition 1.5
.

With notation as above, for any
a\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
we have

[D_{o},M_{a}]=\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}(a)\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}},

acting on
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
.

From this it is clear that
[D_{o},M_{a}]

is a bounded operator. Because of this, we can define a seminorm on

{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
that will play a central role in this paper.

Notation 1.6
.

For notation as above, define

L^{D_{o}}
on
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
by

L^{D_{o}}(a)=\|[D_{o},M_{a}]\|.

We will develop the properties of
L^{D_{o}}
in later sections.

The results described in this section are crucial for some of the proofs
of our main results. But they are often not entirely adequate for the
following reason. The usual definition of a first-order
differential calculus requires that the bimodule

\Omega
be generated by the range of
{\mathrm{d}}
.
As we will see, for many examples the bimodule
\Omega_{o}

that we have used above does not have this property.
But before turning to those examples, we will soon consider two
classes of example for which
\Omega_{o}
essentially
does have this property.
For now we will make
a general observation about
\Omega
for our situation. Notice that its
elements will be finite sums of the form

\sum_{k}a_{k}{\mathrm{d}}b_{k}
for
a_{k},b_{k}\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
.
(One uses the derivation property to show that right multiplication
of such sums by elements of
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
are again of this form.)
Let
{\mathcal{K}}
be a Hilbert space, and let
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{K}})
be the algebra of bounded
operators on
{\mathcal{K}}
. Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a
*
-subalgebra of
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{K}})
, and let

D
be a (possibly unbounded) self-adjoint operator on
{\mathcal{K}}
having the
property that
[D,a]
extends to a bounded operator on
{\mathcal{K}}
for any

a\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
(so that if a few more axioms are satisfied,

({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{K}},D)
is a spectral
triple as defined by Connes
[
20
,
18
,
19
,
21
]
).
We can view
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{K}})
as an

{\mathcal{A}}
-
{\mathcal{A}}
-bimodule in the evident way. Then the map

\delta:a\to[D,a]
is a derivation from
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
into
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{K}})
. Connes sets

\Omega_{D}
to be the
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-subbimodule of
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{K}})
generated by
the range of
\delta
(see section 7.2 of
[
47
]
.), so that its elements are finite sums of the form

\sum_{k}a_{k}[D,b_{k}]
for
a_{k},b_{k}\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
. Connes views
\Omega_{D}

as a space of first-order differential forms on
{\mathcal{A}}
.

Let us see what
\Omega_{D_{o}}
is for
D_{o}
as defined earlier in this section.
Thus
{\mathcal{K}}={\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
, completed. For
M
denoting the left regular
representation of
{\mathcal{A}}
on itself, we see that
M\otimes\kappa
is a
representation of
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
on
{\mathcal{K}}
. Its restriction to

{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
is an
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-bimodule map of
\Omega_{o}

into
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{K}})
. For an element
\sum_{k}a_{k}{\mathrm{d}}b_{k}
of

\Omega
we have

(M\otimes\kappa)(\sum_{k}a_{k}{\mathrm{d}}b_{k})=\sum_{k}M_{a_{k}}\sum_{j}M_{E_{j}(b_{k})}\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}}=\sum_{k}M_{a_{k}}[D_{o},M_{b_{k}}].

We thus obtain:

Proposition 1.7
.

For notation as above,
\Omega_{D_{o}}
is the image of
\Omega_{o}
under the map
M\otimes\kappa
.

2.
Invariance under the group actions

Since the action
\mathrm{Cad}
of
G
on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
is by orthogonal operators
for our chosen inner product, it extends to
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
as an
action by
*
-algebra automorphisms
(Bogoliubov automorphisms), which we still denote by
\mathrm{Cad}
.
Since
G
acts on both
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
, we let
\gamma
denote the
corresponding diagonal action
\alpha\otimes\mathrm{Cad}
on
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
.
It is just an extension of our earlier action
\gamma
of
G

on
\Omega_{o}
defined in Notation
1.1
.
It carries the smooth version into itself.

We want
to have a representation of G by unitary operators on the Hilbert-space
completion of

{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
that is compatible with the action
\alpha
on
{\mathcal{A}}
, and
that commutes with
D_{o}
. As discussed in
[
29
]
, the action
of
G
on
{\mathcal{S}}
corresponding to the action of
G

on
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
can, in general, at best be implemented by a
projective representation on
{\mathcal{S}}
. In
[
29
]
it is shown
how to handle the case of projective representations, but it is
also remarked there that if
G
is a simply connected semisimple
compact Lie group then every projective representation of
G

is equivalent to an ordinary (unitary) representation of
G
, so
that for these groups there is a unitary representations on
{\mathcal{S}}

corresponding to the action of
G
on
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
.

So for the moment, we will simply assume that there is a
unitary representation,
\sigma
, of
G
on
{\mathcal{S}}
that is
compatible with the action
\mathrm{Cad}
of
G
on
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
and
the representation
\kappa
of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
on
{\mathcal{S}}
in the
sense that

(2.1)

\kappa_{\mathrm{Cad}_{x}(q)}=\sigma_{x}\kappa_{q}\sigma^{*}_{x}

for all
q\in{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
and
x\in G
. We then define a
representation,
\tilde{\sigma}
, of
G
on
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}

by
\tilde{\sigma}=\alpha\otimes\sigma
.

Proposition 2.1
.

With assumptions and notation as above,
the operator
D_{o}
on
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}

commutes with the representation
\tilde{\sigma}
of
G

on
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
.

Proof.

Let
a\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
and
\psi\in{\mathcal{S}}
and
x\in G
. Then

\displaystyle D_{o}(\tilde{\sigma}_{x}(a\otimes\psi))

\displaystyle=D_{o}(\alpha_{x}(a)\otimes\sigma_{x}(\psi))=\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}(\alpha_{x}(a)))\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}}(\sigma_{x}(\psi))

\displaystyle=\sum\alpha_{x}(\alpha_{\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits_{x}^{-1}(E_{j})}(a))\otimes\sigma_{x}(\kappa_{\mathrm{Cad}_{x}^{-1}(\epsilon_{j})}(\sigma_{x}^{-1}(\sigma_{x}(\psi))))

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{x}\otimes\sigma_{x})(\sum(\alpha_{\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits_{x}^{-1}(E_{j})}(a))\otimes(\kappa_{\mathrm{Cad}_{x}^{-1}(\epsilon_{j})}(\psi))

\displaystyle=\tilde{\sigma}_{x}(D_{o}(a\otimes\psi)),

where for the third equality we have used the compatibility condition,
and for the fifth equality we have used the fact that
\{\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits_{x}^{-1}(E_{j})\}

is equally well a basis for
{\mathfrak{g}}
, with dual basis
\{\mathrm{Cad}_{x}^{-1}(\epsilon_{j})\}
.
∎

The representation
\tilde{\sigma}
is unitary, so it extends to a unitary
representation on the Hilbert space completion
of
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
. We will discuss further the possible existence
of the representation
\sigma
in Section
7
.

Proposition 2.2
.

With assumptions and notation as above,
the seminorm
L^{D_{o}}
defined in Notation
1.6

is invariant under the action
\alpha
in the sense that

L^{D_{o}}(\alpha_{x}(a))=L^{D_{o}}(a)

for all
a\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
and
x\in G
.

Proof.

Note that for any
a,b\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
,
\psi\in{\mathcal{S}}
and
x\in G
we have

\displaystyle(\tilde{\sigma}_{x}M_{a}\tilde{\sigma}_{x}^{-1})(b\otimes\psi)

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{x}\otimes\sigma_{x})(M_{a}\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}})(\alpha_{x}^{-1}\otimes\sigma_{x}^{-1})(b\otimes\psi)

\displaystyle=(\alpha_{x}(a(\alpha_{x}^{-1}(b))\otimes\psi=M_{\alpha_{x}(a)}(b\otimes\psi),

where we have used
M
to denote the left action of
{\mathcal{A}}
on both

{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
. Thus

(\tilde{\sigma}_{x}M_{a}\tilde{\sigma}_{x}^{-1})=M_{\alpha_{x}(a)}.

Since
\tilde{\sigma}_{x}
commutes with
D_{o}
, it follows that

[D_{o},M_{\alpha_{x}(a)}]=D_{o}\tilde{\sigma}_{x}M_{a}\tilde{\sigma}_{x}^{-1}-\tilde{\sigma}_{x}M_{a}\tilde{\sigma}_{x}^{-1}D_{o}=\tilde{\sigma}_{x}[D_{o},M_{a}]\tilde{\sigma}_{x}^{-1}.

Since
\tilde{\sigma}_{x}
is a unitary operator for the inner product on

{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
, we obtain the desired equality.
∎

3.
Charge Conjugation

In
[
29
]
it is shown that a Dirac operator constructed in the way described above possesses an
important structure on its domain, namely, a “real structure”,
related to a charge conjugation operator, as first used by Connes
[
19
]

for non-commutative geometry.
We do not need to use this structure later,
so we will give here only a brief description of it, with references to
the literature for further details.

Since
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
is the complexification of the Clifford algebra

C\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
over
{\mathbb{R}}
, it has the standard complex conjugation operator,
which is a conjugate-linear algebra automorphism. We denote it by

q\mapsto\bar{q}
for
q\in{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
. Let
\chi
denote
the usual grading automorphism on
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
determined by

\chi(\mu)=-\mu
for
\mu\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
. By definition, the charge conjugation
at the level of the Clifford algebra is the conjugate-linear automorphism,

c
, obtained by composing complex conjugation with
\chi
. Note that on
the even subalgebra

{\mathbb{C}}\ell^{e}({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
it is just complex conjugation.

But the operator that we need is a conjugate-linear operator

\mathsf{C}_{\mathcal{S}}
on
{\mathcal{S}}
that implements

c
for the representation
\kappa
, that is, such that

\kappa_{c(q)}=\mathsf{C}_{\mathcal{S}}\kappa_{q}\mathsf{C}_{\mathcal{S}}^{-1}

for all
q\in{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
. Furthermore, the operator
\mathsf{C}_{\mathcal{S}}
is required
to respect the inner product on
{\mathcal{S}}
in the sense that

\langle\mathsf{C}_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi),\mathsf{C}_{\mathcal{S}}\phi\rangle=\langle\phi,\psi\rangle

for all
\psi,\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}
.
Then
\mathsf{C}_{\mathcal{S}}
is unique up to a scaler multiple of modulus 1.
It is normalized to satisfy
\mathsf{C}_{\mathcal{S}}^{2}=\pm I^{\mathcal{S}}
, where the sign
depends on
\dim({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
.

Then the charge-conjugation operator,
\mathsf{C}
, on

{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
is defined on elementary tensors by

\mathsf{C}(a\otimes\psi)=a^{*}\otimes\mathsf{C}_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi).

It is conjugate-linear,
and respects the
{\mathcal{A}}
-valued inner product on
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
.
Its most important property is that

[a,\mathsf{C}b^{*}\mathsf{C}^{-1}]=0

for all
a,b\in{\mathcal{A}}
, so that
b\mapsto\mathsf{C}b^{*}\mathsf{C}^{-1}

gives a right action of

{\mathcal{A}}
on
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
such that
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
is an

{\mathcal{A}}
-
{\mathcal{A}}
-bimodule. When
\dim({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
is even we also have

\mathsf{C}{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\pm{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mathsf{C}
,
where the sign depends on
\dim({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
.
All this is summarized by saying that the charge conjugation operator

\mathsf{C}
provides a “real structure” on
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
.

For the Dirac operator
D_{o}
constructed above we then have the very
important condition that

[[D_{o},a],\mathsf{C}b^{*}\mathsf{C}^{-1}]=0

for all
a,b\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
. It is called the “first-order condition”, and reflects
the fact that
D_{o}
is like a differential operator of order one. Furthermore,
we have
\mathsf{C}D_{o}=\pm D_{o}\mathsf{C}
, where again the sign
depends on
\dim({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
. When
{\mathcal{H}}
is the Hilbert-space completion of

{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
, the triple
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{H}},D_{o})
is an example of the notion of
“spectral triple” introduced by Connes, and
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{H}},D_{o},C)

is an example of a “real spectral triples”. For details about all of this see

[
9
,
19
,
18
,
21
,
23
,
30
,
84
,
83
]
.

Remark 3.1
.

The results obtained in these first sections suggest the following questions:

1. For a given compact connected Lie group
G
, how can one characterize which of its ergodic
actions
({\mathcal{A}},\alpha)
have the property that the sub-bimodule,
\Omega
, of
\Omega_{o}={\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}

generated by the range of the derivation
{\mathrm{d}}
is (finitely generated) projective as a
right
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-module?

2. Among those actions for which
\Omega
is a projective module, how does one characterize those
such that
\Omega
also admits a “real structure” of the kind sketched above?

I have not investigated these questions.

Note that much concerning the classification of ergodic actions
of connected compact Lie groups is a mystery.
Good answers are known only for
G
commutative
[
61
]
,
or for
G=SU(2)

[
85
]
(and subsequent papers) as far as I know.

4.
Casimir operators

Dirac found his famous equation that predicted the existence of the positron
because he was looking for a first-order
differential operator that is a square root of the Klein-Gordan operator,
which is the appropriate
Laplace-type operator for flat space-time. So it is of interest to examine
the square of the
Dirac operator that we have defined above to see whether it is related to a
Laplace operator. For a Lie group,
G
, whose Lie algebra

{\mathfrak{g}}
has a chosen
Ad
-invariant
(non-degenerate) inner product, the appropriate Laplace operators
come from the degree-2 Casimir element in the universal enveloping
algebra
\mathcal{U}({\mathfrak{g}})
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
The Casimir element depends on the choice of inner product. Let

\{E_{j}\}
be a basis for
{\mathfrak{g}}
that is orthonormal for the chosen inner product.
Then the Casimir element,
C
, is defined by
C=\sum_{j}(E_{j})^{2}
.
(We do not include the minus sign which is often used, so the image
of
C
by the infinitesimal version of a
unitary representations of
G
will be a non-positive operator.).
Then
C
is in the center of
\mathcal{U}({\mathfrak{g}})
,
and so for any irrep
(U,{\mathcal{H}})

of
G
, with corresponding representation of
{\mathfrak{g}}
and
\mathcal{U}({\mathfrak{g}})
,
the operator

U_{C}
will be a scalar multiple of the identity operator on
{\mathcal{H}}
.
We will find this useful in Section
9
for determining the spectrum
of the Dirac operator when
G=SU(2)
. Anyway, for
D_{o}
acting on

{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
as
D_{o}=\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}\otimes\kappa_{e_{j}}
we have
(as in the first displayed equation in the proof of
theorem 6.3 of
[
29
]
):

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\{\epsilon_{j}\}
be the basis for
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
dual to the orthonormal basis
\{E_{j}\}

for
{\mathfrak{g}}
. Then

D_{o}^{2}=-\alpha_{C}\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}}+\sum_{j<k}\alpha_{[E_{j},E_{k}]}\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}\epsilon_{k}}

Proof.

Since in
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
we have
\epsilon_{j}\epsilon_{k}=-\epsilon_{k}\epsilon_{j}
and
\epsilon_{j}^{2}=-1
, we have

\displaystyle D_{o}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}\alpha_{E_{j}}\alpha_{E_{k}}\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}}\kappa_{\epsilon_{k}}

\displaystyle=\ \sum_{j}(\alpha_{E_{j}})^{2}\otimes(\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}})^{2}+\sum_{j<k}\alpha_{E_{j}}\alpha_{E_{k}}\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}\epsilon_{k}}+\sum_{j>k}\alpha_{E_{j}}\alpha_{E_{k}}\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}\epsilon_{k}}

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{C}\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}}+\sum_{j<k}(\alpha_{E_{j}}\alpha_{E_{k}}-\alpha_{E_{k}}\alpha_{E_{j}})\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}\epsilon_{k}}

\displaystyle=-\alpha_{C}\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}}+\sum_{j<k}\alpha_{[E_{j},E_{k}]}\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}\epsilon_{k}}\ ,

as desired.
∎

It is appropriate to view
\alpha_{C}
as the Laplace operator on
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
, and so the term

-\alpha_{C}\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}}
is very analogous to what one obtains for the square of the Dirac operator
on flat
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. The second term can be viewed as some kind of curvature term
analogous to the curvature term in the Lichnerowicz formula
[
12
,
28
,
55
]
,
but I do not know how to define a general version of curvature that
would make this precise.

For later use in Section
9

we need the following result about the image of
C
under the
representation

\tilde{\sigma}=\alpha\otimes\sigma
defined just before Proposition
2.1
.
We let
\sigma
also denote the corresponding representation of
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Then
\tilde{\sigma}
, as a representation of
{\mathfrak{g}}
, is given by

\tilde{\sigma}_{X}=\alpha_{X}\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}}+I^{\mathcal{A}}\otimes\sigma_{X}
for any
X\in{\mathfrak{g}}
,
where
I^{\mathcal{S}}
and
I^{\mathcal{A}}
denote the identity operators on
{\mathcal{S}}

and
{\mathcal{A}}
respectively.

Proposition 4.2
.

For notation as above, we have

\tilde{\sigma}_{C}=\alpha_{C}\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}}+2\sum_{j}(\alpha_{E_{j}}\otimes\sigma_{E_{j}})+I^{\mathcal{A}}\otimes\sigma_{C},

Proof.

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}_{C}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}(\alpha_{E_{j}}\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}}+I^{\mathcal{A}}\otimes\sigma_{E_{j}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}(\alpha_{E_{j}})^{2}\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}}+2\sum_{j}(\alpha_{E_{j}}\otimes\sigma_{E_{j}})+\sum_{j}I^{\mathcal{A}}\otimes(\sigma_{E_{j}})^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{C}\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}}+2\sum_{j}(\alpha_{E_{j}}\otimes\sigma_{E_{j}})+I^{\mathcal{A}}\otimes\sigma_{C}.

∎

The operator
\sum_{j}(\alpha_{E_{j}}\otimes\sigma_{E_{j}})
from the middle term above, looks somewhat
like our expression for the Dirac operator. We will see in Section
9
that for the case of

G=SU(2)
it does in fact essentially coincide with the Dirac operator. This gives us
a strong tool for computing the spectrum of the Dirac operator.

The results above have some resonance with results in the neighborhood of equation 2.7,
theorem 2.13 and theorem 2.21 of

[
46
]
relating Dirac operators and Casimir elements, but that paper is aimed only at
homogeneous spaces
G/K
where
G
is a compact semisimple group and

K
is a connected subgroup of
G
whose rank is the same as that of
G
.
That setting is very pertinent to the coadjoint orbits that we will consider later.

5.
First facts about spinors

Let
{\mathfrak{m}}
be a finite-dimensional Hilbert space over
{\mathbb{R}}
, and
let
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
denote the complex
Clifford algebra for
{\mathfrak{m}}
,
much as discussed above for
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
.
For its standard involution
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
is a C*-algebra.

There is a special
element,
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
, called the “chirality element”
[
30
]
,
that is a suitably normalized
product of all the elements of a basis for

{\mathfrak{m}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
, and that has the properties that

{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{2}=1
,
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\neq 1
and
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*}={\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
.
Let
n=\dim({\mathfrak{m}})
.
If
n
is even then
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
is
isomorphic to a full matrix algebra. For this case
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

splits an irreducible representation of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})

into two subspaces of equal dimension that are carried
into themselves by the subalgebra
{\mathbb{C}}\ell^{e}({\mathfrak{m}})
of even elements of

{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
. There is a standard way of explicitly constructing
an irreducible representation of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
on a Fock-space.
We will need to use some of the main steps of that construction
in Section
13
.

When
n
is odd,
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is in the center of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})

and it splits
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
into the direct sum of 2
full matrix algebras. Thus up to equivalence
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})

has two inequivalent irreducible representations, neither of which is faithful.
The subalgebra,
{\mathbb{C}}\ell^{e}({\mathfrak{m}})
, of even element of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})

is itself a Clifford algebra
on a vector space of even dimension, and so is a full matrix algebra,
which has a unique irreducible representation (up to isomorphism).
We will view the two irreducible representations of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})

as being the irreducible representation of

{\mathbb{C}}\ell^{e}({\mathfrak{m}})
, extended to
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
by sending
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

to
+I
for one of them, and to
-I
for the other. (Notice that the restriction to

{\mathbb{C}}\ell^{e}({\mathfrak{m}})
of any (unital) representation of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
will be a faithful
representation of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell^{e}({\mathfrak{m}})
.)

Now let
\kappa
be a representation of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
on
a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
{\mathcal{S}}
.
To deal with the fact that
\kappa
need not be a faithful representation
of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
,
we need the following technical result.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let notation be as above, and let
\epsilon_{1},\cdots,\epsilon_{p}
be elements of
{\mathfrak{m}}
.
Let
{\mathcal{D}}
be any unital C*-algebra, and let
({\mathcal{K}},M)
be a faithful
representation of
{\mathcal{D}}
. Let

d_{1},\cdots,d_{p}
be elements
of
{\mathcal{D}}
, and let
t=\sum d_{j}\otimes\epsilon_{j}
, viewed as an
element of the C*-algebra
{\mathcal{D}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
. Then

\|(M\otimes\kappa)(t)\|=\|t\|.

Proof.

Since
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
is finite dimensional, there is a unique
C*-algebra norm on
{\mathcal{D}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
.
If the dimension
n
of
{\mathfrak{m}}
is even, then the representation

\kappa
of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
must be faithful, and so the homomorphism

M\otimes\kappa
between C*-algebras must be faithful and so
isometric. This gives the desired result.

If
n
is odd, then
because
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is an odd unitary element of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
, left
multiplication by
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is an isometry from the odd subspace of

{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
onto
{\mathbb{C}}\ell^{e}({\mathfrak{m}})
. Thus multiplication by

1_{\mathcal{D}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is an isometry that carries
t
into

{\mathcal{D}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\ell^{e}({\mathfrak{m}})
.
But the restriction of
\kappa
to

{\mathbb{C}}\ell^{e}({\mathfrak{m}})
is faithful, and so
M\otimes\kappa
is faithful
and so an isometry from

{\mathcal{D}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\ell^{e}({\mathfrak{m}})
into
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{K}})\otimes{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{S}})
.
Consequently

\|t\|=\|(1_{\mathcal{D}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})t\|=\|(M\otimes\kappa)(1_{\mathcal{D}}\otimes{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})t\|=\|(M\otimes\kappa)(t)\|,

since
\kappa_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}=\pm I^{\mathcal{S}}
.
∎

The following proposition, which will be of use later,
is a typical way in which we will apply the above Lemma.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a unital C*-algebra, and let
\alpha
be an ergodic
action of the Lie group
G
on
{\mathcal{A}}
. Fix a choice of a
finite-dimensional spinor
space
{\mathcal{S}}
, and use it to construct the Dirac operators
D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}

for
{\mathcal{A}}
as in equation
1.1
. Then

L^{D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}}(a)=\|[D_{o},M_{a}]\|=\|\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}(a)\otimes{\epsilon_{j}}\|,

where the norm on the right is that on the C*-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
.
Thus
L^{D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}}
is independent of the choice of the spinor
space
{\mathcal{S}}
.

Proof.

We use Lemma
5.1
for the last equality in

\displaystyle\|[D_{o},M_{a}]\|

\displaystyle=\|\sum M_{\alpha_{E_{j}}(a)}\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}}\|

\displaystyle=\|(M\otimes\kappa)(\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}(a)\otimes{\epsilon_{j}})\|=\|\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}(a)\otimes{\epsilon_{j}}\|.

∎

The following proposition will be important for the proof of our main theorem.
In that proof the
\theta
here will be a Berezin symbol map.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
be unital C*-algebras, and let
\alpha
and
\beta
be ergodic
actions of the Lie group
G
on
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
. Fix a choice of a
finite-dimensional spinor
space
{\mathcal{S}}
, and use it to construct Dirac operators
D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}
and

D_{o}^{\mathcal{B}}
for
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
as above.
Let
L^{D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}}
and
L^{D_{o}^{\mathcal{B}}}
be the seminorms determined
by
D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}
and
D_{o}^{\mathcal{B}}
as
in Notation
1.6
.
Let
\theta
be a
unital completely positive operator from

{\mathcal{A}}
to
{\mathcal{B}}
that intertwines the actions
\alpha
and
\beta
,
so that it carries
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
into
{\mathcal{B}^{\infty}}
.
Then for any
a\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
we have

L^{D_{o}^{\mathcal{B}}}(\theta(a))\leq L^{D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}}(a).

Proof.

According to Proposition
5.2

\displaystyle L^{D_{o}^{\mathcal{B}}}({\theta(a)})

\displaystyle=\|\sum\beta_{E_{j}}(\theta(a))\otimes\epsilon_{j}\|=\|\sum\theta(\alpha_{E_{j}}(a))\otimes\epsilon_{j}\|

\displaystyle=\|(\theta\otimes I^{{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})})\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}(a)\otimes\epsilon_{j}\|\leq\|\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}(a)\otimes\epsilon_{j}\|.

because
\|(\theta\otimes I^{{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})})\|=1
since

\theta
is a unital completely positive operator.
(See section II.6.9 of
[
13
]
.)
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

With the assumptions of Proposition
5.3
,
let
\hat{\theta}=\theta\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}}
, viewed as a
map from
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
to

{\mathcal{B}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
.
Then

\hat{\theta}D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}=D_{o}^{\mathcal{B}}\hat{\theta}

as operators from
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
to

{\mathcal{B}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
. Consequently
if
\Psi
is an eigenvector for
D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}
with eigenvalue
\lambda
then

\hat{\theta}\Psi
is an eigenvector for
D_{o}^{\mathcal{B}}
with eigenvalue
\lambda
if

\hat{\theta}\Psi\neq 0
.

Proof.

For
a\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
and
\psi\in{\mathcal{S}}
we have

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}(D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}(a\otimes\psi))

\displaystyle=\hat{\theta}(\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}(a)\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}}(\psi))=\sum\theta(\alpha_{E_{j}}(a))\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}}(\psi)

\displaystyle=\sum\beta_{E_{j}}(\theta(a))\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}}(\psi)=D_{o}^{\mathcal{B}}(\hat{\theta}(a\otimes\psi)),

as needed.
∎

6.
The C*-metrics

In this section we examine the C*-metrics that are determined
by the Dirac operators constructed in Section
1
.
In the literature there are small variations in the definition of a “C*-metric”.
The following definition is appropriate for this paper. For this purpose
a “C*-normed
*
-algebra” means a normed
*
-algebra whose
norm satisfies the C*-algebra identity
\|a^{*}a\|=\|a\|^{2}
, so that its
completion is a C*-algebra. For us the main class of examples
is
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
as used earlier.

Definition 6.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a unital C*-normed
*
-algebra. By a C*-metric on

{\mathcal{A}}
we mean a seminorm
L
on
{\mathcal{A}}
having the following properties.
For any
a,b\in{\mathcal{A}}
:

(1)

L(a)=0
if and only if
a\in{\mathbb{C}}1_{\mathcal{A}}
.

(2)

L(a^{*})=L(a)
.

(3)

L
is lower semi-continuous with respect to the norm of
{\mathcal{A}}
.

(4)

L
satisfies the Leibnitz inequality

L(ab)\leq L(a)\|b\|+\|a\|L(b).

(5)

Let
S({\mathcal{A}})
be the state space of
{\mathcal{A}}
. Define a metric,
\rho_{L}
,
on
S({\mathcal{A}})
by

(6.1)

\rho^{L}(\mu,\nu)=\sup\{|\mu(a)-\nu(a)|:a^{*}=a\ \mathrm{and}\ L(a)\leq 1\}.

Without further hypotheses this metric can take value
+\infty
. We require
that the topology on
S({\mathcal{A}})

from this metric coincide with the weak-
*
topology on
S({\mathcal{A}})
. Then,
in particular,

\rho^{L}
will never take value
+\infty
. (The condition
a^{*}=a
in the
definition of
\rho^{L}(\mu,\nu)
can be omitted without changing

\rho^{L}(\mu,\nu)
, as explained just before definition 2.1 of
[
69
]
.)

If
{\mathcal{A}}
is actually a C*-algebra, and if
L
is a seminorm on
{\mathcal{A}}

that is permitted to take the value
+\infty
, but is semi-finite in the sense that

{\mathcal{A}}_{f}=\{a:L(a)<\infty\}
is dense in
{\mathcal{A}}
, and if the restriction of

L
to
{\mathcal{A}}_{f}
satisfies the 5 properties above, then we will also call

L
a C*-metric (on
{\mathcal{A}}
).

A pair
({\mathcal{A}},L)
consisting of a unital C*-normed
*
-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
and a C*-metric

L
on
{\mathcal{A}}
is called a
compact C*-metric space
.

Here is the motivating example. Let
(X,\rho)
be a compact metric space,
and let
A
be the C*-algebra
C(X)
of all continuous complex-valued
functions on
X
. Let
L^{\rho}
assign to each function its Lipschitz constant,
that is,

L^{\rho}(f)=\sup\{|f(x)-f(y|/\rho(x,y):x,y\in X\ \mathrm{and}\ x\neq y\}.

Then
L^{\rho}
is a C*-metric. Furthermore, one can recover
\rho
from

L^{\rho}
. To see this, notice that the state space
S(A)
is just the
set of probability measures on
X
. Let
\rho^{L^{\rho}}
be the metric on

S(A)
defined by equation (
6.1
) for
L^{\rho}
. Then

\rho(x,y)=\rho^{L^{\rho}}(\delta_{x},\delta_{y})
, where
\delta_{z}
denotes
the delta-measure at
z
for any
z\in X
.

We remark that property 5 is often the most difficult to verify for examples,
but having
S({\mathcal{A}})
compact for the
\rho^{L}
-topology (property
5) is crucial for the definitions of quantum Gromov-Hausdorff
distance which we will discuss later.

We will not explicitly need all of the next few remarks,
but they give important context to the definition of C*-metrics.
Suppose that
L
is a C*-metric on a
unital C*-normed
*
-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
, and let

(6.2)

{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{A}}^{1}=\{a\in{\mathcal{A}}:L(a)\leq 1\}.

Let
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
be the completion of
{\mathcal{A}}
,
let
{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}_{\mathcal{A}}^{1}
be the closure of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{A}}^{1}
in the C*-algebra

{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
, and let

{\bar{L}}
denote the corresponding “Minkowski functional”
on
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
, defined by setting, for
c\in{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
,

{\bar{L}}(c)=\inf\{r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}:c\in r{\bar{\mathcal{L}}}_{\mathcal{A}}^{1}\},

with value
+\infty
if there is no such
r
.
Then
{\bar{L}}
is a seminorm on

{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
(often taking value
+\infty
),
and the proof of proposition
4.4
of
[
68
]
tells us
that because
L
is lower semicontinuous,
{\bar{L}}
is an extension
of
L
. We call
{\bar{L}}
the
closure
of
L
. We see
that the set
\{c\in{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}:{\bar{L}}(c)\leq 1\}
is closed in

{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
. We say that the original seminorm
L
on
A
is
closed
if
{\mathcal{L}}_{\mathcal{A}}^{1}
is already closed in
{\bar{A}}
, or, equivalently, is
complete for the norm on
A
. Clearly if
L
is closed, then it is
lower semicontinuous. If
L
is closed and is not defined on all of

{\bar{A}}
, then
{\bar{L}}
is obtained simply by giving it value

+\infty
on all the elements of
{\bar{A}}
that are not in
A
. It is
clear that if
L
is semifinite then so is
{\bar{L}}
. We recall that
a unital subalgebra
B
of a unital algebra
A
is said to be spectrally
stable in
A
if for any
b\in B
the spectrum of
b
as an element
of
B
is the same as its spectrum as an element of
A
, or equivalently,
that any
b
that is invertible in
A
is invertible in
B
. From
proposition 3.1 of
[
73
]
one easily obtains:

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
L
be a C*-metric on a
unital C*-normed
*
-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
.
Then the closure of
L
is a C*-metric.
If
L
is a closed C*-metric, then

A^{f}
is a spectrally-stable subalgebra of

\bar{A}
that is carried into itself under the
holomorphic functional calculus of
\bar{A}
.

We now continue our discussion of Dirac operators.
Our discussion is very close to that given in section 4 of

[
67
]
, but part of it will be
general enough to also apply in later sections.

We continue with the notation of Section
1
.
Thus, for
a\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
,

[D_{o},M_{a}]=\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}(a)\otimes\kappa_{\\
e_{j}},

acting on
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
, and we define a seminorm,
L^{D_{o}}
, on
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
by

L^{D_{o}}(a)=\|[D_{o},M_{a}]\|.

It is shown in theorem 4.2 of
[
67
]
that

L^{D_{o}}
satisfies property 5 of Definition
6.1
.
This will also follow from the considerations below.

For the proof of our main theorem we need certain
bounds on
L^{D_{o}}
. For this purpose
we define a different seminorm,
L_{d}
, on
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
by

L_{d}(a)=\|{\mathrm{d}}a\|,

where we view
{\mathrm{d}}a
as a linear transformation from
{\mathfrak{g}}

to
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
, each of which is a normed space, with the norm on
{\mathfrak{g}}

coming from its inner product dual to the
inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
. Thus

(6.3)

L_{d}(a)=\sup\{\|\alpha_{X}(a)\|:X\in{\mathfrak{g}}\ \ \mathrm{and}\ \|X\|\leq 1\}.

It is shown in theorem 3.1 of
[
67
]
that

L_{d}
satisfies property 5 of Definition
6.1
.
It is easy to check the other conditions of Definition
6.1
,
and thus we conclude that
L_{d}
is in fact a C*-metric.

We will obtain bounds on
L^{D_{o}}
in terms of
L_{d}
. For this purpose
we can for convenience choose the basis vectors
\epsilon_{j}

for
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
to be orthonormal. Then as elements of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})

they will satisfy the relations
\epsilon_{j}^{*}=-\epsilon_{j}
,
\epsilon_{j}^{2}=-1
and

\epsilon_{j}\epsilon_{k}=-\epsilon_{k}\epsilon_{j}
if
j\neq k
. The following lemma is probably
well known, but I have not seen it in the literature.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{C}}
be unital C*-algebras, and let

\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{m}
be elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
that satisfy
the relations
\epsilon_{j}^{*}=-\epsilon_{j}
,
\epsilon_{j}^{2}=-1
and

\\
e_{j}\epsilon_{k}=-\epsilon_{k}\epsilon_{j}
if
j\neq k
. Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{m}

be elements of
{\mathcal{A}}
, and let
t=\sum a_{j}\otimes\epsilon_{j}
,
an element of
A\otimes C
(for any C*-norm).
Then

\sup\{\|a_{j}\|:1\leq j\leq m\}\ \leq\|t\|\leq\sum\{\|a_{j}\|:1\leq j\leq m\}.

Proof.

For each
j
let
p_{j}
be the spectral projection of
\epsilon_{j}
for the
eigenvalue
+i
. Note that
p_{j}\neq 0
. From the third
relation above one quickly sees that
p_{j}\epsilon_{k}p_{j}=0
if
j\neq k
.
It follow that

(1\otimes p_{j})t(1\otimes p_{j})=i(a_{j}\otimes p_{j})
,
so that
\|a_{j}\|\leq\|t\|
, and the first inequality holds,
The second inequality holds immediately from the definition of
t
.
∎

Proposition 6.4
.

In terms of the notation used before the lemma, for
any

a\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
we have

L_{d}(a)\leq L^{D_{o}}(a)\leq nL_{d}(a),

where
n=\dim({\mathfrak{g}})
.

Proof.

According to Proposition
5.2
we have

L^{D_{o}}(a)=\|\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}(a)\otimes{\epsilon_{j}}\|
,
where the norm is that on
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
.
So we can apply the above Lemma with with
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
.
Any
X\in{\mathfrak{g}}
with
\|X\|=1
can serve as one element, say
E_{1}
, of an
orthonormal basis for
{\mathfrak{g}}
. Thus for any such
X
we conclude
from the above Lemma that

\|\alpha_{X}(a)\|\leq\|\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}(a)\otimes\epsilon_{j}\|\leq\sum\|\alpha_{E_{j}}(a)\|
.
∎

From the inequalities in Proposition
6.4
it is easily seen that the
metric on
S({\mathcal{A}})
from
L^{D_{o}}
is equivalent (not necessarily equal)
to the metric from
L_{d}
.
Consequently we conclude, as in theorem 4.2 of
[
67
]
, that
L^{D_{o}}
itself
satisfies property 5 of Definition
6.1
.
Furthermore,
L^{D_{o}}
is lower semi-continuous, as seen in example 2.4
of
[
73
]
. It is easily verified that
L^{D_{o}}
satisfies the Leibniz inequality, and even
is strongly Leibniz in the sense defined in definition 1.1 of
[
73
]
. Thus
we have obtained:

Proposition 6.5
.

With notation as above,

L^{D_{o}}
is a C*-metric on
{\mathcal{A}}
.

But there is an even more
basic general C*-metric on
{\mathcal{A}}
,
which plays the principal role in
[
67
,
70
,
74
]
,
and which will be crucial for the proof of our main theorem.
From the chosen inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
, and its
dual on
{\mathfrak{g}}
, we obtain a
Riemannian metric on
G
, which is both right and left-invariant
because the inner product is
\mathrm{Cad}
-invariant.
From that Riemannian metric we obtain
a corresponding continuous length-function,

\ell
, on
G
(coming from path lengths determined
by the Riemannian metric), which is constant on conjugacy classes.
For any ergodic action
\alpha
of
G

on a unital C*-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
we define a seminorm
L_{\ell}
on
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
by

(6.4)

L_{\ell}(a)=\sup\{\|\alpha_{x}(a)-a\|/\ell(x):x\in G\ \ \mathrm{and}\ x\neq 0\}.

(where the value
+\infty
is permitted).
Notice that this seminorm is well-defined for any continuous length-function
on
G
(of which there are many), and that
G
need not be a Lie group.
In fact, for any compact group
G
, any continuous length-function on
G

(which for our definition is constant on conjugacy classes),
and any ergodic action of
G
on any unital C*-algebra
A
,
theorem 2.3 of
[
67
]
tells us that

L_{\ell}
satisfies property 5 of Definition
6.1
.
It is easy to check the other conditions of Definition
6.1
,
and thus we conclude that
L_{\ell}
is in fact a C*-metric.

We then have the following inequality. The short proof is contained in
the proof of theorem 3.1 of
[
67
]
, but we include the
proof here for the reader’s convenience.

Proposition 6.6
.

With notation as above, for any
a\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
we have

L_{\ell}(a)\leq L_{d}(a).

Proof.

Let
a\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
, and let
c
be a smooth path in
G

from
e_{G}
to
x\in G
. Then
\phi
, defined
by
\phi(t)=\alpha_{c(t)}(a)
, is smooth, and so we have

\displaystyle\|\alpha_{x}(a)-a\|

\displaystyle=\|\int\phi^{\prime}(t)dt\|\leq\int\|\alpha_{c(t)}(\alpha_{c^{\prime}(t)}a)\|dt

\displaystyle=\int\|\alpha_{c(t)}({\mathrm{d}}a(c^{\prime}(t))\|dt\leq\|da\|\int\|c^{\prime}(t)\|dt\ .

But the last integral is just the length of
c
. Thus from the definition
of the ordinary metric on
G
as in infimum over all smooth paths,
with its length function
\ell
using paths from
e_{G}
to
x\in G
,
we obtain
\|\alpha_{x}(a)-a\|\leq\|da\|\ell(x)
. Thus for all
x\in G

\|\alpha_{x}(a)-a\|/\ell(x)\leq\|da\|\ ,

from which the desired result follows immediately.
∎

On combining Propositions
6.4
and
6.6
, we obtain:

Corollary 6.7
.

With notation as above, for any
a\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
we have

L_{\ell}(a)\leq L^{D_{o}}(a).

be be able to apply the important bounds on

More about spinors

It is best if as our spinor bundle we can
use a representation of the Clifford algebra on
{\mathcal{S}}
that is irreducible.
For some compact
Riemannian manifolds this can not be done. In this section we
collect further algebraic facts and establish the
conventions and notation that we need in order to understand when this
can be done. Proofs of the
assertions made below for which no proof is given here
can be found in
[
12
,
28
]
. Also useful are
[
30
,
55
]
, but
they use slightly different conventions.

Let
{\mathfrak{m}}
be a finite dimensional Hilbert space over
{\mathbb{R}}

of dimension at least 3.
Let
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
denote the complex
Clifford algebra for
{\mathfrak{m}}
,
much as discussed in Section
1
for
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
.
Let
\mathop{\mathrm{Spin}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{m}})
be the subgroup of the group of invertible
elements of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
generated by products of two
elements of
{\mathfrak{m}}
of length 1. Conjugation of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
by
elements of
\mathop{\mathrm{Spin}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{m}})
carries
{\mathfrak{m}}
into itself, and this gives a
group homomorphism of
\mathop{\mathrm{Spin}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{m}})
onto
\mathop{\mathrm{SO}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{m}})

whose kernel is
\{1,-1\}
. In this way
\mathop{\mathrm{Spin}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{m}})
is the
simply-connected covering group of
\mathop{\mathrm{SO}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{m}})
, and these two
groups have naturally isomorphic Lie algebras. The
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear span
of products of two orthogonal elements of
{\mathfrak{m}}
is a Lie

{\mathbb{R}}
-sub-algebra,
\mathop{\mathrm{spin}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{m}})
, of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
with its additive
commutator as Lie bracket. Exponentiation in
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})

,

by conjugation.
Then the representation

of

\beta
and the action
\kappa

of

on

in the sense much as given above in

for

.

Suppose now that

, and that

.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
G
be a connected simply-connected

.

Let

8.
Dirac operators for matrix algebras

In this section we consider the action
\alpha
of
G

on
{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
where
{\mathcal{H}}
is the Hilbert space of an irrep

U
of
G
, and the action
\alpha
on
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})

is by conjugation by this representation. Since
{\mathcal{A}}
is finite
dimensional, we have
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}={\mathcal{A}}
. For any
X\in{\mathfrak{g}}
and

T\in{\mathcal{A}}
we have
\alpha_{X}(T)=[U_{X},T]
.

Since the center of
G
will act
trivially on
{\mathcal{A}}
, we can factor by the connected component of the
center. The resulting group will be semisimple. Thus for the remainder
of this section we will assume that
G
is a compact connected
semisimple Lie group.

In general the representation
({\mathcal{H}},U)
need not be faithful. Its kernel
at the Lie-algebra level is an ideal of
{\mathfrak{g}}
. But
{\mathfrak{g}}
, as a semisimple
Lie algebra, is the direct sum of its minimal ideals, each of which
is a simple Lie algebra.
Denote the Lie-algebra-kernel of
U
by
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
. It must be the direct sum of
some of these minimal ideals. Denote the direct sum of the remaining
minimal ideals by
{\mathfrak{g}}_{U}
, so that
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{g}}_{U}\oplus{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
. Clearly

U
is faithful on
{\mathfrak{g}}_{U}
and trivial on
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
.
We identify
{\mathfrak{g}}_{U}^{\prime}
with the subspace
of
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
consisting of linear functionals on
{\mathfrak{g}}
that take value 0
on
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
, and similarly for
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}^{\prime}
. Clearly
{\mathfrak{g}}_{U}
and
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
are

\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits
-invariant, and so
{\mathfrak{g}}_{U}^{\prime}
and
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}^{\prime}
are

\mathrm{Cad}
-invariant.

For any
T\in{\mathcal{A}}
it is clear that
{\mathrm{d}}T(X)=\alpha_{X}(T)=0
for any
X
in
{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
,
so that
{\mathrm{d}}T\in{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathfrak{g}}_{U}^{\prime}
. Thus the range of
{\mathrm{d}}

is in
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathfrak{g}}_{U}^{\prime}
. In
[
76
]
it is shown that
for the quotient of
G
by the kernel of
U
, the sub-bimodule
of its
\Omega_{o}
that is generated by the range of
{\mathrm{d}}
is
exactly that
\Omega_{o}
itself. But
{\mathfrak{g}}_{U}
is exactly
the Lie algebra of the quotient of
G
by the kernel of
U
. We see in this
way that the cotangent bundle,
\Omega({\mathcal{A}})
, of
{\mathcal{A}}
is exactly

\Omega({\mathcal{A}})={\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathfrak{g}}_{U}^{\prime}.

Notice that for the original
\Omega_{o}
we have

\Omega_{o}={\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}=\Omega({\mathcal{A}})\oplus({\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}^{\prime})
.

We assume that, as before, we have chosen a
\mathrm{Cad}
-invariant inner
product on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
. It restricts to a
\mathrm{Cad}
-invariant inner
product on
{\mathfrak{g}}_{U}^{\prime}
.
We then form the complex Clifford algebra
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}_{U}^{\prime})
. We note
that it is a
\mathrm{Cad}
-invariant unital C*-subalgebra of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
.
Since
\Omega({\mathcal{A}})={\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathfrak{g}}_{U}^{\prime}
, the Clifford
bundle for
{\mathcal{A}}
is exactly

{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathcal{A}})={\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}_{U}^{\prime}).

Let
({\mathcal{S}},\kappa)
be a choice of an irreducible
*
-representation
of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}_{U}^{\prime})
.
We then form the spinor bundle
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
.
It is a Hilbert space with its inner product coming from using
the (unique) tracial state on
{\mathcal{A}}
. We then form the corresponding
Dirac operator,
D
, on
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
much as in Section
1
,
so that

D(T)=(I^{\mathcal{A}}\otimes\kappa)\circ({\mathrm{d}}\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}})(T)=\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}(T)\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}}

for
T\in{\mathcal{A}}
, where now
\{E_{j}\}
is a basis for
{\mathfrak{g}}_{U}
.

Definition 8.1
.

With notation as above, the operator
D
defined just above is the Dirac operator
for
{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
for the given inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
. (Thus if the representation

({\mathcal{H}},U)
is faithful on
{\mathfrak{g}}
then
D
coincides with
D_{o}
.)

Let
\hat{G}
be the simply-connected covering group of
G
.
Then Proposition
7.1
tells us that there is a representation
\sigma

of
\hat{G}
on
{\mathcal{S}}
that is compatible with
the action of
\hat{G}
(through
G
)
on
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}_{U})
in the
sense that

\kappa_{\mathrm{Cad}_{x}(q)}=\sigma_{x}\kappa_{q}\sigma^{*}_{x}

for all
q\in{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}_{U})
and
x\in\hat{G}
. We then define a
representation,
\tilde{\sigma}
, of
\hat{G}
on
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}

by
\tilde{\sigma}=\alpha\otimes\sigma
.
It follows from a very slight modification of Proposition
2.1

to account for our using
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}_{U}
instead of
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
,
that
D
commutes with
this action. Then, a very slight modification of Proposition

2.2
tells us that
the corresponding C*-metric
L^{D}
is invariant under the action

\alpha
of
\hat{G}
in the sense that for
any
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
we have

L^{D}(\alpha_{x}(a))=L^{D}(a)

for all
x\in\hat{G}
.

According to Proposition
5.2
, for any
T\in{\mathcal{A}}

L^{D_{o}}(T)=\|[D_{o},M_{a}]\|=\|\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}(T)\otimes{\epsilon_{j}}\|

where the norm on the right is that on
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
.
If we choose a basis,
\{E_{j}:j\in{I_{U}}\}
, for
{\mathfrak{g}}_{U}
and adjoin to it a basis for

{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
, then from the fact that
\alpha_{X}(a)=0
for any
X\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{0}
we see
that

L^{D_{o}}(T)=\|\sum_{j\in I_{U}}\alpha_{E_{j}}(T)\otimes{\epsilon_{j}}\|.

But
\sum_{j\in I_{U}}\alpha_{E_{j}}(a)\otimes{\epsilon_{j}}
is in

{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}_{U}^{\prime})={\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathcal{A}})
. Then regardless of the choice of
spinors for
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}_{U}^{\prime})
, it follows from Proposition
5.2

that for the corresponding Dirac operator
D
we have

L^{D}(T)=\|\sum_{j\in I_{U}}\alpha_{E_{j}}(T)\otimes{\epsilon_{j}}\|.

Since the norm on
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}_{U}^{\prime})
is just the the restriction to

{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}_{U}^{\prime})
of the norm on
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
, the norm on the right
can be taken to be the norm of
{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
.
We thus obtain the following proposition, which will be important
for the proof of our main theorem.

Proposition 8.2
.

For any compact connected Lie group
G
and any irrep

({\mathcal{H}},U)
and corresponding action
\alpha
of
G
on
{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
,
and for notation as above, we have

L^{D}=L^{D_{o}}.

9.
The fuzzy sphere

We now illustrate our general results obtained so far by working them
out explicitly for the case that has received the most attention in
the quantum-physics literature, namely the case of
G=SU(2)

and its irreps. This will permit us to compare our
Dirac operator with those proposed in the physics literature.

We now establish our conventions and notation. As a basis for

{\mathfrak{g}}=su(2)
we take the product by
i
of the Pauli matrices
[
82
]
,
so

E_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&i\\
i&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\quad E_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\quad\quad E_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\
0&-i\end{pmatrix}.

Then
E_{1}E_{2}=E_{3}
, and cyclic permutations of the indices.
Consequently
[E_{1},E_{2}]=2E_{3}
and cyclic permutations
of this, which are Lie algebra relations. Furthermore
(E_{j})^{2}=-I_{2}

for
j=1,2,3
. We see that
\mathrm{trace}((E_{j})^{2})=-2
.
This leads us to define the
Ad
-invariant inner product on

{\mathfrak{g}}
by
\langle X,Y\rangle=-(1/2)\mathrm{trace}(XY)
, so that the

E_{j}
’s form an orthonormal basis for
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
As Cartan subalgebra for
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathbb{C}}=sl(2,{\mathbb{C}})
we choose
the
{\mathbb{C}}
-span of
E_{3}
,

Let
({\mathcal{H}}^{n},U^{n})
be an irrep of
G
,
where the label
n
is the highest weight of the representation.
Thus
\dim({\mathcal{H}}^{n})=n+1
. We let
U^{n}
also denote the
corresponding “infinitesimal” representation of
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathbb{C}}
on
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
.
As done in the introduction,
we set
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}={\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}^{n})
, and we let
\alpha

be the action of
G
on
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}
by conjugation by
U^{n}
.
Its infinitesimal version is given by
\alpha_{X}(T)=[U^{n}_{X},T]

for all
T\in{\mathcal{B}}^{n}
and
X\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathbb{C}}
.

According to Theorem 4.1 of
[
76
]
, the cotangent bundle for
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}
is

\Omega_{n}={\mathcal{B}}^{n}\otimes_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{B}}^{n}\otimes_{\mathbb{C}}{{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}}
.
We let
\{\epsilon_{j}\}_{j=1}^{3}
be the basis for
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
that is dual to the
basis
\{E_{j}\}
for
{\mathfrak{g}}
chosen above.
It will be orthonormal for the inner product
on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
that is dual to our chosen inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}
. Any other

\mathrm{Cad}
-invariant inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
will be a scalar multiple
of this one. If needed, that scalar can be pulled along through the
calculations we do below.

The Clifford algebra
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
is generated by
\{\epsilon_{j}\}_{j=1}^{3}

with the relations

\epsilon_{j}\epsilon_{k}+\epsilon_{k}\epsilon_{j}=-2\langle\epsilon_{j},\epsilon_{k}\rangle=-2\delta_{jk}.

Since
{\mathfrak{g}}
is 3-dimensional,
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
is isomorphic to

M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})\oplus M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
, while
{\mathbb{C}}\ell^{e}({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
is isomorphic to

M_{2}({\mathbb{C}})
. Let
\kappa
be an irreducible representation of

{\mathbb{C}}\ell^{e}({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
on a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{S}}
(necessarily of dimension 2,
and unique up to unitary equivalence).
The chirality element for
{\mathbb{C}}({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
is
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}\epsilon_{3}
. We extend

\kappa
to
{\mathbb{C}}({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
by setting either
\kappa_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=I^{\mathcal{S}}
or
\kappa_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=-I^{\mathcal{S}}

(as discussed in Section
5
)
to obtain the two inequivalent irreducible representations of
{\mathbb{C}}({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
.
The Clifford bundle for
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}
is
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathcal{B}}^{n})={\mathcal{B}}^{n}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
,
and the spinor bundle is
{\mathcal{S}}({\mathcal{B}}^{n})={\mathcal{B}}^{n}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
.
The Dirac operator on
{\mathcal{S}}({\mathcal{B}}^{n})
is defined on elementary tensors by

D(T\otimes\psi)=\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}(T)\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}}(\psi)=\sum[U^{n}_{E_{j}},T]\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}}(\psi)

for
T\in{\mathcal{B}}^{n}
and
\psi\in{\mathcal{S}}
,
that is,

D=\sum_{j}\alpha_{E_{j}}\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}}.

Since
SU(2)
is simply connected, Proposition
7.1
tells us that
corresponding to its action
\mathrm{Cad}
on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}

there is a homomorphism of it into the subgroup
\mathop{\mathrm{Spin}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})

of the group of invertible elements
of the even subalgebra
{\mathbb{C}}\ell^{e}({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
.
This homomorphism, which we will denote by
\widehat{\mathrm{Cad}}
,
plays the role of the
\tilde{\pi}
of Proposition
7.1
. It
is easy to describe, and we will need a precise description of it below.
The
E_{j}
’s happen to also be elements of
SU(2)
.
To try to avoid confusion, we denote them by
x_{j}=E_{j}
when we view
them as elements of
SU(2)
.
We note that
(x_{j})^{-1}=-x_{j}
. It is easily checked that

\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits_{x_{j}}(E_{k})=E_{k}
if

and

if

.
Consequently
\mathrm{Cad}_{x_{j}}(\epsilon_{k})=\epsilon_{k}
if

and

if

.
Set
\hat{\epsilon}_{1}=\epsilon_{2}\epsilon_{3}
and cyclic permutations of the indices.
The

\mathop{\mathrm{Spin}}\nolimits({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})

.
Consequently

.

, then every element of

can be expressed (see proposition VII.5.5 of

.

With notation as above,

∎

Theorem 9.2

For notation as above,

Proof.

We examine,

\sum_{j}\alpha_{E_{j}}\otimes\sigma_{E_{j}}=\sum_{j}\alpha_{E_{j}}\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}}=D.

On the other hand,
if we choose the spinor representation for which
\kappa({\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=+I_{2}
,
then we will obtain

\sum_{j}(\alpha_{E_{j}}\otimes\sigma_{E_{j}})=-D.

When this is combined with the formula for

\tilde{\sigma}_{C}
in Proposition
4.2
,
we obtain the formula in the statement of the theorem.
∎

To use the formula of the above theorem in order to
determine the spectrum of
D
, we need to recall the
well-known facts about the action of the Casimir
element
C
on irreducible representations of
SU(2)
.
We define the following elements of
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathbb{C}}
.

H=-iE_{3},\quad E=-(E_{2}+iE_{1})/2,\quad F=(E_{2}-iE_{1})/2.

They satisfy the familiar relations

[H,E]=2E,\quad[H,F]=-2F,\quad[E,F]=H.

Then in
{\mathcal{U}}({\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathbb{C}})
we find (as in equation 1.3.8 of
[
39
]
)

C=\sum_{j=1}^{3}E_{j}^{2}=-(H^{2}+2(EF+FE)).

For our purposes it is useful to change this using

EF-FE=H
to obtain

C=-(H^{2}+2H+4FE).

This permits us to easily determine, as follows,
the scaler multiple of the identity operator to which
C
is taken
by any irrep of
SU(2)
.
Let
({\mathcal{H}}^{n},U^{n})
be the irrep with
highest weight
n
.
This means that
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
contains an eigenvector,

\xi_{n}
, for
H
of eigenvalue
n
such that
E\xi_{n}=0
.
Consequently,
U_{C}^{n}\xi_{n}=-(H^{2}+2H)\xi_{n}=-n(n+2)\xi_{n}
. Thus:

Proposition 9.3
.

For the irrep
({\mathcal{H}}^{n},U^{n})
of

SU(2)
of highest weight
n
we have
U_{C}^{n}=-n(n+2)I^{{\mathcal{H}}^{n}}
.

We now determine the spectrum of
D
.
We choose the spinor representation for which
\kappa({\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=-I_{2}
.
The description of the spectrum of
D
is most attractive if
we state it in terms of
D^{\prime}=D+2
.

Theorem 9.4
.

The spectrum of
D^{\prime}
consists of the even numbers

\pm 2k
for
1\leq k\leq n
together with
2(n+1)
. For each
k
with
1\leq k\leq n

the multiplicity of the eigenvalue
\pm 2k
is
2k=\dim({\mathcal{H}}^{2k-1})
, while the multiplicity of

2(n+1)
is
2(n+1)=\dim({\mathcal{H}}^{2n+1})
. The spectrum of
D
is just the spectrum of

D^{\prime}
shifted by
-2
.

Proof.

It suffices to determine the spectrum of

\tilde{\sigma}_{C}-\alpha_{C}\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}}-I^{\mathcal{A}}\otimes\sigma_{C},

which is an operator on
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
. This operator involves
three different representations of
SU(2)
. Now for any compact group

G
and unitary representation
({\mathcal{H}},U)
the representation
\alpha
on

{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
by conjugation by
U
is unitarily equivalent to the representation

U\otimes\overline{U}
on
{\mathcal{H}}\otimes\overline{{\mathcal{H}}}
, where

(\overline{{\mathcal{H}}},\overline{U})
is the representation dual to

({\mathcal{H}},U)
. But for
G=SU(2)
all representations are self-dual
[
79
]
.
Thus for an irrep
({\mathcal{H}}^{n},U^{n})
of

SU(2)
the corresponding representation
\alpha
on
{\mathcal{B}}^{n}
is equivalent to
the representation
U^{n}\otimes U^{n}
on
{\mathcal{H}}^{n}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}^{n}
.
But this representation is known (see section 8-3 of
[
82
]
) to decompose in such a way that

(\alpha,{\mathcal{B}}^{n})
is equivalent to

\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}({\mathcal{H}}^{2k},U^{2k}).
Thus

{\mathcal{B}}^{n}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
is equivalent to

\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}({\mathcal{H}}^{2k}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}^{1})
with corresponding
representations
\alpha\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}}
,
I^{\mathcal{A}}\otimes\sigma
, and
\tilde{\sigma}=\alpha\otimes\sigma
.
For each
k
set
W_{k}={\mathcal{H}}^{2k}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}^{1}
.

Since
U^{1}_{C}=-3I^{{\mathcal{H}}^{1}}
, we see that

I^{\mathcal{A}}\otimes\sigma_{C}
on
W_{k}
is
-3I^{W_{k}}
.

We also see that

\alpha_{C}\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}}
on
W_{k}
is
-2k(2k+2)I^{W_{k}}=-4(k^{2}+k)I^{W_{k}}
.

Furthermore,
W_{k}={\mathcal{H}}^{2k}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}^{1}
decomposes for
\tilde{\sigma}

as
{\mathcal{H}}^{2k+1}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}^{2k-1}
for
k\geq 1
, while
W_{0}={\mathcal{H}}^{1}
.
Thus we see that for
k\geq 1
we have

\tilde{\sigma}_{C}
on
{\mathcal{H}}^{2k+1}\subset W_{k}
is
-(2k+1)(2k+3)I^{{\mathcal{H}}^{2k+1}}
,
while

\tilde{\sigma}_{C}
on
{\mathcal{H}}^{2k-1}\subset W_{k}
is
-(2k-1)(2k+1)I^{{\mathcal{H}}^{2k-1}}
,
while

\tilde{\sigma}_{C}
on
W_{0}={\mathcal{H}}^{1}
is
-3I^{{\mathcal{H}}^{1}}
.

It follows that for
k\geq 1
we have

\pm 2D
on
{\mathcal{H}}^{2k+1}\subset W_{k}
is

-((2k+1)(2k+3)-4(k^{2}+k)-3)I^{{\mathcal{H}}^{2k+1}}=-4kI^{{\mathcal{H}}^{2k+1}}
,
while

\pm 2D
on
{\mathcal{H}}^{2k-1}\subset W_{k}
is

-((2k-1)(2k+1)-4(k^{2}+k)-3)I^{{\mathcal{H}}^{2k-1}}=+4(k+1)I^{{\mathcal{H}}^{2k-1}}
,
while

\pm 2D
on
W_{0}={\mathcal{H}}^{1}
is
((-3)-(0)-(-3))I^{{\mathcal{H}}^{1}}=0I^{{\mathcal{H}}^{1}}
.

We can conveniently assemble all of this in the following way.
Let us keep the minus sign, and divide by 2. We obtain for
k\geq 1

D
on
{\mathcal{H}}^{2k+1}\subset W_{k}
is
+2kI^{{\mathcal{H}}^{2k+1}}
,

while

D
on
{\mathcal{H}}^{2k-1}\subset W_{k}
is
-2(k+1)I^{{\mathcal{H}}^{2k-1}}
,

while

D
on
W_{0}={\mathcal{H}}^{1}
is
0I^{{\mathcal{H}}^{1}}
.

Then let
D^{\prime}=D+2
. For each
k
with
1\leq k\leq n
we see that on the
copy of

{\mathcal{H}}^{2k-1}
in
W_{k-1}
we have
D^{\prime}=2kI^{{\mathcal{H}}^{2k-1}}
,

while on the copy of

{\mathcal{H}}^{2k-1}
in
W_{k}

we have
D^{\prime}=-2kI^{{\mathcal{H}}^{2k-1}}
.

Finally, for

{\mathcal{H}}^{2n+1}
in
W_{n}
we have
D^{\prime}=2(n+1)I^{{\mathcal{H}}^{2n+1}}
.
∎

10.
Homogeneous spaces and the Dirac operator for G

In this and the next sections we reformulate the (usual) construction of
Dirac operators on coadjoint orbits in terms just of algebras and modules,
without any mention of coordinate patches or charts, refining the approach in
Section
1
. This is essential for the proof of our main theorem.
We follow fairly closely parts of
[
71
,
72
]
, but here we emphasize
cotangent bundles rather than tangent bundles.

We begin working in the following generality.
We let
G
be any compact connected Lie group. We let
K

be any closed subgroup of
G
, and we form the corresponding
homogeneous space
G/K
. Many of the results in this section are
adaptations to our context of some of the results in
[
71
]
.
We let
{\mathcal{A}}=C(G/K)
and we let
\alpha
be the action of
G
on
{\mathcal{A}}

by left translation. Then
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
, formed using
\alpha
, is also the smooth
structure on
G/K
coming from that on
G
.

As in Section
1
, we set
\Omega_{o}={\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
, we chose
a spinor space
{\mathcal{S}}
for
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
(which need not be irreducible),
and we define
D_{o}
on
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
. Since now

{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}=C^{\infty}(G/K)
, we can
express
D_{o}
in terms of functions
as follows. Let
f,g\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
and
\psi\in{\mathcal{S}}
. Then

[D_{o},M_{f}](g\otimes\psi)
is in
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
, so we can evaluate
it at any
y\in G/K
, to obtain an element of

{\mathcal{S}}
, as follows:

\displaystyle[D_{o},M_{f}](g\otimes\psi)(y)

\displaystyle=\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}(f)(y)g(y)\otimes\kappa_{\epsilon_{j}}\psi

\displaystyle=g(y)\kappa_{\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}(f)(y)\epsilon_{j}}\psi.

But
{\mathrm{d}}f_{y}
is the element
\sum\alpha_{E_{j}}(f)(y)\epsilon_{j}
of
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}\subset{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
, so we
see that
[D_{o},M_{f}]
is given by the operator-valued function

[D_{o},M_{f}]_{y}=1_{\mathcal{A}}\otimes\kappa_{{\mathrm{d}}f_{y}}.

(Compare this with proposition 9.11 of
[
30
]
.) Consequently

\|[D_{o},M_{f}]\|=\|\kappa_{{\mathrm{d}}f_{y}}\|
. But for any
\mu\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
we have

\|\kappa_{\mu}\|^{2}=\|\langle\mu,\mu\rangle 1_{\mathcal{A}}\|=\|\mu\|^{2}
, and
it is easy to check that this extends to
\mu\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}
. Thus we obtain
the following result, which will be of importance for the proof
of our main theorem.

Proposition 10.1
.

With notation as above we have

L_{D_{o}}(f)=\|[D_{o},M_{f}]\|=\sup\{\|{\mathrm{d}}f_{y}\|:y\in G/K\},

where the norm comes from the inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
.

We can put this in a more familiar form in terms of the dual inner product
on
{\mathfrak{g}}
. For all
y
there will exist an element,
\mathrm{grad}_{y}f
,
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
such that
{\mathrm{d}}f_{y}(X)=\langle X,\mathrm{grad}_{y}f\rangle
for all
X\in{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Thus

L_{D_{o}}(f)=\sup\{\|\mathrm{grad}_{y}f\|:y\in G/K\}.

All of the above applies to the case in which
K=\{e_{G}\}
, that is,
to the algebra
{\mathcal{A}}=C(G)
.
Because any Lie group is parallelizable, the cotangent bundle for
G
actually is

{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
. So this case is also one to which all of the results
of Section
1
immediately apply. Once one applies
Proposition
7.1
concerning
the possibility of choosing the space
{\mathcal{S}}
to be irreducible,
one obtains:

Proposition 10.2
.

Let
G
be a connected simply-connected
compact Lie group, and let a
\mathrm{Cad}
-invariant inner product be chosen
for
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
. Then an irreducible spinor space
{\mathcal{S}}
with
compatible action of
G
can be found, and the
operator
D_{o}
on
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
constructed as in Section
1

is the correct Dirac
operator for
G
, invariant under the action of
G
on itself
by left translation. We denote it by
D^{G}
.

We return to the general situation with
{\mathcal{A}}=C(G/K)
.
Since for any
f\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
and
X\in{\mathfrak{g}}
the expression
\alpha_{X}(f)
gives
the same function regardless of whether
f
is viewed as an element
of
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
or of
C^{\infty}(G)
,
it is clear from the formulas above that
D_{o}

is simply the restriction of
D^{G}
to
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
.

A standard argument (see the text
around proposition 9.12 of
[
30
]
)
shows that
L_{D^{G}}(f)
is the Lipschitz constant
for any
f\in C^{\infty}(G)
for the
ordinary metric on
G
coming from the Riemannian metric determined
by our chosen inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
.

11.
Dirac operators for homogeneous spaces

We continue to work here in the following generality.
We let
G
be any compact connected Lie group. We let
K

be any closed subgroup of
G
, and we form the corresponding
homogeneous space
G/K
. Again, many of the results in this section are
adaptations to our context of results in
[
71
,
72
]
.

We let
{\mathfrak{k}}
be the Lie algebra of
K
. Let
f\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}=C^{\infty}(G/K)
,
and let
X\in{\mathfrak{g}}
. As before, we view
f
as a function on
G

when convenient.
Then for any
x\in G
we have

{\mathrm{d}}f_{x}(X)=D^{t}_{o}f((\exp(-tX))x)=D^{t}_{o}f(x\exp(-t\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits_{x}^{-1}(X))).

Since
f(xs)=f(x)
for all
x\in G
and
s\in K
, we see from this
that
{\mathrm{d}}f_{xs}(X)=df_{x}(X)
for all
x\in G
and
s\in K
, and
that if
\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits_{x}^{-1}(X)\in{\mathfrak{k}}
for some
x\in G
then
df_{x}(X)=0
.
Let

{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}=\{\mu\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}:\mu(X)=0\ \mathrm{for\ all}\ X\in{\mathfrak{k}}\},

and let
{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}
be its complexification.
Notice that
{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}
is carried into itself by the restriction of
\mathrm{Cad}
to
K
.
If
X\in{\mathfrak{k}}

then
\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits_{x}^{-1}(\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits_{x}(X))\in{\mathfrak{k}}
, so
{\mathrm{d}}f_{x}(\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits_{x}(X))=0
.
This means that
\mathrm{Cad}_{x}^{-1}(df_{x})\in{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}
for all
x\in G
.
Let

\Omega(G/K)=\{\omega\in C^{\infty}(G/K,{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}):\omega(x)\in\mathrm{Cad}_{x}({\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}})\ \mathrm{for\ all}\ x\in G\}.

The calculations above show that
{\mathrm{d}}f\in\Omega(G/K)
.
For pointwise operations,
\Omega(G/K)
is an
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-module.
It is easy to check that
\Omega(G/K)
is carried into itself by the action
\gamma
of
G

on
\Omega_{o}(G)
.
By very minor adjustments to the discussion in section 6 of
[
76
]
one sees that

\Omega(G/K)
is indeed the complexified cotangent bundle for
G/K
(as is
basically well-known
[
45
]
).

However, the fact that the range spaces of the
\omega
’s depend on
x
complicates
calculations. But by taking advantage of the fact that we are working
on a homogeneous space, we can make a small change that makes the situation considerably
more transparent
(much as done in connection with notation 4.2 of
[
71
]
).
For each
\omega\in\Omega(G/K)
define
\hat{\omega}
on

G
by
\hat{\omega}(x)=\mathrm{Cad}_{x}^{-1}(\omega(x))
.
Then it is easily checked that
\hat{\omega}
is in the
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-module

\hat{\Omega}(G/K)=\{\omega\in C^{\infty}(G,{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}):\omega(xs)=\mathrm{Cad}^{-1}_{s}(\omega(x))\ \mathrm{for}\ x\in G,s\in K\}.

This is exactly the complexification of the space given in notation 6.1
of
[
76
]
, where a proof is indicated for the well-known fact
that it too can be viewed as the space of smooth cross sections of
the cotangent bundle of
G/K
.
(It is an “induced representation”
[
71
]
. There are many more
induced representations in the next pages.)

We then define
\hat{\mathrm{d}}

by
\hat{\mathrm{d}}f=\widehat{{\mathrm{d}}f}
for all
f\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
. Then for
x\in G

and
X\in{\mathfrak{g}}
we have

\displaystyle\langle X,\hat{\mathrm{d}}f_{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle X,\mathrm{Cad}_{x}^{-1}({\mathrm{d}}f_{x})\rangle=\langle\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits_{x}(X),{\mathrm{d}}f_{x}\rangle

\displaystyle=D^{t}_{o}(f(\exp(-t\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits_{x}(X)x))=D^{t}_{o}(f(x\exp(-tX)),

so that
\hat{\mathrm{d}}
is defined in terms of the
right
action of
G
on
itself.
Clearly
\hat{\mathrm{d}}
is again a derivation,
and the first-order differential calculi
(\hat{\Omega}(G/K),\hat{\mathrm{d}})
and

(\Omega(G/K),{\mathrm{d}})
are easily seen to be isomorphic. Thus we can use the latter.
It is easily checked that

\widehat{\gamma_{x}\omega}=\alpha_{x}(\hat{\omega})
, so that the left-translation action
\alpha

of
G
on itself is the appropriate one to use on
\hat{\Omega}(G/K)
.

We now assume, as usual, that a
\mathrm{Cad}
-invariant inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}

has been chosen.
We can then give a simple description of the Clifford bundle for
{\mathcal{A}}
,
much as done in section 7 of
[
71
]
.
We first let
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime})
be the complex Clifford algebra for
{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}
for
the restriction to
{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}
of the given
inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
, much as in Section
1
. Since the restriction to
K
of the
action
\mathrm{Cad}
on
{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}
is by
isometries, it extends (as Bogoliubov automorphisms)
to an action on

{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime})
, which we denote again by
\mathrm{Cad}
. We then set

{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)=\{{\mathcal{W}}\in C^{\infty}(G,{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime})):{\mathcal{W}}(xs)=\mathrm{Cad}^{-1}_{s}({\mathcal{W}}(x))\ \mathrm{for}\ x\in G,s\in K\}.

Then
{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)
with pointwise product is easily
seen to be a C*-algebra that effectively contains

{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
in its center.
Since
{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}\subset{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime})
and
{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}
generates
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime})
as a unital algebra,
it is also easily seen that
\hat{\Omega}(G/K)\subset{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)
and that

\hat{\Omega}(G/K)
together with
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
generate
{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)
as a unital algebra.
For our situation
{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)
is the appropriate Clifford-algebra
bundle.

Much as in Section
7
, we choose
a spinor space
{\mathcal{S}}
for
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime})
(which need not be irreducible
or faithful).
We need to assume that there is a
unitary representation,
\rho
, of
K
on
{\mathcal{S}}
that is
compatible with the restricted action
\mathrm{Cad}
of
K
on
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime})
and
the representation
\kappa
of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime})
on
{\mathcal{S}}
, in the
sense that

(11.1)

\kappa_{\mathrm{Cad}_{s}(q)}=\rho_{s}\kappa_{q}\rho^{-1}_{s}

for all
q\in{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
and
s\in K
.
Since
K
is never semisimple in the situation in which
we are most interested, namely that of coadjoint orbits,
we can not in general
apply the arguments used
for Proposition
7.1
to obtain a spinor space that is irreducible.
This is a serious issue, reflecting the fact that not
all homogeneous spaces are
\mathop{\mathrm{Spin}}\nolimits^{c}
.
Much of the next section will be devoted to dealing with this issue
for coadjoint orbits.
But we can always choose
{\mathcal{S}}={\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime})

(with inner product from its canonical trace)
with
\rho=\mathrm{Cad}

restricted to
K
. This leads to the Dirac-Hodge
operator, as discussed in section 8 of
[
71
]
.

Whatever way
{\mathcal{S}}
and
\rho
are chosen, we then set

{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)=\{\Psi\in C^{\infty}(G,{\mathcal{S}}):\Psi(xs)=\rho_{s}^{-1}\Psi(x)\ \mathrm{for\ all}\ s\in K\}.

Proposition 11.1
.

The evident pointwise
product of elements of
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
by elements of

{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)
, using the representation
\kappa
of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime})
on
{\mathcal{S}}
,
carries
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
into itself.

Proof.

This uses the
compatibility relation (
11.1
). Let
{\mathcal{W}}\in{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)
and
\Psi\in{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
.
Then for
x\in G
and
s\in K
we have

\displaystyle({\mathcal{W}}\Psi)(xs)

\displaystyle=\kappa_{{\mathcal{W}}(xs)}\Psi(xs)=\kappa_{\mathrm{Cad}_{s}^{-1}({\mathcal{W}}(x))}(\rho_{s}^{-1}(\Psi(x))

\displaystyle=\rho_{s}^{-1}(\kappa_{{\mathcal{W}}(x)}\Psi(x))=\rho_{s}^{-1}({\mathcal{W}}\Psi)(x)

as desired.
∎

There is an evident pointwise
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-valued inner product on
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
,
using the inner product on
{\mathcal{S}}
and the fact that
\rho
is unitary.
We denote it again by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}
.

Our Dirac operator
D
will be an operator on
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
.
Because of the way
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
is defined,
we cannot simply define
D
on elementary tensors as
done in previous sections. We need to use connections, as done in
[
71
,
72
]
.
We follow somewhat closely the development in section 5 of
[
72
]
.

Let
{\mathfrak{m}}
be the orthogonal complement of
{\mathfrak{k}}
for the inner product on

{\mathfrak{g}}
that is dual to our chosen inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
.
Then
{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}
can be viewed as the dual vector space to
{\mathfrak{m}}
.
The tangent bundle,
{\mathcal{T}}(G/K)
, of
G/K
is given by

{\mathcal{T}}(G/K)=\{V\in C^{\infty}(G,{\mathfrak{m}}):V(xs)=\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits_{s}^{-1}(V(x))\text{ for }x\in G,s\in K\}.

It is an
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-module for the pointwise product, and
G
acts on it by translation.
We denote this translation action again by
\alpha
. Each
V\in{\mathcal{T}}(G/K)
determines a derivation,
\delta_{V}
, of
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
by

(\delta_{V}f)(x)=D_{0}^{t}(f(x\exp(tV(x))).

We denote the complexifcation of
{\mathcal{T}}(G/K)
by
{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}(G/K)
.
For
V\in{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}(G/K)
we define
\delta_{V}
in terms of the real
and imaginary parts of
V
.

There is an evident
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-linear pairing of
{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}(G/K)

with
\hat{\Omega}(G/K)
, coming from the pairing of
{\mathfrak{m}}
with
{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}
.
We denote it by
\langle V,\omega\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}
.
Both
{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}(G/K)
and
\hat{\Omega}(G/K)
are finitely-generated projective

{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-modules (in accordance with Swan’s theorem) because
they are induced modules. See proposition 2.2 of
[
71
]
.
It is then easily verified that
\hat{\Omega}(G/K)

is the
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-module dual of
{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}(G/K)

via the pairing mentioned above.

We define a connection,
\nabla^{c}
, on
\hat{\Omega}(G/K)
by

(11.2)

(\nabla_{V}^{c}(\omega))(x)=D_{0}^{t}(\omega(x\exp(tV(x)))

for
V\in{\mathcal{T}}(G/K)
and
\omega\in\hat{\Omega}(G/K)
, extended to

{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}(G/K)
by linearity. It is often called “the canonical connection”.
(One can not expect that it is always a Levi-Civita connection
in the sense of definition 8.8 of
[
30
]
).

We then extend
\nabla^{c}
to
{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)
, and
define a connection,
\nabla^{\mathcal{S}}
, on

{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
, by the same formula, setting

(11.3)

(\nabla_{V}^{c}({\mathcal{W}}))(x)=D_{0}^{t}({\mathcal{W}}(x\exp(tV(x)))),

and

(11.4)

(\nabla_{V}^{\mathcal{S}}(\Psi))(x)=D_{0}^{t}(\Psi(x\exp(tV(x)))),

for
V\in{\mathcal{T}}(G/K)
,
{\mathcal{W}}\in{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)
, and
\Psi\in{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
, extended to

{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}(G/K)
by linearity.
These are all evidently
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-linear in
V
.
We will need shortly the evident Leibniz rule

(11.5)

\nabla_{V}^{\mathcal{S}}(f\Psi)=\delta_{V}(f)\Psi+f\nabla_{V}^{\mathcal{S}}(\Psi).

We can view
\kappa
as a bilinear form on

{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)\times{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
with values in
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
, and so we
have the Leibniz rule

(11.6)

\nabla_{V}^{c}(\kappa_{\mathcal{W}}\Psi)=\kappa_{\nabla_{V}^{c}{\mathcal{W}}}\Psi+\kappa_{\mathcal{W}}(\nabla_{V}^{c}\Psi)

for any
V\in{\mathcal{T}}(G/K)^{\mathbb{C}}
,
{\mathcal{W}}\in{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)
and
\Psi\in{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
.
One can check that the connection
on
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
is compatible with the
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-valued inner product
in the sense of the Leibniz rule

\delta_{V}(\langle\Psi,\Phi\rangle_{\mathcal{A}})=\langle\nabla_{V}^{c}\Psi,\Phi\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}+\langle\Psi,\nabla_{V}^{c}\Phi\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}

for any
V\in{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}(G/K)
and
\Psi,\Phi\in{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
,
but we do not need this fact later.

We can now define the Dirac operator,
D
, on
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
as follows.
Let
\Psi\in{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
. Define
{\mathrm{d}}\Psi
on
{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}(G/K)
by

({\mathrm{d}}\Psi)(V)=\nabla^{\mathcal{S}}_{V}\Psi
. Then
{\mathrm{d}}\Psi
is an
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-linear
map from
{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}(G/K)
into
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
, and so
{\mathrm{d}}\Psi

can be viewed as an element of
\hat{\Omega}(G/K)\otimes{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
,
since
\hat{\Omega}(G/K)
is the
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-dual of
{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}(G/K)
.
By means of the inclusion of
\hat{\Omega}(G/K)
into
{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)

we can view
{\mathrm{d}}\Psi
as an element of

{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)\otimes{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
. We can then apply
\kappa
to obtain
an element of
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
. That is, with the above understanding,
we define
D
on
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
by

D(\Psi)=\kappa({\mathrm{d}}\Psi)

for
\Psi\in{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
.
We can express
D
in a more familiar and concrete form by using
a biframe as follows (much as done in proposition 2.9 of
[
72
]
).
Let
\{\epsilon_{j}\}
be a basis for
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
,
and let
\{E_{j}\}
be the dual basis for
{\mathfrak{g}}
. Let
P
be the
orthogonal projection from
{\mathfrak{g}}
onto
{\mathfrak{m}}
, and let
P^{\prime}
be the
orthogonal projection, from
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
onto
{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}
,
extended to the complexifications.
Notice that
P
commutes with the restriction to
K

of
\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits
, and similarly for
P^{\prime}
. For each
j
define an element,

\mathsf{E}_{j}
, of
{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}(G/K)
by

\mathsf{E}_{j}(x)=P\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits^{-1}_{x}(E_{j}),

and an element,

\eta_{j}
, of
\Omega(G/K)
by

\eta_{j}(x)=P^{\prime}\mathrm{Cad}^{-1}_{x}(\epsilon_{j}).

Then the pair
(\{\mathsf{E}_{j}\},\{\eta_{j}\})
is a biframe for
{\mathcal{T}}(G/K)

in the sense that it has the reproducing property

V=\sum\langle V,\eta_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}\mathsf{E}_{j}

for all
V\in{\mathcal{T}}^{\mathbb{C}}(G/K)
. Then

({\mathrm{d}}\Psi)(V)\ =\ \nabla^{\mathcal{S}}_{V}\Psi\ =\ \nabla^{\mathcal{S}}_{\sum\langle V,\eta_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}\mathsf{E}_{j}}\Psi\ =\sum\langle V,\eta_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{A}}\nabla^{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathsf{E}_{j}}\Psi,

so that

{\mathrm{d}}\Psi=\sum\eta_{j}\otimes\nabla^{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathsf{E}_{j}}\Psi,

where
\eta_{j}
can be viewed as an element of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)

via the inclusion
\Omega(G/K)\subset{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)
.
Then

D\Psi=\sum\kappa_{\eta_{j}}(\nabla^{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathsf{E}_{j}}\Psi).

When we combine the
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-valued
inner product on
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
with integration
by the left-invariant probability
measure on
G/K
, we obtain an ordinary inner product
on
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
. On completing
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
for this inner product, we obtain a
Hilbert space. In this way we can view
D
as a densely defined operator on a
Hilbert space.

Definition 11.2
.

The Dirac operator,
D
, for the given
\mathrm{Cad}
-invariant inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
,
and the given spinor bundle, is the operator
D
defined above.

As explained in theorem 1.7i of
[
65
]
and proposition 9.4 of
[
30
]
and later pages,
spinor bundles for Clifford bundles are not in general unique. The tensor product of a
spinor bundle by a line bundle will be another spinor bundle, and all the irreducible
spinor bundles are related in this way. Within our setting of equivariant bundles,
we need to tensor with
G
-equivariant line bundles. These correspond exactly to
the characters, that is, one-dimensional representations, of
K
. This is all relevant
to coadjoint orbits because in that case
K
always has non-trivial characters. All of our discussion of
this following equation 5.2 of
[
72
]

carries over to the present situation with only very minor changes.
So we will not discuss this further in this paper, and refer the interested reader to
[
72
]
.

Because essentially all of the operations defined above commute with the
actions of G by left translation, it is easily checked that:

Proposition 11.3
.

The Dirac operator
D
commutes with the action of G on
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
by left translation.

For
f\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
let
M_{f}
denote the operator on
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
coming from

{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
being an
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-module. Notice that
\hat{\mathrm{d}}f\in{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)

via
\hat{\Omega}(G/K)\subset{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)
so that

\kappa_{\hat{\mathrm{d}}f}
is an operator on
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
. Then
much as in proposition 8.3 of
[
71
]
we have

Proposition 11.4
.

For any
f\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
we have

[D,M_{f}]=\kappa_{\hat{\mathrm{d}}f}.

Proof.

For
f\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
and
\Psi\in{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
the Leibniz rule

{\mathrm{d}}(f\Psi)=(\hat{\mathrm{d}}f)\otimes\Psi+f{\mathrm{d}}\Psi

follows from equation
11.5
.
Then, since
\kappa
is
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
-linear, we have

\displaystyle[D,M_{f}]\Psi

\displaystyle=D(f\Psi)-fD(\Psi)=\kappa({\mathrm{d}}(f\Psi))-f\kappa({\mathrm{d}}\Psi)

\displaystyle=\kappa(\hat{\mathrm{d}}f\otimes\Psi+f{\mathrm{d}}\Psi)-\kappa(f{\mathrm{d}}\Psi)=\kappa_{\hat{\mathrm{d}}f}\Psi.

∎

Recall that for any
f\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
and
x\in G
we have
\hat{\mathrm{d}}f_{x}\in{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}
,
so that
\|\hat{\mathrm{d}}f_{x}\|_{{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}}
is defined in terms of the inner product on
{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}
.

Theorem 11.5
.

For any
f\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
we have

\|[D,M_{f}]\|=\sup\{\|\hat{\mathrm{d}}f_{x}\|_{{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}}:x\in G\}=\sup\{\|{\mathrm{d}}f_{x}\|_{{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}}:x\in G\}.

Proof.

Since
{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)
is a
*
-subalgebra of the C*-algebra

{\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime})=C(G,{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}))
, and we are viewing
\hat{\mathrm{d}}f

as an element of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell(G/K)
, we have

\|\hat{\mathrm{d}}f\|=\sup\{\|\hat{\mathrm{d}}f_{x}\|_{{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime})}:x\in G\}.

But, just as seen before Proposition
10.1
, if
\mu\in{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}

then
\|\mu\|_{{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime})}=\|\mu\|_{{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}}
.
The first equality in the statement
of the theorem follows directly from this.

Because
\mathrm{Cad}
is isometric on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}
, we have

\|\hat{\mathrm{d}}f_{x}\|_{{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}}=\|{\mathrm{d}}f_{x}\|_{{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}}
for all
x\in G
.
This gives the second equality in the statement of the theorem.
∎

When we recall the notation of Proposition
10.1
and earlier,
we see that we obtain:

Corollary 11.6
.

For any
f\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
we have

\|[D,M_{f}]\|=\|[D_{o},M_{f}]\|

Thus the corresponding C*-metrics on
{\mathcal{A}}
are equal. This will be
important for the proof of our main theorem.

For the reasons given immediately after Proposition
10.1
,
we then obtain:

Corollary 11.7
.

For any
f\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
we have

\|[D,M_{f}]\|=\sup\{\|\mathop{\mathrm{grad}}\nolimits_{x}f\|_{{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathbb{C}}}:x\in G\}.

Now a standard argument (e.g., see the text between definition 9.12
and exercise 9.7 of
[
30
]
) shows that if we denote
by
d
the ordinary metric on a Riemannian manifold
N

coming from its Riemannian metric, then for any two
points
p
and
q
of
N
we have

d(p,q)=\sup\{|f(p)-f(q)|:\|\mathop{\mathrm{grad}}\nolimits_{f}\|_{\infty}\leq 1\}.

On applying this to
G/K
, we obtain,
for
d
now the ordinary metric on
G/K
from our Riemannian metric,

d(p,q)=\sup\{|f(p)-f(q)|:\|[D,M_{f}]\|\leq 1\}.

This is the formula on which Connes focused for general Riemannian
manifolds
[
20
,
19
]
, as it shows that the Dirac operator contains
all of the metric information (and much more) for the manifold.
This is his motivation for advocating that metric data
for “non-commutative spaces” be encoded by providing
them with a “Dirac operator”.

We do not need to know the formal self-adjointness of
D
in this paper.
We refer the interested reader to theorem 6.1 of
[
72
]
, and to
theorem 8.4 of the most recent arXiv version of
[
71
]
.
(The published version of
[
71
]
has a serious error in the
proof of theorem 8.4.)

12.
Complex structure on coadjoint orbits

We want to show that in the case of coadjoint orbits we can choose a
spinor bundle whose fibers are irreducible. The path to showing this
involves showing that coadjoint orbits have complex structures. In fact,
coadjoint orbits can be equipped with Kähler structures,
as described in section 1 of
[
72
]
(and see also the theorem
at the end of section 3 of
[
14
]
), but we do not
need the full force of that fact. We will discuss in Section
18

why our approach does not always fit perfectly
with the Kähler structures.
So here we construct the complex structures directly,
along the lines discussed in
[
2
]
. This requires
the basic facts concerning weights and roots of compact
semisimple Lie groups. In the next few paragraphs we follow
somewhat closely the notation and
development in section 1 of
[
72
]
.

We assume now that
G
is a connected compact semisimple
Lie group, with Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
. The coadjoint orbits are the
orbits in
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
for the action
\mathrm{Cad}
. Fix
\mu_{\diamond}\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
,
with
\mu_{\diamond}\neq 0
.
Let
K
denote the
\mathrm{Cad}
-stability subgroup of
\mu_{\diamond}
, so that
x\mapsto\mathrm{Cad}_{x}(\mu_{\diamond})
gives a
G
-equivariant diffeomorphism from
G/K
onto the
\mathrm{Cad}
-orbit of
\mu_{\diamond}
. We will usually work with
G/K
rather than the orbit itself.

We choose an
\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits
-invariant inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}
, for example, the negative
of the Killing form of
{\mathfrak{g}}
(since
G
is compact).
This actually is not much more general than choosing
the negative of the Killing form itself, because if
{\mathfrak{g}}
is simple, then every

\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits
-invariant inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}
is just a scaler multiple of
the negative of the Killing form, whereas if
{\mathfrak{g}}
is just semisimple,
then every

\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits
-invariant inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}
just arises from taking various
scaler multiples of
the negatives of the Killing forms on its simple ideals.

The
action
\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits
of
G
on
{\mathfrak{g}}
is by orthogonal operators,
and so the action
\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
on
{\mathfrak{g}}
is by skew-adjoint operators .
There is a unique
Z_{\diamond}\in{\mathfrak{g}}
such that

(12.1)

\mu_{\diamond}(X)=\langle X,Z_{\diamond}\rangle\text{ for all }X\in{\mathfrak{g}}.

It is easily seen that the
\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits
-stability subgroup of
Z_{\diamond}
is again
K
.

Let
T_{\diamond}
be the closure in
G
of the one-parameter group
r\mapsto\exp(rZ_{\diamond})
, so that
T_{\diamond}
is a torus subgroup of
G
. Then it is easily seen that
K
consists exactly of all the elements of
G
that commute with all the elements of
T_{\diamond}
. Note that
T_{\diamond}
is contained in the center of
K
(but need not coincide with the center). Since each element of
K
will lie in a torus subgroup of
G
that contains
T_{\diamond}
, it follows that
K
is the union of the tori that it contains, and so
K
is connected (corollary 4.22 of
[
44
]
). Thus for most purposes we can just work with the Lie algebra,
{\mathfrak{k}}
, of
K
when convenient. In particular,
{\mathfrak{k}}=\{X\in{\mathfrak{g}}:[X,Z_{\diamond}]=0\}
, and
{\mathfrak{k}}
contains the Lie algebra,
{\mathfrak{t}}_{\diamond}
, of
T_{\diamond}
.

Let
{\mathfrak{m}}={\mathfrak{k}}^{\perp}
with respect to chosen inner product.
Since
\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits
preserves the inner product, we see that
{\mathfrak{m}}
is carried into
itself by the restriction of
\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits
to
K
. Thus
[{\mathfrak{k}},{\mathfrak{m}}]\subseteq{\mathfrak{m}}
.
As we have seen in Section
11
,

{\mathfrak{m}}
can be conveniently identified with the tangent
space to
G/K
at the coset
K
(which corresponds to the point
\mu_{\diamond}

of the coadjoint orbit). To define a complex structure on
G/K
we need to
define a complex structure on
{\mathfrak{m}}

that commutes with the action of
K
via
\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits
.

Fix a choice of a maximal torus,
T
, of
G
that contains
T_{\diamond}
.
Then
T
is contained in
K
, and is a maximal torus in
K
. (Thus we
are in the setting to which the results of
[
46
]
apply, since
K
has
the same rank as
G
. Our
{\mathfrak{k}}
is the
\mathfrak{r}
there, while our
{\mathfrak{m}}

is the
\mathfrak{p}
there, up to complexification.
But we will not use results from that paper.) We denote the
Lie algebra of
T
by the traditional
{\mathfrak{h}}
. Since
K
is compact, it is reductive,
and so
{\mathfrak{k}}
splits as
{\mathfrak{k}}={\mathfrak{h}}_{m}\oplus{\mathfrak{k}}_{s}
where
{\mathfrak{k}}_{s}
is the
semisimple subalgebra
{\mathfrak{k}}_{s}=[{\mathfrak{k}},{\mathfrak{k}}]
of
{\mathfrak{k}}

and
{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathfrak{m}}
is the center of
{\mathfrak{k}}
(so
{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathfrak{m}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{h}}
).
Thus
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{m}}\oplus{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathfrak{m}}\oplus{\mathfrak{k}}_{s}
.
Furthermore,
{\mathfrak{h}}
splits as
{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathfrak{m}}\oplus{\mathfrak{h}}_{s}

where
{\mathfrak{h}}_{s}
is a Cartan subalgebra of
{\mathfrak{k}}_{s}
.

For any finite-dimensional unitary representation
({\mathcal{H}},\pi)

of
T
we let
\pi
also denote the corresponding representation of
{\mathfrak{h}}
. For each
H\in{\mathfrak{h}}

the operator
\pi_{H}
is skew-adjoint, and so its eigenvalues are purely imaginary. Since
the
\pi_{H}
’s all commute with each other, they are simultaneously diagonalizable. Because
we need to keep track of the structure over
{\mathbb{R}}
, we will use a convention for the weights of
a representation that is slightly different from the usual convention. We used it previously
in
[
76
]
. If
\xi\in{\mathcal{H}}
is a common
eigenvector for the
\pi_{H}
’s, there will be a linear
functional
\alpha
on
{\mathfrak{h}}
(with values in
{\mathbb{R}}
)
such that

\pi_{H}(\xi)=i\alpha(H)\xi

for all
H\in{\mathfrak{h}}
.
For each
\alpha\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{\prime}
(where
{\mathfrak{h}}^{\prime}
denotes the dual vector space to
{\mathfrak{h}}
) we set

{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha}=\{\xi\in{\mathcal{H}}:\pi_{H}(\xi)=i\alpha(H)\xi\ \ \mathrm{for\ all}\ H\in{\mathfrak{h}}\}.

If there are non-zero vectors in
{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha}
then we say that
\alpha
is a
weight
of the
representation
({\mathcal{H}},\pi)
. We denote the set of all weights for this representation by

\Delta_{\pi}
. Then

{\mathcal{H}}=\bigoplus\{{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha}:\alpha\in\Delta_{\pi}\}.

We let
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathbb{C}}
denote the complexification of
{\mathfrak{g}}
, with inner product
coming from that on
{\mathfrak{g}}
, and corresponding unitary representation

\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits
of
G
on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathbb{C}}
.
The non-zero weights for
\mathop{\mathrm{Ad}}\nolimits
and
\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits

acting on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathbb{C}}
,
are called the “roots” of
G
. We denote the set
of roots simply by
\Delta
. Because we are dealing with the
complexification of a representation
over
{\mathbb{R}}
, if
\alpha\in\Delta
then

-\alpha\in\Delta
.
For each root
\alpha
we let
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathbb{C}}_{\alpha}
denote the corresponding root space.
It is
a standard fact that these root spaces are all of dimension 1, and
that
[{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathbb{C}}_{\alpha},{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathbb{C}}_{-\alpha}]

In the standard way

79

, of

We want to choose usual elements

Let

be a finite-dimensional unitary
representation of

{\mathfrak{g}}
to a representation (still denoted by

{\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}}

Let

with

{\mathfrak{g}}

Thus it is appropriate to define an involution on

by

so that

for all

Notice that for all

The following result is well-known. It occurs with proof as proposition 1.1
of

Proposition 12.1
.

With notation as above, for each
\alpha\in\Delta^{+}
we can choose
H_{\alpha}\in i{\mathfrak{h}}
and

E_{\alpha}\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{\mathbb{C}}_{\alpha}
such that
[E_{\alpha},E_{\alpha}^{*}]=H_{\alpha}
and

[H_{\alpha},E_{\alpha}]=2E_{\alpha}
. Setting

F_{\alpha}=E_{\alpha}^{*}
, we then obtain
[H_{\alpha},F_{\alpha}]=-2F_{\alpha}
.

Notice that
{\mathfrak{k}^{\mathbb{C}}}=\{X\in{\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}}:[Z_{\diamond},X]=0\}

If
X\in{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}^{\mathbb{C}}
then
[Z_{\diamond},X]=i\alpha(Z_{\diamond})X
.
It follows that

{\mathfrak{m}^{\mathbb{C}}}=\bigoplus\{{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}^{\mathbb{C}}:\alpha(Z_{\diamond})\neq 0\},

and that

{\mathfrak{k}^{\mathbb{C}}}={\mathfrak{h}^{\mathbb{C}}}\oplus\bigoplus\{{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}^{\mathbb{C}}:\alpha(Z_{\diamond})=0\}.

It then follows that

{\mathfrak{k}}_{s}^{\mathbb{C}}={\mathfrak{h}}_{s}^{\mathbb{C}}\oplus\bigoplus\{{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}^{\mathbb{C}}:\alpha(Z_{\diamond})=0\}.

Thus it is appropriate to set

\Delta_{s}=\{\alpha\in\Delta:\alpha(Z_{\diamond})=0\}=\{\alpha\in\Delta:\alpha(Z)=0\ \mathrm{for\ all}\ Z\in{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathbb{C}}\},

and to identify
\Delta_{s}
with the root system for
{\mathfrak{k}}_{s}^{\mathbb{C}}
with respect
to its Cartan subalgebra
{\mathfrak{h}}_{s}^{\mathbb{C}}
. It is also appropriate to set

\Delta_{\mathfrak{m}}=\{\alpha\in\Delta:\alpha(Z_{\diamond})\neq 0\}=\{\alpha\in\Delta:\ \mathrm{there\ is\ a}\ Z\in{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathfrak{m}}^{\mathbb{C}}\ \mathrm{with}\ \alpha(Z)\neq 0\}.

Note that
\Delta_{\mathfrak{m}}
is “invariant” with respect to
\Delta_{s}

in the sense that if
\alpha\in\Delta_{\mathfrak{m}}
and
\beta\in\Delta_{s}
and if

\alpha+\beta\in\Delta
, then
\alpha+\beta\in\Delta_{\mathfrak{m}}
.

We need a partial order on
\Delta_{\mathfrak{m}}
, given by specifying
a subset
\Delta_{\mathfrak{m}}^{+}
with the usual properties,
but which in addition is
\Delta_{s}
-invariant in the sense that if

\alpha\in\Delta_{\mathfrak{m}}^{+}
and
\beta\in\Delta_{s}
and if
\alpha+\beta\in\Delta
, then
\alpha+\beta\in\Delta_{\mathfrak{m}}^{+}
.
In general there are many such partial orders, coming from
Weyl-chamber-type considerations. (See
[
2
]
.)
But there is one such order canonically associated with

, namely specified by

\Delta_{\mathfrak{m}}^{+}=\{\alpha\in\Delta_{\mathfrak{m}}:\alpha(Z_{\diamond})>0\}.

Notice that this makes sense because
\alpha(Z_{\diamond})\in{\mathbb{R}}
since

Z_{\diamond}\in{\mathfrak{h}}
and
\alpha\in{\mathfrak{h}}^{\prime}
.
It is clear that
\Delta_{\mathfrak{m}}^{+}
is
\Delta_{s}
-invariant.

It is then natural to set
{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}=\bigoplus\{{\mathfrak{g}}_{\alpha}^{\mathbb{C}}:\alpha\in\Delta_{\mathfrak{m}}^{+}\}
,
and similarly for
{\mathfrak{n}}^{-}
.
Then
{\mathfrak{m}^{\mathbb{C}}}={\mathfrak{n}}^{+}\oplus{\mathfrak{n}}^{-}
.

Notation 12.2
.

Define an operator,
J
, on
{\mathfrak{m}^{\mathbb{C}}}
by

J(X)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}iX&\text{if }X\in{\mathfrak{n}}^{+},\\
-iX&\text{if }X\in{\mathfrak{n}}^{-}.\end{array}\right.

Notice that

is isometric.

We show now that

commutes with the
\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits
-action of
{\mathfrak{k}}^{\mathbb{C}}
on
{\mathfrak{m}^{\mathbb{C}}}
.
This uses the
\Delta_{s}
-invariance of
\Delta_{\mathfrak{m}}^{+}
.
Let
\beta\in\Delta_{s}
. Then for any
\alpha\in\Delta_{\mathfrak{m}}^{+}

J(\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{E_{\beta}}(E_{\alpha}))=J([E_{\beta},E_{\alpha}])=i[E_{\beta},E_{\alpha}]=[E_{\beta},iE_{\alpha}]=\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits_{E_{\beta}}(J(E_{\alpha}))

since

if
\alpha+\beta\in\Delta
. If instead

Thus

commutes with the
\mathop{\mathrm{ad}}\nolimits
-action of
{\mathfrak{k}}^{\mathbb{C}}
on
{\mathfrak{n}}^{+}

commutes with the

{\mathfrak{k}}^{\mathbb{C}}

and so on

It is clear from the definition of

, for

the identity operator on
{\mathfrak{m}^{\mathbb{C}}}

Ordinary complex conjugation on
{\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}}
with respect to
{\mathfrak{g}}
carries

onto

{\mathfrak{g}}_{-\alpha}^{\mathbb{C}}
for each

onto

.
It follows that

carries

onto itself, and so is a complex structure on

. Of course

commutes
with the actions of

and
{\mathfrak{k}}
on
{\mathfrak{m}}
.

Let
\alpha\in\Delta_{\mathfrak{m}}^{+}
, and consider

where the bar denotes the ordinary complex conjugation. Set

They are invariant under complex conjugation, and so they are in
{\mathfrak{g}}
, and they span

and that for any

[Z,X_{\alpha}]=\alpha(Z)Y_{\alpha}=\alpha(Z)J(X_{\alpha})\quad\mathrm{and}\quad[Z,Y_{\alpha}]=-\alpha(Z)X_{\alpha}=\alpha(Z)J(Y_{\alpha}),

Thus if we view

{\mathfrak{h}}_{\mathfrak{m}}
on
{\mathfrak{m}}

is isometric on
{\mathfrak{m}}

since earlier we saw that it is isometric on
{\mathfrak{m}^{\mathbb{C}}}

Bridges with symbols

The definition of quantum Gromov-Hausdorff distance between compact
C*-metric spaces has evolved over the years since the first definition
was proposed in
[
69
]
. At present the definition with the best properties is
Latrémolière’s dual Gromov-Hausdorff propinquity
[
49
]
, and it forms
the base for the spectral propinquity
[
54
]
.
But its definition can be somewhat
difficult to work with directly for some classes of examples,
including the examples we are considering. Latrémolière had slightly
earlier introduced a somewhat stronger definition that
he called the Gromov-Hausdorff propinquity
[
50
]
.
This definition works well for our examples. So in this section we will recall the definition of the
Gromov-Hausdorff propinquity and explain
how it relates to our situation. With this as preparation, in the next section we will
prove that a suitable sequence of matrix algebras, equipped with C*-metrics
coming from Dirac operators,
converges for the Gromov-Hausdorff propinquity to the
coadjoint orbit for a given highest-weight vector.
(This will imply convergence also for the dual Gromov-Hausdorff propinquity).

For any two unital C*-algebras

and
{\mathcal{B}}

and

are unital injective homomorphisms of

{\mathcal{A}}
and

into
{\mathcal{D}}
, and
\omega
is a self-adjoint element
of
{\mathcal{D}}
such that 1 is an element of the spectrum
of
\omega
and

We will often omit mentioning the injections

is given by

\mathrm{height}(\Pi)=\max\{\mathrm{Haus}_{\rho_{\mathcal{A}}}(S_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}(\omega),S({\mathcal{A}})),\ \mathrm{Haus}_{\rho_{\mathcal{B}}}(S_{1}^{\mathcal{B}}(\omega),S({\mathcal{B}}))\},

where the Hausdorff distances are with respect to the indicated
metrics.
The length of
\Pi
is then defined by

\mathrm{length}(\Pi)=\max\{\mathrm{reach}(\Pi),\mathrm{height}(\Pi)\}.

Latrémolière defines the length of a finite path ( a “trek”) of bridges to be the sum of the lengths
of the individual bridges. He then defines the Gromov-Hausdorff propinquity
between two compact C*-metric spaces to be the infimum
of the lengths of all finite paths between them. (This gives the triangle inequality.)
He proves the remarkable fact that if
the propinquity between two compact C*-metric spaces is 0,
then they are isometric in the sense that there is an isomorphism
between the C*-algebras
that carries the C*-metric on one to the C*-metric on the other.
Thus the propinquity is a metric on the set of isometry classes of compact
C*-metric spaces. But we will not need to deal directly with
finite paths of bridges because we will prove that, for the sequences of
bridges that we will
construct, already their lengths will converge to 0.

For the main context of this paper there is extra structure
available to help with measuring the lengths of bridges.
The next paragraph is strongly motivated by the discussion of
bridges with conditional expectations in sections 4 and 5 of
[
74
]
.

Definition 15.5
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
be unital C*-algebras,
and let

\Pi=({\mathcal{D}},\omega)
be a bridge from
{\mathcal{A}}
to
{\mathcal{B}}
. By a
pair of symbols
for

\Pi
we mean a pair of unital completely positive maps

(\sigma^{\mathcal{A}},\sigma^{\mathcal{B}})
such that
\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}
maps
{\mathcal{D}}
to
{\mathcal{A}}
and

\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}(\omega)=1_{\mathcal{A}}
, while

\sigma^{\mathcal{B}}
maps
{\mathcal{D}}
to
{\mathcal{B}}
and
\sigma^{\mathcal{B}}(\omega)=1_{\mathcal{B}}
.

(Thus
\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}
and
\sigma^{\mathcal{B}}
are “definite” on
\omega
.
We do not require
any relation between these two maps.)

For our applications we define
\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}
by

(15.2)

\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}(F)(x)=\mathrm{tr}_{\mathcal{B}}(F(x)\alpha_{x}(P))

for any
F\in{\mathcal{D}}=C(G/K,{\mathcal{B}})
,
where
\mathrm{tr}_{\mathcal{B}}
is the un-normalized
trace on
{\mathcal{B}}
.
We use the term “symbol” in definition
15.5
because
the restriction of this particular
\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}
to
{\mathcal{B}}
is exactly
the Berezin contravariant symbol map, and the
restriction to
{\mathcal{A}}
of the particular

\sigma^{\mathcal{B}}
that we will define just below
is exactly the Berezin covariant symbol map,
that play an essential role in
[
70
]
.

For our applications we define
\sigma^{\mathcal{B}}
by

(15.3)

\sigma^{\mathcal{B}}(F)=d_{\mathcal{H}}\int_{G/K}F(x)\alpha_{x}(P))\ dx,

where
d_{\mathcal{H}}
is the dimension of the Hilbert
space of the irrep,
and
dx
refers to the
G
-invariant probability measure
on
G/K
. It is easily seen
[
74
]
that

\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}
and
\sigma^{\mathcal{B}}
are unital and completely positive,
and furthermore that they intertwine the actions of

G
on
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
with the diagonal action of
G
on

{\mathcal{D}}={\mathcal{A}}\otimes{\mathcal{B}}
.

Definition 15.6
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
be unital C*-algebras and
and let

\Pi=({\mathcal{D}},\omega)
be a bridge from
{\mathcal{A}}
to
{\mathcal{B}}
.
Let

L^{\mathcal{A}}
and
L^{\mathcal{B}}
be C*-metrics on
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
.
Let
(\sigma^{\mathcal{A}},\sigma^{\mathcal{B}})
be a pair of symbols for
\Pi
.
We say that this pair of symbols is
compatible
with the
C*-metrics
L^{\mathcal{A}}
and
L^{\mathcal{B}}
if their restrictions to
{\mathcal{B}}

and
{\mathcal{A}}
satisfy

L^{\mathcal{A}}(\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}(b))\leq L^{\mathcal{B}}(b)\quad\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\quad L^{\mathcal{B}}(\sigma^{\mathcal{B}}(a))\leq L^{\mathcal{A}}(a)

for all
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
and
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
. (Here we are viewing

{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
as subalgebras of
{\mathcal{D}}
in the evident way
discussed in the second paragraph of this section.)

We now show how to use a compatible pair of symbols to
obtain an upper bound for the reach of
a bridge
\Pi
.
Let
b\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}_{\mathcal{B}}
be given. As an approximation to
\omega b
by
an element of the form
a\omega
for some
a\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}_{\mathcal{A}}
we take

a=\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}(b)
. It is indeed in
{\mathcal{L}}^{1}_{\mathcal{A}}
by the
compatibility condition. This prompts us to set

(15.4)

\gamma^{\mathcal{B}}=\sup\{\|\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}(b)\omega-\omega b\|_{\mathcal{D}}:b\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}_{\mathcal{B}}\}.

Interchanging the roles of
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, we define
\gamma^{\mathcal{A}}

similarly. We then see that

(15.5)

\mathrm{reach}(\Pi)\leq\max\{\gamma^{\mathcal{A}},\gamma^{\mathcal{B}}\}.

We will see shortly
why this upper bound is useful for our applications.

We now consider the height of
\Pi
. For this we need to consider
S_{1}(\omega)

as defined above. Because

\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}
is positive and unital, its
composition with any
\mu\in S({\mathcal{A}})
is in
S({\mathcal{D}})
.
By definition
\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}(\omega)=1_{\mathcal{A}}
. Thus for every
\mu\in S({\mathcal{A}})

we obtain an element,
\phi_{\mu}
, of
S_{1}(\omega)
, defined by

\phi_{\mu}(d)=\mu(\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}(d))

for all
d\in D
. This provides us with a substantial collection of elements
of
S_{1}(\omega)
.
Since to estimate the height of
\Pi
we need to
estimate the distance from each
\mu\in S({\mathcal{A}})
to

S_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}(\omega)
, we can hope that
\phi_{\mu}
restricted
to
{\mathcal{A}}
is relatively close to
\mu
. Accordingly, for any

a\in{\mathcal{A}}
we
compute

|\mu(a)-\phi_{\mu}(a)|=|\mu(a)-\mu(\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}(a))|\leq\|a-\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}(a)\|.

This prompts us to set

(15.6)

\delta^{\mathcal{A}}=\sup\{\|a-\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}(a)\|:a\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}_{\mathcal{A}}\}.

Then we see that

\rho_{L^{\mathcal{A}}}(\mu,\ \phi_{\mu}|_{\mathcal{A}})\leq\delta^{\mathcal{A}}

for all
\mu\in S({\mathcal{A}})
.
We define
\delta^{\mathcal{B}}
in the same way, and obtain the
corresponding estimate for the distances from elements
of
S({\mathcal{B}})
to the restriction of
S_{1}(\omega)
to
{\mathcal{B}}
.
In this way we see that

(15.7)

\mathrm{height}(\Pi)\leq\max\{\delta^{\mathcal{A}},\delta^{\mathcal{B}}\}.

(Notice that
\delta^{\mathcal{A}}
involves what
\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}
does on
{\mathcal{A}}
,
whereas
\gamma^{\mathcal{A}}
involves what
\sigma^{\mathcal{B}}
does on
{\mathcal{A}}
.)

While this bound is natural within this context, it turns out not to be
so useful for our main applications. The following steps might
not initially seem to be natural, but in the next section
we will
see that for our main applications they are quite
useful. Our
notation is as above. For any
\nu\in S({\mathcal{B}})
we easily see that

\nu\circ\sigma^{\mathcal{B}}\circ\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}\in S(D)
.
But from the relation between the symbols and

\omega
it is easily seen that

\nu\circ\sigma^{\mathcal{B}}\circ\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}(\omega)=1
, so that

\nu\circ\sigma^{\mathcal{B}}\circ\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}
is in
S_{1}(\omega)
.
Let us
denote its restriction to
{\mathcal{B}}
by
\psi_{\nu}
, so that

\psi_{\nu}\in S_{1}^{\mathcal{B}}(\omega)
. Then
\psi_{\nu}
can be used as an
approximation to
\nu
by an element of
S_{1}^{\mathcal{B}}(\omega)
. Now
for any
b\in{\mathcal{B}}
we have

|\nu(b)-\psi_{\nu}(b)|=|\nu(b)-(\nu\circ\sigma^{\mathcal{B}}\circ\sigma^{\mathcal{A}})(b)|\leq\|b-\sigma^{\mathcal{B}}(\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}(b))\|.

Notation 15.7
.

In terms of the above notation we set

\hat{\delta}^{\mathcal{B}}=\sup\{\|b-\sigma^{\mathcal{B}}(\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}(b))\|:b\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}_{\mathcal{B}}\}.

It follows that

\rho_{L^{\mathcal{B}}}(\nu,\ \psi_{\nu})\leq\hat{\delta}^{\mathcal{B}}

for all
\nu\in S({\mathcal{B}})
, so that

(15.8)

\mathrm{Haus}_{\rho_{\mathcal{B}}}(S_{1}^{\mathcal{B}}(\omega),S({\mathcal{B}}))\}\leq\hat{\delta}^{\mathcal{B}}.

We define
\hat{\delta}^{\mathcal{A}}
in the same way, and obtain the
corresponding bound for the distances from elements
of
S({\mathcal{A}})
to
S_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}(\omega)
.
In this way we obtain:

Proposition 15.8
.

For notation as above,

\mathrm{height}(\Pi)\leq\max\{\min\{\delta^{\mathcal{A}},\hat{\delta}^{\mathcal{A}}\},\min\{\delta^{\mathcal{B}},\hat{\delta}^{\mathcal{B}}\}\}.

16.
The proof that matrix algebras converge to coadjoint orbits

We continue with the notation of the previous sections, so
G
is a
connected compact semisimple Lie group.
Not every
\mu\in{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
is associated with an irrep of
G
.
To be associated with an irrep, the restriction of
\mu
to any Cartan
subalgebra
{\mathfrak{h}}
of
{\mathfrak{g}}
must exponentiate to a one-dimensional representation of the corresponding maximal torus, and so in particular must be “integral”.
But then it can correspond to a weight of many different irreps. To be
viewed as corresponding to a unique (equivalence class of an) irrep,

\mu
must be “dominant”
with respect to some choice of Cartan subalgebra and positive root system.

So let us fix an irrep
({\mathcal{H}},U)
of
G
.
Fix a choice of a maximal torus in
G
with Cartan subalgebra
{\mathfrak{h}}
,
and a choice of a positive root system
\Delta^{+}
for it. Then choose elements

H_{\alpha},E_{\alpha},F_{\alpha}
in
{\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}}
as described in Proposition
12.1
.
Let
\xi_{\diamond}
be a highest-weight vector of length 1 for the irrep.
As a weight vector it is an eigenvector of
U_{H}
for all

H\in{\mathfrak{h}}
. The fact that it is a highest-weight vector means
exactly that
U_{E_{\alpha}}\xi_{\diamond}=0
for all
\alpha\in\Delta^{+}
. Define
\mu_{\diamond}
in

{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
by

\mu_{\diamond}(X)=-i\langle\xi_{\diamond},U_{X}\xi_{\diamond}\rangle.

(We take the inner product on
{\mathcal{H}}
to be linear in the
second variable.)
Up to the sign,
\mu_{\diamond}
is exactly the “equivariant momentum map” of
equation 23 of
[
48
]
.
Because
U_{X}
is skew-symmetric for all
X\in{\mathfrak{g}}
, we
see that
\mu_{\diamond}
is
{\mathbb{R}}
-valued on
{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Note that
\mu_{\diamond}
does not depend on the phase of
\xi_{\diamond}
.
We extend
\mu_{\diamond}
to
{\mathfrak{g}^{\mathbb{C}}}
and
{\mathfrak{h}^{\mathbb{C}}}
in
the usual way. Because
\xi_{\diamond}
is a highest-weight vector,
we clearly have

\mu_{\diamond}(E_{\alpha})=0
for all
\alpha\in\Delta^{+}
, and then
\mu_{\diamond}(F_{\alpha})=0
for
all
\alpha\in\Delta^{+}
because
F_{\alpha}=E_{\alpha}^{*}
. Furthermore, because

[E_{\alpha},E_{a}^{*}]=H_{\alpha}
and

[H_{\alpha},E_{\alpha}]=2E_{\alpha}
and
[H_{\alpha},F_{\alpha}]=-2F_{\alpha}
, the
triplet
(H_{\alpha},E_{\alpha},F_{\alpha})
generates via
U
a representation
of
sl(2,{\mathbb{C}})
,
for which the spectrum of
U_{H_{\alpha}}
must consist
of integers. In particular,
i\mu_{\diamond}(H_{\alpha})
is an integer, necessarily
non-negative, in fact equal to
\|F_{\alpha}\xi_{\diamond}\|^{2}
.
We see in this way that
\mu_{\diamond}
is a quite special
element of
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
.

Let
\mu
denote the weight of
\xi_{\diamond}
, so that

U_{H}(\xi_{\diamond})=i\mu(H)\xi_{\diamond}
for all
H\in{\mathfrak{h}}
.
Comparison with the definition of
\mu_{\diamond}
shows that
\mu
is
simply the restriction of
\mu_{\diamond}
to
{\mathfrak{h}}
. It is clear
that
\mu_{\diamond}
is determined by
\mu
in the sense that
\mu_{\diamond}

has value
0
on the
\mathop{\mathrm{Kil}}\nolimits
-orthogonal complement of
{\mathfrak{h}^{\mathbb{C}}}
.
Thus from now on
we will let
\mu_{\diamond}
also denote the weight of
\xi_{\diamond}
.
(Thus the special properties of
\mu_{\diamond}
mean that,
as a weight,
\mu_{\diamond}
is a “dominant integral weight”.)

We now construct the sequence of matrix algebras that we
will show converges to
the coadjoint orbit of
\mu_{\diamond}
.
For each positive integer

let
({\mathcal{H}}^{m},U^{m})
be an irreducible representation of
G

with highest weight

.
All the

\mathrm{Cad}
-stability group,

.
Then let
{\mathcal{B}}^{m}={\mathcal{L}}({\mathcal{H}}^{m})

with action

of

As before, we let

as for Definition
15.1

\rho_{\mathcal{A}}
is. It follows that

\mathrm{height_{D}}(\Pi_{m})=\mathrm{Haus}_{\rho_{D^{m}}}(S_{1}^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}(\omega),S({\mathcal{B}}^{m}))

where
\rho_{D^{m}}
is the metric on
S({\mathcal{B}}^{m})
determined by
the Dirac operator
D^{m}
.

Recall from Notation
15.7
that the general definition of
\hat{\delta}^{\mathcal{B}}

is given by

\hat{\delta}^{\mathcal{B}}=\sup\{\|b-\sigma^{\mathcal{B}}(\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}(b))\|:b\in{\mathcal{L}}^{1}_{\mathcal{B}}\}.

When this definition is applied using
\sigma^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\circ\sigma_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}
, and

{\mathcal{L}}_{D^{m}}^{1}
or
{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell m}^{1}
,
we will denote it by

\hat{\delta}^{D^{m}}
(as above) or
\hat{\delta}_{\ell}^{m}
respectively.
From equation (
15.8
) we see that

\mathrm{Haus}_{\rho_{D^{m}}}(S_{1}^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}(\omega),S({\mathcal{B}}^{m}))\}\leq\hat{\delta}^{D^{m}}.

But from the containments of display
(
16.2
) it follows immediately that

\hat{\delta}^{D^{m}}\leq\hat{\delta}_{\ell}^{m}.

From this and inequality (
15.8
) we immediately obtain:

Proposition 16.5
.

For all
m
we have
\mathrm{height_{D}}(\Pi_{m})\leq\hat{\delta}_{\ell}^{m}
.

But the paragraph just before proposition 6.7 of
[
74
]

(where the
\hat{\delta}^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}
there is our
\hat{\delta}_{\ell}^{m}
)
explains how theorem 11.5 of
[
73
]
gives a
proof that as
m
goes to
\infty
the sequence

\{\hat{\delta}_{\ell}^{m}\}
converge to 0.
We thus obtain:

Proposition 16.6
.

The sequence
\{\mathrm{height_{D}}(\Pi_{m})\}
converges to 0
as
m
goes to
\infty
.

Combining this with Proposition
16.4
, we obtain
the main theorem of this paper:

Theorem 16.7
.

The sequence
\{\mathrm{length_{D}}(\Pi_{m})\}
converges to 0
as
m
goes to
\infty
. Thus the sequence
\{({\mathcal{B}}^{m},L^{D^{m}})\}

of compact C*-metric spaces converges to the
compact C*-metric space
\{({\mathcal{A}},L^{D^{\mathcal{A}}})\}

for Latrémolière’s propinquity.

17.
The linking Dirac operator

In this section we offer some generalities that place bridges
in a more Dirac-operator-like setting.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
be unital C*-algebras, and let
\Pi=({\mathcal{D}},\omega)
be a bridge from

{\mathcal{A}}
to
{\mathcal{B}}
(so we view
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
as unital subalgebras of
{\mathcal{D}}
).
Let
{\mathcal{E}}
be a unital C*-algebra that contains
{\mathcal{D}}
as a unital C*-subalgebra.
Then
({\mathcal{E}},\omega)
is equally well a bridge from
{\mathcal{A}}
to
{\mathcal{B}}
.
The corresponding bridge-norm

N_{\Pi}
on
{\mathcal{A}}\oplus{\mathcal{B}}
defined by

(17.1)

N_{\Pi}(a,b)=\|a\omega-\omega b\|

is the same regardless of whether we use the bridge
({\mathcal{D}},\omega)

or the bridge
({\mathcal{E}},\omega)
. And every state in
S_{1}(\omega)
for
{\mathcal{D}}

has an extension (perhaps not unique)
to a state on
{\mathcal{E}}
, which is then clearly in the
S_{1}(\omega)

for
{\mathcal{E}}
. From these observations, it is easily seen that for any given
C*-metrics
L^{\mathcal{A}}
and
L^{\mathcal{B}}
on
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
,
the reach and the height of the bridges
({\mathcal{D}},\omega)
and
({\mathcal{E}},\omega)

are the same. Thus these two bridges have the same length.

Suppose now that
({\mathcal{H}},\pi)
is a faithful (non-degenerate)
representation of
{\mathcal{D}}
. We can then view
{\mathcal{D}}
as a unital subalgebra
of
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
. The above comments then apply, and
({\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}),\omega)

is a bridge from
{\mathcal{A}}
to
{\mathcal{B}}
that has the same length as the bridge

({\mathcal{D}},\omega)
.

Definition 17.1
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
be unital C*-algebras.
By a
Hilbert bridge
from
{\mathcal{A}}
to
{\mathcal{B}}
we mean a quadruple

({\mathcal{H}},\pi^{\mathcal{A}},\pi^{\mathcal{B}},\omega)
, where
{\mathcal{H}}
is a Hilbert space,

\pi^{\mathcal{A}}
and
\pi^{\mathcal{B}}
are faithful (non-degenerate) representations of

{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
on
{\mathcal{H}}
, and
\omega
is a self-adjoint operator on
{\mathcal{H}}

that has 1 in its spectrum. We will often view
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
as
unital C*-subalgebras
of
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
, and omit
\pi^{\mathcal{A}}
and
\pi^{\mathcal{B}}
in our notation.

As mentioned after Definition
15.4
,
Latrémolière defines his propinquity in terms of finite paths (which he calls ”treks”)
of bridges between C*-metric spaces (See
[
50
]
.)
He defines the length of a trek to be the sum of the lengths of the bridges in the trek,
and he defines the propinquity from
{\mathcal{A}}
to
{\mathcal{B}}
to be the infimum of the lengths
of all treks from
{\mathcal{A}}
to
{\mathcal{B}}
. Actually, Latrémolière does not require that the pivot

\omega
be self-adjoint. I am unaware of an example where this makes a difference,
but since the propinquity is defined as an infimum, the propinquity requiring pivots
to be self-adjoint will be greater than or equal to the propinquity not requiring that.

From the comments made just before Definition
17.1
,
it is clear that if one insists on defining the propinquity using only Hilbert bridges,
it will never-the-less coincide with the usual propinquity (using self-adjoint pivots).

Suppose now that
\Pi=({\mathcal{H}},\omega)
is a Hilbert bridge from
{\mathcal{A}}
to
{\mathcal{B}}
.
Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{o}={\mathcal{H}}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}
. For
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
viewed as
subalgebras of
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
, define a representation of
{\mathcal{A}}\oplus{\mathcal{B}}

on
{\mathcal{H}}_{o}
by

(a,b)\to\begin{pmatrix}a&0\\
0&b\end{pmatrix},

and define a bounded operator,
D_{\omega}
, on
{\mathcal{H}}_{o}
by

D_{\omega}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\omega\\
\omega&0\end{pmatrix}.

Then

[D_{\omega},(a,b)]=\begin{pmatrix}0&\omega b-a\omega\\
\omega a-b\omega&0\end{pmatrix},

so that

\|[D_{\omega},(a,b)]\|=\|a\omega-\omega b\|\vee\|a^{*}\omega-\omega b^{*}\|,

where
\vee
means “maximum”. We have used here the fact that

\omega
is self-adjoint.
Notice that the bridge seminorm
N_{\Pi}
(equation (
17.1
))
of
\Pi
is in general not a
*
-seminorm.
Much as in theorem 6.2 of
[
73
]
, define a
*
-seminorm,
\hat{N}_{\Pi}
,
on
{\mathcal{A}}\oplus{\mathcal{B}}
by

\hat{N}_{\Pi}(a,b)=N_{\Pi}(a,b)\vee N_{\Pi}(a^{*},b^{*}).

Then we see that

\|[D_{\omega},(a,b)]\|=\hat{N}_{\Pi}(a,b).

Of course,
\hat{N}_{\Pi}

agrees with
N_{\Pi}
on self-adjoint elements.

Let
({\mathcal{H}}^{\mathcal{A}},D^{\mathcal{A}})
and
({\mathcal{H}}^{\mathcal{B}},D^{\mathcal{B}})
be Dirac
operators for
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, so that there are nice dense subalgebras

{\mathcal{A}}^{\infty}
and
{\mathcal{B}}^{\infty}
whose elements have bounded
commutators with the Dirac operators. Let
L^{\mathcal{A}}
and

L^{\mathcal{B}}
be the corresponding C*-metrics.
Then for any
r\in{\mathbb{R}}^{+}

we construct a Dirac-like
operator,
D_{r}
, for
{\mathcal{A}}\oplus{\mathcal{B}}
on

{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathcal{A}}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}_{o}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathcal{B}}

given by

D_{r}=D^{\mathcal{A}}\oplus r^{-1}D_{\omega}\oplus D^{\mathcal{B}}.

We let
{\mathcal{A}}\oplus{\mathcal{B}}
act on
{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathcal{A}}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}_{o}\oplus{\mathcal{H}}^{\mathcal{B}}

in the evident way. Then for
a\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\infty}

and
b\in{\mathcal{B}}^{\infty}
we have

[D_{r},(a,b)]=[D^{\mathcal{A}},a]\oplus r^{-1}[D_{\omega},(a,b)]\oplus[D^{\mathcal{B}},b].

Thus

\|[D_{r},(a,b)]\|=L^{\mathcal{A}}(a)\vee r^{-1}\hat{N}_{\Pi}(a,b)\vee L^{\mathcal{B}}(b).

It is not difficult to check that
D_{r}
determines a
C*-metric,
L_{r}
, on
{\mathcal{A}}\oplus{\mathcal{B}}
, and that
L_{r}

is compatible with
L^{\mathcal{A}}

and
L^{\mathcal{B}}
(in the sense
that its quotients on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\infty}
and
{\mathcal{B}}^{\infty}
coincide with
L^{\mathcal{A}}

and
L^{\mathcal{B}}
, as discussed around definition 4.7 of
[
75
]
)
if and only if
r\geq r_{\Pi}
, where
r_{\Pi}
is the reach
of the bridge. (This is proposition 4.8 of
[
75
]
.)
Thus
D_{r}
can be used to bound
the quantum Gromov-Hausdorff distance
(as defined in
[
69
]
)
between the C*-metric spaces
({\mathcal{A}},L^{\mathcal{A}})
and
{\mathcal{B}},L^{\mathcal{B}})
.
In particular,
r_{\Pi}
can be defined as the smallest
r
such that

D_{r}
is compatible with
L^{\mathcal{A}}
and
L^{\mathcal{B}}
. Thus we can determine
the reach of the bridge
\Pi
by considering the
D_{r}
’s. But I do not see
a way of determining the height of
\Pi
directly in terms of the
D_{r}
’s.
For that purpose one seems to need to remember
\omega
and the
subspace
{\mathcal{H}}_{o}
.

18.
Deficiencies

We have given above a somewhat unified construction of Dirac operators
for matrix algebras and homogeneous spaces, in such a way that when
the homogeneous space is the coadjoint orbit of an integral weight vector,
the corresponding sequence of matrix algebras converges to the coadjoint
orbit for Latrémolière’s propinquity. Heuristically, the matrix algebras,
with metric shape given by their Dirac operators, converge for a suitable
quantum Gromov-Hausdorff distance to the coadjoint orbit with metric
shape given by its Dirac operator

There are several deficiencies with this picture, which we discuss here.
The first deficiency is that Dirac operators for Riemannian metrics are usually
defined by means of
the Levi–Civita connection, which is the unique torsion-free connection
compatible with the Riemannian metric. But our construction does not always
yield that Dirac operator.
In essence, we have been using the dual of the “canonical connection”
on the tangent bundle. As is well-known, and explained in section 6 of
[
71
]
,
for homogeneous spaces
the Levi–Civita connection agrees with the canonical connection exactly for
the symmetric spaces. Many coadjoint orbits are not symmetric spaces,
and so for those our approach does not give the usual Dirac operator.
But our approach does give the usual Dirac operator for those coadjoint
orbits that are symmetric spaces, which include the 2-sphere and complex
projective spaces. For non-commutative algebras such as our matrix algebras
it is far from clear how one might define “torsion-free” for connections. (But
see definition 8.8 of
[
30
]
.)

However, we saw in Theorem
14.3
that for the Dirac operators we constructed we have

\|[D,M_{f}]\|=\sup\{\|{\mathrm{d}}f_{x}\|_{{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}}:x\in G\}

for any
f\in{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}=C^{\infty}(G/K)
. Whereas on the last page of
[
71
]

we saw that the same result was obtained when using the Levi–Civita connection
(though phrased in terms of the tangent bundle instead of the cotangent bundle, so

\|{\mathrm{d}}f_{x}\|_{{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime{\mathbb{C}}}}=\|\mathop{\mathrm{grad}}\nolimits_{f}(x)\|
).
In
[
71
]
only the Hodge-Dirac operator was considered, that is, the spinor
bundle was assumed to be
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{m}})
itself. But by arguments elaborating
on the proof of Proposition
5.2
one can see that
\|[D,M_{f}]\|
is
independent of the choice of spinor bundle. The same formula for
\|[D,M_{f}]\|
is
discussed in the comments following proposition 5.10 of
[
72
]
specifically
for the case of homogeneous spaces that can have a
G
-invariant almost complex
structure, which as we saw includes the case of coadjoint orbits.
Now a standard argument (e.g., following definition 9.13 of
[
30
]
) shows that if
we denote by
\rho
the ordinary metric on a Riemannian manifold
N
coming from
its Riemannian metric, then for any two points
p
and
q
of
N
we have

\rho(p,q)=\sup\{|f(p)-f(q)|:\|\mathop{\mathrm{grad}}\nolimits_{f}\|_{\infty}\leq 1\}.

On applying this to either the Dirac operator using the canonical connection,
or the Dirac operator using the Levi–Civita connection
we obtain

(18.1)

\rho(p,q)=\sup\{|f(p)-f(q)|:\|[D,M_{f}]\|\leq 1\}

for
\rho
now the
ordinary metric on
G/K
from our Riemannian metric. Thus as far as the
metric aspects are concerned, these two Dirac operators are equivalent, and
we can recover the ordinary metric from either one.
Consequently, the matrix algebras equipped with their Dirac operators
from our construction do still converge to the coadjoint orbit equipped
with the Dirac operator for the Levi–Civita connection.
Formula (
18.1
)
is the formula on which Connes focused for general Riemannian
manifolds
[
20
,
19
,
21
]
, as it shows that the Dirac operator contains all of
the metric information (and much more) for the manifold. This is his motivation
for advocating that metric data for “non-commutative spaces” be encoded by
providing them with a “Dirac operator”.

A second deficiency of our somewhat unified approach is that coadjoint orbits
actually carry a
G
-invariant Kähler structure, which includes not just a complex
structure
J
, but also closely related symplectic and Riemannian structures.
In the paragraph of
[
14
]
that contains equations 3.53 through 3.59 a proof is
given that the inner product on
{\mathfrak{m}}
(or equivalently on
{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}
) corresponding to
the Riemannian metric of the Kähler structure can not
be extended to a
G
-invariant
inner product on
{\mathfrak{g}}
unless
the coadjoint orbit is a symmetric space. Since the inner products on
{\mathfrak{m}}^{\prime}
that
we have been using in our approach have always been restrictions of
G
-invariant
inner products on
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
, the Dirac operators we have constructed can
not be the ones for the Riemannian metric of the
Kähler structure except if the coadjoint orbit is a symmetric space.
(The results in
[
46
]
do not apply to the Kähler Riemannian metrics for the same
reason, as seen from condition (b) following equation 1.3 of
[
46
]
.
Dirac operators for all the Kähler Riemannian metrics are constructed in
[
72
]
).
In particular, if the coadjoint orbit
corresponding to a highest-weight vector is not a symmetric space,
then the sequence of matrix algebras equipped with the Dirac operators that we
have constructed will not converge to the coadjoint orbit when that orbit is equipped
with the Riemannian metric of the Kähler structure. This follows from Latrémolière’s remarkable
theorem
[
50
]
that if the propinquity between two C*-metric spaces is 0 then
they are isometrically isomorphic.

Thus our general approach works quite well for coadjoint orbits that are symmetric spaces,
and somewhat less well otherwise.

At present it is far from clear to me how one might modify our general approach in
a way that would correct either of these two deficiencies.

At the end of Section
9
we already discussed for the case of
the sphere the relations between our construction of Dirac

for other coadjoint orbits the relations between our construction and
several proposals in the literature.

related to complex projective spaces, using a Schwinger-Fock construction.
The methods are quite different
from those of the present paper, and our discussion for the case of
the sphere given at the end of Section
9
applies here also.
(Since
{\mathbb{C}}P^{n}
is not a spin-manifold when
n
is even, one
does not expect to have a charge-conjugation operator in those cases.)
One of the authors of
[
27
]
says in
[
40
]
that the “formulation”
“seems ill suited to couple the fermions with gauge fields”. In the last
section of
[

on the relevant matrix algebras that seems to be more closely related
to that in the present paper. But that Dirac operator is not explored
there, though some clarification of the notation used there is given
in
[
41
]
(though that paper does not mention matrix algebras).

In
[
1
]
the full flag-manifold for
G=SU(3)
is considered, so the subgroup

K
is the maximal torus of
G
. Then
G/K
is not a symmetric space,
but it is a spin manifold. For the corresponding matrix algebras, twisted

The dimension of
{\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime}
is 8, and so the irreducible representation
of
{\mathbb{C}}\ell({\mathfrak{g}}^{\prime})
has dimension 16. That is the dimension

is not a

It would be interesting
to find a natural framework that would lead to Dirac operators

Constructing matrix-algebra approximations to spheres of

in
[

20.
Spectral Propinquity

As in
[
54
]
, we say that a spectral triple
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{H}},D)
is
metric if the seminorm
L^{D}
on
{\mathcal{A}}
defined by

L^{D}(a)=\|[D,a]\|
is a C*-metric, the issue being whether the
topology it determines on
S({\mathcal{A}})
coincides with the
weak-
*
topology. In definition 4.2 of
[
54
]

(and just before theorem 1.21 of
[
53
]
)
Latrémolière defines a metric on
isomorphism classes of spectral triples, which he calls
the
spectral propinquity
. In this section we will examine
the spectral propinquity for the spectral triples studied in the
earlier sections of this paper. We will not give here a complete
explanation of the general spectral propinquity. We will
give some definitions and proofs, but we refer the reader
to Latrémolière’s papers for more details.

Latrémolière’s spectral propinquity is based on his dual Gromov-Hausdorff
propinquity
[
49
]
,
which is based on “tunnels” instead of “bridges”. We recall
its definition here, but in a restricted simpler form that is sufficient
for our needs. We first recall:

Definition 20.1
.

Let
({\mathcal{D}},L^{\mathcal{D}})
and
({\mathcal{A}},L^{\mathcal{A}})
be compact C*-metric spaces.
By a
quantum isometry
from
({\mathcal{D}},L^{\mathcal{D}})
to
({\mathcal{A}},L^{\mathcal{A}})

we mean a unital
*
-homomorphism,
\pi
, from
{\mathcal{D}}
onto

{\mathcal{A}}
such that
L^{\mathcal{A}}
is the quotient seminorm of
L^{\mathcal{D}}
,
that is, that for all
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
we have

L^{\mathcal{A}}(a)=\inf\{L^{\mathcal{D}}(d):\pi(d)=a\}.

If
\pi
is actually a
*
-isomorphism such that
L^{\mathcal{D}}=L^{\mathcal{A}}\circ\pi
,
then we say that
\pi
is a
full isometry
.

The reason for using the term “isometry” here is that if

\pi
is a quantum isometry then
the corresponding map
\pi^{*}
from
S({\mathcal{A}})
into
S({\mathcal{D}})

is an isometry for the metrics
\rho^{L^{\mathcal{A}}}

and
\rho^{L^{\mathcal{D}}}
,
as already seen in proposition 3.1 of
[
69
]
. If
\pi

is a full isometry, then
\pi^{*}
is an isometry onto

S({\mathcal{D}})
(and conversely).

Definition 20.2
.

Let
({\mathcal{A}},L^{\mathcal{A}})
and
({\mathcal{B}},L^{\mathcal{B}})
be compact C*-metric spaces.
By a
tunnel
from
({\mathcal{A}},L^{\mathcal{A}})
to
({\mathcal{B}},L^{\mathcal{B}})

we mean a compact C*-metric space
({\mathcal{D}},L^{\mathcal{D}})
together with
quantum isometries
\pi^{\mathcal{A}}
and
\pi^{\mathcal{B}}
from
{\mathcal{D}}

onto
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
respectively.

This definition is quite reminiscent of the set-up for the definition of ordinary
Gromov-Hausdorff distance. For our setting in which
{\mathcal{A}}=C(G/K)

and
{\mathcal{B}}^{m}={\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}^{m})
the tunnel we use is
({\mathcal{D}}^{m},L^{{\mathcal{D}}^{m}})

where

{\mathcal{D}}^{m}={\mathcal{A}}\oplus{\mathcal{B}}^{m}

with its evident projections onto
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
, while
L^{{\mathcal{D}}^{m}}

is defined by

(20.1)

L^{{\mathcal{D}}^{m}}(a,b)=L^{\mathcal{A}}(a)\vee L^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}(b)\vee r^{-1}N(a,b)

where
N
is the bridge-norm defined in equation (
17.1
)
using the pivot as defined in equation (
15.1
), and
r

is large enough to ensure that the evident projections onto
{\mathcal{A}}

and
{\mathcal{B}}
are quantum isometries (and
\vee
means “max”).
It is easy to see that this means
exactly that
r\geq r_{\Pi}
where
r_{\Pi}
is the reach of
\Pi

as defined in Definition
15.2
. (We remark that the seminorm
defined by equation (
20.1
) looks just like the
seminorm in the statement of theorem 5.2 of
[
69
]
for the
“bridges” as defined in that paper, and used in definition 6.1
and theorem 6.2 of
[
73
]
. So the terminology of
“bridges” and “tunnels” has gotten a bit scrambled.)

Latrémolière then defines for any tunnel its “extent”, which is a specific way of defining its length. Let
{\mathcal{T}}=({\mathcal{D}},L^{\mathcal{D}},\pi^{\mathcal{A}},\pi^{\mathcal{B}})
be a tunnel from
({\mathcal{A}},L^{\mathcal{A}})
to

({\mathcal{B}},L^{\mathcal{B}})
as in Definition
20.2
. Let
\pi^{\mathcal{A}}_{*}
denote
composition of elements of
S({\mathcal{A}})
with
\pi^{\mathcal{A}}
. Then, as indicated above,

\pi^{\mathcal{A}}_{*}
, is an isometry from
S({\mathcal{A}})
into
S({\mathcal{D}})
. In the same way
we define
\pi^{\mathcal{B}}_{*}
. The extent of
{\mathcal{T}}
,

\mathrm{ext}({\mathcal{T}})
, is defined to be

\mathrm{ext}({\mathcal{T}})=\max\{\mathrm{dist}_{H}(\pi^{\mathcal{A}}_{*}(S({\mathcal{A}})),S({\mathcal{D}})),\ \mathrm{dist}_{H}(\pi^{\mathcal{B}}_{*}(S({\mathcal{B}})),S({\mathcal{D}}))\},

where
\mathrm{dist}_{H}
denotes Hausdorff distance with respect to the
metric on
S({\mathcal{D}})
determined by
L^{\mathcal{D}}
. Latrémolière then defines the “dual propinquity” between
({\mathcal{A}},L^{\mathcal{A}})
and
({\mathcal{B}},L^{\mathcal{B}})
to be the infimum of
the extents of all tunnels from
({\mathcal{A}},L^{\mathcal{A}})
to
({\mathcal{B}},L^{\mathcal{B}})

(with no need for “treks” etc.).
Latrémolière showed in
[
49
]
the remarkable fact that the dual propinquity
is a metric on isometry classes of compact C*-metric spaces, and that
this metric is complete on the space of isometry classes.
He also shows that the propinquity, which we used
in earlier parts of this paper, dominates the dual propinquity, so our earlier
convergence results, such as Theorem
16.7
,
imply convergence for the dual propinquity too.
But the propinquity is probably not complete, though there is no
known counter-example as far as I know.

Latrémolière defines the spectral propinquity between metric spectral triples
of the form
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{H}},D)

in two steps. In the first step,
the Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}
is viewed as a left module over
{\mathcal{A}}
that
is equipped with a suitable seminorm, using
D
.
Latrémolière had earlier
[
51
]
defined
a metric on isometry classes of such modules, which he
called the
modular propinquity
. (It uses “modular tunnels”.)
In the second step
one considers a covariance condition with respect to the
one-parameter group of unitary operators generated by
D
.

In

Definition 20.3
.

We say that
({\mathcal{A}},L^{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{M}},{\mathsf{D}})
is
a
special metrical C*-correspondence
if

({\mathcal{A}},L^{\mathcal{A}})
is a compact C*-metric
space, while
{\mathcal{M}}
is a Hilbert space on which there is a

*
-representation of
{\mathcal{A}}
, and

{\mathsf{D}}
is a norm defined on a dense
subspace
\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{D}})
of
{\mathcal{M}}
, such that

(1)

For all
\xi\in\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{D}})
we have
\|\xi\|\leq{\mathsf{D}}(\xi)
.

(2)

The subset
\{\xi\in\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{D}}):{\mathsf{D}}(\xi)\leq 1\}

of
{\mathcal{M}}
is totally bounded.

(3)

For all

and all
\xi\in\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{D}})
we have

{\mathsf{D}}(a\xi)\leq(\|a\|+L^{\mathcal{A}}(a)){\mathsf{D}}(\xi).

(In particular, if
L^{\mathcal{A}}(a)<\infty
, then
a\xi\in\mathrm{dom}({\mathsf{D}})
.)

For our purposes, the main general example is given by theorem 2.7
of

Theorem 20.4
.

Let
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{H}},D)
be a metrical spectral triple, with

L^{D}(a)=\|[D,a]\|
the corresponding C*-metric on
{\mathcal{A}}
.
Define the norm
{\mathsf{D}}
on
\mathrm{dom}(D)
to be the graph-norm of

D
, that is,

{\mathsf{D}}(\xi)=\|\xi\|+\|D\xi\|.

Then
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{H}},L^{D},{\mathsf{D}})
is a special metrical C*-correspondence.

Accordingly we
let
{\mathsf{D}}^{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathsf{D}}^{m}

be the norms on
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{m}
defined in terms of
D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}
and
D_{o}^{m}
much as in Theorem
20.4
.
We want to
show that the special metrical C*-correspondences

({\mathcal{B}}^{m},L^{D_{o}^{m}},{\mathcal{S}}^{m},{\mathsf{D}}^{m})

converge to the special metrical C*-correspondence

({\mathcal{A}},L^{D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}},{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}},{\mathsf{D}}^{\mathcal{A}})

for the modular propinquity.

The definition of the modular propinquity involves a definition of what is meant by
a tunnel between two metrical C*-correspondences. (Latrémolière calls these
“modular tunnels”). We will not give the general definition. Instead we
now begin constructing the modular tunnels we need. We start with the
core of the construction. To some extent we follow the steps that Latrémolière used to construct
the corresponding modular tunnels for the case of non-commutative tori
in section 3.3 of
[
53
]
. We use the notation of Section
1

as well as that following Definition
20.2
.

Recall that
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{m}={\mathcal{B}}^{m}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
, dense
subspaces of the corresponding Hilbert spaces.
We let

\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}=\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}_{m}\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}}
and

\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}=\sigma^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}}
, where
\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}_{m}
and

\sigma^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}
are as defined in the paragraph preceding Proposition
16.1
. So
\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}
maps
{\mathcal{S}}^{m}
to
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}
,
while
\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}

maps
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}
to
{\mathcal{S}}^{m}
.
Since
\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}_{m}
and

\sigma^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}
intertwine the actions
\alpha
of
G
on
{\mathcal{A}}
(and so on
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}
)
and
{\mathcal{B}}^{m}
, as mentioned just before
Definition

,
\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}
and
\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}
intertwine
the actions
\alpha\otimes I^{\mathcal{S}}
of
G
on
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}

and
{\mathcal{B}}^{m}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
.
From formula
1.1
it follows that

D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}=\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}D_{o}^{m}
and

D_{o}^{m}\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}=\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}
.
It is easily seen that
\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}_{m}
has operator norm 1
(because we are essentially using normalized traces on

{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}^{m}
). Since
\sigma^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}
is easily seen to be the Hilbert-space
adjoint of
\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}_{m}
(as shown early in section 2 of
[

]
),

\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}
is the Hilbert-space
adjoint of
\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}
, and they both have operator norm 1 (i.e.
are contractions of norm 1) for the Hilbert-space norms
on
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{m}
.

The following proposition is the analog of much of theorem 3.25 of
[
53
]
. Notice that it does not explicitly involve the C*-algebras

{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}^{m}
, just as theorem 3.25 does not involve the C*-algebras

{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
and
{\mathcal{A}}_{\infty}
of
[
53
]
.

Proposition 20.5
.

With notation as above, let
{\mathcal{S}}_{t}={\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}\oplus{\mathcal{S}}^{m}
, with its evident
pre-Hilbert-space structure. For any
\epsilon>0
define
a norm
{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}
(for “tunnel norm”)
on
{\mathcal{S}}_{t}
by

(20.2)

{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}(\xi,\eta)={\mathsf{D}}^{\mathcal{A}}(\xi)\vee{\mathsf{D}}^{m}(\eta)\vee(1/\epsilon)\|\xi-\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}(\eta)\|

for
(\xi,\eta)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{t}
. For any
\epsilon>0

there is a natural number
N
such that if
m\geq N
then

{\mathsf{D}}^{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathsf{D}}^{m}
coincide with the quotient norms of
{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}
on

{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{m}
for the evident projections from
{\mathcal{S}}_{t}

onto
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}^{m}
.

Proof.

Let
\eta\in{\mathcal{S}}^{m}
be given. Choose
\xi\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}
to be

\xi=\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}\eta
, so that the third term in the definition
of
{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}
is 0. Thus to show that the quotient norm of
{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}

on
{\mathcal{S}}^{m}
is
{\mathsf{D}}^{m}
we only need to show that

{\mathsf{D}}^{\mathcal{A}}(\xi)\leq{\mathsf{D}}^{m}(\eta)
. But this follows immediately
from the facts that
\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}
is of norm 1 and

D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}=\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}D_{o}^{m}
as seen above.
This part of the proof is independent of any choice of
\epsilon
.

Let
\epsilon>0
be given, and let
\xi\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}
be given.
Choose
\eta\in{\mathcal{S}}^{m}
to be

\eta=\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\xi
. Then
{\mathsf{D}}^{m}(\eta)\leq{\mathsf{D}}^{\mathcal{A}}(\xi)
because

\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}
is of norm 1 and

D_{o}^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}=\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}
as seen above.
Thus we need to show that there is an
N
such that if
m\geq N

then
(1/\epsilon)\|\xi-\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}(\eta)\|\leq{\mathsf{D}}^{\mathcal{A}}(\xi)
, that is,

(20.3)

\|\xi-\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}(\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\xi)\|\leq\epsilon(\|\xi\|+\|D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}\xi\|).

Notice that since
\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}
and
\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}
have norm 1, the
left-hand side of equation
(
20.3
)
is
\leq 2\|\xi\|
. Thus
equation
(
20.3
)
will be satisfied if

2\|\xi\|\leq\epsilon(\|\xi\|+\|D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}\xi\|).

Suppose now that
\xi
is an
eigenvector for
D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}
with eigenvalue
\lambda
, so that the right-hand
side of equation (
20.3
) is
\epsilon(1+|\lambda|)\|\xi\|
. It is then clear that
equation (
20.3
) is satisfied if
|\lambda|\geq 2/\epsilon
.

In Proposition
1.4
we saw that there is a basis for
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}

consisting of eigenvectors of
D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}
. As mentioned there, the
eigenvalues of
D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}
, counted with multiplicity, converge in
absolute value to
\infty
. (A somewhat indirect
proof of this fact is given in theorem 5.5 of
[
29
]
, whose method
is to compare
D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}
to the usual Dirac operator on
G
itself, which
is handled by the usual methods for Dirac operators on compact
manifolds, as presented for example in section 4.2 of
[
28
]
.) Accordingly,
the span of the eigenspaces of
D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}
for eigenvalues
\lambda
for which

|\lambda|\leq 2/\epsilon
, is finite-dimensional. We denote this span by
{\mathcal{K}}
.

Then for
{\mathcal{K}}^{\perp}
we can choose an orthonormal basis,
\{\xi_{j}\}
,
consisting of eigenvectors of
D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}
, and for each
j
the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}

for
\xi_{j}
will satisfy
|\lambda_{j}|>2/\epsilon
. If
\xi\in{\mathcal{K}}^{\perp}
is in the domain of

D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}
so that for its expansion
\xi=\sum z_{j}\xi_{j}
(with
z_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}
)
the sequence
\{|z_{j}\lambda_{j}|\}
is square-summable, we have

\|D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}\xi\|^{2}=\sum|z_{j}|^{2}|\lambda_{j}|^{2}\geq(\sum|z_{j}|^{2})(2/\epsilon)^{2}=(2/\epsilon)^{2}\|\xi\|^{2}.

Thus
\|\xi\|\leq(\epsilon/2)\|D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}\xi\|
, so that

\|\xi-\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}(\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\xi)\|\leq\epsilon(\|\xi\|+\|D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}\xi\|)
,
as desired.

Finally, let
\xi
be an element of
{\mathcal{K}}
.
In proposition 4.13 of
[
77
]
Sain proves, in the more general context
of compact quantum groups, that (for our notation above)

\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}_{m}(\sigma^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}(a))
converges in norm to
a

for every
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
as
m
goes to
\infty
. (The proof is not difficult.)
It follows that
\sigma^{\mathcal{A}}_{m}(\sigma^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}(a))
converges to
a

with respect to the Hilbert-space norm on
L^{2}({\mathcal{A}},\tau)
,
and from this it follows that

\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}(\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\xi)
converges to
\xi

for each
\xi
in
{\mathcal{K}}
(since
{\mathcal{K}}
is contained in
the algebraic tensor product
{\mathcal{A}^{\infty}}\otimes{\mathcal{S}}
as follows from
Proposition
1.4
). Since
{\mathcal{K}}
is finite-dimensional, it
follows that we can find a natural number
N
such that
for every
m\geq N
and every
\xi\in{\mathcal{K}}
we have

(20.4)

\|\xi-\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}(\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\xi)\|\leq\epsilon\|\xi\|\leq\epsilon(\|\xi\|+\|D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}\xi\|).

Putting together the steps above, we find that if
m\geq N
then

{\mathsf{D}}^{\mathcal{A}}
coincides with the quotient norm of
{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}
on

{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}
, as needed.
∎

We now put the above result into the framework
that Latrémolière uses in
[
53
]
to treat the spectral propinquity
for Dirac operators on quantum tori. We follow closely the
pattern around theorem 3.25 of
[
53
]
.

To begin with, according to the proof of the triangle
inequality for the dual modular
propinquity given in theorem 3.1 of
[
52
]
,
and as done for non-commutative tori in theorem 3.25 of
[
53
]
,
we must view
{\mathcal{S}}_{t}={\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}\oplus{\mathcal{S}}^{m}

as a Hilbert module over the 2-dimensional C*-algebra

{\mathcal{C}}={\mathbb{C}}\oplus{\mathbb{C}}
(i.e. the algebra of functions on
a 2-point space).
This means that we view
{\mathcal{S}}_{t}
as a module over
{\mathcal{C}}
in the
evident way, and that we define a
{\mathcal{C}}
-valued inner product
on
{\mathcal{S}}_{t}
by

\langle(\xi,\eta),(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})\rangle_{\mathcal{C}}=(\langle\xi,\xi^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}},\langle\eta,\eta^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{m}}).

For each
\epsilon>0
as used in the above
proposition, we define a C*-metric,
Q_{\epsilon}
, on
{\mathcal{C}}

by
Q_{\epsilon}(z,w)=(1/\epsilon)|z-w|
(so the distance between the two points
is
\epsilon
). This makes
{\mathcal{C}}
into a compact quantum
metric space. Then we have the evident projections of
{\mathcal{C}}

onto the two copies of
{\mathbb{C}}
(one-point spaces with trivial
metric), so that
({\mathcal{C}},Q_{\epsilon})
is a tunnel between these two one-point
spaces.

Altogether the above structures, with the properties we have
obtained, form a modular tunnel
between the special metrical C*-correspondences

({\mathcal{B}}^{m},L^{D_{o}^{m}},{\mathcal{S}}^{m},{\mathsf{D}}^{m})
and

({\mathcal{A}},L^{D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}},{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}},{\mathsf{D}}^{\mathcal{A}})

for large enough
m
,
except that there is one further
property that must be verified, namely what Latrémolière calls
the “inner Leibniz property”. This requires that

Q_{\epsilon}((\xi,\eta),(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime}))\leq 2{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}((\xi,\eta)){\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}((\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})).

For this we may need to make the natural number
N

of Proposition
20.5
somewhat larger.
Specifically, we replace
\epsilon
with
\epsilon/2
and
choose
N
large enough that in
equation (
20.2
) we have
1/\epsilon
replaced by
2/\epsilon
.
In particular, we have

\|\xi-\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta\|\leq(\epsilon/2)TN((\xi,\eta))

for all
(\xi,\eta)\in{\mathcal{S}}_{t}
, and
similarly for
(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})
.
Then

\displaystyle|\langle\xi,\xi^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}}-\langle\eta,\eta^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{m}}|

\displaystyle\leq|\langle\xi,\xi^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}}-\langle\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta,\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}}|+|\langle\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta,\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}}-\langle\eta,\eta^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{m}}|,

and, for the first term,

\displaystyle|\langle\xi,\xi^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}}-\langle\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta,\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}}|

\displaystyle\leq|\langle\xi-\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta,\xi^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}}|+|\langle\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta,\xi^{\prime}-\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}}|

\displaystyle\leq\|\xi-\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta\|\|\xi^{\prime}\|+\|\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta\|\|\xi^{\prime}-\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta^{\prime}\|

\displaystyle\leq(\epsilon/2){\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}(\xi,\eta){\mathsf{D}}^{\mathcal{A}}(\xi^{\prime})+{\mathsf{D}}^{m}(\eta)(\epsilon/2){\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}(\xi,\eta){\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime}),

while for the second term, since
\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}
is the
adjoint of
\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}
,

\displaystyle|\langle\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta,\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}}-\langle\eta,\eta^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{m}}|=|\langle\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta,\eta^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}}-\langle\eta,\eta^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{m}}|

\displaystyle=|\langle\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta-\eta,\eta^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}}|\leq\|\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta-\eta\|\|\eta^{\prime}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta-\eta\|{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime}).

To handle the term
\|\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta-\eta\|

we argue much as we did to obtain equation (
20.3
). We
seek to show that

(20.5)

\|\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta-\eta\|\leq\epsilon(\|\eta\|+\|D_{o}^{m}\eta\|).

(so that
\|\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta-\eta\|\leq\epsilon{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}(\xi,\eta)
).
The left-hand side of equation (
20.5
) is
\leq 2\|\eta\|
. Thus
equation (
20.5
) will be satisfied if

2\|\eta\|\leq\epsilon(\|\eta\|+\|D_{o}^{m}\eta\|).

Suppose now that
\eta
is an
eigenvector for
D_{o}^{m}
with eigenvalue
\lambda
, so that the right-hand
side of equation (
20.5
) is
\epsilon(1+|\lambda|)\|\eta\|
. It is then clear that
equation (
20.5
) is satisfied if
|\lambda|\geq 2/\epsilon
.
Let
{\mathcal{K}}_{m}
be the span of the eigenspaces of
D_{o}^{m}
for
eigenvalues
\lambda
for which
|\lambda|\leq 2/\epsilon
. Then we can argue exactly
as in the fourth paragraph of the proof of Proposition
20.5

to conclude that if
\eta\in{\mathcal{K}}_{m}^{\perp}
then

\|\eta\|\leq(\epsilon/2)\|D_{o}^{m}\eta\|
, so that

\|\eta-\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}(\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}(\eta))\|\leq\epsilon(\|\eta\|+\|D_{o}^{m}\eta\|)
.

To treat
{\mathcal{K}}_{m}
we recall first that
\sigma_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}
is always injective.
(This follows immediately from theorem 3.1 of
[
69
]
, for which it is
crucial that the range of
P^{m}
is spanned by a
highest
-weight
vector.) Thus
\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}
is injective. Furthermore, from Proposition

5.4
we see that

\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}
carries eigenvectors of
D_{o}^{m}
to eigenvectors of

D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}
with the same eigenvalue (but maybe of different norm), so that

\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}
carries
{\mathcal{K}}_{m}
into
{\mathcal{K}}
.
We now use the arguments in section 4 of

[
77
]
. As long as
\epsilon<1/2
, which we now assume (increasing
N

if necessary), equation (
20.4
) implies that
\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}
,
as an operator on
{\mathcal{K}}
, satisfies

\|I_{\mathcal{K}}-\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\|<1/2
,
so that
\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}\theta^{\beta^{m}}
is invertible
and
\|(\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}})^{-1}\|<2
.
In particular,
\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}
is onto
{\mathcal{K}}
, and so is invertible
as an operator from
{\mathcal{K}}_{m}
onto
{\mathcal{K}}
. Consequently,

\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}
is invertible as an operator from
{\mathcal{K}}

to
{\mathcal{K}}_{m}
, and

(\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}})^{-1}=(\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}\theta^{\beta^{m}})^{-1}\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}
,
so that
\|(\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}})^{-1}\|<2
.
Since

I_{{\mathcal{K}}_{m}}-\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}=\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}(I_{{\mathcal{K}}_{m}}-\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}})(\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}})^{-1},

we see, using equation (
20.4
) and our replacement of
\epsilon

by
\epsilon/2
, that

\|I_{{\mathcal{K}}_{m}}-\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\|\leq 2\|I_{{\mathcal{K}}_{m}}-\theta_{m}^{\mathcal{A}}\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\|\leq\epsilon

for all
m\geq N
. Thus

\|\eta-\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta\|\leq\epsilon\|\eta\|\leq\epsilon{\mathsf{D}}^{m}(\eta)

for all
\eta\in{\mathcal{K}}_{m}
. Putting this together with the result of
the previous paragraph, we obtain inequality (
20.5
),
so that
\|\theta^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}}\theta^{{\mathcal{A}}}_{m}\eta-\eta\|\leq\epsilon{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}(\xi,\eta)
.
Putting this together with the several inequalities obtained before
inequality (
20.5
), we obtain

|\langle\xi,\xi^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}}-\langle\eta,\eta^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{m}}|\leq 2\epsilon{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}(\xi,\eta){\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime}),

so that

Q_{\epsilon}((\xi,\eta),(\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime}))=(1/\epsilon)|\langle\xi,\xi^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}}}-\langle\eta,\eta^{\prime}\rangle_{{\mathcal{S}}^{m}}|\leq 2{\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}((\xi,\eta)){\mathsf{T}}{\mathsf{N}}((\xi^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})),

as needed.

When we apply the definition of the “extent” of a modular tunnel,
as given in definition 4.2 of
[
52
]
, to the
modular tunnel sketched above, the extent of this
modular tunnel is the extent of the tunnel

({\mathcal{C}},Q_{\epsilon})
, which is
\epsilon
(just as in the case
for the non-commutative tori, for which see the last paragraph
in the proof of theorem 3.25 of
[

]
).

For metrical C*-correspondences the modular propinquity
between them is defined to be the infimum of the extents
of all modular tunnels between them.
For the special metrical C*-correspondences corresponding
to the spectral triples
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}},D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}})
and

({\mathcal{B}}^{m},{\mathcal{S}}^{m},D_{o}^{m})
, the tunnels constructed
in Proposition
20.5
and the following discussion
show that the sequence

\{({\mathcal{B}}^{m},L^{D_{o}^{m}},{\mathcal{S}}^{m},{\mathsf{D}}^{m})\}
of
special metrical C*-correspondences converges
to the special metrical C*-correspondence

({\mathcal{A}},L^{D_{o}^{\mathcal{A}}},{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}},{\mathsf{D}}^{\mathcal{A}})

for the dual modular propinquity.

In theorem 4.8 of
[
52

Latrémolière proves the remarkable
fact that if the dual modular propinquity between two
metrical C*-correspondences is 0
(using a change of terminology he made in later papers),
then these two metrical C*-correspondences are fully
isometric, meaning unitary equivalent in an appropriate sense.
The application of this to spectral triples is given in proposition
2.25 of
[
54
]
.
For
{\mathcal{A}}=C(G/K)
as usual, and with
D
being the Dirac operator
defined in Definition
14.1
, acting on
{\mathcal{S}}(G/K)
,
it is easily seen that the special metrical C*-correspondence
corresponding to the spectral triple
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{S}}(G/K),D)
,
as defined in Theorem
20.4
,
is not unitarily equivalent to the special metrical C*-correspondence
corresponding to the spectral triple

({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}},D^{\mathcal{A}}_{o})
. Consequently the sequence of
spectral triples
\{({\mathcal{B}}^{m},{\mathcal{S}}^{m},D_{o}^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}})\}

can not converge to the spectral triple
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{S}}(G/K),D)

for the spectral propinquity. Recall from
Definition
8.1
that when the unitary
representation
({\mathcal{H}},U)
is faithful on
{\mathfrak{g}}
the Dirac operators
for the corresponding matrix algebras coincide
with the
D_{o}^{m}
’s
(and when the unitary representation is not faithful, the corresponding
Dirac operators are close to that).

I expect that when the covariance condition in the definition of the spectral propinquity, as described in section 3 of
[
54
]
, is taken into account,
it will be seen that the spectral triples
\{({\mathcal{B}}^{m},{\mathcal{S}}^{m},D_{o}^{{\mathcal{B}}^{m}})\}

do converge to the spectral triple
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathcal{A}},D^{\mathcal{A}}_{o})
for the spectral
propinquity. But I have not checked that.
