Abstract
We provide four case studies that use Bayesian machinery to making inductive reasoning. Our main motivation relies in offering several instances where the Bayesian approach to data analysis is exploited at its best to perform complex tasks, such as description, testing, estimation, and prediction. This work is not meant to be either a reference text or a survey in Bayesian statistical inference. Our goal is simply to provide several examples that use Bayesian methodology to solve data-driven problems. The topics we cover here, include problems in Bayesian nonparametrics, Bayesian analysis of times series, and Bayesian analysis of spatial data.



Keywords:
Bayesian Models. Gibbs Sampler. Hierarchical Models. Markov chain Monte Carlo. Statistical Inference.

1
Introduction

In this manuscript, we provide four case studies that use Bayesian machinery to making inductive reasoning. Our main motivation relies in offering several instances where the Bayesian approach to data analysis is exploited at its best to perform complex tasks, such as description (probabilistic summary of main data patterns), testing (evaluation of competing theories of data formation), estimation (evaluation of parameters in a presumed model), and prediction (forecast of missing or future observations).

We focus on model-based approaches to perform statistical inference. In particular, our case studies are totally based on Bayesian statistics. A statistical model fully describes the data generating process under which a given dataset might have arisen. Thus, the vector of observations
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
is assumed to be generated by an unknown probability distribution
P
with probability density/mass function
p
. Therefore, we have that all information in the data is contained in
P
.

Furthermore, in Bayesian statistics we treat the model parameters that index
P
,
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots)\in\Theta
, as random variables, which in turn are assigned a prior distribution
\Pi
with probability density/mass function
p
(even though it is an abuse of mathematical notation, since the probability density/mass function associated with
P
is also denoted with
p
). The prior distribution captures the uncertainty of the researcher about the value of
\bm{\theta}
before observing
\bm{y}
(another way to think about the prior is as summarizing all information about
\bm{\theta}
that is external to
\bm{y}
).

If we use probability theory to express all forms of uncertainty associated with our statistical model
\mathcal{M}=(p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\theta}),p(\bm{\theta}))
, then Bayes theorem can base used to update our knowledge about
\bm{\theta}
, through the posterior distribution

p(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{y})\propto\frac{p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\theta})\,p(\bm{\theta})}{\int_{\Theta}p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\theta})\,p(\bm{\theta})\,\text{d}\bm{\theta}}\,.

The posterior combines information in the data with any other information external to it that has been encoded in the prior. Again, we slightly abuse notation by using
p
to denote the distribution of
\bm{y}\mid\bm{\theta}
as well as that of
\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{y}
.

The Bayesian approach is convenient for several reasons. First of all, in a Bayesian setting all forms of uncertainty quantification are granted. Moreover, it is natural to think about hierarchies, and therefore, borrowing of information (hierarchical models are used in non-Bayesian approaches, but they are not as natural). Also, with the advent of Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) and other simulation-based approaches, computation has become in some ways easier than computation for non-Bayesian approaches (particularly for complex models). Lastly, since both observations and parameters are random variables, dealing with missing and censored data is simpler.

This work is not meant to be either a reference text or a survey in Bayesian statistical inference. Our goal is simply to provide several examples that use Bayesian methodology to solve data-driven problems. Therefore, we expect that the reader has a working understating of Bayesian methods for model building and computation. For a deep treatment of Bayesian data analysis methods, we refer the reader to
Gelman et al., 2014
,
Robert, 2007
,
Jackman, 2009
, among many others. Finally, the topics we cover here, include problems in Bayesian nonparametrics (e.g.,
Müller et al., 2015
), Bayesian analysis of times series (e.g.,
Prado and West, 2010
), and Bayesian analysis of spatial data (e.g.,
Banerjee et al., 2014
).

The rest of the document is structured as follows: Section
2
presents an example using synthetic data in the context of nonparamteric regression; Section
3
offers the analysis of a times series using dynamic linear models; Section
4
provides an illustration of an space-time model for areal unit data; Section
5
exhibits a case regarding species sampling models; finally, Section
6
gives a brief discussion.

2
Nonparametric regression modeling

Consider the Gaussian process (GP) regression setting (e.g.,
Taddy and Kottas, 2010
) with a single continuous covariate,

y_{i}=f(x_{i})+\epsilon_{i}\,,\qquad i=1,\ldots,n\,,

(1)

where
y_{i}
and
x_{i}
are the response and covariate observations, respectively,
\epsilon_{i}
are independent and identically distributed (iid) random errors from a
\textsf{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
distribution, and
f(\cdot)
is the regression function, which is assigned a GP prior with constant mean function
\textsf{E}(f(x)\mid\mu)=\mu
, and power exponential covariance function
\textsf{Cov}(f(x),f(x^{\prime})\mid\tau,\phi)=\tau^{2}\exp{\left\{-\phi|x-x^{\prime}|^{\alpha}\right\}}
, with unknown
\tau>0
and
\phi>0
, but fixed
0<\alpha\leq 2
(the special cases for
\alpha=1
and
\alpha=2
correspond to the Exponential and Gaussian covariance functions, respectively). Here,
\alpha
defines the smoothness of the function,
\tau^{2}
determines the amplitude of oscillation, and
\phi
delimits the volatility of orientation. Lastly, recall that based on the GP definition, for any collection of index points
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
, the random vector
(x_{t_{1}},\ldots,x_{t_{n}})
follows an
n
-variate Normal distribution with mean vector
(\mu(t_{1}),\ldots,\mu(t_{n}))
, and positive definite covariance matrix whose
(i,j)
-th element is given by
\textsf{Cov}(x_{t_{i}},x_{t_{j}})=\tau^{2}\exp{\left\{-\phi|t_{i}-t_{j}|^{\alpha}\right\}}
.

In order to perform full Bayesian inference under this setting, we consider the following independent prior distributions for the remaining model parameters:

\sigma^{2}\sim\textsf{IG}(a_{\sigma},b_{\sigma})\,,\qquad\mu\sim\textsf{N}(a_{\mu},b_{\mu})\,,\qquad\tau^{2}\sim\textsf{IG}(a_{\tau},b_{\tau})\,,\qquad\phi\sim\textsf{U}(a_{\phi},b_{\phi})\,.

Note that by letting
\bm{\theta}=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
, with
\theta_{i}=f(x_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, denote the unknown function values, it follows that
\bm{y}\mid\bm{\theta},\sigma^{2}\sim\textsf{N}_{n}(\bm{\theta},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})
and
\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\mu},\mathbf{H}\sim\textsf{N}_{n}(\bm{\mu},\mathbf{H})
, with
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
,
\bm{\mu}=\mu\mathbf{1}
and
\mathbf{H}=\tau^{2}\mathbf{C}
, where
\mathbf{C}=\left[\exp{\left\{-\phi|x_{i}-x_{j}|^{\alpha}\right\}}\right]
, which means that once
\bm{\theta}
has been integrated out
\bm{y}\mid\bm{\mu},\sigma^{2},\mathbf{H}\sim\textsf{N}_{n}(\bm{\mu},\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H})
.

Now, we provide a Gibbs sampling algorithm designed to draw samples from the posterior distribution

p(\mathbf{\Upsilon}\mid\bm{y})\propto p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\theta},\sigma^{2})\,p(\bm{\theta}\mid\bm{\mu},\mathbf{H})\,p(\mu)\,p(\sigma^{2})\,(\tau^{2})\,p(\phi)\,,

where
\mathbf{\Upsilon}=(\bm{\theta},\sigma^{2},\mu,\tau^{2},\phi)
. Let
\mathbf{\Upsilon}^{(b)}
be the vector of model parameters at iteration
b
of the algorithm, for
b=1,\ldots,B
. Given a starting point
\mathbf{\Upsilon}^{(0)}
, we consider a Gibbs sampler (with a Metropolis step) updating
\mathbf{\Upsilon}^{(b-1)}
to
\mathbf{\Upsilon}^{(b)}
until convergence as follows:

1.

Sample
\bm{\theta}\mid\sigma^{2},\mu,\tau^{2},\phi,\bm{y}\sim\textsf{N}_{n}(\mathbf{m}_{\bm{\theta}},\mathbf{V}_{\bm{\theta}})
, with

\mathbf{V}_{\bm{\theta}}=\left(\sigma^{-2}\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{H}^{-1}\right)^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{m}_{\bm{\theta}}=\mathbf{V}_{\bm{\theta}}\left(\sigma^{-2}\bm{y}+\mathbf{H}^{-1}\bm{\mu}\right)\,.

2.

Sample
\sigma^{2}\mid\bm{\theta}\sim\textsf{IG}\left(a_{\sigma}+\frac{n}{2},b_{\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_{i}-\theta_{i})^{2}\right)
.

3.

Sample
\mu\mid\bm{\theta},\tau^{2},\phi\sim\textsf{N}(m_{\mu},V_{\mu})
, with

V_{\mu}=(b_{\mu}^{-1}+\mathbf{1}^{\textsf{T}}\,\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{1})^{-1}\qquad\text{and}\qquad m_{\mu}=V_{\mu}\left[a_{\mu}b_{\mu}^{-1}+\mathbf{1}^{\textsf{T}}\,\mathbf{H}^{-1}\bm{\theta}\right]\,.

4.

Sample
\tau^{2}\mid\bm{\theta},\mu,\phi\sim\textsf{IG}\left(a_{\tau}+\frac{n}{2},b_{\tau}+\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\mu})^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{C}^{-1}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\mu})\right)
.

5.

Sample
\phi
using a Metropolis step:

a.

Set
\eta=\text{logit}((\phi-a_{\phi})/(b_{\phi}-a_{\phi}))
.

b.

Sample
\eta^{*}\sim\textsf{N}(\eta,\delta)
, with
\delta
a tuning parameter.

c.

Compute
r=\exp{\left\{\log p_{\eta}(\eta^{*}\mid\bm{\theta},\mu,\tau^{2})-\log p_{\eta}(\eta\mid\bm{\theta},\mu,\tau^{2})\right\}}
, with

\log p_{\eta}(x\mid\bm{\theta},\mu,\tau^{2})=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\log|\mathbf{H}^{-1}|-(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\mu})^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{H}^{-1}(\bm{\theta}-\bm{\mu})\right)+x-2\log(1+e^{x})+k\,,

where
k
is a constant.

d.

Update
\phi
to
\phi^{*}=(b_{\phi}-a_{\phi})\text{expit}(\eta^{*})+a_{\phi}
with probability
r
.

Now, in order to test the previous algorithm, we generate synthetic data according to model (
1
) with
\sigma=0.2
,
x_{i}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny iid}}}{\sim}}\textsf{U}(-3,3)
, for
i=1,\ldots,100
, and the true regression function
f(x)=0.3+0.4x+0.5\sin(2.7x)+\frac{1.1}{1+x^{2}}
(this example is included in a technical report by Radford Neal, which is available on-line from
http://www.cs.toronto.edu/˜radford/mc-gp.abstract.html
). Panel (a) in Figure
1
shows the simulated dataset.
Here, we adopt an empirical Bayes approach by setting
a_{\mu}=\bar{y}
,
a_{\sigma}=a_{\tau}=2
,
b_{\mu}=b_{\sigma}=b_{\tau}=s^{2}_{y}/3
, since marginally
\textsf{E}(y_{i})=a_{\mu}=\bar{y}
and
\textsf{Var}(y_{i})=b_{\mu}+b_{\sigma}+b_{\tau}=s^{2}_{y}
; and also,
a_{\phi}=0.1
and
b_{\phi}=10
, which let
\phi
vary across a wide range of values. Thus, we fit the model with
\alpha=1
and run the algorithm choosing
\delta
(step 5.) carefully to produce a reasonable average mixing rate between 30% and 50%.

3.1
Autoregressive model

As a naive analysis of the series, we consider a model of the form

y_{t}=\phi_{1}y_{t-1}+\ldots+\phi_{p}y_{t-p}+\epsilon_{t}\,,\qquad\epsilon_{t}\mid v\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny iid}}}{\sim}}\textsf{N}(0,\upsilon)\,,

(2)

which corresponds to an autoregressive model of order
p
,
\textsf{AR}(p)
. We fit this model to the data using a reference prior distribution of the form
p(\bm{\phi},\upsilon)\propto 1/\upsilon
, with
\bm{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{p})
, and the conditional likelihood

p(\bm{y}\mid y_{1},\ldots,y_{p},\bm{\phi},\upsilon)=\prod_{t=p+1}^{T}\textsf{N}(y_{t}\mid\mathbf{f}_{t}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{\phi},\upsilon)=\textsf{N}_{T-p}(\bm{y}\mid\mathbf{F}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{\phi},\upsilon\mathbf{I})

(3)

where
\bm{y}=(y_{p+1},\ldots,y_{T})
since the first
p
observations are considered as initial values,
\mathbf{f}_{t}=(y_{t-1},\ldots,y_{t-p})
for
t=p+1,\ldots,T
, and
\mathbf{F}=[\mathbf{f}_{p+1},\ldots,\mathbf{f}_{T}]
. This formulation corresponds to a regular linear model setting, and therefore, we can apply standard theory
(
Prado and West, 2010
, pp. 19-22)
.

Combining the likelihood (
3
) with the prior distribution
p(\bm{\phi},\upsilon)
, we can easily obtain samples from the posterior distribution
p(\bm{\phi},\upsilon\mid\bm{y})
using direct sampling as follows: first, sample
\upsilon
from
\textsf{IG}((n-p)/2,(n-p)\hat{\upsilon}^{2}/2)
, and then, for each
\upsilon
in the previous step sample
\bm{\phi}
from a
\textsf{N}_{p}(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\text{mle}},\upsilon\,(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{\textsf{T}})^{-1})
. Using
p=3
, it follows that
n=T-p=597
,
(n-p)\hat{\upsilon}^{2}=7.6759
is the residual sum of squares, which in turn leads to
\hat{v}_{\text{mle}}=0.0128
the MLE of
v
, and
\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{\text{mle}}=(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{F}^{\textsf{T}})^{-1}\mathbf{F}\bm{y}=(0.2283,0.0018,0.1146)
is the MLE of
\bm{\phi}
. Posterior summaries of 25000 samples from
p(\bm{\phi},\upsilon\mid\bm{y})
are provided in Table
2
. We see that the posterior parameter estimates are very similar to their MLE counterpart.

3.2
Dynamic linear model

Now, going forward in the analysis, we consider a popular model given in
(
Tsay, 2010
, p. 561)
for detecting outliers:

\displaystyle y_{t}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{t}\alpha_{t}+x_{t}+\epsilon_{t}\,,\quad\gamma_{t}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny iid}}}{\sim}}\textsf{Ber}(0.2)\,,\quad\alpha_{t}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny iid}}}{\sim}}\textsf{N}(0,0.1)\,,\quad\epsilon_{t}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny iid}}}{\sim}}\textsf{N}(0,0.01)\,,

(4)

\displaystyle x_{t}

\displaystyle=\phi_{1}x_{t-1}+\ldots+\phi_{p}x_{t-p}+\eta_{t}\,,\quad\eta_{t}\mid\omega\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny iid}}}{\sim}}\textsf{N}(0,\omega)\,.

Under this formulation,
\gamma_{t}
denotes the presence or absence of an additive outlier at time
t
, and
\alpha_{t}
its corresponding magnitude when present, and. Thus, additive outliers are allowed to occur at every time point with a probability equal to 0.2. Furthermore,
x_{t}
defines an autoregressive process with order
p
. Then, we have
3T+p+1
model parameters, namely,
\bm{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{T})
,
\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{T})
,
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{T})
,
\bm{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{p})
, and
\omega
. Also, we also assume the conjugate priors
\phi_{j}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny iid}}}{\sim}}\textsf{N}(a_{\phi},b_{\phi})
, for
j=1,\ldots,p
, and
\omega\sim\textsf{IG}(a_{\omega},b_{\omega})
.

Note that model (
4
) is a non-Gaussian dynamic linear model (DLM). However, conditional on the outlier indicators
\gamma_{t}
, this model can be written as a standard DLM. For instance, with
p=3
the model aquires the form:

\displaystyle y_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{\theta}_{t}+\nu_{t}\,,\qquad\nu_{t}\mid V_{t}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny ind}}}{\sim}}\textsf{N}(0,V_{t})\,,

(5)

\displaystyle\bm{\theta}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{G}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}+\bm{\omega}_{t}\,,\qquad\bm{\omega}_{t}\mid\mathbf{W}_{t}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny ind}}}{\sim}}\textsf{N}_{p}(\bm{0},\mathbf{W}_{t})\,,

with

\mathbf{F}=(1,0,0)\,,\qquad\bm{\theta}_{t}=(x_{t},x_{t-1},x_{t-2})\,,\qquad V_{t}=v_{\gamma_{t}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0.01,&\hbox{$\gamma_{t}=0$;}\\
0.11,&\hbox{$\gamma_{t}=1$,}\end{array}\right.

and

\mathbf{G}=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}&\phi_{2}&\phi_{3}\\
1&0&0\\
0&1&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\,,\qquad\bm{\omega}_{t}=(\eta_{t},0,0)\,,\qquad\mathbf{W}_{t}=\mathbf{W}=\begin{pmatrix}\omega&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}\,.

We use the previous fact to develop the second step of the MCMC algorithm described below. There, we implement a forward filtering backward sampling algorithm
(
Prado and West, 2010
, p. 137)
to get samples from
p(\bm{\theta}_{1:T}\mid\bm{\gamma},\bm{\phi},\omega)
, which automatically allow us to draw samples from
p(\bm{x}\mid\bm{\gamma},\bm{\phi},\omega)
.

Now, we describe the general form of the MCMC to obtain samples from the posterior distribution

p(\mathbf{\Upsilon}\mid\bm{y})\propto p(\bm{y}\mid\bm{\gamma},\bm{\alpha},\bm{x})\,p(\bm{x}\mid\bm{\phi},w)\,p(\bm{\gamma})\,p(\bm{\alpha})\,p(\bm{\phi})\,p(w)\,,

where
\mathbf{\Upsilon}=(\bm{\gamma},\bm{\alpha},\bm{x},\bm{\phi},\omega)
and
\bm{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{T})
. Full conditional distributions are derived looking at the dependencies in
p(\mathbf{\Upsilon}\mid\bm{y})
. Let
\mathbf{\Upsilon}^{(b)}
be the vector of model parameters at iteration
b
of the algorithm, for
b=1,\ldots,B
. Given a starting point
\mathbf{\Upsilon}^{(0)}
, we consider a Gibbs sampler (with a FFBS step) updating
\mathbf{\Upsilon}^{(b-1)}
to
\mathbf{\Upsilon}^{(b)}
until convergence as follows:

1.

Sample
\alpha_{t}\mid\theta_{t,1},\gamma_{t},y_{t}\sim\textsf{N}(m_{t},V_{t})
, for
t=1,\ldots,T
, with

V_{t}=\frac{1}{10(10\gamma_{t}+1)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad m_{t}=100V_{t}\gamma_{t}(y_{t}-\theta_{t,1})\,.

2.

Sample
\gamma_{t}\mid\theta_{t,1},y_{t}\sim\textsf{Ber}(p_{t})
, for
t=1,\ldots,T
, with

\frac{p_{t}}{1-p_{t}}=\frac{1}{4\sqrt{11}}\,\exp\left\{-\tfrac{1}{2}(y_{t}-\theta_{t,1})^{2}(1/0.11-1/0.01)\right\}\,.

3.

Sample
\bm{\phi}\mid\bm{\theta}_{1:T},\omega\sim\textsf{N}_{p}(\mathbf{m}_{\phi},\mathbf{V}_{\bm{\phi}})
, with

\mathbf{V}_{\bm{\phi}}=\left(b_{\phi}^{-1}\mathbf{I}+\frac{1}{\omega}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}^{\textsf{T}}\right)^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{m}_{\phi}=\mathbf{V}_{\bm{\phi}}\left(a_{\phi}b_{\phi}^{-1}\mathbf{1}+\frac{1}{\omega}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\theta_{t,1}\bm{\theta}_{t-1}\right)\,.

4.

Sample
\omega\mid\bm{\theta}_{1:T},\bm{\phi}\sim\textsf{IG}(A_{\omega},B_{\omega})
, with

A_{\omega}=a_{\omega}+\frac{T}{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad B_{\omega}=b_{\omega}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\theta_{t,1}-\bm{\phi}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{\theta}_{t})^{2}\,.

5.

Sample
\bm{\theta}_{t}
, for
t=1,\ldots,T
, using a FFBS step. Here we use standard DLM notation, in particular that corresponding to the Kalman filter
(
West and Harrison, 1999
, Theorem 4.1, pp. 103-105)
:

a.

Use the filter equations to compute
\mathbf{a}_{t}
,
\mathbf{R}_{t}
,
\mathbf{m}_{t}
, and
\mathbf{C}_{t}
, for
t=1,\ldots,T
:

\mathbf{a}_{t}=\mathbf{G}_{t}\mathbf{m}_{t-1}\,,\,\mathbf{R}_{t}=\mathbf{G}_{t}\mathbf{C}_{t-1}\mathbf{G}_{t}^{\textsf{T}}+\mathbf{W}_{t}\,,\,\mathbf{m}_{t}=\mathbf{a}_{t}+\mathbf{A}_{t}e_{t}\,,\,\mathbf{C}_{t}=\mathbf{R}_{t}-Q_{t}\mathbf{A}_{t}\mathbf{A}_{t}^{\textsf{T}}\,,

with
e_{t}=y_{t}-f_{t}
,
f_{t}=\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{a}_{t}
, and
Q_{t}=\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{R}_{t}\mathbf{F}_{t}+V_{t}
.

b.

At time
t=T
, sample
\bm{\theta}_{T}\sim\textsf{N}_{p}(\mathbf{m}_{T},\mathbf{C}_{T})
.

c.

For
t=(T-1),\ldots,0
, sample
\bm{\theta}_{t}\sim\textsf{N}(\mathbf{m}_{t}^{*},\mathbf{C}_{t}^{*})
, where

\mathbf{m}_{t}^{*}=\mathbf{m}_{t}+\mathbf{B}_{t}(\bm{\theta}_{t+1}-\mathbf{a}_{t+1})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{C}_{t}^{*}=\mathbf{C}_{t}-\mathbf{B}_{t}\mathbf{R}_{t+1}\mathbf{B}_{t}^{\textsf{T}}\,,

with
\mathbf{B}_{t}=\mathbf{C}_{t}\mathbf{G}_{t+1}^{\textsf{T}}\mathbf{R}_{t+1}^{-1}
.

Now, we implement the MCMC provided above. To do so, we need to choose appropriate values for
a_{\phi}
,
b_{\phi}
,
a_{\omega}
, and
b_{\omega}
.
First,
a_{\phi}=0
and
b_{\phi}=0.25
are reasonable values in accordance with our introductory analysis of the series using a standard autoregressive model.
On the other hand, we weakly concentrate the prior distribution of
\omega
around
\hat{\upsilon}_{\text{mle}}=0.0128
from our introductory analysis, by setting
a_{\omega}=2
and
b_{\omega}=\hat{\upsilon}_{\text{mle}}
, which leads to
\textsf{E}(\omega)=\hat{\upsilon}_{\text{mle}}
with infinite variance. The results presented below are based on
B=25000
samples from the posterior distribution
p(\mathbf{\Upsilon}\mid\bm{y})
obtained after a burn-in period of
5000
iterations. Chains mixed very well and there is no sign of lack of convergence. Effective sample sizes range from 398.7 to 29019.3.

Areal unit space-time modeling

We consider an ecological application in which a spatiotemporal model for areal unit data is considered for modeling a set of binary observations
y_{i,t}=y_{t}(\bm{s}_{i})
, for
i=1,\ldots,I
and
t=1,\ldots,T
, in a discrete time period over a fixed lattice
\bm{s}_{1},\ldots,\bm{s}_{I}
. Here, we consider the Google flu data corresponding to the first 12 weeks of 2013 for the contiguous continental states in the United States (Alaska and Hawaii are omitted from the analysis). Thus, we record a binary variable
y_{i,t}
that is equal to 1 if the Google flue index for state
i
during week
t
is greater than 7500 cases, and 0 otherwise.

We have that the observations
y_{i,t}
are Bernoulli distributed with probability of success
\pi_{i,t}
, i.e.,
y_{i,t}\mid\pi_{i,t}\sim\textsf{Ber}(\pi_{i,t})
.
Here, we consider the following areal hierarchical generalized linear model
(
Banerjee et al., 2014
, Sec. 6.4)
including a temporal component:

\displaystyle y_{i,t}\mid\omega_{i,t}

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\hbox{if $\omega_{it}>0$;}\\
0,&\hbox{if $\omega_{it}\leq 0$,}\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\omega_{i,t}\mid\bm{\beta},\bm{\gamma},\xi,\theta_{i,t},\phi_{i,t},\kappa_{t}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny ind}}}{\sim}}\textsf{N}\left(\bm{x}_{i}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{\beta}+\bm{z}_{i}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{\gamma}+\xi t+\theta_{i,t}+\phi_{i,t},1/\kappa_{t}\right)\,,

with a hierarchical prior distribution of the form

\displaystyle\beta_{k}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny iid}}}{\sim}}\textsf{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{0})\,,

\displaystyle\gamma_{\ell}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny iid}}}{\sim}}\textsf{CAR}(1/\sigma^{2}_{0})\,,

\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle\sim\textsf{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{0})\,,

(8)

\displaystyle\theta_{i,t}\mid\tau_{t}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny ind}}}{\sim}}\textsf{N}(0,1/\tau_{t})\,,

\displaystyle\phi_{i,t}\mid\lambda_{t}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny ind}}}{\sim}}\textsf{CAR}(\lambda_{t})\,,

\displaystyle\kappa_{t}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny iid}}}{\sim}}\textsf{G}(\nu_{0}/2,\nu_{0}/2)\,,

\displaystyle\tau_{t}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny iid}}}{\sim}}\textsf{G}(a_{\tau},b_{\tau})\,,

\displaystyle\lambda_{t}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny iid}}}{\sim}}\textsf{G}(a_{\lambda},b_{\lambda})\,,

for
k=1\ldots,K
,

, where
\nu_{0}
is a fixed positive integer,
\sigma^{2}_{0},a_{\tau},b_{\tau},a_{\lambda},b_{\lambda}
are fixed positive real numbers, and
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{K})
,
\bm{x}_{i}=\bm{x}(\bm{s}_{i})=(x_{1}(\bm{s}_{i}),\ldots,x_{K}(\bm{s}_{i}))

\bm{\gamma}=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{L})
, and
\bm{z}_{i}=\bm{z}_{t}(\bm{s}_{i})=(z_{1}(\bm{s}_{i}),\ldots,z_{L}(\bm{s}_{i}))

The link function
g(\cdot)
relating the
\pi_{i,t}
and the linear predictor is based in a Student-
t
distribution with
\nu_{0}
degrees of freedom, which is particularly useful to detect rare events (those with small
\pi_{i,t}

\omega_{i,t}=\eta_{i,t}+\phi_{i,t}+\epsilon_{i,t}
with
\eta_{i,t}=\bm{x}_{i}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{\beta}+\bm{z}_{i}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{\gamma}+\xi t+\theta_{i,t}

\epsilon_{i,t}\sim\textsf{N}(0,1/\kappa_{t})
and
\kappa_{t}\sim\textsf{G}(\nu_{0}/2,\nu_{0}/2)

\epsilon_{i,t}
(and therefore
\omega_{it}

t
with
\nu_{0}
degrees of freedom. Thus, due to the symmetry of the
t

\textsf{Pr}\left(y_{i,t}=1\mid\omega_{i,t}\right)=\textsf{Pr}\left(\epsilon_{it}\leq\eta_{i,t}\right)
, and therefore,
\pi_{i,t}=F_{\nu_{0}}(\eta_{i,t})
, where
F_{\nu_{0}}
is the cumulative distribution functions of a random variable following a
t
distribution with
\nu_{0}
degrees of freedom, which means that the link function is
g=F_{\nu_{0}}
.

Note that the main effects for non-spatiotemporal covariates have a standard linear regression structure
\bm{x}_{i,t}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{\beta}
, with a weakly informative prior for
\bm{\beta}

(
Gelman et al., 2014
, p. 55)
. Also, the main effects for space covariates have a standard linear regression structure
\bm{z}_{i,t}^{\textsf{T}}\bm{\gamma}
, following an improper conditionally autoregressive (CAR) model for
\bm{\gamma}

(
Banerjee et al., 2014
, p. 81)
. Furthermore, the main effects for time are included in the linear predictor through
\delta_{t}=\xi t
, with a weakly informative prior for
\xi
. Lastly, the main effects for spatiotemporal interactions
\theta_{i,t}+\phi_{i,t}
have two components. On the one hand,
\theta_{i,t}
captures region-wide heterogeneity (unstructured variation) by means of
\textsf{N}(0,1/\tau_{t})
, where
\tau_{t}
is a precision parameter controlling the magnitude of
\theta_{i,t}
. And on the other,
\phi_{i,t}
captures regional clustering (spatially structured variability) via
\textsf{CAR}(\lambda_{t})
, where
\lambda_{t}
is a precision parameter as before.

CAR models are very convenient computationally, since the method for exploring the posterior distribution is in itself a conditional algorithm, the Gibbs sampler, which also eliminates the need for matrix inversion. However, CAR models have two main theoretical and computational challenges, namely, model impropriety and precision parameter selection.
Banerjee et al., 2014
recommend to ignore impropriety and work with the intrinsic CAR specification, since we are using it just as a prior distribution and not to model the data directly. Even though this is the usual approach it requires some care: This improper CAR prior is a pairwise difference prior that is identified only up to an additive constant. Thus, in order to identify an intercept term (say
\beta_{1}
), we must add the constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{I}\phi_{i,t}=0
(this constrain is imposed numerically during computation). Moreover,
\tau_{t}
and
\lambda_{t}
cannot be chosen arbitrarily large, since
\theta_{i,t}
and
\phi_{i,t}
would become unidentifiable. After all, we have only a single
y_{i,t}
in order to estimate two random effects for each
i
and each
t
. To make the prior specification
\tau_{t}\sim\textsf{G}(a_{\tau},b_{\tau})
and
\lambda_{t}\sim\textsf{G}(a_{\lambda},b_{\lambda})
sensible,
Banerjee et al., 2014
recommend a prior specification that leads to

\textsf{SD}(\theta_{i,t})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau_{t}}}\approx\frac{1}{0.7\sqrt{\bar{m}\,\lambda_{t}}}\approx\textsf{SD}(\phi_{i,t})\,,

(9)

where

Species sampling modeling

We consider the species sampling model (SSS)

p(X_{n+1}\in B\mid X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\sum_{k=1}^{K^{n}}q^{n}_{k}(\bm{m}^{n})\delta_{\tilde{X}_{k}}(B)+q^{n}_{K^{n}+1}(\bm{m}^{n})G_{0}(B)

where
G_{0}
is a non-atomic measure
(
Rodríguez and Quintana, 2015
)
. First of all, in order to understand and develop the MCMC algorithms presented below, we find an expression for
\textsf{E}\left(K^{n}\right)
, the expected value of the number of distinct species among
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
.

Let
W_{1},\ldots,W_{n}
be a sequence of binary random variables such that

W_{i}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\hbox{$X_{i}$ corresponds to a species that has not been observed in $X_{1},\ldots,X_{i-1}$;}\\
0,&\hbox{otherwise,}\end{array}\right.

for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Since the first observation is necessarily a new observation we have that
W_{1}\equiv 1
and

\textsf{E}\left(K^{n}\right)=\textsf{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}W_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\textsf{E}\left(W_{i}\right)=1+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\textsf{E}\left(W_{i}\right).

Let
\mathcal{M}_{k}
denote the set of all vectors of length
k
with positive, integer entries that add up to
n
, i.e.,
\mathcal{M}_{k}:=\{(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}:m_{1}+\ldots+m_{k}=n\}
. Case by case, we see that

\displaystyle\textsf{E}\left(W_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}p(m^{1}_{1},1)

\displaystyle\textsf{E}\left(W_{3}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}p(m^{2}_{1},1)+\sum_{\mathcal{M}_{2}}p(m^{2}_{1},m^{2}_{2},1)

\displaystyle\textsf{E}\left(W_{4}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}p(m^{3}_{1},1)+\sum_{\mathcal{M}_{2}}p(m^{3}_{1},m^{3}_{2},1)+\sum_{\mathcal{M}_{3}}p(m^{3}_{1},m^{3}_{2},m^{3}_{3},1)

\displaystyle\hskip 4.26773pt\vdots

\displaystyle\textsf{E}\left(W_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}p(m^{i-1}_{1},1)+\sum_{\mathcal{M}_{2}}p(m^{i-1}_{1},m^{i-1}_{2},1)+\ldots+\sum_{\mathcal{M}_{i-1}}p(m^{i-1}_{1},\ldots,m^{i-1}_{i-1},1)

and therefore

\textsf{E}\left(W_{i}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\sum_{\mathcal{M}_{k}}p(m^{i-1}_{1},\ldots,m^{i-1}_{k},1).

Recalling the following fact given in
Pitman, 1995
,

p(m^{i-1}_{1},\ldots,m^{i-1}_{k},1)=p(m^{i-1}_{1},\ldots,m^{i-1}_{k})\,q_{k+1}^{i-1}(m^{i-1}_{1},\ldots,m^{i-1}_{k})\,,\quad i=2,\ldots,n,

we finally get that the expected value of the number of distinct species is given by

\textsf{E}\left(K^{n}\right)=1+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\sum_{\mathcal{M}_{k}}p(m^{i-1}_{1},\ldots,m^{i-1}_{k})\,q_{k+1}^{i-1}(m^{i-1}_{1},\ldots,m^{i-1}_{k})\,.

In order to verify this result, we consider two particular scenarios, namely, the Chinese restaurant process (CRP) and the Pitman-Yor process (PYP):

CRP

In this case, the predictive probability function (PPF) is given by

q_{k+1}^{i-1}(m_{1}^{i-1},\ldots,m_{k}^{i-1})=\frac{\theta}{\theta+i-1}

(
Müller and Rodriguez, 2013
, p. 83)
, and therefore

\textsf{E}\left(K^{n}\right)=1+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{\theta}{\theta+i-1}\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\sum_{\mathcal{M}_{k}}p(m^{i-1}_{1},\ldots,m^{i-1}_{k})=1+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{\theta}{\theta+i-1}

since

\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\sum_{\mathcal{M}_{k}}p(m^{i-1}_{1},\ldots,m^{i-1}_{k})=1\,,\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

which leads to

\textsf{E}\left(K^{n}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\theta}{\theta+i-1}

This expression is the same one given in
Rodríguez and Quintana, 2015
.

PYP

In this case the PPF is given by
q_{k+1}^{i-1}(m_{1}^{i-1},\ldots,m_{k}^{i-1})=\frac{\theta+\sigma k}{\theta+i-1}

(
Müller and Rodriguez, 2013
, p. 85)
, which means that

\displaystyle\textsf{E}\left(K^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\sum_{\mathcal{M}_{k}}p(m^{i-1}_{1},\ldots,m^{i-1}_{k})\,\frac{\theta+\sigma k}{\theta+i-1}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{\theta}{\theta+i-1}\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\sum_{\mathcal{M}_{k}}p(m^{i-1}_{1},\ldots,m^{i-1}_{k})

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{\sigma}{\theta+i-1}\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\sum_{\mathcal{M}_{k}}k\,p(m^{i-1}_{1},\ldots,m^{i-1}_{k})

and therefore

\textsf{E}\left(K^{n}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\theta}{\theta+i-1}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{\sigma}{\theta+i-1}\,\zeta_{i}

with

\zeta_{i}:=\sum_{k=1}^{i-1}\sum_{\mathcal{M}_{k}}k\,p(m^{i-1}_{1},\ldots,m^{i-1}_{k})\,,\quad i=2,\ldots,n.

Now, consider the stick breaking prior of the form
G=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\omega_{l}\delta_{X^{*}_{l}}
where
X_{l}^{*}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny iid}}}{\sim}}G_{0}
,
\omega_{l}=z_{l}\prod_{k<l}(1-z_{k})
, and
z_{l}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny iid}}}{\sim}}H^{\bm{\theta}}
for all
l=1,2\ldots
, with
H^{\bm{\theta}}
a probability distribution on
[0,1]
indexed by the vector of parameters
\bm{\theta}
. In what follows we show that the exchangeable partition probability function (EPPF) induced by this stick breaking prior is given by

p(m_{1}^{n},\ldots,m_{K^{n}}^{n}\mid\bm{\theta})=\sum_{\bm{\sigma}\in\mathcal{P}_{K^{n}}}\left[\prod_{k=1}^{K^{n}}\frac{\gamma_{\bm{\theta}}\left(m^{n}_{\sigma_{k}},\sum_{j=k+1}^{K^{n}}m^{n}_{\sigma_{j}}\right)}{1-\gamma_{\bm{\theta}}\left(0,\sum_{j=k}^{K^{n}}m^{n}_{\sigma_{j}}\right)}\right]

(11)

where
\mathcal{P}_{K^{n}}
denotes the set of all permutations of
\{1,\ldots,K^{n}\}
,
\bm{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{K^{n}})
, and
\gamma_{\bm{\theta}}(x,y)=\textsf{E}\left(z^{x}(1-z)^{y}\right)
with
z\sim H^{\bm{\theta}}
.

If
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
is a iid sample from
G
, then this sequence is exchangeable and there exists a positive probability of ties among the
X_{i}
s. Furthermore, the EPPF associated with this prior is given by

p(\bm{m}^{n}\mid\bm{\theta})=\sum_{(j_{1},\ldots,j_{K^{n}})\in\mathfrak{I}_{K^{n}}}\textsf{E}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{K^{n}}\omega_{j_{k}}^{m^{n}_{k}}\right)

(12)

where
\bm{m}=(m_{1}^{n},\ldots,m_{K^{n}}^{n})
and
I_{K^{n}}
is the set of all possible sequences of distinct positive integers of length
K^{n}

(
Pitman, 1996
, p. 256)
. Then, by expanding the summation, the previous expression becomes

p(\bm{m}^{n}\mid\bm{\theta})=\sum_{j_{1}}\sum_{j_{2}\notin\{j_{1}\}}\sum_{j_{3}\notin\{j_{1},j_{2}\}}\ldots\sum_{j_{K^{n}}\notin\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{K^{n}-1}\}}\textsf{E}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{K^{n}}\left(z_{j_{k}}\prod_{h<j_{k}}(1-z_{h})\right)^{m^{n}_{k}}\right)\,.

First, we consider the scenario in which
j_{1}<j_{2}<\ldots<j_{K^{n}}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{K^{n}}\left(z_{j_{k}}\prod_{h<j_{k}}(1-z_{h})\right)^{m^{n}_{k}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{K^{n}}z_{j_{k}}^{m^{n}_{k}}(1-z_{1})^{m^{n}_{k}}(1-z_{2})^{m^{n}_{k}}\ldots(1-z_{j_{k}-1})^{m^{n}_{k}}

\displaystyle=\left[z_{j_{1}}\ldots z_{K^{n}}\right]^{S_{1}^{n}}\cdot\left[(1-z_{1})\ldots(1-z_{j_{1}-1})\right]^{S_{1}^{n}}\cdot\left[(1-z_{j_{1}})\ldots(1-z_{j_{2}-1})\right]^{S_{2}^{n}}

\displaystyle\hskip 8.50012pt\,\cdot\left[(1-z_{j_{2}})\ldots(1-z_{j_{3}-1})\right]^{S_{3}^{n}}\cdot\ldots\cdot\left[(1-z_{j_{(K^{n}-1)}})\ldots(1-z_{j_{(K^{n})}-1})\right]^{S_{K^{n}}^{n}}

where
S_{k}^{n}=\sum_{j=k}^{K^{n}}m^{n}_{j}
for
k=1,\ldots,K^{n}
.

Associating terms and recalling the fact that
z_{l}\mathrel{\overset{\makebox[0.0pt]{\mbox{\tiny iid}}}{\sim}}H^{\bm{\theta}}
for
l=1,2,\ldots
we get that

\textsf{E}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{K^{n}}\omega_{j_{k}}^{m^{n}_{k}}\right)=\prod_{k=1}^{K^{n}}\textsf{E}\left(z^{m_{k}}(1-z)^{S^{n}_{k+1}}\right)\left(\textsf{E}\left((1-z)^{S_{k}}\right)\right)^{d_{k}}

where
z\sim H^{\bm{\theta}}
and
d_{k}=j_{k}-j_{k-1}-1
for
k=1,\ldots,K^{n}
, which represent the sizes of the “gaps” between he labels of two consecutive observed species, with the convention
j_{0}=0

(
Rodríguez and Quintana, 2015
, p. 14)
.

Now, recognizing the symmetry of the summation in (
12
) and all the possible “gaps” we obtain that

\displaystyle p(\bm{m}^{n}\mid\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{d_{1}=0}^{\infty}\ldots\sum_{d_{K^{n}}=0}^{\infty}\textsf{E}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{K^{n}}\omega_{j_{k}}^{m^{n}_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{K^{n}}\left\{\textsf{E}\left(z^{m_{k}}(1-z)^{S^{n}_{k+1}}\right)\sum_{d_{k}=0}^{\infty}\left(\textsf{E}\left((1-z)^{S_{k}}\right)\right)^{d_{k}}\right\}

Since the las summation is a geometric series then we finally get that

p(\bm{m}^{n}\mid\bm{\theta})=\prod_{k=1}^{K^{n}}\frac{\textsf{E}\left(z^{m_{k}}(1-z)^{S^{n}_{k+1}}\right)}{1-\textsf{E}\left((1-z)^{S_{k}}\right)}

which precisely corresponds to the expression given in (
12
).

We consider the data discussed in
Lijoi et al., 2007
. The basic sample consists of
n=2586
expressed sequence tags and this gives
K^{n}=1825
different CDNA fragments each of which represents a unique gene. If
r_{i}
denotes the number of clusters of size
i
, then the dataset gives
r_{i}=1434,253,71,33,11,6,2,3,1,2,2,1,1,1,2,1,1
with
i\in\{1,2,...,14\}\cup\{16,23,27)\}
. This means we are observing 1434 clusters of size 1, 253 clusters of size 2, and so on.

Here, we develop MCMC algorithms for performing Bayesian estimation of the parameters of a CRP and a PYP using this dataset. These algorithms were design according to the posterior distribution

p(\bm{\theta}|\bm{m}^{n})\propto p(\bm{m}^{n}|\bm{\theta})\,p(\bm{\theta})

where
\bm{\theta}
is the vector of parameters of the process and
\bm{m}^{n}=(m_{1}^{n},\ldots,m_{K^{n}}^{n})
. Below we describe the algorithms for performing Bayesian estimation of
\bm{\theta}
. Let
\bm{\theta}^{(m)}
denote the parameter vector at iteration
m
of the algorithm,
m=0,1,\ldots,M
. Given a starting point of the parameter
\bm{\theta}^{(0)}
, we run the sampler updating
\bm{\theta}^{(m-1)}
to
\bm{\theta}^{(m)}
until convergence according to the corresponding description.

CRP

Here
\bm{\theta}=\theta
and

p(\theta|\bm{m}^{n})\propto\left[\theta^{K^{n}}\frac{\Gamma(\theta)}{\Gamma(\theta+n)}\,\prod_{j=1}^{K^{n}}\Gamma(m_{j}^{n})\right]\times\textsf{G}(\theta|a_{\theta},b_{\theta})\,.

The MCMC algorithm is as follows:

1.

Compute
\eta^{(m-1)}=\log\theta^{(m-1)}
.

2.

Sample
\eta^{*}\sim\textsf{N}(\eta^{(m-1)},b_{\eta})
, where
b_{\eta}
is a tuning parameter.

3.

Compute
r=p_{\eta}(\eta^{*}|\bm{m}^{n})/p_{\eta}(\eta^{(m-1)}|\bm{m}^{n})
where
p_{\eta}(\eta|\bm{m}^{n})=p(e^{\eta}|\bm{m}^{n})\times e^{\eta}
.

4.

Set

\eta^{(m)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\eta^{*},&\hbox{with probability $r$;}\\
\eta^{(m-1)},&\hbox{with probability $1-r$.}\end{array}\right.

5.

Compute
\theta^{(m)}=\exp{\left\{\eta^{(m)}\right\}}
.

6.

Repeat until convergence.

Notice that in the Metropolis step above we do the transformation
\eta=\log(\theta)
to consider the range of
\theta
in the random-walk proposal. The corresponding Jacobian (
e^{\eta}
) is taken into account in the computations through
p_{\eta}(\eta|\bm{m}^{n})
.

Compute

.

3.

Repeat until convergence.

6
Discussion

In this work we have offered four detailed case studies that use Bayesian modeling techniques in order to answer complex questions. At every instance, we have provided specifics about model formulation, prior elicitation, simulation-based algorithms computation, and posterior inference. We hope that the reader benefits from such effort and considers the Bayesian paradigm a natural way to solve data-driven problems.

Even though the Bayesian approach to statistical inference is extremely beneficial, some challenges remain. In particular, we recommend consider alternative inference methods in order to account for “big data”, which is currently an active research area in computational statistics (e.g., variational methods). See for example
Ormerod and Wand, 2010
.

Notation

.
