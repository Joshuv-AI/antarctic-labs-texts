A Strong Nominal Semantics for Fixed-point Constraints
Ali K. Caires-Santos a myemail, Maribel Fernández b coemail, Daniele Nantes-Sobrinho a c ccoemail

Abstract
Nominal algebra includes α \alpha -equality and freshness constraints on nominal terms endowed with a nominal set semantics that facilitates reasoning about languages with binders. Nominal unification is decidable and unitary, however, its extension with equational axioms such as Commutativity (which is finitary) is no longer finitary unless permutation fixed-point constraints are used. In this paper, we extend the notion of nominal algebra by introducing fixed-point constraints and provide a sound semantics using strong nominal sets. We show, by providing a counter-example, that the class of nominal sets is not a sound denotation for this extended nominal algebra. To recover soundness we propose two different formulations of nominal algebra, one obtained by restricting to a class of fixed-point contexts that are in direct correspondence with freshness contexts and another obtained by using a different set of derivation rules.

Introduction

Nominal algebra
[
16
,
15
]
provides a sophisticated framework to interpret and reason algebraically about systems with binding. The term language of nominal algebra is an extension of first-order terms, called nominal terms
[
22
]
. Nominal syntax includes
names
(representing object-level variables, or
atoms
), along with meta-level variables (called just
variables
) and constructs to specify binding (nominal
abstraction
). The
\alpha
-equivalence relation between nominal terms is defined using name
permutations
and an auxiliary
freshness
relation.

Gabbay and Mathijssen
[
16
]
define a sound and complete proof system for nominal algebra, where derivation rules are subject to freshness conditions
a\#t
(read “
a
is fresh for
t
”
[
22
]
).
Nominal algebra with freshness constraints has a semantics in the class of nominal sets (sets with a finitely supported permutation action) which have a rich structure to reason about names, the permutation of names, and name binding. This approach to the specification of syntax with binding has many advantages: Matching and unification modulo
\alpha
-equivalence are decidable
[
22
]
and moreover there are efficient algorithms to compute unique most general unifiers (pairs of a freshness context and a substitution) for solvable unification problems
[
8
,
19
]
. These algorithms have been used to implement rewriting, functional languages and logic programming languages (see, e.g.,
[
14
,
21
,
9
]
). Disunification problems are also decidable
[
5
]
, as well as the more general class of equational problems
[
6
,
3
]
.

Since applications in theorem proving and rewrite-based programming languages often require the use of associative-commutative operators, nominal unification algorithms have been extended to deal with equational axioms such as Associativity (A) and Commutativity (C)
[
7
]
. C-unification and AC-unification are decidable and finitary, however, although nominal C-unification and nominal AC-unification are decidable, they are not finitary when solutions are expressed using substitutions and freshness constraints.
To overcome this limitation, fixed-point constraints
\pi\curlywedge t
(read “the name permutation
\pi
fixes
t
”) should be used: indeed, nominal unification modulo C and AC theories is finitary if solutions are expressed using substitutions, freshness and fixed-point constraints; moreover, freshness constraints can in turn be expressed using fixed-point constraints
[
4
]
.

The relation between freshness and fixed-point equations was initially proposed by Pitts
[
1
]
, by the equivalence
a\#x\iff\reflectbox{$\mathsf{N}$}c.(a\ c)\cdot x=x
, where
a
and
c
are atoms and
x
is an element of a nominal set. The equivalence uses the

\mathsf{N}

(‘new’) quantifier that quantifies over new/fresh names. Thus, the equivalence means that
a
is fresh for
x
if and only if swapping
a
for any new name
c
in
x
does not alter
x
, in other words, the action of the permutation
(a\ c)
fixes
x
. The fixed-point constraints
\pi\curlywedge t
are more general, since
\pi
is not restricted to be of the form
(a\ c)
for a new name
c
. For example, depending on the chosen model, a fixed-point constraint
(a\ b)\curlywedge X
does not necessarily imply
a\#X
. Thus, the semantics of general fixed-point constraints differs from the semantics of freshness constraints and it needs to be determined so that the meaning of the developments in nominal techniques using fixed-point constraints can be established.

In this paper we address this gap. We consider nominal algebras that use fixed-point constraints instead of freshness constraints. Initially, the semantics for nominal algebras with fixed-point constraints was expected to use nominal sets as carriers. However, through the analysis presented in this paper, we show that this assumption is not valid by providing counterexamples.
We also show that soundness can be recovered by using a subclass of the class of nominal sets, called strong nominal sets, or by strengthening the derivation rules using a restricted class of fixed-point contexts and the

\mathsf{N}

quantifier from nominal logic.

Summarising, our main contributions are:

(1)

A novel semantic interpretation of general fixed-point constraints.

(2)

The definition of a notion of
strong nominal algebra
as well as notions of strong axioms and theories.

(3)

A counter-example showing that the class of nominal sets is not a sound denotation for nominal theories using general fixed-point constraints.

(4)

A proof that strong nominal sets are a sound denotation for nominal theories with fixed-point constraints.

(5)

An alternative formulation of a nominal theory with fixed-point constraints, whose denotational semantics is the whole class of nominal sets.

(6)

An analysis of the correspondences between approaches with freshness and fixed-point constraints.

The above results open the way for a generalisation of the algorithms used to check nominal equational problems to the case where the signature includes associative and commutative operators. Recall that equational problems are first-order formulas where the only predicate is equality. They have numerous applications in programming and theorem proving (see, e.g.,
[
11
,
10
,
12
,
13
,
18
]
).
Extensions to nominal syntax with
\alpha
-equality and freshness constraints have already been studied
[
6
]
, but in order to consider syntax modulo equational theories and preserve the properties of the algorithms (in particular to preserve finitary unification) fixed-point constraints are needed, together with a corresponding notion of nominal algebra that can provide sound denotations for the problems. In future work, we will develop algorithms to solve nominal equational problems modulo associative and commutative axioms.

We say that a nominal term is

Write

We recall some basic definitions on nominal sets
[

]
.

A

, for every

. Sometimes, we will write

, when we want to make

For

write
{\tt pfix}(B)=\{\pi\in{\mathtt{Perm}}(\mathbb{A})\mid\forall a\in B.~\pi(a)=a\}
, that is,
{\tt pfix}(B)
is the set of permutations that fix pointwise the elements of

. A set of atomic names
B\subseteq\mathbb{A}

when for all permutations

,

. Additionally, we say that

if for all permutations

,

.

A

is a
{\mathtt{Perm}}(\mathbb{A})
-set

all of whose elements are finitely supported.
\mathscr{X,Y,Z}
will range over nominal sets. A nominal set is

if every element is strongly supported by a finite set
2
2

2

The class of strong nominal sets is a subclass of the class of nominal sets
.
The

of an element

is the

, then

For any nominal sets

The set
\mathbb{A}^{*}=\bigcup\{a_{1}\cdots a_{n}\mid\forall i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.a_{i}\in\mathbb{A}\wedge(j\neq i\Rightarrow a_{j}\neq a_{i})\}
, that is, the set of finite words over distinct atoms, is a strong nominal set when equipped with the permutation action given by
\pi\cdot(a_{1}\cdots a_{n})=\pi(a_{1})\cdots\pi(a_{n})
, and
\mathtt{supp}(a_{1}\cdots a_{n})=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
.

(5)

\mathbb{F}(\Sigma)
with the action
\pi\cdot a\equiv\pi(a),\pi\cdot[a]t\equiv[\pi(a)]\pi\cdot t
and
\pi\cdot\mathtt{f}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\equiv\mathtt{}(\pi\cdot t_{1},\ldots,\pi\cdot t_{n})
forms a nominal set and
\mathtt{supp}(g)=\mathtt{Atm}(g)
for all
g\in\mathbb{F}(\Sigma)
. The relation
\sim
defined by
g\sim g^{\prime}
iff
~\vdash_{\tt T}g=g^{\prime}
is an equivariant equivalence relation. The set
\mathbb{F}(\Sigma)/_{\sim}
is a nominal set and
\mathtt{supp}(\overline{g})=\bigcap\{\mathtt{supp}(g^{\prime})\mid g^{\prime}\in\overline{g}\}
for any
\overline{g}\in\mathbb{F}(\Sigma)/_{\sim}
(see Proposition 2.30
[
1
]
). We denote
\mathbb{F}(\Sigma)/_{\sim}
by
\overline{\mathbb{F}}({\tt T},\Sigma)
. In the case where
{\tt T}=\emptyset
, the relation
\sim
is the
\alpha
-equivalence relation between ground terms and
\mathtt{supp}(\overline{g})=\mathtt{fn}(g)
for all
\overline{g}\in\overline{\mathbb{F}}(\emptyset,\Sigma)
.

3
Semantics

In this section we provide a semantics for fixed-point constraints using nominal sets, and denote its semantic interpretation as
\curlywedge_{\tt sem}
. We then build upon the semantic definition of
\curlywedge
and define concepts such as (strong)
\Sigma
-algebras, valuations, interpretations, models, etc. These are necessary ingredients to determine validity of judgements in terms of
\curlywedge_{\tt sem}
, and consequently, establish soundness of nominal algebra with fixed-point constraints.

Below we overload the symbol
=
to denote equality between elements of a nominal set.

3.1
Semantics for
\curlywedge

The semantics of fixed-point constraints
\curlywedge_{\tt sem}
differs from
\#_{\tt sem}
and it is defined in terms of fixed-point equations, and not in terms of the support. For an element of a nominal set
x\in\mathscr{X}
, if
a\notin\mathtt{supp}(x)
, we write

a\#_{\tt sem}\ x
. We refer to
[
16
]
for more details about the semantics of nominal algebra defined by freshness constraints. Below we define
\curlywedge_{\tt sem}
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathscr{X}
be a nominal set,
x\in|\mathscr{X}|,a\in\mathbb{A},
and
\pi\in{\mathtt{Perm}}(\mathbb{A})
. We write
\pi\curlywedge_{\tt sem}x
to denote that
\pi\cdot x=x
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathscr{X}
be a nominal set and
x\in|\mathscr{X}|
. If
\mathtt{dom}(\pi)\cap\mathtt{supp}(x)=\emptyset
then
\pi\curlywedge_{\tt sem}x
.

Proof 3.3
.

Direct from the definition of support.∎

However, the other direction does not hold in general: if we take
x=\{a,b\}
and
\pi=(a\ b)
as in Example
2.3
(
2
),
\pi\curlywedge_{\tt sem}x
but
\mathtt{dom}(\pi)\cap\mathtt{supp}(x)=\{a,b\}
. The converse does hold for strong nominal sets.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\mathscr{X}
be a nominal set and
x\in|\mathscr{X}|
. Then

a\in\mathtt{supp}(x)\iff\{c\mid(a\ c)\curlywedge_{\tt sem}x\}~\text{ is finite}

Proof 3.5
.

The left-to-right implication follows by showing that
\{c\mid(a\ c)\curlywedge_{\tt sem}x\}\subseteq\mathtt{supp}(x)
. The right-to-left implication follows by contradiction and Lemma
3.2
.∎

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\mathscr{X}
be a strong nominal set,
x\in|\mathscr{X}|
, and
\pi,\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}\in{\mathtt{Perm}}(\mathbb{A})
. If
\gamma_{i}\curlywedge_{\tt sem}x
for every
i=1,\ldots,n
, and
\mathtt{dom}(\pi)\subseteq\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathtt{dom}(\gamma_{i}),
then
\pi\curlywedge_{\tt sem}x
.

Proof 3.7
.

For each
i=1,\ldots,n
the following hold:

\gamma_{i}\curlywedge_{\tt sem}x\iff\gamma_{i}\cdot x=x\iff\gamma_{i}\in{\tt pfix}(\mathtt{supp}(x)).

The first equivalence comes from Definition
3.1
and the second from the fact that
\mathscr{X}
is a strong nominal set. Then by the inclusion
\mathtt{dom}(\pi)\subseteq\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathtt{dom}(\gamma_{i})
, we deduce that
\pi\in{\tt pfix}(\mathtt{supp}(x))
which implies, by definition, that
\pi\curlywedge_{\tt sem}x
.∎

Note that Theorem
3.6
is not true in the class of nominal sets. For instance, take
\mathscr{X}
as
\mathcal{P}_{\tt fin}(\mathbb{A})
,
x=\{a,b\}
,
\pi=(a\ c)
,

\gamma_{1}=(a\ b)
,
\gamma_{2}=(c\ d)
.
Then,
\gamma_{1}\curlywedge_{\tt sem}x,\gamma_{2}\curlywedge_{\tt sem}x
, and
\mathtt{dom}(\pi)\subseteq\mathtt{dom}(\gamma_{1})\cup\mathtt{dom}(\gamma_{2})
, but
\pi\not\mathrel{\curlywedge}_{\tt sem}x
.

3.2
Strong Nominal
\Sigma
-algebras

We can now define a semantics for nominal algebra with fixed-point constraints in (strong) nominal sets.

Definition 3.8

((Strong)
\Sigma
-algebra)
.

Given a signature
\Sigma
, a (strong) nominal
\Sigma
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
consists of:

(1)

A (strong) nominal set
\mathscr{A}=(|\mathscr{A}|,\cdot)
- the domain.

(2)

An equivariant map
\mathtt{atom}^{\mathfrak{A}}:\mathbb{A}\to|\mathscr{A}|
to interpret atoms; we write the interpretation
\mathtt{atom}(a)
as
a^{\mathfrak{A}}\in|\mathscr{A}|
.

(3)

An equivariant map
\mathtt{abs}^{\mathfrak{A}}:\mathbb{A}\times|\mathscr{A}|\to|\mathscr{A}|
such that
\{c\in\mathbb{A}\mid(a\ c)\not\mathrel{\curlywedge}_{\tt sem}\mathtt{abs}^{\mathfrak{A}}(a,x)\}
is finite, for all
a\in\mathbb{A}
and
x\in|\mathscr{A}|
(by Lemma
3.4
this is equivalent to saying that
a\notin\mathtt{supp}(\mathtt{abs}^{\mathfrak{A}}(a,x))
); we use this to interpret abstraction.

(4)

An equivariant map
f^{\mathfrak{A}}:|\mathscr{A}|^{n}\to|\mathscr{A}|
, for each
\mathtt{f}:n
in
\Sigma
to interpret term-formers.

We use
\mathfrak{A,B,C}
to denote (strong)
\Sigma
-algebras.

Remark 3.9
.

The standard definition of the condition in item 3 via freshness constraints is
a\#_{\tt sem}\mathtt{abs}^{\mathfrak{A}}(a,x)
(see, for instance,
[
16
]
). Here, we use the following equivalence (valid in nominal sets):

\displaystyle a\#_{\tt sem}\mathtt{abs}^{\mathfrak{A}}(a,x)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow a\notin\mathtt{supp}(\mathtt{abs}^{\mathfrak{A}}(a,x))

\displaystyle(\text{Definition 4.2,~\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{DBLP:journals/logcom/GabbayM09}{}{}]}})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\{c\in\mathbb{A}\mid(a\ c)\not\mathrel{\curlywedge_{\tt sem}}\mathtt{abs}^{\mathfrak{A}}(a,x)\}\text{ is finite}

\displaystyle(\text{Consequence of Lemma~\ref{thm:support-char}})

A
valuation

\varsigma
in a (strong)
\Sigma
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
maps unknowns
X\in\mathbb{V}
to elements

\varsigma(X)\in|\mathscr{A}|
. Below we define an equivariant function
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}:T(\Sigma,\mathbb{A},\mathbb{V})\to|\mathscr{A}|
to interpret nominal terms w.r.t. a valuation
\varsigma
.

Definition 3.10

(Interpretation)
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
be a (strong)
\Sigma
-algebra. Suppose that
t\in T(\Sigma,\mathbb{A},\mathbb{V})
and consider a valuation
\varsigma
in
\mathfrak{A}
. The interpretation
\llbracket t\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
, or just
\llbracket t\rrbracket_{\varsigma}
, if
\mathfrak{A}
is understood, is defined inductively by:

\llbracket a\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}=a^{\mathfrak{A}}

\llbracket\pi\cdot X\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}=\pi\cdot\varsigma(X)

\llbracket{\tt f}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}=f^{\mathfrak{A}}(\llbracket t_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma},\ldots,\llbracket t_{n}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma})

\llbracket[a]t\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}=\mathtt{abs}(a^{\mathfrak{A}},\llbracket t\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma})
.

Lemma 3.11
.

The term-interpretation map
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
is equivariant, that is,
\pi\cdot\llbracket t\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}=\llbracket\pi\cdot t\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
for any permutation
\pi
and term
t
.

Proof 3.12
.

The proof is by induction on the structure of the term
t
.∎

Definition 3.13

(Context and Judgement Validity)
.

For any (strong)
\Sigma
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
:

•

\llbracket\Upsilon\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
is valid iff
\pi\curlywedge_{\tt sem}\llbracket X\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
, for each
\pi\curlywedge X\in\Upsilon
;

•

\llbracket\Upsilon\vdash\pi\curlywedge t\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
is valid iff
\llbracket\Upsilon\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
(valid) implies
\pi\curlywedge_{\tt sem}\llbracket t\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
;

•

\llbracket\Upsilon\vdash t=u\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
is valid iff
\llbracket\Upsilon\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
(valid) implies
\llbracket t\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}=\llbracket u\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
.

More generally,
\llbracket\Upsilon\vdash\pi\curlywedge t\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}
is valid iff
\llbracket\Upsilon\vdash\pi\curlywedge t\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
is valid for all valuations
\varsigma
. Similarly for
\llbracket\Upsilon\vdash t=u\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}
.

Then a (strong) model of a theory is an interpretation that validates its axioms as follows:

Definition 3.14

((Strong) Model)
.

Let
{\tt T}=(\Sigma,Ax)
be a theory. A (strong) model of
{\tt T}
is a (strong)
\Sigma
-algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
such that

\displaystyle\llbracket\Upsilon\vdash t=u\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}\text{ is valid for every axiom }\Upsilon\vdash t=u\text{ in $Ax$ and every valuation $\varsigma$.}

We are now ready to define validity with respect to a theory.

Definition 3.15
.

For any theory
{\tt T}
, define (strong) validity with respect to
{\tt T}
for judgement forms as follows:

•

Write
\Upsilon\vDash_{\tt T}\pi\curlywedge t
(resp.
\Upsilon\vDash_{\tt T}^{s}\pi\curlywedge t
) iff
\llbracket\Upsilon\vdash\pi\curlywedge t\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}
is valid for all models
\mathfrak{A}
of
{\tt T}
(resp. all strong models of
{\tt T}
).

•

Write
\Upsilon\vDash_{\tt T}t=u
(resp.
\Upsilon\vDash_{\tt T}^{s}t=u
) iff
\llbracket\Upsilon\vdash t=u\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}
is valid for all models
\mathfrak{A}
of
{\tt T}
(resp. all strong models of
{\tt T}
).

Lemma 3.16
.

Define the singleton interpretation
\mathfrak{S}
to have as domain the strong nominal set
\{\star\}
, with
\mathtt{atom}^{\mathfrak{S}},\mathtt{abs}^{\mathfrak{S}},f^{\mathfrak{S}}
constant functions with value
\star
. Then
\mathfrak{S}
is a strong
\Sigma
-algebra and a model for every theory.

Proof 3.17
.

To verify that
\mathfrak{S}
is a model of
{\tt T}
, check that
\llbracket\Upsilon\vdash t=u\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varsigma}
is valid for each axiom
\Upsilon\vdash t=u\in Ax
and valuation
\varsigma
(which maps all unknowns to
\star
). If
\llbracket\Upsilon\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varsigma}
is valid, then
\llbracket t\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varsigma}=\llbracket u\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\varsigma}=\star
, confirming
\mathfrak{S}
as a model.∎

Remark 3.18
.

Lemma
3.16
shows that we do not need to require the map
\mathtt{atom}
to be injective.

3.3
Counter-Example: Soundness Failure

Soundness of a derivation system means that derivability implies validity. More precisely, in a sound system the following should hold:

(Soundness)
Suppose
{\tt T}=(\Sigma,Ax)
is a theory.

(1)

If
\Upsilon\vdash\pi\curlywedge t
then
\Upsilon\vDash_{\tt T}\pi\curlywedge t
.

(2)

If
\Upsilon\vdash_{\tt T}t=u
then
\Upsilon\vDash_{\tt T}t=u
.

Note that by definition, for a judgement to be valid it has to hold in all models
\mathfrak{A}
of
{\tt T}
for every valuation
\varsigma
. We present two counter-examples that illustrate two different points where soundness fails.

3.3.1
Rule
\curlywedge{\bf var}
.

The problem arises from the rule
(\curlywedge{\bf var})
:

\mathtt{dom}(\pi^{\pi^{\prime-1}})\subseteq\mathtt{dom}(\mathtt{perm}(\Upsilon|_{X}))

(\curlywedge\textbf{var})

\Upsilon\vdash\pi\curlywedge\pi^{\prime}\cdot X

Claim 1
.

There exist a
\Sigma
-algebra
\mathfrak{A}
, a valuation
\varsigma
and a derivation using rule
(\curlywedge{\bf var})
such that

\Upsilon\vdash\pi\curlywedge\pi^{\prime}\cdot X
but
\llbracket\Upsilon\vdash\pi\curlywedge\pi^{\prime}\cdot X\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
is not valid.

First of all, fix an enumeration of
\mathbb{V}=\{X_{1},X_{2},\ldots\}
and
\mathbb{A}=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots\}
. Now, let’s give the nominal set
\mathcal{P}_{\tt fin}(\mathbb{A})
a
\Sigma
-algebra structure.

(1)

Consider the domain of
\mathfrak{A}
as the nominal set
\mathscr{A}=(\mathcal{P}_{\tt fin}(\mathbb{A}),\cdot)
presented in Example
2.3
(
2
).

(2)

Define
\mathtt{atom}^{\mathfrak{A}}\colon\mathbb{A}\to\mathcal{P}_{\tt fin}(\mathbb{A})
by
\mathtt{atom}^{\mathfrak{A}}(a)=\{a\}
.

(3)

Define
\mathtt{abs}^{\mathfrak{A}}:\mathbb{A}\times\mathcal{P}_{\tt fin}(\mathbb{A})\to\mathcal{P}_{\tt fin}(\mathbb{A})
by
\mathtt{abs}^{\mathfrak{A}}(a,B)=B\setminus\{a\}
.

(4)

For each term-former
{\tt f}\colon n\in\Sigma
, we associate a map
f^{\mathfrak{A}}\colon\mathcal{P}_{\tt fin}(\mathbb{A})^{n}\to\mathcal{P}_{\tt fin}(\mathbb{A})
defined by
f^{\mathfrak{A}}(B_{1},\ldots,B_{n})=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}B_{i}.

It is easy to see that the functions
\mathtt{atom}^{\mathfrak{A}},\mathtt{abs}^{\mathfrak{A}},f^{\mathfrak{A}}
are equivariant.

Define the valuation
\varsigma:\mathbb{V}\to\mathcal{P}_{\tt fin}(\mathbb{A})
by
\varsigma(X_{i})=\{a_{i},a_{i+1}\}
. Now, let’s build a derivation using the rule
(\curlywedge{\bf var})
and interpret it in
\mathfrak{A}
. Consider
\Upsilon=\{(a_{1}\ a_{2})\curlywedge X_{1},(a_{3}\ a_{4})\curlywedge X_{1}\}
and write
\gamma_{1}=(a_{1}\ a_{2})
and
\gamma_{2}=(a_{3}\ a_{4})
. Take
\pi=(a_{1}\ a_{3})
. Hence

\mathtt{dom}(\pi)=\{a_{1},a_{3}\}\subseteq\{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3},a_{4}\}=\mathtt{dom}(\gamma_{1})\cup\mathtt{dom}(\gamma_{2}).

So
\Upsilon\vdash\pi\curlywedge X_{1}
is derivable. All we need to do now is to verify the validity of
\llbracket\Upsilon\vdash\pi\curlywedge X_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
. In order to so, we need to prove that

If
\llbracket\Upsilon\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
is valid then
\pi\curlywedge_{\tt sem}\llbracket X_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
.

First, observe that
\llbracket\Upsilon\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
is in fact valid:

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\cdot\llbracket X_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}\cdot\{a_{1},a_{2}\}=\{a_{1},a_{2}\}=\llbracket X_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}\cdot\llbracket X_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{2}\cdot\{a_{1},a_{2}\}=\{a_{1},a_{2}\}=\llbracket X_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}.

However,

\displaystyle\pi\cdot\llbracket X_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}

\displaystyle=\pi\cdot\varsigma(X_{1})

\displaystyle=\pi\cdot\{a_{1},a_{2}\}

\displaystyle=\{a_{3},a_{2}\}\neq\{a_{1},a_{2}\}=\varsigma(X_{1})=\llbracket X_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}.

3.3.2
Non-strong Axioms.

Let
\mathtt{C}
be the commutativity axiom:
\mathtt{C}=\{\vdash X+Y=Y+X\}
. Consider the
\Sigma
-algebra with domain
\overline{\mathbb{F}}(\mathtt{C},\Sigma)
, that is the set of ground nominal terms quotiented by
\mathtt{C}
and
\alpha
-equality. Let
[t]_{\mathtt{C}}
denote the equivalence class of
t\in\mathbb{F}(\Sigma)
modulo
\alpha,\mathtt{C}
.

Consider the same permutations
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\pi
as above, so again we can derive
\Upsilon\vdash\pi\curlywedge X_{1}
.

Take
\varsigma(X_{1})=[a_{1}+a_{2}]_{\mathtt{C}}
.
Then
\llbracket\Upsilon\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
is valid:

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\cdot\llbracket X_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}=[a_{2}+a_{1}]_{\mathtt{C}}=\llbracket X_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}\qquad\gamma_{2}\cdot\llbracket X_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}=[a_{2}+a_{1}]_{\mathtt{C}}=\llbracket X_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}.

However,
\pi\not\mathrel{\curlywedge}_{\tt sem}\llbracket X_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
since
\pi\cdot\llbracket X_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}=[a_{3}+a_{2}]_{\mathtt{C}}\not=[a_{1}+a_{2}]_{\mathtt{C}}=\llbracket X_{1}\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
.

Remark 3.19
.

\overline{\mathbb{F}}(\mathtt{C},\Sigma)
is not a strong nominal set: note that
(a\ b)\curlywedge_{\tt sem}[a+b]_{\mathtt{C}}
, since
(a\ b)\cdot[a+b]_{\mathtt{C}}=[a+b]_{\mathtt{C}}
, but
(a\ b)\not\mathrel{\curlywedge}_{\tt sem}a
, i.e.,
(a\ b)\notin{\tt pfix}(\mathtt{supp}([a+b]_{\mathtt{C}}))={\tt pfix}(\{a,b\})
.

4
Recovering Soundness: Strong Models

To avoid the problems discussed in the previous section, we now focus on strong models. With these models, it is possible to ensure soundness.

Theorem 4.1

(Soundness for strong
\Sigma
-algebras)
.

Suppose
{\tt T}=(\Sigma,Ax)
is a theory. Then the following hold:

(1)

If
\Upsilon\vdash_{\tt T}\pi\curlywedge t
then
\Upsilon\vDash_{\tt T}^{s}\pi\curlywedge t
.

(2)

If
\Upsilon\vdash_{\tt T}t=u
then
\Upsilon\vDash_{\tt T}^{s}t=u
.

Proof 4.2
.

The proof is done by induction on the derivation of
\Upsilon\vdash\pi\curlywedge t
, focusing on the last rule applied. Here, we demonstrate the most interesting case, where the last rule is (
\curlywedge
var
):

\mathtt{dom}(\pi^{\pi^{\prime-1}})\subseteq\mathtt{dom}(\mathtt{perm}(\Upsilon|_{X}))

(\curlywedge\textbf{var})

\Upsilon\vdash\pi\curlywedge\pi^{\prime}\cdot X

Suppose that
\llbracket\Upsilon\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
is valid. We need to show that
\pi\cdot\llbracket\pi^{\prime}\cdot X\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}=\llbracket\pi^{\prime}\cdot X\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
.

If
\mathtt{perm}(\Upsilon|_{X})=\{\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}\}
, then from the inclusion
\mathtt{dom}(\pi^{\pi^{\prime-1}})\subseteq\mathtt{dom}(\mathtt{perm}(\Upsilon|_{X}))
we get

\mathtt{dom}(\pi^{\pi^{\prime-1}})\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathtt{dom}(\gamma_{i}).

Moreover, the validity of
\llbracket\Upsilon\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
implies that
\gamma_{i}\curlywedge_{\tt sem}\llbracket X\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
for every
\gamma_{i}\curlywedge X\in\Upsilon
. Thus,
\pi^{\pi^{\prime-1}}\cdot\llbracket X\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}=\llbracket X\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
by Theorem
3.6
. The result follows by noticing that
\pi^{\pi^{\prime-1}}\cdot\llbracket X\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}=\llbracket X\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}\iff\pi\cdot\llbracket\pi^{\prime}\cdot X\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}=\llbracket\pi^{\prime}\cdot X\rrbracket^{\mathfrak{A}}_{\varsigma}
.

4.1
Strong Axioms

Example
3.3.2
demonstrates that there exist theories
{\tt T}
for which
\overline{\mathbb{F}}({\tt T},\Sigma)
is not a strong nominal set. This observation rises the following question: are there theories
{\tt T}
for which
\overline{\mathbb{F}}({\tt T},\Sigma)
forms a strong nominal set? We propose to characterise such theories by defining a notion of
strong axiom
, and we will refer to them as
strong theories
.

In the definition below, let
<_{lex}
be the lexicographic ordering of the positions in a term: e.g.,
1.2<_{lex}2.2
,
1.1<_{lex}1.2
.

Given a term
t
, we can order the occurrences of variables in
t
according to their position: we write
X<_{t}Y
if
X
occurs in
t
at a position
p
and
Y
occurs in
t
at a position
q
such that
p<_{lex}q
.

We say that a term
t
is well-ordered if
<_{t}
is a strict partial order, i.e., there is no
X,Y\in\mathtt{Var}(t)
such that
X<_{t}Y
and
Y<_{t}X
. We will only consider axioms with well-ordered first-order terms.

Definition 4.3
.

An axiom
\Upsilon\vdash t=u
is strong if the following hold:

(1)

t
and
u
are first-order terms (i.e., they are built using function symbols and variables), and
\Upsilon=\emptyset
;

(2)

u
and
t
are well-ordered;

(3)

the order of the variables that occur in
t
and in
u
is compatible: i.e., if
X<_{t}Y
then it is not the case that
Y<_{u}X
.

Note that it could be that the variables
X
and
Y
occur in
t
and not in
u
, the important fact is that there are no disagreements in the ordering in
t
and
u
.

Condition (
1
) excludes axioms such as
Ax=\{~\vdash a=b\}
,
Ax=\{~\vdash a+b=b+a\}
and
Ax=\{~\vdash\mathtt{f}([a]X,[b]Y)=\mathtt{g}([b]X,[a]Y)\}
. Condition (
2
) excludes axioms such as distributivity
\mathtt{D}=\{~\vdash X*(Y+Z)=X*Y+X*Z\}
. Permutative theories, where axioms have the form
~\vdash\mathtt{f}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\mathtt{f}(X_{\rho(1)},\ldots,X_{\rho(n)})
, for some permutation
\rho
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, do not satisfy the definition either, i.e. permutative axioms are not strong.

Example 4.4

(Strong Axioms)
.

The following axioms (and their combinations) are strong:
Associativity

5
Recovering Soundness: Alternative Approaches

In the previous section we proved soundness with respect to strong models (where carriers are strong nominal sets). Here
our goal is to recover soundness while using a nominal set semantics. For this, we need to address the mismatch between the derivations and the notion of validity. We can proceed in different ways:

(1)

we can change the form of the judgements and adapt the rules to ensure only the ones that are valid in all models can be derived; or

(2)

we do not change the judgements but we change the derivation rules to prevent the derivation of judgements that are not valid in all models.

The challenge with the second approach is to ensure the system is sufficiently powerful to derive all the valid judgements. The challenge with the first is to ensure that we do not lose expressive power. In this section we show how to address these challenges: we define two sound systems for nominal algebra with fixed-point constraints and nominal set semantics, one with similar rules as before but stronger judgements and another with the same judgements as before but stronger rules.

5.2
Using More Powerful Rules

In this section, we discuss an alternative approach to nominal algebra with fixed-point constraints that is also sound for all nominal set models. The changes proposed in
Section

5.1
focused on restricting the form of the fixed-point contexts, which became equivalent to freshness contexts. Alternatively, we can keep the judgements and rules in

Suppose

(2)

\Upsilon\vDash_{\tt T}t=u

.

In both cases, the proof is by induction on the rule applied. The interesting case is for the rule

.

Then the derivation is

\pi^{\rho^{-1}}\in\langle\mathtt{perm}(\Upsilon|_{X})\rangle

6
Discussion, Applications and Future Work

In the standard nominal approach, using freshness constraints, nominal unification problems
[
22
]
, denoted
{\sf Pr}
, are finite sets of constraints of the form
s\approx^{?}t
and
a\#^{?}t
. Solutions to (nominal) unification problems are pairs
(\Delta,\sigma)
consisting of a freshness context
\Delta
and a substitution
\sigma
such that: (i)
\Delta\vdash s\sigma\approx t\sigma
, for each
s\approx^{?}t\in{\sf Pr}
; (ii)
\Delta\vdash a\#t\sigma
, for each
s\#^{?}t\in{\sf Pr}
; and (iii)
\Delta\vdash X\sigma\approx X\sigma\sigma
, for all
X\in\mathtt{Var}({\sf Pr})
. Derivability in items (i)-(iii) is established by the rules in Figure
3
.

We are interested in nominal equational unification problems, that is, in solving equations in the ground term algebra when in addition to
\alpha
-equivalence and freshness constraints, one is also interested in equational theories (such as commutativity, associativity, etc). Nominal equational unification problems, denoted
{\sf Pr}_{\tt E}
, are finite sets of constraints
s\approx_{\tt E}^{?}t
and
a\#^{?}t
. Solutions of
{\sf Pr}_{\tt E}
are defined in terms of
\approx_{\tt E}
, as expected, and rely on extending the rules in Figure
3
with the appropriate rules for the equational theory
{\tt E}
. For example, in the case of the commutative theory
C
: first we need to specify the commutative function symbol
\mathtt{f^{C}}
and the axiom that establishes the commutativity for this symbol
{\tt C}=\{~\vdash\mathtt{f^{C}}(X,Y)=\mathtt{f^{C}}(Y,X)\}
. Now we extend and modify the rules of
Figure

3
as follows: (i) replace
\approx
for
\approx_{\tt C}
in all the rules;
(ii) add the rule
\frac{\Delta\vdash t_{1}\approx_{\tt C}s_{i}\hskip 8.19447pt\Delta\vdash t_{2}\approx_{\tt C}s_{3-i}}{\Delta\vdash\mathtt{f^{C}}(t_{1},t_{2})\approx_{\tt C}\mathtt{f^{C}}(s_{1},s_{2})}
. Together, these rules implement
\alpha,{\tt C}
-equality.

Nominal unification algorithms are defined in terms of simplification rules that can be seen as bottom-up applications of derivation rules defining
\approx
and
\#
. More details can be found in
[
3
]
.

Example 6.1
.

Consider the nominal
C
-unification problem
{\sf Pr}_{\tt C}=\{\mathtt{f^{C}}(X,Y)\approx_{\tt C}^{?}\mathtt{f^{C}}(c,(a\ b)\cdot X)\}
. This problem reduces to two simpler problems:

\displaystyle\mathbin{\rotatebox[origin={r}]{30.0}{$\Longrightarrow$}}\{X\approx_{\tt C}^{?}c,Y\approx_{\tt C}^{?}(a\ b)\cdot X\}

\displaystyle{\sf Pr}_{\tt C}

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow\{Y\approx_{\tt C}^{?}c,X\approx_{\tt C}^{?}(a\ b)\cdot X\}

The top branch has a solution
\{X\mapsto c,Y\mapsto c\}
. The bottom branch is more problematic due to the fixed-point equation

\{X\approx_{\tt C}^{?}(a\ b)\cdot X\}
.

We have seen in previous works on nominal unification (without equational theories) that the usual simplification rule for fixed-point equations on variables is

\pi\cdot X\approx\pi^{\prime}\cdot X\Longrightarrow ds(\pi,\pi^{\prime})\#X
.
However, this rule is not complete for the commutative case, since we lose solutions such as
X\mapsto\mathtt{f^{C}}(a,b)
,
X\mapsto\mathtt{f^{C}}(\mathtt{f^{C}}(a,b),\mathtt{f^{C}}(a,b))
,
X\mapsto\forall[c]g(\mathtt{f^{C}}(a,b),c)
, etc. We have shown in
[
2
]
that there are infinite independent solutions for this problem.

Currently, there are two approaches to solving nominal equational problems:

(1)

Do not attempt to solve fixed-point equations and leave them as part of solutions
[
3
]
. Thus, solutions of unification problems would be triples
(\Delta,\sigma,P)
, where
P
is a finite set of fixed-point equations of the form
X\approx_{E}\pi\cdot X
, and we can use extension of
Figure

3
to validate solutions.

(2)

Use a different and more expressive formalism, using fixed-point constraints that capture fixed-point equations
[
4
]
. Solutions to unification problems are again pairs
(\Upsilon,\sigma)
, but with fixed-point contexts, and solutions are validated using the rules in
Figure

1
taking into account the axioms defining the theory
{\tt E}
.

First, with the approach (i) the solutions to Example
6.1
are expressed by the triples
(\emptyset,\{X\mapsto c,Y\mapsto c\},\emptyset)
and
(\emptyset,\{Y\mapsto c\},\{X\approx_{\tt C}^{?}(a\ b)\cdot X\})
. Second, with the approach (ii) the solutions to the problem in Example
6.1
are expressed by the pairs
(\emptyset,\{X\mapsto c,Y\mapsto c\})
and
(\{(a\ b)\curlywedge X\},\{Y\mapsto c\}\})
, and concrete instances
\delta
of
X
that validate
(a\ b)\curlywedge X
, when composed with
\{Y\mapsto c\}
(i.e.
\{Y\mapsto c\}\delta
), are also solutions to the initial problem.

7
Conclusion

We formalised the intentional semantics for fixed-point constraints and verified that strong nominal sets are a sound denotational semantics for nominal algebra with fixed-point constraints. We proposed alternatives to regain soundness for the whole class of nominal sets, by proposing more powerful rules or stronger judgements and new rules. Our developments provide a sound approach for solving nominal equational problems modulo strong theories. However, the best approach to deal with non-strong theories, such as the permutative theories, still have to be investigated. Investigations towards completeness are under development and will be reported in future work.
