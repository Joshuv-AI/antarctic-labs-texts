Matrix majorization in large samples
Muhammad Usman Farooq 1 , Tobias Fritz 2 , Erkka Haapasalo 3 , Marco Tomamichel 3,4

Abstract
. One tuple of probability vectors is more informative than another tuple when there exists a single stochastic matrix transforming the probability vectors of the first tuple into the probability vectors of the other. This is called matrix majorization. Solving an open problem raised by Mu et al , we show that if certain monotones — namely multivariate extensions of Rényi divergences — are strictly ordered between the two tuples, then for sufficiently large n n , there exists a stochastic matrix taking the n n -fold Kronecker power of each input distribution to the n n -fold Kronecker power of the corresponding output distribution. The same conditions, with non-strict ordering for the monotones, are also necessary for such matrix majorization in large samples. Our result also gives conditions for the existence of a sequence of statistical maps that asymptotically (with vanishing error) convert a single copy of each input distribution to the corresponding output distribution with the help of a catalyst that is returned unchanged. Allowing for transformation with arbitrarily small error, we find conditions that are both necessary and sufficient for such catalytic matrix majorization. We derive our results by building on a general algebraic theory of preordered semirings recently developed by one of the authors. This also allows us to recover various existing results on majorization in large samples and in the catalytic regime as well as relative majorization in a unified manner.

Introduction

Statistical experiments
are at the basis of much of classical statistics and its applications, such as hypothesis testing
[
15
,
26
,

Given two statistical experiments, one may naturally ask which one is more “informative”, for example by asking which one yields the higher expected payoff in the varied tests one might apply. Thus, the comparison of statistical experiments becomes a highly relevant question, going back to the work by Blackwell
[
3
,
4

[

Blackwell’s formalization of the idea that a statistical experiment
P=\{p^{\vartheta}\}_{\vartheta\in\Theta}

on some (standard Borel) measurable space is more informative than another statistical experiment

is by requiring that there be a statistical map

One may think of this equation as saying that the experiment

can be simulated by first executing

than with

majorizes

We are particularly interested in the case where both the outcome space for the probability measures and the parameter space

8

with non-negative entries majorizes another matrix

In addition to studying majorization between single statistical experiments, we can also look for conditions when one experiment is more informative than another when each is repeated independently. In the language of matrix majorization, we are interested in whether the condition

(1)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((p^{(1)})^{\otimes n},\ldots,(p^{(d)})^{\otimes n}}}\right)\succeq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left((q^{(1)})^{\otimes n},\ldots,(q^{(d)})^{\otimes n}}}\right)

[

28

2.
Preliminaries

With our upcoming applications in mind, in this section we review the mathematical prerequisites pertaining to probability vectors and preordered semirings, and we review existing separation theorems (
Vergleichsstellensätze
) which characterize the induced large-sample preorders on sufficiently well-behaved preordered semirings.

2.1.
Basic notation

We present some basic notation and definitions for finite-outcome probability distributions that we will use throughout this work. We follow the convention
\mathbb{N}:=\{1,2,\ldots\}
. We denote by
\log{}
and, respectively,

the logarithm and, respectively, exponential function with respect to a fixed but otherwise undetermined basis greater than 1. For every

\|x\|_{1}:=|x_{1}|+\cdots+|x_{n}|

(9)

\displaystyle x\oplus y

\displaystyle=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}),

(10)

\displaystyle x\otimes y

\displaystyle=(x_{i}y_{j})_{i,j},

where the latter one is viewed as a vector of length

For every

2.2.
Preordered semirings and separation theorems

Definition 1

(preordered commutative semiring)
.

A
preordered commutative semiring
is a tuple (
S,+,\cdot,0,1,\trianglelefteq
) where
S
is a set equipped with two binary operations
+
,
\cdot
that are both commutative and associative, have respective neutral elements
0
and
1
, and satisfy

(13)

0\cdot x=0,\qquad(x+y)\cdot z=x\cdot z+y\cdot z

for all
x,y,z\in S
. The preorder
\trianglelefteq
(a reflexive and transitive binary relation) is required to be such that
a\trianglelefteq b
implies
a+c\trianglelefteq b+c
and
ac\trianglelefteq bc
for all
a,b,c\in S
.

We will use the notation
a\trianglelefteq b
and
b\trianglerighteq a
interchangeably.
Note that unlike in rings, subtraction is typically not possible in semirings.
From now on and throughout the paper, we use ‘semiring’ as shorthand for ‘commutative semiring’.

Definition 2

(preordered semidomain
[
12
]
)
.

A preordered semiring
S
is a
preordered semidomain
if it has no zero divisors, i.e.,
xy=0
for
x,y\in S
implies that
x=0
or
y=0
, and if
x=0
is the only
x\in S
with
0\trianglelefteq x\trianglelefteq 0
.

Moreover, a semiring
S
is
zerosumfree
if
x+y=0
for
x,y\in S
implies
x=0=y
. Further, we define an equivalence relation
\sim
in a preordered semiring
S
via
x\sim y
if there is a finite sequence
z_{0},\ldots,z_{n}\in S
such that for all
i
we have
z_{i}\trianglelefteq z_{i+1}
or
z_{i}\trianglerighteq z_{i+1}
, and also
z_{0}=x
and
z_{n}=y
, or equivalently if there is a zigzag
x\trianglelefteq z_{1}\trianglerighteq z_{2}\trianglelefteq\cdots\trianglerighteq z_{n}\trianglelefteq y
. In other words,
\sim
is the equivalence relation generated by
\trianglelefteq
.

We denote by
\mathbb{R}_{+}
the preordered semiring of non-negative real numbers equipped with the usual sum and product and with the usual
\leq
-relation as preorder. We further define the semiring of
tropical reals

\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}
as coinciding with
\mathbb{R}_{+}
as a preordered set, and where the multiplication and ordering of
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}
are the usual ones, but where the sum

is given by

instead. We further utilize
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}
and
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}
, where the superscript
op
stands for reversing the preorder. (Reversing the preorder on a preordered semiring produces another preordered semiring.)

An important property that a preordered semiring
S
may or may not have is
polynomial growth
, defined as follows.

Definition 3

(polynomial growth
[
12
]
)
.

For a preordered semiring
S
, we say that
S
is of

if it possesses a
power universal pair

(u_{-},u_{+})
, i.e., nonzero

such that:

•

u_{-}\trianglelefteq u_{+}
,

•

For all non-zero

such that

, there is
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
u_{-}^{k}y\trianglelefteq u_{+}^{k}x
.

If

has a multiplicative inverse, this pair can be replaced with a single

u\trianglerighteq 1
which has the property that, whenever
x\trianglelefteq y
for non-zero

, there is
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
y\trianglelefteq xu^{k}
.

Definition 4

(monotone homomorphism)
.

Given a preordered semiring
S
, let
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}\}
. Then a
monotone homomorphism
to
\mathbb{K}
is a map
\Phi:S\to\mathbb{K}
such that for all

,

•

\Phi
is additive, i.e.
\Phi(a+b)=\Phi(a)+\Phi(b)
,

•

\Phi
is multiplicative, i.e.
\Phi(ab)=\Phi(a)\cdot\Phi(b)
,

•

\Phi
is normalized, i.e.
\Phi(0)=0
and
\Phi(1)=1
,

•

and
\Phi

As we will see, the last condition specializes to that of Schur convexity in the case of the majorization semiring.

Finally, when
S
is a preordered semiring, we say that

are

if

(14)

\displaystyle x^{n}\trianglelefteq y^{n}

for all sufficiently large

. Moreover,

\displaystyle xa\trianglelefteq ya.

Theorem 5

(Theorem 7.15 in
[
12
]
)
.

Let

be a zerosumfree preordered semiring of polynomial growth with a power universal element

and such that

. Let

is power universal. If, for every
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}\}
and every monotone homomorphism
\Phi:S\to\mathbb{K}
such that

, then

(a)

x^{n}\trianglelefteq y^{n}
for all sufficiently large

, and

(b)

ax\trianglelefteq ay

Remark 6
.

For a tropical homomorphism
\Phi:S\to\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}
as in the theorem, every power
\Phi(\cdot)^{\lambda}

If instead

Theorem

The intended applications of the theory presented here deal with questions of transformability of one finite statistical experiment (typically described by a matrix) to another in large samples or catalytically. This comparison of experiments gives the preorder we study for the majority of this work. In this framework, the monotone homomorphisms and derivations giving conditions for the large-sample and catalytic transformability in the above separation theorems can be interpreted as monotones quantifying the information content in an experiment. Later we will briefly discuss the applications to quantum theory where these maps should be thought of as resource monotones for quantum state resources.

is the number of rows of
P
.
Similarly, in cases (iii) and (iv), we find that

(66)

\Phi(P)=\max_{1\leq i\leq n}\prod_{k=1}^{d}(p^{(k)}_{i})^{\alpha_{k}}.

Since the functions of this form are indeed clearly homomorphisms, it only remains to determine for which parameter tuples they are monotone and nondegenerate. One easily sees

(67)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&\cdots&1\\
1&\cdots&1\end{array}}}\right)\succeq\big(2\,\cdots\,2\big)\succeq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&\cdots&1\\
1&\cdots&1\end{array}}}\right),

implying, due to monotonicity, that

(68)

\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&\cdots&1\\
1&\cdots&1\end{array}}}\right)=\Phi(2\,\cdots\,2).

In cases (i) and (ii), this means that
\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{d}=1
and, in cases (iii) and (iv), this means that
\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}=0
.

Let us focus on (i) and (ii) for the moment. There, we now know that
\Phi
must be of the form
f_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}
with some parameters
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}\in A
. Let us now show that
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}\in A_{-}
in case (i) and
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}\in A_{+}
in case (ii) are necessary. For
P,Q\in\mathcal{V}^{d}_{n}
and
t\in[0,1]
, and with the
(n\times 2n)
-column-stochastic matrix
T
defined by
T(a\oplus b)=a+b
for all
a,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, note that in case (i) we have

(69)

\displaystyle\Phi\big(tP+(1-t)Q\big)={}

\displaystyle\Phi\big(T\big[tP\boxplus(1-t)Q\big]\big)

(70)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle\Phi\big(tP\boxplus(1-t)Q\big)

(71)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\Phi(tP)+\Phi\big((1-t)Q\big)

(72)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle t\Phi(P)+(1-t)\Phi(Q),

i.e.,
\Phi
is convex. The final equality holds due to
\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{d}=1
. Similarly, in case (ii), we find that
\Phi
must be concave. Let us define the single-row function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
through

(73)

\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}):=\Phi\big((x_{1}\,\cdots\,x_{d})\big)=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}

for all
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}
. Naturally,
\varphi
must also be convex in case (i) and concave in case (ii). To analyze the convexity properties of
\varphi
, let us compute its Hessian matrix,

(74)

\displaystyle H_{\varphi}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial x_{i}\,\partial x_{j}}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})}}\right)_{i,j=1}^{d}

(75)

\displaystyle=\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{x_{i}x_{j}}}}\right)_{i,j=1}^{d}\star\underbrace{(\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}-\delta_{i,j}\alpha_{i})_{i,j=1}^{d}}_{=:\mathcal{A}}

where
\star
denotes the Schur product, i.e., the entry-wise product of matrices of the same shape. Because Schur multiplying with a positive semi-definite matrix preserves the semi-definiteness of a matrix and
\varphi
takes positive values, we find that, upon Schur multiplying with the positive semi-definite matrix
(x_{i}x_{j})_{i,j=1}^{d}
, that
H_{\varphi}
is positive (respectively negative) semi-definite if and only if
\mathcal{A}
is positive (respectively negative) semi-definite. According to Lemma 8 of
[
21
]
, we have:

•

\mathcal{A}
is positive semi-definite if and only if
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}\in A_{-}
.

•

\mathcal{A}
is negative semi-definite if and only if
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}\in A_{+}
.

Therefore the convexity (concavity) of
\varphi
implies the conditions for the parameters
\textstyle\alpha
mentioned in the claim, where the case that
\textstyle\alpha
is a standard basis vector
e_{k}
can be excluded since then
f_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}
is given by
f_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}(P)=\|p^{(k)}\|_{1}
, which is degenerate.

Next we show that all these homomorphisms are indeed monotone and nondegenerate. So for case (i), let
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}\in A_{-}
, in which case we know per the above that
\varphi
is convex. Let
P\in\mathcal{V}^{d}_{n}
with rows
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
and let
T=(T_{i,j})_{i,j}
be an
(m\times n)
-stochastic matrix, for which we assume no zero rows without loss of generality and write
T_{i}:=\sum_{j=1}^{n}T_{i,j}
. We write
Q:=TP\in\mathcal{V}^{d}_{m}
with rows
q_{1},\dots,q_{m}
. Since
\varphi
also preserves scalar multiplication due to
\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{d}=1
, we have

(76)

\displaystyle f_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}(Q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varphi(q_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}T_{i,j}p_{j}}}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{i}\,\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{T_{i,j}}{T_{i}}p_{j}}}\right)

(77)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}T_{i}\,\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{T_{i,j}}{T_{i}}\varphi(p_{j})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}T_{i,j}\varphi(p_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\varphi(p_{j})=f_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}(P),

where the second step is by the preservation of scalar multiplication and the inequality follows by the convexity of
\varphi
. Thus,
f_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}:S^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is indeed monotone for
\alpha\in A_{-}
. For case (ii), the same argument with the inequality the other way shows that
f_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}:S^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}
is monotone for all
\alpha\in A_{+}
. To finish the proof in cases (i) and (ii), it remains to prove nondegeneracy whenever
\textstyle\alpha
is not a standard basis vector. Let us show that, whenever there is an index
k
with
\alpha_{k}\neq 0,1
, then
f_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}
is nondegenerate. Define the matrix

(78)

P_{t}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}\frac{1}{2}&\cdots&\frac{1}{2}&1-t&\frac{1}{2}&\cdots&\frac{1}{2}\\[2.0pt]
\frac{1}{2}&\cdots&\frac{1}{2}&t&\frac{1}{2}&\cdots&\frac{1}{2}\end{array}}}\right)\in\mathcal{V}^{d}_{<\infty},

where the nontrivial column is the
k
-th. Note that, whenever
0<s\leq t\leq\frac{1}{2}
, then
P_{s}\succeq P_{t}
. One easily sees that the values

(79)

f_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}(P_{t})=2^{\alpha_{k}-1}\big((1-t)^{\alpha_{k}}+t^{\alpha_{k}}\big)

are not constant in
t
whenever
\alpha_{k}\neq 0,1
, showing that
f_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}
is non-degenerate whenever
\textstyle\alpha
is not a standard basis vector. Thus we have proven the claim as far as cases (i) and (ii) are concerned.

Let us now turn to the tropical cases (iii) and (iv). In case (iii), we can use reasoning analogous to case (i) to show that
\Phi
is quasi-convex, i.e.,

(80)

\Phi\big(tP+(1-t)Q\big)\leq\max\{\Phi(P),\Phi(Q)\}

for all
P,Q\in\mathcal{V}^{d}_{<\infty}
and
t\in(0,1)
. In particular, the single-row function
\varphi
has to be a quasi-convex function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{>0}
. This quasi-convexity is equivalent to convexity of the sub-level sets

(81)

S_{c}:=\{(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}\,|\,x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{d}^{\alpha_{d}}\leq c\}

for all
c>0
. Let us assume that
\alpha_{1}>0
without loss of generality (by permuting columns if necessary). Considering then the first coordinate
x_{1}
as the dependent variable, we observe that
S_{c}
contains precisely all points that are non-strictly below the graph of the function
w:\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

(82)

w(x_{2},\ldots,x_{d})=\frac{x_{2}^{\mu_{2}}\cdots x_{d}^{\mu_{d}}}{c},

where
\mu_{k}:=-\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{1}}
for
k=2,\dots,d
, which are now coefficients that sum to
1
again. As we already saw earlier, the Hessian matrix of
w
is easily found to be

(83)

H_{w}(x_{2},\ldots,x_{d})=w(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{x_{i}x_{j}}}}\right)_{i,j=2}^{d}\star\underbrace{(\mu_{i}\mu_{j}-\delta_{i,j}\mu_{i})_{i,j=2}^{d}}_{=:\,\mathcal{B}}.

So for the sets
S_{c}
to be convex,
w
must be concave, or equivalently
H_{w}
must be negative semi-definite. This is equivalent, by the same argument as above, to
\mu_{2},\dots,\mu_{d}\geq 0
, or equivalently
\alpha_{k}\leq 0
for
k\geq 2
. Therefore
\Phi=f^{\mathbb{T}}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}
is monotone only if
\alpha\in B_{-}
. Since
f^{\mathbb{T}}_{0}
is the homomorphism that simply maps all nonzero
P
to
1
, this homomorphism is degenerate, and we can conclude
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}\in B_{-}\setminus\{0\}
. This establishes one direction of (iii).

For the same and only direction in case (iv), let now
\Phi:S^{d}\to\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}
be a nondegenerate monotone homomorphism. Similarly as in case (iii), we now have

(84)

\displaystyle\Phi\big(tP+(1-t)Q\big)\geq\max\{\Phi(P),\Phi(Q)\}.

for all
P,Q\in\mathcal{V}^{d}_{<\infty}
and
t\in(0,1)
. We next show that, if
P\neq Q
have the same support, then
\Phi(P)=\Phi(Q)
. In that case, we can find
s,t\in(0,1)
and
P^{\prime},Q^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}^{d}_{<\infty}
such that

(85)

sP+(1-s)P^{\prime}=Q,\qquad tQ+(1-t)Q^{\prime}=P.

Thus, according to (
84
), we have

(86)

\Phi(P)\geq\max\{\Phi(Q),\Phi(Q^{\prime})\}\geq\Phi(Q)\geq\max\{\Phi(P),\Phi(P^{\prime})\}\geq\Phi(P),

i.e.,
\Phi(P)=\Phi(Q)
. This means that
\Phi
is simply a function of the support of its argument. But then if
Q
is a stochastic matrix of rank
1
, then

(87)

Q\succeq(1\,\cdots\,1)\succeq Q,

so that
\Phi(Q)=1
. But then if
P\in\mathcal{V}^{d}_{<\infty}
is nonzero, we can find any stochastic matrix of rank
1
with the same support, and therefore conclude
\Phi(P)=1
. Therefore there is no nondegenerate homomorphism to
\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\rm op}
, and case (iv) is finished.

Finally, we need to show that the maps
f^{\mathbb{T}}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}
with
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}\in B_{-}\setminus\{0\}
are nondegenerate monotone homomorphisms
S^{d}\to\mathbb{T}\mathbb{R}_{+}
. The fact that they are homomorphisms is straightforward to verify, so let us focus on monotonicity. Per the above, we know that the single-row function
\varphi
is quasi-convex. We also know that
f^{\mathbb{T}}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}(tP)=f^{\mathbb{T}}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}(P)
for all positive scalars
t
by
\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{d}=0
. Then with the same
P
and
Q=TP
as above in the corresponding proof for case (i), we have a similar argument as given there,

(88)

\displaystyle f^{\mathbb{T}}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}(Q)

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\varphi(q_{i})=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}T_{i,j}p_{j}}}\right)

(89)

\displaystyle=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(T_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{T_{i,j}}{T_{i}}p_{j}}}\right)=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{T_{i,j}}{T_{i}}p_{j}}}\right)

(90)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{1\leq i\leq m}\max_{1\leq j\leq 1}\varphi(p_{j})=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}\varphi(p_{j})=f^{\mathbb{T}}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}(P).

Let us show that all these homomorphisms are non-degenerate if
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}\neq 0
, or equivalently if there is
k
with
\alpha_{k}>0
. With
P_{t}
as in (
78
), we get

(91)

f^{\mathbb{T}}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}(P_{t})=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2(1-t)}}\right)^{\alpha_{k}}

for all

. This is clearly a non-constant function, meaning that
f^{\mathbb{T}}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\alpha$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\alpha$}}}}
is non-degenerate.
∎

Let us now go on to studying the monotone derivations at the degenerate homomorphisms
f_{e_{k}}:S^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
with
f_{e_{k}}(P)=\|p^{(k)}\|
for
k=1,\ldots,d
. For these, the Leibniz rule is

(92)

\Delta(P\boxtimes Q)=\Delta(P)\|q^{(k)}\|_{1}+\|p^{(k)}\|_{1}\Delta(Q).

Turning our attention to the maps
\Delta^{(k)}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\gamma$}}}}
of (
39
), we easily see that these are additive (under
\boxplus
) and satisfy (
92
). We next show that these maps with
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\gamma$}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
are precisely the monotone derivations at
f_{e_{k}}
.

Proposition 14
.

Let
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. On matrices
P\in\mathcal{V}^{d}_{<\infty}
with
\|p^{(k)}\|_{1}\in\mathbb{Q}
, the monotone derivations on
S^{d}
at
f_{e_{k}}
are precisely the
\Delta^{(k)}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\gamma$}}}}
with
\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\gamma$}}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
.

Note that
\Delta^{(k)}_{\mathchoice{\mbox{\uline{$\displaystyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\textstyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptstyle\gamma$}}}{\mbox{\uline{$\scriptscriptstyle\gamma$}}}}
does not actually depend on
\gamma_{k}
, so the value of this component is completely arbitrary.

Proof.

Let us fix
k=1
; the other cases are similar. Let
\Delta:S^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a monotone derivation at
f_{e_{1}}
. Define
\delta:\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{d}\cup\{(0,\ldots,0)\}\to\mathbb{R}
as the one-row function

(93)

\delta(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})=\Delta\big(x_{1}\,\cdots\,x_{d}\big).

We first show that
\delta(x,\ldots,x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
. The Leibniz rule immediately gives that
\delta(0,\ldots,0)=0=\delta(1,\ldots,1)
. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we easily see that

(94)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&\cdots&1\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\cdots&1\end{array}}}\right)\succeq(n\,\cdots\,n)\succeq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&\cdots&1\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\cdots&1\end{array}}}\right)

where the all-1 matrix is
(n\times d)
. Using the monotonicity and additivity of
\Delta
, we have

(95)

\delta(n,\ldots,n)=\Delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}1&\cdots&1\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&\cdots&1\end{array}}}\right)}}\right)=n\delta(1,\ldots,1)=0.

The Leibniz rule then easily gives
\delta(1/n,\ldots,1/n)=-n^{-2}\delta(n,\ldots,n)=0
as well. Finally, for all
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,

(96)

\delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{m}{n},\ldots,\frac{m}{n}}}\right)=m\underbrace{\delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{n},\ldots,\frac{1}{n}}}\right)}_{=0}+\underbrace{\delta(m,\ldots,m)}_{=0}\frac{1}{n}=0,

showing that
\delta(x,\ldots,x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}
.

Let now
p\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
be a column vector of any length
n\in\mathbb{N}
and such that
\|p\|_{1}\in\mathbb{Q}
. Using the easily seen fact that

(97)

(p,\ldots,p)\succeq(\|p\|_{1}\,\cdots\,\|p\|_{1})\succeq(p,\ldots,p),

the monotonicity of
\Delta
, and the vanishing property proven above for
\delta
, we see that

(98)

\Delta(p,\ldots,p)=\delta(\|p\|_{1},\ldots,\|p\|_{1})=0.

Let then
P\in\mathcal{V}^{d}_{n}
be such that
\|p^{(1)}\|_{1}\in\mathbb{Q}
, where we assume full support without loss of generality. Using the Leibniz rule several times and the above vanishing property, we obtain

(99)

\displaystyle\Delta(P)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta(p^{(1)}_{i},\ldots,p^{(d)}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p^{(1)}_{i}\cdot 1,p^{(1)}_{i}\cdot\frac{p^{(2)}_{i}}{p^{(1)}_{i}},\ldots,p^{(1)}_{i}\cdot\frac{p^{(d)}_{i}}{p^{(1)}_{i}}}}\right)

(100)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\delta(p^{(1)}_{i},\ldots,p^{(1)}_{i})+p^{(1)}_{i}\delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,\frac{p^{(2)}_{i}}{p^{(1)}_{i}},\ldots,\frac{p^{(d)}_{i}}{p^{(1)}_{i}}}}\right)}}\right]

(101)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\Delta(p^{(1)},\ldots,p^{(1)})}_{=0}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}p^{(1)}_{i}\delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,\frac{p^{(2)}_{i}}{p^{(1)}_{i}},\ldots,\frac{p^{(d)}_{i}}{p^{(1)}_{i}}}}\right)

(102)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p^{(1)}_{i}\delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,\frac{p^{(2)}_{i}}{p^{(1)}_{i}}\cdot 1,1\cdot\frac{p^{(3)}_{i}}{p^{(1)}_{i}},\ldots,1\cdot\frac{p^{(d)}_{i}}{p^{(1)}_{i}}}}\right)

(103)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p^{(1)}_{i}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,\frac{p^{(2)}_{i}}{p^{(1)}_{i}},1,\ldots,1}}\right)+\delta\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,1,\frac{p^{(3)}_{i}}{p^{(1)}_{i}},\ldots,\frac{p^{(d)}_{i}}{p^{(1)}_{i}}}}\right)}}\right]

(104)

\displaystyle=\cdots=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\ell=2}^{d}p^{(1)}_{i}g_{\ell}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{p^{(\ell)}_{i}}{p^{(1)}_{i}}}}\right),

where, for all
\ell=2,\ldots,d
, we have used

(105)

g_{\ell}(x):=\delta(1,\ldots,1,x,1,\ldots,1),

where
x
appears in the
\ell
-th slot. Using the Leibniz rule, one easily sees that, for all
x,y>0
and all
\ell
,

(106)

g_{\ell}(xy)=g_{\ell}(x)+g_{\ell}(y),

so that
g_{\ell}
satisfies the functional equation associated with the logarithm. We show that
g_{\ell}
is a multiple of the logarithm by showing that it is continuous on
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Let
x,y>0
and

. Using the fact that

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}t&\cdots&t&tx&t&\cdots&t\\
1-t&\cdots&1-t&(1-t)y&1-t&\cdots&1-t\end{array}}}\right)

(109)

\displaystyle\succeq\big(1\,\cdots\,1\,tx+(1-t)y\,1\,\cdots\,1\big),

where
x
and
y
appear in the
\ell
-th column, applying
\Delta
gives, by monotonicity and the derivation property of
\delta
,

(110)

tg_{\ell}(x)+(1-t)g_{\ell}(y)\geq g_{\ell}\big(tx+(1-t)y\big),

where the left-hand side is obtained by the formula (
104
).

Thus,
g_{\ell}
is convex and hence continuous on its domain
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. This means that there are
\gamma_{2},\ldots,\gamma_{d}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(111)

g_{\ell}=-\gamma_{\ell}\log{}

for every
\ell=2,\ldots,d
. We now aim at showing that
\gamma_{\ell}\geq 0
. Defining, for all
t\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, the matrix

(112)

P_{t}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}\frac{1}{2}&\cdots&\frac{1}{2}&1-t&\frac{1}{2}&\cdots&\frac{1}{2}\\[2.0pt]
\frac{1}{2}&\cdots&\frac{1}{2}&t&\frac{1}{2}&\cdots&\frac{1}{2}\end{array}}}\right),

where the entries containing

are in the
\ell
-th column, we note that the function

(113)

\Delta(P_{t})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:MatrDerivForm}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\gamma_{\ell}\log{\frac{1}{4t(1-t)}}

should be non-increasing in

, which implies

. By (

1

Conclusion

In this work we have connected several problems of majorization to the theory of preordered semirings. Using this framework, we have derived new sufficient and generically necessary conditions for large-sample and catalytic matrix majorization, i.e., Blackwell dominance in the finite-outcome setting. In this latter context, we have in particular identified the relevant monotone quantities as
matrix
\textstyle\alpha
-divergences
which can be viewed as a generalization of the one-parameter family of the standard Rényi divergences.

We would like to point out that this theory has some well-known connections not only to problems of classical statistics, but also to
resource theories
in the context of quantum information processing. All basic quantum devices — quantum states, channels, and observables — can also be described as suitable generalizations of statistical experiments (see, e.g.,
[
18
]
). Specifically, in the resource theory of entanglement of pure quantum states, where we may say that a pure (vector) state
\varphi
of a compound system
AB
is ‘above’ another pure state
\psi
if there is a local-operations-and-classical-communication (LOCC) quantum channel
\Phi
such that
\Phi(|\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi|)=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|
. It is well-known
[
22
]
that this happens if and only if the Schmidt vector of
\psi
majorizes that of
\varphi
. Thus one obtains conditions for catalytic LOCC-ordering for pure states from Theorem
41
, as also pointed out in
[
17
]
. Similar results also hold for incoherent transformations of pure states
[
9
]
.

Multiple majorization of quantum states is also a relevant question. This is simply the quantum counterpart of matrix majorization: Given
d
-tuples
(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{d})
and
(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d})
of quantum states (possibly on different systems), we may ask if there is a quantum channel
\Lambda
such that
\Lambda(\rho_{k})=\sigma_{k}
for
k=1,\ldots,d
. The one-shot conditions for this problem can be obtained, e.g., from
[
7
,
24
]
. The algebraic machinery consisting of the
Vergleichsstellensätze
from
[
12
,
13
]
is applicable also in this quantum case: We can build a quantum majorization semiring of polynomial growth in the same way as we did for matrix majorization. In this way, majorization of quantum statistical experiments in large samples or catalytically can be characterized by inequalities involving the monotone homomorphisms and monotone derivations (jointly forming its test spectrum) on the quantum majorization semiring.

The problem with this approach is, however, that the characterization of the test spectrum of the quantum majorization semiring remains, for now, intractable. For one thing, we cannot similarly decompose the additive maps in the quantum case as we did in Propositions
13
and
14
, because the states in the
d
-tuple typically have different eigenbases. Problems arise already in the relative case
d=2

[
27
]
, where an innumerable family of quantum divergences generalizing the classical Rényi divergences has been defined and we do not yet know which of them are actually relevant for majorization questions. Therefore we cannot give specific and explicit conditions for quantum majorization in large samples or catalytically yet, even in the case
d=2
. Some special cases can be investigated with the methodology presented in the current work, but this will be the topic of future research. Also, using relaxed preorders like a quantum version of submajorization may be fruitful, as then one is sometimes able to narrow things down to a manageable family of sufficient conditions
[
23
,
6
]
.

Appendix A
The majorization and submajorization semirings

In these appendices we concentrate on (simple) majorization of probability vectors and rederive some previously established results on large-sample and catalytic majorization in this mode. We will see that, as in the case of matrix majorization, the new algebraic machinery at our disposal is remarkably powerful in questions on simple majorization as well.

A probability vector

Remark 27

Lemma 32
.

Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\Delta:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be such that

(214)

p\succeq_{0,1}q\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\Delta(p)\geq\Delta(q)

for all
p,q\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
.
Suppose that there is a continuous map
\Lambda:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

such that
\Delta(p)=\Lambda(p)
for all
p\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{n}
. Then
\Delta(p)=\Lambda(p)
for all
p\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
with
\|p\|_{1}\in\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof.

We prove the claim by proving the following: For every
p\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
with
\|p\|_{1}\in\mathbb{Q}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there are
q,r\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{n}
such that
q\succeq_{0,1}p\succeq_{0,1}r
and
\|q-r\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon
as well as
\|q-p\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon
. This is enough since applying
\Delta
and
\Lambda
to this inequality gives

(215)

\Lambda(q)=\Delta(q)\geq\Delta(p)\geq\Delta(r)=\Lambda(r),

where we have used the
\succeq_{0,1}
-monotonicity of
\Delta
.
Since the set of nonnegative vectors of fixed normalization is compact, we know that
\Lambda
is uniformly continuous on it. Therefore taking
\varepsilon\to 0
then shows that
\Delta(p)=\Lambda(p)
. Thus it suffices to prove that there are
q
and
r
as above.

To this end, we may freely assume that our given
p=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{m},0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
is already in non-increasing order with
p_{m}>0
, since the monotonicity of
\Delta
implies that
\Delta
is invariant under permutations. By restricting to
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{m}
we may furthermore assume that
m=n
, so that
p
has full support. By rescaling everything by the rational
\|p\|_{1}^{-1}
, we may also assume that
\|p\|_{1}=1
. For given
\varepsilon>0
we put
\delta:=\frac{\varepsilon}{4(n-1)}
. For every
i=1,\ldots,n-1
, we fix a rational number
q_{i}\in[p_{i},p_{i}+\delta]
such that
q_{1}\geq\cdots\geq q_{n-1}
. We then define the rational number
q_{n}:=1-\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}q_{i}
, which by
\|p\|_{1}=1
satisfies

(216)

p_{n}-(n-1)\delta\leq q_{n}\leq p_{n}.

For sufficiently small
\varepsilon
this in particular guarantees that
q_{n}>0
, and therefore
q=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n})
is a probability vector with
\|q\|_{0}=\|p\|_{0}
. The entries of
q
are already in non-increasing order as well, and we have
\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}
for all
k=1,\ldots,n-1
by construction. Thus
p\preceq_{0,1}q
. Moreover,

(217)

\|q-p\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(q_{i}-p_{i})+p_{n}-q_{n}\leq 2(n-1)\delta=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

To construct the desired vector
r
, let us also assume that
p
is not uniform, since we can just take
r=p
in the uniform case. Let then
\ell
be the unique index with
p_{\ell}>p_{\ell+1}=p_{n}
(i.e., the final
n-\ell
entries in
p
coincide with the lowest probability
p_{n}
). Let us define

(218)

\delta^{\prime}:=\min\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\frac{\varepsilon}{4\ell},\frac{n-\ell}{n}(p_{\ell}-p_{\ell+1})}}\right\}.

We then fix, for all
i=1,\ldots,\ell
, positive rational numbers
r_{i}
such that
\frac{n-\ell-1}{n-\ell}\delta^{\prime}\leq p_{i}-r_{i}\leq\delta^{\prime}
and
r_{1}\geq\cdots\geq r_{\ell}
. We also let

(219)

r_{\ell+1}=\cdots=r_{n}=\frac{1}{n-\ell}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}r_{i}}}\right)\leq p_{n}+\frac{\ell}{n-\ell}\delta^{\prime}.

The definition of
\delta^{\prime}
guarantees that

(220)

r_{\ell}\geq p_{\ell}-\delta^{\prime}\geq p_{\ell+1}+\frac{\ell}{n-\ell}\delta^{\prime}\geq r_{\ell+1}=\cdots=r_{n},

so that the whole vector
r:=(r_{1},\ldots,r_{n})
is in non-increasing order as well and all its entries are non-zero. We immediately see that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}r_{i}
for all
k=1,\ldots,\ell
. If
\ell=n-1
, this already means that
p\succeq_{0,1}r
. We now show the same in general by proving the remaining inequalities. For
j=1,\ldots,n-\ell-1
,

(221)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\ell+j}r_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}r_{i}+\sum_{i=\ell+1}^{\ell+j}r_{i}

(222)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}p_{i}-\ell\frac{n-\ell-1}{n-\ell}\delta^{\prime}+\sum_{i=\ell+1}^{\ell+j}p_{i}+j\frac{\ell}{n-\ell}\delta^{\prime}

(223)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell+j}p_{i}-\ell\frac{n-\ell-1-j}{n-\ell}\delta^{\prime}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\ell+j}p_{i},

where we use

in the final inequality, which is the last case that needs to be considered before the normalization equation

. Thus,

. Moreover,

(224)

\|p-r\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}(p_{i}-r_{i})+\sum_{i=\ell+1}^{n}(r_{i}-p_{i})\leq 2\ell\delta^{\prime}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2},

where the final inequality follows from the definition of
\delta^{\prime}
.

To sum up, we have

and
\|q-r\|_{1}\leq\|q-p\|_{1}+\|p-r\|_{1}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=\varepsilon
, and hence the auxiliary result is proven.

\displaystyle 0
