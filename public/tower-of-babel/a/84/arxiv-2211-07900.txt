Parameterized Inapproximability of the Minimum Distance Problem over all Fields and the Shortest Vector Problem in all ℓ p \ell_{p} Norms Thanks: A preliminary version of this work appeared at STOC 2023 [ BCGR23 ] .
Huck Bennett, Mahdi Cheraghchi, Venkatesan Guruswami, João Ribeiro

Abstract
We prove that the Minimum Distance Problem ( MDP ) on linear codes over any fixed finite field and parameterized by the input distance bound is 𝖶 ⁡ [ 1 ] \mathsf{W}[1] -hard to approximate within any constant factor. We also prove analogous results for the parameterized Shortest Vector Problem ( SVP \mathrm{SVP} ) on integer lattices. Specifically, we prove that SVP \mathrm{SVP} in the ℓ p \ell_{p} norm is 𝖶 ⁡ [ 1 ] \mathsf{W}[1] -hard to approximate within any constant factor for any fixed p > 1 p>1 and 𝖶 ⁡ [ 1 ] \mathsf{W}[1] -hard to approximate within a factor approaching 2 2 for p = 1 p=1 . (We show hardness under randomized reductions in each case.) These results answer the main questions left open (and explicitly posed) by Bhattacharyya, Bonnet, Egri, Ghoshal, Karthik C. S., Lin, Manurangsi, and Marx (Journal of the ACM, 2021) on the complexity of parameterized MDP and SVP \mathrm{SVP} . For MDP , they established similar hardness for binary linear codes and left the case of general fields open. For SVP \mathrm{SVP} in ℓ p \ell_{p} norms with p > 1 p>1 , they showed inapproximability within some constant factor (depending on p p ) and left open showing such hardness for arbitrary constant factors. They also left open showing 𝖶 ⁡ [ 1 ] \mathsf{W}[1] -hardness even of exact SVP in the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} norm.

Introduction

Error correcting codes and point lattices are fundamental mathematical objects, and computational problems on them have a wide range of applications in computer science including to robust communication, cryptography, optimization, complexity theory, and more. Indeed, because computational problems on codes and lattices are so ubiquitous, a highly active line of research spanning decades has worked to understand the complexity of the problems themselves.
In particular, a great deal of work has studied the complexity of the Minimum Distance Problem (
MDP
) (and its affine version, the Nearest Codeword Problem (
NCP
)) on linear error correcting codes
[
BMvT78
,
ABSS97
,
Var97
,
DMS03
,
CW12
]
.
Similarly, a large amount of work has studied the complexity of the analogous problems on lattices, the Shortest Vector Problem (
\mathrm{SVP}
) (and its affine version, the Closest Vector Problem (
\mathrm{CVP}
))
[
vEB81
,
ABSS97
,
Ajt98
,
Mic00
,
Kho05
,
HR12
]
.

In
\textnormal{MDP}_{q}
, the goal is, given a linear error correcting code
\mathcal{C}
over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and a distance bound
k
as input, to determine whether or not the minimum Hamming weight of a nonzero codeword in
\mathcal{C}
is at most
k
.
Similarly, in
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
the goal is, given a lattice
\mathcal{L}
and a distance bound
k
as input, to determine whether or not the
\ell_{p}
norm of some nonzero vector in
\mathcal{L}
is at most
k
.
1
1

1

The
\ell_{p}
norm used is fixed and independent of the input. One may also consider
\mathrm{SVP}
with respect to arbitrary norms, but it is most commonly considered with respect to
\ell_{p}
norms (and especially with respect to the Euclidean norm
\ell_{2}
) as is the case in this work.

One may also consider
\gamma
-approximate versions of these problems for
\gamma\geqslant 1
, which we denote by
\gamma
-
\textnormal{MDP}_{q}
and
\gamma
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
, respectively.
(In what follows we refer to linear error correcting codes over finite fields simply as “codes.” We define codes, lattices, and computational problems on them formally in
Sections

2.3
and
2.4
.)

In the 1990s, the field of
parameterized complexity
, in which the running time of an algorithm for a given computational problem is considered not just as a function of the problem’s input size
n
but also in terms of some parameter
k
, developed and matured.
The fundamental notion of efficiency in the study of parameterized algorithms is
fixed-parameter tractability
, which means that the algorithm runs in time
f(k)\cdot\poly(n)
for some (possibly fast-growing) function
f(k)
depending on the parameter
k
but not the input length.
A computational problem (formally, problem-parameter pair) with such an algorithm is called
fixed-parameter tractable
(FPT), and the set of all such problems forms the complexity class
\mathsf{FPT}
.
On the other hand, the canonical notion of inefficiency for parameterized problems is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness, which is analogous to
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness in the non-parameterized setting.
To show
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness of a given problem, it suffices to give an
FPT reduction
from a known
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard problem to that problem.
Giving such a reduction in particular implies that the problem reduced to is not in
\mathsf{FPT}
unless
\mathsf{W}[1]=\mathsf{FPT}
, which is widely conjectured not to be the case.
(Determining whether
\mathsf{FPT}
is equal to
\mathsf{W}[1]
is a major open question, and is the analog of the
\mathsf{P}
versus
\mathsf{NP}
question in the parameterized world.)
See the books by Downey and Fellows
[
DF99
,
DF13
]
for comprehensive references on parameterized complexity.

Parameterized complexity of
MDP
and
\mathrm{SVP}
.
As part of the development of parameterized complexity as a whole, substantial interest arose in the parameterized complexity (specifically,
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness) of computational problems on codes and lattices. This was especially true for
MDP
and
\mathrm{SVP}
, where in each case the parameter

-hard. It was one of the few remaining open problems from

]

5

We do not consider the case of

1.2
Technical overview

1.2.1
Parameterized inapproximability of
\gamma
-
\textnormal{MDP}_{q}

Inapproximability results for
MDP
and
\mathrm{SVP}
follow the blueprint originally pioneered by Ajtai
[
Ajt98
]
, Micciancio
[
Mic00
]
, and Khot
[
Kho05
]
for lattices and Dumer, Micciancio, and Sudan
[
DMS03
]
for codes. In each case, the idea is to reduce the affine versions of the problems (
NCP
and
\mathrm{CVP}
, respectively), for which
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness results were long known, to the linear versions (
MDP
and
\mathrm{SVP}
, respectively).

The DMS reduction from
NCP
to
MDP
.

We start by illustrating the Dumer-Micciancio-Sudan (DMS) reduction from
NCP
to
MDP
, which is based on analogous reductions of Ajtai
[
Ajt98
]
and Micciancio
[
Mic00
]
from
\mathrm{CVP}
to
\mathrm{SVP}
.
An instance of
NCP
consists of a linear code
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(G)\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
generated by a matrix
G\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times n}
and a target
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
, and the goal is to minimize the distance
\mathrm{dist}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{C})
of
\boldsymbol{t}
to its closest codeword, i.e., the minimum Hamming weight of
G\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{t}
over all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, where the Hamming weight of a vector
\boldsymbol{v}
is
\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert_{0}=|\{i:\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\neq 0\}|
.
A natural reduction to MDP will produce the instance
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\text{span}(\mathcal{C},\boldsymbol{t})
generated by
G^{\prime}=(G\mid\boldsymbol{t})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times(n+1)}
.
If we restrict to codewords
G\boldsymbol{x}+\beta\boldsymbol{t}
of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
that use the target in the combination, i.e., have
\beta\neq 0
, then the minimum distance of such a codeword equals the Hamming distance
\mathrm{dist}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{C})
of
\boldsymbol{t}
to
\mathcal{C}
. Under this (unreasonable) restriction we have a reduction that preserves the objective value. The obvious trouble though is that
\mathcal{C}
(and hence
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
) might have short codewords of weight much smaller than
\mathrm{dist}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{C})
. In this case, the minimum distance of
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
will equal the distance of
\mathcal{C}
, and have nothing to do with
\boldsymbol{t}
. Note, however, that this reduction
does
work if
\lambda(\mathcal{C})>k
, where
\lambda(\mathcal{C})=\min_{\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}\lVert\boldsymbol{c}\rVert_{0}
is the minimum Hamming weight of
\mathcal{C}
.
Further, starting from a gap-
\gamma
version of
NCP
asking if
\mathrm{dist}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{C})\leqslant k
or
\mathrm{dist}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{C})>\gamma k
, we would get hardness of a gap-
\gamma
version of
MDP
if
\lambda(\mathcal{C})>\gamma k
.

A natural goal is therefore to increase the distance of
\mathcal{C}
without increasing the proximity parameter in
NCP
by too much. This was achieved in
[
DMS03
]
by encoding the message
according to
\mathcal{C}
as well as a second code
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m^{\prime}}
with generator matrix
\tilde{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}}
with large distance, say
D
.
Further,
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
will be a
locally dense code
in the sense that one can find a “bad list decoding configuration” comprising a center
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m^{\prime}}
that has a large number of codewords of
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
within distance
\alpha D
for some
\alpha<1
; we call
\alpha
the
relative radius
of the locally dense code. (One can in fact efficiently construct such locally dense codes with any constant relative radius
\alpha>1/2
using randomness
[
DMS03
]
.) The number of codewords will be so large that one can sample a linear map
T
that with high probability projects these codewords
onto

\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. If
\tilde{G}
is the generator matrix of the locally dense code
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
, the reduction, which will use randomness to pick both the center
\boldsymbol{s}
and the projection
T
, will produce the instance of
MDP
generated by

\begin{pmatrix}GT\tilde{G}&\boldsymbol{t}\\
\tilde{G}&\boldsymbol{s}\end{pmatrix}.

(1)

The completeness of the reduction follows because for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
that might satisfy
\lVert G\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{t}\rVert_{0}\leqslant k
, there will be a codeword
\tilde{G}\boldsymbol{y}\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
within distance
\alpha D
from
\boldsymbol{s}
that projects to
\boldsymbol{x}
under
T
. Thus multiplying the generator matrix in
Equation

1
by
(\boldsymbol{y}^{T},-1)^{T}

will yield a nonzero codeword of weight at most
k+\alpha D
. Since the distance of
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
is
D
, codewords which don’t use the last column of
Equation

1
will have Hamming weight at least
D
. If
\alpha D+k<D
, which is possible to ensure provided
\alpha<1
, we get a gap.

Challenges in the FPT setting.

It is reasonable to wonder whether the DMS reduction above works directly in the FPT setting.
However, as already pointed out in
[
BBE+21
, Section 2.1]
, one quickly runs into some obstacles.
Indeed, the locally dense codes used in
[
DMS03
]
have minimum distance
D
which depends on the input code dimension, and this is necessary to ensure that we can sample the linear map
T
with the desired properties.
This is because the existence of
T
implies that there must be at least
|\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}|=q^{n}
codewords in
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
of Hamming weight at most
\alpha D
.
Since the distance threshold of the resulting
MDP
instance is
k^{\prime}=\alpha D+k
, it follows that
k^{\prime}
depends on the input code dimension
n
, and so the DMS reduction is not FPT.

To overcome these issues,
[
BBE+21
]
modify both the problem they reduce from as well as the reduction itself.
First, instead of reducing from
NCP
to
MDP
, they reduce from a variant of
NCP
they call the
Sparse Nearest Codeword Problem
(SNCP), where the Hamming weight of the coefficient vector realizing the nearest codeword is also taken into account.
More precisely, the objective function
\mathrm{dist}(\boldsymbol{t},\mathcal{C})
of
NCP
is replaced by

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}(\|G\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{t}\|_{0}+\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{0}),

where
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(G)
.
It is not hard to reduce
NCP
to SNCP in the FPT setting, and this allows
[
BBE+21
]
to avoid having to sample the linear map
T
.
Second, they replace locally dense codes by another variant which they call
locally suffix dense codes
(LSDCs).
These are codes
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m^{\prime}}
with minimum distance
D
and generator matrix

\begin{pmatrix}I_{n}&0\\
\tilde{G}_{1}&\tilde{G}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}}

which have the property that, given any prefix
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, for most “suffix centers”
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m^{\prime}-n}
there is a suffix
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m^{\prime}-n}
within Hamming distance
\alpha D
of
\boldsymbol{s}
such that
(\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{u})\in\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
.
With the help of these notions,
[
BBE+21
]
consider the
MDP
instance generated by

\begin{pmatrix}G&0&\boldsymbol{t}\\
I_{n}&0&0\\
\tilde{G}_{1}&\tilde{G}_{2}&\boldsymbol{s}\end{pmatrix},

where
\boldsymbol{s}
is sampled uniformly at random from
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m^{\prime}-n}
.
The proof that this reduction works is similar to the one sketched above for the DMS reduction.
The main challenge is to efficiently construct LSDCs with appropriate parameters, in particular with minimum distance
D

independent
of
m^{\prime}
,
n^{\prime}
, and
n
.
Unfortunately, known constructions of locally dense codes do not yield LSDCs with the desired properties.
In the binary setting
q=2
,
[
BBE+21
]
showed that one can take
\tilde{C}
to be a binary BCH code
[
Hoc59
,
BR60
]
with design minimum distance
D
.
This ingenious approach allows them to prove that
\gamma
-
\textnormal{MDP}_{2}
is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard.

It is instructive to discuss more precisely why the choice of binary BCH codes as LSDCs works, and why it cannot be extended to other finite fields.
Binary BCH codes with minimum distance
D
have codimension
\approx\left\lfloor\frac{D-1}{2}\right\rfloor\log(m^{\prime}+1)
.
The crucial fact that makes the counting analysis of
[
BBE+21
]
go through is that
\left\lfloor\frac{D-1}{2}\right\rfloor
is also the
unique decoding radius
for the binary BCH code, i.e., Hamming balls of this radius centered on BCH codewords are disjoint.
In other words, binary BCH codes almost meet the sphere packing bound.
One would hope that replacing binary BCH codes with
q
-ary BCH codes would suffice to show
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness of
\gamma
-
\textnormal{MDP}_{q}
more generally.
However,
q
-ary BCH codes with minimum distance
D
have codimension
\approx\lfloor(D-1)(1-1/q)\rfloor\log_{q}(m^{\prime}+1)
(see
Theorem

3.4
),
while the unique decoding radius remains
\left\lfloor\frac{D-1}{2}\right\rfloor
.
Put differently,
q
-ary BCH codes for
q>2
are no longer close to the sphere packing bound, which breaks the analysis from
[
BBE+21
]
. In fact, for
q>2
, it is not known if there exist
q
-ary codes with
rate vs. distance trade-off close to the sphere packing bound.
Therefore, it seems challenging to make the approach from
[
BBE+21
]
work as is over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, for
q>2
.

Our approach: Khot for codes.

We succeed in overcoming the barriers that
[
BBE+21
]
faced and establish the
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness of
\gamma
-
\textnormal{MDP}_{q}
for arbitrary finite fields
\mathbb{F}_{q}
via a different and arguably simpler (direct) reduction from
NCP
to
MDP
.
Our key insight is to adapt Khot’s reduction
[
Kho05
]
from
\mathrm{CVP}
to
\mathrm{SVP}
to the coding-theoretic setting. We are able to meet the requirements of such a reduction with locally dense codes constructed from
q
-ary BCH codes.

This approach is quite natural. In fact, early lecture notes of Khot
[
Kho04
]
showed how to use this strategy to reduce from
NCP
to
MDP
in the special case of binary codes. Our reduction is more general and requires more careful analysis in that it works with arbitrary locally dense codes with constant relative radius
\alpha<1
, works over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and not just
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, and requires a more careful analysis of the the distance bound
k^{\prime}
in the output
MDP
instance as a function of the distance bound
k
in the input
NCP
instance. However, the core idea is the same.

More precisely, given an instance
(G,\boldsymbol{t},k)
of
\gamma
-
\textnormal{NCP}_{q}
with
G\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times n}
and
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
and an appropriate locally dense code
(\tilde{G},\boldsymbol{s})
with
\tilde{G}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m^{\prime}\times n^{\prime}}
and
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m^{\prime}}
, we consider the intermediate code
\mathcal{C}_{\textnormal{int}}
spanned by the generator matrix

G_{\textnormal{int}}=\begin{pmatrix}G&0&-\boldsymbol{t}\\
0&\tilde{G}&-\boldsymbol{s}\end{pmatrix}.

This is analogous to the intermediate lattice introduced in Khot’s reduction
[
Kho05
]
from
\mathrm{CVP}
to
\mathrm{SVP}
, with the difference being that we replace the
\mathrm{CVP}
instance by an
NCP
instance and the locally dense
lattice
by a locally dense
code
.
Note that it may happen that
\mathcal{C}_{\textnormal{int}}
contains low weight vectors even when
(G,\boldsymbol{t},k)
is a NO instance of
\gamma
-
\textnormal{NCP}_{q}
.
This is, however, not a show-stopper, as it in fact suffices to show that there are
many more
low weight vectors in
\mathcal{C}_{\textnormal{int}}
when
(G,\boldsymbol{t},k)
is a YES instance of
\gamma
-
\textnormal{NCP}_{q}
than when
(G,\boldsymbol{t},k)
is a NO instance.
Indeed, if this holds then we can
sparsify

\mathcal{C}_{\textnormal{int}}
by intersecting it with an appropriate random code
\mathcal{C}_{\textnormal{rand}}
so that, with high probability, all low weight vectors are eliminated in the NO case, but at least one low weight vector survives in the YES case.
Again, this is analogous to the lattice sparsfication performed in Khot’s reduction
[
Kho05
]
,
Finally, the
\gamma^{\prime}
-
\textnormal{MDP}_{q}
instance is obtained by computing a generator matrix
G_{\textnormal{final}}
of
\mathcal{C}_{\textnormal{final}}=\mathcal{C}_{\textnormal{int}}\cap\mathcal{C}_{\textnormal{rand}}
and outputting
(G_{\textnormal{final}},k^{\prime})
for some appropriate
k^{\prime}
.

To guarantee that the reduction is FPT, we need to ensure that
k^{\prime}\leqslant f(k)
for some function
f
. In fact, in our reduction
k
only increases by a linear factor, i.e., we get
k^{\prime}\leqslant f(k)=O(k)
.
We briefly sketch how to establish the desired properties of
\mathcal{C}_{\textnormal{int}}
and choose
k^{\prime}
.
Suppose that
(\tilde{G},\boldsymbol{s})
is a locally dense code with minimum distance
D
and such that there are at least
N
vectors
\boldsymbol{y}
satsifying
\|\tilde{G}\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{s}\|_{0}\leqslant\alpha D
for some
\alpha\in(1/2,1)
.
If
(G,\boldsymbol{t},k)
is a YES instance of
\gamma
-
\textnormal{NCP}_{q}
, i.e., there exists
\boldsymbol{x}
such that
\|G\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{t}\|_{0}\leqslant k
, then multiplying
G_{\textnormal{int}}
by
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},1)^{T}
yields a codeword of weight at most
k^{\prime}=\alpha D+k
.
As a result, there are at least
N
vectors in
\mathcal{C}_{\textnormal{int}}
of weight at most
k^{\prime}
, which we call
good
.
On the other hand, if
(G,\boldsymbol{t},k)
is a NO instance of
\gamma
-
\textnormal{NCP}_{q}
and
D>\gamma k
, then it is not hard to see that every codeword of weight at most
\gamma k
in
\mathcal{C}_{\textnormal{int}}
is of the form
G_{\textnormal{int}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{0},0)^{T}
, and so there are at most
q^{n}
such
annoying
vectors in
\mathcal{C}_{\textnormal{int}}
, where
n=\dim(\mathcal{C}(G))
. (The “good” versus “annoying” vectors terminology was also introduced in
[
Kho05
]
.)

We conclude that the reduction works and is FPT if we are able to construct a
q
-ary locally dense code
(\tilde{G},\boldsymbol{s})
as above under the constraints that (i)
D=g(k)>\gamma k
, (ii)
k^{\prime}=\alpha D+k\ll\gamma k
, and (iii)
N\gg q^{n}
(so that there many more good vectors in YES instances than annoying vectors in NO instances and the sparsification step works).
While the approach of
[
BBE+21
]
described above required
q
-ary codes of codimension
\approx\frac{D}{2}\log_{q}(m^{\prime})
, which are not known to exist for
q>2
, we show that to construct locally dense codes satisfying our constraints it is enough to consider
q
-ary codes with minimum distance
D\approx\gamma k
and codimension
\approx\beta D\log_{q}(m^{\prime})
for
any

\beta<1
!
Therefore, we can use
q
-ary BCH codes of length
m^{\prime}=\poly(m)
with design minimum distance
D\approx\gamma k
, which have codimension
\approx D(1-1/q)\log_{q}(m^{\prime})
for any prime power
q
.

This approach shows
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness of
\gamma^{\prime}
-
\textnormal{MDP}_{q}
for some approximation factor
\gamma^{\prime}>1
; in fact, we get hardness with
\gamma^{\prime}\approx 1/\beta\approx 1/\alpha
.
We can then amplify this approximation factor
\gamma^{\prime}
in a standard manner via
tensoring
to obtain
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness of
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
-
\textnormal{MDP}_{q}
for every
\gamma^{\prime\prime}\geqslant 1
.
For more details, see
Section

3
.

1.2.2
Parameterized inapproximability of
\gamma
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}

We first define locally dense lattices, which are analogous objects to locally dense codes, and which are important both for understanding the issues with
[
BBE+21
]
and our ways of handling them. A
locally dense lattice
(with respect to the
\ell_{p}
norm) is a lattice
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
together with a shift
\boldsymbol{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
such that
\mathcal{L}-\boldsymbol{s}
contains many vectors of
\ell_{p}
norm at most
\alpha\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})
for some constant
\alpha=\alpha(p)\in(1/2,1)
, where
\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})=\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{p}
.
As is the case for locally dense codes, we call
\alpha
the
relative radius
of the corresponding locally dense lattice.

As in
Section

1.2.1
, we start by explaining the original approach from
[
BBE+21
]
towards showing
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness of
\gamma
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
for
p>1
and some approximation factor
\gamma>1
, and why it fails to resolve the problems we tackle.
To recall,
[
BBE+21
]
proved that
\gamma
-
\mathrm{CVP}_{p}
is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard for every fixed
p,\gamma\geqslant 1
.
Then, they simply noted that Khot’s initial reduction
[
Kho05
]
from
\mathrm{CVP}_{p}
to
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
(which is similar to our FPT reduction from
NCP
to
MDP
discussed in
Section

1.2.1
) is itself an FPT reduction if the parameters of the locally dense lattice from
[
Kho05
]
(which is based on binary BCH codes) are chosen appropriately.
Combining this observation with the
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness of
\gamma
-
\mathrm{CVP}_{p}
immediately yields that
\gamma^{\prime}
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard for some approximation factor
\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma^{\prime}(p)>1
.
However, despite achieving this nice result, the approach of
[
BBE+21
]
has two significant shortcomings.

Showing inapproximability of
\mathrm{SVP}
in all
\ell_{p}
norms (including
\ell_{1}
).

The first limitation of the approach in
[
BBE+21
]
is the use of Khot’s locally dense lattices, which do not suffice to show either
\mathsf{NP}
- or
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness of
\mathrm{SVP}
in the
\ell_{1}
norm.
More specifically, Khot’s locally dense lattices have relative radius
\alpha=\alpha(p)>(1/2+1/2^{p})^{1/p}
, which suffices to show
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness of parameterized
\gamma
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
(and
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness of non-parameterized
\gamma
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
) for any

\gamma^{\prime}=\gamma^{\prime}(p)<1/\alpha(p)<(1/2+1/2^{p})^{-1/p}\ \text{,}

and no better. Plugging
p=1
into the right-hand side of this equation shows that Khot’s reduction does not yield hardness even for exact
\mathrm{SVP}_{1}
(i.e., for
\gamma^{\prime}
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{1}
with
\gamma^{\prime}=1
). Indeed, this issue is what kept Khot’s reduction from showing
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness of
\mathrm{SVP}_{1}
in
[
Kho05
]
and what kept
[
BBE+21
]
from showing
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness of
\mathrm{SVP}_{1}
.

Despite Khot’s reduction not working, other reductions nevertheless showed
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness of (even approximating)
\mathrm{SVP}_{1}
.
Unfortunately, as
[
BBE+21
]
notes, these reductions fail both because of their use of non-integral lattices and the fact that rounding real-valued lattice bases to integral ones in a black-box way amounts to a non-FPT reduction, since the minimum distance of the resulting lattices will depend on their dimension. (Multiplying rational lattice bases by the least common multiple of their entries’ denominators causes a similar problem.)
First, Micciancio
[
Mic00
]
showed hardness of
\gamma
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{1}
for any
\gamma<2
using locally dense lattices constructed from prime number lattices. However, these locally dense lattices are non-integral and even non-rational.
8
8

8

We note in passing that Micciancio did in fact carefully analyze rounding these locally dense lattices to get integral ones, but emphasize again that this rounding causes the minimum distance of the resulting lattices to depend on their dimension.

Second, Regev and Rosen
[
RR06
]
showed how to use efficiently computable linear norm embeddings to reduce
\gamma
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{2}
to
\gamma^{\prime}
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
for any
p\geqslant 1
and any constant
\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma
.
Combined with Khot’s work
[
Kho05
]
, which showed
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness of approximating
\mathrm{SVP}_{2}
to within any constant factor
\gamma
,
[
RR06
]
implies that
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
for any
p
(and in particular,
\mathrm{SVP}_{1}
) is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard to approximate within any constant factor as well.
However, the norm embeddings given in
[
RR06
]
use random Gaussian projection matrices, and therefore output non-integral lattices. Moreover, using different, integral distributions for the projection matrices also does not obviously work.

We overcome this first issue of Khot’s locally dense lattices not working in the
\ell_{1}
norm by instantiating Khot’s reduction with different locally dense lattices.
Specifically, we instantiate Khot’s reduction with the locally dense lattices constructed in recent work of Bennett and Peikert
[
BP23
]
, which are built from Reed-Solomon codes.
These locally dense lattices meet all of the requirements necessary for the proof of
Theorem

1.2
. Namely, they are efficiently constructible; their base lattices
\mathcal{L}
are integral; they can be constructed so that
\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})
does not depend on the dimension of the input lattice
\mathcal{L}
; and for
p\in[1,\infty)
they have
\ell_{p}
relative radius
\alpha(p)\approx 1/2^{p}<1
. In particular, they have
\ell_{1}
relative radius
\alpha(1)\approx 1/2
(which is essentially optimal by the triangle inequality), and so Khot’s reduction shows hardness of
\gamma
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{1}
for any constant
\gamma<2
.
(We again note that the largest approximation factor
\gamma=\gamma(p)
for which Khot’s reduction shows parameterized hardness of
\gamma
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
is
\gamma\approx 1/\alpha
, where
\alpha=\alpha(p)
is the relative radius of the locally dense lattice used, and this is where the bound on the approximation factor
\gamma=\gamma(p)
in
Theorem

1.2
comes from.)

Showing inapproximability of
\gamma
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
for all
p>1
and all
\gamma
.

The second main shortcoming of the approach in
[
BBE+21
]
is that it is not clear how to amplify the approximation factor
\gamma>1
for which they get
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness of
\gamma
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
(for
p>1
), to an arbitrary constant. As in the case of codes, the natural thing to try for amplifying hardness is to take the
tensor product
of the input
\mathrm{SVP}
instance with itself. The idea of tensoring is, given an instance
(B,k)
of
\mathrm{SVP}
as input, to output the
\mathrm{SVP}
instance
(B\otimes B,k^{2})
, where
B\otimes B
is the Kronecker product of the input basis matrix
B
with itself. Unfortunately, unlike for codes, tensoring does not work in general for lattices. Indeed, although it always holds that
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}(B)\otimes\mathcal{L}(B))\leqslant\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{L}(B))^{2}
, the converse is not always true or even “close to true”; see, e.g.,
[
HR12
, Lemma 2.3]
.

Although Haviv and Regev
[
HR12
]
showed that Khot’s original
\mathrm{SVP}_{2}
instances have properties that
do
in fact allow them to tensor nicely,
this is
not the case
for the
\mathrm{SVP}
instances obtained in
[
BBE+21
]
.
Indeed, the (crucial!) subtlety is that the standard
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness proof for approximate
\mathrm{CVP}_{p}
proceeds via a reduction from approximate
Exact Set Cover

[
ABSS97
,
Kho05
,
HR12
]
, and the resulting
\mathrm{CVP}_{p}
instances enjoy important additional properties that are then inherited by the
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
instances in
[
Kho05
]
.
Parameterized inapproximability of Exact Set Cover is known under the (randomized) Gap-ETH and PIH assumptions, and this is what allowed
[
BGKM18
]
to show parameterized hardness of
\gamma
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{2}
for any constant
\gamma\geqslant 1
; see also
Remark

1.4
.
However, it is not currently known whether approximate Exact Set Cover is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard, and so
[
BBE+21
]
generate their
\mathrm{CVP}_{p}
instances via a different reduction from (the dual version of)
\textnormal{NCP}_{q}
with a suitably large prime
q
instead.
9
9

9

The exact version of this problem is known to be
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard, see
[
CFK+15
, Section 13.6.3]
.

As a result, important properties no longer hold when
[
BBE+21
]
use these alternative
\mathrm{CVP}_{p}
instances to create
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
instances via Khot’s reduction.
Namely, it is no longer true that every lattice vector with at least one odd coordinate has large Hamming weight, a property that is needed to ensure that the
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
instance tensors nicely in
[
HR12
]
.

It is also sensible to wonder whether Khot’s
augmented tensor product

[
Kho05
]
, which he introduced in his original work to overcome issues with tensoring, can nevertheless be used to boost the approximation factor of the
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
instances generated in
[
BBE+21
]
.
However, the augmented tensor product cannot be applied in the FPT setting unless the short lattice vectors in the base
\mathrm{SVP}
instances also have have short coefficient vectors (i.e., coefficient vectors whose
\ell_{p}
norm is independent of lattice dimension). The
\mathrm{SVP}
instances in
[
BBE+21
]
do not
seem to have this property.

Our solution.

In order to construct
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard
\mathrm{SVP}
instances that tensor nicely and thereby prove
Theorem

1.3
, we give a reduction directly from approximate
\textnormal{NCP}_{2}
to approximate
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
for any
p>1
.
10
10

10

We could also use
\mathrm{CVP}
as an intermediate problem in the reduction as is done in
[
BBE+21
]
, but that does not obviously make the reduction simpler or more modular.

Our reduction is a variant of the reductions in Khot
[
Kho05
]
and Haviv and Regev
[
HR12
]
, and again we instantiate the reduction with locally dense lattices constructed from binary BCH codes similar to those used by Khot
[
Kho05
,
BBE+21
]
.
We emphasize that although the proofs of
Theorems

1.2
and
1.3
both use variants of Khot’s reduction, the key to proving
Theorem

1.2
was to instantiate Khot’s reduction with different locally dense lattices and the key to
Theorem

1.3
was to reduce from a different
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard problem.
Moreover, ensuring that the characteristic of the underlying codes in the
NCP
instances that we reduce from matches that of the underlying BCH codes in the locally dense lattices that we use seems essential for our analysis. Indeed, our
NCP
instances and locally dense lattices both use codes over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, whereas
[
BBE+21
]
reduced from
NCP
instances over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
for larger prime
q
.

Our reduction allows us to construct
\gamma
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{2}
instances with some constant
\gamma>1
that meet the sufficient conditions given in
[
HR12
]
to be amplified to
\gamma^{\prime}
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{2}
instances for arbitrarily large constant
\gamma^{\prime}
. These conditions roughly say that the base lattices
\mathcal{L}
in the
\mathrm{SVP}
instance must be such that all vectors
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
satisfy at least one of the following: (1)
\boldsymbol{v}
has Hamming weight at least
d
for some distance bound
d
, (2)
\boldsymbol{v}\in 2\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
has Hamming weight at least
d/4
, or (3)
\boldsymbol{v}\in 2\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
\boldsymbol{v}
has very high
\ell_{2}
norm. The minimum distances of lattices in which all vectors satisfy either conditions (1) or (2) behave nicely under tensoring, but condition (3) makes the analysis subtle. However, we are essentially able to rely on the analysis in
[
HR12
]
. Moreover, modifying the analysis in
[
HR12
]
a bit additionally allows us to extend our result to
\ell_{p}
norms for
p>1
.
(The omission of
p=1
is yet again for the same reason as in
[
Kho05
,
BBE+21
]
; it is because of the binary BCH-code-based locally dense lattices that we use.)

1.3
Additional related work

Interest in the complexity of computational problems on codes and lattices more broadly goes back several decades.
We survey the most closely related work here.

Complexity of
NCP
and
MDP
.

Berlekamp, McEliece, and van Tilborg
[
BMvT78
]
showed that certain problems related to linear codes, such as the exact version of
NCP
, are
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.
They also conjectured that the exact version of
MDP
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.
This conjecture remained open until groundbreaking work of Vardy
[
Var97
]
, who showed that exact
MDP
is indeed
\mathsf{NP}
-hard.
Not long after, Dumer, Micciancio, and Sudan
[
DMS03
]
showed that approximate
MDP
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard under randomized reductions.
Follow-up work by Cheng and Wan
[
CW12
]
, Austrin and Khot
[
AK14
]
, and Micciancio
[
Mic14
]
showed that approximate
MDP
is
\mathsf{NP}
-hard under deterministic reductions.
The unparameterized fine-grained hardness of
NCP
and
MDP
was recently studied by Stephens-Davidowitz and Vaikuntanathan
[
SV19
]
.

On the parameterized front, Downey, Fellows, Vardy, and Whittle
[
DFVW99
]
showed, among other things, that the exact version of
NCP
is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard, and infamously conjectured that

-hard.
As discussed above, the status of this conjecture did not budge until the seminal work

\textnormal{NCP}_{q}
(for all constant

\gamma
-
\textnormal{MDP}_{2}

Complexity of
\mathrm{CVP}
and
\mathrm{SVP}
.

The study of the complexity of lattice problems was initiated by van Emde Boas

, who showed that

-hard. He also showed that

1.4
Open problems

We highlight two interesting directions for future research:

•

The reductions that we use to prove all of our main theorems are randomized and have two-sided error due to our randomized constructions of locally dense codes and lattices and due to our use of sparsification. It would be a groundbreaking contribution to find ways to derandomize these reductions and obtain deterministic parameterized hardness results for
MDP
and
\mathrm{SVP}
.
We note that when it comes to showing
\mathsf{NP}
-hardness (instead of
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness), we know deterministic reductions from
NCP
to
MDP

[
CW12
,
AK14
,
Mic14
]
and randomized reductions with one-sided error from
\mathrm{CVP}
to
\mathrm{SVP}

[
Mic12
]
. Additionally, we note that showing deterministic (
\mathsf{NP}
-)hardness of
\mathrm{SVP}
in the non-parameterized setting is a major open question.

•

We have shown that
\gamma
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard for any fixed
p>1
and
\gamma\geqslant 1
.
When
p=1
, we showed that
\gamma
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard when
\gamma\in[1,2)
.
We leave it as a fascinating open problem to extend our
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness result for all
\gamma\geqslant 1
to

as well.
This is an important missing piece of our understanding of the parameterized hardness of approximate
\mathrm{SVP}
in
\ell_{p}
norms.

\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{s},\boldsymbol{t}

2.1
Probability theory

We denote random variables by uppercase letters such as
X
,
Y
, and
Z
.
Throughout this work we consider only discrete random variables supported on finite sets.
Given a random variable
X
, we denote its expected value by
\E[X]
and its variance by
\Var[X]
.
We write the indicator random variable for an event
E
as
\mathbf{1}_{\{E\}}
.

We will make use of the following standard corollary of Chebyshev’s inequality.
For completeness, we provide a short proof.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}
be pairwise independent random variables over
\{0,1\}
such that
\Pr[X_{i}=1]=p>0
for
i=1,\dots,N
.
Then, it holds that

\Pr\left[\forall i\in[N],X_{i}=0\right]\leqslant\frac{1}{pN}.

Proof.

Let
X=\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}
and note that
\E[X]=pN
.
We have

\Pr\left[X=0\right]\leqslant\Pr\left[\left|X-\E[X]\right|\geqslant pN\right]\leqslant\frac{\Var[X]}{(pN)^{2}}=\frac{(1-p)pN}{(pN)^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{pN},

where the second inequality follows from Chebyshev’s inequality and the equality holds due to the pairwise independence of the
X_{i}
’s.
∎

2.2
Parameterized promise problems and FPT reductions

We recall basic definitions related to parameterized promise (decision) problems and Fixed-Parameter Tractable (FPT) reductions between such problems.
We refer the reader to
[
DF99
]
for an excellent discussion on parameterized algorithms and reductions.

Definition 2.2

(Parameterized language)
.

A set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{N}
is said to be a
parameterized language
(with respect to the rightmost coordinate).

Definition 2.3

(Parameterized promise problem)
.

The tuple of parameterized languages
\Pi=(\Pi_{\textnormal{YES}},\Pi_{\textnormal{NO}})
is said to be a
parameterized promise problem
if
\{x:(x,k)\in\Pi_{\textnormal{YES}}\}\cap\{x:(x,k)\in\Pi_{\textnormal{NO}}\}=\emptyset
for every parameter choice
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 2.4

(Randomized FPT reductions with two-sided error)
.

We say that a randomized algorithm is a
randomized FPT reduction with two-sided error
from the parameterized promise problem
\Pi
to the parameterized promise problem
\Pi^{\prime}
if the following properties hold:

•

On input
(x,k)
, the algorithm runs in time at most
T(k)\cdot|x|^{c}
for some computable function
T(\cdot)
and some absolute constant
c>0
and outputs a tuple
(x^{\prime},k^{\prime})
;

•

It holds that
k^{\prime}\leqslant g(k)
for some computable function
g(\cdot)
;

•

If
(x,k)\in\Pi_{\textnormal{YES}}
, it holds that
\Pr\left[(x^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in\Pi^{\prime}_{\textnormal{YES}}\right]\geqslant 2/3
, where the probability is taken over the randomness of the algorithm;

•

If
(x,k)\in\Pi_{\textnormal{NO}}
, it holds that
\Pr\left[(x^{\prime},k^{\prime})\in\Pi^{\prime}_{\textnormal{NO}}\right]\geqslant 2/3
, where the probability is taken over the randomness of the algorithm.

Note that if there is a randomized FPT reduction with two-sided error from
\Pi
to
\Pi^{\prime}
, it follows that there is a randomized FPT algorithm (i.e., an algorithm running in time
T(k)\cdot|x|^{c}
for some computable function
T(\cdot)
on input an instance
(x,k)
) for deciding
\Pi
with two-sided error whenever there is such an algorithm for deciding
\Pi^{\prime}
.
The success probability of any such algorithm can be amplified in a standard manner.

In this work we focus on the parameterized complexity class
\mathsf{W}[1]
.
It is well-known that the parameterized Clique problem, in which we are given as input a graph
G
and a positive integer
k
(with
k
being the parameter of interest) and must decide whether
G
contains a clique of size
k
, is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-complete.
That is, parameterized Clique is in
\mathsf{W}[1]
and it is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard, i.e., there is an FPT reduction from every problem in
\mathsf{W}[1]
to it (see, e.g.,
[
CFK+15
, Theorem 13.18]
).
Therefore, one may
define

\mathsf{W}[1]
to be the class of all parameterized problems with FPT reductions to Clique.
We refrain from discussing
\mathsf{W}[1]
in more detail; for an extensive discussion, see
[
DF99
, Chapters 9 to 11]
.

It is widely believed that
\mathsf{W}[1]
problems cannot be decided by FPT algorithms, even if randomness with two-sided error is allowed.
We say that a parameterized promise problem
\Pi^{\prime}
is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard under randomized reductions
if there is a randomized FPT reduction with two-sided error from a
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard problem
\Pi
to
\Pi^{\prime}
.
The existence of such a reduction shows that
\Pi^{\prime}
is likely intractable from a parameterized perspective.

2.3
Coding problems

Let
\mathcal{C}(G):=\{{G\boldsymbol{x}:\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}}\}
denote the code generated by the generator matrix
G\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times n}
(note that here
\mathcal{C}(G)
is the
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-span of the
columns
of
G
).
Alternatively, we may see
\mathcal{C}(G)
as the kernel of the parity-check matrix
H\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{(m-n)\times m}
which spans the dual subspace of
\mathcal{C}(G)
, i.e.,
\mathcal{C}(G)=\{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}:H\boldsymbol{y}=\boldsymbol{0}\}
.
We call sets of the form
\boldsymbol{u}+\mathcal{C}=\{\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{c}:\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathcal{C}\}
with
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
the cosets of
\mathcal{C}
.
We write
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{0}=|\{i\in[m]:\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\neq 0\}
for the Hamming weight of a vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
and call
\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{0}
the Hamming distance between
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
.
For a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
, let
\lambda(\mathcal{C})=\min_{\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathcal{C}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}\|\boldsymbol{c}\|_{0}
be the Hamming minimum distance of
\mathcal{C}
, and let
\mathrm{dist}(\boldsymbol{y},\mathcal{C}):=\min_{\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathcal{C}}\lVert\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{c}\rVert_{0}
denote the Hamming distance between a vector
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
and
\mathcal{C}
. Let
\mathcal{B}_{q,m}(r)=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{0}\leqslant r\}
denote the Hamming ball of radius
r
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
.

We define two fundamental promise problems from coding theory.

Definition 2.5

(Nearest Codeword Problem)
.

The
\gamma
-approximate Nearest Codeword Problem over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
(
\gamma
-
\textnormal{NCP}_{q}
)
is the decisional promise problem defined as follows.
On input a generator matrix
G\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times n}
, target
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
, and distance parameter
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, the goal is to decide between the following two cases when one is guaranteed to hold:

•

(YES)
\mathrm{dist}(\mathcal{C}(G),\boldsymbol{t})\leqslant k
,

•

(NO)
\mathrm{dist}(\mathcal{C}(G),\boldsymbol{t})>\gamma k
.

The parameter of interest is
k
.

Remark 2.6
.

A scaling argument shows that the NO case in
Definition

2.5
is equivalent to

•

(NO)
\mathrm{dist}(\mathcal{C}(G),\alpha\boldsymbol{t})>\gamma k
for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}\setminus\{0\}
.

The following results establish the
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness and parameterized fine-grained hardness of NCP.

Theorem 2.7

(
[
BBE+21
, Theorem 5.1, adapted]
)
.

For any prime power
q\geqslant 2
and real number
\gamma\geqslant 1
it holds that
\gamma
-
\textnormal{NCP}_{q}
is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard.

Theorem 2.8

(
[
Man20
, Corollary 5, adapted]
)
.

For any fixed prime power
q
and
\gamma\geqslant 1
and any function
T
, assuming randomized Gap-ETH, there is no randomized algorithm running in time
T(k)n^{o(k)}
which decides
\gamma
-
\textnormal{NCP}_{q}
with probability at least
2/3
, where
n
is the dimension of the input code.

We remark that
[
BBE+21
]
states
Theorem

2.7
as the
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness of the “
\gamma
-MLD
q
” problem (where “MLD” stands for “Maximum Likelihood Decoding” and the parameter of interest is again the input distance
k
), which is equivalent to the
\gamma
-
\textnormal{NCP}_{q}
problem.
More precisely, the input to the
\gamma
-MLD
q
problem consists of a parity-check matrix
H\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{h\times m}
, a target
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m}
, and a distance bound
k
, and we must decide whether there exists a vector
\boldsymbol{e}
with
\|\boldsymbol{e}\|_{0}\leqslant k
such that
H\boldsymbol{e}=H\boldsymbol{t}
(i.e.,
\boldsymbol{t}
and
\boldsymbol{e}
have the same syndrome), or whether all such vectors
\boldsymbol{e}
have Hamming weight larger than
\gamma k
.
This is equivalent to
\gamma
-
\textnormal{NCP}_{q}
because we can efficiently compute the generator matrix
G
of the code with parity-check matrix
H
and vice-versa, and because
H\boldsymbol{e}=H\boldsymbol{t}
if and only if
\boldsymbol{t}=\boldsymbol{c}+\boldsymbol{e}
for some codeword
\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathcal{C}(G)
.
Moreover,
[
BBE+21
]
only stated the result for prime
q
.
However, direct inspection of
[
BBE+21
, Section 5.2]
shows that their proof also yields the more general version stated in
Theorem

2.7
.
In particular,
[
BBE+21
, Definition 5.3]
, including the two observations there, generalizes to arbitrary finite fields.

Definition 2.9

(Minimum Distance Problem)
.

The
\gamma
-approximate Minimum Distance Problem over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
(
\gamma
-
\textnormal{MDP}_{q}
)
is the decisional promise problem defined as follows. On input a generator matrix
G\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m\times n}
and distance parameter
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, the goal is to decide between the following two cases when one is guaranteed to hold:

•

(YES)
\lambda(\mathcal{C}(G))\leqslant k
,

•

(NO)
\lambda(\mathcal{C}(G))>\gamma k
.

The parameter of interest is
k
.

2.3.1
Tensoring codes

The tensor product of linear codes is an important operation for building new codes with interesting properties by combining two linear codes.
In particular, tensoring can be used to boost the approximation factor in
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness results for NCP and MDP from some constant
\gamma>1
to an arbitrary constant.

Given two linear codes
\mathcal{C}(G_{1})
and
\mathcal{C}(G_{2})
with
G_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m_{i}\times n_{i}}
and minimum distance
d_{i}
for
i=1,2
, we define the associated tensor product code as

\mathcal{C}(G_{1})\otimes\mathcal{C}(G_{2}):=\mathcal{C}(G_{1}\otimes G_{2})\ \text{,}

where
G_{1}\otimes G_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{m_{1}m_{2}\times n_{1}n_{2}}
is the Kronecker product of
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
.
Furthermore, we have

\lambda(\mathcal{C}(G_{1})\otimes\mathcal{C}(G_{2}))=d_{1}\cdot d_{2}\ \text{.}

(2)

See, e.g.,
[
DMS03
, Section V.B]
for a proof.

Suppose that we know that
\gamma
-
\textnormal{MDP}_{q}
is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard (under randomized reductions) for
some

\gamma>1
.
Then, using
Equation

2
, we can immediately conclude that for any integer
c\geqslant 1
,
\gamma^{c}
-
\textnormal{MDP}_{q}
is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard (under randomized reductions) by considering the tensored
MDP
instances
(B^{\otimes c},k^{c})
,
where
B^{\otimes c}
denotes the
c
-fold Kronecker product of
B
with itself.
In particular, constructing tensored
MDP
instances in this way gives an FPT self-reduction from
\gamma
-
\textnormal{MDP}_{q}
to
\gamma^{c}
-
\textnormal{MDP}_{q}
.

2.4
Lattice problems

Let
\mathcal{L}(B)=\{B\boldsymbol{x}:\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\}
denote the lattice generated by the matrix
B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.
We call
n
the rank of
\mathcal{L}(B)
and write
\det(\mathcal{L}(B))=\sqrt{\det(B^{T}B)}
for the determinant of
\mathcal{L}(B)
, where
B^{T}
denotes the transpose of
B
.
For
p\in[1,\infty)
, we write
\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|{x_{i}}\right|^{p}\right)^{1/p}
for the
\ell_{p}
norm of a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
We use
\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})
to denote the
\ell_{p}
norm of a shortest nonzero vector in
\mathcal{L}
and set
\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\mathcal{L},\boldsymbol{t}):=\min_{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}}\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{t}\|_{p}
.
We write
\mathcal{B}^{(p)}_{m}(r)=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\|\boldsymbol{x}\|_{p}\leqslant r\}
for the closed, centered
\ell_{p}
ball of radius
r
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

We define two fundamental promise problems related to lattices.

Definition 2.10

(Closest Vector Problem)
.

The
\gamma
-approximate Closest Vector Problem with respect to the
p
-norm (
\gamma
-
\mathrm{CVP}_{p}
)
is the decisional promise problem defined as follows.
On input a generator matrix
B\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
, a target
\boldsymbol{t}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
, and a distance parameter
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, the goal is to decide between the following two cases when one is guaranteed to hold:

•

(YES)
\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\mathcal{L}(B),\boldsymbol{t})\leqslant k
,

•

(NO)
\mathrm{dist}_{p}(\mathcal{L}(B),\alpha\boldsymbol{t})>\gamma k
for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
.

The parameter of interest is
k
.

The definition above is a slight (but widely used) variant of the original Closest Vector Problem, since in the NO case we also require all multiples
\alpha\boldsymbol{t}
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
to be far from
\mathcal{L}(B)
.
The following results about the
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hardness and parameterized fine-grained hardness of this variant of CVP are known to hold.

Theorem 2.11

(
[
BBE+21
, Theorem 7.2]
)
.

For any real numbers
\gamma,p\geqslant 1
it holds that
\gamma
-
\mathrm{CVP}_{p}
is
\mathsf{W}[1]
-hard.

Theorem 2.12

(
[
Man20
, Corollary 6, adapted]
)
.

For any fixed
p,\gamma\geqslant 1
and any function
T
, assuming randomized Gap-ETH, there is no randomized algorithm running in time
T(k)n^{o(k^{p})}
which decides
\gamma
-
\mathrm{CVP}_{p}
with probability at least
2/3
, where
n
is the rank of the input lattice.

Definition 2.13

(Shortest Vector Problem)
.

The
\gamma
-approximate Shortest Vector Problem with respect to the
\ell_{p}
-norm (
\gamma
-SVP
p
)
is the decisional promise problem defined as follows.
On input a generator matrix
B\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
and a distance parameter
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, the goal is to decide between the following two cases when one is guaranteed to hold:

•

(YES)
\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L}(B))\leqslant k
,

•

(NO)
\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L}(B))>\gamma k
.

The parameter of interest is
k
.

2.4.1
Tensoring lattices

Analogously to the coding setting, we can also consider the tensor product of lattices.
Given two lattices
\mathcal{L}(B_{1})
and
\mathcal{L}(B_{2})
with basis matrices
B_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m_{1}\times n_{1}}
and
B_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m_{2}\times n_{2}}
, we define the associated tensor product lattice as

\mathcal{L}(B_{1})\otimes\mathcal{L}(B_{2}):=\mathcal{L}(B_{1}\otimes B_{2})\ \text{,}

where
B_{1}\otimes B_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m_{1}m_{2}\times n_{1}n_{2}}
is the Kronecker product of
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
.
The tensor product lattice is independent of the bases we choose for the two underlying lattices.

Unlike for codes, it is not true that repeated tensoring of lattices allows us to generically boost the approximation factor in known hardness results for
\mathrm{CVP}
and
\mathrm{SVP}
.
Indeed, while it always holds that

\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L}(B_{1})\otimes\mathcal{L}(B_{2}))\leqslant\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L}(B_{1}))\cdot\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L}(B_{2}))\ \text{,}

it may happen that the left-hand side of this inequality is significantly smaller than the right-hand side.
For an example, see
[
HR12
, Lemma 2.3]
.
Therefore, additional effort is required to prove special structural properties of our
\mathrm{CVP}
and
\mathrm{SVP}
instances to ensure that tensoring them allows us to boost the approximation factor in our hardness results.

2.5
Locally dense codes and lattices

Our randomized FPT reductions from NCP to MDP and from CVP to SVP use families of
locally dense
codes and lattices with appropriate parameters.
Precise definitions of such objects follow below.

Definition 2.14

(Locally dense code)
.

Fix a real number
\alpha\in(0,1)
, positive integers
d,N,m,n
, and a prime power
q
.
A
(q,\alpha,d,N,m,n)
-locally dense code
is specified by a generator matrix

We assume that the first two properties do not hold and show that the third property holds in that case.
Since there is a basis matrix of
\mathcal{L}
with at most
d^{p}
nonzero rows, we conclude that
r\leqslant d^{p}
and that every vector in
\mathcal{L}
has Hamming weight at most
d^{p}
.
By the properties of
(B,k)
, this implies that
\mathcal{L}\subseteq 2\mathbb{Z}^{m}
.
Therefore, it must be the case that there is a basis matrix of
\mathcal{L}
that has at most
(d/2)^{p}
nonzero rows, and so every vector in
\mathcal{L}
has Hamming weight at most
(d/2)^{p}
.
As a result, we also gather that all nonzero vectors
\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathcal{L}
satisfy
\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{p}>d^{c+3p/2}
, and so

\det(\mathcal{L})\geqslant\left(\frac{\lambda_{1}^{(2)}(\mathcal{L})}{\sqrt{r}}\right)^{r}\geqslant\left(\frac{d^{-p/2}\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L})}{\sqrt{r}}\right)^{r}>d^{r(c+p/2)},

where the first inequality follows from
Lemma

B.1
, the second inequality holds by
Equation

18
and the fact that every vector in
\mathcal{L}
has Hamming weight at most
d^{p}
, and the third inequality uses the fact that
r\leqslant d^{p}
.
∎

We will also need the following technical lemma from
[
HR12
]
, which is specific for the
\ell_{2}
norm.

Lemma B.3

(
[
HR12
, Claim 3.6]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
be integer lattices of rank
r\geqslant 1
generated by the bases

U=(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{u}_{r})
and
W=(\boldsymbol{w}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{w}_{r})
, respectively.
Consider the vector
\boldsymbol{v}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\boldsymbol{u}_{i}\otimes\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{2}
.
Then, it holds that

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}\geqslant\sqrt{r}(\det(\mathcal{L}_{1})\cdot\det(\mathcal{L}_{2}))^{1/r}.

We are now ready to prove
Lemma

5.1
.

Proof of
Lemma

5.1
.

It suffices to show that if
(B,k)
is a NO instance of
\gamma
-
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
satisfying the conditions from the lemma statement,
\mathcal{L}_{1}=\mathcal{L}(B)
, and
\mathcal{L}_{2}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{c^{\prime}}\mathcal{L}
for some integer
1\leqslant c^{\prime}<c
and such that
\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L}_{2})>d^{c^{\prime}}
, then

\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{2})>d^{c^{\prime}+1}.

Consider an arbitrary nonzero vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathcal{L}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{L}_{2}
.
As shown in
[
HR12
, Proof of Lemma 3.4]
,
we can write
\boldsymbol{v}=B^{\prime}_{1}(B^{\prime}_{2})^{T}
for
full-column-rank matrices
B^{\prime}_{1}
and
B^{\prime}_{2}
(note that
B^{\prime}_{1}
and
B^{\prime}_{2}
have the same number of columns)
such that
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{i}=\mathcal{L}(B^{\prime}_{i})\subseteq\mathcal{L}_{i}
for
i=1,2
.
We now proceed by case analysis based on
Lemma

B.2
applied to
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{1}
:

•

B^{\prime}_{1}
has more than
d^{p}
nonzero rows:
In this case, more than
d^{p}
rows of
B^{\prime}_{1}(B^{\prime}_{2})^{T}
are nonzero vectors from
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{2}
, and so

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{p}>d\cdot\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{2})\geqslant d\cdot\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L}_{2})>d^{c^{\prime}+1}.

•

B^{\prime}_{1}
has only even entries and has more than
(d/2)^{p}
nonzero rows:
In this case, more than
(d/2)^{p}
rows of
B^{\prime}_{1}(B^{\prime}_{2})^{T}
are even multiples of nonzero vectors from
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{2}
, and so

\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{p}>2((d/2)^{p})^{1/p}\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{2})\geqslant d\cdot\lambda_{1}^{(p)}(\mathcal{L}_{2})>d^{c^{\prime}+1}.

•

\det(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{1})>d^{r(c+p/2)}
and
B^{\prime}_{1}
has at most
(d/2)^{p}
nonzero rows. In particular,
r\leqslant(d/2)^{p}
and every vector in
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{1}
has Hamming weight at most
(d/2)^{p}
:
In this case, we have

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{p}

\displaystyle\geqslant d^{\frac{p}{\max(2,p)}-\frac{p}{2}}\|\boldsymbol{v}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\geqslant d^{\frac{p}{\max(2,p)}-\frac{p}{2}}\sqrt{r}(\det(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{1})\cdot\det(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{2}))^{1/r}

\displaystyle\geqslant d^{\frac{p}{\max(2,p)}-\frac{p}{2}}\sqrt{r}\det(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{1})^{1/r}

\displaystyle>d^{\frac{p}{\max(2,p)}-\frac{p}{2}}\cdot d^{c+p/2}

\displaystyle\geqslant d^{c^{\prime}+1}\ .

The first inequality follows from
Equation

17
and the fact that
\boldsymbol{v}
has support size at most
d^{p}
.
The second inequality holds via
Lemma

B.3
applied to
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{2}
, which are both rank-
r
lattices.
The third inequality uses the fact that
\det(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{2})\geqslant 1
, which holds because
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{2}
is a non-trivial integer lattice (this in turn holds by the definition of
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{2}
and the fact that
\boldsymbol{v}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
.).
The fourth inequality holds by the lower bound on
\det(\mathcal{L}^{\prime}_{1})
and the fact that
c^{\prime}\leqslant c-1
.∎
