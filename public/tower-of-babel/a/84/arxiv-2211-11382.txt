Sebastian Allmeier, Nicolas Gast

Abstract
. Mean field approximation is a powerful technique which has been used in many settings to study large-scale stochastic systems. In the case of two-timescale systems, the approximation is obtained by a combination of scaling arguments and the use of the averaging principle. This paper analyzes the approximation error of this ‘average’ mean field model for a two-timescale model ( 𝑿 , 𝒀 ) (\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y}) , where the slow component 𝑿 \boldsymbol{X} describes a population of interacting particles which is fully coupled with a rapidly changing environment 𝒀 \boldsymbol{Y} . The model is parametrized by a scaling factor N N , e.g. the population size, which as N N gets large decreases the jump size of the slow component in contrast to the unchanged dynamics of the fast component. We show that under relatively mild conditions, the ‘average’ mean field approximation has a bias of order O ⁡ ( 1 / N ) O(1/N) compared to 𝔼 ⁡ [ 𝑿 ] \mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{X}] . This holds true under any continuous performance metric in the transient regime, as well as for the steady-state if the model is exponentially stable. To go one step further, we derive a bias correction term for the steady-state, from which we define a new approximation called the refined ‘average’ mean field approximation whose bias is of order O ⁡ ( 1 / N 2 ) O(1/N^{2}) . This refined ‘average’ mean field approximation allows computing an accurate approximation even for small scaling factors, i.e., N ≈ 10 − 50 N\approx 10-50 . We illustrate the developed framework and accuracy results through an application to a random access CSMA model.



1.
Introduction

The mean field approximation finds widespread application when interested in analyzing the macroscopic behavior of large-scale stochastic systems composed of interacting particles. Its assets lie in a reduction of the model complexity, simplified analysis of the system due to absence of stochastic components, and reduction of computation time compared to a stochastic simulation. The mean field approximation can even yield closed form solutions for the steady-state, e.g., for the well known JSQ(d) model
(
Mitzenmacher 2001
)
. The mean field approximation is generally given by a set of ordinary differential equations which arise from the assumption that, for large system sizes, the evolution of the particles are stochastically independent of another. This idea works well if the number of particles is large and if the particles can be clustered into a few groups with statistically identical behavior
(
Kurtz 1970
;
Kurtz 1978
;
Benaim and Boudec 2008
;
Le Boudec et al. 2007
;
Gast and Gaujal 2012
)
. The framework established by Kurtz
(
Kurtz 1970
)
to derive (weak) convergence results for the stochastic system justifying the use of the mean field approximations finds sustained attention in the literature.

More recently, the authors of
(
Gast 2017
;
Ying 2016
)
showed that for finite system sizes the bias of the mean field approximation is of order
1/N
when compared to the mean behavior of the system. Here,
N
is the scaling parameter, which usually refers to the number of homogeneous particles in the system. Additional works such as
(
Gast and Van Houdt 2017
;
Gast et al. 2019
)
introduced corrections, called refinement terms, which effectively increase the rate of accuracy of the approximation and therefore the rate of convergence. The most notable term is the first-order bias refinement, since it offers a convincing trade-off between a significant accuracy gain and additional computation cost.

While these classical mean field results hold for a broad class of models, most of the results can not be transferred to systems with more intricate dynamics such as the two-timescale case, studied for instance in
(
Benaim and Boudec 2008
;
Bordenave et al. 2010
)
. A two-timescale process consists of two coupled components, one evolving slowly compared to the other. The slowly evolving component is often represented by a system of interacting particles, where the state of each particle evolves as a function of the empirical distribution of all particles but also as a function of the state of the fast component, e.g., a fast changing environment. These types of processes and their ‘averaged’ mean field adaptation have been of interest since the 1960s and became increasingly relevant in the study of modern and complex systems. We refer to
(
Pavliotis and Stuart 2008
)
for an extensive literature discussion. An important area of application comes from the field of computer networks. Examples include loss networks
(
Hunt and Kurtz 1994
)
, large-scale random access networks with interference graphs
(
Cecchi et al. 2021
;
Castiel et al. 2021
)
or storage networks
(
Feuillet and Robert 2014
)
. Another recent field of literature from which we draw inspiration are chemical reaction networks. The works of Kang et al.
(
Kang and Kurtz 2013
;
Kang et al. 2014
)
and Ball et al.
(
Ball et al. 2006
)
establish central limit theorems for large multiscale models motivated by biological and chemical processes. Another application in the field of biology is given by
(
Robert and Vignoud 2021
)
who use the mean field idea to study neural plasticity models.

Contributions

The aforementioned papers underline that the mean field idea can be adapted to two-timescale models and prove that the ‘average’ mean field approximation is asymptotically exact as
N
goes to infinity. They do not, however, provide theoretical results which guarantee the accuracy or performance bounds of the approximation for finite systems sizes. This paper aims at filling this gap.
We derive accuracy bounds for the ‘average’ mean field approximation in the transient regime and steady-state which show that its bias is of order
O(1/N)
,
N
being the scaling parameter of the stochastic system. We further derive bias correction terms for the steady-state, from which we define a new approximation called the refined ‘average’ mean field approximation, whose bias is of order
O(1/N^{2})
.
To prove these accuracy bounds, we develop a framework for two-timescale stochastic models whose slow component is comparable to the concept of density dependent population processes as introduced by Kurtz
(
Kurtz 1978
)
. Based on this representation, we utilize a combination of generator comparison techniques, Poisson equations as well as derivative bounds on the solution of the Poisson equation. This allows us to bound the bias with respect to the scaling parameter
N
. To take it a step further, we then prove the existence of correction terms which approximate the bias of the ‘average’ mean field and allow defining the refined ‘average’ mean field. To support the practical application of the refinements, we provide an algorithmic way to compute the correction terms. This includes methods to numerically solve the Poisson equation and obtain its derivatives. We illustrate the computation of the refinement terms and confirm the accuracy of the obtained bounds by considering a random access CSMA model. Using the example we show that even for relatively small
N\approx 10
the refined ‘average’ mean field approximation almost exactly indicates the steady-state of the model.

Methodological Advances & Technical Challenges

To obtain our results, we build on the recent line of work on Stein’s method
(
Stein 1986
)
. This method allows calculating the distance between two random variables by looking at the distance between the generators of two related systems. Recently, the method reemerged in publications within the stochastic network community, in particular the works of Braverman et al.
(
Braverman et al. 2016
;
Braverman and Dai 2017
;
Braverman 2022
)
. In this paper, we use the Poisson equation idea in two ways. First and foremost, we use a Poisson equation, called the ‘fast’ Poisson equation, to bound the distance between a function – in this case the drift – and its average version given by the steady-state distribution of the fast process as introduced in Section
4.2
. This step is integral to our paper and constitutes the building block to obtain the error bounds and closed form expressions as it allows to analyze the distance between the coupled stochastic process and its decoupled counterpart. The analysis however bears many technical intricacies one needs to overcome. This includes solving the Poisson equation, stating its derivative bounds and deducing computable expressions used to calculate the bias term.
Second, for our steady-state results, we make use of another Poisson equation to compare the stochastic system to the equilibrium point of the approximation. The latter is related to the methods used in
(
Ying 2016
;
Gast and Van Houdt 2017
)
but significantly extends the ideas as the derivation of the bias exhibits novel refinement terms which originate from the coupling and correct the error of the ‘averaging’ method used for the mean field. The closely linked technical challenges include obtaining numerically feasible formulas for the ‘new’ refinement terms. Here, we utilize the derived solution of the ‘fast’ Poisson equation to further specify the fluctuations of the stochastic system around the equilibrium point. Carefully analyzing the combination of the two Poisson equations enables us to obtain the new refinement terms. The closed-form bias terms that we obtained from the steady-state analysis are significantly more complicated than the ones of
(
Gast and Van Houdt 2017
)
as they further correct the error made by the averaging method.

Applicability & Numerical Difficulties

In our paper, we make use of a CSMA model to demonstrate the applicability of our results.
There are several other examples captured by our framework, such as the Michaelis-Menten enzyme model of
(
Kang et al. 2014
)
or the storage network investigated in
(
Feuillet and Robert 2014
)
. For the latter, the authors observe that when looking at the right timescale the loss of the network can be characterized by a local equilibrium which is obtained using the averaging method. The biochemical Michaelis-Menten enzyme model describes the dynamics between three time-varying species, the enzyme, a substrate and a product. Following the description of the model as in
(
Kang et al. 2014
)
and by using the right scaling arguments, the model exhibits two timescales, the fast reacting and state changing enzymes and the slower changing concentrations of the substrate and product. Our framework can be used on both examples to guarantee accuracy results and can be used to compute refined approximations.

To compute the ‘average’ mean field and refinement terms, one has to overcome numerical difficulties which arise from the averaging method. First, in order to compute the ‘average’ drift, one needs to compute the steady-state probabilities of the fast system, which might not be available in the closed form as for the CSMA model. For this case we provide computational notes in the appendix which aim to facilitate the computation. Another problem for the refinement term is the need for the first and second derivatives of the ‘average’ drift. For the CSMA model we used symbolic representation of the transition rates from which we define the drift and its average version. This method allows to numerically compute the derivatives using ‘sympy’, a Python library for symbolic computation. This method is relatively easy to implement but has a very large computation time. This computation time could be reduced by implementing a faster computation of the derivatives. In fact, to compute the derivative of the matrix
K^{+}
which is closely linked to the solution of the ‘fast’ Poisson, we provide supplementary computational notes which describe how to obtain an efficient implementation.

2.
Stochastic System and Mean Field Approximation

We consider a two-timescale, coupled, continuous time Markov chain
(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}^{(N)},\boldsymbol{Y}_{s}^{(N)})_{s\geq 0}
parametrized by a scaling factor
N
, for which we study the behavior as
N
tends to infinity. As we will see in the examples,
N
typically represents the number of objects that interact together. This section introduces the precise model and fixes notations.

2.1.
Model

For a fixed scaling parameter
N
, the stochastic process
(\boldsymbol{X}^{(N)}_{s},\boldsymbol{Y}^{(N)}_{s})_{s\geq 0}
is a continuous time Markov chain that evolves in a state-space
\mathcal{X}^{(N)}\times\mathcal{Y}
. The set
\mathcal{Y}
is finite and does not depend on
N
. We further require that for all
N
, the sets
\mathcal{X}^{(N)}
are subsets of a convex and compact set
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
. In what follows, unless it is ambiguous in the context, we drop the dependence on
N
to lighten the notations.

This model has two-timescales in the sense that the size order of jumps of
\boldsymbol{Y}_{s}
is
N
-times larger than the ones of
\boldsymbol{X}_{s}
. More precisely, we assume that there exists a finite number of transitions
(\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}
with their corresponding transition rate functions
\alpha_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}\geq 0
, both being independent of
N
, such that for all possible states
(\boldsymbol{X}_{s},\boldsymbol{Y}_{s})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
:

(1)

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{X}_{s},\boldsymbol{Y}_{s})\text{ jumps to }(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}+\ell/N,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\text{ at rate }N\alpha_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{s},\boldsymbol{Y}_{s}).

The above defines continuous time Markov chains with discrete state-space whose realizations are Càdlàg, i.e., right continuous with a left limit for every time
t
. Note that in Equation (
1
), we assume that the transition rates
\alpha
are defined for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
(and not just for
x\in\mathcal{X}^{(N)}
).

The notion of slow-fast system comes from the fact that the jumps of the slow component
\mathcal{X}
are
O(N)
times smaller than the jumps of the fast component
\mathcal{Y}
while transition rates are of the same scale. As we will see later, the different timescales imply that for large
N
, the slow component
\boldsymbol{X}_{s}
will ’see’ the fast component
\boldsymbol{Y}_{s}
as if it is stationary with distribution
(\pi_{\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}))_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}
which we formally define in Section
2.2
.

2.2.
Drift, Average Drift and Mean Field Approximation

The jumps of the stochastic system (
1
) can affect the fast and/or the slow component. In what follows, we construct an approximation that consists
(i)
in considering that the slow component is not stochastic but evolves deterministically according to its drift (which is its average change), and
(ii)
in using a time-averaging method that shows the stochastic process
\boldsymbol{Y}
as being in some stationary state given
\boldsymbol{x}
. This leads us to two definitions:

(i)

We call the drift of the slow system (or more concisely the drift) the average change of
\boldsymbol{X}_{s}
. It is the sum over all possible transitions of the rate of transition multiplied by the changes that such transitions induce on
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
. By the form of the transitions in (
1
), if the process starts in
(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},\boldsymbol{Y}_{t})=(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
, the drift is given by:

(2)

\displaystyle F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):=\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}\alpha_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}.

This drift function depends on the state of the fast system
\boldsymbol{y}
.

(ii)

For the fast component, we define a transition kernel
K_{\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x})
that is the rate at which the process
\boldsymbol{Y}
jumps from
\boldsymbol{y}
to
\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\neq\boldsymbol{y}
(divided by
N
), with the usual convention that
K_{\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{x})=-\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\neq\boldsymbol{y}}K_{\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x})
:

(3)

\displaystyle K_{\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{\ell}\alpha_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}).

As
\mathcal{Y}
is finite, for a fixed
\boldsymbol{x}
,
K(\boldsymbol{x})
is a matrix that corresponds to the kernel of a continuous time Markov chain. Our assumptions will imply that for all
\boldsymbol{x}
, the process associated with
K(\boldsymbol{x})
has a unique stationary distribution, which we denote by the vector
\pi(\boldsymbol{x})=(\pi_{\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{x}))_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}
.

Based on the drift (
2
) of the stochastic system, we define its ‘average’ version
\bar{F}
by averaging over the stationary distribution of the fast component. That is:

\displaystyle\bar{F}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\sum_{y}\pi_{y}(\boldsymbol{x})F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}).

For an initial state
\boldsymbol{x}
and
t\geq 0
, we call the mean field approximation the solution
\phi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})
of the initial value problem

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})=\bar{F}(\phi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})),\qquad\phi_{0}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{x}.

Such an approximation is also called a fluid approximation.

3.
Main Results

This section includes our main results which are threefold. In

3.1.
Transient Regime

Our model is a two timescale model, which makes it amenable to be analyzed by time-averaging methods such as the one used in
(
Benaim and Boudec 2008
;
Robert and Vignoud 2021
;
Cecchi et al. 2021
;
Castiel et al. 2021
)

Our first result, Theorem

approximates the average behavior of the slow component of the two-timescale system with a bias asymptotically equivalent to

(
Gast et al. 2019
)
for classical density dependent process.

Consider the two-timescale stochastic system
(\boldsymbol{X}^{(N)},\boldsymbol{Y}^{(N)})_{t\geq 0}
as introduced in Section
2

\boldsymbol{X}_{0}^{(N)},\boldsymbol{Y}_{0}^{(N)}=\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}
, and recall that
\phi(\boldsymbol{x})
is the solution of the ODE (
4
) with initial condition
\boldsymbol{x}
. Further, assume
(

A
1

)
and
(

A
2

)
. For any

and

such that:

\displaystyle\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}N\left(\mathbb{E}[h(\boldsymbol{X}^{(N)}_{t})\mid\boldsymbol{X}_{0}^{(N)},\boldsymbol{Y}_{0}^{(N)}=\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}]-h\bigl(\phi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr)\right)=C_{h}(t).

Main element of the proofs.

The full proof of this theorem is given in Section

•

The first is to approximate the infinitesimal generator

4.1.
Stochastic Semi-Groups and Generators

Given the stochastic process of Section
2.1
, we define the stochastic semi-group operator which maps a pair of initial values
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
and a function
h
to the expected value of the system at time
t
. This semi-group
\psi_{t}
associates to a function
h:\mathcal{X}^{(N)}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
the function
\psi_{t}h:\mathcal{X}^{(N)}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as:

(5)

\displaystyle\psi_{t}h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\mathbb{E}[h(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},\boldsymbol{Y}_{t})\mid X_{0},Y_{0}=\boldsymbol{x},y].

Using the right continuity of the slow-fast system, it is easy to verify that
\psi_{t}
is indeed a
C_{0}
-semi-group (see a more precise definition in Definition
1
in Appendix
A.1
).

The infinitesimal generator of the stochastic process is the operator
L
that maps a function
h\in\mathcal{D}^{k}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
to
Lh:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by:

(6)

\displaystyle Lh(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}N\alpha_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\left(h(x+\ell/N,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})-h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\right).

Note that
Lh
is obtained by considering average infinitesimal change of the stochastic system starting in
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle Lh(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\bigl(\psi_{t}h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})-\psi_{0}h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\bigr)/t=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[h(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},\boldsymbol{Y}_{t})\mid X_{0},Y_{0}=\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}]-h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\bigr)/t.

Similarly to the notations of the semi-group and generator of the stochastic process, for a given function
h\in\mathcal{D}^{k}(\mathcal{X})
, we denote by
\Phi_{t}h(\boldsymbol{x}):=h(\phi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))
the
C_{0}
-semi-group corresponding to ODE (
4
). For differentiable
h
, the infinitesimal generator is given by

(7)

\displaystyle\Lambda h(\boldsymbol{x}):=D_{x}h(\boldsymbol{x})\bar{F}(\boldsymbol{x}).

To strengthen intuition, the time derivative of
h(\phi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))
can be expressed as

(8)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}h(\phi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))=D_{x}h(\phi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))\bar{F}(\phi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))=\Phi_{t}\Lambda h(\boldsymbol{x}).

By commuting
\Lambda
and
\Phi_{t}
we have
\Lambda\Phi_{t}h(\boldsymbol{x})=D_{x}(h\circ\phi_{t})(\boldsymbol{x})\bar{F}(\boldsymbol{x})
which is equal to (
8
) by the results of Appendix
A.1
. We will use this property to prove the theorems of the following section.

Abuse of Notations and Dependence on the Fast Component

The semi-group and generator of the stochastic system are generally defined for functions
h
in
\mathcal{D}^{k}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
, i.e., functions which depend on the slow and fast component whereas the semi-group and generator of the ODE are defined for
h
in
\mathcal{D}^{k}(\mathcal{X})
that do
not
depend on the fast component.
What we refer to as abuse of notation is the notation we use for the mapping
\psi_{t}
of a function
h\in\mathcal{D}^{k}(\mathcal{X})
.
\psi_{t}h
still depends on the fast component even if
h
does not (since the evolution
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
depends on the state of
\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}
and the initial values
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
):

(9)

\displaystyle\psi_{t}h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\mathbb{E}[h(\boldsymbol{X}_{t})\mid\boldsymbol{X}_{0},\boldsymbol{Y}_{0}=\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}],

and

\displaystyle Lh(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}N\alpha_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\bigl(h(\boldsymbol{x}+\ell/N)-h(\boldsymbol{x})\bigr).

In essence, this allows to use the notion of the semi-group and generator to functions of the slow process.
For consistency, we do the same for the ODE: for an arbitrary
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
,
\phi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):=\phi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})
and therefore
\Phi_{t}h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=h(\phi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}))=h(\phi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))
which is motivated by the abuse of notation in (
9
). This will merely be used in the proofs and allows focusing on integral proof ideas instead of complex notations.

4.2.
Generator of the Fast Process and Regularity of the Poisson Equation

The generator
L
describes the changes induced by the transitions of the fast and slow process. In our analysis, it will be useful to analyze the changes due to the jumps in
Y
only. We denote by
L_{\text{fast}}
the generator of the Markov chain induced by
K(\boldsymbol{x})
that takes as input a function
h\in\mathcal{D}^{k}(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y})
and associates another function
L_{\text{fast}}h
defined by:

\displaystyle L_{\text{fast}}h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}K_{\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x})(h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})-h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})).

Compared to (
1
),
L_{\text{fast}}
is independent of
N
because the transition rates are rescaled by
1/N
.

Under Assumption
(

A
2

)
, for all
\boldsymbol{x}
, the matrix
K(\boldsymbol{x})
characterizes a Markov chain on the state space
\mathcal{Y}
that has a unique stationary distribution denoted by
\pi(\boldsymbol{x})
.
\pi_{y}(\boldsymbol{x})
is the stationary probability of
y\in\mathcal{Y}
of the Markov chain induced by the kernel
K(\boldsymbol{x})
.
Subsequently, we will study the distance between the ’true’ stochastic process
\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}
and an averaged system where the distribution of
\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}
is replaced by the stationary distribution
\pi(\boldsymbol{x})
. To quantify the error made when replacing
\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}
with the stationary distribution, we consider the following Poisson equation:

(10)

\displaystyle h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\pi_{y}(\boldsymbol{x})=L_{\text{fast}}G^{\text{fast}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}).

For a given function
h:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
(
n
is arbitrary but finite), a function
G^{\text{fast}}_{h}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
that satisfies the above equation is called a solution to this Poisson equation. We will have particular interest in
h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{x}}
namely when the drift
F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
is compared to its ‘average’ version
\bar{F}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{y}F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\pi(\boldsymbol{y})
.

The existence of a regular solution to this Poisson equation is guaranteed by the following Lemma
1
. Note that the solution of the above Poisson equation is not unique: If
G^{\text{fast}}_{h}
is a solution, then for any constant
c\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a function
G^{\text{fast}}_{h}+c
is also a solution. Later in the proofs of the theorems, when we talk about ‘a solution of the Poisson equation’, we refer to the solution given in Lemma
1
.

Lemma 1.

Assume
(

A
2

)
. Then for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{X}
:

(1)

The Markov chain corresponding to
K(\boldsymbol{x})
has a unique stationary distribution that we denote by
\pi(\boldsymbol{x})
. We denote by
\Pi(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathds{1}\ \pi^{T}(\boldsymbol{x})
the matrix where each line is equal to
\pi(\boldsymbol{x})
.

(2)

The matrix
(K(\boldsymbol{x})+\Pi(\boldsymbol{x}))
is invertible and its inverse is a generalized inverse of
K(\boldsymbol{x})
.

(3)

Define
K^{+}(\boldsymbol{x})=(K(\boldsymbol{x})+\Pi(\boldsymbol{x}))^{-1}(I-\Pi(\boldsymbol{x}))
, then, for all functions
h:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
G_{h}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}K^{+}_{\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x})h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})
is a solution of the Poisson Equation (
10
).

If, in addition, Assumption
(

A
1

)
holds, then
K^{+}(\boldsymbol{x})
is twice differentiable in
\boldsymbol{x}
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
B.1
. Note in particular, this result implies that if
h
is (twice) differentiable in
\boldsymbol{x}
then the same holds true for
G^{\text{fast}}_{h}
.

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
1
- Transient State Proof

The proof of Theorem
1
can be decomposed in two main parts. We first use a generator transformation to show that the slow system is well approximated by a system whose drift is
D_{x}h(\boldsymbol{X}_{t})\ F(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},\boldsymbol{Y}_{t})
(

). This leads us to treat terms of the form
\bar{F}(\boldsymbol{X}_{t})-F(\boldsymbol{X}_{t},\boldsymbol{Y}_{t})

4.3.1.
Error due to replacing the stochastic jumps of
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
by the drift

For
h\in\mathcal{D}^{2}(\mathcal{X})
recall that the
C_{0}
-semi-groups of the stochastic system and of the ODE are defined as

\displaystyle\psi_{s}h(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{0})=\mathbb{E}[h(\boldsymbol{X}_{s})\mid\boldsymbol{X}_{0},\boldsymbol{Y}_{0}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{0}]\quad\text{ and }\quad\Phi_{t}h(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{0})=h(\phi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})).

We define
\nu_{s}h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):=\psi_{s}\Phi_{t-s}h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
and rewrite

(11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h(\boldsymbol{X}_{t})-h(\phi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))\mid\boldsymbol{X}_{0},\boldsymbol{Y}_{0}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{0}]=\nu_{t}h(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{0})-\nu_{0}h(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{0})=\int_{0}^{t}\frac{d}{ds}\nu_{s}h(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{0})ds.

To show that the last equation indeed holds true, observe that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\nu_{s}h(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{ds}\psi_{s}\Phi_{t-s}h(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{0})

(12)

\displaystyle=L\psi_{s}\Phi_{t-s}h(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{0})-\psi_{s}\Lambda\Phi_{t-s}h(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{0}).

By regularity assumptions on the transition rates and bounded state space the above equation is finite for all
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
and time
s\geq 0
. The dominated convergence theorem thus justifies the interchange of derivative and integral and validates the last equality.
As pointed out in Appendix
A.1
,
\psi_{s}
and
L
, the stochastic semi-group and its infinitesimal generator, commute, i.e.,
L\psi_{s}=\psi_{s}L
. Hence,

(
12
)

\displaystyle=\psi_{s}(L-\Lambda)\Phi_{t-s}h(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{0})

(13)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[(L-\Lambda)\Phi_{t-s}h(\boldsymbol{X}_{s},\boldsymbol{Y}_{s})\mid\boldsymbol{X}_{0},\boldsymbol{Y}_{0}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{0}],

where the last line follows by definition of
\psi_{s}
.
Let
g(\boldsymbol{x})=h(\phi_{t-s}(\boldsymbol{x}))
.
g
is twice differentiable with respect to the initial condition
\boldsymbol{x}
as it lies in
\mathcal{D}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
by Lemma
3
. By the use of Taylor expansion rewrite
Lg
as

\displaystyle Lg(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}N\alpha_{\ell,y^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})(g(\boldsymbol{x}+\frac{\ell}{N})-g(\boldsymbol{x}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}\alpha_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})D_{x}g(\boldsymbol{x})\ell+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}\alpha_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})D^{2}_{x}g(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot(\ell,\ell)+o(\frac{\left\|g\right\|_{2,1}\sup_{\ell}\left|\ell\right|^{2}}{N})

\displaystyle=Dg(\boldsymbol{x})F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}\alpha_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})D^{2}_{x}g(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot(\ell,\ell)+o(\frac{C_{R}\left\|g\right\|_{2,1}\sup_{\ell}\left|\ell\right|^{2}}{N}),

with
D^{2}g(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot(\ell,\ell)=\sum_{m,n}\frac{\partial^{2}g}{\partial x_{m}\partial x_{n}}(\boldsymbol{x})\ell_{m}\ell_{n}
. The convergence depends further on the Hölder norm of
h(\phi(x))
, bounds on the transition rates
C_{R}
and jump sizes
\sup\left|\ell\right|
.
The generator of the ODE related semi-group,
\Lambda
, introduced in Equation (
7
), maps
g
to
\Lambda g(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=D_{x}g(\boldsymbol{x})\bar{F}(\boldsymbol{x})
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle(L-\Lambda)g(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=D_{x}g(\boldsymbol{x})(F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})-\bar{F}(\boldsymbol{x}))+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}\alpha_{\ell,y^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})D^{2}_{x}g(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot(\ell,\ell)+o(1/N).

4.3.2.
Error due to replacing the drift by the average drift

Next, we have a closer look at the first summand of the right hand side in the above equation. Denote by
G^{\text{fast}}_{F}
a solution of the Poisson equation (

’ of (

does not depend on

Combining the above with equation (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h(\boldsymbol{X}_{t})-h(\phi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}))]=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[L_{\text{fast}}D_{x}(h\circ\phi_{t-s})(\boldsymbol{X}_{s})\cdot G^{\text{fast}}_{F}(\boldsymbol{X}_{s},\boldsymbol{Y}_{s})]

(15)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}[\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}}\alpha_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{s},\boldsymbol{Y}_{s})D^{2}_{x}(h\circ\phi_{t-s})(\boldsymbol{X}_{s})\cdot(\ell,\ell)]ds+o(1/N),

\displaystyle 0=\frac{1}{N}H_{t}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{N}H_{0}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})-\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{N}D_{x}\bigl(D_{x}h(\phi_{t-s}(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}))\cdot\bar{F}(\phi_{t-s}(\boldsymbol{X}_{s}))\bigr)\cdot G^{\text{fast}}_{F}(\boldsymbol{X}_{s},\boldsymbol{Y}_{s})]ds

\displaystyle(L_{\text{fast}}-\frac{1}{N}L)D_{x}(h\circ\phi_{t-s})(X_{s})G^{\text{fast}}_{F}(X_{s},Y_{s})

4.4.
Proof of Theorem

- Steady-State Proof

Considering
\mathbb{E}[h(\boldsymbol{X}_{\infty},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\infty})-\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}}\pi_{y}(\phi_{\text{\tiny$\infty$}})h(\phi_{\text{\tiny$\infty$}},\boldsymbol{y})]
, first, add and subtract the term

into the equation, yielding

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h(\boldsymbol{X}_{\infty},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\infty})-\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}}\pi_{\boldsymbol{y}}(\phi_{\text{\tiny$\infty$}})h(\phi_{\text{\tiny$\infty$}},\boldsymbol{y})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[h(\boldsymbol{X}_{\infty},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\infty})-\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}}\pi_{\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\infty})h(\boldsymbol{X}_{\infty},\boldsymbol{y})]

(B)

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\mathbb{E}[\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}}\pi_{\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\infty})h(\boldsymbol{X}_{\infty},\boldsymbol{y})-\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}}\pi_{\boldsymbol{y}}(\phi_{\text{\tiny$\infty$}})h(\phi_{\text{\tiny$\infty$}},\boldsymbol{y})].

(‘fast’ Poisson Equation)

\displaystyle L_{\text{fast}}G^{\text{fast}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})-\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}}\pi_{\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{x})h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}),

(‘slow’ Poisson Equation)

\displaystyle\Lambda G^{\text{slow}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}}\pi_{\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{x})h(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})-\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}}\pi_{\boldsymbol{y}}(\phi_{\text{\tiny$\infty$}})h(\phi_{\text{\tiny$\infty$}},\boldsymbol{y}).

Recall that the existence and regularity properties of the function

. For

)

)

.

Next we use that taking the expectation of the generator

\mathbb{E}[Lg(\boldsymbol{X}_{\infty},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\infty})]=0.

By using Lemma

, it follows

(
A
)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[L_{\text{fast}}G^{\text{fast}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\infty},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\infty})-\frac{1}{N}LG^{\text{fast}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\infty},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\infty})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}\alpha_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\infty},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\infty})\ell\ D_{x}G^{\text{fast}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\infty},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})+o(1/N)].

For the second term, we apply the

which yields

(17)

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:B}=\mathbb{E}[\Lambda G^{\text{slow}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\infty})-LG^{\text{slow}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\infty},\boldsymbol{Y}_{\infty})].

Note that even if

\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
because the rate functions that appear in the generator

\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}

To illustrate our results, we consider the unsaturated CSMA random-access networks studied in
(

)
. In this paper, the authors use a two-scale model to study the performance of a CSMA algorithm with many nodes. The slow process corresponds to the arrival of jobs and the fast process corresponds to the activation and deactivation of nodes. The authors of this paper derive a mean field approximation and show that it is asymptotically exact. With our methods, we go two steps further:

By using Theorem

(

5.1.
Model Description

We consider a model with
C
server types, with
N
statistically identical servers for each class. All servers communicate through a wireless medium using a random-access protocol and have a finite buffer size
B
. The
C
classes form an interference graph
G=(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{E})
with
\mathcal{C}=\{1,\dots,C\}
the classes and
\mathcal{E}
the network specific edges. (see for instance Figure
1
for examples of graphs with three or five classes). This interference graph indicates that two servers cannot transmit simultaneously if they either are of the same class or belong to an adjacent class. For each class
c\in\mathcal{C}
, we will denote by
y_{c}\in\{0,1\}
a variable that equals
1
if a node of class
c
is transmitting and
0
otherwise. We denote by
\mathcal{Y}
the set of possible activation vectors
y\in\{0,1\}^{C}
. It is equal to the set of the independent sets of the graph, i.e., all activity vectors for which an active node has only inactive neighbors. For instance, for the graph with three classes which are linearly connected as shown in Figure
1(a)
, the set of feasible states is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}=\{(0,0,0),(1,0,0),(0,1,0),(0,0,1),(1,0,1)\}.

Any node can turn active if there are no neighboring active nodes. Once its transmission is finished, it changes back to an idle state. For a given class
c
, we define
\mathcal{Y}_{c}^{+}:=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:y_{c}=1\}
as the subset of states for which node
c
is active, and by
\mathcal{Y}_{c}^{-}:=\{y\in\mathcal{Y}:y_{c}=y_{d}=0\ \forall d\text{ s.t. }(c,d)\in\mathcal{E}\}
the subset of states from which node
c
can turn active, i.e., all neighboring nodes are inactive. For instance, for the linear
3
node graph of Figure
1(a)
and the class
c=1
this yields the following sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{1}^{+}=\{(1,0,0),(1,0,1)\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{1}^{-}=\{(0,0,0),(0,0,1)\}.

As in
(
Cecchi et al. 2021
)
, in this non-saturated model, we consider that if a node is in class
c
, new packets arrive to this node at rate
\lambda_{c}>0
. If a node has a packet to transmit and no neighboring node is transmitting, then this node becomes active at rate
\nu_{c}>0
. We assume that a transmission from a node of class
c
takes an exponential time of duration
1/(N\mu_{c})>0
, after which the packet leaves the system.

5.2.
Two-scale model representation

The model as described above fits in our two-timescale representation. To see why, for each class
c\in\mathcal{C}
and buffer size
b\in\{0\dots B\}
, we define
X^{(N)}_{t,(c,b)}
as the fraction of servers of class
c
that have at least
b
jobs in their queue at time
t
. We denote by
\boldsymbol{X}_{t}
the vector of all possible
X_{t,(c,b)}
for all
c\in\mathcal{C}
and
b\in\{0\dots B\}
. The fast component
\boldsymbol{Y}_{t}
is the activation at time
t
:
Y_{t,(c)}=1
if a node of class
c
is transmitting at time
t
and
0
otherwise.

Using this representation, we characterize the possible transitions. Given a state pair
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
, the transitions are represented as a transition vector of the form
(\ell/N,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})
and a corresponding transition rate
N\alpha_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
such that the state
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
jumps to
(\boldsymbol{x}+\ell/N,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})
at rate
N\alpha_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
. The transitions can be distinguished into three types:

•

Arrival of a packet to a server of class
c\in\mathcal{C}
:

\displaystyle\quad{\begin{array}[]{lcll}(\frac{e_{c,i}}{N},\boldsymbol{y})&\text{at rate}&N\lambda_{c}(1-x_{c,i})&\text{for }i=1,\\
(\frac{e_{c,i}}{N},\boldsymbol{y})&\text{at rate}&N\lambda_{c}(x_{c,i-1}-x_{c,i})&\text{for }2\leq i\leq B.\end{array}}

•

Back-off of a server of class
c\in\mathcal{C}
with at least one packet if the class activity vector allows the back-off, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}_{c}^{-}
:

\displaystyle\quad{\begin{array}[]{lcll}(-\frac{e_{c,i}}{N},\boldsymbol{y}+e_{c})&\text{at rate}&N\nu_{c}(x_{c,i}-x_{c,i+1})&\text{for }1\leq i<B,\\
(-\frac{e_{c,i}}{N},\boldsymbol{y}+e_{c})&\text{at rate}&N\nu_{c}x_{c,i}&\text{for }i=B.\\
\end{array}}

•

Transmission completion of an active node of class
c\in\mathcal{C}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{+}_{c}
:

\displaystyle\quad{\begin{array}[]{lcll}(0,\boldsymbol{y}-e_{c})&\text{at rate}&N\mu_{c}.&\\
\end{array}}

We denote the set of all possible transition vectors
(\ell/N,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})
from a state pair
(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
by
\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
.

5.3.
Steady-State distribution
\pi_{y}(x)
and average drift

By using the above transition definitions, the matrix
K
is given by:

•

If
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{-}_{c}
, then
K(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}+e_{c})=N\nu_{c}x_{c,1}
,

•

If
\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}^{+}_{c}
, then
K(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}-e_{c})=N\mu_{c}
,

all other entries of the matrix being
0
.

This representation is used by the authors of
(
Cecchi et al. 2021
)
to derive the product-form stationary distribution for a fixed server state
\boldsymbol{x}
. This product form is closely related to the product-form stationary distribution of saturated networks as found in
(
van de Ven et al. 2010
;
Wang and Kar 2005
;
Boorstyn et al. 1987
)
: The quantity
\pi_{\boldsymbol{y}}(x)
is calculated as follows:

\displaystyle\pi_{\boldsymbol{y}}(x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{Z(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})}{Z(\boldsymbol{x})},

with

\displaystyle Z(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\prod_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\bigl(\frac{\nu_{c}}{\mu_{c}}x_{c,1}\bigr)^{y_{c}},

\displaystyle Z(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}Z(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}).

Following our definition of Section
2.2
the drift and its average version are generically defined by:

\displaystyle F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{T}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})}\alpha_{\ell,\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\ell,

and

\displaystyle\bar{F}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{Y}}\pi_{\boldsymbol{y}}(\boldsymbol{x})F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}).

For the drift
F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
of the random access model this leads to the closed form expression

\displaystyle F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\bigl(\sum_{1<i\leq B}e_{c,i}\ \lambda_{c}(x_{c,i-1}-x_{c,i})+e_{c,1}\ \lambda_{c}(1-x_{c,1})

\displaystyle\qquad\quad-\mathbf{1}_{\{y\in\mathcal{Y}_{c}^{-}\}}(\sum_{1\leq i<B}e_{c,i}\ \nu_{c}(x_{c,i}-x_{c,i+1})+e_{c,B}\ \nu_{c}x_{c,B})\bigr).

It should be clear that assumption
(

A
1

)
holds in our case because the rates given in Section
5.2
are all continuous in
\boldsymbol{x}
(in fact they are all linear). Moreover, the model also satisfies Assumption
(

A
2

)
: For a given
\boldsymbol{x}
, the set of irreducible states for
K(\boldsymbol{x})
contains all the feasible activation vectors
y
such that
y_{c}=0
if
x_{(c,1)}=0
. The condition
x_{{c,1}}=0
implies that the nodes of class
c
do not have any packets to transmit. The situation of Assumption
(

A
3

)
is more complicated. To the best of our knowledge, there has not been a complete stability characterization for the unsaturated random access CSMA model. Cecchi et al. show in
(
Cecchi et al. 2021
)
that in the case of a complete interference graph stability conditions can be derived which assure global exponential stability. They further conjectured that similar results hold for general interference graphs. In our analysis, we assume that
(

A
3

)
holds.

Conclusion

In this paper we investigate the accuracy of the classical
averaging method
that is used to study two timescale models. We study a generic two timescale model and show that under mild regularity conditions, the bias of this ‘average’ mean field approximation is of order
O(1/N)
. This result holds for any finite time-horizon, and extends to the steady-state regime under the classical assumption that the system has a unique and stable fixed point. Our results show the existence of a bias term
C_{h}(t)
for any regular function
h
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[h(\boldsymbol{X}_{t})]=\underbrace{\underbrace{h(\Phi_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}))}_{\text{classical `average' mean field}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{N}C_{h}(t)}_{\text{$O(1/N)$ expansion of the bias}}}_{\text{refined `average' mean field}}+\quad o(1/N).

For the steady-state regime
t=+\infty
, we propose an algorithmic method to calculate this term
C_{h}
. This correction term can be computed by solving linear systems and is therefore easily numerically computable. We show on an example that, similarly to what was done for classical one timescale models
(
Gast and Van Houdt 2017
)
, the bias term leads to an approximation that is almost exact for small values of
N
like
N=10,20
.

An interesting open question would be to obtain a characterization of
C_{h}(t)
for the transient regime. Yet, it is not clear to us if those expressions would be usable as their size grows quickly with the system size. From an application point of view, our examples show that the new approximation leads to very accurate estimates for CSMA models. We believe that the same should hold for other multiscale models.
