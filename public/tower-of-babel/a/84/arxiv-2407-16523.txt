The cogrowth inequality from Whitehead’s algorithm
Asif Shaikh

Abstract
This article focuses on non-cyclic free factors H ≤ F m H\leq F_{m} of the free group F m F_{m} with finite rank m > 2 m>2 , and specifically addresses the implications of Ascari’s refinement of the Whitehead automorphism φ \varphi for H H as introduced in [ 1 ] . Ascari showed that if the core Δ H \Delta_{H} of H H has more than one vertex, then the core Δ φ ⁡ ( H ) \Delta_{\varphi(H)} of φ ⁡ ( H ) \varphi(H) can be derived from Δ H \Delta_{H} . We consider the regular language L H L_{H} of reduced words from F m F_{m} representing elements of H H , and employ the construction of ℬ H \mathscr{B}_{H} described in [ 4 ] . ℬ H \mathscr{B}_{H} is a finite ergodic, deterministic automaton that recognizes L H L_{H} . Extending Ascari’s result, we show that for the aforementioned free factors H H of F m F_{m} , the automaton ℬ φ ⁡ ( H ) \mathscr{B}_{\varphi(H)} can be obtained from ℬ H \mathscr{B}_{H} . Further, we present a method for deriving the adjacency matrix of the transition graph of ℬ φ ⁡ ( H ) \mathscr{B}_{\varphi(H)} from that of ℬ H \mathscr{B}_{H} and establish that α H < α φ ⁡ ( H ) \alpha_{H}<\alpha_{\varphi(H)} , where α H , α φ ⁡ ( H ) \alpha_{H},\alpha_{\varphi(H)} represent the cogrowths of H H and φ ⁡ ( H ) \varphi(H) , respectively, with respect to a fixed basis X X of F m F_{m} . The proof is based on the Perron-Frobenius theory for non-negative matrices.

Introduction

The automorphism problem for a free group
F_{m}
, where
m\geq 2
, addresses the question of whether there exists an automorphism
\varphi:F_{m}\rightarrow F_{m}
such that
\varphi(w)=w^{\prime}
, for any given pair of elements
w
and
w^{\prime}\in F_{m}
. In a seminal paper
[
15
]
, J.H.C. Whitehead presented an algorithm to solve this problem. An element (or word)
w\in F_{m}
is primitive if it belongs to some basis of
F_{m}
. Whitehead’s algorithm is based on the below theorem. For a proof of the theorem below, the reader is referred to
[
15
]
.

Theorem A

(Whitehead)
.

Let
w
be a cyclically reduced word, which is primitive and not a single letter. Then there is a Whitehead automorphism
\varphi
such that the cyclic length of
\varphi(w)
is strictly smaller than the cyclic length of
w
.

This theorem was originally established by Whitehead using topological techniques involving three-manifolds. In a later development, Rapaport
[
10
]
provided a purely algebraic proof, her approach was subsequently refined by Higgins and Lyndon
[
7
]
. These algebraic approaches not only confirmed Whitehead’s result but also broadened its accessibility within combinatorial group theory. This result addresses the primitivity problem, as a word w is primitive if and only if the minimal length of its orbit under automorphisms is 1.

The idea of extending Whitehead’s minimization framework to finitely generated subgroups by minimizing the number of vertices in the core graph was proposed by Gersten
[
5
]
, and was rigorously developed and proven by Kalajdzievski
[
8
]
. Further, Roig, Ventura, and Weil
[
11
]
provided a polynomial-time algorithm through a graphical reformulation and analysis of cut-capacity in Whitehead hypergraphs. The subgroup analog addresses the free factor problem, as a subgroup
H
is a free factor if and only if the minimum size of an element in its automorphic orbit is 1.

In a notable refinement, Ascari
[
1
]
constructed an algorithm that ensures the reduction of the size of the core graph by collapsing certain edges. Let
\Delta_{H}
be the core graph of
H\leq F_{m}
. It is the minimal finite, connected, and folded graph that contains all reduced loops corresponding to words in
H
, capturing the essential structure of
H
without any redundant paths (see Section
2.2
for the formal definition). This result is formalized in the following theorem:

Theorem B
.

(Theorem
3.4
)

Let
H\leq F_{m}
be a free factor and suppose
\Delta_{H}
has more than one vertex. Then there is a Whitehead automorphism
\varphi
such that
\Delta_{\varphi(H)}
has strictly fewer vertices and strictly fewer edges than
\Delta_{H}
. Additionally, the automorphism
\varphi
can be chosen in such a way that
\Delta_{\varphi(H)}
can be obtained from
\Delta_{H}
by collapsing certain edges of
\Delta_{H}
.

The significance of this result lies in its production of the simplified core graph for the image subgroup, achieved by reducing both the number of vertices and edges. This controlled reduction opens up intriguing possibilities for studying the dynamical properties of the subgroup, particularly in terms of its cogrowth and entropy.

An important aspect of subgroup dynamics in free groups is the study of cogrowth, which refers to the growth rate of the number of reduced words in a subgroup relative to the group. The relationship between cogrowth and the spectral radius of a simple random walk on the Schreier graph of a subgroup, as first introduced by Grigorchuk
[
6
]
, has become a fundamental tool for understanding the asymptotic and probabilistic properties of the subgroup via its Schreier graph. In this context, minimizing the size of the core of subgroup can offer valuable insights into both its cogrowth and the related entropy, which reflect the subgroup’s structural properties. This motivates the results discussed in this paper.

We first extend Ascari’s result to the ergodic automaton
\mathscr{B}_{H}
introduced in
[
4
]
, which recognizes the language
L_{H}
of reduced words in
F_{m}
that represent elements of
H
. While
[
4
]
uses the notation
\widehat{\mathscr{D}}_{H}
for this automaton, we adopt the simplified notation
\mathscr{B}_{H}
. Ascari considers the case where
H
is a free factor of
F_{m}
, but for our purposes, we require
H
to be a non-cyclic free factor, i.e.,
rk(H)>1
. This ensures that the automaton
\mathscr{B}_{H}
is connected and the cogrowth of
H
is strictly greater than 1, both of which are essential for our spectral comparison. We next describe how the automaton
\mathscr{B}_{H}
associated with
H
changes when we apply
\varphi
to
H
.

Theorem C
.

(Theorem
4.1
)

Let
\varphi
be the automorphism given by Theorem
B
. Then the ergodic automaton
\mathscr{B}_{\varphi(H)}
that recognizes
L_{\varphi(H)}
can be obtained from
\mathscr{B}_{H}
by collapsing certain edges of
\mathscr{B}_{H}
.

Let
M=M_{H}
and
M_{1}=M_{\varphi(H)}
denote the adjacency matrices of the transition diagrams corresponding to
\mathscr{B}_{H}
and
\mathscr{B}_{\varphi(H)}
, respectively. The irreducibility of
M
and
M_{1}
follows from the ergodicity of
\mathscr{B}_{H}
and
\mathscr{B}_{\varphi(H)}
. Denote by
\lambda=\lambda_{H}
and
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{\varphi(H)}
the Perron-Frobenius eigenvalues of
M
and
M_{1}
, respectively. We derive the following cogrowth inequality:

Theorem D
.

(Theorem
4.3
)

Let
\varphi
be the automorphism given by Theorem

. Then
\lambda<\lambda_{1}.

This inequality suggests that the Whitehead techniques, when extended to the context of automata, reveal structural and dynamical changes in the subgroup. By moving from core graphs to ergodic automata, we gain a more refined, language-theoretic perspective on these phenomena. Hence, it is natural to ask whether the automorphisms arising from Ascari’s method, or those obtained via cut-capacity arguments, yield the maximal cogrowth representative within the Whitehead automorphic orbit of a given subgroup
H\leq F_{m}
. More generally,
given a finitely generated subgroup
H\leq F_{m}
, can one determine the Whitehead automorphism
\varphi
of
F_{m}
such that the cogrowth
\alpha_{\varphi(H)}
is maximized over the Whitehead automorphic orbit of
H
? Is there an effective method or algorithm to find such a Whitehead automorphism? We refer to this as the Whitehead maximal cogrowth problem. To the best of our knowledge, this question remains open.

We conclude the introduction by outlining the contents of the rest of the paper. Section 2 revisits the fundamental definitions and terminologies from the theory of automata. Additionally, it presents the construction of the ergodic automaton

that recognizes

, as introduced in
[
4
]
. In Section 3, we review Ascari’s refinement of the Whitehead algorithm for subgroups, as discussed in
[
1
]
. The proofs of Theorems
C
and
D
are completed in Section 4. Finally, we conclude with an illustrative example and a section outlining several open problems for future research.

Preliminaries

We adopt the following conventions and terminologies. We use

We denote the set of all finite words over the alphabet

are commonly referred to as (formal)

over the alphabet
\Sigma
. A language

is called as

is defined as a quintuple

\Sigma

Let

or simply
\mathscr{B}
be the transition diagram of
\mathscr{B}
, that is
\mathscr{B}
is a labeled directed graph with vertex set
Q
and the directed labeled edges are described by the transition function
\delta
with labels from
\Sigma
. Namely, vertex
q
is connected with vertex
q^{\prime}
with an edge labeled by

, if
q^{\prime}\in\delta(q,x)
. (For example, Figure
4(a)
is a depiction of a transition diagram for an automaton.) Let

be an edge in
\mathscr{B}
. We use

and

to denote origin, terminus and the label of the edge

, respectively. In the context of
\mathscr{B}
, a directed path

in

is called

if

,
t(e_{i})=o(e_{i+1}),
for
i=1,\cdots,(n-1)
,
t(e_{n})\in F
. Let

be a word over
\Sigma
. The automaton

accepts the word

if there is an admissible path

such that

. The language recognized by

.

An automaton

is

if its transition diagram is strongly connected, that is, for any two states

and

there exists a path connecting

to
q^{\prime}
. A language

is

. An automaton

Whitehead’s algorithm

In this section, we focus on the results obtained in
[
1
]
regarding the refinement of Whitehead’s algorithm.

3.1

Whitehead automorphism

We begin our discussion by providing the definitions of the Whitehead automorphism of
F_{m}
and the Whitehead graph.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
a\in\Sigma=X\cup X^{-1}
and let
A\subseteq\Sigma\setminus\{a,a^{-1}\}
. The
Whitehead automorphism

\varphi=(A,a)
is given by
a\mapsto a
and

\begin{cases}x_{j}\mapsto x_{j}&\text{if }x_{j},x_{j}^{-1}\not\in A,\\
x_{j}\mapsto ax_{j}&\text{if }x_{j}\in A\text{ and }x_{j}^{-1}\not\in A,\\
x_{j}\mapsto x_{j}a^{-1}&\text{if }x_{j}\not\in A\text{ and }x_{j}^{-1}\in A,\\
x_{j}\mapsto ax_{j}a^{-1}&\text{if }x_{j},x_{j}^{-1}\in A.\\
\end{cases}

Definition 3.2
.

Let
w
be a cyclically reduced word. The
Whitehead graph
of
w
is the undirected graph defined as follows:

1.

The alphabet
\Sigma
is the vertex set.

2.

For every pair of consecutive letters in
w
, include an undirected edge between the inverse of the first letter and the second. Additionally these is also an edge connecting the inverse of the last letter of
w
to the first letter of
w
.

Notice that,
w
being cyclically reduced, in the Whitehead graph of
w
, we never have any edge connecting a vertex to itself.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
w
be a cyclically reduced word. A vertex
a
in the Whitehead graph of
w
is called a
cut vertex
if it is non-isolated and at least one of the following two configurations take place:

1.

The connected component of
a
doesn’t contain
a^{-1}
.

2.

The connected component of
a
becomes disconnected if we remove
a
.

The notion of a cut vertex used here is a variant of the classical graph-theoretic definition, adapted to the structure of the Whitehead graph. In standard graph theory, a cut vertex is one whose removal increases the number of connected components, this is reflected in the second condition of our definition. The first condition arises from a phenomenon noted in
[
14
]
and formalized in Proposition 2.2(b) of
[
13
]
: a cyclically reduced word may contribute edges to multiple components of the Whitehead graph, and when a generator
a
and its inverse
a^{-1}
appear in different components, this indicates the possibility of a length-reducing Whitehead automorphism. Including this scenario in our definition ensures that the graph-theoretic notion of cut vertex accurately captures all algebraically meaningful situations relevant to automorphic reducibility. We recall Whitehead’s theorem below.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
w
be a cyclically reduced word, which is primitive but not a single letter. Then the Whitehead graph of
w
contains a cut vertex. Further, there is a Whitehead automorphism
\varphi
such that the cyclic length of
\varphi(w)
is strictly smaller than the cyclic length of
w
.

We state a refinement of Whitehead’s theorem. See Theorem 3.7 in
[
1
]
.

Theorem 3.2
.

The automorphism in
Theorem
3.1
can be chosen in such a way that every
a
or
a^{-1}
letter, which is added when we apply
\varphi
to
w
letter by letter, immediately cancels (in the cyclic reduction process).

4.3

The Inequality

We recall the following standard theorem (see Theorem 1.5 of
[
2
]
or Theorem 1.6 on page 23 and Exercises 2.1 on page 39 of
[
12
]
), which holds significant importance in our analysis. It’s important to clarify that by ‘vector’ we refer to column vectors in the context of this discussion.

Theorem 4.2
.

(Perron-Frobenius). Suppose that
N
is an irreducible, non-negative integral matrix. Then there is a unique positive eigenvector
\overrightarrow{w}
of norm one for
N
, and its associated eigenvalue satisfies
\eta\geq 1
. If
\eta=1
, then
N
is a transitive permutation matrix. Moreover if
\overrightarrow{u}
is a positive vector and
\beta>0
satisfies

(N\overrightarrow{u})_{i}\leq\beta\overrightarrow{u}_{i}
for each
i
and

(N\overrightarrow{u})_{j}<\beta\overrightarrow{u}_{j}
for some
j
, then
\eta<\beta.

Recall that
M=(m_{ij})
and
M_{1}
are the adjacency matrices of the deterministic and ergodic automata
\mathscr{B}_{H}
and
\mathscr{B}
, respectively. As a result, they are non-negative, integral (with entries either
0
or
1
), and irreducible. Since
H
is a non-cyclic free factor of
F_{m}
, and
\varphi(H)
is as well, this implies that
\lambda>1
and
\lambda_{1}>1
. With all the required machinery in place, we are now ready to prove that the Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue of
M
is strictly smaller than that of
M_{1}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\lambda
and
\lambda_{1}
be the Perron-Frobenious eigenvalue of
M
and
M_{1}
, respectively, where
M
and
M_{1}
are as described in Corollary
4.2
.
Then
\lambda<\lambda_{1}.

Proof.

Recall that
Q_{H}=Q\cup S
. We use the notation
(q,x_{k}^{\epsilon})
for states in
Q
and
(s,a^{\epsilon})
for states in
S
. Let
R_{(v,x_{k}^{\epsilon})}
and
C_{(v,x_{k}^{\epsilon})}
denote the rows and columns of the matrix
M
corresponding to the state
(v,x_{k}^{\epsilon})\in Q_{H}
, and let
R^{\prime}_{(q,x_{k}^{\epsilon})}
and
C^{\prime}_{(q,x_{k}^{\epsilon})}
denote the rows and columns of the matrix
M_{1}
corresponding to the state
(q,x_{k}^{\epsilon})\in Q
.

Choose a positive vector
\overrightarrow{v}
in
\mathbb{R}^{|Q|}
so that
M_{1}\overrightarrow{v}=\lambda_{1}\overrightarrow{v}
, and let
\overrightarrow{u}
be the vector in
\mathbb{R}^{|Q|+|S|}
defined by
u_{(q,x_{k}^{\epsilon})}=v_{(q,x_{k}^{\epsilon})}
for
{(q,x_{k}^{\epsilon})}\in Q
. We will determine the remaining
|S|
components of
\overrightarrow{u}
based on the block decomposition of
M
described in Corollary
4.1
.

By Theorem
4.1
, in the column
C_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}
in
M
, there are
d
entries with the value
1
and the rest are
0
. The rows in which these
d
entries appear are
R_{o(e_{i})}
, respectively, where
e_{i},i=1,\cdots,d
, and are the edges in the transition graph of
\mathscr{B}_{H}
terminating at
(s,a^{\epsilon})
with label
a^{\epsilon}.
For each
o(e_{i})\in Q,i=1,\cdots,d
, we use Corollary
4.1
to write

(M\overrightarrow{u})_{o(e_{i})}=b_{i}+u_{(s,a^{\epsilon})},

where
b_{i}=\displaystyle\sum_{(q,x_{k}^{\epsilon^{\prime}})\in Q}m_{o(e_{i})(q,x_{k}^{\epsilon^{\prime}})}u_{(q,x_{k}^{\epsilon^{\prime}})}.
Also, for
(s,a^{\epsilon})\in S
, we write

(M\overrightarrow{u})_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}=\sum_{(q,x_{k}^{\epsilon^{\prime}})\in Q}m_{(s,a^{\epsilon})(q,x_{k}^{\epsilon^{\prime}})}u_{(q,x_{k}^{\epsilon^{\prime}})}=b_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}.

By Corollary
4.2
, the corresponding rows
R^{\prime}_{o(e_{i})}
of
M_{1}
can be obtained from
M
by applying row addition

R_{o(e_{i})}+R_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}\rightarrow R_{o(e_{i})},

where
i=1,\dots,d
, and removing rows
R_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}
and columns
C_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}
from the resultant, where
(s,a^{\epsilon})\in S
.

Using the above relationships between rows
R_{o(e_{i})},R_{(s,a^{\epsilon})},
and the column
C_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}
of
M
, and the corresponding rows
R^{\prime}_{o(e_{i})}
of
M_{1}
, for
i=1,\cdots,d
, we obtain

(M\overrightarrow{u})_{o(e_{i})}+(M\overrightarrow{u})_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}-u_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}=(M_{1}\overrightarrow{v})_{o(e_{i})}=\lambda_{1}u_{o(e_{i})}.

Substituting the expressions for
(M\overrightarrow{u})_{o(e_{i})}
and
(M\overrightarrow{u})_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}
, we obtain

b_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}=\lambda_{1}u_{o(e_{i})}-b_{i}.

Since
b_{i}>0
and
\lambda_{1}u_{o(e_{i})}>b_{i}
, it follows that
0<b_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}<\lambda_{1}u_{o(e_{i})}.

To ensure that
M\overrightarrow{u}\leq\lambda_{1}\overrightarrow{u}
, we require

(M\overrightarrow{u})_{o(e_{i})}=b_{i}+u_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}\leq\lambda_{1}u_{o(e_{i})}\quad\text{and}\quad(M\overrightarrow{u})_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}=b_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}\leq\lambda_{1}u_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}.

Thus, we need to choose
u_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}
such that

\frac{b_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}}{\lambda_{1}}\leq u_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}\leq b_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}.

This interval is non-empty, since
b_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}>0
and
\lambda_{1}>1
. Therefore, for each
(s,a^{\epsilon})\in S
, we choose

u_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}=\frac{b_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}}{\lambda_{1}}
. Then,

(M\overrightarrow{u})_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}=b_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}=\lambda_{1}u_{(s,a^{\epsilon})},\quad(M\overrightarrow{u})_{o(e_{i})}=b_{i}+u_{(s,a^{\epsilon})}<\lambda_{1}u_{o(e_{i})}.

Hence, for this choice, we have
(M\overrightarrow{u})_{(v,x^{\epsilon})}\leq\lambda_{1}u_{(v,x^{\epsilon})}
for all
{}_{(v,x^{\epsilon})}\in Q_{H}
, with strict inequality for
(v,x^{\epsilon})=o(e_{i}),
where
i=1,\cdots,d
.
By Theorem
4.2
, we conclude that
\lambda<\lambda_{1}.
This completes the proof.

Open Problem

The results established in this article suggest several directions for further exploration. We pose the following open questions:

Question 6.1
.

Can similar cogrowth inequalities be formulated for conjugacy classes of subgroups, using restricted core graphs (obtained by removing the possible hair ending at the root in the core graph) as the natural invariant?

Question 6.2
.

Is it possible to extend Whitehead-type reductions to tuples of subgroups? If so, how do these reductions interact with product automorphisms and their associated graphs? What new insights can be gained by studying cogrowth in this extended context?

Question 6.3
.

Can the techniques discussed in this article be extended to arbitrary finitely generated subgroups of
F_{m}
, or are there inherent limitations that restrict the results to non-cyclic free factors? In particular, can tools such as the Marshall Hall Theorem or properties of finite-index subgroups be used to generalize the inequality between the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalues of the associated matrices
M
and
M_{1}
?

Question 6.4
.

Is it possible to prove Corollary
4.3
using methods inspired by Gromov, thus avoiding the use of Perron–Frobenius theory? How would this alternative approach compare with the current proof in terms of technique and applicability, as discussed in
[
3
]
?

Building on the Whitehead maximal cogrowth problem mentioned in the introduction, another open question arises regarding the effectiveness of certain automorphisms. The cogrowth inequality suggests that among all images of a non-cyclic free factor
H
under automorphisms of
F_{m}
, those subgroups whose core graphs have a single vertex achieve the highest cogrowth (or entropy).

Question 6.5
.

Given a finitely generated subgroup
H\leq F_{m}
, can one determine an automorphism
\varphi\in Aut(F_{m})
such that the cogrowth
\alpha_{\varphi(H)}
is maximized over the automorphic orbit of
H
? Is there an effective method or algorithm to find such an automorphism?
