Robustness Over Time: Understanding Adversarial Examples’ Effectiveness on Longitudinal Versions of Large Language Models
Yugeng Liu Tianshuo Cong Zhengyu Zhao Michael Backes Yun Shen Yang Zhang

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) undergo continuous updates to improve user experience. However, prior research on the security and safety implications of LLMs has primarily focused on their specific versions, overlooking the impact of successive LLM updates. This prompts the need for a holistic understanding of the risks in these different versions of LLMs. To fill this gap, in this paper, we conduct a longitudinal study to examine the adversarial robustness – specifically misclassification, jailbreak, and hallucination – of three prominent LLMs: GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and LLaMA. Our study reveals that LLM updates do not consistently improve adversarial robustness as expected. For instance, a later version of GPT-3.5 degrades regarding misclassification and hallucination despite its improved resilience against jailbreaks, and GPT-4 demonstrates (incrementally) higher robustness overall. Moreover, larger model sizes do not necessarily yield improved robustness. Specifically, larger LLaMA models do not uniformly exhibit improved robustness across all three aspects studied. Importantly, minor updates lacking substantial robustness improvements can exacerbate existing issues rather than resolve them. By providing a more nuanced understanding of LLM robustness over time, we hope our study can offer valuable insights for developers and users navigating model updates and informed decisions in model development and usage for LLM vendors.

Methodology.

Our investigation focuses on assessing the robustness of popular LLMs over time, including closed-source models (GPT-3.5 and GPT-4) as well as the open-source LLaMA-1/2.

Our primary goal is to evaluate if an LLM is robust when the ICL components are replaced by their adversarial counterparts.
Specifically, we explore two attack scenarios: 1) classification tasks and 2) generation tasks.
Within these scenarios, we consider three representative adversarial goals: misclassification

Findings.

In summary, we make the following key findings.
First, we demonstrate that GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and LLaMA are all vulnerable to adversarial queries, which are persistent across different versions.
For instance, in the classification task using the SST2 dataset, GPT-3.5 v0125 achieves a performance of 0.583 with clean samples. However, this performance significantly decreases to 0.264 when fed with various types of adversarial examples.

In addition, we notice no significant robustness improvement for LLMs during the upgrade process.

gpt-3.5-turbo-0613

gpt-3.5-turbo-0125
(GPT-3.5 v0125).

gpt-4-0125-preview

For the LLaMA family, upgrading models does not improve the robustness for many cases (see

and

The overall performance of the latest version (LLaMA-3 family) is much worse in all the tasks.
In addition, our findings reveal that increasing LLM size does not guarantee enhanced robustness.

Through the lens of misclassification evaluation, we conduct weekly tests on the GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 models to evaluate the minor update process.
Our experiments show that model providers may update the LLMs to add new features over time.
In general, these updates could nonetheless influence the overall performance of the LLMs, which can still exacerbate existing issues rather than resolve them.

Impact.

We hope that our study can offer valuable insights for future studies on the robustness of LLMs.
It is critical that developers prioritize the consideration of model robustness during the upgrade and update.
Proactively incorporating robustness-enhancing techniques and conducting rigorous evaluation will bolster the resilience of an LLM to both known and unknown challenges.

Threat Modeling

LLMs can perform completely different tasks, such as answering questions, summarizing documents, translating languages, and completing sentences.
Within these tasks, according to different requirements and purposes, we categorize them into two types:

•

Classification Task.

LLMs are usually used to classify the input sentences, such as sentiment analysis (positive or negative) and linguistic acceptability (acceptable or not).
These tasks usually require users to provide clear descriptions and corresponding candidate labels.
Then, LLMs generate the prediction label to assist users.

•

Generation Task.

In this task, LLMs typically need to produce “open-ended” text based on the inputs from the user, with the goal of creating coherent, meaningful, and contextually appropriate answers.
These answers are obtained from pre-trained knowledge of LLMs or some external searches.

Since in-context learning is the most widely used inference framework on both classification tasks and generation tasks, we inject adversarial examples into the components of in-context learning to evaluate the robustness of continuously updated LLMs.
In this paper, we summarize all the queries into two types of in-context learning methods, i.e., zero-shot learning and few-shot learning.

In-context Learning (ICL)

Overview.

The core idea of In-context Learning (ICL) is to learn from analogies implied in contextual information.
ICL requires a few examples to form a demonstration context and feed them into LLM.
It does not modify the parameters of an LLM and relies on the model to learn the pattern hidden in the demonstration (and accordingly generate the correct output).
As such, ICL effectively reduces the computation costs for adapting LLMs to new tasks, as fine-tuning is not required.
In general, there are two categories of in-context learning: zero-shot learning and few-shot learning.

For zero-shot learning, the query includes only the description and the question but without any demonstrations, while few-shot learning means that the query also includes a few demonstrations.

Zero-shot Learning.

Zero-shot learning
[
58
]
is a capability enabled by LLMs, allowing them to generalize to tasks or domains they have never been explicitly trained on, no matter the classification or generation task.
As a special case of ICL, the query of zero-shot learning to an LLM (termed
Q_{zero}
in this paper) only contains two elements: description and question (see
2(a)
), which can be formulated as follows.

Q_{zero}=\emph{Description}+\emph{Question}.

(1)

Here
Description
serves as an instructional guide for LLMs, which could provide necessary information such as task characterization and the response format.

Question
is an inquiry for a specific task.
Zero-shot learning relies on the capability of an LLM to infer both the input distribution and the desired output from a query without any demonstrations
[
56
]
.
Therefore, although the LLMs may not have been pre-trained on specific tasks, they can still perform accurate results.
Because of the simple
Q_{zero}
structure, zero-shot learning offers excellent convenience and becomes the most common approach to querying LLMs in real-world scenarios.

Few-shot Learning.

Few-shot learning
[
7
]
includes a few examples to form a learning context that enables LLMs to better understand the task.
In other words, compared to zero-shot learning, few-shot learning enables LLMs to quickly adapt to new tasks by learning from an extra element, i.e.,
Demonstration
.
Thus, the query of few-shot learning,
Q_{few}
, can be formulated as:

Q_{few}=\emph{Description}+\emph{Demonstration}+\emph{Question}.

(2)

Demonstration
typically consists of a handful of user-generated question-answer pairs.
For the classification problem, the answer is always the label of the question, while in the generation problem, the pair is the example within a similar semantic meaning or the same format and style.
We show an example of
Q_{few}
in the classification problem in
2(b)
.
In general, few-shot learning can better guide the LLMs in learning a more accurate mapping between questions and desired answers.
In this paper, following
[
67
]
, we specifically consider 3-shot learning (i.e., three question-answer pairs in
Demonstration
).

Attack Taxonomy

In this section, we primarily discuss the taxonomy of adversarial attacks on LLMs.
The goal of adversarial attacks is to manipulate the behavior of LLMs to elicit misleading or undesirable responses through adversarial examples in
clean
queries
[
67
,
52
,
54
]
.
We consider three adversarial attacks against LLMs.
The first is
misclassification
(see
Section 3.1
), which tries to perturb normal input to induce the model to deviate from correct predictions.
In addition, a more representative adversarial attack is
jailbreak
(see
Section 3.2
).
Jailbreak attacks aim to modify the input to bypass the safeguard within LLMs, thereby forcing LLMs to generate certain disallowed, unsafe, or harmful statements.
Furthermore, another adversarial attack, named
hallucination
(see
Section 3.3
), is to produce coherent and grammatically correct but factually incorrect or nonsensical outputs.

Misclassification

Previous studies have proposed various effective methods to generate adversarial examples against language models.
However, different works consider attacking different components of the ICL framework.
For instance, Zhu et al.
[
67
]
proposed PromptBench, a dataset consisting of adversarial descriptions.
Their experimental results demonstrate that descriptions are notably vulnerable to adversarial attacks primarily because they serve as a critical context that shapes the responses of LLMs and guides their cognitive outputs.
However, they only focus on a single version of LLMs.
Wang et al.
[
53
]
proposed AdvGLUE, a dataset comprising adversarial questions, but the target models are traditional small-scale language models.
Above all, to fully evaluate the robustness of the LLMs, we consider different types of adversarial queries, i.e., each element of a query can be clean or adversarial.

Jailbreak

Jailbreak attacks refer to a scenario where a user intentionally tries to trick or manipulate the LLMs to bypass their built-in safety, ethical, or operational guidelines, thereby inducing the LLMs to produce toxic responses.
Previous works
[
68
,
61
,
12
,
8
,
30
]
have proposed a new class of adversarial attacks that can jailbreak safety-aligned language models.
Among them, to test the robustness of both closed-source and open-source LLMs against jailbreak attacks, we launch black-box
optimization-based
jailbreak attacks, including GPTfuzz
[
61
]
, PAIR
[
8
]
and TAP
[
30
]
.
More concretely, these methods are systematically automated prompt-level jailbreaks optimized by outputs or coordinates of LLMs.
Note that all of the above methods are zero-shot learning.
We attempt to use few-shot learning for jailbreaking as well.
However, the results indicate that once an answer in the demonstration involves a jailbreaking response, the LLM is more likely to reject the query, regardless of the optimization method used.
Therefore, in this paper, we do not discuss few-shot learning.
In addition, beyond the scope of jailbreak queries, the method for adversarial-based techniques needs a detailed description for modification.
More specifically, a surrogate or teacher model leverages the outputs of the target LLM to refine or alter the description.
Consequently, within our framework, we assume that the question itself invariably originates from a clean source, but the descriptions are classified as either clean or adversarial, as delineated in
Equation 1
.
Given the adversarial query
Q_{zero}^{\mathsf{jb}}
, we have the following adversarial queries in two scenarios:

Q_{zero}^{\mathsf{jb}}:=\{Q_{zero}^{\mathsf{CC}},Q_{zero}^{\mathsf{AC}}\}.

In this paper, the adversary is assumed to have black-box access to the LLMs for the following three adversarial-based optimized jailbreak attacks.

•

GPTfuzz
[
61
]
To automate the generation of jailbreak templates for LLMs, GPTfuzz starts with human-written templates.
It uses a series of random mutations to generate new inputs and evaluate them with the assistance of LLMs.

•

PAIR
[
8
]
PAIR is a systematically automated prompt-level jailbreak.
PAIR adopts an
attacker
LLM to discover and improve the jailbreak prompts and uses a
judge
LLM to evaluate the responses from the
target
LLM.

•

TAP
[
30
]
More advanced than PAIR, TAP utilizes three LLMs: an
attacker
, an
evaluator
, and a
target
.
The task is to generate the jailbreaking prompts using tree-of-thoughts reasoning.
The
evaluator
first assesses the generated prompts and evaluates whether the jailbreaking attempt would be successful or not, and then evaluates the generated prompts from the
target
.

Hallucination

Hallucination refers to a phenomenon in which the model generates incorrect, nonsensical, or imaginary text.
In particular, hallucination represents a unique form of adversarial example that can lead LLMs to provide erroneous responses to confusion about the given question.
This confusion stems from logical inaccuracies rather than linguistic mistakes in the misclassification of datasets.
Since we deploy LLMs in an open-domain chat setting without specific task constraints, the responses can be seen as a repository of world knowledge.
The factors contributing to hallucination are varied, potentially from the data source or context.
Thus, studying whether an LLM is vulnerable to hallucination is pivotal in examining LLM robustness.
We generate the hallucination answers on three domains: Question Answering (QA), Dialogue, and Summarization.
We consider these hallucination answers as adversarial examples.
The given queries contain questions with both the correct and hallucination answers, and the LLM needs to make the correct choice between these two types of answers.
Therefore, we only consider zero-shot learning as the adversarial query:

Q_{zero}^{\mathsf{hl}}:=\{Q_{zero}^{\mathsf{A}}\}.

Evaluation Setup

In this section, we introduce our experimental settings and protocols.
We first mainly elaborate on two ways of model evolution over time: upgrade and update (see
Section 4.1
).
An upgrade often involves substantial and fundamental changes.
It usually does not depend on previous content and involves replacing the old with the new.
On the other hand, an update refers to smaller-scale, minor, incremental, and gradual improvements within a single version.
It often requires modifications (or improvements) based on the existing foundation and cannot be separated from the original basis.
We then outline the corresponding datasets for testing different categories of LLMs (see
Section 4.2
).
Finally, we introduce the evaluation metrics to summarize the results (see
Section 4.3

In this paper, we consider the following three evaluation metrics (

Note that for different tasks,
CTS
has different meanings:
(1) For the misclassification task,
CTS
signifies the
clean prediction accuracy
, thereby reflecting the normal utility of the LLMs.
(2) Regarding the jailbreak task,CTS represents
rejection rate
, reflecting the capability of LLMs rejecting jailbreak attempts.
Note that for the hallucination task, we do not need
CTS
to qualify the answers.
Therefore, for all tasks, a
higher

CTS
(
\uparrow
) indicates a stronger model foundation utility.

•

Robust Test Score (RTS)
measures the success score given the adversarial queries,
It offers multiple angles to evaluate the robustness of the LLMs on different tasks:
(1) For misclassification tasks,
RTS
quantifies the prediction accuracy on adversarial examples.
(2) For jailbreak tasks,
RTS
denotes the rejection rate of LLMs against jailbreak prompts.
(3) For hallucination tasks,
RTS
stands for the correct rate for selecting the correct answers.
As a result, a
higher

RTS
(
\uparrow
) indicates better robustness against adversarial queries.

•

Performance Drop Rate (PDR)
, which was introduced by
[
67
]
, aims to quantify the extent of performance decline under adversarial attacks.
In general,
lower

PDR
(
\downarrow
) indicates superior model robustness.

PDR
can be formulated as:

PDR=\frac{CTS-RTS}{CTS}.

CTS
and
RTS

]

few-shot learning to better instruct GPT-4 to judge the harmfulness of the responses (see

In this section, we introduce the results of adversarial-based jailbreak attacks.
Our evaluation employs three adversarial-based optimization methods, with an initial seed description and a question.

This indicates that the latest version is not necessarily the most effective.

This highlights a clear trade-off between misclassification performance and resistance to jailbreak tests across all models.
This trade-off poses a considerable challenge for the model provider, as optimizing the performance of LLMs while maintaining adequate defenses against jailbreak attempts.
Identifying the trade-off is particularly significant, underscoring the complex robustness and the subtle balance required in LLMs to address diverse safety and performance criteria.

Second, for the 13B family, the

For GPTFuzz attacks, the v1 model has a higher

for the other two attacks compared to the first version, with v2 Chat further showcasing a similar trend.
For

For GPTFuzz, the v1 model is more resistant to jailbreak attacks than its upgraded versions when analyzing

In general, the updated versions in LLaMA models have indeed fortified their defenses against adversarial-based jailbreak attacks.
From another perspective, previous work

This trend indicates that larger models may benefit from increased computational capacity and potentially more sophisticated learning capabilities.
However, they also present a larger attack surface or are more vulnerable to adversarial-based jailbreak attacks.
Increasing the model size and capacity does not linearly translate to improved security or safety and may, in fact, introduce some new vulnerabilities or exacerbate existing ones instead.

Evaluation on Hallucination

In this section, we analyze the hallucination phenomena in LLMs.
We curate three types of hallucination datasets: Dialogue, QA, and Summarization.
These datasets serve as the basis for evaluating the robustness of longitudinal LLMs across various capability dimensions.
We list the results in
Table 5

GPT-3.5.

The first three columns of

Expressly, in Dialogue and QA datasets, v0613 has the highest

In addition, v1106 has the lowest

The results underscore the complexity of addressing hallucination across GPT-3.5 models.
They highlight the importance of targeted improvements and the need for a multifaceted approach to enhancing model robustness.

GPT-4.

The overall performance of GPT-4 in hallucination tests indicates a better level of proficiency.

Meanwhile, for summarization, v1106 has the highest

This means that the latest version is not the best choice in terms of hallucination.

LLaMA.

For the LLaMA family, the
RTS

Model providers should consider integrating expansive foundational information and adopting advanced training methodologies in future upgrades.

Takeaways

Our comprehensive evaluation reveals a concerning trend across various versions of GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and the LLaMA models, indicating a general oversight of robustness as a critical metric in the model upgrade process.
Specifically, for GPT-3.5, v1106, despite its superior performance in jailbreak tests, shows the worst performance in both misclassification and hallucination evaluations, rendering its overall performance less than satisfactory.
Due to its favorable cost-effectiveness, GPT-3.5 is a preferred choice among many users.
However, it is worth considering v0613 for specific classification tasks as it potentially provides a better balance between performance and robustness.
For GPT-4, v0125 stands out for its better overall performance, whereas the latest version, v0409, is worse than v0125 in all the tasks.
Regarding the LLaMA family, upgrading models does not improve the robustness.
The LLaMA-3 families perform worst in misclassification and jailbreak tasks, whereas these models demonstrate a significant limitation in distinguishing correct answers.
In addition, our findings underscore that an increase in model size does not guarantee enhanced robustness.
Furthermore, larger models entail higher GPU resource consumption, which may not align with the cost-effectiveness priorities.
Therefore, smaller models may offer a more attractive cost-benefit for those prioritizing efficiency and resource management.
In light of these insights, we suggest that model providers prioritize robustness as a critical component of the model development and upgrade process.

Takeaways

Model providers may update the LLMs to add more new features over time.
While these updates initially leave the parameters of the pre-trained model ostensibly unchanged, they could nonetheless exert a subtle influence on the overall performance of the LLMs.
Transitioning from one version to another, mainly when there are no significant improvements in robustness, can exacerbate existing issues rather than resolve them, losing the purpose of updating the model.
Consequently, model providers should adopt a standardized approach to evaluating the performance of LLMs during the update.

Adversarial Attacks.

We focus on adversarial attacks that manipulate legitimate inputs to mislead a trained model to produce incorrect outputs in the NLP domain
[
62
]
.
These attacks commonly manipulate the input text at character-, word-, and sentence-level to attain the attack goals (i.e., targeted or untargeted attacks).
Similar to adversarial attacks in computer vision domain, they can be categorized into black-box attacks (paraphrase
[
18

2

In the NLP domain, those attacks have been successfully applied to attack various applications, such as optical character recognition
[

[
9
]
, visual question answering
[
59
]
, etc.
Our objective here is not to devise novel adversarial attacks against LLMs.
Rather, we use existing methods to understand whether LLMs can be challenged by carefully crafted textual adversarial examples and whether/how these adversarial examples can be transferred to different versions of an LLM.

Large language models (LLMs) have become a prominent area of research and application in the NLP domain, driven primarily by the transformer architecture

.
These models are trained on massive text data and boast a substantial number of parameters, often exceeding hundreds of billions.

Discussion

In this study, we do not focus on generating adversarial examples on the
Adversarial Description
dataset and
Adversarial Question
dataset.
The primary reason behind our decision lies in the considerable financial cost of generating such adversarial examples, as it requires us to query GPT models for an extensive duration, spanning several weeks.
Furthermore, it is worth noting that no universally applicable standard template for the ICL exists.
Certain queries may prove effective only when applied to specific datasets, and even minor modifications in the queries or choice of words can yield vastly different classification results, increasing the difficulty of queries.
Despite these limitations, our investigation reveals that the adversarial examples derived from a traditional model continue to significantly impact LLMs, aligning with previous works
[
67
,
68
]
.
Given that organizations such as OpenAI and Meta have not open-sourced the training datasets for their models, there exists a potential risk of inadvertently evaluating models on their training sets.
While our analysis suggests a markedly low probability of our evaluation test set intersecting with the training set, accurately assessing this risk for future model iterations remains challenging.
It is generally a trending topic to construct or select non-overlap datasets for LLM evaluation nowadays
[
64
]
.
In the future, we could also choose the datasets that do not overlap with the training data, e.g., the dataset with a CC BY-SA 4.0 license or constructed by ourselves.
In general, we believe it is important for the model provider to use enhancing adversarial robustness methods in model upgrades and updates.
In the future, we will continue to measure the new features of LLMs after the model updates.

Conclusion

We comprehensively assess the robustness of the longitudinal versions of LLMs, focusing on GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and LLaMA through the lens of misclassification, jailbreak, and hallucination evaluation.
Our empirical results consistently demonstrate that, for all the LLMs, the upgraded and updated model does not exhibit heightened robustness against the proposed adversarial queries compared to its predecessor.
In general, our analysis reveals a prevalent trend of decreased adversarial robustness in the many upgraded and updated versions.
Our findings reinforce the importance of understanding and assessing the robustness aspect when updating LLMs, calling for enhanced focus on comprehensive evaluation and reinforcement strategies to counter evolving adversarial challenges.
