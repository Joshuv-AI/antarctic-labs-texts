Particle Trajectories for Quantum Maps
Yonah Borns-Weil, Izak Oltman

Abstract
. We study the trajectories of a semiclassical quantum particle under repeated indirect measurement by Kraus operators, in the setting of the quantized torus. In between measurements, the system evolves via either Hamiltonian propagators or metaplectic operators. We show in both cases the convergence in total variation of the quantum trajectory to its corresponding classical trajectory, as defined by the propagation of a semiclassical defect measure. This convergence holds up to the Ehrenfest time of the classical system, which is larger when the system is “less chaotic”. In addition, we present numerical simulations of these effects. In proving this result, we provide a characterization of a type of semi-classical defect measure we call uniform defect measures. We also prove derivative estimates of a function composed with a flow on the torus.

The framework of “quantum trajectories” has gotten increasing attention in recent years. Instead of treating measurement as a one-time event, researchers consider many measurements obtained over time and study their effects on the particle being measured, described mathematically via quantum instruments. The sequence of measured values is called the
quantum trajectory
, and is a random variable worthy of study.

In this paper, we take quantum trajectories into the setting of the semiclassical quantized torus, as studied in Bouzouina–De Biévre
[
BD96
]
, Schenck
[
Sch09
]
, Dyatlov–Jezequel
[
DJ21
]
, and others. The spaces involved are all finite-dimensional, which makes numerical simulations easier than in PDE-based models. Our result is inspired by the recent work of Benoist, Fraas, and Fröhlich
[
BFF22
]
, who prove in the PDE setting that the trajectory of a repeatedly observed quantum particle in the semiclassical limit approaches the natural notion of a classical trajectory. See Figure
1
for a simulation of this quantum-classical correspondence of particle trajectories on the quantized torus and §
4
for more numerics. We strengthen the result in
[
BFF22
]
in our setting by allowing the time to depend on the semiclassical parameter, and we relate the rate of convergence to the “chaotic behavior” of the underlying classical system. This matches the intuition that a less chaotic transformation should be well-approximated for longer by its classical counterpart. Additionally, we illustrate the results numerically to demonstrate in the chaotic case that our time restriction is essentially optimal.

1.1.
Model

In our setting, a quantum particle is modeled by a density operator (see Definition
2.13
)
\rho_{N}
on the quantized
2d-
dimensional torus
H_{N}
(see (
2.8
)) where
N\in\mathbb{N}
is proportional to the reciprocal of the semi-classical parameter
h
. This particle is “indirectly measured” by conjugating by Kraus operators (see Definition
2.14
)
\Op_{N}(f_{q})
where
\{f_{q}\}_{q\in\Omega}
are fixed elements of
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2d})
with uniformly bounded derivatives (such that
\Op_{N}(f_{q})
provide a resolution of the identity),
\Omega
is a compact metric space with finite Borel measure
\nu
, and
\Op_{N}
is the quantization of functions on the torus as described in §
2.4
. The measurement procedure is further described in §
2.5
.

After observing the particle, we evolve it for one unit of time by conjugating
\rho_{N}
by a unitary operator
U
, which is either a quantization of a fixed symplectic matrix
M
(defined in §
2.2
) or Schrödinger evolution
e^{-\frac{i}{h}P}
for a fixed Hamiltonian
P
. If we repeat this process
n
times, we get a resulting measure on the space of trajectories. Suppose
F\subset\Omega^{n+1}
, then the probability of obtaining the set of trajectories
F
is given by:

\displaystyle\int_{F}\operatorname{tr}_{H_{N}}\left(\Phi_{N,{q_{n}}}^{*}\circ\dots\circ\Phi_{N,{q_{0}}}^{*}(\rho_{N})\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q_{0})\cdots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q_{n})

where

\displaystyle\Phi_{N,{q_{i}}}^{*}(\rho):=U\Op_{N}(f_{q_{i}})\rho_{N}\Op_{N}(f_{q_{i}})^{*}U^{*}.

Therefore, we have a “quantum probability measure” given by

(1.1)

\mathbb{P}^{(n)}_{N,\rho}:=\operatorname{tr}_{H_{N}}\left(\Phi_{N,{q_{n}}}^{*}\circ\dots\circ\Phi_{N,{q_{0}}}^{*}\rho\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q_{0})\cdots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q_{n})

We now describe the trajectory of a corresponding classical particle. Let
\mu
be the defect measure (defined in Definition
2.5
) of
\rho_{N}
. We interpret
\mu
as the probability distribution of the initial position and momentum of a classical particle. We apply an “approximate measurement,” which computes an observable quantity
q
to be in
E_{0}\subseteq\Omega
with probability
\int_{E_{0}}|f_{q_{0}}(\zeta)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q_{0}).
The particle is then allowed to classically evolve for one unit of time by a flow
\phi^{t}
on
\mathbb{T}^{2d}
which is either multiplication by the symplectic matrix
M
or else the nonlinear Hamiltonian evolution
\text{exp}(H_{p})
. We again repeat the process of alternating measurement and evolution, though we must remark that unlike in the quantum case, the measurement does not affect the location of the particle. Conditioned on initially having position/momentum
\zeta
, we have the probability of classically measuring
(q_{0},\dots q_{n})
in a measurable set
F\subset\Omega^{n+1}
to be

\int_{F}|f_{q_{n}}(\phi^{n}(\zeta))|^{2}\cdots|f_{q_{0}}(\zeta)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q_{0})\cdots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q_{n}).

One can easily check that
(q_{n})_{n}
is a process of independent random variables (although not identically distributed). As the initial value of
\zeta
was taken to be randomly chosen from the distribution
\mu
, we take the classical probability of measuring
(q_{0},\dots,q_{n})\in F
to be

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2d}}\int_{F}|f_{q_{n}}(\phi^{n}(\zeta))|^{2}\cdots|f_{q_{0}}(\zeta)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q_{0})\cdots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q_{n})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(\zeta).

We therefore have a “classical probability measure” given by

(1.2)

P^{(n)}_{\mu}:=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2d}}|f_{q_{n}}(\phi^{n}(\zeta))|^{2}\cdots|f_{q_{0}}(\zeta)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(\zeta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q_{0})\cdots\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q_{n}).

Note that the functions
f_{q}
are allowed to overlap so that
P^{(n)}_{\mu}
is a probability of trajectories in
\Omega^{n+1}
while
\phi^{t}
is the unique trajectory on the torus.

The goal of this paper is to describe in what sense, and at what quantitative rate, the measure
\mathbb{P}^{(n)}_{N,\rho}
approaches
P^{(n)}
as
N
grows large.

We can state our main result.

Theorem

(Main result)
.

Suppose
\rho=\rho_{N}:H_{N}\to H_{N}
is a density operator with semiclassical defect measure
\mu
. Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
(q_{N,0},q_{N,1},\dots,q_{N,n})
be random variables with law
\mathbb{P}_{N,\rho}^{(n)}
given by (
1.1
) and let
(q_{0},q_{1},\dots,q_{n})
be random variables with law
P_{\mu}^{(n)}
given by (
1.2
). Then as
N\to\infty
,
(q_{N,0},q_{N,1},\dots,q_{N,n})
converges weakly to
(q_{0},q_{1},\dots,q_{n})
.

In proving this, we show uniform convergence of
\mathbb{P}_{N,\rho}^{(n)}
to
P_{\mu}^{(n)}
and hence

\mathbb{P}_{N,\rho}^{(n)}\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}P_{\mu}^{(n)}

in total variation. For a stronger version of Theorem
Theorem
, see Theorem
1
, in which we provide a quantitative rate of convergence in the case where the number of steps
n
approaches the Ehrenfest time (defined in
2.4
).

1.2.
Previous Work

The understanding that a measurement affects a quantum state goes back to the early days of quantum mechanics. In his paper on the uncertainty principle, Heisenberg
[
Hei27
]
(see
[
Hei83
]
for an English translation) discussed the so-called “collapse” of the wave function, which was later rephrased in mathematical terms by von Neumann
[
Von13
]
. When a system is only partially or indirectly measured, the necessary framework is that of positive operator-valued measures, which were first studied by Naimark
[
Neu43
]
. The analog of wave-function collapse was introduced by Davies and Lewis
[
DL70
]
with the development of quantum instruments.

Further research focused on multiple measurements as a probabilistic process, which could be modeled in either discrete or continuous time. The first analysis of continuous-time measurements was due to Davies
[
Dav69
]
, and required extensive machinery from stochastic calculus (See
[
BG09
]
or
[
Hol03
]
for a brief introduction). Meanwhile, the discrete-time model consisting of repeated applications of a quantum instrument, while requiring substantially fewer technicalities, has nonetheless demonstrated a rich variety of behavior and is a subject of current study. Kümmerer and Maassen
[
KM03
]
demonstrated ergodicity of a repeated measurement process and showed that the states approach a pure state provided the measurement operators do not have a “dark” subspace
[
KM06
]
. Ballesteros et al.
[
Bal+18
]
show that when the dynamic is trivial, the state of the system localizes in space.

If the state is allowed to evolve in-between measurements, the situation becomes more complex. The mathematical analysis of such a quantum trajectory, which approximates a classical one, was done by Ballesteros et al.
[
Bal+21
]
following extensive physical evidence of the phenomenon, see the exposition by Figari
[
FT13
]
. That paper studied particles, initially in the set of normal states, evolving under a quadratic Hamiltonian. The motivation for our work came from the study of the semiclassical case under a more general Hamiltonian by Benoist, Fraas, and Fröhlich in
[
BFF22
]
. We adapt their framework to the setting of quantum maps.
This has two advantages: we can improve on the number of measurements exhibiting classical/quantum correspondence and we can provide (more easily than in the PDE setting) numerical simulations. In particular, the numerics indicate the accuracy of the dynamical bound on the number of measurements.

2.
Background

In this section, we provide a background on the measurement procedure as well as the semiclassical analysis tools required to prove our main result. Throughout this paper, we consider
h=(2\pi N)^{-1}>0
as the semiclassical parameter where
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

2.1.
Semiclassical Analysis on the Real Line

Physically, we are often concerned with how our quantum mechanical world approximately produces classical mechanics. This limit is achieved by taking a semiclassical parameter
h\in(0,1)
to be very small, and the math involved is called
semiclassical analysis
.

Definition 2.1

(symbol class)
.

For each
\delta\in[0,1/2)
, the
symbol class

S_{\delta}
is defined as

S_{\delta}:=\{a\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}):\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d},\,|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}a(x,\xi)|\leqslant C_{\alpha,\beta}h^{-\delta|\alpha+\beta|}\}.

Of most importance is the case where
\delta=0
, though technical steps of the proof of Theorem
1
will require us to consider general
\delta
. Nevertheless, if
\delta
is omitted it will be taken to be
0
. Elements of
S_{\delta}
(called
symbols
) may depend on
h
, though we require that the constants
C_{\alpha,\beta}
in the definition be uniform in
h
. Real-valued symbols correspond to classical observables, which can be “quantized” to get quantum observables as follows.

Definition 2.2

(Weyl quantization)
.

For
\delta\in[0,1/2)
, the
Weyl quantization
of a symbol
a\in S_{\delta}
is an operator
\Op_{h}a:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(sometimes written
a(x,hD)
) given by

(\Op_{h}(a)u)(x):=\frac{1}{(2\pi h)^{d}}\iint e^{\frac{i}{h}\langle x-y,\xi\rangle}a\left(\frac{x+y}{2},\xi\right)u(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi,

for
u\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

A theorem of Calderón and Vaillancourt states that if
\delta\in[0,1/2)
and
a\in S_{\delta}
, then
\Op_{h}(a)
is a bounded operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, with bound

(2.1)

\|\Op_{h}(a)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\leqslant C\|a\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}+C\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|\leqslant K}h^{\frac{|\alpha|+|\beta|}{2}}\|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}a\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

where
C>0
and
K\in\mathbb{N}
depends only on
d
. (In fact, we remark that (
2.1
) even holds for the threshold scaling
\delta=\frac{1}{2}
, though in this case, all derivatives of
a
in the sum will contribute equal orders of magnitude.)

If
a\in S
is real-valued,
\Op_{h}(a)
is a self-adjoint operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and more generally we have

\Op_{h}(a)^{*}=\Op_{h}(\overline{a}).

Weyl quantizations enjoy good properties under composition. In particular, if
\delta\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
and
a,b\in S_{\delta}
, then

\Op_{h}(a)\Op_{h}(b)=\Op_{h}(a\#b)

where
a\#b\in S_{\delta}
is the
Moyal product
given explicitly by

a\#b(x,\xi):=e^{ih\sigma(D_{x},D_{\xi},D_{y},D_{\eta})}(a(x,\xi)b(y,\eta))\Big|_{\begin{subarray}{c}y=x\\
\eta=\xi\end{subarray}}

from which one may derive the asymptotic summation formula

(2.2)

a\#b(x,\xi)\sim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{i^{k}h^{k}}{k!}\sigma(D_{x},D_{\xi},D_{y},D_{\eta})^{k}(a(x,\xi)b(y,\eta))\Big|_{\begin{subarray}{c}y=x\\
\eta=\xi\end{subarray}}

where in these formulas
\sigma(x,\xi,y,\eta):=\langle\xi,y\rangle-\langle x,\eta\rangle
, and
a\sim\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}h^{k}a_{k}
means that for each
K\in\mathbb{N}
,
(a-\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}h^{k}a_{k})\in h^{K}S_{\delta}.

In particular, one has that if
a,b\in S_{\delta}
, then
(\Op_{h}a)(\Op_{h}b)-\Op_{h}(ab)=\Op_{h}(r)
where
r
has
S_{\delta}
seminorms at most
O(h^{1-2\delta})
, with constants depending only on finitely many derivatives of
a
and
b
. More generally, given
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in S_{\delta}
, we have by an easy induction together with
(\ref{eq:CV})
that

(2.3)

\left\|\Op_{h}(a_{1})\cdots\Op_{h}(a_{n})-\Op_{h}(a_{1}\cdots a_{n})\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=\poly(n)O(h^{1-2\delta}).

Finally, we will need a theorem of Egorov, which gives a sense in which quantum operators evolve in time analogously to their classical counterparts. We provide the quantitative version due to Bouzouina and Robert
[
BR02
]
, see also Zworski
[
Zwo12
, Chapter 11.4]
. We first require the following definition.

Definition 2.3

(Lyapunov exponent)
.

For any dynamical system given by a flow
\phi_{t}
on
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\mathbb{T}^{2d}
(smooth in time), define the
Lyapunov exponent

\Gamma
to be

\Gamma=\lim_{t\to\infty}\sup_{y}\frac{1}{t}\log|\partial_{y}\phi_{t}(y)|

and analogously for iterative maps.

Given
p\in S
, let
\phi_{t}
be evolution by the classical Hamiltonian flow of
p
, induced by the Hamiltonian vector field
X_{p}=\partial_{\xi}p\partial_{x}-\partial_{x}p\partial_{\xi}
. On the quantum side, a particle evolves by the Schrödinger equation

hD_{t}\psi=P\psi,

so
\psi(t)=e^{-\frac{i}{h}tP}\psi(0)
and observables
A
evolve in the Heisenberg picture by
A(t)=e^{\frac{i}{h}tP}Ae^{-\frac{i}{h}tP}
. Egorov’s theorem states that this evolved operator is equal, up to order
h
, to the quantization of the classically observed symbol of
A
, for
t
less than
Ehrenfest time

t_{E}\sim\log\frac{1}{h}
. Specifically, we have that if
\Gamma
is the Lyapunov exponent of
\phi_{t}
and

(2.4)

|t|\leqslant T+\frac{\delta}{\Gamma+\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{h}

for some
\delta<\frac{1}{2}
and
\varepsilon>0
, then

(2.5)

e^{\frac{i}{h}tP}\Op_{h}(a)e^{-\frac{i}{h}tP}-\Op_{h}(\phi_{t}^{*}a)=\Op_{h}(r)

for
a\in S
, and
r\in S_{\delta}
has all seminorms of order
O(h^{2-3\delta})
.
In cases where
\Gamma=0
, such as a completely integrable classical system, some stronger results are available, see
[
BR02
]
.

2.2.
Fourier Integral Operators

As a generalization of the operators
e^{-\frac{i}{h}tP}
in Egorov’s theorem, we briefly discuss Fourier integral operators. One may view the operator
e^{-\frac{i}{h}P}
as quantizing a transformation (or equivalently, a “change of variables”) on phase space; namely the symplectomorphism on
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by
(x,\xi)\mapsto\phi_{t}(x,\xi)
. It is then natural to generalize the quantization to arbitrary symplectomorphisms. This abstraction will be rewarded when we work on the quantized torus in §
2.4
, on which the most natural classical transformations do not arise from a flow at all. Let
\phi:T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}
be any symplectomorphism. We consider only unitary Fourier integral operators here, and only consider their action in the case
\delta=0
. For the purposes of this paper, define a
Fourier integral operator quantizing
\phi
in the following way.

Definition 2.4

(quantized flow)
.

For
\phi:T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}\to T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Fourier integral operator quantizing
\phi
is the operator
U:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that the Egorov-type relation

U^{-1}\Op_{h}(a)U=\Op_{h}(b)

holds for any
a\in S
with
b=\phi^{*}a+O_{S}(h)
.

In particular (using these definitions), Egorov’s theorem is precisely the statement that
e^{\frac{i}{h}tP}
is a one-parameter family of Fourier integral operators, which quantize the classical Hamiltonian flow
\phi_{t}
.

Perhaps the most important Fourier integral operators are the
metaplectic operators

[
Zwo12
, Chapter 11.3]
, which is the special case when
\phi
is a linear symplectomorphism. In this case for
M\in\operatorname{Sp}(2d,\mathbb{R})
, we write the corresponding metaplectic operator as
\widehat{M}
. The metaplectic operators are unitary and benefit from an exact version of (
2.4
), ie they satisfy

\widehat{M}^{-1}\Op_{h}(a)\widehat{M}=\Op_{h}(M^{*}a)

for all
a\in S
, with no error. We may remark that while the metaplectic operators define a projective unitary representation of
\operatorname{Sp}(2d,\mathbb{R})
, they are in fact a unitary representation of its double cover, which is known as the
metaplectic group
. This is analogous to projective representations of rotation operators on spinor spaces and leads similarly to a non-canonical choice of phase for a metaplectic operator. As we will only encounter metaplectic operators when we are conjugating by them, this phase vanishes and we need not concern ourselves with it.

2.3.
Semiclassical Defect Measures

We now discuss semiclassical defect measures, which give a quantitative answer to where in phase space a quantum particle “is” in the semiclassical limit as
h\to 0
.

Definition 2.5

(defect measure)
.

We say an
h
-dependent set of density operators
\rho_{h}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, has
defect measure

\mu
if for all
a\in C^{\infty}_{0}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

(2.6)

\lim_{h\to 0}\operatorname{tr}\left(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(a)\right)=\int_{T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.

If
\psi_{h}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we say it has defect measure
\mu
if the density operator
\left\lvert\psi_{h}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_{h}\right\rvert
has defect measure
\mu
.

Having a semiclassical defect measure is a very special property of a sequence, corresponding to being “essentially classical” as
h\to 0
. Nevertheless, such a limit necessarily exists along a subsequence of
h
’s, as the following proposition states.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\rho_{h}
be an
h
-dependent family of density operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then there is a nonnegative Radon measure
\mu
on
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a sequence
h_{j}\to 0
such that

\lim_{j\to\infty}\operatorname{tr}\left(\rho_{h_{j}}\Op_{h_{j}}(a)\right)=\int_{T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu

for all
a\in C_{0}^{\infty}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The proof of Proposition
2.6
is essentially the same as
[
Zwo12
, Theorem 5.2]
, which proves it in the special case of pure states.

In many natural cases, we may take the limit in (
2.6
) to be uniform in the symbol
a
, in which case we refer to
\mu
as a
uniform defect measure
. For a precise characterization of aforesaid measures, see Proposition
A.1
. In particular, in this paper, we will work on the quantized torus
H_{N}
(see §
2.4
) where all defect measures are uniform.

For semiclassical pseudodifferential operators on the real line, there are standard classes of states with known defect measures
[
BFF22
, Proposition 3.3]
which we restate in Proposition
2.7
. For a full proof (in French) see
[
LP93
, §3]
and a proof for less general coherent states see
[
Zwo12
, Chapter 5.1]
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Fix
g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|g\|_{L^{2}}=1
, define

\psi_{h}(x):=h^{-\frac{d\beta}{2}}g\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{h^{\beta}}\right)\exp\left(i\dfrac{x\cdot\xi_{0}}{h}\right),

and let
\rho_{h}=\left\lvert\psi_{h}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_{h}\right\rvert.
Then
\rho_{h}
has defect measure
\mu
, given as follows:

•

If
\beta=0
,
\mu=\delta_{\xi=\xi_{0}}|g(x-x_{0})|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
,

•

If
\beta=1
,
\mu=\delta_{x=x_{0}}|\hat{g}(\xi-\xi_{0})|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi
,

•

If
\beta\in(0,1)
,
\mu=\delta_{x=x_{0},\xi=\xi_{0}}
.

Here
\hat{g}
is Fourier transform of
g
.

In the last case of Proposition
2.7
, the states
\psi_{h}
are called
generalized coherent states
.

In fact, as we will be working with
S_{\delta}
classes and must control the error of our estimates, we will need a slightly stronger definition to accommodate
h
-dependent symbols.

Definition 2.8

(uniform defect measure)
.

If
\rho_{h}
has defect measure
\mu
,
\delta\in[0,\frac{1}{2})
, and
\theta>0
, then we say
\mu
is a
uniform
(\delta,\theta)
-defect measure
provided

(2.7)

\left|\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(a))-\int_{T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right|=O(h^{\theta})

holds for all
a=a_{h}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap S_{\delta}
with constant depending only on the symbol norms of
a
.

We easily see that if
\mu
is a
(\delta,\theta)
-defect measure, it is necessarily a
(\delta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})
-defect measure for all
\delta^{\prime}\leqslant\delta
and
\theta^{\prime}\leqslant\theta
. To address a potential point of confusion, note that in (
2.7
) we are enlarging the space of symbols,
a
, used in (
2.6
). These symbols are allowed to depend on
h
with derivatives growing in
h
, provided they do not grow too fast, and their support may not be uniformly bounded in
h
.

We can now state a refinement of Proposition
2.7
using this new terminology.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\rho_{h}
be as in Proposition
2.7
. Then the defect measures
\mu
given in Proposition
2.7
are uniform
(\delta,\theta)
-defect measures with
\delta,\theta
given as follows:

•

If
\beta\in\{0,1\}
, then
\delta<\frac{1}{2}
and
\theta<1-2\delta
,

•

If
\beta\in(0,1)
, then
\delta<\min(\beta,1-\beta)
and
\theta<\min(\beta-\delta,1-\beta-\delta)
.

The proof of Proposition
2.9
is straightforward and given in Appendix
B.1
.

2.4.
The Quantized Torus

We now give an introduction to the quantized torus, and the pseudodifferential calculus on the Hilbert space of corresponding quantum states.

For each
N\in\mathbb{N}
, define

(2.8)

\displaystyle H_{N}:=\mathrm{span}\left\{\frac{1}{N^{\frac{d}{2}}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\delta_{x=k+\frac{n}{N}}:n\in\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}^{d}\right\}.

We may remark that
H_{N}
consists precisely of the elements of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
which are periodic in physical and Fourier space under the semiclassical Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{h}
, given by

\mathcal{F}_{h}u(\xi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi h)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int e^{\frac{i}{h}x\cdot\xi}u(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

where
h=(2\pi N)^{-1}
. For simplicity define

(2.9)

\displaystyle Q_{n}=\frac{1}{N^{\frac{d}{2}}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\delta_{x=k+\frac{n}{N}},

and define a Hilbert space structure on
H_{N}
such that
(Q_{n})
is an orthonormal basis of
H_{N}
. Though it is not necessary here, we may note that this is an elementary example of the general geometric Toeplitz quantization studied in
[
Del19
]
.

We now give an overview of the pseudodifferential calculus on
H_{N}
, which also may be found in
[
CZ10
]
,

[
DJ21
]
,
[
Sch09
]
. Let
a\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2d})
. Intuitively,
a
corresponds to a classical observable, with
\mathbb{T}^{2d}
playing the role of phase space. We see that
a
lifts to a doubly-periodic function
\tilde{a}
on
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is in the symbol class
S
. By
[
CZ10
, §2.3]

\Op_{N}a
maps
H_{N}
(as a subset of
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
) to itself, so we may define

(2.10)

\Op_{N}a:=\Op_{h}\tilde{a}|_{H_{N}}:H_{N}\to H_{N}

which is also given in coordinates
[
CZ10
, Lemmma 2.4]
by

\Op_{N}(a)Q_{j}=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}A_{mj}Q_{m}

with

A_{mj}=\sum_{k,l\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{a}(k,j-m-lN)(-1)^{kl}e^{\pi i\frac{(j+m)k}{N}},

where
\hat{a}
is the (non-semiclassical) Fourier series in both variables. Furthermore, we may define an analogue of
S_{\delta}
symbols as

S_{\delta}(\mathbb{T}^{2d}):=\{a\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2d}):\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},\,|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}\partial_{\xi}^{\beta}|\leqslant C_{\alpha\beta}N^{\delta|\alpha+\beta|}\}

from which the obvious generalization of (
2.10
) can be defined.

Most results from pseudodifferential calculus carry over to
H_{N}
, see
[
CZ10
,
DJ21
]
for details. In particular, the formulas for compositions and Egorov’s theorem are the same and follow immediately, and we have a version of the Calderón-Vaillancourt theorem
[
CZ10
, Proposition 2.7]
that says

\|\Op_{N}(a)\|_{H_{N}}\leqslant\|a\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2d})}+o(1),

where
\|\cdot\|_{H_{N}}
is the norm given by the Hilbert space structure. In fact, one can directly bound
\Op_{N}(a)
by the corresponding map on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

(2.11)

\|\Op_{N}(a)\|_{H_{N}}\leqslant\|\Op_{h}(\tilde{a})\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

see
[
DJ21
, §2.2.3]
for an explanation using the direct integral decomposition of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

A particular class of metaplectic operators can also be defined on
H_{N}
. Let
M\in\operatorname{Sp}(2d,\mathbb{Z})
be an integer symplectic matrix, and let
N
be even. Define
\widehat{M}_{N}=\widehat{M}|_{H_{N}}
, which can be shown
[
DJ21
, §2.2.4]
to be a unitary map on
H_{N}
. We then have an analogous exact Egorov’s theorem:

(2.12)

\Op_{N}(a)\widehat{M}_{N}=\widehat{M}_{N}\Op_{N}(a\circ M).

The definition of a defect measure on
H_{N}
is also identical. Given an
N
-dependent sequence of density operators on
H_{N}
, we say they have
defect measure

\mu
if for every
a\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2d})
,

\lim_{N\to\infty}\operatorname{tr}\left(\rho_{N}\Op_{N}(a)\right)=\int_{T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.

The proof of Proposition
A.1
shows that every defect measure on the torus is uniform. In particular, we have the following result to be an immediate consequence.

Proposition 2.10
.

If
\rho_{N}
are density operators on
H_{N}
with defect measure
\mu
, then
\mu
is a probability measure, and for every
\varepsilon>0
there is an
h_{0}
such that if
h<h_{0}
and
\|a^{(\alpha)}\|\leqslant 1
for all
|\alpha|<K(d)
, then

\left|\operatorname{tr}_{H_{N}}(\rho_{N}\Op_{N}(a))-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2d}}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right|\leqslant\varepsilon.

The notion of a
(\delta,\theta)
-defect measure (
2.7
) also carries over to the quantized torus case, where the symbols in this case are in
S_{\delta}(\mathbb{T}^{2d})
.

Proposition
2.7
, which gives the defect measures of generalized coherent states, has a natural analog on the quantized torus. We do not claim these values of
\delta,\theta
to be sharp.

Proposition 2.11
.

Given
g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d};\mathbb{C})
such that
\|g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}=1
, and
\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
, define

\displaystyle\psi_{N}:=\sum_{k\in(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{d}}N^{-\frac{d}{2}}g\left(\frac{k}{N}\right)e^{2\pi ik\cdot\xi_{0}}Q_{k},

and set
\rho_{N}:=C_{N}\left\lvert\psi_{N}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_{N}\right\rvert
with
C_{N}:=\|\psi_{N}\|_{H_{N}}^{-2}
(for
Q_{k}
defined in (
2.9
)). Then
\rho_{N}
has the semiclassical defect measure
\mu=\delta_{\xi=\xi_{0}}|g(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x
. In particular,
\mu
is a
(\delta,\theta)
-defect measure for
\delta<(d+2)^{-1}
,
\theta<1-(d+2)\delta.

To prepare for the next proposition, we define the map
\Pi_{N}:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to H_{N}
by

(2.13)

(\Pi_{N}u)_{n}=\frac{1}{N^{\frac{d}{2}}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}u\left(k+\frac{n}{N}\right)Q_{n}.

Proposition 2.12
.

Given
\beta\in(0,1)
and
g\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, define

\displaystyle g_{N}(x):=N^{\frac{d\beta}{2}}g(N^{\beta}(x-x_{0}))e^{2\pi iNx\cdot\xi_{0}}.

Next, define
\psi_{N}:=\Pi_{N}g_{N}
, and
\rho_{N}:=C_{N}^{\prime}\left\lvert\psi_{N}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_{N}\right\rvert
, where
C_{N}^{\prime}:=\|\psi_{N}\|_{H_{N}}^{-2}
. Then
\rho_{N}
has the semiclassical defect measure
\mu=\delta_{x=x_{0},\xi=\xi_{0}}
, which is a
(\delta,\theta)
-defect measure for
\delta<\min(\beta,\frac{1-\beta}{d+1})
,
\theta<\min(\beta-\delta,1-\beta-(d+1)\delta)
.

Remark 1
.

We can easily see that
C_{N},C_{N}^{\prime}=1+o(1)
. Indeed, by Riemann sums:

C_{N}^{-1}=N^{-d}\sum_{k\in(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{d}}\left|g\left(\frac{k}{N}\right)\right|^{2}=1+O(N^{-1}),

so
C_{N}=1+O(N^{-1})
. Similarly,

\begin{gathered}(C_{N}^{\prime})^{-1}=N^{-d(\beta-1)}\sum_{m\in(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{d}}\left|g\left(N^{\beta}\left(\frac{m}{N}-x_{0}\right)\right)\right|^{2}\\
=N^{-d}\sum_{m\in(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{d}}N^{d\beta}\left|g\left(N^{\beta}\left(\frac{m}{N}-x_{0}\right)\right)\right|^{2}=1+O(N^{-(1-\beta)}),\end{gathered}

so
C_{N}^{\prime}=1+O(N^{-(1-\beta)})
.

In particular, this shows that the constants
C_{N}
,
C_{N}^{\prime}
in Propositions
2.11
and
2.12
can actually be taken to be
1
without affecting the value of the defect measure, or even the possible values of
(\delta,\theta)
. They are only included so the density operators match our physical motivation of states with norm exactly
1
.

The proofs of Propositions
2.11
and
2.12
are given in Appendix
B.2
.

2.5.
Measurement Procedure

We provide a brief background on the quantum mechanics used in this paper. For a more comprehensive overview, see
[
BG09
]

[
Hol03
]
. To model particles in quantum mechanics, we begin by fixing a Hilbert space
H
, known as the
state space
. Particles in our model are described by
density operators
.

Definition 2.13

(density operator)
.

A density operator on
H
is a symmetric, positive semidefinite, trace-class operator on
H
with trace
1
.

Quantum particles in this paper are measured to have some value in
\Omega
(a locally compact metric space) by a type of quantum instrument called
Kraus operators
.

Definition 2.14

(Kraus operators)
.

Suppose
\Omega
is a locally compact metric space with Borel measure
\nu
. And suppose for
q\in\Omega
,
A_{q}
is a bounded operator on
H
,
q\mapsto A_{q}
is measurable, and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}A_{q}^{*}A_{q}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q)=I.

Then we call
A_{q}
’s
Kraus operators
.

Given a quantum particle described by a density operator
\rho
and Kraus operators
A_{q}
on
\Omega
, the probability of measuring
\rho
in
F\subset\Omega
(a measurable set) is:

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left(\int_{F}A_{q}\rho A_{q}^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q)\right).

After measurement within
F
, the state changes to:

\displaystyle\rho(F):=\frac{\int_{F}A_{q}\rho A_{q}^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q)}{\operatorname{tr}\left(\int_{F}A_{q}\rho A_{q}^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q)\right)}.

For each measurable set
F\subset\Omega
, we can define
\mathcal{I}(F):=\rho\mapsto\int_{F}A_{q}\rho A_{q}^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q)
. In this case,
\mathcal{I}
is an example of a quantum instrument.

This paper considers a specific class of Kraus operators. The Hilbert space we work on is the quantized torus
H_{N}
(recall §
2.4
). We fix a compact metric space
\Omega
with finite Borel measure
\nu(q)
, and let
f_{q}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2d})
satisfy

(2.14)

\int_{\Omega}\Op_{N}(f_{q})^{*}\Op_{N}(f_{q})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q)=Id,

where
\Op_{N}
is defined in (
2.10
). Additionally, assume that
f_{q}
are in
S
(recall Definition
2.1
) uniformly in
q
, meaning
\|\partial^{\alpha}f_{q}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant C_{\alpha}
with constant independent of
q
. These
\Op_{N}(f_{q})
are the Kraus operators we will consider and we will refer to
f_{q}
as the symbols of the Kraus operators.

As a natural example of the above construction, the reader may consider the example where
\Omega=\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q)=\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}q
, and
f_{q}(x,\xi)=f(x-q)
, where
\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}|f(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=1
. Then conjugation by
\Op_{N}(f_{q})
corresponds intuitively to an “approximate position measurement” (see also
[
Bal+18
]
), where the approximation approaches a (nonexistent) “exact” position measurement as
f
approaches a delta function.

2.6.
Evolution Procedure

This paper models quantum particles which are repeatedly measured and evolved. The previous section discussed the measurement procedure, here we discuss the evolution procedure.

We consider two different evolution procedures. Particles will either be evolved by (1) a metaplectic operator or (2) exponentiation of a quantization of a Hamiltonian on the torus. In the first case, we fix
M\in\operatorname{Sp}(2d,\mathbb{Z})
and let
\widehat{M}_{N}
be the corresponding metaplectic operator acting on
H_{N}
(recall §
2.2
). In the second case, we fix
p\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2d})
, and let
P=\Op_{N}(p)
(recall (
2.10
)).

Define an operator
\Phi_{N,q}^{*}
on density operators given by

(2.15)

\Phi_{N,q}^{*}(\rho):=U\Op_{N}(f_{q})\rho\Op_{N}(f_{q})^{*}U^{-1}

where
U
is a unitary operator given either by
U=\widehat{M}_{N}
or
U=e^{-2\pi iNP}
.
We remark that
\Phi^{*}_{N,q}(\rho)
is
U\tilde{\rho}(q)U^{-1}
where
\tilde{\rho}(q)=\Op_{N}(f_{q})\rho\Op_{N}(f_{q})^{*}
is an un-normalized a posteriori state of the instrument determined by Kraus operators
\Op_{N}(f_{q})
. We stress again that
\Phi^{*}_{N,q}(\rho)
is not a density operator, as it is not normalized; the trace will be taken at the end and interpreted as the probability density of the trajectory
(q_{0},\dots,q_{n})
.

The evolution of the quantum system by
U
corresponds to a classical dynamical system
\phi_{t}:\mathbb{T}^{2d}\to\mathbb{T}^{2d}
. In the case of evolving by the metaplectic operator
\widehat{M_{N}}
,
\phi_{t}:=M^{t}
(where
t
takes integer values). In the case of evolving by
e^{-2\pi iNt\Op_{N}(p)}
, the corresponding classical dynamical system is
\phi_{t}:=\exp(tH_{p})
where
H_{p}
is the Hamiltonian vector field generated by
p
.

The quantitative convergence rates of the measures on the quantum and classical trajectories depend on the Lyapunov exponents (recall Definition
2.3
) of these corresponding classical dynamical systems. A system with a larger Lyapunov exponent is more exponentially sensitive to initial conditions, and hence seen as “more chaotic.” In particular, completely integrable systems have Lyapunov exponents equal to zero.

Lyapunov exponents appear crucially in the following bound on symbol seminorms of a time-evolved symbol.

Proposition 2.15
.

Suppose
\phi_{t}
is a smooth dynamical system on
\mathbb{T}^{2d}
where
t
either takes values in
\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
or
\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
(in which case we assume
\phi_{t}
is smooth in time), with Lyapunov exponent
\Gamma
. Suppose
a\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2d})
, and define
a_{t}=a\circ\phi_{t}
. Then for
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{2d}
,
\varepsilon>0
,

\|\partial^{\alpha}a_{t}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant C_{\alpha,\varepsilon}e^{(\Gamma+\varepsilon)|\alpha|t}\sup_{|\beta|\leqslant|\alpha|}\|\partial^{\beta}a\|_{L^{\infty}}.

In particular if
a\in S
and
t\leqslant\frac{\delta}{\Gamma+\varepsilon}\log\frac{1}{h}
, then
a_{t}\in S_{\delta}
with seminorms independent of
t
.

A version of Proposition
2.15
on manifolds is discussed in
[
AN07
, §5.2]
,
[
DG14
, Appendix C]
, and
[
DJN22
, §5.2]
. We present a proof of Proposition
2.15
in Appendix
C
, adapting the arguments of the mentioned references to the simpler case of the torus.

3.
Main Result and Proof

We are now able to provide a quantitative proof of our result.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
H_{N}
is the quantized torus of dimension
2d
,
\rho=\rho_{N}:H_{N}\to H_{N}
is a set of density operators depending on
N
with semiclassical defect measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{T}^{2d}
,
\Omega
is a compact metric space with finite Borel measure
\nu
,
f_{q}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2d})
satisfy (
2.14
),
P_{\mu}^{(n)}
is defined by (
1.2
), and
\mathbb{P}_{N,\rho}^{(n)}
is defined by (
1.1
), with
\Phi_{N,q}^{*}
defined by
(\ref{eq:defPhi})
. Then for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have as
N\to\infty
the convergence in total variation

\mathbb{P}_{N,\rho}^{(n)}\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}P_{\mu}^{(n)}.

Furthermore, we have the quantitative estimate that if
\mu
is a
(\delta,\theta)
-defect measure as defined in (
2.7
) and
\varepsilon>0
, then there are
C_{0},C_{1}
independent of
n
such that if

(3.1)

n<\frac{\delta\log(N/C_{0})}{\Gamma+\varepsilon},

then

(3.2)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbb{P}_{N,\rho}^{(n)}-P_{\mu}^{(n)}\right\|_{TV}\leqslant C_{1}N^{-\min(1-2\delta,\theta)}

where
\Gamma
is the Lyapunov exponent of
M
(metaplectic case) or
\phi_{t}
(Hamiltonian evolution case).

Remark 2
.

The condition (
3.1
) can be recognized as the
Ehrenfest time
given in (
2.4
).

Proof of Theorem
1
.

This proof goes along the lines of
[
BFF22
, Theorem 3.1]
, but gives quantitative bounds. The version for
U=e^{-2\pi iNP}
requires the quantitative version of Egorov’s theorem given in (
2.5
).

We first consider when
U=\widehat{M}_{N}.
We have

(3.3)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}^{(n)}_{N,\rho}(q_{0},\dots,q_{n})=\operatorname{tr}_{H_{N}}\left(\prod_{i=n}^{0}\left(\widehat{M}_{N}\Op_{N}(f_{q_{i}})\right)\rho\prod_{i=0}^{n}\left(\Op_{N}(f_{q_{i}})^{*}\widehat{M}_{N}^{-1}\right)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q)\\
&=\operatorname{tr}_{H_{N}}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{n}\left(\Op_{N}(f_{q_{i}})^{*}\widehat{M}_{N}^{-1}\right)\prod_{i=n}^{0}\left(\widehat{M}_{N}\Op_{N}(f_{q_{i}})\right)\rho\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q)\\
&=\operatorname{tr}_{H_{N}}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{n}\left(\Op_{N}(f_{q_{i}}\circ M^{i})^{*}\right)\prod_{i=n}^{0}\left(\Op_{N}(f_{q_{i}}\circ M^{i})\right)\rho\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q)\\
&=\left(\operatorname{tr}_{H_{N}}\left(\Op_{N}(|f_{q_{0}}|^{2}\cdots|f_{q_{n}}\circ M^{n}|^{2})\rho\right)+\poly(n)O(N^{-(1-2\delta)})\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q)\\
&=\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2d}}|f_{q_{0}}(\zeta)|^{2}\cdots|f_{q_{n}}(M^{n}\zeta)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu+\poly\log(N)(O(N^{-(1-2\delta)})+O(N^{-\theta}))\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q).\end{split}

The first three equalities follow from the definition of
\Phi_{N,q_{j}}^{*}
, commutativity of the trace, the exact Egorov property (
2.12
) of
\widehat{M}_{N}
, and the unitarity of
\widehat{M}_{N}^{*}
. We then used Proposition
2.15
to see that for each
i
,
f\circ M^{i}\in S_{\delta}
, for

\displaystyle\delta\geqslant\frac{(\Gamma+\varepsilon)n}{\log(N)}

so that we could use the composition rule for pseudodifferential operators (
2.3
) together with (
2.11
) to get the fourth equality. We lastly used that
\rho
has
(\delta,\theta)
-defect measure
\mu
to get the last equality. Note that we may remove the
\poly\log(N)
term by adjusting
\varepsilon
and replacing
\delta
with a slightly smaller
\delta^{\prime}
.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}P^{(n)}_{\mu}=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{2d}}|f_{q_{0}}(\zeta)|^{2}\cdots|f_{q_{n}}(M^{n}\zeta)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q).

We may now integrate in
q
and apply the dominated convergence theorem (using that the
\|f_{q}\|
are uniformly bounded in
q
) to get the theorem for the case that
U=\widehat{M}_{N}
.

When
U=e^{-2\pi iNP}
, the proof is the same but uses the quantitative version of Egorov’s theorem (
2.5
) on the second line of (
3.3
), to give

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}_{H_{N}}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{n}\left(\Op_{N}(f_{q_{i}})^{*}e^{2\pi iNP}\right)\prod_{i=n}^{0}\left(e^{-2\pi iNP}\Op_{N}(f_{q_{i}})\right)\rho\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q)

\displaystyle\quad=\left(\operatorname{tr}_{H_{N}}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{n}\left(\Op_{N}(f_{q_{i}}\circ\phi_{i})^{*}\right)\prod_{i=n}^{0}\left(\Op_{N}(f_{q_{i}}\circ\phi_{i})\right)\rho\right)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\left(\poly\log(N)O(N^{-(2-3\delta)})\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q).

The
O(N^{-(2-3\delta)})
error is dominated by the
O(N^{-(1-2\delta)})
error later on in (
3.3
), and the remainder of the proof is the same.
∎

4.
Numerical Illustrations

Here we present numerical simulations to illustrate the results of this paper. In these numerics, we simulate the evolution of a state on the quantization of the 2-dimensional torus. The measurement procedure will use Kraus operators
f_{q}(x,\xi)=f(x-q)
, with

(4.1)

\displaystyle f(x)=c\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\left(\frac{-(x-2\pi k)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)\right)^{1/2}

where
\sigma>0
is a parameter to be chosen
2
2

2

\sigma
can be viewed as the measurement precision. The smaller the value of
\sigma
, the more precise.
and
c>0
is such that
\int_{\mathbb{T}}|f|^{2}=1
. At each time step, after observation, we evolve the particle with the metaplectic operator
\widehat{M}_{N}
(as defined in §
2.4
). For now, we let
M
be Arnold’s cat map,

(4.2)

\displaystyle M=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}.

We also choose the initial state to be
\rho=\rho(N)=|\psi_{N}\rangle\langle\psi_{N}|
with

(4.3)

\displaystyle\psi_{N}=\Pi_{N}\left(\frac{N^{\frac{\beta}{2}}}{\sigma^{\prime}\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(\frac{-((x-x_{0})N^{\beta})^{2}}{2(\sigma^{\prime})^{2}}+2\pi iNxp_{0}\right)\right)

with
\Pi_{N}
defined in (
2.13
) and some fixed
x_{0},p_{0}\in[0,1]
and
\sigma^{\prime}>0
. By Proposition
2.12
,
\rho
has semiclassical defect measure
\delta_{x=x_{0},\xi=p_{0}}
when
\beta\in(0,1)
.

Let
Q_{i}
be the observed location of the state at time
i
. These
Q_{i}
’s are random variables, and their joint distribution is described by (

). To numerically simulate this distribution, we note that formally
\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{i=0}^{n}\left\{Q_{i}=q_{i}\right\}\right)
can be written

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Q_{n}=q_{n}\;\middle|\;\bigcap_{i=0}^{n-1}\left\{Q_{i}=q_{i}\right\}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(Q_{n-1}=q_{n-1}\;\middle|\;\bigcap_{i=0}^{n-2}\left\{Q_{i}=q_{i}\right\}\right)\cdots\mathbb{P}(Q_{0}=q_{0}).

Therefore to sample

from this distribution, we first sample

with law
\mathbb{P}(Q_{0}=q_{0})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q_{0})
, and get a value, which we call
\bar{q}_{0}
. Then we sample
Q_{1}
with law

\mathbb{P}\left(Q_{1}=q_{1}\;\middle|\;Q_{0}=\bar{q}_{0}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q_{1}).

We continue this process to get values of

.

For our choice of initial state and Kraus operators, the probability density functions of these distributions greatly simplify. Working in the basis of
H_{N}
described in §
2.4
, we write
|\psi_{N}\rangle
as a vector
v(0)\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
(the argument of
v
will denote the time at which the state is to be observed). It is a straightforward computation that, with respect to this basis,

\Op_{N}(f_{q})=\operatorname{diag}(f(x_{1}-q),f(x_{2}-q),\dots,f(x_{N}-q))

where
x_{i}=i/N
. To ease notation, let
\widehat{M}_{N}:=U
and
\Op_{N}(f_{q}):=A_{q}
. Then, by (
1.1
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Q_{0}=q_{0})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}(UA_{q_{0}}v(0)\bar{v}(0)^{t}A_{q_{0}}U^{*})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}(A_{q_{0}})_{k,k}^{2}v(0)_{k}^{2}=\sum_{k}f(x_{k}-q_{0})^{2}v(0)_{k}^{2}.

Observe that this is the convolution of the probability density functions
f(x)^{2}
and
\sum_{k}1_{x_{k}}v(0)_{k}^{2}
(call random variables with these distributions
F
and
V(0)
respectively). Here
1_{x_{k}}
is
1
at
x_{k}
and zero everywhere else. Therefore to sample
Q_{0}
, we sample from the random variable
F+V(0)
to get
\bar{q}_{0}
.

To sample
Q_{i}
, for
i>1
, we proceed in a similar manner. Assuming we have already computed that
Q_{j}=\bar{q}_{j}
for
j<i
, we must sample a random variable with distribution

(4.4)

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}&\left(Q_{i}=q_{i}\;\middle|\;\bigcap_{j=1}^{i-1}Q_{j}=\bar{q}_{j}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q_{i})\\
&=\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{j=1}^{i-1}Q_{j}=\bar{q}_{j}\right)^{-1}\operatorname{tr}\left(A_{q_{i}}\prod_{j=1}^{i}(UA_{\bar{q}_{i-j}})v(0)v(0)^{t}\prod_{j=0}^{i-1}(A_{\bar{q}_{j}}U^{*})A_{q_{i}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\nu(q_{i}).\end{split}

Let:

\displaystyle v(i)=\prod_{j=1}^{i}(UA_{\bar{q}_{i-j}})v(0),

so that (
4.4
) is
c\sum_{k}f(x_{k}-q_{i})^{2}v(i)_{j}^{2}
, with
c^{-1}=\sum_{k}v(i)_{k}^{2}
. Like before, let
V(i)
be a random variable with probability density function
\sum_{k}v(i)_{k}^{2}1_{x_{k}}
. Therefore, to sample
Q_{i}
, sample from
V(i)+F
. This proves the following proposition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose
\rho=|\psi_{N}\rangle\langle\psi_{N}|
is an initial state (as defined by (
4.3
)),
f(x-q)
are the symbols of Kraus operators (given by (
4.1
)), and
n>0
. Then a random trajectory of a quantum particle with distribution
\mathbb{P}^{(n)}_{N,\rho}
, described by (
1.1
), can be sampled by applying Algorithm
1
by storing the values
q_{0},q_{1},\dots,q_{n}
(representing the observed locations of the particle at each instance of time).

Algorithm 1

Quantum Particle Evolution

1:

\textbf{v}\leftarrow|\psi_{N}\rangle

2:

while

i\leqslant n

do

3:

q_{i}\leftarrow
sampled random number from distribution
\sum_{k}1_{x_{k}}\textbf{v}_{k}^{2}

4:

g\leftarrow
sampled random number from Gaussian distribution of mean
0
, variance
\sigma^{2}

5:

q_{i}\leftarrow q_{i}+g\pmod{1}

6:

\textbf{v}\leftarrow\operatorname{diag}(f(x_{1}-q_{i}),\dots,f(x_{N}-q_{i}))\textbf{v}

7:

\textbf{v}\leftarrow\hat{M}_{N}\textbf{v}

8:

\textbf{v}\leftarrow\left\|\textbf{v}\right\|^{-1}\textbf{v}

9:

i\leftarrow i+1

10:

end

while

In this algorithm, we use the fact that in the fixed basis of
H_{N}

(4.5)

\displaystyle(\widehat{M})_{j,k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\exp\left(\pi i(2k^{2}-2jk+j^{2})/N\right),

Appendix A
Uniformity of Defect Measures

Proposition
2.6
motivates the definition of defect measures, but this definition allows for some undesirable consequences, which show up in the following pathological example. Let
f\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1
, and define
\psi_{h}(x)=f\left(x+h^{-1}\right).
It is easy to show that
\psi_{h}
has defect measure equal to the zero measure, corresponding intuitively to the mass having “escaped to infinity.” Indeed, one has for any
a\in C^{\infty}_{0}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})
supported in a ball of radius
R
in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}\cong T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
h<(2R)^{-1}
, and
\chi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
\chi(x)=1
on
|x|\geqslant 2R
,
\chi=0
on
|x|\leqslant R
that

(A.1)

\Op_{h}(a)\psi_{h}=\Op_{h}(a)\Op_{h}(\chi)\psi_{h}=O(h^{\infty})

which gives

(A.2)

\lim_{h\to 0}\langle\Op_{h}(a)\psi_{h},\psi_{h}\rangle=0.

Unfortunately, there is no way to quantify the rate of decay of (
A.2
), as it depends on not just the symbol norms of
a
but also the location of its support. In addition, the resulting measure is not a probability measure, which defies our intuition of the defect measure representing the classical location of the particle in phase space.
We are therefore motivated to make a more restrictive definition of defect measures to prohibit examples such as (
A.1
). Call
\mu
a
uniform semiclassical defect measure
of
\rho_{h}
if for any
a\in S
,

(A.3)

\lim_{h\to 0}\operatorname{tr}\left(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(a)\right)=\int_{T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.

The reason for the name is given in the following proposition. We use the standard multi-index notation. For
a\in C^{\infty}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{2d}
, we let

\displaystyle a^{(\alpha)}(x)=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{2d}\partial_{j}^{\alpha_{j}}\right)a(x).

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\rho_{h}
be a set of density operators with defect measure
\mu
. The following are equivalent.

(1)

\mu
is a uniform defect measure.

(2)

For all
\varepsilon>0
, there is an
h_{0}(\varepsilon)>0
such that if
0<h<h_{0}
and
a\in C^{\infty}_{0}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|a^{(\alpha)}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant 1
for all
|\alpha|<K(d)
then

\left|\operatorname{tr}\left(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(a)\right)-\int_{T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right|\leqslant\varepsilon.

(3)

For all
\varepsilon>0
, there is an
h_{0}(\varepsilon)>0
such that if
0<h<h_{0}
and
a\in S
with
\|a^{(\alpha)}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant 1
for all
|\alpha|<K(d)
then

\left|\operatorname{tr}\left(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(a)\right)-\int_{T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right|\leqslant\varepsilon.

(4)

\mu
is a probability measure.

Proof.

We trivially see that
(3)\implies(2)
and
(3)\implies(1)
.

To show
(2)\implies(3)
, let
a\in S
, and let
\chi_{k}\in C^{\infty}_{0}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})
be smooth partition of unity of
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}
, chosen as in
[
Zwo12
, Theorem 4.23]
, with the first
K(d)
derivatives chosen small enough. By the Cotlar-Stein-Knapp lemma, we have the limit in the strong operator topology:

\lim_{K\to\infty}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{K}\Op_{h}(\chi_{k}a)\right)\psi=\Op_{h}(a)\psi

for every
\psi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. We need the following brief lemma.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
A_{K}
be a sequence of uniformly bounded operators converging to
A
in the strong topology, and let
\rho
be self-adjoint and trace class. Then

\lim_{K\to\infty}\operatorname{tr}(\rho A_{K})=\operatorname{tr}(\rho A).

Proof of Lemma
A.2
.

By subtracting we may assume
A=0
, and without loss of generality let
\sup_{K}\|A_{K}\|=1
. Let
\psi_{j}
be an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of the compact operator
\rho
, so

\rho=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\left\lvert\psi_{j}\right\rangle\left\langle\psi_{j}\right\rvert

with
\lambda_{j}\to 0
. as
j\to\infty
. Let
\varepsilon>0
, and choose
J
such that
\lambda_{j}<\varepsilon
for
j>J
. Then

|\operatorname{tr}(\rho A_{K})|=\left|\sum_{j\leqslant J}\lambda_{j}\langle A_{K}\psi_{j},\psi_{j}\rangle+\sum_{j\leqslant J}\lambda_{j}\langle A_{K}\psi_{j},\psi_{j}\rangle\right|\leqslant\sum_{j\leqslant J}\left|\lambda_{j}\langle A_{K}\psi_{j},\psi_{j}\rangle\right|+\varepsilon\xrightarrow{K\to\infty}\varepsilon

which shows
\operatorname{tr}(\rho A_{K})\xrightarrow{K\to\infty}0
as desired.
∎

By Lemma
A.2
, we see that for fixed
h
,

\lim_{K\to\infty}\operatorname{tr}\left(\rho_{h}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\Op_{h}(\chi_{k}a)\right)=\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(a))

and similarly as
\mu
is a finite measure

\lim_{K\to\infty}\int_{T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\chi_{k}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu.

Then letting
\varepsilon>0
and picking the
h_{0}(\varepsilon)
from part
(2)
we get

\left|\operatorname{tr}\left(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(a)\right)-\int_{T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right|=\lim_{K\to\infty}\left|\operatorname{tr}\left(\rho_{h}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\Op_{h}(\chi_{k}a)\right)-\int_{T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{k=0}^{K}\chi_{k}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right|\leqslant\varepsilon

To show
(1)\implies(3)
, let
a\in S
, and let
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})
be supported in a ball of radius
R
, such that
\mu(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{R}(0))\leqslant\varepsilon/8
. We also note that
\chi
may be chosen so its first
K(d)
derivatives are small (possibly making
R
larger). Then we have that

\operatorname{tr}(\Op_{h}(a)\Op_{h}(1-\chi)\rho_{h})\leqslant\|\Op_{h}(a)\|\operatorname{tr}(\Op_{h}(1-\chi)\rho_{h})\leqslant C\operatorname{tr}(\Op_{h}(1-\chi)\rho_{h})

for
C
depending on the first
K(d)
derivatives of
a
. Next we apply (
A.3
) with the symbol
1-\chi
(which is independent of
a
) to get that

\displaystyle C\operatorname{tr}(\Op_{h}(1-\chi)\rho_{h})=C\varepsilon/8+o(1)

as
h\to 0
. So that there exists
h_{0}>0
such that for
0<h<h_{0}
we have
C\operatorname{tr}(\Op_{h}(1-\chi)\rho_{h})<\varepsilon/4
. With this, the triangle inequality, and symbol calculus, we have for

(A.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\operatorname{tr}\left(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(a)\right)-\int_{T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}}a\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right|&\leqslant\left|\operatorname{tr}\left(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(a)\Op_{h}(\chi)\right)-\int_{T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}}a\chi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right|+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\\

where for the last inequality we have chosen possibly smaller
h_{0}(\varepsilon)

We now prove
(3)

. Given

be the set of

\left|\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(b\chi))-\int_{B_{R}(0)}b\chi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right|<\frac{\varepsilon}{4}

for all

. Then for

) makes

to be an open set in

. Let

\overline{Y}

To show
(3)\implies(4)
, we simply see that

\int_{T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=\lim_{h\to 0}\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(1))=\lim_{h\to 0}\operatorname{tr}\rho_{h}=1.

Here we use that the function
f(x)=1
is in the symbol class
S
.

Finally, we show
(4)\implies(3)
. Let
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})
, so

\operatorname{tr}\left(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(1-\chi)\right)-\int_{T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}}1-\chi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu=-\left(\operatorname{tr}\left(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(\chi)\right)-\int_{T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\chi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu\right)\to 0

as
h\to 0
, so in particular (
A.3
) holds in the special case of the noncompact symbol
1-\chi
. But that was the only symbol used in the proof of
(1)\implies(3)
, so in particular shows
(4)\implies(3)
as well. This shows all equivalences and hence completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Coherent States

In this appendix, we prove Propositions
2.9
,
2.11
, and
2.12
, which give examples of
(\delta,\theta)
-defect measures.

B.1.
Coherent States on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

We prove Proposition
2.9
. The proof is essentially the same as
[
Zwo12
, §5.1 Examples 1 and 2]
, but is quantitative and keeps track of errors.

Proof of Proposition
2.9
.

We first consider the case when
\beta=0
. In this case, we can without loss of generality assume
x_{0}=0
, by absorbing it into the definition of
g
. Then

\psi_{h}(x)=g(x)e^{\frac{i}{h}\langle x,\xi_{0}\rangle},

so for
a\in S_{\delta}\cap C_{0}^{\infty}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(a))

\displaystyle=\langle\Op_{h}(a)\psi_{h},\psi_{h}\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\frac{1}{(2\pi h)^{d}}\left(\iint e^{\frac{i}{h}\langle x-y,\xi-\xi_{0}\rangle}g(y)\,a\left(\frac{x+y}{2},\xi\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\right)\overline{g(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int\frac{1}{(2\pi h)^{d}}\left(\iint e^{\frac{i}{h}\langle y,\xi\rangle}g(x-y)\,a\left(x-\frac{y}{2},\xi+\xi_{0}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\right)\overline{g(x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x.

We evaluate the inner double integral using the explicit form of stationary phase given in
[
Zwo12
, Theorem 3.17]
.

\begin{gathered}\iint e^{\frac{i}{h}\langle y,\xi\rangle}g(x-y)\,a\left(x-\frac{y}{2},\xi+\xi_{0}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\\
\sim(2\pi h)^{d}\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{h^{k}}{i^{k}k!}\langle D_{y},D_{\xi}\rangle^{k}\left.g(x-\cdot_{y})a\left(x-\frac{\cdot_{y}}{2},\cdot_{\xi}+\xi_{0}\right)\right|_{y=0,\xi=0}\right)\\
=(2\pi h)^{d}\left(g(x)a(x,\xi_{0})+O(h^{1-2\delta})\right)\end{gathered}

with
\sim
referring to asymptotic summation and the last equality coming from
a
being an element of
S_{\delta}
. Then

\left|\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(a))-\int|g(x)|^{2}a(x,\xi_{0})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right|\leqslant O(h^{1-2\delta})\int|g(x)|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=O(h^{1-2\delta})

which gives the result for
\beta=0
. The case of
\beta=1
follows from taking the semiclassical Fourier transform.

We now treat the case when
0<\beta<1
. Here we have

\psi_{h}(x)=h^{-\frac{d\beta}{2}}g\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{h^{\beta}}\right)e^{\frac{i}{h}\langle x,\xi_{0}\rangle}.

We again have that
\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(a))=\langle\Op_{h}(a)\psi_{h},\psi_{h}\rangle
. We now approximate
\Op_{h}(a)
by its left quantization defined as:

\Op_{h}^{L}(a)u(x):=\frac{1}{(2\pi h)^{d}}\iint e^{\frac{i}{h}\langle x-y,\xi\rangle}a(x,\xi)u(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi.

By
[
Zwo12
, Theorem 4.13]
,
\Op_{h}(a)=\Op_{h}^{L}(a)+O(h^{1-2\delta})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
. Therefore

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(a))=\langle\Op_{h}^{L}(a)\psi_{h},\psi_{h}\rangle+O(h^{1-2\delta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi h)^{d}}\int\overline{g\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{h^{\beta}}\right)}\left(\iint h^{-d\beta}g\left(\frac{y-x_{0}}{h^{\beta}}\right)e^{\frac{i}{h}\langle x-y,\xi-\xi_{0}\rangle}a(x,\xi)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+O(h^{1-2\delta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi h)^{d}}\int\overline{g\left(\frac{x-x_{0}}{h^{\beta}}\right)}\int e^{\frac{i}{h}\langle x-x_{0},\xi\rangle}a(x,\xi+\xi_{0})\left(\int h^{-d\beta}g\left(\frac{y}{h^{\beta}}\right)e^{-\frac{i}{h}\langle y,\xi\rangle}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\quad+O(h^{1-2\delta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi h)^{d}}\iint\overline{g\left(\frac{x}{h^{\beta}}\right)}e^{\frac{i}{h}\langle x,\xi\rangle}a(x+x_{0},\xi+\xi_{0})\hat{g}\left(\frac{\xi}{h^{1-\beta}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+O(h^{1-2\delta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\iint\overline{g(x)}\hat{g}(\xi)e^{i\langle x,\xi\rangle}a\left(h^{\beta}x+x_{0},h^{1-\beta}\xi+\xi_{0}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+O(h^{1-2\delta}).

Because
\|g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=1
, we have by the Fourier inversion formula that:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\iint\overline{g(x)}\hat{g}(\xi)e^{i\langle x,\xi\rangle}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=1

so that

\displaystyle|\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(a))-a(x_{0},\xi_{0})|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\iint\overline{g(x)}\hat{g}(\xi)e^{i\langle x,\xi\rangle}(a\left(h^{\beta}x+x_{0},h^{1-\beta}\xi+\xi_{0}\right)-a(x_{0},\xi_{0}))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right|+O(h^{1-2\delta}).

By the mean value theorem and the fact that
a\in S_{\delta}\cap C_{0}^{\infty}(T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we have for
x,\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle|a\left(h^{\beta}x+x_{0},h^{1-\beta}\xi+\xi_{0}\right)-a(x_{0},\xi_{0})|

\displaystyle\leqslant(h^{\beta}|x|+h^{1-\beta}|\xi|)\|\nabla a\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C(h^{\beta}|x|+h^{1-\beta}|\xi|)h^{-\delta}.

So, using that
g
is Schwartz,

\displaystyle|\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{h}\Op_{h}(a))-a(x_{0},\xi_{0})|

\displaystyle\leqslant C\iint|g(x)||\hat{g}(\xi)|x||\xi|((h^{\beta}|x|+h^{1-\beta}|\xi|)h^{-\delta})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=O(h^{\beta-\delta})+O(h^{1-\beta-\delta})+O(h^{1-2\delta}).

Noting that
1-2\delta=\beta-\delta+1-\beta-\delta
shows the
O(h^{1-2\delta})
term to be superfluous and gives the desired result.
∎

B.2.
Coherent States on the Quantized Torus

We provide full proofs of Propositions
2.11
and
2.12
, though we do not claim our parameters to be optimal.

Proof of Proposition
2.11
.

For this proof, we work in the orthonormal basis
(Q_{n})
. Then for
a\in S_{\delta}(\mathbb{T}^{2d})
,
\Op_{N}(a)
has a representation as a matrix
A
with

(B.1)

A_{mj}=\sum_{l,r\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{a}(l,j-m-rN)(-1)^{\langle r,l\rangle}e^{\pi i\frac{\langle j+m,l\rangle}{N}}.

Let
M=\lfloor N^{\gamma}\rfloor
for
\gamma\in(\delta,1)
. We see that if
|j-m|>M
(in distance
\operatorname{mod}N
), then for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{>d}

(B.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}|A_{mj}|&\leqslant\sum_{l,r\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|\hat{a}(l,j-m-rN)|\\
&\leqslant C\sum_{l,r\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\langle l\rangle^{-(d+1)}\|(\partial_{x}^{d+1}+1)\partial_{\xi}^{k}a\|_{L^{\infty}}|j-m-rN|^{-k}\\
&\leqslant CN^{(d+1+k)\delta}\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|j-m-rN|^{-k}.\end{split}

Note that there exists a large constant
M_{0}>0
(independent of
N
) such that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
|r|>M_{0}\end{subarray}}|j-m-rN|^{-k}\leqslant 1.

Therefore:

\displaystyle\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}|j-m-rN|^{-k}\leqslant M_{0}M^{-k}+1\leqslant CN^{-\gamma k}

for sufficiently large
N
. Therefore

\displaystyle\begin{split}|A_{mj}|&\leqslant CN^{(d+1)\delta}N^{k\delta}N^{-k\gamma}=CN^{-k(\gamma-\delta)+(d+1)\delta}\end{split}

where
C=C(a,\delta,k)
is independent of
N
. Taking
k
arbitrarily large gives that
|A_{mj}|=O(N^{-\infty})
, so (as with pseudodifferential operators on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
), the mass of the kernel is concentrated on the diagonal and falls off super-polynomially away from it. We also note that even when
j
and
m
are close, we may remove one of the sums in (
B.1
) up to an
O(N^{-\infty})
error. Specifically we see that when
|j-m|\leqslant M
,
k\in\mathbb{N}

\left|A_{mj}-\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{a}(l,j-m)e^{\pi i\frac{\langle j+m,l\rangle}{N}}\right|=O(N^{-\infty}).

Indeed

\displaystyle\left|A_{mj}-\sum_{l\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\hat{a}(l,j-m)e^{\pi i\frac{\langle j+m,l\rangle}{N}}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,r\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
r\neq 0\end{subarray}}\hat{a}(l,j-m-rN)(-1)^{\langle r,l\rangle}e^{\pi i\frac{\langle j+m,l\rangle}{N}}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,r\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
r\neq 0\end{subarray}}|\hat{a}(l,j-m-rN)|.

Note that there exists
c_{d}>0
such that for all
r\neq 0
,

\displaystyle|j-m-rN|\geqslant c_{d}N^{\gamma}|r|

therefore for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{>d}
, using that
a\in S_{\delta}(\mathbb{T}^{2d})
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,r\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
r\neq 0\end{subarray}}|\hat{a}(l,j-m-rN)|

\displaystyle\leqslant C\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l,r\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\\
r\neq 0\end{subarray}}\langle l\rangle^{-(d+1)}|j-m-rN|^{-k}\|(\partial_{x}^{d+1}+1)\partial_{\xi}^{k}a\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leqslant CN^{\delta(d+1+k)}\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{\neq 0}}c_{d}N^{-k\gamma}\langle r\rangle^{-k}

by the same reasoning as in (
B.2
). By Fourier inversion in the first variable, we have in that case

(B.3)

A_{mj}=\mathscr{F}_{2}a\left(\frac{j+m}{2N},j-m\right)+O(N^{-\infty})

where
\mathscr{F}_{2}
is the Fourier coefficient in the second component (so
\mathscr{F}_{2}a
acts on
\mathbb{T}^{d}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}
).

Then we compute

(B.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\operatorname{tr}_{H_{N}}(\rho_{N}\Op_{N}(a))=C_{N}\left\langle\psi(N)\right\rvert\Op_{N}(a)\left\lvert\psi(N)\right\rangle\\
&=C_{N}\sum_{m,j\in(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{d}}\frac{1}{N^{d}}\overline{g(m/N)}A_{mj}g(j/N)e^{2\pi i(j-m)\xi_{0}}\\
&=C_{N}\sum_{|m-j|\leqslant M}\frac{1}{N^{d}}\overline{g(m/N)}g(j/N)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}a\left(\frac{j+m}{2N},\xi\right)e^{2\pi i(m-j)(\xi-\xi_{0})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi+O(N^{-\infty})\\
&=C_{N}\sum_{|m-j|\leqslant M}\frac{1}{N^{d}}\overline{g(m/N)}g(j/N)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}a\left(\frac{j+m}{2N},\xi+\xi_{0}\right)e^{2\pi i(m-j)\xi}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi+O(N^{-\infty})\end{split}

But when
|j-m|\leqslant M
, we have

\left|a\left(\frac{j+m}{2N},\xi+\xi_{0}\right)-a\left(\frac{m}{N},\xi+\xi_{0}\right)\right|\leqslant MN^{-1}\|\partial a\|_{L^{\infty}}\leqslant CMN^{-(1-\delta)}

and

\left|g\left(\frac{m}{N}\right)-g\left(\frac{j}{N}\right)\right|\leqslant CMN^{-1},

so

(B.5)

\begin{gathered}\overline{g(m/N)}g(j/N)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}a\left(\frac{j+m}{2N},\xi+\xi_{0}\right)e^{2\pi i(m-j)\xi}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi\\
=|g(m/N)|^{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}a\left(\frac{m}{N},\xi+\xi_{0}\right)e^{2\pi i(m-j)\xi}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi+O(MN^{-(1-\delta)})\end{gathered}

As there are
O(N^{d}M^{d})
pairs
(j,m)
with
|m-j|\leqslant M
, the errors (
B.5
) sum in (
B.4
) to give

(B.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\operatorname{tr}_{H_{N}}(\rho_{N}\Op_{N}(a))\\
&=\sum_{m\in(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{d}}\frac{1}{N^{d}}|g(m/N)|^{2}\sum_{j:|m-j|\leqslant M}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}a\left(\frac{m}{N},\xi+\xi_{0}\right)e^{2\pi i(m-j)\xi}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\xi+O(M^{d+1}N^{-(1-\delta)})\end{split}

where we have used Remark
1
to remove the
C_{N}
.
The inner sum in (
B.6
) is just a partial Fourier series, and as
\gamma>\delta
, it differs from the full Fourier series by order
O(N^{-\infty})
. This gives

(B.7)

\operatorname{tr}_{H_{N}}(\rho_{N}\Op_{N}(a))=\sum_{m\in(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{d}}\frac{1}{N^{d}}|g(m/N)|^{2}a\left(\frac{m}{N},\xi_{0}\right)+O(M^{d+1}N^{-(1-\delta)}).

But (
B.7
) is just a Riemann sum, and we have the estimate

\left|\sum_{m\in(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})^{d}}\frac{1}{N^{d}}|g(m/N)|^{2}a\left(\frac{m}{N},\xi_{0}\right)-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}a(x,\xi_{0})|g(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right|=O(N^{-(1-\delta)}).

Therefore

\left|\operatorname{tr}_{H_{N}}(\rho_{N}\Op_{N}(a))-\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}a(x,\xi_{0})|g(x)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right|\leqslant O(M^{d+1}N^{-(1-\delta)})=(N^{(d+1)\gamma-(1-\delta)})

for all
\gamma>\delta
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.12
.

Let
a\in S_{\delta}(\mathbb{T}^{2d})

A=\Op_{N}(a)

(Q_{n})

M=\lfloor N^{\gamma}\rfloor
for
\gamma\in(\delta,1)

But as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\psi_{m}&=N^{-\frac{d}{2}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}g_{N}\left(\frac{m}{N}+k\right)\\

by throwing away all but the first term in the sum.
In fact, for many

\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\quad+O(M^{d+1}N^{(d+1)(\beta-1)}R_{\varepsilon}^{d})

B.7

O(N^{-\infty})

to ignore the
C_{N}^{\prime}

O(N^{-\infty})

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{N}\Op_{N}(a))

\displaystyle=\int N^{d\beta}|g(N^{\beta}(x-x_{0}))|^{2}a(x,\xi_{0})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x+O(M^{d+1}N^{\beta-1}N^{d\varepsilon})

with the last line using that

. We substitute and use

to obtain

\begin{gathered}\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}N^{d\beta}|g(N^{\beta}(x-x_{0}))|^{2}a(x,\xi_{0})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x-a(x_{0},\xi_{0})\right|\\
\leqslant\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|g(y)|^{2}\left(a(N^{-\beta}y+x_{0},\xi_{0})-a(x_{0},\xi_{0})\right)dy\right|+\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus N^{\beta}\mathbb{T}^{d}}|g(y)|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right|\\
\leqslant C\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|g(y)|^{2}N^{-\beta}N^{\delta}|y|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\right|+O(N^{-\infty})\leqslant O(N^{\delta-\beta}).\end{gathered}

This gives

|\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{N}\Op_{N}(a))-a(x_{0},\xi_{0})|\leqslant O(N^{\delta-\beta})+O(M^{d+1}N^{\beta-1}N^{d\varepsilon})

which completes the proof by taking
\varepsilon
and
\gamma-\delta
small enough.
∎

Appendix C
Estimating Derivatives of a Flow

Here we present a proof of Proposition
2.15
which we restate here for convenience.

Proposition C.1
.

Suppose
\phi^{t}:\mathbb{T}^{2d}\to\mathbb{T}^{2d}
is a smooth dynamical system on
\mathbb{T}^{2d}
with Lyapunov exponent
\Lambda
where
t
either takes integer values, or values in
\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
. Let
a\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2d})
. Then for all
\varepsilon>0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{2d}
, there exists
C=C(\varepsilon,\alpha)
such that:

\displaystyle|\partial^{\alpha}(a\circ\phi^{t})(x)|\leqslant Ce^{(\Lambda+\varepsilon)t|\alpha|}\|a\|_{C^{|\alpha|}}.

Here
C^{k}
is the norm defined as

\displaystyle\|a\|_{C^{K}}=\sum_{|\alpha|\leqslant k}\|\partial^{\alpha}a\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{2d})}.

This proof of Proposition
C.1
essentially follows
[
DJN22
, §5.2]
but in the simpler setting of the torus. To our knowledge, such a proof in this setting is not present in the current literature. In an effort to improve readability, we include examples throughout the proof, at the cost of being rather voluminous.

Proof.

We prove Proposition
C.1
by strong induction on the order of
\alpha
. We trivially have the claim when
|\alpha|=0
. If
|\alpha|=1
, let
i
be such that
\partial^{\alpha}=\partial_{i}
. Then we have:

\displaystyle\partial^{\alpha}_{x}(a\circ\phi^{t})(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{2d}(\partial_{j}a)\circ(\phi^{t}(x))\partial_{i}(\phi_{t}(x))_{j}

where
(\phi^{t}(x))_{j}
is the
j
th component of
\phi^{t}(x)
. By the definition of the Lyapunov exponent, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
C=C(\varepsilon)>0
such that:

\displaystyle|\partial_{i}\phi^{t}(x)|\leqslant Ce^{(\Lambda+\varepsilon)t},

so that

\displaystyle|\partial^{\alpha}_{x}(a\circ\phi^{t})(x)|\leqslant Ce^{(\Lambda+\varepsilon)t}\|a\|_{C^{1}}.

To ease notation in the remained of the proof, define
\lambda:=\Lambda+\varepsilon
.

Before proving the inductive step, we show how to go from
\alpha
of order
1
to order
2
. We will use the following notation for Jacobians and tensors. If
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, then
Df
will be the vector with
i
th component
\partial_{i}f
. Note that
Df
will be a
(0,1)
tensor. We define
D^{2}f
as the
(0,2)
tensor with
i,j
entry
(D^{2}f)_{i,j}=\partial_{i}\partial_{j}f
. If
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\to\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, then we let
D\phi
be the
(1,1)
tensor with
i,j
entry
(D\phi)_{j}^{i}=\partial_{j}\phi_{i}
. We let
D^{2}\phi
denote the
(1,2)
tensor with
i,j,k
entry
(D^{2}\phi)_{j,k}^{i}=(\partial_{j}\partial_{k}\phi_{i})
.

Observe that:

\displaystyle(D(a\circ\phi^{n})(x))_{i}=\partial_{i}(a\circ\phi^{n})(x)=\sum_{j}\partial_{j}((a\circ\phi^{n-1})\circ\phi^{1}(x))(\partial_{i}\phi_{j}^{1})(x)

so that

\displaystyle D(a\circ\phi^{n})(x)=(D(a\circ\phi^{n-1})\circ\phi^{1}(x))D\phi^{1}(x)

where the product of two tensors is the contraction of the upper and lower components. That is,
(D(a\circ\phi^{n-1})\circ\phi^{1})
is a
(0,1)
tensor which can be written
A_{\mu}
, and
D\phi^{1}
is a
(1,1)
tensor which can be written
B_{\gamma}^{\nu}
. In this case
AB
is the
(0,1)
tensor contracting the lower component of
A
with the upper component of
B
:
(AB)_{\gamma}=\sum_{\nu}A_{\nu}B^{\nu}_{\gamma}
.

Similarly

\displaystyle(D^{2}(a\circ\phi^{n})(x))_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\partial_{i}\partial_{j}(a\circ\phi^{n})(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,\ell}((\partial_{k}\partial_{\ell}(a\circ\phi^{n-1}))\circ\phi^{1}(x))\partial_{j}\phi^{1}_{\ell}(x)\partial_{i}\phi^{1}_{k}(x)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{k}(\partial_{k}(a\circ\phi^{n-1})\circ\phi^{1}(x))\partial_{j}\partial_{i}\phi^{1}_{k}(x)

so that

\displaystyle D^{2}(a\circ\phi^{n})(x)

\displaystyle\quad=(D^{2}(a\circ\phi^{n-1})\circ\phi^{1}(x))(D\phi^{1}(x)\otimes D\phi^{1}(x))+(D(a\circ\phi^{n-1})\circ\phi^{1}(x))D^{2}\phi^{1}(x)

Therefore

\displaystyle\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}D(a\circ\phi^{n+1})(x)\\
D^{2}(a\circ\phi^{n+1})(x)\end{pmatrix}^{t}}_{:=A_{n+1}(x)^{t}}=\begin{pmatrix}D(a\circ\phi^{n})\circ\phi^{1}(x)\\
D^{2}(a\circ\phi^{n})\circ\phi^{1}(x)\end{pmatrix}^{t}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}D\phi^{1}(x)&D^{2}\phi^{1}(x)\\
0&D\phi^{1}(x)\otimes D\phi^{1}(x)\end{pmatrix}}_{:=M(x)},

so that

(C.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}A_{n}(x)&=A_{n-1}(\phi^{1}(x))M(x)=A_{n-2}(\phi^{2}(x))M(\phi^{1}(x))M(x))\\
&=A_{0}(\phi^{n}(x))M(\phi^{n-1}(x))M(\phi^{n-2}(x))\cdots M(x).\end{split}

By the upper triangular structure of
M
, it is easy to check that

(C.2)

\displaystyle M(\phi^{n-1}(x))M(\phi^{n-2}(x))\cdots M(x)=\begin{pmatrix}M_{1}(x)&M_{2}(x)\\
0&M_{3}(x)\end{pmatrix}

where:

\displaystyle M_{1}(x)

\displaystyle:=\prod_{j=1}^{n}((D\phi^{1})\circ(\phi^{n-j}(x))),

\displaystyle M_{2}(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(M_{2})_{j}(x),

\displaystyle M_{3}(x)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(((D\phi^{1})\circ\phi^{n-j}(x))\otimes((D\phi^{1})\circ\phi^{n-j}(x)))

with
(M_{2})_{j}(x)
defined as

(C.3)

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}((D\phi^{1})\circ(\phi^{n-i}(x)))\right)((D^{2}\phi^{1})\circ\phi^{n-j}(x))\left(\prod_{i=j+1}^{n}((D\phi^{1})\circ(\phi^{n-i}(x)))^{\otimes 2}\right)

where
a^{\otimes 2}:=a\otimes a
.

We then have, by (
C.1
) and (
C.2
),

(C.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}|D^{2}(a\circ\phi^{n})(x)|&=|(Da\circ\phi^{n}(x))M_{2}(x)+(D^{2}a\circ\phi^{n}(x))M_{3}(x)|\\
&\leqslant C\|a\|_{C^{1}}|M_{2}(x)|+C\|a\|_{C^{2}}|M_{3}(x)|\end{split}

where the absolute value of a tensor denotes the maximum of the absolute value of all entries.

But now observe that

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}((D\phi^{1})\circ(\phi^{n-i}(x))

\displaystyle=D(\phi^{j-1})\circ\phi^{n-j+1}(x),

\displaystyle\prod_{i=j+1}^{n}((D\phi^{1})\circ(\phi^{n-i}(x)))^{\otimes 2}

\displaystyle=(D(\phi^{n-j})(x))^{\otimes 2}

so that

(C.5)

\displaystyle|M_{3}(x)|

\displaystyle=|D(\phi^{n})(x)|^{2}\leqslant Ce^{2\lambda n}

by the inductive hypothesis and setting
a
to be coordinate functions. And we also have, by (
C.3
), that

\displaystyle|(M_{2})_{j}(x)|

\displaystyle\leqslant Ce^{\lambda(j-1)}e^{2\lambda(n-j)}\|D^{2}\phi^{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}=C^{\prime}e^{2\lambda n-\lambda j-\lambda}

where we use that
\phi^{1}
is a smooth function on a compact set so that
\|D^{2}\phi^{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}<\infty
. But we stress that this norm only comes up
once
in each
(M_{2})_{j}
term so that the constant at the end is
independent
of
n
. We therefore have that:

(C.6)

\displaystyle|M_{2}(x)|\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{n}|(M_{2})_{j}(x)|\leqslant Ce^{2\lambda n-\lambda}\sum_{j=1}^{n}e^{-\lambda j}\leqslant Ce^{2\lambda n}(1-e^{-\lambda})^{-1}

We can absorb the
(1-e^{-\lambda})^{-1}
term into the constant
C
to get, using (
C.4
), (
C.5
), and (
C.6
), that

\displaystyle|D^{2}(a\circ\phi^{n})(x)|\leqslant C\|a\|_{C^{2}}e^{2\lambda n}.

Now if the flow is continuous in time and
t\in\mathbb{R}_{\geqslant 0}
, we let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be such that
n\leqslant t\leqslant n+1
and observe that:

\displaystyle|D^{2}(a\circ\phi^{t})(x)|=|D^{2}(a\circ\phi^{t-n}\circ\phi^{n})(x)|\leqslant C\|a\circ\phi^{t-n}\|_{C^{2}}e^{2\lambda n}.

Note that
s:=t-n\in[0,1]
which is a compact set, so that:

(C.7)

\displaystyle\|a\circ\phi^{t-n}\|_{C^{2}}\leqslant\|a\|_{C^{2}}\max_{s\in[0,1]}\|\phi^{s}\|_{C^{2}}.

We can then absorb
\max_{s\in[0,1]}\|\phi^{s}\|_{C^{2}}
into the constant
C
to get the result for continuous time.

Now we present the general inductive step, but the reader should keep in mind that morally the idea is the same as above, but with more notation. Suppose we have for all
k^{\prime}\leqslant k-1
and
|\alpha|=k^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle|\partial^{\alpha}(a\circ\phi^{t})(x)|\leqslant C\|a\|_{C^{k^{\prime}}}e^{k^{\prime}\lambda t}.

We then fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and aim to bound
D^{k}(a\circ\phi^{n})(x)
. Define
A_{n}(x)
as the
1\times k
vector of tensors whose
i
th entry is
D^{i}(a\circ\phi^{n})(x)
. By writing

(C.8)

\displaystyle D^{i}(a\circ\phi^{n})=D^{i-1}((D(a\circ\phi^{n-1})\circ\phi^{1})D\phi^{1})(x),

and repeatedly applying the product rule, we can find a
k\times k
matrix of tensors,
M(x)
such that
A_{n}(x)=A_{n-1}(\phi^{1}(x))M(x)
. Note that
M(x)
will be upper triangular. Indeed, for any
i\leqslant k
,
D^{i}(a\circ\phi^{n})
is a linear combination of
D^{j}(a\circ\phi^{n-1})\circ\phi^{1}
, for
j\leqslant i
. Also, for each
i,j=1,\dots,k
, the
i,j
entry of
M(x)
must be a
(i,j)
tensor because it will be paired with a
(0,i)
tensor to get a
(0,j)
tensor. Each component of
(M(x))_{i,j}
will contain
j
derivatives of the components of
\phi^{1}(x)
with a coefficient depending on
k
. Lastly note that for each
i=1,\dots,k
,
(M(x))_{i,i}=(D\phi^{1}(x))^{\otimes i}
as this is the term in (
C.8
) when all derivatives fall on
D(a\circ\phi^{n-1}\circ\phi^{1})
.

We then recursively use the relation of
A_{n}
to
A_{n-1}
to get that:

\displaystyle A_{n}(x)=A_{0}(\phi^{n}(x))\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}M(\phi^{n-j}(x))\right).

For
i=1,\dots,k
, let
M_{i}
be the
(i,k)
entry of
\prod_{j=1}^{n}M(\phi^{n-j})
, so that:

(C.9)

\displaystyle D^{k}(a\circ\phi^{n})(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}(D^{j}(a)\circ\phi^{n}(x))M_{j}(x).

It now suffices to provide appropriate estimates of
M_{j}(x)
. For
i=1,\dots,n
let
B^{i}:=M(\phi^{n-i})(x)
. Then for each fixed
j=1,\dots,k
:

(C.10)

\displaystyle M_{j}=\sum_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\dots,\ell_{n-1}}B^{1}_{j,\ell_{1}}B^{2}_{\ell_{1},\ell_{2}}\cdots B^{n}_{\ell_{n-1},k}.

Because
B^{i}
are upper triangular, this sum is nonzero only if
\ell_{m}\leqslant\ell_{m+1}
for each
m=1,\dots,n-1
, and
j\leqslant\ell_{1}
. Each term in the sum can be expressed as a path on a grid of
k\times(n+1)
nodes. Labeling the bottom left node
(1,1)
, every path must begin at
(j,1)
and end at
(k,n+1)
. The path can only go to the right or up. That is
(m,n)
can only go to
(m^{\prime},n+1)
for
m^{\prime}\geqslant m
(provided that the path terminates at
(k,n+1)
). The edges of the path give the index of
B^{i}
in the sum. That is, if the nodes
(m,i),(n,i+1)
are on the path, then we include the term
B^{i}_{m,n}
in the sum.

For example, if
k=4
and
n=5
, then the following path represents the term
B^{1}_{1,2}B^{2}_{2,2}B_{2,2}^{3}B^{4}_{2,4}B^{5}_{4,4}
.

Each path can be represented as a vector whose
i
th component is
m
where
(m,i)
is on the path, e.g. the example path is written as
(1,2,2,2,4,4)
.

Each path will consist of flat sections (where the
i
and
i-1
components of the vector representation are the same) and jumps (where the component of the vector representation is not flat). In the above example, the jumps are at indices
2
and
5
. The corresponding entry of
B^{i}
for the jumps will be individually norm-bounded. Recall that
B^{i}_{j,\ell}
will be a
(j,\ell)
tensor where all terms are derivatives of
\phi^{1}
. As long as
(j,\ell)\neq(1,k)
, at most
k-1
derivatives are taken of
\phi^{1}
. Note that
B_{1,k}
can only appear once in any term in the sum (
C.10
), and this is bounded by the
C^{k}
norm of
\phi^{1}
. We therefore can apply the inductive hypothesis to get that
|B^{i}_{j,\ell}(x)|\leqslant Ce^{\lambda\ell}
. Next, we bound the flat sequences. Suppose such a sequence is
\prod_{i=j_{1}}^{j_{2}}B_{\ell,\ell}^{i}
. Recall that
B_{\ell,\ell}^{i}=(D\phi^{1})^{\otimes\ell}\circ(\phi^{n-i}(x))
, so that

(C.11)

\displaystyle\left|\prod_{i=j_{1}}^{j_{2}}B_{\ell,\ell}^{i}(x)\right|=|(D(\phi^{j_{2}-j_{1}+1})\circ\phi^{n-j_{2}+1}(x))^{\otimes\ell}|\leqslant Ce^{\ell(j_{2}-j_{1}+1)}.

This follows by the inductive hypothesis by setting
a
to be coordinate functions. Now suppose we have a term of the sum (
C.10
) whose path has a vector representation
(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n+1})
with jumps at indices
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\dots,\ell_{s}
. We assume that there is a jump at index
2
and the path ends in a flat section (as in the example), but all cases can be treated similarly. We can then bound this term of the sum (using (
C.11
)) by:

\displaystyle|B_{a_{\ell_{1}-1},a_{\ell_{1}}}^{\ell_{1}}|\cdots|B_{a_{\ell_{s}-1},a_{\ell_{s}}}^{\ell_{s}}|\left|\prod_{i=\ell_{1}+1}^{\ell_{2}-1}B^{i}_{a_{\ell_{1}},a_{\ell_{1}}}\right|\cdots\left|\prod_{i=\ell_{s}+1}^{n+1}B^{i}_{a_{\ell_{s}},a_{\ell_{s}}}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant Ce^{\lambda(a_{\ell_{1}}+\cdots+a_{\ell_{s}})}e^{\lambda a_{\ell_{1}}(\ell_{2}-\ell_{1}-1)}\cdots e^{\lambda a_{\ell_{s}}(n+1-\ell_{s}-1)}

\displaystyle=Ce^{\lambda(a_{\ell_{1}}(\ell_{2}-\ell_{1})+\cdots+a_{\ell_{s-1}}(\ell_{s}-\ell_{s-1})+a_{\ell_{s}}(n+1-\ell_{s}))}

\displaystyle=Ce^{\lambda(a_{2}+\cdots+a_{n+1})}.

The last equality follows by noting that
\ell_{i+1}-\ell_{i}
is the length of each flat section and is where the sequence has values
a_{\ell_{i}}
. Therefore:

(C.12)

\displaystyle|M_{j}(x)|\leqslant C\left|\sum_{(a_{n})\in\mathcal{A}_{j,k}}e^{\lambda(a_{2}+\dots+a_{n+1})}\right|

where
\mathcal{A}_{j,k}
is the set of all sequences
(a_{n})
of length
n+1
such that
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
a_{1}=j
,
a_{n+1}=k
, and
a_{i}\leqslant a_{i+1}
. Let
V
be the
k\times k
upper triangular matrix such that
V_{\ell,m}=e^{m\lambda}
for
\ell\leqslant m
and zero otherwise. Then by the exact same argument for computing products of upper triangular matrices, we see that

(C.13)

\displaystyle(V^{n})_{j,k}=\sum_{(a_{n})\in\mathcal{A}_{j,k}}e^{\lambda(a_{2}+\dots+a_{n+1})}.

Because the eigenvalues of
V
are distinct,
V
is diagonalizable. We can then write the entries of
V^{n}
as linear combinations (with coefficients independent of
n
) of the eigenvalues of
V
raised to the
n
th power. That is there exist
c_{j,\ell,k}\in\mathbb{R}
independent of
n
such that

\displaystyle(V^{n})_{j,k}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}e^{\lambda\ell n}c_{j,\ell,k}.

But then we immediately see that there exists a
C=C(k)>0
such that
|(V^{n})_{j,k}|\leqslant Ce^{\lambda kn}
for
j=1,\dots,k
. We therefore have that
|M_{j}(x)|\leqslant Ce^{n\lambda k}
, so that (
C.9
) becomes:

\displaystyle|D^{k}(a\circ\phi^{n})(x)|\leqslant C\|a\|_{C^{k}}e^{n\lambda k}.

Applying the exact same argument at the end of the case
k=2
to go from discrete time to continuous time completes the proof.

∎
