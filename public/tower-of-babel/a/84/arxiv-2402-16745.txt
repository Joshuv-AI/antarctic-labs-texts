Abstract
. We study the gradient flow of an energy with mixed homogeneity which is at the interface of Finsler and sub-Riemannian geometry.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
35H20, 35B09, 35R03, 53C17, 58J60

1.
Introduction

Singular spaces occupy a prominent position in analysis and geometry. Examples of basic interest are Alexandrov spaces and Finsler manifolds. These latter ambients have received considerable attention over the past few decades as they often occur as scaling limits of crystalline or Riemannian structures, see
[
21
]
and especially the seminal work
[
3
]
. In this paper we introduce a nonlinear evolution equation which represents the gradient flow of an energy which is at the interface of Finsler and sub-Riemannian geometry. Our main objective is to understand in detail the relevant heat kernel.
To keep the presentation self-contained we confine ourselves to the model setting of a product of Euclidean spaces, i.e.,
\mathbb{R}^{N}=\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
, with coordinates
z\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. We assume that on each of the two layers,
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, Minkowski norms
\Phi
and
\Psi
have been assigned. By this we mean that

\Phi^{2}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0\})
,
\Psi^{2}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{k}\setminus\{0\})
, and that these functions are strictly convex and
1
-homogeneous. We respectively denote by
\Phi^{0}
and
\Psi^{0}
their dual norms defined as in (
2.2
) below.
The levels sets of the functions
\Phi^{0}
and
\Psi^{0}
are often referred to as
Wulff shapes
. This situation represents a simplified, yet significant, model for the more general one of a Riemannian manifold in which the tangent space is stratified in layers, each endowed with a different anisotropic structure, and having mixed homogeneities weighted according to the relative position in the stratification.

We are interested in the
L^{2}
gradient flow

(1.1)

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial\mathscr{E}^{\Phi,\Psi}(f)}{\partial f}

of the following energy

(1.2)

\mathscr{E}^{\Phi,\Psi}(f)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\Phi(\nabla_{z}f)^{2}+\frac{\Phi^{0}(z)^{2}}{4}\Psi(\nabla_{\sigma}f)^{2}\right)dzd\sigma.

Notable features of (
1.2
) are:

(i)

The nonlinear dependence on the degenerate gradient

(1.3)

\nabla_{X}f=(X_{1}f,...,X_{m}f,X_{m+1}f,...,X_{N}f)

associated with the
N
vector fields

(1.4)

X_{i}=\partial_{z_{i}},\ i=1,...,m,\ \ \ X_{m+j}=\frac{\Phi^{0}(z)}{2}\ \partial_{\sigma_{j}},\ j=1,...,k.

(note from (
1.4
) that for
i=1,...,N
one has
X_{i}^{\star}=-X_{i}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. We also note that, under the given assumptions on
\Phi
we have
\Phi^{0}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{0\})
, see
[
20
, Corollary 1.7.3]
);

(ii)

It degenerates along the manifold
M=\{0\}_{z}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}
, but because of the anisotropic nature of the dual norm
\Phi^{0}(z)
, it does so at different scales along regions of approach to
M
;

(iii)

It is invariant with respect to the family of mixed dilations in
\mathbb{R}^{N}

(1.5)

\delta_{\lambda}(z,\sigma)=(\lambda z,\lambda^{2}\sigma),\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \lambda>0;

Returning to (
1.1
), a standard argument shows that the relevant evolution PDE attached to (
1.2
) is

(1.6)

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=\Delta_{\Phi}(f)+\frac{\Phi^{0}(z)^{2}}{4}\Delta_{\Psi}(f),

where we have respectively denoted by
\Delta_{\Phi}
and
\Delta_{\Psi}
the Finsler Laplacians in the spaces
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, see (
2.6
) below. Furthermore, if with
\delta_{\lambda}
as in (
1.5
) we define

(1.7)

\Delta_{\lambda}(z,\sigma,t)=(\delta_{\lambda}(z,\sigma),\lambda^{2}t),

then if
f
solves (
1.6
), so does also
f\circ\Delta_{\lambda}
, for every
\lambda>0
.

We emphasise that (
1.6
) is a nonlinear evolution equation with quadratic growth in the degenerate “gradient” (
1.3
). By this we mean that there exist constants
\gamma,\gamma^{\star}>0
such that

(1.8)

\gamma\ |\nabla_{X}f|^{2}\leq\langle\mathscr{A}(\nabla_{X}f),\nabla_{X}f\rangle\leq\gamma^{\star}\ |\nabla_{X}f|^{2}.

To explain (
1.8
), note that (
1.3
) and (
1.4
) give

(1.9)

\nabla_{X}f=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{z}f\\
\frac{\Phi^{0}(z)}{2}\nabla_{\sigma}f\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, thanks to the
1
-homogeneity of
\Phi
and
\Psi
, if we let

\mathscr{A}(\nabla_{X}f)=\begin{pmatrix}\Phi(\nabla_{z}f)\nabla\Phi(\nabla_{z}f)\\
\frac{\Phi^{0}(z)}{2}\Psi(\nabla_{\sigma}f)\nabla\Psi(\eta)\end{pmatrix},

then the PDE (
1.6
) can be alternatively written as

(1.10)

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}\mathscr{A}_{i}(\nabla_{X}f),

where the functions
\mathscr{A}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
are the components of the vector field

\mathscr{A}(\nabla_{X}f)=(\mathscr{A}_{1}(\nabla_{X}f),...,\mathscr{A}_{N}(\nabla_{X}f))^{T}.

We now note that

(1.11)

\displaystyle\langle\mathscr{A}(\nabla_{X}f),\nabla_{X}f\rangle

\displaystyle=\Phi(\nabla_{z}f)\langle\nabla\Phi(\nabla_{z}f),\nabla_{z}f\rangle+\frac{\Phi^{0}(z)^{2}}{4}\Psi(\nabla_{\sigma}f)\langle\nabla\Psi(\nabla_{\sigma}f),\nabla_{\sigma}f\rangle

\displaystyle=\Phi(\nabla_{z}f)^{2}+\frac{\Phi^{0}(z)^{2}}{4}\Psi(\nabla_{\sigma}f)^{2},

where in the last equality we have used the
1
-homogeneity of
\Phi
and
\Psi
, which is well-known to be equivalent to the Euler equations

\langle\xi,\nabla\Phi(\xi)\rangle=\Phi(\xi),\ \ \ \ \ \ \langle\eta,\nabla\Psi(\eta)\rangle=\Psi(\eta).

By the condition (
2.7
) below, from (
1.9
) and (
1.11
) we finally infer the existence of
\gamma,\gamma^{\star}>0
such that (
1.8
) hold. Although the present work is not concerned with the local theory of the PDE (
1.6
),
we remark that the growth (
1.8
) would allow to apply the results in
[
4
]
, once the relevant volume doubling condition and Poincaré inequality are available. For these aspects see the seminal works
[
13
]
,
[
19
]
, and also the more recent paper
[
5
]
that develops the local theory in Finsler manifolds with Ricci lower bounds.

To state our first result, we need to introduce some notation. Henceforth, points of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
will be denoted by
z,\zeta
, etc., points of
\mathbb{R}^{k}
by
\sigma,\tau,\lambda
, etc. We will use the notation
X=(z,\sigma),Y=(\zeta,\tau)
, etc. for points in the product space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Since the notation
\nabla_{X}
will no longer appear in this work, there will be no risk of confusion of this notation with the subscript in (
1.8
), (
1.9
) and (
1.11
) above. Also, we will respectively indicate with
\sigma_{\Phi}
and
\sigma_{\Psi}
the intrinsic measures of the Wulff spheres defined by

(1.12)

\sigma_{\Phi}=\int_{\{\Phi^{0}(z)=1\}}\frac{dH^{m-1}(z)}{|\nabla\Phi^{0}(z)|},\ \ \ \ \ \sigma_{\Psi}=\int_{\{\Psi^{0}(\sigma)=1\}}\frac{dH^{k-1}(\sigma)}{|\nabla\Psi^{0}(\sigma)|},

where
H^{m-1}
and
H^{k-1}
denote
(m-1)
-dimensional and
(k-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, respectively. Recall the classical formulas
\sigma_{m-1}=H^{m-1}(\mathbb{S}^{m-1})=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{m}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{m}{2})}
,
\sigma_{k-1}=H^{k-1}(\mathbb{S}^{k-1})=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{k}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{k}{2})}
. It is clear from (
1.12
) that, in the linear isotropic case
\Phi(z)=|z|
,
\Psi(\sigma)=|\sigma|
, one has
\sigma_{\Phi}=\sigma_{m-1}
,
\sigma_{\Psi}=\sigma_{k-1}
. For a number
\nu\in\mathbb{C}
, we will denote with
J_{\nu}
the Bessel function of the first kind and order
\nu
, and indicate by

(1.13)

G_{\nu}(z)=z^{-\nu}J_{\nu}(z).

We have the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every
X=(z,\sigma)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and
t>0
the function

(1.14)

\displaystyle\mathscr{G}(X,t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{m-1}\sigma_{k-1}}{\sigma_{\Phi}\sigma_{\Psi}}\frac{(2\pi)^{-\frac{k}{2}}(4\pi)^{-\frac{m}{2}}}{t^{\frac{m}{2}+k}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{u}{\sinh u}\right)^{\frac{m}{2}}e^{-\frac{u}{\tanh u}\frac{\Phi^{0}(z)^{2}}{4t}}G_{\frac{k}{2}-1}(\frac{u\Psi^{0}(\sigma)}{t})\ u^{k-1}\ du,

is a solution of the equation (
1.6
). Moreover, for every
t>0
we have

(1.15)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\mathscr{G}(X,t)dX=1.

Remark 1.2
.

We emphasise that Theorem
1.3
below proves that, in fact, the function (
1.14
) is a fundamental solution for (
1.6
).

To state our second result, we mention that in the recent paper
[
7
]
the authors have studied in the product space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
the following degenerate energy with mixed homogeneity

(1.16)

\mathscr{E}_{\alpha,p}(u)=\frac{1}{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(\Phi(\nabla_{z}u)^{2}+\frac{\Phi^{0}(z)^{2\alpha}}{4}\Psi(\nabla_{\sigma}u)^{2}\right)^{\frac{p}{2}}dzd\sigma,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ 1<p<\infty.

It is clear that (
1.2
) corresponds to the special case
\alpha=1
and
p=2
of (
1.16
). One of their main result is an explicit fundamental solution for the Euler-Lagrange equation of (
1.16
). To formulate the relevant result, consider the following anisotropic Minkowski gauge

(1.17)

\Theta(z,\sigma)=\left(\Phi(z)^{2(\alpha+1)}+4(\alpha+1)^{2}\Psi(\sigma)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2(\alpha+1)}}.

In
[
7
]
a new Legendre transformation
\Theta^{0}
was introduced,
namely

(1.18)

\Theta^{0}(z,\sigma)^{\alpha+1}=\underset{\Theta(\xi,\tau)=1}{\sup}\ \bigg(|\langle z,\xi\rangle|^{\alpha+1}+4(\alpha+1)^{2}\langle\sigma,\tau\rangle\bigg).

The remarkable feature of (
1.18
) is underscored by the following result, established in
[
7
, Proposition 3.3]
:

(1.19)

\Theta^{0}(z,\sigma)=\left(\Phi^{0}(z)^{2(\alpha+1)}+4(\alpha+1)^{2}\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2(\alpha+1)}},

where
\Phi^{0}
and
\Psi^{0}
are the classical dual Minkowski norms defined as in (
2.2
) below. The reader should observe the perfect symmetry between (
1.17
) and (
1.19
). Furthermore, in
[
7
, Theorem 1.2]
the authors proved that the function

(1.20)

\mathscr{E}_{\alpha,p}(z,\sigma)=\begin{cases}C_{\alpha,p}\ \Theta^{0}(z,\sigma)^{-\frac{Q-p}{p-1}},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ p\not=Q,\\
\\
C_{\alpha}\ \log\Theta^{0}(z,\sigma),\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ p=Q,\end{cases}

is a fundamental solution, with pole in
(0,0)
, of the Euler-Lagrange equation of (
1.16
). The number
Q
in (
1.20
) is given by

(1.21)

Q=Q_{\alpha}=m+(\alpha+1)k.

It is clear from (
1.20
) that such number plays the role of a dimension. In (
1.20
), the positive constants
C_{\alpha,p}
and
C_{\alpha}
are implicitly given, and they involve the Wulff shapes of the gauge
\Theta^{0}
. However, in order to fully understand the gradient flow of the energy (
1.2
), it is important to have an explicit knowledge of the constants. For this reason, in Section
3
we turn to this problem and in Lemma
3.4
we show that, with
\sigma_{\Phi}
and
\sigma_{\Psi}
as in (
1.12
) above, then

(1.22)

\sigma_{\alpha,p}=\sigma_{\Phi}\sigma_{\Psi}\ \frac{B(\frac{m+\alpha p}{2(\alpha+1)},\frac{k}{2})}{2^{k+1}(\alpha+1)^{k}},

where we have denoted by
B(x,y)
Euler beta function, see (
3.7
). Since in
[
7
, Theorem 1.2]
it was proved that

(1.23)

C_{\alpha,p}=\begin{cases}\frac{p-1}{Q-p}\left(\sigma_{\alpha,p}\right)^{-1/(p-1)},\ \ \ \ \ p\not=Q,\\
\\
\sigma_{\alpha,Q}^{-1/(Q-1)},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ p=Q,\end{cases}

it is clear that (
1.22
) provides an expression of
C_{\alpha,p}
.
We explicitly note from (
1.19
), (
1.20
) and (
1.21
) that, when
\alpha=1
and
p=2
, we have

(1.24)

\Theta^{0}(z,\sigma)=\left(\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}+16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{4}},

and

(1.25)

Q=m+2k.

We also mention that the above theorem (
1.20
) generalised a result first established in
[
10
]
for the linear case
\Phi(z)=|z|
,
\Psi(\sigma)=|\sigma|
, when
p=2
.

To put our second result in some historical context, consider the standard Gauss-Weierstrass kernel in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
(here, we are assuming
n\not=2
)

G(x,t)=(4\pi t)^{-\frac{n}{2}}e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}}{4t}},\ \ \ \ \ \ t>0.

A well-known manifestation of the fact that
G(x,t)
is a fundamental solution of the heat equation, and of the Bochner subordination principle, is that the
L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
function defined by

E(x)\overset{def}{=}\int_{0}^{\infty}G(x,t)dt,

provides a fundamental solution of
-\Delta
with pole in
0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Furthermore, an elementary, yet beautiful computation, shows that

E(x)=\frac{1}{(n-2)\sigma_{n-1}}|x|^{2-n}.

The next theorem shows that, surprisingly, despite its strongly nonlinear character, the evolution equation (
1.6
) above displays the same linear phenomenon as the standard heat equation. Furthermore, Theorem
1.3
shows the remarkable fact that, had we not known the magic Minkowski gauge
\Theta^{0}
in (
1.24
), by running the nonlinear heat flow (
1.1
) we are forced to discover it!

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathscr{G}(X,t)
be the function defined in (
1.14
) of Theorem
1.1
. For every
t>0
, we have

(1.26)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathscr{G}(X,t)dt=\frac{C_{1,2}}{\Theta^{0}(z,\sigma)^{Q-2}},

where
\Theta^{0}
is as in (
1.24
),
Q
as in (
1.25
), and
C_{1,2}
is the constant, identified by (
1.22
), and corresponding to the case
\alpha=1
and
p=2
in (
1.23
) above.
As a consequence of (
1.20
), the function defined by the left-hand side of (
1.26
) is a fundamental solution of the nonlinear equation

(1.27)

\Delta_{\Phi}(u)+\frac{\Phi^{0}(z)^{2}}{4}\Delta_{\Psi}(u)=0.

This proves that
\mathscr{G}(X,t)
is not only a solution to (
1.6
), but in fact it is a fundamental solution.

2.
Minkowski norms

Let
M:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
be a Minkowski norm in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By this we mean that
M^{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\})
is a strictly convex function such that
M(\lambda x)=|\lambda|M(x)
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. By strict convexity we mean that the Hessian matrix
\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}(M^{2})
in positive definite in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
. Since all norms in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
are equivalent, there exist constants
\beta\geq\alpha>0
such that

(2.1)

\alpha|\xi|\leq M(\xi)\leq\beta|\xi|.

We denote by

(2.2)

M^{0}(x)=\underset{M(\xi)=1}{\sup}\ \langle x,\xi\rangle,

its Legendre transform, also known as the dual norm of
M
. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality trivially holds

|\langle x,y\rangle|\leq M(x)M^{0}(y).

A basic property of the norms
M
and
M^{0}
is the following, see
[
3
, Lemma 2.1]
, and also (3.12) in
[
6
]
,

(2.3)

M(\nabla M^{0}(x))=M^{0}(\nabla M(x))=1,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}.

Given a function
u\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, an elementary, yet useful, consequence of the homogeneity of
M
is

(2.4)

\langle\nabla M(\nabla u(x)),\nabla u(x)\rangle=M(\nabla u(x)).

A less obvious basic fact is the following formula, which can be found in
[
3
, Lemma 2.2]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
, one has

M^{0}(x)\nabla M(\nabla M^{0}(x))=x,\ \ \ \ \ \ M(x)\nabla M^{0}(\nabla M(x))=x.

The Euler-Lagrange equation of the energy

(2.5)

\mathscr{E}_{M}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int M(\nabla u)^{2}dx

is the so-called Finsler Laplacian

(2.6)

\Delta_{M}(u)=\operatorname{div}(M(\nabla u)\nabla M(\nabla u))=0.

It is worth emphasising here that the operator in (
2.6
) is quasilinear, but not linear, unless of course
M(x)=|x|
. However, the operator
\Delta_{M}
is elliptic. In fact, since
M^{2}
is homogeneous of degree
2
, Euler formula gives

\langle\nabla(M^{2})(\xi),\xi\rangle=M(\xi)^{2},

and from (
2.1
) we thus have for every
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

(2.7)

\alpha^{2}|\xi|^{2}\leq\langle\nabla(M^{2})(\xi),\xi\rangle\leq\beta^{2}|\xi|^{2}.

An important property of the dual norm is the following result which follows from (
2.3
) and from Lemma
2.1
, see
[
6
]
and
[
9
]
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Consider the function

\psi(x)=M^{0}(x).

Then if
k\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, and
v=k\circ\psi
, one has in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}

\Delta_{M}(v)=k^{\prime\prime}(\psi)+\frac{n-1}{\psi}k^{\prime}(\psi).

A remarkable consequence of Proposition
2.2
is that the nonlinear operator
\Delta_{M}
acts linearly on functions of the dual norm
M^{0}
.
The Finsler heat equation arises from the gradient flow of the energy (
2.5
). It is the quasilinear PDE in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}

(2.8)

\partial_{t}u=\Delta_{M}(u).

In the framework of Finsler manifolds, an in-depth study of (
2.8
) has been carried in the works
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
, see also
[
1
]
for the case of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Using Proposition
2.2
it is possible to construct the following notable explicit solution of the Finsler heat equation, see
[
17
, Example 4.3]
, and also
[
1
]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

The function

G(x,t)=t^{-\frac{n}{2}}e^{-\frac{M^{0}(x)^{2}}{4t}},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},t>0,

solves the heat equation in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,\infty)
, i.e.,

\partial_{t}G-\Delta_{M}(G)=0.

We leave it as an exercise to the reader to verify that the function
G(x,t)
satisfies the following Proposition
2.4
, the Finsler counterpart of the extremal case of the famous result in
[
15
]
. A Li-Yau theory in Finsler manifolds was developed in the cited work
[
18
]
. In connection with the present work, a version of the theory in
[
2
]
will be presented in a forthcoming article.

Proposition 2.4
.

The following identity is true in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,\infty)

M(\nabla\log G)^{2}-\partial_{t}(\log G)=\frac{n}{2t}.

Using the coarea formula one can easily show that

(2.9)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}G(x,t)dx=2^{n-1}\Gamma(n/2)\sigma_{M},

where we have let

(2.10)

\sigma_{M}=\int_{\{M^{0}(x)=1\}}\frac{dH^{n-1}(x)}{|\nabla M^{0}(x)|}.

Notice that, on the one hand the coarea formula gives

(2.11)

\operatorname{Vol}_{n}(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\Phi^{0}(x)<r\})=\int_{0}^{r}\int_{\{M^{0}(x)=s\}}\frac{dH^{n-1}(x)}{|\nabla M^{0}(x)|}ds,

and therefore

(2.12)

\frac{d}{dr}\operatorname{Vol}_{n}(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\Phi^{0}(x)<r\})=\int_{\{M^{0}(x)=r\}}\frac{dH^{n-1}(x)}{|\nabla M^{0}(x)|}.

On the other hand, a rescaling gives

(2.13)

\operatorname{Vol}_{n}(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\Phi^{0}(x)<r\})=\omega_{M}\ r^{n},

and therefore

(2.14)

\frac{d}{dr}\operatorname{Vol}_{n}(\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\Phi^{0}(x)<r\})=n\omega_{M}\ r^{n-1}.

Equating (
2.12
) and (
2.14
), we infer

(2.15)

\int_{\{M^{0}(x)=r\}}\frac{dH^{n-1}(x)}{|\nabla M^{0}(x)|}=n\omega_{M}\ r^{n-1},\ \ \ \ \ \ r>0.

In particular, when
r=1
we obtain from (
2.10
) and (
2.15
)

(2.16)

\sigma_{M}=n\omega_{M}.

Remark 2.5
.

The identity (
2.16
) can also be directly obtained by the well-known formula of Minkowski for the volume, which gives

(2.17)

\omega_{M}=\frac{1}{n}\int_{\{M^{0}(x)=1\}}\langle x,\nu\rangle dH^{n-1}(x).

If we now use the first identity in Lemma
2.1
, the fact that
\nu=\frac{\nabla M^{0}}{|\nabla M^{0}|}
, and Euler formula
\langle\nabla M(p),p\rangle=M(p)
, applied with
p=\nabla M^{0}(x)
, we find on
\{M^{0}(x)=1\}

\langle x,\nu\rangle=M^{0}(x)\langle\nabla M(\nabla M^{0}(x)),\frac{\nabla M^{0}(x)}{|\nabla M^{0}(x)|}\rangle=\frac{M(\nabla M^{0}(x))}{|\nabla M^{0}|}=\frac{1}{|\nabla M^{0}|},

where in the last equality we have used (
2.3
). Substituting in (
2.17
), and using (
2.10
), we obtain (
2.16
).

We will need the following useful observation.

Lemma 2.6
.

Suppose
f(x)=f^{\star}(|x|)
, for some measurable function
f^{\star}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
, and consider the function
F(x)=f^{\star}(M^{0}(x))
. Then

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}F(x)dx=\frac{\sigma_{M}}{\sigma_{n-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)dx,

where as customary
\sigma_{n-1}=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}}{\Gamma(n/2)}
.

Proof.

The coarea formula and (
2.15
) give

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}F(x)dx

\nu+\mu=\frac{m}{2}+k-1,\ \ \ \ 1-\mu+\nu=-\frac{m}{2}.

Notice that, since
m\geq 1,k\geq 1
, we have
\nu+\mu\geq\frac{1}{2}>0
, and since
z\not=0
, all requirements in (
5.4
) are fulfilled. Since

\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}=\frac{u^{2}}{16\tanh^{2}u}(\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}+16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u),\ \ \ \frac{\beta^{2}}{\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}}=\frac{16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u}{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}+16\Psi(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u},

we obtain from (
5.5
)

(5.7)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{\frac{m}{2}+\frac{k}{2}-1}e^{-t\frac{u}{\tanh u}\frac{\Phi^{0}(z)^{2}}{4}}J_{\frac{k}{2}-1}(u\Psi^{0}(\sigma)t)\ dt=\frac{2^{1-\frac{k}{2}}(u\Psi^{0}(\sigma))^{\frac{k}{2}-1}\Gamma(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}{\Gamma(\frac{k}{2})(\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}+16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u)^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}}

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{16\tanh^{2}u}{u^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}F\left(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1),-\frac{m}{4};\frac{k}{2};\frac{16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u}{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}+16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{4^{\frac{m}{2}+k-1}2^{1-\frac{k}{2}}\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{\frac{k}{2}-1}\Gamma(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}{\Gamma(\frac{k}{2})}\frac{u^{\frac{k}{2}-1}(\tanh^{2}u)^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}}{u^{\frac{m}{2}+k-1}(\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}+16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u)^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}}

\displaystyle\times F\left(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1),-\frac{m}{4};\frac{k}{2};\frac{16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u}{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}+16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u}\right).

Substituting (
5.7
) in (
5.3
), we find

(5.8)

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{4^{\frac{m}{2}+k-1}2^{1-\frac{k}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}{\Gamma(\frac{k}{2})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}u}\right)^{\frac{m}{4}}\frac{(\tanh^{2}u)^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}}{(\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}+16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u)^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}}

\displaystyle\times F\left(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1),-\frac{m}{4};\frac{k}{2};\frac{16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u}{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}+16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u}\right)\ du,

It might be helpful for the reader to notice that in the above computation a first little miracle has happened: the powers of
u
have disappeared from the integrand. This crucial aspect hides an important geometric information. To proceed in the computation we somehow need to kill the enemy, i.e., the very unpleasant factor

\frac{1}{(\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}+16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u)^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}}

in the integral (
5.8
). The appropriate tool for the job is the following Kummer’s relation (one of many), which prescribes the change of the hypergeometric function
F
under linear transformations (see formula (3) on p. 105 in
[
8
]
),

(5.9)

F(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;x)=(1-x)^{-\alpha}F\left(\alpha,\gamma-\beta;\gamma;\frac{x}{x-1}\right),\ \ \ \ \ \ x\not=1,\ |\arg(1-x)|<\pi.

Comparing (
5.8
) with (
5.9
), it appears evident that we should apply the latter with the choice

\displaystyle\frac{x}{x-1}=\frac{16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u}{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}+16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u},\ \ \ \gamma=\frac{k}{2},\ \ \ \gamma-\beta=-\frac{m}{4},\ \ \ \alpha=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1).

This choice gives

x=-\frac{16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u}{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}},\ \ \ \ \ \ \beta=\frac{k}{2}+\frac{m}{4}.

What is crucial for us is that

1-x=\frac{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}+16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u}{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}}\ \not=\ 0,

and therefore

(1-x)^{\alpha}=\left(\frac{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}+16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u}{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}.

Applying (
5.9
) we thus find

(5.10)

\displaystyle F\left(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1),-\frac{m}{4};\frac{k}{2};\frac{16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u}{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}+16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}+16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u}{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}

\displaystyle\times F\left(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1),\frac{m}{4}+\frac{k}{2};\frac{k}{2};-\frac{16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u}{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}}\right).

Substitution of (
5.10
) into (
5.8
) gives

(5.11)

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{4^{\frac{m}{2}+k-1}2^{1-\frac{k}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}{\Gamma(\frac{k}{2})\Phi^{0}(z)^{2(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}u}\right)^{\frac{m}{4}}(\tanh^{2}u)^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}

\displaystyle\times F\left(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1),\frac{m}{4}+\frac{k}{2};\frac{k}{2};-\frac{16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u}{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}}\right)\ du.

Formula (
5.11
) represents the second little miracle in the proof of Theorem
1.3
. Our next objective is to finally compute in closed form the integral in the right-hand side of (
5.11
). With this in mind, we intend to make the crucial change of variable
y=\tanh^{2}u
, which gives
dy=\frac{2\tanh u}{\cosh^{2}u}du
.
To do this we rearrange part of the integrand in the following way

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{\sinh^{2}u}\right)^{\frac{m}{4}}(\tanh^{2}u)^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}u}\right)^{\frac{m}{4}-1}(\tanh^{2}u)^{\frac{k-2}{2}}\ \frac{2\tanh u}{\cosh^{2}u}.

We need to be a bit careful here, and distinguish the case
k=1
from
k\geq 2
, but the relevant details are left to the reader. Assuming that
k\geq 2
we rewrite (
5.11
) as follows.

(5.12)

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{4^{\frac{m}{2}+k-1}2^{1-\frac{k}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}{2\ \Gamma(\frac{k}{2})\Phi^{0}(z)^{2(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(1-\tanh^{2}u\right)^{\frac{m}{4}-1}(\tanh^{2}u)^{\frac{k-2}{2}}\ \frac{2\tanh u}{\cosh^{2}u}

\displaystyle\times F\left(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1),\frac{m}{4}+\frac{k}{2};\frac{k}{2};-\frac{16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}\tanh^{2}u}{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}}\right)\ du.

Performing the above stated change of variable in the integral in the right-hand side of (
5.12
), we finally reach the conclusion that

(5.13)

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{4^{\frac{m}{2}+k-1}2^{1-\frac{k}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}{2\ \Gamma(\frac{k}{2})\Phi^{0}(z)^{2(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(1-y\right)^{\frac{m}{4}-1}y^{\frac{k}{2}-1}\

\displaystyle\times F\left(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1),\frac{m}{4}+\frac{k}{2};\frac{k}{2};-\frac{16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}}{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}}\ y\right)\ dy.

The form of the integral in the right-hand side of (
5.15
) represents the third miracle in the proof of the theorem. To unravel it, we apply the following formula due to H. Bateman, see
[
8
, (2) on p. 78]
, which gives

(5.14)

\int_{0}^{1}(1-y)^{\gamma-c-1}y^{c-1}F(\alpha,\beta;c;ay)dy=\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(\gamma-c)}{\Gamma(\gamma)}F(\alpha,\beta;\gamma;a),

provided that

\Re\gamma>\Re c>0,\ \ \ \ \ a\not=1,\ \ \ \ \ |\arg(1-a)|<\pi.

Applying (
5.14
) with

\gamma=\frac{m}{4}+\frac{k}{2},\ \ \ c=\frac{k}{2},\ \ \ \alpha=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1),\ \ \ \beta=\frac{m}{4}+\frac{k}{2},\ \ \ \ a=-\frac{16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}}{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}},

we finally obtain

(5.15)

\displaystyle\mathscr{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{4^{\frac{m}{2}+k-1}2^{-\frac{k}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)\Gamma(\frac{m}{4})}{\Gamma(\frac{m}{4}+\frac{k}{2})\Phi^{0}(z)^{2(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}}\ F\left(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1),\frac{m}{4}+\frac{k}{2};\frac{m}{4}+\frac{k}{2};-\frac{16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}}{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}}\right).

If we now use the following elementary, yet important property, which can be directly verified from (
5.6
), see also
[
8
, (4) on p. 101]
,

(5.16)

F(\alpha,\beta;\beta;-a)=(1+a)^{-\alpha},

we reach the remarkable conclusion that

(5.17)

F\left(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1),\frac{m}{4}+\frac{k}{2};\frac{m}{4}+\frac{k}{2};-\frac{16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2}}{\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}}\right)=\frac{\Phi^{0}(z)^{2(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}}{(\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}+16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}}.

Substituting (
5.17
) in (
5.15
), we have

(5.18)

\mathscr{I}=\frac{4^{\frac{m}{2}+k-1}2^{-\frac{k}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{m}{4})\Gamma(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}{\Gamma(\frac{m}{4}+\frac{k}{2})}(\Phi^{0}(z)^{4}+16\Psi^{0}(\sigma)^{2})^{-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}.

To more conveniently represent the constant in the right-hand side of (
5.18
), we now use (
3.14
)
in which we take
x=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)
, to find

\frac{\Gamma(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)}{\Gamma(\frac{m}{4}+\frac{k}{2})}=\pi^{-1/2}2^{\frac{m}{2}+k-2}\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1)).

Substituting in (
5.18
), and keeping (
1.24
) and (
1.25
) in mind, we obtain

(5.19)

\mathscr{I}=\pi^{-1/2}2^{\frac{3}{2}m+\frac{5}{2}k-4}\Gamma(\frac{m}{4})\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1))\ \Theta^{0}(z,\sigma)^{-(Q-2)}.

If we now remember that we need to multiply the right-hand side of (
5.19
) by the constant in (
5.2
), we finally reach the conclusion that

(5.20)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathscr{G}(X,t)\ dt=\frac{\sigma_{m-1}\sigma_{k-1}}{\sigma_{\Phi}\sigma_{\Psi}}\frac{2^{\frac{m}{2}+2k-4}\Gamma(\frac{m}{4})\Gamma(\frac{1}{2}(\frac{m}{2}+k-1))}{\pi^{\frac{m+k+1}{2}}}\ \Theta^{0}(z,\sigma)^{-(Q-2)}.

In view of (
3.10
) in Proposition
3.5
, the equation (
5.20
) finally proves (
1.26
).

∎
