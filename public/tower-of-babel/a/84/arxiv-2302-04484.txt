Finite index rigidity of hyperbolic groups
Nir Lazarovich

Abstract
We prove that the topological complexity of a finite index subgroup of a hyperbolic group is linear in its index. This follows from a more general result relating the size of the quotient of a free cocompact action of a hyperbolic group on a graph to the minimal number of cells in a simplicial classifying space for the group. As a corollary we prove that any two isomorphic finite-index subgroups of a non-elementary hyperbolic group have the same index.

Introduction

We say that a group
G
is
finite index rigid
(or
satisfies the volume condition

[
7
]
) if it does not contain isomorphic finite index subgroups of different indices.
For example, the infinite cyclic group
\mathbb{Z}
is not finite index rigid (
e.g.

2\mathbb{Z}\simeq 3\mathbb{Z}
).
In contrast, the following families of groups are finite index rigid: finite groups; groups with non-zero (rational) Euler characteristic (
e.g.
non-abelian free groups and hyperbolic surface groups); groups with some non-zero
\ell^{2}
-betti number; lattices in simple Lie groups
[
23
]
; groups with deficiency
\geq 2

[
24
]
; most 3-manifold groups
[
31
,
32
]
; groups with infinitely many ends
[
28
]
(cf. also §6 of
[
21
]
); and groups which have a JSJ decomposition with a maximal hanging Fuchsian group
[
28
]
.

In all of the above cases, the proofs rely on the existence of a non-zero numerical invariant which depends linearly on the index of a finite index subgroup.
In this paper we find such an invariant for hyperbolic groups:

Theorem A
.

Every non-elementary hyperbolic group is finite index rigid.

To put this theorem in context, note that Sela
[
26
]
proved that one-ended hyperbolic groups are co-Hopfian, i.e. they are not isomorphic to their proper subgroups.
On the other hand, Stark-Woodhouse
[
27
]
found an example of a one-ended hyperbolic group with two isomorphic subgroups of different indices – a finite-index subgroup which is isomorphic to an infinite-index subgroup.
The theorem above shows that it is impossible to find such an example in which both subgroups have finite index.

As an immediate corollary we obtain:

Corollary 1.1
.

Let
\varphi\in\Out(F_{d})
be an atoroidal outer automorphism of the free group
F_{d}
, then
F_{d}\rtimes\left<\varphi^{m}\right>\simeq F_{d}\rtimes\left<\varphi^{n}\right>
if and only if
|m|=|n|
.

Proof.

The group
G=F_{d}\rtimes\left<\varphi\right>
is hyperbolic by
[
8
]
. For every
k
the group
F_{d}\rtimes\left<\varphi^{k}\right>
is a subgroup of
G
of index
|k|
. The corollary now follows by
A
.
∎

As mentioned above, the common strategy for proving finite index rigidity is to associate some “complexity” to a group and to show that it is proportional to its index.
To elucidate this, consider the case of a non-abelian free group
G=F_{d}
and consider a finite index subgroup
A\leq G
. We have

1-\rank(A)=\chi(A)=[G:A]\chi(G)

(1.1)

where
\chi(G)
denotes the Euler characteristic of
G
.
On the left hand side of
Eq.

1.1
, we have a number that depends only on the isomorphism type of
A
(and can be thought of measuring the “complexity” of the free group
A
), while on the right we have a number that depends linearly on the index
[G:A]
(and on
G
).
It easily follows from
Eq.

1.1
that if two finite index subgroups
A,B\leq G
are isomorphic then
[G:A]=[G:B]
.

The proof of
A
relies on a similar relation between complexity and index (or more generally covolume) in a discrete hyperbolic setting.
For a compact simplicial complex
\bar{X}
, we denote by
\Vol(\bar{X})
the total number of cells of
\bar{X}
, and by
\Vol_{i}(\bar{X})
the total number of cells in its
i
-skeleton.
For
i\leq m
and a group
G
define
\Cx_{i,m}(G)
to be the minimal
\Vol_{i}(\bar{L})
where
\bar{L}
runs over all
m
-dimensional simplicial complexes such that
\pi_{1}(\bar{L})=G
and whose universal cover is
(m-1)
-connected.
(If such a complex
\bar{L}
does not exist then
\Cx_{i,m}(G)=\infty
.)
The value of
\Cx_{i,m}(G)
can be thought of as measuring the topological complexity of
G
, similar to the Kneser complexity
k(M)
measuring the minimal triangulation of a manifold
M
.
For
m=1
this invariant is related to the rank
\Cx_{1,1}(G)\asymp\rank(G)
(see §
2
for the definition of the notation
\asymp
). For
i=m=2
, if
G
is one-ended,
\Cx_{2,2}(G)\asymp T(G)
, where
T(G)
is Delzant’s T-invariant
[
11
]
defined by

T(G)=\min\left\{\;\;\sum_{r\in R}\max\{|r|-2,0\}\;\;:\;\;\text{for all presentations }G=\left<S\;|\;R\right>\;\;\right\}.

Our main result is the following relation between covolume and complexity.

Theorem B
.

Let
X
be a locally finite one-ended hyperbolic graph. Then, there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
so that if
G
acts on
X
freely and cocompactly then

\Vol(X/G)\;\asymp_{X}\;\Cx_{2,m}(G).

In fact, it suffices to take
m=\dim(\partial X)+1
where
\partial X
is the Gromov boundary and
\dim
is its topological covering dimension. In particular, if
\dim(\partial X)=1
we get a similar inequality for Delzant’s
T
invariant.

In the smooth setting,
i.e.
when
X
is a non-positively curved Riemannian manifold, relations between covolume of lattices and measurements of complexity were extensively studied
[
1
,
2
,
5
,
9
,
11
,
12
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
19
,
29
,
24
]
.
We note that in the discrete setting of
B
, the inequality
\Cx_{i,m}(G)\ll_{X}\Vol(X/G)
is almost immediate from the contractibility of the Rips complex and the definition of
\Cx_{i,m}(G)
(see §
2
for the definition of the notation
\ll
). Therefore, most of the effort is in proving the other inequality, namely
\Vol(X/G)\ll_{X}\Cx_{2,m}(G)
. Loosely, this inequality states that the more volume
X/G
has the more complicated the group
G
should be as an abstract group.
To prove this inequality we start with some compact complex
\bar{L}
that realizes
\Cx_{2,m}(G)
(for large enough
m
that depends only on
X
). In §
3
–§
5
, drawing on ideas of Delzant
[
10
]
and Rips-Sela
[
25
]
, we use Mineyev’s rational bicombing
[
22
]
on
X
to obtain a singular weighted pattern
\bar{\mathcal{F}}
on the 2-skeleton of
\bar{L}
. In §
6
, we prove that the total weight of the pattern
\bar{\mathcal{F}}
is bounded above by
\Vol_{2}(\bar{L})=\Cx_{2,m}(G)
. In §
7
, using cohomological methods of Bestvina-Mess
[
6
]
, we show that a continuous
G
-equivariant map from the universal cover of
\bar{L}
to
X
must be quasi-surjective with a constant that depends only on
X
(“uniform quasi-surjectivity”
Proposition

7.1
). In §
8
, we use this to show that the total weight of the pattern
\bar{\mathcal{F}}
is bounded below by the volume
\Vol(X/G)
. Combining the two inequalities gives

\Vol(X/G)\ll_{X}\text{ total weight of $\bar{\mathcal{F}}$ }\ll_{X}\Cx_{2,m}(G).

As a corollary of
B
one gets the desired relation between index and complexity:

Theorem C
.

Let
G
be a one-ended hyperbolic group. Then, there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that if
H
is a finite index subgroup of
G
then

[G:H]\;\asymp_{G}\;C_{2,m}(H).

B
implies
C
.

Let
X=\Cay(G,S)
be the Cayley graph of
G
with respect to some finite generating set
S
. The result now follows by
B
since a finite index subgroup
H
acts freely and cocompactly on
X
and
\Vol(X/H)\asymp_{X}[G:H]
.
∎

Theorem
C
implies Theorem
A
.

By Sykiotis
[
28
]
it suffices to prove the theorem for one-ended groups.
Let
G
be a one-ended hyperbolic group.
Following Reznikov
[
24
]
, we define

\underline{C}_{2,m}(G):=\liminf_{H\leq G,\;[G:H]<\infty}\frac{C_{2,m}(H)}{[G:H]}

where the limit is taken over the lattice of finite index subgroups (note that we do not assume residual finiteness of
G
).
It is easy to see that
\underline{C}_{2,m}(G)
is multiplicative, that is, for all
H\leq G
of finite index

\underline{C}_{2,m}(H)=[G:H]\cdot\underline{C}_{2,m}(G).

It is also clear that
\underline{C}_{2,m}(G)
is a group isomorphism invariant.
By Theorem
C
, there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
that depend on
G
, such that for every finite index subgroup
H\leq G
,

\alpha\leq\frac{C_{2,m}(H)}{[G:H]}\leq\beta.

Taking the limit inferior we get
0<\underline{C}_{2,m}(G)<\infty.

It follows that
G
is finite index rigid: if
H_{1},H_{2}\leq G
are two isomorphic finite index subgroups of
G
then

[G:H_{2}]\cdot\underline{C}_{2,m}(G)=\underline{C}_{2,m}(H_{2})=\underline{C}_{2,m}(H_{1})=[G:H_{1}]\cdot\underline{C}_{2,m}(G)

and it follows that
[G:H_{1}]=[G:H_{2}]
.
∎

Notation and conventions

Asymptotic notation

We write
M\ll_{X}N
if there exists
0<\alpha<\infty
that depends on
X
(but does not depend on
M,N
) such that
M\leq\alpha N
.

We write
M\asymp_{X}N
if
M\ll_{X}N
and
N\ll_{X}M
, or equivalently there exist
0<\alpha,\beta<\infty
that depend on
X
(but not on
M,N
) such that
\alpha N\leq M\leq\beta N
. Similarly, we write
M\asymp_{X}N
when
\alpha,\beta
depend on
X
.

Constants.

Throughout the text, a cocompact one-ended simplicial hyperbolic graph
X
will be fixed.

We will use the letter
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
(in bold) to denote some non-specified “big” positive constant that depends only on
X
, one can think of
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
as a placeholder for
O_{X}(1)
(or more precisely
\Theta_{X}(1)
since we assume that the constant is positive). Each appearance of
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
thus stands for a (possibly) different constant that depends only on
X
.
For instance, the inequality “
M\leq{\boldsymbol{\delta}}N
” stands for “
M\ll_{X}N
”, namely, “there exists
\delta_{1}=\delta_{1}(X)
such that
M\leq\delta_{1}N
”.
In particular, the reader should expect to see things like
2{\boldsymbol{\delta}}={\boldsymbol{\delta}}={\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{2}
, implications of the sort “
M_{1}\leq{\boldsymbol{\delta}}N_{1}\text{ and }M_{2}\leq{\boldsymbol{\delta}}N_{2}\implies M_{1}M_{2}\leq{\boldsymbol{\delta}}N_{1}N_{2}
” and “if
r\leq{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
then the number of vertices in a ball of radius
r
in
X
is at most
{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
”,
etc.

In some arguments we use
\delta_{1},\delta_{2},\dots
(and other Greek letters) to denote specific constants that depend only on
X
. Note that these are not placeholders for
O_{X}(1)
, but rather explicit numbers, and so we will write explicitly “there exists
\delta_{1}=\delta_{1}(X)
such that …”.

Simplicial complexes.

Let
X
be a simplicial complex.
By abuse of notation we denote its topological realization by
X
. We denote by
X^{i}
its (oriented)
i
-simplices, by
X^{(i)}
its
i
-skeleton and by
C_{i}(X)
its rational
i
-chains.
For a simplex
s\in X^{i}
we denote its inverse orientation by
-s
and its unoriented simplex by
|s|
.
Formally,
s\in X^{i}
is an
(i+1)
-tuple of vertices of
X
ordered up to an even permutation, and
|s|
is simply the unordered set corresponding to
s
.
Let
a=\sum_{s}a_{s}s\in C_{i}(X)
be a rational i-chain (where
a_{s}=0
for all but finitely many
s\in X^{i}
). When
i\geq 1
, we will assume that
a_{s}\geq 0
and if
a_{s}>0
then
a_{-s}=0
. We denote the oriented support of
a
by
\supp(a)=\{s\;|\;a_{s}\neq 0\}\subseteq X^{i}
, its unoriented support by
|\supp(a)|=\{|s|\;\mid\;a_{s}\neq 0\}
, its 1-norm by
\|a\|_{1}=\sum_{s}|a_{s}|
, and its
\infty
-norm by
\|a\|_{\infty}=\max_{s}|a_{s}|
. On
0
-cycles we also have the augmentation map
\varepsilon(\sum_{s}a_{s}s)=\sum_{s}a_{s}
.
For an (oriented) edge
e
, denote its endpoints by
e_{+},e_{-}
so that
\partial e=e_{+}-e_{-}
.

For
x,y\in X^{0}
, we denote by
d(x,y)\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
the length of the shortest path between them in
X^{(1)}
, this length is achieved by some geodesic, by which we mean a sequence of vertices
x=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}=y
such that any consecutive pair is an edge of
X
. We denote such a geodesic by
{\llbracket{x},{y}\rrbracket}
.
We denote by
[x,y]=\{z\in X^{0}\;|\;d(x,z)+d(z,y)=d(x,y)\}
the interval between
x,y
. Equivalently,
[x,y]
is the union of all geodesics from
x
to
y
.

For a subset
A\subseteq X^{0}
we denote its
r
-neighborhood by
N_{r}(A)=\{b\in X^{0}\;|\;\exists a\in A:d(a,b)\leq r\}
, and if the subset is a singleton
A=\{a\}
we simply denote
B_{r}(a)
(the closed ball of radius
r
around
a
).
For subsets
A,B\subseteq X^{0}
we denote by
d(A,B)
their Hausdorff distance, i.e.
d(A,B)=\inf\{r\;|\;(B\subseteq N_{r}(A))\wedge(A\subseteq N_{r}(B))\}.

In particular, viewing simplices as subsets of
X^{0}
this defines a distance between simplices.
For a subset
A\subseteq X
, we denote by
\mathcal{N}_{r}(A)=\{f\in X^{1}\;|\;\exists a\in A:d(\{a\},f)\leq r\}
the set of edges of
X
which are at Hausdorff distance at most
r
from some element of
A
. In other words, the set
\mathcal{N}_{r}(A)
is the set of edges both of whose endpoints are at distance at most
r
from some element of
A
. As before,
\mathcal{B}_{r}(a)=\mathcal{N}_{r}(\{a\})
denotes the edges in the closed ball of radius
r
around
a
.

A
simplicial action
of a group
G
on
X
is an action of
G
on the vertices of
X
which maps simplices to simplices.
Such an action gives rise to an action of
G
on the realization of
X
by extending the action on the vertices linearly to simplices.
The action is free if the stabilizers of simplices are trivial in
G
, or in other words, if the induced action on the realization of
X
is free.
Note that even if the action
G\curvearrowright X
is free, the quotient
X/G
is not necessarily simplicial.
To remedy this we can consider the action of
G
on the barycentric subdivision
\dot{X}
of
X
. This action is free and
\dot{X}/G
is simplicial.
Therefore, throughout this text, by a
free simplicial action

G\curvearrowright X
we will mean a free simplicial action such that
X/G
is simplicial.

Throughout the text, we will assume that
X
is hyperbolic,
i.e.
for all
x,y,z\in X
and choices of geodesics
{\llbracket{x},{y}\rrbracket},{\llbracket{x},{z}\rrbracket},{\llbracket{z},{y}\rrbracket}
connecting them,
{\llbracket{x},{y}\rrbracket}\subset N_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}({\llbracket{x},{z}\rrbracket}\cup{\llbracket{z},{y}\rrbracket})
. In particular,
[x,y]\subseteq N_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}({\llbracket{x},{y}\rrbracket})
.

Globally stable bicombing

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X
be a simplicial graph, let
G\curvearrowright X
be a simplicial action, and let
q:X^{0}\times X^{0}\to C_{1}(X)
.

(GSB1)

q
is
a
\mathbb{Q}
-bicombing
if for all
x,y\in X^{0}
,
\partial q(x,y)=y-x
and
q(x,x)=0
,

(GSB2)

q
is
quasigeodesic
if for all
x,y\in X^{0}
and geodesic
{\llbracket{x},{y}\rrbracket}
,
|\supp q(x,y)|\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}({\llbracket{x},{y}\rrbracket})
and
\|q(x,y)\|_{1}\leq{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\cdot d(x,y)
,

(GSB3)

q
is
G
-equivariant
if for all
g\in G
, and
x,y\in X^{0}
,
gq(x,y)=q(gx,gy)
,

(GSB4)

q
is
anti-symmetric
if for all
x,y\in X^{0}
,
q(x,y)=-q(y,x)
, and

(GSB5)

q
has
bounded defect
if for all
x,y,z\in X^{0}
,
\|q(x,y)+q(y,z)+q(z,x)\|_{1}\leq{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
.

A
globally stable bicombing
is a function
q
that satisfies all of the above with respect to the group
\Aut(X)
.

Remark 3.2
.

When
X
is cocompact, the inequality
\|q(x,y)\|_{1}\leq{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\cdot d(x,y)
is also implied by the bounded defect and equivariance properties. To see this, note that if
d(x,y)=n
there is a path
x=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}=y
in
X
. By using the bounded defect
n
times we get
\|q(x,y)\|_{1}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\|q(x_{i},x_{i+1})\|_{1}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\cdot n
. There are finitely many orbits of edges in
X
, and so by the equivariance of
q
,
\|q(x_{i},x_{i+1})\|_{1}\leq{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
. It follows that

\|q(x,y)\|_{1}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\|q(x_{i},x_{i+1})\|_{1}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\cdot n\leq{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\cdot n={\boldsymbol{\delta}}\cdot d(x,y).

Theorem 3.3

(Mineyev
[
22
, Theorem 10]
)
.

Every locally finite hyperbolic graph with a cocompact group action, supports a globally stable bicombing.

Remark 3.4
.

In
[
22
]
, the group is assumed to act freely on the graph, however, this is not actually used in the proof except at the very beginning where an equivariant geodesic bicombing is assumed. This can be replaced with the rational geodesic bi-combing
p(x,y)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
where
n=n(x,y)
is the number of geodesics connecting
x,y
and
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
are the obvious 1-chains corresponding to them. This geodesic bi-combing

is clearly
\Aut(X)
-invariant.
The rest of the construction in
[
22
]
is purely geometric and remains
\Aut(X)
-equivariant.

q
is fixed on our fixed locally finite cocompact hyperbolic graph

.

Lemma 3.5
.

For all
x,y\in X^{0}
,
\|q(x,y)\|_{\infty}\leq{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
.

Proof.

Let
e
be an edge in
\supp q(x,y)
, and let
z=e_{+}
be its endpoint.
By
(GSB2)
, there exists
\delta_{1}=\delta_{1}(X)
such that
\supp q(w_{1},w_{2})\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{\delta_{1}}({\llbracket{w_{1}},{w_{2}}\rrbracket})
for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in X
.
Let
x^{\prime}
(resp.
y^{\prime}
) be the point in
{\llbracket{x},{z}\rrbracket}
(resp.
{\llbracket{y},{z}\rrbracket}
) at distance
\delta_{1}+1
from

if such exists, otherwise take

(resp.
y^{\prime}=y
).
Note that
d(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq 2\delta_{1}+2\leq{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
, and by
(GSB2)
,
\|q(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\|_{1}\leq{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\cdot d(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
.

By the choice of
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}
we have
e\notin\mathcal{N}_{\delta_{1}}({\llbracket{x},{x^{\prime}}\rrbracket})\cup\mathcal{N}_{\delta_{1}}({\llbracket{y^{\prime}},{y}\rrbracket})
,
and by
(GSB2)
, this implies that
e
is not in the support of
q(x,x^{\prime})
nor

.
Applying the bounded defect
(GSB5)
twice we get
\|q(x,y)-q(x,x^{\prime})-q(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})-q(y^{\prime},y)\|_{1}\leq{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
. Since

is not in the support of
q(x,x^{\prime}),q(y^{\prime},y)
we see that the coefficient of

is bounded by
\|q(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\|_{1}+{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\leq{\boldsymbol{\delta}}
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

There exists
\rho
such that for all
x\neq y\in X^{0}
and for all

a_{z}=q(x,y)|_{\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(z)}
of

to the edges in the ball of radius
\rho
around

\|a_{z}\|_{1}\geq 1
.

Proof.

By
(GSB2)
, there exists
\delta_{1}=\delta_{1}(X)
such that
q(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{\delta_{1}}({\llbracket{x^{\prime}},{y^{\prime}}\rrbracket})
for all
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in X
and geodesics
{\llbracket{x^{\prime}},{y^{\prime}}\rrbracket}
.
Set
\rho=2\delta_{1}+1
.
Denote
a=q(x,y)
, and let
a_{z}=q(x,y)|_{\mathcal{N}_{\rho}(z)}
as in the statement.
Fix a geodesic
{\llbracket{x},{z}\rrbracket}
between
x
and
z
, and
{\llbracket{z},{y}\rrbracket}
between

and
y
. Their concatenation

to

.
Set
a_{x}
to be the restriction of

to the set

\{e\in\supp a-\supp a_{z}:\exists w\in{\llbracket{x},{z}\rrbracket}:d(w,e)\leq\delta_{1}\}

And similarly,

Since

The supports of

\supp a_{y}

If we denote,
b_{x}=\partial a_{x}+x
, and

, then
\partial\alpha_{z}=b_{y}-b_{x}
.
Since
b_{x}
and

are homologous 0-chains they have the same image under the augmentation map
\varepsilon(b_{x})=\varepsilon(x)=1
. Similarly
\varepsilon(b_{y})=\varepsilon(y)=1
.
Finally, since

and

have disjoint supports, we get

∎

Proof.

The corollary follows from

4
Weighted singular patterns

Dunwoody
[
13
]
introduced patterns on 2-dimensional complexes in order to study actions on trees.
Inspired by Delzant’s foliation
[
10
]
for the Rips-Sela stable cylinders
[
25
]
, we introduce in this section a looser notion of patterns –
weighted singular pattern
– that will be used in the setting of actions on hyperbolic spaces.
Weighted singular patterns will differ from Dunwoody’s patterns in three ways: the tracks of the pattern can intersect (similar to the patterns that give rise to CAT(0) cube complexes
[
3
,
4
]
), they can have singular points (similar to Delzant’s foliation
[
10
]
), and they will be weighted. We will see in §
5
how, starting from an action of a group
G
on a hyperbolic space with a globally stable bicombing, one can define a weighted singular pattern on a 2-dimensional simplicial complex
\bar{K}
with
\pi_{1}(\bar{K})=G
.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
K
be a 2-dimensional simplicial complex.
A
(singular) pattern

\mathcal{F}
on
K
is an immersed graph
\mathcal{F}\looparrowright K
such that:

(P1)

The vertices of
\mathcal{F}
are called
connectors
and they are sent injectively to the interiors of edges and 2-simplices of
K
, they are called
regular
or
singular
accordingly.

(P2)

The edges of
\mathcal{F}
are called
segments
, and their interiors are sent to straight line segments in the interior of 2-simplices of
K
(in particular, they intersect transversely), segments which connect two regular connectors are called
regular
, and are otherwise
singular
.
Every singular segment has a regular connector as one of its endpoints, and it does not meet any other segment in the interior of the 2-simplex to which it belongs.

(P3)

There are finitely many connectors and segments in each simplex.

(P4)

Each connector
c
is the endpoint of exactly one segment in each 2-simplex containing
c
in its closure.

The connected components of
\mathcal{F}
are called
tracks
.
A
regular pattern
is a pattern without singular connectors.

See
Fig.

1
for an example of a singular pattern. Note that tracks are connected components of
\mathcal{F}
and not its image, and so, in
Fig.

1
the pattern has five tracks.

Figure 1
:
A singular pattern (in cyan) on a 2-complex (in black) consisting of three 2-simplices with a common edge. The regular connectors are shown by full circles, and singular connectors by empty circles.

Definition 4.2
.

A
weighted singular pattern
, is a a pair
(\mathcal{F},\mathbf{w})
where
\mathcal{F}
is a singular pattern, and
\mathbf{w}
is a function from the set of connectors to non-negative reals
\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}

such that
\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{c})=0
for every singular connector
\mathbf{c}
in
\mathcal{F}
.

Assuming
K
is finite, the
weight of a track

\mathbf{t}
is the maximal weight of a connector
\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{t})=\max_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{c})
. The
total weight
of
\mathcal{F}
is the sum of weights of its tracks, i.e.
\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{F})=\sum_{\mathbf{t}\subseteq\mathcal{F}}\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{t})
.

The
defect

\df_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{s})
of a segment
\mathbf{s}
in a weighted singular pattern
(\mathcal{F},\mathbf{w})
, is
\df_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{s})=|\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{c}_{1})-\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{c}_{2})|
where
\mathbf{c}_{1},\mathbf{c}_{2}
are the endpoints of
\mathbf{s}
.
The
defect

\df_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{t})
of a track
\mathbf{t}
is defined to be the sum of defects of segments in
\mathbf{t}
, i.e.
\df_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{t})=\sum_{\mathbf{s}\subseteq\mathbf{t}}\df_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{s})
.
Finally, the
defect
of
(\mathcal{F},\mathbf{w})
is the sum of defects of its tracks,
\df_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathcal{F})=\sum_{\mathbf{t}\subseteq\mathcal{F}}\df_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{t})
, or equivalently, the sum of defects of its segments,
\df_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathcal{F})=\sum_{\mathbf{s}\subseteq\mathcal{F}}\df_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{s})
. We will write
\df(\cdot)
instead of
\df_{\mathbf{w}}(\cdot)
if
\mathbf{w}
is clear from the context.

A weighted singular pattern
(\mathcal{F},\mathbf{w})
is
perfect
if it is regular and
\df(\mathcal{F})=0
.

We denote
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{F}
if
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is a subgraph of
\mathcal{F}
, and its immersion
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\looparrowright K
is the restriction of
\mathcal{F}\looparrowright K
to
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
. If
(\mathcal{F}^{\prime},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}),(\mathcal{F},\mathbf{w})
are weighted, and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
we write
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\leq\mathbf{w}
if
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}(\mathbf{c})\leq\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{c})
for every connector
\mathbf{c}
in
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
(\mathcal{F},\mathbf{w})
be a weighted singular pattern on a compact simplicial complex
K
. Then there is a perfect weighted pattern
(\mathcal{F}^{\prime},\mathbf{w}^{\prime})
such that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{F}
,
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\leq\mathbf{w}
and
\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{F})\leq\mathbf{w}^{\prime}(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})+\df_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathcal{F})
.

Proof.

Define
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
on
\mathcal{F}
by
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}(\mathbf{c})=\max\{0,\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{t})-\df_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{t})\}
for every connector
\mathbf{c}
in a track
\mathbf{t}
of
\mathcal{F}
.

Let us first show that
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\leq\mathbf{w}
: Let
\mathbf{c}
be a connector, let
\mathbf{t}
be the track containing
\mathbf{c}
and let
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
be the connector in
\mathbf{t}
with the maximal weight, i.e.
\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{t})=\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{c}^{\prime})
.
There is a (simple) path of segments
\mathbf{s}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{s}_{n}
in
\mathbf{t}
between
\mathbf{c}
and
\mathbf{c}^{\prime}
. By the definition of the defect, we get

\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{t})-\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{c})=\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{c}^{\prime})-\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{c})\leq\df_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{s}_{1})+\dots+\df_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{s}_{n})\leq\df_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{t}).

It follows that
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}(\mathbf{c})=\max\{0,\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{t})-\df_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{t})\}\leq\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{c})
.

In particular, if
\mathbf{c}
is a singular connector then
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}(\mathbf{c})\leq\mathbf{w}(\mathbf{c})=0
. Hence,
(\mathcal{F},\mathbf{w}^{\prime})
is a weighted singular pattern.

By definition, all connectors in
\mathbf{t}
have the same weight with respect to
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
, and so
\df_{\mathbf{w}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{F})=0
. Define
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
to be the tracks in
\mathcal{F}
on which
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\neq 0
, we immediately get that
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is regular (since any singular connector has weight
0
).
Finally, the equality
\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{F})\leq\mathbf{w}^{\prime}(\mathcal{F}^{\prime})+\df_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathcal{F})
is clear from the definition of
\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
.
∎

7
Uniform quasi-surjectivity

The next proposition shows that continuous quasi-isometries between hyperbolic groups are, in a sense, uniformly quasi-surjective. For this purpose, we will make use of the boundary
\partial G
of a hyperbolic group
G
. The boundary
\partial G
is a compact metrizable space which has a finite topological (covering) dimension,
\dim(\partial G)<\infty
(see
[
18
]
).

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
Y
be a hyperbolic simplicial complex with a proper cocompact group action. Assume that
Y
is
m
-dimensional and
(m-1)
-connected where
m=\dim(\partial Y)+1
.
Then, there exists
\xi=\xi(Y)
such that for any group
G
that acts on
Y
properly and cocompactly, any
m
-dimensional,
(m-1)
-connected simplicial complex
L
on which
G
acts properly and cocompactly, and any continuous
G
-equivariant quasi-isometry
\Phi:L\to Y

N_{\xi}(\Phi(L))=Y
.

Proof.

Since
\Aut(Y)
acts cocompactly on
Y
, it suffices to show that the intersection
\Phi(L)\cap B_{\xi}\neq\emptyset
where
B_{\xi}
is the ball of radius
\xi
around some fixed point

in
Y
.

The orbit map of the action
G\curvearrowright Y
is a quasi-isometry which induces a boundary homeomorphism
\partial\Phi:\partial G\to\partial Y
.

By Bestvina-Mess
[
6
]
we have

m-1=\dim\partial Y=\max\{k\;|\;\check{H}^{k}(\partial Y)\neq 0\}

where
\check{H}^{k}
is the reduced

[
30
]

Consider the long exact sequence for the pair

in Čech cohomology

\check{H}^{m-1}(Y\cup\partial Y)\to\check{H}^{m-1}(\partial Y)\to\check{H}^{m}(Y\cup\partial Y,\partial Y).

Note that
\check{H}^{m}(Y\cup\partial Y,\partial Y)\simeq H_{c}^{m}(Y)
, where
H_{c}^{m}
is the
m
-th compactly supported cohomology with
\mathbb{Z}
coefficients. In
Lemma

7.2
we will prove
\check{H}^{m-1}(Y\cup\partial Y)=0

By the exact sequence we get the following embedding

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\check{H}^{m-1}(\partial Y)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{c}^{m}(Y\cup\partial Y,\partial Y)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D}

Similarly, for

,

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\check{H}^{m-1}(\partial L)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H_{c}^{m}(L\cup\partial L,\partial L).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D}

The map
\Phi\cup\partial\Phi
is a continuous map of the pairs

and by the naturality of the map

in the long exact sequence of the pair, we get the following commutative diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\check{H}^{m-1}(\partial Y)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{m}_{c}(Y)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\check{H}^{m-1}(\partial L)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces H^{m}_{c}(L)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\simeq}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\partial\Phi)^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Phi^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D}

Fix some

, then

is compactly supported, so there exists

such that

Assume for the sake of contradiction that
\Phi(L)\cap B_{\xi}=\emptyset

\Phi^{*}(D(a))=0

On the other hand,

is a homeomorphism, and so

is an isomorphism. By the commutative diagram we get the following contradiction

0=\Phi^{*}(D(a))=D((\partial\Phi)^{*}(a))\neq 0.

∎

Recall that for a graph

If

Lemma 7.2
.

Let

then
\check{H}^{m-1}(Y\cup\partial Y)=0

Proof.

Consider a contractible Rips complex

Since

\check{H}^{m-1}(Y\cup\partial Y)\hookrightarrow\check{H}^{m-1}(R^{(m)}\cup\partial R)

and so it suffices to show that
\check{H}^{m-1}(R^{(m)}\cup\partial R)=0
.

Consider the long exact sequence for the pair
(R\cup\partial R,R^{(m)}\cup\partial R)

\check{H}^{m-1}(R\cup\partial R)\to\check{H}^{m-1}(R^{(m)}\cup\partial R)\to\check{H}^{m}(R\cup\partial R,R^{(m)}\cup\partial R).

By
[
6
]
,
R\cup\partial R
is contractible so
\check{H}^{m-1}(R\cup\partial R)=0
. We also have

\check{H}^{m}(R\cup\partial R,R^{(m)}\cup\partial R)=H_{c}^{m}(R-R^{(m)})=0

since
R-R^{(m)}
has only cells of dimension
>m
. It follows from the long exact sequence that
\check{H}^{m-1}(R^{(m)}\cup\partial R)=0
as desired.
∎
