Gelfand Triplets, Continuous and Discrete Bases and Legendre Polynomials Preprint: AIP/123-QED
E. Celeghini 1, M. Gadella 2, M. A. del Olmo 2

Abstract
We consider a basis of square integrable functions on a rectangle, contained in ℝ 2 {\mathbb{R}}^{2} , constructed with Legendre polynomials, suitable, for instance, for the analogical description of images on the plane or in other fields of application of the Legendre polynomials in higher dimensions. After extending the Legendre polynomials to any arbitrary interval of the form [ a , b ] [a,b] , from its original form on [ − 1 , 1 ] [-1,1] , we generalize the basis of Legendre polynomials to two dimensions. This is the first step to generalize the basis to n n -dimensions. We present some mathematical constructions such as Gelfand triples appropriate on this context. “Smoothness” of functions on space of test functions and some other properties are revisited, as well as te continuity of generators of s ​ u ​ ( 1 , 1 ) su(1,1) on this context.

Bases on the Real Line and Orthogonal Polynomials

[a,b]
, either finite or infinite, i.e.,
-\infty\leq a<b\leq+\infty

cambanis
.
One standard procedure described in Quantum Mechanics textbooks comes after the association of, in general almost all
x\in[a,b]
with respect to some measure, a generalized vector,

|x\rangle
, outside the Hilbert space, although in the dual
\Phi^{\times}
of a Gelfand triplet (RHS) of the form given in (
2

celebrated Gelfand-Maurin theorem

;
ROB
;
ANT
;
MEL
;
GG1
;
GG2

role on the discussion in the sequel.

In most of examples studied so far
olmo2016
;
olmo2019
;
CGO22
;
CGO20
, we have the similar structure: an infinite dimensional separable Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{E_{a,b}}
equipped as in (
2
), where

E_{a,b}:=[a,b]\subset{\mathbb{R}}
. To all (or almost all with respect to some measure)
x\in[a,b]
, it corresponds a generalized vector on the dual,

|x\rangle
(related to
\langle x|\in\Phi^{\times}
) see (
2
), fulfilling some formal properties of orthogonality and completeness, which are usually presented as, respectively,

\langle x|y\rangle=\delta(x-y)\,,\qquad\int_{a}^{b}|x\rangle dx\langle x|={\mathbb{I}}\,,\qquad\quad\;\forall\;x,y\;\in\,E_{a,b}\,.

(3)

The abstract Hilbert space admits a representation
{L}^{2}(E_{a,b})
, where an orthonormal basis (complete orthonormal set) is a sequence of functions. In our examples, we
have chosen bases of special functions
\{F_{n}(x)\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
for which the orthonormality and completeness relations read, respectively, as

\int_{a}^{b}\,F_{n}(x)\;F_{m}(x)\;dx\;=\,\delta_{n,m}\,,\qquad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\;F_{n}(x)\;F_{n}(y)\;=\;\delta(x-y)\,,\quad\;\forall\;x,y\in E_{a,b}\,.

(4)

In each of our examples, we have constructed a Gelfand triple of the form

\Phi_{E_{a,b}}\subset L^{2}(E_{a,b})\subset\Phi^{\times}_{E_{a,b}}\,,

(5)

such that
F_{n}(x)\in\Phi_{E_{a,b}}
. We have shown that there exists a unitary operator,
U:{\mathcal{H}}_{E_{a,b}}\longmapsto{L}^{2}(E_{a,b})
, not necessarily unique, such
that if
\Phi=U\Phi_{E_{a,b}}
, then

\Phi\subset{\mathcal{H}}_{E_{a,b}}\subset\Phi^{\times}\,,

(6)

is an abstract Gelfand triple with the property that
\{|x\rangle\}_{x\in E_{a,b}}\subset\Phi^{\times}_{E_{a,b}}
.
Hereafter, we construct an orthonormal basis,
\{|n\rangle\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
on the abstract Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{E_{a,b}}

such that
|n\rangle:=UF_{n}
. It is shown that it satisfies the formal expression

|n\rangle\,:=\int_{a}^{b}\;|x\rangle\;F_{n}(x)\;dx\,.

(7)

Orthonormality and completeness relations for
\{|n\rangle\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
are given by the following respective relations:

\langle n|m\rangle\;=\;\delta_{n,m}\,,\qquad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\;|n\rangle\langle n|\;=\;{\mathbb{I}}\,,\quad\forall\;n,m\in{\mathbb{N}}\,,

(8)

where
\mathbb{I}
is the identity on
{\mathcal{H}}_{E_{a,b}}

In terms of the generalized vectors
\{|x\rangle\}_{x\in E_{a,b}}
and the orthonormal basis

, we may write the functions
\{F_{n}(x)\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

F_{n}(x)\;:=\;\langle x|n\rangle\;=\;\langle n|x\rangle\,.

(9)

An arbitrary vector

can also be expressed in terms of both, the orthonormal basis

as well as in the set of generalized
vectors

|f\rangle\;=\;\int_{a}^{b}\;|x\rangle\,dx\,\langle x|f\rangle\;\;=\;\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\;|n\rangle\;\langle n|f\rangle\,.

(10)

From this point of view, we may assert that the set

form a
generalized continuous basis
for all vectors

(11)

Obviously, the

\langle g|f\rangle\;=\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\;g_{n}^{*}\cdot f_{n}\;=\;\int_{a}^{b}g^{*}(x)\cdot f(x)dx\,,\qquad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|f_{n}|^{2}\;=\,\int_{a}^{b}|f(x)|^{2}dx\,.

(12)

III
Legendre polynomials and their generalization

The Legendre polynomials,

\ P_{n}(x):=\frac{1}{2^{n}\,n!}\,\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}\,(x^{2}-1)^{n}\,,

(15)

are defined on the interval
[-1,1]
and verify the following orthonormality and completeness relations:

\int_{-1}^{1}\,P_{n}(x)\,(n+1/2)\,P_{m}(x)\;dx\;=\,\delta_{n,m}\,,\qquad\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\;P_{n}(x)\,(n+1/2)\,P_{n}(y)\;=\;\delta(x-y)\,.

(16)

This means that the normalized Legendre polynomials (NLP) are

K_{n}(x):=\sqrt{n+1/2}\,\ P_{n}(x)\,.

(17)

They
are an orthonormal basis on
L^{2}[-1,1]

[-1,1]
. From this, we may construct an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}[a,b]

a

b

W_{n}(a,b,x):=\sqrt{\frac{2}{b-a}}\;\;\;K_{n}\left(\frac{2x}{b-a}-\frac{b+a}{b-a}\right)\,,\qquad\;n\in{\mathbb{N}}\,,\;\;a\leq x\leq b\,.

(18)

When
b-a=1-(-1)
, i.e., the interval
[a,b]
has the same width than
[-1,1]
, the argument of
P_{n}(x)
reduces to
x-(b+a)/2
. Functions

are the
generalized Legendre polynomials

[a,b]

IV
Legendre polynomials and
SU(1,1)

Let us define the following differential operators,
J_{\pm}
as

J_{\pm}=\frac{2}{b-a}\,\left[\pm\,(X-a)(X-b)D_{x}+\left(X-\frac{b+a}{2}\right)\,\left(N+\frac{1}{2}\pm\frac{1}{2}\right)\ \right]\,\sqrt{\frac{N+\frac{1}{2}\pm 1}{N+1/2}}\,,

(21)

where the position operator
X
and the derivative operator
D_{x}
act on functions of their own domain as
usual, i.e.,

(X\,f)(x)=x\,f(x)\,,\qquad D_{x}f(x)=\frac{d\,f(x)}{dx}\,,

(22)

and
N
is the so called
number operator
, acting on the GLP
W_{n}(a,b,x)
as

N\,W_{n}(a,b,x)=n\,\,W_{n}(a,b,x)\,,

(23)

The operators
J_{\pm}
act on
W_{n}(a,b,x)
as

J_{+}\,W_{n}(a,b,x)=(n+1)\,W_{n+1}(a,b,x)\,,\qquad J_{-}\,W_{n}(a,b,x)=n\,W_{n-1}(a,b,x)\,.

(24)

Equation (
21
) leads to the following pair of diagonal operators:

\begin{array}[]{lll}J_{+}\,J_{-}-N^{2}&=&\displaystyle-\frac{4}{(b-a)^{2}}\,(X-a)(X-b)\\[11.38092pt]
&&\displaystyle\;\times\left[-(X-a)(X-b)\,D_{x}^{2}\,-2\left(X-\frac{b+a}{2}\right)\,D_{x}\;+N(N+1)\right]\,,\end{array}

(25)

and

\begin{array}[]{lll}J_{-}\,J_{+}-(N+1)^{2}&=&\displaystyle-\frac{4}{(b-a)^{2}}\,(X-a)(X-b)\\[11.38092pt]
&&\displaystyle\;\times\left[-(X-a)(X-b)\,D_{x}^{2}\,-2\left(X-\frac{b+a}{2}\right)\,D_{x}\;+N(N+1)\right].\end{array}

(26)

They verify the following relations for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

\left(J_{+}\,J_{-}-N^{2}\right)\,W_{n}(a,b,x)=0\,,\qquad\left(J_{-}\,J_{+}-(N+1)^{2}\right)\,W_{n}(a,b,x)=0\,.

(27)

Let us define a new operator
J_{3}:=N+1/2
, so that for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

J_{3}\,W_{n}(a,b,x)=(n+1/2)\,W_{n}(a,b,x)\,.

(28)

These operators close the following commutation relations on the linear space spanned by the set of GLP

\{W_{n}(a,b,x)\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

[J_{3}\,,J_{\pm}]=\pm\,J_{\pm}\,,\qquad\quad[\,J_{+}\,,J_{-}\,]\,=\,-2\,J_{3}\,.

(29)

These are the commutation relations corresponding to the Lie algebra
su(1,1)
. In addition to the commutation relations (
29
), we have the anticommutation
rule
\{J_{+},J_{-}\}=-2J_{3}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}
.

The linear space spanned by the set of GLP,
\{W_{n}(a,b,x)\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, for every pair of real numbers
a<b
, supports a unitary irreducible representation
of the group
SU(1,1)
with quadratic Casimir
\mathcal{C}=-1/4
, so that

\left({\mathcal{C}}\,+\frac{1}{4}\right)\,W_{n}(a,b,x)\equiv\left(J_{3}^{2}\,-\frac{1}{2}\,\{J_{+},J_{-}\}+\frac{1}{4}\right)\,W_{n}(a,b,x)=0\,,

(30)

which comes after (
25
) and (
26
). Equation (
29
) is nothing else that the generalization of the usual Legendre equation on
[-1,1]
given as

\left(X\,D_{x}^{2}+D_{x}-\frac{1}{4}\,X+N+\frac{1}{2}\right)\,P_{n}(x)=0\,,

(31)

to the interval
[a,b]
.

On the smoothness of functions on

It is clear that the operators

(

continuous. This goes through the construction of a suitable
Gelfand triplet or rigged Hilbert space. Let us start with this construction for

and then, generalize the procedure to

Thus, our goal is the construction of a triplet

,  for which we just need to determine the space

\Phi_{a,b}
. Let

be an arbitrary function in

, we know that this function can be spanned as

(42)

Let us choose
\Phi_{a,b}
as the space of functions

, as given in (
42
), for which

(43)

Note that, for
k=0
, we have the Hilbert space norm. This is a sequence of seminorms, in fact norms, that produce a locally convex topology on
\Phi_{a,b}
. Since this sequence
of seminorms, contains the Hilbert space norm, then, the topology on
\Phi_{a,b}
is finer than the Hilbert space norm topology. Further considerations show that
\Phi_{a,b}

with this topology is a complete metrizable space, and hence Frèchet
simon1980
.

The series in (

(44)

Then, for any

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}f^{m}\,W_{m}(a,b,x)\right|\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|f^{m}|\,|W_{m}(a,b,x)|\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{b-a}}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|f^{m}|\,\frac{m+1}{m+1}.

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{2}{b-a}}\;\sqrt{\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|f^{m}|^{2}(m+1)^{2}}\;\sqrt{\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(m+1)^{2}}}\,,

(45)

where the last inequality is just the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality. All the series converge in this last term. Then, after the Weierstrass M-Theorem, the considered series

Before proceeding with our presentation, let us give an important result: let

be a linear operator on a locally convex space
\Phi

This result will be applied in the sequel in order to prove the continuity of the generators of

and other linear transformations that we introduce in the sequel.

, we have

x\,f(a,b,x)=x\langle a,b,x|f\rangle=(Qf)(a,b,x)=\langle a,b,x|\widetilde{Q}f\rangle\,.

(82)

Consequently,

x\langle a,b,x|=\langle a,b,x|\widetilde{Q}\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;\widetilde{Q}|a,b,x\rangle=x\,|a,b,x\rangle\,,

(83)

so that each of the functionals
|a,b,x\rangle\in\Phi^{\times}
is an eigenfunctional of the multiplication operator with eigenvalue
x\in[a,b]
.

From the above discussion, it is obvious that
|m\rangle\in\Phi
,
m=0,1,2,\dots
. This allows us to give a second interpretation to the identity on
\mathcal{H}
given as

I:=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}|m\rangle\langle m|\,.

(84)

According to definitions (
78
) and (
79
), we have that
\langle m|a,b,x\rangle=W_{m}(a,b,x)
, so that

|a,b,x\rangle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}|m\rangle\langle m|a,b,x\rangle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}W_{m}(a,b,x)\,|m\rangle\,.

(85)

If
|F\rangle
were an arbitrary functional in
\Phi^{\times}
, we would have

|F\rangle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}|m\rangle\langle m|F\rangle\,,

(86)

so that
I
as in (
84
) can be looked at an identity on
\Phi^{\times}
and this identity provides a nice series representation of each functional in
\Phi^{\times}
. This series converges in the sense that

\langle f|F\rangle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\langle f|m\rangle\langle m|F\rangle\,,\qquad\forall f\in\Phi\,.

(87)

Unitary mappings preserve the scalar product, so that if
f,g\in\Phi
and
f(a,b,x)=U^{-1}f
and
g(a,b,x)=U^{-1}g
, one has

\langle f|g\rangle=\int_{a}^{b}f^{*}(a,b,x)g(a,b,x)\,dx=\int_{a}^{b}\langle f|a,b,x\rangle\langle a,b,x|g\rangle\,dx\,.

(88)

If we omit the arbitrary
f\in\Phi
, we have the following formal expression:

|g\rangle=\int_{a}^{b}\langle a,b,x|g\rangle\,|a,b,x\rangle\,dx\,.

(89)

This formal expression gives an split of each
g\in\Phi
in terms of the so called
continuous basis

\{|a,b,x\rangle\}
, where
a
and
b
are fixed real numbers with
a<b
and
x\in[a,b]
. In (
85
), we have written the elements of this continuous basis in terms of the discrete basis (or orthonormal basis)
\{|m\rangle\}
. Then an inversion formula should be in order. Obviously, this comes directly from (
89
), since for all
m=0,1,2,\dots
, we have

|m\rangle=\int_{a}^{b}\langle a,b,x|m\rangle\,|a,b,x\rangle\,dx=\int_{a}^{b}W_{m}(a,b,x)\,|a,b,x\rangle\,dx\,.

(90)

Also, if we omit the arbitrary
f,g\in\Phi
in (
88
), we obtain the following formal identity:

\mathcal{I}=\int_{a}^{b}|a,b,x\rangle\langle a,b,x|\,dx\,.

(91)

Since after (
89
)
\mathcal{I}g=g
, this
\mathcal{I}
as in (
91
) represents the canonical injection
\mathcal{I}:\Phi\longmapsto\Phi^{\times}
.

VI.2.2
On the essential self-adjointness of
Q
on
\Phi_{a,b}
.

After the definition (
67
) and its continuity on
\Phi_{a,b}
, one sees that
Q
is symmetric on
\Phi_{a,b}
.
Effectively, we have to prove that
\langle Q\,f,g\rangle=\langle f,Q\,g\rangle
for all
f,g\in\Phi_{a,b}
. Taking into account (
67
), we have that

\begin{array}[]{lll}\langle Q\,f,g\rangle=\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}K^{m}\,W_{m}(a,b,x)\right)^{*}\,\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}g^{n}\,W_{n}(a,b,x)\right)\,dx&=&\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}[K^{m}]^{*}\,g^{m}\\[11.38092pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\alpha\,[f^{m}]^{*}\,g^{m}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\beta_{m-1}\,[f^{m-1}]^{*}\,g^{m}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{m+1}\,[f^{m+1}]^{*}g^{m}\,.\end{array}

(92)

We also have that

\langle f,Q\,g\rangle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}[f^{m}]^{*}\,\left(\alpha\,g^{m}+\beta_{m-1}\,g^{m-1}+\gamma_{m+1}\,g^{m+1}\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\,\alpha\,[f^{m}]^{*}\,g^{m}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\beta_{m-1}\,[f^{m}]^{*}\,g^{m-1}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{m+1}\,[f^{m}]^{*}\,g^{m+1}\,.

(93)

From the series of (
92
) we arrive to

\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\beta_{m-1}\,[f^{m-1}]^{*}g^{m}\;\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m-1=n}}{{=}}\;\;\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\beta_{n}\,[f^{n}]^{*}g^{n+1}\,,\qquad\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{m+1}\,[f^{m+1}]^{*}g^{m}\;\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle m+1=n}}{{=}}\;\;\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}\,[f^{n}]^{*}g^{n-1}

(94)

Using the definitions (
65
) we easily proof that
\beta_{n}=\gamma_{n+1}
and
\gamma_{n}=\beta_{m-1}
. Hence we arrive to

\langle Q\,f,g\rangle=\langle f,Q\,g\rangle
,
\forall\,f,g\in\Phi_{a,b}
.

Then, in order to prove the essential self adjointness of
Q
on
\Phi_{a,b}
, we need to show that the range of
Q\pm iI
, where
I
is the identity operator, is dense on
L^{2}[a,b]
. Equivalently, we may show that if there exists a function
f\in L^{2}[a,b]
such that for all
m=0,1,2,\dots

\langle f|(Q\pm iI)\,W_{m}\rangle=0\,

(95)

then, this function is the zero function. Let us show that this is the case with
+i
, the case with
-i
being identical. For an arbitrary value of
m=0,1,2,\dots
, we have that, after (
64
), we have

\langle f|(Q+iI)\,W_{m}\rangle=(\alpha+i)\langle f|W_{m}\rangle+\beta_{m-1}\langle f|W_{m-1}\rangle+\gamma_{m+1}\langle f|W_{m+1}\rangle=0\,.

(96)

Note that
c_{m}:=\langle f|W_{m}\rangle
is the
m
-th component of
f
in the orthonormal basis
\{W_{m}(a,b,x)\}
, so that
f(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}c_{m}\,W_{m}(a,b,x)
in the norm sense. This gives the following sequence of equations (
\beta_{-1}=0
):

(\alpha+i)c_{m}+\beta_{m-1}c_{m-1}+\gamma_{m+1}c_{m+1}=0\,,\qquad m=0,1,2,\dots\,.

(97)

It is interesting to see that all
c_{m}
may be written in terms of
c_{0}
. Let us begin with (
97
) with
m=0
. It gives:

(\alpha+i)c_{0}+\gamma_{1}c_{1}=0\Longrightarrow c_{1}=-\frac{\alpha+i}{\gamma_{1}}\,c_{0}\,.

(98)

For
m=1
, one has

(\alpha+i)c_{1}+\beta_{0}c_{0}+\gamma_{2}c_{2}=0\Longrightarrow c_{2}=-\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}}\left[\frac{(\alpha+i)^{2}}{\gamma_{1}}+\beta_{0}\right]c_{0}\,.

(99)

Similar equations for
m=3,4
give, respectively,

c_{3}=\left\{-\frac{1}{\gamma_{3}}(\alpha+i)\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}}\left[\frac{(\alpha+i)^{2}}{\gamma_{1}}+\beta_{0}\right]+\frac{(\alpha+i)\beta_{1}}{\gamma_{3}\gamma_{1}}\right\}c_{0}\,,

(100)

and

c_{4}=\left\{\frac{1}{\gamma_{4}}(\alpha+i)\frac{1}{\gamma_{3}}(\alpha+i)\frac{1}{\gamma_{2}}\left[\frac{(\alpha+i)^{2}}{\gamma_{1}}+\beta_{0}\right]+\frac{(\alpha+i)\beta_{1}}{\gamma_{3}\gamma_{1}}-\frac{\beta_{2}}{\gamma_{4}\gamma_{2}}\left[\frac{(\alpha+i)^{2}}{\gamma_{1}}+\beta_{0}\right]\right\}c_{0}\,.

(101)

We have obtained enough terms to understand the behaviour of
c_{m}=[-]_{m}c_{0}
. In fact,
[-]_{m}
is the sum of fractions having the same number of terms in the numerator and the denominator. A term by term analysis shows that
[-]_{m}
does not go to zero as
m\longmapsto\infty
. For instance, let us study the first term in
[-]_{m}
, which has the following form:

\displaystyle\frac{(\alpha+i)^{m}}{\gamma_{m}\gamma_{m-1}\dots\gamma_{1}}=\left[\frac{a+b+2i}{2}\right]^{m}\left(\frac{b-a}{2}\right)^{-m}\,C_{m}^{-1}C_{m-1}^{-1}\dots C_{1}^{-1}\,,

(102)

where

C_{m}=\frac{m}{2m+1}\sqrt{\frac{m+1/2}{m-1/2}}\,.

(103)

Observe that the sequence with general term
C_{m}
is a decreasing sequence, with
1/2<C_{m}\leq\sqrt{3}/3
. Then,
C_{m}^{-1}C_{m-1}^{-1}\dots C_{1}^{-1}>(3/\sqrt{3})^{m}
. Also consider

\left[\frac{a+b+2i}{2}\right]^{m}\left(\frac{b-a}{2}\right)^{-m}=\left[\frac{a+b}{b-a}+\frac{2i}{b-a}\right]^{m}\,.

(104)

This is a complex number with modulus

\left[\left|\frac{a+b}{b-a}\right|^{2}+\frac{4}{(b-a)^{2}}\right]^{m/2}\,,

(105)

so that if
a
and
b
had the same sign, it results that (
105
) goes to infinite as
m\longmapsto\infty
. This may not be the case if
b
were positive and
a
negative. A similar analysis can be made of all other terms except the last one, which contain a term of the form
(\alpha+i)^{p}
in the numerator and a product of
p
terms of the form
\gamma_{i}
(indeed more) in the denominator. Then, if
a
and
b
have different sign they may go to zero as
m
goes to infinite. We even take into account that the number of terms in
c_{m}
increase with
m
, the sum may go to zero. So far the situation is not clear.
The difference is in the last term, which has the form,

r_{m}:=\frac{\beta_{2m-2}\dots\beta_{0}}{\gamma_{2m}\dots\gamma_{2}}\,.

(106)

Let us show that the quotient
\beta_{2m-2}/\gamma_{2m}>1
,
m=1,2\dots
. Note that all therms of the form
(b-a)/2
cancel out here. In fact,

\frac{\beta_{2m-2}}{\gamma_{2m}}=\sqrt{\frac{2m-3/2}{2m+1/2}}\,\frac{2m-1}{4m-3}\,\frac{4m+1}{2m}\,.

(107)

Let us square the right hand side in (
107
) and then, multiply by 2 numerator and denominator in the first factor. We obtain,

\frac{4m-3}{4m+1}\,\frac{(2m-1)^{2}}{(4m-3)^{2}}\,\frac{(4m+1)^{2}}{4m^{2}}=\frac{(2m-1)^{2}}{4m^{2}}\,\frac{4m+1}{4m-3}=\frac{N}{D}\,,

(108)

where
N
and
D
stands for numerator and denominator, respectively. Note that
N=D+1
, so that
N>D
and, hence,
\sqrt{N/D}>1
.
Then, the expression in (
106
) is always bigger than one and therefore, the series
\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}|r_{m}|^{2}
always diverges. The conclusion is that the series
\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}|c_{m}|^{2}
always diverges, so that it does not exist a function
f(x)\in L^{2}[a,b]
verifying (
95
) for all
m=0,1,2,\dots
. The essentially self adjointness of
Q
is, then, proved.

However
D_{x}
is not even symmetric. Effectively, from (
51
) and for short
W_{m}\equiv W_{m}(a,b,x)
we can compute, for instance,

\langle W_{m}|D_{x}W_{m+1}\rangle=\frac{2}{b-a}\,(2n+1)\,\frac{\sqrt{n+3/2}}{\sqrt{n+1/2}}\,\langle W_{m}|W_{m}\rangle=\frac{2}{b-a}\,(2n+1)\,\frac{\sqrt{n+3/2}}{\sqrt{n+1/2}}\neq 0\,.

(109)

On the other hand,

\langle D_{x}W_{m}|W_{m+1}\rangle=0\Longrightarrow\langle D_{x}W_{m}|W_{m+1}\rangle\neq\langle W_{m}|D_{x}W_{m+1}\rangle\,.

(110)

The point is that if
f(x)\in\Phi_{a,b}
, in general
f(b)\neq 0
and
f(a)\neq 0
, so that

\langle D_{x}f|g\rangle=\int_{a}^{b}f^{\prime}(x)\,g(x)\,dx=f(b)g(b)-f(a)g(a)-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\,g^{\prime}(x)\,dx\,.

(111)

Nevertheless, the action of
D_{x}\pm iI
on
\Phi_{a,b}
is dense in
L^{2}[a,b]
. The proof is a bit cumbersome, although not complicated.

VII
On the continuity of the generators of
su(1,1)

To begin with, we should first define the action of
J_{\pm}
on
\Phi_{a,b}
, which could be made after (
24
).
Thus, the action of
J_{+}
on
f(x)\in\Phi_{a,b}
should be defined as

J_{+}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}f^{m}\,W_{m}(a,b,x):=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}f^{m}\,(m+1)\,W_{m+1}(a,b,x)\,.

(112)

We need to show that this is a good definition in the sense that the sum converges to an element of
\Phi_{a,b}
and that
J_{+}
is continuous on
\Phi_{a,b}
. The former is just
a trivial consequence of the definition of
\Phi_{a,b}
(it is obviously linear). To prove the continuity, take any seminorm
p_{k}
, then, for any
f(x)\in\Phi_{a,b}
we have

\displaystyle[p_{k}(J_{+}f)]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|f^{m}|^{2}\,(m+1)^{2}\,(m+2)^{2k}\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\,|f^{m}|^{2}\,(m+1)^{2}\,(2m+2)^{2k}

(113)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2^{2k}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\,|f^{m}|^{2}\,(m+1)^{2(k+1)}=2^{2k}\,[p_{k+1}f]^{2}\,,\quad k=0,1,2,\dots\,,

which, after (
46
), shows the continuity of
J_{+}
on
\Phi_{a,b}
.

The definition of
J_{-}
on
\Phi_{a,b}
should be given by

J_{-}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}f^{m}\,W_{m}(a,b,x):=\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}f^{m}\,m\,W_{m-1}(a,b,x)\,.

(114)

The operator
J_{-}
is obviously linear and well defined on
\Phi_{a,b}
. Furthermore, for all
f(x)\in\Phi_{a,b}

[p_{k}(J_{-}f)]^{2}=\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}|f^{m}|^{2}\,m^{2}\,(m-1+1)^{2k}\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}|f^{m}|^{2}\,(m+1)^{2(k+1)}=[p_{k+1}f]^{2}\,,\quad k=0,1,2,\dots\,.

(115)

This shows the continuity of
J_{-}
on
\Phi_{a,b}
.

Analogously, we define
J_{3}
on
\Phi_{a,b}
as

J_{3}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}f^{m}\,W_{m}(a,b,x)=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}f^{m}\,\left(m+\frac{1}{2}\right)\,W_{m}(a,b,x)\,.

(116)

By repeating the same argument, we show that
J_{3}
is well defined and continuous on
\Phi_{a,b}
. Note that all the three operators, as well as their products and linear combinations
may be extended by continuity to the dual
\Phi^{\times}_{a,b}
, provided that this dual be endowed with any topology compatible with the dual pair, usually the weak
or the strong topologies
HOR
.

These results may be extended to the general situation studied in Section 5. In the simplest case of dimension equal to two, an arbitrary function

f(\mathbf{x})\in L^{2}([\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}]^{2})
can be written as (
42
)

f(\mathbf{x})\equiv f(x_{1},x_{2})=\sum_{{\mathbf{m}}={\mathbf{0}}}^{\infty}\;f_{[{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}]}^{\mathbf{m}}\;W_{\mathbf{m}}({{\mathbf{a}}},{{\mathbf{b}}},{{\mathbf{x}}})=\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}=0}^{\infty}f^{m_{1},m_{2}}_{[{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}]}\,W_{m_{1}}(a_{1},b_{1},x_{1})\,W_{m_{2}}(a_{2},b_{2},x_{2})\,.

(117)

The space
\Phi_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
is defined as the space of functions
f(x_{1},x_{2})\in L^{2}([\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}])
such that (see (
39
))

[p_{k_{1},k_{2}}f]^{2}:=\sum_{{m_{1}},{m_{2}}=0}^{\infty}\big|f^{m_{1},m_{2}}_{[{\mathbf{a}},{\mathbf{b}}]}\big|^{2}\,(m_{1}+1)^{2k_{1}}\,(m_{2}+1)^{2k_{2}}<\infty\,,\quad k_{1},k_{2}=0,1,2,\dots\,.

(118)

All the tensor products of the operators
J_{\pm}
and
J_{3}
(also their linear combinations and products) act linearly and are continuous on
\Phi_{\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}}
. Proofs are
exactly as above for dimension one. The extension of all these ideas to n dimensions is straightforward.

VIII
Concluding remarks

In this paper we present a generalization of the Legendre polynomials to any finite interval
[a,b]
of the real line.
These new functions,
W(m,a,b,x)
, constitute an orthonormal and countable basis for the square integrable functions defined in this interval. We use these functions to construct bases in ‘
n
-rectangular’ domains

\mathcal{R}^{n}=[a_{1},b_{1}]\times[a_{2},b_{2}]\times\dots\times[a_{n},b_{n}]\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}

as
W(m_{1},a_{1},b_{1},x_{1})\otimes W(m_{2},a_{2},b_{2},x_{2})\otimes\dots\otimes W(m_{n},a_{n},b_{n},x_{n})
.

A practical application of this construction could be the reconstruction of images in a kind of ‘analogical’ procedure in 2D or 3D either in black and white or in color. To this end, we span some function in terms of series of
W
-functions and just keep a finite number of terms.
Note that from the enormous contents of information of an image, we -the humans- can just use a minimal part. The problem is to give a criteria on how to obtain the relevant part of the information.

Using these Legendre polynomials, we construct Gelfand a triplet supporting a representation of
su(1,1)
by continuous operators on that triplet. We show a property of smoothness on this triplet and the possibility of constructing a multiplication operator on it, which enables us for the construction of so called
continuous
bases. Relations between usual
discrete
orthonormal basis and continuous bases are well established. All these notions may be extended to
n
-dimensional systems.
