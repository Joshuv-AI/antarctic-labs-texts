Kronecker-Factored Approximate Curvature for Physics-Informed Neural Networks
Felix Dangel, Johannes Müller *, Marius Zeinhofer *

Abstract
Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) are infamous for being hard to train. Recently, second-order methods based on natural gradient and Gauss-Newton methods have shown promising performance, improving the accuracy achieved by first-order methods by several orders of magnitude. While promising, the proposed methods only scale to networks with a few thousand parameters due to the high computational cost to evaluate, store, and invert the curvature matrix. We propose Kronecker-factored approximate curvature (KFAC) for PINN losses that greatly reduces the computational cost and allows scaling to much larger networks. Our approach goes beyond the established KFAC for traditional deep learning problems as it captures contributions from a PDE’s differential operator that are crucial for optimization. To establish KFAC for such losses, we use Taylor-mode automatic differentiation to describe the differential operator’s computation graph as a forward network with shared weights. This allows us to apply KFAC thanks to a recently developed general formulation for networks with weight sharing. Empirically, we find that our KFAC-based optimizers are competitive with expensive second-order methods on small problems, scale more favorably to higher-dimensional neural networks and PDEs, and consistently outperform first-order methods and LBFGS.

Introduction

Neural network-based approaches to numerically solve partial differential equations (PDEs) are growing at an unprecedented speed.
The idea to train network parameters to minimize the residual of a PDE traces back to at least
Dissanayake & Phan-Thien [15]
,
Lagaris et al. [28]
, but was only recently popularized under the name
deep Galerkin method
(DGM) and
Physics-informed neural networks
(PINNs) through the works of
Sirignano & Spiliopoulos [52]
,
Raissi et al. [50]
.
PINNs are arguably one of the most popular network-based approaches to the numerical solution of PDEs as they are easy to implement, seamlessly incorporate measurement data, and promise to work well in high dimensions.
Despite their immense popularity, PINNs are notoriously difficult to optimize
[
57
]
and fail to provide satisfactory accuracy when trained with first-order methods, even for simple problems
[
64
,
41
]
.
Recently, second-order methods that use the function space geometry to design preconditioners have shown remarkable promise in addressing the training difficulties of PINNs
[
64
,
41
,
14
,
24
,
42
]
.
However, these methods require solving a linear system in the network’s high-dimensional parameter space at cubic computational iteration cost, which prohibits scaling such approaches.
To address this, we build on the idea of Kronecker-factored approximate curvature (KFAC) and apply it to Gauss-Newton matrices of PINN losses which greatly reduces the computational cost:

•

We use higher-order forward (Taylor) mode automatic differentiation to interpret the computation graph of a network’s input derivatives as a larger net with weight sharing (
Section

3.1
).

•

We use this weight sharing view to propose KFAC for Gauss-Newton matrices of objectives with differential operators, like PINN losses (
Sections

3.3
and
14
).
Thanks to the generality of Taylor-mode and KFAC for weight sharing layers
[
17
]
, our approach is widely applicable.

•

We show that, for specific differential operators, the weight sharing in Taylor-mode can be further reduced by absorbing the reduction of partial derivatives into the forward propagation, producing a more efficient scheme.
For the prominent example of the Laplace operator, this recovers and generalizes the
forward Laplacian
framework
[
29
]
(
Sections

3.2
and
9
).

•

Empirically, we find that our KFAC-based optimizers are competitive with expensive second-order methods on small problems, scale more favorably to higher-dimensional neural networks and PDEs, and consistently outperform first-order methods and LBFGS (
Section

4
).

2
Background

For simplicity, we present our approach for multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) consisting of fully-connected and element-wise activation layers.
However, the generality of Taylor-mode automatic differentiation and KFAC for linear layers with weight sharing allows our KFAC to be applied to such layers (e.g. fully-connected, convolution, attention) in arbitrary neural network architectures.

Flattening & Derivatives

We vectorize matrices using the
first-index-varies-fastest
convention, i.e. column-stacking (row index varies first, column index varies second) and denote the corresponding flattening operation by
\flatten
.
This allows to reduce derivatives of matrix- or tensor-valued objects back to the vector case by flattening a function’s input and output before differentiation.
The Jacobian of a vector-to-vector function
{\bm{a}}\mapsto{\bm{b}}({\bm{a}})
has entries
[\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{a}}}{\bm{b}}]_{i,j}=\nicefrac{{\partial{b}_{i}}}{{\partial{a}_{j}}}
.
For a matrix-to-matrix function
{\bm{A}}\mapsto{\bm{B}}({\bm{A}})
, the Jacobian is
\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{A}}}{\bm{B}}=\mathrm{J}_{\flatten{\bm{A}}}\flatten{\bm{B}}
.
A useful property of
\flatten
is
\flatten({\bm{A}}{\bm{X}}{\bm{B}})=({\bm{B}}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{A}})\flatten{{\bm{X}}}
for matrices
{\bm{A}},{\bm{X}},{\bm{B}}
which implies
\mathrm{J}_{\bm{X}}({\bm{A}}{\bm{X}}{\bm{B}})={\bm{B}}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{A}}
.

Sequential neural nets

Consider a
sequential neural network

u_{{\bm{\theta}}}=f_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(L)}}\circ f_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(L-1)}}\circ\ldots\circ f_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)}}
of depth
L\in\mathbb{N}
. It consists of layers
f_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l-1)}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l)}}
,
{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}\mapsto{\bm{z}}^{(l)}=f_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}}({\bm{z}}^{(l-1)})
with trainable parameters
{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p^{(l)}}
that transform an input
{\bm{z}}^{(0)}\coloneqq{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\coloneqq h^{(0)}}
into a prediction
u_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}})={\bm{z}}^{(L)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(L)}}
via intermediate representations
{\bm{z}}^{(l)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l)}}
.
In the context of PINNs, we use networks with scalar outputs (
h^{(L)}=1
) and denote the concatenation of all parameters by
{\bm{\theta}}=({\bm{\theta}}^{(1)\top},\dots,{\bm{\theta}}^{(L)\top})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
.
A common choice is to alternate fully-connected and activation layers.
Linear layers map
{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}\mapsto{\bm{z}}^{(l)}={\bm{W}}^{(l)}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}
using a weight matrix
{\bm{W}}^{(l)}=\flatten^{-1}{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l)}\times h^{(l-1)}}
(bias terms can be added as an additional column and by appending a
1
to the input).
Activation layers map
{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}\mapsto{\bm{z}}^{(l)}=\sigma({\bm{z}}^{(l-1)})
element-wise for a (typically smooth)
\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.

2.1
Energy Natural Gradients for Physics-Informed Neural Networks

Let us consider a domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the partial differential equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}u=f\quad\text{in }\Omega\,,\qquad u=g\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega\,,

with right-hand side
f
, boundary data
g
and a differential operator

\mathcal{L}
, e.g. the negative Laplacian
-\mathcal{L}u=\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}u=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{{x}_{i}}^{2}u
.
We parametrize
u
with a neural net and train its parameters
{\bm{\theta}}
to minimize the loss

\displaystyle\begin{split}L({\bm{\theta}})&=\frac{1}{2N_{\Omega}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\Omega}}(\mathcal{L}u_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}_{n})-f({\bm{x}}_{n}))^{2}+\frac{1}{2N_{\partial\Omega}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\partial\Omega}}(u_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}^{\text{b}}_{n})-g({\bm{x}}^{\text{b}}_{n}))^{2}\\
&\eqqcolon L_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})+L_{\partial\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})\end{split}

(1)

with points
\{{\bm{x}}_{n}\in\Omega\}_{n=1}^{N_{\Omega}}
from the domain’s interior, and points
\{{\bm{x}}^{\text{b}}_{n}\in\partial\Omega\}_{n=1}^{N_{\partial\Omega}}
on its boundary.

1

The second regression loss can also include other constraints like measurement data.

First-order optimizers like gradient descent and Adam struggle at producing satisfactory solutions when used to train PINNs
[
9
]
.
Instead, function space-inspired second-order methods have lately shown promising results
[
42
]
.
We focus on
energy natural gradient descent (ENGD
[
41
]
)
which—applied to PINN objectives like (
1
)—corresponds to the Gauss-Newton method
[
6
, Chapter 6.3]
.
ENGD mimics Newton’s method
in function space
up to a projection onto the model’s tangent space and a discretization error that vanishes quadratically in the step size, thus providing locally optimal residual updates.
Alternatively, the Gauss-Newton method can be motivated from the standpoint of operator preconditioning, where the Gauss-Newton matrix leads to optimal conditioning of the problem
[
14
]
.

Natural gradient methods perform parameter updates via a preconditioned gradient descent scheme
{\bm{\theta}}\leftarrow{\bm{\theta}}-\alpha{\bm{G}}({\bm{\theta}})^{+}\nabla L({\bm{\theta}})
, where
{\bm{G}}({\bm{\theta}})^{+}
denotes the pseudo-inverse of a suitable
Gramian matrix

{\bm{G}}({\bm{\theta}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D\times D}
and
\alpha
is a step size.
ENGD for the PINN loss (
1
) uses the Gramian

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\bm{G}}({\bm{\theta}})&=\frac{1}{N_{\Omega}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\Omega}}\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\mathcal{L}u_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}_{n})\right)^{\top}\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\mathcal{L}u_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}_{n})+\frac{1}{N_{\partial\Omega}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\partial\Omega}}\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}u_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}_{n}^{\text{b}})\right)^{\top}\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}u_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}_{n}^{\text{b}})\\
&\eqqcolon{\bm{G}}_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})+{\bm{G}}_{\partial\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})\,.\end{split}

(2)

(
2
) is the Gauss-Newton matrix of the residual
{\bm{r}}({\bm{\theta}})=(\nicefrac{{{\bm{r}}_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})^{\top}}}{{\sqrt{N_{\Omega}}}},\nicefrac{{{\bm{r}}_{\partial\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})^{\top}}}{{\sqrt{N_{\partial\Omega}}}})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{\Omega}+N_{\partial\Omega}}
with interior and boundary residuals
r_{\Omega,n}({\bm{\theta}})=\mathcal{L}u_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}_{n})-f({\bm{x}}_{n})
and
r_{\partial\Omega,n}({\bm{\theta}})=u_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}_{n}^{\text{b}})-g({\bm{x}}_{n}^{\text{b}})
.

2.2
Kronecker-factored Approximate Curvature

We review Kronecker-factored approximate curvature (KFAC) which was introduced by
Heskes [22]
,
Martens & Grosse [38]
in the context of maximum likelihood estimation to approximate the per-layer Fisher information matrix by a Kronecker product to speed up approximate natural gradient descent
[
1
]
.
The Fisher associated with the loss
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2N}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left\lVert u_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}_{n})-y_{n}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
with targets
y_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
is

{\bm{F}}({\bm{\theta}})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}u_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}_{n})\right)^{\top}\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}u_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}_{n})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}u_{n}\right)^{\top}\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}}u_{n}\quad\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D\times D}\,,

(3)

where
u_{n}=u_{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{x}}_{n})
, and it coincides with the classical Gauss-Newton matrix
[
37
]
.
The established KFAC approximates (
3
).
While the boundary Gramian
{\bm{G}}_{\partial\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
has the same structure as
{\bm{F}}({\bm{\theta}})
, the interior Gramian
{\bm{G}}_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
does not as it involves derivative rather than function evaluations of the net.

KFAC tackles the Fisher’s per-layer block diagonal,
{\bm{F}}({\bm{\theta}})\approx\operatorname{diag}({\bm{F}}^{(1)}({\bm{\theta}}),\dots,{\bm{F}}^{(L)}({\bm{\theta}}))
with
{\bm{F}}^{(l)}({\bm{\theta}})=\nicefrac{{1}}{{N}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}}u_{n})^{\top}\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}}u_{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p^{(l)}\times p^{(l)}}
.
For a fully-connected layer’s block, let’s examine the term
\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}}u_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}})
from
Equation

3
for a fixed data point.
The layer parameters
{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}=\flatten{\bm{W}}^{(l)}
enter the computation via
{\bm{z}}^{(l)}={\bm{W}}^{(l)}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}
and we have
\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{W}}^{(l)}}{\bm{z}}^{(l)}={{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{I}}

[
10
, e.g.]
.
Further, the chain rule gives the decomposition
\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{W}}^{(l)}}u=(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{z}}^{(l)}}u)\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{W}}^{(l)}}{\bm{z}}^{(l)}={{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}}^{\top}\otimes\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{z}}^{(l)}}u
.
Inserting into
{\bm{F}}^{(l)}({\bm{\theta}})
, summing over data points, and using the expectation approximation
\sum_{n}{\bm{A}}_{n}\otimes{\bm{B}}_{n}\approx N^{-1}(\sum_{n}{\bm{A}}_{n})\otimes(\sum_{n}{\bm{B}}_{n})
from
Martens & Grosse [38]
, we obtain the KFAC approximation for linear layers in supervised square loss regression with a network’s output,

{\bm{F}}^{(l)}({\bm{\theta}})\approx\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}_{n}{{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}_{n}}^{\top}\right)}_{\eqqcolon{\bm{A}}^{(l)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l-1)}\times h^{(l-1)}}}\otimes\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{z}}^{(l)}}u_{n}\right)^{\top}\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{z}}^{(l)}}u_{n}\right)}_{\eqqcolon{\bm{B}}^{(l)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l)}\times h^{(l)}}}\,.

(4)

It is cheap to store and invert by inverting the two Kronecker factors.

3
Kronecker-Factored Approximate Curvature for
PINNs

ENGD’s Gramian is a sum of PDE and boundary Gramians,
{\bm{G}}({\bm{\theta}})={\bm{G}}_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})+{\bm{G}}_{\partial\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
.
We will approximate each Gramian separately with a block diagonal matrix with Kronecker-factored blocks,
{\bm{G}}_{\bullet}({\bm{\theta}})\approx\diag({\bm{G}}^{(1)}_{\bullet}({\bm{\theta}}),\dots,{\bm{G}}^{(L)}_{\bullet}({\bm{\theta}}))
for
\bullet\in\{\Omega,\partial\Omega\}
with
{\bm{G}}^{(l)}_{\bullet}({\bm{\theta}})\approx{\bm{A}}^{(l)}_{\bullet}\otimes{\bm{B}}^{(l)}_{\bullet}
.
For the boundary Gramian
{\bm{G}}_{\partial\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
, we can re-use the established KFAC from
Equation

4
as its loss corresponds to regression over the network’s output.
The interior Gramian
{\bm{G}}_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
, however, involves PDE terms in the form of network derivatives and therefore
cannot
be approximated with the existing KFAC.
It requires a new approximation that we develop here for the running example of the Poisson equation and more general PDEs (
Equations

9
and
14
).
To do so, we need to make the dependency between the weights and the differential operator
\mathcal{L}u
explicit.
We use Taylor-mode automatic differentiation to express this computation of higher-order derivatives as forward passes of a larger net with shared weights, for which we then propose a Kronecker-factored approximation, building on KFAC’s recently-proposed generalization to linear layers with weight sharing
[
17
]
.

3.1
Higher-order Forward Mode Automatic Differentiation as Weight Sharing

Here, we review higher-order forward mode, also known as
Taylor-mode
, automatic differentiation
[
19
,
18
,
3
, tutorial in
Appendix

C
]
.
Many PDEs only incorporate first- and second-order partial derivatives and we focus our discussion on second-order Taylor-mode for MLPs to keep the presentation light.
However, one can treat higher-order PDEs and arbitrary network architectures completely analogously.

Taylor-mode propagates directional (higher-order) derivatives.
We now recap the forward propagation rules for MLPs consisting of fully-connected and element-wise activation layers.
Our goal is to evaluate first-and second-order partial derivatives of the form
\partial_{{x}_{i}}u,\partial^{2}_{{x}_{i},{x}_{j}}u
for
i,j=1,\dots,d
.
At the first layer, set
{\bm{z}}^{(0)}={\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\partial_{x_{i}}{\bm{z}}^{(0)}={\bm{e}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e., the
i
-th basis vector and
\partial^{2}_{x_{i},x_{j}}{\bm{z}}^{(0)}={\bm{0}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

For a linear layer
f_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}}({\bm{z}}^{(l-1)})={\bm{W}}^{(l)}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}
, applying the chain rule yields the propagation rule

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}^{(l)}

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}^{(l)}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}\quad\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l)}}\,,

(5a)

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}}{\bm{z}}^{(l)}

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}^{(l)}\partial_{x_{i}}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}\quad\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l)}}\,,

(5b)

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{x_{i},x_{j}}{\bm{z}}^{(l)}

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}^{(l)}\partial^{2}_{x_{i},x_{j}}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}\quad\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l)}}\,.

(5c)

The propagation rule through a nonlinear element-wise activation layer
{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}\mapsto\sigma({\bm{z}}^{(l-1)})
is

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}^{(l)}

\displaystyle=\sigma({\bm{z}}^{(l-1)})\quad\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l)}}\,,

(6a)

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}}{\bm{z}}^{(l)}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\prime}({\bm{z}}^{(l-1)})\odot\partial_{x_{i}}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}\quad\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l)}}\,,

(6b)

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{x_{i},x_{j}}{\bm{z}}^{(l)}

\displaystyle=\partial_{x_{i}}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}\odot\sigma^{\prime\prime}({\bm{z}}^{(l-1)})\odot\partial_{x_{j}}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}+\sigma^{\prime}({\bm{z}}^{(l-1)})\odot\partial^{2}_{x_{i},x_{j}}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}\quad\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l)}}\,.

(6c)

Forward Laplacian

For differential operators of special structure, we can fuse the Taylor-mode forward propagation of individual directional derivatives in
Equations

5
and
6

E.g., to compute not the full Hessian but only the Laplacian, we can simplify the forward pass, which yields the

To the best of our knowledge, this connection has not been pointed out in the literature.
Concretely, by summing (
5c

6c
) over

, we obtain the Laplacian forward pass for linear and activation layers

\displaystyle\Delta_{\bm{x}}{\bm{z}}^{(l)}

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}^{(l)}\Delta_{\bm{x}}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}\quad\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l)}}\,,

(7a)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\bm{x}}{\bm{z}}^{(l)}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\prime}({\bm{z}}^{(l-1)})\odot\Delta_{\bm{x}}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sigma^{\prime\prime}({\bm{z}}^{(l-1)})\odot(\partial_{x_{i}}{{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}})^{\odot 2}\quad\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l)}}\,.

(7b)

This reduces computational cost, but is restricted to PDEs that involve second-order derivatives only via the Laplacian, or a partial Laplacian over a sub-set of input coordinates (e.g. heat equation,
Section

4
).
For a more general second-order linear PDE operator
{\mathcal{L}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}c_{i,j}\partial^{2}_{{x}_{i},{x}_{j}}
, the forward pass for a linear layer is
\mathcal{L}{\bm{z}}^{(l)}={\bm{W}}^{(l)}\mathcal{L}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l)}}
, generalizing (
7a
), and similarly for
Equation

7b

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}{\bm{z}}^{(l)}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\prime}({\bm{z}}^{(l-1)})\odot\mathcal{L}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}c_{i,j}\sigma^{\prime\prime}({\bm{z}}^{(l-1)})\odot\partial_{x_{i}}{{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}}\odot\partial_{x_{j}}{{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}}\quad\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l)}}\,,

see
Section

C.3
for details. This is different from
[
30
]
, which transforms the input space such that the coefficients are diagonal with entries

Importantly, the computation of higher-order derivatives for linear layers boils down to a forward pass through the layer with weight sharing over the different partial derivatives (

3.2
KFAC for Gauss-Newton Matrices with the Laplace Operator

Let’s consider the Poisson equation’s interior Gramian block for a linear layer (suppressing
\Omega

\displaystyle{\bm{G}}^{(l)}_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{W}}^{(l)}}\Delta_{\bm{x}}u_{n}\right)^{\top}\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{W}}^{(l)}}\Delta_{\bm{x}}u_{n}\,.

To avoid introducing a new convention, we rely on the KFAC approximation for linear layers with weight sharing developed by

KFAC for Generalized Gauss-Newton Matrices Involving General PDE Terms

To generalize the previous section, let’s consider the general

,

\Psi(u,D_{{\bm{x}}}u,\dots,D^{k}_{{\bm{x}}}u)={\bm{0}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M},

(10)

where
D^{m}_{\bm{x}}u
collects all partial derivatives of order
m
.
For
m\in\{0,\dots,k\}
there are
S_{m}=\binom{d+m-1}{d-1}
independent partial derivatives and the total number of independent derivatives is
S\coloneqq\sum_{m=0}^{k}S_{m}=\binom{d+k}{k}
.

\Psi
is a smooth mapping from all partial derivatives to
{\mathbb{R}}^{M}
,
\Psi\colon\mathbb{{\mathbb{R}}}^{S}\to\mathbb{{\mathbb{R}}}^{M}
.
To construct a PINN loss for
Equation

10
, we feed the residual
{\bm{r}}_{\Omega,n}({\bm{\theta}})\coloneqq\Psi(u_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}_{n}),D_{{\bm{x}}}u_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}_{n}),\dots,D^{k}_{{\bm{x}}}u_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}_{n}))\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M}
where
D^{m}_{{\bm{x}}}u_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times S_{m}}
into a smooth convex criterion function
\ell\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{M}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,

L_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\ell({\bm{r}}_{\Omega,n}({\bm{\theta}}))\,.

(11)

The generalized Gauss-Newton (GGN) matrix
[
51
]
is the Hessian of
L_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})
when the residual is linearized w.r.t.
{\bm{\theta}}
before differentiation. It is positive semi-definite and has the form

\displaystyle{\bm{G}}_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\mathrm{J}_{\bm{\theta}}{\bm{r}}_{\Omega,n}({\bm{\theta}})\right)^{\top}{\bm{\Lambda}}({\bm{r}}_{\Omega,n})\left(\mathrm{J}_{\bm{\theta}}{\bm{r}}_{\Omega,n}({\bm{\theta}})\right)\,,

(12)

with
{\bm{\Lambda}}({\bm{r}})\coloneqq\nabla^{2}_{{\bm{r}}}\ell({\bm{r}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{M\times M}\succ 0
the criterion’s Hessian, e.g.
\ell({\bm{r}})=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\lVert{\bm{r}}\rVert_{2}^{2}
and
{\bm{\Lambda}}({\bm{r}})={\bm{I}}_{M}
.

Generalizing the second-order Taylor-mode from
Section

3.1
to higher orders for the linear layer, we find

\displaystyle D_{\bm{x}}^{m}{\bm{z}}^{(l)}={\bm{W}}^{(l)}D_{\bm{x}}^{m}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}\qquad\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l)}\times S_{m}}

(13)

for any
m
.
Hence, we can derive a forward propagation for the required derivatives where a linear layer processes at most
S
vectors
2
2

2

Depending on the linear operator, one may reduce weight sharing, as demonstrated for the Laplacian in
Section

3.1
.
, i.e. the linear layer’s weight is shared over the matrices
D^{0}_{\bm{x}}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}\coloneqq{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)},D_{\bm{x}}^{1}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)},\dots,D_{\bm{x}}^{k}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)}
. Stacking them into a matrix
{\bm{Z}}^{(l-1)}_{n}=({\bm{z}}^{(l-1)},D_{\bm{x}}^{1}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)},\dots,D_{\bm{x}}^{k}{\bm{z}}^{(l-1)})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l-1)}\times S}
(and
{\bm{Z}}^{(l)}_{n}
for the outputs), the chain rule yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\bm{G}}^{(l)}_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{W}}^{(l)}}{\bm{Z}}^{(l)}_{n}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{Z}}^{(l)}_{n}}{\bm{r}}_{\Omega,n}\right)^{\top}{\bm{\Lambda}}({\bm{r}}_{\Omega,n})\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{Z}}^{(l)}_{n}}{\bm{r}}_{\Omega,n}\right)\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{W}}^{(l)}}{\bm{Z}}^{(l)}_{n}\right)\\

&=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n,s,s^{\prime}=1}^{N,S,S}{\bm{Z}}^{(l-1)}_{n,s}{\bm{Z}}^{(l-1)\top}_{n,s^{\prime}}\otimes\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{Z}}^{(l)}_{n,s}}{\bm{r}}_{\Omega,n}\right)^{\top}{\bm{\Lambda}}({\bm{r}}_{\Omega,n})\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{Z}}^{(l)}_{n,s^{\prime}}}{\bm{r}}_{\Omega,n}\right)\end{split}

where
{\bm{Z}}_{n,s}^{(l-1)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{h^{(l-1)}}
denotes the
s
-th column of
{\bm{Z}}_{n}^{(l-1)}
.
Following the same steps as in

, we apply the KFAC-expand approximation from

to obtain the generalization of

:

KFAC for the GGN matrix of a general PDE operator

\displaystyle\begin{split}\hskip-17.22217pt{\bm{G}}_{\Omega}^{(l)}({\bm{\theta}})&\approx\left(\!\!\frac{1}{NS}\!\!\sum_{n,s=1}^{N,S}\!\!{\bm{Z}}^{(l-1)}_{n,s}{\bm{Z}}^{(l-1)\top}_{n,s^{\prime}}\!\!\right)\otimes\left(\!\!\frac{1}{N}\!\!\sum_{n,s=1}^{N,S}\!\!\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{Z}}^{(l)}_{n,s}}{\bm{r}}_{\Omega,n}\right)^{\top}\!\!\!{\bm{\Lambda}}({\bm{r}}_{\Omega,n})\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{Z}}^{(l)}_{n,s}}{\bm{r}}_{\Omega,n}\right)\!\!\right)\!\!\\

To bring this expression even closer to

with

KFAC for variational problems

Our proposed KFAC approximation is not limited to PINNs and can be used for variational problems of the form

\displaystyle\min_{u}\int_{\Omega}\ell(u,\partial_{{\bm{x}}}u,\dots,\partial^{k}_{{\bm{x}}}u)\mathrm{d}{\bm{x}}\,,

where

is a convex function.
We can perceive this as a special case of the setting above with

and other variational approaches to solve PDEs

Algorithmic Details

To design an optimizer based on our KFAC approximation, we re-use techniques from the original KFAC
[
38
]
& ENGD
[
41
]
algorithms.

shows pseudo-code for our method on the Poisson equation.

At iteration
t
, we approximate the per-layer interior and boundary Gramians using our derived Kronecker approximation (
Equations

9
and
14
),

{\bm{G}}_{\Omega,t}^{(l)}\approx{\bm{A}}_{\Omega,t}^{(l)}\otimes{\bm{B}}_{\Omega,t}^{(l)}
and
{\bm{G}}_{\partial\Omega,t}^{(l)}\approx{\bm{A}}_{\Omega,t}^{(l)}\otimes{\bm{B}}_{\Omega,t}^{(l)}
.

Exponential moving average and damping

For preconditioning, we accumulate the Kronecker factors
{\bm{A}}^{(l)}_{\bullet,t},{\bm{B}}^{(l)}_{\bullet,t}
over time using an exponential moving average
\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{(l)}_{\bullet,t}=\beta\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{(l)}_{\bullet,t-1}+(1-\beta){\bm{A}}^{(l)}_{\bullet,t}
of factor
\beta\in[0,1)
(identically for
\hat{{\bm{B}}}^{(l)}_{\bullet,t}
), similar to the original KFAC.
Moreover, we apply the same constant damping of strength
\lambda>0
to all Kronecker factors,
\tilde{{\bm{A}}}^{(l)}_{\bullet,t}=\hat{{\bm{A}}}^{(l)}_{\bullet,t}+\lambda{\bm{I}}
and
\tilde{{\bm{B}}}^{(l)}_{\bullet,t}=\hat{{\bm{B}}}^{(l)}_{\bullet,t}+\lambda{\bm{I}}
such that the curvature approximation used for preconditioning at step
t
is

\displaystyle{\bm{G}}_{\bullet,t}\approx\diag\left(\tilde{{\bm{A}}}^{(1)}_{\Omega,t}\otimes\tilde{{\bm{B}}}_{\Omega,t}^{(1)},\dots,\tilde{{\bm{A}}}^{(L)}_{\Omega,t}\otimes\tilde{{\bm{B}}}_{\Omega,t}^{(L)}\right)+\diag\left(\tilde{{\bm{A}}}^{(1)}_{\partial\Omega,t}\otimes\tilde{{\bm{B}}}_{\partial\Omega,t}^{(1)},\dots,\tilde{{\bm{A}}}^{(L)}_{\partial\Omega,t}\otimes\tilde{{\bm{B}}}_{\partial\Omega,t}^{(L)}\right)\,.

Gradient preconditioning

Given layer
l
’s mini-batch gradient
{\bm{g}}_{t}^{(l)}=\nicefrac{{\partial L({\bm{\theta}}_{t})}}{{\partial{\bm{\theta}}^{(l)}_{t}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p^{(l)}}
, we obtain an update direction
{\bm{\Delta}}_{t}^{(l)}=-(\tilde{{\bm{A}}}^{(l)}_{\Omega,t}\otimes\tilde{{\bm{B}}}^{(l)}_{\Omega,t}+\tilde{{\bm{A}}}^{(l)}_{\partial\Omega,t}\otimes\tilde{{\bm{B}}}^{(l)}_{\partial\Omega,t})^{-1}{\bm{g}}_{t}^{(l)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p^{(l)}}
using the trick of
[
38
, Appendix I]
to invert the Kronecker sum via eigen-decomposing all Kronecker factors.

Learning rate and momentum

From the preconditioned gradient
{\bm{\Delta}}_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D}
, we consider two different updates
{\bm{\theta}}_{t+1}={\bm{\theta}}_{t}+{\bm{\delta}}_{t}
we call
KFAC
and
KFAC*
.
KFAC uses momentum over previous updates,
\hat{{\bm{\delta}}}_{t}=\mu{\bm{\delta}}_{t-1}+{\bm{\Delta}}_{t}
, and
\mu
is chosen by the practitioner.
Like ENGD, it uses a logarithmic grid line search, selecting
{\bm{\delta}}_{t}=\alpha_{\star}\hat{{\bm{\delta}}}_{t}
with
\alpha_{\star}=\argmin_{\alpha}L({\bm{\theta}}_{t}+\alpha\hat{{\bm{\delta}}}_{t})
where
\alpha\in\{2^{-30},\dots,2^{0}\}
.
KFAC* uses the automatic learning rate and momentum heuristic of the original KFAC optimizer.
It parametrizes the iteration’s update as
{\bm{\delta}}_{t+1}(\alpha,\mu)=\alpha{\bm{\Delta}}_{t}+\mu{\bm{\delta}}_{t}
, then obtains the optimal parameters by minimizing the quadratic model
m({\bm{\delta}}_{t+1})=L({\bm{\theta}}_{t})+{\bm{\delta}}_{t+1}^{\top}{\bm{g}}_{t}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}{\bm{\delta}}_{t+1}^{\top}({\bm{G}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})+\lambda{\bm{I}}){\bm{\delta}}_{t+1}
with the exact damped Gramian.
The optimal learning rate and momentum
\argmin_{\alpha,\mu}m({\bm{\delta}}_{t+1})
are

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{\star}\\
\mu_{\star}\end{pmatrix}=-\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{\Delta}}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{G}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}){\bm{\Delta}}_{t}+\lambda\left\lVert{\bm{\Delta}}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}&{\bm{\Delta}}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{G}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}){\bm{\delta}}_{t}+\lambda{\bm{\Delta}}^{\top}_{t}{\bm{\delta}}_{t}\\
{\bm{\Delta}}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{G}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}){\bm{\delta}}_{t}+\lambda{\bm{\Delta}}^{\top}_{t}{\bm{\delta}}_{t}&{\bm{\delta}}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{G}}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}){\bm{\Delta}}_{t}+\lambda\left\lVert{\bm{\delta}}_{t}\right\rVert^{2}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{\Delta}}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{g}}_{t}\\
{\bm{\delta}}_{t}^{\top}{\bm{g}}_{t}\end{pmatrix}

(see
[
38
, Section 7]
for details).
The computational cost is dominated by the two Gramian-vector products with
{\bm{\Delta}}_{t}
and
{\bm{\delta}}_{t}
.
By using the Gramian’s outer product structure
[
12
,
45
]
, we perform them with autodiff
[
48
,
51
]
using one Jacobian-vector product each, as recommended in
[
38
]
.

Computational complexity

Inverting layer
l
’s Kronecker approximation of the Gramian requires
{\mathcal{O}}({h^{(l)}}^{3}+{h^{(l+1)}}^{3})
time and
{\mathcal{O}}({h^{(l)}}^{2}+{h^{(l+1)}}^{2})
storage, where
h^{(l)}
is the number of neurons in the
l
-th layer, whereas inverting the exact block for layer would require
{\mathcal{O}}({h^{(l)}}^{3}{h^{(l+1)}}^{3})
time and
{\mathcal{O}}({h^{(l)}}^{2}{h^{(l+1)}}^{2})
memory.
In general, the improvement from the Kronecker factorization depends on how close to square the weight matrices of a layer are, and therefore on the architecture.
In practise, the Kronecker factorization usually significantly reduces memory and run time.
Further improvements can be achieved by using structured Kronecker factors, e.g. (block-)diagonal matrices
[
32
]
.

We use the forward Laplacian framework in our implementation, which we found to be significantly faster and more memory efficient than computing batched Hessian traces, see
Section

C.4
.

5
Discussion and Conclusion

We extended the concept of Kronecker-factored approximate curvature (KFAC) to Gauss-Newton matrices of Physics-informed neural network (PINN) losses that involve derivatives, rather than function evaluations, of the neural net.
This greatly reduces the computational cost of approximate natural gradient methods, which are known to work well on PINNs, and allows them to scale to much larger nets.
Our approach goes beyond the established KFAC for traditional supervised problems as it captures contributions from a PDE’s differential operator that are crucial for optimization.
To establish KFAC for such losses, we use Taylor-mode autodiff to view the differential operator’s compute graph as a forward net with shared weights, then apply the recently-developed formulation of KFAC for linear layers with weight sharing.
Empirically, we find that our KFAC-based optimizers are competitive with expensive second-order methods on small problems and scale to high-dimensional neural networks and PDEs while consistently outperforming first-order methods and LBFGS.

Limitations & future directions

While our implementation currently only supports MLPs and the Poisson and heat equations, the concepts we use to derive KFAC (Taylor-mode, weight sharing) apply to arbitrary architectures and PDEs, as described in
Section

3.3
.
We are excited that our current algorithms show promising performance when compared to second-order methods with sophisticated heuristics.
In fact, the original KFAC optimizer itself
[
38
]
relies heavily on such heuristics that are said to be crucial for its performance
[
8
]
.
Our algorithms borrow components, but we did not explore all bells and whistles, e.g. adaptive damping and heuristics to distribute damping over the Kronecker factors.
We believe our current algorithm’s performance can further be improved, e.g. by exploring (1) updating the KFAC matrices less frequently, as is standard for traditional KFAC, (2) merging the two Kronecker approximations for boundary and interior Gramians into a single one, (3) removing matrix inversions
[
31
]
, (4) using structured Kronecker factors
[
32
]
, (5) computing the Kronecker factors in parallel with the gradient
[
11
]
, (6) using single or mixed precision training
[
40
]
, and (7) studying cheaper KFAC flavours based on the empirical Fisher
[
27
]
or input-based curvature
[
2
,
49
]
.

In all our experiments, we tune the following optimizer hyper-parameters and otherwise use the PyTorch default values:

•

•

-\Delta u(x,y)=2\pi^{2}\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)
on the unit square
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
with sine product right-hand side and zero boundary conditions
u(x,y)=0
for
(x,y)\in\partial[0,1]^{2}
.
We choose a single set of training points with
N_{\Omega}=900,N_{\partial\Omega}=120
.
The
L_{2}
error is evaluated on a separate set of
9\,000
data points using the known solution
u_{\star}(x,y)=\sin(\pi x)\sin(\pi y)
.
Each run is limited to a compute time of
1\,000

s
.
We compare three MLP architectures of increasing size, each of whose linear layers are Tanh-activated except for the final one: a shallow
2\to 64\to 1
MLP with
D=257
trainable parameters, a five layer
2\to 64\to 64\to 48\to 48\to 1
MLP with
D=$9\,873$
trainable parameters, and a five layer
2\to 256\to 256\to 128\to 128\to 1
MLP with
D=$116\,097$
trainable parameters.
For the biggest architecture, full and per-layer ENGD lead to out-of-memory errors and are thus not tested in the experiments.

A.3
5d Poisson Equation

Setup

We consider a five-dimensional Poisson equation
-\Delta u({\bm{x}})=\pi^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{5}\cos(\pi{x}_{i})
on the five-dimensional unit square
{\bm{x}}\in[0,1]^{5}
with cosine sum right-hand side and boundary conditions
u({\bm{x}})=\sum_{i=1}^{5}\cos(\pi{x}_{i})
for
{\bm{x}}\in\partial[0,1]^{5}
.
We sample training batches of size
N_{\Omega}=$3\,000$,N_{\partial\Omega}=500
and evaluate the
L_{2}
error on a separate set of
30\,000
data points using the known solution
u_{\star}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{i=1}^{5}\cos(\pi{x}_{i})
.
All optimizers except for KFAC sample a new training batch each iteration.
KFAC only re-samples every 100 iterations because we noticed significant improvement with multiple iterations on a fixed batch.
To make sure that this does not lead to an unfair advantage of KFAC, we conduct an additional experiment where we also tune the batch sampling frequency, as well as other hyper-parameters; see
Section

A.5
.
The results presented in this section are consistent with this additional experiment (compare the rightmost column of
Figure

A7
and the leftmost column of
Figure

A9
).
Each run is limited to 3000 s.
We compare three MLP architectures of increasing size, each of whose linear layers are Tanh-activated except for the final one: a shallow
5\to 64\to 1
MLP with
D=449
trainable parameters, a five layer
5\to 64\to 64\to 48\to 48\to 1
MLP with
D=$10\,065$
trainable parameters, and a five layer
5\to 256\to 256\to 128\to 128\to 1
MLP with
D=$116\,864$
trainable parameters.
For the biggest architecture, full and layer-wise ENGD lead to out-of-memory errors and are thus not tested in the experiments.

A.6
PINN Loss for the Heat Equation

Consider the
(\tilde{d}+1)
-dimensional homogeneous heat equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(t,\tilde{{\bm{x}}})-\kappa\Delta_{\tilde{{\bm{x}}}}u(t,\tilde{{\bm{x}}})=0

with spatial coordinates
\tilde{{\bm{x}}}\in\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{\tilde{d}}
and time coordinate
t\in\mathrm{T}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}
where
\mathrm{T}
is a time interval and
\kappa>0
denotes the heat conductivity. In this case, our neural network processes a
(d=\tilde{d}+1)
-dimensional vector
{\bm{x}}=(t,\tilde{{\bm{x}}}^{\top})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and we can re-write the heat equation as

\displaystyle\partial_{{x}_{1}}u({\bm{x}})-\kappa\sum_{d=2}^{d}\Delta_{{x}_{d}}u({\bm{x}})=0\,.

In the following, we consider the unit time interval
\mathrm{T}=[0;1]
, the unit square
\Omega=[0;1]^{\tilde{d}}
and set
\kappa=\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
.
There are two types of constraints we need to enforce on the heat equation in order to obtain unique solutions: initial conditions and boundary conditions.
As our framework for the KFAC approximation assumes only two terms in the loss function, we combine the contributions from the boundary and initial values into one term.

To make this more precise, consider the following example solution of the heat equation, which will be used later on as well.
As initial conditions, we use
u_{0}(\tilde{{\bm{x}}})=u(0,\tilde{{\bm{x}}})=\prod_{i=1}^{\tilde{d}}\sin(\pi\tilde{{x}}_{i})
for
\tilde{{\bm{x}}}\in\Omega
.
For boundary conditions, we use
g(t,\tilde{{\bm{x}}})=0
for
(t,\tilde{{\bm{x}}})\in\mathrm{T}\times\partial\Omega
.
The manufactured solution is

\displaystyle u_{\star}(t,\tilde{{\bm{x}}})=\exp\left(-\frac{\pi^{2}\tilde{d}t}{4}\right)\prod_{i=1}^{\tilde{d}}\sin(\pi[\tilde{{x}}_{i}])\,.

The PINN loss for this problem consists of three terms: a PDE term, an initial value condition term, and a spatial boundary condition term,

\displaystyle L({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\Omega}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\Omega}}\left(\partial_{t}u_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}_{n}^{\Omega})-\frac{1}{4}\Delta_{\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{n}}u_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}_{n}^{\Omega})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N_{\partial\Omega}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\partial\Omega}}\left(u_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}_{n}^{\partial\Omega})-g({\bm{x}}_{n}^{\partial\Omega})\right)^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{0}}\left(u_{{\bm{\theta}}}(0,{\bm{x}}_{n}^{0})-u_{0}({\bm{x}}_{n}^{0})\right)^{2}

with
{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\Omega}\sim\mathrm{T}\times\Omega
, and
{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\partial\Omega}\sim\mathrm{T}\times\partial\Omega
, and
{\bm{x}}_{n}^{0}\sim\{0\}\times\Omega
.
To fit this loss into our framework which assumes two loss terms, each of whose curvature is approximated with a Kronecker factor, we combine the initial value and boundary value conditions into a single term.
Assuming
N_{\partial\Omega}=N_{0}=\nicefrac{{N_{\text{cond}}}}{{2}}
without loss of generality, we write

\displaystyle L({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{N_{\Omega}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\Omega}}\left\lVert\partial_{t}u_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}_{n}^{\Omega})-\frac{1}{4}\Delta_{\tilde{{\bm{x}}}_{n}}u_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}_{n}^{\Omega})-y_{n}^{\Omega}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}}_{L_{\Omega}({\bm{\theta}})}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{N_{\text{cond}}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\text{cond}}}\left\lVert u_{{\bm{\theta}}}({\bm{x}}_{n}^{\text{cond}})-y_{n}^{\text{cond}}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}}_{L_{\text{cond}}({\bm{\theta}})}

with domain inputs
{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\Omega}\sim\mathrm{T}\times\Omega
and targets
y_{n}^{\Omega}=0
, boundary and initial condition targets
y_{n}^{\text{cond}}=u_{\star}({\bm{x}}_{n}^{\text{cond}})
with initial inputs
{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\text{cond}}\sim\{0\}\times\Omega
for
n=1,\dots,\nicefrac{{N_{\text{cond}}}}{{2}}
and boundary inputs
{\bm{x}}_{n}^{\text{cond}}\sim\mathrm{T}\times\partial\Omega
for
n=\nicefrac{{N_{\text{cond}}}}{{2}}+1,\dots,N_{\text{cond}}
.
This loss has the same structure as the PINN loss in
Equation

1
.

A.11
9+1-d Logarithmic Fokker-Planck Equation with Random Search

For a given drift
{\bm{\mu}}:[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and diffusivity
\sigma:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}

the Fokker-Planck equation with initial probability density
p_{0}
is given by

\partial_{t}p+\operatorname{div}({\bm{\mu}}p)-\frac{1}{2}\Tr(\sigma\sigma^{\top}\nabla^{2}p)=0,\quad p(0)=p_{0},

which is posed on
[0,1]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Note that
p(t,\cdot)
is a probability density on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
for all
t\in[0,1]
. We transform the above equation into logarithmic
space via
q=\log(p)
. Then
q
solves

\partial_{t}q+\operatorname{div}({\bm{\mu}})+\nabla q\cdot{\bm{\mu}}-\frac{1}{2}\|\sigma^{\top}\nabla q\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}(\sigma\sigma^{\top}\nabla^{2}q)=0,\quad q(0)=\log p_{0}.

For the concrete example of the main text, we set
{\bm{\mu}}(t,{\bm{x}})=-\frac{1}{2}x
and
\sigma=\sqrt{2}{\bm{I}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
.
We consider a 9+1 dimensional Fokker-Planck equation in logarithmic space and replace the unbounded
domain by
[0,1]\times[-5,5]^{d}
. Precisely, we aim to solve the
equation

\partial_{t}q(t,{\bm{x}})-\frac{d}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\nabla q(t,{\bm{x}})\cdot{\bm{x}}-\|\nabla q(t,{\bm{x}})\|^{2}-\Delta q(t,{\bm{x}})=0,\quad q(0)=\log(p^{*}(0)),

where
d=9
,
t\in[0,1]
and
{\bm{x}}\in[-5,5]
. The solution
q^{*}=\log(p^{*})
is
given as
p^{*}(t,{\bm{x}})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\exp(-t){\bm{I}}+(1-\exp(-t))2{\bm{I}})
.
The PINN loss includes the PDE residual and the initial conditions.
We model the solution with a medium sized tanh-activated
MLP with
D=$118\,145$
and the layer structure

10\to 256\to 256\to 128\to 128\to 1
and use batch sizes of

N_{\Omega}=$3\,000$
,
N_{\partial\Omega}=$1\,000$
. Each run is
assigned a budget of
6\,000

s
.

A15
visualizes the results.

Appendix C
Taylor-Mode Automatic Differentiation & Forward Laplacian

PINN losses involve differential operators of the neural network, for instance the Laplacian.
Recently,
Li et al. [29]
proposed a new computational framework called
forward Laplacian
to evaluate the Laplacian and the neural network’s prediction in one forward traversal.
To establish a Kronecker-factorized approximation of the Gramian, which consists of the Laplacian’s gradient, we need to know how a weight matrix enters its computation.
Here, we describe how the weight matrix of a linear layer inside a feed-forward net enters the Laplacian’s computation when using the forward Laplacian framework.
We start by connecting the forward Laplacian framework to Taylor-mode automatic differentiation
[
18
,
3
]
, both to make the presentation self-contained and to explicitly point out this connection which we believe has not been done previously.

C.1
Taylor-Mode Automatic Differentiation

The idea of Taylor-mode is to forward-propagate Taylor coefficients, i.e. directional derivatives, through the computation graph. We provide a brief summary based on its description in
[
3
]
.

Taylor series and directional derivatives

Consider a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and its
K
-th order Taylor expansion at a point
{\bm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
along a direction
\alpha{\bm{v}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\alpha)=f({\bm{x}}+\alpha{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle=f({\bm{x}})+\alpha\left(\frac{\partial f({\bm{x}})}{\partial{\bm{x}}}\right)^{\top}{\bm{v}}+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2!}{\bm{v}}^{\top}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}f({\bm{x}})}{\partial{\bm{x}}^{2}}\right){\bm{v}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\frac{\alpha^{3}}{3!}\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}i_{3}}\left(\frac{\partial^{3}f({\bm{x}})}{\partial{\bm{x}}^{3}}\right)_{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}{v}_{i_{1}}{v}_{i_{2}}{v}_{i_{3}}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\ldots

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\frac{\alpha^{K}}{K!}\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{K}}\left(\frac{\partial^{K}f({\bm{x}})}{\partial{\bm{x}}^{K}}\right)_{i_{1},\dots,i_{K}}{v}_{i_{1}}\cdots{v}_{i_{K}}\,.

We can unify this expression by introducing the
K
-th order directional derivative of
f
at
{\bm{x}}
along
{\bm{v}}
,

\displaystyle\partial^{K}f({\bm{x}})\underbrace{\left[{\bm{v}},\ldots,{\bm{v}}\right]}_{K\,\text{times}}\coloneqq\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{K}}\left(\frac{\partial^{K}f({\bm{x}})}{\partial{\bm{x}}^{K}}\right)_{i_{1},\dots,i_{K}}{v}_{i_{1}}\dots{v}_{i_{K}}\,.

This simplifies the uni-directional Taylor expansion to

\displaystyle\hat{f}(\alpha)=f({\bm{x}}+\alpha{\bm{v}})

\displaystyle=f({\bm{x}})+\alpha\partial f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}}]+\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2!}\partial^{2}f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}},{\bm{v}}]+\frac{\alpha^{3}}{3!}\partial^{3}f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}},{\bm{v}},{\bm{v}}]

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+\ldots+\frac{\alpha^{K}}{K!}\partial^{K}f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}},\ldots,{\bm{v}}]

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{\alpha^{k}}{k!}\partial^{k}f({\bm{x}})\left[\otimes^{k}{\bm{v}}\right]\eqqcolon\sum_{k=1}^{K}w^{f}_{k}\alpha^{k}

where we have used the notation
\otimes^{k}{\bm{v}}
to indicate
k
copies of
{\bm{v}}
, and introduced the
k
-th order Taylor coefficient
w^{f}_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}
of
f
.
This generalizes to vector-valued functions:
If
f
’s output was vector-valued, say
f({\bm{x}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{c}
, we would have Taylor-expanded each component individually and grouped coefficients of same order into vectors
{\bm{w}}_{k}^{f}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{c}
such that
[{\bm{w}}_{k}^{f}]_{i}
is the
k
-th order Taylor coefficient of the
i
th component of
f
.

A note on generality:

In this introduction to Taylor-mode, we limit the discussion to the computation of higher-order derivatives along a single direction
{\bm{v}}
, i.e.
\partial^{K}f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}},\dots,{\bm{v}}]
.
This is limited though, e.g. if we set
K=2
then we can compute
\partial^{2}f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}},{\bm{v}}]={\bm{v}}^{\top}(\nicefrac{{\partial^{2}f({\bm{x}})}}{{\partial{\bm{x}}^{2}}}){\bm{v}}
.
We can set
{\bm{v}}={\bm{e}}_{i}
to the
i
-th standard basis vector to compute the
i
-th diagonal element of the Hessian.
But we cannot evaluate off-diagonal elements, as this would require multi-directional derivatives, like
\partial^{2}f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{e}}_{i},{\bm{e}}_{j\neq i}]
.
A more general description of Taylor-mode for multi-directional Taylor series along
M
directions,
\hat{f}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{M})=f({\bm{x}}+\alpha_{1}{\bm{v}}_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{M}{\bm{v}}_{M})
, which require more general directional derivatives
\partial^{K}f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{v}}_{K}]
(each vector can be different) are discussed in
[
25
]
.
We will use this formulation later to generalize the forward Laplacian scheme to more general weighted sums of second-order derivatives in
Section

C.3
.

Composition rule

Next, we consider the case where
f=g\circ h
is a composition of two functions. Starting from the Taylor coefficients
{\bm{w}}_{0}^{h},\dots{\bm{w}}_{K}^{h}
of
\hat{h}(\alpha)=h({\bm{x}}+\alpha{\bm{v}})
, the Taylor coefficients
{\bm{w}}_{0}^{f},\dots,{\bm{w}}_{K}^{f}
of
\hat{f}(\alpha)=f({\bm{x}}+\alpha{\bm{v}})
follow from Faà di Bruno’s formula
[
18
,
3
]
:

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}_{k}^{f}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{part}(k)}\frac{1}{n_{1}!\dots n_{K}!}\partial^{|\sigma|}g({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})\left[\otimes_{s\in\sigma}{\bm{w}}_{s}^{h}\right]

(C20)

In the above,
\mathrm{part}(k)
is the set of all integer partitionings of
k
; a set of sets.
|\sigma|
denotes the length of a set
\sigma\in\mathrm{part}(k)
,
n_{i}
is the count of integer
i
in
\sigma
, and
{\bm{w}}_{0}^{h}=h({\bm{x}})
.

Second-order Taylor-mode
Our goal is the computation of second-order derivatives of
f
w.r.t.
{\bm{x}}
.
So let’s work out
Equation

C20
up to order
K=2
.
The zeroth and first order are simply the forward pass and the forward-mode gradient chain rule.
For the second-order term, we need the integer partitioning of 2, given by
\mathrm{part}(2)=\left\{\{1,1\},\{2\}\right\}
.
This results in

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}_{0}^{f}

\displaystyle=g({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})\,,

(C21a)

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}_{1}^{f}

\displaystyle=\partial g({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})[{\bm{w}}_{1}^{h}]\,,

(C21b)

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}_{2}^{f}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}g({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})[{\bm{w}}_{1}^{h},{\bm{w}}_{1}^{h}]+\partial g({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})[{\bm{w}}_{2}^{h}]\,.

(C21c)

We can also express
{\bm{w}}_{1}^{f},{\bm{w}}_{2}^{f}
in terms of Jacobian- and Hessian-vector products of
g
,

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}_{1}^{f}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{w}}_{0}^{h}}g({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})\right){\bm{w}}_{1}^{h}\,,

(C22a)

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}_{2}^{f}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}{{\bm{w}}_{1}^{h}}^{\top}\frac{\partial^{2}\left[g({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})\right]_{1}}{\partial{{\bm{w}}_{0}^{h}}^{2}}{\bm{w}}_{1}^{h}\\
\vdots\\
{{\bm{w}}_{1}^{h}}^{\top}\frac{\partial^{2}\left[g({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})\right]_{D}}{\partial{{\bm{w}}_{0}^{h}}^{2}}{\bm{w}}_{1}^{h}\end{pmatrix}+\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{w}}_{0}^{h}}g({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})\right){\bm{w}}_{2}^{h}\,.

(C22b)

Note that first-order Taylor-mode (
Equation

C22a
) corresponds to the standard forward-mode autodiff which pushes forward error signals through Jacobian-vector products.

C.2
Forward Laplacian

Our goal is to compute the Laplacian of
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{c}
(in practise,
c=1
),

\displaystyle\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}f({\bm{x}})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\begin{pmatrix}\partial^{2}[f({\bm{x}})]_{1}[{\bm{e}}_{i},{\bm{e}}_{i}]\\
\vdots\\
\partial^{2}[f({\bm{x}})]_{c}[{\bm{e}}_{i},{\bm{e}}_{i}]\end{pmatrix}\coloneq 2\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\bm{w}}_{2,i}^{f}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{c}\,,

(C23)

where
{\bm{e}}_{i}
is the
i
-th standard basis vector,
[f({\bm{x}})]_{j}
is the
j
-th component of
f({\bm{x}})
, and we have introduced the second-order Taylor coefficients
{\bm{w}}_{2,i}^{f}
of
f
along
{\bm{e}}_{i}
.
The Laplacian requires computing, then summing, the second-order Taylor coefficients of
d
Taylor approximations
\{f({\bm{x}}+{\bm{e}}_{i})\}_{i=1,\dots,d}
.

Naive approach

We can use Taylor-mode differentiation to compute all these components in one forward traversal. Adding the extra loop over the Taylor expansions we want to compute in parallel, we obtain the following scheme from
Equation

C21
,

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}_{0}^{f}

\displaystyle=g({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})\,,

(C24a)

\displaystyle\left\{{\bm{w}}_{1,i}^{f}\right\}_{i=1,\dots,d}

\displaystyle=\left\{\partial g({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})[{\bm{w}}_{1,i}^{h}]\right\}_{i=1,\dots,d}\,,

(C24b)

\displaystyle\left\{{\bm{w}}_{2,i}^{f}\right\}_{i=1,\dots,d}

\displaystyle=\left\{\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}g({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})[{\bm{w}}_{1,i}^{h},{\bm{w}}_{1,i}^{h}]+\partial g({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})[{\bm{w}}_{2,i}^{h}]\right\}_{i=1,\dots,d}\,.

(C24c)

Forward Laplacian framework

Computing the Laplacian via
Equation

C24
first computes, then sums, the diagonal second-order derivatives
\{{\bm{w}}_{2,i}^{f}\}_{i=1,\dots,d}
.
Note that we can pull the sum inside the forward propagation scheme, specifically
Equation

C24c
, and push-forward the summed second-order coefficients. This simplifies
Equation

C24
to

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}_{0}^{f}

\displaystyle=g({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})\,,

(C25a)

\displaystyle\left\{{\bm{w}}_{1,i}^{f}\right\}_{i=1,\dots,d}

\displaystyle=\left\{\partial g({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})[{\bm{w}}_{1,i}^{h}]\right\}_{i=1,\dots,d}\,,

(C25b)

\displaystyle\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\bm{w}}_{2,i}^{f}}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}f({\bm{x}})}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial^{2}g({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})[{\bm{w}}_{1,i}^{h},{\bm{w}}_{1,i}^{h}]\right)+\partial g({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})\underbrace{\left[\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\bm{w}}_{2,i}^{h}\right]}_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}g({\bm{x}})}\,.

(C25c)

Equation

C25
is the forward Laplacian framework from
Li et al. [29]
for computing the Laplacian of a neural network.
Here, we have derived it from Taylor-mode automatic differentiation.
Note that
Equation

C25
requires less computations and memory than
Equation

C24
because we can pull the summation from the Laplacian into the forward propagation scheme.

C.2.1
Forward Laplacian for Elementwise Activation Layers

We now describe
Equation

C25
for the case where
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{c}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{c}
acts element-wise via
\sigma:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
We will write
\sigma(\bullet),\sigma^{\prime}(\bullet),\sigma^{\prime\prime}(\bullet)
to indicate element-wise application of
\sigma
, its first derivative
\sigma^{\prime}
, and second derivative
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
to all elements of
\bullet
.
Further, let
\odot
denote element-wise multiplication, and
(\bullet)^{\odot 2}
element-wise squaring.
With that, we can write the Jacobian as
\mathrm{J}_{h({\bm{x}})}g({\bm{x}})=\diag(\sigma(h({\bm{x}})))
where
\diag(\bullet)
embeds a vector
\bullet
into the diagonal of a matrix.
The Hessian of component
i
is
\nicefrac{{\partial^{2}[g(h({\bm{x}}))]_{i}}}{{\partial h({\bm{x}})^{2}}}=[\sigma^{\prime\prime}(h({\bm{x}}))]_{i}{\bm{e}}_{i}{\bm{e}}_{i}^{\top}
.
Inserting
Equation

C22
into
Equation

C25
and using the Jacobian and Hessian expressions of the element-wise activation function yields the following forward Laplacian forward propagation:

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}_{0}^{f}

\displaystyle=\sigma({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})\,,

(C26a)

\displaystyle\left\{{\bm{w}}_{1,i}^{f}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\sigma^{\prime}({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})\odot{\bm{w}}_{1,i}^{h}\right\}_{i=1,\dots,d}\,,

(C26b)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\bm{w}}_{2,i}^{f}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{\prime\prime}({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})\odot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left({\bm{w}}_{1,i}^{h}\right)^{\odot 2}\right)+\sigma^{\prime}({\bm{w}}_{0}^{h})\odot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\bm{w}}_{2,i}^{h}\right)\,.

(C26c)

C.2.2
Forward Laplacian for Linear Layers

Now, let
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{in}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{out}}}
be a linear layer with weight matrix
{\bm{W}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{out}}\times D_{\text{in}}}
and bias vector
{\bm{b}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{out}}}
.
Its Jacobian is
\mathrm{J}_{h({\bm{x}})}({\bm{W}}h({\bm{x}})+{\bm{b}})={\bm{W}}
and the second-order derivative is zero.
Hence,
Equation

C25
for linear layers becomes

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}_{0}^{f}

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}{\bm{w}}_{0}^{h}+{\bm{b}}\,,

(C27a)

\displaystyle\left\{{\bm{w}}_{1,i}^{f}\right\}_{i=1,\dots,d}

\displaystyle=\left\{{\bm{W}}{\bm{w}}_{1,i}^{h}\right\}_{i=1,\dots,d}\,,

(C27b)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\bm{w}}_{2,i}^{f}

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\bm{w}}_{2,i}^{h}\right)\,.

(C27c)

We can summarize
Equation

C27
in a single equation by grouping all quantities that are multiplied by
{\bm{W}}
into a single matrix, and appending a single row of ones or zeros to account for the bias:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{w}}_{0}^{f}&{\bm{w}}_{1,1}^{f}&\dots&{\bm{w}}_{1,d}^{f}&\sum_{i=1}^{D}{\bm{w}}_{2,i}^{f}\end{pmatrix}}_{\coloneq{\bm{T}}^{f}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{out}}\times(d+2)}}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{W}}&{\bm{b}}\end{pmatrix}\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}{\bm{w}}_{0}^{h}&{\bm{w}}_{1,1}^{h}&\dots&{\bm{w}}_{1,d}^{h}&\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\bm{w}}_{2,i}^{h}\\
1&0&\dots&0&0\end{pmatrix}}_{\coloneq{\bm{T}}^{h}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(D_{\text{in}}+1)\times(d+2)}}\,,

or, in compact form,

\displaystyle{\bm{T}}^{f}

\displaystyle=\tilde{{\bm{W}}}{\bm{T}}^{h}\,.

(C28)

Equation

C28
shows that the weight matrix
\tilde{{\bm{W}}}^{(l)}=({\bm{W}}^{(l)}\ {\bm{b}}^{(l)})
of a linear layer
f^{(l)}
inside a neural network
f^{(L)}\circ\ldots\circ f^{(1)}
is applied to a matrix
{\bm{T}}^{(l-1)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{in}}\times(d+2)}
during the computation of the net’s prediction and Laplacian via the forward Laplacian framework and yields another matrix
{\bm{T}}^{(l)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{D_{\text{out}}\times(d+2)}
.

C.3
Generalization of the Forward Laplacian to Weighted Sums of Second Derivatives

The Laplacian is of the form
\Delta_{{\bm{x}}}f=\sum_{i}\partial^{2}f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{e}}_{i},{\bm{e}}_{i}]
and we previously described the forward Laplacian framework of
Li et al. [29]
as a consequence of pulling the summation into Taylor-mode’s forward propagation.
Here, we derive the forward propagation to more general operators of the form
\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}\partial^{2}f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{e}}_{i},{\bm{e}}_{j}]
, which contain the Laplacian for
c_{i,j}=\delta_{i,j}
.

As mentioned in
Section

C.1
, this requires a generalization of Taylor-mode which computes derivatives of the form
\partial^{K}f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}},\dots,{\bm{v}}]
, where the directions
{\bm{v}}
must be identical. We start with the formulation in
[
25
]
which expresses the
K
-th multi-directional derivative of a function
f=g\circ h
through the composites’ derivatives (all functions can be vector-to-vector)

\displaystyle\partial^{K}f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{v}}_{K}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{part}(\{1,\dots,K\})}\partial^{|\sigma|}g(h({\bm{x}}))\left[\otimes_{\eta\in\sigma}\partial^{|\eta|}h({\bm{x}})\left[\otimes_{l\in\eta}{\bm{v}}_{l}\right]\right]\,.

(C29)

Here,
\mathrm{part}(\{1,\dots,K\})
denotes the set of all set partitions of
\{1,\dots,K\}
(
\sigma
is a set of sets). E.g.,

\displaystyle\mathrm{part}(\{1\})

\displaystyle=\{\{\{1\}\}\}\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{part}(\{1,2\})

\displaystyle=\{\{\{1,2\}\},\{\{1\},\{2\}\}\}\,,

\displaystyle\mathrm{part}(\{1,2,3\})

\displaystyle=\{\{\{1,2,3\}\},\{\{1\},\{2,3\}\},\{\{1,2\},\{3\}\},\{\{1,3\},\{2\}\},\{\{1\},\{2\},\{3\}\}\}\,.

To make this more concrete, let’s consider
Equation

C29
for first- and second-order derivatives,

\displaystyle\partial f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}}]

\displaystyle=\partial g(h({\bm{x}}))[\partial h({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}}]]\,,

(C30a)

\displaystyle\partial^{2}f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}}_{1},{\bm{v}}_{2}]

\displaystyle=\partial g^{2}(h({\bm{x}}))[\partial h({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}}_{1}],\partial h({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}}_{2}]]+\partial g(h({\bm{x}}))[\partial h^{2}({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}}_{1},{\bm{v}}_{2}]]\,.

(C30b)

From
Equation

C30
, we can see that if we want to compute a weighted sum of second-order derivatives
\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}\partial^{2}f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}}_{i},{\bm{v}}_{j}]
, we can pull the sum inside the second equation,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}\partial^{2}f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}}_{i},{\bm{v}}_{j}]&=\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}\partial^{2}g(h({\bm{x}}))[\partial h({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}}_{i}],\partial h({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}}_{j}]]\\
&\phantom{=}+\partial g(h({\bm{x}}))\left[\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}\partial^{2}h({\bm{x}})[{\bm{v}}_{i},{\bm{v}}_{j}]\right]\,.\end{split}

(C31)

Hence, we can propagate the collapsed second-order derivatives, together with all first-order derivatives along
{\bm{v}}_{1},{\bm{v}}_{2},\dots
. The only difference to the forward Laplacian is how second-order effects of an operation are incorporated (first term in
Equation

C31
).

We now specify
Equations

C29
and
C31
for linear layers and element-wise activation functions.

For a linear layer
g:h({\bm{x}})\mapsto{\bm{W}}h({\bm{x}})+{\bm{b}}
, we have
\partial^{m>1}g(h(x))[{\bm{v}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{v}}_{m}]={\bm{0}}
, and thus

\displaystyle\partial f(x)[{\bm{v}}]

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}\partial h(x)[{\bm{v}}]\,,

(C32a)

\displaystyle\partial^{2}f(x)[{\bm{v}}_{1},{\bm{v}}_{2}]

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}\partial^{2}h(x)[{\bm{v}}_{1},{\bm{v}}_{2}]\,,

(C32b)

\displaystyle\partial^{K}f(x)[{\bm{v}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{v}}_{K}]

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}\partial^{K}h(x)[{\bm{v}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{v}}_{K}]\,.

(C32c)

The last equation is because only the set partition
\{1,\dots,K\}
contributes to
Equation

C29
.

For elementwise activations
g:h(x)\mapsto\sigma(h(x))
with
\sigma:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
applied component-wise, we have the structured derivative tensor
[\partial^{m}g(h(x))]_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}=\partial^{m}\sigma(h(x)_{i_{1}})\delta_{i_{1},\dots,i_{m}}
and multi-directional derivative
\partial^{K}g(h({\bm{x}}))[{\bm{v}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{v}}_{K}]=\partial^{K}\sigma({\bm{x}})\odot{\bm{v}}_{1}\odot\dots\odot{\bm{v}}_{K}
.
Equation

C30
becomes

\displaystyle\partial f(x)[{\bm{v}}]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\prime}(h(x))\odot\partial h(x)[{\bm{v}}]\,,

(C33a)

\displaystyle\partial^{2}f(x)[{\bm{v}}_{1},{\bm{v}}_{2}]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\prime\prime}(h(x))\odot\partial h(x)[{\bm{v}}_{1}]\odot\partial h(x)[{\bm{v}}_{2}]+\sigma^{\prime}(h(x))\odot\partial^{2}h(x)[{\bm{v}}_{1},{\bm{v}}_{2}]\,.

(C33b)

As shown in
Equation

C30b
, for both
Equations

C32
and
C33
, we can pull the summation inside the propagation scheme. Specifically, to compute
\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}\partial^{2}f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{e}}_{i},{\bm{e}}_{j}]
, we have for linear layers

\displaystyle f({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=g(h({\bm{x}}))\,,

(C34a)

\displaystyle\partial f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{e}}_{i}]

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}\partial h({\bm{x}})[{\bm{e}}_{i}]\,,\qquad i=1,\dots,d\,,

(C34b)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}\partial^{2}f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{e}}_{i},{\bm{e}}_{j}]}

\displaystyle={\bm{W}}\left({\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}\partial^{2}h({\bm{x}})[{\bm{e}}_{i},{\bm{e}}_{j}]}\right)\,.

(C34c)

and for activation layers

\displaystyle f({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\sigma(h({\bm{x}}))\,,

(C34d)

\displaystyle\partial f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{e}}_{i}]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\prime}(h({\bm{x}}))\odot\partial h({\bm{x}})[{\bm{e}}_{i}]\,,\qquad i=1,\dots,d\,,

(C34e)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}\partial^{2}f({\bm{x}})[{\bm{e}}_{i},{\bm{e}}_{j}]}&=\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}\sigma^{\prime\prime}(h({\bm{x}}))\odot\partial h({\bm{x}})[{\bm{e}}_{i}]\odot\partial h({\bm{x}})[{\bm{e}}_{j}]\\
&\phantom{=}+\sigma^{\prime}(h({\bm{x}}))\odot\left({\color[rgb]{0.2305,0.2695,0.8906}\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}\partial^{2}h({\bm{x}})[{\bm{e}}_{i},{\bm{e}}_{j}]}\right)\,.\end{split}

(C34f)

(the summed second-order derivatives that are forward-propagated are highlighted).
This propagation reduces back to the forward Laplacian
Equations

C26
and
C27
when we set
c_{i,j}=\delta_{i,j}
.
In contrast to other attempts to compute such a weighted sum of second-order derivatives by reducing it to (multiple) partial standard forward Laplacians
[
30
]
, we do not need to diagonalize the coefficient matrix and can compute the linear operator in one forward propagation.

Appendix D
Backpropagation Perspective of the Laplacian

Here, we derive the computation graphs for the Laplacian and its associated Gramian when using reverse-mode AD, aka backpropagation.
In contrast to the Taylor-mode perspective, the resulting expressions cannot be interpreted as simple weight-sharing.
This complicates defining a Kronecker-factored approximation for the Gramian without introducing new approximations that are different from
Eschenhagen et al. [17]
, rendering the Taylor-mode perspective advantageous.

We start by deriving the Laplacian
\Delta u\coloneqq\Tr(\nabla_{\!{{\bm{x}}}}^{2}u)
of a feed-forward NN (see
Section

2.1
), assuming a single data point for simplicity (see
Section

D.1
) and abbreviating
u_{{\bm{\theta}}}
as
u
.
The goal is to make the Laplacian’s dependence w.r.t. a weight
{\bm{W}}^{(i)}
in one layer of the network explicit.
Then, we can write down the Jacobian
\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{W}}^{(i)}}\Delta u
(see
Section

D.2
) which is required for the Gramian in
Equation

2
(see
Section

D.3
).
We do this based on the concept of
Hessian backpropagation

[
10

D.1
Hessian Backpropagation and Backward Laplacian

Gradient backpropagation describes a recursive procedure to compute gradients by backpropagating a signal via vector-Jacobian products (VJPs).
A similar procedure can be derived to compute Hessians w.r.t. nodes in a graph (
{\bm{z}}^{(i)}
or
{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}
).
We call this recursive procedure Hessian backpropagation
[
10
]
.

Gradient backpropagation

As a warm-up, let’s recall how to compute the gradient
\nabla_{\!{{\bm{\theta}}}}u=(\nabla_{\!{{\bm{\theta}}^{(1)}}}u,\dots,\nabla_{\!{{\bm{\theta}}^{(L)}}}u)
.
We start by setting
\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(L)}}}u=\nabla_{\!{u}}u=1
(assuming
u
is scalar for simplicity), then backpropagate the error via VJPs according to the recursion

\displaystyle\begin{split}\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}}u&=\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}{\bm{z}}^{(i)}\right)^{\top}\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}u\,,\\
\nabla_{\!{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}}u&=\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}{\bm{z}}^{(i)}\right)^{\top}\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}u\,\end{split}

(D35)

for
i=L,\dots,1
.
This yields the gradients of
u
w.r.t. all intermediate representations and parameters.

Hessian backpropagation

Just like gradient backpropagation, we can derive a recursive scheme for the Hessian.
Recall the Hessian chain rule

\nabla^{2}(f\circ g)=(\mathrm{J}g)^{\top}\nabla^{2}f(g)(\mathrm{J}g)+\sum_{k}(\nabla_{g}f)_{k}\cdot\nabla^{2}g_{k},

(D36)

where
g_{i}
denotes the individual components of
g
, see
[
53
]
.
The recursion for computing Hessians of
u

w.r.t. intermediate representations and parameters starts by initializing the
recursion with
\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(L)}}}^{2}u=\nabla_{\!{u}}^{2}u=0
, and then
backpropagating according to (see
Dangel et al. [10]
for details)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}}^{2}u&=\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}{\bm{z}}^{(i)}\right)^{\top}\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}^{2}u\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}{\bm{z}}^{(i)}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{h^{(i)}}\left(\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}}^{2}[{\bm{z}}^{(i)}]_{k}\right)[\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}u]_{k}\,,\\
\nabla_{\!{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}}^{2}u&=\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}{\bm{z}}^{(i)}\right)^{\top}\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}^{2}u\left(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}{\bm{z}}^{(i)}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{h^{(i)}}\left(\nabla_{\!{{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}}}^{2}[{\bm{z}}^{(i)}]_{k}\right)[\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}u]_{k}\end{split}

(D37)

for
i=L,\dots,1
.
The first term takes the incoming Hessian (w.r.t. a layer’s output) and sandwiches it between the layer’s Jacobian.
It can be seen as backpropagating curvature from downstream layers.
The second term adds in curvature introduced by the current layer.
It is only non-zero if the layer is nonlinear.
For linear layers, convolutional layers, and ReLU layers, it is zero.

Hessian backpropagation through nonlinear layers

We mostly consider nonlinear layers without trainable parameters and consist of a componentwise nonlinearity
z\mapsto\sigma(z)
for some
\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
The Jacobian of such a nonlinear layer is given by
\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}{\bm{z}}^{(i)}=\diag(\sigma^{\prime}({\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}))
and the Hessian terms are given by
\nabla^{2}_{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}[{\bm{z}}^{(i)}]_{k}=\sigma^{\prime\prime}({\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}_{k}){\bm{e}}_{k}{\bm{e}}_{k}^{\top}
where
{\bm{e}}_{k}
is the unit vector along coordinate
k

With these two identities we can backpropogate the input Hessian through such layers via

\displaystyle\begin{split}\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}}^{2}u&=\left(\diag(\sigma^{\prime}({\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}))\right)^{\top}\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}^{2}u\left(\diag(\sigma^{\prime}({\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}))\right)\\
&\phantom{=}+\sum_{k=1}^{h^{(i)}}\sigma^{\prime\prime}({\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}_{k}){\bm{e}}_{k}{\bm{e}}_{k}^{\top}[\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}u]_{k}\,.\end{split}

\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{W}}}\left({\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}{\bm{W}}\right)={\bm{I}}\otimes{\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}+{\bm{K}}\left({\bm{I}}\otimes{\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\right)\,,

{\bm{K}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\dim({\bm{Z}})\times\dim({\bm{Z}})}
(denoting

{\bm{Z}}
, modifies the order from first-varies-fastest to last-varies-fastest, i.e.

{\bm{K}}\flatten({\bm{Z}})=\flatten({\bm{Z}}^{\top})\,.

Re-introducing the layer indices, the expressions in
Equation

D40
become

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{W}}^{(i)}}{\bm{z}}^{(i)}&={{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{I}}\\
\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{W}}^{(i)}}\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}}u\,,&={\bm{I}}\otimes\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}u\\

(D44)

We will now use symmetries in the objects used during Hessian backpropagation to simplify this further.
At a first glance, it looks like the Gramian consists of 16 terms, as there are 4 summands from the Jacobians in
Equation

D43
.
However, we can simplify into 9 terms:

First,
\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}^{2}u
is symmetric, that is

\displaystyle\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{W}}^{(i)}}\left({{\bm{W}}^{(i)}}^{\top}\left(\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}^{2}u\right){\bm{W}}^{(i)}\right)

\displaystyle={\bm{I}}\otimes\left[{{\bm{W}}^{(i)}}^{\top}\left(\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}^{2}u\right)\right]+{\bm{K}}\left({\bm{I}}\otimes\left[{{\bm{W}}^{(i)}}^{\top}\left(\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}^{2}u\right)\right]\right)\,,

and the transposed Jacobian is

\displaystyle{\bm{I}}\otimes\left[\left(\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}^{2}u\right){\bm{W}}^{(i)}\right]+\left({\bm{I}}\otimes\left[\left(\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}^{2}u\right){\bm{W}}^{(i)}\right]\right){\bm{K}}^{\top}\,.

Second, we multiply the transpose Jacobian onto
\nabla_{\!{\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}}^{2}u}}\Delta u
, which inherits symmetry from the Hessian,
[\nabla_{\!{\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}}^{2}u}}\Delta u]_{j,k}=[\nabla_{\!{\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}}^{2}u}}\Delta u]_{k,j}
.
Due to this symmetry, the action of
{\bm{K}}
(or
{\bm{K}}^{\top}
) does not alter it,

\displaystyle{\bm{K}}^{\top}\left(\nabla_{\!{\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}}^{2}u}}\Delta u\right)=\nabla_{\!{\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}}^{2}u}}\Delta u\,.

In other words, it does not matter how we flatten (first- or last-varies-fastest).
This simplifies the VJP (last line in
Equation

D42
) to

\displaystyle\left({\bm{I}}\otimes\left[\left(\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}^{2}u\right){\bm{W}}^{(i)}\right]\right)\nabla_{\!{\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}}^{2}u}}\Delta u+\left({\bm{I}}\otimes\left[\left(\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}^{2}u\right){\bm{W}}^{(i)}\right]\right){\bm{K}}^{\top}\nabla_{\!{\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}}^{2}u}}\Delta u

\displaystyle=2\left({\bm{I}}\otimes\left[\left(\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}^{2}u\right){\bm{W}}^{(i)}\right]\right)\nabla_{\!{\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}}^{2}u}}\Delta u\,.

We can now write down the simplified Jacobian from
Equation

D41
, whose self-outer product forms the Gramian block for a linear layer’s weight matrix,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{W}}^{(i)}}\Delta u&=\underbrace{\left({{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}^{\top}\otimes{\bm{I}}\right)^{\top}\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}\Delta u}_{(1)}\\
&\phantom{=}+\underbrace{\left({\bm{I}}\otimes\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}u\right)^{\top}\nabla_{\!{\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}}u}}\Delta u}_{(2)}\\
&\phantom{=}+\underbrace{2\left({\bm{I}}\otimes\left[\left(\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i)}}}^{2}u\right){\bm{W}}^{(i)}\right]\right)\nabla_{\!{\nabla_{\!{{\bm{z}}^{(i-1)}}}^{2}u}}\Delta u}_{(3)}\,,\end{split}

(D45)

where (1) is the contribution from the forward pass, (2) is the contribution from the gradient backpropagation, and (3) is the contribution from the Hessian backpropagation. The Jacobians from
Equation

D43
allow to express the Gramian in terms of Kronecker-structured expressions consisting of 9 terms in total.

Conclusion

One problem of computing the Laplacian and its Jacobian with backpropagation according to
Equation

D45
is that if we write out the Gramian’s block
{\bm{G}}_{\Omega}^{(i)}=\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{W}}^{(i)}}\Delta u(\mathrm{J}_{{\bm{W}}^{(i)}}\Delta u)^{\top}
, we obtain 9 terms of different structure.
Defining a single Kronecker product approximation would involve introducing new approximations on top of those employed by
Eschenhagen et al. [17]
.
Therefore, the forward Laplacian, or Taylor-mode, perspective we choose in the main text is advantageous as it allows to define KFAC without introducing new approximations.
