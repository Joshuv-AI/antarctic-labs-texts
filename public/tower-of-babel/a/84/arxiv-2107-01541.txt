A second look at the Kurth solution in galactic dynamics
Markus Kunze, [2ex] Universität Köln, Institut für Mathematik, Weyertal 86-90D - 50931 Köln, Germanyemail: mkunze@mi.uni-koeln.de[1ex] Key words: Vlasov-Poisson system, Kurth solution, galactic dynamics

Abstract
The Kurth solution is a particular non-isotropic steady state solution to the gravitational Vlasov-Poisson system. It has the property that by means of a suitable time-dependent transformation it can be turned into a family of time-dependent solutions. Therefore, for a general steady state Q ⁡ ( x , v ) = Q ~ ​ ( e Q , β ) Q(x,v)=\tilde{Q}(e_{Q},\beta) , depending upon the particle energy e Q e_{Q} and β = ℓ 2 = | x ∧ v | 2 \beta=\ell^{2}=|x\wedge v|^{2} , the question arises if solutions f f could be generated that are of the form f ⁡ ( t ) = Q ~ ​ ( e Q ​ ( R ⁡ ( t ) , P ⁡ ( t ) , B ⁡ ( t ) ) , B ⁡ ( t ) ) f(t)=\tilde{Q}\Big(e_{Q}(R(t),P(t),B(t)),B(t)\Big) for suitable functions R R , P P and B B , all depending on ( t , r , p r , β ) (t,r,p_{r},\beta) for r = | x | r=|x| and p r = x ⋅ v | x | p_{r}=\frac{x\cdot v}{|x|} . We are going to show that, under some mild assumptions, basically if R R and P P are independent of β \beta , and if B = β B=\beta is constant, then Q Q already has to be the Kurth solution. Introduction

It is a remarkable fact that very few of Bob Glassey’s influential papers
concern the Vlasov-Poisson system. Certainly there is an in-depth treatment
of the existence of global solutions in his book
[
3
]
,
but apart from that only
[
4
,
5
]
comes to this author’s mind.
Maybe this is due to Glassey’s mathematical formation in the tradition of
John, Nirenberg, Segal, Strauss … that he liked better hyperbolic equations,
and in particular the relativistic Vlasov-Maxwell system.

For this reason, Glassey would have probably not paid much attention to the present paper,
but being a polite person, he would nevertheless have found some friendly words for it.
In addition, this paper has no hard analytic proofs,
which Glassey could do so well. Let us only mention
[
6
]
on global existence
for the ‘
2.5
’ dimensional relativistic Vlasov-Maxwell system, jointly with Jack Schaeffer,
which is not so well-known (in the sense that not many people have read
it in all detail), but which is a true masterpiece. One has to use all kinds of structures
in the system and is not allowed to loose the tiniest part of an
\varepsilon

to close the argument in the end.

Here we are going to consider the Vlasov-Poisson system in the gravitational case,
which is given by

\partial_{t}f(t,x,v)+v\cdot\nabla_{x}f(t,x,v)-\nabla_{x}U_{f}(t,x)\cdot\nabla_{v}f(t,x,v)=0,

(1.1)

where

\Delta_{x}U_{f}(t,x)=4\pi\rho_{f}(t,x),\quad\lim_{|x|\to\infty}U_{f}(t,x)=0,\quad\rho_{f}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f(t,x,v)\,dv,

(1.2)

for
(t,x,v)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Therefore

U_{f}(t,x)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}\frac{\rho_{f}(t,y)}{|y-x|}\,dy.

(1.3)

The system possesses an abundance of solutions
Q=Q(x,v)

that are independent of time. Let

e_{Q}(x,v)=\frac{1}{2}\,|v|^{2}+U_{Q}(x)

denote the particle energy and let

\ell^{2}(x,v)=|L|^{2}=|x|^{2}|v|^{2}-(x\cdot v)^{2}

be the square of the angular momentum
L=x\wedge v
, respectively.
Then both
e_{Q}
and
\ell^{2}
are conserved along solutions
of the characteristic equations
\ddot{X}(s)=-\nabla U_{Q}(X(s))
;
note that also
U_{Q}
is independent of time.
Next recall that a function
g=g(x,v)
is said to be spherically symmetric,
if
g(Ax,Av)=g(x,v)
for all
A\in{\rm SO}(3)
and
x,v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
Now it is the content of Jeans’s theorem that the distribution function
Q

of every spherically symmetric steady state solution has to be of the form

Q(x,v)=\tilde{Q}(e_{Q}(x,v),\ell^{2}(x,v))

for a suitable function
\tilde{Q}
of two variables; see
[
1
, Section 2]

for a precise formulation. Such steady state solutions are called non-isotropic,
in contrast to the isotropic ones, which can be written as
Q(x,v)=\tilde{Q}(e_{Q}(x,v))
;
a solution of the latter form will necessarily be spherically symmetric,
[
2
,
11
]
.

In this paper we will have a closer look at
one particular and non-isotropic steady state solution
Q
,
which has been found by Kurth in 1978 and which will be denoted
by
Q_{{\rm K}}
in the sequel; see
[
7
]
. It is surrounded
by time-periodic solutions
f_{\varepsilon}(t)
such that
f_{\varepsilon}\to Q_{{\rm K}}

as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Since the
f_{\varepsilon}(t)
are semi-explicit, the Kurth solution
is a good testing ground for all kinds of questions, including some from numerics
[
10
]
.
It is very degenerate in many respects, so an important issue is
to understand whether it reflects what happens ‘generically’ close to steady states
(in a sense to be made precise), or on the contrary it is just a peculiarity.
The Kurth solution is given by

\displaystyle Q_{{\rm K}}(x,v)=\frac{3}{4\pi^{3}}\,\frac{1}{(1-|x|^{2}-|v|^{2}+|x\wedge v|^{2})^{1/2}}\quad\mbox{where}\quad(\ldots)>0\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,|x\wedge v|<1,

\displaystyle\mbox{and}\quad Q_{{\rm K}}(x,v)=0\quad\mbox{else},

(1.4)

for
x,v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Then (see Lemma
3.1
below) its charge density

\rho_{Q_{{\rm K}}}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}Q_{{\rm K}}(x,v)\,dv=\frac{3}{4\pi}\,{\bf 1}_{B_{1}(0)}(x)

is, up to a factor, the characteristic function of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
The solution to
\Delta U_{Q_{{\rm K}}}=4\pi\rho_{Q_{{\rm K}}}

and
U_{Q_{{\rm K}}}(x)\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty
is given by

U_{Q_{{\rm K}}}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c@{\quad:\quad}c}\frac{1}{2}\,|x|^{2}-\frac{3}{2}&|x|\leq 1\\[4.30554pt]
-\frac{1}{|x|}&|x|>1\end{array}\right..

(1.5)

Next consider the second-order ODE
\ddot{\phi}=-\frac{1}{\phi^{2}}+\frac{1}{\phi^{3}}

and let
\phi_{\varepsilon}
denote the solution such that
\phi_{\varepsilon}(0)=1
and
\dot{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(0)=\varepsilon
.
It follows that
\phi_{\varepsilon}
is periodic for
\varepsilon<1
(in fact
|\varepsilon|<1
), and its period is calculated to be

T_{\varepsilon}=\frac{2\pi}{(1-\varepsilon^{2})^{3/2}}
. Defining

f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)=Q_{{\rm K}}\Big(\frac{x}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)},\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)v-\dot{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(t)x\Big),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\quad x,v\in\mathbb{R}^{3},

(1.6)

the
f_{\varepsilon}(t)
are
T_{\varepsilon}
-periodic and (formal) solutions
to the gravitational Vlasov-Poisson system; see Lemma
3.2
below.
We may also write

f_{\varepsilon}(t)=Q_{{\rm K}}\circ\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(t),\quad\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(t)(x,v)=\Big(\frac{x}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)},\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)v-\dot{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(t)x\Big).

(1.7)

The associated density is

\rho_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)\,dv=\frac{3}{4\pi}\,\frac{1}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)^{3}}\,{\bf 1}_{\{|x|<\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)\}}=\frac{1}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)^{3}}\,\rho_{Q_{{\rm K}}}\Big(\frac{x}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)}\Big),

resulting in the potential

U_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\frac{1}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)}\,U_{Q_{{\rm K}}}\Big(\frac{x}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)}\Big).

(1.8)

The function
Q_{{\rm K}}
is spherically symmetric, hence so is
f_{\varepsilon}(t)
, since

f_{\varepsilon}(t,Ax,Av)=Q_{{\rm K}}\Big(A\frac{x}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)},A[\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)v-\dot{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(t)x]\Big)=Q_{{\rm K}}\Big(\frac{x}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)},\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)v-\dot{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(t)x\Big)=f_{\varepsilon}(t,x,v)

for
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
A\in{\rm SO}(3)
,
x,v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Therefore we may re-express everything
in the adapted spherically symmetric variables

r=|x|,\quad p_{r}=\frac{x\cdot v}{|x|},\quad\ell=|x\wedge v|.

To begin with,

\displaystyle e_{Q_{K}}(r,p_{r},\ell^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,|v|^{2}+U_{Q_{{\rm K}}}(r)=\frac{1}{2}\,p_{r}^{2}+U_{{\rm eff,K}}(r,\ell^{2}),

(1.9)

\displaystyle\quad U_{{\rm eff,K}}(r,\ell^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U_{Q_{{\rm K}}}(r)+\frac{\ell^{2}}{2r^{2}}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c@{\quad:\quad}c}\frac{r^{2}}{2}-\frac{3}{2}+\frac{\ell^{2}}{2r^{2}}&r\leq 1\\[4.30554pt]
-\frac{1}{r}+\frac{\ell^{2}}{2r^{2}}&r\geq 1\end{array}\right.,

by (
1.5
). Here
U_{{\rm eff,K}}
is called the effective potential,
and henceforth we will sometimes write
e
instead of
e_{Q_{{\rm K}}}
. Also,

1-|x|^{2}-|v|^{2}+|x\wedge v|^{2}=1-r^{2}-p_{r}^{2}-\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{2}}+\ell^{2}=-2(1+e)+\ell^{2},

(1.13)

so that

\displaystyle\tilde{Q}_{{\rm K}}(e,\ell^{2})=\frac{3}{4\pi^{3}}\,\frac{1}{(-2(1+e)+\ell^{2})^{1/2}}\quad\mbox{where}\quad(\ldots)>0\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,\ell<1,

\displaystyle\mbox{and}\quad\tilde{Q}_{{\rm K}}(e,\ell^{2})=0\quad\mbox{else}.

(1.14)

In spherically symmetric coordinates, the
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(t)
from (
1.7
) are identified with

\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(t)(r,p_{r})=\Big(\frac{r}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)},\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)p_{r}-\dot{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(t)r\Big),

(1.15)

since
f_{\varepsilon}(t)=Q_{{\rm K}}\circ\Lambda_{\varepsilon}(t)
and

\displaystyle 1-\Big|\frac{x}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)}\Big|^{2}-|\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)v-\dot{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(t)x|^{2}+\Big|\frac{x}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)}\wedge[\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)v-\dot{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(t)x]\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{r^{2}}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2}}-\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2}\,\Big(p_{r}^{2}+\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{2}}\Big)+2\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)\dot{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(t)\,rp_{r}-\dot{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2}r^{2}+\ell^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{r^{2}}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2}}-(\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)p_{r}-\dot{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(t)r)^{2}-\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2}\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{2}}+\ell^{2}=F\Big(\frac{r}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)},\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)p_{r}-\dot{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(t)r,\ell^{2}\Big)

for

F(r,p_{r},\ell^{2})=1-r^{2}-p_{r}^{2}-\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{2}}+\ell^{2}.

(1.16)

Now we are in position to describe the main result of this paper.
Writing
\beta=\ell^{2}
, the Kurth solution is

Q_{{\rm K}}=\tilde{Q}_{{\rm K}}(e_{Q},\beta)=\tilde{Q}_{{\rm K}}\Big(e_{Q}(r,p_{r},\beta),\beta\Big),

whereas the neighboring
f_{\varepsilon}(t)=f_{\varepsilon}(t,r,p_{r},\beta)
can be expressed as

f_{\varepsilon}(t)=\tilde{Q}_{{\rm K}}\Big(e_{Q}(R_{\varepsilon}(t),P_{\varepsilon}(t),B_{\varepsilon}(t)),B_{\varepsilon}(t)\Big)

for

\displaystyle R_{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R_{\varepsilon}(t,r,p_{r},\beta)=\frac{r}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)},

(1.17)

\displaystyle P_{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{\varepsilon}(t,r,p_{r},\beta)=\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)p_{r}-\dot{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(t)r,

(1.18)

\displaystyle B_{\varepsilon}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle B_{\varepsilon}(t,r,p_{r},\beta)=\beta,

(1.19)

according to (
1.15
), (
1.13
) and (
1.16
).
It should be remarked (as is verified in Lemma
3.3
below) that defining

H_{\varepsilon,{\rm K}}(t,r,p_{r},\beta)=-\frac{\dot{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(t)}{\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)}\,r\,p_{r}-\frac{1}{2}\,(\dot{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2}-\phi_{\varepsilon}(t)\ddot{\phi}_{\varepsilon}(t))\,r^{2},

then

\frac{d}{dt}\,Z_{\varepsilon}(t)=J\nabla H_{\varepsilon,{\rm K}}(t,Z_{\varepsilon}(t),B_{\varepsilon}(t)),

(1.20)

where

Z_{\varepsilon}=(R_{\varepsilon},P_{\varepsilon}),\quad J=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
-1&0\end{array}\right).

In other words, the time evolution of
Z_{\varepsilon}
is governed by
the time-dependent Hamiltonian
H_{\varepsilon,{\rm K}}
. Also note that both

R_{\varepsilon}
and
P_{\varepsilon}
are in fact independent of
\beta
, and
B_{\varepsilon}=\beta
is constant.

Thus, for a general steady state

Q(x,v)=\tilde{Q}(e_{Q},\beta)
, the question arises
if solutions
f
could be found that are of the form

f(t)=\tilde{Q}\Big(e_{Q}(R(t),P(t),B(t)),B(t)\Big)

(1.21)

for suitable functions
R
,
P
and
B
, all depending on
(t,r,p_{r},\beta)
,
such that the evolution of
Z=(R,P)
is Hamiltonian.
For the moment it will play no role if the
f
come in a family of
f_{\varepsilon}

that is close to
Q
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, or if the function(s) are periodic or not.

We are going to show that, basically, if
R
and
P
are independent of
\beta
,
and if
B=\beta
is constant, then
Q
already has to be the Kurth solution
Q_{{\rm K}}
.

Theorem 1.1

Suppose that the functions

(R,P,B)(t)=(R(t,r,p_{r},\beta),P(t,r,p_{r},\beta),B(t,r,p_{r},\beta))

are such that
f(t)=f(t,r,p_{r},\beta)

is a solution to the gravitational Vlasov-Poisson system and moreover
R
and
P

are independent of
\beta
, and
B=\beta
is constant:

(R,P,B)(t)=(R(t,r,p_{r}),P(t,r,p_{r}),\beta).

Let there exist a Hamiltonian
H=H(t,r,p_{r})
such that

\partial_{t}Z=J\nabla H(t,Z)
is satisfied for
Z=(R,P)
.
In addition, we assume that

(a)

\partial_{e}\tilde{Q}\neq 0
on the support of
Q

and
U^{\prime}_{Q}(0)=0
;

(b)

\partial_{r}R>0
and there is a function
\sigma(t)

such that

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{R(t,\delta,p_{r})}{\delta}=\sigma(t);

(1.22)

(c)

for the Jacobian of the map
(r,p_{r})\mapsto(R(0,r,p_{r}),P(0,r,p_{r}))

we have

\det\Big(\frac{\partial(R(0,r,p_{r}),P(0,r,p_{r}))}{\partial(r,p_{r})}\Big)=1.

(1.23)

Then, defining
\alpha=U^{\prime\prime}_{Q}(0)
, we must have

U^{\prime}_{Q}(r)=\alpha r,\quad R(t,r)=\frac{r}{\phi(t)},\quad P(t,r,p_{r})=\phi(t)p_{r}-\dot{\phi}(t)r,

where
\phi
solves

\ddot{\phi}(t)=\alpha\,\Big(-\frac{1}{\phi(t)^{2}}+\frac{1}{\phi(t)^{3}}\Big).

Remark 1.2

(a) The proof in Section
2
is a physics-style
calculation, and we are not very precise about, for instance, the regularity
of
\tilde{Q}
. However, this is not the main focus of the paper and missing details
could be filled in easily.

(b) The constant on the right-hand side of (
1.23
) needs not be
1
,
any other number
\neq 0
would also work.

(c) Concerning hypothesis (b), it will turn out in the proof
that
\partial_{r}R\neq 0
, see (
2.10
) below.
Thus we are going to assume
\partial_{r}R>0
without loss of generality.
Also
R=R(t,r)
will be shown to be independent of
p_{r}
.
Hence (
1.22
) means that in fact

\sigma(t)=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{R(t,\delta)}{\delta}=\partial_{r}R(t,0)

is required to exist. This can be guaranteed for instance
if we suppose that
H\in C^{2}
.

(d) When we started to look into the question if in general solutions
of the form (
1.21
) could be found, this author was convinced
that
\beta
should play no role for the argument, in the sense that
everything will be constant in
\beta
. Theorem
1.1
indicates that
actually the situation is much more complicated, and that, in a vague sense,
some ‘phase mixing’ would be needed in order that (
1.21
)
could provide a time-dependent solution.

.
Therefore (
2.9
) shows that
\det DZ(t)=1
, and hence

(\partial_{r}R)(t,r)(\partial_{p_{r}}P)(t,r,p_{r})-(\partial_{p_{r}}R)(t,r)(\partial_{r}P)(t,r,p_{r})=1,

which in our case is

(\partial_{r}R)(t,r)(\partial_{p_{r}}P)(t,r,p_{r})=1

(2.10)

for all
(t,r,p_{r})
. At fixed
t
we are going to apply the change of variables

(r,p_{r})\mapsto(R(t,r),P(t,r,p_{r}))=Z(t,r,p_{r})=(R,P)

to (
2.8
), which has
\det DZ(t,r,p_{r})=1
by (
2.10
)
and
R(t,0)=0
due to assumption (b). Then we get

\displaystyle\partial_{r}U_{f}(t,\tilde{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\tilde{r}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\beta\int_{0}^{R(t,\tilde{r})}dR\int\,dP\,\tilde{Q}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\,P^{2}+U(R)+\frac{\beta}{2R^{2}},\beta\Big)

(2.11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\tilde{r}^{2}}\int_{0}^{R(t,\tilde{r})}dR\int\,dP\int\,d\beta\,\tilde{Q}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\,P^{2}+U(R)+\frac{\beta}{2R^{2}},\beta\Big).

On the other hand,

\displaystyle U^{\prime}(\tilde{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi}{\tilde{r}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\tilde{r}}r^{2}\rho_{Q}(r)\,dr=\frac{4\pi}{\tilde{r}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\tilde{r}}dr\,r^{2}\int dv\,Q(x,v)

(2.12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\tilde{r}^{2}}\int_{0}^{\tilde{r}}dr\int dp_{r}\int d\beta\,\tilde{Q}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\,p_{r}^{2}+U(r)+\frac{\beta}{2r^{2}},\beta\Big).

Comparing (
2.11
) to (
2.12
), we have shown that

\partial_{r}U_{f}(t,r)=\frac{R(t,r)^{2}}{r^{2}}\,U^{\prime}(R(t,r))

(2.13)

is verified. From (
2.6
), (
2.13
) and (
2.7
)
it follows that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P(\partial_{t}P+p_{r}(\partial_{r}P))-P\,\partial_{r}U_{f}(t,r)(\partial_{p_{r}}P)+U^{\prime}(R)(\partial_{t}R+p_{r}(\partial_{r}R))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P(\partial_{t}P+p_{r}(\partial_{r}P))-P\,\frac{R^{2}}{r^{2}}\,U^{\prime}(R)\,(\partial_{p_{r}}P)+U^{\prime}(R)(\partial_{t}R+p_{r}(\partial_{r}R))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P(\partial_{t}P+p_{r}(\partial_{r}P))-P\,\frac{R^{2}}{r^{2}}\,U^{\prime}(R)\,(\partial_{p_{r}}P)+R^{3}P\frac{1}{r^{3}}(\partial_{p_{r}}P)\,U^{\prime}(R)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P}{r^{3}}\Big[r^{3}(\partial_{t}P+p_{r}(\partial_{r}P))-rR^{2}\,U^{\prime}(R)\,(\partial_{p_{r}}P)+R^{3}(\partial_{p_{r}}P)\,U^{\prime}(R)\Big],

so that

r^{3}(\partial_{t}P+p_{r}(\partial_{r}P))+R^{2}(R-r)\,U^{\prime}(R)\,(\partial_{p_{r}}P)=0.

(2.14)

Furthermore, by (
2.7
),

\partial_{p_{r}}P^{2}=2P(\partial_{p_{r}}P)=2\,\frac{r^{3}}{R^{3}}\,(\partial_{t}R+p_{r}(\partial_{r}R)),

and since
R
is independent of
p_{r}
, integration
\int dp_{r}
yields

P^{2}(t,r,p_{r})-P^{2}(t,r,0)=2\,\frac{r^{3}}{R^{3}}\,p_{r}\,(\partial_{t}R)+\frac{r^{3}}{R^{3}}\,p_{r}^{2}\,(\partial_{r}R).

(2.15)

In addition, integration
\int dp_{r}
of (
2.10
) leads to

(\partial_{r}R)(P(t,r,p_{r})-P(t,r,0))=p_{r}.

Combining this relation with (
2.15
), we get

P(t,r,p_{r})+P(t,r,0)=2\,(\partial_{r}R)\frac{r^{3}}{R^{3}}\,(\partial_{t}R)+(\partial_{r}R)\frac{r^{3}}{R^{3}}\,p_{r}\,(\partial_{r}R),

which in turn implies that

\displaystyle P(t,r,p_{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Big(P(t,r,p_{r})-P(t,r,0)+P(t,r,p_{r})+P(t,r,0)\Big)

(2.16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{p_{r}}{2\,\partial_{r}R}+(\partial_{r}R)\,\frac{r^{3}}{R^{3}}\,\Big(\partial_{t}R+\frac{1}{2}\,p_{r}\,(\partial_{r}R)\Big);

there are only
R
’s on the right-hand side, which are independent of
p_{r}
.
If we take the derivative w.r. to
t
, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{p_{r}}{2\,(\partial_{r}R)^{2}}\,(\partial^{2}_{tr}R)+(\partial^{2}_{tr}R)\,\frac{r^{3}}{R^{3}}\,\Big(\partial_{t}R+\frac{1}{2}\,p_{r}\,(\partial_{r}R)\Big)

\displaystyle-\,(\partial_{r}R)\,(\partial_{t}R)\,\frac{3r^{3}}{R^{4}}\,\Big(\partial_{t}R+\frac{1}{2}\,p_{r}\,(\partial_{r}R)\Big)+(\partial_{r}R)\,\frac{r^{3}}{R^{3}}\,\Big(\partial^{2}_{tt}R+\frac{1}{2}\,p_{r}\,(\partial^{2}_{tr}R)\Big).

Due to (
2.14
) and (
2.10
), we get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{3}\partial_{t}P+r^{3}p_{r}(\partial_{r}P)+R^{2}(R-r)\,U^{\prime}(R)\,(\partial_{p_{r}}P)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{3}\Big[-\frac{p_{r}}{2\,(\partial_{r}R)^{2}}\,(\partial^{2}_{tr}R)+(\partial^{2}_{tr}R)\,\frac{r^{3}}{R^{3}}\,\Big(\partial_{t}R+\frac{1}{2}\,p_{r}\,(\partial_{r}R)\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 15.00002pt-\,(\partial_{r}R)\,(\partial_{t}R)\,\frac{3r^{3}}{R^{4}}\,\Big(\partial_{t}R+\frac{1}{2}\,p_{r}\,(\partial_{r}R)\Big)+(\partial_{r}R)\,\frac{r^{3}}{R^{3}}\,\Big(\partial^{2}_{tt}R+\frac{1}{2}\,p_{r}\,(\partial^{2}_{tr}R)\Big)+p_{r}(\partial_{r}P)\Big]

\displaystyle+R^{2}(R-r)\,U^{\prime}(R)\,\frac{1}{(\partial_{r}R)}.

Taking
p_{r}=0
, this yields

0=\frac{r^{6}}{R^{3}}\Big[(\partial^{2}_{tr}R)\,(\partial_{t}R)-(\partial_{r}R)\,(\partial_{t}R)^{2}\,\frac{3}{R}+(\partial_{r}R)\,(\partial^{2}_{tt}R)\Big]+R^{2}(R-r)\,U^{\prime}(R)\,\frac{1}{(\partial_{r}R)}.

(2.17)

If
t
is fixed, then the map
(r,p_{r})\mapsto(R(t,r),P(t,r,p_{r}))=Z(t,r,p_{r})

is symplectic, owing to (
2.10
), and it is generated by the ‘point transformation’

r\mapsto R(t,r)
. Thus, using
[
9
, equ. (1.44)]
, there is a scalar function

v=v(t,r)
such that

P(t,r,p_{r})=\frac{1}{\partial_{r}R(t,r)}\,(p_{r}-\partial_{r}v(t,r)).

(2.18)

Therefore, from (
2.16
),

\frac{1}{\partial_{r}R}\,(p_{r}-\partial_{r}v(t,r))=P=\frac{p_{r}}{2\,\partial_{r}R}+(\partial_{r}R)\,\frac{r^{3}}{R^{3}}\,\Big(\partial_{t}R+\frac{1}{2}\,p_{r}\,(\partial_{r}R)\Big).

(2.19)

Taking once again
p_{r}=0
, we see that

\partial_{r}v(t,r)=-(\partial_{r}R)^{2}\,(\partial_{t}R)\,\frac{r^{3}}{R^{3}}.

(2.20)

If we plug this relation back to (
2.19
), it follows that

(\partial_{r}R)^{3}\,\frac{r^{3}}{R^{3}}=1,

or

\partial_{r}R=\frac{R}{r}.

For
\delta>0
integration yields

R(t,r)=r\,\frac{R(t,\delta)}{\delta}\,e^{C(t)}

for a suitable function
C(t)
. By assumption, taking the limit
\delta\to 0
,
we get

R(t,r)=a(t)r,

where
a(t)=\sigma(t)e^{C(t)}
. Thus (
2.17
) simplifies to

0=\frac{r^{3}}{a(t)^{3}}\Big[\dot{a}(t)^{2}r-a(t)\,\dot{a}(t)^{2}r^{2}\,\frac{3}{a(t)r}+a(t)\ddot{a}(t)r\Big]+a(t)^{2}r^{3}(a(t)-1)\,U^{\prime}(a(t)r)\,\frac{1}{a(t)},

which is

U^{\prime}(a(t)r)=\frac{2\dot{a}(t)^{2}-a(t)\ddot{a}(t)}{a(t)^{4}(a(t)-1)}\,r.

Replacing
a(t)r
by
r
, this leads to

U^{\prime}(r)=\frac{2\dot{a}(t)^{2}-a(t)\ddot{a}(t)}{a(t)^{5}(a(t)-1)}\,r.

Since the variables are separated, we deduce that there is
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\frac{U^{\prime}(r)}{r}=\alpha=\frac{2\dot{a}(t)^{2}-a(t)\ddot{a}(t)}{a(t)^{5}(a(t)-1)}

(2.21)

for all
(t,r)
. Thus if we set
\phi(t)=\frac{1}{a(t)}
,
then
\dot{\phi}(t)=-\frac{\dot{a}(t)}{a(t)^{2}}
and, by (
2.21
),

\ddot{\phi}(t)=-\frac{\ddot{a}(t)}{a(t)^{2}}+2\,\frac{\dot{a}(t)^{2}}{a(t)^{3}}=\frac{1}{a(t)^{3}}\,(2\dot{a}(t)^{2}-a(t)\ddot{a}(t))=\alpha\,a(t)^{2}(a(t)-1)=\alpha\,\Big(-\frac{1}{\phi(t)^{2}}+\frac{1}{\phi(t)^{3}}\Big).

Moreover, we also have

R(t,r)=\frac{r}{\phi(t)}.

Next, using (
2.20
),

\partial_{r}v(t,r)=-a(t)^{2}\,\dot{a}(t)r\,\frac{r^{3}}{a(t)^{3}r^{3}}=-\frac{\dot{a}(t)}{a(t)}\,r=\frac{\dot{\phi}(t)}{\phi(t)}\,r,

and hence upon integration

v(t,r)=\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\dot{\phi}(t)}{\phi(t)}\,r^{2}+\gamma(t)

for a suitable function
\gamma(t)
. Thus (
2.18
) implies that

P(t,r,p_{r})=\frac{1}{\partial_{r}R(t,r)}\,(p_{r}-\partial_{r}v(t,r))=\phi(t)\,\Big(p_{r}-\frac{\dot{\phi}(t)}{\phi(t)}\,r\Big)=\phi(t)\,p_{r}-\dot{\phi}(t)\,r.

Lastly,
U^{\prime}(0)=0
in conjunction with (
2.21
) shows that

\alpha=\lim_{r\to 0}\frac{U^{\prime}(r)}{r}=U^{\prime\prime}(0).

This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

\Box

Lemma 3.1

One has

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}Q_{{\rm K}}(x,v)\,dv=\frac{3}{4\pi}\,{\bf 1}_{B_{1}(0)}(x)

Proof :
We only consider
r=|x|<1
. First note that

1-|x|^{2}-|v|^{2}+|x\wedge v|^{2}=1-r^{2}-p_{r}^{2}-\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{2}}+\ell^{2}>0

means that
p_{r}^{2}<(1-r^{2})(1-\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{2}})\leq 1-r^{2}
and also

\ell^{2}<r^{2}-\frac{r^{2}p_{r}^{2}}{1-r^{2}}=:\ell_{0}^{2}.

In spherical symmetry we have
dv=\frac{2\pi}{r^{2}}\,dp_{r}\,d\ell\,\ell
.
Therefore by (
1
),

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{3}}Q_{{\rm K}}(x,v)\,dv

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\pi}{r^{2}}\int_{|p_{r}|\leq\sqrt{1-r^{2}}}dp_{r}\int_{0}^{\ell_{0}}d\ell\,\ell\,Q_{{\rm K}}(r,p_{r},\ell)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2\pi^{2}r^{2}}\int_{|p_{r}|\leq\sqrt{1-r^{2}}}dp_{r}\int_{0}^{\ell_{0}}d\ell\,\ell\,\frac{1}{(1-r^{2}-p_{r}^{2}-\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{2}}+\ell^{2})^{1/2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2\pi^{2}}\,\frac{1}{1-r^{2}}\int_{|p_{r}|\leq\sqrt{1-r^{2}}}dp_{r}\int_{0}^{\ell_{0}}d\ell\,(-1)\frac{d}{d\ell}\,\Big(1-r^{2}-p_{r}^{2}-\frac{\ell^{2}}{r^{2}}+\ell^{2}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2\pi^{2}}\,\frac{1}{1-r^{2}}\int_{|p_{r}|\leq\sqrt{1-r^{2}}}dp_{r}\,(1-r^{2}-p_{r}^{2})^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{\pi^{2}}\,\int_{0}^{1}ds\,(1-s^{2})^{1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3}{4\pi},

as was to be shown.

\Box
