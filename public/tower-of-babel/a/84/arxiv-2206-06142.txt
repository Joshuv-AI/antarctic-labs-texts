Correlation length of the angular mode for an approximate U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) symmetry during inflation
Chengcheng Han

Abstract
It is known that a light scalar field obtains fluctuations in the de Sitter inflationary background. Such fluctuations could provide an initial condition for baryogenesis through the Affleck-Dine mechanism, where an approximate U ​ ( 1 ) B U(1)_{B} symmetry is usually assumed. However, an interpretation of the baryon number generation in this way is strongly related to the correlation length of the angular mode. In this work, we calculate the correlation length of the angular mode for a model exhibiting an approximate U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) symmetry. We find that for a massive nearly non-interacting field, the correlation length of the angular mode is determined by the mass parameter of the model and it is similar to H − 1 ​ exp ⁡ ( H 2 / m 2 ) H^{-1}\exp(H^{2}/m^{2}) . Applying this result to baryogenesis via the Affleck-Dine mechanism with a stochastic origin, we find that only for m ≪ 𝒪 ⁡ ( 0.1 ) ​ H m\ll\mathcal{O}(0.1)H (assuming N ∗ = 60 N_{*}=60 ) can the correlation length of the baryon number density be much larger than our current horizon size, such that we live in the baryon-rich region. If this is not true, at early times our universe would consist of numerous patches of baryon-rich and anti-baryon-rich regions with the average baryon number being nearly zero.

It is widely believed the universe undergoes fast expansion in its early stage (inflation), characterized by a quasi-de Sitter space-time

The period of inflation not only solves the flatness problem, the horizon problem, but also seeds the primordial fluctuations of the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) temperature, which finally resulted in the large structure of our universe. 2
General formalism

In this section, we present the general formalism for our calculation. This part is based on the work
Starobinsky:1994bd
and we just summarize the main result here. During inflation, the scalar fields follow a stochastic process, which can be described by the Fokker-Planck equation,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho(\varphi,t)}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{3H}\left[\rho(\varphi,t)\nabla^{2}V(\varphi)+\nabla V(\varphi)\cdot\nabla\rho(\varphi,t)\right]+\frac{H^{3}}{8\pi^{2}}\nabla^{2}\rho(\varphi,t)~,

(1)

where
V(\varphi)
is the potential for
\varphi
and
\rho(\varphi,t)
is the probability distribution function. Here
\nabla
only acts on the field space.

To solve the Fokker-Planck equation, one can assume,

\displaystyle\rho(\varphi,t)=\exp\left(-\frac{4\pi^{2}V(\varphi)}{3H^{4}}\right)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\Phi_{n}(\varphi)e^{-{\Lambda_{n}}(t-t_{0})}~.

(2)

Define

\displaystyle v(\varphi)\equiv\frac{4\pi^{2}}{3H^{4}}V(\varphi)~,

(3)

then
\Phi_{n}
satisfies the following eigenvalue equation,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[-\nabla^{2}+\left({\nabla v(\varphi)}^{2}-\nabla^{2}v(\varphi)\right)\right]\Phi_{n}(\varphi)=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{H^{3}}\Lambda_{n}\Phi_{n}(\varphi)~.

(4)

The coefficients
a_{n}
are given by the initial condition of
\rho
at
t=t_{0}
as

\displaystyle a_{n}=\int d\varphi\rho(\varphi,t_{0})e^{v(\varphi)}\Phi_{n}(\varphi)~.

(5)

Note that there is always a solution which is called the static equilibrium solution with
\Lambda_{0}=0
.

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}(\varphi)=\mathcal{N}^{-1/2}e^{-v(\varphi)}~,

(6)

where
\mathcal{N}
is calculated by normalization condition

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\equiv\int e^{-2v(\varphi)}d\varphi~.

(7)

After enough time has passed, any solution will finally approach the static equilibrium solution,

\displaystyle\rho_{q}(\varphi)\equiv\mathcal{N}^{-1}\exp\left(-\frac{8\pi^{2}}{3H^{4}}V(\varphi)\right)=\mathcal{N}^{-1}e^{-2v(\varphi)}~.

(8)

We can calculate the average of any variable
F(\varphi)
and
F^{2}(\varphi)
in terms of the equilibrium solution,

\displaystyle\langle F\rangle=\int d\varphi F(\varphi)\rho_{q}(\varphi)~,

(9)

and

\displaystyle\langle F^{2}\rangle=\int d\varphi F^{2}(\varphi)\rho_{q}(\varphi)~.

(10)

On the other hand, in the equilibrium state, we can derive the correlation function for any variable
F(\varphi)
,

\displaystyle G_{F}(|t_{1}-t_{2}|)\equiv\langle F(\varphi({\bf x},t_{1}))F(\varphi({\bf x},t_{2}))\rangle~.

(11)

We have

\displaystyle G_{F}(t)=\int F(\varphi)\Xi(\varphi,t)d\varphi~,

(12)

where

\displaystyle\Xi(\varphi,t)\equiv\int F(\varphi_{1})\rho_{q}(\varphi_{1})\Pi[\varphi(t)|\varphi_{1}(0)]d\varphi_{1}~,

(13)

satisfying the Fokker-Planck equation with the initial condition,

\displaystyle\Xi(\varphi,0)=F(\varphi)\rho_{q}(\varphi)~,

(14)

and
\Pi[\varphi_{2}(t)|\varphi_{1}(0)]
is the conditional probability to find
\varphi(t)=\varphi_{2}
provided that
\varphi(0)=\varphi_{1}
.
Since
\Xi(\varphi,t)
satisfies the Fokker-Planck equation, it can be written as,

\displaystyle\Xi(\varphi,t)=\exp\left(-\frac{4\pi^{2}V(\varphi)}{3H^{4}}\right)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A_{n}\Phi_{n}(\varphi)e^{-{\Lambda_{n}}t}~,

(15)

where

\displaystyle A_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int F(\varphi)\rho_{q}(\varphi)e^{v}\Phi_{n}(\varphi)d\varphi=\mathcal{N}^{-1}\int F(\varphi)e^{-v}\Phi_{n}(\varphi)d\varphi

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{-1/2}\int F(\varphi)\Phi_{0}\Phi_{n}(\varphi)d\varphi~.

Then we have

\displaystyle G_{F}(|t|)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}A_{n}e^{-\Lambda_{n}t}\int F(\varphi)e^{-v}\Phi_{n}(\varphi)d\varphi=\mathcal{N}\sum_{n=0}A_{n}^{2}e^{-\Lambda_{n}t}=\sum_{n=0}\tilde{A}_{n}^{2}e^{-\Lambda_{n}t}~,

(17)

with

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int F(\varphi)\Phi_{0}(\varphi)\Phi_{n}(\varphi)d\varphi~.

(18)

For a long enough period of time, the dominant contribution of
G_{F}(t)
comes from the lowest state
\Lambda_{\rm min}
with
\tilde{A}_{\rm min}\neq 0
. We can use the above formula to calculate the average of the variable
F
and
F^{2}
,

\displaystyle\langle F\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\int F(\varphi)\rho_{q}d\varphi\right)=\left(\int F(\varphi)\Phi_{0}(\varphi)\Phi_{0}(\varphi)d\varphi\right)=\tilde{A}_{0}~,

\displaystyle{\langle F^{2}\rangle}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G_{F}(0)=\sum_{n=0}\tilde{A}_{n}^{2}~.

(19)

If we define the fluctuations of
F
as
\delta F=F-\langle F\rangle
, then

\displaystyle{\langle(\delta F)^{2}\rangle}=\langle F^{2}\rangle-\langle F\rangle^{2}=\sum_{n=1}\tilde{A}_{n}^{2}~.

(20)

For the spatial correlation function
\xi_{F}({\bf x})=\langle F(\varphi({\bf x_{1}},t))F(\varphi({\bf x_{2}},t))\rangle
(
{\bf x=x_{1}-x_{2}}
), in the large
{\bf|x|}
limit, we just need to take
t\rightarrow 2H^{-1}\log(aH{\bf|x|})
,

\displaystyle\xi_{F}({\bf x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}\tilde{A}_{n}^{2}(aH{\bf|x|})^{-2\Lambda_{n}H^{-1}}~.

(21)

For the case of
\tilde{A}_{0}=0
, we can define the correlation length that the variable
F(\varphi)
satisfies,

\displaystyle\xi_{F}(R_{c})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle G_{F}(0)/2~.

(22)

Then within the length scale
R_{c}
we can take
F
approximately as a constant. Usually this scale is decided by the lowest eigenvalue
\Lambda_{\min}
with
\tilde{A}_{\rm min}\neq 0
.

3
Example for a single field

In this section, we will use the above formulas to calculate the example of a single scalar field where only a mass term is included. These results are already known and there is nothing new here. But this result is important to allow us to extend to the case of multi-dimensions, particularly into 2-dimensions which we are interested in.
Now the potential is

\displaystyle V(\varphi)=\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\varphi^{2},~~~v(\varphi)\equiv\frac{4\pi^{2}}{3H^{4}}V(\varphi)~.

(23)

The eigenvalue equation is,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\varphi^{2}}+\left({v^{\prime}(\varphi)}^{2}-v^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)\right)\right]\psi_{n}(\varphi)=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{H^{3}}\Lambda_{n}\psi_{n}(\varphi)~.

(24)

We can define an effective potential as,

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}=\frac{1}{2}\left(v^{\prime}(\varphi)^{2}-v^{\prime\prime}(\varphi)\right)=\frac{1}{2}\omega^{2}\varphi^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\omega~,

(25)

where
\omega=\frac{4\pi^{2}m^{2}}{3H^{4}}
. Then the eigenvalue equation becomes,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\varphi^{2}}+\omega^{2}\varphi^{2}\right]\psi_{n}(\varphi)-\frac{1}{2}\omega\psi_{n}(\varphi)=\frac{4\pi^{2}}{H^{3}}\Lambda_{n}\psi_{n}(\varphi)~.

(26)

This is nothing but the eigenvalue equation for an oscillator with a frequency
\omega
. The difference is that the zero energy has been removed by the terms
-\frac{1}{2}\omega\psi_{n}(\varphi)
. One can easily find that the eigenvalues are,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{n}=\frac{H^{3}}{4\pi^{2}}n\omega=\frac{nm^{2}}{3H},~n=0,1,2...~.

(27)

The eigenfunctions can be written as,

\displaystyle\psi_{n}=N_{n}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\alpha^{2}\varphi^{2}\right)H_{n}(\alpha\varphi),~~N_{n}=(\alpha/\sqrt{\pi}2^{n}n!)^{1/2}~,

(28)

with
\alpha=\sqrt{\omega}
and
H_{n}
is the Hermite polynomials. For simplicity, we can use the Dirac Bra-ket notation to describe the eigenfunction
\psi_{n}
as
|n\rangle
with the eigenvalue
\Lambda_{n}
. We also have the following relation,

\displaystyle\varphi|n\rangle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\sqrt{\frac{n}{2}}|n-1\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{n+1}{2}}|n+1\rangle\right]~.

(29)

It is then not difficult to find that for
F(\varphi)=\varphi^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle F\rangle=\langle\varphi^{2}\rangle=\langle 0|\varphi^{2}|0\rangle=\frac{1}{2\alpha^{2}}=\frac{3H^{4}}{8\pi^{2}m^{2}}~.

(30)

For
F(\varphi)=\varphi
, we have

\displaystyle\langle 0|\varphi|0\rangle=0,~~\langle 0|\varphi|1\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\alpha}~=\tilde{A}_{1}

(31)

The spatial correlation function of
F(\varphi)=\varphi
is

\displaystyle\xi({\bf x})=|\tilde{A}_{1}|^{2}(aH{\bf|x|})^{-2\Lambda_{1}H^{-1}}=\langle\varphi^{2}\rangle(aH{\bf|x|})^{-2\Lambda_{1}H^{-1}}~.

(32)

Then we have

\displaystyle R_{c}=H^{-1}\exp{\left(\log 2/2\Lambda_{1}\right)}=H^{-1}\exp{\left(\frac{3{\rm\log}2}{2}\frac{H^{2}}{m^{2}}\right)}\approx H^{-1}\exp{\left(\frac{H^{2}}{m^{2}}\right)}~,

(33)

therefore, the correlation length is totally decided by the mass of the field(we set the
a=1
at the end of inflation). For
N_{*}=60
, if the mass is
m\lesssim 0.1H
, the correlation length
\varphi
can be much larger than our observed universe. If
m\gtrsim 0.13H
(assuming
N_{*}=60
), in the early time our current universe is composed of different patches with different
\varphi
following a distribution described by
\rho_{q}(\varphi)
.

Correlation length of the angular mode for an exact
U(1)
symmetry

In this section, we will calculate the correlation length of the angular mode for an exact
U(1)
symmetry. In this case, the
\theta
is massless and the correlation length may appear to be very large. We will show that this naive estimation is wrong. For multi-dimension Fokker-Planck equation,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{2}\Phi_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\left[(\nabla v)^{2}-\nabla^{2}v\right]\Phi_{n}=\frac{4\pi^{2}\Lambda_{n}}{H^{3}}\Phi_{n}~.

(34)

Let us look at a simple case,

\displaystyle V(\phi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m^{2}|\phi|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\phi_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\phi_{2}^{2}~,

(35)

where
\phi=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\phi_{1}+i\phi_{2})
. From our previous calculation, it shows that the universe is divided into different patches with a correlation length of
R_{c}\approx H^{-1}\exp\left(\frac{H^{2}}{m^{2}}\right)
. However, if we use the radial-angular coordinates
\phi=\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}}e^{i\theta}
, we have

\displaystyle V(\phi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m^{2}|\phi|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}m^{2}r^{2}~.

(36)

Then we have one massive mode and one massless mode. One may argue that the correlation length for
\theta
should be much larger than
R_{c}
. We will show that it is not true through two different coordinate representations.

,
\phi_{2}

\phi_{1},\phi_{2}

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(\phi_{1})\tilde{\psi}_{l}(\phi_{2}),~\Lambda_{n,l}=\frac{(n+l)m^{2}}{3H},~n,l=0,1,2...~.

There is a degeneracy for the eigenvalue

\displaystyle\Psi_{0,0}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(\phi_{1})\tilde{\psi}_{0}(\phi_{2}),~\Lambda_{0}=0

\displaystyle\Psi_{0,1}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(\phi_{1})\tilde{\psi}_{1}(\phi_{2}),~\Lambda_{0,1}=\frac{m^{2}}{3H}

\displaystyle\Psi_{1,0}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(\phi_{1})\tilde{\psi}_{0}(\phi_{2}),~\Lambda_{1,0}=\frac{m^{2}}{3H}~.

(40)

We can also use the Dirac Bra-ket notation

(55)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int R_{0,0}(r)R_{0,1}(r)rdr\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}~.

Numerically, we find
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}_{0,1}\approx 0.63
, which is totally consistent with the previous calculation in which we used the
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
coordinates.

5
Correlation length of the angular mode for an approximate
U(1)
symmetry

For the case of an approximate
U(1)
symmetry, we add a small breaking term for the
U(1)
symmetry, which is a necessary condition for the baryogenesis by the Affleck-Dine mechanism. In this scenario, one may argue that for the approximate
U(1)
symmetry,
\theta
is not exactly massless and obtains a finite (small) mass, such that the correlation length could be much longer. In the following, we will show, that even if
U(1)
is an approximate symmetry, the correlation length of
\theta
is still close to
R_{c}
.
Let us consider an example,

\displaystyle V(\phi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m^{2}|\phi|^{2}+\left[\frac{1}{2}|\mu|^{2}\phi^{2}+h.c\right]

(56)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(m^{2}+|\mu|^{2})\phi_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(m^{2}-|\mu|^{2})\phi_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}m_{1}^{2}\phi_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m_{2}^{2}\phi_{2}^{2}~,

where the
|\mu|^{2}
term breaks the
U(1)
symmetry and we assume that such a term is much smaller than the
m^{2}
term. In this case, we still have an exact solution.

\displaystyle\Psi_{n,l}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(\phi_{1})\tilde{\psi}_{l}(\phi_{2}),~\Lambda_{n,l}=\frac{(nm_{1}^{2}+lm_{2}^{2})}{3H},~n,l=0,1,2...~.

(57)

Here we list some of the wave functions with lowest eigenvalues,

\displaystyle\Psi_{0,0}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(\phi_{1})\tilde{\psi}_{0}(\phi_{2}),~\Lambda_{0}=0

(58)

\displaystyle\Psi_{0,1}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi_{0}(\phi_{1})\tilde{\psi}_{1}(\phi_{2}),~\Lambda_{1}=\frac{m^{2}_{2}}{3H^{2}}

(59)

\displaystyle\Psi_{1,0}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi_{1}(\phi_{1})\tilde{\psi}_{0}(\phi_{2}),~\Lambda_{2}=\frac{m^{2}_{1}}{3H^{2}}~.

(60)

The degeneracy of the spectrum has been broken because
m_{1}>m_{2}
. Now again, the lowest eigenvalue with non-vanishing
\tilde{A}
is
\frac{m^{2}_{2}}{3H}
with

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{0,1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{\phi_{2}}{\sqrt{\phi_{1}^{2}+\phi_{2}^{2}}}\Psi_{0,0}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})\Psi_{0,1}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})d\phi_{1}d\phi_{2}~.

(61)

For
|\mu|^{2}\ll m^{2}
, the value of
\tilde{A}_{0,1}
is essentially not changed. But the eigenvalue has been changed from
\frac{m^{2}}{3H^{2}}
into
\frac{m^{2}_{2}}{3H^{2}}
and the correlation length indeed becomes larger

\displaystyle R_{\sin\theta}\approx H^{-1}\exp\left({H^{2}}/{(m^{2}-|\mu|^{2})}\right)~.

(62)

Since we are considering the case of
\mu^{2}\ll m^{2}
, this value is still close to
R_{c}
. More importantly, in the limit of
\mu^{2}\rightarrow 0
, we get the exact
U(1)
symmetry result.

To be complete, we also calculate the result in the
r,\theta
coordinates. In this case we can use the formulas used in perturbation theory for quantum mechanics. We have

\displaystyle V(\phi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m^{2}|\phi|^{2}+\left[\frac{1}{2}|\mu|^{2}\phi^{2}+h.c\right]

(63)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}m^{2}r^{2}+\frac{1}{2}|\mu|^{2}r^{2}\cos 2\theta~.

Since

\displaystyle\Phi_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{-1/2}e^{-v}~,

(64)

obviously

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{0}=\int\Phi_{0}^{2}\sin\theta rdrd\theta=0~.

(65)

Note that
\Phi_{0}\neq\Phi^{(0)}_{0,0}
since
\Phi_{0}
is the exact eigenfunction with the breaking term included. Now we need to find out the lowest eigenvalue with
\tilde{A}\neq 0
.
The effective potential can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left[(\nabla v)^{2}-\nabla^{2}v\right]=\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{4\pi^{2}}{3H^{4}}\right)^{2}m^{4}r^{2}\frac{4\pi^{2}m^{2}}{3H^{4}}\right]+\left(\frac{4\pi^{2}}{3H^{4}}\right)^{2}|\mu|^{2}m^{2}r^{2}\cos 2\theta+\mathcal{O}(\mu^{4})

(66)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({1}/{2}\omega_{0}^{2}r^{2}-\omega_{0}\right)+\left(\omega_{0}\omega_{1}r^{2}\right)\equiv\mathcal{V}_{0}+\mathcal{V}_{b}~,

where we define
\omega_{0}=\frac{4\pi^{2}m^{2}}{3H^{4}}
and
\omega_{1}=\frac{4\pi^{2}|\mu|^{2}}{3H^{4}}
.
Clearly
\mathcal{V}_{b}
breaks the degeneracy of the eigenvalues for states with

\displaystyle\Phi^{(0)}_{0,1}(r,\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R^{(0)}_{0,1}(r)\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\cos\theta,

\displaystyle{\Phi^{\prime}}^{(0)}_{0,1}(r,\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R^{(0)}_{0,1}(r)\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\sin\theta~.

(67)

However, it does not mix these two states, and the eigenvalues of each state become,

\displaystyle\lambda^{b}_{0,1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{0,1}^{(0)}+\frac{H^{3}}{4\pi^{2}}\int{{\Phi_{0,1}^{(0)}}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{b}rdrd\theta

\displaystyle{\lambda^{\prime}}^{b}_{0,1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{0,1}^{(0)}+\frac{H^{3}}{4\pi^{2}}\int{{{{\Phi^{\prime}}^{(0)}_{0,1}}}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{b}rdrd\theta~.

(68)

We have

\displaystyle\frac{H^{3}}{4\pi^{2}}\int{{\Phi_{0,1}^{(0)}}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{b}rdrd\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\omega_{0}}{3H}\frac{|\mu|^{2}}{2}\int{R^{(0)}_{0,1}}^{2}{r^{3}}dr

\displaystyle\frac{H^{3}}{4\pi^{2}}\int{{{{\Phi^{\prime}}^{(0)}_{0,1}}}}^{2}\mathcal{V}_{b}rdrd\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\omega_{0}}{3H}\frac{|\mu|^{2}}{2}\int{R^{(0)}_{0,1}}^{2}{r^{3}}dr~.

(69)

The last integration seems to be not easily determined. Notice that it is the average of
r^{2}=\phi_{1}^{2}+\phi_{2}^{2}
for the state with
\Lambda=\frac{m^{2}}{3H}
. We can use the
\phi_{1}

\phi_{2}
coordinates to estimate this integration, since we have

\displaystyle\langle\Psi^{(0)}_{0,1}|\phi_{1}^{2}+\phi_{2}^{2}|\Psi^{(0)}_{0,1}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\Psi^{(0)}_{1,0}|\phi_{1}^{2}+\phi_{2}^{2}|\Psi^{(0)}_{1,0}\rangle=\frac{2}{\omega_{0}}~.

(70)

then we get

\displaystyle\int{R^{(0)}_{0,1}}^{2}{r^{3}}dr=\frac{2}{\omega_{0}}~.

(71)

In the end we find

\displaystyle\lambda^{b}_{0,1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(m^{2}+|\mu|^{2})\frac{1}{3H}

\displaystyle{\lambda^{\prime}}^{b}_{0,1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(m^{2}-|\mu|^{2})\frac{1}{3H}~.

(72)

Since we have
\mu^{2}\ll m^{2}
, we do not expect
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{0,1}
to change too much. The essential thing here is that the
\lambda
has matched the value of the exact solutions in the
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
representation and the correlation length is determined by the eigenvalue. Since the correlation length is mainly determined by the lowest eigenvalue with
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}\neq 0
, we arrive at the same conclusion, that the correlation length of the angular mode should not be much larger than
R_{c}
.

One can also extend this result to other small
U(1)
breaking cases, from which we can then conclude that even if a small breaking term is included, the correlation length
\theta
can not be much larger than
R_{c}
.

6
Correlation length for the baryon number density

It is known that a scalar field which carries baryon charge can generate a baryon asymmetry through the Affleck-Dine mechanism. In the following, we analyse the correlation length of the baryon number density. The displacement of the field originates from quantum fluctuations. For the potential, we have

\displaystyle V(\phi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m^{2}|\phi|^{2}+\left[\kappa_{n}\phi^{n}+h.c\right]~,

(73)

and

\displaystyle\dot{n}_{B}+3Hn_{B}={\rm Im}(\phi\frac{\partial V}{\partial\phi})~.

(74)

The baryon number density at
H=m
can be estimated as,

\displaystyle n_{B}\sim\frac{n\kappa_{n}{\rm Im}(\phi^{n})}{m}=\frac{n\kappa_{n}r^{n}\sin n\theta_{0}}{2^{n/2}m}~.

(75)

Since
\sin n\theta_{0}
is randomly distributed, the average is given by
\langle n_{B}\rangle=0
. But this just means that the whole universe has an average baryon number of zero, but not necessarily in our observed universe. If the correlation length of
n_{B}
is much larger than our current universe, we may still live in a region with a positive baryon number. To calculate the correlation length, we set
n=4
as an example,

\displaystyle V(\phi)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m^{2}|\phi|^{2}+\left[\kappa\phi^{4}+h.c\right]

(76)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}m^{2}r^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\kappa r^{4}\cos 4\theta~.

The baryon number density is

\displaystyle n_{B}=\frac{\kappa r^{4}\sin 4\theta}{2m}=\kappa\frac{4\phi_{1}\phi_{2}(\phi_{1}^{2}-\phi_{2}^{2})}{2m}~.

(77)

The correlation function is

\displaystyle\langle n_{B}(0)n_{B}(t)\rangle=\sum\tilde{A}_{n}^{2}e^{-\Lambda_{n}t}~,

(78)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int n_{B}\Phi_{0}(\varphi)\Phi_{n}(\varphi)d\varphi~.

(79)

Due to the
\sin 4\theta
term, the major contribution is from
\Phi_{0,4}
with eigenvalue
\lambda_{0,4}=\frac{4m^{2}}{3H}
. Numerically, we find that

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{0,4}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\frac{\kappa r^{4}}{2m}R_{0,0}(r)R_{0,4}rdr\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{1.8\kappa}{m\omega^{2}}~.

(80)

Then

\displaystyle\langle n_{B}(0)n_{B}(t)\rangle\approx\frac{3\kappa^{2}}{m^{2}\omega^{4}}e^{-\frac{4m^{2}}{3H}t}~.

(81)

It is also useful to consider the
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}
coordinates. For
\Lambda=\frac{4m^{2}}{3H}
, there are five states,

|0,4\rangle
,
|4,0\rangle
,
|1,3\rangle
,
|3,1\rangle
,
|2,2\rangle
. By defining
f(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})=4\phi_{1}\phi_{2}(\phi_{1}^{2}-\phi_{2}^{2})
, it is not difficult to find that

\displaystyle\langle 0,0|f(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})|4,0\rangle=\langle 0,0|f(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})|0,4\rangle=\langle 0,0|f(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})|2,2\rangle=0~.

(82)

Then we only need to calculate,

\displaystyle|\langle 0,0|f(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})|1,3\rangle|=|\langle 0,0|f(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})|3,1\rangle|=\frac{\sqrt{6}}{\omega^{2}}~.

(83)

Now we have two non-vanishing
\tilde{A}
, which when summed together gives,

\displaystyle\langle n_{B}(0)n_{B}(t)\rangle=\frac{3\kappa^{2}}{m^{2}\omega^{4}}e^{-\frac{4m^{2}}{3H}t}~.

(84)

The spatial correlation of
n_{B}
is

\displaystyle\langle n_{B}(0)n_{B}({\bf x})\rangle\approx\frac{3\kappa^{2}}{m^{2}\omega^{4}}(aH{\bf|x|})^{-\frac{8m^{2}}{3H^{2}}}~,

(85)

which is consistent with the work
Hook:2015foa
.
Now we find that the correlation length of
n_{B}
is

\displaystyle R_{n_{B}}\approx H^{-1}\exp{\left(\frac{H^{2}}{4m^{2}}\right)}~.

(86)

which is much smaller than
R_{c}
. Note that even if
\langle n_{B}\rangle=0
, once
R_{n_{B}}
is much larger than our current universe, we could live in a baryon-rich universe, which is the case for
m\ll 0.06H
for
N_{*}=60
.

To end, we give one example of successful baryogenesis models despite the angular mode having a
R_{c}
that is much smaller than our current universe. One such example is baryogenesis from sneutrino decay
Hamaguchi:2001gw
, the total baryon number is

\displaystyle{n_{B}}=\epsilon n_{\phi}\approx{\epsilon}m|\phi|^{2}~.

(87)

Since
n_{B}
is independent of the
\theta
, in the whole universe we could have the same sign of
n_{B}
.

7
Discussion and conclusion

In this work, we calculated the correlation length of the angular mode for models with an approximate
U(1)
symmetry. We find that for a massive nearly non-interacting fields, the correlation length of the angular mode is determined by the mass parameter, which is approximately
R_{c}=H^{-1}\exp(H^{2}/m^{2})
. Applying this result to baryogenesis through the Affleck-Dine mechanism, if
m\sim H
, the correlation length of the angular mode would be much smaller than our current horizon size and the average of the baryon number in our universe would be nearly zero.

Appendix A
Numerical calculation of
R_{n,l}

Although
Mathematica 12
provides a function “NDEigensystem” to solve the eigensystem, we can directly apply it to the
R_{n,l}
function. The reason is that the normalization of the
R_{n,l}
function is

\displaystyle\int R_{n,l}(r)R_{n,l}(r)rdr=1~.

(88)

To preform the numerical calculation, we can define
R_{n,l}=\chi_{n,r}r^{-1/2}
, then the normalization of
\chi
becomes

\displaystyle\int\chi_{n,l}(r)\chi_{n,l}(r)dr=1~,

(89)

then

\displaystyle R^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi^{\prime}r^{-1/2}-\frac{1}{2}\chi r^{-3/2}~,

(90)

\displaystyle R^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi^{\prime\prime}r^{-1/2}-\chi^{\prime}r^{-3/2}+\frac{3}{4}\chi r^{-5/2}~.

(91)

Notice we have

\displaystyle-\left[R^{\prime\prime}_{n,l}+\frac{R^{\prime}_{n,l}}{r}-\frac{l^{2}}{r^{2}}R_{n,l}\right]+\left[(v^{\prime})^{2}-v^{\prime\prime}-\frac{v^{\prime}}{r}\right]R_{n,l}=\frac{8\pi^{2}\Lambda_{n,l}}{H^{3}}R_{n,l}~,

(92)

and

\displaystyle-\left[\chi^{\prime\prime}_{n,l}+\frac{1}{4r^{2}}\chi_{n,l}-\frac{l^{2}}{r^{2}}\chi_{n,l}\right]+\left[(v^{\prime})^{2}-v^{\prime\prime}-\frac{v^{\prime}}{r}\right]\chi_{n,l}=\frac{8\pi^{2}\Lambda_{n,l}}{H^{3}}\chi_{n,l}~.

(93)

Then we can use
\chi_{n,l}
to perform all the numerical calculations instead of
R_{n,l}
.
