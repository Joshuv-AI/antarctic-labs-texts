Degree Heterogeneity in Higher-Order Networks: Inference in the Hypergraph 𝜷 \bm{\beta} -Model
Sagnik Nandy and Bhaswar B. Bhattacharya

Abstract
. The 𝜷 \bm{\beta} -model for random graphs is commonly used for representing pairwise interactions in a network with degree heterogeneity. Going beyond pairwise interactions, Stasi et al. 2014 introduced the hypergraph 𝜷 \bm{\beta} -model for capturing degree heterogeneity in networks with higher-order (multi-way) interactions. In this paper we initiate the rigorous study of the hypergraph 𝜷 \bm{\beta} -model with multiple layers, which allows for hyperedges of different sizes across the layers. To begin with, we derive the rates of convergence of the maximum likelihood (ML) estimate s and establish their minimax rate optimality. We also derive the limiting distribution of the ML estimate s and construct asymptotically valid confidence intervals for the model parameters. Next, we consider the goodness-of-fit problem in the hypergraph 𝜷 \bm{\beta} -model. Specifically, we establish the asymptotic normality of the likelihood ratio (LR) test under the null hypothesis, derive its detection threshold, and also its limiting power at the threshold. Interestingly, the detection threshold of the LR test turns out to be minimax optimal, that is, all tests are asymptotically powerless below this threshold. The theoretical results are further validated in numerical experiments. In addition to developing the theoretical framework for estimation and inference for hypergraph 𝜷 \bm{\beta} -models, the above results fill a number of gaps in the graph 𝜷 \bm{\beta} -model literature, such as the minimax optimality of the ML estimates and the non-null properties of the LR test, which, to the best of our knowledge, have not been studied before.

Fix

\displaystyle p_{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{r}}:=\frac{e^{\beta_{v_{1}}+\ldots+\beta_{v_{r}}}}{1+e^{\beta_{v_{1}}+\ldots+\beta_{v_{r}}}}.

It is worth noting that, since the degrees
(defined later in (
2.3

Stasi et al. 2014
proposed two algorithms for computing the maximum likelihood (ML) estimates for the hypergraph
\bm{\beta}

Summary of Results

\bm{\beta}
-model

•

Estimation

Specifically, we show that given a sample

Inference
: In Section

•

Testing
: In Section
3
we study the goodness-of-fit problem for the hypergraph
\bm{\beta}
-model, that is, given

(1.5)

\displaystyle H_{0}:\bm{\beta}_{s}=\bm{\gamma}\quad\mbox{versus}\quad H_{1}:\bm{\beta}_{s}\neq\bm{\gamma}.

We show that the likelihood ratio (LR) statistic for this problem (centered and scaled appropriately) is asymptotically normal under

\bm{\beta}
-model for the graph case itself.

In Section
4

1.2.
Related Work on the Graph
\bm{\beta}
-Model

As mentioned before,
Chatterjee et al. 2011
initiated the rigorous study of estimation in the graph
\bm{\beta}
-model. They derived, among others things, the convergence rate of the ML estimate in the
L_{\infty}
norm. Thereafter,
Rinaldo et al. 2013
derived necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of the ML estimate in terms of the polytope of the degree sequences.

Yan and Xu 2013
proved the asymptotic normality of ML estimate and later,
Yan et al. 2016b
derived the properties of a moment based estimator.

Karwa and Slavkovic 2016
studied the problem of estimation in the
\bm{\beta}
-model under privacy constraints.

In the context of hypothesis testing,
Mukherjee et al. 2018
considered the problem of sparse signal detection in the
\bm{\beta}
-model, that is, testing whether all the node parameters are zero versus whether a (possibly) sparse subset of them are non-zero. Recently,
Yan et al. 2022
derived the asymptotic properties of the LR test for the goodness-of-fit problem in the graph
\bm{\beta}
-model, under the null hypothesis.

The graph
\bm{\beta}
-model has also been generalized to incorporate additional information, such as covariates, directionality, sparsity, and weights (see
Chen et al. 2021
,
Chen and Olvera-Cravioto 2013
,
Graham 2017
,
Stein and Leng 2020
,
Yan et al. 2016a
,
Hillar and Wibisono 2013
,
Yan et al. 2019
,
Wahlström et al. 2017
,
Zhang et al. 2023
and the references therein). For other exponential random graph models with functions of the degrees as sufficient statistics, see
Mukherjee 2020
and
Xu and Mukherjee 2021
.

Maximum Likelihood Estimation in Hypergraph

-model using the ML method. In Section

2.1.
Rates of Convergence

Given a sample
H_{n}\sim\mathsf{H}_{n,[r]}(n,\bm{B})
from the
r
-

\bm{\beta}
-model

(2.1)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}L_{n}(\bm{B})=\prod_{2\leq s\leq r}\prod_{\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{s}\}\in{[n]\choose s}}\frac{e^{(\beta_{s,v_{1}}+\ldots+\beta_{s,v_{s}})\mathbbm{1}\{\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{s}\}\in E(H_{n})\}}}{1+e^{\beta_{s,v_{1}}+\ldots+\beta_{s,v_{s}}}}.}

Therefore, the negative log-likelihood is given by

\displaystyle\ell_{n}(\bm{B})

\displaystyle:=-\log L_{n}(\bm{B})

(2.2)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{s=2}^{r}\left\{\sum_{v=1}^{n}\beta_{s,v}d_{s}(v)-\sum_{\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{s}\}\in{[n]\choose s}}\log{\left(1+\exp\left(\beta_{s,v_{1}}+\ldots+\beta_{s,v_{s}}\right)\right)}\right\},

where

(2.3)

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}d_{s}(v):=\sum_{\bm{e}\in E(H_{n}):|\bm{e}|=s}\mathbbm{1}\{v\in\bm{e}\},}

is the
s
-degree of the vertex
v\in[n]
, that is, the number of hyperedges of size
s
in
H_{n}
passing through

.
The negative log-likelihood in (
2.1
) can be re-written as:

(2.4)

\ell_{n}(\bm{B})=\sum_{s=2}^{r}\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{s}),

where

(2.5)

\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}):=\sum_{\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{s}\}\in{[n]\choose s}}\log{\left(1+\exp\left(\beta_{s,v_{1}}+\ldots+\beta_{s,v_{s}}\right)\right)}-\sum_{v=1}^{n}\beta_{s,v}d_{s}(v).

Note that (
2.4
) is separable in
\bm{\beta}_{2},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{r}
, hence, the ML estimate of
\bm{B}=(\bm{\beta}_{2},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{r})
is given by
\hat{\bm{B}}=(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2},\ldots,\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{r})
, where

(2.6)

\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}:=\argmin_{\bm{\beta}}\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}).

Therefore, differentiating (

) with respect to
\bm{\beta}_{s}
and setting the gradient to zero we can conclude
that the ML estimate
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}
satisfies the following set of gradient equations: For all

and
2\leq s\leq r
,

(2.7)

d_{s}(v)=\sum_{\{v_{2},\ldots,v_{s}\}\in{{[n]\backslash\{v\}}\choose{s-1}}}\frac{e^{\hat{\beta}_{s,v}+\hat{\beta}_{s,v_{2}}+\ldots+\hat{\beta}_{s,v_{s}}}}{1+e^{\hat{\beta}_{s,v}+\hat{\beta}_{s,v_{2}}+\ldots+\hat{\beta}_{s,v_{s}}}},

where
{{[n]\backslash\{v\}}\choose{s-1}}
denotes the collection of all

[n]\backslash\{v\}
.
Stasi et al. 2014
presented two algorithms for computing the ML estimate,

an iterative proportional scaling algorithm and a fixed point algorithm, and showed that both algorithms converge if the ML estimate exists.

\|\bm{x}\|_{\infty}
and

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose

Theorem

provides the rates for the

•

For the rate in the

•

The proof of the rate in the

Here the idea is to decompose, for each

Using the part of the log-likelihood not depending on
\beta_{s,u}
we first analyze the properties of the constrained leave-one-out ML estimate, which is the maximizer of the part of the log-likelihood not depending on
\beta_{s,u}
in a neighborhood of the leave-one-out true parameter. Then from the part of the log-likelihood depending on
\beta_{s,u}
we obtain, by a Taylor expansion around the true parameter value

, the

2.8
) with an extra additive error term which depends on the constrained leave-one-out ML estimate. Using the bound on the latter obtained earlier we show this error term is negligible compared to the
L_{\infty}
rate in (
2.8
).

norm:

(2.10)

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\bm{\beta}}}\max_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\beta}_{s}\in\mathcal{B}_{M}}}\mathbb{P}\left(\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}_{s}\|_{2}\geq C\sqrt{\frac{1}{n^{s-2}}}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

Moreover, for estimation in the
L_{\infty}
norm the following holds:
there exists
n_{0}\geq 0
such that for all
n\geq n_{0}

(2.11)

\displaystyle\min_{\hat{\bm{\beta}}}\max_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\beta}_{s}\in\mathcal{B}_{M}}}\mathbb{P}\left(\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}-\bm{\beta}_{s}\|_{\infty}\geq C{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n^{s-1}}}}\right)\geq 1-\delta.

This result shows that the ML estimate is minimax rate optimal in both the
L_{2}
and the
L_{\infty}
metrics. The proof of Theorem
2.2
is given in Appendix
B
. The bounds in (
2.10
) and (
2.11
) are proved using Fano’s lemma. For the bound in (
2.10
) we construct
2^{\Theta(n)}
well-separated points in the parameter space which have ‘small’ average Kulbeck-Leibler (KL) divergence with the origin (see Appendix
B.1
) and for the bound in (
2.11
) we construct
n
well separated points which have ‘small’ KL-divergence with the origin (see Appendix
B.2
)

2.3.
Central Limit Theorems and Confidence Intervals

The results obtained in the previous section show that the vector ML estimates are consistent in the
L_{\infty}
-norm. However, for conducting asymptotically precise inference on the individual model parameters, we need to understand the limiting distribution of the ML estimates. In Theorem
2.3
below we show that the finite dimensional distributions of the ML estimates (appropriately scaled) converge to a multivariate Gaussian distribution. Using this result in Theorem
2.4
we construct joint confidence sets for any finite collection of parameters. Towards this, for
H_{n}\sim\mathsf{H}_{n,[r]}(n,\bm{B})
denote the variance of the
s
-degree of the node
v\in[n]
as:

(2.12)

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}(v)^{2}:=\Var[d_{s}(v)]=\sum_{\{v_{2},\ldots,v_{s}\}\in{{[n]\backslash\{v\}}\choose{s-1}}}\frac{e^{\beta_{s,v}+\beta_{s,v_{2}}+\ldots+\beta_{s,v_{s}}}}{(1+e^{\beta_{s,v}+\beta_{s,v_{2}}+\ldots+\beta_{s,v_{s}}})^{2}}.

Then we have the following result:

Theorem 2.3
.

Suppose
H_{n}\sim\mathsf{H}_{n,[r]}(n,\bm{B})
is a sample from the
r
-layered hypergraph
\bm{\beta}
-model as defined in (
1.2
).

Fix any collection of integers
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{r}\geq 1
and any subsets of nodes
J_{1},J_{2},\ldots,J_{r}
with cardinalities
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{r}
, respectively.

Then as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

(2.13)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}[\bm{D}_{2}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2}-\bm{\beta}_{2})]_{J_{2}}\\
[\bm{D}_{3}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{3}-\bm{\beta}_{3})]_{J_{3}}\\
\vdots\\
[\bm{D}_{r}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{r}-\bm{\beta}_{r})]_{J_{r}}\end{pmatrix}\overset{D}{\rightarrow}\mathcal{N}_{\sum_{s=2}^{r}a_{s}}(\bm{0},\bm{I}),

where
\bm{D}_{s}=\mathrm{diag}\,(\sigma_{s}(v))_{v\in[n]}
,
for
2\leq s\leq r
and for any vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
[\bm{x}]_{J_{s}}=(x_{v})_{v\in[J_{s}]}^{\top}
.

Remark 2.1
.

Observe that since
\bm{\beta}_{s}\in\mathcal{B}_{M}
for all
2\leq s\leq r
,
O(\sigma_{s}(v)^{2})\asymp n^{s-1}
for all
v\in[n]
. Therefore, the convergence rate of
[(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\beta}_{s})]_{J_{s}}
is of order
n^{(s-1)/2}
for all
2\leq s\leq r
.

The proof of Theorem
2.3
is given in Appendix
C.1
. The idea of the proof is to linearize
\hat{\beta}_{s,v}-\beta_{s,v}
in terms of the
s
-degree of the node
v\in[n]
. Since the
s
-degree of a node is the sum of independent random variables, applying Lindeberg’s CLT gives the result in (
2.13
).
In the special case of the
r
-uniform model, Theorem
2.3
can be written in the following simpler form:

Corollary 2.2
.

Suppose
H_{n}\sim\mathsf{H}_{n,r}(n,\bm{\beta})
is a sample from the
r
-uniform hypergraph
\bm{\beta}
-model as defined in (
1.3
). For all
v\in[n]
, let

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma(v)^{2}:=\sum_{\{v_{2},\ldots,v_{r}\}\in{{[n]\backslash\{v\}}\choose{r-1}}}\frac{e^{\beta_{v}+\beta_{v_{2}}+\ldots+\beta_{v_{r}}}}{1+e^{\beta_{v}+\beta_{v_{2}}+\ldots+\beta_{v_{r}}}}}.

Then for any collection of
a\geq 1
indices
J:=\{v_{1},\cdots,v_{a}\}\in{[n]\choose a}
,
as
n\rightarrow\infty
,

[\bm{D}]_{J}([\hat{\bm{\beta}}]_{J}-[\bm{\beta}]_{J})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}_{a}(\bm{0},\bm{I}),

where
\bm{D}=\mathrm{diag}\,(\sigma(v))_{v\in[n]}
,
[\bm{D}]_{J}=\mathrm{diag}\,(\sigma(v))_{v\in J}
,
[\hat{\bm{\beta}}]_{J}=(\hat{\beta}_{v})_{v\in[J]}^{\top}
, and
[\bm{\beta}]_{J}=(\beta_{v})_{v\in[J]}^{\top}
.

To use the above results to construct confidence sets for the parameters, we need to consistently estimate the elements of the matrix
\bm{D}_{s}
. Note that the natural plug-in estimate of
\sigma_{s}(v)
is

(2.14)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}_{s}(v)^{2}:=\sum_{\{v_{2},\ldots,v_{s}\}\in{{[n]\backslash\{v\}}\choose{s-1}}}\frac{e^{\hat{\beta}_{s,v}+\hat{\beta}_{s,v_{2}}+\ldots+\hat{\beta}_{s,v_{s}}}}{(1+e^{\hat{\beta}_{s,v}+\hat{\beta}_{s,v_{2}}+\ldots+\hat{\beta}_{s,v_{s}}})^{2}}.

This estimate turns out to be consistent for
\sigma_{s}(v)
, leading to the following result (see Appendix
C.2
for the proof):

Theorem 2.4
.

Suppose
H_{n}\sim\mathsf{H}_{n,[r]}(n,\bm{B})
is a sample from the
r
-layered hypergraph
\bm{\beta}
-model as defined in (
1.2
).
Fix any collection of integers
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{r}\geq 1
and any subsets of nodes
J_{1},J_{2},\ldots,J_{r}
with cardinalities
a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{r}
, respectively.

Then, for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
,

(2.15)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\{\sum_{s=2}^{r}([(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\beta}_{s})]_{J_{s}})^{\top}[\hat{\bm{D}}_{s}^{2}]_{J_{s}}([(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\beta}_{s})]_{J_{s}})\leq\chi^{2}_{\sum_{s=2}^{r}a_{s},1-\alpha}\right\}\right)=1-\alpha,

where
\hat{\bm{D}}_{s}^{2}=\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\sigma}_{s}(v)^{2})_{v\in[n]}
,
[\hat{\bm{D}}_{s}^{2}]_{J_{s}}=\mathrm{diag}(\hat{\sigma}_{s}(v)^{2})_{v\in J_{s}}
, for
2\leq s\leq r
, and for
a\geq 1
,
\chi^{2}_{a,1-\alpha}
is the
(1-\alpha)
-th quantile of the chi-squared distribution with
a
degrees of freedom.

3.
Goodness-of-Fit: Asymptotics of the Likelihood Ratio Test and Minimax Detection Rates

In this section we consider the problem of testing for goodness-of-fit in the hypergraph
\bm{\beta}
-model. In particular, given
\bm{\gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a sample
H_{n}\sim\mathsf{H}_{n,[r]}(n,\bm{B})
, with
\bm{B}=(\bm{\beta}_{2},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{r})
, we consider the following hypothesis testing problem: For
2\leq s\leq r
,

(3.1)

\displaystyle H_{0}:\bm{\beta}_{s}=\bm{\gamma}\quad\mbox{versus}\quad H_{1}:\bm{\beta}_{s}\neq\bm{\gamma}.

This section is organized as follows: In Section
3.1
we derive the asymptotic distribution and detection rates of the likelihood ratio (LR) test for the problem (
3.1
). In Section
3.2
we show that the detection rate of the LR test is minimax optimal for testing in
L_{2}
norm. Rates for testing in
L_{\infty}
norm are derived in Section
3.3
.

3.1.
Asymptotics of the Likelihood Ratio Test

Consider the LR statistic for the testing problem (
3.1
):

(3.2)

\displaystyle\log\Lambda_{n,s}=\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\gamma})-\ell_{n,s}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}),

where
\ell_{n,s}
is the
negative
log-likelihood function (
2.5
) and
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}
is the ML estimate (
2.6
). The following theorem proves the limiting distribution of the LR statistic (
3.2
) under
H_{0}
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose
\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{B}_{M}
.
Then under
H_{0}
,

(3.3)

\lambda_{n,s}:=\frac{2\log\Lambda_{n,s}-n}{\sqrt{2n}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(0,1),

for
\log\Lambda_{n,s}
as defined in (
3.2
).

The proof of Theorem
3.1
is given in Appendix
D.1
. To prove the result we first
expand
\log\Lambda_{n,s}
around the null parameter
\bm{\gamma}
and derive an asymptotic expansion of
\lambda_{n,s}
in terms of the sum of squares of the
s
-degree sequence
(d_{s}(1),d_{s}(2),\ldots,d_{s}(n))^{\top}
(see (
D.1
)). Since the degrees are asymptotically independent (recall Theorem
2.3
), we can show that the sum of squares of the degrees (appropriately centered and scaled) is asymptotically normal (see Proposition
D.1
), establishing the result in (
3.3
).

Theorem
3.1
shows that the LR test

(3.4)

\displaystyle\phi_{n,s}:=\mathbbm{1}\left\{|\lambda_{n,s}|>z_{\alpha/2}\right\},

where
z_{\alpha/2}
is the
(1-\alpha/2)
-th quantile of the standard normal distribution, has asymptotic level
\alpha
. To study the power of this test consider the following testing problem:

(3.5)

\displaystyle H_{0}:\bm{\beta}_{s}=\bm{\gamma}\quad\mbox{versus}\quad H_{1}:\bm{\beta}_{s}=\bm{\gamma}^{\prime},

where
\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}\neq\bm{\gamma}
is such that
\|\bm{\gamma}-\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}\|_{2}=O(1)
. Recall that
\bm{d}_{s}=(d_{s}(1),d_{s}(2),\ldots,d_{s}(n))^{\top}
is the vector of
s
-degrees. Also,
\Cov_{\bm{\gamma}}[\bm{d}_{s}]
will denote the covariance matrix of the vector of
s
-degrees (see (
C.2
)).

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose (
3.3
) holds and
\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}

be
as in (
3.5
),
then
the asymptotic power of the test
\phi_{n,s}
defined in (
3.4
) satisfies:

(3.6)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}}[\phi_{n,s}]=\begin{cases}\alpha&\mbox{if\; $\|\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}-\bm{\gamma}\|_{2}\ll n^{-\frac{2s-3}{4}}$,}\\
1&\mbox{if \;$\|\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}-\bm{\gamma}\|_{2}\gg n^{-\frac{2s-3}{4}}$.}\end{cases}

Moreover, if
n^{\frac{2s-3}{4}}\|\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}-\bm{\gamma}\|_{2}\rightarrow\tau\in(0,\infty)
, then there exists
\eta\in(0,\infty)
depending on
\tau
such that

\eta=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{(\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}-\bm{\gamma})^{\top}\Cov_{\bm{\gamma}}[\bm{d}_{s}](\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}-\bm{\gamma})}{\sqrt{n}},

where the limit always exists along a subsequence
, and

(3.7)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}}[\phi_{n,s}]=\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{N}(-\tfrac{\eta}{\sqrt{2}},1)\right|>z_{\alpha/2}\right).

The proof of Theorem
3.2
is given in Appendix
D.2
. It entails analyzing the asymptotic distribution of the scaled LR statistic
\lambda_{n,s}
under
H_{1}
as in (
3.5
). Specifically, we show that when
\|\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}-\bm{\gamma}\|_{2}\ll n^{-\frac{2s-3}{4}}
, then
\lambda_{n,s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, hence the LR test (
3.3
) is asymptotically powerless in detecting
H_{1}
. On the other hand, if
\|\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}-\bm{\gamma}\|_{2}\gg n^{-\frac{2s-3}{4}}
, then the
\lambda_{n,s}
diverges to infinity, hence the LR test is asymptotically powerful. In other words,
n^{-\frac{2s-3}{4}}
is the detection threshold in the
L_{2}
norm of the LR test.
We also derive the limiting power function of the LR test at the threshold
n^{\frac{2s-3}{4}}\|\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}-\bm{\gamma}\|_{2}\rightarrow\tau\in(0,\infty)
. In this case,
\lambda_{n,s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(-\eta/\sqrt{2},1)
, where ‘effective signal strength’
\eta
is the limit of the scaled Mahalanobis distance between
\bm{\gamma}
and
\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}
, where the dispersion matrix is the covariance matrix of the degrees.
Here, the Mahalanobis distance between two vectors
\bm{\gamma}
and
\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}
with the dispersion matrix
\bm{\Sigma}
refers to the quantity
(\bm{\gamma}-\bm{\gamma}^{\prime})^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}(\bm{\gamma}-\bm{\gamma}^{\prime})
.

In the next section we will show that this detection rate is, in fact, minimax optimal.

3.2.
Minimax Detection Rate in the
L_{2}
Norm

In this section we will show that the detection threshold of the LR test obtained in Theorem
3.2
is information-theoretically tight.
To formalize this consider the testing problem: For
\varepsilon>0
and
\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{B}_{M}
,

(3.8)

\displaystyle H_{0}:\bm{\beta}_{s}=\bm{\gamma}\quad\mbox{versus}\quad H_{1}:\|\bm{\beta}_{s}-\bm{\gamma}\|_{2}\geq\varepsilon.

The worst-case risk of a test function
\psi_{n}
for the testing problem (
3.8
) is defined as:

(3.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\psi_{n},\bm{\gamma})=\mathbb{P}_{H_{0}}(\psi_{n}=1)+\sup_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{M}}:\|\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}-\bm{\gamma}\|_{2}\geq\varepsilon}\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}}(\psi_{n}=0),

which is the sum of the Type I error and the maximum possible Type II error of the test function
\psi_{n}
.
Given
H_{n}\sim\mathsf{H}_{n,s}(n,\bm{\beta}_{s})
, for some
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\beta}_{s}\in\mathcal{B}_{M}}
, and
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{n}
(depending on
n
), a sequence of test functions
\psi_{n}
is said to be
asymptotically powerful
for (
3.9
), if for all
\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{B}_{M}

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{R}(\psi_{n},\bm{\gamma})=0
. On the other hand, a sequence of test functions
\psi_{n}
is said to be
asymptotically powerless
for (
3.9
), if there exists
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{B}_{M}}
such that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{R}(\psi_{n},\bm{\gamma})=1
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Given
H_{n}\sim\mathsf{H}_{n,s}(n,\bm{\beta}_{s})
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{B}_{M}}
, consider the testing problem (
3.8
). Then the following hold:

(a)

The LR test (
3.4
) is asymptotically powerful for (
3.8
), when
\varepsilon\gg n^{-\frac{2s-3}{4}}
.

(b)

On the other hand, all tests are asymptotically powerless for (
3.8
), when
\varepsilon\ll n^{-\frac{2s-3}{4}}
.

The result in Theorem
3.3
(a) is a direct consequence of Theorem
3.2
. The proof of Theorem
3.3
(b) is given in Appendix
E.1
. For this we chose
\bm{\gamma}=\bm{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and randomly perturb (that is, randomly add or subtract
\varepsilon/\sqrt{n}
) the coordinates of
\bm{\gamma}
to construct
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\beta}_{s}\in\mathcal{B}_{M}}
satisfying
\|\bm{\beta}_{s}-\bm{\gamma}\|_{2}\geq\varepsilon
. Then a second-moment calculation of the likelihood ratio shows that detecting these two models is impossible for
\varepsilon\ll n^{-\frac{2s-3}{4}}
. These results combined show that
n^{-\frac{2s-3}{4}}
is the minimax detection rate for the testing problem (
3.8
) and the LR test attain the minimax rate.

Remark 3.1
.

(Comparison between testing and estimation rates.) Recall from (
2.8
) and (
2.10
) that the minimax rate of estimating
\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}
in the
L_{2}
norm is
n^{-\frac{s-2}{2}}
. On the other hand, Theorem
3.3
shows that the minimax rate of testing in the
L_{2}
norm is
n^{-\frac{2s-3}{4}}\ll n^{-\frac{s-2}{2}}
. For example, in the graph case (where
s=2
), the estimation rate is
\Theta(1)
whereas the rate of testing is
n^{-\frac{1}{4}}
. This is an instance of the well-known phenomenon that high-dimensional estimation is, in general, harder that testing in the squared-error loss.

3.3.
Testing in the
L_{\infty}
Norm

In this section we consider the goodness-of-fit problem when
the
separation is measured in the
L_{\infty}
norm. This complements our results on estimation in
the

L_{\infty}
norm in Theorem
2.1
. Towards this, as in (
3.8
), consider the testing problem: For
\varepsilon>0
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\gamma}\in\mathcal{B}_{M}}
,

(3.10)

\displaystyle H_{0}:\bm{\beta}_{s}=\bm{\gamma}\quad\mbox{versus}\quad H_{1}:\|\bm{\beta}_{s}-\bm{\gamma}\|_{\infty}\geq\varepsilon.

In this case the minimax risk of a test function is defined as in (
3.9
) with the
L_{2}
norm
\|\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}-\bm{\gamma}\|_{2}
replaced by the
L_{\infty}
norm
\|\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}-\bm{\gamma}\|_{\infty}
. Then consider the test:

(3.11)

\displaystyle\phi_{n,s}^{\max}:=\mathbbm{1}\left\{\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\gamma}\|_{\infty}\geq\,2C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n^{s-1}}}\right\},

where
C:=C(s,M)>0
is chosen according to (
2.8
) such that

\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\kappa}}\left(\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\kappa}\|_{\infty}\leq\,C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n^{s-1}}}\right)\rightarrow 1,

for all
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\kappa}\in\mathcal{B}_{M}}
. This implies,
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\gamma}}[\phi_{n,s}^{\max}]\rightarrow 0
. Also, for
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{M}}
such that
\|\bm{\gamma}-\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\geq\varepsilon
,

(3.12)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}}[\phi_{n,s}^{\max}]=\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}}\left(\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\gamma}\|_{\infty}\geq\,2C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n^{s-1}}}\right)\geq\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}}\left(\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\,C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n^{s-1}}}\right)\rightarrow 1,

whenever
\varepsilon\gg\sqrt{\log n/n^{s-1}}
. This is because
\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq\,C\sqrt{\log n/n^{s-1}}
implies,

\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\gamma}\|_{\infty}\geq\|\bm{\gamma}-\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}-\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\geq\varepsilon-C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n^{s-1}}}\geq 2C\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n^{s-1}}},

whenever
\varepsilon\gg\sqrt{\log n/n^{s-1}}
. This implies that the test

\phi_{n,s}^{\max}
in (
3.11
) is asymptotically powerful for (
3.10
) whenever
\varepsilon\gg\sqrt{\log n/n^{s-1}}
. The following result shows that this rate is optimal (up to a factor of
\sqrt{\log n}
) for testing in the
L_{\infty}
norm.

Theorem 3.4
.

Given
H_{n}\sim\mathsf{H}_{n,s}(n,\bm{\beta}_{s})
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bm{\gamma},\bm{\beta}_{s}\in\mathcal{B}_{M}}
, consider the testing problem (
3.10
). Then the following hold:

(a)

The test
\phi_{n,s}^{\max}
in (
3.12
) is asymptotically powerful for (
3.10
), when
\varepsilon\gg\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n^{s-1}}}
.

(b)

On the other hand, all tests are asymptotically powerless for (
3.10
), when
\varepsilon\ll\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{n^{s-1}}}
.

The proof of Theorem
3.4
(b) is given in Appendix
E.2
. Note that in this case minimax rates of estimation and testing are the same, since the effect of high-dimensional aggregation does not arise when separation is measured in the
L_{\infty}
norm.

2.1
involves showing the following: (1) a concentration bound on the gradient of negative log-likelihood
\ell_{n,s}
(recall (
2.5
)) at the true parameter value
\bm{B}=(\bm{\beta}_{2},\ldots,\bm{\beta}_{r})
, and (2) the strong convexity of
\ell_{n,s}
in a neighborhood of the true parameter. We begin with the concentration of the gradient
\nabla{\ell}_{n,s}
in both the
L_{2}
and the
L_{\infty}
norms:

Lemma A.1
.

Suppose the assumptions of Theorem
2.1
hold. Then for each
2\leq s\leq r
, there exists a constant
C>0
(depending on
r
and
M
) such that the following hold:

(A.1)

\displaystyle\|\nabla{\ell}_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{s})\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\,n^{s}\quad\text{ and }\quad\|\nabla{\ell}_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{s})\|^{2}_{\infty}\leq C\,n^{s-1}\log n,

with probability
1-O\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\right)
.

The next step is to establish the strong convexity of
\ell_{n,s}
. Towards this we need to show that the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}\ell_{n,s})
of the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}\ell_{n,s}
(appropriately scaled) is bounded away from zero in a neighborhood of the true value
\bm{\beta}_{s}
. This is the content of the following lemma, which also establishes a matching upper bound on the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}\ell_{n,s})
of the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}\ell_{n,s}
.

Lemma A.2
.

Suppose the assumptions of Theorem
2.1
hold. Fix
2\leq s\leq r
and a constant
K>0
.
Then there exists constants
C_{1}^{\prime},C_{2}^{\prime}>0
(depending on
r
and
M
) such that the following hold:

(A.2)

\displaystyle{C_{1}^{\prime}e^{-s\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}_{s}\|_{\infty}}n^{s-1}\leq\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}{\ell}_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}))\leq\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}{\ell}_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}))\leq{C}_{2}^{\prime}n^{s-1}.}

As a consequence, there exists a constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
(depending on
r
,
K
, and
M
) such that the following hold:

(A.3)

\displaystyle C_{1}n^{s-1}\leq\inf_{\bm{\beta}:\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}_{s}\|_{2}\leq K}\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}{\ell}_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}))\leq\sup_{\bm{\beta}:\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}_{s}\|_{2}\leq K}\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}{\ell}_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}))\leq{C}_{2}n^{s-1}.

The proofs of Lemma
A.1
and Lemma
A.2
are given in Appendix
A.1.2
and Appendix
A.1.3
, respectively. We first apply these results to prove the rate of convergence in the
L_{2}
norm in Theorem
2.1
.

A.1.1.
Deriving the
L_{2}
Norm Bound in (
2.8
)

To begin with suppose the ML equations (
2.7
) have a solution
\hat{\bm{B}}=(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2},\ldots,\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{r})
. This implies,
\nabla\ell_{n,s}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s})=0
, for
2\leq s\leq r
,
where
\ell_{n,s}
is as defined in (
2.5
).
For
2\leq s\leq r
and
0\leq t\leq 1
, define

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bar{\bm{\beta}}}_{s}(t):=t\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}+(1-t)\bm{\beta}_{s},

and
g_{s}(t):=(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\beta}_{s})^{\top}\nabla\ell_{n,s}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bar{\bm{\beta}}}_{s}(t))
. Note that
\nabla\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{s}(1))=\nabla\ell_{n,s}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s})=0
. Hence, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

(A.4)

\displaystyle|g_{s}(1)-g_{s}(0)|=|(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\beta}_{s})^{\top}\nabla\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{s})|\leq\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\beta}_{s}\|_{2}\cdot\|\nabla\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{s})\|_{2}.

Also,

(A.5)

\displaystyle g_{s}^{\prime}(t)=(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\beta}_{s})^{\top}\nabla^{2}\ell_{n,s}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bar{\bm{\beta}}}_{s}(t))(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\beta}_{s})\geq\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}\ell_{n,s}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bar{\bm{\beta}}}_{s}(t)))\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\beta}_{s}\|_{2}^{2}.

We now consider two cases: To begin with assume
s\geq 3
. By Lemma
A.2
, given a constant
K>0
there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
(depending on
r,K,M
) such that

(A.6)

\displaystyle\inf_{\bm{\beta}:\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}_{s}\|_{2}\leq K}\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}))\geq C_{1}n^{s-1}.

Note that
\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\bar{\bm{\beta}}}_{s}(t)-\bm{\beta}_{s}\|_{2}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}t}\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\beta}_{s}\|_{2}
. Then

\displaystyle|g_{s}(1)-g_{s}(0)|\geq g_{s}(1)-g_{s}(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}g_{s}^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{\min\{1,\frac{K}{\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\beta}_{s}\|_{2}}\}}g_{s}^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\geq C_{1}n^{s-1}\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\beta}_{s}\|_{2}^{2}\min\left\{1,\frac{K}{\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\beta}_{s}\|_{2}}\right\},

where the last step follows from (
A.5
) and (
A.6
). Therefore, by (
A.4
) and Lemma
A.1
, with probability
1-O(\frac{1}{n^{2}})
,

(A.7)

\displaystyle\min\{\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}-\bm{\beta}_{s}\|_{2},K\}\lesssim_{r,K,M}\frac{1}{n^{s-1}}\cdot\|\nabla\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{s})\|_{2}\lesssim_{r,K,M}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n^{s-2}}}.

Since
K>0
is fixed and the
RHS of (
A.7
)
converges to zero for
s\geq 3
, the
L_{2}
norm bound in (
2.8
) follows, under the assumption that ML equations (
2.7
) have a solution.

Next, suppose
s=2
. Since
\|\bar{\bm{\beta}}_{2}(t)-\bm{\beta}_{2}\|_{\infty}=t\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2}-\bm{\beta}_{2}\|_{\infty}
. Since
t\in[0,1]
, by Lemma
A.2
,

(A.8)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}\ell_{n,2}(\bar{\bm{\beta}}_{2}(t)))\geq C_{1}^{\prime}e^{-2t\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2}-\bm{\beta}_{2}\|_{\infty}}n\geq C_{1}^{\prime}e^{-2\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2}-\bm{\beta}_{2}\|_{\infty}}n,

for some constant
C_{1}^{\prime}>0
depending on
M
.
Then

\displaystyle|g_{2}(1)-g_{2}(0)|\geq g_{2}(1)-g_{2}(0)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}g_{2}^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\geq C_{1}^{\prime}n\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2}-\bm{\beta}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}e^{-2\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2}-\bm{\beta}_{2}\|_{\infty}}.

Therefore, by (
A.4
) and Lemma
A.1
, with probability
1-o(1)
,

(A.9)

\displaystyle\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2}-\bm{\beta}_{2}\|_{2}e^{-2\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2}-\bm{\beta}_{2}\|_{\infty}}\leq\frac{1}{C_{1}^{\prime}n}\cdot\|\nabla\ell_{n,2}(\bm{\beta}_{2})\|_{2}\leq C^{\prime},

for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
depending on
M
. Hence, if there exists a bounded solution to (
2.7
), the
L_{2}
norm rate will follow for
s=2
.

To complete the proof we need to show that bounded solution to equation (
2.7
) exists.
To this end, for
2\leq s\leq r
, denote by
\mathcal{D}_{s}
, the set of all possible degree sequences in an
s
-uniform hypergraph on
n
vertices. Moreover, let
\mathcal{R}_{s}
be the set of all expected degree sequences in a hypergraph on
n
vertices sampled from the
s
-uniform model (
1.3
).
The following result shows that any convex combination of
s
-degree
sequences in
\mathcal{D}_{s}
can be reached by the limit of expected degree sequences of the
s
-uniform hypergraph
\bm{\beta}
-model. This was proved in the graph case (
s=2
) by Chatterjee et al.
[
10
, Theorem 1.4]
. Here, we show that the same holds for all
2\leq s\leq r
.

Proposition A.1
.

Fix
2\leq s\leq r
and let
\mathcal{D}_{s}
and
\mathcal{R}_{s}
be as defined above. Then
\mathrm{conv}\,(\mathcal{D}_{s})=\bar{\mathcal{R}}_{s}
,
where
\mathrm{conv}\,(\mathcal{D}_{s})
denotes the convex hull of
\mathcal{D}_{s}
and
\widebar{\mathcal{R}}_{s}
is the closure of
\mathcal{R}_{s}
.

The proof of the above result is given in Appendix
F
. Using this proposition we now show the existence of bounded solutions of the ML equations (
2.7
). Note that by Proposition
A.1
, given
H_{n}\sim\mathsf{H}_{n,[r]}(n,\bm{B})
the
s
-degree sequence
(d_{s}(1),\ldots,d_{s}(n))\in\mathcal{D}_{s}\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{R}}_{s}
. This implies, there exists a sequence
\{\bm{x}_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}\in\mathcal{R}_{s}
satisfying

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\bm{x}_{t}=(d_{s}(1),\ldots,d_{s}(n)).

Since
\bm{x}_{t}\in\mathcal{R}_{s}
, there exists
\{\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2}^{(t)},\ldots,\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{r}^{(t)}\}
such that

(A.10)

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(\bm{x}_{t})_{v}=\sum_{\{v_{2},\ldots,v_{s}\}\in{{[n]\backslash\{v\}}\choose{s-1}}}\frac{e^{\hat{\beta}^{(t)}_{s,v}+\hat{\beta}^{(t)}_{s,v_{2}}+\ldots+\hat{\beta}^{(t)}_{s,v_{s}}}}{1+e^{\hat{\beta}^{(t)}_{s,v}+\hat{\beta}^{(t)}_{s,v_{2}}+\ldots+\hat{\beta}^{(t)}_{s,v_{s}}}}},

for
1\leq v\leq n
and

2\leq s\leq r
. In other words, for each
t\geq 0
,
\{\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2}^{(t)},\ldots,\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{r}^{(t)}\}
is a solution of the ML equations (
2.7
) with
(d_{s}(1),d_{s}(2),\ldots,d_{s}(n))
replaced by
\bm{x}_{t}
.
By the previous argument, there exists
C>0
(not depending on
t
) such that with probability
1-o(1)
,

\max_{2\leq s\leq r}\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{(t)}_{s}\|_{\infty}\leq C,

for all
t\geq 0
. Therefore, the sequence
\{(\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{(t)}_{2},\ldots,\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{(t)}_{r})\}_{\geq 0}
has a limit point. This limit point is a solution to (
2.7
) (by taking limit as
t\rightarrow\infty
in (
A.10
)) and is bounded. Finally, since
\ell_{n,s}
is strongly convex for
\bm{\beta}\in\mathcal{B}_{M}
(see (
A.2
)), if the gradient equations have a bounded solution, it is the unique minimizer. Therefore, there exists a unique bounded solution to (
2.7
) which is the minimizer of
\ell_{n,s}
.

A.1.2.
Proof of Lemma
A.1

Recalling (
2.7
) note that, for
v\in[n]
,
v
-th coordinate of the gradient of
\nabla\ell_{n,s}
is given by:

(A.11)

\displaystyle\nabla\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{s})_{v}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[d_{s}(v)]-d_{s}(v)

where

(A.12)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[d_{s}(v)]=\sum_{\{v_{2},\ldots,v_{s}\}\in{{[n]\backslash\{v\}}\choose{s-1}}}\frac{e^{\beta_{s,v}+\beta_{s,v_{2}}+\ldots+\beta_{s,v_{s}}}}{1+e^{\beta_{s,v}+\beta_{s,v_{2}}+\ldots+\beta_{s,v_{s}}}}.

Since
d_{s}(v)
is the sum of
O(n^{s-1})
independent random variables
bounded by
1
, by Hoeffding’s inequality and the union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\nabla\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{s})\|_{\infty}^{2}\geq 4\,C_{s,M}n^{s-1}\log n\right)\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}},

for some constant
C_{s,M}>0
(depending on
s
and
M
). This establishes the second bound in (
A.1
).

Next, we prove the first bound in (
A.1
). Denote by
\mathbb{B}^{n}:=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|\bm{x}\|_{2}\leq 1\}
the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By
[
48
, Lemma 5.2]
, we can construct an
\frac{1}{2}
-net
\mathcal{V}
of
\mathbb{B}^{n}
satisfying
\log|\mathcal{V}|\leq C_{1}n
for some constant
C_{1}>0
.
By a
\frac{1}{2}
-net of
\mathbb{B}^{n}
, we refer to a set
\mathcal{V}
, such that for all
u\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
there exists
v\in\mathcal{V}
satisfying
\|u-v\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
Now, for any unit vector
\bm{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{n})^{\top}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and the corresponding point
\bm{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n})^{\top}\in\mathcal{V}
, recalling (
A.11
) gives,

(A.13)

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{n}a_{v}\nabla\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{s})_{v}=\sum_{v=1}^{n}a_{v}\left(\mathbb{E}[d_{s}(v)]-d_{s}(v)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v=1}^{n}b_{v}\left(\mathbb{E}[d_{s}(v)]-d_{s}(v)\right)+\Delta,

where

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle:=\sum_{v=1}^{n}(a_{v}-b_{v})\left(\mathbb{E}[d_{s}(v)]-d_{s}(v)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{v=1}^{n}(a_{v}-b_{v})^{2}\sum_{v=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}[d_{s}(v)]-d_{s}(v)\right)^{2}}

(A.14)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\sum_{v=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}[d_{s}(v)]-d_{s}(v)\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{s})\|_{2},

by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and the fact that
\|\bm{a}-\bm{b}\|_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Using the above in (
A.13
) gives,

(A.15)

\displaystyle\sum_{v=1}^{n}a_{v}\nabla\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{s})_{v}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v=1}^{n}b_{v}\left(\mathbb{E}[d_{s}(v)]-d_{s}(v)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{s})\|_{2}.

Maximizing over
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{B}^{n}
and
\bm{b}\in\mathcal{V}
on both sides of (
A.15
) and rearranging the terms gives,

(A.16)

\displaystyle\|\nabla\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{s})\|_{2}\leq 2\max_{\bm{b}\in\mathcal{V}}\sum_{v=1}^{n}b_{v}\left(\mathbb{E}[d_{s}(v)]-d_{s}(v)\right).

For
\bm{e}=(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{s})\in{[n]\choose s}
denote
\bm{\beta}_{s,\bm{e}}=(\beta_{s,u_{1}},\beta_{s,u_{2}},\ldots,\beta_{s,u_{s}})^{\top}
. Hence, by (
A.16
), Hoeffding’s inequality, and union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\|\nabla\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{s})\|_{2}^{2}>4C^{2}n^{s}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\bm{b}\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{v=1}^{n}b_{v}\left(\mathbb{E}[d_{s}(v)]-d_{s}(v)\right)>2Cn^{\frac{s}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{b}\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{v=1}^{n}\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}:v\in\bm{e}}b_{v}\left\{\frac{e^{\bm{\beta}_{s,\bm{e}}^{\top}\bm{1}}}{1+e^{\bm{\beta}_{s,\bm{e}}^{\top}\bm{1}}}-\mathbbm{1}\{\bm{e}\in E(H_{n})\}\right\}>2Cn^{\frac{s}{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\bm{b}\in\mathcal{V}}e^{-\frac{2C^{2}n}{\sum_{v=1}^{n}b_{v}^{2}}}\leq 2^{C_{1}n}e^{-2C^{2}n}\rightarrow 0,

by choosing
C>C_{1}
to be large enough. This proves the first inequality in (
A.1
).
\Box

A.1.3.
Proof of Lemma
A.2

For
\bm{e}=(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{s})\in{[n]\choose s}
and
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\ldots,\beta_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denote
\bm{\beta}_{\bm{e}}=(\beta_{u_{1}},\beta_{u_{2}},\ldots,\beta_{u_{s}})^{\top}
. Recalling (
2.7
) note that,
the Hessian matrix
\nabla^{2}\ell_{n,s}
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta})=

\displaystyle\sum_{u,v\in[n]}\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}\frac{e^{\bm{\beta}^{\top}_{\bm{e}}\bm{1}}}{(1+e^{\bm{\beta}^{\top}_{\bm{e}}\bm{1}})^{2}}\bm{\eta}_{u}\bm{\eta}_{v}^{\top}\mathbbm{1}\{u,v\in\bm{e}\},

where
\bm{\eta}_{u}
is the
u
-th basis vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for
1\leq u\leq n
.

Note that for
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
since
\bm{\beta}_{s}\in\mathcal{B}_{M}
, we have
,

{|\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{\bm{e}}|\leq s\|\bm{\beta}\|_{\infty}\leq s\|\bm{\beta}_{s}\|_{\infty}+s\|\bm{\beta}_{s}-\bm{\beta}\|_{\infty}.}

Hence,

(A.17)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}e^{-s(M+\|\bm{\beta}_{s}-\bm{\beta}\|_{\infty})}\leq\frac{e^{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{\bm{e}}}}{(1+e^{\bm{1}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{\bm{e}}})^{2}}\leq 1.

For
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, consider

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{\top}\nabla^{2}\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta})\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v\in[n]}\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}\frac{e^{\bm{\beta}^{\top}_{\bm{e}}\bm{1}}}{(1+e^{\bm{\beta}^{\top}_{\bm{e}}\bm{1}})^{2}}x_{u}x_{v}\mathbbm{1}\{u,v\in\bm{e}\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}\frac{e^{\bm{\beta}^{\top}_{\bm{e}}\bm{1}}}{(1+e^{\bm{\beta}^{\top}_{\bm{e}}\bm{1}})^{2}}\left(\sum_{u,v\in[n]}x_{u}x_{v}\mathbbm{1}\{u,v\in\bm{e}\}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}\frac{e^{\bm{\beta}^{\top}_{\bm{e}}\bm{1}}}{(1+e^{\bm{\beta}^{\top}_{\bm{e}}\bm{1}})^{2}}\left(\sum_{u\in[n]}x_{u}\mathbbm{1}\{u\in\bm{e}\}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}e^{-s(M+\|\bm{\beta}_{s}-\bm{\beta}\|_{\infty})}\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}\left(\sum_{u\in[n]}x_{u}\mathbbm{1}\{u\in\bm{e}\}\right)^{2},

where the last step uses (
A.17
). Observe that for any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}\left(\sum_{u\in[n]}x_{u}\mathbbm{1}\{u\in\bm{e}\}\right)^{2}=\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{L}\bm{x},

where

\bm{L}:=\sum_{u,v\in[n]}\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}\bm{\eta}_{u}\bm{\eta}_{v}^{\top}\mathbbm{1}\{u,v\in\bm{e}\}=\left({n-1\choose s-1}-{n-2\choose s-2}\right)\bm{I}_{n}+{n-2\choose s-2}\bm{1}\bm{1}^{\top},

where
\bm{I}_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix and
\bm{1}=(1,1,\ldots,1)^{\top}
. Similarly, we can show from (
A.17
) that for any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\bm{x}^{\top}\nabla^{2}\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta})\bm{x}\leq\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{L}\bm{x}.

Thus, for
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

(A.18)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}e^{-s(M+\|\bm{\beta}_{s}-\bm{\beta}\|_{\infty})}\lambda_{\min}\left(\bm{L}\right)\leq\lambda_{\min}(\nabla^{2}\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}))\leq\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}))\leq\lambda_{\max}\left(\bm{L}\right).

Note that
\bm{L}
is a circulant matrix with 2 non-zero eigenvalues:

{n-1\choose s-1}\quad\text{ and }\quad{n-1\choose s-1}-{n-2\choose s-2}.

Further
, there exists constants
C_{1}^{\prime\prime},C_{2}^{\prime\prime}>0
(depending on
r
), such that

{n-1\choose s-1}\leq C_{1}^{\prime\prime}n^{s-1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad{n-1\choose s-1}-{n-2\choose s-2}\geq C_{2}^{\prime\prime}\,n^{s-1}.

This implies, from (
A.18
), that there exists constants
C_{1}^{\prime},C_{2}^{\prime}>0
(depending on
r
and
M
) such that (
A.2
) hold.
The result in (
A.3
) from hold from (
A.2
) by noting that
\|\bm{\beta}_{s}-\bm{\beta}\|_{\infty}\leq\|\bm{\beta}_{s}-\bm{\beta}\|_{2}
.

A.2.
Convergence Rate in the
L_{\infty}
Norm

Suppose
H_{n}\sim\mathsf{H}_{n,[r]}(n,\bm{B})

be
as in the statement of Theorem
2.1
. From the arguments in Appendix
A.1
we know that, with probability
1-o(1)
, the ML equations (
2.7
) have a bounded solution
\hat{\bm{B}}=(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{2},\ldots,\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{r})
, that is,
\nabla\ell_{n,s}(\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s})=0
, for
2\leq s\leq r
, and
\max_{2\leq s\leq r}\|\hat{\bm{\beta}}_{s}\|_{\infty}=O(1)
. To establish the rate in
L_{\infty}
norm we decompose the likelihood for the
s
-th layer as follows.

\displaystyle\ell_{n,s}(\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{s}\}\in{[n]\choose s}}\log\left(1+e^{\beta_{v_{1}}+\ldots+\beta_{v_{s}}}\right)-\sum_{v=1}^{n}\beta_{v}d_{s}(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}\left\{\log\left(1+e^{\bm{\beta}^{\top}_{e}\bm{1}}\right)-\mathbbm{1}\{\bm{e}\in E(H_{n})\}\bm{\beta}^{\top}_{e}\bm{1}\right\}

(A.19)

\displaystyle=\ell^{+}_{n,s}(\beta_{u}|\bm{\beta}_{\bar{u}})+\ell^{-}_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{\bar{u}}),

where
\bm{\beta}_{\bar{u}}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{u-1},\beta_{u+1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
,

\displaystyle\ell^{+}_{n,s}(\beta_{u}|\bm{\beta}_{\bar{u}})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}:u\in\bm{e}}\left\{\log\left(1+e^{\bm{\beta}^{\top}_{e}\bm{1}}\right)-\mathbbm{1}\{\bm{e}\in E(H_{n})\}\bm{\beta}^{\top}_{e}\bm{1}\right\}

(A.20)

\displaystyle\ell^{-}_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{\bar{u}})

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}:u\notin\bm{e}}\left\{\log\left(1+e^{\bm{\beta}^{\top}_{e}\bm{1}}\right)-\mathbbm{1}\{\bm{e}\in E(H_{n})\}\bm{\beta}^{\top}_{e}\bm{1}\right\}.

Fix a constant
K>0
and define

(A.21)

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{\beta}}^{\circ}_{s,\bar{u}}=\argmin_{\bm{\beta}_{\bar{u}}:\|\bm{\beta}_{\bar{u}}-\bm{\beta}_{s,\bar{u}}\|_{2}\leq K}\ell^{-}_{n,s}(\bm{\beta}_{\bar{u}}),

where
\bm{\beta}_{s,\bar{u}}=(\beta_{s,1},\ldots,\beta_{s,u-1},\beta_{s,u+1},\ldots,\beta_{s,n})
. This is the leave-one-out ML estimate on the constrained set

, where
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n}
are i.i.d Rademacher random variables, taking values
\{\pm 1\}
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
. Clearly,
\|\bm{\gamma}-\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}\|_{2}=\varepsilon
. Then, for
H\in\mathcal{H}_{n,s}
, the
\pi_{n}
integrated likelihood ratio is given by

L_{\pi_{n}}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\eta}}\left[\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\gamma}^{\prime}}(H)}{\mathbb{P}_{\bm{0}}(H)}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\eta}}\left[\prod_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}\frac{2e^{w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})X_{\bm{e}}}}{1+e^{w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})}}\right],

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}X_{\bm{e}}:=\mathbbm{1}\{\bm{e}\in E(H)\}}
,
\bm{\eta}:=(\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n})
, and
w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e}):=\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{u\in\bm{e}}\eta_{u}
, for
\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}
.
Then

L_{\pi_{n}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\eta},\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}\left[\prod_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}\frac{4e^{(w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})+w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e}))X_{\bm{e}}}}{(1+e^{w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})})(1+e^{w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e})})}\right],

where
\eta_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\eta_{n}^{\prime}
are i.i.d Rademacher random variables which are independent of
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n}
,
\bm{\eta}^{\prime}:=(\eta_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\eta_{n}^{\prime})
, and
w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e}):=\frac{\varepsilon}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{u\in\bm{e}}\eta_{u}^{\prime}
, for
\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}
. Taking expectation with respect to
H_{0}
gives,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{H_{0}}[L^{2}_{\pi_{n}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\eta},\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}\left[\prod_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}\frac{2\,(e^{(w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})+w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e}))}+1)}{(1+e^{w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})})(1+e^{w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e})})}\right]

(E.3)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\eta},\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}\left[\prod_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}2\,\left\{\psi(w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e}))\psi(w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e}))+(1-\psi(w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})))(1-\psi(w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e})))\right\}\right],

where
\psi(x)
is the logistic function as defined in Lemma
A.4
. Using the Taylor expansions of
\psi(x)
and
1-\psi(x)
around
0
, we can show that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

\psi(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}+\frac{x}{4}+\frac{x^{3}}{48}\text{ and }1-\psi(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{x}{4}+\frac{x^{3}}{48}.

As a consequence, for
\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}
,

\displaystyle 2\,\left\{\psi(w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e}))\psi(w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e}))+(1-\psi(w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})))(1-\psi(w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e})))\right\}

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{1}{4}w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e})+\frac{1}{24}(w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})^{3}+w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e})^{3})+\frac{1}{24^{2}}w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})^{3}w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e})^{3}.

Using this bound in (
E.1
) gives,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{H_{0}}[L^{2}_{\pi_{n}}]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\eta},\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}\left[\prod_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}\left(1+\frac{1}{4}w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e})+\frac{1}{24}(w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})^{3}+w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e})^{3})+\frac{1}{24^{2}}w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})^{3}w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e})^{3}\right)\right]

(E.4)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\eta},\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}\left[e^{\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}\left\{\frac{1}{4}w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e})+\frac{1}{24}(w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})^{3}+w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e})^{3})+\frac{1}{24^{2}}w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})^{3}w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e})^{3}\right\}}\right],

since
1+x\leq e^{x}
.

Recalling the definition of
w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})
observe that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})^{3}\right|\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{3}}{n^{\frac{3}{2}}}\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}\left(\sum_{u\in\bm{e}}|\eta_{u}|\right)^{3}\leq\varepsilon^{3}n^{s-\frac{3}{2}}.

Hence,

(E.5)

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{2\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})^{3}}\right]\leq e^{2\varepsilon^{3}n^{s-\frac{3}{2}}}\rightarrow 1,

since
\varepsilon\ll n^{-\frac{2s-3}{4}}
and, for
s\geq 2
,
-\frac{s}{2}+\frac{3}{4}>0
.
Similarly, it can be shown that

(E.6)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{2\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})^{3}w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e})^{3}}\right]=1.

Then Hölder’s inequality applied to (
E.1
) followed by (
E.5
) and (
E.6
) gives

(E.7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{H_{0}}[L^{2}_{\pi}]

\displaystyle\leq\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\eta},\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}\left[e^{\frac{3}{4}\sum_{e\in{[n]\choose s}}w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e})}\right]\right\}^{1/3}(1+o(1)).

Next, observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}w_{\bm{\eta}}(\bm{e})w_{\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}(\bm{e})

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{n}\left\{\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}}\left(\sum_{u\in\bm{e}}\eta_{u}\right)\left(\sum_{v\in\bm{e}}\eta^{\prime}_{v}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{n}\left\{{n-1\choose s-1}\sum_{u=1}^{n}\eta_{u}\eta^{\prime}_{u}+{n-2\choose s-2}\sum_{1\leq u\neq v\leq n}\eta_{u}\eta^{\prime}_{v}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-2}\sum_{u=1}^{n}\eta_{u}\eta^{\prime}_{u}+\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-3}\sum_{1\leq u\neq v\leq n}\eta_{u}\eta^{\prime}_{v}

(E.8)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-2}\sum_{u=1}^{n}\eta_{u}\eta^{\prime}_{u}+\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-3}\left\{\left(\sum_{u=1}^{n}\eta_{u}\right)\left(\sum_{v=1}^{n}\eta^{\prime}_{v}\right)-\sum_{u=1}^{n}\eta_{u}\eta^{\prime}_{u}\right\}.

Note that
\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-3}\left|\sum_{u=1}^{n}\eta_{u}\eta^{\prime}_{u}\right|\leq\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-2}
. Hence,

(E.9)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\frac{9}{4}\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-3}\sum_{u=1}^{n}\eta_{u}\eta^{\prime}_{u}}\right]\lesssim e^{\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-2}}\rightarrow 1,

since
\varepsilon\ll n^{-\frac{2s-3}{4}}
.
From (
E.7
), by Hölder’s inequality followed by (
E.1
) and (
E.9
) gives

(E.10)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{H_{0}}[L^{2}_{\pi}]

\displaystyle\lesssim_{s}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\eta},\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}\left[e^{\frac{9}{4}\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-2}\sum_{u=1}^{n}\eta_{u}\eta^{\prime}_{u}}\right]\right\}^{1/9}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\eta},\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}\left[e^{\frac{9}{4}\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-3}\left(\sum_{u=1}^{n}\eta_{u}\right)\left(\sum_{v=1}^{n}\eta^{\prime}_{v}\right)}\right]\right\}^{1/9}(1+o(1)).

Denote
X_{n}:=\sum_{u=1}^{n}\eta_{u}
and
Y_{n}:=\sum_{v=1}^{n}\eta^{\prime}_{u}
. Since
X_{n}
and
Y_{n}
are independent and each of them is a sum of i.i.d. Rademacher random variables,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\eta},\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}\left[e^{\frac{9}{4}\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-3}X_{n}Y_{n}}\right]=\mathbb{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\frac{9}{4}\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-3}X_{n}Y_{n}}|Y_{n}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\cosh\left(\frac{9}{4}\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-3}Y_{n}\right)\right)^{n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[e^{\frac{81}{16}\varepsilon^{4}n^{2s-5}Y_{n}^{2}}\right],

where last step uses
\cosh(x)\leq e^{x^{2}}
, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Since
|Y_{n}|\leq n
, this implies,

(E.11)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\eta},\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}\left[e^{\frac{9}{4}\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-3}X_{n}Y_{n}}\right]\leq e^{\frac{81}{16}\varepsilon^{4}n^{2s-5}Y_{n}^{2}}\leq e^{\frac{81}{16}\varepsilon^{4}n^{2s-3}}\rightarrow 1,

since
\varepsilon\ll n^{-\frac{2s-3}{4}}
.
Next, observe that
\eta_{u}\eta^{\prime}_{u}
, for
u=1,\cdots,n
, are i.i.d. Rademacher random variables. Again using
\cosh(x)\leq e^{x^{2}}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we can show that

(E.12)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{\eta},\bm{\eta}^{\prime}}\left[e^{\frac{9}{4}\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-2}\sum_{u=1}^{n}\eta_{u}\eta^{\prime}_{u}}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\cosh\left(\frac{9}{4}\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-2}\right)\right)^{n}\leq e^{\frac{81}{16}\varepsilon^{4}n^{2s-3}}\rightarrow 1,

since
\varepsilon\ll n^{-\frac{2s-3}{4}}
.
Hence, using (
E.11
) and (
E.12
) in (
E.10
) gives,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}E_{H_{0}}[L^{2}_{\pi}]=1.

By Lemma
E.1
, this completes the proof of Theorem
3.3
(b).

E.2.
Testing Lower Bound in
L_{\infty}
Norm: Proof of Theorem
3.4
(b)

We choose
\bm{\gamma}=\bm{0}
,
\varepsilon\ll(\log n/n^{s-1})^{1/2}
, and construct a prior
\pi_{n}
on
H_{1}
as in (
3.8
) as follows: Suppose
\bm{\gamma}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with

\bm{\gamma}_{u}=\varepsilon\bm{e}_{u},

for
u\in[n]
, where
\bm{e}_{1},\ldots,\bm{e}_{n}
being the canonical basis vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then
\pi_{n}
assigns probability
1/n
to each
\bm{\gamma}_{u}
. Clearly,
\|\bm{\gamma}-\bm{\gamma}_{u}\|_{\infty}=\varepsilon
for all
u\in[n]
. Then, for
H\in\mathcal{H}_{n,s}
, the
\pi_{n}
integrated likelihood ratio is given by

L_{\pi_{n}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{u\in[n]}\prod_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}:u\in\bm{e}}\frac{2e^{\varepsilon X_{\bm{e}}}}{1+e^{\varepsilon}},

where
X_{\bm{e}}:=\bm{1}\{\bm{e}\in E(H)\}
.
Then

L_{\pi_{n}}^{2}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{u\in[n]}\prod_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}:u\in\bm{e}}\frac{4e^{2\varepsilon X_{\bm{e}}}}{(1+e^{\varepsilon})^{2}}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{u\neq v\in[n]}\prod_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}:u,v\in\bm{e}}\frac{4e^{2\varepsilon X_{\bm{e}}}}{(1+e^{\varepsilon})^{2}}.

Observe that

(E.13)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{H_{0}}\left[\prod_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}:u\in\bm{e}}\frac{4e^{2\varepsilon X_{\bm{e}}}}{(1+e^{\varepsilon})^{2}}\right]=\left(2\,\psi(\varepsilon)^{2}+2\,(1-\psi(\varepsilon))^{2}\right)^{{n\choose s-1}},

where
\psi(x)=\frac{e^{x}}{1+e^{x}}
. Similarly,

(E.14)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{H_{0}}\left[\prod_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}:u,v\in\bm{e}}\frac{4e^{2\varepsilon X_{\bm{e}}}}{(1+e^{\varepsilon})^{2}}\right]=\left(2\,\psi(\varepsilon)^{2}+2\,(1-\psi(\varepsilon))^{2}\right)^{{n\choose s-2}}.

Since
\varepsilon\ll(\log n/n^{s-1})^{1/2}
, a Taylor expansion around zero gives
\psi(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2})
. Hence,

2\psi(\varepsilon)^{2}+2(1-\psi(\varepsilon))^{2}=1+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

Therefore, by (
E.14
) and using
1+x\leq e^{x}
gives,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{H_{0}}[L_{\pi_{n}}^{2}]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}e^{O(\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-1})}+e^{O(\varepsilon^{2}n^{s-2})}\rightarrow 1,

since
\varepsilon\ll(\log n/n^{s-1})^{1/2}
.
By Lemma
E.1
, this completes the proof of Theorem
3.4
(b).

Appendix F
Proof of Proposition
A.1

Define
g=(g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{n}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
where
g_{u}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
for
u\in[n]
, as follows:

g_{u}(\bm{x})=\sum_{\bm{e}\in{[n]\choose s}:u\in\bm{e}}\frac{e^{\bm{x}^{\top}_{e}\bm{1}}}{1+e^{\bm{x}^{\top}_{e}\bm{1}}},

where
\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})^{\top}
and
\bm{x}_{\bm{e}}=(x_{u_{1}},x_{u_{2}},\ldots,x_{u_{s}})^{\top}
for
\bm{e}=(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{s})
.
Observe that
\mathcal{R}_{s}
is the range of
g
. Since the expected degree of a vertex is a weighted combination of all the possible degrees in
s
-uniform hypergraphs on
n
vertices, this implies
\bar{\mathcal{R}}_{s}\subseteq\mathrm{conv}\,(\mathcal{D}_{s})
.

To show the other side, for every
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we define,

f_{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}-\sum_{\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{s}\}\in{[n]\choose s}}\log(1+e^{x_{v_{1}}+\ldots+x_{v_{s}}}).

Since the probability of observing an
s
-uniform hypergraph with parameter
\bm{x}
and
s
-degree sequence
\bm{d}_{s}=(d_{s}(1),\ldots,d_{s}(n))
is

\frac{e^{\sum_{v=1}^{n}d_{s}(v)x_{v}}}{\prod_{\{v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{s}\}\in{[n]\choose s}}(1+e^{x_{v_{1}}+\ldots+x_{v_{s}}})}.

and is less than
1
, taking logarithm on both sides we get
f_{\bm{d}_{s}}(\bm{x})\leq 0
. Further as
f_{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})
depends linearly on
\bm{y}
, we have
f_{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})\leq 0
for all
\bm{y}\in\mathrm{conv}\,(\mathcal{D}_{s})
and
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Now, let us fix
\bm{y}\in\mathrm{conv}\,(\mathcal{D}_{s})
. It can be shown that the Hessian
\nabla^{2}f_{\bm{y}}(\bm{x})
is uniformly bounded, hence, by
[
10
, Lemma 3.1]
there exists a sequence
\{\bm{x}_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
such that
\nabla f_{\bm{y}}(\bm{x}_{k})\rightarrow 0
. Observing that
\nabla f_{\bm{y}}(\bm{x}_{k})=\bm{y}-g(\bm{x})
, we get
g(\bm{x}_{k})\rightarrow\bm{y}
. As
\bm{y}\in\mathrm{conv}\,(\mathcal{D}_{s})
is arbitrary, this implies
\mathrm{conv}\,(\mathcal{D}_{s})\subseteq\bar{\mathcal{R}}_{s}
.
\Box
