Multidimensional Brownian Risk Models with Random Trend
Goran Popivoda, Timofei Shashkov

Abstract




Abstract:
Let
\boldsymbol{B}(t)=(B_{1}(t),\dots,B_{d}(t))^{\top}
,
t\in[0,T]
,
d\geq 2
be a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion with independent components and let
\boldsymbol{\eta}=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{d})^{\top}
be a random vector independent of
\boldsymbol{B}
such that

\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{K}_{1}\leq\boldsymbol{\eta}\leq\boldsymbol{K}_{2}\right\}=\mathds{P}\left\{K_{11}\leq\eta_{1}\leq K_{21},\dots,K_{1d}\leq\eta_{d}\leq K_{2d}\right\}=1,

where
\boldsymbol{K}_{1}=(K_{11},\dots,K_{1d})^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{K}_{2}=(K_{21},\dots,K_{2d})^{\top}
are fixed
d
-dimensional vectors.

The goal of this paper is to derive asymptotics of

\mathds{P}\left\{\exists_{t\in[0,T]}:X_{1}(t)>a_{1}u,\dots,X_{d}(t)>a_{d}u\right\},\ \ \boldsymbol{X}(t)=\left(X_{1}(t),\dots,X_{d}(t)\right)^{\top}=A\boldsymbol{B}(t)-\boldsymbol{\eta}t

as
u\to\infty
under certain restrictions on the random vector
\boldsymbol{\eta}
and constants
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}
.

Key Words:
multivariate Brownian risk model; Brownian motion; simultaneous ruin probability;
simultaneous ruin time; ruin time approximation, random trend.

AMS Classification:
Primary 60G15; secondary 60G70

1.
Introduction

For a given insurance portfolio, let us consider the risk model defined by

R(t)=u+ct-\sigma Z(t),\ \ \ \ \ t\geq 0,

where
Z(t)
is a centered Gaussian risk process with a.s. continuous sample paths,
u>0
is the initial capital,
c>0
is the premium rate and
\sigma>0
is a constant.

The ruin probability over the time horizon
[0,T]
,
T>0
is defined by

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}_{T}(u)\coloneqq\mathds{P}\left\{\inf_{t\in[0,T]}R(t)<0\right\}=\mathds{P}\left\{\sup_{t\in[0,T]}(\sigma Z(t)-ct)>u\right\}

and in view of, e.g.,
[
1
,
2
]
, for
Z
being a standard Brownian motion,
for any
u\geq 0

\displaystyle\widetilde{\psi}_{T}(u)=\Phi\left(-\frac{u}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}-\frac{c\sqrt{T}}{\sigma}\right)+e^{-2cu/\sigma^{2}}\Phi\left(-\frac{u}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}+\frac{c\sqrt{T}}{\sigma}\right),

where
\Phi
is the distribution function of a
N(0,1)
random variable.

The asymptotic behavior of the classical ruin probability is a commonly investigated topic (see, for example,
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
). The primary obstacle in analyzing of extreme values of vector-valued Gaussian processes is that the Slepian inequality, a key tool in studying the excursion probabilities of Gaussian processes, cannot be applied to general vector-valued Gaussian processes. Therefore, there is a lack of an universal approach for investigating the excursion probabilities in the vector-valued case.

The two-dimensional Brownian motion risk model, a natural limiting model of several general bivariate insurance risk models, was recently studied in
[
8
]
. In
[
9
]
, the two-dimensional Brownian risk model’s simultaneous ruin probability and simultaneous ruin time approximations are obtained as the initial capital increases to infinity, while the paper
[
10
]
considers the simultaneous ruin probability of a
d
-dimensional Gaussian process that consists of mutually independent centered Gaussian processes with stationary increments.

The paper
[
11
]
derives asymptotic approximations for the Parisian and cumulative Parisian ruin probabilities, as well as the simultaneous ruin time, in the two-dimensional Brownian risk model as the initial capital approaches infinity.

This subject’s actuality has been confirmed by papers such as
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
7
]
. Some of these papers have examined non-simultaneous multidimensional models.

Let us now consider
d\geq 2
portfolios with the risk vector-process
\boldsymbol{R}(t)
defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{R}(t)=(R_{1}(t),\dots,R_{d}(t))^{\top}=\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{c}t-A\boldsymbol{B}(t),\quad t\geq 0

such that
R_{i}(t)
is the risk process that corresponds to the
i
-th portfolio,
i=1,\dots,d
. Here
A
is a non-degenerate
d\times d
matrix,
\boldsymbol{B}(t)=(B_{1}(t),\dots,B_{d}(t))^{\top}
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion with independent components,
\boldsymbol{u}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{d})^{\top}
is a vector of initial capitals and
\boldsymbol{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{d})^{\top}
is a vector of premium rates. Even though it is natural to assume in applications that the vector
\boldsymbol{c}
has only nonnegative components, we do not impose such conditions here.

In this paper, we study the event of simultaneous bankruptcy of all
d
insurance companies. To be more specific,
denote

\psi_{T}(\boldsymbol{u}):=\mathds{P}\left\{\exists t\in[0,T]:\boldsymbol{W}(t)-\boldsymbol{c}t>\boldsymbol{u}\right\},

where
\boldsymbol{W}(t)=A\boldsymbol{B}(t)
. In
[
16
, Thm
2.3
]
, it was shown that

\psi_{T}(\boldsymbol{a}u)\sim\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{a}}\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(T)>\boldsymbol{a}u+\boldsymbol{c}T\right\}

as
u\to\infty
, where
\boldsymbol{a}
has at least one positive component and
\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{a}}\in(0,\infty)
is a constant (see
[
16
, Thm
2.3
]
or
Theorem

2.3
of the present paper). Hereafter by
\sim
we denote asymptotic equivalence as
u\to\infty
.

So far we have considered
A
and
\boldsymbol{c}
to be fixed, but the natural question is whether one can obtain asymptotics of a similar form, when
A
and
\boldsymbol{c}
are assumed to be random. In general this is a challenging problem. However, under some natural assumptions, this problem can be solved, for example, if we assume
\boldsymbol{c}
to be random (and we below denote it by
\boldsymbol{\eta}
), but
A
to be fixed. We also assume that
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is independent of
\boldsymbol{W}(t)
. In fact, it turns out that

\psi_{T}(\boldsymbol{a}u)\sim\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{a}}\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(T)>\boldsymbol{a}u+\boldsymbol{\eta}T\right\},\ \ \text{ as }u\to\infty,

where
\mathcal{C}_{\boldsymbol{a}}
is the same as for the case of a fixed trend and where we assumed that
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is almost surely a uniformly bounded random vector, i.e., there exist two
d
-dimensional constants
\boldsymbol{K}_{1}=(K_{11},\dots,K_{1d})^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{K}_{2}=(K_{21},\dots,K_{2d})^{\top}
such that

(1)

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{K}_{1}\leq\boldsymbol{\eta}\leq\boldsymbol{K}_{2}\right\}=\mathds{P}\left\{K_{11}\leq\eta_{1}\leq K_{21},\dots,K_{1d}\leq\eta_{d}\leq K_{2d}\right\}=1.

We organize the paper in the following way: in Section
2
we present and discuss the main result (
Theorem

2.3
) followed by illustrating examples. In Section 3 we first provide a brief outline of the proof of
Theorem

2.3
and then discuss all auxiliary statements in detail.

2.
Main result

To formulate our main result, we need to introduce the quadratic programming problem.

2.1.
Quadratic programming problem

Before formulating the quadratic programming problem, we introduce some auxiliary notation.

Unless otherwise stated, we assume that the bold designations are vector-valued and that the nonbold designations are scalars.

For some set
F=\{f_{1},f_{2},\dots,f_{l}\}\subset\{1,\dots,k\}
and some
k
-dimensional vector
\boldsymbol{x}
, we denote
\boldsymbol{x}_{F}:=(x_{f_{1}},x_{f_{2}},\dots,x_{f_{l}})
, an
l
-dimensional vector that is obtained from the vector
\boldsymbol{x}
by taking only coordinates with indices from the set
F
.

Similarly, we define
\Sigma_{F_{1}F_{2}}
, where
\Sigma
is a
k\times k
matrix and
F_{1},F_{2}
are subsets of
\{1,\dots,k\}
with
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
elements, respectively: the
l_{1}\times l_{2}
matrix
\Sigma_{F_{1}F_{2}}
is formed from
\Sigma
by taking only coefficients
\Sigma_{f_{1},f_{2}}
, where
f_{1}\in F_{1}
and
f_{2}\in F_{2}
.

For example, if

\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{pmatrix}
,

F_{1}=\{1,2\}
and
F_{2}=\{2\}
, then

\Sigma_{F_{1}F_{2}}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{12}\\
a_{22}\end{pmatrix}
.

The next proposition, which is stated, for example, in
[
17
, Lem
2.1
]
, discusses the quadratic programming problem.

Proposition 2.1
.

[Quadratic programming problem]
Let
\Sigma
be a
d\times d
positive definite matrix and let
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus(-\infty,0]^{d}
.
The quadratic programming problem

\Pi_{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{a})
: ”minimize
\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}
under constrain
\boldsymbol{x}\geq\boldsymbol{a}
” has a unique solution
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
, and there exists a unique nonempty
index set
I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,d\}
with
m\leq d
elements such that

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{I}=\boldsymbol{a}_{I},\quad(\Sigma_{II})^{-1}\boldsymbol{a}_{I}>\boldsymbol{0}_{I},

and if
I^{c}:=\{1,\ldots,d\}\setminus I\not=\emptyset
, then

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{I^{c}}=\Sigma_{{I^{c}}I}(\Sigma_{II})^{-1}\boldsymbol{a}_{I}\geq\boldsymbol{a}_{I^{c}}.

Next,

\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\geq\boldsymbol{a}}\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{I}^{T}(\Sigma_{II})^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{I}>0

and for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}=\boldsymbol{x}_{F}^{T}(\Sigma_{FF})^{-1}\boldsymbol{a}_{F},

with
F
being an arbitrary subset of
\{1,\ldots,d\}
such that
I\subset F
. If
\boldsymbol{a}=(a,\ldots,a)^{\top},a\in(0,\infty)
,
then
2\leq\left\lvert I\right\rvert\leq d
, and
{I^{c}}
is empty if
\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{a}>\boldsymbol{0}
.

Conversely, if for some nonempty index set

I\subset\{1,\ldots,d\}
we have

(\Sigma_{II})^{-1}\boldsymbol{a}_{I}>\boldsymbol{0}_{I},\quad\Sigma_{{I^{c}}I}(\Sigma_{II})^{-1}\boldsymbol{a}_{I}\geq\boldsymbol{a}_{I^{c}},

then
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
with
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{I^{c}}=\Sigma_{{I^{c}}I}(\Sigma_{II})^{-1}\boldsymbol{a}_{I},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{I}=\boldsymbol{a}_{I}
is the solution of
\Pi_{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{a})
.

Remark 2.2
.

From
Proposition

2.1
, it follows that
\left(\Sigma^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}\right)_{I^{c}}=\boldsymbol{0}_{I^{c}}
.

Indeed, for all
\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
by
Proposition

2.1
, we have

\boldsymbol{x}_{I}^{T}(\Sigma^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}})_{I}+\boldsymbol{x}_{I^{c}}^{T}(\Sigma^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}})_{I^{c}}=\boldsymbol{x}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}=\boldsymbol{x}_{I}^{T}(\Sigma_{II}^{-1})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{I}.

Therefore

\boldsymbol{x}_{I^{c}}^{T}(\Sigma^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}})_{I^{c}}=\boldsymbol{x}_{I}^{T}(\Sigma_{II}^{-1})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}_{I}-\boldsymbol{x}_{I}^{T}(\Sigma^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}})_{I}.

Since the right-hand side in the last equality is independent of
\boldsymbol{x}_{I^{c}}
, we can conclude that
\left(\Sigma^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}\right)_{I^{c}}=\boldsymbol{0}_{I^{c}}
.

2.2.
Main result

Here and below all operations and relations for vectors such as
"+","/","<",
and multiplication by scalar constants, are assumed to be componentwise. For example,
\boldsymbol{x}/\boldsymbol{y}:=\left(x_{1}/y_{1},\dots,x_{k}/y_{k}\right)^{\top}
for
k
-dimensional vectors
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{y}
.

Let
\boldsymbol{W}(t)=A\boldsymbol{B}(t)
, where
A
is a fixed non-degenerate
d\times d
real matrix and
\boldsymbol{B}
is a
d
-dimensional Brownian motion with independent components,
d\geq 2
. Let also
\Sigma
be the covariance matrix of
\boldsymbol{W}
.

Denote by
\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle
for
k
-dimensional vectors
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}
, the standard scalar product on the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, i.e.,

\langle\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}y_{i}.

Using the self-similarity of Brownian motion without loss of generality, we assume in the sequel that
T=1
.
Denote then

\psi_{1}(\boldsymbol{a}u)=\mathds{P}\left\{\exists_{t\in[0,1]}:\boldsymbol{W}(t)-\boldsymbol{\eta}t>\boldsymbol{a}u\right\}.

Using notation from
Proposition

2.1
, we formulate the main result.

Theorem 2.3
.

Under the above stated conditions,
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus(-\infty,0]^{d}

\psi_{1}(\boldsymbol{a}u)\sim\left(\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}\right)I_{a}\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u+\boldsymbol{\eta}\right\},\text{ as }u\to\infty,

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\Sigma^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}
and

I_{a}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{|I|}}\mathds{P}\left\{\exists_{t\geq 0}:\boldsymbol{W}_{I}(t)-\boldsymbol{a}_{I}t>\boldsymbol{x}_{I}\right\}e^{\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{I},\boldsymbol{x}_{I}\rangle}d\boldsymbol{x}_{I}\in(0,\infty).

We continue with illustrating examples and discussions.

Example 2.4
.

Consider the case where
d=2
and
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\rho&\rho^{*}\end{pmatrix}
, with
\boldsymbol{a}=(1,a)^{\top}
, where
a\leq 1
. Then the covariance matrix of the vector
\boldsymbol{W}(1)=A\boldsymbol{B}(1)=(B_{1}(1),\rho B_{1}(1)+\rho^{*}B_{2}(1))^{\top}

is equal to
\begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\
\rho&1\end{pmatrix}.
If
a>\rho
, then
(1,a)
is an optimal solution to the quadratic programming problem and
I=\{1,2\}
.

If
a\leq\rho
, then
(1,\rho)
is an optimal solution with
I=\{1\}
.

Therefore, using the result of
Theorem

2.3
, we obtain the following result.

Corollary 2.5
.

If
a>\rho
, then

\psi_{1}(u,au)\sim I_{a}\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\mathds{P}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}W_{1}(1)-\eta_{1}>u\\
W_{2}(1)-\eta_{2}>au\end{array}\right\},\quad u\to\infty,

where

I_{a}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathds{P}\left\{\exists_{t\geq 0}:\begin{array}[]{ccc}W_{1}(t)-t>x_{1}\\
W_{2}(t)-at>x_{2}\end{array}\right\}e^{\lambda_{1}x_{1}+\lambda_{2}x_{2}}\,\text{\rm d}\boldsymbol{x},

and
\lambda_{1}=(1-a\rho)/(1-\rho^{2})
,
\lambda_{2}=(a-\rho)/(1-\rho^{2})
.

If
a\leq\rho
, then
I_{a}=2,\lambda_{1}=1
and

\psi_{1}(u,au)\sim 2\mathds{P}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}W_{1}(1)-\eta_{1}>u\\
W_{2}(1)-\eta_{2}>au\end{array}\right\},\quad u\to\infty.

Discuss the model, which generalizes the Example
2.4
.

Example 2.6
.

(
Equi correlated risk model
)
.
Let
A
be such that
\Sigma=AA^{T}
with
\Sigma_{ii}=1
for all
i=1,\dots,d
and
\Sigma_{ij}=\rho\in(-1/(d-1),1)
, if
i\neq j
,
i,j\in\{1,\dots,d\}
.

As it was shown in
[
10
]

\Sigma^{-1}=\left[I_{d}-\boldsymbol{1}\boldsymbol{1}^{T}\frac{\rho}{1+\rho(d-1)}\right]\frac{1}{1-\rho},

where hereafter
I_{d}
is a
d
-dimensional identity matrix and
\boldsymbol{1}=(1,\dots,1)^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Moreover, according to
[
10
]
if
a_{1}\geq\dots\geq a_{d}
,
a_{1}>0
, then
|I|=d
if and only if

(2)

\displaystyle a_{d}>\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}}{1+\rho(d-1)}.

Additionally

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{I}=\frac{1}{1-\rho}\left[\boldsymbol{a}_{I}-\rho\frac{\sum_{i\in I}a_{i}}{1+\rho(|I|-1)}\boldsymbol{1}_{I}\right].

Take
a_{1}=\dots=a_{d}=a>0
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}=(\eta,\dots,\eta)^{T}
, where
\eta
is some bounded random variable. Then, according to (
2
),
I=\{1,\dots,d\}
, and by
Theorem

2.3
, we obtain that

\psi_{1}(\boldsymbol{a}u)\sim\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\right)I_{a}\mathds{P}\left\{\forall i\in\{1,\dots,d\}:W_{i}(1)>au+\eta\right\},\text{ as }u\to\infty,

where

I_{a}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathds{P}\left\{\exists_{t\geq 0}:\boldsymbol{W}(t)-\boldsymbol{a}t>\boldsymbol{x}\right\}e^{\langle\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{x}\rangle}d\boldsymbol{x}\in(0,\infty).

In the following two examples, we show how the asymptotics can be simplified for specific distributions of
\boldsymbol{\eta}
.

Example 2.7
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\eta}=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{d})^{T}
, where
\eta_{k}
is a Bernoulli random variable with parameter
p_{k}\in[0,1]
for all
k\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, and
\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{d}
are jointly independent.
Then it turns out that

\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u+\boldsymbol{\eta}\right\}\sim\prod_{k=1}^{d}(1-p_{k})\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u\right\}

as
u\to\infty
. Indeed, since
\boldsymbol{\eta}
is independent of
\boldsymbol{W}(1)
, by the law of total probability,

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u+\boldsymbol{\eta}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\{0,1\}^{d}}\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u+\boldsymbol{\eta}|\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{k}\right\}\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{k}\right\}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=1}^{d}(1-p_{k})\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u\right\}+\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\{0,1\}^{d}:\boldsymbol{k}\neq\boldsymbol{0}}\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u+\boldsymbol{k}\right\}\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{k}\right\}

Observe that

\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\{0,1\}^{d}:\boldsymbol{k}\neq\boldsymbol{0}}\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u+\boldsymbol{k}\right\}\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{k}\right\}=o\left(\prod_{k=1}^{d}(1-p_{k})\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u\right\}\right),

as
u\to\infty
.
Indeed, there exists a uniformly in
\boldsymbol{k}
bounded from above and below constant
D_{2}(\boldsymbol{k})
such that

\frac{1}{u^{|I|}}\phi_{\Sigma}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}u+\boldsymbol{k})\sim D_{2}(\boldsymbol{k})\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u+\boldsymbol{k}\right\},

as
u\to\infty
uniformly in
\boldsymbol{k}
(see
Lemma

3.4
below). Hence, there exists a constant
r_{0}>0
that is independent of
\boldsymbol{k}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\{0,1\}^{d}:\boldsymbol{k}\neq\boldsymbol{0}}\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u+\boldsymbol{k}\right\}\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{k}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq r_{0}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\{0,1\}^{d}:\boldsymbol{k}\neq\boldsymbol{0}}\frac{1}{u^{|I|}}\phi_{\Sigma}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}u+\boldsymbol{k})

\displaystyle=r_{0}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\{0,1\}^{d}:\boldsymbol{k}\neq\boldsymbol{0}}\frac{1}{u^{|I|}}\phi_{\Sigma}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}u)e^{-(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}u)^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{k}-\frac{\boldsymbol{k}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{k}}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq r_{0}\frac{1}{u^{|I|}}\phi_{\Sigma}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}u)\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\{0,1\}^{d}:\boldsymbol{k}\neq\boldsymbol{0}}e^{-(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}u)^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{k}},

where we used the fact that
\Sigma^{-1}
is a positive definite matrix.
By
Remark

2.2

(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}u)^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{k}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{I}^{T}\boldsymbol{k}_{I}u,\ \text{ where }\ \boldsymbol{\lambda}_{I}=\left(\Sigma^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}\right)_{I}>\boldsymbol{0}_{I}.

Hence, since
\boldsymbol{k}_{I}\geq\boldsymbol{0}_{I}
, by
Lemma

3.4
, we conclude that

r_{0}\frac{1}{u^{|I|}}\phi_{\Sigma}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}u)\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\{0,1\}^{d}:\boldsymbol{k}\neq\boldsymbol{0}}e^{-(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}u)^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{k}}\leq 2^{d}r_{2}\frac{1}{u^{|I|}}\phi_{\Sigma}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}u)e^{-r_{1}u}=o\left(\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u\right\}\right).

where

r_{1}=\min_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\{0,1\}^{d}:\boldsymbol{k}\neq\boldsymbol{0}}\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{I}^{T}\boldsymbol{k}_{I}\right)>0

and
r_{2}
is a positive constant. Therefore, it follows that

\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u+\boldsymbol{\eta}\right\}\sim\prod_{k=1}^{d}(1-p_{k})\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u\right\},

as
u\to\infty
. According to
Theorem

2.3
, we obtain that

\psi_{1}(\boldsymbol{a}u)\sim\prod_{i=k}^{d}(1-p_{k})\left(\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}\right)I_{a}\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u\right\}

as
u\to\infty
.

Example 2.8
.

Let now
\boldsymbol{\eta}\sim Uni([0,1]^{d})
, i.e. uniformly distributed on
[0,1]^{d}
vector-valued random variable. Moreover, to make calculations simpler, assume that
I=\{1,\dots,d\}
. Observe first that by the law of total probability, we have that

\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u+\boldsymbol{\eta}\right\}=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u+\boldsymbol{c}\right\}d\boldsymbol{c}.

Hence, by
Lemma

3.4
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u+\boldsymbol{\eta}\right\}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{1}{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\right)u^{d}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\phi_{\Sigma}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}u+\boldsymbol{c})d\boldsymbol{c}=\frac{1}{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\right)u^{d}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}e^{-\frac{(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}u+\boldsymbol{c})^{T}\Sigma^{-1}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}u+\boldsymbol{c})}{2}}d\boldsymbol{c}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\right)u^{d}}e^{-\frac{(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}u)^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{a}}u}{2}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}e^{\frac{-2u\boldsymbol{c}^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{c}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{c}}{2}}d\boldsymbol{c}

\displaystyle\sim\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u\right\}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}e^{\frac{-2u\boldsymbol{c}^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{c}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{c}}{2}}d\boldsymbol{c}

as
u\to\infty
. Using dominated convergence theorem, one can obtain that

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{d}}e^{\frac{-2u\boldsymbol{c}^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\boldsymbol{c}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol{c}}{2}}d\boldsymbol{c}=\frac{1}{u^{d}}\int_{u[0,1]^{d}}e^{\frac{-2\boldsymbol{c}^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\left(\boldsymbol{c}/\boldsymbol{u}\right)^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\left(\boldsymbol{c}/\boldsymbol{u}\right)}{2}}d\boldsymbol{c}\sim\frac{1}{u^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}}e^{-\boldsymbol{c}^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}}d\boldsymbol{c}=\frac{1}{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\right)u^{d}}.

Therefore we obtain that

\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u+\boldsymbol{\eta}\right\}\sim\frac{1}{\left(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\right)u^{d}}\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u\right\}

\displaystyle\alpha_{u}(a,\boldsymbol{x})e^{x_{1}+K(x_{2})x_{2}-\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{2(1-\rho^{2})}}

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\left\{\exists_{t\in[0,\Lambda]}:\boldsymbol{X}_{u}(t)>\boldsymbol{0}\right\}e^{x_{1}(1+\frac{(\boldsymbol{h}_{\max}(x_{1}))_{1}}{u})+K(x_{2})x_{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}}{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)^{T}\Sigma^{-1}\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{x}}{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}}\right)},

where
(\boldsymbol{h}_{\max}(x_{1}))_{1}
is the first coordinate of the vector function
(\boldsymbol{h}_{\max}(x_{1}))_{1}
. Since
\Sigma^{-1}
is a positive definite matrix, there exists
\delta>0
such that
\Sigma^{-1}-\delta I_{d}
is positive definite too. Hence, we have that

\displaystyle\alpha_{u}(a,\boldsymbol{x})e^{x_{1}+K(x_{2})x_{2}-\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{2(1-\rho^{2})}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\left\{\exists_{t\in[0,\Lambda]}:\boldsymbol{X}_{u}(t)>\boldsymbol{0}\right\}e^{x_{1}(1+\frac{(\boldsymbol{h}_{\max}(x_{1}))_{1}}{u})+K(x_{2})x_{2}}e^{-\frac{\delta x_{1}^{2}}{u^{2}}-\delta x_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathds{P}\left\{\exists_{t\in[0,\Lambda]}:\boldsymbol{X}_{u}(t)>\boldsymbol{0}\right\}e^{x_{1}(1+\frac{(\boldsymbol{h}_{\max}(x_{1}))_{1}}{u})+K(x_{2})x_{2}-\delta x_{2}^{2}}.

If
x_{1}>0
, then using Borel’s inequality
[
18
, Lem
4.5
]
one can check that there exist positive constants
\overline{C}
and
C
such that for large enough

\mathds{P}\left\{\exists_{t\in[0,\Lambda]}:\boldsymbol{X}_{u}(t)>\boldsymbol{0}\right\}\leq\mathds{P}\left\{\exists_{t\in[0,\Lambda]}:uD_{1}(t_{u})-t-x_{1}+\frac{tx_{1}}{u^{2}}>0\right\}\leq\overline{C}e^{-Cx_{1}^{2}}.

Hence, one can define the function
F

\displaystyle F(x_{1},x_{2})=\begin{cases}\overline{C}e^{-Cx_{1}^{2}+2x_{1}+K(x_{2})x_{2}-\delta x_{2}^{2}},\ &\text{ if }x_{1}>0;\\
e^{\frac{x_{1}}{2}+K(x_{2})x_{2}-\delta x_{2}^{2}},\ &\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

It is easy to see that
F
is integrable on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and that

\alpha_{u}(a,\boldsymbol{x})e^{x_{1}+K(x_{2})x_{2}-\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{2(1-\rho^{2})}}\leq F(\boldsymbol{x}).

The function
\alpha(\boldsymbol{x})e^{x_{1}+K(x_{2})x_{2}-\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{2(1-\rho^{2})}}
is integrable by the similar reasons, using Borel’s inequality, so we omit the proof here. Thus

\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\alpha_{u}(a,\boldsymbol{x})-\alpha(\boldsymbol{x}))e^{x_{1}-\frac{x_{2}^{2}-2(c_{2}-\rho c_{1})x_{2}}{2(1-\rho^{2})}}d\boldsymbol{x}\Big|\to 0,

uniformly in
\boldsymbol{c}\in[K_{11},K_{21}]\times[K_{12},K_{22}]
. In addition, there exists a constant
\overline{K}
such that

\frac{(c_{2}-\rho c_{1})}{1-\rho^{2}}<\overline{K}.

Therefore, we obtain that, since
\alpha(\boldsymbol{x})=1
for
x_{1}<0,x_{2}<0
, then

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\alpha(\boldsymbol{x})e^{x_{1}-\frac{x_{2}^{2}-2(c_{2}-\rho c_{1})x_{2}}{2(1-\rho^{2})}}d\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{x_{1}<0,x_{2}<0}e^{x_{1}-\frac{x_{2}^{2}-2(c_{2}-\rho c_{1})x_{2}}{2(1-\rho^{2})}}d\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{x_{1}<0,x_{2}<0}e^{x_{1}+\overline{K}x_{2}-\frac{x_{2}^{2}}{2(1-\rho^{2})}}d\boldsymbol{x}=:C_{\min}>0.

Hence, we can conclude that indeed

\frac{\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(\alpha_{u}(a,\boldsymbol{x})-\alpha(\boldsymbol{x}))e^{x_{1}-\frac{x_{2}^{2}-2(c_{2}-\rho c_{1})x_{2}}{2(1-\rho^{2})}}d\boldsymbol{x}\Big|}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\alpha(\boldsymbol{x})e^{x_{1}-\frac{x_{2}^{2}-2(c_{2}-\rho c_{1})x_{2}}{2(1-\rho^{2})}}d\boldsymbol{x}}\to 0,

uniformly in
\boldsymbol{c}
or, equivalently,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\alpha_{u}(a,\boldsymbol{x})e^{x_{1}-\frac{x_{2}^{2}-2(c_{2}-\rho c_{1})x_{2}}{2(1-\rho^{2})}}d\boldsymbol{x}\sim\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\alpha(\boldsymbol{x})e^{x_{1}-\frac{x_{2}^{2}-2(c_{2}-\rho c_{1})x_{2}}{2(1-\rho^{2})}}d\boldsymbol{x},

uniformly in
\boldsymbol{c}
, as
u\to\infty
. Since

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\alpha(\boldsymbol{x})e^{x_{1}-\frac{x_{2}^{2}-2(c_{2}-\rho c_{1})x_{2}}{2(1-\rho^{2})}}d\boldsymbol{x}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathds{P}\left\{\exists_{t\in[0,\Lambda]}:W_{1}(t)-t-x_{1}>0\right\}e^{x_{1}}dx_{1}\int_{\boldsymbol{x}_{U}<\boldsymbol{0}_{U}}e^{-\frac{x_{2}^{2}-2(c_{2}-\rho c_{1})x_{2}}{2(1-\rho^{2})}}dx_{2},

by
Lemma

3.4
we can conclude that

\mathds{P}\left\{\exists_{t\in[\delta(u,\Lambda),1]}:\boldsymbol{W}(t)-\boldsymbol{\eta}t>\boldsymbol{a}u\,\middle|\,\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{c}\right\}\sim\left(\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}\right)I_{a}(\Lambda)\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u+\boldsymbol{c}\right\}

uniformly in
\boldsymbol{c}
. Here we used that
U=\emptyset
if
a<\rho
and
U=\{2\}
if
a=\rho
.

Hence by the law of total probability, we obtain that

M(u,\Lambda)\sim\left(\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}\right)I_{a}(\Lambda)\mathds{P}\left\{\boldsymbol{W}(1)>\boldsymbol{a}u+\boldsymbol{\eta}\right\},\quad u\to\infty.

That finishes the proof of Lemma
3.3
.
