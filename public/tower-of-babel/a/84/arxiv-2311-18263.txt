Asymptotic stability and cut-off phenomenon for the underdamped Langevin dynamics
Seungwoo Lee, Mouad Ramil, Insuk Seo

Abstract
. In this article, we provide detailed analysis of the long-time behavior of the underdamped Langevin dynamics. We first provide a necessary condition guaranteeing that the zero-noise dynamical system converges to its unique attractor. We also observed that this condition is sharp for a large class of linear models. We then prove the so-called cut-off phenomenon in the small-noise regime under this condition. This result provides the precise asymptotics of the mixing time of the process and of the distance between the distribution of the process and its stationary measure. The main difficulty of this work relies on the degeneracy of its infinitesimal generator which is not elliptic, thus requiring a new set of methods.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Underdamped Langevin dynamics

We start by introducing the underdamped Langevin dynamics which are
the main concern of the current article. In statistical physics the
evolution of a molecular system at a fixed temperature
T
consisting
of
N
particles can be modeled by the underdamped Langevin dynamics

(q_{t},\,p_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=3N
,
where
q_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
p_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote
the vectors of positions and momenta of the particles. The process

(q_{t},\,p_{t})
satisfies a system of stochastic differential equations
of the form

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathrm{d}q_{t}=M^{-1}p_{t}\mathrm{d}t,\\
&\mathrm{d}p_{t}=F(q_{t})\mathrm{d}t-\gamma M^{-1}p_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\gamma\beta^{-1}}\mathrm{d}B_{t}\;,\end{aligned}\right.

where
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is the mass matrix,
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

is the force acting on the particles,
\gamma>0
is the friction
parameter, and
\beta^{-1}=k_{B}T
with
k_{B}
the Boltzmann constant
and
T
the temperature of the system. Such dynamics can be used
to compute thermodynamic quantities of the process.

Thermodynamic quantities are averages of given observables against
the stationary distribution of (
1.1
). They capture
the characteristics of the system at equilibrium and offer numerous
applications in biology, chemistry and materials science. However,
the computation of such quantities poses a certain number of computational
challenges. In fact, direct numerical integration is in general very
difficult when the number of particles
N
is large and thus
the space dimension when computing integrals against the stationary distribution. This is why stochastic
approaches are generally preferred and consist in computing the time-average
of the observable. Using the ergodicity of (
1.1
),
such time averages shall converge to the expected thermodynamic limit.
However, the speed of convergence relies heavily on how fast the process (
1.1
)
converges to its stationary distribution. Therefore, one area of research
is to understand the dependency of this speed of convergence to equilibrium
in the low temperature regime, i.e.
\beta^{-1}
goes to
0
. In
the current article, we completely solve this problem by verifying
the so-called the cut-off phenomenon for the underdamped Langevin
processes under a suitable reasonable condition on the vector field

F
.

Main results of the article

To be more precise, we set
M=\mathbb{I}_{d}
and
\epsilon=\gamma k_{B}T>0

so that we can rewrite (
1.1
) as

(1.2)

\begin{cases}\mathrm{d}q_{t}^{\epsilon}=p_{t}^{\epsilon}\mathrm{d}t,\\
\mathrm{d}p_{t}^{\epsilon}=-F(q_{t}^{\epsilon})\mathrm{d}t-\gamma p_{t}^{\epsilon}\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\epsilon}\mathrm{d}B_{t}\;,\end{cases}

Then, we are interested in the behavior of the
2d
-dimensional process

X_{t}^{\epsilon}=(q_{t}^{\epsilon},\,p_{t}^{\epsilon})
in the low-temperature
regime, i.e., the regime where
\epsilon
tends to
0
. We first
provide the necessary condition (cf. Assumption
2.2
)
to the vector field
F
under which the zero-temperature dynamics,
i.e., the dynamics (
1.2
) with
\epsilon=0
converges, as

t\rightarrow\infty,
to a unique stable equilibria of the dynamics.
As we can observe from the discussion below, this condition is very
close to the necessary and sufficient condition in the sense that
for the large-class of linear model our condition is indeed necessary
and sufficient.

Under this condition, we demonstrate in Proposition
2.6
that
the process
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
is positive recurrent and therefore
admits a unique stationary probability measure
\mu_{\epsilon}
.
Then, by the standard erodicity argument, the distribution of the
process
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
, starting from any fixed point in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
converges to
\mu_{\epsilon}
, as
t\rightarrow\infty
(thermalization).
The precise quantitative analysis of this convergence is the main
problem confronted in the current article. In fact, we prove the so-called

cut-off phenomenon
which will be explained in
the next subsection.

1.2.
Cut-off phenomenon

The convergence to equilibrium for stochastic diffusion processes
sometimes exhibits a brutal decrease around a cut-off time where the
total-variation distance between the law of the process and its equilibrium
goes from a value close to
1
to a value close to
0
. This fact
is known as
cut-off phenomenon
.

Figure 1.1.
Illustration of the cut-off phenomenon: the red line is the graph of the function
d(t)=\textrm{d}_{\textrm{TV}}(Y_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\pi_{\epsilon})
which abruptly drops from values close to
1
to values close to
0
at
t=t_{\epsilon}
in the window of size
w_{\epsilon}

Definition 1.1

(Cut-off phenomenon)
.

For
\epsilon>0
, let
(Y_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
be a Markov
process with stationary probability measure
\pi_{\epsilon}
. We
denote by
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\cdot,\,\cdot\right)
the
total-variation distance (for the precise definition, we refer to Definition
2.7
) so that
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Y_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\pi_{\epsilon}\right)

represents the total-variation distance between the distribution of

Y_{t}^{\epsilon}
(for fixed time
t\geq 0
) and
\pi_{\epsilon}
.
Then, we say that the process
(Y_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}

exhibits
the cut-off
at mixing time
t^{\epsilon}
with the window
w^{\epsilon}

satisfying

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}t^{\epsilon}=\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{w^{\epsilon}}{t^{\epsilon}}=0\;,

if we have

(1.3)

\begin{cases}\lim_{c\rightarrow+\infty}\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Y_{t^{\epsilon}+cw^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon},\,\pi_{\epsilon}\right)=0\;,\\
\lim_{c\rightarrow-\infty}\liminf_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Y_{t^{\epsilon}+cw^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon},\,\pi_{\epsilon}\right)=1\;.\end{cases}

We refer to Figure
1.1
for an illustration of the cut-off
phenomenon. The cut-off phenomenon implies that, the distribution of the process
Y_{t}
converges to its stationary measure abruptly at the mixing time
t_{\epsilon}
within the window of size
w_{\epsilon}\ll t_{\epsilon}
.

Moreover, we say that there is
profile cut-off

or
profile thermalisation
with the profile

G:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\,1]
which is a decreasing function with

G(-\infty)=1
and
G(+\infty)=0
if we have

(1.4)

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Y_{t^{\epsilon}+cw^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon},\,\pi_{\epsilon}\right)=G(c)

for all
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Of course, the profile cut-off is a stronger
notion than cut-off since (
1.4
) immediately implies (
1.3
).

Remark 1.2
.

Although we explain the cut-off phenomenon in the regime
\epsilon\rightarrow 0

to match with the main result of the current article, the other models
can exhibit the cut-off phenomenon in a different regime, e.g.,
N\rightarrow\infty

where
N
is the number of sites in a spin system.

In the modern study of the mixing behavior of the statistical phyics
systems, the cut-off phenomenon has been verified for various stochastic

for high-temperature spin systems such as the Glauber dynamics of
the Curie-Weiss type mean-field model,

,
3
,

]
,
the Glauber dynamics of the Ising/Potts model on lattices
[

,

,

,

]
,
the Swandsen-Wang dynamics of the Ising/Potts model
[
27
]
,
and the FK-dynamics of the random-cluster model
[
8
]
.
The cut-off phenomenon is also widely observed in the interacting
particle systems such as the zero-range processes
[
25
]
,
the exclusion processes
[
9
,
5
,
30
]
,
and the east process
[
7
]
. It is also demonstrated
that the cut-off phenomenon is universally observed for the random
walks on nicely expanding graphs, see
[

,
19
,

]
.
We also refer to the monograph
[

]
for the introduction to

1.3.
Cut-off phenomenon for underdamped Langevin dynamics

In
[
2
]
, the cut-off phenomenon for the
overdamped

Langevin dynamics
(\overline{q}_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

defined by

(1.5)

\mathrm{d}\overline{q}_{t}^{\epsilon}=-F(\overline{q}_{t}^{\epsilon})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2\epsilon}\mathrm{d}B_{t}\;,

where
F\in\mathcal{C}^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
where
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denotes the
d
-dimensional Brownian motion
has been established. In this work, it has been assumed that there
exist constants
\delta,C,\eta>0
such that for all
q,q^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(1.6)

\langle q,\mathrm{D}F(q^{\prime})q\rangle\geq\delta|q|^{2}\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;|F(q)|\leq C\mathrm{e}^{\eta|q|^{2}}\;.

In particular, the first condition implies the coercivity of the process, i.e., there is a strong contracting property for the process
(\overline{q}_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}

starting from different starting points. Under these conditions, the
cut-off for the process
(\overline{q}_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}

starting from any fixed point is proven in
[
2
]

where the mixing time is of order
\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}
and the
window is of order
1
. The mixing time is computed explicitly in

[
2
, Lemma 2.1]
. The authors also provide in
[
2
, Corollary 2.9]

a necessary and sufficient condition under which profile thermalisation
holds.

Our main purpose in this work is to extend the existence of such cut-off
phenomenon to the underdamped Langevin dynamics. Several difficulties
appear when considering the underdamped Langevin dynamics. The first
one concerns the asymptotic stability of the associated deterministic
dynamics
(\epsilon=0)
which is a crucial element of the proof in
the overdamped case (
1.5
). In fact, to the best of our knowledge,
when
F
is not in gradient form (i.e.,
F=\nabla V
for some
V
),
no criterion was available in the literature to ensure such stability.
We can also show that the same condition appearing in the overdamped
case (
1.6
) is not enough to ensure stability
in the underdamped case. We are able however to provide in this work
a sharp criterion on
F
ensuring global stability. This criterion
is even shown to be necessary and sufficient when
F(q)=\mathbb{M}q

with
\mathbb{M}
being a normal matrix (i.e.
\mathbb{M}\mathbb{M}^{\dagger}=\mathbb{M}^{\dagger}\mathbb{M}
).

In the proof of the cut-off, the main difficulty arises from the lack
of the ellipticity of the underdamped Langevin dynamics, for which
the diffusion matrix is not even invertible. Generally, this hypoellipticity
causes multitude of fundamental difficulties in the study of the underdamped
Langevin dynamics, and the current work also has to confront this
difficulty.

In the proof of the cut-off for the overdamped Langevin dynamics,
the solution
\Sigma
to a matrix Lyapunov equation of the form

(1.7)

\textup{D}F(0)\Sigma+\Sigma\,\textup{D}F(0)^{\dagger}=\mathbb{I}_{d}

plays a significant role. However, in the underdamped case, the matrix
in the right-hand side which comes from the diffusion matrix of the
process is not invertible. Therefore, the standard result applied
does not ensure the positive definiteness of the solution. We shall
however prove such property by using the inherent dependency between
position and velocity coordinates. Moreover, in the overdamped Langevin
case, it is shown that the solution to the matrix Lyapunov equation (
1.7
)
is the long-time limit of the covariance matrix of a Gaussian process
used to approximate the overdamped process
(\overline{q}_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
.
As we shall see later, such proof is not applicable as it crucially
uses the ellipticity of the process. Finally, one also needs to provide
a control in
\epsilon
of the tails of the stationary distribution

\mu^{\epsilon}
for which there is not much literature when
F

is not conservative.

All in all, such difficulties are fundamental and require deeper understanding
of the underdamped Langevin dynamics which we provide in this work.
Thus, we strongly believe these new results can be used in further
work involving the underdamped Langevin dynamics.

2.2.
Asymptotic stability analysis of zero-noise dynamics

To state our first main result, we introduce the following assumption

Assumption 2.2
.

The vector

.

Theorem 2.5
.

Suppose that
F(q)=\mathbb{M}q
with
\mathbb{M}

being a normal matrix, i.e.
\mathbb{M}\mathbb{M}^{\dagger}=\mathbb{M}^{\dagger}\mathbb{M}
.
Then, the dynamics (
2.1
) is asymptotically stable
if and only if

satisfies Assumption
2.2
.

The proof of this theorem will be given in Section

. In
particular, this theorem is a consequence of Theorem

which completely characterizes the necessary and sufficient condition
on

under which the dynamics (

2.3.
Thermalization of underdamped Langevin dynamics

Let us assume that
F
satisfies Assumption
2.2
so that
the zero-noise dynamics exhibits the global exponential stability
towards
0
stated in our first result Theorem
2.3
.
We shall prove in Section
4.4
that the underdamped
process
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
admits in fact a unique stationary
distribution
\mu^{\epsilon}
.

Proposition 2.6
.

The process
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
admits a unique stationary
distribution
\mu^{\epsilon}
.

Our second result investigates the
thermalization behavior of the process
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}

in the regime
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
to its stationary measure
\mu_{\epsilon}
. Our concern is to describe
in more detail the underlying cut-off phenomenon involved in this convergence
when
\epsilon
goes to zero.

This convergence is studied under the total variation distance defined
below.

Definition 2.7

(Total variation distance)
.

For two probability measures
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2d}

(with the measure space consisting of Lebesgue measurable sets), we
define the total variation distance between
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{2}

as

\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(\pi_{1},\,\pi_{2})=\sup_{A}\left|\pi_{1}(A)-\pi_{2}(A)\right|\;,

where the supremum is taken over all Lebesgue measurable sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. When
the law of a random vector
Z_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
is
\pi_{i}

(
i=1,\,2)
, we can substitute
\pi_{i}
above with
Z_{i}
. For
instance,
\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(Z_{1},\,\pi_{2})
and
\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(Z_{1},\,Z_{2})

also represent
\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(\pi_{1},\,\pi_{2})
.

Stationary measure

An important difficulty arising in the study of
\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(X_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\mu_{\epsilon})

is that the stationary measure
\mu^{\epsilon}
cannot be given explicitly
when the force
F
does not write as a gradient function. However,
for the purpose of this work, we are able to compute an asymptotic
behavior of
\mu^{\epsilon}
when
\epsilon
goes to zero. In fact,
we show in Theorem
2.8
that the probability measure

\mu^{\epsilon}
behaves in the low-temperature regime (
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
)
as the law of a given centered Gaussian variable. Such approximation
is known for the overdamped model from
[
1
,
2
]
,
but as far as the author’s knowledge, this constitutes the first result
for the underdamped model.

To explain this result, we define two
2d\times 2d
matrices
\mathbb{A}

and
\mathbb{J}
by

(2.12)

\mathbb{A}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{O}_{d}&\mathbb{I}_{d}\\
-\textup{D}F(0)&-\gamma\mathbb{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix}\;\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\;\mathbb{J}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{O}_{d}&\mathbb{O}_{d}\\
\mathbb{O}_{d}&\mathbb{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix}\;,

where
\textup{D}F
represent the Jacobian of
F
at
(0,0)
, and where
\mathbb{O}_{d}

and
\mathbb{I}_{d}
denote the zero matrix of size
d\times d

and the
d
-dimensional identity matrix, respectively. Then, by Lemma

5.4
with
(\mathbb{U},\,\mathbb{W})=(\mathbb{A}^{\dagger},\,\mathbb{J})

(cf. the paragraph after the proof of Lemma
5.4
), there
exists a unique
2d\times 2d
matrix
\Sigma
satisfying

(2.13)

\mathbb{A}\Sigma+\Sigma\mathbb{A}^{\dagger}=-\mathbb{J}\;.

Moreover, by the same lemma the matrix
\Sigma
is symmetric positive
definite. We show then that we can approximate
\mu^{\epsilon}
by
the law of the
2d
-dimensional centered Gaussian variable with covariance
matrix
2\epsilon\Sigma
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.2
holds.
Then, the matrix
\Sigma
defined in (
2.13
) is such that

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(0,2\epsilon\Sigma),\,\mu^{\epsilon}\right)=0\;.

The proof of this Theorem is given in Section
8
. The appearance
of this theorem is similar to
[
2
, Proposition 3.6]

for the overdamped Langevin dynamics. However, in our case,
\Sigma

is solution to a matrix Lyapunov equation (
2.13
)
where
the right-hand side matrix
\mathbb{J}
is not invertible
and therefore

[
2
, Lemma C.4]
which was crucially used in the
proof of
[
2
, Proposition 3.6]
is not applicable.

Remark 2.9

(Gradient model)
.

It was shown in
[
26
, Proposition 2.1]
that the
Gibbs measure

\mu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)=\mu_{\epsilon}(x)\mathrm{d}x:=\frac{1}{Z_{\epsilon}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\gamma}{\epsilon}V(x)\right\}\mathrm{d}x\;,

where
V(x)=\frac{1}{2}|p|^{2}+U(q)
and
Z_{\epsilon}
is the normalizing
constant, is a stationary probability distribution if and only if
F=\nabla U
for
some
U\in\mathcal{C}^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\mathbb{R})
. In this
case, by the global asymptotic stability (Theorem
2.3
)
and Remark
2.4
-(4), the potential function
U
achieves
its global minimum at
0
. Let us assume without loss of generality
that
U(0)=0
(since adding a constant to
U
does not affect the
dynamical system). Then, by the Laplace asymptotics, we can derive

Z_{\epsilon}\simeq\frac{(2\pi\epsilon/\gamma)^{d}}{\sqrt{\det\mathbb{H}}}\;,

where
\mathbb{H}=\nabla^{2}U(0)
denotes the Hessian of
U
at

0
. Thus, by taking the second order Taylor approximation of
U

at
0
(note that
U(0)=0
and
\nabla U(0)=0
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mu_{\epsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{Z_{\epsilon}}\textup{e}^{-\frac{\gamma}{\epsilon}V(x)}

\displaystyle\simeq\;\frac{\sqrt{\det\mathbb{H}}}{(2\pi\epsilon/\gamma)^{d}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\gamma}{2\epsilon}\left(|p|^{2}+\left\langle q,\,\mathbb{H}q\right\rangle\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\;\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi\epsilon)^{2d}\det\Sigma}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\left(\left\langle x,\,\widehat{\Sigma}^{-1}x\right\rangle\right)\right\}\;,

where
\widehat{\Sigma}
is the
2d\times 2d
matrix defined by

(2.14)

\widehat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{\gamma}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{H}^{-1}&\mathbb{O}_{d}\\
\mathbb{O}_{d}&\mathbb{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix}\;.

Therefore, we can conclude that
\mu_{\epsilon}
is close to the
centered Gaussian distribution with covariance matrix
\epsilon\widehat{\Sigma}

when
\epsilon
is close to zero. By a direct computation (note that
in this case
\mathrm{D}F(0)=\mathbb{H})
, we can check that
\widehat{\Sigma}/2

is indeed the solution to the equation (
2.13
), and hence

\widehat{\Sigma}=2\Sigma
.

Cut-off phenomenon

To quantify the total variation distance
\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(X_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\mu_{\epsilon})

in a precise manner when
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, we first have to state the technical lemma below.
We remark that this lemma is the underdamped version of
[
2
, Lemma 2.1]
.
Although the proof is very similar to to that of
[
2
, Lemma 2.1]

based on
[
2
, Lemmata B.1 and B.2]
and the Hartman-Grobman
theorem, we will provide its proof in Section
7.1

in order to explain the nature of the different constants appearing
in the statement of the lemma in the underdamped setting.

Lemma 2.10
.

Under Assumption
2.2
, for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\setminus\{0\}
,
there exist constants

\begin{cases}\eta=\eta(x)>0,\;\nu=\nu(x)\geq 0,\;\tau=\tau(x)\geq 0\;,\\
m=m(x)\in\{1,\ldots,2d\}\;,\\
\theta_{1}=\theta_{1}(x),\ldots,\theta_{m}=\theta_{m}(x)\in[0,2\pi)\;,\end{cases}

and linearly independent complex vectors
v_{1}=v_{1}(x),\ldots,v_{m}=v_{m}(x)\in\mathbb{C}^{2d}

such that

(2.15)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left|\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\eta t}}{t^{\nu}}X_{t+\tau}(x)-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathrm{e}^{i\theta_{k}t}v_{k}\right|=0\;.

The constants appearing in the previous lemma are determined by the
matrix
\textup{D}F(0)
and the starting location
x
in a complex
manner. Roughly speaking,
\eta
is the smallest positive real part
of the eigenvalues of the matrix
-\mathbb{A}
defined in (
2.12
)
and
\nu
is the size of Jordan block associated with the eigenvalue
with smallest positive real part minus
1
. These constants depend
on
x
since some eigenvalues must not be considered when
x
is
of special direction. For more detail, we refer to the proof in Section

7.1
and Remark
7.1
.

We are now ready to provide our result on the thermalization of the
underdamped process
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
(
1.2
).
We shall from now on suppose the following control on the jacobian

\textup{D}F
.

Assumption 2.11
.

There exist constants

C,\,\rho>0
such that

|\textup{D}F(q)|\leq C\mathrm{e}^{\rho|q|^{2}}\;\;\;\text{for all }q\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\;.

The second main result is the following theorem.

Theorem 2.12
.

Let Assumptions
2.2
and
2.11

hold. For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\setminus\{0\}
, recall the constants

\eta(x),\,\nu(x)
and
\tau(x)
from Lemma
2.10
,
and define the mixing time as

(2.16)

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{2\eta(x)}\log\left(\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\right)+\frac{\nu(x)}{\eta(x)}\log\log\left(\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\right)+\tau(x)\;.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{w\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)+w}^{\epsilon}(x),\,\mu_{\epsilon}\right)=0\;\text{ and}

(2.17)

\displaystyle\lim_{w\rightarrow\infty}\liminf_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)-w}^{\epsilon}(x),\,\mu_{\epsilon}\right)=1\;.

The previous theorem asserts that there is a cut-off phenomenon at
the mixing time
t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)
with window of size

O(1)
. Under a condition detailed below, we have a stronger cut-off
notion called profile cut-off. We say that the underdamped Langevin
dynamics starting at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\setminus\{0\}
exhibits
a profile cut-off at the mixing time
t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)

and window
O(1)
with the cut-off function
\Lambda:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\,1]

which is a decreasing function such that
\Lambda(-\infty)=1
and

\Lambda(+\infty)=0
if

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)+w}^{\epsilon}(x),\,\mu_{\epsilon}\right)=\Lambda(w)

for all
w\in\mathbb{R}
. Of course, this profile cut-off implies
the cut-off (
2.17
).

Theorem 2.13
.

Let Assumptions
2.2
and
2.11

hold and let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\setminus\{0\}
. Recall the mixing
time
t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)
from (
2.16
)
and constants
\theta_{k}(x)
’s and vectors
v_{k}(x)
’s from Lemma

2.10
. Then, the underdamped Langevin dynamics starting
at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
exhibits a profile cut-off at the mixing
time
t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)
and window
O(1)
if and only
if the limit

(2.18)

r:=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left|\Sigma^{-1/2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathrm{e}^{i\theta_{k}t}v_{k}\right|

exists. Moreover, in this case
r>0
, the cut-off function is given by

\Lambda(w)=2\int_{0}^{\sqrt{2}(1/2\eta)^{\nu}e^{-w\eta}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt\;.

We note that the limit (
2.18
) exists if all the eigenvalues
of matrix
-\mathbb{A}
defined (
2.12
) with smallest real part
are real. In particular, if all the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{A}
are
real, then the limit (
2.18
) exists.

The proof of Theorems
2.12
and
2.13

will be given in Section
7
based on the analyses carried
out in Sections
4
-
6
. In particular, these theorems
are direct consequences of Theorem
7.2
.

Linear Models

In this section, we analyze the case when the force

is linear,
i.e.
F(q)=\mathbb{M}q
. We shall see in this section that Assumption

2.2
is indeed a sharp, i.e., necessary and sufficient
condition ensuring asymptotic stability for a large class of

Notation
.

The following notation will be used
in the current section.

•

Writing a complex number as
a+ib
implicitly assumes that
a,\,b\in\mathbb{R}
.
Similarly, writing a complex vector as
u+iv
implicitly assumes
that
u
and
v
are real vectors.

•

For a square matrix
\mathbb{M}
, we denote by

the set of (possibly complex) eigenvalues of
\mathbb{M}
.

•

For a square matrix
\mathbb{M}
, we denote by
\mathbb{\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}}

and
\mathbb{\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}}
the symmetric, and skew-symmetric

\mathbb{M}

(3.1)

\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{M}+\mathbb{M}^{\dagger})\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{M}-\mathbb{M}^{\dagger})

3.1.
Elementary lemmata

We start with some elementary lemmata from linear algebra.

Lemma 3.1
.

For each
d\times d
matrix
\mathbb{M}
and
\gamma>0

(which is the constant appearing in (
1.2
)), define

(3.2)

\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{M}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{O}_{d}&-\mathbb{I}_{d}\\
\mathbb{M}&\gamma\mathbb{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix}\;.

Then, all the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{M}}
have positive
real part if and only if

\mathrm{Sp}(\mathbb{M})\subset\{a+ib:a>0\text{ and }\gamma^{2}a>b^{2}\}\;.

Remark 3.2
.

This ensures that the spectrum of
\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{M}}
shall be included in the following red parabolic region of the complex eigenspace.

\mathrm{Re}
\mathrm{Im}

Proof.

We note that, for
d\times d
matrices
\mathbb{B}_{11},\,\mathbb{B}_{12},\,\mathbb{B}_{21}
and

\mathbb{B}_{22}
such that
\mathbb{B}_{11}\mathbb{B}_{21}=\mathbb{B}_{21}\mathbb{B}_{11}
,
we have the following determinant formula for the block matrix:

\det\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{B}_{11}&\mathbb{B}_{12}\\
\mathbb{B}_{21}&\mathbb{B}_{22}\end{pmatrix}=\det\left(\mathbb{B}_{11}\mathbb{B}_{22}-\mathbb{B}_{12}\mathbb{B}_{21}\right)\;.

Using this formula, we can deduce that, for any
t\in\mathbb{R},

\det\left(\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{M}}-t\mathbb{I}_{2d}\right)=\det\left(\mathbb{M}-t(\gamma-t)\mathbb{I}_{d}\right)\;.

From this expression, we can deduce that
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
is
an eigenvalue of
\mathbb{\mathbb{T}_{M}}
if and only if
h(\lambda)=-\lambda^{2}+\gamma\lambda

is an eigenvalue of
\mathbb{M}
. One can check
h:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

is a bijection between
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\gamma>\text{Re}(z)>0\}

and
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\textrm{Im}(z)^{2}<\gamma^{2}\text{{Re}}(z)\}
.
Since sum of two roots of
h(\lambda)=c
is
\gamma
, all the eigenvalues
of
\mathbf{\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{M}}}
have positive real part if
and only if all of the real parts of eigenvalues of
\mathbf{\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{M}}}
are
positive and less then
\gamma
.
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

For a
d\times d
matrix
\mathbb{M}
, define
the symmetric matrix
\mathbb{K}_{\mathbb{M}}
by

(3.3)

\mathbb{K}_{\mathbb{M}}=\gamma^{2}\mathbb{M}^{s}+(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}\mathbb{M}^{s}-\mathbb{M}^{s}\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})\;.

Denote by
w=u+iv
an unit eigenvector (i.e.,
|u|^{2}+|v|^{2}=1
)
of
\mathbb{M}
associated with the eigenvalue

. Then,

(1)

it holds that

\left\langle\mathbb{K}_{\mathbb{M}}w,\,w\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}=\langle\mathbb{K}_{\mathbb{M}}u,u\rangle+\langle\mathbb{K}_{\mathbb{M}}v,v\rangle=\gamma^{2}a-b^{2}\;,

where
\langle\,,\,\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}
is the Hermitian scalar
product in
\mathbb{C}^{d}
.

(2)

It holds that

\langle(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}\mathbb{M}^{s}-\mathbb{M}^{s}\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})w,\,w\rangle_{\mathbb{C}}=\langle(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}\mathbb{M}^{s}-\mathbb{M}^{s}\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})u,\,u\rangle+\langle(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}\mathbb{M}^{s}-\mathbb{M}^{s}\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})v,\,v\rangle\geq 0\;.

By looking at the real and imaginary parts of the equation
\mathbb{M}w=zw

with
\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}+\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}
,

, and

, respectively, we get

(3.4)

\displaystyle(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}+\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})u=au-bv\;,

(3.5)

\displaystyle(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}+\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})v=av+bu\;.

Since
\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}
is skew-symmetric,
[u\cdot
(
3.4
)

(

)

and

3.4
)

3.5
)

\displaystyle\langle\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}u,\,u\rangle+\langle\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}v,\,v\rangle=a\;\;\;\text{and\;\;\;}2\langle\mathbb{\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}}u,\,v\rangle=-b\;,

where we used the fact that
|u|^{2}+|v|^{2}=1
. Next,
[(\mathbb{\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}}
\times
(
3.4
)
)\cdot u+(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}\times
(
3.5
)
)\cdot v]

yields that

\displaystyle\langle\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}+\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})u,\,u\rangle+\langle\mathbb{\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}}(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}+\mathbb{\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}})v,\,v\rangle

\displaystyle=-b\langle\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}v,\,u\rangle+b\langle\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}u,\,v\rangle=-b^{2}\;,

where the last equality follows from the second identity of (
3.6
)
and the skew-symmetry of
\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}
. Combining this
with the first identity of (

), we get

\gamma^{2}a-b^{2}=\gamma^{2}(\langle\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}u,\,u\rangle+\langle\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}v,\,v\rangle)+\langle\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}+\mathbb{\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}})u,\,u\rangle+\langle\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}+\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})v,\,v\rangle\;.

Recalling the definition of
\mathbb{K}_{\mathbb{M}}
completes the
proof of part (1).

Next we turn to part (2). By the symmetry of
\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}

and skew-symmetry of
\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}
, it suffices to prove
that

(3.7)

\langle\mathbb{\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}}u,\,u\rangle+\langle\mathbb{\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}}\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}v,\,v\rangle\leq 0\;.

Observe that

\displaystyle=a\langle\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}u,\,u\rangle+a\langle\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}v,\,v\rangle\;.

By the first identity of (

3.2.
Asymptotic stability of linear models

In this subsection, we suppose that the force

\mathbb{M}
. Here, we completely
characterize the asymptotic stability of the linear dynamical system
(

Theorem 3.4

(Asymptotic stability: linear case)
.

For the linear model with a force
F
given
by (
3.9
), the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is asymptotically
stable if and only if one of the following conditions holds:

(1)

\mathrm{Sp}(\mathbb{M})\subset\{a+ib:a>0\text{ and }\gamma^{2}a>b^{2}\}
.

(2)

\mathbb{K}_{\mathbb{M}}
is positive definite on the
subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{d}
spanned by eigenvectors of
\mathbb{M}
.

In particular, if
\mathbb{K}_{\mathbb{M}}
is positive definite,
then the process

is asymptotically stable.

Proof.

When the force
F
is given by (
3.9
), the ODE (
2.1
)
describing the dynamics
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
can be written as

(3.10)

\frac{\mathrm{d}X_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{M}}X_{t}\;,

where
\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{M}}
is the matrix given in (
3.2
).
It is well-known that the dynamical
system of the form (
3.10

only if
\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{M}}
admits only eigenvalues with positive
real part. Thus, by Lemma

, the dynamics
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}

is asymptotically stable if and only if

\mathrm{Sp}(\mathbb{M})\subset\{a+ib:a>0\text{ and }\gamma^{2}a>b^{2}\}\;.

By Lemma

-(1), this is equivalent to the positive
definiteness of
\mathbb{K}_{\mathbb{M}}
on the subspace of
\mathbb{C}^{d}

spanned by eigenvectors of
\mathbb{M}
and the proof is completed.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

It is well-known that in the linear model (
3.10
), the process

(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is even exponentially stable in the sense that

for some constants

We shall see below that the asymptotic stability of (

can be obtained by looking only at the eigenvalues of
\gamma^{2}\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}+(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})^{2}

instead of
\mathbb{K}_{\mathbb{M}}
.

Proposition 3.6
.

For the linear model with force
F
given by
(
3.9
), the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is asymptotically
stable if the
d\times d
symmetric matrix
\gamma^{2}\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}+(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})^{2}

is positive definite.

Proof.

Suppose that
\gamma^{2}\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}+(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})^{2}

is positive definite. Then, by Lemma
3.3
-(2), the matrix

\mathbb{K}_{\mathbb{M}}
is positive definite on the subspace of

\mathbb{C}^{d}
spanned by eigenvectors of
\mathbb{M}
. Hence,
the proof is completed by Theorem
3.4
.
∎

We show in the next proposition that the condition obtained in the
proposition above is actually a necessary and sufficient condition
when
\mathbb{M}
is a normal matrix, i.e., satisfies
\mathbb{M}\mathbb{M}^{\dagger}=\mathbb{M}^{\dagger}\mathbb{M}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Assume that the force
F
is given by (
3.9
)
where
\mathbb{M}
is a normal matrix. Then, the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}

is asymptotically stable if and only if
\gamma^{2}\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}+(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})^{2}

is positive definite.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.6
, it suffices to prove that when
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}

is asymptotically stable, the matrix
\gamma^{2}\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}+(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})^{2}

is positive definite. Suppose that
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is asymptotically
stable. Since a normal matrix is unitarily similar to a diagonal matrix,
we can write
\mathbb{M}=\mathbb{U}\mathbb{D}\mathbb{U}^{*}
where

\mathbb{U}
is a unitary matrix and

\mathbb{D}=\textup{diag}\left(a_{1}+ib_{1},\,\,\dots,\,a_{d}+ib_{d}\right)

is a diagonal matrix. Then, we can write

(3.11)

\gamma^{2}\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}+(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})^{2}=\mathbb{U}\widetilde{\mathbb{D}}\mathbb{U^{*}}\;,

where the diagonal matrix
\widetilde{\mathbb{D}}
is given by

\widetilde{\mathbb{D}}=\textup{diag}\left(\gamma^{2}a_{1}-b_{1}^{2},\,\,\dots,\,\gamma^{2}a_{d}-b_{d}^{2}\right)\;.

Hence, since we assumed that
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is asymptotically stable,
by Theorem
3.4
, the matrix
\widetilde{\mathbb{D}}

is positive definite matrix. By (
3.11
), the proof is
completed.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

The previous proposition also implies that, for the linear model with
force
F
given by (
3.9
) with a normal matrix
\mathbb{M}
,
the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is asymptotically stable if and only
if the matrix
\mathbb{K}_{\mathbb{M}}
is positive definite, since
we have
\mathbb{\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}}=\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}

when
\mathbb{M}
is normal.

We are now able to prove Theorem
2.5
. Note that
we shall prove Theorem
2.3
in the next section and
we shall assume below that Theorem
2.3
holds.

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

Assume that
F(q)=\mathbb{M}q
with a normal matrix
\mathbb{M}
,
and that the dynamics (
2.1
) is asymptotically stable. Define

U:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}

as

U(q)=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}q,\,q\right\rangle\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\ell(q)=\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}q\;.

Then, since
\nabla U(q)=\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}q
, we have
F(q)=\nabla U(q)+\ell(q)
.
Moreover, by Proposition
3.7
, the matrix
\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}+\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})^{2}

is positive definite. Hence, we can find
\beta\in(0,\,\gamma)
which
is close enough to
\gamma
so that

\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}+\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})^{2}\geq\alpha\mathbb{I}_{d}

for some
\alpha>0
. Then, we have

F(q)\cdot q-\frac{|\ell(q)|^{2}}{\beta^{2}}=\left\langle\mathbb{M}q,\,q\right\rangle-\frac{|\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}}q|^{2}}{\beta^{2}}=\left\langle\left(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{s}}+\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}(\mathbb{M}^{\textup{a}})^{2}\right)q,\,q\right\rangle\geq\alpha|q|^{2}\;.

Since in this case
U
is at most of quadratic growth in the sense
of (
2.9
) one can easily deduce by Remark
2.4
-(5)
that Assumption
2.2
is satisfied. Since the other direction
concerning the implication of global asymptotic stability from Assumption

2.2
, is the content of Theorem
2.3
we
can conclude this proof.
∎

4.
Lyapunov Function

In the remainder of this article, we shall always assume that Assumption

2.2
holds (while we do not assume Assumption
2.11

until further notice). In this section, we construct a Lyapunov function
which decays along the trajectory of
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. In particular,
we are able to prove the global exponential stability of
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}

stated in Theorem
2.3
and also provide moment estimates
for the underdamped Langevin dynamics
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}

in Sections
4.2
and
4.3
.

4.1.
Lyapunov function

In this section, we provide a Lyaponov function
H:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

with respect to the dynamical system (
2.1
). In the underdamped
model, finding such a function is not a trivial task since there is
no global contraction in general even for the energy function

(4.1)

\widehat{H}(x)=\frac{1}{2}|p|^{2}+U(q)

as the time-derivative

\frac{\textrm{d}\widehat{H}}{\mathrm{d}t}(X_{t})=-\gamma|p_{t}|^{2}-\left\langle p_{t},\,\ell(q_{t})\right\rangle

has no reason to be negative for all
(q_{t},p_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
.
Our idea is to cleverly modify the function
\widehat{H}
so as to
obtain a Lyapunov function.

Definition 4.1

(Lyapunov function
H
)
.

Recall constants
\alpha,\,\beta>0
from Assumption

2.2
. Let us take a constant
\lambda>0
small enough
so that these three conditions

(4.2)

\frac{\lambda(\gamma-\lambda)}{2}\leq\alpha\;,\qquad\frac{2\lambda}{\gamma-\lambda}\leq\alpha\;,\;\;\;\;\text{and}\qquad\beta^{2}\leq\gamma(\gamma-\lambda)

are simultaneously satisfied, where the last condition can be satisfied
since
\beta\in(0,\,\gamma)
. We define the Lyapunov function
H:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

as follows

(4.3)

H(x):=\frac{1}{2}|p|^{2}+\frac{\gamma-\lambda}{2}\left\langle q,\,p\right\rangle+\frac{(\gamma-\lambda)^{2}}{4}|q|^{2}+U(q)\;.

Notice that the function
H
is obtained by modifying the quadratic
part of
\widehat{H}
defined in (
4.1
).

From now on the constant
\lambda
shall always refer to the constant
defined in (
4.2
). The next result follows immediately
from the definition of
H
.

Lemma 4.2
.

There exist constants
\kappa_{0}>0
such that

\frac{1}{\kappa_{0}}|x|^{2}\leq H(x)\leq\kappa_{0}|x|^{2}+U(q)\;\;\;\text{for all }x=(q,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\;.

In addition, if
U
is at most of quadratic growth in the sense of
(
2.9
), we can remove
U(q)
term at the right-hand side
and thus the function
H
is comparable to the quadratic function.

Proof.

Since we assumed that
U\geq 0
, it suffices to observe that

\frac{1}{6}|p|^{2}+\frac{(\gamma-\lambda)^{2}}{16}|q|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}|p|^{2}+\frac{\gamma-\lambda}{2}\left\langle q,\,p\right\rangle+\frac{(\gamma-\lambda)^{2}}{4}|q|^{2}\leq\frac{3}{4}|p|^{2}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}|q|^{2}\;.

∎

Next we shall show that
H
is a Lyapunov function.

Lemma 4.3
.

It holds that

(4.4)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}H(X_{t})\leq-\lambda H(X_{t})

for all
t\geq 0
, where
\lambda
is the constant given in Definition

4.1
.

Proof.

By a direct computation, we can deduce that
-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}H(X_{t})-\lambda H(X_{t})

is equal to

(4.5)

\left\langle p_{t},\,\ell(q_{t})\right\rangle+\frac{\gamma}{2}|p_{t}|^{2}+\frac{\gamma-\lambda}{2}\left\langle F(q_{t}),\,q_{t}\right\rangle-\lambda\frac{(\gamma-\lambda)^{2}}{4}|q|^{2}-\lambda U(q)\;.

By Assumption
2.2
and an elementary inequality

\left\langle p_{t},\,\ell(q_{t})\right\rangle+\frac{\gamma}{2}|p_{t}|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\gamma}|\ell(q_{t})|^{2}\geq 0\;,

we can notice that (
4.5
) is bounded from below by

\frac{\gamma-\lambda}{2}\left[\left(\alpha-\frac{\lambda(\gamma-\lambda)}{2}\right)|q_{t}|^{2}+\left(\alpha-\frac{2\lambda}{\gamma-\lambda}\right)U(q_{t})+\left(\frac{1}{\beta^{2}}-\frac{1}{\gamma(\gamma-\lambda)}\right)|\ell(q_{t})|^{2}\right]

which is non-negative by (
4.2
). This completes the
proof.
∎

4.2.
Global exponential stability of zero-noise dynamics

The proof of the global exponential stability of the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}

now follows immediately from the construction of the above Lyapunov
function
H
.

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

By Lemma
4.3
, we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}H(X_{t}))}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}H(X_{t})}{\mathrm{d}t}+\lambda H(X_{t})\right)\leq 0

for all
t\geq 0
. Thus, we get

(4.6)

\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}H(X_{t}(x))\leq H(x)\;.

Hence, the proof is completed by recalling Lemma
4.2
.
∎

We next discuss two consequences of Theorem
2.3
which
will be used later. Define
G:\mathbb{R}^{2d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2d}
as

(4.7)

G(x)=(-p,\,F(q)+\gamma p)\;,

so that we can rewrite the ODE (
2.1
) satisfied by
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}

as follows:

(4.8)

\frac{\textup{d}X_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-G(X_{t})\;.

The following is the first direct consequence of Theorem
2.3

Corollary 4.4
.

All the complex eigenvalues of the matrix

\textup{D}F(0)
have positive real part. In particular,
\textup{D}F(0)

is invertible.

Proof.

It is well-known that local asymptotic
stability around the equilibrium point
(0,0)
of
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}

implies that all the complex eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix
\textup{D}G(0,\,0)

have positive real part, where
G
is the function defined in (
4.7
).
Since
\textup{D}G(0,\,0)=\mathbb{T}_{\textup{D}F(0)}
where the
matrix
\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{M}}
is the one defined in (
3.2
),
the proof follows from Lemma
3.1
.
∎

Notation 4.5
.

From now on,
\kappa
and
\kappa_{0}

always refer to the constants appearing in Theorem
2.3

and Lemma
4.2
, respectively.

For
r>0
, let us define
\mathcal{D}_{r},\,\mathcal{H}_{r}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}

as follows

(4.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{r}

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}:|x|\leq r\}\;,

(4.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{r}

\displaystyle=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}:|x|^{2}\leq\kappa\left(r^{2}+\max_{y\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}U(y)\right)\right\}\;.

Note that both
\mathcal{D}_{r}
and
\mathcal{H}_{r}
are
2d
-dimensional
closed balls. The following remark explains why we define
\mathcal{D}_{r}

and
\mathcal{H}_{r}
.

Remark 4.6
.

By Theorem
2.3
, if
x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
,
then we have
X_{t}(x)\in\mathcal{H}_{r}
for all
t\geq 0
. In other
words, the trajectory
(X_{t}(x))_{t\geq 0}
is contained in the closed
ball
\mathcal{H}_{r}
.

Notation 4.7
.

From this moment on, different
appearances of a constant may stand for different constants unless
otherwise mentioned as in Notation
4.5
. For instance,
the constant
C(r)
represents a constant depending on the parameter

r
, but different appearances of
C(r)
may refer to different
quantities. In addition, since we are interested in the regime
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
,
all the statements below implicitly assume that
\epsilon
is sufficiently
small compared to the other parameters.

Let us now state the following Corollary which is a consequence of
Theorem
2.3
.

Corollary 4.8
.

For all
r>0
, there exists a constant
C(r)>0

such that

\left|X_{t}(u)-X_{t}(u^{\prime})\right|\leq e^{C(r)t}|u-u^{\prime}|

for all
u,\,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
and for all
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

By (
4.8
), we have

(4.11)

\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\left|X_{t}(u)-X_{t}(u^{\prime})\right|^{2}=2\left\langle X_{t}(u)-X_{t}(u^{\prime}),\,G(X_{t}(u^{\prime}))-G(X_{t}(u))\right\rangle\;.

By Remark
4.6
and the mean-value theorem, we have that

\left|G(X_{t}(u))-G(X_{t}(u^{\prime}))\right|\leq C(r)\left|X_{t}(u)-X_{t}(u^{\prime})\right|

for all
u,\,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
where
C(r)=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{H}_{r}}\left\|\textup{D}G(x)\right\|
.
Hence, by (
4.11
) and the Cauchy Schwarz inequality, we
get

\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\left|X_{t}(u)-X_{t}(u^{\prime})\right|^{2}\leq C(r)\left|X_{t}(u)-X_{t}(u^{\prime})\right|^{2}

for all
u,\,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
which concludes the proof.
∎

4.3.
Moment estimates

Another consequence of the existence of the Lyapunov function
H

is the control of the moments for the process
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
.

Proposition 4.9
.

For all
n\geq 0
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, and

t\geq 0
, it holds that

(4.12)

\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)|^{2n}\right]\leq\kappa_{0}^{n}\omega_{n}\left(H(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}+\frac{d\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)^{n},

where
\kappa_{0}
is the constant appearing in Lemma
4.2

(cf. Notation
4.5
), and the sequence
(\omega_{n})_{n\geq 0}

is defined by
\omega_{0}=\omega_{1}=1
and

\omega_{k}=\prod_{j=2}^{k}(d+2(j-1))\;\;\;\text{for }k\geq 2\;.

First we need to analyze the following recurrence relation which allows
us to introduce the sequence
(\omega_{k})_{k=0}^{\infty}
appearing
in Proposition
4.9
.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
(a_{n}(\cdot))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}
be a sequence
of continuous functions on
[0,\,\infty)
such that
a_{0}\equiv 1

and

(4.13)

a_{n}^{\prime}(t)\leq-n\lambda a_{n}(t)+n\epsilon(d+2(n-1))a_{n-1}(t)

for all
n\geq 1
and
t\geq 0
. Then, for the sequence
(\omega_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}

defined in Proposition
4.9
, we have that

(4.14)

a_{n}(t)\leq\omega_{n}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{a_{k}(0)\mathrm{e}^{-k\lambda t}}{\omega_{k}}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)^{n-k}+d\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)^{n}\right]

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
t\geq 0
.

Proof.

We prove this lemma by induction on
n
. Note that (
4.14
)
is immediate for
n=0
. Now let us take
n=1
in (
4.13
)
so that we obtain
a_{1}^{\prime}(t)\leq-\lambda a_{1}(t)+\epsilon d
. By
considering the differential of
\textup{e}^{\lambda t}a_{1}(t)
,
we can readily get
a_{1}(t)\leq\textup{e}^{-\lambda t}a_{1}(0)+\frac{\epsilon d}{\lambda}

and thus we obtain (
4.14
) for
n=1
.

Now assume that
n\geq 2
and that
(
4.14
)
is satisfied
at
n-1
. By considering the differential of
\textup{e}^{n\lambda t}a_{n}(t)

and applying (
4.13
), we get

a_{n}(t)\leq\mathrm{e}^{-n\lambda t}\left[a_{n}(0)+n\epsilon(d+2(n-1))\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{n\lambda s}a_{n-1}(s)\mathrm{d}s\right]\;.

By the induction hypothesis and the definition of
\omega_{n}
, we
can bound the right-hand side from above by

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-n\lambda t}a_{n}(0)+\mathrm{e}^{-n\lambda t}n\epsilon\omega_{n}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k}\frac{a_{k}(0)}{\omega_{k}}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)^{n-1-k}\frac{\textup{e}^{(n-k)\lambda t}-1}{(n-k)\lambda}+d\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)^{n-1}\frac{\textup{e}^{n\lambda t}-1}{n\lambda}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\omega_{n}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{a_{k}(0)\mathrm{e}^{-k\lambda t}}{\omega_{k}}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)^{n-k}+d\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)^{n}\right]

which concludes the proof.
∎

We are now able to prove Proposition
4.9
.

Proof of Proposition
4.9
.

By Ito’s formula and a similar computation to Lemma
4.3
,
we have that, for all
t\geq 0
,

(4.15)

\mathrm{d}H(X_{t}^{\epsilon})\leq-\big[\lambda H(X_{t}^{\epsilon})-\epsilon d\big]dt+\sqrt{2\epsilon}\left\langle p_{t}^{\epsilon}+\frac{\gamma-\lambda}{2}q_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\right\rangle\;.

Therefore, again by Ito’s formula, for
n\geq 1
, we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}H(X_{t}^{\epsilon})^{n}=\;

\displaystyle nH(X_{t}^{\epsilon})^{n-1}\mathrm{d}H(X_{t}^{\epsilon})+\frac{1}{2}\times n(n-1)H(X_{t}^{\epsilon})^{n-2}\times 2\epsilon\left|p_{t}^{\epsilon}+\frac{\gamma-\lambda}{2}q_{t}^{\epsilon}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t

(4.16)

\displaystyle\leq\;

\displaystyle H(X_{t}^{\epsilon})^{n-1}\left[\left(-n\lambda H(X_{t}^{\epsilon})+n\epsilon(d+2(n-1))\right)\textup{d}t+n\sqrt{2\epsilon}\left\langle p_{t}^{\epsilon}+\frac{\gamma-\lambda}{2}q_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\mathrm{d}B_{t}\right\rangle\right]\;,

where the last line used (
4.15
) and the elementary bound

\frac{1}{2}\left|p+\frac{\gamma-\lambda}{2}q\right|^{2}\leq H(x)\;.

Now let us fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and let

a_{n}(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[H(X_{t}^{\epsilon}(x))^{n}\right]\;\;\;\text{for }n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\text{ and }t\geq 0\;.

Then, by the bound (
4.16
), the sequence
(a_{n}(\cdot))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}

satisfies the relation (
4.13
), and therefore by Lemma
4.10
,
we get (since
a_{k}(0)=H(x)^{k}
)

\mathbb{E}\left[H(X_{t}^{\epsilon}(x))^{n}\right]\leq\omega_{n}\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{H(x)^{k}\mathrm{e}^{-k\lambda t}}{\omega_{k}}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)^{n-k}+d\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)^{n}\right]\;.

Since
\omega_{k}\geq 1
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, we have a rough
bound

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{H(x)^{k}\mathrm{e}^{-k\lambda t}}{\omega_{k}}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)^{n-k}+d\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)^{n}\leq\left(H(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}+\frac{d\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)^{n}

which is sufficient to conclude the proof along with Lemma
4.2
.
∎

The following exponential moment estimate is a direct consequence
of Proposition
4.9
and will be used later.

Corollary 4.11
.

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,
t\geq 0
, and

a\in\left(0,\,\frac{1}{2(d+2)\kappa_{0}\left(H(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}+\frac{d\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)}\right)
,
we have that

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{exp}\left(a|X_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)|^{2}\right)\right]<2\;.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.9
, we get

(4.17)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{a^{n}|X_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)|^{2n}}{n!}\right]

\displaystyle\leq a^{n}\frac{\kappa_{0}^{n}\omega_{n}}{n!}\left(H(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}+\frac{d\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)^{n}\;.

Note that for
n\geq 2
we have the bound

(4.18)

\omega_{n}=\prod_{j=2}^{n}(d+2(j-1))\leq\prod_{j=2}^{n}(d+2)(j-1)<(d+2)^{n}n!

which also trivially holds for
n=0,\,1
. Combining (
4.17
)
and (
4.18
), we can deduce that, for
0<a<\frac{1}{2(d+2)\kappa_{0}\left(H(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}+\frac{d\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{exp}\left(a|X_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)|^{2}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a^{n}\frac{\kappa_{0}^{n}\omega_{n}}{n!}\left(H(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}+\frac{d\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left\{a\kappa_{0}(d+2)\left(H(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}+\frac{d\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)\right\}^{n}<\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}=2\;.

∎

4.4.
Uniqueness of stationary distribution

Let us conclude this section by showing that indeed the underdamped
process
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
admits a unique
stationary distribution
\mu_{\epsilon}
.
In order to do that let us first show the irreducibility of
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
.

Lemma 4.12
.

The process
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
is irreducible.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that for all
T>0
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and any non-empty open set
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,

\mathbb{P}(X^{\epsilon}_{T}(x)\in\mathcal{A})>0\;.

The proof is similar to
[
24
, Lemma 3.4]
and relies on classical tools from control theory. Let us fix
T>0
. It is enough to show the above property for any arbitrary
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and any open ball
\mathcal{A}=\mathrm{B}(y,\delta)
for arbitrary
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and
\delta>0
.

First we shall define a path function
\phi\in\mathcal{C}^{2}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\left(\phi(0),\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\mathrm{d}t}(0)\right)=x\;,\qquad\left(\phi(T),\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\mathrm{d}t}(T)\right)=y\;,

which existence can be guaranteed by
[
14
, Lemma 4.1]
for instance. Now define a function
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{d})
by
f(0)=0
and for all
t\in[0,T]
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\phi}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}(t)+\gamma\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)+F(\phi(t))=\sqrt{2\epsilon}\frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}t}(t)\;.

Let
\Phi(t)=(\phi(t),\mathrm{d}\phi(t)/\mathrm{d}t)
. On the event
\{\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|B_{t}-f(t)|\leq\alpha\}
, by Gronwall’s lemma, there exists a constant
C>0
increasing with
\alpha
such that

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|X^{\epsilon}_{t}-\Phi(t)|\leq\sqrt{2\epsilon}\alpha\mathrm{e}^{CT}.

As a result, taking
\alpha
small enough with respect to
\delta,T
ensures that
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|X^{\epsilon}_{t}-\Phi(t)|\leq\frac{\delta}{2}
, thus
X^{\epsilon}_{T}\in\mathcal{A}
. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}(X^{\epsilon}_{T}(x)\in\mathcal{A})\geq\mathbb{P}\big(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}|B_{t}-f(t)|\leq\alpha\big)>0

by the support theorem for the Brownian motions see
[
31
, Theorem 4.20]
.
∎

We are now able to prove Proposition
2.6
.

Proof of Proposition
2.6
.

The proof relies on the criterias developed in
[
33
]
ensuring the existence of a unique stationary distribution. Such criterias immediately follow from the existence of the Lyapunov function
H
in Lemma
4.3
, the moment estimates in Proposition
4.9
in addition with the irreducibility of the process shown in Lemma
4.12
. This concludes the proof.
∎

5.
Approximation by a Gaussian Process

In this section, we provide crucial estimates allowing us to analyze
the thermalization of the underdamped Langevin dynamics
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}

in the regime
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. More precisely, we approximate
the process
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
by a Gaussian perturbation
of the deterministic process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
(
2.1
).
This approximation will be carried out in terms of the
L^{p}
-distance
between the processes, and the total variation distance between the associated
laws. Namely Proposition
7.3
is the key challenge in
the analysis of the total variation distance to the equilibrium measure

\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(X_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\mu_{\epsilon})
.

We note that a similar approximation is also crucially used in the
investigation of the thermalization of the overdamped Langevin dynamics
in
[
2
, Section 3.2]
, but it turns out that the
proof of this approximation for the underdamped dynamics is much more
complicated since the dynamics is degenerate, and there is no global
contraction for the process
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
. In
[
2
]
,
the strong coercivity assumption (H) was crucially used which implied
the strong local contraction in the zero-noise dynamics (cf.
[
2
, the second display of Section 3]
),
while we are not able to expect similar property in the underdamped
case.

5.1.
Heuristic explanation

We start by explaining the heuristic argument leading to the main
Gaussian approximation, which idea is similar to the study of the
overdamped case
[
2
, Section 3.2]
. For
\epsilon>0
,
define the process
(Y_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

as

Y_{t}^{\epsilon}=\frac{X_{t}^{\epsilon}-X_{t}}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}}\;.

Write
(\widetilde{B}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
a
2d
-dimensional process defined
by

(5.1)

\widetilde{B}_{t}=(0,\,B_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\;,

where
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion appearing
in (
1.2
) so that we can write the SDE satisfied by
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}

as (cf. (
4.7
))

(5.2)

\textup{d}X_{t}^{\epsilon}=-G(X_{t}^{\epsilon})\textup{d}t+\sqrt{2\epsilon}\textup{d}\widetilde{B}_{t}\;.

Then, by (
4.8
) and (
5.2
), we can write

\textup{d}Y_{t}^{\epsilon}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}}\left[G(X_{t}^{\epsilon})-G(X_{t})\right]\text{d}t+\textup{d}\widetilde{B}_{t}\simeq-\textup{D}G(X_{t})Y_{t}^{\epsilon}\textup{d}t+\textup{d}\widetilde{B}_{t}\;.

Note that the Jacobian
\textup{D}G(x)
depends only on the position
vector
q
of
x=(q,\,p)
since

(5.3)

\mathbb{A}(q):=-\textup{D}G(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{O}_{d}&\mathbb{I}_{d}\\
-\textup{D}F(q)&-\gamma\mathbb{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix}\;.

Additionally we expect that
Y_{t}^{\epsilon}=\frac{X_{t}^{\epsilon}-X_{t}}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}}

is close to the Gaussian process
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined by the
SDE

(5.4)

\textup{d}Y_{t}=\mathbb{A}(q_{t})Y_{t}\textup{d}t+\textup{d}\widetilde{B}_{t}\;,

with initial condition
Y_{0}=0
, when
\epsilon
goes to zero.
With this observation in mind, we shall approximate the underdamped
Langevin dynamics
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
by the Gaussian process

(Z_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
defined by

(5.5)

Z_{t}^{\epsilon}=X_{t}+\sqrt{2\epsilon}Y_{t}\;.

This approximation is formalized in more details in Proposition
5.3

of the next subsection.

Remark 5.1
.

The followings are remarks on the matrix
\mathbb{A}(q)

and processes
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0},(Z_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
defined
above.

(1)

We shall always couple the processes
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0},
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
,
and
(Z_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
naturally; i.e., we assume that
the processes
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0},\,(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
and

(Z_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
start together at some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,
and that the processes
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
and
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}

share the same Brownian motion
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. When we would
like to stress that the starting point of these processes are
x
,
we denote them by
(X_{t}(x))_{t\geq 0}
,
(X_{t}^{\epsilon}(x))_{t\geq 0}

and
(Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(x))_{t\geq 0}
. Note that the process
(Y_{t}(x))_{t\geq 0}

does not start at
x
; instead it always starts at
0
. However,
the process depends on
x
through the process
q_{t}=q_{t}(x)

appearing in the equation (
5.4
).

(2)

For the simplicity of notation, we use the abbreviation

\mathbb{A}_{t}(x)=\mathbb{A}(q_{t}(x))

and we shall even say
\mathbb{A}_{t}=\mathbb{A}_{t}(x)
when there
is no risk of confusion regarding the starting point
x
.

(3)

We write
\mathbb{A}:=\mathbb{A}(0)
for the convenience of notation.
Then, since
\mathbb{A}=-\textup{D}G(0)
, by the global exponential
stability established in Theorem
2.3
, all the eigenvalues
of
\mathbb{A}
have negative real part.

(4)

Since
\mathbb{A}(q)\rightarrow\mathbb{A}
as
q\rightarrow 0
and
since
q_{t}(x)\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
by Theorem

2.3
, we have

(5.6)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{A}_{t}(x)=\mathbb{A}\;\;\;\;\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\;.

5.2.
Gaussian approximation of the underdamped Langevin dynamics

Notation 5.2
.

Recall the constant
\lambda

and function
H(\cdot)
from Definition
4.1
, and the constant

\kappa
from Theorem
2.3
. Then, define

(5.7)

T(x)=T^{\delta}(x)=\max\left\{\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\frac{\kappa\left(|x|^{2}+U(q)\right)}{\delta^{2}},\,0\right\}\;\;\;\;;\;x=(q,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\;,

where
\delta>0
is a sufficiently small constant which will be specified
later in Lemma
5.5
. The definition of
T(x)
will also
be explained therein. We also from now on fix a parameter
\theta\in(0,\,1/3)
,
and we shall always suppose that
\epsilon>0
is small enough so
that
\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\theta}}>T(x)
, since we will focus on the
Gaussian approximation in the time interval
[T(x),\,1/\epsilon^{\theta}]

in the next proposition.

The following proposition is the underdamped version of
[
2
, Lemma 3.1]

which provides a quantitative estimate on the error appearing in the

X_{t}^{\epsilon}
by
Z_{t}^{\epsilon}
on the

.

Proposition 5.3

(Gaussian approximation)
.

For all
r>0
,
n\geq 1
and
\nu\in(0,1)
, there exists a
constant
\alpha>0
(independent of
r,n
and
\nu
)
and a constant
C(n,\,r)>0
such that

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\sup_{t\in[T(x),\,1/\epsilon^{\theta}]}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)-Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)|^{n}\right]\leq C(n,\,r)\epsilon^{n/2}\left[\frac{e^{-\alpha nt}}{\epsilon^{n\nu}}+\epsilon^{n(1-2\theta)}\right]

for all small enough
\epsilon>0
.

As we have mentioned earlier, the proof of this proposition is more
involved than its analogous in the overdamped case
[
2
, Lemma 3.1]

since the underdamped Langevin dynamics does not have a strong global
contraction which is a crucial property assumed in
[
2
]
.
We introduced

to resolve this issue. The proof of this proposition
will be given at the end of the current section.

5.3.
Matrix equations

In this subsection, we provide a lemma allowing us to solve a certain
class of Lyapunov matrix equations which shall appear frequently throughout
this work. Note that in contrary to the overdamped case studied in
[
2
]
,
the next lemma requires more work since we do not assume that the
matrix
\mathbb{W}
appearing in (
5.10
) is positive
definite. This is related to the fact that the underdamped Langvin
dynamics is degenerate.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\mathbb{W}
be a
2d\times 2d
symmetric
non-negative matrix such that, for some
a>0
,

(5.8)

x\cdot\mathbb{W}x\geq a|p|^{2}\text{ \;\;\;\;for all }x=(q,\,p)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\;.

Let
\mathbb{U}
be a
2d\times 2d
matrix such that all the eigenvalues
of
\mathbb{U}
have negative real part and moreover, when we write

\mathbb{U}
as block-form

(5.9)

\mathbb{U}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{U}_{11}&\mathbb{U}_{12}\\
\mathbb{U}_{21}&\mathbb{U}_{22}\end{pmatrix}\;,

where all
\mathbb{U}_{ij}
’s are
d\times d
matrices, the matrix

\mathbb{U}_{21}
is invertible. Then, there exists a unique
2d\times 2d

real matrix
\mathbb{X}
such that

(5.10)

\mathbb{U}^{\dagger}\mathbb{X}+\mathbb{X}\mathbb{U}=-\mathbb{W}\;.

Moreover,
\mathbb{\mathbb{X}}
is symmetric positive definite.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{U}
is stable i.e., all the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{U}

have negative real part, by the assumption of the lemma, by
[
10
, Theorem 2 in p.414]

there exists a unique solution
\mathbb{X}
to the matrix equation
(
5.10
) and moreover by
[
10
, Theorem 3 in p.414]
,
this unique solution
\mathbb{X}
admits the representation

(5.11)

\mathbb{X}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbb{U}^{\dagger}t}\mathbb{W}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbb{U}t}\,\mathrm{d}t\;.

From this representation, the symmetry and non-negative definiteness
of
\mathbb{X}
is immediate. It now remains to prove the positive
definiteness of
\mathbb{X}
which follows from the conditions on

\mathbb{W}
and
\mathbb{U}
given in the lemma. By the representation
(
5.11
), we can write for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,

(5.12)

\displaystyle\left\langle x,\,\mathbb{X}x\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\langle\mathrm{e}^{\mathbb{U}t}x,\,\mathbb{W}\mathrm{e}^{\mathbb{U}t}x\right\rangle\mathrm{d}t\;.

Writing
\mathrm{e}^{\mathbb{U}t}x=(\widehat{q}_{t},\,\widehat{p}_{t})

so that by (
5.9
), we have the following system of ODE
satisfied by
(\widehat{q}_{t},\,\widehat{p}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
:

(5.13)

\begin{cases}\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\widehat{q}_{t}=\mathbb{U}_{11}\widehat{q}_{t}+\mathbb{U}_{12}\widehat{p}_{t}\;,\\
\frac{\textup{d}}{\textup{d}t}\widehat{p}_{t}=\mathbb{U}_{21}\widehat{q}_{t}+\mathbb{U}_{22}\widehat{p}_{t}\;.\end{cases}

Now we suppose that
\left\langle x,\,\mathbb{X}x\right\rangle=0
,
then in view of (
5.8
) and (
5.12
), we have
\widehat{p}_{t}\equiv 0
and hence by the second equation of (
5.13
) and by the invertibility of
\mathbb{U}_{21}
, we also
have
\widehat{q}_{t}\equiv 0
.
Thus, we can conclude that
x=(\widehat{q}_{0},\,\widehat{p}_{0})=0

the positive definiteness of
\mathbb{X}
follows.
∎

By Theorem
2.3
, Lemma
3.1
and Corollary
4.4
; the
global exponential stability ensures necessarily that both
\mathbb{A}

and
\mathbb{A^{\dagger}}
satisfy all the requirements imposed to

\mathbb{U}
in the previous lemma. Hence, from now on, we denote
by
\Gamma
the solution to equation (
5.10
) with

(\mathbb{U},\,\mathbb{W}):=(\mathbb{A},\,\mathbb{I}_{2d})
. Then,
by Lemma
5.4
,
\Gamma
is a positive definite symmetric
matrix satisfying

(5.14)

\mathbb{A}^{\dagger}\Gamma+\Gamma\mathbb{A}=-\mathbb{I}_{2d}\;.

Let us take
\xi>0
small enough so that

(5.15)

\xi|x|^{2}\leq\left\langle x,\,\Gamma x\right\rangle\leq\frac{1}{\xi}|x|^{2}\;\;\;\text{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\;.

5.4.
Preliminary moment estimates on
Y_{t}

In this section, we provide a control on the moments of
Y_{t}
.
We start from observing that, by Ito’s formula (cf. (
5.4
)),
we can write

(5.16)

\mathrm{d}\langle Y_{t},\Gamma Y_{t}\rangle=2\langle Y_{t},\,\Gamma\mathbb{A}(q_{t})Y_{t}\rangle\mathrm{d}t+2\langle\Gamma Y_{t},\mathrm{d}\widetilde{B}_{t}\rangle+M\mathrm{d}t\;,

where
\Gamma
is the matrix defined through (
5.14
)
and where
M=\sum_{i=d+1}^{2d}\Gamma_{ii}
. The following lemma provides
a control on the drift appearing in the right-hand side of the equation
above. In particular, the next lemma fully characterizes the constant

\delta>0
appearing in Notation
5.2
in the definition
of
T(x)=T^{\delta}(x)
in (
5.7
).

Lemma 5.5
.

There exists
\delta>0
such that, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}

and
t\geq T(x)=T^{\delta}(x)
, we have

\langle y,\,\Gamma\mathbb{A}_{t}(x)y\rangle\leq-\frac{\xi}{4}\langle y,\,\Gamma y\rangle\;\;\;\text{for all }y\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}\;,

where the constant
\xi
is the one appearing in (
5.15
).

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{A}(q)\underset{q\rightarrow 0}{\longrightarrow}\mathbb{A}
,
there exists
\delta>0
small enough such that, for all
|q|\leq\delta
,

(5.17)

\left\|(\mathbb{A}(q)-\mathbb{A})^{\dagger}\Gamma+\Gamma(\mathbb{A}(q)-\mathbb{A})\right\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\;.

By Theorem
2.3
and definition (
5.7
) of

T(x)
, if
t\geq T(x)
, we have

(5.18)

|q_{t}(x)|^{2}\leq|X_{t}(x)|^{2}\leq\kappa\left(|x|^{2}+U(q)\right)\textup{e}^{-\lambda t}\leq\delta^{2}

and thus we have, by (
5.17
),

(5.19)

\left\|(\mathbb{A}_{t}(x)-\mathbb{A})^{\dagger}\Gamma+\Gamma(\mathbb{A}_{t}(x)-\mathbb{A})\right\|=\left\|(\mathbb{A}(q_{t}(x))-\mathbb{A})^{\dagger}\Gamma+\Gamma(\mathbb{A}(q_{t}(x))-\mathbb{A})\right\|\leq\frac{1}{2}\;.

Hence, for
t\geq T(x)
, by (
5.19
)
and (
5.14
), we get

\displaystyle 2\langle y,\,\Gamma\mathbb{A}_{t}(x)y\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle y,\,\left[\mathbb{A}_{t}(x)^{\dagger}\Gamma+\Gamma\mathbb{A}_{t}(x)\right]y\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle y,\,\left[\mathbb{A}^{\dagger}\Gamma+\Gamma\mathbb{A}\right]y\right\rangle+\frac{1}{2}|y|^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}|y|^{2}\leq-\frac{\xi}{2}\langle y,\,\Gamma y\rangle\;,

where the last inequality comes from (
5.15
).
∎

The next lemma provides a control on the moments of
Y_{t}(x)
in
the time-interval
[0,T(x)]
.

Lemma 5.6
.

For all
n\geq 1
and
r>0
, there exists a
constant
C(n,\,r)>0
such that

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\sup_{t\in[0,\,T(x)]}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle^{n}\right]\leq C(n,\,r)\;.

Proof.

By Remark
4.6
, for
x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
, the entire
path
(X_{t}(x))_{t\geq 0}
is contained in the compact set
\mathcal{H}_{r}
,
we get

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\sup_{t\geq 0}\|\mathbb{A}(q_{t}(x))\|\leq\sup_{x=(q,\,p)\in\mathcal{H}_{r}}\|\mathbb{A}(q)\|=C(r)\;.

Thus, there exists a constant
C(r)>0
such that, for all
x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}

and
t\geq 0
,

(5.20)

\langle\mathbb{A}(q_{t}(x))Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle\leq C(r)\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle\;.

Now we prove the statement by induction. First consider
the case
n=1
. Fix
x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
. Then, by (
5.16
)
and (
5.20
), we have

\mathrm{d}\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle\leq C(r)\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle\mathrm{d}t+2\langle\Gamma Y_{t}(x),\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{B}_{t}\rangle+M\mathrm{d}t\;.

Therefore, by the Gronwall inequality we get (since
Y_{0}(x)=0
)

\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle\right]\leq Mte^{C(r)t}\;\;\;\;\text{for all }t\geq 0\;.

This proves the initial case of the induction since

(5.21)

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}T(x)<\infty\;.

Next we suppose that the statement holds for
1,\,\dots,\,n-1
and
look at the statement for
n\geq 2
. By Ito’s formula (cf. (
5.16
))
and (
5.20
), there exists a constant
C(r)>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle^{n}\leq

\displaystyle\;n\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle^{n-1}\left[\left(C(r)\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle+M\right)\mathrm{d}t+2\langle\Gamma Y_{t}(x),\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{B}_{t}\rangle\right]

(5.22)

\displaystyle+\frac{M}{2}n(n-1)\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle^{n-2}\mathrm{d}t\;,

and therefore we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle^{n}\right]\leq\;

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(nC(r)\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Y_{s}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{s}(x)\rangle^{n}\right]+nM\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Y_{s}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{s}(x)\rangle^{n-1}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{n(n-1)}{2}M\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Y_{s}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{s}(x)\rangle^{n-2}\right]\bigg)\mathrm{d}s\;.

By the induction hypothesis, there exist constants
C_{1}(n,\,r),\,C_{2}(n,\,r)>0

such that for all
t\in[0,T(x)]
and
x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle^{n}\right]\leq C_{1}(n,\,r)\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Y_{s}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{s}(x)\rangle^{n}\right]\mathrm{d}s+C_{2}(n,\,r)t\;.

Thus, the proof is again completed by the Gronwall inequality and
(
5.21
).
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

For all
n\geq 1
and
r>0
, there exists a constant

C(n,\,r)>0
such that

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\sup_{t:t\geq T(x)}\mathbb{E}\left[|Y_{t}(x)|^{n}\right]\leq C(n,\,r)\;.

Proof.

By the positive definiteness of the matrix
\Gamma
, it suffices
to prove that

(5.23)

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\sup_{t:t\geq T(x)}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle^{n}\right]\leq C(n,\,r)\;.

We prove this bound by mathematical induction as well. First consider
the case
n=1
. By (
5.16
) and Lemma
5.5
,
we have, for all
t\geq T(x)
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle\right]}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\xi}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle\right]+M\;.

Consequently, for all
t\geq T(x)
, by the Gronwall inequality

\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle\right]\leq e^{-\frac{\xi(t-T(x))}{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Y_{T(x)}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{T(x)}(x)\rangle\right]+\frac{2M}{\xi}

and hence the proof of the case
n=1
of (
5.23
) is completed
by Lemma
5.6
.

Next we suppose that the statement holds for
1,\,\dots,\,n-1
and
prove the statement for
n
. By (
5.22
) and Lemma
5.5
,
we have, for all
t\geq T(x)
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle^{n}\leq\;

\displaystyle n\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle^{n-1}\left[\left(-\frac{\xi}{2}\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle+M\right)\mathrm{d}t+2\langle\Gamma Y_{t},\mathrm{d}\widetilde{B}_{t}\rangle\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{M}{2}n(n-1)\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle^{n-2}\mathrm{d}t\;.

Hence, by the induction hypothesis, we get for all
x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}

and
t\geq T(x)
,

\frac{\textup{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle^{n}\right]}{\textup{d}t}\leq-\frac{\xi n}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle^{n}\right]+C(n,\,r)\;.

Therefore, the proof is completed by the Gronwall
inequality and Lemma
5.6
.
∎

We conclude this subsection with the control of the supremum of the
moments of
Y_{t}(x)
on the time interval
[T(x),1/\epsilon^{\theta}]
.

Lemma 5.8
.

For all
r>0
and
n\geq 1
, there exists a
constant
C(n,\,r)>0
such that

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[T(x),\,1/\epsilon^{\theta}]}|Y_{t}(x)|^{n}\right]\leq\frac{C(n,\,r)}{\epsilon^{n\theta/2}}

for all small enough
\epsilon>0
, where
\theta
is a constant
introduced in Notation
5.2
.

Proof.

By (
5.16
), for all
t\geq T(x)
, we have

\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle\leq\langle Y_{T(x)}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{T(x)}(x)\rangle+2\int_{T(x)}^{t}\langle\Gamma Y_{s}(x),\,\textup{d}\widetilde{B}_{s}\rangle+Mt

and therefore by Lemma
5.6
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[T(x),\,1/\epsilon^{\theta}]}\langle Y_{t}(x),\,\Gamma Y_{t}(x)\rangle^{n}\right]

(5.24)

\displaystyle\leq 3^{n-1}\left(C(n,r)+2^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[T(x),\,1/\epsilon^{\theta}]}\left|\int_{T(x)}^{t}\langle\Gamma Y_{r}(x),\mathrm{\,d}\widetilde{B}_{r}\rangle\right|^{n}\right]+\left(\frac{M}{\epsilon^{\theta}}\right)^{n}\right)\;.

By the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality, it holds that

(5.25)

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[T(x),\,1/\epsilon^{\theta}]}\left|\int_{T(x)}^{t}\langle\Gamma Y_{r}(x),\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{B}_{r}\rangle\right|^{n}\right]\leq C(n)\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{T(x)}^{1/\epsilon^{\theta}}|\Gamma Y_{r}(x)|^{2}\mathrm{d}r\right)^{n/2}\right]\;.

By the trivial bound
|\Gamma Y_{r}|^{2}\leq||\Gamma||^{2}|Y_{r}|^{2}
and Hölder’s inequality, the right-hand
side of the previous display is bounded from above by

(5.26)

C(n)\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\theta}}\right)^{n/2-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{T(x)}^{1/\epsilon^{\theta}}|Y_{r}(x)|^{n}\mathrm{d}r\right)\right]\leq C(n)\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\theta}}\right)^{n/2}\;,

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
5.7
. Hence,
by (
5.25
) and (
5.26
), we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[T(x),\,1/\epsilon^{\theta}]}\left|\int_{T(x)}^{t}\langle\Gamma Y_{r}(x),\,\mathrm{d}\widetilde{B}_{r}\rangle\right|^{n}\right]\leq C(n,r)\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\theta}}\right)^{n/2}\;.

Inserting this to (
5.24
) and recalling (
5.15
),
we get

\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[T(x),\,1/\epsilon^{\theta}]}|Y_{t}(x)|^{2n}\right]\leq\frac{C(n,r)}{\epsilon^{n\theta}}\;,

which concludes the proof.
∎

5.5.
Quadratic Gronwall inequality

In this section we provide a proof of a quadratic Gronwall inequality which shall be crucial in the proof of Proposition
5.3
.

Lemma 5.9
.

Suppose that a non-negative function
u\in\mathcal{C}^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathbb{R}_{+})
satisfies

(5.27)

\frac{\mathrm{d}u(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}\leq a-bu(t)+cu(t)^{2}

for all
t\geq 0
, where
a,b,c>0
are constants satisfying
\delta:=b^{2}-4ac>0
. Denote by

\alpha=\frac{b-\sqrt{\delta}}{2c}\;,\quad\text{}\quad\beta=\frac{b+\sqrt{\delta}}{2c}

two solutions of quadratic equation
a-bx+cx^{2}=0
. Let
M>1
and suppose that

(5.28)

u(0)\leq\frac{M\alpha+\beta}{M+1}\;.

Then, for all
t\geq 0
, we have

u(t)\leq\alpha+\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{Mc}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{\delta}t}\;.

Proof.

The proof is divided into several cases.

[Case 1:
u(0)<\alpha
]
In this case, we claim that
u(t)<\alpha
for all
t\geq 0
. Suppose not so that we can define

T_{\alpha}=\inf\{t>0:u(t)=\alpha\}<\infty\;.

Observe that

a-bu(t)+cu(t)^{2}=c(u(t)-\alpha)(u(t)-\beta)>0

for all
t\in[0,T_{\alpha})
. Hence,
It follows from (
5.27
) that, for all
t\in[0,T_{\alpha})
,

\frac{1}{c(u(t)-\alpha)(u(t)-\beta)}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}\leq 1\;.

Integrating over
[0,t]\subset[0,T_{\alpha})
ensures that

\log\left(\frac{\beta-u(t)}{\alpha-u(t)}\right)\leq\log\left(\frac{\beta-u(0)}{\alpha-u(0)}\right)+\sqrt{\delta}t\;.

As a result, for all
t\in[0,T_{\alpha})
,

u(t)\leq\alpha-\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{c}\frac{1}{\left(\frac{\beta-u(0)}{\alpha-u(0)}\right)\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{\delta}t}-1}\;.

Now we get a contradiction by letting
t\nearrow T_{\alpha}<\infty
which yields

\alpha\leq\alpha-\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{c}\frac{1}{\left(\frac{\beta-u(0)}{\alpha-u(0)}\right)\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{\delta}T_{\alpha}}-1}<\alpha\;.

[Case 2:
u(0)=\alpha
]
In this case, let us take
\eta>0
small enough so that
\delta_{\eta}:=b^{2}-4(a+\eta)c>0
. By (
5.27
), for all
t\geq 0
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}u(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}\leq a+\eta-bu(t)+cu(t)^{2}\;.

Then

u(0)=\alpha=\frac{b-\sqrt{\delta}}{2c}<\frac{b-\sqrt{\delta_{\eta}}}{2c}\;.

As a result, the same reasoning as in Case 1 ensures that for all
t\geq 0
and
\eta>0
,

u(t)\leq\frac{b-\sqrt{\delta_{\eta}}}{2c}\;.

Taking
\eta\rightarrow 0
yields that

u(t)\leq\alpha
for all
t\geq 0
.

[Case 3:
u(0)>\alpha
]
Let us now finally consider the case

\alpha<u(0)\leq\frac{M\alpha+\beta}{M+1}
for a constant
M>1
. Note here that, since
\beta>\alpha
, we have

\alpha<\frac{\beta+M\alpha}{M+1}<\beta

and hence we have
u(0)\in(\alpha,\beta)
. Let us now define

T_{\beta}=\inf\{t>0:u(t)=\beta\}\;.

Then, for all
t\in[0,T_{\alpha}\land T_{\beta})
, we have

a-bu(t)+cu(t)^{2}=c(u(t)-\alpha)(u(t)-\beta)<0

and therefore, integrating the equation as in Case 1, we get

\log\left(\frac{\beta-u(t)}{u(t)-\alpha}\right)\geq\log\left(\frac{\beta-u(0)}{u(0)-\alpha}\right)+\sqrt{\delta}t\;.

As a result, for all
t\in[0,T_{\alpha}\land T_{\beta})
,

(5.29)

u(t)\leq\alpha+\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{c}\frac{1}{\left(\frac{\beta-u(0)}{u(0)-\alpha}\right)\mathrm{e}^{\sqrt{\delta}t}+1}\leq\alpha+\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{Mc}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{\delta}t}\;,

where the second inequality follows from (
5.28
). Since
\alpha+\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{Mc}=\alpha+\frac{\beta-\alpha}{M}<\beta

the previous bound itself implies that
T_{\beta}=\infty
, and hence (
5.29
) holds for
t\in[0,\,T_{\alpha})
. Since we have
u(t)\leq\alpha
for all
t\in[T_{\alpha},\,\infty)
by Case 2, we are done.
∎

Remark 5.10
.

Unlike to the ordinary Gronwall’s inequality, the function
u(t)
may blow-up when we start from
u(0)>\beta
.

5.6.
Proof of Proposition
5.3

We are now ready to prove Proposition
5.3
which
is the most computational part of the current article.

Proof of Proposition
5.3
.

The proof is divided into several steps. Let us fix
r>0
and
x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
.
We shall omit the dependency on
x
in this proof; for instance,
we write
X_{t}^{\epsilon}
and
\mathbb{A}_{t}
instead of
X_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)

and
\mathbb{A}_{t}(x)
, respectively.

[Step 1]
Preliminary analysis

Define the process
(W_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
as

(5.30)

W_{t}^{\epsilon}:=\frac{X_{t}^{\epsilon}-Z_{t}^{\epsilon}}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}}=\frac{X_{t}^{\epsilon}-X_{t}}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}}-Y_{t}\;.

In view of (
5.15
), it suffices to show that, for all

t\in[T(x),\,\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\theta}}]
,

(5.31)

\mathbb{E}\left[\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle^{n}\right]\leq C(n,\,r)\left[\frac{e^{-c_{1}nt}}{\epsilon^{n\nu}}+\epsilon^{n(1-2\theta)}\right]\;.

By (
1.2
), (
2.1
), and (
5.4
) (since
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}

and
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
share the same Brownian motion), we can write

\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}=2\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma\mathbb{A}_{t}W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle-2\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma V_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle\;,

where

(5.32)

V_{t}^{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
F(q_{t}^{\epsilon})-F(q_{t})-\textup{D}F(q_{t})(q_{t}^{\epsilon}-q_{t})\end{pmatrix}\;.

Hence, by Lemma
5.5
and the positive-definiteness of

\Gamma
, we have, for
t\geq T(x)
,

(5.33)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{\xi}{2}\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle-2\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma V_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle\leq-c\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle+C|V_{t}^{\epsilon}|^{2}

for small enough constant
c>0
and large enough constant
C>0
.

[Step 2]
Control of
|V_{t}^{\epsilon}|^{2}

In view of (
5.33
), the next step is to control

|V_{t}^{\epsilon}|^{2}
. To that end, define the following event
(cf. Notation
4.5
)

(5.34)

\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}=\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}(x):=\left\{\sup_{s\in[0,\,1/\epsilon^{\theta}]}|X_{s}^{\epsilon}|^{2}\leq\kappa_{0}H(x)+1\right\}\;.

Write

I_{\epsilon}:=[T(x),\,1/\epsilon^{\theta}]

and fix
t\in I_{\epsilon}
. As observed in (
5.18
), we
have
|q_{t}|\leq\delta
. On the other hand, under the event
\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}
,
we have
|q_{t}^{\epsilon}|\leq\sqrt{\kappa_{0}H(x)+1}
. Therefore,
by the mean-value theorem (since
F\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R}^{d})
)
and by (
5.32
), we have

(5.35)

|V_{t}^{\epsilon}|\leq\frac{C(r)}{\epsilon^{1/2}}|q_{t}^{\epsilon}-q_{t}|^{2}

under the event
\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}
, where

C(r)=\penalty\ \sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\,\sup_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\,|z|\leq\delta+\sqrt{\kappa_{0}H(x)+1}}|D^{2}F(z)|\;.

By definition of
W_{t}^{\epsilon}
(cf (
5.30
)), we have

|q_{t}^{\epsilon}-q_{t}|^{2}\leq|X_{t}^{\epsilon}-X_{t}|^{2}=2\epsilon|Y_{t}+W_{t}^{\epsilon}|^{2}\leq 4\epsilon\left[|Y_{t}|^{2}+|W_{t}^{\epsilon}|^{2}\right]\;.

Combining this with (
5.35
) and applying (
5.15
),
we get

(5.36)

\displaystyle|V_{t}^{\epsilon}|

\displaystyle\leq C(r)\epsilon^{1/2}\left(\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle+|Y_{t}|^{2}\right)\;.

We remind that this inequality holds under the event
\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}

and for
t\in I_{\epsilon}
.

[Step 3]
Control of
\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle

In this step, we always put ourself under the event
\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}

and our aim is to provide a control on
\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle
.
By (
5.33
) and (
5.36
), we get, for
t\in I_{\epsilon}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle\leq-c_{0}\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle+C_{0}(r)\epsilon\left[\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle^{2}+|Y_{t}|^{4}\right]\;

for some constants
c_{0}
,
C_{0}(r)>0
. In the remainder of the proof, the constant
c_{0}
and
C_{0}(r)
always refer to the constants appearing in this bound.

Let us now apply the quadratic Gronwall inequality stated in Lemma
5.9
to the function
t\geq 0\mapsto\langle W_{t+T_{x}}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t+T_{x}}^{\epsilon}\rangle
. In order to do that, write

(5.37)

\delta_{\epsilon}:=c_{0}^{2}-4C_{0}(r)^{2}\epsilon^{2}\sup_{s\in I_{\epsilon}}|Y_{s}|^{4}\;.

Then, we define as in Lemma
5.9
,

\alpha_{\epsilon}=\frac{c_{0}-\sqrt{\delta_{\epsilon}}}{2C_{0}(r)\epsilon}\;,\quad\text{}\quad\beta_{\epsilon}=\frac{c_{0}+\sqrt{\delta_{\epsilon}}}{2C_{0}(r)\epsilon}\;.

Note that we shall later condition on the event
\delta_{\epsilon}>0
and therefore we can think of
\alpha_{\epsilon}
and
\beta_{\epsilon}
as real numbers.

Let us now take
\nu\in(0,1)
and define, for
\epsilon
small enough,

M_{\epsilon}:=\epsilon^{\nu-1}>1\;.

It follows then from Lemma
5.9
that under the
event
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}
defined by

(5.38)

\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}=\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}(x):=\left\{\delta_{\epsilon}>\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{2},\langle W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon}\rangle\leq\frac{\beta_{\epsilon}+M_{\epsilon}\alpha_{\epsilon}}{M_{\epsilon}+1}\right\}\;,

one has that, for all
t\in I_{\epsilon}
,

\displaystyle\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\alpha_{\epsilon}+\frac{\sqrt{\delta_{\epsilon}}}{M_{\epsilon}C_{0}(r)\epsilon}\mathrm{e}^{-\sqrt{\delta_{\epsilon}}(t-T_{x})}\leq\alpha_{\epsilon}+\frac{c_{0}\mathrm{e}^{c_{0}T_{x}/\sqrt{2}}}{\epsilon^{\nu}C_{0}(r)}\mathrm{e}^{-c_{0}t/\sqrt{2}}\;,

where the last inequality follows from the inequality
c_{0}^{2}/2\leq\delta_{\epsilon}\leq c_{0}^{2}
. Therefore, we get

\displaystyle\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle^{n}

\displaystyle\leq 2^{n-1}\left(\alpha_{\epsilon}^{n}+C(n,r)\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-nc_{0}t/\sqrt{2}}}{\epsilon^{n\nu}}\right)\;.

Moreover, by an elementary inequality
1-\sqrt{1-x}\leq x
for
x\in[0,\,1]
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{\epsilon}^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{c^{n}}{(2C_{0}(r)\epsilon)^{n}}\left(1-\sqrt{1-\frac{4C_{0}(r)^{2}\epsilon^{2}\sup_{s\in I_{\epsilon}}|Y_{s}|^{4}}{c_{0}^{2}}}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{0}^{n}}{(2C_{0}(r)\epsilon)^{n}}\left(\frac{4C_{0}(r)^{2}\epsilon^{2}\sup_{s\in I_{\epsilon}}|Y_{s}|^{4}}{c_{0}^{2}}\right)^{n}\leq C(n,r)\epsilon^{n}\sup_{s\in I_{\epsilon}}|Y_{s}|^{4n}\;.

Consequently, for
t\in I_{\epsilon}
, by Lemma
5.8
,
we can conclude that

(5.39)

\mathbb{E}\left[\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}\cap\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}}\right]\leq C(n,r)\left(\epsilon^{n(1-2\theta)}+\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-nc_{0}t/\sqrt{2}}}{\epsilon^{n\nu}}\right)\;.

This allows us to control
\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle^{n}

in (
5.31
) on the event
\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}\cap\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}
.
It remains now to provide a control on the event
\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}^{c}\cup\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}^{c}
.

[Step 4]
Control of
\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle

on the event
\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}^{c}\cup\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}^{c}
.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and (
5.15
),
we have

(5.40)

\mathbb{E}\left[\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}^{c}\cup\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}^{c}}\right]^{2}\leq C(n)\mathbb{E}\left[|W_{t}^{\epsilon}|^{4n}\right]\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}^{c}\cup\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}^{c})\;.

By Proposition
4.9
, Theorem
2.3
,
and Lemma
5.7
, for
t\in I_{\epsilon}
and
x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[|W_{t}^{\epsilon}|^{4n}\right]\leq\frac{3^{4n-1}}{(2\epsilon)^{2n}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{\epsilon}|^{4n}\right]+|X_{t}|^{4n}+(2\epsilon)^{2n}\mathbb{E}\left[|Y_{t}|^{4n}\right]\right)\leq\frac{C(n,\,r)}{\epsilon^{2n}}\;,

and inserting this to (
5.40
) yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\langle W_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{t}^{\epsilon}\rangle^{n}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}^{c}\cup\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}^{c}}\right]\leq\frac{C(n,\,r)}{\epsilon^{n}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}^{c}\cup\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}^{c})^{1/2}\;.

Therefore, it suffices to prove that

(5.41)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}^{c})\leq C(n,\,r)\epsilon^{4n(1-\theta)}\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}^{c})\leq C(n,\,r)\epsilon^{4n(1-\theta)}

to complete the proof of (
5.31
).

[Step 5]
Estimate of
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}^{c})

By Ito’s formula and similar computation carried
out in the proof of Proposition
4.9
, we get

H(X_{t}^{\epsilon})\leq H(x)+\sqrt{2\epsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle p_{s}^{\epsilon}+\frac{\gamma-\lambda}{2}q_{s}^{\epsilon},\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\right\rangle+d\epsilon t\;.

Thus, by Lemma
4.2
, for
t\in[0,1/\epsilon^{\theta}]
,
we have

|X_{t}^{\epsilon}|^{2}\leq\kappa_{0}H(x)+\kappa_{0}\sqrt{2\epsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle p_{s}^{\epsilon}+\frac{\gamma-\lambda}{2}q_{s}^{\epsilon},\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\right\rangle+\kappa_{0}d\epsilon^{1-\theta}\;.

Therefore, by definition (
5.34
), for
\epsilon
small
enough such that
\kappa_{0}d\epsilon^{1-\theta}\leq\frac{1}{2}
,
we have

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}^{c})\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\kappa_{0}\sqrt{2\epsilon}\sup_{t\in[0,1/\epsilon^{\theta}]}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle p_{s}^{\epsilon}+\frac{\gamma-\lambda}{2}q_{s}^{\epsilon},\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\right\rangle\right|>\frac{1}{2}\right)\;.

By the Markov inequality, the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality and
the Hölder inequality, we can bound the probability at the right-hand
side by

\displaystyle C(n)\epsilon^{4n}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{t\in[0,1/\epsilon^{\theta}]}\left|\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle p_{s}^{\epsilon}+\frac{\gamma-\lambda}{2}q_{s}^{\epsilon},\,\mathrm{d}B_{s}\right\rangle\right|^{8n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C(n)\epsilon^{4n}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{1/\epsilon^{\theta}}\left|p_{s}^{\epsilon}+\frac{\gamma-\lambda}{2}q_{s}^{\epsilon}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\right)^{4n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C(n)\epsilon^{4n}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{1/\epsilon^{\theta}}\left|p_{s}^{\epsilon}+\frac{\gamma-\lambda}{2}q_{s}^{\epsilon}\right|^{8n}\mathrm{d}s\right)\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\theta}}\right)^{4n-1}\right]\;.

Since
|p_{s}^{\epsilon}+\frac{\gamma-\lambda}{2}q_{s}^{\epsilon}|^{2}\leq C|X_{s}^{\epsilon}|^{2}

for some large enough constant
C>0
, we can conclude from the previous
bound and Proposition
4.9
that

(5.42)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}^{c})\leq C(n,\,r)\epsilon^{4n(1-\theta)}\;.

This completes the proof of the first inequality in (
5.41
).

[Step 6]
Estimate of
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}^{c})

In view of the definition (
5.38
) of
\mathcal{B}_{\epsilon}

and (
5.15
), it suffices to prove that, for all
c(r)>0
,
there exists a constant
C(n,\,r)>0
such that

(5.43)

\mathbb{P}\left[\delta_{\epsilon}\leq\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{2}\right]\leq C(n,\,r)\epsilon^{4n(1-\theta)}\;\;\;\;\text{and\;\;\;\;}\mathbb{P}\left[\langle W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon}\rangle>\frac{\beta_{\epsilon}+M_{\epsilon}\alpha_{\epsilon}}{M_{\epsilon}+1}\right]\leq C(n,\,r)\epsilon^{4n(1-\theta)}\;

By definition (
5.37
) of
\delta_{\epsilon}
and by Lemma
5.8
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\delta_{\epsilon}\leq\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\epsilon^{2}\sup_{s\in I_{\epsilon}}|Y_{s}|^{4}\geq\frac{c_{0}^{2}}{8C_{0}(r)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C(n,r)\epsilon^{4n}\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{s\in I_{\epsilon}}|Y_{s}|^{8n}\right]

\displaystyle\leq C(n,r)\epsilon^{4n(1-\theta)}.

Regarding the second estimate in (
5.43
), by (
5.42
) it is enough to control

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\{\langle W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon}\rangle>\frac{\beta_{\epsilon}+M_{\epsilon}\alpha_{\epsilon}}{M_{\epsilon}+1}\right\}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}\right].

Moreover, since
\beta_{\epsilon}\geq\frac{c}{2C(r)\epsilon}
and using the Markov inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\left\{\langle W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon}\rangle>\frac{\beta_{\epsilon}+M_{\epsilon}\alpha_{\epsilon}}{M_{\epsilon}+1}\right\}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left[\left\{\langle W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon}\rangle>\frac{c_{0}}{2C_{0}(r)\epsilon(M_{\epsilon}+1)}\right\}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}\right]

(5.44)

\displaystyle\leq C(N,r)(\epsilon M_{\epsilon}+\epsilon)^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon}\rangle^{N}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}}\right],

where we are free to take
N\geq 1
large enough such that
\nu N\geq 4n(1-\theta)
.

Next step is to control the expectation appearing in the right-hand
side of the above inequality. By (
5.15
) and by the
definition (
5.30
) of
W_{t}^{\epsilon}
, we have

(5.45)

\langle W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon}\rangle^{N}\leq C(N)\left[\frac{1}{\epsilon^{N}}|X_{T(x)}^{\epsilon}-X_{T(x)}|^{2N}+|Y_{T(x)}|^{2N}\right]\;.

By definition (
5.34
) of
\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}
and Theorem

2.3
, we can find a large enough
R(r)>0
such that,
for all
x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
we have

(5.46)

|X_{t}^{\epsilon}|,\,|X_{t}|\leq R(r)\;\;\;\text{for all }t\in[0,\,T(x)]

under
\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}
. Hence, by (
4.8
), (
5.2
) and the standard argument
based on Gronwall’s lemma along with (
5.46
), we obtain

(5.47)

|X_{T(x)}^{\epsilon}-X_{T(x)}|^{2N}\leq C(N,\,r)\epsilon^{N}\sup_{s\in[0,\,T(x)]}|B_{s}|^{2N}

under
\mathcal{A}^{\epsilon}
. Inserting this to (
5.45
)
and applying Lemma
5.7
, we get

(5.48)

\mathbb{E}\left[\langle W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon}\rangle^{N}\mathbf{1}_{\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}}\right]\leq C(N,\,r)\;.

Reinjecting into (
5.44
) ensures that

\mathbb{P}\left[\left\{\langle W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon},\,\Gamma W_{T(x)}^{\epsilon}\rangle>\frac{\beta_{\epsilon}+M_{\epsilon}\alpha_{\epsilon}}{M_{\epsilon}+1}\right\}\cap\mathcal{A}_{\epsilon}\right]\leq C(N,r)\epsilon^{\nu N}\;.

This completes the proof since we can take
N
so that
\nu N\geq 4n(1-\theta)
.
∎

6.
Covariance Matrix of the Gaussian approximation

Denote by
\Sigma_{t}(x)
the covariance matrix of the Gaussian random
vector
Y_{t}(x)
(cf. (
5.4
)). In this section, we investigate
the behavior of
\Sigma_{t}(x)
in the regime
t\rightarrow\infty

and
t\rightarrow 0
. In the regime
t\rightarrow\infty
, we prove
that
\Sigma_{t}(x)
converges to
\Sigma
defined in (
2.13
).
On the other hand, it is clear that
\Sigma_{t}(x)\rightarrow\mathbb{O}_{2d}

as
t\rightarrow 0
. We investigate the speed of these two convergences.
Using this convergence analysis we will be able to provide estimates
on the total variation distance
\textup{d}_{\textup{TV}}(Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(x),\,Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(y))

for
x,\,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
when
t\rightarrow 0
or
t\rightarrow\infty
.

6.1.
Covariance matrix of
Y_{t}

We first show that the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{t}(x)
satisfies
an ODE. Using this ODE we will then provide short and long-time asymptotics
of
\Sigma_{t}(x)
.

Lemma 6.1
.

The covariance matrix
\Sigma_{t}(x)
satisfies

\frac{\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t}(x)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\mathbb{J}+\mathbb{A}_{t}(x)\Sigma_{t}(x)+\Sigma_{t}(x)\mathbb{A}_{t}(x)^{\dagger}

where
\mathbb{J}
is the matrix defined in (
2.12
).

Proof.

Let us denote by
(\Pi_{t})_{t\geq 0}=(\Pi_{t}(x))_{t\geq 0}
the solution
of the matrix differential equation

(6.1)

\dot{\Pi}_{t}=-\Pi_{t}\mathbb{A}_{t}(x)\text{\;\;\;\;for }t\geq 0

with initial condition
\Pi_{0}=\mathbb{I}_{2d}
so that we can write
the process
(Y_{t}(x))_{t\geq 0}
as follows

Y_{t}(x)=\Pi_{t}^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\Pi_{s}\mathrm{d}\widetilde{B}_{s}\;\;\;;\;t\geq 0\;,

where
(\widetilde{B}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the process defined in (
5.1
).
In particular the covariance matrix
\Sigma_{t}(x)
is given by:

(6.2)

\Sigma_{t}(x)=\Pi_{t}^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\Pi_{s}\mathbb{J}\Pi_{s}^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}s\right)(\Pi_{t}^{-1})^{\dagger}\;.

The remaining computation is routine. By (
6.1
), we have

\frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi_{t}^{-1}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\Pi_{t}^{-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}\Pi_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Pi_{t}^{-1}=\mathbb{A}_{t}(x)\Pi_{t}^{-1}

and thus a direct computation of (
6.2
) yields

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t}(x)}{\mathrm{d}t}=\;

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{t}(x)\Pi_{t}^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\Pi_{s}\mathbb{J}\Pi_{s}^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}s\right)(\Pi_{t}^{-1})^{\dagger}+\Pi_{t}^{-1}\Pi_{t}\mathbb{J}\Pi_{t}^{\dagger}(\Pi_{t}^{-1})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle+\Pi_{t}^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\Pi_{s}\mathbb{J}\Pi_{s}^{\dagger}\mathrm{d}s\right)(\mathbb{A}_{t}(x)\Pi_{t}^{-1})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\;

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{t}(x)\Sigma_{t}(x)+\mathbb{J}+\Sigma_{t}(x)\mathbb{A}_{t}^{\dagger}(x)\;.

∎

6.2.
Long-time behavior of the covariance matrix

If the matrix
\Sigma_{t}(x)
converges to some matrix
\Sigma_{\infty}
,
then, by Lemma
6.1
and (
5.6
), we expect
that the matrix
\Sigma_{\infty}
satisfies
\mathbb{A}\Sigma_{\infty}+\Sigma_{\infty}\mathbb{A}^{\dagger}=-\mathbb{J}
.
Thus, by (
2.13
) we should have
\Sigma_{\infty}=\Sigma
.
This heuristic argument will be rigorously and quantitatively justfied
in Lemma
6.2
below. Before stating and proving the
lemma, we recall several well-known facts from linear algebra.

(1)

Frobenius norm.
The Frobenius norm of a square matrix
\mathbb{M}

is defined by

\|\mathbb{M}\|_{\textup{F}}=\textup{tr}(\mathbb{M}\mathbb{M}^{\dagger})^{1/2}\;.

It is well-known that
\|\mathbb{M}\|\leq\|\mathbb{M}\|_{\textup{F}}

and the Frobenius norm is submultiplicative, i.e., for all square
matrices
\mathbb{M}
and
\mathbb{M}^{\prime}
of same size, it holds that

(6.3)

\|\mathbb{M}\mathbb{M}^{\prime}\|_{\textup{F}}\leq\|\mathbb{M}\|_{\textup{F}}\|\mathbb{M}^{\prime}\|_{\textup{F}}\;.

(2)

We can infer from
Von Neumann’s trace inequality
that,
if
\mathbb{S}
is a positive semi-definite symmetric matrix, and

\mathbb{T}
is a diagonalizable matrix of same size, then we have

(6.4)

\textup{tr}(\mathbb{S}\mathbb{T})\leq\textup{tr}(\mathbb{S})\|\mathbb{T}\|\;.

If in addition
\mathbb{T}
is a positive definite symmetric matrix
whose smallest eigenvalue is
c>0
, then we have

(6.5)

\textup{tr}(\mathbb{S}\mathbb{T})\geq c\,\textup{tr}(\mathbb{S})\;.

Now we state the main result for the long-time behavior of the covariance
matrix
\Sigma_{t}(x)
.

Lemma 6.2
.

For all
r>0
, there exist constants
C(r)>0
and
\alpha(r)>0

such that

(6.6)

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\left\|\Sigma_{t}(x)-\Sigma\right\|_{\textup{F}}\leq C(r)e^{-\alpha(r)t}\;\;\;\;\text{for all }t\geq 0\;.

Proof.

Fix
x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
and we ignore the dependency of the matrices
to
x
throughout the proof. We define the symmetric matrix
\Delta_{t}=\Delta(x)
and the matrix
\mathbb{D}_{t}
by:

\Delta_{t}:=\Sigma_{t}-\Sigma\;,\qquad\mathbb{D}_{t}=\mathbb{A}_{t}-\mathbb{A}\;.

Then, by (
2.13
) and Lemma
6.1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{J}+\mathbb{A}_{t}\Sigma_{t}+\Sigma_{t}\mathbb{A}_{t}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{A}_{t}\Delta_{t}+\Delta_{t}\mathbb{A}_{t}^{\dagger}+\mathbb{D}_{t}\Sigma+\Sigma\mathbb{D}_{t}^{\dagger}\;.

Recall the positive definite symmetric matrix
\Gamma
defined in
(
5.14
). By the previous computation on
\frac{\mathrm{d}\Delta_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}
,
we get

(6.7)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t}\Gamma)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left((\mathbb{A}_{t}\Delta_{t}+\Delta_{t}\mathbb{A}_{t}^{\dagger})\Gamma\Delta_{t}\Gamma\right)+\mathrm{tr}\left(\Delta_{t}\Gamma(\mathbb{A}_{t}\Delta_{t}+\Delta_{t}\mathbb{A}_{t}^{\dagger})\Gamma\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathrm{tr}\left((\mathbb{D}_{t}\Sigma+\Sigma\mathbb{D}_{t}^{\dagger})\Gamma\Delta_{t}\Gamma\right)+\mathrm{tr}\left(\Delta_{t}\Gamma(\mathbb{D}_{t}\Sigma+\Sigma\mathbb{D}_{t}^{\dagger})\Gamma\right)

(6.8)

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{tr}\left(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t}(\Gamma\mathbb{A}_{t}+\mathbb{A}_{t}^{\dagger}\Gamma)\right)+2\mathrm{tr}\left(\Delta_{t}\Gamma(\mathbb{D}_{t}\Sigma+\Sigma\mathbb{D}_{t}^{\dagger})\Gamma\right)\;.

Moreover, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

(6.9)

\mathrm{tr}\left(\Delta_{t}\Gamma(\mathbb{D}_{t}\Sigma+\Sigma\mathbb{D}_{t}^{\dagger})\Gamma\right)\leq||\Delta_{t}||_{F}||\Gamma(\mathbb{D}_{t}\Sigma+\Sigma\mathbb{D}_{t}^{\dagger})\Gamma||_{F}\;.

Denote
by
c_{0}>0
the smallest eigenvalue of the positive definite symmetric
matrix
\Gamma
. Then since
\Delta_{t}^{2}
and
\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t}

are positive semi-definite matrices and
\Gamma
is symmetric positive
definite we obtain from (
6.5
) that

(6.10)

\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t}\Gamma)\geq c_{0}\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t})=c_{0}\mathrm{tr}(\Gamma\Delta_{t}^{2})\geq c_{0}^{2}\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{t}^{2})=c_{0}^{2}||\Delta_{t}||_{F}^{2}\;.

Furthermore, using the submultiplicativity in (
6.3
) and Theorem
2.3
along with the fact that
F
is
\mathcal{C}^{2}
on the set
\mathcal{H}_{r}
, we deduce by Remark
4.6
that there exists a constant
C(r)>0
such that

||\Gamma(\mathbb{D}_{t}\Sigma+\Sigma\mathbb{D}_{t}^{\dagger})\Gamma||_{F}\leq C(r)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}.

As a result, reinjecting into (
6.9
),

(6.11)

\mathrm{tr}\left(\Delta_{t}\Gamma(\mathbb{D}_{t}\Sigma+\Sigma\mathbb{D}_{t}^{\dagger})\Gamma\right)\leq C(r)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\left(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t}\Gamma\right)}\;.

Additionally, by (
5.14
)

(6.12)

\mathrm{tr}\left(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t}(\Gamma\mathbb{A}_{t}+\mathbb{A}_{t}^{\dagger}\Gamma)\right)=\mathrm{tr}\left(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t}(\Gamma\mathbb{D}_{t}+\mathbb{D}_{t}^{\dagger}\Gamma)\right)-\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t})\;.

Since
\Gamma\mathbb{D}_{t}+\mathbb{D}_{t}^{\dagger}\Gamma
is a
symmetric matrix and since
\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t}
is a positive
semi-definite symmetric matrix, by (
6.4
), we have

\mathrm{tr}\left(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t}(\Gamma\mathbb{D}_{t}+\mathbb{D}_{t}^{\dagger}\Gamma)\right)\leq\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t})\left\|\Gamma\mathbb{D}_{t}+\mathbb{D}_{t}^{\dagger}\Gamma\right\|\;.

Note that we verified in (
5.19
) that
\left\|\Gamma\mathbb{D}_{t}+\mathbb{D}_{t}^{\dagger}\Gamma\right\|\leq\frac{1}{2}

for
t\geq T(x)
and therefore by (
6.8
), (
6.11
), (
6.12
),
and applying (
6.4
) we get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t}\Gamma)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t})+C(r)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\left(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t}\Gamma\right)}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2\|\Gamma\|}\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t}\Gamma)+C(r)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}\left(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t}\Gamma\right)}

\displaystyle\leq-c_{1}\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t}\Gamma)+c_{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}

for some constants
c_{1}=c_{1}(r),c_{2}=c_{2}(r)>0
and for
t\geq T(x)
. Accordingly, by integrating
\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t}\Gamma)\mathrm{e}^{c_{1}t}
for
t\geq T(x)
we get

\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t}\Gamma)\mathrm{e}^{c_{1}t}\leq\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{T(x)}\Gamma\Delta_{T(x)}\Gamma)\mathrm{e}^{c_{1}T(x)}+c_{2}\int_{T(x)}^{t}\mathrm{e}^{(c_{1}-\lambda)s}\mathrm{d}s\;.

Therefore,

(6.13)

\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{t}\Gamma\Delta_{t}\Gamma)\leq\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{T(x)}\Gamma\Delta_{T(x)}\Gamma)\mathrm{e}^{-c_{1}(t-T(x))}+\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}-\lambda}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\;,

where the second inequality uses (
6.4
). Next
Combining (
6.13
) and (
6.10
) proves that

(6.14)

\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{t}^{2})\leq C(r)e^{-\alpha t}

for all
t\geq T(x)
for some
\alpha=\alpha(r)>0
. On the other hand, by Lemma
5.6
,
there exists a constant
C(r)>0
such that

(6.15)

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\sup_{t\in[0,\,T(x)]}\mathrm{tr}(\Delta_{t}^{2})\leq C(r)\;.

Thus, the proof is completed by (
6.14
), (
6.15
),
and the definition of
\Delta_{t}
.
∎

6.3.
Small-time behavior of the covariance matrix

In order to get the small-time asymptotics of the inverse
\Sigma_{t}(x)^{-1}

we investigate the behavior of
\Sigma_{t}(x)
in the regime
t\rightarrow 0
.
This part of the proof is interestingly much more involved than its
overdamped analogous studied in
[
2
]
, since

\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t}(x)}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{t=0}=\mathbb{J}

is not invertible. To resolve this issue, we go up to the third order
expansion of
\Sigma_{t}
around
t\simeq 0
.

Lemma 6.3
.

For all
r>0
, there exists
C(r)>0
such
that

(6.16)

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\left\|\Sigma_{t}(x)-\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}(x)\right\|_{\textup{F}}\leq C(r)t^{4}

for all small enough
t>0
, where

(6.17)

\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{t^{3}}{3}\mathbb{I}_{d}&\left(\frac{t^{2}}{2}-\gamma\frac{t^{3}}{2}\right)\mathbb{I}_{d}\\
\left(\frac{t^{2}}{2}-\gamma\frac{t^{3}}{2}\right)\mathbb{I}_{d}&\left(t-\gamma t^{2}+\frac{2}{3}\gamma^{2}t^{3}\right)\mathbb{I}_{d}-\frac{t^{3}}{6}(\textup{D}F(x)+\textup{D}F(x)^{\dagger})\end{pmatrix}\;.

Proof.

We fix
x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
and ignore the dependency on
x
in
the notation throughout this proof. By Lemma
6.1
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\Sigma_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{A}_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Sigma_{t}+\Sigma_{t}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{A}_{t}^{\dagger}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\mathbb{A}_{t}(\mathbb{J}+\mathbb{A}_{t}\Sigma_{t}+\Sigma_{t}\mathbb{A}_{t}^{\dagger})+(\mathbb{J}+\mathbb{A}_{t}\Sigma_{t}+\Sigma_{t}\mathbb{A}_{t}^{\dagger})\mathbb{A}_{t}^{\dagger}\;,

and consequently (since
\Sigma_{0}=\mathbb{O}_{2d}
), we get

\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\Sigma_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\right|_{t=0}=\mathbb{A}\mathbb{J}+\mathbb{J}\mathbb{A}^{\dagger}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbb{I}_{d}\\
\mathbb{I}_{d}&-2\gamma\mathbb{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix}\;.

Continuing the computation, we can also obtain

\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}\Sigma_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t^{3}}\right|_{t=0}=\begin{pmatrix}2\mathbb{I}_{d}&-3\gamma\mathbb{I}_{d}\\
-3\gamma\mathbb{I}_{d}&-(\textup{D}F(x)+\textup{D}F(x)^{\dagger})+4\gamma^{2}\mathbb{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix}\;.

Then, note that the matrix
\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}=\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}(x)

defined in (
6.17
) stands for the third order Taylor
expansion of
\Sigma_{t}
, i.e.,

\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}=t\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\Sigma_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}\right|_{t=0}+\frac{t^{2}}{2}\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\Sigma_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t^{2}}\right|_{t=0}+\frac{t^{3}}{6}\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}\Sigma_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t^{3}}\right|_{t=0}\;.

From now on we denote by
\mathbb{O}(t,\,x)
a
2d\times 2d
matrix
which is continuous in
(t,\,x)\in[0,\,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{2d}
.
Then, by the Taylor theorem, we can write

(6.18)

\Sigma_{t}(x)-\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}(x)=t^{4}\mathbb{O}(t,\,x)

and we can conclude that (
6.16
) holds since
F\in\mathcal{C}^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\mathbb{R}^{d})

by Assumption
2.2
.
∎

Next we get a bound on the norm of
\Sigma_{t}^{-1/2}
for small
times.

Lemma 6.4
.

For all
r>0
, there exists
C(r)>0
such
that

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\left\|\Sigma_{t}(x)^{-1/2}\right\|_{\textup{F}}\leq\frac{C(r)}{t^{3/2}}

for all small enough
t>0
.

Proof.

For
t>0
, define a
2d\times 2d
matrix
\Xi_{t}(x)
by

\Xi_{t}(x):=\frac{12}{t^{3}}\begin{pmatrix}\left(1-\gamma t+\frac{2}{3}\gamma^{2}t^{2}\right)\mathbb{I}_{d}-\frac{t^{2}}{6}(\textup{D}F(x)+\textup{D}F(x)^{\dagger})&-\left(\frac{t}{2}-\gamma\frac{t^{2}}{2}\right)\mathbb{I}_{d}\\
-\left(\frac{t}{2}-\gamma\frac{t^{2}}{2}\right)\mathbb{I}_{d}&\frac{t^{2}}{3}\mathbb{I}_{d}\end{pmatrix}\;,

so that we can write

(6.19)

\Xi_{t}(x)=\frac{12}{t^{3}}\left[\mathbb{U}+t\mathbb{O}(t,\,x)\right]\;\;\;\;\text{where }\mathbb{U}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{I}_{d}&\mathbb{O}_{d}\\
\mathbb{O}_{d}&\mathbb{O}_{d}\end{pmatrix}\;.

A direct computation shows that

{\widehat{\Sigma}}_{t}(x)\,\Xi_{t}(x)=\mathbb{I}_{2d}+t\mathbb{O}(t,\,x)\;,

and therefore by (
6.18
) and (
6.19
), we get

{\Sigma}_{t}(x)\,\Xi_{t}(x)=\mathbb{I}_{2d}+t\mathbb{O}(t,\,x)\;.

Since
\left[\mathbb{I}_{2d}+t\mathbb{O}(t,\,x)\right]^{-1}=\mathbb{I}_{2d}+t\mathbb{O}(t,\,x)

for small enough
t\geq 0
, we get from (
6.19
) that

\Sigma_{t}(x)^{-1}=\Xi_{t}(x)\big[\mathbb{I}_{2d}+t\mathbb{O}(t,\,x)\big]^{-1}=\frac{12}{t^{3}}\big[\mathbb{U}+t\mathbb{O}(t,\,x)\big]

and hence

\Sigma_{t}(x)^{-1/2}=\frac{\sqrt{12}}{t^{3/2}}\big[\mathbb{U}+t\mathbb{O}(t,\,x)\big]\;.

This completes the proof.
∎

6.4.
Total variation distance between Gaussian processes

In this subsection, we provide estimates on the total variation distances
between
Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)
and
Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(y)
in the long
time asymptotics. For
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a
d\times d
symmetric
positive definite matrix
\mathbb{K}
, denote by
\mathcal{N}(v,\,\mathbb{K})

the Gaussian distribution centered at
v
with covariance matrix
\mathbb{K}
.
Before stating and proving Lemma
6.7
, we recall
serveral lemma from
[
2
]
.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and let
\mathbf{\mathbb{S}},\mathbf{\mathbb{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2d\times 2d}
be
two symmetric positive definite matrices. Let
c\neq 0
be a fixed constant. Then, we have

(1)

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{N}(cx,c^{2}\mathbb{S}),\mathcal{N}(cy,c^{2}\mathbb{T}))=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{N}(x,\mathbb{S}),\mathcal{N}(y,\mathbb{T}))
.

(2)

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{N}(x,\mathbb{S}),\mathcal{N}(y,\mathbb{T}))=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{N}(x-y,\mathbb{S}),\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{T}))
.

(3)

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{N}(x,\mathbb{S}),\mathcal{N}(y,\mathbb{S}))=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{S}^{-1/2}x,\mathbb{I}_{2d}),\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{S}^{-1/2}y,\mathbb{I}_{2d}))
.

(4)

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{S}),\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{T}))=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{T}^{-1/2}\mathbb{ST}^{-1/2}),\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{2d}))
.

Proof.

We refer to
[
2
, Lemma A.1]
for the proof.
∎

Lemma 6.6
.

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
,

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal{N}(x,\mathbb{I}_{2d}),\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbb{I}_{2d}))=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\int_{0}^{|x|/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{s^{2}}{2}}\mathrm{d}s\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}|x|.

Proof.

We refer to
[
2
, Lemma A.2]
for the proof.
∎

Using these properties, let us now derive suitable upper-bounds on
\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(x),\,Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(y)\right)
which will be used later on.

Lemma 6.7
.

For all
r>0
, there exist constants
c_{0}(r)>0

and
C(r)>0
such that

\sup_{x,\,y\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(x),\,Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(y)\right)\leq\frac{C(r)}{\epsilon^{1/2}}e^{-c_{0}t}\;.

Proof.

Fix
r>0
and
x,\,y\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
. Recall from the definition
(
5.5
) that we have

(6.20)

Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)\sim\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(x),\,2\epsilon\Sigma_{t}(x))\;.

Hence, by the triangle inequality, we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(x),\,Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(y)\right)\leq\;

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(x),\,2\epsilon\Sigma_{t}(x)),\,\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(x),\,2\epsilon\Sigma)\right)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(y),\,2\epsilon\Sigma),\,\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(y),\,2\epsilon\Sigma_{t}(y))\right)

(6.21)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(x),\,2\epsilon\Sigma),\,\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(y),\,2\epsilon\Sigma)\right)\;.

By Lemma
6.5
-(1) and (2), the first distance in
the right-hand side equals to

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(0,\,2\epsilon\Sigma_{t}(x)),\,\mathcal{N}(0,\,2\epsilon\Sigma)\right)=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(0,\,\Sigma_{t}(x)),\,\mathcal{N}(0,\,\Sigma)\right)\;.

By
[
4
, Theorem 1.1 and display (2)]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(0,\,\Sigma_{t}(x)),\,\mathcal{N}(0,\,\Sigma)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\Sigma_{t}(x)\Sigma^{-1/2}-\mathbb{I}_{2d}\right\|_{\textup{F}}

(6.22)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}\left\|\Sigma_{t}(x)-\Sigma\right\|_{\textup{F}}

where the second inequality follows from the submultiplicativity of
Frobenius norm, i.e., (
6.3
). Hence, by Lemma
6.2
,
we get

(6.23)

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(x),\,2\epsilon\Sigma_{t}(x)),\,\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(x),\,2\epsilon\Sigma)\right)\leq C(r)e^{-\alpha(r)t}\;.

Note that the second distance at the right-hand side of (
6.21
)
has the same bound. Now we turn to the last distance in (
6.21
).
By Lemma
6.5
-(1), (2) and (3), we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(x),\,2\epsilon\Sigma),\,\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(y),\,2\epsilon\Sigma)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\Sigma^{-1/2}\frac{X_{t}(x)-X_{t}(y)}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}},\,\mathbb{I}_{2d}\right),\,\mathcal{N}(0,\,\mathbb{I}_{2d})\right)\;.

Hence, by Lemma
6.6
, we have

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(x),\,2\epsilon\Sigma),\,\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(y),\,2\epsilon\Sigma)\right)\leq\frac{\left\|\Sigma^{-1/2}\right\|}{\sqrt{4\pi\epsilon}}|X_{t}(x)-X_{t}(y)|\;.

Therefore, by Theorem
2.3
, there exists a constant

C(r)>0
such that

(6.24)

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(x),\,2\epsilon\Sigma),\,\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(y),\,2\epsilon\Sigma)\right)\leq\frac{C(r)}{\epsilon^{1/2}}e^{-\lambda t}\;.

Summing up (
6.23
) and (
6.24
) complete the proof.
∎

Next we provide a bound which we shall use for the study of the small-time
asymptotics.

Lemma 6.8
.

For all
r>0
, there exist constants
c_{1}(r),\,c_{2}(r)>0

such that

\sup_{u,\,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(u),\,Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(u^{\prime})\right)\leq c_{1}(r)\left[\left(\frac{e^{c_{2}(r)t}}{t^{3/2}\epsilon^{1/2}}+1\right)|u-u^{\prime}|+t\right]

for all small enough
t>0
.

Proof.

We fix
r>0
and
u,\,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{r}
. By (
6.20
)
and the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(u),\,Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(u^{\prime})\right)\leq

\displaystyle\;\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(u),\,2\epsilon\Sigma_{t}(u)),\,\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(u^{\prime}),\,2\epsilon\Sigma_{t}(u))\right)

(6.25)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(u^{\prime}),\,2\epsilon\Sigma_{t}(u)),\,\mathcal{N}(X_{t}(u^{\prime}),\,2\epsilon\Sigma_{t}(u^{\prime}))\right)\;.

By Lemma
6.5
-(2), (3), the first term at the
right-hand side can be written as

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\Sigma_{t}(u)^{-1/2}\frac{X_{t}(u)-X_{t}(u^{\prime})}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}},\,\mathbb{I}_{2d}\right),\,\mathcal{N}(0,\,\mathbb{I}_{2d})\right)\;.

By Corollary
4.8
, Lemma
6.4
, and Lemma
6.6
this is bounded from above by

(6.26)

\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\epsilon}}\left\|\Sigma_{t}(u)^{-1/2}\right\|\left|X_{t}(u)-X_{t}(u^{\prime})\right|\leq c_{1}(r)\frac{e^{c_{2}(r)t}}{t^{3/2}\epsilon^{1/2}}|u-u^{\prime}|

for some constants
c_{1}(r),\,c_{2}(r)>0
.

On the other hand, by Lemma
6.5
-(1), (3), the
second term at the right-hand side of (
6.25
) equals to

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(0,\,\Sigma_{t}(u)),\,\mathcal{N}(0,\,\Sigma_{t}(u^{\prime}))\right)\;.

By the same argument with (
6.22
) and Lemma
6.4
,
this distance is bounded from above by

(6.27)

\frac{3}{2}\left\|\Sigma_{t}(u)^{-1/2}\right\|_{\textup{F}}^{2}\left\|\Sigma_{t}(u^{\prime})-\Sigma_{t}(u)\right\|_{\textup{F}}\leq\frac{C(r)}{t^{3}}\left\|\Sigma_{t}(u^{\prime})-\Sigma_{t}(u)\right\|_{\textup{F}}\;.

By Lemma
6.3
and mean-value theorem along with
explicit formular (
6.17
) for
\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}
,
we get

\displaystyle\left\|\Sigma_{t}(u^{\prime})-\Sigma_{t}(u)\right\|_{\textup{F}}

\displaystyle\leq C(r)t^{4}+\left\|\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}(u^{\prime})-\widehat{\Sigma}_{t}(u)\right\|_{\textup{F}}

\displaystyle=C(r)t^{4}+Ct^{3}\left\|\textup{D}F(u^{\prime})-\textup{D}F(u)\right\|_{\textup{F}}\leq C(r)\left(t^{4}+t^{3}|u^{\prime}-u|\right)\;.

Inserting this to (
6.27
), we can conclude that the second
term at the right-hand side of (
6.25
) is bounded by
C(r)\left(t+|u^{\prime}-u|\right)
.
This bound along with (
6.25
) and (
6.26
) complete
the proof.
∎

by gathering the results obtained in the previous section. From now
on, we suppose that both Assumptions

and

7.1.
Long-time behavior on zero-noise dynamics

Let us start this section with the proof of Lemma
2.10
which follows the steps of
[
2
, Lemma B.2]
,
since it is one of the crucial tools in the sharp estimate of the
total variation distance.

Proof of Lemma
2.10
.

By the Hartman-Grobman theorem, there exist neighborhoods
\mathcal{U}
,
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2d}

of the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and a diffeomorphism
\phi:\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}

that maps the path
(X_{t}(x))_{t\geq 0}
to the linearized path
(e^{\mathbb{A}t}\phi(x))_{t\geq 0}

for all
x\in\mathcal{U}
where
\mathbb{A}=-\textup{D}G(0)
) (cf.
(
4.8
)).

We first prove the lemma when
x\in\mathcal{U}
. Denote by
-\lambda_{1},\,\dots,\,-\lambda_{N}\in\mathbb{C}

the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{A}
so that
\textup{Re}(\lambda_{i})>0

for all
i\in\llbracket 1,\,N\rrbracket
by Remark
5.1
-(3).
Denote by
\{w_{j,\,k}:j\in[1,\,N],\,k\in[1,\,N_{j}]\}
the Jordan
basis of
\mathbb{A}
, i.e.,

\mathbb{A}w_{j,\,k}=-\lambda_{j}w_{j,\,k}+w_{j,\,k+1}\;\;\;\;;\;j\in[1,\,N],\,k\in[1,\,N_{j}]\;,

where
w_{j,\,N_{j}+1}=0
. Expanding
x
by using this Jordan basis
in a way that

(7.1)

x=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{j}}x_{j,\,k}w_{j,\,k}\;.

Here, by the Putzer spectral method (cf.
[
2
, Proof of Lemma B.1.]
),
we can write

e^{\mathbb{A}t}x=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{j}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{t^{k-i}e^{-\lambda_{j}t}}{(k-i)!}x_{j,\,i}w_{j,\,k}\;.

Then, define

\displaystyle\eta

\displaystyle=\eta(x):=\min\left\{\textup{Re}(\lambda_{j}):x_{j,\,k}\neq 0\text{ for some }k\in\llbracket 1,\,N_{j}\rrbracket\right\}>0\;,

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle=\nu(x):=\max\left\{N_{j}-k:x_{j,\,k}\neq 0\text{ for some }k\in\llbracket 1,\,N_{j}\rrbracket\right\}\geq 0\;\text{and}

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=I(x):=\{j\in[1,\,N]:\textrm{Re}(\lambda_{j})=\eta\text{ and }x_{j,\,N_{j}-\nu}\neq 0\}\;,

so that we can write

(7.2)

\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\eta t}}{t^{\nu}}e^{\mathbb{A}t}x=\sum_{j\in I}\frac{e^{-(\lambda_{j}-\eta)t}}{\nu!}x_{j,\,N_{j}-\nu}w_{j,\,N_{j}}+\varepsilon_{t}\;,

where
\varepsilon_{t}\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
. Since

\lambda_{j}-\eta
is pure imaginary for
j\in I
, by writing

\lambda_{j}-\eta=-i\theta_{j}\;\;\;\text{and}\;\;\;v_{j}=\frac{x_{j,\,N_{j}-\nu}}{\nu!}w_{j,\,N_{j}}\;,

we can deduce from (
7.2
) that

(7.3)

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\left|\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\eta t}}{t^{\nu}}e^{\mathbb{A}t}x-\sum_{k\in I}\mathrm{e}^{i\theta_{k}t}v_{k}\right|=0\;.

In
[
2
, Lemma B.2.]
, using the Hartman-Grobman
theorem mentioned above, it is proven that we can replace
e^{\mathbb{A}t}x

at (
7.3
) with
X_{t}(x)
. Therefore, we can derive
(
2.15
) with
\tau(x)=0
in the case
x\in\mathcal{U}
.

For general
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, define
\tau(x)>0
as

\tau(x)=\inf\{t>0:X_{t}(x)\in\mathcal{U}\}+1

so that
X_{\tau(x)}(x)\in\mathcal{U}
. Note that
\tau(x)<\infty

follows from Theorem
2.3
. Since
X_{t+\tau(x)}(x)=X_{t}(X_{\tau(x)}(x))
,
we also have (
2.15
) with the constants defined at
X_{\tau(x)}(x)\in\mathcal{U}

in the previous step.
∎

Remark 7.1
.

In some special cases, the constants obtained in
the previous lemma can be simplified.

(1)

For
x\in\mathcal{U}
with
x_{j,\,k}\neq 0
for all
j,\,k
(which
happens almost everywhere in
\mathcal{U}
with respect to the Lebesgue
measure),
\eta
is the smallest real part of eigenvalue, and
\nu+1

is the largest size of Jordan block associated with an eigenvalue
of smallest real part.

(2)

When
\mathbb{A}
is diagonalizable, then
\nu=0
.

(3)

When all eigenvalues of
\mathbb{A}
are positive real, we have
\theta_{k}=0

for all
k\in I
and all vectors
v_{k}
are real vectors.

7.2.
Strategy to estimate total-variation distance

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and recall the constants and vectors defined
in Lemma
2.10
. The mixing time for the process
(X_{t}^{\epsilon}(x))_{t\geq 0}

is defined as

t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x):=\frac{1}{2\eta}\log\left(\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\right)+\frac{\nu}{\eta}\log\log\left(\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\right)+\tau(x)\;.

For
t\geq\tau(x)
define
v(t,\,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and
D^{\epsilon}(t,\,x)\in[0,\,1]

as

(7.4)

\displaystyle v(t,\,x)

\displaystyle:=\frac{(t-\tau(x))^{\nu}}{\mathrm{e}^{\eta(t-\tau(x))}}\Sigma^{-1/2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathrm{e}^{i\theta_{k}(t-\tau(x))}v_{k}\;,

(7.5)

\displaystyle D^{\epsilon}(t,\,x)

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}}v(t,\,x),\,\mathbb{I}_{2d}\right),\,\mathcal{N}(0,\,\mathbb{I}_{2d})\right)\;.

Our main result regarding the estimate of the total variation distance
to the equilibrium is the following theorem.

Theorem 7.2
.

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and
w\in\mathbb{R}
,
it holds that

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\left|\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)+w}^{\epsilon}(x),\,\mu^{\epsilon}\right)-D^{\epsilon}(t_{\textup{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)+w,\,x)\right|=0\;.

We note here that Theorems
2.12
and
2.13

are a simple consequence of this theorem. It remains to analyze the
behavior of
D^{\epsilon}(t_{\textup{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)+w)
for

w\in\mathbb{R}
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, and this step is identical
to
[
2
]
as for the overdamped Langevin dynamics.
Hence, we formally conclude the proof of these theorems by assuming
Theorem
7.2
.

Proof of Theorems
2.12
and
2.13
.

The proof of Theorems
2.12
and
2.13

are identical to that of
[
2
, Corollary 2.7]

and
[
2
, Corollary 2.9]
, respectively.
∎

The basic strategy to prove Theorem
7.2
is based on the
coupling argument developed in
[
2
]
, but due
to the degeneracy and lack of global contraction, we need to considerably
refine the argument. Our scheme of proof allows us to tackle the degeneracy
in (
1.2
) and could be extended to other processes with
similar degeneracy. We explain now the structure of this proof.

The first step is to control the total variation distance between
the process
(X_{t}^{\epsilon}(x))_{t\geq 0}
and its Gaussian approximation

(Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(x))_{t\geq 0}
using the moment estimates obtained
in Proposition
5.3
.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
(t^{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
be a sequence
of positive real numbers such that

(7.6)

\alpha_{0}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\leq t^{\epsilon}\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\theta}}

for some constant
\alpha_{0}>0
, where
\theta
is the constant
defined in Notation
5.2
. Then, for any
r>0
, we have

(7.7)

\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x),\,Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)\right)=0\;.

This proposition is proven in Section
7.3
. The next step
is to estimate the distribution of the Gaussian process

.

Proposition 7.4
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
and let
(t^{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}

be a sequence of positive reals such that

(7.8)

t^{\epsilon}\geq t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)-c

for some constant
c>0
for all small enough
\epsilon
. Then, we
have

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\left|\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x),\,\mathcal{N}(0,2\epsilon\Sigma)\right)-D^{\epsilon}(t^{\epsilon},\,x)\right|=0\;.

The remaining part of this article is devoted to the proof of Propositions

7.3
,
7.4
, and Theorems
2.8

and
7.2
. Let us start with the proof of Theorem
7.2

assuming that Propositions
7.3
,
7.4
, and

2.8
hold.

Proof of Theorem
7.2
.

By the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\bigg|\,\big|\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)+w}^{\epsilon}(x),\,\mu^{\epsilon}\right)-D^{\epsilon}(t_{\textup{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)+w,\,x)\big|

\displaystyle\;\;\;-\big|\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)+w}^{\epsilon}(x),\,\mathcal{N}(0,2\epsilon\Sigma)\right)-D^{\epsilon}(t_{\textup{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)+w,\,x)\big|\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)+w}^{\epsilon}(x),\,Z_{t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)+w}^{\epsilon}(x)\right)+\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(0,2\epsilon\Sigma),\,\mu^{\epsilon}\right)\;.

Therefore, the proof is completed by Propositions
7.3

and
7.4
with
t^{\epsilon}=t_{\mathrm{mix}}^{\epsilon}(x)+w

(which satisfies (
7.6
) and (
7.8
)) and Theorem

2.8
.
∎

7.3.
Proof of Proposition
7.3

We start by proving the following lemma which provides an upper-bound
on the total variation distance between the random variables
X_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)

and
Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)
for small times
t>0

Lemma 7.5
.

For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
, we have that

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t}^{\epsilon}(x),Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{2\epsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|F(q_{s}^{\epsilon}(x))-F(q_{s}(x))-\textup{D}F(q_{s}(x))\left(q_{s}^{\epsilon}(x)-q_{s}(x)\right)\right|^{2}\right]\textup{d}s\;.

Proof.

Write
Z_{t}^{\epsilon}=(\widetilde{q}_{t}^{\epsilon},\,\widetilde{p}_{t}^{\epsilon})

so that we can rewrite the SDE describing the process
(Z_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}

as

(7.9)

\begin{cases}\textup{d}\widetilde{q}_{t}^{\epsilon}=\widetilde{p}_{t}^{\epsilon}\textup{d}t\\
\textup{d}\widetilde{p}_{t}^{\epsilon}=-(F(q_{t})+\textup{D}F(q_{t})(\widetilde{q}_{t}^{\epsilon}-q_{t})+\gamma\widetilde{p}_{t}^{\epsilon})\textup{d}t+\sqrt{2\epsilon}\,\textup{d}B_{t}\;.\end{cases}

Now, denote by
\mathbb{P}_{X}
and
\mathbb{P}_{Z}
the law of
the processes
(X_{s}^{\epsilon}(x))_{s\in[0,\,t]}
and
(Z_{s}^{\epsilon}(x))_{s\in[0,\,t]}
,
respectively. Then, by Pinsker’s inequality, we have

(7.10)

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t}^{\epsilon}(x),\,Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)\right)^{2}\leq-2\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}_{X}}\left[\log\frac{\textup{d}\mathbb{P}_{Z}}{\textup{d}\mathbb{P}_{X}}\right]\;.

By the Girsanov theorem, (
1.2
), and (
7.9
), we
have

\displaystyle\log\frac{\textup{d}\mathbb{P}_{Z}}{\textup{d}\mathbb{P}_{X}}=\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}}\int_{0}^{t}\left\langle F(q_{s}^{\epsilon}(x))-F(q_{s}(x))-\textup{D}F(q_{s}(x))\left(q_{s}^{\epsilon}(x)-q_{s}(x)\right),\,\textup{d}B_{s}\right\rangle

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{4\epsilon}\int_{0}^{t}\left|F(q_{s}^{\epsilon}(x))-F(q_{s}(x))-\textup{D}F(q_{s}(x))\left(q_{s}^{\epsilon}(x)-q_{s}(x)\right)\right|^{2}\textup{d}s\;.

Inserting this to (
7.10
), we can complete the proof.
∎

Our strategy is to first couple the processes
(X_{t}^{\epsilon}(x))_{t\geq 0}

and
(Z_{t}^{\epsilon}(x))_{t\geq 0}
for small times as explained
in Proposition
7.6
, similarly to what was done in
[
2
]
.
To that end, we need to introduce the following small time scales.
Recall the constants
\alpha_{1},\,\alpha_{2}>0
from Proposition

5.3
and the constant
\alpha_{0}>0
from the statement
of Proposition
7.3
. Let us take
\sigma
small enough
so that

(7.11)

0<\sigma<\min\left(\frac{2\alpha_{1}\alpha_{0}}{3},\,\frac{2\alpha_{2}}{3}\right)\;.

Additionally, let us take
\nu
small enough from Proposition

5.3
such that

(7.12)

0<\nu<\min\left(\frac{\sigma}{2},\alpha_{1}\alpha_{0}-\frac{3\sigma}{2}\right).

Let us now define the time scale
\delta^{\epsilon}
by
\delta^{\epsilon}:=\epsilon^{\sigma}
.
The following is the first step in the approximation of
X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)

by
Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)
in the total variation distance.

Proposition 7.6
.

Let
(t^{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
be a sequence
of positive reals satisfying (
7.6
). Then, for all
r>0
,
we have

\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x),\,Z_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x))\right)=0.

Proof.

Let
r_{0}>0
be small enough so that we have for all
x\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{0}}
,

(7.13)

T(x)=0\;\;\;\text{and\;\;\;}H(x)<\frac{1}{16(d+2)\kappa_{0}\rho}\;,

where
T(x)
is defined in Notation
5.2
, and the constants

\kappa_{0}
and
\rho
are the ones appearing in Lemma
4.2

and Assumption
2.11
, respectively.

Since
X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)=X_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x))
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x),Z_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x))\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(u),Z_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(u)\right)\mathbb{P}(X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)\in\mathrm{d}u)

(7.14)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{D}_{r_{0}}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(u),Z_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(u)\right)\mathbb{P}(X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)\in\mathrm{d}u)+\mathbb{P}(|X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)|>r_{0})\;.

By Chebychev inequality and Proposition
4.9
, we have

(7.15)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)|>r_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)|^{2}\right]}{r_{0}^{2}}\leq\frac{\kappa_{0}}{r_{0}^{2}}\left[H(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda(t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon})}+\frac{d\epsilon}{\lambda}\right]\;.

Next we fix
u\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{0}}
. Then, by Lemma
7.5
,
we have

(7.16)

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(u),\,Z_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(u)\right)^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}\left[A^{\epsilon}(1)+A^{\epsilon}(2)\right]\;,

where

\displaystyle A^{\epsilon}(1)=\int_{0}^{\delta^{\epsilon}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|F(q_{s}^{\epsilon}(u))-F(q_{s}(u))\right|^{2}\right]ds\;,

\displaystyle A^{\epsilon}(2)=\left\|\textup{D}F(q_{s}(u))\right\|\int_{0}^{\delta^{\epsilon}}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{s}^{\epsilon}(u)-X_{s}(u)|^{2}\right]ds\;.

Let us first look
A^{\epsilon}(1)
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
and Assumption
2.11
, we have

\displaystyle\big|F(q_{s}^{\epsilon}(u))-F(q_{s}(u))\big|^{2}

\displaystyle=\big|\int_{0}^{1}\textup{D}F(q_{s}(u)+\zeta(q_{s}^{\epsilon}(u)-q_{s}(u)))\,(q_{s}^{\epsilon}(u)-q_{s}(u))\mathrm{d}\zeta\bigg|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\big|q_{s}^{\epsilon}(u)-q_{s}(u)\big|^{2}\int_{0}^{1}|\textup{D}F(q_{s}(u)+\zeta(q_{s}^{\epsilon}(u)-q_{s}(u)))|^{2}\mathrm{d}\zeta

\displaystyle\leq C\,\big|q_{s}^{\epsilon}(u)-q_{s}(u)\big|^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{e}^{2\rho|q_{s}(u)+\zeta(q_{s}^{\epsilon}(u)-q_{s}(u))|^{2}}\mathrm{d}\zeta

\displaystyle\leq C\big|q_{s}^{\epsilon}(u)-q_{s}(u)\big|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{4\rho|q_{s}(u)|^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{4\rho|q_{s}^{\epsilon}(u)|^{2}}\;.

By Theorem
2.3
, we have for
u=(q,p)

|q_{s}(u)|^{2}\leq\kappa\left(|u|^{2}+U(q)\right)

for all
s\geq 0
. Inserting this bound in the exponential above and
noticing that

(7.17)

\big|q_{s}^{\epsilon}(u)-q_{s}(u)\big|^{2}\leq\big|X_{s}^{\epsilon}(u)-X_{s}(u)\big|^{2}\leq 2\left[\big|X_{s}^{\epsilon}(u)-Z_{s}^{\epsilon}(u)\big|^{2}+2\epsilon\big|Y_{s}(u)\big|^{2}\right]\;,

and then applying Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we get

A^{\epsilon}(1)\leq C\mathrm{e}^{4\rho\kappa\left(|u|^{2}+U(q)\right))}\int_{0}^{\delta^{\epsilon}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathrm{e}^{8\rho|X_{s}^{\epsilon}(u)|^{2}}\right]^{1/2}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\big|X_{s}^{\epsilon}(u)-Z_{s}^{\epsilon}(u)\big|^{4}\right]^{1/2}+2\epsilon\mathbb{E}\left[\big|Y_{s}\big|^{4}\right]^{1/2}\right)\mathrm{d}s\;.

Since
u\in\mathcal{D}_{r_{0}}
, by (
7.13
), for all small
enough
\epsilon>0
,

8\rho<\frac{1}{2(d+2)\kappa_{0}\left(H(u)+\frac{d\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)}\leq\frac{1}{2(d+2)\kappa_{0}\left(H(u)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda s}+\frac{d\epsilon}{\lambda}\right)}\;,

and therefore by Corollary
4.11
, Proposition
5.3

and Lemma
5.7
, we get

(7.18)

A^{\epsilon}(1)\leq C(n,\,r_{0})\epsilon^{1-2\nu}\delta^{\epsilon}\;.

On the other hand, by Theorem
2.3
, the second inequality
of (
7.17
), Proposition
5.3
and Lemma

5.7
, we also get

(7.19)

A^{\epsilon}(2)\leq C(n,\,r_{0})\epsilon^{1-2\nu}\delta^{\epsilon}\;.

Inserting (
7.15
), (
7.16
), (
7.18
) and
(
7.19
) to (
7.14
) yields

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x),Z_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x))\right)\leq C(n,\,r_{0})(\delta^{\epsilon}\epsilon^{-2\nu})^{1/2}+\frac{\kappa_{0}}{r_{0}^{2}}\left[H(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda(t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon})}+\frac{d\epsilon}{\lambda}\right]\;.

This completes the proof since
\delta^{\epsilon}\epsilon^{-2\nu}=\epsilon^{\sigma-2\nu}
and
\sigma>2\nu
by (
7.12
). Besides,
\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda(t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon})}
converge
to
0
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.
∎

Next we control the total variation distance between
Z_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x))

and
Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)
.

Proposition 7.7
.

Let
(t^{\epsilon})_{\epsilon>0}
be a sequence
of positive reals satisfying (
7.6
). Then, for all
r>0
,
it holds that

\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\sup_{x\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)),\,Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)\right)=0\;.

Proof.

Since
Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)=Z_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(Z_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x))
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)),\,Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(u),\,Z_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(u^{\prime})\right)\mathbb{P}(X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)\in\mathrm{d}u,\,Z_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)\in\mathrm{d}u^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{u,\,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(u),\,Z_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(u^{\prime})\right)\mathbb{P}(X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)\in\mathrm{d}u,\,Z_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)\in\mathrm{d}u^{\prime})

(7.20)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{P}(|X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)|>r)+\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)|>r)\;.

By the Markov inquality and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)|>r)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}(|X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)|^{2})}{r^{2}}\leq\frac{2}{r^{2}}\left[\mathbb{E}(|X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)-Z_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)|^{2})+\mathbb{E}(|Z_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)|^{2})\right]\;,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)|>r)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{r^{2}}\mathbb{E}(|Z_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)|^{2})\;.

Since by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have

\mathbb{E}(|Z_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)|^{2})\leq 2|X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}(x)|^{2}+4\epsilon\mathbb{E}(|Y_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}(x)|^{2})\;,

we get from Theorem
2.3
, Proposition
5.3

and Lemma
5.7
that

(7.21)

\mathbb{P}(|X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)|>r)+\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)|>r)\leq\frac{C(r)}{r^{2}}\left[\epsilon+\textup{e}^{-\lambda(t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon})}\right]\;.

Next we compute the integral at the right-hand side of (
7.20
).
By Lemma
6.8
and Proposition
5.3
,
this integral is bounded by

\displaystyle\int_{u,\,u^{\prime}\in\mathcal{D}_{r}}\left[\frac{C(r)}{(\delta^{\epsilon})^{3/2}\epsilon^{1/2}}|u-u^{\prime}|+\delta^{\epsilon}\right]\mathbb{P}(X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)\in\mathrm{d}u,\,Z_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)\in\mathrm{d}u^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C(r)}{(\delta^{\epsilon})^{3/2}\epsilon^{1/2}}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)-Z_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)|\right]+\delta^{\epsilon}\leq\frac{C(r)}{(\delta^{\epsilon})^{3/2}}\left[\frac{\textup{e}^{-\alpha_{1}(t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon})}}{\epsilon^{\nu}}+\epsilon^{\alpha_{2}}\right]+\delta^{\epsilon}\;.

By this computation, (
7.20
), and (
7.21
), we can conclude
that

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)),\,Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)\right)\leq C(r)\left[\epsilon+e^{-\lambda(t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon})}+\frac{1}{(\delta^{\epsilon})^{3/2}}\left(\frac{\textup{e}^{-\alpha_{1}(t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon})}}{\epsilon^{\nu}}+\epsilon^{\alpha_{2}}\right)\right]+\delta^{\epsilon}\;.

The right-hand side tends to
0
by (
7.6
),
(
7.11
) and (
7.12
).
∎

Now we are ready to prove Proposition
7.3
.

Proof of Proposition
7.3
.

By the triangle inequality, we have

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon},\,Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}\right)\leq\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon},\,Z_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x))\right)+\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(X_{t^{\epsilon}-\delta^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)),\,Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}\right)

and therefore the proof is completed by Propositions
7.6

and
7.7
.
∎

7.4.
Proof of Proposition
7.4

Proof of Proposition
7.4
.

By (
6.20
) and Lemma
6.5
(1),
we can write

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x),\,\mathcal{N}(0,2\epsilon\Sigma)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(X_{t^{\epsilon}}(x),\,2\epsilon\Sigma_{t^{\epsilon}}(x)),\,\mathcal{N}(0,2\epsilon\Sigma)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{X_{t^{\epsilon}}(x)}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}},\,\Sigma_{t^{\epsilon}}(x)\right),\,\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)\right)\;.

Therefore, by the triangle inequality and Lemma
6.5
(2),

\displaystyle\left|\,\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x),\mathcal{N}(0,2\epsilon\Sigma)\right)-\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{X_{t^{\epsilon}}(x)}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}},\,\Sigma\right),\,\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{X_{t^{\epsilon}}(x)}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}},\,\Sigma_{t^{\epsilon}}(x)\right),\,\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{X_{t^{\epsilon}}(x)}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}},\,\Sigma\right)\right)=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{t^{\epsilon}}(x)),\,\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)\right)\;.

By Lemma
6.2
and the fact that
t^{\epsilon}\rightarrow\infty

(by (
7.4
)), the right-hand side converges to
0
, and
hence, it suffices to prove that

(7.22)

\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\left|\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{X_{t^{\epsilon}}(x)}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}},\,\Sigma\right),\,\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)\right)-D^{\epsilon}(t^{\epsilon},\,x)\right|=0\;.

By Lemma
6.5
(3),

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{X_{t^{\epsilon}}(x)}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}},\,\Sigma\right),\,\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)\right)=\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\Sigma^{-1/2}\frac{X_{t^{\epsilon}}(x)}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}},\,\mathbb{I}_{2d}\right),\,\mathcal{N}(0,\,\mathbb{I}_{2d})\right)\;.

Hence, recalling the definitions (
7.4
) of
v(t,\,x)

and (
7.5
) of
D^{\epsilon}(t,\,x)
, and applying the
triangle inequality along with Lemmas
6.5
(2) and
6.6
,
we can bound the absolute value at (
7.22
) from above by

\displaystyle\left|\,\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\Sigma^{-1/2}\frac{X_{t^{\epsilon}}(x)}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}},\,\mathbb{I}_{2d}\right),\,\mathcal{N}(0,\,\mathbb{I}_{2d})\right)-\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{v(t^{\epsilon},\,x)}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}},\,\mathbb{I}_{2d}\right),\,\mathcal{N}(0,\,\mathbb{I}_{2d})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\epsilon}}\left(\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{t^{\epsilon}}(x)-v(t^{\epsilon},\,x)\right),\,\mathbb{I}_{2d}\right),\,\mathcal{N}\left(0,\,\mathbb{I}_{2d}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\epsilon}}\left|\Sigma^{-1/2}X_{t^{\epsilon}}(x)-v(t^{\epsilon},\,x)\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\epsilon}}\left|\frac{(t^{\epsilon}-\tau(x))^{\nu}}{\mathrm{e}^{\eta(t^{\epsilon}-\tau(x))}}\Sigma^{-1/2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\eta(t^{\epsilon}-\tau(x))}}{(t^{\epsilon}-\tau(x))^{\nu}}X_{t^{\epsilon}}-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathrm{e}^{i\theta_{k}(t^{\epsilon}-\tau(x))}v_{k}\right)\right|\;.

The last line converges to
0
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
by
Lemma
2.10
and since by (
7.8
) we have

\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow\infty}\frac{(t^{\epsilon})^{\nu}}{\epsilon^{1/2}\mathrm{e}^{\eta t^{\epsilon}}}<\infty\;.

∎

8.
Proof of Theorem
2.8

It only remains to prove Theorem
2.8
to conclude this
work. The proof of this theorem is also based on Propositions
7.3

and
7.4
. In addition to these propositions, we shall
need the following a priori variance bound on the stationary density

\mu_{\epsilon}
.

Lemma 8.1
.

There exists a constant
c>0

independent of
\epsilon
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|x|^{2}\mu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)\leq c\epsilon\;.

Proof.

This proof is similar to the proof provided in
[
2
]

and is inspired from
[
29
, p.122, Section 5]
.
Let
L>0
. Then, for all
t\geq 0
, by the stationarity of
\mu_{\epsilon}

and Jensen’s inequality we get

(8.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}(|x|^{2}\land L)\mu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)|^{2}\land L\right]\mu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[|X_{t}^{\epsilon}(x)|^{2}\right]\land L\right)\mu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)\;.

We bound the last expectation using Proposition
4.9
.
Then letting
t\rightarrow\infty
and applying the dominated convergence
theorem, we get

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}(|x|^{2}\land L)\mu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left(c_{3}\epsilon\land L\right)\mu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)\;.

Finally, letting
L\rightarrow\infty
and applying the monotone convergence
theorem completes the proof.
∎

The main ingredients in the proof of Theorem
2.8
are
Propositions
7.3
,
7.4
, and the following
lemma providing a rough a priori bound
\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\theta}}

on the mixing time.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
t_{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\theta}}

where
\theta
is the constant defined in Notation
5.2
.
Then, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have that

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x),\,\mu^{\epsilon}\right)=0\;.

Proof.

We fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}
. Denote by
p_{t}^{\epsilon}(\cdot,\,\cdot)

the transition kernel of the process
(X_{t}^{\epsilon})_{t\geq 0}
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x),\,\mu^{\epsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left|p_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x,\,z)-\mu^{\epsilon}(z)\right|\textup{d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left(p_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x,\,z)-p_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(y,\,z)\right)\mu^{\epsilon}(y)\,\textup{d}y\right|\textup{d}z

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\left|p_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x,\,z)-p_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(y,\,z)\right|\mu^{\epsilon}(y)\,\textup{d}y\textup{d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x),\,X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(y)\right)\mu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}y)\;.

Take
R
large enough so that
x\in\mathcal{D}_{R}
. By Lemma
8.1

and the Markov inequality, there exists
c>0
such that

\mu^{\epsilon}(\mathcal{D}_{R}^{c})\leq\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2d}}|x|^{2}\mu_{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}x)}{R^{2}}\leq\frac{c\epsilon}{R^{2}}

and therefore we get

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x),\,\mu^{\epsilon}\right)\leq\int_{\mathcal{D}_{R}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x),\,X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(y)\right)\mu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}y)+\frac{c\epsilon}{R^{2}}\;.

By Proposition
7.3
,

\limsup_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{\mathcal{D}_{R}}\left|\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x),\,X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(y)\right)-\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x),\,Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(y)\right)\right|\mu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}y)=0

since both
x
and
y
at the integrand belong to the set
\mathcal{D}_{R}
.
On the other hand, by Lemma
6.7
, we have that (note
that
x\in\mathcal{D}_{R}
)

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\int_{\mathcal{D}_{R}}\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x),\,Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(y)\right)\mu^{\epsilon}(\mathrm{d}y)=0

and the proof is completed.
∎

Now we prove Theorem
2.8
.

Proof of Theorem
2.8
.

Let
t^{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\theta}}
where
\theta
is
the constant defined in Notation
5.2
. Then, by the triangle
inequality, for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}

\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(0,2\epsilon\Sigma),\,\mu^{\epsilon}\right)\leq\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(0,2\epsilon\Sigma),\,Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)\right)+\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x),\,X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)\right)+\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(X_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x),\,\mu^{\epsilon}\right)\;.

By Proposition
7.3
and Lemma
8.2
, the
second and the third term at the right-hand side converge to
0

as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. On the other hand, by the triangle inequality,
the first term at the right-hand side is bounded from above by

\left|\mathrm{d}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(\mathcal{N}(0,2\epsilon\Sigma),\,Z_{t^{\epsilon}}^{\epsilon}(x)\right)-D^{\epsilon}(t^{\epsilon})\right|+D^{\epsilon}(t^{\epsilon})\;.

By Proposition
7.3
, the first term tends to
0
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
,
while it is easy to check that
D^{\epsilon}(t^{\epsilon})\rightarrow 0

for
t^{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{\theta}}
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.
This completes the proof.
∎
