Bordism classes of loops and Floer’s equation in cotangent bundles
Filip Broćić, Dylan Cant

Abstract
. For each representative 𝔅 \mathfrak{B} of a bordism class in the free loop space of a manifold, we associate a moduli space of finite length Floer cylinders in the cotangent bundle. The left end of the Floer cylinder is required to be a lift of one of the loops in 𝔅 \mathfrak{B} , and the right end is required to lie on the zero section. Under certain assumptions on the Hamiltonian functions, the length of the Floer cylinder is a smooth proper function, and evaluating the level sets at the right end produces a family of loops cobordant to 𝔅 \mathfrak{B} . The argument produces arbitrarily long Floer cylinders with certain properties. We apply this to prove an existence result for 1-periodic orbits of certain Hamiltonian systems in cotangent bundles, and also to estimate the relative Gromov width of starshaped domains in certain cotangent bundles. The moduli space is similar to moduli spaces considered in [ AS10 , Abo12 ] for Tonelli Hamiltonians. The Hamiltonians we consider are not Tonelli, but rather of “contact-type” in the symplectization end.



1.
Introduction

Let
M
be a compact
n
-dimensional manifold. This paper concerns the relationship between the free loop space of
M
and moduli spaces of solutions to Floer’s equation in
T^{*}M
. Such a relationship is well-known, and the most famous example is that the symplectic homology of
T^{*}M
is isomorphic to the homology of the free loop space. This is originally due to
[
Vit95
,
Vit96
]
, and was deduced using the generating function approach to symplectic homology of
[
Tra94
]
. Subsequently, other proofs were discovered, notably the heat-flow approach of
[
SW06
]
, and the Morse theory approach of
[
AS06
,
APS08
,
AS10
,
AS15
]
and
[
Abo12
,
Abo15
]
.

This paper concerns a different approach and relates bordism classes in the free loop space to moduli spaces of Floer cylinders. As application, we are able to prove an existence result for
1
-periodic orbits of a large class of Hamiltonian systems, Theorem
1.1
, and estimate the relative Gromov width of star-shaped domains in certain cotangent bundles (relative the zero section), Theorem
1.2
. Our approach also has the advantage of circumventing the need to consider indices, orientations of moduli spaces, or any chain complexes at all.

1.1.
Applications

We begin by explaining the applications of our method, and then describe the main technical result of the paper in §
1.2
.

1.1.1.
An existence theorem for contact-at-infinity Hamiltonian systems

A Hamiltonian system
H_{t}:T^{*}M\to\mathbb{R}
is called
contact-at-infinity
if it satisfies:

H_{t}(q,e^{s}p)=e^{s}H_{t}(q,p),

for all
s\geq 0
and all
(q,p)
outside of a compact set. The significance of such Hamiltonians is that their flow
\varphi_{t}
is equivariant with respect to the Liouville flow, outside of a compact set. It is well-known that quotient space:

Y=(T^{*}M-\text{zero section})/\mathbb{R}

inherits a canonical contact structure (as the ideal boundary of a Liouville manifold), and the flow of contact-at-infinity Hamiltonian induces a contact isotopy of
Y
.

As a special case, one can fix a starshaped domain
\Omega\subset T^{*}M
, as defined in §
2.2.1
, and define
r_{\Omega}:T^{*}M\to[0,\infty)
by requiring
r_{\Omega}(\partial\Omega)=1
and
r_{\Omega}(q,e^{s}p)=e^{s}r_{\Omega}(q,p)
. This function
r_{\Omega}
will not be smooth along the zero section, however, one can consider Hamiltonian systems
H_{t}
so that
H_{t}=ar_{\Omega}
outside a compact set. Such Hamiltonians are contact-at-infinity, and can be used to define a version of symplectic homology for the domain
\Omega
.

More generally, one can consider Hamiltonians which satisfy
H_{t}=(h_{t}\circ\pi)r_{\Omega}
outside a compact set, where
h_{t}:Y\to\mathbb{R}
is a 1-periodic family of functions and
\pi
is the quotient projection
(T^{*}M-\text{zero section})\to Y
. Such Hamiltonian systems are considered in, e.g.,
[
MU19
,
Ulj22
]
.

The first application of our methods is the following existence result:

Theorem 1.1
.

Any contact-at-infinity Hamiltonian
H_{t}
which is non-positive on a fiber
T^{*}M_{q_{0}}
will have at least one contractible 1-periodic orbit (we only require non-positivity outside of some starshaped domain).

A more general result is proved in Theorem
1.5
.

We remark that there are contact-at-infinity Hamiltonians which have no 1-periodic orbits. For example, one can take a canonical transformation lifting an isotopy in the base without 1-periodic orbits (on a manifold with vanishing Euler characteristic). In this case, the generating Hamiltonians are linear and non-vanishing on each fiber.

1.1.2.
An estimate on the relative Gromov width of starshaped domains

Recall that the

(W,L)

is a symplectic manifold and

which maps

onto
L

B(a)
is the ball of symplectic capacity
a
. This notion is considered in
[
BC06
,
BC07
,
BC09a
,
BC09b
,
BCS21
,
Bro23
]
.

Our method produces an estimate for the relative Gromov width of starshaped domains
\Omega\subset T^{*}M
under the assumption that
M
has an
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
-action
\zeta

Theorem 1.2

Let

One example is the manifold

where the action is

[

, Corollary 1.3]

In §
1.5.1

1.2.1.
Admissible data

Admissibility of

H_{t}\circ\rho_{\sigma}(z)=e^{\sigma}H_{t}(z)

J_{\rho_{\sigma}(z)}\mathrm{d}\rho_{\sigma,z}=\mathrm{d}\rho_{\sigma,z}J_{t,z}

and

. Moreover, we require that

has no 1-periodic orbits outside
\Omega

H_{t}
is
1

is translation invariant in the ends. See §
2.2.2
for more details.

1.2.2.
Bordism classes of loops

\mathfrak{B}_{1}:P_{1}\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to M
and
\mathfrak{B}_{2}:P_{2}\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to M
are

provided that the compact manifolds
P_{1},P_{2}
are cobordant, say
P_{1}\sqcup P_{2}=Q
, and there is a function

\mathfrak{B}_{1},\mathfrak{B}_{2}
at the two boundary components. Notice that the restricted evaluation maps
\mathfrak{B}_{1}(-,t_{0})
and
\mathfrak{B}_{1}(-,t_{0})
are also cobordant maps of
P_{1},P_{2}
into

.

1.2.3.
Sufficiently negative Hamiltonians

Let
\mathfrak{B}(\pi,t)
be a family of loops in
M
, and let
H_{t}
be an admissible Hamiltonian for a star-shaped domain
\Omega\subset T^{*}M
. Say that
H_{t}
is
sufficiently negative
for
\mathfrak{B},\Omega
provided that:

(1)

\gamma(t)\not\in\Omega\text{ for all $t$ and }\mathrm{pr}(\gamma(t))=\mathfrak{B}(\pi,t)\text{ for some }\pi\implies{A}(\gamma)\leq 0.

Here
{A}(\gamma)=\int H_{t}(\gamma)-\gamma^{*}\lambda
, see §
2.2.3
. Since
{A}(\rho_{\sigma}(\gamma))=e^{\sigma}{A}(\gamma)
for loops satisfying the hypothesis of (
1
), it is clear that the sufficiently negative condition is necessary to ensure an a priori bound on the action of lifts of
\mathfrak{B}
. The condition is also sufficient, as proved in Proposition
3.3
.

1.3.
Statement of the main result

Let
(H_{t},J_{t})
be admissible for a starshaped domain and
\mathfrak{B}
a family of loops, let
{M}:={M}(H_{t},J_{t},\mathfrak{B})
, and let
\ell:{M}\to(0,\infty)
be the coordinate projection
(u,\pi,\ell)\mapsto\ell
. Abbreviate
d=\dim P
.

Theorem 1.3
.

If
H_{t}
is sufficiently negative for
\mathfrak{B}
,
\Omega
, then
\ell:{M}\to(0,\infty)
is proper. Moreover, there exist
C^{\infty}
small perturbations
\delta_{t}
supported in
\Omega
so that

{M}^{\prime}:={M}(H_{t}+\delta_{t},J_{t},\mathfrak{B})

is a smooth manifold of dimension

. The map
\ell
is smooth, and for any regular value
\ell_{0}
, the fiber
{M}^{\prime}(\ell_{0}):={M}^{\prime}\cap\left\{\ell=\ell_{0}\right\}
is a compact

\mathrm{ev}_{\ell_{0}}:(u,\pi,\ell_{0},t)\in{M}^{\prime}(\ell_{0})\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\mapsto u(\ell_{0},t)\in M,

is smooth and cobordant (as a bordism class of loops) to the original class
\mathfrak{B}
. Moreover, the projection map
\pi:{M}^{\prime}(\ell_{0})\to P
has mapping degree
1
. In particular
{M}^{\prime}(\ell_{0})
is never empty.

The proof of the theorem is the main goal of the paper. The compactness statements are proved in §
3
, while the transversality and gluing statements are proved in §
4
.

One can interpret the main result as an
evolution equation
, in the sense that one begins with some initial condition
\mathfrak{B}
and evolves it using the non-linear Floer equation, obtaining cobordant families of loops. Unlike the typical
parabolic
evolution equations (such as heat flow), the Floer equation is an
elliptic
equation, and it is well-known that the flow is not well-posed. The sufficiently negative assumption in Theorem
1.3
is necessary to control the energy of the Floer cylinders; without this control one would not be able to conclude the existence of solutions for large values of the parameter
\ell
.

1.5.1.
Sharpness of the estimate

In this section we show that, for certain starshaped domains in
T^{*}M
, for
M=T^{n}
, the estimate in Theorem
1.2
is sharp. Let

M:=M(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=\mathbb{R}/a_{1}\mathbb{Z}\times\dots\times\mathbb{R}/a_{n}\mathbb{Z},

where
0<a_{1}\leq\dots\leq a_{n}

q_{1},\dots,q_{n}

\mathbb{R}/a_{i}\mathbb{Z}

p_{1},\dots,p_{n}

T^{*}M

\Omega(r):=\left\{p_{1}^{2}+\dots+p_{n}^{2}\leq r^{2}\right\}

Proposition 1.6
.

Let
\mathfrak{B}(\pi,t)=(q_{1}+a_{1}t,q_{2},\dots,q_{n})\in M

(\Omega(r),M)
equals
-2\kappa(\Omega(r),\mathfrak{B})=2a_{1}r

Proof

Observe that
\Omega(r)

[

B(2a_{1}r)\to D^{n}(a_{1}/2)\times D^{n}(r),

where
B(a)
is the ball of symplectic capacity
a
(i.e., has radius
(a/\pi)^{1/2}
). The symplectic embedding constructed maps
\mathbb{R}^{n}\cap B(a)
onto the disk
D^{n}(a_{1}/2)\times\left\{0\right\}
. Thus the relative Gromov width is at least
2a_{1}r
.

It remains to show that
-\kappa(\Omega(r),\mathfrak{B})=a_{1}r
. This follows from the calculations in the proof of Theorem
1.5
. Indeed, (
3
) implies:

\kappa(\Omega(r),\mathfrak{B})=\int_{0}^{1}\min_{\left|p\right|^{2}\leq r^{2}}\sum p_{i}q_{i}^{\prime}(t)\,\mathrm{d}t=-a_{1}r,

using that
q^{\prime}(t)=(a_{1},0,\dots,0)
. This completes the proof.

1.6.
Outline of paper and discussion of results

The rest of the paper is centered around proving Theorem
1.3
.

In §
2
, we gather various facts about Floer’s equation in Liouville manifolds (of which the cotangent bundle is a special case). A somewhat novel fact is the maximum principle we employ, §
2.2.5
. Our approach to the maximum principle is based on the arguments in
[
MU19
]
, but works for a larger class of complex structures. Unlike
[
MU19
]
, our approach does not rely on the Aleksandrov maximum principle, but assumes bounds on the first derivatives. In §
3.1.2
we explain how to obtain these first derivative bounds using bubbling analysis.

The compactness statements are in §
3
. In §
4
, we prove various transversality, index theory, and gluing statements.

We should emphasize that, throughout, we work with a fairly large class of complex structures, namely those which are
\omega
-tame and translation invariant in the ends. The main results could be proved using only complex structures which are of SFT type in the ends (à la
[
BEH+03
]
), but, in order to estimate the relative Gromov width in §

, one needs to allow arbitrary
\omega
-compatible complex structures near the zero section. This complicates the gluing analysis in §
4.3
compared to, e.g.,
[
AS10
, §4.6]
, as we cannot assume that
J_{p}TM_{p}=T^{*}M_{p}
holds for
p\in M
.

An

is a symplectic form. The associated
Liouville vector field

is defined by the equation
Z\mathbin{{\hbox to6.09pt{\vbox to6.09pt{\pgfpicture\makeatletter\hbox{\hskip 3.04544pt\lower-0.2pt\hbox to0.0pt{\lxSVG@begingroup@{_scopebegin} \lxSVG@begingroup@{stroke} \lxSVG@begingroup@{fill} \lxSVG@setlinewidth{\the\pgflinewidth}\lxSVG@begingroup@{stroke-width} \lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nullfont\hbox to0.0pt{\lxSVG@begingroup@{_scopebegin} {{}{{}}{}

. If

is the time
\sigma
flow by

\rho_{\sigma}^{*}\lambda=e^{\sigma}\lambda
.

A

is an exact symplectic manifold
W
whose Liouville flow

is complete and defines a free and proper
\mathbb{R}
-action. In this case, there is an odd-dimensional quotient space

. The kernel of the Liouville form

\xi
on

W
is the symplectization of
(Y,\xi)
, and typically denote it

.

A
Liouville manifold
is an exact symplectic manifold
W
which contains a compact

-invariant set
\mathrm{Sk}(W)
, called the

, so that:

(i)

the complement
\mathrm{Sk}(W)^{c}
is the symplectization of a compact manifold
Y
, and

(ii)

for any

,
\rho_{\sigma}(z)
eventually enters any neighborhood of
\mathrm{Sk}(W)^{c}
as
\sigma\to-\infty
,

Essentially, one thinks of a Liouville manifold as a symplectization with its negative end “filled.” Liouville manifolds are studied in greater detail in §

.

2.1.
Symplectizations

Let
W
be a symplectization and let
Y=W/\mathbb{R}
, with
\xi
being the projection of
\ker\lambda
. Note that
\xi
is cooriented by requiring that
\lambda
is positive on transverse vectors. The “exact symplectomorphism type” of
(W,\lambda)
is completely determined by the (cooriented) contactomorphism type of
(Y,\xi)
:

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(SY_{1},\lambda_{1}),(SY_{2},\lambda_{2})
be symplectizations. Every contactomorphism
\varphi:Y_{1}\to Y_{2}
preserving the coorientation is induced by a unique equivariant diffeomorphism
\Phi:SY_{1}\to SY_{2}
satisfying
\Phi^{*}\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{1}
.

Proof
.

The result is well-known and left to the reader.

Contact Hamiltonians

A smooth function

is called a

provided that one of the following equivalent conditions hold:

(i)

,

(ii)

H\circ\rho_{\sigma}=e^{\sigma}H
,

(iii)

X_{H}\mathbin{{\hbox to6.09pt{\vbox to6.09pt{\pgfpicture\makeatletter\hbox{\hskip 3.04544pt\lower-0.2pt\hbox to0.0pt{\lxSVG@begingroup@{_scopebegin} \lxSVG@begingroup@{stroke} \lxSVG@begingroup@{fill} \lxSVG@setlinewidth{\the\pgflinewidth}\lxSVG@begingroup@{stroke-width} \lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\nullfont\hbox to0.0pt{\lxSVG@begingroup@{_scopebegin} {{}{{}}{}

,

These conditions imply that the flow
\Phi_{t}
of

commutes with the Liouville flow. In particular,
\Phi_{t}
descends to a contact isotopy
\varphi_{t}:Y\to Y
. Every contact isotopy arises in this fashion.

If
Q
is a non-vanishing contact Hamiltonian, then
h=H/Q
is invariant under the Liouville flow, and hence
h
induces a function
Y\to\mathbb{R}
. Other authors often call functions

“contact Hamiltonians,” and this is related to our notion via this correspondence

. The additional data of a non-vanishing contact Hamiltonian is equivalent to a choice of contact form via
\alpha=\lambda/Q
.

2.1.2.
Legendrians and Lagrangians

A Lagrangian
L
in
SY
is called
cylindrical
if
L
is
Z
-invariant. In particular,
Z
is tangent to
L
. It follows that
\lambda|_{L}=0
, and hence cylindrical Lagrangians are exact (in a strong sense).

Cylindrical Lagrangians are lifts of Legendrians
\Lambda\subset Y
, and we will denote this fact by writing
L=S\Lambda
.

2.1.3.
Translation invariant complex structures and metrics

A complex structure
J
on
SY
is called
translation invariant
provided
J\mathrm{d}\rho_{\sigma}=\mathrm{d}\rho_{\sigma}J
. We say that
J
is
\omega
-
tame
provided
\omega(-,J-)
is a positive
2
-tensor, and say
J
is
\omega
-
compatible
if
\omega(-,J-)
is also symmetric. In either case, we denote:

\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{J}^{2}=\omega(v,Jv).

A Riemannian metric
\left|-\right|
is called
translation invariant
provided that
\left|\mathrm{d}\rho_{\sigma}v\right|=\left|v\right|
. We note that
\left\lVert-\right\rVert_{J}^{2}
is not translation invariant. However, if
J
is translation invariant and
Q
is a positive contact Hamiltonian then
\left|v\right|^{2}=Q(p)^{-1}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{J}^{2}
is a translation invariant metric (where
v\in T_{p}W
).

2.2.
Liouville manifolds

Let
(W,\lambda)
be a Liouville manifold with skeleton
\mathrm{Sk}(W)
. Let
SY=\mathrm{Sk}(W)^{c}
. The contact manifold
Y
is called the
ideal boundary
of
W
.

2.2.1.
Star-shaped domains

Let
\Sigma\subset SY
be the level set
\left\{r=1\right\}
for a positive contact Hamiltonian
r
. Then:

\Omega:=\left\{r\leq 1\right\}\cup\mathrm{Sk}(W)

is a compact codimension
0
submanifold in
W
(with boundary
\partial\Omega=\Sigma
). Domains of this form are referred to as
starshaped domains
. By construction, there is a bijection between starshaped domains and positive contact Hamiltonians on
SY
.

2.2.2.
Admissible Hamiltonians and complex structures

An
admissible Hamiltonian for a starshaped domain
\Omega
is a 1-periodic family of functions
H_{t}
which are contact Hamiltonians outside
\Omega
, and so that every solution to:

(4)

\gamma^{\prime}(t)=X_{H_{t}}(\gamma(t))

passes through the interior of
\Omega
. If there is no risk of ambiguity, we will abbreviate the associated Hamiltonian vector field by
X_{t}=X_{H_{t}}
. It can be shown that admissibility is a generic condition, essentially because the
1
-periodic orbits at infinity should be isolated, but they always occur in a 1-dimensional family (by the equivariance with the Liouville flow).

We say that a 1-periodic family of complex structures
J_{t}
is
admissible for
\Omega
provided
J_{t}
is
\omega
-tame and
J_{t}
is translation invariant on
\Omega^{c}
.

The key consequence of admissibility is the following estimate:

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
H_{t},J_{t}
be an admissible Hamiltonian and complex structure for
\Omega
. For any
N>0
there is
\sigma\geq 0
so that, for
C^{1}
loops
\gamma
,

\gamma(t)\not\in\rho_{\sigma}(\Omega)\text{ for all $t$}\implies\mathfrak{n}(\gamma):=\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\gamma^{\prime}(t)-X_{t}\right\rVert^{2}_{J_{t}}\mathrm{d}t>N.

Proof
.

See
[
MU19
, Lemma 3.6]
for a similar result. If
\gamma(t)\not\in\Omega
for all
t
, then compute
\mathfrak{n}(\rho_{s}(\gamma))=e^{s}\mathfrak{n}(\gamma)
for
s\geq 0
. In particular, if the result holds for
\sigma,N
, then it holds for
\sigma+s,e^{s}N
. In particular, it suffices to prove the result for
N
sufficiently small.

Suppose, in search of a contradiction, that the statement does not hold for any
N
with
\sigma=0
. Then we can find a sequence
\gamma_{n}
remaining outside of
\Omega
so that
\mathfrak{n}(\gamma_{n})\to 0
. The goal is to prove that
\gamma_{n}
converges to a periodic orbit of
X_{t}
, contradicting the admissibility condition.

We consider
\gamma(t)
as living in
SY
, noting that
X_{t}
extends to all of
SY
by equivariance with Liouville flow.

Fix a translation invariant metric
\left|v\right|^{2}
, which is comparable to
Q^{-1}\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{J_{t}}^{2}
since
J_{t}
is translation invariant. Since
Q
is bounded from below on
\Omega^{c}
, conclude:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\left|\gamma_{n}^{\prime}(t)-X_{t}(\gamma_{n})\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t=0

Since
\left|X_{t}\right|
is uniformly bounded, we conclude that
\int\left|\gamma_{n}^{\prime}(t)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t
is bounded. It follows that
\gamma_{n}
has bounded diameter and is equicontinuous, and hence converges in
C^{0}
to a
1
-periodic limit
\gamma_{\infty}
(potentially after flowing by the Liouville flow and passing to a subsequence).

Let
\mu(t)
be the unique solution of
\mu^{\prime}(t)=X_{t}(\mu(t))
with
\mu(t_{0})=\gamma_{\infty}(t_{0})
(we do not claim that
\mu
is periodic). We claim that
\mu(t)=\gamma_{\infty}(t)
on a neighborhood of
t_{0}
. Without loss, work in coordinate charts, set
t_{0}=0
, and estimate:

\left|\gamma_{n}(t)-\mu(t)\right|\leq\epsilon_{n}+t^{1/2}[\int_{0}^{t}\left|\gamma_{n}^{\prime}(t)-X_{t}(\gamma_{n})\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t]^{1/2}+C_{X}t\max_{0\leq\tau\leq t}\left|\gamma_{n}(\tau)-\mu(\tau)\right|,

where
\epsilon_{n}=\left|\gamma_{n}(0)-\gamma_{\infty}(0)\right|
. Here we have used the fact that:

\int_{0}^{t}\left|X_{t}(\gamma_{n}(\tau))-X_{t}(\mu(\tau))\right|\mathrm{d}\tau\leq C_{X}t\max_{0\leq\tau\leq t}\left|\gamma_{n}(\tau)-\mu(\tau)\right|,

so
C_{X}
depends on the
C^{1}
size of
X
. For
t
small, we have
C_{X}t<1/2
. One obtains:

\max_{0\leq\tau\leq t}\left|\gamma_{n}(\tau)-\mu(\tau)\right|\leq 2\epsilon_{n}+2t^{1/2}[\int_{0}^{t}\left|\gamma_{n}^{\prime}(t)-X_{t}(\gamma_{n})\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}t]^{1/2},

and taking the limit
n\to\infty
one concludes, for
t
small, that
\gamma_{\infty}(t)=\mu(t)
. It follows that
\gamma_{\infty}(t)
is an integral curve for
X_{t}
, and hence
X_{t}
has a periodic orbit outside of
\Omega
. This contradicts admissibility, and completes the proof.

2.2.3.
Hamiltonian action functional

Given an admissible Hamiltonian
H_{t}
, the associated action functional is defined for
\gamma\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z},W)
and is given by:

{A}(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}H_{t}(\gamma(t))\mathrm{d}t-\gamma^{*}\lambda.

The critical points of
{A}
are precisely the solutions to (
4
).

2.2.4.
Floer cylinders

Given data
H_{t},J_{t}
admissible for
\Omega
the
Floer equation
is:

(5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u:[a,b]\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to W\\
&\partial_{s}u+J_{t}(u)(\partial_{t}u-X_{t}(u))=0,\end{aligned}\right.

allowing
a=-\infty
or
b=+\infty
. Abbreviate
u(s)=u(s,-)
, so that
u(s)
is considered as a loop. The Floer equation is interpreted as the negative gradient flow for the symplectic action:

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
u
solve (
5
), and let
{A}
be the action functional for
H_{t}
. Then:

E(u)=\int\left\lVert\partial_{s}u\right\rVert_{J_{t}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t=\int\left\lVert\partial_{t}u-X_{t}(u)\right\rVert_{J_{t}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t={A}(u(a))-{A}(u(b)).

Proof
.

The computation is standard, and is left to the reader.

2.2.5.
Maximum principle

The next result is a crucial ingredient in proving various moduli spaces are compact.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
u_{n}:[a_{n},b_{n}]\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to W
be a sequence of Floer cylinders so that:

(i)

H_{t},J_{t}
are admissble for
\Omega
,

(ii)

b_{n}-a_{n}
remains bounded from below by a positive number,

(iii)

E(u_{n})
remains bounded,

(iv)

\left|\partial_{s}u_{n}\right|
,
\left|\partial_{t}u_{n}\right|
remain bounded (for a metric which is translation invariant outside of
\Omega
)

Then there is
\sigma\geq 0
so that
u_{n}([a_{n},b_{n}]\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})\subset\rho_{\sigma}(\Omega)
for all
n
.

Proof
.

See
[
MU19
, Theorem 3.10]
for a similar result. Without loss of generality, suppose that
a_{n}=0
and
b_{n}=\ell_{n}
. The case when
a_{n}=-\infty
or
b_{n}=\infty
is similar. Suppose that
\ell_{n}\geq\ell
,
E(u_{n})\leq E
, and
\left|\partial_{s}u_{n}\right|,\left|\partial_{t}u_{n}\right|
remain bounded by
C
.

Proposition
2.2
with
N=2E/\ell
guarantees some
\sigma_{0}
so that

\gamma(t)\not\in\rho_{\sigma_{0}}(\Omega)\text{ for all $t$}\implies\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\gamma^{\prime}-X_{t}(\gamma)\right\rVert_{J_{t}}^{2}\mathrm{d}t>2E/\ell.

Since

E(u_{n})=\int_{0}^{\ell_{n}}\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\partial_{t}u_{n}-X_{t}(u_{n})\right\rVert_{J_{t}}^{2}\mathrm{d}t\leq E,

we conclude that, for every
s
, there is
s^{\prime}
so
\left|s-s^{\prime}\right|\leq\ell/2
so that
u_{n}(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})\in\rho_{\sigma_{0}}(\Omega)
for some
t^{\prime}
. But then, since the first derivative is bounded by
C
, we conclude that, for any
t
,

\mathrm{dist}(u_{n}(s,t),\rho_{\sigma_{0}}(\Omega))\leq C\left|t-t^{\prime}\right|+C\left|s-s^{\prime}\right|\leq C(1+\ell/2),

Clearly we can pick
\sigma
large enough so that the
C(1+\ell/2)
ball (as measured by the translation invariant metric) around
\rho_{\sigma_{0}}(\Omega)
is contained in
\rho_{\sigma}(\Omega)
. It follows that
u(s,t)\in\rho_{\sigma}(\Omega)
for all
s,t
. Clearly
\sigma
depends only on
\ell,C,E
. This completes the proof.

The requirement that the first derivatives of
u_{n}
are bounded is often achievable using bubbling analysis, see §
3.1.2
.

2.2.6.
The monotonicity theorem for Darboux balls

Let
B(a)\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be the open ball of capacity
a
, i.e., if
\Pi
is a complex line, then
\Pi\cap B(a)
has symplectic area
a
. The monotonicity theorem asserts that every holomorphic curve which “properly” passes through the center of the ball has symplectic area at least
a
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
(\Sigma,\partial\Sigma,j)
be a compact Riemann surface and
u:\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a holomorphic curve (for the standard complex structure) so that
u(\partial\Sigma)\cap B(a)=\emptyset
and
u^{-1}(0)\neq\emptyset
. Then the symplectic area of
u
is at least
a
.

Proof
.

We sketch the argument. See
[
Wen15
, §5.2]
for more details.

It suffices to prove the theorem when
a=1
. Let
Q(z)=\pi\left|z\right|^{2}
, so
Q<1
cuts out
B(1)
. Interpret
SY=\mathbb{C}^{n}-\left\{0\right\}
as the symplectization of the standard contact sphere
Y
, so that
Q
is a contact Hamiltonian.

Analytic continuation implies that
u^{-1}(0)
is a finite (non-empty) set
\Gamma
. Interpret
u
as a
punctured
holomorphic map
\Sigma-\Gamma\to SY
, so that
\partial\Sigma
is mapped into in the region
Q\geq 1
. It can be shown that the standard complex structure is of SFT type (for the standard Reeb field
R=X_{Q}
), in the sense of
[
BEH+03
]
, and thus
u
will be asymptotic at its punctures to Reeb orbits. The contact form is
\alpha=\lambda/Q
. Standard results from SFT then imply that the integral of
\mathrm{d}\alpha
will be at least the minimal positive action of a Reeb orbit. It is not hard to see that:

\text{minimal positive action}\leq\int_{\Sigma-\Gamma}\mathrm{d}\alpha=\int_{\partial\Sigma}\alpha\leq\int_{\partial\Sigma}\lambda=\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}\lambda=\text{symplectic area}.

It is also a standard fact that, with our conventions, the minimal positive action of a Reeb orbit is exactly
1
. Thus we conclude the desired result.

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
(\Sigma,\partial\Sigma)
be a compact Riemann surface,
\partial\Sigma=\partial\Sigma_{1}\sqcup\partial\Sigma_{2},
and suppose
u:\Sigma\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
is holomorphic with
u(\partial\Sigma_{1})\cap B(a)=\emptyset
,
u(\partial\Sigma_{2})\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
u^{-1}(0)\neq\emptyset
. Then the symplectic area of
u
is at least
a/2
.

Proof
.

Use the Schwarz reflection principle and apply the previous result, bearing in mind we are using the standard complex structure.

3.
A priori energy bounds and compactness of the moduli space

3.1.
Energy bounds imply bounds on all derivatives

The results in this section explain why, for Floer’s equation on Liouville manifolds, energy bounds are sufficient to obtain strong compactness results. In general, energy bounds are
not
sufficient because of the bubbling phenomenon, but bubbling can be precluded in Liouville manifolds.

3.1.1.
Elliptic regularity

Let
W
be an almost complex manifold. The elliptic regularity result in this section show that
C^{1}
bounds are enough to ensure convergence results for sequences of solutions to holomorphic type equations. The result is stated for maps defined on either
\Sigma(1)=D(1)
, in which case
\partial\Sigma(1)=\emptyset
, or
\Sigma(1)=D(1)\cap\overline{\mathbb{H}}
, in which case
\partial\Sigma(1)=D(1)\cap\mathbb{R}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose that
J_{n,z}\to J_{\infty,z}
,
A_{n,z}\to A_{\infty,z}
,
L_{n,z}\to L_{\infty,z}
are
C^{\infty}
convergent sequences of complex structures, vector fields, and totally real submanifolds, respectively, and
u_{n}:\Sigma(1)\to W
satisfies:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{s}u_{n}+J_{n,z}(u_{n})\partial_{t}u_{n}=A_{n,z}(u_{n})\\
&u_{n}(z)\in L_{n,z}\text{ for }z\in\partial\Sigma(1)\end{aligned}\right.

Suppose
u_{n}(0)
converges and
\left|\partial_{s}u_{n}\right|,\left|\partial_{t}u_{n}\right|
remain bounded, as measured by a complete metric on the target
W
. Then there is a smooth map
u_{\infty}
satisfying:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{s}u_{\infty}+J_{\infty,z}(u_{\infty})\partial_{t}u_{\infty}=A_{\infty,z}(u_{\infty})\\
&u_{\infty}(z)\in L_{\infty,z}\text{ for }z\in\partial\Sigma(1)\end{aligned}\right.

so that
u_{n}
has a subsequence converging to
u_{\infty}
in
C^{\infty}(\Sigma(r),W)
for every
r<1
.

Proof
.

The result is a well-known bootstrapping argument. One reduction is that it suffices to prove the result for
r
sufficiently small (and then apply the result to a covering by small disks). See
[
Wen20
, Proof of Theorem 2.24]
. Another reduction is that one can replace
u_{n}(z)
by
\Phi_{n,z}(u_{n}(z))
so that
\Phi_{n,z}
converges and
\Phi_{n,z}(L_{n,z})=L
for a fixed totally real submanifold
L
. In coordinates near the limit of
u_{n}(0)
, one can arrange that
L
is
\mathbb{R}^{n}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Moreover, one can apply a
z
-dependent coordinate change so that
J|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}=J_{0}|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
is the standard complex structure. For a final reduction, replace
u_{n}(s,t)
by
u_{n}(s,t)+a_{n}(s)+tb_{n}(s)
where
a_{n},b_{n}
are
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued. Then we can arrange so that
\partial_{s}u_{n}+J_{0}\partial_{t}u_{n}=0
holds along
\partial\Sigma(r)
. The
C^{\infty}
size of
a_{n},b_{n}
(and the other coordinate changes) are controlled by the
C^{\infty}
sizes of
A_{n,z}
,
J_{n,z}
,
L_{n,z}
.

Given an equation of the form
\partial_{s}u+J_{z}(u)\partial_{t}u_{n}=A_{z}(u)
, one can apply
\partial_{s}-J_{z}(u)\partial_{t}
to both sides to obtain an equation involving the standard Laplacian
\Delta=\partial_{s}^{2}+\partial_{t}^{2}
.

One then shows that
u_{n}
is bounded in
W^{k,2}(\Sigma(r))
for every
k
by using the elliptic estimates for the Laplacian with Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions (applied to the real and imaginary parts), see
[
RS01
, Appendix C]
and
[
CC23
]
. Then one uses the Sobolev embedding theorem to prove that
u_{n}
is bounded in
C^{k}(\Sigma(r))
for all
k
. A standard extension of the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem ensures that
C^{k}
bounds for all
k
ensures subsequences which converge in
C^{\infty}
. The details are left to the reader.

3.1.2.
A priori bounds on first derivatives

Bubbling analysis for holomorphic curve type equations allows one to conclude that either a sequence
u_{n}
has a bounded first derivative, or there exists a non-constant holomorphic plane
v:\mathbb{C}\to W
or half-plane with bounded first derivative. One obtains
v
by rescaling shrinking regions on the domain of
u_{n}
, and then applying the elliptic regularity results from §
3.1.1
. The energy of
v
will be bounded from above by the limiting energy of
u_{n}
, using the conformal invariance of energy. For details on bubbling analysis see
[
CC23
, Appendix C]
.

In Liouville manifolds and symplectizations, there are no finite energy holomorphic planes or half planes, and hence
C^{1}
bounds follow from energy bounds. The precise non-existence statement is:

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
\Sigma=\mathbb{C}
or
\Sigma=\overline{\mathbb{H}}
. Let
(W,\lambda)
be an exact symplectic manifold, let
L
be an exact Lagrangian, and let
J
be an
\omega
-tame complex structure. There is no non-constant holomorphic map
u:\Sigma\to(W,L)
whose image lies in a compact set. In addition, if
W
is a Liouville manifold (or positive half of a symplectization),
L
is cylindrical outside a compact set, and
J
is translation invariant outside a compact set, then there are no non-constant holomorphic maps
u:\Sigma\to(W,L)
with finite symplectic area.

Proof
.

We argue by contradiction. The first part is proved via an elementary differential inequality. Let
\Sigma(r)=\Sigma\cap D(r)
, and define:

A(r)=\int_{\Sigma(r)}\left\lVert\partial_{s}u\right\rVert^{2}_{J_{s}}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t=\int_{\Sigma(r)}u^{*}\mathrm{d}\lambda.

Since
u
is non-constant,
A(r)
is positive. Using Stokes’ theorem, estimate:

A(r)^{2}\leq CrA^{\prime}(r)\implies\frac{1}{C}\log(r_{2}/r_{1})\leq\frac{1}{A(r_{1})}-\frac{1}{A(r_{2})},

where
r_{1}<r_{2}
and
C
depends on the (bounded) size of
\lambda
on the image of
u
(which we assume is precompact). Take the limit as
r_{2}\to\infty
to conclude a contradiction.

For the second part, we appeal to the following fact: for every
\delta>0
, there is an
\epsilon>0
so that, if a holomorphic cylinder/strip
u:[-R-1,R+1]\times S\to W
, where
S=\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
or
S=[0,1]
, satisfies
\int\left|\partial_{s}u\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t<\epsilon
, then the diameter of
u([-R,R]\times S)
is less than
\delta
(using some metric which is translation invariant in the ends). In the case when
S=[0,1]
we require that
u
takes boundary values on
L
. This fact can be proved using the mean-value property for the energy density
\left|\partial_{s}u\right|^{2}
, see
[
MS12
,
RS01
]
. A similar argument is given in
[
CC23
, Proposition 6.1]
.

Since
u
has finite symplectic area, and
\left|-\right|=Q^{-1}\left\lVert-\right\rVert_{J}
defines a translation invariant metric, we conclude that regions of the form
\Sigma\setminus\Sigma(r)
have bounded diameter for
r
sufficiently large. Since the translation invariant metric is complete, this implies that
u
has a precompact image, and hence we can apply the first part. This completes the proof.

3.2.
A priori energy estimate for sufficiently negative Hamiltonians

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose that
H_{t}
is sufficiently negative for
\Omega,\mathfrak{B}
, and let
\gamma(t)
be a lift of
\mathfrak{B}(\pi,t)
for some
\pi
. Then:

(6)

{A}(\gamma)\leq\sup\left\{{A}(\eta):\eta(t)\in\Omega\text{ for all $t$, and $\eta$ is a lift of $\mathfrak{B}$}\right\}<\infty.

Proof
.

Let
r
be a contact Hamiltonian so that
r(\partial\Omega)=1
. If
\gamma(t)\in\Omega
for all
t
, then we are done. Otherwise, there is some interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]\subset\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
so that
r(\gamma(t))>1
for all
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
. Consider the contribution to the action coming from this arc:

a=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}H_{t}(\gamma(t))\mathrm{d}t-\gamma^{*}\lambda.

We claim that
a\leq 0
. To see why, pick some loop
\xi(t)
lifting
\mathfrak{B}(\pi,t)
so
\xi(t)=\gamma(t)
for
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
and
r(\xi(t))>1
for all
t
. Then, if
a>0
, we can use positive functions
\beta(t)
supported in
(t_{0},t_{1})
to make
{A}(\rho_{\beta(t)}\xi(t))
arbitrarily large, using the identity:

(7)

{A}(\rho_{\beta(t)}\xi(t))=\int_{0}^{1}e^{\beta(t)}(H_{t}(\xi(t))\mathrm{d}t-\xi^{*}\lambda).

This contradicts the hypothesis that
H_{t}
was sufficiently negative for
\mathfrak{B}
.

Since
t_{0},t_{1}
were arbitrary, we conclude that
H_{t}(\gamma(t))\mathrm{d}t-\gamma^{*}\lambda\leq 0
holds pointwise whenever
r(\gamma(t))>1
(using standard orientation of
\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
). Pick
\beta(t)=-\log(r(\gamma(t)))
for
t\in[t_{0},t_{1}]
and suppose
\beta(t)\leq 0
is supported in region where
r(\gamma(t))>1
. Then:

{A}(\gamma(t))\leq{A}(\rho_{\beta(t)}\gamma(t)),

using (
7
). We have achieved
\rho_{\beta(t)}\gamma(t)\in\Omega
. Repeating this argument shows that
{A}(\gamma)
is less than the action of a loop which arbitrarily close to
\Omega
. Taking a limit completes the proof.

Combining Propositions
3.3
and
2.3
, one concludes that, if
H_{t}
is sufficiently negative for
\Omega,\mathfrak{B}
, then, for any
(u,\pi,\ell)\in{M}(H_{t},J_{t},\mathfrak{B})
,

\displaystyle E(u)={A}(u(0))-{A}(u(\ell))

\displaystyle\leq\sup\left\{{A}(\eta):\eta(t)\in\Omega\text{ and }\eta\text{ lifts $\mathfrak{B}$}\right\}-\int_{0}^{1}\min_{M}H_{t}\,\mathrm{d}t.

and a straightforward estimate for
{A}(\eta)
completes the proof of Lemma
1.4
.

3.3.
Compactness of the moduli space

Let
{M}={M}(\mathfrak{B},H_{t},J_{t})
be the moduli space from §
1.2
, and suppose that
H_{t}
is sufficiently negative for
\mathfrak{B}
.

The results of §
3.2
imply that any sequence
(u_{n},\pi_{n},\ell_{n})\in{M}
will satisfy an a priori energy bound
E(u_{n})\leq E({M})<\infty
. If
\ell_{n}
remains bounded from below, there is enough room in the domain to perform bubbling analysis, and we conclude from §
3.1.2
that the derivatives of
u_{n}
will be bounded. The maximum principle §
2.2.5
applies and we conclude
u_{n}
satsfies
C^{0}
bounds.

If
\ell_{n}
also remains bounded from above, then standard applications of Arzelà-Ascoli and elliptic regularity imply that
(u_{n},\pi_{n},\ell_{n})
will converge, after passing to a subsequence. In other words, the map
\ell:{M}\to(0,\infty)
is proper. This completes the proof of the properness part of Theorem
1.3
.

It remains to analyze the case when
\ell_{n}\to 0
and when
\ell_{n}\to\infty
.

3.3.1.
Length shrinking to zero

Assume
H_{t}
is sufficiently negative for
\mathfrak{B}
, to ensure the a priori energy bound. If
(u_{n},\pi_{n},\ell_{n})
is a sequence in
{M}
and
\ell_{n}\to 0
, then the domain
[0,\ell_{n}]\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
degenerates and there may not be enough space to apply bubbling analysis. However, one can apply the conformal reparametrization:

v_{n}:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}/\ell_{n}^{-1}\mathbb{Z}\hskip 28.45274ptv_{n}(s,t)=u_{n}(\ell_{n}s,\ell_{n}t),

to obtain a new sequence with better control on the domain. The Floer equation for
u_{n}
implies the following equation for
v_{n}
:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{s}v_{n}+J_{\ell_{n}t}(v_{n})(\partial_{t}v_{n}-\ell_{n}X_{\ell_{n}t}(v_{n}))=0,\\
&\mathrm{pr}\circ v_{n}(0,t)=\mathfrak{B}(\pi_{n},\ell_{n}t),\\
&v_{n}(1,t)\in M.\end{aligned}\right.

The bubbling analysis from §
3.1.2
can be applied and one concludes that
v_{n}
has a bounded first derivative. In particular
v_{n}
remains a bounded distance from the zero section (using the fact that its right end lies on the zero section).

If
\left|\partial_{s}v_{n}\right|
does not converge to zero, there is
s_{n},t_{n},\epsilon
so that
\left|\partial_{s}v_{n}(s_{n},t_{n})\right|>\epsilon
for some subsequence. Pass to a further subsequence to ensure that
\ell_{n}t_{n}
and
\pi_{n}
converge to some
t_{\infty}\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
,
\pi_{\infty}\in P
, respectively. By the elliptic regularity result in §
3.1.1
, the rotated solutions:

w_{n}(s,t)=v_{n}(s,t+t_{n}),

have a subsequence which converges on compact sets to a
non-constant
solution
w_{\infty}
of the problem:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &w_{\infty}:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}\to W,\\
&\partial_{s}w_{\infty}+J_{t_{\infty}}(w_{\infty})\partial_{t}w_{\infty}=0,\\
&\mathrm{pr}\circ w_{\infty}(0,t)=\mathfrak{B}(\pi_{\infty},t_{\infty}),\\
&w_{\infty}(1,t)\in M.\end{aligned}\right.

Since
w_{\infty}
is holomorphic and has boundary on Lagrangians on which the primitive
\lambda
vanishes,
w_{\infty}
must have zero energy and hence must be constant. This contradicts our assumption, and hence the derivatives
\left|\partial_{s}v_{n}\right|
must uniformly converge to zero.

This line of reasoning implies:

Proposition 3.4
.

For
\epsilon>0
, there is
\ell_{0}=\ell_{0}(H_{t},J_{t},\mathfrak{B})
so that every
(u,\pi,\ell)\in{M}
with
\ell\leq\ell_{0}
satisfies
\mathrm{dist}(u(s,t),\mathfrak{B}(\pi,t))<\epsilon.

Proof
.

Suppose not. Then there is a sequence
u_{n},\pi_{n},\ell_{n}
with
\ell_{n}\to 0
but which remains
\epsilon
far from
\mathfrak{B}(\pi_{n},t)
. The conformal reparametrization
v_{n}(s,t)=u_{n}(\ell_{n}s,\ell_{n}t)
then remains
\epsilon
far from
\mathfrak{B}(\pi_{n},t\ell_{n})
(in the uniform distance). As shown above, the derivatives of
v_{n}
converge to zero. Since the left end of
v_{n}
is a lift of
\mathfrak{B}(\pi_{n},t\ell_{n})
and the right end lies on the zero section, the distance between
v_{n}(s,t)
and
\mathfrak{B}(\pi_{n},t\ell_{n})
must eventually be uniformly less than
\epsilon
, as desired.

3.3.2.
Length diverging to infinity

Consider a sequence
(u_{n},\pi_{n},\ell_{n})
with
\ell_{n}\to\infty
and the reparametrizations
w_{n}(s,t)=v_{n}(s+\ell_{n},t)
defined on
(-\ell_{n},0]\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. The gradient bound and consequent maximum principle imply that
w_{n}
converges on compact sets to a limit map
w:(-\infty,0]\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to W
. The elliptic regularity results from §
3.1.1
imply the convergence is in
C^{\infty}
on compact sets, the limit
w
is smooth and satisfies:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &w:(-\infty,0]\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to T^{*}M,\\
&\partial_{s}w+J_{t}(w)(\partial_{t}w-X_{H_{t}}(w))=0,\\
&w(0,t)\in M.\end{aligned}\right.

Convergence on compact sets implies:

\int\left\lVert\partial_{s}w\right\rVert_{J_{t}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int\left\lVert\partial_{s}u_{n}\right\rVert_{J_{t}}^{2}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t.

Since
\left\lVert\partial_{s}w\right\rVert_{J_{t}}^{2}=\left\lVert\partial_{t}w-X_{H_{t}}(w)\right\rVert_{J_{t}}^{2}
, a standard argument in Floer theory implies that, for every sequence
s_{n}\to-\infty
, there is a subsequence so
w(s_{n},-)
converges to a 1-periodic orbit for
X_{H_{t}}
. This is sufficient for the results in §
1.4
and §
1.5
.

4.
Transversality, index theory, and gluing

The goals of this section are to prove the moduli space
{M}
is a manifold whose dimension equals
\dim P+1
(transversality and index theory), and to give an explicit description of the moduli space when the length parameter
\ell
tends to zero (gluing).

4.1.
Transversality

The arguments in this section are standard. Experts can safely skip to §
4.2
.

4.1.1.
Injectivity results for Floer cylinders

It is well-known that moduli spaces of holomorphic maps will be transverse for generic domain independent data near sufficiently injective maps. At the other extreme, one can achieve transversality without any injectivity assumptions if one allows domain dependent data. The Floer equation (
5
) is only partially domain dependent, as the data is
s
-independent.

Fix
u:[a,b]\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to W
solving Floer’s equation (
5
) with
-\infty<a<b<\infty
. Following
[
FHS95
]
, let us say that a point
(s_{0},t_{0})
is
regular
provided that (i)
\partial_{s}u(s_{0},t_{0})\neq 0
, and (ii)
u(s,t_{0})=u(s_{0},t_{0})
if and only if
s=s_{0}
. Roughly speaking, regular points are “injective points” for the slices
t=t_{0}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose that
\partial_{s}u
is not identically zero. Then the set of regular points is open and dense.

Proof
.

The argument follows
[
FHS95
, Theorem 4.3]
. That the regular points are open is straightforward to show, and is left to the reader.

One shows
\xi=\partial_{s}u
solves an equation of the form:

\partial_{s}\xi+J(z)\partial_{t}\xi+A(z)\xi=0.

Then the similarity principle, see
[
FHS95
, Theorem 2.2]
or
[
Wen20
, Theorem 2.32]
, implies that
\xi=\partial_{s}u
is either identically zero or has isolated zeroes. Since we assume
\partial_{s}u
is not identically zero, the set of points where (i) holds is open and dense.

Next, suppose that
z_{0}=(s_{0},t_{0})
satisfies (i) and
s_{0}\in(a,b)
, but
z_{0}
cannot be approximated by points which satisfy (ii). Let
P_{-}(z_{0})=(a,t_{0})
and
P_{+}(z_{0})=(b,t_{0})
. By slightly shifting
z_{0}
, we may suppose that the
\delta
-square around
z_{0}
, denoted
Q_{\delta}(z_{0})
, satisfies
u(Q_{\delta}(z_{0}))\cap u(Q_{\delta}(P_{\pm}(z_{0})))=\emptyset
for
\delta
sufficiently small (this uses that
\partial_{s}u(z_{0})\neq 0
). Roughly speaking,
u(z_{0})
remains away from the pieces of the left and right boundary circles with the same
t
coordinate.

By the argument in the first paragraph, there are only finitely many points
z
in the rectangle
[a+\delta,b-\delta]\times[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}+\delta]
with
\partial_{s}u(z)=0
. Abbreviate
U(z)=(t(z),u(z))
. Shifting
z_{0}
and shrinking
\delta
, we may further suppose that:

z_{1}\in Q_{\delta}(z_{0})\text{ and }U(z_{2})=U(z_{1})\implies\partial_{s}u(z_{2})\neq 0.

By the covering argument given on
[
FHS95
, pp. 262]
, one can fix
\epsilon
and construct:

\phi:Q_{\delta}(z_{0})\to(a,b)\times(t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}+\delta)

so that
\phi(z)\neq z
,
U(\phi(z))=U(z)
, and so the diameter of
\phi
is smaller than
\epsilon
(after shifting
z_{0}
slightly and shrinking
\delta
small enough). Morally, the fact that we cannot make
U
injective near
z_{0}
is used to construct
\phi
.

Then
u(\phi(z))=u(z)
for
\phi(s,t)=(f(s,t),t)
. Since the
s
-derivative of
u
is non-zero on the image of
\phi
, and
\phi
has a small diameter,
f(s,t)
is smooth (appealing to the constant rank version of the inverse function theorem). Invoking the Floer equation yields:

\partial_{s}u(\phi)\cdot\partial_{s}f+J(u(\phi))\partial_{s}u(\phi)\cdot\partial_{t}f=-J(u(\phi))(\partial_{t}u(\phi)-X_{t}(u(\phi))).

Since
\partial_{s}u
and
J(u)\partial_{s}u
are linearly independent, there is a unique solution
\partial_{s}f=1
and
\partial_{t}f=0
. Thus we conclude
u(s+s^{\prime},t)=u(s,t)
for some
s^{\prime}\neq 0
. This holds for
s,t\in Q_{\delta}(z_{0})
, but it extends to the entire circle
\left\{s_{0}\right\}\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
by unique continuation.

Finally, the fact that the actions of the two loops
u(s_{0},-)
and
u(s_{0}+s^{\prime},-)
are the same implies that the energy of
u
restricted to the region
[s_{0},s_{0}+s^{\prime}]\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
vanishes. This contradicts the fact that
\partial_{s}u
has isolated zeroes, and this completes the proof.

4.1.2.
Linearization framework and the Sard-Smale theorem

In order to prove the moduli space has the structure of a finite dimensional manifold, one embeds it into an appropriate Banach manifold and applies the implicit function theorem.

As in §
1.2
, fix a smooth family
\mathfrak{B}(\pi,t)
of loops in
M
,
\pi\in P
,
t\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, and let
W=T^{*}M
. Fix some
p>2
.

Let
{W}^{1,p}
be the Banach manifold of triples
(u,\pi,\ell)
where
\ell>0
,

\left\{\begin{aligned} &u:[0,\ell]\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to W\text{ is of class }W^{1,p},\\
&\mathrm{pr}\circ u(0,t)=\mathfrak{B}(\pi,t),\\
&u(\ell,t)\in M.\end{aligned}\right.

One thinks of this as a total space of a bundle
{W}^{1,p}\to P\times(0,\infty)
. It is straightforward exercise in nonlinear analysis to locally trivialize this bundle and identify it with an open subset of a Banach space. The Banach manifold
{W}^{1,p}
is separable, in the sense that it can be covered by countably many open sets homeomorphic to open subsets of separable Banach spaces. This can be proved by observing that
{W}^{1,p}
includes continuously into a
C^{0}
space, and
C^{0}
small neighborhoods are identified with open subsets of genuine
W^{1,p}
vector spaces (using exponential type maps). Since
C^{0}
spaces have a countable dense set of smooth elements, and
W^{1,p}
vector spaces are separable, one concludes that
{W}^{1,p}
is separable.

Fix smooth data
H_{t},J_{t}
admissible for a star-shaped domain
\Omega
. Let
{H}
be a Banach manifold of smooth Hamiltonians
H_{t}^{\prime}
which agree with
H_{t}
on the complement of
\Omega
. We require
{H}
to be sufficiently large so that the image of the linearization of
H_{t}^{\prime}\mapsto X_{H_{t}^{\prime}}
contains sections taking any chosen value at
p\in\Omega^{\mathrm{int}}
, supported in arbitrarily small balls around
p
. A good choice is Floer’s
C^{\epsilon}
space introduced in
[
Flo88
, §5]
, see also
[
Wen20
, §B]
. One lets
{H}
be the space of
H_{t}^{\prime}
agreeing with
H_{t}
on
\Omega^{c}
and so that:

\sum\epsilon_{k}\left\lVert\nabla^{k}(H_{t}^{\prime}-H_{t})\right\rVert<\infty,

for a sequence
\epsilon_{k}>0
decaying sufficiently quickly, where the norms are measured using any complete metric on
W
. This is separable, see
[
Wen20
, Lemma B.4]
.

Let
{L}^{p}\to{W}^{1,p}\times{H}
be the Banach manifold bundle whose fiber over
(u,\pi,\ell,H_{t}^{\prime})
consists of
L^{p}
sections of
u^{*}TW
.

The nonlinear problem is described by the section
{S}:{W}^{1,p}\times{H}\to{L}^{p}
given by:

{S}(u,\pi,\ell,H_{t}^{\prime})=\partial_{s}u+J_{t}(u)(\partial_{t}u-X_{t}^{\prime}(u)),

where
\mathrm{d}\lambda(X_{t}^{\prime},-)=-\mathrm{d}H^{\prime}_{t}
. If
{S}(u,\pi,\ell,H_{t}^{\prime})=0
, then the linearized operator is well-defined, and is described as a map:

\mathrm{L}{S}:T{W}^{1,p}_{u,\pi,\ell}\oplus T{H}_{H_{t}^{\prime}}\to{L}^{p}_{u}.

Proposition 4.2
.

The linearized operator
\mathrm{L}{S}
is surjective at every point
(u,\pi,\ell,H_{t}^{\prime})
in
{S}^{-1}(0)
(moreover, the linearized operator has a right inverse).

Proof
.

The argument is standard, see
[
MS12
, §8.3]
,
[
FHS95
, §5]
. The restriction of
\mathrm{L}{S}
to the space of variations fixing
(\pi,\ell,H_{t}^{\prime})
is a Cauchy-Riemann operator
D
on the cylinder
[0,\ell]\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
with totally real boundary conditions. Since
u
has many regular points
z_{0}
in
u^{-1}(\Omega)
, and
{H}
is sufficiently large, the restriction of the linearized operator
\mathrm{L}{S}
to variations in
H_{t}^{\prime}
is transverse to the cokernel of any Cauchy-Riemann operator. Briefly, the reason is that the cokernel of
D
is represented by the orthogonal complement to the image of
D
. Duality theory for partial differential operators implies this orthogonal complement is the kernel of an adjoint Cauchy-Riemann operator
D^{*}
. If the full linearization was not surjective, then we would conclude nonzero elements
\eta\in\ker D^{*}
orthogonal to the linearization of
{S}
in directions tangent to
{H}
. One then shows that
\eta
would need to vanish at every regular point of
u
in
u^{-1}(\Omega)
. Applying analytic continuation to
\eta
implies that
\eta
is identically zero, contradicting our assumption. That the operator has a right inverse follows from the fact
D
is Fredholm.

We want a surjective linearized operator for fixed
H_{t}
. The following result provides a way to achieve this.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
E_{i}
,
i=1,2,3
, be separable Banach spaces, and
F:E_{1}\times E_{2}\to E_{3}
is a smooth map whose linearization has a right inverse at all points in
F^{-1}(0)
. Then
F^{-1}(0)
is a smooth Banach manifold. Pick
(e_{1},e_{2})
and let
G_{1}
be the restriction of
F
to
E_{1}\times\left\{e_{2}\right\}
and
G_{2}
the restriction of
\mathrm{pr}:E_{1}\times E_{2}\to E_{2}
to
F^{-1}(0)
. Then the linearization of
G_{1}
at
(e_{1},e_{2})
is Fredholm if and only if the linearization of
G_{2}
at
(e_{1},e_{2})
is. In this case, the cokernel of the linearization of
G_{1}
is canonically identified with the cokernel of the linearization of
G_{2}
.
As a consequence, if
e_{2}
is a regular value of
G_{2}
, then the linearization of
G_{1}
is surjective.

Proof
.

The argument is a straightforward analysis of Fredholm operators and some linear algebra. See
[
Wen20
, §8.1]
,
[
MS12
, Lemma A.3.6]
.

Applying the above result (locally) with
E_{1}={W}^{1,p}
,
E_{2}={H}
and
E_{3}={L}^{p}
, one concludes that, if
H_{t}^{\prime}
is a regular value for the projection
{S}^{-1}(0)\to{H}
, then the linearization of
{S}
restricted to
{W}^{1,p}\times\left\{H_{t}^{\prime}\right\}
will be surjective. The final ingredient is the Sard-Smale theorem which guarantees a Baire generic subset of regular values of the projection
{S}^{-1}(0)\to{H}
(since it is a smooth map with Fredholm linearization between separable Banach manifolds), see
[
MS12
, §A.5]
. Thus we conclude:

Proposition 4.4
.

For any admissible data
J_{t},H_{t}
, there exist arbitrarily small perturbations
H_{t}^{\prime}
agreeing with
H_{t}
outside
\Omega
so that the moduli space
{M}(J_{t},H_{t}^{\prime})
is a smooth manifold whose dimension equals the Fredholm index of the linearization of:

(u,\pi,\ell)\in{W}^{1,p}\mapsto\partial_{s}u+J_{t}(u)(\partial_{t}u-X_{t}(u)),

and this linearization is surjective.

To complete the proof of the dimension part of Theorem
1.3
, it remains to prove that this Fredholm index equals
\dim P+1
. This is the subject of the next section.

4.2.
Index theory

As a first reduction, observe that the tangent space to
{W}^{1,p}
splits into the infinite dimensional piece consisting of variations which fix
\pi
and
\ell
and a finite dimensional complement of dimension
\dim P+1
. Thus it suffices to prove that the linearization restricted to variations fixing
\pi
and
\ell
has Fredholm index
0
.

4.2.1.
Travelling coordinates

Fix
(u,\pi,\ell)\in{M}(J_{t},H_{t})
. It is important to identify a neighborhood of
u
with a linear space. A convenient way to do this is via
travelling coordinates
.

Abbreviate
\Sigma=[0,\ell]\times\mathbb{R}
. For each
z\in\Sigma
, choose an open embedding:

\varphi_{z}:B(1)^{n}\to M

so that
\mathrm{pr}\circ u(z)=\varphi_{z}(0)
. This can be constructed using Riemannian normal coordinates. Moreover, if
\mathfrak{B}(\pi,t)
is orientable, we can arrange that
\varphi_{(s,t+1)}=\varphi_{(s,t)}
, while, if
\mathfrak{B}(\pi,t)
is non-orientable, we can arrange that
\varphi_{(s,t+1)}=\varphi_{(s,t)}\rho
where
\rho
is a linear reflection. Let us be general and suppose that
\varphi_{(s,t+1)}=\varphi_{(s,t)}\rho
for some orthogonal linear map
\rho
.

The maps
\varphi_{z}
induce canonical embeddings:

\Phi_{z}:B(1)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to T^{*}M\text{ so }\Phi_{z}^{*}\lambda=\sum p_{i}\mathrm{}q_{i}.

There is a smooth map
w
so that
u(z)=\Phi_{z}(w(z))
. The Floer equation for
u
implies that
w
satisfies the problem:

(8)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{s}w+J_{z}(w)\partial_{t}w=A_{z}(w)\\
&w(0,t)\in 0\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\\
&w(\ell,t)\in B(1)\times 0\\
&Rw(s,t+1)=w(s,t).\end{aligned}\right.

Here
R
is the linear symplectic extension of the orthogonal transformation
\rho
. This produces a linearized operator:

D(\eta)=\partial_{s}\eta+J(z)\partial_{t}\eta+B(z)\eta,

for
\eta\in W^{1,p}
satisfying
\eta(0,t)\in 0\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\eta(\ell,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times 0
and
R\eta(s,t+1)=\eta(s,t)
.

It is standard that all such operators
D
are Fredholm, see, e.g.,
[
MS12
,
Can22
]
. Since
J(z)
is compatible with the standard symplectic structure on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, it can be deformed through the space of complex structures until
J(z)=J_{0}
is the standard almost complex structure. This deformation does not change the Fredholm index. Similarly deform
B(z)
until
B(z)=0
.

Proposition 4.5
.

The operator
D_{0}\eta=\partial_{s}\eta+J_{0}\partial_{t}\eta
is an isomorphism between the spaces:

(i)

\eta\in W^{1,p}
with
\eta(0,t)\in 0\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\eta(\ell,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times 0
and
R\eta(s,t+1)=\eta(s,t)
, and

(ii)

\xi\in L^{p}
with
R\xi(s,t+1)=\xi(s,t)
.

Proof
.

Certainly
D_{0}
is well-defined with the advertised domain (i) and codomain (ii). Liouville’s theorem on the non-existence of bounded holomorphic functions implies
D_{0}
is injective. Thus it remains only to prove that
D_{0}
is surjective.

It is a fact that
\kappa\mapsto D_{0}\kappa
is surjective from
W^{1,p,\delta}([0,\ell]\times\mathbb{R})\to L^{p,\delta}([0,\ell]\times\mathbb{R})
, for small
\delta
, where the
\delta
superscript denotes an exponentially weighted space, using the same boundary conditions for
W^{1,p,\delta}
. The argument is similar to
[
Sal97
, §2.3]
and is proved in detail in
[
Can22
, Theorem 6.20]
.

Then, given
\xi
satisfying
R\xi(s,t+1)=\xi(s,t)
, we can find
\mu
compactly supported in
[0,\ell]\times\mathbb{R}
so
\xi(s,t)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}R^{k}\mu(s,t+k)
. Indeed, if
\beta(t)
is a bump function and
\mu(s,t)=\beta(t)\xi(s,t)
, then the above will hold if:

\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\beta(t+k)=1,

which can be achieved for
\beta
supported in
(-1,1)
.

Using the aforementioned surjectivity of
D_{0}
on the exponentially weighted space, there is
\kappa\in W^{1,p,\delta}
so
D_{0}\kappa=\mu
. Thus:

\eta=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}R^{k}\kappa\implies D_{0}\eta=\xi,

and
\eta\in W^{1,p}
will have the correct periodicity and boundary conditions. Indeed, this produces an explicit right inverse.

Thus we have shown that the linearized operator has index
0
when we restrict to variations fixing
\pi
and
\ell
.

4.3.
Gluing

The goal in this section is to give an explicit description of the moduli space
{M}(H_{t},J_{t})
in the region where
\ell
is very small. See
[
AS10
, §4.6]
for related discussion.

Introduce the notation
{M}(\ell_{0})={M}\cap\left\{\ell=\ell_{0}\right\}
. Consider the two maps:

(i)

\pi_{\ell_{0}}:{M}(\ell_{0})\to P
given by
(u,\pi,\ell_{0})\mapsto\pi
,

(ii)

\mathrm{ev}_{\ell_{0}}:{M}(\ell_{0})\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to M
given by
(u,\pi,\ell_{0},t)\mapsto u(\ell_{0},t)
.

The goals in this section are to prove that (a) sufficiently small
\ell_{0}
are regular values for the projection
\ell:{M}\to(0,\infty)
, (b) for
\ell_{0}
sufficiently small,
\pi:{M}(\ell_{0})\to P
is a diffeomorphism and, (c) with respect to this identification,
\mathrm{ev}
is
C^{0}
close to
\mathfrak{B}
. The cobordism part of Theorem
1.3
follows, since
C^{0}
close maps are automatically cobordant, and it is clear that the slices
{M}(\ell_{1})
for large
\ell_{1}
are cobordant to the slices for small
\ell_{0}
(the moduli space is the cobordism).

4.3.1.
Travelling coordinates, revisited

Fix
\pi_{0}\in P
, and for
\pi
sufficiently close to
\pi_{0}
, let
\varphi_{t}^{\pi}
be a path of embeddings
B(1)^{n}\to M
so that
\varphi_{t}^{\pi}(0)=\mathfrak{B}(\pi,t)
and
\varphi_{t+1}^{\pi}=\varphi_{t}^{\pi}\rho
where
\rho:B(1)\to B(1)
is a fixed orthogonal transformation (one can take
\rho
to either be a reflection or the identity map, depending on whether
TM
is orientable along the loop). Let
\Phi_{t}^{\pi}:B(1)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to W
be the canonical extension of
\varphi_{t}^{\pi}
, and let
R
be the linear symplectic extension of
\rho
.

Appealing to Proposition
3.4
, for
\ell\leq\ell_{0}
sufficiently small, every element
(u,\pi,\ell)\in{M}
will satisfy
u(s,t)=\Phi_{t}^{\pi}(w(s,t)),
where
w
satisfies (
8
). It is expedient to consider the conformal reparametrization
w(s,t)=v(\ell s,\ell t)
, where
v
is defined on
[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}/\ell^{-1}\mathbb{Z}
, as in §
3.3.1
. Then
v
satisfies:

(9)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{s}v+J_{t\ell}^{\pi}(v)\partial_{t}v=\ell A^{\pi}_{z\ell}(v)\\
&v(0,t)\in 0\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ and }v(1,t)\in B(1)\times 0\\
&Rv(s,t+\ell^{-1})=v(s,t).\end{aligned}\right.

Notice that as
\ell\to 0
, the equation for
v
converges to the holomorphic curve equation.

4.3.2.
Linearized operator is uniformly an isomorphism

The non-linear problem (
9
) is posed for functions
v:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}/\ell^{-1}\mathbb{Z}\to B(1)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and hence we can linearize it at any map
v
, even those which do not solve (
9
). We linearize by considering variations in the map
v
, and obtain the Cauchy-Riemann type operator:

D_{v,\pi,\ell}(\eta)=\partial_{s}\eta+J^{\pi}_{t\ell}(v)\partial_{t}\eta+B_{\pi,\ell}(z)\cdot\eta,

where
B_{\pi,\ell}(z)
satisfies the estimate:

\left|B_{\pi,\ell}(z)\right|\leq C(\left|v\right|+\left|\partial_{t}v\right|+\ell),\text{ where }C=C(H_{t},J_{t},\mathfrak{B}).

A limiting argument yields:

Proposition 4.6
.

For sufficiently small
\gamma>0
and
\pi
nearby
\pi_{0}
there exists
\delta>0
so that:

\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}}+\ell<\delta\implies\gamma\left\lVert\eta\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}}\leq\left\lVert D_{v,\pi,\ell}(\eta)\right\rVert_{L^{p}}

In other words, the linearized operator
D_{v,\pi,\ell}
is uniformly injective.

Proof
.

For each
\tau\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
, consider the limiting linearized problem:

\left\{\begin{aligned} &D^{\infty}_{\pi,\tau}(\eta)=\partial_{s}\eta+J^{\pi}_{\tau}(0)\partial_{t}\eta\\
&\eta\in W^{1,p}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{2n})\\
&\eta(0,t)\in 0\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\text{ and }\eta(1,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times 0.\end{aligned}\right.

As in the proof of Proposition
4.5
, this is an isomorphism to
L^{p}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{2n})
, since the two boundary conditions are transverse totally real-subspaces. In particular, we can pick
\gamma
large enough so that:

6\gamma\left\lVert\eta\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}}\leq\left\lVert D^{\infty}_{\pi,\tau}(\eta)\right\rVert_{L^{p}}.

for all
\pi
near
\pi_{0}
and
\tau\in\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}
. This fixes
\gamma
.

Suppose the statement does not hold for any
\delta
. Then there exists
v_{n},\pi_{n},\ell_{n}
so that
\left\lVert v_{n}\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}}+\ell_{n}
converges to zero and
\pi_{n}
converges, but
D_{n}:=D_{v_{n},\pi_{n},\ell_{n}}
fails the desired estimate (for some
\eta_{n}
).

Suppose, as a special case, that
\eta_{n}(s,t)
is supported in a region where
t\in I_{n}
and
I_{n}
has length
3L
. Let
t^{*}_{n}
be the center of
I_{n}
. By picking
\tau_{n}=t_{n}^{*}\ell_{n}
, we conclude that, for any
\theta
,

\left\lVert D_{n}(\eta_{n})-D^{\infty}_{\pi_{n},\tau_{n}}(\eta_{n})\right\rVert_{L^{p}}\leq\theta\left\lVert\eta_{n}\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}},

for
n
large enough. This follows from the computation:

D_{n}(\eta_{n})-D^{\infty}_{\pi_{n},\tau_{n}}(\eta_{n})=(J_{t\ell_{n}}^{\pi_{n}}(v_{n})-J_{\tau}^{\pi_{n}}(0))\partial_{t}\eta_{n}+B_{\pi,\ell}(z)\eta_{n},

appealing to standard estimates for the
W^{1,p}
norm, and using
\left|t\ell_{n}-\tau\right|\leq 3\ell_{n}
. How large
n
needs to be depends on
L,\theta
, how quickly
\left\lVert v_{n}\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}}+\ell_{n}
decays, and on the derivatives of
\tau,v\mapsto J^{\pi_{n}}_{\tau}(v)
, but is otherwise independent of
\eta_{n}
. Then:

(6\gamma-\theta)\left\lVert\eta_{n}\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}}\leq\left\lVert D_{n}(\eta_{n})\right\rVert_{L^{p}}.

In general, cover the domain of
\eta_{n}
by regions
[0,1]\times I
where
I
has length
3L
so that each point is contained in at most four of the intervals
2

2

If
\ell_{n}^{-1}
is an integer multiple of
L/3
, one can replace “four” by “three.”
and at least one middle third. Using translations of bump functions
\beta
which are
1
on
[L,2L]
and supported in
[0,3L]
, conclude that:

(6\gamma-\theta)\left\lVert\beta\eta_{n}\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}(I)}\leq\left\lVert\beta D_{n}(\eta_{n})\right\rVert_{L^{p}(I)}+CL^{-1}\left\lVert\eta_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(I)},

where we assume that the first derivative of
\beta
is bounded by
L^{-1}
, and where
C
depends only on the values of the complex structure
J_{t}^{\pi}
. Sum over all the intervals to conclude:

(6\gamma-\theta)\left\lVert\eta_{n}\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}}\leq 4\left\lVert D_{n}(\eta_{n})\right\rVert_{L^{p}}+4CL^{-1}\left\lVert\eta_{n}\right\rVert_{L^{p}}.

Make
L
large enough that
4CL^{-1}<\gamma
, and pick
\theta<\gamma
. For
n
sufficiently large:

\gamma\left\lVert\eta_{n}\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}}\leq\left\lVert D_{n}(\eta_{n})\right\rVert_{L^{p}},

contradicting the assumption.

As a corollary, we conclude that, for
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{W^{1,p}}+\ell<\delta
, the linearization
D_{v,\pi,\ell}
has a bounded inverse (since it is injective and has Fredholm index
0
), and that the norm of the inverse is bounded by
1/\gamma
.

4.3.3.
Existence, uniqueness, and the implicit function theorem

Proposition 4.7
.

For sufficiently small
\epsilon>0
,
\ell>0
, and
\pi
nearby
\pi_{0}
, there exists a unique solution of (
9
) in the
W^{1,p}
ball of radius
\epsilon
around
0
. This solution depends smoothly on
\ell
and
\pi
.

Proof
.

Uniqueness and smooth dependence follows from implicit function theorem in Banach spaces, and the existence follows from Newton’s iteration, as explained in
[
MS12
, §A.3]
.

For existence and uniqueness (with fixed
\ell,\pi
), one uses §
4.3.2
to ensure the hypotheses of
[
MS12
, Proposition A.3.4]
(with
c_{\mathrm{MS}}=\gamma^{-1}
,
\delta_{\mathrm{MS}}=\epsilon
,
x_{0,\mathrm{MS}}=x_{1,\mathrm{MS}}=0
, where the subscript
\mathrm{MS}
signifies terms in the statement of their proposition). The details are left to the reader.

Returning to the global problem, we conclude that, for
\ell_{0}
sufficiently small, the projection map
{M}(\ell_{0}):={M}(H_{t},J_{t},\mathfrak{B})\cap\left\{\ell=\ell_{0}\right\}\to P
is surjective and has a locally defined smooth right inverse (using the smoothly varying solutions guaranteed by Proposition
4.7
).

However, Proposition
3.4
and the derivative bounds on the conformal reparametrization from §
3.3.1
imply that every solution in
{M}(\ell_{0})
enters the
W^{1,p}
ball of radius
\epsilon
around
0
, after applying the coordinate changes and conformal reparametrizations needed to pass from
u
to
v
. Thus the local smooth right inverses
P\to{M}(\ell_{0})
patch together to be a global smooth inverse.

Since the
W^{1,p}
norm controls the
C^{0}
norm,
\mathrm{ev}_{\ell_{0}}:{M}(\ell_{0})\times\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\to M
is
C^{0}
close to
\mathfrak{B}
, with respect to the identification of
{M}(\ell_{0})
with
P
, so
\mathrm{ev}_{\ell_{0}}
is cobordant to
\mathfrak{B}
. Since the mapping degree of
{M}(\ell_{0})\to P
is stable under cobordisms, the proof of Theorem
1.3
is complete.
