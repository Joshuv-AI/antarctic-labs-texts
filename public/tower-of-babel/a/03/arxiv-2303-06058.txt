A General Recipe for the Analysis of Randomized Multi-Armed Bandit Algorithms
Dorian Baudry dorian.baudry@stats.ox.ac.uk, Kazuya Suzuki, Junya Honda honda@i.kyoto-u.ac.jp

Abstract
In this paper we propose a general methodology to derive regret bounds for randomized multi-armed bandit algorithms. It consists in checking a set of sufficient conditions on the family of distributions and on the sampling probability of each arm to prove a logarithmic regret. As a direct application we revisit two bandit algorithms, Minimum Empirical Divergence (MED) and Thompson Sampling (TS), under various models for the distributions including single parameter exponential families, Gaussian distributions, bounded distributions, or distributions satisfying some conditions on their moments. In particular, we prove that MED is asymptotically optimal for all these models, but also provide a simple regret analysis for a variant of some TS algorithms for which the optimality is already known. We then further illustrate the interest of our approach, by analyzing a new Non-Parametric TS algorithm ( h h -NPTS), adapted to some families of unbounded reward distributions with a bounded h h -moment . This model can for instance capture some non-parametric families of distributions whose variance is upper bounded by a known constant.

Introduction

A Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB) is a problem in which a learner sequentially picks an action among
K
alternatives, called arms, and collects a random reward. The rewards collected from an arm
k\in[K]
are all drawn independently from a distribution
F_{k}
, of mean
\mu_{k}
. At each time step
t
the learner chooses an arm
A_{t}
, adapting her strategy in order to maximize the expected sum of rewards. For a time horizon
T
this is equivalent to minimizing the
regret
, formally defined as

\mathcal{R}_{T}(F_{1},\dots,F_{K})=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\mu^{\star}-\mu_{A_{t}})\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Delta_{k}\mathbb{E}\left[N_{k}(T)\right]\;,

(1)

where
N_{k}(T)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\text{1}(A_{t}=k)
is the total number of selections of arm
k
up to time
T
, and
\Delta_{k}
is the
sub-optimality gap
of arm
k
:
\Delta_{k}=\mu^{\star}-\mu_{k}
, for
\mu^{\star}=\max_{j\in\{1,\dots,K\}}\mu_{j}
. Assuming that
F_{1},\dots,F_{K}
come from the same family of distributions
\mathcal{F}
,
Lai and Robbins 1985
and
Burnetas and Katehakis 1996
proved (respectively for single-parametric and general families) that a uniformly efficient bandit algorithm
1
1

1

\forall(F_{1},\dots,F_{K})\in\mathcal{F}^{K}
:
\forall\alpha>0
,
\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(T)]=o(T^{\alpha})
for all
k
satisfying
\Delta_{k}>0
.
satisfies the following lower bound for any sub-optimal arm
k
:

\liminf_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(T)]}{\log(T)}\!\geq\!\frac{1}{\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{k},\mu^{\star})}\;,\;\;\;\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{k},\mu^{\star})=\inf_{G\in\mathcal{F}}\left\{\mathrm{KL}(F_{k},G)\!:\!\mathbb{E}_{X\sim G}[X]\!>\!\mu^{\star}\right\}\;,

(2)

where
\mathrm{KL}(.,.)
denotes the Kullback-Leibler divergence between two distributions.
We call an algorithm
asymptotically optimal
if it admits a regret upper bound
matching
this lower bound.
Furthermore, if for each
k\in[K]
it holds that
\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(T)]\leq C_{k}\log(T)
,
for some problem-dependent constant
C_{k}
, we say that the algorithm achieves a
logarithmic regret
.

Families of distributions

The lower bound presented in (
2
) depends on the characteristics of the family of distributions
\mathcal{F}
, on which assumptions have to be made before designing bandit algorithms. For instance,
Single Parameter Exponential Families
(SPEF) are a usual parametric model (see Definition
10
).
They include several usual distributions such as Bernoulli, Poisson, or Gaussian distributions with known variance.
In some cases a multiparameter exponential family is also considered such as
Gaussian distributions with unknown variances
.

In other cases, non-parametric assumptions may be more suitable. For instance,
one can consider the model where the rewards are supported in a
known bounded range

[b,B]
, or the model of
\sigma
-
sub-Gaussian
distributions
(see for example Definition 5.2 in
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
),
the latter of which essentially assumes that their tails are no heavier than the
Gaussian distribution with variance
\sigma^{2}
.

When the distributions are
heavy-tailed
, some
moment condition
may be assumed (see e.g.
Bubeck et al. 2013
):
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\nu}[|X|^{1+\epsilon}]\leq B
for known
\epsilon>0,B>0
. More recently,
Agrawal et al. 2020
introduced a more general assumption:
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[h(|X|)]\leq B
for a known convex function
h
satisfying
x=o(h(|x|))
. We name for convenience such assumption an (uncentered)
h
-moment condition
. We further consider in this paper its
centered
version:
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[h(|X-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[X]|)]\leq B
, that we simply call a
centered

h
-moment condition.
Some interesting examples would be
(a variant of) the
\sigma
-sub-Gaussian model and a family of distributions with bounded variance.
In Section
5
we propose a simple policy based on bootstrapping to tackle these families of distributions. Its analysis is enabled by the theoretical framework that we propose in this work.

1.1
Asymptotically optimal bandit algorithms

There is a vast literature on MABs, so the following review of related works is non-exhaustive. We refer for instance to
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
for a broader survey.

In this study, we mainly focus on
instance-dependent
regret bounds. The lower bound established in Equation (
2
) provides the best achievable asymptotic rate for this kind of guarantees. For that reason, in this section we proposed a review of related works primarily focused on the main families of asymptotically optimal policies.

The most celebrated approach in bandits is certainly
optimism in face of uncertainty

(
Agrawal 1995
;
Auer et al. 2002
)
. The simple UCB1 policy
(
Auer et al. 2002
)
achieves logarithmic regret for bounded-support
distributions, while the more sophisticated KL-UCB principle provides optimal algorithms for SPEF
(
Cappé et al. 2013
)
, bounded distributions
(
Cappé et al. 2013
;
Agrawal et al. 2021
)
, and uncentered
h
-moment conditions when
h(x)=x^{1+\epsilon}
for
\epsilon>0

(
Agrawal et al. 2021
)
. To do that, these policies build on
upper confidence bounds
, that are well-calibrated for the family of distributions considered. In the cases where asymptotic optimality is achieved (KL-UCB policies), these confidence bounds involve the
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}
function presented in (
2
).

A second celebrated class of policies,
Thompson Sampling
(TS) dates back to the work of
Thompson 1933
. TS algorithms gained renewed attention in the early 2010s, following several studies that demonstrated their strong theoretical and practical performance. Notably,
Chapelle and Li 2011
established the strong empirical performance of TS, and
Kaufmann et al. 2012
;
Agrawal and Goyal 2012
first proved the asymptotic optimality for the Bernoulli case. Later,
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
proposed a simplified version of the proof, inspiring subsequent instance-dependent analyses of TS and other randomized policies (Theorem 36.2 in
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
, Theorem 1 in
Kveton et al. 2019b
).
At its core, TS is a Bayesian algorithm that, at each time step, uses an appropriate conjugate prior/posterior to
sample a
parameter
for each arm (typically, their expectation),
and choose the best arm in this sampled environment.
From an instance-dependent perspective
, several TS algorithms
based on non-informative priors
are optimal: with Jeffreys priors for SPEF
(
Korda et al. 2013
)
, with well-tuned inverse-gamma priors for Gaussian distributions
(
Honda and Takemura 2014
)
, and with Dirichlet prior/posteriors for bounded distributions
(
Riou and Honda 2020
)
.

A third family notably includes several optimal algorithms:
Minimum Empirical Divergence
policies. MED
(
Honda and Takemura 2011
)
and IMED
(
Honda and Takemura 2015
)
are respectively the randomized and deterministic versions of this principle. The former is optimal for multinomial distributions
(
Honda and Takemura 2011
)
, while the latter is optimal for semi-bounded distributions
(
Honda and Takemura 2015
)
and SPEF
(
Pesquerel et al. 2021
)
. More recently,
(
Bian and Jun 2022
;
Qin et al. 2023
)
analyzed variants of MED under the name
Maillard Sampling
(MS),
after re-discovering the algorithm from the work of
Maillard 2011

respectively for sub-Gaussian and bounded distributions.
Prior to these works, MED/MS was also reinvented under the name
SoftElim
by
Boutilier et al. 2020
, which the authors later extended to contextual bandits in
Kveton et al. 2021b
. In these studies, the algorithms are analyzed through the lens of policy gradient methods, with the authors comparing the performance of this approach to other policies, such as Thompson Sampling.

More recently, several works have focused on developing alternative nonparametric algorithms to these three approaches.
First,
Kveton et al. 2019a
and
Kveton et al. 2019b
established a general framework for exploration based on
bootstrapping
.

The NPTS algorithm
(
Riou and Honda 2020
)
, which is related to the Bayesian bootstrap (see
Tiapkin et al. 2022
), can also be categorized within this line of work, in addition to being a TS-based policy.
This is also true for the variant of NPTS introduced in Section
5
of this paper, designed for distributions characterized by an
h
-moment condition.
Finally, it has been shown that some algorithms based on
sub-sampling

(
Baransi et al. 2014
;
Chan 2020
;
Baudry et al. 2020
;
Baudry et al. 2021a
)
are optimal for SPEF or Gaussian distributions without requiring prior knowledge of the distribution family.

Among the various types of policies,
randomized
algorithms –the focus of our study– are of particular practical interest. We refer to Chapter 1 of
Russo et al. 2018
, that provides an overview of industrial applications of Thompson Sampling in recent years.
Randomizing the pulled arm at each iteration
also plays a crucial role in natural extensions of MAB where rewards are accrued concurrently (see Chapter 6.4 of
Russo et al. 2018
). For instance, a recent work by
Gautron et al. 2024
highlights the importance of randomization in a crop-management problem in agriculture, where the algorithm operates over a limited number of periods (years), but collects data from several independent locations during each period. In this case, randomizing actions at each period is essential to ensure empirical performance within the time constraints.

1.2
Other standard performance metrics

While the focus of this work is on providing a framework for proving instance-dependent regret bounds for randomized bandit algorithms, we also present, for completeness, other performance metrics commonly considered in the literature.

In frequentist analyses, it is also standard to consider the
worst-case
expected regret (or
problem-independent
regret) alongside the instance-dependent regret. While the latter captures the first-order asymptotic behavior of the regret, the former also requires clean bounds on the second-order terms in order to obtain a result close to the optimal
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{KT})
bound (where the
\mathcal{O}
notation only captures universal constants), for
any

K
-armed bandit problem with arms belonging to the family
\mathcal{F}
. Following the notation from Eq. (
1
), the worst-case regret is simply defined by

\mathcal{R}_{T}=\sup_{(F_{1},\dots,F_{K})\in\mathcal{F}^{K}}\mathcal{R}_{T}(F_{1},\dots,F_{K})\;,

and thus reflects the worst possible scaling of the regret in terms of all possible problem parameters.
Hence, when an explicit upper bound on the instance-dependent regret is available for any possible instance, it is possible to derive a corresponding bound on the worst-case regret. Notably, it is well established that an upper bound of
\mathcal{R}_{T}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{KT\log(T)})
is achieved by UCB in the sub-Gaussian case
(
Auer et al. 2002
)
, as well as by the Beta-Bernoulli TS algorithm
(
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
)
.
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
further obtained
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{KT\log(K)}
for TS with Gaussian prior.
Bian and Jun 2022
;
Qin et al. 2023
proved that this bound also holds for Maillard Sampling for sub-Gaussian distributions (which is, as discussed above, MED with the Gaussian divergence). Furthermore,
Qin et al. 2023
proved that the Bernoulli version of MED/MS satisfies
\mathcal{R}_{T}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\mu^{\star}(1-\mu^{\star})KT\log(K)})
. In this viewpoint, the results presented in the next sections are not specially tailored for the problem-independent bound, but can still be used to derive such a bound for some cases as we will see in Section
3.3.2
. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, worst-case bounds have only been achieved for sub-Gaussian (including bounded) families of distributions, while our goal is to cover more diverse (e.g. nonparametric/unbounded) families, for which it is already difficult to obtain logarithmic problem-dependent bounds.
For these reasons, in this work the primary focus is the problem-dependent analysis. Still, we discuss in Section
3.3
how the theoretical framework that we introduce in Section
3
can be used to derive problem-independent regret bounds.

Another widespread metric to analyze bandit algorithms, particularly Bayesian policies like Thompson Sampling, is the
Bayesian regret
. Unlike the worst-case regret, this metric considers the expected regret
averaged
over instances drawn from a given prior
p_{\mathcal{F}}
. Specifically, it is defined as

\mathcal{R}_{T}^{\text{Bayes}}=\mathbb{E}_{(F_{1},\dots,F_{K})\sim(p_{\mathcal{F}})^{K}}\left[\mathcal{R}_{T}(F_{1},\dots,F_{K})\right]\;.

In particular,
Russo and Van Roy 2014
proposed a simple framework from which most later Bayesian analyses are derived. Unlike the frequentist bounds, Bayesian guarantees can account for the value of the prior (see, e.g.,
Lu and Van Roy 2019
), which is especially useful in applications where the learner encounters multiple problem instances drawn from the same prior
(
Kveton et al. 2021a
)
.

In the remainder of the paper, we present simple regret analyses applied to the frequentist regret.
Specifically, for Thompson Sampling, we focus on instances of algorithms that use non-informative priors, which do not require prior knowledge of the problem.
For unbounded (e.g. Gaussian) distributions, non-informative priors are often improper, and Bayesian regret is not defined.
Instead, these policies are directly comparable to frequentist ones and are known to achieve strong instance-dependent guarantees
(
Kaufmann et al. 2012
;
Honda and Takemura 2014
;
Riou and Honda 2020
)
.

1.3
Outline and contributions

After comparing the results obtained in the literature, a striking observation is that diverse policies (KL-UCB, TS, IMED) are all proved to be asymptotically optimal for almost the same families of distributions (SPEF and bounded distributions in particular). This raises two intriguing questions:

What are the fundamental properties shared by these families of distributions? Given a family of distribution, are all these algorithms different variants of the same exploration strategy?

After detailing some notation and algorithms in Section
2
, we propose in Section
3
an answer to the first question, by exhibiting a common property (divided in four sub-properties) shared by all the families of distributions that we introduced. This set of properties, that we formalize in Assumption
2
,
allows us to derive a unified regret analysis for randomized algorithms, depending on upper and lower bounds on the
sampling probability
of each arm. We then confirm in Section
4
that it easily captures MED and a variant of TS, that we call TS
⋆
, proving their asymptotic optimality.
In TS
⋆
, posterior sampling is used within an algorithmic structure that allows to derive explicit upper and lower bounds on the sampling probability of each arm in the analysis. Furthermore, TS
⋆
offers the additional benefit of improved computational efficiency compared to TS in some nonparametric settings, where the cost of posterior sampling increases with time.

Our result not only covers some families of distributions
\mathcal{F}
where the optimality of MED has not been proved, but also leads to a simplified analysis of some known TS policies under a slight change of the algorithms
(the TS
⋆
framework)
. We also prove that some nonparametric families of distributions defined by
h
-moment conditions satisfy the above sufficient property for the optimality, so MED is the first algorithm with the optimal regret bound for the centered case. This setting has a special technical difficulty since the space of the distributions is not compact nor convex unlike the uncentered case.

To demonstrate the strength of our generic framework, we then propose a new Thompson Sampling algorithm, named
h
-NPTS, for the nonparametric models with uncentered or centered
h
-moment conditions in Section
5
. We show that its sampling probability can be bounded in a form such that the analysis can be captured within the same framework as MED, which completes the picture that MED and
TS
⋆
can be interpreted as two variants of the same exploration strategy.
Furthermore,
h
-NPTS is an efficient alternative to MED and the existing optimal algorithm
(
Agrawal et al. 2020
)
in the uncentered case, as it does not require any optimization procedure.
In Section
6
, we present numerical experiments on synthetic problems that confirm our theoretical findings.

Preliminaries

In this section we introduce some notation to describe randomized bandit algorithms. We then detail MED and TS

2.1
Notation and terminology

At each step
t

\pi
chooses an arm
A_{t}
based on

\mathcal{H}_{t-1}=(A_{1},X_{1},\dots,A_{t-1},X_{t-1})

of each arm

p_{k}^{\pi}(t)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}_{\pi}(A_{t}=k|\mathcal{H}_{t-1})\;.

This probability depends on the empirical distribution of each arm
k
, that we denote by
F_{k}(t)
. We also sometimes use this notation for the empirical cumulative distribution function (cdf) with a slight abuse of notation. Throughout the paper, we denote by
\mu_{k}(t)
the empirical mean of an arm
k
at time
t
,
formally defined by
\mu_{k}(t)=\frac{1}{N_{k}(t)}\sum_{s=1}^{T}X_{s}\text{1}(A_{s}=k)
, and by
\mu^{\star}(t)=\max_{k\in[K]}\mu_{k}(t)
the best empirical mean. We also use for convenience the notation
F_{k,n}
and
\mu_{k,n}
, denoting respectively the empirical distribution and mean corresponding to the
n
first observations collected from an arm
k
.
We formally define these quantities as follows: given a sequence of observations collected under the policy
\pi
, we define by
(s_{k,i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
the increasing sequence of (random) times where arm
k
has been pulled. Hence,
s_{k,i}
denotes the time of the
i
-th pull of arm
k
. Then, if
s_{k,n}
exists we can define

\mu_{k,n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{s_{k,i}}\;,\quad\text{and}\quad F_{k,n}:x\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\text{1}(X_{s_{k,i}}\leq x)\;.

We now introduce in details the two policies that are the focus of this paper: Minimum Empirical Divergence (MED) and a slight variant of Thompson Sampling (TS) that we call TS

2.2.2
Thompson Sampling

Following Algorithm 4.2 in
Russo et al. 2018
, an iteration of a generic Thompson Sampling algorithm is described as follows: first, a model is sampled using posterior sampling. Then, the algorithm selects the best arm based on the sampled model. In the context of
K
-armed bandits, this results in an
index policy
, where the posterior sampling step provides a sampled expectation
\widetilde{\mu}_{k}(t)
for each arm
k\in[K]
, and the arm with the highest sampled expectation,
A_{t}=\arg\max_{k\in[K]}\widetilde{\mu}_{k}(t)
, is pulled. In this framework, upper and lower bounding the sampling probability of each arm is challenging because it depends on the distribution functions of all
(\widetilde{\mu}_{k}(t))_{k\in[K]}
, since
p_{k}^{\text{TS}}(t)=\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\mu}_{k}(t)\geq\max_{j\neq k}\widetilde{\mu}_{j}(t))
. To address this, we propose an alternative framework, which we refer to as TS
⋆
, to simplify the analysis of the arms’ sampling probabilities.

Assuming that the learner is provided a TS sampler that can return a sampled mean
\widetilde{\mu}_{k}(t)
for each arm given their history
, which is the same pre-requisite as for standard TS, an iteration of
the proposed variant TS
⋆
performs the following steps:

1.

Use the best empirical average as a reference threshold
\mu^{\star}(t)=\max_{k\in[K]}\mu_{k}(t)
.

2.

Sample a parameter
only
for arms that are empirically sub-optimal: for
k\in[K]
, request a sampled mean
\widetilde{\mu}_{k}(t)
only if
\mu_{k}(t)<\mu^{\star}(t)
.

3.

Choose an arm uniformly at random from

\mathcal{A}_{t}=\underbrace{\{k\in[K]:\mu_{k}(t)=\mu^{\star}(t)\}}_{\text{Empirical Best arm(s)}}\cup\underbrace{\{k\in[K]:\mu_{k}(t)<\mu^{\star}(t)\text{ and }\widetilde{\mu}_{k}(t)\geq\mu^{\star}(t)\}}_{\text{\parbox{199.3061pt}{\centering Emp. sub-optimal arms with a posterior sample above the reference threshold\@add@centering}}}.

This structural change makes TS
⋆
use posterior sampling only for arms for which an exploration strategy is necessary, specifically those that appear sub-optimal based on past observations. Intuitively, comparing with the reference mean
\mu^{\star}(t)
mimics the long-term behavior of TS: as
t
becomes large, both the posterior distribution and the empirical mean of the best arm are expected to be closely concentrated around the true mean (due to a sample size that grows linearly with
t
). Thus, comparing a sample
\widetilde{\mu}_{k}(t)
to
\mu^{\star}(t)
should become equivalent to comparing it to a posterior sample of the empirical best arm. Consequently, we can expect the asymptotic behavior of TS and TS
⋆
to be similar. Section
6
confirms this intuition, with numerical experiments demonstrating that the two algorithms have very close performance even for small horizons, in several settings.

Analysis of the sampling probabilities under TS
⋆

We now prove the theoretical properties of TS
⋆
that motivated its introduction. We first introduce a formal name for the probability that an expectation under a sampled model exceeds a given threshold, conditioned on past observations: we call
\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\mu}_{k}(t)\geq\mu^{\star}(t)|\mathcal{H}_{t-1})
a
Boundary Crossing Probability
(BCP) in the rest of the paper, and we keep this terminology when
\mu^{\star}(t)=\mu
is fixed (which will be always clear from context). In the following result, we prove the relation between the BCP and the sampling probabilities under TS
⋆
.

Lemma 0

(
Bounding sampling probabilities with the BCP under TS
⋆
)
.

Under TS
⋆
, for any
t\in[T]
and
k\in[K]
it holds that

p_{k}^{\text{\rm TS${}^{\star}$}}(t)\leq\mathbb{P}(k\in\mathcal{A}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1}),

and that

p_{k}^{\text{\rm TS${}^{\star}$}}(t)\geq\frac{1}{2}\max_{k^{\prime}\neq k}p_{k^{\prime}}^{\text{\rm TS${}^{\star}$}}(t)\times\mathbb{P}(k\in\mathcal{A}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1})\;.

In addition,
\mathbb{P}(k\in\mathcal{A}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1})=\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\mu}_{k}(t)\geq\mu^{\star}(t)|\mathcal{H}_{t-1})\coloneqq

[BCP]
if
\mu_{k}(t)<\mu^{\star}(t)
, and is equal to
1
otherwise.

Proof.

We first remark that the sampling probability of an arm
k\in[K]
can be expressed as
p_{k}^{\text{TS${}^{\star}$}}=\mathbb{P}(k\in\mathcal{A}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1})\times\mathbb{P}(A_{t}=k|k\in\mathcal{A}_{t},\mathcal{H}_{t-1})
, by translating the two steps of the policy, where we recall that
\mathcal{A}_{t}
is the candidate set used at time step
t
. The upper bound is direct from that expression, and so we now consider the lower bound.

We remark that
|\mathcal{A}_{t}|=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\text{1}(j\in\mathcal{A}_{t})
, and that conditioning on
\{k\in\mathcal{A}_{t}\}
fixes the
k

1

\mathcal{H}_{t-1}
in the notation for clarity, the probability to choose
A_{t}=k

\forall k\in[K],\;\mathbb{P}(A_{t}=k|k\in\mathcal{A}_{t})=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}_{t}|}\Bigg|k\in\mathcal{A}_{t}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{1+\sum_{j\in[K],j\neq k}\text{1}(j\in\mathcal{A}_{t})}\right]\;.

Then, for any
k^{\prime}\neq k
we introduce the notation
S_{k,k^{\prime}}(t)=\sum_{j\in[K],j\neq(k,k^{\prime})}\text{1}(j\in\mathcal{A}_{t})
, and use this result to further obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(A_{t}=k|k\in\mathcal{A}_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1+\sum_{j\in[K],j\neq k^{\prime}}\text{1}(j\in\mathcal{A}_{t})}{1+\sum_{j\in[K],j\neq k}\text{1}(j\in\mathcal{A}_{t})}\times\frac{1}{1+\sum_{j\in[K],j\neq k^{\prime}}\text{1}(j\in\mathcal{A}_{t})}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\text{1}(k\in\mathcal{A}_{t})+1+S_{k,k^{\prime}}(t)}{\text{1}(k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{t})+1+S_{k,k^{\prime}}(t)}\times\frac{1}{1+\sum_{j\in[K],j\neq k^{\prime}}\text{1}(j\in\mathcal{A}_{t})}\right]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\times\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{1+\sum_{j\in[K],j\neq k^{\prime}}\text{1}(j\in\mathcal{A}_{t})}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\times\mathbb{P}(A_{t}=k^{\prime}|k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{t})\;,

where in the second line we used that the left-hand term inside the expectation admits a lower bound by
1/2
, that is matched by taking
S_{k,k^{\prime}}(t)=0
,
\text{1}(k\in\mathcal{A}_{t})=0
and
\text{1}(k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{t})=1

p_{k}^{\text{TS}}(t)\geq\mathbb{P}(k\in\mathcal{A}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1})\times\frac{1}{2}\frac{p_{k^{\prime}}^{\text{TS${}^{\star}$}}(t)}{\mathbb{P}(k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1})}
and using that
\mathbb{P}(k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{t}|\mathcal{H}_{t-1})\in[0,1]
.

3
Theoretical results: generic analysis of randomized policies

In the previous section we introduced some examples of algorithms entering into the scope of our analysis of randomized policies. We now introduce the main theoretical results of this paper,
introducing the general objective of our approach and its potential applications.

Summary

In this section, we propose a generic recipe to derive a logarithmic regret upper bound for a randomized policy
\pi
. It consists in

1.

upper and lower bounding the arms’
sampling probabilities
under the policy
\pi
as a function of
D_{\pi}
, for some divergence
D_{\pi}
, and

2.

proving a set of generic properties of
D_{\pi}
for the family of distributions
\mathcal{F}
.

After detailing the aforementioned conditions, we prove that they are sufficient to obtain logarithmic regret bounds for the policy
\pi
on
\mathcal{F}
. More precisely, our main result consists in showing that under these conditions, the following bound can be obtained:

\forall c>0,\;\forall k:\Delta_{k}>0,\;\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(T)]\leq(1+c)\frac{\log(T)}{D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu^{\star})}+o_{c}(\log(T)),

(4)

where we use the notation
o_{c}(\log(T))
to represent poly-logarithmic terms in
T
with a scaling smaller than
\log(T)
, along with problem-dependent constants that depend on
c
. These constants are finite for a fixed
c
, but can become large as
c
goes to
0
(infinite for
c=0
). Proving a bound of the form of Eq. (
4
) for some function
D_{\pi}
is sufficient to guarantee an
instance-dependent
logarithmic regret for the policy
\pi
. Furthermore, if the bound is proved for
D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu^{\star})=\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{k},\mu^{\star})
, it holds that
\pi
is an
asymptotically optimal
policy.

Outline

In the next Section
3.1
, we start by formalizing the two assumptions that we propose to achieve this objective. In Section
3.2
we formally state the main theoretical result of this work, and discuss its proof. Then, in Section
3.3
we compare our main results, Theorem
5
and Lemma
6
, with other generic theorems from the literature. We also discuss how the analysis that we introduce can lead to problem-independent guarantees. We leave the applications of these results to specific policies and families of distributions for Section
4

3.1
Proposed conditions – what to verify in order to use the recipe

Let us consider a randomized policy
\pi

\mathcal{F}

\pi

\mathcal{F}

3.1.1
Upper and lower bound on the arms’ sampling probabilities

We start with a condition that strictly depends on the randomized policy
\pi

D=D_{\pi}

\mathcal{P}

\mathbb{R}

F\in\mathcal{P}

is non-decreasing,
\lim_{x\to-\infty}F(x)=0

Assumption 1

(Upper and lower bound on

.

\pi

(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

\forall t\in[T],\;p_{k}^{\pi}(t)\leq C_{N_{k}(t)}\exp\left(-N_{k}(t)\frac{D(F_{k}(t),\mu^{\star}(t))}{1+a_{N_{k}(t)}}\right),

(5)

where the sequence

\forall k^{\prime}\in[K],k^{\prime}\neq k:\;p_{k}^{\pi}(t)\geq p_{k^{\prime}}^{\pi}(t)\times c_{N_{k}(t)}^{-1}\exp\left(-N_{k}(t)\frac{D(F_{k}(t),\mu^{\star}(t))}{1+a_{N_{k}(t)}}\right)\;,

Additionally,

The general interpretation of this assumption is that, in our framework, the sampling probability of each arm must be driven by a term that is exponentially decreasing in the product of two parameters: the number of observations for that arm, and an appropriate divergence between its empirical distribution and the best empirical mean given all observations collected at the current time step. This last term,
\exp\left(-N_{k}(t)\frac{D(F_{k}(t),\mu^{\star}(t))}{1+a_{N_{k}(t)}}\right)

2

Lemma 0
.

Let
D

\displaystyle\forall k^{\prime}\neq k,\;p_{k}^{\text{MED}}(t)

\displaystyle=p_{k^{\prime}}^{\text{MED}}(t)\times e^{N_{k^{\prime}}(t)\frac{D\left(F_{k^{\prime}}(t),\mu^{\star}(t)\right)}{1+a_{N_{k}(t)}}}\times e^{-N_{k}(t)\frac{D(F_{k}(t),\mu^{\star}(t))}{1+a_{N_{k}(t)}}}

Comments

The requirements we impose on the sequences

and

Lastly, we can comment on the form of the lower bound in Eq. (

These properties might look a bit heavy, but their level of generality is useful to tackle all the settings covered in this paper,

In the following, we use the example of bounded distributions to demonstrate that, for certain families and divergences, the assumption can be straightforward to verify, while also motivating the general form of its statement. We then provide further intuition on the interpretation of each condition (A1)–(A4).

Example: distributions supported on
[0,1]

In many usual cases the assumption
is relatively easy to verify. For example, let us consider bounded distributions with range
[0,1]
and choose
D(F_{n},\mu)=2(\mu-\mu_{n})^{2}\text{1}(\mu\geq\mu_{n})
, as would be done in Maillard Sampling
(

;
Bian and Jun 2022
)
. Then, (A1) is direct from the definition of
D
, and a simple computation proves (A3) with
\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{F}}=2\epsilon^{2}
. The remaining properties can then be obtained with minimal effort using Hoeffding’s inequality. We prove (A4) by writing that

\mathbb{P}(D(F_{n},\mu_{F})>x)=\mathbb{P}(\mu_{n}<\mu-\sqrt{x/2})\leq e^{-2n(\sqrt{x/2})^{2}}=e^{-nx}\;,

and for (A2) it is easy to verify that, for any
\mu\geq\mu_{F}
and
\delta\in(0,D(F,\mu)]

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(2(\mu-\mu_{n})^{2}\text{1}(\mu\geq\mu_{n})\leq 2(\mu-\mu_{F})^{2}-\delta)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\mu_{n}\geq\mu_{F}+\sqrt{\frac{\delta}{2}}\right)\leq e^{-n\delta}\;.

Then, summing for all values of
n
provides a constant
A_{\delta}=\frac{1}{1-e^{-\delta}}\sim\frac{1}{\delta}
, which proves (A2).

Moreover, we motivate the generic formulation of the assumptions by considering the same bounded setting, but for the divergence

corresponding to the lower bound (Eq. (
2

\delta
). Then, Lemma 13 in

provides
\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{F}}=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2(\mu_{F}-\epsilon)(B-\mu_{F})}
in (A3), which depends both on
\mu_{F}
and
\epsilon
. Finally, using Lemma 6 in

\alpha_{n}=e(n+2)
when proving (A4), which thus depends on

Additional intuitions

In some sense, we can say that Assumption
2
generalizes the properties that are usually derived from concentration inequalities on the empirical means, for simple choices of
D
as in our example. This generalization is particularly useful for the analysis of non-parametric models, as the last case presented above, where
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}
cannot be expressed as a function of empirical means alone.

Intuitively, the functions
D(F,\mu)
of interest measure how “far” a distribution
F
is to the class of distributions from
\mathcal{F}
with expectation larger than
\mu
.
In the rest of the paper we will typically consider
D
close to
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}
for the families considered, since this choice can lead to instance-dependent optimality, but other choices are possible (e.g. multiples of the squared gap or Bernoulli KL divergence for bounded distributions). Regarding the properties that we expect from
D
, we can first remark that (A2) is slightly weaker than assuming that
D
is continuous in the first argument w.r.t. some metric and that each
F_{k,n}
converges “fast enough” to some neighborhood of
F_{k}
. It ensures that the expected number of sample sizes for which the empirical distribution of a sub-optimal arm is “too close to
\mu^{\star}
” is finite. On the contrary, (A4) guarantees that an empirical distribution that would make the optimal arm appear sub-optimal (its expectation is under-estimated) has a low probability of being very “far” from the true distribution.
Finally, (A3) ensures that divergence
D
allows to properly separate the different mean levels, so that it can be used to distinguish an arm of mean
\mu
from an arm of mean
\mu-\epsilon
for any
\epsilon>0
.

Remark 0

(
Mean estimates
)
.

While the Assumptions
4
(relaxation of Assumption
1
) and
2
are sufficient to avoid additional assumptions on the concentration of the mean estimates
(\mu_{k,n})_{k\in[K],n\in[N]}
,
it is reasonable to assume that better estimates might lead to better practical performance. For that reason, we discuss in Appendix
B.4
the properties of some robust estimates, that can be used in the case that
\mathcal{F}
contains heavy-tailed distributions.

3.2
Regret bound under the generic assumptions

Now that we proposed precise generic assumptions on the randomized algorithm and the family of distribution, we can present the main result of this work, which is a formalization of the objective that we set with Equation (
4
).

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of distributions and
(F_{1},\dots,F_{K})\in\mathcal{F}^{K}
be a MAB. Let
\pi
be a randomized policy satisfying Assumption
1
or Assumption
4
(upper and lower bounds on the sampling probabilities), for a function
D_{\pi}
satisfying Assumption
2
on
\mathcal{F}
(generic properties of a divergence), and for sequences
(a_{n},c_{n},C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Then, for any
c>\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}
, if
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
positive
it holds that

\forall k:\Delta_{k}>0,\quad\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(T)]\leq(1+c)\frac{\log(T)}{D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu^{\star})}+o_{c}(\log(T))\;.

Furthermore, the bound holds for
a_{n}=0
(for any value of
n
), if
D_{\pi}
is upper bounded as follows: for any
\mu\leq\mu^{\star}
there exists a constant
D_{\mu}^{+}\in\mathbb{R}
such that for any
F\in\mathcal{F}
it holds that
D_{\pi}(F,\mu)\leq D_{\mu}^{+}
.

Theorem
5
confirms the general methodology to analyze randomized policies, presented at the beginning of this section: proving that the policy
\pi
satisfies Assumptions
1
(or
4
, in the appendix), and
2
on the problem considered is sufficient to deduce a logarithmic regret bound. Conversely, this result also provides a general methodology to derive new theoretically sound bandit algorithms for a given family
\mathcal{F}
, by first finding a suitable function
D_{\pi}
that makes Assumption
2
hold with
D\coloneqq D_{\pi}
, and then designing a randomized algorithm
\pi
satisfying Assumption
1
with this same divergence, the easiest way being to implement the corresponding MED policy.

Details of the proof of Theorem
5
can be found in Appendix
B
, where we first prove an intermediate regret bound in Appendix
B.2
, and then complete the proof in Appendix
B.3
. In the next section, we propose a proof sketch in the context where Assumption
1
holds, with a simplification of the intermediate result in that case. We then compare this result with other generic regret bounds that can be found in the literature in Section
3.3
.

3.2.1
Proof sketch of Theorem
5

In a first part, we only use Assumptions
1
and property (A1) of Assumption
2
to exhibit the first-order logarithmic term, sub-logarithmic terms, and a last term independent of
T
that will require the additional assumptions to be further analyzed.

Lemma 0
.

Let
\pi
be a policy satisfying Assumption
1
for a function
D_{\pi}
satisfying (A1) of Assumption
2
. Then, for any
c>\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}
and for any
\epsilon>0
, it holds for any sub-optimal arm
k
that

\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(T)]\leq(1+c)\frac{\log(T)}{D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1})}+B(c,\epsilon)+o(\log(T))\;,

(8)

where the terms captured in
o(\log(T))
can be upper bounded independently of
c
, and with

\displaystyle B(c,\epsilon)=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(D_{\pi}(F_{k,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)\frac{1+c}{1+a_{n}}\leq D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1})\right)}_{b_{1}}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\underbrace{\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}c_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\text{1}(\mu_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon)e^{n\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)}{1+a_{n}}}\right]}_{b_{2}}.

In Appendix
B.2
, we prove a more general version of Lemma
6
under Assumption
4
, from which Lemma
6
can be deduced. While the general proof scheme is inspired by the analysis of Maillard Sampling by
Bian and Jun 2022
and Thompson Sampling by
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
, our main effort consists in adapting the arguments to accommodate the broader assumptions that we consider. This adaptation enables us to obtain an explicit bound on the additional
o(\log(T))
term in (
8
).
In fact, its scaling in
T
only depends on
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. We refer to Remark
26
for further discussion, where we notably obtain the following simplification for the case where
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is upper bounded by a constant,

\max_{n\in\mathbb{N}}C_{n}\leq C<+\infty\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(T)]\leq(1+c)\frac{\log(T\cdot D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1}))+C}{D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1})}+B(c,\epsilon)\;.

(9)

Interpretation of
B(c,\epsilon)

This term encapsulates the elements in the upper bound that require to carefully analyze the properties of
D_{\pi}
on the family of distributions
\mathcal{F}
, and hence motivated the design of properties (A2)–(A4) in Assumption
2
. Intuitively,
B(c,\epsilon)
captures the regret caused by
low-probability events
, as typically considered in the literature. Indeed, the first term (denoted by
b_{1}
) essentially corresponds to regret caused by
over-performance
of a sub-optimal arm, which is the counterpart of events of the form
\left\{\mu_{k,n}\geq\mu_{k}+\alpha\Delta_{k}\right\}
(for some constant
\alpha>0
) in some other analyses. On the other hand, the second term is an upper bound of the expected regret caused by potential
under-performance
of the best arm. Its derivation critically relies on the lower bound in Assumption
1
, which permits to upper bound ratios of sampling probabilities. We will discuss in the next section how these two terms relate to other generic analyses proposed in the literature, and we now discuss how Assumptions
2
(A2–A4) allows us to further upper bound
B(c,\epsilon)
by instance-dependent constants, and thus complete the proof of Theorem
5
.

Further upper bounding
B(c,\epsilon)

We start with the term
b_{1}
, and demonstrate that (A1) and (A2) in Assumption
2
allows to upper bound it by a constant. For simplicity, we discuss the case when
a_{n}=0
, leaving the other cases for the appendix. Then, the first step consists in, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, writing the probability (
n
-th term of
b_{1}
) as

\mathbb{P}\left(D_{\pi}(F_{k,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)\leq D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)-\left(D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)-\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1})}{1+c}\right)\right)\;.

It is then natural to try to use (A2) with
\mu=\mu_{1}-\epsilon
and for some parameter
\delta
satisfying

\displaystyle\delta\leq\left(D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)-\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1})}{1+c}\right)_{+}.

(10)

If we set
\epsilon=0
then we have
D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)-\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1})}{1+c}=\frac{cD_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1})}{1+c}>0
.
Therefore, thanks to the continuity of
D_{\pi}
in the second argument given in (A1), for any
\delta\in\left(0,\frac{cD_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1})}{1+c}\right)
there exists

\epsilon>0
(depending on
\delta
and
c
) satisfying (
10
).
For the rest of the analysis we fix
(\epsilon,\delta)
satisfying the above relation, guaranteeing both that
\epsilon>0
and
\delta>0
.

We now sketch how assumptions (A3) and (A4) lead to an upper bound of
b_{2}
. We first exploit the event
\{\mu_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon\}
to upper bound
D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)
by
D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1})-\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}
in the expectation. We can thus obtain that

\displaystyle b_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\text{1}(\mu_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon)e^{n\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)}{1+a_{n}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}c_{n}e^{-n\frac{\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}}{1+a_{n}}}\times\mathbb{E}\left[\text{1}(\mu_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon)e^{n\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1})}{1+a_{n}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}c_{n}\times e^{-n\frac{\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}}{1+a_{n}}}\mathbb{E}\left[e^{n\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1})}{1+a_{n}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}c_{n}\times e^{-n\frac{\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}}{1+a_{n}}}+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}c_{n}e^{-n\frac{\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}}{1+a_{n}}}\times\mathbb{E}\left[e^{n\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1})}{1+a_{n}}}-1\right]\;.

We then further upper bound
\mathbb{E}\left[e^{n\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1})}{1+a_{n}}}-1\right]
for a fixed value of
n
. Using that this is the expectation of a non-negative random varialbe, we first write that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{n\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1})}{1+a_{n}}}-1\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(e^{n\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1})}{1+a_{n}}}-1>x\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)>\frac{1+a_{n}}{n}\log\left(1+x)\right)\right)\mathrm{d}x\;.

We then conclude by using (A4). For the case
a_{n}>0
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{n\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1})}{1+a_{n}}}-1\right]\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\alpha_{n}}{(1+x)^{1+a_{n}}}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\alpha_{n}}{a_{n}}\;.

Finally, for the case when
a_{n}=0
and the divergence is upper bounded, we similarly obtain the upper bound
\alpha_{n}\times nD_{\mu_{1}}^{+}
(for
D_{\mu_{1}}^{+}
defined in Theorem
5
). In all cases, the scaling of these terms multiplied by
c_{n}
(in
n
) guarantees that
b_{2}
is finite. Furthermore, an explicit upper bound can be obtained for specific instances of all parameters.

3.3
Discussions

In this section, we discuss the regret upper bound established in Theorem
5
, along with the intermediate result introduced in its proof sketch (Lemma
6
). We begin by comparing Lemma
6
with other general analyses of Thompson Sampling (TS) from the literature and then expand on how these results can lead to problem-independent bounds.

3.3.1
Comparison with other generic results from the literature

As previously mentioned, the proof of Lemma
6
is inspired by the modern analysis of TS in
Agrawal and Goyal 2013
;
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
. Their work has inspired subsequent authors to derive general theorems that provide a framework for the analysis of TS (index) policies. Notably, Theorem 1 in
Kveton et al. 2019b
, applied to a bootstrapping policy, and Theorem 36.2 in
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
fall into this category. When they are applied to different policies, the bounds of the two theorems match. For completeness, we restate the latter below, adapting the notation to align with the conventions used in this paper.

Theorem 7

(Theorem 36.2 in
Lattimore and Szepesvári 2020
)
.

Consider a TS policy, as defined in Algorithm 4.2 from
Russo et al. 2018
. Then, for any
\epsilon>0
,

\forall k:\;\Delta_{k}>0,\;\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(T)]\leq 1+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{T-1}\text{1}(G_{kn}>1/T)\right]}_{R_{1}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{T-1}\left(\frac{1}{G_{1n}}-1\right)\right]}_{R_{2}}\;,

with
G_{kn}=\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\mu}_{k,n}\geq\mu_{1}-\epsilon|X_{k,s_{1}},\dots,X_{k,s_{n}})
, and
\widetilde{\mu}_{k,n}
denotes the expectation of arm
k
under a model randomly sampled from the sampler distribution, using as inputs the
n
first observations collected from arm
k
. By convention,
\widetilde{\mu}_{k0}
is a sample from the prior distribution.

The quantities
(G_{k,n})_{k\in[K],n\in\mathbb{N}}
corresponds to the Boundary Crossing Probabilities (BCPs). We can compare Theorem
7
with the intermediate step in our analysis, presented in Lemma
6
. Below, we outline a simplified comparison between the two results. Our key observations are:

•

The terms
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
from the lemma are analogous to
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, in Theorem
7
, but are more explicit thanks to Assumption
1
.

•

After completing the comparison we find that an additional term, not covered by the lemma, remains in Theorem
7
. As a result, analyzing TS using Theorem
7
requires a deeper understanding of the properties of BCPs than using Lemma
6
to analyze its TS
⋆
counterpart, a distinction we elaborate on further.

Detailed comparison

To formalize the comparison, let us assume that the BCPs instead of
p_{k}^{\pi}(t)
satisfy the bounds of Assumption
1
for some divergence
D
. For simplicity, we omit multiplicative terms and directly assume that
G_{k,n}=e^{-nD(F_{k,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)}
. The arguments below can be adapted as needed, as demonstrated in the proof of Theorem
5
. From this, it is straightforward to derive the first-order logarithmic term in Lemma
6
, and the component
b_{1}
of
B(c,\epsilon)
. For any
c>0
, let us define
n_{k}(t)=\left\lceil\frac{1+c}{D(F_{k},\mu_{1})}\log(T)\right\rceil
. It holds that

\displaystyle\forall c>0,\;R_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{n_{k}(T)-1}\text{1}(G_{kn}>1/T)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=n_{k}(T)}^{T-1}\text{1}(G_{kn}>1/T)\right]

\displaystyle\leq n_{k}(T)+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=n_{k}(T)}^{T-1}\text{1}\left(G_{kn}>1/T,D(F_{k,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)\leq\frac{D(F_{k},\mu_{1})}{1+c}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=n_{k}(T)}^{T-1}\text{1}\left(G_{kn}>1/T,D(F_{k,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)\geq\frac{D(F_{k},\mu_{1})}{1+c}\right)\right]\;.

The fact that we introduced the event
\{D(F_{k,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)\geq\frac{D(F_{k},\mu_{1})}{1+c}\}
now allows to provide an upper bound depending on term
b_{1}
of Lemma
6
, as detailed in the following,

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle\leq n_{k}(T)+\sum_{n=n_{k}(T)}^{T-1}\mathbb{P}\left(D(F_{k,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)\leq\frac{D(F_{k},\mu_{1})}{1+c}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=n_{k}(T)}^{T-1}\text{1}\left(G_{kn}>1/T,D(F_{k,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)\geq\frac{D(F_{k},\mu_{1})}{1+c}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq n_{k}(T)+\sum_{n=n_{k}(T)}^{T-1}\mathbb{P}\left(D(F_{k,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)\leq\frac{D(F_{k},\mu_{1})}{1+c}\right)

\displaystyle\leq 1+\frac{1+c}{D(F_{k},\mu_{1})}\log(T)+b_{1}\;.

Hence, it is relatively straightforward to convert
R_{1}
into some of the terms involved in Lemma
6
, under the requested assumption on the BCP. We can then discuss the term
R_{2}
, corresponding to the regret incurred due to under-estimating the best arm. Let us omit again some multiplicative constants in the approximation
p_{1,n}\approx G_{1,n}
when discussing Lemma
6
. Then, considering that
p_{1,n}=G_{1,n}
and that
G_{1,n}\geq c_{n}^{-1}e^{-nD(F_{1,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)}
in the case when
\mu_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon
, for some sequence
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we can directly write that

\displaystyle R_{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{T-1}\left(\frac{1}{G_{1n}}-1\right)\text{1}(\mu_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{T-1}\left(\frac{1}{G_{1n}}-1\right)\text{1}(\mu_{1,n}\geq\mu_{1}-\epsilon)\right]

\displaystyle\leq b_{2}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{T-1}\left(\frac{1}{G_{1n}}-1\right)\text{1}(\mu_{1,n}\geq\mu_{1}-\epsilon)\right]}_{b_{3}}\;,

where the second line is obtained by following the same steps as the proof of Theorem
5
,
ignoring the
-1
term. This completes the demonstration that under the assumptions of Lemma
6
we can translate the bound of Theorem
7
(applied to standard TS) into the bounds of Lemma
6
up to an additional term, denoted by
b_{3}
above.

Interpretation of
b_{3}
and benefits of TS
⋆

Let us now discuss the term
b_{3}
. Intuitively, the regret due to the under-performance of the best arm, when it is not significantly mis-estimated, should not be very large. However, from a technical standpoint, it is not straightforward to obtain tight bounds on the terms contributing to
b_{3}
. One possible approach, followed for instance by
Riou and Honda 2020
, is to split the expectation into two scenarios: one where
\mu_{1,n}\in[\mu_{1}-\epsilon,\mu_{1}-\epsilon/2]
and another where
\mu_{1,n}\geq\mu_{1}-\epsilon/2
. For the latter, an exponentially decreasing upper bound on
1-G_{1,n}
is sufficient due to the gap between
\mu_{1}-\epsilon
and
\mu_{1}-\epsilon/2
. Indeed, in this case a large deviation occurs when the sampled expectation is below
\mu_{1}-\epsilon
. However, the intermediate case can be more challenging in some settings, as it requires precise bounds on the BCP in that specific range. Choosing again the analysis of NPTS in
Riou and Honda 2020
for comparison, our analysis of NPTS
⋆
does not require their Lemma 17, whose proof is quite technical and heavily specific to the considered model.

For this reason, our claim that the TS
⋆
framework simplifies the analysis of TS stems from the observation that analyzing TS
⋆
does not require these additional properties on the BCP, which can be difficult to obtain. Specifically, under the TS
⋆
framework, it is necessary to lower-bound the BCP
only in the case where the best arm experiences a large deviation
(in the sense of the large deviation principle, see
Dembo and Zeitouni 1998
), that is potentially making it appear sub-optimal.

We can finally mention that in
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
the authors provide a fully explicit bound on
b_{2}
for Beta-Bernoulli and Gaussian Thompson Sampling, respectively obtained in their Lemmas 2.9 and 2.13. However, the proofs are also very technical and specific to each distribution considered. Hence, there does not seem to be a clear way to generalize this specific part of their analysis to other families of distributions.

3.3.2
Scaling of the constants and problem-independent analysis

In this section, we discuss the additional steps required to transform the instance-dependent regret bound of Theorem
5
into problem-independent bounds.
For clarity, we focus on the regret bounds for MED policies, that all satisfy Assumption
1
(see Lemma
2
) for some divergence
D
and a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
chosen by the decision-maker, with constants
c_{n}=C_{n}=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The arguments presented below can however be adapted with other values (leading to different rates in the results).

By following the proof structure of Theorem
5
, and Remark
26
(
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is constant), if
D
satisfies Assumption
2
on
\mathcal{F}
then the regret upper bound is obtained by proving that

\forall k:\Delta_{k}>0,\;\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(T)]\leq\frac{(1+c)\log(T\cdot D(F_{k},\mu_{1}))+C}{D(F_{k},\mu_{1})}+A_{\delta_{c}}+\sum_{n=1}^{T}(d_{n}+1)e^{-n\frac{\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}}{1+a_{n}}}\;,

(11)

where the sequence
(d_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is defined as
d_{n}=\frac{\alpha_{n}}{a_{n}}\wedge\alpha_{n}nD_{\mu_{1}}^{+}
, which unifies the two bounds obtained in Theorem
5
, whether
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is strictly positive or not. The terms in this bound are explicitly detailed in Assumption
2
and the proof of Theorem
5
:

•

\delta_{c}=D(F_{k},\mu_{1})\times\frac{c-a_{\infty}}{4(1+c)}
, with
a_{\infty}=\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}
,

•

A_{\delta_{c}}
is an upper bound on
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(D(F_{k,n},\mu_{1})\leq D(F_{k},\mu_{1})-\delta_{c})
, from (A2),

•

For a fixed value of
c
,
\epsilon
is defined as the unique solution of

D(F_{k},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)=D(F_{k},\mu_{1})\times\frac{1+\frac{c-a_{\infty}}{2}}{1+c}\;,

•

\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}
is a lower bound for
D(F_{1,n},\mu_{1})-D(F_{1,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)
, from (A4), when
\mu_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon
.

Previously, we aimed to optimize the first-order logarithmic term of the bound, so we proved that for any (small) value of
c
we could derive an upper bound with asymptotic scaling
(1+c)\frac{\log(T)}{D(F_{k},\mu_{1})}
. Hence, the parameter of interest was
c
, and we tuned other parameters like
\epsilon
as a function of
c
in the analysis. Under a problem-independent perspective, we focus on how
each
term scales with respect to problem parameters. Hence, we propose to re-examine the bound in Eq. (
11
) by fixing
\epsilon
proportional to the gap, for instance
\epsilon=\frac{\Delta_{k}}{3}
, as typical in such analysis. We recall from Assumption
1
that
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-increasing, so
a_{1}=\max_{n\in[N]}a_{n}
.

Re-writing of the regret bound

To proceed further with the analysis, we need to understand three key terms from (
11
): (1) how
the maximum possible value of

\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}

in (A3)
scales with
(\epsilon,\mu_{1})
, (2) what is an appropriate value of
c
given
\epsilon
,
(3) how
A_{\delta}
in (A2) scales as a function of
\delta
.
To start with, we solve the problem of choosing a suitable value of
c
, distinct from the one used for the problem-dependent analysis and with a different relationship with
\epsilon=\frac{\Delta_{k}}{3}
, by using the convenient tuning

c=(1+a_{1})\frac{D(F_{k},\mu_{1})}{D(F_{k},\mu_{1}-2\epsilon)}-1,

which allows to use
\delta\coloneqq D(F_{k},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)-D(F_{k},\mu_{1}-2\epsilon)
in the part of the proof where assumption (A2) is involved.
We can then apply (A3) to obtain the inequality
\delta\geq\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}-\epsilon}
. For convenience, we define
\gamma_{k}=\min\{\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}-\epsilon},\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}\}
for the rest of the analysis. By substituting all constants into the regret bound (
11
), we obtain that

\forall k:\Delta_{k}>0,\;\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(T)]\leq(1+a_{1})\frac{\log(T\cdot D(F_{k},\mu_{1}))+C}{D\left(F_{k},\mu_{1}-\frac{2\Delta_{k}}{3}\right)}+A_{\gamma_{k}}+\sum_{n=1}^{T}(d_{n}+1)e^{-n\frac{\gamma_{k}}{1+a_{n}}}\;.

(12)

It is clear that further upper bounds can only be obtained with precise bounds on
A_{\gamma_{k}}
(A2),
\gamma_{k}
(A3),
(\alpha_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
(A4), and on
D\left(F_{k},\mu_{1}-\frac{2\Delta_{k}}{3}\right)
. These bounds have to be specified for each policy and family of distributions considered. In the following, we first present the case of the sub-Gaussian divergence, which allows to highlight the strengths and limitations of the generic framework that we propose regarding the derivation of problem-independent guarantees.

Sub-Gaussian case

We consider sub-Gaussian distributions, and the corresponding divergence
D:(F,\mu_{1})\mapsto\frac{(\mu_{1}-\mu_{F})^{2}}{2}\text{1}(\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{F})
. Note that this corresponds to the MED instance known as
Maillard Sampling

(
Maillard 2011
;
Bian and Jun 2022
)
or
SoftElim

(
Boutilier et al. 2020
)
. We also assume that the means belong to a finite range
\mathcal{R}=[\mu_{0}^{-},\mu_{0}^{+}]
, with
\mu_{0}^{+}-\mu_{0}^{-}\coloneqq R<\infty
. This is necessary to obtain problem-independent bounds, and otherwise we could consider a problem where one pull of a sub-optimal arm could already cost a linear regret. We introduce the notation
\lesssim
to avoid writing (problem-independent) multiplicative constants, and prove the following result.

Lemma 0

(Problem-independent bound for sub-Gaussian MED)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the family of
1
-sub-Gaussian distributions with means in
\mathcal{R}=[\mu_{0}^{-},\mu_{0}^{+}]\subset\mathbb{R}
, and consider MED implemented with
D:(F,\mu_{1})\in\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{R}\mapsto\frac{(\mu_{1}-\mu_{F})^{2}}{2}\text{1}(\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{F})
and a non-negative and non-increasing sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Then, the upper bound presented in Eq. (
12
) provides the problem-independent regret bound

\mathcal{R}_{T}\lesssim K^{\frac{1}{4}}T^{\frac{3}{4}}\wedge\sqrt{KT\times(\log(K)+a_{T}^{-1})}\;.

Proof.

First, we use Hoeffding’s inequality and direct computation to obtain the following bounds

A_{\gamma_{k}}\lesssim\frac{1}{\gamma_{k}},\quad\text{and}\quad\gamma_{k}=D\left(F_{k},\mu_{k}+\frac{\Delta_{k}}{3}\right)=\frac{\Delta_{k}^{2}}{18},

and in addition Hoeffding’ inequality also provides that
\alpha_{n}=1
in (A4) for the setting considered.
Then, plugging these values in (
12
) we obtain that for any
\Delta>0
(that we will tune later) the following bound holds,

\mathcal{R}_{T}\lesssim\Delta T+\sum_{k:\Delta_{k}>\Delta}\left\{\frac{\log(T\Delta_{k}^{2})}{\Delta_{k}}+\frac{1}{\Delta_{k}}+\underbrace{\Delta_{k}\sum_{n=1}^{T}\left\{\frac{1}{a_{n}}\wedge n\frac{R^{2}}{2}\right\}e^{-n\frac{\Delta_{k}^{2}}{36}}}_{S}\right\}\;.

(13)

In the worst case (e.g. if
a_{T}=0
), the right-hand term is dominated by the sum
S
, and by putting the range
R
in the constants we obtain that

\mathcal{R}_{T}\lesssim\Delta T+\sum_{k:\Delta_{k}>\Delta}\Delta_{k}\sum_{n=1}^{T}ne^{-n\frac{\Delta_{k}^{2}}{18}}\lesssim\Delta T+\frac{K}{\Delta^{3}}\;,

which further provides
\mathcal{R}_{T}\lesssim K^{\frac{1}{4}}T^{\frac{3}{4}}
, by choosing
\Delta=(K/T)^{\frac{1}{4}}
. This gives the first part of the bound, and we now prove the potential refinement depending on
a_{T}
. First, we use that
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-increasing and thus lower bounded by
a_{T}
. Hence, if
a_{T}>0
it holds that
S\leq\frac{a_{T}^{-1}}{\Delta_{k}}
, which in turn provides

\mathcal{R}_{T}\lesssim\Delta T+\sum_{k:\Delta_{k}>\Delta}\frac{\log(T\Delta_{k}^{2})+a_{T}^{-1}}{\Delta_{k}}.

It then remains to choose
\Delta\gtrsim\sqrt{(\log(K)+a_{T}^{-1})\frac{K}{T}}
to prove the desired bound, where the multiplicative constant here only comes from making sure that
\frac{\log(T\Delta_{k}^{2})}{\Delta_{k}}
is non-increasing in
\Delta_{k}
for
\Delta_{k}\geq\Delta
. This proves the lemma.
∎

Discussion

The scaling of the second term in the regret bound is directly influenced by the choice of the sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, determined by the decision-maker. On the other hand, while the bound
K^{\frac{1}{4}}T^{\frac{3}{4}}
does not reach the optimal
\sqrt{KT}
rate, it remains valid universally. This proves that the generic analysis that we propose leads to sub-linear problem-independent bounds in addition to the logarithmic problem-dependent bound.

To gain further insight, we adopt the notion of the
minimax ratio
(as discussed in
Bian and Jun 2022
;
Qin et al. 2023
), which is the scaling of
\mathcal{R}_{T}/\sqrt{KT}
. From the bound of the lemma, it becomes evident that the decision-maker can optimize this ratio by choosing a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with a lower bound
a>0
. Then, the minimax ratio
\sqrt{\log(K)}
is obtained, matching the result proved by
Qin et al. 2023
for Maillard Sampling. However, this optimization comes at some cost for problem-dependent performance: the best achievable asymptotic rate becomes
(1+a)\frac{\log(T)}{D(F_{k},\mu_{1})}
. Therefore, our approach does not simultaneously yield the best problem-dependent rate (obtained only if
\lim_{n\to+\infty}a_{n}=0
) and the minimax ratio of
\sqrt{\log(K)}
, contrarily to the more involved proofs of
Qin et al. 2023
for kl-MS or
Agrawal and Goyal 2017
for Gaussian TS with known variance.

We believe that the looseness of the problem-independent bound for
a_{n}=0
comes from the fact that, with (A3) and (A4), we tried to identify minimal properties needed to upper bound

\sum_{n=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\text{1}(\mu_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon)e^{n\frac{D(F_{1,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)}{1+a_{n}}}\right]\coloneqq\;b_{2}\text{ from Lemma\penalty\ \ref{th::first_bound}},

instead of computing the expectation in a more precise manner with more assumptions. We propose to discuss the case of Gaussian distributions to gain further insights. Indeed, with
a_{n}=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
b_{2}
can be computed explicitly as follows,

F_{1}=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},1)\Longrightarrow b_{2}=\sum_{n=1}^{T}\frac{e^{-n\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}}}{\epsilon\sqrt{2\pi n}}\Longrightarrow b_{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}},\;

(14)

where we omit the computation because it is straightforward, and the last step is proved by splitting the sum depending on the value of
n
with respect to
\epsilon^{-2}
.
By using this bound we can replace
S\lesssim K/\Delta^{3}
in (
13
) with
\Delta\epsilon^{-2}\approx\Delta^{-1}
for
\epsilon\approx\Delta
, which leads to the guarantee of
\mathcal{R}_{T}\lesssim\sqrt{KT\log(K)}
for the Gaussian case.
This proves that better problem-independent bounds requires more precise analysis of the term
b_{2}
in the regret upper bound provided by Lemma
6
. While this is certainly a limitation of our framework, we believe that it is interesting that at least sub-linear problem-independent bounds can be derived under the generic assumptions that we propose. We leave the investigation of problem-independent upper bounds that would be both tighter and obtained under generic assumptions for future work.

Generalization beyond the sub-Gaussian case

To the best of our knowledge, in the literature problem-independent guarantees are always achieved by a reduction to the sub-Gaussian case discussed above, in the sense that all the quantities involved in the regret bounds are expressed as squared gaps. We refer for instance to
Qin et al. 2023
;
Jin et al. 2022
for recent examples of such reduction in the case of exponential families of distributions, respectively for the analysis of MS and TS. It is straightforward that this reduction can be used within our framework, if the following properties are established in addition of Assumption
2
.

Assumption 3

(Additional properties for problem-independent analysis)
.

The divergence
D
satisfies Assumption
2
, and in addition it holds that

1.

\forall k:\Delta_{k}>0
,
D\left(F_{k},\mu_{k}+\frac{\Delta_{k}}{3}\right)\gtrsim\Delta_{k}^{2}
(the divergence scales with squared gaps),

2.

\forall k:\Delta_{k}>0
,
\delta_{\frac{\Delta_{k}}{3},\mu_{1}-\frac{\Delta_{k}}{3}}\gtrsim\Delta_{k}^{2}
(same for the difference of divergences in (A4)),

3.

in (A2),
A_{\delta}\lesssim\frac{1}{\delta}
,

where we recall that we use the notation
\lesssim
to hide absolute constants.

This assumption is a summary of the ingredients needed for the proof steps of Lemma

to hold, from which the next proposition naturally follows.

Proposition 0

(Problem-independent bounds for MED (general case))
.

If MED is implemented with a sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

on

This assumption is easy to verify for simple parametric settings (see

Hence, in the remainder of this work we focus on applications of Theorem

4
Applications of Theorem
5
: regret analysis of various policies

In this section, we propose some applications of the proposed framework by proving instance-dependent regret guarantees for some MED under various models for the family of distributions, and for some TS
⋆
policies adapted from existing TS policies.

In each case, we demonstrate that the policies in question satisfy Assumptions
1
, for a divergence satisfying Assumption
2
, enabling us to apply Theorem
5
. For TS
⋆
policies we believe that our framework offers a simplified proof compared to the existing analyses of their TS counterpart.

For both policy types, verifying Assumption
1
is made easier by the groundwork laid in Section
3.1.1
. Specifically, we showed in Lemma
2
that MED policies meet the conditions of Assumption
1
, and Corollary
3
provides a method to prove this for TS
⋆
policies by bounding the Boundary Crossing Probabilities (BCP) in the specific case considered.

The results in this section address the key question raised in Section
1
: there is indeed a set of fundamental properties shared by all the main families of distributions that have been studied in the bandit literature, that seems to account for the success of common algorithmic principles across them. Assumption
2
appears to capture these shared properties. Moreover, we prove that this assumption typically holds for
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}
, or a function that coincides with
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}
once the empirical distribution of a sub-optimal arm is sufficiently close to its true distribution, thereby establishing the asymptotic optimality of the considered policies.

4.1
Single-Parameter Exponential Families (SPEF)

We start by recalling the definition of these families of distributions.

Definition 0
.

\mathcal{F}
is a SPEF if there exists a set
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}
, a measure
\eta
and some function

such that

\forall\nu\in\mathcal{F}\;,\exists\theta\in\Theta\;:\;\forall x\in\mathbb{R}\;,\;\frac{\mathrm{d}\nu_{\theta}}{\mathrm{d}\eta}(x)=\exp(\theta x-b(\theta))\;.

We thus denote this specific SPEF by
\mathcal{F}_{b}^{\Theta}
.

Furthermore, for any parameter
\theta\in\Theta

\nu_{\theta}
is
\mu_{\theta}=b^{\prime}(\theta)
(one-to-one mapping). Hence, we can indifferently write the KL-divergence between two distributions
\nu_{\theta}
and
\nu_{\theta^{\prime}}
in terms of their parameters or as a function of their expectations. We choose the second formulation, and further recall that for any SPEF it holds that

\forall F\in\mathcal{F}_{b}^{\Theta}:\;\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}_{b}^{\Theta}}(F,\mu)=\mathrm{kl}(\mu_{F},\mu)\coloneqq\int_{\mu_{F}}^{\mu}\frac{x-\mu_{F}}{V_{b}(x)}\mathrm{d}x\;,

(15)

for
\mu\geq\mu_{F}
, and
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}_{b}^{\Theta}}(F,\mu)=0
otherwise. The function

is used to denote the variance of the distribution with expectation
x
. A proof for this expression of
\mathrm{kl}
can be found for instance in
(
Jin et al. 2022
)
.
SPEF include many standard parametric types of distributions: Bernoulli, Gaussian with known variance, Exponential, Poisson, …, and thus are a standard model in bandits.

For any SPEF
\mathcal{F}_{b}^{\Theta}

We defer the proof to Appendix

(16)

In addition, the formulation of

in Eq. (

4.2
Gaussian distributions with unknown variances

We now apply our framework to a second type of parametric families of distributions: Gaussian distributions with unknown variances, that we denote by
\mathcal{F}_{G}

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}_{G}}(F,\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{(\mu-\mu_{F})^{2}}{\sigma_{F}^{2}}\right)\times\text{1}(\mu\geq\mu_{F})\;,

with

\displaystyle\quad\mu_{F}=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim F}[X]\quad\text{and}\quad\sigma_{F}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim F}\left[(X-\mathbb{E}_{X\sim F}[X])^{2}\right]\;.

\mu^{-}\in\mathbb{R}

. We further assume that all policies discussed below clip the empirical estimates they use to satisfy these constraints. In Remark
15

2
on

4.3
Bounded distributions

In this part we consider the family of distributions with a bounded support
[0,B]
, that we denote by
\mathcal{F}_{B}
. The particularity of this example is that it is well established that several divergences can lead to theoretically sound regret bounds. We consider the optimal one, which is the KL-divergence involved in the lower bound (
2
), that can be formulated (see e.g.
Honda and Takemura 2015
) as the following optimization problem,

\forall F\in\mathcal{F}_{B},\mu\in[0,B]:\;\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}_{B}}(F,\mu)=\max_{\lambda\in\left(0,\frac{1}{B-\mu}\right]}\mathbb{E}_{X\sim F}\left[\log\left(1-\lambda(X-\mu)\right)\right]\;.

(17)

This choice is not the most popular in the literature because it is harder to compute than some relaxations: for
B=1
, one can also consider the Bernoulli divergence
\mathrm{kl}(\mu_{F},\mu)
, or the
\frac{1}{4}
-sub-Gaussian divergence,
2(\mu-\mu_{F})^{2}
. It is folklore (see e.g.
Cappé et al. 2013
) that they satisfy the following ordering,

\forall F\in\mathcal{F}_{1},\mu\in[\mu_{F},1]:\;\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}_{1}}(F,\mu)\geq\mathrm{kl}(\mu_{F},\mu)\geq 2(\mu-\mu_{F})^{2}\;.

However, in the case where a distribution
F
has a small density near the boundaries of the support,
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu)
can be much larger than its relaxations. We refer to
Baudry et al. 2023
for a formal discussion of this phenomenon, as well as concrete illustrations. They prove that in some cases asymptotically optimal algorithms can perform significantly better both in theory and practice that non-optimal ones, which motivates further investigation of this setting. We first prove the following result.

Lemma 0
.

Assumption
2
holds on
\mathcal{F}_{B}
for the
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}_{B}}
divergence.

The proof relies on several results that can be found in the literature

Honda and Takemura 2010

D.3
for the details. We obtain for instance that

4.4

We now consider families of distributions satisfying a

Agrawal et al. 2021

\frac{h(|x|)}{|x|^{1+\eta}}\to+\infty

\mathcal{F}_{h,B}=\left\{F\in\mathcal{P}:\;\mathbb{E}_{X\sim F}\left[h(|X|)\right]<B\;,\;\mathbb{E}_{X\sim F}[h(|X|)^{2+\epsilon_{F}}]<+\infty\text{ for some }\epsilon_{F}>0\right\}\;.

4.4.2

The centered case, i.e.​ considering a moment condition of the form

for the divergence

D:(F,\mu)\mapsto\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}_{h,B}^{c}}(F,\mu)\text{1}(F\in\mathcal{F}).

The detailed proof can be found in Appendix
D.5
, as well as the proof of the following regret bound.

Corollary 0

(Instance-dependent optimality of MED on
\mathcal{F}_{h,B}^{c}
)
.

The version of MED implemented with the divergence
D
presented in Lemma
20
, and
a_{n}=0
for all
n
, is asymptotically optimal on
\mathcal{F}_{h,B}^{c}
.

Some illustrative examples of families covered by this result are distributions with bounded variance (
\mathbb{E}_{F}[(X-\mu_{F})^{2}]<B
), or some characterization of a subgaussian model (assuming that
\mathbb{E}_{F}[\exp(s^{-1}(X-\mu_{F})^{2})]<B
for some known
s,B
). We detail this second setting in Appendix
D.6
. To the best of our knowledge, MED is for instance the first algorithm with regret guarantees for the bounded variance setting, and the first algorithm to match the lower bound of
Burnetas and Katehakis 1996
for a subgaussian model.

4.5
Summary and discussion

We conclude this section by proposing a summary of the results that we obtained, and discussing the insights that can be derived from them.

Asymptotic optimality of MED

First,
Corollaries
12
–
21

of Theorem
5
establish that MED is asymptotically optimal for all the families of distributions introduced in Section
1
. This finding may not be very surprising for parametric and bounded settings (though not proved yet except for the multinomial case), but is particularly insightful for models involving
h
-moment conditions given in (
18
). Indeed, this setting was so far tackled only in the uncentered case and for
h(|x|)=|x|^{1+\epsilon}
for some
\epsilon>0
, for the regret minimization problem
(
Bubeck et al. 2013
;
Agrawal et al. 2021
)
.
We allow more general definitions of
h
inspired by
Agrawal et al. 2020
and tackle the centered case, under the additional assumptions that
\mathbb{E}_{F}[h(|X|)^{2+\epsilon}]<+\infty
for some
\epsilon>0
and that the means admit a known lower bound. This analysis is permitted by the abstraction of the sufficient properties required for regret upper bounds that we propose in Assumption
2
.

Simple analysis of TS
⋆

The second type of contributions presented in this section is the novel analysis of some existing TS algorithms implemented according to our proposed variant TS
⋆
. In each case, the additional work after completing the analysis of MED consists in upper and lower bounding the BCP in a form that matches (
7
) with the function
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}
. Thanks to this reduction, the same posterior sampling method seems easier to analyze through the TS
⋆
framework than under the standard (index policy) TS.

TS as an approximation of MED?

Regarding their very similar guarantees and analysis, we can reasonably interpret that MED and TS
⋆
are actually two variants of the same exploration strategy
, at least in their asymptotic behavior
.
We propose the interpretation
that TS
⋆
can be seen as a way to approximate MED through sampling of the parameters, instead of directly computing
D_{\pi}
.
As discussed in Section
2.2.2
, since there is strong evidence that TS
⋆
and TS are (again, at least asymptotically) equivalent, we believe that this insight carries over the standard TS framework
. In the light of this result, a natural question to ask is if TS or MED should be preferred in practice. In our opinion, in the case of non-informative priors, this depends on two factors: (1) the ability to provide a TS with tight bounds on its BCP under the model
\mathcal{F}
, and (2) how costly it is to compute
D_{\pi}
compared to performing the sampling step of TS. For example, for parametric families the function
D_{\pi}
is very easy to compute, and so MED may be an interesting option, but for non-parametric families computing
D_{\pi}
(chosen close to
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}
) at each step may be burdensome and TS may be more appealing. This is the main motivation for the novel Non-Parametric TS algorithm that we propose in the next section, for non-parametric families of distributions satisfying an
h
-moment condition.

5
A simple NPTS algorithm for families with
h
-moment conditions

In this section we illustrate the benefits of the generic approach proposed in this paper for the design and analysis of novel bandit algorithms. We consider the family of distributions satisfying a
centered

h
-moment condition, which was already introduced in the previous section. The definition of this family and the assumptions made on the function
h
are detailed in Section
4.4
. We choose to study more specifically this family of distributions because it can capture non-parametric assumptions outside the traditional scope of bounded and sub-gaussian distributions. For instance, for any constants
m>1,B>0
,
\epsilon>0
and
\mu^{-}\in\mathbb{R}
, we can provide algorithms adapted to a family of distributions with bounded moments:

\mathcal{F}_{m}^{B}=\left\{F\in\mathcal{P}\;,\mathbb{E}_{F}[|X-\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]|^{m}]<B\;,\;\mathbb{E}_{F}[|X|^{2m+\epsilon}]<+\infty,\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]\geq\mu^{-}\right\}\;,

as we proved in Section
4.4.2
.
This model may be relevant in some problems where limited prior knowledge on the distributions of rewards is available.
Bubeck et al. 2013
;
Agrawal et al. 2021
provided algorithms for the uncentered version of this constraint, but they do not assume that
\mathbb{E}_{F}[|X|^{2m+\epsilon_{F}}]<+\infty
and so their model can consider heavier-tail distributions than our definition. We think that providing algorithms for the centered case is a significant advance, since it captures the class of bounded-variance distributions and a subgaussian model as discussed before, even if additional assumptions are needed for the analysis. These assumptions are furthermore not used by the algorithms that we study.
From the technical viewpoint, the centered case is more difficult, mainly because
\mathcal{F}_{m}^{B}
becomes no more convex.

Lemmas
18
and
20
respectively state that Assumption
2
holds for the divergence satisfying
D_{\pi}(F,\mu)=\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu)\text{1}(F\in\mathcal{F})
for any
F\in\mathcal{F}
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
, in both the centered and uncentered case, and hence that the corresponding MED algorithm (with
a_{n}=0
) is asymptotically optimal. However, computing
D_{\pi}
requires to solve an optimization problem at each time step, which can be computationally expensive. Building on the findings of previous sections, we naturally consider a novel Thompson Sampling algorithm for families
\mathcal{F}
satisfying a centered
h
-moment condition, as a computationally efficient version of MED.
We present all of our results and algorithms for the centered case and omit details for uncentered for simplicity, since the adaptation to the uncentered case is straightforward by replacing all expressions of the type
h(|x-\mu|)
(for some
x\in\mathbb{R}
and a “target mean”
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
) by
h(|x|)
in the algorithm and the proofs.
See also Remark
37
in Appendix
E.3

for this point.

5.1
From NPTS to
h
-NPTS

In this section we present how
h
-NPTS is derived from the existing NPTS policy.

Non-Parametric TS

We build on NPTS
(
Riou and Honda 2020
)
, since the
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}
for bounded distributions and families based on
h
-moment conditions are similarly expressed as optimization problems. Considering some data
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
and their upper bound
B
, a sampling step of NPTS returns a re-weighted mean of the form

\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}X_{i}+w_{n+1}B\;,\text{ with }(w_{1},\dots,w_{n+1})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n+1}\;,

(21)

where
\mathcal{D}_{n+1}
is the Dirichlet distribution
\mathcal{D}_{n+1}\coloneqq\text{Dir}(1,\dots,1)
, which is the uniform distribution on the simplex of dimension
n+1
. To adapt this principle, we need to tackle two questions. First, we need to replace the upper bound
B
by another “exploration bonus”, as the role of
B
is to ensure that the best arm has a reasonable chance to be sampled even if its first draws are bad. Then, we need to introduce the
h
-moment condition in the algorithm.

h
-NPTS

We propose the
h
-NPTS algorithm, combining the structure of TS
⋆
(Algorithm
3
) and a sampling step inspired by NPTS. At each time step, we first build a set
\mathcal{A}
of candidate arms with the currently optimal arms, and arms with empirical distributions that do not yet satisfy the desired
h
-moment condition.
Thanks to our assumptions on
\mathcal{F}
, this check will fail only a finite number of times in expectation. Then, “challenger” arms (currently suboptimal arms) are only
compared to the best empirical mean, that we denote by
\widehat{\mu}^{\star}
. As in NPTS, we draw some weights
(w_{1},\dots,w_{n+1})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n+1}
. Finally, we check if
there exists an exploration bonus
x\in\mathbb{R}
such that the “re-weighted” empirical distribution over
(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n},x)
belongs to the family
\mathcal{F}
with expectation at least
\widehat{\mu}^{*}
, which is expressed as

\exists x\geq\widehat{\mu}^{\star}:\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}X_{i}+w_{n+1}x\geq\widehat{\mu}^{\star}\;,\;\text{and }\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}h(|X_{i}-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}|)+w_{n+1}(h(|x-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}|)-\gamma)\leq B\;,

(22)

where
\gamma>0
is a parameter of the algorithm that slightly advantages the exploration bonus, that we introduce for technical reasons. If (
22
) holds for an arm
k
, then it is added to
\mathcal{A}
. Interestingly, this condition can be checked with a closed formula, and so no optimization is needed.
To be more specific (see Appendix
E.4
for details), (
22
) is equivalent to

h\left(\left(\frac{1}{w_{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\widehat{\mu}^{\star}-X_{i})\right)^{+}\right)\leq B+\gamma+\frac{1}{w_{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(B-h(|X_{i}-\widehat{\mu}^{\star}|))\;,

(23)

where
(x)^{+}=\max\{x,0\}
. This check is relatively easy to implement, which is another advantage of the new algorithm structure that we introduced. On the other hand, working with (
22
) will be more convenient in the theoretical analysis. We provide the detailed
h
-NPTS in Algorithm
4
, that is arguably much simpler to implement than other optimal algorithms for this setting.

5.2
Analysis

Since we already proved Assumption
2
in Section
4.4
, in order to derive a regret upper bound thanks to Theorem
5
, it remains to prove Assumption
4
on the sampling probabilities for
h
-NPTS.
Thanks to the TS
⋆
framework, as discussed in Section
2.2.2
, this can be done by upper and lower bounding the
Boundary Crossing Probabilities
(BCP) of
h
-NPTS. To do that, for any parameters
\gamma>0,\eta>0
we define the following function

\Lambda_{\eta,\gamma}^{\star}(F,\mu)=\max_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{R}_{2}^{\eta,\gamma}}\mathbb{E}_{F}\left[\log(1-\alpha(X-\mu)-\beta(B-h(|X-\mu|))\right]\;,

where
\mathcal{R}_{2}^{\eta,\gamma}=\{(\alpha,\beta)\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2}\;:\;\forall x\in\mathbb{R}\;,\;1-\alpha(x+\eta-\mu)-\beta(B+\gamma-h(|x-\mu|)\geq 0\}
. When
\eta=0
we use the notation
\mathcal{R}^{\gamma}
and
\Lambda_{\gamma}^{\star}
. We prove in Appendix
E.5
that the sampling probabilities of
h
-NPTS satisfy Assumption
4
, with
D_{\pi}:(F,\mu)\mapsto\Lambda_{\gamma}^{\star}(F,\mu)\text{1}(F\in\mathcal{F})
, where
\Lambda_{\gamma}^{\star}
matches
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}
for

\gamma\downarrow 0
.
The proof is based on the following upper and lower bounds on the BCP.

Lemma 0

(Bounds on the BCP)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. observations with empirical cdf
F_{n}
, and consider any threshold
\mu^{\star}>\mathbb{E}_{F_{n}}[X]
.
Using the notation
Z_{i}=h(|X_{i}-\mu|)
for
i\in[n]
and
Z_{n+1}=h(|X_{n+1}-\mu|)-\gamma
, let the Boundary Crossing Probability be

\text{\emph{[BCP]}}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\left(\exists X_{n+1}\geq\mu^{\star}:\;\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{i}X_{i}\geq\mu^{\star},\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{i}Z_{i}\leq B\right)\;.

Firstly, there exists a mapping
C
such that for any
\eta>0
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}

\text{\emph{[BCP]}}\leq C(F_{n},\eta)\times e^{-n\Lambda_{\eta,\gamma}^{\star}(F_{n},\mu^{\star})}\;,

(24)

where
C
is continuous w.r.t. the Wasserstein metric in
F_{n}
, is continuous in
\eta
, and scales in
\eta^{-2}
. Furthermore, for any
\delta\geq 0
, there exists two constants
n_{\delta,\gamma}\in\mathbb{N}
and
c_{\delta}>0
such that

\text{\emph{[BCP]}}\geq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}c_{\delta}\times e^{-n\left(\Lambda_{\gamma}^{\star}(F_{n},\mu^{\star})+\delta\right)}&\mbox{if }n\geq n_{\delta,\gamma}\;,\\
e^{-n\log(nC_{B,\mu})}&\hskip-34.1433pt\mbox{if }n\leq n_{\delta,\gamma},\;\text{ with }C_{B,\mu}=\max\left\{\frac{3h^{-1}(B)-\mu}{h^{-1}(B)-\mu},\frac{B}{B-h\left(\frac{h^{-1}(B)-\mu}{2}\right)}\right\}\;.\end{array}\right.

The detailed proofs and constants for the upper and lower bounds are presented respectively in Appendices
E.1
and
E.3
. These two results are novel, and our proof techniques are of independent interest. Indeed, some recent works have focused on proving similar anti-concentration bounds for weighted sums, with weights drawn from a Dirichlet distribution
(
Riou and Honda 2020
;
Baudry et al. 2021b
;
Tiapkin et al. 2022
)
. Unfortunately, their results are not enough for our setting since they consider the BCP associated with the weighted sum (
21
) (bounded distributions),
while we need to consider the joint distribution of the weighted sum and the moment condition as given in (
22
).
Instead,
as an intermediate step in our analysis we prove a novel lower bound for this case in Appendix
E.2
(Lemma
35
). This bound can thus compare with the results provided in these works. We can for instance remark that the lower bound of
Tiapkin et al. 2022
is tighter than ours, but they require to increase the exploration bonus in (
21
) from
B
to
2B
and its parameter in the Dirichlet distribution from
1
to
\Omega(\log(n))
, which is not suitable to our setting.

Proof ideas

We upper bound the BCP by partitioning the possible values for
X_{n+1}
as
(x_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}=(\mu+j\eta)_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and using a union bound. Then, we upper bound each term using the Chernoff method, and denoting by
(\alpha_{n}^{\star},\beta_{n}^{\star})
the optimizers of
\Lambda_{\eta,\gamma}^{\star}(F_{n},\mu))
we obtain that

\text{[BCP]}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-n\Lambda_{\eta,\gamma}^{\star}(F_{n},\mu^{\star})}}{1-\alpha_{n}^{\star}(x_{j}+\eta-\mu)-\beta_{n}^{\star}(B+\gamma-h(|x_{j}-\mu|))}\;.

At this step, we can remark that once
x_{j}
is large enough the denominator is decreasing and dominated by the term
h(|x_{j}-\mu|)
, that satisfies
h(|x_{j}-\mu|)\geq|x_{j}-\mu|^{1+\eta}
for some
\eta>0
when
x_{j}
is large enough by definition of
\mathcal{F}
. Hence, only a finite number of terms are actually significant and the sum converges.

For the lower bound, we select a proper value for
X_{n+1}
, use exponential tilting to get the term in
e^{-n\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}(F_{n},\mu^{\star})}
from a change of distribution, and then analyze the probability that (
22
) hold under the new (more favorable) distribution of the weights. The parameter
\gamma>0
ensures that even in the least favorable cases this probability is larger than a constant when
n
is large enough, which concludes the proof. The result may still hold for
\gamma=0
, but proving it would require additional technicality. Finally, for
n\leq n_{\delta}
our lower bound is simply
\mathbb{P}\left(w_{n+1}\geq 1-\frac{1}{C_{B,\mu}n}\right)
.

We now state the main result of this section, which is a regret upper bound for
h
-NPTS.

Theorem 23

(Logarithmic regret for
h
-NPTS)
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is defined by a centered
h
-moment condition (Eq. (
20
)), then for any
c>0
,
\gamma>0
and for any sub-optimal arm
k
,
h
-NPTS satisfies

\mathbb{E}[N_{k}(T)]\leq\frac{1+c}{\Lambda_{\gamma}^{\star}(F_{k},\mu^{\star})}\log(T)+o_{c}(\log(T))\;.

We detail the proof in Appendix
E.5
, where we show that Lemma
22
gives sufficient bounds on the sampling probability of each arm in
h
-NPTS, and then use Corollary
5
. As
\gamma>0
, the algorithm may not be asymptotically optimal, since
\mathcal{R}_{2}^{\gamma}\subset\mathcal{R}_{2}^{0}
. However, this is the case only for problems for which arm
k
is “far” from being optimal, so the constant before the logarithm is small: if for some distribution
F
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
the optimizers in
\mathcal{R}_{2}^{0}
belong to
\mathcal{R}_{2}^{\gamma}
then
\Lambda_{\gamma}^{\star}(F,\mu)=\Lambda_{0}^{\star}(F,\mu)
.

Theorem
23
completes the picture that
h
-NPTS is an easy to implement, computationally efficient, and theoretically sound alternative to MED for distributions satisfying a centered
h
-moment condition. In our opinion, our methodology to derive and analyze this algorithm shows another interest of the general insights provided in this paper: we first tried to find the best function
D_{\pi}
for which MED would work for this family, and then provided a TS algorithm in order to approach this MED strategy with sampling.

Remark 0

(Further generalization of
h
-NPTS)
.

As stated above, the algorithm and its analysis are first easily translated to the uncentered case. Furthermore, we think that the same proof techniques as for Lemma
22
may be used with only minor changes if several conditions with functions
h_{1},\dots,h_{m}
and constants
B_{1},\dots,B_{m}
were considered, for
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

Experimental Conclusions

These experiments, conducted in both parametric and non-parametric settings, provide strong empirical support for the claim in Section
2.2.2
that TS
⋆
is a close variant of TS. The two policies exhibit nearly identical performance across most cases, with TS
⋆
occasionally outperforming TS slightly in terms of regret, with significantly improved computational efficiency in non-parametric settings. Our results show that MED consistently underperforms compared to TS and TS
⋆
in parametric settings but matches their performance in non-parametric cases. The finite-time performance gap between MED and TS in parametric settings could be attributed to potential polynomial multiplicative factors (bounded by
[c_{n},C_{n}]
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
) in the sampling probabilities for sub-optimal arms. Finally, our experiments with
h
-NPTS confirm that it is indeed an efficient approach –in terms both of performance and computation time compared to MED– to tackle in a principled way the families of distributions characterized by a simple non-parametric assumption.

7
Conclusion and perspectives

In this paper we provided a general recipe to derive regret bounds for randomized bandit algorithms, that allowed us to revisit the Minimum Empirical Divergence (MED) and Thompson Sampling (TS) algorithms for several families of distributions. Guided by our theoretical results, we suggest that
a slight variant of
TS may be interpreted as a way to approximate MED through sampling, that is appealing when the sampling probabilities of MED are hard to compute. Driven by these new insights, we could study in more details some families of distributions satisfying some
h
-moment conditions, for example
distributions with a known variance upper bound. We proved the optimality of a MED algorithm in this setting, and proposed the more computationally efficient
h
-NPTS. While its analysis is intricate,
h
-NPTS is very simple to implement and has (close to optimal) logarithmic regret, making it an appealing solution under such model.

An open question remains as to whether supplementary conditions could be identified to achieve the
\sqrt{KT}
problem-independent bound up to logarithmic factors, which would enable a unified approach to addressing all facets of frequentist regret analysis.
Finally, an interesting research direction may be to find an equivalent unified proof for deterministic algorithms, that would for instance allow to analyze KL-UCB and IMED under the same framework. In particular, it would be interesting to identify if the same Assumption
2
is sufficient for these algorithms, and how Assumption
1
translates to index comparisons in the deterministic case.

B.1
Presentation of Assumption
4
(relaxation of Assumption
1

The relaxation is based on two simple facts from the analysis: (1) the upper bound on the sampling probability is only necessary when the empirical distribution of a sub-optimal arm is “close enough” to its true distribution, and (2) a slightly looser lower bound is sufficient in the analysis if an arbitrary precision in the exponential term can be reached, and (3) these two conditions are only needed for “large enough” sample size. Hence, while its statement is relatively heavy (compared to Assumption
1
), the intuition behind the relaxation is very natural. While we showed that all MED policies satisfy Assumption
1
directly, we need the results to hold under Assumption
4
for the analysis of several TS
⋆
policies, including
h
-NPTS in Section
5
.

Assumption 4

(Relaxation of Assumption
1
)
.

Upper bound:

F_{k}
(defined with any metric), denoted by

such that, for all
t\in[T]

N_{k}(t)\geq n_{0},\;F_{k}(t)\in\mathcal{B}(F_{k})\;\Longrightarrow\;p_{k}^{\pi}(t)\leq C_{N_{k}(t)}\exp\left(-N_{k}(t)\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{k}(t),\mu_{\star}(t))}{1+a_{N_{k}(t)}}\right)\;,

(25)

where

through the definition of

for some

,

For any

\left\{\mathcal{G}(t)\cap\bar{\mathcal{J}}_{k}(t)\right\}\subset\mathcal{M}_{k}(t)\coloneqq\left\{\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{k}(t),\mu_{1}-\epsilon)}{1+a_{N_{k}(t)}}\leq\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1})}{1+c}\right\}\cup\{F_{k}(t)\notin\mathcal{B}_{k}(F_{k})\}\;.

For simplicity we denote by
\mathcal{M}_{k,n}
the corresponding event replacing
F_{k}(t)
by

. Then,

(F2)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=u}^{T-1}\text{1}(A_{t+1}=k,N_{k}(t)>u,\mathcal{G}(t),\bar{\mathcal{J}}_{k}(t))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=u}^{T-1}\text{1}(A_{t+1}=k,N_{k}(t)>u,\mathcal{M}_{k}(t))\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=u}^{T-1}\sum_{n=u}^{T}\text{1}(A_{t+1}=k,N_{k}(t)=n,\mathcal{M}_{k,n})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{n=u}^{T-1}\text{1}(\mathcal{M}_{k,n})\sum_{t=u}^{T-1}\text{1}(A_{t+1}=k,N_{k}(t)=n)\right]

We then upper bound
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{M}_{k,n})
by the sum of the probabilities of the two events, and thus obtain that

(\text{F2})\leq\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{k}(t),\mu_{1}-\epsilon)}{1+a_{N_{k}(t)}}\leq\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1})}{1+c}\right)+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(F_{k,n}\notin\mathcal{B}(F_{k})),

which gives the term
B_{1}
in the statement of Lemma

4
. Note that this term is upper bounded by
0
under Assumption
1

.

Upper bounding (F3)

We use a proof scheme from

to obtain that

(F3)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=u}^{T-1}\text{1}(N_{k}(t)>u,A_{t+1}=k,\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=u}^{T-1}\text{1}(N_{k}(t)>u,\bar{\mathcal{G}}_{t})\times p_{1}^{\pi}(t)\times\frac{p_{k}^{\pi}(t)}{p_{1}^{\pi}(t)}\right]

B.3
Proof of Theorem

We start the proof of the theorem from the intermediate bound of Lemma

. The remaining steps consist in further upper bounding
B(c,\epsilon)
, assuming that Assumption

holds in full. We recall that

\displaystyle B(c,\epsilon)=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\sum_{n=u}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(D_{\pi}(F_{k,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)\frac{1+c}{1+a_{n}}\leq D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1})\right)}_{B_{1}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sum_{n=1}^{T}c_{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\text{1}(\mu_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon)e^{n\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)+\eta}{1+a_{n}}}\right]}_{B_{2}}

Upper bounding
B_{1}

We first re-arrange this term in order to use (A2) in Assumption
2
.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D_{\pi}(F_{k,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)\frac{1+c}{1+a_{n}}\leq D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1})\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(D_{\pi}(F_{k,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)\leq D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)-\left(D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)-D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1})\frac{1+a_{n}}{1+c}\right)\right)\;.

The rest of the argument consist in finding an appropriate tuning of
\epsilon
, as a function of
c
such that the right-hand term
\delta_{\epsilon,c}\coloneqq D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)-D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1})\frac{1+a_{n}}{1+c}
(that will be the
\delta
used in (A2)) is a positive constant.
We first remove
a_{n}
by using the notation
a_{\infty}=\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}
, and since
c>a_{\infty}
, we can deduce that there exists a rank

such that, for all

. Hence, we obtain that

\forall n\geq n_{c},\;\delta_{\epsilon,c}\geq D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)-D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1})\left(1-\frac{c-a_{\infty}}{2(1+c)}\right)\;.

We now proceed to tune
\epsilon
, first recalling that
D_{\pi}
is continuous and non-decreasing in the second argument ((A1) in
2
). Although choosing
\epsilon>0
is necessary to upper bound

, if we could consider

we would be able to use

\delta_{0,c}=D(F_{k},\mu_{1})\times\frac{c-a_{\infty}}{2(1+c)}>0.

Additionally, it is immediate that for
\epsilon\geq\Delta_{k}
we would get
\delta_{\Delta_{k},c}\leq 0
. Hence, by the intermediate value theorem we are guaranteed that there exists a value of
\epsilon
, that we denote by
\epsilon_{c}
, satisfying

\epsilon_{c}>0,\text{and }\delta_{c}\coloneqq\delta_{\epsilon_{c},c}=D(F_{k},\mu_{1})\times\frac{c-a_{\infty}}{4(1+c)}>0\;.

Then, using property (A2) of Assumption
2
provides that
B_{1}\leq n_{c}+A_{\delta_{c}}<+\infty
, which also fixes
\epsilon
to
\epsilon_{c}
for the rest of the proof, although we keep writing
\epsilon
for simplicity.

Upper bounding
B_{2}

We directly start by using (A4), which states that there exists a constant
\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}
such that, if the empirical distribution of arm

satisfies
\mu_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon
, then

D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1})\geq D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)+\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}\;.

Then, just as we fixed
\epsilon
as a function of
c
in last paragraph, we now fix the parameter
\eta
of Lemma
25
as a function of
\epsilon\coloneqq\epsilon_{c}
(hence, as a function of
c
). Indeed, we choose
\eta=\frac{\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}}{2}
, and thus get that, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\text{1}(\mu_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon)e^{n\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)+\eta}{1+a_{n}}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-n\frac{\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}}{2(1+a_{n})}}\times\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\text{1}(\mu_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon)e^{n\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1})}{1+a_{n}}}\right]}_{B_{2}^{\prime}}\;,

and we then further upper bound the term denoted by
B_{2}^{\prime}
using (A4), as follows. We first write that

B_{2}^{\prime}\leq\mathbb{P}(\mu_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon)+\mathbb{E}\left[e^{n\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1})}{1+a_{n}}}-1\right]\;,

where we dropped the indicator in the expectation. Then, remarking that the random variable inside the expectation is non-negative we can further write that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{n\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1})}{1+a_{n}}}-1\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(e^{n\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{n},\mu_{1})}{1+a_{n}}}-1>x\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(D_{\pi}(F_{n},\mu_{1})>\frac{1+a_{n}}{n}\log\left(1+x)\right)\right)\mathrm{d}x

Let us first consider the case
a_{n}>0
in the theorem. Then, by using (A4) we simply get that

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{n\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1})}{1+a_{n}}}-1\right]\leq\alpha_{n}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{(1+x)^{1+a_{n}}}=\frac{\alpha_{n}}{a_{n}}\;,

so in that case we obtain that

\displaystyle B_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}e^{-n\frac{\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}}{2(1+a_{n})}}\times\mathbb{P}(\mu_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon)+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\alpha_{n}\times c_{n}}{a_{n}}e^{-n\frac{\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}}{2(1+a_{n})}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}e^{-n\frac{\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}}{2(1+a_{n})}}+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\alpha_{n}\times c_{n}}{a_{n}}e^{-n\frac{\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}}{2(1+a_{n})}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(1+a_{n})}{\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}}+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\alpha_{n}\times c_{n}}{a_{n}}e^{-n\frac{\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}}{2(1+a_{n})}}\;,

where we wrote the first line to show that an exponential bound for
\mathbb{P}(\mu_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon)
can further tighten the result, but is not necessary. We thus obtain that

B_{2}\leq\frac{2(1+a_{n})}{\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}}+\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\frac{\alpha_{n}\times c_{n}}{a_{n}}e^{-n\frac{\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}}{2(1+a_{n})}}\;.

(32)

(\alpha_{n},c_{n},a_{n}^{-1})
lead to the conclusion that
\frac{\alpha_{n}\times c_{n}}{a_{n}}=o\left(e^{-n\frac{\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{1}}}{4(1+a_{n})}}\right)
, so the sum converges. This proves that
B_{2}<+\infty
.

We now consider the second case, where
a_{n}=0
but
D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1})\leq D_{\mu_{1}}^{+}
for some constant
D_{\mu_{1}}^{+}<+\infty
. This time, we first obtain that

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{n\frac{D_{\pi}(F_{1,n},\mu_{1})}{1+a_{n}}}-1\right]\leq\alpha_{n}\int_{0}^{e^{\frac{nD_{\mu_{1}}^{+}}{1+a_{n}}}-1}\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{1+x}=\alpha_{n}\times nD_{\mu_{1}}^{+}\;.

We then apply the exact same steps as in the previous case and reach the same conclusion, with the term
\frac{\alpha_{n}\times c_{n}}{a_{n}}
being replaced by
\alpha_{n}\times c_{n}\times nD_{\mu_{1}}^{+}
. This concludes the proof of Theorem
5
.

∎

B.4
Mean estimate for heavy-tailed distributions

We now show that if the mean is expected to concentrate slowly, a robust estimator may be used instead of the empirical mean. As an example, we consider a truncation-based estimator presented for instance in

Bubeck et al. 2013
.
We consider a sequence
(u_{n})_{n\geq 0}
and further assume that
\mathbb{E}[|X|^{1+\delta}]\leq B
for some
\delta>0,B\geq 0
. Given observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, the truncation-based estimator returns

\mu_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\text{1}(|X_{i}|\leq u_{n}).

Then, denoting by
\widetilde{\mu}_{n}
the expectation of the truncated variable we can follow the proof of
Bubeck et al. 2013
to first show that
\widetilde{\mu}_{n}\geq\mu_{1}-\frac{B}{u_{n}^{\epsilon}}
(Markov inequality), and then obtain with Bernstein inequality that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\text{1}(|X_{i}|\leq u_{n})\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}\text{1}(|X_{i}|\leq u_{n})-\widetilde{\mu}_{n})\leq(\mu_{1}-\widetilde{\mu}_{n})-\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{8}}{nBu_{n}^{1-\epsilon}+\frac{1}{3}u_{n}\epsilon}\right)\;,

and so, for any
\epsilon>0
,
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}c_{n}\mathbb{P}(\mu_{n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon)
converges for instance by taking
u_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n)
:
u_{n}=n
leads to a bound on the probabilities that is exponentially decreasing in
n^{\epsilon}
.

Note that this change has no incidence on the proof of Theorem
5
, as long as the family
\mathcal{F}
and the bandit algorithm satisfy the other assumptions. Indeed, in the proof of the theorem the explicit form of
\mu_{n}
is never used.

BCP for TS with conjugate posterior for SPEF

Lemma 0

(BCP for SPEF)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a SPEF. Assume that each arm
k
from the bandit problem satisfies
\mu_{k}\in[\mu_{0}^{-},\mu_{0}^{+}]
for some finite and known constants
\mu_{0}^{-},\mu_{0}^{+}
. Then, the BCP of SPEF-TS
⋆
(Algorithm
5
) with a conjugate prior/posterior satisfies Equation (
7
), so the algorithm satisfies Assumption
1
. Furthermore,
c_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n)
and
C_{n}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
.

Proof.

We directly obtain the result using Lemma 4 of
Kaufmann 2018
, stating that for any
(\mu_{k,n},\mu)\in[\mu_{0}^{-},\mu_{0}^{+}]^{2}
(note that in Algorithm
5
the means are clipped if this is not the case) it holds that

An^{-1}e^{-n\mathrm{kl}(\mu_{k,n},\mu)}\leq\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\mu}_{k,n}\geq\mu)\leq B\sqrt{n}e^{-n\mathrm{kl}(\mu_{k,n},\mu)}\;,

(33)

for some positive constants
A,B
.
∎

The assumption that the means belong to a range
[\mu_{0}^{-},\mu_{0}^{+}]
may be reasonable in practice and avoids difficult cases in the limits of the support, that would require specific arguments for each SPEF.

The polynomial terms in the upper and lower bounds may be improved, for instance by using that (see
Kaufmann 2018
) that if the prior has density
p
, then for any threshold
\mu_{\star}
the BCP satisfies

\pi_{n}(\mu\geq\mu_{\star})=\frac{\int_{\mu_{\star}}^{\mu_{0}^{+}}p(x)\exp\left(-n\mathrm{kl}(\mu_{n},x)\right)\mathrm{d}x}{\int_{\mu_{0}^{-}}^{\mu_{0}^{+}}p(x)\exp\left(-n\mathrm{kl}(\mu_{n},x)\right)\mathrm{d}x}\;,

(34)

and then using that (see e.g.
Jin et al. 2022
) for any
\mu\leq\mu^{\prime}
,
\mathrm{kl}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=\int_{\mu^{\prime}}^{\mu}\frac{x-\mu}{V(x)}\mathrm{d}x
, and so

\frac{(\mu^{\prime}-\mu)^{2}}{2V}\leq\mathrm{kl}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})\leq\frac{(\mu^{\prime}-\mu)^{2}}{2v}\;.

(35)

This quadratic form for the
\mathrm{kl}
then allows to use results on Gaussian integrals to derive finer upper and lower bounds on the BCP.

C.4
BCP for NPTS for bounded distributions

We use the following result to analyze NPTS
⋆
, implemented according to Algorithm
7
.

Lemma 0

(Bounds on the BCP for NPTS
⋆
)
.

NPTS
⋆
satisfies Assumption
4
with
D_{\pi}=\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}
, as its Boundary Crossing Probability satisfy
Equation (
25
) (upper bound) and (
26
) (lower bound) for
(c_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
that is polynomial in the parameter
\eta
considered in the equation, and
C_{n}=\mathcal{O}(n)
.

Proof.

Our proof is based on Lemmas 15 (upper bound) and 14 (lower bound) in
Riou and Honda 2020
. In their Lemma 15, the authors prove that the BCP satisfies for any
\eta\in(0,1)
and
\mu\geq\mu_{k,n}

\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\mu}_{k,n}\geq\mu)\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\exp\left(-n\left(\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{k,n},\mu)-\eta\frac{\mu}{B-\mu}\right)\right)\;.

Hence, we can use this result directly by choosing
\eta=1/n
. We then obtain

\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\mu}_{k,n}\geq\mu)\leq ne^{\frac{\mu}{B-\mu}}\exp\left(-n\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{k,n},\mu)\right)\;,

which is sufficient to prove that Equation (
25
) holds for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Slightly adapting the lower bound presented in Lemma 14 of
Riou and Honda 2020
, we obtain that for
n
large enough and
\mu\geq\mu_{k,n}

\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\mu}_{k,n}\geq\mu)\geq Cn^{-\frac{M+2}{2}}\exp\left(-n\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(\widetilde{F}_{k,n},\mu)\right)\;,

where
\widetilde{F}_{k,n}
is a
discretized
version of the empirical distribution
F_{k,n}
, considering the truncated observations
\widetilde{X}_{k,i}=\frac{1}{M}\left\lfloor MX_{k,i}\right\rfloor
for some integer
M\in\mathbb{N}
, and for some constant
C=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)^{M}e^{-(M+1)/12}
.

Fixing
M
, we can control the deviation of
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(\widetilde{F}_{k,n},\mu)
from
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(\widetilde{F}_{k,n},\mu)
. First, we remark that in the worst case
1/M
is subtracted to all observations. In that case, reaching the mean
\mu
is equivalent to reaching the mean
\mu+1/M
with the initial (non-truncated) distribution and upper bound
B+1/M
. We formalize this by denoting by
\mathcal{F}_{B}
the family of distributions bounded by some constant
B
, and writing that

\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}_{B}}(\widetilde{F}_{k,n},\mu)\leq\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}_{B+1/M}}(F_{k,n},\mu+1/M)\leq\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}_{B}}(F_{k,n},\mu+1/M)\;.

We conclude by using Lemma 5 in
Cappé et al. 2013
:

\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}_{B}}(F_{k,n},\mu+1/M)\leq\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}_{B}}(F_{k,n},\mu)+\frac{1}{M(B-\mu)}\;.

Finally, to obtain the desired form of Equation (
26

\eta>0
(different from the
\eta
above) we simply choose

, which provides

\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{\mu}_{k,n}\geq\mu)\geq\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\right)^{\frac{B-\mu}{\eta}}e^{-\left(\frac{B-\mu}{\eta}+1\right)/12}n^{-\left(\frac{B-\mu}{2\eta}+1\right)}\exp\left(-n(\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{k,n},\mu)+\eta)\right)\;.

This is sufficient to conclude that Equation (
26
) holds, and hence to verify Assumption
4
. The polynomial
(c_{n})
depends on
\eta
, which is not a problem since a fixed value of
\eta>0
is considered in the proofs where this lower bound is used. We can remark that we could also prove the lower bound of Assumption
1
directly by choosing
\eta=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{n})
.
∎

D.1
Single-Parameter Exponential Families (SPEF)

In Section
4.1

\mathrm{kl}
when it takes as arguments the expectation of the two distributions. We now prove Lemma

.

(A1):

\mathrm{kl}
is known to be continuous in its second argument, non-decreasing (direct with (
15

\mathrm{kl}(\mu,\mu^{\prime})=0
if
\mu\geq\mu^{\prime}
by definition.

(A2):

we use the continuity of the
\mathrm{kl}
in the first argument: for any
\delta>0
, there exists a constant
\delta^{\prime}>0
for which
\{x:\mathrm{kl}(x,\mu_{1})\leq\mathrm{kl}(\mu_{k},\mu_{1})-\delta\}\subset\{x:x\geq\mu_{k}+\delta^{\prime}\}
. Then, using (
16
) we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{kl}(\mu_{k,n},\mu_{1})\leq\mathrm{kl}(\mu_{k},\mu_{1})-\delta)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\mu_{k,n}\geq\mu_{k}+\delta^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-n\mathrm{kl}(\mu_{k}+\delta^{\prime},\mu_{k})\right)\;,

which is enough to prove (A2).

(A3)

holds with
\delta_{\epsilon,\mu}=\mathrm{kl}(\mu_{1}-\epsilon,\mu_{1})
. Indeed, with (
15
) for
\mu_{n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon
we can write that

\displaystyle\mathrm{kl}(\mu_{n},\mu_{1})-\mathrm{kl}(\mu_{n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mu_{1}-\epsilon}^{\mu_{1}}\frac{x-\mu_{n}}{V(x)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mu_{1}-\epsilon}^{\mu_{1}}\frac{x-(\mu_{1}-\epsilon)}{V(x)}\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\mu_{1}-\epsilon}^{\mu_{1}}\frac{\mu_{1}-\epsilon-\mu_{n}}{V(x)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\mathrm{kl}(\mu_{1}-\epsilon,\mu_{1})+(\mu_{1}-\epsilon-\mu_{n})\int_{\mu_{1}-\epsilon}^{\mu_{1}}\frac{1}{V(x)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\geq\mathrm{kl}(\mu_{1}-\epsilon,\mu_{1})\;.

Finally,
(A4)
also holds directly with
\alpha_{n}=1
using Chernoff inequality (
16
), by mapping the level sets of
\mathrm{kl}
with level sets for the mean.

D.2
Gaussian distributions with unknown variances

In this section we prove Lemma
13
and Corollary
14
. This case is interesting because the family is still parametric but the fact that there are two parameters make the proofs less direct than for SPEF. We first prove the lemma, and then detail how the proof scheme of Theorem
6
can still be used to prove Corollary
14
.

Proof of Lemma
13

Let us consider a distribution
F_{n}
, that is the empirical distribution of
n
observations, and denote by
\mu_{n}
and
\sigma_{n}^{2}
respectively its expectation and variance, clipped to the range
[\mu_{0}^{-},+\infty)
and
(0,V]
. Then, for any
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
we recall that

\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{n},\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{(\mu-\mu_{n})^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\right)\text{1}(\mu\geq\mu_{n})\;,

which is obtained by computing the KL-divergence between the distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{n},\sigma_{n}^{2})
and the distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma_{n}^{2}+(\mu-\mu_{n})^{2})
. We recall that we consider
D_{\pi}=\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}
. First, the function is clearly continuous and non-decreasing in
\mu\geq\mu_{n}
, and
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{n},\mu_{n})=0
by definition, so
(A1)
holds.

(A2)

does not require a tight concentration inequality. We use a similar argument as in Section
D.1
: for a fixed
\delta>0
, if
D_{\pi}(F_{k,n},\mu_{1})\leq D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1})-\delta
then there exists two constants
\delta_{1}>0
and
\delta_{2}>0
, such that either
|\mu_{k,n}-\mu_{k}|>\delta_{1}
or
\sigma_{k,n}/\sigma_{k}\geq 1+\delta_{2}
. Essentially, at least one of the two empirical parameters is deviating from its true value. Hence, using Chernoff inequality for the means and Lemma 7 of
Chan 2020
for the standard deviation we obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D_{\pi}(F_{k,n},\mu_{1})\leq D_{\pi}(F_{k},\mu_{1})-\delta\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(\mu_{k,n}\geq\mu_{k}+\delta_{1})+\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{\sigma_{k,n}}{\sigma_{k}}\geq 1+\delta_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-n\frac{\delta_{1}^{2}}{2\sigma_{k}^{2}}}+e^{\frac{n-1}{2}(\log(1+\delta_{2})-\delta_{2})},

which proves that (A2) holds, since
\log(1+\delta_{2})-\delta_{2}<0
(it is furthermore equivalent to
\frac{\delta_{2}^{2}}{2}
for small
\delta_{2}
). Note that for this property the arguments are unaffected by clipping.

(A3)

is the condition that requires clipping the empirical parameters. Indeed, for any
\mu\geq\mu_{n}
, using the notation
\Delta_{n}=\mu-\mu_{n}
and that
\Delta_{n}\geq\epsilon
we can first obtain the following lower bound:

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{n},\mu_{1})-\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{n},\mu_{1}-\epsilon)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{\epsilon^{2}-2\Delta_{n}\epsilon}{\Delta_{n}^{2}+\sigma_{n}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\times\frac{2\Delta_{n}\epsilon-\epsilon^{2}}{\Delta_{n}^{2}+\sigma_{n}^{2}}\;.

Unfortunately this term can be arbitrarily small if
\Delta_{n}^{2}
or
\sigma_{n}^{2}
are too large. With clipped estimates, we can however use that
\sigma_{n}^{2}\leq V
and
\Delta_{n}\leq R_{\mu}\coloneqq\mu-\mu_{0}^{-}
. To simplify the analysis, we consider two case. First, if
\Delta_{n}\geq\sigma_{n}
we obtain that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\times\frac{2\Delta_{n}\epsilon-\epsilon^{2}}{\Delta_{n}^{2}+\sigma_{n}^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\times\frac{2\Delta_{n}\epsilon-\epsilon^{2}}{\Delta_{n}^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\times\left(2\frac{\epsilon}{\Delta_{n}}-\left(\frac{\epsilon}{\Delta_{n}}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{R}+\frac{\epsilon}{\Delta_{n}}\left(1-\frac{\epsilon}{\Delta_{n}}\right)\right)\geq\frac{\epsilon}{4R}\;.

This gives a first part of the result. For the second part, we consider
\Delta_{n}\leq\sigma_{n}
, and simply obtain that

\frac{1}{2}\times\frac{2\Delta_{n}\epsilon-\epsilon^{2}}{\Delta_{n}^{2}+\sigma_{n}^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{4}\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{\sigma_{n}^{2}}\geq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4V}\;,

which proves (A3) with
\delta_{\epsilon,\mu}=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{4\left\{R\vee V\right\}}
.

(A4):

Contrarily to (A2), we need this time a tight concentration inequality for
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{n},\mu_{1})
, and hence cannot handle separately the mean and variance. To do that, we directly express the distribution of
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}
according to the Student distribution. We present our result in Lemma
30
below, which is (to the best of our knowledge) a novel result of independent interest.

Lemma 0

(Concentration of
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{n},\mu_{1})
for Gaussian distributions)
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is the family of gaussian distributions,
F\in\mathcal{F}
has mean
\mu_{1}
and
F_{n}
is the empirical distribution computed from
n
i.i.d. observations from
F
, then for any
n\geq 2
,
x_{0}>0
and
x\geq x_{0}
it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{n},\mu_{1})\geq x\right)\leq\sqrt{\frac{8}{\pi(1-e^{-2x_{0}})}}e^{-(n-1)x}\;.

Proof.

We know that
S_{n}=\sqrt{n}\frac{\Delta_{n}}{\sigma_{n}}
follows a Student distribution with
n-1
degrees of freedom. We write the probability in (A3) in terms of
S_{n}
, and obtain

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{n},\mu_{1})\geq x)=\mathbb{P}\left(S_{n}\geq\sqrt{n}\sqrt{e^{2x}-1}\right)\;.

We then compute the density of
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{n},\mu_{1})
according to the density of the Student distribution with
n-1
degrees of freedom, denoted by
f_{n}
, using the formula for monotonous transformations of continuous random variables:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{n},\mu_{1})\leq x)}{\mathrm{d}x}=\sqrt{n}\frac{e^{2x}}{\sqrt{e^{2x}-1}}\times f_{n}(\sqrt{n(e^{2x}-1)})\;.

Now we use that
f_{n}(y)=C_{n}e^{-\frac{n}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{y^{2}}{n-1}\right)}
, with
C_{n}=\frac{\Gamma(n/2)}{\sqrt{(n-1)\pi}\Gamma((n-1)/2)}
, and obtain

f_{n}(\sqrt{n(e^{2x}-1)})=C_{n}e^{-\frac{n}{2}\log\left(1+\frac{n(e^{2x}-1)}{n-1}\right)}\leq C_{n}e^{-nx}\;.

(38)

We then consider
x\geq x_{0}>0
, and obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{n},\mu_{1})\leq x)}{\mathrm{d}x}\leq\sqrt{\frac{n}{1-e^{-2x}}}C_{n}e^{-(n-1)x}\leq\sqrt{\frac{n}{1-e^{-2x_{0}}}}C_{n}e^{-(n-1)x}\;.

By integrating this bound (since all the terms are positive), we obtain that for
x\geq x_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{n},\mu_{1})\geq x)

\displaystyle=\int_{x}^{+\infty}\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{n},\mu_{1})\leq y)

\displaystyle=\int_{x}^{+\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{n},\mu_{1})\leq y)}{\mathrm{d}y}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\int_{x}^{+\infty}\sqrt{\frac{n}{1-e^{-2x_{0}}}}C_{n}e^{-(n-1)y}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{n}{1-e^{-2x_{0}}}}\frac{C_{n}}{n-1}e^{-(n-1)x}\;.

We now analyze more precisely
C_{n}
. Using a basic property of the Gamma function, we have that
\Gamma(n/2)=(n/2)\Gamma((n-2)/2)
, so
C_{n}\leq\frac{n}{2\sqrt{(n-1)\pi}}
. Hence, for
n\geq 2
it holds that

\sqrt{n}\frac{C_{n}}{n-1}\leq\frac{n^{3/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}(n-1)^{3/2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(\frac{n}{n-1}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\leq\sqrt{\frac{8}{\pi}}\;,

which gives the final result.
∎

We remark that the result does not directly provide (A4) as the bound decreases exponentially with

instead of
n
, and a multiplicative sequence that depend on the choice of a constant

. This completes the proof of Lemma

, and we now discuss the adaptation of the proof of Theorem

D.4

Consider a positive convex function

\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{F}=\left\{F\in\mathcal{P}:\;\mathbb{E}\left[h(|X|)\right]\leq B\;,\;\mathbb{E}[h(|X|)^{2+\epsilon_{F}}]<+\infty\text{ for some }\epsilon_{F}>0\right\}\;,

\displaystyle D_{\pi}(F,\mu)\coloneqq\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu)\times\text{1}(F\in\mathcal{F})\;,

As this kind of family of distribution is not much studied in the literature yet, we recall for the unfamiliar reader some important properties from

Theorem 32

For any

\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu)=\max_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\mathcal{R}_{2}}\mathbb{E}_{F}\left[\log\left(1-(X-\mu)\lambda_{1}-(B-h(|X|))\lambda_{2}\right)\right]\;,

where
\mathcal{R}_{2}=\left\{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in(\mathbb{R}^{2})^{+}:\forall y\in\mathbb{R},1-(y-\mu)\lambda_{1}-(B-h(|y|))\lambda_{2}\geq 0\right\}
. Then, the following properties hold,

•

The maximum is attained at a unique
(\lambda_{1}^{\star},\lambda_{2}^{\star})\in\mathcal{R}_{2}
.

•

For
\mu\geq\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]
and
F\in\mathcal{F}
or for
\mu=\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]
and
F\notin\mathcal{F}
any distribution
G^{\star}
achieving infimum in the primal problem satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{G^{\star}}[X]=\mu
and
\mathbb{E}_{G^{\star}}[h(|x|)]=B
. Furthermore,

\frac{dF}{dG^{\star}}=\frac{1}{1-(X-\mu)\lambda_{1}^{\star}-(B-h(|\mu|))\lambda_{2}^{\star}}\;.

•

If
\int_{y\in\text{Supp}(F)}dG^{\star}<1
, then
\text{Supp}(G^{\star})=\text{Supp}(F)\cup\{y_{0}\}
, where

1-(y_{0}-\mu)\lambda_{1}^{\star}-(B-h(|y_{0}|))\lambda_{2}^{\star}=0\;.

•

(Lemma 4) The set
\mathcal{F}
is uniformly integrable and compact in the Wasserstein metric. For
\mu\leq h^{-1}(B)
and
F\in\mathcal{F}

\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu)
is increasing, the mapping
\mu\mapsto\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu)
is continuous, convex, and twice differentiable, and the mapping
F\mapsto\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu)
is continuous and convex (under the Wasserstein metric). Furthermore, for
F\in\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu)\leq\mathcal{K}_{\mathcal{F}}^{+}\coloneqq\log\left(\frac{h^{-1}(B)}{2(h^{-1}(B)-\mu)}\right)
(achieved for the Dirac distribution in
-h^{-1}(B)
),
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mathbb{E}_{F}[X])=0
and
\frac{\partial\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mathbb{E}_{F}[X])}{\partial\mu}=0
.

We now provide another important result for our proof, that motivates the second moment condition in the definition of
\mathcal{F}
.

Lemma 0

(part of Theorem 2 in
Fournier and Guillin 2015
)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables drawn from a distribution
F
satisfying
\mathbb{E}[|X|^{a}]<+\infty
for some
a>2
. Denote by
F_{n}
the empirical distribution corresponding to
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
. Then the Wasserstein distance between
F_{n}
and
F
, denoted by
W(F_{n},F)
, satisfies for any
x\geq 0

\mathbb{P}\left(W(F_{n},F)\geq x\right)=\mathcal{O}(n^{-(a-1)})\;.

For any
\delta>0
, we know that
W(F_{n},F)\leq\delta\Rightarrow|\mathbb{E}_{F_{n}}[X]-\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]|\leq\delta
. Hence, Lemma
33
can be used directly to get under our assumptions that
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(F_{k,n}\notin\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{O}(1)
for any arm
k
. Furthermore, since
h(|X|)^{2+\epsilon_{F}}\geq|X|^{2+2\eta+\eta\epsilon_{F}}
for some
\eta>0
then
\sum\mathbb{P}(\mu_{1,n}\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon)
converges for any
\epsilon>0
.

We can now prove that (A1)–(A4) hold for the family
\mathcal{F}
defined in (
18
).

(A1)

is proven by Theorem
32
.

(A2):

We already proved that
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(F_{k,n}\notin\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{O}(1)
for any arm
k
. We now consider possible deviations of the empirical
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}
by some
\delta>0
. As usual, we resort to the continuity of
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}
in its first argument: for any
\mu>\mu_{k},\delta>0
, if
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{k,n},\mu)\leq\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{k},\mu)-\delta
then there exists
\delta_{1}>0
such that
W(F_{k,n},F_{k})\geq\delta_{1}
. Using Lemma
33
, we obtain that
\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(W(F_{k,n},F_{k})\geq\delta_{1})=\mathcal{O}(1)
, which concludes the proof that (A2) holds.

(A3):

First, using the convexity of
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}
in its second argument we obtain that for any
F\in\mathcal{F}
,

\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu_{1})-\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu_{1}-\epsilon)\geq\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu_{F}+\epsilon)-\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu_{F})=\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu_{F}+\epsilon)>0\;.

Then, we simply use the compactness of the family
\mathcal{F}
(see Theorem
32
) to prove (A3) with
\delta_{\epsilon,\mu_{F}}=\min_{F\in\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon}\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu_{F}+\epsilon)>0
(which is achieved by some distribution
F
).

(A4)

is proven by Theorem 11 in
Agrawal et al. 2020
, with
\alpha_{n}\coloneqq(n+1)^{2}\alpha
for some constant
\alpha>0
.

D.5
Distribution satisfying a centered
h
-moment condition

We now prove Lemma
20
, on the families of distributions satisfying a centered
h
-moment condition. As in the uncentered case, we consider a positive convex function
h
satisfying
h(0)=0
and
\frac{h(|x|)}{|x|^{1+\eta}}\rightarrow+\infty
for some
\eta>0
. Then, a family
\mathcal{F}
of distributions satisfying a
centered

h
-moment condition is defined as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=\{F\in\mathcal{P}:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{F}\left[h(|X-\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]|)\right]<B\;,\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]\geq\mu_{0}^{-}\;,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{F}[h(|X-\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]|)^{2+\epsilon_{F}}]<+\infty\text{ for some }\epsilon_{F}>0\}\;,

for known constants
B>0,\mu_{0}^{-}\in\mathbb{R}
. Compared with the previous section (uncentered case), we add the requirement that the means are lower bounded by a known constant
\mu_{0}^{-}
, in order use arguments based on compactness. For this problem, the lower bound on the regret is defined by the following function,

\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}:(F,\mu)\mapsto\inf_{G\in\mathcal{P}:\;\mathbb{E}_{G}[X]\geq\mu\;,\;\mathbb{E}_{F}[h(|X-\mathbb{E}_{G}[X])|)]<B}\mathrm{KL}(F,G)\;.

This setting is more difficult than the uncentered case, because the optimization problem is in general not convex anymore, and working directly on
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}
is intricate. However, we can express in a form that is closer to the
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}
function for uncentered conditions,

\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu_{1})=\inf_{\mu\in[\mu_{1},+\infty)}\mathcal{K}(F,\mu)\;,\text{with}\;\mathcal{K}(F,\mu)=\inf_{G\in\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{KL}(F,G)\quad\text{s.t }\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathbb{E}_{G}[X]=\mu\\
\mathbb{E}_{G}[h(|X-\mu|)]\leq B\end{array}\;.

The proof is direct by considering the distribution
G^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}
for which
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu_{1})=\mathrm{KL}(F,G^{\star})
. The main interest of this transformation is to fix
\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]
inside the
h
-moment condition, obtaining something similar to the uncentered case. We consider the properties of this quantity for a distribution
F\in\mathcal{F}
, with
\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]\coloneqq\mu_{F}\leq\mu_{1}
. First,
\mathcal{K}
can be expressed very similarly to the
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}
function for the uncentered
h
-moment condition, with

\mathcal{K}(F_{n},\mu)=\max_{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\mathcal{R}}\mathbb{E}_{F_{n}}\left[\log(1-\lambda_{1}(X-\mu)-\lambda_{2}(B-h(|X-\mu|)\right]\;,

where
\mathcal{R}=\{(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\;:\;\forall x\in\mathbb{R}\;,1-\lambda_{1}(x-\mu)-\lambda_{2}(B-h(|x-\mu|))\geq 0\}
. Hence, for fixed
(F_{n},\mu)
all the properties that were derived for the uncentered conditions can be easily translated to the function
\mathcal{K}
. Then, the natural question it to ask whether the minimum over
\mu
is achieved in
\mu_{1}
or not. We prove that this is the case by writing
\mathcal{K}
in terms of the
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}
function for the family satisfying the
un-centered

h
-moment condition. First, we denote by
F_{-\mu}
the cdf of the translation by
-\mu
of the distribution
\nu_{F}
of cdf
F
,
F_{-\mu}:x\mapsto F(x+\mu)
(e.g. if
F
is the empirical cdf of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
then
F_{-\mu}
is the empirical cdf of
X_{1}-\mu,\dots,X_{n}-\mu
). Now, let us denote by
G^{\star}
the distribution satisfying
\mathcal{K}(F,\mu)=\mathrm{KL}(F,G^{\star})
. First, it directly holds that
\mathrm{KL}(F,G^{\star})=\mathrm{KL}(F_{-\mu},G_{-\mu}^{\star})
. Then, by definition
G^{\star}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{G^{\star}}[X]=\mu
, so
\mathbb{E}_{G_{-\mu}^{\star}}[X]=0
, and
\mathbb{E}_{G^{\star}}[h(|X-\mu|)]\leq B
so
\mathbb{E}_{G_{-\mu}^{\star}}[h(|X|)]\leq B
. Hence, if we denote by
\mathcal{F}_{\text{uc}}
the family defined by the uncentered
h
-moment condition corresponding to same function
h
and constant
B
as
\mathcal{F}
it holds that
G_{-\mu}^{\star}\in\mathcal{F}_{\text{uc}}
. Then, using from Theorem
32
that the constraints are binding in the solutions of
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}_{\text{uc}}}
we finally obtain that for any cdf
F
and threshold
\mu
,

\mathcal{K}(F,\mu)\coloneqq\mathrm{KL}(F,G^{\star})=\mathrm{KL}(F_{-\mu},G_{-\mu}^{\star})=\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}_{\text{uc}}}(F_{-\mu},0)\;,

and with this notation
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}_{\text{uc}}}(F_{-\mu_{F}},0)=0
.
With this re-writing and because
F\in\mathcal{F}
, it now becomes clear that
\mathcal{K}(F,\mu)
is increasing in
\mu
for
\mu\geq\mu_{F}
, since the objective remains the same and the distribution shifts further away from it.
Hence, we obtain that

\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu)=\mathcal{K}(F,\mu)=\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}_{\text{uc}}}(F_{-\mu},0)\;.

As a consequence, we can use several of the continuity and concentration properties from the previous section to prove that Assumption
2
holds for

D_{\pi}(F,\mu)=\mathcal{K}(F,\mu)\text{1}(\mu\geq\mu_{F},F\in\mathcal{F})\;.

(A1)

holds because of the continuity of
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}^{uc}}
with respect to its first argument (Theorem
32
) under the Wasserstein metric
W
. Indeed,
\mu^{\prime}\rightarrow\mu\Rightarrow W(F_{-\mu^{\prime}},F_{-\mu})\rightarrow 0
. It is also non-decreasing, and
D_{\pi}(F,\mu)=0
if
\mu_{F}\geq\mu
.

(A2)

holds thanks to the same arguments as for the uncentered case, using upper bounds on
\mathbb{P}(W(F_{n},F)\geq\delta)
for
\delta>0
, provided by Lemma
33
and the definition of the family.

(A4):

The concentration inequality of Theorem 11 in
Agrawal et al. 2020
can still be used, so as in the uncentered case (A4) holds with
\alpha_{n}=\mathcal{O}((n+1)^{2})
.

(A3):

Using the same techniques as for the proof of Theorem 12 in
Agrawal et al. 2020
for
\mathcal{F}^{\text{uc}}
we can prove that the intersection of
\mathcal{F}
and the set of distributions with mean bounded by
[\mu_{0}^{-},\mu_{1}-\epsilon]
is uniformly integrable and compact. Hence, with the same compactness argument as for
\mathcal{F}^{\text{uc}}
, we conclude (thanks to the assumptions that all means are larger than
\mu_{0}^{-}
) on the existence of

\delta_{\epsilon,\mu}=\min_{F\in\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]\leq\mu_{1}-\epsilon}\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu)-\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F,\mu-\epsilon)>0\;.

This completes the proof that (A1)–(A5) hold for the centered
h
-moment condition. We see that the main difficulty of this setting was to express the optimization problem giving the
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}
function for this setting into the more convenient form provided by uncentered conditions.

D.6
A variant of the sub-gaussian case as a centered
h
-moment condition

In this appendix we explain that the centered
h
-moment condition with
h(x)=e^{\frac{x^{2}}{s}}
for some
s>0
and a constant
B>0
can be viewed as an alternative characterization of subgaussian families of distributions.
It is known that there are many characterization of subgaussian tails (see Proposition 2.5.2 of
Vershynin 2018
).
Among them, the most typical definition of subgaussian distributions in the bandit literature is

\displaystyle\mathrm{SG}_{1}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\left\{F:\forall t\in\mathbb{R},\,\mathrm{E}_{F}[\mathrm{e}^{tX}]\leq\mathrm{e}^{t\mu_{F}+\frac{\sigma^{2}t^{2}}{2}}\right\}\;,

where
\mu_{F}
denotes the expectation of the distribution of cdf
F
.

Interestingly, when asymptotic optimality in the sense of (
2
) is not targeted this definition is very convenient since the Chernoff method easily provides some concentration inequalities for the empirical means, which is sufficient for the analysis of the algorithm tackling subgaussian distributions in the literature. However, as the family is defined by infinitely many constraints (due to the parameter
t
) the function
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\text{SG}_{1}(\sigma)}
has a complicated form. To target asymptotic optimality, it may be easier to work with an alternative characterization, using that sub-Gaussianity implies the following
h
-moment condition:

F\in\mathrm{SG}_{1}(\sigma)\Rightarrow\mathrm{E}_{F}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\frac{(X-\mu_{F})^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}}\right]\leq 2\;.

Hence, we can consider a subgaussian model through the following family of distribution

\displaystyle\mathrm{SG}_{2}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\left\{F:\mathrm{E}_{F}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\frac{(X-\mu_{F})^{2}}{8\sigma^{2}}}\right]\leq 2,\;\mathrm{E}_{F}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\frac{(X-\mu_{F})^{2}}{3\sigma^{2}}}\right]<+\infty\right\}\;,

that fits into the scope of our analysis if we intersect it with the set
\{F\in\mathcal{P}:\;\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]\geq\mu^{-}\}
for any constant
\mu^{-}\in\mathbb{R}
.
We can easily verify that
F\in\mathrm{SG}_{1}(\sigma)
satisfies the above second moment condition since

\mathbb{P}(|X|\geq t)\leq 2e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}}

for
X\sim F
and all
t>0
,
and thus

\mathrm{E}_{F}\left[\mathrm{e}^{\frac{(X-\mu_{F})^{2}}{3\sigma^{2}}}\right]\leq 1+\int_{1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(|X-\mathbb{E}_{F}[X]|>\sqrt{3\sigma^{2}\log(t)})\mathrm{d}t\leq 1+2\int_{1}^{+\infty}t^{-3/2}\mathrm{d}t<+\infty\;.

Then, we can easily verify that

\mathrm{SG}_{1}(\sigma)\subset\mathrm{SG}_{2}(\sigma)\subset\mathrm{SG}_{1}(\sqrt{24}\sigma)\;,

and so even though
\mathrm{SG}_{1}
and
\mathrm{SG}_{2}
do not exactly match, we are sure that all distributions in
\mathrm{SG}_{2}(\sigma)
are still subgaussian (for a potentially larger parameter).
Similar relations can also be seen in many other characterizations of subgaussian distributions (see
Vershynin 2018
).

Appendix E
NPTS for centered
h
-moment conditions

In this appendix we prove Lemma
22
, with the proof for the upper bound on the BCP in Appendix
E.1
and the proof for the lower bounds in Appendix
E.3
. In Appendix
E.2
we provide a novel lower bound on the BCP of NPTS, for bounded distributions, that showcases the general intuition of the proof of Appendix

.

E.1
Upper bound

We start by proving the upper bound presented in Lemma
22
. To do that, we use the notation
Z_{i}=h(|X_{i}-\mu|)
for
i\in[n]
and
Z_{n+1}=h(|X_{n+1}-\mu|)-\gamma
, and consider

\text{(P)}\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\left(\exists X_{n+1}:\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{i}X_{i}\geq\mu,\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{i}Z_{i}\leq B\right)\;.

The general sketch of the proof is the following: (1) we prove an upper bound for a fixed value of
X_{n+1}
using Chernoff method, (2) we use a union bound on a partition of the possible values for
X_{n+1}
, and (3) we show that a finite number of terms in this union bound are significant. For any cdf
F
, threshold
\mu
and random variable
Z_{X}
determined by
X
we use the notation

\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}(F,\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{F}\left[\log\left(1-(X-\mu)\alpha-(B-Z_{X})\beta\right)\right]\;,

where
\mathcal{R}_{2}\coloneqq(\alpha,\beta)\in\left\{(\alpha,\beta)\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2}:\forall y\in\mathbb{R},1-(y-\mu)\alpha-(B-Z_{y})\beta\geq 0\right\}
. Our first result is the following upper bound for a given value of
X_{n+1}
.

Lemma 0

(Upper bound on the BCP)
.

Consider fixed observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n},X_{n+1}
, and denote by
F_{n+1}
their empirical cdf. Then, for any
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{R}_{2}
it holds that

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{i}X_{i}\geq\mu,\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{i}Z_{X_{i}}\leq B\right)\leq\exp(-(n+1)\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}(F_{n+1},\mu))\;.

Proof.

We use the notation
\widetilde{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n+1})
and
\widetilde{Z}=(Z_{X_{1}},\dots,Z_{X_{n+1}})
for simplicity, and denote by
R=(R_{1},\dots,R_{n+1})
a vector of
n+1
i.i.d. observations drawn from an exponential distribution
\epsilon(1)
. Using usual properties of the Dirichlet distribution (see Chapter 23 of
MacKay 2003
) and Chernoff method, for any
\alpha>0
,
\beta>0
it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{w\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}(w^{T}\widetilde{X}\geq\mu,w^{T}\widetilde{Z}\leq B)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{R\sim\mathcal{E}(1)^{n}}(R^{T}(\widetilde{X}-\mu)\geq 0,R^{T}(B-\widetilde{Z})\geq 0)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{R\sim\mathcal{E}(1)^{n}}\left(e^{\alpha(R^{T}(\widetilde{X}-\mu)}\geq 1,e^{\beta(R^{T}(B-\widetilde{Z}))}\geq 1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{R\sim\mathcal{E}(1)^{n}}\left(e^{\alpha(R^{T}(\widetilde{X}-\mu)+\beta(R^{T}(B-\widetilde{Z}))}\geq 1\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{R\sim\mathcal{E}(1)^{n}}\left[e^{\alpha(R^{T}(\widetilde{X}-\mu)+\beta(R^{T}(B-\widetilde{Z}))}\right]\;.

Then, if
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{R}_{2}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{w\sim\mathcal{D}_{n}}(w^{T}\widetilde{X}\geq\mu,w^{T}\widetilde{Z}\leq B)

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}\mathbb{E}_{R_{i}\sim\mathcal{E}(1)}\left[e^{\alpha(R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)+\beta(R_{i}(B-Z_{X_{i}}))}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}\frac{1}{1-\left(\alpha(X_{i}-\mu)+\beta(B-Z_{X_{i}})\right)}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-(n+1)\mathbb{E}_{F_{n+1}}\left[\log\left(1-\left(\alpha(X-\mu)+\beta(B-Z_{X_{i}})\right)\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-(n+1)\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}(F_{n+1},\mu)\right)\;.

We use this result by partitioning the set of possible values for
X_{n+1}
. Consider a step
\eta>0
, and the sequence
(x_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
defined by
\forall j\in\mathbb{N}
,
x_{j}=\mu+j\eta
.

(P)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\exists X_{n+1}\in[x_{j},x_{j+1}]:\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{i}X_{i}\geq\mu,\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{i}Z_{X_{i}}\leq B\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}X_{i}+w_{n+1}x_{j+1}\geq\mu,\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}Z_{X_{i}}+w_{n+1}Z_{x_{j}}\leq B\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\underbrace{\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}X_{i}+w_{n+1}(x_{j}+\eta)\geq\mu,\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}Z_{X_{i}}+w_{n+1}Z_{x_{j}}\leq B\right)}_{P_{j}}\;.

At this step we can use Lemma
34
, and isolating the term depending on
x_{j}
from the others we obtain

\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}P_{j}\leq\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\exp\left(-n\Lambda_{\alpha(j),\beta(j)}(F_{n},\mu-\eta)\right)}{1-\alpha(j)(x_{j}+\eta-\mu)-\beta(j)(B+\gamma-h(x_{j}-\mu))}\;,

(40)

for any choice of sequence
(\alpha(j),\beta(j))_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
. In the rest of the proof we use that when
x_{j}
is large enough the denominator becomes small, and so only a finite number of terms are dominant in the sum.

Upper bound for large
x_{j}

We consider
(\alpha_{n}^{\star},\beta_{n}^{\star})=\text{argmax}_{\alpha,\beta}\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}(F_{n},\mu-\eta)
. We first use that
\beta_{n}^{\star}>0
, since
\beta_{n}^{\star}\in\mathcal{R}_{2}
, so when
x_{j}
is large enough the denominator scales with
\beta_{n}^{\star}h(x_{j}-\mu)
, which is larger than the other terms since
x^{1+\gamma}=o(h(x))
for some
\gamma>0
. Hence, there exists a constant
y_{n}
such that for any
x_{j}\geq y_{n}
it holds that

1-\alpha_{n}^{\star}(x_{j}+\eta-\mu)-\beta_{n}^{\star}(B+\gamma-h(x_{j}-\mu))\geq\frac{\beta_{n}^{\star}}{2}h(x_{j}-\mu)\;,

and so the part of the sum corresponding to all
x_{j}\geq y_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{j\geq 1:x_{j}\geq y_{n}}^{+\infty}P_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\beta_{n}^{\star}}\left(\sum_{j\geq 1:x_{j}\geq y_{n}}h(j\eta)^{-1}\right)e^{-n\Lambda_{\alpha_{n}^{\star},\beta_{n}^{\star}}(F_{n},\mu-\eta)}\;,

where we know that
\sum h(j\eta)^{-1}
converges since
x^{1+\eta_{0}}=o(h(x))
for some
\eta_{0}>0
.

Upper bound for
x_{j}\leq y_{n}

In this case, we define
(\alpha_{n}^{\star},\beta_{n}^{\star})
as

(\alpha_{n}^{\star},\beta_{n}^{\star})=\max_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{R}_{2}^{\gamma,\eta}}\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}(F,\mu)\;,

with
\mathcal{R}_{2}^{\gamma,\eta}=\{(\alpha,\beta)\in(\mathbb{R}^{+})^{2}:\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\;\;1-\alpha(x+\eta-\mu)-\beta(B+\gamma-h(|x-\mu|))\geq 0\}
.
We further define
x^{\star}=\text{argmin}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{1-\alpha_{n}^{\star}(x+\eta-\mu)-\beta_{n}^{\star}(B+\gamma-h(|x-\mu|))\right\}
.
x^{\star}
depends on
F_{n}
, but we omit reference to
F_{n}
for lighter notation. We use again the upper bound of
\sum P_{j}
provided by (
40
). The main task, that we tackle in the following, is to lower bound the denominator by a constant depending on the empirical distribution
F_{n}
and
\eta
.

Since the partition
(x_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
defined at the beginning of the proof is arbitrary (and only used in the proof but not by the algorithm), we can slightly modify it to avoid arbitrarily large values when
x_{j}
is close to
x^{\star}
. Let
j^{\star}\in\mathbb{N}
be the index satisfying
x^{\star}\in[x_{j^{\star}},x_{j^{\star}+1}]
. In order to introduce some distance between
x^{\star}
and
x_{j^{\star}}
,
x_{j^{\star}+1}
, we simply redefine
x_{j^{\star}}=x^{\star}-\frac{\eta}{2}
and
x_{j^{\star}+1}=x^{\star}-\frac{\eta}{2}
.
We furthermore use that
d(x_{j})\coloneqq 1-\alpha_{n}^{\star}(x^{\star}+\eta-\mu)-\beta_{n}^{\star}(B-h(x^{\star}-\mu))\geq 0
and is minimized in
x^{\star}
. By convexity of
h
, this quantity
is minimized either in
x_{j^{\star}}
or
x_{j^{\star}+1}
. Thanks to the change made to the discretization scheme, this provides

\inf_{j\in\mathbb{N}}d(x_{j})\geq\min\left\{d\left(x^{\star}+\frac{\eta}{2}\right),d\left((x^{\star}-\frac{\eta}{2}\right)\right\}>0\;.

Furthermore, the right-hand term only depends on
\eta
and on the empirical distribution
F_{n}
(through
x^{\star}
).
Hence, for
x_{j}\leq y_{n}
we can state that there exists a non-negative constant
D(F_{n},\eta)
such that

\frac{1}{1-\alpha_{n}^{\star}(x_{j}-\mu)-\beta_{n}^{\star}(B-h(x_{j}-\mu))}\leq D(F_{n},\eta)\;,

where
D(F_{n},\eta)
is continuous in
F_{n}
w.r.t. the Wasserstein metric since both
(\beta_{n}^{\star},x^{\star})
are continuous under this metric (as a consequence of Berge’s maximum theorem,
Berge 1997
). Finally, we remark that there are no more than
y_{n}/\eta
terms satisfying
x_{j}\leq y_{n}
and obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k\geq 1:x_{j}<y_{n}}P_{j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k\geq 1:x_{j}<y_{n}}D(F_{n},\eta)e^{-n\Lambda_{\alpha_{n}^{\star},\beta_{n}^{\star}}(F_{n},\mu)}

\displaystyle\leq y_{n}\frac{D(F_{n},\eta)}{\eta}e^{-n\Lambda_{\alpha_{n}^{\star},\beta_{n}^{\star}}(F_{n},\mu)}.

Summary

Combining our two results, we obtain that

\text{(P)}\leq C(\alpha_{n}^{\star},\beta_{n}^{\star},\eta)\exp\left(-n\Lambda_{\alpha_{n}^{\star},\beta_{n}^{\star}}(F_{n},\mu)\right)\;,

(41)

where

C(\alpha_{n}^{\star},\beta_{n}^{\star},\eta)=y_{n}\frac{D(F_{n},\eta)}{\eta}+\frac{2}{\beta_{n}^{\star}}\sum_{k=\left\lceil\frac{y_{n}-\mu}{\eta}\right\rceil}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{h(\eta k)}\;.

As stated above the constant
C(\alpha_{n}^{\star},\beta_{n}^{\star},\eta)
depend on
F_{n}
through the optimizers
(\alpha_{n}^{\star},\beta_{n}^{\star})
(as they also determine
y_{n}
and
x^{\star}
), that depends on
\gamma
and
\eta
since they belong to
\mathcal{R}_{2}^{\gamma,\eta}
. Finally, with the same arguments as for the continuity of
D
, it is clear that
C
is continuous in all parameters, which concludes the proof of the result.
∎

E.2
Lower bound: Bounded case

In this section we present a novel lower bound for the BCP of NPTS in the bounded case, that can also serve as a warm-up to present the idea for the slightly more complicated
h
-moment condition setting. We assume that all observations are bounded by some constant
B>0
, and that the algorithm is using the additional support
X_{n+1}=B+\gamma
for some
\gamma>0
.

Lemma 0

(Novel lower bound for the BCP of NPTS)
.

If NPTS is implemented with exploration bonus
B+\gamma
for some
\gamma>0
, then for any
\delta>0
there exists a constant
n_{\delta,\gamma}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\text{\emph{[BCP]}}\geq\frac{1-e^{-\frac{\delta}{4}}}{2e^{1+\delta}}\times e^{-n\left(\Lambda_{\alpha_{n}^{\star}}(F_{n},\mu^{\star})+\delta\right)}\;\quad\mbox{if}\quad n\geq n_{\delta,\gamma}\;,

and
\alpha_{n}^{\star}=\text{argmax}_{\alpha\in\left[0,\frac{1}{B+\gamma-\mu}\right]}\Lambda_{\alpha_{n}^{\star}}(F_{n},\mu^{\star})
.

Proof.

Recall that

\text{[BCP]}\geq\mathbb{P}_{w\sim\mathcal{D}_{n+1}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{i}X_{i}\geq\mu^{\star}\right)\;,

with
X_{n+1}=B+\gamma
. For any cdf
F_{n+1}:x\mapsto\frac{1}{n+1}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\text{1}(X_{i}\leq x)
we define

\Lambda_{\alpha}(F_{n},\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{F_{n+1}}\left[\log\left(1-\alpha(X-\mu)\right)\right]\;.

We recall that any vector
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n+1})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n+1}
can be re-written as a normalized sum of
\epsilon(1)
exponential random variables. Hence, we can write the BCP as follows:

\text{[BCP]}=\mathbb{P}_{R_{1}\sim\epsilon(1),\dots,R_{n+1}\sim\epsilon(1)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu^{\star})\geq 0\right)\;,

where
(R_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are i.i.d. and follow an exponential distribution
\mathcal{E}(1)
. To lower bound this quantity, we will perform a change of measure for each variable
R_{i}
. In particular, we propose to consider a variable that has expectation
G_{i}
, for some
(G_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n+1}
. We can do that by choosing exponential random variables
\epsilon\left(\frac{1}{G_{i}}\right)
: this technique is known as exponential tilting. If
\widetilde{f}_{i}
denotes the density of this random variable and
f
denotes the density of the distribution
\epsilon(1)
, it holds that

\forall x\geq 0:\;\frac{f(x)}{\widetilde{f}_{i}(x)}=G_{i}e^{-\left(1-\frac{1}{G_{i}}\right)x}

We further denote by and
\mathbb{P}
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
their respective product. Using
R^{T}U=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)
, we now express the BCP using the distributions of densities
\widetilde{f}_{i}
,

\displaystyle\text{[BCP]}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\text{1}\left(R^{T}U\geq 0\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\text{1}\left(R^{T}U\geq 0\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}\frac{f(R_{i})}{\widetilde{f}_{i}(R_{i})}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}G_{i}\times\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\text{1}\left(R^{T}U\geq 0\right)e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\left(1-\frac{1}{G_{i}}\right)R_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\log\left(\frac{1}{G_{i}}\right)}\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\text{1}\left(R^{T}U\geq 0\right)e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\left(1-\frac{1}{G_{i}}\right)R_{i}}\right]\;.

Then, our proof will consists in: first, deriving the term
\max_{\alpha}\Lambda_{\alpha}(F_{n},\mu)+\delta
from the exponential term, and then showing that the expectation can be lower bounded by a constant. To do that, for any arbitrary constant
\eta_{1}>0
and
for
i\leq n+1
we consider

G_{i}=\frac{1}{1-\alpha(X_{i}-\mu-\eta_{1})}\quad\text{ for some }\alpha>0\;,

so that
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left(\frac{1}{G_{i}}\right)=\Lambda_{\alpha}(F_{n},\mu+\eta_{1})
. Now, let us choose

\alpha=\alpha^{\star}\coloneqq\text{argmax}\,\Lambda_{\alpha}(F_{n+1},\mu+\eta_{1})\;,

and then
\Lambda_{\alpha^{\star}}(F_{n+1},\mu+\eta_{1})=\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}}(F_{n+1},\mu+\eta_{1})
, where
\mathcal{F}
is the family of distributions bounded by
B+\gamma
, and the weights
(G_{i})_{i\in[n+1]}
associated with support
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
define a probability distribution of mean equal to
\mu+\eta_{1}
. Furthermore, it holds that

\displaystyle\alpha\leq\alpha_{M}\coloneqq\frac{1}{B+\gamma-\mu-\eta_{1}},

(42)

but also that

\forall i\in[n]\;,\;X_{i}\leq B\Rightarrow\frac{1}{1+\alpha_{M}(\mu+\eta_{1})}\leq G_{i}\leq\frac{B+\gamma-\mu-\eta_{1}}{\gamma}\;.

This property is crucial for the rest of our proof, and motivates the choice of
X_{n+1}
as
B+\gamma
instead of
B
: it ensures that all the exponential variables
R_{1},\dots,R_{n}
have a reasonable expectation, that does not depend on
n
: only
G_{n+1}
can potentially be large (in the case when the empirical distribution is very bad, e.g.
X_{1}=X_{2}=\dots=X_{n}=0
).

We now write that

\text{[BCP]}\geq e^{-(n+1)\Lambda_{\alpha}(F_{n+1}\mu+\eta_{1})}\times\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\text{1}\left(R^{T}U\geq 0\right)e^{-\alpha R^{T}\widetilde{U}}\right]\;,

with
\widetilde{U}=(X_{1}-\mu-\eta_{1},\dots,X_{n+1}-\mu-\eta_{1})
, and after remarking that for all
i
,

1-\frac{1}{G_{i}}=\alpha(X_{i}-\mu-\eta_{1}).

We now use that
\alpha\leq\alpha_{M}\coloneqq\frac{1}{B+\gamma-\mu-\eta_{1}}
to handle the exponential term inside the expectation. Then, for any
\delta>0
we write that

\text{[BCP]}\geq e^{-(n+1)\left(\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}(F_{n+1},\mu+\eta_{1})+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)}\times P_{\alpha}\;,

(43)

where we defined

P_{\alpha}=\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(R^{T}U\geq 0,R^{T}\widetilde{U}\leq(n+1)\frac{\delta}{2\alpha_{M}}\right)\;.

(44)

In some cases, it may happen that
G_{n+1}
is large (at most
n+1
), and in that case the exponential variable as variance
n^{2}
. Hence, we would like to control very precisely the deviations of
R_{n+1}
. For this reason, for some constant
c>0
we consider the event

E_{n+1}\coloneqq\left\{R_{n+1}\in\left[G_{n+1},\;(1+c)G_{n+1}\right]\right\}\;.

Using the cdf of exponential variables, this event has exactly a probability of

\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}(R_{n+1}\in\left[G_{n+1},\;(1+c)G_{n+1}\right])=e^{-\frac{G_{n+1}}{G_{n+1}}}-e^{-\frac{G_{n+1}(1+c)}{G_{n+1}}}=e^{-1}(1-e^{-c})\;.

We now re-write
P_{\alpha}
as

\displaystyle P_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(R^{T}U\geq 0,R^{T}\widetilde{U}\leq(n+1)\frac{\delta}{2\alpha_{M}},E_{n+1}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}(E_{n+1})\mathbb{P}\left(R^{T}U\geq 0,R^{T}\widetilde{U}\leq(n+1)\frac{\delta}{2\alpha_{M}}|E_{n+1}\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-1}(1-e^{-c})\left(1-\underbrace{\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}(R^{T}U\leq 0,R^{T}\widetilde{U}\geq n\frac{\delta}{2\alpha_{M}}|E_{n+1})}_{p_{1}}\right)\;.

The rest of the proof consists in showing that for
n
large enough the terms
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are small. We consider the first event in
p_{1}
. Using that
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}[R^{T}U]=(n+1)\eta

\displaystyle\left\{R^{T}U\leq 0\right\}

\displaystyle\subset\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)+G_{n+1}(X_{n+1}-\mu)\leq 0\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)\right]\leq-(n+1)\eta\right\}\;,

and so under
E_{n+1}
the first event implies a deviation of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)
from its mean. Next, considering the second event it holds that

\displaystyle\left\{R^{T}\widetilde{U}\geq(n+1)\frac{\delta}{2\alpha_{M}}\right\}\subset\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)+(1+c)G_{n+1}(X_{n+1}-\mu)\geq(n+1)\frac{\delta}{2\alpha_{M}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)\right]\leq(n+1)\frac{\delta}{2\alpha_{M}}-cG_{n+1}(B+\gamma-\mu)\right\}\;.

Then, we use that
G_{n+1}\leq n+1
to tune
c
in order to obtain something similar to the first event.

For the above goal, let us consider the case of

\displaystyle c=\frac{\delta}{4\alpha_{M}(B+\gamma-\mu)}.

(45)

In this case we obtain that

\displaystyle\left\{R^{T}\widetilde{U}\geq(n+1)\frac{\delta}{2\alpha_{M}}\right\}\subset\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)\right]\leq(n+1)\frac{\delta}{4\alpha_{M}}\right\}\;.

Then, using the notation
\eta^{\prime}=\min\left\{\eta,\frac{\delta}{4\alpha_{M}}\right\}
we obtain with the Tchebychev inequality that

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\alpha}\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)\right|\geq(n+1)\eta^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}}\times\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}^{2}(X_{i}-\mu)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}\eta^{\prime 2}}(B-\mu)^{2}\max_{i=1,\dots,n}G_{i}\times\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(B-\mu)^{2}}{(n+1)\eta^{\prime 2}}\frac{B+\gamma-\mu-\eta_{1}}{\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n+1}\times\frac{(B-\mu)^{2}}{\eta^{\prime 2}}\left(1+\frac{B-\mu}{\gamma}\right)\;.

(46)

As this bound is independent of
F_{n}
, we obtain that for any
\eta>0
, there exists
n_{\eta}\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
p_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}
for
n\geq n_{\eta}
, under the case of (
45
).

Here note that
c
in (
45
) goes to
\frac{\delta}{4}
as
\eta\to 0

since
\alpha_{M}(B+\gamma-\mu)\to 1
holds from (
42
).
For this reason,
we now take

c=\delta/4
instead of (
45
), under which a result similar to (
46
) holds and there still exists
n_{\eta}\in\mathbb{N}
satisfying
p_{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}
for
n\geq n_{\eta}
if we choose
\eta
small enough.
For such
n\geq n_{\eta}
,
it holds that
P_{\alpha}\geq\frac{e^{-1}(1-e^{-c})}{2}=\frac{e^{-1}(1-e^{-\frac{\delta}{4}})}{2}
(plugging our result in Equation (
44
)) and so using (
43
) we obtain that

\text{[BCP]}\geq\frac{e^{-1}(1-e^{-\frac{\delta}{4}})}{2}e^{-(n+1)\left(\Lambda_{\alpha}(F_{n+1},\mu+\eta)+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)}\;.

We now tune
\eta
as

\eta=\max\left\{\eta>0:\;\forall F_{n}\in\mathcal{F}\;,\;\Lambda_{\alpha^{\star}}(F_{n+1},\mu+\eta)\leq\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}^{\gamma}}(F_{n+1},\mu)+\frac{\delta}{2}\right\}\;.

Due to the compactness of
\mathcal{F}
, this quantity exists, is strictly positive, and depends on
\delta
, and goes to
0
as
\delta
goes to
0
. Finally, we remark that since
X_{n+1}
has a negative contribution to
\mathcal{K}_{\inf}(F_{n+1},\mu)
(expressed as a sum of
n+1
logarithmic terms, the
(n+1)
-th one being the minimum),
then it holds that

(n+1)\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}^{\gamma}}(F_{n+1},\mu)\leq n\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}^{\gamma}}(F_{n},\mu)\;

and consequently, we conclude that for any
\delta>0
, there exists a constant
n_{\delta}
such that for all
n\geq n_{\delta}
it holds that

\text{[BCP]}\geq\frac{1-e^{-\frac{\delta}{4}}}{2e^{1+\delta}}e^{-n\left(\mathcal{K}_{\inf}^{\mathcal{F}^{\gamma}}(F_{n},\mu)+\delta\right)}\;,

which concludes our proof for this result.
∎

Remark 0
.

Chernoff method would lead to a tighter upper bound for
p_{1}
, however we choose Tchebychev inequality for simplicity since knowing the right value of
n_{\delta}
is not necessary for our algorithm.

E.3
Lower bound:
h
-moment condition

In this section we prove the lower bounds on the BCP in Lemma
22
. The proof follows the exact same steps as the proof of Lemma
35
presented in Section
E.2
, with some additional technicality due to the slightly more complicated definition of the family
\mathcal{F}
. We still write all the details for completeness.
We first recall that for observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
the BCP is expressed as

[BCP]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{w\sim\mathcal{D}_{n+1}}\left(\exists X_{n+1}\geq\mu^{\star}:\;\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{i}X_{i}\geq\mu^{\star},\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{i}Z_{X_{i}}\leq B\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{w\sim\mathcal{D}_{n+1}}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{i}X_{i}\geq\mu^{\star},\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{i}Z_{X_{i}}\leq B\right)\quad\mbox{for arbitrary $X_{n+1}\geq\mu^{\star}$}\;,

with
Z_{X_{i}}=h(|X_{i}-\mu|)
for
i\in[n]
and
Z_{X_{n+1}}=h(X_{n+1}-\mu)-\gamma
.
To provide a lower bound on this quantity we will choose
X_{n+1}
so that the bound is expressed in terms of the quantity

\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}(F,\mu,B)=\mathbb{E}_{F}\left[\log\left(1-\alpha(X-\mu)-\beta(B-Z_{X})\right)\right]\;,

given some parameters
\alpha,\beta,\mu,B
and a cdf
F
.

We recall that any vector
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{n+1})\sim\mathcal{D}_{n+1}
can be re-written as a normalized sum of
\epsilon(1)
exponential random variables.
Hence, we can write the BCP as follows:

\text{[BCP]}\geq\mathbb{P}_{R_{1}\sim\epsilon(1),\dots,R_{n+1}\sim\epsilon(1)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)\geq 0,\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}R_{i}(B-Z_{X_{i}})\geq 0\right)\;,

where
(R_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are i.i.d. and follow an exponential distribution
\mathcal{E}(1)
. We perform an exponential tilting of the distributions, considering the exponential variables with expectation
\mathbb{E}[R_{i}]=G_{i}
, for some sequence
(G_{i})_{i=1,\dots,n+1}
, so
R_{i}\sim\epsilon\left(\frac{1}{G_{i}}\right)
. If
\widetilde{f}_{i}
denotes the density of this random variable and
f
denotes the density of the distribution
\epsilon(1)
, it holds that

\forall x\geq 0:\;\frac{f_{i}(x)}{\widetilde{f}_{i}(x)}=G_{i}e^{-\left(1-\frac{1}{G_{i}}\right)x}.

We further denote by and
\mathbb{P}
and
\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}
their respective product. Using the notation
R^{T}U=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)
and
R^{T}V=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}R_{i}(B-Z_{X_{i}})
, we now re-write the BCP using the distributions of densities
\widetilde{f}_{i}
,

\displaystyle\text{[BCP]}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\text{1}\left(R^{T}U\geq 0,R^{T}V\geq 0\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\text{1}\left(R^{T}U\geq 0,R^{T}V\geq 0\right)\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}\frac{f_{i}(R_{i})}{\widetilde{f}_{i}(R_{i})}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{n+1}G_{i}\times\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\text{1}\left(R^{T}U\geq 0,R^{T}V\geq 0\right)e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\left(1-\frac{1}{G_{i}}\right)R_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\log\left(\frac{1}{G_{i}}\right)}\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\text{1}\left(R^{T}U\geq 0,R^{T}V\geq 0\right)e^{-\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\left(1-\frac{1}{G_{i}}\right)R_{i}}\right]\;.

We want to make the term
\max_{\alpha,\beta}\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}(F_{n},\mu,B)+\delta
come from the exponential term in front of the expectation, and to show that the expectation can be lower bounded by a constant. To do that, we consider two arbitrary constants
\eta_{1}>0,\eta_{2}>0
, and for
i\leq n+1
we define

G_{i}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{1-\alpha(X_{i}-\mu-\eta_{1})-\beta(B-\eta_{2}-Z_{X_{i}})},&i\in[n],\\
\frac{1}{1-\alpha(X_{i}-\mu-\eta_{1})-\beta(B+\gamma-\eta_{2}-Z_{X_{i}})},&i=n+1,\end{cases}

for some constants
(\alpha,\beta)
.
We still center the
h
-moment in
\mu
, as we want the objective inside the expectation to be strictly easier to reach with our change of distribution. However, we keep the notation
\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}
with a slight abuse for simplicity.

We then choose
X_{n+1}
in the BCP as

X_{n+1}=\text{argmin}_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\{1-\alpha_{n}^{\star}(x-\mu-\eta_{1})-\beta_{n}^{\star}(B-\eta_{2}-h(|x-\mu|))\}\;.

(47)

Then, by definition of
(\alpha_{n}^{\star},\beta_{n}^{\star})
we obtain that

\displaystyle 1-\alpha_{n}^{\star}(X_{i}-\mu-\eta_{1})

\displaystyle-\beta_{n}^{\star}(B-\eta_{2}-h(|X_{i}-\mu|))

\displaystyle\geq 1-\alpha_{n}^{\star}(X_{n+1}-\mu-\eta_{1})-\beta_{n}^{\star}(B-\eta_{2}-h(|X_{n+1}-\mu|))\quad\text{(by \eqref{eq::X_n+1})}

\displaystyle\geq\beta_{n}^{\star}\gamma+\underbrace{1-\alpha_{n}^{\star}(X_{n+1}-\mu-\eta_{1})-\beta_{n}^{\star}(B+\gamma-\eta_{2}-Z_{n+1})}_{\geq 0}

\displaystyle\geq\overline{\beta}\gamma\coloneqq\left(\min\limits_{F_{n}\in\mathcal{F}:\mathbb{E}_{F_{n}}[X]\leq\mu}\beta_{n}^{\star}\right)\gamma\;,

where
\overline{\beta}
is well-defined and satisfies
\overline{\beta}>0
by compactness of
\mathcal{F}
.
With the same argument, we can also deduce the existence of a (universal) upper bound
X^{+}
of the potential additional support
X_{n+1}
(exploiting the continuity of mapping
F\mapsto\alpha_{F}^{\star}
,
F\mapsto\beta_{F}^{\star}
in
F
, and that the additional support satisfies
\alpha_{F}^{\star}=h^{\prime}(X-\mu)\beta_{F}^{\star}
). Now, given that
X_{n+1}
cannot be arbitrarily large,
W(F_{n},F_{n+1})=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
, where
W
denotes the Wasserstein metric. Furthermore, using the continuity of the mapping
F\mapsto 1-\alpha_{F}^{\star}(x-\mu-\eta_{1})-\beta_{F}^{\star}(B-\eta_{2}-h(|x-\mu|))
for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
, we finally conclude that there exists a rank
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for any
n\geq n_{0}
it holds that

\forall i\in[n]\;,\;G_{i}\leq\frac{2}{\gamma\overline{\beta}}\;.

This property ensures that all the variables
G_{1},\dots,G_{n}
have a reasonable expectation, that does not depend on
n
but on
\gamma
: only
G_{n+1}
can potentially be large (at most
n+1
).

We now get back to lower bounding the BCP, writing that

\text{[BCP]}\geq e^{-n\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}(F_{n},\mu+\eta_{1},B-\eta_{2})}\times\mathbb{E}_{\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}}\left[\text{1}\left(R^{T}U\geq 0,R^{T}V\geq 0\right)e^{-\alpha R^{T}\widetilde{U}-\beta R^{T}\widetilde{V}}\right]\;,

with
\widetilde{U}=(X_{1}-\mu-\eta_{1},\dots,X_{n+1}-\mu-\eta_{1})
and
\widetilde{V}=(B-\eta_{2}-Z_{X_{1}},\dots,B+\gamma-\eta_{2}-Z_{X_{n+1}}
, and after remarking that for all
i\leq n
(and with
B+\gamma

i=n+1
),

1-\frac{1}{G_{i}}=\alpha(X_{i}-\mu-\eta_{1})+\beta(B-\eta_{2}-Z_{X_{i}}).

Then, we handle the exponential term inside the expectation by first using from
Agrawal et al. 2020
(their proof can be simply adapted to get the centered condition instead of the uncentered one) that
\alpha
and
\beta
are both upper bounded respectively by some constants
\alpha_{M}
and
\beta_{M}
depending only on
\mu+\eta_{1}
and
B-\eta_{2}
. Then, for any
\delta>0
we write that

\text{[BCP]}\geq e^{-n\left(\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}(F_{n},\mu+\eta_{1},B-\eta_{2})+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)}\times P_{\alpha,\beta}\;,

where we defined

P_{\alpha,\beta}=\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(R^{T}U\geq 0,R^{T}V\geq 0,R^{T}\widetilde{U}\leq n\frac{\delta}{4\alpha_{M}},R^{T}\widetilde{V}\leq n\frac{\delta}{4\beta_{M}}\right)\;.

As in the bounded case, we control separately the term
R_{n+1}
since it is the only term with an expectation that can depend on
n
. Considering a constant
c>0
, we define the event

E_{n+1}\coloneqq\left\{R_{n+1}\in\left[G_{n+1},\;(1+c)G_{n+1}\right]\right\}\;.

Using the cdf of exponential variables, this event has exactly a probability of

\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\beta}(R_{n+1}\in\left[G_{n+1},\;(1+c)G_{n+1}\right])=e^{-\frac{G_{n+1}}{G_{n+1}}}-e^{-\frac{G_{n+1}(1+c)}{G_{n+1}}}=e^{-1}(1-e^{-c})\;,

so we obtain that

\displaystyle P_{\alpha,\beta}

\displaystyle\geq\widetilde{\mathbb{P}}\left(R^{T}U\geq 0,R^{T}V\geq 0R^{T}\widetilde{U}\leq(n+1)\frac{\delta}{4\alpha_{M}},R^{T}\widetilde{V}\geq\frac{\delta}{4\beta_{M}},E_{n+1}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\beta}(E_{n+1})\mathbb{P}\left(R^{T}U\geq 0,R^{T}V\geq 0,R^{T}\widetilde{U}\leq(n+1)\frac{\delta}{4\alpha_{M}},R^{T}\widetilde{V}\leq(n+1)\frac{\delta}{4\alpha_{M}}|E_{n+1}\right)

\displaystyle=p_{c}\left(1-\underbrace{\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\beta}(R^{T}U\leq 0,R^{T}\widetilde{U}\geq n\frac{\delta}{4\alpha_{M}}|E_{n+1})}_{p_{1}}-\underbrace{\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\beta}(R^{T}V\leq 0,R^{T}\widetilde{V}\geq n\frac{\delta}{4\beta_{M}}|E_{n+1})}_{p_{2}}\right)\;,

with
p_{c}=e^{-1}(1-e^{-c})
. The rest of the proof consists in showing that for
n
large enough the terms
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
are small. We now consider that
(\alpha,\beta)=\text{argmax}\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}(F_{n+1},\mu+\eta_{1},B-\eta_{2})
. We consider the first event in
p_{1}
. Using that
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha,\beta}[R^{T}U]=(n+1)\eta

\displaystyle\left\{R^{T}U\leq 0\right\}

\displaystyle\subset\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)+G_{n+1}(X_{n+1}-\mu)\leq 0\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)\right]\leq-(n+1)\eta_{1}\right\}\;,

and so under
E_{n+1}
the first event implies a deviation of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)
from its mean. Then, to handle the second event we use that

\displaystyle\left\{R^{T}\widetilde{U}\geq(n+1)\frac{\delta}{4\alpha_{M}}\right\}\subset\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)+(1+c)G_{n+1}(X_{n+1}-\mu)\geq(n+1)\frac{\delta}{4\alpha_{M}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)\right]\leq(n+1)\frac{\delta}{4\alpha_{M}}-cG_{n+1}(X^{+}-\mu)\right\}\;.

Then, we use that
G_{n+1}\leq n+1
to obtain a first condition on
c
. We arbitrarily consider
c\leq\frac{\delta}{8\alpha_{M}(X^{+}-\mu)}
, to obtain that

\displaystyle\left\{R^{T}\widetilde{U}\geq(n+1)\frac{\delta}{4\alpha_{M}}\right\}\subset\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)\right]\leq(n+1)\frac{\delta}{8\alpha_{M}}\right\}\;.

Hence, using the notation
\eta_{1}^{\prime}=\min\left\{\eta,\frac{\delta}{4\alpha_{M}}\right\}
we get

p_{1}\leq\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\beta}\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)\right|\geq(n+1)\eta_{1}^{\prime}\right)\;.

(48)

We then perform the exact same steps with
p_{2}
. This time, it will depend on the sign of
B-h(X_{n+1}-\mu)+\gamma
. If
B-h(X_{n+1}-\mu)+\gamma>0
, we obtain similarly that

\displaystyle\left\{R^{T}V\leq 0\right\}\subset\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(B-h(|X_{i}-\mu|)-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)\right]\leq-(n+1)\eta_{2}\right\}\;\text{, and}

\displaystyle\left\{R^{T}\widetilde{V}\leq\frac{\delta}{4\beta_{M}}\right\}\subset\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(B-Z_{X_{i}})-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(B-Z_{X_{i}})\right]\geq(n+1)\left(\frac{\delta}{4\beta_{M}}-c(B+\gamma)\right)\right\}\;.

So, this time we can require for instance
c\leq\frac{\delta}{8\beta_{M}(B+\gamma)}
. On the other hand, if
B-h(X_{n+1}-\mu)+\gamma\leq 0
this time we have

\displaystyle\left\{R^{T}V\leq 0\right\}\subset\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(B-h(|X_{i}-\mu|)-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(X_{i}-\mu)\right]\leq-(n+1)\eta_{2,c}\right\}\;,

\displaystyle\left\{R^{T}\widetilde{V}\leq\frac{\delta}{4\beta_{M}}\right\}

\displaystyle\subset\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(B-Z_{X_{i}})-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(B-h(|X_{i}-\mu|))\right]\geq(n+1)\frac{\delta}{4\beta_{M}}\right\}\;,

with
\eta_{2,c}=\eta_{2}-c(h(X^{+}-\mu)-B-\gamma)
, and so this time we can ask that
c\leq\frac{\eta_{2}}{2(h(X^{+}-\mu)-B-\gamma)}
. Hence, if all requirements on
c
are satisfied we can define
\eta_{2}^{\prime}=\min\left\{\frac{\eta_{2}}{2(h(X^{+}-\mu)-B-\gamma)},\frac{\delta}{8\beta_{M}(B+\gamma)},\eta_{2}\right\}
, and we proved that

p_{2}\leq\mathbb{P}_{\alpha,\beta}\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}R_{i}(B-h(X_{i}-\mu))-\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}(B-h(|X_{i}-\mu|)\right|\geq(n+1)\eta_{2}^{\prime}\right)\;.

(49)

Then, just as in the proof for the bounded case we can use the Tchebychev inequality to further upper bound
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
from (
48
) and (
49
), and obtain that

\displaystyle p_{1}\leq\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}\eta_{1}^{\prime 2}}\times\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}^{2}(X_{i}-\mu)^{2}\;,

and

\displaystyle p_{2}\leq\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}\eta_{2}^{\prime 2}}\times\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}^{2}(B-h(|X_{i}-\mu|))^{2}\;.

This time the quadratic terms depending on
X_{i}
cannot be discarded as easily as in the bounded case. However, we can use a similar trick to the one we used to prove the upper bound in Lemma
22
, remarking that if
X_{i}
is too large then
G_{i}
is necessarily very small. Indeed, using that
\beta\geq\beta_{m}
and that when
X_{i}
is large then
G_{i}^{2}\sim\frac{1}{\beta(B-h(|X_{i}-\mu|))^{2}}
, we obtain that there exists a constant
C>0
such that for
|X_{i}-\mu|\geq C
it holds that

G_{i}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\beta_{m}(B-h(|X_{i}-\mu|))^{2}}\;.

Using this result and the notation
A_{C}=\max_{|x-\mu|\geq C}\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{\beta_{m}(B-h(|x-\mu|))^{2}}
, we first obtain that

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}\eta_{1}^{\prime 2}}\left(C^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}^{2}+2nA_{C}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}\eta_{1}^{\prime 2}}\left(C^{2}\max_{i=1,\dots,n}G_{i}\times\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}+2nA_{C}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}\eta_{1}^{\prime 2}}\left(C^{2}(n+1)\max_{i=1,\dots,n}G_{i}+2nA_{C}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(n+1)\eta_{1}^{\prime 2}}\left(\frac{2C^{2}}{\gamma\beta_{m}}+2A_{C}\right)=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})\;.

Similarly, we obtain that

\displaystyle p_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}\eta_{2}^{\prime 2}}\left((B-h(C))^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}^{2}+\frac{2n}{\beta_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{(n+1)\eta_{2}^{\prime 2}}\left(\frac{2(B-h(C))^{2}}{\gamma\beta_{m}}+\frac{2}{\beta_{m}}\right)=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})\;.

As this bound is independent of
F_{n}
, we obtain that for any
\eta_{1}>0,\eta_{2}>0
, there exists a rank
n_{\eta_{1},\eta_{2}}
such that
p_{1}\leq\frac{1}{4}
and
p_{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}
for
n\geq n_{\eta}
. In that case, it holds that

\text{[BCP]}\geq\frac{e^{-1}(1-e^{-c})}{2}e^{-(n+1)\left(\Lambda_{\alpha_{n}^{\star},\beta_{n}^{\star}}(F_{n+1},\mu+\eta_{1},B-\eta_{2})+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)}\;,

where this bound holds if
c\leq\min\left\{\frac{\delta}{8\alpha_{M}(X^{+}-\mu)},\frac{\delta}{8\beta_{M}(B+\gamma)},\frac{\eta_{2}}{2(h(X^{+}-\mu)-B-\gamma)}\right\}
, so
c
is of order
\mathcal{O}(\min\{\delta,\eta_{2}\})
. We now tune
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
. For simplicity we consider

\eta_{1}=\eta_{2}=\max\left\{\eta>0:\;\forall F_{n}\in\mathcal{F}\;,\;\max_{\alpha,\beta}\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}(F_{n},\mu+\eta,B-\eta)\leq\max_{\alpha,\beta}\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}(F_{n},\mu,B)+\frac{\delta}{2}\right\}\;.

Thanks to the compactness of
\mathcal{F}
, this quantity exists, is strictly positive, and only depends on
\delta
. This allows us to conclude that for any
\gamma>0
and
\delta>0
, there exists two constants
n_{\mathcal{F}}
and
c_{\delta}
such that for all
n\geq n_{\mathcal{F}}
it holds that

\text{[BCP]}\geq c_{\delta}e^{-n\max_{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{R}_{2}^{\gamma}}\left(\Lambda_{\alpha,\beta}(F_{n},\mu,B)+\delta\right)}\;,

which concludes the part of the proof corresponding to
n\geq n_{\mathcal{F}}
.

We finish the proof by showing that the BCP can also be lower bounded for
n\leq n_{\mathcal{F}}
. However, this bound does not need to be tight since it will not impact the validity of the conditions that we want to prove. We provide a simple lower bound by fixing the value of the additional support to
X_{n+1}=\frac{\mu+h^{-1}(B)}{2}
. We show that for an appropriate constant
c
depending only on
B
and
\mu
, if the empirical distribution belongs to
\mathcal{F}
then it holds for any
n\geq 1
that

\text{[BCP]}\geq\mathbb{P}\left(w_{n+1}\geq 1-\frac{1}{cn}\right)\geq e^{-n\log(nc)}\;.

Indeed, if
F_{n}\in\mathcal{F}
then for all
i
,
\mu-h^{-1}(nB)\leq X_{i}\leq\mu+h^{-1}(nB)
under the event
\{w_{n+1}\geq 1-\frac{1}{cn}\}
we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{i}X_{i}

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-\frac{1}{cn}\right)\left(\frac{\mu+h^{-1}(B)}{2}\right)-\frac{h^{-1}(nB)-\mu}{cn}

\displaystyle=\mu+\frac{h^{-1}(B)-\mu}{2}-\frac{1}{cn}\left(\frac{\mu+h^{-1}(B)}{2}+h^{-1}(nB)-\mu\right)\;,

and

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}

\displaystyle w_{i}h(|X_{i}-\mu|)\leq Z_{X_{n+1}}+\frac{1}{cn}\sum_{i=1}^{n}h(|X_{i}-\mu|)\leq h\left(\frac{h^{-1}(B)-\mu}{2}\right)+\frac{B}{c}

Then, the tuning on
c
is made to ensure that the bounds are satisfying for all values of
n
. We can verify that this is true if

c\geq C_{B,\mu}\coloneqq\max\left\{\frac{3h^{-1}(B)-\mu}{h^{-1}(B)-\mu},\frac{B}{B-h\left(\frac{h^{-1}(B)-\mu}{2}\right)}\right\}\;.

Remark 0

(Adaptation for the un-centered condition)
.

The same proof scheme leads to a similar result for the un-centered condition, simply with a different constant
C_{B,\mu}
. Indeed, we can fix the same value for
X_{n+1}
, and write the same results using instead that for all
i
,
-h^{-1}(nB)\leq X_{i}\leq h^{-1}(nB)
. The constant
C_{B,\mu}
would become

C_{B,\mu}\coloneqq\max\left\{\frac{3h^{-1}(B)+\mu}{h^{-1}(B)-\mu},\frac{B}{B-h\left(\frac{\mu+h^{-1}(B)}{2}\right)}\right\}

E.4
Validity of the simplified check

In this section we prove that for any parameter
\gamma>0
, observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n+1}
and weights
w_{1},\dots,w_{n+1}
the following condition

\exists x\geq\mu^{\star}:\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}X_{i}+w_{n+1}x\geq\mu^{\star}\;,\;\text{and }\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}h(|X_{i}-\mu^{\star}|)+w_{n+1}(h(|x-\mu^{\star}|)-\gamma)\leq B\;,

is equivalent to

h\left(\left(\frac{1}{w_{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\mu^{\star}-X_{i})\right)^{+}\right)\leq B+\gamma+\frac{1}{w_{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(B-h(|X_{i}-\mu^{\star}|))\;,

where
(x)^{+}=\max\{x,0\}
.

Assume that
x
is a solution to the first (mean) constraint. Then, using that
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}w_{i}=1
and that
\{w_{n+1}=0\}
has probability zero it must hold that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(X_{i}-\mu^{\star})+w_{n+1}(x-\mu^{\star})\geq 0\Rightarrow x\geq\mu^{\star}+\frac{1}{w_{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\mu^{\star}-X_{i})\;.

Then, doing the same with the second constraint it must also hold that

h(x-\mu^{\star})\leq B+\gamma+\frac{1}{w_{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(B-h(|X_{i}-\mu^{\star}|))\;.

We then consider the two cases, depending on the sign of
\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\mu^{\star}-X_{i})
. If it is positive, as
h
is a bijection on the positive line we directly get that

h\left(\frac{1}{w_{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\mu^{\star}-X_{i})\right)\leq h(x-\mu^{\star})\leq B+\gamma+\frac{1}{w_{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(B-h(|X_{i}-\mu^{\star}|))\;.

In the case that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\mu^{\star}-X_{i})\leq 0
, it is only necessary to check if the condition holds for
x=0
, that is if

0=h(0)\leq B+\gamma+\frac{1}{w_{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(B-h(|X_{i}-\mu^{\star}|))\;.

Hence, the formulation of (
23
) is just a way to summarize the two cases and (
22
)
\Rightarrow
(
23
). Furthermore, if (
23
) holds then (
23
) holds with either
x=\mu^{\star}
or
x=\mu^{\star}+\frac{1}{w_{n+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}w_{i}(\mu^{\star}-X_{i})
, which concludes the proof.

E.5
Proof of Theorem
23

We already proved in Lemmas
18
and
20
that (A1)–(A4) hold for families defined by an
h
-moment condition. Adapting these results for
D_{\pi}(F,\mu)=\Lambda_{\gamma}^{\star}(F,\mu)\text{1}(F\in\mathcal{F})
would be direct (we proved them for
\Lambda_{0}^{\star}(F,\mu)
with this notation), so we omit the proof for simplicity. The rest of the proof consists in proving that Assumption
4
holds.

Upper bound

Lemma
22
states that there exists a mapping
C
such that for any
\eta>0
and any
n\in\mathbb{N}

\text{[BCP]}\leq C(F_{n},\eta)\times e^{-n\Lambda_{\eta,\gamma}^{\star}(F_{n},\mu^{\star})}\;,

where
C
is continuous w.r.t. the Wasserstein metric in
F_{n}
, is continuous in
\eta
, and scales in
\eta^{-2}
. For any cdf
F\in\{F_{2},\dots,F_{K}\}
(sufficient to prove the result) and
\epsilon>0
, we define a neighborhood of
F
as
\mathcal{B}=\{F_{n}\in\mathcal{F}:\;,W(F_{n},F)\leq\epsilon\}
. If
F_{n}
denotes the empirical distribution of the observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
drawn i.i.d. from
F
, using Theorem 2 in
Fournier and Guillin 2015
and that
\mathbb{E}_{F}[|X|^{2+\xi}]<+\infty
for some
\xi>0
(by definition of
\mathcal{F}
) we obtain that

\sum_{n=1}^{+\infty}\mathbb{P}(W(F_{n},F)\geq\epsilon)<+\infty\;.

Using this result, by continuity we can first choose
\epsilon
small enough so that for instance
C(F_{n},\eta)\leq 2C(F,\eta)
for any
\eta
small enough. Then, we choose
\eta^{-1}=\mathcal{O}(n^{a})
for some
a>0
, so that (1)
C(F_{n},\eta)
is a polynomial in
n
, and (2)
e^{-n\Lambda_{\eta,\gamma}^{\star}(F_{n},\mu^{\star})}\leq 2e^{-n\Lambda_{\gamma}^{\star}(F_{n},\mu^{\star})}
. With these choices of parameters, we satisfy all the conditions of Assumption
4
regarding the upper bound on
p_{k}^{\pi}(t)
.

Lower bound

The formulation of the lower bound in Lemma
22
already fits Assumption
4
regarding the lower bound part.
Indeed, the lower bound in Lemma
22
becomes that of Assumption
4
if we set

(n_{\eta},c_{n},p_{0})
in Assumption
4
to

(n_{\delta,\gamma},c_{\delta}^{-1},e^{-n_{\delta,\gamma}\log(n_{\delta,\gamma}C_{B,\mu})})
.
This concludes the proof.
