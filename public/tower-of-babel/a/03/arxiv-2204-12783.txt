Abstract
We investigate the problem of reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS)-aided near-field localization of a user equipment (UE) served by a base station (BS) under phase-dependent amplitude variations at each RIS element. Through a misspecified Cramér-Rao bound (MCRB) analysis and a resulting lower bound (LB) on localization, we show that when the UE is unaware of amplitude variations (i.e., assumes unit-amplitude responses), severe performance penalties can arise, especially at high signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs). Leveraging Jacobi-Anger expansion to decouple range-azimuth-elevation dimensions, we develop a low-complexity approximated mismatched maximum likelihood (AMML) estimator, which is asymptotically tight to the LB. To mitigate performance loss due to model mismatch, we propose to jointly estimate the UE location and the RIS amplitude model parameters. The corresponding Cramér-Rao bound (CRB) is derived, as well as an iterative refinement algorithm, which employs the AMML method as a subroutine and alternatingly updates individual parameters of the RIS amplitude model. Simulation results indicate fast convergence and performance close to the CRB. The proposed method can successfully recover the performance loss of the AMML under a wide range of RIS parameters and effectively calibrate the RIS amplitude model online with the help of a user that has an a-priori unknown location.

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p}\left\{\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\eta_{i}\partial\eta_{j}}\log\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{\eta})\Big|_{\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}\right\},

(17)

\displaystyle[\boldsymbol{B}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}]_{ij}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p}\left\{\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta_{i}}\log\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{\eta})\frac{\partial}{\partial\eta_{j}}\log\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{\eta})\Big|_{\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}}\right\},

(18)

for

, with
\eta_{i}
denoting the
i
th element of
\boldsymbol{\eta}
.

Since the value of the pseudo-true parameter is generally not of interest, the

is used to establish the
LB
of any MS-unbiased estimator with respect to the true parameter value
[
41
]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{p}\{(\hat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(\boldsymbol{y})-\overline{\boldsymbol{\eta}})(\hat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}(\boldsymbol{y})-\overline{\boldsymbol{\eta}})^{\mathsf{T}}\}\succeq{\rm{LB}}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}),

(19)

where

\displaystyle{\rm{LB}}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0})\triangleq{\rm{MCRB}}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0})+(\overline{\boldsymbol{\eta}}-\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0})(\overline{\boldsymbol{\eta}}-\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0})^{\mathsf{T}}~.

(20)

The last term
in (

is a bias term; that is,

III-D

Localization Accuracy vs. Number of Transmissions

In order to illustrate the relationship between the localization accuracy and the number of transmissions, we analyze how the trace of the MCRB or the LB changes with respect to the number of transmissions,

We prove that if repetitive RIS phase profiles are used, the MCRB and LB do not decay with

Lemma 2
.

For the
k

T^{(k)}
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(k)}=\{\theta^{(k)}_{t,m}\}_{\{t=1,m=1\}}^{\{T^{(k)},M\}}
, and
\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}^{(k)}
, respectively, where

.

•

We assume that
T^{(2)}=KT^{(1)}
, where
K\in\mathbb{N}
.

•

Moreover, for the phase profiles, for any
1\leq m\leq M
, assume that

\theta^{(2)}_{t,m}=\theta^{(1)}_{(t\text{ mod }T^{(1)}),m}

(32)

where
\big(t\text{ mod }T^{(1)}\big)
denotes the remainder of the division of

by
T^{(1)}

Let
{\rm{MCRB}}\big(T^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}^{(k)}\big)
,
{\rm{LB}}\big(T^{(k)},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}^{(k)}\big)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}<\frac{\Tr\big\{{\rm{MCRB}}\big(T^{(2)},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}^{(2)}\big)\big\}}{\Tr\big\{{\rm{MCRB}}\big(T^{(1)},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}^{(1)}\big)\big\}}<1,

See Appendix

.

∎

Lemma

IV
Scenario-I: Mismatched Estimator

In this section, we focus on estimator design for Scenario-I. First, we derive the plain
MML
estimator, which entails computationally prohibitive high-dimensional non-convex optimization. To circumvent this, we then propose a low-complexity estimator capitalizing on the Jacobi-Anger expansion, which reduces the problem to a series of line searches over range, azimuth, and elevation domains.

IV-A

Mismatched Maximum Likelihood (MML) Estimator

Given the pdf of the assumed model in (
12
),
the
MML
estimator is given by
[
41
]

\hat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\text{MML}}(\boldsymbol{y})=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}}\log\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{\eta}).

(33)

Under some regularity conditions, it can be shown that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\text{MML}}(\boldsymbol{y})
is asymptotically MS-unbiased and its error covariance matrix is asymptotically equal to the
{\rm{MCRB}}(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0})

[
41
, Thm. 2]
. Hence, the covariance matrix of the MML estimator is asymptotically tight to the MCRB.

From (
12
) and (
33
), the MML estimator based on the received signal
\boldsymbol{y}
in (
1
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\text{MML}}(\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}}\log\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{y}\lvert\boldsymbol{\eta})=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{y}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right\rVert.

(34)

Since this problem is in the same form as in (
21
), it can be reduced to a 3-D optimization problem as discussed in Section
III-C
. In order to solve the resulting problem, initialization can be very critical as we are faced with a non-convex optimization problem. During the estimation process, we do not have access to the true position
\overline{\boldsymbol{p}}
. Hence, we cannot use the true position vector
\overline{\boldsymbol{p}}
for the initialization. If an arbitrarily chosen position vector is used for the initialization, the global optimal solution of (
33
) cannot always be obtained. To find a remedy for this issue, we next propose an approximated version of the MML estimator
in (
34
)
, namely, the approximate mismatched maximum likelihood (AMML) estimator.
Additionally, to reduce the complexity of the proposed algorithm, we propose a
Jacobi-Anger expansion
based
approach
when the number of transmissions is sufficiently large

[
27
,
43
]
,
[
57
]
.

Let us first re-write

in (

Scenario-II & Scenario-III: RIS-aided Localization Under Known RIS Amplitude Model

In this section, we investigate RIS-aided localization under Scenario-II and Scenario-III, where the UE is assumed to be aware of the RIS amplitude model in (

V-A

Scenario-II: Known RIS Amplitude Model with Unknown Parameters

In this scenario, in order to parameterize the unknown system parameters, we will use
\boldsymbol{\eta}=[\text{Re}(\alpha)\,\text{Im}(\alpha)\,\boldsymbol{p}^{\mathsf{T}}\,\beta_{\text{min}}\,\kappa\,\phi]^{\mathsf{T}}
, which consists of both the channel parameters and the RIS model parameters. Then, by
[
27
, Eq. 9]
, the Fisher Information matrix (FIM),
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\in\mathbb{R}^{8\times 8}
, can be expressed as

\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{\eta})=\frac{2}{N_{0}}\text{Re}\left\{\left(\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right)^{\mathsf{H}}\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\eta}}\right\}.

(60)

In order to compute the derivatives of
\boldsymbol{\mu}
with respect to the first five entries of
\boldsymbol{\eta}
, we can use the derivatives given in Appendix
E
by replacing the
\tilde{w}_{t,m}
terms with
w_{t,m}
. For the last three entries of
\boldsymbol{\eta}
, i.e., for the RIS related parameters, the
derivatives used in (
60
)
are
provided
in Appendix
G
.

After obtaining the FIM, by computing
\Tr\{\boldsymbol{J}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\}_{3:5,3:5}
, we can obtain the CRB for estimating the UE position. Moreover, the ML estimator can be stated as

\hat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{\text{ML}}(\boldsymbol{y})=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{8}}\log p(\boldsymbol{y}).

(61)

As discussed in Section
III-C
, the estimate for
\overline{\alpha}
can uniquely be determined for given estimates of
\overline{\boldsymbol{p}}
,
\overline{\beta}_{{\rm{min}}}
,
\overline{\kappa}
, and
\overline{\phi}
. That is, this problem can be reduced to a 6-dimensional optimization problem. Similar to the mismatched scenario (Scenario-I), the initialization is an important issue for this scenario, as well. For practical implementations, we propose an approximate version of the ML estimator, called the approximate maximum likelihood (AML) estimator, in the next section.

V-B

Scenario-II: AML Estimator

In this part, our goal is to solve the problem of
joint localization and online RIS calibration
in (
61
), which involves estimating the UE location
\overline{\boldsymbol{p}}
and the RIS model parameters
\overline{\beta}_{{\rm{min}}}
,
\overline{\kappa}
, and
\overline{\phi}
simultaneously. To accomplish this in an efficient manner, we propose a low-complexity estimator as an alternative to the high-dimensional non-convex optimization in (
61
).

Let us write the observations in (
1
) in a vector form as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}=\bar{\alpha}\,\boldsymbol{W}^{\mathsf{T}}(\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}})(\overline{\boldsymbol{p}})\sqrt{E_{s}}+\boldsymbol{n},

(62)

where
\boldsymbol{W}(\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}})=[\boldsymbol{w}_{1}(\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}})\,\ldots\,\boldsymbol{w}_{T}(\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}})]\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times T}
is the matrix of RIS profiles as a function of the parameters
\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}=[\overline{\beta}_{{\rm{min}}}~\overline{\kappa}~\overline{\phi}]^{\mathsf{T}}
of the RIS amplitude model in (
7
) and (
8
), and

is the additive noise vector. From (
7
) and (
8

can be expressed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{W}(\overline{\boldsymbol{\zeta}})=\left(\overline{\beta}_{{\rm{min}}}\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{1}(\overline{\kappa},\overline{\phi})+\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{2}(\overline{\kappa},\overline{\phi})\right)\odot e^{j\boldsymbol{\Theta}},

(63)

where

denotes the RIS phase shifts with
[\boldsymbol{\Theta}]_{m,t}=\theta_{m,t}
,

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{1}(\overline{\kappa},\overline{\phi})\triangleq{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{M}{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{T}^{\mathsf{T}}-\left(\frac{\sin(\boldsymbol{\Theta}-\overline{\phi})+1}{2}\right)^{\overline{\kappa}}~,

(64)

\boldsymbol{\Gamma}_{2}(\overline{\kappa},\overline{\phi})\triangleq\left(\frac{\sin(\boldsymbol{\Theta}-\overline{\phi})+1}{2}\right)^{\overline{\kappa}}~,

(65)

{{\boldsymbol{1}}}_{M}
is an all-ones vector of size
M
, and
\sin(\cdot)
and
(\cdot)^{\overline{\kappa}}
operations are element-wise.
Plugging

VII
Concluding Remarks

We have studied the problem of RIS-aided near-field localization under amplitude variations of individual RIS elements as a function of the applied phase shifts, which is a practical model based on equivalent RIS circuit models of reflecting elements
[
35
]
. First, through the
MCRB
analysis, we have quantified localization performance losses due to model misspecification when the UE is unaware of the RIS amplitude model, and developed an algorithm that achieves the corresponding
LB
. Second, under a known RIS amplitude model, we have derived the corresponding CRB and proposed a low-complexity algorithm for joint UE localization and online calibration of RIS model parameters. Extensive simulations over a broad range of operating conditions demonstrate the following key results:

•

Significance of the Knowledge of RIS Amplitude Model:
Being unaware of the true RIS amplitude model and assuming conventional unit-amplitude RIS responses can severely degrade the localization accuracy, with the losses being more pronounced at higher SNRs and higher amplitude fluctuations (small
\overline{\beta}_{{\rm{min}}}
and large
\overline{\kappa}
in (
8
)).

•

Localization under Model Mismatch:
Under the unknown RIS amplitude model, the proposed Jacobi-Anger expansion based low-complexity method in Algorithm
1
can provide near-optimal localization performance very close to the corresponding
LB
.

•

Joint Localization and RIS Calibration:
Under the known RIS amplitude model, it is possible to recover the performance losses incurred by model misspecification using the proposed algorithm in Algorithm
2
, which can calibrate the RIS model online with the aid of an unknown-location UE and subsequently refine the UE location with an accuracy that asymptotically attains the CRB.

Based on these outcomes, future research will focus on localization-optimal passive beamforming at the RIS under the realistic RIS amplitude model in (
8
), considering wideband signals and unobstructed LoS scenarios.
In addition, data-driven approaches will be considered to learn the mapping from RIS phases to amplitudes in the absence of a specific functional form as in (
8
), which would enable generalizing the proposed approach to any type of RIS.

Proof of Lemma
1

Based on the definition of the KL divergence and the system model in Section
II
, (
14
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\eta}_{0}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}}\int p(\boldsymbol{y})\log\left(\frac{p(\boldsymbol{y})}{\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{\eta})}\right)\,\text{d}\boldsymbol{y}

(76)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}}-\int p(\boldsymbol{y})\log\tilde{p}(\boldsymbol{y}|\boldsymbol{\eta})\,\text{d}\boldsymbol{y}

(77)

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}}\int p(\boldsymbol{y})\left\lVert\boldsymbol{y}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right\rVert^{2}\,\text{d}\boldsymbol{y}

(78)

where the second equality is due to the independence of
p(\boldsymbol{y})
from
\boldsymbol{\eta}
, and the last equality is obtained from (
12
). Then, it can be shown that the following equations hold:

\displaystyle\int p(\boldsymbol{y})\left\lVert\boldsymbol{y}-\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right\rVert^{2}\,d\boldsymbol{y}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\int p(\boldsymbol{y})\left|y_{t}-\tilde{{\mu}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right|^{2}\,\text{d}\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\underbrace{\left(\prod_{t^{\prime}\neq t}\int p(y_{t^{\prime}})\,\text{d}y_{t^{\prime}}\right)}_{=1}\left(\int p(y_{t})\left|y_{t}-\tilde{{\mu}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right|^{2}\,\text{d}y_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\int p(y_{t})\left|y_{t}-\tilde{{\mu}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right|^{2}\,\text{d}y_{t}.

(79)

We now introduce
\epsilon_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})=\mu_{t}-\tilde{{\mu}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
, so that
\left|y_{t}-\tilde{{\mu}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right|^{2}=\left|y_{t}-\mu_{t}+\epsilon_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right|^{2}
, and the integral expression in
(
79
) can be manipulated as follows:

\displaystyle\int p(y_{t})\left|y_{t}-\tilde{{\mu}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right|^{2}\,\text{d}y_{t}=\int p(y_{t})\left|y_{t}-\mu_{t}\right|^{2}\,\text{d}y_{t}+

\displaystyle\left|\epsilon_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right|^{2}\int p(y_{t})\,\text{d}y_{t}+2\int p(y_{t})\text{Re}\left((y_{t}-\mu_{t})^{*}\epsilon_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right)\,\text{d}y_{t}.

(80)

Since

and
\int p(y_{t})\,\text{d}y_{t}=1
, the expression in (

) can be simplified as

\int p(y_{t})\left|y_{t}-\tilde{{\mu}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right|^{2}\,\text{d}y_{t}=N_{0}+\left|\epsilon_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right|^{2}.

(81)

By combining (
78
), (
79
) and (

), we finally obtain that

\displaystyle\bar{\boldsymbol{\eta}}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(N_{0}+\left|\epsilon_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right|^{2}\right)=\argmin_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{5}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|\epsilon_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})\right|^{2},

which completes the proof.

Appendix C
Computational Complexity of Algorithm
1

First assume that
T<T_{\rm{thr}}
. It is clear that complexity of Algorithm
1
is dominated by the 2-D search for estimating azimuth and elevation angles. In the algorithm we equate
I_{\text{max}}
to
5
. That is, for Algorithm
1
, it is sufficient to analyze the computational complexity of the 2-D search given in (
39
). Let us define
\boldsymbol{x}(\vartheta,\varphi)\triangleq{\boldsymbol{Q}}(\vartheta,\varphi)\sqrt{E_{s}}
. The computational cost of
\boldsymbol{x}(\vartheta,\varphi)
is simply equal to
\mathcal{O}(TM)
. One should note that, (
39
) is equivalent to the following problem

(\widehat{\vartheta},\widehat{\varphi})=\argmax_{\vartheta,\varphi}\frac{\boldsymbol{x}(\vartheta,\varphi)^{\mathsf{H}}\boldsymbol{y}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{x}(\vartheta,\varphi)\right\rVert^{2}}~.

(93)

Hence after computing
\boldsymbol{x}(\vartheta,\varphi)
, we need to search over
\vartheta
and
\varphi
. Therefore, the overall cost of Algorithm
1
for
T\leq T_{\rm{thr}}
is
\mathcal{O}(TK^{2}M)
.

When
T\geq T_{\rm{thr}}
, the complexity of Algorithm
1
is dominated by the estimation of
\vartheta
. To estimate
\vartheta
, we need to compute
\boldsymbol{X}(\vartheta)\triangleq{\boldsymbol{Q}}\boldsymbol{G}^{\mathsf{T}}(\vartheta)
first. The computational cost of
\boldsymbol{X}(\vartheta)
is
\mathcal{O}\left(TM(2N+1)\right)
. Then, we need to compute the pseudo-inverse of
\boldsymbol{X}(\vartheta)
, whose computational cost is given by
\mathcal{O}\left(T(2N+1)^{2}\right)
since
T\geq 2N+1
. After computing the pseudo-inverse of
\boldsymbol{X}(\vartheta)
, we need to search
\vartheta
over
[0,\pi/2]
to find the minimum of
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{X}(\vartheta)\boldsymbol{X}^{\dagger}(\vartheta)\boldsymbol{y}\right\rVert

1

\mathcal{O}\left(T^{2}(2N+1)K\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(TM(2N+1)K\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(TK(2N+1)\text{max}\{T,M\}\right)=\mathcal{O}\left(TM(2N+1)K\right)
.

Derivation of Entries in the MCRB

Let
\boldsymbol{\eta}
be given by
\boldsymbol{\eta}=[\alpha_{r}\,\alpha_{i}\,\mathrm{x}\,\mathrm{y}\,\mathrm{z}]^{\mathsf{T}}
. Also, define
\boldsymbol{p}\triangleq[\mathrm{x}\,\mathrm{y}\,\mathrm{z}]^{\mathsf{T}}
,
b_{m}\triangleq[(\boldsymbol{p})]_{m}
, and
\alpha\triangleq\alpha_{r}+j\alpha_{i}
. We also introduce
\boldsymbol{u}=\frac{\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{RIS}}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{RIS}}\right\rVert}
and for any
1\leq m\leq M
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{m}=\frac{\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{p}_{m}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{p}_{m}\right\rVert}

and
\boldsymbol{u}_{m}=[u_{m,x}\,u_{m,y}\,u_{m,z}]^{\mathsf{T}}
.Then, the first and second derivatives of
\tilde{{\mu}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\eta}
are given as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{{\mu}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})}{\partial\alpha_{r}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}b_{m}\tilde{w}_{t,m}s_{t},\,\frac{\partial\tilde{{\mu}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})}{\partial\alpha_{i}}=j\sum_{m=1}^{M}b_{m}\tilde{w}_{t,m}s_{t}.

For
\nu\in\{\mathrm{x}\,\mathrm{y}\,\mathrm{z}\}
, we can write

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{{\mu}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})}{\partial\nu}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{{\mu}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})}{\partial\alpha_{r}\partial\nu}

\displaystyle=-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\sum_{m=1}^{M}b_{m}\left(u_{m,\nu}-u_{\nu}\right)\tilde{w}_{t,m}s_{t},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{{\mu}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})}{\partial\alpha_{i}\partial\nu}

\displaystyle=j\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{{\mu}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})}{\partial\alpha_{r}\partial\nu},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\tilde{{\mu}}_{t}(\boldsymbol{\eta})}{\partial\nu\partial\nu}=-\alpha\frac{4\pi^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\sum_{m=1}^{M}b_{m}\left(u_{m,\nu}-u_{\nu}\right)^{2}\tilde{w}_{t,m}s_{t}

In this part, we will show that the near-field steering vector in (

4

\displaystyle d_{m}^{2}=d^{2}+q_{m}^{2}-2dq_{m}\sin(\vartheta)\cos(\varphi-\psi_{m})~,

(94)

where
d_{m}=\left\lVert\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{p}_{m}\right\rVert

\psi_{m}

\boldsymbol{p}_{m}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{RIS}}
and the X-axis, i.e.,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}_{m}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{RIS}}=q_{m}[\cos(\psi_{m})~\sin(\psi_{m})~0]^{\mathsf{T}}~.

(95)

Re-arranging (
94
) and assuming
d\gg q_{m}
yields

\displaystyle d_{m}

\displaystyle=d\sqrt{1+\frac{q_{m}^{2}}{d^{2}}-2\frac{q_{m}}{d}\sin(\vartheta)\cos(\varphi-\psi_{m})}~,

\displaystyle\approx d\left(1-\frac{q_{m}}{d}\sin(\vartheta)\cos(\varphi-\psi_{m})\right)~,

(96)

where the approximation in (
96
) is obtained via first-order Taylor expansion of
f(x)=\sqrt{1+x^{2}-2x\kappa}
around
x=0
for constant
\kappa

[
48
, Eq. (6)]
,
[
61
, Eq. (3)]
,
[
47
, Eq. (12)]
. Using the approximation in (
96
), the phase term in (
3
) can be written as

\displaystyle\left\lVert\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{p}_{m}\right\rVert-\left\lVert\boldsymbol{p}-\boldsymbol{p}_{\text{RIS}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=d_{m}-d~,

\displaystyle\approx-q_{m}\sin(\vartheta)\cos(\varphi-\psi_{m})~,

(97)

which leads to the standard far-field steering vector

\displaystyle[(\boldsymbol{p})]_{m}
