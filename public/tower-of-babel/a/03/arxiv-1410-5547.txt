Abstract
. We show that a smooth radially symmetric solution u u to the graphic Willmore surface equation is either a constant or the defining function of a half sphere in ℝ 3 {\mathbb{R}}^{3} . In particular, radially symmetric entire Willmore graphs in ℝ 3 {\mathbb{R}}^{3} must be flat. When u u is a smooth radial solution over a punctured disk D ⁡ ( ρ ) \ { 0 } D(\rho)\backslash\{0\} and is in C 1 ​ ( D ​ ( ρ ) ) C^{1}(D(\rho)) , we show that there exist a constant λ \lambda and a function β \beta in C 0 ​ ( D ​ ( ρ ) ) C^{0}(D(\rho)) such that u ′′ ​ ( r ) = λ 2 ​ log ⁡ r + β ⁡ ( r ) u^{\prime\prime}(r)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\log r+\beta(r) ; moreover, the graph of u u is contained in a graphical region of an inverted catenoid which is uniquely determined by λ \lambda and β ⁡ ( 0 ) \beta(0) . It is also shown that a radial solution on the punctured disk extends to a C 1 C^{1} function on the disk when the mean curvature is square integrable.



1.
Introduction

A Willmore surface in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is a smoothly immersed surface that satisfies the equation

\Delta_{g}H+\frac{1}{2}H^{3}-2H^{2}K=0

(1.1)

where
H=\kappa_{1}+\kappa_{2}
and
K=\kappa_{1}\kappa_{2}
are the mean curvature and the Gauss curvature of the surface respectively and
\Delta_{g}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator in the metric
g
on the surface, which is induced from the Euclidean metric in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
via the immersion.

For a smooth graph
(x,y,u(x,y))
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, the area element is
v=\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}
and
H=\mbox{div}\left(\frac{Du}{v}\right)
, where
D
is the Euclidean gradient operator on functions in the
xy
-plane. Fro graphs, as shown in
[
3
]
, the Willmore surface equation (
1.1
) can be written in an elegant form:

\mbox{div}\left(\frac{1}{v}\left(\left(I-\frac{Du\otimes Du}{v^{2}}\right)D(vH)-\frac{1}{2}H^{2}Du\right)\right)=0.

(1.2)

Inspired by the classical theorem of Bernstein for the minimal surface equation, one asks when an entire Willmore graph is a plane. An entire smooth solution to (
1.2
) is shown to be affine if
H
is square integrable
[
2
]
,
[
5
]
; the approach taken in
[
2
]
is geometric in nature, rather than dealing with the fourth order partial differential equation directly. It has remained an interesting open question on the radially symmetric case: is an entire radial solution to (
1.2
) constant? In this paper, we answer this question affirmatively.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
u(r)
be a smooth solution to (
1.2
) defined on a disk
D(\rho)
in the
xy
-plane centered at the origin with radius

\rho
, where
r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
. Suppose that
u(0)=c
and the mean curvature of of the graph
(x,y,u(r))
at
(0,0,c)
is
2a
. Then

(1)

if
a=0
, then
u\equiv c
;

(2)

if
a\neq 0
, the graph is contained in a half sphere centered at
(0,0,c)
of radius
\frac{1}{|a|}
.

Consequently, a global radially symmetric solution to (
1.2
) must be constant.

The main observation in this paper can be summarized as follows. The fourth order equation (
1.2
) reduces to a third order equation due to its divergence free structure; the latter further reduces, in the radially symmetric case, to an ordinary differential equation of second order for
u^{\prime}
since the zeroth order term
u
itself is absent from the equation (
1.2
). The singularity at
r=0
in the second order equation is surprisingly mild. Even more, the difference of any two solutions to the second order equation satisfies an equation which admits a contraction property that leads to uniqueness of solutions to an initial value problem. The desired rigidity then follows. In section
2
we will prove Theorem
1.1
.

In section
3
, we will extend the techniques developed in section
2
to study radially symmetric solutions with a point singularity at the origin. Our main focus is to classify the radially symmetric solutions to the Willmore surface
equation on a punctured disk
D(\rho)\backslash\{0\}
, which is in
C^{1}(D(\rho))
. Such a solution
u
will be shown to have the following decomposition property:

u^{\prime\prime}(r)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\log r+\beta(r),\,\,\,\,\mbox{where }\beta\in C^{0}[0,\rho);

which is equivalent to the mean curvature decomposes as:

H(r)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\log r+\gamma(r),\,\,\,\,\mbox{where }\gamma\in C^{0}[0,\rho).

Further, we will prove that such a surface is contained in the image of an inversion in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
of a catenoid, where the inversion and the catenoid are uniquely determined by the information at the origin:
\lambda
and
\beta(0)
(or equivalently
\gamma(0)
). We now describe the corresponding catenoid and inversion.

A catenoid
\mathcal{C}_{c}
may be defined by the parametrization

{\mathcal{F}}_{c}(t,\theta)=(|c|\cosh t\cos\theta,|c|\cosh t\sin\theta,ct),

where
c\neq 0
(here we allow
c<0
). The inversion of this catenoid under the mapping

I_{a}(x,y,z)=\frac{(x,y,z-a)}{|(x,y,z-a)|^{2}}

is a closed surface with
0\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
as its only nonsmooth point, in particular, away from finitely many circles that are perpendicular to the
z
-axis, the inverted catenoid can be written as several graphs over the
xy
-plane, among them, we will identify which one contains the graph of
u
(translated by the constant vector
(0,0,u(0))
).
Let

h(t)=\frac{|c|\cosh t}{c^{2}\cosh^{2}t+(ct-a)^{2}}

be the distance of
I_{a}({\mathcal{F}}_{c}(t,\theta))
to the
z
-axis. Direct computations shows that
h
has only finitely many critical points and denote them by
t_{1}<\cdots<t_{m}
. Then the inversion of
\mathcal{C}_{c}\backslash(\bigcup_{i}{\mathcal{F}}_{c}(\{t_{i}\}\times S^{1}))

consists of the following
m+1
graphs

I_{a}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{c}((-\infty,t_{1})\times S^{1})\right),\,\,\,\,I_{a}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{c}((t_{1},t_{2})\times S^{1}\right),\cdots,I_{a}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{c}((t_{m},+\infty)\times S^{1}\right)

where the first one and the last one are graphs over a punctured disk and the rest are graphs over an annulus. Let

t_{c,a}=t_{m},\,\,\,\,R_{c,a}=h(t_{c,a}),\,\,\,\,\Sigma_{c,a}=I_{a}\left({\mathcal{F}}_{c}((t_{m},+\infty)\times S^{1})\right).

The disk
D(R_{c,a})
in the
xy
-plane is the largest domain
over which
\Sigma_{c,a}
can be defined (see Section
3.2
for more details).

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
u(r)
be a smooth solution to (
1.2
) defined on a disk
D(\rho)\backslash\{0\}
in the
xy
-plane centered at the origin with radius
\rho
. Suppose
u\in C^{1}(D(\rho))
. Then

(1)

there exist a constant
\lambda
and a function
\beta\in C^{0}([0,\rho))
such that

u^{\prime\prime}(r)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\log r+\beta(r),\,\,\,\,r>0;

(2)

the graph of
u
is contained in
(0,0,u(0))+\Sigma_{c,a}
where

c=-\frac{\lambda}{4}\,\,\,\,{and}\,\,\,\,a=\frac{\lambda}{4}\log\frac{|\lambda|}{8}+\frac{3\lambda}{8}-\frac{\beta(0)}{2}

and
\rho
is no larger than
R_{c,a}
and
u\in C^{1,\alpha}(D(\rho))
for any
0<\alpha<1
.

The regularity assumption
u\in C^{1}(D(\rho))
in Theorem
1.2
will be shown to hold when the mean curvature
H
is square integrable over
D(\rho)
.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
u(r)
be a smooth solution to (
1.2
) defined on a punctured disk
D(\rho)\backslash\{0\}
in the
xy
-plane. Suppose that the Willmore energy
\int_{D(\rho)}|H|^{2}d\mu
is finite. Then
u
can be extended at
0
as a
C^{1,\alpha}
function on
D(\rho)
and (1) and (2) in Theorem
1.2
hold.

For rigidity of Willmore surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
that are not necessarily graphs, Bryant showed
[
1
]
, among other things, that an embedded closed Willmore surface of genus zero must be a round sphere; Rigoli
[
6
]
showed that for a complete
Willmore immersion
f:\Sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
, if there
exists a vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
\langle n+Hf,a\rangle\equiv 0
, then
f(\Sigma)
must be a plane.

2.
Smooth Radially symmetric solutions

Let
f
be a smooth radially symmetric function defined on the disk

D(\rho)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
centered at the origin with radius
\rho\in(0,\infty]
. The divergence of the smooth radial vector field
X=f(r)\frac{\partial}{\partial r}
, where
r=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
for
(x,y)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, is given by

\mbox{div}X=\frac{(rf)^{\prime}}{r}=f^{\prime}+\frac{f}{r}.

(2.1)

Moreover,
f^{\prime}(0)
must be 0. In fact, if we set
\tilde{f}(x,y)=f(r)
, then
\tilde{f}(x,y)=\tilde{f}(-x,-y)
. It follows that both
\tilde{f}_{x}
and
\tilde{f}_{y}
vanish at the origin; hence

f^{\prime}(0)=\tilde{f}_{x}(0,0)\cos\theta+\tilde{f}_{y}(0,0)\sin\theta=0.

Now consider the graph defined by a smooth radially symmetric function
u(r)
on
D(\rho)
. Letting
w=u^{\prime}
, we have

v=\sqrt{1+w^{2}}\ \ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \ v^{\prime}=\frac{ww^{\prime}}{v}.

Note that

w(0)=u^{\prime}(0)=0.

We calculate the mean curvature of the graph as follows

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{div}\left(\frac{w}{v}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right)

(2.2)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{w}{v}\right)^{\prime}+\frac{w}{rv}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{w^{\prime}}{v}-\frac{w^{2}w^{\prime}}{v^{3}}+\frac{w}{rv}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{w^{\prime}}{v^{3}}+\frac{w}{rv}.

Two consequences are immediate. First, at the origin, we have

H(0)=w^{\prime}(0)+\lim_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{w}{rv}=2w^{\prime}(0).

(2.3)

Second, we have

(vH)^{\prime}=\frac{w^{\prime\prime}}{v^{2}}-\frac{2w(w^{\prime})^{2}}{v^{4}}+\frac{w^{\prime}}{r}-\frac{w}{r^{2}}.

(2.4)

To simplify the divergence free radial vector field in the Willmore surface equation (
1.2
), we first compute

\displaystyle\left(I-\frac{Du\otimes Du}{v^{2}}\right)D(vH)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\left(1-\frac{u_{x}^{2}}{v^{2}}\right)(vH)_{x}-\frac{u_{x}u_{y}}{v^{2}}\left(vH\right)_{y}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}

(2.5)

\displaystyle+\left(-\frac{u_{x}u_{y}}{v^{2}}(vH)_{x}+\left(1-\frac{u_{y}^{2}}{v^{2}}\right)(vH)_{y}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{1+u_{y}^{2}}{v^{2}}(vH)_{x}-\frac{u_{x}u_{y}}{v^{2}}(vH)_{y}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}

\displaystyle+\left(-\frac{u_{x}u_{y}}{v^{2}}(vH)_{x}+\frac{1+u_{x}^{2}}{v^{2}}(vH)_{y}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}.

Next, we convert the partial derivatives in
x,y
of the radially symmetric functions
u,vH
into the derivatives in
r
:

u_{x}=u_{r}\frac{x}{r},\,\,\,\,u_{y}=u_{r}\frac{y}{r},\,\,\,\,(vH)_{x}=(vH)_{r}\frac{x}{r},\,\,\,\,(vH)_{y}=(vH)_{r}\frac{y}{r}.

(2.6)

It follows by substituting (
2.6
) into (
2.5
) that

\displaystyle\hskip 21.33955pt\left(I-\frac{Du\otimes Du}{v^{2}}\right)D(vH)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v^{2}}\left(\left({1+u_{r}^{2}\frac{y^{2}}{r^{2}}}\right)(vH)_{r}\frac{x}{r}-{u_{r}^{2}}(vH)_{r}\frac{xy^{2}}{r^{3}}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}

(2.7)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{v^{2}}\left(-{u_{r}^{2}}(vH)_{r}\frac{x^{2}y}{r^{3}}+\left(1+u_{r}^{2}\frac{x^{2}}{r^{2}}\right)(vH)_{r}\frac{y}{r}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v^{2}}(vH)_{r}\left(\frac{x}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+\frac{y}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v^{2}}(vH)_{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}.

Define a radially symmetric function by

f=\left<\frac{1}{v}\left(\left(I-\frac{Du\otimes Du}{v^{2}}\right)D(vH)-\frac{1}{2}H^{2}Du\right),\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right>.

(2.8)

Using (
2.2
), (
2.4
) and (
2.7
), we find

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v}\left(\frac{1}{v^{2}}(vH)_{r}-\frac{1}{2}H^{2}w\right)

(2.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v}\left(\frac{1}{v^{2}}\left(\frac{w^{\prime\prime}}{v^{2}}-\frac{2w(w^{\prime})^{2}}{v^{4}}+\frac{w^{\prime}}{r}-\frac{w}{r^{2}}\right)\right)-\frac{1}{2v}H^{2}w

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{w^{\prime\prime}}{v^{5}}-\frac{2w(w^{\prime})^{2}}{v^{7}}+\frac{1}{v^{3}}\left(\frac{w^{\prime}}{r}-\frac{w}{r^{2}}\right)-\frac{w}{2v}\left(\frac{(w^{\prime})^{2}}{v^{6}}+\frac{w^{2}(w^{\prime})^{2}}{r^{2}v^{2}}+2\frac{w^{\prime}w}{rv^{4}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v^{5}}\left(w^{\prime\prime}+\left(-2\frac{w}{v^{2}}-\frac{w}{2v^{2}}\right)(w^{\prime})^{2}+\left(v^{2}-w^{2}\right)\frac{w^{\prime}}{r}-v^{2}\frac{w}{r^{2}}-v^{2}\frac{w^{3}}{2r^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v^{5}}\left(w^{\prime\prime}-\frac{5w}{2\left(1+w^{2}\right)}(w^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{w^{\prime}}{r}-\left(1+w^{2}\right)\frac{w}{r^{2}}-\left(1+w^{2}\right)\frac{w^{3}}{2r^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{v^{5}}\left(w^{\prime\prime}+\left(\frac{w}{r}\right)^{\prime}-\frac{5w}{2\left(1+w^{2}\right)}(w^{\prime})^{2}-\frac{w^{3}}{2r^{2}}\left(3+w^{2}\right)\right).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
u
be a smooth radially symmetric function that solves the Willmore surface equation (
1.2
) on a disk
D(\rho)
in the
xy
-plane centred at the origin. Then
f=0
.

Proof.

Using the function defined in (
2.8
), the Willmore surface equation (
1.2
) reads

\mbox{div}\left(f\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right)=0.

(2.10)

By (
2.1
), the above equation reduces to

(rf)^{\prime}=0.

Then

f=\frac{C}{r}

(2.11)

for some constant
C
. Furthermore, for any
0<r_{0}<\rho
, integrating the Willmore equation (
2.10
) and using Stokes’ theorem

0=\int_{D(r_{0})}\mbox{div}\left(f\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right)=\int_{\partial D(r_{0})}f=2\pi\,C.

Thus,
C=0
.
\hfill\Box

Then, Lemma
2.1
and (
2.9
) yield

Lemma 2.2
.

F=(x,y,u(r))
is Willmore if and only if

w^{\prime\prime}+\left(\frac{w}{r}\right)^{\prime}-\varphi(\omega)=0,

(2.12)

where

\varphi(\omega)=\frac{5w}{2(1+w^{2})}(w^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{w^{3}}{2r^{2}}(3+w^{2}).

For (
2.12
), we now establish a uniqueness result for an initial value problem.

Lemma 2.3
.

There exists
\epsilon>0
such that the equation (
2.12
) has at most one solution on
[0,\epsilon]
with
initial data
w(0)=0
, and
w^{\prime}(0)=a
.

Proof.

Let
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
be solutions to (
2.12
) on
[0,r_{0}]
for some
r_{0}>0
. Then we have

(w_{1}-w_{2})^{\prime\prime}+(\frac{w_{1}-w_{2}}{r})^{\prime}=\varphi(w_{1})-\varphi(w_{2}).

(2.13)

We have

\displaystyle\varphi(w_{1})-\varphi(w_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{5w_{1}}{2\left(1+w_{1}^{2}\right)}(w_{1}^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{w_{1}^{3}}{2r^{2}}\left(3+w_{1}^{2}\right)-\frac{5w_{2}}{2\left(1+w_{2}^{2}\right)}(w_{2}^{\prime})^{2}-\frac{w_{2}^{3}}{2r^{2}}\left(3+w_{2}^{2}\right)

(2.14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{5w_{1}}{2(1+w_{1}^{2})}-\frac{5w_{2}}{2\left(1+w_{2}^{2}\right)}\right)(w_{1}^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{5w_{2}(w_{1}^{\prime}+w_{2}^{\prime})}{2(1+w_{2}^{2})}\left(w_{1}^{\prime}-w_{2}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle+3\,\frac{w_{1}^{3}-w_{2}^{3}}{2r^{2}}+\frac{w_{1}^{5}-w_{2}^{5}}{2r^{2}}.

To estimate the three terms on the right hand side of the above identity, we first observe

\left|\frac{w_{1}}{(1+w_{1}^{2})}-\frac{w_{2}}{(1+w_{2}^{2})}\right|=\left|\frac{(1-w_{1}w_{2})(w_{1}-w_{2})}{(1+w_{1}^{2})(1+w_{2}^{2})}\right|\leq|w_{1}-w_{2}|.

Next, let

\Lambda=\|w_{1}\|_{C^{1}([0,r_{0}])}+\|w_{2}\|_{C^{1}([0,r_{0}])}.

(2.15)

Since
w_{i}(0)=0
, for
r\leq r_{0}
, it holds

|w_{i}(r)|=\left|\int^{r}_{0}w^{\prime}(s)ds\right|\leq r\|w_{i}\|_{C^{1}([0,r_{0}])}.

There exists some positive constant
C(\Lambda)
which depends only on
\Lambda
such that:

\displaystyle\left|\frac{w_{i}}{r}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\Lambda),\,\,\,\,i=1,2;

\displaystyle\left|\frac{w_{1}^{3}-w_{2}^{3}}{r^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|w_{1}^{2}+w_{1}w_{2}+w_{2}^{2}|}{r^{2}}|w_{1}-w_{2}|\leq C(\Lambda)|w_{1}-w_{2}|,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{w_{1}^{5}-w_{2}^{5}}{r^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(\Lambda)r^{2}|w_{1}-w_{2}|.

Then

|\varphi(w_{1})-\varphi(w_{2})|\leq C(r|w_{1}^{\prime}-w_{2}^{\prime}|+|w_{1}-w_{2}|),\,\,\,\,r\in[0,r_{0}],

(2.16)

where
C
depends only on
\Lambda
and
r_{0}
.

Let
\phi=w_{1}-w_{2}
. We have
\phi(0)=\phi^{\prime}(0)=0
, hence
\lim_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\phi}{r}=0
; therefore by (
2.13
),

\phi^{\prime}(r)+\frac{\phi(r)}{r}=\int_{0}^{r}(\varphi(w_{1}(t))-\varphi(w_{2}(t)))dt.

Then

(r\phi(r))^{\prime}=r\int_{0}^{r}(\varphi(w_{1}(t))-\varphi(w_{2}(t)))dt.

By (
2.16
),

\displaystyle|(r\phi(r))^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cr\int_{0}^{r}(|\phi(t)|+|t\phi^{\prime}(t)|)dt

(2.17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Cr\int_{0}^{r}(|\phi(t)|+\left|(t\phi(t))^{\prime}-\phi(t)\right|)dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2Cr\int_{0}^{r}(|\phi(t)|+|(t\phi(t))^{\prime}|)dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2Cr\int_{0}^{r}\left(\left|\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}(s\phi(s))^{\prime}ds\right|+|(t\phi(t))^{\prime}|\right)dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4Cr^{2}\|(t\phi)^{\prime}\|_{C^{0}([0,r])},

where
r\in(0,r_{0}]
.

Given
r_{1}\in(0,r_{0})\cap(0,\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2C}})
, if
(r\phi)^{\prime}
is not identically
0
on
[0,r_{1}]
, then we can find
r_{2}\in(0,r_{1}]
, such that

0<|(r\phi)^{\prime}(r_{2})|=\|(r\phi)^{\prime}\|_{C^{0}([0,r_{2}])}.

Thus, (
2.17
) yields

0<|(r\phi)^{\prime}(r_{2})|\leq 4Cr_{2}^{2}|(r\phi)^{\prime}(r_{2})|<\frac{1}{2}|(r\phi)^{\prime}(r_{2})|,

which is impossible. Therefore,
(r\phi)^{\prime}=0
on
[0,r_{1}]
. It follows that

is a constant which must be 0 since
\phi(0)=0
. We conclude that
\phi=0
on
[0,r_{1}]
. Then
w_{1}=w_{2}
on
[0,\epsilon]
for
\epsilon=\min\{r_{0},\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}C}\}
.

\hfill\Box

is solution to (

(3.1)

where

\varphi(\omega)=\frac{5w}{2(1+w^{2})}(w^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{w^{3}}{2r^{2}}(3+w^{2}).

We now establish a uniqueness result for an initial value problem of (
3.1
).

Lemma 3.2
.

There exists
\epsilon>0
such that the equation (
3.1
) has at most one solution on
(0,\epsilon]
with the
initial data

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}w(r)=0,\,\,\,\,\lim_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left(w^{\prime}(r)-\frac{\lambda}{2}\log r\right)=b.

(3.2)

Proof.

Let
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
be solutions to (
3.1
) on
(0,r_{0}]
for some
0<r_{0}<1
with (
3.2
) satisfied. Let
\phi=w_{1}-w_{2}
. It follows that

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\phi^{\prime}(r)=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\phi(r)=0

and
\phi
can be extended to a function in
C^{1}([0,r_{0}])
with

\phi(0)=0
and
\phi^{\prime}(0)=0
.
Hence
\lim_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\phi}{r}=0
, and by integration

\phi^{\prime}(r)+\frac{\phi(r)}{r}=\int_{0}^{r}\left(\varphi(w_{1}(t))-\varphi(w_{2}(t))+\lambda\frac{v_{1}^{5}(t)-v_{2}^{5}(t)}{t}\right)dt.

Then

(r\phi(r))^{\prime}=r\int_{0}^{r}\left(\varphi(w_{1}(t))-\varphi(w_{2}(t))+\lambda\frac{(1+w_{1}^{2})^{\frac{5}{2}}-(1+w_{2}^{2})^{\frac{5}{2}}}{t}\right)dt.

From (
3.2
), we may find
\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda_{1}(r_{0},b,\lambda)
such that

|w_{1}^{\prime}(r)|+|w^{\prime}_{2}(r)|<\Lambda_{1}\log\frac{1}{r}.

(3.3)

and

\frac{|w_{i}(r)|}{r}\leq\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}|w_{i}^{\prime}|\leq\Lambda_{1}\log\frac{1}{r},\,\,\,\,i=1,2.

Moreover,

\left|\frac{(1+w_{1}^{2}(r))^{\frac{5}{2}}-(1+w_{2}^{2}(r))^{\frac{5}{2}}}{r}\right|\leq\frac{C}{r}|w_{1}^{2}-w_{2}^{2}|\leq C|\phi|\log\frac{1}{r}.

Applying (
2.14
), similar to the proof of (
2.16
) by using (
3.3
) instead of (
2.15
), we have

\left|\varphi(w_{1}(r))-\varphi(w_{2}(r))+\lambda\frac{(1+w_{1}^{2}(r))^{\frac{5}{2}}-(1+w_{2}^{2}(r))^{\frac{5}{2}}}{r}\right|\leq C(|\phi(r)|+r|\phi^{\prime}|)\log^{2}\frac{1}{r}.

Then using the arguments in (
2.17
), we have

\displaystyle|(r\phi(r))^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle Cr\int_{0}^{r}(|\phi(t)|+|t\phi^{\prime}(t)|)\log^{2}\frac{1}{t}dt

(3.4)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 4Cr\left(r\log^{2}r-2r\log r+2r\right)\|(t\phi)^{\prime}\|_{C^{0}([0,r])}\,

where
r\in(0,r_{0}]
.

Given
r_{1}\in(0,r_{0})
, such that

4Cr_{1}\left(r_{1}\log^{2}r_{1}-2r_{1}\log r_{1}+2r_{1}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}.

If
(r\phi)^{\prime}
is not identically
0
on
[0,r_{1}]
, then we can find
r_{2}\in(0,r_{1}]
, such that

0<|(r\phi)^{\prime}(r_{2})|=\|(r\phi)^{\prime}\|_{C^{0}([0,r_{2}])}.

Thus, (
3.4
) yields

0<|(r\phi)^{\prime}(r_{2})|\leq\frac{1}{2}|(r\phi)^{\prime}(r_{2})|,

which is impossible. Therefore,
(r\phi)^{\prime}=0
on
[0,r_{1}]
, which implies that
w_{1}=w_{2}
on
[0,\epsilon]
for
\epsilon=\min\{r_{0},r_{1}\}
.

\hfill\Box

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
w\in C^{2}((0,a_{0}])
be a solution to (
3.1
) on
(0,a_{0}]
. If
\lim_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}w(r)=0
,
then
w(r)-\frac{\lambda}{2}r\log r\in C^{1}([0,a_{0}])
, and consequently
\lim_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left(w^{\prime}(r)-\frac{\lambda}{2}\log r\right)
exists.

Proof.

We assume
|w|<\epsilon_{1}<1
on
(0,a],a\leq a_{0},a<1
.
First of all, we prove

\int_{r}^{a}\left((w^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{w^{2}}{2t^{2}}\right)<C\left(\frac{w^{2}}{2r}+|w|\log\frac{1}{r}+\int_{r}^{a}\frac{|w|}{t}dt+1\right),\,\,\,\,r\in(0,a),

(3.5)

where
C
is a positive constant depends only on
a
,
w^{\prime}(a)
,
\lambda
and the upper bound of
w
in
[0,a]
. For simplicity, we will use
C
to denote some uniform constant in the rest of the proof.

Multiplying both sides of (
3.1
) by
w
and then integrating from
r
to
a
, since
w\varphi\geq 0
, we have

\int_{r}^{a}\left(w^{\prime\prime}w+\left(\frac{w}{t}\right)^{\prime}w\right)\geq\int_{r}^{a}\frac{\lambda}{t}v^{5}w\geq-C\int_{r}^{a}\frac{|w|}{t}dt.

Then integrating by parts implies

-w^{\prime}(r)w(r)-\frac{w^{2}(r)}{r}-\int_{r}^{a}\frac{(w^{2})^{\prime}}{2t}-\int_{r}^{a}(w^{\prime})^{2}\geq-C\left(1+\int_{r}^{a}\frac{|w|}{t}dt\right),

then applying integration by parts again and absorbing the terms evaluated at
a
into
C
, we have

-w^{\prime}(r)w(r)-\frac{w^{2}(r)}{r}+\frac{w^{2}(r)}{2r}-\int_{r}^{a}\frac{w^{2}}{2t^{2}}-\int_{r}^{a}(w^{\prime})^{2}\geq-C\left(1+\int_{r}^{a}\frac{|w|}{t}dt\right).

Rearranging terms, we arrive at

\int_{r}^{a}\left((w^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{w^{2}}{2t^{2}}\right)dt\leq C\left(1+\int_{r}^{a}\frac{|w|}{t}dt\right)-w(r)\frac{(rw(r))^{\prime}}{r}+\frac{w^{2}(r)}{2r}.

(3.6)

To estimate
(rw(r))^{\prime}
, integrating (
3.1
) again,

\frac{(rw(r))^{\prime}}{r}=-\int_{r}^{a}\left(\frac{5w(w^{\prime})^{2}}{2(1+w^{2})}+\frac{3w^{3}+w^{5}}{2t^{2}}+\lambda\frac{v^{5}-1}{t}\right)dt+\lambda\log\frac{r}{a}+w^{\prime}(a)+\frac{w(a)}{a}.

(3.7)

Then, absorbing the terms evaluated at
a
into a uniform constant
C
, we have

\left|\frac{(rw(r))^{\prime}}{r}\right|\leq\epsilon_{1}\int_{r}^{a}\left((w^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{w^{2}}{2t^{2}}\right)+C\log\frac{1}{r}.

(3.8)

Putting (
3.8
) into (
3.6
) and recalling
|w|<\epsilon_{1}
, we get

\int_{r}^{a}\left((w^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{w^{2}}{2t^{2}}\right)\leq C\left(1+\int_{r}^{a}\frac{|w|}{t}dt\right)+\epsilon_{1}^{2}\int_{r}^{a}\left((w^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{w^{2}}{2t^{2}}\right)+C|w|\log\frac{1}{r}+\frac{w^{2}(r)}{2r}

and in turn we conclude the proof of (
3.5
).

Secondly, we prove

|w(r)|<Cr\log\frac{1}{r}.

(3.9)

By (
3.5
) and (
3.8
),

|rw^{\prime}+w|\leq C\left(w^{2}+r\log\frac{1}{r}\right).

Fix an
\epsilon_{2}\in(0,1)
and choose
a^{\prime}
such that
|w|<{\epsilon_{2}}/{C}
on

(0,a^{\prime})
. It is well-known that

\big|\,|rw|^{\prime}\,\big|\leq|(rw)^{\prime}|.

Therefore

\left|(r|w|)^{\prime}\right|\leq\epsilon_{2}|w|+Cr\log\frac{1}{r},\,\,\,\,r\in(0,a^{\prime}).

Then

r|w|^{\prime}+(1-\epsilon_{2})|w|\leq Cr\log\frac{1}{r}.

Thus

\left(r^{1-\epsilon_{2}}|w|\right)^{\prime}\leq Cr^{1-\epsilon_{2}}\log\frac{1}{r},

then

r^{1-\epsilon_{2}}|w(r)|\leq C\int_{0}^{r}t^{1-\epsilon_{2}}\log\frac{1}{t}dt\leq\frac{C}{2-\epsilon_{2}}\left(r^{2-\epsilon_{2}}\log\frac{1}{r}+r^{2-\epsilon_{2}}\right)

and (
3.9
) is established by dividing
r^{1-\epsilon_{2}}
.

Now we can finish the proof.
Putting (
3.9
) into (
3.5
), we are led to

\int_{0}^{a}\left((w^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{w^{2}}{2t^{2}}\right)dt<+\infty,

then

\int_{0}^{a}\left|\frac{5w}{2(1+w^{2})}(w^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{3w^{3}+w^{5}}{2t^{2}}+\lambda\frac{v^{5}-1}{t}\right|dt<+\infty.

(3.10)

For convenience, set

\psi(r)=-\int_{r}^{a}\left(\frac{5w}{2(1+w^{2})}(w^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{3w^{3}+w^{5}}{2t^{2}}+\lambda\frac{v^{5}-1}{t}\right)dt,

which is continuous in
[0,a]
by (
3.10
). By (
3.7
),

w(r)=\frac{1}{r}\int_{0}^{r}t\psi(t)dt+\frac{\lambda}{2}r\log r+Br,

where

B=\frac{1}{2}\left(-\lambda\log a+w^{\prime}(a)+\frac{w(a)}{a}\right)-\frac{\lambda}{4}.

Thus,

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\left(w(r)-\frac{\lambda}{2}\log r\right)^{\prime}=B+\frac{\psi(0)}{2}.

It is easy to check that the function
w(r)-\frac{\lambda}{2}r\log r
is differentiable at 0 with derivative
B+\frac{\psi(0)}{2}
. Therefore,
w(r)-\frac{\lambda}{2}r\log r\in C^{1}([0,a_{0}])
.

\hfill\Box

3.2.
Inverted catenoids

A catenoid
\mathcal{C}_{c}
is defined by

{\mathcal{F}}_{c}(t,\theta)=(|c|\cosh t\cos\theta,|c|\cosh t\sin\theta,ct),

where
c\neq 0
.
The inversion of
\mathcal{C}_{c}
under the map
I_{a}:{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
defined in the introduction is given by

(x,y,z)=\left(\frac{|c|\cosh t\cos\theta}{R^{2}},\frac{|c|\cosh t\sin\theta}{R^{2}},\frac{ct-a}{R^{2}}\right),

where

R^{2}=c^{2}\cosh^{2}t+(ct-a)^{2}.

Let

h(t)=\frac{|c|\cosh t}{R^{2}},\,\,\,\,\bar{h}(t)=\frac{ct-a}{R^{2}}.

Then
I_{a}(\mathcal{C}_{c})
is the surface of revolution generated by rotating the curve

\sigma(t)=(h(t),0,\bar{h}(t))

about the
z
-axis.
Since
h(+\infty)=h(-\infty)=0
,
h
has at least 1 critical point. Direct calculation shows that
h
has only finitely many (in fact, at most three) critical points. A tangent vector of
\sigma
is given by
\sigma^{\prime}(t)=(h^{\prime}(t),0,\bar{h}^{\prime}(t))
, so
t
is a critical point of
h
if and only if the tangent line of
\sigma(t)
is parallel to the
z
-axis. Let
t_{1}<\cdots<t_{m}
be the critical points of
h
and set
t_{0}=-\infty
,
t_{m+1}=+\infty
, then

I_{a}({\mathcal{F}}_{c}((t_{i},t_{i+1})\times S^{1}))
is a graph over

D(h(t_{i+1}))\backslash D(h(t_{i}))
, which cannot be extended as a smooth graph over a larger domain, where
i=0,\cdots,m
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Given
\lambda,b\in\mathbb{R}
, there exists a unique pair of numbers
c
and
a
, such that

\Sigma_{c,a}
is the graph of a function
U(r)
with

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left(U^{\prime\prime}(r)-\frac{\lambda}{2}\log r\right)=b.

Moreover,
U^{\prime}
satisfies the equation (
3.1
).

Proof.

After changing parametrization, a catenoid can be written as

(\rho\cos\theta,\rho\sin\theta,u(\rho)),\,\,\,\,\mbox{where}\,\,\,\,u(\rho)=c\operatorname{arccosh}\frac{\rho}{|c|}.

In this parametriation,
R=\sqrt{\rho^{2}+(u(\rho)-a)^{2}}
.
Suppose
\Sigma_{c,a}
is the graph of
U(r)
. We have

r=\frac{\rho}{R^{2}},\,\,\,\,\mbox{ and }\,\,\,\,U(r)=\frac{u(\rho)-a}{R^{2}}=\frac{r}{\rho}(u(\rho)-a),\,\,\,\,r\in(0,R_{c,a}).

We now calculate the expansion of
U
at
r=0
. It is clear

\frac{1}{\rho}=r\left(1+\frac{(u(\rho)-a)^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)

(3.11)

and

\displaystyle U(r)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{2}(u(\rho)-a)\left(1+\frac{(u(\rho)-a)^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)

(3.12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{2}u(\rho)-ar^{2}+\frac{r^{2}}{\rho^{2}}O(u^{3}(\rho)),\,\,\,\,\mbox{ as }\rho\rightarrow+\infty.

By the definition of
\Sigma_{c,a}
,
\frac{u(\rho)}{c}\rightarrow+\infty
as
\rho\rightarrow+\infty
,
then by taking log of

e^{\frac{u(\rho)}{c}}\frac{1+e^{-2\frac{u(\rho)}{c}}}{2}=\cosh\frac{u(\rho)}{\rho}=\frac{\rho}{|c|},

we are led to

\displaystyle u(\rho)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c\log\rho+c\log 2-c\log|c|-c\log\left(1+e^{-2\frac{u(\rho)}{c}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c\log\rho+c\log 2-c\log|c|+o(1),\,\,\,\,\mbox{as $\rho\to+\infty$.}

Using (
3.11
), we get

\displaystyle u(\rho)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c\log r+c\log 2-c\log|c|-\log\left(1+\frac{(u(\rho)-a)^{2}}{\rho^{2}}\right)+o(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-c\log r+c\log 2-c\log|c|+o(1),\,\,\,\,\mbox{as $\rho\to+\infty$}.

By (
3.12
),

U(r)=-cr^{2}\log r+(c\log 2-c\log|c|-a)r^{2}+o(r^{2}),\,\,\,\,\mbox{ as $r\to 0$.}

It follows that
U(r)+cr^{2}\log r
is in
C^{2}([0,\rho))
and

\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\left(U^{\prime\prime}(r)+2c\log r\right)=2(c\log 2-c\log|c|-a)-3c.

Set

c=-\frac{\lambda}{4},\,\,\,\,a=\frac{\lambda}{4}\log\frac{|\lambda|}{8}+\frac{3\lambda}{8}-\frac{b}{2}.

Then the uniquely determined
\Sigma_{c,a}
is the graph of
U
.

\hfill\Box
