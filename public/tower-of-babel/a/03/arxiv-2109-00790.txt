Tracy–Widom method for Jánossy density and joint distribution of extremal eigenvalues of random matrices
\name \fname Shinsuke M. \surname Nishigaki∗

Abstract
The Jánossy density for a determinantal point process is the probability density that an interval I I contains exactly p p points except for those at k k designated loci. The Jánossy density associated with an integrable kernel 𝐊 ≐ ( φ ⁡ ( x ) ​ ψ ​ ( y ) − ψ ⁡ ( x ) ​ φ ​ ( y ) ) / ( x − y ) \mathbf{K}\doteq(\varphi(x)\psi(y)-\psi(x)\varphi(y))/(x-y) is shown to be expressed as a Fredholm determinant Det ⁡ ( 𝕀 − 𝐊 ~ | I ) \mathrm{Det}(\mathbb{I}-\tilde{\mathbf{K}}|_{I}) of a transformed kernel 𝐊 ~ ≐ ( φ ~ ​ ( x ) ​ ψ ~ ​ ( y ) − ψ ~ ​ ( x ) ​ φ ~ ​ ( y ) ) / ( x − y ) \tilde{\mathbf{K}}\doteq(\tilde{\varphi}(x)\tilde{\psi}(y)-\tilde{\psi}(x)\tilde{\varphi}(y))/(x-y) . We observe that 𝐊 ~ \tilde{\mathbf{K}} satisfies Tracy and Widom’s criteria if 𝐊 \mathbf{K} does, because of the structure that the map ( φ , ψ ) ↦ ( φ ~ , ψ ~ ) (\varphi,\psi)\mapsto(\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\psi}) is a meromorphic SL ⁡ ( 2 , ℝ ) \mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R}) gauge transformation between covariantly constant sections. This observation enables application of the Tracy–Widom method to Jánossy densities, expressed in terms of a solution to a system of differential equations in the endpoints of the interval. Our approach does not explicitly refer to isomonodromic systems associated with Painlevé equations employed in the preceding works. As illustrative examples we compute Jánossy densities with k = 1 , p = 0 k=1,p=0 for Airy and Bessel kernels, related to the joint distributions of the two largest eigenvalues of random Hermitian matrices and of the two smallest singular values of random complex matrices.

2
Jánossy density

First we collect some facts on determinantal point processes (DPPs).
Let
\mathfrak{X}
be a countable set.
Consider an ensemble of finite subsets of
\mathfrak{X}
consisting of
N
elements (“particles”)
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{N})
,
and assign to them a joint probability in a determinantal form:

\displaystyle P(n_{1},\ldots,n_{N})=\frac{1}{N!}\det\left[K(n_{i},n_{j})\right]_{i,j=1}^{N},\quad n_{i}\in\mathfrak{X}.

(2)

Here
\mathbf{K}=\left[K(n,m)\right]_{n,m\in\mathfrak{X}}
is an operator in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathfrak{X})
,
i.e., an infinite-dimensional matrix indexed by the points of
\mathfrak{X}
.
The operator

\mathbf{K}
,
which we also call a kernel, is required to be real, symmetric, projective, and normalized:

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{K}^{*}=\mathbf{K}^{t},\quad\mathbf{K}\cdot\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{K},\quad\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbf{K}=N.

(3)

These requirements lead to the
k
-point correlation function
\rho_{k}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
,
i.e., the joint probability that
k
particles occupy the points
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
,
to be given in a determinantal form as well:

\displaystyle\rho_{k}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})=\det\left[K(n_{i},n_{j})\right]_{i,j=1}^{k}:=\det{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}.

(4)

The Jánossy density
J_{k}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k};{I})

is defined as a probability that there is no particle in a subset

{I}\subset\mathfrak{X}
except for
k
particles, one at each of the
k
designated loci
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}

(see part (2) in Fig.
3
).

Figure 3:

(1)
N
particles distributed exclusively on
N
loci
n_{1},\ldots,n_{N}
in
\mathfrak{X}
.
(2) Exactly
k
particles in
{I}
, one at each of the
k
designated loci
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
in
{I}
.
(3)
k
particles, one at each of the
k
designated loci
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}

and other exactly
p
particles on
p
undesignated loci in
{I}
.

The restriction that
n_{k}\in I
could actually be lifted.
Using Eq. (
4
),
the Jánossy density is given in terms of the kernel restricted on
{I}
,

\mathbf{K}_{I}=\left[K(n,m)\right]_{n,m\in{I}}

(see, e.g., page 341 of Ref.
[
9
]
, where it is denoted as
\pi(X)
):

\displaystyle J_{k}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k};{I})

\displaystyle=\det(\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{K}_{I})\cdot\det\left[\langle n_{i}|\mathbf{K}_{I}(\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{K}_{I})^{-1}|n_{j}\rangle\right]_{i,j=1}^{k}.

(5)

We interchangeably use notations
A(n,m)=\left\langle n|\mathbf{A}|m\right\rangle
for the
(n,m)
-element of a matrix
\mathbf{A}
.
Now, making use of an identity

\det{\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}{\mathbf{A}}&{\mathbf{B}}\\
{\mathbf{C}}&{\mathbf{D}}\end{array}\right|}=\det{\mathbf{D}}\cdot\det\left({\mathbf{A}}-{\mathbf{C}}{\mathbf{D}}^{-1}{\mathbf{B}}\right)
repeatedly,
we present two alternative expressions for the Jánossy density
[
17
]
:

\displaystyle J_{k}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k};{I})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\det\left|\begin{array}[]{ll}\left[-K(n_{i},n_{j})\right]_{i,j=1,\ldots,k}&\left[-K(n,n_{j})\right]_{n\in{I};\;j=1,\ldots,k}\\
\left[-K(n_{i},m)\right]_{i=1,\ldots,k;\;m\in{I}}&\left[\delta_{nm}-K(n,m)\right]_{n,m\in{I}}\end{array}\right|

\displaystyle:=(-1)^{k}\det\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}-{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}&-\boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}\\
-\boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}^{t}&\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{K}_{I}\end{array}\right|

\displaystyle=\det{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\cdot\det\left(\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{K}_{I}+(\boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}^{t}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathit{k}})_{I}\right).

(10)

Equation (
10
) indicates that

the Jánossy density is a gap probability for a DPP with a transformed kernel

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{K}}=\mathbf{K}-\boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}^{t}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathit{k}},

(11)

multiplied by the
k
-point correlation function
\det{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}
.
The projectivity and the normalization conditions (
3
) with
N\mapsto N-k

can be verified for
\tilde{\mathbf{K}}

in a straightforward manner.
Note that

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{p}(m_{1},\ldots,m_{p};n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}):=\frac{\rho_{p+k}(m_{1},\ldots,m_{p},n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}{\rho_{k}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}=\det\left[\tilde{K}(m_{i},m_{j})\right]_{i,j=1}^{p}

(12)

represents the

joint probability that

particles occupy the points
m_{1},\ldots,m_{p}

under the presumption that
k
particles already occupy the points
n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}
.
Obviously, this fact could as well be deduced from the very definition of the conditional joint probabilities;
e.g., for
k=1
, the transformed kernel
{\displaystyle\tilde{K}(m,m^{\prime})=K(m,m^{\prime})-\frac{K(m,n)K(n,m^{\prime})}{K(n,n)}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{1}(m;n)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{2}(m,n)}{\rho_{1}(n)}=\frac{K(m,m)K(n,n)-K(m,n)K(n,m)}{K(n,n)}=\tilde{K}(m,m),

\displaystyle\tilde{\rho}_{2}(m_{1},m_{2};n)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{3}(m_{1},m_{2},n)}{\rho_{1}(n)}

(13)

\displaystyle=\frac{K(m_{1},m_{1})K(m_{2},m_{2})K(n,n)\pm(\mbox{5 terms})}{K(n,n)}=\det\left[\tilde{K}(m_{i},m_{j})\right]_{i,j=1}^{2},

etc.,
without any recourse to Jánossy densities.
We could have reversed the course of derivation of Jánossy densities (
10
)
and started backward from Eq. (
12
).

Generalization to the probability

that there are exactly

particles in
{I}

except for
k
particles, one at each of the
k

we introduce a parameter

is given by

\displaystyle J_{k,p}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k};{I})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p!}\left(-\partial_{{z}}\right)^{p}\det(\mathbb{I}-{z}\mathbf{K}_{I})\cdot\det\left[\langle n_{i}|\mathbf{K}_{I}(\mathbb{I}-{z}\mathbf{K}_{I})^{-1}|n_{j}\rangle\right]_{i,j=1}^{k}\bigg|_{{z}=1},

(14)

as the derivation of Eq. (
1

The product of two determinants in Eq. (
14

\displaystyle(-1)^{k}\det\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}-{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}&-\sqrt{{z}}\boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}\\
-\sqrt{{z}}\boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}^{t}&\mathbb{I}-{z}\mathbf{K}_{I}\end{array}\right|=\det{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\cdot\det\bigl(\mathbb{I}-{z}\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{I}\bigr).

Either by definition or from Eqs. (

) and (

),
the Jánossy density
J_{k,p}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k};{I})
reduces to:
(i) for

, the gap probability
E_{0}({I})=\det(\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{K}_{I})

(or its generalization

E_{p}({I})=1/p!\,\left(-\partial_{{z}}\right)^{p}\left.\det(\mathbb{I}-{z}\mathbf{K}_{I})\right|_{{z}=1}
)
of finding no (or exactly
p
) particles in
{I}
, and
(ii) formally for
{I}=\emptyset
and

, to the
k
-point correlation function (
4
).
Note also that the factor
\det{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}=\rho_{k}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})
in

) and (

)
is canceled when we consider the

probability
\tilde{J}_{k,p}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k};{I})

that there are exactly
p
particles in a subset

{I}
under the condition that
k
particles are already at each of the
k
designated loci,

\displaystyle\tilde{J}_{k,p}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k};{I})=\frac{1}{p!}\left.\left(-\partial_{{z}}\right)^{p}\det\bigl(\mathbb{I}-{z}\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{I}\bigr)\right|_{{z}=1}.

(17)

All the above formulas carry over to a continuous DPP on
\mathbb{R}

and for a set of intervals
I\subset\mathbb{R}
.
Trivial modifications are needed to regard
\mathbf{K}
and
\mathbf{K}_{I}
as integral operators

\displaystyle(\mathbf{K}\cdot f)(x)=\int dy\,K(x,y)f(y)~~,~~(\mathbf{K}_{I}\cdot f)(x)=\int_{I}dy\,K(x,y)f(y)

(18)

acting on the Hilbert space of square-integrable functions
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
L^{2}(I)
,
and to reinterpret joint probabilities
\rho_{k},\tilde{\rho}_{k},J_{k,p},\tilde{J}_{k,p}

as joint probability distributions.
For the case of continuous DPPs
the expression
\left\langle x|\mathbf{A}|y\right\rangle=A(x,y)
(denoted as
\mathbf{A}\doteq A(x,y)
in TW) means that
the integral operator
\mathbf{A}
has a kernel equal to
A(x,y)
.
Namely, the Jánossy density
J_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k};{I})

is defined as the probability density of finding exactly
k
particles in
{I}

and one at each of the
k
infinitesimal intervals
(x_{i},x_{i}+dx_{i})\subset{I}
,
and is given by the Fredholm determinant
{\rm Det\,}(\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{K}_{I})

times the (ordinary)
determinant of the resolvent kernel of
\mathbf{K}_{I}
:

\displaystyle J_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k};{I})

\displaystyle={\rm Det\,}(\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{K}_{I})\cdot\det\left[\left\langle x_{i}|\mathbf{K}_{I}(\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{K}_{I})^{-1}|x_{j}\right\rangle\right]_{i,j=1}^{k}

\displaystyle=\det{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\cdot{\rm Det\,}\bigl(\mathbb{I}-\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{I}\bigr)~~,~~\tilde{\mathbf{K}}=\mathbf{K}-\boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}^{t}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}.

(19)

Likewise its generalization
J_{k,p}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k};{I})
is given by

\displaystyle J_{k,p}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k};{I})=\det{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}\cdot\frac{1}{p!}\left.\left(-\partial_{{z}}\right)^{p}{\rm Det\,}\bigl(\mathbb{I}-{z}\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{I}\bigr)\right|_{{z}=1}.

(20)

Finally we note that the joint probability distribution of
k
leftmost or rightmost particles is derived
from the Jánossy density for a semi-finite interval
I=(s_{k},\infty)
or
(-\infty,s_{k})
as

\displaystyle P_{1\cdots k}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}~~\partial_{s_{k}}J_{k-1}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1};(s_{k},\infty))&(s_{1}\geq\cdots\geq s_{k})\\
-\partial_{s_{k}}J_{k-1}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k-1};(-\infty,s_{k}))&(s_{1}\leq\cdots\leq s_{k})\end{array}\right..

3
Applicability of the Tracy–Widom method

3.1
Inheritance of the Tracy–Widom criteria

Consider a kernel of an integral operator
\mathbf{K}
of the Christoffel–Darboux form

\displaystyle K(x,y)=\frac{\varphi(x)\psi(y)-\psi(x)\varphi(y)}{x-y},

(23)

with its component functions satisfying a pair of linear differential equations

\displaystyle m(x)\frac{d}{dx}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\varphi(x)\\
\psi(x)\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{rr}A(x)&B(x)\\
-C(x)&-A(x)\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\varphi(x)\\
\psi(x)\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle~\mbox{with some polynomials}\ m,A,B,C.

The tracelessness of the
2\times 2
matrix on the right-hand side of Eq. (
3.1
) is essential.
As a unifying approach to their preceding works on the sine
[
18
]
, Airy
[
3
]

and Bessel kernels
[
4
]
,
Tracy and Widom have shown in TW
that the Fredholm determinant
\mathrm{Det}(\mathbb{I}-\mathbf{K}_{I})
of an operator
\mathbf{K}

satisfying the criteria (
23
), (
3.1
) is always determined through a closed system of PDEs
in the boundary points
\{a_{i}\}\in\partial I
.
This involves the boundary values of the functions

Q_{j}(x)=((\mathbb{I}-\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{I})^{-1}\cdot x^{j}{\varphi})(x)
and

P_{j}(x)=((\mathbb{I}-\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{I})^{-1}\cdot x^{j}{\psi})(x)
,
and the inner products of
Q_{j}
and
P_{j}
with
\varphi
and
\psi
such as

u_{j}=\int_{I}dx\,\varphi(x)Q_{j}(x)
.
A large part of the TW system (Eqs. (1.7a)–(1.9) and (2.12)–(2.18) of TW) is universal and the rest
(Eqs. (2.25), (2.26) of TW) parametrically depends on the coefficients of the polynomials

m(x)=\sum_{j}\mu_{j}x^{j}
,
A(x)=\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}x^{j}
, etc.

Now we present a theorem:

Theorem 1.

If the kernel of
\mathbf{K}
satisfies the TW criteria (
23
), (
3.1
),
so does the transformed kernel of
\tilde{\mathbf{K}}
.

Proof.

Since the kernel of
\tilde{\mathbf{K}}^{(k)}

for the Jánossy density
J_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k};{I})
is obtained from the kernel of
\tilde{\mathbf{K}}^{(k-1)}
for
the Jánossy density
J_{k-1}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1};{I})
by adding an extra locus of particle
x_{k}=t
,

\displaystyle\tilde{K}^{(k)}(x,y)=\tilde{K}^{(k-1)}(x,y)-\tilde{K}^{(k-1)}(x,t)\tilde{K}^{(k-1)}(t,t)^{-1}\tilde{K}^{(k-1)}(t,y),

(30)

by induction it is sufficient to prove Theorem 1 for
k=1
.
Then the transformed kernel is

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(x,y)=K(x,y)-K(x,t)K(t,t)^{-1}K(t,y).

(31)

Here we assumed that the density of particles
\rho_{1}(t)=K(t,t)=\varphi^{\prime}(t)\psi(t)-\psi^{\prime}(t)\varphi(t)

at the designated locus
t
is nonzero
(otherwise the Jánossy density would vanish by definition).
The transformed kernel (
31
) is again of the Christoffel–Darboux form

\displaystyle\tilde{K}(x,y)=\frac{\tilde{\varphi}(x)\tilde{\psi}(y)-\tilde{\psi}(x)\tilde{\varphi}(y)}{x-y}~,

(32)

where

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{l}{\displaystyle\tilde{\varphi}(x)={\varphi}(x)-\frac{K(x,t)}{K(t,t)}{\varphi}(t)=\varphi(x)-\frac{b(a\varphi(x)-b\psi(x))}{x-t}}\\
{\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}(x)={\psi}(x)-\frac{K(x,t)}{K(t,t)}{\psi}(t)=\psi(x)-\frac{a(a\varphi(x)-b\psi(x))}{x-t}}\end{array}~~\mbox{with}~~\begin{array}[]{l}{\displaystyle a:=\frac{\psi(t)}{\sqrt{\rho_{1}(t)}}}\\
{\displaystyle b:=\frac{\varphi(t)}{\sqrt{\rho_{1}(t)}}}\end{array}.

Using Eq. (
3.1
), they are shown to satisfy a set of linear differential equations

\displaystyle m(x)\frac{d}{dx}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{\varphi}(x)\\
\tilde{\psi}(x)\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{rr}\tilde{A}(x)&\tilde{B}(x)\\
-\tilde{C}(x)&-\tilde{A}(x)\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\tilde{\varphi}(x)\\
\tilde{\psi}(x)\end{array}\right]

with

\displaystyle\tilde{A}(x)

\displaystyle={A}(x)+\frac{a^{2}{B}(x)-b^{2}{C}(x)}{x-t}-\frac{ab\left(2ab{A}(x)+a^{2}{B}(x)+b^{2}{C}(x)-m(x)\right)}{(x-t)^{2}},

\displaystyle\tilde{B}(x)

\displaystyle={B}(x)-\frac{2b(b{A}(x)+a{B}(x))}{x-t}+\frac{b^{2}\left(2ab{A}(x)+a^{2}{B}(x)+b^{2}{C}(x)-m(x)\right)}{(x-t)^{2}},

(43)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}(x)

\displaystyle={C}(x)+\frac{2a(a{A}(x)+b{C}(x))}{x-t}+\frac{a^{2}\left(2ab{A}(x)+a^{2}{B}(x)+b^{2}{C}(x)-m(x)\right)}{(x-t)^{2}}.

Since the coefficient functions

,
{A},{B}
and

are polynomials in

,
so are the new coefficient functions after redefinition

,

(x-t)^{2}\tilde{{A}}(x)\mapsto\tilde{{A}}(x)
, etc. ∎

a=\frac{\sqrt{t}\left(J_{\nu-1}(\sqrt{t})-J_{\nu+1}(\sqrt{t})\right)}{4\sqrt{\rho_{1}(t)}}~,~b=\frac{J_{\nu}(\sqrt{t})}{\sqrt{\rho_{1}(t)}}~,~\rho_{1}(t)=\frac{1}{4}\left(J_{\nu}(\sqrt{t}){}^{2}-J_{\nu-1}(\sqrt{t})J_{\nu+1}(\sqrt{t})\right).

The TW system of ODEs again takes the form (
67
), with the first two nonuniversal equations replaced by

\displaystyle s(s-t)^{2}{}\,q_{0}^{\prime}{}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{2}\left(\alpha_{j}+\sum_{k=0}^{1}\alpha_{j+k+1}v_{k}{}+\sum_{k=0}^{2}\gamma_{j+k+1}u_{k}{}\right)q_{j}{}+2t\,v_{0}q_{0}-2v_{1}q_{0}-v_{0}q_{1}~,

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=0}^{2}\left(\beta_{j}+\sum_{k=0}^{1}\alpha_{j+k+1}u_{k}{}+\sum_{k=0}^{1}\beta_{j+k+1}v_{k}{}\right)p_{j}{}-2t\,u_{0}p_{0}+2u_{1}p_{0}+u_{0}p_{1}~,

(75)

\displaystyle s(s-t)^{2}{}\,p_{0}^{\prime}{}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{3}\left(-\gamma_{j}+\sum_{k=0}^{1}\alpha_{j+k+1}w_{k}{}+\sum_{k=0}^{2}\gamma_{j+k+1}\tilde{v}_{k}{}\right)q_{j}{}+2t\,w_{0}q_{0}-2w_{1}q_{0}-w_{0}q_{1}~,

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=0}^{2}\left(-\alpha_{j}+\sum_{k=0}^{1}\alpha_{j+k+1}\tilde{v}_{k}{}+\sum_{k=0}^{1}\beta_{j+k+1}w_{k}{}\right)p_{j}{}-2t\,\tilde{v}_{0}p_{0}+2\tilde{v}_{1}p_{0}+\tilde{v}_{0}p_{1}~.

and the next eight universal equations sign-flipped:

\displaystyle u_{0}^{\prime}=q_{0}q_{0},u_{1}^{\prime}=q_{0}q_{1},u_{2}^{\prime}=q_{0}q_{2},v_{0}^{\prime}=q_{0}p_{0},v_{1}^{\prime}=q_{0}p_{1},v_{2}^{\prime}=q_{0}p_{2},w_{0}^{\prime}=p_{0}p_{0},w_{1}^{\prime}=p_{0}p_{1}.

(76)

Note that by setting the left endpoint
a_{1}
of
I
to
0

a_{1}
either vanish or decouple.
Accordingly all quantities are treated as functions of the right endpoint
s

alone, and their parametric dependence on
t
is implicit.
Boundary conditions for
s\ll 1
are:

\displaystyle q_{0}(s)

\displaystyle\simeq\tilde{\varphi}(s)~,~p_{0}(s)\simeq\tilde{\psi}(s)~,

\displaystyle u_{k}(s)

\displaystyle\simeq\int_{0}^{s}dx\,x^{k}\tilde{\varphi}(x)^{2}~,~v_{k}(s)\simeq\int_{0}^{s}dx\,x^{k}\tilde{\varphi}(x)\tilde{\psi}(x)~,~w_{k}(s)\simeq\int_{0}^{s}dx\,x^{k}\tilde{\psi}(x)^{2}~.

(77)

The diagonal resolvent and the Fredholm determinant

\displaystyle R(s)=-\partial_{s}\log\mathrm{Det}(\mathbb{I}-\tilde{\mathbf{K}}_{(0,s)})=p_{0}(s)q_{0}^{\prime}(s)-q_{0}(s)p_{0}^{\prime}(s).

5
Conclusion and perspectives

In this article we have shown that the TW method is applicable to the evaluation of
Jánossy densities and joint eigenvalue distributions for a kernel

\mathbf{K}\doteq(\varphi(x)\psi(y)-\psi(x)\varphi(y))/(x-y)

if it is applicable to the gap probability.
Essential to the inheritance of the TW criteria from

\mathbf{K}
to the transformed kernel

\tilde{\mathbf{K}}\doteq(\tilde{\varphi}(x)\tilde{\psi}(y)-\tilde{\psi}(x)\tilde{\varphi}(y))/(x-y)

is the structure that the map between the component functions

(\varphi,\psi)\mapsto(\tilde{\varphi},\tilde{\psi})

is an
\mathrm{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})
gauge transformation to a covariantly constant section
of an
\mathbb{R}^{2}
-bundle with an
\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{R})
connection

\mathcal{A}(x)=\frac{1}{m(x)}{\small\begin{bmatrix}~~A(x)\!\!\!&\!\!\!~~B(x)\\
-C(x)\!\!\!&\!\!\!-A(x)\end{bmatrix}}.

Our formulation generalizes the spirit of Ref.
[
16
]
,
which computed a special case of Jánossy density for the sine kernel by the TW method.
As the simplest examples we evaluated the joint distributions of the two extremal eigenvalues

P_{12}(t,s)
for the Airy and Bessel kernels by the TW method
and by the quadrature approximation as well, and confirmed their agreement to very high accuracies.
These results (Figs.
4
and
5
)
precisely fit the measured histograms from Gaussian-randomly generated matrices (Fig.
2
).

We list the pros and cons of our approach.
In contrast to the model-specific approaches in the preceeding works

[
12
,
13
,
15
,
16
]
, which computed only
the first-order Jánossy densities for the Bessel, Airy, and sine kernels,
our method is universally and systematically applicable to any kernel satisfying the TW criteria,
including but not limited to
q
-orthogonal, beyond-Airy, and various finite-
N
kernels,
and to any
k
th-order Jánossy densities.
In exchange, the intrinsic connection between our formulation and the
isomonodromic systems associated with Painlevé transcendents and integrability in these works
is completely obscured.
Our approach is not well suited for asymptotic analysis for
|t-s|\gg 1
or
|t-s|\ll 1
, either.

Finally we comment on possible extensions and physical applications of our approach.

•

The joint distribution
P_{1\cdots k}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
of the first
k
extremal eigenvalues
is trivially obtained by repeating the procedure (
30
)
(k-1)
times,
which increases the order of the polynomials
\tilde{A}(x)
, etc. by
2(k-1)
.
The joint distribution
P_{p_{1}\cdots p_{\ell}}(s_{p_{1}},\ldots,s_{p_{\ell}})
of
the
p_{1}\mathrm{th},\ldots,p_{\ell}
th extremal eigenvalues follows from
P_{1\cdots k}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})

by integrating out
k-\ell
eigenvalues in an ordered cell, such as

P_{13}(s_{1},s_{3})=\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{3}}ds_{2}\,P_{123}(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})
.

•

For the applicability of the TW method to inherit from
\mathbf{K}
to
\tilde{\mathbf{K}}
,
the requirement of the Chrsitoffel–Darboux form (
23
),
characteristic of
\mathrm{U}(N)
invariant ensembles, can actually be relaxed to
more generic, asymmetric kernels of
the integrable class
[
25
]
:

\displaystyle K(x,y)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{r}\frac{f_{\ell}(x)g_{\ell}(y)}{x-y}:=\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}(x)\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathit{g}}(y)}{x-y}~~,~~\boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}(x)\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathit{g}}(x)=0.

(80)

Here
r
-component real functions

\boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}(x)=\left(f_{1}(x),\ldots,f_{r}(x)\right)^{t}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathit{g}}(y)=\left(g_{1}(y),\ldots,g_{r}(y)\right)^{t}

are covariantly constant sections for some meromorphic
\mathfrak{sl}(r,\mathbb{R})
connections

\mathcal{A}(x)
and
\mathcal{B}(y)
,
respectively.
In this generalized case,
an
\mathrm{SL}(r,\mathbb{R})
gauge transformation on them,

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{ll}{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}(x)\mapsto\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}}(x)=\boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}(x)-\frac{K(x,t)}{K(t,t)}\boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}(t)=\mathcal{U}(x)\boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}(x)}\\
{\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathit{g}}(y)\mapsto\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathit{g}}}(y)=\boldsymbol{\mathit{g}}(y)-\frac{K(t,y)}{K(t,t)}\boldsymbol{\mathit{g}}(t)=\mathcal{U}(y)^{-1\,t}\boldsymbol{\mathit{g}}(y)}\end{array},~~\mathcal{U}(x)=\mathbb{I}-\frac{\boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}(t)\boldsymbol{\mathit{g}}(t)^{t}}{\rho_{1}(t)(x-t)}

maps
K(x,y)
to
\tilde{K}(x,y)=\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}}(x)\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathit{g}}}(y)/(x-y)

while retaining
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathit{f}}}(x)\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathit{g}}}(x)=0.

An example of a kernel of type (
80
) is the Pearcey kernel (with
r=3
) governing
spectral correlations of random matrices in an external source,

H=H_{\mathrm{GUE}}+c\,\mathrm{diag}(\mathbb{I}_{N/2},-\mathbb{I}_{N/2})

in the critical regime where a gap in the eigenvalue support closes at the origin
[
26
]
.
This ensemble schematically models the QCD Dirac operator at finite temperature

[
27
]
.
Application of our strategy to its Jánossy density
by the generalized TW method
[
28
]
will be reported in a separate publication.

•

Ensembles of Dirichlet

L
-functions are acknowledged as ideal quantum-chaotic systems for
their distributions of zeroes on the critical line
[
29
,
30
]
.
It is well anticipated but worth verifying
that the joint distributions of the two smallest zeroes of

L
-functions are described by Jánossy densities for the Bessel kernels (
79
)
at
\nu=\pm 1/2
, depending on the sign in the functional equation
of the
L
-functions.

•

In the context of noncritical string theory,
conditioning the loci of some (
k
) of
N
eigenvalues of matrix models at (multi)criticality
(i.e., beyond Airy)
outside their main support has been interpreted as introducing
k
ZZ branes to the Liouville theory

[
31
,
32
]
.
As all efforts have been concentrated on extracting
leading
nonperturbative corrections to the free energy
in the large-
N
limit, it is worthwhile to apply our analytic strategy for computing
the Jánossy density
J_{k}(\{x\};I)
to those models and obtain unapproximated,
fully nonperturbative free energy that incorporates
all
D-brane contributions.

•

It seems less promising to extend our strategy to Jánossy densities of
quaternion kernels
[
11
]
governing orthogonal and symplectic ensembles
[
33
]
,
or transitive ensembles interpolating different symmetry classes.
Nevertheless, the observation that the Jánossy density for these cases
is expressed as a Fredholm Pfaffian of the transformed quaternion kernel

[
34
,
35
]
,

\mathrm{Det}\left(\mathbb{I}-(\mathbf{K}-\boldsymbol{\mathit{k}}^{t}{\boldsymbol{\kappa}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathit{k}})_{I}\right)^{1/2}
,
always permits numerical evaluation by the quadrature approximation.
Currently we are exploring
the application of this strategy to
the quaternion kernel of the chGSE-chGUE transitive ensemble
[
36
]
,
to obtain individual distributions of the staggered Dirac operator of two-color QCD
at finite baryon-number chemical potential
\mu
and
with dynamical quarks of masses

m_{f}

introduced as the conditioned eigenvalues
x_{f}=-m_{f}^{2}
,
extending our previous work
[
6
]
on the quenched case.
