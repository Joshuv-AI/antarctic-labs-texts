Laplace Power-expected-posterior priors for generalized linear models with applications to logistic regression


Abstract
Power-expected-posterior (PEP) methodology, which borrows ideas from the literature on power priors, expected-posterior priors and unit information priors, provides a systematic way to construct objective priors. The basic idea is to use imaginary training samples to update a noninformative prior into a minimally-informative prior. In this work, we develop a novel definition of PEP priors for generalized linear models that relies on a Laplace expansion of the likelihood of the imaginary training sample. This approach has various computational, practical and theoretical advantages over previous proposals for non-informative priors for generalized linear models. We place a special emphasis on logistic regression models, where sample separation presents particular challenges to alternative methodologies. We investigate both asymptotic and finite-sample properties of the procedures, showing that is both asymptotic and intrinsic consistent, and that its performance is at least competitive and, in some settings, superior to that of alternative approaches in the literature.

Introduction

Generalized linear models (GLMs, e.g., see
McCullagh & Nelder 2019
) are one of the main workhorses of statistical analysis. Indeed, GLMs are widely used both to model data directly and as building blocks for more complex hierarchical models. However, in spite of their broad adoption, prior elicitation for general GLMs in the absence of subjective information remains an open problem, particularly in settings where the main goal is variable selection. Because standard non-informative priors for GLMs that work well for parameter estimation are often improper, they cannot be used in model selection problems because they typically lead to ill-defined Bayes factors (e.g., see
Berger et al. 2001
).

Within the subclass of Gaussian linear models, the literature on so-called “objective” or “default” priors is extensive. Examples include point-mass spike-&-slab priors
(
Mitchell & Beauchamp 1988
;
Geweke 1996
)
,
g
-priors
(
Zellner 1986
)
, mixtures of
g
-priors
(
Zellner & Siow 1980
;
Liang et al. 2008
)
, unit information priors
(
Kass & Wasserman 1995
)
, intrinsic Bayes factors
(
Berger & Pericchi 1996a
)
, fractional Bayes factors
(
O’Hagan 1995
;
De Santis & Spezzaferri 2001
)
, non-local priors
(
Johnson & Rossell 2010
;
Johnson & Rossell 2012
)
and power-expected-posterior priors
(
Fouskakis et al. 2015
)
, among other approaches. See
Bayarri et al. 2012
for a review and a discussion of desirable properties. The literature on default priors for GLMs is more limited, with three main approaches dominating. These include those introduced by
Bové et al. 2011
and
Li & Clyde 2018
, both of which consider modifications of mixtures of g-priors that are suitable for GLMs, and
Fouskakis et al. 2018
, who considers extensions of power-expected-posterior priors that relies on unnormalized power likelihoods. One feature shared by all three approaches is that they can be thought of as being based on the idea of calibrating (possibly improper) priors using either real or imaginary training samples (e.g., see
Berger & Pericchi 1996b
and
Pérez & Berger 2002
).

In this paper we introduce a variant of the power-expected-posterior (PEP) prior for GLMs that we call the Laplace PEP, or LPEP. While the formulation is general, this manuscript emphasizes the development of the LPEP for logistic regression models. This is because this subclass of models provides the best illustration of the theoretical and practical advantages of our approach over existing ones. For example, we note that the prior described in
Li & Clyde 2018
is improper when the maximum likelihood estimator of the regression coefficients under the observed data does not exist. In the case of logistic regression, this happens when there is separation among the groups (e.g., see
Albert & Anderson 1984
,
Lesaffre & Albert 1989
and
Heinze & Schemper 2002
). Separation is reasonably common in practical applications, especially in problems with relatively small samples and several unbalanced and highly predictive risk factors (e.g., see Section
6.1
). A similar issue arises with the PEP priors introduced in
Fouskakis et al. 2018
since the imaginary training samples are not restricted to yield finite maximum likelihood estimators. Furthermore, both versions of the PEP prior proposed by
Fouskakis et al. 2018
are computationally intractable, requiring the use of reversible Jump Markov Chain Monte Carlo algorithms
(
Green 1995
;
Dellaportas et al. 2002
)
. Our LPEP is carefully designed to deal with separation issues, and it is well defined as long as at least one training sample exists that yields finite maximum likelihood estimates under the full model. Furthermore, the LPEP can be written as a location-and-scale mixture of Gaussian priors and is therefore computationally tractable and easy to incorporate into standard Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithms that rely on data augmentation (e.g.,
Polson et al. 2013
). Finally, the mixture structure also simplifies the theoretical study of the prior, allowing us to show that the procedure is both asymptotically consistent and intrinsically consistent.

It is important to stress that the focus of this manuscript is on priors for variable selection that place positive probability on specific coefficients being exactly zero. An alternative approach is to use continuous shrinkage priors. Examples include the Bayesian Lasso
(
Park & Casella 2008
)
, the Horseshoe prior
(
Carvalho et al. 2010
)
, the Normal-Gamma prior
(
Brown & Griffin 2010
)
, the Dirichlet-Laplace prior
(
Bhattacharya et al. 2015
)
, global-local shrinkage priors
(
Polson & Scott 2012
)
, the Beta-prime prior
(
Bai & Ghosh 2018
)
, the tail-adaptive shrinkage prior
(
Lee et al. 2020
)
and the Horseshoe-pit prior
(
Denti et al. 2021
)
. Continuous shrinkage priors tend to have computational advantages and are very effective in predictive settings. However, because they place probability zero on any one value of the parameter space, variable selection can be performed only by thresholding the posterior distributions of the model coefficients. While ad-hoc techniques have been devised for this purpose (e.g., see
Li & Pati 2017
), thresholding tends to work well mostly in settings where enough prior information is available to establish practical significance.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section
2
reviews the general definition and properties of PEP priors. Section
3
defines the general LPEP and conditions under which it is well defined. Section
4
discusses in detail the special case of the LPEP for logistic regression, including theoretical properties such as intrinsic and model selection consistency and computational strategies. Sections
5
and
6
presents empirical results from simulation studies as well as three different real datasets. Finally, Section
7
discusses our results and future directions for research.

2
Power-expected-posterior priors: A brief review

Power-expected-posterior (PEP) priors
(
Fouskakis et al. 2015
)
extend the expected-posterior (EP) priors introduced by
Pérez & Berger 2002
by controlling the amount of information contained in the prior using the prior power approach originally developed by
Ibrahim et al. 2000
and
Chen et al. 2000
in the context of subjective priors based on historical information.

Briefly, let
{\boldsymbol{y}}
denote the
n
-dimensional vector containing the observed data,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
index the model space, and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
represent vector of parameters under model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. We start with a (potentially improper) prior
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{N}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
under model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and introduce an
n^{*}
-dimensional vector of
imaginary
training samples arising from a distribution
m^{*}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
. The EP prior is then constructed as

\displaystyle\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{EP}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=\int\frac{f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)}{\int f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}m^{*}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})d{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}

In words, the EP priors use the imaginary training sample
\mathbf{y}^{*}
to update the original prior
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{N}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
, and addresses the possible effect of using any particular training sample by averaging over the distribution
m^{*}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
. The use of a common
m^{*}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})
properly calibrates the priors across the different values of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, even in situations where
m^{*}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})
is improper.
Pérez & Berger 2002
discuss various possible choices of
m^{*}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})
in both informative and non-informative settings.

Note that an implicit assumption in the formulation of the PEP is that the training sample
\mathbf{y}^{*}
must be large enough so that the posterior based on it is proper, i.e.,

\displaystyle\int f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}<\infty

(1)

for any
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
in the support of
m^{*}
. However, large the values of
n^{*}
will produce priors that are relatively concentrated. To balance these two goals, it is common to choose
n^{*}
as the size of the minimum training sample required to satisfy (
1
) across all models. This complicates the implementation of this prior in situations where data is not independent and identically distributed, such as in regression models.

In summary, even though the EP prior attempts to ameliorate the effect of the
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
by averaging over
m^{*}
and by using training samples that are as small as possible, in some applications the prior might be quite concentrated, and therefore highly informative. Power-expected-posterior priors
(
Fouskakis et al. 2015
)
address this drawback by scaling the likelihood of the imaginary sample,

\displaystyle\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{PEP}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=\int\frac{\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},{\delta}\right)\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)}{\int\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},{\delta}\right)\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}m^{*}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\delta)f(\delta\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma})d\delta d{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}

where
\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},{\delta}\right)=\frac{f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}}{\int f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}
is the normalized power likelihood for the training sample
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
based on model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, and
\delta
is the power parameter. If
\delta=1
, then PEP prior reduces to the EP prior, while values of
\delta>1
yield priors with a larger variance (and therefore, less information) than the EP prior. A particularly appealing choice is
\delta=n^{*}
(or, alternatively, a prior on
\delta
that is concentrated around
n^{*}
), which leads to a prior that can be considered as being unit information
(
Kass & Wasserman 1995
)
. Note that
\delta
plays a similar role to the
g
parameter involved in the definition of (mixtures of)
g
priors, so that treating
\delta
as random will typically lead to priors that have heavier tails, and are therefore more robust (in the sense of
Dawid 1973
and
Andrade & O’Hagan 2011
).

Being able to use the parameter
\delta
to control the amount of information contained in the prior means that the choice of the size of training sample is less critical in the case of PEP priors. In the sequel, we work with
n^{*}=n
, a choice that is particularly convenient when dealing with GLMs and other regression models. Indeed, taking
n^{*}=n
allows us to select
{\boldsymbol{X}}^{*}
, the design matrix associated with the training sample
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
, as
{\boldsymbol{X}}^{*}={\boldsymbol{X}}
, the design matrix associated with the observed data. A further implicit assumption moving forward is that
n>p
.

The PEP prior was originally derived for model selection in Gaussian linear model. In that case, computing the normalizing constant
\int f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
associated with
\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},{\delta}\right)
is straightforward. Indeed, for most standard choices of
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)
, the induced PEP can be written as a location-and-scale mixture of Gaussian distributions, dramatically simplifying computation within a Markov chain Monte Carlo framework. This property, however, does not extend to other GLMs. To address this issue,
Fouskakis et al. 2018
introduce two slightly different modifications of the PEP framework that rely on the unnormalized power likelihood
f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\delta}}
rather than
\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},{\delta}\right)
: the concentrated reference PEP (CRPEP) and the diffuse reference PEP (DRPEP). However, while the use of the unnormalized power likelihood avoids some of the computational difficulties associated with the original PEP prior, many of them remain. In particular, neither
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{CRPEP}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
nor
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{DRPEP}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
belong to standard families of distributions. This prevents closed-form integration of the regression coefficients and therefore requires the use Reversible Jump Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithms. Furthermore, the definition of the CRPEP and the DRPEP and the computational approach introduced by the authors (which relies on Laplace approximations to compute certain normalizing constants needed for the acceptance probabilities of various Metropolis-Hastings steps) implicitly assume that the maximum likelihood estimate of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
exists for any training sample
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
and model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. However, this constraint, which as we discussed in the introduction might be binding for some classes of GLMs, is not accounted for in the definition of the
m^{*}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\delta)
CRPEP and DRPEP.

3
The Laplace power-expected-posterior prior for Generalized Linear Models

Instead of working with the unnormalized power likelihood as in
Fouskakis et al. 2018
, in this paper we propose replacing the likelihood of the imaginary samples with its Laplace approximation
before
raising it to the power
1/\delta
. Hence, the name Laplace PEP, or LPEP. More concretely, let the observations
{\boldsymbol{y}}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})^{T}
be generated from a likelihood of the form

f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}h(y_{i},\tau)\exp\left\{\frac{T(y_{i})G\left(\eta\left({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\gamma}\right)\right)+A\left(\eta\left({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\gamma}\right)\right)}{d(\tau)}\right\}

where
E(y_{i})=\eta({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\gamma})=\theta_{i}
for some appropriate link function
\eta
,
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}=(1,x_{i,1},\dots,x_{i,p})
is the
p+1
dimensional vector of regressors associated with observation
y_{i}
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in S
is the
p+1
dimensional vector of regression coefficients (including the intercept),
S
is a connected open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{p+1}
,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{\prime}=(\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{p})
is a binary vector of length
p+1
such that for all
j\in\{1,\dots,p\},\gamma_{j}=1
if the
j
-th variable is included in the model (i.e., if
\beta_{j}
is different from zero) and
\gamma_{j}=0
otherwise and
\gamma_{0}=1
(i.e., intercept is always included in the model),
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\gamma}
denote the sub-vectors of
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
with length
p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1
where
p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\gamma_{j}
that include only those components for which the corresponding
\gamma_{j}
is equal to 1, and
\tau
is an overdispersion parameter. In order to simplify our exposition, and following standard practice in the literature, in the sequel we treat
\tau
as known. The generalization of our approach to situations where
\tau
is unknown is relatively straightforward as
\tau
will typically be a parameter that is common to all models under consideration, and can therefore be safely assigned a standard (potentially improper) non-informative prior
(
Berger et al. 1998
)
. When
\tau
is known, the normalizing constant
h(y_{i},\tau)
can be dropped, and the scaling function
d(\tau)
can be absorbed within the function
T
and
A
, leading to the somewhat simpler expression:

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[T^{*}(y_{i})G\left(\eta\left({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\gamma}\right)\right)+A^{*}\left(\eta\left({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\gamma}\right)\right)\right]\right\},

(2)

where
A^{*}(\cdot)=A(\cdot)/d(\tau)
and
T^{*}(\cdot)=T(\cdot)/d(\tau)
.

The second order Laplace approximation to (
2
) is given
by

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)

\displaystyle\approx f^{L}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)\propto\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\gamma}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\gamma}({\boldsymbol{y}})\right)^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}})\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\gamma}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\gamma}({\boldsymbol{y}})\right)\right\},

(3)

where
\tilde{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[T^{*}(y_{i})G\left(\eta\left({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\gamma}\right)\right)+A^{*}\left(\eta\left({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\gamma}\right)\right)\right]
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}})
denotes the maximum likelihood estimate for
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
based on sample
{\boldsymbol{y}}
, and
{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}})
is the
(p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1)\times(p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1)
observed information matrix with entries

[{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}})]_{j,j^{\prime}}=-\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},j}\partial\beta_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},j^{\prime}}}\tilde{\ell}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\right|_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\mathbf{y})}.

In the case of regular exponential families, it is well known that this approximation is accurate up to an
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{n})
order term (e.g., see
Schwarz 1978
and
Haughton 1988
). With this in mind, we define the LPEP as

\displaystyle\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{LPEP}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=\int\frac{\tilde{f}^{L}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},{\delta}\right)\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)}{\int\tilde{f}^{L}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},{\delta}\right)\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}m^{*}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid{\boldsymbol{X}})f(\delta\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma})d\delta d{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},

(4)

where
{\boldsymbol{X}}
is the
n\times(p+1)
design matrix whose rows correspond to the
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{\prime}
vectors.

Defining the LPEP using (
3
) instead of (
2
) dramatically simplifies computation. Indeed, using (
3
) implies that
\tilde{f}^{L}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},{\delta}\right)
is proportional to a Gaussian kernel,

\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},{\delta}\right)\propto\delta^{-\frac{p_{\gamma}+1}{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\delta}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\gamma}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\gamma}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})\right)^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\gamma}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\gamma}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})\right)\right\},

and, therefore, for standard choices of
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)
(such as the flat prior
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)\propto 1
),
\pi^{LPEP}
corresponds to a location-and-scale mixture of Gaussian distributions.

Besides using the the Laplace approximation of
f({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
, the definition in (
4
) differs from that in
Fouskakis et al. 2018
in terms of the structure of the distribution of the imaginary samples
m^{*}
in two important ways. First, note that we
do not
make
m^{*}
dependent of the scaling factor
\delta
. This makes intuitive sense (there is no obvious reason why the power factor used to re-scale the information in the training sample should also affect how the training sample is generated) and simplifies both posterior computation and theoretical analysis. On the other hand,
m^{*}
is made to depend explicitly on the design matrix
{\boldsymbol{X}}
. In particular, we take

\displaystyle m^{*}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid{\boldsymbol{X}})

\displaystyle\propto\tilde{m}^{*}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid{\boldsymbol{X}})\mathbf{1}\left(\mathbf{y}^{*}\in A({\boldsymbol{X}})\right),

(5)

where
\mathbf{1}\left(\mathbf{y}^{*}\in A({\boldsymbol{X}})\right)
is the indicator function on the set
A({\boldsymbol{X}})
and

\displaystyle A({\boldsymbol{X}})

\displaystyle=\left\{\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left(\tilde{{\boldsymbol{y}}}\right)\mbox{ exists and is finite for all }\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right\}.

(6)

At first sight, the computational implementation of (
6
) might seem daunting, as it in principle requires that the existence of the maximum likelihood estimator of the parameters be checked for every possible model under consideration. However, as the following theorem shows, for a broad class of GLMs that includes binomial and multinomial models with logistic and probit links, as well as Poisson models with the canonical logarithmic link, it is enough to check relatively simple conditions on the loglikelihood funciton for the full model.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
denote the log-likelihood function of the GLM in (
2
) associated with model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}=(1,1,\ldots,1)
denote the full model (i.e., the model that includes all potential regressors).
Assume that

(i)

\ell_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}})
is continuous and striclty concave on
S_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}}

(ii)

\lim_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}}\to\partial S_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}})=-\infty
, where
\partial S_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}}
represents the closure of
S_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}}

Then,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}})
, the maximum likelihood estimator, exists under any other model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.

The proof of Theorem
3.1
, which is relatively straightforward, can be seen in Appendix
A
. Similar theorems covering wider classes of GLMs can be derived using the results in
Wedderburn 1976
, but this version suffices for our purposes.

To conclude, it might be helpful to expand on the relationship between the LPEP prior as we defined it in this Section and other similar proposals in the literature within the context of Gaussian linear models. Note that, while the Laplace approximation in (
3
) is exact for the Gaussian linear model, our definition of the LPEP is not equivalent to the PEP prior in
Fouskakis et al. 2015
because the predictive distribution
m^{*}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})
used the generate the training samples does not depend on
\delta
. On the other hand, the LPEP for the Gaussian linear model looks similar to a mixture of
g
priors
Liang et al. 2008
. In particular, while the covariance matrix of the Gaussian kernels involved is given by
\delta\left\{{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{X}}\right\}^{-1}
in both priors, the conditional mean of the LPEP depends on the training sample and is not zero. However, depending on the choice of
m^{*}(\mathbf{y}^{*})
, the intrinsic prior associated with the LPEP does correspond to a mixture of
g
priors (please see Section
4.1.3
).

4
The LPEP prior for logistic regression

In the sequel, we illustrate the LPEP in the context of logistic regression, where the likelihood function for model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
can be written as

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=\exp\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\log(1+\exp\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\})\right]\right\},

(7)

and
S_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\mathbb{R}^{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1}
. A natural choice for
\pi^{N}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
in this setting is the (improper) flat prior
\pi^{N}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\propto 1
, so that

\displaystyle\pi^{LPEP}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\in\{0,1\}^{n}}\Bigg[\int\phi_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}),\delta{\boldsymbol{H}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\right)\right)f(\delta\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma})d\delta\Bigg]m^{*}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid{\boldsymbol{X}}),

(8)

where
\phi_{p}\left(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)
denotes the density of the
p
-variate normal distribution with mean vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})
is the maximum likelihood estimator of
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
based on the training sample
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
(which, while not available in closed form, can be easily evaluated),

{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\hat{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})(1-\hat{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}))}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}^{\prime}={\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}){\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},

where

\hat{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})=\frac{1}{1+\exp\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})\right\}},

and

{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})=\diag\left\{\frac{1}{\hat{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},1}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})(1-\hat{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},1}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}))},\ldots,\frac{1}{\hat{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},n}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})(1-\hat{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},n}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}))}\right\}.

Note that the loglikelihood function
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\log\left(1+\exp\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}\right)\right]
associated with (
7
) is continuous everywhere for any model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
(and, in particular, for
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}
). Furthermore, as long as the full design matrix
{\boldsymbol{X}}
is full rank,
{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})
is strictly positive definite for any
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. Hence, the model satisfies condition (i) in
Theorem
3.1
. To verify that condition (ii) is satisfied, it is enough to show that the training sample
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
is not separable under the full design matrix
{\boldsymbol{X}}
.
Konis 2007
discusses an efficient approach to detect separation in logistic regression models that relies on linear programming. This approach has been implemented in the
R
package
detectseparation

(
Kosmidis & Schumacher 2020
)
.

We discuss next the choice of
\tilde{m}^{*}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})
in (
5
). A common approach is to select
m^{*}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})
as the predictive under the the null model. In that spirit, for the logistic regression model (and, more generally, for any binary regression model, independently of the link function used) we set

\displaystyle\tilde{m}^{*}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{*}+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(n-\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{*}+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle=\int t^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{*}}(1-t)^{n-\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}^{*}}\,\frac{t^{-\frac{1}{2}}(1-t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)}dt,

a Beta-Binomial distribution with both parameters equal to
\frac{1}{2}
. This choice is particularly appealing because it corresponds to the predictive distribution under the null model and its reference/Jeffreys prior.

To complete the specification of the LPEP we must specify the mixing distribution for the exponent
\delta
. In this manuscript we consider three alternatives. The first version of the LPEP we investigate is the unit information LPEP (UI-LPEP) obtained by fixing
\delta=n^{*}
. We also consider a version of the hyper-g/n prior discussed in
Liang et al. 2008
and
Li & Clyde 2018
,

\displaystyle f^{HGN}(\delta)

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{\delta}{n^{*}}\right)^{-2}.

We call this the HGN-LPEP The median of the hyper-g/n prior is equal to
n^{*}
, and the prior places much of its mass around this value. It can therefore be considered as a relaxation of the unit information version of the prior. Note that, under this hyperprior,
\delta
is independent of the model under consideration.

Finally, we consider a version of the robust prior recommended by
Bayarri et al. 2012
,

\displaystyle f^{R}(\delta|\boldsymbol{\gamma})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1)^{1/2}}\frac{(n^{*}+1)^{1/2}}{(\delta+1)^{3/2}}\mathbf{1}\left(\delta>\frac{n^{*}-p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1}\right),

which we call the R-LPEP The robust prior satisfies a number of compelling desiderata for Gaussian linear regression models. For example, its expectation is
\mathcal{O}(n^{*})
. However, note that, unlike our previous two choices, this prior depends on the model size.

4.1
Properties of the LPEP prior for logistic regression

4.1.1
Proper prior

Note that our choice of
m^{*}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid{\boldsymbol{X}}\right)
is proper, and that because of the constraint on the value of the training samples,

\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},\delta\right)=\frac{\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},{\delta}\right)\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)}{\int\tilde{f}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},{\delta}\right)\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}

is also proper for every
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. Therefore, the LPEP prior in (
8
) is also proper for every
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.

4.1.2
Tail behavior

It straightforward to see that the unit information version of the LPEP (where
\delta=n^{*}
),
\pi^{LPEP-UI}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
has Gaussian tails. On the other hand, as the following theorem shows, the hyper-g/n and the robust versions of the LPEP have heavier (polynomial) tails in every direction.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let

\zeta^{HGN}(s\mid{\boldsymbol{v}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\left.\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{LPEP-HGN}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\right|_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=s{\boldsymbol{v}}}

and

\zeta^{R}(s\mid{\boldsymbol{v}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})=\left.\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{LPEP-R}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\right|_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=s{\boldsymbol{v}}}

for any vector
{\boldsymbol{v}}
such that
\|{\boldsymbol{v}}\|=1
. Then there exist functions
c^{HGN}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{v}})
and
c^{R}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{v}})
such that

\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{\zeta^{HGN}(s\mid{\boldsymbol{v}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})}{\left(1+s^{2}/(p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1)\right)^{-\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+2}{2}}}=c^{HGN}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{v}})<\infty

and

\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{\zeta^{R}(s\mid{\boldsymbol{v}},\boldsymbol{\gamma})}{\left(1+s^{2}/(p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1)\right)^{-\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+2}{2}}}=c^{R}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{v}})<\infty

for every direction
{\boldsymbol{v}}
and model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.

The proof, which is presented in Appendix
B
, extends results originally presented in
Bayarri et al. 2012
.

One important consequence of this result is that, from an estimation (rather than model selection) perspective,
\pi^{LPEP-HGN}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
and
\pi^{LPEP-R}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
are robust, in the sense of having bounded influence in the case of likelihood-prior conflict (e.g., see
Andrade & O’Hagan 2006
and
Andrade & O’Hagan 2011
).

4.1.3
Intrinsic consistency

In addition to being proper, under mild conditions the LPEP converges to a non-degenerate prior as the size of the training sample increases. Naturally, the exact form of the intrinsic prior depends on the asymptotic regime for the covariates associated with new observations, as well as the exact prior used for
\delta
. Theorem
4.2
below provides a relevant example.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume that, as
n
(and therefore,
n^{*}
) grows, the covariate vectors
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots
satisfy either of the following two conditions:

(i)

If
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots
forms a deterministic sequence, then
\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{X}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
.

(ii)

If
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{2},\ldots
are random, then they are independent and identically distributed from a distribution with mean
\mathbf{0}
and finite covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
.

Then, the unit information (
\delta=n^{*}
), hyper-g/n and robust versions of the LPEP have proper, non-degenerate intrinsic priors of the form

\displaystyle\lim_{n^{*}\to\infty}\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{LPEP-UI}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=\phi_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\mathbf{0},4\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right]^{-1}\right),

\displaystyle\lim_{n^{*}\to\infty}\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{LPEP-HGN}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=\int\phi_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\mathbf{0},4\delta^{*}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right]^{-1}\right)\left(1+\delta^{*}\right)^{-2}d\delta^{*},

and

\displaystyle\lim_{n^{*}\to\infty}\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{LPEP-R}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=\int\phi_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\mathbf{0},4\delta^{*}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right]^{-1}\right)\frac{1}{2(p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1)^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left(\frac{1}{\delta^{*}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\mathbf{1}\left(\delta^{*}>\frac{1}{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1}\right)d\delta^{*},

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
denotes the square submatrix of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
that includes only the rows and columns for which
\gamma_{j}=1
.

A proof of this result (versions of which have been discussed in
Li & Clyde 2018
) can be seen in Appendix
C
. Interestingly, we note that these are the same intrinsic priors associated with the prior in
Bové et al. 2011
under the same asymptotic regime for
{\boldsymbol{X}}
.

4.1.4
Model selection consistency

Model selection consistency refers to the ability of the procedure to choose the correct model as
n\to\infty
. Intuitively, because the amount of information in
\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{LPEP}
is kept approximately constant as
n^{*}
increases, we would expect that the associated Bayes factors would behave asymptotically like those computed from the Bayesian Information Criteria, which have been well studied and are known to be consistent. The following theorem, a proof of which can be seen in Appendix
D
, formalizes that intuition for the unit information prior.

Theorem 4.3
.

Assume that a sequence of observations
y_{1},y_{2},\ldots
is generated from some model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}\in\{1\}\times\{0,1\}^{p}
(i.e., one of the models considered by our procedure), and that
p
is fixed. For the unit information LPEP with
g=n
, and under appropriate regularity conditions for how the covariate vectors
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},1},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},2},\ldots
are generated, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathsf{Pr}^{LPEP}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}\mid{\boldsymbol{y}})=1.

4.1.5
Information consistency

Information consistency refers to the behavior of the model selection criteria for fixed sample
n
, as the observed sample
{\boldsymbol{y}}
becomes increasingly more “extreme”. In the case of logistic regression, because the sample space for
{\boldsymbol{y}}
is finite for any
n
, traditional issues of information inconsistency do not arise.

4.2
Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling

The LPEP prior can be easily combined with the Polya-Gamma augmentation of
Polson et al. 2013
to generate an efficient Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithm for variable selection in logistic regression. For this purpose it is convenient to re-express (
4
) as a hierarchical prior where

\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},\delta,\boldsymbol{\gamma}\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}),\delta\left\{{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})\right\}^{-1}\right),

with
y^{*}\sim m^{*}\left(y^{*}\mid{\boldsymbol{X}}\right)
and
\delta\sim f(\delta|\boldsymbol{\gamma})
.

Now, from Theorem
3.1
of
Polson et al. 2013
, we can write

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\exp\left\{y_{i}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}}{1+\exp{(\boldsymbol{x}_{i,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})}}

\displaystyle\propto\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left(\exp\left\{(y_{i}-1/2){\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left\{-\frac{\omega_{i}}{2}\left({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)^{2}\right\}f(\omega_{i}\mid 1,0)d\omega_{i}\right),

where
f(\omega\mid a,b)
denotes the density of a Pòlya-Gamma random variate with parameters
a
and
b
. Therefore, after introducing a vector of auxiliary random variables
\boldsymbol{\omega}=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n})
,

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\delta\mid{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\omega})

\displaystyle\propto f(\boldsymbol{\gamma})f(\delta|\boldsymbol{\gamma})\phi_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}),\delta{\boldsymbol{H}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})\right)\phi_{n}\left({\boldsymbol{z}}\mid{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}\right),

(9)

where
{\boldsymbol{z}}=((y_{1}-1/2)/\omega_{1},\ldots,(y_{n}-1/2)/\omega_{n})^{T}
,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}=\diag\left\{\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}\right\}
,
f(\boldsymbol{\gamma})
is a prior on
2^{p}
dimensional model space and
f(\delta|\boldsymbol{\gamma})
is the prior on scale parameter
\delta
.

It is straightforward to see that
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
can be integrated out of (
9
), yielding

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\delta\mid{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\omega})=\int f(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\delta\mid{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\omega})d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\propto f(\boldsymbol{\gamma})f(\delta|\boldsymbol{\gamma})\phi_{n}({\boldsymbol{z}}|{\boldsymbol{m}}_{{\boldsymbol{z}}}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},{\boldsymbol{V}}_{{\boldsymbol{z}}}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}),

(10)

where

{\boldsymbol{m}}_{{\boldsymbol{z}}}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}={\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})
,
{\boldsymbol{V}}_{{\boldsymbol{z}}}^{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\boldsymbol{\Omega}^{-1}+\delta{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}){\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}
and, as before,
\phi_{p}(\cdot\mid\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})
denotes the density of the
p
-variate normal distribution with mean vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}
and covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
.
Various versions of Metropolis-Hastings algorithms can be implemented to explore the space of model (e.g., see section 4.5 of
George & McCulloch 1997
).

Once the model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
and the exponent
\delta
have been updated, the regression coefficients can be sampled using the fact that
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\delta,{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\omega}\sim\mathsf{N}\left({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},{\boldsymbol{V}}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)
,
where

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{V}}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\boldsymbol{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{z}}+\frac{1}{\delta}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})\right),

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{V}}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}

\displaystyle=\left({\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{\delta}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})\right)^{-1}

(11)

Conditional on
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, the remaining parameters
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
and
\boldsymbol{\omega}
can be easily sampled using either Gibbs sampling or random-walk Metropolis-Hastings steps. Further details of the computational algorithm can be see in Appendix
E
.

6.3
HOUSE107
: Determinants of legislator behavior in 107
th
U.S. House of Representatives

Most analyses of congressional voting treat all roll-call votes in the same way, independently of the type of vote. This might mask considerable variation in voting behavior across different types of votes. For example,
Jessee & Theriault 2012
provide empirical evidence that the forces affecting legislators’ voting on procedural and final passage matters have exhibited important changes over time, with differences between these two vote types becoming larger, particularly in recent congresses.

One shortcoming of the methodology presented in
Jessee & Theriault 2012
is that it provides legislature-wide measures of agreement among vote types, but cannot ascribe observed differences to individual legislators. Recently,
Lofland et al. 2017
and
Moser et al. 2021
developed methodology that enables the identification of differences in voting behavior across votes types for individual legislators. In this section we analyze a dataset where the response variable corresponds to estimates of whether each legislator in the 107
th
U.S. House of Representatives share the same voting behavior across final passage, amendment and procedural votes. These estimates are obtained using the model introduced in
Moser et al. 2021
. Hence, in this case,
y_{i}=1
if the
i
-th legislator voting preferences remain unchanged across all three vote types, and
y_{i}=0
otherwise. The goal of this analysis is to understand whether a group of 26 characteristics of the legislator or its constituency affect the likelihood of such changes. Linking voting behavior with these underlying characteristics can provide important insights into the workings of a political system (e.g., see
Facchini & Steinhardt 2011
,
O’Roark & Wood 2011
and
Cragg et al. 2013
).

Detailed description of the variables is available in supplementary materials. All the results for the Bayesian procedures in this section are based on the same settings and number of iterations as those used in the previous Sub-section for the
GUSTO-I

shows the marginal PIPs for all 26 variables, along with a list of variables selected by the penalized likelihood methods. Note that, as in previous illustrations, the penalized likelihood methods tend to select a superset of the variables selected by the Bayesian approaches, with LASSO selecting the largest superset. Furthermore, there is broad agreement among all Bayesian procedures. For example, all techniques, except both versions of DRPEP, assign high PIP to

). One place where the different procedures do seem to disagree is whether

(the median income in the district represented by the legislator) explains voting behavior. All LPEP procedures agree in providing weak to moderate evidence
against
the inclusion of this variable, while most other procedures provide weak to moderate evidence

. Interestingly, the two versions of the DRPEP seem to disagree, with the unit information version providing weak evidence in favor of its inclusion and the hyper-g/n providing weak evidence against it.

7
Discussion

The results from our theoretical and empirical studies show that the LPEP approach to creating non-informative priors for logistic regression is at least competitive, and in some cases superior, to existing techniques in terms of model selection and parameter estimation performance. The differences are particularly striking when comparing the LPEP with the original CRPEP and DRPEP approaches proposed in
Fouskakis et al. 2018
. Aside from the increased computational expense that makes the practical application of CRPEP and DRPEP prior challenging, our empirical results show that the behavior of these procedures can be greatly affected by the choice of hyperpriors. Furthermore, both procedures can lead to unexpected results that substantially deviate from the consensus of other Bayesian and non-Bayesian methods.

We were surprised by the poor behavior of some of the LCL procedures in some of our simulation studies. One point to note is that the setup of the simulations in Section
5
(
n=500
,
p=100
) was only briefly studied in
Li & Clyde 2018
. Indeed, most of the simulation studies in
Li & Clyde 2018
focus on settings involving fewer covariates (
p=20
), which are similar to those in the simulation study that we present in our supplementary materials. In this lower dimensional setting, LCL behaves quite well. Disentangling the role of the prior distribution from that of the various approximations used to speed up computation in the
BAS
package is one particular challenge in understanding these negative results. However, by comparing the results for LCL against its “exact” version (LCE), as well as those under the unit information (
\delta=n
) with those under the robust and hyper-g/n versions of the procedure, it would seem like the use of the Laplace approximation for the Bayes factor is not the culprit. Instead, these results seem to driven by a combination of some sensitivity to the choice of hyperprior for
\delta
and issues with the default approximation procedure implemented in
BAS
to integrate over
\delta
. Interestingly, the sensitivity to the hyperprior does not seem to be present for the LPEP procedures. We believe that this is an area that is in need of further investigation in the future.

This paper focuses mostly on developing the LPEP for logistic regression. However, the formulation is very general and can be extended to many other generalized linear models. Many of the computational advantages of our procedure extend to binomial, negative binomial and multinomial logic models where the data augmentation approach of
Polson et al. 2013
can be readily applied. This is also true for probit models in which computation can rely on the data augmentation approach of
Albert & Chib 1993
, as well as for loglinear regression using the approach of
Frühwirth-Schnatter et al. 2009
. These extensions will be explored elsewhere.

One final brief note about our theoretical results. Our asymptotic analyses assume that the number of covariates
p
is fixed with the sample size
n
. We believe that our results can be extended to situations in which
p
grows with
n
(as long
n
remains larger than
p
), potentially along the lines of
Barber et al. 2016
. However, this requires the careful introduction of additional constraints that ensure that the maximum likelihood estimates under the training sample remains finite as both
p
and
n
(and therefore, as
n^{*}
grow). We plan to explore this question in our future work.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
3.1
(Existence of MLEs)

The two conditions in the theorem together imply that the maximum likelihood estimator for the full model,

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}}=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}}}\,\ell_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}}),

exists, is finite, and, furthermore,
is unique (e.g., see
Mäkeläinen et al. 1981
).

Now, note that for any other model

\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, maximizing
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
is equivalent to maximizing
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}})
subject to the constraint
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}}\in S_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, where
S_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\{\boldsymbol{\theta}\in S_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}}:\theta_{j}=0\mbox{ if }\gamma_{j}=0\}
and that
S_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is also an open connected set for all
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.
Because
\ell_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}})
is continuous and strongly concave, then its restriction to
S_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is also continuous and strongly concave for any
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
. Furthermore, we also have
\lim_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\to\partial S_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}\ell_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=-\infty
because
\partial S_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\subset\partial S_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{F}}
. Therefore,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
also exists and is finite and unique for any
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.
\Box

q=\frac{1}{2}{\boldsymbol{v}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\mathbf{y}^{*}){\boldsymbol{v}}
,
m=\frac{n^{*}+1}{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1}>1
since
n^{*}>p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
. Now, from Lemma 2 in
Bayarri et al. 2012
,

\lim_{s\to\infty}\left(s^{2}\right)^{\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+2}{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{\lambda}{m-\lambda}\right)^{\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1}{2}}\lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}\exp\left\{-\left(\frac{\lambda}{m-\lambda}\right)qs^{2}\right\}d\lambda=c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},2}({\boldsymbol{v}},y^{*}),

where
0<c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},2}({\boldsymbol{v}},y^{*})<\infty
, and therefore

\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{\zeta^{R}(s\mid{\boldsymbol{v}},\boldsymbol{\gamma},{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})}{\left(1+s^{2}/(p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1)\right)^{-\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+2}{2}}}=c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},1}({\boldsymbol{v}},y^{*})c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},2}({\boldsymbol{v}},y^{*})=c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},3}({\boldsymbol{v}},y^{*}).

To complete the proof simply define
c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{v}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}}m^{*}(\mathbf{y}^{*})c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},3}({\boldsymbol{v}},y^{*})
. Since we have a finite number of terms in the sum and each term is both positive and finite, so is
c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{v}})
.

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem
4.2
(Intrinsic consistency)

Note that if
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
leads to separability, then
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{**}=\mathbf{1}-{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
does as well. Hence, our choice of
m^{*}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\mid{\boldsymbol{X}}\right)
is symmetric and, as
n^{*}\to\infty
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})\overset{p}{\to}\mathbf{0}
under
m^{*}
. In turn, this implies that
\hat{\theta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})\overset{p}{\to}1/2
for all
i
, and therefore
\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{\prime}{\boldsymbol{W}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}){\boldsymbol{X}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\overset{p}{\to}\frac{1}{4}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
. This completes the proof when
g=n
.

In the case where
\delta
is given a prior distribution define
\delta=n^{*}\delta^{*}
. Then, under
f^{HGn}(\delta)
,
\delta^{*}
has density

f^{HGn}(\delta^{*})=\left(1+\delta^{*}\right)^{-2}

which is a proper, non-degenerate prior. The argument for the robust prior follows along similar lines.

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem
4.3
(Model selection consistency)

Since

Pr(\boldsymbol{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}\mid{\boldsymbol{y}})=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}\neq\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\frac{f(\boldsymbol{\gamma})}{f(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T})}\frac{m^{LPEP}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}})}{m^{LPEP}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}({\boldsymbol{y}})}}

it is enough to show that

\frac{m^{LPEP}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}})}{m^{LPEP}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}({\boldsymbol{y}})}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{P}0.

The proof follows along similar lines as that in
Li & Clyde 2018
. We start by assuming similar regularity conditions:

(i)

The true model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}
is among the
2^{p}
models under consideration, with
p
fixed.

(ii)

For every
i=1,2,\ldots
, the vector
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
is such that
\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}
is bounded by a constant.

(iii)

For all
n
, the smallest eigenvalue of
\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{X}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{X}}
is bounded from below by a positive constant.

Note that these conditions imply the weak consistency and asymptotic normality of the maximum likelihood estimators for all models under consideration (e.g., see
Fahrmeir & Kaufmann 1985
).

Consider first

m_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})=\int f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\phi_{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\right),n{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\right)\right)d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
. Using a Laplace approximation of
f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
,

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})

\displaystyle=f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)^{T}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)\right\}\exp\left\{R\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)\right\}

where
R\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)
is the residual form the second order Taylor expansion of the loglikelihood. Note that, to simplify notation, we have let
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\right)
and
{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}={\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\right)
.

Hence, following
(
Tierney & Kadane 1986
)
,

m_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\right)=f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)n^{-\frac{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1}{2}}\left(\frac{\left|{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|}{\left|{\boldsymbol{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\right|}\right)^{1/2}\\
\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-{\boldsymbol{d}}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{d}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right]\right\}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\right)

where

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{d}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*},

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

\displaystyle={\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{n}.{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

As before, we simplify notation by letting
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\right)
, and
{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}={\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\right)
. From this,

\frac{m^{LPEP}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{y}})}{m^{LPEP}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}({\boldsymbol{y}})}=\frac{f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)}{f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\right)}n^{(p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}-p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})/2}\\
\frac{\mathsf{E}_{y^{*}}\left\{\left(\frac{\left|{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|}{\left|{\boldsymbol{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\right|}\right)^{1/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-{\boldsymbol{d}}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{E}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{d}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right]\right\}\right\}}{\mathsf{E}_{y^{*}}\left\{\left(\frac{\left|{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\right|}{\left|{\boldsymbol{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}})\right|}\right)^{1/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}+\frac{1}{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}-{\boldsymbol{d}}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}{\boldsymbol{E}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}{\boldsymbol{d}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\right]\right\}\right\}}\,\mathcal{O}(1)

Consider first the term

\frac{f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)}{f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\right)}n^{(p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}-p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})/2}.

This is just Schwartz criterion, which is well known to be consistent in this setting. Indeed, under the regularity conditions (i)-(iii), Lemma A.3 in the supplementary materials of
Li & Clyde 2018
can be applied to show that, as
n
increases, the likelihood ratio
\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}=\frac{f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)}{f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\right)}
has the following behavior.

(a)

If
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}\subset\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, then
\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}=\mathcal{O}_{P}(1)
.

(b)

If
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}\not\subset\boldsymbol{\gamma}
then
\Lambda_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}=\mathcal{O}_{P}\left(e^{-c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}n}\right)
for some positive constant
c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
.

Hence, if
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}\subset\boldsymbol{\gamma}
then necessarily
p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}<p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
and

\frac{f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)}{f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\right)}n^{(p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}-p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})/2}=\mathcal{O}_{P}(n^{-c})

for some positive constant
c
and therefore it tends to zero as
n\to\infty
. On other hand,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}\not\subset\boldsymbol{\gamma}

\frac{f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)}{f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\left({\boldsymbol{y}}\mid\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\right)}n^{(p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}-p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})/2}=\mathcal{O}_{P}\left(e^{-c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}n}\right)

for some positive constant
c_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
no matter whether
p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}<p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
or
p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\geq p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, and again it converges to zero as
n\to\infty
.

All that remains now is to show that

\frac{\mathsf{E}_{y^{*}}\left\{\left(\frac{\left|{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|}{\left|{\boldsymbol{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\right|}\right)^{1/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-{\boldsymbol{d}}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{E}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{d}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right]\right\}\right\}}{\mathsf{E}_{y^{*}}\left\{\left(\frac{\left|{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\right|}{\left|{\boldsymbol{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}})\right|}\right)^{1/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}+\frac{1}{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}-{\boldsymbol{d}}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}{\boldsymbol{E}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}{\boldsymbol{d}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}}\right]\right\}\right\}}

(12)

is, at most,
\mathcal{O}_{P}(1)
. To do this, we consider the behavior of

\left(\frac{\left|{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|}{\left|{\boldsymbol{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\right|}\right)^{1/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{1}{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-{\boldsymbol{d}}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{E}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{d}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right]\right\}

in probability as both
{\boldsymbol{y}}
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
grow. Because of dominated convergence, the behavior in probability with respect to
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
is the same as that in expectation.

•

First, note that
\frac{\left|{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|}{\left|{\boldsymbol{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|}=\left|\mathbf{I}+\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|^{-1}=\left|\mathbf{I}+\frac{1}{n}\left(n{\boldsymbol{H}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right)\right|^{-1}
. Under regularity conditions (ii) and (iii),
\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{P}{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
and
\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{P}{\boldsymbol{J}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
where
{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
and
{\boldsymbol{J}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
are
(p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1)\times(p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1)
constant and strictly positive definite matrices with finite entries (please see below). Hence,

\left|\mathbf{I}+\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right|^{-1}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{P}1.

We only elaborate on the proof that
\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{P}{\boldsymbol{J}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
; the argument for
\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is analogous. Furthermore, we focus on the diagonal elements of the matrix since the off-diagonal elements are bounded by the diagonal ones because the matrix is, by construction at least semi-positive definite. Since
\left\|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\right\|_{2}<c
for all
i
and some constant
c
, we have
|x_{i,j}|<c
. Therefore
\left[\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right]_{j,j}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{w}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}x_{i,j}^{2}<\frac{c^{2}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\left(1+\exp\left\{-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}\right)^{2}}{\exp\left\{-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}}
. Now, the maximum likelihood estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is not necessarily consistent when
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\neq\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{T}
, but it does converge in probability to a finite constant (e.g., see
Fahrmeir 1990
). Combined with the fact that the
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}
s have compact support, this implies that
\left(1+\exp\left\{-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}^{T}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right\}\right)^{-1}
converges in probability to a constant
\theta^{(0)}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}
that, for all
i
, is bounded away from 0 and 1, i.e.,
0<a\leq\theta^{(0)}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma},i}\leq b<1
for some constants
a
and
b
. Therefore,
\left[\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right]_{j,j}<\frac{c^{2}}{\min\{a(1-a),b(1-b)\}}<\infty
.

•

Next, consider
\frac{1}{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
. Because of our choice for
m^{*}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})
, the results in
Fahrmeir 1990
imply that
\frac{1}{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
converges in distribution to a point mass at zero. This is because the null model is the closest model (in the Kullback–Leibler sense) to
m^{*}({\boldsymbol{y}}^{*})
. Therefore we also have

\frac{1}{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{P}0.

•

Finally, consider
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-{\boldsymbol{d}}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{E}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{d}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
. Substituting back the expressions for
{\boldsymbol{d}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
and
{\boldsymbol{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
, expanding the quadratic form and combining similar terms we have:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-{\boldsymbol{d}}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{E}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{d}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left[{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*}\right)^{-1}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right]\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\\
+\frac{2}{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*}\right)^{-1}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*}\right)^{-1}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

Now, from the Woodbury matrix identity we have

\left[{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*}\right)^{-1}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\right]=\left({\boldsymbol{H}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+n{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*-1}\right)^{-1}

and therefore

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-{\boldsymbol{d}}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{E}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{d}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{H}}^{-1}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+n{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*-1}\right)^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\\
+\frac{2}{n}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*}\right)^{-1}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*T}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\left({\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+\frac{1}{n}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{*}\right)^{-1}{\boldsymbol{H}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{*}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}

Note that the last two terms converge in probability to
0
, while the first term is
\mathcal{O}_{P}\left(1\right)
.

Appendix E
Details of the Markov chain Monte Carlo algorithm for logistic regression

Using the hierarchical representation of the LPEP prior discussed at the start of Section
4.2
, the posterior distribution for the augmented model can be written as

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{y}^{*},\delta\mid\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{X})\propto f_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\boldsymbol{\omega})\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{LPEP}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\delta,\boldsymbol{y}^{*})f(\delta|\boldsymbol{\gamma})f(\boldsymbol{\gamma})f(\boldsymbol{\omega})m^{*}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*}).

From this, it is easy to devise samplers for the full conditional posterior distributions of various blocks of parameters. We focus below on the more general setting where
\delta
has been assigned a hyperprior. The simplifications for the case where
\delta
is fixed are straightforward and we do not discuss them explicitly.

1.

Since the prior support of
\delta
maybe dependent on model indicator
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, the parameters
(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\delta,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
are updated jointly by sampling from
f(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\delta,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\omega})
given in (
9
). To do this, we write

f(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\delta,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\omega})=f(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\delta\mid{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\omega})f(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\delta,{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\omega}),

where
f(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\delta\mid{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\omega})\propto\int f(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\delta,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\omega})d\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
.

The expression for the conditional posterior
f(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\delta\mid{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\omega})
, up to a proportionality constant, is given by (
10
).
To generate samples from it, we generate proposal by combining a random walk Metropolis-Hastings algorithm for
\boldsymbol{\gamma}

(
George & McCulloch 1997
)
and a reflective Gaussian random walk for
\delta
(similar to section 2.1 of
Thawornwattana et al. 2018
). More specifically, we factorize the joint proposal for
(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\delta)
as:

\displaystyle q\left(\delta^{(prop)},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(prop)}\mid\delta,\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)=q\left({\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(prop)}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma}\right)q\left(\delta^{(prop)}\mid\delta,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(prop)}\right).

For
q({\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(prop)}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma})
, we use a symmetric random walk proposal similar to equation (46) of
George & McCulloch 1997
as follows:

•

We define two probability vectors
p_{1}=(0.9,0.1)
and
p_{2}=(0.6,0.2,0.15,0.05)
.

•

Each time, we decide on one of two types of moves according to the probability vector
p_{1}
.

–

If a move type 1 is selected, then the proposed new model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)}
is generated by randomly flipping
d\in\{1,2,3,4\}
components of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
with probability
p_{2,d}
. The components of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
to be flipped are selected uniformly at random given
d
.

–

If a move type 2 is selected, then the proposed model
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)}
is generated by removing one variable currently included in the model and replacing it with a variable that is currently excluded, leaving the dimensionality of the model unchanged. The variables to add and remove are chosen uniformly at random within each set.

Next, given
\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)}
, we propose
\delta
using a reflective Gaussian random walk with a left reflection boundary
a_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)}}
. More specifically, we define
\delta^{(prop)}=a_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)}}+|\epsilon-a_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)}}|
where
\epsilon\sim N(\delta,\tau^{2})
and
a_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=0
under the hyper-g/n prior and

a_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=\frac{n-p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}{p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+1}
under the robust prior. In both cases we found
\tau=n/2
to be an efficient tuning parameter in our studies.

Since the proposal distribution of
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, given by
q({\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(prop)}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma})
is symmetric, the proposed model
(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)},\delta^{(prop)})
is then accepted with probability

\min\left\{\frac{f(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)},\delta^{(prop)}\mid{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\omega})}{f(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\delta\mid{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\omega})}\frac{q(\delta\mid\delta^{(prop)},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})}{q(\delta^{(prop)}\mid\delta,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(prop)})},1\right\},

where

q\left(\delta^{(prop)}\mid\delta,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(prop)}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\tau}\Bigg[\exp\Bigg\{-\frac{1}{2\tau^{2}}(\delta^{(prop)}-\delta)^{2}\Bigg\}+\\
\exp\Bigg\{-\frac{1}{2\tau^{2}}(2a_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(prop)}}-\delta^{(prop)}-\delta)^{2}\Bigg\}\Bigg],\quad\quad\delta^{(prop)}\geq a_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{(prop)}}.

Note that, when move type 2 is selected, since
p_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
remains unchanged,
q(\delta\mid\delta^{(prop)},{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})=q(\delta^{(prop)}\mid\delta,{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{(prop)})
and the acceptance probability simplifies further. Similar simplification is observed under hyper-g/n prior since proposal reflection boundary,
a_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}=0
.

Once the model
(\boldsymbol{\gamma},\delta)
has been sampled, the regression coefficients can be updated using the fact that
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\delta,{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*},\boldsymbol{\omega}\sim\mathsf{N}\left({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},{\boldsymbol{V}}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right)
,
where
{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
and
{\boldsymbol{V}}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
are given in (
11
).

2.

A posteriori, the entries of
\boldsymbol{\omega}
are conditionally independent from each other. Following
Polson et al. 2013
, it is straightforward to see that

\omega_{i}\mid\boldsymbol{\gamma},\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}},\delta,{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}\sim PG(1,\boldsymbol{x}_{i,\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{T}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})
. Implementations of the samplers for the Pòlya-Gamma distribution are available, for example, in the
R
package
BayesLogit
.

3.

The conditional distribution of
\boldsymbol{y}^{*}
is proportional to

\displaystyle\pi(\boldsymbol{y}^{*}\mid\delta,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}})\propto\pi_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{LPEP}(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}\mid\delta,\boldsymbol{y}^{*})m^{*}(\boldsymbol{y}^{*})

While this distribution is supported over the finite set
\{0,1\}^{n}
, a direct sampler is difficult to construct in part because of its (typically) large size of the support. Hence, we rely again on Metropolis-Hastings steps.

In order to ensure adequate mixing of the algorithm, we consider both local and global proposals. At each iteration, the algorithm selects local moves with probability
0.7
and global moves with probability
0.3
.

•

For the local moves, we propose new
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*(prop)}
by randomly flipping
d\in\{1,2,3,4,5\}
components of
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{*}
with probability
(0.5,0.2,0.15,0.10,0.05)
