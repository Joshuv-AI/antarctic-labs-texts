Generalized Lens Categories via Functors 𝒞 op → 𝖢𝖺𝗍 \mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to{\mathsf{Cat}}
David I. Spivak

Abstract
Lenses have a rich history and have recently received a great deal of attention from applied category theorists. We generalize the notion of lens by defining a category 𝖫𝖾𝗇𝗌 F {\mathsf{Lens}}_{F} for any category 𝒞 \mathcal{C} and functor F : 𝒞 op → 𝖢𝖺𝗍 F\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to{\mathsf{Cat}} , i.e. an indexed category, using a variant of the Grothendieck construction. All of the mathematics in this note is straightforward; the purpose is simply to see lenses in a broader context where some closely-related examples, such as ringed spaces and open continuous dynamical systems, can be included.

Chapter 1
Introduction

Roughly speaking, a
lens
is bi-directional map
\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{get}\\
\mathrm{put}\end{pmatrix}\colon\begin{pmatrix}c\\
x\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}d\\
y\end{pmatrix}
between pairs; the two parts have the following form:

\mathrm{get}\colon c\to d\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{put}\colon c\times y\to x.

(1)

Lenses have recently received a great deal of attention from applied category theorists. One reason is that they show up in many disparate places, such as database updates, learning algorithms, open games, open dynamical systems, and wiring diagrams. Lenses have been broadly generalized to so-called profunctor optics; see e.g.
[
Ril18
]
.

We will discuss what seems to be a completely different direction of generalization: we associate a notion of
generalized lens category
to an arbitrary indexed category. Namely for every category
\mathcal{C}
and (pseudo-) functor
F\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to{\mathsf{Cat}}
, we define a category
{\mathsf{Lens}}_{F}
using a variant of the Grothendieck construction. The idea is that a morphism in the Grothendieck construction consists of two parts, which turn out to be the above get and put maps from lens theory. Taking
\mathcal{C}={\mathsf{Set}}
, we can recover the usual category of lenses in a couple of ways (see
Examples

3.5
and
3.10
). The most basic is to take
F
to be the slice category functor
{\mathsf{Slice}}\colon{\mathsf{Set}}^{\mathrm{op}}\to{\mathsf{Cat}}
, embed each
\begin{pmatrix}c\\
x\end{pmatrix}
as the projection
\pi_{c}\colon c\times x\to c
in
{\mathsf{Slice}}(c)
, and note that for any choice of function
\mathrm{get}\colon c\to d
, the square shown below is a pullback:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c\times x}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c\times y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d\times y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces d}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{c}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{put}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{d}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathrm{get}}

(2)

The function indicated as
\mathrm{put}
does not have quite the same form as in
Eq.

1
: there is an extra factor of
c
in the codomain. However, to be a morphism in the slice category
{\mathsf{Slice}}(c)
, such a function
c\times y\to c\times x
in
{\mathsf{Slice}}(c)
must commute with the projections. Thus it has no choice with regards to the
c
-factor in the codomain, and hence the only remaining choice is that of a function
\mathrm{put}\colon c\times y\to x
, thus recovering the notion of morphism
\begin{pmatrix}c\\
x\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}d\\
y\end{pmatrix}
from
Eq.

1
. This idea, to think of the an object
\begin{pmatrix}c\\
x\end{pmatrix}
not as a simple pair but as a
dependent
pair (
x
dependent on
c
), is the main thrust of this note.

All of the results we discuss here are straightforward to prove. The proposed contribution is to provide a setting in which open continuous dynamical systems, ringed spaces, and dependent lenses—none of which fit in with the usual definition of lens but all of which seem to be quite similar to it in spirit—can be included in the theory. We also provide a construction of lenses in an arbitrary symmetric monoidal category, which is known but seems not to have been written down explicitly before.

Example 2.3

(Simple lenses)

In functional programming the emphasis has often been on what we call

Example 2.5

(Moore machines)

Given a pair of sets

Definition 2.6

(Commutative comonoid)
.

Let
(\mathcal{C},I,\otimes,\sigma)
be a symmetric monoidal category. A
commutative comonoid in
\mathcal{C}
consists of a tuple
(c,\epsilon,\delta)
where
c\in\mathcal{C}
,
\epsilon\colon c\to I
and
\delta\colon c\to c\otimes c)
satisfy the axioms:

1.

\delta_{c}\mathbin{\fatsemi}(c\otimes\epsilon_{c})=c
,

2.

\delta_{c}\mathbin{\fatsemi}\sigma_{c,c}=\delta_{c}
, and

3.

\delta_{c}\mathbin{\fatsemi}(\delta_{c}\otimes c)=\delta_{c}\mathbin{\fatsemi}(c\otimes\delta_{c})
.

We refer to
\epsilon
as the
counit
and
\delta
as the
comultiplication
. We sometimes write
c
to denote the comonoid, leaving
\epsilon
and
\delta
implicit.

A
morphism
of commutative comonoids
(c,\epsilon_{c},\delta_{c})\to(d,\epsilon_{d},\delta_{d})
is a morphism
f\colon c\to d
in
\mathcal{C}
such that
\epsilon_{c}=f\mathbin{\fatsemi}\epsilon_{d}
and
\delta_{c}\mathbin{\fatsemi}(f\otimes f)=f\mathbin{\fatsemi}\delta_{d}
. We denote the category of commutative comonoids and their morphisms by
{\mathsf{Comon}}_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Chapter 3
Generalized lens categories

We define the lens category
{\mathsf{Lens}}_{F}
for any functor
F\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to{\mathsf{Cat}}
and then give several examples.

3
Definition and examples of
{\mathsf{Lens}}_{F}

The Grothendieck construction comes in two variants.

Definition 3.1

(Grothendieck constructions)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category and
F\colon\mathcal{C}\to{\mathsf{Cat}}
. The
covariant Grothendieck construction of
F
consists of a category
{\mathsf{Gr}}(F)
and a functor
\pi_{F}\colon{\mathsf{Gr}}(F)\to\mathcal{C}
, defined as follows.

\displaystyle\ob({\mathsf{Gr}}(F))

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{c\in\ob(\mathcal{C})}\ob(F(c))

\displaystyle{\mathsf{Gr}}(F)\big((c,x),(d,y)\big)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{f\in\mathcal{C}(c,d)}\Hom_{F(d)}\big(F(f)(x),y\big)

That is, an object in
{\mathsf{Gr}}(F)
is a pair
(c,x)
where
c\in\mathcal{C}
and
x\in F(c)
. A morphism
(c,x)\to(d,y)
is a pair
(f,f^{\sharp})
where
f\colon c\to d
and
f^{\sharp}\colon F(f)(x)\to y
is a morphism in the category
F(d)
. The identity on
(c,x)
is
(\id_{c},\id_{x})
, and the composite of
(f,f^{\sharp})
and
(g,g^{\sharp})
is given by

(f,f^{\sharp})\mathbin{\fatsemi}(g,g^{\sharp})\coloneqq\left((f\mathbin{\fatsemi}g),\big(F(g)(f^{\sharp})\mathbin{\fatsemi}g^{\sharp}\big)\right).

The functor
\pi_{F}\colon{\mathsf{Gr}}(F)\to\mathcal{C}
sends
(c,x)\mapsto c
and
(f,f^{\sharp})\mapsto f
.

Given a functor
F\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to{\mathsf{Cat}}
, the
contravariant Grothendieck construction of
F
consists of a category
{\mathsf{Gr}}^{\mathrm{o}}(F)
and a functor
\pi_{F}\colon{\mathsf{Gr}}^{\mathrm{o}}(F)\to\mathcal{C}
, defined as follows:

\displaystyle\ob({\mathsf{Gr}}^{\mathrm{o}}(F))

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{c\in\ob(\mathcal{C})}\ob(F(c))

\displaystyle{\mathsf{Gr}}^{\mathrm{o}}(F)\big((c,x),(d,y)\big)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{f\in\mathcal{C}(c,d)}\Hom_{F(c)}\big(x,F(f)(y)\big)

Identities, composition, and the functor
\pi_{F}
are defined analogously.

For any functor
F\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to{\mathsf{Cat}}
, let
F^{\mathrm{p}}\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to{\mathsf{Cat}}
denote its pointwise opposite,
F^{\mathrm{p}}(c)\coloneqq F(c)^{\mathrm{op}}
. The following is straightforward.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to{\mathsf{Cat}}
be a functor, and let
F^{\mathrm{p}}\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to{\mathsf{Cat}}
be its pointwise opposite. The following three categories are naturally isomorphic:

1.

{\mathsf{Gr}}(F)^{\mathrm{op}}
, the opposite of the covariant Grothendieck construction of
F
,

2.

{\mathsf{Gr}}^{\mathrm{o}}(F^{\mathrm{p}})
, the contravariant Grothendieck construction of the pointwise opposite of
F
,

3.

the analogous category with objects
\bigsqcup_{c\in\ob(\mathcal{C})}\ob(F(c))
and morphisms

\Hom((c,x),(d,y))\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{f\in\mathcal{C}(c,d)}\Hom_{F(c)}\big(F(f)(y),x\big).

(4)

Moreover, these isomorphisms commute with the functors
\pi_{F}
to
\mathcal{C}
.

Remark 3.4
.

The Grothendieck construction of a
{\mathsf{Cat}}
-valued functor

always yields a (split) fibration over

and vice versa, so generalized lens categories can be viewed simply as split fibrations. However we chose

to be the fiberwise opposite of

Example 3.5

(Dependent lenses)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a category with pullbacks. There is a functor
{\mathsf{Slice}}\colon\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{op}}\to{\mathsf{Cat}}
given on an object

by the slice category
{\mathsf{Slice}}(c)\coloneqq\mathcal{C}/c
over

, and on morphisms

in

by pullback. That is, for every object

in

we obtain an object

using the following pullback diagram in

:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b\times_{c}x}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces x}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces b}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces c}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{\mathsf{Slice}}(f)(p)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lrcorner}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}

This extends to morphisms in
{\mathsf{Slice}}(c)
using the universal property of pullbacks.

The category
{\mathsf{Lens}}_{{\mathsf{Slice}}}
has as objects pairs
\begin{pmatrix}c\\

where
p\colon x\to c
, and as morphisms pairs
\begin{pmatrix}f\\
f^{\sharp}\end{pmatrix}
where

and

. We can think of objects in
{\mathsf{Lens}}_{{\mathsf{Slice}}}
as dependent lenses; for example if
\mathcal{C}={\mathsf{Set}}
, then each object

may assign non-isomorphic fibers to different elements of

.

Note that we can find the category of lenses from
Definition

2.2
inside of
{\mathsf{Lens}}_{F}
. Indeed, it is isomorphic to the full subcategory spanned by all pairs
\begin{pmatrix}c\\
\pi\end{pmatrix}
for which
\pi\colon c\times x\to c
is the projection for some

. This was discussed in the introduction around
Eq.

. We will recover the category of lenses in a completely different way in

.

More importantly, the get functor
\pi_{F}\colon{\mathsf{Lens}}_{{\mathsf{Slice}}}\to\mathcal{C}
is not only a fibration but a bifibration. Indeed, the functor
{\mathsf{Slice}}(f)\colon{\mathsf{Slice}}(d)\to{\mathsf{Slice}}(c)
has a left adjoint
\Sigma_{f}\colon{\mathsf{Slice}}(c)\to{\mathsf{Slice}}(d)
, which one may call the
dependent sum along
f
, for any morphism

in
\mathcal{C}
. The name “dependent lens” is actually most appropriate when

not only has pullbacks but is locally cartesian closed. This simply means that each
{\mathsf{Slice}}(f)
additionally has a right adjoint
\Pi_{f}\colon{\mathsf{Slice}}(c)\to{\mathsf{Slice}}(d)
, called the
dependent product along
f
. In this case
\pi_{F}
is a trifibration.

(Open continuous dynamical systems)

Recall that a differentiable manifold

has a tangent bundle
TM
and a submersion
\pi_{M}\colon TM\to M

(A,B)

defines an open continuous dynamical system with inputs

Example 3.7

(Cotangent bundles)
.

Consider again the lens category for the functor
\mathsf{Subm}\colon{\mathsf{Mfd}}^{\mathrm{op}}\to{\mathsf{Cat}}
, as in
Example

3.6
. Whereas in that example we considered objects given by tangent bundles and found that certain morphisms between them were continuous dynamical systems, here we consider objects given by cotangent bundles and find that we get lenses between them canonically, without making choices.

To begin, note that a differentiable manifold
M
also has a cotangent bundle
T^{*}M
; its fiber over each point
m\in M
is the dual to the tangent space there, i.e. it is the vector space
T^{*}_{m}M\coloneqq{\mathsf{Vect}}(T_{m}M,{\mathbb{R}})
of linear maps from the tangent space to the ground field
{\mathbb{R}}
. Given a differentiable function
f\colon M\to N
, the derivative (Jacobian) defines a map
Tf\colon TM\to TN
, in particular over each point
m\in M
a linear transformation
T_{m}M\to T_{f(m)}N
. This in turn induces a linear transformation
{\mathsf{Vect}}(T_{f(m)}N,{\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathsf{Vect}}(T_{m}M,{\mathbb{R}})
for each point
m
, and these assemble into a morphism
f^{\sharp}\colon f^{*}(T^{*}N)\to T^{*}M
of bundles over
M
. All together we have canonically obtained a morphism
\begin{pmatrix}f\\
f^{\sharp}\end{pmatrix}\colon\begin{pmatrix}M\\
T^{*}M\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}N\\
T^{*}N\end{pmatrix}
in the lens category
{\mathsf{Lens}}_{\mathsf{Subm}}
, and hence a functor
{\mathsf{Mfd}}\to{\mathsf{Lens}}_{\mathsf{Subm}}
.

Example 3.8

(Ringed spaces)
.

The category of ringed spaces from algebraic geometry is an example of a generalized lens category. There is a functor
\mathsf{Sh}\colon{\mathsf{Top}}^{\mathrm{op}}\to{\mathsf{Cat}}

{\mathsf{Top}}
is the category of topological spaces and
\mathsf{Sh}(X)

X

f\colon X\to Y
in
{\mathsf{Top}}

f^{*}
which sends a sheaf on

X

\mathsf{Sh}
on morphisms.

The category
{\mathsf{Lens}}_{\mathsf{Sh}}
of
\mathsf{Sh}

\begin{pmatrix}X\\
\mathcal{O}_{X}\end{pmatrix}

\mathcal{O}_{X}

\mathcal{O}_{X}\end{pmatrix}\to\begin{pmatrix}Y\\
