Entanglement generation from athermality
A. de Oliveira Junior ∗, Jeongrak Son ∗, Jakub Czartowski, Nelly H. Y. Ng

Abstract
We investigate the thermodynamic constraints on the pivotal task of entanglement generation using out-of-equilibrium states through a model-independent framework with minimal assumptions. We establish a necessary and sufficient condition for a thermal process to generate bipartite qubit entanglement, starting from an initially separable state. Consequently, we identify the set of system states that cannot be entangled, when no external work is invested. In the regime of infinite temperature, we analytically construct this set; while for finite temperature, we provide a simple criterion to verify whether any given initial state is or is not entanglable. Furthermore, we provide an explicit construction of the future thermal cone of entanglement — the set of entangled states that a given separable state can thermodynamically evolve to. We offer a detailed discussion on the properties of this cone, focusing on the interplay between entanglement and its volumetric properties. We conclude with several key remarks on the generation of entanglement beyond two-qubit systems, and discuss its dynamics in the presence of dissipation.

A powerful toolkit for answering this question is the

However, LOCC operations are naturally motivated only after entanglement has been distributed; if one is instead at the preparation phase in a local lab, prior to entanglement distribution, then it is more reasonable to consider a resource theory that contains entangling operations.
If entangling operations are allowed, the main limiting factor must come from quantum control and energetics

;
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2013
;
Brandão et al. 2015
;

This model-independent framework allows one to investigate general thermodynamic processes by relying solely on the assumptions of total energy conservation and thermality of environments. Its broad scope has allowed us to establish bounds on the size of the bath required for the thermalization of a many-body system

In this work, we systematically probe the capability of using thermal processes to generate entanglement when given access to some initial state. In the particular case of two qubits,
we derive necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of a thermal process that transforms a given separable initial state into a final state that is entangled. These conditions depend only on the energetic populations of the two-qubit system. A key insight that led to this finding, is the observation that a generic entangling strategy can be decomposed into two independent procedures: one that is explicitly achieved by open system dynamics (interaction with heat bath that results in changes of energetic population), and another closed unitary operation, i.e. an optimal rotation within the global energy subspace. The consistency of our results is confirmed by the problem addressed in Refs.
Verstraete et al. 2001
;
Ishizaka and Hiroshima 2000
, where the authors investigate two-qubit mixed states and determine unconstrained global unitary operations that generate maximal entanglement. We approach the considered problem with additional restrictions on the alllowed operations, motivated by cornerstone findings on thermodynamic resource theories.
To be specific, we assume that entangling operations must fall into the set of thermal operations
Janzing et al. 2000
;
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2013
.

Using the above framework we provide an
explicit construction of the set of separable states that can become entangled. The degree of entanglement in this set can be assessed either by the volume of the set or by their negativity. In this regard, our results show that a Bell pair can always be thermodynamically produced at any temperature regime, provided that the system is initialized in its pure excited state. We also discuss the generation of entanglement in the presence of dissipation, examining the behavior of the volume of the future thermal cone of entanglement under Markovian thermal processes, and comparing it with the closed dynamics case. Finally, we offer some remarks on how our results can be extended to systems beyond two qubits.

III
Setting the scene

We investigate entanglement generation in an
N
-partite system whose initial state and the non-interacting Hamiltonian are given as

\rho\in\mathrm{conv}\quantity( \bigotimes_{i=1}^N\rho_i)\quad\text{and}\quad H=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}^{\otimes(i-1)}\otimes H_{i}\otimes\mathbbm{1}^{\otimes(N-i)}.

(4)

The assumption that the Hamiltonian is non-interacting is natural, and excludes the trivial scenario of entangling simply via a fully thermalizing process.
The Hilbert space of the composite system is denoted by
\mathcal{H}=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}_{i}
.
The energy level structure creates a natural division of the full Hilbert space into energy-degenerate subspaces
\mathcal{V}_{E}
,

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{E}\mathcal{V}_{E},

(5)

where
\mathcal{V}_{E}=\mathrm{span}\left\{\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}:H\ket{\psi}=E\ket{\psi}\right\}
.
Note that energy-preserving unitaries acting on
\mathcal{H}
are those that can be written as
U=\bigoplus_{E}U_{E}
, where each unitary
U_{E}
acts on
\mathcal{V}_{E}
.
In other words, they do not mix states from different energy subspaces – an essential feature for subsequent considerations.

III.1
Thermal operations

The multipartite system interacts with the thermal environment that is initialized in a Gibbs state

\gamma_{\textsf{R}}=\frac{e^{-\beta H_{\textsf{R}}}}{\Tr(e^{-\beta H_{\ms R}})},

(6)

with a Hamiltonian
H_{\textsf{R}}
and an inverse temperature
\beta
.
We make minimal assumptions on the joint system-bath dynamics: the composite system is closed and thus evolves unitarily; in addition, this unitary evolution is energy-preserving. Formally, this leads to a set of operations known as
thermal operations
(TOs)
Janzing et al. 2000
, which transform the state
\rho
as follows:

\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\Tr_{\textsf{R}}\left[U\left(\rho\otimes\gamma_{\textsf{R}}\right)U^{\dagger}\right],

(7)

where
U
is a joint unitary that commutes with the total Hamiltonian of the system and the bath

[U,H\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{\textsf{R}}+\mathbbm{1}\otimes H_{\textsf{R}}]=0,

(8)

and the environment Hamiltonian
H_{\textsf{R}}
can be chosen arbitrarily. Given that there are no further constraints on
U
, the system and the bath can develop arbitrarily strong correlations (including entanglement).

III.2
State transformation conditions

We focus on
energy-incoherent
states
\rho
, which means that each partition is (block) diagonal in the energy eigenbasis, resulting in an output state that is also (block) diagonal. Mathematically, the multipartite state
\rho
commutes with the Hamiltonian, i.e.,
[\rho,H]=0
. Under this assumption, the state
\rho
can be fully represented by a probability vector
\boldsymbol{p}
consisting of the eigenvalues of
\rho
, which correspond to populations in the energy eigenbasis,
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\outerproduct{E_i}{E_i}
. We henceforth directly refer to
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},...,p_{d})
as energy-incoherent states, which live within the probability simplex

\Delta_{d}=\quantity{\v{p} = (p_1, ..., p_d) \in\mathbbm{R}_{\geq 0}^d : \sum_i p_i = 1}.

(9)

Analogously, the thermal equilibrium state of the system, Eq. (
6
) with
H_{\textsf{R}}
replaced by
H
, can be represented by a vector of thermal populations, denoted as
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
.

When considering transformations between different energy-incoherent states under thermal operations, an important aspect of a state is its associated
\beta
-order – a permutation which arranges populations, rescaled with respect to the Gibbs state, in a non-increasing order.

Definition 1

(
\beta
-ordering)
.

Suppose that population vectors
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, corresponding to a
d
-dimensional system state and its Gibbs state with respect to the ambient temperature
1/\beta
, are given. The
\beta
-ordering of
\boldsymbol{p}
is given as a permutation
\pi_{\boldsymbol{p}}\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
of energy levels within the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{d}
, such that the element-wise ratio between
\boldsymbol{p}
and

is non-increasing, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{p_{(\pi_{\boldsymbol{p}})i}}{\gamma_{(\pi_{\boldsymbol{p}})i}}\geq\frac{p_{(\pi_{\boldsymbol{p}})j}}{\gamma_{(\pi_{\boldsymbol{p}})_{j}}},\quad\text{for all}\quad i\leq j.

(10)

For simplicity, we also denote a
\beta
-ordering

as a vector
\boldsymbol{\pi}=(\pi(1),\pi(2),\cdots,\pi(d))
in the rest of the manuscript. For the specific case of

, we denote the

).

Finally, the set of states
\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})
that can be achieved via thermal operations from a given initial state

is called the
future thermal cone

;
de Oliveira Junior et al. 2022
. This set is a convex polytope comprising at most
d!
extreme points, each corresponding to different
\beta
-orderings
\boldsymbol{\pi}

Korzekwa 2017
;
de Oliveira Junior et al. 2022
. Mathematically, it is represented by:

\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})=\operatorname{conv}[{\boldsymbol{p}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}},\boldsymbol{\pi}\in{\cal S}_{d}}],

(11)

where
\boldsymbol{p}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
are extreme points characterized by thermomajorization

(see Lemma
5
, Appendix
A

Again, for
d=4

\boldsymbol{p}^{\star}

Although not explicitly stated, it is important to mention that the existence of a thermal operation that induces the transition between incoherent states
\boldsymbol{p}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{p}^{\prime}
is equivalent to the existence of a Gibbs-preserving (GP) stochastic matrix mapping between these states

Therefore, when studying the thermodynamic interconversion problem between block diagonal states, one can focus on stochastic matrices and probability vectors instead of CPTP maps and density matrices. Nonetheless, our forthcoming discussion will primarily center on the concept of thermal cones, rather than on GP matrices or thermal operations.

IV
Entanglability under thermal operations

In this section, we introduce the main definitions that will enable us to derive the necessary and sufficient conditions for entangling a separable state under thermal operations. At first glance, this might appear abstract due to its generality, but these notions will be applied to a specific case in Section
V
.

We begin by defining a subset of unitary operations that preserve the energy of the system and keep the energy level structure intact. Note that this is motivated by capturing the thermodynamic constraints in producing entanglement when the system is closed, i.e. when the unitary dynamics satisfy energy conservation. We refer to this subset as
energy subspace-unitaries
, denoted by
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{subs}}
. Formally, this set is given by

\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{subs}}=\quantity{U_{\mathrm{subs}} = \bigoplus_E U_{E}},

(12)

where
U_{E}
acts on
\mathcal{V}_{E}
subspace of definite energy
E
. Consequently, any unitary operation from this set is an admissible operation within the framework of thermal operations.

Next, we introduce an entanglement measure
\mathcal{M}
, which is non-negative for all separable states,
\mathcal{M}(\rho_{\textsf{sep}})\geq 0
. One such entanglement measure which we focus on is derived from the famous Horodecki-Peres positive partial transpose (PPT) criterion
Peres 1996
;
Horodecki et al. 1996
, which states that separable states have positive partial transpose,
\rho^{T_{\textsf{A}}}\geq 0
; using this, we define
\mathcal{M}(\rho)\coloneqq\min_{\ket{\psi}}\expectationvalue{\rho^{T_A}}{\psi}=\lambda_{-}
as the minimal eigenvalue of the partial transpose.
Therefore, the quantity

f(\boldsymbol{p})\coloneqq\min_{U\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{subs}}}\mathcal{M}\quantity(U\rho U^{\dagger})

(13)

is a good witness for the thermodynamic capacity of creating entanglement from
\boldsymbol{p}
via closed, energy-conserving system dynamics.

Definition 2

(Subspace (non-)entanglable set)
.

Given a state
\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{d}
, the subspace non-entanglable set is defined as the set of states that remain PPT under subspace rotations, i.e.

\mathbbm{NE}\coloneqq\quantity{\v{p} \, \big| \, f(\v{p})\geq 0}.

(14)

The function
f
acts as an entanglement witness, i.e.
f(\boldsymbol{p})\leq 0
implies the possibility of creating entanglement. Furthermore, the
subspace entanglable set
is defined as the complement of
\mathbbm{NE}
, i.e.
\mathbbm{E}\coloneqq\Delta_{d}\setminus\mathbbm{NE}
.

This definition can be extended by employing multiple entanglement measures
\mathcal{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{M}_{M}
, resulting in multiple witnesses
f_{1},\ldots,f_{M}
, and requiring that all these functions yield positive values.

It is important to note that, states in the set
\mathbbm{E}
are entanglable even when neither heat or work exchange are allowed.
Notably, when thermal resources are available, i.e. under thermal operations, the set of entanglable states expand.

Definition 3

(Future thermal cone of entanglement)
.

The future thermal cone of entanglement of a state
\boldsymbol{p}
is defined as

\mathcal{T}_{+}^{\mathrm{ENT}}(\boldsymbol{p})\coloneqq\quantity{\v{q} \, \big| \, f(\v{q})<0,\ \v{q}\in\mathcal{T}_{+}(\v p)}=\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})\cap\mathbbm{E}.

(15)

Now we introduce the primary object of this paper: the set of states that cannot become entangled under thermal operations.

Definition 4

(Thermally non-entanglable set)
.

Given the ambient temperature
\beta^{-1}
, the thermally non-entanglable set is defined as

\mathbbm{TNE}(\beta)=\quantity{\v{p}\, \big| \, \min_{\v{q}\in\mathcal{T}_{+}(\v{p})}f(\v{q}) \geq 0},

(16)

with the entanglement measure
\mathcal{M}
.
Analogous to
\mathbbm{E}
, we also define the set of thermally entanglable states
\mathbbm{TE}(\beta)\coloneqq\Delta_{d}\setminus\mathbbm{TNE}(\beta)
.

By definition,
\boldsymbol{p}
is thermally non-entanglable if and only if its future cone of entanglement is empty, i.e.

\boldsymbol{p}\notin\mathbbm{TNE}(\beta)~~\Longleftrightarrow~~\mathcal{T}_{+}^{\mathrm{ENT}}(\boldsymbol{p})=\varnothing.

(17)

We can then characterize
\mathbbm{TNE}(\beta)
with the following proposition.
Hereafter, we drop the explicit dependence on
\beta
in
\mathbbm{TNE}
and
\mathbbm{TE}
when there is no concern for confusion.

Proposition 1

(Thermally non-entanglable set)
.

Suppose that the entanglement measure
\mathcal{M}
is a concave function. Then the set
\mathbbm{TNE}
can be written as

\mathbbm{TNE}=\quantity{\v{p}\mid\forall_\pi f(\v{p}_\pi) \geq 0}.

(18)

When
\beta E=0
, the above expression simplifies to

\mathbbm{TNE}=\bigcap_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{d}}\quantity{\v{p}\mid f(\Pi\v{p}) \geq 0}=\vcentcolon\bigcap_{\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{d}}\,\Omega_{\pi}\ ,

(19)

where
\Pi
represents a permutation matrix corresponding to
\pi\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
; in particular, for
\pi=\mathrm{id}
we have
\Omega_{\mathrm{id}}=\mathbbm{NE}
.

Definition
3
also allows us to quantify the entanglement generation power using geometric measures.
The volume of the future thermal cone of entanglement can gauge how useful an initial state is for producing entangled states. Given a state
\boldsymbol{p}
, this quantity is defined as

\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{ENT}}_{\mathrm{TO}}(\boldsymbol{p})\coloneqq\frac{V\left[\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{ENT}}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})\right]}{V(\Delta_{d})},

(20)

where
V
is the volume measured using the Euclidean metric. Likewise, the volume of (non-)entanglable sets, defined in Definitions
2
and
4
, are denoted as

\mathbf{V}_{\mathbbm{X}}\coloneqq\frac{V({\mathbbm{X}})}{V(\Delta_{d})}\quad,\quad\mathbbm{X}=\mathbbm{NE},\ \mathbbm{E},\ \mathbbm{TNE}.

(21)

V
Two-qubit systems

Let us now apply the general framework we developed to a specific scenario: two-qubit entanglement.
In this section, we find an efficient criterion for determining whether a two-qubit state
\boldsymbol{p}
is thermally non-entanglable, which only checks if
\boldsymbol{p}^{\star}
is subspace non-entanglable.
We also study the geometry of the future thermal cone of entanglement and the thermally non-entanglable set for different ambient temperatures.

V.1
Subspace entanglable set for two qubits

Assume that Alice and Bob share a pair of qubits that are initially prepared in a separable state
\rho_{\textsf{AB}}
.
Each of them is described by a local Hamiltonian
H_{A}=H_{B}=E\outerproduct{1}{1}
, and they are allowed to interact with a single heat bath via thermal operations. If there exists a thermal operation that maps
\rho_{\textsf{AB}}
to
\sigma_{\textsf{AB}}
, where
[\rho_{\textsf{AB}},H]=0
and
[\sigma_{\textsf{AB}},H]=0
, then

\sigma_{\textsf{AB}}
can be expressed as

\sigma_{\textsf{AB}}=q_{1}\outerproduct{00}{00}+q_{2}\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}+q_{3}\outerproduct{\psi_{\perp}}{\psi_{\perp}}+q_{4}\outerproduct{11}{11},

(22)

where
\ket{\psi},\lvert\psi^{\perp}\rangle\in\mathcal{V}_{E}
and
\langle\psi|\psi^{\perp}\rangle=0
. This observation follows from the fact that one can freely rotate the populations within the degenerate energy subspace. The unitary that connects
\ket{\psi}=U\ket{10}
and
\ket{\psi_{\perp}}=U\ket{01}
can be written with two parameters
\theta,\phi\in[0,2\pi]

U=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta e^{i\phi}\\
-\sin\theta e^{-i\phi}&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\oplus\mathbbm{1}_{\mathcal{H}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{E}},

(23)

apart from an irrelevant phase factor, global to
\mathcal{V}_{E}
.
Then the final state
\sigma
reads

\!\!\sigma=\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}&0&0&0\\
0&q_{2}\cos^{2}\theta+q_{3}\sin^{2}\theta&\frac{(q_{3}-q_{2})}{2}\sin 2\theta e^{i\phi}&0\\
0&\frac{(q_{3}-q_{2})}{2}\sin 2\theta e^{-i\phi}&q_{2}\sin^{2}\theta+q_{3}\cos^{2}\theta&0\\
0&0&0&q_{4}\end{pmatrix}.

(24)

For a two-qubit system, separability can be fully determined by the positive partial transposition (PPT) criterion
Peres 1996
;
Horodecki et al. 1996
. When the partial transposition is applied to the state
\sigma
in Eq. (
24
), its smallest eigenvalue is

\!\!\!\lambda_{-}=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(q_{1}+q_{4}\right)-\sqrt{\left[(q_{2}-q_{3})^{2}\sin^{2}2\theta+(q_{1}-q_{4})^{2}\right]}\right],

(25)

which is minimized when
\sin^{2}2\theta=1
1
1

1

This minimum value coincides with the case where we diagonalize the energy degenerate subspace in Bell basis, i.e.,
\ket{\psi},\lvert\psi_{\perp}\rangle=\lvert\Psi^{\pm}\rangle
.
. Thus,
\lambda_{-}
can become negative if and only if

4q_{1}q_{4}<(q_{2}-q_{3})^{2}.

(26)

In other words, a state with population vector
\boldsymbol{q}=(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},q_{4})
is subspace entanglable if and only if Eq. (
26
) is true. We will refer to this inequality as the entanglement constraint or criterion. In addition, we define an associated entanglement measure

f(\boldsymbol{q}):=4q_{1}q_{4}-(q_{2}-q_{3})^{2},

(27)

which detects entanglement whenever
f(\boldsymbol{q})<0
.

V.2
Thermally entanglable states

We now construct the set of states that can generate entanglement with the help of a thermal environment. In the simple case of

(Criterion for determining

.

Let

Thermal processes permit the local bypassing of strict energy-preservation, thereby offering advantage in terms of entanglement generation.
Yet, thermal noise inevitably accompanies thermal processes, impacting the amount of achievable entanglement.
The negative impact of thermal noise is anticipated to be minimized at lower environmental temperatures.
We confirm this first by observing that the set of non-entanglable states

\beta\rightarrow\infty

Lemma 3
.

The set
\mathbbm{TNE}(\infty)=\{(1,0,0,0)\}
, i.e. it contains only the ground state.

Proof.

Given any other initial state, it is possible to thermalize one of the subsytems; in the zero-temperature limit (
\beta\rightarrow\infty
), this transforms a state
\boldsymbol{p}=(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},p_{4})
into
\boldsymbol{q}=(p_{1}+p_{3},p_{2}+p_{4},0,0)
or
(p_{1}+p_{2},p_{3}+p_{4},0,0)

f
always becomes negative. Thus, for any state
\boldsymbol{p}\neq(1,0,0,0)

\boldsymbol{q}
is subspace entanglable, i.e.
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbbm{TE}
.
∎

One may be tempted to view the ground state
(1,0,0,0)
then as useless for entanglement generation; however, this is not true. In particular, the ground state becomes thermally entanglable whenever
\beta^{-1}\neq 0
: one can thermalize one of the subsystems to obtain
\boldsymbol{q}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\beta E}}(1,e^{-\beta E},0,0)
, which is subspace entanglable.
What is observed is that as long as there is a certain amount of athermality in the initial state (with respect to environment temperature), we can often trade it for some amount of entanglement.

Given the above, it is then intuitive to see that given an initial state
\boldsymbol{p}

\beta_{C}

\boldsymbol{p}

26

\beta<\beta_{C_{1}}
or
\beta>\beta_{C_{2}}
.

A particularly clear analysis can be done when the initial state themselves are thermal, having a temperature
\beta_{\textsf{S}}\neq\beta

D

Thermal initial states are written as

\boldsymbol{p}=\frac{1}{Z_{\textsf{S}}}(1,e^{-\beta_{\textsf{S}}E},e^{-\beta_{\textsf{S}}E},e^{-2\beta_{\textsf{S}}E}),

(28)

\beta_{\textsf{S}}>\beta

\beta

(1,2,3,4)

Finding the explicit form of the extreme point
\boldsymbol{p}^{\star}
and solving

\displaystyle\beta_{C_{1}}\equiv\beta_{\textsf{S}}-E^{-1}\log\left[1+2e^{-\beta_{\textsf{S}}E}\left(\sqrt{e^{2\beta_{\textsf{S}}E}+1}-1\right)\right]\ .

(29)

Whenever the ambient temperature
\beta^{-1}
is higher than the critical temperature
\beta_{C_{1}}^{-1}

\boldsymbol{p}
becomes thermally entanglable.
We can obtain a simple approximation

On the other hand, when the system is hotter than its surrounding, i.e.
\beta_{\textsf{S}}<\beta

However, in the same limit
\beta_{\textsf{S}}E\gg 1

.
In other words, if

|\beta_{S}-\beta|\geq E^{-1}\log 3,

(30)

then we may operate such a thermal machine to generate entanglement. See Fig.

As mentioned earlier, the temperature difference between the system and the environment is a resource for entanglement generation, as it is for many other thermodynamic tasks. This process can be conceptualized as a type of heat engine which aims not to output work, but entanglement. For instance, consider a 3-stroke thermal-entanglement engine whose working substance is a two-qubit system. This engine operates between an ambient bath at temperature
\beta^{-1}
and a working bath with temperature
\beta^{-1}_{W}:\absolutevalue{\beta^{-1}_W - \beta^{-1}_C}>0
. The cycle proceeds as follows:
(i)
the two-qubit system thermalises with the working bath, reaching equilibrium at the working bath’s temperature
\beta_{W}^{-1}
;
(ii)
the system is then brought into contact with the ambient bath, which is at temperature
\beta^{-1}

(iii)
the generated entanglement is used, and the system is returned to its initial state, which is energy-incoherent.

V.3
Quantifiers for achievable entanglement under thermal operations

In the previous sections, we focused on determining whether a state is entanglable or not using thermal resources. Here, we are additionally interested in the extent of entanglement that thermal operations can produce, and how this varies across different temperature regimes.
Two figures of merit can be used to quantify this extent: the
thermal volume of entanglement

\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{ENT}}_{\mathrm{TO}}(\boldsymbol{p})
as defined in Eq. (
20

Vidal and Werner 2002
that the initial state can produce. In particular, the thermal volume of entanglement
is a thermodynamic monotone, in other words,

3

\mathcal{T}_{+}^{\mathrm{ENT}}(\boldsymbol{p})

\mathbbm{E}

\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})

, which is on the boundary of
\mathbbm{E}

VI.1
Generation of entanglement in the presence of dissipation

So far, our discussion revolved around the assumption that any energy-preserving system-bath dynamics is accessible.
However, one can also consider the scenario where the system undergoes an open dynamics described by a Lindblad master equation
Kossakowski 1972
;
Gorini et al. 1976
;
Lindblad 1976
,

\frac{d\rho(t)}{dt}=-i\quantity[H, \rho(t)]+\mathcal{L}_{t}\quantity[\rho(t)],

(34)

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
denotes the commutator and
\mathcal{L}_{t}
is the Lindbladian having the general form

\mathcal{L}_{t}(\rho)=\sum_{i}r_{i}(t)\left[L_{i}(t)\rho L_{i}(t)^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\Bigl\{L_{i}(t)^{\dagger}L_{i}(t),\rho\Bigl\}\,\!\right]\!,\!

(35)

with
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
denoting the anticommutator,
L_{i}(t)
being jump operators, and
r_{i}(t)\geq 0
being non-negative jump rates.
Furthermore, assuming that the quantum system interacts with a large heat bath implies that the system Gibbs state is a stationary solution of the dynamics,
\mathcal{L}_{t}(\gamma)=0
and that the Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}_{t}
commutes with the generator of the Hamiltonian dynamics
-i[H,\cdot]
for all times
t
.

The dynamics generated by master equations that satisfy two aforementioned properties lead to what is known as a Markovian thermal process, which is known to be a strict subset of thermal operations
Lostaglio and Korzekwa 2022
. The differences between them arise from our degree of control on the bath. Nonetheless, the entanglement criterion given in Eq. (

) remains valid for Markovian thermal processes. Consequently, we can study the entanglement generation in the presence of dissipation using the same criterion function
f
.

The additional restriction stemming from the Markovianity of the Lindblad master equations is the most evident when considering the highest excited state
\boldsymbol{p}=(0,0,0,1)
as the initial state.
As analysed in Section
V-C
, the volume of the future thermal cone of entanglement
\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{ENT}}_{\mathrm{TO}}(\boldsymbol{p})
defined in Eq. (

) remains maximal for all inverse temperatures
\beta

In Fig.

\mathbf{V}^{\mathrm{ENT}}_{\mathrm{TO}}(\boldsymbol{p})>0
indicates that the entanglement generation via dissipative dynamics is possible.
However, the difference between the entanglement generation via thermal operations is the most pronounced in the high-temperature limit, i.e. when
\beta

VI.2
Remarks on higher-dimensional systems

The entanglement structure for multipartite systems with more than two parties is notoriously complex.
Multipartite entanglement can be classified using equivalence classes defined with invertible local transformations. Two states are considered to have the same class of entanglement if one can be transformed into the other via LOCC with a non-zero probability
Dür et al. 2000
. Even in the simplest three-qubit systems, one encounters two distinct classes of entanglement: GHZ-like entanglement and W-like entanglement
Dür et al. 2000
;
Acín et al. 2001
, represented by the states

\displaystyle\ket{\mathrm{GHZ}}=\frac{\ket{000}+\ket{111}}{\sqrt{2}},\ \ \ket{\mathrm{W}}=\frac{\ket{001}+\ket{010}+\ket{100}}{\sqrt{3}}.

(36)

The
n
-qubit extensions of two classes exist. In particular,
n
-qubit GHZ states are defined as

\ket{\mathrm{GHZ}_n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\underbrace{\ket{0\hdots 0}}_{n}+\underbrace{\ket{1\hdots 1}}_{n}\right),

(37)

an equal superpositions of all
n
qubits being in the ground state and in the excited state. The concept of W-like entanglement is similarly generalized to
n
-qubit Dicke states

\ket{D_{m}}=\binom{n}{m}^{-1/2}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}P_{\sigma}\quantity(\underbrace{\ket{0\hdots 0}}_{n-m}\otimes\underbrace{\ket{1\hdots 1}}_{m})\ ,

(38)

where the sum goes over all permutations
\sigma
on
n
elements and
P_{\sigma}
are the respective matricial representations.
These states form a basis for the symmetric subspace of an
n
-qubit system.

Remarkably, different energy level structures give rise to different entanglement structures that can be achieved using thermal operations. Let us assume that the Hamiltonian of the
i
-th qubit is
H_{i}=E_{i}\left|1\right\rangle\!\!\left\langle 1\right|
. First, if all the energy gaps are equal, i.e.
E_{i}=E
, then the
n
-qubit Hilbert space is divided into energy-degenerate subspaces:

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{i=0}^{n}\mathcal{V}_{iE},

(39)

each of which spanned by states with a fixed number of excitations
i
and thus the energy equal to
i\cdot E
.
With this energy-level structure, Dicke states
\ket{D_i}
, or W-like entanglement in the three-qubit case, can be generated via thermal operations with nonzero components within the energy-degenerate subspaces.

On the other hand, if we set
E_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}E_{i}
, we find that the degenerate subspace corresponding to this energy

\mathcal{V}_{E_{n}}=\mathrm{span}\quantity(
|\underbrace{1\hdots 1}_{n-1}0\rangle,\,
|\underbrace{0\hdots 0}_{n-1}1\rangle)

(40)

contains states possessing GHZ-like entanglement. Specifically, in the case of three qubits, this configuration leads to GHZ states up to a NOT operation on the third qubit. Similar arguments can be easily extended to systems with larger local dimensions.

Concerning systems with larger local dimensionality, we note that PPT criterion, based on negativity of partial transpose of a state, is a complete entanglement measure not only for two-qubit systems, but also for qubit-qutrit systems as well
Horodecki et al. 1996
. In particular, we may consider a system in an energy-incoherent state
\rho
described by population
\boldsymbol{p}=\quantity(p_1,\hdots,p_6)
and subject to local hamiltonians
H_{1}=\outerproduct{1}{1}
and
H_{2}=\outerproduct{1}{1}+2\outerproduct{2}{2}
. Based on similar arguments as for the qubit-qubit systems, if any of the following two conditions,

\displaystyle 4\,p_{1}\min(p_{4},p_{5})

\displaystyle<(p_{2}-p_{3})^{2},

(41)

\displaystyle 4p_{6}\min(p_{2},p_{3})

\displaystyle<(p_{4}-p_{5})^{2},

is satisfied, one of the eigenvalues of the partial transpose
\rho^{T_{A}}
can become negative under energy-preserving unitary operations, and therefore, the state is entanglable. Similar expressions can be easily put forward for qubit-qudit systems.

It should be highlighted, however, that in general, complete entanglement measures for multipartite systems beyond
2\times 2
and
2\times 3
do not exist, unlike the one based on the PPT criterion, which is effective for two-qubit and two-qutrit cases
Peres 1996
;
Horodecki et al. 1996
, which is reflected in the appearance of so-called bound entanglement
Gaida and Kleinmann 2023
.
Nevertheless, there exist partial entanglement measures, such as the 3-tangle for three-qubit systems
Coffman et al. 2000
, can be used to provide an outer approximation of the entanglable and non-entanglable sets in such scenarios.

VII
Outlook

In this paper, we have investigated the interplay between entanglement and thermodynamics.
The minimal thermodynamic assumptions we made, namely that the system-bath is closed and evolves via an energy-preserving unitary, constrain the final entanglement achievable from given initial states. By applying the PPT criterion and constructing future thermal cones, we derived necessary and sufficient conditions for two-qubit initial states that can be transformed into entangled states within thermal operations framework.
It turns out that any separable state, apart from a cluster of states around the thermal state, can become entangled.
In other words, our investigation demonstrates that entanglement generation is possible, given certain degree of athermality in the initial state.
Furthermore, we captured how the entanglement generating capability varies with the ambient temperature, by analysing the volume of entanglable initial state set, and found that low temperature allows more states to become entanglable.
Nevertheless, when the initial state is fixed, we discovered that lowering temperature is not always beneficial and that more detailed considerations on the geometry of thermal cones are needed.

There are several promising avenues for extending and generalizing the results presented in this paper. A concrete and direct generalization consists of using an auxiliary system, a catalyst, to facilitate a process that would not occur spontaneously; see recent reviews
Datta et al. 2023
;
Lipka-Bartosik et al. 2023a
. The idea of using a catalyst in our context arises from Ref.
Lipka-Bartosik et al. 2023b
, where the authors demonstrate that catalysis enables the creation of otherwise inaccessible types of correlations by accessing those locked in non-degenerate energy subspaces. As we have discussed, the entanglement generation capacity depends on the structure of degenerate energy subspaces. Employing a catalyst not only increases the number of degenerate energy subspaces but also lift some of the dynamical restrictions imposed by energy conservation. In this regard, the initial steps demonstrating the utility of generating entanglement via thermal operations with the aid of a strict catalyst were presented in Ref.
Czartowski and de Oliveira Junior 2024
. Furthermore, in Appendix
E
, we provide an example where correlated catalysis enables the generation of entanglement from an initial state that cannot be entangled without a catalyst. This raises the intriguing question of how one can systematically study the generation of entanglement with the assistance of a catalyst.

In this work, we only considered entanglement generation from initially energy-incoherent states.
When we focus on the entanglement generated between degenerate energy levels, coherence between different energy levels cannot contribute.
Nevertheless, coherence can still nontrivially affect the entanglement, including those between energy levels with different energy values.
Currently, tools and methods available for analysing coherent state transformations under thermal operations are rather limited
Korzekwa 2017
;
Lostaglio et al. 2015
;
Gour et al. 2018
.
For two-qubit systems, existing techniques to characterize single qubit coherent state transformations
Lostaglio et al. 2015
;
Korzekwa 2017
may be adapted to study the entanglement generation.
This direction of study will extend our results to the realm where three resources – entanglement, athermality, and coherence – compete and cooperate with each other.

We make a few final remarks on existing literature, to which our work can connect.
First, it would be interesting to study the conventional quantifiers of entanglement such as entanglement cost and distillable entanglement
Horodecki et al. 2009
which are originally defined via LOCC, and evaluate them with regard to thermodynamic constraints instead. In particular, the gap between these quantities would then quantify the amount of irreversibility when it comes to the local preparation of entanglement.
Lastly, entanglement in continuous-variable (CV) systems is relatively less explored.
Extending our analysis to CV systems would be extremely fruitful, considering their widespread use in practical experiments
Andersen et al. 2010
involving optomechanics
Rocheleau et al. 2009
;
Teufel et al. 2011
, and superconducting setups
Reiter et al. 2013
.
For instance, we may use the toolkits developed in the study of Gaussian thermal operations
Serafini et al. 2020
for an analysis similar to what have been done in the current work, and aim to explicate CV entanglement results, such as the recent no-go result
Longstaff et al. 2023
for the CV steady-state entanglement generation.

We briefly review state transition rules under thermal operations and the notion of the future thermal cone, a set of state that can be transformed from a given initial state with thermal operations.
First, we introduce thermomajorization relations, which fully characterize the convertibility between two states via thermal operations.
The thermomajorization curves, associated to each energy-incoherent state, are defined as the following:

Definition 5

(Thermomajorization curve)
.

Suppose that the Gibbs state
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\Delta_{d}
with respect to the system Hamiltonian
H
and the ambient inverse temperature
\beta
is given.
For each state
\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{d}
, we define the thermomajorisation curve of
\boldsymbol{p}
as a piecewise-linear function
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{p}}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
composed of linear segments connecting the point
(0,0)
and the elbow points
\{(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\gamma_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{p}}^{-1}(i)},~\sum_{i=1}^{k}p_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{p}}^{-1}(i)})\}_{k=1}^{d}
.
Here
\pi_{\boldsymbol{p}}
is the
\beta
-ordering of
\boldsymbol{p}
, which imposes
p_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{p}}^{-1}(i)}/\gamma_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{p}}^{-1}(i)}\geq p_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{p}}^{-1}(j)}/\gamma_{\pi_{\boldsymbol{p}}^{-1}(j)}
for all
i\leq j
.

Note that thermomajorization curves are always concave and non-decreasing by definition.
For the Gibbs state
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, the thermomajorization curve
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is a straight line from
(0,0)
to
(1,1)
.
From the concavity of the curve and the fact that any
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{p}}
passes points
(0,0)
and
(1,1)
, any thermomajorization curve is above the curve for the Gibbs state. i.e.
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(x)\geq\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
.
See Fig.
9
(a) for examples of thermomajorization curves.

Using thermomajorization curves, thermomajorization relations between two states can be established.

Definition 6

(Thermomajorization relation)
.

The thermomajorization relation is a preorder between two energy-incoherent states.
If two states
\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}\in\Delta_{d}
have corresponding thermomajorization curves
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{p}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{q}}
, such that
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(x)\geq\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{q}}(x)
for all
x\in[0,1]
, we say
\boldsymbol{p}
thermomajorizes
\boldsymbol{q}
, or more simply,
\boldsymbol{p}\succ_{\beta}\boldsymbol{q}
.

Since the thermomajorization relation gives a preorder, not a total order, there exist pairs of states
\boldsymbol{p}
and
\boldsymbol{q}
such that neither
\boldsymbol{p}\succ_{\beta}\boldsymbol{q}
nor
\boldsymbol{q}\succ_{\beta}\boldsymbol{p}
holds.
Such pairs are dubbed incomparable.
Reamrkably, thermomajorization relations coincide with the state convertibility under thermal operations.

Theorem 4

(Theorem 2 of Ref.
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2013
)
.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{p},\boldsymbol{q}\in\Delta_{d}
describe two energy-incoherent states of a system with Hamiltonian
H
with access to thermal environments with the inverse temperature
\beta
.
If
\boldsymbol{p}\succ_{\beta}\boldsymbol{q}
, than
\boldsymbol{p}
can be transformed to
\boldsymbol{q}
via some thermal operation.

In particular, the Gibbs state
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is thermomajorized by any other state and thus any state can be converted to the Gibbs state.
Conversely, the most excited state with
\boldsymbol{p}=(0,\cdots,0,1)
thermomajorizes all other states in
\Delta_{d}
and any state can be converted from it.

Theorem
4
provides an efficient way of checking whether a transition from one state to another is feasible.
However, it is also possible to characterize the entire set of states that can be attained from an initial state via thermal operations.
We refer to such set with an initial state
\boldsymbol{p}
as the future thermal cone of
\boldsymbol{p}
and denote it with a symbol
\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})
.

Lemma 5

(Lemma 12 of Ref.
Lostaglio et al. 2018
)
.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{p}\in\Delta_{d}
.
The future thermal cone
\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})
is a convex polytope with at most
d!
extreme points each corresponding to permutations
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in{\cal S}_{d}
.
The extreme point
\boldsymbol{p}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
corresponding to the permutation
\boldsymbol{\pi}
can be identified by first constructing its following thermomajorization curve
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{p}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}

1.

The
x
-coordinates of the
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{p}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}
elbow points are
x_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\gamma_{\pi^{-1}(i)}
for
k\in\left\{1,\dots,d\right\}
.

2.

The
y
-coordinates of the
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{p}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}
elbow points are
y_{k}=\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{p}}(x_{k})
for
k\in\left\{1,\dots,d\right\}
.

Therefore,
\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{p}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}}
is the piecewise-linear curve that connects
(0,0)
and elbow points

The thermomajorization relation between the initial state
\boldsymbol{p}
and extreme states

\mathcal{L}_{\boldsymbol{p}}

Furthermore, by construction, each state
\boldsymbol{p}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}}
has the
\beta
-ordering
\boldsymbol{\pi}
.
Lemma

enables us to characterize the future thermal cone of

Theorem 6

(Future thermal cone)
.

The future thermal cone of a
d

{\cal T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})=\operatorname{conv}[\{\boldsymbol{p}^{\boldsymbol{\pi}},\boldsymbol{\pi}\in{\cal S}_{d}\}]\,.

(42)

See Fig.

To show that the set of states defined by

f[\lambda(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})+(1-\lambda)(x_{2},y_{2},z_{3})]\geq\lambda f(x_{1},y_{1},z_{1})+(1-\lambda)f(x_{2},y_{2},z_{2}),

\displaystyle p^{(\pm)}_{3}=-2p_{1}+p_{2}\pm 2\sqrt{p_{1}-2p_{1}p_{2}},

By focusing on the boundary conditions, we can simplify the analysis concerning convexity. Specifically, when considering two arbitrary boundary points, the right-hand side of Eq. (

\displaystyle f[\lambda(x_{1},y_{1},p^{(\pm)}_{3})+(1-\lambda)(x_{2},y_{2},p^{(\pm)}_{3})]

\displaystyle f[\lambda(x_{1},y_{1},p^{(\pm)}_{3})+(1-\lambda)(x_{2},y_{2},p^{(\mp)}_{3})]

\displaystyle A\cap{\operatorname{Bd}}_{d}(B)\subset{\operatorname{Bd}}_{d-1}(A).

(61)

Proof.

For the first part of the proposition, suppose that there exists a point
x\notin{\operatorname{Bd}}_{d}(A)
such that
x\in A\cap{\operatorname{Bd}}_{d}(B)
.
Then there exists a convex subset
Y
of
A
having
d+2
extreme points and containing
x
in
\mathrm{int}(Y)
.
Yet, this set
Y
is also a convex subset of
B
having the same property, which contradicts
x\in A\cap{\operatorname{Bd}}_{d}(B)
.

The second part of the proposition follows directly from combining Eq. (
60
) and the additional assumption
[{\operatorname{Bd}}_{d}(A)\setminus{\operatorname{Bd}}_{d-1}(A)]\cap{\operatorname{Bd}}_{d}(B)=\emptyset
.
∎

Now we proceed to prove Theorem
2
, which states that
\boldsymbol{p}^{\star}\in\mathbbm{E}
whenever
\boldsymbol{p}\in\mathbbm{TE}
.
First, consider mixtures of the initial state
\boldsymbol{p}
and the Gibbs state
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
that can be written as
\boldsymbol{p}^{(x)}=(1-x)\boldsymbol{p}+x\boldsymbol{\gamma}
for some
x\in[0,1]
.
Future thermal cones between these states have a hierarchy
\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(y)})\subset\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(x)})
whenever
y\geq x
.
Then there exists a coefficient
a\in(0,1)
, such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)}\right)\subset\mathbbm{NE},\quad\partial\left(\mathcal{T}_{+}\left(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)}\right)\right)\cap\partial\left(\mathbbm{NE}\right)\neq\emptyset,

(62)

since
\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})\not\subset\mathbbm{NE}
, while
\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})\subset\mathrm{int}(\mathbbm{NE})
.
The Gibbs state
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
is always full-rank and so is
\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)}
, i.e.
\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)}\in\mathrm{int}(\Delta_{4})
and
\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})\subset\mathrm{int}(\Delta_{4})
.

Now we show that
[{\operatorname{Bd}}_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)}))\setminus{\operatorname{Bd}}_{0}(\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)}))]\cap\partial(\mathbbm{NE})=\emptyset
.
Let
\boldsymbol{q}
be a state on the edge of
\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)})
but not on the vertex of it, i.e.
\boldsymbol{q}\in[{\operatorname{Bd}}_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)}))\setminus{\operatorname{Bd}}_{0}(\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)}))]
.
Since
\mathbbm{NE}
is convex, if
\boldsymbol{q}\in\partial(\mathbbm{NE})
, the entire edge containing
\boldsymbol{q}
should be included within
\partial(\mathbbm{NE})
.
Recall that
\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)})
is a polyhedron whose edges are defined as segments of lines on which two of the populations are fixed, e.g.

\boldsymbol{q}=(p^{(a)}_{1},p^{(a)}_{2},t,1-p^{(a)}_{1}-p^{(a)}_{2}-t)
and for some
t\in(t_{1},t_{2})
.
Then the entire edge can be written as a set of states

\displaystyle\left\{\left(p^{(a)}_{1},p^{(a)}_{2},t,1-p^{(a)}_{1}-p^{(a)}_{2}-t\right)\,\big|\,t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]\right\}\subset\partial(\mathbbm{NE}).

(63)

Yet, it is impossible to satisfy
f(p^{(a)}_{1},p^{(a)}_{2},t,1-p^{(a)}_{1}-p^{(a)}_{2}-t)=0
for a finite range
t\in[t_{1},t_{2}]
, which contradicts Eq. (
63
).
The same can be proven for any two fixed populations.
Likewise,
[{\operatorname{Bd}}_{2}(\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)}))\setminus{\operatorname{Bd}}_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)}))]\cap\partial(\mathbbm{NE})=\emptyset
: if any point on
[{\operatorname{Bd}}_{2}(\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)}))\setminus{\operatorname{Bd}}_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)}))]
is included in
\partial(\mathbbm{NE})
, at least one entire edge of
\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)})
should also be included, which is not possible.

Using Proposition
8
twice with
A=\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)})
and
B=\mathbbm{NE}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)})\cap\partial(\mathbbm{NE})=\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)})\cap\partial(\mathbbm{NE})\subset{\operatorname{Bd}}_{0}(\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)})),

(64)

i.e. only extreme points of
\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)})
can be included in the set
\partial(\mathbbm{NE})
.

We finally use Lemma
7
.
Since
\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)}\in\partial(\mathbbm{TE})
, there must exist
\boldsymbol{q}\in\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)})
with
\beta
-ordering
(2,1,3,4)
that is in
\partial(\mathbbm{E})=\partial(\mathbbm{NE})
.
However, only extreme points of
\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p}^{(a)})
can be in
\partial(\mathbbm{NE})
, implying that the
(2,1,3,4)
extreme point

\displaystyle(1-a)\boldsymbol{p}^{\star}+a\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\partial(\mathbbm{NE}).

(65)

From
\boldsymbol{\gamma}\in\mathrm{int}(\mathbbm{NE})
, we obtain
\boldsymbol{p}^{\star}\in\mathbbm{E}
, which concludes the proof.

Appendix D
Thermal initial states

In this section, we derive the critical temperatures above or below which the initially thermal state become entanglable.
We denote the inverse temperature of the system as
\beta_{\textsf{S}}
and that of the environment as
\beta
.
To simplify the notation, we use Boltzmann weights
\Delta_{\textsf{S}}\coloneqq e^{-\beta_{\textsf{S}}E}
,
\Delta\coloneqq e^{-\beta E}
, , and partition functions
Z_{\textsf{S}}\coloneqq(1+\Delta_{\textsf{S}})^{2}
,
Z\coloneqq(1+\Delta)^{2}
.
Then the initial state can be written as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}=\frac{1}{Z_{\textsf{S}}}\left(1,\Delta_{\textsf{S}},\Delta_{\textsf{S}},\Delta_{\textsf{S}}^{2}\right).

(66)

When
\beta_{S}>\beta
, i.e. when the system is cooler than the environment, the
\beta
-ordering of
\boldsymbol{p}
is
(1,2,3,4)
.
The extreme point
\boldsymbol{p}^{\star}
of
\mathcal{T}_{+}(\boldsymbol{p})
, corresponding to the ordering
\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\star}=(2,1,3,4)
can be easily obtained by maximally exchanging populations (referred to as the
\beta
-swapping in Refs.
Lostaglio et al. 2018
;
Czartowski et al. 2023
;
Son and Ng 2024
) between first two levels
\ket{00}
and
\ket{01}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}^{\star}=\frac{1}{Z_{\textsf{S}}}\left(1-\Delta+\Delta_{\textsf{S}},\Delta,\Delta_{\textsf{S}},\Delta_{\textsf{S}}^{2}\right).

(67)

We want the entanglement measure to be negative, i.e.

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{p}^{\star})=\frac{1}{Z_{\textsf{S}}^{2}}\left(4\Delta_{\textsf{S}}^{2}\left[1-(\Delta-\Delta_{\textsf{S}})\right]-(\Delta-\Delta_{\textsf{S}})^{2}\right)<0.

(68)

Solving this inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\Delta>\Delta_{C_{1}}\coloneqq\Delta_{\textsf{S}}\quantity(1+2\sqrt{1+\Delta_{\ms{S}}^{2}} - 2\Delta_{\ms{S}}),

(69)

equivalent to Eq. (
29
) in Section
V-B
.

When the system temperature is low, that is, when
\Delta_{\textsf{S}}\ll 1
, Eq. (
69
) can be written with the Taylor expansion

\displaystyle\Delta_{C_{1}}=3\Delta_{\textsf{S}}+O(\Delta_{\textsf{S}}^{2}),

(70)

yielding the approximation
\beta_{C_{1}}E\simeq\beta_{\textsf{S}}E-\log 3
, up to the leading order.

Now, consider the other case where
\beta_{\textsf{S}}<\beta
; the system is now hotter than the surrounding environment, and the
\beta
-order of the initial state
\mathbf{p}
is now
(4,3,2,1)
.
Thermomajorization relations reveal that the extreme point
\boldsymbol{p}^{\star}
now has two distinct forms depending on the environment temperature
\beta
.
If
1>\Delta+\Delta^{2}
, that is,
\beta E\geq-\log[\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}]
, the extreme state is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}^{\star}=\frac{1}{Z_{\textsf{S}}}\left(1+(\Delta_{\textsf{S}}-\Delta)(1+\Delta),\Delta_{\textsf{S}}(1+\Delta_{\textsf{S}}-\Delta),\Delta,\Delta^{2}\right).

(71)

On the other hand, when
\beta E<-\log[\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}]
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{p}^{\star}=\frac{1}{Z_{\textsf{S}}}\left(\frac{\Delta_{\textsf{S}}}{\Delta},\Delta_{\textsf{S}}(1+\Delta_{\textsf{S}}-\Delta),1+\Delta_{\textsf{S}}+\Delta_{\textsf{S}}\Delta-\Delta-\frac{\Delta_{\textsf{S}}}{\Delta},\Delta^{2}\right).

(72)

For the latter case Eq. (
72
), the entanglement measure is evaluated as

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{p}^{\star})=\frac{1}{Z_{\textsf{S}}^{2}\Delta^{2}}\left(4\Delta_{\textsf{S}}\Delta^{3}-(\Delta_{\textsf{S}}-\Delta)^{2}(1+\Delta(\Delta_{\textsf{S}}-\Delta))^{2}\right).

(73)

However, in the range where Eq. (
72
) is valid,
1\geq\Delta_{\textsf{S}}>\Delta>\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}
, entanglement measure is always positive and there is no critical inverse temperature
\beta_{C_{2}}
.
For the former case Eq. (
71
) with
1\geq\Delta_{\textsf{S}}>\Delta
and
\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}\geq\Delta>0
,

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{p}^{\star})=\frac{1}{Z_{\textsf{S}}^{2}}\left[-(\Delta_{\textsf{S}}-\Delta)^{2}(1+\Delta_{\textsf{S}})^{2}+4\Delta^{2}\left(1+\Delta_{\textsf{S}}-(1-\Delta_{\textsf{S}})\Delta+\Delta^{2}\right)\right]

(74)

and the solution for
f(\boldsymbol{p}^{\star})=0
can be obtained by solving this quartic equation.
Unfortunately, there is no simple explicit solution for this equation.
To approximate the critical value, let us assume that
\Delta_{\textsf{S}}\ll 1
, i.e.
\beta_{\textsf{S}}E\gg 1
.
Ignoring the
Z_{\textsf{S}}
factor in Eq. (
74
) and taking only the leading order terms of
\Delta
and
\Delta_{\textsf{S}}
, we get the approximation

\displaystyle Z_{\textsf{S}}^{2}f(\boldsymbol{p}^{\star})=3\Delta^{2}+2\Delta\Delta_{\textsf{S}}-\Delta_{\textsf{S}}^{2}+O(\Delta_{\textsf{S}}^{3}),

(75)

and the solution of
f(\boldsymbol{p}^{\star})=0
is approximately
\Delta\simeq\frac{\Delta_{\textsf{S}}}{3}
.
In other words,
\beta_{C_{2}}E\simeq\beta_{\textsf{S}}E+\log 3
.
