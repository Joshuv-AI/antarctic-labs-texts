Abstract
. Normal form bisimilarities are a natural form of program equivalence resting on open terms, first introduced by Sangiorgi in call-by-name. The literature contains a normal form bisimilarity for Plotkin’s call-by-value λ \lambda -calculus, Lassen’s enf bisimilarity , which validates all of Moggi’s monadic laws and can be extended to validate η \eta . It does not validate, however, other relevant principles, such as the identification of meaningless terms—validated instead by Sangiorgi’s bisimilarity—or the commutation of 𝗅𝖾𝗍 \sf{let} s. These shortcomings are due to issues with open terms of Plotkin’s calculus. We introduce a new call-by-value normal form bisimilarity, deemed net bisimilarity , closer in spirit to Sangiorgi’s and satisfying the additional principles. We develop it on top of an existing formalism designed for dealing with open terms in call-by-value. It turns out that enf and net bisimilarities are incomparable , as net bisimilarity does not validate Moggi’s laws nor η \eta . Moreover, there is no easy way to merge them. To better understand the situation, we provide an analysis of the rich range of possible call-by-value normal form bisimilarities, relating them to Ehrhard’s relational model.

1.
Introduction

The study of program equivalences for
\lambda
-calculi is an important topic where semantical and operational techniques meet. Properties of program equivalences are notoriously difficult to prove. Even the equivalence of two terms might be challenging to establish, if the notion of equivalence is
Morris 1968
’ contextual equivalence
(
Morris 1968
)
, or some variant still involving a universal quantification, such as
Abramsky 1990
’s applicative bisimilarity
(
Abramsky 1990
)
. Another difficulty is the fact that properties of program equivalences are brittle, as they are not preserved by extensions of the calculus under study, nor by restrictions, and not even by changing the evaluation strategy within the same calculus.

It is well-known that applicative bisimilarity is fully abstract for contextual equivalence in the untyped call-by-name weak
\lambda
-calculus (where
weak
stands for reduction only out of abstractions, which is standard in functional languages)
(
Abramsky 1990
)
, as well as in Plotkin’s call-by-value weak
\lambda
-calculus
(
Egidi
et al. 1992
;
Pitts 2012
)
. Therefore, one might be led to think that the call-by-name/call-by-value switch is quite robust in the weak setting, even if it is known that its robustness breaks in a probabilistic setting, as shown by
Dal Lago
et al. 2014
.

This paper stems from the observation that another natural program equivalence,
Sangiorgi 1994
’s
normal form bisimilarity

(
Sangiorgi 1994
)
(shortened to nf-bisimilarity), behaves differently in call-by-name (shortened to CbN) and call-by-value (CbV), already in the untyped effect-free weak case.

Normal Form Bisimilarity

Normal form bisimulations are program equivalences that, instead of comparing terms
externally
, depending on how they behave
in contexts
, compare them
internally
, by looking at the structure of their (infinitary)
normal forms
. A distinctive feature of nf-bisimulations is that they directly manipulate
open terms
, to the point that
Sangiorgi 1994
rather used to call them
open bisimulations
in his seminal paper
(
Sangiorgi 1994
)
.

Sangiorgi’s CbN nf-bisimilarity is not fully abstract for contextual equivalence, being sound but not complete. The failure of full abstraction is compensated by the fact that CbN nf-bisimilarity is easier to establish than applicative similarity, because of the absence of quantification over arguments. Typically, it is easy to show that different fix-points combinators—which are the paradigmatic terms with infinitary normal forms—are nf-bisimilar, while it is hard to show that they are applicative bisimilar.

There exists a CbV nf-bisimilarity,
Lassen 2005a
’s
enf bisimilarity

\simeq_{\mathit{enf}}

(
Lassen 2005a
)
, which is considered the CbV nf-bisimilarity of reference. Like Sangiorgi’s, it is sound but not complete for CbV contextual equivalence
\simeq_{C}^{v}
. It is not an off-the-shelf adaptation of Sangiorgi’s to CbV, as it has an
extra clause
about evaluation contexts, exploiting the well-known issues of Plotkin’s CbV
\lambda
-calculus with open terms; issues that are discussed at length by
Accattoli and
Guerrieri 2016
;
Accattoli and
Guerrieri 2018
;
Accattoli and
Guerrieri 2022
.

The incompleteness of both CbN and CbV nf-bisimulations for the weak
\lambda
-calculus at first sight suggests that they are robust with respect to the CbN/CbV switch. In fact, they are
not
. The two bisimilarities are indeed incomplete for very different reasons.

Incompleteness of Enf.

Here are four important reasons why enf bisimilarity is incomplete.

(1)

Eta
: enf bisimilarity does not validate
\eta_{v}
, the CbV variant of
\eta
-equivalence, even if Lassen himself shows how to refine enf to correct this aspect.

(2)

CbN duplication
: enf bisimilarity does not equate duplications of terms that are not
by value
: terms such as
(\lambda x.yxx)t
and
ytt
, are contextually equivalent for any
t
, also for terms
t
that are
not
values, while in general they are not enf bisimilar. In richer CbV settings with state or probability, contexts discriminate more, and those terms are not contextually equivalent, but in the pure case they are. This aspect, referred here to as
cost-sensitiveness
, is not necessarily a drawback, as it keeps the program equivalence closer to CbV intuition.

(3)

Commutation of (independent)
\sf{let}
s
: in the pure case—or in the presence of commutative effects—contextual equivalence validates the following commutation
\equiv_{com}
(presented with
\sf{let}
s for readability, but easily presentable without them, expanding
\sf{let}
s as
\beta
-redexes):

{\sf let}\ x=u\ {\sf in}\ {\sf let}\ y=s\ {\sf in}\ t\ \ \equiv_{com}\ \ {\sf let}\ y=s\ {\sf in}\ {\sf let}\ x=u\ {\sf in}\ t
if
x\notin{\tt fv}(s)
and
y\notin{\tt fv}(u)
.

Enf bisimulations do not equate these terms.

(4)

\Omega
-terms/meaningless terms
: enf bisimilarity does not equate terms contextually equivalent to the paradigmatic looping/meaningless
\lambda
-term
\Omega
, referred here to as
\Omega
-terms
. This is connected to the mentioned issues of Plotkin’s calculus with open terms, as they cause
false normal forms
, that is,
\Omega
-terms that are normal. In CbN, instead, all
\Omega
-terms diverge.

Point 2 is acceptable, because a bisimilarity validating CbN duplication in CbV needs to go beyond comparing the structure of normal forms, as the example shows, thus necessarily departing from the format of nf-bisimilarities. Thus, we shall consider cost-sensitivity as
inherent
to CbV nf-bisimilarities, which need not be corrected.
Point 3 is specific to CbV, because in CbN
\sf{let}
s disappear during evaluation, while in CbV with open terms they might end up in normal forms. It is thus disappointing that commuting
\sf{let}
s are not captured. More precisely, it would be desirable to have a notion of nf-bisimilarity where the validation of commuting
\sf{let}
s can be added/removed modularly, as to adapt to different CbV settings (pure/(non-)commutative-effects). Point 4 is a stark difference between CbV and CbN, because Sangiorgi’s bisimilarity
does equate
the
\Omega
-terms of weak CbN.

\Omega
-terms

The failure of enf bisimilarity with respect to
\Omega
-terms is relevant, as all natural denotational models and program equivalences identify
\Omega
-terms (of the modelled notion of evaluation). Roughly, different semantics are built by distinguishing various partitions of non-
\Omega
-terms, but they put all terms contextually equivalent to the idle loop
\Omega
in the same class—indeed, what would be the advantage of partitioning them in classes? For strong CbN evaluation, in particular, the equational identification of
\Omega
-terms is the concept around which
Barendregt 1984
’s classic book
(
Barendregt 1984
)
is built (therein
\Omega
-terms are called
unsolvable terms
, see Sect.
2
).

Two Possible Approaches.

To overcome some of the mentioned incompleteness via a nf-bisimilarity, one can act on two different levels:
changing the underlying calculus
, which provides the normal forms to compare, or
changing the nf-bisimilarity
, that is, the notion of comparison. For instance,
Biernacki
et al. 2019a
address the problem of
\Omega
-terms (called therein
deferred diverging terms
) by providing an alternative definition of enf bisimilarity. Commuting
\sf{let}
s, however, do not seem to be capturable by a similar tweak of enf-bisimilarity, as the commutation does not behave well in Plotkin’s CbV calculus. At first sight, changing the underlying calculus is less viable, as in general it changes the notion of contextual equivalence.

The Value Substitution Calculus.

The starting point of this paper is the
value substitution calculus
(shortened to VSC), a CbV
\lambda
-calculus due to
Accattoli and
Paolini 2012
and related to linear logic proof nets
(
Accattoli 2015
)
. The VSC solves the issues of Plotkin’s calculus with open terms via an extension of the rewriting rules, while–crucially–retaining the same notion of contextual equivalence
(
Accattoli and
Guerrieri 2022
)
. Moreover, the proof nets inception of the calculus makes it easy to deal with the commutation of
\sf{let}
s, which can be modularly added via a notion of
structural equivalence
, compatible with the rewriting rules by design. Therefore, the VSC is a natural candidate for designing a CbV nf-bisimilarity improving on some of the incompleteness of Lassen’s by changing the underlying calculus.

Net Bisimilarity.

Using the VSC, we introduce a CbV nf-bisimilarity validating the commutation of
\sf{let}
s and identifying
\Omega
-terms. The obtained
net bisimilarity

\simeq_{\mathit{net}}
and the proof of its
compatibility
(that is, its stability by context closure)—the challenging property to prove for bisimilarities—are the main contributions of this paper. Compatibility implies soundness with respect to contextual equivalence, and it is proved adapting
Lassen 1999
’s variant
(
Lassen 1999
)
for nf-bisimilarities of Howe’s method. As it is often the case for nf-bisimilarities, ours is sound but not complete. In particular—as for enf bisimilarity—it is
cost-sensitive
.

The crafting of net bisimilarity rests on a sophisticated analysis of CbV and the VSC. We start with the off-the-shelf adaptation of Sangiorgi’s nf-bisimilarity to the VSC,
without Lassen’s extra clause
, as it is unclear how to adapt the clause to the VSC. We then refine the adaptation by comparing normal forms modulo the (proof nets) structural equivalence of the VSC—which includes commuting
\sf{let}
s—whence the name
net
bisimilarity. We actually go further, introducing a
parametric
nf-bisimilarity, where parts of the structural equivalences can be turned off and on at will—because some (such as commuting
\sf{let}
s) fail in extensions of CbV with non-commutative effects—thus defining a
family
of CbV nf-bisimilarities, all proved compatible via a
single abstract proof
.

Our result is however more a new beginning than the end of the story: net bisimilarity, indeed, is
not
a refinement of Lassen’s
\simeq_{\mathit{enf}}
. In fact, the two are
incomparable
, because net bisimilarity is incomplete in yet some other ways, which are instead validated by Lassen’s.

Moggi’s Laws

It is well-known that Plotkin’s CbV
\lambda
-calculus is defective, and not just because of open terms. Plotkin himself showed the incompleteness of his continuation-passing translation
(
Plotkin 1975
)
. To both solve the issue and modeling extensions with effects, Moggi extended Plotkin’s calculus with equations corresponding to laws for monads
(
Moggi 1988
;
Moggi 1989
)
, that are sound for contextual equivalence. Lassen’s enf bisimulations verify these laws, showing that it is possible to capture the laws in the notion of nf-bisimulation rather than by changing the underlying calculus. In particular, it is Lassen’s extra clause that allows enf to capture Moggi’s laws.

Moggi’s laws, however, are not rules of the VSC, and are not captured by net bisimilarity. Additionally, it is unclear how to extend net bisimulations as to satisfy Moggi’s laws. Once more, there are two options: extending the underlying calculus (the VSC) or the nf-bisimilarity (
\simeq_{\mathit{net}}
). Both options however break properties that are crucial for the proof that net bisimilarity is compatible. Another disappointing fact is that the addition of
\eta_{v}
to net bisimilarity requires one of Moggi’s laws, so it is also unclear how to add
\eta_{v}
to net bisimulations.

Impasse and Beyond

Summing up, it seems that one cannot have the cake (a framework for nf-bisimilarity satisfying either commuting
\sf{let}
s and
\Omega
-terms, or Moggi’s laws and
\eta_{v}
) and eat it too (extend the framework as to capture the missing half), or at least it is far from evident how to do it. The exploration of such an
impasse
is the other main contribution of the paper. We study two further program equivalences, a naive bisimilarity
\simeq_{nai}
and the program equivalence
\simeq_{type}
induced by a model, which are sort of the intersection and the union of enf and net bisimilarities.

Naive Bisimilarity
\simeq_{nai}
= No Cake and No Eating

We consider the off-the-shelf adaptation of Sangiorgi’s CbN nf-bisimulations to
Plotkin’s CbV, obtaining
naive nf-bisimulations
, which are strictly weaker than both enf and net bisimulations, as they do not identify commuting
\sf{let}
s,
\Omega
-terms, Moggi’s laws, nor
\eta_{v}
. The experiment is instructive because we show that naive bisimulations, despite their weakness, are enough to provide easy proofs of bisimilarity for fix-point combinators—Lassen’s extra clause plays no role in that. Naive bisimilarity also gives us the opportunity to gently introduce the proof technique that we use for net bisimilarity.

Type Equivalence
\simeq_{type}
= Cake and Eating, Universally

At the other end of the spectrum, we investigate the program equivalence given by the equational theory of Ehrhard’s CbV relational model
(
Ehrhard 2012
)
. We call it
type equivalence
because the model is presented as a multi type system (a variant of intersection types). Such a model was already extensively studied in connection with the VSC by
Accattoli and
Guerrieri 2018
;
Accattoli and
Guerrieri 2022
. Its equational theory does not have a presentation via nf-bisimulations, nor any other characterization, but it is nonetheless possible to study it via the multi type system. It turns out that type equivalence, similarly to nf-bisimilarities, is compatible and sound, but not complete for contextual equivalence, because it is cost-sensitive. It is not an easily usable equivalence, as it is based on a
universal
quantification over the typings for a term, but it provides interesting insights.

Our results are that both enf and net bisimilarities are
included
in type equivalence. Therefore, the two bisimilarities are joinable. Since both are sound, they are obviously joinable in a cost-insensitive setting, as they are both included in contextual equivalence. Our results show that they are also joinable in a
cost-sensitive
program equivalence, thus suggesting that a nf-bisimilarity joining the two might be possible. Crafting it, and especially proving that it is compatible, is left to future work.

2.
Preliminaries

Contexts

All along the
paper we use (many notions of)
contexts
,
i.e.
terms with exactly one hole, noted
\langle\cdot\rangle
. Plugging a term
t
in a context
C
, noted
C\langle t\rangle
, possibly captures free variables of
t
. For instance
(\lambda x.\langle\cdot\rangle)\langle x\rangle=\lambda x.x
, while
(\lambda x.y)\{y{\shortleftarrow}x\}=\lambda z.x
.

Preorders

We shall mostly deal with simulations, rather than bisimulations, since the result for equivalences shall always follow by simply considering the symmetric notions. Given a preorder/similarity
\precsim_{X}
for some
X
, we denote with
\simeq_{X}
the corresponding equivalence/bisimilarity.

(In)Equational Theories and Compatibility

Good program preorder/equivalences are
(in)equational theories
, that is, they contain the reduction of the calculus and they are
compatible
, defined as: if
t\precsim t^{\prime}
then
C\langle t\rangle\precsim C\langle t^{\prime}\rangle
for all contexts
C
, that is, that they are stable by context closure. Reduction is usually trivially included in similarities while compatibility is usually non-trivial to prove.

Contextual Equivalence

The standard of reference for program equivalences is contextual equivalence, that can be defined abstractly as follows.

Definition 2.1 (Contextual Preorder and Equivalence).

Given a language of terms
\mathcal{T}
with its associated notion of contexts
C
and predicate stating the termination of evaluation
\Downarrow_{e}
, we define the associated
contextual preorder

\precsim_{\mathcal{C}}^{e}
and
contextual equivalence

\simeq_{\mathcal{C}}^{e}
as follows:

•

t\precsim_{\mathcal{C}}^{e}t^{\prime}
if
C\langle t\rangle~{\Downarrow_{e}}
implies
C\langle t^{\prime}\rangle~{\Downarrow_{e}}
for all contexts
C
such that
C\langle t\rangle
and
C\langle t^{\prime}\rangle
are closed terms.

•

t\simeq_{\mathcal{C}}^{e}t^{\prime}
is the equivalence relation induced by
\precsim_{C}
, that is,
t\simeq_{\mathcal{C}}^{e}t^{\prime}\iff t\precsim_{\mathcal{C}}^{e}t^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime}\precsim_{\mathcal{C}}^{e}t
.

A relation
{\mathcal{R}}
is sound for the contextual preorder
\precsim_{\mathcal{C}}^{e}
when
{\mathcal{R}}\subseteq\precsim_{\mathcal{C}}^{e}
. Soundness follows from compatibility and
adequacy for
\Downarrow_{e}
, defined as: if
t~{\mathcal{R}}~t^{\prime}
then
t\Downarrow_{e}
implies
t^{\prime}\Downarrow_{e}
.

Proposition 2.2.

Let
\precsim
be a compatible and adequate preorder. Then
\precsim\subseteq\precsim_{\mathcal{C}}^{e}
.

Proof.

Suppose
u\precsim u^{\prime}
.
Let
C
any closing context for
u
and
u^{\prime}
.
By compatibility,
C\langle u\rangle\precsim C\langle u^{\prime}\rangle
.
By adequacy,
C\langle u\rangle\Downarrow_{e}
implies
C\langle u^{\prime}\rangle\Downarrow_{e}
, that is,
u\precsim_{\mathcal{C}}^{e}u^{\prime}
.
∎

We shall see that normal form simulations are defined in such a way that they are adequate, so that soundness follows directly from compatibility. Note that soundness without compatibility is useless: the relation
{\mathcal{R}}\coloneqq\{(xy,xy)\}
is sound but not compatible.

Diamond

A rewriting notion that shall play a role is the
diamond property
, which is the following one-step strengthening of confluence for a reduction
\to
: if
t\to u_{1}
,
t\to u_{2}
, and
u_{1}\neq u_{2}
then exists
s
such that
u_{1}\to s
and
u_{2}\to s
. Some well-known facts: the diamond property implies confluence but not vice-versa; if
\to
is diamond and there is a terminating reduction from
t
then there are no diverging reductions from
t
; all reductions to normal form, if any, have the same length. Roughly, the diamond property is a relaxed form of determinism, where non-deterministic choices have no impact on the result nor on the length of the evaluation leading to it.

\Omega
-terms, Solvability, and Scrutability

A cornerstone of the theory of the (CbN)
\lambda
-calculus is the study of what here we call
\Omega
-terms
, that is, terms that are contextually equivalent to
\Omega
, the paradigmatic looping term. Such a notion has been extensively studied for
head
(CbN) contextual equivalence, for which
\Omega
-terms are better known as
unsolvable terms
, and often labeled as
meaningless terms
. Actually, the definition of unsolvable term (here omitted) is different, as it does not mention
\Omega
nor contextual equivalence, but equivalent to the one of
\Omega
-term. Equational theories that identify all unsolvable terms were first studied by
Wadsworth 1971
;
Wadsworth 1976
and
Barendregt 1971
;
Barendregt 1977
, and it is the leading theme of
Barendregt 1984
’s book
(
Barendregt 1984
)
. Unsolvable terms can be characterized as those terms that diverge with respect to head reduction, as proved by Wadsworth.

Adopting
weak
head (CbN) contextually equivalence gives a different set of
\Omega
-terms. As for the head case, these
\Omega
-terms coincide with a natural set of terms defined without mentioning
\Omega
, namely
(CbN) inscrutable terms
(for the definition see Appendix
C
(p.
C
)), first studied by
Ronchi
Della Rocca and Paolini 2004
(under the name
not potentially valuable terms
), who provide the following useful characterization, akin to Wadsworth’s for unsolvable terms.

Theorem 2.3 (Diverging characterization of CbN scrutability).

A term
t
is CbN inscrutable (thus a weak CbN
\Omega
-term) if and only if the weak head reduction of
t
diverges.

For instance,
\Omega
is inscrutable but
\lambda x.\Omega
and
x\Omega
are not (while
\lambda x.\Omega
is unsolvable). From the characterization, it follows that every inscrutable term is unsolvable, but not vice-versa.

In CbV, there are analogous notions of unsolvable and inscrutable terms, but they lack analogous
diverging characterizations
in Plotkin’s CbV
\lambda
-calculus. They have been thoroughly studied by
Accattoli and
Guerrieri 2022
in the VSC, where instead they admit diverging characterizations. Surprisingly, an equational theory identifying CbV unsolvable terms is necessarily inconsistent. The right notion of meaningless term in CbV is actually given by CbV inscrutable terms. In Appendix
C
(p.
C
), one can find the technical definition of CbV inscrutable terms as well as the proof that they coincide with CbV
\Omega
-terms (relying on the VSC introduced in Sect.
9
).

In this paper, we shall not consider unsolvable terms, as we only consider
weak
reductions. Thus, CbN/CbV
\Omega
-terms
shall always be relative to weak CbN/CbV contextually equivalence, and coinciding with CbN/CbV inscrutable terms.

3.
Background About Normal Form Bisimulations

Normal form bisimulations are program equivalences that, instead of comparing terms
externally
, depending on how they behave
in contexts
, they compare them
internally
, by looking at the structure of their (infinitary)
normal forms
. Let us give the simplest possible example.

Let
\rightarrow_{wh}
be weak head reduction, also known as CbN evaluation, which is a deterministic reduction and it is defined as
(\lambda x.t)us_{1}\ldots s_{k}\rightarrow_{wh}t\{x{\shortleftarrow}u\}s_{1}\ldots s_{k}
with
k\geq 0
. Normal form simulations are usually based on a big-step presentation of
\rightarrow_{wh}
: we write
t\Downarrow_{wh}\,u
if the
\rightarrow_{wh}
-evaluation of
t
terminates on
u
, and
t\not\Downarrow_{wh}\,
otherwise.
The following notion of simulation was first considered by
Sangiorgi 1994
. Our presentation is slightly different but equivalent.

Partiality and Divergence.

Nf-simulations rely on a partial notion of evaluation (with respect to full

Checking that they have the same structure for the partially evaluated part of the term, and

•

Asking that the non-evaluated sub-terms of

The use of a partial notion of evaluation is crucial, as it allows fine discriminations related to divergence, which would be blurred if one would only consider full normal forms. CbN nf-similarity, indeed, discriminates between the following forms of divergence:

(3)

Open Terms.

Nf-simulations are different from most other notions of program equivalence in that they have to deal with open terms, because, even when the terms to compare are closed, the sub-terms on which the comparison is iterated might be open.

4.
Plotkin’s Call-by-Value and Open Terms

Here we recall Plotkin’s CbV
\lambda
-calculus, and pay attention to some aspects that are often neglected, as they shall be relevant in the following sections.

The CbV
\lambda
-Calculus.

A
value

v
is a variable or an abstractions. At the rewriting level, we consider only weak evaluation, that is, out of abstractions. We define it in three variants, from left to right, from right to left, and in an unspecified order, which are discussed next.

\begin{array}[]{r@{\hspace{.4cm}}rlll}\textsc{Terms}\hskip 11.38092pt&t,u,s&\Coloneqq&v\mid tu\\
\textsc{Values}\hskip 11.38092pt&v&\Coloneqq&x\mid\lambda x.t\par\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{r@{\hspace{.4cm}}rlll}\textsc{(General) Contexts}\hskip 11.38092pt&C&\Coloneqq&\langle\cdot\rangle\mid tC\mid Ct\mid\lambda x.C\\
\textsc{Weak Contexts}\hskip 11.38092pt&W&\Coloneqq&\langle\cdot\rangle\mid tW\mid Wt\\
\textsc{Left Contexts}\hskip 11.38092pt&L&\Coloneqq&\langle\cdot\rangle\mid vL\mid Lt\\
\textsc{Right Contexts}\hskip 11.38092pt&R&\Coloneqq&\langle\cdot\rangle\mid tR\mid Rv\end{array}

Definition 4.1 (Reductions).

Let root
{\beta_{v}}
reduction
\mapsto_{{\beta_{v}}}
defined as
(\lambda x.t)v\mapsto_{{\beta_{v}}}t\{x{\shortleftarrow}v\}
. Then

{\beta_{v}}
, weak, left (to right), and right (to left) reduction, noted
\rightarrow_{{\beta_{v}}}
,
\rightarrow_{w}
,
\rightarrow_{l}
, and
\rightarrow_{r}
, are defined as follows: if
t\mapsto_{{\beta_{v}}}u
then

\begin{array}[]{c@{\hspace{1cm}}c@{\hspace{1cm}}c@{\hspace{1cm}}c}C\langle t\rangle\rightarrow_{{\beta_{v}}}C\langle u\rangle\hfil\hskip 28.45274pt&W\langle t\rangle\rightarrow_{w}W\langle u\rangle\hfil\hskip 28.45274pt&L\langle t\rangle\rightarrow_{l}L\langle u\rangle\hfil\hskip 28.45274pt&R\langle t\rangle\rightarrow_{r}R\langle u\rangle\end{array}

It is standard that
\rightarrow_{l}
and
\rightarrow_{r}
are deterministic and contained in
\rightarrow_{w}
, which is non-deterministic but diamond, while
\rightarrow_{{\beta_{v}}}
is non-deterministic and confluent but not diamond.

Closed Terms.

If terms are closed and evaluation is weak, which are a standard assumption in the study of functional languages, then call-by-value evaluation has some very nice properties, summed up by the next proposition.

Proposition 4.2.

Let
t
be a closed term.

(1)

t
is a weak/left/right normal form if and only if
t
is an abstraction.

(2)

On
t
, left and right reduction are full with respect to weak evaluation, that is, if
t\rightarrow_{w}u
then there exist
s
and
r
such that
t\rightarrow_{l}s
and
t\rightarrow_{r}r
.

Intuitively, left and right reduction are two equivalent ways of turning weak reduction into a deterministic reduction, on closed terms.
In CbV, contextual preorder
\precsim_{C}^{v}
is defined with respect to evaluation to a value, which can be equivalently expressed as termination of weak, left, or right reduction. Importantly, for
\precsim_{C}^{v}
one considers only the reduction of closed terms, as the definition of
\precsim_{C}^{v}
is based on contexts closing the terms to compare.

Open Terms, Stuck Redexes, and The Inequivalence of the Three Strategies.

As soon as one considers open terms, the good properties of the system break. The only ones which survive are the diamond property of weak reduction and the fact that left and right reduction are deterministic. But weak normal forms now have a complex shape, as there can be
stuck redexes
such as
(\lambda x.t)(yy)
which cannot be reduced because their argument is normal and not a value.

Stuck redexes unfortunately break the equivalence of left, right, and weak reduction: left, right, and weak normal forms are all different notions, because left and right reduction are no longer full with respect to weak reduction. For instance,
t\coloneqq xx(\mathtt{I}\mathtt{I})
is a left normal form which is not a weak/right normal form, because
t\rightarrow_{w}xx\mathtt{I}
, which is weak/right normal. Similarly,
\mathtt{I}\mathtt{I}(xx)
is a right normal form which is not left/weak normal, and
xx(\mathtt{I}\mathtt{I})(xx)
is a left/right normal form which is not weak normal. Perhaps more worrying is the fact that stuck redexes introduce suspicious distinctions between contextually equivalent terms:
\Omega
and
\Omega^{L}\coloneqq(\lambda x.\delta)(yz)\delta
are contextually equivalent, but the first one diverges while the second one is normal, because of the stuck redex.

The CbV translation of

(1)

Effects
: commutativity holds in the pure CbV setting but it often fails in extensions of CbV with effects, because many effects are order-dependent (think of the order of writes on a memory cell). Therefore, it is an equivalence that one might want to be able to modularly add or remove from a notion of bisimilarity, rather than always validate it.

(2)

Unorientable
: being symmetric, commutativity cannot be oriented as a rewriting rule. Therefore, any nf-bisimulation validating it needs to be able to compare normal forms up to some deformation of terms.

The reason why some equivalences at times appear in restricted forms is also related to effects. With non-commutative effects one has to fix a deterministic evaluation strategy, typically left-to-right, and this constrains the shape of equivalences forcing a sub-term to be a value

(resp.

(resp.

), and adopting a non-specified order induces the extended versions of Moggi’s laws and the unrestricted version of the proof nets equivalences.

CbN Duplication and CbN Erasure

The last equivalences that we consider are CbN duplication and CbN erasure. We do not actually know how to characterize CbN duplication independently of erasure with an axiom, or a set of axioms, but we discuss a specific case, to illustrate the idea.

•

CbN Duplication
:
(\lambda x.yxx)u\equiv_{dup}yuu
;

•

CbN Erasure
:
(\lambda x.t)u\equiv_{era}t
with
x\notin{\tt fv}(t)
.

In CbV, duplication and erasure are included in
{\beta_{v}}
-conversion if
u
is a value, but not otherwise. For arbitrary terms, erasure is unsound in CbV, because erasing a sub-term might turn divergence into termination: for instance
t\coloneqq(\lambda x.z)(yy)
is not contextually equivalent to

in CbV, because
C\langle t\rangle
diverges while
C\langle u\rangle
terminates with respect to the context
C\coloneqq(\lambda y.\langle\cdot\rangle)\delta
.

Duplication, instead, is sound for arbitrary terms, the idea being that terminating (resp. diverging) once, or terminating (resp. diverging) twice does not affect termination (resp. divergence). It is however a principle somewhat clashing with the nature of CbV. The cornerstone of CbV is the idea that one should reduce arguments
before
substituting them, and CbN duplication does exactly the opposite. Intuitively, a CbV program equivalence validating CbN duplication is
qualitative
or
cost-insensitive
, as it only observes termination, while one rejecting it is somehow
cost-sensitive
, as it distinguishes between differently efficient ways of realizing the same qualitative behavior.

Root rewriting rules

In VSC,

In

Examples:

A key point is that

-redexes, because only values can be substituted.
The multiplicative rule

The CbV discipline is entirely encoded in the exponential rule

CbV is about

Equational Benchmarks and the Value Substitution Calculus

Structural Equivalence

The translation of the VSC to proof nets maps some terms with ES to the same proof net. The induced identification of terms is expressed by structural equivalence
(
Accattoli and
Paolini 2012
;
Accattoli 2015
)
.

Definition 10.1 (Structural equivalence
\equiv_{str}
).

Structural equivalence
\equiv_{str}
is defined as the smallest compatible equivalence relation generated by union of the following root rules.

\begin{array}[]{rllrcc}(ts)[x{\shortleftarrow}u]&\equiv_{@l}&t[x{\shortleftarrow}u]s&\mbox{if }x\not\in{\tt fv}(s)\\
(ts)[x{\shortleftarrow}u]&\equiv_{@r}&ts[x{\shortleftarrow}u]&\mbox{if }x\not\in{\tt fv}(t)\\
t[x{\shortleftarrow}u][y{\shortleftarrow}s]&\equiv_{ass}&t[x{\shortleftarrow}u[y{\shortleftarrow}s]]&\mbox{if }y\not\in{\tt fv}(t)\\
t[y{\shortleftarrow}s][x{\shortleftarrow}u]&\equiv_{com}&t[x{\shortleftarrow}u][y{\shortleftarrow}s]&\mbox{if }x\not\in{\tt fv}(s)\mbox{ and }y\not\in{\tt fv}(u)\end{array}

These axioms preserve the number and type of the constructors in terms, they only rearrange the order. In particular, structurally equivalent terms have the same number of ES. Note that the axioms simply express the constructor-and-scope-preserving commutation of ES with applications and ES themselves (but not abstractions, as that would break Prop.
10.2
below).

Additionally, structural equivalence behaves very well with respect to evaluation: it commutes with reduction rules—and is therefore postponable—preserving the number and kind of steps. This is expressed by the following proposition. In the literature, what is below called
strong commutation
is usually called
strong bisimulation
. We prefer to change the terminology here to avoid confusion with
nf-(bi)simulations
, as the concept is similar and yet different (no need to observe normal forms, and it preserves the number of steps).

{toappendix}

Proposition 10.2 (
\equiv_{str}
strongly commutes with
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{vsc}}
,
Accattoli and
Paolini 2012
).

Let
a\in\{{\mathtt{m}},\mathsf{e}_{\lambda},\mathsf{e}_{y}\}
. Structural equivalence
\equiv_{str}
strongly commutes with
\rightarrow_{\mathtt{vsc}}
:
if
t\equiv_{str}u
and
t\rightarrow_{a}t^{\prime}
then
u\rightarrow_{a}u^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime}\equiv_{str}u^{\prime}
.

In fact,
\equiv_{str}
-equivalence classes are an isomorphic representation of proof nets, as the proof net
P_{t}
associated to
t
does the same exact rewriting steps as
t
, that is, the translation from
t
to
P_{t}
also strongly commutes with evaluation (turning term steps into proof nets steps, and vice-versa), see
Accattoli 2015
. Consequently,
\equiv_{str}
-equivalent terms are
indistinguishable
and should be equated by any sensible notion of program equivalence on pure terms (extensions with effects can invalidate some cases of structural equivalence, typically
\equiv_{com}

Revisiting the Benchmarks From Calculi

The equivalences of Moggi’s can be re-understood via structural equivalence. The idea is that by applying

13.
Conclusions

Motivated by the fact that Lassen’s enf bisimilarity
\simeq_{\mathit{enf}}
—the normal form bisimilarity of reference in CbV—does not identify
\Omega
-terms and commuting
\sf{let}
s, we introduced
net bisimilarity

\simeq_{\mathit{net}}
, which does identify them. It turns out, however, that
\simeq_{\mathit{net}}
does not validate Moggi’s laws nor
\eta_{v}
, which are instead validated by
\simeq_{\mathit{enf}}
. Additionally, it is unclear how to extend either enf or net bisimilarity as to catch the other one.

Such a problematic duality led us to develop a sharp analysis of CbV and of the principles that can be validated or not by normal form bisimulations. The analysis shows that the semantic landscape of CbV is considerably richer and more sophisticated than the CbN one.

Concretely, our analysis contributed two further equivalences. First, a naive bisimilarity
\simeq_{nai}
, that mainly provides a better understanding of Lassen’s tricky definition of enf simulations. Second, the type equivalence
\simeq_{type}
induced by Ehrhard’s multi types, which subsumes both enf and net bisimilarity, and includes
\eta_{v}
-equivalence, while retaining their cost-sensitive aspect. In practice,
\simeq_{type}
is not really usable for comparing programs, but it provides a sharp theoretical tool.

Future Work

Type equivalence suggests that it could be possible to find a normal form bisimilarity merging the enf and net ones. We are actively working on this challenging problem. A related question is finding an axiomatization of
\simeq_{type}
, for which some sort of separation theorem should be developed.
We would also like to investigate how net bisimilarity
\simeq_{\mathit{net}}
relates to the topics connected to
\simeq_{\mathit{enf}}
, such as game semantics
(
Jaber and
Murawski 2021
)
, extensions with effects
(
Dal Lago and
Gavazzo 2019
;
Biernacki
et al. 2019a
;
Biernacki
et al. 2020
)
, and the
\pi
-calculus
(
Durier
et al. 2022
)
.
