Dipoles in blackbody radiation: Momentum fluctuations, decoherence, and drag force
Kanupriya Sinha, Peter W. Milonni

Abstract
A general expression is derived for the momentum diffusion constant of a small polarizable particle in blackbody radiation, and is shown to be closely related to the long-wavelength collisional decoherence rate for such a particle in a thermal environment. We show how this diffusion constant appears in the steady-state photon emission rate of two dipoles induced by blackbody radiation. We consider in addition the Einstein–Hopf drag force on a small polarizable particle moving in a blackbody field, and derive its fully relativistic form from the Lorentz transformation of forces.



I
Introduction

One of the ways in which the quantized nature of the electromagnetic field is manifested is in the recoil of particles in scattering, absorption, and emission processes
shore
. In spontaneous emission, for example, a classical treatment of the field would not account for the recoil of an atom because the field from the atom would have zero linear momentum, a consequence of the inversion symmetry of the field about the atom. Einstein
ein1
showed that atoms in an isotropic field with spectral energy density
\rho(\omega)
undergo a mean-square momentum gain resulting from the recoil momentum of magnitude
\hbar\omega/c
in each emission and absorption event. Assuming the atoms have a mean kinetic energy
\left\langle\frac{1}{2}m{\bf v}^{2}\right\rangle=\frac{3}{2}k_{B}T
in equilibrium, this mean-square momentum increase is compensated by a drag force in just such a way as to yield the Planck distribution for
\rho(\omega)
. In earlier work, Einstein and Hopf
eh1
;
eh2
obtained classical expressions for the variance of the fluctuating momentum and the drag force on a small polarizable particle in blackbody radiation and used these expressions to derive the Rayleigh–Jeans spectrum. In this paper we derive a simple quantum-mechanical generalization of their expression for the momentum variance and diffusion constant. A similar generalization of the Einstein–Hopf formula for the drag force was obtained earlier
mkr
. We show that, not surprisingly, these two generalizations of Einstein’s expressions can be used to extend Einstein’s analysis for atoms
ein1
to any small polarizable particle in thermal equilibrium with radiation.

The expression we obtain in the Heisenberg picure for the momentum diffusion constant is an ensemble average over random momentum kicks. We show that this momentum diffusion constant appears also in the rate of photon emission from two localized dipoles in blackbody radiation, and has the same form as the center-of-mass decoherence rate of a particle in a thermal photon environment. Such blackbody-induced decoherence was previously analyzed in a scattering-theory framework
joos
, wherein which-path information carried by the thermal photons scattering off the particle causes the particle’s center of mass to decohere and localize in the position basis. If the characteristic thermal wavelength
\lambda_{\mathrm{th}}
of the electromagnetic environment is larger than the coherence length
\Delta x
, each scattered thermal photon only gains partial information about the particle’s position (‘long-wavelength limit’); for shorter wavelengths a single scatterer has sufficient information to resolve a coherent superposition (‘short-wavelength limit’). It will be shown that the momentum diffusion constant is related specifically to the long-wavelength limit of the blackbody-induced center-of-mass decoherence rate.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
II
we calculate the momentum variance and diffusion constant based first on a model in which the particle experiences random, instantaneous, and statistically independent momentum kicks. This is followed by a more rigorous calculation which is simplified by treating the blackbody field at first as a classical stochastic field as in the original Einstein–Hopf theory
eh1
;
eh2
. Following Einstein and Hopf we treat the electric field and its spatial derivative as independent stochastic processes; a proof of this independence is given in Appendix
A
. In Section
III
we calculate the photon emission rate for two dipoles in blackbody radiation. We show how the reduction with the dipole separation of an interference term in this rate involves the diffusion constant. In Section
IV
we derive the relativistic form of the drag force on a moving particle in blackbody radiation directly from the Lorentz transformation of forces. We discuss in Section
V
some aspects of our calculations as they relate to previous work, and conclude with remarks relating to Einstein’s derivation of the Planck spectrum and the applicability of our results to atoms in blackbody radiation. Appendix
B
gives a brief derivation of the momentum diffusion constant due to scattering of air molecules and the corresponding center-of-mass decoherence rate.

It might be worth noting that while Campbell’s theorem does not account for Bose-Einstein statistics, it can be used, as shown in Appendix
B

IV
Drag Force

As noted in the Introduction, Einstein and Hopf
eh1
;
eh2
and later Einstein
ein1
showed that there is a drag force
F=-\xi mv
on a polarizable particle moving with velocity
v
(
v\ll c
) with respect to a frame in which there is an isotropic (blackbody) field of spectral energy density
\rho(\omega)
. A more general expression for
\xi
was derived much later
mkr
, and more recently the drag force has been derived for relativistic particles
dedkov
;
pieplow
;
volo
;
kim
. We now derive the relativistic form of the drag force based in part on “energy kicks”
\hbar\omega
, in the same heuristic spirit as in the treatment of momentum kicks in Section
II.1
. The particle is assumed to have a uniform velocity
v
along the
x
axis in the laboratory frame S, and we are interested in the force
F_{x}
on the particle in this frame. The Lorentz transformations relating the spacetime coordinates and the momenta

(p_{x},p_{x}^{\prime}
) and energies (
\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
) in the laboratory frame and the particle’s rest frame S
′
are

\displaystyle x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma(x-vt),

(61)

\displaystyle t^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma\left(t-\frac{vx}{c^{2}}\right),

(62)

\displaystyle p_{x}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma\left(p_{x}-\frac{v}{c^{2}}\mathcal{E}\right),

(63)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma\left(\mathcal{E}-vp_{x}\right),

(64)

where
dx/dt=v
and as usual
\gamma=(1-v^{2}/c^{2})^{-1/2}
. These transformations relate the force
F_{x}
in S to the force
F_{x}^{\prime}
in S
′
:

F_{x}=\frac{dp_{x}}{dt}=\frac{d}{dt^{\prime}}\left[\gamma\left(p_{x}^{\prime}+\frac{v}{c^{2}}\mathcal{E}^{\prime}\right)\right]\frac{dt^{\prime}}{dt}=\frac{dp_{x}^{\prime}}{dt^{\prime}}+\frac{v}{c^{2}}\frac{d\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}{dt^{\prime}}=F_{x}^{\prime}+\frac{v}{c^{2}}\frac{d\mathcal{E}^{\prime}}{dt^{\prime}}.

(65)

The force on the particle has two contributions,
F_{\rm ind}
resulting from the dipole moment induced by the fluctuating blackbody field, and
F_{\rm dd}
resulting from the dipole’s own radiation field and fluctuations
dedkov
;
pieplow
;
volo
;
kim
.
F_{\rm ind}
is the force originally considered by Einstein and Hopf
ein1
;
eh1
;
eh2
, while
F_{\rm dd}
is needed to obtain the fully relativistic expression for the force that has been of interest in recent work
dedkov
;
pieplow
;
volo
;
kim
.

IV.1
Calculation of the force

In Section

we calculated the force when the electric field is along a single direction
z
and then exploited the isotropy of the field to include the contributions from all three components of the field. The situation is more complicated for a particle in motion because in its reference frame (S

) the fields are different in different directions and are related to those in S by

\displaystyle E_{x}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{x}({\bf x},t),

\displaystyle E_{y}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma\left[E_{y}({\bf x},t)-\frac{v}{c}B_{z}({\bf x},t)\right],

\displaystyle E_{z}^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma\left[E_{z}({\bf x},t)+\frac{v}{c}B_{y}({\bf x},t)\right],

(66)

along with the corresponding transformations for the magnetic fields. The force

d_{0}
(

\displaystyle E^{\prime}({\bf x}^{\prime},t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle E_{x}({\bf x},t)=i\left(\frac{2\pi\hbar\omega}{V}\right)^{1/2}\left[a_{{\bf k}\lambda}e^{-i(\omega t-{\bf k}\cdot{\bf x})}-{a}^{{\dagger}}_{{\bf k}\lambda}e^{i(\omega t-{\bf k}\cdot{\bf x})}\right]e_{{\bf k}\lambda x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i\left(\frac{2\pi\hbar\omega}{V}\right)^{1/2}\left[a_{{\bf k}\lambda}e^{-i(\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}t^{\prime}-{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf x}^{\prime})}-{a}^{{\dagger}}_{{\bf k}\lambda}e^{i(\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}}t^{\prime}-{\bf k}^{\prime}\cdot{\bf x}^{\prime})}\right]e_{{\bf k}\lambda x}.

We have used the fact that

\displaystyle\omega{{}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma(\omega-vk_{x}),

\displaystyle k_{x}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\gamma\left(k_{x}-\frac{v}{c^{2}}\omega\right),\ \ \ k_{y}^{\prime}=k_{y},\ \ \ k_{z}^{\prime}=k_{z}.

since

where we have defined

from all field modes:

\displaystyle[F^{\prime}_{\rm ind}]_{xx}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{V}{8\pi^{3}}\int d^{3}k\left(\frac{4\pi\hbar\omega}{V}\right)k_{x}^{\prime}\alpha_{I}(\omega_{{\bf k}^{\prime}})n(\omega)\sum_{\lambda=1}^{2}e^{2}_{{\bf k}\lambda x}

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar c}{2\pi^{2}}\int d^{3}kkk_{x}\left(1-\frac{k_{x}^{2}}{k^{2}}+1-\frac{k_{y}^{2}}{k^{2}}+1-\frac{k_{z}^{2}}{k^{2}}\right)\alpha_{I}(\omega)n(\gamma(\omega+vk_{x}))

where

n(\gamma(\omega+vk_{x}))\cong n(\omega)+vk_{x}\frac{dn}{d\omega}=n(\omega)-vk_{x}\frac{3\pi^{2}c^{3}}{\hbar\omega^{4}}\left[\rho(\omega)-\frac{\omega}{3}\frac{d\rho}{d\omega}\right],

which follows from Eq. (

(82)

for

QObook
, the preferential emission into forward modes results in a friction force due to recoil in the opposite direction.

One can consider the thermal drag for the previous examples of an electron and a dielectric sphere. The non-relativistic thermal drag coefficient for an electron in blackbody radiation can be obtained from Eq. (

\displaystyle\xi_{e}=\frac{32\pi^{3}\hbar}{135m_{e}}\left(\frac{e^{2}}{m_{e}c^{2}}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{k_{B}T}{\hbar c}\right)^{4}.

(83)

Similarly, the non-relativistic thermal drag on a dielectric sphere is found from Eq. (

V
Discussion

The average photon number
n(\omega)
in thermal equilibrium appears differently in the momentum diffusion constant, the decoherence rate, and the drag force. This is because the momentum diffusion constant has a fourth-order dependence on the electric field, whereas the decoherence rate and drag force have a second-order dependence.

The momentum diffusion constant we have obtained for a small polarizable particle in blackbody radiation, for example, involves the factor
n^{2}(\omega)+n(\omega)
. The two contributions correspond respectively to the “wave” and “particle” contributions to the Einstein fluctuation formula for blackbody radiation
QObook
. But in the two examples we considered—a free electron and a dielectric sphere—the
n^{2}(\omega)
contribution is negligible and the momentum diffusion constant has effectively only a “particle” contribution from the field. It then has the same form as the collisional decoherence rate
\Lambda
.

In the case of decoherence due to collisions with gas molecules
diosi
;
adler
, in contrast, no “wave” or Bose–Einstein factor appears because the de Broglie wavelengths of the gas molecules are so small that quantum statistics are irrelevant.

Of course the
n^{2}(\omega)
and
n(\omega)
terms in the momentum variance and the drag force are both essential in determining the Planck spectrum. In thermal equilibrium the rate of change of kinetic energy due to momentum diffusion,
(1/2m)\langle\Delta p^{2}\rangle/\Delta t
, plus the rate of change
F_{x}v=-m\xi v^{2}=-2\xi\langle\frac{1}{2}mv^{2}\rangle=-\xi k_{B}T
due to the drag force, must add to zero. (
m
is the particle mass and
\xi
is defined by Eq. (
82
).) Since for our purposes only the nonrelativistic momentum diffusion rate was needed, we consider here the nonrelativistic drag force. The condition for thermal equilibrium of the field, assuming thermal equipartition of the particles’ kinetic energy, is then

\frac{1}{2m}\frac{8\hbar^{2}}{3\pi c^{5}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\omega^{5}\alpha_{I}(\omega)[n^{2}(\omega)+n(\omega)]+k_{B}T\frac{4\hbar}{3\pi c^{5}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\omega^{5}\alpha_{I}(\omega)\frac{dn}{d\omega}=0,

(90)

or

\frac{dn}{d\omega}=-\frac{\hbar}{k_{B}T}(n^{2}+n).

(91)

This follows independently of
\alpha_{I}(\omega)
, assuming
\alpha_{I}(\omega)>0
as implied by our use of the optical theorem for Rayleigh scattering. If we assume the form of the momentum diffusion constant obtained in Section
II.1
, we obtain

\frac{dn}{d\omega}=-\frac{\hbar}{k_{B}T}n

(92)

instead of Eq. (
91
), with the solution

n(\omega)=(\rm const.)\times e^{-\hbar\omega/k_{B}T}.

(93)

Together with Eq. (
6
), this implies the Wien form of the spectrum. If instead we assume the momentum diffusion constant
\propto n^{2}
obtained with classical stochastic wave theory by Einstein and Hopf, we obtain

\frac{dn}{d\omega}=-\frac{\hbar}{k_{B}T}n^{2}

(94)

instead of Eq. (
91
), with the solution

n(\omega)=k_{B}T/\hbar\omega,

(95)

when a constant of integration is chosen to give the Rayleigh–Jean spectrum. The solution of Eq. (
91

n(\omega)=(e^{\hbar\omega/k_{B}T}-1)^{-1},

(96)

when a constant interpretable in terms of a chemical potential is chosen so that the chemical potential is zero for photons, resulting in the Planck spectrum for
\rho(\omega)

\rho(\omega)

91

ein1

The fluctuation-dissipation relation (Eq. (
85

to obtain the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution. That is, the Fokker–Planck equation with the drift and diffusion terms satisfying Eq. (

\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=-\xi\frac{\partial}{\partial v}(vf)+\frac{\xi k_{B}T}{m}\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial v^{2}},

\langle\frac{1}{2}mv^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}k_{B}T

\alpha_{I}(\omega)>0
