Abstract
Aims. In this paper we present cosmological parameter forecasts for the so-called Euclid 6 × \times 2pt statistics, which include the galaxy clustering and weak lensing main probes together with previously neglected cross-covariance and cross-correlation signals between imaging/photometric and spectroscopic data. The aim is understanding the impact of such cross-terms on the expected Euclid performance. Methods. We adopt the Fisher information matrix approach to produce 6 × \times 2pt cosmological forecasts from Euclid , considering two different techniques: the so-called harmonic and hybrid approaches, respectively. In the first, we treat all the different Euclid probes in the same way, i.e. we consider only angular 2pt-statistics for spectroscopic and photometric galaxy distributions, as well as for weak lensing, fully analysing all their possible cross-covariances and cross-correlations in the spherical harmonic domain. In the second, thanks to lessons learnt from the harmonic approach, we do not account for negligible cross-covariances between the 3D spectroscopic galaxy distribution and the 2D photometric/imaging data, but consider the combination of their cross-correlation with the auto-correlation signals. Results. We find that both cross-covariances and cross-correlation signals between the two Euclid main probes, i.e. the spectroscopic galaxy sample and the photometric/imaging data, have a negligible impact on the cosmological parameter constraints and, therefore, on the Euclid performance. In the case of the hybrid approach, we attribute this result to the effect of the cross-correlation between weak lensing and photometric data, which is dominant with respect to other cross-correlation signals, and to the better performance of the full anisotropic 3D spectroscopic galaxy clustering with respect to the projected one. In the case of the 2D harmonic approach, we attribute this result to two main theoretical limitations of the 2D projected statistics implemented in this work according to the analysis of Euclid Collaboration: Blanchard et al. (2020) : the high shot noise and the limited redshift range of the spectroscopic sample, with respect to the photometric one, together with the suppression of radial information from subdominant contributions such as redshift-space distortions and lensing magnification. Therefore, our analysis suggests that 2D and 3D Euclid data can be safely treated as independent, with a great saving in computational resources.

Introduction

Euclid
is a medium-sized ESA mission devoted to the investigation of the nature of dark matter (DM) and dark energy (DE) and the study of the galaxy formation and evolution
(
Laureijs et al. 2011
;
Euclid Collaboration: Mellier et al. 2024
)
. The
Euclid
satellite was launched on July 1 2023 and will observe about one third of the sky, performing one of the largest galaxy surveys ever made. It will probe the last 10 billion years of the Universe expansion history via its main cosmological probes which are weak lensing (
\mathrm{WL}
) and galaxy clustering (
\mathrm{GC}
). Through
\mathrm{WL}
measurements it is possible to probe the matter distribution of the Universe, as
\mathrm{WL}
represents the slight deformation of galaxy images induced by the gravitational potential produced by such a distribution.

\mathrm{GC}
consists in the determination of the statistical properties of the distribution of galaxies, the so-called
dark matter tracers
. In particular, it is characterised by the so-called baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO), whose scale can be adopted as a
standard ruler
and used to constrain the expansion rate of the Universe in different redshift bins.

Euclid
will study these probes with two instruments: the Visible Imager (VIS)
(
Cropper et al. 2016
;
Euclid Collaboration: Jahnke et al. 2024
)
and the Near-Infrared Spectro-Photometer (NISP)
(
Maciaszek et al. 2022
;
Euclid Collaboration: Cropper et al. 2024
)
. VIS will provide high resolution images of around
1.5
billion galaxies for weak lensing measurements. NISP, used in the photometric mode, will allow measurements of the photometric redshifts of the same galaxies observed with VIS, when combined with ground-based photometry. When used in the spectroscopic mode, NISP will measure the spectroscopic redshifts of around
20
million
\mathrm{H}\alpha
-emitting galaxies, with a precision better than a factor of
50
with respect to the photometric redshift determination.
Euclid
will therefore produce two galaxy samples, a photometric and a spectroscopic one. In this sense, the
\mathrm{GC}
probe can be split into the photometric galaxy clustering (
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
) and the spectroscopic galaxy clustering (
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
).
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
and the so-called
Euclid

3
\times
2pt
statistics (composed by WL,
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
and their cross-correlation) represent the two main probes of
Euclid
.

In this work we present
Euclid
cosmological parameter forecasts which include the cross-correlations between
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
and
3
\times
2pt
statistics. The data analysis of the survey needs in fact to be accurately planned, and to this aim, pre-data forecasts of the expected scientific performance are needed. In a previous official
Euclid
forecast
(
Euclid Collaboration: Blanchard
et al. 2020
, hereafter ‘EP-VII’)
it has been shown that the cross-correlation (XC) between
\mathrm{WL}
and
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
significantly improves the
Euclid
constraints on cosmological parameters.
The aim of this work is to extend previous analyses which neglected the cross-correlations between the imaging/photometric probes and the spectroscopic probe, in order to understand their impact on the expected constraints from
Euclid
. In particular, we include the cross-correlations between
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
and
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
and between
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
and
\mathrm{WL}
, which defines the so-called
Euclid

6
\times
2pt
statistics, forecasting the impact of these cross-correlations on the
Euclid
performance.
Several works in the literature have investigated how to analyze and combine photometric and spectroscopic surveys, studying different approaches to minimising the information loss
(
Asorey et al. 2012
;
Eriksen & Gaztanaga 2015
;
Joudaki et al. 2018
;
Camera et al. 2018
;
Loureiro et al. 2019
;
Grasshorn Gebhardt & Jeong 2020
;
Taylor et al. 2022
)
. Recently,
Taylor & Markovič (2022)
also studied the theoretical modelling of the cross-covariance between photometric and spectroscopic probes and their impact on the forecast of the measurements of cosmological parameters.

This paper is organised as follows. In
Sect.

2
we describe in detail the adopted modelling of the
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
,
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
and
\mathrm{WL}
observables. In
Sect.

3

\times

cosmological parameter forecasts from
Euclid
; we adopt two different techniques: the so-called
harmonic
and
hybrid

An angular power spectrum is essentially the harmonic transform of a two-point angular correlation function. Each cosmological probe

a_{\ell m}^{A}=\int\mathrm{d}\Omega\,Y_{\ell m}^{*}(\hat{n})\,f^{A}(\hat{n})\;.

This forecast employs 10 tomographic redshift bins for the

2.1
Weak lensing

The gravitational field of large-scale cosmic structure deflects the path of light rays emitted by distant galaxies, distorting the images of the galaxies detected by the observers
(
Kaiser et al. 2000
;
Bacon et al. 2000
;
Kitching et al. 2017
;
Lemos et al. 2017
)
. At the linear level these distortions can be decomposed locally into convergence
\kappa
and a (complex) shear
\gamma
, which are respectively related to the size and shape distortion of the image.

In this work we only consider the shear signal
\gamma
, following
10
. The corresponding weight function for cosmic shear in
Eq.

6
is

W_{i}^{\gamma}(z)=\frac{3}{2}\,\left(\frac{H_{0}}{c}\right)^{2}\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\,(1+z)\,r(z)\,\int_{z}^{z_{\rm max}}\mathrm{d}z^{\prime}\,n^{\rm ph}_{i}(z^{\prime})\,\frac{r(z^{\prime})-r(z)}{r(z^{\prime})}\;.

(11)

The integral makes it clear that weak lensing is a cumulative effect: the detected shapes of galaxies are influenced by all the matter along the line of sight.

The forecast also includes the
intrinsic alignment
(IA), which is one of the main observable effects altering weak lensing measurements
(
Joachimi et al. 2015
;
Kiessling et al. 2015
;
Kirk et al. 2015
)
. IA refers to the alignments of nearby galaxies caused by tidal interactions which occur during galaxy formation and evolution. This produces spurious correlations over the ones due to cosmic shear.
The IA effect can be included in the lensing angular power spectrum. A possible way is by using the extended nonlinear alignment model
(
Bridle & King 2007
)
which consists in the following addition to the cosmic shear weight function

W_{i}^{\rm wl}(z)=W_{i}^{\gamma}(z)-\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{IA}}\,C_{\mathrm{IA}}\,\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\frac{H(z)\,\mathcal{F}_{\rm IA}(z)}{D(z)\,c}\,n^{\rm ph}_{i}(z)\;.

(12)

In the above equation,
D(z)
is the linear scale-independent growth factor. The function
\mathcal{F}_{\rm IA}
is defined as

\mathcal{F}_{\rm IA}(z)=(1+z)^{\eta_{\mathrm{IA}}}\,\left[\frac{\langle L\rangle(z)}{L_{\star}(z)}\right]^{\beta_{\mathrm{IA}}}\;,

(13)

with
\langle L\rangle(z)
and
L_{\star}(z)
are the mean and characteristic luminosity functions respectively. The intrinsic alignment parameters
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{IA}}
,
\eta_{\mathrm{IA}}
,
\beta_{\mathrm{IA}}
are treated as nuisance parameters in the analysis. The reference values for the IA parameters are the same adopted in
10
, namely
\{\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{IA}}=1.72,\,\eta_{\mathrm{IA}}=-0.41,\,\beta_{\mathrm{IA}}=2.17\}
. The parameter
C_{\mathrm{IA}}=0.0134
is fixed, since it is completely degenerate with
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{IA}}
.

2.2
Photometric galaxy clustering

The Galaxy Clustering probes exploit the statistical properties of the galaxy distribution.
For the
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
probe,
W^{\mathrm{ph}}_{i}(z)
in
Eq.

6
is the galaxy clustering weight function

W^{\mathrm{ph}}_{i}(z)=b^{\mathrm{ph}}(z)\,\frac{H(z)}{c}\,n^{\mathrm{ph}}_{i}(z)\;,

(14)

where
b
is the galaxy bias and
n^{\mathrm{ph}}_{i}(z)
is defined through
Eqs.

8
and
10
.

Galaxies are biased tracers of the dark matter distribution; the former are related to latter through the galaxy bias
b
:

\delta^{\rm g}(k,z)=b(z)\,\delta(k,z)\;,

(15)

with
k
being the wavenumber. In general the galaxy bias is a function of
z
and
k
, but in this work the
k
-dependence has been neglected, following the treatment of
10
. Moreover, as in
10
, we neglect subdominant contributions to clustering such as
redshift-space distortions
(RSD) and lensing magnification.

The redshift evolution of the galaxy bias is modelled as in
10
: for
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
a piecewise function is employed such that

b^{\mathrm{ph}}(z)=b^{\mathrm{ph}}_{i}=\sqrt{1+\bar{z}_{i}}\;,\qquad z_{i}^{-}<z<z_{i}^{+}\;,

(16)

where
\bar{z}_{i}=(z_{i}^{-}+z_{i}^{+})/2
is the mean redshift of the

Spectroscopic galaxy clustering

The

Spectroscopic redshift determination in

The spectroscopic galaxy clustering is usually treated by using the 3D Fourier galaxy power spectrum.
In this work, we also treated the

The 3D Fourier approach can model several physical effects, such as RSD, the Alcock-Paczynski (AP) projection effects, the nonlinear damping of the Baryon Acoustic Oscillations (BAO), and the residual shot noise. The full nonlinear model for the 3D power spectrum of the

3
Fisher information matrix

The Fisher information matrix is defined as the expectation value of the Hessian of the log-likelihood:

F_{\alpha\beta}=-\left\langle\frac{\partial^{2}\ln L}{\partial\theta_{\alpha}\partial\theta_{\beta}}\right\rangle\;,

(18)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are the model parameter indices, including both cosmological and nuisance parameters. The expected parameter covariance matrix is the inverse of the Fisher matrix:

\mathcal{C}_{\alpha\beta}=\left(F^{-1}\right)_{\alpha\beta}\;.

(19)

The marginalised
1\,\sigma
uncertainties, where
\sigma
is the Gaussian standard deviation, on the model parameters are the square roots of the diagonal elements of the parameter covariance matrix:

\sigma_{\alpha}=\sqrt{\mathcal{C}_{\alpha\alpha}}\;.

(20)

One of the metrics used to assess the scientific performance of
Euclid
is the
w_{0}\text{-}w_{a}
Figure-of-Merit (
\mathrm{FoM}
) which has been defined as in
10

\mathrm{FoM}\equiv\sqrt{\operatorname{det}\left(\tilde{F}_{w_{0}w_{a}}\right)}=\left[\sigma_{w_{0}}^{2}\,\sigma_{w_{a}}^{2}-\mathcal{C}^{2}_{w_{0}w_{a}}\right]^{-1/2}\;.

(21)

The symbol
\tilde{F}_{w_{0}w_{a}}
denotes the Fisher information matrix relative to the dark energy equation of state parameters
w_{0}
and
w_{a}
,see
Eq.

1
, marginalised over all the other free parameters.
The
\mathrm{FoM}
is inversely proportional to the area of the
1\,\sigma
marginalised contour ellipse in the
w_{0}\text{-}w_{a}
plane. Tighter constraints on
w_{0}
and
w_{a}
lead to smaller ellipses, which in turn means higher FoMs.

In this work we assume that the data vector
\mathcal{D}
, which contains the values of the
C(\ell)
’s or the
P(k)
’s considered, is distributed according to a multivariate Gaussian. Under this assumption, the Fisher matrix element
F_{\alpha\beta}
can be calculated as

F_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}}{\partial\theta_{\alpha}}\,\operatorname{Cov}^{-1}\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}\right]\,\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}}{\partial\theta_{\beta}}\;,

(22)

where
\operatorname{Cov}\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}\right]
represents the covariance matrix.

3.1
Harmonic space approach

The assumption that the
a^{A}_{\ell m}
coefficients of the observed fields follow a Gaussian distribution leads to the analytical expression for the Fisher matrix presented in
Eq.

22
. In this case the Gaussian covariance matrix for the harmonic power spectra
C^{AB}_{ij}({\ell})
is

\operatorname{Cov}\left[C^{AB}_{ij}({\ell}),C^{CD}_{km}({\ell^{\prime}})\right]=\frac{\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}}{2\,\ell+1}\\
\times\left[\Sigma^{AC}_{ik}(\ell)\,\Sigma^{BD}_{jm}(\ell^{\prime})+\Sigma^{AD}_{im}(\ell)\,\Sigma^{BC}_{jk}(\ell^{\prime})\right]\;,

(23)

with
\delta_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}
the Kronecker delta symbol.

Under the assumption that we account for the partial sky coverage only through the observed fraction of the sky
f_{\rm sky}
, the masking effects are negligible, and the galaxy counts are affected by Poisson shot noise only, the matrices
\Sigma^{AB}_{ij}(\ell)
can be expressed by

\Sigma^{AB}_{ij}(\ell)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{f_{\rm sky}\,\Delta\ell}}\,\left(C^{AB}_{ij}({\ell})+N_{ij}^{AB}(\ell)\right)\;,

(24)

where
N^{AB}_{ij}(\ell)
is the Poisson shot noise matrix described later in
Sect.

3.4
and
\Delta\ell
is the spacing between the multipoles in which the
C(\ell)
are sampled.

The data-vector in the harmonic space approach contains the independent values of the
C(\ell)
’s to be included in the computation.
Since the tomographic angular power spectra are matrices, it is convenient to
vectorise
them. In this context the term “vectorise” refers to matrix vectorisation. In this paragraph we are providing some examples of matrix vectorization for our probes; a formal description can be found in
Appendix

A
.

Let us consider the
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
auto-correlation
C^{\mathrm{phph}}_{ij}({\ell})
at fixed multipole
\ell
; this is usually represented as a
10\times 10
symmetric matrix with
10(10+1)/2=55
independent components.
This data vector can be also represented as a row vector of the independent components:

\mathbf{C}(\ell)=\left\{\mathbf{C}_{1}(\ell),\mathbf{C}_{2}(\ell),\dots,\mathbf{C}_{55}(\ell)\right\}\;,

(25)

where the index of the vector enumerates the
55
independent components of
\mathbf{C}(\ell)
considered in the analysis.

When considering 3 probes –
\mathrm{WL}
,
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
, and
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
– there are
6
angular power spectra matrices:

•

3 auto-correlations:
\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell)
,
\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{spsp}}(\ell)
,
\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell)
;

•

3 cross-correlations:
\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlph}}(\ell)
,
\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlsp}}(\ell)
,
\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phsp}}(\ell)
.

The angular power spectra are evaluated in a grid of
N_{\ell}
multipoles; the full data-vector
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}
also includes all the values of the power spectra evaluated over all multipoles, viz.

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}=\left\{\mathbf{C}(\ell_{1}),\mathbf{C}(\ell_{2}),\dots,\mathbf{C}(\ell_{N_{\ell}})\right\}\;.

(26)

The covariance matrix associated with this data-vector is block diagonal, since the Kronecker delta of
Eq.

23
ensures that different multipoles are uncorrelated:

\operatorname{Cov}\left[\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}},\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}\right]=\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}(\ell_{1}),\mathbf{C}(\ell_{1})\right]&0&0\\
0&\ddots&0\\
0&0&\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}(\ell_{{N_{\ell}}}),\mathbf{C}(\ell_{N_{\ell}})\right]\end{pmatrix}\;.

(27)

The diagonal blocks,
\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}(\ell),\mathbf{C}(\ell)\right]
, account for all the correlations between the
\mathbf{C}(\ell)
’s:

\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}(\ell),\mathbf{C}(\ell)\right]=\\
\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}_{1}(\ell),\mathbf{C}_{1}(\ell)\right]&\cdots&\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}_{1}(\ell),\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{N}}(\ell)\right]\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{N}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}_{1}(\ell)\right]&\cdots&\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{N}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{N}}(\ell)\right]\end{pmatrix}\;,

(28)

where the single blocks
\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}_{i}(\ell),\mathbf{C}_{j}(\ell)\right]
are computed according to
Eq.

23
.
With these definitions, the Fisher matrix element
F_{\alpha\beta}
can be calculated from
Eq.

22
as

F_{\alpha\beta}=\sum_{\ell=\ell_{1}}^{\ell_{N_{\ell}}}F_{\alpha\beta}(\ell)=\sum_{\ell=\ell_{1}}^{\ell_{N_{\ell}}}\frac{\partial\mathbf{C}(\ell)}{\partial\theta_{\alpha}}^{T}\,\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}(\ell),\mathbf{C}(\ell)\right]^{-1}\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{C}(\ell)}{\partial\theta_{\beta}}\;,

(29)

where the first equality follows from the block diagonal form of the covariance.

3.2
Naming conventions

For later convenience, here are reported the adopted conventions for naming the Fisher matrices that have been computed in this work:

1.

The name of a Fisher matrix is representative of the data vector, and it is composed by different labels, which identify the
C(\ell)
’s contained in the data-vector itself.

2.

Within the name of a Fisher matrix, the auto-correlation
C^{\rm AA}(\ell)
of the probe
\rm A
is labelled simply as
\rm A
, while the cross-correlation
C^{\rm AB}(\ell)
between the two probes
\rm A
and
\rm B
is denoted as
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B})
.

3.

Square brackets are used to delimit the data-vector extent.

4.

The pairwise cross-covariances between the
C(\ell)
’s included in a given data-vector are always considered in the computation of the corresponding Fisher matrix.

5.

The sum of two Fisher matrices
[F_{1}]
and
[F_{2}]
is simply denoted by
[F_{1}]+[F_{2}]
. This simple sum corresponds to combine
[F_{1}]
and
[F_{2}]
without taking into account the cross-covariances between their data-vectors, i.e. it is an
independent sum
.

Let us return to the example where only the auto-correlation of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
is considered.
In this case the data-vector at fixed
\ell
is

\mathbf{C}(\ell)=\left\{\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell)\right\}\;,

(30)

and ranges over
55
elements (assuming
10
tomographic bins). Consequently, the covariance matrix is made by a single
55\times 55
block:

\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell)\right]\;,

(31)

which takes into account the auto-covariance of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
only.

When considering two or more probes, multiple combinations can be constructed, depending whether or not
cross-covariances
and
cross-correlations
are included in the computation. In this work we reserve the word “cross-covariance” to the off-diagonal blocks of the covariance matrix
Eq.

28
, e.g. the blocks
\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{ph}_{i}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{wl}_{j}(\ell)\right]
. The term “cross-correlation” (signal) is instead used to denote the data-vector entry corresponding to the correlation between two probes, e.g.
\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlph}}(\ell)
.

The possible combinations that can be constructed using two probes,
\rm A
and
B
, are described in detail in Appendix
A
.

3.3
Hybrid approach

The harmonic space approach has both advantages and disadvantages. One of the main advantages of the harmonic space approach is the straightforward way to compute cross-covariances. The main disadvantage of treating the clustering signal in the harmonic domain is that the projection on the celestial sphere results in a partial loss of information from the galaxy density distribution along the radial direction.

For
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
, this loss is limited, since the redshift resolution is already hampered by large photometric errors.
In the spectroscopic sample, the redshifts are measured with much better precision, therefore the 2D projection results in a larger loss of constraining power from
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
. The tomographic technique can be employed in order to partially recover the radial information about the distances of the galaxies.

The natural approach to treat
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
is the “3D” or
P_{k}
.
Directly using a 3D galaxy power spectrum allows us to naturally exploit the high redshift resolution of the spectroscopic sample. Nonetheless, in this case it is difficult to compute cross-correlations and cross-covariances between
\mathrm{WL},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
, and
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
.

In this work we therefore considered two approaches to combine
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
with
\mathrm{WL}
and
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
. The first is to treat all probes with the angular power spectra, as described in the previous section. In the second approach, the Fisher matrix contribution for the
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
auto-correlation is taken into account by adding
a posteriori
the Fisher matrix computed in the 3D approach of
10
, referred here as
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}(P_{k})
.

The core idea of the latter approach is to add the
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
auto-correlation using the 3D observable and its cross-correlations with the other probes, treating it as a 2D observable, in order to be able to compute the covariance matrix analytically: for this reason we dubbed it the hybrid approach.

The starting point of the hybrid approach is to include the harmonic
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
auto-correlation and the harmonic
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}\times\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
cross-correlation in the data-vector

\mathbf{C}(\ell)=\left\{\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phsp}}(\ell)\right\}\;,

(32)

with covariance matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathrm{phph},\mathrm{phph}\right]&\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathrm{phph},\mathrm{phsp}\right]\\
\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathrm{phsp},\mathrm{phph}\right]&\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathrm{phsp},\mathrm{phsp}\right]\\
\end{pmatrix}\;,

(33)

where we have dropped
\mathbf{C}
and the exponent in order to have a lighter notation.

We refer to the resulting Fisher matrix with the notation
[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}+\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})]
.

The Fisher matrix of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}(P_{k})
is independently added:

F_{\alpha\beta}[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}+\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})\,+\,\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}(P_{k})]\equiv\\
F_{\alpha\beta}[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}+\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})]+F_{\alpha\beta}[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}(P_{k})]\;.

(34)

This procedure is equivalent to neglecting the covariance terms between the
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
auto-correlation and the other observables. More details are elaborated in Appendix
A.1
.

3.4
Poisson shot noise

The Poisson shot noise
(
Baldauf et al. 2013
)
has been implemented similarly to what has been done in
10
. It is assumed that only the auto-correlation
C(\ell)
’s in the same tomographic bin are affected by a shot noise, which is independent of the multipole:

N^{AB}_{ij}(\ell)=\delta_{AB}\,\delta_{ij}\,N^{A}_{i}\;.

(35)

The quantity
N^{A}_{i}
represents the shot noise associated with the probe
A
at tomographic bin
i
. For photometric and spectroscopic galaxy clustering in the harmonic domain this is simply given by

\displaystyle N^{\rm ph}_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv\left[\int_{z_{i}^{-}}^{z_{i}^{+}}\mathrm{d}z\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}^{\rm ph}}{\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}\Omega}\right]^{-1}\;,

(36)

\displaystyle N^{\rm sp}_{i}

\displaystyle\equiv\left[\int_{z_{i}^{-}}^{z_{i}^{+}}\mathrm{d}z\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}^{\rm sp}}{\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}\Omega}\right]^{-1}\;.

(37)

For WL the definition is instead given by the
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
shot noise multiplied by the variance
\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}
of the intrinsic galaxy ellipticity

N^{\rm wl}_{i}\equiv\sigma^{2}_{\epsilon}\,\left[\int_{z_{i}^{-}}^{z_{i}^{+}}\mathrm{d}z\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{N}^{\rm ph}}{\mathrm{d}z\mathrm{d}\Omega}\right]^{-1}\;.

(38)

The value assumed for
\sigma_{\epsilon}
is
0.3
as in
10
.

As the Kronecker delta
\delta_{AB}
of
Eq.

35
states, no shot noise has been considered for the cross-correlation
C(\ell)
’s. It is in fact commonly assumed for the noises of different tracers to be uncorrelated
(
10
)
. This approximation is expected to work well for the cross-correlation between weak lensing and galaxy clustering, since these are different tracers of the same underlying dark matter distribution.

In the cross-correlation of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
with
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
the tracers are the galaxies for both the probes, so in principle a shot noise term in the cross power spectra (cross-noise) should be present. In this work we checked the
\delta_{\rm phsp}
approximation of
Eq.

35
with the following approach. Given that the Poisson shot noise affecting the two-point function comes from the count of galaxy
self-pairs
– see the introduction of
Baldauf et al. (2013)
– the cross-noise is due to those galaxies which are both in the photometric and in the spectroscopic sample. The scenario with the highest noise is therefore the one in which the spectroscopic sample is a
proper subset
of the photometric one. So, assuming this worst case scenario, a
subtraction
of the spectroscopic galaxy distribution from the photometric one has been performed. After the subtraction there is be no more overlap between the two samples, and their cross-noise becomes zero by construction. It has been checked that the constraints do not change appreciably after the subtraction, with the FoM and the marginalised uncertainties of the free parameters being negligibly affected.
However, we point out that the removal of the spectroscopic galaxies from the photometric samples might cause a bias in the clustering signal approach; the approach we followed was just a way to quantify the impact of the shot noise in the worst case scenario.

The shot noise affects in a direct way the diagonal covariance blocks corresponding to the auto-correlation power spectra, as it enters in all the factors of
Eq.

23
. This is what happens for example in the case of the auto-covariance of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}

\operatorname{Cov}\left[C^{\mathrm{spsp}}_{ij}({\ell}),C^{\mathrm{spsp}}_{km}({\ell})\right]\propto\Big\{\left[C^{\mathrm{spsp}}_{ik}+N^{\mathrm{spsp}}_{ik}\right]\,\left[C^{\mathrm{spsp}}_{jm}+N^{\mathrm{spsp}}_{jm}\right]\\
+\left[C^{\mathrm{spsp}}_{im}+N^{\mathrm{spsp}}_{im}\right]\,\left[C^{\mathrm{spsp}}_{jk}+N^{\mathrm{spsp}}_{jk}\right]\Big\}\;,

(39)

where the
\ell
dependence on the right-hand side (e.g.
N^{\mathrm{spsp}}_{ik}(\ell)
) has been omitted for compactness.
However, the fact that the auto-correlation power spectra are contaminated by the shot noise indirectly alters also the other blocks of the covariance matrix. For example the diagonal block corresponding to the auto-covariance of
\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phsp}}(\ell)
reads

\operatorname{Cov}\left[C^{\mathrm{phsp}}_{ij}({\ell}),C^{\mathrm{phsp}}_{km}({\ell})\right]\propto\Big\{\left[C^{\mathrm{phph}}_{ik}+N^{\mathrm{phph}}_{ik}\right]\,\left[C^{\mathrm{spsp}}_{jm}+N^{\mathrm{spsp}}_{jm}\right]\\
+C^{\mathrm{phsp}}_{im}\,C^{\mathrm{phsp}}_{jk}\Big\}\;.

(40)

and therefore also the terms coming from the inclusion of the
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})
are affected by the shot noise of both
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
and
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
. Moreover, since the number of galaxies in the spectroscopic sample is
\sim 80
times smaller than the galaxies in the photometric one, from
Eq.

37
it is clear that the
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
shot noise is larger than the one associated with
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
. In order to quantify the impact of the
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
noise, the forecasts have been performed also in an unrealistic alternative scenario, where this noise is artificially reduced as if the number of spectroscopic galaxies were equal to the number of the photometric ones. This is done in practice by introducing an alternative reduced shot noise for
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
, defined as follows

\tilde{N}^{\rm sp}_{i}\equiv\frac{N_{\rm sp}^{\rm tot}}{N_{\rm ph}^{\rm tot}}\,N^{\rm sp}_{i}\;,

(41)

where
N_{\mathrm{sp}}^{\mathrm{tot}}
(
N_{\mathrm{ph}}^{\mathrm{tot}}
) is the total number of spectroscopic (photometric) galaxies, computed by integrating the galaxy distribution over its redshift range. As it is discussed in
Sect.

4
it turns out that the results in the harmonic approach drastically change using this reduced noise.
We emphasise that this test was performed only to check what is the origin of the different GCsp and GCph constraints in the harmonic approach and it is not representative of any realistic survey scenario.

\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}

Table

Anyway the contribution is always smaller than
3\%
, decreasing as the number
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
bins increase. This trend can be understood as follows: the finer the tomographic binning of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
the smaller the support of the integrand of the off-diagonal terms
\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phsp}}(\ell)
. Intuitively, the value of the integral over each of the
4
thick bins is diluted into more thinner bins when a finer binning is used. Hence the off-diagonal block
\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{spsp}}(\ell)\right]
and its transposed counterpart become larger and sparser as the number of bins is increased, and therefore the cross-covariance contribution becomes smaller. Physically this could be understood considering that for 40 bins the loss of information due to the projection transversely to the line of sight is less severe than for 4 bins only.

The same trend is observed also for the marginalised uncertainties on the cosmological parameters, as it can be seen by
Fig.

2
. The parameters mostly affected by the covariance are
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}
and
h
, whose constraints in the 4 bin setting worsen by
4\%
and
6\%
respectively. However, as soon as the binning is refined, the effect gets smaller, becoming about
0.9\%
for
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}
and
2.5\%
for
h
. The uncertainties on
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
,
n_{\textrm{s}}
, and
\sigma_{8}
are instead affected by less than
2\%
for all the binning settings. This outcome is confirmed also by
Fig.

7
, which reports the relative marginalised uncertainties and the FoM as horizontal bars. The
[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}+\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}]
bars (in blue) always have practically the same length as the
[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}]+[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}]
bars (in cyan), and they look more and more similar as the number of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
bins increases.

In conclusion the cross-covariance between
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
and
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
can be considered negligible, as it does not change the uncertainties on the cosmological parameters by more than
6\%
and the FoM by more than
3\%
.

Hybrid approach vs harmonic approach

As
Fig.

4
shows, the hybrid approach performs better than the harmonic one in constraining almost all the cosmological parameters, even if the constraining power of the harmonic approach significantly improves with increasing the number of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
tomographic bins. The harmonic approach has a FoM of
69
(

) for
4
(

\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}

\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}

and

and

\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}

than the hybrid one. The

4

20\%
larger than in the hybrid one. Instead, when
40
bins are used for the harmonic approach, the

uncertainty given by the hybrid approach is a few percents larger than the one given by the former.

To conclude, for the combination of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
and
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}

40

Comparing photometric and spectroscopic clustering in the harmonic domain

In the harmonic approach, two alternative configurations have been considered, both for
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
and
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
. The baseline configuration for
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
consists in using all the
10
redshift bins reported in
Table

2
, while in the alternative configuration only the
4
bins strictly contained in the range
[0.9,1.8]
are considered.

For spectroscopic clustering, the noise baseline settings correspond to using the shot noise as calculated from
Eq.

37
, while the alternative consists in using the artificially reduced version
Eq.

41
. In fact, from further investigation it turned out that a great limitation of the harmonic approach is due to the shot noise associated with the
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
auto-correlation
C(\ell)
’s. This is in fact much higher than the one associated with
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
, since the expected number of
\mathrm{H}\alpha
-emitting galaxies in the
Euclid
catalogue (
\sim$2\text{\times}{10}^{7}$
) is smaller than the expected number of galaxies in the photometric sample (
\sim$1.6\text{\times}{10}^{9}$
). Therefore, in order to quantify the impact of the spectroscopic shot noise, the forecasts include also the artificially reduced shot noise setting, as explained in
Sect.

3.4
.

The results are shown in
Fig.

5
: in the left panel the standard
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
shot noise of
Eq.

37
is employed, while in the right one the reduced noise of
Eq.

41
is used. Lowering the shot noise systematically boosts the results of the harmonic approach by about one order of magnitude, making its performance comparable or even better than in the hybrid case. In particular, as can be expected, the observable which most improves its performance is the harmonic
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
auto-correlation.

Instead, when considering the behaviour of the hybrid

Fisher matrix, as compared to the corresponding one in the harmonic domain, its performance remains stable against the change of the shot noise level, since the possible change of the latter would enter only
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}(P_{k})

Fig.

. The constraints from
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}

Eqs.

14
and
17

W_{i}^{\rm ph}
differing from the spectroscopic one
W_{i}^{\rm sp}

shows also the two probes in the baseline configuration, in which
10

) is used for

\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
significantly affects its performances, making the uncertainties larger.

The conclusion is therefore that the shot noise and the redshift range of the galaxy sample are what make the difference between
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
and
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
in terms of constraining power, when both are treated in the harmonic domain. When
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
is restricted to the same range of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
and the shot noise of the latter is reduced to the same level of the former, their constraints become comparable.

A similar result for another experiment other than

\mathrm{WL}
and the transverse component of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}

fast full-sky mock galaxy catalogues, finding that the off-diagonal (cross-covariance) terms were negligible with respect to the diagonal (auto-covariance) ones.

6
Main results and conclusions

In this work we presented the results of the first
Euclid
forecasts that include all the pairwise correlations between the main probes: weak lensing (
\mathrm{WL}
), photometric galaxy clustering (
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
), and spectroscopic galaxy clustering (
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
). We have studied these correlations from two points of view. First, off-diagonal terms of the covariance matrix, the
cross-covariances
, that account for the statistical correlation between two different probes. Second, as additional observables, the
cross-correlation signals
which can be sensitive to cosmological parameters.

This work is a natural extension of the
Euclid
IST forecast
10
, considering
\mathrm{WL}
and
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
and their correlation in the 2D harmonic domain. The
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
was instead studied in the 3D Fourier domain, and to a first approximation was assumed to be independent from the other two probes. Here we extend the harmonic formalism also to
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
, with the aim of including the
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})
and
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{WL},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})
correlations in the analysis as well. Differently, a work
2
2

2

Dournac et al, in preparation.
complementary to this one introduces a new data vector, essentially the ratio of the correlation of the two samples. Since this data set is free from sampling variance, it aims to achieve a
significant improvement in the final constraints on the cosmological parameters.

We have considered two different approaches to include the cross-correlations in the analysis: the
harmonic
approach and the
hybrid
approach.

In the harmonic approach all the observables – the two-point correlation functions – are treated in the harmonic domain, i.e. using the
C(\ell)
’s formalism. This approach allows us to naturally include all cross-covariances between the observables, computed via
Eq.

23
. Nonetheless, it has the disadvantage of significantly lowering the constraining power of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
, since the integral along the line of sight prevents to fully exploit the accurate radial information provided by the spectroscopic clustering. In order to recover such information, we tried to refine the tomographic binning of the projected
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
, from
4
bins (the baseline setting) up to a maximum of
40
bins, but in any case we neglected RSD in the angular
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
power spectra.

In the hybrid approach, all the observables are studied in the harmonic domain – including
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{WL},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})
and
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})
– except for the
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
auto-correlation function. We have considered it as an independent observable, adding the
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
Fisher matrix that had been computed in
10
, using the 3D Fourier power spectrum as observable. The main advantage of this approach is that it fully exploits the potential of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
, keeping the information from radial BAO and RSD available thanks to accurate spectroscopic redshift measurements. At the same time the
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})
and
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{WL},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})
cross-correlations are correctly included in the analysis as harmonic two-point functions, i.e.
C(\ell)
’s. However, this approach comes with a drawback.
It is not obvious how to compute the cross-covariance terms between the 3D
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
auto-correlation and the 2D
3
\times
2pt
observables. Therefore, according to the conclusions from the analysis in the harmonic domain (which correctly accounts for the projected part of such cross-covariances), indicating that the impact of this “projected” cross-covariance on the
Euclid
performance is negligible, and assuming that it is a good approximation of the full 3D-2D covariance (especially because the
3
\times
2pt
statistics only depends on transverse modes), in the hybrid approach we neglect the cross-covariances between 3D and 2D probes.
However, we would like to point out that the methodology presented in this paper is not meant to be used with forthcoming Euclid data, but as a tool to forecast the impact of cross-correlations and covariances among the Euclid photometric and spectroscopic probes.

We summarise the results of our analysis in the three cases considered: the combination of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
and
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
, the combination of
\mathrm{WL}
and
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
, and the so-called
6
\times
2pt
, i.e. the full combination of
\mathrm{WL},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
altogether. In the latter case – which is the most interesting one for the
Euclid
data analysis – we present the results in an optimistic and pessimistic scenarios. Instead, for the two pairwise combinations
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
-
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
and
\mathrm{WL}
-
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
, we report the results for the optimistic scenario alone.

Combination of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
and
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}

In the harmonic approach, the full analysis,
[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}+\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}+\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})]
, i.e. including the cross-covariance and cross-spectrum, provides a FoM of 69 in the baseline setting of 4 spectroscopic bins. For the combination of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
and
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
, the cross-covariance between the two can be considered practically negligible, as it does not change the uncertainties on the cosmological parameters by more than
6\%
and the FoM (computed considering only the
3
\times
2pt
statistics associated with GC) by more than
3\%
, with respect to the reference combination in the harmonic domain,
[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}]+[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}]
, of the two probes taken as independent. In the harmonic approach, the
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})
cross-correlation signal starts to be significant only when using
40
bins, yielding a maximum FoM of
153
, which is
38\%
higher than for the
[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}]+[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}]
combination. Regarding the uncertainties on
w_{0}
and
w_{a}
, the improvement given by the
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})
cross-correlation is 5 and 6% respectively in the baseline
4
bins setting, while it is
23\%
and
25\%
when using
40
bins. The gain on the uncertainties on the other parameters is less than
5\%
with
4
bins and ranges from
10\%
to
20\%
with
40
bins.

In the hybrid approach, the full analysis,
[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}+\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})]+[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}(P_{k})]
, including the cross-spectrum but neglecting the cross-covariance, gives instead a FoM of
234
, which is much higher than in the harmonic case. However, in the hybrid case the impact of the
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})
signal is negligible, since combining the probes as independent,
[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}]+[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}(P_{k})]
, provides a FoM of
230
, which is only
1.7\%
lower than in the full case. The gain on the marginalised uncertainties is even smaller, being always less than
1\%
. Therefore, it is possible to conclude that the
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})
cross-correlation can be neglected in the combination of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
with
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
.

Combination of
\mathrm{WL}
and
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}

In the harmonic approach, the full analysis,
[\mathrm{WL}+\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}+\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{WL},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})]
, i.e. including the cross-covariance and cross-spectrum, provides a FoM of 103 in the baseline setting of 4 spectroscopic bins. For the combination of
\mathrm{WL}
and
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
, the cross-covariance is even more negligible than for the GC case above, impacting the constraints always by less than
1\%
with respect to the independent combination,
[\mathrm{WL}]+[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}]
, computed in the harmonic domain. In this domain, the
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{WL},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})
cross-correlation signal improves the constraints almost independently of the number of tomographic bins used for
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
. The percentage gain on the corresponding FoM, produced by the
\rm XC
inclusion, is
38\%
and
33\%
with
4
and
40
bins, respectively. This seemingly counterintuitive trend has been explained in
Sect.

4.2
: it is due to the fact that the performance of the harmonic
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
auto-spectrum alone improves faster than for
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{WL},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})
when refining the spectroscopic binning. The value of the FoM provided by the full combination,
[\mathrm{WL}+\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}+\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{WL},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})]
, increases to
188
with
40
bins, i.e. by
\sim 45\%
with respect to the baseline
4
-bin setting.
The
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{WL},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})
signal also improves the uncertainties on
\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}
and
\sigma_{8}
, by
12\%
–
18\%
for the former and
11\%
–
16\%
for the latter. The error decrease for
\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}
and

5\%
to
8\%
for the former and from
5\%
to
2\%
on the latter. Finally, the uncertainty on

is the only exception to this trend, with an improvement only by
1\%
–

.

In the hybrid approach, the full analysis,
[\mathrm{WL}+\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{WL},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})]+[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}(P_{k})]
provides a FoM of
183
, which is comparable to the harmonic case for the 40-bin setting, but is
\sim 56\%
larger than the 4-bin case. In the hybrid approach, the impact of the
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{WL},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})
signal is not negligible, since the FoM is
15\%
larger than for the independent combination,
[\mathrm{WL}]+[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}(P_{k})]

The improvements on the marginalised uncertainties are all in the range between

, with no significant differences between the parameters. The uncertainty on

improves the most, by about

, while the smallest improvement is on

.

In conclusion, either the cross-covariances (here computed only in the harmonic approach) or the cross-correlations (computed both in the harmonic and hybrid approaches) between the two
Euclid
main probes, i.e.
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
and the so-called
3
\times
2pt
statistics, have a negligible impact on the cosmological parameter constraints and, therefore, on the
Euclid
performance.
Regarding the cross-covariance impact, this issue was addressed also in
Taylor & Markovič (2022)
, but following a different approach: they derived an analytical expression for the Gaussian cross-covariance between the
3
\times
2pt
statistics and the
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
multipoles. Also this approach leads to a negligible impact of the cross-covariance, hence corroborating our findings.

In the case of the hybrid approach, we attribute this result to the effect of the
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{WL},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}})
cross-correlation which is dominant with respect to the other cross-correlations, and to the higher performance of the full anisotropic 3D
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
probe with respect to the projected one.

In the case of the 2D harmonic approach, we attribute this result to two main limitations of the 2D projected
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
approximation: the high shot noise and the limited redshift range of the sample, with respect to the
3
\times
2pt
statistics, together with the suppression of radial information, as RSD. We have found that, under two conditions,
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
in harmonic space becomes equivalent to
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
in terms of constraining power, as it can be seen from
Fig.

6
. The first condition is to reduce the shot noise of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
to the same level of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
. The second is to restrict the tomographic bins of
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
to the
4
photometric bins contained in the
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
redshift range. Under these same conditions,
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{WL},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}})
and
\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{WL},\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}})
equally contribute to the
Euclid
performance, as we show in
Fig.

12
. Nonetheless, these conditions are not realistic.

Finally, we would like to point out to the reader that in our work we focused on the impact of the Euclid photometric and spectroscopic probes on the cosmological parameters’ determination, neglecting the implications for systematics. For instance, as studied in
Newman (2008)
, it is possible to use the photometric and spectroscopic cross-correlations to calibrate the photometric galaxy density. On the same side, with a
6
\times
2pt
pt analysis it is possible to perform the so-called “shear-ratio test”
(
Jain & Taylor 2003
)
; although there is not a great deal of cosmological information encoded in these data, they can be used to calibrate the shears and redshifts of the photometric sources
(
Johnson et al. 2017
)
.

Future extensions of this work will overcome some approximations that have been made.
First, we have computed the
C(\ell)
’s making use of the Limber approximation. It has been shown in
Fang et al. (2020)
that this may result in a biased analysis. Using the exact expression for computing the angular power spectra would help to prevent this issue. Second, we have computed the
C(\ell)
’s covariance with
Eq.

23
as in
10
, and this formula only accounts for the Gaussian contributions. Comparison with covariances estimated from
N
-body simulations showed that the inclusion of non-Gaussian effects may be necessary in order to reach a better agreement with simulations
(
Krause et al. 2017
)
. In order to obtain a more realistic signal-to-noise ratio we have performed some forecasts including the SSC contribution as computed in
Lacasa & Grain (2019)
: the results of this study are not modified, as we found both the cross-covariance and the cross-correlation between the photometric and spectroscopic probes to be negligible even in this scenario. Third, in what we have called the hybrid approach we have neglected the covariances between the Fourier
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
auto-correlation and the
3
\times
2pt
probe. Our calculations in harmonic space suggest that these terms may be negligible. Nonetheless, providing analytical modelling of these terms when
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}
is studied in 3D Fourier space would surely help to confirm our findings.

Appendix A
Details of the cross-covariance

There are three possible angular power spectra that can be constructed from two probes
\rm A
and
\rm B
:

•

auto power spectrum of
\rm A
,
C^{\rm AA}(\ell)
;

•

auto power spectrum of
\rm B
,
C^{\rm BB}(\ell)
;

•

cross power spectrum between
\rm A
and
\rm B
,
C^{\rm AB}(\ell)
.

As a concrete example, consider
\mathrm{A}=\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
and
\mathrm{B}=\mathrm{WL}
.

Combining
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
and
\mathrm{WL}
as independent probes

When assuming
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
and
\mathrm{WL}
to be independent, the resulting Fisher matrix will be given by the sum of the Fishers of the two single probes. According to the conventions explained in
Sect.

3.2
, the resulting Fisher is denoted as
[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}]+[\mathrm{WL}]
. This case is equivalent to building a data-vector including the two auto-correlations

\mathbf{C}(\ell)=\left\{\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell)\right\}\;.

(46)

and setting to zero the off-diagonal blocks of the associated covariance matrix

\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}(\ell),\mathbf{C}(\ell)\right]=\\
\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell)\right]&0\\[4.30554pt]
0&\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell)\right]\end{pmatrix}\;.

(47)

This is equivalent to neglecting the cross-covariance between the auto-correlations of the two single probes, which is given by the block

\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell)\right]\;.

Since the matrix in
Eq.

47
is block diagonal, its inverse is of the same form:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\[4.30554pt]
0&B\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}A^{-1}&0\\[4.30554pt]
0&B^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\;.

(48)

Therefore the matrix product entering the Fisher matrix element is simply given by the bilinear form between
(\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell)_{,\alpha},\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell)_{,\alpha})^{\sf T}
and a block-diagonal covariance matrix, whose diagonal blocks are
\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell)\right]^{-1}
and
\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell)\right]^{-1}
. The resulting Fisher matrix element is

\displaystyle F^{\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}+\mathrm{WL}}_{\alpha\beta}(\ell)

\displaystyle=\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell)_{,\alpha}\right]^{T}\,\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell)\right]^{-1}\,\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell)_{,\beta}

\displaystyle+\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell)_{,\alpha}\right]^{T}\,\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell)\right]^{-1}\,\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell)_{,\beta}

\displaystyle=F^{\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}}_{\alpha\beta}(\ell)+F^{\mathrm{WL}}_{\alpha\beta}(\ell)\;.

(49)

which is the simple sum of the fisher elements associated with the single probes.

Combining
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
and
\mathrm{WL}
with cross-covariance

The Fisher matrix in this case is denoted as
[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}+\mathrm{WL}]
. The data-vector is the same as the previous one (Eq.
46
), but the off-diagonal blocks of the covariance matrix are taken into account

\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}(\ell),\mathbf{C}(\ell)\right]=\\
\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell)\right]&\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell)\right]\\[4.30554pt]
\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell)\right]&\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell)\right]\end{pmatrix}\;.

(50)

This matrix is not block-diagonal, hence, when inverting it, the blocks will mix with each other. There exist some formulas based on the Schur complement
(
Gupta A. K. 2000
)
for the inverse of a
2\times 2
block matrix, but writing it down does not help to enlighten what happens in this case. From an intuitive point of view, the cross-covariance between two observables should worsen the constraints with respect to combining the two probes as independent. This can be understood with the following argument. If two observables exhibit a non-zero cross-covariance, there will be a mutual correlation between the two. In particular, a change in one of the two – for example induced by a variation of the cosmological parameters – will statistically induce a corresponding variation in the other. This in turn means that the two observables will share an amount of cosmological information, and therefore the total information coming from their combination will be
less
than the direct sum of the two pieces of information carried individually by the two of them.

Combining
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
,
\mathrm{WL}
and their cross-correlation

Here both the covariance and the cross-correlation between
\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}
and
\mathrm{WL}
are taken into account. The resulting Fisher matrix is denoted as
[\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}}+\mathrm{WL}+\mathrm{XC}(\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{ph}},\mathrm{WL})]
, and the data-vector includes accordingly the maximal set of the available
C(\ell)
’s

\mathbf{C}(\ell)=\left\{\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phwl}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell)\right\}\;,

(51)

and the covariance is the full
3\times 3
block matrix associated with this data-vector has the following entries

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}(\ell),\mathbf{C}(\ell)\right]_{11}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell)\right]

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}(\ell),\mathbf{C}(\ell)\right]_{12}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phwl}}(\ell)\right]

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}(\ell),\mathbf{C}(\ell)\right]_{12}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phph}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell)\right]

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}(\ell),\mathbf{C}(\ell)\right]_{22}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phwl}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phwl}}(\ell)\right]

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}(\ell),\mathbf{C}(\ell)\right]_{13}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phwl}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell)\right]

\displaystyle\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}(\ell),\mathbf{C}(\ell)\right]_{33}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell),\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{wlwl}}(\ell)\right]\;.

(52)

In this case the new information coming from the cross-correlation is added to the data-vector, and this contribution is expected to tighten the resulting constraints with respect to the uncorrelated sum. In particular, the cross-correlation is itself a function of the cosmological parameters, meaning that its value is be sensitive to a variation of the parameters themselves. In this sense it is said that adding the
cross-correlation signal
is expected to provide more cosmological information, therefore improving the combined constraints.
On the other hand, also all the cross-covariances between the
C(\ell)
’s are being considered in this case, and this tends to worsen the constraints, as explained in the previous paragraph. So there are two concurring effects, and in principle it is not obvious which of the two is dominant. The forecasts performed in this work show that the tightest constraints are actually obtained when both the cross-covariance and cross-correlation are included.

Hybrid approach cross-covariance

The hybrid approach is equivalent to
using the data vector

\mathbf{D}=\left\{\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phsp}}(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{N}),\,\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{phsp}}(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{N}),\,\mathrm{GC}_{\mathrm{sp}}(P_{k})\right\}\;,

(53)

and computing the Fisher matrix with
Eq.

22
by using a covariance matrix that can be symbolically written as

\begin{pmatrix}\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathrm{phph},\mathrm{phph}\right]&\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathrm{phph},\mathrm{phsp}\right]&0\\
\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathrm{phsp},\mathrm{phph}\right]&\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathrm{phsp},\mathrm{phsp}\right]&0\\
0&0&\operatorname{Cov}\left[\mathrm{spsp},\mathrm{spsp}\right]\\
\end{pmatrix}\;.

(54)

The upper left
2\times 2
sector contains the covariances between the elements of the
C^{\mathrm{phph}}({\ell})
and
C^{\mathrm{phsp}}({\ell})
matrices, organised in block-diagonal form for all multipoles as in
Eq.

27
. Analogously, the lower right corner block represents the auto-covariance of the spectroscopic galaxy Fourier power spectrum for all wave-numbers and redshifts considered.
