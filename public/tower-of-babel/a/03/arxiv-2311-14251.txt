Optimal 1-bit Error Exponent for 2-hop Relaying with Binary-Input Channels
Yan Hao Ling, Jonathan Scarlett

Abstract
In this paper, we study the problem of relaying a single bit over a tandem of binary-input channels, with the goal of attaining the highest possible error exponent in the exponentially decaying error probability. Our previous work gave an exact characterization of the best possible error exponent in various special cases, including when the two channels are identical, but the general case was left as an open problem. We resolve this open problem by deriving a new converse bound that matches our existing achievability bound.



I
Introduction

The problem of relaying a single bit over tandem of channels has recently gained attention from a number of perspectives, with Huleihel, Polyanskiy, and Shayevitz adopting conventional relaying-based terminology and connecting the problem to that of many-hop relaying (information velocity)
[
1
]
, and Jog and Loh motivating the same problem from the perspective of multi-agent teaching and learning
[
2
]
(see also earlier works such as
[
3

4

). These works focused on a tandem of binary symmetric channels (BSCs), and our subsequent work
[
5
]
showed that in this case, the optimal 2-hop exponent is identical to the 1-hop exponent of the weaker channel.

In
[
5
]
, we additionally considered general binary-input discrete memoryless channels, which we believe to be a natural next step after BSCs given that channel symmetry is not always to be expected in practice (whether in the context of relay channels or teaching/learning). Specifically, we gave achievability and converse bounds that match in certain special cases, with a notable example being that the “1-hop = 2-hop” finding remains true when the two channels are identical. However, we also gave examples where such a result is provably false, and where gaps remained in our bounds.

In this paper, we close all of the remaining gaps from
[
5
]
for the case of binary-input discrete memoryless channels. To do so, we derive an improved converse that matches the achievability result therein. This converse is proved via entirely different techniques to
[
5
]
, where we used a genie argument and a reduction to communication with feedback that appears to be inherently loose.

Chernoff Divergence and its Properties

In this subsection, we let
Q
be a generic binary-input discrete memoryless channel, and write
Q_{x}=Q(\cdot|x)
. We also use
P
(and sometimes
P^{\prime}
) in a generic manner, either to mean a distribution on some alphabet

or a conditional distribution on

(i.e., another discrete memoryless channel). Accordingly, in this subsection, the notations

For two probability distributions
P,P^{\prime}
over some finite set

, the Chernoff divergence with parameter

(
0<s<1
) is given by

d_{\rm C}(P,P^{\prime},s)=-\log\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}P(x)^{1-s}P^{\prime}(x)^{s}.

(23)

We also define
d_{\rm C}(P,P^{\prime},0)
and
d_{\rm C}(P,P^{\prime},1)
by continuity, e.g.,
d_{\rm C}(P,P^{\prime},0)=\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}d_{\rm C}(P,P^{\prime},s)
. Recall from the discussion following Theorem
2
that we only need to consider scenarios where
d_{\rm C}
is finite.

As an alternative for convenient notation, if
P
is a memoryless channel then we define the Chernoff divergence associated with two channel inputs by

\displaystyle d_{\rm C}(x,x^{\prime},P,s)=d_{\rm C}(P_{x},P_{x^{\prime}},s),

(24)

where
P_{x}(\cdot)=P(\cdot|x)
. For any positive integer
k
, we let
P^{k}
denote the
k

P
, with probability mass function
P^{k}(\vec{y}|\vec{x})=\prod_{i=1}^{k}P(y_{i}|x_{i})
.
For two sequences
\vec{x},\vec{x}^{\prime}
of length

, we use the notation

similarly to (
24

.
We will use a well-known tensorization property of

Lemma 8

Since the proof of Theorem

\displaystyle\qquad+(\sqrt{2n}+1)\log\frac{1}{p_{\min}}+\log 4-t\sum_{i=1}^{k}\log\frac{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)})}{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)})}

Lemma 30
.

For each

\widetilde{P}_{t}(y_{1\ldots k})=\exp\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{\rm C}(\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)},\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)},P,t)\right)\\
\times P(y_{1\ldots k}|\Theta=0)^{1-t}P(y_{1\ldots k}|\Theta=1)^{t}

(171)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Big(\exp\left(d_{\rm C}(\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)},\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)},P,t)\right)P(y_{i}|x_{0}^{(i)})^{1-t}P(y_{i}|x_{1}^{(i)})^{t}\Big)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}P(y_{i}|x_{0}^{(i)})^{1-t}P(y_{i}|x_{1}^{(i)})^{t}\exp(d_{\rm C}(\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)},\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)},P,t))

as required.

Lemma 31
.

Under the preceding definitions, it holds that

\sum_{\vec{y}\in A_{2}}\widetilde{P}_{t}(\vec{y})\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(177)

Proof.

Recall the definitions
p_{e,0}(y_{1\ldots k})
and
p_{e,1}(y_{1\ldots k})
from (
8
)–(
9
). Observe that when
k=n
, the conditioning on
\Theta
becomes irrelevant due to the Markov chain relation
\Theta\to y_{1\ldots n}\to\hat{\Theta}
. Thus, for any sequence
y_{1\ldots n}
, we have

p_{e,0}(y_{1\ldots n})=\mathbb{P}_{\vec{z}\sim Q^{n}(\cdot|\vec{w}(y_{1\ldots n}))}(\vec{z}\in D_{1})

(178)

and

p_{e,1}(y_{1\ldots n})=\mathbb{P}_{\vec{z}\sim Q^{n}(\cdot|\vec{w}(y_{1\ldots n}))}(\vec{z}\in D_{0}),

(179)

where
D_{0},D_{1}
are decoding regions corresponding to
\Theta=0
and
\Theta=1
respectively. Since
D_{0}
and
D_{1}
form a partition of the output space, we have
p_{e,0}(y_{1\ldots n})+p_{e,1}(y_{1\ldots n})=1
, which implies that either
p_{e,0}(y_{1\ldots n})\geq\frac{1}{2}
or
p_{e,1}(y_{1\ldots n})\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

By the definition of
A_{2}
, every element of
A_{2}
has length exactly
n
, and (
121
)–(
122
) must both be false. If
p_{e,0}(y_{1\ldots n})\geq\frac{1}{2}
, then the negation of (
121
) (along with the crude bounds
t\leq 1
and
E(n-k)\geq 0
) implies

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)})}{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)})}\right|

\displaystyle~~\geq(\sqrt{2n}+3)\log\frac{1}{p_{\min}}+\log 4+\log p_{e,0}(y_{1\ldots n})

\displaystyle~~\geq\sqrt{2n}\log\frac{1}{p_{\min}}.

(180)

Similarly, if
p_{e,1}(y_{1\ldots n})\geq\frac{1}{2}
, then the negation of (
122
) implies

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)})}{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)})}\right|

\displaystyle~~\geq(\sqrt{2n}+4)\log\frac{1}{p_{\min}}+\log 4+\log p_{e,1}(y_{1\ldots n})

\displaystyle~~\geq\sqrt{2n}\log\frac{1}{p_{\min}}.

(181)

Since one of these two cases must hold, we conclude that for each
y_{1\ldots n}\in A_{2}
,

\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)})}{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)})}\right|\geq\sqrt{2n}\log\frac{1}{p_{\min}}.

(182)

Let
B
be the set of all strings of length
n
such that (
123
) and (
182
) are true, noting that
A_{2}\subseteq B
. Instead of doing the summation over
A_{2}
, it is easier to sum over
B
. Thus, we we will instead show that

\sum_{\vec{y}\in B}\widetilde{P}_{t}(\vec{y})\leq\frac{1}{2},

(183)

or equivalently,

\mathbb{P}_{y_{1\ldots n}\sim\widetilde{P}_{t}}\Bigg(\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)})}{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)})}\right)d_{\rm C}^{\prime}(\vec{x}_{0},\vec{x}_{1},P^{n},t)<0\\
\hbox{ and }\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)})}{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)})}\right|\geq\sqrt{2n}\log\frac{1}{p_{\min}}\Bigg)\leq\frac{1}{2}.

(184)

Observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{y_{1\ldots n}\sim\widetilde{P}_{t}}\left(\log\frac{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)})}{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)})}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:Ptilde_def}}}{{=}}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}P(y|\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)})^{1-t}P(y|\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)})^{1-t}\exp(d_{\rm C}(\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)},\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)},P,t))

\displaystyle\hskip 128.0374pt\times\left(\log\frac{P(y|\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)})}{P(y|\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)})}\right)

(185)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:expected_s_log}}}{{=}}d_{\rm C}^{\prime}(\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)},\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)},P,t).

(186)

Under the distribution
\widetilde{P}_{t}
, the entries of
y_{1\ldots n}
are independent, and therefore

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\frac{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)})}{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)})}

(187)

is a sum of independent variables with expected value

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left(\log\frac{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)})}{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)})}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{\rm C}^{\prime}(\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)},\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)},P,t)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lem.}~\ref{lem:iid}}}{{=}}d_{\rm C}^{\prime}(\vec{x}_{0},\vec{x}_{1},P^{n},t)

(188)

and the variance is bounded above by

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\log\frac{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)})}{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)})}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max_{y_{i}\,:\,\widetilde{P}_{t}^{(i)}(y_{i})>0}\left(\log\frac{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{0}^{(i)})}{P(y_{i}|\vec{x}_{1}^{(i)})}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq n\left(\log\frac{1}{p_{\min}}\right)^{2}.

(189)

The event that
\vec{y}\in B
is equivalent to (

and having absolute value at least

, which means that it must deviate from its expected value by at least
\sqrt{2n}\log\frac{1}{p_{\min}}
. We can thus use Chevyshev inequality to obtain

\sum_{\vec{y}\in B}\widetilde{P}_{t}(\vec{y})\leq\frac{n\big(\log\frac{1}{p_{\min}}\big)^{2}}{(\sqrt{2n}\log\frac{1}{p_{\min}})^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}.

(190)

which completes the proof of Lemma
177

4
.
∎

VII
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied the error exponent of relaying a single bit over a tandem of channels. We proved a matching converse bound to the protocol in
[
5
]
whenever the two channels have binary inputs.

In
[
5
]
, we also raised another open problem regarding channels with more than two inputs:
Does there exist a pair of DMCs
P
,
Q
, such that every restriction of
Q
to two inputs gives a strictly suboptimal learning rate?
Since the present paper only deals with binary-input channels, this problem still remains open. The techniques used in this paper do not appear to generalize easily to channels with more than two inputs; for instance, the use of Lemma
16
requires bounding certain error probabilities by the number of positions in which the strings are 0 and 1 respectively, and it is unclear to what extent this can be extended to non-binary inputs. At a more conceptual level, having all three inputs could, in principle, be beneficial compared to having only the “best two” of them; for example, a third input could be used by the relay to convey useful information such as the degree of uncertainty.
