Nonperturbative collapse models for collisionless self-gravitating flows
Niels Fardeau, Thomas Buchert, Fosca Al Roumi, Fereshteh Felegary

Abstract
Structure formation in the Universe has been well-studied within the Eulerian and Lagrangian perturbation theories, where the latter performs substantially better in comparison with N-body simulations. Standing out is the celebrated Zel’dovich approximation for dust matter. In this work, we recall the description of gravitational noncollisional systems and extend both the Eulerian and Lagrangian approaches by including, possibly anisotropic, velocity dispersion. A simple case with plane symmetry is then studied with an exact, nonperturbative approach, and various approximations of the derived model are then compared numerically. A striking result is that linearized Lagrangian solutions outperform models based on Burgers’ equation in the multistream regime in comparison with the exact solution. These results are finally extended to a 3D case without symmetries, and master equations for the evolution of all parts of the perturbations are derived. The particular 3D case studied corresponds to a maximally anisotropic collapse, which involves an approximation based on the estimation of importance of the different levels of spatial derivatives of the local deformation field.



I
Introduction

Structures in the Universe, such as galaxies and their clustering into superunits, originate in the gravitational collapse of large, dilute matter clouds. These collapsing clouds are
well-described by a fluid of massive particles in gravitational interaction but with no collisions with each other, a so-called collisionless self-gravitating flow, treated in terms of deviations from the homogeneous expansion of the Universe, described by the commonly adopted Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker (FLRW) models. Gravitational interactions in these systems are to date mostly described in Newtonian theory
bonnor_jeans_1957
;
peebles:book
, although first studies of this kind were done in the general-relativistic (GR) framework
lifshitz46
;
lifshitzkhalatnikov
. The dynamics of the collapse is described by the laws of hydrodynamics, which can be derived from conservation laws in Hamiltonian phase space through various approaches, including continuum
mean-field
approximation (Vlasov equation) or the Klimontovic
coarse-graining
approach
binney_galactic_2008
;
buchert_adhesive_2005
. However, these equations are highly nonlinear and do not admit analytical solutions in the general case. First attempts at describing structure formation in the late Universe used linearized versions of perturbation theory, referred to as the
Eulerian perturbation theory
. This theory is based on the assumption that the density contrast (i.e., the dimensionless deviation from the mean density of the universe model) is small. However, although the volume-averaged density contrast over large regions of the Universe (typically of radius
\sim{10}{\rm Mpc}
) is of order unity, it can reach much larger values in galaxy clusters (
\sim 10
) or in galaxies themselves (
\sim 10^{5}
). For this reason, the Eulerian perturbation theory is insufficient, and other approaches have to be investigated.

Part of the nonlinearity of the hydrodynamical equations stems from the convective derivative that accounts for the motion of the fluid elements. For this reason, the Eulerian description of the fluid is not best-suited, and a Lagrangian approach is more efficient
serrin
,
ehlersbuchert
,
buchert_varenna
. In the Lagrangian picture, fluid elements themselves constitute the coordinate system, assumed to be constant along the flow, so that there is no longer the need to account for their motion with respect to an Eulerian reference system. Following what was done in Eulerian perturbation theory, new perturbative approaches, based on the Lagrangian framework, appeared. Among them is the celebrated
Zel’dovich approximation

zeldovich70a
;
zeldovich70b
, which for the first time described the collapsing elements as highly anisotropic structures (
pancakes
),
rather than spherically symmetric structures as it was understood before. These theories provide solutions for higher-order approximations and encompass nonlinearity to a better extent than Eulerian theories, yielding much more accurate results, even competing with those of many-body simulations.
For a summary and a comprehensive list of references, see the historical account on the Lagrangian approach in Newtonian cosmology in the otherwise GR-based review
Universe
.

II
The Euler-Jeans-Newton system

To set notations, we use Einstein’s summation convention for repeated indices regardless of their (up or down) position (the Newtonian metric is Euclidean). Derivatives with respect to Eulerian coordinates
\{x_{i}\}
are denoted by a subscript comma, and derivatives with respect to Lagrangian coordinates
\{X_{i}\}
are denoted by a subscript vertical bar, e.g.

\partialderivative{f}{x_i}:=\partial_{i}f:=f_{,i}\ ,\quad\partialderivative{f}{X_i}:=f_{|i}.

Vector differential operators without a subscript indicate differentiation with respect to Eulerian coordinates. Vector differential operators with subscript 0 indicate differentiation with respect to Lagrangian coordinates, e.g.

\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{A}=A_{k,k}\ ,\quad\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{0}\cdot\mathbf{A}=A_{k|k}.

Other coordinate systems will be explicitly denoted by an appropriate subscript.

II.1
General formulation

We consider a fluid embedded into Galilei-Newton spacetime described by its Hamiltonian phase space density
f(t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})
in Eulerian phase space coordinates for positions and velocities. Under the hypothesis that the acceleration
\mathbf{b}
is given by the gravitational field
\mathbf{g}
and is therefore velocity-independent, the evolution equation for the phase space density
f
is given by the Vlasov equation (see e.g.
binney_galactic_2008
;
buchert:adhesive
),

\partialderivative{f}{t}+v_{i}\partialderivative{f}{x_i}+g_{i}\partialderivative{f}{v_i}=0.

(1)

The first moments of this equation provide evolution equations in space for the restmass and momentum density (higher-order velocity tensors are constructed in the same fashion),

\rho:=m\int f\,d^{3}v,\quad\rho\overline{v}_{i}:=m\int v_{i}f\,d^{3}v,

(2)

in the form

\displaystyle\partialderivative{t}\rho+\partialderivative{x_i}(\rho\overline v_i)=0,

(3a)

\displaystyle\partialderivative{t}(\rho\overline v_i)+\partialderivative{x_j}(\rho\overline{v_iv_j})=\rho g_{i}.

(3b)

Introducing the reduced second velocity moment tensor,

\Pi_{ij}:=\rho\quantity(\overline{v_iv_j}-\overline v_i\overline v_j),

(4)

and the Lagrangian time-derivative
d_{t}=\partial_{t}+\overline{v}_{k}\partial_{k}
, we can write (
3b
) as the
Euler-Jeans
equation

\rho\derivative{t}\overline{v}_{i}=\rho g_{i}-\partialderivative{x_j}\Pi_{ij}.

(5)

The reduced second velocity moment tensor is symmetric and acts like a stress tensor, the divergence of which creates a (multistream) force that counteracts the gravitational field strength. An evolution equation can be found for this tensor, using the second velocity moment of the Vlasov equation (details can be found in Appendix
A
), in the form

\derivative{t}\Pi_{ij}=-\quantity[\pdv{\overline v_k}{x_k}\Pi_{ij}+\pdv{\overline v_i}{x_k}\Pi_{jk}+\pdv{\overline v_j}{x_k}\Pi_{ki}]-\partialderivative{x_k}L_{ijk},

(6)

where
L_{ijk}
is the third reduced velocity moment

L_{ijk}:=\rho\overline{(v_{i}-\overline{v}_{i})(v_{j}-\overline{v}_{j})(v_{k}-\overline{v}_{k})}.

(7)

However, this equation cannot yield a closed system, due to the introduction of the third-order tensor
L_{ijk}
. One way to close it is to consider that deviations from the mean velocity are small (which is valid in the pre-virialization regime and before any shell-crossing), typically
|\mathbf{v}-\overline{\mathbf{v}}|\sim\epsilon|\overline{\mathbf{v}}|
, which allows to estimate the higher-order moments
\Pi_{ij}\sim\rho\epsilon^{2}|\overline{\mathbf{v}}|^{2}
and
L_{ijk}\sim\rho\epsilon^{3}|\overline{\mathbf{v}}|^{3}
. If
\epsilon
is small enough, one can drop the
L_{ijk}
term in (
6
). For later stages of evolution, the omission of the third-order term remains phenomenological, which should be kept in mind when solutions of the following system are studied. With this truncation of the hierarchy, the resulting system, along with the Newtonian field equations for the gravitational field strength, is called the
Euler-Jeans-Newton
(EJN) system and reads:

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\rho=-\rho\overline{v}_{k,k},

(8a)

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\overline{v}_{i}=g_{i}-\dfrac{1}{\rho}\Pi_{ij,j},

(8b)

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\Pi_{ij}=-\quantity[\overline v_{k,k}\Pi_{ij}+\overline v_{i,k}\Pi_{jk}+\overline v_{j,k}\Pi_{ki}],

(8c)

\displaystyle g_{[i,j]}=0,\quad g_{k,k}=\Lambda-4\pi G\rho.

(8d)

III
Lagrangian formulation of the Euler-Jeans-Newton system

In this section, we base our analysis on the detailed investigation for an isotropic-pressure supported fluid in
adler_lagrangian_1998
, and generalize to nonisotropic velocity dispersion. The reader may also consult the investigation in
ruth:vorticity
within the Lagrangian picture, emphasizing the impact of anisotropic stresses in dispersion-supported fluids.

III.1
Lagrangian picture

We consider that the Eulerian coordinates
\mathbf{x}
can be related to the Lagrangian coordinates,
\mathbf{X}
,
via
a time-dependent diffeomorphism
\mathbf{f}
, that is

\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{X},t),

(12)

with
f(\mathbf{X},t_{0})=\mathbf{X}
. Lagrangian coordinates stay attached to fluid elements along their trajectories, meaning in particular that
{\derivative{t}}{\mathbf{X}}={\bf 0}
. The Lagrangian time-derivative thus merely reads as the partial time-derivative in the Lagrangian frame,

\derivative{t}=\evaluated{\pdv{t}}_{\vb X}=\evaluated{\pdv{t}}_{\vb x}+\mathbf{v}\cdot\gradient.

(13)

We also define the Jacobian matrix of the transformation from Lagrangian to Eulerian coordinates, and its determinant,

J_{ik}(\mathbf{X},t):=\partialderivative{f_i}{X_k},\quad J(\mathbf{X},t):=\det J_{ik}(\mathbf{X},t).

(14)

According to usual calculus rules, volume elements then transform as

d^{3}x=Jd^{3}X.

(15)

In the Lagrangian framework, the diffeomorphism
\mathbf{f}
is the only dynamical variable. The velocity and acceleration fields are directly given by the definitions

\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{X},t)=\dot{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{X},t),\quad\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{X},t)=\ddot{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{X},t).

(16)

The continuity equation, which represents the total restmass conservation within a compact domain
D_{t}
(evolving in time in the Eulerian frame, but its boundaries are comoving with the fluid), is solved by an exact integral for the density field as a functional of
\mathbf{f}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\derivative{t}M_{D_{t}}=0

\displaystyle=\derivative{t}\int_{D_{t}}\rho(\mathbf{x},t)\,d^{3}x=\derivative{t}\int_{D_{t_{0}}}\rho(\mathbf{X},t)J(\mathbf{X},t)\,d^{3}X

\displaystyle=\int_{D_{t}}\dfrac{1}{J}\derivative{t}\quantity(\rho(\vb X,t)J(\vb X,t))\,d^{3}x,{}

(17)

which implies that the quantity
\rho J
is conserved along flow lines, i.e.

\rho J=\rho_{\rm i}J_{\rm i}:=C(\mathbf{X})\Rightarrow\rho(\mathbf{X},t)=\dfrac{C(\mathbf{X})}{J(\mathbf{X},t)}.

(18)

(Since
J_{\rm i}=1
,
C=\rho_{\rm i}
.)

III.2
Lagrangian derivative

In the process of getting rid of Eulerian coordinates, we need to transfer Eulerian derivatives to the Lagrangian frame
adler_lagrangian_1998
. To do so, we consider the inverse diffeomorphism of
\mathbf{f}
,

\mathbf{h}:=\mathbf{f}^{-1},\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{x},t).

(19)

Using this definition, we can write

\partialderivative{x_i}=\partialderivative{h_k}{x_i}\partialderivative{X_k}=J^{-1}_{ik}\partialderivative{X_k},

(20a)

and with the definition of the adjoint matrix,

\mathrm{ad}\,J_{ik}:=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{klm}\epsilon_{ipq}f_{p|l}f_{q|m},

(20b)

we get the transformation rule

\partialderivative{x_i}=\dfrac{1}{2J}\epsilon_{klm}\epsilon_{ipq}f_{p|l}f_{q|m}\partialderivative{X_k}.

(20c)

To further lighten the notations, we define the functional determinant

\mathcal{J}(A,B,C)=\dfrac{\partial(A,B,C)}{\partial(X_{1},X_{2},X_{3})}=\epsilon_{ikl}A_{|i}B_{|k}C_{|l}.

(21a)

The functional determinant satisfies the usual rules of determinants, plus the Leibniz rule,

\mathcal{J}(AD,B,C)=A\mathcal{J}(D,B,C)+D\mathcal{J}(A,B,C).

(21b)

Using this notation and Eq. (
20c
), the Eulerian derivative of a field
\cal A
can be written as

{\cal A}_{,i}=\dfrac{1}{2J}\epsilon_{ikl}\mathcal{J}({\cal A},f_{k},f_{l}).

(22)

III.3
Lagrangian form of the field equations

As it has been said above, the continuity equation and the momentum conservation equation are automatically solved in the Lagrangian picture through definitions and an exact integral,

\mathbf{v}=\dot{\mathbf{f}},\quad\mathbf{b}=\ddot{\mathbf{f}},\quad\rho=\dfrac{\rho_{0}J_{0}}{J}.

(23)

Let us now consider the field equations of the Euler-Jeans-Newton system. With (

\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{b}+\dfrac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{\psi},\quad\psi_{i}\equiv\Pi_{ij,j}=\pi_{ij,j}.

Using vector identities, Equation (

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf{b}+\dfrac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{\psi}-\dfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\gradient\rho\times\boldsymbol{\psi}=\mathbf{0},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{b}+\dfrac{1}{\rho}\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}-\dfrac{1}{\rho^{2}}\boldsymbol{\psi}\cdot\gradient\rho=\Lambda-4\pi G(\rho+3p_{H}).

(25b)

As we may consider a relativistic Friedmannian background, the homogeneous pressure

(26b)

Replacing

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{J}\mathcal{J}(\ddot{f}_{k},f_{k},f_{i})+\dfrac{1}{2\rho J}\epsilon_{pqr}\mathcal{J}\quantity(\dfrac{1}{J}\J(\pi_{kp},f_q,f_r),f_k,f_i)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}+\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3},

(30b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{III}(\mathbb{A})=\det\mathbb{A}=\dfrac{1}{6}\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{lmn}A_{il}A_{jm}A_{kn}=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}.

In particular, we are interested in the invariants of the gradient of the deformation field

IV
Nonperturbative plane-symmetric gravitational collapse with velocity dispersion

IV.1
Motivation

The first two sections of this work gave the theoretical framework needed to investigate large-scale structure formation in the late Universe. In the following, we will apply this framework to systems with isotropic (plane-symmetric) and anisotropic (three-dimensional) velocity dispersion, with a nonperturbative approach.

Before diving into those cases, it is useful to remind the results obtained from standard Eulerian perturbation theory (see e.g.
peebles:book
, for a systematic list of the different approximations involved and the notion of “nonperturbative”, see
nonperturbative
). Starting from the Euler-Jeans-Newton system in the Eulerian comoving picture (
11
), considering only isotropic pressure with a dynamical equation of state
\pi_{ij}=p\delta_{ij}=\alpha(\rho)\delta_{ij}
, and keeping only terms of order 1 in peculiar-fields, one obtains the following Eulerian evolution equation for the density contrast
\delta
:

\evaluated{\pdv[2]{t}}_{\vb q}\delta+2H\evaluated{\pdv{t}}_{\vb q}\delta-4\pi G\rho_{H}\delta=\dfrac{\alpha^{\prime}}{a^{2}}\laplacian_{\mathbf{q}}\delta.

(34)

The right-hand side of this equation may not be linear due to the term
\alpha^{\prime}(\rho)
, which can have any dependence on
\rho
and shall then be linearized too. Here we can also introduce a characteristic length for self-gravitating systems with pressure, called
Jeans’ length
, and reading

L_{J}^{2}=\dfrac{\alpha^{\prime}}{4\pi G\rho_{H}},

(35)

so that the above equation can be rewritten as

\evaluated{\pdv[2]{t}}_{\vb q}\delta+2H\evaluated{\pdv{t}}_{\vb q}\delta-4\pi G\rho_{H}\delta=4\pi G\rho_{H}\dfrac{L_{J}^{2}}{a^{2}}\laplacian_{\mathbf{q}}\delta.

(36)

Jeans’ length represents the typical size of the self-gravitating system below which the system collapses under its own gravity, and above which it expands (for more details on Eulerian perturbation theory and self-gravitating systems stability, see
binney_galactic_2008
).

Moving now to the LJN system (
29
) with isotropic pressure, using the ansatz (
31
) and linearizing with respect to
\mathbf{P}
, one obtains a similar evolution equation for the longitudinal (irrotational) part
\mathbf{P}^{L}
of
\mathbf{P}
:

\ddot{\mathbf{P}}^{L}+2H\dot{\mathbf{P}}^{L}-4\pi G\rho_{H}\mathbf{P}^{L}=\dfrac{\alpha^{\prime}}{a^{2}}\laplacian_{0}\mathbf{P}^{L},

(37)

which can be written in terms of
\delta
using the (linearized) relation
\delta=-\gradient_{0}\cdot\mathbf{P}=-\gradient_{0}\cdot\mathbf{P}^{L}
. However, it is to be noted that Lagrange-linear equations are not Euler-linear, due to the presence of the convective derivative. The Lagrangian approach is
intrinsically nonlinear
. Yet, although Lagrange-linear approximation allows us to encompass nonlinearity to some extent, it is still a perturbative approach and relies on various approximations, which can be found in detail in
nonperturbative
,
along with the most general evolution equation for the perturbations.

In the following, we will first solve the LJN system (
29
) in the case of plane symmetry
exactly
using Burgers’ equation and Fourier analysis, which removes the anisotropy of velocity dispersion and renders the equations more manageable. Then, we will move on to three-dimensional anisotropic deviations, which we will solve by using a “locally one-dimensional” approach arising from a maximal anisotropy hypothesis.

IV.3
Explicit solution

Equation (
50
) has been solved in
al_roumi_matiere_2011
after linearization in
P_{1}
using Fourier analysis, and the following expression was found:

P_{1}(X_{1},t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}C_{1}(X_{1}-S)A(S,t)dS,

(51)

where

\displaystyle A(S,t)=

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{y\to 0^{+}}\quantity(\dfrac{a}{2y}+2\Re\qty[\mathcal{G}(S)\int_{\;\qty(\sqrt{\frac{6c}{a}}-iS)\;y}^\infty\dfrac{e^{-z}}{z}\dd z])

\displaystyle\text{with}\ \ \mathcal{G}(S)=\dfrac{aS^{2}+i\sqrt{6ac}S}{2c\quantity(\sqrt{\frac{6c}{a}}-iS)}

\displaystyle\text{and}\ \ C_{1}(X_{1})=\int k_{1}^{2}e^{-k_{1}^{2}}e^{ik_{1}X_{1}}\differential k_{1}.

(52)

Here, we want to be more general than the linear approximation. In the case of plane symmetry, both the peculiar-acceleration
\mathbf{w}
and the peculiar-velocity
\mathbf{u}
have only one nonzero component in the direction
q_{1}
, and are therefore parallel, so that we can write in general

\mathbf{w}=h(\mathbf{q},t)\mathbf{u}.

(53)

This condition is not generally verified in the absence of symmetry in the model, but is usually postulated as an approximation (so-called “slaving condition”). Assuming further that the proportionality factor
h
is the same as in the Eulerian linear
dust
(pressureless) model (and in particular, only depends on time), we obtain the so-called “adhesion approximation”
gurbatov89
,
buchert_adhesive_2005
;
extending
:

\mathbf{w}=h(t)\mathbf{u}\ \ ~~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1},}}~~h(t)=4\pi G\rho_{H}\dfrac{b}{\dot{b}}\ \ ~~{{\color[rgb]{0,0,1},}}~~\ddot{b}+2H\dot{b}-4\pi G\rho_{H}b=0.

(54)

This approximation is supposed to be valid in the weakly nonlinear regime with pressure, before any shell-crossing singularities appear. Injecting this approximation into the EJN system (
11
), along with the expression (
49
) for the pressure term, changing the time variable to
b
and rescaling the peculiar-velocity as
\tilde{u}=u_{1}/a\dot{b}
, we obtain the equation:

\derivative{\tilde u}{b}=\mu(\rho,t)\partialderivative[2]{\tilde u}{q_1},\quad\mu(\rho,t)=3\beta\rho_{H,0}^{2}(1+\delta)\dfrac{b}{a^{4}\dot{b}^{2}}.

(55)

The coefficient
\mu
formally plays the role of a time and density-dependent viscosity term, although the equations are fully time-reversible. If
\mu
is high enough, the viscosity term in the above equation can prevent the system from developing shell-crossing singularities. Setting
\mu
as constant, the above equation is Burgers’ equation, which solutions are known analytically
kevorkian
. Using the Cole-Hopf transform

\tilde{u}=-2\mu\partialderivative{q_1}\ln\phi,

(56)

Burgers’ equation (with periodic boundary conditions) turns into a heat equation for

\partialderivative{\phi}{b}=\mu\partialderivative[2]{\phi}{q_1}.

(57)

This equation can be solved using Fourier analysis, and then inverting the Cole-Hopf transform leads to the general solution of (
55
) with constant viscosity and initial condition

\displaystyle\tilde{u}(q_{1},b)=-2\mu\partialderivative{q_1}\ln\mathfrak{A}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{A}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi\mu b}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\mathfrak{B}\differential s;

\displaystyle\mathfrak{B}=[-\dfrac{(q_{1}-s)^{2}}{4\mu b}-\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{s}\tilde{U}(\xi)\differential\xi].

IV.4
Numerical simulation results

In order to compare the different approximations that were made above with the exact solution of the nonperturbative equation (
50
), we numerically integrate it and compare the predicted evolution at different times with the various approximations. All simulations start from the time of recombination, where the scale factor is taken to be unity. Every equation is integrated using implicit second-order finite-differences schemes
kevorkian
. We first integrate both the exact and the linearized versions of Equation (
50
) in Lagrangian coordinates (recall that the overdot is merely a partial time-derivative). The resulting

is then mapped to Eulerian Hubble-comoving space as

, where

is the initial peculiar-velocity field. Boundary conditions are assumed periodic.

55
) is integrated in Eulerian Hubble-comoving coordinates, where the total derivative

reads

and boundary conditions are assumed periodic. Finally, the same procedure is applied to solve Burgers’ equation with constant viscosity, where the value of
\mu
is taken as the average value of the time-dependent viscosity coefficient over the integration interval. However, there is always a degree of freedom in the definition of
\mu
, as the constant
\beta
is
a priori
unknown. In our simulations, the value of
\beta
will be manually adjusted so that Burgers’ solution coincides best with the exact solution.

V
Three-dimensional gravitational collapse with velocity dispersion

In this section we aim at extending the results previously obtained in the plane-symmetric case to a more general case without any particular symmetry, in the spirit of
doroshkevichetal
.

V.1
Maximal anisotropy and estimate of importance of derivatives

Three-dimensional collapsing fluids were first understood in the case of spherically-symmetric systems, which allowed for nonlinear analytical developments. However, the work of Zel’dovich (e.g., among many others,
zeldovich70a
;
zeldovich70b
) has shown that these systems are not spherically symmetric, but instead
pancake
-like, exhibiting maximal anisotropy in one particular direction. Another way of saying this is to consider the scalar invariants of the Lagrangian perturbation tensor
P_{i|j}
(that have been defined previously). Maximal anisotropy implies that this tensor has one dominating eigenvalue, which we may call
\lambda_{1}
:
\lambda_{1}\gg\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
, which implies that the first scalar invariant is dominating the other two:
\mathrm{I}\gg\mathrm{II},\mathrm{III}
, see the appendix of
vigneron:darkmatter
for a recent collection of arguments and numerical tests.

This approximation only takes into account the qualitative kinematics of the collapse, without any consideration on the magnitude of the individual coefficients
P_{i|j}
. However, it can be shown in the context of general relativity that both the perturbation gradient
P_{i|j}
and its first derivatives
P_{i|jk}
, respectively related to the perturbations of the 3-metric
g_{ij}
and the spatial connection coefficients
\Gamma^{i}_{\,jk}
, can be considered small in regular systems (i.e., without singularities)
buchert_geometrical_2009
. However, the second derivatives (related to the spatial curvature) are not typically small in those systems. Although in Newtonian cosmology spacetime is flat, the analogy and the two-scale argument of
buchert_geometrical_2009
prevails algebraically in the Lagrangian representation of the Newtonian system with the Lagrangian metric,
g_{k\ell}=\delta_{ij}f^{i}_{\ |k}f^{j}_{\ |\ell}
, and the same arguments allow to estimate the smallness in magnitude of the different levels of derivatives. The reader may consult the explicit presentation of this correspondence in
correspondence
.

Taking into account both of these approximations (i.e. the perturbation gradient itself and its first derivatives are small), one can simplify the expressions involved in Equations (
29
) by neglecting all nonlinear terms in the perturbations or their first derivatives, along with terms proportional to II and III, but keeping second derivatives.

V.2
Field equations

Using the hypotheses made above, the remaining terms involved in (
29
) read:

\displaystyle J=a^{3}(1+\mathrm{I})=a^{3}(1+P_{k|k}),

(66a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(J,f_{i},f_{j})=a^{5}\epsilon_{kij}\mathrm{I}_{|k},

(66b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{J}(J,f_{i},f_{j}),f_{i},f_{j})=2a^{7}\mathrm{I}_{|kk},

(66c)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{kij}\mathcal{J}(\ddot{f}_{k},f_{i},f_{j})=6\dfrac{\ddot{a}}{a}a^{3}(1+\mathrm{I})+4Ha^{3}\dot{\mathrm{I}}+2a^{3}\ddot{\mathrm{I}}.

(66d)

Moreover, considering that the anisotropy
\Delta_{ij}
is bound to the same level of approximation imposed on the whole perturbation gradient, the last term evaluates to

\epsilon_{kij}\epsilon_{pqr}\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{J}(\Delta_{kp},f_{q},f_{r}),f_{i},f_{j})=4a^{4}\Delta_{kp|pk}.

(66e)

Injecting all the above expressions into the field equation (
29b
), one obtains:

\displaystyle 3\dfrac{\ddot{a}}{a}+2H\dfrac{\dot{\mathrm{I}}}{1+\mathrm{I}}+\dfrac{\ddot{\mathrm{I}}}{1+\mathrm{I}}-\dfrac{\alpha^{\prime}}{a^{2}}\dfrac{\mathrm{I}_{|kk}}{(1+\mathrm{I})^{3}}+\dfrac{a}{C_{H}}\dfrac{\Delta_{kp|pk}}{1+\mathrm{I}}

\displaystyle=\Lambda-4\pi G\quantity(\dfrac{C_H}{a^3(1+\I)}+3p_H).

(67)

Injecting Friedmann’s equation (
41
) and linearizing with respect to
\mathrm{I}
then yields:

\ddot{\mathrm{I}}+2H\,\dot{\mathrm{I}}-4\pi G\dfrac{C_{H}}{a^{3}}\mathrm{I}=\dfrac{\alpha^{\prime}}{a^{2}}(1-2\mathrm{I})\laplacian_{0}\,\mathrm{I}+\dfrac{a}{C_{H}}\Delta_{kp|pk}.

(68)

The form of this equation is reassuring, as the left-hand side is the well-known time-derivative operator appearing in every perturbative development discussed in Section
IV.1
. The Laplacian term on the right-hand side is also the same as in Lagrangian-linear perturbation theory, reminiscent of Equation (
37
). The remaining term encodes the effects of the anisotropy of the velocity dispersion. It is to be noted though, that the Lagrangian Laplacian appearing in the right-hand side is only Lagrangian due to the linearization in the first invariant
\rm I
and its gradient. Quadratic terms in
\rm I
and all Lagrange-nonlinear terms neglected above sum up to the full Eulerian Laplacian.

Now we proceed by determining equations of state for the isotropic pressure
p
and the anisotropic dispersion
\Delta_{ij}
, in the form
p=\alpha(\rho)
and
\Delta_{ij}=\beta_{ij}(\rho,\mathbf{v})
. The (full) velocity field
\mathbf{v}
, being the source of anisotropy in the system, the anisotropic part of the velocity dispersion tensor must be a function of the latter, and not only of the density. At this point it is useful to introduce the kinematic decomposition of the full Eulerian velocity gradient
v_{i,j}
:

v_{i,j}=\dfrac{1}{3}\theta\delta_{ij}+\sigma_{ij}+\omega_{ij},

(69)

where
\theta=v_{k,k}=-\dot{\rho}/\rho
is the rate of expansion,
\sigma_{ij}
the shear tensor (symmetric and traceless) and
\omega_{ij}
the vorticity tensor (antisymmetric). Using the definition (
9a
) of the peculiar-velocity, we can write this decomposition for the peculiar-velocity as

\dfrac{1}{a}\partialderivative{u_i}{q_j}=\dfrac{1}{3}(\theta-3H)\delta_{ij}+\sigma_{ij}+\omega_{ij}.

(70)

Injecting this decomposition into the Euler-Jeans equation (
11c
), we obtain:

\derivative{t}\quantity(p\delta_{ij}+\Delta_{ij})+\dfrac{5}{3}\theta\;\quantity(p\delta_{ij}+\Delta_{ij})=-2\quantity[p\sigma_{ij}+\sigma_{ik}\Delta_{kj}].

(71)

Taking the trace of this equation and taking into account that both
\sigma_{ij}
and
\Delta_{ij}
are traceless yields

3\dfrac{\dot{p}}{p}+5\theta=-2\sigma_{ik}\Delta_{ki}.

(72)

This has once again the expected form, leading in the isotropic limit
\Delta_{ij}=0
to the classical polytropic solution
p\propto\rho^{5/3}
reminiscent of an adiabatic ideal gas, which is a correct analogy if we consider our system as a dilute fluid of massive particles, the difference being the kinetic pressure source which does not originate from collisions between particles but from (isotropic) velocity dispersion. However, here the anisotropic part of the velocity dispersion has a feedback effect on the isotropic pressure, which alters the equation of state and makes
p
become also velocity-dependent (
via
the shear tensor).

\sigma_{ij}
and
\Delta_{ij}

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\quantity(p\delta_{ij}+\Delta_{ij})+\dfrac{5}{3}\theta\;\quantity(p\delta_{ij}+\Delta_{ij})=-2p\sigma_{ij},

(73a)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\dot{p}}{p}+\dfrac{5}{3}\theta=0,

(73b)

and the trace equation is immediately solved by
p=\kappa\rho^{5/3}
, where
\kappa
can be set constant with appropriate initial conditions. Injecting back this solution into the first equation then yields

\dot{\Delta}_{ij}+\dfrac{5}{3}\theta\Delta_{ij}=-2\kappa\rho^{5/3}\sigma_{ij}.

(74)

It is interesting here to see that
\Delta_{ij}
indeed depends on the total velocity field
\mathbf{v}
, and not only on the peculiar-velocity, despite the Hubble-velocity being isotropic. For the sake of consistency, we translate this equation into the Lagrangian picture. Using the integrals (
23
) for
\rho
and
\mathbf{v}
, we obtain the following expressions:

\displaystyle\theta=\dfrac{\dot{J}}{J},

(75a)

\displaystyle p=\kappa\dfrac{C_{H}}{J^{5/3}},

(75b)

\displaystyle\sigma_{ij}=\dfrac{1}{4J}\quantity(\epsilon_{jk\ell}\J(\dot f_i,f_k,f_\ell)+\epsilon_{ik\ell}\J(\dot f_j,f_k,f_\ell)).

(75c)

Within our approximations on the importance of different levels of derivatives, these reduce to

\displaystyle\theta=3H+\dot{\mathrm{I}},

(76a)

\displaystyle p=\dfrac{C_{H}^{5/3}}{a^{5}}\quantity(1-\dfrac{5}{3}\I),

\displaystyle\ddot{\mathrm{I}}+2H\,\dot{\mathrm{I}}-4\pi G\dfrac{C_{H}}{a^{3}}\mathrm{I}

(77a)

(77b)

Yet this system is not closed, as it contains nine unknowns (three coefficients of the perturbation vector
\mathbf{P}
and six coefficients of the symmetric anisotropic velocity dispersion tensor) and only eight equations (one scalar equation (

\gradient_{0}\times\quantity[\ddot{\vb P}+2H\dot{\vb P}+\dfrac{a}{C_H}\grad_0\cdot\vb\Delta]={\bf 0}.

V.3
Discussion

Looking at the previous set of equations governing the evolution of the perturbation gradient, it is interesting to note the similarity with the plane-symmetric case, and in particular Equations (
45
) and (
50
). Indeed, linearizing the former with respect to
P_{1},P_{1|1}
and
P_{1|11}
as prescribed by the GR analogy, we recover an equation much similar to (
79a
). The main difference between the two comes from the equation of state, which for the plane-symmetric case was
p\propto\rho^{3}
and in the approximate 3D case is
p\propto\rho^{5/3}
. This only changes the coefficient in front of the Laplacian, which we may call the
speed of sound
. Indeed, for such a self-gravitating fluid, the speed of sound waves can be defined as

c_{s}^{2}=\derivative{p}{\rho}=\alpha^{\prime}(\rho)=\dfrac{5}{3}\kappa\rho_{H}^{2/3},

(80)

which allows to interpret Equation (
79
) as a nonlinear wave equation, rewritten in the form

\displaystyle\ddot{P}-\dfrac{c_{s}^{2}}{a^{2}}\laplacian_{0}P+2H\dot{P}-4\pi G\rho_{H}P

\displaystyle=\dfrac{8}{3}\dfrac{c_{s}^{2}}{a^{2}}P\laplacian_{0}P+\dfrac{1}{\rho_{H}a^{2}}\Delta_{kp|pk}\ .

(81)

The first two terms form the d’Alembertian of
P
which drives the evolution of waves in the fluid. The other terms are a damping term in
\dot{P}
, source terms in
P
and
\Delta
, and a nonlinear term in
P\laplacian_{0}P
. The linearized trace equation thus describes the propagation of “sound waves” in a self-gravitating fluid with velocity dispersion. However, the nonlinearity that appears here is a highly approximated result where higher-order nonlinear terms in
P
and
\gradient_{0}P
were neglected.

The other main difference with the plane-symmetric case is that the off-diagonal terms of the perturbation gradient are
a priori
nonzero. The expression for
\Delta_{ij}
is then not immediate, and is instead given by the evolution equation (
79b
). The nonzero anisotropic dispersion then becomes a source term for the vorticity in Equation (
79c
). Indeed, one can show that the vorticity vector
\omega_{i}=1/2\,\epsilon_{ijk}\omega_{kj}
(where
\omega_{ij}=v_{[i,j]}
is the vorticity tensor as defined previously) can be expressed in the linearized Lagrangian picture as

\omega_{i}=\dfrac{1}{2}\epsilon_{ijk}\dot{\mathfrak{P}}_{kj},

(82)

so that Equation (
79c
) reads:

\dot{\omega}_{i}+2H\omega_{i}+\dfrac{2}{a^{2}\rho_{H}}\epsilon_{ijk}\Delta_{kp|pj}=0.

(83)

(A perturbative expansion for the vorticity field’s evolution equation is given in
ruth:vorticity
.)
Now let us look at an interesting limiting case. Going back to our main hypothesis that the perturbation gradient is dominated by one eigenvalue, which can be time and space-dependent, we consider that
locally
, the eigendirections of the perturbation gradient and the velocity dispersion tensor
\pi_{ij}
are coincident, so that we can diagonalize both of them simultaneously. This way, we can locally restrict ourselves to a case where
\pi_{ij}
is also dominated by one eigenvalue, which physically corresponds to the velocity dispersion being significant only in the direction of the collapse. This
locally 1D
approximation leads to a situation that is similar to the plane-symmetric case, where we consider the velocity dispersion tensor to only have one significant term on its diagonal, with all other terms being negligible along with their derivatives. The vorticity equation then becomes the usual linearized Kelvin-Helmholtz transport equation
serrin
,
ehlersbuchert
,

\dot{\boldsymbol{\omega}}+2H\boldsymbol{\omega}={\bf 0},

(84a)

which is solved by the linearized exact Cauchy integral

\boldsymbol{\omega}=\dfrac{\mathbf{\Omega}}{a^{2}},\quad\mathbf{\Omega}=\boldsymbol{\omega}(\mathbf{X},t=t_{\rm i}).

(84b)

VI
Conclusion and outlook

In this work, the formulation of the Newtonian equations describing a collisionless, self-gravitating fluid with velocity-dispersion has been derived both in standard Eulerian coordinates
(\mathbf{x},t)
, as well as in Hubble-comoving coordinates
(\mathbf{q},t)
, leading to the general Euler-Jeans-Newton system under the hypothesis of truncation of the third reduced velocity moment. Then the Lagrangian formulation of this system has been derived, leading to the Lagrange-Jeans-Newton system, consisting of only 10 independent equations (4 field equations and 6 evolution equations for the components of
\Pi_{ij}
) instead of 14 in the Euler-Jeans-Newton system (as Euler and continuity equations are automatically solved in the Lagrangian picture).
6
6

6

Both of these systems are overdetermined, but can be rewritten (over a simply connected spatial domain) using the gravitational potential
\Phi
such that
\mathbf{g}=-\gradient\Phi
with
\Phi
the solution of Poisson’s equation
\laplacian\Phi=4\pi G\rho-\Lambda
instead of the gravitational field equations for
\mathbf{g}
,
\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\mathbf{g}={\bf 0},\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\mathbf{g}=\Lambda-4\pi G\rho
, where the existence of
\Phi
is ensured by Poincaré’s lemma.

Using the Lagrangian framework, the special case of plane symmetry was studied, reducing the four field equations to only one nontrivial equation for the perturbation field
P_{1}
. This case would describe the collapse of a string embedded into a three-dimensional relativistic background. The remaining equation is nonlinear and does not admit analytical solutions, but it can be linearized and then solved analytically using Fourier analysis (see
al_roumi_matiere_2011
). Trying another path to approximate the solution of the general, nonlinear equation, the “adhesion approximation” has been applied to the Euler-Jeans-Newton system, resulting into an equation for the peculiar-velocity field in the form of Burgers’ equation, where the effective viscosity coefficient depends on both space and time. From there, two further approximations were made, the first being to merely get rid of the spatial dependence and considering only a time-dependent coefficient, and the second to consider a constant coefficient, the value of which being obtained by time-averaging the previous time-dependent coefficient. The interest of the latter approximation is the existence of rather simple analytical solutions to Burgers’ equation with constant coefficient. To compare these two new models to the linearized Lagrangian equation, both the exact equation and the three approximations were integrated numerically and results compared with each other at the time of shell-crossing. Overall, all three approximations are in good agreement with the exact solution, but the “adhesion approximation” performs a bit worse than the
Lagrange-linear approximation. This may come from the neglected spatial dependence of the viscosity coefficient, which can be taken into account in the form of Equation (
64
). It might be interesting to investigate numerical solutions to this equation in order to extend the idea of the “adhesion approximation”.

Investigating on the intuitive idea that the size and density of the initial collapsing cloud has an impact on the dynamics of the said collapse, the exact Lagrangian equation was integrated for various initial domain sizes and densities, and the time of first shell-crossing was identified. Results show that the crossing time expressed in terms of the scale factor
a(t)-1
is, as expected, a decreasing function of the initial density and an increasing function of the initial size of the cloud. For very small or very large clouds, this behavior is well-approximated by a power law of the initial cloud size, and overall exhibits a nontrivial dependence on the dimensionless parameter
\tilde{\beta}
linked to the initial density. Further investigation of the parameter space is needed to conclude on any analytical relation between those quantities.

In the last section of this work, the Lagrangian formalism was applied to the general case of a three-dimensional collapsing cloud without any particular symmetry. In this case it has been shown that the dynamics of the collapse can be rendered
locally
one-dimensional using physical considerations on the magnitude of the invariants of the perturbation tensor. Within this approximation, rather simple evolution equations were found for the various kinematic parts of the Lagrangian perturbation tensor. In particular, the equation for its trace part is similar to the one obtained in the plane-symmetric approximation, except for the equation of state linking the trace of the velocity dispersion tensor to the density. An interesting further work would be to study this equation under various approximations. In particular, dropping the nonlinear term and assuming a spatially constant proportionality between
P
and
\Delta_{ij|ji}
(which can be justified by the coinciding eigendirections of the tensors) may open possible analytical developments on the obtained master equation.

A.1
Moments of the Vlasov equation

We consider the phase space density as a function of Eulerian phase space coordinates and time,
f(x_{i},v_{i},t)
, and we start by specifying our integration domain. We take a domain
\Omega^{v}
included in the velocity phase subspace. We first calculate the zeroth velocity moment of the Vlasov equation, expressing the conservation of the phase space volume
binney_galactic_2008
:

m\int_{\Omega^{v}}\quantity(\pdv{f}{t}+v_i\pdv{f}{x_i}+g_i\pdv{f}{v_i})\,d^{3}v.

(A.1)

The first two terms are readily integrated by swapping the integral and the differential operators:

\displaystyle m\int_{\Omega^{v}}\partialderivative{f}{t}=\partialderivative{t}\int_{\Omega^{v}}mf\,d^{3}v=:\partialderivative{t}\rho,

(A.2)

\displaystyle m\int_{\Omega^{v}}v_{i}\partialderivative{f}{x_i}=\partialderivative{x_i}\int_{\Omega^{v}}mv_{i}f\,d^{3}v=:\partialderivative{x_i}(\rho\overline{v}_i).

(A.3)

In the third term, as
\mathbf{g}
does not depend on the velocities, we can take it out of the integral and use Stokes’ theorem to write

m\int_{\Omega^{v}}g_{i}\partialderivative{f}{v_i}=mg_{i}\int_{\partial\Omega^{v}}f\,d^{2}\!A^{v}.

(A.4)

Now we make the physical assumption that the high-velocity states are rare,

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}\quad v^{n}f(\cdot,\cdot,\mathbf{v})\xrightarrow[v\to\infty]{}0.

(A.5)

In practice, we suppose that the phase space density is cut off at some finite velocity that is contained in the velocity subspace.
Under this assumption, we have in particular that, for a large enough
\Omega^{v}
,

\int_{\partial\Omega^{v}}v^{n}f\,d^{2}\!A^{v}=0,

(A.6)

and the third term we were calculating vanishes, leaving the desired continuity equation for the rest mass density
\rho
. Now, we consider the first velocity moment:

m\int_{\Omega^{v}}v_{i}\quantity(\pdv{f}{t}+v_j\pdv{f}{x_j}+g_j\pdv{f}{v_j})\,d^{3}v.

(A.7)

As before, the first two terms are readily integrated

\displaystyle m\int_{\Omega^{v}}v_{i}\partialderivative{f}{t}=\partialderivative{t}\int_{\Omega^{v}}mv_{i}f\,d^{3}v=:\partialderivative{t}(\rho\overline{v_i}),

(A.8)

\displaystyle m\int_{\Omega^{v}}v_{i}v_{j}\partialderivative{f}{x_j}=\partialderivative{x_j}\int_{\Omega^{v}}mv_{i}v_{j}f\,d^{3}v:=\partialderivative{x_j}(\rho\overline{v_iv_j}).

(A.9)

The third term can be integrated by parts:

\displaystyle m\int_{\Omega^{v}}v_{i}g_{j}\partialderivative{f}{v_j}\,d^{3}v=mg_{j}\int_{\Omega^{v}}v_{i}\partialderivative{f}{v_j}\,d^{3}v

\displaystyle=mg_{j}\quantity\bigg(\int_{\del\Omega^v}v_i f\,d^2\!A^v-\int_{\Omega^v} f\underbrace{\pdv{v_i}{v_j}}_{=\delta_{ij}}\,d^3v).

(A.10)

The integrated term vanishes as
f
is cut off, and we are left with

m\int_{\Omega^{v}}v_{i}g_{j}\partialderivative{f}{v_j}\,d^{3}v=-g_{i}\int_{\Omega^{v}}mf\,d^{3}v=-\rho g_{i},

(A.11)

which yields the momentum conservation equation. To obtain the Euler-Jeans equation, we start by injecting the Lagrangian time-derivative into this equation, which yields:

\derivative{t}(\rho\overline{v}_i)-\overline{v}_{j}\partialderivative{x_j}(\rho\overline v_i)=\rho g_{i}-\partialderivative{x_j}(\rho\overline{v_iv_j}).

(A.12)

Now, using the continuity equation,

\derivative{t}\rho=-\rho\boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot\overline{\mathbf{v}},

(A.13)

we rewrite

\derivative{t}(\rho\overline{v}_i)=\rho\derivative{t}\overline{v}_{i}+\overline{v}_{i}\derivative{t}\rho=\rho\derivative{t}\overline{v}_{i}-\rho\overline{v}_{i}\partialderivative{x_j}\overline{v}_{j},

(A.14)

which leads to

\displaystyle\rho\derivative{t}\overline{v}_{i}=\rho g_{i}-\partialderivative{x_j}(\rho\overline{v_iv_j})+\overline{v}_{j}\partialderivative{x_j}(\rho\overline v_i)+\rho\overline{v}_{i}\partialderivative{x_j}\overline{v}_{j}

\displaystyle=\rho g_{i}-\partialderivative{x_j}(\rho\overline{v_iv_j})+\partialderivative{x_j}\quantity(\rho\overline v_i\overline v_j)

\displaystyle=\rho g_{i}-\partialderivative{x_j}\quantity(\rho\qty(\overline{v_iv_j}-\overline v_i\overline v_j))=:\rho g_{i}-\partialderivative{x_j}\Pi_{ij},

(A.15)

where we have defined the reduced second velocity moment
\Pi_{ij}=\rho\overline{(v_{i}-\overline{v}_{i})(v_{j}-\overline{v}_{j})}=\rho\quantity(\overline{v_iv_j}-\overline v_i\overline v_j)
.

A.2
Evolution of the reduced second velocity moment

We calculate the second velocity moment of the Vlasov equation, using the same techniques as above, and find

\partialderivative{t}\quantity\big(\rho\overline{v_iv_j})+\partialderivative{x_k}\quantity\big(\rho\overline{v_iv_jv_k})=\rho\quantity\big(g_i\overline v_j+g_j\overline v_i).

(A.16)

Re-expressing the third reduced velocity moment,

\displaystyle L_{ijk}:=\rho\overline{(v_{i}-\overline{v}_{i})(v_{j}-\overline{v}_{j})(v_{k}-\overline{v}_{k})}

\displaystyle=\rho\quantity(\overline{v_iv_jv_k}-\overline v_i\overline v_j \overline v_k)-\quantity[\overline v_i\Pi_{jk}+\overline v_j\Pi_{ki}+\overline v_k\Pi_{ij}],\

(A.17)

writing the Lagrangian time-derivative of
\Pi_{ij}
,

\derivative{t}\Pi_{ij}=\partialderivative{t}\quantity\big(\rho\overline{v_iv_j})-\partialderivative{t}\quantity\big(\rho\overline v_i\overline v_j)+\overline{v}_{k}\partialderivative{x_k}\Pi_{ij},

(A.18)

and using the previous velocity moment equations, we arrive at

\derivative{t}\Pi_{ij}=-\quantity[\pdv{\overline v_k}{x_k}\Pi_{ij}+\pdv{\overline v_i}{x_k}\Pi_{jk}+\pdv{\overline v_j}{x_k}\Pi_{ki}]-\partialderivative{x_k}L_{ijk}.

(A.19)

\displaystyle(1+\delta)\rho_{H}\derivative{t}\quantity({v}_{H_i}+u_i)

B.2
Numerical scheme

Consider a generic partial differential equation (PDE) of the form

\partialderivative{u}{t}=L(u)+N(u)+g,

(B.8)

where
u
is the unknown function of space and time,
L
is a linear spatial differential operator acting on
u
,
N
is a nonlinear operator acting on
u
(that does not imply any time-differentiation) and
g
is a given function of space and time. Let
\Delta t,\Delta x
be the time and space steps and
N,J
be the number of time and space intervals, so that the discretized space and time variable read
\quantity{x_j}=x_{0},\dots,x_{J}
and
\quantity{t_n}=t_{0},\dots,t_{N}
. Let
\mathbf{U}^{n}
be the approximation of the solution at time
t_{n}
. We denote respectively by
\mathbb{L}^{n}
,
\mathbf{N}(\mathbf{U}^{n})
and
\mathbf{g}^{n}
the matrix representation of the linear operator
L
, the approximation of
N(u)
and of
g
at time
t_{n}
, after taking into account boundary conditions. In all the calculations, the first spatial derivatives will be computed using a first-order centered differences scheme and the second spatial derivatives with the standard three-point centered differences scheme:

\partialderivative{u}{x}\longrightarrow\dfrac{U_{j+1}^{n}-U_{j-1}^{n}}{2\Delta x},\quad\partialderivative[2]{u}{x}\longrightarrow\dfrac{U_{j+1}^{n}-2U_{j}^{n}+U_{j-1}^{n}}{\Delta x^{2}}.

(B.9)

At each time step, the evolution of
u
is approximated using a second-order accurate Crank-Nicholson scheme for the linear part of the equation, and a second-order accurate Adams-Bashforth scheme for the nonlinear part. Taking into account the periodic boundary conditions that sum up to identifying
x_{0}
with
x_{J}
, we obtain the following expression at each time step:

\displaystyle U^{n+1}_{J}=U^{n+1}_{0},

(B.10a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{n+1}_{[0:J-1]}=\Bigg(\mathds{1}-\dfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{L}^{n+1}\Bigg)^{-1}\ \mathfrak{O}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{O}=\Bigg[\Bigg(\mathds{1}+\dfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb{L}^{n}\Bigg)\mathbf{U}^{n}_{[0:J-1]}+\dfrac{\Delta t}{2}\Bigg(3\mathbf{N}\Bigg(\mathbf{U}^{n}_{[0:J-1]}\Bigg)

\displaystyle-\mathbf{N}\Bigg(\mathbf{U}^{n-1}_{[0:J-1]}\Bigg)\Bigg)+\dfrac{\Delta t}{2}\Bigg(\mathbf{g}^{n+1}+\mathbf{g}^{n}\Bigg)\Bigg],

(B.10b)

where
\mathbf{U}^{n}_{[0:J-1]}
denotes the vector constituted of the first
J
components of
\mathbf{U}^{n}
. The first time step cannot use a second order Adams-Bashforth scheme for the nonlinear term, and we therefore jump-start the solver using a simple forward Euler scheme:

\displaystyle U^{1}_{J}=U^{1}_{0},

(B.11a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}^{1}_{[0:J-1]}=\quantity(\mathds 1-\dfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb L^{1})^{-1}\Bigg[\quantity(\mathds 1+\dfrac{\Delta t}{2}\mathbb L^{0})\mathbf{U}^{0}_{[0:J-1]}

\displaystyle+\Delta t\,\mathbf{N}\quantity(\vb U^{0}_{[0:J-1]})+\dfrac{\Delta t}{2}\quantity(\vb g^{1} + \vb g^0)\Bigg].

(B.11b)

\partialderivative{u}{b}=\mu\partialderivative[2]{u}{q}-u\partialderivative{u}{q}.

(B.12)

Here, there is no source term hence
g=0
. We identify the linear and nonlinear terms,

L=\mu\partialderivative[2]{q},\quad N(u)=-u\partialderivative{u}{q},

(B.13)

and their following discrete approximation, taking into account periodic boundary conditions, reads:

\mathbb{L}^{n}=\dfrac{\mu(t_{n})}{\Delta q^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}-2&1&&&1\\
