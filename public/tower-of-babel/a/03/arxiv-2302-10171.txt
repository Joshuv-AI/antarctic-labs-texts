Positivity for partial tropical flag varieties
Jorge Alberto Olarte

Abstract
. We study positivity notions for the tropicalization of type A flag varieties and the flag Dressian. We focus on the hollow case, where we have one constituent of rank 1 and another of corank 1. We characterize the three different notions that exist for this case in terms of Plücker coordinates. These notions are the tropicalization of the totally non-negative flag variety, Bruhat subdivisions and the non-negative flag Dressian. We show the first example where the first two concepts above do not coincide. In this case the non-negative flag Dressian equals the tropicalization of the Plücker non-negative flag variety and is equivalent to flag positroid subdivisions. Using these characterizations, we provide similar conditions on the Plücker coordinates for flag varieties of arbitrary rank that are necessary for each of these positivity notions, and conjecture them to be sufficient. In particular we show that regular flag positroid subdivisions always come from the non-negative Dressian. Along the way, we show that the set of Bruhat interval polytopes equals the set of twisted Bruhat interval polytopes and we introduce valuated flag gammoids.

3.1.
Mixed subdivisions

Recall that a function

on the vertices of a polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
induces a regular subdivision
\mathcal{S}_{w}(P)
.
The cells in
\mathcal{S}_{w}(P)
are the convex hull of all

that minimize

for some
x\in(\mathbb{R}^{*})^{n}
, which we write
\mathcal{S}_{w}(P)^{x}
.
We also get a polyhedral subdivision
\Sigma_{w}
of
(\mathbb{R}^{*})^{n}
, where for every polytope
Q\in\mathcal{S}_{w}(P)
there is a cell
Q^{\vee}\in\Sigma_{w}
coonsisting of all
x\in(\mathbb{R}^{*})^{n}
such that
\mathcal{S}_{w}(P)^{x}=Q
.

Now consider a Minkowski sum of polytopes
P_{1}+\dots+P_{k}
.
Given a tuple of functions
\textbf{w}=(w_{1},\dots w_{k})
where
w_{i}:V(P_{i})\to\mathbb{R}
, the (coherent) mixed subdivision
\mathcal{S}_{\textbf{w}}(P_{1}+\dots+P_{k})
consists of all polytopes of the form

\mathcal{S}_{w_{1}}(P_{1})^{x}+\dots+\mathcal{S}_{w_{k}}(P_{k})^{x}.

The dual complex
\Sigma_{\textbf{w}}
is the common refinement of all
\Sigma_{i}
.
Equivalently, mixed subdivisions are the subdivisions induced by the projection of a product of simplices to the Minkowski sum.

3.2.
Flag matroids

Matroids have many equivalent definitions.
Of most importance to us, is the polyhedral approach intiated by Gelfand, Goresky, MacPherson and Serganova

We begin by introducing the polytopes that will play a role.
First we have the

The

A

We write

Two matroids

The motivation behind this definition is that if a linear space sits inside another linear space, then the corresponding representable matroids form a quotient.
A

The matroids

The

The

A polytope is a flag matroid polytope if and only if it is a

A

3.3.
Valuated matroids

A

with underlying matroid

consists exclusively of matroids.
Equivalently
[
38
]

satisfies the

(

)
for pairs of subsets
S\in{[n]\choose d-1}
and
T\in{[n]\choose d+1}
.
An
initial matroid
of

is a matroid appearing in the subdivision, and we write
\mu^{x}:=\mathcal{S}_{\mu}(P_{M})^{x}
.
A valuated matroid can be interpreted as a function from
{[n]\choose d}\to\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
by setting the value of all non-bases to infinity.
A matroid can be considered as a valuated matroid with trivial valuation, that is, by setting
0
to all of its bases and
\infty
to the non-bases.

The Dressian

\operatorname{Dr}(d,n)
is the space of all valuated matroids of rank
d
over
[n]
.
It can be given a fan structure as a subfan of the secondary fan of
\Delta(d,n)
, or, equivalently, by the terms in which the three-term Plücker relations achieve the minimum
[
33
]
.

A pair of valuated matroids

of ranks
d_{1}<d_{2}
form a

if they satisfy the
tropical incidence relations
(
1.2
),
for every pair of subsets
S\in{[n]\choose d_{1}-1}
and
T\in{[n]\choose d_{2}+1}
.
If we restrict to trivially valuated matroids we recover the concept of matroid quotient described above.

A
valuated flag matroid

{\bm{\mu}}=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{s})
is a chain of valuated matroids such that every pair of them form a quotient.

The flag Dressian

\operatorname{Fl}\operatorname{Dr}(\textbf{d};n)
consists of all valuated flag matroids of rank
d
.
A sequence of valuated matroids
{\bm{\mu}}=(\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{s})
is a flag valuated matroid if and only if the supports
(M_{1},\dots,M_{s})
form a flag matroid polytope and the mixed subdivision
\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\mu}}(M_{1}+\dots+M_{s})
consists exclusively of flag matroid polytopes
[
8
, Theorem 4.4.2]
.
Therefore a flag matroid can also be thought of as a function from the vertices of a flag matroid polytope to
\mathbb{R}
.
Again we write
{\bm{\mu}}^{x}:=\mathcal{S}_{\bm{\mu}}(P_{\textbf{M}})^{x}
for
initial flag matroids
.

The
tropical linear space

given by a valuated matroid
\mu
is the set of points

that satisfy that

\bigoplus_{i\in[n]}x_{i}\odot\mu_{T\backslash i}\kern 5.0pt\text{ achieves the minimum at least twice.}

It is the subcomplex of

(as defined in
Section

where

is a loop free matroid in the subdivision of induced by

[
38
]
.

Since all tropical linear spaces contain
\mathbb{R}(1,\dots,1)
in the lineality space, we think of tropical linear spaces as sitting inside the
tropical projective plane

Similar to the non-tropical case, a tropical linear space is contained in another tropical linear space if and only if the corresponding valuated matroids form a quotient

20
]

Hence valuated flag matroids

are cryptomorphic to flags of tropical linear spaces

A valuated matroid

is said to be

(or

A beautiful fact about the tropicalization of linear spaces is that tropicalizing commutes with taking Plücker coordinates, i.e. the tropicalization of a linear space is given by the valuated matroid obtained by taking the valuation of the Plücker coordinates the linear space

3.4.
Positivity

The following two notions of positivity exist for the flag variety.
The
Plücker non-negative flag variety

\operatorname{Fl}^{\geq 0}(\textbf{d},\mathbb{R}^{n})
consists of all flag of linear spaces whose Plücker cooridnates are non-negative.
In other words,
\operatorname{Fl}^{\geq 0}(\textbf{d},\mathbb{R}^{n})

\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
such that all top

i\in[s]
.
A stronger notion, is the
totally non-negative
(or just

)

\operatorname{Fl}^{\textsl{tnn}}(\textbf{d},\mathbb{R}^{n})

These two notions agree if and only if the rank

]

A flag matroid

4.
Bruhat polytopes

The following is an ad hoc introduction to Bruhat (interval) polytopes for type
A
, in a purely combinatorial fashion.
For simplicity, we drop the “interval” and call them just Bruhat polytopes, following
[
22
]
.
For details on generalized Bruhat polytopes for a general flag variety
G/P
we suggest
[
42
, Section 6]
.

A
word
for a permutation
v
is sequence of
w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{m})
where
v=w_{1}\cdots w_{m}
using the precomposition product and each
w_{i}
is a transposition of the form
\tau_{j}:=(j,j+1)
.
We say the word is
reduced
if it is length
m
is minimal among all words for
v
.
A subword of

is a (not necessarily consecutive) subsequence
w^{\prime}=(w_{i_{1}},\dots w_{i_{m^{\prime}}})
where
1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{m^{\prime}}\leq m
.
The
Bruhat order
on the symmetric group
\operatorname{Sym}(n)
is given by
u\leq v
if and only if for any (equivalently every) reduced word
w
for
v
there is a subword
w^{\prime}
for
u
.
The
length
of a permutation
v
is the minimal length of a word for
v
.

Two different definitions have been used simultaneously in the literature to assign a polytope to a Bruhat interval.
These are based on two different ways of identifying
\operatorname{Sym}(n)
with the vertices of the permutahedron
\Pi_{n}
.

\displaystyle\phi:\operatorname{Sym}_{n}

\displaystyle\to\operatorname{Vert}(\Pi_{n})

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto(x(1),\dots,x(n))

\displaystyle\psi:\operatorname{Sym}_{n}

\displaystyle\to\operatorname{Vert}(\Pi_{n})

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto x\cdot e_{\textbf{n}}

and

is given by the action of
\operatorname{Sym}_{n}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of permuting coordinates.
Following terminology introduced in
[

.

5.
The geometry of hollow flag matroids

In this section we study hollow valuated flag matroids
(\mu,\nu)\in\operatorname{Dr}(1,n-1;n)

These correspond to a point inside the tropical hyperplane in
\mathbb{TP}^{n-1}

The vertices of

(n-2)

However, to make notation simpler and more intuitive, we are going to work with a translation of

(and all matroid polytopes of rank

(-1,\dots,-1)
so that points have only two non-zero coordinates, which are equal to
-1
and
1
.
We make a slight abuse of notation by also calling this polytope
\Delta(1,n-1;n)
.
This way

is a centrally symmetric polytope and its matroid subdivisions are induced by the coordinate hyperplanes as we explain below.
In
Figure

we see a finest subdivision of the cuboctahedron
\Delta(1,3;4)
where two of the coordinate hyperplanes each simultaneously slice up
\Delta(1,3;4)

Two of the polytopes are the Minkowski sum of a tetrahedron with a segment while the other two are the Minkowski sum of two triangles.

Figure 2.

Every subpolytope of the simplices
\Delta(1,n)
and
\Delta(n-1,n)
is a matroid.
By equation (
1.2
), flag matroids

of rank

of

The polytope

The most simple type of non-trivial polytopal subdivision is splitting a polytope in two by a hyperplane.
The matroid subdivisions that arise in

We now introduce a language which is useful to describe the paramtrization of

This is based on the class of gammoids introduced by Mason in

which was generalized in

to valuated matroids.
We adapt this construction to (valuated) flag matroids, an call them (valuated) flag gammoids.
For details on the classical theory of gammoids and the related construction of transversal matroids we refer to

Consider a directed graph
\Gamma=(V,E)

We identify

Given sets

and ends in a vertex in

The collection of subsets

form the bases of rank

A matroid that can be constructed this way is called a

[

Now suppose each edge

]

Proof.

Definition 7.2

A (valuated) flag matroid

Proposition 7.3

Proof.

Let

Suppose you want to translate the flag of tropical linear spaces by an amount

However the converse is far from trivial.
It was proven in

We ask if we can generalize this in the flag setting, and moreover, to the non-strict setting:

Notice that this question is similar in the local vs global spirit of some of the positivity questions we deal with in this paper.

Remark 7.5

The construction in
[

]
has several convention differences to this one.
First, the elements of the matroid are placed as sinks as and look at linkings onto them from marked sources.
However in the classical construction of gammoids
[

30
]
the arrows go the other way, looking instead at likings from a subset of elements of a matroid onto marked sinks, which is what we use here.
Moreover, the twisted convention of Bruhat polytopes is used instead of the untwisted.
From the proof of

we have that to change conventions one takes a Bruhat interval for the dual and then takes inverse of the permutations in the interval.
Notice that a word for the inverse of a permutation can be obtained by reversing the order of the letters.
So together the change of these conventions amounts to doing the same construction in
[
13
, Definition 3.11]
for

where

while reversing all arrows and placing the arrows from right to left in the figures therein.

8.

In this section we will prove part 1. (

We begin with the following lemma:

Lemma 8.1
.

A sequence of real numbers
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
satisfies (
1.4
) if and only if there exists
x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}
such that
\lambda_{i}=x_{i}\oplus x_{i-1}
for every

(where

Proof.

First, if such

exist, then

\lambda_{i-1}\oplus\lambda_{i}=(x_{i-1}\oplus x_{i-2})\oplus(x_{i+1}\oplus x_{i})\leq x_{i-1}\oplus x_{i}=\lambda_{i}.

Now suppose that
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
satisfy (
1.4
).
Let
x_{i}:=\max(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i+1})
.
We have that
x_{i}\oplus x_{i-1}=\max(\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i+1})\oplus\max(\lambda_{i+1},\lambda_{i})
is larger than
\lambda_{i}
because both terms are larger than
x_{i}

But for both terms to be larger than

, we would have that

, which contradicts (
1.4
).
∎

Theorem 8.2
