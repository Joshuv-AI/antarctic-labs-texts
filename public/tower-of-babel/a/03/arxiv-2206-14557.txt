Hopf and pre-Lie algebras in regularity structures
Ilya Chevyrev

Abstract
These lecture notes aim to present the algebraic theory of regularity structures as developed in [ Hairer14 , BHZ19 , BCCH21 ] . The main aim of this theory is to build a systematic approach to renormalisation of singular SPDEs; together with complementary analytic results, these works give a general solution theory for a wide class of semilinear parabolic singular SPDEs in the subcritical regime. We demonstrate how ‘positive’ and ‘negative’ renormalisation can be described using interacting Hopf algebras, and how the renormalised non-linearities in SPDEs can be computed. For the latter, of crucial importance is a pre-Lie structure on non-linearities on which the negative renormalisation group acts through pre-Lie morphisms. To show the main aspects of these results without introducing many notations and assumptions, we focus on a special case of the general theory in which there is only one equation and one noise. These lectures notes are an expansion of the material presented at a minicourse with the same title at the Master Class and Workshop “Higher Structures Emerging from Renormalisation” at the ESI, Vienna, in November 2021.

Introduction

These lecture notes aim to present the main algebraic structures which appear in the theory of regularity structures designed to solve singular stochastic partial differential equations (SPDEs) of the form

1.1
Motivation: SPDEs and power counting

When trying to solve (
1
) in the singular regime, the main problem which appears is

multiplication of distributions
.
In the rest of this subsection, we discuss this problem, how it ties into the equation,
and potential ways to solve it.
A brief outline of the structure of the lecture notes is given at the end of this subsection.

We start by recalling a classical result from harmonic analysis, often referred to as Young’s product theorem
[
Young36
]
.

Proposition 1.2

Let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. Denote by
C^{\alpha}=C^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
the Hölder–Besov space of periodic distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
of regularity
\alpha
.
Then multiplication
C^{\infty}\times C^{\infty}\to C^{\infty}
extends to a continuous bilinear map
C^{\alpha}\times C^{\beta}\to C^{\alpha\wedge\beta}
,
if and only if
\alpha+\beta>0
.

Notation 1.3

We will use
\alpha-
to denote a number slightly below
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
\alpha-=\alpha-\kappa
for
\kappa>0
arbitrarily small but fixed.
This will make power-counting much simpler.

To see how to apply Proposition
1.2
to SPDEs,
consider first the ODE with multiplicative noise

\partial_{t}u=f(u)\xi\;\hbox{,}

(2)

posed on
u\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
\xi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to L(\mathbb{R}^{m},\mathbb{R}^{n})
is smooth.
We often think of
\xi=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}
,
where
W
is the ‘driving path’.

Assume
\xi\in C^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
.
One expects
u
to have at best one more derivative than
\xi
, i.e. we can try to solve for
u\in C^{\alpha+1}\Rightarrow f(u)\in C^{\alpha+1}
.
Therefore we can use Proposition
1.2
to make analytic sense of the product
f(u)\xi
if and only if
\alpha+\alpha+1>0
,
i.e.
\alpha>-\frac{1}{2}
.
In this case, we can indeed solve for (
2
) with
u\in C^{\alpha+1}
–
a variant of this result was shown
by Lyons
[
Lyons-Young
]

as a precursor to his introduction of rough paths
[
Lyons
]
.
In particular, there is a deterministic solution theory for SDEs driven by fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H>\frac{1}{2}
since sample paths of this process are almost surely in

C^{H-}
.

However, this deterministic analysis comes short of solving (
2
) for
\xi=\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}W}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}
where
W
is a sample paths of Brownian motion as we have
\xi\in C^{\alpha}
with
\alpha<-\frac{1}{2}
in this case.

Remark 1.4

One may wonder if Hölder–Besov spaces
C^{\alpha}
are not the correct function space to use – perhaps if we looked at a different space (e.g. some more general Orlicz or Besov space) we can recover continuity of the product, even in the regime of Brownian motion.
Unfortunately, another result of Lyons
[
TerryPath
]
is that there exists no Banach space containing smooth paths and allowing to approximate almost every realisation of Brownian motion to which
(y,x)\mapsto\int_{0}^{1}y_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t}
extends as a continuous bilinear map (see also
[
MR2314753
, Sec. 1.5.1]
).
Since
\int_{0}^{1}y_{t}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x_{t}
is a simple example of an ODE driven by
(x,y)
,
this suggests that no amount of deterministic
linear
functional analysis is able to recover Itô calculus.
(See
[
Chevyrev22Heat
]
for an ill-posedness result of a similar flavour for a non-linear heat equation with rough initial conditions.)

Remark 1.5

In case
n=1
, one can actually solve for
u
as
u_{t}=e^{X_{t}f}(u_{0})
, where
X_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\xi_{s}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
and
e^{xf}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the flow for the ODE
\dot{y}_{t}=xf(y)
.
Hence the product
f(u)\xi=f(e^{X_{t}f}(u_{0}))\partial_{t}X_{t}=\partial_{t}\mathop{\mathrm{exp}}\nolimits(X_{t}f)(u_{0})

is perfectly well-defined.
This explicit solution formula is the reason SDEs with a
1
-dimensional driver (or more generally when the drivers are paired with commuting vector fields)
are stable under the uniform norm on driving paths.

1.1.1
Stochastic quantisation of
\Phi^{4}_{d-1}

Another famous example, this time with a multi-dimensional domain, is the
\Phi^{4}_{d-1}

(\partial_{t}-\Delta)u=-u^{3}+\xi\;\hbox{,}\qquad u\colon\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}\;\hbox{,}

(3)

where
\xi
is a (space-time) white noise on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

At this point, let us recall the Schauder estimates for parabolic equations and the Kolmogorov-type theorem on the regularity of white noise.
1
1

1

These results require us to work with the parabolic rather than Euclidean scaling, see the start of Section

Theorem 1.6

(Schauder estimates)

If
(\partial_{t}-\Delta)v=\zeta
, where
\zeta\in C^{\alpha}
,
then
v\in C^{\alpha+2}
.

Theorem 1.7

(Kolmogorov regularity)

Almost surely
\xi\in C^{-\frac{d}{2}-\frac{1}{2}-}

Applying these results, we see that we can at best solve for

in

If

We will now explore this example in more detail as motivation for the later sections.

1.1.2
d=3
: Da Prato–Debussche trick

If

3

]

Consider

(\partial_{t}-\Delta)v=\xi\;\hbox{,}\qquad u=v+X\;.

Then Schauder estimates imply

and

(\partial_{t}-\Delta)X=-(v+X)^{3}=-(v^{3}+3v^{2}X+3vX^{2}+X^{3})\;.

(4)

We notice that, if we can make sense of

and

as functions, say, in

to be in
C^{2-}

Luckily, Wick renormalisation indeed allows us to give a sensible definition to

Proposition 1.8

Let
v_{\varepsilon}=v*\delta_{\varepsilon}
for
\varepsilon>0
, where
\delta_{\varepsilon}
is a smooth mollifier converging to the Dirac delta as
\varepsilon\to 0

Then there exist deterministic constants
C_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
for
\varepsilon>0
and random space-time distributions
v^{:2:}
,

(called Wick powers)
such that

v^{2}_{\varepsilon}-C_{\varepsilon}\to v^{:2:}

and
v^{3}-3C_{\varepsilon}v\to v^{:3:}

in
C^{0-}
in probability
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
Moreover,
C_{\varepsilon}\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

The idea is now to replace
v^{3}
and
v^{2}
in (
4
) by
v^{:3:}
and
v^{:2:}
respectively.
As indicated above, we are then able to solve for

in
C^{2-}
and

.
Furthermore, we can obtain the following approximation result.

Theorem 1.9

Following the notation from Proposition
1.8
,
let
\xi_{\varepsilon}=\xi*\delta_{\varepsilon}
.
Then the solutions to

(\partial_{t}-\Delta)u_{\varepsilon}=-u_{\varepsilon}^{3}+3C_{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}+\xi_{\varepsilon}

in
C^{0-}
in probability as

Note that since

The distributions

1.1.3

Let us now consider (

This time

Let us try the trick one more time, defining

We see that, even if we can make sense of

If we could make sense of

1.2
Picard iterations and trees

A potential way to solve (
1
) is to first consider smooth
\xi
and rewrite the equation in mild formulation as

u=Gu_{0}+G*(F(u,\nabla u,\ldots\xi,\nabla\xi,\ldots))\;\hbox{,}

where
G=(\partial_{t}-\mathcal{L})^{-1}
is the Green’s function of
\partial_{t}-\mathcal{L}
,
*
denotes space-time convolution, and
(Gu_{0})(t,\cdot)=e^{t\mathcal{L}}u_{0}
is the harmonic extension of the initial condition.

It is then natural to define the map

\mathcal{M}(u)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{{=}}Gu_{0}+G*(F(u,\nabla u,\ldots\xi,\nabla\xi,\ldots))\;\hbox{,}

so that the solution to (
1
) is a fixed point
\mathcal{M}(u)=u
.
Under favourable conditions, namely if
\mathcal{M}
is a contraction, the iterations
u^{(0)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{{=}}0
,
u^{(n)}=\mathcal{M}(u^{(n-1)})
converge to a unique fixed point
u
as
n\to\infty
.

Writing
F(u,\xi)
instead of
F(u,\nabla u,\ldots\xi,\nabla\xi,\ldots)
,
let us
formally
Taylor expand

F(u,\xi)=\sum_{\alpha}\frac{D^{\alpha}F(0)}{\alpha!}(u,\xi)^{k}\;.

Then each iteration

u^{(0)}=0\;\hbox{,}\qquad u^{(1)}=Gu_{0}\;\hbox{,}\qquad u^{(2)}=Gu_{0}+G(F(u^{(1)},\xi))\;\hbox{,}\qquad\ldots

can be formally written as a series of functions which are multilinear in the jets of
Gu_{0}
and
\xi
, and involve iterated convolutions with
G
.
For example, one of the functions in
u^{(3)}
could be a multiple of
G(Gu_{0}(G*\partial_{i}\xi)^{2}\xi)
.

This naturally leads to a collection of ‘abstract symbols’ (trees) which will be used as placeholders for these functions.
This abstraction helps to detach the terms that appear in this ‘formal’ fixed point with the concrete functions these trees will represent.

Definition 1.13

Let
\mathfrak{L}=\{\Xi,\mathcal{I}\}

Convention 1.16

(Multisets vs. functions)

For a set
A
,
we will frequently identify functions in
\mathbb{N}^{A}

with multisets of
A
in the obvious way.
In particular, the zero element
0\in\mathbb{N}^{A}
corresponds to the empty multiset
\emptyset
.
Likewise
k\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
will often be treated as a multiset of
[d]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny\rm def}}}{{=}}\{1,\ldots,d\}
.

We will write every tree
\tau\in\mathfrak{T}
symbolically as follows.
Let
\mathfrak{N}\subset\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}^{d}}
denote the set of functions
\mathbb{N}^{d}\to\mathbb{N}
with finite support.
According to Remark
1.16
,
we will implicitly treat every
L\in\mathfrak{N}
as a multiset of
\mathbb{N}^{d}
with finitely many elements.
We then write every
\tau\in\mathfrak{T}
in one of the following two ways
