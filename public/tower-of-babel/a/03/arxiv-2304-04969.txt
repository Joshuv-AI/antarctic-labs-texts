Poisson Equation and Application to Multi-Scale SDEs with State-Dependent Switching
Xiaobin Sunsun, Yingchao Xie

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the averaging principle and central limit theorem for multi-scale stochastic differential equations with state-dependent switching. To accomplish this, we first study the Poisson equation associated with a Markov chain and the regularity of its solutions. As applications of the results on the Poisson equations, we prove three averaging principle results and two central limit theorems results. The first averaging principle result is a strong convergence of order 1 / 2 1/2 of the slow component X ε X^{\varepsilon} in the space C ⁡ ( [ 0 , T ] , ℝ n ) C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{n}) . The second averaging principle result is a weak convergence of X ε X^{\varepsilon} in C ⁡ ( [ 0 , T ] , ℝ n ) C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{n}) . The third averaging principle result is a weak convergence of order 1 1 of X t ε X^{\varepsilon}_{t} in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} for any fixed t ≥ 0 t\geq 0 . The first central limit theorem type result is a weak convergence of ( X ε − X ¯ ) / ε (X^{\varepsilon}-\bar{X})/\sqrt{\varepsilon} in C ⁡ ( [ 0 , T ] , ℝ n ) C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{n}) , where X ¯ \bar{X} is the solution of the averaged equation. The second central limit theorem type result is a weak convergence of order 1 / 2 1/2 of ( X t ε − X ¯ t ) / ε (X^{\varepsilon}_{t}-\bar{X}_{t})/\sqrt{\varepsilon} in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} for fixed t ≥ 0 t\geq 0 . Several examples are given to show that all the achieved orders are optimal.

1.
Introduction

Stochastic differential equations (SDEs) with state-dependent switching are used to describe the evolution of the following complicated system: There are countably many scenarios
\mathbb{S}
for the system; in each scenario, the system evolves according to a certain diffusion; the system can switch between scenarios and the switching depends on the current location or state of the system. The switching process is typically modeled by a right-continuous pure jump process. More precisely, we will use
X_{t}
to denote the spatial location (or state) of the system is in at time
t
and
\alpha_{t}
to denote the scenario that the system is in at time
t
. SDEs with state-dependent switching consist of the following system of stochastic equations:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dX_{t}=b(X_{t},\alpha_{t})dt+\sigma(X_{t},\alpha_{t})dW_{t},\\
\mathbb{P}\{\alpha_{t+\Delta}=j|\alpha_{t}=i,X_{s},\alpha_{s},s\leqslant t\}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle q_{ij}(X_{t})\Delta+o(\Delta),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ i\neq j\\
1+q_{ii}(X_{t})\Delta+o(\Delta),\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ i=j,\end{array}\right.\\
(X_{0},\alpha_{0})=(x,\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{S},\end{cases}

where
\{W_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is a standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
with natural filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
,
b
is a function from
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{S}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\sigma
is a function from
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{S}
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
Q(x)=(q_{ij}(x))_{1\leq i,j\leq m_{0}}
is a
Q
-matrix depending on
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

SDEs with state-dependent switching are widely used in various fields, including finance, engineering, biology, and physics. In finance, this kind of systems are used to model the behavior of financial assets and derivatives under the influence of both random market fluctuations and state-dependent trading strategies. In engineering, this kind of systems are used to study the dynamics of systems subjected to switching control policies that depend on the system’s current state. Due to the intricate interplay between the diffusions and the switching mechanism, SDEs with state-dependent switching are more difficult to study than traditional SDEs.

Recently, SDEs with state-dependent switching have attracted the attention of many researchers, with ongoing efforts aimed at deepening our understanding of these complex dynamical systems and exploring their applications. For recent results in this area, we refer our readers to

In this paper, we focus on situations where the switching process
\alpha_{t}
evolves at a much faster rate compared to the diffusion process
X_{t}
. This scenario is particularly relevant in some neuron models (see
[
37
, Section 3]
).
We introduce a small positive parameter
\varepsilon
that represents the ratio of the time scales between the slow and fast components. The switching process exhibits rapid jumps on a time scale of
O(\varepsilon^{-1})
, which is described by the state-dependent transition matrix
Q^{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}Q(x)
, while the diffusion process
X_{t}
evolves on a slower time scale of
O(1)
. So we are interested in the following system of stochastic equations:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}dX^{\varepsilon}_{t}=b(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{t})dt+\sigma(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{t})dW_{t},\\
\mathbb{P}\left(\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{t+\Delta}=j|\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{t}=i,X^{{\varepsilon}}_{s},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{s},s\leqslant t\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-1}q_{ij}(X^{{\varepsilon}}_{t})\Delta+o(\Delta),\quad i\neq j,\\
1+\varepsilon^{-1}q_{ii}(X^{{\varepsilon}}_{t})\Delta+o(\Delta),\quad i=j,\end{array}\right.\\
(X^{\varepsilon}_{0},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{0})=(x,\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{S}.\end{cases}

More generally, one can consider the case
Q^{\varepsilon}(x)=\tilde{Q}(x)+\varepsilon^{-1}Q(x)
, in which the switching process exhibits two distinct time scales (see
[
58
, Part IV]
or
[
37
, Subsection 4.3]
). However, in this paper, we will only consider the case mentioned in the paragraph above, that is, we will only consider the case when
\tilde{Q}(x)\equiv 0
.

Due to the different time scales and the interactions between the fast and slow components, multi-scale SDEs with state-dependent switching are difficult to study directly. It is very helpful to find a simpler equationthat captures the long-term evolution of the system. This leads to the study of the limit behavior of the solution
X^{\varepsilon}
of (
1.2
) as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This study is usually called the averaging principle. The limit process
\bar{X}
is the unique solution of the following averaged equation:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle d\bar{X}_{t}=\bar{b}(\bar{X}_{t})dt+\bar{\sigma}(\bar{X}_{t})dW_{t},\\
\bar{X}_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{array}\right.

where
\bar{b}
and
\bar{\sigma}
are the corresponding averaged coefficients (see Eq.
(
2.3.2
)
below).

In the literature, two kinds of limits are usually considered: strong and weak convergences. Let
(\mathbb{H},\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{H}})
be a Banach space and
\mathbb{C}
be a class of functions
f:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
Suppose
Y
and
\{Y_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
are
\mathbb{H}
-valued random variables.

•

\{Y_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
is said to converges strongly to
Y
in
\mathbb{H}
if

(1.4)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\|Y_{n}-Y\|_{\mathbb{H}}=0.

Furthermore, we say the strong convergence is of order
k>0
if

(1.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|Y_{n}-Y\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leqslant Cn^{-k},\quad\forall n\geqslant 1,

where
C>0
is a constant.

•

\{Y_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
is said to converges weakly to
Y
in
\mathbb{H}
under the set
\mathbb{C}
if

(1.6)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}|\mathbb{E}\phi(Y_{n})-\mathbb{E}\phi(Y)|=0,\quad\forall\phi\in\mathbb{C}.

Furthermore, we say the weak convergence is of order
k>0
if

(1.7)

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}\phi(Y_{n})-\mathbb{E}\phi(Y)|\leqslant C_{\phi}n^{-k},\quad\forall n\geqslant 1,\phi\in\mathbb{C},

where
C_{\phi}>0
is a constant which might depends on
\phi
.

The parameter
n\to\infty
can be interpreted in different ways. In our case, we will use the parameter
\varepsilon\to 0
. It is clear that if
\mathbb{C}
is the set of bounded continuous functions on
\mathbb{H}
, weak convergence corresponds to convergence in law. On the other hand, if
\mathbb{C}
represents the set of Lipschitz functions on
\mathbb{H}
, the weak convergence order is at least as good as the strong convergence order. In general, the weak order depends on the specific class
\mathbb{C}
being considered.

Poisson equation
: We first study the following Poisson equation on a countable space
\mathbb{S}
:

-Q(x)\Phi(x,\cdot)(i)=F(x,i),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},i\in\mathbb{S},

where
Q(x)
is the generator of an
\mathbb{S}
-valued Markov chain
\{\alpha^{x}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
.
Assume that
F(x,i)
satisfies the "
central condition
":

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{S}}F(x,i)\mu^{x}_{i}=0,\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
\mu^{x}
is the unique invariant measure of
\{\alpha^{x}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
. Under some suitable conditions, the above Poisson equation admits a solution

\Phi(x,i)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}F(x,\alpha_{t}^{x,i})dt,

where
\{\alpha^{x,i}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
stands for the Markov chain
\{\alpha^{x}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
with initial value
\alpha^{x}_{0}=i
.

In the last few decades, the Poisson equation and its applications in diffusion approximation and central limit type theorems in various stochastic multi-scale systems, as well as in finding optimal convergence orders in both strong and weak senses, have been intensively studied.
For example, Pardoux and Veretennikov
[
38
,
39
]
studied the regularity of solutions to the following Poisson equation:

-\mathcal{L}(x)\Phi(x,\cdot)(y)=b(x,y),

where
b
satisfies the similar "
central condition
" and

\mathcal{L}(x)h(x,\cdot)(y)=\sum_{i,j}a_{ij}(x,y)\frac{\partial^{2}h(x,y)}{\partial{y_{i}}\partial{y_{j}}}+\sum_{i}f_{i}(x,y)\frac{\partial h(x,y)}{\partial{y_{i}}}.

The regularity results were further applied to a study on the diffusion approximation for two-scaled diffusion processes. However, the papers mentioned above mainly focused on Poisson equations associated with diffusion processes and the techniques of these papers do not work in our discrete setting. The first purpose of this paper is to establish the regularity of the solution
\Phi(x,i)
of the Poisson equation associated with a Markov chain. Specifically, under suitable conditions on
Q(x)
and
F(x,i)
, we will study the derivatives
\partial_{x}\Phi(x,i)
and
\partial^{2}_{x}\Phi(x,i)
of
\Phi(x,i)
with respect to
x
.

Averaging principle
: As an application of
the Poison equation, we study the strong and weak convergences of solutions of
(
1.2
)
.
For strong convergence, we get that when
\sigma(x,\alpha)=\sigma(x)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}\left|X_{t}^{\varepsilon}-\bar{X_{t}}\right|^{p}\right)\leq C_{p,T}\varepsilon^{p/2},\quad\forall\varepsilon\in(0,1],

where
\bar{X}
is the solution of the corresponding averaged equation as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Note that condition
\sigma(x,\alpha)=\sigma(x)
is necessary, otherwise the strong convergence may fails (see Remark
2.11
).

In Example
2.8
we will show that the strong order
1/2
above is optimal. We focus on the case when the drift coefficient
b(x,\alpha)
only satisfies the monotonicity condition with respect to
x
, particularly the case when
b(x,\alpha)=-|x|^{2}x+\tilde{b}(\alpha)
, where
\tilde{b}
is a bounded function on
\mathbb{S}
.
The papers
[
48
]
and
[
10
]
have examined the optimal strong convergence order when the switching process is also a diffusion. However, the results and techniques of these papers don’t fit into our setting.

For weak convergence, it is important to consider the dependence of
\sigma(x,\alpha)
on
\alpha
. We will study the weak convergences of
\{X^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
in
C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}\}_{\varepsilon\in(0,1]}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for fixed
t\geq 0
, respectively. For the former, we will use the Poisson equation technique and the martingale problem method to identify the limiting process. For the latter, we will use combine the Poisson equation and Kolmogorov equation to get a weak convergence of order
1
under the set
C^{4}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the space of functions on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with all partial derivatives up to order 4 being continuous and of polynomial growth. This order
1
will be shown to be optimal by a concrete example (see Example
2.10
).

Central limit theorem
:
It is also very important to study the fluctuations of
X^{\varepsilon}
around the averaged process
\bar{X}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. For this, it is crucial to examine the asymptotic behavior of the deviation between
X^{\varepsilon}
and
\bar{X}
.

Z_{t}^{\varepsilon}:=\frac{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}-\bar{X}_{t}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}},\ \ \text{as}\penalty\ \varepsilon\to 0.

Consequently, the second goal of this paper is to establish weak convergence of
\{Z^{\varepsilon}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
to
Z
in
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where
Z
is the unique solution of a new stochastic differential equation that incorporates an additional explicit stochastic integral term.

The central limit theorem (CLT), also known as normal deviations in classical multi-scale SDEs, has been extensively studied. For instance, Khasminskii
[
26
]
investigated normal deviations in a specific class of slow-fast stochastic systems with vanishing noise in the slow equation. Cerrai
[
8
]
extended Khasminskii’s results to the infinite-dimensional case. However, the techniques used in
[
8
,
26
]
are not applicable to fully coupled stochastic systems. In a different context, Wang and Roberts
[
55
]
examined the normal deviations of a stochastic system driven by additive noise in infinite dimensions, where a martingale problem approach was employed to characterize the limiting process. Recently, Hong et al.
[
25
]
investigated the CLT for a class of multi-scale McKean-Vlasov SDEs, where the martingale representation theorem was used to identify the corresponding limiting process.

In the case when
\sigma\equiv 0
Pakdaman et al.
[
37
]
used an asymptotic expansion technique to derive a CLT. Subsequently, the averaging principle and CLT for a specific class of multi-scale piecewise-deterministic Markov processes in infinite dimensions were investigated in
[
20
]
and
[
21
]
respectively. The martingale problem approach was used in
[
37
,
20
,
21
]
to identify the limiting process. However, the corresponding weak convergence order has not been addressed in these papers.

In this paper, we will use the Poisson equation technique and the associated martingale problem to characterize the limiting process. Furthermore, we will combine the Kolmogorov backward equation and the Poisson equation to study weak convergence order for
\{Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}\}_{\varepsilon>0}
under the space
C^{4}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Specifically, we will show that for any
\phi\in C^{4}_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

|\mathbb{E}\phi(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t})-\mathbb{E}\phi(Z_{t})|\leqslant C_{\phi}\varepsilon^{1/2},

where
C_{\phi}>0
is a constant dependent on
\phi
. We will show that the order
1/2
above is optimal by a specific example (see Example
2.8
). As far as we know, these are the first results on the optimal weak convergence order, erms of the CLT, for the stochastic system
(
1.2
)
.

2.4.
Examples

In this subsection, we give several concrete examples to illustrate our convergence orders are optimal. For simplicity, we only consider the 1D case.

Example 2.8
.

Considering:

\displaystyle dX^{\varepsilon}_{t}=b(\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{t})dt+dW_{t},\quad X^{\varepsilon}_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R},

\alpha^{\varepsilon}
is a
\{1,2\}
-valued Markov chain with the generator

For

\end{array}\right).

Since the averaged coefficient
\bar{b}(x)=0
, the corresponding averaged equation is given by

\bar{X}_{t}=x+W_{t}.

Refer to
[
29

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|X^{\varepsilon}_{t}-\bar{X}_{t}\right|^{2}=\varepsilon t+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\left(e^{-\frac{2t}{\varepsilon}}-1\right),

which implies that the strong convergence order
1/2
in
(
2.19
)
is optimal.

Furthermore, we have

Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\frac{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}-\bar{X}_{t}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\int^{t}_{0}b(\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{s})ds.

It is easy to check that the corresponding limiting process
Z_{t}=\tilde{W}_{t}
via the limiting equation
(
2.24
)
, where
\tilde{W}
is a
1
D standard Brownian motion. Taking
\phi(x)=x
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}\phi(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t})-\mathbb{E}\phi(Z_{t})|=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}Z^{\varepsilon}_{t}-\mathbb{E}Z_{t}|=\Big|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\int^{t}_{0}\mathbb{E}b(\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{s})ds\Big|

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\int^{t}_{0}\left[\mathbb{P}(\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{s}=1)-\mathbb{P}(\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{s}=2)\right]ds\Big|

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\int^{t}_{0}\left[p^{\varepsilon}_{11}(s)-p^{\varepsilon}_{12}(s)\right]ds\Big|

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\int^{t}_{0}e^{-2s/\varepsilon}ds=\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{2}\left(1-e^{-\frac{2t}{\varepsilon}}\right),

which implies that the weak convergence order
1/2
in
(
2.25
)
is optimal.

Remark 2.9
.

Choosing
\phi(x)=x^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}\phi(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t})-\mathbb{E}\phi(Z_{t})|=\left|\mathbb{E}(Z^{\varepsilon}_{t})^{2}-\mathbb{E}Z_{t}^{2}\right|=\Big|\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}-\bar{X}_{t}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\Big)^{2}-t\Big|=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(1-e^{-\frac{2t}{\varepsilon}}\right).

This implies the weak convergence order is
1
which is bigger than
1/2
. Hence, the weak order may vary depending on the class
\mathbb{C}
under consideration.

Example 2.10
.

Considering:

(2.26)

\displaystyle dX^{\varepsilon}_{t}=b(\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{t})dt+\sigma(\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{t})dW_{t},\quad X^{\varepsilon}_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R},

where
\alpha^{\varepsilon}
is the same Markov chain as in Example
2.8
, and assume

\bar{\sigma}(x)=1

Taking

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\phi(X^{\varepsilon}_{t})-\mathbb{E}\phi(\bar{X}_{t})\right|=\left|\mathbb{E}X^{\varepsilon}_{t}-\mathbb{E}\bar{X}_{t}\right|=\Big|\int^{t}_{0}\mathbb{E}b(\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{s})ds\Big|=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left(1-e^{-\frac{2t}{\varepsilon}}\right).

This means that the weak convergence order

in
(
2.23

is optimal.

Remark 2.11
.

Let

in Eq.

In this section, we focus on the study of Poisson equation

Proposition 3.1

2

(3.2)

\sup_{t\geqslant 0,i\in\mathbb{S}}|\partial_{x_{k}}\partial_{x_{l}}P^{x}_{t}f(i)|\leqslant C\left(\|\partial_{x_{k}}Q(x)\|_{\ell}\|\partial_{x_{l}}Q(x)\|_{\ell}+\|\partial_{x_{k}}\partial_{x_{l}}Q(x)\|_{\ell}\right).

(3.4)

\displaystyle|\partial_{x_{k}}\partial_{x_{l}}P^{x}_{t}f(i)\!-\!\partial_{x_{k}}\partial_{x_{l}}P^{x}_{t}f(i^{\prime})|\!\leq\!C\left(\|\partial_{x_{k}}Q(x)\|_{\ell}\|\partial_{x_{l}}Q(x)\|_{\ell}\!+\!\|\partial_{x_{k}}\partial_{x_{l}}Q(x)\|_{\ell}\right)e^{-\lambda t}(1\!+\!t^{2}).

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{k}}P^{x}_{t}f(i)=\int^{t}_{0}P^{x}_{s}\left[\partial_{x_{k}}Q(x)\right]P^{x}_{t-s}f(i)ds.

\displaystyle\leqslant\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\int^{t}_{0}\sup_{j\in\mathbb{S}}|\partial_{x_{k}}Q(x)\left[P^{x}_{t-s}(f-\mu^{x}(f))\right](j)|ds

\displaystyle\leqslant\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x_{k}}Q(x)\|_{\ell}\int^{t}_{0}\sup_{j\in\mathbb{S}}|P^{x}_{t-s}f(j)-\mu^{x}(f)|ds

\displaystyle\leqslant\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x_{k}}Q(x)\|_{\ell}\int^{t}_{0}\sup_{j\in\mathbb{S}}\|p^{x}_{j\cdot}(t-s)-\mu^{x}\|_{\text{var}}ds

\displaystyle\leqslant\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle C\|\partial_{x_{k}}Q(x)\|_{\ell}\int^{t}_{0}e^{-\lambda(t-s)}ds\leqslant\frac{C}{\lambda}\|\partial_{x_{k}}Q(x)\|_{\ell}

and for any

\displaystyle|\partial_{x_{k}}P^{x}_{t}f(i)-\partial_{x_{k}}P^{x}_{t}f(i^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\Big|\int^{t}_{0}\big\{P^{x}_{s}[\partial_{x_{k}}Q(x)][P^{x}_{t-s}(f-\mu^{x}(f))](i)-P^{x}_{s}\left[\partial_{x_{k}}Q(x)\right][P^{x}_{t-s}(f-\mu^{x}(f))](i^{\prime})\big\}ds\Big|

\displaystyle\int^{t}_{0}\|p^{x}_{i\cdot}(s)-p^{x}_{i^{\prime}\cdot}(s)\|_{\text{var}}\sup_{j\in\mathbb{S}}|\partial_{x_{k}}Q(x)\left[P^{x}_{t-s}(f-\mu^{x}(f))\right](j)|ds

\displaystyle\leqslant\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x_{k}}Q(x)\|_{\ell}\int^{t}_{0}\|p^{x}_{i\cdot}(s)-p^{x}_{i^{\prime}\cdot}(s)\|_{\text{var}}\sup_{j\in\mathbb{S}}\|p^{x}_{j\cdot}(t-s)-\mu^{x}\|_{\text{var}}ds

(3.6)

\displaystyle C\|\partial_{x_{k}}Q(x)\|_{\ell}\int^{t}_{0}e^{-\lambda s}e^{-\lambda(t-s)}ds\leqslant C\|\partial_{x_{k}}Q(x)\|_{\ell}e^{-\lambda t}t.

Similarly, by

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{k}}\partial_{x_{l}}P^{x}_{t}f(i)=\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle+\int^{t}_{0}P^{x}_{s}\left[\partial_{x_{k}}Q(x)\right]\partial_{x_{l}}P^{x}_{t-s}f(i)ds.

Thus using
(
2.4
)

(

)
, we can obtain

\displaystyle|\partial_{x_{k}}\partial_{x_{l}}P^{x}_{t}f(i)|\leqslant\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle C\|\partial_{x_{l}}Q(x)\|_{\ell}\int^{t}_{0}\sup_{j\in\mathbb{S}}\left|\left[\partial_{x_{k}}Q(x)\right]\left[P^{x}_{t-s}(f-\mu^{x}(f))\right](j)\right|ds

\displaystyle+C\int^{t}_{0}\sup_{j\in\mathbb{S}}\left|\left[\partial_{x_{k}}\partial_{x_{l}}Q(x)\right]\left[P^{x}_{t-s}(f-\mu^{x}(f))\right](j)\right|ds

\displaystyle+C\int^{t}_{0}\sup_{j\in\mathbb{S}}\left|\left[\partial_{x_{k}}Q(x)\right]\left[\partial_{x_{l}}P^{x}_{t-s}f-\partial_{x_{l}}P^{x}_{t-s}f(i))\right](j)\right|ds

For any

\displaystyle\left\langle\bar{b}(x_{1})-\bar{b}(x_{2}),x_{1}-x_{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\left\langle\mu^{x_{1}}(b(x_{1},\cdot))-\mu^{x_{2}}(b(x_{2},\cdot)),x_{1}-x_{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leqslant\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\left|\mu^{x_{1}}(b(x_{1},\cdot))-\mathbb{E}b(x_{1},\alpha^{x_{1},i}_{t})\right||x_{1}-x_{2}|+\left|\mu^{x_{2}}(b(x_{2},\cdot))-\mathbb{E}b(x_{2},\alpha^{x_{2},i}_{t})\right||x_{1}-x_{2}|

\displaystyle C(1+|x_{1}|^{k}+|x_{2}|^{k})e^{-\lambda t}|x_{1}-x_{2}|+\left\langle\mathbb{E}b(x_{1},\alpha^{x_{1},i}_{t})-\mathbb{E}b(x_{2},\alpha^{x_{2},i}_{t}),x_{1}-x_{2}\right\rangle.

4.1.2.
Mollifying approximation

Using
(
3.24
)
,
1
-
3
imply the Poisson equation’s solution
\Phi(x,\alpha)
is polynomial growth and local Lipschitz continuous with respective to
x
, which is not smooth enough for applying Itô’s formula on
\Phi
later. Thus a mollifying convergence argument will be introduced firstly.

Let
\rho:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,1]
be a smooth function such that for
m\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\rho(z)dz=1,\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|z\right|^{m}\rho(z)dz\leq C_{m},\quad\left\|\nabla^{m}\rho(z)\right\|\leq C_{m}\rho(z).

Define a mollifying approximation of
H:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
as follows:

\displaystyle H_{m}(x,i):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}H(x-z,i)\rho^{m}(z)dz.

We have the following lemma:

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose there exist
C,k>0
such that for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(4.8)

\displaystyle\|H(x,\cdot)-H(y,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leqslant C(1+|x|^{k}+|y|^{k})|x-y|,\quad\|H(x,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leqslant C(1+|x|^{k}).

Then for
m\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

(4.9)

\displaystyle\|H(x,\cdot)-H_{m}(x,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leqslant C(1+|x|^{k})m^{-1},\quad\|H_{m}(x,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leqslant C(1+|x|^{k}),

(4.10)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x}H_{m}(x,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leqslant C(1+|x|^{k}),\quad\|\partial^{2}_{x}H_{m}(x,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leqslant C(1+|x|^{k})m,

where
C>0
is a constant independent of
m
and
x
.

Proof.

(i)
The proof of
(
4.9
)
.
For
m\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\left|H_{m}(x,i)-H(x,i)\right|=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}H(x-z,i)\rho^{m}(z)dz-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}H(x,i)\rho^{m}(z)dz\Big|

\displaystyle\leqslant\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1+|x|^{k}+|z|^{k})||z|\rho^{m}(z)dz

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle C(1+|x|^{k})m^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|mz\right|m^{n}\rho(mz)dz+Cm^{-k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|mz|^{k}m^{n}\rho(mz)dz

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle C(1+|x|^{k})m^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|y\right|\rho(y)dy+Cm^{-k}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|y\right|^{k}\rho(y)dy

\displaystyle\leqslant\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle C(1+|x|^{k})m^{-1},

which implies

\|H(x,\cdot)-H_{m}(x,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leqslant C(1+|x|^{k})m^{-1}.

Consequently, we have for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\|H_{m}(x,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leqslant\|H(x,\cdot)-H_{m}(x,\cdot)\|_{\infty}+\|H(x,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leqslant C(1+|x|^{k}).

(ii)
The proof of
(
4.10
)
.
For
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
i\in\mathbb{S}
,

\displaystyle\left|H_{m}(x_{1},i)-H_{m}(x_{2},i)\right|=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}H(x_{1}-z,i)\rho^{m}(z)dz-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}H(x_{2}-z,i)\rho^{m}(z)dz\Big|

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1+|x_{1}|^{k}+|x_{2}|^{k}+|z|^{k})|x_{1}-x_{2}|\rho^{m}(z)dz

\displaystyle\leq(1+|x_{1}|^{k}+|x_{2}|^{k})|x_{1}-x_{2}|

implies

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{x}H_{m}(x,\cdot)\right\|_{\infty}\leq C(1+|x|^{k}).

Since

\displaystyle\partial_{x}H_{m}(x,i)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}H(z,i)\partial_{x}\left[\rho^{m}(x-z)\right]dz=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}H(x-z,i)m^{n+1}\nabla\rho(mz)dz.

Then by
(
4.8
)
and
(
4.7
)
, we get

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{x}H_{m}(x_{1},i)-\partial_{x}H_{m}(x_{2},i)\right\|\leq\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left|H(x_{1}-z,i)-H(x_{2}-z,i)\right|\left|\nabla\rho(mz)\right|\cdot m^{n+1}dz

\displaystyle\leq\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle C\left|x_{1}-x_{2}\right|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1+|x_{1}|^{k}+|x_{2}|^{k}+|z|^{k})\rho(mz)m^{n+1}dz

\displaystyle\leq\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle C(1+|x_{1}|^{k}+|x_{2}|^{k})|x_{1}-x_{2}|m,

which implies

\displaystyle\left\|\partial_{x}^{2}H_{m}(x,\cdot)\right\|_{\infty}\leq C(1+|x|^{k})m.

The proof is complete.
∎

\displaystyle C_{p,T}\varepsilon^{p/2}\big(1+|x|^{k_{p}}\big)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}\big(\sup_{0\leqslant t\leqslant T}|X_{t}^{\varepsilon}-\bar{X_{t}}|^{p}\big)+C_{p,T}m^{p}\varepsilon^{p}(1+|x|^{k_{p}}).

Using Kunita’s first inequality (see

4.2.3.
Proof of Theorem

We prove our second result in the averaging principle.

Proof of Theorem

Let

\sigma\{\varphi_{t},\varphi\in C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{n}),t\leqslant t_{0}\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(U(X_{t})-U(X_{t_{0}})-\int^{t}_{t_{0}}\bar{\mathcal{L}}U(X_{s})ds\right)\Psi_{t_{0}}(X)\right]=0,\quad t\leqslant t_{0}\leqslant T,

\displaystyle U(X^{\varepsilon_{k}}_{t})=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle U(X^{\varepsilon_{k}}_{t_{0}})+\int^{t}_{t_{0}}\langle\partial_{x}U(X^{\varepsilon_{k}}_{s}),b(X^{\varepsilon_{k}}_{s},\alpha^{\varepsilon_{k}}_{s})\rangle ds+\int^{t}_{t_{0}}\langle\partial_{x}U(X^{\varepsilon_{k}}_{s}),\sigma(X^{\varepsilon_{k}}_{s},\alpha^{\varepsilon_{k}}_{s})dW_{s}\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\int^{t}_{t_{0}}\text{Tr}\left[(\sigma\sigma^{\ast})(X^{\varepsilon_{k}}_{s},\alpha^{\varepsilon_{k}}_{s})\cdot\partial^{2}_{x}U(X^{\varepsilon_{k}}_{s})\right]ds.

in

\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left\{\left[U(X^{\varepsilon_{k}}_{t})-U(X^{\varepsilon_{k}}_{t_{0}})\right]\Psi_{t_{0}}(X^{{\varepsilon}_{k}})\right\}=\mathbb{E}\left\{\left[U(X_{t})-U(X_{t_{0}})\right]\Psi_{t_{0}}(X)\right\}

Proof of Theorem
2.5
:
Fixed

\tilde{u}^{t}(s,x):=u(t-s,x)
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}\phi(\alpha,x):=\langle b(x,\alpha),\nabla\phi(x)\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}[\sigma\sigma^{*}(x,\alpha)\nabla^{2}\phi(x)]

\mathcal{F}_{t}
-martingale.
Note that

\tilde{u}^{t}(0,x)=\mathbb{E}\phi(\bar{X}^{x}_{t})

\partial_{s}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,X^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})=-\bar{\mathcal{L}}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\cdot)(X^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\phi(X^{{\varepsilon}}_{t})-\mathbb{E}\phi(\bar{X}_{t})\right|=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\int^{t}_{0}-\bar{\mathcal{L}}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\cdot)(X^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})ds+\mathbb{E}\int^{t}_{0}\mathcal{L}_{1}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\cdot)(\alpha^{{\varepsilon}}_{s},X^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})ds\right|

(4.34)

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}\!\int^{t}_{0}\!\langle b(X^{{\varepsilon}}_{s},\alpha^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})\!-\!\bar{b}(X^{{\varepsilon}}_{s}),\partial_{x}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,X^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}\big[(\sigma\sigma^{\ast}(X^{{\varepsilon}}_{s},\alpha^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})\!-\!\bar{\sigma}\bar{\sigma}(X^{{\varepsilon}}_{s}))\partial_{x}^{2}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,X^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})\big]ds\Big|.

,

. By Itô’s formula and following a similar argument in

)

\displaystyle\tilde{u}^{t}(t,\bar{X}_{t},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{t})=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}\int^{t}_{0}\left\langle\partial_{z}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\bar{X}_{s},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{s}),\left(b(X^{{\varepsilon}}_{s},\alpha^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})-\bar{b}(X^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})\right)\right\rangle ds

\displaystyle+\int^{t}_{0}\!\!\left\langle\partial_{x}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\bar{X}_{s},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{s}),\sigma(\bar{X}_{s})dW_{s}\right\rangle\!+\!\int^{t}_{0}\big\langle\partial_{z}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\bar{X}_{s},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{s}),\partial_{x}\sigma(\bar{X}_{t})\cdot\eta_{s}^{\varepsilon}dW_{s}\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\tilde{u}^{t}(0,x,0)+\int^{t}_{0}\partial_{s}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\bar{X}_{s},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})ds+\int^{t}_{0}(\mathcal{L}_{1}+\mathcal{L}_{2}+\mathcal{L}_{3})\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\bar{X}_{s},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})ds

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\sum^{n}_{j=1}\int^{t}_{0}\partial_{z_{i}}\partial_{z_{j}}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\bar{X}_{s},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})\left[F\otimes\Phi+(F\otimes\Phi)^{\ast}\right]_{ij}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{s})ds

\displaystyle+\sqrt{{\varepsilon}_{k}}\Big[R^{{\varepsilon}}_{\partial_{z}\tilde{u},\Phi}(t,0)+\int^{t}_{0}\langle\Phi(X_{s}^{\varepsilon},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{s}),\partial_{s}\partial_{z}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\bar{X}_{s},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})\rangle ds

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+\langle\Phi(x,\alpha),\partial_{z}\tilde{u}^{t}(0,x,0)\rangle-\langle\Phi(X_{t}^{\varepsilon},\alpha_{t}^{\varepsilon}),\partial_{z}\tilde{u}^{t}(t,\bar{X}_{t},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{t})\rangle\Big]

\displaystyle+\int^{t}_{0}\!\left\langle\partial_{x}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\bar{X}_{s},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{s}),\sigma(\bar{X}_{s})dW_{s}\right\rangle\!+\!\!\int^{t}_{0}\big\langle\partial_{z}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\bar{X}_{s},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{s}),\partial_{x}\sigma(\bar{X}_{t})\cdot\eta_{s}^{\varepsilon}dW_{s}\big\rangle,

where

is defined in

5.17
)
with
{\varepsilon}_{k}
and
\partial_{z}U
are replaced by
{\varepsilon}
and

respectively.

Note that

\tilde{u}^{t}(t,\bar{X}_{t},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{t})=\phi(\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{t}),\tilde{u}^{t}(0,x,0)=\mathbb{E}\phi(Z^{x,0}_{t})

\partial_{s}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\bar{X}_{s},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})=-\mathcal{L}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\bar{X}_{s},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{s}),

we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\phi(\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{t})-\mathbb{E}\phi(Z^{x,0}_{t})\right|=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\Big|\mathbb{E}\int^{t}_{0}-\mathcal{L}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\bar{X}_{s},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})ds+\mathbb{E}\int^{t}_{0}(\mathcal{L}_{1}+\mathcal{L}_{2}+\mathcal{L}_{3})\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\bar{X}_{s},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})ds

\displaystyle+\!\frac{1}{2}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\sum^{n}_{j=1}\mathbb{E}\!\int^{t}_{0}\!\partial_{z_{i}}\partial_{z_{j}}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\bar{X}_{s},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})\left[F\otimes\Phi\!+\!(F\otimes\Phi)^{\ast}\right]_{ij}(X_{s}^{\varepsilon},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{s})ds

\displaystyle+\sqrt{{\varepsilon}}\mathbb{E}\Big[R^{{\varepsilon}}_{\partial_{z}\tilde{u},\Phi}(t,0)+\int^{t}_{0}\langle\Phi(X_{s}^{\varepsilon},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{s}),\partial_{s}\partial_{z}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\bar{X}_{s},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})\rangle ds

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\langle\Phi(x,\alpha),\partial_{z}\tilde{u}^{t}(0,x,0)\rangle-\langle\Phi(X_{t}^{\varepsilon},\alpha_{t}^{\varepsilon}),\partial_{z}\tilde{u}^{t}(t,\bar{X}_{t},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{t})\rangle\Big]\Big|

(5.29)

\displaystyle\leqslant\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\Big|\int^{t}_{0}\text{Tr}\big[\partial^{2}_{z}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\bar{X}_{s},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})\left[F\otimes\Phi+(F\otimes\Phi)^{\ast}\right](X_{s}^{\varepsilon},\alpha_{s}^{\varepsilon})\big]

\displaystyle+\sqrt{{\varepsilon}}\Big|\mathbb{E}\big[R^{{\varepsilon}}_{\partial_{z}\tilde{u},\Phi}(t,0)+\int^{t}_{0}\langle\Phi(X_{s}^{\varepsilon},\alpha^{\varepsilon}_{s}),\partial_{s}\partial_{z}\tilde{u}^{t}(s,\bar{X}_{s},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{s})\rangle ds

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\langle\Phi(x,\alpha),\partial_{z}\tilde{u}^{t}(0,x,0)\rangle\!-\!\langle\Phi(X_{t}^{\varepsilon},\alpha_{t}^{\varepsilon}),\partial_{z}\tilde{u}^{t}(t,\bar{X}_{t},\eta^{{\varepsilon}}_{t})\rangle\big]\Big|.

For any

, define
