The Toda and Painlevé Systems Associated with Semiclassical Matrix-Valued Orthogonal Polynomials of Laguerre Type This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Painlevé Equations and Applications in Memory of Andrei Kapaev. The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/Kapaev.html
Mattia CAFASSO † and Manuel D. DE LA IGLESIA ‡

Abstract
Consider the Laguerre polynomials and deform them by the introduction in the measure of an exponential singularity at zero. In [Chen Y., Its A., J. Approx. Theory 162 (2010), 270–297] the authors proved that this deformation can be described by systems of differential/difference equations for the corresponding recursion coefficients and that these equations, ultimately, are equivalent to the Painlevé III equation and its Bäcklund/Schlesinger transformations. Here we prove that an analogue result holds for some kind of semiclassical matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials of Laguerre type.

Painlevé equations; Toda lattices; Riemann–Hilbert problems; matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials.

Introduction

The relation between integrable systems and orthogonal polynomials goes back to the seminar paper of Moser
[
18
]
. There, the author made the remarkable observation that, given a family of orthogonal polynomials associated to a measure
w
on the real axis, the related tridiagonal Jacobi matrix can be interpreted as a Lax matrix for the Toda equations, where the independent variable of equations plays the role of deformation parameters for the measure
w
. Starting from the nineties, this result and various generalizations (to orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle, to multiple orthogonal polynomials and many other types of special polynomials) played a central role in the field of random matrices, and the study of the equations satisfied by orthogonal polynomials found applications to 2D quantum gravity
[
14
]
and to the computations of gap probabilities and partition functions associated to random models, both in the discrete and continuous setting (see, for instance,
[
1
]
and
[
16
]
).

The scope of this paper is to work on the relation between
matrix-valued
orthogonal polynomials and
non-commutative
integrable equations. This is a relatively new field, in which few examples have been worked out. In
[
17
]
, it had been shown that matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials (on the real line) satisfy a non-commutative version of the Toda equations, and in
[
6
]
one of the authors provided an analogue result for matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle, upon replacing Toda equations with the (multi-component) Ablowitz–Ladik hierarchy (a well known integrable discretisation of the non-linear Schrödinger one). In both cases, the main tool had been the theory of quasi-determinants (Schur complements), which were known to be related to non-commutative integrable systems since the pioneering work of Etingof, Gelfand and Retakh
[
13
]
. Schur complements had been used also in the series of papers
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
and in
[
19
]
, where a non-commutative version of the Painlevé II equation was introduced, and studied later on in
[
5
]
using Riemann–Hilbert techniques.

In 2011, independently in
[
8
]
and
[
15
]
, it was established a general theory to study matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials through Riemann–Hilbert problems, thus extending the well known result of Fokas, Its and Kitaev
[
14
]
from the scalar to the matrix case. This theory, in our opinion, provides one of the best ways to deduce, in a uniform way, non-linear equations related to matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials, using the well known technique of reducing the Riemann–Hilbert problem to a simpler one, where the jumps are constant. In
[
8
]
, this technique has been used to deduce a matrix version of the discrete Painlevé I equation, while in
[
7
]
we proved that the Christoffel–Darboux kernel associated to certain matrix-valued Hermite polynomials satisfies a non-commutative version of the Painlevé IV equation.

The goal of this paper is to give another instance of this relation between non-commutative Painlevé equations and matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials. The results we present are to be thought as a non-commutative analogue of the results in
[
9
]
. There, the authors studied the (scalar) orthogonal polynomials associated to the measure

w(x,s):=x^{\alpha}{\rm e}^{-x-s/x}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, which is a deformation of the usual Laguerre measure, where the deformation is induced by the parameter
s
and changes the behaviour of the measure at zero. Using two different approaches (ladder operators and Riemann–Hilbert techniques) Chen and Its proved that some quantities
a_{n}(s)
and
b_{n}(s)
, which ultimately can be expressed through the recursion coefficients of the orthogonal polynomials, satisfy a differential and a difference system, which can be reduced, respectively, to the Painlevé III equation and a discrete analogue of it, which is conjectured to be a composition of the basic Schlesinger transformations of Painlevé III.

The non-commutative analogue of the difference and differential systems for
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
are given here in Theorems
4.3
and
4.5
. The derivation, which is based upon the Riemann–Hilbert method established in
[
15
]
, is not a straightforward generalisation of the one for the scalar case: namely one has to push a little bit further the analysis of the behavior of the solution of the Riemann–Hilbert problem at the two singular points
0
and
\infty
. Also, while the systems of
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
were of first-order, here our two systems are of second-order, which is a phenomenon that, in some way, we already observed in the previous paper
[
7
]
(and also, to some extent, in
[
5
]
for the case of Painlevé XXXIV).

The paper is organized as follows: in the first section we define the matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials we want to study, construct the related Riemann–Hilbert problem and the corresponding Lax triple (
2.15
). In the second section, the compatibility conditions of the Lax triples are computed, together with some additional relations that, in the scalar case, are deduced from the compatibility conditions but here they have to be computed in a different way (see Proposition
3.4
). Finally, in the third section, our main results, Theorems
4.3
and
4.5
, are stated and deduced, with straightforward (but sometimes lengthy) computations. The last section gives some additional relations holding for a special class of matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials that were introduced in
[
10
]
.

2
From the Riemann–Hilbert problem to the Lax system

Consider the following
N\times N
weight matrix

\displaystyle\bm{W}(s;x):=x^{\alpha}{\rm e}^{-x-s/x}\bm{T}(x)\bm{T}^{*}(x),\qquad x\in[0,\infty),\qquad\alpha>0,\qquad s>0.

Observe that
\bm{W}(s;x)
is a weight matrix of Laguerre type perturbed by a multiplicative factor
{\rm e}^{-s/x}
, which induces an infinitely strong zero at the origin. For simplicity, the matrix-valued function
\bm{T}(x)
is chosen such that

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{x}\bm{T}(x)\big)\bm{T}^{-1}(x)=\frac{\bm{B}}{x},

(2.1)

where
\bm{B}
is an arbitrary
N\times N
constant matrix, independent of
s
, such that all moments of
\bm{W}(s;x)
are finite (entrywise). Observe that in this case, the solution of (
2.1
) is given by
\bm{T}(x)=x^{\bm{B}}={\rm e}^{\bm{B}\log x}
.

Given such a weight we construct (if existing) a sequence of
monic
matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials
\{\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}(s;x)\}_{n\geq 0}
which is uniquely characterized by these two conditions:

•

\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}(s;x)
is monic of order
n
:

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}(s;x)=x^{n}\bm{I}_{N}+\cdots,

•

For any
n,m\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}(s;x)\bm{W}(s;x)\bm{\hat{P}}^{*}_{m}(s;x)\mathrm{d}x=\bm{\gamma}_{n}^{-1}(s)\delta_{n,m},

where
\bm{\gamma}_{n}(s)
is the inverse of the
n^{th}
(matrix-valued) norm of our family of matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials. Note that, for any
n
,
\bm{\gamma}_{n}(s)
is a Hermitian matrix.

As in the scalar case, the existence of the sequence of matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials can be equivalently restated as the existence of the solution to a certain (matrix-valued) Riemann–Hilbert boundary value problem, as it has been proven in
[
15
]
. This is a general result that does not depend on the particular form of the measure
\bm{W}(s;x)
and we make reference to
[
15
]
for its general formulation. Applied to our case, we have the following proposition:

Proposition 2.1
.

The sequence of matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials
\{\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}(s;x)\}_{n\geq 0}
exists if and only if, for any
n\geq 0
, it exists a block matrix-valued function
\bm{Y}^{(n)}(s;z)
which is analytic on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{[0,\infty)}
and such that the following three conditions are satisfied:

•

The boundary values
\bm{Y}_{+}^{(n)}
,
\bm{Y}_{-}^{(n)}
on the
(
standardly oriented
)
contour
(0,\infty)
satisfy the following jump condition:

\displaystyle\bm{Y}_{+}^{(n)}(s;x)=\bm{Y}_{-}^{(n)}(s;x)\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{I}_{N}&x^{\alpha}{\rm e}^{-x-s/x}\bm{T}(x)\bm{T}^{*}(x)\\
0&\bm{I}_{N}\end{matrix}\right),\qquad x\in(0,\infty).

(2.2)

•

At infinity the solution
\bm{Y}^{(n)}
has the following asymptotic behaviour:

\displaystyle\bm{Y}^{(n)}(s;z)=\left(\bm{I}_{2N}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\bm{Y}_{-k}^{(n)}(s)}{z^{k}}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}z^{n}\bm{I}_{N}&0\\
0&z^{-n}\bm{I}_{N}\end{matrix}\right),\qquad z\rightarrow\infty.

(2.3)

•

At the point
0
the solution
\bm{Y}^{(n)}
is non–singular:

\displaystyle\bm{Y}^{(n)}(s;z)=\bm{Q}^{(n)}(s)\left(\bm{I}_{2N}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\bm{Y}^{(n)}_{k}(s)z^{k}\right),\qquad z\rightarrow 0.

(2.4)

Indeed, one can write the solution of the Riemann--Hilbert problem above in terms of matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials

While
\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}(s;x)
is, strictly speaking, defined on the positive real axis, below we denote with
\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}(s;z)
its analytic continuation on the complex plane. Also, we adopt the convention that for any matrix-valued function
\bm{P}(z)

\bm{P}^{*}(z):=\left(\bm{P}(\bar{z})\right)^{*}
.
as follows

\displaystyle\bm{Y}^{(n)}(s;z)=\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}(s;z)&\mathcal{C}(\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}\bm{W})(s;z)\\
-2\pi i\bm{\gamma}_{n-1}(s)\bm{\hat{P}}_{n-1}(s;z)&-2\pi i\bm{\gamma}_{n-1}(s)\mathcal{C}(\bm{\hat{P}}_{n-1}\bm{W})(s;z)\end{matrix}\right).

(2.5)

Here we denoted with
\mathcal{C}
the Cauchy transform, applied to (possibly) matrix-valued functions
\bm{F}(x)
in such a way that

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\bm{F})(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\bm{F}(x)}{x-z}\mathrm{d}x.

We can normalize our polynomials in the following way

\displaystyle\bm{P}_{n}(s;x)=\bm{\kappa}_{n}(s)\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}(s;x),\qquad\mbox{with}\quad\bm{\gamma}_{n}(s)=\bm{\kappa}^{*}_{n}(s)\bm{\kappa}_{n}(s).

As it is customary, in order to derive differential and difference equations from our family of matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials we will reduce the Riemann–Hilbert problem (
2.2
)–(
2.4
) to a one with
constant
jumps for
\bm{\Psi}^{(n)}(s;z):=\bm{Y}^{(n)}(s;z)\bm{R}(s;z)
, with
\bm{R}(s;z)
to be defined. Then
\bm{\Psi}^{(n)}(s;z)
will satisfy a Lax system of three equations, whose coefficients will depend on the entries of
\bm{Y}^{(n)}_{-1}(s)
and
\bm{Q}^{(n)}(s)
defined below. The two propositions and the corollary below express
\bm{Y}^{(n)}_{-1}(s)
and
\bm{Q}^{(n)}(s)
in function of meaningful quantities for the corresponding matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials. In the following, we denote

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}(s;x)=x^{n}\bm{I}_{N}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\bm{a}_{n,j}(s)x^{j}.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
15

)
.

All coefficients
\bm{a}_{n,j}(s)
of the monic matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials are real matrices. Additionally we have

\displaystyle\bm{Y}^{(n)}_{-1}(s)=\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{a}_{n,n-1}(s)&-\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\bm{\gamma}_{n}^{-1}(s)\vskip 2.84526pt\\
-2\pi i\bm{\gamma}_{n-1}(s)&-\bm{a}^{*}_{n,n-1}(s)\end{matrix}\right).

(2.6)

For the next proposition, we introduce the quantities
\bm{p}_{n}(s),\bm{q}_{n}(s)
, defined by

\displaystyle\bm{p}_{n}(s):=\mathcal{C}(\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}\bm{W})(s;0)\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}^{*}(s;0),

\displaystyle\bm{q}_{n}(s):=2\pi i\bm{\gamma}_{n-1}(s)\bm{\hat{P}}_{n-1}(s;0)\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}^{-1}(s;0).

(2.7)

Proposition 2.3
.

The matrix
\bm{Q}^{(n)}(s)=\bm{Y}^{(n)}(s;0)
and its inverse
\bm{Q}^{(-n)}(s)
can be written as

\displaystyle\bm{Q}^{(n)}(s)=\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{I}_{N}&\bm{p}_{n}(s)\\
-\bm{q}_{n}(s)&\bm{I}_{N}+\bm{q}_{n}(s)\bm{p}^{*}_{n}(s)\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}(s;0)&0\\
0&\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}^{-*}(s;0)\end{matrix}\right),

(2.8)

\displaystyle\bm{Q}^{(-n)}(s)=\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}^{-1}(s;0)&0\\
0&\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}^{*}(s;0)\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{I}_{N}+\bm{p}_{n}(s)\bm{q}^{*}_{n}(s)&\bm{p}_{n}^{*}(s)\\
-\bm{q}_{n}^{*}(s)&\bm{I}_{N}\end{matrix}\right).

(2.9)

Proof.

The first formula (
2.8
) is a direct computation of the definition of
\bm{Y}^{(n)}(s;z)
evaluated at
z=0
(see (
2.5
)), the definition of
\bm{p}_{n}(s),\bm{q}_{n}(s)
in (
2.7
) and the Liouville–Ostrogradski formula (see
[
15
, Proposition 2.8]
), i.e.,

\displaystyle 2\pi i\bm{\gamma}_{n-1}(s)\big(\bm{\hat{P}}_{n-1}(s;z)\mathcal{C}(\bm{W}\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}^{*})(s;z)-\mathcal{C}(\bm{\hat{P}}_{n-1}\bm{W})(s;z)\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}^{*}(s;z)\big)=\bm{I}_{N},

(2.10)

evaluated at
z=0
. The second formula (
2.9
) is a consequence of the definition of the inverse of the Riemann–Hilbert problem (
2.5
), which can be found in formula (2.16) of
[
15
]
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\bm{Y}^{(-n)}(s;z)=\left(\begin{matrix}-2\pi i\mathcal{C}(\bm{W}\bm{\hat{P}}_{n-1}^{*})(s;z)\bm{\gamma}_{n-1}(s)&-\mathcal{C}(\bm{W}\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}^{*})(s;z)\\
2\pi i\bm{\hat{P}}_{n-1}^{*}(s;z)\bm{\gamma}_{n-1}(s)&\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}^{*}(s;z)\end{matrix}\right).

Therefore
\bm{Q}^{(-n)}(s)=\bm{Y}^{(-n)}(s;0)
is a consequence of the definition of
\bm{p}_{n}(s)
,
\bm{q}_{n}(s)
in (
2.7
) and the Hermitian transpose version of the Liouville–Ostrogradski formula (
2.10
).
∎

A simple but important consequence of this proposition is the corollary below.

Corollary 2.4
.

For any
n\geq 0
the matrices
\bm{p}_{n}(s)
and
\bm{q}_{n}(s)
in (
2.7
) are skew-Hermitian. Therefore
\bm{Q}^{(n)}(s)
and
\bm{Q}^{(-n)}(s)
can be rewritten in the following simplified way

\displaystyle\bm{Q}^{(n)}(s)=\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{I}_{N}&\bm{p}_{n}(s)\\
-\bm{q}_{n}(s)&\bm{I}_{N}-\bm{q}_{n}(s)\bm{p}_{n}(s)\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}(s;0)&0\\
0&\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}^{-*}(s;0)\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\bm{Q}^{(-n)}(s)=\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}^{-1}(s;0)&0\\
0&\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}^{*}(s;0)\end{matrix}\right)\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{I}_{N}-\bm{p}_{n}(s)\bm{q}_{n}(s)&-\bm{p}_{n}(s)\\
\bm{q}_{n}(s)&\bm{I}_{N}\end{matrix}\right).

Proof.

It suffices to compute the quantity
\bm{Q}^{(n)}(s)\bm{Q}_{n}^{(-n)}(s)=\bm{I}_{N}
using the equations (
2.8
) and (
2.9
) above, which gives
\bm{p}_{n}(s)+\bm{p}_{n}^{*}(s)=0
for the (block) entry
(1,2)
and
\bm{q}_{n}(s)+\bm{q}_{n}^{*}(s)=0
for the (block) entry
(2,1)
. Using this,
\bm{Q}^{(-n)}(s)\bm{Q}_{n}^{(n)}(s)=\bm{I}_{N}
holds immediately.
∎

We are now ready to state the main result of this Section, giving a Lax system associated to our matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials. In the following we denote by
\bm{\alpha}_{n}(s)
and
\bm{\beta}_{n}(s)
the recursion coefficients associated to our family of matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials, such that, for every
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle x\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}(s;x)=\bm{\hat{P}}_{n+1}(s;x)+\bm{\alpha}_{n}(s)\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}(s;x)+\bm{\beta}_{n}(s)\bm{\hat{P}}_{n-1}(s;x),

(2.11)

and we recall that we have the identity

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}_{n}(s)=\bm{\gamma}_{n}^{-1}(s)\bm{\gamma}_{n-1}(s).

(2.12)

Moreover, we define

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}_{3}:=\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{I}_{N}&0\\
0&-\bm{I}_{N}\end{matrix}\right).

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
\bm{Y}^{(n)}(s;z)
be the solution of the Riemann–Hilbert problem (
2.2
)–(
2.4
) and

\displaystyle\bm{R}(s;z):=\left(\begin{matrix}{\rm e}^{-\frac{1}{2}(z+\frac{s}{z})}z^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\bm{T}(s;z)&0\\
0&{\rm e}^{\frac{1}{2}(z+\frac{s}{z})}z^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\bm{T}^{-*}(s;z)\end{matrix}\right).

(2.13)

Then the function

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}^{(n)}(s;z):=\bm{Y}^{(n)}(s;z)\bm{R}(s;z)

(2.14)

satisfies the following equations

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\bm{\Psi}^{(n)}(s;z)=\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\sigma}_{3}+\frac{\bm{A}_{-1}^{(n)}(s)}{z}+\frac{\bm{A}_{-2}^{(n)}(s)}{z^{2}}\right)\bm{\Psi}^{(n)}(s;z),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\bm{\Psi}^{(n)}(s;z)=-\frac{\bm{A}_{-2}^{(n)}(s)}{sz}\bm{\Psi}^{(n)}(s;z),

\displaystyle\bm{\Psi}^{(n+1)}(s;z)=\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{(n)}(s;z)\bm{\Psi}^{(n)}(s;z),

(2.15)

where the matrices
\bm{A}_{-1}^{(n)}(s),\bm{A}_{-2}^{(n)}(s)
and
\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{(n)}(s;z)
are explicitly given, in terms of the matrix-valued orthogonal polynomials, by

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{-1}^{(n)}(s)=\left(\begin{matrix}(n+\alpha/2)\bm{I}_{N}+\bm{B}&-\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\bm{\gamma}_{n}^{-1}(s)\\
2\pi i\bm{\gamma}_{n-1}(s)&-(n+\alpha/2)\bm{I}_{N}-\bm{B}^{*}\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\bm{A}_{-2}^{(n)}(s)=\left(\begin{matrix}\dfrac{s}{2}(\bm{I}_{N}-2\bm{p}_{n}(s)\bm{q}_{n}(s))&-s\bm{p}_{n}(s)\\
-s\bm{q}_{n}(s)(\bm{I}_{N}-\bm{p}_{n}(s)\bm{q}_{n}(s))&-\dfrac{s}{2}(\bm{I}_{N}-2\bm{q}_{n}(s)\bm{p}_{n}(s))\end{matrix}\right),

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{(n)}(s;z)=\left(\begin{matrix}z\bm{I}_{N}-\bm{\alpha}_{n}(s)&\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\bm{\gamma}_{n}^{-1}(s)\\
-2\pi i\bm{\gamma}_{n}(s)&0\end{matrix}\right).

(2.16)

Proof.

The third equation in (
2.15
) as well as the expression of
\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{(n)}(s;z)
does not depend on the particular weight we choose and their derivation can be found in
[
15
, Theorem 2.16]
. For the first equation in (
2.15
), observe that
\bm{\Psi}^{(n)}(s;z)
satisfies a Riemann–Hilbert problem with jumps independent on
z
and
s
. More precisely, its jumps are localised on the positive real axis (because of the original jumps of
\bm{Y}^{(n)}(s;z)
) and, for general
\alpha>0
, on the negative real axis, because of the determination of
z^{\alpha}
. Hence, the expression
(\partial_{z}\bm{\Psi}^{(n)}(s;z))\bm{\Psi}^{(-n)}(s;z)
is analytic on
{\mathbb{C}}\mathbb{P}^{1}\setminus\{0,\infty\}
, and studying its behaviour at the singular points we can conclude that

\displaystyle(\partial_{z}\bm{\Psi}^{(n)}(s;z))\bm{\Psi}^{(-n)}(s;z)=-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\sigma}_{3}+\frac{\bm{A}_{-1}^{(n)}(s)}{z}+\frac{\bm{A}_{-2}^{(n)}(s)}{z^{2}},

(2.17)

for some matrices
\bm{A}_{-1}^{(n)}(s),\bm{A}_{-2}^{(n)}(s)
to be determined. For
\bm{A}^{(n)}_{-1}(s)
, we develop the left hand side of (
2.17
) at infinity. Because of the particular shape of
\bm{R}(s;z)
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\bm{A}^{(n)}_{-1}(s)=\left(\begin{matrix}(n+\alpha/2)\bm{I}_{N}+\bm{B}&0\\
0&-(n+\alpha/2)\bm{I}_{N}-\bm{B}^{*}\end{matrix}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\big[\bm{\sigma}_{3},\bm{Y}_{-1}^{(n)}(s)\big],

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
denotes the standard commutator operator. This last equation together with the Proposition
2.2
gives the desired form of
\bm{A}^{(n)}_{-1}(s)
. For
\bm{A}^{(n)}_{-2}(s)
, we expand the equation (
2.17
) around zero and we obtain that

\displaystyle\bm{A}^{(n)}_{-2}(s)=\frac{s}{2}\bm{Q}^{(n)}(s)\bm{\sigma}_{3}\bm{Q}^{(-n)}(s),

and this equation, together with the Corollary
2.4
, gives the precise form of
\bm{A}^{(n)}_{-2}(s)
. The second equation in (
2.15
) is deduced similarly, but it is simpler. Indeed, the expression

\displaystyle(\partial_{s}\bm{\Psi}^{(n)}(s;z))\bm{\Psi}^{(-n)}(s;z)

has a singularity only at zero, and expanding around this point one finds

\displaystyle(\partial_{s}\bm{\Psi}^{(n)}(s;z))\bm{\Psi}^{(-n)}(s;z)=-\frac{1}{2z}\bm{Q}^{(n)}(s)\bm{\sigma}_{3}\bm{Q}^{(-n)}(s)=-\frac{\bm{A}_{-2}^{(n)}(s)}{sz}.

∎

3
The Lax equations and some monodromy identities

Let us denote

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}(s;z):=-\frac{1}{2}\bm{\sigma}_{3}+\frac{\bm{A}_{-1}^{(n)}(s)}{z}+\frac{\bm{A}_{-2}^{(n)}(s)}{z^{2}},\qquad\bm{\mathcal{B}}^{(n)}(s;z):=-\frac{\bm{A}_{-2}^{(n)}(s)}{sz}.

(3.1)

The compatibility conditions between the equations in (
2.15
) give rise to three matrix equations of the form

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}(s;z)-\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\bm{\mathcal{B}}^{(n)}(s;z)+\big[\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}(s;z),\bm{\mathcal{B}}^{(n)}(s;z)\big]=0,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{(n)}(s;z)+\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{(n)}(s;z)\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}(s;z)-\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{(n+1)}\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{(n)}(s;z)=0,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{(n)}(s;z)+\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{(n)}(s;z)\bm{\mathcal{B}}^{(n)}(s;z)-\bm{\mathcal{B}}^{(n+1)}(s;z)\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{(n)}(s;z)=0.

(3.4)

The equation (
3.2
) describes the isomonodromic deformation of the first equation in (
2.15
) with respect to the parameter
s
and will give rise to equations of Painlevé type; analogously the equation (
3.3
) describes the isomonodromic deformation of the first equation in (
2.15
) with respect to the discrete parameter
n

4
The non-commutative Toda and Painlevé systems

In the scalar case, Chen and Its managed to give a closed system of two first-order difference and differential equations for the two variables
\bm{a}_{n}
,
\bm{b}_{n}
. This system is immediately seen to be equivalent to the Painlevé III equation for the variable
\bm{a}_{n}
(see
[
9
, Lemma 5 and Theorem 1]
). Here we prove that, in the matrix case, it is possible to give a system (of the first-order) of difference and differential equations for the
four
variables
\bm{a}_{n}
,
\bm{b}_{n}
,
\bm{B}_{n}
,
\hat{\bm{B}}_{n}
, where the presence of the last two variables is a clear consequence of the presence of the non-commutative constant matrix
\bm{B}
. Additionally, one can further reduce the system to a system of second-order difference and differential equations for the two variables
\bm{a}_{n}
,
\bm{b}_{n}
. We need first the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
n\geq 0
, the following equation holds:

\displaystyle\bm{\gamma}_{n}^{-1}(s)\bm{b}^{*}_{n}(s)\bm{\gamma}_{n}(s)=\bm{a}_{n}^{-1}(s)\bm{b}_{n}(s)\bm{a}_{n}(s).

(4.1)

Moreover, the derivative of
\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}(s;0)
can be expressed as

\displaystyle s\dot{\bm{\hat{P}}}_{n}(s;0)\bm{\hat{P}}_{n}^{-1}(s;0)=n\bm{I}_{N}+\bm{B}-\hat{\bm{B}}_{n}(s)+\bm{a}_{n}^{-1}(s)\bm{b}_{n}(s).

(4.2)

Proof.

For the first (
4.1
), using (
3.5
) and the fact that
\bm{p}_{n}
and
\bm{q}_{n}
are skew-Hermitian, we have that

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{n}\bm{\gamma}_{n}^{-1}\bm{b}^{*}_{n}\bm{\gamma}_{n}=2\pi is\bm{p}_{n}s\bm{q}_{n}^{*}\bm{p}_{n}^{*}\bm{\gamma}_{n}=(s\bm{p}_{n}\bm{q}_{n})(2\pi is\bm{p}_{n}\bm{\gamma}_{n})=\bm{b}_{n}\bm{a}_{n}.

For the second (
4.2
) we first observe that, expanding
\big(\partial_{s}\bm{\Psi}^{(n)}(s;z)\big)\bm{\Psi}^{(-n)}(s;z)
around zero, the constant term in
z
should give zero. This gives the equation

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{Q}}^{(n)}(s)=\frac{1}{2}\bm{Q}^{(n)}(s)\big[\bm{Y}_{1}^{(n)}(s),\bm{\sigma}_{3}\big].

On the other hand (
3.21
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{s}{2}\bm{Q}^{(n)}(s)\big[\bm{Y}_{1}^{(n)}(s),\bm{\sigma}_{3}\big]=\bm{A}_{-1}^{(n)}(s)\bm{Q}^{(n)}(s)-\bm{Q}^{(n)}(s)\left(\begin{matrix}\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\bm{I}_{N}+\bm{B}&0\\
0&-\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\bm{I}_{N}-\bm{B}^{*}\end{matrix}\right),

so that we get

\displaystyle s\dot{\bm{Q}}^{(n)}(s)=\bm{A}_{-1}^{(n)}(s)\bm{Q}^{(n)}(s)-\bm{Q}^{(n)}(s)\left(\begin{matrix}\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\bm{I}_{N}+\bm{B}&0\\
0&-\dfrac{\alpha}{2}\bm{I}_{N}-\bm{B}^{*}\end{matrix}\right).

The equation (
4.2
) is just the
(1,1)
entry of this last identity.
∎

5
Additional relations for special situations

The differential relations obtained from the transformation (
2.14
) of the Riemann–Hilbert problem are not unique in the sense that we could have always introduced an unitary matrix-valued function
\bm{S}(z)
inside the factorization of the weight matrix
\bm{W}(s;z)
of the form

\displaystyle\bm{W}(s;z):=z^{\alpha}{\rm e}^{-z-s/z}\bm{T}(z)\bm{S}(z)\bm{S}^{*}(z)\bm{T}^{*}(z).

Although the weight matrix is the same and the solution of the Riemann–Hilbert problem is unique, if we perform the transformation (
2.13
) with
\bm{T}\bm{S}
instead of
\bm{T}
then we should expect a new Lax pair with additional information. This was already pointed out in
[
15
, Section 3]
, where the authors applied this approach to simplify considerably the differential relations for some Hermite-type matrix-valued examples. The situation in the scalar case is pointless and it is not possible to get new relations from this approach (see
[
15
, Proposition 3.9]
). Nevertheless in order to produce new interesting relations, we have to take an specific choice of the matrix
\bm{B}
.

Let us call
\bm{\chi}(z):=(\partial_{z}\bm{S}(z))\bm{S}^{*}(z)
the log-derivative of
\bm{S}
. Then the log-derivative of
\bm{T}\bm{S}
is given by

\displaystyle\big(\partial_{z}(\bm{T}(z)\bm{S}(z))\big)\bm{S}^{*}(z)\bm{T}^{-1}(z)=\frac{\bm{B}}{z}+z^{\bm{B}}\bm{\chi}(z)z^{-\bm{B}}.

Denoting
\bm{H}(z):=z^{\bm{B}}\bm{\chi}(z)z^{-\bm{B}}
, if we manage to find a matrix-valued unitary function
\bm{S}
such that
z\bm{H}(z)
is a matrix polynomial, then it will be possible to compute explicitly the new coefficient
\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}(s;z)
in (
3.1
), and we can get new compatibility conditions. In this case we have

\displaystyle\bm{H}(z)=\bm{\chi}(z)+\operatorname{ad}_{\bm{B}}(\bm{\chi}(z))\log z+\operatorname{ad}^{2}_{\bm{B}}(\bm{\chi}(z))\frac{\log^{2}z}{2}+\cdots=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{ad}^{k}_{\bm{B}}(\bm{\chi}(z))\frac{\log^{k}z}{k!},

where
\operatorname{ad}_{\bm{X}}(\bm{Y})
is the commutator
\big[\bm{X},\bm{Y}\big]
. Here we define recursively
\operatorname{ad}^{n+1}_{\bm{X}}(\bm{Y})\!=\!\operatorname{ad}_{\bm{X}}(\operatorname{ad}^{n}_{\bm{X}}(\bm{Y}))
for
n\geq 1
with
\operatorname{ad}^{0}_{\bm{X}}(\bm{Y})=\bm{Y}
. One possible choice was given in
[
10
, Section 6.2]
. Consider
\bm{B}
and
\bm{B}_{0}
satisfying
[\bm{B},\bm{B}_{0}]=\bm{B}_{0}
and that
\bm{B}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}-\bm{B}_{0}
is Hermitian. Then
\bm{B}
and
\bm{B}_{0}
have a special structure. In this case, it was proven in
[
10
]
that
\bm{B}=\bm{Z}\bm{J}\bm{Z}^{-1}
and
\bm{B}_{0}=\bm{Z}\bm{L}\bm{Z}^{-1}
, where
\bm{L}
and
\bm{J}
are the nilpotent and diagonal matrices given by

\displaystyle\bm{L}=\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}\nu_{k}\bm{E}_{k,k+1},\qquad\nu_{k}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\},\qquad\bm{J}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}(N-k)\bm{E}_{k,k},

and
\bm{Z}
is the transformation matrix given by

\displaystyle\bm{Z}=(z_{ij})_{i,j=1,\ldots N},\qquad z_{ij}=\begin{cases}0,&\hbox{if $i>j$;}\\
1,&\hbox{if $i=j$;}\\
\displaystyle\prod_{l=1}^{j-i}\frac{\nu_{i+l-1}}{c_{i+l}-c_{i}},&\hbox{if $i<j$,}\end{cases}

where
c_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,N,
are the diagonal entries of
\bm{J}^{2}+\alpha\bm{J}
. Here
\bm{E}_{ij}
is a matrix with 1 at entry
(i,j)
and 0 elsewhere. Observe now that
\bm{B}

is not any matrix
and it only depends on
N-1
free parameters. For instance, for
N=2
we have only one free parameter and

\displaystyle\bm{B}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&-\dfrac{\nu_{1}}{\alpha+1}\\
0&0\end{matrix}\right),\qquad\bm{B}_{0}=\left(\begin{matrix}0&\nu_{1}\\
0&0\end{matrix}\right).

The weight matrix
\bm{W}(s;x)
is given in this case by

\displaystyle\bm{W}(s;x)=x^{\alpha}{\rm e}^{-x-s/x}\left(\begin{matrix}x^{2}+\dfrac{\nu^{2}(x-1)^{2}}{(\alpha+1)^{2}}&-\dfrac{\nu(x-1)}{\alpha+1}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
-\dfrac{\nu(x-1)}{\alpha+1}&1\end{matrix}\right),\qquad x\in[0,\infty),\qquad\alpha,s>0.

Let
\bm{S}(z)=z^{i(\bm{B}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}-\bm{B}_{0})}
. Observe that, since
\bm{B}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}-\bm{B}_{0}
is Hermitian we have that
\bm{S}(z)
is a unitary matrix-valued function. Therefore, the matrix-valued function
\bm{\chi}(z)
is then given by

\displaystyle\bm{\chi}(z)=i\left(\frac{\bm{B}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}-\bm{B}_{0}}{z}\right).

A straightforward computation using that
[\bm{B},\bm{B}_{0}]=\bm{B}_{0}
gives

\displaystyle\bm{H}(z)=z^{\bm{B}}\bm{\chi}(z)z^{-\bm{B}}=i\left(\frac{\bm{B}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}}{z}-\bm{B}_{0}\right).

Therefore, the first equation in the Lax triple (
2.15
) can be written as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\bm{\Psi}^{(n)}(s;z)=\big(\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}(s;z)+\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}_{\bm{H}}(s;z)\big)\bm{\Psi}^{(n)}(s;z),

where
\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}(s;z)
is defined in (
3.1
) and

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}_{\bm{H}}(s;z)=\bm{H}_{0}^{(n)}+\frac{\bm{H}_{-1}^{(n)}(s)}{z},

where

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{0}^{(n)}=-i\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{B}_{0}&0\\
0&\bm{B}_{0}^{*}\end{matrix}\right)

and

\displaystyle\bm{H}_{-1}^{(n)}(s)=i\left(\begin{matrix}\bm{B}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}+[\bm{B}_{0},\bm{a}_{n,n-1}(s)]&\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\bm{\gamma}_{n}^{-1}(\bm{B}_{0}^{*}-\bm{L}_{n}(s))\vskip 2.84526pt\\
-2\pi i(\bm{B}_{0}^{*}-\bm{L}_{n-1}(s))\bm{\gamma}_{n-1}&\big(\bm{B}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}+[\bm{B}_{0},\bm{a}_{n,n-1}(s)]\big)^{*}\end{matrix}\right).

Here we are using the notation

\displaystyle\bm{L}_{n}(s)=\bm{\gamma}_{n}(s)\bm{B}_{0}\bm{\gamma}_{n}^{-1}(s).

From the first compatibility condition (
3.2
) we get a new relation

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}_{\bm{H}}(s;z)+\big[\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}_{\bm{H}}(s;z),\bm{\mathcal{B}}^{(n)}(s;z)\big]=0,

while form the second compatibility condition (
3.3
) we get

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{(n)}(s;z)\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{(n)}_{\bm{H}}(s;z)=\bm{\mathcal{A}}^{(n+1)}_{\bm{H}}(s;z)\bm{\mathcal{U}}^{(n)}(s;z).

The first one gives two new relations

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{n}\bm{\beta}_{n}(\bm{B}_{0}-\bm{L}_{n-1}^{*})+(\bm{B}_{0}-\bm{L}_{n}^{*})\bm{a}_{n}^{-1}\bm{b}_{n}(s\bm{I}_{N}-\bm{b}_{n})=\big[\bm{B}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}+\big[\bm{B}_{0},\bm{a}_{n,n-1}\big],\bm{b}_{n}\big],

and

\displaystyle(s\bm{I}_{N}-\bm{b}_{n})(\bm{B}_{0}-\bm{L}_{n}^{*})-(\bm{B}_{0}-\bm{L}_{n}^{*})\bm{a}_{n}^{-1}\bm{b}_{n}\bm{a}_{n}=\big(\bm{B}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}+[\bm{B}_{0},\bm{a}_{n,n-1}]\big)\bm{a}_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad{}-\bm{a}_{n}\big(\bm{B}_{n}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}_{n}+\big[\bm{L}_{n},\bm{\gamma}_{n}\bm{a}_{n,n-1}\bm{\gamma}_{n}^{-1}\big]\big)^{*}.

The second one gives another two new relations

\displaystyle(\bm{B}_{0}-\bm{L}_{n+1}^{*})\bm{\beta}_{n+1}-\bm{\beta}_{n}(\bm{B}_{0}-\bm{L}_{n-1}^{*})=\big[\bm{B}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}+[\bm{B}_{0},\bm{a}_{n,n-1}],\bm{\alpha}_{n}\big]+[\bm{\alpha}_{n},\bm{B}_{0}\bm{\alpha}_{n}],

and

\displaystyle\bm{B}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}+[\bm{B}_{0},\bm{a}_{n,n-1}]-\big(\bm{B}_{n}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}_{n}+\big[\bm{L}_{n},\bm{\gamma}_{n}\bm{a}_{n,n-1}\bm{\gamma}_{n}^{-1}\big]\big)^{*}=\bm{B}_{0}\bm{\alpha}_{n}-\bm{\alpha}_{n}\bm{L}_{n}^{*}.

Also from Proposition
3.4
we can get two new relations

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{n}\bm{\beta}_{n}(\bm{B}_{0}-\bm{L}_{n-1}^{*})-(s\bm{I}_{N}-\bm{b}_{n})(\bm{B}_{0}-\bm{L}_{n}^{*})\bm{a}_{n}^{-1}\bm{b}_{n}+s\big(\hat{\bm{B}}_{n}^{2}+\alpha\hat{\bm{B}}_{n}\big)

\displaystyle=(s\bm{I}_{N}-\bm{b}_{n})\big(\bm{B}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}+[\bm{B}_{0},\bm{a}_{n,n-1}]\big)+\bm{a}_{n}\big(\bm{B}_{n}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}_{n}+\big[\bm{L}_{n},\bm{\gamma}_{n}\bm{a}_{n,n-1}\bm{\gamma}_{n}^{-1}\big]\big)^{*}\bm{a}_{n}^{-1}\bm{b}_{n},

and

\displaystyle\bm{a}_{n}^{-1}\bm{b}_{n}\big(\bm{B}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}+[\bm{B}_{0},\bm{a}_{n,n-1}]\big)-\big(\bm{B}_{n}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}_{n}+\big[\bm{L}_{n},\bm{\gamma}_{n}\bm{a}_{n,n-1}\bm{\gamma}_{n}^{-1}\big]\big)^{*}\bm{a}_{n}^{-1}\bm{b}_{n}

\displaystyle\qquad{}+\bm{\beta}_{n}(\bm{B}_{0}-\bm{L}_{n-1}^{*})+\bm{a}_{n}^{-1}\bm{b}_{n}(\bm{B}_{0}-\bm{L}_{n}^{*})\bm{a}_{n}^{-1}\bm{b}_{n}=0.

Combining these last two ones we get the reduction

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{B}}_{n}^{2}+\alpha\hat{\bm{B}}_{n}=\bm{B}^{2}+\alpha\bm{B}+[\bm{B}_{0},\bm{a}_{n,n-1}]+(\bm{B}_{0}-\bm{L}_{n}^{*})\bm{a}_{n}^{-1}\bm{b}_{n}.
