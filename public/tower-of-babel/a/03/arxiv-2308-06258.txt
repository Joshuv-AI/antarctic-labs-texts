Conforming Finite Element Function Spaces in Four Dimensions, Part II: The Pentatope and Tetrahedral Prism Journal: Computers & Mathematics with Applications
David M. Williams, Nilima Nigam

Abstract
In this paper, we present explicit expressions for conforming finite element function spaces, basis functions, and degrees of freedom on the pentatope and tetrahedral prism elements. More generally, our objective is to construct finite element function spaces that maintain conformity with infinite-dimensional spaces of a carefully chosen de Rham complex. This paper is a natural extension of the companion paper entitled “Conforming Finite Element Function Spaces in Four Dimensions, Part I: Foundational Principles and the Tesseract" by Nigam and Williams, (2023). In contrast to Part I, in this paper we focus on two of the most popular elements which do not possess a full tensor-product structure in all four coordinate directions. We note that these elements appear frequently in existing space-time finite element methods. In order to build our finite element spaces, we utilize powerful techniques from the recently developed ‘Finite Element Exterior Calculus’. Subsequently, we translate our results into the well-known language of linear algebra (vectors and matrices) in order to facilitate implementation by scientists and engineers.

Introduction

Finite Element Exterior Calculus (FEEC) is a powerful and elegant framework for constructing exact sequences of finite element approximation spaces in arbitrary dimensions, (see for instance, the landmark paper
[
1
]
). There has been considerable literature dedicated to the development and analysis of FEEC on simplicial, tensorial, and prism-like elements. Our goal in this and a companion paper
[
2
]
, is to restrict these results to the specific case of
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, and to present an explicit construction of these families of finite elements. Notable previous contributions in this direction are due to
[

4

[

[
2
]
, we presented several practical examples in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
to motivate our definition of a de Rham complex and the associated traces. We shall only briefly review this material in the present paper.

In this work, we focus on developing conforming finite element function spaces for the
pentatope
and
tetrahedral prism
. The pentatope is a generalization of the triangle to four dimensions, and the tetrahedral prism is a generalization of the triangular prism to four dimensions. In what follows, we review some of the relevant literature on these elements.

Let us now turn our attention to the tetrahedral prism. Meshes of tetrahedral prisms can be generated in a very straightforward fashion, as we only need to extrude an existing tetrahedral mesh in order to generate a completely valid, boundary-conforming mesh of tetrahedral prisms (see above). To the authors’ knowledge, no one has explicitly constructed high-order finite element spaces which are the equivalents of H(curl)- or H(div)-conforming spaces on pentatopes or tetrahedral prism elements. Now, it is important to note that there are inherent difficulties associated with constructing these finite element spaces due to the absence of a complete tensor-product structure in all four coordinate directions.

1.2
Overview of the Paper

The remainder of this paper is outlined as follows. In section 2, we introduce some notation and essential ideas. In section 3, we introduce our particular de Rahm complex, and the associated derivative operators, Sobolev spaces, and maps. In sections 4 and 5, we present explicit conforming finite element spaces on the pentatope and tetrahedral prism, respectively. Finally, in section 6, we summarize the contributions of this paper.

2.1
The Reference Pentatope

Consider the following definition of a reference pentatope

\displaystyle\widehat{K}:=\mathfrak{T}^{4}:=\left\{x=\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}\big|-1\leq x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\leq 1,\;x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{4}\leq-2\right\},

with vertices

\displaystyle v_{1}

\displaystyle=[1,-1,-1,-1]^{T},\quad v_{2}=[-1,1,-1,-1]^{T},\quad v_{3}=[-1,-1,1,-1]^{T},

\displaystyle v_{4}

\displaystyle=[-1,-1,-1,1]^{T},\quad v_{5}=[-1,-1,-1,-1]^{T}.

Next, we introduce the definition of an arbitrary pentatope
K
with vertices
v_{1}^{\prime},v_{2}^{\prime},\ldots,v_{5}^{\prime}
.
There exists a bijective mapping between the reference pentatope and the arbitrary pentatope
\phi:\widehat{K}\rightarrow K

\displaystyle\phi\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{5}v_{i}^{\prime}N_{i}\left(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\right),

where

\displaystyle N_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{x_{1}+1}{2},\quad N_{2}=\frac{x_{2}+1}{2},\quad N_{3}=\frac{x_{3}+1}{2},\quad N_{4}=\frac{x_{4}+1}{2},\quad N_{5}=-\frac{x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{4}}{2}-1.

2.4
Degrees of Freedom

Throughout this paper, our construction of finite element triples will strongly depend on the use of well-known dofs on tetrahedral and triangular-prismatic
facets
, and their associated faces, edges, and vertices. For the sake of completeness, we review these dofs in what follows.

2.4.1
Vertex Degrees of Freedom

In accordance with standard principles from differential geometry, vertex degrees of freedom are only well-defined for 0-forms. For the pentatope and tetrahedral prism, we specify the vertex degrees of freedom
\Sigma^{k,0}(v)
as the vertex values of the polynomial 0-form. We note that there are 5 such degrees of freedom for the pentatope
\mathfrak{T}^{4}
and 8 such degrees of freedom for the tetrahedral prism
\mathfrak{N}^{4}
.

2.4.2
Edge Degrees of Freedom

Next, we recall that the edge degrees of freedom are only defined for 0- and 1-forms. One may define
e
as an edge of an element
\widehat{K}
, where
\widehat{K}
=
\mathfrak{T}^{4}
or
\mathfrak{N}^{4}
, and let
u\in V_{k}\Lambda^{0}(\widehat{K})
be a 0-form proxy. Next, one may construct edge degrees of freedom for
u
as follows

M_{e}(u):=\left\{\int_{e}\text{Tr}(u)q,\,\qquad\forall q\in P^{k-2}(e),\qquad\mbox{ for each edge }\,e\,\mbox{ of }\,\widehat{K}\right\}.

(2.2)

For pentatopes
\mathfrak{T}^{4}
there are
10(k-1)
such degrees of freedom, and for tetrahedral prisms
\mathfrak{N}^{4}
there are
16(k-1)
such degrees of freedom.

Consider a 1-form proxy
U\in V_{k}\Lambda^{1}(\widehat{K})
. One may define its edge degrees of freedom as follows

M_{e}(U):=\left\{\int_{e}\mathrm{Tr}(U)\cdot\tau q,\,\qquad\forall q\in P^{k-1}(e),\qquad\mbox{ for each edge }\,e\,\mbox{ of }\,\widehat{K}\right\},

(2.3)

where
\tau
is a unit vector in the direction of
e
.
For
\mathfrak{T}^{4}
, there are
10k
such degrees of freedom, and for
\mathfrak{N}^{4}
, there are
16k
such degrees of freedom.

2.4.3
Face Degrees of Freedom

In addition, we recall that face degrees of freedom are only defined for 0-, 1- and 2-forms. We let
f
denote a single face of an element
\widehat{K}
; in the context of the present paper, this face can be either triangular or quadrilateral.

Triangular faces:

Consider polynomial 0-forms
u\in V_{k}\Lambda^{0}(\widehat{K})
. The associated face degrees of freedom on a triangular face
f=\mathfrak{T}^{2}
are defined as

M_{f}(u):=\left\{\int_{f}\text{Tr}(u)q,\quad\forall q\in P^{k-3}(f)\right\}.

(2.4)

Consider polynomial 1-forms
U\in V_{k}\Lambda^{1}(\widehat{K})
for which the face degrees of freedom are

M_{f}(U):=\left\{\int_{f}(\mathrm{Tr}(U)\times\nu)\cdot(q\times\nu),\quad\forall q\in(P^{k-2}(f))^{2},\quad q\cdot\nu=0\right\},

(2.5)

where
\nu
denotes a unit normal vector to the face
f
. These definitions are slightly different from the standard definitions of edge degrees of freedom for Nedelec-type elements, but can be shown to be the same, (see Remark 5.31 in
[
30
]
).

Finally, consider polynomial 2-forms,
U\in V_{k}\Lambda^{2}(\widehat{K})
for which the face degrees of freedom are

M_{f}(U):=\left\{\int_{f}(\mathrm{Tr}(U)\cdot\nu)q,\quad\forall q\in P^{k-1}(f)\right\}.

(2.6)

Quadrilateral faces:

Consider polynomial 0-forms
u\in V_{k}\Lambda^{0}(\widehat{K})
. The associated face degrees of freedom on a quadrilateral face
f=\mathfrak{H}^{2}
are defined as

M_{f}(u):=\left\{\int_{f}\text{Tr}(u)q,\quad\forall q\in Q^{k-2,k-2}(f)\right\}.

(2.7)

Consider polynomial 1-forms
U\in V_{k}\Lambda^{1}(\widehat{K})
for which the face degrees of freedom are

M_{f}(U):=\left\{\int_{f}(\mathrm{Tr}(U)\times\nu)\cdot q,\quad\forall q\in Q^{k-2,k-1}\times Q^{k-1,k-2}(f)\right\}.

(2.8)

Finally, consider polynomial 2-forms
U\in V_{k}\Lambda^{2}(\widehat{K})
for which the face degrees of freedom are

M_{f}(U):=\left\{\int_{f}(\mathrm{Tr}(U)\cdot\nu)q,\quad\forall q\in Q^{k-1,k-1}(f)\right\}.

(2.9)

2.4.4
Facet Degrees of Freedom

We recall that facet degrees of freedom are only defined for 0-, 1-, 2-, and 3-forms. The elements under consideration in this paper will only have tetrahedral or triangular-prismatic facets.

Tetrahedral facets:

Let
\mathcal{F}=\mathfrak{T}^{3}
denote a tetrahedral facet.
For 0-forms
u\in V_{k}\Lambda^{0}(\widehat{K})
, we can specify facet degrees of freedom as

M_{\mathcal{F}}(u):=\left\{\int_{\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{Tr}(u)q,\qquad q\in P^{k-4}(\mathcal{F})\right\}.

(2.10)

For polynomial 1-forms
U\in V_{k}\Lambda^{1}(\widehat{K})
, we specify the facet degrees of freedom as

M_{\mathcal{F}}(U):=\left\{\int_{\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{Tr}(U)\cdot q,\qquad q\in(P^{k-3}(\mathcal{F}))^{3}\right\}.

(2.11)

For polynomial 2-forms
U\in V_{k}\Lambda^{2}(\widehat{K})
, we specify the facet degrees of freedom as

M_{\mathcal{F}}(U):=\left\{\int_{\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{Tr}(U)\cdot q,\qquad q\in(P^{k-2}(\mathcal{F}))^{3}\right\}.

(2.12)

Lastly, for polynomial 3-forms
U\in V_{k}\Lambda^{3}(\widehat{K})
, we specify the facet degrees of freedom as

M_{\mathcal{F}}(U):=\left\{\int_{\mathcal{F}}\text{Tr}(U)q,\qquad q\in P^{k-1}(\mathcal{F})\right\}.

(2.13)

Triangular-prismatic facets:

Let
\mathcal{F}=\mathfrak{N}^{3}
denote a triangular-prismatic facet.
For 0-forms
u\in V_{k}\Lambda^{0}(\widehat{K})
, we can specify facet degrees of freedom as

M_{\mathcal{F}}(u):=\left\{\int_{\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{Tr}(u)q,\qquad q\in Q^{k-2}(\mathfrak{H}^{1})\times P^{k-3}(\mathfrak{T}^{2})\right\}.

(2.14)

For polynomial 1-forms
U\in V_{k}\Lambda^{1}(\widehat{K})
, we specify the facet degrees of freedom as

\displaystyle M_{\mathcal{F},1}(U)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\int_{\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{Tr}(U)\cdot q,\qquad q\in Q^{k-2}(\mathfrak{H}^{1})\times\left[P^{k-2}(\mathfrak{T}^{2}),P^{k-2}(\mathfrak{T}^{2}),0\right]^{T}\right\},

\displaystyle M_{\mathcal{F},2}(U)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\int_{\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{Tr}(U)\cdot q,\qquad q\in Q^{k-1}(\mathfrak{H}^{1})\times\left[0,0,P^{k-3}(\mathfrak{T}^{2})\right]^{T}\right\},

\displaystyle M_{\mathcal{F}}(U)

\displaystyle:=M_{\mathcal{F},1}(U)\cup M_{\mathcal{F},2}(U).

(2.15)

For polynomial 2-forms
U\in V_{k}\Lambda^{2}(\widehat{K})
, we specify the facet degrees of freedom as

\displaystyle M_{\mathcal{F},1}(U)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\int_{\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{Tr}(U)\cdot q,\qquad q\in Q^{k-1}(\mathfrak{H}^{1})\times\left[P^{k-2}(\mathfrak{T}^{2}),P^{k-2}(\mathfrak{T}^{2}),0\right]^{T}\right\},

\displaystyle M_{\mathcal{F},2}(U)

\displaystyle:=\left\{\int_{\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{Tr}(U)\cdot q,\qquad q\in Q^{k-2}(\mathfrak{H}^{1})\times\left[0,0,P^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{2})\right]^{T}\right\},

\displaystyle M_{\mathcal{F}}(U)

\displaystyle:=M_{\mathcal{F},1}(U)\cup M_{\mathcal{F},2}(U).

(2.16)

Lastly, for polynomial 3-forms
U\in V_{k}\Lambda^{3}(\widehat{K})
, we specify the facet degrees of freedom as

M_{\mathcal{F}}(U):=\left\{\int_{\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{Tr}(U)q,\qquad q\in Q^{k-1}(\mathfrak{H}^{1})\times P^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{2})\right\}.

(2.17)

3
Sobolev Spaces and Associated Mappings

In accordance with the standard FEEC approach, we introduce the
de Rahm
complex for smooth functions in three dimensions

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}C^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\nabla}&C^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{3})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\nabla\times}&C^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{3})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\nabla\cdot}&C^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}).\end{matrix}

Here, we have used the conventional derivative operators for three dimensions: namely,
\nabla
denotes the gradient,
\nabla\times
denotes the curl, and
\nabla\cdot
denotes the divergence.

Next, we can also define the de Rahm complex for smooth functions in four dimensions

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}C^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\mathrm{grad}}&C^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\mathrm{skwGrad}}&C^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{K})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\mathrm{curl}}&C^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\mathrm{div}}&C^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}).\end{matrix}

Here, we have introduced new first-derivative operators for functions in
\mathbb{R}^{4}
. We will provide precise definitions for these operators in what follows.

In four dimensions, ‘
grad
’ is the standard gradient operator which can be applied to a scalar,
u\in L^{2}\left(\Omega,\mathbb{R}\right)
, such that
\left[\text{grad}u\right]_{i}=\partial_{i}u
for
i=1,2,3,4
. In addition, ‘
skwGrad
’ is an antisymmetric gradient operator which can be applied to a 4-vector,
E\in L^{2}\left(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right)
, as follows

\displaystyle\left[\text{skwGrad}\,E\right]=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left[\text{Grad}\,E\right]^{T}-\left[\text{Grad}\,E\right]\right),

where
[\mathrm{Grad}E]_{ij}=\partial_{j}E_{i}
for
i=1,2,3,4
and
j=1,2,3,4
.
Next, ‘
curl
’ is a derivative operator which can be applied to a
4\times 4
skew-symmetric matrix,
F\in L^{2}\left(\Omega,\mathbb{K}\right)
as follows

\displaystyle\left[\text{curl}\,F\right]_{i}=\sum_{k,l=1}^{4}\varepsilon_{ijkl}\partial_{j}F_{kl},

where
\varepsilon_{ijkl}
is the Levi-Civita tensor. Lastly, ‘
div
’ is the standard divergence operator which acts on a 4-vector,
G\in L^{2}\left(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right)
, such that
\left[\text{div}\,G\right]=\partial_{i}G_{i}
for
i=1,2,3,4
.

For the sake of completeness, we can also define the ‘Curl’ and ‘Div’ operators, which are isomorphic to the ‘skwGrad’ and ‘curl’ operators, respectively. In particular, ‘
Curl
’ is a derivative operator which can be applied to a 4-vector,
E\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4})
, as follows

\displaystyle\left[\text{Curl}\,E\right]_{ij}=\sum_{k,l=1}^{4}\varepsilon_{ijkl}\partial_{k}E_{l},

and ‘
Div
’ is a derivative operator which can be applied to a
4\times 4
skew-symmetric matrix,
F\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{K})
, as follows

\displaystyle\left[\text{Div}\,F\right]_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{4}\partial_{j}F_{ij}.

It turns out that the first-derivative operators (above) satisfy the following relations

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{1}\left(d^{\left(0\right)}\omega\right)

\displaystyle=\text{grad}\left(\Upsilon_{0}\omega\right),\qquad\qquad\qquad\;\omega\in\Lambda^{0}(\Omega):=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,\Lambda^{0}),

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{2}\left(d^{\left(1\right)}\omega\right)

\displaystyle=\text{skwGrad}\left(\Upsilon_{1}\omega\right),\qquad\qquad\omega\in\Lambda^{1}(\Omega):=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,\Lambda^{1}),

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{3}\left(d^{\left(2\right)}\omega\right)

\displaystyle=\text{curl}\left(\Upsilon_{2}\omega\right),\qquad\qquad\qquad\;\omega\in\Lambda^{2}(\Omega):=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,\Lambda^{2}),

\displaystyle\Upsilon_{2}\left(d^{\left(3\right)}\omega\right)

\displaystyle=\text{div}\left(\Upsilon_{3}\omega\right),\qquad\qquad\qquad\;\,\omega\in\Lambda^{3}(\Omega):=\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,\Lambda^{3}).

In accordance with these relations, the following diagram commutes

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,\Lambda^{0})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{d^{\left(0\right)}}&\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,\Lambda^{1})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{d^{\left(1\right)}}&\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,\Lambda^{2})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{d^{\left(2\right)}}&\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,\Lambda^{3})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{d^{\left(3\right)}}&\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,\Lambda^{4})\cr\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\Upsilon_{0}$}\left\downarrow\vrule height=18.0002pt,width=0.0pt,depth=11.99983pt\right.{\vrule height=30.00035pt,width=0.0pt,depth=19.99971pt}&&\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\Upsilon_{1}$}\left\downarrow\vrule height=18.0002pt,width=0.0pt,depth=11.99983pt\right.{\vrule height=30.00035pt,width=0.0pt,depth=19.99971pt}&&\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\Upsilon_{2}$}\left\downarrow\vrule height=18.0002pt,width=0.0pt,depth=11.99983pt\right.{\vrule height=30.00035pt,width=0.0pt,depth=19.99971pt}&&\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\Upsilon_{3}$}\left\downarrow\vrule height=18.0002pt,width=0.0pt,depth=11.99983pt\right.{\vrule height=30.00035pt,width=0.0pt,depth=19.99971pt}&&\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\Upsilon_{4}$}\left\downarrow\vrule height=18.0002pt,width=0.0pt,depth=11.99983pt\right.{\vrule height=30.00035pt,width=0.0pt,depth=19.99971pt}\cr\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\text{grad}}&\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\text{skwGrad}}&\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,\mathbb{K})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\text{curl}}&\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\text{div}}&\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})\cr\end{matrix}

In addition, the first-derivative operators can be used to construct the following Sobolev spaces

\displaystyle H\left(\text{grad},\Omega,\mathbb{R}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{u\in L^{2}\left(\Omega,\mathbb{R}\right):\text{grad}\,u\in L^{2}\left(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right)\right\},

\displaystyle H\left(\text{skwGrad},\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{E\in L^{2}\left(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right):\text{skwGrad}\,E\in L^{2}\left(\Omega,\mathbb{K}\right)\right\},

\displaystyle H\left(\text{curl},\Omega,\mathbb{K}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{F\in L^{2}\left(\Omega,\mathbb{K}\right):\text{curl}\,F\in L^{2}\left(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right)\right\},

\displaystyle H\left(\text{div},\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{G\in L^{2}\left(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right):\text{div}\,G\in L^{2}\left(\Omega,\mathbb{R}\right)\right\},

and

\displaystyle H\left(\text{Curl},\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{E\in L^{2}\left(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right):\text{Curl}\,E\in L^{2}\left(\Omega,\mathbb{K}\right)\right\},

\displaystyle H\left(\text{Div},\Omega,\mathbb{K}\right)

\displaystyle=\left\{F\in L^{2}\left(\Omega,\mathbb{K}\right):\text{Div}\,F\in L^{2}\left(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right)\right\}.

In accordance with these definitions, we can introduce the L2 de Rahm complex in four dimensions

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}H(\mathrm{grad},\Omega,\mathbb{R})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\mathrm{grad}}&H(\mathrm{skwGrad},\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\mathrm{skwGrad}}&H(\mathrm{curl},\Omega,\mathbb{K})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\mathrm{curl}}&H(\mathrm{div},\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4})&\mathop{\hbox to30.00005pt{\rightarrowfill}}\limits^{\mathrm{div}}&L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}).\end{matrix}

Next, it is important for us to characterize the behavior of our function spaces on the boundary of the domain,
\partial\Omega
. With this in mind, we can introduce the following trace identity for 1-forms

\displaystyle\left(\text{tr}^{(1)}E\right)(F)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(n\times E\right):F\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\text{Curl}\,E\right):F\,dx-\int_{\Omega}\left(\text{curl}\,F\right)\cdot E\,dx,

(3.1)

where
E\in H\left(\text{Curl},\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right)
and
F\in H\left(\text{curl},\Omega,\mathbb{K}\right)
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\left(\text{tr}^{(1)}E\right)(F)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left[E\otimes n-n\otimes E\right]:F\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\text{Div}\,F\right)\cdot E\,dx-\int_{\Omega}F:\left(\text{skwGrad}\,E\right)\,dx,

(3.2)

where
E\in H\left(\text{skwGrad},\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right)
and
F\in H\left(\text{Div},\Omega,\mathbb{K}\right)
. Next, the following trace identity holds for 2-forms

\displaystyle\left(\text{tr}^{(2)}F\right)(E)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(n\times F\right)\cdot E\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\text{curl}\,F\right)\cdot E\,dx-\int_{\Omega}\left(\text{Curl}\,E\right):F\,dx,

(3.3)

where
E\in H\left(\text{Curl},\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right)
and
F\in H\left(\text{curl},\Omega,\mathbb{K}\right)
.
Finally, the following trace identity holds for 3-forms

\displaystyle\left(\text{tr}^{(3)}G\right)(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}\left(G\cdot n\right)u\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(\text{div}\,G\right)u\,dx+\int_{\Omega}G\cdot\left(\text{grad}\,u\right)dx,

(3.4)

where
G\in H(\text{div},\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4})
and
u\in H(\text{grad},\Omega,\mathbb{R})
.

There are two cross-product operators which are defined in the trace identities above. In particular, the cross-product operator between a pair of 4-vectors is given by

\displaystyle\left[M\times N\right]_{ij}=\sum_{k,l=1}^{4}\varepsilon_{ijkl}M_{k}N_{l},

where
M\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
and
N\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
. Furthermore, the cross-product operator between a 4-vector and a
4\times 4
skew-symmetric matrix is given by

\displaystyle\left[M\times U\right]_{i}=\sum_{k,l=1}^{4}\varepsilon_{ijkl}M_{j}U_{kl},

where
M\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
and
U\in\mathbb{K}
.

In accordance with the equations above, the traces for 0-forms, 1-forms, 2-forms, and 3-forms can be defined as follows

\displaystyle\text{0-forms}\qquad u

\displaystyle=\Upsilon_{0}\omega,\qquad\text{tr}(u)=u|_{\partial\Omega},

\displaystyle\text{1-forms}\qquad E

\displaystyle=\Upsilon_{1}\omega,\qquad\text{tr}(E)=\frac{1}{2}\left(E\otimes n-n\otimes E\right)|_{\partial\Omega},

\displaystyle\text{2-forms}\qquad F

\displaystyle=\Upsilon_{2}\omega,\qquad\text{tr}(F)=\left(n\times F\right)|_{\partial\Omega},

\displaystyle\text{3-forms}\qquad G

\displaystyle=\Upsilon_{3}\omega,\qquad\text{tr}(G)=\left(G\cdot n\right)|_{\partial\Omega},

where

\displaystyle u\in H\left(\text{grad},\Omega,\mathbb{R}\right),\qquad\qquad\;\;\,\text{tr}(u)\in H^{1/2}\left(\partial\Omega,\mathbb{R}\right),

\displaystyle E\in H\left(\text{skwGrad},\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right),\qquad\text{tr}(E)\in H^{-1/2}\left(\partial\Omega,\mathbb{K}\right),

\displaystyle F\in H\left(\text{curl},\Omega,\mathbb{K}\right),\qquad\qquad\;\;\,\text{tr}(F)\in H^{-1/2}\left(\partial\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right),

\displaystyle G\in H\left(\text{div},\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right),\qquad\qquad\;\,\text{tr}(G)\in H^{-1/2}\left(\partial\Omega,\mathbb{R}\right).

We note that the traces of 4-forms are not well-defined.

s

4.1
Degrees of Freedom on the Reference Pentatope,
\mathfrak{T}^{4}

We now return our attention to the sequence of spaces in Eqs. (
4.1a
)–(
4.1e
). Our objective is to construct degrees of freedom for these spaces. There are already well-known sets of degrees of freedom for simplicial elements using wedge products, as in
[
31
]
, etc. Unfortunately, while the wedge product is mathematically elegant, it is frequently difficult for engineers and programmers to interpret and use for implementation purposes. In order to address this issue, in this section we provide an alternative, more explicit construction of the degrees of freedom. In addition, these degrees of freedom are shown to be unisolvent.

To specify the degrees of freedom for an
s
-form on the pentatope, we will make use of the preceding discussions; in particular, several degrees of freedom can be specified by using well-known trace degrees of freedom on
\mathfrak{T}^{3}
(tetrahedra),
\mathfrak{T}^{2}
(triangles),
\mathfrak{T}^{1}
(edges), and
\mathfrak{T}^{0}
(vertices). Recall from
Table 1
that
\mathfrak{T}^{4}
has 5 vertices, 10 edges, 10 triangular faces, and 5 tetrahedral facets. Our task is reduced to specifying the remaining interior degrees of freedom, and ensuring unisolvency.

4.1.1

\mathfrak{T}^{4}

The polynomial 0-forms on
\mathfrak{T}^{4}
are denoted by
V_{k}\Lambda^{0}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}):=P^{k}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})
. This space has dimension

\displaystyle\text{dim}(V_{k}\Lambda^{0}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}))={k+4\choose 4}=\frac{1}{24}(k+1)(k+2)(k+3)(k+4).

The dual space
\Sigma^{k,0}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})

We can decompose
\Sigma^{k,0}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})
into trace and volume degrees of freedom. For the trace degrees of freedom,
\Sigma_{trace}^{k,0}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})

2.2
), (
2.4
), and (
2.10
).
The total number of trace degrees of freedom is, therefore

\displaystyle\text{dim}\left(\Sigma_{trace}^{k,0}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right)

\displaystyle=5+10\,\text{dim}(P^{k-2}(\mathfrak{T}^{1}))+10\,\text{dim}(P^{k-3}(\mathfrak{T}^{2}))+5\,\text{dim}(P^{k-4}(\mathfrak{T}^{3}))

\displaystyle=5+10{k-1\choose k-2}+10{k-1\choose k-3}+5{k-1\choose k-4}

\displaystyle=\frac{5}{6}k(k^{2}+5).

We can also specify volume degrees of freedom on
\mathfrak{T}^{4}
for the 0-form proxy
u
as follows

\Sigma^{k,0}_{vol}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}):=\left\{u\rightarrow\int_{\mathfrak{T}^{4}}uq,\qquad q\in P^{k-5}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right\}.

(4.7)

It immediately follows that

\displaystyle\text{dim}\left(\Sigma^{k,0}_{vol}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right)={k-1\choose 4}=\frac{1}{24}(k-4)(k-3)(k-2)(k-1).

.

We begin by noting that

\displaystyle\text{dim}(V_{k}\Lambda^{0}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}))=\text{dim}(\Sigma^{k,0}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}))=\text{dim}(\Sigma_{trace}^{k,0}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}))+\text{dim}(\Sigma^{k,0}_{vol}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})).

E
vanish, then in particular those associated with the traces vanish.
Moreover, the trace of
E
on to any facet
\mathcal{F}
is a
1
-form on this tetrahedron, and
\mathrm{Tr}(E)
vanishes on
\mathcal{F}
. We now integrate by parts on
\mathcal{F}
to see that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{F}}q\cdot\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{skwGrad}(E))\,dx=\int_{\mathcal{F}}q\cdot\nabla\times(\mathrm{Tr}(E))\,dx=\int_{\mathcal{F}}\left(\nabla\times q\right)\cdot\mathrm{Tr}(E)\,dx=0.

This equation holds for any sufficiently smooth
q
, and in particular for
q\in(P^{k-1}(\mathcal{F}))^{3}
. Choosing
q=\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{skwGrad}(E))
on
\mathcal{F}
shows that
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{skwGrad}(E))=0
on
\mathcal{F}
.
∎

The next theorem uses the previous lemmas to establish unisolvency.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
E\in V_{k}\Lambda^{1}(K)
be a polynomial 1-form for which all the degrees of freedom
\Sigma^{k,1}(K)
vanish. Then
E\equiv 0
.

Proof.

In accordance with Eq. (
3.2
)

\displaystyle\left(\text{tr}^{(1)}E\right)(F)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial K}\left[E\otimes n-n\otimes E\right]:F\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{K}\left(\text{Div}\,F\right)\cdot E\,dx-\int_{K}F:\left(\text{skwGrad}\,E\right)\,dx,

(4.11)

for
F\in H(\mathrm{Div},K,\mathbb{K})
. We note that if
F\in\mathcal{L}\left((P^{k-3}(K))^{6}\right)
then it is automatically in
H(\mathrm{Div},K,\mathbb{K})
.
Since
\mathrm{tr}(E)=0
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{K}\left(\text{Div}\,F\right)\cdot E\,dx=\int_{K}F:\left(\text{skwGrad}\,E\right)\,dx,

for each
F\in\mathcal{L}\left((P^{k-3}(K))^{6}\right)
. Since the volumetric degrees of freedom vanish, we can set
q=\mathrm{Div}\,F
in Eq. (
4.9
), and obtain the following

\int_{K}F:\left(\text{skwGrad}\,E\right)\,dx=0\qquad\forall F\in\mathcal{L}\left((P^{k-3}(K))^{6}\right).

(4.12)

Now, let
\mathcal{F}
be a tetrahedral facet of the element
K
. Using the previous lemma establishes that
\mathrm{skwGrad}(E)
has vanishing traces on
\mathcal{F}
. Let us denote
B:=\mathrm{skwGrad}(E)=\mathcal{L}\left(\begin{bmatrix}B_{12},B_{13},B_{14},B_{23},B_{24},B_{34}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\right).
Consider the trace on to the facet on the hyperplane
x_{4}=0
, (see Eq. (
3.7
)). Since

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(B)=2\begin{bmatrix}B_{23}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},0)\\[4.30554pt]
-B_{13}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},0)\\[4.30554pt]
B_{12}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},0)\\[4.30554pt]
0\end{bmatrix}=0,

it follows that
B_{23}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},0)=B_{13}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},0)=B_{12}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},0)=0.

Similarly, the trace of
B
on to the plane
x_{3}=0
vanishes, from which we see
B_{12}(x_{1},x_{2},0,x_{4})=B_{14}(x_{1},x_{2},0,x_{4})=B_{24}(x_{1},x_{2},0,x_{4})=0
. Consequently,
B_{12}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})=x_{3}x_{4}r_{12}
for some
r_{12}\in P^{k-3}(K)
. Similar considerations on all the other facets imply that

\displaystyle\mathrm{skwGrad}(E)=B=\mathcal{L}\left(\begin{bmatrix}x_{3}x_{4}r_{12}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{2}x_{4}r_{13}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{2}x_{3}r_{14}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{1}x_{4}r_{23}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{1}x_{3}r_{24}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{1}x_{2}r_{34}\end{bmatrix}\right),\qquad r_{ij}\in P^{k-3}(K),\qquad\text{for}\quad i=1,2,3,4,\quad j=1,2,3,4.

But then choosing
F=\mathcal{L}\left(\begin{bmatrix}r_{12},r_{13},r_{14},r_{23},r_{24},r_{34}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\right)
in Eq. (
4.12
), we get that

\displaystyle 0=\int_{K}F:\left(\text{skwGrad}\,E\right)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{K}\left(x_{3}x_{4}r_{12}^{2}+x_{2}x_{4}r_{13}^{2}+x_{2}x_{3}r_{14}^{2}+x_{1}x_{4}r_{23}^{2}+x_{1}x_{3}r_{24}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}r_{34}^{2}\right)dx,

from which it follows that
r_{ij}=0
, (as the products of the form
x_{3}x_{4}
,
x_{2}x_{4}
, etc. are strictly non-negative on
K
). From this we conclude that
B=\mathrm{skwGrad}(E)=0
in
K
, and consequently from Lemma
4.5
,
E=\mathrm{grad}(p)
for some
p\in P^{k}(K)
. Since the traces of
E
vanish, we can choose
p=0
on the facets, faces, edges, and vertices of
K
, which allows us to write

\displaystyle p=x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}\hat{r},\quad\hat{r}\in P^{k-4}(K).

But since the volumetric degrees of freedom of
E
vanish, we can pick

\displaystyle q=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\partial_{1}(\hat{r})+\hat{r}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{2}\partial_{2}(\hat{r})+\hat{r}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{3}\partial_{3}(\hat{r})+\hat{r}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{4}\partial_{4}(\hat{r})+\hat{r}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\hat{r}_{x_{1}}+\hat{r}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{2}\hat{r}_{x_{2}}+\hat{r}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{3}\hat{r}_{x_{3}}+\hat{r}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{4}\hat{r}_{x_{4}}+\hat{r}\end{bmatrix},

in Eq. (
4.9
), in order to obtain

\displaystyle 0=\int_{K}E\cdot q\,dx=\int_{K}\mathrm{grad}(p)\cdot q\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{K}\left(x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}(x_{1}\hat{r}_{x_{1}}+\hat{r})^{2}+x_{1}x_{3}x_{4}(x_{2}\hat{r}_{x_{2}}+\hat{r})^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}x_{4}(x_{3}\hat{r}_{x_{3}}+\hat{r})^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}(x_{4}\hat{r}_{x_{4}}+\hat{r})^{2}\right)dx.

All the coordinate functions of
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}
are non-negative in
K
. Therefore, the integral above only vanishes if
(x_{1}\hat{r}_{x_{1}}+\hat{r})=0
,
(x_{2}\hat{r}_{x_{2}}+\hat{r})=0
, etc.. This in turn is impossible unless
\hat{r}=0
. But then
p
, and consequently
E=\mathrm{grad}(p)
vanishes.
∎

4.1.3
Dofs for 2-forms on
\mathfrak{T}^{4}

The polynomial 2-forms on
\mathfrak{T}^{4}
are associated with skew-symmetric matrices

\displaystyle V_{k}\Lambda^{2}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\left((P^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}))^{6}\right)\oplus

\displaystyle\{\tilde{P}^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})B_{1}+\tilde{P}^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})B_{2}+\tilde{P}^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})B_{3}+\tilde{P}^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})B_{4}\}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}\left((P^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}))^{6}\right)\oplus\left\{B\in\mathcal{L}\left((\tilde{P}^{k}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}))^{6}\right)|Bx=0\right\}.

The dimension of the second space above is the same as that of
(\tilde{P}^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}))^{3}+\tilde{P}^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{3})
. Please consult
A
for proof of this fact. Altogether, the dimension of the entire space is

\displaystyle\text{dim}\left(V_{k}\Lambda^{2}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right)

\displaystyle=6{k+3\choose k-1}+3{k+2\choose 3}+{k+1\choose 2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}k\left(k^{3}+8k^{2}+19k+12\right).

Face and facet traces are well-defined for polynomial 2-forms on
\mathfrak{T}^{4}
. Therefore, we specify the degrees of freedom corresponding to
V_{k}\Lambda^{2}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})
as

\displaystyle\Sigma^{k,2}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})=\Sigma^{k,2}_{vol}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\cup\Sigma_{trace}^{k,2}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}),

where
\Sigma_{trace}^{k,2}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})
are the trace degrees of freedom corresponding to the 10 triangular faces and 5 tetrahedral facets, as given by Eqs. (
2.6
) and (
2.12
). The dimension of this space is

\displaystyle\text{dim}\left(\Sigma_{trace}^{k,2}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right)

\displaystyle=10\,\text{dim}(P^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{2}))+5\,\text{dim}\left((P^{k-2}(\mathfrak{T}^{3}))^{3}\right)

\displaystyle=10{k+1\choose 2}+15{k+1\choose 3}

\displaystyle=\frac{5}{2}k(k^{2}+2k+1).

We can also specify volume degrees of freedom on
\mathfrak{T}^{4}
for a 2-form proxy
F
as

\Sigma^{k,2}_{vol}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}):=\left\{\int_{\mathfrak{T}^{4}}F:q,\qquad q\in\mathcal{L}\left((P^{k-3}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}))^{6}\right)\right\}.

(4.13)

The dimension of this space is

\displaystyle\text{dim}\left(\Sigma^{k,2}_{vol}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right)=6{k+1\choose 4}=\frac{1}{4}(k-2)(k-1)k(k+1).

It can easily be confirmed that

\displaystyle\text{dim}\left(V_{k}\Lambda^{2}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right)=\text{dim}\left(\Sigma^{k,2}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right)=\text{dim}\left(\Sigma_{trace}^{k,2}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right)+\text{dim}\left(\Sigma^{k,2}_{vol}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right).

Once again, unisolvency of the finite element will follow if we can establish that the vanishing of all dofs for an arbitrary
u\in V_{k}\Lambda^{2}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})
implies that
u\equiv 0.

Following our analysis of the 1-forms, it is more convenient to work on the mapped element
K
whose vertices are given in Eq. (
4.10
). In addition, we follow a similar strategy as before in order to establish unisolvency. First, we show that if
F\in V_{k}\Lambda^{2}(K)
has vanishing curl, it must be that
F=\mathrm{skwGrad}(E)
for some
E\in(P^{k}(K))^{4}
. Next, we show that if the trace degrees of freedom of
F
vanish, then
\mathrm{curl}(F)=0
on the facets of
K
. This helps us establish that a 2-form
F\in V_{k}\Lambda^{2}(K)
with vanishing degrees of freedom has vanishing curl on the entirety of
K
, and furthermore that
F
itself vanishes.

Lemma 4.8
.

If
F\in V_{k}\Lambda^{2}(K)
has vanishing curl, then
F\equiv\mathrm{skwGrad}(E)
for some
E\in(P^{k}(K))^{4}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{curl}(F)=0
,
F=\mathrm{skwGrad}(E)
for some sufficiently smooth 1-form
E
. In addition, since
F\in V_{k}\Lambda^{2}(K)
, in accordance with Eq. (
4.1b
) we deduce that

\displaystyle E=A+C,\quad A\in(P^{k}(K))^{4},\quad C\in(\widetilde{P}^{k+1}(K))^{4}.

It remains for us to show that
C=[c_{1},c_{2},c_{3},c_{4}]^{T}=0
where
c_{i}\in\widetilde{P}^{k+1}(K).

From Remark (
4.1
), we easily verify that
\mathrm{skwGrad}(C)\in\left\{B\in\mathcal{L}\left((\tilde{P}^{k}(K))^{6}\right)|Bx=0\right\}
and hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{skwGrad}(C)x=\begin{bmatrix}x\cdot\partial_{1}(C)-x\cdot\mathrm{grad}(c_{1})\\[4.30554pt]
x\cdot\partial_{2}(C)-x\cdot\mathrm{grad}(c_{2})\\[4.30554pt]
x\cdot\partial_{3}(C)-x\cdot\mathrm{grad}(c_{3})\\[4.30554pt]
x\cdot\partial_{4}(C)-x\cdot\mathrm{grad}(c_{4})\end{bmatrix}=0.

But since
c_{i}
is a homogeneous polynomial,
x\cdot\mathrm{grad}(c_{i})=(k+1)c_{i}
, and therefore

\displaystyle x\cdot\partial_{i}(C)=(k+1)c_{i},\qquad i=1,2,3,4.

This is only possible if
c_{i}=0
for each
i
. As a result, it immediately follows that
C=0
.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
F\in V_{k}\Lambda^{2}(K)
be a polynomial 2-form for which all the degrees of freedom
\Sigma^{k,2}(K)
vanish. Then
\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{curl}(F))\equiv 0
on the facets of
K
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a tetrahedral facet of
K
. Since
\text{curl}(F)
is a 3-form, its trace on
\mathcal{F}
is a 3-form. The divergence theorem on
\mathcal{F}
, and the vanishing of traces of
F
gives

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{F}}\mathrm{Tr}(\mathrm{curl}(F))q\,dx=\int_{\mathcal{F}}\nabla\cdot(\mathrm{Tr}(F))q\,dx=-\int_{\mathcal{F}}(\mathrm{Tr}(F))\cdot\nabla q\,dx=0,

(4.14)

for any sufficiently smooth
q
, and in particular for
q\in P^{k-1}(K)
.
Therefore, upon setting
q=\nabla\cdot(\mathrm{Tr}(F))
in Eq. (
4.14
), we find that
\nabla\cdot(\mathrm{Tr}(F))=\mathrm{Tr}(\text{curl}(F))=0
on each
\mathcal{F}
, and on the entire boundary of
K
.
∎

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
F\in V_{k}\Lambda^{2}(K)
be a polynomial 2-form for which all the degrees of freedom
\Sigma^{k,2}(K)
vanish. Then
F\equiv 0.

Proof.

We first observe from Eq. (
3.3
) that

\displaystyle\left(\text{tr}^{(2)}F\right)(E)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial K}\left(n\times F\right)\cdot E\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{K}\left(\text{curl}\,F\right)\cdot E\,dx-\int_{K}\left(\text{Curl}\,E\right):F\,dx,

where
E\in H\left(\text{Curl},K,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right)
. Since all the trace degrees of freedom of
F\in V_{k}\Lambda^{2}(K)
vanish,
F
has zero trace, and therefore

\displaystyle\int_{K}\left(\text{curl}\,F\right)\cdot E\,dx=\int_{K}\left(\text{Curl}\,E\right):F\,dx.

Now, if we pick
E\in(P^{k-2}(K))^{4}
then
E\in H\left(\text{Curl},K,\mathbb{R}^{4}\right)
, and
\mathrm{Curl}(E)\in\mathcal{L}\left((P^{k-3}(K))^{6}\right).
Since the volumetric dofs vanish for
F
, we set
q=\mathrm{Curl}(E)
in Eq. (
4.13
), and we obtain

\int_{K}\left(\text{curl}\,F\right)\cdot E\,dx=0,\qquad\forall E\in(P^{k-2}(K))^{4}.

(4.15)

From the previous lemma, the trace of
\mathrm{curl}(F)
vanishes on the facets and so it is a 3-form bubble in
(P^{k-1}(K))^{4}
, and we can write

\displaystyle\mathrm{curl}(F)=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\psi_{1}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{2}\psi_{2}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{3}\psi_{3}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{4}\psi_{4}\end{bmatrix},\qquad\psi_{i}\in P^{k-2}(K),\qquad i=1,2,3,4.

Upon choosing
E=[\psi_{1},\psi_{2},\psi_{3},\psi_{4}]^{T}
in Eq. (
4.15
), we obtain

\displaystyle 0=\int_{K}\left(\text{curl}\,F\right)\cdot E\,dx=\int_{K}\sum_{i=1}^{4}x_{i}\psi_{i}^{2}\,dx.

But
x_{i}\geq 0
in
K
and so we are guaranteed that
\psi_{i}=0
for
i=1,2,3,4
. This shows that
\mathrm{curl}(F)=0
in
K
.

Next, in accordance with Lemma
4.8
, we can immediately deduce that
F=\mathrm{skwGrad}(\mathcal{E})
for some
\mathcal{E}\in(P^{k}(K))^{4}
. In turn, the vanishing of the traces of
F
allows us to pick
\mathcal{E}
to also have vanishing traces. Recalling Eq. (
3.6
), we can obtain the following trace formula on the facet
x_{4}=0

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{E})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&\mathcal{E}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},0)\\[4.30554pt]
0&0&0&\mathcal{E}_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},0)\\[4.30554pt]
0&0&0&\mathcal{E}_{3}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},0)\\[4.30554pt]
-\mathcal{E}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},0)&-\mathcal{E}_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},0)&-\mathcal{E}_{3}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},0)&0\end{bmatrix}=0,

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathcal{E}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},0)=\mathcal{E}_{2}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},0)=\mathcal{E}_{3}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},0)=0.

Similar considerations apply for the other facets, allowing us to obtain the following expression for
\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}=\begin{bmatrix}x_{2}x_{3}x_{4}g_{1}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{1}x_{3}x_{4}g_{2}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{1}x_{2}x_{4}g_{3}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}g_{4}\end{bmatrix},\quad g_{i}\in P^{k-3}(K),\qquad i=1,2,3,4.

Furthermore

\displaystyle\mathrm{skwGrad}(\mathcal{E})=\mathcal{L}\left(\begin{bmatrix}x_{3}x_{4}\left(\partial_{1}(x_{1}g_{2})-\partial_{2}(x_{2}g_{1})\right)\\[4.30554pt]
x_{2}x_{4}\left(\partial_{1}(x_{1}g_{3})-\partial_{3}(x_{3}g_{1})\right)\\[4.30554pt]
x_{2}x_{3}\left(\partial_{1}(x_{1}g_{4})-\partial_{4}(x_{4}g_{1})\right)\\[4.30554pt]
x_{1}x_{4}\left(\partial_{2}(x_{2}g_{3})-\partial_{3}(x_{3}g_{2})\right)\\[4.30554pt]
x_{1}x_{3}\left(\partial_{2}(x_{2}g_{4})-\partial_{4}(x_{4}g_{2})\right)\\[4.30554pt]
x_{1}x_{2}\left(\partial_{3}(x_{3}g_{4})-\partial_{4}(x_{4}g_{3})\right)\end{bmatrix}\right).

We can now pick
q
in Eq. (
4.13
) as follows

\displaystyle q=\mathcal{L}\left(\begin{bmatrix}q_{12}\\[4.30554pt]
q_{13}\\[4.30554pt]
q_{14}\\[4.30554pt]
q_{23}\\[4.30554pt]
q_{24}\\[4.30554pt]
q_{34}\end{bmatrix}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{1}(x_{1}g_{2})-\partial_{2}(x_{2}g_{1})\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{1}(x_{1}g_{3})-\partial_{3}(x_{3}g_{1})\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{1}(x_{1}g_{4})-\partial_{4}(x_{4}g_{1})\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{2}(x_{2}g_{3})-\partial_{3}(x_{3}g_{2})\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{2}(x_{2}g_{4})-\partial_{4}(x_{4}g_{2})\\[4.30554pt]
\partial_{3}(x_{3}g_{4})-\partial_{4}(x_{4}g_{3})\end{bmatrix}\right),

in order to obtain

\displaystyle 0=\int_{K}F:q\,dx=\int_{K}\mathrm{skwGrad}(\mathcal{E}):q\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{K}\left(x_{3}x_{4}q_{12}^{2}+x_{2}x_{4}q_{13}^{2}+x_{2}x_{3}q_{14}^{2}+x_{1}x_{4}q_{23}^{2}+x_{1}x_{3}q_{24}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}q_{34}^{2}\right)dx.

But then each
q_{ij}=0
, and in turn, it is easy to check that each
g_{i}
must vanish. Finally, it follows that
\mathcal{E}=0
and
F=\mathrm{skwGrad}(\mathcal{E})
must vanish.
∎

4.1.4
Dofs for 3-forms on
\mathfrak{T}^{4}

The polynomial 3-forms on
\mathfrak{T}^{4}
are associated with 4-vectors. The corresponding degrees of freedom have the following dimension

\displaystyle\text{dim}\left(V_{k}\Lambda^{3}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right)

\displaystyle=\text{dim}((P^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}))^{4})+\text{dim}(\tilde{P}^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}))

\displaystyle=4{k+3\choose 4}+{k+2\choose 3}=\frac{1}{6}k(k+1)(k+2)(k+4).

Only facet traces are defined for 3-forms, as given by Eq. (
2.13
). Therefore

\displaystyle\text{dim}\left(\Sigma_{trace}^{k,3}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right)

\displaystyle=5\,\text{dim}\left(P^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{3})\right)

\displaystyle=5{k+2\choose 3}=\frac{5}{6}k(k+1)(k+2).

We define the volume degrees of freedom for the 3-form proxy
G
as follows

\Sigma^{k,3}_{vol}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}):=\left\{\int_{\mathfrak{T}^{4}}G\cdot q,\qquad q\in(P^{k-2}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}))^{4}\right\}.

(4.16)

The dimension of this space is

\displaystyle\text{dim}\left(\Sigma^{k,3}_{vol}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right)=4{k+2\choose 4}=\frac{1}{6}(k-1)k(k+1)(k+2).

Then, we define

\displaystyle\Sigma^{k,3}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}):=\Sigma_{trace}^{k,3}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\cup\Sigma^{k,3}_{vol}(\mathfrak{T}^{4}),

and note that

\displaystyle\text{dim}\left(V_{k}\Lambda^{3}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right)=\text{dim}\left(\Sigma^{k,3}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right)=\text{dim}\left(\Sigma_{trace}^{k,3}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right)+\text{dim}\left(\Sigma^{k,3}_{vol}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right).

Therefore, unisolvency will be guaranteed by establishing the following result.

Lemma 4.11
.

Consider
G\in V_{k}\Lambda^{3}(K)
a polynomial 3-form for which all the degrees of freedom
\Sigma^{k,3}(K)
vanish. Then
G\equiv 0.

Proof.

This proof closely follows the strategy outlined in
[
30
]
, with reference element
K
given by Eq. (
4.10
).

We begin by introducing
v\in P^{k-1}(K)
. In accordance with integration by parts (Eq. (
3.4
)), the vanishing of trace and volume degrees of freedom for
G
, and Eq. (
4.16
), one obtains

\displaystyle\int_{K}(\text{di}v\,G)v=-\int_{K}G\cdot(\text{grad}\,v)=0.

By choosing
v=\text{div}(G)
, we see that
\text{div}(G)=0
in
K
.

Now,
G=p+\hat{r}x
for
p\in(P^{k-1}(K))^{4}
and
\hat{r}\in\tilde{P}^{k-1}(K)
by definition (Eq. (
4.1d
)). In addition, it is easy to check that
\text{div}(\hat{r}x)=(k+4)\hat{r}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\text{div}(G)=\text{div}(p)+(k+4)\hat{r}\Rightarrow\hat{r}=-\frac{1}{k+4}\text{div}(p)\in P^{k-2}(K).

But this is not possible unless the degree
k-1
polynomial
\hat{r}=0
. With this in mind, we observe the following

\displaystyle G=p\in(P^{k-1}(K))^{4}\Rightarrow G=\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\phi_{1}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{2}\phi_{2}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{3}\phi_{3}\\[4.30554pt]
x_{4}\phi_{4}\end{bmatrix},\quad\phi_{i}\in P^{k-2}(K).

This reformulation is possible because
G
is a 3-form bubble with vanishing traces given by Eq. (
3.8
). If
k>1
, we can pick
q=[\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\phi_{3},\phi_{4}]^{T}
in Eq. (
4.16
), from which it will follow that
\phi_{i}=0
, (and hence
G\equiv 0)
. If
k=1
, then trivially
\phi_{i}=0.

∎

4.1.5
Dofs for 4-forms on
\mathfrak{T}^{4}

The polynomial 4-forms on
\mathfrak{T}^{4}
are associated with scalars. Traces for 4-forms are not well-defined. Instead, we specify interior degrees of freedom for the 4-form proxy
q

(4.17)

\displaystyle\text{dim}\left(V_{k}\Lambda^{4}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right)

\displaystyle=\text{dim}\left(\Sigma^{k,4}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right)=\text{dim}\left(\Sigma^{k,4}_{vol}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})\right)

\displaystyle={k+3\choose 4}=\frac{1}{24}k(k+1)(k+2)(k+3).

It then remains to prove unisolvency.

Lemma 4.12
.

Consider
q\in V_{k}\Lambda^{4}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})
a polynomial 4-form for which all the degrees of freedom
\Sigma^{k,4}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})
vanish. Then
q\equiv 0.

Proof.

Suppose that
q\in V_{k}\Lambda^{4}(\mathfrak{T}^{4})
has vanishing degrees of freedom as given by Eq. (
4.17
); then setting
p=q
shows that
q\equiv 0
.
∎

\displaystyle\text{dim}\left(V_{k}\Lambda^{4}(\mathfrak{N}^{4})\right)

\displaystyle=\text{dim}\left(\Sigma^{k,4}(\mathfrak{N}^{4})\right)

\displaystyle=\text{dim}\left(DG^{k-1}\left(\mathfrak{T}^{1}\right)\times DG^{k-1}\left(\mathfrak{T}^{3}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{6}k^{2}(k+1)(k+2).

The 4-forms have no facet degrees of freedom. As a result, all the degrees of freedom are volumetric. The volumetric degrees of freedom for the 4-form proxy
q
can be expressed as follows

\displaystyle\Sigma^{k,4}_{vol}(\mathfrak{N}^{4}):=\left\{q\rightarrow\int_{\mathfrak{N}^{4}}qp,\qquad p\in dP^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{1})\times dP^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{3})\right\}.

(5.12)

It then remains for us to prove unisolvency.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
q\in V_{k}\Lambda^{4}(\mathfrak{N}^{4})
be a polynomial 4-form for which all the degrees of freedom
\Sigma^{k,4}(\mathfrak{N}^{4})
vanish. Then
q\equiv 0.

Proof.

We begin by choosing a generic
q
, such that

\displaystyle q=\sum_{ij\ell m}q_{ij\ell m}v_{ij\ell}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})v_{m}(x_{4}),

where

\displaystyle v_{ij\ell}(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in dP^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{3}),\quad v_{m}(x_{4})\in dP^{k-1}(\mathfrak{T}^{1}).

The proof follows immediately by setting
p=q
in Eq. (
5.12
). Under these circumstances, the degrees of freedom are only guaranteed to vanish if
q
vanishes.
∎

6
Conclusion

This paper has introduced fully explicit H(skwGrad)-, H(curl)-, and H(div)-conforming finite element spaces, basis functions, and degrees of freedom for pentatopes and tetrahedral prism elements. This exercise has been performed with the aid of FEEC. In order to facilitate the implementation of these methods, whenever possible, we have dispensed with the language of differential forms and instead used the language of linear algebra in order to simplify the presentation. We hope that the resulting finite elements will be used extensively in space-time finite element methods. In particular, the finite element spaces on the pentatope should help facilitate space-time methods on both partially-unstructured and fully-unstructured pentatopic meshes. In addition, the finite element spaces on tetrahedral prisms will help facilitate space-time methods on extruded, partially-unstructured, tetrahedral-prismatic meshes.

Looking ahead, there are many opportunities for continued development of four-dimensional finite elements. For example, we note that the recent paper of Petrov et al.
[
33
]
develops four-dimensional, hybrid meshes composed of cubic pyramids, bipentatopes, and tesseracts. To our knowledge, finite element spaces on the cubic pyramid and bipentatope have not been investigated outside of Petrov et al.’s work, (which itself only covers the H1- and L2-conforming cases for cubic pyramids). We anticipate that FEEC techniques can be used to construct a broader range of conforming finite element spaces on these non-simplicial elements in the near future.
