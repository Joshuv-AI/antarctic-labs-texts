Construction of Exact Solutions to Nahm’s Equations For the Multimonopole
H.W. Braden, Sergey A. Cherkis, Jason M. Quinones

Abstract
. We construct high rank solutions to Nahm’s equations for boundary conditions that correspond to the Dirac multimonopole. Here, the spectral curve is explicitly known and we achieve the integration by constructing a basis of polynomial tuples that forms a frame for the flow of the eigenline bundle over the curve.



1.
Introduction

Nahm’s equations, discovered in the context of monopoles in gauge theory
[
Nah80b
]
, arise naturally in hyperkähler geometry
and representation theory and have become an area of independent interest. These equations
are

(1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{dT_{1}}{ds}+[T_{0},T_{1}]&=[T_{2},T_{3}],\\
\frac{dT_{2}}{ds}+[T_{0},T_{2}]&=[T_{3},T_{1}],\\
\frac{dT_{3}}{ds}+[T_{0},T_{3}]&=[T_{1},T_{2}],\\
\end{split}

where
T_{0}(s),T_{1}(s),T_{2}(s),T_{3}(s)
are antihermitian
n\times n
matrix-valued functions and the real parameter
s
lies in an interval, or union of intervals, depending on the problem. The boundary condition for the
T_{*}
’s
also depend critically on the problem. Nahm’s equations form an integrable system, a system of nonlinear ordinary differential equations with sufficiently many conserved quantities to be solved by means of algebraic geometry,
and integrable systems techniques (such as the Baker-Akhiezer function) may be applied to their
solution.

In this paper we construct high rank solutions to Nahm’s equations for the interval
(0,\infty)

subject to the boundary conditions

(2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\underset{s\to 0}{\lim}&sT_{0}(s)=0,\quad\lim_{s\to 0}sT_{j}(s)=\frac{i\sigma_{j}}{2},\ j=1,2,3,\\
\lim_{s\to\infty}&(T_{0}(s),T_{1}(s),T_{2}(s),T_{3}(s))\in\text{ad}_{U(n)}(0,i\tau_{1},i\tau_{2},i\tau_{3}).\end{split}

Here, the triplet
\sigma_{j}
is a specified
n
-dimensional irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{su}(2)
,
[\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j}]=2i\epsilon_{ijk}\sigma_{k}
, and
by a constant gauge transformation we take this to be
\sigma_{3}=\text{diag}(n-1,n-3,\dots,-n+1)
and
\sigma_{+}:=\frac{\sigma_{1}+i\sigma_{2}}{2}=[\sqrt{j(n-j)}\delta_{j,j-1}].
The chosen triplet
(\tau_{1},\tau_{2},\tau_{3})
is regular in

; that is the stabilizer of
\tau_{1},\tau_{2}
, and
\tau_{3}
is a maximal torus. This problem was related to the nonabelian Toda equation in
[

where it was studied in terms of the

The boundary conditions just described correspond to the Dirac
U(1)
multimonopole
[
Nah80a
]
.
As with a great number of other integrable systems, including the Korteweg-de Vries equation and the nonlinear Schrödinger equations, Nahm’s equations arise from a symmetry reduction of the anti-self-dual equation (ASD)

(3)

\displaystyle\ast F_{A}=-F_{A},

for a principal

The symmetry reduction of ASD (

Nah80b
]

[
Nah83
]
.
As we remarked earlier, the monopole that corresponds to the solution of Nahm’s equations with the boundary conditions above is the Dirac
U(1)
multimonopole
[
Nah80a
]
.
Much study has been devoted to the Nahm transform and the 4-manifolds

for which this exists

[

Nak93

In this paper we solve Nahm’s equations by constructing an orthonormal basis of polynomials that satisfy a matching condition, following the algebro-geometric method of integrating Nahm’s equations. This method uses the Lax representation with spectral parameter
\zeta\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
for matrix-valued functions

and

such that Nahm’s equations are equivalent to

(4)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}L(s,\zeta)=[L(s,\zeta),M(s,\zeta)].

The Lax equation implies that the spectrum of
L(s,\zeta)
is independent of the variable

(as
\tr L^{k}
are the conserved quantities of this system thanks to (
4

in

(5)

\displaystyle\det(\eta\mathds{1}-L(s,\zeta))=0,

for
\eta\frac{\partial}{\partial\zeta}\in T\mathbb{P}^{1}

[

]

. The integration concludes with writing a solution for Nahm’s equations in terms of an appropriate frame of the linear flow.

The task is then to construct an orthogonal frame of

. For our boundary conditions we may express this frame in terms of polynomial tuples satisfying conditions we will prescribe; we give two equivalent systems of linear equations for the construction of these polynomials.
The polynomial tuples have significance beyond the construction of Nahm solutions. The Nahm transform between monopoles and Nahm solutions requires solving for normalizable zero modes to the associated Dirac operators. Once one obtains the orthogonal basis of polynomial tuples, it is a straightforward operation to construct all normalizable zero modes to the Nahm-sided Dirac operator following
[
BE18
]
and to the monopole-sided Dirac operator following
[
LP15
]

Next, using the two lemmas from the literature below, we may

\displaystyle T_{j}(s)=\frac{i\sigma_{j}}{2s}+g_{0}i\tau_{j}g_{0}^{-1}+b_{j}(s),

First, the behavior near the pole at

Lemma 1

(
[

)

Suppose the Nahm solution

\displaystyle T_{j}-\frac{i\sigma_{j}}{2s}\in L^{2}([0,\epsilon)),

\displaystyle T_{j}-\frac{i\sigma_{j}}{2s}\in L^{\infty}([0,\epsilon]).

The precise statement of the decay is given by the following lemma.

Lemma 2

(

Let

It is in the proof of this lemma where we see the requirement for

The critical set

Further analysis then shows that with

In the next few sections, we construct a particular solution to (

3.
The Spectral Approach

The spectral method of integration begins with a Lax equation that is equivalent to the system of Nahm’s equations. The mathematical formalism of this approach to integrating Nahm’s equations was originally carried out by Hitchin in 1983
[
Hit83
]
for the
SU(2)
monopole. We adopt the same approach.

In this section, we discuss the Lax pair as well as the properties of the associated spectral curve
\mathcal{C}
and eigenline bundle. We prove that for
s\in(0,\infty)
, the space of global sections of the line bundle
L^{s}(n-1)
over
\mathcal{C}
has dimension
n
.

3.1.
Lax Equation

The Lax pair
(L,M)
satisfying

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}L=[L,M]

for Nahm’s equations are given by

(12)

L^{N}=-i(T_{1}+iT_{2})+2iT_{3}\zeta+i(T_{1}-iT_{2})\zeta^{2},\ M^{N}=T_{0}-i(T_{3}+(T_{1}-iT_{2})\zeta),

in the North patch

of

\mathbb{P}^{1}=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
, and by

(13)

L^{S}=i(T_{1}-iT_{2})+2iT_{3}\frac{1}{\zeta}-i(T_{1}+iT_{2})\frac{1}{\zeta^{2}},\ \ M^{S}=T_{0}+i(T_{3}-(T_{1}+iT_{2})\frac{1}{\zeta}),

in the South patch
\{\zeta\neq 0\}
of

\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

Crucially,
L
and
M
have transition functions given by

(14)

\displaystyle L^{S}=\frac{1}{\zeta^{2}}L^{N},\ \ \ M^{S}=M^{N}+\frac{1}{\zeta}L^{N},

and
L
and
M
satisfy the reality relationship

(15)

\displaystyle L^{N}(-1/\bar{\zeta})^{\dagger}=-L^{S}(\zeta),\ \ \ \ \ M^{N}(-1/\bar{\zeta})^{\dagger}=-M^{S}(\zeta).

Conversely, if a Lax pair
(L,M)
is holomorphic with respect to the spectral parameter
\zeta\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
and satisfies the transition functions (
14
) as well as the reality conditions (
15
), then it follows that
(L,M)
has the form (
12

) with
(T_{0},T_{1},T_{2},T_{3})

solving Nahm’s equations.

; and so on.

\displaystyle L^{N}(s)

\displaystyle=e^{\zeta\sigma_{-}}\left(\frac{\sigma_{+}}{s}+a_{0}+a_{1}s\sigma_{-}+a_{2}s^{2}\sigma_{-}^{2}+\dots+a_{n-1}s^{n-1}\sigma_{-}^{n-1}\right)e^{-\zeta\sigma_{-}}

\displaystyle=e^{\zeta\sigma_{-}}\begin{pmatrix}a_{0}&s^{-1}&0&0&\ldots&0\\
a_{1}s&a_{0}&s^{-1}&0&\ldots&0\\
a_{2}s^{2}&a_{1}s&a_{0}&s^{-1}&&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&&\\
a_{n-2}s^{n-2}&&&&&s^{-1}\\
a_{n-1}s^{n-1}&a_{n-2}s^{n-2}&&&a_{1}s&a_{0}\end{pmatrix}e^{-\zeta\sigma_{-}}.

The coefficients
a_{j}=a_{j}(\zeta)
are determined by the spectral curve. At this stage we have sufficiently simplifed

and

\frac{d}{ds}+M^{N}=e^{\zeta\sigma_{-}}\Bigg[\frac{d}{ds}+\frac{\sigma_{3}}{2s}\Bigg]e^{-\zeta\sigma_{-}}

to solve the linear problem. Clearly
U=e^{\zeta\sigma_{-}}s^{-\sigma_{3}/2}U^{\prime}
for any constant

\begin{split}s^{\sigma_{3}/2}&\left(\frac{\sigma_{+}}{s}+a_{0}+a_{1}s\sigma_{-}+a_{2}s^{2}\sigma_{-}^{2}+\dots+a_{n-1}s^{n-1}\sigma_{-}^{n-1}\right)s^{-\sigma_{3}/2}\\
&=\left(\sigma_{+}+a_{0}+a_{1}\sigma_{-}+a_{2}\sigma_{-}^{2}+\dots+a_{n-1}\sigma_{-}^{n-1}\right).\end{split}

Thus

0=(L^{N}(s)-\eta)U=e^{\zeta\sigma_{-}}s^{-\sigma_{3}/2}\left[\sigma_{+}+a_{0}+a_{1}\sigma_{-}+a_{2}\sigma_{-}^{2}+\dots+a_{n-1}\sigma_{-}^{n-1}-\eta\right]U^{\prime}

and we have reduced the problem to the eigenvectors of a
constant matrix. If

is in rational canonical form, and so with eigenvectors proportional to
(1,\eta,\eta^{2},\ldots,\eta^{n-1})^{T}
for the eigenvalue

(25)

\displaystyle{U}^{\prime}=R(\zeta)\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\dots&1\\
p_{1}(\zeta)&p_{2}(\zeta)&\dots&p_{n}(\zeta)\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\\
p_{1}(\zeta)^{n-1}&p_{2}(\zeta)^{n-1}&\dots&p_{n}(\zeta)^{n-1}\end{pmatrix}\Lambda

for some diagonal matrix
\Lambda
.
From this we see that the eigenspaces of
{L^{N}}
remain one-dimensional
at
\zeta=a_{ij}
and we may set

to be the identity matrix. It follows that

(26)

\displaystyle U_{i}(a_{ij})=U_{j}(a_{ij}).

Our analysis has thus far been for
s
in the vicinity of
0
, but the results of the last section show that the eigenspace of

remains one-dimensional for all finite

Each row of the matrix
U
gives us a section

of the line bundle
L^{s}(n-1)
. Crucially, there is an isomorphism between the sections of
L^{s}(n-1)
and the sections

\displaystyle\begin{split}q_{i}(a_{ij})&=e^{-sr_{ij}}q_{j}(a_{ij})\text{ for }a_{ij}\text{ a double point of }\mathcal{C},\\
q^{N}(\zeta)&=\zeta^{n-1}q^{S}(\zeta).\end{split}

The isomorphism is obtained by using the splitting
\frac{p_{j}(\zeta)}{\zeta}=-h_{j}^{+}(\zeta)-h_{j}^{-}(\zeta)
, where
h_{j}^{+}(\zeta)=p_{j}^{3}+(p_{j}^{1}-ip_{j}^{2})\zeta
and
h_{j}^{-}(\zeta)=-\frac{p_{j}^{1}+ip_{j}^{2}}{\zeta}+p_{j}^{3}=\overline{h_{j}^{+}(-1/\overline{\zeta})}

and then setting

(28)

\displaystyle q^{N}(\zeta)=u^{N}(\zeta)\,\text{diag}(e^{sh_{j}^{+}(\zeta)}),\ \ \ q^{S}(\zeta)=u^{S}(\zeta)\,\text{diag}(e^{-sh_{j}^{-}(1/\zeta)}).

Here we have used that
h_{i}^{+}(a_{ij})-h_{j}^{+}(a_{ij})=-r_{ij}
.

The transition functions for

to the

-th sheet is a polynomial in

of degree at most

. By writing

is a row of polynomials in

∎

We now show that the space of sections of

; we will need this fact to prove invertibility of the linear systems we use to construct the polynomials in later sections.

Proposition 4
.

For
s\in(0,\infty)
, the dimension of

is
n
.

Proof.

A direct application of the Riemann-Roch formula
[
Hit83
, pp.165-166]
implies that if
\dim H^{0}(\mathcal{C},L^{s}(n-2))=0
, then
\dim H^{0}(\mathcal{C},L^{s}(n-1))=n
. Therefore, in this proof, we consider the line bundle
L^{s}(n-2)
and show the dimension of its global sections is zero.

A section of
L^{s}(n-2)
corresponds to an
n
-tuple of degree
n-2
polynomials satisfying matching conditions

(29)

\displaystyle P_{i}(s,a_{ij})=e^{-sr_{ij}}P_{j}(s,a_{ij}),

in the same vein as (
27
).
The matching conditions (
29
) form a linear system for the unknown polynomial coefficients. The
s
dependence enters the linear system via functions
e^{-sr_{ij}}
. Upon writing this linear system in the form
Ax=0
(such a matrix
A
is given in Subsection
5.2
) the
s
-dependent terms in
A
are in the form
e^{-s\Delta}
for
\Delta>0
and it is known that
L^{s}(n-2)
has no nonzero sections when
s
is large
[
Bie07
, Cor. 3.3]
.

The null space of
A
may then be written as a series in
e^{-s\Delta}
for various positive
\Delta
,
and we then write the polynomials as a formal series, which we denote as its perturbation expansion,

(30)

\displaystyle P_{i}(s,\zeta)=g_{i}(\zeta)+e^{-s\Delta^{\prime}_{i}}g_{i}^{\prime}(\zeta)+e^{-s\Delta_{i}^{\prime\prime}}g^{\prime\prime}_{i}(\zeta)+\dots

with
0<\Delta_{i}^{\prime}<\Delta_{i}^{\prime\prime}<\dots
and
s
-independent polynomials
g_{i}(\zeta)
,
g_{i}^{\prime}(\zeta)
,
g_{i}^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)
,
\dots
of degree at most
n-2
.
Taking the limit of (

) as
s\to\infty
shows
\underset{s\to\infty}{\lim}P_{i}(s,a_{ij})=0
. A polynomial that satisfies
P_{i}(a_{ij})=0
is either a multiple of the polynomial
A_{i}(\zeta)=\underset{j\neq i}{\prod}(\zeta-a_{ij})
or it is identically zero. Since
A_{i}(\zeta)
has degree exceeding

, we conclude that
\underset{s\to\infty}{\lim}P_{i}(s,\zeta)=0
.
It follows that the perturbation expansion must begin

(31)

\displaystyle P_{i}(s,\zeta)=e^{-s\Delta_{i}}g_{i}(\zeta)+e^{-s\Delta_{i}^{\prime}}g^{\prime}_{i}(\zeta)+e^{-s\Delta_{i}^{\prime\prime}}g_{i}^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)+\dots

with
0<\Delta_{i}<\Delta_{i}^{\prime}<\Delta_{i}^{\prime\prime}<\dots
and
s
-independent polynomials
g_{i}(\zeta)
,
g_{i}^{\prime}(\zeta)
,
g_{i}^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)
,
\dots
of degree at most
n-2
.

The expansion of the matching conditions
P_{i}(s,a_{ij})=e^{-sr_{ij}}P_{j}(s,a_{ij})
at zero-order is the system

(32)

\displaystyle g_{i}(a_{ij})=e^{-s(r_{ij}+\Delta_{j}-\Delta_{i})}g_{j}(a_{ij}),\ \ \ \text{ for }i\neq j.

If
g_{i}(a_{ij})\neq 0
, then the

-independence of the perturbation polynomials implies
r_{ij}+\Delta_{j}-\Delta_{i}=0
. However, we make the observation that for a fixed pair of

we cannot have both

(33)

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}+r_{ij}-\Delta_{i}=0\text{ and }\Delta_{i}+r_{ij}-\Delta_{j}=0

as that implies
r_{ij}=0
. Therefore, if
g_{i}(a_{ij})\neq 0
, we must have
g_{i}(a_{ji})=0
to avoid this case. This gives

many points that

must be zero at. However
g_{i}(\zeta)
is degree at most

so the solution to zero-order is

.

By induction, subsequent orders are identical to the zero-order case. We conclude from the perturbation expansion of our section that

\displaystyle(P_{1}(s,\zeta),\dots,P_{n}(s,\zeta))\equiv(0,\dots,0),

so that there is no nonzero global section of
L^{s}(n-2)
.
∎

3.4.
Inner Product Structure

The reality conditions (
15
) imply that the frame
U
of
H^{0}(\mathcal{C},L^{s}(n-1))
for

must satisfy an additional condition of orthonormality with respect to an inner product on

. We now review this inner product structure on

The antipodal map on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
lifts to an antiholomorphic involution on

given by

(34)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}:(\zeta,\eta)\mapsto(-{1}/{\bar{\zeta}},-{\bar{\eta}}/{\bar{\zeta}^{2}}).

We have seen that the spectral curve
\mathcal{C}\subset T\mathbb{P}^{1}
is invariant under (

The involution induces an antiholomorphic involution

on the set of line bundles

where
\sigma
acts on a section

by

(35)

\displaystyle\sigma(u(\zeta,\eta))=\overline{u(-1/\bar{\zeta},-\bar{\eta}/\bar{\zeta}^{2})}.

The inner product
\langle\ ,\ \rangle
on

introduced by Hitchin
[
Hit83
, pp.179-181]
arises in the following manner. Given sections

, then

is a section of

and can be written uniquely in the North patch as

(36)

\displaystyle u\sigma(v)^{N}=c_{0}(s)\eta^{n-1}+c_{1}(s,\zeta)\eta^{n-2}+\dots+c_{n-1}(s,\zeta),

for
c_{i}(s,\zeta)
a degree
2i
polynomial in
\zeta
.
The inner product is then defined by setting

(37)

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle:=c_{0}(s)

for
c_{0}(s)
of (
36
), which is independent of
\zeta
.

The
c_{0}
term defining the inner product
\langle u,v\rangle
on
H^{0}(\mathcal{C},L^{s}(n-1))
may be written in terms of the corresponding row of polynomials (
28
) as in
[
Bie07
, Eqn 4.8]
. Given
p=(P_{1}(\zeta),\dots,P_{n}(\zeta))
and
r=(R_{1}(\zeta),\dots,R_{n}(\zeta))
of polynomials satisfying (

), Lagrangian interpolation for (

u\sigma(v)
above
\zeta\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
on each sheet

gives that

(38)

\displaystyle\langle p,r\rangle=(-\zeta)^{n-1}\overset{n}{\underset{i=1}{\sum}}\frac{P_{i}(\zeta)\overline{R_{i}(-1/\bar{\zeta})}}{\underset{i\neq j}{\prod}(p_{i}(\zeta)-p_{j}(\zeta))}.

3.5.
The line bundles

We conclude this section with some comments on the line bundles in our construction. For a nonsingular curve the
identification of the Picard group and the Jacobian leads to describing the flow of the integrable system in terms of a flow on the Jacobian. For the
su(2)
charge
n
-monopole the
curve is smooth of genus
(n-1)^{2}
and the Jacobian is (a complex torus) of this dimension. When a curve degenerates
the Jacobian is replaced by a generalized Jacobian, generically a product of
\mathbb{C}
’s,
\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
’s and a Jacobian of smaller genus. For the case at hand the normalization of our curve is the sum of
n

\mathbb{P}^{1}
’s and line bundles are restricted by
the matching conditions (
27
). To get a line bundle on
our curve
\mathcal{C}
we choose line bundles
\mathcal{L}_{i}
on each
\mathbb{P}^{1}_{i}

and then identify the fibre of
\mathcal{L}_{i}
at
p_{i}
with the fibre of
\mathcal{L}_{j}
at
p_{j}

This identification is only determined up to scaling by an element of
\mathbb{C}
. Now consider first the
case
n=2
. Here if we choose two different identifications, differing say by a scaling factor of
\lambda
, we get the same line bundle on
\mathcal{C}
because we can apply the automorphism
1\times\lambda

to the line bundle
\mathcal{L}_{1}\coprod\mathcal{L}_{2}
on

\mathbb{P}^{1}_{1}\coprod\mathbb{P}^{1}_{2}
and this will take the first identification to the second.
For the general case we have the possibility of
n-1
such scalings. Here there are
n(n-1)
points
of intersection and so we obtain
n(n-1)-(n-1)=(n-1)^{2}
worth of scalings.
These scalings account for the different line bundles (our generalized Jacobian).

4.
From Frames to Nahm Solutions

We have described the process of obtaining an orthonormal frame
U
for
H^{0}(\mathcal{C},L^{s}(n-1))
with

from a Nahm solution
(T_{0},T_{1},T_{2},T_{3})

an appropriate Nahm solution
(T_{0},T_{1},T_{2},T_{3})

Proposition 5
.

[
Bie07
, Prop 5.1]
The set of orthonormal frames

of
H^{0}(\mathcal{C},L^{s}(n-1))
for

is in 1-1 correspondence with the set of solutions

to Nahm’s equations satisfying the boundary conditions

\displaystyle\underset{s\to 0}{\lim}(sT_{0},sT_{1},sT_{2},sT_{3})=(0,\frac{i\rho_{1}}{2},\frac{i\rho_{2}}{2},\frac{i\rho_{3}}{2}),\underset{s\to\infty}{\lim}(T_{0},T_{1},T_{2},T_{3})\in\text{ad}_{U(n)}(0,i\tau_{1},i\tau_{2},i\tau_{3}),

with
\vec{\rho}
some
n
dimensional irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{su}(2)
.

Now, suppose we are given an orthonormal frame
U
of
H^{0}(\mathcal{C},L^{s}(n-1))
. Define the Lax pair
(L,M)
in the North patch by

(39)

\displaystyle\begin{split}L^{N}(s,\zeta)&:=U^{N}(s,\zeta)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}p_{1}(\zeta)&&&\\
&p_{2}(\zeta)&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&p_{n}(\zeta)\end{smallmatrix}\right)U^{N}(s,\zeta)^{-1},\\
M^{N}(s,\zeta)&:=-\frac{dU^{N}(s,\zeta)}{ds}U^{N}(s,\zeta)^{-1},\end{split}

and in the South patch by

\displaystyle\begin{split}L^{S}(s,\frac{1}{\zeta})&:=U^{S}(s,\frac{1}{\zeta})\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\frac{p_{1}(\zeta)}{\zeta^{2}}&&&\\
&\frac{p_{2}(\zeta)}{\zeta^{2}}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\frac{p_{n}(\zeta)}{\zeta^{2}}\end{smallmatrix}\right)U^{S}(s,\frac{1}{\zeta})^{-1},\\
M^{S}(s,\frac{1}{\zeta})&:=-\frac{dU^{S}(s,\frac{1}{\zeta})}{ds}U^{S}(s,\frac{1}{\zeta})^{-1},\end{split}

with each
p_{j}(\zeta)=x_{j}^{1}+ix_{j}^{2}-2x_{j}^{3}\zeta-(x_{j}^{1}-ix_{j}^{2})\zeta^{2}
determined by the configuration of points

arising from the boundary conditions as

.
From the transition function of
U
given in Proposition
3
, it follows that the Lax pair
(L,M)
satisfies the desired transition functions (
14
). It is also a simple exercise to see that orthonormality of

implies
(L,M)
satisfies the reality conditions of (
15
).

We shall now give an elementary proof that the Lax pair
(L,M)
is holomorphic in
\zeta
; this is not immediately obvious because the columns of
U
fail to be linearly independent at the double points
\zeta=a_{ij}
of the spectral curve, which means the inverse of
U
is meromorphic in
\zeta
.
Moreover we show that
L
is quadratic in
\zeta
and

is linear in
\zeta
from which it follows that
(L,M)
may be written in the form (
12
) with
(T_{0},T_{1},T_{2},T_{3})
satisfying Nahm’s equations.

Instead of the frame
U
, it is more convenient to work with the corresponding matrix of polynomials

defined by (
28
).
Q(s,\zeta)
is orthonormal with respect to the inner product (
38
) so the inverse

is

(40)

\displaystyle\footnotesize Q^{N}(s,\zeta)^{-1}=(-\zeta)^{n-1}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{\overset{n}{\underset{j=2}{\prod}}(p_{1}(\zeta)-p_{j}(\zeta))^{-1}}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&&{\overset{n-1}{\underset{j=1}{\prod}}(p_{n}(\zeta)-p_{j}(\zeta))^{-1}}\end{smallmatrix}\right)Q^{N}(s,-1/\bar{\zeta})^{\dagger},

where

denotes the complex conjugate transpose.

Lemma 6
.

L^{N}(s,\zeta)
defined in Equation (

\zeta
.

Proof.

From (
39
), (
40
) the
uv
-th entry of

is given by

(41)

\displaystyle L^{N}_{uv}=\sum_{s=1}^{n}p_{s}(\zeta)\frac{(-\zeta)^{n-1}Q_{us}(\zeta)\overline{Q_{vs}(-1/\bar{\zeta})}}{\overset{n}{\underset{l\neq s}{\prod}}(p_{s}(\zeta)-p_{l}(\zeta))}.

The possible poles of

at

arise from the two terms

(42)

\displaystyle p_{i}(\zeta)\frac{(-\zeta)^{n-1}Q_{ui}(\zeta)\overline{Q_{vi}(-1/\bar{\zeta})}}{\underset{l\neq i}{\prod}(p_{i}(\zeta)-p_{l}(\zeta))}+p_{j}(\zeta)\frac{(-\zeta)^{n-1}Q_{uj}(\zeta)\overline{Q_{vj}(-1/\bar{\zeta})}}{\underset{l\neq j}{\prod}(p_{j}(\zeta)-p_{l}(\zeta))}.

, and as

The latter factor appears as the only possible poles of

.

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\underset{j\neq i}{\prod}(p_{i}(\zeta)-p_{j}(\zeta))}=0.

Since this sum is equal to zero, there cannot be a pole at

; consequently

. Similarly,

is holomorphic in

) of

\zeta
.
∎

Lemma 7
.

is a linear function in

.

Proof.

The

-th entry of

, and similarly

) of

.
∎

5.1.
A Direct Approach

There is a direct approach to the linear system of polynomials satisfying the matching conditions.
For a row of polynomials
(Q_{1},\dots,Q_{n})
(we drop the row index
l
for ease of notation) we can simply take as unknowns all the coefficients

\{q_{ij}:\ i=0,\dots,n-1,j=1,\dots,n\}
of

\displaystyle Q_{1}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=q_{01}+q_{11}\zeta+\dots+q_{(n-1)1}\zeta^{n-1},

\displaystyle Q_{n}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=q_{0n}+q_{1n}\zeta+\dots+q_{(n-1)n}\zeta^{n-1},

and subject
\{q_{ij}\}
to the conditions
(A)
.
For each such row we may form the
n^{2}\times 1
column vector

\mathfrak{q}:=(q_{01},q_{02},\dots,q_{0n},\dots,q_{(n-1)1},q_{(n-1)n},\dots,q_{(n-1)n})^{T}

with all of the unknown coefficients. Then

[(1,\zeta,\ldots,\zeta^{n-1})\otimes\hat{e}_{i}]\mathfrak{q}=Q_{i}(\zeta)

where
\hat{e}_{i}
is the
1\times n
row vector with a
1
in the
i
-th place and
zero otherwise. The matching condition

Q_{i}(a_{ij})=e^{-sr_{ij}}Q_{j}(a_{ij})
then
takes the form

[(1,a_{ij},a_{ij}^{2},\ldots,a_{ij}^{n-1})\otimes(e^{sr_{ij}/2}\hat{e}_{i}-e^{-sr_{ij}/2}\hat{e}_{j})]\,\mathfrak{q}=0.

For each of the
n
basis elements
q_{l}
we may construct the
corresponding column vectors
\mathfrak{q}_{l}
and assemble these columns to form a
{n^{2}\times n}
matrix

\mathcal{P}
and the matching conditions
then yield the linear system

(50)

\displaystyle\Xi\,\mathcal{P}=0,

where
\Xi
is the
n(n-1)\times n^{2}
matrix whose rows, labelled by
(ij)
,
are

(1,a_{ij},a_{ij}^{2},\ldots,a_{ij}^{n-1})\otimes(e^{sr_{ij}/2}\hat{e}_{i}-e^{-sr_{ij}/2}\hat{e}_{j}).

Proposition 8
.

The orthogonal basis
\{q_{1},\dots,q_{n}\}
for
s\in(0,\infty)
, with

q_{l}=\left(Q_{1}^{(l)}(\zeta),Q_{2}^{(l)}(\zeta),\ldots,Q_{n}^{(l)}(\zeta)\right)

for
Q_{i}^{(l)}(\zeta)
a monic polynomial of maximal degree
n-1
and satisfying the vanishing conditions
Q_{i}^{(l)}(\infty)=0
for
i<l
and
Q_{i}^{(l)}(0)=0
for
i>l
, is constructible from the linear system (
50
).

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the
{n^{2}\times n}
be the matrix of unknowns where all of the coefficients in
q_{l}
are placed in column
\mathfrak{q}_{l}
described above. The vanishing condition
Q^{(l)}_{i}(\infty)=0
, i.e.
Q^{(l)}_{i}(\zeta)
has degree (at most)
n-2
, for
i<l
and so the bottom
n
rows of
\mathcal{P}
is a lower triangular matrix

; the monic condition means that
\hat{L}
has
1
’s along the diagonal. The vanishing condition
Q^{(l)}_{i}(0)=0
for

makes the top
n
rows of
\mathcal{P}
an upper triangular matrix

. The linear system
\Xi\,\mathcal{P}=0
of (
50
) thus takes the form

(51)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}a&B&c\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}U\\
\tilde{P}\\
\hat{L}\end{pmatrix}=0.

which, provided the matrix
\begin{pmatrix}a&B\end{pmatrix}
is invertible, may be solved by writing,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}a&B\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}U\\
\tilde{P}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-c\hat{L},\quad\begin{pmatrix}a&B\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}U\\
\tilde{P}\end{pmatrix}\hat{L}^{-1}

\displaystyle=-c,\quad\begin{pmatrix}U\hat{L}^{-1}\\
\tilde{P}\hat{L}^{-1}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=-\begin{pmatrix}a&B\end{pmatrix}^{-1}c.

By taking the

decomposition
[
TB97
]
of the top

rows of
-\begin{pmatrix}a&B\end{pmatrix}^{-1}c
one solves for the unknowns
U
and
\hat{L}^{-1}
; then applying
\hat{L}
to the remaining rows of
-\begin{pmatrix}a&B\end{pmatrix}^{-1}c
one solves for
\tilde{P}
.

Finally the invertibility of the

n(n-1)\times n(n-1)
matrix

\begin{pmatrix}a&B\end{pmatrix}=\left((1,a_{ij},a_{ij}^{2},\ldots,a_{ij}^{n-2})\otimes(e^{sr_{ij}/2}\hat{e}_{i}-e^{-sr_{ij}/2}\hat{e}_{j})\right)

is equivalent to

, for

, which was proved in Proposition
4
: this block of
\Xi
encodes the matching conditions for the polynomials corresponding to sections of
L^{s}(n-2)
. We shall give an alternate description of the basis described here in Proposition
11
.
∎

At this stage we see Hitchin’s result regarding the sections at
s=0
. The null space of the matrix
(a,B)\big|_{s=0}
is spanned by the
(n-1)
vectors of the form
(0,\ldots,0,1,0\ldots,0)^{T}\otimes(1,1,\ldots,1)^{T}
and these yield the sections
r(\zeta)(1,1,\ldots,1)

of
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{C}}(n-1)
of Hitchin.

5.2.
A Lagrange-Interpolation Approach

We may also use Lagrange interpolation to obtain an equivalent linear system for the construction of the collection of polynomials satisfying
(A)
. This method was used by Lamy-Poirier in
[
LP15
]
to impose algebraic conditions on the unknowns, and in this subsection, we further simplify the algebraic conditions to a linear system and prove its invertibility.
The advantage of this approach is that the matrix of the linear system is smaller, having size
\frac{1}{2}{n(n-1)}\times\frac{1}{2}{n(n-1)}
, and the solution to the system is obtained through a single matrix inversion, rather than by matrix inversion followed by an UL decomposition.

The linear system is obtained by the following steps.

Step 1:

Write the Lagrange interpolation polynomial for
Q_{k}(\zeta)
,
k=1,2,\dots,n
in terms of the variables
Q_{j}(a_{kj})
from the matching conditions.

Step 2:

Rewrite the Lagrangian polynomials in terms of only the variables
Q_{j}(a_{kj})
with
k<j
for
j=2,3,\dots,n
.

Step 3:

Evaluate the interpolation polynomials
Q_{k}(\zeta)
at
a_{jk}
for
j<k
to obtain
\frac{n(n-1)}{2}
linear equations for the unknowns
Q_{u}(a_{vu})
with
v<u
for
u=2,3,\dots,n
.

We will now describe these steps in more detail, ending the subsection with the

n=3
example.

Step 1:

As in
[
LP15
]
, supposing the values
Q_{j}(a_{kj})
for
j\neq k
are fixed, then Lagrange interpolation for the degree
n-1
polynomial
Q_{k}(\zeta)
using the matching conditions
Q_{k}(a_{kj})=e^{-sr_{kj}}Q_{j}(a_{kj})
for
j\neq k
gives the degree
n-1
polynomial

(52)

\displaystyle Q_{k}(\zeta)=C_{k}A_{k}(\zeta)+\sum_{j\neq k}e^{-sr_{kj}}Q_{j}(a_{kj})\underset{l\neq k,j}{\prod}\frac{\zeta-a_{kl}}{a_{kj}-a_{kl}},\ k=1,\dots,n.

Here we define

(53)

A_{k}(\zeta):=\overset{n}{\underset{j\neq k}{\prod}}(\zeta-a_{kj}).

The value
C_{k}
is some constant independent of
\zeta
, although we do not need to make it independent of
s
.

Step 2:

The interpolation polynomials (
52
) satisfy the matching conditions by construction and, altogether, they utilize the
n(n-1)
many variables
Q_{u}(a_{vu})
,
u=1,\dots,n
with both
v<u
and
v>u
. We may however rewrite (
52
) in terms of only
Q_{u}(a_{vu})
with
v<u
for
u=2,\dots,n
.

Lemma 9
.

The variables
Q_{u}(a_{vu})
with
v>u
in the Lagrange interpolation polynomials (
52
) may be expressed in terms of
Q_{u}(a_{vu})
with
v<u
.

Proof.

The Lagrange interpolation polynomial
Q_{1}(\zeta)
is already written in terms of
Q_{u}(a_{1u})
for
1<u
, so we obtain
Q_{1}(a_{j1})
for
j>1
in terms of
Q_{u}(a_{1u})
by evaluating
Q_{1}(\zeta)
at
a_{j1}
.

We use induction for
k=2,\dots,n
. The induction hypothesis is that
Q_{p}(a_{qp})
for
p<k
have been written in terms of
Q_{u}(a_{vu})
for
v<u
. Then for
Q_{k}(\zeta)
of (
52
),

(54)

\displaystyle\begin{split}Q_{k}(\zeta)=C_{k}A_{k}(\zeta)&+\sum_{j<k}e^{-sr_{kj}}\underset{\text{rewritten by induction hypothesis}}{\underbrace{Q_{j}(a_{kj})}}\underset{l\neq k,j}{\prod}\frac{(\zeta-a_{kl})}{(a_{kj}-a_{kl})}\\
&+\sum_{k<j}e^{-sr_{kj}}\underset{\text{desired variables}}{\underbrace{Q_{j}(a_{kj})}}\underset{l\neq k,j}{\prod}\frac{(\zeta-a_{kl})}{(a_{kj}-a_{kl})}.\end{split}

Thus, we may evaluate
Q_{k}(\zeta)
at
a_{jk}
for
j>k
to obtain
Q_{k}(a_{jk})
in terms of
Q_{u}(a_{vu})
with
v<u
.
∎

Step 3:
The final step is to evaluate the polynomials
Q_{k}(\zeta)
at
a_{jk}
for
j<k
to give us the linear system in the unknowns
X=\{Q_{u}(a_{vu})\}
,

. This may be written as

AX=B

where
B
is expressed linearly in terms of the

’s with coefficients which are products of
e^{-sr_{ij}}
’s and rational in the

’s. This system can be solved.

Lemma 10
.

The
\frac{1}{2}{n(n-1)}\times\frac{1}{2}{n(n-1)}

of the linear system for the unknowns

is invertible when

.

Proof.

Set all

so that
Q_{k}(\zeta)
are only degree
n-2

satisfying the matching conditions
Q_{i}(a_{ij})=e^{-sr_{ij}}Q_{j}(a_{ij})
. This is the system

∎

Thus far the coefficients

5.3.
Perturbative Expansion of Polynomials

We would like to understand the behavior of the basis of
n
-tuple polynomials satisfying
(A)
for large
s
. We do this by constructing its approximate solution, which we call its perturbative expansion, for large
s
in terms of a series in the small parameters
e^{-sr_{ij}}
. The higher-order terms in the series become successively smaller and we give a method for constructing the approximate solution to arbitrary order.
From this, we see that the matrix of our basis becomes diagonal as
s\to\infty
so that the corresponding Nahm solution satisfies the boundary condition (
49
).

Consider the first element of the basis

{q}_{1}=(Q_{1}^{(1)},\ldots,Q_{n}^{(1)})
; the story is analogous for other elements (and we will again drop the superscripts for ease of notation).
Set
Q_{j}^{\prime}(\zeta):=\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}Q_{j}(\zeta)
. The limit as
s\to\infty
of the matching conditions
Q_{j}(a_{ji})=e^{-sr_{ij}}Q_{i}(a_{ji})
gives

Q^{\prime}_{j}(a_{ji})=0.

In addition to this,
the conditions of
(A2)
on
{q}_{1}

states that
Q^{\prime}_{j\neq 1}(\zeta)
vanishes at
0
and at
n-1
many other points
a_{jk}
. Since
Q^{\prime}_{j\neq 1}(\zeta)
is a polynomial of degree at most
n-1
, we must have
Q^{\prime}_{j\neq 1}(\zeta)\equiv 0
. As
Q^{\prime}_{1}(\zeta)
vanishes at
a_{1k}
for
k=2,3,\dots,n
and is a monic polynomial, then

Q^{\prime}_{1}(\zeta)=\overset{n}{\underset{k=2}{\prod}}(\zeta-a_{1k})=A_{1}(\zeta)

where
A_{i}(\zeta)
was defined in (
53
).

We are seeking an expansion for the first basis element, which we have called the perturbation expansion for large
s
, with

(55)

\displaystyle Q_{1}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=A_{1}(\zeta)+e^{-s\Delta_{1}}q_{1}(\zeta)+e^{-s\Delta_{1}^{\prime}}q_{1}^{\prime}(\zeta)+\dots

(56)

\displaystyle Q_{j\neq 1}(\zeta)

\displaystyle=e^{-s\Delta_{j}}\zeta q_{j}(\zeta)+e^{-s\Delta_{j}^{\prime}}\zeta q_{j}^{\prime}(\zeta)+\dots

with
0<\Delta_{k}<\Delta_{k}^{\prime}<\Delta_{k}^{\prime\prime}<\dots
. The polynomials
q_{k}(\zeta)
are degree less than or equal to
n-2
in
\zeta
and independent of
s
.

The method of obtaining the perturbation expansion to arbitrary order is given as follows. The zeroth order is
Q_{1}=A_{1}(\zeta)
,
Q_{j\neq 1}(\zeta)=0
. By induction, given the expansion at
n
-th order, the
(n+1)
-st order of
Q_{1}
is the Lagrangian interpolation polynomial for the values
e^{-sr_{1i}}e^{-s\Delta_{i}^{(n)}}\tilde{q}^{(n)}_{i}(a_{1i})
at the points
a_{1i}
, for
1<i\leq n
, where
e^{-s\Delta_{i}^{(n)}}\tilde{q}^{(n)}_{i}(\zeta)
is the
n
-th order of
Q_{i}(\zeta)
. That is, the
(n+1)
-st order of
Q_{1}
is given by

(57)

\displaystyle\overset{n}{\underset{i=2}{\sum}}e^{-s(\Delta_{i}^{(n)}+r_{i1})}\tilde{q}_{i}^{(n)}(a_{1i})\prod_{k\neq 1,i}\frac{\zeta-a_{1k}}{a_{1i}-a_{1k}}.

The
(n+1)
-st order of

is similar, except each term has an extra factor to account for the vanishing conditions on the basis. Explicitly, the
n+1
-st order is

(58)

\displaystyle\overset{n}{\underset{i\neq j}{\sum}}e^{-s(\Delta_{i}^{(n)}+r_{ij})}\tilde{q}_{i}^{(n)}(a_{ji})\frac{\zeta}{a_{ji}}\prod_{k\neq j,i}\frac{\zeta-a_{jk}}{a_{ji}-a_{jk}}.

The points for the Lagrangian interpolation for the
(n+1)
-st order of
Q_{j}
comes from the matching conditions

(59)

\displaystyle Q_{j}(a_{ji})=e^{-sr_{ij}}Q_{i}(a_{ji}).

In general, for the

is either kept or eliminated according to the vanishing conditions. The limit as

of the matrix
