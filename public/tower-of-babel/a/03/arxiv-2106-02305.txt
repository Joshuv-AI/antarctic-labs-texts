Local Adaptivity in Federated Learning: Convergence and Consistency
Jianyu Wang, Zheng Xu, Zachary Garrett, Zachary Charles, Luyang Liu, Gauri Joshi

Abstract
The federated learning (FL) framework trains a machine learning model using decentralized data stored at edge client devices by periodically aggregating locally trained models. Popular optimization algorithms of FL use vanilla (stochastic) gradient descent for both local updates at clients and global updates at the aggregating server. Recently, adaptive optimization methods such as AdaGrad have been studied for server updates. However, the effect of using adaptive optimization methods for local updates at clients is not yet understood. We show in both theory and practice that while local adaptive methods can accelerate convergence, they can cause a non-vanishing solution bias, where the final converged solution may be different from the stationary point of the global objective function. We propose correction techniques to overcome this inconsistency and complement the local adaptive methods for FL. Extensive experiments on realistic federated training tasks show that the proposed algorithms can achieve faster convergence and higher test accuracy than the baselines without local adaptivity.



1
Introduction

Federated learning (FL) is an emerging paradigm to perform distributed machine learning model training on decentralized edge clients (
e.g.,
mobile phones or IoT devices) under the orchestration of a central server, while keeping private training data on the client devices
[
1
]
. In the cross-device FL setting, a global model is usually trained by a collaborative process of thousands or even millions of participating clients. In each training round of FL, the central server broadcasts the global model to a random subset of clients to perform local model training, and each participated client only uploads the model parameter changes back to the server. Then, the server aggregates local changes to update the global model and continues the next round. This FL training procedure was proposed by
McMahan et al. 2017
as the
federated averaging
(
FedAvg
) algorithm, and widely applied in diverse applications
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.

Recently,
Reddi et al. 2020
proposed a generalization of
FedAvg
referred to as
FedOpt
.
FedOpt
is a flexible algorithmic framework that allows the clients and the server to choose different optimization methods (which are referred to as
ClientOpt
and
ServerOpt
) more general than stochastic gradient descent (SGD) in
FedAvg
. The key idea is to treat the aggregated local changes from clients as a “pseudo-gradient” and use it as input to
ServerOpt
when updating the global model. A few previous works explored the choices of server optimizer (
ServerOpt
) in the
FedOpt
framework. For example,
Hsu et al. 2019
,
Wang et al. 2020a
used SGD with momentum at the server while keeping client optimizer as SGD and observed significant empirical improvements.

Adaptive methods, such as
AdaGrad

[
9
,
10
]
,
Adam

[
11
]
, have achieved superior empirical performance over SGD in centralized training of machine learning models for some applications. In particular,
Adam
and its variants
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
are widely recognized as the preferred optimizers for language-related training tasks. Motivated by their superior performance,
Reddi et al. 2020
studied a specific class of
FedOpt
, where
ClientOpt
is still SGD but
ServerOpt
uses adaptive methods. It has been validated through extensive experiments in
[
6
,
15
]
that the server-only adaptive methods can achieve faster convergence than vanilla
FedAvg
in many federated training tasks.

Despite the success of using server-side adaptive methods, the power of adaptivity has not been fully utilized in the
FedOpt
framework, particularly on the client side. Instead of using adaptive methods only in
ServerOpt
for periodical updates per communication round, one can also choose to use adaptive methods in
ClientOpt
for every local iteration, or in both
ClientOpt
and
ServerOpt
. Client adaptive methods can accelerate the local convergence by better utilizing the geometric information of the local objectives. Unfortuantely, there are little to no literature exploring this promising direction. It remains an open problem whether the faster local convergence can be translated into faster global convergence and savings in communication rounds.

In this paper, we explore the usage of local adaptive methods and provide affirmative answers to the question:
Can federated learning effectively use adaptive optimization methods on local clients?

Specifically, our main contributions are listed as follows.

1. We identify that naively changing
ClientOpt
in the
FedOpt
framework from SGD to adaptive methods or other stateful optimizers can be problematic, as it does not define how to update client optimizer states (
i.e.,
pre-conditioners and momentum buffers) across rounds. We propose to overcome this discontinuity issue by restarting the update of client optimizer states at the beginning of each round. This simple method enables the usage of local adaptivity in
FedOpt
. We also show that, on many practical training tasks, using adaptive optimizers on clients can achieve faster convergence and higher test accuracy than previous methods.

2. We further provide a theoretical analysis for strongly convex functions to understand the effects of various client optimizers. Our theorem suggests that performing
FedOpt
can be viewed as finding the fixed points of some operator
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}]
, the expression of which is determined by client optimizers. Local adaptivity can change
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}]
in a way that yields faster convergence. However, this enhanced optimization may come with the cost of a
non-vanishing
solution bias – the point we converge to may be far away from the global minimizer of the original objective function, as illustrated in
Figure

1
.

3. In order to overcome the side effects of using adaptive client optimizers, we propose (1) local correction technique that can help to mitigate the non-vanishing solution bias; (2) global correction technique that can help to perserve the fast convergence property of adaptive methods. Using one or both of them on the top of adaptive client optimizers can achieve the highest test accuracy on all considered federated training tasks.

also study the phenomenon that
FedAvg

Section

FedOpt
framework, there are many more building blocks that can accelerate the convergence or improve the practical performance, for instance, using control variates

,

,

, regularizing the local objective functions

,

, sampling important clients more frequently

,

,

2
Preliminaries

The Federated Learning Setup.

In federated learning, the goal is to minimize the objective

\displaystyle F(\bm{x})={\mathbb{E}}_{i\sim\mathcal{C}}[F_{i}(\bm{x})]

(1)

where
F_{i}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{i}|}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{D}_{i}}f_{i}(\bm{x},\xi)
denotes the local objective function at client
i
, and
f_{i}(\bm{x},\xi)
is the loss function where
\xi
represents one data sample from the local dataset. In contrast to classic distributed learning, federated learning does not allow clients to share their local data with others or with the central server. As a consequence, the local data distributions
\mathcal{D}_{i}
may differ across clients. The term
\mathcal{C}
denotes the distribution over
the collection of
all clients. The probability of selecting client
i
is
w_{i}=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{C}}(i)
, which is proportional to the dataset size of client
i
. Thus, for a finite set of
M
clients, the objective function becomes
F(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}F_{i}(\bm{x})
.

Operator View of Client Optimizers.
In this paper, we are going to use operators to represent the general optimizers on clients. In particular, for each client
i
, we have the following definition.

Definition 1

(Client Optimizer Operator)
.

We define an operator
\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x};k,\Xi_{i},\bm{s}_{i}):\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which outputs the updated model after performing
k
steps of a given client optimizer on the local objective
F_{i}
, from initial model
\bm{x}
, with initial optimizer states
\bm{s}_{i}
and random sources
\Xi_{i}
(e.g., noise in stochastic gradients).

The operator
\mathcal{A}_{i}
defines how the local models and the client optimizer states (
i.e.,
pre-conditioners and momentum buffers) are updated. When the parameters
k,\bm{s}_{i},\Xi_{i}
are given, the output of
\mathcal{A}_{i}
will only depend on the starting point
\bm{x}
. The specific form of
\mathcal{A}_{i}
changes with the choice of optimizers and its analytical expression can be complicated. For example, when the client optimizer is pre-conditioned SGD (a generalized version of
AdaGrad
)
[
30
]
, we have

\displaystyle\textstyle\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x};k,\Xi_{i},\bm{s}_{i})=\bm{x}-\eta_{i}\sum_{s=0}^{k-1}\bm{P}_{i}^{(s)}g_{i}(\bm{x}^{(s)};\xi^{(s)})

(2)

where
\eta_{i}
is the client learning rate,
\bm{x}^{(s)}
is the local model after
s
local iterations,
g(\bm{x}^{(s)};\xi^{(s)})
denotes the stochastic gradient evaluated on a random mini-batch
\xi^{(s)}
, and
\bm{P}_{i}^{(s)}
is referred to as the
pre-conditioner
. In vanilla SGD, the pre-conditioner is just the identity matrix; In
AdaGrad
, the pre-conditioner is updated as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{P}_{i}^{(s)}=((\bm{P}_{i}^{(s-1)})^{-2}+\diag\{g(\bm{x}^{(k)};\xi^{(k)})g(\bm{x}^{(k)};\xi^{(k)})^{\top}\})^{-\frac{1}{2}}

(3)

where
\bm{P}_{i}^{(-1)}=\bm{s}_{i}
is the initial optimizer states. In this example, the random sources
\Xi_{i}=\{\xi^{(s)}\}
represents all randomness in the stochastic gradients.

Operator View of
FedOpt
.

Based on
1
, at each round
t
of
FedOpt
, the server broadcasts the global model
\bm{x}^{(t)}
as an initial point to clients. Then, client
i
uses
\mathcal{A}_{i}
to obtain the locally updated model as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{x}_{i}^{(t,\tau_{i})}=\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x}^{(t)};\tau_{i},\Xi_{i}^{(t)},\bm{s}_{i}^{(t)}).

(4)

After that, the server aggregates local models changes
\bm{x}^{(t)}-\bm{x}_{i}^{(t,\tau_{i})}
and uses
ServerOpt
to update the global model to
\bm{x}^{(t+1)}
. Since the server optimizer does not influence the properties of
\mathcal{A}_{i}
and the main purpose of this paper is to understand the effects of client optimizers, we assume
ServerOpt
is gradient descent (GD) unless stated otherwise, which means that each client participates every round and there is no server pre-conditioner. Our results can be naturally extended to other server optimizers and sampled clients without changing the main insights on client optimizers. Then, the GD-based global update rule of
FedOpt
can be written as

\displaystyle\textstyle\bm{x}^{(t+1)}=\bm{x}^{(t)}-\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}[\bm{x}^{(t)}-\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x}^{(t)};\tau_{i},\Xi_{i}^{(t)},\bm{s}_{i}^{(t)})]

(5)

where
\alpha
denotes the server learning rate for
ServerOpt
.

3
How to Apply Adaptive Methods on Clients

In this section, we describe the challenges of using local adaptivity in
FedOpt
and propose a simple, yet effective method to enable the usage of local adaptivity in
FedOpt
without incurring additional communication costs.

Challenge in
FedOpt
: Discontinuity of Client Optimizer States.

According to the update rules
Equations

4
and
5
of
FedOpt
, we observe that
FedOpt
can not be directly applied to adaptive client optimizers, as it lacks the articulation of handling the initial optimizer states
\bm{s}_{i}
across rounds and clients.
To be specific, in
FedOpt
, the client updates are isolated not only from each other but also from different rounds due to the local iterations. While
FedOpt
uses
ServerOpt
to bridge the gap between local iterates in different rounds (
i.e.,
it specifies how to obtain
\bm{x}^{(t+1)}
from
\{\bm{x}_{i}^{(t,\tau_{i})}\}
), it does not define how to update the initial optimizer states
\bm{s}_{i}
across rounds. We refer to this problem as
discontinuity of client optimizer states
.

A natural but impractical idea to tackle the discontinuity problem is to let each client inherit its own client optimizer states from previous rounds. However, these previous states can be stale and inaccurate because they are evaluated at point
\bm{x}_{i}^{(t-1,\tau_{i})}
and do not take account of the possibly dramatic changes from
\bm{x}_{i}^{(t-1,\tau_{i})}
to the current iterate
\bm{x}^{(t)}
. When the server only selects a random subset of clients at each round, the staleness will be further exacerbated, as one client may be disconnected from the server for multiple rounds. Besides, this solution is impossible in many applications of FL (
e.g.,
the cross-device setting) that requires clients to be stateless and not to maintain any persistent states across rounds, due to privacy and system constraints
[
1
]
.
Furthermore, one can also choose to synchronize the local optimizer states at the end of each round and use the synchronized states as the initial value for next round. For example,
Yu et al. 2019
has used a similar strategy to synchronize momentum buffer when the clients use momentum SGD. However, in order to aggregate and broadcast the additional optimizer states, this strategy would incur doubled or tripled communication costs compared to
FedOpt
with vanilla SGD client optimizer.

Our Proposal: Restarting Client Optimizer States.

To work with the above challenges, we propose a simple yet effective approach. Instead of applying potentially complicated mechanisms to synchronize optimizer states across clients, we restart the updates of client optimizer states at the beginning of each round. That is, resetting the pre-conditioners to a constant and resetting the momentum buffers to zero. This strategy does not require any information from previous rounds, so it is compatible with application settings that require stateless clients. Also, it does not incur addition communication costs, as only the local model changes are aggregated.

To verify the advantage of this simple strategy, in
Figure

1(b)
, we provide simulation results on a toy problem of quadratic functions with deterministic gradients. The result shows the restarting strategy on local
AdaGrad
(
i.e.,
the green curve) can achieve significantly faster convergence speed, comparing to vanilla
FedAvg
(
i.e.,
the blue curve) when the tolerance to the global optimal is relatively large. The fast convergence is also observed on practical federated training tasks (see
Figure

2
and more discussions in
Section

6
).

4
Effects of Client Optimizers on Convergence and Consistency

Another important observation from the toy example in
Figure

1(b)
is that local
AdaGrad
converges to a point which is quite far away from the global optimum. Even if we decay the client learning rate, this inconsistency problem still cannot be mitigated or becomes even worse. In this section, we will theoretically analyze the effect of client optimizers on the convergence and provide insights into the trade-off between convergence speed and the consistency of the solution.

FedOpt
Performs Fixed Point Iteration.

When using the restarting strategy proposed in
Section

3
, the argument
\bm{s}_{i}
representing the initial optimizer state in the operator
\mathcal{A}_{i}
can be omitted since it is set to its default initial value and is the same on all clients. We further define an operator
\mathcal{A}(\bm{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x};\tau_{i},\Xi_{i})
. Accordingly, the global update rule (see Eqn.
Equation

5
) of
FedOpt
is equivalent to a stochastic fixed point iteration, shown as follows:

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{(t+1)}=(1-\alpha)\bm{x}^{(t)}+\alpha{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}(\bm{x}^{(t)})]+\alpha(\mathcal{A}(\bm{x}^{(t)})-{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}(\bm{x}^{(t)})])

(6)

where
\alpha
denotes the server learning rate and the expectation is taken over all random sources in local stochastic gradients at the current round. The deterministic version of
Equation

6
is known as Mann’s iteration
[
32
]
and will converge to the fixed points of
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}]
under certain conditions. Replacing server GD with another server optimizer can accelerate the convergence but it will not influence the properties and the fixed points of the operator
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}]
.

We will analyze the convergence of
FedOpt
(update rule of which is
Equation

6
) in the setting where each local objective function
F_{i}(\bm{x})
is
L_{i}
-Lipschitz smooth and
\mu_{i}
-strongly convex. Besides, we make the following assumptions on the properties of each client optimizer
\mathcal{A}_{i}
and show how the global convergence is influenced by these properties. If the client optimizer is SGD, then the contractive local operator and bounded cumulative variance properties described below follow directly from the strong convexity and smoothness of the objective function, suggesting the following assumptions are reasonable and mild.

Assumption 1

(Contractive Local Operator)
.

The local expected operator
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}_{i}]
is contractive, satisfying that
\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x};k,\Xi)]-{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{y};k,\Xi)]\right\|^{2}\leq h_{i}(k)\cdot\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\|^{2}
for any
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
0<h_{i}(k)<1
is a decreasing function of the number of local steps
k
.

Assumption 2

(Bounded Cumulative Variance)
.

We assume the local stochastic gradient
g_{i}
is an unbiased estimator of
\nabla F_{i}
and has bounded variance:
{\mathbb{E}}\left\|g_{i}(\bm{x})-\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x})\right\|^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}
. Similarly, the local operator
\mathcal{A}_{i}
has bounded cumulative variance:
{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x};k,\Xi)-{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x};k,\Xi)]\right\|^{2}\leq q_{i}(k)\cdot\sigma^{2}
, where
q_{i}(k)>0
is a non-decreasing function of the number of local steps
k
.

The function
h_{i}(k)
in
1
measures the local training progress of
ClientOpt
. In intuition, when
ClientOpt
has faster convergence rate or takes more local steps, the output of
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}_{i}]
will be closer to the minimum
\bm{x}_{i}^{*}
, and hence
h_{i}
will become smaller. Besides, the function
q_{i}(k)
in
2
quantifies how the noise in stochastic gradients are accumulated through
k
local updates. The analytical forms of
h_{i},q_{i}
depend on the choice of
ClientOpt
. For example, when the client optimizer is SGD with fixed client learning rate, we formally prove in
Appendix

B
that
h_{i}(k)=(1-\eta_{i}\mu_{i})^{2k}
and
q_{i}(k)=\eta_{i}^{2}k
. For adaptive optimizers, such as
AdaGrad
and
Adam
, while it is difficult to obtain the analytical form of
h_{i}
, we empirically validate
1
in
Section

B.4
and found they can yield smaller
h_{i}
values than vanilla SGD.

Theorem 1

(Convergence of
FedOpt
and Minimizer Inconsistency)
.

Under
1
and
2
, the operator
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}]
is contractive and has a unique fixed point
\widetilde{\bm{x}}
. After total
T
communication rounds, the global iterates of
FedOpt
converge to
\widetilde{\bm{x}}
at the following rate for some positive constant
c
:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|\bm{x}^{(T)}-\widetilde{\bm{x}}\|^{2}\leq c\cdot\left(\left[\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}h_{i}\right]^{T}\frac{\|\bm{x}^{(0)}-\widetilde{\bm{x}}\|^{2}}{1-\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}h_{i}}+\frac{1}{T}\frac{\sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}^{2}q_{i}}{(1-\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}h_{i})^{2}}\right).

(7)

In the presence of data heterogeneity, the fixed point
\widetilde{\bm{x}}
of
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}]
is not necessarily the same as the optimum
\bm{x}^{*}
of the global objective
F
. We refer to this problem as minimizer inconsistency.

Better Client Optimizers Can Accelerate the Global Convergence.

1
provides a nice connection between local and global convergence. Specifically, if clients use better client optimizers that have smaller contraction constant
h_{i}
, then the worst-case global convergence to the fixed point
\widetilde{\bm{x}}
can be improved, as the error bound
Equation

7
monotonically increases with
h_{i}
. Therefore, a natural strategy to speedup the global convergence is to separately minimize
h_{i}
on each client while keeping
q_{i}
unchanged. One can achieve this by either tuning a proper learning rate for each client, or using adaptive client optimizers which could reduce the effective local condition number. Intuitively, using better client optimizers makes the operator
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}]
to be more contractive, and hence, it becomes easier to find its fixed points (as corroborated in
Figure

1(a)
, the loss surface is better conditioned when using local
AdaGrad
). This acceleration effects is complementary to that of the server optimizer, which changes the form of the fixed point iteration without affecting
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}]
. Therefore, one can use both better client and server optimizers to get the best performance, as we will later illustrate in
Section

6
.

The Minimizer Inconsistency Problem.

1
also shows that the speedup effects of client optimizers may come with a price: the fixed point
\widetilde{\bm{x}}
can be different from the optimal minimizer
\bm{x}^{*}
. When the objective function is quadratic,
ClientOpt
is GD and all clients have the same learning rates
\eta
and number of local steps
\tau
, it has been shown in
[
19
,
20
]
that
\|\widetilde{\bm{x}}-\bm{x}^{*}\|=\mathcal{O}(\eta(\tau-1))
such that one can balance the trade-off between convergence speed and the minimizer inconsistency by gradually decaying the client learning rate. However, through a simple counterexample below, we will show that
using adaptive optimizers or different hyper-parameters on clients leads to an additional gap between
\widetilde{\bm{x}}
and
\bm{x}^{*}
, which does not vanish to zero with the learning rate
. This non-vanishing bias explains our empirical observations in
Figure

1(b)
and calls for new techniques to overcome it.

Quadratic Example for
1
.

In this toy example, we assume the loss function for client
i
is given by
F_{i}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{2}\bm{x}^{\top}\bm{H}_{i}\bm{x}-\bm{e}_{i}^{\top}\bm{x}+\bm{c}_{i}
, where
\bm{H}_{i}
is symmetric and positive definite and
\bm{e}_{i},\bm{c}_{i}
are some vectors. Accordingly, the minimum of
F_{i}
is simply
\bm{x}^{*}_{i}=\bm{H}_{i}^{-1}\bm{e}_{i}
and the minimum of the global objective is
\bm{x}^{*}=(\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\bm{H}_{i})^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\bm{H}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{*}
.

Theorem 2
.

For the quadratic problem, if the client optimizer is preconditioned GD with a fixed pre-conditioner
\bm{P}_{i}
, then
\widetilde{\bm{x}}=(\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}[\bm{I}-\bm{K}_{i}])^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}[(\bm{I}-\bm{K}_{i})\bm{x}_{i}^{*}]\neq\bm{x}^{*}
, where
\bm{K}_{i}=(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i})^{\tau_{i}}
. If we let
\eta_{i}=\gamma_{i}\eta
and
\eta
approach to zero, then it follows that

\displaystyle\textstyle\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\widetilde{\bm{x}}=(\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\gamma_{i}\tau_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i})^{-1}(\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\gamma_{i}\tau_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{*})\neq\bm{x}^{*}.

(8)

2
shows that as long as one of the factors (
i.e.,
client learning rate, the number of local steps, the pre-conditioners) is different across clients, then there would appear a non-vanishing gap between
\widetilde{\bm{x}}
and
\bm{x}^{*}
. In order to get an intuition behind this phenomenon, we can check the exact expression of
\bm{x}^{*}-\mathcal{A}(\bm{x}^{*})
. If
\widetilde{\bm{x}}=\bm{x}^{*}
, then
\bm{x}^{*}-\mathcal{A}(\bm{x}^{*})
should always be zero. However, note that

\displaystyle\textstyle{\bm{x}^{*}-\mathcal{A}(\bm{x}^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}[\bm{I}-(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i})^{\tau_{i}}]\bm{H}_{i}^{-1}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{*}).}

(9)

In Eqn.
Equation

9
,
each local objective’s gradient is implicitly weighted by a matrix

\bm{M}_{i}=[\bm{I}-(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i})^{\tau_{i}}]\bm{H}_{i}^{-1}
. The aggregated local changes
\bm{x}^{*}-\mathcal{A}(\bm{x}^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\bm{M}_{i}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{*})
can be consisdered as a skewed version of the global gradient
\nabla F(\bm{x}^{*})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{*})=0
. Only if
\bm{M}_{i}=\bm{M}_{j}
for all client pairs
i,j
, we can conclude that
\bm{x}^{*}=\mathcal{A}(\bm{x}^{*})
and hence
\widetilde{\bm{x}}=\bm{x}^{*}
. However, due to the data heterogeneity, the condition
\bm{M}_{i}=\bm{M}_{j}
does not hold. Furthermore, if we omit higher order terms
\eta_{i}^{2}
in
Equation

9
, then we get the following via Taylor approximation:

\displaystyle\textstyle[\bm{x}^{*}-\mathcal{A}(\bm{x}^{*})]/(\max_{i}\eta_{i}\tau_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\frac{\eta_{i}\tau_{i}\bm{P}_{i}}{\max_{i}\eta_{i}\tau_{i}}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{*})+\mathcal{O}(\eta_{i}\tau_{i})

(10)

where the first term (on the right hand side) corresponds to the non-vanishing gap between
\widetilde{\bm{x}}
and
\bm{x}^{*}
, and the second term can be omitted when the client learning rate is sufficiently small. It is worth noting that if
\eta_{i}=0
or
\tau_{i}=0
, then
\bm{x}=\mathcal{A}(\bm{x})
for any
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, as there is no optimization progress at all. Therefore, in order to exclude this invalid solution, we divide
(\max_{i}\eta_{i}\tau_{i})
in
Equation

10
.

This toy example also illustrates how local adaptivity can accelerate convergence. In particular, when the local pre-conditioners are ideal (
i.e.,
inverse of local Hessians),
FedOpt
can converge to
\widetilde{\bm{x}}
in just one round by choosing proper server and client learning rates. But if the local pre-conditioner are the same across all clients (
\bm{P}_{i}=\bm{P}
), then the convergence of
FedOpt
will depend on the condition numbers of
\{\bm{P}\bm{H}_{i}\}
and require multiple rounds.

Connections with Previous Works on Minimizer Inconsistency.

When client learning rates, number of local steps are the same across all clients, and non-adaptive, deterministic
ClientOpt
are used,
\|\widetilde{\bm{x}}-\bm{x}^{*}\|
can vanish to zero along with the learning rates. This phenomenon has been observed and analyzed by few recent literature in different forms, see
[
19
,
21
,
20
]
.
1
generalizes these results by allowing heterogeneous local hyper-parameters and adaptive, stochastic client optimizers. In addition, the non-vanishing bias was studied in
[
18
]
by assuming different local learning rates and local steps at clients. In this paper, we further generalize the results by showing that even when the learning rates and local steps are the same, using local adaptive methods will lead to a non-vanishing gap. We summarize the differences in

of

5
Correction Techniques to Overcome the Non-vanishing Solution Bias

As we discussed in
Section

4
, while using faster client optimizers and exploiting local structures can help improve the global convergence, this strategy results in a non-vanishing gap between the converged point
\widetilde{\bm{x}}
and the optimal solution
\bm{x}^{*}
. Inspired by the quadratic example in
Section

4
, we propose (i) a local correction method that reweights local gradients before sending the client updates to the server in order to overcome the non-vanishing part of minimizer inconsistency, and (ii) a global correction method applied by the server when aggregating the client updates in order to preserve fast convergence.

Local Correction: Reweighting Local Gradients.

From
Equation

10
in the quadratic example, one can observe that the non-vanishing minimizer inconsistency comes from the fact that
FedOpt

implicitly and improperly
weights each local gradients by a matrix
\bm{N}_{i}=\eta_{i}\tau_{i}\bm{P}_{i}
. So a natural solution to overcome the non-vanishing bias is to normalize the local model changes before aggregating them on the server. Instead of sending
\bm{x}-\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x})=\bm{N}_{i}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x})+\mathcal{O}(\eta_{i}^{2})
to the server, each client can send the locally normalized version
\bm{N}_{i}^{-1}(\bm{x}-\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x}))
. This simple change can ensure that the first term in
Equation

10
is always zero no matter which value is the client learning rate. As a consequence, there is no non-vanishing solution bias. The local correction technique also automatically avoids the invalid solution
\bm{x}=\mathcal{A}(\bm{x}),\forall\bm{x}
by dividing the client learning rate.

The local correction technique can be extended to more general settings and work with any adaptive optimizers, such as
AdaGrad
,
Adam
. If the local model changes of a specific client optimizer can be written as

\bm{x}-\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x};\tau_{i})=\eta_{i}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{i}-1}\bm{B}_{i}^{(k)}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})
where
\bm{B}_{i}^{(k)}
is symmetric and positive-definite and
\bm{x}^{(k)}
denotes the
k
-th iterate during local updates, then we can choose the local correction matrix to be
\bm{N}_{i}=\eta_{i}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{i}-1}\bm{B}_{i}^{(k)}
such that

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\bm{N}_{i}^{-1}(\bm{x}^{*}-\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x}^{*}))=\underbrace{\textstyle{\sum_{i=1}^{M}}w_{i}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{*})}_{=\nabla F(\bm{x}^{*})=0}+\mathcal{O}(\eta_{i}\tau_{i})=\mathcal{O}(\eta_{i}\tau_{i}).

(11)

If the client optimizer is
AdaGrad
, then
\bm{B}_{i}^{(k)}
is just the pre-conditioner
\bm{P}_{i}^{(k)}
used at the
k
-th local iteration; if the client optimizer is
Adam
, then
\bm{B}_{i}^{(k)}
is a weighted summation of local pre-conditioners. In
Section

A.3
, we provide the pseudo-code of finding the expression of
\bm{N}_{i}
for common optimizers; and in
Appendix

F
, we formally prove that local correction can help
FedOpt
with deterministic client optimizers converge to the stationary points of the original objective function (even when it is non-convex). For stochastic client optimizers, we empirically validate the effectiveness of local correction through extensive experiments in
Section

6
.

Global Correction: Preserving the Fast Convergence.
Equation
Equation

11
shows that using the local correction technique may change the scale of the aggregated model updates. In particular, the first-order approximation of the aggregated model updates just equals to plain gradients (the first term in
Equation

11
) and may lose the local pre-conditioning effects. In order to address this problem, one can use either adaptive server optimizer that are more robust to the scale of its inputs, or a novel global correction technique that can help to preserve the scale of local updates. To be specific, in global correction, the server uses
\bm{N}_{s}^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\bm{N}_{i}^{-1}[\bm{x}-\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x})]
as the pseudo-gradient of
ServerOpt
, where
\bm{N}_{s}
is given as
\bm{N}_{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\bm{N}_{i}^{-1}
. There may also exist other better choices of the matrix
\bm{N}_{s}
. As long as
\bm{N}_{s}
does not depend on the client index
i
, it will not influence the fixed point, to which the algorithm converge. We name this technique as global correction, because it is applied on the server side. In order to obtain
\bm{N}_{s}
, the clients need to send the
local correction matrices
\bm{N}_{i}
to the server
. But the server does not need to broadcast the correction matrix
\bm{N}_{s}
back. Therefore, if matrices
\{\bm{N}_{i}\}
are diagonal, then the communication cost per round of using global correction is only
1.5\times
than that of without using it.

7
Conclusions

In this paper, we first propose techniques that enable the use of adaptive optimization methods for local updates at clients in federated learning. Through the analysis on effects of client optimizers for smooth and strongly convex functions, we show that although local adaptive methods can accelerate the convergence in some scenarios, it introduces an additional non-vanishing gap between the converged point and the optimal solution. To mitigate the side effects of using local adaptivity, we further propose local and global correction techniques. We verified the advantages of the proposed methods regarding fast convergence, better test accuracy with extensive experiments on benchmark datasets.

Two Useful Lemmas

Before we dive into the proofs, we would like to first introduce two useful lemmas, which will be repeatedly applied later on.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose function
F
is twice-differentiable. Then, for any points
\bm{x},\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla F(\bm{x})-\nabla F(\bm{y})=\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})

(12)

where
\bm{H}=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}F(\bm{y}+s(\bm{x}-\bm{y}))\mathrm{d}s
.

Proof.

Due to the linearity of integral, we have

\displaystyle\nabla F(\bm{x})-\nabla F(\bm{y})=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}F(\bm{y}+s(\bm{x}-\bm{y}))(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\mathrm{d}s=\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y}).

(13)

∎

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
are random variables with the same random sources
\xi
. Then, if a function
F
is
L
-lipschitz smooth and
\mu
-strongly convex, then we have

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F(\bm{x})-\nabla F(\bm{y})]\right\|^{2}\geq

\displaystyle\mu^{2}\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}-\bm{y}]\right\|^{2}

(14)

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F(\bm{x})-\nabla F(\bm{y})]\right\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle L\left<{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}-\bm{y}]},\,{{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F(\bm{x})-\nabla F(\bm{y})]}\right>

(15)

\displaystyle(\mu+L)\left<{{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F(\bm{x})-\nabla F(\bm{y})]},\,{{\mathbb{E}}[(\bm{x}-\bm{y})]}\right>\geq

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F(\bm{x})-\nabla F(\bm{y})]\right\|^{2}+\mu L\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}-\bm{y}]\right\|^{2}

(16)

Proof.

Based on lemma 1, we have
{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F(\bm{x})-\nabla F(\bm{y})]={\mathbb{E}}[\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})]
. And hence,

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F(\bm{x})-\nabla F(\bm{y})]\right\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\left<{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})]},\,{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})]}\right>

(17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left<{\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})},\,{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})]}\right>.

(18)

Since
\mu\preccurlyeq\bm{H}\preccurlyeq L
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mu\left<{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}-\bm{y}]},\,{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})]}\right>\leq{\mathbb{E}}\left<{\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})},\,{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})]}\right>\leq L\left<{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}-\bm{y}]},\,{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})]}\right>.

(19)

Combining

18
and
19
, we prove the second inequality in Lemma 2. Furthermore, note that

\displaystyle\left<{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}-\bm{y}]},\,{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})]}\right>={\mathbb{E}}\left<{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}-\bm{y}]},\,{\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})}\right>\geq\mu{\mathbb{E}}\left<{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}-\bm{y}]},\,{(\bm{x}-\bm{y})}\right>.

(20)

We have

\displaystyle\left<{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})]},\,{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})]}\right>\geq\mu^{2}\left<{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}-\bm{y}]},\,{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}-\bm{y}]}\right>.

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[H(\bm{x}-\bm{y})-\mu(\bm{x}-\bm{y})]\right\|^{2}\leq

(22)

Besides, note that

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[H(\bm{x}-\bm{y})-\mu(\bm{x}-\bm{y})]\right\|^{2}=

\displaystyle-2\mu\left<{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})]},\,{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}-\bm{y}]}\right>.

(23)

22
and rearranging, we obtain that

\displaystyle(L+\mu)\left<{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})]},\,{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}-\bm{y}]}\right>\geq\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{H}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})]\right\|^{2}+\mu L\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}-\bm{y}]\right\|^{2}.

(24)

Here we complete the proof of the last inequality in Lemma 2.
∎

B.2
Proof of Assumption 1 for Vanilla SGD Client Optimizer

For the ease of writing, we denote
\bm{x}^{(k)}=\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x};k,\Xi)
and
\bm{y}^{(k)}=\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{y};k,\Xi)
where
\Xi=\{\xi^{(0)},\dots,\xi^{(k-1)}\}
represents the random sources through
k
local steps. According to the update rule of SGD, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k+1)}]-{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{y}^{(k+1)}]=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}-\eta g_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})]-{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{y}^{(k)}-\eta g_{i}(\bm{y}^{(k)})]

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}-\bm{y}^{(k)}]-\eta_{i}{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})-\nabla F_{i}(\bm{y}^{(k)})].

(26)

We are going to use
induction
to prove
\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}-\bm{y}^{(k)}]\right\|^{2}\leq(1-\eta_{i}\mu_{i})^{2k}\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\|^{2}
. When
k=1
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(1)}-\bm{y}^{(1)}]\right\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}-\eta_{i}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x})-\nabla F_{i}(\bm{y})\right\|^{2}

(27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\|^{2}+\eta_{i}^{2}\left\|\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x})-\nabla F_{i}(\bm{y})\right\|^{2}-2\eta_{i}\left<{\bm{x}-\bm{y}},\,{\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x})-\nabla F_{i}(\bm{y})}\right>

(28)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{2\mu_{i}L_{i}}{\mu_{i}+L_{i}}\right)\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\|^{2}+\left(\eta_{i}^{2}-\frac{2\eta_{i}}{\mu_{i}+L_{i}}\right)\left\|\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x})-\nabla F_{i}(\bm{y})\right\|^{2}

(29)

where the last inequality comes from the Lipschitz smoothness and strongly convexity of
F_{i}
. When the client learning rate satisfies
\eta_{i}(\mu_{i}+L_{i})<2
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(1)}-\bm{y}^{(1)}]\right\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{2\mu_{i}L_{i}}{\mu_{i}+L_{i}}\right)\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\|^{2}+\left(\eta_{i}^{2}\mu_{i}^{2}-\frac{2\mu_{i}\eta_{i}}{\mu_{i}+L_{i}}\right)\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\|^{2}

(30)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\eta_{i}\mu_{i})^{2}\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\|^{2}.

(31)

So Assumption 1 is true when
k=1
. Now we assume that Assumption 1 holds for some
k>1
and examine the value of
\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k+1)}-\bm{y}^{(k+1)}]\right\|
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k+1)}-\bm{y}^{(k+1)}]\right\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}-\bm{y}^{(k)}]\right\|^{2}+\eta_{i}^{2}\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})-\nabla F_{i}(\bm{y}^{(k)})]\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle-2\eta\left<{{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}-\bm{y}^{(k)}]},\,{{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})-\nabla F_{i}(\bm{y}^{(k)})]}\right>.

(32)

According to
Equations

14
and
16
of Lemma 2, we have

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k+1)}-\bm{y}^{(k+1)}]\right\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{2\eta_{i}\mu_{i}L_{i}}{\mu_{i}L_{i}}\right)\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}-\bm{y}^{(k)}]\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\left(\eta_{i}^{2}-\frac{2\eta_{i}}{\mu_{i}+L_{i}}\right)\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})-\nabla F_{i}(\bm{y}^{(k)})]\right\|^{2}

(33)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{2\eta_{i}\mu_{i}L_{i}}{\mu_{i}L_{i}}\right)\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}-\bm{y}^{(k)}]\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\left(\eta_{i}^{2}\mu_{i}^{2}-\frac{2\eta_{i}\mu_{i}^{2}}{\mu_{i}+L_{i}}\right)\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}-\bm{y}^{(k)}]\right\|^{2}

(34)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\eta_{i}\mu_{i})^{2}\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}-\bm{y}^{(k)}]\right\|^{2}

(35)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\eta_{i}\mu_{i})^{2(k+1)}\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\|^{2}.

(36)

Here we complete the induction procedure and prove that Assumption 1 holds for vanilla SGD client optimizer and
h_{i}(k)=(1-\eta_{i}\mu_{i})^{2k}
.

B.3
Proof of Assumption 2 for Vanilla SGD Client Optimizer

For the ease of writing, we define
\bm{x}^{(k)}=\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x};k)
and
\overline{\bm{x}}^{(k)}={\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}]
. We are going to use induction to prove that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\bm{x}^{(k)}-\overline{\bm{x}}^{(k)}\right\|^{2}\leq k\eta_{i}^{2}\sigma^{2}.

(37)

When
k=1
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\bm{x}^{(1)}-\overline{\bm{x}}^{(1)}\right\|^{2}={\mathbb{E}}\left\|-\eta_{i}g_{i}(\bm{x};\xi)+\eta_{i}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x})\right\|^{2}\leq\eta_{i}^{2}\sigma^{2}.

(38)

We assume that
2
holds for some
k>1
. Then, according to the update rule of SGD, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\bm{x}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k+1)}]\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\bm{x}^{(k)}-\eta_{i}g_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})-{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}]+\eta_{i}{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})]\right\|^{2}

(39)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\|-\eta_{i}g_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})+\eta_{i}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})+\bm{x}^{(k)}-\eta_{i}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})-{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}]+\eta_{i}{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})]\right\|^{2}

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{i}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left\|g_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})-\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})\right\|^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\bm{x}^{(k)}-\eta_{i}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})-{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}]+\eta_{i}{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})]\right\|^{2}

(41)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\eta_{i}^{2}\sigma^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\bm{x}^{(k)}-\eta_{i}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})-{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}]+\eta_{i}{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})]\right\|^{2}

(42)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\eta_{i}^{2}\sigma^{2}+{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\bm{x}^{(k)}-{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}]\right\|^{2}+\eta_{i}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})-{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})]\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle-2\eta_{i}\left<{\bm{x}^{(k)}-{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}]},\,{\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})-{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})]}\right>

(43)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(k+1)\eta_{i}^{2}\sigma^{2}+\eta_{i}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})-{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})]\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle-2\eta_{i}{\mathbb{E}}\left<{\bm{x}^{(k)}-{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}]},\,{\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})-{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})]}\right>.

(44)

Then, we define
\epsilon=\bm{x}^{(k)}-\overline{\bm{x}}^{(k)}
. Accordingly, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})-{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})]\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\epsilon}\left\|\nabla F_{i}(\overline{\bm{x}}^{(k)}+\epsilon)-{\mathbb{E}}_{\zeta}[\nabla F_{i}(\overline{\bm{x}}^{(k)}+\zeta)]\right\|^{2}

(45)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\epsilon}\left\|{\mathbb{E}}_{\zeta}[\nabla F_{i}(\overline{\bm{x}}^{(k)}+\epsilon)-\nabla F_{i}(\overline{\bm{x}}^{(k)}+\zeta)]\right\|^{2}

(46)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L_{i}{\mathbb{E}}_{\epsilon}\left<{{\mathbb{E}}_{\zeta}[\epsilon-\zeta]},\,{{\mathbb{E}}_{\zeta}[\nabla F_{i}(\overline{\bm{x}}^{(k)}+\epsilon)-\nabla F_{i}(\overline{\bm{x}}^{(k)}+\zeta)]}\right>

(47)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L_{i}{\mathbb{E}}_{\epsilon}\left<{\epsilon},\,{{\mathbb{E}}_{\zeta}[\nabla F_{i}(\overline{\bm{x}}^{(k)}+\epsilon)-\nabla F_{i}(\overline{\bm{x}}^{(k)}+\zeta)]}\right>

(48)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle L_{i}{\mathbb{E}}\left<{\bm{x}^{(k)}-{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}]},\,{\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})-{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})]}\right>

(49)

where
Equation

47
is because of the second inequality
Equation

15
in Lemma 2. As a consequence, when
\eta_{i}L_{i}<2
, we have

\displaystyle\eta_{i}{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})-{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})]\right\|^{2}\leq 2\eta_{i}{\mathbb{E}}\left<{\bm{x}^{(k)}-{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k)}]},\,{\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})-{\mathbb{E}}[\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})]}\right>.

(50)

Substituting
Equation

50

Equation

44
, it follows that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\bm{x}^{(k+1)}-{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}^{(k+1)}]\right\|^{2}\leq(k+1)\eta_{i}^{2}\sigma^{2}.

(51)

Here we complete the induction and prove that
2
holds for SGD and

.

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem 1

We first prove
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}]
is a contractive operator. Based on its definition, we have

\displaystyle\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}(\bm{x})]-{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}(\bm{y})]\right\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x})-\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{y})]\right\|^{2}

(52)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x})-\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{y})]\right\|^{2}

(53)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}h_{i}\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\|^{2}

(54)

where
Equation

53
comes from the Jensen’s inequality and
Equation

54
is based on Assumption 1. Since
0\leq\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}h_{i}<1
, according to the Banach fixed-point theorem
[
32
]
, operator
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}]
is contractive and has a unique fixed point, denoted by
\widetilde{\bm{x}}
.

Then, according to the global update rule
Equation

6
of
FedOpt
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\bm{x}^{(t+1)}-\widetilde{\bm{x}}\right\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\left\|(1-\alpha)(\bm{x}^{(t)}-\widetilde{\bm{x}})+\alpha({\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}(\bm{x}^{(t)})]-\widetilde{\bm{x}})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x}^{(t)})-{\mathbb{E}}[\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\mathcal{A}(\bm{x}^{(t)})]\right\|^{2}.

(55)

The first term in
Equation

55
can be bounded as follows

\displaystyle\left\|(1-\alpha)(\bm{x}^{(t)}-\widetilde{\bm{x}})+\alpha({\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}(\bm{x}^{(t)})]-\widetilde{\bm{x}})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\alpha)\left\|\bm{x}^{(t)}-\widetilde{\bm{x}}\right\|^{2}+\alpha\left\|{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}(\bm{x}^{(t)})]-\widetilde{\bm{x}}\right\|^{2}

(56)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left[1-\alpha(1-\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}h_{i})\right]\left\|\bm{x}^{(t)}-\widetilde{\bm{x}}\right\|^{2}

(57)

where
Equation

56
comes from the fact that for any vectors
\bm{a},\bm{b}
, we have
\left\|\alpha\bm{a}+(1-\alpha)\bm{b}\right\|^{2}\leq\alpha\left\|\bm{a}\right\|^{2}+(1-\alpha)\left\|\bm{b}\right\|^{2}
, and the last inequality is from the contraction property of

. For the second term in

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x}^{(t)})-{\mathbb{E}}[\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\mathcal{A}(\bm{x}^{(t)})]\right\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x}^{(t)})-{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x}^{(t)})]\right\|^{2}

(58)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}^{2}q_{i}

(59)

Substituting

and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\bm{x}^{(t+1)}-\widetilde{\bm{x}}\right\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\left[1-\alpha(1-\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}h_{i})\right]\left\|\bm{x}^{(t)}-\widetilde{\bm{x}}\right\|^{2}+\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}^{2}q_{i}.

(60)

Taking the total expectation on both sides, one can get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\bm{x}^{(t+1)}-\widetilde{\bm{x}}\right\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\left[1-\alpha(1-\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}h_{i})\right]{\mathbb{E}}\left\|\bm{x}^{(t)}-\widetilde{\bm{x}}\right\|^{2}+\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}^{2}q_{i}.

(61)

In order to get the final convergence rate, we need a technical lemma from
[
35
]
, stated as follows.

Lemma 3

(
Stich 2019
)
.

Suppose there are two non-negative sequences
\{r_{t}\},\{s_{t}\}
that satisfy the relation

\displaystyle r_{t+1}\leq(1-a\gamma_{t})r_{t}-b\gamma_{t}s_{t}+c\gamma_{t}^{2}

for all

\displaystyle\frac{b}{W_{T}}\sum_{t=0}^{T}s_{t}w_{t}+ar_{T+1}\leq 32dr_{0}\left(1-\frac{a}{d}\right)^{\frac{T}{2}}+\frac{36c}{aT}\leq 32dr_{0}\exp\left[-\frac{aT}{2d}\right]+\frac{36c}{aT}

By setting

where

Proof of
2

In the quadratic problem, we can write down the analytical expression of operator
\mathcal{A}_{i}
. Specifically, for the
K
-th local iterate of client
i
, we have

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{(k+1)}=

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{(k)}-\eta_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)})

(86)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{(k)}-\eta_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i}(\bm{x}^{(k)}-\bm{x}_{i}^{*})

(87)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i})(\bm{x}^{(k)}-\bm{x}_{i}^{*})+\bm{x}_{i}^{*}.

(88)

That is,

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{(k+1)}-\bm{x}_{i}^{*}=(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i})^{k+1}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i}^{*}).

(89)

According to the definition of
\mathcal{A}_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x};\tau_{i})=

\displaystyle(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i})^{\tau_{i}}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i}^{*})+\bm{x}_{i}^{*},

(90)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\bm{x})=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}[(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i})^{\tau_{i}}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{i}^{*})+\bm{x}_{i}^{*}].

(91)

We first show that
\mathcal{A}
is contractive. Note that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\bm{x})-\mathcal{A}(\bm{y})=

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i})^{\tau_{i}}\right](\bm{x}-\bm{y}).

(92)

Therefore, as long as the operator norm of
\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i})^{\tau_{i}}
is smaller than
1
, the operator
\mathcal{A}
is contractive and has a unique fixed point
\widetilde{\bm{x}}
. Next, we are going to find the analytical expression of
\widetilde{\bm{x}}
. We have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{x}}-\mathcal{A}(\widetilde{\bm{x}})=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}[\bm{I}-(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i})^{\tau_{i}}](\widetilde{\bm{x}}-\bm{x}_{i}^{*})=0.

(93)

After minor rearranging, it follows that

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{x}}=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}[\bm{I}-(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i})^{\tau_{i}}]\right]^{-1}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}[\bm{I}-(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i})^{\tau_{i}}]\bm{x}_{i}^{*}\right].

(94)

When
\eta_{i}=\gamma_{i}\eta
and
\eta
approaches to zero, we have
\bm{I}-(\bm{I}-\eta_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i})^{\tau_{i}}\simeq\eta_{i}\tau_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i}
and

\displaystyle\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0}\widetilde{\bm{x}}=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\gamma_{i}\tau_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i}\right]^{-1}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\gamma_{i}\tau_{i}\bm{P}_{i}\bm{H}_{i}\bm{x}_{i}^{*}\right].

(95)

Here we complete the proof.

Appendix F
Proof for the Convergence of Local Correction

F.1
Main Results

Without loss of generalities, suppose that at the
t
-th round, the local model changes of client
i
can be written as

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{(t)}-\mathcal{A}_{i}(\bm{x}^{(t)};\tau_{i})=\eta_{i}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{i}-1}\bm{B}_{i}^{(t,k)}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}_{i}^{(t,k)})

(96)

where
\eta_{i}
is the client learning rate,
\{\bm{B}_{i}^{(t,k)}\}
are symmetric and positive definite matrices, and
\bm{x}_{i}^{(t,k)}
denotes the local iterate after performing
k
local steps. When the local correction technique is applied, the client will send the following normalized local changes to the server:

\displaystyle\bm{h}_{i}^{(t)}=\frac{1}{\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{i}-1}\bm{B}_{i}^{(t,k)}}\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{i}-1}\bm{B}_{i}^{(t,k)}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}_{i}^{(t,k)}):=\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{i}-1}\bm{A}_{i}^{(t,k)}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}_{i}^{(t,k)})

(97)

where
\bm{A}_{i}^{(t,k)}=\bm{B}_{i}^{(t,k)}/\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{i}-1}\bm{B}_{i}^{(t,k)}
and
\sum_{k=0}^{\tau_{i}-1}\bm{A}_{i}^{(t,k)}=\bm{I}
. Then, the server will aggregate the normalized local changes and update the global model as follows

\displaystyle\bm{x}^{(t+1)}=\bm{x}^{(t)}-\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\bm{h}_{i}^{(t)}

(98)

where
\alpha
denotes the server learning rate.

Our convergence analysis will be centered around the following assumptions.

Assumption 3
.

Each local objective is Lipschitz smooth, that is,
\left\|\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x})-\nabla F_{i}(\bm{y})\right\|\leq L\left\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\right\|,\forall i\in[M]
.

Assumption 4
.

The matrices
\{\bm{A}_{i}^{(t,k)}\}
are positive-definite symmetric matrices and have bounded operator norm:
\left\|\bm{A}_{i}^{(t,k)}\right\|_{\tiny{\mbox{op}}}\leq\Lambda/\tau_{i}
.

Assumption 5
.

The pre-conditioned gradients at each local iteration have bounded norm, i.e.,
\left\|\bm{B}_{i}^{(t,k)}\nabla F_{i}(\bm{x}_{i}^{(t,k)})\right\|\leq G
.

Theorem 3

(Convergence Guarantee for Local Correction Technique)
.

Suppose all clients have the same client learning rate
\eta
and the same number of local sptes
\tau
. Under
3
,
4
and
5
, if the server learning rate is set as
\alpha=\eta\tau\leq 1/L
and the client learning rate is

\displaystyle\eta=\min\left\{\frac{1}{\tau L},\frac{1}{\tau T^{\frac{1}{3}}}\left(\frac{D}{L^{2}\Lambda G^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{3}}\right\}

(99)

where
D=F(\bm{x}^{(0)})-F_{\text{inf}}
, then the global iterate
Equation

98
converges at the following rate:

\displaystyle\min_{t\in[0,T]}\left\|\nabla F(\bm{x}^{(t)})\right\|^{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{T}+\frac{\Lambda^{\frac{1}{3}}G^{\frac{2}{3}}}{T^{\frac{2}{3}}}\right).

(100)

It is worth noting that the convergence rate
Equation

100
matches previous results in
[
39
,
40
]
in the deterministic, non-IID data setting.
3
shows that using adaptive client optimizers together with local correction can preserve the same convergence rate as vanilla GD client optimizer and there is no non-vanishing solution bias.

F.2
Technical Lemmas

Lemma 4
.

Suppose
\bm{A}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},k\in[1,K]
are symmetric positive definite matrices.

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{A}_{k}\bm{b}_{k}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|\bm{A}_{s}\right\|_{\tiny{\mbox{op}}}^{2}\left\|\bm{A}_{s}^{-1}\right\|_{\tiny{\mbox{op}}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|\bm{A}_{k}\right\|_{\tiny{\mbox{op}}}\left\|\bm{b}_{k}\right\|^{2}

(101)

where
\bm{A}_{s}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{A}_{k}
.

Proof.

We define
\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{k}=(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{A}_{k})^{-1}\bm{A}_{k}=\bm{A}_{s}^{-1}\bm{A}_{k}
. It directly follws that
\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{k}=\bm{I}
. For the left hand side of
Equation

101
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{A}_{k}\bm{b}_{k}\right\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{A}_{s}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{k}\bm{b}_{k}\right\|^{2}=\left\|\bm{A}_{s}\overline{\bm{b}}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|\bm{A}_{s}\right\|_{\tiny{\mbox{op}}}^{2}\left\|\overline{\bm{b}}\right\|^{2}

(102)

where
\overline{\bm{b}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{k}\bm{b}_{k}
. On the other hand, let
\bm{v}_{k}=\bm{b}_{k}-\overline{\bm{b}}
and note that

\displaystyle\trace\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{k}\bm{b}_{k}\bm{b}_{k}^{\top}\right)=

\displaystyle\trace\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{k}(\bm{b}_{k}-\overline{\bm{b}}+\overline{\bm{b}})(\bm{b}_{k}-\overline{\bm{b}}+\overline{\bm{b}})^{\top}\right)

(103)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\trace\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{k}\left(\bm{v}_{k}\bm{v}_{k}^{\top}+\bm{v}_{k}\overline{\bm{b}}^{\top}+\overline{\bm{b}}\bm{v}_{k}^{\top}+\overline{\bm{b}}\ \overline{\bm{b}}^{\top}\right)\right)

(104)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\trace\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{k}\bm{v}_{k}\bm{v}_{k}^{\top}\right)}_{\geq 0}+\underbrace{\trace\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{k}\bm{v}_{k}\overline{\bm{b}}^{\top}\right)}_{=0}+\trace\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{k}\overline{\bm{b}}\bm{v}_{k}^{\top}\right)+\trace\left(\overline{\bm{b}}\ \overline{\bm{b}}^{\top}\right).

(105)

For the third term, we have

\displaystyle\trace\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{k}\overline{\bm{b}}\bm{v}_{k}^{\top}\right)=\trace\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{v}_{k}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{k}\overline{\bm{b}}\right)=\trace\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{v}_{k}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{k}^{\top}\overline{\bm{b}}\right)=0.

(106)

Therefore, we can obtain that

\displaystyle\left\|\overline{\bm{b}}\right\|^{2}=\trace\left(\overline{\bm{b}}\ \overline{\bm{b}}^{\top}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\trace\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{k}\bm{b}_{k}\bm{b}_{k}^{\top}\right)

(107)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{b}_{k}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{k}\bm{b}_{k}

(108)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|\widetilde{\bm{A}}_{k}\right\|_{\tiny{\mbox{op}}}\left\|\bm{b}_{k}\right\|^{2}

(109)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\bm{A}_{s}^{-1}\right\|_{\tiny{\mbox{op}}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|\bm{A}_{k}\right\|_{\tiny{\mbox{op}}}\left\|\bm{b}_{k}\right\|^{2}.

(110)

Substituting
Equation

110
into
Equation

102
, it follows that

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{K}\bm{A}_{k}\bm{b}_{k}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|\bm{A}_{s}\right\|_{\tiny{\mbox{op}}}^{2}\left\|\bm{A}_{s}^{-1}\right\|_{\tiny{\mbox{op}}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|\bm{A}_{k}\right\|_{\tiny{\mbox{op}}}\left\|\bm{b}_{k}\right\|^{2}.

(111)

∎
