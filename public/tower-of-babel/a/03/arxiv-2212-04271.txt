Differentiation identities for hypergeometric functions
Hayato Motohashi

Abstract
. It is well-known that differentiation of hypergeometric function multiplied by a certain power function yields another hypergeometric function with a different set of parameters. Such differentiation identities for hypergeometric functions have been used widely in various fields of applied mathematics and natural sciences. In this expository note, we provide a simple proof of the differentiation identities, which is based only on the definition of the coefficients for the power series expansion of the hypergeometric functions.

1.
Introduction

The theory of hypergeometric functions is fundamental in
mathematical physics
since they contain as special cases almost all the special functions commonly used for centuries.
Among the family of hypergeometric functions, the most well-known ones are Gauss hypergeometric function and Kummer confluent hypergeometric function,
and their natural extension is known as
generalized hypergeometric function
[
1
,
2
]
.
A lot of efforts have been made to deepen understanding of the hypergeometric functions, which have accumulated hundreds of useful formulae
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
,
such as relation to other special functions, series expansions, differential properties, contiguous relations, variable transformations, integral representations etc.
These formulae clarify the nature of the hypergeometric functions from various aspects, and have been widely used as powerful tools in diverse research fields
[
7
]
.

Among such formulae, let us focus on the differentiation identity for the hypergeometric functions.
Consider the Gauss hypergeometric function as an example, which is defined by an infinite series

(1)

{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}a,b\\
c\end{matrix};z\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{k!}\dfrac{(a)_{k}(b)_{k}}{(c)_{k}}z^{k},

where
(a)_{k}
is the Pochhammer symbol defined by

(2)

(a)_{k}=\dfrac{\Gamma(a+k)}{\Gamma(a)}=\begin{cases}1,&(k=0),\\
a(a+1)\cdots(a+k-1),&(k>0).\end{cases}

Here, it is assumed that
c
is not a nonpositive integer to avoid the singular behavior of the coefficients in (
1
).
The series converges on
|z|<1
.
Several differentiation identities are well-known in the literature.
One of the famous identities is as follows (see, e.g.,
[
4
, Eq. (15.2.4)]
):

(3)

\dfrac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}\left[z^{c-1}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}a,b\\
c\end{matrix};z\right)\right]=(c-n)_{n}z^{c-n-1}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}a,b\\
c-n\end{matrix};z\right).

While it is typically not clearly mentioned in the literature, this identity does not hold if
c\in\mathbb{Z}_{<n+1}
.
For
c\in\mathbb{Z}_{<n+1}
, the right-hand side is singular.
For
c\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
, the left-hand side is also singular.
However, for
c=1,2,\cdots,n
, the left-hand side is not singular, despite that the right-hand side is singular.
It is thus natural to ask whether there exists a different kind of identity for the exceptional case
c=1,2,\cdots,n
to rewrite the left-hand side in terms of a hypergeometric function with another set of parameters.
While most books do not make any mention of such exceptional cases, there indeed exists different forms of the differentiation identities for these cases, some of which can be found in
[
5
, page 44]
.

Of course, one can derive the formula for the case
c=1,2,\cdots,n
by applying the analytic continuation to the right-hand side of (
3

It is then possible to enlarge the validity of the formula and to obtain interesting pairs of functions demonstrated above.
However, such a derivation is based on the assumption that one already knows the original formula (
3

Thus, this strategy may not work for more general functions, if no differentiation identities are known for those functions.

The aim of this expository note is to provide a simple derivation of the differentiation identities for the generalized hypergeometric function, treating the well-known cases such as (
3
) and lesser-known exceptional cases on an equal footing.
The advantage of this derivation is that,
unlike the approach relying on the analytic continuation mentioned above, it is based only on the definition of the coefficients for the power series expansion of the hypergeometric functions and does not require a priori knowledge of the differentiation identities.
The strategy presented here may work for more general functions
[
8
]
, while the coefficients for the power series expansion are in general not so simple.

In the rest of the paper, we derive differentiation identities for the generalized hypergeometric function, including the hypergeometric function and confluent hypergeometric function as special cases, in a concise and systematic way to exhaust all the possible identities, some of which are omitted in the literature.

2.
Prelude

In this section we summarize preliminary definitions and notations.
Generalized hypergeometric function is defined formally by an infinite series

(5)

{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\cdots,a_{p}\\
b_{1},\cdots,b_{q}\end{matrix};z\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}z^{k},

with coefficients

(6)

c_{k}=\dfrac{1}{k!}\dfrac{(a_{1})_{k}\cdots(a_{p})_{k}}{(b_{1})_{k}\cdots(b_{q})_{k}},

with the Pochhammer symbol
(a)_{k}
defined in (
2
).
Here,
p,q\in\mathbb{N}
, and other parameters and the variable
z
are complex numbers in general.

The case with
(p,q)=(2,1)
yields the hypergeometric function, whereas the case with
(p,q)=(1,1)
yields the confluent hypergeometric function.
For these cases, the notations
F(a,b;c;z)={}_{2}F_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}a,b\\
c\end{matrix};z\right)
and
M(a;c;z)={}_{1}F_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}a\\
c\end{matrix};z\right)
are also commonly used in the literature.
These two hypergeometric functions are especially important and have been extensively studied since they are exact solutions for second-order differential equations known as (confluent) hypergeometric equations, which encompass a broad class of differential equations appearing in mathematical physics.

For simplicity, we use the notation
{\bf a}=(a_{1},\cdots,a_{p})
and
{\bf b}=(b_{1},\cdots,b_{q})
and express the generalized hypergeometric function (
5
) as
{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)
.
We also adopt the notation
{\bf a}+c=(a_{1}+c,\cdots,a_{p}+c)
and
{\bf b}+c=(b_{1}+c,\cdots,b_{q}+c)
.
Further, we write the product of the Pochhammer symbols as
({\bf a})_{k}=(a_{1})_{k}\cdots(a_{p})_{k}
and
({\bf b})_{k}=(b_{1})_{k}\cdots(b_{q})_{k}
.
The coefficient (
6
) is then written as
c_{k}=\dfrac{1}{k!}\dfrac{({\bf a})_{k}}{({\bf b})_{k}}
.
By definition, the value of
{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)
remains the same under any permutation of the arguments of
{\bf a}
, and any permutation of the arguments of
{\bf b}
.
Therefore, for the following, when we focus on an argument of
{\bf a}
or
{\bf b}
, we always take the first argument
a_{1}
or
b_{1}
without loss of generality, and write the rest arguments as
\hat{\bf a}=(a_{2},a_{3},\cdots,a_{p})
or
\hat{\bf b}=(b_{2},b_{3},\cdots,b_{q})
.
Note that
\hat{\bf a}
is empty for
p=1
, and
\hat{\bf b}
is empty for
q=1
.

The radius of convergence of the generalized hypergeometric series (
5
) is as follows
[
1
,
2
]
:
If
p<q+1
, the series converges for all values of
z
on the complex plane, provided that the coefficients (
6
) are not singular.
If
p>q+1
, the series does not converge except for
z=0
, and hence the function is defined only when the series terminates.
If
p=q+1
, the series converges when
|z|<1
.
Whether it converges for the case
p=q+1
when
|z|=1
is more subtle.
It converges when
z=1
if

(7)

\Re\left(\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}a_{j}\right)>0,

and when
z=-1
if

(8)

\Re\left(\sum_{j=1}^{q}b_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}a_{j}\right)+1>0.

If none of the upper parameters
{\bf a}
is a nonpositive integer, we
assume that none of the lower parameters
{\bf b}
is a nonpositive integer to avoid the possibility of singular coefficients.
On the other hand, if one or more of the upper parameters
{\bf a}
are nonpositive integer, the series (
5
) terminates and
{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)
becomes a polynomial.
For instance, if
a_{1}=-m
such that
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, the coefficient
c_{k}
vanishes for
k\geq m+1
, and hence
{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)
is at most
m
-th order polynomial.
Namely, we can write down the generalized hypergeometric function as a finite series as

(9)

{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}-m,\hat{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}c_{k}z^{k}.

This expression is also valid even if
b_{1}=-m-\ell
such that
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
provided that we define

(10)

{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}-m,\hat{\bf a}\\
-m-\ell,\hat{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)=\lim_{b_{1}\to-m-\ell}\left(\lim_{a_{1}\to-m}{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1},\hat{\bf a}\\
b_{1},\hat{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)\right).

This definition is a natural generalization of the one for the Gauss hypergeometric function, which is commonly used in the literature, see, e.g.,

[
6
, Eq. (15.2.5)]
or
[
9
, page 39]
.
Throughout the present paper, we adopt the definition (
10
).
In other words, for the case of (
9
), we relax the assumption on the lower parameters
{\bf b}
as follows: None of the lower parameters
{\bf b}
is a negative integer
-k
such that
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{<m}
.

By definition, it holds that

(11)

{}_{p+1}F_{q+1}\left(\begin{matrix}c,{\bf a}\\
d,{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)={}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right),

if
c=d
.
For brevity, for the following we call this reduction procedure “cancellation” between
c
and
d
.

3.
Main Theorem

Theorem 1
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
r\in\mathbb{C}
, the following differentiation identities for the generalized hypergeometric function
{}_{p}F_{q}
hold:

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}\left[z^{r}{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)\right]

(12)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(r-n+1)_{n}z^{r-n}{}_{p+1}F_{q+1}\left(\begin{matrix}r+1,{\bf a}\\
r-n+1,{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right),&(r\not\in\mathbb{Z}_{<n}),\\
\dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!}\dfrac{({\bf a})_{n-r}}{({\bf b})_{n-r}}{}_{p+1}F_{q+1}\left(\begin{matrix}n+1,{\bf a}+n-r\\
n-r+1,{\bf b}+n-r\end{matrix};z\right),&(r=0,1,\cdots,n),\\
(r-n+1)_{n}z^{r-n}{}_{p+1}F_{q+1}\left(\begin{matrix}r+1,{\bf a}\\
r-n+1,{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)+\dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!}\dfrac{({\bf a})_{n-r}}{({\bf b})_{n-r}}{}_{p+1}F_{q+1}\left(\begin{matrix}n+1,{\bf a}+n-r\\
n-r+1,{\bf b}+n-r\end{matrix};z\right),&(r\in\mathbb{Z}_{<0}),\end{cases}

(13)

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)=\dfrac{({\bf a})_{n}}{({\bf b})_{n}}{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf a}+n\\
{\bf b}+n\end{matrix};z\right),

(14)

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}\left[z^{a_{1}+n-1}{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)\right]=(a_{1})_{n}z^{a_{1}-1}{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}a_{1}+n,\hat{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right),

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}\left[z^{b_{1}-1}{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)\right]

(15)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(b_{1}-n)_{n}z^{b_{1}-n-1}{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf a}\\
b_{1}-n,\hat{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right),&(b_{1}\not\in\mathbb{Z}_{<n+1}),\\
\dfrac{n!}{(n-b_{1}+1)!}\dfrac{({\bf a})_{n-b_{1}+1}}{({\bf b})_{n-b_{1}+1}}{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf a}+n-b_{1}+1\\
n-b_{1}+2,\hat{\bf b}+n-b_{1}+1\end{matrix};z\right),&(b_{1}=1,2,\cdots,n+1),\end{cases}

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}\left[z^{r}{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}1,\hat{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)\right]

(16)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\dfrac{n!(\hat{\bf a})_{n-r}}{({\bf b})_{n-r}}{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}n+1,\hat{\bf a}+n-r\\
{\bf b}+n-r\end{matrix};z\right),&(r=0,1,\cdots,n),\\
\dfrac{n!(\hat{\bf a})_{n-r}}{({\bf b})_{n-r}}{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}n+1,\hat{\bf a}+n-r\\
{\bf b}+n-r\end{matrix};z\right)+(r-n+1)_{n}z^{r-n}{}_{p+1}F_{q+1}\left(\begin{matrix}r+1,1,\hat{\bf a}\\
r-n+1,{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right),&(r\in\mathbb{Z}_{<0}).\end{cases}

From this point of view, we prove Theorem

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}(-1)^{n-r}\dfrac{n!}{(c)_{n-r}}e^{-z}{}_{1}F_{1}\left(\begin{matrix}c-r-1\\

4.
Proof of Theorem
1

We consider a differentiation

(36)

\dfrac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}\left[z^{r}{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)\right]=\dfrac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}c_{k}z^{k+r}.

According to which terms in the series survive after performing the term-by-term differentiation in the right-hand side, we consider the following three cases separately:

(1)

r\not\in\mathbb{Z}_{<n}
,

(2)

r=0,1,\cdots,n
,

(3)

r\in\mathbb{Z}_{<0}
.

Note that the first two cases overlap for
r=n
, corresponding to the similar overlapping in (
12
) and (
15
), for which the formulae coincide.
Below we prove the identity (
12
) for each case, from which we derive other identities (
13
)–(
16
) by using the reduction (
11
).

4.1.
r\not\in\mathbb{Z}_{<n}

In this case, all the terms in the series in the right-hand side of (
36
) survive after performing the term-by-term differentiation.
We thus obtain

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}z^{k+r}

\displaystyle=(k+r)(k+r-1)\cdots(k+r-n+1)z^{k+r-n}

(37)

\displaystyle=(k+r-n+1)_{n}z^{k+r-n}.

Using the definitions (
5
) and (
6
), and an identity

(38)

\dfrac{(a+k)_{n}}{(a)_{n}}=\dfrac{(a+n)_{k}}{(a)_{k}},

we obtain

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}\left[z^{r}{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{k!}\dfrac{({\bf a})_{k}}{({\bf b})_{k}}(k+r-n+1)_{n}z^{k+r-n}

\displaystyle=(r-n+1)_{n}z^{r-n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{k!}\dfrac{(r+1,{\bf a})_{k}}{(r-n+1,{\bf b})_{k}}z^{k}

(39)

\displaystyle=(r-n+1)_{n}z^{r-n}{}_{p+1}F_{q+1}\left(\begin{matrix}r+1,{\bf a}\\
r-n+1,{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right),

which establishes the first line of (
12
) for the case
r\not\in\mathbb{Z}_{<n}
.

By virtue of the relation (
11
),
{}_{p+1}F_{q+1}\left(\begin{matrix}r+1,{\bf a}\\
r-n+1,{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)
in the right-hand side of (
39
) can be reduced to
{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf a}^{\prime}\\
{\bf b}^{\prime}\end{matrix};z\right)
with some
{\bf a}^{\prime}
and
{\bf b}^{\prime}
if an upper parameter coincides with a lower parameter.
While such “cancellation” between argument(s) of
{\bf a}
and
{\bf b}
may be possible, it should be studied on a case-by-case basis.
Here, we focus on nontrivial cases peculiar to (
39
), which show up by a cancellation involving
r+1
or
r-n+1
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that the first argument of
{\bf a}
or
{\bf b}
is a counterpart of the cancellation.

Clearly, there are two ways of cancellation.
If
r=a_{1}+n-1
,
a_{1}
and
r-n+1
cancel each other, and
then (
39
) reduces to the identity (
14
).
Note that, however, the condition
r\not\in\mathbb{Z}_{<n}
now reads
a_{1}\not\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
, so (
14
) for
a_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\leq 0}
needs to be proved separately.
For the case
a_{1}=0
, (
14
) holds trivially.
We shall provide the proof of (
14
) for the remaining case
a_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{<0}
in §
4.3
.

On the other hand, if
r=b_{1}-1
,
b_{1}
and
r+1
cancel each other, and
then (
39
) reduces to the first line of (
15
).
The condition
r\not\in\mathbb{Z}_{<n}
prohibits
b_{1}-n
to be a nonpositive integer,
which is implicitly assumed for the right-hand side of the first line of (
15
) to avoid the singularity of the coefficients.

4.2.
r=0,1,\cdots,n

In this case, the first
n-r
terms vanish after performing the term-by-term differentiation in (
36
).
As a result, we obtain

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}\left[z^{r}{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf a}\\
{\bf b}\end{matrix};z\right)\right]

\displaystyle=n!c_{n-r}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(n+k)!}{n!k!}\dfrac{c_{n-r+k}}{c_{n-r}}z^{k}

\displaystyle=n!c_{n-r}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{(n+1)_{k}}{k!}\dfrac{(n-r)!}{(n-r+k)!}\dfrac{({\bf a})_{n-r+k}}{({\bf a})_{n-r}}\dfrac{({\bf b})_{n-r}}{({\bf b})_{n-r+k}}z^{k}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!}\dfrac{({\bf a})_{n-r}}{({\bf b})_{n-r}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{k!}\dfrac{(n+1,{\bf a}+n-r)_{k}}{(n-r+1,{\bf b}+n-r)_{k}}z^{k}

(40)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{n!}{(n-r)!}\dfrac{({\bf a})_{n-r}}{({\bf b})_{n-r}}{}_{p+1}F_{q+1}\left(\begin{matrix}n+1,{\bf a}+n-r\\
n-r+1,{\bf b}+n-r\end{matrix};z\right),

where we used an identity

(41)

\dfrac{(a)_{k+n-r}}{(a)_{n-r}}=(a+n-r)_{k}.

The right-hand side of (
40
) is nothing but the second line of (
12
).

Again, let us consider nontrivial reduction of
{}_{p+1}F_{q+1}
in the right-hand side of (
40
) to
{}_{p}F_{q}
by using (
11
).
There are three ways of nontrivial cancellation.
The simplest cancellation occurs if
r=0
, for which
n+1
and
n-r+1
cancel.
In this case, after cancellation, (
40
) yields (
13
).

For

, in parallel to the case considered in §
4.1
, the factor
n+1
or
n-r+1
cancel with
b_{1}+n-r
or
a_{1}+n-r
respectively, where we focused on the first arguments of
{\bf a}
and
{\bf b}
without loss of generality.
If
r=b_{1}-1
, where
b_{1}=1,2,\cdots,n+1
is required,
(
40
) yields the second line of (
15
).
On the other hand, if
a_{1}=1
, (
40
) yields (
16
).

4.3.
r\in\mathbb{Z}_{<0}

Let us denote

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}\left[z^{-m}{}_{p}F_{q}\left(\begin{matrix}{\bf a}\\

(44)

\displaystyle\dfrac{(-m-n+1+k)_{n}}{(-m-n+1)_{n}}c_{k}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{k!}\dfrac{(-m+1,{\bf a})_{k}}{(-m-n+1,{\bf b})_{k}},

\displaystyle\dfrac{(k+1)_{n}}{(1)_{n}}\dfrac{c_{m+n+k}}{c_{m+n}}

\displaystyle\,\,\,\,\,\,+\dfrac{n!}{(n+m)!}\dfrac{({\bf a})_{n+m}}{({\bf b})_{n+m}}{}_{p+1}F_{q+1}\left(\begin{matrix}n+1,{\bf a}+n+m\\
