A High-Order Asymptotic Analysis of the Benjamin-Feir Instability Spectrum in Arbitrary Depth
Ryan P. Creedon, Bernard Deconinck

Abstract
We investigate the Benjamin-Feir (or modulational) instability of Stokes waves, i.e. , small-amplitude, one-dimensional periodic gravity waves of permanent form and constant velocity, in water of finite and infinite depth. We develop a perturbation method to describe to high-order accuracy the unstable spectral elements associated with this instability, obtained by linearizing Euler’s equations about the small-amplitude Stokes waves. These unstable elements form a figure-eight curve centered at the origin of the complex spectral plane, which is parameterized by a Floquet exponent. Our asymptotic expansions of this figure-eight are in excellent agreement with numerical computations as well as recent rigorous results by Berti, Maspero, & Ventura [ 6 , 7 ] . From our expansions, we derive high-order estimates for the growth rates of the Benjamin-Feir instability and for the parameterization of the Benjamin-Feir figure-eight curve with respect to the Floquet exponent. We are also able to compare the Benjamin-Feir and high-frequency instability spectra analytically for the first time, revealing three different regimes of the Stokes waves, depending on the predominant instability. Keywords : Euler’s equations, Stokes waves, Benjamin-Feir (or modulational) instability, spectral instability, perturbation methods

Introduction

In 1847, Stokes
[
35
]
derived a formal asymptotic expansion for the small-amplitude, periodic traveling wave solutions of the full water wave equations in infinite depth, see Figure
1
for a schematic. Seventy-five years later, Nekrasov
[
32
]
and Levi-Civita
[
27
]
proved the validity of these expansions if the amplitude
\varepsilon
of the waves is sufficiently small. Not long after, Struik
[
36
]
extended this analysis to finite depth. The stability with respect to sideband perturbations of these solutions, now known as Stokes waves, was investigated first experimentally by Benjamin
&
Feir
[
5
]
in the late 1960s and immediately after supported by independent formal calculations by Benjamin
[
4
]
and Whitham
[
37
]
using distinct methods. Both calculations suggest that Stokes waves are modulationally unstable with respect to longitudinal sideband perturbations provided
\kappa h>\alpha_{BW}
, where
\kappa
is the wavenumber of the Stokes waves,
h
is the constant depth of the water, and
\alpha_{BW}=1.3627827567...
. This instability is now known as the Benjamin-Feir or modulational instability.

In the years since the pioneering work of Benjamin and Whitham, several papers have explored the Benjamin-Feir instability experimentally, numerically, and analytically. It is impossible to summarize all these works here, rather we highlight a few works that are most relevant to our investigation. For a more comprehensive history of the Benjamin-Feir instability, see
[
12
,
20
,
38
]
and, in different contexts,
[
17
,
26
,
40
]
.

Figure 1
:
A schematic of a
2\pi/\kappa
-periodic Stokes wave
\eta_{S}
in finite depth
h
. The wave travels uniformly to the left or right, depending on its velocity. In the case of infinite depth, Stokes derived an asymptotic expansion for
\eta_{S}
and its velocity as power series in a small parameter
\varepsilon
related to the amplitude of the wave.

Beginning in the 1970s, Bryant
[
9
,
10
]
studied the stability of Stokes waves in shallow water (
\kappa h<\alpha_{BW}
) with respect to co-periodic longitudinal and transverse perturbations, respectively. Around the same time, Longuet-Higgins
[
28
,
29
]
considered the stability of Stokes waves with respect to sub- and super-harmonic longitudinal perturbations in infinitely deep water.
In 1982, McLean
[
31
]
generalized the results of Bryant and Longuet-Higgins by investigating the stability of Stokes waves in finite depth with respect to sub- and super-harmonic transverse perturbations.

All these results are obtained by linearizing the full water wave equations about a Stokes wave and numerically solving a resulting spectral problem. The elements of the spectrum
\lambda
give the exponential growth rates of the perturbations, and the collection of all such
\lambda
is called the stability spectrum of the Stokes wave. The Benjamin-Feir instability occurs when four eigenvalues of the stability spectrum collide at the origin of the complex spectral plane and separate, each eigenvalue occupying a distinct quadrant, as the amplitude
\varepsilon
of the Stokes wave increases. This is a consequence of the quadrafold symmetry of the spectrum due to the Hamiltonian structure of the water wave equations
[
21
,
39
]
.

Despite numerical evidence of these eigenvalues close to the origin in the complex spectral plane, a proof of their existence did not appear until 1995. In the classic work of Bridges
&
Mielke
[
8
]
, techniques from spatial dynamics and center manifold theory are used to prove the existence of unstable eigenvalues close to the origin whenever
\kappa h>\alpha_{BW}
and
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small. The proof is valid for any finite depth but fails in water of infinite depth. Only in the past two years have Nguyen
&
Strauss
[
33
]
developed a proof based on Lyapunov-Schmidt reduction that works in both finite and infinite depth. Another proof based on periodic Evans functions appeared in the literature more recently
[
22
]
.

In 2011, Deconinck
&
Oliveras
[
17
]
revisited the numerical computations of McLean and others, also allowing for more general quasi-periodic perturbations parameterized by a Floquet exponent
\mu

[
16
]
. For fixed
\varepsilon
, this results in a continuous stability spectrum that can be decomposed as a union over all Floquet exponents of point spectra containing a countable number of finite-multiplicity eigenvalues
[
24
]
. To our knowledge,
[
17
]
is the first work displaying full stability spectra of Stokes waves in finite and infinite depth (to within machine precision) that exhibits a figure-eight curve at the origin of the complex spectral plane associated with the Benjamin-Feir instability, see a schematic in Figure
2
. Evidence of instabilities away from the origin, referred to as high-frequency instabilities, was also demonstrated in
[
17
]
. These high-frequency instabilities have been analytically explored with Trichtchenko in
[
15
]
and by Hur
&
Yang in
[
22
]
.

In this manuscript, we are concerned with high-order asymptotic approximations of the blue figure-eight curve. For the corresponding approximations of the high-frequency instabilities in orange, see

.

In recent months, seminal work by Berti, Maspero,
&
Ventura
[
6
,
7
]
has confirmed the existence of the Benjamin-Feir figure-eight in finite and infinite depth, provided
\kappa h>\alpha_{BW}
and
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small. The proof of both cases relies on the Hamiltonian and reversibility properties of the water wave equations together with Kato’s theory of similarity transformations
[
25
]
and KAM theory. Reported in these works are explicit expressions for the figure-eight curves, up to real analytic functions of the Floquet exponent
\mu
and the amplitude of the Stokes waves
\varepsilon
. A low-order approximation of the curves is also given.

In this work, we obtain high-order asymptotic expansions of the Benjamin-Feir figure-eight curve in finite and infinite depth. In particular, we seek high-order asymptotic estimates for the interval of Floquet exponents parameterizing the figure-eight and for the most unstable eigenvalue. Using the results of
[
15
]
, this allows us to compare the Benjamin-Feir and high-frequency growth rates analytically. This comparison suggests three regimes for Stokes waves: (i) shallow water (
\kappa h<\alpha_{BW}
), in which only high-frequency instabilities exist, (ii) intermediate water (
\alpha_{BW}<\kappa h<\alpha_{DO}(\varepsilon)=1.4308061674...+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)
), in which both instabilities exist but high-frequency instabilities dominate, and (iii) deep water (
\kappa h>\alpha_{DO}(\varepsilon)
), in which both instabilities are present, but the Benjamin-Feir instability dominates.

Our method to obtain these high-order asymptotic approximations is a modification of that developed for high-frequency instabilities in
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. Although the method is formal, it offers a more direct approach to the Benjamin-Feir figure-eight curve and produces results consistent with numerical computations (for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
) as well as with rigorous results appearing in
[
6
,
7
]
. The method loses validity for sufficiently large
\varepsilon
, when the Benjamin-Feir instability spectrum separates from the origin and changes its topology, see Figure
3
. Some of the lower-order details of our method are also presented by Akers
[
3
]
for the Benjamin-Feir instability in infinite depth, although this work uses different conventions for the water wave equations and underlying Stokes waves. In contrast, our expressions are in one-to-one correspondence with those appearing in
[
6
,
7
]
, giving confidence in the rigorous results as well as in our asymptotic calculations.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
The AFM Formulation of the Water Wave Equations

The Euler equations governing the dynamics of a one-dimensional periodic water wave free of surface tension over an inviscid, irrotational, and two-dimensional bulk are

\displaystyle\phi_{xx}+\phi_{zz}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\hskip 5.69046pt\textrm{in}\quad\{(x,z):|x|<\pi/\kappa~\textrm{and}-h<z<\eta\},

(1a)

\displaystyle\eta_{t}+\eta_{x}\phi_{x}

\displaystyle=\phi_{z},\quad\textrm{on}\quad z=\eta,

(1b)

\displaystyle\phi_{t}+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\phi_{x}^{2}+\phi_{z}^{2}\right)+g\eta

\displaystyle=0,\quad\hskip 5.69046pt\textrm{on}\quad z=\eta,

(1c)

\displaystyle\phi_{z}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\hskip 1.9919pt\textrm{on}\quad z=-h,

(1d)

together with the periodicity conditions

\displaystyle\eta(-\pi/\kappa,t)

\displaystyle=\eta(\pi/\kappa,t),

(2a)

\displaystyle\quad\phi_{x}(-\pi/\kappa,z,t)=\phi_{x}(\pi/\kappa,z,t),

\displaystyle\quad\phi_{z}(-\pi/\kappa,z,t)=\phi_{z}(\pi/\kappa,z,t).

(2b)

Here
x
is the horizontal coordinate,
z
is the vertical coordinate,
t
is time,
\eta(x,t)
is the surface profile of the water wave relative to the
z=0
reference level,
\phi(x,z,t)
is the velocity potential in the bulk,
g
is the magnitude of the vertical acceleration due to gravity,
h
is the average depth of the water (assuming constant bathymetry), and
2\pi/\kappa
is the period of the wave.

Many formulations of water waves exist that avoid the bulk variable
\phi
but whose dynamics are fully equivalent to (
1
)-(
2
),
e.g.
,
[
11
,
19
,
34
,
39
]
. We use the Ablowitz-Fokas-Musslimani (AFM) formulation
[
1
,
2
]
, which only involves the surface variables
\eta
and
q=\phi(x,\eta,t)
and avoids computations of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator. The AFM equations can be written as

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi/\kappa}^{\pi/\kappa}e^{-i\kappa mx}\Big[\eta_{t}\cosh\left(\kappa m\left(\eta+h\right)\right)+iq_{x}\sinh\left(\kappa m\left(\eta+h\right)\right)\Big]dx

\displaystyle=0,\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},

(3a)

\displaystyle q_{t}+\frac{1}{2}q_{x}^{2}+g\eta-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left(\eta_{t}+\eta_{x}q_{x}\right)^{2}}{1+\eta_{x}^{2}}

\displaystyle=0.

(3b)

Throughout this work, we refer to (
3a
) and (
3b
) as the nonlocal and local equations, respectively.

Moving to a traveling frame
x\rightarrow x-ct
with velocity
c
and rescaling variables as in
[
15
]
, one arrives at the nondimensional AFM equations

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{-imx}\Big[\left(\eta_{t}-c\eta_{x}\right)\cosh\left(m\left(\eta+\alpha\right)\right)+iq_{x}\sinh\left(m\left(\eta+\alpha\right)\right)\Big]dx

\displaystyle=0,\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},

(4a)

\displaystyle q_{t}-cq_{x}+\frac{1}{2}q_{x}^{2}+\eta-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left(\eta_{t}-c\eta_{x}+\eta_{x}q_{x}\right)^{2}}{1+\eta_{x}^{2}}

\displaystyle=0,

(4b)

where
\alpha=\kappa h
is the aspect ratio of the depth of the water to the period of the surface waves modulo
2\pi
. These nondimensionalized equations are equivalent to the dimensional equations ((
3a
)-(
3b
)) in the traveling frame with
\kappa=1
and
g=1
.

Remark 2.1.1
.

In infinitely deep water, the local equation (
4a
) is unchanged while the nonlocal equation (
4b
) becomes

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{-imx+|m|\eta}\Big[|m|\left(\eta_{t}-c\eta_{x}\right)+imq_{x}\Big]dx=0,\quad m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}.

(5)

2.2
Small-Amplitude Stokes Waves

We seek time-independent solutions
\eta_{S}=\eta_{S}(x)
and
q_{S}=q_{S}(x)
of (
4a
)-(
4b
) that satisfy

(i)

\eta_{S}
and
q_{S}
are infinitely differentiable with respect to
x
,

(ii)

\eta_{S}
and
q_{S,x}
are
2\pi
-periodic with respect to
x
(but not necessarily
q_{S}
),

(iii)

\eta_{S}
,
q_{S,x}
, and
c
depend analytically on a small parameter
\varepsilon
such that

\displaystyle\eta_{S}\big|_{\varepsilon=0}=0=q_{S,x}\big|_{\varepsilon=0}\quad\textrm{and}\quad||\eta_{S}||_{\textrm{L}^{2}}=\varepsilon+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)\quad\textrm{as}\quad\varepsilon\rightarrow 0,

(iv)

\eta_{S}
and
q_{S,x}
are even in
x
without loss of generality, and
c(\varepsilon)
is even in
\varepsilon
,

(v)

\eta_{S}
has zero average.

These solutions correspond to the small-amplitude Stokes waves discussed in the Introduction. Their existence is proven in
[
27
,
32
,
36
]
. Expansions of
\eta_{S}
,
q_{S}
, and
c
as power series in
\varepsilon
are carried out in
[
15
]
using the AFM equations (
4a
)-(
4b
). These expansions take the form

\displaystyle\eta_{S}(x;\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\cos(x)+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\eta_{j}(x)\varepsilon^{j},

(6a)

\displaystyle q_{S}(x;\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{c_{0}}\sin(x)+\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}q_{j}(x)\varepsilon^{j},

(6b)

\displaystyle c(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=c_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}c_{2j}\varepsilon^{2j},

(6c)

where
c_{0}^{2}=\tanh(\alpha)
. In what follows, we study right-traveling waves so that
c_{0}=\sqrt{\tanh(\alpha)}>0
.

The higher-order corrections of
\eta_{S}
and
q_{S}
(for
j>1
) take the form

\displaystyle\eta_{j}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\quad\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=2\\
\ell~\textrm{even}\end{subarray}}^{j}\hat{N}_{j,\ell}\cos(\ell x),&\textrm{for}~j~\textrm{even},\\
\quad\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=3\\
\ell~\textrm{odd}\end{subarray}}^{j}\hat{N}_{j,\ell}\cos(\ell x),&\textrm{for}~j~\textrm{odd},\end{cases}

(7a)

\displaystyle q_{j}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\quad\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{j,0}x+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=2\\
\ell~\textrm{even}\end{subarray}}^{j}\hat{Q}_{j,\ell}\sin(\ell x),&\textrm{for}~j~\textrm{even},\\
\quad\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=3\\
\ell~\textrm{odd}\end{subarray}}^{j}\hat{Q}_{j,\ell}\sin(\ell x),&\textrm{for}~j~\textrm{odd},\end{cases}

(7b)

where
\hat{N}_{j,\ell}
and
\hat{Q}_{j,\ell}
depend only on the aspect ratio
\alpha
. Explicit representations of these coefficients as well as those for
c
can be found in
[
15
]
to
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)
. Taking their limits as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
gives the corresponding coefficients in infinite depth.

Remark 2.2.1
.

The expansion of
q_{S}
contains terms proportional to
x
due to the induced mean flow of the traveling frame, so
q_{S}
is not periodic in general. Despite this,
q_{S,x}
is periodic, which implies that the horizontal velocity at the surface is periodic.

2.3
The Stability Spectrum of Stokes Waves

Extending our periodic domain to the whole real line, we perturb the Stokes waves using

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\eta(x,t;\varepsilon,\rho)\\
q(x,t;\varepsilon,\rho)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{S}(x;\varepsilon)\\
q_{S}(x;\varepsilon)\end{pmatrix}+\rho\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{\rho}(x,t)\\
q_{\rho}(x,t)\end{pmatrix}+\mathcal{O}\left(\rho^{2}\right),

(8)

where
|\rho|\ll 1
is a parameter independent of
\varepsilon
and
\eta_{\rho}
and
q_{\rho}
are sufficiently smooth functions of
x
and
t
that are bounded over the real line for each
t\geq 0
. If (
8
) satisfies the AFM equations to
\mathcal{O}(\rho)
, then, since the Stokes waves are periodic
[
15
,
17
]
,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\eta_{\rho}(x,t)\\
q_{\rho}(x,t)\end{pmatrix}=e^{\lambda t+i\mu x}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{N}(x)\\
\mathcal{Q}(x)\end{pmatrix}+c.c.,

(9)

where
c.c.
denotes the complex conjugate of what precedes,
\mu\in(-1/2,1/2]
is the Floquet exponent, and
{\bf w}=(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{Q})^{T}
is a sufficiently smooth,
2\pi
-periodic eigenfunction of the spectral problem

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\varepsilon}{\bf w}(x)=\lambda\mathcal{R}_{\mu,\varepsilon}{\bf w}(x),

(10)

with corresponding eigenvalue
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.
In general, the linear operators
\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\varepsilon}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\mu,\varepsilon}
depend nonlinearly on
\varepsilon
and
\mu
. Explicitly,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(1,1)}&\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(1,2)}\\
\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(2,1)}&\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(2,2)}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathcal{R}_{\mu,\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{R}_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(1,1)}&0\\
\mathcal{R}_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(2,1)}&1\end{pmatrix},

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(1,1)}[\mathcal{N}(x)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{inx}\mathcal{F}_{n}\big[c\,\mathcal{C}_{n+\mu}\mathcal{D}_{x}\mathcal{N}(x)+(n+\mu)\left(c\,\mathcal{S}_{n+\mu}\eta_{S,x}-i\mathcal{C}_{n+\mu}q_{S,x}\right)\mathcal{N}(x)\big],

(12a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(1,2)}[\mathcal{Q}(x)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{inx}\mathcal{F}_{n}\big[-i\mathcal{S}_{n+\mu}\mathcal{D}_{x}\mathcal{Q}(x)\big],

(12b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(2,1)}[\mathcal{N}(x)]

\displaystyle=\eta_{S,x}{\zeta}^{2}\mathcal{D}_{x}\mathcal{N}(x)-\mathcal{N}(x),

(12c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(2,2)}[\mathcal{Q}(x)]

\displaystyle=-{\zeta}\mathcal{D}_{x}\mathcal{Q}(x),

(12d)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(1,1)}[\mathcal{N}(x)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{inx}\mathcal{F}_{n}\left[\mathcal{C}_{n+\mu}\mathcal{N}(x)\right],

(12e)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(2,1)}[\mathcal{N}(x)]

\displaystyle=-\eta_{S,x}{\zeta}\mathcal{N}(x),

(12f)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{k}=\cosh(k(\eta_S+\alpha)),\quad\quad\mathcal{S}_{k}=\sinh(k(\eta_S+\alpha)),\quad\quad\mathcal{D}_{x}=i\mu+\partial_{x},\quad\quad{\zeta}=\frac{q_{S,x}-c}{1+\eta_{S,x}^{2}},

(13)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{n}[f(x)]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{-inx}f(x)dx,

(14)

for any
f(x)\in\textrm{L}^{2}_{\textrm{per}}(-\pi,\pi)
. The eigenvalues
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
of (
10
) correspond to the exponential growth rates of the perturbations
\eta_{\rho}
and
q_{\rho}
. Consequently, we define the stability spectrum of a Stokes wave with amplitude
\varepsilon
as the union of these eigenvalues over
\mu\in(-1/2,1/2]
.

Remark 2.3.1
.

The eigenvalues of (
10
) for fixed
\mu\in(-1/2,0)
are conjugate to the eigenvalues with Floquet exponent
-\mu
, so we may restrict
\mu\in[0,1/2]
without loss of generality.

Remark 2.3.2
.

In infinite depth,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(1,1)}[\mathcal{N}(x)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!e^{inx}\mathcal{F}_{n}\big[e^{|n+\mu|\eta_{S}}|n+\mu|\big(c\mathcal{D}_{x}\mathcal{N}(x)\!\!+\!\!\big(c\eta_{S,x}|n+\mu|-i(n+\mu)q_{S,x}\big)\mathcal{N}(x)\big)\big],

(15a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(1,2)}[\mathcal{Q}(x)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{inx}\mathcal{F}_{n}\big[e^{|n+\mu|\eta_{S}}\big(-i(n+\mu)\mathcal{D}_{x}\mathcal{Q}(x)\big)\big],

(15b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mu,\varepsilon}^{(1,1)}[\mathcal{N}(x)]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}e^{inx}\mathcal{F}_{n}\big[e^{|n+\mu|\eta_{S}}|n+\mu|\mathcal{N}(x)\big].

(15c)

All other operators remain the same as above.

For generic choices of
\varepsilon
and
\mu
, the spectral problem (
10
) can only be solved numerically, see
[
17
]
, for example. However, in the special case
\varepsilon=0
, (
10
) reduces to a constant-coefficient problem,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}ic_{0}(\mu+D)\cosh(\alpha(\mu+D))&(\mu+D)\sinh(\alpha(\mu+D))\\
-1&ic_{0}(\mu+D)\end{pmatrix}{\bf w_{0}}(x)

\displaystyle\hskip-99.58464pt=\lambda_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\cosh(\alpha(\mu+D))&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}{\bf w_{0}}(x),

(16)

with
D=-i\partial_{x}
. We solve (
16
) to find

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}=-i\Omega_{\sigma}(\mu+n),\quad\quad\sigma=\pm 1,\quad\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},

(17)

for

\displaystyle\Omega_{\sigma}(k)=-c_{0}k+\sigma\omega(k),\quad\quad\omega(k)=\textrm{sgn}(k)\sqrt{k\tanh(\alpha k)}.

(18)

Equation (
18
) is the linear dispersion relation of the nondimensional AFM equations in a frame traveling at velocity
c_{0}
. The parameter
\sigma
specifies the branch of this dispersion relation. Since both branches are real-valued, all eigenvalues
\lambda_{0}
are imaginary, and the zero-amplitude Stokes waves are spectrally stable.

For almost all
\mu
and
n
, (
17
) is a simple eigenvalue with one-dimensional eigenspace spanned by

\displaystyle{\bf w_{0}}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\dfrac{-i\sigma}{\omega(\mu+n)}\end{pmatrix}e^{inx}.

(19)

In order for
\lambda_{0}
to enter the right-half plane and give rise to an instability for
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
, it must be non-simple. That is, at least two eigenvalues must collide at
\lambda_{0}
for the eigenvalues to leave the imaginary axis
[
18
,
30
]
. There are a countably infinite number of pairs
(\mu,n)
for which this occurs
[
13
]
. Most of these pairs correspond to eigenvalues away from the origin in the complex spectral plane that generate high-frequency instabilities for
0<\varepsilon\ll 1

[
15
,
18
,
22
]
. The pairs
(0,-1)
,
(0,0)
, and
(0,1)
, however, correspond to the eigenvalue at the origin. This eigenvalue generates the Benjamin-Feir instability in sufficiently deep water for
0<\varepsilon\ll 1

[
6
,
7
,
8
,
22
,
33
]
.

Remark 2.3.3
.

In infinite depth, (
16
) becomes

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}ic_{0}(\mu+D)|\mu+D|&(\mu+D)^{2}\\
-1&ic_{0}(\mu+D)\end{pmatrix}{\bf w_{0}}(x)=\lambda_{0}\begin{pmatrix}|\mu+D|&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}{\bf w_{0}}(x).

(20)

The corresponding eigenvalues take the form (
17
) with

\displaystyle\Omega_{\sigma}(k)=-c_{0}k+\sigma\omega(k),\quad\quad\omega(k)=\textup{sgn}(k)\sqrt{|k|}.

(21)

2.4
A Spectral Perturbation Method for the Benjamin-Feir Instability

To reveal the structure in our perturbation calculations, we denote the zero eigenvalue of the
\varepsilon=0
spectrum and its corresponding zero Floquet exponent by
\lambda_{0}
and
\mu_{0}
, respectively. However, since
\lambda_{0}=0
and
\mu_{0}=0
, one can omit these parameters in what follows.

The eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}
is not simple with algebraic multiplicity 4 and geometric multiplicity 3
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
22
,
33
]
. The corresponding eigenspace is spanned by

\displaystyle{\bf w_{0,-1}}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
i/c_{0}\end{pmatrix}e^{-ix},\quad\quad{\bf w_{0,0}}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix},\quad\quad{\bf w_{0,1}}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-i/c_{0}\end{pmatrix}e^{ix}.

(22)

The most general eigenfunction corresponding to
\lambda_{0}
is

\displaystyle{\bf w_{0}}(x)=\beta_{0,-1}{\bf w_{0,-1}}(x)+\beta_{0,0}{\bf w_{0,0}}(x)+\beta_{0,1}{\bf w_{0,1}}(x),

(23)

where
\beta_{0,\nu}
for
\nu\in\{0,\pm 1\}
are (for now) arbitrary constants.

Remark 2.4.1
.

The generalized eigenspace is spanned by (
20
) together with the generalized eigenvector
{\bf v_{0,0}}=(1,0)^{T}
. We mention this for completeness, but in practice, we only need (
22
) to approximate the unstable eigenvalues corresponding to the Benjamin-Feir instability.

Remark 2.4.2
.

Without loss of generality, one of the constants
\beta_{0,\nu}
can be set to 1 since
{\bf w}_{0}
is unique only up to a nonzero scalar. We retain all three constants in our calculations for reasons that become more clear when we consider infinite depth.

We turn on the
\varepsilon
parameter and track the unstable eigenvalues near the origin for
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
. These eigenvalues trace out a figure-eight curve centered at the origin, as mentioned in the Introduction. To track these eigenvalues and their corresponding eigenfunctions, we formally expand in powers of
\varepsilon
:

\displaystyle\lambda(\varepsilon)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}\varepsilon^{j},

(24a)

\displaystyle{\bf w}(x;\varepsilon)

\displaystyle={\bf w_{0}}(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}{\bf w_{j}}(x)\varepsilon^{j}.

(24b)

As the curve deforms with
\varepsilon
, so too does its parameterization in terms of the Floquet exponent. Hence, as in
[
15
]
, we expand this parameter as well, writing

\displaystyle\mu(\varepsilon)=\mu_{0}+\varepsilon\mu_{1}\big(1+r(\varepsilon)\big),\quad\quad\textrm{with}\quad\quad r(\varepsilon)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}r_{j}\varepsilon^{j},

(25)

where
\mu_{1}
assumes an interval of values symmetric about zero and
r(\varepsilon)
captures the higher-order deformations of this interval, see Figure
4
.

Remark 2.4.3
.

For sufficiently large
\varepsilon
, the figure-eight curve separates from the origin (Figure
3
), and thus, the parameterizing interval of Floquet exponents separates into two disjoint intervals. Ansatz (
25
) cannot account for this effect, which limits our analysis of the Benjamin-Feir instability spectrum to sufficiently small
\varepsilon
.

We proceed as follows. Expansions (
24a
), (
24b
), and (
25
) are substituted into the full spectral problem (
10
). Powers of
\varepsilon
are equated, generating a hierarchy of linear inhomogeneous equations for the eigenfunction corrections
{\bf w_{j}}
. Each of these equations is solvable only if the Fredholm alternative removes secular inhomogeneous terms. This leads to a set of solvability conditions that impose constraints on the eigenvalue corrections
\lambda_{j}
as well as corrections to the constants appearing in
{\bf w_{0}}
. Corrections to the Floquet exponent require an additional constraint called the
regular curve condition

[
14
,
13
,
15
]
, which ensures the eigenvalue corrections remain bounded as one approaches the intersection of the figure-eight curve with the imaginary axis.

We present this method in more detail in the sections that follow, first in finite depth and then in infinite depth, where more care is needed. Results of the method are compared directly with numerical computations of the Benjamin-Feir instability using methods presented in
[
16
,
17
]
. To our knowledge, this is the first time that analytical and numerical descriptions of the Benjamin-Feir figure-eight curve have been quantitatively compared.

A schematic of the parameterizing interval of Floquet exponents for the Benjamin-Feir figure-eight curve as a function of

. The gray-shaded region indicates the leading-order approximation of this interval
\varepsilon\mu_{1}
, where
\mu_{1}
is an interval of values symmetric about zero. The blue-shaded region indicates the true interval as a function of

and is a uniform rescaling of the leading-order behavior by a factor of
1+r(\varepsilon)
, where
r
is an analytic function of

such that
r(\varepsilon)=o(1)
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}
. The boundaries of the true interval may be subtended by curves that are concave up or down, depending on
\alpha
.

3
The Benjamin-Feir Spectrum in Finite Depth

3.1
The
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
Problem

Substituting (
24a
), (
24b
), and (
25
) into the full spectral problem (
10
), terms of
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{0})
necessarily cancel by our choice of
\lambda_{0}
,
{\bf w_{0}}
, and
\mu_{0}
above. At
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
, we find

\displaystyle\Big(\mathcal{L}_{0}-\lambda_{0}\mathcal{R}_{0}\Big){\bf w_{1}}=-\mathcal{L}_{1}{\bf w_{0}}+\mathcal{R}_{0}\Big(\lambda_{1}{\bf w_{0}}\Big)+\mathcal{R}_{1}\Big(\lambda_{0}{\bf w_{0}}\Big),

(26)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{j}=\frac{1}{j!}\frac{\partial^{j}}{\partial\varepsilon^{j}}\mathcal{L}_{\mu(\varepsilon),\varepsilon},\quad\quad\mathcal{R}_{j}=\frac{1}{j!}\frac{\partial^{j}}{\partial\varepsilon^{j}}\mathcal{R}_{\mu(\varepsilon),\varepsilon}.

(27)

The operator
\mathcal{L}_{0}-\lambda_{0}\mathcal{R}_{0}
is not invertible for
(\lambda_{0},\mu_{0})=(0,0)
. A solution
{\bf w_{1}}
of
(
26
) exists only if the inhomogeneous terms are orthogonal to the nullspace of the adjoint of
\mathcal{L}_{0}-\lambda_{0}\mathcal{R}_{0}
by the Fredholm alternative. A direct calculation shows

\displaystyle\textrm{Null}\left(\left(\mathcal{L}_{0}-\lambda_{0}\mathcal{R}_{0}\right)^{\dagger}\right)=\textrm{Span}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1\\
ic_{0}\end{pmatrix}e^{-ix},\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-ic_{0}\end{pmatrix}e^{ix}\right\},

(28)

where
\left(\mathcal{L}_{0}-\lambda_{0}\mathcal{R}_{0}\right)^{\dagger}
denotes the adjoint operator with respect to the standard complex inner-product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
on
\textrm{L}^{2}_{\textrm{per}}(-\pi,\pi)\times\textrm{L}^{2}_{\textrm{per}}(-\pi,\pi)
. From (
28
), we arrive at three solvability conditions for (
26
):

\displaystyle\left<-\mathcal{L}_{1}{\bf w_{0}}+\mathcal{R}_{0}\Big(\lambda_{1}{\bf w_{0}}\Big)+\mathcal{R}_{1}\Big(\lambda_{0}{\bf w_{0}}\Big),\begin{pmatrix}1\\
ic_{0}\end{pmatrix}e^{-ix}\right>

\displaystyle=0,

(29a)

\displaystyle\left<-\mathcal{L}_{1}{\bf w_{0}}+\mathcal{R}_{0}\Big(\lambda_{1}{\bf w_{0}}\Big)+\mathcal{R}_{1}\Big(\lambda_{0}{\bf w_{0}}\Big),\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\right>

\displaystyle=0,

(29b)

\displaystyle\left<-\mathcal{L}_{1}{\bf w_{0}}+\mathcal{R}_{0}\Big(\lambda_{1}{\bf w_{0}}\Big)+\mathcal{R}_{1}\Big(\lambda_{0}{\bf w_{0}}\Big),\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-ic_{0}\end{pmatrix}e^{ix}\right>

\displaystyle=0.

(29c)

Simplifying (
29a
) and (
29c
) leads to

\displaystyle 2\beta_{0,-1}\left(\lambda_{1}+i\mu_{1}c_{g}\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(30a)

\displaystyle 2\beta_{0,1}\left(\lambda_{1}+i\mu_{1}c_{g}\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(30b)

respectively, where
c_{g}
denotes the group velocity of
\Omega_{1}
(
18
) evaluated at
k=1
. Explicitly,

\displaystyle c_{g}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha(1-c_{0}^{4})-c_{0}^{2}}{2c_{0}}.

(31)

In contrast to (
29a
) and (
29c
), (
29b
) reduces to a trivial equality and does not contribute an additional solvability condition.

Remark 3.1.1
.

If
c_{0}>0
, a direct calculation shows that
c_{g}<0
for
\alpha>0
.

If we require
\beta_{0,\nu}\neq 0
so that the eigenspace of
\lambda_{0}
remains three-dimensional, (
30a
) and (
30b
) imply

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=-i\mu_{1}c_{g}.

(32)

Since
\mu_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
, the unstable eigenvalues of the Benjamin-Feir instability are imaginary to
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
.

Remark 3.1.2
.

If
\beta_{0,\nu}=0
for some
\nu
, one recovers the imaginary spectrum near the origin, as opposed to the figure-eight curve.

Before proceeding to
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)
, we solve (
26
) subject to (
32
). The solution
{\bf w_{1}}
decomposes into a direct sum of a particular solution
{\bf w_{1,p}}
and a homogeneous solution
{\bf w_{1,h}}
:

\displaystyle{\bf w_{1}}(x)={\bf w_{1,p}}(x)+{\bf w_{1,h}}(x).

(33)

The particular solution can be written as

\displaystyle{\bf w_{1,p}}(x)=\sum_{j=-2}^{2}{\bf w_{1,j}}e^{ijx},

(34)

where
{\bf w_{1,j}}={\bf w_{1,j}}(\alpha,\beta_{0,\nu},\mu_{1})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
. The homogeneous solution is

\displaystyle{\bf w_{1,h}}(x)=\beta_{1,-1}{\bf w_{0,-1}}(x)+\beta_{1,0}{\bf w_{0,0}}(x)+\beta_{1,1}{\bf w_{0,1}}(x),

(35)

coinciding with the eigenspace of
\lambda_{0}
. The coefficients
\beta_{1,\nu}
represent first-order corrections to the zeroth-order eigenfunction correction
{\bf w}_{0}
and are undetermined constants at this order.

Remark 3.1.3
.

The expressions for
{\bf w_{1,j}}
as well as for all other algebraic expressions that are too cumbersome to include explicitly in this manuscript are found in our companion Mathematica files. See the Data Availability Statement at the end of this manuscript for access to these files.

3.2
The
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)
Problem

At
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)
, the spectral problem (
10
) takes the form

\displaystyle\Big(\mathcal{L}_{0}-\lambda_{0}\mathcal{R}_{0}\Big){\bf w_{2}}=-\sum_{j=1}^{2}\mathcal{L}_{j}{\bf w_{2-j}}+\mathcal{R}_{0}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{2}\lambda_{k}{\bf w_{2-k}}\Big)+\sum_{j=1}^{2}\mathcal{R}_{j}\Big(\sum_{k=0}^{2-j}\lambda_{k}{\bf w_{2-j-k}}\Big).

(36)

Proceeding as above, we obtain three nontrivial solvability conditions for (
36
):

\displaystyle 2\beta_{0,-1}\big(\lambda_{2}+ic_{g}r_{1}\mu_{1}\big)+\beta_{0,0}S_{2,-1}\mu_{1}+i\Big(U_{2,-1}\beta_{0,1}+\big(T_{2,-1}\mu_{1}^{2}+V_{2,-1}\big)\beta_{0,-1}\Big)

\displaystyle=0,

(37a)

\displaystyle\beta_{0,0}T_{2,0}\mu_{1}^{2}+iS_{2,0}\mu_{1}\big(\beta_{0,-1}+\beta_{0,1}\big)

\displaystyle=0,

(37b)

\displaystyle 2\beta_{0,1}\big(\lambda_{2}+ic_{g}r_{1}\mu_{1}\big)+\beta_{0,0}S_{2,1}\mu_{1}+i\Big(U_{2,1}\beta_{0,-1}+\big(T_{2,1}\mu_{1}^{2}+V_{2,1}\big)\beta_{0,1}\Big)

\displaystyle=0,

(37c)

where the subscripted coefficients
S
,
T
,
U
, and
V
are all real-valued functions of the aspect ratio
\alpha
. More explicitly, we have:

\displaystyle S_{2,-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha+5c_{0}^{2}-2\alpha c_{0}^{4}-c_{0}^{6}+\alpha c_{0}^{8}}{4c_{0}^{2}},

(38a)

\displaystyle T_{2,-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}-c_{0}^{2}\left(-1+\alpha c_{0}^{2}\right)\left(-2\alpha+c_{0}^{2}+3\alpha c_{0}^{4}\right)}{4c_{0}^{3}},

(38b)

\displaystyle U_{2,-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-2\hat{N}_{2,2}c_{0}^{2}\left(1-3c_{0}^{4}\right)-8\hat{Q}_{2,2}c_{0}^{3}-4c_{0}^{4}+c_{0}^{8}}{4c_{0}^{3}},

(38c)

\displaystyle T_{2,0}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{2}-2\alpha c_{0}^{2}+\left(1-2\alpha^{2}\right)c_{0}^{4}-2\alpha c_{0}^{6}+\alpha^{2}c_{0}^{8}}{4c_{0}^{2}},

(38d)

where
\hat{N}_{2,2}
and
\hat{Q}_{2,2}
are the second-order corrections of the Stokes waves, see Subsection 2.2 and Appendix A of
[
15
]
for more details. The remaining coefficients follow from the identities

\displaystyle S_{2,-1}=-S_{2,1},~T_{2,-1}=-T_{2,1},~U_{2,-1}=-U_{2,1},~V_{2,-1}=U_{2,-1},~S_{2,-1}=c_{0}S_{2,0},

(39)

which hold for
\alpha>0
. The proofs of these identities are shown in the companion Mathematica files.

Proposition 3.2.1
.

For all
\alpha>0
, we have
S_{2,-1}>0
,
T_{2,-1}>0
, and
T_{2,0}<0
.

Proof.

Substituting
c_{0}=\sqrt{\tanh(\alpha)}
in (
38a
), we arrive at

\displaystyle T_{2,-1}=\frac{1}{8}\csch(\alpha)\sech^{3}(\alpha)\left(2\alpha+3\sinh(2\alpha)+\sinh(4\alpha)\right),

(40)

from which
S_{2,-1}>0
follows immediately for
\alpha>0
.

Doing the same for (
38b
), we arrive at

\displaystyle T_{2,-1}=\frac{-1-4\alpha^{2}+8\alpha^{2}\cosh(2\alpha)+\cosh^{2}(2\alpha)-4\alpha\sinh(2\alpha)}{16\tanh^{3/2}(\alpha)\cosh^{4}(\alpha)},

(41)

after some work.
Rearranging terms in the numerator,

\displaystyle T_{2,-1}=\frac{\left(-1-4\alpha^{2}+\cosh^{2}(2\alpha)\right)+4\alpha\cosh(2\alpha)\left(2\alpha-\tanh(2\alpha)\right)}{16\tanh^{3/2}(\alpha)\cosh^{4}(\alpha)}.

(42)

Using the Taylor series of
\cosh
, we have
-1-4\alpha^{2}+\cosh^{2}(2\alpha)>0
immediately. Using the well-known bound
\tanh(|k|)<|k|
for
k\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
2\alpha-\tanh(2\alpha)>0
. It follows that
T_{2,-1}>0
for
\alpha>0
.

Lastly, for (
38d
),

\displaystyle T_{2,0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{64}\csch(\alpha)\sech^{3}(\alpha)\left(e^{4\alpha}-(1+4\alpha)\right)\left(e^{-4\alpha}-(1-4\alpha)\right),

(43)

after some work. Using
\exp(k)>1+k
for
k>0
, we immediately conclude
T_{2,0}<0
for
\alpha>0
, as desired.
∎

Equations (
37a
)-(
37c
) constitute a nonlinear system for the unknown variables
\lambda_{2}
and
\beta_{0,\pm 1}
. The first-order Floquet correction
\mu_{1}
and first-order rescaling of the Floquet interval
r_{1}
appear as parameters in this system. Because of the symmetry of the Floquet interval corresponding to the Benjamin-Feir figure-eight curve, we consider
\mu_{1}>0
without loss of generality, as mentioned before. Also appearing as a parameter in our system is
\beta_{0,0}
, the coefficient of the zeroth mode of
{\bf w_{0}}
. Without loss of generality, we normalize the eigenfunction
{\bf w}
so that
\beta_{0,0}>0
. Under these assumptions, we solve (
37a
)-(
37c
) for
\lambda_{2}
. Using the identities listed in (
39
) as well as the inequalities in the claim above, we find

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{2,R}+i\lambda_{2,I},

(44)

where

\displaystyle\lambda_{2,R}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\sqrt{T_{2,-1}\left(\frac{2\left(S_{2,-1}S_{2,0}-U_{2,-1}T_{2,0}\right)}{T_{2,0}}-T_{2,-1}\mu_{1}^{2}\right)},

(45a)

\displaystyle\lambda_{2,I}

\displaystyle=-r_{1}\mu_{1}c_{g}.

(45b)

Defining

\displaystyle e_{2}=4T_{2,-1},\quad

\displaystyle e_{BW}=\frac{S_{2,-1}S_{2,0}-U_{2,-1}T_{2,0}}{T_{2,0}},\quad\textrm{and}

(46a)

\displaystyle\Delta_{BW}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{e_{2}\left(8e_{BW}-e_{2}\mu_{1}^{2}\right)},

(46b)

(
45a
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\lambda_{2,R}=\pm\frac{\mu_{1}}{8}\Delta_{BW}.

(47)

For (
47
) to be nonzero, we must have
e_{BW}>0
. It is well-known (see, for instance,
[
7
]
) that
e_{BW}>0
only if
\alpha>\alpha_{BW}=1.3627827567...
, the critical threshold for modulational instability originally found in
[
4
,
37
]
. A plot of
e_{BW}
as a function of
\alpha
is shown in Figure
5
.

Remark 3.2.1
.

The variables
e_{2}
and
e_{BW}
correspond directly to the variables
e_{22}
and
e_{WB}
in
[
7
]
, respectively. Using the expressions for
S_{2,-1}
,
U_{2,-1}
,
S_{2,0}
, and
T_{2,0}
above, we obtain an explicit representation of
e_{BW}
:

\displaystyle e_{BW}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left(-1+8\alpha^{2}+\cosh(4\alpha)-4\alpha\sinh(4\alpha)\right)\tanh^{3/2}(\alpha)}\biggr(-4+8\alpha^{2}+8\cosh(2\alpha)\phantom{)}

\displaystyle\phantom{(}\quad\quad\quad+5\cosh(4\alpha)+2\alpha\Big(-9\coth(\alpha)+18\alpha\csch^{2}(2\alpha)-2\sinh(4\alpha)+3\tanh(\alpha)\Big)\biggr).

(48)

The root of this expression for
\alpha>0
is the critical threshold
\alpha_{BW}
.

Provided

\displaystyle 0<\mu_{1}<M,\quad M=\sqrt{\frac{8e_{BW}}{e_{2}}}.

) and second-order eigenvalue corrections (

) and (

. One finds

Remark 3.2.2
.

For the remainder of this work, we restrict to the positive branch of (
47
) and, therefore, obtain a parameterization only for the half loop of the figure-eight curve in the first quadrant of the complex plane. By quadrafold symmetry of the stability spectrum (

Both (

) and (

. This results in ambiguity at

\displaystyle\lambda_{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{1}}{8}\Delta_{BW}\varepsilon^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right),

\displaystyle\lambda_{I}

\displaystyle=-\mu_{1}c_{g}\varepsilon+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right),

which is a lemniscate of Huygens (or Gerono)

The
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right)

\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right)

are given by the solid blue and light blue curves, respectively, while those for the imaginary part are given by the solid orange and red curves, respectively.

In addition to a higher-order description of the figure-eight curve, we can estimate its most unstable eigenvalue by examining the critical points of (
85
) with respect to
\mu_{1}
. For ease of notation, let
\lambda_{2,R}
and
\lambda_{4,R}
denote the second- and fourth-order corrections of (
85
), respectively, and let
\mu_{1,*}
denote the critical points. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu_{1}}\biggr(\lambda_{2,R}(\alpha,\mu_{1})\varepsilon^{2}+\lambda_{4,R}(\alpha,\mu_{1})\varepsilon^{4}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{5}\right)\biggr)\biggr|_{\mu_{1,*}}=0.

(87)

Dropping terms of
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{5}\right)
and smaller, we arrive at an algebraic equation for the critical points:

\displaystyle\lambda_{2,R}^{\prime}(\alpha,\mu_{1,*})+\lambda_{4,R}^{\prime}(\alpha,\mu_{1,*})\varepsilon^{2}

\displaystyle=0,

(88)

where primes denote differentiation with respect to
\mu_{1}
. When
\varepsilon=0
, (
88
) has positive solution

\displaystyle\mu_{1,*_{0}}=2\sqrt{\frac{e_{BW}}{e_{2}}},

(89)

coinciding with the first-order correction of the most unstable Floquet exponent (
54
). When
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
, we expect
\mu_{1,*}
to bifurcate smoothly from
\mu_{1,*_{0}}
. Since the small parameter in (
88
) appears as
\varepsilon^{2}
, we expand
\mu_{1,*}
in
\varepsilon^{2}
, yielding

\displaystyle\mu_{1,*}=\mu_{1,*_{0}}+\varepsilon^{2}\mu_{1,*_{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right).

(90)

Substituting (
90
) into (
88
),

\displaystyle\mu_{1,*_{2}}=-\frac{\lambda_{4,R}^{\prime}(\alpha,\mu_{1,*_{0}})}{\lambda_{2,R}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha,\mu_{1,*_{0}})}.

(91)

at
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)
. To simplify notation further, we drop the functional dependencies above, denoting
\lambda_{4,R}^{\prime}(\alpha,\mu_{1,*_{0}})
and
\lambda_{2,R}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha,\mu_{1,*_{0}})
instead by
\lambda_{4,R,*}^{\prime}
and
\lambda_{2,R,*}^{\prime\prime}
, respectively. Substituting (
91
) into (
25
), we arrive at an asymptotic expansion for the Floquet exponent of the most unstable eigenvalue on the higher-order half-loop:

\displaystyle\mu_{*}

\displaystyle=\left(2\sqrt{\frac{e_{BW}}{e_{2}}}\right)\varepsilon-\left(2\frac{\Lambda_{4,R}^{(1,2,M)}}{\Lambda_{4,R}^{(1,1,M)}}\sqrt{\frac{e_{BW}}{e_{2}}}+\frac{\lambda_{4,R,*}^{\prime}}{\lambda_{2,R,*}^{\prime\prime}}\right)\varepsilon^{3}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right).

(92)

If instead we substitute (
90
) into (
85
), we obtain an asymptotic expansion for the real part of the most unstable eigenvalue. Using our simplified notation above,

\lambda_{R,*}=\lambda_{2,R,*}\varepsilon^{2}+\biggr(\lambda_{2,R,*}^{\prime}\mu_{1,*_{2}}+\lambda_{4,R,*}\biggr)\varepsilon^{4}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{5}\right).

(93)

Unpacking this notation, we obtain the more explicit expansion

\displaystyle\lambda_{R,*}

\displaystyle=\frac{e_{BW}}{2}\varepsilon^{2}+\frac{e_{BW}}{256c_{0}\Lambda_{4,R}^{(1,1,M)}e_{2}^{2}T_{2,0}^{4}}\biggr(c_{0}e_{2}\left(\Lambda_{4,R}^{(1,1,M)}\Lambda_{4,R,*}^{(1,2)}-\Lambda_{4,R}^{(1,2,M)}\Lambda_{4,R,*}^{(1,1)}\right)\phantom{)}

\displaystyle\phantom{(}\quad\quad\quad-4\frac{T_{2,0}}{e_{BW}}\left(\Lambda_{4,R}^{(1,1,M)}\Lambda_{4,R,*}^{(2,2)}-\Lambda_{4,R}^{(1,2,M)}\Lambda_{4,R,*}^{(2,1)}\right)\biggr)\varepsilon^{4}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{5}\right),

(94)

where
\Lambda_{4,R,*}^{(j,\ell)}
denotes
\Lambda_{4,R}^{(j,\ell)}
evaluated at
\mu_{1}=\mu_{1,*_{0}}
.
A similar calculation determines the asymptotic expansion for the imaginary part of this eigenvalue. After some work,

\lambda_{I,*}=-2c_{g}\varepsilon\sqrt{\frac{e_{BW}}{e_{2}}}+\left(-c_{g}\left(\frac{\lambda_{4,R,*}^{\prime}}{\lambda_{2,R,*}^{\prime\prime}}\right)+2\sqrt{\frac{e_{BW}}{e_{2}}}\left(\frac{c_{g}\Lambda^{(1,2,M)}_{4,R}}{\Lambda^{(1,1,M)}_{4,R}}+\frac{\Lambda_{3,I,*}}{32e_{2}T_{2,0}^{2}}\right)\right)\varepsilon^{3}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right),

(95)

where
\Lambda_{3,I,*}
denotes
\Lambda_{3,I}
evaluated at
\mu_{1}=\mu_{1,*_{0}}
. Expansions (

3.5
Comparison of the Benjamin-Feir and High-Frequency Instabilities

High-frequency instabilities of Stokes waves were first explored numerically by Deconinck
&
Oliveras
[
17
]
. Unlike the Benjamin-Feir instability, these instabilities give rise to unstable spectra away from the origin in the complex spectral plane (Figure
2
), resulting in high-frequency spatial oscillations that are not commensurate or nearly commensurate with the fundamental period of the Stokes waves. A complete asymptotic analysis of high-frequency instabilities has been explored by the authors with Trichtchenko
[
15
]
. Since then,
[
22
]
has validated some of these formal asymptotic results rigorously.

For sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, the largest high-frequency instability is closest to the origin. As shown in
[
15
]
, its most unstable eigenvalue has asymptotic expansion

\displaystyle\lambda_{R,*}^{(\textrm{HF})}=\frac{|\mathcal{S}_{2}|}{2\sqrt{\omega(k_{0})\omega(k_{0}+2)}}\varepsilon^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right),

(96)

where
\mathcal{S}_{2}
is a complicated, but explicit, function of the aspect ratio
\alpha
,
\omega
is given by (
18
), and
k_{0}
is an implicit function of
\alpha
defined as the unique solution of

\displaystyle\Omega_{1}(k_{0})=\Omega_{-1}(k_{0}+2),

(97)

for
\Omega_{\sigma}
in (
18
). The leading-order behavior of this instability is
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)
, similar to the Benjamin-Feir case:

\displaystyle\lambda_{R,*}^{(\textrm{BFI})}=\frac{e_{BW}}{2}\varepsilon^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right).

(98)

By comparing coefficients of the leading-order terms in (
96
) and (
98
), we can directly compare the largest growth rates of the high-frequency and Benjamin-Feir instabilities for all
\alpha>0
, see Figure
11
. To our knowledge, this is the first time the growth rates of these two instabilities have been compared using analytical methods. A numerical comparison was is available in
[
17
]
.

For shallow water,
\alpha<\alpha_{BW}
, only high-frequency instabilities are present. For deep water,
\alpha>\alpha_{BW}
, we have two distinct behaviors. When
\alpha_{BW}<\alpha<\alpha_{DO}
, the high-frequency instabilities dominate the Benjamin-Feir instabilities. When
\alpha>\alpha_{DO}
, the Benjamin-Feir instability dominates. The critical threshold
\alpha_{DO}
that distinguishes these behaviors in deep water is well-approximated by the implicit solution of

\displaystyle\frac{|\mathcal{S}_{2}|}{\sqrt{\omega(k_{0})\omega(k_{0}+2)}}=e_{BW},

(99)

for
k_{0}
defined in (
97
). If we solve (
99
) numerically, we find
\alpha_{DO}=1.4308061674...
, matching the numerical result presented in
[
17
]
to four significant digits.

Remark 3.5.1
.

If the
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)
corrections are included in expansions (
96
) and (
98
), then
\alpha_{DO}=\alpha_{DO}(\varepsilon)
, where
\alpha_{DO}(0)=1.4308061674...
. Using dominant balance, we argue that the next correction of
\alpha_{DO}
appears at
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)
, but obtaining this correction explicitly is a computational challenge.

4
The Benjamin-Feir Spectrum in Infinite Depth

4.1
A Few Remarks about Infinite Depth

Our analysis so far applies to the Benjamin-Feir instability spectrum in water of finite depth. In this section, we consider the special case of infinite depth, when
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
. Unfortunately, it is not possible to let
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
using the expressions obtained in Section 3. Indeed, we have already seen that this limit is singular in the eigenfunction coefficients
\beta_{0,\pm 1}
, which affects the description of the figure-eight curve at
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right)
and subsequent orders. The peculiarities of this limit are what cause the failure of the proof by Bridges
&
Mielke
[
8
]
in infinitely deep water, necessitating the alternative proof by Nguyen
&
Strauss
[
33
]
. This also leads to the qualitative differences in the exact representations of the figure-eight curve in finite and infinite depth, as seen in
[
6
,
7
]
.

In this section, we outline the steps of our spectral perturbation method applied to the Benjamin-Feir instability in infinite depth, starting with the unperturbed problem. Replacing the finite-depth operators in the spectral problem (
10
) with their infinite-depth equivalents, see Subsection 2.3, we find the following spectral data at
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{0}\right)
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{0}=0,\quad\quad\mu_{0}

\displaystyle=0,\quad\quad\textrm{and}

(100a)

\displaystyle{\bf w_{0}}(x)=\beta_{0,-1}{\bf w_{0,-1}}(x)+

\displaystyle\beta_{0,0}{\bf w_{0,0}}(x)+\beta_{0,1}{\bf w_{0,1}}(x),

(100b)

where
\beta_{0,j}\in\mathbb{C}
are undetermined at this order and

\displaystyle{\bf w_{0,-1}}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
i\end{pmatrix}e^{-ix},\quad{\bf w_{0,0}}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix},\quad\textrm{and}\quad{\bf w_{0,1}}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-i\end{pmatrix}e^{ix}.

(101)

A direct calculation shows that
{\bf w_{0}}
above is in fact the limit of
{\bf w_{0}}
in finite depth (
23
) as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
.

To proceed to higher order, we expand the infinite-depth operators as power series in
\varepsilon
. Some of these operators involve expressions of the form
|n+\mu|
, where
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. When we expand
\mu
using (
25
), it becomes necessary to expand
|n+\mu|
as well. To obtain these expansions, we exploit the following identity:

\displaystyle|a+b|=|a|+\text{sgn}(a)b,

(102)

provided
a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
such that
|b|<|a|
. Substituting (
25
) into
|n+\mu|
, equating
\mu_{0}=0
, and applying (
102
) for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
yields

\displaystyle|n+\mu|=\begin{cases}|n|+\text{sgn}(n)\varepsilon\mu_{1}(1+r(\varepsilon)),&n\neq 0,\\
|\varepsilon\mu_{1}|(1+r(\varepsilon)),&n=0.\end{cases}

(103)

Consequently, all infinite-depth operators involving
|n+\mu|
require two expansions: one for
n\neq 0
and one for
n=0
.

4.2
The
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon\right)
Problem

The
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon\right)
problem in infinite depth takes the same form as (
26
) with the finite-depth operators replaced by their infinite-depth equivalents and the expansions of these operators carried out appropriately. Three solvability conditions are obtained from this problem. One results in a trivial equality, similar to finite depth, and the remaining two are

\displaystyle\beta_{0,-1}\left(\lambda_{1}-i\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(104a)

\displaystyle\beta_{0,1}\left(\lambda_{1}-i\frac{\mu_{1}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=0.

(104b)

Imposing
\beta_{0,\pm}\neq 1
as in finite depth, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=i\frac{\mu_{1}}{2},

(105)

which is consistent with (
32
), since
c_{g}\rightarrow-1/2
as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
.

Having satisfied the solvability conditions, we solve the
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon\right)
problem for the first-order eigenfunction correction in infinite depth,

\displaystyle{\bf w_{1}}(x)={\bf w_{1,p}}(x)+\beta_{1,-1}{\bf w_{0,-1}}(x)+\beta_{1,0}{\bf w_{0,0}}(x)+\beta_{1,1}{\bf w_{0,1}}(x),

(106)

where the coefficients
\beta_{1,j}\in\mathbb{C}
are undetermined at this order and

\displaystyle{\bf w_{1,p}}(x)

\displaystyle=\beta_{0,-1}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
i\end{pmatrix}e^{-2ix}+\frac{1}{2}i\mu_{1}\beta_{0,-1}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}e^{-ix}+\frac{1}{2}i\mu_{1}\beta_{0,0}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}+\frac{1}{2}i\mu_{1}\beta_{0,1}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}e^{ix}

(107)

\displaystyle+\beta_{0,1}\begin{pmatrix}1\\
-i\end{pmatrix}e^{2ix},

which coincides with the
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
limit of the corresponding particular solution in finite depth.

4.3
The
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)
Problem

Similar to (
36
) in finite depth, this problem has three nontrivial solvability conditions

\displaystyle 2\beta_{0,-1}\left(\lambda_{2}-i\frac{1}{2}r_{1}\mu_{1}\right)+i\left(-\beta_{0,1}+\left(\frac{1}{4}\mu_{1}^{2}-1\right)\beta_{0,-1}\right)

\displaystyle=0,

(108a)

\displaystyle\beta_{0,0}\mu_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=0,

(108b)

\displaystyle 2\beta_{0,1}\left(\lambda_{2}-i\frac{1}{2}r_{1}\mu_{1}\right)+i\left(\beta_{0,-1}+\left(-\frac{1}{4}\mu_{1}^{2}+1\right)\beta_{0,1}\right)

\displaystyle=0.

(108c)

Equations (
108a
) and (
108c
) are the limits of their respective equations (
37a
) and (
37c
) in the finite-depth case, since

\displaystyle\quad S_{2,-1}\rightarrow 0,~T_{2,-1}\rightarrow\frac{1}{4},~U_{2,-1}\rightarrow-1,~V_{2,-1}\rightarrow-1,

(109)

as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
. The same is true for the second equation (
108b
) if one divides the finite-depth equation (
37b
) by
T_{2,0}
first. Then, because

\displaystyle S_{2,0}\rightarrow 1,~T_{2,0}\rightarrow-\infty,

(110)

as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
, the rescaled (
37b
) tends to (
108b
). The unbounded growth of
T_{2,0}
as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
is the reason for the differences between the finite depth and infinite depth calculations, as mentioned in Subsection 3.3.

Equation (
108b
) implies
\mu_{1}=0
or
\beta_{0,0}=0
. Both numerical
[
17
]
and rigorous results
[
6
,
33
]
suggest
\mu_{1}\neq 0
, and we choose
\beta_{0,0}=0
.

Remark 4.3.1
.

We avoided normalizing
{\bf w}
at the start of our analysis because
\beta_{0,0}=0
in infinite depth. Indeed, had we chosen a non-zero normalization for
\beta_{0,0}
, we would need to renormalize our asymptotic expansions in infinite depth to avoid inconsistencies at higher order.

The remaining solvability conditions (
108a
) and (
108c
) form a nonlinear system of two equations in the three unknowns
\lambda_{2}
and
\beta_{0,\pm 1}
. Without loss of generality, we choose
\beta_{0,-1}
as a free parameter and solve for
\lambda_{2}
and
\beta_{0,1}
. As in finite depth, we restrict our analysis to
\mu_{1}>0
. Solving for
\lambda_{2}
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{2,R}+i\lambda_{2,I},

(111)

with

\displaystyle\lambda_{2,R}

\displaystyle=\pm\frac{\mu_{1}}{8}\sqrt{8-\mu_{1}^{2}},

(112a)

\displaystyle\lambda_{2,I}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}r_{1}\mu_{1}.

(112b)

Equations (
112a
) and (
112b
) are the limits of (
45a
) and (
45b
), respectively, since

\displaystyle e_{2}\rightarrow 1,~e_{BW}\rightarrow 1,

(113)

and
c_{g}\rightarrow-1/2
as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
. For
\lambda_{2}
to have a nonzero real part, we must have

\displaystyle 0<\mu_{1}<2\sqrt{2},

(114)

which is consistent with (
49
) as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
. Floquet exponents satisfying (
114
) parameterize a single loop of the Benjamin-Feir figure-eight curve in the upper-half complex plane. If we repeat our analysis with
\mu_{1}<0
, we find
-2\sqrt{2}<\mu_{1}<0
, which parameterizes the remaining loop of the figure-eight. Combined, the full parameterizing interval of the figure-eight curve in infinite depth is

\displaystyle\mu\in\varepsilon\left(-2\sqrt{2},2\sqrt{2}\right)\left(1+r_{1}\varepsilon\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right).

(115)

To simplify the remaining analysis, we restrict to a half-loop of the figure-eight curve by choosing the positive branch of (
112a
), as in finite depth.

The imaginary correction (
112b
) and Floquet parameterization (
115
) depend on the first-order rescaling parameter

. Assembling our expansions for the real and imaginary parts of the half loop in infinite depth,

\displaystyle\lambda_{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mu_{1}}{8}\varepsilon^{2}\sqrt{8-\mu_{1}^{2}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right),

(116a)

\displaystyle\lambda_{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mu_{1}\varepsilon-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mu_{1}\varepsilon^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right).

(116b)

These expansions agree well with numerical computations for sufficiently small

\displaystyle 4\left(-1+4\sqrt{2}\varepsilon-12\varepsilon^{2}+8\sqrt{2}\varepsilon^{3}-4\varepsilon^{4}\right)\lambda_{R}^{2}=2\varepsilon^{2}\left(-1+4\sqrt{2}\varepsilon-4\varepsilon^{2}\right)\lambda_{I}^{2}+\lambda_{I}^{4},

(117)

\beta_{2,j}\in\mathbb{C}
are undetermined at this order and

\displaystyle{\bf w_{2,p}}(x)=\sum_{j=-3}^{3}{\bf w_{2,j}}e^{ijx},

(124)

where
{\bf w_{2,j}}={\bf w_{2,j}}(\beta_{0,-1},\beta_{1,\nu},r_{1})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
, see the companion Mathematica files for details.

4.4
The
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right)
Problem

The solvability conditions of the
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right)
problem form a
3\times 3
linear system

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{1,0}\\
\lambda_{3}\\
\beta_{1,1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}f_{3,1}\\
f_{3,2}\\
f_{3,3}\end{pmatrix},

(125)

where
\mathcal{M}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{1}&2\beta_{0,-1}&-i\\
-\mu_{1}^{2}&0&0\\
-\mu_{1}&2\beta_{0,1}&-\frac{i}{4}\left(-4+8i\lambda_{2}+4r_{1}\mu_{1}+\mu_{1}^{2}\right)\end{pmatrix},

(126)

and

\displaystyle f_{3,1}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{4}\beta_{1,-1}\biggr(4+8i\lambda_{2}+4r_{1}\mu_{1}-\mu_{1}^{2}\biggr),

(127a)

\displaystyle f_{3,2}

\displaystyle=-i\mu_{1}^{2}\biggr(\beta_{0,-1}+\beta_{0,1}\biggr),

(127b)

\displaystyle f_{3,3}

\displaystyle=-i\biggr(\beta_{1,-1}+\frac{1}{4}\beta_{0,1}\mu_{1}\Big(6-4r_{2}+4i\lambda_{2}+\mu_{1}^{2}\Big)\biggr).

(127c)

If we substitute expressions for
\lambda_{2}
and
\beta_{0,1}
on the half-loop into (
126
), we find

\displaystyle\textrm{det}\left(\mathcal{M}\right)=\mu_{1}^{3}\beta_{0,-1}\sqrt{8-\mu_{1}^{2}},

(128)

implying (
125
) has a unique set of solutions for
0<\mu_{1}<2\sqrt{2}
, as desired. The solution for
\lambda_{3}
is

\displaystyle\lambda_{3}=\lambda_{3,R}+i\lambda_{3,I},

(129)

with

\displaystyle\lambda_{3,R}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu_{1}\left(2\mu_{1}+r_{1}\left(-4+\mu_{1}^{2}\right)\right)}{4\sqrt{8-\mu_{1}^{2}}},

(130a)

\displaystyle\lambda_{3,I}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{16}\mu_{1}\left(16-8r_{2}+\mu_{1}^{2}\right).

(130b)

To avoid singular behavior in
\lambda_{3,R}
as
\mu_{1}\rightarrow 2\sqrt{2}
, we choose
r_{1}
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu_{1}\rightarrow 2\sqrt{2}}\Big(2\mu_{1}+r_{1}(-4+\mu_{1}^{2})\Big)=0,

(131)

according to the regular curve condition. We find
r_{1}=-\sqrt{2}
, justifying our prior claim.

Given
r_{1}=-\sqrt{2}
, we see that (
130a
) and (
130b
) are nonzero for generic choices of
0<\mu_{1}<2\sqrt{2}
. Hence, we have obtained a higher-order correction to both the real and imaginary parts of the figure-eight curve in infinite depth. This contrasts with the finite-depth case, where an imaginary correction only was found at
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right)
.

To characterize this higher-order correction, it is necessary to determine the value of the second-order rescaling parameter
r_{2}
appearing in (
130b
). We show at the next order that
r_{2}=13/8
, using the regular curve condition. Assuming this for now, we assemble our expansions for the real and imaginary parts along a half-loop of this higher-order curve

\displaystyle\lambda_{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\mu_{1}\varepsilon^{2}\sqrt{8-\mu_{1}^{2}}\left(1+\left(\frac{2(-2\mu_{1}+\sqrt{2}(-4+\mu_{1}^{2}))}{8-\mu_{1}^{2}}\right)\varepsilon\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right),

(132a)

\displaystyle\lambda_{I}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mu_{1}\varepsilon\Big(1-\sqrt{2}\varepsilon-\frac{1}{8}\big(3+\mu_{1}^{2}\big)\varepsilon^{2}\Big)+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right),

(132b)

respectively. The interval of Floquet exponents for the entire curve has asymptotic expansion

\displaystyle\mu\in\varepsilon\left(-2\sqrt{2},2\sqrt{2}\right)\left(1-\sqrt{2}\varepsilon+\frac{13}{8}\varepsilon^{2}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right).

(133)

These expansions agree well with numerical computations for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, see Figure
15
. In theory, one could eliminate the dependence of
\mu_{1}
from (
132a
) and (
132b
) to obtain an algebraic curve that approximates the true figure-eight to
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right)
, but this process is cumbersome and provides little insight into the behavior of the true figure-eight curve.

Proceeding as in Section 3.4, we can derive an asymptotic expansion for the most unstable eigenvalue on this figure-eight curve and for its corresponding Floquet exponent. In particular, if we let
\mu_{1,*}
denote a critical point of (
132a
), then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu_{1}}\Biggr(\frac{1}{8}\mu_{1}\varepsilon^{2}\sqrt{8-\mu_{1}^{2}}\left(1+\left(\frac{2(-2\mu_{1}+\sqrt{2}(-4+\mu_{1}^{2}))}{8-\mu_{1}^{2}}\right)\varepsilon\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)\Biggr)\biggr|_{\mu_{1}=\mu_{1,*}}=0.

(134)

Dropping terms of
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)
and smaller, we arrive at the following equation for
\mu_{1,*}
:

\displaystyle 32-12\mu_{1,*}^{2}+\mu_{1,*}^{4}-\varepsilon\big(32\sqrt{2}+32\mu_{1,*}-24\mu_{1,*}^{2}\sqrt{2}-2\mu_{1,*}^{3}+2\mu_{1,*}^{4}\sqrt{2}\big)=0.

(135)

When
\varepsilon=0
, the only positive solution of (
135
) is
\mu_{1,*_{0}}=2
, which coincides with (
121b
) from the previous order. When
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
,

\displaystyle\mu_{1,*}=\mu_{1,*_{0}}+\varepsilon\mu_{1,*_{1}}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right),

(136)

since
\varepsilon
appears as the small parameter in (
135
). Substituting (
136
) into (
135
), we find at
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon\right)
that
\mu_{1,*_{1}}=-3+2\sqrt{2}.
Thus, the Floquet exponent of the most unstable eigenvalue on the figure-eight has asymptotic expansion

\displaystyle\mu_{*}

\displaystyle=\left(2+(-3+2\sqrt{2})\varepsilon+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)\right)\varepsilon\left(1-\varepsilon\sqrt{2}+\frac{13}{8}\varepsilon^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right)\right),

(137)

which simplifies to

\displaystyle\mu_{*}=2\varepsilon-3\varepsilon^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right).

Substituting (
136
) into (
132a
) and (
132b
), we obtain asymptotic expansions

\displaystyle\lambda_{R,*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}-\varepsilon^{3}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right),

(138a)

\displaystyle\lambda_{I,*}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon-\frac{3}{2}\varepsilon^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right),

(138b)

for the real and imaginary part of this most unstable eigenvalue on the half-loop, respectively. These expansions agree well with numerical computations (Figure
16
), although not to the same degree as the corresponding results in finite depth. This is a result of resolving the higher-order figure-eight curve in infinite depth at
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right)
as opposed to
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)
. To complete our analysis of the solvability conditions (
125
), we report solutions for
\beta_{1,0}
and
\beta_{1,1}
on the half-loop with
r_{1}=-\sqrt{2}
. We find

\displaystyle\beta_{1,0}

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}\beta_{0,-1}\left(\sqrt{8-\mu_{1}^{2}}+\frac{i}{4}\mu_{1}\right),

(139a)

\displaystyle\beta_{1,1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\left(\mu_{1}\left(-4-4\mu_{1}\sqrt{2}+3\mu_{1}^{2}\right)\beta_{0,-1}+2\left(-4+\mu_{1}^{2}\right)\beta_{1,-1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+\frac{i\mu_{1}}{8\sqrt{8-\mu_{1}^{2}}}\left(\left(16\sqrt{2}+\mu_{1}\left(-16-4\mu_{1}\sqrt{2}+3\mu_{1}^{2}\right)\right)\beta_{0,-1}+2\left(-8+\mu_{1}^{2}\right)\beta_{1,-1}\right),

(139b)

where
\beta_{0,-1}
and
\beta_{1,-1}
depend on the normalization of
{\bf w}
.

Finally, we solve the
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{3}\right)
problem subject to the solvability conditions (
125
), arriving at an expression for the third-order eigenfunction correction

\displaystyle{\bf w_{3}}(x)=\sum_{j=-4}^{4}{\bf w_{3,j}}e^{ijx}+\beta_{3,-1}{\bf w_{0,-1}}(x)+\beta_{3,0}{\bf w_{0,0}}(x)+\beta_{3,1}{\bf w_{0,1}}(x)

4.5
The
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)
Problem

The solvability conditions at
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{4}\right)
also form a
3\times 3
linear system

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{2,0}\\
\lambda_{4}\\
\beta_{2,1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}f_{4,1}\\
f_{4,2}\\
f_{4,3}\end{pmatrix},

(141)

where
\mathcal{M}
is the same as before and

\displaystyle f_{4,1}

\displaystyle=-\biggr(\beta_{2,-1}\biggr(2\lambda_{2}+\frac{i}{4}\left(-4+\mu_{1}4\sqrt{2}+\mu_{1}^{2}\right)\biggr)+\beta_{1,-1}\biggr(2\lambda_{3}+\frac{\mu_{1}}{4}\big(-4ir_{2}+4\lambda_{2}\phantom{)}\phantom{)}\phantom{)}

\displaystyle\phantom{(}\phantom{(}\quad\quad\quad\hskip-7.96674pt+i\big(6+\mu_{1}^{2}\big)\big)\biggr)-\frac{1}{4}\mu_{1}\beta_{1,0}\left(4\sqrt{2}+\mu_{1}\right)+\beta_{0,-1}\biggr(-2i-i\lambda_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{3}\mu_{1}+\lambda_{2}\mu_{1}^{2}\phantom{)}

\displaystyle\phantom{(}\phantom{(}\quad\quad\quad\hskip-7.96674pt-\frac{i}{8}\mu_{1}\left(12\sqrt{2}+8r_{3}+\mu_{1}\left(3+2\mu_{1}\left(\sqrt{2}-\mu_{1}\right)\right)\right)\biggr)+i\beta_{0,1}\left(-2+\frac{3}{16}\mu_{1}^{2}\right)\biggr),

(142a)

\displaystyle f_{4,2}

\displaystyle=-i\mu_{1}^{2}\biggr(\beta_{1,-1}-\frac{i}{4}\beta_{1,0}\big(8\sqrt{2}+\mu_{1}\big)+\beta_{1,1}-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{0,-1}\big(4\sqrt{2}+\mu_{1}\big)-2\beta_{0,1}\sqrt{2}\biggr),

(142b)

\displaystyle f_{4,3}

\displaystyle=-\biggr(i\beta_{2,-1}+\mu_{1}\beta_{1,0}\big(\sqrt{2}-\frac{1}{4}\mu_{1}\big)+\beta_{1,1}\biggr(2\lambda_{3}+\frac{i}{4}\mu_{1}\left(6-4r_{2}+4i\lambda_{2}+\mu_{1}^{2}\right)\biggr)\phantom{)}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+i\beta_{0,-1}\big(2-\frac{3}{16}\mu_{1}^{2}\big)+\beta_{0,1}\biggr(2i+i\lambda_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{2}\mu_{1}^{2}-\frac{i}{8}\mu_{1}\big(12\sqrt{2}+8r_{3}-8i\lambda_{3}\phantom{)}\phantom{)}

\displaystyle\phantom{(}\phantom{(}\phantom{(}\phantom{(}\quad\quad\quad\hskip-15.36429pt+\mu_{1}\big(-3\phantom{)}+2\mu_{1}\big(\sqrt{2}+\mu_{1}\big)\big)\big)\biggr)\biggr).

(142c)

Solving for
\lambda_{4}
on the half loop, we find

\displaystyle\lambda_{4}=\lambda_{4,R}+i\lambda_{4,I},

(143)

with

\displaystyle\lambda_{4,R}

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}\left(\frac{-2176+32r_{2}\left(32-12\mu_{1}^{2}+\mu_{1}^{4}\right)+\mu_{1}\left(1024\sqrt{2}+\mu_{1}\left(432-64\mu_{1}\sqrt{2}-92\mu_{1}^{2}+5\mu_{1}^{4}\right)\right)}{128\left(8-\mu_{1}^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\right),

\displaystyle\lambda_{4,I}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{16}\mu_{1}\left(16\sqrt{2}+8r_{3}+\mu_{1}\left(8+3\mu_{1}\sqrt{2}\right)\right).

(144b)

For ease of notation, let
\Lambda_{4,R}
denote the numerator of (
144a
). A direct calculation shows that
\Lambda_{4,R}
factors as follows:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{4,R}

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}\left(2\sqrt{2}-\mu_{1}\right)\Big(-544\sqrt{2}+240\mu_{1}+168\mu_{1}^{2}\sqrt{2}+52\mu_{1}^{3}-10\mu_{1}^{4}\sqrt{2}-5\mu_{1}^{5}\phantom{)}

\displaystyle\phantom{(}\quad\quad\quad\hskip-12.80365pt-32r_{2}\left(2\sqrt{2}+\mu_{1}\right)\left(-4+\mu_{1}^{2}\right)\Big).

(145)

It appears that (
144a
) already satisfies the regular curve condition, since
\Lambda_{4,R}\rightarrow 0
as
\mu_{1}\rightarrow 2\sqrt{2}
. However, the factor of
8-\mu_{1}^{2}
in the denominator of (

) is one power larger than at the previous order (
130a
). Thus, we cannot guarantee regular behavior of
\lambda_{4,R}
if only the first factor of (

) tends to zero as
\mu_{1}\rightarrow 2\sqrt{2}
. We must also impose similar behavior on the second factor:

\displaystyle\lim_{\mu_{1}\rightarrow 2\sqrt{2}}\Big(-544\sqrt{2}+240\mu_{1}+168\mu_{1}^{2}\sqrt{2}+52\mu_{1}^{3}-10\mu_{1}^{4}\sqrt{2}-5\mu_{1}^{5}\phantom{)}-32r_{2}\left(2\sqrt{2}+\mu_{1}\right)\left(-4+\mu_{1}^{2}\right)\Big)=0.

(146)

Solving (
146
) for
r_{2}
gives us the desired result
r_{2}=13/8
. As a consequence, the final expression for the fourth-order real part correction (
144a
) becomes

\displaystyle\lambda_{4,R}

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}\left(\frac{-512+\mu_{1}\left(1024\sqrt{2}+\mu_{1}\left(-192+\mu_{1}\left(-64\sqrt{2}+5\mu_{1}\left(-8+\mu_{1}^{2}\right)\right)\right)\right)}{128\left(8-\mu_{1}^{2}\right)^{3/2}}\right).

(147)

Since (
144b
) and (
147
) are generically nonzero for
0<\mu_{1}<2\sqrt{2}
, we have found another higher-order approximation to a half loop of the figure-eight curve in infinite depth, up to the unknown third-order rescaling parameter
r_{3}
. Presumably, one can determine this value at
\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{5}\right)
using the techniques presented in this section. We stop here, since we have already obtained a higher-order approximation to the figure-eight curve at the previous order.

5
Conclusions

Building on work by Akers
[
3
]
and collaborations with Trichtchenko
et al.

[
13
,
14
,
15
]
, we have developed a formal perturbation method to compute high-order asymptotic approximations of the Benjamin-Feir figure-eight curve, present in the stability spectrum of small-amplitude Stokes waves in water of sufficient depth. Unlike traditional methods in spectral perturbation theory
[
25
]
, this method allows us to approximate the entire curve at once.

Using our method, we are able to determine

(i)

the Floquet exponents that parameterize the figure-eight curve,

(ii)

the real and imaginary parts of the most unstable eigenvalue on the figure-eight curve, and

(iii)

algebraic curves asymptotic to the figure-eight curve.

We compare these expressions directly with numerical computations of the figure-eight curve using methods presented in
[
17
]
. To our knowledge, this is the first time a numerical and analytical description of the Benjamin-Feir instability have been compared. Excellent agreement between these descriptions is found in finite and infinite depth, even for modest values of the Stokes wave amplitude
\varepsilon
. Our expressions are also consistent with the rigorous results of Berti
et al.

[
6
,
7
]
and their heuristic approximations of the figure-eight curve.

In addition, our asymptotic results elucidate key differences between the behavior of the Benjamin-Feir instability spectrum in finite and infinite depth. In particular, the first-order rescaling parameter
r_{1}
for the Floquet parameterization of the figure-eight curve vanishes in finite depth, while
r_{1}=-\sqrt{2}
in infinite depth. Consequently, the second-order rescaling correction
r_{2}
is singular in finite depth as one approaches infinitely deep water,
i.e.
, as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
. This singularity propagates to the imaginary part of the figure-eight curve at third order and to the real part of the figure-eight curve at fourth order. Thus, the limit as
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
is singular for the Benjamin-Feir instability, illustrating the breakdown of compactness mentioned in
[
7
,
8
,
33
]
.

Using asymptotic results in this work and in
[
15
]
, we are able to compare the Benjamin-Feir instability and the most unstable high-frequency instability for the first time analytically. Our analysis suggests three natural regimes for the water wave problem:

(i)

Shallow water, which occurs when
\kappa h<\alpha_{BW}=1.3627827567...
and only high-frequency instabilities are present,

(ii)

Intermediate water, which occurs when
\alpha_{BW}<\kappa h<\alpha_{DO}(\varepsilon)=1.4308061674...+\mathcal{O}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\right)
and both instabilities are present, but high-frequency instabilities dominate,

(iii)

Deep water, which occurs when
\kappa h>\alpha_{DO}(\varepsilon)
and both instabilities are present, but the Benjamin-Feir instability dominates.

Here,
\kappa
is the wavenumber of the Stokes wave and
h
is the depth of the water. These regimes are supported by numerical computations in
[
17
]
. We conclude that Stokes waves of all depths and all wavenumbers are unstable to the Benjamin-Feir instability, high-frequency instabilities, or both.
