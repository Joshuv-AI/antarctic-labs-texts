Stochastic Mirror Descent in Average Ensemble Models
Taylan Kargin, Fariborz Salehi, Babak Hassibi

Abstract
The stochastic mirror descent (SMD) algorithm is a general class of training algorithms, which includes the celebrated stochastic gradient descent (SGD), as a special case. It utilizes a mirror potential to influence the implicit bias of the training algorithm. In this paper we explore the performance of the SMD iterates on mean-field ensemble models. Our results generalize earlier ones obtained for SGD on such models. The evolution of the distribution of parameters is mapped to a continuous time process in the space of probability distributions. Our main result gives a nonlinear partial differential equation to which the continuous time process converges in the asymptotic regime of large networks. The impact of the mirror potential appears through a multiplicative term that is equal to the inverse of its Hessian and which can be interpreted as defining a gradient flow over an appropriately defined Riemannian manifold. We provide numerical simulations which allow us to study and characterize the effect of the mirror potential on the performance of networks trained with SMD for some binary classification problems.

Introduction

Machine learning models have been successfully used in a wide variety of applications ranging from spam detection, to image classification, to natural language processing. Despite this great success, our theoretical understanding on why various machine learning methods demonstrate the performances they do is still at an early stage.

To obtain some understanding of the behavior of machine learning algorithms we shall focus on the structure/architecture of the model, as well as the training mechanism that generates a suitable model with respect to the available data. With regards to the architecture, a common theme among most modern machine learning models (e.g. artificial/convolutional/recurrent neural networks) is that they repetitively use simple blocks such as perceptrons in ANNs, and convolution filters in CNNs. The idea of combining multiple simple models has its roots in classical statistics and is known as boosting. The justification is that a simple model often possesses a low variance while its bias can be potentially high; therefore, combining multiple such models can reduce the bias while keeping the variance low.

The optimization algorithm maps the training data to model parameters. Its role is especially critical in modern setups where the number of parameters can be overwhelmingly larger than the number of inputs. In such overparameterized settings, there are often many models that perfectly fit the training data. Thus, the specific choice of the optimization algorithm determines the resulting model in the interpolating regime. In fact, understanding the connection between the optimization algorithm and the generalization performance of the resulting model is an important open problem.

In this paper we attempt to study these aspects by analyzing the convergence behavior of the stochastic mirror descent (SMD) iterates on a general class of models, known as
average ensemble models
where the output is generated by taking an average of similar models that only differ in their choice of parameters. Two-layer neural networks, e.g., can be viewed as a special case of the average ensemble.

In particular, we address the impact of the potential function used in SMD on the parameters of the average ensemble model. Introduced by
Nemirovskii and Yudin 1983
for convex optimization problems with underlying geometric structure, SMD is as a generalization of stochastic gradient descent (SGD) where the updates are
mirrored
to a dual domain using a
potential function
. It has been shown that choice of the potential function plays a pivotal role in determining the implicit bias of the SMD in the interpolating regime
Azizan et al. 2019
,
Gunasekar et al. 2020a
.

1.1
Contributions and Prior Work

In this paper, we develop a mean-field type result for average ensemble models trained with SMD. We consider a general class of convex loss functions that includes the squared loss as a special case. In particular, we derive a partial differential equation that describes the "time evolution" of the distribution of the parameters in the network as training progresses. This distribution can be used to compute the risk, or generalization error, of the trained model. Our result generalizes similar mean-field results for SGD, with the difference being that the Hessian of the potential function appears. We further give a geometric interpretation at the "distributional" level by showing an equivalent formulation of our result as a geometric flow on an appropriate Riemannian manifold. We finally provide numerical results to illustrate the applicability of the method and to showcase the effect of the potential function on the performance and implicit bias of the resulting trained network.

A mean-field characterization of two-layer neural networks trained with SGD under quadratic loss was studied by
Mei et al. 2018
,
Sirignano and Spiliopoulos 2020
, and
Rotskoff and Vanden-Eijnden 2019
. A similar line of work is given by
Chizat and Bach 2018
where more general loss functions are considered for gradient descent algorithm.
Mean field results with similar flavor to ours have been given in
Raginsky and Bouvrie 2012
and
Borovykh et al. 2020
in the context of distributed convex optimization. These papers consider the problem of minimizing a fixed objective function with noisy information by many "interacting optimizers" coupled through an interaction matrix. Despite being insightful, the setting presented in these papers does not fit into the case of learning with average ensemble models.
An important connection between mirror descent and natural gradient descent on dual (Riemannian) space was established by
Raskutti and Mukherjee 2014
. Works by
Gunasekar et al. 2020a
and
Gunasekar et al. 2020b
advanced this geometric viewpoint by showing a correspondence between continuous-time mirror descent and Riemannian gradient flow on the primal domain governed by the Hessian matrix of the potential function.

2.2
Problem Setup

Consider an online learning setting where we sequentially observe a fresh sample of data, i.e.,
(\mathbf{x}^{k},y^{k})
for
k=1,2,\ldots
. Here
\mathbf{x}^{k}\in\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the feature vector, and
y^{k}\in\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is the label. As a shorthand, we will interchangeably use
\mathbf{z}:=(\mathbf{x},y)
. Our goal is to learn a model that generates an estimate of the label given the feature vector as its input. A common approach for modeling is known as the average ensemble, where the output is generated by taking the average of simpler models that only differ in their parameters. This approach is advantageous from the bias-variance perspective. Since simpler models have a low variance (yet potentially a high bias), the average ensemble generates a low-variance estimate of the labels with a reasonably good bias.

Given a parameter space
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\sigma:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
, consider the following parametric family of model functions,

\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}=\{\sigma(\cdot,\bm{\theta}):\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y};\;\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{S}\}.

(2)

This parametric representation of model functions has applications in various settings. For instance, by setting
d=p
and defining
\sigma(\mathbf{v}_{1},\mathbf{v}_{2})=\sigma^{\star}(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{T}\mathbf{v}_{2})
, the class
\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}
represents the generalized linear models with nonlinearity
\sigma^{\star}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

The average ensemble model generates an estimate by taking the average of
n
models in
\mathcal{H}_{\sigma}
. In other words, for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
the estimate is computed as follows,

\hat{y}=h_{n}(\mathbf{x};\,\bm{\Theta}):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma(\mathbf{x},{\bm{\theta}}_{i}),

(3)

where
\bm{\Theta}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\theta}_{1},\penalty\ \bm{\theta}_{2},\ldots,\penalty\ \bm{\theta}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
.

As explained above, average ensemble models are used in many practical settings. A popular instance of such models is the two-layer neural network, where
{\bm{\theta}}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{i},\penalty\ w_{i}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
, and the output is computed as,

\hat{y}_{NN}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{w_{i}}{n}\bar{\sigma}(\mathbf{x},\bar{\bm{\theta}}_{i}),

(4)

where
\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
represents the weights of the last layer of the network and
\bar{\sigma}(\cdot)
is the activation function.

Given a loss function
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and a training dataset
\{(\mathbf{x}^{k},y^{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}
, our goal is to minimize the empirical risk

\inf_{\Theta\in\mathcal{S}^{n}}\left\{\hat{R}_{n}(\Theta):=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\ell(y^{k},\,h_{n}(\mathbf{x}^{k};\,\Theta))\right\}\penalty\ ,

(5)

over parameters
\bm{\Theta}
. We assume that the training data points are generated independently from a distribution, i.e.,
(\mathbf{x}^{k},y^{k})\sim\mu(d\mathbf{z})
. The loss function
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is assumed to be convex w.r.t. its second argument. Important examples are the quadratic loss,
\ell(u,v)=\frac{1}{2}(u-v)^{2}
, and the logistic loss,
\ell(u,v)=\log\big(1+\exp(-u\times v)\big)
. However, the problem is non-convex whenever
\sigma
is.
One can rewrite the average ensemble model as an integral over the empirical distribution of the parameters,
\hat{\rho}_{n}(d\bm{\theta}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\delta_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}(d\bm{\theta})
, i.e.,

h_{n}(\mathbf{x};\,\bm{\Theta})=\langle\hat{\rho}_{n},\sigma(\mathbf{x},.)\rangle.

(6)

This new form leads us to define ensemble averages by any probability distribution on
\mathcal{S}
. Namely, we define

h(\mathbf{x};\,\rho)=\langle\rho,\sigma(\mathbf{x},.)\rangle,

(7)

for any
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
. We call this the
mean-field ensemble model
and the former the
finite ensemble model
. It is easy to check that
h_{n}(\mathbf{x};\,\bm{\Theta})=h(\mathbf{x};\,\hat{\rho}_{n})
. Note that whenever the loss function
\ell
is convex, the mean-field ensemble model function
h(\mathbf{x};\,\cdot):\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
is also convex in the space of probability measures
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
regardless of
\sigma(\cdot)
.

2.2.1
Stochastic Mirror Descent Updates

Mirror descent algorithms introduced by
Nemirovskii and Yudin 1983
, and their stochastic variants, are commonly used iterative methods that exploit a potential function to impose certain attributes in the resulting model. Assume
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\mathcal{Y}=\mathbb{R}
, and
\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Here, we consider the mirror descent updates initialized at
\bm{\Theta}^{0}\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
where, for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
, the updates are defined as,

\nabla\psi(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{k+1})=\nabla\psi(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{k})-\frac{\tau}{n}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}_{i}}\ell(y^{k+1},\,h_{n}(\mathbf{x}^{k+1};\,\Theta^{k})),

(8)

and
k
runs from
0
to
K-1
. Here
\psi(\cdot)
is a strongly convex and differentiable potential function,
\tau
a fixed scaling of the step-size, and
\bm{\Theta}^{k}
the parameters after the
k^{\text{th}}
update. Strong convexity of
\psi(\cdot)
ensures the invertibility of its gradient which makes the updates well-defined (see
Bubeck 2015
).

After evaluating the gradient of the loss function (w.r.t.
\bm{\theta}_{i}
) and using the identity (
6
), we can rewrite the SMD updates for
i=1,2,\ldots,n
and
k=0,\dots,K-1
as

\begin{cases}\hat{\rho}^{k}_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\bm{\theta}_{i}^{k}}\penalty\ ,\\
\nabla\psi(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{k+1})=\nabla\psi(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{k})+\frac{\tau}{n}\mathbf{F}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{k},\,\hat{\rho}^{k},\,\mathbf{z}^{k+1})\penalty\ ,\end{cases}

(9)

where
{\hat{\rho}}^{k}
denotes the empirical distribution of the parameters after the
k^{\text{th}}
update. The function
\mathbf{F}
is the gradient of the loss w.r.t.
\bm{\theta}
, which is defined as,

\mathbf{F}(\bm{\theta},{\rho},\mathbf{z}):=-\partial_{2}\ell\big(y;\langle\rho,\penalty\ \sigma(\mathbf{x},\cdot)\rangle\big)\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\sigma(\mathbf{x},\bm{\theta}),

(10)

where
\partial_{2}
indicates the derivative w.r.t. the second argument. It is worth noting that the potential function,
\psi(\cdot)
, is often chosen to enforce some structure on the resulting parameter. Setting
\psi(\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}
gives the SGD iterates.

Our goal is to compute the (converging) distribution of the parameters in the asymptotic regime where
n,K
go to infinity at a fixed ratio.
This distribution plays a pivotal role in understanding the generalization behavior of the resulting model. In particular, the expected risk can be computed as,

R(\rho):=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim\mu(d\mathbf{z})}\big[\ell\big(y;\langle\rho,\penalty\ \sigma(\mathbf{x},\cdot)\rangle\big)\big]\penalty\ ,

(11)

where
\rho:=\rho(d\bm{\theta})
denotes the (empirical) distribution.

3
Main Result

In this section, we present the main result of the paper, i.e., the characterization of the converging distribution of the SMD iterates. The resulting distribution is given as the solution to a continuous-time nonlinear PDE. We consider the converging distribution in the asymptotic regime where
K,n\rightarrow\infty
at a fixed ratio,
\delta:=\frac{K}{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{++}
.

In Section
3.1
, we explain how to map SMD updates to a continuous-time process on probability distributions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Consequently, a PDE will be derived in Section
3.2
whose evolution is captured by the Hessian of the potential function, and the function
\mathbf{F}
defined in (
10
). Finally, Section
3.3
incorporates the main result of the paper which indicates that the distribution of the mirror descent updates weakly converges to the solution of the PDE.

We require the following set of assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

(i)

\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is bounded and
\sigma(\mathbf{x},\,\cdot)\in\mathcal{C}_{b}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

(ii)

The loss function
\ell:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is convex and
\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
w.r.t. its second argument.

(iii)

The mirror function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
\lambda-
strongly convex and
\psi\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

(iv)

The initial parameters
\bm{\Theta}^{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\theta}_{1}^{0},\penalty\ \ldots,\penalty\ \bm{\theta}_{n}^{o}\end{pmatrix}
are sampled i.i.d. from a distribution
\rho^{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

(v)

The data distribution has finite
4^{\text{th}}
-order moments, i.e.,
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{4}(\mathbb{R}^{p+1})

Assumptions
(i)
and
(v)
are required for well-definiteness of expectations and together with
(iv)
, they will guarantee our convergence result. Convexity of loss function
\ell
ensures convexity of the expected risk of the mean-field ensemble which model (
11
). Assumptions
(ii)
and
(iv)
guarantee uniqueness of the resulting PDE. Finally, assumption
(iii)
is needed for non-singularity of the Hessian of the mirror function.

3.1
Continuous Time Viewpoint

Recall from the previous section

3.2
A Partial Differential Equation

Here we introduce a non-linear partial differential equation (PDE) whose solution will determine the converging distribution of SMD updates. Let
\mathbf{H}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{d}_{++}
be the Hessian operator of the mirror potential function
\psi(\cdot)
, i.e.,

\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta}):=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial{\bm{\theta}}^{2}}\psi(\bm{\theta})\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ \bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(13)

Note that
\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})\succeq\lambda\mathbf{I}_{d}
due to the strong convexity of the mirror potential. Consider the following PDE on probability measures with the initial condition
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\cdot\left(\rho_{t}\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})^{-1}\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},\rho_{t})\right),

(14)

where
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\cdot\mathbf{J}
denotes the divergence of the vector field
\mathbf{J}
2
2

2

For a vector field
\mathbf{F}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, defined as
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{bmatrix}F_{1}(\mathbf{x}),F_{2}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,F_{d}(\mathbf{x})\end{bmatrix}^{T}
, the divergence is defined as,

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\mathbf{F}=\frac{\partial F_{1}}{\partial{x_{1}}}+\frac{\partial F_{2}}{\partial{x_{2}}}+\ldots+\frac{\partial F_{d}}{\partial{x_{d}}}.

, and
\mathbf{v}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined as,

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},\rho)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x},y)\sim\mu(d\mathbf{z})}\left[\mathbf{F}(\bm{\theta},{\rho},\mathbf{x},y)\right],

(15)

\displaystyle\penalty\displaystyle=\int\mathbf{F}(\bm{\theta},{\rho},\mathbf{x},y)\mu(d\mathbf{z})\penalty\ ,

where
\mathbf{F}
is the negative gradient of the loss function defined in (
10
). An alternative but equivalent definition for the velocity field
\mathbf{v}
is given as

\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},\rho)=-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\frac{\delta R}{\delta\rho}(\bm{\theta},\rho),

(16)

where
\frac{\delta R}{\delta\rho}
denotes the functional (Fréchet) derivative of the expected risk functional

We note that the PDE (
14

\rho_{t}
is a solution of (

) if it solves the following equation for any test function

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\,t}\langle\rho_{t},f\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\rho_{t},\nabla f(\cdot)^{T}\mathbf{H}(\cdot)^{-1}\mathbf{v}(\cdot,\rho_{t})\rangle,

(17)

\displaystyle=\int\nabla f(\bm{\theta})^{T}\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})^{-1}\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},\rho_{t})\rho_{t}(d\bm{\theta}).

The PDE (
14

\mathbf{H}^{-1}(\bm{\theta})\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},\rho)
. The distortion of the drift term is due to inverse Hessian of the mirror potential while the impact of the data distribution is captured by

14

Remark 1
.

The PDE in (
14
) is presented in a general form. For specific choices of the loss function, the drift term
\mathbf{v}
can be simplified. There are variety of approaches to solve this nonlinear PDE; a common one is fixed-point iteration starting from a (reasonable) guess for the solution in the path space and making iterative updates according to (
14
). Discussion of methods for solving PDEs is beyond the scope of this paper, and we refer the reader to

3.3
Converging distribution

Our main result is Theorem
1
, which states that the time evolution of the empirical distribution of the parameters converges to the solution of the PDE (
14
) (initialized with
{\rho}^{0}
). Equivalently, the empirical distribution after
k=r\cdot n
updates can be approximated the solution of the PDE at time
t=r\cdot\tau

Theorem 1

(Converging Distribution of SMD)
.

Let
\{(\mathbf{x}^{k},y^{k})\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
denote a dataset where
(\mathbf{x}^{k},y^{k})\sim\mu(d\mathbf{z})
. Consider the stochastic mirror descent updates in (
9
) and assume that Assumption
1
holds. As
n,K\rightarrow\infty
at a fixed ratio
\delta=\frac{K}{n}
, the continuous-time process
\{\bar{\rho}_{n}(t):t\in[0,T]\}
defined in (
12
) converges weakly to
\{\rho_{t}^{\star}:t\in[0,T]\}
in the Skorokhod path space
\mathcal{D}([0,T];\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S}))
where
\rho_{t}^{\star}
is the solution of the following PDE for
t\in[0,T]
,

\partial_{t}{\rho_{t}^{\star}}=-\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\cdot\left({\rho_{t}^{\star}}\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})^{-1}\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},{\rho_{t}^{\star}})\right),

with
\rho_{0}^{\star}={\hat{\rho}}^{0}
. In other words, the empirical distribution of parameters resulting from SMD iterates converges to the solution of the partial differential equation (
14
).

Remark 2
.

As a side technical note, the convergence in Theorem
1

, denote by
\bar{\rho}_{n}[f]:=\{\langle\bar{\rho}_{n}(t),f\rangle\,:\,t\in[0,T]\}

\rho^{\star}[f]:=\{\langle{\rho_{t}^{\star}},f\rangle\,:\,t\in[0,T]\}
the processes generated by projecting function

\mathcal{D}([0,T];\mathcal{\mathbb{R}})
. Theorem
1
states that the (Skorokhod) distance between these two càdlàg processes converges to zero in probability as

4
Riemannian Formulation

In this section, we give an informal interpretation of equation (
14
) in a geometric sense. The main idea is to reformulate PDE (
14
) on a Riemannian manifold on which the metric tensor is defined by
\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})
.

Theorem 2
.

Assume the entries of
[\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})]_{ij}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be smooth functions of
\bm{\theta}
, and equip the parameter space
\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a Riemannian metric
g_{ij}(\bm{\theta}):=[\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})]_{ij}
. Assume, in addition, that the initial distribution admits a density with respect to volume measure on
(\mathcal{S},g)
as
\rho_{0}(\bm{\theta})
=
p(\bm{\theta},0)dV_{g}(\bm{\theta})
. Then, PDE (
14
) takes the following form on the Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{S},g)

\frac{\partial p(\bm{\theta},t)}{\partial t}=\operatorname{div}_{g}\left(p(\bm{\theta},t)\operatorname{grad}_{g}\frac{\delta R}{\delta\rho}(\bm{\theta},p_{t})\right).

(22)

where
\operatorname{div}_{g}
and
\operatorname{grad}_{g}
denote the divergence and gradient operators on
(\mathcal{S},g)
, respectively.

The insight behind this theorem is to define a new geometry on
\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
from the Hessian
\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})
of the potential. Defining Riemannian metrics from Hessians of smooth functions is a widely-used technique (
Shima and Yagi 1997
).

For a comparison, we rewrite the PDE (
21
) by inserting the definition of
\mathbf{v}
as

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\cdot\left(\rho_{t}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\frac{\delta R}{\delta\rho}(\bm{\theta},\rho_{t})\right).

(23)

When contrasted with the above equation derived for Euclidean mirror potential, the PDE (
22
) has the same form where divergence and gradient operators on Euclidean space are replaced by their Riemannian counterparts.

In lieu of this observation, we give an interpretation of (
22
) as a geometric flow describing the time evolution of parameter distribution on the Riemannian manifold defined by the Hessian of the potential. In this new geometry, steepest descent directions no longer follow straight lines, but rather follow geodesics
5
5

5

Geodesics are the shortest distance paths connecting points on a Riemannian manifold.
imposed by the mirror potential.

Proof.

We start by defining the gradient operator on
(\mathcal{S},g)
. For a smooth function
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathcal{S})
, its gradient at a point
\bm{\theta}\in\mathcal{S}
with respect to metric
g
is defined as

\operatorname{grad}_{g}f:=\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})^{-1}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{\theta})

(24)

We thus obtain the following identity for
\mathbf{H}^{-1}\mathbf{v}

\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})^{-1}\mathbf{v}(\bm{\theta},\rho)=-\operatorname{grad}_{g}\frac{\delta R}{\delta\rho}(\bm{\theta},\rho).

(25)

Inserting this back to equation (
14
) gives

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\cdot\left(\rho_{t}\operatorname{grad}_{g}\frac{\delta R}{\delta\rho}(\bm{\theta},\rho_{t})\right).

(26)

For a smooth vector field
\mathbf{J}(\bm{\theta})
, the Riemannian divergence operator on
(\mathcal{S},g)
is defined as

\operatorname{div}_{g}\mathbf{J}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\operatorname{det}{\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})}}}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}\cdot\left(\sqrt{\operatorname{det}{\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})}}\mathbf{J}(\bm{\theta})\right)

(27)

Note that
\sqrt{\operatorname{det}{\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})}}
also appears in the definition of the volume measure
dV_{g}(\bm{\theta})
on
(\mathcal{S},g)
as,

dV_{g}(\theta)=\sqrt{\operatorname{det}{\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})}}d\bm{\theta}.

(28)

In other words, infinitesimal volumes on
(\mathcal{S},g)

are distorted by a factor of
\sqrt{\operatorname{det}{\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})}}
.
Inserting the new divergence operator on
(\mathcal{S},g)
to (
26
), we rewrite it as

\partial_{t}\frac{\rho_{t}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{det}{\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})}}}=\operatorname{div}_{g}\left(\frac{\rho_{t}}{\sqrt{\operatorname{det}{\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})}}}\operatorname{grad}_{g}\frac{\delta R}{\delta\rho}(\bm{\theta},\rho_{t})\right).

(29)

Using the assumption on the initial distribution, the solution of at any time step can be expressed similarly

\displaystyle\rho_{t}(d\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle=p(\bm{\theta},t)dV_{g}(\bm{\theta}),

(30)

\displaystyle=p(\bm{\theta},t)\sqrt{\operatorname{det}{\mathbf{H}(\bm{\theta})}}d\bm{\theta}.

Then, the resulting (
22
) follows immediately.

Consider a binary classification problem with two-dimensional inputs and a data distribution given by

Outline of the Proof

Here we give a brief outline of the proof of Theorem
1
. The techniques we use are similar to those of
Wang et al. 2017
and
Sirignano and Spiliopoulos 2020
.

We start by introducing equivalent mirror domain counterparts of SMD iterates at (
8
) and proceed with analyzing the exchangeable Markov process in the mirror domain. For
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\bm{\omega}:=\nabla\psi(\bm{\theta})
denotes its mirror domain counterpart, and
\pi:=\rho\circ\nabla\psi^{-1}
denotes probability measure in the mirror domain

6

6

For a probability measure
\rho
on a set
\mathcal{S}
and a function
f:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}

\rho

(\rho\circ f^{-1})(B):=\rho(f^{-1}(B))

.
. We rewrite SMD iterations with this new set of notations as follows

\begin{cases}\hat{\pi}_{n}^{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{\bm{\omega}_{i}^{k}}\penalty\ ,\\
\bm{\omega}_{i}^{k+1}=\bm{\omega}_{i}^{k}+\frac{\tau}{n}\mathbf{G}(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{k},\hat{\pi}_{n}^{k},\mathbf{z}^{k+1})\penalty\ ,\end{cases}

(35)

where
\mathbf{G}(\bm{\omega},\pi,\mathbf{z}):=\mathbf{F}(\nabla\psi^{-1}(\bm{\omega}),\pi\circ\nabla\psi,\mathbf{z})
.

We continue by characterizing the asymptotic limit of mirror domain empirical distributions
\{\hat{\pi}_{n}^{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}
, and consequently convert the results to the original parameter domain by a change of variables. To characterize the evolution of the empirical measure of parameters, we analyze the change in integral of a test function. Let

\displaystyle\langle

\displaystyle\hat{\pi}_{n}^{k+1},f\rangle-\langle\hat{\pi}_{n}^{k},f\rangle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[f(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{k+1})-f(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{k})\right],

(36)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{\tau}{n}\nabla f(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{k})^{T}\mathbf{G}(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{k},\hat{\bm{\pi}}_{n}^{k},\mathbf{z}^{k+1})+r_{i}^{k}\right],

where the last equality is simply the first-order Taylor expansion and
r_{i}^{k}
is the remainder term which is given as,

r_{i}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{k+1}-\bm{\omega}_{i}^{k})^{T}\nabla^{2}f(\bar{\bm{\omega}}_{i}^{k})(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{k+1}-\bm{\omega}_{i}^{k})

\displaystyle d^{k}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\tau}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla f(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{k})^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mu(d\mathbf{z})}\left[\mathbf{G}(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{k},\hat{\pi}_{n}^{k},\mathbf{z})\right]

(38)

\displaystyle m^{k}

\displaystyle:=\frac{\tau}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla f(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{k})^{T}\mathbf{G}(\bm{\omega}_{i}^{k},\hat{\pi}_{n}^{k},\mathbf{z}^{k+1})-d^{k}

(39)

\displaystyle r^{k}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}^{k}

(40)

Rewriting (
36
) in terms of these variables gives,

\langle\hat{\pi}_{n}^{k+1},f\rangle-\langle\hat{\pi}_{n}^{k},f\rangle=d^{k}+m^{k}+r^{k}.

(41)

Notice that using the definition of the drift function (
15

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mu(d\mathbf{z})}\left[\mathbf{G}(\bm{\omega},\hat{\pi},\mathbf{z})\right]
can be written as,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim\mu(d\mathbf{z})}\left[\mathbf{G}(\bm{\omega},\hat{\pi},\mathbf{z})\right]=\mathbf{v}(\nabla\psi^{-1}(\bm{\omega}),\,\hat{\pi}\circ\nabla\psi).

(42)

We use the shorthand notation
\mathbf{u}(\bm{\omega},\,\pi):=\mathbf{v}(\nabla\psi^{-1}(\bm{\omega}),\,\pi\circ\nabla\psi)
to denote the mirror domain counterpart of
\mathbf{v}
. Consider a continuous-time process

similar to our earlier definition in (
12

41

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\pi}_{n}(t),f\rangle

\displaystyle-\langle\hat{\pi}^{0},f\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor{nt/\tau}\rfloor-1}\left(\langle\hat{\pi}_{n}^{k+1},f\rangle-\langle\hat{\pi}_{n}^{k},f\rangle\right)

(43)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor{nt/\tau}\rfloor-1}d^{k}+\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor{nt/\tau}\rfloor-1}m^{k}+\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor{nt/\tau}\rfloor-1}r^{k}.

The first sum on the RHS can be written as,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\lfloor{nt/\tau}\rfloor-1}d^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau}{n}\sum_{l=0}^{\lfloor{nt/\tau}\rfloor-1}\langle\hat{\pi}_{n}^{k},\nabla f(\cdot)^{T}\mathbf{u}(\cdot,\,\hat{\pi}_{n}^{k})\rangle,

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{[\,t\,]_{n}}\langle\bar{\pi}_{n}(s),\,\nabla f(\cdot)^{T}\mathbf{u}(\cdot,\bar{\pi}_{n}(s))\rangle ds.

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\pi}_{n}(t),\,f\rangle=\langle\hat{\pi}^{0},\,f\rangle

7
Conclusion

In this paper we explored the performance of mirror descent iterates through a mean-field lens for ensemble average models. We characterized the time evolution of the distribution of the parameters through a nonlinear PDE and gave a Riemannian interpretation. It would be interesting to further study the effect of the mirror potential on the performance of SMD-trained models for different learning problems via analytic and numerical study of this PDE. It would also be interesting to generalize these results to other network models and learning algorithms.
