A kinetic chemotaxis model and its diffusion limit in slab geometry
H. Egger a,b , K. Hellmuth c , N. Philippi b , and M. Schlottbom d

Abstract
. Chemotaxis describes the intricate interplay of cellular motion in response to a chemical signal. We here consider the case of slab geometry which models chemotactic motion between two infinite membranes. Like previous works, we are particularly interested in the asymptotic regime of high tumbling rates. We establish local existence and uniqueness of solutions to the kinetic equation and show their convergence towards solutions of a parabolic Keller-Segel model in the asymptotic limit. In addition, we prove convergence rates with respect to the asymptotic parameter under additional regularity assumptions on the problem data. Particular difficulties in our analysis are caused by vanishing velocities in the kinetic model as well as the occurrence of boundary terms.



Keywords:

chemotaxis, Keller-Segel system, slab geometry, local existence, diffusion limit, asymptotic analysis, convergence rate

1.
Introduction

The term
chemotaxis
is generally used to describe the motion of cells or bacteria in response to the gradient of a chemical stimulus
[
34
,
40
]
. It has been recognized as a fundamental mechanism in applications like immune response
[
46
]
, embryological development
[
21
]
, or bacterial population dynamics
[
35
]
.
Starting from stochastic descriptions, the first macroscopic models for chemotaxis describing the evolution of the particle densities were derived by Patlak
[
39
]
, Keller and Segel
[
33
]
. We refer to
[
30
,
32
]
and
[
40
]
for a survey of results and additional references.
Alt and co-workes
[
2
,
37
]
formulated multi-dimensional kinetic models, describing the particle densities, and investigated their diffusion limits by an asymptotic analysis.
The rigorous justification of the diffusion limit was put forward in
[
31
]
for the one-dimensional case, and in
[
38
]
and
[
16
]
for multiple space dimensions.

Scope.

In this paper, we study a kinetic model for chemotaxis and its diffusion limit in
slab geometry
, which is of relevance for the propagation of bacteria when passing membranes or small slabs of porous media, soil or biological gels
[
8
,
28
,
45
]
. Numerical studies for chemotaxis in slab geometry can be found in
[
14
,
29
]
.
Corresponding diffusion limits have been considered in

[
5
]
in the context of radiative transfer.

Let us note that the particle density in slab geometry is a function of one space and one velocity variable which, in contrast to
[
31
,
33
,
39
]
, varies continuously between
\pm 1
. The kinetic equation hence does not degenerate into a system of two coupled hyperbolic equations.

Main results.

The focus of this paper lies on a rigorous asymptotic analysis for chemotaxis in slab geometry. In particular, we establish

•

local well-posedness of a kinetic model for chemotaxis in slab geometry and a-priori estimates for solutions which are explicit in the asymptotic parameter;

•

convergence to solutions of a one-dimensional Keller-Segel system in the diffusion limit of vanishing asymptotic parameter and quantitative convergence rates.

Similar to previous work, we use energy-estimates and fixed-point arguments to establish existence and uniqueness of solutions to the kinetic model and to prove convergence to solutions of the limit system.
To the best of our knowledge, our results can, however, not be deduced from previous work: The assumptions in
[
16
]
do not allow to treat scattering operators of the form considered here and the work
[
5
]
does not include the nonlinear coupling to the equation for the chemoattractant. The results of
[
31
]
are valid for a discrete velocity model, but do not generalize to the case of slab geometry with continuous velocities.
As mentioned in
[
40
]
, the asymptotic analysis for the kinetic chemotaxis model is in line with that for the radiative transfer equation. We thus follow a similar approach as outlined in
[
6
,
18
,
24
]

and derive suitable extensions to chemotaxis in slab geometry.

2.2.
Model problem and main assumptions

The differential equations governing the kinetic chemotaxis model to be considered in the rest of the manuscript are given by

(2)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon v\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}+\sigma(u^{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\alpha v\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}\bar{u}^{\varepsilon}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathcal{Q}\times(0,T),

(3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bar{c}^{\varepsilon}-D\partial_{xx}\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}+\beta\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\gamma\bar{u}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathcal{X}\times(0,T).

Recall that
\bar{u}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{V}}u^{\varepsilon}(x,v)\,dv
denotes the velocity average of the population density
u^{\varepsilon}
, whereas the concentration
\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}
does not depend on
v
naturally.
The two equations are complemented by the boundary and initial conditions

(4)

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\bar{g}_{u}\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma^{in}\times(0,T),\qquad\qquad

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}(0)

\displaystyle=\bar{u}_{0}\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }\mathcal{Q},

(5)

\displaystyle\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\bar{g}_{c}\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\mathcal{X}\times(0,T),\qquad

\displaystyle\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}(0)

\displaystyle=\bar{c}_{0}\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }\mathcal{X}.

The boundary and initial data are thus chosen independent of the velocity
v
. This facilitates the analysis in the asymptotic regime but could be relaxed to some extent.
For our arguments, we will consider different time horizons
0<T\leq T^{*}
and asymptotic parameters
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon^{*}
with
T^{*},\varepsilon^{*}>0
fixed, and we make use of the following assumptions which allow us to establish the existence of sufficiently regular solutions to the model (
2
)–(
5
).

Assumption 1
.

The parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\geq 0
and
D,\varepsilon^{*},T^{*}>0
are given constants.
Furthermore
\sigma\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X})
with
0<\sigma_{min}\leq\sigma(x)\leq\sigma_{max}
for a.a.
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
The initial data satisfy
\bar{u}_{0}\in H^{2}(\mathcal{X})
,
\bar{c}_{0}\in H^{4}(\mathcal{X})
,
and the two boundary data
\bar{g}_{u},\bar{g}_{c}\in W^{2,\infty}(0,T^{*};\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
We use the same notation
\bar{g}_{u},\bar{g}_{c}
for the linear extensions of the boundary data to function
\bar{g}_{u},\bar{g}_{c}\in W^{2,\infty}(0,T^{*};W^{2,\infty}(\mathcal{X}))
.
In addition, we require the compatibility conditions
\bar{g}_{u}(0)=\bar{u}_{0}
,
\bar{g}_{c}(0)=\bar{c}_{0}
, and
\partial_{t}\bar{g}_{c}(0)=D\partial_{xx}\bar{c}_{0}-\beta\bar{c}_{0}+\gamma\bar{u}_{0}
hold for
x\in\partial\mathcal{X}
.

2.3.
Main results

The above conditions allow us to establish the existence of a unique regular solution of the problem and to derive corresponding a-priori bounds. Their explicit dependence on the parameter
\varepsilon
will be of importance for the asymptotic analysis later on.

Theorem 2
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Then, there exists a time horizon
T>0
such that for any
0<\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon^{*}
the system (
2
)–(
5
) has a unique solution

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}\in C^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))\qquad\text{with}\qquad v\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}\in C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(Q)),

\displaystyle\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}\in H^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))\cap H^{1}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathcal{X})).

Moreover, the following a-priori bounds hold with a uniform constant
C>0
:

\displaystyle(a)\quad

\displaystyle\|u^{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}\leq C;\qquad

\displaystyle(b)\quad

\displaystyle\|u^{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}\leq C\varepsilon;

\displaystyle(c)\quad

\displaystyle\|u^{\varepsilon}-\bar{g}_{u}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Gamma^{out};|v|d\Gamma))}\leq C\sqrt{\varepsilon};\qquad

\displaystyle(d)\quad

\displaystyle\|\varepsilon\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{0}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}\leq C;

\displaystyle(e)\quad

\displaystyle\|v\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}\leq C;\qquad

\displaystyle(f)\quad

\displaystyle\|\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}\|_{H^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}\leq C;

\displaystyle(g)\quad

\displaystyle\|\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}\leq C;\qquad

\displaystyle(h)\quad

\displaystyle\|\varepsilon\partial_{t}\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}\|_{L^{4}(0,T;W^{1,\infty}(\mathcal{X}))}\leq C.

The existence of a unique solution will be established via fixed-point arguments and results about the linearized evolution problems corresponding to the two differential equations. The bounds for the solution are derived by careful energy estimates and interpolation arguments. The detailed proof is presented in Section
3
.

The second main result of the paper is concerned with the behavior of solutions in the asymptotic limit
\varepsilon\to 0
. Our main findings can be summarized as follows.

Theorem 3
.

Let Assumption
1
hold and
(u^{\varepsilon},\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon})
be the solutions of (

)–(
5
) provided by Theorem
2

\varepsilon\to 0
.
Then

\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}\to\bar{c}^{\,0}
in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathcal{X}))
, where

is the unique weak solution of the Keller-Segel system

(6)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bar{u}^{0}-\partial_{x}(\bar{\mu}\partial_{x}\bar{u}^{0}-\bar{\chi}\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,0}\bar{u}^{0})

\displaystyle=0\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathcal{X}\times(0,T),

(7)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bar{c}^{\,0}-D\partial_{xx}\bar{c}^{\,0}+\beta\bar{c}^{\,0}

\displaystyle=\gamma\bar{u}^{0}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathcal{X}\times(0,T),

with
motility and chemotactic coefficient

(8)

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}(x)=\sigma(x)^{-1}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{V}}v^{2}\ dv\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bar{\chi}(x)=\alpha\bar{\mu}(x),

\displaystyle\bar{u}^{0}

\displaystyle=\bar{g}_{u}\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\mathcal{X}\times(0,T),\qquad

\displaystyle\bar{u}^{0}(0)

\displaystyle=\bar{u}_{0}\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }\mathcal{X},

(10)

\displaystyle\bar{c}^{\,0}

\displaystyle=\bar{g}_{c}\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial\mathcal{X}\times(0,T),\qquad

\displaystyle\bar{c}^{0}(0)

\displaystyle=\bar{c}_{0}\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }\mathcal{X}.

If, in addition,

3.2.
Linearized kinetic equation

As a next step, we establish existence of a unique solution to (
12

Proposition 5
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Then for any

as given by Proposition

Proof.

Similar to before, we define
w=u-\bar{g}_{u}
, which can be seen to satisfy

(16)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}w+\varepsilon v\partial_{x}w+\sigma(w-\bar{w})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon v\bar{f}_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}\bar{f}_{2}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathcal{Q}\times(0,T)

(17)

\displaystyle w(0)

\displaystyle=\bar{w}_{0}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on }\mathcal{Q},

with
\bar{f}_{1}=\alpha\partial_{x}\bar{c}\,\bar{z}-\partial_{x}\bar{g}_{u}
,
\bar{f}_{2}=-\partial_{t}\bar{g}_{u}
,
\bar{w}_{0}=\bar{u}_{0}-\bar{g}_{u}(0)
, and homogeneous inflow boundary conditions

on
\Gamma^{in}\times(0,T)
.
This can be written as an abstract Cauchy problem

(18)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}w+\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}w

\displaystyle=f,\qquad t>0,

(19)

\displaystyle w(0)

\displaystyle=\bar{w}_{0},

on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathcal{Q})
, with right hand side

, and densely defined linear operator

,
\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}w=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v\partial_{x}w+\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon^{2}}(w-\bar{w})

\displaystyle D(\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon})=\{w\in L^{2}(Q):v\partial_{x}w\in L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}),\ w|_{\Gamma^{in}}=0\}.

We note that

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A}^{\varepsilon}w,w\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\langle v\partial_{x}w,w\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\langle\sigma(w-\bar{w}),w\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}}\geq 0,

for all

is dissipative

From our assumptions, we further see that

Remark 6
.

Since

generates a semigroup of contractions, we also obtain the bounds

\displaystyle\|w\|_{C([0,T];L^{2}(Q))}\leq\|\bar{w}_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Q})}+\|f\|_{L^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}.

Inserting

\displaystyle\|w\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}^{2}

\displaystyle w^{\prime}(0)

By our assumptions, we have

Step 1

Step 2

Let

Then the two functions

Further note that the regularity of

The additional regularity of the solution is established by bootstrapping. For completeness, let us briefly sketch the main arguments:
Interpolation estimates

\displaystyle-\langle\varepsilon^{-1}\sigma(u^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle-\bar{u}^{\varepsilon}),\sigma^{-1}v\partial_{x}\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}_{T}}=-\langle\varepsilon^{-1}u^{\varepsilon},v\partial_{x}\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}_{T}}=\langle\varepsilon^{-1}v\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon},\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}_{T}}

\displaystyle=-\langle\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon},\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}_{T}}-\langle\varepsilon^{-2}\sigma(u^{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}^{\varepsilon}),\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}_{T}}+\langle\varepsilon^{-1}\alpha v\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}\bar{u}^{\varepsilon},\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}_{T}}

\displaystyle=\langle u^{\varepsilon},\partial_{t}\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}_{T}}+\langle u^{\varepsilon}(0),\tilde{\psi}(0)\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}}.

By combination of these results and those of Proposition
10
, we conclude that

\displaystyle\langle v\partial_{x}\bar{u}^{0},\sigma^{-1}v\partial_{x}\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}_{T}}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\langle v\partial_{x}u^{\varepsilon},\sigma^{-1}v\partial_{x}\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}_{T}}

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha v\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,0}\bar{u}^{0},\sigma^{-1}v\partial_{x}\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}_{T}}+\langle\bar{u}^{0},\partial_{t}\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}_{T}}+\langle\bar{u}_{0},\tilde{\psi}(0)\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}}.

Step 3.

By definition of
\bar{\chi}
and
\bar{\mu}
, the previous results,
further yield that

\displaystyle 2\langle\bar{\mu}\partial_{x}\bar{u}^{0},\partial_{x}\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}_{T}}

\displaystyle=\langle v\partial_{x}\bar{u}^{0},\sigma^{-1}v\partial_{x}\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}_{T}}

\displaystyle=2\langle\bar{\chi}\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,0}\bar{u}^{0},\partial_{x}\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}_{T}}+2\langle\bar{u}^{0},\partial_{t}\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}_{T}}+2\langle\bar{u}_{0},\tilde{\psi}(0)\rangle_{\mathcal{X}}.

This shows that the limit
(\bar{u}^{0},\bar{c}^{\,0})
provided by Proposition
10
satisfies (
26
).
In particular,

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}\bar{u}^{0},\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}_{T}}

\displaystyle=-\langle\bar{\mu}\partial_{x}\bar{u}^{0}-\bar{\chi}\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,0}\bar{u}^{0},\partial_{x}\tilde{\psi}\rangle_{\mathcal{X}_{T}}

for all
\tilde{\psi}\in C_{0}^{\infty}((0,T);H_{0}^{1}(\mathcal{X}))
. From the regularity of
\bar{u}^{0}
and
\bar{c}^{\,0}
, we infer that

\displaystyle\bar{\mu}\partial_{x}\bar{u}^{0}-\bar{\chi}\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,0}\bar{u}^{0}\in L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{X})).

This shows that
\bar{u}^{0}
has a weak time derivative
\partial_{t}\bar{u}^{0}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\mathcal{X}))
and that the variational identity (
25
) holds for a.a.
t\in(0,T)
.

Step 4.

By combination of (
25
) and (
26
), we can immediately see that
\bar{u}^{0}(0)=\bar{u}_{0}
.
Now let
{\mathrm{tr}}:u\mapsto u|_{\Gamma}
denote the trace operator for
u\in U=\{v\in L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}):v\partial_{x}u\in L^{2}(\mathcal{Q})\}
.
By use of the integation-by-parts formula (
1
), we obtain

(27)

\displaystyle\|{\mathrm{tr}}\,u\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma;|v|d\Gamma)}^{2}=\int_{\Gamma}|{\mathrm{tr}}\,u|^{2}|v|\,d\Gamma=2\int_{\mathcal{Q}}v\partial_{x}u\,u\,d(x,v)\leq 2\|v\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Q})}\|u\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Q})}.

This shows that the trace operator
{\mathrm{tr}}:L^{2}(0,T;U)\to L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Gamma;|v|d\Gamma))
is continuous as a mapping between these spaces.
By Proposition
10
, we know that
\bar{u}^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\bar{u}^{0}
weakly in
L^{2}(0,T;U)
, and hence
{\mathrm{tr}}\,u^{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup{\mathrm{tr}}\,\bar{u}^{0}
weakly in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Gamma;|v|d\Gamma))
.
Since
{\mathrm{tr}}\,u^{\varepsilon}=\bar{g}_{u}
on
\Gamma^{in}\times(0,T)
, we infer that
{\mathrm{tr}}\,\bar{u}^{0}=\bar{g}_{u}
on
\Gamma^{in}\times(0,T)
, and since
\bar{u}^{0}
is independent of
v
, we further conclude that
\bar{u}^{0}=\bar{g}^{u}
on
\partial\mathcal{X}\times(0,T)
in a trace sense.
In summary, we thus have shown that
\bar{u}^{0}
satisfies the initial and boundary conditions (
9
), and hence
(\bar{u}^{0},\bar{c}^{\,0})
is a weak solution of (
6
)–(
10
).

Step 5.

Since every sequence of solutions
(u^{\varepsilon},\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon})
to (
2
)–(
5
) for
\varepsilon\to 0
has a subsequence which converges weakly to a weak solution
(\bar{u}^{0},\bar{c}^{\,0})
of (
6
)–(
10
) and since the weak solution to this problem is unique, we obtain weak convergence with
\varepsilon\to 0
for any sequence
(u^{\varepsilon},\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon})
of solutions.
This concludes the first part of the proof of Theorem
3
.

5.
Proof of Theorem
3
, Part 2

In this section, we establish the quantitative convergence rates announced in Theorem
3
.
Following standard arguments
[
18
,
24
]
, we decompose

(28)

\displaystyle u^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\bar{u}^{0}+\varepsilon v\bar{u}^{1}+\phi^{\varepsilon}\quad\text{with}\quad\bar{u}^{1}=-\sigma^{-1}\partial_{x}\bar{u}^{0}+\alpha\sigma^{-1}\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,0}\bar{u}^{0},

(29)

\displaystyle\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\bar{c}^{\,0}+\bar{\eta}^{\varepsilon}.

To conclude the proof of the theorem, we need to estimate
\bar{u}^{1}
and the two remainder terms
\phi^{\varepsilon}
and
\bar{\eta}^{\varepsilon}
defined by the previous expressions. This will be done in the sequel.

Step 1.

From the regularity of the limit solution
(\bar{u}^{0},\bar{c}^{\,0})
provided by Proposition
8
, we deduce that
\|\bar{u}^{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X}))}
,
\|\partial_{x}\bar{u}^{1}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}
, and
\|\partial_{t}\bar{u}^{1}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}
are uniformly bounded.
In addition, we have

(30)

\overline{v\bar{u}^{1}}=0,\qquad\text{ and consequently}\qquad\bar{\phi}^{\varepsilon}=\bar{u}^{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}^{0}.

As a consequence, some of the terms in the following investigations will vanish.

Step 2.

Using the equations defining
\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}
and
\bar{c}^{\,0}
, we see that
\bar{\eta}^{\varepsilon}=\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}-\bar{c}^{\,0}
satisfies

(31)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bar{\eta}^{\varepsilon}-D\partial_{xx}\bar{\eta}^{\varepsilon}+\beta\bar{\eta}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\gamma\bar{\phi}^{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{X}\times(0,T),

together with homogeneous initial and boundary conditions.
From standard a-priori estimates for parabolic equations
[
27
]
, we thus obtain

\displaystyle\|\bar{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}\leq\|\bar{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\mathcal{X}))}+\|\bar{\eta}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\mathcal{X}))}\leq C\|\phi^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}.

It therefore remains to establish the appropriate bounds for the second remainder
\phi^{\varepsilon}
.

Step 3.

From the equations defining
u^{\varepsilon}
,
\bar{u}^{0}
, and
\bar{u}^{1}
, we can see that
\phi^{\varepsilon}
solves

(32)

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}\phi^{\varepsilon}+\varepsilon v\partial_{x}\phi^{\varepsilon}+\sigma(\phi^{\varepsilon}-\bar{\phi}^{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\varepsilon f^{\varepsilon}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathcal{Q}\times(0,T),

(33)

\displaystyle\phi^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=g^{\varepsilon}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on }\Gamma^{in}\times(0,T),

(34)

\displaystyle\phi^{\varepsilon}(0)

\displaystyle=\phi_{0}^{\varepsilon}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{on }\mathcal{Q}

with

f^{\varepsilon}=\alpha v\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}\bar{u}^{\varepsilon}-\varepsilon\partial_{t}\bar{u}^{0}-v\partial_{x}\bar{u}^{0}-\varepsilon^{2}v\partial_{t}\bar{u}^{1}-\varepsilon v^{2}\partial_{x}\bar{u}^{1}-\sigma v\bar{u}^{1}
,

g^{\varepsilon}=-\varepsilon v\bar{u}^{1}
, and initial value
\phi^{\varepsilon}_{0}=-\varepsilon v\bar{u}^{1}(0)
.
Following the arguments in
[
23
]
,
we split
\phi^{\varepsilon}=\phi^{\varepsilon}_{f}+\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}
with
\phi^{\varepsilon}_{f}
solving the same system, but with homogeneous boundary conditions, and
\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}
satisfying the equations with homogeneous right hand side and homogeneous initial conditions.

Step 3a.

As a preliminary result, we establish
L^{p}
bounds for
\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}
, elaborating explicitly the dependence on
\varepsilon
.
To do so, we multiply (
32
) with
f^{\varepsilon}=0
by
|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}|^{p-2}\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}
, which yields

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\tfrac{1}{p}\partial_{t}|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}|^{p}+\varepsilon v\tfrac{1}{p}\partial_{x}|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}|^{p}=\sigma(\bar{\phi}^{\varepsilon}_{g}-\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g})|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}|^{p-2}\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}.

Integration over
\mathcal{Q}\times(0,t)
, application of the integration-by-parts formula (
1
), and of the inhomogeneous boundary and homogeneous initial conditions for
\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}
yields

(35)

\displaystyle\tfrac{\varepsilon^{2}}{p}\|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}(t)\|^{p}_{L^{p}(\mathcal{Q})}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma^{out}}\tfrac{\varepsilon}{p}|v||\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}|^{p}\ d\Gamma\ ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma^{in}}\tfrac{\varepsilon}{p}|v||\varepsilon v\bar{u}^{1}|^{p}\ d\Gamma\ ds+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathcal{Q}}\sigma(\bar{\phi}^{\varepsilon}_{g}-\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g})|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}|^{p-2}\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}\ d(x,v)\ ds.

By Hölder’s inequality, we see that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{V}}\bar{u}|u|^{p-2}u\ dv

\displaystyle\leq\left(\int_{\mathcal{V}}|\bar{u}|^{p}\ dv\right)^{1/p}\left(\int_{\mathcal{V}}(|u|^{p-1})^{\tfrac{p}{p-1}}\ dv\right)^{(p-1)/p}

for any
u\in L^{p}(\mathcal{V})
, and Jensen’s inequality further yields
\left(\int_{\mathcal{V}}|\bar{u}|^{p}\ dv\right)^{1/p}\leq\left(\int_{\mathcal{V}}|u|^{p}\ dv\right)^{1/p}
for any
p\geq 2
.
By combination of these estimates, we conclude that the last term in the second line of (
35
) is non-positive.
By simplification of the inequality, we thus obtain

\displaystyle\|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}(t)\|_{L^{p}(\mathcal{Q})}\leq\varepsilon^{-1/p}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Gamma^{in}}|v|^{2}|\varepsilon\bar{u}^{1}|^{p}\ d\Gamma\ ds\right)^{1/p}\leq C\varepsilon^{1-1/p}.

For the last step, we used the uniform boundedness of
\bar{u}^{1}
.
By letting
p\to\infty
, we obtain

(36)

\displaystyle\|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Q}))}=\lim_{p\to\infty}\|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{p}(\mathcal{Q}))}\leq C^{\prime}\varepsilon.

Since
\mathcal{X}
is bounded, we further obtain
\|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}\leq C\|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\mathcal{Q}))}\leq C^{\prime\prime}\varepsilon
, which is the desired bound for the first component of
\phi^{\varepsilon}
.

Step 3b.

We start with having a closer look at the right hand side of (
32
).
From the definition of
\bar{u}^{1}
, we see that
\sigma\bar{u}^{1}=\alpha\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,0}\bar{u}^{0}-\partial_{x}\bar{u}^{0}
.
Further using (
7
), we can rewrite

(37)

\displaystyle f^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\alpha v(\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}\bar{u}^{\varepsilon}-\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,0}\bar{u}^{0})-\varepsilon^{2}v\partial_{t}\bar{u}^{1}-\varepsilon[v^{2}\partial_{x}\bar{u}^{1}+\partial_{x}(\bar{\mu}\partial_{x}\bar{u}^{0}-\bar{\chi}\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,0}\bar{u}^{0})].

Using the formulas defining
\bar{\mu}
,
\bar{\chi}
, and
\bar{u}^{1}
, we see that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{V}}v^{2}\partial_{x}\bar{u}^{1}+\partial_{x}(\bar{\mu}\partial_{x}\bar{u}^{0}-\bar{\chi}\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,0}\bar{u}^{0})\,dv=0,

and hence
\bar{f}^{\varepsilon}=0
.
Let us note that the function
f^{\varepsilon}
can be treated like the term
v\bar{f}_{1}
in the system (
16
)–(
17
). From the energy estimate (
21
), we may thus conclude that

\displaystyle\|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{f}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(0,t;L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}\leq e^{t}(\|\phi_{f}^{\varepsilon}(0)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Q})}+\|f^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,t;L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}^{2})

for any
0\leq t\leq T
.
From the bounds for
\bar{u}_{1}
, we obtain
\|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{f}(0)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathcal{Q})}\leq C_{0}\varepsilon^{2}
.
The right hand side (
37
) may be split into
f^{\varepsilon}=f^{\varepsilon}_{0}+\varepsilon f^{\varepsilon}_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}f^{\varepsilon}_{2}
.
From the bounds for
\bar{u}^{0}
and
\bar{u}^{1}
, we immediately obtain that
\|\varepsilon f^{\varepsilon}_{1}+\varepsilon^{2}f^{\varepsilon}_{2}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}\leq C\varepsilon
.
Using Hölder and triangle inequalities, the remaining term can be estimated as follows

\displaystyle\|f_{0}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\|\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}\bar{u}^{\varepsilon}-\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,0}\bar{u}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha(\|\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}-\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,0}\|_{L^{2}(L^{\infty})}\|\bar{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}+\|\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,0}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{\infty})}\|\bar{u}^{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}).

By previous a-priori estimates, we can bound
\|\bar{u}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}\leq C
and
\|\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,0}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{\infty})}\leq C
.
Using the equation defining
\bar{\eta}^{\varepsilon}=\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}-\bar{c}^{\,0}
, the definition of
\phi^{\varepsilon}
, and the left identity of (
30
), we see that the two terms involving differences can be further bounded by

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}-\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,0}\|_{L^{2}(L^{\infty})}^{2}+\|\bar{u}^{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}^{0}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}\leq C_{2}\|\phi^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(L^{2})}^{2}\leq C_{3}(\|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{f}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2})}+\varepsilon^{2}).

In the last step, we used the bound for
\|\phi_{g}^{\varepsilon}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(L^{2})}
from before.
In summary, we thus get

\displaystyle\|f^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(0,t;L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}^{2}\leq C_{1}\|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{f}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,t;L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}+C_{2}\varepsilon^{2}.

Inserting this into the energy estimate for
\phi^{\varepsilon}_{f}
and applying a Grönwall inequality then leads to the uniform bound
\|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{f}\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}\leq C_{2}^{\prime}\varepsilon^{2}
.

Step 3c.

By combination of the previous estimates, we see that

\|\phi^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}\leq\|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{f}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}+\|\phi^{\varepsilon}_{g}\|_{L^{\infty}(L^{2})}\leq C\varepsilon.

From the definition of
\phi^{\varepsilon}
and the uniform bounds for
\bar{u}^{1}
, we then get

\|u^{\varepsilon}-\bar{u}^{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}\leq\varepsilon\|v\bar{u}^{1}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}+\|\phi^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}\leq C\varepsilon.

From the arguments of Step 2, we conclude that
\|\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon}-\bar{c}^{\,0}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathcal{Q}))}\leq C\varepsilon
as well, which proves the convergence rates stated in Theorem
3
. ∎

6.
Discussion

We established well-posedness of a kinetic chemotaxis model in slab geometry and convergence to a drift-diffusion system of Keller-Segel type in the asymptotic limit. The results are valid on a finite time interval
[0,T]
with
T>0
depending on the problem data.

From the global well-posedness of the limit problem in one space dimension, one would expect that the results should be valid for
T=\infty
. A different analysis would, however, be required, probably involving the positivity of solutions and uniform bounds in
L^{1}
; compare with the proofs in
[
25
]
for the corresponding limit problem.
Another way to obtain global-in-time results is to replace the nonlinear coupling term in (
2
) by
\varepsilon\alpha v\Psi(\partial_{x}\bar{c}^{\,\varepsilon})\bar{u}^{\varepsilon}
for a suitable bounded function
\Psi
, which acts as
flux limiting
. Our results can be generalized to this case.
Such models were recently introduced to avoid nonphysical blow-up behavior and were shown to attain global solutions
[
41
]
; also see
[
13
,
7
]
for the related analysis of the flux-limited Keller-Segel model in multiple space dimensions.

Let us note that our results can be extended quite naturally to related chemotaxis models on networks which were introduced as mathematical models for dermal wound healing
[
12
]
or the growth of slime molds
[
10
]
.
The fact that the velocity space
\mathcal{V}=(-1,1)
involves a whole interval here means that agents can actually move at different speeds. Such systems have been studied numerically in
[
11
]
.
The asymptotic analysis for somewhat simplified coupling conditions has been derived
[
42
]
, and corresponding results for the limiting Keller-Segel model on networks were obtained in
[
10
,
25
]
.

Possible topics for future research include the extension of our results to more complex interaction mechanisms, involving multiple species, different chemo-attractants, or more complex coupling mechanisms; see
[
9
,
26
,
43
]
for some recent work in this direction.
The design of asymptotic preserving numerical schemes for chemotaxis in slab geometry and related models on networks, in a similar spirit to
[
29
,
36
,
14
]
, would be a topic for future research.
