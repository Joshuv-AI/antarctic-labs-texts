Estimating entropy production in a stochastic system with odd-parity variables
Dong-Kyum Kim, Sangyun Lee, Hawoong Jeong

Abstract
Entropy production (EP) is a central measure in nonequilibrium thermodynamics, as it can quantify the irreversibility of a process as well as its energy dissipation in special cases. Using the time-reversal asymmetry in a system’s path probability distribution, many methods have been developed to estimate EP from only trajectory data. However, estimating the EP of a system with odd-parity variables, which prevails in nonequilibrium systems, has not been covered. In this study, we develop a machine learning method for estimating the EP in a stochastic system with odd-parity variables through multiple neural networks. We demonstrate our method with two systems, an underdamped bead-spring model and a one-particle odd-parity Markov jump process.



I
Introduction

Entropy production (EP) plays an important role in the field of stochastic thermodynamics because of its diverse relations to the dissipation, free energy, and fundamental bounds on the fluctuation of an observable. Through the fluctuation relation
Seifert 2005
, it has been found that EP has a symmetry that enables us to measure the free energy difference from nonequilibrium experimental data
Collin et al. 2005
. Also, trade-off relations between EP and the relative error of physical quantities have been discovered recently
Barato and Seifert 2015
;
Proesmans and den
Broeck 2017
;
Hasegawa and Van Vu 2019
;
Koyuk and Seifert 2020
. Such relations can provide a tighter version of the thermodynamic second law and a universal bound for fluctuations of diverse currents.

Following from the importance, various estimation methods for EP have been developed, such as the plug-in method
Roldán and Parrondo 2010
and compression algorithm
Avinery et al. 2019
. Also, the development of thermodynamic uncertainty relations (TURs) open the possibility of measuring the EP in overdamped Langevin processes
Gingrich et al. 2016
. To estimate the exact EP with TURs, we have to measure the relative error of EP, which presents us with circular reasoning. To resolve this point, a few optimization strategies have been established
Li et al. 2019
;
Van Vu et al. 2020
;
Otsubo et al. 2020
;
Dechant and Sasa
. However, measuring EP remains challenging because of the curse of dimensionality.

Recently, machine learning based algorithms for EP estimation have been developed to efficiently mitigate such issues by using a neural network
Kim et al. 2020
;
Otsubo et al.
;
Bae et al.
. These methods employ neural networks as an EP estimator
Kim et al. 2020
, where the estimator is trained for optimizing the variational representation of the EP
Donsker and Varadhan 2020
. Owing to the great power of neural networks, these approaches work well even in high-dimensional systems. Moreover, unlike the TURs methods, the neural estimator can provide stochastic EP directly from trajectory data without any correction
Otsubo et al.
.

Despite such progress, the developed methods have only considered systems with absent odd-parity variables, of which the sign changes under time-reversal operation
Spinney and Ford 2012
;
Lee et al. 2013
;
Lee et al. 2021
. For instance, underdamped Langevin dynamics includes an odd-parity variable called momentum
Risken 1991
; in many cases, active matter or other biological systems are often modeled with underdamped dynamics
Shankar and Marchetti 2018
;
Dabelow et al. 2019
. To estimate the EP of such systems, existing methodologies require additional information because the definition of EP in these systems differs from that in systems without odd-parity variables. Indeed, TURs for underdamped Langevin systems require detailed information about the systems
Van Vu and Hasegawa 2019
;
Lee et al. 2019
;
Lee et al. 2021
, and therefore we cannot exactly measure EP from only trajectory data using TURs. Due to these difficulties, to our knowledge, methods for estimating EP in a stochastic system with odd-parity state variables have yet to be developed.

In this article, we propose a machine learning based method for estimating EP by using trajectory data obtained from a stochastic system with odd-parity variables. The proposed method can be applied to Langevin dynamics and Markov jump processes. For a system with continuous state variables, we demonstrate our estimation method with an underdamped bead-spring model in Sec.
II
, and for the Markov jump case, we apply our approach in Sec.
III
to a one-particle odd-parity Markov jump process in which the state variables are discrete and the time interval between jumps is continuous.

II
Underdamped bead-spring model

In the underdamped bead spring model,
N
beads are coupled to the next nearest beads. Let
\bm{q}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N},v_{1},\dots,v_{N})
be a state vector of this model, and its time reversal counterpart is denoted as
\widetilde{\bm{q}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{N},-v_{1},\dots,-v_{N})
where
x_{i}
and
v_{i}
are the position and velocity of the
i
-th bead, respectively. Then, the dynamics of the state
\bm{q}
with time
\tau
is governed by the following underdamped Langevin equation:

\displaystyle\dot{\bm{q}}(\tau)=-\mathsf{M}\cdot\bm{q}(\tau)+\sqrt{2\mathsf{D}}\cdot\bm{\xi}(\tau),

(1)

with

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{0}&-\mathsf{I}\\
\mathsf{A}&\mathsf{\Gamma}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathsf{D}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{0}\\
\mathsf{0}&\mathsf{T}\end{pmatrix},

(2)

where
[\mathsf{A}]_{ij}=(2\delta_{i,j}-\delta_{i,j+1}-\delta_{i+1,j})k/m
,
[\mathsf{\Gamma}]_{ij}=\gamma\delta_{i,j}
, and
[\mathsf{T}]_{ij}=\delta_{i,j}\gamma k_{\rm B}T_{i}/m
. Otherwise,
\bm{\xi}
, is a Gaussian noise vector with zero mean and variance given by
\langle\xi_{i}(\tau)\xi_{j}(\tau^{\prime})\rangle=\delta_{ij}\delta(\tau-\tau^{\prime})
, and
\langle\dots\rangle
denotes the ensemble average.
\mathsf{0}
and
\mathsf{I}
are the
N\times N
null and identity matrices,
\gamma
is the friction coefficient, and
k
is a spring constant. We set
k=k_{\rm B}=\gamma=1

1
1

1

Here, we note that
[\mathsf{\Gamma}]_{ij}
is not
\delta_{i,j}\gamma/m
but
\delta_{i,j}\gamma
. This means that the governing equation is not reduced to an overdamped Langevin equation when mass
m
approaches zero because the velocity autocorrelation is proportional to
e^{-\tau\gamma}
rather than
e^{-\tau\gamma/m}
.
.

For data preparation, we sample an ensemble of trajectories from Eq. (
1
).
\bm{q}_{1}
is sampled from steady-state distribution (SSD)
p(\bm{q})
, and we use the second-order integrator to sample a trajectory
(\bm{q}_{1},\bm{q}_{2},\dots,\bm{q}_{L})
, where
\bm{q}_{t}\equiv\bm{q}(t\Delta\tau)
with fixed sampling interval
\Delta\tau

Vanden-Eijnden and Ciccotti 2006
. In this case, the stochastic EP per step is defined as

\displaystyle\Delta S_{\rm tot}(\bm{q}_{t},\bm{q}_{t+1})=\ln{\frac{p(\bm{q}_{t+1}|\bm{q}_{t})}{p(\widetilde{\bm{q}}_{t}|\widetilde{\bm{q}}_{t+1})}}+\ln{\frac{p(\bm{q}_{t})}{p(\bm{q}_{t+1})}},

(3)

where the first (second) term on the right-hand side is the environment EP (system entropy change)
Seifert 2012
.

In order to estimate Eq. (
3
), we employ the objective function proposed in Ref.
Kim et al. 2020
:

\displaystyle J_{\theta_{1}}=\langle\Delta S_{\theta_{1}}(\bm{q}_{t},\bm{q}_{t+1})-e^{-\Delta S_{\theta_{1}}(\bm{q}_{t},\bm{q}_{t+1})}+1\rangle_{t},

(4)

where

\displaystyle\Delta S_{\theta_{1}}(\bm{q}_{t},\bm{q}_{t+1})=h_{\theta_{1}}(\bm{q}_{t},\bm{q}_{t+1})-h_{\theta_{1}}(\widetilde{\bm{q}}_{t+1},\widetilde{\bm{q}}_{t}),

and
\langle\dots\rangle_{t}
denotes the expectation over
t
and the ensemble. Here, we use a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) for
h_{\theta_{1}}
, where
\theta_{1}
represents the trainable neural network parameters. Using the stochastic gradient ascent method, we train the neural network
h_{\theta_{1}}
to find the optimal parameter
\theta_{1}^{\star}
which gives the maximum value of Eq. (
4
). The optimal parameter
\theta_{1}^{\star}
gives

\displaystyle\Delta S_{\theta_{1}^{\star}}(\bm{q}_{t},\bm{q}_{t+1})=\ln{\frac{p(\bm{q}_{t},\bm{q}_{t+1})}{p(\widetilde{\bm{q}}_{t+1},\widetilde{\bm{q}}_{t})}},

(5)

and this can be split into

\displaystyle\Delta S_{\theta_{1}^{\star}}(\bm{q}_{t},\bm{q}_{t+1})=\ln{\frac{p(\bm{q}_{t+1}|\bm{q}_{t})}{p(\widetilde{\bm{q}}_{t}|\widetilde{\bm{q}}_{t+1})}}+\ln{\frac{p(\bm{q}_{t})}{p(\bm{q}_{t+1})}}+\ln{\frac{p(\bm{q}_{t+1})}{p(\widetilde{\bm{q}}_{t+1})}}.

Therefore, the maximum value of
J_{\theta_{1}}
is

\displaystyle J_{\theta_{1}^{\star}}

\displaystyle=\left<\ln{\frac{p(\bm{q}_{t+1}|\bm{q}_{t})}{p(\widetilde{\bm{q}}_{t}|\widetilde{\bm{q}}_{t+1})}}+\ln{\frac{p(\bm{q}_{t})}{p(\bm{q}_{t+1})}}+\ln{\frac{p(\bm{q}_{t+1})}{p(\widetilde{\bm{q}}_{t+1})}}\right>_{t}

(6)

\displaystyle=\langle\Delta S_{\rm tot}(\bm{q}_{t},\bm{q}_{t+1})\rangle_{t}+\left<\ln{\frac{p(\bm{q})}{p(\widetilde{\bm{q}})}}\right>.

(7)

The last term of Eq. (
7
) is the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the SSD
p(\bm{q})
and its mirror state distribution
p(\widetilde{\bm{q}})
. Equation (
7
) can be written as

\displaystyle J_{\theta_{1}^{\star}}=\dot{\sigma}\Delta\tau+\left<\Delta S_{\rm as}(\bm{q})\right>,

(8)

where
\dot{\sigma}
is the EP rate and
\Delta S_{\rm as}(\bm{q})
is the asymmetry in SSD-given state
\bm{q}
:

\displaystyle\Delta S_{\rm as}(\bm{q})=\ln{\frac{p(\bm{q})}{p(\widetilde{\bm{q}})}}.

(9)

To measure the EP rate
\dot{\sigma}
, we need to estimate
\Delta S_{\rm as}(\bm{q})
. To do so, we define another objective function:

\displaystyle J_{\theta_{0}}=\langle\Delta S_{\theta_{0}}(\bm{q}_{t})-e^{-\Delta S_{\theta_{0}}(\bm{q}_{t})}+1\rangle_{t},

(10)

where
\Delta S_{\theta_{0}}(\bm{q}_{t})=h_{\theta_{0}}(\bm{q}_{t})-h_{\theta_{0}}(\widetilde{\bm{q}}_{t})
and
h_{\theta_{0}}
is another MLP. Using the same method as before, we maximize Eq. (
10
).
The optimal parameter
\theta_{0}^{\star}
gives

\displaystyle\Delta S_{\theta_{0}^{\star}}(\bm{q}_{t})

\displaystyle=\ln{\frac{p(\bm{q}_{t})}{p(\widetilde{\bm{q}_{t}})}},

(11)

\displaystyle J_{\theta_{0}^{\star}}

\displaystyle=\langle\Delta S_{\rm as}(\bm{q})\rangle.

(12)

Using the two trained neural estimators, the estimations of the stochastic EP
\Delta S_{\rm tot}
and EP rate
\dot{\sigma}
are, respectively,

\displaystyle\Delta\hat{S}_{\rm tot}(\bm{q}_{t},\bm{q}_{t+1})=\Delta S_{\theta_{1}}(\bm{q}_{t},\bm{q}_{t+1})-\Delta S_{\theta_{0}}(\bm{q}_{t+1}),

(13)

and

\displaystyle\hat{\dot{\sigma}}=\frac{J_{\theta_{1}}-J_{\theta_{0}}}{\Delta\tau}.

(14)

III
One-particle odd-parity Markov jump process

Next, we consider a one-particle Markov jump process on a ring with
N
sites
Lee et al. 2013
. In this model, the particle state is defined as
\bm{q}=(n,v)
, and its time reversal state is denoted as
\widetilde{\bm{q}}=(n,-v)
where
n\in\{1,\dots,N\}
is the position variable and
v\in\{-1,+1\}
is velocity variable. The master equation is given as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}p(\bm{q},t)=\sum_{\bm{q}^{\prime}\neq\bm{q}}[\omega_{\bm{q},\bm{q}^{\prime}}p(\bm{q}^{\prime},t)-\omega_{\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{q}}p(\bm{q},t)],

(15)

where
\omega_{\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{q}}
denotes the transition rate for the jump from
\bm{q}
to
\bm{q}^{\prime}
, and the transition rates are given as
\omega_{(n\pm v,\pm v),(n,\pm v)}=f_{\pm v}
and
\omega_{(n,\mp v),(n,\pm v)}=r_{\pm v}
.
For a jump of waiting time
\tau
(
\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{q}^{\prime}
,
\tau
), the entropy production
Lee et al. 2013
;
Martínez et al. 2019
is written as

\displaystyle\Delta{S}_{\rm tot}(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{\prime},\tau)=

\displaystyle\ln{\frac{\omega_{\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{q}}e^{-\tau/\tau_{\bm{q}}}}{\omega_{\tilde{\bm{q}},\tilde{\bm{q}}^{\prime}}e^{-\tau/\tau_{\widetilde{\bm{q}}}}}}+\ln{\frac{p(\bm{q})}{p(\bm{q}^{\prime})}},

(16)

where
p(\bm{q})\equiv p(\bm{q},\infty)
is the steady state of Eq. (
15
). The rightmost term in Eq. (
16
) is the system EP of the steady state.
The first term on the right-hand side denotes the EP due to the jump and the wait.
The numerator
\omega_{\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{q}}\psi_{\bm{q}}(\tau)
is the transition rate
\bm{q}\rightarrow\bm{q}^{\prime}
after waiting for time
\tau
in the state
\bm{q}
. The denominator
\omega_{\tilde{\bm{q}},\tilde{\bm{q}}^{\prime}}e^{-\tau/\tau_{\widetilde{\bm{q}}}}
is the corresponding reversed transition rate with
\tau
waiting time in the state
\widetilde{\bm{q}}
after a jump
\widetilde{\bm{q}}^{\prime}\rightarrow\widetilde{\bm{q}}
. The mean waiting time of the state
\bm{q}
is
\tau_{\bm{q}}=1/\sum_{\bm{q}^{\prime}\neq\bm{q}}\omega_{\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{q}}
.

For a Markov jump process, we represent a trajectory
\Gamma
as a sequence of jumps:

\displaystyle\Gamma=\{({\bm{q}}_{1}\rightarrow{\bm{q}}_{2},\tau_{1}),\dots,({\bm{q}}_{i}\rightarrow{\bm{q}}_{i+1},\tau_{i}),\dots\},

(17)

where
q_{i}
denotes the
i
-th state. To train neural networks with the sequence of jumps, we rearrange Eq. (
17
) into

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta{S}_{\rm tot}(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{\prime},\tau)=&\ln{\frac{R(\widetilde{\bm{q}}^{\prime})}{R(\bm{q}^{\prime})}}+\ln{\frac{R(\widetilde{\bm{q}})}{R(\bm{q})}}\\
&+\ln{\frac{\rho_{\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{q}}R(\bm{q})}{{\rho}_{\widetilde{\bm{q}},\widetilde{\bm{q}}^{\prime}}R(\widetilde{\bm{q}}^{\prime})}}+\ln{\frac{\psi_{\bm{q}}(\tau)R({\bm{q}})}{\psi_{\widetilde{\bm{q}}}(\tau)R(\widetilde{\bm{q}})}}\\
&+\ln{\frac{\tau_{\bm{q}}\tau_{\widetilde{\bm{q}}^{\prime}}}{\tau_{\widetilde{\bm{q}}},\tau_{\bm{q}^{\prime}}}}\end{split}

(18)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\Delta{S}_{\rm alt}(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{\prime},\tau)+\ln{\frac{\tau_{\bm{q}}\tau_{\widetilde{\bm{q}}^{\prime}}}{\tau_{\widetilde{\bm{q}}},\tau_{\bm{q}^{\prime}}}},

(19)

where
\rho_{\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{q}}\equiv\tau_{\bm{q}}\omega_{\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{q}}
denotes the jump probability from
\bm{q}
to
\bm{q}^{\prime}
and
R_{\bm{q}}\equiv\tau^{-1}_{\bm{q}}p(\bm{q})/\sum_{\bm{q}^{\prime}}\tau^{-1}_{\bm{q}^{\prime}}p(\bm{q}^{\prime})
is the stationary probability distribution of jump probabilities
\rho_{\bm{q}^{\prime},\bm{q}}

Martínez et al. 2019
.
The waiting time distribution (WTD) in the state
\bm{q}
is denoted by
\psi_{\bm{q}}(\tau)\equiv\frac{e^{-\tau/\tau_{\bm{q}}}}{\tau_{\bm{q}}}
.
The last term of Eq. (
18
), which consists of mean waiting times, can be directly measured from without any estimator hence we focus on estimating
\Delta{S}_{\rm alt}(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{\prime},\tau)
. Because the average of the last term is zero, an averaged total entropy production and an averaged alternative entropy production are the same
\langle\Delta S_{\rm tot}\rangle=\langle\Delta S_{\rm alt}\rangle
.
To estimate the stochastic EP [Eq. (
18
)], we require three kinds of estimators:
\Delta S_{\theta_{0}}
,
\Delta S_{\theta_{1}}
, and
\Delta S_{\theta_{2}}
.
The first and second terms on the right-hand side of Eq. (
18
) can be estimated with
\Delta S_{\theta_{0}}(\bm{q})
and
\Delta S_{\theta_{0}}(\bm{q}^{\prime})
, respectively.
The third term can be estimated with
\Delta S_{\theta_{1}}(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{\prime})
.
We use the same objective functions as the previous section, Eq. (
10
) and Eq. (
4
), for training
\Delta S_{\theta_{0}}
and
\Delta S_{\theta_{1}}
, but we develop a different kind of estimator with a new objective function for estimating the fourth term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
18
):

\displaystyle J_{\theta_{2}}=\langle\Delta S_{\theta_{2}}(\bm{q},\tau)-e^{-\Delta S_{\theta_{2}}(\bm{q},\tau)}+1\rangle_{\tau},

(20)

where

\displaystyle\Delta S_{\theta_{2}}(\bm{q},\tau)=h_{\theta_{2}}(\bm{q},\tau)-h_{\theta_{2}}(\widetilde{\bm{q}},\tau).

(21)

The optimal parameter
\theta_{2}^{\star}
gives

\displaystyle\Delta S_{\theta_{2}^{\star}}(\bm{q},\tau)

\displaystyle=\ln{\frac{\psi_{\bm{q}}(\tau)}{\psi_{\widetilde{\bm{q}}}(\tau)}}+\ln{\frac{R_{\bm{q}}}{R_{\widetilde{\bm{q}}}}},

(22)

\displaystyle J_{\theta_{2}^{\star}}

\displaystyle=\langle\Delta S_{\theta_{2}}(\bm{q},\tau)\rangle_{\tau}+\langle\Delta S_{\rm as}(\bm{q})\rangle.

(23)

The first term of Eq. (
22
) is the contribution from the waiting time
\tau
to the EP. We denote this contribution as

\displaystyle\Delta{S}_{\rm WTD}(\bm{q},\tau)\equiv\ln{\frac{\psi_{\bm{q}}(\tau)}{\psi_{\widetilde{\bm{q}}}(\tau)}}.

(24)

Then, using these three trained neural estimators, we can estimate the EP
\Delta S_{\rm alt}
and EP rate
\dot{\sigma}
with the following equations:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta{S}_{\rm alt}(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{\prime},\tau)&=\Delta S_{\theta_{1}}(\bm{q},\bm{q}^{\prime})+\Delta S_{\theta_{2}}(\bm{q},\tau)\\
&-\Delta S_{\theta_{0}}(\bm{q})-\Delta S_{\theta_{0}}(\bm{q}^{\prime}),\end{split}

(25)

and

\displaystyle\hat{\dot{\sigma}}=\frac{J_{\theta_{1}}+J_{\theta_{2}}-2J_{\theta_{0}}}{\tau_{\rm avg}},

(26)

where
\tau_{\rm avg}
denotes the average of the waiting time
\tau
from the trajectory data. To handle the discrete state variables, we replace the input layers of
h_{\theta_{0}}
,
h_{\theta_{1}}
, and
h_{\theta_{2}}
with an embedding layer that converts discrete values into continuous space vectors. The dimension of the embedding layer is 128; other neural network configurations and training setup are the same as in the previous section.

,

,

, and

IV
Discussion

Unlike systems with only even-parity variables, in which EP can be estimated with a single neural estimator
Kim et al. 2020
, we require additional estimators to treat the mirror state asymmetry
\Delta S_{\rm as}
[Eq. (
9
)] and waiting-time contribution
\Delta S_{\rm WTD}
[Eq. (
24
)] in the estimation of EP in systems with odd-parity variables.

The first example in this work demonstrated that EP in the underdamped bead-spring model can be estimated by two neural estimators
\Delta S_{\theta_{0}}
and
\Delta S_{\theta_{1}}
which are trained to maximize
J_{\theta_{0}}
and
J_{\theta_{1}}
, respectively. However, this method has a limitation when
J_{\theta_{0}}
and
J_{\theta_{1}}
values are very close to each other; a small difference between these two values may cause a high variance in the EP estimations. This problem comes the training of two independent objective functions
J_{\theta_{0}}
and
J_{\theta_{1}}
. As shown in Eq. (
12
) and Eq. (
8
),
J_{\theta_{1}}
includes contribution from
\langle\Delta S_{\rm as}\rangle
[Eq. (
9
)], so if the
\langle S_{\rm as}\rangle
contribution in
J_{\theta_{1}}
is dominant, then estimating the EP from the difference between
J_{\theta_{0}}
and
J_{\theta_{1}}
might be hard. Also,

[Eq. (

and

We stress that our method is the first tool to estimate EP in a Markov jump process with odd-parity variables.
As shown in Eq. (
24
), the WTD does not contribute to the EP when the system has no odd-parity variables. However, if the accessible states are coarse-grained variables, then the WTD plays an important role despite the absence of odd-parity variables. In a related work, Martínez
et al.

Martínez et al. 2019
developed a method using WTD statistics to infer the EP from trajectories of coarse-grained states whose dynamics are described as semi-Markov processes; this method can infer EP even in the absence of observable currents. Also, a recent work by Skinner and Dunkel
Skinner and Dunkel 2021
uses the fluctuation in WTD to infer the EP rate in the hidden Markov processes of two coarse-grained states. As a future work, developing an estimator for EP when odd-parity variables are coarse-grained will be an intriguing task, because the inferred EP from the trajectory of coarse-grained states can be larger than the actual EP when odd-parity variables are coarse-grained
Kawaguchi and Nakayama 2013

Esposito and Parrondo 2015
.

In summary, we have developed a machine learning based method that can estimate the stochastic EP and EP rate in a fluctuating system with odd-parity variables via neural networks. Our method was demonstrated with both an underdamped Langevin process and Markov jump process with odd-parity variables. It is expected to provide a practical tool for studying nonequilibrium systems in general experimental situations.
