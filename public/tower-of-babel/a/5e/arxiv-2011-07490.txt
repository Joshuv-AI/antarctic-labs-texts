1.
Introduction

The development of a thermodynamically consistent implicit constitutive theory for continuum mechanics was initiated by Rajagopal in
[
16
]
. While, as was noted in
[
16
]
, implicit constitutive theories within a purely mechanical format had been studied earlier, “these early studies do not recognize the important role thermodynamics has to play in the response of materials, nor do they provide a systematic framework that allows one to incorporate issues concerning constraints and material symmetry.” In contrast, the theoretical framework proposed in
[
16
]
is a systematic and thermodynamically consistent way of justifying implicit and nonlinear relationships between stress, strain and their derivatives for elastic and viscoelastic materials. In classical models of such bodies, the strain is given as a function of the stress, which leads to an explicit, usually linear, relationship between the Cauchy stress tensor
\mathbb{T}
and the linearised strain tensor
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
(and the derivative
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t}
in the viscoelastic case) under the small displacement gradient assumption. However, it is well-known that linear models are insufficient to describe all physical phenomena in the small strain range; see, for example,
[
24
]
,
[
13
]
and
[
8
]
, to name just a few, thus justifying the need for alternative models. One of the key contributions in
[
19
]
was to use linearisations of nonlinear constitutive models to justify the presence of small strain in nonlinear models.

We are interested in a particular subclass of these implicit constitutive models, which represent a generalisation of the classical Kelvin–Voigt model to strain-limiting viscoelastic solids.
Mathematically, the term strain-limiting refers to the fact that the strain is
a priori
bounded. As we study viscoelastic solids rather than elastic ones, a linear combination of the linearised strain and its time derivative will be taken to be a bounded function of the stress.
In this paper, we will focus on the rigorous mathematical analysis of the initial-boundary-value problem that results from such models.

Strain-limiting behaviour has been exhibited in experimental procedures by certain biological matter
[
7
]
, for example. Models of
strain-limiting materials are also of importance in the study of fracture mechanics and crack propagation. Fracture of a brittle material can occur in the small-strain range and is caused by a large stress. However, if we use a linear constitutive relation to model a body, the strain will behave like
O(r^{-\frac{1}{2}})
, where
r
is the distance to the crack tip
[
20
]
. This contradicts the small displacement gradient assumption under which the model is derived. Hence, a better model might ensure that the strain is
a priori
bounded, as in the case of strain-limiting models.
Another class of problems in which strain-limiting models are of relevance are those that concern bodies that undergo a small local deformation in the presence of a concentrated load. Concentration of load causes a large stress in a small subregion of the body. Thus, in order to ensure that the assumptions under which the model has been derived are not violated, one should ensure that the strain remains small.
We refer to
[
25
]
and the references therein for further discussion on such models.

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}):=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{|\mathbb{T}|}\frac{t}{(1+t^{a})^{\frac{1}{a}}}\,\mathrm{d}t,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bigg(\frac{|\boldsymbol{u}_{t}|^{2}}{2}+f_{\alpha}^{*}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}))\bigg)+\alpha(\mathbb{T}-\mathbb{T}_{0,\alpha}):(F_{\alpha}(\mathbb{T})-F_{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}_{0,\alpha}))\,\mathrm{d}x

1.2.
Statement of the model problem.

We will make some mathematical simplifications in order to make the rigorous mathematical analysis of the problem more manageable.
Neglecting (
1.6
)
1
and assuming that
\rho_{0}\equiv 1
, we obtain the following system of equations:

(1.9)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{tt}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}_{\mathbf{X}}\mathbb{T}+\boldsymbol{f},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{t})+\alpha\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=F(\mathbb{T}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{T}^{\mathrm{T}},

in
[0,\infty)\times\Omega
, with suitable initial and boundary conditions.
We note that if, more generally,
\rho_{0}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})
is such that
\rho_{0}
is uniformly bounded away from
0
, then it is not much more difficult to include a variable density.

A further simplification that we will make is to take
\Omega=(0,1)^{d}
, with dimension
d\geq 2
, and to supplement the problem with a periodic boundary condition. This allows us to work with Fourier basis functions in a finite-dimensional approximation of the problem, leading to a sequence of semi-discrete numerical approximations. Because of this simplification, we can view the work here as a time-dependent analogue of the problem studied in
[
5
]
, dealing with viscoelastic solids rather than elastic ones.
We note that despite its geometrical simplicity, one can still use the framework presented here to study the effects of concentrated loads that are active in the neighbourhood of the centre point of the periodic cell, assuming that the side-length of the cell is large enough so that the effects of concentrations are not effective in the neighbourhood of the boundary of the cell. Trivially, the results of the paper extend to any axiparallel parallelepiped
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
, independent of the edge-lengths of
\Omega
.

The form of
F
studied in this paper has already been considered in the purely elastic case, for example, in
[
5
]
. For elastic solids, it has been shown in
[
5
]
that a unique weak solution to the static problem in the periodic setting exists for every
a\in(0,\frac{2}{d})
and a renormalised solution exists for every
a\in(0,\infty)
, with coincidence of these two notions of solution for the range
a\in(0,\frac{2}{d})
; and in the case of a homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition the existence of a unique weak solution was shown for
a\in(0,\frac{1}{d})
in
[
3
]
. When considering mixed Dirichlet–Neumann boundary conditions, the authors of
[
1
]
were able to show existence of a weak solution up to a penalisation on the Neumann part of the boundary. The extension of this work to the time-dependent problem, as well as further open problems, will be discussed at the end of the paper.

The constitutive relation proposed by Rajagopal in
[
18
]
and
[
19
]
is in fact

(1.10)

F(\mathbb{T})=f_{0}(\tr\mathbb{T},\tr\mathbb{T}^{2})\,\mathbb{I}+\frac{\mathbb{T}}{\mu_{0}(1+|\mathbb{T}|^{a})^{\frac{1}{a}}}.

However, we note that under suitable, physically reasonable, structural assumptions on the real-valued-function
f_{0}
the extension of the results in this paper to a problem with a constitutive relation given by (
1.10
) is fairly straightforward. Hence, for the sake of simplicity of the exposition, we shall assume in what follows that
f_{0}\equiv 0
and
\mu_{0}=1
and focus on the key difficulty in the analysis: dealing with the second summand on the right-hand side of (
1.10
).

A similar problem to (
1.9
) is discussed in
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
; the authors considered a quasi-static problem in both papers, by which we mean that the term
\boldsymbol{u}_{tt}
was omitted from (
1.9
). The function
F
there was given by (
1.10
) with
f_{0}\equiv 0
. It was shown that a solution to the problem exists, using an elliptic regularisation technique similar to the one employed in this paper, combined with a fixed-point argument.
Appropriate bounds were then found in order to allow the regularisation parameter to go to
0
.
A marked difference here compared to the analysis in those papers is that the stress
\mathbb{T}
was only shown there to belong to the space
C([0,T];\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Omega})^{d\times d})
, where
\mathcal{M}(\overline{\Omega})
is the space of Radon measures on
\overline{\Omega}
.
In contrast, we shall be able to ensure that the stress is at least in the space
L^{1}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega)^{d\times d})
in any number of space dimensions
d
; and, in three space dimensions, if
a\in(0,\frac{2}{7})
, then
\mathbb{T}\in L^{1+\delta}(0,T;L^{1+\delta}(\Omega))
for a
\delta>0
, the value of which depends only on
a
.
Furthermore, we are able to show that the constitutive relation holds in a pointwise sense rather than in a variational sense, as was the case in
[
14
]
and
[
15
]
. This then enables us to prove the validity of the constitutive relation by making use of the fact that the stress tensor is an integrable function.

There are some important differences between the multi-dimensional problem (
2.1
) and its one-dimensional counterpart. In one space dimension the symmetric gradient reduces to simply the first derivative in the spatial variable. The one-dimensional time-dependent problem has been studied in
[
9
]
in the context of travelling wave solutions. A substitution was used in order to reduce the problem in two dependent variables to a problem in a single dependent variable.
This work was expanded upon in
[
11
]
, where the existence of a strong solution to the one-dimensional time-dependent problem was proved on the domain
\Omega=\mathbb{R}
. The proof relied on a substitution argument that is specific to the one-dimensional case. Although the regularity of the solutions is much stronger in
[
11
]
, the authors were only able to prove local-in-time existence. Here, we shall prove global-in-time existence of weak solutions in any number of space dimensions.

The paper
[
17
]
, with corresponding mathematical analysis contained in
[
2
]
and
[
4
]
, deals with a different generalisation of the Kelvin–Voigt model, where the stress is decomposed into an elastic component
\mathbb{T}_{e}
and a dissipative component
\mathbb{T}_{f}
associated with a viscous fluid. Linearising under the assumption (
1.4
), the constitutive relations are
\mathbb{T}_{e}=h_{1}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})
and
\mathbb{T}_{f}=h_{2}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t})
. However, a key point is that the functions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
are not assumed to be bounded. In particular, the problem is not strain-limiting, and some of the key technical difficulties that we encounter here for the strain-limiting problem (
2.1
) are therefore absent.

We are now ready to proceed with our analysis of the strain-limiting problem (
2.1
). The paper is structured as follows. Section
2
introduces the mathematical problem alongside the definition of a weak solution and the statement of the regularised problem, which forms the basis of the weak compactness argument that is at the heart of our proof of the existence of global-in-time large-data weak solutions. Furthermore, some useful auxiliary results that will be used in the proof are given. In Section
3
, we focus on proving the existence of a solution to the regularised problem (
2.5
) by a Galerkin method using a basis of trigonometric polynomials. We also prove various bounds on the sequence of solutions that are independent of the regularisation parameter. Certain bounds may only be proved under stronger restrictions on the data. In Section
4
we take the limit in the regularisation parameter and show that the accumulation point of the sequence of solutions is in fact the unique weak solution of (
2.1
). A key difficulty is showing that the sequence of stress tensors converges pointwise a.e. and then using this to prove that the weak form of the PDE does indeed hold. Then, under further restrictions on the dimension
d
and the parameter
a
, we show that a stronger convergence result can in fact be proved for the sequence of stress tensors. The main difficulty is showing that the sequence of approximate stress tensors is bounded in a reflexive Lebesgue space, from which we are able to deduce strong convergence in
L^{1}((0,T)\times\Omega)
. Finally, in Section
5
, we discuss future work and related open problems.

2.
Formulation of problem and auxiliary results

Let
\Omega:=(0,1)^{d}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
.
For a fixed final time
T>0
, we define the time-space cylinder
Q:=(0,T)\times\Omega
.
Given a space
\mathcal{F}
of real-valued functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\mathcal{F}_{\#}
denote the subspace of functions
f\in\mathcal{F}
that are
1
-periodic, i.e., periodic with respect to each of the
d
co-ordinate directions. Let
\mathcal{F}_{*}
be the subspace of functions from
\mathcal{F}_{\#}
whose integral over
\Omega
is equal to
0
. For example,
L^{p}_{\#}(\Omega)
consists of
1
-periodic functions
f
such that
|f|^{p}
is integrable over
\Omega
.
Let
\|\cdot\|_{p}
denote the usual norm on
L^{p}(\Omega)
for
p\in[1,\infty]
. When considering the norm in
L^{p}(\Omega_{0})
where
\Omega_{0}\neq\Omega
, we will state it explicitly.
Furthermore,
\mathcal{F}^{d}
denotes the space of
d
-component vector functions such that each component is an element of
\mathcal{F}
. The space
\mathcal{F}^{d\times d}
is defined analogously.
If

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\alpha>0
and

. Suppose that
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d}

with

(3.1)

for a constant

. Furthermore, let

be given.
Then, there exists a unique weak solution
(\boldsymbol{u},\mathbb{T})
of the regularised problem (
2.5
) in the sense of Definition
2.2
. In addition, the following bound holds:

(3.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\boldsymbol{u}(t)\|_{2}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(t)\|_{2}+\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u})\|_{L^{n+1}(Q)}+\int_{Q}|\mathbb{T}|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\bigg(\int_{\Omega}|\mathbb{T}(t)|^{1-a}\chi_{\{|\mathbb{T}(t)|\geq 1\}}\bigg)\leq C,

where
C
is a positive constant that is independent of

and

is the indicator function of any measurable set

The regularity requirements on the initial data are higher than one might expect. It is for a technical reason that we demand

for a

is continuously embedded into

. This will allow us to deduce the strong convergence in

of a certain sequence of approximations of

to be bounded in

is natural since we eventually want to take the limit as

and thus we would like

to be well-defined.
The weakening of the conditions on

will be discussed in the first remark in Section

Proof.

We will construct a weak solution of (

For each

for every

with

is defined by

The initial conditions are

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}^{m}(0)=P^{m}\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\quad\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(0)=P^{m}\boldsymbol{v}_{0},

\displaystyle(\dot{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{m}(t),\dot{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}^{m}(t))

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\gamma}^{m}(0)

\dot{\boldsymbol{v}}(t)\rightarrow\mathbf{0}
strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
as
t\rightarrow 0+
, as well as
\boldsymbol{v}
,
\dot{\boldsymbol{v}}\in C([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)^{d})
with Lemma 7.3 of
[
22
]
. Using the monotonicity of
F_{n}
, it follows that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\dot{\boldsymbol{v}}(t)|^{2}}{2}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\mathbb{T}_{1}-\mathbb{T}_{2}):(F_{n}(\mathbb{T}_{1})-F_{n}(\mathbb{T}_{2}))\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(2\alpha^{2}+\alpha)|\dot{\boldsymbol{v}}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s+\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\dot{\boldsymbol{v}}(t)|^{2}}{4}\,\mathrm{d}x.

Applying Grönwall’s inequality, we deduce that

\int_{\Omega}\frac{|\dot{\boldsymbol{v}}(t)|^{2}}{4}\,\mathrm{d}x+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(\mathbb{T}_{1}-\mathbb{T}_{2}):(F_{n}(\mathbb{T}_{1})-F_{n}(\mathbb{T}_{2}))\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s=0,

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
. Thus
\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{1}=\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}_{2}
a.e. in
Q
. Since
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(0)=\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(0)
, it follows that
\boldsymbol{u}_{1}=\boldsymbol{u}_{2}
and
F_{n}(\mathbb{T}_{1})=F_{n}(\mathbb{T}_{2})
a.e. in
Q
. Noting that
F_{n}
is a bijection, we must have
\mathbb{T}_{1}=\mathbb{T}_{2}
a.e. in
Q
. In particular,
(\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\mathbb{T}_{1})=(\boldsymbol{u}_{2},\mathbb{T}_{2})
and we have uniqueness of weak solutions of (
2.5
).
∎

We would like to use this approximation in order to show that a weak solution exists to the strain-limiting problem (
2.1
). To do this, we must obtain further
a priori
estimates. Since we are working in the periodic setting, we will be able to do this when working with the Galerkin approximation
(\boldsymbol{u}^{m},\mathbb{T}^{m})
of the regularised problem, rather than the weak solution of (

) itself.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that the hypotheses of Theorem
3.1
hold and that additionally we have
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in W^{1,2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{f}\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d})
.
Let
(\boldsymbol{u}^{m},\mathbb{T}^{m})
be the solution of the Galerkin approximation from

as in the proof of Theorem

.
There exists a constant

independent of

and

such that

(3.20)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\nabla\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\int_{Q}\frac{|\nabla\mathbb{T}^{m}|^{2}}{(1+|\mathbb{T}^{m}|)^{1+a}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C(a,\alpha,\Omega,T)\Big(\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{f}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\Big).

Moreover, if we also have that
\boldsymbol{f}\in W^{1,2}([0,T+\tilde{\epsilon});L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d})
for some
\tilde{\epsilon}>0
, then

(3.21)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\int_{Q}\frac{|\dot{\mathbb{T}}^{m}|^{2}}{(1+|\mathbb{T}^{m}|)^{1+a}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq C(a,\alpha,\Omega,T)\Big(\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\dot{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{d}+\|\boldsymbol{f}(0)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}+(1+f^{-1}(C_{1}))\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{2,2}^{2}\Big),

for every
m\geq m_{0}
, where
C_{1}\in(0,1)
and

are the constants from the proof of Theorem
3.1
and

is defined on
[0,\infty)
by

. The constant
C
on the right-hand side of (

) is independent of
m
and
n
.

Proof.

We start with (
3.20
). Derivation does not increase the degree of a trigonometric polynomial. In particular,
\nabla\cdot\nabla(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}^{m})
is a trigonometric polynomial of degree at most
m
. Furthermore it has integral over
\Omega
equal to
0
as a result of periodicity. Thus
\nabla\cdot\nabla(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}^{m})
is a valid test function in (
3.3
). It follows that

(3.22)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}\cdot(\nabla\cdot\nabla(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}^{m}))+\mathbb{T}^{m}:\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\nabla\cdot\nabla(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}^{m}))

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{f}\cdot(\nabla\cdot\nabla(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}^{m}))\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}:\nabla(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}^{m})+(\nabla\cdot\mathbb{T}^{m})\cdot(\nabla\cdot\nabla(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}^{m}))

\displaystyle-\nabla\boldsymbol{f}:\nabla(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}^{m})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big(\frac{|\nabla\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}|^{2}}{2}+\alpha\nabla\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}:\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\Big)-\alpha|\nabla\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}|^{2}+\nabla\mathbb{T}^{m}\,\vdots\,\nabla F_{n}(\mathbb{T}^{m})

\displaystyle-\nabla\boldsymbol{f}:\nabla(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}^{m})\,\mathrm{d}x.

We use
\nabla\mathbb{S}
to denote the third order tensor
(\partial_{k}S_{ij})_{i,j,k}
and if
\mathcal{S}_{1}
,
\mathcal{S}_{2}
are third order tensors, we let “
\vdots
” denote the triple scalar product

\mathcal{S}_{1}\,\vdots\,\mathcal{S}_{2}=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}(\mathcal{S}_{1})_{ijk}(\mathcal{S}_{2})_{ijk}.

We justify the transition to the last line of (
3.22
) as follows. For ease of notation, we write
\mathbb{S}=\mathbb{T}^{m}
and
\boldsymbol{v}=\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}^{m}
in the following calculation. Using integration by parts and the periodic boundary conditions, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(\nabla\cdot\mathbb{S})\cdot(\nabla\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial S_{ij}}{\partial x_{j}}\frac{\partial^{2}v_{i}}{\partial x_{k}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial S_{ij}}{\partial x_{k}}\frac{\partial^{2}v_{i}}{\partial x_{k}\partial x_{j}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\partial S_{ij}}{\partial x_{k}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}+\frac{\partial v_{j}}{\partial x_{i}}\Big)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla\mathbb{S}\,\vdots\,\nabla\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}x.

Manipulating (
3.22
) in an almost identical way as in the calculation that resulted (
3.10
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}^{m}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\nabla\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\int_{Q}\frac{|\nabla\mathbb{T}^{m}|^{2}}{(1+|\mathbb{T}^{m}|)^{1+a}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq C(a,\alpha,T)\Big(\|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\nabla\boldsymbol{f}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\Big),

where
C
is a constant that is independent of
n
and
m
. Thus (
3.20
) holds.

For (
3.21
), we recall that the weak solution
(\boldsymbol{u}^{m},\mathbb{T}^{m})
can be extended to the larger interval
(0,T+\epsilon)
for some
\epsilon=\epsilon_{m,n}>0
. We define the undivided difference quotient with increment
h
in the time variable by

\Delta^{h}_{t}g(t,x)=g(t+h,x)-g(t,x),

for any function
g
. Provided that
h>0
is sufficiently small, we deduce that
\Delta^{h}_{t}\boldsymbol{u}^{m}
,
\Delta^{h}_{t}\mathbb{T}^{m}
and
\Delta^{h}_{t}\boldsymbol{f}
are well-defined on
(0,T]
. From now on, we will assume that
h>0
is small enough so that this is true.

From (
3.3
) we have that

(3.23)

\int_{\Omega}\Delta^{h}_{t}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(t)\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\Delta^{h}_{t}\mathbb{T}^{m}(t):\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\Delta^{h}_{t}\boldsymbol{f}(t)\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x,

for every
\boldsymbol{v}\in V_{m}
and
t\in(0,T)
. Setting
\boldsymbol{v}=\Delta^{h}_{t}(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}^{m})(t)
in (
3.23
), we deduce that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\bigg[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big(\frac{|\Delta^{h}_{t}\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}|^{2}}{2}+\alpha\Delta^{h}_{t}\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}\cdot\Delta^{h}_{t}\boldsymbol{u}^{m}\Big)-\alpha|\Delta^{h}_{t}\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}|^{2}+\Delta^{h}_{t}\mathbb{T}^{m}:\Delta^{h}_{t}F_{n}(\mathbb{T}^{m})

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\Delta^{h}_{t}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\Delta^{h}_{t}(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}^{m})\bigg]\,\mathrm{d}x.

Integrating over
(0,t)
for arbitrary
t\in(0,T)
, manipulating in the usual way and dividing through by
h^{2}
yields

\displaystyle\frac{\|\Delta^{h}_{t}\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(t)\|_{2}^{2}}{h^{2}}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\Delta^{h}_{t}\mathbb{T}^{m}}{h}:\frac{\Delta^{h}_{t}F(\mathbb{T}^{m})}{h}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq C(a,\alpha,T)\Big(\frac{\|\Delta^{h}_{t}\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(0)\|_{2}^{2}}{h^{2}}+\frac{\|\Delta^{h}_{t}\boldsymbol{u}^{m}(0)\|_{2}^{2}}{h^{2}}+\frac{\|\Delta^{h}_{t}\boldsymbol{f}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}}{h^{2}}\Big).

Using the regularity properties of
(\phi_{i})_{i=1}^{\infty}
and the coefficients
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{m}
of the finite-dimensional solution, we may use Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem when taking the limit as
h\rightarrow 0+
to deduce that

(3.24)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\int_{Q}\frac{|\dot{\mathbb{T}}^{m}|^{2}}{(1+|\mathbb{T}^{m}|)^{1+a}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C(a,\alpha,T)\Big(\|\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(0)\|_{2}^{2}+\|\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(0)\|_{2}^{2}+\|\dot{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq C(a,\alpha,T)\Big(\|\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(0)\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\dot{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\Big).

To bound
(\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(0))_{m}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)^{d}
, independent of
m
, we note that by the structure of the finite-dimensional problem the following holds:

(3.25)

\displaystyle\|\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(0)\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(0)\cdot\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(0)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}-\mathbb{T}^{m}(0):\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(0))+\boldsymbol{f}(0)\cdot\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(0)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\Big(\mathrm{div}(F_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(P^{m}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))))+\boldsymbol{f}(0)\Big)\cdot\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(0)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\|\mathrm{div}(F_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(P^{m}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))))\|_{2}+\|\boldsymbol{f}(0)\|_{2}\Big)\|\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(0)\|_{2}.

To bound the first term on the right-hand side of (
3.25
), we first note that

(3.26)

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{div}(F_{n}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(P^{m}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}))))\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|D(F_{n}^{-1})(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(P^{m}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0})))\|_{\infty}\|D(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(P^{m}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0})))\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|D(F_{n}^{-1})(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(P^{m}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0})))\|_{\infty}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{2,2},

where
F_{n}^{-1}
is continuously differentiable by Lemma
2.5
. By properties of symmetric, positive definite matrices, we are also able to show that there exists a constant
C_{a}
depending only on the parameter
a
such that

(3.27)

|D(F_{n}^{-1})(\mathbb{S})|=|(DF_{n})^{-1}(F_{n}^{-1}(\mathbb{S}))|\leq C_{a}(1+|F_{n}^{-1}(\mathbb{S})|^{a+1}),

for every
\mathbb{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
The first equality comes from use of the inverse function theorem.
Using (
3.27
) and (
3.26
) in (
3.25
) yields

\displaystyle\|\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(0)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{f}(0)\|_{2}+\|(DF_{n})^{-1}(\mathbb{T}^{m}(0))\|_{\infty}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{2,2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{f}(0)\|_{2}+C_{a}(1+f^{-1}(C_{1})^{a+1})\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{2,2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}_{*}(\Omega))}+C_{a}(1+f^{-1}(C_{1})^{a+1})\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{2,2},

for every
m\geq m_{0}
, where
m_{0}
and
C_{1}
are the constants from the proof of Theorem
3.1
. Substituting this into (
3.24
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{m}(t)\|_{2}^{2}+\int_{Q}\frac{|\dot{\mathbb{T}}^{m}|^{2}}{(1+|\mathbb{T}^{m}|)^{1+a}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq C(a,\alpha,T)\Big(\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}_{*}(\Omega))}+(1+f^{-1}(C_{1})^{a+1})\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{2,2}+\|\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\dot{\boldsymbol{f}}\|_{L^{2}(Q)}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq C(a,\alpha,T,\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\boldsymbol{f}),

for every
m\geq m_{0}
, where
C
is a constant that is independent of
m
and
n
. Thus (
3.20
) is satisfied and the proof is complete.
∎

4.
Existence of a solution to the strain-limiting problem

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\alpha>0
and
a>0
. Assume that
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in W^{1,2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d}
are given such that
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in W^{k+1,2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d}
for some
k>\frac{d}{2}
with

\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\|_{\infty}\leq C_{*}<1,

for a constant
C_{*}\in(0,1)
. Let
\boldsymbol{f}
be an element of
W^{1,2}(0,T+\epsilon;L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d})\cap L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d})
, for an
\epsilon>0
. Then, there exists a unique weak solution
(\boldsymbol{u},\mathbb{T})
of the strain-limiting problem (
2.1
) in the sense of Definition
2.1
.

Furthermore, if
(\boldsymbol{u}^{n},\mathbb{T}^{n})
denotes the weak solution of (
2.5
) for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the following convergence results hold:

•

\boldsymbol{u}^{n}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\boldsymbol{u}
weakly-* in
L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d})
;

•

\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}
weakly-* in
L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d})
;

•

\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}
weakly-* in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d})
;

•

\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}^{n}\rightharpoonup\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}
weakly in
L^{p}(0,T;W^{1,p}_{*}(\Omega)^{d})
for every
p\in[1,\infty)
;

•

\mathbb{T}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}
pointwise a.e. in
Q
.

Proof.

The proof of uniqueness is almost identical to that of Theorem
3.1
so we only prove existence here.
First, we would like to show that
(\mathbb{T}^{n})_{n}
converges pointwise a.e. on
Q
. To this end, we use an argument similar to one in
[
5
]
but adapted to the time-dependent problem under consideration here.
We let
(\boldsymbol{u}^{n,m},\mathbb{T}^{n,m})
denote the solution to the Galerkin approximation from
V_{m}
of the regularised problem with the parameter
n
, and
(\boldsymbol{u}^{n},\mathbb{T}^{n})
the weak solution of (
2.5
). We define sequences
(\mathbb{S}^{n,m})_{m}
and
(s^{n,m})_{m}
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}^{n,m}=\frac{\mathbb{T}^{n,m}}{(1+|\mathbb{T}^{n,m}|)^{a+1}}\quad\text{ and }\quad s^{n,m}=\frac{1}{(1+|\mathbb{T}^{n,m}|)^{a+1}}.

Trivially, we have

\|\mathbb{S}^{n,m}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}+\|s^{n,m}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q)}\leq 2.

From direct computation, we get

|\dot{\mathbb{S}}^{n,m}|+|\dot{s}^{n,m}|\leq C\frac{|\dot{\mathbb{T}}^{n,m}|}{(1+|\mathbb{T}^{n,m}|)^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{a}{2}}},

for a constant
C
depending only on
a
. An analogous result holds for the spatial derivatives. Using (
3.20
) and (
3.21
), it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{Q}|\dot{\mathbb{S}}^{n,m}|^{2}+|\dot{s}^{n,m}|^{2}+|\nabla\mathbb{S}^{n,m}|^{2}+|\nabla s^{n,m}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C,

for every
m\geq m_{0}
, where
C
is a constant that is independent of
m
and
n
. By weak compactness, for each fixed
n
, the sequence
(\mathbb{S}^{n,m})_{m}
converges weakly in
W^{1,2}(0,T;L^{2}_{\#}(\Omega)^{d\times d})
and
L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,2}_{\#}(\Omega)^{d\times d})
. However, by the pointwise convergence of
(\mathbb{T}^{n,m})_{m}
to
\mathbb{T}^{n}
, it follows that the limit of
(\mathbb{S}^{n,m})_{m}
in the above spaces is
\mathbb{S}^{n}=\frac{\mathbb{T}^{n}}{(1+|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)^{a+1}}
. An analogous result holds for
(s^{n,m})_{m}
and
s^{n}=(1+|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)^{-a-1}
.
Furthermore, by weak lower semi-continuity of the norm we have

\displaystyle\int_{Q}|\mathbb{S}^{n}|^{2}+|\dot{\mathbb{S}}^{n}|^{2}+|\nabla\mathbb{S}^{n}|^{2}+|s^{n}|^{2}+|\dot{s}^{n}|^{2}+|\nabla s^{n}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C,

where
C
is independent of
n
. Applying the Aubin–Lions lemma, we deduce that
(\mathbb{S}^{n})_{n}
and
(s^{n})_{n}
converge strongly in
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}_{\#}(\Omega)^{d\times d})
and
L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}_{\#}(\Omega))
, respectively. Thus, up to a subsequence that we do not relabel, the sequences converge pointwise a.e. on
Q
. Let us call the limits
\overline{\mathbb{S}}
and
\overline{s}
, respectively.

However, by Fatou’s lemma and (
3.2
), we know that
\overline{s}^{-1-a}
is an element of
L^{1}(Q)^{d\times d}
. Thus
\overline{s}>0
a.e. on
Q
and we deduce that
\mathbb{T}^{n}=S^{n}(s^{n})^{-1}
converges pointwise a.e. on
Q
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. We denote the limit
\mathbb{T}
. By Fatou’s lemma, we have
\mathbb{T}\in L^{1}(Q)^{d\times d}
.

Thanks to the bounds stated in (
3.2
), (
3.20
) and (
3.21
), we deduce that the following convergence results hold:

•

\boldsymbol{u}^{n}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\boldsymbol{u}
weakly-* in
L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d})
;

•

\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}
weakly-* in
L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d})
;

•

\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}
weakly-* in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d})
;

•

\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}^{n}{\rightharpoonup}\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}
weakly in
L^{p}(0,T;W^{1,p}_{*}(\Omega)^{d})
for every
p\in[1,\infty)
.

The attainment of the initial data (
3.1
) follows immediately by reasoning in much the same way as we did in the proof of Theorem
3.1
. As a matter of fact, we have the following stronger result:

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow 0+}\Big(\|\boldsymbol{u}(t)-\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\|_{1,2}+\|\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}(t)-\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\|_{1,2}\Big)=0.

For (
2.3
), we note that
(F(\mathbb{T}^{n}))_{n}
converges pointwise a.e. on
Q
to
F(\mathbb{T})
. Also
(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}^{n}))_{n}
converges weakly in
L^{2}(Q)^{d\times d}
to
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u})
. Since pointwise and weak limits in
L^{2}(Q)
must coincide, (
2.3
) is immediate if we can show that
(\frac{\mathbb{T}^{n}}{n(1+|\mathbb{T}^{n}|^{1-\frac{1}{n}})})_{n}
converges pointwise a.e. in
Q
to
\mathbf{0}
. However, we have

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\bigg|\frac{\mathbb{T}^{n}}{n(1+|\mathbb{T}^{n}|^{1-\frac{1}{n}})}\bigg|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{Q}\frac{1}{n^{2}}+\frac{|\mathbb{T}^{n}|^{\frac{2}{n}}}{n^{2}}\chi_{\{|\mathbb{T}^{n}|\geq 1\}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|Q|}{n^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}\int_{Q}\frac{|\mathbb{T}^{n}|^{1+\frac{1}{n}}}{n}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{n},

where
C
is a constant that is independent of
n
. Hence the sequence converges strongly in
L^{2}(Q)^{d\times d}
(and thus pointwise a.e. in
Q
, up to subsequence) to
\mathbf{0}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
as required.

It remains to show that (
2.2
) holds.
We use similar reasoning to that in
[
1
]
but we repeat all the details for completeness.
Let
\tau\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
,
\boldsymbol{v}\in C^{1}_{*}(\overline{\Omega})^{d}
and
\chi\in C([0,T])
. Since
\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}(t)
and
\boldsymbol{f}(t)
are elements of
L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d}
, we can use any
\boldsymbol{v}\in W^{n+1}_{\#}(\Omega)^{d}
as a test function in (
2.6
), rather than only those with integral over
\Omega
equal to
0
. Thus
\tau(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\boldsymbol{v}\chi
is a valid test function in (
2.6
). We integrate the result over
(0,T)
to get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\int_{Q}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}\cdot\chi\boldsymbol{v}\tau(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)+\mathbb{T}^{n}:\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\chi\boldsymbol{v}\tau(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|))-\boldsymbol{f}\cdot(\chi\boldsymbol{v}\tau(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{Q}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}\cdot\chi\boldsymbol{v}\tau(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)+\tau(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\chi\mathbb{T}^{n}:\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})-\boldsymbol{f}\cdot(\chi\boldsymbol{v}\tau(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)+\chi\mathbb{T}^{n}:\nabla\tau(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\otimes\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

Using the compactness of the support of
\tau
and the pointwise convergence of
(\mathbb{T}^{n})_{n}
, we know that
\tau(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\rightarrow\tau(|\mathbb{T}|)
and
\tau(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\mathbb{T}^{n}\rightarrow\tau(|\mathbb{T}|)\mathbb{T}
strongly in
L^{p}(Q)
for every
p\in[1,\infty)
. Thus we have

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\chi\boldsymbol{v}\tau(|\mathbb{T}|)+\chi\tau(|\mathbb{T}|)\mathbb{T}:\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})-\chi\tau(|\mathbb{T}|)\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{Q}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{n}\cdot\chi\boldsymbol{v}\tau(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)+\tau(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\chi\mathbb{T}^{n}:\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})-\boldsymbol{f}\cdot(\chi\boldsymbol{v}\tau(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\quad=-\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{Q}\chi\mathbb{T}^{n}:\nabla\tau(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\otimes\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

We replace
\tau
by
\tau_{k}\in C^{1}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
where
\tau_{k}
is such that

\displaystyle\tau_{k}(s)=\begin{cases}1,&\quad|s|\leq k,\\
0,&\quad|s|\geq 2k,\end{cases}

and
|\tau_{k}^{\prime}(s)|\leq\frac{C}{k}
for a constant
C
that is independent of
k
. Letting
k\rightarrow\infty
and using Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem, we deduce that

(4.1)

\int_{Q}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}\cdot\chi\boldsymbol{v}+\chi\mathbb{T}:\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})-\chi\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=-\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{Q}\chi\mathbb{T}^{n}:\nabla\tau_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\otimes\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

The term inside the limit on the right-hand side can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\chi\mathbb{T}^{n}:\nabla\tau_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\otimes\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=\int_{Q}\chi b(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)F(\mathbb{T}^{n})_{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\Big(\tau_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\Big)v_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
b
is defined on
[0,\infty)
by
b(t)=(1+t^{a})^{\frac{1}{a}}
. Now define
B_{k}
on
[0,\infty)
by

B_{k}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}b(s)\tau_{k}^{\prime}(s)\,\mathrm{d}s.

With this is mind, we get

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\chi b(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)F(\mathbb{T}^{n})_{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\Big(\tau_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\Big)v_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\int_{Q}\chi b(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\tau^{\prime}_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\Big(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|\Big)F(\mathbb{T}^{n})_{ij}v_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\int_{Q}\chi\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\Big(B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\Big)F(\mathbb{T}^{n})_{ij}v_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\quad=-\int_{Q}\chi B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)F(\mathbb{T}^{n})_{ij}\frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}+\chi B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\Big(F(\mathbb{T}^{n})_{ij}\Big)v_{i}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\quad=-\int_{Q}\chi B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)F(\mathbb{T}^{n})_{ij}\frac{\partial v_{i}}{\partial x_{j}}+\chi B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\mathcal{A}_{ijpq}(\mathbb{T}^{n})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}\Big(T^{n}_{pq}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{T})
is the fourth-order tensor defined by

\mathcal{A}_{ijpq}(\mathbb{T})=\frac{\partial}{\partial T_{pq}}\left(\frac{T_{ij}}{(1+|\mathbb{T}|^{a})^{\frac{1}{a}}}\right).

We note that we can define an inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
by

(\mathbb{S},\mathbf{U})_{\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{T})}=\sum_{i,j,p,q=1}^{d}\mathcal{A}_{ijpq}(\mathbb{T})S_{ij}U_{pq}.

Using this definition, it follows that

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{Q}\chi\mathbb{T}^{n}:\nabla\tau_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\otimes\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\Big|

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\int_{Q}|\chi B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)||F(\mathbb{T}^{n})||\nabla\boldsymbol{v}|+\Big|\Big(\Big(\delta_{jl}B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\frac{v_{i}}{|\boldsymbol{v}|}\Big)_{i,j},\chi|\boldsymbol{v}|\partial_{l}\mathbb{T}^{n}\Big)_{\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\Big|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\Big(\int_{Q}\Big(\Big(\delta_{jl}B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\frac{v_{i}}{|\boldsymbol{v}|}\Big)_{i,j},\delta_{jl}B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\frac{v_{i}}{|\boldsymbol{v}|}\Big)_{i,j}\Big)_{\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\cdot\Big(\int_{Q}(\chi|\boldsymbol{v}|\partial_{l}\mathbb{T}^{n},\chi|\boldsymbol{v}|\partial_{l}\mathbb{T}^{n})_{\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\int_{Q}|\chi B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)||F(\mathbb{T}^{n})||\nabla\boldsymbol{v}|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

Thanks to the definition of
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{T})
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{Q}(\chi|\boldsymbol{v}|\partial_{l}\mathbb{T}^{n},\chi|\boldsymbol{v}|\partial_{l}\mathbb{T}^{n})_{\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{Q}\chi^{2}|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}\nabla\mathbb{T}^{n}\,\vdots\,\nabla F(\mathbb{T}^{n})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{Q}\chi^{2}|\boldsymbol{v}|^{2}\nabla\mathbb{T}^{n}\,\vdots\,\nabla F_{n}(\mathbb{T}^{n})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C(\chi,\boldsymbol{v})\int_{Q}\nabla\mathbb{T}^{n}\,\vdots\,\nabla F_{n}(\mathbb{T}^{n})\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C,

where
C
is a positive constant that is independent of
n
.
Furthermore, we have

(4.2)

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\Big(\Big(\delta_{jl}B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\frac{v_{i}}{|\boldsymbol{v}|}\Big)_{i,j},\delta_{jl}B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\frac{v_{i}}{|\boldsymbol{v}|}\Big)_{i,j}\Big)_{\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C(d)\int_{Q}\frac{|B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)|^{2}}{(1+|\mathbb{T}^{n}|^{a})^{\frac{1}{a}}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
C(d)
is a positive constant depending only on
d
.
By the choice of
\tau_{k}
, we have
B_{k}(t)=0
if
t\leq k
. If
t\geq k
, we have

(4.3)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} |B_{k}(t)|&=\Big|\int_{k}^{t}\tau^{\prime}_{k}(s)b(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\Big|\\
&\leq\frac{C}{k}\int_{k}^{2k}b(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\leq C(1+k)\\
&\leq C(1+t),\end{aligned}

where
C
is independent of
k
and
t
. Furthermore,
B_{k}
is uniformly bounded on
[0,\infty)
. Using this and (
4.3
), taking the limit in (
4.2
) we get

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{Q}\Big(\Big(\delta_{jl}B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\frac{v_{i}}{|\boldsymbol{v}|}\Big)_{i,j},\delta_{jl}B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\frac{v_{i}}{|\boldsymbol{v}|}\Big)_{i,j}\Big)_{\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}C\int_{Q}\frac{|B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}|)|^{2}}{(1+|\mathbb{T}|^{a})^{\frac{1}{a}}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}C\int_{\{|\mathbb{T}|>k\}}1+|\mathbb{T}|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\quad\quad=0,

where the transition to the final line follows from the fact that
\mathbb{T}\in L^{1}(Q)^{d\times d}
.
In particular, we deduce that

(4.4)

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{Q}\Big|\Big(\Big(\delta_{jl}B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\frac{v_{i}}{|\boldsymbol{v}|}\Big)_{i,j},\chi|\boldsymbol{v}|\partial_{l}\mathbb{T}^{n}\Big)_{\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{T}^{n})}\Big|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=0.

By similar reasoning, we have

(4.5)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{Q}|B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)||F(\mathbb{T}^{n})||\nabla\boldsymbol{v}|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{Q}|B_{k}(|\mathbb{T}|)||F(\mathbb{T})||\nabla\boldsymbol{v}|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}C(\boldsymbol{v})\int_{\{|\mathbb{T}|>k\}}1+|\mathbb{T}|\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=0.

Combining (
4.4
) and (
4.5
), it follows that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{Q}\chi\mathbb{T}^{n}:\nabla\tau_{k}(|\mathbb{T}^{n}|)\otimes\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t=0.

Returning to (
4.1
) and noting that
\chi
is arbitrary, we deduce that

(4.6)

\int_{\Omega}\ddot{\boldsymbol{u}}(t)\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\mathbb{T}(t):\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\,\mathrm{d}x,

for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
and every
\boldsymbol{v}\in C^{1}_{*}(\overline{\Omega})^{d}
. Using Lemma
2.3
, it follows that (
4.6
) holds for every
\boldsymbol{v}\in W^{1,2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d}
such that
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v})\in L^{\infty}_{\#}(\Omega)^{d}
. Hence
(\boldsymbol{u},\mathbb{T})
is a weak solution of the strain-limiting problem (
2.1
) and the proof is complete.
∎

Although we have a full existence result from Theorem
4.1
, under the condition that
a
is small and the dimension is
3
we can improve the convergence result of Theorem
4.1
for
(\mathbb{T}^{n})_{n}
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
\alpha>0
,
a\in(0,\frac{2}{7})
and
d=3
. Assume that
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in W^{1,2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d}
are such that
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in W^{k+1,2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d}
for some
k>\frac{d}{2}
with

\displaystyle\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0})\|_{\infty}\leq C_{*}<1.

Suppose that
\boldsymbol{f}\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d})
. Let
(\boldsymbol{u}^{n},\mathbb{T}^{n})
be the unique weak solution of the regularised problem (
2.5
). Then there exists a couple
(\boldsymbol{u},\mathbb{T})
, the unique weak solution of (
2.1
), such that

\mathbb{T}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}\quad\text{ strongly in }L^{1}(0,T;L^{1}_{\#}(\Omega)^{d\times d}).

Proof.

Under the conditions of the theorem, if
(\boldsymbol{u}^{n,m},\mathbb{T}^{n,m})
denotes the solution to the Galerkin approximation from
V_{m}
, then we have

(4.7)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\Big(\int_{\Omega}|\mathbb{T}^{n,m}(t)|^{1-a}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)+\int_{Q}\frac{|\nabla\mathbb{T}^{n,m}|^{2}}{(1+|\mathbb{T}^{n,m}|)^{a+1}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C,

for every
m\geq m_{0}
, where
C
is independent of
n
and
m
.
We no longer require the indicator function in the first term on the left-hand side because
a<1
.
From now on, we will assume that
m\geq m_{0}
.
Using the Sobolev embedding theorem, for every
p\in(2,6]
we have

(4.8)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\int_{\Omega}|\mathbb{T}^{n,m}|^{\frac{p(1-a)}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{2}{p}}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C(p,a)\Big(\int_{Q}\frac{|\nabla\mathbb{T}^{n,m}|^{2}}{(1+|\mathbb{T}^{n,m}|)^{a+1}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t+1\Big).

We note that we can choose
p
sufficiently large from the interval
(2,6]
so that
p(1-a)/2
is greater than
1
. We may take
p
larger if needed in subsequent calculations, but
p
will remain bounded by
6
.

Let
q\in(1,\infty)
, to be determined later. Let
q^{\prime}=\frac{q}{q-1}
denote the Hölder conjugate of
q
. Using Hölder’s inequality, for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
, we have

(4.9)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\mathbb{T}^{n,m}|^{1+\frac{a}{q^{\prime}}}\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\Big(\int_{\Omega}|\mathbb{T}^{n,m}|^{1-a}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}}\Big(\int_{\Omega}|\mathbb{T}^{n,m}|^{1+2a(q-1)}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}}\Big(\int_{\Omega}|\mathbb{T}^{n,m}|^{1+2a(q-1)}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}},

where the constant
C
comes from the first term on the left-hand side of (
4.7
). To bound the other factor on the right-hand side of (
4.9
), we would like to use (
4.8
). A pair of simultaneous restrictions on
p
and
q
that enable this are

(4.10)

\frac{1}{q}\leq\frac{2}{p},

and

(4.11)

1+2a(q-1)\leq\frac{p(1-a)}{2}.

To show that we may find
p\in(2,6]
and
q>1
such that the above holds when
a\in(0,\frac{2}{7}]
, we first set
p=2q
. Then (
4.10
) automatically holds. We will now demand that
q\in(1,3]
because
p\in(2,6]
.

We write
a=\frac{1}{3}-\delta
for a
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{3})
. Then (
4.11
) reduces to

\frac{1}{3\delta}+2\leq 3q.

If
\delta\geq\frac{1}{21}
, then
\frac{1}{3\delta}+2\leq 9
and
q
can be chosen sufficiently large from the interval
(1,3]
so that

\frac{1}{3\delta}+2\leq 3q\leq 9.

Thus (
4.11
) holds if
\delta\geq\frac{1}{21}
, which is simply equivalent to
a\leq\frac{2}{7}
. Thus we have that

\displaystyle\int_{Q}|\mathbb{T}^{n,m}|^{1+\frac{a}{q^{\prime}}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C^{\frac{1}{q^{\prime}}}\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\int_{\Omega}|\mathbb{T}^{n,m}|^{1+2a(q-1)}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq C\Big[\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\int_{\Omega}|\mathbb{T}^{n,m}|^{\frac{p(1-a)}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{1}{q}}\,\mathrm{d}t+1\Big]

\displaystyle\leq C\Big[\int_{0}^{T}\Big(\int_{\Omega}|\mathbb{T}^{n,m}|^{\frac{p(1-a)}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}x\Big)^{\frac{2}{p}}\,\mathrm{d}t+1\Big]

\displaystyle\leq C(a,\alpha,\boldsymbol{u}_{0},\boldsymbol{v}_{0},\boldsymbol{f},\Omega,T),

where
C
is a positive constant that is independent of
n
and
m
. We note that the choices of
p
and
q
were made independent of
n
and
m
. Fix
\delta=\frac{a}{q^{\prime}}>0
. Then, for a constant
C
that is independent of
n
and
m
, we get

\int_{Q}|\mathbb{T}^{n,m}|^{1+\delta}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C.

Using the convergence results in Theorems
3.1
, we deduce that

\int_{Q}|\mathbb{T}^{n}|^{1+\delta}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t.

It follows that
\mathbb{T}^{n}\rightharpoonup\mathbb{T}
weakly in
L^{1+\delta}(Q)^{d\times d}
. Reasoning as we did in the proof of Theorem
3.1
and noting that
(\frac{\mathbb{T}^{n}}{n(1+|\mathbb{T}^{n}|^{1-\frac{1}{n}})})_{n}
converges to
\mathbf{0}
strongly in
L^{2}(Q)^{d\times d}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, we deduce that
\mathbb{T}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{T}
strongly in
L^{1}(0,T;L^{1}_{\#}(\Omega)^{d\times d})
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Using these two facts, we may repeat the reasoning from the proof of Theorem
3.1
to deduce that
(\boldsymbol{u},\mathbb{T})
is a weak solution of (
2.1
) under these weaker conditions.
∎

Remark
.

We remark that the results here hold under lower regularity requirements on the data. Indeed, rather than
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in W^{k+1,2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d}
for some
k>\frac{d}{2}
, we need only assume that
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in W^{2,2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d}
. However, in this case we need an extra level of approximation.
This is required to deal with the convergence of
(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(P^{m}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0})))_{m}
.
We consider the following regularised problem:

(4.12)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{tt}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}(\mathbb{T})+\boldsymbol{f},

\displaystyle\text{ in }Q,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}_{t}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{T}}{(1+|\mathbb{T}|^{a})^{\frac{1}{a}}}+\frac{\mathbb{T}}{n}

\displaystyle\text{ in }Q,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{k},

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}_{t}(0,\cdot)

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{v}_{0}^{k},

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega.

The functions
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{k}
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}^{k}
are chosen so that they are elements of
C^{\infty}_{*}(\overline{\Omega})^{d}
such that if
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
,
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}\in L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d}
with
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in W^{2,2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d}\cap W^{1,\infty}_{*}(\Omega)^{d}
, then

•

\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{k}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
strongly in
L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d}
;

•

\boldsymbol{v}_{0}^{k}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{v}_{0}
strongly in
L^{2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d}
;

•

\boldsymbol{v}_{0}^{k}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{k}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
strongly in
W^{2,2}_{*}(\Omega)^{d}
;

•

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0}^{k}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{k})\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0})
weakly-* in
L^{\infty}(\Omega)^{d\times d}
.

We deduce that such an approximating sequence exists by applying the proof of Lemma 4.1 in
[
5
]
to
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}
. We deduce the existence of a weak solution to (
4.12
) by using a finite-dimensional approximation as in Theorem
3.1
. Then we take the limit as
k\rightarrow\infty
for each fixed
n
. Rather than considering
f^{-1}(C_{1})
as we did in the proof of Theorem
3.1
, we use
f_{n}^{-1}(\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0}^{k}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{k})\|_{\infty})
in the bounds on
(\boldsymbol{u}^{n,k},\mathbb{T}^{n,k})
. This is uniformly bounded in

, so we may deduce appropriate convergence results for each fixed

. However, since we only have weak-* convergence of
(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{v}_{0}^{k}+\alpha\boldsymbol{u}_{0}^{k}))_{k}
, we must obtain bounds of the type (
3.15
) and (
3.21
) for
(\boldsymbol{u}^{n},\mathbb{T}^{n})
from scratch. Since we have chosen the regularisation

, we have the following bound:

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\Big(|\dot{\mathbb{T}}^{n,k}|^{2}+|\nabla\mathbb{T}^{n,k}|^{2}+|\mathbb{T}^{n,k}|^{2}\Big)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\leq C(n),

where
C
is a constant that is independent of

and
(\boldsymbol{u}^{n,k},\mathbb{T}^{n,k})
is the weak solution of (
4.12
). Thus if
(\boldsymbol{u}^{n},\mathbb{T}^{n})
is the solution of (

) but with

,
\boldsymbol{v}_{0}^{k}
replaced by

. Since we are no longer using approximate initial data, we may use

5.
Conclusion and open problems

We have proved the existence of a unique global-in-time large-data weak solution to a class of initial-boundary-value problems that model the motion of implicitly constituted strain-limiting viscoelastic solids, without any restrictions on the parameters in the model or the number of space dimensions. Related studies have only been pursued to date in the quasi-static case or in one space dimension. The results presented here complement those for elastic solids in the static case discussed in
[
5
]
.
Admittedly, our focus on the periodic setting here is a notable simplification. The extension of the results presented in this paper to problems on general domains, with Dirichlet and mixed Dirichlet–Neumann boundary conditions will be reported in a forthcoming paper.

An interesting further challenge is the proof of the existence of weak solutions to the purely elastic unsteady strain-limiting model. Although the analysis presented in this paper suggests that such an existence result might be within reach, it has to be borne in mind that the viscoelastic term has a regularising effect, whose absence in the purely elastic case is likely to lead to additional technical complications.

Finally, a related class of problems that has been discussed in the literature are stress-rate type models. For these the constitutive relation is of the form

(5.1)

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u})=F(\mathbb{T})-\gamma\mathbb{T}_{t},

where
F
is an appropriately chosen function and
\gamma>0
. In the case when
F
is bounded, it would be interesting to investigate the existence and uniqueness of solutions to (
2.1
), where the constitutive relation is now replaced by (
5.1
). Rudimentary comparisons of solutions to the stress-rate problem and the strain-rate problem are performed in
[
10
]
, but results concerning the existence of global weak solutions to multidimensional stress-rate type models are still lacking.
