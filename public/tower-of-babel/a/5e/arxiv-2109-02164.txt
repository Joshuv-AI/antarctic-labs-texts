Estimation of cluster functionals for regularly varying time series: runs estimators
Youssouph Cissokho, Rafał Kulik

Abstract
Cluster indices describe extremal behaviour of stationary time series. We consider runs estimators of cluster indices. Using a modern theory of multivariate, regularly varying time series, we obtain central limit theorems under conditions that can be easily verified for a large class of models. In particular, we show that blocks and runs estimators have the same limiting variance.

Introduction

Consider
a stationary, regularly varying
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued time series
\boldsymbol{X}=\{\boldsymbol{X}_{j},j\in\mathbb{Z}\}
.
We are interested in its extremal behaviour. A classical approach to this problem is to calculate the
extremal index
. If
|\cdot|
is an arbitrary norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then the extremal index
\theta
(if exists) of
\{|\boldsymbol{X}_{j}|,j\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is defined as a parameter in the limiting distribution of the maxima. With
Q
being the quantile function of
|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}|
and
a_{n}=Q(1-1/n)
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(a_{n}^{-1}\max_{j=1,\ldots,n}\{|\boldsymbol{X}_{1}|,\ldots,|\boldsymbol{X}_{n}|\}\leq x)=\exp(-\theta x^{-\alpha})\;,\ \ x>0\;.

The parameter
\theta\in(0,1]
indicates the amount of clustering, with
\theta=1
(the case of extremal independence) meaning no-clustering of large values.

The extremal index is just one parameter that describes clustering of extremes.
Informally speaking, it arises as the limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[H((\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{r_{n}})/u_{n})]}{r_{n}\mathbb{P}(\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>u_{n})}\;,

for the particular choice of function
H:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{R}
, and a suitable choice of the scaling sequence
u_{n}\to\infty
and the block size
r_{n}\to\infty
. (Formally speaking,
(\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{r_{n}})
is a random element of
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{r_{n}}
, while the domain of
H
is
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}
. This inconsistency will be explained later).

In particular, the extremal index is achieved by applying a suitable functional to a cluster:

\displaystyle H((\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{r_{n}})/u_{n})=\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\max\{\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\right|,\ldots,\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{r_{n}}\right|\}>u_{n}\right\}}\;.

That is,

\displaystyle\theta=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}(\max\{\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\right|,\ldots,\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{r_{n}}\right|\}>u_{n})}{r_{n}\mathbb{P}(\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>u_{n})}\;.

(1.1)

Informally speaking, a cluster is a triangular array
(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}/u_{n},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{r_{n}}/u_{n})
with
r_{n},u_{n}\to\infty
that converges in distribution in a certain sense. Cluster indices are obtained by applying the appropriate functional
H
to the cluster.
The functionals are defined on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}
, the space of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued sequences, and are such that their values do not depend on coordinates that are equal to zero.
More precisely, for
\boldsymbol{X}=\{\boldsymbol{X}_{j},j\in\mathbb{Z}\}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
i\leq j\in\mathbb{Z}
, we denote
\boldsymbol{X}_{i,j}=(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{j})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{(j-i+1)}
.
Then, we identify
H(\boldsymbol{X}_{i,j})
with

H(({\boldsymbol{0}},\boldsymbol{X}_{i,j},{\boldsymbol{0}}))
, where
{\boldsymbol{0}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}
is the zero sequence. Such functionals
H
will be called
cluster functionals
.

Let
\left|\cdot\right|
be an arbitrary norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\{u_{n}\}
,
\{r_{n}\}
be such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}u_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}r_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}n\mathbb{P}(\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>u_{n})=\infty\;,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}r_{n}/n=\lim_{n\to\infty}r_{n}\mathbb{P}(\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>u_{n})=0\;.

(
\mathcal{R}(r_{n},u_{n})
)

Given a cluster functional
H
on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}
, we want to estimate the limiting
quantity

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{*}(H)=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{*}_{n,r_{n}}(H)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[H(\boldsymbol{X}_{1,r_{n}}/u_{n})]}{r_{n}\mathbb{P}(\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>u_{n})}\;.

(1.2)

To guarantee existence of the limit we will require additional anticlustering assumptions on the time series
\{\boldsymbol{X}_{j},j\in\mathbb{Z}\}
. For
\boldsymbol{x}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{j},j\in\mathbb{Z}\}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}
define
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}|
. The cluster indices of interest are, among others:

•

the extremal index obtained with
H_{1}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\boldsymbol{x}^{*}>1\right\}}
,
\boldsymbol{x}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{j},j\in\mathbb{Z}\}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}
;

•

the cluster size distribution obtained with

\displaystyle H_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right|>1\right\}}=m\right\}}\;,\ \ \boldsymbol{x}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{j},j\in\mathbb{Z}\}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}\;,\ \ m\in\mathbb{N}\;;

(1.3)

•

the stop-loss index of a univariate time series obtained with

\displaystyle H_{3}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}(x_{j}-1)_{+}>\eta\right\}}\;,\ \ \boldsymbol{x}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{j},j\in\mathbb{Z}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}\;,\ \ \eta>0\;;

(1.4)

•

the large deviation index of a univariate time series obtained with

\displaystyle H_{4}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbbm{1}{\left\{K(\boldsymbol{x})>1\right\}}\;,\ \ K(\boldsymbol{x})=\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}x_{j}\right)_{+}\;,\ \ \boldsymbol{x}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{j},j\in\mathbb{Z}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}\;;

(1.5)

•

the ruin index of a univariate time series obtained with

\displaystyle H_{5}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbbm{1}{\left\{K(\boldsymbol{x})>1\right\}}\;,\ \ K(\boldsymbol{x})=\sup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{j\leq i}x_{j}\right)_{+}\;,\ \ \boldsymbol{x}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{j},j\in\mathbb{Z}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}\;.

(1.6)

As indicated above, the extremal index is the classical quantity that arises in the extreme value theory for dependent sequences. Similarly, the cluster size distribution has been studied in
[
Hsi91
]
and
[
DR10
]
.
The large deviation index was studied under the name
cluster index
in
[
MW13
,
MW14
]
. It quantifies the effect of dependence in large deviations results.

Several methods of estimation of the limit
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{*}(H)
in (
1.2
) may be
employed. The natural one is to consider a
statistics based on disjoint blocks of size
r_{n}
, cf.
[
DR10
]
and
[
KS20
]
,

\displaystyle{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\nu}}}^{*}_{n,r_{n}}(H):=\frac{1}{n\mathbb{P}(\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>u_{n})}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{n}}H(\boldsymbol{X}_{(i-1)r_{n}+1,ir_{n}}/u_{n})\;,

where
m_{n}=[n/r_{n}]
is the number of disjoint blocks.
The data-based estimator is constructed as follows. Let
k_{n}\to\infty
be a sequence of integers and define
u_{n}
by
k_{n}=n\mathbb{P}(\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>u_{n})
. Let
{\left|\boldsymbol{X}\right|}_{(n:1)}\leq\cdots\leq{\left|\boldsymbol{X}\right|}_{(n:n)}
be order statistics from
\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\right|,\ldots,\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{n}\right|
.
Define

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{*}_{n,r_{n}}(H)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{k_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{m_{n}}H(\boldsymbol{X}_{(i-1)r_{n}+1,ir_{n}}/{\left|\boldsymbol{X}\right|}_{(n:n-k_{n})})\;.

(1.7)

The general asymptotic theory for disjoint blocks estimators was developed in
[
DR10
]
. See also
[
KS20
, Chapter 10]
. The limiting variance of the disjoint blocks estimator can be represented as

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{*}

\displaystyle(\{H-{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{*}(H)\mathcal{E}\}^{2})\;,

(1.8)

where
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right|>1\right\}}
. This result was established (implicitly) in
[
DR10
]
, but the form of the limiting variance is again given in
[
KS20
, Chapter 10]
.

Another approach to estimation of
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{*}(H)
is to consider the sliding blocks statistics

\displaystyle{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}^{*}_{n,r_{n}}(H):=\frac{1}{q_{n}r_{n}\mathbb{P}(\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>u_{n})}\sum_{i=0}^{q_{n}-1}H\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{i+1,i+r_{n}}/u_{n}\right)\;

(1.9)

and
and the corresponding estimator defined in terms of order statistics:

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{*}_{n,r_{n}}(H)=\frac{1}{r_{n}k_{n}}\sum_{i=0}^{q_{n}-1}H\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{i+1,i+r_{n}}/{\left|\boldsymbol{X}\right|}_{(n:n-k_{n})}\right)\;.

(1.10)

Here,
q_{n}=n-r_{n}-1
is the number of sliding blocks.
In
[
DN20
]
the authors used the framework of
[
DR10
]
and showed that the limiting variance of the sliding blocks estimator never exceeds that of the disjoint blocks estimator. In case of the extremal index, both variances were proven to be equal. In
[
CK21
]
it was shown that the limiting variances for both disjoint and sliding blocks estimators agree and are given by the expression in (
1.8
) for an arbitrary choice of
H
. We note at this point that the methodology used in
[
DR10
,
DN20
,
KS20
,
CK21
]
fits into Peak Over Threshold (PoT) framework. On the other hand, in the Block Maxima (BM) framework, sliding blocks estimators yield typically smaller variance; see
[
BS18b
,
BS18a
]
. As of this moment, there is no thorough explanation of these phenomena and no formal comparison between PoT and BM framework. See
[
FdH15
]
for some partial results and
[
BZ18
]
for a recent review.

In the present paper we are interested in the so-called
runs estimators
. In the context of the extremal index, this approach goes back to
[
WN98
]
and stems from the following representation of the extremal index:

\displaystyle\theta=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\max\{\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{1}\right|,\ldots,\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{r_{n}}\right|\}\leq u_{n}\mid\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>u_{n})\;.

(1.11)

We note that

\displaystyle\theta=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>u_{n})}\mathbb{E}[\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\mathcal{A}(({\boldsymbol{X}_{0}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{r_{n}})/u_{n})=0\right\}}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>u_{n}\right\}}]\;,

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sup\{j:\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right|>1\}\;

gives the position of the last exceedence above 1 in a particular block.
Recall again the convention

\mathcal{A}(({\boldsymbol{X}_{0}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{r_{n}})/u_{n})=\mathcal{A}(({\boldsymbol{0}},{\boldsymbol{X}_{0}},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{r_{n}})/u_{n})
. Then,
\mathcal{A}
is an example of so-called
anchoring map
. Special cases of anchoring maps were considered in
[
Has18
]
and
[
BP18
]
, while in
[
KS20
]
their connection to cluster indices
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{*}(H)
was thoroughly investigated.
It turns out that with an arbitrary choice of the anchoring map
\mathcal{A}
we have

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{*}(H)=\mathbb{E}[H^{\mathcal{A}}(\boldsymbol{Y})]\;,

where

\displaystyle H^{\mathcal{A}}(\boldsymbol{x})=H(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x})=0\right\}}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|>1\right\}}\;.

This motivates the following runs statistics:

\displaystyle{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{*}_{n,r_{n}}(H^{\mathcal{A}})=\frac{1}{n\mathbb{P}(\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>u_{n})}\sum_{i=r_{n}+1}^{n-r_{n}}H^{\mathcal{A}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{i-r_{n},i+r_{n}}/u_{n}\right)\;.

(1.12)

Indeed,
under the appropriate conditions,

Proposition

2.7
gives

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{*}_{n,r_{n}}(H^{\mathcal{A}})]=\mathbb{E}[H^{\mathcal{A}}(\boldsymbol{Y})]={\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{*}(H)\;.

The data-based
runs estimator is then

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}_{n,r_{n}}(H^{\mathcal{A}})=\frac{1}{k_{n}}\sum_{i=r_{n}+1}^{n-r_{n}}H^{\mathcal{A}}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{i-r_{n},i+r_{n}}/{\left|\boldsymbol{X}\right|}_{(n:n-k)}\right)\;.

The main result of this paper is
Theorem

3.5
, the asymptotic normality of the appropriately normalized estimator

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{*}_{n,r_{n}}(H^{\mathcal{A}})
. We show, in particular, that the limiting variance agrees with the one for the disjoint blocks and sliding blocks estimators; cf.
[
DR10
]
,
[
KS20
, Chapter 10]
,
[
CK21
]
.
Furthermore, we prove that we cannot achieve variance reduction by considering a linear combination of runs estimators with a different choice of anchoring maps
\mathcal{A}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}
. Indeed, it turns out that
{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{*}_{n,r_{n}}(H^{\mathcal{A}})
and
{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\xi}}}^{*}_{n,r_{n}}(H^{\tilde{\mathcal{A}}})
are totally dependent in the limit.
We note in passing that even though general ideas of proofs are similar to those of
[
CK21
]
, however, technicalities are significantly different. Differences stem primarily from conditioning on
\{\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{j}\right|>u_{n}\}
used in case of the runs estimators.

Thus, from the theoretical point of view the limiting behaviour of all (disjoint blocks, sliding blocks, runs) estimators is the same. However, for finite samples a bias has to be taken into account. We note first that the theoretical finite-sample bias for both disjoint and sliding blocks estimators is the same. This can be also seen in extensive simulation studies in
[
CK21
]
. On the other hand, we were not able to get an useful formula for the bias in the runs estimator case. As such we relied on simulations. It turns out that runs estimator are typically heavily biased when estimation of the extremal index is concerned. However, the runs estimators may have an advantage when other cluster indices are considered.

2.1
Notation

Let
|\cdot|
be a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For a sequence
\boldsymbol{x}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{j},j\in\mathbb{Z}\}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}
and
i\leq j\in\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
we denote
\boldsymbol{x}_{i,j}=(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{j-i+1}
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{i,j}^{*}=\max_{i\leq l\leq j}|\boldsymbol{x}_{l}|
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}|
. By
{\boldsymbol{0}}
we denote the zero sequence; its dimension can be different in each of its occurrences.

By
\ell_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
we denote the set of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued sequences which tend to zero at infinity. Likewise,
\ell_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

consists of sequences such that
\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}|<\infty
.

2.2
Classes of functions

Functionals
H
are defined on
\ell_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with the convention
H(\boldsymbol{x}_{i,j})=H(({\boldsymbol{0}},\boldsymbol{x}_{i,j},{\boldsymbol{0}}))
. For
s>0
, the function
H_{s}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by
H_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})=H(\boldsymbol{x}/s)
.
We consider the following classes:

•

\mathcal{L}
is the class of bounded real-valued functions defined on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}
that are either
Lipschitz continuous with respect to the uniform norm or almost
surely continuous with respect to the distribution of the tail process
\boldsymbol{Y}
.
This class includes functions like
\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\boldsymbol{x}^{*}>1\right\}}
,
\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}|>1\right\}}
.
See Remark 6.1.6 in
[
KS20
]
.

•

\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{L}
is the class of shift-invariant functionals with support separated
from
{\boldsymbol{0}}
. In particular, for
H\in\mathcal{A}
,
H({\boldsymbol{0}})=0
. The class
\mathcal{A}
includes
\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\boldsymbol{x}^{*}>1\right\}}
.

•

\mathcal{K}
is the class of shift-invariant functionals
K:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{R}
defined on

\ell_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that
K({\boldsymbol{0}})=0
and which are
Lipschitz continuous with constant
L_{K}
, i.e.

\displaystyle|K(\boldsymbol{x})-K(\boldsymbol{y})|\leq L_{K}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\right|\;,\ \ \boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\ell_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\;.

•

\mathcal{B}\subset\mathcal{L}
is the class of functionals
H
of the form
H=\mathbbm{1}{\left\{K>1\right\}}
,
where
K\in\mathcal{K}
.
Functionals in
\mathcal{B}
may have support which is not
separated from
{\boldsymbol{0}}
. The typical example is
H(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\sum_{j}|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}|>1\right\}}
; note that
H\not\in\mathcal{A}
.

We will also need
the map
\mathcal{E}
is defined on
\ell_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by

\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right|>1\right\}}
. Note that
\mathcal{E}
is shift-invariant, with the support separated from zero, but is not bounded.

2.3
Tail and spectral tail process

Let
\boldsymbol{X}=\{\boldsymbol{X}_{j},j\in\mathbb{Z}\}
be a stationary, regularly varying time series with values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and tail index
\alpha
. In particular,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}(|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}|>tx)}{\mathbb{P}(|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}|>x)}=t^{-\alpha}

for all
t>0
.
Then, there exists a sequence
\boldsymbol{Y}=\{\boldsymbol{Y}_{j},j\in\mathbb{Z}\}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(x^{-1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},\dots,\boldsymbol{X}_{j})\in\cdot\mid|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}|>x)\mbox{ converges weakly to }\mathbb{P}((\boldsymbol{Y}_{i},\dots,\boldsymbol{Y}_{j})\in\cdot)

as
x\to\infty
for all
i\leq j\in\mathbb{Z}
.
We call
\boldsymbol{Y}
the tail process.
See
[
BS09
]
.
We note that, in particular,
|\boldsymbol{Y}_{0}|
has Pareto distribution with the density
\alpha x^{-\alpha-1}
,
x>1
. As such, it follows automatically that
\boldsymbol{Y}^{*}=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}|\boldsymbol{Y}_{j}|>1
.
Equivalently, viewing
\boldsymbol{X}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
as random elements with values in
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}
, we have for every bounded or
non-negative functional
H
on
(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}
, continuous with respect to the product topology,

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[H(x^{-1}\boldsymbol{X})\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>x\right\}}]}{\mathbb{P}(\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>x)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[H(\boldsymbol{Y})]\;.

The spectral tail process
\{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{j},j\in\mathbb{Z}\}
is defined by
\boldsymbol{\Theta}=\left|\boldsymbol{Y}_{0}\right|^{-1}\boldsymbol{Y}
and is independent of the tail process
\boldsymbol{Y}
.

2.4
Anchoring maps

Definition 2.1

(Anchoring map)
.

A measurable map
\mathcal{A}:(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
is called an anchoring map if the following two properties hold:

An(i):

\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x})=j
implies
\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right|\geq\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|\wedge 1
;

An(ii):

\mathcal{A}(B\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x})+1
, where
B
is a backsift operator.

Three basic examples of anchoring maps are:

•

The infargmax functional:

\mathcal{A}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{y})=\inf\{j:\boldsymbol{y}_{-\infty,{j}}^{*}=\boldsymbol{y}^{*}\}
;

•

The first exceedence above one:
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{x})=\inf\{j:\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right|>1\}
;

•

The last exceedence above one:
\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sup\{j:\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right|>1\}
.

In what follows we use the convention
\inf\emptyset=+\infty
. We note that
\mathcal{A}^{(0)}
is
0
-homogeneous, while
\mathcal{A}_{s}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{A}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{x}/s)
and
\mathcal{A}_{s}^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{x}/s)
are increasing and decreasing in
s
, respectively, but they are not
0
-homogeneous. This will play a role in the proofs.

A special importance is given to the time index 0. In particular,

•

If
\mathcal{A}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
, then
\boldsymbol{x}_{-\infty,-1}^{*}<\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{1,\infty}^{*}\leq\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|
;

•

If
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
, then
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{-\infty,-1}\leq 1
and
\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|>1
;

•

If
\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
, then
\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{1,\infty}\leq 1
and
\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|>1
.

Applying an anchoring map to a finite block, say
\boldsymbol{x}_{-r,r}
with
r\in\mathbb{N}
, is equivalent to applying it
\boldsymbol{x}=({\boldsymbol{0}},\boldsymbol{x}_{-r,r},{\boldsymbol{0}})
. For example,
\mathcal{A}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{-r,r})=0
means that
\boldsymbol{x}_{-r,-1}^{*}<\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{1,r}^{*}\leq\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|
. This in turn implies also that
\mathcal{A}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{-s,s})=0
for
0<s<r
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{-r,r})=0\Rightarrow\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{-s,s})=0\;,\ \ 0<s<r

for
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{A}^{(1)},\mathcal{A}^{(2)}
. However, we do not know if this important property (used explicitly in the proofs) holds for any anchoring map. As such, in the paper we focus on the three anchoring maps introduced above.

Furthermore, note that An(ii) gives that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h-r,h+r})=h\Leftrightarrow\mathcal{A}(B^{-h}\boldsymbol{x}_{-r,r})=0\;.

(2.1)

Indeed, consider for example
\mathcal{A}^{(0)}
. Then
\mathcal{A}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h-r,h+r})=h
means that

\boldsymbol{x}^{*}_{h-r,{h}-1}<\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{h}\right|
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{h+1,h+r}\leq\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{h}\right|
. Set
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}=B^{-h}\boldsymbol{x}
, so that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{-r}=\boldsymbol{x}_{h-r}
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}=\boldsymbol{x}_{h}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{r}=\boldsymbol{x}_{h+r}
. Thus,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{-r,-1}<\left|\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\right|
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1,r}\leq\left|\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\right|
. This in turn means that

\mathcal{A}^{(0)}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{x}}_{-r,r})=\mathcal{A}^{(0)}(B^{-h}\boldsymbol{x}_{-r,r})=0
.

2.5
Cluster measure and cluster indices

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an anchoring map.
If

\displaystyle{\vartheta}=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{Y})=0)\;.

(2.2)

We want to emphasize that

Therefore,

Recall that
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\right|>1\right\}}

The property An(i) of the anchoring maps implies

\displaystyle\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{Y})=h)\leq\sum_{h\in\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{P}(\left|\boldsymbol{Y}_{h}\right|>1)\;.

(2.3)

By

the latter series is finite if an appropriate anticlustering condition holds (see

(

Definition 2.2

3
Central limit theorem for runs estimators

In this section we introduce and study runs estimators of cluster indices. A pseudo-estimator is defined in (
3.4
). Its limiting covariance (for different anchoring maps) is studied in
Lemma

3.2
. In particular, for two different anchoring maps, the runs statistics are totally dependent. As a consequence we cannot reduce the limiting variance for the estimation of
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}^{*}(H)
by considering linear combinations of the runs statistics.
In

3.5

The limiting variance agrees with the one for the disjoint blocks and sliding blocks estimators.

.

Assume that

\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\mathcal{A}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{h-r_{n},h+r_{n}}/u_{n}\right)=h\right\}}

\mathcal{I}(h,t)
are omitted);

\displaystyle W_{12}:=\pm\|H\|\mathbb{E}\left[\Big(\mathcal{I}(0,s)-\mathcal{I}(0,t)\Big)\left\{H_{s}-H_{t}\right\}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{h-r_{n},h+r_{n}}/u_{n}\right)\right]\;,

(both indicators of the anchoring maps and
\mathcal{I}(h,t)

(5.43)

\displaystyle W_{22}\leq\pm 2\|H\|\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\mathcal{A}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{-r_{n},r_{n}}/u_{n}\right)=0\right\}}\mathcal{I}(0,t)\left\{H_{s}-H_{t}\right\}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{-r_{n},r_{n}}/u_{n}\right)\right]\;,

(5.44)

\displaystyle W_{12}+W_{21}\leq 4\|H\|^{2}\left(\mathbb{P}(\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>u_{n}s)-\mathbb{P}(\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>u_{n}t)\right)\;.

(5.45)

\displaystyle\pm 2\|H\|\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\mathcal{A}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{-r_{n},r_{n}}/u_{n}\right)=0\right\}}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>s_{0}u_{n}\right\}}\left\{H_{s}-H_{t}\right\}\left(\boldsymbol{X}_{-r_{n},r_{n}}/u_{n}\right)\right]}{\mathbb{P}(\left|\boldsymbol{X}_{0}\right|>u_{n})}\;,

5.8
Random entropy

In this section we discuss validity of
3.4
. We cannot check this condition for arbitrary functionals
H
and anchoring maps
\mathcal{A}
, however, we will see that the conditions is satisfied for most relevant cases considered in the paper.

Recall the class

\mathcal{G}=\{H^{\mathcal{A}}_{s},s\in[s_{0},t_{0}]\}=\{H(\boldsymbol{x}/s)\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}/s)=0\right\}}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|>s\right\}},\;\;s\in[s_{0},t_{0}]\}.

We start first with
H
of the form

\displaystyle H_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbbm{1}{\left\{K(\boldsymbol{x})>s\right\}}\;,

(5.55)

where

K:\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{R}
. This is the case of the functionals that lead to the extremal index, the large deviation index and the ruin index.

Since
\mathcal{A}^{(0)}
is 0-homogeneous, it does not play a role in calculating the class entropy. Then

H_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|>s\right\}}=\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\min\{K(\boldsymbol{x}),\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|\}>s\right\}}\;.

Hence, the map
s\to H_{s}(\boldsymbol{x})\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|>s\right\}}
is decreasing. Therefore, VC(
\mathcal{G})=2
.

As for
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\mathcal{A}^{(1)}(\boldsymbol{x}/s)=0\right\}}=\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{-\infty,-1}^{*}\leq s,\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|>s\right\}}\;.

Thus,

\displaystyle H(\boldsymbol{x}/s)\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}/s)=0\right\}}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|>s\right\}}=\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\min\{K(\boldsymbol{x}),\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|\}>s\right\}}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{-\infty,-1}^{*}\leq s\right\}}\;.

Now, the class

\mathcal{F}=\{(-\infty,s)\times(s,+\infty):s\in\mathbb{R}\}

has the VC-index
3
. By
[
KS20
, Example C.4.14]
the class
\mathcal{G}
has VC-index at most 3.

Similarly, for
\mathcal{A}^{(2)}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\mathcal{A}^{(2)}(\boldsymbol{x}/s)=0\right\}}=\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1,\infty}^{*}\leq s,\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|>s\right\}}\;.

Thus,

\displaystyle H(\boldsymbol{x}/s)\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\mathcal{A}(\boldsymbol{x}/s)=0\right\}}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|>s\right\}}=\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\min\{K(\boldsymbol{x}),\left|\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\right|\}>s\right\}}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1,\infty}^{*}\leq s\right\}}\;

and again
the class
\mathcal{G}
has VC-index at most 3.

In summary, for functionals
H
given in (
5.55
) and the anchoring maps
\mathcal{A}^{(0)}
,
\mathcal{A}^{(1)}
,
\mathcal{A}^{(2)}
the class
\mathcal{G}
has the VC-index at most 3 and hence the random entropy
3.4
is satisfied.

is

is also decreasing. Thus, the VC-index of
\mathcal{G}
is at most 2. The random entropy condition is satisfied.
