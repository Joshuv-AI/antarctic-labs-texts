Finite-frequency prethermalization in periodically driven ergodic systems
Lorenzo Correale, Leticia F. Cugliandolo, Marco Schirò, Alessandro Silva

Abstract
We investigate the periodically driven dynamics of many-body systems, either classical or quantum, finite-dimensional or mean-field, displaying an unbounded phase-space. We find that the inclusion of a smooth periodic drive atop an otherwise ergodic dynamics leads to a long-lived prethermalization, even at moderate driving frequencies. In specific asymptotic limits, we compute the corresponding prethermal Hamiltonian from an analytical perturbation scheme.



Introduction
—
Periodically-driven systems
are known to host a great deal of non-trivial phenomena.
For example, an inverted pendulum can be stabilized by an external force oscillating at sufficiently high frequency
Kapitza 1951
, while a kicked-rotor exhibits an integrability-to-chaos transition
Chirikov 1971
that is counter-intuitively suppressed by the inclusion of quantum fluctuations
Fishman et al. 1982
. Similarly, a driven quantum single particle system may localize in energy space resulting in bounded heating
Gefen and Thouless 1987
.
While single-particle systems have been the focus of research in the past decades, attention has recently shifted towards many-particle ones, which have proven to be valuable tools to reproduce experimentally a wide array of novel phases of matter , within the field of
Floquet engineering

Oka and Kitamura 2019
.
However, a major obstacle to their practical realization has been the fact that periodically driven isolated many-body systems tend to quickly heat up to a featureless infinite-temperature state, due to the absence of conservation laws
D’Alessio and Rigol 2014
;
Lazarides et al. 2014
;
Ponte et al. 2015
;
Genske and Rosch 2015
.

Infinite-temperature thermalization can be avoided by many-body localization
Khemani et al. 2016
;
Else et al. 2016
;
Yao et al. 2017
and integrability
Russomanno et al. 2012
;
Citro et al. 2015
;
Gritsev and Polkovnikov 2017
;
Ishii et al. 2018
;
Prosen 1998a
;
Prosen 1998b
;
Prosen 1999
.
For generic short-range interacting systems, heating can be suppressed only in a finite time window.
For example, a prethermalization induced by a high-frequency drive
\Omega
has been observed in numerical studies both in classical
Howell et al. 2019
;
Pizzi et al. 2021
;
Sadia et al. 2022
and quantum systems
Kuwahara et al. 2016
;
Mori et al. 2016
;
Abanin et al. 2017a
;
Abanin et al. 2017b
;
Peronaci et al. 2018
;
Peronaci et al. 2020
;
Ho et al. 2023
;
Torre and Dentelski 2021
, as well as in some experimental setups
Neyenhuis et al. 2017
;
Rubio-Abadal et al. 2020
;
Peng et al. 2021
;
Shkedrov et al. 2022
. In this case, the corresponding prethermal Hamiltonian is asymptotically close to the average over a period,
\overline{H(t)}
, of the true periodic Hamiltonian
H(t)
. While most literature has concentrated on systems with a locally bounded energy spectrum, high-frequency prethermalization has been conjectured as a generic outcome also for unbounded spectra
Hodson and Jarzynski 2021
, as supported by numerical studies on periodically kicked systems
Rajak et al. 2018
;
Rajak et al. 2019
;
Sadia et al. 2022

Shkedrov et al. 2022

From a broader perspective, prethermalisation is also observed close to an integrable point, both in quenched systems
Mori et al. 2018
as well as under periodic drive
Weidinger and Knap 2017
.
Beyond these scenarios, the conventional expectation is that a non-integrable system with intermediate-coupling interactions and drive frequency should rapidly thermalize. In this work, we show that this expectation is not met when a system with an unbounded energy spectrum is driven under a smooth periodic force.

We investigate two systems, namely the 1D lattice

Discussion and conclusion
— In conclusion, our investigation of the dynamics of ergodic systems, with unbounded phase space and driven by a smooth periodic force, revealed clear signatures of prethermalization to a Gibbs-like state.
This is indicated either by a saturation of the energy density to a finite value or by the emergence of an approximate fluctuation-dissipation relation. For the
\phi^{4}
model, we also proposed a perturbative scheme which allowed us
to determine the corresponding thermal asymptotic state.
Differently from previous studies, prethermalization occurs at all frequencies and the corresponding effective Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}_{F}
is generically distinct from the time-averaged one,
\overline{H(t)}
.
A deeper understanding of the connection between
{\mathcal{H}}_{F}
and
\overline{H(t)}
could be crucial for advancing the experimental realization of long-lived many-body Hamiltonians even at moderate frequencies.
Joanes and Gill 1998

Appendix C
2. Detailed calculations on multiple-scale analysis

In this section, we aim to provide a review on the multiple-scale analysis (MSA) that we employed in the Letter. We will focus again on the Hamilton dynamics of the classical lattice
\phi^{4}
model, governed by the equations

\ddot{x}_{i}+\omega_{0}^{2}x_{i}+\lambda x_{i}^{3}=B_{0}\sin(\Omega t)+g\ (x_{i+1}-2x_{i}+x_{i-1})\ ,

(19)

for
i=1\ldots N
. We rescale the model parameters as
B_{0}=\epsilon\tilde{B}_{0}
,
\lambda=\epsilon\tilde{\lambda}
and
g=\epsilon\tilde{g}
. We also rescale the harmonic coupling as
\omega_{0}^{2}=\Omega^{2}-\epsilon\Delta\omega^{2}
. We will work in the limit of small
\epsilon
, while keeping all other parameters of order one.
As outlined in the Letter, the starting point of the MSA consists in introducing a redundant time variable
\tau=\epsilon t
.
\tau
defines a long time scale, since it becomes significant only when

t
is of order
\epsilon^{-1}
or larger. Although we expect that the actual solution
\mathbf{x}(t)
to Eq. (
19
) is a function of
t
alone, MSA seeks solutions which are functions of
both variables
t
and
\tau
, treated as independent from each other. Such expression of
\mathbf{x}(t)
as a function of two variables is an artifice to remove secular effects; the
actual solution has
t
and
\tau
related by
\tau=\epsilon t
. The formal procedure consists in assuming a perturbative expansion in powers of
\epsilon
:

x_{i}(t)=x_{0,i}(t,\tau)+\epsilon\ x_{1,i}(t,\tau)+\dots\ ,

(20)

for small
\epsilon
.
We use the chain rules
d/dt=\partial_{t}+\epsilon\partial_{\tau}
for partial differentiation to compute the derivatives of the variables
x_{i}(t)
, obtaining

\frac{dx_{i}}{dt}=\frac{\partial x_{0,i}}{\partial t}+\epsilon\Big(\frac{\partial x_{1,i}}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial x_{0,i}}{\partial\tau}\Big)+O(\epsilon^{2})

(21)

and

\frac{d^{2}x_{i}}{dt^{2}}=\frac{\partial^{2}x_{0,i}}{\partial t^{2}}+\epsilon\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}x_{1,i}}{\partial t^{2}}+2\ \frac{\partial^{2}x_{0,i}}{\partial t\ \partial\tau}\Big)+O(\epsilon^{2})\ .

(22)

By substituting Eq. (
22
) in Eq. (
19
) and collecting powers of
\epsilon
, we deduce

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}x_{0,i}}{\partial t^{2}}+\Omega^{2}x_{0,i}=0\ ,

(23)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}x_{1,i}}{\partial t^{2}}+\Omega^{2}x_{1,i}=-2\frac{\partial^{2}x_{0,i}}{\partial t\ \partial\tau}+\Delta\omega^{2}x_{0,i}+\tilde{g}(x_{0,i+1}-2x_{0,i}+x_{0,i-1})-\tilde{\lambda}x_{0,i}^{3}+\tilde{B}_{0}\sin(\Omega t)\ .

(24)

The general solution to Eq. (
23
) is

x_{0,i}(t,\tau)=A_{i}(\tau)e^{i\Omega t}+A_{i}^{*}(\tau)e^{-i\Omega t}\ .

(25)

By plugging this expression into Eq. (
24
), we derive

\frac{\partial^{2}x_{1,i}}{\partial t^{2}}+\Omega^{2}x_{1,i}=2~\text{Re}\Big[e^{i\Omega t}\big(-2i\Omega\frac{dA_{i}}{d\tau}+\Delta\omega^{2}A_{i}+\tilde{g}(A_{i+1}-2A_{i}+A_{i-1})-3\tilde{\lambda}A_{i}^{2}A_{i}^{*}-\frac{i}{2}\tilde{B}_{0}\big)-\tilde{\lambda}e^{i3\Omega t}A_{i}^{3}\Big]\ .

(26)

The slowly varying amplitude
A_{i}(\tau)
and its complex conjugate
A_{i}^{*}(\tau)
are determined by imposing that secular terms, proportional either to
e^{i\Omega t}
or
e^{-i\Omega t}
, do not appear in the next order, that is on the right-hand-side of Eq. (
24
), as these would predict a divergence which we do not observe in the complete numerical solution of this problem.
The vanishing of such secular terms thus leads to the following equation of motion for
A_{i}(\tau)
:

\frac{dA_{i}}{d\tau}=\frac{1}{2i\Omega}\left[\Delta\omega^{2}A_{i}-3\tilde{\lambda}A_{i}^{2}A_{i}^{*}-\frac{i}{2}\tilde{B}_{0}+\tilde{g}(A_{i+1}-2A_{i}+A_{i-1})\right]\ .

(27)

The equation of motion for
A_{i}^{*}(\tau)
is just the complex conjugate of Eq. (
27
).
We rewrite Eq. (
27
) in terms of the coordinates
u_{i}(\tau)=A_{i}(\tau)+A_{i}^{*}(\tau)
and
v_{i}(\tau)=[A_{i}(\tau)-A_{i}^{*}(\tau)]/i
, which consequently obey the equations:

\left\{\begin{split}\frac{du_{i}}{d\tau}&=\frac{1}{2\Omega}\left[\Delta\omega^{2}v_{i}+\tilde{g}(v_{i+1}-2v_{i}+v_{i-1})-\frac{3}{4}\tilde{\lambda}(u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2})v_{i}-\tilde{B}_{0}\right]\;,\\
\frac{dv_{i}}{d\tau}&=\frac{1}{2\Omega}\left[-\Delta\omega^{2}u_{i}-\tilde{g}(u_{i+1}-2u_{i}+u_{i-1})+\frac{3}{4}\tilde{\lambda}(u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2})u_{i}\right]\ .\end{split}\right.

(28)

In terms of
u_{i}(\tau)
and
v_{i}(\tau)
, the leading order solution, which satisfies Eq. (
23
), reads
x_{0,i}(t)=u_{i}(\tau)\cos(\Omega t)+v_{i}(\tau)\sin(\Omega t)
.
The system of Eqs. (
28
) is Hamiltonian, as it can be rewritten as

\left\{\begin{split}\frac{du_{i}}{d\tau}&=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{F}}{\partial v_{i}}\;,\\
\frac{dv_{i}}{d\tau}&=\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}_{F}}{\partial u_{i}}\;,\end{split}\right.

(29)

with the many-body effective Hamiltonian:

\mathcal{H}_{F}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})=\frac{1}{2\Omega}\sum_{i}\Big\{-\frac{\Delta\omega^{2}}{2}(u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2})+\frac{\tilde{g}}{2}\left[(u_{i+1}-u_{i})^{2}+(v_{i+1}-v_{i})^{2}\right]+\frac{3\tilde{\lambda}}{16}(u_{i}^{2}+v_{i}^{2})^{2}+\tilde{B}_{0}v_{i}\Big\}\ .

Appendix D
3. Dynamical equations for the correlation and response function in the p-spin spherical model

The aim of this section is to derive and discuss the closed set of equations of motions governing the dynamics of the correlation and response functions,
C(t,t^{\prime})
and
R(t,t^{\prime})
, of the
p
-spin spherical model (PSM) investigated in the Letter.
We anticipate that, in analogy to the results obtained in Refs.
Berthier et al. 2001
;
Busiello et al. 2004
, which studied the periodically driven dynamics of the PSM coupled to a thermal bath, the equations describing its Floquet dynamics have Schwinwger-Dyson structure and are given by

\displaystyle[M\partial_{t}^{2}+z(t)]R(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime\prime}\;\Sigma(t,t^{\prime\prime})R(t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime})\;,

\displaystyle[M\partial_{t}^{2}+z(t)]C(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime\prime}\;\Sigma(t,t^{\prime\prime})C(t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime})+\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}\;D(t,t^{\prime\prime})R(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\ +

(30)

\displaystyle+B(t)\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}\;R(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})B(t^{\prime\prime})\;.

(31)

We will define the self-energy
\Sigma(t,t^{\prime})
and the vertex
D(t,t^{\prime})
later on in this section.
The time-dependent Lagrange multiplier
z(t)
enforces the spherical constraint and has an expression in terms of
C

and
R
as well.
In the Letter, we integrate numerically Eqs. (
D
) and (
31
) to compute the correlation and response function, evolving from the equal-time conditions:

\displaystyle C(t,t)

\displaystyle=1\ ,\quad\quad\quad\quad\partial_{t}C(t,t^{\prime})|_{t^{\prime}\to t^{-}}=\partial_{t}C(t,t^{\prime})|_{t^{\prime}\to t^{+}}=0\ ,

(32)

\displaystyle R(t,t)

\displaystyle=0\ ,\quad\quad\quad\quad\partial_{t}R(t,t^{\prime})|_{t^{\prime}\to t^{-}}=\frac{1}{M}\ ,\quad\quad\quad\quad\partial_{t}R(t,t^{\prime})|_{t^{\prime}\to t^{+}}=0\ ,

(33)

resulting from the spherical constraint and the causal structure of the response function.
Then, following Refs.
Thomson et al. 2020
;
Cugliandolo et al. 2017
, we compute the energy density of the system as

e(t)=-\frac{M}{2}\partial_{t}^{2}C(t,t^{\prime})\big|_{t^{\prime}\to t^{-}}-\frac{1}{p}\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime\prime}\;\left[\Sigma(t,t^{\prime\prime})C(t,t^{\prime\prime})+D(t,t^{\prime\prime})R(t,t^{\prime\prime})\right]\ .

(34)

This section is organized as follows. In Section
E
, we derive Eqs. (
D
) and (
31
) using the Schwinger-Keldysh formalism in the large
N
limit. In Section
F
, we show how to determine the Lagrange multiplier
z(t)
explicitly, in terms of the correlation and response functions. Finally, in Section
G
, we discuss the predictor-corrector algorithm, that we use to integrate Eqs. (
D
) and (
31
) numerically.

Appendix E
3.1 Derivation of the Schwinger-Dyson equations

We begin by reminding that the Hamiltonian of the driven quantum PSM is defined as

\hat{H}_{J}=\frac{1}{2M}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ \hat{\Pi}_{i}^{2}-\mathcal{J}(t)\sum_{i_{1}<\dots<i_{p}}J_{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}}\hat{\sigma}_{i_{1}}\cdots\hat{\sigma}_{i_{p}}-B(t)\sum_{i}\hat{\sigma}_{i}

(35)

with all-to-all couplings
J_{i_{1},\dots,i_{p}}
, independently sampled from a Gaussian distribution with zero mean and variance
\overline{J^{2}}=2J^{2}p!/N^{p-1}
. The spins
\hat{\sigma}_{i}
obey the usual spherical constraint
\sum_{i}\braket{\hat{\sigma}_{i}^{2}}=N
(on average). The canonical commutation relations
[\hat{\sigma}_{j},\hat{\Pi}_{k}]=i\hbar\delta_{jk}
hold.
We added a time-dependent factor
{\mathcal{J}}(t)

to allow for other kinds of drives.

The unitary dynamics generated by the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
35
) can be expressed through a path integral on the Schwinger-Keldysh contour
Kamenev 2011
, whose generating functional reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}[J]

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+}\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}\exp\left[i\left(S[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+}]-S[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}]\right)/\hbar\right]\braket{\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+}|\hat{\rho}(0)|\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}}\ .

(36)

Here,
\hat{\rho}(0)
represents the element of the initial density matrix at
t=0
and is chosen to be a random infinite-temperature initial state, which is uncorrelated with the disorder. The last factor in the path integral is then just an irrelevant factor.
The action
S
can be defined in terms of a quadratic term
S_{0}
and a disordered interaction term
V_{J}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
as follows:

\displaystyle S[\boldsymbol{\sigma},J]

\displaystyle=S_{0}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\;\phantom{.}\mathcal{J}(t)V_{J}\big(\boldsymbol{\sigma}(t)\big),

(37)

\displaystyle S_{0}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\;\left[\frac{M}{2}\dot{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}^{2}-\frac{z(t)}{2}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}-N)\right]-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\;B(t)\sigma_{i}(t),

(38)

\displaystyle V_{J}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i_{1}<...<i_{p}}^{N}J_{i_{1}...i_{p}}\sigma_{i_{1}}...\sigma_{i_{p}}.

(39)

The quadratic part
S_{0}
contains a kinetic term, chosen such that the eventual dynamical equations are written in terms of second derivatives with respect to time. The time-dependent Lagrange multiplier
z(t)
enforces the constraint
\sum_{i}^{N}\sigma_{i}^{2}=N
. We also included in
S_{0}
the coupling to the time-dependent field
B(t)
.
This action can be split into components
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+}
and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}
residing on the forward and backwards Keldysh contours respectively, and
recast as

\displaystyle S[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-},J]

\displaystyle=S_{0}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+}]-S_{0}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}]-\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\;\mathcal{J}(t)\left[V_{J}\big(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+}(t)\big)-V_{J}\big(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}(t)\big)\right]

(40)

where the relative minus sign comes from reversing the integration limits on the reverse contour.

E.1
System-Bath Coupling

The coupling between the system and bath can be treated exactly as in Ref.
Cugliandolo and Lozano 1999
.
We couple the quantum
p
-spin Hamiltonian linearly to a bath of harmonic oscilllators, assumed to be in thermal equilibrium, for a time window
[0,t_{b}]
. This coupling can be described by the functional
Kamenev 2011
:

\displaystyle S_{bath}=\int_{0}^{t_{b}}dt\int_{0}^{t_{b}}dt^{\prime}\;\big\{-[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+}(t)-\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}(t)]\eta(t-t^{\prime})[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+}(t^{\prime})+\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}(t^{\prime})]

(41)

\displaystyle+i~[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+}(t)-\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}(t)]\nu(t-t^{\prime})[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+}(t^{\prime})-\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}(t^{\prime})]~\big\}

where
\eta
and
\nu
are the correlation and response functions of the bath, and are time-translation invariant due to the bath being in equilibrium. They are given by

\displaystyle\eta(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\Theta(t-t^{\prime})\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega I(\omega)\sin[\omega(t-t^{\prime})]\;,

(42)

\displaystyle\nu(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}I(\omega)\coth\left(\frac{1}{2}\beta\hbar\omega\right)\cos[\omega(t-t^{\prime})]\;,

(43)

where
I(\omega)
is the spectral function of the bath. We choose an Ohmic bath with
I(\omega)=\frac{1}{\pi}\exp\left(-|\omega|/\Lambda\right)
and set the integration cutoff to be
\Lambda=5
.

E.2
Disorder Averaging

For an initial condition
\hat{\rho}(0)
uncorrelated with the disorder, we can perform the disorder average explicitly. As in the Letter, we assume that the distribution of the disorder variable
J_{i_{1}...i_{p}}
is given by the Gaussian distribution

\displaystyle P[J]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{N^{p-1}}{\pi p!J^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{N^{p-1}}{p!J^{2}}\sum_{i_{1}\neq...\neq i_{p}}(J_{i_{1}...i_{p}})^{2}\right)

(44)

with zero average and variance
\overline{(J_{i_{1}...i_{p}})^{2}}=(p!J^{2})/(2N^{p-1})
.
The disorder average reduces to just averaging over the terms including the
V_{J}(\boldsymbol{\sigma})
, which are the only ones depending on the disorder. By an straightforward calculation, we obtain the disorder-averaged generating functional:

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{Z}[J]}=\int\mathcal{D}{\sigma}^{-}\mathcal{D}\sigma^{+}\exp\left[i\left(S_{eff}[\boldsymbol{\sigma^{+}},\boldsymbol{\sigma^{-}}]\right)/\hbar\right]\ .

(45)

The effective action at the exponent is given by:

\displaystyle S_{\rm eff}[\boldsymbol{\sigma^{+}},\boldsymbol{\sigma^{-}}]

\displaystyle=S_{0}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+}]-S_{0}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}]-V_{D}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}]+S_{bath}[\boldsymbol{\sigma^{+}},\boldsymbol{\sigma^{-}}],

(46)

\displaystyle V_{D}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}]

\displaystyle=\frac{iN}{4}\int dtdt^{\prime}\;{\mathcal{J}(t)\mathcal{J}(t^{\prime})J^{2}}\sum_{\alpha,\beta=\pm}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\alpha\beta\left(\frac{1}{N}\sigma_{i}^{\alpha}(t)\sigma_{i}^{\beta}(t^{\prime})\right)^{p}

(47)

where
\alpha,\beta=\pm
are the Schwinger-Keldysh contours.

E.3
Transformed Order Parameters

The contribution to the action containing at most quadratic terms in the spins
\sigma_{i}^{\alpha}(t)
can be written down as:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\hbar}S_{\rm eff}^{(2)}[\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{+},\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{-}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,\alpha,\beta}\int dtdt^{\prime}\;\sigma_{i}^{\alpha}(t)O_{p}^{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})\sigma_{i}^{\beta}(t^{\prime})-\sum_{i,\alpha}\int\frac{dt}{\hbar}\ \alpha B(t)\sigma_{i}^{\alpha}(t)\ ,

(48)

where again
\alpha,\beta=\pm
.
The matrix elements
O^{\alpha\beta}_{p}(t,t^{\prime})
, appearing in Eq. (
48
), are explicitly given by

\displaystyle O_{p}^{++}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hbar}[M\partial_{t}^{2}+z^{+}(t)]\delta(t-t^{\prime})-\frac{2}{\hbar}(i\nu(t-t^{\prime})-\eta(t-t^{\prime}))\Theta(t-t_{b})\Theta(t^{\prime}-t_{b}),

(49)

\displaystyle O_{p}^{+-}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hbar}(2\eta(t-t^{\prime})+2i\nu(t-t^{\prime}))\Theta(t-t_{b})\Theta(t^{\prime}-t_{b}),

(50)

\displaystyle O_{p}^{-+}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hbar}(-2\eta(t-t^{\prime})+2i\nu(t-t^{\prime}))\Theta(t-t_{b})\Theta(t^{\prime}-t_{b}),

(51)

\displaystyle O_{p}^{--}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\hbar}[M\partial_{t}^{2}+z^{-}(t)]\delta(t-t^{\prime})-\frac{2}{\hbar}(i\nu(t-t^{\prime})+\eta(t-t^{\prime}))\Theta(t-t_{b})\Theta(t^{\prime}-t_{b}).

(52)

E.4
Saddle-Point Equations

To decouple the
p
-body interaction term, we introduce a new set of variables
Q^{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})
, by using the following trivial representation of the number
1
:

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\int\prod_{\alpha\beta}\mathcal{D}Q^{\alpha\beta}\delta\left(\frac{1}{N}\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\alpha}(t)\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\beta}(t^{\prime})-Q^{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})\right),

(53)

\displaystyle\propto\int\prod_{\alpha\beta}\mathcal{D}Q^{\alpha\beta}\mathcal{D}\lambda^{\alpha\beta}\exp\left(-\frac{i}{2}\lambda^{\alpha\beta}\left(\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\alpha}(t)\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{\beta}(t^{\prime})-NQ^{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})\right)\right).

(54)

Then, using a compact notation, the final form of the generating functional is

\overline{\mathcal{Z}[J]}=\int\mathcal{D}Q\mathcal{D}\lambda\;\exp\{NS[Q,\lambda]\}

(55)

where

S[Q,\lambda]=\sum_{\alpha\beta}\int dtdt^{\prime}\Big\{\frac{i}{2}\lambda^{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})Q^{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})-\frac{1}{4}Q_{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})\Big\}+\log Z[Q,\lambda]

(56)

and

\begin{split}Z[Q,\lambda]=\int\mathcal{D}{\sigma}^{-}\mathcal{D}\sigma^{+}\exp\Big\{&-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\int dtdt^{\prime}\sigma^{\alpha}(t)[iO_{p}^{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})+i\lambda^{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})]\sigma^{\beta}(t^{\prime})+\\
&-i\sum_{\alpha}\int\frac{dt}{\hbar}\alpha B(t)\sigma_{i}^{\alpha}(t)\Big\}\end{split}

(57)

is the action of a single effective spin.
Then, defining the matrix
M^{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})=iO_{p}^{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})+i\lambda^{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})
, the saddle point equations for the
N\to\infty
limit read

\displaystyle Q^{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})=\braket{\sigma^{\alpha}(t)\sigma^{\beta}(t^{\prime})}=(M^{-1})^{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})+m(t)m(t^{\prime})

(58)

\displaystyle i\lambda^{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})=\frac{p}{2}F[Q]^{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})

(59)

The average
\braket{\dots}
in Eq. (
58
) is performed over the partition function
Z[Q,\lambda]
and the magnetization,

m(t)=\braket{\sigma(t)}=\sum_{\beta}\int dt^{\prime}\;\frac{i}{\hbar}(M^{-1})^{\alpha\beta}(t,t^{\prime})\beta B(t^{\prime})\ ,

(60)

does not actually depend on the Keldysh index
\alpha
, like every other one-time quantity. The matrices used in Eq. (
58
) are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}Q^{++}(t,t^{\prime})&Q^{+-}(t,t^{\prime})\\
Q^{-+}(t,t^{\prime})&Q^{--}(t,t^{\prime})\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathcal{M}(t,t^{\prime})=\begin{bmatrix}M^{++}(t,t^{\prime})&M^{+-}(t,t^{\prime})\\
M^{-+}(t,t^{\prime})&M^{--}(t,t^{\prime})\end{bmatrix}

(61)

\displaystyle F[Q](t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}[Q^{++}(t,t^{\prime})]^{p-1}&-[Q^{+-}(t,t^{\prime})]^{p-1}\\
-[Q^{-+}(t,t^{\prime})]^{p-1}&[Q^{--}(t,t^{\prime})]^{p-1}\end{bmatrix}\frac{\mathcal{J}(t)\mathcal{J}(t^{\prime})J^{2}}{\hbar^{2}}

(62)

We used the same notation of Ref.
Cugliandolo and Lozano 1999
. Manipulating Eq. (
58
), we can rewrite the response function as

R(t,t^{\prime})=\frac{i}{\hbar}[Q^{++}(t,t^{\prime})-Q^{+-}(t,t^{\prime})]=\frac{i}{\hbar}[(M^{-1})^{++}(t,t^{\prime})-(M^{-1})^{+-}(t,t^{\prime})]\ .

(63)

Then, by fixing
\alpha=+1
in Eq. (
60
), the magnetization becomes

m(t)=\int dt^{\prime}\;R(t,t^{\prime})B(t^{\prime})\ .

(64)

Using Eq. (
59
) and applying the matrix
\mathcal{M}
on both sides of Eq. Eq. (
58
), we obtain the dynamical equations for all the two-point correlators on the Keldysh contour, represented in the following compact form:

\displaystyle iO_{p}\otimes\mathcal{Q}(t,t^{\prime})=\mathcal{I}-\frac{p}{2}F[Q]\otimes\mathcal{Q}(t,t^{\prime})+\mathcal{S}\ B(t)\braket{\sigma(t^{\prime})}

(65)

where the matrix elements
\mathcal{S}^{\alpha\beta}=\alpha
do not depend on the time indices.

E.5
Dynamical Equations

Following the same prescription described in Ref.
Cugliandolo and Lozano 1999
, it is straightforward to obtain a closed set of dynamical equations for the response function
R(t,t^{\prime})
and the (symmetric) correlation function

\displaystyle C(t,t^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}[Q^{+-}(t,t^{\prime})+Q^{-+}(t,t^{\prime})]\ .

(66)

In particular, the equations of motion for the response function
R(t,t^{\prime})
are obtained by taking the difference of the
++
and
+-
components of Eq. (
65
), while the ones for
C(t,t^{\prime})
are obtained by taking the addition of the
+-
and
-+
components. After some algebra, the result is given by:

\displaystyle[M\partial_{t}^{2}+z(t)]R(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})+\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime\prime}\;\Sigma(t,t^{\prime\prime})R(t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime})\ ,

(67)

\displaystyle[M\partial_{t}^{2}+z(t)]C(t,t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime\prime}\;\Sigma(t,t^{\prime\prime})C(t^{\prime\prime},t^{\prime})+\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}\;D(t,t^{\prime\prime})R(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})+\ ,

(68)

\displaystyle+B(t)\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}\;R(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})B(t^{\prime\prime})\ .

Here, we have defined the self-energy
\Sigma(t,t^{\prime})
and the vertex
D(t,t^{\prime})
as

\displaystyle\Sigma(t,t^{\prime})=

\displaystyle-4\eta(t-t^{\prime})\Theta(t_{b}-t)\Theta(t_{b}-t^{\prime})\ +

(69)

\displaystyle-\frac{p\mathcal{J}(t)\mathcal{J}(t^{\prime})J^{2}}{\hbar}\textrm{Im}\left[C(t,t^{\prime})-\frac{i\hbar}{2}R(t,t^{\prime})\right]^{p-1},

\displaystyle D(t,t^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\ 2\hbar\nu(t-t^{\prime})\Theta(t_{b}-t)\Theta(t_{b}-t^{\prime})\ +

(70)

\displaystyle+\frac{p\mathcal{J}(t)\mathcal{J}(t^{\prime})J^{2}}{2}\textrm{Re}\left[C(t,t^{\prime})-\frac{i}{2}(\hbar R(t,t^{\prime})+\hbar R(t^{\prime},t))\right]^{p-1}.

Appendix F
3.2 The evolution equation for the Lagrange multiplier

In this section we discuss how to self-consistently determine the Lagrange multiplier
z(t)
.
The approach typically used in the literature
Cugliandolo and Lozano 1999
consists in evaluating Eqs. (
D
) and (
31
) at equal times
t^{\prime}=t
:

z(t)=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime\prime}\;\Big[\Sigma(t,t^{\prime\prime})C(t^{\prime\prime},t)+D(t,t^{\prime\prime})R(t,t^{\prime\prime})\Big]-M\partial_{t}^{2}C(t,t^{\prime})\big|_{t^{\prime}\to t}\ .

(71)

We observe that Eq. (
71
) leads to ambiguities, as it tautologically depends on the second derivative of
C(t,t^{\prime})
at equal times.
This issue is irrelevant while the system is coupled to a thermal bath
Cugliandolo and Lozano 1999
, which naturally leads
z(t)
to thermalize, or when the total energy is conserved, as the second derivative can be replaced by a causal expression containing the conserved energy density
Thomson et al. 2020
;
Cugliandolo et al. 2017
. However, for
t>t_{b}
none of these two condition is met in our protocol, as the system evolves under a unitary dynamics generated by a time-dependent Hamiltonian. In this case, Eq. (
71
) does not determine
z(t)
.
Here we solve this issue by determining
z(t)
from the spherical constraint, according to the following procedure.
We take the total derivative of the constraint equation
C(t,t)=1
multiple times and obtain:

\begin{split}0&=\frac{d}{dt}C(t,t)\ =\lim_{t^{\prime}\to t}\partial_{t}C(t,t^{\prime})\;,\\
0&=\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}C(t,t)=\lim_{t^{\prime}\to t}\ [\partial^{2}_{t}C(t,t^{\prime})+\partial_{t}\partial_{t^{\prime}}C(t,t^{\prime})]\;,\\
0&=\frac{d^{3}}{dt^{3}}C(t,t)=\lim_{t^{\prime}\to t}\ [\partial^{3}_{t}C(t,t^{\prime})+3\partial^{2}_{t}\partial_{t^{\prime}}C(t,t^{\prime})]\;.\\
\end{split}

(72)

We used a compact notation

\partial_{t}=d/dt
and
we also used the symmetry relation
C(t,t^{\prime})=C(t^{\prime},t)
in the last equality of each line of Eqs. (
72
). It is easy to realize that the first line of Eqs. (
72
) corresponds to some of the equal-time conditions described in Eqs. (
33
) and that the second line is useless, as
\partial_{t}\partial_{t^{\prime}}C(t,t^{\prime})
cannot be computed using the dynamical equations.
The third line is instead the one that we use to determine
z(t)
. Specifically, we observe that the right-hand-side of its second equality can be rewritten in terms of partial derivatives, performed with respect to
t
and
t^{\prime}
respectively, of both sides of Eq. (
31
). After some algebra, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{dz}{dt}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime\prime}\;\Big[\partial_{t}\Sigma(t,t^{\prime\prime})C(t,t^{\prime\prime})+\partial_{t}D(t,t^{\prime\prime})R(t,t^{\prime\prime})+3\Sigma(t,t^{\prime\prime})\partial_{t}C(t,t^{\prime\prime})+3D(t,t^{\prime\prime})\partial_{t}R(t,t^{\prime\prime})\Big]\ +

\displaystyle+\partial_{t}B(t)\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime\prime}\;R(t,t^{\prime\prime})B(t^{\prime\prime})+3B(t)\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime\prime}\;\partial_{t}R(t,t^{\prime\prime})B(t^{\prime\prime})

(73)

From Eq. (
F
) it is easy to determine the Lagrange multiplier in a causal form, as

\displaystyle z(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}\;\Big[\partial_{t^{\prime}}\Sigma(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})C(t,t^{\prime\prime})+\partial_{t^{\prime}}D(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})R(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})+3\Sigma(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\partial_{t^{\prime}}C(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})+3D(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\partial_{t^{\prime}}R(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})\Big]+

\displaystyle\ +\int_{t_{0}}^{t}dt^{\prime}\int_{0}^{t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime\prime}\;\Big[\partial_{t^{\prime}}B(t^{\prime})R(t,t^{\prime\prime})B(t^{\prime\prime})+3B(s)\partial_{t^{\prime}}R(t^{\prime},t^{\prime\prime})B(t^{\prime\prime})\Big]+z(t_{0})

(74)

provided that we can access the value
z(t_{0})
, for some
t_{0}
. Thus we first solve numerically the dissipative dynamics generated from Eqs. (
D
) and (
31
), for
0<t<t_{b}
, using the standard expression from Eq. (
71
). Subsequently, we solve the “closed dynamics” for
t>t_{b}
using the expression from Eq. (
F
) for
t_{0}=t_{b}
and by using the knowledge of
z(t_{b})
that we obtained by integrating the dissipative dynamics.

Appendix G
3.3 Predictor-corrector scheme for the Mode-Coupling equations

To solve Eqs. (
D
) and (
31
) in the Letter, we first introduce a discrete time step
\Delta t
and we discretize the times as
t=n\Delta t
,
t^{\prime}=m\Delta t
, etc. for
n
and
m
non-negative integers. We use a “forward” discretization scheme for the time derivatives, that is

\partial_{t}f(t,t^{\prime})\approx(f_{n+1,m}-f_{n,m})/\Delta t\ ,

(75)

for every two-point function
f(t,t^{\prime})
.
With this notation, the discretized version of Eqs. (
D
) and (
31
) equations is

\left\{\begin{split}R_{n+1,m}&=R_{n,m}+\Pi^{(R)}_{n,m}\Delta t/M\;,\\
C_{n+1,m}&=C_{n,m}+\Pi^{(C)}_{n,m}\Delta t/M\;,\\
\Pi^{(R)}_{n+1,m}&=\Pi^{(R)}_{n,m}-z_{n}\Delta tR_{n,m}+\delta_{n,m}+\Delta tF^{(1)}_{n,m}\;,\\
\Pi^{(C)}_{n+1,m}&=\Pi^{(C)}_{n,m}-z_{n}\Delta tC_{n,m}+\Delta tF^{(2)}_{n,m}\;,\end{split}\right.

where

\left\{\begin{split}F^{(1)}_{n,m}&\ =\ \Delta t\sum_{j=m}^{n}\Sigma_{nj}R_{jm}\;,\\
F^{(2)}_{n,m}&\ =\ \Delta t\ \Big[\sum_{j=0}^{m}D_{nj}R_{jm}+\sum_{j=0}^{n}\Sigma_{nj}C_{jm}\Big]\;,\\
\Sigma_{n,m}&\ =\ -\frac{p\mathcal{J}(n\Delta t)\mathcal{J}(m\Delta t)J^{2}}{\hbar}\ \text{Im}\Big[C_{n,m}-\frac{i\hbar}{2}R_{n,m}\Big]^{p-1}~-\ 4\eta_{n,m}\Theta(t_{b}-n\Delta t)\Theta(t_{b}-m\Delta t)\;,\\
D_{n,m}&\ =\ \frac{p\mathcal{J}(n\Delta t)\mathcal{J}(m\Delta t)J^{2}}{\hbar}\ \text{Re}\Big[C_{n,m}-\frac{i\hbar}{2}\big(R_{n,m}+R_{m,n})\Big]^{p-1}~+\ 2\hbar\nu_{n,m}\Theta(t_{b}-n\Delta t)\Theta(t_{b}-m\Delta t)\;.\end{split}\right.

(76)

The equal time conditions, from Eqs. (
33
), here assume the simple form

C_{n,n}=0\ ,\qquad\qquad\quad R_{n,n}=\Pi^{(R)}_{n,n}=\Pi^{(C)}_{n,n}=0\ .

(77)

It is easy to note that Eqs. (
G
) are causal, implying that we can iteratively compute each minor matrix
C_{0\leq i\leq n,0\leq j\leq n}
from the knowledge of
C_{0\leq i\leq n-1,0\leq j\leq n-1}
. To understand in detail why, we notice that the row
C_{n,0\leq j\leq n-1}
can be straightforwardly computed from the minor
C_{0\leq i\leq n-1,0\leq j\leq n-1}
using Eqs. (
G
), then the column
C_{0\leq i\leq n-1,n}
is immediately obtained from the symmetry relation
C_{n,m}=C_{m,n}
and the diagonal element
C_{n,n}=1
is given by the spherical constraint. The same reasoning holds for the response function, with the only difference that
R_{n,m}
is not symmetric and
R_{0\leq i\leq n-1,n}=0
due to causality.

We aim to integrate Eqs. (
D
) and (
31
) up to some finite time
t_{max}=n_{steps}\Delta t
. With the discretization we chose, the error we make is of order
n_{steps}\Delta t^{2}
. In order to increase the precision of our results, we improve our method by employing a predictor-corrector algorithm, already used in a similar context in Ref.
Haldar et al. 2020
. In a nutshell, we first predict the
(n+1)
-th row of the correlation and response function using Eq. (
G
), and we then correct our result by inserting the result of the prediction in the right-hand sides of the following equations of motion:

\left\{\begin{split}\Pi^{(R)}_{n+1,m}&=\Pi^{(R)}_{n,m}-\Delta t\frac{z_{n}R_{n,m}+z_{n+1}R_{n+1,m}}{2}+\delta_{n,m}+\Delta t\frac{F^{(1)}_{n,m}+F^{(1)}_{n+1,m}}{2}\;,\\
\Pi^{(C)}_{n+1,m}&=\Pi^{(C)}_{n,m}-\Delta t\frac{z_{n}C_{n,m}+z_{n+1}C_{n+1,m}}{2}+\delta_{n,m}+\Delta t\frac{F^{(2)}_{n,m}+F^{(2)}_{n+1,m}}{2}\;,\\
R_{n+1,m}&=R_{n,m}+\Delta t\frac{\Pi^{(R)}_{n,m}+\Pi^{(R)}_{n+1,m}}{2m}\;,\\
C_{n+1,m}&=C_{n,m}+\Delta t\frac{\Pi^{(C)}_{n,m}+\Pi^{(C)}_{n+1,m}}{2m}\;.\end{split}\right.

(78)

For each
n
-th step, we take a loop over the predictor-corrector scheme
\mathcal{N}_{L}
times. In this way, the error we make is of order
n_{steps}\Delta t^{(1+\mathcal{N}_{L})}
. For the results presented in the Letter, we have always fixed
\mathcal{N}_{L}=2
.

At this point, we observe that Eqs. (
78
) alone still do not determine the Lagrange multiplier
z_{n}
, which is in principle determined by the non-causal equation (equivalent to Eq. (
71
) of the Letter):

z_{n}=-\frac{\Pi^{(C)}_{n+1,n}-\Pi^{(C)}_{n,n}}{\Delta t}+\Delta tF^{(2)}_{n,m}\ .

(79)

As long as the system is coupled to a thermal bath, we solve this issue by making the physical assumption that, due to dissipation,
z_{n}
converges at large times to a stationary value, as also observed in Ref.
Cugliandolo and Lozano 1999
. Due to this asymptotic convergence, we can safely replace the difference
\Pi^{(C)}_{n+1,n}-\Pi^{(C)}_{n,n}
with the one evaluated at the previous time step,
\Pi^{(C)}_{n,n}-\Pi^{(C)}_{n,n-1}
, in Eq. (
79
).
However, this substitution is not valid for
t>t_{b}
, where the dynamics is isolated and periodically driven. In the latter scenario, we proceed as discussed in Section
F
and include the discretized version of Eq. (
F
) in the system of Eqs. (
G
), so that also
z_{n}
can be computed using the predictor-corrector algorithm.
