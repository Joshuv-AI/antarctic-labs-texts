Migdal-Eliashberg theory as a classical spin chain
Emil A. Yuzbashyan, Boris L. Altshuler

Abstract
We formulate the Migdal-Eliashberg theory of electron-phonon interactions in terms of classical spins by mapping the free energy to a Heisenberg spin chain in a Zeeman magnetic field. Spin components are energy-integrated normal and anomalous Green’s functions and sites of the chain are fermionic Matsubara frequencies. The Zeeman field grows linearly with the spin coordinate and competes with ferromagnetic spin-spin interaction that falls off as the square of the inverse distance. The spin-chain representation makes a range of previously unknown properties plain to see. In particular, infinitely many new solutions of the Eliashberg equations both in the normal and superconducting states emerge at strong coupling. These saddle points of the free-energy functional correspond to spin flips. We argue that they are also fixed points of kinetic equations and play an essential role in far from equilibrium dynamics of strongly coupled superconductors. Up to an overall phase, the frequency-dependent gap function that minimizes the free energy must be non-negative. There are strong parallels between our Eliashberg spins and Anderson pseudospins, though the two sets of spins never coincide.

2
Spin-flip solutions

Let us determine when spin-flip solutions of Eliashberg equations exist. The spin chain has equilibria with any number of spins at arbitrary positions being antiparallel to their fields. However, to be also solutions of the Eliashberg equations these configurations must satisfy the inequality (
24
). With the help of Eq. (

) we rewrite this inequality as

e_{n}|\bm{b}_{n}|+2\pi^{2}\lambda T\geq 0.

(31)

First, it is clear that
e_{n}=+1
always works, i.e., stationary configurations of spins (equilibria) with no spin flips are always solutions of the Eliashberg equations.

Now suppose
\bm{S}_{n}
is antiparallel to its effective field
\bm{b}_{n}
. Then,
e_{n}=-1
and Eq. (

2\pi^{2}\lambda T\geq|\bm{b}_{n}|.

(32)

We see that no spin flips are allowed at weak coupling,
\lambda\to 0

T=0
for any finite
\lambda

in this inequality is either negligible or zero, while the right-hand side is positive. Conversely, arbitrary spin flips are permitted in the strong coupling limit
\lambda\to\infty
at any finite
T
, because this term is infinite and positive. Therefore, we expect that at finite temperature, spin-flip solutions first appear at
\lambda\sim 1
.

The existence of spin-flip solutions in the double limit
\lambda\to\infty
and
T\to 0
depends on whether the product
\lambda T
goes to zero (spin-flip solutions do not exist), infinity (solutions with any set of
\bm{S}_{n}
antiparallel to
\bm{b}_{n}
exist), or a finite value (spin-flip solutions may or may not exist). Recall that
\lambda=g^{2}/\Omega^{2}
and that we are increasing
\lambda
by decreasing
\Omega

in the double limit

and
\Omega\to 0
and there are numerous such solutions in the regime

We mentioned in the previous subsection that
m=n
contributions to the self-energies

and
\Phi_{n}
in Eqs. (

Spin-flip solutions occur already in the normal state where

Let us also determine the lowest temperature at which normal (

We argue in Sec.

As mentioned in the Introduction, the Migdal-Eliashberg theory breaks down at a certain finite

VI
Stationary points of the free energy

This section contains several results regarding stationary points of the free energy.
One is that at the minimum of the free energy
\Delta(\omega_{n})=e^{i\phi}|\Delta_{n}|
,
where
|\Delta_{n}|
is even in
\omega_{n}
and
e^{i\phi}
is an overall phase. In other words, up to an overall phase
\Delta(\omega_{n})
is non-negative and even. Another is that any spin configuration where
\Delta(\omega_{n})
is real, but changes sign, cannot be a
local or global minimum and is higher in energy than the global minimum by an amount proportional to the system size. In particular, spin flips correspond to saddle points. Fixing the overall phase, we also show that there cannot be a continuous family of stationary points connected to the global minimum and argue based on symmetry that the global minimum is unique.

We have seen in Sec.
IV
that stationary points of the free energy are the equilibria of the spin chain. At weak coupling only
equilibria where each spin is parallel to its magnetic field (
e_{n}=+1
) are stationary points. As
\lambda
increases, equilibrium configurations with arbitrary number of spins antiparallel to their fields (
e_{n}=-1
) become stationary points as well. These spin-flip solutions of the Eliashberg equations are necessarily saddle points. Indeed, suppose
\bm{S}_{n}
is antiparallel
to
\bm{b}_{n}
. Then, its contribution
h_{n}=-\bm{b}_{n}\cdot\bm{S}_{n}
to the spin Hamiltonian is positive and decreases when we rotate

\bm{S}_{n}
keeping all other spins fixed. Given that there are also spins parallel to their fields, the contribution of any of them increases when it deviates from its equilibrium position. Therefore, such solutions cannot be local minima or maxima, but are saddle points.

We see that at a minimum all spins must be parallel to their fields.
This condition is only necessary, but not sufficient as there can be a collective mode involving many spins that lowers the free energy. This happens, for example, when the normal state loses stability at
T=T_{c}
. In the normal state all spins are parallel to their fields and yet it is a saddle point and not a minimum below
T_{c}
. Hence, for the minimum we take
e_{n}=+1
for all
n
in Eq. (
41

S_{n}^{z}=\frac{\omega_{n}}{\sqrt{\omega_{n}^{2}+|\Delta_{n}|^{2}}},\quad S_{n}^{+}=\frac{\Delta_{n}}{\sqrt{\omega_{n}^{2}+|\Delta_{n}|^{2}}},

(44)

and

\omega_{n}\Delta_{n}=\pi T\sum_{m}\lambda_{nm}\frac{\omega_{n}\Delta_{m}-\Delta_{n}\omega_{m}}{\sqrt{\omega_{m}^{2}+|\Delta_{m}|^{2}}}

(45)

is the corresponding gap equation.

IX
Conclusion

In this paper, we mapped the Migdal-Eliashberg theory of superconductivity to a classical Heisenberg spin chain in Zeeman magnetic field. Lattice sites are fermionic Matsubara frequencies
\omega_{n}
. The interaction between spins is purely ferromagnetic. It depends on the phonon spectrum and falls off as
(\omega_{n}-\omega_{m})^{-2}
at large separation between spins. The Zeeman field is proportional to
\omega_{n}
, the spin coordinate along the chain. As an example, the spin Hamiltonian for the Holstein model is

H_{s}=-2\pi\sum_{n}\omega_{n}S_{n}^{z}-\pi^{2}Tg^{2}\sum_{nm}\frac{{\bm{S}_{n}}\cdot{\bm{S}_{m}}-1}{(\omega_{n}-\omega_{m})^{2}+\Omega^{2}},

(65)

where
T
is the temperature,
g
is the electron-phonon coupling,
\Omega
is the renormalized Einstein phonon frequency, and

\bm{S}_{n}^{2}=1
. The free-energy density of the system of fermions interacting through phonons is
f=\nu_{0}TH_{s}
.

The spin-chain formulation made the analysis of the free-energy functional simple. In particular, we saw that, up to an overall phase
e^{i\phi}
, the Eliashberg order parameter
\Delta(\omega_{n})
is non-negative and even at the global minimum and there can be no continuous families of stationary points that include the global minimum. We also discussed symmetry and non-integrability arguments that further support our claim that the global minimum is unique.

It became apparent that the free energy acquires infinitely many new saddle points (which are new, additional solutions of the Eliashberg equations) at strong coupling, which correspond to spin flips. We saw that these saddle points most likely play a significant role in the collisionless far from equilibrium dynamics of strongly coupled superconductors. Apparently, they are the fixed points of the corresponding kinetic equations and give rise to rich multi-soliton dynamics. The new saddle points emerge and proliferate just before the breakdown of the Migdal-Eliashberg theory and, moreover, these two phenomena have similar origin as we discussed in Sec.
IV2
.
It is evident that the spin chain has unstable equilibria where a number of spins are flipped against their magnetic fields. However, without the spin-chain representation these solutions are hard to notice and indeed they have not been seen before.

Our spin-flip solutions of Eliashberg equations are new and unrelated to the continuous family of sign-alternating solutions for
\Delta_{n}
conjectured by Wu
et. al.

andreygamma2
at
\lambda=\infty
. We believe that this conjecture is internally inconsistent in several ways. Therefore, the claims by the aforementioned reference of vanishing of the superconducting
T_{c}
, destruction of the superconducting phase coherence, etc., are unsubstantiated.
Thinking in terms of the spin chain also made it clear that any sign-alternating
\Delta_{n}
costs a macroscopic amount of energy to create
at all
\lambda
and therefore cannot contribute to thermal equilibrium properties of a macroscopic system. Most importantly, because as we discussed above the Migdal-Eliashberg theory breaks down at finite
\lambda
, its strong coupling limit is unphysical altogether.

Classical spins in the Migdal-Eliashberg theory (Eliashberg spins) are in many ways similar to Anderson spins in the BCS theory of superconductivity, though the two sets of spins never coincide. Common to both spin notions is that three real numbers, the normal Green’s function and real and imaginary parts of the anomalous Green’s function integrated over single-fermion energy (Eliashberg) or frequency (Anderson), become the three components of a classical spin vector. Eliashberg spins exist at any temperature and any
\lambda
, while Anderson spins are well defined in and out of equilibrium, but only at
T=0
and in the BCS limit of the Migdal-Eliashberg theory. Equilibrium configurations of Anderson and Eliashberg spins map into each other under the interchange
of single-fermion energy
\xi_{\bm{p}}
with fermionic Matsubara frequency
\omega_{n}
and of the BCS gap
\Delta
with Eliashberg gap function
\Delta_{n}
.
An interesting open question is to study the short-time far from equilibrium dynamics of strongly coupled conventional superconductors with the help of Eliashberg spins.

) with a Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation in the particle-particle and particle-hole channels with three fields
\Phi_{\bm{i}}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)
,
\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\uparrow}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)
, and
\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\downarrow}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)
:

\begin{split}S_{\mathrm{eff}}=\sum_{\bm{i}}\int\!\!\!\!\int d\tau^{\prime}d\tau\left[\frac{\Phi^{*}_{\bm{i}}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)\Phi_{\bm{i}}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)}{D(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}+\sum_{\sigma}\frac{\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\sigma}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\sigma}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})}{2D(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}\right]+\\
+\sum_{x^{\prime}x}\Psi^{\dagger}_{x^{\prime}}M_{x^{\prime}x}\Psi_{x}+\sum_{x}\left(K_{x}^{\dagger}\Psi_{x}+\Psi_{x}^{\dagger}K_{x}\right)\!,\end{split}

(69)

where
\Psi_{x}
and
K_{x}
are two-component Nambu fields,
M_{x^{\prime}x}
is a
2\times 2
matrix in the Nambu space,

\Psi_{x}=\begin{bmatrix}\psi_{\uparrow x}\\
\psi^{*}_{\downarrow x}\\
\end{bmatrix}\!,\quad K_{x}=\begin{bmatrix}J_{{\bm{i}}\uparrow}(\tau)\\
-J_{{\bm{i}}\downarrow}^{*}(\tau)\\
\end{bmatrix}\!,\quad M_{x^{\prime}x}=\begin{bmatrix}G_{0{\bm{i}}{\bm{j}}}^{-1}\delta(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)-i\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\uparrow}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)\delta_{{\bm{i}}{\bm{j}}}&\Phi_{{\bm{i}}}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)\delta_{{\bm{i}}{\bm{j}}}\\
\Phi_{{\bm{i}}}^{*}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\delta_{{\bm{i}}{\bm{j}}}&{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{0{\bm{i}}{\bm{j}}}^{-1}\delta(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)+i\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\downarrow}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})\delta_{{\bm{i}}{\bm{j}}}\\
\end{bmatrix}\!,

(70)

and
{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muG\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}_{0{\bm{i}}{\bm{j}}}^{-1}=\partial_{\tau}\delta_{{\bm{i}}{\bm{j}}}-h_{\bm{j}\bm{i}}+\mu\delta_{{\bm{i}}{\bm{j}}}
. By definition the fields
\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\sigma}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)
are Hermitian,

\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\sigma}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)=\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\sigma}^{*}(\tau,\tau^{\prime}).

(71)

The statement here is that integrating
e^{-S_{\mathrm{eff}}}
with
S_{\mathrm{eff}}
from Eq. (
69
) over the fields
\Phi_{\bm{i}}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)
and
\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\sigma}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)
, we obtain Eq. (
67
). On the stationary point, the fields
\Phi_{\bm{i}}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)
and
\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\sigma}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)
depend only on the difference
\tau^{\prime}-\tau
and represent the anomalous and normal self-energies (see below).

The action (
69
) is quadratic in fermion fields. Performing the Gaussian integral over these fields, we obtain the effective action
for the fields
\Phi_{\bm{i}}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)
and
\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\sigma}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)
:

\begin{split}S_{\mathrm{eff}}=\sum_{\bm{i}}\int\!\!\!\!\int d\tau^{\prime}d\tau\left[\frac{\Phi^{*}_{\bm{i}}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)\Phi_{\bm{i}}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)}{D(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}+\sum_{\sigma}\frac{\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\sigma}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\sigma}(\tau,\tau^{\prime})}{2D(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}\right]-\mathrm{Tr}\ln M-\sum_{x^{\prime}x}K^{\dagger}_{x^{\prime}}M^{-1}_{x^{\prime}x}K_{x}.\end{split}

(72)

It is convenient to transform the matrices and vectors in
S_{\mathrm{eff}}
to fermionic Matsubara frequencies
\omega_{n}
and the eigenbasis of the single-electron Hamiltonian
\hat{h}
with a unitary matrix

U_{\tau n,{\bm{i}}\alpha}=\sqrt{T}\begin{bmatrix}\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}e^{-i\tau\omega_{n}}&0\\
0&\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}e^{-i\tau\omega_{n}}\\
\end{bmatrix}\!,

(73)

which leaves
S_{\mathrm{eff}}
invariant, since it is a scalar (matrix trace). Here
\pi_{\alpha}
are the eigenstates of
\hat{h}
,

\sum_{\bm{j}}h_{{\bm{i}}{\bm{j}}}\pi_{{\bm{j}}\alpha}=\epsilon_{\alpha}\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}.

(74)

For example, the matrix
M
transforms into
(U^{\dagger}MU)_{nm,\alpha\beta}=\sum_{{\bm{i}}{\bm{j}}}\int\!\!\int d\tau^{\prime}d\tau U^{\dagger}_{n\tau,\alpha{\bm{i}}}M_{{\bm{i}}\tau,{\bm{i}}^{\prime}\tau^{\prime}}U_{\tau^{\prime}m,{\bm{i}}^{\prime}\beta}
and similarly
K\to U^{\dagger}K
and
\Psi\to U^{\dagger}\Psi
. Carrying out this transformation in Eq. (
72
), we find

\begin{split}{S}_{\mathrm{eff}}=T\!\!\!\!\sum_{nml\alpha\beta}\left[\left(\Phi^{\alpha\beta}_{n+l,m+l}\right)^{*}D^{-1}_{l}\Phi_{nm}^{\alpha\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma}\Sigma_{\sigma,m+l,n+l}^{\alpha\beta}D^{-1}_{l}\Sigma_{\sigma nm}^{\beta\alpha}\right]-\mathrm{Tr}\,\!\ln M-TK^{\dagger}M^{-1}K.\end{split}

(75)

Here
D_{l}^{-1}
is the bosonic Matsubara frequency
\omega_{l}
component of
1/D(\tau)
,

\Phi_{nm}^{\alpha\beta}=T\sum_{\bm{i}}\!\int\!\!\!\int d\tau^{\prime}d\tau e^{i\omega_{n}\tau^{\prime}}\pi^{*}_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}\Phi_{\bm{i}}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)\pi_{{\bm{i}}\beta}e^{-i\omega_{m}\tau},

(76)

and similarly for
\Sigma_{\sigma nm}
. Hermiticity of
\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\sigma}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)
implies Hermiticity of
\Sigma_{\sigma nm}^{\alpha\beta}
, since the transformation is unitary.
The matrix
M
in the new basis reads as

M=\begin{bmatrix}(-i\omega_{n}+\xi_{\alpha})\delta_{nm}-i\Sigma_{\uparrow nm}^{\alpha\beta}&\Phi_{nm}^{\alpha\beta}\\
\left(\Phi_{mn}^{\beta\alpha}\right)^{*}&(-i\omega_{n}-\xi_{\alpha})\delta_{nm}+i\Sigma_{\downarrow,-m,-n}^{\alpha\beta}\\
\end{bmatrix},\\

(77)

and the source fields are

\begin{split}K_{\alpha m}=\begin{bmatrix}J_{\uparrow\alpha m}\\
-J_{\downarrow,\alpha^{\prime},-m}^{*}\\
\end{bmatrix},\quad J_{\uparrow\alpha m}=\sum_{\bm{i}}\int d\tau e^{i\omega_{m}\tau}\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}^{*}J_{{\bm{i}}\uparrow}(\tau),\quad J_{\downarrow\alpha^{\prime}m}=\sum_{\bm{i}}\int d\tau e^{i\omega_{m}\tau}(\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}^{*})^{*}J_{{\bm{i}}\downarrow}(\tau).\end{split}

(78)

Note that
\alpha^{\prime}
labels the state
\pi_{\alpha}^{*}
, which is related to
\pi_{\alpha}
by time-reversal operation and
A_{-m,-n}
stands for
A(-\omega_{m},-\omega_{n})
.

2
Stationary point

The Eliashberg equations are a stationary point of the effective action, such that the fields
\Phi_{\bm{i}}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)
and
\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\sigma}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)
are spatially uniform and depend only on the time difference,

\Phi_{\bm{i}}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)=\Phi(\tau^{\prime}-\tau),\quad\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\sigma}(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)=\Sigma_{\sigma}(\tau^{\prime}-\tau).

(79)

Equation (
76
) then implies

\Phi_{nm}^{\alpha\beta}=\Phi_{n}\delta_{nm}\delta_{\alpha\beta},\quad\Sigma_{\sigma nm}^{\alpha\beta}=\Sigma_{\sigma n}\delta_{nm}\delta_{\alpha\beta}.

(80)

In words, off-diagonal matrix elements are zero and diagonal ones depend only on the Matsubara frequency.

The derivative of the action (
75
) with respect to any off-diagonal matrix element is a sum of terms each of which is proportional to one of the other off-diagonal matrix elements. This means that setting these matrix elements to zero automatically solves all stationary point equations for them. To determine stationary point equations for the diagonal matrix elements, we substitute Eq. (
80
) into Eq. (
75
). Setting also the sources to zero, we obtain

\begin{split}{S}_{\mathrm{eff}}=TN\sum_{nl}\left[\Phi_{n+l}^{*}D_{l}^{-1}\Phi_{n}+\Sigma_{n+l}D_{l}^{-1}\Sigma_{n}-\chi_{n+l}D_{l}^{-1}\chi_{n}\right]-\sum_{n\alpha}\ln\left[(\omega_{n}+\Sigma_{n})^{2}+|\Phi_{n}|^{2}+(\chi_{n}+\xi_{\alpha})^{2}\right],\end{split}

(81)

where
N
is the number of sites (which is equal to the number of the single-electron states
\pi_{\alpha}
) and

\Sigma_{n}=\frac{\Sigma_{\uparrow n}-\Sigma_{\downarrow,-n}}{2},\quad i\chi_{n}=\frac{\Sigma_{\uparrow n}+\Sigma_{\downarrow,-n}}{2},

(82)

where
A_{-n}
means
A(-\omega_{n})
.
Minimizing the effective action with respect to
\Phi_{n}^{*},\Sigma_{n}
and
\chi_{n}
, we derive three Eliashberg equations

\begin{split}\Phi_{n}=\frac{T}{N}\sum_{m\alpha}D_{n-m}\frac{\Phi_{m}}{(\omega_{m}+\Sigma_{m})^{2}+|\Phi_{m}|^{2}+(\chi_{m}+\xi_{\alpha})^{2}},\\
\Sigma_{n}=\frac{T}{N}\sum_{m\alpha}D_{n-m}\frac{\omega_{m}+\Sigma_{m}}{(\omega_{m}+\Sigma_{m})^{2}+|\Phi_{m}|^{2}+(\chi_{m}+\xi_{\alpha})^{2}},\\
\chi_{n}=-\frac{T}{N}\sum_{m\alpha}D_{n-m}\frac{\xi_{\alpha}+\chi_{m}}{(\omega_{m}+\Sigma_{m})^{2}+|\Phi_{m}|^{2}+(\chi_{m}+\xi_{\alpha})^{2}},\end{split}

(83)

where

D_{n-m}=\frac{\alpha^{2}M^{-1}}{(\omega_{n}-\omega_{m})^{2}+\Omega^{2}}.

(84)

When
\xi_{\alpha}
are symmetric with respect to zero,
\chi_{n}\equiv 0
solves the last equation in (
83
).

As is normally done in the Migdal-Eliashberg theory, we now send the Fermi energy to infinity and take the density of states per site per spin orientation,
\nu_{0}
, to be constant. We implement this limit in Eq. (
81
) by integrating the logarithm over
\xi_{\alpha}
from
-\mathbb{\Lambda}
to
\mathbb{\Lambda}
, discarding the constant term that depends only on
\mathbb{\Lambda}
, and then taking the limit
\mathbb{\Lambda}\to\infty
. The result is

\begin{split}{S}_{\mathrm{eff}}=TN\sum_{nl}\left[\Phi_{n+l}^{*}D_{l}^{-1}\Phi_{n}+\Sigma_{n+l}D_{l}^{-1}\Sigma_{n}\right]-2\pi\nu_{0}N\sum_{n}\sqrt{(\omega_{n}+\Sigma_{n})^{2}+|\Phi_{n}|^{2}}.\end{split}

(85)

Eliashberg equations become

\begin{split}\Phi_{n}=\pi T\sum_{m}\lambda(\omega_{n}-\omega_{m})\frac{\Phi_{m}}{\sqrt{(\omega_{m}+\Sigma_{m})^{2}+|\Phi_{m}|^{2}}},\\
\Sigma_{n}=\pi T\sum_{m}\lambda(\omega_{n}-\omega_{m})\frac{\omega_{m}+\Sigma_{m}}{\sqrt{(\omega_{m}+\Sigma_{m})^{2}+|\Phi_{m}|^{2}}},\end{split}

(86)

with

\lambda(\omega_{n}-\omega_{m})=\frac{g^{2}}{(\omega_{n}-\omega_{m})^{2}+\Omega^{2}},\quad g^{2}=\nu_{0}\alpha^{2}M^{-1}.

(87)

The fermionic part of the free-energy functional per site is
f=TS_{\mathrm{eff}}/N
,

\begin{split}f=\nu_{0}T^{2}\sum_{nl}\left[\Phi_{n+l}^{*}\Lambda_{l}\Phi_{n}+\Sigma_{n+l}\Lambda_{l}\Sigma_{n}\right]-2\pi\nu_{0}T\sum_{n}\sqrt{(\omega_{n}+\Sigma_{n})^{2}+|\Phi_{n}|^{2}},\end{split}

(88)

where
\Lambda_{l}
is the discrete Fourier transform of
1/\lambda(\tau)
at bosonic Matsubara frequency
\omega_{l}
.

3
Spatially nonuniform stationary point

For future reference let us also consider spatially nonuniform solutions

\Phi_{\bm{i}}(\tau^{\prime},\tau)=\Phi_{\bm{i}}(\tau^{\prime}-\tau),\quad\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\sigma}(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)=\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}\sigma}(\tau^{\prime}-\tau).

(89)

Let
\Phi_{{\bm{i}}n}
be the Fourier transform of
\Phi_{\bm{i}}(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)
with respect to
\tau^{\prime}-\tau
. Its
matrix elements in the eigenbasis of the single-particle Hamiltonian
h_{{\bm{i}}{\bm{j}}}
are
\Phi_{n}^{\alpha\beta}=\sum_{\bm{i}}\pi^{*}_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}\Phi_{{\bm{i}}n}\pi_{{\bm{i}}\beta}
. If the eigenstates
\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}
are localized or delocalized but highly oscillatory, the off-diagonal,
\alpha\neq\beta
, matrix elements are negligible. In this case, a suitable ansatz for the stationary point is

\Phi_{nm}^{\alpha\beta}=\Phi_{n}^{\alpha}\delta_{nm}\delta_{\alpha\beta},\quad\Sigma_{\sigma nm}^{\alpha\beta}=\Sigma_{\sigma n}^{\alpha}\delta_{nm}\delta_{\alpha\beta}.

(90)

The derivation of the stationary point equations is similar to the uniform case. The effective action now reads as

\begin{split}{S}_{\mathrm{eff}}=T\sum_{nl\alpha}\left[(\Phi_{n+l}^{\alpha})^{*}D_{l}^{-1}\Phi_{n}^{\alpha}+\Sigma_{n+l}^{\alpha}D_{l}^{-1}\Sigma_{n}^{\alpha}-\chi_{n+l}^{\alpha}D_{l}^{-1}\chi_{n}^{\alpha}\right]-\sum_{n\alpha}\ln\left[(\omega_{n}+\Sigma_{n}^{\alpha})^{2}+|\Phi_{n}^{\alpha}|^{2}+(\chi_{n}^{\alpha}+\xi_{\alpha})^{2}\right],\end{split}

(91)

where

\Sigma_{n}^{\alpha}=\frac{\Sigma_{\uparrow n}^{\alpha}-\Sigma_{\downarrow,-n}^{\alpha}}{2},\quad i\chi_{n}^{\alpha}=\frac{\Sigma_{\uparrow n}^{\alpha}+\Sigma_{\downarrow,-n}^{\alpha}}{2}.

(92)

It is incorrect to minimize
{S}_{\mathrm{eff}}
with respect to
\Phi_{n}^{\alpha}
, because
\Phi_{n}^{\alpha}=\sum_{\bm{i}}|\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}|^{2}\Phi_{{\bm{i}}n}
are not independent. The original independent variables are
\Phi_{{\bm{i}}n}
and the matrix
R_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}=|\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}|^{2}
that
relates
\Phi_{n}^{\alpha}
to
\Phi_{{\bm{i}}n}
is degenerate. For example, the completeness relation
\sum_{\alpha}\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}\pi_{{\bm{j}}\alpha}=\delta_{{\bm{i}}{\bm{j}}}
implies
\sum_{\alpha}R_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}=1
meaning that the columns of the matrix
R
are linearly dependent. Similarly, the normalization condition
\sum_{\bm{i}}|\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}|^{2}=1
says that the rows of
R
are linearly dependent too. The same applies to
\Sigma_{n}^{\alpha}
and
\chi_{n}^{\alpha}
.

We should instead minimize Eq. (
91
) with respect to
\Phi_{{\bm{i}}n}
,
\Sigma_{{\bm{i}}n}
, and
\chi_{{\bm{i}}n}
using

\frac{\partial\Phi_{n}^{\alpha}}{\partial\Phi_{{\bm{i}}n}}=|\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}|^{2},\mbox{ etc.}

(93)

We obtain

\begin{split}\sum_{\alpha}\Phi_{n}^{\alpha}|\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}|^{2}=T\sum_{m\alpha}D_{n-m}\frac{\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}|\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}|^{2}}{(\omega_{m}+\Sigma_{m}^{\alpha})^{2}+|\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}|^{2}+(\chi_{m}^{\alpha}+\xi_{\alpha})^{2}},\\
\sum_{\alpha}\Sigma_{n}^{\alpha}|\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}|^{2}=T\sum_{m\alpha}D_{n-m}\frac{(\omega_{m}+\Sigma_{m}^{\alpha})|\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}|^{2}}{(\omega_{m}+\Sigma_{m}^{\alpha})^{2}+|\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}|^{2}+(\chi_{m}^{\alpha}+\xi_{\alpha})^{2}},\\
\sum_{\alpha}\chi_{n}^{\alpha}|\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}|^{2}=-T\sum_{m\alpha}D_{n-m}\frac{(\xi_{\alpha}+\chi_{m}^{\alpha})|\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}|^{2}}{(\omega_{m}+\Sigma_{m}^{\alpha})^{2}+|\Phi_{m}^{\alpha}|^{2}+(\chi_{m}^{\alpha}+\xi_{\alpha})^{2}}.\end{split}

(94)

If the single-particle Hamiltonian
h_{{\bm{i}}{\bm{j}}}
has the periodicity of the lattice,
\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}
are plane waves and
|\pi_{{\bm{i}}\alpha}|^{2}=N^{-1}
. Then,

\Phi_{n}^{\alpha}=\Phi_{n}
,
\Sigma_{n}^{\alpha}=\Sigma_{n}
,
\chi_{n}^{\alpha}=\chi_{n}
, and we recover the usual Eliashberg equations (
83
).

4
Green’s functions

Let us evaluate the Fourier transforms
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma\alpha n}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha n}
of the normal and anomalous thermal Green’s functions at the stationary point. The latter are defined as

\mathcal{G}_{\sigma\alpha}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})=-\langle T_{\tau}c_{\sigma\alpha}(\tau)c^{\dagger}_{\sigma\alpha}(\tau^{\prime})\rangle,\quad\mathcal{F}_{\alpha}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})=\langle T_{\tau}c_{\downarrow\alpha^{\prime}}(\tau)c_{\uparrow\alpha}(\tau^{\prime})\rangle.

(95)

We obtain
\mathcal{G}_{\sigma\alpha n}
and
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha n}
by differentiating the partition function
{\cal Z}
with respect to the source fields,

T\mathcal{G}_{\sigma\alpha n}=\frac{1}{\cal Z}\frac{\partial^{2}{\cal Z}}{\partial J_{\sigma\alpha n}\partial J^{*}_{\sigma\alpha n}},\quad T\mathcal{F}_{\alpha n}=\frac{1}{\cal Z}\frac{\partial^{2}{\cal Z}}{\partial J^{*}_{\downarrow\alpha^{\prime}n}\partial J^{*}_{\uparrow\alpha,-n}}.

(96)

The source fields enter through the
K^{\dagger}M^{-1}K
term in the effective action (
75
). At the stationary point this term becomes

\begin{split}K^{\dagger}M^{-1}K=\sum_{\alpha n}K_{n\alpha}^{\dagger}M^{-1}_{\alpha n}K_{n\alpha}=\sum_{\alpha n}\frac{1}{\Theta_{\alpha n}}\left[\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\phantom{\prod}$\hss}a^{+}_{\alpha n}J^{*}_{A}J_{A}-\Phi_{n}J^{*}_{A}J^{*}_{A^{\prime}}-(\Phi_{n})^{*}J_{A^{\prime}}J_{A}+a^{-}_{\alpha n}J_{A^{\prime}}J^{*}_{A^{\prime}}\right],\end{split}

(97)

where
a^{\pm}_{\alpha n}=i(\omega_{n}+\Sigma_{n})\pm(\xi_{\alpha}+\chi_{n})
, the labels
A
and
A^{\prime}
stand for
\uparrow\!\!\alpha n
and
\downarrow\!\alpha^{\prime},\!-n
, respectively, and

\Theta_{\alpha n}=(\omega_{n}+\Sigma_{n}^{\alpha})^{2}+|\Phi_{n}^{\alpha}|^{2}+(\chi_{n}^{\alpha}+\xi_{\alpha})^{2}=-\det M_{\alpha n}
.

The source-dependent part of the partition function at the stationary point is
{\cal Z}_{s}=e^{-TK^{\dagger}M^{-1}K}
. Using Eq. (
96
), we find

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}_{\uparrow\alpha n}=-\frac{i(\omega_{n}+\Sigma_{n})+\xi_{\alpha}+\chi_{n}}{(\omega_{n}+\Sigma_{n})^{2}+|\Phi_{n}|^{2}+(\chi_{n}+\xi_{\alpha})^{2}},\\
\mathcal{G}_{\downarrow\alpha n}=-\frac{i(\omega_{n}-\Sigma_{-n})+\xi_{\alpha}+\chi_{-n}}{(\omega_{n}-\Sigma_{-n})^{2}+|\Phi_{-n}|^{2}+(\chi_{-n}+\xi_{\alpha})^{2}},\\
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha n}=-\frac{\Phi_{-n}}{(\omega_{n}-\Sigma_{-n})^{2}+|\Phi_{-n}|^{2}+(\chi_{-n}+\xi_{\alpha})^{2}}.\end{split}

(98)

Suppose there is a time reversal symmetry so that
\Sigma_{\uparrow n}=\Sigma_{\downarrow n}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\uparrow\alpha n}=\mathcal{G}_{\downarrow\alpha n}
. Equation (
82
) then implies that
\Sigma_{n}
is odd in frequency and
\chi_{n}
is even. It further follows from the above formulas for
\mathcal{G}_{\uparrow\alpha n}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\downarrow\alpha n}
that
|\Phi_{n}|
is even and
we arrive at the usual expressions for the thermal Green’s functions in the Migdal-Eliashberg theory

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}_{\uparrow\alpha n}=\mathcal{G}_{\downarrow\alpha n}\equiv\mathcal{G}_{\alpha n}=-\frac{i(\omega_{n}+\Sigma_{n})+\xi_{\alpha}+\chi_{n}}{(\omega_{n}+\Sigma_{n})^{2}+|\Phi_{n}|^{2}+(\chi_{n}+\xi_{\alpha})^{2}},\\
\mathcal{F}_{\alpha n}=-\frac{\Phi_{n}}{(\omega_{n}+\Sigma_{n})^{2}+|\Phi_{n}|^{2}+(\chi_{n}+\xi_{\alpha})^{2}}.\end{split}

(99)

Appendix B
Path integral formulation of the Migdal-Eliashberg theory for dispersing phonons

Here we derive Eliashberg equations and free energy for arbitrary phonon spectrum and momentum-dependent electron-phonon coupling. We will see that these quantities have the same form as for the Holstein model, but with a more general kernel which now involves an integral over the phonon spectrum.

Our starting point is the standard electron-phonon Hamiltonian

\begin{split}H=\sum_{{\bm{p}}\sigma}\xi_{\bm{p}}c^{\dagger}_{{\bm{p}}\sigma}c_{{\bm{p}}\sigma}+\sum_{\bm{q}}{\omega_{0}({\bm{q}})}b^{\dagger}_{\bm{q}}b_{\bm{q}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{{\bm{p}}{\bm{q}}\sigma}\frac{\alpha_{{\bm{q}}}}{\sqrt{2M\omega_{0}({\bm{q}})}}c^{\dagger}_{{\bm{p}}+{\bm{q}}\sigma}c_{{\bm{p}}\sigma}\left[b^{\dagger}_{-{\bm{q}}}+b_{\bm{q}}\right].\end{split}

(100)

Hermitian property requires
\alpha_{\bm{q}}=\alpha_{-{\bm{q}}}^{*}
. For simplicity, we take
\alpha_{\bm{q}}
and
\omega_{0}({\bm{q}})
to depend only on the magnitude of the momentum
{\bm{q}}
. Integrating out phonons, we obtain an effective action for the fermionic fields

\begin{split}S_{\mathrm{eff}}=T\sum_{\mathsf{p}\sigma}\psi^{*}_{\mathsf{p}\sigma}G_{0\mathsf{p}}^{-1}\psi_{\mathsf{p}\sigma}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{T^{3}}{N}\sum_{\mathsf{p}\mathsf{p}^{\prime}\mathsf{q}\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\frac{|\alpha_{q}|^{2}M^{-1}}{\omega_{m}^{2}+\omega_{q}^{2}}\psi^{*}_{\mathsf{p}+\mathsf{q}\sigma}\psi_{\mathsf{p}\sigma}\psi^{*}_{\mathsf{p}^{\prime}\!-\mathsf{q}\sigma^{\prime}}\psi_{\mathsf{p}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}},\end{split}

(101)

where
G_{0\mathsf{p}}^{-1}=-i\omega_{n}+\xi_{\bm{p}}
, and
\mathsf{p}=(\omega_{n},{\bm{p}})
;
\mathsf{q}=(\omega_{m},{\bm{q}})
are the frequency-momentum 4-vectors. As in Eq. (
68
) we replace the bare phonon frequencies
\omega_{0}(q)
with the renormalized frequencies
\omega_{q}

in the denominator of the effective electron-electron interaction.

It is helpful to rewrite the interaction as

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\frac{T^{2}}{N}\frac{|\alpha_{q}|^{2}M^{-1}}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\omega_{q}^{2}}\psi^{*}_{\mathsf{p}+\mathsf{q}\sigma}\psi_{\mathsf{p}\sigma}\psi^{*}_{\mathsf{p}^{\prime}-\mathsf{q}\sigma^{\prime}}\psi_{\mathsf{p}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}=\psi^{*}_{\mathsf{p}_{1}^{\prime}\uparrow}\psi^{*}_{-\mathsf{p}_{2}^{\prime}\downarrow}D^{\mathsf{p}_{1}^{\prime}\mathsf{p}_{2}^{\prime}}_{\mathsf{p}_{1}\mathsf{p}_{2}}\psi_{-\mathsf{p}_{2}\downarrow}\psi_{\mathsf{p}_{1}\uparrow}-\\
-\frac{1}{2}\psi^{*}_{\mathsf{p}_{1}^{\prime}\uparrow}\psi_{\mathsf{p}_{2}^{\prime}\uparrow}D^{\mathsf{p}_{1}^{\prime}\mathsf{p}_{2}^{\prime}}_{\mathsf{p}_{1}\mathsf{p}_{2}}\psi^{*}_{\mathsf{p}_{2}\uparrow}\psi_{\mathsf{p}_{1}\uparrow}-\frac{1}{2}\psi^{*}_{\mathsf{p}_{1}^{\prime}\downarrow}\psi_{\mathsf{p}_{2}^{\prime}\downarrow}D^{\mathsf{p}_{1}^{\prime}\mathsf{p}_{2}^{\prime}}_{\mathsf{p}_{1}\mathsf{p}_{2}}\psi^{*}_{\mathsf{p}_{2}\downarrow}\psi_{\mathsf{p}_{1}\downarrow},\end{split}

(102)

where we treat
D
as a matrix with matrix elements

D^{\mathsf{p}_{1}^{\prime}\mathsf{p}_{2}^{\prime}}_{\mathsf{p}_{1}\mathsf{p}_{2}}=D_{\mathsf{q}}\delta_{\mathsf{p}_{1}^{\prime},\mathsf{p}_{1}+q}\delta_{\mathsf{p}_{2}^{\prime},\mathsf{p}_{2}+\mathsf{q}},\quad D_{\mathsf{q}}=\frac{T^{2}}{N}\frac{|\alpha_{q}|^{2}M^{-1}}{\omega_{n}^{2}+\omega_{q}^{2}}.

(103)

As before, we decouple the interaction with fields
\Phi
and
\Sigma_{\sigma}
:

\begin{split}S_{\mathrm{eff}}=T\!\!\!\!\!\sum_{\mathsf{p}_{1}^{\prime}\mathsf{p}_{2}^{\prime}\mathsf{p}_{1}\mathsf{p}_{2}}\left\{\Phi_{\mathsf{p}_{1}^{\prime}\mathsf{p}_{2}^{\prime}}^{*}[D^{-1}]^{\mathsf{p}_{1}^{\prime}\mathsf{p}_{2}^{\prime}}_{\mathsf{p}_{1}\mathsf{p}_{2}}\Phi_{\mathsf{p}_{1}\mathsf{p}_{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma}\Sigma_{\sigma\mathsf{p}_{1}^{\prime}\mathsf{p}_{2}^{\prime}}^{*}[D^{-1}]^{\mathsf{p}_{1}^{\prime}\mathsf{p}_{2}^{\prime}}_{\mathsf{p}_{1}\mathsf{p}_{2}}\Sigma_{\sigma p_{1}p_{2}}\right\}+T\sum_{\mathsf{p}_{1}\mathsf{p}_{2}}\Psi_{\mathsf{p}_{1}}^{\dagger}M_{\mathsf{p}_{1}\mathsf{p}_{2}}\Psi_{\mathsf{p}_{2}},\end{split}

(104)

where

M_{\mathsf{p}_{1}\mathsf{p}_{2}}=\begin{pmatrix}[-i\omega_{n}+\xi_{{\bm{p}}_{1}}]\delta_{\mathsf{p}_{1}\mathsf{p}_{2}}-i\Sigma_{\uparrow\mathsf{p}_{1}\mathsf{p}_{2}}&\Phi_{\mathsf{p}_{1}\mathsf{p}_{2}}\\
\Phi_{\mathsf{p}_{2}\mathsf{p}_{1}}^{*}&[-i\omega_{n}-\xi_{{\bm{p}}_{1}}]\delta_{\mathsf{p}_{1}\mathsf{p}_{2}}+i\Sigma_{\downarrow,-\mathsf{p}_{2},-\mathsf{p}_{1}}\\
\end{pmatrix},\quad\Psi_{p}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{\mathsf{p}\uparrow}\\
\psi^{*}_{-\mathsf{p}\downarrow}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(105)

Integrating out the fermions, we find

2
BCS theory as the weak coupling limit of the Migdal-Eliashberg theory

Confusion exists in the literature to this day regarding whether or not the BCS theory is the weak coupling limit of the Migdal-Eliashberg theory
mars1
;
mars2
. This confusion arises from attempts to compare cutoff-dependent quantities which are illegitimate within the BCS theory. The BCS theory is
only valid in the limit
\Delta_{\mathrm{BCS}}/\Xi\to 0
, where
\Delta_{\mathrm{BCS}}
is the BCS ground state gap and
\Xi
is the cutoff of the order of
the Debye energy for acoustic phonons and
\Omega_{0}
for Einstein phonons (Holstein model).

In other words, one should take the limit

\Xi\to\infty
and the coupling constant
\lambda\to 0
while keeping
\Delta_{\mathrm{BCS}}\propto\Xi e^{-1/\lambda}
fixed. Only quantities that survive this limit are legitimate within the BCS theory. In particular, it is meaningless to compare
\Delta_{\mathrm{BCS}}/\Xi
or
T_{c}/\Xi
, but, for example,
\Delta_{\mathrm{BCS}}/T_{c}
, the condensation energy, the normalized jump in the specific heat are meaningful and their BCS values should agree with those in the weak coupling limit of the Migdal-Eliashberg theory, which they indeed do
bardeen
;
mitr
;
marsiglio
;
carbotte
.

It is well-understood how the BCS theory emerges from the Migdal-Eliashberg theory in the weak coupling limit. Here we reproduce known arguments within our path integral framework. Our aim in doing so is to be able to compare later the weak and strong coupling limits of the Migdal-Eliashberg theory as well as Anderson and Eliashberg spins.
We define the weak coupling limit as the limit where the ratio of the energy gap to the characteristic phonon frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{ch}}
goes to zero. This is equivalent to
\omega_{\mathrm{ch}}\to\infty
(cf.
\omega_{\mathrm{ch}}=\Omega\to 0
in the strong coupling limit of the Holstein model [see Eq. (
5
)]).

Since relevant energies and frequencies are of the order of the gap,
\omega_{n}
in the denominator is negligible in the effective action (
101
). Then, the interaction is instantaneous and the effective action corresponds to the Hamiltonian

H=\sum_{{\bm{p}}\sigma}\xi_{\bm{p}}c^{\dagger}_{{\bm{p}}\sigma}c_{{\bm{p}}\sigma}-\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{{\bm{p}}{\bm{p}}^{\prime}{\bm{q}}\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\frac{\alpha_{q}^{2}M^{-1}}{\omega_{q}^{2}}c^{\dagger}_{{\bm{p}}+{\bm{q}}\sigma}c_{{\bm{p}}\sigma}c^{\dagger}_{{\bm{p}}^{\prime}-{\bm{q}}\sigma^{\prime}}c_{{\bm{p}}^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}}.

(118)

The hierarchy of scales
\varepsilon_{F}\gg\omega_{\mathrm{ch}}\gg\Delta_{\mathrm{BCS}}
implies that the pairing interaction is confined to the vicinity of the Fermi surface and that
momenta of phonons mediating the interaction are of the order of the Fermi momentum. Therefore, the range of the electron-electron interaction is of order of the Fermi wavelength
\lambda_{F}
. The characteristic length scale (coherence length) in a weakly coupled superconductor is of the order of
v_{F}/\Delta_{\mathrm{BCS}}
, which is orders of magnitude larger than
\lambda_{F}
. We see that the dimensionless range of interactions goes to zero in the weak coupling limit. This means the interaction potential is a delta function in the position space and a constant in the momentum space. Since the pairing occurs at the Fermi surface in this limit, we determine the constant by averaging the interaction over this surface,

\mathrm{const}=\frac{1}{2p_{F}^{2}}\int_{0}^{2p_{F}}\frac{\alpha_{q}^{2}M^{-1}qdq}{\omega_{q}^{2}}=\frac{\lambda}{\nu_{0}},

(119)

where we used Eq. (
5
). This is very similar to how we obtained Eq. (
114
) in the Migdal-Eliashberg theory. The interaction in the position basis takes the form
c^{\dagger}_{\sigma}({\bm{r}})c_{\sigma}({\bm{r}})c^{\dagger}_{\sigma^{\prime}}({\bm{r}})c_{\sigma^{\prime}}({\bm{r}})
For
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}
this is equal to
c^{\dagger}_{\sigma}({\bm{r}})c_{\sigma}({\bm{r}})
, which we absorb into the chemical potential term.
We are left with
\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}
terms, i.e., with the BCS Hamiltonian

H=\sum_{{\bm{p}}\sigma}\xi_{\bm{p}}c^{\dagger}_{{\bm{p}}\sigma}c_{{\bm{p}}\sigma}-\lambda\delta\sum_{{\bm{p}}{\bm{p}}^{\prime}{\bm{q}}}c^{\dagger}_{{\bm{p}}+{\bm{q}}\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{{\bm{p}}^{\prime}-{\bm{q}}\downarrow}c_{{\bm{p}}^{\prime}\downarrow}c_{{\bm{p}}\uparrow},

(120)

where
\delta=(\nu_{0}N)^{-1}
is the single-particle level spacing at the Fermi energy.

Both BCS and Eliashberg theories are mean-field theories. In mean-field approach, only Cooper pairs with zero total momentum determine the ground state and low-lying excitation spectrum. Keeping only such pairs, we end up with the reduced BCS Hamiltonian
bcs

H=\sum_{{\bm{p}}\sigma}\xi_{\bm{p}}c^{\dagger}_{{\bm{p}}\sigma}c_{{\bm{p}}\sigma}-\lambda\delta\sum_{{\bm{p}}{\bm{p}}^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{-{\bm{p}}\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{{\bm{p}}\downarrow}c_{{\bm{p}}^{\prime}\downarrow}c_{-{\bm{p}}^{\prime}\uparrow}.

(121)

We expect the above sequence of steps leading to Eq. (
121
) to be exact when we take both the weak coupling and thermodynamic limits. The neglect of frequency and momentum dependencies of the electron-electron interaction is valid at energies much smaller than
\omega_{\mathrm{ch}}
. It resulted in infinite-range interaction in momentum space between Cooper pairs in Eq. (
121
) [or, equivalently, spins
in Eq. (
53
)]. Therefore, this infinite-range interaction has to be cut off at
\Xi\sim\omega_{\mathrm{ch}}
. Any quantity to which energies
of order
\omega_{\mathrm{ch}}
contribute is beyond the BCS theory.

The weak coupling limit of the Migdal-Eliashberg theory, the BCS theory, is universal in the sense that it is independent of the phonon dispersion and momentum dependence of the electron-phonon coupling. There is only one low-energy scale in this limit: the BCS gap
\Delta_{\mathrm{BCS}}
. The gap itself
cannot
be determined accurately from within the BCS theory. However, the ratio of any other energy to the gap, e.g.,
T_{c}/\Delta_{\mathrm{BCS}}
is well defined. The strong coupling limit of the Migdal-Eliashberg theory is similarly universal
combescot
.
