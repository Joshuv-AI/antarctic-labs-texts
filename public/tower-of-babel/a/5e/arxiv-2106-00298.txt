Gaps between prime divisors and analogues in Diophantine geometry
Efthymios Sofos

Abstract
. Erdős considered the second moment of the gap-counting function of prime divisors in 1946 1946 and proved an upper bound that is not of the right order of magnitude. We prove asymptotics for all moments. Furthermore, we prove a generalisation stating that the gaps between primes p p for which there is no ℚ p \mathbb{Q}_{p} -point on a random variety are Poisson distributed.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification

14G05, 60F05; 11K65.

1.
Introduction

What are the typical gaps between the prime divisors of a randomly selected integer? For
m\in\mathbb{N}

we let
\omega(m)
be the number of distinct prime divisors of
m
and

p_{i}(m)
be the
i
-th smallest prime divisor of
m
, so that

\log\log p_{1}(m)<\ldots<\log\log p_{\omega(m)}(m)

is a finite sequence that depends on
m
.
It is not difficult to show that for almost all
m
and almost all
1\leqslant i\leqslant\omega(m)
,
one has
\log\log p_{i}(m)\sim i
, hence,
\log\log p_{i+1}(m)-\log\log p_{i}(m)
is typically bounded.
A natural question is to count the number of gaps exceeding a fixed constant
z\geqslant 0
, i.e. estimate

\omega_{z}(m):=\sharp\left\{1\leqslant i<\omega(m):\log\log p_{i+1}(m)-\log\log p_{i}(m)>z\right\}.

Erdős
[
6
, pg. 534]
was the first to study this question. He showed that for almost all
m
the
function
\omega_{z}(m)
is well-approximated by
\mathrm{e}^{-z}\omega(m)

by proving an upper bound for the second moment:

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}\cap[1,n]}\left(\omega_{z}(m)-\mathrm{e}^{-z}\log\log n\right)^{2}=o((\log\log n)^{3/2}),\text{ as }n\to+\infty.

However, it turns out that this is not of the right order of magnitude.
Here we prove asymptotics
not just for the second moment, but for all moments:

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix any
z\geqslant 0
and
r\geqslant 0
.
Then

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{N}\cap[1,n]}\left(\omega_{z}(m)-\frac{\log\log n}{\mathrm{e}^{z}}\right)^{r}=\mu_{r}((1-2z\mathrm{e}^{-z})\mathrm{e}^{-z}\log\log n)^{r/2}(1+o(1)),\text{ as }n\to+\infty,

where
\mu_{r}
is the
r
-th moment of the standard normal distribution.

As a consequence, for all
\alpha<\beta\in\mathbb{R}
one has

\lim_{n\to+\infty}\frac{1}{n}\sharp\left\{m\in\mathbb{N}\cap[1,n]:\frac{\omega_{z}(m)-\mathrm{e}^{-z}\log\log m}{((1-2z\mathrm{e}^{-z})\mathrm{e}^{-z}\log\log m)^{1/2}}\in(\alpha,\beta]\right\}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\alpha}^{\beta}\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}t.

Setting
z=0

we recover the much celebrated
Erdős–Kac theorem
[
7
]
. Our method is different from that of
Erdős
[
6
]

in that it relies on Stein’s method on normal approximations
[
18
]
.
This allows us to deal with certain sums of dependent random variables that arise when modelling
\omega_{z}(m)
.
Stein’s method has been rarely used in number theory, for example, by Harper
[
9
]
.

There are many generalisations of the Erdős–Kac theorem to functions of the form
\sum_{p\mid m}g(p)
but they do not cover
\omega_{z}(m)
, as
g(p)
would have to be a function of
m
as well.
Galambos
[
8
, Theorem 2]
studied the values of a function that is somewhat related to our
\omega_{z}
, namely the cardinality of
i<\omega(m)
for which
\log\log p_{i+1}(m)-\log\log p_{i}(m)>z+\log\log\log m
.
His results and method are rather different as they are suited to values of large gaps, while our result relates to small gaps. A function similar to Galambos’ occurs in the recent work of
Chan–Koymans–Milovic–Pagano
[
4
, §4]
on the negative Pell equation.

Remark 1.2
.

At the cost of a non-self sufficient argument,
the number theoretic part of the proof of Theorem
1.1
, (namely, Lemma
2.9
)
can be alternatively verified via the Kubilius model
[
5
, §12]
. The approximation of
\omega(m)
by
\mathrm{e}^{-z}\log\log m

means that the gaps in the sequence
\{\log\log p_{i}(m)\}_{i\geqslant 1}
are Poissonian. It is worth mentioning that the occurrence of
Poisson distribution in other areas of Probabilistic
Number Theory is not uncommon, see the work of
de Koninck–Galambos
[
3
]
,
Harper
[
9
]
,
Granville
[
11
]
and Kowalski–Nikeghbali
[
12
]
, for example.

Remark 1.3

(Further developments)
.

The interested reader may wonder whether one can use tools from analysis
to make explicit the term
o(1)
in Theorem
1.1
.
In the case of the Erdős–Kac theorem this was done by Rényi and Turán
[
15
]
using complex analysis.
After seeing the first version of this paper on arXiv, R. de la Bretèche and G. Tenenbaum
proved an explicit error term using methods quite different from
ours (namely, Fourier analysis); see their preprint
[
4
]
for details.

1.1.
Generalisations in Diophantine geometry

In §
3
we provide a generalisation of Theorem
1.1
, given by Theorem
3.2
.
In brief terms it states that the gaps between primes
p
for which a typical variety over
\mathbb{Q}
has no
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-points
obey the Poisson distribution. A statement analogous to the Erdős–Kac theorem was proved by Loughran–Sofos
[
14
]

by using geometric input from the work of
Loughran–Smeets
[
13
]
.

2.
The proof of Theorem
1.1

2.1.
Defining the model

The letter
z
will denote a fixed non-negative real number throughout §
2
.
As usual, we denote
\exp(z):=\mathrm{e}^{z}
.
For a prime
p
and a positive integer
m
we define

\delta_{p,z}(m):=\begin{cases}1,&\text{ if }p\mid m\text{ and }m\text{ is not divisible by any prime in }(p,p^{\exp(z)}],\\
0,&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

In particular,
\omega_{z}(m)=\sum_{p}\delta_{p,z}(m)
, where the sum is over all primes. Our plan, initially, is to follow
the Kubilius model idea (see Billingsley
[
1
, Equations (1.8),(1.9)]
)
to define Bernoulli
random variables
B_{p}
that model the behavior of
\delta_{p,z}
.
For this we use the random variables
X_{p}
as follows: for every prime
p
the random variable
X_{p}
is defined so that

P[X_{p}=1]=\frac{1}{p},\hskip 28.45274ptP[X_{p}=0]=1-\frac{1}{p}

and such that
X_{p}
are independent.
In particular, the mean
E[X_{p}]
equals
1/p
, thus,
X_{p}=1
models
the event that a random integer
m
is divisible by a fixed prime
p
.
Let
[\cdot]
denote the integer part. The independence of
X_{p}
is related to the Chinese Remainder Theorem.

To model
\delta_{p,z}
we must also take into account the fact that each prime
q
in the range
(p,p^{\exp(z)}]
must not divide
m
.
Thus, we are naturally led to define

(2.1)

{B_{p}:=X_{p}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\textrm{ prime }\\
p<q\leqslant p^{\exp(z)}\end{subarray}}(1-X_{q}).}

We will later prove that
\sum_{p}B_{p}
is a good model for
\omega_{z}=\sum_{p}\delta_{p,z}
in the sense that
their moments agree asymptotically.

Remark 2.1

(Independence break-down)
.

Definition (
2.1
) leads to a major difference between this paper and the proofs of the Erdős–Kac theorem,
namely, the variables
B_{p}
are dependent. Indeed, for all primes
p<q
with
q\leqslant p^{\exp(z)}
, the quantity

E[B_{p}B_{q}]
vanishes while none of
E[B_{p}],E[B_{q}]
does.

2.2.
Distribution and moments of the model via Stein’s method

For any positive
N
we define

S_{N}=\sum_{p\leqslant N}B_{p}

and denote its expectation and variance respectively by

c_{N}:=E\left[S_{N}\right]\ \ \textrm{ and }\ \ s_{N}^{2}:=\textrm{Var}\left[S_{N}\right].

Our goal in this section is to prove that
(S_{N}-c_{N})/s_{N}
converges in law to the standard normal distribution as
N\to\infty

and that its moments are asymptotically Gaussian. This will be done respectively in Propositions
2.5
and
2.7
.
We first need a few preparatory estimates.

Lemma 2.2
.

We have

(2.2)

{E[B_{p}]=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-z}}{p}+O\left(\frac{1}{p\log p}\right),}

(2.3)

{c_{N}=\mathrm{e}^{-z}\log\log N+O(1)}

and

(2.4)

{s_{N}^{2}=\left(1-\frac{2z}{\mathrm{e}^{z}}\right)\frac{\log\log N}{\mathrm{e}^{z}}+O(1).}

Proof.

Recall that Mertens’ theorem states that
\sum_{p\leqslant T}1/p=\log\log T+c+O(1/\log T)
for some constant
c
.
The independence of
X_{p}
yields

E[B_{p}]=\frac{1}{p}\prod_{p<q\leqslant p^{\exp(z)}}\left(1-\frac{1}{q}\right),

which, by
the approximation
1-\varepsilon=\exp(-\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^{2}))
for
|\varepsilon|\leqslant 1
and Mertens’ theorem is

\frac{1}{p}\exp\left(-\sum_{p<q\leqslant p^{\exp(z)}}\frac{1}{p}+O\left(\sum_{p<q\leqslant p^{\exp(z)}}\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)\right)=\frac{\exp(-\log\log p^{\exp(z)}+\log\log p+O(1/\log p))}{p}.

Since
\exp\left(O(1/\log p)\right)=1+O(1/\log p)
, this is sufficient for (
2.2
). The estimate (
2.3
) is directly deduced from it and the
fact that
\sum_{p}(p\log p)^{-1}
converges. Next, denoting
h_{p}=E[B_{p}]

we have

s_{N}^{2}=\sum_{p\leqslant N}E\left[(B_{p}-h_{p})^{2}\right]+2\sum_{p<q\leqslant N}E\left[(B_{p}-h_{p})(B_{q}-h_{q})\right].

First note that
E\left[(B_{p}-h_{p})^{2}\right]=E\left[B_{p}\right]-h_{p}^{2}=h_{p}(1-h_{p})
.
Further, if
q>p^{\exp(z)}
then
B_{p}
and
B_{q}
are independent, hence,
E\left[(B_{p}-h_{p})(B_{q}-h_{q})\right]=0
.
If
p<q\leqslant p^{\exp(z)}
then

E[B_{p}B_{q}]
vanishes, hence

E\left[(B_{p}-h_{p})(B_{q}-h_{q})\right]=-E\left[B_{p}\right]h_{q}-h_{p}E\left[B_{q}\right]+h_{p}h_{q}=-h_{p}h_{q}.

We obtain

\displaystyle s_{N}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\leqslant N}h_{p}(1-h_{p})-2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<q\leqslant\min\{N,p^{\exp(z)}\}\end{subarray}}h_{p}h_{q}

\displaystyle=c_{N}-\sum_{p\leqslant N}h_{p}^{2}-2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant N^{\exp(-z)}\\
p<q\leqslant p^{\exp(z)}\end{subarray}}h_{p}h_{q}-2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\exp(-z)}<p\leqslant N\\
p<q\leqslant N\end{subarray}}h_{p}h_{q}.

By (
2.2
) we have
h_{p}\ll 1/p
, hence,
\sum_{p}h_{p}^{2}=O(1)
and

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\exp(-z)}<p\leqslant N\\
p<q\leqslant N\end{subarray}}h_{p}h_{q}\ll\left(\sum_{N^{\exp(-z)}<p\leqslant N}\frac{1}{p}\right)^{2}=O(1).

Hence, (
2.3
) gives

(2.5)

{s_{N}^{2}=\mathrm{e}^{-z}\log\log N-2\sum_{p\leqslant N^{\exp(-z)}}h_{p}\sum_{p<q\leqslant p^{\exp(z)}}h_{q}+O(1).}

Using (
2.2
) we see that

\sum_{p\leqslant N^{\exp(-z)}}h_{p}\sum_{p<q\leqslant p^{\exp(z)}}h_{q}=\sum_{p\leqslant N^{\exp(-z)}}h_{p}\sum_{p<q\leqslant p^{\exp(z)}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-z}}{q}+O\left(\frac{1}{q\log q}\right)\right),

which, by Mertens’ theorem and
\sum_{q>t}(q\log q)^{-1}\ll(\log t)^{-1}
, equals

\displaystyle\sum_{p\leqslant N^{\exp(-z)}}h_{p}\left(\frac{z}{\mathrm{e}^{z}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\log p}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\leqslant N^{\exp(-z)}}\left(\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-z}}{p}+O\left(\frac{1}{p\log p}\right)\right)\left(\frac{z}{\mathrm{e}^{z}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\log p}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{z}{\mathrm{e}^{2z}}\left(\sum_{p\leqslant N^{\exp(-z)}}\frac{1}{p}\right)+O(1)=\frac{z}{\mathrm{e}^{2z}}(\log\log N)+O(1).

Injecting this into (
2.5
) concludes the proof.∎

Lemma 2.3
.

For all
u\in\mathbb{N},\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{u}

and primes
p_{1},\ldots,p_{u}

we have

E\left[\prod_{i=1}^{u}|B_{p_{i}}-E[B_{p_{i}}]|^{r_{i}}\right]=O_{\mathbf{r}}\left(\frac{1}{\mathrm{rad}(p_{1}\cdots p_{u})}\right),

where
\mathrm{rad}
denotes the radical.

Proof.

We write the factorisation into prime powers of
\prod_{i=1}^{u}p_{i}^{r_{i}}

as
\prod_{j=1}^{v}q_{j}^{s_{j}}
, where
q_{j}
are
v
distinct primes.
This implies that

E\left[\prod_{i=1}^{u}|B_{p_{i}}-E[B_{p_{i}}]|^{r_{i}}\right]=E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{v}|B_{q_{j}}-E[B_{q_{j}}]|^{s_{j}}\right].

Using
|B_{q_{j}}-E[B_{q_{j}}]|\leqslant B_{q_{j}}+E[B_{q_{j}}]\leqslant X_{q_{j}}+E[X_{q_{j}}]=X_{q_{j}}+1/{q_{j}}

and
the binomial theorem yields

|B_{q_{j}}-E[B_{q_{j}}]|^{s_{j}}\leqslant\left(X_{q_{j}}+1/{q_{j}}\right)^{s_{j}}=\sum_{t_{j}\in[0,s_{j}]}{s_{j}\choose t_{j}}\frac{X_{q_{j}}^{t_{j}}}{q_{j}^{s_{j}-t_{j}}}\ll_{\mathbf{s}}\max_{t_{j}\in[0,s_{j}]}\frac{X_{q_{j}}^{t_{j}}}{q_{j}^{s_{j}-t_{j}}},

hence,

E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{v}|B_{q_{j}}-E[B_{q_{j}}]|^{s_{j}}\right]\ll_{\mathbf{s}}\max_{\mathbf{t}\in[0,s_{1}]\times\cdots\times[0,s_{v}]}E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{v}\frac{X_{q_{j}}^{t_{j}}}{q_{j}^{s_{j}-t_{j}}}\right].

By the independence of the
X_{q}

we infer that

E\left[\prod_{j=1}^{v}\frac{X_{q_{j}}^{t_{j}}}{q_{j}^{s_{j}-t_{j}}}\right]=\prod_{j=1}^{v}\frac{E[X_{q_{j}}^{t_{j}}]}{q_{j}^{s_{j}-t_{j}}}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
t_{j}=0\end{subarray}}^{v}\frac{1}{q_{j}^{s_{j}}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
t_{j}\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}^{v}\frac{E[X_{q_{j}}]}{q_{j}^{s_{j}-t_{j}}}\leqslant\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
t_{j}=0\end{subarray}}^{v}\frac{1}{q_{j}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
t_{j}\geqslant 1\end{subarray}}^{v}E[X_{q_{j}}]=\prod_{j=1}^{v}\frac{1}{q_{j}}.

The proof now concludes by noting that

\prod_{j=1}^{v}q_{j}
is the radical of
\prod_{i=1}^{u}p_{i}^{r_{i}}
.
∎

.

Lemma 2.4

(Stein)
.

Let
T

be a finite set and
for each
t\in T
, let

Z_{t}
be
a real random
variable and
T_{t}

a subset of
T

such that

E[Z_{t}]=0
,

E[Z_{t}^{4}]<\infty

and

E[\sum_{t\in T}Z_{t}\sum_{s\in T_{t}}Z_{s}]=1
.
Then for all real
b
,

(2.6)

{\left|P\left[\sum_{t\in T}Z_{t}\leqslant b\right]-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{b}\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}t\right|\leqslant 4(\Psi_{1}+\Psi_{2}+\Psi_{3}),}

where the terms
\Psi_{i}
are defined through

\Psi_{1}=E\left[\sum_{t\in T}\left|E\left[Z_{t}|Z_{s},s\notin T_{t}\right]\right|\right],\Psi_{2}^{2}=E\left[\sum_{t\in T}|Z_{t}|\left(\sum_{s\in T_{t}}Z_{s}\right)^{2}\right]

and

\Psi_{3}^{2}=E\left[\left\{\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{s\in T_{t}}(Z_{t}Z_{s}-E[Z_{s}Z_{t}])\right\}^{2}\right].

Proposition 2.5
.

Fix
z\geqslant 0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
.
For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\left|P\left[S_{N}\leqslant c_{N}+bs_{N}\right]-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{b}\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}t\right|\ll_{z}(\log\log N)^{-1/4},

where the implied constant depends at most on
z
.
In particular,
(S_{N}-c_{N})/s_{N}
converges in law to the standard normal distribution as
N\to\infty
.

Proof.

We will apply
Lemma
2.4

with

•

T
being the set of primes in

[2,N]
,

•

T_{p}

being the set of primes in

[p^{\exp(-z)},p^{\exp(z)}]\cap[2,N]
,

•

Z_{p}=(B_{p}-E[B_{p}])/s_{N}
for

p\in T
.

Let
Y_{p}:=B_{p}-E[B_{p}]
.
Note that if
q\notin T_{p}

then
Z_{q}
and
Z_{p}
are independent, hence,
E[Y_{p}Y_{q}]=0
.
Therefore,

s_{N}^{2}=\sum_{p,q\leqslant N}E[Y_{p}Y_{q}]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant N\\
q\in T_{p}\end{subarray}}E[Y_{p}Y_{q}],

which verifies

E[\sum_{p\in T}Z_{p}\sum_{q\in T_{p}}Z_{q}]=1
.
We next
observe that
since for
every
q\notin T_{p}

the random variables

Z_{q}
and
Z_{p}
are independent,
one obtains

E\left[Z_{p}|Z_{q},q\notin T_{t}\right]=E\left[Z_{p}\right]=0,

therefore

(2.7)

{\Psi_{1}=0.}

Next, we use Lemma
2.3
to obtain

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}^{2}s_{N}^{3}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant N,q\in T_{p}\end{subarray}}E\left[|Y_{p}|Y_{q}^{2}\right]+2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant N,q_{1}<q_{2}\in T_{p}\end{subarray}}E\left[|Y_{p}|Y_{q_{1}}Y_{q_{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant N,q\in T_{p}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{pq}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant N,q_{1},q_{2}\in T_{p}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{pq_{1}q_{2}}.

The sum
\sum_{q\in T_{p}}1/q
is bounded only in terms of
z
by Mertens’ theorem.
It shows that

(2.8)

{\Psi_{2}^{2}\ll s_{N}^{-3}\sum_{p\leqslant N}\frac{1}{p}\ll(\log\log N)^{-1/2},}

owing to (
2.4
).

To bound
\Psi_{3}
we write

\mathscr{C}_{p}:=\sum_{q\in T_{p}}\left(Y_{p}Y_{q}-E[Y_{p}Y_{q}]\right)

to obtain

(2.9)

{\Psi_{3}^{2}s_{N}^{4}=\sum_{p\leqslant N}E\left[\mathscr{C}_{p}^{2}\right]+2\sum_{p_{1}<p_{2}\leqslant N}E\left[\mathscr{C}_{p_{1}}\mathscr{C}_{p_{2}}\right].}

Furthermore,
E\left[\mathscr{C}_{p}^{2}\right]
can be written as

\sum_{q\in T_{p}}E\left[\left(Y_{p}Y_{q}-E[Y_{p}Y_{q}]\right)^{2}\right]+2\sum_{q_{1}<q_{2}\in T_{p}}E\left[\left(Y_{p}Y_{q_{1}}-E[Y_{p}Y_{q_{1}}]\right)\left(Y_{p}Y_{q_{2}}-E[Y_{p}Y_{q_{2}}]\right)\right],

which can
be seen to be

\ll\sum_{q\in T_{p}}\frac{1}{pq}+\sum_{q_{1}<q_{2}\in T_{p}}\frac{1}{pq_{1}q_{2}}

by Lemma
2.3
. Alluding to

\sum_{q\in T_{p}}1/q\ll 1

shows that

(2.10)

{\sum_{p\leqslant N}E\left[\mathscr{C}_{p}^{2}\right]\ll\sum_{p\leqslant N}\frac{1}{p}\ll\log\log N.}

Let us now
observe that
if

p_{2}>p_{1}^{\exp(2z)}

then

T_{p_{1}}\cap T_{p_{2}}=\varnothing
,
therefore

\mathscr{C}_{p_{1}}

and
\mathscr{C}_{p_{2}}
are independent.
Since for every
p
we have

E[\mathscr{C}_{p}]=0
by definition,
we get

E\left[\mathscr{C}_{p_{1}}\mathscr{C}_{p_{2}}\right]=\prod_{i=1}^{2}E\left[\mathscr{C}_{p_{i}}\right]=0
.
Thus,

(2.11)

{\sum_{p_{1}<p_{2}\leqslant N}E\left[\mathscr{C}_{p_{1}}\mathscr{C}_{p_{2}}\right]=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}<p_{2}\leqslant N\\
p_{2}\leqslant p_{1}^{\exp(2z)}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{1}\in T_{p_{1}}\\
q_{2}\in T_{p_{2}}\end{subarray}}E\left[\left(Y_{p_{1}}Y_{q_{1}}-E[Y_{p_{1}}Y_{q_{1}}]\right)\left(Y_{p_{2}}Y_{q_{2}}-E[Y_{p_{2}}Y_{q_{2}}]\right)\right].}

By Lemma
2.3
this is

\ll\sum_{p_{1}\leqslant N}\sum_{p_{1}<p_{2}\leqslant p_{1}^{\exp(2z)}}\sum_{q_{1}\in T_{p_{1}}}\sum_{q_{2}\in T_{p_{2}}}\frac{1}{\textrm{rad}(p_{1}p_{2}q_{1}q_{2})}.

For any positive integer
c
and prime
q
we have
\textrm{rad}(cq)=\textrm{rad}(c)\frac{q}{\gcd(q,c)}
, hence, the sum over
q_{2}
is

\frac{1}{\textrm{rad}(p_{1}p_{2}q_{1})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{2}\in T_{p_{2}}\\
q_{2}\in\{p_{1},p_{2},q_{1}\}\end{subarray}}1+\frac{1}{\textrm{rad}(p_{1}p_{2}q_{1})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{2}\in T_{p_{2}}\\
q_{2}\notin\{p_{1},p_{2},q_{1}\}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{q_{2}}\leqslant\frac{3+\sum_{q\in T_{p_{2}}}1/q}{\textrm{rad}(p_{1}p_{2}q_{1})}\ll_{z}\frac{1}{\textrm{rad}(p_{1}p_{2}q_{1})}

by Mertens’ theorem. Hence, (
2.11
) is

\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}\leqslant N\\
p_{1}<p_{2}\leqslant p_{1}^{\exp(2z)}\end{subarray}}\sum_{q_{1}\in T_{p_{1}}}\frac{1}{\textrm{rad}(p_{1}p_{2}q_{1})}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}\leqslant N\\
p_{1}<p_{2}\leqslant p_{1}^{\exp(2z)}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\textrm{rad}(p_{1}p_{2})}\left\{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{1}\in T_{p_{1}}\\
q_{1}\in\{p_{1},p_{2}\}\end{subarray}}1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q_{1}\in T_{p_{1}}\\
q_{1}\notin\{p_{1},p_{2}\}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{q_{1}}\right\}.

The two sums over
q_{1}
in the right-hand side are both bounded only in terms of
z
.
This can be proved similarly as before with the sum over
q_{2}
.
We obtain the bound

\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}\leqslant N\\
p_{1}<p_{2}\leqslant p_{1}^{\exp(2z)}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{\textrm{rad}(p_{1}p_{2})}=\sum_{p_{1}\leqslant N}\frac{1}{p_{1}}\sum_{p_{1}<p_{2}\leqslant p_{1}^{\exp(2z)}}\frac{1}{p_{2}}\ll\sum_{p_{1}\leqslant N}\frac{1}{p_{1}}\ll\log\log N.

This shows that the quantity in (
2.11
) is
\ll\log\log N
, which, when combined with (
2.10
),
can be fed into (
2.9
)
to yield
\Psi_{3}^{2}s_{N}^{4}\ll\log\log N
. Invoking (
2.4
) provides us with

\Psi_{3}\ll 1/\sqrt{\log\log N}
. Together with (
2.7
)-(
2.8
) it implies that

\left|P\left[S_{N}\leqslant c_{N}+bs_{N}\right]-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{b}\mathrm{e}^{-t^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}t\right|\ll_{z}(\log\log N)^{-1/4}

owing to Stein’s bound (
2.6
). Finally,
letting
N\to\infty
shows that
(S_{N}-c_{N})/s_{N}
converges in law to the standard normal distribution.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

We next prove asymptotics for the moments of
(S_{N}-c_{N})/s_{N}
. This is possibly the central proof in the present paper.
The argument is a modification of the one by Billingsley
[
1
, Lemma 3.2]
, which relies on a version of
the dominated convergence theorem. However,
the underlying random variables are now dependent, thus,
we need to introduce the notion of
linked indices
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Fix
z\geqslant 0
and a positive integer
r
.
Then we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}E\left[\left(\frac{S_{N}-c_{N}}{s_{N}}\right)^{r}\right]=\mu_{r},

where
\mu_{r}
is the
r
-th moment of the standard normal distribution.

Proof.

Take
2k
to be the least strictly positive integer with
r<2k
,
so that Proposition
2.5
with
[
19
, Example 2.21]
implies that it suffices to
prove that

\sup_{N\geqslant 1}\left|E\left[\left(\frac{S_{N}-c_{N}}{s_{N}}\right)^{2k}\right]\right|

is bounded only in terms of
k
and
z
. Equivalently, by (
2.4
) it suffices to show

E\left[\left(S_{N}-c_{N}\right)^{2k}\right]=E\left[\left(\sum_{p\leqslant N}(B_{p}-E[B_{p}])\right)^{2k}\right]\ll_{k,z}(\log\log N)^{k}.

The left side equals

\sum_{u=1}^{2k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{u}\\
2k=\sum_{i=1}^{u}r_{i}\end{subarray}}\frac{(2k)!}{r_{1}!\cdots r_{u}!}\sum_{p_{1}<\ldots<p_{u}\leqslant N}E\left[\prod_{i=1}^{u}(B_{p}-E[B_{p}])^{r_{i}}\right].

Using Lemma
2.3
we see that the contribution
of the terms with
u\leqslant k
is

\ll_{k}\max_{1\leqslant u\leqslant k}\left(\sum_{p\leqslant N}\frac{1}{p}\right)^{u}\ll_{k}(\log\log N)^{k}.

Therefore,

(2.12)

{E\left[\left(S_{N}-c_{N}\right)^{2k}\right]\ll\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in[k+1,2k]\\
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{u}:=\sum_{i}r_{i}=2k\end{subarray}}\sum_{p_{1}<\ldots<p_{u}\leqslant N}E\left[\prod_{i=1}^{u}(B_{p}-E[B_{p}])^{r_{i}}\right]+(\log\log N)^{k},}

with an implied constant that is independent of
N
.

For given
u\in\mathbb{N}
,
z\geqslant 0
and primes
p_{1}<\ldots<p_{u}
we say that two consecutive integers
i,i+1
in
[1,u]

are
linked
if and only if
p_{i+1}\leqslant p_{i}^{\exp(z)}
. In particular,
p_{i+1}
lies in a relatively small interval, hence, its contribution will be small.
Denote the number of linked pairs
(i,i+1)
by
\ell(\mathbf{p})
. By Lemma
2.3
we obtain

\sum_{p_{1}<\ldots<p_{u}\leqslant N}E\left[\prod_{i=1}^{u}(B_{p}-E[B_{p}])^{r_{i}}\right]\ll_{z}\left(\sum_{p\leqslant N}\frac{1}{p}\right)^{u-\ell(\mathbf{p})}\ll(\log\log N)^{u-\ell(\mathbf{p})},

where we used the estimate
\sum_{p_{i}<p_{i+1}<p_{i}^{\exp(z)}}1/p_{i}\ll_{z}1
whenever
i
and
i+1
are linked.
Hence, the contribution of all prime vectors
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{u})

with at least
\ell(\mathbf{p})\geqslant u-k
linked pairs is at most

\ll(\log\log N)^{u-\ell(\mathbf{p})}\ll(\log\log N)^{k},

which is acceptable.
By (
2.12
) we obtain

(2.13)

{E\left[\left(S_{N}-c_{N}\right)^{2k}\right]\ll\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in[k+1,2k]\\
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{N}^{u}:=\sum_{i}r_{i}=2k\end{subarray}}\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{1}<\ldots<p_{u}\leqslant N\\
\ell(\mathbf{p})<u-k\end{subarray}}E\left[\prod_{i=1}^{u}(B_{p}-E[B_{p}])^{r_{i}}\right]+(\log\log N)^{k},}

We will now show that every sum over
p_{i}

in (
2.13
) vanishes.
Denoting the cardinality of
1\leqslant i\leqslant u
with
r_{i}=1
by
a

we see that
the number of
i
with
r_{i}\geqslant 2
is
u-a
.
Since
2k=\sum_{i=1}^{u}r_{i}
we get
2k\geqslant a+2(u-a)
.
Equivalently,
2(u-k)\leqslant a
,
hence, by
\ell(\mathbf{p})<u-k

one gets

(2.14)

{2\ell(\mathbf{p})<\sharp\{i\in[1,u]:r_{i}=1\}.}

We now partition the integers in
[1,u]

into disjoint subsets
\mathscr{A}_{1},\ldots,\mathscr{A}_{r}
using the following rules:

•

if
i
and
i+1
are in
S_{j}
then they are linked,

•

if
i\in S_{a}
and
i+1\in S_{b}
for some
a\neq b

then
i
and
i+1
are not linked.

The inequality
s\leqslant 2(-1+s)
for
s\geqslant 2

gives

\sharp\{i\in[1,u]:i\textrm{ linked to some index}\}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j\leqslant r\\
2\leqslant\sharp\mathscr{A}_{j}\end{subarray}}\sharp\mathscr{A}_{j}\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant j\leqslant r\\
2\leqslant\sharp\mathscr{A}_{j}\end{subarray}}2(-1+\sharp\mathscr{A}_{j}).

This equals
2\ell(\mathbf{p})
since each
\mathscr{A}_{j}
has
-1+\sharp\mathscr{A}_{j}
linked pairs and the total number of links is
\ell(\mathbf{p})
.
By (
2.14
) we infer that there exists an index
j
for which
r_{j}=1

and that is not linked to any other index. This implies
that the following random variables are independent:

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant u\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}(B_{p_{i}}-E[B_{p_{i}}])^{r_{i}}\ \ \ \text{ and }\ \ \ (B_{p_{j}}-E[B_{p_{j}}])^{r_{j}}=B_{p_{j}}-E[B_{p_{j}}].

Since
E\left[B_{p_{j}}-E[B_{p_{j}}]\right]=0

we infer that every expectation in the right hand side of (
2.13
) vanishes. This concludes the proof.
∎

2.3.
Justifying the model

Let
n
be a positive integer and denote by
\Omega_{n}
the uniform probability space
\mathbb{N}\cap[1,n]
.
Our goal now becomes to show that, as
n\to\infty
,
the moments of
\omega_{z}(m)
for
m
in
\Omega_{n}

are asymptotically the same as the moments of
S_{N}
for some parameter
N=N(n)\to\infty
.
Recall (
2.1
). For technical reasons we will first work with a truncated version of
\omega_{z}
, namely,

(2.15)

{\omega_{z,N}(m)=\sum_{p\leqslant N}\delta_{p,z}(m),}

where
N=N(n)
.
The function
\delta_{p,z}
imposes simultaneous coprimality conditions of
m
with several primes in large intervals and to deal with this
we shall need the Fundamental Lemma of Sieve Theory
[
10
, Corollary 6.10]
.

Lemma 2.8

(Fundamental Lemma of Sieve Theory)
.

Let
\mathscr{P}
be a set of primes. Given any sequence
a_{m}\geqslant 0
for
m\in\mathbb{N}

and any square-free
d\leqslant x
that is only divisible by primes in
\mathscr{P}

we assume that

\sum_{m\leqslant x\atop m\equiv 0\left(\textnormal{mod}\ d\right)}a_{m}=Xg(d)+r_{d}

for some real numbers
X,r_{d}

and a multiplicative function
g
.
Assume that
0\leqslant g(p)<1

and that there exist constants
K>1,\kappa>0
such that

\prod_{w\leqslant p<y\atop p\in\mathscr{P}}(1-g(p))^{-1}\leqslant K\left(\frac{\log y}{\log w}\right)^{\kappa}

holds for all
2\leqslant w<y
.
Then for all
D\geqslant y\geqslant 2
we have

(2.16)

{\sum_{m\leqslant x\atop p\in\mathscr{P},p<y\Rightarrow p\nmid m}a_{m}=X\left(\prod_{p<y\atop p\in\mathscr{P}}(1-g(p))\right)\{1+O(\mathrm{e}^{-s})\}+O\left(\sum_{d<D\atop p\mid d\Rightarrow p\in\mathscr{P}}\mu^{2}(d)|r_{d}|\right),}

where
s=\log D/\log y
and the implied constants depend at most on
\kappa
and
K
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Assume that there exists a function
N:[1,\infty)\to[1,\infty)
satisfying

(2.17)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}N(n)=+\infty,

(2.18)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{(\log N(n))(\log\log\log N(n))}{\log n}\neq+\infty.

Fix
z\geqslant 0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{m\in\Omega_{n}}\left[\left(\frac{\omega_{z,N}-c_{N}}{s_{N}}\right)^{k}\right]=\mu_{k},

where
\mu_{k}
is the
k
-th moment of the standard normal distribution.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.7
and (
2.17
)
it is sufficient to prove

(2.19)

{\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(E_{m\in\Omega_{n}}\left[\left(\frac{\omega_{z,N}(m)-c_{N}}{s_{N}}\right)^{k}\right]-E\left[\left(\frac{S_{N}-c_{N}}{s_{N}}\right)^{k}\right]\right)=0.}

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
. By (
2.15
), the fact that
\delta_{p,z}\in\{0,1\}
and the binomial theorem we obtain

(2.20)

{E_{m\in\Omega_{n}}\left[\omega_{z,N}(m)^{r}\right]=\sum_{u=1}^{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\ldots,r_{u}\in\mathbb{N}\\
r_{1}+\ldots+r_{u}=r\end{subarray}}\frac{r!}{r_{1}!\cdots r_{u}!}\sum_{p_{1}<\cdots<p_{u}\leqslant N}E_{m\in\Omega_{n}}\left[\delta_{p_{1},z}(m)\cdots\delta_{p_{u},z}(m)\right].}

Let
\mathscr{P}
be the set of all primes in
\bigcup_{i=1}^{u}(p_{i},p_{i}^{\exp(z)}]
and let
a_{m}
be the indicator function of integers divisible by
p_{1}\cdots p_{u}
.
In particular,

E_{m\in\Omega_{n}}\left[\delta_{p_{1},z}(m)\cdots\delta_{p_{u},z}(m)\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant m\leqslant n\\
p\in\mathscr{P}\Rightarrow p\nmid m\end{subarray}}a_{m}.

We assume that
p_{i+1}>p_{i}^{\exp(z)}
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,u-1
since otherwise the sum clearly vanishes.
We will now use Lemma
2.8

with
X=n/(p_{1}\cdots p_{u}),g(d)=1/d,D=\sqrt{n},y=N^{2\exp(z)}.

If
d
is divisible only by primes in
\mathscr{P}
then it is coprime to
p_{1}\cdots p_{u}
, hence,

\sum_{m\leqslant n\atop m\equiv 0\left(\textnormal{mod}\ d\right)}a_{m}=\left[\frac{n}{p_{1}\cdots p_{u}d}\right],

thus,
|r_{d}|\leqslant 1
because

r_{d}
is the fractional part of
X/d
. Furthermore, we can take
K
to be any large fixed positive constant and
\kappa=1
,
owing to

\prod_{w\leqslant p<y\atop p\in\mathscr{P}}(1-g(p))^{-1}=\prod_{w\leqslant p<y\atop p\in\mathscr{P}}(1-1/p)^{-1}\leqslant\prod_{w\leqslant p<y}(1-1/p)^{-1}\ll\frac{\log y}{\log w}.

The bound
|r_{d}|\leqslant 1
, means that
\sum_{d\leqslant D}\mu^{2}(d)|r_{d}|\leqslant D=\sqrt{n}
.
Since
p_{u}\leqslant N
, every prime in
\mathscr{P}
is strictly smaller than
y
, hence, (
2.16
)
gives

(2.21)

{E_{m\in\Omega_{n}}\left[\delta_{p_{1}}\cdots\delta_{p_{u}}\right]=\left\{\prod_{i=1}^{u}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\prod_{p_{i}<q\leqslant p_{i}^{\exp(z)}}\left(1-1/p\right)\right\}\left\{1+O\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\log n}{4\exp(z)\log N}}\right)\right\}+O(n^{-1/2}),}

where the implied constant depends at most on
r
and
z
.

By the binomial theorem we get

E\left[S_{N}^{r}\right]=E\left[\left(\sum_{p\leqslant N}B_{p}\right)^{r}\right]=\sum_{u=1}^{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r_{1},\ldots,r_{u}\in\mathbb{N}\\
r_{1}+\ldots+r_{u}=r\end{subarray}}\frac{r!}{r_{1}!\cdots r_{u}!}\sum_{p_{1}<\cdots<p_{u}\leqslant N}E\left[B_{p_{1}}\cdots B_{p_{u}}\right]

and we note that we can restrict the sum over
p_{i}
to the terms with
p_{i+1}>p_{i}^{\exp(z)}
for all
i
,
since otherwise
E\left[B_{p_{1}}\cdots B_{p_{u}}\right]=0
. Under this restriction the random variables
B_{p_{i}}
are independent, hence,

\prod_{i=1}^{u}\frac{1}{p_{i}}\prod_{p_{i}<q\leqslant p_{i}^{\exp(z)}}\left(1-1/p\right)=E\left[B_{p_{1}}\cdots B_{p_{u}}\right].

We infer from (
2.20
) and (
2.21
) that

\left|E_{m\in\Omega_{n}}\left[\omega_{z,N}(m)^{r}\right]-E\left[S_{N}^{r}\right]\right|\ll_{r}E\left[S_{N}^{r}\right]\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\log n}{4\exp(z)\log N}}+n^{-1/2}\sum_{u=1}^{r}\sum_{p_{1}<\ldots<p_{u}\leqslant N}1.

By (
2.3
) this is
\ll(\log\log N)^{r}\exp(-\frac{\log n}{4\exp(z)\log N})+n^{-1/2}N^{r}
.
Thus, the difference in (
2.19
) is

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle s_{N}^{-k}\sum_{r=0}^{k}{k\choose r}(-c_{N})^{k-r}\left(E_{\Omega_{n}}\left[\omega_{z,N}^{r}\right]-E\left[S_{N}^{r}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\ll

\displaystyle s_{N}^{-k}\left\{\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\log n}{4\exp(z)\log N}}(c_{N}+\log\log N)^{k}+n^{-1/2}(N+c_{N})^{k}\right\}.

We need to show that this vanishes asymptotically and by (
2.4
) and (
2.17
)
it suffices to show

(2\log\log N)^{k}\leqslant\exp\left(\frac{\log n}{4\exp(z)\log N}\right)\ \ \textrm{ and }\ \ (2N)^{k}\leqslant n^{1/2}.

Both of these inequalities can be directly inferred from (
2.17
)-(
2.18
).
∎

Lemma 2.10
.

Assume that there exists a function
N:[1,\infty)\to[1,\infty)
satisfying

(2.22)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log\log N(n)}{\log\log n}=1,

(2.23)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{(\log N(n))\sqrt{\log\log n}}{\log n}=+\infty.

z\geqslant 0
. Then

{s_{N}}((1-2z\mathrm{e}^{-z}){\mathrm{e}}^{-z}\log\log n)^{-1/2}\to 1
as

and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\max\left\{\left|(\omega_{z}-\mathrm{e}^{-z}\log\log n)-(\omega_{z,N}-c_{N})\right|:m\in\mathbb{N}\cap[1,n]\right\}}{\sqrt{\log\log n}}=0.

Proof.

Combining (
2.4
) and (
2.22
) one immediately gets

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{s_{N}}{((1-2z\mathrm{e}^{-z}){\mathrm{e}}^{-z}\log\log n)^{1/2}}=1.

For any

\left|(\omega_{z}-\mathrm{e}^{-z}\log\log n)-(\omega_{z,N}-c_{N})\right|\leqslant\sum_{p>N}\delta_{p,z}(m)+|\mathrm{e}^{-z}(\log\log n)-c_{N}|.

) gives

The proof now concludes by using (

).
∎

3.
Poissonian gaps for local solubility in families of varieties

Serre’s problem
[
16
]

on the probability that a random variety over
\mathbb{Q}
has a
\mathbb{Q}
-rational point has
recently received a lot of attention due to its extension by Loughran–Smeets
[
13
]
to a very general setting, namely,
for any dominant morphism
f:V\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
, where,
V
is a smooth projective variety over
\mathbb{Q}
and
f
has
a geometrically integral generic fibre. The fibres of
f
form an infinite family of varieties
and typically one is interested in how often they have a
\mathbb{Q}
-rational point.
Imposing the harmless condition that the generic fibre of
f
is geometrically integral, it is easy to see that
for every
x
outside of some proper Zariski closed set
the function

\omega_{f}(x):=\sharp\left\{p\textrm{ prime}:(f^{-1}(x))(\mathbb{Q}_{p})=\varnothing\right\},

is bounded
due to the Lang–Weil estimates and Hensel’s lemma. This function
helps us in understanding the density of fibres with a
\mathbb{Q}
-rational point.
Ordering
\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{Q})
by
the standard Weil height
H
on
\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{Q})
and assuming that a certain invariant
\Delta(\pi)
is non-vanishing,
Loughran and Sofos
[
14
]
recently
proved the analogue of Erdős–Kac’s theorem for
\omega_{f}(x)
,
namely that

\frac{\omega_{f}(x)-\Delta(\pi)\log\log H(x)}{(\Delta(\pi)\log\log H(x))^{1/2}}

converges in law to the standard normal distribution. This was the first instance of an Erdős–Kac law in Diophantine geometry.

Our goal in this section is to go further and study the gaps between the primes
p
counted by
\omega_{f}(x)
.
For
x\in\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{Q})
with
f^{-1}(x)
smooth we let
p_{i}(x)
be the
i
-th smallest prime number for which
f^{-1}(x)
has no
\mathbb{Q}_{p}
-point.
We then define for all
z\geqslant 0
,

\omega_{f,z}(x):=\sharp\{i\geqslant 1:\log\log p_{i+1}(x)-\log\log p_{i}(x)>z\}.

Before stating our theorem we must recall the definition of the invariant
\Delta(f)
that is due to Loughran and Smeets
[
13
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
f:V\to X
be a dominant proper morphism of smooth irreducible varieties over a
field
k
of characteristic
0
. For each point

x\in X
with residue field
\kappa(x)
,
the absolute Galois group
\Gal(\overline{\kappa(x)}/\kappa(x))

of the residue field acts on the irreducible
components of

f^{-1}(x)_{\overline{\kappa(x)}}:=f^{-1}(x)\times_{\kappa(x)}\overline{\kappa(x)}

of multiplicity

.
