Modelling 1D Partially Absorbing Boundaries for Brownian Molecular Communication Channels
Caglar Koca, Ozgur B. Akan

Abstract
Molecular Communication (MC) architectures suffer from molecular build-up in the channel if they do not have appropriate reuptake mechanisms. The molecular build-up either leads to intersymbol interference (ISI) or reduces the transmission rate. To measure the molecular build-up, we derive analytic expressions for the incidence rate and absorption rate for one-dimensional MC channels where molecular dispersion obeys the Brownian Motion. We verify each of our key results with Monte Carlo simulations. Our results contribute to the development of more complicated models and analytic expressions to measure the molecular build-up and the impact of ISI in MC.



I
Introduction

Molecular communication (MC) is the primary mode of communication in the nature. It is prevalent both among organisms, in the forms of pollen, spores and pheromones and within organisms in the forms of neurotransmitters, enzymes and hormones
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. However, unlike the electromagnetic waves, the information carrying molecules (IcM) tend to accumulate in the medium. Thus, MC architectures, especially when used in a confined volume, need a
reuptake
mechanism to prevent accumulation. In this work, we pave the way for building complex models describing molecular reuptake.

In an MC channel, the IcM build-up in the medium either dramatically reduces the transmission rate or leads to inter-symbol interference (ISI)
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. In nature, healthy organisms have well functioning reuptake mechanisms to mitigate ISI, which is one of the bottlenecks of MC
[
9
,
10
]
. Realising this, many prominent works include an absorbing receiver in their envisioned MC architectures to reduce the time to prepare the medium for the next transmission
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
]
. However, in SMC, absorption does not occur at the receivers but on the dedicated reuptake molecules. Thus, these works are not directly applicable to SMC
[
17
,
18
]
.

Apart from the absorbing receiver assumption, most of the existing works in the MC literature generally use either simple mechanisms to describe the reuptake or ignore it altogether. The simple mechanisms include constant ratio reuptake on the boundaries and infinite volume assumption
[
19
]
. Infinite volume assumption unnecessitates any reuptake mechanism as IcM can disperse to infinity. The system models that do not include any reuptake mechanism implicitly use the infinite volume assumption, allowing the IcM to disperse to and from the infinity
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
.

More realistic models solve a boundary value problem (BVP). Depending on the system model, reuptake may occur at the receiver
[
23
]
or at the boundaries of the volume
[
24
,
25
]
. Solving the BVPs are easier for perfectly absorbing boundaries, as they only use the Dirichlet boundary condition, whereas partially absorbing boundaries use a combination of Dirichlet and Von Neumann boundary conditions. There are works in the literature that tackle this issue for spherical boundaries
[
26
,
27
]
or works which might be extended to include the reuptake process
[
28
]
.

In cases where BVPs are too hard to solve, Monte Carlo simulations are frequently used to approximate a solution
[
29
,
30
]
. However, when we use Monte Carlo simulations to model partially absorbing boundaries further complications arise, as the rate of absorption depends on the space step used in the system
[
31
,
32
]
. If the absorption rate to the diffusion coefficient ratio is too high, then the space step might be too small to complete the simulation in reasonable time. Alternatively, a large space step would necessitate a large absorption probability, which might even exceed 1.

To address this issue, we derive analytic expressions for the second incidence rate in 1D. While our primary motivation is to find the absorption rate in MC, we believe second incidence rates might be of interest to the researchers of other disciplines, such as finance. Then, using the second incidence rates, we reach an analytic result for the absorption rate of the IcM. Although we work in a one-dimensional domain, our results can be extended to higher dimensional systems.

Our itemised major contributions in this work are

•

deriving an expression for the second incidence rate in a semi-infinite 1D medium,

•

deriving an expression for the second incidence rate in a bounded 1D medium,

•

deriving an expression for the absorption rate in a bounded 1D medium.

The rest of this paper is organised as follows. In Sec.
II
, we present the background information and the system model. In Sec.
III
, we find the second incidence rate in a discretised, one-dimensional semi-infinite medium. In Sec.
IV
, we derive an approximate expression for the second incidence rate in a medium bounded at both ends. Then, in Sec.
V
, we obtain an exact analytical expression for the second incidence rate in a bounded volume. We reach an analytical expression for the absorption rate in Sec.
VI
. Sec.
VII
is the conclusion.

II
System Model

In this work, assume a Brownian Motion,
\mathcal{B}
, in a one-dimensional domain with partially reflective end point(s). Except for Sec.
III

2L
. We discretise this domain into
2H

\displaystyle\Delta{x}=\frac{H}{L}.

(1)

After discretisation, we choose nodes as the centre poins of these
2H

[-H+0.5,-H+1.5,\dots-0.5,0.5,\dots,H-0.5]

x=-H+0.5
is the leftmost node of the domain. Apart from Sec.
III

x=H

We use the fixed jump model for
\mathcal{B}

\mathcal{B}[N-1]\notin\{-H+0.5,H-0.5\}

\mathcal{B}[N]=\begin{cases}&\mathcal{B}[N-1]+1,\hskip 2.84526pt\text{w.p. 0.5}\\
&\mathcal{B}[N-1]-1,\hskip 2.84526pt\text{w.p. 0.5}\end{cases}.

(2)

(
2

(3)

\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}[N]=\mathcal{B}[N-1].

(4)

(5)

Our focus in this work is to model the second incidence rate. Accordingly, unless otherwise stated, we assume that the first incidence occurs at
t=0
. We use the rate of first incidence as derived in
[
24
]
when needed.

III
Second Incidence Rate with one Boundary

In this section, we start with a semi-infinite domain with a partially reflective boundary at
x=H
, i.e.,
\mathcal{B}\in(-\infty,H]
. By definition, to find the rate of second incidence, we assume that
\mathcal{B}
was incident at
x=H
. Thus, without loss of generality, we can shift
\mathcal{B}
in time steps such that
\mathcal{B}[0]=H-0.5
. In this section, since we only focus on the second incidence rate, we choose the probability of absorption,
P_{A}
as 1.

Assuming the second incidence happens at time step
M
, we calculate
\mathrm{P^{(S)}}[M>N]
, the probability that the second incidence has not yet occurred in the next
N
time steps, where

\displaystyle N=\frac{t}{\Delta{t}}.

(6)

Let
\mathcal{B}
take
n_{l}
steps to the
-x
direction and
n_{r}
steps to the
+x
direction, satisfying
n_{r}+n_{l}=N
. Since we know that for
n_{r}>n_{l}
,
\mathcal{B}
necessarily hits
x=H
at least once within the first
N
time steps. Thus, we choose
n_{l}\geq n_{r}
. Using Bertrand’s Ballot Theorem with ties allowed, we reach

\displaystyle\mathrm{P^{(S)}}[M>n_{r}+n_{l}|n_{r},n_{l}]=\frac{n_{l}-n_{r}+1}{n_{l}+1}.

(7)

Since ties are allowed, (
7
) includes cases where
\mathcal{B}
returns back to
x=H-0.5
but
\mathcal{B}
never exceeds
H
. Moreover, the incidences occur only at odd values of
N
, due to the
\mathcal{B}[0]=H-0.5
initial condition.

Since there are
2^{N}
sequences of
N
-long
+x
and
-x
movements, the probability that
\mathcal{B}
is never incident on the boundary within the first
N
time steps is

\displaystyle\mathrm{P^{(S)}}[M>N]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{k=n}^{N}\binom{N}{k}\frac{2k-N+1}{k+1},

(8)

where

n=\left\lceil\frac{N}{2}\right\rceil.

(9)

The binomial term in (
8
) provides the number of sequences with
k
moves in the
+x
direction and the fractional term gives the probability that a given sequence with
k
moves in the
+x
direction satisfies (
7
). The choice of limits for the summation reflects that
k
, the number of steps taken in the
+x
direction, is larger than
N-k
, the number of steps in the
-x
direction.

Starting with (
8
), we continue as

\displaystyle\mathrm{P^{(S)}}[M>N]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{k=n}^{N}\binom{N}{k}\left(2-\frac{N+1}{k+1}\right),

(10)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{k=n}^{N}2\binom{N}{k}-\binom{N+1}{k+1},

(11)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\binom{2n}{n}.

(12)

Now, we can use (
12
) to find
\mathrm{P^{(S)}}[M=N]
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathrm{P^{(S)}}[M=N]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{P^{(S)}}[M>N-2]-\mathrm{P^{(S)}}[M>N],

(13)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N-2}}\binom{N-1}{n-1}-\frac{1}{2^{N}}\binom{N+1}{n},

(14)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N-2}}\binom{N-2}{n-1}\frac{1}{N},

(15)

for odd
N
and
\mathrm{P^{(S)}}[M=N]=0
for even
N
.

Using the Stirling approximation of the form

\displaystyle\log(N!)=\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi N)+N\ln(N)-N,

(16)

(
12
) turns into

\displaystyle\mathrm{P^{(S)}}[M>2n]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2^{2n}}\frac{\sqrt{2\pi 2{n}}\left(\frac{2n}{e}\right)^{2n}}{\left(\sqrt{2\pi n}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}\right)^{2}},

(17)

\displaystyle\approx\sqrt{\frac{1}{\pi n}}.

(18)

Similarly, (
15
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathrm{P^{(S)}}[M=2n+1]

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{2n\sqrt{\pi(n-1)}},

(19)

\displaystyle\approx\sqrt{\frac{1}{4\pi n^{3}}}.

(20)

Note that both (
18
) and (
20
) are as good as the Stirling approximation, i.e., they are inaccurate for small
m
. Thus, for smaller values, it might be better to use (
12
) and (
15
) rather than their approximations.

N
vs. time steps following the first hit. We employ semi-log plot for the outer figure, to increase the visibility of the data for large
N
, while the zoomed in plot is linear. We iterate the simulation for 20000000 times to obtain the simulation results. To simplify the plot, we only plot the simulation results only for odd
N
, i.e., we do not show the null hit probability at even
N
. Similarly, we choose
\mathrm{P^{(S)}}[N]=\mathrm{P^{(S)}}[N-1]
for even
N
to prevent zigzagging of the theoretical results.

Fig.
1
illustrates the agreement between the simulations and (
20
). As expected, for small
N
for which the Stirling approximation is inaccurate, (
20
) does not return particularly accurate results. However, for large
N
, our theory is in unison with the simulation results. Also evident from Fig.
1
, (
15
) gives the exact incidence probability. Since binomial terms grow exponentially, (
15
) has limited use. Regardless, we can use both (
15
) and (
20
) for small and large
N
respectively to get an accurate and robust result.

Note that the second incidence probability is given by a Pareto distribution with a scale parameter
x_{m}=(1/2\pi)
and a shape parameter
\alpha=0.5
, where (
18
) and (
20
) are the the cumulative and probability distribution functions respectively. Pareto distribution with such parameters is heavy-tailed, i.e., the expectation value of the second incidence time step is infinite. Although seemingly counter-intuitive, this is expected, as
\mathcal{B}
makes larger and larger swings into
-\infty
direction as long as
\mathcal{B}
survives.

IV
Time to Second Incidence in a Finite Domain

Due to the expected second hitting time being infinity, the results that we obtained in Sec.
III
does not offer much help in modelling realistic systems, as
\mathcal{B}
traversing to and from either
-\infty
or
\infty
is not realistic. In finance, we know that price of a stock would crash at zero. In MC, IcM would reflect from the sides of the
finite
container it is in.

To find the second incidence rate for a realistic system, as we describe in Sec.
II
, we move to a finite system with boundaries located at
x=\pm H
. Since we focus on the second incidence, we assume that the first incidence already happened and
\mathcal{B}
is now located at
L-\Delta{x}
. We also choose the boundary at
x=\pm H
are absorbing as the second incidence occurs when
\mathcal{B}
is incident on
x=\pm H
, and any further incidences are no longer second incidence but higher order incidences.

Note that we choose
L-\Delta{x}
instead of
L-\Delta{x}/2
, as described in Sec.
II
. The reasoning behind our choice is that simulations cannot capture half time steps, i.e., starting at
L-\Delta{x}/2
,
\mathcal{B}
is incidence at
x=H
at
\Delta{t}/2
with a probability of
0.5
. However, the computer simulations cannot capture the half time steps. Thus, we altered the
\mathcal{B}[0]
to fit the simulation results to the theory.

Illustration of the reflection principle, as outlined in
[
24
]
. Each subfigure shows one of the countably infinite paths
\mathcal{B}
can use, starting at
x=L-\Delta{x}
and ending at
x=x_{1}
.

Using the start point and the end point of
\mathcal{B}
does not divulge enough information whether
\mathcal{B}
were incident on the boundaries. It may return back to the domain after it crossed the boundary at one, or many, incidences. To account for the crossings, we use the reflection principle
[
24
]
. We illustrate the reflection principle in Fig.
2
. Among the four possible paths
\mathcal{B}
might take, only the path outlined in Fig.
2
(a) does not cross any of the boundaries. In Fig.
2
(b),
\mathcal{B}
crosses the boundary at
x=H
, in (c) at
x=-H
, and in (d) first at
x=-H
and at
x=H
, even though they all conclude at the same point in space.

To discount paths involving boundary crossings, we adjust (11) of
[
24
]
as described in Sec. 4(b) of the same work. The probability that the time steps for the next hit is smaller than
M
is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathrm{P}&[N<M]=1-\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}\times\\
&\phantom{ooo}\left[\Phi\left(\frac{2iH+1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{(2i-2)H+1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\right],\end{split}

(21)

where
\Phi(.)
is the standard normal cumulative distribution function.

Assuming
H\gg 1
, we use Taylor expansion on (
21
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}

\displaystyle[N<M]\!=\!1-\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}\!\times\!\left[\Phi\left(\frac{2iH}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\!+\!\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\phi\left(\frac{2iH}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\phantom{oooo}\left.-\Phi\left(\frac{(2i-2)H}{\sqrt{N}}\right)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\phi\left(\frac{(2i-2)H}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\right],

(22)

where
\phi(.)
is the standard normal probability distribution function.

We first manipulate the
\Phi(.)
terms of (
22
) as,

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\!\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}\times\left[\Phi\left(\frac{2iH}{\sqrt{N}}\right)-\Phi\left(\frac{(2i-2)H}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\right],

(23)

\displaystyle=\lim_{Q\rightarrow\infty}2\sum_{i=-Q}^{Q}(-1)^{i}\Phi\left(\frac{2iH}{\sqrt{N}}\right),

(24)

\displaystyle=\lim_{Q\rightarrow\infty}1\!+\!2\!\sum_{i=1}^{Q}(-1)^{i}\!\left[\Phi\left(\frac{2iH}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\!+\!\Phi\left(\frac{-2iH}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\right].

(25)

Since we know that
\Phi(\alpha)+\Phi(-\alpha)=1
, (
23
) clearly diverges. However, we realise that
\Phi(.)
terms vanish as long as the limits of integration are symmetric. Thus, we rearrange (
24
) to (
25
) where each term sums to zero, allowing us to calculate the Cesaro sum of the series as zero. After
\Phi(.)
terms vanish, (
22
) becomes

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}[N<M]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=-\infty}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}\left[\phi\left(\frac{2iH}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\!+\!\phi\left(\frac{-2iH}{\sqrt{N}}\right)\right],

(26)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\phi(0)}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{4}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}\phi\left(\frac{2iH}{\sqrt{N}}\right),

(27)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi N}}\vartheta_{4}(0;q),

(28)

where

q=\exp\left(-\frac{2H^{2}}{N}\right)

(29)

and
\vartheta_{4}(z;q)
is the fourth Jacobi Theta function of the form

\vartheta_{4}(z;q)=1+2\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(-q)^{i^{2}}\cos(2iz).

(30)

As we stated in Sec.
III
,
\mathcal{B}
cannot be incident on the boundary at even
N
, however (
28
) returns a positive value for both even and odd
N
. Thus, to ensure that
\mathrm{Pr}[M>N]=\mathrm{Pr}[M>N-1]
for even
N
, we modify (
28
) as

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}[N<M]=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi(N+\eta)}}\vartheta_{4}\left(0;\exp\left(\frac{-2H^{2}}{N+\eta}\right)\right),

(31)

where

\displaystyle\eta\equiv(N+1)\hskip-5.69054pt\pmod{2}.

(32)

With Fig.
3
, we present the parity of the simulation results and (
31
). The discrepancy for small
N
, clearly visible in Fig.
3
(a) is stemmed from the fact that the starting point for (
31
) uses the Brownian Motion defined with (
5
), instead of (
2
). However, for large
N
, both models converge as expected. Thus, (
31
) provides a very accurate picture to the probability of not hitting the boundary.

The probability of not hitting vs. number of timesteps following the first hit for

.

We find the rate of second incidence as the derivative of (
31
), i.e.,

\displaystyle R_{2}[N]

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}N}\mathrm{Pr}[N<M],

(33)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi N^{3}}}\vartheta_{4}(0;q)+\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi N}}\frac{\mathrm{d}q}{\mathrm{d}N}\frac{\mathrm{d}\vartheta_{4}(0;q)}{\mathrm{d}q},

(34)

\displaystyle=\!\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi N^{3}}}\left[-\vartheta_{4}(0;q)\!+\!\frac{4H^{2}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{i}i^{2}q^{i^{2}}\right].

(35)

Although (
35
) does not have a closed expression, we know that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}q}\vartheta_{4}(0;q)=-\gamma\frac{1}{q}\frac{\mathrm{d^{2}}}{\mathrm{d}z^{2}}\vartheta_{4}(z;q)\Bigg|_{z=0},

(36)

which is evaluated very efficiently by computers. Note that
\gamma
is a constant that depends on the exact definition of
\vartheta_{4}(q;z)
. With the definition in (
30
),
\gamma=1/4
, however if the summands are in the form
(-q)^{i^{2}}\cos(2\pi iz)
, then
\gamma=1/(4\pi^{2})
.

V
Corrections for the Second Incidence Rate with Two Boundaries for Small
N

As we discuss in Sec.
IV
, the accuracy of (
35
) is limited to large
N
. In this section, we solve the second incidence problem for small
N
.

In this section, we find
\mathrm{P^{(S)}_{x^{\prime}}}
, the probability of
\mathcal{B}
not hitting the boundary in a semi-infinite domain, if
\mathcal{B}[0]=H-x^{\prime}
. In other words, we extend (
12
) to the cases where
\mathcal{B}
is not adjacent to the boundary at the start. To this end, we first find a recursive relation and then a closed-form expression. Due to the size of their proofs, we state them as theorems and leave the proofs to the Appendix.

Theorem 1
.

(Extended Ballot Theorem)
Let
\mathcal{B}
be a Brownian Motion with
\mathcal{B}[0]=H-x^{\prime}
. The probability that
\mathcal{B}
does not hit
x=H
after
N
time steps can be found with the recursive relation

\displaystyle\hskip-2.84526pt\mathrm{P^{(S)}_{x^{\prime}}}[M>N]\!

\displaystyle=\!2\mathrm{P^{(S)}_{x^{\prime}-1}}[M>N+1]\!-\!\mathrm{P^{(S)}_{x^{\prime}-2}}[M>N].

(37)

While (
37
) is sufficient to find
\mathrm{P^{(S)}_{x^{\prime}}}[M>N]
, it is not computationally efficient for large
x^{\prime}
. When
\mathcal{B}[0]
is very close to one boundary, it is far from the other one. In other words, small
x^{\prime}
implies a large
2H-x^{\prime}
, which necessitates a computationally efficient method. Hence, we derive a closed form expression for (
37
) with Theorem
2
.

Theorem 2
.

The probability that
\mathcal{B}
with
\mathcal{B}[0]=H-x
does not hit
x=H
after
N
time steps is

\displaystyle\mathrm{P^{(S)}_{x^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle[M>N]=\frac{2^{\xi^{\prime}}(\xi+1)^{\xi^{\prime}}(-1)^{\xi}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\Delta+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\Delta+1\right)}\times

(38)

\displaystyle\phantom{ooo}{}_{3}F_{2}\left(-\xi,\xi+\xi^{\prime}+1,\Delta+\frac{1}{2};\frac{1}{2}+\xi^{\prime},\Delta+1;1\right),

where

\Gamma
is the Gamma function and
{}_{3}F_{2}
is the hypergeometric function of the form

{}_{3}F_{2}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3};b_{1},b_{2};f)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a_{1})_{i}(a_{3})_{i}(a_{3})_{i}}{(b_{1})_{i}(b_{2})_{i}}\frac{f^{i}}{i!},

(39)

with
(a)_{i}

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we derive expression for the second incidence rate, higher incidence rate and for the absorption rate in a Brownian Molecular Communication Channel. We verify each of our critical findings with Monte Carlo simulations.

Currently, our results are directly applicable to cuboid volumes with opposing partially absorbing boundaries. To address this shortcoming, we are already extending our work to three dimensional volumes with some or all boundaries being partially absorbing. The main limitation of our work is the
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
complexity, limiting the scalability of our results. To remedy this, we plan to develop an approximate expression that is computed in
\mathcal{O}(N)
and accurate for small absorption rates. Finally, we plan to apply our findings to Synaptic Molecular Communication in a future work.
