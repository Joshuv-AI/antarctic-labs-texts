Abstract




These lecture notes were written for CIRM SMF School

1.
Introduction

There are two main approaches to solve inverse
coefficient determination
problems for wave equations: the Boundary Control method and an approach based on geometric optics.
These notes focus on the Boundary Control method, but we will have a brief look at the geometric optics as well.

1.1.
Boundary Control method

The Boundary Control method can be used to solve several coefficient determination problems for wave equations.
The method originates from
[
2
]
, and it allows for building a rather comprehensive theory of inverse problems of coefficient determination type. Indeed, many such problems
can be reduced to inverse boundary spectral problems,
which again can be reduced to coefficient determination problems for wave equations. This is the case for inverse problems for heat and non-stationary Schroödinger equations
[
10
]
,
as well as for several time-fractional
[
12
]
and space-fractional
[
7
]
equations. Inverse problems for linear elliptic equations on a wave guide
[
5
]
, and some (very special) non-linear elliptic equations
[
4
]
are also covered by this theory.

The method has been generalized for symmetric systems of wave equations with scalar leading order
[
13
]
and for wave equations on Lorentzian manifolds satisfying a curvature bound
[
1
]
.

In these notes we give an introductory exposition of the method. For an in-depth presentation, we refer to the monograph
[
11
]
.

1.2.
Geometric optics based approach

Geometric optics constructions are well-suited for solving inverse problems with time-dependent coefficients. In the case of time-independent coefficients, this approach has not reached the same geometric generality as the Boundary Control method.

The geometric optics based approach goes back to
[
19
]
. It has been generalized for wave equations on real analytic Lorentzian manifolds satisfying a convex foliation condition
[
18
,
17
]
, as well as for certain static and stationary Lorentzian manifolds
[
8
,
9
]
.

At the moment it is not clear which of the two methods works better in the Lorentzian context. This context is natural since it respects the idea that coordinates do not exist a priori in nature, and hence should play no role in the mathematical formulation of physical models.

2.
Boundary Control method in
1+1
dimensions

Consider the following initial-boundary value problem

(1)

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}+q)u=0;&\text{for }(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times(0,1);\\
\left.u\right|_{x=0}=f;\\
\left.u\right|_{x=1}=0;\\
\left.u\right|_{t=0}=\left.\partial_{t}u\right|_{t=0}=0,\end{cases}

where
f
is a boundary source,
q
is a potential, and
\mathbb{R}^{+}
stands for the interval
(0,\infty)
.
For simplicity, we will assume that
q\in C^{\infty}(0,1)
, a smooth function up to the end points of the interval
[0,1]
, and that
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
, a smooth compactly supported function. Then (
1
) has a unique solution that satisfies
u\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+}\times(0,1))
, see e.g. the theory in Section 7.2 of
[
6
]
.

We write
u^{f}=u
for the solution to (
1
) to emphasize its dependence on the source
f
.
Define the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map

(2)

\Lambda f=\left.\partial_{x}u^{f}\right|_{x=0}.

We will study the following inverse problem: determine the potential
q
given the operator
\Lambda
.

2.1.
Finite speed of propagation in the case
q=0

The finite speed of propagation property for the acoustic wave equation plays a central role in both direct and inverse theory.
To illustrate the property in the simplest possible case, let us
consider (
1
) with
q=0
.
We set

\mathcal{E}(t,x)=|\partial_{t}u(t,x)|^{2}+|\partial_{x}u(t,x)|^{2}.

1
Conservation of energy.

Suppose that
u\in C^{2}(\overline{{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times(0,1)})
satisfies

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})u=0\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{+}\times(0,1)\\
\left.u\right|_{x=0}=\left.u\right|_{x=1}=0.\end{cases}

Then the global energy

\displaystyle E(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{E}(t,x)dx

satisfies
\partial_{t}E(t)=0
for all
t>0
.

Proof.

We write,

\partial_{t}E(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}(t,x)dx\\
=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\partial_{t}u(t,x)\partial_{t}^{2}u(t,x)+\partial_{x}u(t,x)\partial_{t}\partial_{x}u(t,x)\right)dx.

By integration by parts, we obtain

\partial_{t}E(t)=\left[\partial_{x}u(t,x)\partial_{t}u(t,x)\right]^{1}_{0}+\int_{0}^{1}\left(\partial_{t}^{2}u(t,x)-\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x)\right)\partial_{t}u(t,x)dx.

The first term is zero since
u(t,0)=u(t,1)=0
, and the second term is zero since
u
satisfies the wave equation.
∎

2
Finite speed of propagation.

Suppose that
u\in C^{2}(\overline{{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\times(0,1)})
satisfies

(3)

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2})u=0\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{+}\times(0,1)\\
\left.u\right|_{x=1}=0.\end{cases}

Then the local energy

\displaystyle E(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{t}^{1}\mathcal{E}(t,x)dx

satisfies
\partial_{t}E(t)\leq 0
for all
t>0
.

Proof.

We write, by using Leibniz integral rule,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(t,t)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{t}^{1}\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}(t,x)dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(t,t)+\int_{t}^{1}\left(\partial_{t}u(t,x)\partial_{t}^{2}u(t,x)+\partial_{x}u(t,x)\partial_{t}\partial_{x}u(t,x)\right)dx.

An integration by parts gives

\partial_{t}E(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(t,t)+\left[\partial_{x}u(t,x)\partial_{t}u(t,x)\right]^{1}_{t}\\
+\int_{t}^{1}\left(\partial_{t}^{2}u(t,x)-\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x)\right)\partial_{t}u(t,x)dx.

Since
u
is the solution of the wave equation the last integral is
0
. Moreover, since
u(t,1)=0
, the second term is
0
at
1
, so that

\partial_{t}E(t)=-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{E}(t,t)-\partial_{t}u(t,t)\partial_{x}u(t,t)=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_{t}u(t,t)+\partial_{x}u(t,t)\right)^{2}\leq 0.

∎

In particular, if
E(0)=0
then
E(t)=0
for all
t>0
.
So if
u
and
\partial_{t}u
vanish initially on
(0,1)
then at time
t
they vanish for
x\in(t,1)
.
To see that this statement is optimal, observe that

\displaystyle u(t,x)=f(t-x)-f(t+x-2)

is a solution to (
3
) for any
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Moreover, if
f(r)
vanishes for
r<0
, then
E(0)=0
.
But
u
does not vanish near the line
t=x
if
f(r)
does not vanish for
r>0
near
r=0
.

2.2.
Finite speed of propagation for general
q

Let us now consider a wave equation with a potential as in (
1
), and prove a finite speed of propagation result in the very natural setting of a diamond, see the set
K
in Theorem
3
below.
The set
K
is visualized in Figure
1
.

It is also possible to study the case where boundary conditions are posed, say at
x=0
and
x=1
as before, and where the diamond intersects the boundary of
\mathbb{R}^{+}\times(0,1)
. However, we leave this case to the reader.

3
Finite speed of propagation.

Let
X>0
and define

K=\{(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}:X-|x|\geq|t|\}.

Let
q\in L^{\infty}(K)
and let
u\in C^{2}(K)
be a solution of the equation

\begin{cases}\left(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}+q(t,x)\right)u=0,&\text{on }K;\\
\left.u\right|_{t=0}=\left.\partial_{t}u\right|_{t=0}=0,&\text{on }(-X,X).\end{cases}

Then
u=0
in
K
.

Proof.

We will consider the case
t>0
. The case
t<0
is analogous and we omit its proof.
Let us set
I(t)=[-X+t,X-t]
and define the energy

E(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{I(t)}\mathcal{E}(t,x)dx.

Using Leibniz integral rule, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E(t)=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(|\partial_{t}u(t,X-t)|^{2}+|\partial_{x}u(t,X-t)|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(|\partial_{t}u(t,-(X-t))|^{2}+|\partial_{x}u(t,-(X-t))|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\int_{I(t)}\left(\partial_{t}u(t,x)\partial_{t}^{2}u(t,x)+\partial_{x}u(t,x)\partial_{t}\partial_{x}u(t,x)\right)dx.

An integration by parts gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E(t)=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(|\partial_{t}u(t,X-t)|^{2}+|\partial_{x}u(t,X-t)|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\left(|\partial_{t}u(t,-(X-t))|^{2}+|\partial_{x}u(t,-(X-t))|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\partial_{t}u(t,X-t)\partial_{x}u(t,X-t)-\partial_{t}u(t,-(X-t))\partial_{x}u(t,-(X-t))

\displaystyle+\int_{I(t)}\partial_{t}u(t,x)\left(\partial_{t}^{2}u(t,x)-\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x)\right)dx.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E(t)=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\bigg(|\partial_{t}u(t,X-t)|-|\partial_{x}u(t,X-t)|\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\bigg(|\partial_{t}u(t,-(X-t))|+|\partial_{x}u(t,-(X-t))|\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle+\int_{I(t)}\partial_{t}u(t,x)\left(\partial_{t}^{2}u(t,x)-\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x)\right)dx.

Since the first two terms are non-positive, it follows

\partial_{t}E(t)\leq-\int_{I(t)}\partial_{t}u(t,x)q(t,x)u(t,x)dx\\
+\int_{I(t)}\partial_{t}u(t,x)\left(\partial_{t}^{2}u(t,x)-\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x)+q(t,x)u(t,x)\right)dx.

Let us use the notation

P=\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}+q.

The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality imply

E(t)=E(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{t}E(s)ds\\
\leq E(0)+2\int_{0}^{t}\int_{I(s)}\left(|\partial_{t}u(s,x)|^{2}+|q(s,x)u(s,x)|^{2}+|Pu(s,x)|^{2}\right)dxds.

Hence,

(4)

E(t)\leq E(0)+C\left(\int_{0}^{t}E(s)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{I(s)}\left(|u(s,x)|^{2}+|Pu(s,x)|^{2}\right)dxds\right),

where
C
is a constant depending on
q
and
X
, which will change from line to line.

Let us now take care of the
|u|^{2}
term caused by the potential
q
. We will use arguments close to the proof in
[
14
, Section 4.2]
.
For
x\in\mathbb{R}
fixed, we know

u(t,x)=u(0,x)+\int_{0}^{t}\partial_{t}u(s,x)ds.

Let us square it, and then, use Cauchy’s inequality and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to obtain

(5)

\displaystyle|u(t,x)|^{2}\leq 2|u(0,x)|^{2}+2t\int_{0}^{t}|\partial_{t}u(s,x)|^{2}ds.

Next, we define

z(t)=E(t)+\int_{I(t)}|u(t,x)|^{2}dx.

Using (
4
) and (
5
), we estimate

z(t)\leq E(0)+C\left(\int_{0}^{t}E(s)ds+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{I(s)}\left(|u(s,x)|^{2}+|Pu(s,x)|^{2}\right)dxds\right)\\
+2\int_{I(t)}|u(0,x)|^{2}dx+2t\int_{I(t)}\int_{0}^{t}|\partial_{t}u(s,x)|^{2}dsdx.

Since we consider
t\in[0,X]
, and since
I(t)\subset I(s)
for
s\in[0,t]
, the last term can be estimated from above by
2X\int_{0}^{t}E(s)ds
. Therefore, we have

z(t)\leq Cz(0)+C\int_{0}^{t}z(s)ds+C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{I(s)}|Pu(s,x)|^{2}dxds.

Using the Gronwall’s inequality in the integral form, see e.g.
[
6
, Appendix B.2.k]
, we obtain

(6)

\displaystyle z(t)\leq C\left(z(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{I(s)}|Pu(s,x)|^{2}dxds\right).

Since
z(0)=0
and
Pu=0
, we conclude that
z(t)=0
for
t\in[0,X]
. Recalling the definition of the function
z
, we see that
u(t,x)=0
for
t\in[0,X]
and
x\in I(t)
.
∎

on

2.3.
Approximate controllability

Let us recall that
u^{f}
is the solution of (
1
), when the boundary source is given by
f
.
The following result is obtained by transposing unique continuation.

1
Approximate controllability.

Let
0<s<T\leq 1
and suppose that
s\leq 1
. Then the set

\mathcal{B}(s,T)=\left\{u^{f}(T,\cdot):f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(T-s,T)\right\}

is a dense subset of
L^{2}(0,s)
.

Here we use the identification

\displaystyle L^{2}(0,s)=\{\phi\in L^{2}(0,1):\supp(\phi)=(0,1)\}.

Note that due to finite speed of propagation the function
u^{f}(T,\cdot)
is supported on
[0,s]
. Hence we may view
\mathcal{B}(s,T)
as a subspace of
L^{2}(0,s)
.

Proof.

To show the density, it is enough to prove that
\mathcal{B}(s,T)^{\perp}=\{0\}
, where

\mathcal{B}(s,T)^{\perp}=\left\{v\in L^{2}(0,s):(v,u)_{L^{2}(0,s)}=0\text{ for all }u\in\mathcal{B}(s,T)\right\}.

Let
h\in\mathcal{B}(s,T)^{\perp}
. Let
\omega
be the solution of the equation

\begin{cases}\left(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}+q(t,x)\right)\omega(t,x)=0&\text{on }(0,T)\times(0,1);\\
\left.\omega\right|_{x=0,1}=0;\\
\left.\omega\right|_{t=T}=0;\\
\left.\partial_{t}\omega\right|_{t=T}=h.\end{cases}

Let
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(T-s,T)
. An integration by parts, together with the boundary and initial conditions for
u^{f}
and
\omega
, give

0=\left(\left(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}+q\right)u^{f},\omega\right)_{L^{2}((0,T)\times(0,1))}-\left(u^{f},\left(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}+q\right)\omega\right)_{L^{2}((0,T)\times(0,1))}\\
=-\int_{0}^{1}u^{f}(T,x)\partial_{t}\omega(T,x)dx-\int_{0}^{T}u^{f}(t,0)\partial_{x}\omega(t,0)dt.

Since
\left.\partial_{t}\omega\right|_{t=T}=h\in\mathcal{B}(s,T)^{\perp}
, the first term of the right-hand side is zero, so that

0=\int_{0}^{T}f(t)\partial_{x}\omega(t,0)dt,

which is true for arbitrary
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(T-s,T)
. Hence,
\partial_{x}\omega(t,0)=0
on
(T-s,T)
.

Let
\tilde{\omega}
be the odd extension of
\omega
to
(0,2T)\times(0,1)
, more precisely,

\tilde{\omega}(t,x)=\begin{cases}\omega(t,x)&\text{if }t\leq T\\
-\omega(2T-t,x)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then
\tilde{\omega}
satisfies

\begin{cases}\left(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{x}^{2}+q(t,x)\right)\tilde{\omega}(t,x)=0&\text{on }(0,2T)\times(0,1);\\
\left.\tilde{\omega}\right|_{x=0}=0&\text{on }(0,2T);\\
\left.\partial_{x}\tilde{\omega}\right|_{x=0}=0&\text{on }(T-s,T+s).\end{cases}

By unique continuation, see Theorem
4
, we obtain that
\tilde{\omega}=0
on

\{(t,x)\in(T-s,T+s)\times(0,1):|x|\leq s-|T-t|\}.

In particular,
\partial_{t}\omega(T,x)=h(x)=0
on
(0,s)
, so that
\mathcal{B}(s,T)^{\perp}=\{0\}
.
∎

2.4.
An integration by parts trick for the inverse problem

Let us define the function

W_{f,h}(t,s)=(u^{f}(t,\cdot),u^{h}(s,\cdot))_{L^{2}(0,1)}.

Then the following holds

2
.

Let
f
,
h\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
. The operator
\Lambda
determines
W_{f,h}(t,t)
for all
t>0
.

Proof.

Since
u^{f}
and
u^{h}
are solutions of (
1
), there holds

(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{s}^{2})W_{f,h}(t,s)=\left(\partial_{x}^{2}u^{f}(t,\cdot)-qu^{f}(t,\cdot),u^{h}(s,\cdot)\right)_{L^{2}(0,1)}\\
-\left(u^{f}(t,\cdot),\partial_{x}^{2}u^{h}(s,\cdot)-qu^{h}(s,\cdot)\right)_{L^{2}(0,1)}.

The two terms with
q
cancel out.
Further, integration by parts gives

(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{s}^{2})W_{f,h}(t,s)=\partial_{x}u^{f}(t,1)u^{h}(s,1)-\partial_{x}u^{f}(t,0)u^{h}(s,0)\\
-u^{f}(t,1)\partial_{x}u^{h}(s,1)+u^{f}(t,0)\partial_{x}u^{h}(s,0).

Boundary conditions for
u^{f}
and
u^{h}
give

(7)

(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{s}^{2})W_{f,h}(t,s)=f(t)\Lambda h(s)-\Lambda f(t)h(s).

Let us denote the right-hand side of (
7
) by
F(t,s)
, then
W_{f,h}
solves

\begin{cases}(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{s}^{2})W_{f,h}(t,s)=F(t,s)&\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{+},\\
W_{f,h}(0,s)=\partial_{t}W_{f,h}(0,s)=0.\end{cases}

Hence,
W_{f,h}
is determined by
F
, and consequently, it is determined by
\Lambda
.
(When evaluating
W_{f,h}(t,t)
, we do not need to impose any boundary condition at
s=0
due to finite speed of propagation. Nonetheless, we could impose, for example,
W_{f,h}(t,0)=0
as this holds in view of the initial conditions satisfied by
u^{h}(s,x)
.)
∎

Consider two potentials
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
. Let us write
u_{1}^{f}
and
u_{2}^{f}
for the solutions of (
1
) with
q
replaced by
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
, respectively. The corresponding Dirichlet-to-Neumann operators, see (
2
), are denoted by
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
.
It follows from the above lemma that

\displaystyle(u_{1}^{f}(t,\cdot),u_{1}^{h}(t,\cdot))_{L^{2}(0,1)}=(u_{2}^{f}(t,\cdot),u_{2}^{h}(t,\cdot))_{L^{2}(0,1)}

whenever
\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda_{2}
.

2.5.
Solution to the inverse problem

We will show that
\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda_{2}
implies
q_{1}=q_{2}
.
For a set
S\subset\mathbb{R}
, we define the indicator function

\displaystyle 1_{S}(x)=\begin{cases}1&x\in S,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

3
.

Assume that
\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda_{2}
.
Let
0<s<T
, let
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
and let

\displaystyle f_{j}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(T-s,T),\quad j=1,2,\dots,

be a sequence such that

(8)

u_{1}^{f_{j}}(T,\cdot)\rightarrow 1_{(0,s)}u_{1}^{f}(T,\cdot)\qquad\text{in }L^{2}(0,1).

Then

u_{2}^{f_{j}}(T,\cdot)\rightarrow 1_{(0,s)}u_{2}^{f}(T,\cdot)\qquad\text{in }L^{2}(0,1).

Proof.

Let
\tilde{f}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(T-s,T)
and let
k=1,2
. We compute

\displaystyle\left\|u_{k}^{\tilde{f}}(T,\cdot)-u_{k}^{f}(T,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}=\left\|u_{k}^{\tilde{f}}(T,\cdot)-1_{(0,s)}u_{k}^{f}(T,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}

(9)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\|\left(1_{(0,s)}-1\right)u_{k}^{f}(T,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\left(u_{k}^{\tilde{f}}(T,\cdot)-1_{(0,s)}u_{k}^{f}(T,\cdot),\left(1_{(0,s)}-1\right)u_{k}^{f}(T,\cdot)\right)_{L^{2}(0,1)}.

Due to finite speed of propagation, we know that
u_{k}^{\tilde{f}}(T,\cdot)
is supported in
(0,s)
. Therefore, the functions

u_{k}^{\tilde{f}}(T,\cdot)-1_{(0,s)}u_{k}^{f}(T,\cdot)\qquad\left(1_{(0,s)}-1\right)u_{k}^{f}(T,\cdot)

have disjoint supports, so that (
2.5
) becomes

(10)

\left\|u_{k}^{\tilde{f}}(T,\cdot)-u_{k}^{f}(T,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}=\left\|u_{k}^{\tilde{f}}(T,\cdot)-1_{(0,s)}u_{k}^{f}(T,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}\\
+\left\|\left(1_{(0,s)}-1\right)u_{k}^{f}(T,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}.

By approximate controllability,

(11)

\displaystyle\inf_{\tilde{f}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(T-s,T)}\left\|u_{k}^{\tilde{f}}(T,\cdot)-u_{k}^{f}(T,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}=\left\|\left(1_{(0,s)}-1\right)u_{k}^{f}(T,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}.

Let
f_{j}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(T-s,T)
satisfy (
8
).
Then

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\left\|u_{1}^{f_{j}}(T,\cdot)-u_{1}^{f}(T,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}=\inf_{\tilde{f}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(T-s,T)}\left\|u_{1}^{\tilde{f}}(T,\cdot)-u_{1}^{f}(T,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}.

Due to Lemma
2
,

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\left\|u_{2}^{f_{j}}(T,\cdot)-u_{2}^{f}(T,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}=\inf_{\tilde{f}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(T-s,T)}\left\|u_{2}^{\tilde{f}}(T,\cdot)-u_{2}^{f}(T,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}.

Using (
10
) and (
11
) we see that

\displaystyle\left\|u_{2}^{f_{j}}(T,\cdot)-1_{(0,s)}u_{2}^{f}(T,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=\left\|u_{2}^{f_{j}}(T,\cdot)-u_{2}^{f}(T,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}-\left\|\left(1_{(0,s)}-1\right)u_{2}^{f}(T,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}\to 0.

∎

1
.

Assume that
\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda_{2}
. Let
f,h\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
and let
0<s<T
. Then

(12)

\displaystyle\left(1_{(0,s)}u_{1}^{f}(T,\cdot),u_{1}^{h}(T,\cdot)\right)_{L^{2}(0,1)}=\left(1_{(0,s)}u_{2}^{f}(T,\cdot),u_{2}^{h}(T,\cdot)\right)_{L^{2}(0,1)}.

Proof.

By Lemma

, there is a sequence
\{f_{j}\}\subset C_{0}^{\infty}(T-s,T)
such that

in

for

. By Lemma

this holds also for

. In view of Lemma

, we have

\left(1_{(0,s)}u_{1}^{f}(T,\cdot),u_{1}^{h}(T,\cdot)\right)_{L^{2}(0,1)}=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\left(u_{1}^{f_{j}}(T,\cdot),u_{1}^{h}(T,\cdot)\right)_{L^{2}(0,1)}\\
=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\left(u_{2}^{f_{j}}(T,\cdot),u_{2}^{h}(T,\cdot)\right)_{L^{2}(0,1)}=\left(1_{(0,s)}u_{2}^{f}(T,\cdot),u_{2}^{h}(T,\cdot)\right)_{L^{2}(0,1)}.

∎

We will need the following lemma. Its proof is given in Section

.

4
.

Let
T\geq 1
, then for any

there is

such that

.

Now we are ready to prove the main result of this section

5
.

If
\Lambda_{1}=\Lambda_{2}
, then

.

Proof.

We consider
T>1
and let
x\in(0,1)
. Let us differentiate the left-hand side of (

\partial_{x}\left(1_{(0,x)}(\cdot)u_{1}^{f}(T,\cdot),u_{1}^{h}(T,\cdot)\right)_{L^{2}(0,1)}\\

Therefore, by (

Due to Lemma

,
for each

3.
Boundary Control method on a Riemannian manifold

The main advantage of the Boundary Control method is that it works in general geometric settings.
We refer to
[
16
]
for an introduction to Riemannian geometry. A reader, who is not interesting in geometry, may simply replace
(M,g)
by a domain
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a smooth boundary
\partial M
.

Let
(M,g)
be a Riemannian manifold with boundary and
q\in C^{\infty}(M)
. Consider the wave equation

(15)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\partial_{t}^{2}u-\Delta_{g}u+qu=0&\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{+}\times M,\\
\left.u\right|_{x\in\partial M}=f,\\
\left.u\right|_{t=0}=\left.\partial_{t}u\right|_{t=0}=0.\end{cases}

Here
\Delta_{g}
is the Laplacian. In local coordinates, it is given as follows

\Delta_{g}u=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\textrm{det}(g)}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\partial_{i}\left(\sqrt{\textrm{det}(g)}g^{ij}\partial_{j}u\right).

In particular, in the case that
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the above expression gives the usual Laplacian, since
g
is the identity matrix.

We define the Dirichlet-to-Neumann map

\displaystyle\Lambda:C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\partial M)\rightarrow C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\partial M)

as follows

\Lambda f=\left.\partial_{\nu}u^{f}\right|_{\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\partial M},

where
u^{f}
is the solution in of (
15
).
Here
\nu
is the exterior unit normal on
\partial M
.
We will study the inverse problem: determine the potential
q
given
\Lambda
and the Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
.

The finite speed of propagation will be formulated in terms of the distance function
d_{g}
on
(M,g)
. That is, it takes the time
d_{g}(x,y)
for a wave to propagate from a point
x\in M
to a point
y\in M
.
We recall the definition of the distance on Riemannian manifold.

If
(M,g)
is is a connected Riemannian manifold and
x,y\in M
, the
distance between
x
and
y
is defined by

d_{g}(x,y)=\inf\{l(\gamma):\gamma\in C_{x,y}\},

where

\displaystyle C_{x,y}=\{\gamma:[0,\ell]\rightarrow M:\gamma

is a piecewise smooth, continuous curve and

\displaystyle\gamma(0)=x,\;\gamma(\ell)=y\}

and

l(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{\ell}\sqrt{g(\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))}dt.

Here
g
is viewed as a bilinear form.
When
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is convex,
d_{g}
is the usual Euclidean distance.

Before entering into the multidimensional geometric case, let us see how non-constant speed of sound gives the speed of propagation in
1+1
dimensions.

3.1.
Finite speed of propagation with nonconstant speed of sound

Let
c\in C^{\infty}([0,1])
and suppose that
c(x)>0
for all
x\in[0,1]
.
Consider a solution
u
to

(16)

\displaystyle(\partial_{t}^{2}-c^{2}\partial_{x}^{2})u=0\quad\text{on }\mathbb{R}^{+}\times(0,1),

which satisfies

\left.u\right|_{x=1}=0.

We set

\mathcal{E}(t,x)=c^{-2}(x)|\partial_{t}u(t,x)|^{2}+|\partial_{x}u(t,x)|^{2}.

We want to find an increasing function
r\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with
r(0)=0
such that the energy

E(t)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{r(t)}^{1}\mathcal{E}(t,x)dx

satisfies

(17)

\partial_{t}E(t)\leq 0.

The slower
r
increases, the better finite speed of propagation result we will get.

We write, by using Leibniz integral rule,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}E(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}r^{\prime}(t)\mathcal{E}(t,r(t))+\frac{1}{2}\int_{r(t)}^{1}\partial_{t}\mathcal{E}(t,x)dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}r^{\prime}(t)\mathcal{E}(t,r(t))+\int_{r(t)}^{1}\left(c^{-2}(x)\partial_{t}u(t,x)\partial_{t}^{2}u(t,x)+\partial_{x}u(t,x)\partial_{tx}u(t,x)\right)dx.

Integration by parts gives

\partial_{t}E(t)=-\frac{1}{2}r^{\prime}(t)\mathcal{E}(t,r(t))+\left[\partial_{x}u(t,x)\partial_{t}u(t,x)\right]^{1}_{r(t)}\\
+\int_{r(t)}^{1}\left(c^{-2}\partial_{t}^{2}u(t,x)+\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x)\right)\partial_{t}u(t,x)dx.

Since
u
is the solution of the wave equation the last integral is
0
.
Moreover, due to the boundary condition, the second term is
0
at
x=1
, so that

\partial_{t}E(t)=-\frac{1}{2}r^{\prime}(t)\mathcal{E}(t,r(t))-\left.\left(\partial_{t}u(t,x)\partial_{x}u(t,x)\right)\right|_{x=r(t)}.

Note that
r^{\prime}>0
since
r
is increasing. Therefore, using a simple inequality

2xy\leq\alpha x^{2}+\frac{1}{\alpha}y^{2},\qquad\text{for }\alpha,x,y>0,

we know that (
17
) holds if

\frac{1}{r^{\prime}(t)}=r^{\prime}(t)c^{-2}(r(t)),

or equivalently,

(18)

r^{\prime}(t)=c(r(t)).

This equation is solvable. Indeed, consider the following function

\rho(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{1}{c(y)}dy.

Since
\rho
is a strictly increasing function, its inverse function exists, so we can set

r(t)=\rho^{-1}(t).

It is easy to check that this function indeed satisfies (
18
). Observe that if
c=1
identically, we obtain
r(t)=t
.

The function
\rho
is the travel time between
0
and
x
: the time necessary for perturbation at
0
to reach
x
. It also can be interpreted as the distance from
0
to
x
.
Indeed, let us consider the metric

g=c^{-2}dx,

on
[0,1]
. Then, for
x\in[0,1]
, we get

d_{g}(0,x)=\inf_{\gamma\in C_{0,x}}\int_{0}^{b}\sqrt{g_{\gamma(t)}(\dot{\gamma}(t),\dot{\gamma}(t))}dt.

Let
\gamma
be a minimizer curve. Then
\gamma:[0,b]\rightarrow[0,x]
needs to be a bijection. By changing the coordinates
\tau=\gamma(t)
, we obtain

d_{g}(0,x)=\int_{0}^{b}\dot{\gamma}(t)\frac{1}{|c(\gamma(t))|}dt=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{1}{|c(\tau)|}d\tau=\rho(x).

Let

7

Let
f
,

\Lambda

for all

Proof.

We write

(\partial_{t}^{2}-\partial_{s}^{2})W_{f,h}(t,s)=\left(\Delta_{g}u^{f}(t,\cdot)-qu^{f}(t,\cdot),u^{h}(s,\cdot)\right)_{L^{2}(M)}\\

.

4.
Geometric optics

In this section, we will construct solutions to

(19)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}u-\Delta u+qu=0,\quad\text{in $(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}$},

that concentrate on light rays, that is, lines of the form

\beta(s)=(s,y+sv),\quad s\in\mathbb{R},

where
y
is a point in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
v
is a unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We write

S^{n-1}=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\ |v|=1\}

for the set of unit vectors, that is, for the unit sphere.
The name light ray comes from the fact that the tangent vector
\dot{\beta}=(1,v)
is light like with respect to the Minkowski metric

(20)

g=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\
&1\\
&&\ddots\\
&&&1\end{pmatrix},

that is,
g(\dot{\beta},\dot{\beta})=0
.

The idea is to find first an approximate solution of the form

e^{i\sigma\phi(t,x)}(a_{0}(t,x)+\sigma^{-1}a_{1}(t,x)+\sigma^{-2}a_{2}(t,x)+\dots),

where
\sigma>0
is a large parameter,
and then an actual solution

u=e^{i\sigma\phi}(a_{0}+\dots)+r_{\sigma},

where the remainder
r_{\sigma}
converges to zero as
\sigma\to\infty
.
We will begin with the single term approximation
e^{i\sigma\phi}a_{0}
and write
a_{0}=a
.

4.1.
Single term ansatz

To simplify the notation, we write
\Box=\partial_{t}^{2}-\Delta
.
The equation
(\Box+q)u=0
is equivalent with

(21)

(\Box+q)r_{\sigma}=-(\Box+q)(e^{i\sigma\phi}a),

and we want to choose
\phi
and
a
so that

(“C”)

\displaystyle\Box(e^{i\sigma\phi}a)=e^{i\sigma\phi}\Box a.

The rationale is that in this case the absolute value of the right-hand side of (
21
) is independent of
\sigma
, and therefore
r_{\sigma}
is at least not blowing up as
\sigma\to\infty
.

It is a simple matter to expand the left-hand side of (“C”) but a useful computational technique is to consider the conjugated wave operator

e^{-i\sigma\phi}\Box e^{i\sigma\phi}=e^{-i\sigma\phi}\partial_{t}^{2}e^{i\sigma\phi}+\dots=e^{-i\sigma\phi}\partial_{t}e^{i\sigma\phi}e^{-i\sigma\phi}\partial_{t}e^{i\sigma\phi}+\dots.

Now
e^{-i\sigma\phi}\partial_{t}e^{i\sigma\phi}=\partial_{t}+i\sigma(\partial_{t}\phi)
and

(e^{-i\sigma\phi}\partial_{t}e^{i\sigma\phi})^{2}=\partial_{t}^{2}+2i\sigma(\partial_{t}\phi)\partial_{t}-\sigma^{2}|\partial_{t}\phi|^{2}+i\sigma(\partial_{t}^{2}\phi).

Treating the spacial derivatives in the same way we get

(22)

\displaystyle e^{-i\sigma\phi}\Box e^{i\sigma\phi}=\Box+i\sigma(2(\partial_{t}\phi)\partial_{t}-2(\nabla\phi)\cdot\nabla+\Box\phi)-\sigma^{2}(|\partial_{t}\phi|^{2}-|\nabla\phi|^{2}).

Therefore for
a\neq 0
, (“C”) is equivalent with the following two equations

(E)

\displaystyle|\partial_{t}\phi|^{2}-|\nabla\phi|^{2}=0,

(T)

\displaystyle 2(\partial_{t}\phi)\partial_{t}a-2(\nabla\phi)\cdot\nabla a+(\Box\phi)a=0.

It is natural to normalize
\phi
so that (E) becomes

|\partial_{t}\phi|^{2}=|\nabla\phi|^{2}=1
.
There is some freedom when choosing a solution to (E), but for our purposes it suffices to use the linear solution
\phi(t,x)=t+v\cdot x
where
v\in S^{n-1}
.

To simplify the notation, we may assume after a rotation that
v\cdot x=-x^{1}
. The functions satisfying
|\nabla\phi|=1
are often called distance functions, and the particular choice
x^{1}
is of course the signed distance to the plane
x^{1}=0
.

The transport equation (T) simplifies now to

\partial_{t}a+\partial_{x^{1}}a=0.

The solutions to this are of the form

a(t,x)=\chi(t-x^{1})\eta(x^{\prime})

where
x^{\prime}=(x^{2},x^{3},\dots,x^{n})
.
Taking
\chi\approx\delta
and
\eta\approx\delta
we obtain
a
that concentrates on the light ray
\beta(s)=(s,s,0)
.

Neither
\phi
nor
a
depend on
q
in the above construction. In order to obtain information on
q
, in the context of inverse problems, there are two typical approaches: use the difference of two solutions, corresponding to different potentials, or use a multi-term approximation. We will take the latter approach.

4.2.
Multi-term ansatz

Let us consider the ansatz,

e^{i\sigma\phi}A,\quad A=a_{0}+\sigma^{-1}a_{1}+\dots+\sigma^{-N}a_{N},

and choose
\phi
and
a_{0}=a
as above.
As we are using a more complicated amplitude, we can ask for more than (“C”), namely

\displaystyle(\Box+q)(e^{i\sigma\phi}A)=\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{-N}),\quad\sigma\gg 1.

We use the conjugation formula (
22
), to obtain

e^{-i\sigma\phi}(\Box+q)e^{i\sigma\phi}A=(\Box+q)A+2i(\partial_{t}+\partial_{x^{1}})(a_{1}+\dots+\sigma^{-N+1}a_{N}).

This is of order
\sigma^{-N}
whenever
a_{1},\dots,a_{N}
solve the transport equations

\partial_{t}a_{j}+\partial_{x^{1}}a_{j}-\frac{i}{2}(\Box+q)a_{j-1}=0,\quad j=1,\dots,N,

or after the change of variables,

s=\frac{t+x^{1}}{2},\quad r=\frac{t-x^{1}}{2},

equivalently
\partial_{s}a_{j}=\frac{i}{2}(\Box+q)a_{j-1}
.
Therefore we may choose

(23)

\displaystyle a_{j}(s,r,x^{\prime})=\frac{i}{2}\int_{-r}^{s}(\Box+q)a_{j-1}(s^{\prime},r,x^{\prime})ds^{\prime}.

Note that
t=0
is equivalent with
s=-r
. The choice of the lower limit
-r
in the integration implies that
a_{j}=0
,
j=1,\dots,N
, when
t=0
.

4.3.
Solving for the remainder

When
\chi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n-1})
, the restrictions of all the amplitudes
a_{j}
,
j=0,1,\dots,N
, are compactly supported in
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We recall that the wave equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box u+qu=F&\text{in $(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}$},\\
u|_{t=0}=\partial_{t}u|_{t=0}=0\end{cases}

has a unique solution
u
satisfying

\lVert u\rVert_{C(0,T;H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+\lVert u\rVert_{C^{1}(0,T;L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq C\lVert F\rVert_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n})},

see e.g.
[
6
, Theorem 7.6]
.
We solve

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box r_{\sigma}+qr_{\sigma}=-(\Box+q)(e^{i\sigma\phi}A)&\text{in $(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}$},\\
r_{\sigma}|_{t=0}=\partial_{t}r_{\sigma}|_{t=0}=0.\end{cases}

As the right-hand side is pointwise of order
\sigma^{-N}
and compactly supported, we see that
r_{\sigma}|_{t=T}=\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{-N})
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

4.4.
Proof of Lemma
4

If
\chi(0)\neq 0
and
\eta(0)\neq 0
then
a
does not vanish anywhere on the line given by
t=x^{1}
.
For large enough
\sigma>0
, the same is true for
e^{i\sigma\phi}A
when restricted on
[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Let us now consider the case
n=1
.
The Sobolev embedding
H^{1}(\mathbb{R})\subset C(\mathbb{R})
, see e.g.
[
3
, Theorem 8.8]
, implies that
r_{\sigma}|_{t=T}=\mathcal{O}(\sigma^{-N})
in
C(\mathbb{R})
.
Therefore
u(T,T)\neq 0
for large
\sigma>0
.
Lemma
4
follows after a suitable translation in time.

Lemma
6
can be shown in a similar manner, but higher regularity energy estimates, see e.g.
[
6
, Theorem 6, p. 412]
, are needed to bound
r_{\sigma}|_{t=T}
in
H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
k
large enough so that
H^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset C(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

5.
Solving an inverse problem using geometric optics

We will consider the inverse problem to determine a compactly supported potential in a slab in the Minkowski space.
This avoids some technicalities appearing in more usual inverse boundary value problems, but it still allows us to introduce the main techniques.

Let
n\geq 2
,
T>0
,
q\in C_{0}^{\infty}((0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and consider the wave equation

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\Box u+qu=0&\text{in $(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}$},\\
u|_{t=0}=u_{0},\ \partial_{t}u|_{t=0}=u_{1}.\end{cases}

Define also the map

L:(C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))^{2}\to C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad L(u_{0},u_{1})=u|_{t=T}.

We will study how to solve the inverse problem to determine
q
given
L
.

5.1.
Reduction to the light ray transform

The function
u=e^{i\sigma\phi}A+r_{\sigma}
constructed in Section
4

solves (
19
) with

u_{0}=(e^{i\sigma\phi}A)|_{t=0},\quad u_{1}=\partial_{t}(e^{i\sigma\phi}A)|_{t=0}.

As
q
vanishes near
t=0
, we see that
u_{0}
and
u_{1}
are independent from
q
.
This again implies that
L
determines
u|_{t=T}
.
Moreover,

\sigma(e^{-i\sigma\phi}u-a_{0})|_{t=T}\to a_{1}|_{t=T}\quad\text{as $\sigma\to\infty$}.

This determines
a_{1}|_{t=T}
.
Recalling (
23
) and using the fact that
\Box a_{0}
does not depend on
q
, we find the integral

\int_{-r}^{T-r}qa_{0}(s^{\prime},r,x^{\prime})ds^{\prime}.

Here we used the fact that
t=T
is equivalent with
s=T-r
.

In
(s,r,x^{\prime})
coordinates
a_{0}=\chi(2r)\eta(x^{\prime})
and the above integral reduces at
(r,x^{\prime})=0
to

\int_{0}^{T}q(s^{\prime},0,0)ds^{\prime}\chi(0)\eta(0).

As
q
vanishes for
t<0
and
t>T
, we can recover
in
(s,r,x^{\prime})
coordinates
\int_{\mathbb{R}}q(s,0,0)ds
, or equivalently, in
(t,x^{1},x^{\prime})
coordinates

\int_{\mathbb{R}}q(\beta(s))ds,\quad\beta(s)=(s,s,0).

Repeating the above argument, after using rotations and translations, we obtain the light ray transform of
q
,

\mathcal{L}q(y,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}q(\beta_{y,v}(s))ds,\quad\beta_{y,v}(s)=(s,y+sv),\ y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ v\in S^{n-1}.

5.2.
Inversion of the light ray transform

The above reduction works also when
n=1
, but inversion of
\mathcal{L}
requires
n\geq 2
. Indeed, if
n=1
, then
\mathcal{L}q=0
for
q(s,r)=q_{0}(s)q_{1}(r)
with
q_{j}
,
j=0,1
, integrating to zero.

For a fixed
v\in S^{n-1}
, consider the change of coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
,

(t,x)=(s,y+sv).

Then
y=x-tv
.
Using this, we obtain the Fourier slicing

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-i\eta\cdot y}\mathcal{L}q(y,v)\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{1+n}}e^{-i\eta\cdot(x-tv)}q(t,x)\,dtdx=\widehat{q}(-\eta\cdot v,\eta).

Here
|\eta\cdot v|\leq|\eta|
.
Moreover, as
n\geq 2
, we can choose a unit vector
w
that is orthogonal to
\eta
.
Then for
a\in[-1,1]
and
\eta\neq 0
, we may choose

v=-\frac{a}{|\eta|}\eta+\sqrt{1-a^{2}}\,w\in S^{n-1}.

This gives
-\eta\cdot v=a|\eta|
,
and we see that the Fourier slicing allows us to recover

\widehat{q}(a|\eta|,\eta)
for any
a\in[-1,1]
.

As
q
is compactly supported,
\hat{q}
is analytic. We know
\hat{q}
in a non-empty open cone (in fact, in the cone of spacelike directions), and therefore everywhere by analytic continuation. This shows that
\mathcal{L}q
determines
q
. By the above reduction also
L
determines
q
.
