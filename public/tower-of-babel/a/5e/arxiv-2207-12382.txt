On Confidence Sequences for Bounded Random Processes via Universal Gambling Strategies
J. Jon Ryu and Alankrita Bhatt

Abstract
This paper considers the problem of constructing a confidence sequence, which is a sequence of confidence intervals that hold uniformly over time, for estimating the mean of bounded real-valued random processes. This paper revisits the gambling-based approach established in the recent literature from a natural two-horse race perspective, and demonstrates new properties of the resulting algorithm induced by Cover (1991)’s universal portfolio. The main result of this paper is a new algorithm based on a mixture of lower bounds, which closely approximates the performance of Cover’s universal portfolio with constant per-round time complexity. A higher-order generalization of a lower bound on a logarithmic function in (Fan et al., 2015), which is developed as a key technique for the proposed algorithm, may be of independent interest.



I
Introduction

Suppose
that
(Y_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
is a
[0,1]
-valued stochastic process such that
\mathop{\mathsf{E}}[Y_{t}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y^{t-1}]\equiv\mu
for any
t\geq 1
, for some unknown mean parameter
\mu\in(0,1)
.
Here we use a shorthand notation
Y^{t}\triangleq(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{t})
.
A
confidence sequence
for the mean parameter
\mu
at level
1-\delta
is defined as a sequence of sets
(\mathcal{C}_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
such that
\mathcal{C}_{t}\subseteq(0,1)
is a function of
Y^{t-1}
and

\P(\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{t},\forall t\geq 1)\geq 1-\delta.

A confidence sequence under this setting can be applied to solving some fundamental problems in statistics, such as sequentially estimating the mean of a bounded distribution with i.i.d. samples
[
32
,
19
]
, or constructing a time-uniform confidence interval for the mean of a fixed set of numbers by random sampling without replacement
[
31
]
.
This notion is better suited in real applications than the classical confidence sets (or intervals) which only applies to a single time instance.
For example, suppose that a practitioner wishes to sequentially estimate the mean of such a stochastic process.
The user wishes to construct a confidence interval, so that she can choose when to stop sample to achieve a desired precision and confidence level a priori.
A confidence sequence allows the user to determine adaptively in the sequential inference setting thanks to the time uniformity; see
[
22
]
and
[
8
]
.

The idea of time-uniform confidence sequences dates back to
Hoeffding 1963
,
Darling and Robbins 1967
, and
Lai 1976
.

Rather ignored for past decades
,
the idea has been revived recently in the statistics community by a series of papers
[
22
,
32
,
12
]
and in the computer science community
[
13
,
19
]
, to name a few, even beyond the boundedness assumptions on the stochastic processes we consider in this paper.

As noted above, this paper studies how to construct a confidence sequence for the mean of bounded random processes (with known support), especially via the “gambling” approach established by a recent line of research.
The most closely related work is
[
32
,
19
]
.

Waudby-Smith and Ramdas 2020b
proposed a gambling framework to construct confidence sequences of bounded real-valued sequences.
Several betting strategies were proposed, and their analytical behavior of resulting confidence sequences were established.

Orabona and Jun 2024
proposed to apply the universal portfolio (UP) method of
Cover 1991
to construct a confidence sequence, and obtained an analytical expression that bounds the UP-induced confidence sequence based on the regret upper bound (which corresponds to a wealth
lower
bound) of UP in terms of the logarithmic wealth.
They demonstrated an excellent empirical performance of the proposed algorithms especially in a small-sample regime.

The proposed algorithms dubbed as PRECiSE, however, is based on a rather intricate regret analysis and does not naturally result in confidence
intervals
.

In their work,
Orabona and Jun 2024
also established a more relaxed lower bound that directly generates confidence intervals without introducing excessive slackness.

The main goal of this paper is twofold.
First, we provide a new perspective of the
two-horse race
to the existing gambling-based approach which was proven effective by
Waudby-Smith and Ramdas 2020a
,
Waudby-Smith and Ramdas 2020b
,
Orabona and Jun 2024
, which admits a more natural interpretation in gambling and leads to a conceptual simplification.
Building upon the framework, we propose a new method to approximate the performance of the UP closely and fast in a more direct manner than
Orabona and Jun 2024
, without invoking the regret analysis of UP.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows.

1.

For
\{0,1\}
-valued sequences, we show that Cover’s UP can be simplified to a universal two-horse race scheme, which can be implemented with constant complexity per round to construct time-uniform confidence intervals.
This observation is simple, but has not been realized in the literature.

2.

For
[0,1]
-valued sequences, we discuss how to exactly compute the wealth of any mixture of wealth processes of constant bettors (such as Cover’s UP) with
O(t)
complexity at round
t
and show that it leads to time-uniform confidence intervals.
To circumvent the cumbersome
O(t)
per-round complexity of the UP, we propose a new UP-like method based on a new lower bound of the wealth of Cover’s UP with constant complexity per round.
Our algorithm is based on Lemma
16
, a higher-order generalization of the proof technique of
Fan et al. 2015
developed for proving exponential inequalities for martingales, which may be of independent interest. Experiments validate that the proposed algorithm gives tight confidence sequences.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
Section
II
is devoted to mathematical preliminaries.
We start with a special case of
\{0,1\}
-valued processes in Section
III
and show that there is a simple two-horse-race-based algorithm that emulates the performance of Cover’s UP.
In Section
IV
, we then study the general case of
[0,1]
-valued processes and propose a new mixture-of-lower-bound approach.
Related work are briefly discussed in Section
V
and
experimental results are presented in Section
VI
.
We conclude the paper in Section
VII
with some remarks.
All the deferred proofs and technical lemmas can be found in Appendix.

II
Preliminaries

At its core, the common technique upon which most, if not all, methods for constructing confidence sequences rely on is the following celebrated inequality by
Ville 1939
from the martingale theory.

Theorem 1

(Ville’s inequality)
.

For a nonnegative supermartingale sequence
(W_{t})_{t=0}^{\infty}
with
W_{0}>0
, for any
\delta>0
, we have

\P\Bigl\{\sup_{t\geq 1}\frac{W_{t}}{W_{0}}\geq\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigr\}\leq\delta.

This statement is a sequential, uniform counterpart for nonnegative supermartingales to Markov’s inequality for nonnegative random variables.
Since this inequality controls the probability that the sequence
(W_{t})_{t=0}^{\infty}
overshoots a certain threshold uniformly over time,
the key idea is to construct a
good
martingale sequence
(W_{t})_{t=0}^{\infty}
out of the given sequence
(Y_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}

such that the wealth sequence grows as rapidly as possible for any
m\neq\mu
,

so that the sequence of events controlled by this inequality can be translated to a
tighter
confidence sequence for a target parameter
\mu
;
the intuition will be visually demonstrated in Fig.
1
in Section
VI-A
.

As explored in
[
10
,
13
,
32
,
19
]
, a natural way to construct a supermartingale is via a gambling, and we thus start by introducing the gambling formalism of this approach.

II-A

Supermartingales from Gambling

A natural way to construct a (super)martingale sequence is to consider the wealth process from a
(sub)fair gambling
.
Let
K\geq 2
and
let
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}
be a set of
odds vectors
.
We consider an abstract setting of gambling defined by the following
multiplicative
game.
Let
\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}>0
be the initial wealth of a gambler.
For each time
t\geq 1
, a gambler chooses a bet
\mathbf{b}_{t}=\mathbf{b}_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t-1})\in\Delta_{K-1}
as a function of the previous odds vectors
{\mathbf{x}}^{t-1}
; such a betting strategy is said to be
causal
(or
nonanticiapting
).

Here,
\Delta_{K-1}\triangleq\{\mathbf{p}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{K}\mathchoice{\colon}{\colon}{\mathchar 58\relax\mspace{1mu}}{\mathchar 58\relax}p_{1}+\ldots+p_{K}=1\}
denotes the
(K-1)
-dimensional probability simplex.

At the end of each round, the odds vector
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
is revealed, and the gambler’s wealth gets
multiplied
by
\langle\mathbf{b}_{t},{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\rangle
.
Therefore, after round
t
, the gambler’s wealth can be written as

\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t})=\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\langle\mathbf{b}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\rangle.

We call the odds vector sequence
({\mathbf{x}}_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}

subfair
, if
\mathop{\mathsf{E}}[{\bf x}_{t}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x}^{t-1}]\leq\mathds{1}
for every
t
, where the inequality holds coordinatewise
and
\mathds{1}\triangleq(1,\ldots,1)
denotes the all-one vector.

Under this condition, the resulting wealth process from this game is always a supermartingale as one may intuitively expect.

Proposition 2
.

In any gambling with the cumulative wealth of the form

\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}=\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\langle\mathbf{b}_{i},{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\rangle
,
if
({\mathbf{x}}_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
is (sub)fair, then any wealth process
(\mathsf{Wealth}_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
attained by a causal betting strategy is a (super)martingale.

Proof.

For every
t
, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathsf{E}}[\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x}^{t-1}]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Wealth}_{t-1}\langle\mathbf{b}_{t},\mathop{\mathsf{E}}[{\bf x}_{t}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x}^{t-1}]\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\mathsf{Wealth}_{t-1}\langle\mathbf{b}_{t},\mathds{1}\rangle=\mathsf{Wealth}_{t-1}.\qed

We remark that this abstract formulation captures several standard settings of gambling as special instances; see Table
I
.
For example, if
\mathcal{M}=\{2{\mathbf{e}}_{1},2{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\}
, it corresponds to
the standard (fair)
coin toss

[
4
]
(or coin betting
[
20
]
) game.

If
\mathcal{M}=\{o_{1}{\mathbf{e}}_{1},\ldots,o_{K}{\mathbf{e}}_{K}\}
for some
o_{1},\ldots,o_{K}>0
, then the game becomes a
horse race
with odds
o_{1},\ldots,o_{K}

[
4
, Chap. 6]
.
The most general case is when
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{K}
, where the game is typically known as a
portfolio selection
in a stock market
[
2
]
.
The
continuous
coin toss problem that corresponds to
\mathcal{M}=\{[2\widetilde{c},2(1-\widetilde{c})]\mathchoice{\colon}{\colon}{\mathchar 58\relax\mspace{1mu}}{\mathchar 58\relax}\widetilde{c}\in[0,1]\}
was considered by
Orabona and Pál 2016
to translate the idea of universal betting for coin toss to the domain of online linear optimization. In what follows, betting strategy and portfolio are used interchangeably, where the latter is used typically when the outcomes are continuous like the stock market.

II-B

Two-Horse Race and Its Continuous Extension

In this paper, we consider the
two-horse race
with odds
o_{1},o_{2}>0
, which corresponds to
\mathcal{M}_{o_{1},o_{2}}\triangleq\{co_{1}{\mathbf{e}}_{1}+(1-c)o_{2}{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\mathchoice{\colon}{\colon}{\mathchar 58\relax\mspace{1mu}}{\mathchar 58\relax}c\in\{0,1\}\}
.
Each odds vector at time
t
can be written as
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=[o_{1}c_{t},o_{2}(1-c_{t})]
with
c_{t}\in\{0,1\}
, where
c_{t}
can be understood as an index of the winning horse.

Given the fixed odds
o_{1}
and
o_{2}
, the odds vector sequence
x_{t}
is fully characterized by the outcomes
c_{t}
so we will often refer to this sequence
c_{t}
instead of the full odds vector
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}
in our discussion below.

Since
c_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, the multiplicative gain at each round is equivalently expressed as

o_{1}c_{i}b_{i}+o_{2}(1-c_{i})(1-b_{i})=(o_{1}b_{i})^{c_{i}}(o_{2}(1-b_{i}))^{1-c_{i}},

where
b_{i}
denotes the fraction of bet on horse 1.

As a natural extension, we also consider the
continuous
two-horse race game with odds
o_{1},o_{2}>0
which corresponds to
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}_{o_{1},o_{2}}\triangleq\{\widetilde{c}o_{1}{\mathbf{e}}_{1}+(1-\widetilde{c})o_{2}{\mathbf{e}}_{2}\mathchoice{\colon}{\colon}{\mathchar 58\relax\mspace{1mu}}{\mathchar 58\relax}\widetilde{c}\in[0,1]\}
. Now, unlike the previous case where there is a single winner for each round, the outcome of the game
\widetilde{c}
is
[0,1]
-valued.
Strictly speaking, this game can be better understood as a
structured
two-stock market, as there is no single winning horse in this setup.

We note that we use
c_{t}\in\{0,1\}
and
\widetilde{c}_{t}\in[0,1]
to denote generic outcomes which could be deterministic, as opposed to the stochastic process
(Y_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
.

II-C

The Blueprint

We now describe the gambling formalism we take in this paper, which we adopt from
[
10
,
13
,
32
,
19
]
.
Most of the following exposition can be also found in
[
32
, Theorem 1]
and
[
32
, Section 4.1]
, but we also include
some new arguments such as the two-horse-race-based exposition and the converse of Corollary
3
stated below.

Recall that we sequentially observe a sequence
(Y_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
such that
\mathop{\mathsf{E}}[Y_{t}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y^{t-1}]\equiv\mu
for every
t\geq 1
and some
\mu\in(0,1)
, and the goal is to estimate
\mu
at each time
t
based on the observation
Y^{t-1}
.
To estimate the unknown
\mu
,
we consider the two-horse race with the odds vector

{\mathbf{x}}_{t}={\mathbf{x}}_{t}(Y_{t};m)=\Bigl[\frac{Y_{t}}{m},\frac{1-Y_{t}}{1-m}\Bigr]

for a parameter
m\in(0,1)
.
Then, as a corollary of Proposition
2
, we can prove:

Corollary 3
.

Assume that
\mathop{\mathsf{E}}[Y_{t}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y^{t-1}]\equiv\mu
for every
t\geq 1
and some
\mu\in(0,1)
.
Then, the wealth process from the continuous two-horse race
with the odds vector
{\bf x}_{t}=[\frac{Y_{t}}{m},\frac{1-Y_{t}}{1-m}]
is a martingale if
m=\mu
.
Conversely, if
m\neq\mu
, there exists a causal betting strategy whose wealth process is strictly submartingale.

Proof.

If
m=\mu
,
({\mathbf{x}}_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
is fair by construction, i.e.,
\mathop{\mathsf{E}}[{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\mathbf{x}}_{t-1}]=[\frac{\mu}{m},\frac{1-\mu}{1-m}]=[1,1]
, so we can apply Proposition
2
.
For the case
m\neq\mu
, we assume
m<\mu
without loss of generality; the proof for
m>\mu
is similar.
Then,
b\mapsto\frac{\mu}{m}b+\frac{1-\mu}{1-m}(1-b)
becomes a strictly increasing function, and

\frac{\mu}{m}b+\frac{1-\mu}{1-m}(1-b)>1

for any
b\in(m,1)
.
Hence, in this case, any constant betting strategy
\mathbf{b}_{t}=[b,1-b]
with
b\in(m,1)
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathsf{E}}[\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x}^{t-1}]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Wealth}_{t-1}\langle\mathbf{b}_{t},\mathop{\mathsf{E}}[{\bf x}_{t}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x}^{t-1}]\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Wealth}_{t-1}\Bigl(\frac{\mu}{m}b+\frac{1-\mu}{1-m}(1-b)\Bigr)

\displaystyle>\mathsf{Wealth}_{t-1},

implying that the wealth process is strictly submartingale.
∎

Suppose that we have a gambling strategy
\mathbf{b}_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t-1};m)
for each
m
, and let
\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t};m)
be the wealth process of the strategy for the two-horse race game in Eq. (
II-C
) parameterized by
m
.
1
1

1

All the gambling strategies
\mathbf{b}_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t-1};m)
considered in the current paper are independent of
m
, but we explicitly include the dependence to subsume a general use-case. For example,
Waudby-Smith and Ramdas 2020a
studied betting strategies that depend on
m
.

Since
(\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}({\bf x}^{t};\mu))_{t\geq 0}
is a martingale (Corollary
3
),
by Ville’s inequality (Theorem
1
), for any
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\P\Bigl\{\sup_{t\geq 1}\frac{\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t};\mu)}{\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}}\geq\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigr\}\leq\delta,

or equivalently,

\P\{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{t}(Y^{t};\delta),\forall t\geq 1\}\geq 1-\delta,

where we define the confidence set

\mathcal{C}_{t}(Y^{t};\delta)\triangleq\Bigl\{m\in(0,1)\mathchoice{\colon}{\colon}{\mathchar 58\relax\mspace{1mu}}{\mathchar 58\relax}\sup_{1\leq i\leq t}\frac{\mathsf{Wealth}_{i}({\mathbf{x}}^{i};m)}{\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}}<\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigr\}.

Since
(\mathcal{C}_{t}(Y^{t};\delta))_{t=1}^{\infty}
is nonincreasing (i.e.,
C_{t-1}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{t}
) by construction, we can interpret that the confidence sequence
(\mathcal{C}_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
sequentially
refines
the estimate for
\mu
at every round
t
by
rejecting
a candidate parameter
m
such that

\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t};m)>\frac{1}{\delta}
.

Here is a high-level interpretation of the gambling approach.
Hypothetically, we run the two-horse race with parameter
m
for each
m\in(0,1)
based on the observation
(Y_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
.
At the very beginning, we start with the entire interval
C_{0}=(0,1)
as the candidate list for
\mu
.
After each round of gambling upon observing
Y_{t}
, we compute the cumulative wealth from each horse race, and if it exceeds a prescribed threshold, which is
1/\delta
for a level-
(1-\delta)
confidence sequence, we remove the associated parameter
m
from the candidate list
C_{t-1}
; at the end of the round,
\mathcal{C}_{t}
then consists of the remaining values.
Intuitively, we can remove such values
m
with confidence as the wealth process from the horse-race with parameter
\mu
is martingale, and thus will not exceed the threshold most likely; that is, if the wealth exceeds the threshold, the parameter
m
is likely not
\mu
.

This intuition explains why we can expect a better gambling strategy to result in a tighter confidence sequence:
a
good
strategy would grow its wealth as fast as possible from the horse races with
m\neq\mu
, and thus rejects those values of
m
at an early stage with few observations.
Note that the converse part of Corollary
3
ensures that there exists a betting strategy that makes the wealth process a strict submartingale.
In an ideal scenario, therefore, one can expect that a good gambling strategy may make arbitrarily large money in the long run from the horse race with
m\neq\mu
, leaving only the true
\mu
in the candidate list.

Remark 4

(Different gambling parameterization)
.

While the setting of gambling we consider is equivalent to those in
[
32
,
19
]
, the literature uses a different convention.
For the two-horse race setting with odds
\frac{1}{m}
and
\frac{1}{1-m}
and a betting strategy
(b_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
, they write the multiplicative wealth as

\frac{1}{m}\widetilde{c}_{t}b_{t}+\frac{1}{1-m}(1-\widetilde{c}_{t})(1-b_{t})=1+\lambda_{t}(m)(\widetilde{c}_{t}-m),

by viewing the single number
\widetilde{c}_{t}-m\in[-m,1-m]
as an outcome of the horse race and defining

\lambda_{t}(m)\triangleq\frac{b_{t}}{m(1-m)}-\frac{1}{1-m}\in\Bigl[-\frac{1}{1-m},\frac{1}{m}\Bigr]

as a
scaled bet
.
The downside of this standard convention is that the (scaled) betting in Eq. (
4
) must depend on the underlying odds (and thus on the parameter
m
) by the range it can take,
which we view as rather unnatural.

We believe that
the horse-race language we adopt in this paper separates
m
from the betting strategy, and thus admits a cleaner interpretation.

II-D

Achievable Wealth Processes and the
Method of Mixtures

As described above, it suffices to directly construct a valid wealth process for a confidence sequence, without explicitly finding a causal betting strategy that achieves it.
In this paper, the central technique we use is to consider a wealth process defined by a
mixture
of a collection of wealth processes.
The purpose of this section is to ensure that we can use any
mixture of wealth processes
(or
mixture wealth
in short) in constructing a confidence sequence.

To show the main result of this section, Corollary
6
, we first state the following theorem which characterizes when a given sequence of nonnegative functions can be
realized
by a causal betting strategy, under a mild regularity assumption on the set of market vectors
\mathcal{M}
that holds for all games in Table
I
.

In the statement below, we use
\circ
to denote a concatenation between two sequences, e.g.,
{\bf x}^{t-1}\circ{\bf x}_{t}={\bf x}^{t}
.

Theorem 5
.

Suppose that

\{o_{1}{\mathbf{e}}_{1},\ldots,o_{K}{\mathbf{e}}_{K}\}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
for some
o_{1},\ldots,o_{K}>0
.
Let
(\Psi_{t}\mathchoice{\colon}{\colon}{\mathchar 58\relax\mspace{1mu}}{\mathchar 58\relax}\mathcal{M}^{t-1}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0})_{t=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of nonnegative functions, where each
\Psi_{t}
at time
t
maps past odds vectors
{\bf x}^{t-1}\in\mathcal{M}^{t-1}
to a nonnegative real number.
Assume that the following conditions hold:

(A1)

(Consistency)
For any
t\geq 1
, any
j\in[K]
, and any
{\mathbf{x}}^{t-1}\in\mathcal{M}^{t-1}
,

\Psi_{t-1}({\mathbf{x}}^{t-1})\geq\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{1}{o_{j}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Psi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t-1}\circ o_{j}{\mathbf{e}}_{j})}.

(A2)

(Convexity)
For any
t\geq 1
, any
j\in[K]
, and any
{\mathbf{x}}^{t}\in\mathcal{M}^{t}
,

\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{x_{tj}}{o_{j}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Psi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t-1}\circ o_{j}{\mathbf{e}}_{j})}\geq\Psi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t}).

Then, there exists a causal betting strategy
\mathbf{b}_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t-1})\in\Delta_{K-1}
that guarantees wealth at least
\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\Psi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t})
for any market sequence
{\mathbf{x}}^{t}\in\mathcal{M}^{t}
.
Conversely, if
\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\Psi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t})
is the wealth achieved by a causal betting strategy given
{\mathbf{x}}^{t}
, then
(\Psi_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
satisfies (A1) and (A2) with equality.

It is worth noting that

Theorem

can be viewed as an analogous result for gambling, which can be viewed as a prediction game under the log score.
Its proof, which can be found in Appendix

; see Eq. (
B-A
) therein for an explicit expression of the betting strategy.

This implies that computing the cumulative wealth of a betting strategy and computing the betting strategy itself are essentially equivalent.
Another immediate consequence of this theorem is that, for a class of sequences of achievable wealth functions, their mixture is always achievable.

Corollary 6
.

Consider a set of betting strategies
(\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\theta})
, where for each strategy parameterized by
\theta\in\Theta
, each of whose wealth is lower bounded by
\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\Psi_{t}^{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}^{t})
for any
t\geq 1
and
{\mathbf{x}}^{t}\in\mathcal{M}^{t}
.
Pick any probability measure
\pi(\theta)
on
\Theta
and define a mixture
\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}^{\pi}({\mathbf{x}}^{t})\triangleq\int\Psi_{t}^{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}^{t})\pi(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta)
.
Then, there always exists a causal betting strategy whose wealth is lower bounded by
\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}^{\pi}({\mathbf{x}}^{t})
.

Proof.

For each
\theta\in\Theta
, let
\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}^{t})
denote the wealth achieved by
(\mathbf{b}_{t}^{\theta})_{t=1}^{\infty}
for
{\mathbf{x}}^{t}
.
Since
(\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{\theta})_{t\geq 0}
satisfies (A1) and (A2) automatically by the converse part of Theorem
5
, so does the
mixture wealth
defined by

\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{\pi}({\mathbf{x}}^{t})\triangleq\int\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{\theta}({\mathbf{x}}^{t})\pi(\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta)

by linearity of expectation.
Therefore, by Theorem
5
, there always exists a causal strategy whose wealth is at least
\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{\pi}({\mathbf{x}}^{t})\geq\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\widetilde{\Psi}_{t}^{\pi}({\mathbf{x}}^{t})
.
∎

Hence, in what follows, we can use any mixture of achievable wealth processes (or its lower bound) to construct a confidence sequence.

III
Two-Horse Race and
\{0,1\}
-Valued Processes

In this section, we first consider a
\{0,1\}
-valued sequence
(Y_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
such that
\mathop{\mathsf{E}}[Y_{t}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y^{t-1}]\equiv\mu
for some
\mu\in(0,1)
.
This special case well motivates the idea of universal gambling and admits a simpler algorithm than the general case.

We consider the gambling problem with odds vectors
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\leftarrow[\frac{Y_{t}}{m},\frac{1-Y_{t}}{1-m}]
, which is a two-horse race game with odds
o_{1}=\frac{1}{m}
and
o_{2}=\frac{1}{1-m}
.
Henceforth, for the two-horse race with odds
o_{1}
and
o_{2}
, for each
\theta\in[0,1]
, we let

\displaystyle\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{\theta}(c^{t};o_{1},o_{2})

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{t}(o_{1}\theta)^{c_{i}}(o_{2}(1-\theta))^{1-c_{i}}

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}(o_{1}\theta)^{\sum_{i=1}^{t}c_{i}}(o_{2}(1-\theta))^{t-\sum_{i=1}^{t}c_{i}}

denote the cumulative wealth of the constant bettor
\theta
after round
t
with respect to
c^{t}\in\{0,1\}^{t}
.

III-A

Motivation

To illustrate the idea of
universal gambling
, let us first consider the wealth achieved by the best constant bettor
in hindsight
:

\displaystyle\max_{\theta\in[0,1]}\frac{\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{\theta}(c^{t};o_{1},o_{2})}{\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}}

\displaystyle=o_{1}^{s_{t}}o_{2}^{t-s_{t}}e^{-th(\frac{s_{t}}{t})},

(6)

where
h(p)\triangleq p\log\frac{1}{p}+(1-p)\log\frac{1}{1-p}
denotes the binary entropy function and
s_{t}\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{t}c_{i}
.
Assume for now that this wealth
were hypothetically achieved
by a causal betting strategy and examine what we can obtain as
a resulting confidence sequence
.
If we plug-in
c_{t}\leftarrow Y_{t}
and
o_{1}\leftarrow\frac{1}{\mu}
and
o_{2}\leftarrow\frac{1}{1-\mu}
, then since the wealth process is a martingale, by Ville’s inequality, we have, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\P\Bigl\{\sup_{t\geq 1}\mu^{-S_{t}}(1-\mu)^{-(t-S_{t})}e^{-th(\frac{S_{t}}{t})}\geq\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigr\}

\displaystyle=\P\Bigl\{\sup_{t\geq 1}td\Bigl(\frac{S_{t}}{t}~\Big\|~\mu\Bigr)\geq\log\frac{1}{\delta}\Bigr\}\leq\delta.

(7)

Here,
S_{t}\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{t}Y_{i}
and
d(p~\|~q)\triangleq p\log\frac{p}{q}+(1-p)\log\frac{1-p}{1-q}
denotes the binary relative entropy function,
and the equality follows from the identity that

d\Bigl(\frac{S_{t}}{t}~\Big\|~\mu\Bigr)=-\frac{S_{t}}{t}\log\mu-\Bigl(1-\frac{S_{t}}{t}\Bigr)\log(1-\mu)-h\Bigl(\frac{S_{t}}{t}\Bigr).

Since
m\mapsto d(x~\|~m)
is convex for any
x\in[0,1]
, Eq. (
7
) yields a time-uniform confidence interval.
Further, since
d(p~\|~q)\geq 2(p-q)^{2}
by Pinsker’s inequality, Eq. (
7
) readily implies

\P\Bigl\{\exists t\geq 1\mathchoice{\colon}{\colon}{\mathchar 58\relax\mspace{1mu}}{\mathchar 58\relax}\mu\notin\Bigl(\frac{S_{t}}{t}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{2t}\log\frac{1}{\delta}},\frac{S_{t}}{t}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{2t}\log\frac{1}{\delta}}\Bigr)\Bigr\}\leq\delta.

Note that this is the time-uniform
version
of the confidence interval implied by Hoeffding’s inequality, which states that, for any
\delta>0
,

\P\Bigl\{\mu\notin\Bigl(\frac{S_{t}}{t}-\sqrt{\frac{1}{2t}\log\frac{2}{\delta}},\frac{S_{t}}{t}+\sqrt{\frac{1}{2t}\log\frac{2}{\delta}}\Bigr)\Bigr\}\leq\delta

for any fixed
t\geq 1
.

In other words, achieving the best wealth in hindsight in Eq. (
6
) would result in the time-uniform Hoeffding in Eq. (
III-A
).

Therefore, it is reasonable to aim to achieve the best wealth in hindsight in Eq. (
6
), which is the very idea of universal gambling.

We note in passing that the time-uniform Hoeffding in Eq. (
III-A
) cannot be constructed in reality, as it violates the law of iterated logarithm (LIL)
[
12
]
. We refer interested readers to Remark
11
for further comments on LIL.
From an online learning perspective, the best wealth in hindsight can only be achieved at the cost of additional regret by a causal betting strategy, and thus the resulting confidence sequence will tend to behave the time-uniform Hoeffding in the long run with an additional slackness due to the regret.

III-B

A Universal Gambling Method via the
Method of Mixtures

We now show that the
method of mixtures
almost achieves Eq. (
6
) and essentially implies Eq. (
III-A
).
We first consider the following wealth process.

Theorem 7
.

There exists a causal betting strategy
such that for any
o_{1},o_{2}
, the wealth is given as

(\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\widetilde{\phi}_{t}(\sum_{i=1}^{t}c_{i};o_{1},o_{2}))_{t\geq 0}
,
where we define

\widetilde{\phi}_{t}(x;o_{1},o_{2})\triangleq o_{1}^{x}o_{2}^{t-x}\frac{B(x+\frac{1}{2},t-x+\frac{1}{2})}{B(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})}

for
x\in[0,t]
and
B(x,x^{\prime})\triangleq\frac{\Gamma(x)\Gamma(x^{\prime})}{\Gamma(x+x^{\prime})}
denotes the beta function.

Proof.

Consider a constant bettor
[b,1-b]\in\Delta_{2}
parameterized
b\in[0,1]
.
If we define

\phi_{t}^{b}(x;o_{1},o_{2})\triangleq(o_{1}b)^{x}(o_{2}(1-b))^{t-x}
for
x\in[0,t]
,
we can write the wealth for a constant bettor as

\displaystyle\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{b}(c^{t};o_{1},o_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{t}(o_{1}c_{i}b+o_{2}(1-c_{i})(1-b))

\displaystyle=\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\phi_{t}^{b}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{t}c_{i};o_{1},o_{2}\Bigr).

We now take a mixture of the wealth processes over the constant bettor
b\in[0,1]
.
We specifically choose the Beta distribution
\pi(b)=B(b\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})\propto b^{\frac{1}{2}}(1-b)^{\frac{1}{2}}
over
b\in[0,1]
with the parameters
(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
.
By Corollary
6
, the mixture wealth
\int\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{b}(c^{t};o_{1},o_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi(b)=\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\widetilde{\phi}_{t}(\sum_{i=1}^{t}c_{i};o_{1},o_{2})
is achievable.
Note that the equality is a consequence of the identity

\int\phi_{t}^{b}(x;o_{1},o_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi(b)=\widetilde{\phi}_{t}(x;o_{1},o_{2})\quad\text{for any $x\in[0,t]$},

which, in turn, follows from the definition of the Beta function.

This concludes the proof.
∎

IV
Continuous Two-Horse Race

and
[0,1]
-Valued Processes

We now consider a more general case where the sequence
Y_{t}
is continuous-valued, i.e.,
Y_{t}\in[0,1]
.
Likewise in the previous section,
we plug-in
\widetilde{c}_{t}\leftarrow Y_{t}
with
o_{1}\leftarrow\frac{1}{\mu}
and
o_{2}\leftarrow\frac{1}{1-\mu}
to construct a subfair odds-vector sequence
({\bf x}_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
for a continuous two-horse race.
Following the same notation, we define

\displaystyle\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{b}(\widetilde{c}^{t};o_{1},o_{2})\triangleq\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\prod_{i=1}^{t}(o_{1}\widetilde{c}_{i}b+o_{2}(1-\widetilde{c}_{i})(1-b))

(13)

as the cumulative wealth of the constant bettor
b
after round
t
with respect to
\widetilde{c}^{t}\in[0,1]^{t}
.

Note that we have an immediate lower bound

o_{1}\widetilde{c}_{i}b+o_{2}(1-\widetilde{c}_{i})(1-b)\geq(o_{1}b)^{\widetilde{c}_{i}}(o_{2}(1-b))^{1-\widetilde{c}_{i}}

by Jensen’s inequality.
Since the lower bound is in the form of the multiplicative wealth
(o_{1}b)^{\widetilde{c}_{i}}(o_{2}(1-b))^{1-\widetilde{c}_{i}}
of the discrete two-horse race, the same time-uniform confidence intervals constructed in Theorem
9
in the previous section is valid as a loose confidence sequence if we simply plug-in
\widetilde{c}^{t}
in place of
Y^{t}
. Note that though
Y^{t}\in\{0,1\}^{t}
was assumed in Theorem
9
, the function defined in the theorem remains well-defined for continuous-valued sequence
Y^{t}\in[0,1]^{t}
.

This lower bound in Eq. (
IV
) can be viewed as a reduction to the standard two-horse race game, since the lower bound becomes tight if and only if
\widetilde{c}_{i}\in\{0,1\}
.

Albeit this results in a very easy-to-implement confidence sequence for continuous-valued sequences,
it turns out that the Jensen gap in Eq. (
IV
) leads to a loose bound.

A more direct approach for this general case is to compute the mixture wealth without invoking Jensen’s inequality.
That is, we will study how to compute the mixture wealth, with a slight abuse of notation,

\displaystyle\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{\pi}(\widetilde{c}^{t};o_{1},o_{2})

\displaystyle\triangleq\int\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{b}(\widetilde{c}^{t};o_{1},o_{2})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi(b)

\displaystyle\geq\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\widetilde{\phi}_{t}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{t}\widetilde{c}_{i};o_{1},o_{2}\Bigr),

where the lower bound is the wealth attained by a mixture portfolio in the previous section and the inequality is followed from Eq. (
IV
).
Here, the equality holds if and only if
\widetilde{c}^{t}\in\{0,1\}^{t}
.
Hereafter, we call
\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{\pi}(\widetilde{c}^{t};o_{1},o_{2})
the
\pi
-mixture wealth
, or the wealth of the
\pi
-mixture portfolio
.
We remark that

Cover 1991
’s universal portfolio (UP) is a
\mathrm{Beta}(b;\alpha,\beta)
-mixture portfolio; note that
\alpha=\beta=1
or
\alpha=\beta=\frac{1}{2}
are the canonical choices due to the simplicity
[
2
]
and the minimax optimality
[
3
]
, respectively.
The beta distribution is a natural choice for an ease of implementation, since it is a
conjugate prior
of the summand
b^{k}(1-b)^{t-k}
, which can be understood as the (unnormalized) binomial distribution.

IV-A

A Mixture Portfolio

We first explore how we can compute a mixture wealth exactly, i.e., without any approximation.
While
Orabona and Jun 2024
alluded to this idea of directly implementing Cover’s UP to compute a confidence sequence, here we explicitly describe the algorithm in a general form with any
\pi
-mixture for a reference.
We also show that the resulting confidence set of any mixture portfolio is always an interval as desired. (This was left as a loose end in the initial version of
[
19
]
(
arXiv:2110.14099v1
), but also resolved in its revision (
arXiv:2110.14099v3
).)

It turns out that the
\pi
-mixture wealth can be computed in
O(t)
at round
t
instead of
O(2^{t})
via a dynamic-programming type recursion,
as shown in the following theorem.
This essentially appeared in the discussion on computing the UP by
Cover and Ordentlich 1996
.
Here we present a general statement for any mixture portfolio.

Theorem 12
.

For any prior distribution
\pi(b)
over
(0,1)
,
the
\pi(b)
-mixture wealth is achievable and can be written as

\displaystyle\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{\pi}(\widetilde{c}^{t};o_{1},o_{2})=\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\sum_{k=0}^{t}o_{1}^{k}o_{2}^{t-k}\psi_{t}^{\pi}(k)\widetilde{c}^{t}(k),

(15)

where we
define

\displaystyle\psi_{t}^{\pi}(k)

\displaystyle\triangleq\int_{0}^{1}b^{k}(1-b)^{t-k}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\pi(b),

(16)

\displaystyle\widetilde{c}^{t}(k)

\displaystyle\triangleq\sum_{z^{t}\in\{0,1\}^{t}\mathchoice{\colon}{\colon}{\mathchar 58\relax\mspace{1mu}}{\mathchar 58\relax}k(z^{t})=k}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\widetilde{c}_{i}^{z_{i}}(1-\widetilde{c}_{i})^{1-z_{i}},

(17)

and
k(z^{t})\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{t}z_{i}
.
Furthermore, for each

, we have

\widetilde{c}^{t}(k)=\begin{cases}(1-\widetilde{c}_{t})\widetilde{c}^{t-1}(0)&\text{if }k=0,\\
\widetilde{c}_{t}\widetilde{c}^{t-1}(k-1)+(1-\widetilde{c}_{t})\widetilde{c}^{t-1}(k)&\text{if }1\leq k\leq t-1\\
\widetilde{c}_{t}\widetilde{c}^{t-1}(t-1)&\text{if }k=t.\end{cases}

Proof.

We first note that we can write the cumulative wealth of any constant bettor
b
as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{b}(\widetilde{c}^{t};o_{1},o_{2})}{\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{z^{t}\in\{0,1\}^{t}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}(o_{1}\widetilde{c}_{i}b)^{z_{i}}(o_{2}(1-\widetilde{c}_{i})(1-b))^{1-z_{i}},

(19)

where the equality follows
by applying the distributive law to Eq. (
13
)
.
To see Eq. (
15
), we first note that continuing from Eq. (
19
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{b}(\widetilde{c}^{t};o_{1},o_{2})}{\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{t}(o_{1}b)^{k}(o_{2}(1-b))^{t-k}\times

\displaystyle\quad\sum_{z^{t}\in\{0,1\}^{t}\mathchoice{\colon}{\colon}{\mathchar 58\relax\mspace{1mu}}{\mathchar 58\relax}k(z^{t})=k}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\widetilde{c}_{i}^{z_{i}}(1-\widetilde{c}_{i})^{1-z_{i}},

(20)

and thus integrating over
b
with respect to
\pi(b)
leads to Eq. (
15
) by the definition of Eq. (
7
).
The recursive update equation in Eq. (
12
) is easy to verify.
∎

IV-A
1
Confidence sequence from mixture portfolio

The wealth process of any mixture portfolio yields a confidence sequence as follows, which turns out to be always in the form of confidence

as desired.

In what follows, we define and denote the empirical mean of
Y^{t}
as
\widehat{\mu}_{t}\triangleq\frac{1}{t}\sum_{i=1}^{t}Y_{i}
.

Theorem 13
.

Let
(Y_{t})_{t=1}^{\infty}
be a
[0,1]
-valued sequence such that
\mathop{\mathsf{E}}[Y_{t}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}Y^{t-1}]\equiv\mu

(a)

For any
\delta\in(0,1]
, we have

IV-A
2
Implementation and complexity

Similar to Theorem
9
, we can apply the Newton–Raphson iteration or the bisect method to numerically compute the confidence intervals.
At each round
t
, however, it takes
O(Mt)
time complexity if
M
is the maximum number of iterations in a numerical root-finding method, since evaluating
h_{t}^{\pi}(\mu;y^{t})
for a given
\mu
takes
\Theta(t)
.
As a result, to process a sequence of length
T
, it takes a quadratic complexity
O(T^{2})
.

As alluded to earlier,
Orabona and Jun 2024
studied the wealth process of Cover’s UP which is the
\mathrm{Beta}(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
-mixture portfolio, and considered a tight lower bound based on a regret analysis of UP.
They first noted that, for any
b\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{Wealth}^{\texttt{UP}}_{t}(\widetilde{c}^{t};\frac{1}{m},\frac{1}{1-m})}{\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{b}(\widetilde{c}^{t})}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{x\in[0,t]}\frac{\widetilde{q}^{\mathsf{KT}}_{t}(x)}{\widetilde{q}_{t}^{b}(x)},

where
\widetilde{q}_{t}^{b}(x)\triangleq b^{x}(1-b)^{t-x}
and
\widetilde{q}^{\mathsf{KT}}_{t}(x)
is defined in Eq. (
12
).
They then considered the following lower bound

\displaystyle\mathsf{Wealth}^{\texttt{UP}}_{t}\Bigl(\widetilde{c}^{t};\frac{1}{m},\frac{1}{1-m}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\max_{b\in[0,1]}\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{b}\Bigl(\widetilde{c}^{t};\frac{1}{m},\frac{1}{1-m}\Bigr)\min_{x\in[0,t]}\frac{\widetilde{q}^{\mathsf{KT}}_{t}(x)}{\widetilde{q}_{t}^{b}(x)}

\displaystyle\geq\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{b^{*}(\widetilde{c}^{t})}\Bigl(\widetilde{c}^{t};\frac{1}{m},\frac{1}{1-m}\Bigr)\min_{x\in[0,t]}\frac{\widetilde{q}^{\mathsf{KT}}_{t}(x)}{\widetilde{q}_{t}^{b^{*}(\widetilde{c}^{t})}(x)},

(21)

where
b^{*}(\widetilde{c}^{t})\triangleq\argmax_{b\in[0,1]}\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{b}(\widetilde{c}^{t})
.
The second term of Eq. (
21
) can be bounded by a closed-form expression of the minimax regret (see
[
19
, Theorem 5]
), and thus, in principle, solving the maximization problem to find
b^{*}(\widetilde{c}^{t})
leads to a confidence sequence.
Since, however, the lower bound is not a quasiconvex function as a function of
m
,
Orabona and Jun 2024
came up with an algorithm, dubbed as PRECiSE-CO96, which numerically computes the intervals based on the final lower bound in Eq. (
21
).
While this algorithm gives empirically tighter intervals for small-sample regime and comparable performance in a long run compared to the existing methods of
Howard et al. 2021
, the algorithm and analysis are rather complicated by nature.

Moreover, the PRECiSE-CO96 still suffers the same per-round time complexity
O(t)
of Cover’s UP, since when computing
b^{*}(\widetilde{c}^{t})
, maximizing
\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{b}(\widetilde{c}^{t})
necessitates to evaluate the function which has
t
summands. They also proposed another algorithm PRECiSE-A-CO96 as a relaxation of PRECiSE-CO96, by invoking a version of an inequality due to
Fan et al. 2015
on their final lower bound, which runs in
O(1)
per round.
We propose a different approach of constant per-round complexity in the next section.

Remark 15

(A special case of discrete processes)
.

For a discrete sequence
\widetilde{c}^{t}\in\{0,1\}^{t}
, it is easy to check that the cumulative wealth in Eq. (
13
) simplifies to

\sum_{k=0}^{t}o_{1}^{k}o_{2}^{t-k}\psi_{t}^{\pi}(k)\widetilde{c}^{t}(k)=o_{1}^{\sum_{i=1}^{t}\widetilde{c}_{i}}o_{2}^{t-\sum_{i=1}^{t}\widetilde{c}_{i}}\psi_{t}^{\pi}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{t}\widetilde{c}_{i}\Bigr).

This implies that the mixture wealth in Eq. (

\{0,1\}
-valued processes without invoking the more general algorithm in Theorem
12
.
Since the horse-race-based method takes
O(T)
complexity for a sequence of length
T
, while the general algorithm may suffer
O(T^{2})
as elaborated below, it is worth treating discrete processes separately as a special case.
We note that this observation was not realized in
[
19
]
.

IV-B

A Mixture-of-Lower-Bounds Portfolio

In this section, we propose a straightforward and computationally efficient alternative to a mixture portfolio, based on a new tight lower bound to the wealth of a constant bettor.

To motivate our development, two remarks on the computational aspects on Cover’s UP are in order. We first note that the choice of the Beta distribution
\mathrm{Beta}(b;\alpha,\beta)
as a mixture distribution
\pi(b)
is closely related to the fact that the Beta distribution is the conjugate prior of the (unnormalized) binomial distribution
b^{k}(1-b)^{t-k}
(see Eq. (
16
)), where the specific choice
\alpha=\beta=\frac{1}{2}
is for its minimax optimality.
Second, we remark that the computational complexity of UP is mainly in computing the term in Eq. (
17
), which originates from the alternative wealth expression in Eq. (
19
) followed by the distributive law applied on Eq. (
13
).
Recall that
Orabona and Jun 2024
considered a lower bound on the wealth of UP to circumvent the computational complexity.

Alternatively, we propose to apply a tight lower bound on the cumulative wealth in Eq. (
13
) of constant bettors first, and then consider a mixture of the lower bounds. In this way, we can detour applying the distributive law and thus avoid the costly recursive expression in Eq. (
17
), resulting in a wealth lower bound that can be computed in constant complexity.
Note that it is still a valid approach for constructing a confidence sequence as PRECiSE is, since
we can always use a
deterministic
lower bound on the wealth process in Eq. (
II-C
), as it only results in a larger (and thus worse) confidence set.

In the following, we first state a new lower bound to be applied on Eq. (
13
). Since a mixture of the lower bounds is considered, we define a new mixture distribution in the form of a conjugate prior, by regarding the form of the lower bounds as an unnormalized exponential family distribution. While the use of conjugate prior is mathematically analogous to the choice of the Beta distribution in Cover’s UP, we remark that our choice of prior is purely based on computational considerations, and we do not claim any optimality of this choice, in contrast to the optimal choice of
\mathrm{Beta}(b;\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
in Cover’s UP.

We now propose to consider a mixture of the lower bounds over

For the sake of computational tractability,

IV-B

Our implementation employs the bisect method to compute the confidence sequence with the approach, as the Newton–Raphson iteration is hard to apply as the derivative of the LBUP wealth with respect to

This is in sharp contrast to Cover’s UP (as shown in Theorem

Remark 20

In general, there is no closed-form expression for this kind of integrals except the special case of

.

IV-B
5
A hybrid approach for the best of both worlds

On the one hand, while the UP approach becomes time-consuming as a sequence gets longer, it demonstrates a great empirical performance especially for a small-sample regime.
On the other hand, the LBUP approach performs almost identically to the UP approach with only constant per-round complexity, but it requires some burn-in samples to be tight enough as a lower bound as demonstrated below in Fig.

To achieve the best of the both worlds, we can hybrid the UP and LBUP approaches: namely, we run the UP for an initial few rounds, then we run the suboptimal lower-bound UP afterwards.

We emphasize that, in principle, any gambling strategy can be run in the first part, but we specifically use UP for its outstanding performance.

Formally, the performance of the hybrid approach can be stated as follows:

Corollary 21
.

Consider the gambling strategy that runs UP for the first
t_{\texttt{UP}}
steps and switches to run LBUP afterwards.
If the LBUP strategy uses the prior of the form

\pi_{m}^{(n)}(b;\bm{\rho}_{n}(y^{t_{\texttt{UP}}};m),\eta_{n}(y^{t_{\texttt{UP}}};m),\bm{\rho}_{n}(\bar{y}^{t_{\texttt{UP}}};\bar{m}),\eta_{n}(\bar{y}^{t_{\texttt{UP}}};\bar{m})),

then this hybrid gambling strategy attains the cumulative wealth defined in Eq. (
31
) at time step
t\geq t_{\texttt{UP}}
.

We remark that the choice of prior in Eq. (
21
) is natural as it is induced by the first part of the sequence
y^{t_{\texttt{UP}}}
.
We empirically demonstrate the effectiveness of this hybrid approach in Section

Related Work

Historically,

Darling and Robbins 1967
first introduced the notion of confidence sequence, and the idea was further studied by
Hoeffding 1963
and
Lai 1976
.
A recent line of work such as
[
22
,
32
,
12
,
13
,
19
]
has brought this idea back to researchers’ attention.

The concept of confidence sequences has a close connection to “safe testing”
[
8
]
.
The idea of gambling for confidence sequences (or equivalently, time-uniform concentration inequalities) can be found in several different areas.
Shafer and Vovk have advocated to use the idea of gambling to establish a game-theoretic theory of probability
[
26
,
25
]
.
Building upon the gambling approach,

Shafer 2021
proposed to test by betting.

Waudby-Smith and Ramdas 2020b
explored the gambling-based approach for constructing confidence sequences in a great detail, and derived and analyzed various betting algorithms.

Hendriks 2021
also explored a similar idea of using betting algorithms and numerically inverting the wealth processes into confidence intervals.

Jun and Orabona 2019
proposed to use coin betting for developing parameter-free online convex optimization algorithms using the notion of
regret
, and constructed a time-uniform concentration inequality that achieves the law of iterated logarithm for sub-Gaussian random vectors in Banach spaces; see
[
13
, Section 7.2]
.

Orabona and Jun 2024
examined the idea of
Cover 1991
’s universal portfolio and its regret analysis to construct tight confidence sequences based on a numerical method.

For a more detailed literature survey on the idea of gambling for confidence sequences, we refer an interested reader to
[
32
, Appendix D]
and
[
19
, Section 2]
, and references therein.

Waudby-Smith and Ramdas 2020a
studied the problem of estimating the mean of a set of deterministic numbers via random sampling without replacement.
As illustrated by
Waudby-Smith and Ramdas 2020b
, any construction of a confidence sequence under the current problem setting (including thus the UP and LBUP algorithms) can be easily converted into a confidence sequence for
the sampling without replacement problem.

The algorithms of
Orabona and Jun 2024
(dubbed as PRECiSE-CO96, PRECiSE-A-CO96, and PRECiSE-R70) based on the regret analysis were inspired by
Rakhlin and Sridharan 2017
, who showed an essential equivalence between the regret guarantee of certain online learning algorithms and concentration inequalities.
In contrast to such a regret-based approach, the current paper illustrates that a sharp concentration can be obtained directly via a deterministic lower bound of supermartingales, without invoking the notion of regret.
We acknowledge, however, that a regret analysis might be essential to analyze the resulting concentration inequality in a closed form, as was done therein.
PRECiSE-A-CO96, a constant-complexity version of PRECiSE-CO96, used a similar idea of lower bounding the multiplicative wealth by an inequality of
Fan et al. 2015
.
The difference in our work is twofold.
First, we rely on Lemma
16
, which is a higher-order-moments generalization of the lower bound; see Appendix
A-B
.
In the next section,
we also empirically show that the LBUP algorithm with
n>1
clearly improves LBUP with
n=1
, which corresponds to the technique of
Fan et al. 2015
and thus
Orabona and Jun 2024
.
Second, rather than maximizing over the betting
b\in[0,1]
and invoking the regret bound, we take a mixture over the lower bound as if it were the target wealth process.
While conceptually being simpler in the sense that it does not invoke a regret bound, the downside of our mixture approach is the computational bottleneck in the numerical integration step; see Remark
20
.
We finally remark that a mix-and-match approach of our higher-order-moments bound (Lemma
16
) and the regret approach
[
19
]
is natural;
we note, however, that maximizing the lower bound over
b
in Lemma
17
may not admit a closed-form expression for
n>1
, which necessitates to invoke a numerical optimization. We do not pursue this direction in the current paper.
Finally,
Orabona and Jun 2024
proposed another variant PRECiSE-R70 inspired by the mixture idea in
[
23
]
and showed that the resulting confidence sequence achieves the law of iterated logarithm.

At a high level,
the lower-bound approach (LBUP) we propose in this paper is similar in spirit to
[
13
,
19
,
32
]
that rely on a wealth lower bound.
Among these, however, LBUP is more closely related to the “lower bound on the wealth (LBOW)” approach of
[
32
]
, in the sense that both LBUP and
[
32
]
rely on a lower bound on
\log(b\frac{y}{m}+(1-b)\frac{1-y}{1-m})
; as alluded to earlier, we note that the lower bound in Lemma 16 generalizes the one used by LBOW.

As alluded to earlier, Lemma
16
is followed from a new property of the function
t\mapsto\log(1+t)
(Lemma
25
), which can be viewed as a higher-order extension of the proof technique of
[
6
, Lemma 4.1]
. Since this inequality has been used to derive exponential inequalities for martingales
[
6
]
and empirical Bernstein inequalities
[
12
,
31
]
, this technique could lead to a tighter result of another problem in this domain.
The idea of the conjugate prior is also common in the literature, including the beta prior of
Cover 1991
and a more recent example in
[
12
]
.
A subtle difference in our approach is that we directly take a mixture over a lower bound with respect to a conjugate prior tailored to the new lower bound we propose, other than aiming to maximize the lower bound with a rather heuristic approximation as done in
[
32
]
.

The topic of universal portfolio selection is a classical topic in information theory that has been studied for more than three decades, and has been established to have intimate connections to problems like data compression and sequential prediction.
An interested reader is referred to
[
4

and the references therein for a detailed treatment of these topics and their interconnections: Chapter 6 explores the horse-race formalism as well as establishes connections between betting on horse races and data compression; Chapters 11 and 13 discuss universal compression and connections to prediction and hypothesis testing; Chapter 14 investigates the closely allied concept of Kolmogorov complexity and the minimum description length (MDL) principle (see also
[
9

and
[
7

for a detailed treatise on the MDL principle); and Chapter 16 delves into portfolio selection and the UP method. Universal portfolio selection still remains a widely studied topic because a method that simultaneously achieves low regret and low time complexity has been elusive—see
[

VII
Concluding Remarks

In this paper, we established the properties of a class of universal-gambling-based algorithms for constructing confidence sequences, and also proposed a new algorithm which closely emulates the performance of the universal-portfolio-based method, with less time complexity.
Our argument is based on interpreting the existing gambling formalism as a (continuous) two-horse race.
We believe that this perspective is conceptually simpler and more intuitive, and thus allows the research community to translate and connect the results from information theory more easily.

One caveat in our experiments is that, as shown in the last columns of Figs.
2
and
3
, the UP method takes less time up to about lengths
10^{5}
in the current implementation, even though the LBUP and HybridUP methods incurs only linear complexity in principle. (Note also that the elapsed time for UP and PRECiSE are comparable.)
The computational bottleneck is in the numerical integration to compute the wealth lower bound in Eq. (
24
), as noted in Remark
20
.
We expect that a specially designed numerical method for the specific integral of our interest may reduce the time complexity and lead to a faster-than-UP implementation.

An intriguing question is whether one can generalize the argument of this paper to construct a confidence sequence for high-dimensional random vectors.
We leave this as a future research direction.

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle{(b)}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle{(b)}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle{(b)}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle{(b)}$\hss}}}}{{\geq}}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{x_{tj}}{o_{j}}\Psi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t})\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}_{{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=o_{j}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle{(c)}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle{(c)}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle{(c)}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle{(c)}$\hss}}}}{{\geq}}\Psi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t}).

Here, (a) follows from the induction hypothesis, (b) follows from Eq. (
B-A
), and (c) follows from (A2).

For the converse, first note that

\langle\mathbf{b}_{t},{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\rangle=\frac{\Psi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t-1}{\mathbf{x}}_{t})}{\Psi_{t-1}({\mathbf{x}}^{t-1})}

for any
{\bf x}_{t}\in\mathcal{M}
by the definition of the multiplicative game.
Then, we have

\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{1}{o_{j}}\Psi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t})\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}_{{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=o_{j}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}}}{\Psi_{t-1}({\mathbf{x}}^{t-1})}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{1}{o_{j}}\langle\mathbf{b}_{t},o_{j}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{b}_{t},\mathds{1}\rangle=1,

which is the condition in (A1) with equality. To verify that (A2) holds, consider

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{x_{tj}}{o_{j}}\Psi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t})\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}_{{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=o_{j}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}}

\displaystyle=\Psi_{t-1}({\mathbf{x}}^{t-1})\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{x_{tj}}{o_{j}}\frac{\Psi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t})\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}_{{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=o_{j}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}}}{\Psi_{t-1}({\mathbf{x}}^{t-1})}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle{(d)}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle{(d)}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle{(d)}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle{(d)}$\hss}}}}{{=}}\Psi_{t-1}({\mathbf{x}}^{t-1})\sum_{j=1}^{K}\frac{x_{tj}}{o_{j}}\langle\mathbf{b}_{t},o_{j}{\mathbf{e}}_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\Psi_{t-1}({\mathbf{x}}^{t-1})\langle\mathbf{b}_{t},{\mathbf{x}}\rangle=\Psi_{t}({\mathbf{x}}^{t}).

Here, (d) follows from Eq. (
B-A
). This concludes the proof.

∎

Proof of Lemma
14

Proof.

Note that, from Eq. (
20
), we can write

\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{b}\Bigl(y^{t};\frac{1}{\widehat{\mu}_{t}},\frac{1}{1-\widehat{\mu}_{t}}\Bigr)=\sum_{k=0}^{t}b^{k}(1-b)^{t-k}\frac{y^{t}(k)}{\widehat{\mu}_{t}^{k}(1-\widehat{\mu}_{t})^{t-k}}.

Hence, to conclude the desired inequality, it suffices to show that the function
b\mapsto\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{b}(y^{t};\frac{1}{\widehat{\mu}_{t}},\frac{1}{1-\widehat{\mu}_{t}})
is maximized at
b=\widehat{\mu}_{t}
, since

\displaystyle\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{b=\widehat{\mu}_{t}}\Bigl(y^{t};\frac{1}{\widehat{\mu}_{t}},\frac{1}{1-\widehat{\mu}_{t}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{t}y^{t}(k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{z^{t}\in\{0,1\}^{t}}\prod_{i=1}^{t}y_{i}^{z_{i}}(1-y_{i})^{1-z_{i}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{t}(y_{i}+(1-y_{i}))=1.

Since
b\mapsto b^{k}(1-b)^{t-k}
is log-concave (Lemma
22
) and a sum of any log-concave functions is also log-concave (Lemma
23
), so is the function
b\mapsto\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{b}(y^{t};\frac{1}{\widehat{\mu}_{t}},\frac{1}{1-\widehat{\mu}_{t}})
.
Hence, we only need to show that the derivative of the function with respect to
b
at
b=\widehat{\mu}_{t}
is zero.
Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\partial}{\mathop{}\!\partial b}\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}^{b}\Bigl(y^{t};\frac{1}{\widehat{\mu}_{t}},\frac{1}{1-\widehat{\mu}_{t}}\Bigr)\Big\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}_{b=\widehat{\mu}_{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{t}ky^{t}(k)}{\widehat{\mu}_{t}}-\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{t}(t-k)y^{t}(k)}{1-\widehat{\mu}_{t}}=0,

by Lemma
27
stated below.

Lemma 27
.

For any
y^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{t}
and
0\leq k\leq t
, define

y^{t}(k)\triangleq\sum_{z^{t}\in\{0,1\}^{t}\mathchoice{\colon}{\colon}{\mathchar 58\relax\mspace{1mu}}{\mathchar 58\relax}k(z^{t})=k}\prod_{i=1}^{t}y_{i}^{z_{i}}(1-y_{i})^{1-z_{i}}

as in Eq. (
17
).
Then,
we have
\sum_{k=0}^{t}y^{t}(k)=t
and

\sum_{k=0}^{t}ky^{t}(k)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}y_{i}.

Proof.

Let
q_{\ell}(y^{t})\triangleq\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\ldots<i_{\ell}\leq t}y_{i_{1}}\ldots y_{i_{\ell}}
for
0\leq\ell\leq t
.
Then, from the recursive equation Eq. (
12
), it is easy to show that

y^{t}(k)=\sum_{\ell=k}^{t}(-1)^{\ell+k}\binom{\ell}{k}q_{\ell}(y^{t}).

With this expression, we then have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{t}ky^{t}(k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{t}\sum_{\ell=k}^{t}(-1)^{\ell+k}\binom{\ell}{k}q_{\ell}(y^{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{t}(-1)^{\ell}q_{\ell}(y^{t})\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}(-1)^{k}k\binom{\ell}{k}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle{(a)}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle{(a)}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle{(a)}$\hss}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle{(a)}$\hss}}}}{{=}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{t}(-1)^{\ell}\ell q_{\ell}(y^{t})\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}(-1)^{k}\binom{\ell-1}{k-1}

\displaystyle=q_{1}(y^{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{t}y_{i},

which is the desired relation.
Here, (a) follows from the identity that
\sum_{j=0}^{n}(-1)^{j}\binom{n}{j}=0
for any
n\geq 1
.
∎

Proof of Lemma

Proof.

We first note that we only need to show the first case where
b\in[m,1)
and
y\geq 0
, since the second case follows by plugging in
b\leftarrow 1-b
,
m\leftarrow 1-m
,
y\leftarrow 1-y
into the first inequality.
Now, by Lemma
25
, we have

\displaystyle\log(1+x)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k=1}^{2n-1}(-1)^{k+1}\frac{x^{k}}{k}+\frac{x^{2n}}{x_{0}^{2n}}\Bigl(\log(1+x_{0})-\sum_{k=1}^{2n-1}(-1)^{k+1}\frac{x_{0}^{k}}{k}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{2n-1}\frac{(\frac{x}{x_{0}})^{2n}(-x_{0})^{k}-(-x)^{k}}{k}+\Bigl(\frac{x}{x_{0}}\Bigr)^{2n}\log(1+x_{0})

for any

g_{m}(b,y)=b\frac{y}{m}+(1-b)\frac{1-y}{1-m}-1

\frac{1-b}{1-m}\leq 1\leq\frac{b}{m}

\displaystyle-g_{m}(b,y)

Appendix C
KT Strategy for Continuous Two-Horse Race

Consider a gambling with odds vector
{\mathbf{x}}_{t}=[o_{1}\widetilde{c}_{t},o_{0}(1-\widetilde{c}_{t})]
for
\widetilde{c}_{t}\in[0,1]
given
o_{1},o_{0}>0
.

Note that the following statement is stated in parallel to Theorem
5
.

Theorem 28
.

Let
(\psi_{t}\mathchoice{\colon}{\colon}{\mathchar 58\relax\mspace{1mu}}{\mathchar 58\relax}[0,t-1]\to\mathbb{R}_{+})
be a sequence of nonnegative potential functions that satisfies the following condition:

(A1’)

(consistency) For any
0\leq x\leq t-1
,

\psi_{t-1}(x)\geq\frac{1}{o_{1}}\psi_{t}(x+1)+\frac{1}{o_{0}}\psi_{t}(x).

(A2’)

(convexity) For any
0\leq x\leq t-1
,

\widetilde{c}_{t}\mapsto\psi_{t}(x+\widetilde{c}_{t})

is convex.

For each
t\geq 1
, define

b_{t}(x)\triangleq\frac{\frac{1}{o_{1}}\psi_{t}(x+1)}{\frac{1}{o_{1}}\psi_{t}(x+1)+\frac{1}{o_{0}}\psi_{t}(x)}.

Then, the betting strategy
b_{t}(\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\widetilde{c}_{i})
satisfies

\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}\geq\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\psi_{t}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{t}\widetilde{c}_{i}\Bigr).

Proof.

We prove by induction.
Assume that

\mathsf{Wealth}_{t-1}\geq\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\psi_{t-1}(x_{t-1}),

where we denote
x_{t-1}\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\widetilde{c}_{i}
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\mathsf{Wealth}_{t}}{\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}}

\displaystyle=(o_{1}\widetilde{c}_{t}b_{t}+o_{0}(1-\widetilde{c}_{t})(1-b_{t}))\frac{\mathsf{Wealth}_{t-1}}{\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}}

\displaystyle\geq(o_{1}\widetilde{c}_{t}b_{t}+o_{0}(1-\widetilde{c}_{t})(1-b_{t}))\psi_{t-1}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\widetilde{c}_{i}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\geq(o_{1}\widetilde{c}_{t}b_{t}+o_{0}(1-\widetilde{c}_{t})(1-b_{t}))\Bigl(\frac{\psi_{t}(x_{t-1}+1)}{o_{1}}+\frac{\psi_{t}(x_{t-1})}{o_{0}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{c}_{t}\psi_{t}(x_{t-1}+1)+(1-\widetilde{c}_{t})\psi_{t}(x_{t-1})

\displaystyle\geq\psi_{t}(x_{t-1}+\widetilde{c}_{t})=\psi_{t}(x_{t}).

This concludes the proof by induction.
∎

Corollary 29
.

Recall the mixture potential defined in Eq. (
7
):

\displaystyle\widetilde{\phi}_{t}(x;o_{1},o_{2})\triangleq o_{1}^{x}o_{0}^{t-x}\frac{B(x+\frac{1}{2},t-x+\frac{1}{2})}{B(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})}.

Then, the wealth of the betting strategy

\mathbf{b}_{t}(\widetilde{c}^{t-1})=\Bigl[b_{t}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\widetilde{c}_{i}\Bigr),1-b_{t}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\widetilde{c}_{i}\Bigr)\Bigr]

with

\displaystyle b_{t}(x)\triangleq\frac{1}{t}\Bigl(x+\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)

for
x=x_{t-1}\in[0,t-1]
,
is lower bounded by

\mathsf{Wealth}_{0}\widetilde{\phi}_{t}\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{t-1}\widetilde{c}_{i};o_{1},o_{2}\Bigr).

Proof.

It is easy to check that the potential satisfies both conditions (A1’) and (A2’).
Indeed, (A1’) holds with equality, and (A2’) follows as a corollary by the logarithmic convexity of the mapping
x\mapsto\psi_{t}(x)
over
[0,t]
.
Therefore, the induced betting strategy defined in Eq. (
28
) achieves the wealth at least the coin betting potential
\psi_{t}(x_{t})
.
Here, note that, for any
o_{1},o_{0}>0
,

\displaystyle b_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{o_{1}}\psi_{t}(x+1)}{\frac{1}{o_{1}}\psi_{t}(x+1)+\frac{1}{o_{0}}\psi_{t}(x)}=\frac{1}{t}\Bigl(x+\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)

for
x=x_{t-1}\in[0,t-1]
, which is equivalent to the KT strategy for the standard even-odds case
o_{1}=o_{0}
.
Invoking Theorem
28
concludes the proof.
∎
