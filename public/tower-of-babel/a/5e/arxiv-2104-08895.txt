Pathlike Co/Bialgebras and their Antipodes with Applications to Bi- and Hopf Algebras Appearing in Topology, Number Theory and Physics This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Algebraic Structures in Perturbative Quantum Field Theory in honor of Dirk Kreimer for his 60th birthday. The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/Kreimer.html

Abstract
We develop an algebraic theory of colored, semigrouplike-flavored and pathlike co-, bi- and Hopf algebras. This is the right framework in which to discuss antipodes for bialgebras naturally appearing in combinatorics, topology, number theory and physics. In particular, we can precisely give conditions for the invertibility of characters that is needed for renormalization in the formulation of Connes and Kreimer. These are met in the relevant examples. In order to construct antipodes, we discuss formal localization constructions and quantum deformations. These allow to define and explain the appearance of Brown style coactions. Using previous results, we can interpret all the relevant coalgebras as stemming from a categorical construction, tie the bialgebra structures to Feynman categories, and apply the developed theory in this setting.

Although the use of Hopf algebras has a long history, the seminal paper

2
Colored algebras and coalgebras

Before delving into the constructions, we review the general notions, recall the key examples and introduce the notion of colored coalgebras, which formalizes the type of coalgebra obtained from a category or equivalently a partial monoid that is colored.
We end with a short overview of the complications that arise in the presence of isomorphisms. One resolution to this problem is to work with equivalence
classes, which is done in Section
5
.

2.1.1
Internal product

There are several complications when working over a commutative ring. First,
there is no canonical way to identify a tensor product of submodules with a submodule.
This may lead to different subcoalgebra structures.
Second, a submodule with the restriction of the coalgebra structure may not yield a coalgebra.
It may not even be possible to give a coalgebra structure.
To avoid these issues, we will use an internal product denoted by

Given a coalgebra

\displaystyle M\boxtimes N:=\{\text{finite~sums~of~}\,x_{(1)}\otimes x_{(2)}\in C\otimes C\,|\,x_{(1)}\in M,\,x_{(2)}\in N\}.

2.2

2.2.1
Path coalgebra

The paradigmatic example and namesake for the article is the

Given a quiver

p=(\vec{e}_{1}\cdots\vec{e}_{n})

Consecutive means that the target vertex

of

is the source vertex

of
\vec{e}_{i+1}
.
By definition for each vertex
v

0
, with source and target
v
. This
the identity at
v
, which is traditionally denoted simply by
v
. Using this notation, let
P(Q)
be the set of paths of

has the coalgebra structure

\displaystyle\Delta(\vec{e}_{1}\cdots\vec{e}_{n})={s(\vec{e}_{1})}\otimes(\vec{e}_{1}\cdots\vec{e}_{n})+\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(\vec{e}_{1}\cdots\vec{e}_{i})\otimes(\vec{e}_{i+1}\cdots\vec{e}_{n})+(\vec{e}_{1}\cdots\vec{e}_{n})\otimes{t(\vec{e}_{n})}.

The counit is
\epsilon(v)=1
and
\epsilon(\vec{e}_{1}\cdots\vec{e}_{n})=0,n\geq 0

There is a grading given by the length of a path.
The grouplike elements are exactly the length
0

are exactly the skew primitive, with
(\vec{e})
being

(s(\vec{e}),t(\vec{e}))
-skew primitive. This coalgebra is not connected if there is more than one vertex.

Remark 2.7

In particular, the complete graph on two vertices

Remark 2.8
.

Note that there are two equivalent, opposite, ways to write down the composition maps,
which correspond to the two ways to write the compositions

\operatorname{Mor}{\vphantom{\times}}_{s}{\times}_{t}\operatorname{Mor}\to\operatorname{Mor}
or
\operatorname{Mor}{\vphantom{\times}}_{t}{\times}_{s}\operatorname{Mor}\to\operatorname{Mor}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hom}(X,Y)\times\operatorname{Hom}(Y,Z)\to\operatorname{Hom}(X,Z),\qquad(\phi_{0},\phi_{1})\mapsto\phi_{0}\cdot\phi_{1}:=\phi_{1}\circ\phi_{0},

(2.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Hom}(Z,Y)\times\operatorname{Hom}(X,Y)\to\operatorname{Hom}(X,Z),\qquad(\phi_{0},\phi_{1})\mapsto\phi_{0}\circ\phi_{1}.

(2.2)

We will call the first monoidal and the second categorical.
The two coproducts are opposites, i.e.,

and

The first version of this for the coproduct is what is used for posets and quivers and fits with the Connes–Kreimer coproduct

,
25
]
,
where the subgraph is on the left and the cograph is on the right, cf. (

more natural from a category point of view and corresponds to the tree Hopf algebra, where the stump is on the left and the branches
on the right, cf. (

).

Note that this ambiguity is non-essential for a coalgebra or a bialgebra.
For a Hopf algebra, it might

make a difference, –

is a Hopf algebra, but

may not be –
but if the Hopf algebra comes from a category

Remark 2.9
.

Path and incidence coalgebras arise from categories as follows: For
P(Q)
the objects are the vertices of

and
M=P(Q)
.
The source
s
and target map

map a path to its start and end vertices, respectively. The composition is the concatenation of paths, and the identities are the length

This is the free category generated by the morphisms corresponding to the edges.

A poset

defines a category whose objects are
S
and whose morphisms are defined as follows.
There is one exactly one morphism
\phi_{x\to y}

between

and

if and only if

. The category is locally finite when the poset is.
The categorical coproduct is

or in the usual poset notation, where one identifies

with the interval

. The identities are
{\mathrm{id}}_{x}=[x,x]
.

This category is the quotient of a quiver category.
The quiver has vertices

and directed edges given by the

(x,y)
-skew primitives. These are the elements

,
x\neq y
, where there is no

.
The quotient is given by identifying two morphisms, i.e., paths,
p
and
q
whenever they have the same source and target.
Categorically speaking this trivializes
\pi_{1}
of the category making each component simply connected.

Algebraically speaking, a small category is the same as a colored monoid which is a particular type of partial monoid.

Definition 2.10
.

A
colored monoid

is a set together with the following data. A set
X
of colors, two morphisms

, and a partial product

\circ\colon M{\vphantom{\times}}_{s}{\times}_{t}M\to M

It is

, which is a section of both

and

A colored monoid is called

It is graded if there is a degree function

A degree function is proper is

The equivalence between colored monoids and categories is given by the identifications

and

with

We will need the following result

In a decomposition finite colored unital monoid any left or right invertible element is invertible.

A categorical coalgebra is a dual construction to that of a colored monoid. In the case of finite

If

2.2.4
Complications from isomorphisms

A colored unital monoid is a groupoid, if all of its elements are invertible in the colored sense, viz there is a morphism
{}^{-1}\colon M\to M
, such that

\phi^{-1}\circ\phi={\mathrm{id}}_{s(\phi)}
and
\phi\circ\phi^{-1}={\mathrm{id}}_{t(\phi)}
.
As a category, this means that all morphisms are isomorphisms.
Given a category
{\mathcal{C}}
, the underlying groupoid
\operatorname{Iso}({\mathcal{C}})
is defined by the objects of
{\mathcal{C}}
with only the isomorphisms.
This groupoid acts from the left and right on morphisms by conjugation
\phi\to(\sigma\!\Downarrow\!\sigma^{\prime})(\phi):=\sigma^{\prime}\circ\phi\circ\sigma^{-1}
.

Lemma 2.12
.

The product of
M
being locally finite necessitates that there are only finitely many objects in each isomorphism class and that all automorphism groups are finite.

An identity morphism
{\mathrm{id}}_{x}
is grouplike in
C[M]
, if and only if
x
is the only element in its isomorphism class and it has no non-trivial automorphisms.

If there is a skew-primitive morphism
\phi\colon x\to y
, then
x
and
y
are the only elements in their isomorphism class and both have no non-trivial automorphisms.

Proof.

For an identity
{\mathrm{id}}_{x}
the deconcatenation coproduct
will have a term
\sigma\otimes\sigma^{-1}
of any
\sigma\in\operatorname{Iso}(x,-)
, which proves the first two statements.

For a decomposition finite noninvertible
\phi\colon x\to y
in the monoidal convention (
2.1
) the coproduct is

\Delta(\phi)=\sum_{\sigma_{x}\in\operatorname{Iso}(X,-),\sigma_{y}\in\operatorname{Iso}(Y,-)}\big[\sigma_{x}\otimes\sigma_{x}^{-1}\cdot\phi+\phi\cdot\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y}^{-1}\big]+\cdots,

(2.3)

where the first summands are always present.
The element
\phi
being skew-primitive means that all the terms except
\phi\otimes{\mathrm{id}}_{y}+{\mathrm{id}}_{x}\otimes\phi
are not present, which is the third statement.
∎

We will call a morphism
\phi

essentially indecomposable
if the only decompositions into two factors have at least
one factor which is an isomorphism. For an identity these are the terms
\sigma\otimes\sigma^{-1}
,
and if
\phi
is not an identity, the corresponding terms of
\Delta(\phi)
are the displayed terms in (
2.3
).

Taking isomorphism classes will make it possibly to remedy the situation arising from too few grouplikes and skew-primitives
in case the action of
\operatorname{Iso}({\mathcal{C}})
behaves nicely; which it does for a Feynman category,
see Section
5.2
.

If there is only one object in each isomorphism class of objects, i.e.,
{\mathcal{C}}
is skeletal, then the sum will only be over automorphisms. In the case of a finite groupoid with just one color, i.e.,
M=G
is a finite group, the deconcatenation coproduct is simply the familiar

\displaystyle\Delta(g)=\sum_{h\in G}gh\otimes h^{-1}=\sum_{h\in G}h\otimes h^{-1}g.

2.3.1
Colored coalgebras

The coalgebra on

, defined by letting all generators be grouplike, i.e.,
\Delta(x)=x\otimes x

.
Such a coalgebra, viz. a coalgebra freely generated by grouplikes, is often called

Lemma 2.13
.

A right
(
or left
)

by

on an

is equivalent to grading by

, that

Proof.

Given a coaction, set
M_{x}=\{m\,|\,\rho(m)=m\otimes x\}
. Each

\rho(m)=\sum_{x}m_{x}\otimes x
is a finite sum and thus the module is the sum of the
M_{x}

\Delta(x)=x\otimes x
so that

.
and the sum is direct. Vice-versa setting

for

defines a coaction. One can proceed similarly for a left coaction.
The fact that left and right coactions commute proves the last statement.
∎

For a coalgebra, given a left comodule

and a right comodule

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M{\vphantom{\square}}_{\lambda}{\square}_{\rho}N}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M\otimes_{R}N}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M\otimes_{R}C\otimes_{R}N.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho_{R}\otimes_{R}{\mathrm{id}}_{N}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces id_{M}\otimes_{R}\rho_{L}}

This is generated by elements

with

which is dual to the condition that

.

c\in F^{Q}_{n}(C)\cap\overline{C}
, by induction there is some
N
such that
\overline{\Delta}^{[N]}(c)=0
.
This is clear for
c\in F_{0}^{Q}C
and if
c\in F^{Q}_{n+1}C\cap\overline{C}
then
\overline{\Delta}(c)\in F^{Q}_{n}C\otimes F^{Q}_{n}C
so there is some
N
such
that
\big(\overline{\Delta}^{[N]}\otimes{\mathrm{id}}\big)\overline{\Delta}(c)=\overline{\Delta}^{[N+1]}(c)=0
.
Vice-versa, assume that

is
\overline{\Delta}
-flat color conilpotent. Again using induction, as
F^{N}_{0}C=F_{0}^{Q}C
, we can assume that
F^{N}_{n-1}C\subset F^{Q}_{n-1}C
. Now, if
c\in\ker\big(\overline{\Delta}^{[n]}\big)
, then, since
C
is
\overline{\Delta}
-flat,
\overline{\Delta}(c)\in\ker\big(\overline{\Delta}^{[n-1]}\otimes{\mathrm{id}}\big)\cap\ker\big({\mathrm{id}}\otimes\overline{\Delta}^{[n-1]}\big)=\ker\big(\overline{\Delta}^{[n-1]}\big)\otimes C\cap C\otimes\ker\big(\overline{\Delta}^{[n-1]}\big)\subset F^{Q}_{n-1}F\otimes F_{n-1}^{Q}C
and

.
∎

Lemma 2.23
.

A color coaugmented coalgebra
C
colored by
X

is
\overline{\Delta}
-flat if
C
is flat. In particular,
\overline{\Delta}
flatness is automatic over a field or if
C
is free.

Proof.

Consider the short exact sequence
0\to\ker\big(\overline{\Delta}^{[n]}\big)\to C\to{{\mathrm{im}}\,}\big(\overline{\Delta}^{[n]}\big)\to 0
. By right exactness of
\otimes
:

\ker\big(\overline{\Delta}^{[n]}\big)\otimes C\to C\otimes C\to{{\mathrm{im}}\,}\big(\overline{\Delta}^{[n]}\big)\otimes C\to 0
is exact. If
C
is flat,
0\to{\mathrm{im}}\,\big(\overline{\Delta}^{[n]}\big)\otimes C^{\otimes n}\to C\otimes C
is exact and hence

\ker\big(\overline{\Delta}^{[n]}\big)\otimes C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i\otimes{\mathrm{id}}}}{{\to}}C\otimes C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\overline{\Delta}^{[n]}\otimes{\mathrm{id}}}}{{\to}}{\mathcal{C}}^{\otimes n}\otimes C

\ker\big(\overline{\Delta}^{[n]}\otimes{\mathrm{id}}\big)=\ker\big(\overline{\Delta}^{[n]}\big)\otimes C
.
∎

Proposition 2.24
.

For a decomposition finite colored monoid
M,
the categorical coalgebra
C[M]
is colored by its objects and color coaugmented.

If

only has identities as invertibles, then

is colored by its grouplikes.
If additionally,

is color nilpotent, then it is color connected.

In particular, if

has a proper degree function, is locally finite and has no non-trivial identities, then it is colored by its grouplikes and is color connected.

Proof.

The first statement is clear by construction. If identities are the only invertible elements,
then by Lemma
2.12
they are grouplike, and
these are also the only semigrouplike elements. Hence,
C[M]
is colored by its grouplikes.
As

is free being color nilpotent implies being color connected by Lemma
2.23

and Proposition
2.22
.
The last statement follows from the previous ones and Corollary
2.21
.
∎

2.3.2
Colored bialgebras and the free bialgebra
FB
[
M
] on a colored monoid

Generally, we will say that a bialgebra has an attribute like colored, color connected etc. if its coalgebra does.

Lemma 2.25
.

Given a colored connected bialgebra colored by
X
the ideal

generated by
x-y
, for

is a coideal and

is Quillen connected.

Proof.

Indeed,
\epsilon(x-y)=1-1=0
and

, so

.
The induced filtration

∎

The following construction is key for many examples. In particular for constructing bialgebras from posets or quivers.

For a set
X
let
X^{\times}=\amalg_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}}X^{\times n}

be the free monoid on
X
. The unit

is represented by the empty product.
If

is a monoid then

additionally carries a componentwise monoidal structure:

(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})=(a_{1}b_{1},\dots,a_{n}b_{n})
.
Consider
R^{(M^{\times})}
as the

free unital algebra
on
M^{\times}
. If

is decomposition finite,
this has a coalgebra structure induced by that of

, whose comultiplication is given by
\Delta(1)=1\otimes 1
and
\Delta((a_{1},\dots,a_{n}))=\sum\big(a_{1}^{(1)},\dots,a_{n}^{(1)}\big)\otimes\big(a_{1}^{(2)},\dots,a_{n}^{(2)}\big)
, where
\Delta(a_{i})=\sum a_{i}^{(1)}\otimes a_{i}^{(2)}
is the coproduct of

. The counit is

\epsilon((a_{1},\dots,a_{n}))=\epsilon(a_{1})\cdots\epsilon(a_{n})
.
These structures make

into a unital counital bialgebra with counit

and unit

, which we will denote by

.

Proposition 2.26
.

If

, then

is colored by

. In this case the underlying algebra structure of

and

Proof.

The coloring is given by
s(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})=(s(a_{1}),\dots,s(a_{n}))
and

. The computations

∎

In this construction the original composition is turned into a coproduct. Then a free product is added to make a bialgebra.
If

is interpreted as a category

.
The case were there already is a monoidal structure, which is not necessarily free,

.

and antipodes

3.1
QT-filtration and convolution inverses

In order to define antipodes, or more generally convolution inverses,
it is useful to regard filtrations, as in good cases one can recursively build up the antipode from its value on the degree
0
part.
To this end, we put forward the definition of a QT-filtration (Quillen–Takeuchi)
that generalizes the classical graded, coalgebra, coradical, Quillen
[
56
]
filtrations and their colored conilpotent versions.
This notion is what is needed to prove a general version of Takeuchi’s Lemma
[
60
]
, for the existence of convolution inverses.

Definition 3.1
.

A
QT-filtration
of a coalgebra
C
is a filtration of
C
by
R
-submodules
F^{QT}_{p}(C)
,
p\geq 0
such that:

1.

F^{QT}_{0}C=S
is a subcoalgebra.

2.

\Delta\big(F^{QT}_{p}C\big)\subset F^{QT}_{p-1}C\boxtimes C+C\boxtimes F^{QT}_{p-1}C
.

We call a QT-filtration a
QT-sequence
if the filtration is exhaustive, i.e.,
C=\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}F_{i}C
and call the QT-sequence
split
,
if
F^{QT}_{0}C
is a direct summand, that is
0\to F^{QT}_{0}C\to C\to F^{QT}_{0}C/C\to 0
is split.

A coaugmented coalgebra is
QT-connected if is has a QT-sequence whose degree
0
part is
{\rm Re}
.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that a QT-filtration is defined by specifying a base, that is a subcoalgebra
S
and setting:

F^{QT}_{0}C=S
and
F^{QT}_{r}C=\big\{x\,|\,\Delta(x)\in C\boxtimes F^{QT}_{0}C+F^{QT}_{p-1}C\boxtimes F^{QT}_{p-1}C+F^{QT}_{0}C\boxtimes C\big\}
. Quillen’s original filtration
[
56
]
for a coaugmented coalgebra is obtained from
S=F^{QT}_{0}={\rm Re}
. In the category of
R
-coalgebras, every coalgebra filtration gives rise to a QT-filtration with
S
being the degree
0
part.

The filtration associated with the grading of an
\mathbb{N}
-graded coalgebra
(
F^{QT}_{r}:=\{x\,|\,\mathrm{deg}x\leq r\}
)
is a QT-filtration based on
F^{QT}_{0}
, because
F^{QT}_{i}\subset F^{QT}_{r}
if
i\leq r
.
F^{QT}_{i}\boxtimes F^{QT}_{j}\subset F^{QT}_{r}\boxtimes F^{QT}_{q}
if
i\leq r
and
j\leq q
.
Given a
{\mathbb{Z}}
graded filtration, with
C_{0}
a subcoalgebra, and with
\Delta(F_{p})\subset F_{p-1}\boxtimes C+C\boxtimes F_{p-1}
,
p>0
and

\Delta(F_{p})\subset F_{p+1}\boxtimes C+C\boxtimes F_{p+1}
,
p<0
,
setting
F^{QT}_{p}=F_{p}+F_{-p}
,
p\geq 0
produces a QT-filtration.

Theorem 3.3
.

Consider a coalgebra
C
with counit
\epsilon
and an algebra
A
with unit
\eta
.
Given a QT-sequence
\big\{F^{QT}_{i}\big\}^{\infty}_{i=0}
on
C
, such that
{\rm Ext}^{1}_{R}\big(C/F^{QT}_{0}C,A\big)=0
,
for any element
f
of
\operatorname{Hom}(C,A)
,
f
is
\star
-invertible if and only if
the restriction
f|_{F^{QT}_{0}C}
is
\star
-invertible in
\operatorname{Hom}\big(F^{QT}_{0}C,A\big)
.

Proof.

First assume that
f
is convolution invertible in
\operatorname{Hom}(C,A)
, and let
g
be
\star
-inverse of
f
. By restriction to
F^{QT}_{0}C
,
see Lemma
2.2
,
it follows that
(\eta\circ\epsilon)|_{F^{QT}_{0}C}=(f\star g)|_{F^{QT}_{0}C}=f|_{F^{QT}_{0}C}\star g|_{F^{QT}_{0}C}
and

(\eta\circ\epsilon)|_{F^{QT}_{0}C}
is the unit of the restricted convolution algebra
\operatorname{Hom}\big(F^{QT}_{0}C,A\big)
. Thus
g|_{F^{QT}_{0}C}
is a right
\star
-inverse
of
f|_{F^{QT}_{0}}
. By symmetry, it is also a left
\star
-inverse and thus the
\star
-inverse in the restricted algebra.

Conversely, assume that
f|_{F^{QT}_{0}C}
is convolution invertible in
\operatorname{Hom}\big(F^{QT}_{0}C,A\big)
. Thus, there is an element

g
of
\operatorname{Hom}\big(F^{QT}_{0}C,A\big)
such that
f\star g=\eta\circ\epsilon=g\star f
on
F^{QT}_{0}C
.
We can extend the
R
-linear map
g
to
C
as the restriction morphisms
\operatorname{Hom}(C,A)\to\operatorname{Hom}\big(F^{QT}_{0}C,A\big)

is surjective due to the assumption that
\mathop{\rm Ext}\big(C/F^{QT}_{0}C,A\big)=0
.
Because the convolution algebra
(\operatorname{Hom}(C,A),\star,\eta\circ\epsilon)
is a unital monoid, if we show that
both
f\star g
and
g\star f
have a
\star
-inverse this will imply
f
has a
\star
-inverse.
Thus, replacing
f
with
f\star g
, we may assume
f|_{F^{QT}_{0}C}=\eta\circ\epsilon|_{F^{QT}_{0}C}

that is
\eta\circ\epsilon|_{F^{QT}_{0}C}-f|_{F^{QT}_{0}C}=0
.
Since the filtration is exhaustive, for every
x\in C
, there exists a natural number
N

such that
x\in F_{N}
and
(f-\eta\circ\epsilon)^{\star(N)}(x)=0
.
Indeed, after using the coassociativity for the diagonal to decrease the filtration degree,
every summand from
\Delta^{[N+1]}(x)=\sum_{x}x_{(1)}x_{(2)}x_{(3)}\cdots x_{(N+1)}
has to contain a factor in
F_{0}C

according to the definition of the QT-filtration, and hence
\eta\circ\epsilon-f
annihilates each term.
So, the infinite sum
h=\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}(\eta\circ\epsilon-f)^{\star i}
is locally finite and
yields a well defined map, where we set
(\eta\circ\epsilon-f)^{\star 0}=\eta\circ\epsilon
.
It is easy to verify that
h
is
R
-linear, so
h\in\operatorname{Hom}(C,A)
. That
h
is the inverse of
f
follows by computation:

f\star h=(f-\eta\circ\epsilon)\star h+\eta\circ\epsilon\star h=(f-\eta\circ\epsilon)\star h+h=-\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}(\eta\circ\epsilon-f)^{\star i}+\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}(\eta\circ\epsilon-f)^{\star i}=(\eta\circ\epsilon-f)^{\star 0}=\eta\circ\epsilon
. The fact that
h\star f=\eta\circ\epsilon

can be checked in the same way. Therefore, we conclude
h
is
\star
-inverse of
f
.
∎

Note that if
F^{QT}_{0}C
is a direct summand, i.e., the sequence
0\to F^{QT}_{0}C\to C\to C/F^{QT}_{0}C\to 0
splits, the Ext group vanishes, and the first condition is automatically met.

We say that a bialgebra has a QT-sequence, if the coalgebra has a QT-sequence. In this situation, Theorem
3.3
yields the generalization of the results of Quillen and Takeuchi, and we recover many of the existence theorems for antipodes as special cases.

Theorem 3.4
.

A bialgebra with a QT-sequence and
{\rm Ext}^{1}_{R}\big(C/F_{0}^{Q}C,C\big)=0
is Hopf, i.e., has an antipode, if and only if
{\mathrm{id}}|_{F^{QT}_{0}C}
has a
\star
inverse.

Corollary 3.5
.

A bialgebra with a split QT-sequence is Hopf if and only if
{\mathrm{id}}_{F_{0}^{Q}C}
has a convolution inverse. In particular, if
C
is a split QT-connected bialgebra, then it is Hopf.

Proof.

The first part directly follows from Theorem
3.4
. If
C
is QT-connected then
S(e)=e
is the required antipode on
F_{0}^{Q}C
.
∎

Proposition 3.6
.

The Quillen filtration
F^{Q}C
, cf. (
2.4
), for a color coaugmented coalgebra colored by
X
is a QT-filtration and a QT-sequence if
C
is connected.
For a
\overline{\Delta}
-flat color coaugmented coalgebra colored by
X
the conilpotent filtration
F^{N}C
, cf. (
2.5
),
is a QT-filtration with
F^{QT}_{0}C=C[X]
which is exhaustive if
\overline{\Delta}
is conilpotent.

Proof.

The first part follows from the fact that in this situation,
\overline{\Delta}=\bigoplus_{g,h\in X\times X}\Delta_{g,h}
and thus
F^{QT}_{r}C\supset F^{Q}_{r}(C)

with
F^{QT}_{0}C=C[X]=\bigoplus_{g\in\operatorname{sgpl}(C)}Rg
.
For the second part:
decompose
c\in F_{r}(C)
as
c_{0}+\bar{c}\in C=C[X]\oplus\bar{C}
with
\bar{c}
in
\ker\big(\overline{\Delta}^{[r]}\big)
, then by Theorem
2.18

\Delta(c)=c_{0}\otimes c_{0}+i_{X}\lambda(\bar{c})\otimes\bar{c}+c\otimes i_{X}\rho(\bar{c})+\overline{\Delta}(\bar{c})
with
\overline{\Delta}(\bar{c})\in\ker\big(\overline{\Delta}^{[r-1]}\big)\otimes{\rm id}
. Thus, by definition of
\overline{\Delta}
-flatness

\Delta(c)\in F_{0}C\boxtimes C\oplus C\boxtimes F_{0}C\oplus F_{r-1}\boxtimes C
.
∎

Corollary 3.7
.

A bialgebra whose coalgebra is colored by
X
and color connected, or
whose coalgebra is a
\overline{\Delta}
-flat color coaugmented coalgebra with colors
X
has an antipode if and only if every generator
x\in C[X]

has a algebra inverse
x^{-1}
.

The Proposition allows to give a recursive definition in both cases.

3.2
Sg-flavored bi- and coalgebras

In this section, we introduce a type of coalgebra which has a QT-filtration based on a decomposition according to
(g,h)
-primitives. This
generalizes path-coalgebras and colored coalgebras and subsumes the notions of
graded connected coalgebras and May’s component coalgebras
[
51
]
.
This type of idea is also used in
[
59
]
.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
C
be a
R
-coalgebra and
g
and
h
be semigrouplike elements.
We define the
(g,h)
-reduced comultiplication to be
\overline{\Delta}_{g,h}(x)=\Delta(x)-x\otimes g-h\otimes x
.

Lemma 3.9
.

Pairs of
\Delta_{g,h}
s have the following associators:

\displaystyle\big(\overline{\Delta}_{g,h}\otimes{\rm id}\big)\overline{\Delta}_{g,h}(x)-\big({\rm id}\otimes\overline{\Delta}_{g,h}\big)\overline{\Delta}_{g,h}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=x^{(1)}\otimes(h-g)\otimes x^{(2)}-x\otimes(h-g)\otimes g-h\otimes(h-g)\otimes x.

The
\overline{\Delta}_{g}:=\Delta_{g,g}
are coassociative,
thus, the iterated reduced comultiplication
\overline{\Delta}_{g}^{[n]}(x)
is well-defined.
Furthermore, if
g
is grouplike and
x\in\ker\epsilon
, then
(\epsilon\otimes{\rm id})\overline{\Delta}_{g}(x)=({\rm id}\otimes\epsilon)\overline{\Delta}_{g}(x)=0
.

Proof.

The first claim follows from the calculation:

\displaystyle\big(\overline{\Delta}_{g,h}\otimes{\rm id}\big)\overline{\Delta}_{g,h}(x)-\big({\rm id}\otimes\overline{\Delta}_{g,h}\big)\overline{\Delta}_{g,h}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad{}=\sum_{x}x^{(11)}\otimes x^{(12)}\otimes x^{(2)}-\sum_{x}x^{(11)}\otimes x^{(21)}\otimes x^{(22)}-x^{(1)}\otimes g\otimes x^{(2)}

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}-h\otimes x^{(1)}\otimes x^{(2)}-x^{(1)}\otimes x^{(2)}\otimes g+x\otimes g\otimes g+h\otimes x\otimes g+h\otimes g\otimes x

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}-\big[x^{(1)}\otimes x^{(2)}\otimes g-x^{(1)}\otimes h\otimes x^{(2)}+x\otimes h\otimes g-h\otimes x^{(1)}\otimes x^{(2)}+h\otimes x\otimes g

\displaystyle\qquad\hphantom{=}{}+h\otimes h\otimes x\big]=x^{(1)}\otimes(h-g)\otimes x^{(2)}-x\otimes(h-g)\otimes g-h\otimes(h-g)\otimes x.

The second claim is straightforward, e.g.,
(\epsilon\otimes{\rm id})\overline{\Delta}_{g}(x)=x-\epsilon(x)g-\epsilon(g)x=0
.
∎

Given a coalgebra
C
, we will inductively define
R
-modules each depending on two semigrouplike elements
g,h\in\operatorname{sgpl}(C)
. These are simultaneously defined for all pairs of such elements and yield pieces of a filtration.

1.

For any two
g,h\in\operatorname{sgpl}(C)\colon F_{0}^{Q}C_{g,h}:=Rg\cap Rh
is the base
R
-module.

2.

Inductively over all pairs:

F_{n}^{Q}C_{g,h}:=\big\{x\,|\,\overline{\Delta}_{g,h}(x)=\in\sum_{t\in\operatorname{sgpl}(C)}F_{n-1}^{Q}C_{g,t}\boxtimes F_{n-1}^{Q}C_{t,h}\big\}
.

In words,
F_{n}^{Q}C_{g,h}
is the subset in which every element
x
has the property that if
\overline{\Delta}_{g,h}(x)=\sum x^{(1)}\otimes x^{(2)}
then for each summand
x^{(1)}\otimes x^{(2)}
there exists a semigrouplike element
t
with
x^{(1)}\in F_{n-1}^{Q}C_{g,t}
and
x^{(2)}\in F_{n-1}^{Q}C_{t,h}
. This is also a
R
-module as every
F_{n}^{Q}C_{g_{1},g_{2}}
with
g_{1},g_{2}\in\operatorname{sgpl}(C)
is a
R
-module inductively.
The
(g,h)
-Quillen component
of
C
is defined to be
F^{Q}C_{g,h}=\sum_{i\geq 0}F^{Q}_{i}C_{g,h}
.

Lemma 3.10
.

The
F^{Q}_{i}C_{g,h}
yield filtrations of the
F^{Q}C_{g,h}
; that is

F_{i}C^{Q}_{g,h}\subset F_{i+1}C^{Q}_{g,h}
for
i\geq 0
.

Proof.

We use induction. Base case: to show that
Rg\cap Rh=F^{Q}_{0}C_{g,h}\subset F^{Q}_{1}C_{g,h}
pick
x\in F^{Q}_{0}C_{g,h}
, say
x=rg
, then
\overline{\Delta}_{g,h}(x)=-x\otimes h\in F_{0}C_{g,h}^{Q}\boxtimes F_{0}C_{h,h}^{Q}
. By induction assume that
F_{n-1}C^{Q}_{g,h}\subset F_{n}C^{Q}_{g,h}
. Let
x\in F^{Q}_{n}C_{g,h}
. Then
\overline{\Delta}_{g,h}(x)\in\sum_{t\in\operatorname{sgpl}(C)}F^{Q}_{n-1}C_{g,t}\boxtimes F_{n-1}C^{Q}_{t,h}\subset\sum_{t\in\operatorname{sgpl}(C)}F_{n}C^{Q}_{g,t}\boxtimes F_{n}C^{Q}_{t,h}
by induction hypothesis, and thus
x\in F_{n+1}C^{Q}_{g,h}
.
∎

Definition 3.11
.

The filtrations of
C^{Q}_{g,h}
yield a common filtration
F^{Q_{\bullet\bullet}}_{r}C:=\sum_{g,h}F^{Q}_{r}C_{g,h}
– the bivariate Quillen filtration.
We say a coalgebra
C
is
sg-flavored
(sg stands for semigrouplike) if the bivariate Quillen filtration is exhaustive, that is
C=\sum_{(g,h),g,h\in\operatorname{sgpl}(C)}C_{g,h}^{Q}
and
split
if
F^{Q_{\bullet\bullet}}_{0}C
is a direct summand.
A bi- or Hopf-algebra is sg-flavored if the underlying coalgebra is sg-flavored.

Remark 3.12
.

Note all semigrouplike elements of
C
lie in
F^{Q_{\bullet\bullet}}_{0}C
and all skew primitive elements
C
lie in
F^{Q_{\bullet\bullet}}_{1}C
. The degree zero component

F_{0}C^{Q}_{g,h}
may be non-empty, if
g,h
are two distinct semigrouplike elements, it may happen that there exist
s,t\in R
such that
sg=th
so that
Rg\cap Rh\not=0
.
This complication does not appear over a ground field
R=k
or if the respective submodules are free over
\mathbb{Z}
, as the equation then implies that
g=h
.

C^{Q}_{g,h}
is never empty. This is clear for
g=h
. For
g\neq h

\Delta(g-h)=g\otimes(g-h)+(g-h)\otimes h
and hence

\overline{\Delta}_{g,h}(g-h)=0\in F^{Q_{\bullet\bullet}}_{0}C_{g,t}\boxtimes F^{Q_{\bullet\bullet}}_{0}C_{t,h}
for any
t
.
Thus, if there is more than one semigrouplike element, the sum
C=\sum_{g,h}C_{g,h}^{Q}
is never direct, as
g-h
is in
\big(C^{Q}_{g,g}+C^{Q}_{h,h}\big)\cap C^{Q}_{g,h}
.

In the case of a field
R=k
,
g-h
generates the intersection and in fact over a field one can
make the sum direct by reducing
C_{g,h}
by
k(g-h)
following the line of arguments presented in
[
53
]
. Note that the corresponding splittings are not unique.

Lemma 3.13
.

The
R
-modules
F^{Q_{\bullet\bullet}}_{r}C:=\sum_{g,h}F_{r}C_{g,h}^{Q}
for
r\geq 0
, form a QT-filtration, which is a QT-sequence if
C
is sg-flavored.

Proof.

By definition the elements in
F^{Q_{\bullet\bullet}}_{0}C
are multiples of semigrouplike elements and hence
F_{0}C
is a subcoalgebra.
Condition (2) for a QT-filtration is satisfied by construction. In the sg-flavored case, the filtration is exhaustive by definition.
∎

Theorem 3.14
.

For an sg-flavored coalgebra with

-invertible in

Proof.

The following proposition explains the usual constructions of antipodes which pass through a connected quotient.

3.3
Pathlike coalgebras

In many applications, we need a notion which is stronger than being sg-flavored, yet is weaker than being QT-connected.

Definition 3.19
.

A sg-flavored coalgebra is said to be
pathlike
if

1.

\operatorname{sgpl}(C)=\operatorname{gpl}(C)
.

2.

F^{Q_{\bullet\bullet}}_{0}C=R^{(\operatorname{gpl})}=\bigoplus_{g\in\operatorname{gpl}(C)}Rg
as a submodule.

We say a pathlike coalgebra is split, if
F_{0}C
is a direct summand, i.e., the sequence of
R
modules
0\to F_{0}C\to C\to C/F_{0}C\to 0
is split.

A bi- or Hopf-algebra is pathlike or split pathlike if the underlying coalgebra is.

At the moment, we will disregard semigrouplike elements that are not grouplike.
Handling such elements is more tricky and they only appear in torsion, but they might be of interest in the situation with modified
counits,
[
25
, Section 2.2]
and
for applications like
[
39
]
.

Example 3.20
.

As expected by the name, the path coalgebra of a quiver
Q
is pathlike. Indeed,
\operatorname{sgpl}=\operatorname{gpl}
equals the set of vertices
V(Q)
. The base is given by the sum of the
Rv\cap Rw=\delta_{v,w}R_{v}
, thus
F^{Q_{\bullet\bullet}}C=\bigoplus_{v\in V}Rv
.
Note that
C_{1}C^{Q}_{g,h}=R\vec{e}+R(g-h)
, if there is a directed edge
\vec{e}
from
g
to
h
. It is split and
F^{Q_{\bullet\bullet}}_{0}C
is free.

The assumptions allow us to simplify and strengthen Theorem
3.14
:

Theorem 3.21
.

Let
C
be pathlike coalgebra and
A
algebra with
\mathop{\rm Ext}\big(C/F^{Q_{\bullet\bullet}}_{0}C,A\big)=0
. An element
f\in\operatorname{Hom}(C,A)
has an
\star
-inverse in the convolution algebra
\operatorname{Hom}(C,A)
if and only if for every grouplike element
g
,
f(g)
has an inverse as ring element in
A
.

In particular, in this situation, a character is
\star
-invertible if and only if it is grouplike invertible.

Furthermore, a pathlike bialgebra
B
is a Hopf algebra if and only if the set of grouplike elements form a group.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.14
,
f
having a
\star
-inverse is equivalent to
the restriction of
f
on
\bigoplus_{g\in\operatorname{gpl}(C)}Rg
considered as an element in
\operatorname{Hom}\big(\bigoplus_{g\in\operatorname{gpl}(C)}Rg,A\big)
having a convolution inverse.
Say that for each
g\in\operatorname{gpl}(C)
,
f(g)\in A
has an inverse then setting
h(g)=f(g)^{-1}
defines a convolution inverse on
F^{Q_{\bullet\bullet}}_{0}C
,
as
(f\star h)(g)=f(g)h(g)=1_{A}=\eta_{A}(\epsilon_{C}(g))
–symmetrically for
h\star g
. On the other hand, if
f
has a convolution inverse
f^{\star-1}
, then the extension of

h(g)=f(g)^{-1}
as above also yields a convolution inverse and these must agree.

Using Corollary
3.5
, and the theorem above, having an antipode on
B
is equivalent to having an antipode on
F_{0}^{Q}C=\bigoplus_{g\in\operatorname{gpl}}Rg
.
The only possible antipode on grouplike elements is
S(g)=g^{-1}
if the inverses exist. Hence the existence is equivalent to the existence of inverses.
∎

The following proposition will cover the targeted examples.

Proposition 3.22
.

A color connected colored coalgebra

is a pathlike coalgebra. In particular,
a
\overline{\Delta}
-flat color conilpotent colored coalgebra is a pathlike coalgebra.

Proof.

Let

be colored by

Being color coaugmented means that all

By definition

∎

Proposition 3.23
.

The coopposite of an sg-flavored, respectively pathlike, coalgebra is also sg-flavored, respectively pathlike.

First,
\operatorname{sgpl}(C^{\rm cop})=\operatorname{sgpl}(C)
.
Now, the definition of the filtration is symmetric and thus it is also exhaustive for

Remark 3.24

This means that there is a Drinfel’d double for sg-flavored or pathlike Hopf algebras, see, e.g.,

Corollary 3.25

The antipode of a split sg-flavored Hopf algebra

Proof.

Using Proposition

and Theorem

, it follows that

is also a Hopf algebra.
Indeed,
{\mathrm{id}}|_{F^{Q_{\bullet\bullet}}_{0}}
is generated by
\operatorname{sgpl}(C)

4
Renormalization, quotients and localization

The obstruction to having a Hopf algebra structure on a pathlike bialgebra is the invertibility of the grouplike elements.
There are basically two approaches to remedy this perceived deficiency. One is adding formal inverses, which is possible by universal constructions.
But, as at the end of the day, for renormalization à la Connes–Kreimer, one actually only needs convolution
inverses for characters, there is another option. This is to restrict the target algebras or to place restrictions on the characters – for instance that
the target algebra is commutative, or that the characters restricted to grouplike elements have special properties. In this case there are universal quotients that the characters factor through.
The commutativity assumption is natural. Namely, if the target algebra is not commutative, then the convolution of two characters need not be a character.
We first briefly explain the setup to give the motivation for these constructions.

4.1
Recollections on renormalization via characters

A renormalization scheme in the Connes–Kreimer formalization of the BPHZ renormalization
[
14
]
, is defined on the convolution algebra of a bialgebra with a Rota–Baxter (RB) algebra, see also
[
17
,
20
,
28
,
50
]
. Readers not familiar with RB algebras may consult Appendix
B
.

A Feynman rule is a character in the convolution algebra
\phi\in\operatorname{Hom}_{R\text{-}{\rm alg}}(B,A)
. The renormalization of
\phi
based on a Rota–Baxter (RB)-operator is a pair of characters

\phi_{\pm}\in\operatorname{Hom}_{R\text{-}{\rm alg}}(B,A_{\pm})

such that
\phi=\phi_{-}^{-1}\star\phi_{+}
, see Appendix
B
for the definition of the subalgebras
A_{\pm}
.
The character
\phi_{+}
is then the renormalized Feynman rule.
It was shown in
[
14
,
15
]
that there is a unique such decomposition for the Connes–Kreimer Hopf algebra of graphs and a commutative RB algebra, if
\phi
is the exponential of an
infinitesimal character. The solution is then given by a recursive formula. This was generalized in
[
19
]
for
B
any connected bialgebra and a character that is the exponential of an infinitesimal character. The following analysis provides the background for a generalization of these results to the case of a bialgebra with a QT-sequence.
If
B
is a Hopf algebra, then the inverse is given by
\phi_{-}^{-1}=\phi_{-}\circ S
, see Proposition
2.6
, but if
A
or the character has special properties, then the full assumption of

4.3

In general, one can formally invert the grouplike elements, this is a universal construction called the group completion if one is speaking of a monoid or the localization
if one is inverting a multiplicative subset. In the current

Given a subalgebra

There is an abstract construction in the general case, but it is not effective if there are no other conditions.
It can be realized as a quotient of the free algebra of

unital, set

\displaystyle T_{R}(A\otimes S^{\rm op})=R\oplus A\otimes S^{\rm op}\oplus A\otimes S^{\rm op}\otimes A\otimes S^{\rm op}\oplus\cdots,

Then

3)

If

I

Proof.

This is a straightforward calculation:

1.

\Delta(a\otimes\bar{1}\otimes a^{\prime}\otimes\bar{s}-aa^{\prime}\otimes\bar{s})=\sum a_{(1)}\otimes\bar{1}\otimes\sum a^{\prime}_{(1)}\otimes\bar{s}\otimes a_{(2)}\otimes\bar{1}\otimes a^{\prime}_{(2)}\otimes\bar{s}-\sum a_{(1)}a^{\prime}_{(1)}\otimes\bar{s}\otimes a_{(2)}a^{\prime}_{(2)}\otimes\bar{s}\equiv 0\mod(I\otimes A+A\otimes I)

For the counit, it is a straightforward check that

5
Colored bialgebras from Feynman categories

Deconcatenation is a way to turn a category or colored monoid into a coalgebra, if certain conditions are met.
One way to obtain a bialgebra from a coalgebra
C
is to consider the free algebra which is the tensor algebra
TC
and
extend
\Delta
using the bialgebra equation. More generally, if the colored monoid has a product structure, that is actually
a monoidal structure for the category, one can ask if the bialgebra equation holds for the product
\mu=\otimes

and the deconcatenation coproduct
\Delta
. The answer is that it does for Feynman categories, see
[
41
]
,
that are suitably decomposition finite, so that
\Delta
is well defined.
The axioms for a Feynman category roughly say that the objects
are a free
\otimes
-monoid on basic objects and that the morphisms are a free monoid on the so-called basic morphisms, which have
\otimes
indecomposables as targets.
There are two cases: one with symmetries, i.e., a symmetric monoidal structure, and one without.
The latter is simpler as the former necessitates to pass to equivalence classes, which also makes the product commutative.
Concretely, we will consider the bialgebras
B({\mathcal{F}})
for non-symmetric and
B^{\rm iso}({\mathcal{F}})
for symmetric Feynman categories.
With some conditions, both cases are color coaugmented and hence pathlike by Proposition
3.22
and hence Theorem
4.14
applies.

The grouplike elements correspond to the objects and are represented by their identities
{\mathrm{id}}_{X}
in the absence of isomorphisms and by their classes for
B^{\rm iso}
.
Note these are usually not
\otimes
-invertible. For this they would have to lie in
\operatorname{Pic}({\mathcal{F}})
,
which is not usually the case. It is, however, a natural assumption, that their images under characters are invertible as this happens in concrete applications.

5.1
Feynman categories and gradings

Notation 5.1
.

\operatorname{Iso}({\mathcal{F}})
is the subcategory of all objects of
{\mathcal{F}}
with their isomorphisms.
For a category
{\mathcal{V}}^{\otimes}
will denote the free (symmetric) monoidal category. This is essentially the category of words (with or without symmetries) in
{\mathcal{V}}
, cf.
[
35
, Section 2.4]
for an overview and
[
31
,
49
]
for details. It comes equipped with a functor
j\colon{\mathcal{V}}\to{\mathcal{C}}^{\otimes}
and the universal property that any functor
F\colon{\mathcal{V}}\to{\mathcal{C}}
to a (symmetric) monoidal category lifts to
F^{\otimes}\colon{\mathcal{V}}^{\otimes}\to{\mathcal{C}}
, viz.
F=F^{\otimes}j
.

The comma category
(F\downarrow G)
for two functors
F\colon\mathcal{D}\to{\mathcal{C}}
and
G\colon\mathcal{E}\to{\mathcal{C}}

has as objects
(X,Y,\phi)
with
X\in\mathcal{D}
,
Y\in{\mathcal{C}}
, and
\phi\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(F(X)\to G(Y))
.
The morphisms are given by commutative diagrams.
That is a morphism from
(X,Y,\phi)\to(X^{\prime},Y,\phi^{\prime})
is given by a pair

,
g\colon Y\to Y^{\prime}
, such that
G(g)\phi=\phi^{\prime}F(f)
.
If
F,G
are clear, we will write
(\mathcal{D}\downarrow\mathcal{E})
for the comma category.
The category
\operatorname{Iso}(\operatorname{ar}(F))
, viz. the underlying groupoid of the arrow category, has the morphisms of
{\mathcal{F}}
as objects while the morphisms of the category are given by pairs of isomorphisms
(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})
which map
f\mapsto\sigma^{-1}f\sigma^{\prime}
when the compositions are defined.

A slice category is the comma category

({\mathcal{C}}\downarrow X)
, where
(X,{\mathrm{id}}_{x})
is a viewed as a subcategory with one object.

Definition 5.2
.

A Feynman category is a triple
({\mathcal{V}},{\mathcal{F}},\imath)
, where
{\mathcal{V}}
is groupoid,
{\mathcal{F}}
is
a (symmetric) monoidal category and
\imath\colon{\mathcal{V}}\to{\mathcal{F}}
is a functor, such that

1.

The free (symmetric) monoidal category
{\mathcal{V}}^{\otimes}
is equivalent to
\operatorname{Iso}({\mathcal{F}})
via
\imath^{\otimes}
.

2.

The groupoid of morphisms
\operatorname{Iso}(\operatorname{ar}({\mathcal{F}}))
is equivalent to the free (symmetric) monoidal category on the morphisms from objects from
{\mathcal{V}}^{\otimes}
to
{\mathcal{V}}
. In formulas
\operatorname{Iso}(\operatorname{ar}({\mathcal{F}}))\simeq

\operatorname{Iso}({\mathcal{V}}^{\otimes}\downarrow{\mathcal{V}})^{\otimes}
.

3.

All slice categories
{\mathcal{F}}
are essentially small.

A Feynman category is
strict
if its monoidal structure is strict.

The objects of
{\mathcal{V}}
, sometimes called vertices, are the basic objects in the following sense.
The first condition says that every object of
{\mathcal{F}}
decomposes as
X\simeq\imath(*_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes\imath(*_{n})
essentially uniquely, where this means up to isomorphisms on the indecomposables and in the symmetric case up to permutations.

Dropping the
\imath
, the second condition means that any morphism
\phi\colon Y\to X
of
{\mathcal{F}}
decomposes into indecomposables as well
\phi=\phi_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\phi_{n}
, where each of the
\phi_{i}\colon Y_{i}\to*_{i}
is a basic morphism, with
X\simeq*_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes*_{n}
and

. Again this is essentially unique, that is up to isomorphisms on the
\phi_{i}
and permutations in the symmetric case. The third condition is technical and used for the existence of certain colimits.

In general there is a natural grading given by
|X|=
word length. That is if
X\simeq*_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes*_{n}
then
|X|=n
. This is well defined due to axiom (i).
This give a grading on the morphisms as
|\phi|=|X|-|Y|
. All the algebras/coalgebra structures are graded by
|\cdot|
.

Definition 5.3
.

A

on a Feynman category

, such that:

\text{deg}(\phi\circ\psi)=\text{deg}(\phi)+\text{deg}(\psi)

\text{deg}(\phi\otimes\psi)=\text{deg}(\phi)+\text{deg}(\psi)
, and

0
and

.

For a

\text{deg}(\phi\circ\psi)\geq\text{deg}(\phi)+\text{deg}(\psi)
.
In addition, a (weak) degree function is called

\text{deg}(\phi)=0
if and only if

is an isomorphism.

In practice, Feynman categories come with a presentation. This is a set of generators of the basic morphisms together with relations among them, cf.
[

The generators are a set
P({\mathcal{F}})\subset({\mathcal{F}}\downarrow{\mathcal{V}})
of non-invertible elements
such that any morphism in

up to isomorphism can be written as
\hat{\phi}_{n}\hat{\phi}_{n-1}\cdots\hat{\phi}_{2}\phi_{1}

and we use the short hand notation

\hat{\phi}={\mathrm{id}}_{*_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathrm{id}}_{*_{k-1}}\otimes\phi\otimes{\mathrm{id}}_{*_{k+1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mathrm{id}}_{*_{n}}

Note that generators

Commonly the sources can be restricted to

We call

We call a presentation

there is a maximal number

We now treat the case with isomorphisms. This will yield commutative but generally noncocommutative bialgebras. In order to define the coproduct, some modifications are needed to avoid an over-counting of decompositions due to

Definition 5.8

Note that parallel to (

If the category

6
Conclusion and outlook

6.1
Conclusion

We have shown that pathlike coalgebras in addition to quiver and incidence coalgebras capture the two types of bialgebras
stemming from Feynman categories, such as those of Baues, Goncharov and those of Connes–Kreimer. This realization provides several outcomes.

The structure of path-like coalgebras exactly answers the question of what the harbinger of the antipode in the various constructions
is. It precisely identifies the grouplike elements as the obstruction to the existence of the antipode.
For characters, their values on theses elements determines their
\star
-invertibility.
This also explains the appearance of quotients. These correspond to restrictions on the characters. The most common,
which says that all grouplike elements have character value 1, leads to the connected quotient. Other restrictions, like grouplike invertible
or grouplike central, yield different
quotients and explain the universality of the quantum deformation, which in turn explain the naturally of Brown’s coaction.

This sets the stage for further work.

6.2
Outlook

Our analysis is based on using filtrations to reduce to an initial part, where the antipode or convolution inverse is known.
The notion of pathlike coalgebras codifies this situation if the initial part is setlike.
Section
2.2.4
, however, can be taken as a clue that there could be some other structures,
which do not necessitate only grouplike elements, but allows for more information about the isomorphisms.
The following example of the Drinfel’d double leads the way to a new research in this direction.
Physically this corresponds to a (finite) gauge group. In the infinite case, one has to take the precautions spelled out in Appendix
A

with the induced structures.

,

Appendix C
Set version of graphs

We give a purely set based, non-graphical version here. A reference for the graphical version is
[
41
, Section 2.1, Appendix A]
.
The basic objects will be
{\mathcal{V}}=\operatorname{Iso}(\mathcal{F}{\rm in}\mathcal{S}{\rm et})
.
This fixes the
\operatorname{Iso}({\mathcal{V}}^{\otimes})
to have as objects finite tuples of sets
(S_{1},\dots,S_{n})
, where the empty set is allowed both as a tuple and in the
entries. The morphisms are componentwise or inner isomorphisms
together with outer permutations, which act as follows:
A permutation
p\in{\mathbb{S}}_{n}
acts by
p(S_{1},\dots,S_{n})=(S_{p(1)},\dots,S_{p(n)})
.
The inner isomorphisms
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}(S_{i})
act as
({\mathrm{id}},\dots,{\mathrm{id}},\sigma,{\mathrm{id}},\dots,{\mathrm{id}})
. The monoidal product is the joining of tuples.
In graphical form, these are aggregates, that is a disjoint unions of corollas.
The outer permutations permute the corollas and the inner isomorphisms the flags of the individual corollas.

There are two versions of the category: the connected and the non-connected version.
To write the relations in a concise fashion, we
abuse notation and write
\phi
for
({\mathrm{id}},\dots,{\mathrm{id}},\phi,{\mathrm{id}},\dots,{\mathrm{id}})
.

For the connected version the morphisms are generated under the monoidal product and concatenation by the isomorphisms and the following morphisms, which called simple edge contractions and simple loop contractions:

\,{\vphantom{\circ}}{}_{s}{\circ}_{t}\colon\ (S,T)\to(S\setminus{\sigma}\amalg T\setminus\{t\}),\qquad\circ_{ss^{\prime}}\colon\ (S)\to(S\setminus\{s,s^{\prime}\}).

These satisfy the following relations.

1.

The basic morphisms are equivariant with respect to isomorphisms in the sense that:
For
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}(S)
,
\sigma^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Aut}(T)
:

\,{\vphantom{\circ}}{}_{\sigma(s)}{\circ}_{\sigma^{\prime}(t)}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})=\sigma\amalg\sigma^{\prime}|_{S\setminus\{s\}\amalg T\setminus\{t\}}\,{\vphantom{\circ}}{}_{s}{\circ}_{t},\qquad\circ_{\sigma(s),\sigma(s^{\prime})}\sigma=\sigma|_{S\setminus\{s,s^{\prime}\}}\circ_{s,s^{\prime}}\!.

For an outer permutation:

\displaystyle p\,\circ_{s,s^{\prime}}=\circ_{s,s^{\prime}}p,\qquad p^{\prime}\,{\vphantom{\circ}}_{p(s)}{\circ}_{p(t)}=\,{\vphantom{\circ}}_{s}{\circ}_{t}p,

where
p^{\prime}
is the image of the permutation in the embedding
{\mathbb{S}}_{n-1}\to{\mathbb{S}}_{n}
given by the induced block permutation.

2.

Loop contractions commute:

\displaystyle\circ_{s_{1},s_{1}^{\prime}}\circ_{s_{2},s_{2}^{\prime}}=\circ_{s_{2},s_{2}^{\prime}}\circ_{s_{1},s_{1}^{\prime}}\!.

3.

Loop and edge contractions commute:

\,{\vphantom{\circ}}{}_{s_{1}}{\circ}_{t_{1}}\circ_{s_{2}s_{2}^{\prime}}=\circ_{s_{2}s_{2}^{\prime}}\,{\vphantom{\circ}}{}_{s_{1}}{\circ}_{t_{1}}.

4.

Edge contractions commute if the “edges” do not form a cycle.
For
s_{1}\in S_{1}
,
t_{1}\in T_{1}
,
s_{2}\in S_{2}
,
t_{2}\in T_{2}
and
|\{S_{1},T_{1},S_{2},T_{2}\}|\geq 3

\,{\vphantom{\circ}}{}_{s_{1}}{\circ}_{t_{1}}\,{\vphantom{\circ}}{}_{s_{2}}{\circ}_{t_{2}}=\,{\vphantom{\circ}}_{s_{1}}{\circ}_{t_{1}}\,{\vphantom{\circ}}{}_{s_{2}}{\circ}_{t_{2}}.

5.

Edge contractions commute even if they form a cycle, only that after one edge contraction, the second edge contraction is a loop contraction.
For
s_{1},s_{2}\in S
,
t_{1},t_{2}\in T
,

\displaystyle\circ_{s_{2},t_{2}}\,{\vphantom{\circ}}{}_{s_{1}}{\circ}_{t_{1}}=\circ_{s_{1},t_{1}}\,{\vphantom{\circ}}{}_{s_{2}}{\circ}_{t_{2}}.

In the non-connected case, there is an additional generating morphism called
simple merger:

\,{\vphantom{\boxminus}}{}_{S}{\boxminus}_{T}\colon\ (S,T)\to(S\amalg T).

These satisfy the relations:

1.

Mergers are equivariant with respect to the internal action

\,{\vphantom{\boxminus}}{}_{S}{\boxminus}_{T}(\sigma,{\mathrm{id}})=(\sigma\amalg{\mathrm{id}})\,{\vphantom{\boxminus}}_{S}{\boxminus}_{T}.

2.

Mergers are equivariant with respect to the outer action.
On
(S_{1},\dots,S_{n})
:

\,{\vphantom{\boxminus}}{}_{S_{i+1}}{\boxminus}_{S_{i}}=\,{\vphantom{\boxminus}}_{S_{i}}{\boxminus}_{S_{i+1}}\tau_{i,i+1}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad p\,{\vphantom{\boxminus}}_{S_{i}}{\boxminus}_{S_{i+1}}=\,{\vphantom{\boxminus}}_{S_{p^{\prime}(i)}}{\boxminus}_{S_{p^{\prime}(i+1}}p^{\prime},

where
p^{\prime}
is the image of the permutation
p
under the embedding
{\mathbb{S}}_{n-1}\to{\mathbb{S}}_{n}
given by the block permutation with block
(i,i+1)
.

3.

Simple mergers commute

\,{\vphantom{\boxminus}}{}_{S_{1}}{\boxminus}_{T_{1}}\,{\vphantom{\boxminus}}{}_{S_{2}}{\boxminus}_{T_{2}}=\,{\vphantom{\boxminus}}_{S_{2}}{\boxminus}_{T_{2}}\,{\vphantom{\boxminus}}{}_{S_{1}}{\boxminus}_{T_{1}}.

4.

Mergers and edge/loop contractions on different sets commute.

5.

If
s\in S
and
t\in T
, then

\,{\vphantom{\circ}}{}_{s}{\circ}_{t}=\circ_{st}\,{\vphantom{\boxminus}}{}_{S}{\boxminus}_{T}.
