The matroid of a graphing
László Lovász

Abstract
Graphings serve as limit objects for bounded-degree graphs. We define the “cycle matroid” of a graphing as a submodular setfunction, with values in [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] , which generalizes (up to normalization) the cycle matroid of finite graphs. We prove that for a Benjamini–Schramm convergent sequence of graphs, the total rank, normalized by the number of nodes, converges to the total rank of the limit graphing.



1
Introduction

Graphings serve as limit objects for bounded-degree graphs
[
3
,
14
]
.
They can be defined as Borel graphs on a Borel probability space with a certain
measure-preservation property. Our goal is to extend the definition of the
cycle matroid of a graph to graphings.

In the cycle matroid of a finite graph
G=(V,E)
, the rank of a set
X\subseteq E
is determined by the partition
\mathcal{P}
of the subgraph
(V,X)
into connected
components: we have
r(X)=|V|-|\mathcal{P}|
. This rank function is submodular:

r(X\cup Y)+r(X\cap Y)\leq r(X)+r(Y)\qquad(X,Y\subseteq E).

(1)

Let us also remark that a set
X
is independent in this matroid if and only if
the rank function
r
, restricted to
X
, is just the cardinality function.

One could introduce the rank function on the edge-set of a graphing by defining
it for finite subsets
X
of the edge set as in the cycle matroid of the graph
formed by
X
(ignoring isolated nodes). One could extend the rank function to
infinite sets of edges by giving it the value
\infty
. We choose a perhaps
more interesting approach, generalizing the
normalized rank function
,
defined for a finite graph
G=(V,E)
as
\rho(X)=r(X)/|V|
. Clearly this
function is submodular, with values in
[0,1]
.

The idea of the generalization is the following observation: Let
\mathcal{P}
be the
partition of the node set
V
into the connected components of subgraph

(V,X)
. For
v\in V
, we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{v}
the partition class containing
v
.
Let
\mathbf{u}
be a uniform random node of
V
, then

{\sf E}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{u}}|}\Big)=\frac{|\mathcal{P}|}{|V|}=1-\rho(X).

(2)

This definition can be extended almost verbatim to partitions of graphings into
connected component; however, measurability issues and infinite classes make it
technically involved to prove submodularity and other properties. To define our
generalization to the infinite case, we consider a standard Borel sigma-algebra

(J,\mathcal{B})
, and “measurable” partitions of
J
into sets in
\mathcal{B}
, leading to
a setfunction defined on these partitions, which is “submodular” in some
sense.

We will be interested in partitions with many finite classes: If all, or almost
all, partition classes are infinite, then the expectation above will be
0
and

\rho(X)=1
.

We define the “cycle matroid” of a graphing as a submodular setfunction
defined on Borel subsets of the edge set
E
, with values in
[0,1]
. This
generalizes the (normalized) cycle matroid of finite graphs. The proof of
submodularity is obtained through a more general result about a function
defined on measurable partitions of a Borel space.

What are the “bases” of this generalized matroid? The results of

[
15
]
suggest that these bases could be defined as finitely additive
measures (charges)
\alpha
on Borel subsets of edges, upper bounded by
\rho
,
with
\alpha(E)=\rho(E)
. We show that certain spanning forests of the graphing
do give rise to such charges, but we don’t have a full characterization.

A next step should be to develop the limit theory for
\rho
. There have been
attempts to define convergence of matroids, at least for special families

[
5
,
11
,
6
,
13
]
, but these don’t seem to apply to our case.

A sequence of finite graphs with all degrees bounded by
D
is said to
locally converge
to a graphing
\mathbf{G}
, if for every
r\geq 1
, the distribution
of the
r
-ball centered at a random node of
G_{n}
converges to the
distribution of the
r
-ball centered at a random point of
\mathbf{G}
. (The notion
of convergence of a bounded-degree graph sequence is due to Benjamini and
Schramm
[
3
]
, with a different construction for the limit object; see

[
14
]
for convergence to graphings.) To develop a limit theory for
cycle matroids, we want to show that if a sequence of bounded-degree graphs

G_{n}
converges to a graphing
\mathbf{G}
, then the setfunctions
\rho_{G_{n}}

converge to the setfunction
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
in some sense. This is not resolved in
this paper; but we do prove that for a Benjamini–Schramm convergent sequence
of graphs, the rank of the edge set, normalized by the number of nodes,
converges to the rank of the limit graphing.

2
\mathcal{B}
-partitions

2.1
The semilattice of
\mathcal{B}
-partitions

Let
(J,\mathcal{B})
be a standard Borel space, let
\mathcal{P}
be a partition of
J
, and
for
X\subseteq J
, define

\mathcal{P}(X)=\cup\{P\in\mathcal{P}:~P\cap X\not=\emptyset\}.

We say that
\mathcal{P}
is a
\mathcal{B}
-partition, if
\mathcal{P}(A)\in\mathcal{B}
whenever
A\in\mathcal{B}
.
For a partition
\mathcal{P}
we denote the class containing
x\in J
by
\mathcal{P}_{x}
.
Clearly
\mathcal{P}_{x}\in\mathcal{B}
if
\mathcal{P}
is a
\mathcal{B}
-partition. We denote by
\mathcal{P}_{\text{\sf fin}}

the family of finite subsets of
\mathcal{P}
.

If
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
are partitions of
J
, we denote by
\mathcal{P}\land\mathcal{Q}
and

\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}
their meet and join in the partition lattice.

Lemma 2.1
.

(a)
If
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
are
\mathcal{B}
-partitions, then so is
\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}
.

(b)
If, in addition,
\mathcal{Q}
has a countable number of classes, then

\mathcal{P}\land\mathcal{Q}
is a
\mathcal{B}
-partition.

Proof.

(a) Let
A\in\mathcal{B}
, and define
U_{0}=A
, and recursively

U_{k+1}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{P}(U_{k}),&\text{if $k$ is even},\\
\mathcal{Q}(U_{k}),&\text{if $k$ is odd}.\end{cases}

Then
U_{k}\in\mathcal{B}
for all
k
, and so
(\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q})(A)=\cup_{k}U_{k}\in\mathcal{B}
.

(b) Let
U=\bigcup_{Q\in\mathcal{Q}}Q\cap\mathcal{P}(Q\cap A)
. We claim that

(\mathcal{P}\land\mathcal{Q})(A)=\{x\in J:~\mathcal{P}_{x}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{x}\cap A\not=\emptyset\}=U.

(3)

The first equation is trivial. To verify the second, assume that
x\in J
has
the property that
\mathcal{P}_{x}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{x}\cap A\not=\emptyset
. Then there is a
y\in\mathcal{P}_{x}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{x}\cap A
. Hence
y\in\mathcal{P}_{x}
, which implies that
\mathcal{P}_{x}=\mathcal{P}_{y}
.
Furthermore,
y\in\mathcal{Q}_{x}\cap A
implies that
\mathcal{P}_{y}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Q}_{x}\cap A)
,
and hence
x\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Q}_{x}\cap A)
. Since trivially
x\in\mathcal{Q}_{x}
, we have
x\in\mathcal{Q}_{x}\cap\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Q}_{x}\cap A)
, so
x\in U
. Conversely, if
x\in Q\cap\mathcal{P}(Q\cap A)
for some
Q
, then clearly
Q=Q_{x}
, and
x\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{Q}_{x}\cap A)
means that

\mathcal{P}_{x}
intersects
\mathcal{Q}_{x}\cap A
, so
\mathcal{P}_{x}\cap\mathcal{Q}_{x}\cap A\not=\emptyset
.

Now
Q\cap\mathcal{P}(Q\cap A)
is clearly a Borel set, and since
\mathcal{Q}
has a countable
number of classes, (
3
) implies that
U=(\mathcal{P}\land\mathcal{Q})(A)
is Borel.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a
\mathcal{B}
-partition,
A\in\mathcal{B}
, and let
A_{k}=\cup\{P\in\mathcal{P}:~|P\cap A|=k\}
. Then
A_{k}\in\mathcal{B}
for
k=0,1,\ldots,\infty
.

This lemma implies that
|\mathcal{P}_{x}|
is a measurable function of
x
(which may
have infinite values). In particular, the union of
k
-element partition
classes is in
\mathcal{B}
, and hence the union
\mathcal{P}_{\text{fin}}
of finite classes of

\mathcal{P}
is in
\mathcal{B}
.

Proof.

We may assume that
(J,\mathcal{B})
is the sigma-algebra of Borel sets in
(0,1)
. Let

C_{k}=\bigcup_{0=r_{0}<r_{1}<\ldots<r_{k}=1\atop r_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}}\bigcap_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathcal{P}\big(A\cap(r_{i},r_{i+1})\big).

Then
C_{k}
is a Borel set, which is the union of partition classes with at
least
k
elements in
A
. So
A_{k}=C_{k}\setminus C_{k+1}
is also Borel.

Given a partition
\mathcal{P}
, let us call a set
S\in\mathcal{B}
a
finite-class
representative
, if
|S\cap P|=1
for every finite
P\in\mathcal{P}
and
|S\cap P|=0

for every infinite
P\in\mathcal{P}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Every
\mathcal{B}
-partition has a finite-class representative.

Proof.

Again, assume that
(J,\mathcal{B})=(0,1)
, and for every finite class
P\in\mathcal{P}
, let

s_{P}
be its minimal element. It suffices to show that

S=\{s_{P}:~P\in\mathcal{P},P\text{ finite}\}

is a Borel set. Indeed, the set
U_{r}=\cup\{P\in\mathcal{P}:~|P\cap(0,r)|=1\}
is Borel
by Lemma
2.2
, and then so is
U_{r}\cap(\cup\mathcal{P}_{\text{\sf fin}})
. Thus the
set

V_{r}=U_{r}\cap(\cup\mathcal{P}_{\text{\sf fin}})\cap(0,r)=\big\{s_{P}:~P\text{ finite},P\cap(0,r)=\{s_{P}\}\big\}.

is Borel, and hence
S=\cup_{r\in\mathbb{Q}\cap(0,1)}V_{r}
is a Borel set.

2.2
Re-randomization

Now let
\pi
be a probability measure on
(J,\mathcal{B})
, and let
\mathbf{u}
be a random
point from
\pi
. If
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{u}}
is finite, then let
\mathbf{v}
be a uniform random
point of
P
; else, let
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}
. We say that
\mathbf{v}
is obtained by
re-randomizing
\mathbf{u}
along
\mathcal{P}
. We say that
\mathcal{P}
has the
re-randomizing property
, if re-randomizing results in a point distributed
according to
\pi
.

Another way to express this property is that if
\mu_{x}
is the uniform
distribution on
\mathcal{P}_{x}
if
\mathcal{P}_{x}
is finite, and
\mu_{x}=\delta_{x}
otherwise,
then the mixture of the measurable family
M=(\mu_{x}:~x\in J)
of measures by

\pi
is
\pi
again. It is easy to check that every partition of a finite set
endowed with the uniform distribution has the re-randomizing property.

The significance of the re-randomizing property for us is expressed by the
following easy fact:

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a
\mathcal{B}
-partition of
J
with the re-randomizing property. Let

f:~J\to\mathbb{R}
be an integrable function such that
f(x)=0
if
\mathcal{P}_{x}
is
infinite, and
\sum_{x\in P}f(x)=0
for every finite class
P\in\mathcal{P}
. Let
\mathbf{x}

be a random point from
\pi
. Then
{\sf E}f(\mathbf{x})=0
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{y}
be obtained from
\mathbf{x}
by re-randomizing along
\mathcal{P}
. Then
{\sf E}f(\mathbf{y})=0
, since this holds for every
\mathbf{x}
. Since
\mathbf{y}
has the same
distribution as
\mathbf{x}
, the lemma follows.

Not every
\mathcal{B}
-partition has the re-randomizing property.

Example 2.5
.

Let
(J,\mathcal{B},\pi)
be the
[0,1]
interval with the Borel sets and the Lebesgue
measure. Partition the interval
[0,1)
into pairs
\{x,2x+1/3\}
, where
0\leq x<1/3
. Then for a random point
\mathbf{u}
from
\pi
, we have

{\sf P}(\mathbf{u}\in[0,1/3))=1/3
, but if we re-randomize to get
\mathbf{v}
, then

{\sf P}(\mathbf{v}\in[0,1/3))=1/2
.

There are two trivial operations on partitions that preserve the re-randomizing
property: we can merge all infinite classes into one, or resolve all infinite
classes into singletons. Let us state two less trivial constructions.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
be
\mathcal{B}
-partitions of
J
such that
\mathcal{Q}
is obtained from

\mathcal{P}
by splitting some of its finite classes. If
\mathcal{P}
has the re-randomizing
property, then so does
\mathcal{Q}
.

Proof.

Let us do the following experiment: let
\mathbf{u}
be a random point from
\pi
; let

\mathbf{v}
be obtained from
\mathbf{u}
by re-randomizing along
\mathcal{P}
; then let
\mathbf{w}
be
obtained by re-randomizing along
\mathcal{Q}
. Then (by the re-randomizing property of
finite partitions)
\mathbf{w}
can be obtained by re-randomizing
\mathbf{u}
along
\mathcal{P}
,
so it has distribution
\pi
. But it can be obtained by re-randomizing
\mathbf{v}

along
\mathcal{Q}
. Since
\mathbf{v}
has distribution
\pi
, this proves the lemma.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
be
\mathcal{B}
-partitions with the re-randomizing property. Then
so is
\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}
.

Proof.

Let us construct a bipartite multigraph
H_{Z}
for every finite class
Z
of

\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}
as follows. Let
V(H_{Z})=U\cup W
, where
U=\{P\in\mathcal{P}:~P\subseteq Z\}
and
W=\{Q\in\mathcal{Q}:~Q\subseteq Z\}
. Let
X\in\mathcal{P}
be connected to
Y\in\mathcal{Q}

by
|X\cap Y|
edges. The edges of
H_{Z}
can be identified with the elements of

Z
, and the degree of
X\in V(H_{Z})
is
|X|
. It is also clear that
H_{Z}
is
connected by the definition of
\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}
.

Let
\mathbf{u}
be a random point of
J
from the distribution
\pi
, and let
Z
be
the partition class of
\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}
containing
\mathbf{u}
. Assume that
Z
is
finite. Let us do a random walk on
H_{Z}
starting at
v^{0}=\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{u}}
. The
distribution
\eta_{Z,k}
of the edge used in step
k
tends to the uniform
distribution on all edges of
H_{Z}
, by standard results on random walks on
graphs.

Let
(v^{0},v^{1},\ldots)
be the sequence of edges of
H_{Z}
in this random walk;
equivalently,
(v^{0},v^{1},\ldots)
is a sequence of points in
Z
. It follows by
the re-randomizing property of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
that for every
k
, the
distribution of
v^{k}
is just
\pi
. It follows that if we choose a random
point
\mathbf{u}
from
\pi
and re-randomize it along
\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}
to get
\mathbf{v}
,
then
\mathbf{v}
has distribution
\pi
, so
\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}
has the re-randomizing
property.

3
A supermodular function on partitions

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a
\mathcal{B}
-partition of
J
. Define

\psi(\mathcal{P})={\sf E}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{u}}|}\Big),

(4)

where
\mathbf{u}
is chosen from the distribution
\pi
. Here
1/|\mathcal{P}_{u}|=0
if

\mathcal{P}_{u}
is an infinite set. Clearly
0\leq\psi(\mathcal{P})\leq 1
. Let us note that if

J
is finite and
\pi
is the uniform distribution on
J
, then

\psi(\mathcal{P})={\sf E}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{u}}|}\Big)=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{P}}\frac{|A|}{|J|}\,\frac{1}{|A|}=\frac{|\mathcal{P}|}{|J|}.

(5)

It is also clear that
\psi
is a decreasing function on partitions (it is
smaller on coarser partitions).

Our main lemma is the following.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(J,\mathcal{B},\pi)
be a standard Borel probability space and let
\psi
be
defined by (
4
). Let
\mathcal{P}
,
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{R}
be
\mathcal{B}
-partitions with
the re-randomizing property such that
\mathcal{R}\leq\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{R}\leq\mathcal{Q}
. Then

\psi(\mathcal{R})+\psi(\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q})\geq\psi(\mathcal{P})+\psi(\mathcal{Q}).

(6)

We see that
\mathcal{R}
is a “proxy” for
\mathcal{P}\land\mathcal{Q}
, which may not be a

\mathcal{B}
-partition. In the next section, when applying the lemma to graphons, we
can choose
\mathcal{R}
to be a natural meet of the partitions
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
(which
will not be
\mathcal{P}\land\mathcal{Q}
).

Proof.

0^{\circ}
Let us prove the finite case first, when
\mathcal{B}=2^{J}
. We don’t have to
worry about
\mathcal{P}\land\mathcal{Q}
being a
\mathcal{B}
-partition, so we can replace
\mathcal{R}
by

\mathcal{P}\land\mathcal{Q}
. Let
K_{n}
be the complete graph on
J
. We consider a partition

\mathcal{P}
as a subgraph
G_{\mathcal{P}}
of
K_{n}
that is the disjoint union of complete
graphs, corresponding to the partition classes. Then
G_{\mathcal{P}\land\mathcal{Q}}=G_{\mathcal{P}}\cap G_{\mathcal{Q}}
and
G_{\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}}
is the transitive closure of
G_{\mathcal{P}}\cup G_{\mathcal{Q}}
.
Furthermore,
n(1-\psi(\mathcal{P}))
is the rank of
G_{\mathcal{P}}
in the cycle matroid of

K_{n}
. The inequality follows by the submodularity of the rank function of the
cycle matroid.

1^{\circ}
As a first step in the proof of the general case, we want to split
the classes of
\mathcal{Q}_{\text{\sf fin}}
into some subsets, and those classes in
\mathcal{R}

contained in a class in
\mathcal{Q}_{\text{\sf fin}}
to singletons, so that the inequality to be
proved remains equivalent.

Let
S
be a finite-class representative for
\mathcal{R}
(which exists by Lemma

2.3
). We split each
Y\in\mathcal{Q}_{\text{\sf fin}}
into
Y\cap S
and
singleton sets, and let
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
be the partition of
J
obtained. Let
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
be
obtained by splitting every class of
\mathcal{R}
contained in
\cup\mathcal{Q}_{\text{\sf fin}}
to
singletons. It is easy to see that
\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}=\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}
.

Let
\mathbf{u}
be a random point from
\pi
, and let
\mathbf{v}
be obtained by
re-randomizing
\mathbf{u}
along
\mathcal{Q}
. If
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{u}}
is infinite, then no partition
class containing
\mathbf{v}
changes when replacing
\mathcal{Q}
by
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
. If
Y=\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{u}}

is finite, then

\frac{1}{|(\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})_{\mathbf{v}}|}=\frac{1}{|(\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q})_{\mathbf{v}}|},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathbf{v}}|}=1.

Using (
5
), we have

{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)=\frac{|Y\setminus S|+1}{|Y|},\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)=\frac{1}{|Y|}.

Furthermore,

{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)=\frac{\big|\mathcal{R}|_{Y}\big|}{|Y|}=\frac{|Y\cap S|}{|Y|}.

Combining these equations, we see that

\displaystyle{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|(\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)+{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}^{\prime}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)-{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)-{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)

\displaystyle={\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|(\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q})_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)+1-{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)-\frac{|Y\setminus S|+1}{|Y|}

\displaystyle={\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|(\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q})_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)+\frac{|Y\cap S|}{|Y|}-{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)-\frac{1}{|Y|}

\displaystyle={\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|(\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q})_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)+{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)-{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)-{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big).

By Lemma
2.4
, this implies that this equation holds when
\mathbf{v}

is replaced by
\mathbf{u}
, and so

\displaystyle\psi(\mathcal{R}^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\psi(\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})-\psi(\mathcal{P})-\psi(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})=\psi(\mathcal{R})+\psi(\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q})-\psi(\mathcal{P})-\psi(\mathcal{Q}),

so considering the pair
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})
instead of
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})
, we may assume that
every class of
\mathcal{R}
is a singleton.

2^{\circ}
Our next goal is to get rid of the non-singleton finite classes of

\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}
. Let
Z
be the union of all finite classes of
\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}
, and
let
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
be obtained by splitting each class of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}

contained in
Z
to singletons. It is easy to see that
Z\in\mathcal{B}
, and that both

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
are
\mathcal{B}
-partitions. By Lemma
2.6
,
both
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
have the re-randomizing property.

We can write the inequality to be proved as

\int\limits_{J}f_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}}(x)\,d\pi(x)\geq 0,

(7)

where

f(x)=f_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}}(x)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_{x}|}+\frac{1}{|(\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q})_{x}|}-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{x}|}-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}_{x}|}.

Let
f^{\prime}=f_{\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}
. Then
f^{\prime}(x)=f(x)
if
x\in J\setminus Z
, and
f^{\prime}(x)=0

if
x\in Z
. By the finite case treated above,
\int\limits_{Z}f(x)\,d\pi(x)\geq 0
,
and hence

\int\limits_{J}f(x)\,d\pi(x)\geq\int\limits_{J}f(x)\,d\pi(x).

So we may replace
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q})
by
(\mathcal{P}^{\prime},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})
; in other words, we may assume
that every class of
\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}
is either infinite or a singleton set.

3^{\circ}
Our next goal is to get rid of non-singleton finite classes
containing a singleton class from the other partition. Let
S
and
T
be the
unions of singleton classes of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
, respectively. For
Y\in\mathcal{Q}
,
define

Y^{\prime}=\begin{cases}Y\setminus S,&\text{if $Y$ is finite},\\
Y,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

If
|Y|>1
, then the class of
\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}
is infinite, so
Y^{\prime}\not=\emptyset
.
Let the partition
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
consist of the nonempty sets
Y^{\prime}
(
Y\in\mathcal{Q}
), along
with those singleton subsets of
S
that are contained in finite classes of

\mathcal{Q}
. We leave
\mathcal{R}
unchanged.

We argue similarly as in
1^{\circ}
. Let
\mathbf{u}
be a random point from
\pi
, and
let
\mathbf{v}
be obtained by re-randomizing
\mathbf{u}
along
\mathcal{Q}
. Fix
\mathbf{u}
, and let

Y=\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{u}}
. Then If
Y
is infinite, then no partition class containing
\mathbf{v}

changes when replacing
\mathcal{Q}
by
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
. If
Y
is finite, then by
1^{\circ}
,
for any point
\mathbf{v}\in Y
,

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{v}}|}=1,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{v}}|}=\frac{1}{|Y|},

and by
2^{\circ}
,

{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|(\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q})_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)=0.

Furthermore, by (
5
),

{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)=\frac{|Y\setminus Y^{\prime}|+1}{|Y|},

and

{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|(\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)=\frac{|Y\setminus Y^{\prime}|}{|Y|}.

Combining these equations,

\displaystyle{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|(\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)+{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)-{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)-{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{|Y\setminus Y^{\prime}|}{|Y|}+1-{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)-\frac{|Y\setminus Y^{\prime}|+1}{|Y|}=0+1-{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)-\frac{1}{|Y|}

\displaystyle={\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|(\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q})_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)+{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)-{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big)-{\sf E}_{\mathbf{v}}\Big(\frac{1}{|\mathcal{Q}_{\mathbf{v}}|}\Big).

As before, this shows that

\displaystyle\psi(\mathcal{P}\land\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\psi(\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})-\psi(\mathcal{P})-\psi(\mathcal{Q}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\psi(\mathcal{P}\land\mathcal{Q})+\psi(\mathcal{P}\lor\mathcal{Q})-\psi(\mathcal{P})-\psi(\mathcal{Q}).

Carrying out the same process with
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
interchanged, we may assume
that for every point, three possibilities remain: it is contained (i) in
singleton classes of both partitions
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
, or (ii) in a singleton
class and an infinite class, or (iii) in two non-singleton classes.

In these three cases we have, respectively,

(i):

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}}(x)

\displaystyle=1+1-1-1=0,

(ii):

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}}(x)

\displaystyle=1+0-1-0=0,

(iii):

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{P},\mathcal{Q}}(x)

\displaystyle\geq 1+0-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}=0.

This proves the Lemma.

4
Cycle matroids of graphings

4.1
Graphing basics

A
graphing
is a quadruple
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mathcal{B},E,\lambda)
, where
(J,E)
is a
graph on the standard Borel sigma-algebra
(J,\mathcal{B})
,
E
is a symmetric Borel
subset of
\binom{J}{2}
, and
\lambda
is a probability measure on
\mathcal{B}
. Let

\deg(u)=\deg_{\mathbf{G}}(u)
denote the degree of
u\in J
in the graph
\mathbf{G}
; for

B\subseteq J
, let
\deg_{B}(u)
denote the number of edges from the point
u\in J
to
B
; for
X\subseteq E
, let
\deg_{X}(u)
denote the number of edges in

X
incident with
u
. For
x\in J
, we denote by
\mathbf{G}_{x}
the connected
component of
\mathbf{G}
containing
x
.

It is assumed that all degrees of the graph
(J,E)
are finite and bounded by
an integer
D\geq 0
, and the “measure-preservation” equation

\int\limits_{A}\deg_{B}(x)\,d\lambda(x)=\int\limits_{B}\deg_{A}(x)\,d\lambda(x)

(8)

is satisfied for all
A,B\in\mathcal{B}
. This condition is equivalent to saying that
for a random point
\mathbf{x}
from distribution
\lambda
, the (connected rooted)
graph
(G_{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{x})
is an involution-invariant random rooted graph as defined
by Benjamini and Schramm
[
3
]
.

The quantity

\overline{d}=\overline{d}_{\mathbf{G}}=\int\limits_{J}\deg(u)\,d\lambda(u)

is the average degree of the graphing. The setfunction

\eta(A\times B)=\frac{1}{\overline{d}}\int\limits_{A}\deg_{B}(x)\,d\lambda(x)

extends to a probability measure on
\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}
, which we call the
edge
measure
of
\mathbf{G}
. Clearly
\eta=\eta_{\mathbf{G}}
is symmetric (invariant under
interchanging the coordinates) and supported on
E
. We can express
\eta(X)

for every Borel set
X\subseteq E
as

\eta(X)=\frac{1}{\overline{d}}\int\limits_{J}\deg_{X}(u)\,d\lambda(u).

(9)

In this paper, a
subgraphing
of a graphing
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mathcal{B},E,\lambda)
is a

4
-tuple
\mathbf{H}=(J,\mathcal{B},F,\lambda)
, where
F\subseteq E
is a Borel set. It is
not entirely obvious that
\mathbf{H}
satisfies the graphing axioms; see Lemma 18.19
in
[
14
]
. Equation (
9
) implies the following expression
for the edge measure of a subgraphing:

\eta_{\mathbf{H}}(X)=\frac{\overline{d}}{\overline{d}_{\mathbf{H}}}\eta(X)\qquad(X\in\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}).

(10)

4.2
The rank function

An obvious way to define a matroid rank function of a graphing would be to
consider finite subsets of edges, and consider the rank function
r(X)
of the
cycle matroid of the graph they form. Every infinite set will have infinite
rank. We explore a more interesting possibility, generalizing the
normalized rank
of the cycle matroid of a graph
G=(V,E)
on
n
nodes.

Let
c(G)
denote the number of connected components of a graph
G
. Recalling
that
r(X)=n-c(V,X)
, we define

\rho(X)=\frac{r(X)}{n}=1-\frac{c(V,X)}{|V|}.

This number is in
[0,1]
. By (
5
), we can express it as

\rho(X)=1-{\sf E}_{\mathbf{u}}\Big(\frac{1}{|X_{\mathbf{u}}|}\Big),

(11)

where
X_{\mathbf{u}}
is the set of nodes of the connected component of
(V,X)

containing
\mathbf{u}
.

Now this setfunction makes sense in the graphing case as well. Let

\mathbf{G}=(J,\mathcal{B},E,\lambda)
be a graphing. For every Borel set
X\subseteq E
, the
quadruple
\mathbf{G}^{X}=(J,\mathcal{B},X,\lambda)
is a graphing. We denote by
\mathcal{P}^{X}
the
partition of
J
into the connected components of
\mathbf{G}^{X}
. Then we can define

\rho(X)=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(X)=1-{\sf E}_{\mathbf{u}}\Big(\frac{1}{|V(\mathbf{G}^{X}_{\mathbf{u}}|}\Big)=1-\psi(\mathcal{P}^{X}),

(12)

where
\mathbf{u}
is a random point from
\lambda
. We can see from this definition
right away a nice property of
\rho(X)
, namely that it is an intrinsic
parameter of the edge set
X
: if
\mathbf{H}
is a subgraphing of
\mathbf{G}
, and
X
is a
Borel subset of
E(\mathbf{H})
, then
\rho_{\mathbf{H}}(X)=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(X)
.

Our goal is to apply the results of Section
3
to the
partitions of
J
into the connected components of sub-graphings, showing that

1-\rho
is supermodular, and so
\rho
is submodular. For this, we need a
simple (but not quite trivial) lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mathcal{B},E,\lambda)
be a graphing, and let
\mathcal{P}
be the partition of

J
into the connected components of
\mathbf{G}
. Then
\mathcal{P}
is a
\mathcal{B}
-partition
with the re-randomizing property.

Proof.

The fact that
\mathcal{P}
is a
\mathcal{B}
-partition, follows e.g. by Lemma 18.2 in

[
14
]
, which asserts that if
A\in\mathcal{B}
, then the neighbors of
A
form
a Borel set. Repeating this argument, the set
A_{k}
of nodes that are at a
distance at most
k
form
A
is a Borel set, and hence
\mathcal{P}(A)=\cup_{k}A_{k}
is
Borel.

To prove that
\mathcal{P}
has the re-randomizing property, let
\mathbf{u}
be a random
point from
\lambda
, let
\mathbf{v}
be obtained from
\mathbf{u}
by re-randomizing along

\mathcal{P}
, and let
\lambda^{\prime}
be the distribution of
\mathbf{v}
. Let
B=\cup\mathcal{P}_{\text{\sf fin}}
.
Since
\lambda^{\prime}|_{J\setminus B}=\lambda|_{J\setminus B}
by definition, it
suffices to show that
\lambda^{\prime}(A)=\lambda(A)
for all
A\in\mathcal{B}
,
A\subseteq B
. For
x,y\in J
, define

f(x,y)=\begin{cases}1/|V(\mathbf{G}_{x})|,&\text{if $x\in B$ and $y\in A\cap V(G_{x})$,}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then

\sum_{y}f(x,y)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{|V(\mathbf{G}_{x})\cap A|}{|V(\mathbf{G}_{x})|},&\text{if $x\in B$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and

\sum_{x}f(x,y)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $y\in A$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

By the Mass Transport Principle (in the form of
[
14
]
, Proposition
18.49),

\displaystyle\lambda(A)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{J}\sum_{x}f(x,y)\,d\lambda(y)=\int\limits_{J}\sum_{y}f(x,y)\,d\lambda(x)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{B}\frac{|V(\mathbf{G}_{x})\cap A|}{|V(\mathbf{G}_{x})|}\,d\lambda(x)={\sf P}(u\in B,u^{\prime}\in A)=\lambda^{\prime}(A).

This proves the re-randomizing property of
\mathcal{P}
.

Another simple fact we need is the following.

Lemma 4.2
.

The setfunction
\rho=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
defined by (
12
) satisfies

\frac{\overline{d}}{1+D}\eta(X)\leq\rho(X)\leq\overline{d}\eta(X).

for all
X\in\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}
.

Proof.

Clearly
|V(\mathbf{G}^{X}_{u})|\geq 1+\deg_{X}(u)
, and hence

1-\frac{1}{|V(\mathbf{G}^{X}_{u})|}\geq 1-\frac{1}{1+\deg(u,X)}=\frac{\deg(u,X)}{1+\deg(u,X)}\geq\frac{\deg(u,X)}{1+D}.

On the other hand,

1-\frac{1}{|V(\mathbf{G}^{X}_{u})|}\leq\deg(u,X),

since both sides are zero if
\deg(u,X)=0
, and the left hand side is at most

1
and the right hand side is at least
1
otherwise. Taking expectation, and
using (
9
), we get the inequalities in the lemma.

Let us state some nice properties of the setfunction
\rho
(see
[
15
]

for more on these properties and their relevance). We say that a setfunction

\varphi:~\mathcal{B}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is
strongly bounded
, if there is a (finite) measure

\alpha
such that
\varphi\leq\alpha
. It is
continuous from above
, if for
every sequence
A_{1}\supseteq A_{2}\supseteq\ldots
of sets
A_{i}\in\mathcal{B}
and

A=\cap_{i}A_{i}
, we have
\varphi(A_{n})\to\varphi(A)

(n\to\infty)
. It is
absolutely continuous with respect to a measure
\pi
, if for every
\varepsilon>0

there is a
\delta>0
such that
\pi(S)<\delta
implies that
|\varphi(S)|<\varepsilon

for every
S\in\mathcal{B}
.

Theorem 4.3
.

The setfunction
\rho
defined on the Borel subsets of
E
by
(
12
) is increasing and submodular. In addition, it is
strongly bounded, continuous from above and absolutely continuous with respect
to a measure
\eta
.

Proof.

To prove submodularity, we use Lemma
3.1
. Equivalently, we
want to prove that
\psi(\mathcal{P}^{X})
is a supermodular setfunction of
X
:

\psi(\mathcal{P}^{X\cup Y})+\psi(\mathcal{P}^{X\cap Y})\geq\psi(\mathcal{P}^{X})+\psi(\mathcal{P}^{Y}).

(13)

Here
\mathcal{P}^{X\cup Y}
etc. are
\mathcal{B}
-partitions with the re-randomizing
property by Lemma
4.1
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{P}^{X\cup Y}=\mathcal{P}^{X}\lor\mathcal{P}^{Y}
. In general, we will not have
\mathcal{P}^{X\cap Y}=\mathcal{P}^{X}\land\mathcal{P}^{Y}
, but

\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{P}^{X\cap Y}
satisfies
\mathcal{R}\leq\mathcal{P}^{X}
and
\mathcal{R}\leq\mathcal{P}^{Y}
, so Lemma

3.1
proves (
13
).

The smoothness properties are trivial by Lemma
4.2
.

4.3
Bases and minorizing measures

Bases of the cycle matroid of a graph are spanning forests, so we expect that
bases of the matroid of a graphing (if meaningful at all) will be connected to
spanning trees. It is suggested in
[
15
]
, that the “bases” for a
general increasing submodular setfunction
\varphi
with
\varphi(\emptyset)=0
should
be
minorizing charges
, i.e., nonnegative finitely additive measures

\alpha
on
\mathcal{B}
such that
\alpha\leq\varphi
.

It was proved (under somewhat different conditions) by Choquet
[
8
]
and
Šipoš
[
21
]
(see also
[
10
]
, Chapter 10), that for every
increasing submodular setfunction
\varphi
defined on Borel sets with

\varphi(\emptyset)=0
and every family
\mathcal{S}
of Borel sets that is totally ordered
by inclusion, there is always a minorizing charge
\alpha
with

\alpha(J)=\varphi(J)
. In our case, Theorem
4.3
implies that
every minorizing charge for
\rho
is in fact countably additive. See

[
15
]
for other matroid-like properties of minorizing measures, like
Steinitz exchange, greedy algorithm, intersection, etc.

So we expect that spanning forests give rise to minorizing measures. In the
finite case, if
X\subseteq E
is a spanning forest, then
\rho(Y)=|Y|/|V|
for
every
Y\subseteq X
, and so
\rho
is additive on
X
. This does not hold for
graphings in general (see Example
4.6
), but we can prove this
at least in a special case. We say that a graphing
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mathcal{B},E,\lambda)
is

hyperfinite
, if for every
\varepsilon>0
there is a Borel set
X\subseteq E

with
\eta(X)<\varepsilon
such that all connected components of
\mathbf{G}\setminus X
are
finite.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mathcal{B},E,\lambda)
be a hyperfinite graphing such that every connected
component is a tree. Then for all Borel sets
U\subseteq E
, we have

\rho(U)=(\overline{d}/2)\eta(U)
.

In particular,
\rho
is a measure. Applying this lemma with
U=E
, we obtain
that
\rho(E)=\overline{d}/2
, so the lemma could be phrased as

\rho(U)=\rho(E)\eta(U)
.

Proof.

First, assume that every connected component
Q
of
\mathbf{G}
is a finite tree.
Then
U\cap E(Q)
is independent in the cycle matroid of
Q
, and hence

\rho_{Q}(U\cap E(Q))=\frac{|U\cap E(Q)|}{|V(Q)|}.

The quantities on both side can be expressed using a random point
\mathbf{q}
of

V(Q)
.

\rho_{Q}(U\cap E(Q))={\sf E}_{\mathbf{q}}\Big(1-\frac{1}{|U_{\mathbf{q}}|}\Big),

and

|U\cap E(Q)|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{q\in V(Q)}\deg_{U}(q)=\frac{|V(Q)|}{2}{\sf E}_{\mathbf{q}}\deg_{U}(\mathbf{q}).

Thus

{\sf E}_{\mathbf{q}}\Big(1-\frac{1}{|U_{\mathbf{q}}|}-\frac{1}{2}\deg_{U}(\mathbf{q})\Big)=0.

Since this holds for every connected component
Q
of
\mathbf{G}
, Lemma

2.4
implies that the same relation holds for a random point

\mathbf{x}
from
\lambda
:

{\sf E}_{\mathbf{x}}\Big(1-\frac{1}{|U_{\mathbf{x}}|}-\frac{1}{2}\deg_{U}(\mathbf{x})\Big)=0.

But here

{\sf E}_{\mathbf{x}}\Big(1-\frac{1}{|U_{\mathbf{x}}|}\Big)=\rho(U)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{1}{2}{\sf E}_{\mathbf{x}}\big(\deg_{U}(\mathbf{x})\big)=\frac{\overline{d}}{2}\eta(U)

This settles the case when all components of
\mathbf{G}
are finite.

Now let
\mathbf{G}
be any hyperfinite forest. Fix an
\varepsilon>0
. By hyperfiniteness,
there is a Borel set
X\subseteq E
such that
\eta(X)<\varepsilon
and every
connected component of
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{G}\setminus X
is finite. By Lemma

4.2
we have
\rho(U\cap X)\leq D\eta(U\cap X)\leq D\varepsilon
, and so

\rho(U\setminus X)\leq\rho(U)\leq\rho(U\setminus X)+\rho(U\cap X)\leq\rho(U\setminus X)+D\varepsilon.

As noted before, we have
\rho(U\setminus X)=\rho_{\mathbf{H}}(U\setminus X)
. Hence by
the special case proved above and by (
10
),

\rho(U\setminus X)=\frac{\overline{d}_{\mathbf{H}}}{2}\eta_{\mathbf{H}}(U\setminus X)=\frac{\overline{d}}{2}\eta(U\setminus X),

and so

\rho(U)\leq\rho(U\setminus X)+D\varepsilon=\frac{\overline{d}}{2}\eta(U\setminus X)+D\varepsilon\leq\frac{\overline{d}}{2}\eta(U)+D\varepsilon.

while

\rho(U)\geq\rho(U\setminus X)=\frac{\overline{d}}{2}\eta(U\setminus X)\geq\frac{\overline{d}}{2}\eta(U)-\varepsilon.

Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, the lemma follows.

We can describe a class of minorizing measures for
\rho
. In the finite case,
these are all the extremal minorizing measures. In the infinite case there must
be others, but constructing all of them will probably be very difficult.

Corollary 4.5
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a graphing and
\mathbf{H}=(J,F)
, a hyperfinite spanning subforest of

\mathbf{G}
. Then

\alpha(X)=\frac{\overline{d}}{2}\eta(X\cap F)

defines a measure on the Borel subsets of
E
such that
\alpha\leq\rho
and

\alpha(E)=\rho(E)
.

Proof.

It is clear that
\alpha
defines a measure. Using (
10
) and
Lemma
4.4
, we get

\alpha(X)=\frac{\overline{d}}{2}\eta(X\cap F)=\frac{\overline{d}}{2}\,\frac{\overline{d}_{\mathbf{H}}}{\overline{d}}\,\eta_{\mathbf{H}}(X\cap F)=\frac{\overline{d}_{\mathbf{H}}}{2}\frac{2}{\overline{d}_{\mathbf{H}}}\rho(X\cap F),

and hence
\alpha(X)\leq\rho(X)
, and
\alpha(E)=\alpha(F)=\rho(F)=\rho(E)
.

Example 4.6
.

We cannot drop the hyperfiniteness assumption in Lemma
4.4
.
Indeed, let
D=2r-1
be an odd integer
(r\geq 3)
, and let
\mathbf{G}
be a graphing
such that every connected component is a
D
-regular tree (such graphings
exist, for example the Bernoulli graphing of a
D
-regular rooted tree, see
e.g.
[
14
]
). By a theorem of Csóka, Lippner and Pilhurko
[
9
]
,
the edge set of
\mathbf{G}
has a partition
E_{0}\cup E_{1}\cup\dots\cup E_{D+1}
such
that
E_{i}\in\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}
,
E_{1},\dots,E_{D+1}
are matchings, and
E_{0}
covers
a set
S_{0}
of measure
0
. Let
U=E_{1}\cup\dots\cup E_{r}
and
W=E\setminus U
.
Then every
u\in J\setminus S_{0}
misses at most one of
E_{1},\dots,E_{D+1}
.
Hence the connected component of
(J,U)
containing
u
has at least
r
nodes,
and the same holds for
(J,W)
. This implies that

\rho(U)+\rho(W)\geq 2\big(1-\frac{1}{r}\big)=2-\frac{2}{r}>1=\rho(U\cup W).

So
\rho
is not even additive.

4.4
Beginnings of a limit theory

A next step should be to develop the limit theory for
\rho
; in other words,
to show that if a graph sequence
(G_{n}:~n=1,2,\dots)
with uniformly bounded
degrees locally converges to a graphing
\mathbf{G}
, then the setfunctions

\rho_{G_{n}}
converge to the setfunction
\rho_{\mathbf{G}}
in some sense. This is not
resolved in this paper; but we do prove that the total rank converges. Given a
graphing
\mathbf{G}=(J,\mathcal{B},E,\lambda)
, we define its
total rank
as

\overline{\rho}(\mathbf{G})=\rho_{\mathbf{G}}(E(\mathbf{G}))=1-{\sf E}\Big(\frac{1}{|V(\mathbf{G}_{\mathbf{u}})|}\Big),

(14)

where
\mathbf{u}
is a random point from
\lambda
. The total rank of a finite graph
is defined analogously.

A graphing parameter
f
is called
locally estimable
, if given an error
bound
\varepsilon>0
and degree bound
D\geq 0
, there are integers
k,r\geq 1

depending only on
\varepsilon
and
D
, such that for any graphing

\mathbf{G}=(J,\mathcal{B},E,\lambda)
with maximum degree
D
, selecting
k
independent
random nodes
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
from
\lambda
and considering their
neighborhoods
B_{r}(x_{1}),\ldots,B_{r}(x_{k})
, we can compute an estimate
R
of

f(G)
such that

{\sf P}\big(|R-f(G)|\geq\varepsilon\big)\leq\varepsilon.

(15)

This definition applies, in particular, to estimating
f
on finite graphs.

Theorem 4.7
.

(a)
The graphing parameter
\overline{\rho}
is locally estimable.
(b)
If

G_{1},G_{2},\ldots
is a sequence of finite graphs with all degrees bounded by

D\geq 0
locally converging to a graphing
\mathbf{G}
, then
\overline{\rho}(G_{n})\to\overline{\rho}(\mathbf{G})

as (
n\to\infty
).

Proof.

Choose
k=\lceil 2/\varepsilon\rceil
. Then for every
x\in J
we have

B_{k}(x)\subseteq V(x)
and

|B_{k}(x)|\begin{cases}=|V(x)|,&\text{if $B_{k}(x)=V(x)$},\\
\geq k,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Hence

\frac{1}{|V(x)|}\leq\frac{1}{|B_{k}(x)|}\leq\frac{1}{|V(x)|}+\frac{1}{k}.

Taking expectation in
x
, we get
