(His monographs was during all these years actively used by students and colleagues, mainly from Portugal and Spain.) Finally, I thank Professor Petronilho’s youngest daughter, Inês, who found these old files and sent them to me.

Abstract
This manuscript contains a small portion of the algebraic theory of orthogonal polynomials developed by Maroni and their applicability to the study and characterization of the classical families, namely Hermite, Laguerre, Jacobi, and Bessel polynomials. It is presented a cyclical proof of some of the most relevant characterizations, particularly those due to Al-Salam and Chihara, Bochner, Hahn, Maroni, and McCarthy. Two apparently new characterizations are also added. Moreover, it is proved through an equivalence relation that, up to constant factors and affine changes of variables, the four families of polynomials named above are the only families of classical orthogonal polynomials.

1.1
The spaces
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}

It is useful to consider OP as test functions living in an appropriate locally convex space (LCS), which we denote by
\mathcal{P}
. This LCS is the set of all polynomials (with real or complex coefficients) endowed with a strict inductive limit topology, so that

\mathcal{P}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{P}_{n}=\mbox{\rm ind\,lim}_{n}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}\;,

(1.2)

where
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is the space of all polynomials of degree at most
n
.
(For the sake of simplicity, we do not distinguish between
polynomial
and
polynomial function
.)
Since
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a finite dimensional vector space, all its norms are equivalent,
so there is no need to specify any particular one.
For the development of the theory to be presented here it is not important to know much about the topology (the definition and basic properties of LCS, including inductive limit topologies, can be found, e.g., in Chapter V of
Reed and Simon’s book
[
13
]
, but the reader should keep in mind that the reason why such topology is introduced is because it implies the following fundamental property:

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\mbox{\rm ind\,lim}_{n}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}
, as in
(\ref{P=indlimPn})
,
and let
\mathcal{P}^{*}
and
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
be the algebraic and the
topological duals of
\mathcal{P}
, respectively. Then

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\mathcal{P}^{*}\,.

(1.3)

Proof.

Obviously,
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{*}
.
To prove that
\mathcal{P}^{*}\subseteq\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
, take
{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{*}
.
From the basic properties of the inductive limit topologies, to prove that
{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}

it suffices to show that the restriction
{\bf u}|\mathcal{P}_{n}
is continuous for every
n
.
But this is a trivial assertion, since
{\bf u}|\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a linear functional
defined on a finite dimensional normed space.
∎

Equality (
1.3
) means that every linear functional defined in
\mathcal{P}
is continuous (for the strict inductive limit topology in
\mathcal{P}
). This is a “curious" property, because, for instance, (
1.3
) is not true for a normed vector space
N
. Indeed,
N^{\prime}=N^{*}
if
{\rm dim}\,N<\infty
, whilst
N^{\prime}\neq N^{*}
whenever
{\rm dim}\,N=\infty
. Note that being a strict inductive limit of the spaces
\mathcal{P}_{n}
, and taking into account that each
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is a proper closed subspace of
\mathcal{P}_{n+1}
(so that
\mathcal{P}
is indeed an hyper strict inductive limit of the spaces
\mathcal{P}_{n}
), the general theory of LCS ensures that
\mathcal{P}
cannot be a metrizable space, and so
a fortiori
it is not a normed space—or, to be more precise, it is not possible to provide
\mathcal{P}
with a norm that generates in it the above inductive limit topology.

In
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
we consider the weak dual topology, which, by definition, is generated by the family of semi-norms

s_{p}:\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\to[0,+\infty[
,
p\in\mathcal{P}
, defined by

s_{p}\big({\bf u}\big)=|\langle{\bf u},p\rangle|\;,\quad{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\;.

(1.4)

It turns out that this family of semi-norms
s_{p}
is equivalent to the family of semi-norms

|\cdot|_{n}:\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\to[0,+\infty[
,
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, defined by

|{\bf u}|_{n}=\max_{0\leq k\leq n}|\langle{\bf u},x^{k}\rangle|\;,\quad{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\;.

(1.5)

Indeed, the following proposition holds.

Proposition 1.2
.

\mathcal{S}=\{s_{p}:p\in\mathcal{P}\}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\sharp}=\{|\cdot|_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
,
with
s_{p}
and
|\cdot|_{n}
given by
(\ref{semip1})
–
(\ref{sharp1})
,
are equivalent families of seminorms in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
, provided
\mathcal{P}=\mbox{\rm ind\,lim}_{n}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}
.

Proof.

Given
p\in\mathcal{P}
, putting
p(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}x^{j}
and
C(p)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}|a_{j}|
, we have

s_{p}({\bf u})=|\langle{\bf u},p\rangle|=\Big|\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}\langle{\bf u},x^{j}\rangle\Big|\leq C(p)|{\bf u}|_{n}\,,\quad\forall{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\,.

On the other hand, given
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, setting
p_{j}(x)=x^{j}
(
j=0,1,\ldots,n
), we have

|{\bf u}|_{n}=\max_{0\leq j\leq n}|\langle{\bf u},x^{j}\rangle|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{n}|\langle{\bf u},x^{j}\rangle|=\sum_{j=0}^{n}s_{p_{j}}({\bf u})\,,\quad\forall{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\,.

Thus,
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\sharp}
are equivalent families of semi-norms
(see
[
13
, p.126]
).
∎

Remark 1.1
.

Since
\mathcal{S}_{\sharp}
is a countable family of seminorms, then

\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is a metrizable space, a metric being given by

\varrho({\bf u},{\bf v})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{n}}\frac{|{\bf u}-{\bf v}|_{n}}{1+|{\bf u}-{\bf v}|_{n}}\;,\quad{\bf u},{\bf v}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\;.

Moreover,
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is a Fréchet space.

1.2
Dual basis in
\mathcal{P}^{*}

In
\mathcal{P}^{*}
, addition and multiplications by scalars can be defined by

\displaystyle\left\langle{\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v},x^{n}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\mathbf{u},x^{n}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\mathbf{v},x^{n}}\right\rangle,\quad\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{P}^{*},

\displaystyle\left\langle{\alpha\mathbf{u},x^{n}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\alpha\left\langle{\mathbf{u},x^{n}}\right\rangle

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
\mathcal{P}^{*}
, endowed with these operations, is a vector space over
\mathbb{C}
. In the vector space
\mathcal{P}^{*}
, the identity for the additivity is denoted by
\mathbf{0}
and called the zero (or the null element). The zero is therefore defined by the relation
\left\langle{\mathbf{0},x^{n}}\right\rangle=0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Of course, the elements of
\mathcal{P}^{*}
can not only be added, but also multiplied (the
Cauchy product
) in order to make the vector space
\mathcal{P}^{*}
into an
algebra
. Since we work mainly on
\mathcal{P}^{*}
instead of
\mathcal{P}
, it would be explicitly build bases in
\mathcal{P}^{*}
. This makes sense, since (
1.3
) allow us writing expansions (finite or infinite sums) of the elements of
\mathcal{P}^{*}
in terms of the elements of a given basis, in the sense of the weak dual topology. Such basis in
\mathcal{P}^{*}
may be achieved in a natural way, by analogy with the case of finitely generated free modules. A
simple set
in
\mathcal{P}
is a sequence of polynomials,
\{R_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
, such that
{\rm deg}\,R_{n}=n
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
(where
R_{0}\equiv\mbox{\rm const.}\neq 0
). To any simple set in
\mathcal{P}
,
\{R_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
, we may associate a
dual basis
, which, by definition, is a sequence of linear functionals
\{{\bf a}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
, being
{\bf a}_{n}:\mathcal{P}\to\mathbb{C}
, such that

\langle{\bf a}_{n},R_{k}\rangle=\delta_{n,k},\quad n,k=0,1,2,\dots\;,

where
\delta_{n,k}
represents the Kronecker symbol (
\delta_{n,k}=1
if
n=k
;
\delta_{n,k}=0
if
n\neq k
).

Proposition 1.3
.

Let
\{R_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be a simple set in
\mathcal{P}
and
\{{\bf a}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
the associated dual basis.
Let
{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{*}
. Then

{\bf u}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\langle{\bf u},R_{n}\rangle\,{\bf a}_{n}\,,

(1.6)

in the sense of the weak dual topology in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Notice first that the assertion makes sense, according with (
1.3
).
To prove it, fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
and let

{\bf s}_{N}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda_{n}{\bf a}_{n},\quad\lambda_{n}=\langle{\bf u},R_{n}\rangle

be the partial sum of order
N
of the series appearing in (
1.6
).
We need to show that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle{\bf s}_{N}-{\bf u},p\rangle=0\;,\quad\forall p\in\mathcal{P}\;.

Clearly, it suffices to prove that this equality holds for
p\in\{R_{0},R_{1},R_{2},\dots\}
.
Indeed, fix
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then, for
N>k
,

\langle{\bf s}_{N}-{\bf u},R_{k}\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\langle{\bf u},R_{n}\rangle\langle{\bf a}_{n},R_{k}\rangle-\langle{\bf u},R_{k}\rangle=0\;,

hence
\;\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle{\bf s}_{N}-{\bf u},R_{k}\rangle=0
.
∎

The polynomial

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
and

(1.15)

Proof.

Assume that

Let

H_{-1}=1\;,\quad H_{n}=\det\big\{[u_{i+j}]_{i,j=0}^{n}\big\}\;,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\;.

One of the most important characterizations of OP relies upon the fact that any

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\{\beta_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}

\{\gamma_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be two arbitrary
sequences of complex numbers, and let
\{P_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}

P_{n+1}(x)=(x-\beta_{n})P_{n}(x)-\gamma_{n}P_{n-1}(x)\;,\quad n=0,1,2,\dots,

\langle{\bf u},1\rangle=u_{0}=\gamma_{0}\;,\qquad\langle{\bf u},P_{n}P_{m}\rangle=0\quad\mbox{\rm if}\quad n\neq m\quad(n,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0})\;.

(1.19)

Moreover,
{\bf u}
is regular and

is the corresponding monic OP

Note the relations

\beta_{n}=\frac{\langle{\bf u},xP_{n}^{2}\rangle}{\langle{\bf u},P_{n}^{2}\rangle}\;,\quad\gamma_{n+1}=\frac{\langle{\bf u},P_{n+1}^{2}\rangle}{\langle{\bf u},P_{n}^{2}\rangle}=\frac{H_{n-1}H_{n+1}}{H_{n}^{2}}\;,\quad n=0,1,\ldots\;.

2
Distributional differential equation

The distributional differential equation has the form

D(\phi{\bf u})=\psi{\bf u}\;,

(2.1)

where
\phi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
and
\psi\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
,
and
{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
is the unknown.
Notice that we do not require
a priori

{\bf u}
to be a regular functional.
We may write

\phi(x)=ax^{2}+bx+c\;,\quad\psi(x)=px+q\;,

(2.2)

being
a,b,c,p,q\in\mathbb{C}
.
We also define, for each integer or rational number
n
,

\psi_{n}=\psi+n\phi^{\prime}\;,\quad d_{n}=\psi_{n/2}^{\prime}=na+p\;,\quad e_{n}=\psi_{n}(0)=nb+q\;.

(2.3)

Notice that
\psi_{n}(x)=d_{2n}x+e_{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
.
Finally, for each
{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
and each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we set

{\bf u}^{[n]}=\phi^{n}{\bf u}\;.

(2.4)

We begin with the following elementary result.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.
Then
{\bf u}
satisfies the distributional differential equation
(\ref{Pearson-DEq})

if and only if the corresponding sequence of moments,
u_{n}=\langle{\bf u},x^{n}\rangle
,
satisfies the second order linear difference equation

d_{n}u_{n+1}+e_{n}u_{n}+n\phi(0)u_{n-1}=0\;,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\;.

(2.5)

Moreover, if
{\bf u}
satisfies
(\ref{Pearson-DEq})
, then
{\bf u}^{[n]}
satisfies

D\big(\phi{\bf u}^{[n]}\big)=\psi_{n}{\bf u}^{[n]}\;,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\;.

(2.6)

Notice that if both
\phi
and
\psi
vanish identically then (
2.1
) reduces to a trivial equation,
so we will exclude this situation from our study.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
. Suppose that
{\bf u}
is regular and satisfies

(\ref{Pearson-DEq})
, being

\phi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
and
\psi\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
,
and assume that at least one of the polynomials
\phi
and
\psi
is nonzero.
Then neither
\phi
nor
\psi
is the zero polynomial, and

\deg\psi=1\;.

(2.7)

Given a monic polynomial
P_{n}
of degree
n
(which needs not to belong to an OP),
we denote by
P_{n}^{[k]}
the monic polynomial of degree
n
defined by

P_{n}^{[k]}(x)=\frac{{\rm d}^{k}}{{\rm d}x^{k}}\,\frac{P_{n+k}(x)}{(n+1)_{k}},\quad k,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\;,

(2.8)

where, for a given
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
,
(\alpha)_{n}
is the
Pochhammer symbol
, defined as

(\alpha)_{0}=1\;,\qquad(\alpha)_{n}=\alpha(\alpha+1)\cdots(\alpha+n-1)\;,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}\;.

(2.9)

Clearly, if
\{P_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is a simple set in
\mathcal{P}
, then so is
\{P_{n}^{[k]}\}_{n\geq 0}
.
Under such conditions, there is a beautiful relation between the associated dual basis:

D^{k}\big(\,{\bf a}_{n}^{[k]}\,\big)=(-1)^{k}(n+1)_{k}\,{\bf a}_{n+k}\;,\quad k,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\,,

(2.10)

where
\{{\bf a}_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
and
\big\{{\bf a}_{n}^{[k]}\,\big\}_{n\geq 0}
are the dual basis
in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
associated with
\{P_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
and
\{P_{n}^{[k]}\}_{n\geq 0}
, respectively.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
, and suppose that

{\bf u}
satisfies the distributional differential equation
(\ref{Pearson-DEq})
,
with
\phi
and
\psi
given by
(\ref{Pearson-PhiPsi})
,
being at least one of these polynomials nonzero.
Suppose further that
{\bf u}
is regular. Then

d_{n}=na+p\neq 0\;,\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\;.

(2.11)

Moreover, if
\{P_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
denotes the monic OP with respect to
{\bf u}
, and

P_{n}^{[k]}
is defined by
(\ref{Pnk-deriv})
, then
{\bf u}^{[k]}=\phi^{k}{\bf u}
is regular and

\{P_{n}^{[k]}\}_{n\geq 0}
is its monic OP, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

We may now establish necessary and sufficient conditions ensuring
the regularity of a given functional
{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
satisfying (

). The next results was proved in
[

(see also
[

in a more general context).

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\setminus\{{\bf 0}\}
,
and suppose that
{\bf u}
satisfies

D(\phi{\bf u})=\psi{\bf u}\;,

(2.12)

where
\phi
and
\psi
are nonzero polynomials such that

\phi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
and
\psi\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
. Set

\phi(x)=ax^{2}+bx+c\,,\quad\psi(x)=px+q\,,\quad d_{n}=na+p\,,\quad e_{n}=nb+q\quad(n\in\mathbb{N}_{0})\,.

Then,
{\bf u}

d_{n}\neq 0\,,\quad\phi\Big(-\frac{e_{n}}{d_{2n}}\Big)\neq 0\,,\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\;.

(2.13)

Moreover, under these conditions, the monic OP

with respect to

with initial conditions
P_{-1}(x)=0
and

\beta_{n}=\frac{ne_{n-1}}{d_{2n-2}}-\frac{(n+1)e_{n}}{d_{2n}}\,,\quad\gamma_{n+1}=-\frac{(n+1)d_{n-1}}{d_{2n-1}d_{2n+1}}\phi\Big(-\frac{e_{n}}{d_{2n}}\Big)\;,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\;.

(2.15)

In addition, for each

,
P_{n}
satisfies the distributional Rodrigues formula

P_{n}{\bf u}=k_{n}\,D^{n}\big(\phi^{n}{\bf u}\big)\;,\quad k_{n}=\prod_{i=0}^{n-1}d_{n+i-1}^{-1}\,.

(2.16)

3
Classical orthogonal polynomials

The
classical functionals
are the regular solutions (in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
) of the distributional equation (
2.1
). The corresponding OP are called
classical orthogonal polynomials
.
In this section we present the most significant results concerning this important class of OP.

3.1
Definition and characterizations

Definition 3.1
.

Let
{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
.

{\bf u}
is called a
classical
functional if the following two conditions hold:

(i)

{\bf u}
is regular;

(ii)

{\bf u}
satisfies the distributional differential equation

D(\phi{\bf u})=\psi{\bf u}\;,

(3.1)

where
\phi
and
\psi
are polynomials fulfilling

\deg\phi\leq 2\;,\quad\deg\psi=1\;.

(3.2)

An OP
\{P_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
with respect to a classical functional
is called a
classical OP
.

Remark 3.1
.

According with Lemma
2.2
, in the above definition
conditions (
3.2
) may be replaced by the weaker conditions

\phi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}\;,\quad\psi\in\mathcal{P}_{1}\;,\quad\{\phi,\psi\}\neq\mathcal{P}_{-1}=\{0\}\;.

(3.3)

Theorem
2.1
gives necessary and sufficient conditions for the existence of solutions
of the distributional equation (
2.1
),
characterizing also such functionals (and, in particular, solving the question of the existence of classical functionals).
Thus, we may state:

a functional
{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}\setminus\{{\bf 0}\}
is classical if and only if
there exist
\phi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
and
\psi\in\mathcal{P}_{1}
such that the following conditions hold:

\begin{array}[]{rl}{\rm(i)}&D(\phi{\bf u})=\psi{\bf u}\,;\\
{\rm(ii)}&na+p\neq 0\;,\quad\displaystyle\phi\left(-\frac{nb+q}{2na+p}\right)\neq 0\;,\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\;,\end{array}

(3.4)

where we have set
\phi(x)=ax^{2}+bx+c
and
\psi(x)=px+q
.

In the next proposition we state several characterizations of the classical OP.
For convenience, we introduce the concept of admissible pair of polynomials.

Definition 3.2
.

(\phi,\psi)
is called an

\langle{\bf v},x^{n}\rangle=\Big\langle{\bf u},\Big(\mbox{$\frac{x-B}{A}$}\Big)^{n}\,\Big\rangle\;,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\;.

Therefore, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle D(\Phi{\bf v}),x^{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=-n\langle{\bf v},\Phi(x)x^{n-1}\rangle=-n\big\langle{\bf u},\big(\tau_{B}\circ h_{A^{-1}}\big)\big(\Phi(x)x^{n-1}\big)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=-n\Big\langle{\bf u},\Phi\Big(\mbox{$\frac{x-B}{A}$}\Big)\Big(\mbox{$\frac{x-B}{A}$}\Big)^{n-1}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=-\Big\langle{\bf u},K\phi(x)\cdot A\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}x}\Big\{\Big(\mbox{$\frac{x-B}{A}$}\Big)^{n}\Big\}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=KA\Big\langle D\big(\phi(x){\bf u}\big),\Big(\mbox{$\frac{x-B}{A}$}\Big)^{n}\Big\rangle=KA\Big\langle\psi(x){\bf u},\Big(\mbox{$\frac{x-B}{A}$}\Big)^{n}\Big\rangle

\displaystyle=\Big\langle{\bf u},\Psi\Big(\mbox{$\frac{x-B}{A}$}\Big)\Big(\mbox{$\frac{x-B}{A}$}\Big)^{n}\Big\rangle=\big\langle{\bf u},\big(\tau_{B}\circ h_{A^{-1}}\big)\big(\Psi(x)x^{n}\big)\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\bf v},\Psi(x)x^{n}\rangle=\langle\Psi{\bf v},x^{n}\rangle\;.

Finally, the last sentence stated in the lemma follows by using Theorem
1.3
.
∎

Theorem 3.2

(canonical representatives of the classical functionals)
.

Let
{\bf u}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
be a classical functional,
so that
{\bf u}
fulfils

D(\phi{\bf u})=\psi{\bf u}\;,

(3.29)

where
\phi(x)=ax^{2}+bx+c
and
\psi(x)=px+q
, subject to the regularity conditions

na+p\neq 0\;,\quad\phi\left(-\frac{nb+q}{2na+p}\right)\neq 0\;,\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\;.

(3.30)

Then, there exists a regular functional
{\bf v}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
such that

{\bf u}\sim{\bf v}\;,\quad D(\Phi{\bf v})=\Psi{\bf v}\;,

(3.31)

where, for each classical functional determined by the pair
(\phi,\psi)
,
the corresponding pair
(\Phi,\Psi)
is given by Table
1
.
More precisely, setting

\Delta=b^{2}-4ac\;;\quad d=\psi\left(-\mbox{$\frac{b}{2a}$}\right)\;\;\mbox{\rm if}\;\;a\neq 0\;,

the following holds:

1. (Hermite)
if
a=b=0
, then:

{\bf v}=\big(\bm{h}_{\sqrt{-p/(2c)}}\circ\bm{\tau}_{q/p}\big){\bf u}={\bf u}_{{}_{H}}\;;

2. (Laguerre)
if
a=0
and
b\neq 0
, then:

{\bf v}=\big(\bm{h}_{-p/b}\circ\bm{\tau}_{c/b}\big){\bf u}={\bf u}_{{}_{L}}^{(\alpha)}\;,\quad\alpha=-1+(qb-pc)/b^{2}\;;

3. (Bessel)
if
a\neq 0
and
\Delta=0
, then:

{\bf v}=\big(\bm{h}_{2a/d}\circ\bm{\tau}_{b/(2a)}\big){\bf u}={\bf u}_{{}_{B}}^{(\alpha)}\;,\quad\alpha=-2+p/a\;;

4. (Jacobi)
if
a\neq 0
and
\Delta\neq 0
, then:

{\bf v}=\big(\bm{h}_{-2a/\sqrt{\Delta}}\circ\bm{\tau}_{b/(2a)}\big){\bf u}={\bf u}_{{}_{J}}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\;,

\alpha=-1+p/(2a)-d/\sqrt{\Delta}\;,\quad\beta=-1+p/(2a)+d/\sqrt{\Delta}\;.

Proof.

Taking into account Lemma
3.1
,
the theorem will be proved if we are able to show that,
for each given pair
(\phi,\psi)
, and for each
corresponding pair
(\Phi,\Psi)
given by Table
1
—where the
“corresponding pair”
(\Phi,\Psi)
is the one in the table such that
\phi
and
\Phi

have the same degree and their zeros the same multiplicity—,
there exist
A,K\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
and
B\in\mathbb{C}
such that the relations

\Phi(x)=K\phi(Ax+B)\;,\quad\Psi(x)=KA\psi(Ax+B)=KA^{2}px+KA(Bp+q)

(3.32)

hold, for appropriate choices of the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta

appearing in Table
1
for the Laguerre, Bessel, and Jacobi cases.
Indeed, considering the four possible cases determined by the polynomial
\phi
, we have:

1. Assume
a=b=0
, i.e.,
\phi(x)=c
. The regularity conditions (
3.30
)
ensure that
p\neq 0
and
c\neq 0
. Therefore, since in this case we require

(\Phi,\Psi)=(1,-2x)
, from (
3.32
) we obtain the equations

1=Kc\;,\quad-2=KA^{2}p\;,\quad 0=Bp+q\;.

A solution of this system of equations is

K=1/c\;,\quad A=\sqrt{-2c/p}\;,\quad B=-q/p\;,

which gives the desired result for the Hermite case, by Lemma
3.1
.

2. Assume
a=0
and
b\neq 0
, so that
\phi(x)=bx+c
.
Since in this case we require
(\Phi,\Psi)=(x,-x+\alpha+1)
, from (
3.32
) we obtain

1=KAb\;,\quad 0=bB+c\;,\quad-1=KA^{2}p\;,\quad\alpha+1=KA(Bp+q)\;.

Solving this system we find

K=-p/b^{2}\;,\quad B=-c/b\;,\quad A=-b/p\;,\quad\alpha=-1+(qb-pc)/b^{2}\;.

Notice that, in this case,

d_{n}=p\;,\quad\phi\left(-\frac{nb+q}{2na+p}\right)=-\frac{b^{2}}{p}\big(n+\alpha+1\big)\;,

hence the regularity conditions (
3.30
) ensure that
p\neq 0

(and so
K
and
A
are well defined, being both nonzero complex numbers) and
-\alpha\not\in\mathbb{N}
.

3. Assume
a\neq 0
and
\Delta=0
. Then
\phi(x)=a\Big(x+\frac{b}{2a}\Big)^{2}
.
In this case we require
(\Phi,\Psi)=\big(x^{2},(\alpha+2)x+2\big)
, hence from (
3.32
) we obtain

1=KA^{2}a\;,\quad 0=B+b/(2a)\;,\quad\alpha+2=KA^{2}p\;,\quad 2=KA(Bp+q)\;.

Therefore, taking into account that

d=\psi\left(-\mbox{$\frac{b}{2a}$}\right)=(2aq-pb)/(2a)
, we deduce

K=4a/d^{2}\;,\quad B=-b/(2a)\;,\quad A=d/(2a)\;,\quad\alpha=-2+p/a\;.

In this case we have

d_{n}=a(n+\alpha+2)\;,\quad\phi\left(-\frac{nb+q}{2na+p}\right)=\frac{d^{2}}{a(2n+\alpha+2)^{2}}\;,

hence conditions (
3.30
) ensure that
-(\alpha+1)\not\in\mathbb{N}

and
d\neq 0
, and so, in particular,
K
is well defined, being both
K
and
A
nonzero complex numbers.

4. Finally, assume
a\neq 0
and
\Delta\neq 0
.
Writing
\phi(x)=a\Big[\Big(x+\frac{b}{2a}\Big)^{2}-\frac{\Delta}{4a^{2}}\Big]
, since
in this case we require
(\Phi,\Psi)=\big(1-x^{2},-(\alpha+\beta+2)x+\beta-\alpha\big)
,
from (
3.32
) we obtain

\begin{array}[]{c}-1=KA^{2}a\;,\quad 0=B+b/(2a)\;,\quad 1=Ka\Big[\Big(B+\frac{b}{2a}\Big)^{2}-\frac{\Delta}{4a^{2}}\Big]\;,\\[5.0pt]
-(\alpha+\beta+2)=KA^{2}p\;,\quad\beta-\alpha=KA(Bp+q)\;.\end{array}

A solution of this system of five equations is

\begin{array}[]{c}K=-4a/\Delta\;,\quad B=-b/(2a)\;,\quad A=-\sqrt{\Delta}/(2a)\;,\\[2.5pt]
\alpha=-1+p/(2a)-d/\sqrt{\Delta}\;,\quad\beta=-1+p/(2a)+d/\sqrt{\Delta}\;.\end{array}

(We choose
A
with the minus sign since whenever
(\phi,\psi)=(\Phi,\Psi)
that choice implies
A=1

and
B=0
, hence
{\bf u}={\bf v}={\bf u}_{J}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
, and so it is a more natural choice.)
Adding and subtracting the last two equations for
\alpha
and
\beta
,
we find
\alpha+\beta+2=p/a
and
\alpha-\beta=-2d/\sqrt{\Delta}
, hence we deduce

d_{n}=a(n+\alpha+\beta+2)\;,\quad\phi\left(-\frac{nb+q}{2na+p}\right)=-\frac{\Delta}{a}\frac{(n+\alpha+1)(n+\beta+1)}{(2n+\alpha+\beta+2)^{2}}\;,

Therefore, conditions (
3.30
) ensure that
-(\alpha+\beta+1)\not\in\mathbb{N}
,

-\alpha\not\in\mathbb{N}
, and
-\beta\not\in\mathbb{N}
. This completes the proof.
∎
