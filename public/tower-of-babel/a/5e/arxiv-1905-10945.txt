Abstract
A new bimodal generative model is proposed for generating conditional and joint samples, accompanied with a training method with learning a succinct bottleneck representation. The proposed model, dubbed as the variational Wyner model, is designed based on two classical problems in network information theory—distributed simulation and channel synthesis—in which Wyner’s common information arises as the fundamental limit on the succinctness of the common representation. The model is trained by minimizing the symmetric Kullback–Leibler divergence between variational and model distributions with regularization terms for common information, reconstruction consistency, and latent space matching terms, which is carried out via an adversarial density ratio estimation technique. The utility of the proposed approach is demonstrated through experiments for joint and conditional generation with synthetic and real-world datasets, as well as a challenging zero-shot image retrieval task.

Introduction

Over the last decade, we have witnessed an uncountable number of successes of deep learning, which are mostly attributed to its power of learning a good, low-dimensional representation of data

The importance of representation learning has become more significant than ever in the last few years, as represented by a recent paradigm shift towards a task-agnostic learning framework

Despite the practical breakthroughs, however, answers to the fundamental questions like “what is a good representation?” and “how can we find such a representation?” are still unsatisfactory.

In this context, we study how to learn a good joint representation of a pair of random vectors

with complex dependence from data, with the following structure:

of
({\bf X},{\bf Y})
;

and

This problem is often referred to as the

in the machine learning literature and has numerous applications including joint and conditional generative tasks (also known as domain transfer or image-to-image translation) and cross-domain retrieval tasks

The main difficulty in learning such joint distributions with disentangled representations is that there have been no proper criterion for cross-domain disentanglement that defines an
optimal
common representation.
Indeed, existing approaches which are mostly from the deep learning literature focus on developing a network architecture and/or a set of ad-hoc loss functions that promote the degree of disentanglement, not defining an optimal common representation of a joint distribution.
Even a few existing information-theoretic proposals on learning a good bottleneck representation such as the famous information bottleneck principle
[
11
]
and a recent proposal
[
12
]
based on interactive information
[
13
]
do not define what an optimal representation is and what they aim to look for.

Observe that there are two bad extremes for the common representation.
On one hand, we can use raw data as the common representation
{\bf Z}={\bf X}
or
{\bf Z}={\bf Y}
, which contain maximal information of the pair, but may not be helpful from the view of a user who wishes to perform a downstream task based on it, as there exists a large degree of
redundancy
.
On the other hand, one may choose a common representation
{\bf Z}
as a constant; albeit being the simplest, it discards essential information about the pair and is thus not useful representation. Hence, it is natural to assume that an optimal representation must lie somewhere in between, i.e., capturing the most
succinct
possible representation, as well as maintaining all the commonality of the pair.

In this paper, as a first proposal to the missing definition of an optimal common representation,
we propose a new representation learning principle inspired by network information theory.
To motivate our perspective, consider the following game between Alice (“encoder”) and Bob (“decoder”) that captures the problem setting of
conditional generation
.
Given an image of a child’s photo
{\bf X}
, Alice is asked to encode
{\bf X}
and send its description
{\bf Z}
to Bob who draws a portrait
{\bf Y}
of how the child will grow up based on it.
In this game, Bob wishes to draw nice adulthood portraits, as various as possible, given a child’s photo.
In this cooperative game, Alice needs to help Bob in the process by providing a
good
description
{\bf Z}
of the child’s photo
{\bf X}
.
Intuitively, seeking the
most succinct description

{\bf Z}
that contains information
common in
{\bf X}
and
{\bf Y}
may be beneficial in their guessing processes, since Alice need not describe any extra information beyond that is contained in
{\bf X}
and Bob need not filter out any redundant information from
{\bf Z}

P. Cuff (

Given a joint distribution

Thus motivated from these observations, in this paper,

Now in the learning setting, where we only have access to the joint distribution

In Section

[

and
[

In Section

We defer the details of training schemes and network architectures to Appendix in Supplementary Material.

II
Probabilistic Models

In this section, we define all probabilistic model components for joint and conditional sampling tasks based on the Markov chain
{\bf X}\leftrightarrow{\bf Z}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y}
and the single-letter characterizations in Fig.
2
(b,d).

II-A

Joint Model

As a generative model for modeling the joint distribution
q_{\mathsf{data}}({\bf x},{\bf y})
, we consider the latent variable model
p_{\theta}({\bf z})p_{\theta}({\bf x}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z})p_{\theta}({\bf y}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z})
.
Here,
{\bf Z}\sim p_{\theta}({\bf z})
signifies the common randomness fed into the
probabilistic
decoders
p_{\theta}({\bf x}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z})
and
p_{\theta}({\bf y}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z})
.
We parameterize the probabilistic decoders
p_{\theta}({\bf x}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z})
and
p_{\theta}({\bf y}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z})
by (deterministic) functions
{\bf x}_{\theta}({\bf z},{\bf u})
and
y_{\theta}({\bf z},{\bf v})
with independent
local randomness

{\bf U}\sim p_{\theta}({\bf u})
and
{\bf V}\sim p_{\theta}({\bf v})
, as depicted in the single letter characterization of distributed simulation (Fig.
2
(d)).
With a slight abuse of notation, we use
{\bf x}_{\theta}({\bf z},{\bf u})
for a shorthand for the degenerate distribution
\delta({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{\theta}({\bf z},{\bf u}))
.

II-B

Conditional Models

To model the conditional distribution
q_{\mathsf{data}}({\bf y}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})
, we consider the bottleneck conditional model
q_{\theta}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})p_{\theta}({\bf y}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z})
that follows
{\bf X}\leftrightarrow{\bf Z}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y}
; note that the decoder
p_{\theta}({\bf y}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z})
is shared by the joint model.
The other direction for modeling
q_{\mathsf{data}}({\bf x}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf y})
is symmetric and thus omitted.

II-C

Variational Encoders

In addition to the base components introduced so far from which we can draw joint and conditional samples, we introduce three additional encoders:

\bullet

A joint encoder
q_{\phi}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})
: it plays a key role of an anchor during training, tying the joint and conditional models.

\bullet

Local encoders
q_{\phi}({\bf u}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf x})
,
q_{\phi}({\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf y})
: these can be viewed as
style extractors
for each modality
{\bf x}
and
{\bf y}
: if we learn a succinct common representation
q_{\phi}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})
(e.g., a shared concept) from
({\bf x},{\bf y})
, then
q_{\phi}({\bf u}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf x})
captures the remaining randomness
{\bf U}
of
{\bf X}
(e.g., texture and style).

We will call these encoders
variational
due to a technical reason to be justified when training objectives are introduced below in Section
III-A
.These encoders can be used in training by allowing us to enforce the reconstruction consistency of the model as shown in the next section, as well as in several inference tasks such as domain translation; see Remark
2
.

II-D

Variational Wyner Model

We call the entire model with all the components introduced above, i.e., in Sections
II-A
–
II-C
,
as the (bimodal)
variational Wyner model
.
We remark that in this general framework we may learn multiple models sharing common components, or learn a single model without training the others, depending on the task at hand.
For example, if one is interested in captioning an image, we may only require learning a conditional model from image to caption, without learning the joint distribution and the conditional distribution of image given caption.
For the cross-domain retrieval task (see Remark
3
and Section
VI-C
), we require to learn both conditional models.
When multiple models are trained simultaneously, common components such as the joint encoder
q_{\phi}({\mathbf{z}}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})
and local encoders
q_{\phi}({\mathbf{u}}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\mathbf{z}},{\mathbf{x}}),q_{\phi}({\mathbf{v}}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\mathbf{z}},{\mathbf{y}})
are
shared
.

Remark 1

(Conditional independence structure)
.

The components of the variational Wyner model may naturally arise considering the cross-domain disentanglement problem, and indeed similar models have been studied in the literature; see e.g.,
[
4
,
12

19

The key structural difference of our model compared to existing ones is the conditioning with the common representation

in the local encoders

q_{\phi}({\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})=q_{\phi}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})q_{\phi}({\bf u}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf x})q_{\phi}({\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf y}),

while the existing models ignore the conditioning with

In experiments, we empirically validate that this conditioning with

III
Training Objectives

In this section, we describe a set of training objectives for effectively training the proposed variational Wyner model.

III-A

Main Objectives

Recall Wyner’s optimization problem from the introduction:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \minimize&I_{{\mathsf{xy\to}}}({\bf X},{\bf Y};{\bf Z})\\
\text{~subject to~}&{\bf X}\leftrightarrow{\bf Z}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y},\end{aligned}

(7)

where the variable is the joint encoder
q_{\phi}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})
.
Hence, this optimization problem seeks a joint encoder
q_{\phi}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})
that captures the minimal common information under the Markovity constraint.

For each model distribution
p_{{\mathsf{model}}}\in\{p_{{\mathsf{\to xy}}},p_{{\mathsf{x\to y}}},p_{{\mathsf{y\to x}}}\}
, our main learning principle is to train it by seeking a succinct common representation characterized by Wyner’s optimization problem, and we reformulate the optimization problem (
7
) for each model.
First, since each model distribution
p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})
follows the Markov chain
{\bf X}\leftrightarrow{\bf Z}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y}
,
we replace the Markovity constraint with the following model consistency

\displaystyle p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})

\displaystyle\equiv q_{\mathsf{data}}({\bf x},{\bf y})q_{\phi}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y}),

i.e., the model
p_{{\mathsf{model}}}
is
consistent
with the target data distribution
q_{\mathsf{data}}({\bf x},{\bf y})
and the variational distribution
q_{\phi}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})
.
Under this model consistency, we can further replace the variational CI
I_{{\mathsf{xy\to}}}({\bf X},{\bf Y};{\bf Z})
with the model CI
I_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf X},{\bf Y};{\bf Z})
, which is defined in (
6
).
Hence, for each model
p_{{\mathsf{model}}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \minimize&I_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf X},{\bf Y};{\bf Z})\\
\text{~subject to~}&p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})\equiv q_{\mathsf{data}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z}).\end{aligned}

(8)

The model consistency can be imposed by introducing a distribution matching term

\mathcal{D}_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{xyz}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=D(p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z}),q_{\mathsf{data}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z}))

and constraining it to be 0 for a choice of positive definite divergence function
D(p,q)
such as
f
-divergences, Wasserstein distance, or maximum mean discrepancy
[
20
]
.
Note that each choice of
D(p,q)
requires different training methods and training procedures.
In this paper, we specifically choose the
symmetric KL divergence

D_{\mathsf{sym}}(p({\bf s}),q({\bf s}))\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(p({\bf s})~\|~q({\bf s})\right)+D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(q({\bf s})~\|~p({\bf s})\right)

which is also known as the
Jeffreys divergence

[
21
]
.
Pu et al. 2017
originally suggested its use in generative modeling as an alternative of the one-sided (reverse) KL diveregence
D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(q~\|~p\right)
of VAEs,
since it can encourage mode-seeking and mass-covering simultaneously.
Unlike the typical VAE training, however, the symmetric KL divergence necessitates an additional trick to deal with intractable density ratios. We illustrate an approximate training method in Section
IV
.

Therefore, Wyner’s optimization problem (
8
) for each model can be written as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \minimize&I_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf X},{\bf Y};{\bf Z})\\
\text{~subject to~}&\mathcal{D}_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{xyz}}=0.\end{aligned}

(9)

We now show that this can be further relaxed as

\displaystyle\minimize~\mathcal{D}_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{xyzuv}}+\lambda_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{CI}}I_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf X},{\bf Y};{\bf Z}),

(10)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{xyzuv}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=D_{\mathsf{sym}}(q_{{\mathsf{xy\to}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v}),p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v}))

(11)

is the divergence between the variational distribution and the model distribution over
({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v})
and
\lambda_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{CI}}>0
is a hyperparameter that trades off matching distributions and seeking succinct representation.

We first relax the equality constraint
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{xyz}}=0
with an inequality constraint
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{xyz}}\leq\epsilon
for some
\epsilon>0
and as in
[
20
]
to convert the problem (
9
) into an unconstrained Lagrangian form

\displaystyle\minimize~\mathcal{D}_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{xyz}}+\lambda_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{CI}}I_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf X},{\bf Y};{\bf Z}),

(12)

where the reciprocal of a Lagrange multiplier
\lambda_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{CI}}>0
controls the model CI of
p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})
.
We then introduce an additional variational relaxation step to train the style extractors
q_{\phi}({\bf u}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf x})
and
q_{\phi}({\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf y})
.
That is, by the monotonicity of
f
-divergences (see Proposition
7
in Appendix), we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{xyz}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=D_{\mathsf{sym}}(q_{\mathsf{data}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z}),p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z}))

\displaystyle\leq D_{\mathsf{sym}}(q_{{\mathsf{xy\to}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v}),p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v}))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{=}}\mathchar 58\relax\mathcal{D}_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{xyzuv}},

(13)

where the equality holds if and only if the composite variational encoders

q_{{\mathsf{xy\to}}}({\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})=q_{\phi}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})q_{\phi}({\bf u}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf x})q_{\phi}({\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf y})

match to the model posterior
p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})
.
After all, we obtain (
10
)
as the final relaxed optimization problem for each model distribution
p_{{\mathsf{model}}}\in\{p_{{\mathsf{\to xy}}},p_{{\mathsf{x\to y}}},p_{{\mathsf{y\to x}}}\}
.

Remark 5

(On the variational common information)
.

An acute reader may suggest to simply control the CI regularization by a single term
I_{{\mathsf{xy\to}}}({\bf X},{\bf Y};{\bf Z})
, the variational CI.
Indeed, given that
q_{\phi}({\mathbf{z}}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\mathbf{x}},{\bf y})
is succinctly learned based on (
7
), one could expect that the rest of the model components would be encouraged to be consistent with the succinctly learned
q_{\phi}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})
, via the distribution matching term
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{xyzuv}}
(
13
).
We empirically found, however, that
I_{{\mathsf{xy\to}}}({\bf X},{\bf Y};{\bf Z})
as a regularization term
is not sufficiently effective to control the model CI’s and leads to unstable training,
compared to directly using the model CI
I_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf X},{\bf Y};{\bf Z})
for regularization.
Intuitively, this phenomenon may be attributed to imperfect distribution matching, which is enforced by minimizing
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{xyzuv}}
in our framework,
due to a lack of samples, a limited expressivity of the parametric models, an imperfect training, or their combinations.

III-B

Auxiliary Objectives

Note that learning a succinct common representation becomes meaningful only when a good degree of consistency between the models and data can be assured.
In principle, solving the optimization problem (
10
)
may suffice for training the target generative models with a succinct common representation.
Since, however, such non-convex optimization problems are hard to solve in general, distribution matching may not take place to begin with.
Hence, in this section, we additionally introduce a set of auxiliary objectives that can considerably improve the degree of distribution matching.

III-B
1
Reconstruction Losses

With the variational encoders, we can further guide the training by imposing certain
reconstruction consistency
in the model, so that the optimization of the encoders and decoders is over a restricted function space that conforms to the consistency.
Note that the model trained with the reconstruction loss terms below can be viewed as a form of
autoencoders
.

For the joint model
p_{{\mathsf{\to xy}}}
, similar to the reconstruction in autoencoders, it is natural to desire that the decoders
{\bf x}_{\theta}({\bf z},{\bf u}),{\bf y}_{\theta}({\bf z},{\bf v})
map the inferred representations
({\bf z}_{o},{\bf u}_{o},{\bf v}_{o})\sim q_{{\mathsf{xy\to}}}({\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x}_{o},{\bf y}_{o})
for a given pair
({\bf x}_{o},{\bf y}_{o})
back to
({\bf x}_{o},{\bf y}_{o})
; hence, we aim to minimize the
joint data reconstruction losses
defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{xy\to x}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}_{q_{{\mathsf{xy\to}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v})p_{\theta}(\hat{{\bf x}}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf u})}[d_{\mathsf{x}}({\bf x},\hat{{\bf x}})]

(14)

and the symmetrically defined
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{xy\to y}}
for some dissimilarity functions
d_{\mathsf{x}}({\bf x},\hat{{\bf x}})
and
d_{\mathsf{y}}({\bf y},\hat{{\bf y}})
.

For the conditional model
p_{{\mathsf{x\to y}}}
, we consider the following consistency on the data space.
Given a data pair
({\bf x}_{o},{\bf y}_{o})
, we first draw a common representation
{\bf z}_{o}\sim q_{\phi}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})
only from
{\bf x}_{o}
and find a local representation of
{\bf y}_{o}
conditioned on
{\bf z}_{o}
, i.e.,
{\bf v}_{o}\sim q_{\phi}({\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z}_{o},{\bf y}_{o})
. Then, we expect the decoder
{\bf y}_{\theta}({\bf z},{\bf v})
to reconstruct
{\bf y}_{o}
from the representation
({\bf z}_{o},{\bf v}_{o})
, which leads to the definition of the
conditional reconstruction loss

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{{\mathsf{x\to y}}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathsf{data}}({\bf x},{\bf y})q_{\theta}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})q_{\phi}({\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf y})p_{\theta}(\hat{{\bf y}}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf v})}[d_{\mathsf{y}}({\bf y},\hat{{\bf y}})]

(15)

from
{\bf x}
to
{\bf y}
; the other direction
\mathcal{R}_{{\mathsf{y\to x}}}
is symmetric.

III-B
2
When Learning Joint and Conditional Models Simultaneously: Common Latent Space Matching Losses

When we wish to train a single model that can perform every direction of inference (i.e., joint and both ways of conditional generation), it is important to enforce the induced aggregated posteriors of the model encoders
q_{\theta}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})
and
q_{\theta}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf y})
in the conditional models to be consistent with the prior distribution
p_{\theta}({\bf z})
, so that they can share the common latent space over
{\bf z}
.
That is, we wish to match the aggregated posterior
p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf z})
to the prior
p_{\theta}({\bf z})
for
p_{{\mathsf{model}}}\in\{p_{{\mathsf{x\to y}}},p_{{\mathsf{y\to x}}}\}
.
Since it is only enforced indirectly by the distribution matching losses
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{xyzuv}}
, we further introduce the
latent matching objectives

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{{\mathsf{model}}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=D_{\mathsf{sym}}(p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf z}),p_{\theta}({\bf z})).

(16)

We remark that this consistency is also enforced by minimizing the distribution matching objective
\mathcal{D}_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{xyzuv}}
.
More precisely, by the monotonicity of
f
-divergences (Proposition
7
), we have
\mathcal{M}_{{\mathsf{model}}}\leq\mathcal{D}_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{xyzuv}}
.
Hence, additionally introducing the term
\mathcal{M}_{{\mathsf{model}}}
should be understood as further encouraging the consistency between the model aggregated posterior
p_{\mathsf{model}}({\bf z})
and the prior, so that it improves the quality of downstream tasks.
We empirically found this objective especially helpful when learning with a paired data from two different domains such as an image-caption dataset, where the hardness of learning the target modalities is imbalanced.

III-B
3
When Learning Both Conditional Models Simultaneously: Cross Matching Loss and Marginal Reconstruction Losses

In some applications such as the cross-domain retrieval task (Remark
3
), we are only interested in learning conditional models of both directions such that they share the same common latent space, without learning the joint distribution.
In this case, we find that matching the two conditional distributions, i.e., minimizing

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{x\leftrightarrow y}}^{\mathsf{xyzuv}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=D_{\mathsf{sym}}(p_{{\mathsf{x\to y}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v}),p_{{\mathsf{y\to x}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v})),

(17)

can improve the quality of representation.

Another auxiliary term we find helpful in this scenario is the
marginal
reconstruction losses

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{y\to y}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}_{q_{\mathsf{data}}({\bf y})q_{\theta}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf y})q_{\phi}({\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf y})p_{\theta}(\hat{{\bf y}}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf v})}[d_{\mathsf{y}}({\bf y},\hat{{\bf y}})]

(18)

and the symmetrically defined
\mathcal{R}_{\mathsf{x\to x}}
, which naturally arise when matching two conditional models
p_{{\mathsf{x\to y}}}
and
p_{{\mathsf{y\to x}}}
.

IV
Training Method

As alluded to earlier, we assume implicit generative models with deterministic decoders, the densities of the model distributions are not computable.
Hence, minimizing the objective functions introduced above requires a GAN-like adversarial technique.
In particular, we adopt a technique proposed by
Pu et al. 2017
, which we call the
variational density ratio estimation
.
After all, the proposed training scheme can be viewed as an adversarial learning method of the variational Wyner model.
We also illustrate some tricks that were empirically effective for training in our experiments.

IV-A

Training with Variational Density Ratio Estimation

Note that all the objective terms proposed above are in the form of either
D_{\mathsf{sym}}(p({\bf s}),q({\bf s}))
for distribution matching and
D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(p({\bf s})~\|~q({\bf s})\right)
for mutual information, except the reconstruction losses.
To training with these divergence terms, we approximate the divergence terms by estimating the density ratio
p({\bf s})/q({\bf s})
via an adversarial technique based on a variational characterization of the Jensen–Shannon divergence; namely, we use the optimal solution
r({\bf s})
of the following maximization problem

\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{JS}}(p({\bf s}),q({\bf s}))=

\displaystyle\max_{r({\bf s})}\psi_{\mathsf{JS}}(r({\bf s});p({\bf s}),q({\bf s})),

(19)

where we define

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathsf{JS}}(r({\bf s});p({\bf s}),q({\bf s}))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}_{p({\bf s})}[\log\sigma(\log r({\bf s}))]+\mathbb{E}_{q({\bf s})}[\log\sigma(-\log r({\bf s}))],

(20)

to estimate the density ratio
p({\bf s})/q({\bf s})
, since the maximum of (
19
) is attained if and only if
r^{*}({\bf s})\equiv p({\bf s})/q({\bf s})
.
Here,
\sigma(x)=1/(1+e^{-x})
denotes the sigmoid function.
Note that this is equivalent to the discriminator objective of the original generative adversarial networks (GANs)
[
22
]
, where
D({\bf s})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sigma(\log r({\bf s}))\in[0,1]
is called the
discriminator
; in this work we view
r({\bf s})=\exp(\sigma^{-1}(D({\bf s})))\in(0,\infty)
as a density ratio estimator but also call a discriminator, slightly abusing the terminology.
While, in principle, the variational characterization of any
f
-divergence by
Nguyen et al. 2010
may be used to train a density estimator in a similar spirit of
f
-GANs
[
24
]
, we empirically observed that other choices of
f
-divergences such as one-sided KL divergences and
\chi^{2}
-divergences result in unstable training (data not shown).

As in a standard GAN training procedure, we alternate between training the variational Wyner model components and training the discriminators batch-by-batch, freezing one while training the other.
When training the variational Wyner model, we freeze the density ratio estimators and estimate
D_{\mathsf{sym}}(p({\bf s}),q({\bf s}))
by plugging in the approximate ratio
r({\bf s})
assuming that
r({\bf s})\approx p({\bf s})/q({\bf s})
, i.e.,

D_{\mathsf{sym}}(p({\bf s}),q({\bf s}))\approx\mathbb{E}_{p({\bf s})}[\log r({\bf s})]-\mathbb{E}_{q({\bf s})}[\log r({\bf s})].

Hence, for each distribution matching objective
\mathcal{D}_{\mathsf{key}}^{\mathsf{xyzuv}}
for
\mathsf{key}\in\{{\mathsf{\to xy}},{\mathsf{x\to y}},{\mathsf{y\to x}},\mathsf{x\leftrightarrow y}\}
, we introduce a corresponding density ratio estimator
r_{\mathsf{key}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v})
and optimize it by the discriminator objective
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathsf{key}}^{\mathsf{xyzuv}}
, where,
e.g.,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{{\mathsf{\to xy}}}^{\mathsf{xyzuv}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\psi_{\mathsf{JS}}(r_{{\mathsf{\to xy}}};q_{{\mathsf{xy\to}}},p_{{\mathsf{\to xy}}}).

(21)

For a latent matching objective
\mathcal{M}_{{\mathsf{model}}}
, we train a discriminator
r_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{latent}}({\bf z})
by maximizing

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{{\mathsf{model}}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=

\displaystyle\psi_{\mathsf{JS}}(r_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{latent}}({\bf z});p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf z}),p_{\theta}({\bf z})),

(22)

for each
{\mathsf{model}}\in\{{\mathsf{x\to y}},{\mathsf{y\to x}}\}
.
The mutual information
I_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf X},{\bf Y};{\bf Z})
term can be handled by the same technique, i.e., training a discriminator
r_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{CI}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})
by maximizing

\displaystyle\tilde{I}_{{\mathsf{model}}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\psi_{\mathsf{JS}}(r_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{CI}};p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z}),p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y})p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf z})),

(23)

so that
r_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{CI}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})\approx p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z}))/p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y})p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf z})
, and approximate the mutual information by the same plug-in approach:

\displaystyle I_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf X},{\bf Y};{\bf Z})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})}\Bigl[\log\frac{p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})}{p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y})p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf z})}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\approx\mathbb{E}_{p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})}[\log r_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{CI}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})].

In the minibatch training of density ratio estimators for CI estimation, in order to to sample from a product distribution
p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y})p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf z})
, we first draw
({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})\sim p_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})
from the joint distribution and then simply permute
{\bf z}
over the batch dimension as a proxy to independent sampling.

IV-B

The Final Discriminator Objective

In experiments, to train the discriminators, we simply added the corresponding objective terms without additional weights.

IV-C

Additional Tricks for Training

To make the training scheme more computationally efficient and stable, we made several important design choices,
including (1) a shared joint data feature map among discriminators, (2) deterministic parameterization of encoders, and (3) the instance noise trick
[
25
]
.

IV-C
1
Shared Feature Map in Discriminators

In principle, for each pair of distributions whose density ratio is required to be estimated, we need a density ratio estimator
r_{{\mathsf{model}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v})
for distribution matching or
r_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{CI}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z})
for CI regularization or
r_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{latent}}({\bf z})
for latent matching.
To reduce the size of the discriminator network, in our implementation we use a single joint feature map
f({\bf x},{\bf y})
which maps the pair
({\bf x},{\bf y})
to a feature vector, and every density ratio estimator that takes
({\bf x},{\bf y})
as an argument is of the form either
r_{{\mathsf{model}}}(f({\bf x},{\bf y}),{\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v})
or
r_{{\mathsf{model}}}^{\mathsf{CI}}(f({\bf x},{\bf y}),{\bf z})
.

IV-C
2
Deterministic Encoders

Following the standard practice, we approximate the proposed objectives in Table
I
which are expectations over model distributions by a Monte Carlo approximation and plug-in it to a gradient-based optimization algorithm.
Note, however, that sampling distributions and taking gradients with respect to a parameter of the sampling distribution may cause
biased
gradient estimates, since, in general,

\nabla_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta}({\bf s})}[f_{\theta}({\bf s})]\neq\mathbb{E}_{p_{\theta}({\bf s})}[\nabla_{\theta}f_{\theta}({\bf s})].

A possible detour is to deploy diagonal Gaussian encoders used in VAEs to invoke the reparametrization trick
[
26
]
.
In this work, even simpler, we parameterize all the encoders (i.e., variational encoders
q_{\phi}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})
,
q_{\phi}({\bf u}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf x})
,
q_{\phi}({\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf y})
and model encoders
q_{\theta}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})
,
q_{\theta}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf y})
) by
deterministic
mappings, which can be viewed as the limiting version of the reparameterization trick with vanishing variances.

IV-C
3
Instance Noise Trick

Since our encoders and decoders are all deterministic, they define
degenerate
model distributions, on which the divergences and mutual information terms may not be properly defined due to disjoint support of paired distributions.
This is a well-known issue of implicit generative models, and we adopt the well-known
instance noise trick
of
Sønderby et al. 2017
from the GAN literature.
Namely, we add small Gaussian noise to all inputs of the discriminators (density estimators), which is equivalent to replacing any distribution
p({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v})
of our consideration with that convolved with a Gaussian kernel.
We empirically observed that this trick is effective, but a proper tuning of the level of Gaussian noise is crucial in stabilizing the training procedure while not blurring out the distributions of our interest.

V
Related Work

V-A

On Wyner’s CI and Related Measures

Wyner’s CI was first studied by
Wyner 1975
to investigate the problem of distributed simulation of two discrete random sources and distributed compression in the so-called Gray–Wyner network
[
27
]
.

Witsenhausen 1976
established a lower bound on this quantity and studied its computability.

Cuff 2013
established the role of Wyner’s CI in its conditional counterpart, i.e., the channel synthesis problem.
Later,
Xu et al. 2016
studied the quantity for a pair of continuous random variables,
and provided its operational justification in the distributed
lossy
compression setting.

Recently, Wyner’s common information has received a lot of attention in the information theory literature, especially in the context of its application for extracting correlation between dependent variables.
A recent line of theoretical work includes a local characterization of Wyner’s CI
[
30
]
and an alternative, Wyner’s-CI-based procedure for canonical correlation analysis
[
31
]
.

There exist several other related dependence measures for a pair of random variables
(X,Y)
in information theory.
The mutual information
I(X;Y)
has significant roles and concrete operational meanings in information theory and statistics, including source and channel coding problems and hypothesis testing problems; see, e.g.,
[
17
]
.
We remark, however, that Wyner’s common information
J(X;Y)
is in general different from the more famous quantity of mutual information
I(X;Y)
.
It is easy to prove that
0\leq I(X;Y)\leq J(X;Y)
. In general, the inequalities can be strict, but when
X
and
Y
are independent,
I(X;Y)=J(X;Y)=0
.

The Gác–Körner–Witsenhausen common information
K(X;Y)

[
32
]
is defined to be the maximum number of common bits per symbol that can be independently extracted from
X
and
Y
.
While it has several applications in secret key generation, it is known that the notion is rather restrictive in the sense that
K(X;Y)
becomes positive only for limited cases
[
32
,
33
]
.
Moreover, this quantity can be defined only for discrete random variables.

The Hirschfeld–Gebelein–Rényi (HGR) maximal correlation
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
is a nonlinear generalization of Pearson correlation coefficient. The HGR maximal correlation is originally defined for a pair of scalar random variables, but it was generalized to quantify a measure of dependence between high-dimensional random vectors; see
[
37
]
.
There also exists a line of work on the maximal correlation and its applications in machine learning from an information-theoretic view;
see a recent paper by
Huang et al. 2019
for an overview on the recent theoretical breakthroughs.

For a more broad treatment on this subject, we refer an interested reader to a recent monograph by
Yu et al. 2022
.

Existing Information-Theoretic Approaches

In this section, we provide an in-depth discussion on two information theoretic approaches
[
11
,
12
]
, elaborating the philosophical differences compared to our approach.

V-B
1
Information Bottleneck Principle

The information bottleneck (IB) principle (or method)
[
11
]
is a widely known information theoretic approach in representation learning.
This approach is usually considered for
discriminative tasks
, i.e., when the target variable
{\bf Y}
is a function of
{\bf X}
and/or even discrete.
Motivated by lossy compression, the IB principle proposes to find a
compressed
representation
{\bf Z}
from the input variable
{\bf X}
(i.e.,
q_{\theta}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})
) while maximizing the relevance of
{\bf Z}
in
predicting
the target variable
{\bf Y}
as the minimizer of the optimization problem

\minimize_{q_{\theta}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})}I({\bf X};{\bf Z})-\beta I({\bf Y};{\bf Z}),

where
({\bf X},{\bf Y},{\bf Z})\sim q_{\mathsf{data}}({\bf x},{\bf y})q_{\theta}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})
and
\beta>0
.

Indeed, the IB principle and the proposed framework are suitable to discriminative tasks and generative tasks, respectively, while they fail to define a good representation structure in the other respective cases.
First, consider a discriminative task such as classification, where typically there is a near functional relationship
{\bf Y}\approx f({\bf X})
between
{\bf X}
and
{\bf Y}
.
The proposed framework principle does not posit an interesting structure in this case, since the trivial choice
{\bf Z}={\bf Y}
makes
{\bf X}
and
{\bf Y}
independent and achieves the minimum
I({\bf X},{\bf Y};{\bf Z})=H({\bf Y})
, whereas the IB principle defines a series of representations of different levels of compression controlled by
\beta
.
Secondly, for a generative task where the pair
({\bf X},{\bf Y})
has many-to-many relationship, guessing
{\bf Y}
based on
{\bf Z}
as a representation of
{\bf X}
, the symmetric Markov assumption
{\bf X}\leftrightarrow{\bf Z}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y}
of our approach is more appropriate than
{\bf Z}\leftrightarrow{\bf X}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y}
of IB; crucially, under the Markov chain
{\bf Z}\leftrightarrow{\bf X}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y}
,
{\bf Y}
is not conditionally independent of
{\bf X}
given
{\bf Z}
in general.
We summarize the differences in Table
II
.

In Appendix
A
, we discuss a connection from the notion of minimal sufficient statistics
[
40
]
to Wyner’s optimization problem and the IB principle.

We finally remark that
Alemi et al. 2017
proposed to train a neural network classifier with a variational relaxation of the IB objective to seek a robust representation and a few variations of this work were proposed to find an invariant factors of a target
{\bf X}
given an attribute
{\bf Y}

[
42
,
43
]
.

Interactive Information Maximization

Recently,
Hwang et al. 2020
proposed a new information-theoretic regularization principle to tackle the cross-domain disentanglement problem.
To seek a disentangled representation
({\bf Z},{\bf U},{\bf V})
under the variational distribution

q_{{\mathsf{xy\to}}}^{\prime}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=q_{\mathsf{data}}({\bf x},{\bf y})q_{\phi}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})q_{\phi}({\bf u}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})q_{\phi}({\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf y}),

they propose to maximize the
interactive information among
{\bf X}
,
{\bf Y}
,
{\bf Z}
to enforce
{\bf Z}
to capture a commonality of
({\bf X},{\bf Y})
, while minimizing
I({\bf Z};{\bf U})
and
I({\bf Z};{\bf V})
to enforce the representations
({\bf Z},{\bf U},{\bf V})
to be independent. Here, the interactive information among
{\bf X}
,
{\bf Y}
,
{\bf Z}
is defined as

I({\bf X};{\bf Y};{\bf Z})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=I({\bf X};{\bf Z})-I({\bf X};{\bf Z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf Y})

and it is symmetric in
({\bf X},{\bf Y},{\bf Z})

[
13
]
.
After all, they proposed to minimize a variational upper bound of a weighted combination of a distribution matching term

D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(q_{{\mathsf{xy\to}}}^{\prime}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v})~\|~p_{{\mathsf{\to xy}}}({\bf x},{\bf y},{\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v})\right)

and
I({\bf Z};{\bf U})+I({\bf Z};{\bf V})-2I({\bf X};{\bf Y};{\bf Z})
.
We remark that since
I({\bf Z};{\bf U})
and
I({\bf Z};{\bf V})
terms should become 0 when the paired distributions exactly match, the essential driving force of cross-domain disentanglement in this framework is from the
maximization
of the interactive information.
In a stark contrast, we propose to
minimize
the information captured in
{\bf Z}
.
Further, this interactive-information maximization framework does not define an optimality criterion for a good representation as alluded to earlier, and the choice of weights in the objective term
I({\bf Z};{\bf U})+I({\bf Z};{\bf V})-2I({\bf X};{\bf Y};{\bf Z})
is rather ad-hoc, based on a computational aspect of the final objective.

V-C

Other Cross-Domain Disentanglement Models and Bimodal Generative Models

One of the most closely related work in the deep learning literature is the cross-domain disentanglement networks proposed by
Gonzalez-Garcia et al. 2018
.
Similar to the variational Wyner model, their model also aims to decompose a joint representation into
shared
(common) and
exclusive
(local) representations explicitly.
The crucial difference is that, in
[
4
]
, one of the key components for disentanglement is the use of a
gradient reversal layer

[
44
]
,
while the variational Wyner model forces to learn succinct information using the CI regularization terms, towards learning the optimally succinct representation characterized by (
1
).

The Wyner model can be viewed as a generalization of the probabilistic model (i.e., encoder and decoder) assumed in two existing joint variational autoencoders (VAEs)— JVAE
[
45
]
and JMVAE
[
46
]
—as alluded in the previous paragraph.
These models implement a similar idea of performing joint and conditional generation tasks via a symmetric Markov chain
{\bf X}\leftrightarrow\mathbf{W}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y}
, where
\mathbf{W}
is the
joint representation
of
({\bf X},{\bf Y})
.
In other words, these models can be derived by removing the local variables
{\bf U}
and
{\bf V}
in the variational Wyner model.

The same decoder structure of the variational Wyner model with the “shared” (
{\bf Z}
) and the “private” (
{\bf U},{\bf V}
) latent variables has been also studied in the context of multi-view learning
[
47
,
48
,
49
,
50
]
mostly based on a linear analysis such as canonical correlation analysis (CCA).
More recently, variational CCA-private (VCCA-private)
[
19
]
was proposed to learn the decoder model with variational encoders
q_{\theta}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})
,
q_{\phi}({\bf u}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})
, and
q_{\phi}({\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf y})
, with the encoder model
q_{\phi}({\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})=q_{\theta}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})q_{\phi}({\bf u}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})q_{\phi}({\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf y})
to directly capture the conditional model from
{\bf X}
to
{\bf Z}
to
{\bf Y}
.
On the other hand, the variational Wyner model relies on the conditional independence structure
q_{\phi}({\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})=q_{\phi}({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})q_{\phi}({\bf u}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf x})q_{\phi}({\bf v}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf z},{\bf y})
, which is naturally induced by the decoder model; see Remark
1
.
We argue that this choice of encoder model in our variational Wyner model may capture better semantic meaning of the local (private) random variables
{\bf U}
and
{\bf V}
, thereby leading a better generative performance; see, e.g., Fig.
4
.

Conditional VAE (CVAE)
[
51
]
directly models the conditional distribution
q_{\mathsf{data}}({\bf y}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})
, obtained by simply conditioning every component in the vanilla VAE for
q_{\mathsf{data}}({\bf y})
with the conditioning variable
{\bf X}
.
If
{\bf Y}
is an image and
{\bf X}
is an attribute of the image, a latent representation
{\bf V}
in CVAE needs to capture the redundant information of
{\bf Y}
, which is not contained in
{\bf X}
, i.e., style information of
{\bf Y}
given
{\bf X}
.
The variational Wyner model can be viewed as a combination of two CVAEs with
{\bf Z}
as a common conditioning variable, being capable of bidirectional sampling in its nature.
Yet, if
{\bf X}
is high-dimensional, the conditional models like CVAE in general tend to overfit the input data of
{\bf X}

[
52
]
.
To address this problem, a subsequent related work, bottleneck conditional density estimation (BCDE)
[
53
]
, proposed to learn joint and conditional VAE models simultaneously by softly tying the parameters of the two models for regularization.
We note that the variational Wyner model naturally addresses such problem by using a unified single probabilistic model for both joint and conditional distribution learning, finding a succinct common representation
{\bf Z}
for regularization.

VII
Concluding Remarks

Cuff’s channel synthesis and Wyner’s distributed simulation provide an information-theoretic characterization of the simplest probabilistic structure that connects one random object to another.
The proposed variational Wyner model finds this succinct structure in a disciplined manner, and provides a theoretically sound alternative to the information bottleneck principle
[
11
]
.
As alluded to earlier in Section
V
,
our approach is the first to define an optimal common representation and learn a generative model towards the optimality.
The experimental results demonstrated the potential of our approach as a new way of learning joint and conditional generation tasks with optimal representation learning that can be further developed and refined for more complex dataset such as auditory, text, or a pair of those.

Albeit its potentially wide applicability, we remark that the proposed model and the accompanied training method may suffer slow training and memory inefficiency, as each divergence and mutual information term requires a separate density ratio estimator.
While we reduce the number of parameters by sharing a joint feature map in the discriminator, it might be crucial to devise a more efficient way to implement the proposed framework with less parameters.

We conclude with future directions.
First, we assumed fully paired data throughout the paper. In practice, however, paired data are limited and we have a plenty of unpaired data.
Investigating on how to incorporate such unpaired data in the current learning framework and studying the role and effect of common information regularization in the
semi-supervised
setting may be a fruitful direction, which will make the developed framework applicable in a much richer context.
Second, in this paper, we motivated the use of Wyner’s common information via a heuristic justification from the resemblance between the generative tasks and the information theory problems. Hence, it would be also interesting to formally establish an operational meaning of the common information
I({\bf X},{\bf Y};{\bf Z})
when learning distributions from samples. For example, can we develop a theory that relates the common information
I({\bf X},{\bf Y};{\bf Z})
with its “generalization error” for a generative model as in
[
68
]
?

Appendix A
From Minimal Sufficient Statistics to the Information Bottleneck Principle and Wyner’s Optimization Problem

In this section, starting from the notion of minimal sufficient statistics
[
40
]
from the statistics literature, we derive the information bottleneck (IB) principle and Wyner’s optimization problem from its relaxation, respectively.
We hope that this discussion highlights similarities and dissimilarities between the IB principle and Wyner’s optimization problem.

A-A

Minimal Sufficient Statistics

Consider a pair of random variables
({\bf X},{\bf Y})
.
A function
{\bf Z}={\bf z}({\bf X})
of
{\bf X}
is said to be a
minimal sufficient statistic
of
{\bf X}
for
{\bf Y}
if (1) (sufficiency)
{\bf Z}
is a sufficient statistic of
{\bf X}
for
{\bf Y}
, i.e.,
{\bf X}\leftrightarrow{\bf Z}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y}
, and (2) (minimality)
{\bf Z}
is a function of any other sufficient statistics.
It can be easily shown that
{\bf Z}={\bf z}({\bf X})
is a minimal sufficient statistic if and only if it is an optimal solution of the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\minimize&&I({\bf X};{\bf Z})\\
&\text{~subject to}&&{\bf X}\leftrightarrow{\bf Z}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y}\\
&\text{~variable}&&{\bf Z}={\bf z}({\bf X}).\end{aligned}

(24)

Here, the optimization is over all possible functions
{\bf z}(\cdot)
over
{\bf x}
.

A-B

The IB Principle

First, note that the Markovity constraint
{\bf X}\leftrightarrow{\bf Z}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y}
in (
24
) can be relaxed as
I({\bf Y};{\bf Z})\geq I({\bf X};{\bf Y})
by the data processing inequality.
Second, optimization over functions
{\bf z}({\bf x})
can be relaxed by considering optimization over a probabilistic mapping
q({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})
, as it subsumes deterministic functions.
Finally, introducing a Lagrangian multiplier
\beta>0
to get rid of the inequality constraint on
I({\bf Y};{\bf Z})
, we obtain a relaxed version of (
24
) in the unconstrained form

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\minimize&&I({\bf X};{\bf Z})-\beta I({\bf Y};{\bf Z})\\
&\text{~variable}&&q({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x}).\end{aligned}

(25)

Note that this is the optimization problem that characterizes the IB principle.
A similar argument can be found in
[
69
]
.

A-C

Wyner’s Optimization Problem

As done above, we first relax optimization over
{\bf z}({\bf x})
by optimization over
q({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})
.
Then, observe that optimizing over
q({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})
is equivalent to
q({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})
under an additional constraint
{\bf Z}\leftrightarrow{\bf X}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y}
.
Hence, we can relax the optimization problem (
24
) as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\minimize&&I({\bf X};{\bf Z})\\
&\text{~subject to}&&{\bf X}\leftrightarrow{\bf Z}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y}\\
&&&{\bf Z}\leftrightarrow{\bf X}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y}\\
&\text{~variable}&&q({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y}).\end{aligned}

(26)

By removing the Markovity constraint
{\bf Z}\leftrightarrow{\bf X}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y}
, we can further relax it as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\minimize&&I({\bf X};{\bf Z})\\
&\text{~subject to}&&{\bf X}\leftrightarrow{\bf Z}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y}\\
&\text{~variable}&&q({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y}).\end{aligned}

(27)

We claim that this is equivalent to Wyner’s optimization problem (
1
).
Indeed, note that we have
I({\bf X},{\bf Y};{\bf Z})=I({\bf X};{\bf Z})+h({\bf Y}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf X})
under the Markov chain
{\bf X}\to{\bf Z}\to{\bf Y}
, since

\displaystyle I({\bf X},{\bf Y};{\bf Z})

\displaystyle=I({\bf X};{\bf Z})+I({\bf Y};{\bf Z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf X})

\displaystyle=I({\bf X};{\bf Z})+h({\bf Y}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf X})+h({\bf Y}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf X},{\bf Z})

\displaystyle=I({\bf X};{\bf Z})+h({\bf Y}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf X}).

Here,
h({\bf Y}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf X},{\bf Z})=0
follows from the Markov chain
{\bf X}\leftrightarrow{\bf Z}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y}
.
Since
h({\bf Y}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf X})
is constant given the target distribution
q({\bf x},{\bf y})
,
(
27
) is equivalent to (
1
).

A-D

Discussion

We remark that we can derive an optimization problem from (
27
) that is directly comparable to the IB principle (
25
).
By applying the same argument in Appendix
A-B
to (
27
), we can relax
{\bf X}\leftrightarrow{\bf Z}\leftrightarrow{\bf Y}
to
I({\bf Y};{\bf Z})\geq I({\bf X};{\bf Y})
and convert it into an unconstrained problem

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\minimize&&I({\bf X};{\bf Z})-\beta I({\bf Y};{\bf Z})\\
&\text{~variable}&&q({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y}).\end{aligned}

(28)

Interestingly, this has a close resemblance to the IB principle
25
.
In particular, (
28
) can be viewed as a relaxation of
25
, since optimizing over
q({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x},{\bf y})
subsumes optimizing over
q({\bf z}\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}{\bf x})
.

Proposition 7

(Monotonicity)
.

For a convex function
f\mathchoice{\colon}{\colon}{\mathchar 58\relax\mspace{1mu}}{\mathchar 58\relax}\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
, let
D_{f}(\cdot\|\cdot)
denote the
f
-divergence.
For any joint distributions
p_{1}(x,y)
and
p_{2}(x,y)
over
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, we have

D_{f}(p_{1}(x)\|p_{2}(x))\leq D_{f}(p_{1}(x,y)\|p_{2}(x,y)).

Here,
p_{i}(x)
is the marginal distribution over
\mathcal{X}
induced by
p_{i}(x,y)
.
In particular, the equality holds if
p_{2}(y\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}x)\equiv p_{1}(y\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}x)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
When
f
is strictly convex, the condition becomes also necessary for the equality.

Proof.

Consider

\displaystyle D_{f}(p_{1}(x,y)\|p_{2}(x,y))

\displaystyle=\int p_{1}(x,y)f\Bigl(\frac{p_{2}(x,y)}{p_{1}(x,y)}\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\int p_{1}(x)p_{1}(y\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}x)f\Bigl(\frac{p_{2}(x)p_{2}(y\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}x)}{p_{1}(x)p_{1}(y\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}x)}\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\geq\int p_{1}(x)f\Bigl(\int p_{1}(y\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}x)\frac{p_{2}(x)p_{2}(y\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}x)}{p_{1}(x)p_{1}(y\mathchoice{\mspace{1mu}\mspace{1mu}}{}{}{}x)}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int p_{1}(x)f\Bigl(\frac{p_{2}(x)}{p_{1}(x)}\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x=D_{f}(p_{1}(x)\|p_{2}(x)).

Here, we use the convexity of
f
and Jensen’s inequality. The equality condition follows from that of the Jensen’s inequality.
∎

Training

We used the Adam optimizer
[
73
]
with
(\beta_{1},\beta_{2})=(0.5,0.999)
, learning rate
10^{-4}
for the generators and
2\times 10^{-4}
for the discriminators
[
58
]
.
We trained for 50 epochs.

In the discriminators, we added a zero-mean Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.25 for each latent variable
{\bf z},{\bf u},{\bf v}
.
For the perturbation in the data variables
{\bf x},{\bf y}
, we did the following, adaptive noise injection based on the standard deviations of each feature dimension.

\bullet

For
{\bf x}
(images), we injected noise to the ResNet feature of dimension 2048. We precomputed the standard deviation
\sigma_{i}^{\textsf{resnet}}
for each dimension
i
.
For each evaluation of a discriminator that takes the ResNet feature as one of its arguments, we injected a zero-mean Gaussian noise of standard deviation
\alpha_{\textsf{resnet}}\times\sigma_{i}^{\textsf{resnet}}
to the dimension
i
, where we used
\alpha_{\textsf{resnet}}=2
.

\bullet

For
{\bf y}
(sentences), we injected noise at an embedding level.
We used an embedding layer that maps a word from the vocabulary of size 1590 to a 128-dimensional dense vectors.
Whenever we computed a discriminator value, we computed the standard deviation
\sigma_{i}^{\textsf{embed}}
of the embedding layer for each embedding dimension
i\in\{1,\ldots,128\}
; note here that the standard deviation changed along training as the embedding layer was set to be trainable.
Then, with a scale of
\alpha_{\textsf{sent}}=0.05
, we added a zero-mean Gaussian noise of standard deviation
\alpha_{\textsf{embed}}\times\sigma_{i}^{\textsf{embed}}
to the embedding dimension
i
.
