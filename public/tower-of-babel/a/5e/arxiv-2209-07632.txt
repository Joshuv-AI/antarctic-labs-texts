Fast hierarchical low-rank view factor matrices for thermal irradiance on planetary surfaces
Samuel F. Potter, Stefano Bertone, Norbert Schörghofer, Erwan Mazarico

Abstract
We present an algorithm for compressing the radiosity view factor model commonly used in radiation heat transfer and computer graphics. We use a format inspired by the hierarchical off-diagonal low rank format, where elements are recursively partitioned using a quadtree or octree and blocks are compressed using a sparse singular value decomposition—the hierarchical matrix is assembled using dynamic programming. The motivating application is time-dependent thermal modeling on vast planetary surfaces, with a focus on permanently shadowed craters which receive energy through indirect irradiance. In this setting, shape models are comprised of a large number of triangular facets which conform to a rough surface. At each time step, a quadratic number of triangle-to-triangle scattered fluxes must be summed; that is, as the sun moves through the sky, we must solve the same view factor system of equations for a potentially unlimited number of time-varying righthand sides. We first conduct numerical experiments with a synthetic spherical cap-shaped crater, where the equilibrium temperature is analytically available. We also test our implementation with triangle meshes of planetary surfaces derived from digital elevation models recovered by orbiting spacecrafts. Our results indicate that the compressed view factor matrix can be assembled in quadratic time, which is comparable to the time it takes to assemble the full view matrix itself. Memory requirements during assembly are reduced by a large factor. Finally, for a range of compression tolerances, the size of the compressed view factor matrix and the speed of the resulting matrix vector product both scale linearly (as opposed to quadratically for the full matrix), resulting in orders of magnitude savings in processing time and memory space.



1
Introduction

Illumination and temperature on the surfaces of airless bodies are dominated by radiative fluxes, from the Sun or from energy reflected or emitted from nearby topography. With the availability of high-resolution topographic maps of planetary surfaces, the modelling of radiative fluxes has become a major computational challenge. When a facet is irradiated by
N
other facets within a direct line of sight, the total flux (radiance) received is the sum of
N
fluxes. For example, Haworth Crater on the Moon (
86.9^{\circ}
S,
4^{\circ}
W) has a diameter of about 51 km and the laser altimeter-derived digital elevation model (DEM) currently available there has a horizontal resolution of 10 m/pixel. This means that fluxes from approximately
2\times 10^{7}
facets must be summed to determine the incoming flux at each point for the entire crater surface, thus requiring on the order of
10^{14}
FLOPs. This rough estimate includes only the first order of reflections and for a single position of the sun.

To dramatically reduce these computational costs, we present our implementation of a novel “fast” algorithm for this problem, with a focus on the rough surfaces of airless bodies such as planets and asteroids or features thereof. In computer graphics, global illumination algorithms which model light propagating in an environment with Lambertian surface reflectances go under the name “radiosity”; this is essentially the model we consider here. Numerous fast radiosity solvers have been proposed in the computer graphics literature, but the calculation of the incident energy on planetary surfaces differs from these applications in several respects.

A major difference between planetary surfaces and the 3D models which were the focus of early radiosity algorithms is that planetary surfaces are discretized into a large number of small polygonal facets conforming to a rough terrain; on the other hand, early algorithms in computer graphics focused on contouring discontinuities in the solution of the radiosity integral equation under the assumption that the domain was composed of a relatively small number of large polygonal facets (e.g., walls, floors, ceilings). Contrary to the approaches taken in computer graphics, we compute a compressed version of the view factor matrix by assuming that hierarchically generated blocks of the radiosity kernel matrix can be compressed using low rank matrix factorizations. The standard form of a discretized radiosity equation is

\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{E}+\diag(\boldsymbol{\rho})\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{B},

(1)

where
\boldsymbol{B},\boldsymbol{E},\boldsymbol{\rho}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
are vectors,
\boldsymbol{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
is a kernel matrix referred to as the “view factor matrix”, and
N
is the number of triangles. The rows and columns of
\boldsymbol{F}
correspond to individual triangular facets of a mesh conforming to a planetary surface, and
\boldsymbol{F}_{ij}
is an approximation of the view factor from the
j

i
th triangle. Our assumption is that for two sets of triangles, indexed by
\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}

\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
is “numerically low rank”. That is, the singular values of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
decay rapidly, and a low-rank approximation of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
is available via the singular value decomposition. This is a reasonable assumption, since the radiosity system is the result of discretizing a second kind boundary integral equation. For comparison, with boundary integral methods for solving elliptic PDEs, the hierarchical matrix format (
\mathcal{H}

[

The radiosity equation is purely geometric. Provided that the surface does not change between time steps, the view factor matrix does not need to be recomputed. In the long-running, high-resolution, time-dependent thermal simulations needed in planetary science, a large number of radiosity MVPs with varying righthand sides are required. This is the justification for our approach: we pay an up front cost which is amortized over a large number of steps to gain access to fast MVPs at each time step.

The scientific applications of such an implementation are manifold. Remotely sensed temperatures are used to infer the thermal properties of the ground and albedo measurements within shadowed regions are affected by scattered light. The Moon has permanently shadowed regions (PSRs), which receive only indirect sunlight and their temperatures are determined by terrain irradiance. These extraordinarily cold regions are thought to harbor water ice

, and are the targets of many upcoming landed and orbital missions to the Moon. The need to predict and quantify surface temperatures inside lunar PSRs is the main motivation behind the work presented here, but the same approach and implementation can be easily adapted to other planetary bodies and features.

Many of the currently available thermal models for planetary surfaces do not use fast algorithms for the evaluation of the terrain irradiance. Instead, they directly evaluate the scattered irradiance between each pair of facets, e.g.

“Radiosity” refers to a family of computer graphics algorithms for global illumination which solve the integral equation for Lambertian flux using the boundary element method

2
Physical description and governing equations

The surface energy balance on an airless body consists of direct solar irradiance, terrain irradiance, and subsurface heat flux.
The direct solar irradiance
Q_{\operatorname{direct}}
is also known as insolation (incoming solar radiation). Its calculation requires knowledge of occlusion due to topography, which can block direct sunlight (terrain shadowing).
Terrain irradiance consists of a short-wavelength contribution
Q_{\operatorname{refl}}
(sunlight reflected from land within view) and a long-wavelength contribution
Q_{\operatorname{IR}}
(thermal infrared emitted from land within the field of view). The latter is also known as “self-heating”.

On a surface
S
, the equation governing the energy balance is

(1-\alpha)(Q_{\operatorname{direct}}+Q_{\operatorname{refl}})+k\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\nabla{T}+\epsilon Q_{\operatorname{IR}}=\epsilon\sigma T^{4},

(2)

where
\alpha
is the albedo,
Q_{\operatorname{direct}}
is the direct incoming solar radiation,
k
is the thermal conductivity,
T
is the temperature,
\boldsymbol{n}
is the unit surface normal,
\epsilon
is the emissivity, and
\sigma
is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant.
In our work, we consider a “Lambert albedo”, meaning that it is independent of the angle of incidence.
We will also assume that the subsurface heat flux is one-dimensional, as vertical heat fluxes are typically much larger than lateral heat fluxes on the large surfaces which we consider. Without subsurface heat conduction, the surface energy balance is

(1-\alpha)(Q_{\operatorname{direct}}+Q_{\operatorname{refl}})+\epsilon Q_{\operatorname{IR}}=\epsilon\sigma T_{\operatorname{eq}}^{4},

(3)

where
T_{\operatorname{eq}}
is in instantaneous equilibrium with the incoming flux and therefore known as the “equilibrium temperature”.
The left-hand side is the absorbed flux.

On an airless body, if we denote the position of the Sun by
\boldsymbol{x}_{\odot}
, the insolation at a point
\boldsymbol{x}
is

Q_{\operatorname{direct}}=\frac{S_{\odot}}{R_{\odot}^{2}}\sin(e_{\odot})V(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{\odot}),

(4)

where
S_{\odot}
is the solar constant,
R_{\odot}
is the distance from the Sun in AU, and
e_{\odot}
is the elevation of the sun, defined to be the angle between the tangent plane at
\boldsymbol{x}
and the unit vector pointing from
\boldsymbol{x}
to
\boldsymbol{x}_{\odot}
. The function
V
is the line-of-sight visibility defined as

V(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $\boldsymbol{y}$ is line-of-sight visible from $\boldsymbol{x}$},\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(5)

When the Sun is treated as a disk of finite extent rather than a point source, the flux can be expressed as a sum over point sources, the contribution of which takes the form of (
4
).

The incoming radiances satisfy the two integral equations

\displaystyle Q_{\operatorname{refl}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{S}F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\alpha(\boldsymbol{y})\Big[Q_{\operatorname{direct}}(\boldsymbol{y})+Q_{\operatorname{refl}}(\boldsymbol{y})\Big]dA(\boldsymbol{y}),

(6)

\displaystyle Q_{\operatorname{IR}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{S}F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\Big[\epsilon(\boldsymbol{y})\sigma T(\boldsymbol{y})^{4}+(1-\epsilon(\boldsymbol{y}))Q_{\operatorname{IR}}(\boldsymbol{y})\Big]dA(\boldsymbol{y}),

(7)

where the view factor (sometimes also called the form factor)
F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
is given by

F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{\cos\big(\theta(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\big)\cos\big(\theta(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})\big)}{\pi|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}|^{2}}V(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}),

(8)

and where
\theta(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
is the angle between the unit surface normal at
\boldsymbol{x}
and the unit vector pointing from
\boldsymbol{x}
to
\boldsymbol{y}
. The visibility function
V
is defined by (
5
).

For comparison, the radiosity equation is the integral equation

B(\boldsymbol{x})=E(\boldsymbol{x})+\rho(\boldsymbol{x})\int_{S}F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})B(\boldsymbol{y})dA(\boldsymbol{y}),

(9)

where
B
is the radiosity (that is, the total radiant flux leaving
S
per unit area), and
E
is the directly emitted energy. This is the form used in computer graphics. The assumption of Lambertian reflectance implies
[
13
]

\alpha(\boldsymbol{x})Q(\boldsymbol{x})=B(\boldsymbol{x}),\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in S,

(10)

so that the integral equations given by (
6
) and (
7
) are simply related to (
9
). In terms of the reflected short-wavelength flux
Q_{\operatorname{vis}}=Q_{\operatorname{refl}}+Q_{\operatorname{direct}}
, and using (
2
) to substitute for temperature, we get

\displaystyle Q_{\operatorname{vis}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=Q_{\operatorname{direct}}(\boldsymbol{x})+\int_{S}F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\alpha(\boldsymbol{y})Q_{\operatorname{vis}}(\boldsymbol{y})dA(\boldsymbol{y}),

(11)

\displaystyle Q_{\operatorname{IR}}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{S}F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})\Big[\big(1-\alpha(\boldsymbol{y})\big)Q_{\operatorname{vis}}(\boldsymbol{y})+H(\boldsymbol{y})\Big]dA(\boldsymbol{y})+\int_{S}F(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})Q_{\operatorname{IR}}(\boldsymbol{y})dA(\boldsymbol{y}),

(12)

where
H=k\,\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\nabla T
is the heat flux from the subsurface.

To model the heat conduction, we use the heat equation

\rho c\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=\nabla\cdot(k\nabla T),

(13)

where
T
is the temperature,
\rho c
is the volumetric heat capacity and
k
is the thermal conductivity. If
\boldsymbol{n}
is the outward facing unit surface normal for
S
, then we can combine (
13
) with the radiation boundary condition

Q_{\operatorname{abs}}(\boldsymbol{x})+k\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{x})\cdot\nabla T(\boldsymbol{x})=\epsilon\sigma T(\boldsymbol{x})^{4},\qquad\boldsymbol{x}\in S,

(14)

where the absorbed flux is
Q_{\operatorname{abs}}=(1-\alpha)(Q_{\operatorname{direct}}+Q_{\operatorname{refl}})+\epsilon Q_{\operatorname{IR}}
. In a simulation, the runtime will be dominated by solving the system described in this section to be spent on the integral equations governing the flux. Our focus is thus to accelerate the solution of integral equations of the form (
9
).

3
Numerical discretization and occlusion

We assume that the surface
S
is available as a triangle mesh with an orientable boundary. We let
N
denote the number of facets in the mesh. We denote the area, centroid, and surface normal of the
i
th triangle by
A_{i}
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
, and
\boldsymbol{n}_{i}
, respectively. We approximate each surface integral by approximating functions defined on
S
with piecewise constant basis functions, integrating over the triangle mesh, and using collocation to solve the integral equations. For instance, to discretize (
6
), we approximate the integral over the triangle using the midpoint rule to get

Q_{\operatorname{refl}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}F(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{y}_{j})\alpha(\boldsymbol{y}_{j})\Big[Q_{\operatorname{direct}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{j})+Q_{\operatorname{refl}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{j})\Big]A_{j},\qquad 1\leq i\leq N.

(15)

This results in an
N\times N
system with matrix
\boldsymbol{F}
whose entries are given by

\boldsymbol{F}_{i,j}=F(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})A_{j}=\frac{\Big[\boldsymbol{n}_{i}\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\Big]\Big[\boldsymbol{n}_{j}\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j})\Big]}{\pi|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}|^{4}}V(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})A_{j}.

(16)

Altogether, we write the discretized version of (
6
) as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{refl}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{F}\diag(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\Big[\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{direct}}+\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{refl}}\Big],

(17)

and similarly for (
7
). Here,
\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
is the
N\times N
diagonal matrix whose
i
th diagonal entry is
\alpha(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
, and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{refl}}
(resp.,
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{direct}}
) is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
whose
i
th entry is
Q_{\operatorname{refl}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
(resp.,
Q_{\operatorname{direct}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
). Sophisticated methods for approximating view factors can be used instead of just the midpoint rule to compute the entries of
\boldsymbol{F}
more accurately. We use the midpoint rule for conceptual simplicity. The methods presented in this work are compatible with more accurate view factor approximations.

Equation

17
can easily be solved by using standard relaxation methods such as the Jacobi iteration. These methods will typically converge to machine precision in
O(1)
iterations—there is no need for preconditioning or more elaborate schemes, so this is what we do. The number of nonzero entries scales like
O(N^{2})
as the mesh is refined, although the visibility factor
V(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
results in a large number of zero entries. This is easy to see: consider two sets of triangles which are pairwise mutually visible, and assume that they account for
pN
of the total triangles, where
0<p<1
. If this triangle mesh is refined uniformly, and in such a manner that this set of triangles remains mutually visible, then there will always be at least
p^{2}N^{2}
entries of
\boldsymbol{F}
which are nonzero. Hence, even in a scene with a high degree of occlusion (such as on the surface of an asteroid), an iterative radiosity solver based on a direct discretization cannot be faster than
O(N^{2})
, thus motivating the need for an asymptotically faster matrix multiply.

3.1
Hierarchically compressing the view factor matrix

The matrices stemming from discretized boundary integral equations are frequently “hierarchically off-diagonal low-rank” (HODLR). The HODLR format and other similar formats such as the hierarchical semiseparable (HSS) format
[
22
]
and the
\mathcal{H}
-matrix format
[
23
]
have found widespread use in compressing the kernel matrices stemming from discretized integral equations and are useful in the design of “fast direct solvers”. The HODLR format is not applicable to radiosity kernel matrices because of its directionality. Instead, we use a rather unstructured hierarchical matrix format assembled using dynamic programming, which we generically refer to as an
\mathcal{H}
-matrix. We explain the dynamic programming algorithm used to assemble the compressed version of
\boldsymbol{F}
in
Section

4
.

We briefly describe our hierarchical matrix format. Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d=2,3
)
be a domain,
\partial\Omega
its boundary, and

\boldsymbol{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
a matrix of the form

\boldsymbol{K}_{ij}=K(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}),\qquad\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\in\partial\Omega,\qquad 1\leq i\leq N,\qquad 1\leq j\leq N.

(18)

Let
\mathcal{I}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{I}_{P}=[N]
and let
\mathcal{J}_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{J}_{Q}=[N]
define row and column index sets which partition
[N]
. Then, there are row and column permutation matrices
\boldsymbol{P}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}
such that

\boldsymbol{K}=\boldsymbol{P}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{K}_{\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{J}_{1}}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{K}_{\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{J}_{Q}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{K}_{\mathcal{I}_{P},\mathcal{J}_{1}}&\cdots&\boldsymbol{K}_{\mathcal{I}_{P},\mathcal{J}_{Q}}\end{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{Q}^{\top}.

(19)

These index vectors and permutations can be generated straightforwardly using spatial data structures such as quadtrees and octrees (see
Section

4.1
). We make fairly weak assumptions on the permuted subblocks of
\boldsymbol{K}
in
Equation

19
. By recursively structuring
\boldsymbol{K}
using a spatial data structure, we create opportunities for compressing subblocks using a combination of their sparsity and low rank compression. The off-diagonal blocks are
not
necessarily numerically low rank (which is instead the assumption for the HODLR format), we are still able to empirically obtain
O(N)
complexity in cases of pratical interest (see
Sections

7
and
8
).

3.2
Visibility and raytracing

A key component of assembling
\boldsymbol{F}
is evaluating the visibility function
V
for a pair of points
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
on the surface
S
. For a pair of points
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
on the surface
S
, we require a means of testing whether the open interval
(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})=\{(1-t)\boldsymbol{x}_{i}+t\boldsymbol{x}_{j}:0<t<1\}
intersects the volume at any point. If
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
(
i\neq j
) are two triangle centroids, we do this by shooting a ray from
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
to
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
and checking whether the ray intersects the
j
th triangle. The number of nonzero entries of the matrix
\boldsymbol{F}
equals the number of pairs of triangles for which
V(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})=1
. So, if we let
\boldsymbol{d}_{ij}=(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i})/|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}|
, we implement
V(x,y)
as:

V_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $\boldsymbol{n}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{d}_{ij}>0$ and $\boldsymbol{n}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{d}_{ji}>0$ and the ray shot from $\boldsymbol{x}_{i}$ hits triangle $j$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

(20)

If
\boldsymbol{n}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{d}_{ij}\leq 0
or
\boldsymbol{n}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{d}_{ji}\leq 0
, then there is no need to shoot a ray to test for occlusion, in which case the cost of evaluating
V(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
is
O(1)
—this is a fast and simple form of visibility culling, which can significantly reduce the number of rays that need to be traced for the types of meshes considered.

We experimented with two libraries which provide acceleration structures for visibility testing: CGAL
[
25
]
and Embree
[
26
]
. CGAL is a library of highly accurate computational geometry algorithms. It provides an axis-aligned bounding box (AABB) tree which allows very accurate raytracing in double precision. Embree is a more recent library which focuses on high-performance raytracing for computer graphics and rendering. It currently only provides a single-precision bounding volume hierarchy (BVH). In practice we find CGAL is about 3–5 times slower. On the other hand, Embree only supports single-precision floating point numbers, while CGAL can handle arbitrary precision arithmetic if necessary. We treat these libraries as black boxes and assume that if we build an acceleration data structure on a triangle mesh with
N
triangles, we can evaluate ray-triangle intersections in
O(\log N)
time. For our numerical tests, we use CGAL.

3.3
Computing the full view factor matrix

In order to compute subblocks of the view factor matrix
\boldsymbol{F}
, we use the following algorithm to assemble it in the compressed sparse row (CSR) format:

Algorithm 1
: Assemble the full view factor matrix in CSR format.

1.

Fix a subset of row indices
\mathcal{I}\subseteq[N]
and column indices
\mathcal{J}\subseteq[N]
.

2.

Iterate over each triangle
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
(
i\in\mathcal{I}
), and find the indices
j\in\mathcal{J}\backslash\{i\}
for which
\boldsymbol{n}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{d}_{ij}>0
and
\boldsymbol{n}_{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{d}_{ji}>0
. For each such index
j
, shoot a ray from
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
to
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
and test for occlusion.

3.

If the
i
th and
j
th triangles are mutually visible, emit the row index pointer, the column index, and the value of
F_{ij}
to incrementally construct the CSR matrix.

This algorithm reduces the number of ray-triangle intersections which need to be performed and only requires us to compute entries of
F_{ij}
which are nonzero.

4
Hierarchical low-rank radiosity

Several fast hierarchical summation algorithms for radiosity have been developed (see, e.g.,
[
15
,
16
]
). Our application differs in that we would like a fast
\boldsymbol{F}
multiply in the case where the underlying mesh consists of a large number of small, triangular facets which contour a rough surface. To this end, our method is inspired by more recent kernel-free fast algorithms based on numerical linear algebra. We assume that there exists some recursive permutation of the entries of
\boldsymbol{F}
which creates opportunities for low rank compression at each level of the recursion. Because of the directionality and sparsity of the kernel, we must continue to refine off-diagonal blocks, unlike more familiar formats (see
Section

3.1
). Despite having a large, constant percentage of zeros, the number of nonzero entries of
\boldsymbol{F}
scales like
O(N^{2})
. After
\boldsymbol{F}
is approximated using this hierarchical format, we can finally think of it as being “data sparse”
[
1
]
.

4.1
Spatial data structures: quadtrees and octrees

Finding the desired permutation of the entries of
\boldsymbol{F}
is straightforward. They can be generated using a spatial data structure such as a quadtree or octree. To explain how this is done, we consider the scattered flux inside an ideal large crater, whose depth is given as the graph of a function:
z=z(x,y)
, so that each triangle centroid has the form
p_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i},z(x_{i},y_{i}))
. A quadtree is a tree whose internal nodes correspond to a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and a partition thereof into at most four point sets, and whose leaf nodes correspond to point sets in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Each internal node contains all of the points in each of its descendant nodes. Quadtrees differ in how they are constructed, for instance in the partitioning scheme used and in how the recursion is terminated. We use a quadtree constructed as follows:

Algorithm 2
: Build quadtree on triangle mesh.

1.

Initially set the roots of the tree to be the centroids of the triangle mesh.

2.

Let
x_{\min}=\min_{1\leq i\leq N}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
be the minimum
x
-coordinate of each centroid, and let
x_{\max},y_{\min}
, and
y_{\max}
be defined similarly.

3.

Let
x_{\operatorname{mid}}=(\boldsymbol{x}_{\min}+\boldsymbol{x}_{\max})/2
and likewise define
y_{\operatorname{mid}}
.

4.

Partition the triangle centroids associated with the current node into four sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\{i:\boldsymbol{x}_{i}<x_{\min}\mbox{ and }y_{i}<y_{\min}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{i:\boldsymbol{x}_{i}<x_{\min}\mbox{ and }y_{i}\geq y_{\min}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{3}

\displaystyle=\left\{i:\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\geq x_{\min}\mbox{ and }y_{i}<y_{\min}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{4}

\displaystyle=\left\{i:\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\geq x_{\min}\mbox{ and }y_{i}\geq y_{\min}\right\}.

5.

For each index set
\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2},\mathcal{I}_{3}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{4}
, if the index set is nonempty, construct an internal node and recursively partition the centroids, starting from Step 2.

If
\mathcal{I}
is the index set for a particular internal node, and
\mathcal{J}
is the index set of one of its children, then
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
. An octree is the analogous spatial data structure in 3D—the main difference is that each internal node has at most 8 child nodes instead of 4. Octrees are useful for topography with overhangs, or for small bodies, such as asteroids and comets.

4.2
Low-rank off-diagonal blocks

Let
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
be two disjoint sets of triangle indices, and let
\varepsilon>0
be a compression tolerance. We now describe a straightforward algorithm which attempts to compute a low-rank approximation of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
, the corresponding subblock of
\boldsymbol{F}
:

Algorithm 3
: Estimate numerical rank of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
.

1.

Use the algorithm of
Section

3.3
to compute
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
in CSR format.

2.

Let
k
be an initial guess for the numerical rank of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
(e.g.,
k\leftarrow 8
).

3.

Compute the
k
-term truncated sparse SVD
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}=\boldsymbol{U}_{k}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{k}\boldsymbol{V}_{k}^{\top}
from the CSR matrix.

4.

Denote the
i
th singular value of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
by
\sigma_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq k
).

5.

Let
q
be the smallest integer such that
\sigma_{q+1}/\sigma_{1}<\varepsilon
, if it exists. If no such
q
exists, continue to the next step. Otherwise, check whether:

\mathtt{nbytes}(\boldsymbol{U}_{q})+\mathtt{nbytes}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{q})+\mathtt{nbytes}(\boldsymbol{V}_{q})<\mathtt{nbytes}(\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}).

(21)

If so, then the truncated SVD that we have found is a low-rank approximation of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
; otherwise, we signal that we have failed to find one.
Note
: here, we look at that size of the
q
-truncated singular vectors. It is also critically important to store
\boldsymbol{U}_{q}
and
\boldsymbol{V}_{q}
in the CSR format, since they will inherit a significant number of nonzeros from
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
.

6.

Increase
k
(e.g., by setting
k\leftarrow 2k
), and jump back to Step 3.

In general, we expect to be able to compute a
k
-term truncated SVD of an
m\times n
matrix in roughly
O(kmn)
time. This means that Algorithm
4.2
computes the
k
-term truncated SVD with the optimal number of terms in
O(kmn\log k)
time. We compute SVDs using ARPACK
[
28
]
.

In Algorithm
4.2
, the SVD is “compatible” with
\boldsymbol{F}
in the following sense. Let’s say that
\boldsymbol{F}
is zero except for a subset of its entries indexed by two (possibly overlapping) index sets
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
. Then the maximum rank of
\boldsymbol{F}
is bounded by
\max(|\mathcal{I}|,|\mathcal{J}|)
, where
|\cdot|
is the cardinality of a set. (This can easily happen: consider a triangle mesh which is the discretization of a sphere with outward-facing surface normal; e.g., a perfectly spherical planet. In this case,
\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{J}=\{\}
and
\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{0}
.) The SVD will locate the index sets
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
automatically and the left and right singular vectors will be correspondingly sparse. That is, there is no artificial fill-in hampering the likelihood of (
21
) to take effect.

Since the left and right singular matrices
\boldsymbol{U}
and
\boldsymbol{V}
are orthogonal matrices, they will nearly always have negative entries. Numerical errors then easily result in small negative fluxes when using our approximate view factor matrix. In practice we set any negative flux to zero.

We briefly comment that accelerating the assembly of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
using something like adaptive cross approximation
[
29
]
or the interpolative decomposition
[
1
]
may not be effective. Both methods assume that the spectrum of the matrix being compressed decays rapidly. This is not the case for the view factor matrix. This is not to say that it is impossible to compute a low rank approximation of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
in subquadratic time, but our belief is that doing so will involve exploiting more detailed knowledge of the radiosiy kernel. We leave this for future work.

4.3
Computing the hierarchically compressed view factor matrix

Using Algorithms
3.3
,
4.1
, and
4.2
, we can assemble the full, hierarchically compressed approximation of
\boldsymbol{F}
. With the compression tolerance
\varepsilon>0
fixed, we proceed as follows:

Algorithm 4
: Assemble hierarchically compressed block
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
.

Input:
The index sets
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
and a tolerance
\varepsilon>0
. Optionally, the CSR version of the block
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
.

Output:
A hierarchically compressed version of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
.

1.

Compute the block
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
using Algorithm
3.3
if it was not supplied.

2.

If
|\mathcal{I}|\cdot|\mathcal{J}|
is less than a user defined threshold, set the current block to be either a sparse CSR or dense version of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
depending on which uses fewer bytes.

3.

Otherwise, estimate the numerical rank
q
of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
using Algorithm
4.2
and compute the corresponding
q
-truncated sparse SVD.

4.

Recursively use this algorithm to hierarchically compress the subblocks of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
, taking care to pass along the corresponding subblock of the CSR version of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
.

5.

Compare the size of the sparse, dense, and truncated SVD version of
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
with the result of applying this algorithm recursively to each of its subblocks. Use the version of this block which requires the fewest number of bytes to be stored.

6.

Finally, if all of the blocks of this hierarchical matrix are sparse (or if all of them are dense), coalesce them into a single dense (or sparse) and set it to be the current block; otherwise, set the current block to be the hierarchical matrix.

To compute
\boldsymbol{F}
itself, we apply Algorithm
4.3
to blocks at the first level of the spatial tree below the root (e.g., at most 16 pairs of index sets at the starting level of a quadtree, and 64 for an octree).

Getting an exact bound on the complexity of this algorithm is difficult, but in practice we observe Algorithm
4.3
to take a similar amount of time as it would take to compute the entire view factor matrix in the CSR format. When we run Algorithm
4.3
, we do not ever need to compute the entire CSR view factor matrix at once, only subblocks of it. For example, starting from the first level of a quadtree, where there are at most four blocks, we can work with the (at most)
4^{2}=16
subblocks of the full view factor matrix one at a time. This signficantly reduces the amount of runtime memory use, which likely also has a positive impact on CPU runtime.

4.4
Multiplying with the hierarchically compressed view factor matrix

Let
\boldsymbol{X}
be a matrix with
N
rows, and let
\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathcal{I}}
be a matrix formed from a subset of its rows indexed by
\mathcal{I}
. After assembling the hierarchically compressed view factor matrix using Algorithm
4.3
, we can easily compute the product
\boldsymbol{Y}=\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{X}
using the following algorithm:

Algorithm 5
: Compute matrix-matrix product with hierarchically compressed
\boldsymbol{F}
.

1.

Initially set
\boldsymbol{Y}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{0}
.

2.

Set the current node to the root of the quadtree (or octree).

3.

For each pair of index sets
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
amongst
\mathcal{I}_{1},\mathcal{I}_{2},\mathcal{I}_{3}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{4}
:

(a)

If
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
is a CSR matrix, a dense matrix, or a truncated SVD, compute
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathcal{J}}
in the obvious way.

(b)

Otherwise,
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}
is a block matrix. Recursively apply this algorithm to compute
\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathcal{J}}
.

(c)

Set
\boldsymbol{Y}_{\mathcal{I}}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{Y}_{\mathcal{I}}+\boldsymbol{F}_{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{J}}\boldsymbol{X}_{\mathcal{J}}
.

To compute a matrix-vector product, we can take
\boldsymbol{X}
to be a matrix with a single column.

HODLR-compressed matrices’ MVPs have a time complexity of
O(N\log N)
. Because of the more complicated structure of the view factor matrix, it is not clear whether we can expect an asymptotically improved MVP in the general case, since it depends delicately on
\partial\Omega
. That said, we can expect it to do no worse than multiplying with the uncompressed view factor matrix because of the way we construct
\boldsymbol{F}
in Algorithm
4.3
. For a reasonable choice of parameters, we observe
O(N)
scaling—see
Sections

7
and
8
.

5
Time integration

5.1
Equilibrium temperatures

To solve the time-independent problem, for a particular Sun position, we compute
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{direct}}
by checking whether the Sun is visible from a centroid
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
using raytracing and computing
Q_{\operatorname{direct}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
from (
4
). We then rearrange Eq. (
17
) to get

\Big(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{F}\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\Big)\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{refl}}=\boldsymbol{F}\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{direct}}.,

(22)

which can be solved in a small number of iterations using a simple Jacobi iteration. Discretizing (
12
) gives:

\Big(\boldsymbol{I}-\boldsymbol{F}\Big)\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{IR}}=\boldsymbol{F}\big(\boldsymbol{I}-\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\big)\big(\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{direct}}+\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{refl}}\big)+\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{H}.

(23)

This system can be solved as before, yielding
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{IR}}
. Finally, we can compute
\boldsymbol{T}
from Eq. (
3
). Equations (
22
) and (
23
) are both of the form Eq. (
1
), with
\boldsymbol{\rho}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}
for
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{refl}}
and
\boldsymbol{\rho}=\boldsymbol{1}
for
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{IR}}
.

5.2
Time integration with subsurface heat storage

To solve the time-dependent problem, we only consider 1D temperature profiles beneath the centroid of each triangle. For each centroid
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
, we let
z_{j}>0
be the depth beneath the surface in the direction
\boldsymbol{n}_{i}
(i.e., the point
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-z_{j}\boldsymbol{n}_{i}
), and let
M
denote the number of layers, so that
1\leq j\leq M
. We discretize (
13
) in 1D with this set of grid points in a flux-conservative manner and use a Crank-Nicolson method with a linearized upper boundary condition. Details are described in
[
30
]
.
We obtain a boundary condition for the surface from (
14
). For a boundary condition in the interior, we prescribe a fixed heat flux
\boldsymbol{H}
, which can be zero. We typically choose
M
fairly small (
M
is typically
20
to
30
), independently of
N
(the number of triangle faces). The cost of driving each subsurface thermal model at each step is then
O(NM)
overall, since the time step only involves solving an
M\times M
tridiagonal system.

To solve the time-dependent problem, we could take an approach similar to the one presented in
section

5.1
, but we consider a more cost-effective alternative, reducing the cost of each time step while maintaining accuracy. Subsurface heat conductance introduces a history dependence in the problem. In this case, there is little incentive to solve the systems describing
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{refl}}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{IR}}
at each time step. Multiplying with
\boldsymbol{F}
once corresponds to computing one order of scattering. If we multiply once at each time step, many orders of reflections are taken into account, with the higher order reflections lagging behind in time. We use the following time-stepping scheme for the time-dependent problem:

Algorithm 6
: Run time-dependent simulation.

Let
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{abs}}^{(n)}
,
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{refl}}^{(n)}
,
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{IR}}^{(n)}
, and
\boldsymbol{T}^{(n)}
be vectors giving the values of
Q_{\operatorname{abs}}
,
Q_{\operatorname{refl}}
,
Q_{\operatorname{IR}}
, and
T
over each triangle at the
n
th time step. To ease notation, we let
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{rad}}^{(n)}
be defined by:

Q_{\operatorname{rad}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})=\epsilon(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\sigma T(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})^{4},\qquad 1\leq i\leq N.

(24)

For
n=0
, we initialize:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{abs}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\big(\boldsymbol{I}-\diag(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\big)\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{direct}}^{(0)}+\boldsymbol{H}^{(0)},

(25)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{refl}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},

(26)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{IR}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},

(27)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{rad}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{abs}}^{(0)}.

(28)

Then, at each time step, we update the fluxes by:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{refl}}^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{F}\operatorname{diag}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\Big[\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{direct}}^{(n+1)}+\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{refl}}^{(n)}\Big],

(29)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{IR}}^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{F}\Big[\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{rad}}^{(n)}+\big(\boldsymbol{I}-\diag(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\big)\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{IR}}^{(n)}\Big],

(30)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{abs}}^{(n+1)}

\displaystyle=\big(\boldsymbol{I}-\diag(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\big)(\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{direct}}^{(n+1)}+\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{refl}}^{(n+1)})+\diag(\boldsymbol{\epsilon})\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{IR}}^{(n+1)}.

(31)

We then update the subsurface temperature profiles by stepping each Crank-Nicolson scheme, with
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{abs}}^{(n)}
and
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{abs}}^{(n+1)}
as input. This yields the surface temperature
\boldsymbol{T}^{(n+1)}
, and hence
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\operatorname{rad}}^{(n+1)}
.

6
Implementation

In this work, we present a prototype implementation which proves the concept—in a future work, we will more carefully address its optimization.
The algorithms described here are available as a Python package:

https://github.com/sampotter/python-flux

Extensive use is made of numpy
[
31
]
and scipy
[
32
]
. In particular, we nearly always store full view factor matrix blocks as instances of the
scipy.sparse.csr_matrix
class. To compute truncated SVDs, we use
scipy.sparse.linalg.svds
, which is a wrapper around ARPACK
[
28
]
. The combination of ARPACK and
csr_matrix
allows us to compute truncated SVDs of view factor matrix blocks in time proportional to the number of nonzero entries, which is critically important for the efficiency of our implementation. Since a large amount of numpy is parallelized “behind the scenes”, our implementation benefits from this, even though we do not focus on parallelism in this work.

7.1
Analytical solution for a bowl-shaped crater

We validate our implementation against the exact solution for the temperature, which is available in the ideal case of a spherical cap.
In this case, the equilibrium temperature in the shadow is given by
[
34
,
35
]

\sigma T_{\operatorname{eq}}^{4}=S_{\odot}\sin e_{\odot}\,\frac{f(1-\alpha)}{1-\alpha f}\left[1+\frac{\alpha(1-f)}{\epsilon}\right],

(32)

where
\alpha
is the albedo,
S_{\odot}
is the solar constant,
e_{\odot}
the elevation of the Sun relative to the horizontal, and
\epsilon
the infrared emissivity. These parameters match
Section

2
. The geometric parameter
f
is given by the diameter-to-depth ratio,
D/d
, of the crater:

\frac{1}{f}=1+\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{D}{d}\right)^{2}

(33)

The temperature in each part of the domain is given by the indirect irradiance, which is constant within the spherical portion, plus the direct irradiance, such that

\epsilon\sigma T_{\operatorname{eq}}^{4}=(1-\alpha)S_{\odot}\times\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sin e_{\odot}&\mbox{sunlit, outside of crater}\\
\sin e+b\sin e_{\odot}&\mbox{sunlit, in crater}\\
b\sin e_{\odot}&\mbox{shadowed, in crater}\\
\end{array}\right.,

(34)

where
e
is the elevation of the Sun relative to the local surface, and

b=f\frac{\epsilon+\alpha(1-f)}{1-\alpha f}.

(35)

See
Figure

1(a)
for a diagram depicting these parameters.

7.2
View factor computation

In this work, our goal is to integrate triangle-to-triangle view factors. Although we use a simple midpoint rule for quadrature, we mention that there are a number of ways of doing so analytically—e.g.,
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
. Combining analytic integration of triangle-to-triangle view factors in the near-field with our currect approach is likely to provide a very reasonable scheme. We emphasize that Algorithm
4.3
remains unchanged if other methods of computing view factors are substituted. Such a change would result in different (more accurate) view factor matrix entries, but that is all. We leave exploration of this issue for future work.

7.3
Crater and shadow contouring

The solution of the system described in
Section

2
is piecewise continuous on
S
. For the spherical cap-shaped crater considered in this section, there will be a jump discontinuity in
T_{\operatorname{eq}}
across the boundaries separating the three regions of
S
. It is important to assess the effect of this discontinuity on the accuracy of the equilibrium temperature,
T_{\operatorname{eq}}
. To this end, we consider two discretizations of the crater: one where the triangle mesh itself contours the rim of the crater and the shadow exactly, and one where only the crater rim is contoured.

For a given
y_{s}
(
-r_{c}\leq y_{s}\leq r_{c}
), a silhouette point
(x_{s},y_{s})
satisfies:

\sqrt{r^{2}-x_{s}^{2}-y_{s}^{2}}+\tan(e_{0})\left(\sqrt{r_{c}^{2}-y_{s}^{2}}-x_{s}\right)=H.

(36)

For a point strictly in the interior of the crater (
x_{s}^{2}+y_{s}^{2}<r_{c}^{2}
), there are two values of
x_{s}
that satisfy this equation: one corresponding to the silhouette line, and another which is on the crater rim which we denote by
x_{c}
. As
x_{s}^{2}+y_{s}^{2}\to r_{c}^{2}
, these two solutions coalesce. To find
x_{s}
, we use a rootfinder. This breaks down near the crater rim; in this case, we extrapolate
x_{s}
from nearby
(y_{s},x_{s}(y_{s}))

y

y_{p}>0

A realistic application in planetary science: de Gerlache crater

To test our numerical method on a more realistic problem, we run a time-dependent simulation of de Gerlache crater at the lunar South Pole (
88.5^{\circ}
S,
87.1^{\circ}
W). This crater hosts a large PSR which acts as a cold trap for volatiles
[
12
]
. Small exposures of surface ice were observed from hyperspectral reflectance data
[
40
]
. Parts of its rim are highly illuminated
[
3
]
, making it a potential site of interest for future robotic and human exploration. The de Gerlache crater has a complex morphology, of interest to test real-world applications of our algorithm. Part of its irregular floor is at a lower elevation which creates strong variations in the temperature field of the PSR.

To mesh de Gerlache, we use a recent NASA Digital Elevation Model (DEM)
[

Algorithm
4.3
hierarchically compresses
\boldsymbol{F}
, recursively moving from one level of a spatial tree to the next until a user-defined block-size or maximum depth is reached. This implicitely defines the total number of leaf nodes. A large number of small blocks results in a smaller size and a reduced the number of FLOPs in the resultant MVP (see algorithm
4.4
). On the other hand, they also increase the assembly time and the complexity of the hierarchical matrix, which impacts the MVP efficiency in our implementation. This results in a fair amount of time spent passing data back and forth between Python and the lower-level libraries (such as BLAS) which implement the MVPs for individual blocks. In this section, we consider the effect of using a fixed level of refinement in the spatial tree to control the number of small blocks which are generated.

On an 8-core machine, we collect timing and memory measurements for the compressed view factor matrices for de Gerlache crater. We also collect timings for computing surface temperatures. Figure 5 shows that the time for each MVP is roughly proportional to
O(N)
in a realistic application, compared to
O(N^{2})
if we use the uncompressed view factor matrix. We plot the blocks of
\boldsymbol{F}
for varying maximum inner areas (

While the size of
\boldsymbol{F}
mainly depends on the chosen tolerance value, the speed of the MVP also strongly depends on the chosen maximum depth of the quadtree. The deeper the tree, the closer the MVP will come to scaling asymptotically like
O(N\log N)
, although the asymptotic prefactor will naturally be larger. The latter is a shortcoming of our current Python implementation, rather than an intrinsic limitation of our approach, and will be addressed in future works. When choosing an appropriate maximum tree depth for the problem at hand, our approach significantly outperforms the uncompressed form factor matrix. Since modeling scattered light and temperature on planetary surfaces involves a large number of MVPs (depending on the time span being analyzed and on the desired time resolution), order of magnitude gains for such simulations are potentially game-changing.

10
Conclusion and future work

Large-scale thermal modeling on the surface of rough airless planetary bodies environment is a computationally demanding problem.
The number of element-to-element fluxes to calculate scales quadratically, and time-dependent calculations are often desired for numerous parameter combinations (scenarios).
Applying the radiosity method naïvely at each time step of a long-running simulation limits the size of problems which can be approached.

Borrowing ideas from the literature on hierarchical matrix factorizations and
n
-body codes, we present an algorithm for compressing the view factor matrix coming about as a result of discretizing the radiosity integral equation. It is not clear a priori whether this should be possible, due to the highly directional and sparse radiosity kernel. While the standard HODLR format fails to effectively compress the view factor matrix, a simple modification of it, which allows for off-diagonal blocks to be explored more deeply, succeeds. Unlike the HODLR format, this no longer guarantees
O(N\log N)
time and space scaling—nevertheless, we observe close to linear scaling in our numerical experiments over a range of tolerances.

We demonstrate empirically that the compressed view factor matrix can be constructed in close to the amount of time it takes to assemble the original uncompressed version by using dynamic programming. Since the geometry (landscape) is static, once the view factor matrix has been assembled, it can be applied repeatedly. We find that for a reasonable set of tolerances, the time complexity scales nearly linearly in our numerical experiments. Additionally, only
O(N)
space is required to store it in memory. In our numerical experiments, we find that this leads to orders of magnitude time and space savings. In these tests, we presented realistic examples showing how planetary scientists might use this method for modeling.

The algorithms here are presented as a prototype Python library. For speed, a future rewrite of the numerical kernel using C++ would be beneficial. The compressed view factor matrix is then the basis for (quicker) repeated computations of the scattered flux to get illumination and/or thermal conditions of the modeled surface at multiple epochs or under different thermophysical property assumptions.
Moreover, new missions and studies allow for ever larger, more detailed shape models of planetary surfaces. Such models are often too large for a typical workstation, and clusters have become a necessity for this kind of application. With our algorithm, one could first assemble—on a cluster—a significantly compressed version of a view factor matrix, but perform subsequent computations interactively on a laptop or workstation, thereby simplifying the model usability and workflow.

There are interesting directions which could be investigated as follow-on projects to the current work. Potentially, it would be possible to achieve a view factor MVP which is faster than
O(N^{2})
without an
O(N^{2})
precompute phase, which would result in concomitant improvements to the time complexity of Algorithm
4.3
. Developing fast direct solvers for our compressed view factor matrix is also of interest. These are algorithms in which a hierarchically compressed version of the inverse of a hierarchically compressed matrix is constructed. Although each solve already runs in
O(N)
time due the radiosity system being extremely well-conditioned, a fast direct solver for this problem might allow one to carry out each time step even faster.
