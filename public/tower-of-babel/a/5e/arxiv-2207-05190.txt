Another Look at the Problem of Many-Normal-Means
Chuanhai Liu

Abstract
Inferring the means in the multivariate normal model X ∼ N n ​ ( θ , I ) X\sim N_{n}(\theta,I) with unknown mean vector θ = ( θ 1 , … , θ n ) ′ ∈ ℝ n \theta=(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n})^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{n} and observed data X = ( X 1 , … , X n ) ′ ∈ ℝ n X=(X_{1},...,X_{n})^{\prime}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n} is a challenging task, known as the problem of many normal means (MNMs). This paper tackles two fundamental kinds of MNMs within the framework of Inferential Models (IMs). The first kind, referred to as the classic kind, is presented as is. The second kind, referred to as the empirical Bayes kind, assumes that the individual means θ i \theta_{i} ’s are drawn independently a priori from an unknown distribution G ( . ) G(.) . The IM formulation for the empirical Bayes kind utilizes numerical deconvolution, enabling prior-free probabilistic inference with over-parameterization for G ( . ) G(.) . The IM formulation for the classic kind, on the other hand, utilizes a latent random permutation, providing a novel approach for reasoning with uncertainty and deeper understanding. For uncertainty quantification within the familiar frequentist inference framework, the IM method of maximum plausibility is used for point estimation. Conservative interval estimation is obtained based on plausibility, using a Monte Carlo-based adaptive adjustment approach to construct shorter confidence intervals with targeted coverage. These methods are demonstrated through simulation studies and a real-data example. The numerical results show that the proposed methods for point estimation outperform traditional James-Stein and Efron’s g g -modeling in terms of mean square error, and the adaptive intervals are satisfactory in both coverage and efficiency. The paper concludes with suggestions for future developments and extensions of the proposed methods. Key Words : Empirical Bayes, Inferential Models, Latent permutation, Numerical deconvolution, Stein’s paradox.



1
Introduction

The problem of many normal means (MNMs) concerns simultaneous inference
about a collection of unknown normal means.
More precisely, we have the observed data
X_{1},...,X_{n}
that are
assumed to have the sampling model

X_{i}=\theta_{i}+Z_{i}\qquad(Z_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}N(0,1);\;i=1,...,n)

(1.1)

with unknown
\theta_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}
for
i=1,...,n.

This problem has proven to be fundamental in statistics,.
The most noticeable contribution dates back to
C. M. Stein

(
Stein 1956
)
,
on the remarkable discovery that
the usual maximum likelihood estimates
\hat{\theta}_{i}=X_{i}

are inadmissible under quadratic loss for
n\geq 3
.
Understanding this problem and developing methods to solve this
problem have resulted in successful research on multiparameter or
high-dimensional problems, including, for example,
the James-Stein shrinkage method
(
James and
Stein 1961
)
,
the empirical Bayes method
(
Efron and
Morris 1972
)
,
the wavelet shrinkage method
(
Donoho and
Johnstone 1995
)

for nonparametric regression and
LASSO
(
Tibshirani 1996
)
for linear regression.
The literature on MNMs itself and methods built up on shrinkage is massive, and thereby it is impossible to provide a complete review here in this note.

We refer to the problem setting described above as the
first or classic
kind,
reflecting Stein’s discovery.
It is perhaps somewhat surprising that the understanding
of the Stein phenomenon has not been very satisfactory, at least not
to a level that would help develop efficient methods based on sound reasoning
with uncertainty, as S. M. Stigler
Stigler 1990
wrote:

The best heuristic explanation that has been offered is a Bayesian argument…
Another explanation that has been offered is that
[the James-Stein estimator]
can be viewed as a relative of a “pre-test” estimator …
But neither of these explanations is fully satisfactory (although
both help render the result more plausible);
the first because it requires special a priori
assumptions where Stein did not, the second because it corresponds
to the result only in the loosest qualitative way. The difficulty
of understanding the Stein paradox is compounded by the fact that
its proof usually depends on explicit computation of the risk function
or the theory of complete sufficient statistics, by a process that
convinces us of its truth without really illuminating the reasons
that it works.

Attributing back to Francis Galton’s knowledge in the 1880s and viewing Stein’s estimation as an inverse regression problem
E(\theta|X)
, Stigler

Stigler 1990
provided a nice interpretation of
the James-Stein (shrinking toward zero) and Efron-Morris
(shrinking toward the sample mean
\bar{X}
) estimators of

\theta
.
However, the fundamental problem of this classic kind has remained unsolved,
due to the difficulty recognized by Stigler

Stigler 1990
:

With no distributional assumptions about the
\theta
’s,
we are of course prevented from looking for an optimal
estimate of “
E(\theta|X)
”.

Our innovative formulation in Section
4

by introducing a latent permutation in addition to unobserved
Z
’s in
(
1.1
)
leads to an inferentially valid
E(\theta|X)

and thus offers a new understanding,
shedding light on this challenging problem from the perspective of Inferential Models (IMs).
The IM framework, which is briefly reviewed in Section 2, provides frequency-calibrated or valid uncertainty quantification.

A popular variant of the classic kind is obtained by modeling
unknown
\theta_{i}
’ s as an unobserved sample drawn
a priori
from a non-parametric
distribution
\mathbb{G}
with the cumulative distribution function (cdf)
G(.)
. In this case, marginalizing out the unobservable

\theta_{i}
’s leads to inference about the mixture distribution

F_{G}(x)=\int\phi(x-\theta)dG(\theta)

(1.2)

from the observed
X
’s. Note that in this case,
X
’s is necessarily a
sample taken from the convoluted distribution
F_{G}(x)
.
Then, inference about
\theta
’s is made
based on their conditional distribution given
X_{i}
’s and an estimate of
G
.
Therefore, the central problem in this case is to estimate
G(.)
.
There is considerable literature on this deconvolution problem,
as in
Laird 1978
,

Carroll and
Hall 1988
,

Stefanski and
Carroll 1990
,

Fan 1991
,

Zhang 1997
,

Hall and
Meister 2007
,

Butucea and
Comte 2009
,
and

Efron 2014
;
Efron 2016
.
For convenience,
we refer to inference via this nonparametric partial Bayes model
as the
second or (nonparametric) empirical Bayes
kind, in contrast to the classic kind.

We examine the two kinds of the MNM problem within the IM framework
in Sections
3
and
4
.
The development of IMs for the empirical Bayes kind
appears relatively straightforward because of its nice
independence structure.
However, following the idea of discretization in
Efron 2014
;
Efron 2016

but not going further for
g
-modeling, our approach is to make
inference via over-parameterization or numerical deconvolution without
the need to impose more identifiability restrictions on
\mathbb{G}
.
It should be noted that, unlike traditional frequentist and Bayesian
methods, IMs can provide valid inference
even if the unknown parameters for
\mathbb{G}
are unidentifiable. For the classic kind, we introduce a sorted standard normal sample
together with a random permutation in place of the sample
Z_{1},...,Z_{n}
in (
1.1
).
This novel IM approach leads to prior-free probabilistic inference, for example, by making use of
the quantity
E(\theta|X)
for point estimation.
As a result, it sheds new light on a satisfactory understanding of the Stein
estimation.

Technically, predictive random sets are used for IM inference. They are constructed to provide
probabilistic inference in terms of belief and plausibility.
It is well known that valid probabilistic inference says more
than but can be used for point and interval estimations.
As a method for point estimation,
the maximum plausibility estimation is discussed in detail.
For interval estimation in the more familiar frequentist inference framework,
exact but conservative interval estimation is obtained based on plausibility.
A Monte Carlo approach is proposed as an adaptive adjustment method to
create shorter confidence intervals with targeted coverage.
This adaptive method is so straightforward in the context of
frequency calibration that it is presented simply as a technical
remark; see Remark
3.4
.
This is illustrated in Section
5.2
for a real-data example.

To evaluate the performance of the proposed methods,
a simulation study is conducted for three representative cases in
Section
5
with the computational methods provided
in the appendices.
The numerical results demonstrate that the proposed methods outperform traditional James-Stein and Efron’s g-modeling
(
Narasimhan and
Efron 2020
)
,
and are comparable to the auto-modeling method proposed recently in
Jiang and
Liu 2022
.
Section
6

concludes the paper with a few remarks,
including a brief discussion of other kinds of the MNM problem,
future potential developments, and extensions.

2
IMs: a brief review

IM is an inferential framework for prior-free probabilistic inference that was first proposed in
Martin and
Liu 2013
. It is based on the ideas of the pivotal method, Fisher’s fiducial argument, and the Dempster-Shafer theory of belief functions. As such, it can also be seen as a unification of the pivotal and fiducial methods.

More specifically, IM uses the so-called unobserved but predictable auxiliary random variables, or simply,
auxiliary variables
U
that have a known distribution

{\mathbf{P}}_{U}
on
{\mathbb{U}}
.
The setup of IMs is a system of equations, called association, representing
a statistical model with the unknown parameter
\theta\in\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{p}
for the observed data
X\in{\mathbb{X}}
through auxiliary variables
U
.
For the sake of clarity, we write this association as follows.

X=a(U,\theta)\qquad(X\in{\mathbb{X}},\theta\in\Theta,U\sim{\mathbf{P}}_{U})

(2.1)

IM inference is based on a so-called valid or frequency-calibrated predictive random set
(PRS)
\mathcal{S}
to predict the unobserved
U
to
produce valid uncertainty quantifications on unknown quantities of interest.
Formally, a PRS
\mathcal{S}
is said to be valid for
U
if
it is independent of
U
and

\mbox{Prob}(\mbox{Prob}(\mathcal{S}\not\ni U)\geq 1-\alpha)\leq\alpha

(2.2)

for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
.
Typically,
\mathcal{S}
is chosen in such a way that

\mbox{Prob}(\mathcal{S}\not\ni U)\sim\mbox{Uniform}(0,1)
,
the standard uniform distribution.
For example, the
\mathcal{S}(U)=\{u:|u|\leq|U|\}
,

U\sim\mbox{N}(0,1)
, is valid for predicting
an unobserved realization drawn from
\mbox{N}(0,1)
.

Given a PRS
\mathcal{S}
for predicting
U
in (
2.1
),
the inverse set-valued mapping

\Theta_{X}(\mathcal{S})=\{\theta:X=a(u,\theta)\,\mbox{ for some }\,u\in\mathcal{S}\}

(2.3)

defines a random set on
\Theta
.
For simplicity, here we assume
\Theta_{X}(\mathcal{S})\neq\emptyset

for all values of
\mathcal{S}

(
c.f.

Leaf and
Liu 2012
for the case
where
\mbox{Prob}(\mathcal{S}=\emptyset)>0
).
IM produces uncertainty assessments of any assertion of interest

A\subset\Theta
, by computing the following lower and upper probabilities

\mbox{bel}_{X}(A)=\mbox{Prob}(\Theta_{X}(\mathcal{S})\subseteq A)

(2.4)

and

\mbox{pl}_{X}(A)=1-\mbox{bel}_{X}(A^{c})=\mbox{Prob}(\Theta_{X}(\mathcal{S})\not\subseteq A^{c})

(2.5)

The probabilities (
2.4
) and (
2.5
)
are also
known as the belief function in
A
and plausibility of
A
, respectively.
A recent discussion of these lower and upper probabilities of IMs
and other existing ones is given in

Liu and
Martin 2021
; see also references therein.

As described above, the IM framework is rooted in
R. A. Fisher’s fiducial argument in the sense of making prior-free probabilistic inference about parameters
by using auxiliary variables to represent the sample model. However,
avoiding the difficulties of R. A. Fisher’s fiducial argument
and related theories
(
Zhang and
Liu 2011
;
Martin
et al. 2010
;
Liu and
Martin 2015
)
,
IM emphasizes frequency-calibrated, termed valid, and efficient
probabilistic inference.
Together with conditional IMs and marginal IMs (
Martin and
Liu 2015a
and

Martin and
Liu 2015b
), this theory also provides a
new mathematical tool to develop exact and efficient frequentist methods.
For example, for the familiar Neyman-Pearson school of thought,
it offers its plausibility regions for unknown parameters as exact Neyman-Pearson confidence regions
(
Martin and
Liu 2013
)
, and for the Fisherian school of thought, its plausibility for assertions of
interest provides a plausibility interpretation of and
a probabilistic alternative to
Fisher’s P-value
(
Martin and
Liu 2014
)
.

However, research on the foundations of statistical
inference has been challenging.
Like other existing approaches other than the standard
Bayes and frequentist schools of thought, including
generalized fiducial
(
Hannig 2009
;
Cui and
Hannig 2022
)
,
the method of confidence distribution
(
Xie
et al. 2011
;
Schweder and
Hjort 2016
)
,
and the Dempster-Shafer theory of belief functions

(
Shafer 1976
;
Shafer 1992
;
Dempster 2008
;
Martin
et al. 2010
)
,
IM is subject to further development with applications to tackle difficult problems.

3.1
The sampling model and IMs association

Without real loss of generality, our jumping-off point is to take a numerical approach
to approximate
G(.)
in (
1.2
) by discretization;
see also
Efron 2014
;
Efron 2016
.
Denote by
\vartheta_{1}<...<\vartheta_{K}
the known space
of
\theta
, and denote by
\gamma_{k}
the unknown probability mass at

\vartheta_{k}
, where

\gamma\equiv(\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{K})\in\left\{(\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{K}):\gamma_{k}\geq 0\mbox{ for all $k=1,...,K$;
$\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}=1$}\right\}

(3.1)

Remark 3.1
.

Take
\vartheta_{k}-\vartheta_{k-1}=\delta
for
all
k=1,...,K
. This discrete approximation can also
be viewed as a numerical approach to mixed distributions,
including both discrete and continuous distributions.

In this scenario,
G(.)
is represented by the
K
-dimensional vector
\gamma
in (
3.1
), that is, in terms of cdf

G(\theta)=\sum_{\vartheta_{k}\leq\theta}\gamma_{k}

and

X_{1},...,X_{i}
is a sample of the convolution of

G(.)
and
N(0,1)
:

F_{\vartheta,\gamma}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\Phi(x-\vartheta_{k})

(3.2)

where
\Phi(.)
denotes the cdf of the standard norm distribution
N(0,1)
.
Furthermore, given
\gamma
and
X
,

\theta_{i}
’s are independent of each other with

\mbox{Pr}(\theta_{i}=\vartheta_{j}|\gamma,X)=\frac{\gamma_{k}\phi(X_{i}-\theta_{j})}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\phi(X_{i}-\vartheta_{k})}\qquad(j=1,...,K)

(3.3)

where
\phi(.)
stands for the probability density function of the standard norm distribution
N(0,1)
.

From (
3.2
) and (
3.3
), we
see that inference about
\theta_{i}
s is reduced to inference
about
\gamma_{k}
’s.
It is known that
F_{\vartheta,\gamma}(X_{i})
’s form a sample
of size
n
from
F_{\vartheta,\gamma}(x)
.
For an IM approach, we take a sorted uniform sample of size of
n
,
denoted by
U_{(1)}<...,<U_{(n)}
, as auxiliary variables
and specify the association as

U_{(i)}=F_{\vartheta,\gamma}(X_{(i)})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\Phi(X_{(i)}-\vartheta_{k})

(3.4)

3.2
A PRS for sorted uniforms

Let
\mathbb{S}_{n}
denote
the sample space of
U_{(i)}
’s,
the simplex space of size
n
:

\mathbb{S}_{n}=\{(u_{1},...,u_{n}):0<u_{1}<...<u_{n}<1\}

Intuitively, we aim to constructing a PRS for

U_{(i)}
’s that should work well for the well-known
non-parametric one-sample test, where it is known that
the cdf values at the observations serve as
the sufficient statistics and form a sample of uniforms.
For simplicity, we use a type of predictive random set
similar to that in
(
Liu and
Xie 2014
)
and
(
Zhang 2010
)

for
(\Phi(Z_{(1)}),...,\Phi(Z_{(n)}))
,

\mathcal{U}=\{u:u\in\mathbb{S}_{n},B(u)\leq B(U)\}

(3.5)

where
U=(U_{(1)},...,U_{(n)})
, the vector of the sorted sample of size
n

from
\mbox{Uniform}(0,1)
and

B(u)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[a_{i}\ln(u_{i})+b_{i}\ln(1-u_{i})\right]

(3.6)

with

a_{i}=\frac{1}{n-i+c_{n}}
,

b_{i}=\frac{1}{i-1+c_{n}}
, and

c_{n}\approx\frac{2}{3}
. This PRS is considered in
Zhang 2010

with
c_{n}=0.7
as an IM approach to the nonparametric
one-sample problem, and it is shown that
the resulting method is comparable to
or better than traditional methods such as
the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test.
A recent theoretical investigation is given in

Liu 2022
.

Recall the familiar results on the marginal distribution of
U_{(i)}
. We have

U_{(i)}\sim\mbox{Beta}(i,n-i+1)

which has its mean and variance given by

\frac{i}{n+1}
and

\frac{i(n-i+1)}{(n+1)^{2}(n+2)}
.
The PRS
defined by (
3.5
) and (
3.6
)
is constructed as weighted logarithms of the marginal densities
with a minor modification so that the medians are approximately most likely

(
c.f.

Kerman 2011
, for the choice of
c_{n}=\frac{2}{3}
)
.
The weights are set to be about inversely proportional to their variances and,
thereby, pay more attention to the tail areas of the underlying sampling distribution.
This leads to
a slightly more general class of PRSs being to use alternative weight
schemes:

a_{i}=w_{i}(i-1+c_{n}),\;b_{i}=w_{i}(n-i+c_{n})

(3.7)

with

w_{i}=\frac{{[i(n-i+1)]^{-\nu/2}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}{[j(n-j+1)]^{-\nu/2}}}

(3.8)

for
i=1,...,n
, where
\nu\in[0,2]
.
The PRS (
3.7
) with
\nu=2
is
used in the numerical examples in Section
5
.

3.4
Maximum plausibility estimation

A straightforward application of these IMs is point estimation.
Given an estimate of
G
, the usual Bayes posterior mean

\hat{\theta}_{i}(\gamma)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\phi(X_{i}-\vartheta_{k})\vartheta_{k}}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\phi(X_{i}-\vartheta_{k})}

(3.10)

provides the most efficient estimator
(
Brown 1971
)
,
in terms of mean square error (MSE):

\mbox{MSE}(\hat{\theta})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{\theta}_{i}-\theta_{i})^{2}

(3.11)

Here, we consider the MPE of
\gamma
.
The smallest PRS with nested focal elements,
obtained with the method of
Leaf and
Liu 2012
for validity,
is given by

\mathcal{U}_{\min}=\left\{u:B(u)\leq B_{\min}\right\}

(3.12)

where

B_{\min}=\min_{\gamma}\mbox{$B\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\Phi(X_{(1)}-\vartheta_{k}),...,\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\Phi(X_{(n)}-\vartheta_{k})\right)$}

(3.13)

This induces the maximum plausibility set for
\gamma

\Gamma_{\min}=\left\{(\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{K}):\;\mbox{$B\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\Phi(X_{(1)}-\vartheta_{k}),...,\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\Phi(X_{(n)}-\vartheta_{k})\right)\leq B_{\min}$}\right\}

(3.14)

Therefore, the maximum plausibility
is necessarily one and is achieved for all
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\min}
.
Since
\Gamma_{\min}
given in (
3.14
) is a set,
we have a set of MPEs of
\theta_{i}
, one for each
\gamma\in\Gamma_{\min}
.

For simple practical applications, we can summarize the above set of MPEs by
two extremes, the lower and upper posterior expectations

\hat{\theta}_{i}^{(\mbox{lower})}=\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\min}}\hat{\theta}_{i}(\gamma)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\hat{\theta}_{i}^{(\mbox{upper})}=\max_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{\min}}\hat{\theta}_{i}(\gamma)

(3.15)

for
i=1,...,n.

When a single value estimate is desired,
the mean of the two extremes may be used, that is,

\hat{\theta}_{i}=\frac{\hat{\theta}_{i}^{(\mbox{lower})}+\hat{\theta}_{i}^{(\mbox{upper})}}{2}

(3.16)

This is used to evaluate PRS performance in the simulation
study in Section
5
.

3.5
Plausibility intervals

Interval estimation can be made to
have the desirable frequency-calibration property.
Suppose that we are interested in the
simultaneous interval estimation, one for each
\theta_{i}
.
Denote by
C_{i}^{(1-\alpha)}(X)

the interval estimate of
\theta_{i}
with the coefficient

1-\alpha
.
A frequency calibration property commonly used in practice
is that the interval satisfies

E\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I\left\{\theta_{i}\in C_{i}^{(1-\alpha)}(X)\right\}\right)\geq 1-\alpha.

(3.17)

In other words, on average at least
1-\alpha
percent of
the true values of
\theta_{i}
’s will be covered,
in a frequentist sense within and across experiments.
This, more precisely, the case with only the equality constraint in (
3.17
)
has been a popular standard in practice

(
Wahba 1983
;
Brown
et al. 2011
)
.

The validity of IM methods that guarantees frequency calibration
has been established in
Martin and
Liu 2013
.
Formally, we have the following result.

Theorem 1

(IMs Validity)
.

Let
B_{1-\pi}
be the
(1-\alpha)
-th quantile of
B(U)

and denote by
\Gamma_{1-\pi}
the
\alpha
-plausibility region

\Gamma_{1-\pi}=\left\{(\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{K}):\;\mbox{$B\left(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\Phi(X_{(1)}-\vartheta_{k}),...,\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\Phi(X_{(n)}-\vartheta_{k})\right)\leq B_{1-\pi}$}\right\}.

(3.18)

Then,

E\left((\gamma_{1},...,\gamma_{n})\in\Gamma_{1-\gamma}\right)\geq 1-\pi
.

Suppose that for each
\gamma\in\Gamma_{1-\pi}
,
we construct the two-sided “confidence interval”
with the left-end point

\theta_{\alpha/2}^{(\mbox{lower})}(\gamma)=\max_{\ell}\left\{\theta_{\ell}:\hat{\theta}_{i}(\gamma)=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\gamma_{k}\phi(X_{i}-\vartheta_{k})}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\phi(X_{i}-\vartheta_{k})}\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}\right\}

(3.19)

and the right-end point

\theta_{\alpha/2}^{(\mbox{upper})}(\gamma)\min_{r}\left\{\theta_{r}:\hat{\theta}_{i}(\gamma)=\frac{\sum_{k=r}^{n}\gamma_{k}\phi(X_{i}-\vartheta_{k})}{\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{k}\phi(X_{i}-\vartheta_{k})}\leq\frac{\alpha}{2}\right\}.

(3.20)

This leads to the following plausibility interval

C_{i}(1-\pi,1-\alpha)\equiv\left[\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{1-\pi}}\theta_{\alpha/2}^{(\mbox{lower})}(\gamma),\;\max_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{1-\pi}}\theta_{\alpha/2}^{(\mbox{upper})}(\gamma)\right]

(3.21)

The following theorem establishes a lower bound for
the “confidence coefficient” of (
3.21
).

Theorem 2

(Coverage Property)
.

The coverage probability of the confidence interval (
3.21
)
is at least
(1-\gamma)(1-\pi)
, i.e.,

\mbox{\rm Pr}\left(C_{i}(1-\pi,1-\alpha)\ni\theta_{i}\right)\geq(1-\pi)(1-\alpha)

for all
\pi\in(0,1)
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

The IMs validity says that
\gamma
is covered by
\Gamma_{1-\pi}

1-\pi
percent. In such a case,
the interval defined by the two end points
(
3.19
) and (
3.20
)
covers the true value, generated from
G
, with probability

1-\alpha
. The claimed result follows from the
multiplication rule of probability.
∎

Theorem
2

provides a way of constructing exact confidence intervals.
Because the set for
\mathbb{G}
or
\gamma
can be large,

Remark 3.2

(Within-experiment Validation)
.

Within-experiment frequency evaluation of confidence interval
is possible when

The fractional number of
\theta_{i}
’s falling outside of
their

n\alpha
and in such cases, the corresponding observed

’s
have probability 0.5 to fall outside of the intervals as well.
On the basis of this argument,
we expect to see that the number of

falling outside of
corresponding individual
1-\alpha
confidence intervals for
\theta_{i}

is about
n\alpha/2
.

Remark 3.3

(Selection of
\pi
as a Way of Modeling)
.

When confidence intervals are excessively large,
modeling can be useful. Efron’s
g
-modeling,
which assumes
\gamma\propto e^{Q\zeta}

for some fixed
(K\times p)
matrix
Q
with
unknown
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, serves a good example.
Alternatively, one can also
choose
\pi
and attribute the ignored relevant error to
the modeling error.

Remark 3.4

(Adaptive Adjustment)
.

Efficient confidence intervals can be constructed via Monte Carlo
as follows. Without loss of generality, suppose that

95\%
CIs are of interest. So, take
1-\alpha=\sqrt{0.95}
,
evaluate coverage via Monte Carlo simulation
with
\gamma\in...

1-\pi=\sqrt{0.95}

for a sequence of
m
, say,
m=10
, quantiles
B(U)
with probabilities

(s/m)\sqrt{0.95}
,
s=1,...,m
.
The MPE estimate of
\mathbb{G}
, for example, can be used to
generate data for this Monte Carlo approach.
The approximate intervals for the real data
are the
(s/m)\sqrt{0.95}
intervals, where
s
is the index of
the simulation intervals with their actual coverage
in the simulated data matching the targeted coverage.
This approach is used in the real-data example in Section
5.2
.

4
IMs for MNMs of the classic kind

4.1
Constructing an association via augmenting latent auxiliary variables

The Stein phenomenon implies that
when performance assessment of inferential methods
involves simultaneous inference about
\theta_{i}
’s,
such as mean square errors for point estimation,
efficient methods can be developed by allowing
the individual estimators of
\theta_{i}
’s to share
risk with each other.
To see this intuitively from an IMs perspective,
we write
Z_{1},...,Z_{n}
in (
1.1
)
in terms of their sorted values
Z_{(1)},...,Z_{(n)}
with
an auxiliary random permutation of
(1,...,n)
, denoted by
(\tau_{1},...,\tau_{n})
:

X_{i}=\theta_{i}+Z_{(\tau_{i})}

(4.1)

Since all the
n!
permutations are equally likely,
the sampling models (
1.1
) and (
4.1
)
are equivalent to each other.
Clearly, efficient simultaneous inference can be obtained, conditional on

(\tau_{1},...,\tau_{n})
.
Here, we develop efficient methods by estimating
the unknown permutation
\tau
as a way to control the level of risk sharing.
Before proceeding, we record a fundamental result that is useful in what follows,
where optimization over
(Z_{(1)},...,Z_{(n)})
and
\tau=(\tau_{1},...,\tau_{n})

can be carried out over
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proposition 1
.

It holds with probability one in terms of long-run frequency that given the observed data
X_{1}
,…,
X_{n}
, the auxiliary variables
(Z_{(1)},...,Z_{(n)})
and
\tau=(\tau_{1},...,\tau_{n})

in (
4.1
) determine the values of
\theta_{i}
’s and are determined by the values of
\theta_{i}
’s.

The proof of Proposition
1
is given in Appendix
A
.

What is interesting here for IMs is that the number of
auxiliary variables is larger than that of the observed data.
For this reason, we call the auxiliary permutation

\tau
a
latent
auxiliary variable.
For technical clarity, we
denote by RP the abbreviation of “random permutation” and
summarize the above discussion into the following lemma, regarding
the equivalence of three sampling associations,
including (
1.1
)
and (
4.1
).

Lemma 1

(Equivalence of Three Sampling Associations)
.

The sampling associations (
1.1
),
(
4.1
), and

X_{\tau_{i}}=\theta_{\tau_{i}}+Z_{i}\qquad(\tau\sim\mbox{RP}(1,...,n);\;Z_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}N(0,1))

(4.2)

are all equivalent in the sense that the induced
sampling distributions of
X
’s, given

\theta
’s, are the same, i.e.,

X_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}N(\theta_{i},1)
for all
i=1,...,n
.

Lemma
1
can be easily
established with standard technical arguments in terms of sampling distributions.
This result allows flexibility in formulating
IMs for prior-free probabilistic inference.
For example, Equation (
4.2
) implies that
the sampling scheme of the classic kind can be viewed
as follows. There is a collection of
\theta
’s, denoted by

\{\theta_{(k)}:k=1,...,n\}
.
One takes
\theta
’s from
\{\theta_{(k)}:k=1,...,n\}
without
replacement,
draws a sample of size
n
from
N(0,1)
, and
creates
X
’s via either (
4.2
) or

X_{\tau_{i}}=\theta_{\tau_{i}}+Z_{(i)}
for
i=1,...,n
.

The enforcement of risk sharing clearly prevents
\tau
from being
marginalized out. Note that different permutations
produce observed data samples as a collection of
data points that look like each other or,
more precisely, have the same distribution.
However, given the observed data, the permutation chosen to generate the data would be the best to be conditioned on for inference via prediction of

Z_{(\tau_{1})},...,Z_{(\tau_{n})}
.
This suggests that the collection of data points
X_{i}
’
be used to establish an association, which we call
an
augmented association
.
In other words, we can use the sampling
model of the order and sufficient statistics
X_{(1)}\leq...\leq X_{(n)}

to create such an augmented association.

Note that the marginal distribution for a random drawn from

\{X_{(1)},...,X_{(n)}\}
is given by

F_{\theta}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\Phi(x-\theta_{k})

(4.3)

In terms of the true realizations of the auxiliary variables,
we can also write (
4.3
) as

F_{\theta}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\Phi(x-[X_{k}-Z_{(\tau_{k})}])

(4.4)

With Equation (
4.4
)
we have the following association

F_{\theta}(X_{(i)})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\Phi(X_{(i)}-[X_{k}-Z_{(\tau_{k})}])

(4.5)

for
i=1,...,n
.
Before proceeding, we make the following two remarks, which motivate
the PRS construction in the next section.

Remark 4.1

(Dependence of Latent Auxiliary Variables)
.

Comparison of (
4.1
) and
(
4.5
) reveals that
(
4.5
)
unsurprisingly depends on
\Phi(Z_{i})
’s. This can be
seen clearly, for example, by the extreme case where all the
\theta_{i}
’s are the same and, thereby,
in this extreme case (
4.1
) and
(
4.5
) are identical.

Remark 4.2

(Sampling W/O Replacement)
.

Although
F_{\theta}(.)
defined in (
4.3
)
is the distribution function for all
X_{i}
’s,

\{X_{(1)},...,X_{(n)}\}
is not a sample of
F_{\theta}(.)
.
This is because
\{X_{(1)},...,X_{(n)}\}
is
equivalent to the ordered sample of observations
X_{i}
’s taken
from the
n
sub-populations
N(\theta_{i},1)

without replacement (of subpopulations),
whereas a sample from
F_{\theta}(.)
, i.e., the
n
subpopulations

N(\theta_{i},1)
, is necessarily drawn with replacement.

4.2
Point and interval estimations

The PRS defined in Section
3
for the sorted uniform samples
can be used for a sorted sample of
\Phi(Z_{i})
’s.
The remaining challenges relate to the specification of a PRS
for latent permutation
\tau
, which will be used for inference through
(
4.5
).
Intuitively, for the largest possible risk sharing,
it is important to control the accuracy of the prediction of
\tau
using PRS.
For the extreme case in this way of reasoning, we would ignore predicting

Z_{(i)}
’s, at least not predicting directly,
i.e.
,
formally predicting
Z_{(i)}
’s with their entire probability sample space.
In other words, predicting
\tau
alone effectively
estimates the collection of
\theta
’s. What is needed is to create a random bound
\mathcal{B}
on

B(F_{\theta}(X_{(1)}),...,F_{\theta}(X_{(n)}))
,
working in the same way as the basic IMs to induce
a random set for
\theta
’s.
Theoretically, valid inference can be made
as long as we use a random bound
\mathcal{B}
that is stochastically
not smaller than
B(F_{\theta}(X_{(1)}),...,F_{\theta}(X_{(n)}))
.
This is rigorously supported by the following theorem.

Theorem 3

(Validity of conservative PRSs)
.

Suppose that
B
is a random variable
that is stochastically larger than

B(F_{\theta}(X_{(1)}),...,F_{\theta}(X_{(n)}))

with
X
and
\theta
given in (
4.1
).
Then inference on
\theta
in the IMs framework
through the induced
random set on the parameter space

\Theta(\mathcal{B})=\left\{\theta:\,\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\;B(F_{\theta}(X_{(1)}),...,F_{\theta}(X_{(n)}))\leq\mathcal{B}\right\}

(4.6)

is valid.

Proof.

This result can be proved by using
arguments similar to those in the proof of
Theorem 2 in
Martin and
Liu 2013
;
see also
Martin 2015
.
∎

In the simulation study of Section
5
, we use

\mathcal{B}=B(U_{(1)},...,U_{(n)})
, where

U_{(1)},...,U_{(n)}
are the sorted values of
n
uniforms.
A heuristic argument for this is that
the sorted sample of
X
s without replacement (of
\theta
s)
spreads more evenly than that with replacement from the
same marginal distribution (
4.3
).
Together with (
4.3
),
this implies that the sorted
X
s have about the same component locations, but smaller variability.
As a result,
\mathcal{B}=B(U_{(1)},...,U_{(n)})

serves as a random bound in Theorem
3

to produce a valid inference on
\theta
, at least for
values of
B(F_{\theta}(X_{(1)}),...,F_{\theta}(X_{(n)}))
that are not large;
see Figure
2
for a pictorial illustration supporting this argument.
Although it guarantees validity, the use of

\mathcal{B}=B(U_{(1)},...,U_{(n)})

can lead to conservative inference.
Since the distribution of

B(F_{\theta}(X_{(1)}),...,F_{\theta}(X_{(n)}))
depends on
\theta
s,
efficient inference can be made by using
the so-called assertion-specific PRS

(
Martin and
Liu 2015a
)
.
More precisely, we summarize this approach into the following theorem,
followed by a remark on its potential applications and computational challenges.

Theorem 4

(Validity of Assertion-specific PRSs for MPE and Constructing CIs)
.

Suppose that the assertion

\{\vartheta^{\prime}_{(k)}:\;k=1,...,n\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

is of interest. Define
the induced random set on
\theta

\Theta(X^{\prime})=\left\{\theta:\,\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n};\;B(F_{\theta}(X_{(1)}),...,F_{\theta}(X_{(n)}))\leq B(F_{\vartheta^{\prime}}(X^{\prime}_{(1)}),...,F_{\vartheta^{\prime}}(X^{\prime}_{(n)}))\right\}

(4.7)

where
X^{\prime}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}N(\vartheta^{\prime}_{i},1)
for

i=1,...,n
.
Then the inference based on
\Theta(X^{\prime})
in the IMs framework
is valid.

Proof.

This result can also be proved by using
arguments similar to those in the proof of
Theorem 2 in
Martin and
Liu 2013
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

According to
Theorems
3

and
4
,
a way to construct efficient confidence regions
is to find the minimum plausibility for any
given
\theta\in\Theta(\mathcal{B})
.
This minimum plausibility is the plausibility
of
\Theta(\mathcal{B})
.
Evaluation of this plausibility appears to be computationally intensive.
It would be interesting to develop efficient computational
methods for this approach.

The theory established so far provides inference about
the
\theta
’s as a collection.
Recall that given the collection
\theta
,
X
’s were generated
by drawing
\theta
’s without replacement.
The standard algebraic operations of applying the Bayes theorem lead to
the following results.

Theorem 5

(Conditional Distribution of
\theta
)
.

Suppose
n\geq 2
and assume that the values of
\theta
,
\{\vartheta_{k}:\vartheta_{k}=\theta_{(k)},k=1,...,n\}
, are known.
Then the partial conditional distribution of
\theta_{i}
given
X_{i}

has the probability mass function (pmf)

\mbox{Pr}(\theta_{i}=\vartheta_{j}|X_{i})=\frac{\phi(X_{i}-\vartheta_{j})}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\phi(X_{i}-\vartheta_{k})}

(4.8)

and the full conditional distribution of
\theta
given
X
has the PMF

\mbox{Pr}(\theta_{1}=\vartheta_{\tau_{1}},...,\theta_{n}=\vartheta_{\tau_{n}}|X_{1},...,X_{n})=\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(X_{j}-\vartheta_{\tau_{j}})}{\sum_{\tau^{\prime}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\phi(X_{j}-\vartheta_{\tau^{\prime}_{j}})}

(4.9)

Theorem
5
makes it possible
to estimate
\theta
’s via
E(\theta|X)
, fulfilling
the required operation to overcome the fundamental difficulty
of making inference for the classic kind (
c.f.
, Section

1
and
Stigler 1990
).
While it is appealing to use the full conditional distribution
(
4.9
) for MPE, for computational simplicity,
here we use the following simple estimator, obtained from
(
4.8
),

\hat{\theta}_{i}=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\vartheta_{k}\phi(X_{i}-\vartheta_{k})}{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\phi(X_{i}-\vartheta_{k})}

(4.10)

for
i=1,...,n
,
with
\{\vartheta_{(k)}:k=1,...,n\}
obtained from
the approach of Theorem
3

or Theorem
4
. The computational strategies for the empirical-Bayes kind
provided in the appendices can be applied here for the classic kind.
More discussion is given in Section
6

on partial conditioning versus full conditioning
in Theorem
5
for
estimation
\theta
.

6
Discussion

Taking an IM approach,
we have investigated making exact and efficient inference for the two
kinds of MNMs.
For the empirical Bayes kind, with IMs one can
aim at producing efficient inference by balancing validity and flexibility.
Since the IM framework offers prior-free probabilistic inference, which
offers more than confidence, IM plausibilities can be used for
point and interval estimations.
In addition to the desired exactness, the efficiency of the resulting
methods is satisfactory, as shown in the simulation study.
Furthermore, in the case where further
g
-modeling is preferred,
the IMs results could be useful for model building
inasmuch as valid non-parametric methods are useful for
model checking;
see
Tukey 1962
for more discussion along this line.

It should be noted that IM works well for overparameterized
\mathbb{G}
.
This can be viewed as a good property of IMs, unlike the frequentist and Bayesian schools
of thought.
Frequentist statisticians often do inference by first constructing point estimators and then developing confidence methods based on point estimators.
For over-parameterized models,
some kinds of regularization via penalty methods are often used.
Bayesians have to fully specify a probability model for everything, making it identifiable in a probabilistic sense with the usual theory of probability.
With IMs, our way of reasoning is done with respect to the auxiliary variables. Nonidentifiability is no longer an issue because we work with set-valued functions/mappings.

There are also other kinds of the MNM problem.
A popular kind is obviously the so-called
normal-normal empirical Bayes, where the unobserved
\theta
’s
are assumed to have followed a normal distribution
with unknown hyper-parameters.
This model has been widely used in applied statistics;
see, for example,

Rubin 1980
,
Morris 1983
,

Gelman
et al. 1995
,

Qiu
et al. 2018
, and references therein.

Our understanding of the classic kind from the IM perspective
motivated the new idea of introducing a latent permutation auxiliary
variable. Although the proposed methods are satisfactory in the context
of our numerical results for illustration and comparison,
there are theoretical challenges remaining
as far as optimal solutions are of interest.
This is mainly due to the technical difficulties in
dealing with data collected via sampling without replacement, whereas
investigations of the empirical Bayesian kind enjoy the
relative simplicity offered by data collection through
sampling with replacement.
In particular, it would be interesting
to develop efficient computational algorithms to evaluate the full conditional expectation
in Theorem
5
.
However, a large difference is expected between
partial and full conditional expectations for small
n

and not for large
n
.

The importance of the MNM problem immediately suggests that our new methods can be developed for MNM types of problem, such as linear and
non-parametric regression. These new methods can also be potentially
extended to situations beyond normal models,
including Poisson and binomial models.
Incidentally, the idea of considering latent associations
would inspire new research on developments of the IM theory
itself as well as methods from other schools of thought. For example,
an alternative to popular penalty methods
for maximum likelihood estimation is to make use of
the idea of using latent associations as a data-adaptive way of
regularization, which has been more or less a standard method
in contemporary high-dimensional statistics.
We plan to work on these topics and report the results elsewhere.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
1

It follows from (
4.1
) that

\theta_{i}=X_{i}-Z_{(\tau_{i})},\qquad(i=1,...,n)

That is, given the observed data
X_{1},...,X_{n}
, the auxiliary variables
(Z_{(1)},...,Z_{(n)})^{\prime}
and
(\tau_{1},....,\tau_{n})^{\prime}
determine the values of
\theta_{i}
’s.

Now, suppose that
\theta_{i}
, the value of the mean of
X_{i}
, is given for all
i=1,...,n.

Then

Z_{(\tau_{i})}=Z_{i}=X_{i}-\theta_{i},\qquad(i=1,...,n)

and, thereby, both
Z_{i}
and
\tau_{i}
are also determined by all
\theta_{i}
’s, given
X_{1},...,X_{n}.
The latter follows from the fact that
Z_{1},...,Z_{n}
are all different from each other with probability one as a sample of size
n
from the standard normal distribution
N(0,1)
.
