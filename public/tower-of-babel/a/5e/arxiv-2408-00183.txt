Introduction

We are interested in analogues of addition theorems that occur
in field extensions
F/K
of a base field
K
. If
S
and
T
are
finite-dimensional
K
-vector subspaces of
F
, we denote by
ST
the

K
-linear span of the set of all products
st
,
s\in S
,
t\in T
.
The general purpose of this area of research is to characterise
subspaces
S
and
T
whose product
ST
has unusually small
dimension: it is naturally inspired by one of the goals of additive
combinatorics which is to characterise subsets
A,B

of a group that have sumsets
A+B
of small cardinality, where
A+B

denotes the set of elements
a+b
,
a\in A
,
b\in B
.

This line of research was arguably triggered by Hou, Leung and Xiang
when they proved
[
HLX02
]
an extension field analogue of Kneser’s
classical addition Theorem for Abelian groups
[
Kne56
]
. The
extension field version essentially states that, given a field extension
F/K
, if
S
and
T
are

K
-linear spaces of finite dimension contained in
F
, then whenever
\dim_{K}\!ST<\dim_{K}\!S+\dim_{K}\!T-1
, it must be the case that the product space

ST
is stabilised by a non-trivial (different from
K
) subfield of

F
. This theorem was initially proved for separable extensions in

[
HLX02
]
, and was shown to be true for all extensions in

[
BSZ18
]
. Versions of Kneser’s Theorem for products of vector
spaces were also shown to hold in other algebras than field extensions

[
BL17
,
MZ15
]
. Many applications of the theory of products of
spaces in the algebra
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with componentwise multiplication are
discussed in
[
Ran15
]
: the latter theory is concerned in particular
with the existence and the construction of products of linear codes
of small dimension, and since many of these codes are constructed by
evaluating functions drawn from a function field over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, this
makes the study of the dimension of products of spaces in function
fields relevant to this topic as well.

If
F/K
does not contain a finite extension of
K
, and if

S,T
are
K
-subspaces of
F
such that
ST\neq F
, then Kneser’s
Theorem for extension fields implies that
\dim_{K}\!ST\geqslant\dim_{K}\!S+\dim_{K}\!T-1
. A study of the limiting case when
\dim_{K}\!ST=\dim_{K}\!S+\dim_{K}\!T-1
was undertaken in
[
BSZ17
]
. A particularly simple case occurs
when
K
is algebraically closed. One can then easily uncover the
structure of the spaces
S
and
T

choosing a discrete valuation
v
on
F
, considering the sets
of integers
v(S)
and
v(T)
and arguing that their Minkowski sum
v(S)+v(T)

must satisfy
|v(S)+v(T)|=|v(S)|+|v(T)|-1
,
from
which addition in
{\mathbb{Z}}
tells us that

v(S)
and
v(T)
must be arithmetic
progressions with the same difference: It can then be inferred that the spaces
S
and
T

must have bases in geometric progression.
Switching to the symmetric case
S=T
for the sake of
simplicity, it becomes then tempting to ask for the structure of
spaces
S
such that

\dim_{K}\!S^{2}=2\dim_{K}\!S-1+\gamma

(1)

for increasing values of
\gamma
. It was suggested in

[
BCZ18
]
that one should draw inspiration from what is often known
as
Freiman’s “
3k-4
” Theorem

[
Fre73
]
, (called
Freiman’s
3k-3

Theorem
in
[
TV06
, Thm. 5.11]
). Recall that it states that, if
A

is a set of integers,

|A+A|=2|A|-1+\gamma

(2)

implies, when
\gamma\leqslant|A|-3
, that
A
is included in an
arithmetic progression of length
|A|+\gamma
. Natural analogues of
such sets
A
are spaces
S
that are of codimension at most

\gamma
inside a space with a basis in geometric
progression. However, the point was made in
[
BCZ18
]
that other
examples of spaces satisfying (
1
) are Riemann-Roch
spaces
{\mathcal{L}}(D)
of an algebraic curve of genus
\gamma
. Note that a
space with a basis in geometric progression is a particular instance of a
Riemann-Roch space in a genus
0
function field. In the present article
we show that Riemann-Roch spaces play the role of arithmetic progressions in
the extension field setting and that the following analogue of
Freiman’s
3k-4
Theorem holds:

Theorem 1
.

Let
K
be a perfect field and
F

be an extension field of
K
in which
K
is algebraically
closed (i.e. any element of
F
that is algebraic over

K
is in
K
). Let
S
be a
K
-subspace of finite dimension in

F
such that
K\subset S
and
F=K(S)
. If

\dim_{K}\!S^{2}=2\dim_{K}\!S-1+\gamma\quad\text{with}\quad 0\leqslant\gamma\leqslant\dim_{K}\!S-3,

then
F
has transcendence degree
1
over
K
and its genus
g

satisfies
g\leqslant\gamma
. Furthermore, there exists a Riemann-Roch
space
{\mathcal{L}}(D)
of
F
that contains
S
and such that

\dim_{K}\!{\mathcal{L}}(D)-\dim_{K}S\leqslant\gamma-g
.

Theorem
1
was first conjectured in
[
BCZ18
]
where the
statement was proved for
\gamma\leqslant 1
. Moreover, it was also proved that, under the conditions of the theorem,
F
must always be of transcendence degree
1
over
K
. Later on, the conjecture was proved
for
\gamma=2
under the additional assumption that
F=K(x)

[
Wes24
]
.

The integer
\gamma
in (
1
)
was called the
combinatorial genus
of the space
S
because
of the way it relates to the geometric genus
g
of the associated
function field. We note that Theorem
1
is not only a
linear analogue of Freiman’s
3k-4
Theorem, but can also be seen a
generalisation since it easily recaptures the original additive
statement. Indeed, if
A
is a set of integers satisfying
(
2
) with
\gamma\leqslant|A|-3
, then, supposing without loss
of generality that
A\subset[0,n=\max A]
and
\mathrm{gcd}(A)=1
, we
may form the vector space
S
generated by
x^{a}
,
a\in A
, inside the
rational function field extension
K(x)
. We have that
S^{2}
is
generated by
x^{a}
,
a\in A+A
, and Theorem
1
therefore
applies. The relevant Riemann-Roch space
{\mathcal{L}}(D)
in the conclusion of
the Theorem can only be
{\mathcal{L}}(nP_{\infty})
, where
P_{\infty}
is the place
at infinity and
n=\max A
, and that
S
is of codimension at most

\gamma
inside
{\mathcal{L}}(nP_{\infty})
means exactly that
A
equals the arithmetic
progression
[0,n]
with at most
\gamma
missing elements.

The way we prove Theorem
1
borrows from Lev and
Smeliansky’s elegant proof of Freiman’s additive
3k-4
Theorem

[
LS95
]
(see
[
TV06
, Th. 5.11]
for a concise description). The
idea was to project the set of integers under study onto a finite
quotient
{\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}}
and apply Kneser’s Theorem in this finite group. Our
starting point will be to view
F=K(S)
as a
finite
extension
of a transcendental extension
K(x)
of
K
, for a suitably chosen element

x\in S
. The goal will then be to apply the extension field version
of Kneser’s Theorem to
K(S)/K(x)
and extract non-trivial information
from it: there will be significant differences with the additive situation
however. In the next section we give an overview of the paper and the
structure of the proof of Theorem
1
, which will draw upon
a number of algebraic and combinatorial techniques.

2
Overview

2.1
The additive case.

To describe the main steps of the proof of Theorem
1
, it
is useful to have in mind the template for Lev and Smeliansky’s proof
of the original
3k-4
Theorem. One starts with a set of integers
A

satisfying (
2
)

|A+A|=2|A|-1+\gamma

with
\min A=0
,
\max A=n
and

\mathrm{gcd}(A)=1
: the goal is to prove that
|A|\geqslant n+1-\gamma
. To this end one reduces the set
A
to the set

\tilde{A}
of integers modulo
n
, so that we have

|\tilde{A}|=|A|-1
. Since the sets
A
and
A+n
have only
n
as a
common element, and since both reduce to
\tilde{A}
modulo
n
, we
have that

|\tilde{A}+\tilde{A}|\leqslant|\tilde{A}|+\gamma\leqslant 2|\tilde{A}|-2.

(3)

Now Kneser’s Theorem tells us that there exists a subgroup
H
of
{\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}}

such that
\tilde{A}+\tilde{A}=\tilde{A}+\tilde{A}+H
and
|\tilde{A}+\tilde{A}|\geqslant 2|\tilde{A}|-|H|

(with (
3
) therefore implying that
|H|>1
). It could happen that

\tilde{A}+\tilde{A}={\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}}
, in which case we are done: indeed, the left-hand
inequality of (
3
) entails precisely that

|\tilde{A}|\geqslant n-\gamma
and hence
|A|\geqslant n+1-\gamma
.
The rest of the proof consists of showing that
H\neq{\mathbb{Z}}/n{\mathbb{Z}}
cannot happen.
This is achieved by partitioning
A
into sets that represent cosets modulo
H
:
by studying the behaviour of sums of these sets, one finds elements of
A+A
that are equal modulo
H
and counting them contradicts the hypothesis
\gamma\leqslant|A|-3
in (
2
). The argument is detailed concisely in
[
TV06
]
.

2.2
The function field setting.

It is shown in
[
BCZ18
]

that under the hypothesis of the Theorem,
the field
F
must have transcendence degree
1
so that it is a function field in one variable.
It is then natural enough to consider the smallest Riemann-Roch space
{\mathcal{L}}(D)
containing
S
as the analogue of the interval
[0,n]
in the additive case. The divisor
D
is therefore the least sum of all the places
P
, counted with suitable multiplicities, for which there exist functions in
S
with poles at
P
. We then identify a function
x
in
S
whose pole divisor is exactly
D
. Next, we augment the space
S
by considering its linear span
\tilde{S}=K(x)S
over
K(x)
. The choice of
x
will ensure that
S\cap xS=Kx
so that
\dim_{K}(S+xS)=2\dim S-1
. This guarantees that

\dim_{K(x)}\tilde{S}^{2}\leqslant\dim_{K(x)}\tilde{S}+\gamma\leqslant 2\dim_{K(x)}\tilde{S}-2

similarly to (
3
). Viewing now
F
as an algebraic extension of
K(x)
(rather than
K
),
we can apply Kneser’s Theorem for field extensions
[
BSZ18
]
which says that there exists a field
L
,

K(x)\subset L\subset F
, such that
\tilde{S}^{2}=L\tilde{S}^{2}
and
\dim_{K(x)}\tilde{S}^{2}\geqslant 2\dim_{K(x)}\tilde{S}-\dim_{K(x)}L
. Since
2\dim_{K(x)}\tilde{S}-\dim_{K(x)}L\leqslant 2\dim_{K(x)}\tilde{S}-2
we have that the field
L
must be strictly larger than
K(x)
.
It will not be difficult to obtain that if
L=F
, then, somewhat similarly to the additive case, the conclusion of Theorem
1
holds. This is a consequence of Clifford’s Theorem and is detailed in Section
3
.

It will remain therefore to show that the situation
L\neq F
cannot occur. At this point, similarities with the additive case break down rather seriously and we are confronted with new challenges.
Following the additive template would be easier if we could prove the existence of an
L
-adapted basis of
S
, i.e. the existence of elements
s_{i}\in S
such that
LS
decomposes as the direct sum of the
Ls_{i}
, and
S
decomposes as the direct sum of
S_{i}=S\cap Ls_{i}
. We could then study the dimensions of the products
S_{i}S_{j}
without fearing the appearance of unexpected linear relations between these subspaces.
We do not know how to argue for the existence of such a decomposition of
S
however, but we did manage to obtain a partial result in this direction. We showed that the dimension over
K
of
L\cap S
is a non-trivial quantity: this is the object of Section
4
. The way this is achieved is by studying the evaluations of functions of
S
on suitably chosen places, and showing that some of these functions must have block-wise constant evaluations, exactly as elements of
L
do. Galois Theory arguments then enable us to show that these evaluations must come from functions of
S
that also fall into
L
.

Section
5
switches from the algebraic techniques developed in Section
4
to a much more combinatorial approach. It uses the result of Section
4
on
S\cap L
to decompose
S
into a direct sum of spaces that is much cruder than what would be provided by a hypothetical
L
-adapted basis, but which turns out to be sufficient to give an evaluation of
\dim_{K}S^{2}
that only just contradicts the hypothesis (
1
) of Theorem
1
.

2.3
A geometric comment.

Using
K(x)S
as we do
may not seem like the most natural approach.
Since the additive case resorted to a quotient of the ring of integers, a tempting analogue,
that may seem closer in spirit to the additive proof, would be to consider a subring of
F
containing
S
and reduce it modulo some ideal.
In Section
4
this is exactly what we do: we choose a place
O
of
K(x)
which splits totally into a sum
Q
of places in
F
and we study
S
modulo
Q
. However, we have not seen how to derive a proof of Theorem
1
using only this approach, even though there exist Kneser-type theorems for such quotients
[
BL17
,
MZ15
]
.

Nevertheless, we believe that using
K(x)S
definitely makes sense from a geometric point of view.
Indeed, the extension
F/K(x)
corresponds to some curve covering

\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
, where
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}

have respective function fields
K(x)
and
F
. The space
K(x)S
can be
interpreted as the restriction of
S
to the generic fibre of the
covering
\mathcal{Y}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
, while reducing

S
modulo
Q
corresponds to a restriction to some special fibre.
In the end, proving the result required us to restrict
S
both to the generic fibre and to some special fibre.

2.4
Organisation of the article

In Section
3
we provide the setup for the proof of Theorem
1
, introduce the subextension fields of

,
K(x)\subset L\subset F
, and prove that the conclusion of the Theorem holds if

. In Section

we study the dimension over

of

Both Sections

and

rely on the results of Section

:
Section

relies upon the result of Section

on the dimension of

, but it is possible to put off its proof and read Section

before Section

The proof of Theorem

developed through Sections

to

uses the hypothesis that the ground field

3.1
Background and Notation

3.1.1
The arithmetic of function fields

Number theoretic background is fairly basic and most of it can be found in Chapters 1 and 3 of
[
Sti09
]
, from which we borrow notation.

Given a field
K
,
the function fields over
K
that we will encounter (see Section
3.2.1
below) will be exclusively function fields in one variable, i.e. finite extensions of the field
K(x)
of rational functions.

In this setting, a
valuation ring
of the function field
F
is a proper subring

K\subsetneq\mathcal{O}\subsetneq F
that is maximal for inclusion. Such rings are
discrete valuation rings, i.e. they are local rings whose unique maximal
ideal is principal. Following
[
Sti09
]
and other authors we call
places
of
F
the maximal ideals of the discrete valuation rings of
F
. The induced discrete valuation of a place
P
is denoted

v_{P}:K^{\times}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
.
In most of the article, with the notable exception of Section
6
, the field

K
will be supposed to be algebraically closed, so that we have
\mathcal{O}/P\simeq K
, meaning places have
degree
1
.
The image of a function
f
by
the evaluation map at
P
:
\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\mathcal{O}/P
, will be denoted by
f(P)
.

A
divisor
of
F
is a formal sum
n_{1}P_{1}+\cdots+n_{s}P_{s}

where the
n_{i}
’s are integers and the
P_{i}
’s are places of
F
.
The
degree
of
D=n_{1}P_{1}+\cdots n_{s}P_{s}
is defined as
\sum_{i}n_{i}
when the places
P_{i}
have degree
1
.
A divisor
D=n_{1}P_{1}+\cdots n_{s}P_{s}
is said to be
positive
if
n_{i}\geqslant 0
for all
i
.
We write
D\geqslant D^{\prime}
to mean that
D-D^{\prime}
is positive. Since
a function
f\in F^{\times}
has non-zero valuation at only a finite number of places,
we can associate to
f
its
principal divisor

(f)_{F}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{P}v_{P}(f)P,

where the sum is taken over all the places of
F
. The positive and negative part
of the above divisor are denoted
(f)_{F,0}
and
(f)_{F,\infty}
which means that

(f)_{F}=(f)_{F,0}-(f)_{F,\infty}
. To lighten notation, when there is no ambiguity on the ambient field, the subscript
F
is omitted and we write
(f),(f)_{0},(f)_{\infty}
.
Principal divisors are known
to have degree
0
.
Finally, given a divisor
D
, the
Riemann–Roch
space associated to
D

is defined as

{\mathcal{L}}_{F}(D)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\{f\in F^{\times}\penalty\ |\penalty\ (f)+D\geqslant 0\}\cup\{0\}.

This is a finite dimensional
K
-vector space. We will again omit the subscript

F
and write
{\mathcal{L}}(D)
when the ambient field is clear from context.
Finally, to any function field is associated
a non-negative invariant
g
called its
genus
and the Riemann–Roch Theorem asserts that

\dim_{K}{\mathcal{L}}(D)\geqslant\deg D+1-g

with equality when
\deg D>2g-2
.

A_{i}=S_{0}
, satisfying condition
3
.
Next, we change the current value of
s
to a value
s\in\{s_{2},\ldots,s_{\kappa}\}
such
that
\mathbf{A}_{i}s\not\subset LS
. Such an
s
exists because
otherwise
LS\mathbf{A}_{i}\subset LS
and
LS
would be stabilised by

\mathbf{A}_{i}
, implying that the stabiliser of
LS^{2}
would not be
L
but a
field containing
\mathbf{A}_{i}
. We now set

\mathbf{A}_{i+1}=\{\mathbf{a}\in\mathbf{A}_{i},\mathbf{a}s\in LS\}\subsetneq\mathbf{A}_{i}
and take
\mathbf{B}_{i+1}
to be any
complementary subspace of
\mathbf{A}_{i+1}
in
\mathbf{A}_{i}
. Finally we set

\mathbf{C}_{i+1}=\mathbf{B}_{i}\oplus\mathbf{C}_{i}
, so that

LS=\mathbf{A}_{i+1}\oplus\mathbf{B}_{i+1}\oplus\mathbf{C}_{i+1}
.

Note that
L\subset\mathbf{A}_{i+1}\subsetneq\mathbf{A}_{i}
, and that
b_{i+1}\geqslant 1
.
By definition of
A_{i+1}^{+}
, we have
A_{i+1}^{+}=A_{i}
: since
A_{i}
does not
satisfy condition
3
, we have

\dim_{K}A_{i+1}^{+}>\dim_{K}S_{0}+(a_{i}-1)\ell.

From Proposition
4.1
,
\dim_{K}S_{0}\geqslant\ell+1-\tau
and, from (
24
),

\dim_{K}S=\kappa\ell+1-\tau
. Thus,

\dim_{K}A_{i+1}^{+}>\dim_{K}S-(\kappa-a_{i})\ell.

But
\kappa-a_{i}=b_{i}+c_{i}=c_{i+1}
: thus, the new triple

(\mathbf{A}_{i+1},\mathbf{B}_{i+1},\mathbf{C}_{i+1})
satisfies
condition
4
. Conditions
1
and
2
are naturally satisfied by construction,
therefore we again have a triple satisfying all conditions of the Lemma except possibly for condition
3
.

As already observed, condition
3
is
trivially satisfied when
\mathbf{A}_{i}=L
. We have therefore constructed a sequence of triples

(\mathbf{A}_{i},\mathbf{B}_{i},\mathbf{C}_{i})
that, since the
\mathbf{A}_{i}
are strictly decreasing,
must eventually lead to
L
-spaces
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}
and an
element
s
satisfying all conditions of the lemma.
∎

From now on we let
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C}
and
s\in S
be fixed
as guaranteed by Lemma
5.3
.

5.3
Studying
S_{0}S
and
S_{0}s\cap S

Let us write
S_{0}=S_{0}^{\prime}\oplus S_{0}^{\prime\prime}

where
S_{0}^{\prime}
is the subspace of
S_{0}
consisting of the elements
y
such
that
ys\in(S+xS)
, i.e.
S_{0}^{\prime}=S_{0}\cap s^{-1}(S+xS)
, and

S_{0}^{\prime\prime}
is any complementary subspace of
S_{0}^{\prime}
in
S_{0}
.
Since
1,x\in S_{0}
we have
S_{0}S\supset(S+xS)
, and we also have
S_{0}S\supset S_{0}^{\prime\prime}s
. By definition of
S_{0}^{\prime\prime}
we have
S_{0}^{\prime\prime}s\cap(S+xS)=\{0\}
, therefore

\dim_{K}S_{0}S\geqslant\dim_{K}(S+xS)+\dim_{K}S_{0}^{\prime\prime}.

(29)

Note that
1,x\in S_{0}^{\prime}
. Let
T_{0}
be the subspace of elements of
S_{0}^{\prime}
whose valuation at
Q_{\infty}
is larger than
-N=v_{\infty}(x)
. We clearly have
S_{0}^{\prime}=T_{0}\oplus Kx
, so that
\dim_{K}T_{0}=\dim_{K}S_{0}^{\prime}-1
.

Lemma 5.4
.

We have
T_{0}s\subset S.

Proof.

We have
T_{0}\subset S_{0}^{\prime}
and by definition of
S_{0}^{\prime}
we have have therefore
T_{0}s\subset S+xS
.
Suppose there exists
\lambda\in T_{0}
such that
\lambda s\not\in S
.
Then, since
\lambda s\in S+xS
, there must be some
y\in S
such that
v_{Q_{\infty}}(\lambda s)=v_{Q_{\infty}}(yx)
, i.e.

v_{Q_{\infty}}(\lambda)+v_{Q_{\infty}}(s)=v_{Q_{\infty}}(y)+v_{Q_{\infty}}(x).

(30)

Since
x,\lambda\in L
, their valuations at
Q_{\infty}
are in
d{\mathbb{Z}}
, so we have

v_{Q_{\infty}}(y)=v_{Q_{\infty}}(s)\bmod d
. Now recall from Lemma
5.3

that
s
is chosen to be one of the elements
s_{1},\ldots,s_{\kappa}
and therefore
(from the end of Section
5.1
)

v_{Q_{\infty}}(s)
is maximal within its class modulo
d
. In other words we have
v_{Q_{\infty}}(y)\leqslant v_{Q_{\infty}}(s)
.
But the definition of
T_{0}
implies
v_{Q_{\infty}}(x)<v_{Q_{\infty}}(\lambda)
, so we have a contradiction with (
30
).
∎

Since
A^{+}\subset S
, Lemma
5.4
implies
T_{0}sA^{+}\subset S^{2}
, and we have

S^{2}\supset S_{0}S+T_{0}sA^{+}.

(31)

We will study the sum
S_{0}S+T_{0}sA^{+}
and its dimension, aiming to derive a contradiction
with the Freiman hypothesis (
23
). More precisely, since
A^{+}\subset\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}
, by condition
1
of Lemma
5.3
we have
A^{+}s\subset LS+\mathbf{B}s
: and since
T_{0}\subset L
and
LS+\mathbf{B}s
is
L
-linear,
we have

T_{0}sA^{+}\subset LS+\mathbf{B}s,

(32)

and finally, since
S_{0}\subset L
, we also have
S_{0}S+T_{0}sA^{+}\subset LS+\mathbf{B}s
.
Therefore,

\dim_{L}L(S_{0}S+T_{0}sA^{+})\leqslant\kappa+b

(recall that
b=\dim_{L}\mathbf{B}
).
Applying Lemma
5.1
to (
31
), we therefore get:

\dim_{K}S^{2}\geqslant\dim_{K}(S_{0}S+T_{0}sA^{+})+(\kappa-1-b)\ell.

(33)

We will find a contradiction with the Freiman hypothesis as soon as we
show that

\dim_{K}(S_{0}S+T_{0}sA^{+})\geqslant 3\dim_{K}S-3-(\kappa-1-b)\ell,

which is now our objective. The dimension of
S_{0}S+T_{0}sA^{+}
will be bounded from below by the sum of two terms:
the dimension of
S_{0}S
given by (
29
) and the contribution of elements of
T_{0}sA^{+}
that
“fall outside of
LS
”, in other words, those that have a nonzero
component in
\mathbf{B}s
.

5.4
The lower bound on
S_{0}S+T_{0}sA^{+}
that contradicts the Freiman hypothesis

Consider the projection map

\Phi\penalty\ :\penalty\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}LS\oplus\mathbf{B}s&\longrightarrow&\mathbf{B}s\\
\bm{\sigma}+\mathbf{b}s&\longmapsto&\mathbf{b}s\end{array}\right.

where
\bm{\sigma}
and
\mathbf{b}
are arbitrary elements of
LS

and
\mathbf{B}
respectively. The following lemma is a simple remark, but is a key to the argument to follow.

Lemma 5.5
.

For any finite-dimensional
K
-subspace
V
of
LS+\mathbf{B}s
and any finite-dimensional
K
-subspace
U
of

L
, we have
\Phi(UV)=U\Phi(V)
.

Proof.

This is a simple application of the
L
-linearity of the map
\Phi
.
∎

All dimensions below are
K
-dimensions. Let us write

\displaystyle\dim(S_{0}S+T_{0}sA^{+})

\displaystyle\geqslant\dim S_{0}S+\dim T_{0}sA^{+}-\dim(S_{0}S\cap T_{0}sA^{+})

\displaystyle\geqslant\dim S_{0}S+\dim T_{0}sA^{+}-\dim(LS\cap T_{0}sA^{+})

(34)

since
S_{0}S\subset LS
. From (
32
) and the rank-nullity theorem we have

\dim T_{0}sA^{+}=\dim\Phi(T_{0}sA^{+})+\dim(\ker\Phi\cap T_{0}sA^{+}).

Therefore, since
LS=\ker\Phi
, inequality (
34
) yields

\dim(S_{0}S+T_{0}sA^{+})\geqslant\dim S_{0}S+\dim\Phi(T_{0}sA^{+}).

Applying Lemma
5.5
we therefore have, since
T_{0}\subset L
,

\dim(S_{0}S+T_{0}sA^{+})\geqslant\dim S_{0}S+\dim T_{0}\Phi(sA^{+}).

(35)

Since
A^{+}s\subset LS+\mathbf{B}s
, applying again the rank-nullity theorem,

\displaystyle\dim\Phi(A^{+}s)

\displaystyle=\dim A^{+}s-\dim(\ker\Phi\cap A^{+}s)

\displaystyle=\dim A^{+}-\dim(LS\cap A^{+}s).

But from the definition of
A^{+}=S\cap(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B})
and conditions
1
and
2
of Lemma
5.3
,
we have
LS\cap A^{+}s=As
. Therefore,

\dim\Phi(A^{+}s)=\dim A^{+}-\dim A.

Lemma
5.2
now gives us the “Cauchy-Davenport inequality”:

\displaystyle\dim T_{0}\Phi(sA^{+})

\displaystyle\geqslant\dim T_{0}+\dim\Phi(A^{+}s)-1

\displaystyle\geqslant\dim T_{0}+\dim A^{+}-\dim A-1

by the above computation of
\dim\Phi(A^{+}s)
.
Substituting into inequality (
35
) we therefore have:

\dim(S_{0}S+T_{0}sA^{+})\geqslant\dim S_{0}S+\dim T_{0}+\dim A^{+}-\dim A-1.

Applying (
29
) we now obtain:

\displaystyle\dim(S_{0}S+T_{0}sA^{+})

\displaystyle\geqslant\dim(S+xS)+\dim S_{0}^{\prime\prime}+\dim T_{0}+\dim A^{+}-\dim A-1

\displaystyle=\dim(S+xS)+\dim S_{0}^{\prime\prime}+\dim S_{0}^{\prime}+\dim A^{+}-\dim A-2

\displaystyle=\dim(S+xS)+\dim S_{0}+\dim A^{+}-\dim A-2

where we have used
S_{0}=S_{0}^{\prime}\oplus S_{0}^{\prime\prime}
and
\dim T_{0}=\dim S_{0}^{\prime}-1
.
Applying conditions
3
and
4
from Lemma
5.3
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\dim(S_{0}S+T_{0}sA^{+})

\displaystyle\geqslant\dim(S+xS)-2+\dim S-c\ell-(a-1)\ell

\displaystyle\geqslant 2\dim S-1-2+\dim S-(\kappa-1-b)\ell

\displaystyle=3\dim S-3-(\kappa-1-b)\ell

where we have used
\dim_{L}LS=\kappa=a+b+c
.
Finally, substituting into (
33
) gives the desired contradiction
with the Freiman hypothesis (
23
). This concludes the proof of Theorem
1
for algebraically closed fields
K
.

6
When
K
is not algebraically closed

Once Theorem
1
is proved in the special case of an algebraically closed ground field
K
, there remains to extend it to the more general case a perfect ground field
K
. Suppose that
K
is perfect and algebraically closed
in
F
. The proof to follow is in the same spirit as that of
[
BCZ18
, § 9]
.
Denote by
\overline{K}
the algebraic closure of
K
. Since
K
is algebraically closed in
F
,
the compositum
F^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\overline{K}F
is a function field over
\overline{K}
. Moreover, from
[
Sti09
, Prop. 3.6.1(b)]
,

\dim_{\overline{K}}\overline{K}T=\dim_{K}T
for any
K
-subspace
T
, so that we have

\dim_{\overline{K}}\overline{K}S=\dim_{K}S

(36)

and also

\dim_{\overline{K}}\overline{K}S^{2}=2\dim_{\overline{K}}\overline{K}S-1+\gamma.

Theorem
1
for algebraically closed fields therefore applies, so that

F^{\prime}
has genus
g\leqslant\gamma
and the least divisor
D^{\prime}
of
F^{\prime}
such that

\overline{K}S\subset{\mathcal{L}}_{F^{\prime}}(D^{\prime})
satisfies

\dim_{\overline{K}}{\mathcal{L}}_{F^{\prime}}(D^{\prime})-\dim_{\overline{K}}S\leqslant\gamma-g.

(37)

From
[
Sti09
, Thm. 3.6.3(b)]
,
F^{\prime}
has the same genus as
F
which yields the upper
bound on the genus. Let
D
be the least divisor of
F
such that
S\subset{\mathcal{L}}_{F}(D)
, there remains to evaluate the codimension of
S
in
{\mathcal{L}}_{F}(D)
.
Following the lines of
[
BCZ18
, Thm. 9.2]
,
one proves easily that
D^{\prime}
is nothing but the
conorm
(also called the
pullback
)
of
D
in
F^{\prime}
(see
[
Sti09
, Def. 3.1.8]
) and, from
[
Sti09
, Thm. 3.6.3(d)]
,

\dim_{\overline{K}}{\mathcal{L}}_{F^{\prime}}(D^{\prime})=\dim_{K}{\mathcal{L}}_{F}(D).

(38)

Therefore, (
36
) and (
38
) together with (
37
)
permits to conclude.

Remark 6.1
.

Note that the assumption “
K
is perfect” can be relaxed to “any finite extension of
K
is primitive”. This is indeed the only requirement for the above argument to hold. Therefore, Theorem
1
remains true, for example, when
K
is a one variable function field over
a finite field.
