Environment-induced entanglement generation for two qubits in the presence of qubit-qubit interaction noise Preprint: APS/123-QED
Muhammad Abdullah Mutahar, Adam Zaman Chaudhry

Abstract
Using an exactly solvable pure dephasing model, we show how entanglement between qubits can be generated via the interaction with a common environment and concurrent application of suitable control pulses. The control pulses are able to effectively remove the detrimental effect of the environment while preserving the indirect interaction between the qubits, thereby leading to the generation of near-perfect entanglement. Furthermore, we also investigate the entanglement dynamics if the qubits are directly interacting; this interaction may even contain a noise term. The present of this additional noise leads to an additional decoherence term. This decoherence term cannot be removed by applying the pulses at the same time to both qubits. Rather, we show that by introducing a time delay between the two pulse sequences, near-perfect entanglement can still be generated via the interaction with the common environment.



I
Introduction

Entanglement is a fundamental concept in quantum mechanics, and one of the key resources in quantum information processing
Nielsen 2000
;
Bennett 1992
;
Bennett et al. 1993
;
Schumacher and Westmoreland 1998
. It finds many practical applications in quantum computation and communication such as quantum cryptography
Ekert 1992
;
Deutsch et al. 1996
and superdense coding
Bennett and Wiesner 1992
. Unfortunately, quantum resources such as entanglement and coherence are very delicate - realistic quantum systems inevitably interact with their surrounding environments, deteriorating these resources very quickly as a result of the decoherence process
Zurek 2003
;
Schlosshauer 2007
;
Breuer et al. 2002
. Consequently, the environment has largely been considered very problematic for quantum technologies. However, with the advent of ‘reservoir engineering’, it has been shown that the system-environment interaction can instead be used to generate useful quantum resources
Zagoskin et al. 2006
;
Neeley et al. 2008
;
Murch et al. 2012
;
Cirac et al. 1993
;
Poyatos et al. 1996
;
Carvalho et al. 2001
. This is carried out by tuning the system-environment coupling and properties of the environment. These techniques have been used experimentally to generate superposition states in superconducting circuits
Shankar et al. 2013
, atomic ensembles
Krauter et al. 2011
, and trapped ions
Barreiro et al. 2011
;
Lin et al. 2013
.

Of key interest to us in this work is the fact that two independent qubits interacting with a common environment can become entangled
Orszag and Hernandez 2010
;
Braun 2002
;
Dajka and Łuczka 2008
. This environment-induced entanglement has several applications, including, but not limited to, quantum control of two-dimensional quantum systems
Romano and D’Alessandro 2006
, entanglement of two-mode squeezed states
An and Zhang 2007
;
Paz and Roncaglia 2009
, and entanglement of charged qubits
Contreras-Pulido and Aguado 2008
. Therefore, it is worthwhile to optimize and enhance this environment-induced entanglement. Attention has been directed towards optimising the properties of the environment to enhance the induced entanglement dynamics
Chaudhry et al. 2015
. On the other hand, considerable work has been done to mitigate the detrimental influence of the environment. Inspired by the technique of dynamical decoupling that uses suitable control fields to remove the effect of the environment
Addis et al. 2014
;
Viola and Lloyd 1998
;
Viola et al. 1999
;
Viola et al. 2000
;
Wu and Lidar 2002
;
Yang et al. 2011
;
Stollsteimer and Mahler 2001
;
Viola and Knill 2003
;
Wocjan 2006
;
Gordon et al. 2008
;
Wu et al. 2009
;
Uhrig 2007a
;
Chaudhry and Gong 2012a
;
Chaudhry and Gong 2012b
, in this paper we investigate the effect of pulse sequences on the entanglement dynamics of two qubits interacting with a common environment. Our hope is that the applied control pulses would be such that the decoherence of the two qubits is highly mitigated while largely leaving the entanglement generating influence of the environment untouched.

We start by considering a system of two independent qubits, initially
in a pure product state, interacting with a common environment. This environment is modeled as a
collection of harmonic oscillators. As the dephasing time is much shorter than the relaxation time scale, we restrict our model to the pure dephasing case. After working out the exact two-qubit dynamics in the presence of pulses, we show that near-perfect entanglement can be generated via the application of pulses. We then further modify our system to include a direct noisy interaction between the two qubits. We show that, as a result of the noise in the interaction between the qubits, an additional decoherence term emerges. Interestingly, to mitigate the influence of this additional term on the entanglement, the pulses cannot be applied at the same time to both qubits. Rather, there must be a time delay. By incorporating such a time-delay, we show how near-perfect entanglement can still be generated.

This paper is organised in the following way. In Sec.
II
, we introduce our basic model and work out its dynamics. In Sec.
III
, we work out the entanglement dynamics to show the near-perfect generation of entanglement with the application of pulses. Then, in Sec.
IV
, we modify our Hamiltonian to accommodate for the qubits interacting directly with each other. This interaction term contains a classical stochastic variable depicting a noisy interaction. We conclude our findings in Sec.
V
, with some details deferred to the appendix.

II
The model and its dynamics

We begin with two qubits interacting with a common bosonic environment. The total system-environment Hamiltonian is then given by (we use
\hbar=1
throughout)

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=H_{S}+H_{B}+H_{I}+H_{C}

(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{0}}{2}\left(\sigma_{z}^{(1)}+\sigma_{z}^{(2)}\right)+\sum_{k}\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}

\displaystyle+\left(\sigma_{z}^{(1)}+\sigma_{z}^{(2)}\right)\sum_{k}\left(g_{k}^{*}b_{k}+g_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle+H_{C}^{(1)}+H_{C}^{(2)}.

Here, the superscripts
(1)
and
(2)
refer to the first qubit and the second qubit,
\omega_{0}
is the spacing between the energy states of a two-level system (assumed to be the same for both qubits),
\sigma_{z}
is the standard Pauli matrix, while
H_{B}=\sum_{k}\omega_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{k}
represents the common bosonic environment composed of a collection of harmonic oscillators. The third term describes the qubit-environment interaction for the qubits, while the last terms,
H_{C}^{(1)}
and
H_{C}^{(2)}
, represent the control fields (pulses) applied to the first and the second qubit respectively. We note that our system Hamiltonian is restricted to the pure-dephasing case of the widely known spin-boson model for two qubits
Palma et al. 1996
;
Reina et al. 2002
. This restriction is allowed if we consider the dynamics within a time scale much shorter than the relaxation
time-scale of the system.

To solve the dynamics, we first consider the free time-evolution operator
U_{0}(t)=\text{exp}[-i(H_{S}+H_{B})t]
. The Hamiltonian in the interaction picture is then defined as

\displaystyle H_{I}(t)

\displaystyle=U_{0}^{\dagger}(t)H_{I}U_{0}(t)+U_{0}^{\dagger}(t)H_{C}^{(1)}U_{0}(t)

(2)

\displaystyle+U_{0}^{\dagger}(t)H_{C}^{(2)}U_{0}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\sigma_{z}^{(1)}+\sigma_{z}^{(2)}\right)\sum_{k}\left(g_{k}^{*}b_{k}e^{-i\omega_{k}t}+g_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}e^{i\omega_{k}t}\right)

\displaystyle+H_{C}^{(1)}(t)+H_{C}^{(2)}(t).

It is useful to move to the toggling frame of the pulses applied on the qubits.
Assuming that
H_{C}^{(1)}(t)
and
H_{C}^{(2)}(t)
generate a series of instantaneous
\pi
rotations of the qubits around an axis orthogonal to the
z
-axis at times
t_{k}
, where
0\leq k\leq n+1
with
n
being the number of pulses applied and
t_{0}=0,t_{n+1}=t
, the interaction Hamiltonian in the toggling frame of the pulses takes the form

\displaystyle H_{T}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(s^{(1)}(t)\sigma_{z}^{(1)}+Z^{(2)}(t)\sigma_{z}^{(2)}\right)

(3)

\displaystyle\times\sum_{k}\left(g_{k}^{*}b_{k}e^{-i\omega_{k}t}+g_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}e^{i\omega_{k}t}\right),

where

\displaystyle Z^{(1)}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}s^{(1)}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime},

\displaystyle Z^{(2)}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}s^{(2)}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}.

As the role of the pulses can essentially be reduced to flipping the sign of
\sigma_{z}
, we have attached a switching function
s(t)
above for both the qubits, and the superscript is there to remind us that the applied pulse sequence need not be the same for both qubits. The switching function is

s(t^{\prime})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}(-1)^{k}\Theta\left(t_{k+1}-t^{\prime}\right)\Theta\left(t^{\prime}-t_{k}\right),

(4)

where
\Theta(t)
is the Heaviside step function. We further denote the times at which pulses are applied
t_{k}
as
t_{k}=\delta_{k}t
with
0\leq\delta_{k}\leq 1
, where
k=1,\ldots,n
.
Next, we move on to evaluating the unitary time evolution operator corresponding to
H_{T}(t)
. This is found to be

\displaystyle U_{I}(t)

\displaystyle=\text{exp}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{2}A_{i}\right].

(5)

Here

\displaystyle A_{1}(t)=

\displaystyle-i\sigma_{z}^{(1)}\sum_{k}\left[\phi^{*(1)}_{k}(t)g_{k}^{*}b_{k}+\phi^{(1)}_{k}(t)g_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}\right]

(6)

\displaystyle-i\sigma_{z}^{(2)}\sum_{k}\left[\phi^{*(2)}_{k}(t)g_{k}^{*}b_{k}+\phi^{(2)}_{k}(t)g_{k}b_{k}^{\dagger}\right],

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\sigma_{z}^{(1)}\chi_{1}(t)-i\sigma_{z}^{(2)}\chi_{2}(t),

where

\displaystyle\phi_{k}^{(1)}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}s^{(1)}(t^{\prime})e^{i\omega_{k}t^{\prime}},

(7)

\displaystyle\phi_{k}^{(2)}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{t}dt^{\prime}s^{(2)}(t^{\prime})e^{i\omega_{k}t^{\prime}},

and
\chi_{1}(t)
and
\chi_{2}(t)
are defined using Eq. (
6
). We work out
A_{2}(t)
to be

\displaystyle A_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=iB_{1}(t)+iB_{2}(t)+iB_{3}(t)\sigma_{z}^{(1)}\sigma_{z}^{(2)}

(8)

\displaystyle+iB_{4}(t)\sigma_{z}^{(1)}\sigma_{z}^{(2)},

with

\displaystyle B_{1}(t)=

\displaystyle i\sum_{k}\left|g_{k}\right|^{2}

(9)

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}s^{(1)}(t_{1})s^{(1)}(t_{2})\sin[\omega_{k}(t_{1}-t_{2})],

\displaystyle B_{2}(t)=

\displaystyle i\sum_{k}\left|g_{k}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}s^{(2)}(t_{1})s^{(2)}(t_{2})\sin[\omega_{k}(t_{1}-t_{2})],

\displaystyle B_{3}(t)=

\displaystyle i\sum_{k}\left|g_{k}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}s^{(1)}(t_{1})s^{(2)}(t_{2})\sin[\omega_{k}(t_{1}-t_{2})],

\displaystyle B_{4}(t)=

\displaystyle i\sum_{k}\left|g_{k}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}s^{(2)}(t_{1})s^{(1)}(t_{2})\sin[\omega_{k}(t_{1}-t_{2})].

The full unitary time evolution operator corresponding to the Hamiltonian in the toggling frame of the control fields then becomes

\displaystyle U(t)

\displaystyle=\text{exp}\left[\frac{-i\omega_{0}}{2}\left(Z^{(1)}(t)\sigma_{z}^{(1)}+Z^{(2)}(t)\sigma_{z}^{(2)}\right)\right]

(10)

\displaystyle\times\text{exp}\left[-iH_{B}t\right]

\displaystyle\times\text{exp}\left[-i\sigma_{z}^{(1)}\chi_{1}(t)\right]\text{exp}\left[-i\sigma_{z}^{(2)}\chi_{2}(t)\right]

\displaystyle\times\text{exp}\left[i\left(B_{1}(t)+B_{2}(t)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\text{exp}\left[i\left(B_{3}(t)+B_{4}(t)\right)\sigma_{z}^{(1)}\sigma_{z}^{(2)}\right].

We now proceed to compute the dynamics of the system in the density matrix formalism, given any arbitrary system-environment initial state. For simplicity we consider the initial state to be a product state of any qubit-system state with the environment in a thermal bath state

\displaystyle\rho(0)

\displaystyle=\rho_{S}(0)\otimes\rho_{B},

(11)

\displaystyle\rho_{B}

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{exp}\left[-\beta H_{B}\right]}{Z_{B}},

\displaystyle Z_{B}

\displaystyle=\text{Tr}_{B}[e^{-\beta H_{B}}].

Here,
\beta
is the inverse temperature and
Z_{B}
is the partition function of the environment.
\text{Tr}_{B}[\hskip 5.69054pt]
represents the trace over the environment states. Given the initial system-environment state, we can work out the state at a later time
t
via
\rho(t)=U(t)\rho(0)U^{\dagger}(t)
. We do so in the basis
\ket{k,l}
given by

\displaystyle\sigma_{z}^{(1)}\ket{k,l}

\displaystyle=k\ket{k,l},

\displaystyle\sigma_{z}^{(2)}\ket{k,l}

\displaystyle=l\ket{k,l}.

It is also useful to define

\displaystyle\bra{k',l'}\rho_{S}(t)\ket{k,l}

\displaystyle=\left[\rho_{S}(t)\right]_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime};k,l}=\text{Tr}_{S}[\rho_{S}(t)P_{kl,k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}],

(12)

where
P_{kl,k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}=\ket{k,l}\bra{k',l'}
, and
\rho_{S}(t)=\text{Tr}_{B}[\rho(t)]
is the density matrix of the two qubits only. Now, it is easy to show that
\left[\rho_{S}(t)\right]_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime};k,l}=\text{Tr}_{S+B}\left[U^{\dagger}(t)P_{kl,k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}U(t)\rho(0)\right]
, where
\text{Tr}_{S+B}
is the total trace over both system and environment. Using the expression for the total time-evolution operator already worked out, we find that

\displaystyle\left[\rho_{S}(t)\right]_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime};k,l}

\displaystyle=\left[\rho_{S}(0)\right]_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime};k,l}

(13)

\displaystyle\times\text{exp}\left[\frac{i\omega_{0}}{2}\left(k\hskip 1.42262ptZ^{(1)}(t)+l\hskip 1.42262ptZ^{(2)}(t)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\text{exp}\left[\frac{-i\omega_{0}}{2}\left(k^{\prime}\hskip 1.42262ptZ^{(1)}(t)+l^{\prime}\hskip 1.42262ptZ^{(2)}(t)\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\text{exp}\left[-iB(t)\left(kl-k^{\prime}l^{\prime}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\text{Tr}_{B}\left[e^{iR_{kl,k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}}\rho_{B}\right].

Here
B(t)=B_{3}(t)+B_{4}(t)
and
R_{kl,k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}=(k-k^{\prime})\chi_{1}(t)+(l-l^{\prime})\chi_{2}(t)
. To further evaluate the trace in the last line above, we use the identity
\text{Tr}_{B}[e^{A}\rho_{B}]=e^{\text{Tr}_{B}[A^{2}\rho_{B}]/2}
, leading to

\text{Tr}_{B}\left[e^{iR_{kl,k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}}\rho_{B}\right]=\text{exp}\left[-\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}_{B}\left[\left(R_{kl,k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\rho_{B}\right]\right],

(14)

with

\displaystyle\text{Tr}_{B}

\displaystyle\left[\left(R_{kl,k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\rho_{B}\right]=

(15)

\displaystyle(k-k^{\prime})^{2}\sum_{r}\left|\phi_{r}^{(1)}(t)\right|^{2}\left|g_{r}\right|^{2}\left(2n_{r}+1\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle(l-l^{\prime})^{2}\sum_{r}\left|\phi_{r}^{(2)}(t)\right|^{2}\left|g_{r}\right|^{2}\left(2n_{r}+1\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2(k-k^{\prime})(l-l^{\prime})\sum_{r}\phi_{r}^{*(1)}(t)\hskip 2.84526pt\phi_{r}^{(2)}(t)\left|g_{r}\right|^{2}\left(n_{r}+1\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle 2(k-k^{\prime})(l-l^{\prime})\sum_{r}\phi_{r}^{(1)}(t)\hskip 2.84526pt\phi_{r}^{*(2)}(t)\left|g_{r}\right|^{2}n_{r}.

Note that
\text{Tr}_{B}\left[b_{r}^{\dagger}b_{r}\rho_{B}\right]=n_{r}=1/(e^{\beta\omega_{r}}-1)
, which is Bose-Einstein distribution since the environment is in thermal equilibrium.

Until this point we have kept the formalism arbitrary with respect to the pulses. The purpose for keeping it arbitrary, as we would see later, is to use this formalism to analyze the more complicated problem where a ‘noisy’ qubit-qubit interaction is present as well. However, for our current purpose, we now assume that both qubits are subjected to the same pulse sequence. This would imply
s^{(1)}(t)=s^{(2)}(t)=s(t)
,
Z^{(1)}(t)=Z^{(2)}(t)=Z(t)
,
\phi_{r}^{(1)}(t)=\phi_{r}^{(2)}(t)=\phi_{r}(t)
and
B_{3}(t)=B_{4}(t)=D(t)
. By this assumption, Eq. (
15
) becomes

\text{Tr}_{B}\left[\left(R_{kl,k^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\right)^{2}\rho_{B}\right]=\\
\left(k+l-k^{\prime}-l^{\prime}\right)^{2}\sum_{r}\left|\phi_{r}(t)\right|^{2}\left|g_{r}\right|^{2}\coth\left(\frac{\beta\omega_{r}}{2}\right).

It is useful to further express our formalism in terms of filter functions
Cywiński et al. 2008
. The action of a pulse sequence is captured by its filter function which is essentially in terms of the Fourier transform of
s(t)
. The filter function is defined as

F(\omega t)=\frac{\omega^{2}}{2}\left|\int_{0}^{t}s(t^{\prime})e^{i\omega t^{\prime}}dt^{\prime}\right|^{2}.

(16)

At this point, the spectral density
J(\omega)
is also introduced via the substitution

\sum_{r}4\left|g_{r}\right|^{2}\rightarrow\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega J(\omega)\delta\left(\omega_{r}-\omega\right).

(17)

This replaces the discrete summation with a continuous spectrum of environment frequencies
\omega
. The spectral density
J(\omega)
is typically expressed using a power law, i.e
J(\omega)=g\frac{\omega^{s}}{\omega_{c}^{s-1}}G(\omega,\omega_{c})
, where
g
is the system-environment coupling strength,
s
is the Ohmicity parameter, and
G(\omega,\omega_{c})
is the cutoff function with
\omega_{c}
being the cutoff frequency. For the sake of simplicity, we choose to work in the Ohmic (
s=1
) regime
Weiss 2012
;
Breuer et al. 2002
. Similarly, although different varieties of cutoff functions are used in literature
Breuer et al. 2002
, for simplicity, we would stick to the exponential cutoff function, that is,
G(\omega,\omega_{c})=\text{exp}\left[-\omega/\omega_{c}\right]
.

In summary, putting everything together, we finally have

\displaystyle\left[\rho_{S}(t)\right]_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime};k,l}

\displaystyle=\left[\rho_{S}(0)\right]_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime};k,l}

(18)

\displaystyle\times\text{exp}\left[\frac{-i\omega_{0}Z(t)}{2}\left(k^{\prime}+l^{\prime}-k-l\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\text{exp}\left[\frac{iD(t)}{2}\left(k^{\prime}l^{\prime}-kl\right)\right]

\displaystyle\times\text{exp}\left[-\frac{\gamma(t)}{4}\left(k+l-k^{\prime}-l^{\prime}\right)^{2}\right],

where

\displaystyle D(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega J(\omega)\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\hskip 2.84526pts(t_{1})

\displaystyle\times\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\hskip 2.84526pts(t_{2})\sin\left[\omega\left(t_{1}-t_{2}\right)\right],

\displaystyle\gamma(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega J(\omega)\frac{F\left(\omega t\right)}{\omega^{2}}\coth\left(\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\right).

III
Generating entanglement

As stated in the introduction, our goal is to analyze the entanglement generation of two qubits under various pulse sequences and evaluate if the pulse sequences can possibly enhance the entanglement generation of the two qubits as compared to the qubits interacting with the common environment without any pulses applied. Let us try to interpret Eq. (
18
). The first factor containing
\text{exp}\left[-i\omega_{0}Z(t)\left(k^{\prime}+l^{\prime}-k-l\right)/2\right]
describes the free evolution of the qubits in the presence of the pulses. The second factor, that is, containing
\text{exp}\left[\frac{iD(t)}{2}\left(k^{\prime}l^{\prime}-kl\right)\right]
describes the indirect interaction of the qubits, modulated by the pulses, due to the common bosonic environment. It is this interaction that generates the entanglement, so our goal would be to preserve this interaction as much as we could. The third term, namely
\left[-\frac{\gamma(t)}{4}\left(k+l-k^{\prime}-l^{\prime}\right)^{2}\right]
, represents the decoherence factor
\gamma(t)
. We would try to minimize this term as much as we can by applying control pulses with short pulse spacing - this, of course, is the idea behind dynamical decoupling. Essentially, the decoherence rate depends on the overlap between the spectral density and the filter function; if pulses are applied rapidly, this overlap becomes very small, leading to a very small decoherence rate. However, the indirect interaction can still survive.

Let us then analyze the functions
D(t)
and
\gamma(t)
in more detail. Starting with
D(t)
,

\displaystyle D(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\hskip 2.84526ptJ(\omega)\hskip 2.84526ptd(\omega,t),

where

\displaystyle d(\omega,t)=\int_{0}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{0}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\hskip 2.84526pts(t_{1})s(t_{2})\sin\left[\omega\left(t_{1}-t_{2}\right)\right].

For
n
pulses, we denote
d(\omega,t)
by
d_{n}(\omega,t)
, which can be simplified to
Tan et al. 2014

\displaystyle d_{n}(\omega,t)=\vartheta(\omega,t)+\nu(\omega,t)-t/\omega,

with

\displaystyle\vartheta(\omega,t)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\omega^{2}}\left[2\sum_{m=1}^{n}(-1)^{m}\sin(\omega t_{m})+(-1)^{n+2}\sin(\omega t)\right],

\displaystyle\nu(\omega,t)=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\omega^{2}}\Biggl\{\sum_{m=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(-1)^{m+j}\left(\sin\left[\omega\left(t_{m+1}-t_{j}\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle-\sin\left[\omega\left(t_{m}-t_{j}\right)\right]\Biggl\}.

For
\vartheta(\omega,t)
, if we assume a very large number of pulses (
n>>1
), we see that the first term effectively approaches zero since it is rapidly switching sign, and, in the high pulse limit,
t_{m+1}
approaches
\approx t_{m}
. The same logic applies to
\nu(\omega,t)
. We are then left with a very simple
d(\omega,t)
in the high pulse limit. Coming towards
\gamma(t)
, it is useful to first define
F_{1}(\omega)=J(\omega)\coth(\beta\omega/2)
, and
F_{2}(\omega)=F(\omega t_{0})/\omega^{2}
. We can then rewrite
\gamma(t)
as

\displaystyle\gamma(t_{0})=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega F_{1}(\omega)F_{2}(\omega).

(19)

It can be seen that the maximum contribution to the integral occurs where the functions
F_{1}(\omega)
and
F_{2}(\omega)
overlap the most. The effect of increasing the number of pulses
n
can essentially be understood as the shifting of the peak of the filter function and hence of
F_{2}(\omega)
towards the side of increasing
\omega
. As
F_{1}(\omega)
is an exponentially decreasing function after
\omega=\omega_{c}
, the overlap of both functions keeps on decreasing as we increase the number of pulses. This also suggests in the high pulse limit (
n>>1
),
\gamma(t)\rightarrow 0
. Therefore, we can expect to get negligible decoherence but significant entanglement due to the indirect interaction encapsulated by
D(t)
.

We now numerically check our claim regarding the generation of entanglement. First, to compute the dynamics of entanglement between the two qubits, we the use concurrence
C(t)

Wootters 1998
. It is defined via the Hermitian matrix

such that

\displaystyle V=\sqrt{\sqrt{\rho_{S}}\hskip 2.84526pt\widetilde{\rho_{S}}\hskip 2.84526pt\sqrt{\rho_{S}}},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho_{S}}=\left(\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y}\right)\rho_{S}^{*}\left(\sigma_{y}\otimes\sigma_{y}\right).

C(\rho_{S})=\text{max}\left[0,\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{4}\right].

The

Now, in order to see the dynamics of entanglement, we should start with the initial system state to be a product state of the two qubits, and then we would analyse if and how the environment generates entanglement in between those qubits. Furthermore, the initial two-qubit state should not be part of a decoherence-free subspace. We begin our analysis by choosing the initial system state to be
\rho_{S}(t)=\ket{+,+}\bra{+,+}
where
\sigma_{x}\ket{+}=\ket{+}

is then given by

\rho_{S}(t)=\frac{1}{4}\begin{pmatrix}1&e^{-i\omega_{0}Z(t)}e^{-\gamma(t)+iD(t)}&e^{-i\omega_{0}Z(t)}e^{-\gamma(t)+iD(t)}&e^{-4\gamma(t)-2i\omega_{0}Z(t)}\\
e^{i\omega_{0}Z(t)}e^{-\gamma(t)-iD(t)}&1&1&e^{-i\omega_{0}Z(t)}e^{-\gamma(t)-iD(t)}\\

We now investigate concurrence
C(t)

V
Conclusion

We started with a two-qubit system interacting with a common bosonic environment and developed the formalism to compute the dynamics of the two qubits without any direct interaction. The results showed that for this model, applying control pulses to the two qubits does enhance the environment-generated entanglement between them. We then proceeded to add a qubit-qubit interaction term in the model Hamiltonian along with noise in this interaction. Due to this noise, the qubits were shown to undergo additional decoherence. To mitigate the effect of this additional term, it was shown that the same pulse sequence cannot be applied to both qubits, that is, there must be a time shift. If such a time shift is allowed, we showed that near perfect entanglement can again be generated. Our results should be useful in the context of reservoir engineering to generate entanglement between two qubits.
