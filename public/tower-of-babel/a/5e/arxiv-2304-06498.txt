On Remoteness Functions of Exact Slow k k -NIM with k + 1 k+1 Piles
Vladimir Gurvich, Denis Martynov, Vladislav Maximchuk, Michael Vyalyi

Abstract




Abstract

Given integer
n
and
k
such that
0<k\leq n

and
n
piles of stones,
two player alternate turns.
By one move it is allowed
to choose any
k
piles and remove
exactly one stone from each.
The player who has to move but cannot is the loser.
Cases
k=1
and
k=n
are trivial.
For
k=2
the game was solved for
n\leq 6
.
For
n\leq 4
the Sprague-Grundy function was
efficiently computed
(for both the normal and misère versions).
For
n=5,6
a polynomial algorithm
computing P-positions was obtained.
Here we consider the case
2\leq k=n-1

and compute Smith’s remoteness function,
whose even values define the P-positions.
In fact, an optimal move
is always defined by the following simple rule:

•

if all piles are odd,
keep a largest one and reduce all other;

•

if there exist even piles,
keep a smallest one of them
and reduce all other.

Such strategy is optimal for both players, moreover,
it allows to win as fast as possible
from an N-position and
to resist as long as possible from a P-position.

Keywords:

Exact Slow NIM, Smith’s Remoteness Function,
Sprague-Grundy Function

AMS subjects: 91A05, 91A46, 91A68

Introduction

We assume that the reader is familiar
with basic concepts of impartial game theory;
see e.g.,
[
1
,

]

1.1
Exact Slow NIM

Game Exact Slow NIM
was introduced in
[
23
]
as follows:
Given two integers
n
and
k

such that
0<k\leq n
and

n
piles containing
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
stones.
By one move it is allowed to reduce any
k
piles
by exactly one stone each.
Two players alternate turns.
One who has to move but cannot is the loser.
In
[
23
]
, this game was denoted NIM
{}^{1}_{=}(n,k)
.
Here we will simplify this notation to NIM
(n,k)
.
We start with three simple properties of this game.

Obviously, value

x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}\;\mod k

is an invariant of game NIM
(n,k)
.
Hence, NIM
(n,k)
is split into

k
independent subgames,
between which there are no moves.

A position
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

will be called
even
or
odd

if all its coordinates are even or odd, respectively.

Every even position of
game NIM
(n,k)
is a P-position,
because each move of the first player
can be repeated by the opponent.
Respectively, every position
x

with exactly
k
odd coordinates
is an N-position, because there is a move from
x

to a P-position.

Remark 1
.

Note that both above claims
can be obviously extended from game NIM
(n,k)

to the slow NIM to arbitrary hypergraph
[
23
]
.
Given a hypergraph

\mathcal{H}\subseteq 2^{[n]}\setminus\{\emptyset\}

on the ground set
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
,
and a nonnegative vector
x=(x_{i}\mid i\in[n])
,
the
hypergraph slow game NIM
H

is defined as follows:
By one move, a player can choose any hyperedge

H\in\mathcal{H}
such that
x_{i}>0
for every
i\in H

and reduce by 1 each
x_{i}
for
i\in H
.
Then, game NIM
(n,k)
corresponds to
the hypergraph slow NIM
H

with
\mathcal{H}=\{H\mid|H|=k\}
.
In any hypergraph slow NIM
H
, every even
x
is a P-position
and, hence,
x
is an N-position when

x_{i}
is odd if and only if
i\in H

for some hyperedge
H\in\mathcal{H}
.

Game NIM
(n,k)
is trivial if
k=1
or
k=n
.
In the first case it ends after
x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}
moves
and in the second one—after
\min(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

moves. In both cases nothing depends on players’ skills.
All other cases are more complicated.

The game was solved for
k=2
and
n\leq 6
.
In
[
24
]
, an explicit formula
for the Sprague-Grundy
(SG) function was found for
n\leq 4

(for both, the normal and misère versions of the game).
This formula allows us to compute the SG function in linear time.
Then, in
[
10
]
the P-positions were found for
n\leq 6
.
For the subgame with even
x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}

a simple formula for the P-positions was obtained.
It allows for a given position, in linear time,
to verify if it is a P-position and, if not,
to find a move from it to a P-position.
The subgame with odd
x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}
is more difficult.
Still, a more sophisticated formula for the P-positions was found and it gives a linear time algorithm for recognizing P-positions.

1.2
Case
n=k+1

In this paper we solve the game in case
n=k+1
.
In every position an optimal move
is always defined by the following simple rule:

if all piles are odd,
keep a largest one and reduce all other;

if there exist even piles,
keep a smallest one of them
and reduce all other.

We will call this strategy the
M-rule
and
the corresponding moves by the
M-moves
.
Obviously, in every position
there exists a unique M-move,
up to re-numeration of piles.
We will show that M-rule solves the game, moreover,
it allows to win as fast as possible in an N-position and
to resist as long as possible in a P-position.

Given a position
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
,
assume that both players follow the M-rule and
denote by
\mathcal{M}(x)
the number of moves
from
x
to a terminal position.
We will prove that
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{R}
, where

\mathcal{R}
is the so-called remoteness function
considered in the next subsection.

Let us note that in some positions
the M-move may be a unique winning move.
For example, in positions
(1,1,2) and (1,1,3) of NIM(3,2)
the M-move reduces 1,1 to 0,0,
and wins immediately, while moves
to (0,1,1) and (0,1,2) are losing.

Let us note
that there exists no M-move to an odd position.
Yet, the latter may be a P- or N-position.
For example, in NIM(3,2), position
(1,1,1) is an N-position,
while (3,3,3) is a P-position,
\mathcal{M}(3,3,3)=4
.
The corresponding sequence of M-moves is:

(3,3,3)\to(2,2,3)\to(1,2,2)\to(0,1,2)\to(0,0,1).

1.3
Smith’s Remoteness Function

In 1966 Smith
[
34
]

introduced a
remoteness
function
\mathcal{R}

for impartial games by the following algorithm.

Consider an impartial game
given by a finite acyclic directed graph
(digraph)
G
.
Set
\mathcal{R}=0
for all terminal positions
of
G
and

\mathcal{R}(x)=1
if and only if
there is a move from
x
to a terminal position.
Delete all labeled positions from
G
and
repeat the above procedure increasing
\mathcal{R}
by 2,
that is, assign 2 and 3 instead of 0 and 1; etc.

This algorithm was considered
as early as in 1901 by Bouton
[
3
]
,
but only for a special graphs
corresponding to the game of NIM.
In 1944 this algorithm was extended to
arbitrary digraphs by von Neumann and Morgenstern
[
31
]
.
In graph theory, the set of positions with even
\mathcal{R}

they called the
kernel
of a digraph;
in theory of impartial games, this set
is referred to as the set of P-positions.

Function
\mathcal{R}
has also the following, stronger, properties:
Position
x
is a P-position if and only if
\mathcal{R}(x)
is even.
In this case the player making a move from
x
cannot win, yet,
can resist at least
\mathcal{R}(x)
moves, but not longer.
Position
x
is an N-position if and only if
\mathcal{R}(x)
is odd.
In this case the player making a move from
x
wins in at most

\mathcal{R}(x)
moves, but not faster.
In both cases the player making a move from
x
should
reduce
\mathcal{R}
by one, which is always possible
unless position
x
is terminal,
in which case
\mathcal{R}(x)=0
.

1.4
Sprague-Grundy and Smith’s Theories

Given
n
impartial games

\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{n}
,
by one move a player chooses one of them
and makes a move in it.
The player who has to move but cannot is the looser.
The obtained game

\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus\Gamma_{n}

is called the
disjunctive compound
.
For example, NIM with
n
piles
is the disjunctive compound of
n
one-pile NIMs.
The SG function
\mathcal{G}
of
\Gamma
is uniquely determined
by the SG functions of
n
compound games by formula

\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)=\mathcal{G}(\Gamma_{1})\oplus\dots\oplus\mathcal{G}(\Gamma_{n})

where
\oplus
is the so-called NIM-sum.
These results were obtained by Bouton

[
3
]
for the special case of NIM and
then extended to arbitrary impartial games
by Sprague
[
36
]
and Grundy
[
19
]
.

Now suppose that
by one move a player makes a move
in each of the
n
compound games,
rather than in one of them.
Again, the player who has to move but cannot is the looser.
The obtained game

\Gamma=\Gamma_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\Gamma_{n}

is called the
conjunctive compound
.
Remoteness function
\mathcal{R}
of
\Gamma
is uniquely determined
by the remoteness functions of
n
compound games by formula

\mathcal{R}(\Gamma)=\min(\mathcal{R}(\Gamma_{1}),\dots,\mathcal{R}(\Gamma_{n}))
.
This result was obtained in 1966 by Smith
[
34
]
.

1.5
On
m
-critical positions of game NIM
(n,n-1)

The relation
x\succeq y
is defined standardly as follows.
Order, monotone increasingly, the coordinates of

n
-vectors
x
and
y
and denote the obtained

n
-vectors by
x^{*}
and
y^{*}
.
We write
x\succeq y
if

x^{*}_{i}\geq y^{*}_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}

and
x\succ y

if, in addition,
x^{*}\neq y^{*}
,
or in other words,
if at least one of these

n
inequalities is strict.

In the set of positions
x
with fixed value
\mathcal{M}(x)

we distinguish minimal with respect
to the relation
\succeq
.
Given a nonnegative integer
m
, a position
x=(x_{1},\dots x_{n})

of game NIM
(n,k)
with
n=k+1
is called
m
-critical if
\mathcal{M}(x)=m
and
x\succ y
fails for any position
y
with
\mathcal{M}(y)=m
.

By this definition, for any position
y

there exists a unique
m=m(y)

such that
y\succeq x
holds
for some
m
-critical
x
and

y\succeq x
fails for
any
(m+1)
-critical
x
.

We will characterize
m
-critical positions
of game NIM
(k+1,k)
as follows.

Theorem 1
.

Position
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})

of game NIM
(n,k)
with
n=k+1

is
m
-critical if and only if
one of the following two cases holds:

(A)

\;\;x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}=km
;

\max(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\leq m
;

if
m
is even then
x
is even
(that is, all
n
coordinates of
x
are even);

if
m
is odd then exactly one of
n
coordinates
of
x
is even.

(B)

\;\;x_{1}+\dots+x_{n}=km+k-1
;

\max(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})<m
;

m
is even,
x
is odd.

The proof will be given in Section
2
.

Note that case (B) provides a large family
of odd P-positions.
Note also that there are odd P-positions beyond
this family too.
For example,
\mathcal{M}(3,5,5)=6
in NIM
(3,2)
.
The corresponding sequence of M-moves is:

(3,5,5)\to(2,4,5)\to(2,3,4)\to(2,2,3)\to(1,2,2)\to(0,1,2)\to(0,0,1).

Recall that, by definition,
an M-move cannot lead to an odd position.

The following statement easily result
from the above theorem.

Corollary 1
.

(i) Let
x
be an
m
-critical position with an even
m
.
Then,
x
is either odd
(
Case
(B))
or even
(
Case
(A))
.
In the latter case, at least one of even coordinates of
x

is strictly less than
m
.
A move from
x
leads
to an odd
(m-1)
-critical position
provided this move reduces
all (maximal) coordinates of
x

that are equal to
m
.
In particular, an M-move has such property.

(ii) For an
m
-critical position
x
with an odd
m

there exists exactly one move
leading to an even
(m-1)
-critical position.
This move keeps the (unique) even coordinate
and reduces all other;
furthermore, this move is a unique M-move.

Proof.

(i)
It is enough to note that
all
n=k+1
coordinates
cannot be equal to
m
, because
in this case their sum would be
(k+1)m

rather than
km
,
as required by Theorem
1
.
All other statements are immediate
from the definitions and Theorem
1
.

(ii)
If the largest coordinate
x_{i}
of
x
is even,
then
x_{i}
is unique and
x_{i}<m
,
since
m
is odd.
Hence, by Theorem
1
,
the M-move is unique and leads to
an
(m-1)
-critical position.
All other statements are immediate
from the definitions and Theorem
1
.
∎

Due to Theorem
2
below, a suitable generalization
of
m
-critical positions on arbitrary game uses
the remoteness function
\mathcal{R}(x)
instead of
\mathcal{M}(x)
.
Characterizing
m
-critical positions of game NIM
(n,k)

remains an open problem.

Conjecture 1
.

For any integer
k,n
and
m

such that
0<k<n
and
0\leq m
, inequalities

km\leq(x_{1}+\dots+x_{n})<k(m+1)\;

and

\;\max(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\leq m

hold for any
m
-critical position

x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
of game NIM
(n,k)
.

It seems difficult to characterize
the
m
-critical positions for
arbitrary
k,n
and
m
.
Consider, for example,

k=3,n=5
and
m=8
.
Computations show that

(1,3,7,7,7),(1,5,5,7,7),(3,3,5,7,7)
, and
(5,5,5,5,5)

are
m
-critical positions of game NIM
(5,3)
,
while
(3,5,5,5,7)
is not; instead
(3,5,5,6,7)
is.

1.6
Main Results

Our main results are
the following two properties of game NIM
(n,n-1)
.

Theorem 2
.

Equalities
m(x)=\mathcal{M}(x)=\mathcal{R}(x)

hold for any position
x
.

Theorem 3
.

Function
\mathcal{R}
can be computed in polynomial time,
even if
n
is a part of the input and integers are presented in binary.

Hence, P-positions can be recognized in polynomial time.

1.7
On P-positions of game NIM
(n,n-1)

Recall that P-positions of an impartial game
can be characterized in two ways, in other words,
the following three statements are equivalent:

x
is a P-position,
\mathcal{G}(x)=0
, and
\mathcal{R}(x)
is even.
Hence,
x
is a P-position of NIM
(n,n-1)

if and only if the number
m(x)=\mathcal{M}(x)=\mathcal{R}(x)
is even.
Using the above theorems, we will obtain a
polynomial algorithm verifying
if
x
is a P-position and if not
computing a move from
x
to a P-position.

Interestingly,
we do not need this algorithm to play NIM
(n,n-1)
.
It is much simpler just to follow the M-rule
(see subsection
1.1
)—a peculiar situation in impartial game theory,
which somewhat downgrade the role of P-positions.

For NIM
(4,3)
some pretty complicated formulas
for P-positions were confirmed by computations; see Appendix.
Yet, we were not able to prove these formulas.
Furthermore, they differ in three cases:

x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3}+x_{4}\equiv 0,1
, or
2\;(\mod 3)
.
Game NIM
(k+1,k)
is split into
k
disjoint subgames,
which makes the general case analysis even more difficult.
Interestingly, the
m
-critical positions,
unlike the P-positions, are defined
by the same formulas for all
k
subgames.

1.8
Related works

The ancient game of NIM was solved
in 1901 by Bouton
[
3
]
.

In 1907 Wythoff
[
38
]
introduced and
solved modified NIM
with two piles of stones, where
by one move a player can either reduce one pile,
or both, but then by the same number of stones.
Wythoff found the P-positions.
The game and its generalizations were studied
in many works; see, for example,
[2, 13, 14, 16–18, 22, 37, 39].

The SG function of this game is still unknown
despite many efforts;
see, for example,
[
2
,
12
,
32
]
.
More precisely, it is unknown
whether SG value
\mathcal{G}(x)
can be computed
in polynomial time for a given position
x
.
In contrast, the remoteness function
\mathcal{R}(x)

can be computed in polynomial time
for the Wythoff game and several its generalizations

[
8
]
.

In 1910 Moore introduced and solved
k
-NIM.
By one move a player can reduce
at most
k
piles, and at least 1.
Moore found the P-positions.
The SG-function is known only in the case

n=k+1
; see Jenkins and Mayberry
[
27
]
.
Recently, their result was considerably strengthened
and extended from hypergraph
k
-NIM with
n=k+1
piles
to the so-called
hypergraph NIM on arbitrary JM hypergraphs;
see
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
9
]

for details.

For Moore’s game, there is a simple formula
for
\mathcal{R}(x)
when
x
is a P-position;
in contrast,
\mathcal{R}(x)
is NP-hard to compute
when
x
is an N-position;
see
[
8
]
.

Both SG and remoteness functions
can be computed in polynomial time
for the game Euclid
[
11
]
;
see also
[
18
,
21
,
28
,
29
]
.
A very simple and elegant formula
for the SG function was given
in 2006 by Nivasch
[
33
]
.
A polynomial algorithm computing
the remoteness function was recently suggested
in
[
8
]
.

2
Proofs of Theorems
1
and
2

Here we always assume that coordinates in a position

x
are ordered in the non-decreasing order and after a move coordinates are re-ordered.

In the case
k=1
, the theorems trivially hold. We assume in this section that
k>1
.

2.1
Function
\mathcal{B}(x)

We introduce an auxiliary function
\mathcal{B}(x)
using conditions (A) from Theorem
1
. Let
|x|=\sum_{i=1}^{k+1}x_{i}
be the
\ell_{1}
-norm of
x
. A position
z=(z_{1},\dots,z_{k+1})
is called
basic
, if

\displaystyle|z|=kb(z),

(1)

\displaystyle z_{i}\leq b(z),

(2)

for even
b(z)
all
z_{i}
are even,

and for odd
b(z)
exactly one
z_{i}
is even.

(3)

Then

\mathcal{B}(x)=\max(b(z):x\succeq z,\ z\ \text{is basic}).

(4)

It is clear that
\mathcal{B}(z)=b(z)
for every basic position
z
. In general, for a basic position
z
such that
b(z)=\mathcal{B}(x)
, we say that
z

supports

x
(or
x
is supported by
z
).
If
\mathcal{B}(x)
is even, then
x
is called
B
-even. Otherwise, it is called
B
-odd.

In general, coordinates in a basic position are not ordered. But it does not matter due to the following observation.

Claim 1
.

If
z\preceq x
for a basic
z
, then there exists a
basic position
z^{*}
such that
z^{*}\preceq x
, and
b(z^{*})=b(z)
, and coordinates of
z^{*}
are ordered in the non-decreasing order.

Proof.

All the conditions (
1
), (
2
) and (
3
) are invariant under permutations of coordinates. If
x
is ordered and
z_{i}>z_{j}
for
i<j
, then
x_{j}\geq x_{i}\geq z_{i}
and
x_{i}\geq z_{i}>z_{j}
. Thus, transposition of
z_{i}
and
z_{j}
gives a basic position. Repeating the transpositions, we come to an ordered basic
z^{*}
such that
z^{*}\preceq x
.
∎

For ordered basic positions we have a useful inequality.

Claim 2
.

If
z
is an ordered basic position, then
z_{1}+z_{2}\geq b(z)
.

Proof.

The arithmetic mean of
z_{1}+z_{2}
,
z_{i}
,
i\geq 3
, is exactly
b(z)
. The claim
follows from
z_{i}\leq b(z)
(the bound condition (
2
)).
∎

From this claim we immediately get a useful corollary.

Corollary 2
.

If
z
is an ordered basic position, then
z_{1}\geq 0
. If
z_{1}=0
then
z=\big(0,b(z),\dots,b(z)\big)
.

A move in NIM
(k+1,k)
preserves exactly one coordinate. We use this coordinate to indicate the move. Thus, a move
i
from a position
x
leads to the position
x-d^{(i)}
, where
d^{(i)}_{j}=1-\delta_{ij}
(
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker symbol).

Let
x\to x^{\prime}
be a move in NIM
(k+1,k)
. To prove main results, we relate
\mathcal{B}(x)
and
\mathcal{B}(x^{\prime})
in claims below.

Claim 3
.

If
\mathcal{B}(x)=0
then
x
is a terminal position in NIM
(k+1,k)
.

Proof.

If
x_{2}>0
then
z=d^{(1)}=(0,1,\dots,1)\preceq x
,
b(z)=1
, the parity condition (
3
) and the bound condition (
2
) hold. Thus
z
is basic and
\mathcal{B}(x)\geq 1
.

If
x_{1}=x_{2}=0
then
x
is terminal.
∎

Claim 4
.

If
x\to x^{\prime}
is a move in NIM
(k+1,k)
then
\mathcal{B}(x^{\prime})\leq\mathcal{B}(x)
.

Proof.

Let
z^{\prime}
be a basic position supporting
x^{\prime}
. Then
z^{\prime}\preceq x^{\prime}\preceq x
and
\mathcal{B}(x)\geq\mathcal{B}(x^{\prime})
.
∎

Claim 5
.

If
x\to x^{\prime}
is a move in NIM
(k+1,k)
from a
B
-odd position
x
, then
\mathcal{B}(x^{\prime})\geq\mathcal{B}(x)-2
.

Proof.

Let
z
be an ordered basic position that supports
x
and
z_{p}
is even (other coordinates in
z
are odd). If
b(z)\leq 2
then the claim trivially holds. For the rest of the proof assume that
b(z)\geq 3
. It implies
z_{2}\geq 2
. If
p=2
, it follows from Claim
3
. If
p\neq 2
, it follows from Claim
2
:
z_{1}+z_{2}\geq b(z)\geq 3
and
z_{1}\leq z_{2}
imply that
z_{2}\geq 2
.

Define
z^{\prime}
as follows.

If
p>1
then
z^{\prime}_{1}=z_{1}-1
,
z^{\prime}_{p}=z_{p}-1
,
z^{\prime}_{i}=z_{i}-2
for
i\notin\{1,p\}

Claim 7

Claim 8

\mathcal{B}(x)=m(x^{\prime})+1

\mathcal{B}(x)\leq m(x^{\prime})+1
.
∎

Claim 9

For any move

From Claim

Lemma 2

\mathcal{M}(x)=\mathcal{B}(x)

Proof of Theorem

Due to Lemma

\mathcal{R}(x)=\mathcal{M}(x)

We prove it by induction on the sum of coordinates. In the proof we use an equivalent definition of

be the set of positions such that there exists a move

.
Then the remoteness function is defined by the following recurrence

\mathcal{R}(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0,&&\text{ if }\mathcal{R}(N^{+}(x))=\emptyset,\\
&1+\min\mathcal{R}(N^{+}(x))\cap 2\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\,,&&\text{ if }\mathcal{R}(N^{+}(x))\cap 2\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\neq\emptyset,\\
&1+\max\mathcal{R}(N^{+}(x))\cap 2\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\,,&&\text{ if }\emptyset\neq\mathcal{R}(N^{+}(x))\subseteq 1+2\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\,.\end{aligned}\right.

(5)

The base case
x=0
trivially holds.

Assume that
\mathcal{R}(x)=\mathcal{M}(x)
for all

\sum_{i}x_{i}<q
. Take

\sum_{i}y_{i}=q
.

If

is exceptional, then, due to Claim

and Corollary

, for any move

, we have

(the last equality is the induction hypothesis) and
\mathcal{R}(y^{\prime})
is odd. Therefore
\mathcal{R}(y)=1+\mathcal{R}(y^{\prime})=\mathcal{M}(y)
.

If
y
is
B
-odd and non-exceptional, then
y\to x=y-d^{(e(y))}
is an M-move to
B
-even position. Due to Claim
12
,
\mathcal{B}(x)=\mathcal{B}(y)-1
. Also,
\mathcal{B}(x)\geq\mathcal{B}(y)-2
for any other move from
y
due to Claim
5
. From (
5
) we conclude that
\mathcal{R}(y)=1+(\mathcal{B}(y)-1)=\mathcal{B}(y)
.

If
y
is
B
-even, it is non-exceptional and there are no moves to
B
-even positions from
y
due to Lemma

. It follows from Claim
10
that there is a move to
x
such that
\mathcal{B}(x)=\mathcal{B}(y)-1
.
From (

\mathcal{R}(y)=1+(\mathcal{B}(y)-1)=\mathcal{B}(y)

∎

3
Structural and algorithmic complexity

It follows from Theorem
2
that
x
is a P position in NIM
(k+1,k)
iff
m(x)=\mathcal{R}(x)
is even.

For a fixed
k
, conditions (A) and (B) in Theorem
1
are easily expressed in Presburger arithmetic (see, e.g.
[
26
]
): they include inequalities and congruences modulo fixed integers. Therefore the predicate
\mathcal{R}(x)=2y
is also expressed in Presburger arithmetic. It implies that the set of P-positions is semilinear
[
26
]
, i.e. can be expressed as a finite union of solutions of systems of linear inequalities combined with
equations modulo some integer (fixed for the set). It was conjectured
in
[
25
]
that the set of P-positions of any multidimensional subtraction game with nonnegative vectors of differences is semilinear. The conjecture
was supported by several sporadic examples from
[
24
,
10
]
. Games NIM
(k+1,k)
provide an infinite family of nontrivial subtraction games having this property.

Thus, for a fixed
k
, there exists a very simple linear time algorithm recognizing P-positions in NIM
(k+1,k)
.
Theorem
3
gives more. It claims that there exists an
algorithm recognizing P-positions even if
k
is a part of the input.

It follows from Theorems
1
and
2
that,
to compute
\mathcal{R}(x)
efficiently, it is enough to compute
\mathcal{B}(x)
efficiently for non-exceptional positions and compute
m(x)
for exceptional ones.
Verifying the conditions (B) in Theorem
1
can be done in polynomial time as well as computing
m(x)
for exceptional positions.

To compute
\mathcal{B}(x)
efficiently for non-exceptional positions, we use an enhanced version of Claim
1
.
We need the reverse lexicographical order on
(k+1)
-dimensional integer vectors, which is defined as follows:
x\mathrel{<_{\text{lex}}}y
if
x_{i}=y_{i}
for all
i>t
and
x_{t}<y_{t}
.

For even
b
, let
\ell(x,b)
be the maximal (w.r.t. the reverse lexicographical order) basic position
z
such that
z\preceq x
,
b(z)=b
.

Lemma 3
.

For all
x
and for all even
b
, there exists
1<t\leq k+2
such that
\ell(x,b)_{i}=b
for
i\geq t
and

0\leq x_{i}-\ell(x,b)_{i}\leq 1
for all
1<i<t
.

Proof.

If
i<j
then decreasing the coordinate
i
by 2 and increasing the coordinate
j
by 2 transforms a basic position
z
to a basic position
z^{\prime}
such that
z\mathrel{<_{\text{lex}}}z^{\prime}
. This operation is called a
shift
.

Note also that re-ordering a vector in the non-increasing order gives a vector that is not smaller than initial one with respect to the reverse lexicographical order.

Take now an ordered basic position
z
such that
z\preceq x
,
b(z)=b
and no shift can be applied to
z
. It means that
z_{i}\geq\min(x_{i}-1,b-1)
for all
1<i\leq k+1
. Since
b
is even, then either
z_{i}\geq x_{i}-1
or
z_{i}=b
.
∎

Note that
\ell(x,b)
is uniquely determined by
t
: there is exactly one even integer among
x_{i}-1
,
x_{i}
. It is a key observation for the proof of Theorem
3
.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

For exceptional positions
m(x)
can be found in polynomial time. For the rest of the proof we assume that
x
is non-exceptional and our goal is to compute
\mathcal{B}(x)
.

Let
b_{t}(x)
,
1<t\leq k+2
, be the maximal value
b(z)
for
B
-even basic positions
z
such that the conditions of Lemma
3
hold for the specific value of
t
.
If there are no
z
satisfying the requirements, then
b_{t}=-\infty
.
Let
E(x)=\max_{t}b_{t}(x)
.

Due to Lemma
3
,
E(x)\leq\mathcal{B}(x)
and equality holds exactly for
B
-even
x
.
Let
x\to x^{\prime}=x-d^{(e(x))}
be an M-move. If
x
is
B
-odd then
x^{\prime}
is
B
-even and
\mathcal{B}(x)=\mathcal{B}(x^{\prime})+1=E(x^{\prime})+1
. Therefore
E(x^{\prime})=\mathcal{B}(x^{\prime})=\mathcal{B}(x)-1>E(x)-1
. Similarly, for a
B
-even position
x
we have
E(x)=\mathcal{B}(x)=\mathcal{B}(x^{\prime})+1>E(x^{\prime})+1
.

It gives the rule to compute
\mathcal{B}(x)
by
E(x)
:

\mathcal{B}(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &E(x),&&\text{if}\ E(x)>E(x^{\prime})+1,\\
&E(x^{\prime})+1,&&\text{if}\ E(x)<E(x^{\prime})+1,\end{aligned}\right.

where
x\to x^{\prime}
is an M-move.

To complete the proof, we provide an efficient algorithm that computes
E(x)
.
For brevity we use the following notation:

A_{t}=\sum_{i=2}^{t-1}2\cdot\lfloor x_{i}/2\rfloor.

To find
b_{t}(x)
, one need to find the maximal
q
satisfying the requirements

\displaystyle 2s+A_{t}+q\cdot 2(k+2-t)=q\cdot 2k,

(6)

\displaystyle 2s\leq x_{1},

\displaystyle 2q\leq x_{t},

and set
b_{t}(x)=2q
. (Here
2s
is the value of
z_{1}
.)

Eq. (
6
) is equivalent to

q\cdot 2(t-2)=2s+A_{t}\,.

Thus, we have two linear inequalities on
q
and the maximal value of
q
is

\min\left(\left\lfloor\frac{2\lfloor x_{1}/2\rfloor+A_{t}}{2(t-2)}\right\rfloor,\lfloor\frac{x_{t}}{2}\rfloor\right)\,.

If
t=2
the first inequality becomes irrelevant.

Combining all the arguments above, we get the polynomial algorithm to compute
\mathcal{B}(x)
and
\mathcal{R}(x)
.
∎
