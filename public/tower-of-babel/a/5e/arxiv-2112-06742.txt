Data-driven modelling of nonlinear dynamics by barycentric coordinates and memory
Niklas Wulkow, Péter Koltai, Vikram Sunkara, Christof Schütte

Abstract
We present a numerical method to model dynamical systems from data. We use the recently introduced method Scalable Probabilistic Approximation (SPA) to project points from a Euclidean space to convex polytopes and represent these projected states of a system in new, lower-dimensional coordinates denoting their position in the polytope. We then introduce a specific nonlinear transformation to construct a model of the dynamics in the polytope and to transform back into the original state space. To overcome the potential loss of information from the projection to a lower-dimensional polytope, we use memory in the sense of the delay-embedding theorem of Takens. By construction, our method produces stable models. We illustrate the capacity of the method to reproduce even chaotic dynamics and attractors with multiple connected components on various examples.



Keywords—
Data-driven modelling, Nonlinear dynamics, Memory, Delay embedding, Scalable Probabilistic Approximation, Barycentric coordinates

MSC classes: 37M05, 37M10, 52B11

1  Introduction

Learning dynamical models of real world systems from observations has become a critical tool in the natural sciences. For example, learning from femtosecond scale atomistic simulations and predicting millisecond scale reaction kinetics is now a key aspect of novel drug discovery
[
BBM20
]
. These advances are made through mathematical properties, such as the identification of metastable states in protein folding
[
LBB+11
]
or tipping points in the Earth’s climate
[
WDKW21
]
.

We can categorise the data-driven techniques for reconstructing dynamics into four partially overlapping families: kernel-based methods, regression methods, neural networks, and probabilistic models. Neural networks methods determine a representation of the dynamics in compositions of affine transformations and nonlinear activation functions
[
TZ19
,
VBW+18
,
BK19
,
CLKB19
,
KLL+21
]
. Kernel-based methods approximate the dynamics using suitable distance measures, the kernels, in the data space
[
GGH21
,
SSAB20
]
. Regression methods determine a linear mapping between nonlinear, often polynomial, transformations of the data and future states of the dynamics (or nonlinear observables thereof), such as DMD
[
TRL+14
]
, EDMD
[
LDBK17
]
or SINDy
[
BPK16a
,
BPK16b
]
. Lastly, probabilistic models use discretisations of the data space and describe the dynamics by transitional probabilities between the discrete states, such as Markov State Models (MSM)
[
HP18
]
and the recently introduced method Scalable Probabilistic Approximation (SPA)
[
GPNH20
]
.

Despite current success of these methods, tackling high-dimensional nonlinear dynamics is still a challenge. This is, because neural networks require an abundance of parameters whose estimation is costly (e.g., due to a non-convex optimization problem), kernel-based methods require a suitable choice of the kernel
[
HO20
]
, regression methods need the right choice of transformations, making a suitable basis desirable (for instance by restricting the parametrization to collective variables or reaction coordinates
[
BKK+17
]
), and can result in ill-conditioned problems
[
FWM10
]
. Lastly, probabilistic models are more stable and do not require strong intuition about the problem, however often suffer from the curse of dimensionality. Also, they typically only allow conclusions on the level of their discretisation. This drawback has been partially remedied by SPA, which chooses a finite set of landmarks in the data space and represents the data points by stochastic vectors which encode the strength of affiliation with the landmarks. This is in contrast to the binary affiliation with boxes as in MSMs and results in a lower loss between original data and SPA representations. Furthermore, it was shown in
[
GPNH20
]
that this leads to high predictive accuracy for complex examples of both high and low dimension in problems from climate science and biology while coming at the same computational complexity as the cheap K-means clustering method.

Based on the chosen number of landmarks,
K,
and their positions in the state space, SPA estimates linear forward models based on the law of total probability. More precisely, SPA was invented for modeling a process
Y(t)
in terms of another process
X(t)
by finding linear relations between finite probabilistic representations of
X
and
Y
. It provides a low-cost algorithm, allowing for simultaneous data-driven optimal discretisation, feature selection, and prediction, especially if
X
and
Y
are both high-dimensional and stochastic. In this article, we show how to extend SPA for usage in a slightly different setting, namely for forecasting a single process
X
.

As discussed in various publications (see, e.g.,
[
LKB18
,
BPK16a
,
CLKB19
,
LDBK17
]
), approximations of the linear but infinite-dimensional Koopman operator can generate sound linear approximations of nonlinear dynamics. However, they require the transformation into a suitable space where the dynamics can be approximated by a finite-dimensional linear propagator. To faithfully reconstruct long-term dynamics, we seek in the present work a nonlinear approximation of the dynamics using the cost-effective SPA representation of our high-dimensional data. As we will discuss in Section
3
, this method also admits a dual view, as it can be seen as a nonlinear reconstruction of dynamics and a linear reconstruction of the transfer operator.

Specifically, we propose not to choose a large number
K
of discrete landmarks and construct a linear model, but rather use a small
K
and construct a nonlinear model.
In particular, we use the outer-products of the memory terms of the SPA representation, later called
path affiliations
, as our nonlinear basis functions. Contrary to convention, we choose a fixed basis over our memory terms, which preserves the probabilistic interpretation of the SPA representations. The dynamics that we learn on the path affiliations are linear and are represented by a stochastic matrix. This has the advantage that the emerging dynamics are
stable
by construction—a widely desirable property
[
FYR+21
,
BBK+21
]
.
We demonstrate that this choice of basis functions in connection with the SPA representation and memory can construct good approximations of high-dimensional nonlinear dynamics from data.

This paper is divided into three parts.
In Section
2
, we introduce the SPA method and deconstruct the different sub-problems. We introduce a new interpretation of SPA representations of data as projections onto a polytope and theoretically explore SPA in the context of dynamical systems.
In Section
3
, we introduce an extension to SPA based on memory which we refer to as mSPA (memory SPA). We then prove using Takens’ Theorem that we can construct topologically equivalent dynamics along the path affiliations. In Section
4
, we show how to use mSPA to learn the dynamics on the SPA representation (the Learning) and then to map it back to the original data space (the Lifting). We compute this on three examples, one simple, one high-dimensional (Kuramoto–Sivashinsky PDE), and lastly on a chaotic system (Lorenz-96).

2  Scalable Probabilistic Approximation (SPA)

Data-driven methods for learning dynamics entail two fundamental steps: the first step being
feature selection
and the second step being
dynamics reconstruction
.
The first step entails projecting the data into a known
feature space
to help delineate the underlying dynamics of the data.
Then, in the second step, a
propagator
over the projected data in the feature space is inferred which recapitulates the dynamics of the full data.
Choosing the right feature spaces is a key bottleneck for most datasets. This is particularly so for high-dimensional datasets; where feature spaces which capture the right geometry of the data, in practise, are found to be sensitive to noise and ill-conditioned when inferring the propagator.
One method which balances the choice of an interpretable feature space and the stability of the propagator is the
Scalable Probabilistic Approximation
(SPA) method
[
GPNH20
,
Hor20
]
.

More precisely, SPA was invented for modeling a process
Y(t)
in terms of another process
X(t)
by finding linear relations between finite probabilistic representations of
X
and
Y
, in the sense that the optimal matrix
\Lambda
of conditional probabilities is computed for which the two finite dimensional probabilistic representations
\Gamma^{X}
and
\Gamma^{Y}
are linearly connected,
\Gamma^{Y}=\Lambda\Gamma^{X}
. SPA was designed for (i) finding optimal representations
\Gamma
and (ii) simultaneously identify the optimal linear model
\Lambda
, especially for cases where
X
and
Y
are both high-dimensional and stochastic. In this article, SPA is used in a slightly different setting, namely for forecasting a single process
X
. We will show how to extend SPA by incorporating memory for allowing for long-term predictions of
X
. To this end, we will present SPA in a form where the two steps (i) and (ii) are presented individually.

2.1
The SPA method revisited

Like other data driven methods, the principle behind SPA can be divided into two steps: the first to project the data onto a convex polytope and secondly to infer a linear propagator over the said polytope.
The first of the two steps, which we refer to as “SPA I”, is formulated as follows: for a sequence of
T\in\mathbb{N}
observations of
(D\in\mathbb{N})
-dimensional data points,
\Xi:=[x_{1}|\cdots|x_{T}],
the
K\in\mathbb{N}
vertex convex polytope to project the data onto is determined by solving for the matrices
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times K}
and
\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times T}
which satisfy:

\begin{split}&\hskip 56.9055pt[\Sigma,\Gamma]=\argmin_{\Sigma^{*},\Gamma^{*}}\|\Xi-\Sigma^{*}\Gamma^{*}\|_{F},\\
\text{subject to,}&\\
&\Sigma:=[\sigma_{1}|\cdots|\sigma_{K}]\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times K},\hskip 9.24994pt\Gamma:=[\gamma_{1}|\cdots|\gamma_{T}]\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times T},\\
\text{with}&\\
&\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{t}^{k}=1\text{ and }\gamma_{t}^{k}\geq 0\text{ for all }t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}\text{ and }k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}.\\
\end{split}

(SPA I)

We can deduce from the above formulation that
\sigma_{\bullet},
the columns of the matrix
\Sigma,
represent a new coordinate system consisting of
K
basis points in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. Furthermore,
\gamma_{\bullet},
the column vectors of
\Gamma,
are simply an orthogonal projection of data points
x_{\bullet}
onto the convex polytope formed by the span of
\{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{K}\}.
For each vertex
k\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
, the value of
\gamma_{\bullet}^{k}
can be interpreted as a closeness/affiliation measure of the data point
x_{\bullet}
to the vertex
\sigma_{k}
. For brevity, here on in, we will refer to the “convex polytope” spanned by vectors
\sigma_{\bullet}
as simply a “polytope” and denote it by
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}.
Further, if
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}
is a simplex, there exists a bijective map between the so-called
barycentric coordinates

\gamma
to points
\Sigma\gamma\in\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}
so that we use
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}
interchangeably for
conv(\Sigma)
and the polytope of the barycentric coordinates since each point in
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}
has a unique representation in barycentric coordinates
[
Gue13
]
. We will refer to
\mathbb{R}^{D}
as the
full space
and the dynamics in this space as the
full system
. We show in Appendix
A.1
that if
K\leq D
, we can always assume
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}
to be a simplex. If
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}
is not a simplex, the representation of a point with barycentric coordinates might not be unique. We address this case later on in this section.

Remark 2.1
.

The idea of projecting to a convex polytope derived from the data has emerged in different sciences under different names, e.g. Archetypal Analysis
[
CB94
]
in computer science and PCCA+
[
DW05
]
in chemistry. The key motivation being that the vertexes have clear interpretabilty given they can be seen as “reference”/“landmark” points in the data space. Furthermore, all data points already in the polytope have no projection error. However, on the downside, this is a linear decomposition of the data, meaning, nonlinear dynamics in the data-space will still be nonlinear on the polytope. In Section
3.1
we introduce a specific nonlinear transformation of these new coordinates that helps to capture nonlinearity.

After projecting the data points from
\mathbb{R}^{D}
into the polytope
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma},
the second step of SPA is to find a column-stochastic matrix
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}
which propagates the projected points in the polytope. We refer to this step as “SPA II”, and formulate it as follows: let the matrices
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times K}
and
\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times T}
be the solution to (
SPA I
) for a dataset
\Xi:=[x_{1}|\cdots|x_{T}],
then find
\Lambda
such that,

\begin{split}\Lambda&=\argmin_{\Lambda^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K}}\|[\gamma_{2}|\cdots|\gamma_{T}]-\Lambda^{*}[\gamma_{1}|\cdots|\gamma_{T-1}]\|_{F},\\
\text{satisfying,}&\\
&\Lambda\geq 0\text{ and }\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Lambda_{k,\bullet}=1.\end{split}

(SPA II)

The propagator
\Lambda
gives a linear approximation of the evolution of the full system on the polytope
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}.
Furthermore, the propagator
\Lambda
being a column-stochastic matrix guarantees that the image of
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}
is again inside the polytope
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}
. In practice, the stability of reconstructions of non-linear dynamics plays an important role in applications (see, e.g.,
[
FYR+21
]
), and we note that the method (
SPA II
) satisfies it by construction.
Though the full system might be nonlinear, using the SPA method reduces the learning of the dynamics on the data-space to learning a linear system on a convex polytope. To date, the literature on the translation of the dynamics after projecting into a polytope is sadly vacuous. For this reason, we will briefly formalise and give commentary on the SPA method in the context of dynamical systems in the following section.

Remark 2.2
.

We have to clarify that in the original SPA paper
[
GPNH20
]
, Gerber et al. refer to (
SPA I
) as the SPA problem and refer to other literature for solving (
SPA II
) (e.g.,
[
SHD01
,
GONH18
,
GCS+13
]
). Hence, the notation of studying the projection and the propagator separately, (
SPA I
) and (
SPA II
), is our interpretation. Furthermore, Gerber et al. prove that the solution to their one step approach is equivalent to the two step (see Theorem 2 in
[
GPNH20
]
). Hence, computing the SPA problem separately or together only differs numerically and not analytically.

2.2
SPA in the context of dynamical systems (Part I)

In this section we formalise the (
SPA I
) map of a dynamical system into a polytope. With this map, we can derive a sufficient condition which can help translate the dynamical system in the full data-space into a new dynamical system in the polytope.

Definition 2.3
.

For
x\in\mathcal{M}
and
\gamma
a
K
-dimensional barycentric coordinate, we define the mapping of
x
into a
K
-vertexed polytope characterised by
\Sigma
as follows:

Case 1:
for
K\leq D
:

\begin{split}&\hskip 45.52458pt\rho_{\Sigma}(x):=\argmin\limits_{\gamma^{*}}\|x-\Sigma\gamma^{*}\|_{2},\hskip 28.45274pt\text{s.t. }\gamma^{*}_{\bullet}\geq 0,\hskip 9.24994pt\|\gamma^{*}\|_{1}=1.\end{split}

(2.1)

Case 2:
for
K>D
:

\begin{split}&\hskip 39.83368pt\rho_{\Sigma}(x,\gamma):=\argmin\limits_{\gamma^{*}}\|\gamma-\gamma^{*}\|_{2}\\
&\text{ s.t. }\gamma^{*}=\argmin\limits_{\gamma^{\prime}}\|x-\Sigma\gamma^{\prime}\|_{2}\text{ with }\gamma^{\prime}_{\bullet},\gamma^{*}_{\bullet}\geq 0\text{ and }\|\gamma^{\prime}\|_{1},\|\gamma^{*}\|_{1}=1.\end{split}

(2.2)

Case 2,
K>D,
is interesting because generally points can be represented using different barycentric coordinates. Therefore, there is no natural choice for a function that maps the data points (
x
) to points in the polytope (
\gamma
). For this reason, we define the mapping
\rho_{\Sigma}
to be dependent not only on
x
but also a reference coordinate
\gamma.
The intuition is that we want the barycentric coordinate of a point to minimize the distance between
x_{t}
and
\Sigma\gamma_{t}
while being closest to the reference coordinate among all those minimizers. This now makes the mapping into the polytope well-defined. This is also very natural if
f
represents time-stepping of a continuous-time dynamical system with small time step.

For Case 1,
K\leq D,
we can always assume that the (
SPA I
) solution is a
(K-1)
-dimensional simplex (a simplex is the convex hull of
K
affine-invariant points; see Lemma
A.1
in Appendix
A.1
). Furthermore, the map
\rho
is also an orthogonal projection (see Lemma
A.2
in
A.1
).

Let us consider a discrete dynamical system on a compact manifold
\mathcal{M}
of the form,

x_{t}=\textbf{f}(x_{t-1}),

where
x_{\bullet}\in\mathcal{M}
and
f:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}.

Let us now restrict ourselves to the case
K>D
. In this case, we can easily construct the following dynamical system for
\gamma_{t}
by choosing
\gamma_{t-1}
as reference coordinate in each time step. Then

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}

\displaystyle=\rho_{\Sigma}(x_{t},\gamma_{t-1}),

(2.3)

we substitute
x_{t}
by
\textbf{f}(x_{t-1})
,

\displaystyle=\rho_{\Sigma}(\textbf{f}(x_{t-1}),\gamma_{t-1}).

(2.4)

Furthermore, by the representation of
x_{t-1}
dependent on
\gamma_{t-1}
,

\displaystyle=\rho_{\Sigma}(\textbf{f}(\Sigma\gamma_{t-1}),\gamma_{t-1}),

(2.5)

and obtain a function solely depending on
\gamma_{t-1}
,

\displaystyle=:\textbf{v}(\gamma_{t-1}).

(2.6)

The function
\textbf{v}:\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}
now defines the dynamical system dependent on the
\gamma_{\bullet}
.

Hence, in (
SPA II
), we are approximating the function
v
by a linear mapping given by the matrix
\Lambda
, that is,
\gamma_{t}\approx\Lambda\gamma_{t-1}.
Furthermore, the long-term behaviour, for
k
future steps, is thus approximated by

\gamma_{t+k}=\textbf{v}^{k}(\gamma_{t})\approx\Lambda^{k}\gamma_{t}.

(2.7)

The propagator
\Lambda
being a column-stochastic matrix keeps the predictions inside the polytope. However,
\Lambda
is still only a linear approximation of
\textbf{v},
making it imprecise in the long term when
v
is nonlinear (see also Appendix
A.2
). In numerous publications, e.g., recently
[
LKB18
]
, the representation of nonlinear dynamics using the linear Koopman operator is discussed. However, this operator is infinite-dimensional.

In Section
3
, we will introduce a memory-based method that takes the projection error into account and propagates states inside the polytope in a nonlinear way. Furthermore, will show how we can use memory to also extend the dynamics into a polytope for the case when
K<D.

3  Memory SPA

In this section, we will present an extension to SPA to better approximate nonlinear dynamics while staying in the polytope setting. The key motivation for the extension comes from the use of memory and the application of Takens’ Theorem. In order to introduce memory, for a dynamical system on a smooth compact
D
-dimensional manifold
\mathcal{M}
, with a generator function
\textbf{f}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
that we assume to be invertible and an observable function
\textbf{g}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
we define a memory
M
-
delay-coordinate map
(
M\in\mathbb{N}
) of the points in the manifold
\mathcal{M}
as the function:

\Phi^{M}(x):=(\textbf{g}(x),\textbf{g}(\textbf{f}^{-1}(x)),\dots,\textbf{g}(\textbf{f}^{-M+1}(x))).

(3.1)

In the above scenario if
M>2D,
we know from Takens theorem, that the
M
-delay-coordinate map,
\Phi^{M},
is an embedding
[
Tak81
]
(see Appendix
B
for the formal construct). Furthermore, Takens’ Theorem asserts the existence of a
topologically equivalent
dynamical system on the image of
\Phi^{M}
given by,

\Phi^{M}(x_{t+1})=\Phi^{M}\circ\textbf{f}\circ(\Phi^{M})^{-1}(\textbf{g}(x_{t}),\dots,\textbf{g}(x_{t-M+1})),

(3.2)

where
(\Phi^{M})^{-1}(\textbf{g}(x_{t}),\dots,\textbf{g}(x_{t-M}))=x_{t}
. Furthermore, we can use the inverse map to reformulate the future state of the dynamical system,
x_{t+1}=\textbf{f}(x_{t}),
as a function of the
M
memory states of the observable,

x_{t+1}=\textbf{f}((\Phi^{M})^{-1}(\textbf{g}(x_{t}),\dots,\textbf{g}(x_{t-M+1}))).

(3.3)

We now harness this property by choosing our observable
g
as the projection of the data
x
onto polytope
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}
. In Takens’ original formulation, the observable
g
is scalar-valued, whereas, the projection onto the polytope is a
K
-dimensional map. However, the theorem has been extended to hold for multivariate observables, e.g.,
[
SYC91
]
. Hence, from here on in, we consider our observables to be multivariate.

3.1
Memory SPA: a path affiliation approximation

We now propose a specific observable that fits the construction of (
SPA II
) particularly well to give a new numerical method. Let
\gamma_{t}
denote the
K
-dimensional barycentric coordinates of a point
x_{t}\in\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}
and let
\gamma_{t}^{i}
denote its
i
th entry. For a fixed memory depth
M
, we define as the
path affiliations
the function

\displaystyle\Psi^{M}(\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M})_{i}

\displaystyle:=\gamma_{t-1}^{i_{1}}\cdot\gamma_{t-2}^{i_{2}}\cdots\gamma_{t-M}^{i_{M}}

(3.4)

\displaystyle J(i)

\displaystyle=[i_{1},\dots,i_{M}]

(3.5)

\Psi^{M}(\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M})
consists of all products of combinations of entries from
\gamma_{t-1}
to
\gamma_{t-M}
.
J:\{1,\dots,K^{M}\}\rightarrow\{1,\dots,K\}^{M}
denotes a function that brings all
K^{M}
different combinations of the indices
1,\dots,K
into a certain order (without loss of generality).
For example, with
K=2
and
M=2
,

\Psi^{2}(\gamma_{t},\gamma_{t-1})=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{t}^{1}\cdot\gamma_{t-1}^{1}\\
\gamma_{t}^{1}\cdot\gamma_{t-1}^{2}\\
\gamma_{t}^{2}\cdot\gamma_{t-1}^{1}\\
\gamma_{t}^{2}\cdot\gamma_{t-1}^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

We can also write the path affiliation function (
3.4
) in the form of a sequence of outer products,

\displaystyle\Psi^{M}(\gamma_{t-1},\cdots,\gamma_{t-M})

\displaystyle:=\gamma_{t-1}\otimes\dots\otimes\gamma_{t-M},

(3.6)

\displaystyle u\otimes v

\displaystyle:=\text{vec}(uv^{T}).

(3.7)

We recall that, for a time
t,
the barycentric coordinates
\gamma_{t}^{\bullet}
denote the closeness/affiliation of their respective data points
x_{t}
to the vertices
\sigma_{\bullet}
from the solution of the (
SPA I
) problem. The function
\Psi^{M}
simply takes the product of the barycentric coordinates of a sequence of time points, thereby, extending the affiliation to include all vertices through time. Furthermore, the path affiliation function is surjective onto a
K^{M}
-vertexed polytope (see Appendix
A.4
for the definition of that polytope) and its image is also a simplex, since path affiliations are stochastic vectors (Appendix
A.3
).
Additionally,
\Psi^{M}
is injective if restricted to the polytope:

Proposition 3.1
.

The path affiliation function
\Psi
as defined in Eq. (
3.4
) is injective.

Proof.

See Appendix
A.5

∎

Now, keeping in line with SPA, we want an operator which can map points from the image of the path affiliation function back into the original polytope
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}.
That is, we want a column-stochastic matrix
\hat{\Lambda}^{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K^{M}},
such that,

\gamma_{t}=\hat{\Lambda}^{M}\,\Psi^{M}(\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M}).

(3.8)

Given the path affiliation function
\Psi^{M}
is nonlinear, we hope that with the combination of
\Psi^{M}
and
\hat{\Lambda}^{M}
we can accurately approximate nonlinear systems. Given that
\hat{\Lambda}^{M}
is column-stochastic, predictions are still restricted to the (
SPA I
) polytope since we multiply a column-stochastic matrix with a stochastic vector.

Now we will formulate our extension to the (
SPA II
) problem. Let us denote by
[\Sigma,\Gamma]
the solution to (
SPA I
) for data points
x_{1},\dots,x_{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
with vertices
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{K}
. Let us define
\psi^{M}_{t-1}:=\Psi^{M}(\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M})
. Then,

\begin{split}\hat{\Lambda}^{M}&:=\argmin_{\hat{\Lambda}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K^{M}}}\left\|[\gamma_{M+1}|\cdots|\gamma_{T}]-\hat{\Lambda}^{*}[\psi^{M}_{M}|\cdots|\psi^{M}_{T-1}]\right\|_{F},\\
\text{satisfying,}&\\
&\hat{\Lambda}^{M}\geq 0\text{ and }\sum_{k=1}^{K}\hat{\Lambda}^{M}_{k,\bullet}=1.\end{split}

(mSPA)

We refer to the matrix
\hat{\Lambda}^{M}
as the
mSPA propagator
. Furthermore, the mSPA propagator
\hat{\Lambda}^{M}
maps path affiliations back onto the (
SPA I
) polytope.

Note that, Eq. (
3.8
) is not a dynamical system in the true sense since it is not closed. In order to define a closed dynamical system, we can simply augment the propagator by defining
\hat{\gamma}_{t}:=[\gamma_{t}^{T},\dots,\gamma_{t-M+1}^{T}]^{T}
and write

\hat{\gamma}_{t}=\Theta^{M}\Psi^{M}(\hat{\gamma}_{t-1})=\Theta^{M}\psi^{M}_{t-1},

(3.9)

where
\psi^{M}_{t-1}:=\Psi^{M}(\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M})
and
\Theta^{M}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\hat{\Lambda}^{M}\\
E\end{smallmatrix}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{K^{M}\times K^{M}}
. Here

is a binary matrix that retains
\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M+1}
from
\psi^{M}_{t-1}
(see Appendix
A.7

Equivalently (by Appendices
A.4
and
A.5
), we can define a dynamical system on the path affiliations by

\psi^{M}_{t}=\Psi^{M}(\Theta^{M}\psi^{M}_{t-1}).

(3.10)

With this, the mSPA propagator, together with the path affiliation function
\Psi^{M}
, progresses the dynamics in the
K^{M}
-vertexed polytope of path affiliations.
Actually, even more can be said about (

\Psi^{M}\circ\Theta^{M}

The precise reason why we use memory terms in the construction of the method has not been discussed yet. Recall that

3.3
SPA in the context of Perron–Frobenius and Koopman operators

Let us investigate the sufficiency of representing
non-linear
dynamics by the proper choice of a
linear
transformation
\hat{\Lambda}^{M}
in
mSPA
from a different perspective than above. Note that this question is reminiscent of the well-known paradigm that so-called transfer operators (Perron–Frobenius and Koopman) are linear representations of arbitrary non-linear dynamics at the cost of acting in infinite dimensions. Indeed, the Perron–Frobenius operator
\mathcal{P}
, that describes the action of the dynamics
f
on distributions, is defined on Dirac measures via
\mathcal{P}\delta_{x}=\delta_{\textbf{f}(x)}
for all
x
.

In which sense do the affiliations
\gamma^{i}_{t}
tell us something about the distribution of the system? For this, let us assume for simplicity that the SPA polytope
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}
with vertices
\sigma_{j}
is a full-dimensional simplex and denote by
\varphi_{i}:\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}\to[0,1]
the unique linear functions with
\varphi_{i}(\sigma_{j})=\delta_{ij}
(the usual “hat functions”). Denoting the
i
-th barycentric coordinate of the point
x\in\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}
by
\gamma^{i}(x)
, we have
\gamma^{i}(x)=\varphi_{i}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}}\varphi_{i}(y)\delta_{x}(y)\,dy
.
If a trajectory of the system starts in a point
x\in\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}
and stays there for all times
t\geq 0
, the approximation in mSPA is built by the observables
\gamma_{t}^{i}=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}}\varphi_{i}(y)\,[\mathcal{P}^{t}\delta_{x}](y)\,dy
.

It is thus natural to ask whether the observations
\gamma^{i}_{t}
are sufficient to represent
\mathcal{P}
. To this end, it is necessary that
(\gamma_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{N}}
can represent the natural space on which
\mathcal{P}
acts; this can for measure-preserving systems be taken to be
L^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma})
, cf.
[
KKS16
]
. Hence, is the mapping
\smash{L^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma})\ni f\mapsto\big(\int_{\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}}\varphi_{i}\,\mathcal{P}^{t}f\big)_{t\in\mathbb{N}}}
injective? Note, that for simplicity we can consider this question for a fixed
i
. By the duality of
\mathcal{P}
and the Koopman operator
\mathcal{K}
. i.e.
\int_{\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}}\varphi_{i}\,\mathcal{P}^{t}f=\int_{\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}}\mathcal{K}^{t}\varphi_{i}\,f
, the equivalent question is: Is
\mathrm{span}\{\varphi_{i},\mathcal{K}\varphi_{i},\mathcal{K}^{2}\varphi_{i},\ldots\}
a dense subspace of
L^{2}(\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma})
?
This latter question has been investigated in ergodic theory
[
EFHN15
, Chapter 18]
, but the technical nature of the results is beyond the scope of the present work.

In conclusion, it seems reasonable to restrict our attention in Eq. (
3.8
) to linear transformations
\hat{\Lambda}^{M}
to find a sufficiently powerful representation of the system at hand. The assumptions under which this is possible will be investigated elsewhere.
In Appendix
F
we discuss the connection between mSPA and methods which focus on finding a finite-dimensional approximation of the Koopman operator.

In the following section, we will put together all the SPA methods into a single numerical scheme.

4  Learning and Lifting

We have now introduced mSPA as a method to reconstruct dynamics in the barycentric coordinates. We will now place it within a numerical scheme which reconstructs dynamics in the full space. In particular, we will use
SPA I
to project to a low-dimensional polytope and mSPA to learn the dynamics. We will introduce an additional mSPA-like step to lift the predicted barycentric coordinates back to the full space.

4.1
Combining mSPA and SPA I

In the mSPA construction we build a map into a small polytope,

1

This problem reads,

\begin{split}\hat{\Lambda}^{\prime M}&=\argmin_{\Lambda^{*}}\|[\gamma^{\prime}_{M}|\cdots|\gamma^{\prime}_{T}]-\Lambda^{*}[\psi_{M}|\cdots|\psi_{T}]\|_{F}\\

Naturally, there are many possible ways of mapping from the memory polytope to the original data space. The simplest is arguably given by solving the problem

4.3
Theoretical observations on the choice of parameters

We derived the evolution of
\gamma
in the form of
\gamma_{t}=\textbf{v}(\gamma_{t-1}\,\dots,\gamma_{t-M})
(see Eq. (
3.16
)). We can find for the approximation error of mSPA

\begin{split}\gamma_{t}-\hat{\Lambda}^{M}\psi^{M}(\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M})&=\gamma_{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{K^{M}}\Lambda_{|i}(\psi^{M}_{t})_{i}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{K^{M}}(\textbf{v}(\gamma_{t-1}\,\dots,\gamma_{t-M})-\Lambda_{|i})(\psi^{M}_{t})_{i}\end{split}

where
\Lambda_{|i}
denotes the
i
th column of
\hat{\Lambda}^{M}
.

We can observe from this that we require
\Lambda_{|i}
to be close to
\textbf{v}(\gamma_{t-1}\,\dots,\gamma_{t-M})
whenever
(\psi^{M}_{t})_{i}
is high, especially when all other entries of
(\psi^{M}_{t})
are low. As a consequence, it is desirable if the entries of
\gamma
differ from each other sufficiently throughout the trajectory because otherwise all
(\psi^{M}_{t})_{i}
are comparably high at all times and the predictions made by multiplication with
\hat{\Lambda}^{M}
are similar regardless of the actual value of the dynamics. This means that the vertices from the solution of
SPA I
should not be too far apart from the domain of the dynamics but rather chosen as tight as possible. Further, the dynamics need to be sufficiently smooth for mSPA to generate accurate predictions, which clearly is the case for the majority of dynamical modelling methods. Suppose that two sequences
[x_{t-1},\dots,x_{t-M}],[x_{\tilde{t}-1},\dots,x_{\tilde{t}-M}]
are close to each other so that also
\psi^{M}_{t-1}
and
\psi^{M}_{\tilde{t-1}}
are similar to each other. Suppose the subsequent values
x_{t}
and
x_{\tilde{t}}
are far apart, as possible in, e.g., chaotic dynamics. Let us assume that only the
i
th entry of the path affiliations is considerably high (this is a rare case but serves an illustrative purpose here; it can, however, happen, if the points in the two sequences are close to a vertex). The mSPA prediction then cannot cover both cases – the behaviour of the dynamics at times
t
and
\tilde{t}
– since it is approximately given by the constant vector
\Lambda_{|i}
. Of course, generally multiple entries of
\psi^{M}
will be of high influence so that
\hat{\Lambda}^{M}
can be chosen in the best possible way to balance the different cases. However, it seems advisable if the vertices are not placed close to bifurcation points of the dynamics because there the scenario explained above can happen: barycentric coordinates and therefore path affiliations can be similar while subsequent values of the dynamics are very different from each other.

Although one could perceive the mSPA formulation of dynamics as a mere coordinate change, it proved challenging to find an analytical representation even of linear systems in the mSPA form of
\gamma_{t}=\hat{\Lambda}^{M}\psi^{M}(\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M})
with or without memory. It seems that mSPA is
not
a method that exactly represents simple systems through coordinate changes. Rather, it is an
approximative
method – with a very specific model form – to forecast systems of varying complexity, as the examples have shown.

Together with our findings on the Lorenz-96 system, the KS equation, the Chua circuit and investigations on simple harmonic oscillators (not shown in the paper), we derived the following intuitions to generally hold true. To obtain high quality using mSPA:

•

Choose the polytope from the
SPA I
problem and scale it such that the trajectory of the barycentric coordinates is as close as possible to the boundary of its domain, the
K
-vertexed unit simplex, without touching it.

•

Choose the memory time step (see Appendix
E
) so that the image of the delay-coordinate map using this memory lag has similar geometry as the original attractor. Further, it seemed that typically, if each sequence of length
M
in the full space data has at least one point that is close to or inside the polytope, mSPA worked well.

•

Choose the memory depth as high as needed to obtain sufficient quality of the model. Clearly, this is constrained by the exponential growth of the term
K^{M}
.

Conclusion

We have introduced a new data-driven method to model nonlinear dynamical systems. For this method, we project data to a lower-dimensional polytope using the novel SPA method. We then model the dynamics on the reduced barycentric coordinates in the polytope, thereby creating a stable dynamical system. To reconstruct the information of the data that is lost by the projection, we use memory of the barycentric coordinates. We defined the path affiliation function which measures the closeness of a sequence of points to each possible sequence of vertices of the polytope and construct a linear column-stochastic matrix that pushes the dynamics forward in time. Therefore, the method does not require prior intuition of the dominant structures behind the dynamics but instead uses a fixed observable.

We showed that (under standard assumptions) there exists a dynamical system on the path affiliations that is topologically conjugate to the original one using a variant of Takens’ Theorem. We further showed in several examples that our method can accurately reconstruct nonlinear dynamical systems of varying dimension and complexity.

In the future, several issues should be addressed: firstly, as pointed out before, Takens gives a potentially nonlinear function between path affiliations and subsequent barycentric coordinates which we approximate by a linear mapping. As briefly discussed earlier, one could replace the linear mSPA propagator by a suitable nonlinear function, e.g., as done in this article using a neural network whose output always gives stochastic vectors. The examples in this article have demonstrated that this could be a worthwhile endeavor. Further, the computation of the linear mSPA propagator could need simplification since the dimension of the path affiliation vectors grows exponentially with memory depth.
Moreover, we observed that the choice of the polytope could strongly influence the quality of the ensuing mSPA reconstruction. The
SPA I
problem produces a polytope which has minimal total distance to the data. It is, however, unclear at this point, if a polytope can be chosen so that the estimation of dynamics becomes easier with mSPA, except for our observation at the end of the previous section. Potentially, the
SPA I
problem could be modified, e.g., regularized, in a way to produce a polytope that is optimal also to capture the dynamics. Additionally, mSPA could be extended for dynamical systems with control input. For this aim, one could solve the
SPA I
problem for the control input space, too, translate inputs to barycentric coordinates and formulate an augmented mSPA problem.

A.2
Linear approximations of nonlinear dynamics are typically insufficient to predict long-term behaviour

With (
SPA II
), we find a matrix
\Lambda
that propagates projection coordinates linearly over time. The linearity plus the fact, that
\Lambda
is a column-stochastic matrix, give severe restrictions on the capacities regarding long-term behaviour of the method: By the Perron-Frobenius Theorem
[
Mey00
]
, a full-rank column-stochastic matrix must have eigenvalues
\lambda_{i}
with
|\lambda_{i}|\leq 1
and at least (and generally exactly) one real eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}=1
. Let us further assume no complex eigenvalues with
|\lambda_{i}|=1
. We can write subsequent application of
\Lambda
using its eigenvectors: Let
u_{1},\dots,u_{K}
be the right eigenvectors of
\Lambda
. Then they form a basis of
\mathbb{R}^{K}
. Let
\gamma_{t}
have a representation with respect to these eigenvectors given by

\gamma_{t}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}u_{k}

where the
a_{k}
are scalar-valued coefficients.
Then

\Lambda^{i}\gamma_{t}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\Lambda^{i}u_{k}=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\lambda_{k}^{i}u_{k}.

All
\lambda_{k}
where
|\lambda_{j}|<1
will vanish with increasing
i
, so that the sum converges to

\gamma^{\star}=a_{1}u_{1}

(or, more generally, to
\sum\limits_{k:\lambda_{k}=1}a_{k}u_{k}
).
Thus, in the long-term, a trajectory of barycentric coordinates created using the linear model must converge to a fixed point. Hence, also in the original state space a long-term prediction must converge to the fixed point
x^{\star}=\Sigma\gamma^{\star}
.

If there exists complex eigenvalues with absolute value
1
, at least oscillating, but generally not truly linear dynamics can be constructed with a linear model.

A.3
Proof that path affiliations are stochastic vectors

Let
\gamma_{t},\dots,\gamma_{t-M+1}
be stochastic vectors, i.e. all entries are at least
0
and sum up to
1
. Then we can prove by induction on
M
that the path affiliation vectors constructed by

\begin{split}\Psi^{M}(\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M})_{i}&=\gamma^{i_{1}}_{t}\gamma^{i_{2}}_{t-1}\cdots\gamma^{i_{M}}_{t-M+1}\\
J(i)&=[i_{1},\dots,i_{M}]\end{split}

are stochastic vectors, too.
J
again is an ordering of the tuples in
\{1,\dots,K\}^{M}
.

Let
u,v
be stochastic vectors of potentially different dimensions. Then

\sum_{i}\Psi^{2}(u,v)=\sum\limits_{i_{1},i_{2}}u_{i_{1}}v_{i_{2}}=\sum\limits_{i_{1}}u_{i_{1}}\sum\limits_{i_{2}}v_{i_{2}}=\sum\limits_{i_{1}}u_{i_{1}}=1,

(A.1)

so that also

\sum\limits_{J(i)=[i_{1},i_{2}]}\Psi^{2}(\gamma_{t-1},\gamma_{t-2})_{i}=\sum\limits_{i_{1},i_{2}}\gamma^{i_{1}}_{t-1}\gamma^{i_{2}}_{t-2}=\sum\limits_{i_{1}}\gamma^{i_{1}}_{t-1}\sum\limits_{i_{2}}\gamma^{i_{2}}_{t-2}=\sum\limits_{i_{1}}\gamma^{i_{1}}_{t-1}=1.

For every
M>1
, it holds

\Psi^{M+1}(\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M},\gamma_{t-M-1})=\Psi^{2}(\Psi^{M}(\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M}),\gamma_{t-M-1})

(at least up to permutation of the entries). By the induction hypothesis that
\Psi^{M}(\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M})
is a stochastic vector, it follows from the induction start in Eq. (

) that also
\Psi^{M+1}(\Psi^{M}(\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M}),\gamma_{t-M-1})
is a stochastic vector.

Proof that the image of the path affiliation function is a polytope

Since each point of the form
\Psi^{M}(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{M})
is a

-dimensional stochastic vector, it lies inside a unit simplex with
K^{M}
vertices. These vertices are the
K^{M}
-dimensional unit vectors. It remains to show that each point in such a polytope can be constructed by application of

to a point

.

Let
[u_{1},\dots,u_{K^{M}}]\in\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}^{M}
. We then demand existence of
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{M}
so that

\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}\\
\vdots\\
u_{K^{M}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{1}^{1}\cdots\gamma_{M}^{1}\\
\vdots\\
\gamma_{1}^{K}\cdots\gamma_{M}^{K}\end{bmatrix}.

For this to hold requires several relations between the entries of
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{M}

as

\displaystyle\gamma_{2}^{1}\cdots\gamma_{M}^{1}=\frac{u_{1}}{\gamma_{1}^{1}},\hskip 9.24994pt\gamma_{2}^{1}\cdots\gamma_{M}^{2}=\frac{u_{2}}{\gamma_{1}^{1}},\hskip 9.24994pt\dots\hskip 9.24994pt\gamma_{2}^{K}\cdots\gamma_{M}^{K}=\frac{u_{K^{M-1}}}{\gamma_{1}^{1}}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\gamma_{1}^{1}(\underbrace{\gamma_{2}^{1}\cdots\gamma_{M}^{1}+\dots+\gamma_{2}^{K}\cdots\gamma_{M}^{K}}_{=\sum_{j}\Psi^{M-1}(\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{M})_{j}=1})=u_{1}+\dots+u_{K^{M-1}}.

This can be done analogously for all entries of
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{M}

\gamma_{1}

\gamma_{1}^{1}

are required, for
\gamma_{1}^{2}

\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{M}

In total, we can see that for each vector

is surjective into the unit simplex with

Let

\begin{split}(\hat{\Lambda}^{M}\psi^{M}_{t})_{j}=&\sum\limits_{J(i)=[i_{1},\dots,i_{M}]}\hat{\Lambda}^{M}_{ji}(\psi^{M}_{t})_{i}\\

=&\sum\limits_{i_{1}}\Lambda_{ji_{1}}\gamma_{t}^{i_{1}}\sum\limits_{i_{2},\dots,i_{M}}\gamma_{t}^{i_{1}}\gamma_{t-1}^{i_{2}}\cdots\gamma_{t-M+1}^{i_{M}}\\
=&\sum\limits_{i_{1}}\Lambda_{ji_{1}}\gamma_{t}^{i_{1}}=(\Lambda\gamma_{t})_{j}.\end{split}

Hence, a
\hat{\Lambda}^{M}
constructed in this way for mSPA is equivalent to applying a solution of the standard
SPA II
problem. As a consequence, the training error of mSPA is always bounded from above by the training error of the standard
SPA II
.
∎

A.7
Choice of
E
in matrix
\Theta^{M}

The task is to extract the terms
\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M+1}
from
\Psi^{M}(\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M})
. For this aim, we need to realize that by summing over all entries of
\psi^{M}_{t-1}
which contain a certain factor
\gamma_{t-k}^{l}
, we obtain
\gamma_{t-k}^{l}
because the sum of all crossproducts (in the sense of the path affiliations) of two stochastic vectors is
1
.

Now let
\hat{\gamma}
be a vector of the form

\hat{\gamma}=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{M}\\
\vdots\\
\gamma_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{\gamma}_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\hat{\gamma}_{KM}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{KM}

where all
\gamma_{i}
are stochastic vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{K}
. We want a
(M-1)K\times K^{M}
matrix
E
that fulfills

E\Psi^{M}(\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M})=\begin{pmatrix}\gamma_{t-1}\\
\vdots\\
\gamma_{t-M+1}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{(M-1)K}.

Let

E_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }(j\in i)\;(\star)\\
0&\,\text{else}\end{cases}

By
(\star)
we mean that
\hat{\gamma}_{i}
is one of the factors in
\Psi(\hat{\gamma})_{j}
.

Let for an arbitrary
i

\hat{\gamma}_{i}=\gamma_{t-k}^{l}
. Then

\begin{split}(E\Psi^{M}(\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M}))_{i}=&\sum\limits_{j=1}^{K^{M}}E_{ij}\Psi^{M}(\gamma_{t-1},\dots,\gamma_{t-M})_{i}\\
=&\sum\limits_{J(i)=[i_{1},\dots,i_{M}],i_{k}=l}\gamma_{t-1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\gamma_{t-M}^{i_{M}}\\
=&\gamma_{t-k}^{l}\underbrace{\sum\limits_{i_{1},\dots,i_{M}}\gamma_{t-1}^{i_{1}}\cdots^{\star}{\gamma}_{t-k}^{l}\cdots\gamma_{t-M}^{i_{M}}}_{=1}=\gamma_{t-k}^{l}\end{split}

where
{}^{\star}{\gamma}_{t-k}^{l}
means that it is omitted in the product.
E
uses the fact that summing over all products of terms in which one specific term
\gamma_{t-k}^{l}
occurs is equal to
\gamma_{t-k}^{l}
. With this, this choice of
E
fulfills the demand we set on it.

A.8
The embedded dynamics are quadratic

As the memory depth
M
is fixed, we will omit it in the notation of the path affiliations. Thus, we write
\psi_{t-1}
and enumerate its elements by multiindices,

\psi_{t-1}^{i_{1}\ldots i_{M}}=\gamma_{t-1}^{i_{1}}\gamma_{t-2}^{i_{2}}\cdots\gamma_{t-M}^{i_{M}},\hskip 9.24994pti_{1},\ldots,i_{M}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}.

Similarly, we enumerate the columns of
\hat{\Lambda}^{M}
by multiindices, and we can rephrase (
3.8
) to

\gamma_{t}^{i}=\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{M}=1}^{K}\hat{\Lambda}^{M}_{i,j_{1}\ldots j_{M}}\,\psi_{t-1}^{j_{1}\ldots j_{M}}.

We now note that for arbitrary
i_{1},\ldots,i_{M-1}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
,

\gamma_{t-1}^{i_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot\gamma_{t-M+1}^{i_{M-1}}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\gamma_{t-1}^{i_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot\gamma_{t-M+1}^{i_{M-1}}\cdot\gamma_{t-M}^{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\psi_{t-1}^{i_{1}\ldots i_{M-1}\,j}.

It then follows by the above that

\displaystyle\psi_{t}^{i\,i_{1}\ldots i_{M-1}}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{t}^{i}\gamma_{t-1}^{i_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot\gamma_{t-M+1}^{i_{M-1}}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{t-1}^{i_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot\gamma_{t-M+1}^{i_{M-1}}\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{M}=1}^{K}\hat{\Lambda}^{M}_{i,j_{1}\ldots j_{M}}\psi_{t-1}^{j_{1}\ldots j_{M}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{K}\psi_{t-1}^{i_{1}\ldots i_{M-1}\,j}\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{M}=1}^{K}\hat{\Lambda}^{M}_{i,j_{1}\ldots j_{M}}\psi_{t-1}^{j_{1}\ldots j_{M}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,j_{1},\ldots,j_{M}=1}^{K}\hat{\Lambda}^{M}_{i,j_{1}\ldots j_{M}}\psi_{t-1}^{j_{1}\ldots j_{M}}\psi_{t-1}^{i_{1}\ldots i_{M-1}\,j}\,.

In summary, the mapping
\psi_{t-1}\mapsto\psi_{t}
is componentwise a quadratic polynomial.

Takens’ Theorem and definitions needed for the extension Theorem

The original delay embedding Theorem of Takens reads

Theorem B.1

(Takens, 1981
[
Tak81
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be a smooth, compact,
d
-dimensional manifold. The set of pairs
(\textup{{f}},\textup{{g}})
for which the delay embedding
\Phi
is an embedding is generic in

\mathcal{D}^{r}(\mathcal{M})
denotes the set of
r
-times continuously differentiable diffeomorphisms mapping from
\mathcal{M}
to
\mathcal{M}

f
on
\mathcal{M}

The fact that

B.2

The thickness exponent measures the degree to which subset

Definition B.3

(Thickness exponent
[
Rob05
]
)
.

Let

In

B.3
Box-counting dimension

The box-counting dimension is a measure for the dimensionality of sets.

Definition B.4

(Box-counting dimension
[
Rob05
]
)
.

The box-counting dimension of a compact set

boxdim(A)=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{-\log(N_{A}(\varepsilon))}{\log(\varepsilon)},

where

The box-counting dimension measures the speed in which the number of boxes needed to cover a set rises when decreasing their size. If

is porportional to

so that

. The box-counting dimension is generally a non-integer value for sets which are not a manifold.

Appendix F
mSPA in connection to Koopman operator based methods

We previously argued that mSPA is conceptually different than common regression methods since we project to a lower-dimensional manifold instead of directly transforming data into a higher-dimensional space. We do, however, want to point out several relations to such methods.

Identifying governing equations for the evolution of observables over time is addressed by different methods. Their common mathematical ground is the approximation of the action of the Koopman operator,
\mathcal{K}
, which is defined as propagating observables, so that
\mathcal{K}\circ\textbf{g}=\textbf{g}\circ\textbf{f}
. The Koopman operator is an infinite-dimensional linear operator
[
KNK+18
]
, for which different methods attempt to find a finite-dimensional approximation. To this end, the observable is typically transformed into a higher-dimensional space through application of scalar-valued basis functions
\textbf{g}_{1},\dots,\textbf{g}_{L}
which are then mapped back into the observation space by multiplication of a coefficient matrix
B
, i.e.,

\mathcal{K}\circ\textbf{g}(x)\approx B[\textbf{g}_{1},\dots,\textbf{g}_{L}]^{T}(x).

(F.1)

In this light, we can observe the following relations to several common system identification techniques.

Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy)

The SINDy problems reads,

\Theta=\argmin_{\Theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times L}}\|\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{g}(x_{1}),\dots,\textbf{g}(x_{T})\end{bmatrix}-\Theta^{\prime}\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{g}_{1}(x_{0})&\dots&\textbf{g}_{1}(x_{T-1})\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\textbf{g}_{L}(x_{0})&\dots&\textbf{g}_{L}(x_{T-1})\end{bmatrix}\|_{F}+c\sum_{i,j=1}^{d,L}|\Theta^{\prime}_{i,j}|.

(F.2)

for a
c\geq 0
.
In SINDy
[
BPK16a
,
BPK16b
]
, one tries to find
B
by solving a regression problem between
[\textbf{g}_{1},\dots,\textbf{g}_{L}]
and
\mathcal{K}\circ\textbf{g}
with the option to add a sparsity constraint. It has proven to an effective method in discovering the rules behind nonlinear dynamics in recent work (e.g.,
[
BBP+17
,
KNP+20
]
). mSPA can be seen as a special case of SINDy with memory terms, a special choice of basis functions – the path affiliations – and additional constraints on
\hat{\Lambda}
. Due to these choices, one is able to generate stable memory-requiring dynamics, such as in Example
4.3
. We have seen in the previous examples that SINDy is prone to find models which are unstable. Further, one has to specify the basis functions in
\eta
beforehand which either requires an intuition about the problem or a high number of basis functions. The latter can quickly yield an ill-conditioned nonlinear regression problem
[
FWM10
]
, so that the ensuing model might produce unstable dynamics. In mSPA we have a specific set of basis functions for which only three parameters,
K,K^{\prime}
and the memory depth
M
have to be specified.

In
[
LKB18
,
CLKB19
]
, two approaches to modelling dynamics are presented that are similar to the Learning and Lifting approach of mSPA. Both are based on the simultaneous learning of dimension reduction and dynamics identification in a single optimization problem. In
[
LKB18
]
, eigenfunctions of the Koopman operator of a dynamical system are found by using an autoencoder neural network. In
[
CLKB19
]
, a framework is presented in which data are compressed, propagated in time and lifted back using basis functions. In contrast to mSPA, both approaches do not use memory to counter the loss of information induced by dimension reduction. Rather, they rely on the data admitting a low dimensional representation which is sufficient to learn dynamics and make the lifting. In future work, mSPA could be modified in the light of this approach to do the dimension reduction, learning and lifting in one step to find the optimal polytope for dynamics reconstruction.

Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD)

The EDMD problem reads,

\textbf{K}=\argmin_{\textbf{K}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}}\|\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{g}_{1}(x_{1})&\dots&\textbf{g}_{1}(x_{T})\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\textbf{g}_{L}(x_{1})&\dots&\textbf{g}_{L}(x_{T})\end{bmatrix}-\textbf{K}^{\prime}\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{g}_{1}(x_{0})&\dots&\textbf{g}_{1}(x_{T-1})\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\textbf{g}_{L}(x_{0})&\dots&\textbf{g}_{L}(x_{T-1})\end{bmatrix}\|_{F}.

(F.3)

Conceptually similar is EDMD
[
WKR14
,
LDBK17
]
which focuses on determining a linear mapping between
[\textbf{g}_{1},\dots,\textbf{g}_{L}]^{T}
and
[\mathcal{K}\circ\textbf{g}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{K}\circ\textbf{g}_{L}]^{T}
with the aim of uncovering eigenvalues and -functions of the Koopman operator. A special case is Hankel Dynamic Mode Decomposition (Hankel-DMD
[
AM16
]
) where one uses memory by choosing
[\textbf{g}_{1},\dots,\textbf{g}_{L}]
as
[\textbf{g},\mathcal{K}^{-1}\circ\textbf{g},\dots,\mathcal{K}^{-M+1}\circ\textbf{g}]
for this aim. In this light, mSPA is conceptually similar to Hankel-DMD, given that both use memory terms, with the observable
g
then being the projection to the polytope
\mathcal{M}_{\Sigma}
. mSPA differs from Hankel-DMD in the facts that it does not apply a linear mapping to the memory terms directly but to the path affiliations and further aims at propagating dynamics rather than identifying characteristics of the Koopman operator. Of course, in mSPA the linear mapping is constrained to be a column-stochastic matrix.

Linear Autoregressive models (AR)

The AR problem reads,

\theta=\argmin_{\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times DM}}\|\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{g}(x_{M})&\dots&\textbf{g}(x_{T})\\
\end{bmatrix}-\theta^{\prime}\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{g}(x_{M-1})&\dots&\textbf{g}(x_{T-1})\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\textbf{g}(x_{0})&\dots&\textbf{g}(x_{T-M})\end{bmatrix}\|_{F}.

(F.4)

Algorithmically equivalent to Hankel-DMD are linear autoregressive models
[
BD91
,
SS11
,
DZZ12
]
. The aim is generally not to find eigenvalues- and functions of the Koopman operator but simply to model the evolution of
g
by a linear approximation of the mapping between
[\textbf{g},\mathcal{K}^{-1}\circ\textbf{g},\dots,\mathcal{K}^{-M+1}\circ\textbf{g}]
and
\mathcal{K}\circ\textbf{g}
. Linear AR models suffer from the same problems as linear models in general: typically they converge to a fixed point and are unable to generate nonlinear behaviour (if not applied to nonlinear coordinate transforms, such as a Fourier transform beforehand, as in
[
PD20
]
). If the dynamics are periodic, linear AR models can be used to find the periodicity but this typically requires a large memory depth (as in Example
4.2
). This again yields the need for a high number of parameters from which unstable models can ensue. As such, they are usually not recommended for the identification of nonlinear dynamics.

Nonlinear AR (NAR)

The NAR problem reads,

\theta=\argmin_{\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times L}}\|\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{g}(x_{M})&\dots&\textbf{g}(x_{T})\\
\end{bmatrix}-\theta^{\prime}\begin{bmatrix}\textbf{g}_{1}(x_{M-1},\dots,x_{0})&\dots&\textbf{g}_{1}(x_{T-1},\dots,x_{T-M})\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
\textbf{g}_{L}(x_{M-1},\dots,x_{0})&\dots&\textbf{g}_{L}(x_{T-1},\dots,x_{T-M})\end{bmatrix}\|_{F}.

(F.5)

In nonlinear AR models, one combines nonlinear basis functions and memory by estimating the function
F
in the model form
x_{t}=F(x_{t-1},\dots,x_{t-M})
. Often one defines basis functions
[\textbf{g}_{1},\dots,\textbf{g}_{L},\mathcal{K}^{-1}\circ\textbf{g}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{K}^{-M+1}\circ\textbf{g}_{L}]^{T}
, making these models a close variant to the ones determined in SINDy. Similarly to SINDy or nonlinear regression problems in general, these are prone to be ill-conditioned, which might cause trajectories generated with the ensuing model to be highly unstable. mSPA represents a nonlinear AR model which, by construction of the basis
\Psi^{M}
and the constraint on the coefficients in
\hat{\Lambda}^{M}
, guarantees stability.

In Example
4.2
we applied these methods to a variant of the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky PDE. Note that system identification for the KS PDE has also been performed in
[
LL21
]
and
[
LLC15
]
. There the authors use a Fourier representation of the solution and estimate a Nonlinear Autoregressive Moving Average Model with Exogeneous Input (NARMAX) model on some of the Fourier modes. NARMAX models are essentially nonlinear AR models which further include moving average terms of stochastic inputs and a control term (the exogeneous input). For more details we refer to
[
LL21
,
LLC15
,
CL15
,
Bil13
]
. The authors produce good results with certain memory depths but admit that they do not always derive stable models (including unstable linear AR models). Additionally, they use a Fourier perspective which is not necessarily sensible for not wave-like evolving systems. We do not see the method deployed in
[
LL21
,
LLC15
]
and mSPA as competition but rather complementary to each other, since NARMAX estimates memory-based dynamics of a manually chosen observable with chosen basis functions and mSPA estimates stable dynamics on the barycentric coordinates of points on the polytope derived with
SPA I
with specific basis functions and the option to lift back into the original state space. Future work could encompass the combination of the two approaches by, e.g., augmentation of mSPA to models with exogenous input, i.e., with control terms.
