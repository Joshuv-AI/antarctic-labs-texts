Learning a Compressive Sensing Matrix with Structural Constraints via Maximum Mean Discrepancy Optimization
Michael Koller, Wolfgang Utschick

Abstract
We introduce a learning-based algorithm to obtain a measurement matrix for compressive sensing related recovery problems. The focus lies on matrices with a constant modulus constraint which typically represent a network of analog phase shifters in hybrid precoding/combining architectures. We interpret a matrix with restricted isometry property as a mapping of points from a high- to a low-dimensional hypersphere. We argue that points on the low-dimensional hypersphere, namely, in the range of the matrix, should be uniformly distributed to increase robustness against measurement noise. This notion is formalized in an optimization problem which uses one of the maximum mean discrepancy metrics in the objective function. Recent success of such metrics in neural network related topics motivate a solution of the problem based on machine learning. Numerical experiments show better performance than random measurement matrices that are generally employed in compressive sensing contexts. Further, we adapt a method from the literature to the constant modulus constraint. This method can also compete with random matrices and it is shown to harmonize well with the proposed learning-based approach if it is used as an initialization. Lastly, we describe how other structural matrix constraints, e.g., a Toeplitz constraint, can be taken into account, too.

Introduction

In the context of possible technologies for up-and-coming cellular systems,
millimeter wave systems with a high number of antennas have been
proposed
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
5

.
One way of controlling hardware costs and power consumption is to connect the antennas to a smaller number of receiver chains,
which is done via an analog mixing network
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
For data transmission, digital precoding is combined with configuring the analog mixing network (analog precoding).

In order to design both of these precoding stages (hybrid precoding),
state information of the high-dimensional channel is necessary.
However, the presence of an analog mixing network makes channel estimation challenging
because the observation vector has a smaller dimension (number of receiver chains) than the channel vector (number of antennas) which is to be estimated.
To facilitate channel estimation in this situation, channel models can play an important role.
In particular, sparsity-related structural knowledge about channels in millimeter wave systems has been exploited,
e.g.,
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
.

This naturally leads to the application of
compressive sensing (CS)
theory in channel estimation contexts
where the analog mixing network then plays the role of the measurement or sensing matrix.
Among the best known conditions suitable measurement matrices should fulfill is the
restricted isometry property (RIP)
(e.g.,
[
14
]
).
Since deterministic constructions of measurement matrices with
RIP
are not known,
the
CS
theory uses random matrices which enjoy favorable properties with high probability
[
14
]
.

One challenge with analog mixing networks is that they cannot realize any random matrix.
Rather, the hardware is constrained to provide matrix entries with constant modulus, i.e.,
only the phase of the entries can be adapted.
Additionally, it can be argued that such phase shifter matrices might only be able to provide a finite number of different phases (quantized phases, e.g.,
[
15
]
),
thus making the realization of random matrices that are dictated by the
CS
theory more difficult.

In this paper, we propose a learning-based approach to find a deterministic measurement matrix for channel estimation.
The main idea is to interpret a matrix with
RIP
as a matrix which (approximately) maps points from a high-dimensional hypersphere to a low-dimensional hypersphere.
Moreover, we suggest to choose the matrix such that in addition the points in its range (on the low-dimensional hypersphere) are uniformly distributed
in order to combat the influence of measurement noise, which typically exists in communications systems.

We use one of the
maximum mean discrepancy (MMD)
metrics
[
16
]
as learning cost function to formalize the idea.

MMD
-based cost functions have had considerable success in machine learning, e.g., for generative networks
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
.
In numerical experiments, the matrix obtained from the proposed algorithm is shown to compete with or outperform the approach based on random matrices.
It may be argued that such a deterministic matrix might be easier to realize via an analog mixing network than random matrices.

Furthermore, we adapt an algorithm from
[
20
]
to the phase shifter constant modulus constraint
and demonstrate its good performance in numerical experiments as well.
In addition, this algorithm is shown to harmonize well with the learning-based approach of the current paper
by providing an initialization of the learning algorithm.

II
Preliminaries

In what follows, the signal model is

\bm{y}=\bm{A}\bm{h}+\bm{n}

(1)

where
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
is called measurement matrix (with
m<n
),
\bm{h}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is called channel,

\bm{y}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
is a measurement or an observation, and
\bm{n}\sim\mathcal{CN}({\bm{0}}_{m},\sigma^{2}\bm{I}_{m})
corresponds to measurement noise.
The matrix
\bm{A}
represents an analog mixing network with phase shifters
so that the matrix entries
[\bm{A}]_{k,l}
have the form

[\bm{A}]_{k,l}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\exp(\imag\phi_{k,l})

(2)

with a phase
\phi_{k,l}\in\mathbb{R}
.
All entries have a constant modulus constraint,
|[\bm{A}]_{k,l}|=1/\sqrt{m}
,
which is why we refer to such a matrix as constant modulus matrix.

This paper provides two methods to determine a constant modulus measurement matrix
\bm{A}

for recovering
\bm{h}
from
(
1
)
.
The main contribution is a novel learning-based algorithm.
Additionally, we adapt a method from the literature to the constant modulus constraint.
This method computes a different matrix
\bm{A}
for every
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
,
whereas the learning-based algorithm yields a single matrix which can be employed for all
SNRs
.
We define the
SNR
as

\mathrm{SNR}=\frac{\|\bm{A}\bm{h}\|^{2}}{\expec[\|\bm{n}\|^{2}]}=\frac{\|\bm{A}\bm{h}\|^{2}}{m\sigma^{2}},

(3)

where the noise variance
\sigma^{2}
is adjusted for every pair
(\bm{A},\bm{h})
to achieve the desired
SNR
.

Since the goal is to estimate the
n
-dimensional channel from an
m
-dimensional observation with
m<n
,
the inverse problem
(
1
)
generally has no unique solution.
However, it is well known from
CS
theory that a unique solution can exist if additional structural information about the channels is available.
One channel model that was studied in this context (see, e.g.,
[
12
,
13
]
) can be written as

\bm{h}=\sum_{k=1}^{p}s_{k}\bm{a}(\theta_{k})

(4)

which corresponds to a sum of
p
paths which arrive from angles
\theta_{k}\in[0,2\pi]
with path gains
s_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
at the base station.
For a uniform linear array at the base station, the steering vector
\bm{a}(\theta_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
has the form

\bm{a}(\theta_{k})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\begin{bmatrix}\exp(\imag 0\cdot\theta_{k})&\dots&\exp(\imag(n-1)\cdot\theta_{k})\end{bmatrix}^{\operatorname{T}}.

(5)

We briefly highlight relevant parts of
CS
theory in the next section
where we then explain how
CS
is used with this channel model.

II-A

Compressive Sensing

CS
studies the inverse problem
(
1
)
for general (no constant modulus constraint) matrices
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
.
In the case where
\bm{h}=\bm{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a
p
-sparse vector (
p
nonzero entries),
it can be recovered if
\bm{B}
has a sufficient
RIP

[
14
]
.
A matrix
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
is said to have the
RIP
if the restricted isometry constant
\delta\geq 0
is small in

(1-\delta)\|\bm{s}\|^{2}\leq\|\bm{B}\bm{s}\|^{2}\leq(1+\delta)\|\bm{s}\|^{2}

(6)

for all
p
-sparse vectors
\bm{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}

[
14
]
.

More generally, it may be assumed that the signals of interest,
\bm{h}
,
allow for a sparse representation
\bm{h}={\bm{\Psi}}\bm{s}
in some basis
{\bm{\Psi}}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}

such that the
RIP
condition is formulated where
\bm{s}
is replaced with
{\bm{\Psi}}\bm{s}
.
Even more generally,
\bm{h}
may lie in an (infinite) union of subspaces or some abstract subset
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}

(see, e.g.,
[
21
,
22
,
23
]
)
and one defines a generalized notion of
RIP
as

(1-\delta)\|\bm{h}\|^{2}\leq\|\bm{B}\bm{h}\|^{2}\leq(1+\delta)\|\bm{h}\|^{2}

(7)

for all
\bm{h}\in\mathcal{H}
.

Algorithm 1

Orthogonal Matching Pursuit (OMP)
[
14
]

0:

matrix
\bm{C}
, observation
\bm{y}
, sparsity
p

1:

S^{(0)}\leftarrow\emptyset
,
\bm{s}^{(0)}\leftarrow{\bm{0}}

2:

for

i=1
to
p

do

3:

j^{*}\leftarrow\argmax_{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\{[\bm{C}^{\operatorname{H}}(\bm{y}-\bm{C}\bm{s}^{(i-1)})]_{j}\}

4:

S^{(i)}\leftarrow S^{(i-1)}\cup\{j^{*}\}

5:

\bm{s}^{(i)}\leftarrow\argmin_{\tilde{\bm{s}}}\{\|\bm{y}-\bm{C}\tilde{\bm{s}}\|,\,\mathrm{support}(\tilde{\bm{s}})\subset S^{(i)}\}

6:

end

for

7:

return

\bm{s}^{(p)}\quad
(
p
-sparse vector)

One way of translating the model
(
4
)
into a
CS
setting is to introduce a so-called dictionary

{\bm{\Psi}}_{L}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{a}(\hat{\theta}_{1})&\dots&\bm{a}(\hat{\theta}_{L})\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times L}

(8)

which collects
L
steering vectors corresponding to a grid of

L
equidistantly sampled angles
\hat{\theta}_{\ell}
in
[0,2\pi]
.
Then, one approximates
\bm{h}\approx{\bm{\Psi}}_{L}\bm{s}
with a
p
-sparse vector
\bm{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{L}
which contains the path gains.
Channel estimation can now be performed by writing

\tilde{\bm{y}}=(\bm{A}{\bm{\Psi}}_{L})\bm{s}+\bm{n}

(9)

and by using for example
orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP)

[
24
,
25
,
26
]
to recover an estimate
\hat{\bm{s}}
of
\bm{s}

so that the channel is estimated as
\hat{\bm{h}}={\bm{\Psi}}_{L}\hat{\bm{s}}
.

OMP
is described in
Algorithm

1
and would be used with
\bm{C}=\bm{A}{\bm{\Psi}}_{L}
, see, e.g.,
[
12
]
.
Note that
(
9
)
is just an auxiliary approximation of
(
1
)

because
{\bm{\Psi}}_{L}\bm{s}
is an approximation of
\bm{h}
.
In particular, channels of the form
(
4
)
do not have an exact representation
in terms of
{\bm{\Psi}}_{L}
whenever
\theta_{k}\notin\{\hat{\theta}_{1},\dots,\hat{\theta}_{L}\}

for some
k=1,\dots,p
.

To obtain a matrix with
RIP
, one usually resorts to drawing random matrices which can fulfill the property with high probability
[
14
]
.
A typical example is a Gaussian random matrix
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}

where the entries are drawn independently with
[\bm{B}]_{k,l}\sim\mathcal{CN}(0,\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}})
.
These, however, cannot be realized via an analog mixing network.
More generally, sub-Gaussian random matrices have been shown to fulfill the
RIP

[
14
]
.
Sub-Gaussian random variables include bounded random variables
[
14
]
.
Thus, it makes sense to consider random constant modulus matrices
\bm{A}

which are randomly drawn with independent entries of the form

\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\exp(\imag\phi)\quad\text{with}\quad\phi\sim\mathcal{U}([0,2\pi])

(10)

where
\phi
is uniformly distributed in the interval
[0,2\pi]
.

III
Learning a Measurement Matrix

To motivate the main idea of this paper, we first rewrite the
RIP
condition
(
7
)
in the form

(1-\delta)\leq\left\|\bm{B}\frac{\bm{h}}{\|\bm{h}\|}\right\|^{2}\leq(1+\delta)

(11)

for all
\bm{h}\in\mathcal{H}
.
Recovery algorithms need
\delta\geq 0
to be small enough.
In the best case, we have
\delta=0
and
(
11
)
reads

\left\|\bm{B}\frac{\bm{h}}{\|\bm{h}\|}\right\|^{2}=1.

(12)

Here,
\tilde{\bm{h}}:=\frac{\bm{h}}{\|\bm{h}\|}
is a vector on the (high-dimensional) hypersphere in
\mathbb{C}^{n}

and according to
(
12
)
the matrix
\bm{B}
has the property
that the vector
\bm{B}\tilde{\bm{h}}
lies on the (low-dimensional) hypersphere in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
.
In the general case, in order for
{\bm{\delta}}
to be as small as possible,

(
11
)
requires the norm
\|\bm{B}\tilde{\bm{h}}\|
to be as close to one as possible.
In other words, we want to find a matrix which brings
\bm{B}\tilde{\bm{h}}
as close to the
hypersphere in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
as possible.

Let
\bm{h},\bm{h}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
be two different vectors
and let
\bm{y}=\bm{B}\bm{h}
and
\bm{y}^{\prime}=\bm{B}\bm{h}^{\prime}
be the corresponding noiseless observations.
The
RIP
formalizes the notion that the distance between
\bm{y}
and
\bm{y}^{\prime}

should be proportional to the distance between
\bm{h}
and
\bm{h}^{\prime}

such that two vectors
\bm{h},\bm{h}^{\prime}
which are far apart from each other
do not lead to the same observation even if the observations are noisy.
As a consequence,
\bm{h}
and
\bm{h}^{\prime}
can still be uniquely recovered if the noise is small.
In this sense, the
RIP
guarantees some robustness against measurement noise.

In the spirit of the above description,
it seems sensible in general to try to find a matrix
\bm{B}

which places two different observations far apart from each other,
so that even in the presence of noise, they are distinguishable.
When we work with the normalized vectors
\tilde{\bm{h}}
,
this means that we now have two goals:
On the one hand,
\|\bm{B}\tilde{\bm{h}}\|
should be close to one in order to achieve a
RIP
,
on the other hand, two different
\bm{B}\tilde{\bm{h}}
and
\bm{B}\tilde{\bm{h}}^{\prime}
should be far apart
from each other.
The latter goal can also be formulated as follows:
We want to find a matrix
\bm{B}
which distributes
\bm{B}\tilde{\bm{h}}
isotropically around the origin.

To cast this idea into an optimization problem, let
\bm{u}
be a random variable which is uniformly distributed on the hypersphere in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
.
That is, we have
\|\bm{u}\|=1
and
\bm{u}
is isotropically distributed around the origin.
Further, let all elements in
\mathcal{H}
be realizations of a random variable
\bm{h}

and consider again the normalized
\tilde{\bm{h}}
.
Then, if
d
denotes a (for now generic) distance between the distributions of
\bm{B}\tilde{\bm{h}}
and
\bm{u}
,
the goal is to choose
\bm{B}
such that this distance is minimized.
Together with the original goal to find a suitable constant modulus matrix
\bm{A}
, we arrive at the abstract optimization problem:

\min_{\bm{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n},|[\bm{A}]_{k,l}|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}}d(\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}},\bm{u}).

(13)

We make this notion more precise in
Section

III-B
.
To this end,
Section

III-A
first reviews the concept of
MMDs
which can be used to define a distance between probability distributions.

The optimization problem
(
13
)
encodes three goals.
In order for
\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}}
to be uniformly distributed on the hypersphere,
the norm
\|\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}}\|
needs to be as close to one as possible
since the norm of
\bm{u}
always equals one.
This corresponds to finding a matrix which has the
RIP
with
\delta
in
(
11
)
as small as possible.
At the same time,
\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}}
needs to be isotropically distributed around the origin
because this is a property of
\bm{u}
.
This corresponds to placing noiseless observations far apart from each other.
Therefore, problem
(
13
)
simultaneously aims for a
RIP
condition as well as for robustness against noise.
Finally, due to the constraints, we obtain a constant modulus matrix.

III-A

Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)

To state problem
(
13
)
more precisely, a distance between the probability distributions of
\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}}
and
\bm{u}
is necessary.
Many distances, for example the Kullback-Leibler divergence, can only be evaluated
if closed-form expressions of the distributions are available or if the distributions are estimated beforehand.
In the general case, we have access to samples of the channel
\bm{h}
, which are, e.g., obtained from a simulation environment or a measurement campaign.
Moreover, the distribution of
\bm{h}
, and thus in particular also of
\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}}
, is not known.
It is therefore more convenient to work with a distance which can be evaluated based on samples from the involved distributions,
i.e., samples of
\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}}
and
\bm{u}
.
In the following, we introduce one of the
MMD
distances which has this property.

Let
(\mathcal{X},d)
be a metric space and let
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}
be two random variables with respective probability measures
p
and
q
.

MMD
provides the possibility to define a metric on the space of probability measures on
\mathcal{X}
.
For a given set
\mathcal{F}
of functions
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
,

MMD
is defined as
[
16
, Definition 2]

\mmd_{\mathcal{F}}(p,q)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}(\expec_{\bm{x}\sim p}[f(\bm{x})]-\expec_{\bm{y}\sim q}[f(\bm{y})]).

(14)

Depending on how
\mathcal{F}
is chosen,
MMD
can be a metric
and then we have
\mmd_{\mathcal{F}}(p,q)=0
if and only if
p=q
, and
\mmd_{\mathcal{F}}(p,q)>0
otherwise.
An overview of suitable function sets
\mathcal{F}
can, e.g., be found in
[
27
]
.

Even if
MMD
is a metric, the evaluation of
(
14
)
, i.e., solving the optimization problem, might still be difficult.
One particularly interesting set
\mathcal{F}
in this regard is the unit ball in a
reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS)
.
A brief introduction into
RKHS
can be found in
[
16
]
.
Every
RKHS
is a Hilbert space which is associated with a kernel
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
.
And, conversely, every kernel defines an associated
RKHS
.
Intuitively speaking, a kernel
k
can represent all functions which are an element of the
RKHS
.
For this reason, the optimization w.r.t. the whole set
\mathcal{F}
in
(
14
)
can be expressed by means of the kernel only
and the computation of the supremum is avoided.

Among the best known kernels is the Gaussian one:

k_{\sigma}:\mathbb{R}^{2m}\times\mathbb{R}^{2m}\to\mathbb{R},\>(\bm{x},\bm{y})\mapsto k_{\sigma}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=e^{-\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\bm{x}-\bm{y}\|^{2}}

(15)

with a parameter
\sigma>0
.
Using the Gaussian kernel,
(
14
)
can be expressed as
[
16
, Lemma 6]

\mmd_{k}^{2}(p,q)=\expec_{\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime}}[k(\bm{x},\bm{x}^{\prime})]\\
-2\expec_{\bm{x},\bm{y}}[k(\bm{x},\bm{y})]+\expec_{\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime}}[k(\bm{y},\bm{y}^{\prime})]

(16)

and it is a metric.
Here,
\bm{x}
and
\bm{x}^{\prime}
are independent random variables with distribution
p
,
and
\bm{y}
and
\bm{y}^{\prime}
are independent random variables with distribution
q
.
The Gaussian kernel is a typical choice when
\mmd_{k}
is used, as, e.g.,
[
17
,
19
,
28
]
demonstrate.
One reason is that the associated
RKHS
enjoys the property of being universal which makes
(
16
)
a metric
[
16
, Theorem 5]
.

If only samples
\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
and
\{\bm{y}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
of
\bm{x}\sim p
and
\bm{y}\sim q
, respectively, are given,

(
16
)
allows for a biased estimate
[
16
, Equation (5)]
:

\mmd_{k}^{2}(\{\bm{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M},\{\bm{y}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\frac{k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{x}_{j})}{M^{2}}\\
-2\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{k(\bm{x}_{i},\bm{y}_{j})}{MN}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{k(\bm{y}_{i},\bm{y}_{j})}{N^{2}}.

(17)

Throughout the paper, we employ this estimate
(
17
)
which converges in probability to
(
16
)

at a rate of
\mathcal{O}((M+N)^{-\frac{1}{2}})
, see
[
16
, Theorem 7]
.
A detailed analysis of the metric can be found in
[
16
]
.

Since the kernel
(
15
)
is differentiable, so is
\mmd_{k}
.
This, together with the fact that only samples from the involved distributions are required,
makes
(
17
)
interesting for learning applications.
For example,
[
17
]
uses
\mmd_{k}
as neural network cost function in the context of data generation.
There, a set of true images is given and the goal is to find a function which takes random noise as argument
and yields artificial new images as function values.
The function is chosen to be a neural network and the goal is to minimize
\mmd_{k}

between its output and the true images,
so that the neural network is able to generate photo-realistic images
which resemble the true ones.
The minimization problem is solved via stochastic gradient descent with impressive results.

III-B

Learning

With the help of
MMD
, we express
(
13
)
as a learning problem.
To this end, we want to replace the notion of a generic distance
d
in
(
13
)
between
\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}}
and
\bm{u}
with
\mmd_{k}
.

For this purpose, we represent
\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}}
and
\bm{u}
in terms of real quantities because it also simplifies the implementation.
Let

\stack:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{2m},\>\bm{z}\mapsto\stack(\bm{z})=[\Re(\bm{z})^{\operatorname{T}},\Im(\bm{z})^{\operatorname{T}}]^{\operatorname{T}}

(18)

denote stacking real and imaginary parts of a complex vector
\bm{z}
into a real vector
\stack(\bm{z})
.
Further, let
\{\tilde{\bm{h}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T_{\mathrm{tr}}}
and
\{\bm{u}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T_{\mathrm{tr}}}
be training samples.
Here,
\{\tilde{\bm{h}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T_{\mathrm{tr}}}
is a sample of normalized channel data points
and
\{\bm{u}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T_{\mathrm{tr}}}
a set of points drawn from a uniform distribution on the hypersphere in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
.
The channel data points may either stem from a measurement campaign or may be artificially generated according to a channel model.
A random vector
\bm{u}
with uniform distribution on the hypersphere in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
is obtained via normalization of an isotropic Gaussian random vector, e.g.,

\bm{u}=\frac{\bm{v}}{\|\bm{v}\|}
with
\bm{v}\sim\mathcal{C}\mathcal{N}({\bm{0}}_{m},\bm{I}_{m})
, which means that sampling is simple.
We then express
(
13
)
as follows:

\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}\\
|[\bm{A}]_{k,l}|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\end{subarray}}\mmd_{k}^{2}(\{\stack(\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{T_{\mathrm{tr}}},\{\stack(\bm{u}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{T_{\mathrm{tr}}}).

(19)

Next, we address the constant modulus constraint
|[\bm{A}]_{k,l}|=1/\sqrt{m}

and express the optimization problem in terms of real optimization variables instead of
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
.
Note that since every entry of
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
has to have the form
[\bm{A}]_{k,l}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\exp(\imag\phi_{k,l})
with a phase
\phi_{k,l}\in\mathbb{R}
,
the constant modulus matrix
\bm{A}
is completely parameterized by
mn
real parameters.
We collect these parameters in a matrix
{\bm{\Phi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

such that we can write
\bm{A}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\exp(\imag{\bm{\Phi}})
where the exponential function is meant to be applied element-wise
and where the constraint
|[\bm{A}]_{k,l}|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}
is already incorporated.
If also
\sin
and
\cos
are applied element-wise,
we can write

\bm{A}=\Re(\bm{A})+\imag\Im(\bm{A})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\cos({\bm{\Phi}})+\imag\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}\sin({\bm{\Phi}})

(20)

so that we get from
\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}}=(\Re(\bm{A})+\imag\Im(\bm{A}))(\Re(\tilde{\bm{h}})+\imag\Im(\tilde{\bm{h}}))
:

\stack(\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}})=\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}})\\
\Im(\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\cos({\bm{\Phi}})}{\sqrt{m}}&-\frac{\sin({\bm{\Phi}})}{\sqrt{m}}\\
\frac{\sin({\bm{\Phi}})}{\sqrt{m}}&\frac{\cos({\bm{\Phi}})}{\sqrt{m}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\tilde{\bm{h}})\\
\Im(\tilde{\bm{h}})\end{bmatrix}.

(21)

Equation

21
reveals that
\stack(\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}})
only depends on
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
via the real matrix
{\bm{\Phi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.
Let us express this as
\stack(\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}})=\stack(\bm{A}({\bm{\Phi}})\tilde{\bm{h}})
so that we can rewrite
(
19
)
as a real optimization problem:

\min_{{\bm{\Phi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}}\mmd_{k}^{2}(\{\stack(\bm{A}({\bm{\Phi}})\tilde{\bm{h}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{T_{\mathrm{tr}}},\{\stack(\bm{u}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{T_{\mathrm{tr}}}).

(22)

In the literature, such optimization problems are solved via stochastic gradient descent,
e.g.,
[
17
,
19
,
28
,
29
]
.
Since gradient descent can be employed and seems to yield good results,

MMD
-based problems are considered to be easy to optimize
[
29
]
.
Accordingly, we also use stochastic gradient descent to solve problem
(
22
)
.
The details are summarized in
Algorithm

2
.
General convergence guarantees are part of ongoing research.
The standard procedure in machine learning is
to employ some form of grid or random search over various hyperparameters of the
stochastic gradient descent algorithm in combination with random initializations
of the optimization parameters.
A validation data set is then used to choose the best solution.
Possible hyperparameter values for
Algorithm

2
are described in
Section

V-B
.

In every iteration of

,
\mmd_{k}^{2}
and its gradient need to be calculated.
With backpropagation, evaluating the gradient has the same order of computation time

[

.

for a batch of

the kernel is evaluated

IV
Related Work

This section reviews prior work related to learning a measurement matrix and describes a modification of one of the presented methods
which allows us to employ it in the context of the signal model described in
Section

II
.

IV-A

Prior Work

The authors of
[
31
]
consider the real-valued signal model
\bm{y}=\bm{B}\bm{h}+\bm{n}

where
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a general real matrix.
Their
Uncertain Component Analysis
determines the matrix as

\bm{B}=\argmax_{\tilde{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},\tilde{\bm{B}}\tilde{\bm{B}}^{\operatorname{T}}=\bm{I}}\prod_{i}\mathrm{Pr}(\bm{h}_{i}\mid\bm{y}_{i};\tilde{\bm{B}})

(23)

with given data points
\bm{h}_{i}
and
\bm{y}_{i}=\tilde{\bm{B}}\bm{h}_{i}
and
where
\mathrm{Pr}(\bm{h}_{i}\mid\bm{y}_{i};\tilde{\bm{B}})
is the posterior probability of the data.
An algorithmic solution based on two fixed-point equations is proposed.

In
[
32
]
, the signal model is
\bm{y}=\bm{B}{\bm{\Psi}}\bm{s}

with
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
{\bm{\Psi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times L}
(
n<L
).
The goal is to minimize the
t
-averaged mutual coherence
\mu_{t}(\bm{B}{\bm{\Psi}})
with resepect to
\bm{B}
.
To compute
\mu_{t}(\bm{B}{\bm{\Psi}})
, the columns of
\bm{B}{\bm{\Psi}}
are normalized and
then the Gram matrix
\bm{G}=(\bm{B}{\bm{\Psi}})^{\operatorname{T}}(\bm{B}{\bm{\Psi}})
is computed.
The average of all entries
|[\bm{G}]_{ij}|\geq t
is defined to be
\mu_{t}(\bm{B}{\bm{\Psi}})
.
The proposed iterative solution algorithm proceeds in two alternating stages.
Starting from some initial
\bm{B}^{(1)}
,
in iteration
i
the Gram matrix
\bm{G}^{(i)}=(\bm{B}^{(i)}{\bm{\Psi}})^{\operatorname{T}}(\bm{B}^{(i)}{\bm{\Psi}})
is computed
and its entries are modified such that
\mu_{t}(\bm{B}^{(i)}{\bm{\Psi}})
decreases,
yielding a new matrix
\hat{\bm{G}}^{(i)}
.
In order to decrease
\mu_{t}(\bm{B}^{(i)}{\bm{\Psi}})
, a
down-scaling factor

\gamma\in(0,1)
,
which is chosen before the algorithm’s main iterations start,
is used to
shrink
all entries of
\bm{G}^{(i)}
whose absolute values are larger than
t
by multiplying them by
\gamma
.
In the second stage, a decomposition
\hat{\bm{G}}^{(i)}\approx(\bm{B}^{(i+1)}{\bm{\Psi}})^{\operatorname{T}}(\bm{B}^{(i+1)}{\bm{\Psi}})
is found
to obtain a new matrix
\bm{B}^{(i+1)}
.
These two stages are alternated for a number of iterations.

Similar to
[
32
]
, the authors of
[
33
]
work with the Gram matrix
\bm{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}
.
Their goal is to choose
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
such that
\bm{G}
is as close to an identity matrix as possible.
To this end,
\bm{B}
is initialized randomly and then a KSVD-like algorithm consisting of
m
(number of rows of
\bm{B}
)
steps is computed.
Additionally, an extension is proposed where also the dictionary
{\bm{\Psi}}
can be optimized.

The goal of
[
34
]
is to choose
\bm{B}
such that a bi-Lipschitz condition is established.
For given data points
\{\bm{h}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}
, this condition is expressed by replacing
\bm{h}
in
(
7
)
with differences
\bm{h}_{i}-\bm{h}_{j}
.
Therefore, the authors first define the secant set

\mathcal{S}=\{\frac{\bm{h}_{i}-\bm{h}_{j}}{\|\bm{h}_{i}-\bm{h}_{j}\|},1\leq i<j\leq T\}

and then express the bi-Lipschitz criterion for this set as
|\|\bm{B}\bm{v}\|^{2}-1|=|\bm{v}^{\operatorname{T}}\bm{B}^{\operatorname{T}}\bm{B}\bm{v}-1|\leq\delta
with
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{S}
.
Using the Gram matrix
\bm{G}=\bm{B}^{\operatorname{T}}\bm{B}
,
this motivates the following optimization problem to find a matrix
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
:

\min_{\bm{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}}\sup_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{S}}|\bm{v}^{\operatorname{T}}\bm{G}\bm{v}-1|\\
\text{s.t.}\quad\bm{G}\succeq 0,\quad\mathrm{rank}(\bm{G})=r,\quad\mathrm{trace}(\bm{G})=b.

(24)

The solution algorithm is based on a relaxation of
(
24
)
.

A related problem is studied in
[
35
]
:

\min_{\bm{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\bm{G}=\bm{G}^{\operatorname{T}},\bm{G}\succeq 0}\mathrm{trace}(\bm{G})\quad\text{s.t.}\,\sup_{\bm{v}\in\mathcal{S}}|\bm{v}^{\operatorname{T}}\bm{G}\bm{v}-1|\leq\delta.

(25)

Once a feasible
\bm{G}^{*}
is determined, the matrix
\bm{B}
is found
via an eigendecomposition of
\bm{G}^{*}
.

An approach motivated by neural network learning is presented in
[
36
]
.
The signal model is
\bm{y}=\bm{B}\bm{s}

with nonnegative sparse vectors
\bm{s}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.
Motivated by an
\ell_{1}
-norm minimization approach,
the authors propose to utilize the following projected subgradient update rule to learn
\bm{B}
:

\bm{s}^{(i+1)}=\bm{s}^{(i)}-\frac{\beta}{i}(\bm{I}-\bm{B}^{\operatorname{T}}\bm{B})\mathrm{sign}(\bm{s}^{(i)})

(26)

with the initialization
\bm{s}^{(1)}=\bm{B}^{\operatorname{T}}\bm{y}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.
Every gradient iteration
(
26
)
is interpreted as a layer and
I
such gradient layers are concatenated.
This is called gradient unrolling.
The final output and estimate for
\bm{s}
is given by
\hat{\bm{s}}=\mathrm{ReLU}(\bm{s}^{(I+1)})
,
where
\mathrm{ReLU}
denotes the usual rectified linear unit
which performs the operation
\max(x,0)
on every element
x
of its argument.
Then,
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
are interpreted as learnable parameters
and stochastic gradient descent is employed to learn
\bm{B}
and
\beta
.
Specifically, given training data
\{\bm{s}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}
, a stochastic gradient descent method is used to solve

\min_{\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},\beta\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\|\bm{s}_{i}-\hat{\bm{s}}_{i}\|^{2}.

(27)

This gradient unrolling method was again employed in
[
37
]
for channel state information feedback.
To this end, a channel
\bm{h}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
is compressed as
\bm{y}=\bm{B}[\Re(\bm{h})^{\operatorname{T}},\Im(\bm{h})^{\operatorname{T}}]^{\operatorname{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

with
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times 2n}
such that the base station
can recover
\bm{h}
from the feedback signal
\bm{y}
via compressive sensing methods.
A modification of
[
36
]
for channel estimation is described in
[
38
]
,
where real and imaginary parts of channels
\bm{h}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
are treated as independent data points
such that a real matrix
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
can be learned.

One challenge with the methods presented so far is that general real-valued matrices
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
are found
whereas we are seeking a complex-valued matrix
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
where all entries
[\bm{A}]_{k,l}
have the same absolute value.
This constant modulus constraint is the main challenge.
In particular, methods which are based on a decomposition of the form
\bm{G}=\bm{B}^{\operatorname{T}}\bm{B}
are difficult to apply
since such a decomposition need not exist when the entries of
\bm{B}
are required to have a constant modulus.
However, a modification of the approach published in
[
20
]
allows us to incorporate the constraint.

The approach investigated in
[
20
]
is based on the model

\bm{y}=\bm{P}_{\Omega}\bm{V}\bm{h}

(28)

where
\bm{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
is a basis (a unitary matrix,
\bm{V}^{\operatorname{H}}\bm{V}=\bm{I}
)
and
\bm{P}_{\Omega}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
performs subsampling by selecting
m=|\Omega|
rows of
\bm{V}
.
Once the row index set
\Omega\subset\{1,2,\dots,n\}
is chosen, the measurement matrix is given by
\bm{B}=\bm{P}_{\Omega}\bm{V}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
.
One strategy is to select the indices
\Omega
randomly.
However, the authors of
[
20
]
argue that one can improve
upon this by considering a data-based selection procedure.

A criterion for selecting
\Omega
for which an exact solution is provided in
[
20
]
can be described as follows.
Let
\{\bm{h}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T}
be given training data with norm one.
Then, select those
m
rows
\bm{v}_{r}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
of
\bm{V}
which maximize

\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\sum_{r\in\Omega}|\langle\bm{v}_{r},\bm{h}_{i}\rangle|^{2}
.
This corresponds to maximizing the average captured energy
\|\bm{P}_{\Omega}\bm{V}\bm{h}_{i}\|^{2}
of the observed data.
We call the procedure LBCS after the paper’s title and it is outlined in
Algorithm

3
.

IV-C

Adaptation of LBCS

Since
(
28
)
considers the noiseless case and since it is assumed that a unitary matrix is already given,
we suggest the following adaptation of the algorithm to obtain a matrix
\bm{A}

which can be employed in the context of the current paper.

Because we need a constant modulus matrix,
the first modification is to divide the entries of
\bm{V}
by their respective absolute values.

Algorithm

3
can then be used to select
m
rows of this constant modulus matrix.
Second, we do a Monte Carlo search over different initializations of the unitary matrix
\bm{V}
.
To explain, we draw a unitary matrix
\bm{V}
uniformly at random,
divide its entries by their absolute values,
and run
Algorithm

3
to obtain a constant modulus matrix
\bm{A}
.
An algorithm for generating random unitary matrices can, e.g., be found in
[
39
]
.
Then, validation data
\{\bm{h}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T_{\mathrm{val}}}
is used to evaluate the channel
estimation performance of the current
\bm{A}
in terms of
mean squared error (MSE)
.
The best matrix
\bm{A}_{\mathrm{MC}}
of
I
such iterations is then the final result of the Monte Carlo search.
The whole procedure is outlined in
Algorithm

4
.

As argued in
[
17
]
, finding the optimal
\sigma
which makes
MMD
most efficient is an open problem,
but a mixture of kernels may serve the intended purpose.
Therefore, we use
\mmd_{k}
in
(
17
)
with the kernel

k(x,y)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}}k_{\sigma}(x,y)

(32)

where
\mathcal{S}=\{2,5,10,20,40,80\}
.

We use Pytorch
[
40
]
to implement the stochastic gradient optimization in
Algorithm

2
with the Adam optimizer
[
41
]
.
There are three hyperparameters: batch size
T
, learning rate (gradient step size)
l_{r}
, and exponential learning rate decay
\beta
.
As described in
[
42
]
, we determine these three hyperparameters via random search.
In detail, we draw
(T,l_{r},\beta)\in[150,1500]\times[10^{-6},5\cdot 10^{-3}]\times[0.94,1]
randomly and run
Algorithm

2
.
This is repeated 64 times and validation data is then used to pick the best of the 64 so-obtained matrices
\bm{A}
.
The number of training samples is always
T_{\mathrm{tr}}=50000
in all cases and settings.
For reasons explained later in
Section

V-E
, training data points
\{\bm{h}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T_{\mathrm{tr}}}
are normalized as follows:

\tilde{\bm{h}}_{i}=\frac{\bm{h}_{i}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{T_{\mathrm{tr}}}\sum_{j=1}^{T_{\mathrm{tr}}}\|\bm{h}_{j}\|^{2}}},\quad\forall i=1,2,\dots,T_{\mathrm{tr}}.

(33)

The termination criterion for
Algorithm

2
is early stopping
[
43
]
.

V-E

Training Data Normalization

Lastly, we want to discuss training data preprocessing for
Algorithm

2
.
The initial idea described in
Section

III
is to regard
\bm{A}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
as a mapping
from one hypersphere (in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
) to another hypersphere (in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
).
This implies to normalize the training data
\{\bm{h}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{T_{\mathrm{tr}}}
via

\tilde{\bm{h}}_{i}=\frac{\bm{h}_{i}}{\|\bm{h}_{i}\|},\quad\forall i=1,2,\dots,T_{\mathrm{tr}}

(34)

such that
\tilde{\bm{h}}_{i}
represents a point on the hypersphere in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

However, if the distribution of
\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}}
now equaled the distribution of
\bm{u}

(cf.
(
13
)
), we would have
\|\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}}\|=1

which means we would have found a matrix with
RIP
constant
\delta
equal to zero,
see
(
11
)
.
This can be expected to be difficult if not impossible.
In particular, if the number of rows,
m
, is significantly smaller than the number of columns,
n
,
then
\delta
can be expected to be relatively large.
On the other hand, if
m
is larger,
the matrix
\bm{A}
is closer to a square matrix and it is easier to find an (approximate) isometry.
With this intuition in mind, the optimization problem may be difficult to solve
if the data is normalized such that every
\tilde{\bm{h}}_{i}
lies exactly on the hypersphere in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
i.e., if we have
\|\tilde{\bm{h}}_{i}\|=1
.
To alleviate the problem, we heuristically introduce some variation in the norm of the training data.
Specifically, we normalize the training data as described in equation
(
33
)
,
where the denominator approximates
\sqrt{\expec[\|\bm{h}\|^{2}]}
.
Consequently, the training data points are only “on average” on the hypersphere in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.
Now, the distribution
\bm{A}\tilde{\bm{h}}
can be equal to the distribution of
\bm{u}
,
but we would not have
\delta=0
.

The effect of the different normalizations is studied in
Figure

5

where we consider two extreme cases: few measurements
m=16
but only a single path
p=1

and many measurements
m=96
with many paths
p=10
.
It can be seen that the normalization
(
33
)
leads to better results than
(
34
)
.
The difference is particularly pronounced in the case
m=16
.
This fits the intuition provided above: as
m
decreases,
\delta
potentially needs to increase.
Hence, training with
(
34
)
is more restrictive in that case.
Motivated by
Figure

5
,
normalization
(
33
)
was used in all other numerical experiments presented in this section.

VI
Conclusion

The anchor point of our studies was the interpretation that a measurement matrix
which has the
RIP
constitutes (approximately) a mapping between hyperspheres.
In order to take measurement noise into account,
we proposed to determine the measurement matrix such
that it maps points uniformly onto the hypersphere in its range.
One of the
MMD
metrics was used to measure the distance between a uniform distribution on the hypersphere
and the distribution in the range of the measurement matrix.
The recent success of
MMD
-based machine learning applications motivated a solution of the optimization problem via stochastic gradient descent.
Further, it was shown that a modified method from the literature also leads to good performance
and that it can harmonize with our approach by providing a suitable initialization for the learning algorithm.

The algorithm’s learning phase requires training data.
This training data may, for example, be obtained by means of a measurement campaign.
The learned matrix will then depend on the measured scenario.
In our experiments, this is reflected by the matrix’s dependency on the number of propagation paths.
In this context, it might be interesting to investigate how the performance behaves when the scenario changes.
One might then attempt to employ a form of domain adaptation
[
43
]
to adaptively update the measurement matrix.

A notable property of the proposed learning algorithm is that it yields one measurement matrix
which can be employed for all
SNRs
.
Numerical experiments demonstrate this property and the great performance of the proposed method.

In this paper, we focused on measurement matrices with a constant modulus constraint.
We translated the constraint into the system of equations shown in
(
21
)
.
For an arbitrary (not necessarily constant modulus) matrix
\bm{C}\in\mathbb{C}^{m\times n}
,
(
21
)
takes the form

\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\bm{C}\tilde{\bm{h}})\\
\Im(\bm{C}\tilde{\bm{h}})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\bm{C})&-\Im(\bm{C})\\
\Im(\bm{C})&\Re(\bm{C})\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Re(\tilde{\bm{h}})\\
\Im(\tilde{\bm{h}})\end{bmatrix}.

(35)

It can be seen that more general structural constraints can be incorporated into the design of
\bm{C}
as well.
For example, the authors of
[
45
]
prove that random Toeplitz matrices can satisfy a
RIP
condition
and use this result to motivate the application of such matrices in a channel estimation context.
A Toeplitz measurement matrix can also be learned with the approach described in
Section

III-B
.
To this end, weight tying can be employed
[
43
]
.
That is, parameters of
\bm{C}
would be shared between rows to enforce a Toeplitz structure.
Here it makes sense to normalize the rows during training.
Similarly, other structured matrices can be learned too.
