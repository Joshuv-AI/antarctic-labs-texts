Rates of convergence for density estimation with generative adversarial networks
Nikita Puchkin npuchkin@hse.ru, Sergey Samsonov svsamsonov@hse.ru, Denis Belomestny denis.belomestny@uni-due.de, Eric Moulines eric.moulines@polytechnique.edu, Alexey Naumov anaumov@hse.ru

Abstract
In this work we undertake a thorough study of the non-asymptotic properties of the vanilla generative adversarial networks (GANs). We prove an oracle inequality for the Jensen-Shannon (JS) divergence between the underlying density 𝗉 ∗ \mathsf{p}^{*} and the GAN estimate with a significantly better statistical error term compared to the previously known results. The advantage of our bound becomes clear in application to nonparametric density estimation. We show that the JS-divergence between the GAN estimate and 𝗉 ∗ \mathsf{p}^{*} decays as fast as ( log ⁡ n / n ) 2 ​ β / ( 2 ​ β + d ) (\log{n}/n)^{2\beta/(2\beta+d)} , where n n is the sample size and β \beta determines the smoothness of 𝗉 ∗ \mathsf{p}^{*} . This rate of convergence coincides (up to logarithmic factors) with minimax optimal for the considered class of densities.

Introduction

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. random elements with values in
\mathsf{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
drawn from a distribution
P^{*}
. We assume that
P^{*}
admits a density
\mathsf{p}^{*}
with respect to a dominating measure
\mu
. The measure
\mu

is not necessarily absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, it can be the counting measure or the Hausdorff measure on a low-dimensional manifold as well. Our goal is to estimate
\mathsf{p}^{*}
based on a finite sample. The problem of density estimation was extensively studied in the literature and encounters numerous approaches such as kernel (see, e.g.,
(
Tsybakov 2008
, Section 1.2)
and
(
McDonald 2017
)
) and k-nearest neighbors density estimators
(
Dasgupta and Kpotufe 2014
)
, wavelet thresholding
(
Donoho et al. 1996
)
, and aggregation
(
Rakhlin et al. 2005
;
Rigollet and Tsybakov 2006
;
Bunea et al. 2007
;
Butucea et al. 2017
;
Dalalyan and Sebbar 2018
)
. Recently,
Goodfellow et al. 2014
have introduced a novel approach for a related problem of generative modeling called generative adversarial networks (or simply GANs). A generative adversarial network consists of a generator and a discriminator. Given a known easy-to-sample distribution on a latent space
\mathsf{Y}
with a density

\phi
, the generator
g:\mathsf{Y}\rightarrow\mathsf{X}
takes i.i.d. samples
Y_{1},\dots,Y_{n}
from
\phi
and produces fake ones
g(Y_{1}),\dots,g(Y_{n})
. The goal of the discriminator
D
is to distinguish between the real samples
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
and
g(Y_{1}),\dots,g(Y_{n})
. Usually,
D
can be thought of as a map
\mathsf{X}\mapsto(0,1)
, where larger values correspond to higher confidence that an input variable is drawn from
\mathsf{p}^{*}
. In practice, both generator and discriminator usually belong to some parametric families (for example, to classes of neural networks). Let us fix positive integers
d_{\mathcal{G}}
and
d_{\mathcal{D}}
and some compact sets
\mathsf{W}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{G}}}
and
\mathsf{\Theta}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathcal{D}}}
. In our paper we assume that

g\in\mathcal{G}=\{g_{w}:w\in\mathsf{W}\}\quad\text{and}\quad D\in\mathcal{D}=\{D_{\theta}:\theta\in\mathsf{\Theta}\}.

As a byproduct of the described generative approach, GANs also provide an implicit density estimate for
\mathsf{p}^{*}
. Indeed, if a statistician manages to find a good generator
g_{w}
, then the density of
g_{w}(Y)
is a reasonable estimate of
\mathsf{p}^{*}
. In
(
Goodfellow et al. 2014
)
, the authors suggested to solve the following minimax problem, also called
vanilla GAN
:

\min_{w\in\mathsf{W}}\>\max_{\theta\in\mathsf{\Theta}}\>\biggl\{L(w,\theta):=\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{E}_{X\sim\mathsf{p}^{*}}\log D_{\theta}(X)+\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{E}_{Y\sim\phi}\log\bigl(1-D_{\theta}(g_{w}(Y))\bigr)\biggr\}.

(1.1)

Here and further in the paper,
\log
stands for the natural logarithm.
The intuition behind (
1.1
) is that if
\mathcal{D}
contained all measurable functions on
\mathsf{X}
with values in
(0,1)
, the minimax problem (
1.1
) would reduce to (see
(
Goodfellow et al. 2014
, Theorem 1)
)

\min_{w\in\mathcal{\mathsf{W}}}\big[\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\log 2\big],

(1.2)

where
\JS
is the Jensen-Shannon divergence (see (
2.1
) for the definition), and
\mathsf{p}_{w}
is the density of
g_{w}(Y)
with
Y\sim\phi
. Unfortunately, since
\mathcal{D}
is a parametric class (and, hence, it cannot contain all the measurable functions), the actual value of
\max_{\theta\in\mathsf{\Theta}}L(w,\theta)
differs from
(\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\log 2)
. Fortunately, the gap between
\max_{\theta\in\mathsf{\Theta}}L(w,\theta)
and
(\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\log 2)
may be rather small for a proper class of discriminators. Since the true distribution
\mathsf{p}^{*}
in (
1.1
) is unknown, we consider a plug-in estimate
\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}}
, where
\widehat{w}
is a solution of the optimization problem

\widehat{w}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}\limits_{w\in\mathsf{W}}\max\limits_{\theta\in\mathsf{\Theta}}L_{n}(w,\theta),

(1.3)

and

L_{n}(w,\theta)=\frac{1}{2n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\log D_{\theta}(X_{i})+\frac{1}{2n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{n}\log\Big(1-D_{\theta}\big(g_{w}(Y_{j})\big)\Big)

(1.4)

is the empirical version of the functional
L(w,\theta)
defined in (
1.1
).

GANs provide a flexible tool for sampling from an unknown distribution, and they have recently become extremely popular among practitioners. Using deep neural network classes
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{D}
, one can reach the state-of-the-art generative performance in many challenging tasks, including image super-resolution
(
Ledig et al. 2017
)
, video synthesis
(
Kim et al. 2018
)
, and many others. Various GAN formulations were later proposed by varying the divergence measure in (
1.2
). For instance, f-GAN
(
Nowozin et al. 2016
)
generalized vanilla GAN by minimizing a general f-divergence; Wasserstein GAN (WGAN)
(
Arjovsky et al. 2017
)
considered the first-order Wasserstein (Kantorovich) distance (
W_{1}
distance); MMD-GAN
(
Dziugaite et al. 2015
)
was based on the maximum mean discrepancy; energy-based GAN
(
Zhao et al. 2016
)
minimizes the total variation distance as discussed in

(
Arjovsky et al. 2017
)
; Quadratic GAN
(
Feizi et al. 2020
)
aimed to find the distribution minimizing the second-order Wasserstein (Kantorovich) distance.

The empirical success of GANs motivated many researchers to analyze their theoretical properties.
For example, in
(
Biau et al. 2021
;
Schreuder et al. 2021
)
, the authors carried out theoretical analysis of WGANs.
In
(
Biau et al. 2021
)
, the authors obtained upper bounds for the excess risk of GANs for parametric classes (including the class of neural networks).
In contrast to
Biau et al. 2021
,
Schreuder et al. 2021
considered generative models based on
\beta
times differentiable transformations of the
d
-dimensional unit hypercube and derived rates of convergence of order
O\left(n^{-\beta/d}\vee n^{-1/2}\right)
for the corresponding
W_{1}
distance.

Liang 2021
used results from the empirical process theory to prove upper bounds for Sobolev GANs (i.e., when generators and discriminators belong to Sobolev classes
\mathcal{W}^{\alpha}
and
\mathcal{W}^{\beta}
, respectively), MMD GANs, WGANs, and vanilla GANs.
In the case of Sobolev GANs, the obtained rate
n^{-(\alpha+\beta)/(2\beta+d)}\vee n^{-1/2}
for the corresponding integral probability metric (IPM) is shown to be minimax optimal.
The research of
Liang 2021
was continued in the works of
Singh and Póczos 2018
;
Uppal et al. 2019
;
Luise et al. 2020
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Vardanyan et al. 2023
where the authors studied the performance of GANs in terms of different IPM losses and the Sinkhorn divergence.
The vanilla GANs were studied in
(
Liang 2021
;
Biau et al. 2020
;
Asatryan et al. 2023
)
.
However, the rates of convergence for them in terms of Jensen-Shannon divergence are not yet fully understood.

Biau et al. 2020
and
Asatryan et al. 2023
improved approximation terms as compared to
Liang 2021
, but it is not clear whether the rates obtained in
(
Biau et al. 2020
;
Asatryan et al. 2023
)
are minimax optimal.
In this work, we provide a refined analysis of the theoretical properties of vanilla GANs and derive minimax optimal rates.

Contributions.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows.

•

We prove (
1
) a sharp oracle inequality for the case when the classes
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{D}
are general parametric classes, which significantly improves the existing inequalities from the works of
Biau et al. 2020
and
Asatryan et al. 2023
.

•

We apply the result of
1
to a nonparametric density estimation problem.
Choosing
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{D}
as classes of neural networks of appropriate architectures with ReQU activation functions, we derive the rates of convergence for the estimate
\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}}
to
the true density
\mathsf{p}^{*}
in terms of the Jensen-Shannon divergence.
Namely, we show that, with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that

\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})\lesssim\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+d}}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n},

(
2
)

provided that the actual density
\mathsf{p}^{*}
is the density of a random variable

g^{*}(Y)
with
Y
being uniformly distributed on
[0,1]^{d}
, for a smooth invertible transform
g^{*}
not necessary belonging to

\mathcal{G}
. We also discuss that previously known bounds do not yield this rate of convergence.

•

We show that the result of
2
is minimax optimal up to a logarithmic factor. Namely, we prove that for any estimate
\widehat{\mathsf{p}}
, it holds that

\sup\limits_{\mathsf{p}^{*}}\mathsf{E}\JS(\widehat{\mathsf{p}},\mathsf{p}^{*})\gtrsim n^{-\frac{2\beta}{2\beta+d}},

(
3
)

where the supremum is taken with respect to the densities
\mathsf{p}^{*}
satisfying the
same regularity assumptions as in
2
. Hence, our results imply the minimax optimality of GANs (up to logarithmic factors) in the context of nonparametric density estimation.

Preliminaries and notations

Kullback-Leibler and Jensen-Shannon divergences.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a bounded domain. For two probability measures on a
measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{B}(\Omega))
with Lebesgue densities
\mathsf{p}
and
\mathsf{q}
, respectively, we define the Kullback-Leibler divergence between them as

\KL(\mathsf{p},\mathsf{q})=\begin{cases}\int\mathsf{p}(x)\log\big(\mathsf{p}(x)/\mathsf{q}(x)\big)\mathrm{d}\mu,\quad\text{if $\mathsf{p}\ll\mathsf{q}$},\\
+\infty,\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Here and further in this paper,
\log
stands for the natural logarithm. By
\JS(\mathsf{p},\mathsf{q})
, we denote the Jensen-Shannon divergence

\JS(\mathsf{p},\mathsf{q})=\frac{1}{2}\KL\left(\mathsf{p},\frac{\mathsf{p}+\mathsf{q}}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\KL\left(\mathsf{q},\frac{\mathsf{p}+\mathsf{q}}{2}\right).

(2.1)

Norms.

For a matrix
A
and a vector
v
, we denote by
\|A\|_{\infty}
and
\|v\|_{\infty}
the maximal
absolute value of entries of
A
and
v
, respectively.

\|A\|_{0}
and
\|v\|_{0}
shall stand for the number of non-zero entries of
A
and
v
,
respectively.
Finally, the Frobenius norm and operator norm of
A
are denoted by
\|A\|_{F}
and

\|A\|
, respectively, and the Euclidean norm of
v
is denoted by
\|v\|
. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
r>0
we write
\mathcal{B}(x,r)=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,\|y-x\|\leqslant r\}
. For a function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we set

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}=\sup_{x\in\Omega}\|f(x)\|,

\displaystyle\|f\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}=\left\{\int_{\Omega}\|f(x)\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right\}^{1/2},

and

\|f\|_{L_{2}(\mathsf{p},\Omega)}=\left(\int\limits_{\Omega}\|f(x)\|^{2}\,\mathsf{p}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu\right)^{1/2}.

Sometimes, we omit the domain
\Omega
in the notations
L_{\infty}(\Omega)
,
L_{2}(\Omega)
,
L_{2}(\mathsf{p},\Omega)
and simply write
L_{\infty}
,
L_{2}
, and
L_{2}(\mathsf{p})
, respectively, if there is no ambiguity.

Smoothness classes.

For any
s\in\mathbb{N}
, the function space
C^{s}(\Omega)
consists of those
functions
over the domain
\Omega
which have partial derivatives up to order
s
in
\Omega
,
and these derivatives are bounded and continuous in
\Omega
. Formally,

\textstyle{C^{s}(\Omega)=\big\{f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}:\quad\|f\|_{C^{s}}:=\max\limits_{|{\bm{\gamma}}|\leqslant s}\|D^{{\bm{\gamma}}}f\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}<\infty\big\},}

where, for any multi-index
{\bm{\gamma}}=(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
, the partial differential operator
D^{{\bm{\gamma}}}
is defined as

D^{{\bm{\gamma}}}f_{i}=\frac{\partial^{|{\bm{\gamma}}|}f_{i}}{\partial x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots\partial x_{d}^{\gamma_{d}}},\quad i\in\{1,\dots,m\}\,,\text{ and }\|D^{{\bm{\gamma}}}f\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}=\max\limits_{1\leqslant i\leqslant m}\|D^{{\bm{\gamma}}}f_{i}\|_{L_{\infty}(\Omega)}\,.

Here we have written
|{\bm{\gamma}}|=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\gamma_{i}
for the order of

D^{{\bm{\gamma}}}
.
To avoid confusion between multi-indices and scalars, we reserve the bold font for the former ones.
For the matrix of first derivatives, we use the usual notation
\nabla f=(\partial f_{i}/\partial x_{j})

i=1,\ldots,m
,
j=1,\ldots,d
. For a function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
,
\varphi\in C^{2}(\Omega)
, we write
\nabla^{2}\varphi(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
for its Hessian at point
x
. For a function
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and any positive number
0<\delta\leqslant 1
, the

Hölder constant
of order
\delta
is given by

[f]_{\delta}:=\max_{i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}}\sup_{x\not=y\in\Omega}\frac{|f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(y)|}{\min\{1,\|x-y\|\}^{\delta}}\;.

(2.2)

Now, for any
\alpha>0
, we set
s=\lfloor\alpha\rfloor
and define the
Hölder ball

\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\Omega,H)
as

\textstyle{\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\Omega,H)=\big\{f\in C^{s}(\Omega):\quad\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}}:=\max\{\|f\|_{C^{s}},\ \max\limits_{|{\bm{\gamma}}|=s}[D^{{\bm{\gamma}}}f]_{\delta}\}\leqslant H\big\}.}

Note that if
f\in\mathcal{H}^{1+\beta}(\Omega,H)
for some
\beta>0,
then, for any
i\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, it holds that

\left|\frac{\partial f_{i}(x)}{\partial x_{j}}-\frac{\partial f_{i}(y)}{\partial x_{j}}\right|\leqslant\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1+\beta}}\cdot\|x-y\|^{1\wedge\beta}\leqslant H\cdot\|x-y\|^{1\wedge\beta}\quad\text{for all $x,y\in\Omega$,}

since
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\beta_{1}}}\leqslant\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\beta_{2}}}
for any

\beta_{2}\geqslant\beta_{1}.
We will also write
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\Omega)
if
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\Omega,H)
for some
H<\infty.

We also introduce a class of
\Lambda
-regular functions
\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}_{\Lambda}(\Omega,H)
,
\Lambda>1
:

\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}_{\Lambda}(\Omega,H)=\left\{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\Omega,H):\Lambda^{-2}\mathrm{I}_{d\times d}\preceq\nabla f(x)^{\top}\nabla f(x)\preceq\Lambda^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d\times d}\text{ for all $x\in\Omega$}\right\}\,,

(2.3)

where for symmetric matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
we write
A\preceq B
if
u^{\top}(B-A)u\geqslant 0
for any
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Neural networks.

To give a formal definition of a neural network, we first fix an activation function

\sigma:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. For a vector
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
we define the shifted activation function
\sigma_{v}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
as

\sigma_{v}(x)=\bigl(\sigma(x_{1}-v_{1}),\dots,\sigma(x_{p}-v_{p})\bigr),\quad x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}.

Given a positive integer
N
and a vector
\mathcal{A}=(p_{0},p_{1},\dots,p_{N+1})\in\mathbb{N}^{N+2}
, a neural network of depth
N+1
(with
N
hidden layers) and architecture
\mathcal{A}
is a function of the form

f:\mathbb{R}^{p_{0}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p_{N+1}}\,,\quad f(x)=W_{N}\circ\sigma_{v_{N}}\circ W_{N-1}\circ\sigma_{v_{N-1}}\circ\dots\circ W_{1}\circ\sigma_{v_{1}}\circ W_{0}\circ x,

(2.4)

where
W_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{i+1}\times p_{i}}
are weight matrices and
v_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{i}}
are shift vectors.
The numbers
p,p_{0},\dots,p_{N+1}
should not be confused with the density
\mathsf{p}(x)
nor with
\mathsf{p}^{*}(x)
, which are always displayed in serif.
Next, we introduce a special subclass class of neural networks of depth
N+1
with architecture
\mathcal{A}
:

\mathsf{NN}(N,\mathcal{A})=\left\{\text{$f$ of the form \eqref{eq:nn}}:\|W_{0}\|_{\infty}\vee\max\limits_{1\leqslant\ell\leqslant N}\left\{\|W_{\ell}\|_{\infty}\vee\|v_{\ell}\|_{\infty}\right\}\leqslant 1\right\}.

The maximum number of neurons in one layer
\|\mathcal{A}\|_{\infty}
is called the width of the neural network. Similarly to
(
Schmidt-Hieber 2020
)
, we consider sparse neural networks assuming that only a few weights are not equal to zero. For this purpose, we introduce a class of neural networks of depth
N+1
with architecture
\mathcal{A}
and at most
s
non-zero weights:

\mathsf{NN}(N,\mathcal{A},s)=\left\{f\in\mathsf{NN}(N,\mathcal{A}):\|W_{0}\|_{0}+\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{N}\left(\|W_{\ell}\|_{0}+\|v_{\ell}\|_{0}\right)\leqslant s\right\}.

3
Theoretical properties of vanilla GANs: a general oracle inequality

We begin with a sharp oracle inequality for general parametric classes of generators
\mathcal{G}
and discriminators
\mathcal{D}
. Following
(
Biau et al. 2020
)
, we impose the next regularity assumptions on generators and discriminators.

Assumption AG
.

For all
g\in\mathcal{G}
, the image of the latent space
\mathsf{Y}
is a subset of
\mathsf{X}
, that is,
g(\mathsf{Y})\subseteq\mathsf{X}
. Moreover, for all
y\in\mathsf{Y}
, the map
w\mapsto g_{w}(y)
is Lipschitz on the parameter space
\mathsf{W}
with a constant
\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}
. That is, for any
y\in\mathsf{Y}
and any
u,v\in\mathsf{W}
, it holds that

\|g_{u}(y)-g_{v}(y)\|\leqslant\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\|u-v\|_{\infty}.

Assumption AD
.

The maps
x\mapsto D_{\theta}(x)
and
\theta\mapsto D_{\theta}(x)
are Lipschitz on the ambient space
\mathsf{X}
and on the parameter set
\mathsf{\Theta}
with constants
\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}
and
\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}
, respectively. More precisely, for any
x,x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathsf{X}
and any
\theta,\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathsf{\Theta}
, the following inequalities hold:

\left|D_{\theta}(x_{1})-D_{\theta}(x_{2})\right|\leqslant\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|\quad\text{and}\quad\left|D_{\theta_{1}}(x)-D_{\theta_{2}}(x)\right|\leqslant\mathsf{L}_{\Theta}\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|_{\infty}.

Moreover, there exist constants
0<D_{\min}\leqslant D_{\max}<1
such that

D_{\theta}(x)\in[D_{\min},D_{\max}]\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathsf{X}$ and $\theta\in\mathsf{\Theta}$}.

We would like to note that the requirement that all functions from
\mathcal{D}
are bounded away from
0
and
1
is
needed for the
\log D_{\theta}
and
\log(1-D_{\theta})
to be well defined. Similar conditions appear in the literature for aggregation with the Kullback-Leibler loss (for instance, in
(
Polzehl and Spokoiny 2006
;
Belomestny and Spokoiny 2007
;
Rigollet 2012
;
Butucea et al. 2017
)
). Finally, similarly to
(
Biau et al. 2020
)
, we require the densities of fake random elements
\mathsf{p}_{w}(x)
,
w\in\mathsf{W}
to fulfil the following property.

Assumption A
\mathsf{p}
.

For all
x\in\mathsf{X}
, the map
w\mapsto\mathsf{p}_{w}(x)
is Lipschitz on
\mathsf{W}
with a constant
\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}
. That is, for any
x\in\mathsf{X}
and any
u,v\in\mathsf{W}
, we have

|\mathsf{p}_{u}(x)-\mathsf{p}_{v}(x)|\leqslant\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\|u-v\|_{\infty}.

Under Assumptions
AG
,
AD
, and
A

p

, we establish the following oracle inequality for the GAN estimate (
1.3
).

Theorem 1
.

Assume
AG
,
AD
, and
A

p

. Let
\mathsf{W}\subseteq[-1,1]^{d_{\mathcal{G}}}
and
\mathsf{\Theta}\subseteq[-1,1]^{d_{\mathcal{D}}}
. Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that

\displaystyle\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}-\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{(\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}+\Delta_{\mathcal{D}})\big[(d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}})\log(2(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1)n)+\log(8/\delta)\big]}{n}}

\displaystyle\quad+C_{\ref{eq:c_d_min_d_max}}\cdot\frac{(d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}})\log(2(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1)n)+\log(8/\delta)}{n}

(3.1)

where

\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}=\min\limits_{w\in\mathsf{W}}\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*}),\quad\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}=\max\limits_{w\in\mathsf{W}}\min\limits_{\theta\in\mathsf{\Theta}}[\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\log 2-L(w,\theta)],

and

C_{\ref{eq:c_d_min_d_max}}=\left(\frac{\log(1/D_{\min})}{D_{\min}^{2}}+\frac{\log\big(1/(1-D_{\max}\big)}{(1-D_{\max})^{2}}\right).

(3.2)

Here
\lesssim
stands for inequality up to an absolute multiplicative constant.

Remark 1
.

Let us recall that we assumed the number of fake samples
m
equal to the sample size
n
. In general, if
m\neq n
,
n
should be replaced by
(m\land n)
in (
1
). This does not affect the bound much, because usually
m\geqslant n
.

Theoretical properties of vanilla GANs were studied in the works of
Liang 2021
;
Biau et al. 2020
;
Asatryan et al. 2023
.
The results of
Asatryan et al. 2023
mainly concern the case of highly smooth generators, so we postpone a comparison with their rates of convergence, and we will return to it after
2
.
To our knowledge, the first upper bound on the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the true density
\mathsf{p}^{*}
and the vanilla GAN estimate
\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}}
was obtained in
(
Liang 2021
, Theorem 13)
.
In
(
Biau et al. 2020
)
, the authors significantly improved the approximation terms in the oracle inequality of
(
Liang 2021
, Theorem 13)
.
The closest result to our Theorem

in the literature is
(
Biau et al. 2020
, Theorem 4.1)
, so let us focus on the comparison of these two results.
First, in
(
Biau et al. 2020
)
the authors assumed that the true density
\mathsf{p}^{*}
is bounded away from
0
and
+\infty
on its support and that
\mathsf{p}_{w}
is uniformly bounded over all
w\in\mathsf{W}
, while we avoid such requirements in our analysis. Second, the oracle inequality for the JS risk of the vanilla GAN estimate, established in
(
Biau et al. 2020
)
under similar assumptions is weaker than the bound from
1
. The authors of the work
(
Biau et al. 2020
)
proved that

\mathsf{E}\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}-\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}}}{n}}.

(3.3)

One can also use McDiarmid’s inequality (see, e.g.
(
Boucheron et al. 2004
, Corollary

) to transform the in-expectation guarantee (
3.3
) into a large deviation bound on

of the form

\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}-\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}}}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}},

(3.4)

which holds with probability at least
1-\delta
.
If the classes
\mathcal{G},

\mathcal{D}
cannot approximate
g^{*}
and the respective optimal discriminator with high accuracy, then
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
are of order
1
. In this case, our rates show no improvements over
(
Biau et al. 2020
)
.
However, in practice one uses rather expressive classes of deep neural networks for
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{D}
, and the bound (
1
) can be significantly better than (
3.4
). In Section
4
, we give an example parametric families
\mathcal{G},\mathcal{D}
such that
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
tend to

polynomially fast as
n
goes to
\infty
. Finally, in contrast to
(
Biau et al. 2020
)
, we specify the dependence of the rate on the constants
\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}
, which may be large, especially in the case of wide and deep networks.

To get further insights of the result of
1
, let us elaborate on the properties of
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
.
The next lemma shows that
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
exhibits quadratic behaviour and its upper bound is closely related to approximation properties of the class of discriminators considered.

Lemma 1
.

Under Assumption (
AD
), for any
w\in\mathsf{W}
and any
\theta\in\mathsf{\Theta}
, it holds that

\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\log 2-L(w,\theta)\geqslant\left\|\frac{\mathsf{p}^{*}}{\mathsf{p}^{*}+\mathsf{p}_{w}}-D_{\theta}\right\|_{L_{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*}+\mathsf{p}_{w})}^{2}

and

\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\log 2-L(w,\theta)\leqslant\frac{C_{\ref{eq:c_delta}}^{2}}{(C_{\ref{eq:c_delta}}-1)^{2}D_{\min}(1-D_{\max})}\left\|\frac{\mathsf{p}^{*}}{\mathsf{p}^{*}+\mathsf{p}_{w}}-D_{\theta}\right\|_{L_{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*}+\mathsf{p}_{w})}^{2},

(3.5)

where

C_{\ref{eq:c_delta}}=1+\sqrt{\frac{D_{\min}}{(1-D_{\min})\log(1/(1-D_{\max})}\wedge\frac{1-D_{\max}}{D_{\max}\log(1/D_{\min})}}.

(3.6)

The proof of
1
is deferred to
Section

C.1
.

1
plays a key role in derivation of faster rates of convergence. It shows that, for each
w\in\mathsf{W}
,
L(w,\theta)
enjoys a similar curvature as the squared loss. This fact remained unnoticed in the literature. Though it is well known that
\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})
has a quadratic behaviour with respect to
\mathsf{p}_{w}
(see, for instance, our
7
below), this fact alone is not enough to derive the uniform Bernstein-type inequality (
1
). Only the combination of
1
and
7
leads us to a new, significantly better result.
In particular, if for any
w\in\mathsf{W}
there exists
\theta(w)\in\mathsf{\Theta}
such that
\|D_{\theta(w)}-\mathsf{p}^{*}/(\mathsf{p}^{*}+\mathsf{p}_{w})\|_{L_{2}(\mathsf{p}^{*}+\mathsf{p}_{w})}\leqslant\varepsilon
, then
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}
. Hence, in this case,

and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality immediately yield that

\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}+\frac{d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}}+\log(1/\delta)}{n}

with probability at least
1-\delta
.
In
(
Biau et al. 2020
)
, the authors could not exploit the quadratic behaviour of
(\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\log 2-L(w,\theta))
properly, and they only proved that

\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}+\sqrt{\frac{d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}}}{n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}}

under more restrictive assumptions.
Finally, we would like to note that, in contrary to the remark in
(
Singh et al. 2018
, Section 10.1)
, the oracle inequality in Theorem 1 does not require the density
\mathsf{p}^{*}
to be bounded away from zero.

4
Example: deep nonparametric density estimation

The goal of this section is to show that GAN estimates achieve minimax rates of convergence in the problem of nonparametric density estimation. From now on, we assume that
\mathsf{X}=\mathsf{Y}=[0,1]^{d}
,
\mu
is the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the generators
g_{w}\in\mathcal{G}
are non-degenerate maps, so the density of the fake samples with respect to
\mu
is defined correctly. In this setup, if a latent random element
Y
is drawn according to the density
\phi
supported on
[0,1]^{d}
, then the corresponding density of
g_{w}(Y)
is given by

\mathsf{p}_{w}(x)=|\det[\nabla g_{w}(g_{w}^{-1}(x))]|^{-1}\phi(g_{w}^{-1}(x)),\quad x\in[0,1]^{d}.

(4.1)

For the ease of exposition, we assume that
Y
is distributed uniformly on
[0,1]^{d}
, so that (
4.1
) simplifies to

\mathsf{p}_{w}(x)=|\det[\nabla g_{w}(g_{w}^{-1}(x))]|^{-1},\quad x\in[0,1]^{d}.

(4.2)

We also impose a structural assumption on the underlying density
\mathsf{p}^{*}
, assuming that it is the density of a random element
g^{*}(Y)
where
g^{*}:[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow[0,1]^{d}
is a smooth regular map and, as before,
Y
has a uniform distribution on
[0,1]^{d}
.

Assumption A
\mathsf{p}^{*}
.

There exist constants
\beta>2
,
H^{*}>0
, and
\Lambda>1
such that
\mathsf{p}^{*}
is
of the form

\mathsf{p}^{*}(x)=|\det[\nabla g^{*}((g^{*})^{-1}(x))]|^{-1},\quad x\in[0,1]^{d},

with
g^{*}\in\mathcal{H}^{1+\beta}_{\Lambda}([0,1]^{d},H^{*})
.

In fact, Assumption
A

p
∗

is not very restrictive and allows for a quite large class of densities.
The celebrated Brenier’s theorem
(
Brenier 1991
)
implies that, for any density
\mathsf{p}
with a finite second moment, there exists a convex almost everywhere differentiable function
\varphi
such that
\nabla\varphi(Y)\sim\mathsf{p}
where
Y\sim\mathcal{U}([0,1]^{d})
.
The Caffarelli’s regularity theory
(
Caffarelli 1991
;
Caffarelli 1992a
;
Caffarelli 1992b
;
Caffarelli 1996
)
extends the Brenier’s result in the following way.
If
\mathsf{p}
is bounded away from zero and infinity,
\Omega=\text{supp}(\mathsf{p})
is convex, and
\mathsf{p}
is in
C^{\beta}(\text{Int}(\Omega))
, then the potential
\varphi
is in
C^{\beta+2}
.

Remark 2
.

8
yields that
\mathsf{p}^{*}
is bounded away from zero and infinity in the considered model. This is a so-called strong density assumption (see, e.g.,
(
Audibert and Tsybakov 2007
, Definition 2.2)
), widely used in statistics.

When applying GANs to the problem of nonparametric density estimation, we shall take
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{D}
to be classes of neural networks with ReQU (rectified quadratic unit) activation functions:

\sigma^{\mathsf{ReQU}}(x)=(x\vee 0)^{2}.

While rectified linear unit (ReLU), defined as

\sigma^{\mathsf{ReLU}}(x)=x\vee 0,

is the most common choice for the activation functions in neural networks, it is not suitable for our purposes.
The reason is that we want to use neural networks as generators.
The density of the fake random elements
g_{w}(Y_{1}),\dots,g_{w}(Y_{n})
is given by (
4.2
)
and, to enforce differentiability, we use the ReQU activation function.
Besides, a recent result of
(
Belomestny et al. 2023
)
on approximation properties of neural networks with ReQU activations can be used to bound
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
from
1
. Since the activation function is fixed, we will write
\sigma(x)
, instead of
\sigma^{\mathsf{ReQU}}(x)
. In this section, we impose the following assumptions on the classes of generators and discriminators.

Assumption AG’
.

Fix
N_{\mathcal{G}},d_{\mathcal{G}}\in\mathbb{N}
, and an architecture
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}}\in\mathbb{N}^{N_{\mathcal{G}}+2}

with the first and the last component equal to
d
. There are constants
H_{\mathcal{G}}>0
and
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}}>1
such that

\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}(\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}},H_{\mathcal{G}},N_{\mathcal{G}},\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}},d_{\mathcal{G}})=\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}}}^{2}([0,1]^{d},H_{\mathcal{G}})\cap\mathsf{NN}(N_{\mathcal{G}},\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}},d_{\mathcal{G}}).

Besides,
g_{w}([0,1]^{d})\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
for all

.

Assumption AD’
.

Fix
N_{\mathcal{D}},d_{\mathcal{D}}\in\mathbb{N}
, and an architecture
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{D}}\in\mathbb{N}^{N_{\mathcal{D}}+2}

with the first and the last components equal to
d
and
1
, respectively.
There are constants

and

\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}(D_{\min},D_{\max},H_{\mathcal{D}},N_{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{D}},d_{\mathcal{D}})=\mathcal{H}^{1}([0,1]^{d},H_{\mathcal{D}})\cap\mathsf{NN}(N_{\mathcal{D}},\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{D}},d_{\mathcal{D}}),

and each

D(x)\in[D_{\min},D_{\max}]\subset[0,1]\quad\text{for all $x\in[0,1]^{d}$}.

According to Assumption
AG’
and the definition of the class

Before applying

Towards the rates of convergence: verifying the conditions of

Lemma 2

Let

(4.3)

The proof of

B.1
.

Assume

4.2
Rates of convergence in nonparametric density estimation with GANs

The discussion in
Section

4.1
implies that

1
can be applied to the setting described in the beginning of
Section

4
. In this section, we go further and provide upper bounds on
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
under assumptions
AD’
,
AG’
, and
A

p
∗

. The key ingredient of our analysis is the recent result of
Belomestny et al. 2023
quantifying the expressiveness of neural networks with ReQU activations. It ensures that for any
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta+1}([0,1]^{d},H^{*})
,
\beta>1
, and any
\varepsilon>0,
there is a neural network with ReQU activation function from the class
\mathcal{H}^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}([0,1]^{d},H^{*}+\varepsilon)
which approximates
f
within the accuracy
\varepsilon
with respect to the norm in
\mathcal{H}^{\lfloor\beta\rfloor}([0,1]^{d})
. We would like to emphasize that, unlike many other results on approximation properties of neural networks,
Belomestny et al. 2023
considers simultaneous approximation of a smooth function and its derivatives, which is crucial for our purposes. This result allows us to derive the optimal rates of convergence for GAN estimates in the nonparametric density estimation problem.
However, we would like to emphasize that the proof of Theorem 2 works for any choice of activation function, which yields simultaneous approximation of a function and its derivatives by a neural network with weights taking their values in
[-1,1]
.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that
A

p
∗

holds with
\beta>2
.
Choose
H_{\mathcal{G}}\geqslant 2H^{*}
,
\Lambda_{\mathcal{G}}\geqslant 2\Lambda
,
D_{\min}\leqslant\Lambda^{-d}/(\Lambda^{d}+\Lambda^{-d})]
,
D_{\max}\geqslant\Lambda^{d}/(\Lambda^{d}+\Lambda^{-d})]
, and
H_{\mathcal{D}}
large enough.
Then there are positive integers
N_{\mathcal{G}}
,
d_{\mathcal{G}}
,
N_{\mathcal{D}}
,
d_{\mathcal{D}}
and the architectures
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{G}}\in\mathbb{N}^{N_{\mathcal{G}}+2},

\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{D}}\in\mathbb{N}^{N_{\mathcal{D}}+2}
such that
AG’
and
AD’
define nonempty sets
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{D}
, respectively. Let us consider the estimator (
1.3
) with
\mathsf{W}=[-1,1]^{d_{\mathcal{G}}}
,
\mathsf{\Theta}=[-1,1]^{d_{\mathcal{D}}},

g_{w}\in\mathcal{G}
and
D_{\theta}\in\mathcal{D}.
For any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
,
\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}}
satisfies the inequality

\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})\lesssim\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{2\beta/(2\beta+d)}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n},

(4.6)

provided that
n\geqslant n_{0}
with
n_{0}
depending only on
\beta,d,\Lambda,H^{*}
, and
H_{\mathcal{G}}
.
In (
4.6
), the notation
\lesssim
stands for an inequality up to a multiplicative constant depending on

, and
H_{\mathcal{D}}
only.

Remark 3
.

The dimensions
d_{\mathcal{G}}
and
d_{\mathcal{D}}
define the complexity of the optimization problem in (
1.3
) and depend on
n
. It follows from the proof of Theorem
2
that

\displaystyle d_{\mathcal{G}}\lesssim\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right)^{d/(2\beta+d)},\quad d_{\mathcal{D}}\lesssim\left(\frac{n}{\log n}\right)^{d/(2\beta+d)}.

Let us note that this dependence of
d_{\mathcal{G}}
and
d_{\mathcal{D}}
on
n
can not be avoided in general.

2
improves the dependence on both
n
and
\delta
in the existing bounds on the JS-divergence between the vanilla GAN estimate
p_{\widehat{w}}
and
p^{*}
.
In
(
Asatryan et al. 2023
, Theorem 3.13 and Theorem 4.4)
, the authors proved that
\mathsf{E}\JS(p_{\widehat{w}},p^{*})
decays as fast as
n^{-1/2}
if
2\beta>d
.
Our results show that in this case the rate of convergence can be much faster.
The reason for suboptimality of
(
Asatryan et al. 2023
, Theorem 4.4)
is the use of the chaining technique to control the global supremum of the empirical process
L_{n}(w,\theta)-L(w,\theta)
.
This approach was successfully applied to WGANs (see, e.g.,
(
Liang 2021
)
) but in the case of vanilla GANs, one can prove better upper bounds for the supremum of
L_{n}(w,\theta)-L(w,\theta)
in a local vicinity of the saddle point.
It is also worth mentioning that, if one uses the bound (
3.3
) from
(
Biau et al. 2020
, Theorem 4.1)
and
(
Belomestny et al. 2023
, Theorem 2)
to control
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
, he will get a suboptimal rate of convergence

\mathsf{E}\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})\lesssim\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{2\beta/(4\beta+d)}.

(4.7)

Finally, the second term
\log(1/\delta)/n
in (
4.6
) significantly improves the standard rate
\sqrt{\log(1/\delta)/n}
which follows from McDiarmid’s inequality.

2
also yields an upper bound on the squared
L_{2}
-distance. Indeed, under the conditions of
2
,
7
and
8
yield that

\|\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}}-\mathsf{p}^{*}\|_{L_{2}(\mu)}^{2}\lesssim\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})\lesssim\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{2\beta/(2\beta+d)}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}.

This coincides (up to logarithmic factors) with the well-known minimax rate of convergence
(\log n/n)^{2\beta/(2\beta+d)}
for estimation of a smooth bounded away from zero density under the squared
L_{2}
-loss.
It turns out that the bound on the JS-divergence from
2
is also minimax optimal up to some logarithmic factors, provided that
\mathsf{p}^{*}
belongs to the class of densities satisfying Assumption
A

p
∗

.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
be a sample of i.i.d. observations generated from a density

\mathsf{p}^{*}
satisfying
A

p
∗

.
Then for any estimate
\widehat{\mathsf{p}}
of
\mathsf{p}^{*}
, that is, measurable function of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},

it holds that

\sup\limits_{\mathsf{p}^{*}}\mathsf{E}\JS(\widehat{\mathsf{p}},\mathsf{p}^{*})\gtrsim n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d)}

with a hidden constant depending on
d
only.

The proof of
3
relies on the van Trees inequality (see
(
Van Trees 1968
, p. 72)
and
(
Gill and Levit 1995
)
).
Though under Assumption
A

p
∗

\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})
is equivalent to
\|\mathsf{p}_{w}-\mathsf{p}^{*}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}
(see
7
and
8
below), we would like to emphasize that
3
does not follow from the existing lower bounds in nonparametric density estimation (see, for instance,
(
Tsybakov 2008
, Exercise 2.10)
). The reason is that the class of admissible densities in Assumption
A

p
∗

is narrower, than
\mathcal{H}^{\beta}([0,1]^{d},H_{0})
,
H_{0}>0
, due to the additional assumption that
\mathsf{p}^{*}
is the density of
g^{*}(Y)
for some
g^{*}\in\mathcal{H}^{1+\beta}_{\Lambda}([0,1]^{d},H^{*})
. If
\mathsf{p}^{*}\in\mathcal{H}^{\beta}([0,1]^{d},H_{0})
, then, according to Brenier’s theorem, there is
\varphi^{*}:[0,1]^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that
\nabla\varphi^{*}(Y)\sim\mathsf{p}^{*}
. However, Caffarelli’s regularity theory does not guarantee that
\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*}
satisfies the condition

\Lambda^{-2}\mathrm{I}_{d\times d}\preceq\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*}(x)^{\top}\nabla^{2}\varphi^{*}(x)\preceq\Lambda^{2}\mathrm{I}_{d\times d},

as required by Assumption
A

p
∗

. Without it, the lower bound will be irrelevant.
Moreover, the theory does not provide a uniform upper bound on the
(\beta+2)
-th derivative of
\varphi^{*}
. In contrary, it states that the
(\beta+2)
-th derivative of
\varphi^{*}
can tend to infinity at the border of
[0,1]^{d}
. For these reasons, we find it necessary to prove an explicit lower bound in our setup.

5
Proofs of the main results

This section contains the proofs of our main results, Theorems
1
,
2
, and
3
. The proof of the upper bounds relies on the uniform high probability bound on
L_{n}(w,\theta)-L(w,\theta)
, given below.

Proposition 1
.

Grant Assumptions
AG
,
AD
, and
A

p

. Let
\mathsf{W}\subseteq[-1,1]^{d_{\mathcal{G}}}
and
\mathsf{\Theta}\subseteq[-1,1]^{d_{\mathcal{D}}}
. Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
and any
\varepsilon\in(0,2]
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left|L_{n}(w,\theta)-L(w,\theta)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant 3C_{\ref{eq:eps_net_const}}\varepsilon+\sqrt{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}}\varepsilon^{1/2}

\displaystyle\quad+4\sqrt{\frac{C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})(d_{\mathcal{G}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+d_{\mathcal{D}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+\log(2/\delta))}{2n}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\frac{C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}\Delta(w,\theta)(d_{\mathcal{G}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+d_{\mathcal{D}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+\log(2/\delta))}{2n}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2(C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}+C_{\ref{eq:C_D_def}})(d_{\mathcal{G}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+d_{\mathcal{D}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+\log(2/\delta))}{3n}

simultaneously for all
w\in\mathsf{W}
and
\theta\in\mathsf{\Theta}
.
Here
\Delta(w,\theta)=\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\log 2-L(w,\theta)
and the constants
C_{\ref{eq:eps_net_const}}
,
C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}
, and
C_{\ref{eq:C_D_def}}
are defined as follows:

C_{\ref{eq:eps_net_const}}=\frac{\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}}{2-2D_{\max}}+\frac{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}}{D_{\min}\wedge(1-D_{\max})},

(5.1)

\displaystyle C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\log^{2}(2D_{\min})}{1/2-D_{\min}}\vee 2\log^{2}2\right)+\left(\frac{\log^{2}(2-2D_{\max})}{D_{\max}-1/2}\vee 2\log^{2}2\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\frac{\log(e/(2D_{\min}))}{2D_{\min}^{2}}+\frac{\log(e/(2-2D_{\max}))}{2(1-D_{\max})^{2}}\right),

(5.2)

C_{\ref{eq:C_D_def}}=\log\left(\frac{1}{D_{\max}}\vee\frac{1}{D_{\min}}\right)\vee\log\left(\frac{1}{1-D_{\max}}\vee\frac{1}{1-D_{\min}}\right)=\log\frac{1}{D_{\min}\land(1-D_{\max})}.

(5.3)

The rest of this section is organized in the following way. Section
5.1
is devoted to the proof of
1
. Then we prove Theorems
1
,
2
, and
3
in Sections
5.2
,
5.3
, and
5.4
, respectively.
In the beginning of these sections, we restate the main results for convenience.

5.1
Proof of
1

Let us start with the sketch of the proof. First, we study large deviations of
L_{n}(w,\theta)-L(w,\theta)
for a fixed pair
(w,\theta)\in\mathsf{W}\times\mathsf{\Theta}
. After that, we show that
L_{n}(w,\theta)
,
L(w,\theta)
, and
\sqrt{\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})}
are Hölder with respect to
w\in\mathsf{W}
and
\theta\in\mathsf{\Theta}
. This and the
\varepsilon
-net argument allow us to derive a uniform large deviation bound on
L_{n}(w,\theta)-L(w,\theta)
. We split the proof into several steps for the sake of readability.

Step 1: large deviation bound on
L_{n}(w,\theta)-L(w,\theta)
.
Under the conditions of
1
, the functional
L(w,\theta)
has a kind of curvature inherited from the properties of the cross-entropy. This special structure allows us to obtain tight large deviation bounds on
|L_{n}(w,\theta)-L(w,\theta)|
. To be more precise, we have the following concentration result.

Lemma 5
.

Fix any
w\in\mathsf{W}
and
\theta\in\mathsf{\Theta}
. Assume the conditions of
1
. Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that

\left|L_{n}(w,\theta)-L(w,\theta)\right|\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}(9\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})+\Delta(w,\theta))\log(2/\delta)}{2n}}+\frac{2C_{\ref{eq:C_D_def}}\log(2/\delta)}{3n},

(5.4)

where
\Delta(w,\theta)=\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\log 2-L(w,\theta)
, and the constants
C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}
,
C_{\ref{eq:C_D_def}}
are defined in (
1
) and (
5.3
), respectively.

The proof of
5
is moved to
Section

C.2
. Our next goal is to convert the concentration inequality (
5.4
) into a uniform bound on
|L_{n}(w,\theta)-L(w,\theta)|
.

Step 2: towards a uniform bound on
L_{n}(w,\theta)-L(w,\theta)
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
be a parameter to be specified later and let
\mathsf{W}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathsf{\Theta}_{\varepsilon}
be the minimal
\varepsilon
-nets of
\mathsf{W}
and
\mathsf{\Theta}
, respectively, with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
. It is straightforward to check that the cardinalities of
\mathsf{W}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathsf{\Theta}_{\varepsilon}
satisfy the inequalities

\left|\mathsf{W}_{\varepsilon}\right|\leqslant\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d_{\mathcal{G}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\left|\mathsf{\Theta}_{\varepsilon}\right|\leqslant\left(\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\right)^{d_{\mathcal{D}}}.

Applying
5
and the union bound, we conclude that, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left|L_{n}(w,\theta)-L(w,\theta)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}(9\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})+\Delta(w,\theta))\log(2|\mathsf{W}_{\varepsilon}||\mathsf{\Theta}_{\varepsilon}|/\delta)}{2n}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2C_{\ref{eq:C_D_def}}\log(2|\mathsf{W}_{\varepsilon}||\mathsf{\Theta}_{\varepsilon}|/\delta)}{3n}

(5.5)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}(9\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})+\Delta(w,\theta))(d_{\mathcal{G}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+d_{\mathcal{D}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+\log(2/\delta))}{2n}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2C_{\ref{eq:C_D_def}}(d_{\mathcal{G}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+d_{\mathcal{D}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+\log(2/\delta))}{3n}

simultaneously for all
(w,\theta)\in\mathsf{W}_{\varepsilon}\times\mathsf{\Theta}_{\varepsilon}
. If we show that
L_{n}(w,\theta)
,
L(w,\theta)
, and
\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})
are Lipschitz or at least Hölder with respect to
w\in\mathsf{W}
and
\theta\in\mathsf{\Theta}
, then we can easily extend the bound (
5.1
) from the
\varepsilon
-net
\mathsf{W}_{\varepsilon}\times\mathsf{\Theta}_{\varepsilon}
to the whole set
\mathsf{W}\times\mathsf{\Theta}
. The Lipschitzness of
L_{n}(w,\theta)
and
L(w,\theta)
easily follows from Assumptions
AG
and
AD
.

Lemma 6
.

Grant Assumptions
AD
and
AG
.
Let
w,w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathsf{W}
and
\theta,\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathsf{\Theta}
. Then it holds that

\left|L_{n}(w_{1},\theta)-L_{n}(w_{2},\theta)\right|\leqslant\frac{\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{\infty}}{2-2D_{\max}}\quad\text{almost surely.}

and

\left|L_{n}(w,\theta_{1})-L_{n}(w,\theta_{2})\right|\leqslant\frac{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|_{\infty}}{D_{\min}\wedge(1-D_{\max})}\quad\text{almost surely.}

Moreover, under Assumptions
AD
and
AG
, we have

\left|L(w_{1},\theta)-L(w_{2},\theta)\right|\leqslant\frac{\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\|w_{1}-w_{2}\|_{\infty}}{2-2D_{\max}}

and

\left|L(w,\theta_{1})-L(w,\theta_{2})\right|\leqslant\frac{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\|\theta_{1}-\theta_{2}\|_{\infty}}{D_{\min}\wedge(1-D_{\max})}

for any
w,w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathsf{W}
and
\theta,\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathsf{\Theta}
.

The proof of
6
is given in
Section

C.3
. The next result plays a crucial role in the analysis of
\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mathsf{p}
and
\mathsf{q}
be the densities of two probability distributions with respect to a dominating measure
\mu
. Then it holds that

\frac{1}{4}\int\frac{(\mathsf{p}(x)-\mathsf{q}(x))^{2}}{\mathsf{p}(x)+\mathsf{q}(x)}\mathrm{d}\mu\leqslant\JS(\mathsf{p},\mathsf{q})\leqslant\frac{\log 2}{2}\int\frac{(\mathsf{p}(x)-\mathsf{q}(x))^{2}}{\mathsf{p}(x)+\mathsf{q}(x)}\mathrm{d}\mu.

(5.6)

7
yields the Hölderness of
\JS^{1/2}(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})
with respect to
w\in\mathsf{W}
, provided that Assumption
A

p

is fulfilled.

Corollary 1
.

Grant Assumption
A

p

. Then, for any
u,v\in\mathsf{W}
, it holds that

\left|\sqrt{\JS(\mathsf{p}_{u},\mathsf{p}^{*})}-\sqrt{\JS(\mathsf{p}_{v},\mathsf{p}^{*})}\right|\leqslant\frac{\sqrt{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\log 2}}{2^{1/4}}\|u-v\|_{\infty}^{1/4}<\sqrt{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}}\|u-v\|_{\infty}^{1/4}.

The proofs of
7
and
1
are moved to
Section

C.4
and
Section

C.5
, respectively. We are ready to prove a uniform bound on large deviations of
L_{n}(w,\theta)-L(w,\theta)
.

Step 3: uniform bound on
L_{n}(w,\theta)-L(w,\theta)
.
Let
E
be the event where (
5.1
) holds. Choose any
(w,\theta)\in\mathsf{W}\times\mathsf{\Theta}
and denote the closest to
(w,\theta)
element of
\mathsf{W}_{\varepsilon}\times\mathsf{\Theta}_{\varepsilon}
by
(w_{\varepsilon},\theta_{\varepsilon})
. Then, due to
6
, the following holds on
E
:

\displaystyle\left|L_{n}(w,\theta)-L(w,\theta)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|L_{n}(w_{\varepsilon},\theta_{\varepsilon})-L(w_{\varepsilon},\theta_{\varepsilon})\right|+2\left(\frac{\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}}{2-2D_{\max}}+\frac{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}}{D_{\min}\vee(1-D_{\max})}\right)\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\left(\frac{\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}}{2-2D_{\max}}+\frac{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}}{D_{\min}\vee(1-D_{\max})}\right)\varepsilon

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\frac{C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}(9\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w_{\varepsilon}},\mathsf{p}^{*})+\Delta(w_{\varepsilon},\theta_{\varepsilon}))(d_{\mathcal{G}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+d_{\mathcal{D}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+\log(2/\delta))}{2n}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2C_{\ref{eq:C_D_def}}(d_{\mathcal{G}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+d_{\mathcal{D}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+\log(2/\delta))}{3n}.

According to
6
and
1
,

\sqrt{\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w_{\varepsilon}},\mathsf{p}^{*})}\leqslant\sqrt{\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})}+\sqrt{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}}\varepsilon^{1/4}

and

\displaystyle\sqrt{\Delta(w_{\varepsilon},\theta_{\varepsilon})}=\sqrt{\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w_{\varepsilon}},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\log 2-L(w_{\varepsilon},\theta_{\varepsilon})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{2\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\log 2-L(w,\theta)+2\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\varepsilon^{1/2}+C_{\ref{eq:eps_net_const}}\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})}+\sqrt{\Delta(w,\theta)}+\sqrt{2\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}}\varepsilon^{1/4}+\sqrt{C_{\ref{eq:eps_net_const}}\varepsilon}.

Thus, on
E
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left|L_{n}(w,\theta)-L(w,\theta)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2C_{\ref{eq:eps_net_const}}\varepsilon+4\sqrt{\frac{C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})(d_{\mathcal{G}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+d_{\mathcal{D}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+\log(2/\delta))}{2n}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\frac{C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}\Delta(w,\theta)(d_{\mathcal{G}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+d_{\mathcal{D}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+\log(2/\delta))}{2n}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\frac{C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\varepsilon^{1/2}(d_{\mathcal{G}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+d_{\mathcal{D}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+\log(2/\delta))}{n}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\frac{C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}C_{\ref{eq:eps_net_const}}\varepsilon(d_{\mathcal{G}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+d_{\mathcal{D}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+\log(2/\delta))}{2n}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2C_{\ref{eq:C_D_def}}(d_{\mathcal{G}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+d_{\mathcal{D}}\log(2/\varepsilon)+\log(2/\delta))}{3n}.

where
C_{\ref{eq:eps_net_const}}
is defined in (
5.1
).
Applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to the fourth and the fifth terms, we get the desired bound.

5.2
Proof of Theorem
1

Theorem 4

(restatement of Theorem
1
)
.

Assume
AG
,
AD
, and
A

p

. Let
\mathsf{W}\subseteq[-1,1]^{d_{\mathcal{G}}}
and
\mathsf{\Theta}\subseteq[-1,1]^{d_{\mathcal{D}}}
. Then, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, it holds that

\displaystyle\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}-\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{(\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}+\Delta_{\mathcal{D}})\big[(d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}})\log(2(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1)n)+\log(8/\delta)\big]}{n}}

\displaystyle\quad+C_{\ref{eq:c_d_min_d_max}}\cdot\frac{(d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}})\log(2(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1)n)+\log(8/\delta)}{n}

where

\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}=\min\limits_{w\in\mathsf{W}}\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*}),\quad\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}=\max\limits_{w\in\mathsf{W}}\min\limits_{\theta\in\mathsf{\Theta}}[\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\log 2-L(w,\theta)],

and

C_{\ref{eq:c_d_min_d_max}}=\left(\frac{\log(1/D_{\min})}{D_{\min}^{2}}+\frac{\log\big(1/(1-D_{\max}\big)}{(1-D_{\max})^{2}}\right).

Here
\lesssim
stands for inequality up to an absolute multiplicative constant.

Let us introduce
\overline{w}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}_{w\in\mathsf{W}}\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})
.
We begin with studying the excess risk

\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}=\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\min\limits_{w\in\mathsf{W}}\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})=\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\overline{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*}),

(5.7)

where
\widehat{w}
is given by (
1.3
).
For any
w\in\mathsf{W}
, let
\theta_{w}^{*}
denote the parameter, correspoding to the best discriminator in
\mathcal{D}
:

\theta_{w}^{*}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits_{\theta\in\mathsf{\Theta}}L(w,\theta).

Then the definition of
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
yields that

\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\overline{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})\leqslant\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}+L(\widehat{w},\theta^{*}_{\widehat{w}})-L(\overline{w},\theta^{*}_{\overline{w}}).

Here we used the fact that for any
w\in\mathsf{W}
and any
\theta\in\mathsf{\Theta}
it holds that
L(w,\theta)+\log 2\leqslant\JS(\mathsf{p}_{w},\mathsf{p}^{*})
(see, e.g.,
(
Goodfellow et al. 2014
, Proposition 1)
or
(
Biau et al. 2020
, Section 2)
) for the explanation.
The expression in the right-hand side of the last inequality can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}+\underbrace{\bigl(L(\widehat{w},\theta^{*}_{\widehat{w}})-L_{n}(\widehat{w},\theta^{*}_{\widehat{w}})\bigr)}_{T_{1}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\bigl(L_{n}(\widehat{w},\theta^{*}_{\widehat{w}})-L_{n}(\overline{w},\theta^{*}_{\overline{w}})\bigr)}_{T_{2}}+\underbrace{\bigl(L_{n}(\overline{w},\theta^{*}_{\overline{w}})-L(\overline{w},\theta^{*}_{\overline{w}})\bigr)}_{T_{3}}.

(5.8)

We split the rest of the proof into several steps for convenience.

Step 1: bounds on
T_{1}
and
T_{3}
.

Let us take

\displaystyle\varepsilon

\displaystyle=\frac{C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}(d_{\mathcal{G}}\log(2n)+d_{\mathcal{D}}\log(2n)+\log(8/\delta))}{C_{\ref{eq:eps_net_const}}n}

\displaystyle\quad\wedge\frac{C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}^{2}(d_{\mathcal{G}}\log(2n)+d_{\mathcal{D}}\log(2n)+\log(8/\delta))^{2}}{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}^{2}n^{2}}\wedge 1.

(5.9)

Then
C_{\ref{eq:eps_net_const}}\varepsilon\leqslant 1/n
,
\sqrt{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\varepsilon}\leqslant 1/n
, and

\log(1/\varepsilon)\leqslant\log n+\log\left(\frac{C_{\ref{eq:eps_net_const}}}{C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}}\vee\frac{\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}^{2}}{C_{\ref{eq:c_D_var}}^{2}}\right)\lesssim\log n+\log(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1).

Applying
1
with
\varepsilon
defined in (
5.2
),
we get that, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, we simultaneously have

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{(\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})+\Delta_{\mathcal{D}})\big[(d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}})\log(2(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1)n)+\log(8/\delta)\big]}{n}}

\displaystyle\quad+C_{\ref{eq:c_d_min_d_max}}\cdot\frac{(d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}})\log(2(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1)n)+\log(8/\delta)}{n}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sqrt{\frac{(\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\widehat{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})-\Delta_{\mathcal{G}})\big[(d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}})\log(2(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1)n)+\log(8/\delta)\big]}{n}}

(5.10)

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\frac{(\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}+\Delta_{\mathcal{D}})\big[(d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}})\log(2(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1)n)+\log(8/\delta)\big]}{n}}

\displaystyle\quad+C_{\ref{eq:c_d_min_d_max}}\cdot\frac{(d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}})\log(2(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1)n)+\log(8/\delta)}{n}

and

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{(\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}+\Delta_{\mathcal{D}})\big[(d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}})\log(2(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1)n)+\log(8/\delta)\big]}{n}}

\displaystyle\quad+C_{\ref{eq:c_d_min_d_max}}\cdot\frac{(d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}})\log(2(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1)n)+\log(8/\delta)}{n}.

(5.11)

Here we used the fact that
\JS(\mathsf{p}_{\overline{w}},\mathsf{p}^{*})=\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
by the definitions of
\overline{w}
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}
. Also, it holds that
\Delta(w,\theta_{w}^{*})\leqslant\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
due to the definitions of
\theta_{w}^{*}
and
\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}
. Note that the hidden constants in (
5.2
) and (
5.2
) are absolute.

Step 2: a bound on
T_{2}
.

It remains to bound
T_{2}
in (
5.2
).
For any
w\in\mathsf{W}
, let us denote

\widehat{\theta}_{w}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}\limits_{\theta\in\mathsf{\Theta}}L_{n}(w,\theta).

By the definition of
\widehat{w}
, we have

L_{n}(\widehat{w},\widehat{\theta}_{\widehat{w}})\leqslant L_{n}(\overline{w},\widehat{\theta}_{\overline{w}}).

Then
T_{2}\leqslant L_{n}(\overline{w},\widehat{\theta}_{\overline{w}})-L_{n}(\overline{w},\theta^{*}_{\overline{w}})
, and, applying
1
with
\varepsilon
from (
5.2
)
again, we obtain that, with probability at least
1-\delta/2
, it holds that

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim L(\overline{w},\widehat{\theta}_{\overline{w}})-L(\overline{w},\theta^{*}_{\overline{w}})

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\frac{(\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}+\Delta(\overline{w},\widehat{\theta}_{\overline{w}}))\big[(d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}})\log(2(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1)n)+\log(8/\delta)\big]}{n}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\frac{(\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}+\Delta_{\mathcal{D}})\big[(d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}})\log(2(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1)n)+\log(8/\delta)\big]}{n}}

\displaystyle\quad+C_{\ref{eq:c_d_min_d_max}}\cdot\frac{(d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}})\log(2(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1)n)+\log(8/\delta)}{n},

where, as before, the hidden constants are absolute.
Since

\Delta(\overline{w},\widehat{\theta}_{\overline{w}})=\Delta(\overline{w},\theta^{*}_{\overline{w}})+L(\overline{w},\theta^{*}_{\overline{w}})-L(\overline{w},\widehat{\theta}_{\overline{w}})\leqslant\Delta_{\mathcal{D}}+L(\overline{w},\theta^{*}_{\overline{w}})-L(\overline{w},\widehat{\theta}_{\overline{w}}),

the following inequality holds on the same event:

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle\lesssim-\left(L(\overline{w},\theta^{*}_{\overline{w}})-L(\overline{w},\widehat{\theta}_{\overline{w}})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\frac{\left(L(\overline{w},\theta^{*}_{\overline{w}})-L(\overline{w},\widehat{\theta}_{\overline{w}})\right)\big[(d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}})\log(2(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1)n)+\log(8/\delta)\big]}{n}}

\displaystyle\quad+\sqrt{\frac{(\Delta_{\mathcal{G}}+\Delta_{\mathcal{D}})\big[(d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}})\log(2(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1)n)+\log(8/\delta)\big]}{n}}

\displaystyle\quad+C_{\ref{eq:c_d_min_d_max}}\cdot\frac{(d_{\mathcal{G}}+d_{\mathcal{D}})\log(2(\mathsf{L}_{\mathcal{G}}\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{X}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{\Theta}}\vee\mathsf{L}_{\mathsf{p}}\vee 1)n)+\log(8/\delta)}{n}.

Maximizing the right-hand side over
(L(\overline{w},\theta^{*}_{\overline{w}})-L(\overline{w},\widehat{\theta}_{\overline{w}}))^{1/2}
, we obtain that

6
Conclusion and future directions

Despite the huge recent interest to theoretical properties of generative adversarial networks, the existing papers mostly focus on the generalization ability of GANs, missing the issues occurring in their practical use. For instance, one of the most challenging problems in the GAN training process is the mode collapse phenomenon
(
Salimans et al. 2016
;
Che et al. 2016
)
, which appears for various loss functions, including Wasserstein GANs, “vanilla” GANs, or GANs with other divergence measures. Moreover, in the present literature on Wasserstein GANs, the estimation error is mostly governed by the rates of convergence of the empirical measure to the population distribution. This leaves a question, whether WGANs are able to produce a distribution estimate, which is strictly better that just the empirical distribution. The first step in this direction was made in the recent work
(
Vardanyan et al. 2023
)
, where the authors imposed regularity conditions on generators. This agrees with the papers on vanilla GANs in a sense that successful generalization requires smoothness of generators and discriminators. Practitioners often use regularization (see, e.g.,
Gulrajani et al. 2017
) for this purpose.
