Twisted Orthogonality Relations For Certain ℤ / m ​ ℤ \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} -graded Algebras
Prashant Arote

Abstract
In this paper we will study the notion of a Frobenius ⋆ \star -algebra and prove some orthogonality relations for the irreducible characters of a Frobenius ⋆ \star -algebra. Then we will study ℤ / m ​ ℤ \mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z} -graded Frobenius ⋆ \star -algebras and prove some twisted orthogonality relations for them.



Introduction

The goal of this paper is to define twisted characters for a special class of
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
-graded Frobenius algebras and prove some orthogonality relations for them.

We begin by recalling the notion of a Frobenius algebra and a symmetric Frobenius algebra.
In this paper we will only deal with symmetric Frobenius algebras.
Further we will give a description of the centre of a symmetric Frobenius algebra and recall some orthogonality relations for their irreducible characters.
All of these results are found in
[
Fo
]
.

In section 2 we define a
Frobenius
\star
-algebra
.
We will prove that Frobenius
\star
-algebras are semisimple and derive some orthogonality relations for their irreducible characters.
An example of a Frobenius
\star
-algebra is the group ring
\mathbb{C}[G]
of a finite group
G
.
Another example is that of a (complexified) based ring, which is discussed in
[
Lu
]
.

In section 3 we will recall the notion of group graded rings and group graded modules over a group graded ring.
Further we state some results about Clifford theory for group graded rings without proof.
We refer to
[
Da
]
for more details.

In section 4 we define the notion of a twisted character for a
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
-graded Frobenius
\star
-algebra and by applying the results from sections 2 and 3 we obtain some orthogonality relations for twisted characters of a
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
-graded symmetric Frobenius
\star
-algebra.

Let us describe an example of twisted characters and orthogonality relations.
Let
G
be a finite group and
\sigma
be a automorphism of
G
of order
m
.
Therefore we get an action of
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
on
G
and on Irr(
G
) (= set of all irreducible representations of G over
\mathbb{C}
upto isomorphism).
Then
\mathbb{C}[G\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}]
is a
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
-graded Frobenius
\star
-algebra. If
\rho\in\mbox{Irr}(G)^{\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}}
, then we can extend
\rho
to a representation
\widetilde{\rho}
of
G\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}
.
In this case the twisted character
\widetilde{\chi}_{\rho}
of
G
corresponding to
\rho

is
\widetilde{\chi}_{\rho}(g)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\chi_{\widetilde{\rho}}(g,\sigma)
.
Then the results of this paper imply that the set of twisted characters

forms an orthogonal basis for the space of
\sigma
-twisted class functions on

.

The twisted orthogonality relations are used by G. Lusztig in his theory of character sheaves for reductive groups over a finite field.
Orthogonality relations for twisted characters of the Grothendieck algebras of
\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z}

1
Frobenius Algebra

Now we begin by recalling the definition of a Frobenius algebra and some of its properties.

Definition 1.1
.

A Frobenius algebra
\mathcal{A}
is a finite dimensional, unital, associative
\mathbb{C}
-algebra equipped with a linear functional
\lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that the bilinear form
on
\mathcal{A}
defined by
\left(a,b\right)=\lambda(ab)
is nondegenerate.
Moreover if
\lambda(ab)=\lambda(ba),\ \forall\ a,\ b\in\mathcal{A}
, then
\mathcal{A}
is called as symmetric Frobenius algebra.

\bullet
A linear functional
\lambda
on an algebra
\mathcal{A}
is said to be a nondegenerate if the bilinear form on
\mathcal{A}
defined by
\left(a,b\right)=\lambda(ab)
is nondegenerate.

\bullet
To make connection more explicit, we say that the pair (
\mathcal{A}
,
\lambda
) is a Frobenius algebra, if
\lambda
is a nondegenerate linear functional on
\mathcal{A}
.

As
\mathcal{A}
is finite dimensional,
\mathcal{A}
∗
=Hom
{}_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{C})
is an (
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{A}
)-bimodule, where for
a\in
\mathcal{A}
and
\gamma\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
we define

\begin{cases}(a.\gamma)(x)=\gamma(xa)\\
(\gamma.a)(x)=\gamma(ax)\end{cases}\forall\ x\in\mathcal{A}

There is an equivalent definition of a Frobenius algebra: A Frobenius algebra
\mathcal{A}
is a finite-dimensional, unital, associative
\mathbb{C}
-algebra equipped with a left
\mathcal{A}
-module isomorphism
\phi
:
\mathcal{A}
\rightarrow\mathcal{A}^{*}
.
In this case it is denoted by the pair (
\mathcal{A}
,
\phi
).

The equivalence between the two definitions is given by:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A},\lambda)\ \rightsquigarrow\ \phi(a)=\lambda(-\cdot a)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A},\phi)\ \rightsquigarrow\ \lambda(x)=\phi(1)(x).

\bullet
(
\mathcal{A}
,
\phi
) is a symmetric Frobenius algebra if and only if
\phi
is a (
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{A}
) bimodule isomorphism between
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}
∗
.

\bullet
If
((a_{i}),(b_{i}))
is an ordered pair of bases for
\mathcal{A}
such that
\phi
(b_{i})(a_{j})=\delta_{ij}
, then we say that the bases
(a_{i}),(b_{i})
are
\mathbf{\phi}\textbf{-dual}
. If
(a_{i})
is a basis of (
\mathcal{A}
,
\phi
), then there exists a second basis (
b_{i}
) of (
\mathcal{A}
,
\phi
) such that
(a_{i})
,
(b_{i})
are
\phi
-dual.

Lemma 1.2
.

Let (a

), (b

) be
\phi
-dual bases for a Frobenius algebra (
\mathcal{A}
,
\phi
),
and let
\gamma

\in

\mathcal{A}
∗
.
Then

\phi^{-1}(\gamma)=\sum_{i}\gamma(a_{i})b_{i}.

Definition 1.3
.

A linear functional
\gamma
on an algebra
\mathcal{A}
is said to be a class functional if
\gamma(ab)=\gamma(ba)\ \forall\ a,\ b\in\mathcal{A}
and cf(
\mathcal{A}
) denotes the set of class functionals on
\mathcal{A}
.

Lemma 1.4
.

Suppose (
\mathcal{A}
,
\phi
) is a symmetric Frobenius algebra then

\phi^{-1}(\mbox{cf}(\mathcal{A}))=Z(\mathcal{A})\ (=\mbox{centre of}\ \mathcal{A}).

Proof.

As (
\mathcal{A}
,
\phi
) is a symmetric Frobenius algebra,
\phi
is a (
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{A}
) bimodule isomorphism between
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{A}
∗
.
We have

\displaystyle\gamma\in cf(\mathcal{A})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\gamma(xy)=\gamma(yx),\ \forall\ x,y\in\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(\gamma\cdot x)(y)=(x\cdot\gamma)(y),\ \forall\ x,y\in\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\gamma\cdot x=x\cdot\gamma,\ \forall\ x\in\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\phi^{-1}(\gamma\cdot x)=\phi^{-1}(x\cdot\gamma),\ \forall\ x\in\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\phi^{-1}(\gamma)\cdot x=x\cdot\phi^{-1}(\gamma),\ \forall\ x\in\mathcal{A}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\phi^{-1}(\gamma)\in Z(\mathcal{A}).

Hence,
\phi^{-1}\left(cf(\mathcal{A})\right)=Z(\mathcal{A})
.
This proves the lemma.
∎

2
Frobenius
\star
-algebra

Now we will define a Frobenius
\star
-algebra and prove some results about it.

Definition 2.1
.

A Frobenius
\star
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
is a finite dimensional, associative, unital
\mathbb{C}
-algebra equipped with a class functional
\lambda\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{A\rightarrow\mathbb{C}}
and a map
\star\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
, such that the following holds:

\bullet\ (a+b)^{\star}=a^{\star}+b^{\star}\ ,\forall\ a,b\ \in\mathcal{A}.

\bullet\ (a^{\star})^{\star}=a,\ \forall\ a\ \in\mathcal{A}.

\bullet\ (ab)^{\star}=b^{\star}a^{\star}\ ,\forall\ a,b\in\mathcal{A}.

\bullet\ (\alpha a)^{\star}=\overline{\alpha}a^{\star}\,,\forall\ a\in\mathcal{A},\forall\ \alpha\in\mathbb{C}.

\bullet

\langle a,b\rangle=\lambda(ab^{*})
is a positive definite hermitian form on
\mathcal{A}
.

Remark 2.2
.

As
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is nondegenerate, so
\lambda
is a nondegenerate class functional.
Therefore
\mathcal{A}
is a symmetric Frobenius algebra.

Example 2.3
.

\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{C}[G]
with
\lambda
is defined as

\lambda
(g)=

\begin{cases}1\ \mbox{if}\ g=e;\\
0\ \mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

\star
-map is defined by

g^{\star}=g^{-1},\ \forall\ g\in G
and extended to
\mathcal{A}
by conjugate linearity.

Now onwards in this section
\mathcal{A}
denotes a Frobenius
\star
-algebra (
\mathcal{A}
,
\lambda

Theorem 2.4
.

\mathcal{A}
is a semi-simple algebra.

Proof.

By Artin-Wedderburn theorem it is enough to prove that
\mathcal{A}
is a semi-simple as a right
\mathcal{A}
-module.
To prove the theorem it is enough to show that for every right ideal
\mathcal{I}
of
\mathcal{A}
there exists another right ideal
\mathcal{J}
of
\mathcal{A}
such that
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{I}\oplus\mathcal{J}
, as right
\mathcal{A}
-modules.

Now for a fix right ideal
\mathcal{I}
of
\mathcal{A}
. Define
\mathcal{J}
as,

\mathcal{J}=\{a\in\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\langle a,y\rangle=0,\forall\ y\in\mathcal{I}\}

i.e.\ \mathcal{J}
is the orthogonal compliment of
\mathcal{I}
in
\mathcal{A}
.
As
\langle\,,\rangle
is a positive definite hermitian form on

\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{I}\oplus\mathcal{J}
as a vector space.
To prove the theorem it is enough to show that
\mathcal{J}
is a right ideal of
\mathcal{A}
. Let
r\in\mathcal{J}
be any element and
a\in

\mathcal{A}
be any element and y
\in\mathcal{I}
. Consider

\langle ra,y\rangle=\lambda(ray^{*})=\langle r,ya^{*}\rangle=0

( since
\mathcal{I}
is right ideal of A)

\Rightarrow\langle ra,y\rangle=0,\ \forall\ y\in\mathcal{I}

\Rightarrow ra\in\mathcal{J},\ \forall\ a\in\mathcal{A},\ \forall\ r\in\mathcal{J}
.
This proves that
\mathcal{J}
is a right ideal of
\mathcal{A}
