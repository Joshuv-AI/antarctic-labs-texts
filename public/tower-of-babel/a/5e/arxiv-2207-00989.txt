Abstract
. We study some discrete invariants of Newton non-degenerate polynomial maps f : 𝕂 n → 𝕂 n f:\mathbb{K}^{n}\to\mathbb{K}^{n} defined over an algebraically closed field of Puiseux series 𝕂 \mathbb{K} , equipped with a non-trivial valuation. It is known that the set 𝒮 ⁡ ( f ) \mathcal{S}(f) of points at which f f is not finite forms an algebraic hypersurface in 𝕂 n \mathbb{K}^{n} . The coordinate-wise valuation of 𝒮 ⁡ ( f ) ∩ ( 𝕂 ∗ ) n \mathcal{S}(f)\cap(\mathbb{K}^{*})^{n} is a piecewise-linear object in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , which we call the tropical non-properness set of f f . We show that the tropical polynomial map corresponding to f f has fibers satisfying a particular combinatorial degeneracy condition exactly over points in the tropical non-properness set of f f . We then use this description to outline a polyhedral method for computing this set, and to recover the fan dual to the Newton polytope of the set at which a complex polynomial map is not finite. The proofs rely on classical correspondence and structural results from tropical geometry, combined with a new description of 𝒮 ⁡ ( f ) \mathcal{S}(f) in terms of multivariate resultants.

1.
Introduction

A polynomial map
f:=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}):\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
is
not proper
at
y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
if there is a sequence
\{x_{k}\}_{k}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
satisfying
\|x_{k}\|\rightarrow\infty
and
f(x_{k})\rightarrow y
. Investigating the set
{\mathcal{S}}(f)
of points for which
f
is not proper was initiated by Jelonek in
[
13
]
, where he described its topological properties and provided computational results. He also used
{\mathcal{S}}(f)
to solve classical problems on the topology of
f

[
15
,
14
,
18
,
19
]
. Currently, describing the geometry and topology of the
Jelonek set
,
{\mathcal{S}}(f)
, can be done exclusively using standard elimination methods
[
13
,
30
, Proposition 7]
. However, these can often be inefficient whenever the polynomials involved have high degrees.

Our main result is forward Theorem
1.2
. We establish in it a correspondence relating the “non-properness” of polynomial maps over a generalized field of Puiseux series to the so-called “tropical non-properness” of certain piecewise-affine maps. We call them
tropical polynomial maps
. Tropical non-properness occurs whenever a preimage under a tropical polynomial map satisfies a certain non-degeneracy condition. We introduce in forward Theorem
1.7
a recipe for recovering the tropical non-properness set by means of polyhedral geometry. Consequently, our results can be applied to reconstruct (see Remark
1.5
) the dual fan of the Newton polytope corresponding to
{\mathcal{S}}(f)
above.

1.1.
Theoretical framework and main results

A polynomial map

K[X]
is integral over
K[Y]

is finite. Note that a map

is not finite exactly at points in its Jelonek set (see e.g.,
[
16
,
30
]

[

]
were later extended to polynomial maps over any above

[

30

27

20
]
.
Accordingly, the set of points in

Let
\Bbbk
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero. In this paper, we consider one-parametric series

with coefficients in

of characteristic zero

]

It is endowed with a function

It is known (see e.g.,

The first correspondence theorem in tropical geometry was shown in

Definition 1.1

(Tropical non-properness)
.

Let

Organization of the paper

2.
Jelonek set from faces of polytopes

We start this section with some notations and definitions. In §
2.1
we define almost face-generic polynomial maps, and show that they form a Zariski open subset in the space of all maps with the same support. In §
2.2

2.1.
Preliminaries on Newton polytopes

A subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a
polyhedron
if it is the intersection of finitely-many closed half-spaces. A
face

\Phi
of a polyhedron
\Pi\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by the intersection of
\Pi
with a hyperplane
H
for which
\Pi
is contained in the closure of one of the two halfspaces determined by
H
. We call
H
a
supporting
hyperplane of
\Phi
. The face
\Phi
is called
proper
if
\Phi\neq\Pi
. We say that
\Phi
is
dicritical
if the outward normal vector of one of its supporting hyperplanes positively spans a dicritical half-line. That is, there exists a functional
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto\alpha_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}x_{n}
that maximizes
\Pi
at
\Phi
, and satisfies
\alpha_{i}>0
and
\alpha_{j}\leq 0
for some
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

If a polyhedron is bounded, then we call it a
polytope
. Given a tuple of polytopes
\Pi:=(\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{n})
, the Minkowski sum of their members is a polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, denoted by
\sum\Pi
. Here, the

of any two subsets

is the coordinate-wise sum
A+B:=\{a+b\penalty\ |\penalty\ a\in A,\penalty\ b\in B\}
. For every integer

, we use
[n]
as a shorthand for
\{1,\ldots,n\}
. The sub-tuple
(\Pi_{i})_{i\in I}
is denoted by

for any

. A tuple
\Gamma:=(\Gamma_{1},\ldots,\Gamma_{n})
of polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be a

(or, simply,

whenever it is clear from the context) of
\Pi
if the subset
\Gamma_{i}
is a face of
\Pi_{i}
(

) and
\sum\Gamma
is a face of
\sum\Pi
. We use the notation
\Gamma\prec\Pi
. We say that
\Gamma\prec\Pi
is a
proper
face if
\sum\Gamma
a proper face of
\sum\Pi
.

Definition 2.6

The tuple

(a)

(b)

Proposition 2.7
.

The set of almost face-generic tuples in

contains a Zariski open subset.

Proof.

Let

and

be the two subsets consisting of tuples in

and

It is enough to show that each of these sets is Zariski open in

Concerning

Concerning

Concerning Equation (

Thus, it is enough to choose

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\Gamma
be a proper face of
\Delta
. Then, the face-resultant

is empty if

is not semi-origin (see Definition

\Gamma^{\prime}
of

\dim{\mathcal{R}}_{\Gamma}(f)\cap{\mathcal{R}}_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(f)\leq n-2

Proof.

Keeping the notations of Definition

, if
\Gamma
is not semi-origin, then for any

(f-w)_{\Gamma}=f_{\Gamma}
. Equation (

) of Definition

shows that

, and thus its projection under

is empty.

Let
\Gamma
be a semi-origin face. Now, we show that
\dim{\mathcal{R}}_{\Gamma}(f)\leq n-1
. Note that if
\dim X_{\Gamma}\leq n-1
, then
\dim\pi(X_{\Gamma})\leq n-1
and we are done.

Assume in what follows that
\Gamma
is semi-origin, and that
\dim X_{\Gamma}\geq n
. Recall that
\mathbb{V}^{*}_{\!\!K}(f_{I\Gamma})
is a complete intersection for every

(Definition

(b)
). Then, it is an easy computation, using the Jacobian matrix

, to show of

is either empty or is a complete intersection. We thus get

. Since
\dim\sum\Gamma\leq n-1
, up to multiplying each polynomial by an appropriate monomial, for any

, the system

is formed by quasi-homogeneous polynomials in

. Consequently, the fibers under the map

have positive dimension. Hence, we get

Now we show that
\dim{\mathcal{R}}_{\Gamma}(f)\cap{\mathcal{R}}_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(f)\leq n-2

and
{\mathcal{R}}_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(f)

is almost face-generic, both

and

are smooth, and thus irreducible. Consequently we obtain

\dim{\mathcal{R}}_{\Gamma}(f)=\dim{\mathcal{R}}_{\Gamma^{\prime}}(f)=n-1

(a)
as

is almost face-generic and
\Gamma\neq\Gamma^{\prime}

2.3.
Topological degree of a map

If
\Pi
is a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we use
\Vol_{n}(\Pi)
to denote its Euclidean volume. Assume now that
\Pi
is a collection of
n
convex bodies
\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then the classical notion of
mixed volume
of
\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{n}
is defined as

V(\Pi)=V(\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{n}):=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-1)^{k+n}\sum_{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k}}\Vol_{n}(\Pi_{i_{1}}+\cdots+\Pi_{i_{k}}),

where the summation between polytopes is the Minkowski sum. For example, if
n=2
, we have

V(\Pi)=\Vol_{2}(\Pi_{1}+\Pi_{2})-\Vol_{2}(\Pi_{1})-\Vol_{2}(\Pi_{2}).

We have the following well-known version of Bernstein’s Theorem
[
1
]
(see e.g.
[
25
]
).

Theorem 2.14
.

Let
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}
be polynomials in
n
variables
K[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]
, such that
V(\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{n})\neq 0
, where
\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{n}
denote their respective Newton polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
I^{*}(g)
denote the number of isolated solutions in
\mathbb{V}^{*}_{\!\!K}(g)
, counted with multiplicities. Then, it holds that
I^{*}(g)\leq V(\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{n})
, with equality if and only if
g_{\Gamma}=\underline{0}
does not have solutions in
\bm{T}
for any proper face
\Gamma
of
\Pi
.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
f:K^{n}\to K^{n}
be a polynomial map. We say that
f
is
dominant
if the determinant of its Jacobian matrix is a polynomial that is not identically zero. If
f
is dominant, there is an integer
k\in\mathbb{N}
and a Zariski open subset
W\subset K^{n}
for which
f^{-1}(w)
has exactly
k
isolated points for any
w\in W
. We call the value
k
the
topological degree of
f
, and we denote it by
\mu(f)
.
∎

Remark 2.16
.

The Jelonek set
{\mathcal{S}}(f)
of the map
f
from Definition
2.15
can be defined as the set of points
w\in K^{n}
at which the polynomial system
f-w=\underline{0}
has less than
\mu(f)
isolated solutions in
K^{n}
counted with multiplicities.

Lemma 2.17
.

For any
f\in K^{A}
representing a dominant almost face-generic map
K^{n}\to K^{n}
, we have
\mu(f)=V(\Delta)
, where
\Delta
is defined in Equation (
2.1
).

Proof.

Take
W=f(\bm{T})\cap({\mathcal{R}}\cup{\mathcal{S}}(f))
, where
{\mathcal{R}}
is the union of all face-resultants of

. Recall that
f
is dominant, and the Jelonek set is a hypersurface
[
13
]
. These facts, together with Proposition
2.7
, imply that
W
is Zariski open in
K^{n}
. Since
w\in f(\bm{T})
, we get
f^{-1}(w)\subset\bm{T}
. By definition, the map is finite outside
{\mathcal{S}}(f)
, and thus has a constant number of preimages (counted with multiplicities)
\mu(f)
that is independent of

(see e.g. §
1.2
). In the notation of Theorem
2.14
, this shows that
\mu(f)=I^{*}(f-w)
. Finally, Theorem
2.14
shows that for any

, it holds that

.
∎

3.
Preliminaries on tropical geometry

We state in this section some well-known facts about tropical geometry (see e.g.
[
3
,
24
,
21
]
, and the references therein). Some of the exposition and notations here are taken from
[
4
,
2
,
7
]
. We will introduce in §
3.1
some tropical objects such as tropical hypersurfaces, and illustrate their relation to their classical analogues. A key result in this section is Theorem
3.5
which relates the common intersection of tropical hypersurfaces to decompositions of the Newton polytopes and their faces.

3.1.
The base field

Let
\Bbbk
be an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero. A
locally convergent generalized Puiseux series
is a formal series of the form

c(t)=\underset{r\in R}{\sum}c_{r}t^{r},

where
R\subset\mathbb{R}
is a well-ordered set that is bounded from below, for which all
c_{r}\in\Bbbk
. We denote by
\mathbb{K}
the set of all locally convergent generalized Puiseux series. It is an algebraically-closed field of characteristic 0
[
22
]
. The field
\mathbb{K}
can be equipped with the function

\begin{array}[]{lccc}\displaystyle\val:&\mathbb{K}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}\\
&0&\longmapsto&-\infty\\
&\underset{r\in R}{\sum}c_{r}t^{r}\neq 0&\longmapsto&-\min_{R}\{r\ |\ c_{r}\neq 0\}.\end{array}

We will call
\val
the
valuation function
or simply,
valuation
. This extends to a map
\Val:\mathbb{K}^{n}\rightarrow(\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\})^{n}
by evaluating
\val
coordinate-wise, i.e.
\Val(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=(\val(z_{1}),\ldots,\val(z_{n}))
.

Remark 3.1
.

In the standard literature (c.f.
[
5
]
), the valuation of an element in
\mathbb{K}
is defined as
-\val
. Our choice for it to be the opposite was motivated by some of the conventions in tropical geometry.

Given a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{K}[z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}]
, its
tropical hypersurface
, denoted by
\mathbb{V}_{\!\mathbb{T}}(f)
, is the subset in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined as
\Val(\mathbb{V}^{*}_{\!\mathbb{K}}(f))
. Tropical hypersurfaces can be determined combinatorially.
Consider the function

(3.1)

\begin{array}[]{lccc}\displaystyle\nu_{f}:&\mathbb{Z}^{n}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}\\
&a&\longmapsto&\begin{cases}\val(c_{s}),&\text{if $s\in S$,}\\
-\infty,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}\\
\end{array}

Its
Legendre transform
is a piecewise-linear convex function
\mathcal{L}(\nu_{f}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}

x\mapsto\max_{s\in S}\{\langle x,s\rangle+\nu_{f}(s)\},

where
\langle\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \rangle:\penalty\ \mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the standard Eucledian product. The set of points
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
at which
\mathcal{L}(\nu_{f})
is not differentiable is called the
corner locus
of
\mathcal{L}(\nu_{f})
. We have the famous fundamental theorem of tropical geometry
[
6
]
,
[
21
, Theorem 3.13]
that was first announced by Kapranov.

Theorem 3.2

(Kapranov)
.

The tropical hypersurface
\mathbb{V}_{\!\mathbb{T}}(f)
of a polynomial
f
defined over
\mathbb{K}
is the corner locus of its Legendre transform
\mathcal{L}(\nu_{f})
.

3.2.
Subdivisions from tropical polynomials

For any two values
a,b\in\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
, their
tropical summation

a\oplus b
is defined as their maximum
\max(a,b)
, and their
tropical multiplication

a\otimes b
is their usual sum
a+b
. This gives rise to a
tropical semi-field

(\mathbb{T},\oplus,\otimes)
, where
\max(a,-\infty)=a
, and
-\infty+a=-\infty
. A
tropical polynomial

F
is defined over the semifield
(\mathbb{T},\oplus,\otimes)
, which gives rise to a function

\begin{array}[]{lccc}F:&\mathbb{T}^{n}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{T}\\
&x&\longmapsto&\displaystyle\max_{s\in S}\{\langle x,s\rangle+\gamma_{s}\},\\
\end{array}

where
S
is a finite set containing all
s\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
for which
\gamma_{s}\in\mathbb{R}
. The set
S
is called the
support
of the tropical polynomial
F
, and the linear terms appearing in
F
are called the
tropical monomials
.
The
tropicalization
of the polynomial
f
above is the tropical polynomial

f^{\trop}(x):=\max_{s\in S}\{\langle x,s\rangle+\val(c_{s})\}.

This coincides with the piecewise-linear convex function
\mathcal{L}(\nu_{f})
defined above, and thus Theorem
3.2
asserts that
\mathbb{V}_{\!\mathbb{T}}(f)
is the corner locus of
f^{\trop}
. Conversely, the corner locus of any tropical polynomial is a tropical hypersurface.

3.2.1.

All polytopes in this paper are assumed to be convex.

Definition 3.3

Let

∎
