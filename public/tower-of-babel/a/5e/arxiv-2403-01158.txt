A Bayesian Committee Machine Potential for Oxygen-containing Organic Compounds
Seungwon Kim, Changmo David Yang, Soohaeng Yoo Willow, Chang Woo Myung

Abstract
Organic compounds containing oxygen atoms are used as an energy source for all living organisms, and they also form proteins by combining them with other atoms. The importance of understanding protein-protein interactions (PPI) and making prediction model with protein and organic compounds is the challenges associated with quantifying their binding affinity. Here, we introduce the active Bayesian Committee Machine (BCM) potential to handle a wide array of 8 groups of CHO to predict oxygen-containing organic compounds. The BCM potential leverages the committee approach to bypass the poor scaling of kernel regressors when dealing with large datasets. Its committee-based structure allows for easy and cost-effective expansion, maintaining both transferrability and scalability. Demonstrating its robustness, the BCM accurately models challenging examples like the protein-molecule interactions. Our systematic benchmarking positions the sparse BCM potential as a promising candidate in the quest for a universal ab initio machine learning potential.

III
Results

The expert Sparse Gaussian Process Regression (SGPR) model for each molecular category was set with 300K and 3 ps, and instantaneously generated through short Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations of relevant molecules. For instance, the training for each group potential is illustrated in FIG. 2. The model trained after MD simulations for each molecular group is then used as the initial model for MD simulations of the subsequent molecule. In this manner, forces for molecules with more atoms are inferred using data from smaller-sized molecules, reducing the need for ab initio calculations for on-the-fly training. Cumulative ab initio samples consist of 420, 404, 363, 355, 387, 274, 299, and 200 for alcohol, aldehyde, carboxylate, ester, ether, sugar, lactone, and olide groups, respectively. Similar MD simulations are repeated to generate an independent test set without immediate training. Performance metrics for each expert SGPR ML model on the trained training set are presented in Table 1, using Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and
R^{2}
.

Figure 3:
Overall test mean absolute errors (MAEs) across different groups of ML potential models, sparse local experts (SLE) and robust Bayesian committee machine (RBCM). MAEs of test sets for energy (meV/atom) and forces(meV/Å) are calculated for 8 groups of training sets.

For SGPR models of alcohol, aldehyde, carboxylate, ester, ether, sugar, lactone, and alkylide groups, a Bayesian Committee Machine (BCM) method is employed to combine them into a single universal model for the smooth estimation of CHO with an arbitrary number of double bonds. The model is reconstructed using both sampled ab initio data and derived descriptors during the training process of ML potential experts for the 8 groups. To assess the efficiency of estimating the values correctly, a sample from the training set is selected, and the estimation efficiency based on the presence or absence of the potential for the group to which the sample belongs is investigated through individual ab initio MD simulations. The results indicate that when the potential for the sample is present in all groups, it requires less than 12 times the energy compared to when it is absent (Table 1, Table S1).

Regarding the combination of 8 groups into a single universal model, consisting of sets of C, H, O atoms, the estimation efficiency for randomly selected molecules not included in the training sets of each group is examined under various conditions. Four types of molecules, Methyl Palmitate (Ester group) with linear carbon single bonds, Cis-9-octadecenal (aldehyde group) with one carbon double bond, cyclonerodiol (alcohol group) with both carbon double bonds and a cyclostructure, and Grandiflorenic acid (Carboxylate group) with complex features and bonds, are chosen. The results show that for the estimation of methyl palmitate, the presence of the ester group requires 3.83 times less energy compared to its absence, for cis-9-octadecenal, it is 4.45 times less, for cyclonerodiol, it is 3.30 times less, and for grandiflorenic acid, it is less than 5.48 times(Table S2). Interestingly, catechin tetramethylether requires less than 1.14 times the energy(Table S2). In both conditions, the percentage value of 1-R2 of the energy estimation is above 99.9 percentile, indicating the ability to estimate energy even in the absence of ML-IAP in that molecular group, relying on information learned from other ML-IAPs.

To confirm whether the 8 ML-IAPs have become a single universal model through BCM, energy estimation and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) for a molecule with a complex structure containing polycycles and double bonds, such as grandiflorenic acid, are performed by removing one ML-IAP at a time from BCM, starting with the one with the lowest energy estimation and RMSE. As a result, Carboxylate, corresponding to the group of the molecule, was the ML-IAP that most accurately predicted energy and force, while the Lactone group was the least accurate. Additionally, starting with the 8 ML-IAPs and sequentially removing the ML-IAP that showed the lowest RMSE, the movement of weights confirmed the energy estimation for each molecule.

IV
Discussion

The CHO molecule exhibits an extremely diverse range of structures, with over 112 million predicted molecules even when considering only 13 carbon atoms
Ruddigkeit
et al.
(
2012
)
. To address this diversity, molecules consisting solely of CHO were organized using internationally agreed-upon IUPAC nomenclature.

The ChEMBL chemical compounds database, with around 2.4 million registered molecules, has less than 5 percent of the data registered in the PubChem database. However, it demonstrates relatively faster data processing speed compared to PubChem. This facilitated the exploration of primary molecular information for the selection of molecules containing an arbitrary number of double bonds, cyclo-structures, aromatic rings, and aromatic polycycles, essential for creating a training set for on-the-fly MLMD execution.

In this study, molecules containing an arbitrary number of double bonds, cyclo-structures, aromatic rings, and aromatic polycycles within each group were selected and added to the training set. The analysis of structural energy, including structural energy analysis of triple bonds between C and C, and shared bonds between molecules, will be considered in the near future. Handling more complex structures requires further efforts to train ML-IAPs and flexibility to expand the current model.

Additionally, the creation of a system to acquire information, including grid shapes and ion positions for C, H, O molecules, using an automated method or building systems based on canonical smiles and unique atomic information when data is unavailable, poses a significant challenge and requires additional research. These efforts aim to establish an efficient dataset for improved molecular dynamics simulations of CHO molecules.

Using the real-time adaptive sampling and SGPR algorithm, training results for 8 groups yielded ML-IAPs consisting of fewer than 500 atoms each. Particularly, the Bayesian Committee Machine (BCM) method was employed to estimate values for previously trained molecular structures and untrained molecular structures. While BCM considers the weight ratio of ML-IAPs based on the structure of the molecule, providing an advantage in energy estimation, it may occasionally offer slightly lower estimates than well-trained single ML-IAP results. The investigation of RMSE for single-molecule energy estimation using integrated ML-IAPs for each group revealed an RMSE of approximately 5.76 meV/Å for untrained molecules, while BCM with 8 grouped ML-IAPs showed approximately 25.12 meV/Å (Table S4). Both sides showed 1-R square percentile values above 99.999 and 99.9 percentile, respectively.

The difference in 1-R square percentile values between single ML-IAPs and BCM-based ML-IAPs paradoxically indicates the effective operation of ML-IAPs through the BCM method. Even for molecules not trained in any group, accuracy is determined based on the specificity of the structure, resulting in higher accuracy for well-trained ML-IAPs in the same group and higher weighting. Therefore, even in situations where ML-IAPs for the desired molecule group are not adequately trained, a module capable of estimating energy with over 99.9 percentile accuracy can be constructed based on the learning of the training set (Table S5, S6). This holds promise for extending the study to more diverse molecular binding methods and molecules composed of multiple atoms beyond CHO. Further research is planned to investigate the criteria for weight due to Covalent loss when calculating single ML-IAPs through the BCM method.

The combination of SGPR algorithm and BCM method demonstrates a fast and robust approach for on-the-fly machine learning of potential energy surfaces. This combination, coupled with an efficient adaptive sampling algorithm, can generate models consisting of groups of ML-IAPs with hundreds of molecules in the training dataset. Furthermore, the expert model approach provides a modular approach for complex polymer compounds, ultimately leading to the creation of a single universal model.
