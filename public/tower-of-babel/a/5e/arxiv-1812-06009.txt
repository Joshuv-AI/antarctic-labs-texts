Solvability = Typability + Inhabitation
Antonio Bucciarelli \rsuper a, Delia Kesner \rsuper b, Simona Ronchi Della Rocca \rsuper c

Abstract
. We extend the classical notion of solvability to a λ \lambda -calculus equipped with pattern matching. We prove that solvability can be characterized by means of typability and inhabitation in an intersection type system 𝒫 {\mathcal{P}} based on non-idempotent types. We show first that the system 𝒫 {\mathcal{P}} characterizes the set of terms having canonical form, i.e. that a term is typable if and only if it reduces to a canonical form. But the set of solvable terms is properly contained in the set of canonical forms. Thus, typability alone is not sufficient to characterize solvability, in contrast to the case for the λ \lambda -calculus. We then prove that typability, together with inhabitation, provides a full characterization of solvability, in the sense that a term is solvable if and only if it is typable and the types of all its arguments are inhabited. We complete the picture by providing an algorithm for the inhabitation problem of 𝒫 {\mathcal{P}} .

Introduction

In these last years there has been a growing interest in
pattern

\lambda
-calculi

[
25
,
20
,
13
,
21
,
19
,
24
]

which are used to model the pattern-matching primitives of functional
programming languages (
e.g.
OCAML, ML, Haskell) and proof assistants
(
e.g.
Coq, Isabelle). These calculi are extensions of the
\lambda
-calculus:
abstractions are written as
\lambda{\tt p}.{\tt t}
, where
{\tt p}
is a
pattern
specifying the expected structure of the argument.
In this paper we restrict our
attention to
pair
patterns, which are expressive enough to
illustrate the challenging notion of solvability in the
framework of pattern
\lambda
-calculi.

We define a calculus with
explicit pattern-matching
called
{\Lambda_{\tt p}}
. The use of
explicit pattern-matching becomes very appropriate to implement
different
evaluation strategies
, thus giving rise to different

programming languages
with
pattern-matching
[
13
,
14
,
3
]
. In all of them, an
application
(\lambda{\tt p}.{\tt t}){\tt u}
reduces to
{\tt t}[{\tt p}/{\tt u}]
, where the
constructor
[{\tt p}/{\tt u}]
is an explicit matching, defined by means of
suitable reduction rules, which are used to decide if the argument

{\tt u}
matches the pattern
{\tt p}
. If the matching is possible, the
evaluation proceeds by computing a substitution which is applied to
the body
{\tt t}
. Otherwise, two cases may arise: either a successful
matching is not possible at all, and then the term
{\tt t}[{\tt p}/{\tt u}]

reduces to a
failure
, denoted by the constant
{\tt fail}
, or
pattern matching could potentially become possible after the
application of some pertinent substitution to the argument
{\tt u}
, in
which case the reduction is simply
blocked
. For example,
reducing
(\lambda\langle{\tt z}_{1},{\tt z}_{2}\rangle.{\tt z}_{1})(\lambda{\tt y}.{\tt y})
leads to a failure,
while reducing
(\lambda\langle{\tt z}_{1},{\tt z}_{2}\rangle.{\tt z}_{1}){\tt y}
leads to a blocking
situation.

We aim to study
solvability
in the

{\Lambda_{\tt p}}
-calculus. Let us first recall this notion
in the framework of the
\lambda
-calculus: a closed
(
i.e.
, without free variables)
\lambda
-term
{\tt t}
is solvable if there
is
n\geq 0
and there are terms
{\tt u}_{1},...,{\tt u}_{n}
such that

{\tt t}{\tt u}_{1}...{\tt u}_{n}
reduces to the identity function. Closed
solvable terms represent meaningful programs: if
{\tt t}
is closed and
solvable, then
{\tt t}
can produce any desired result when applied to a
suitable sequence of arguments. The relation between solvability and
meaningfulness is also evident in the semantics: it is sound to equate
all unsolvable terms, as in Scott’s original model

D_{\infty}

[
26
]
. This notion can be easily extended to open
terms, through the notion of
head context
, which does the job of
both closing the term and then applying it to an appropriate sequence
of arguments. Thus a
\lambda
-term
{\tt t}
is solvable if there is a
head context
{\tt H}
such that, when
{\tt H}
is filled by

{\tt t}
, then
{\tt H}{[}{\tt t}{]}
is closed and reduces to the
identity function.

In order to extend the notion of solvability to the

{\Lambda_{\tt p}}
-calculus, it is clear that pairs have to be taken into account.
A relevant question is whether a pair should be considered as
meaningful. At least two choices are possible: a
lazy
semantics
considering any pair to be meaningful, or a
strict
one requiring
both of its components to be meaningful. We chose a lazy approach, in
fact in the operational semantics of
{\Lambda_{\tt p}}
the constant
{\tt fail}
is
different from
\langle{\tt fail},{\tt fail}\rangle
: if a term reduces to
{\tt fail}
we
do not have any information about its result, but if it reduces to

\langle{\tt fail},{\tt fail}\rangle
we know at least that it represents a pair. In
fact, being a pair is already an observable property, which in
particular is sufficient to unblock an explicit matching,
independently from the solvability of
its components. As a consequence, a term
{\tt t}
is
defined
to be

solvable
iff there exists a head context

{\tt H}
such that
{\tt H}{[}{\tt t}{]}
is closed and reduces
to a pair. Thus for example, the term
\langle{\tt t},{\tt t}\rangle
is always
solvable, also when
{\tt t}
is not
solvable. Our notion of solvability turns out to be conservative with respect
to the same notion for the
\lambda
-calculus (see
Theorem
5
).

In this paper we characterize solvability for
the
{\Lambda_{\tt p}}
-calculus through two different and complementary notions
related to a type assignment system with non-idempotent intersection
types, called
{\mathcal{P}}
. The first one is
typability
, that gives the
possibility to construct a typing derivation for a given term, and the
second one is
inhabitation
, which gives the possibility to
construct a term from a given typing. More precisely, we first supply
a notion of
canonical form
such that reducing a term to some
canonical form is a
necessary
but
not a sufficient

condition for being solvable. In fact, canonical
forms may contain blocking explicit matchings, so that we need to
guess whether or not there exists a substitution being able to
simultaneously
unblock all these blocked forms. Our type system

{\mathcal{P}}

characterizes
canonical forms: a term
{\tt t}
has a canonical
form if and only if it is typable in system
{\mathcal{P}}

(Theorem
3.2
). Types are of the shape

{\tt A}_{1}\to{\tt A}_{2}\to...\to{\tt A}_{n}\to\sigma
, for
n\geq 0
, where

{\tt A}_{i}
are multisets of types and
\sigma
is a type. The use of
multisets to represent the non-idempotent intersection is standard,
namely
[\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{m}]
is just a notation for

\sigma_{1}\cap...\cap\sigma_{m}
. By using the type system
{\mathcal{P}}
we can
supply the following
characterization
of
solvability (Theorem
5
): a closed term
{\tt t}
in the

{\Lambda_{\tt p}}
-calculus is solvable if and only if
{\tt t}
is
typable in system
{\mathcal{P}}
, let say with a type of the shape
{\tt A}_{1}\to{\tt A}_{2}\to...\to{\tt A}_{n}\to\sigma
(where
\sigma
is a type derivable for a pair),
and for all
1\leq i\leq n
there is a term
{\tt t}_{i}
inhabiting the
type
{\tt A}_{i}
. In fact, if
{\tt u}_{i}
inhabits the type
{\tt A}_{i}
, then

{\tt t}{\tt u}_{1}...{\tt u}_{n}
, resulting from plugging
{\tt t}
into the head
context
\Box{\tt u}_{1}...{\tt u}_{n}
, reduces to a pair. The extension of
this notion to open terms is obtained by suitably adapting the notion
of head context of the
\lambda
-calculus to our pattern calculus.

The property of being solvable in our calculus is clearly
undecidable. More precisely, the property of having a canonical
form is undecidable, since
{\Lambda_{\tt p}}
extends the
\lambda
-calculus, the

\lambda
-terms having a
{\Lambda_{\tt p}}
-canonical form are exactly the solvable ones,
and solvability of
\lambda
-terms is an undecidable property. But
our characterization of solvability through the inhabitation property
of
{\mathcal{P}}
does not add a further level of undecidability: in fact we
prove that inhabitation for system
{\mathcal{P}}
is
decidable
, by
designing a sound and complete inhabitation algorithm for it. The
inhabitation algorithm presented here is a non trivial extension of
the one given in
[
10
,
11
]

for the
\lambda
-calculus, the difficulty of the extension being due to
the explicit pattern matching.

Relation with
\lambda
-calculus

Let us
recall the existing characterizations of solvability for the
\lambda
-calculus:

(1):

{\tt H}{[}{\tt t}{]}
reduces to the identity for an appropriate head context
{\tt H}
;

(2):

{\tt t}
has a head normal form;

(3):

{\tt t}
can be typed in a
suitable intersection type system.

Statement
(1)
is the definition of solvability, Statement
(2)
(resp.
(3)
) is known as the
syntactical
(resp.
logical
) characterization of solvability. The syntactical
characterization,
i.e.

(2)

\Leftrightarrow

(1)
has been
proved in an untyped setting using the standardization theorem
(see
[
6
]
). The logical characterization,
i.e.

(3)

\Leftrightarrow

(1)
, uses the syntactical one: it is
performed by building an intersection type assignment system
characterizing terms having head normal form (see for
example
[
16
]
). Then the implication
(3)

\Rightarrow

(2)
corresponds to the soundness of the
type system (proved by means of a subject reduction property), while

(2)

\Rightarrow

(3)
states its completeness (proved by
subject expansion).

Traditional systems in the literature characterizing solvability for

\lambda
-calculus are for example
[
7
,
22
]
, where
intersection is
idempotent
. Exactly the same
results hold for
non-idempotent
intersection types, for example
for the type system
[
15
,
10
]
, which is a
restriction of
{\mathcal{P}}
to
\lambda
-terms.

How does the particular case of the
\lambda
-calculus fit in the
“solvability = typability + inhabitation” frame? We address this
issue in the following digression. Let
\iota
be a type which is
peculiar to some subset of “solvable” terms, in the sense that any
closed term of type
\iota
reduces to a solvable term in that set (in
the present work, such a subset contains all the pairs, in the case of
the
\lambda
-calculus, it is
the singleton containing only the identity). Then a type
\tau
of
the form
{\tt A}_{1}\rightarrow\ldots\rightarrow{\tt A}_{n}\rightarrow\iota
may be viewed as a
certificate, establishing that, by applying a closed term
{\tt t}:\tau
to
a sequence of closed arguments
{\tt u}_{i}:{\tt A}_{i}
, one gets a term that
reduces to a term in such a subset. This is summarized by the slogan
“solvability = typability + inhabitation”.
In the case of the call-by-name
\lambda
-calculus, however,
typability alone already guarantees
solvability. The mismatch is only apparent,
though: any closed, head normal term of the
\lambda
-calculus,

i.e.
any term of the shape
\lambda{\tt x}_{1}\ldots{\tt x}_{n}.{\tt x}_{j}{\tt t}_{1}\ldots{\tt t}_{m}\ (n,m\geq 0)
, may be assigned a type of the form

{\tt A}_{1}\rightarrow\ldots\rightarrow{\tt A}_{n}\rightarrow\iota

where all the
{\tt A}_{i}
’s are empty except the one corresponding to the
head variable
{\tt x}_{j}
, which is of the shape

[\underbrace{[\,]\rightarrow\ldots\rightarrow[\,]}_{m}\rightarrow\iota]
. The problems of finding inhabitants of the empty type
and of
\underbrace{[\,]\rightarrow\ldots\rightarrow[\,]}_{m}\rightarrow\iota
are
both trivial. Hence, “solvability = typability + inhabitation”
does hold for the
\lambda
-calculus, too, but the “inhabitation” part
is trivial in that particular case. This is due, of course, to the
fact that the head normalizable terms of the
\lambda
-calculus coincide
with both the solvable terms and the typable ones.

But in other settings, a term may be both typable and non
solvable, the types of (some of) its arguments
being non-inhabited (Theorem
5
).

{\cal N}

.

A term

The Type System
{\mathcal{P}}

In this section we present a type system for the
{\Lambda_{\tt p}}
-calculus, and
we show that it characterizes terms having canonical form,
i.e.
that a
term
{\tt t}
is typable if and only if
{\tt t}
has canonical form.
The
set
{\mathcal{T}}
of types
is generated by the following grammar:

\hfill\begin{array}[]{llll}\mbox{(Types)}&\sigma,\tau&::=&\alpha\mid\pi\mid{\tt A}\rightarrow\sigma\\
\mbox{(Product Types)}&\pi&::=&\times({\tt A},{\tt B})\\
\mbox{(Multiset Types)}&{\tt A},{\tt B}&::=&[\sigma_{k}]_{k\in K}\\
\end{array}\hfill

where
\alpha
ranges over a countable set of constants,
K

is a (possibly empty) finite set of indices, and a multiset is an
unordered list of (not necessarily different) elements. The arrow type
constructor
\rightarrow
is right associative.

Typing environments
, written
\Gamma,\Delta,\Lambda
, are functions from variables to multiset types, assigning the
empty multiset to almost all the variables. The
domain
of
\Gamma
,
written
{\tt dom}(\Gamma)
, is the set of variables whose image is different
from
[\,]
. We may write
\Gamma\#\Delta
iff

{\tt dom}(\Gamma)\cap{\tt dom}(\Delta)=\emptyset
.

Notation
\thethm
.

Sometimes we will use symbols
\mu,\nu
to range over the union of
types and multiset types. We abbreviate by the constant
o
the
product type
\times([\,],[\,])
.
We write
\sqcup
to denote multiset union and
\sqsubseteq
multiset
inclusion; these operations take multiplicities into account.
Moreover, abusing the notation we will use
\in
to denote both set
and multiset membership.

Given typing environments
\{\Gamma_{i}\}_{{i\in I}}
, we write

+_{{i\in I}}\Gamma_{i}
for the environment which maps
{\tt x}
to

\sqcup_{{i\in I}}\Gamma_{i}({\tt x})

domain. Note that

{\tt t}

{\tt H}
is always typed.
∎

3.1.
On the Typing of Patterns

The system
{\mathcal{P}}
features two kinds of typings: those of the form

\Gamma\vdash{\tt t}:\mu
, for terms, and those of the form

\Gamma\Vdash{\tt p}:{\tt A}
, for patterns. These are, of course, fundamentally
dissymetric notions:
\vdash
is undecidable and non deterministic (a
given term may have several types in a given environment), whereas

\Vdash
is decidable and deterministic. As a matter of fact the
unique
type
{\tt A}
such that

\Gamma\Vdash{\tt p}:{\tt A}
could have been denoted by
\Gamma({\tt p})
, as
we do for
variables. However, we decided to keep the typing judgements
\Gamma\Vdash{\tt p}:{\tt A}
since they allow for a clearer formulation of
the typing rules of
{\mathcal{P}}
.
Some preliminary definitions are given below
to prove the uniqueness of the typing of patterns.

Given two patterns
{\tt p}
and
{\tt q}
, we say that
{\tt p}

occurs
in
{\tt q}
if

•

either
{\tt p}={\tt q}

•

or

{\tt q}=\langle{\tt q}_{1},{\tt q}_{2}\rangle
and either
{\tt p}
occurs in
{\tt q}_{1}
or
{\tt p}
occurs occurs in
{\tt q}_{2}
.

Remark that, by linearity of patterns, at most one of the conditions in the second item above may hold.

If
{\tt p}
occurs in
{\tt q}
, the multiset type
{\tt A}_{\tt p}^{\tt q}
is defined as follows:

•

{\tt A}_{\tt p}^{\tt p}={\tt A}

•

[\times({\tt A},{\tt B})]_{\tt p}^{\langle{\tt q}_{1},{\tt q}_{2}\rangle}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\tt A}_{\tt p}^{{\tt q}_{1}}&\mbox{if ${\tt p}$ occurs in ${\tt q}_{1}$}\\
{\tt B}_{\tt p}^{{\tt q}_{2}}&\mbox{if ${\tt p}$ occurs in ${\tt q}_{2}$}\\
\end{array}\right.

•

{\tt A}_{\tt p}^{\langle{\tt q}_{1},{\tt q}_{2}\rangle}
is undefined if
{\tt A}
is not of the shape
[\times({\tt C},{\tt B})]
, for some
{\tt C},{\tt B}
.

Typings of patterns can be characterized as follows:

Lemma
\thethm
.

For every environment
\Gamma
and pattern
{\tt p}
,
\Gamma\Vdash{\tt p}:{\tt A}
if and only if
{\tt dom}(\Gamma)\subseteq{\tt fv}({\tt p})
and for all
{\tt q}
occurring in
{\tt p}
,
{\tt A}^{\tt p}_{\tt q}
is defined and
\Gamma|_{\tt q}\Vdash{\tt q}:{\tt A}^{\tt p}_{\tt q}
.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
):
If
\Gamma\Vdash{\tt p}:{\tt A}
, then
{\tt dom}(\Gamma)\subseteq{\tt fv}({\tt p})
by Lemma
3
. The proof is
by induction on
{\tt p}
. If
{\tt p}={\tt x}
then either
{\tt x}\not\in{\tt dom}(\Gamma)
and

{\tt A}=[\,]
, or
\Gamma({\tt x})={\tt B}\not=[\,]
and
{\tt A}={\tt B}^{\tt x}_{\tt x}={\tt B}
.
If the considered pattern is
\langle{\tt p},{\tt q}\rangle
, then the last rule of its type derivation is

\displaystyle\penalty\Gamma\Vdash{\tt p}:{\tt B}\hskip 11.38092pt\Delta\Vdash{\tt q}:{\tt C}\hskip 11.38092pt{\tt p}\#{\tt q}

\displaystyle\Gamma+\Delta\Vdash\langle{\tt p},{\tt q}\rangle:[\times({\tt B},{\tt C})]

where
{\tt A}=[\times({\tt B},{\tt C})]
.
By the induction hypothesis
{\tt dom}(\Gamma)\subseteq{\tt fv}({\tt p})
,
{\tt dom}(\Delta)\subseteq{\tt fv}({\tt q})
and
for every
{\tt p}^{\prime},{\tt q}^{\prime}
occurring respectively in
{\tt p}
and
{\tt q}
,
\Gamma|_{{\tt p}^{\prime}}\Vdash{\tt p}^{\prime}:{\tt B}_{{\tt p}^{\prime}}^{{\tt p}}
and

\Delta|_{{\tt q}^{\prime}}\Vdash{\tt q}^{\prime}:{\tt B}_{{\tt q}^{\prime}}^{{\tt q}}
.
Note that every pattern occurring in
\langle{\tt p},{\tt q}\rangle
is either
\langle{\tt p},{\tt q}\rangle
itself or it occurs
in exactly one of the two components of the pair. In the first case the proof is trivial.
In the second one, since the domains of
\Gamma
and
\Delta
are
disjoint, by linearity of
\langle{\tt p},{\tt q}\rangle
,
\Gamma|_{{\tt p}^{\prime}}+\Delta|_{{\tt p}^{\prime}}=(\Gamma+\Delta)|_{{\tt p}^{\prime}}

and
\Gamma|_{{\tt q}^{\prime}}+\Delta|_{{\tt q}^{\prime}}=(\Gamma+\Delta)|_{{\tt q}^{\prime}}
,
and the proof follows by induction.

(
\Leftarrow
): The proof is again by induction on
{\tt p}
.
∎

Example
\thethm
.

Let
{\tt p}=\langle\langle{\tt x},{\tt y}\rangle,{\tt w}\rangle
and

\Gamma={\tt x}:[\alpha,\beta],{\tt y}:[\gamma]
. The (sub)patterns
occurring in
{\tt p}
are
{\tt x}
,
{\tt y}
,
{\tt w}
,
\langle{\tt x},{\tt y}\rangle
and
{\tt p}
. In the
typing
environment
\Gamma
restricted to its free variables, each (sub)pattern can be
typed by a unique multiset:

\begin{array}[]{lll@{\hspace{1.5cm}}lll}\Gamma|_{\tt x}&=&{\tt x}:[\alpha,\beta]\Vdash{\tt x}:[\alpha,\beta]\hfil\hskip 42.67912pt&\Gamma|_{\langle{\tt x},{\tt y}\rangle}&=&\Gamma\Vdash{\langle{\tt x},{\tt y}\rangle}:[\times([\alpha,\beta],[\gamma])]\\
\Gamma|_{\tt y}&=&{\tt y}:[\gamma]\Vdash{\tt y}:[\gamma]\hfil\hskip 42.67912pt&\Gamma|_{\tt p}&=&\Gamma\Vdash{\tt p}:[\times([\times([\alpha,\beta],[\gamma])],[\,])]\\
\Gamma|_{\tt w}&=&\emptyset\Vdash{\tt w}:[\,]\hfil\hskip 42.67912pt\\
\end{array}

If we denote by
{\tt A}
the type
[\times([\times([\alpha,\beta],[\gamma])],[\,])]
, then it is easy to verify that:

\begin{array}[]{lll@{\hspace{1.5cm}}lll}{\tt A}^{\tt p}_{\tt x}&=&[\alpha,\beta]\hfil\hskip 42.67912pt&{\tt A}^{{\tt p}}_{\langle{\tt x},{\tt y}\rangle}&=&[\times([\alpha,\beta],[\gamma])]\\
{\tt A}^{\tt p}_{\tt y}&=&[\gamma]\hfil\hskip 42.67912pt&{\tt A}^{\tt p}_{\tt p}&=&{\tt A}\\
{\tt A}^{\tt p}_{\tt w}&=&[\,]\hfil\hskip 42.67912pt\\
\end{array}

The following corollary follows immediately.

Corollary
\thethm
.

For every pattern
{\tt p}
and every environment
\Gamma
such that
{\tt dom}(\Gamma)\subseteq{\tt fv}({\tt p})
, there exists a unique multiset
{\tt A}
such that
\Gamma\Vdash{\tt p}:{\tt A}
.
Moreover if
{\tt p}=\langle{\tt p}_{1},{\tt p}_{2}\rangle
then
{\tt A}=[\times({\tt B},{\tt C})]
, for some
{\tt B},{\tt C}
.

3.2.
Main Properties of system
{\mathcal{P}}

We are going to define the notion of
typed occurrences
of a
typing derivation, which plays an essential role in the rest of this
paper: indeed, thanks to the use of non-idempotent intersection types,
a combinatorial argument based on a measure on typing derivations
(
cf.
Lemma
3.2
(
1
)), allows to prove
the termination of reduction of redexes occurring in typed occurrences

Given a typing derivation

,
the occurrence of
{\tt u}

in the hole of
{\tt C}
is a
typed occurrence of
\Pi

if and only if
{\tt u}
is the
subject of a meaningful subderivation of
\Pi
. More precisely:

Definition
\thethm
.

Given a type derivation
\Pi
, the set of

of
\Pi

,

as follows.

If
\Pi

\Pi

\langle{\tt u},{\tt v}\rangle

(

.

•

If
\Pi
ends with
({\tt abs})

with subject
\lambda{\tt p}.{\tt u}
and
premise
\Pi^{\prime}
then

{\tt toc}(\Pi):=\{\Box\}\cup\{\lambda{\tt p}.{\tt C}\mid{\tt C}\in{\tt toc}(\Pi^{\prime})\}
.

•

If
\Pi

({\tt app})

with subject
{\tt t}{\tt u}
and
premises
\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}

with subjects
{\tt t}

and
{\tt u}

{\tt toc}(\Pi):=\{\Box\}\cup\{{\tt C}{\tt u}\mid{\tt C}\in{\tt toc}(\Pi_{1})\}\cup\{{\tt t}{\tt C}\mid{\tt C}\in{\tt toc}(\Pi_{2})\}
.

•

If
\Pi

(\mathtt{sub})

\Pi_{1}
and
\Pi_{2}

with subjects

and

•

If

, with premises

.

Example
\thethm
.

Given the following derivations

The type assignment system
{\mathcal{P}}
enjoys the fundamental properties of
subject reduction and subject expansion, based respectively on
substitution and anti-substitution properties whose proofs
can be found in
[
2
]
.

Lemma
\thethm

(
Substitution/Anti-Substitution Lemma).

(1)

If
\Pi_{\tt t}\triangleright\Gamma_{\tt t};{\tt x}:{\tt A}\vdash{\tt t}:\tau

and
\Pi_{\tt u}\triangleright\Gamma_{\tt u}\vdash{\tt u}:{\tt A}
, then
there exists
\Pi\triangleright\Gamma_{\tt t}+\Gamma_{\tt u}\triangleright{\tt t}\{{\tt x}/{\tt u}\}:\tau

such
that
{\tt meas}(\Pi)\leq{\tt meas}(\Pi_{\tt t})+{\tt meas}(\Pi_{\tt u})
.

(2)

If
\Pi\triangleright\Gamma\vdash{\tt t}\{{\tt x}/{\tt u}\}:\tau
, then there
exist derivations
\Pi_{t}
and
\Pi_{\tt u}
, environments

\Gamma_{\tt t},\Gamma_{\tt u}
and multiset
{\tt A}
such that

\Pi_{\tt t}\triangleright\Gamma_{\tt t};{\tt x}:{\tt A}\vdash{\tt t}:\tau
,

\Pi_{\tt u}\triangleright\Gamma_{\tt u}\vdash{\tt u}:{\tt A}
and
\Gamma=\Gamma_{\tt t}+\Gamma_{\tt u}
.

On the other hand, the measure of
any typing derivation is not increasing by reduction. Moreover, the
measure strictly decreases for the reduction steps that are

typed
. This property makes easier the proof of the “only
if” part of Theorem
3.2
.

Lemma
\thethm
.

(1)

(Weighted Subject Reduction)

If
\Pi\triangleright\Gamma\vdash{\tt t}:\tau
and
{\tt t}\rightarrow{\tt t}^{\prime}
, then
\Pi^{\prime}\triangleright\Gamma\vdash{\tt t}^{\prime}:\tau
and
{\tt meas}(\Pi^{\prime})\leq{\tt meas}(\Pi)
. Moreover, if the
reduced redex occurs in a typed occurrence of
\Pi
, then
{\tt meas}(\Pi^{\prime})<{\tt meas}(\Pi)
.

(2)

(Subject Expansion)

If
\Pi^{\prime}\triangleright\Gamma^{\prime}\vdash{\tt t}^{\prime}:\sigma
and
{\tt t}\rightarrow{\tt t}^{\prime}
, then
\Pi\triangleright\Gamma\vdash{\tt t}:\sigma
.

Proof.

Both proofs are by induction on the reduction relation

{\tt t}\rightarrow{\tt t}^{\prime}
. For the base cases (reduction at the
root position): the rules
({\tt dB}),({\tt subs}),({\tt match}_{s})
are treated
exactly as in
[
2
]
, and the rules

({\tt match}_{f}),({\tt app}_{f}),({\tt rem}_{f}),({\tt lem}_{f}),({\tt la}_{f}),({\tt abs}_{f})

do not apply since the term
{\tt t}
(resp.
{\tt t}^{\prime}
) would not be typable.
All the inductive cases are straightforward, and in particular,
when the reduction occurs in an untyped position, then
the measures of
\Pi
and
\Pi^{\prime}
are equal.
∎

Given
\Pi\triangleright\Gamma\vdash{\tt t}:\tau
, the term
{\tt t}
is said to be in

\Pi
-normal form
, also written
\Pi
-nf
, if for every
typed occurrence
{\tt C}\in{\tt toc}(\Pi)
such that
{\tt t}={\tt C}{[}{\tt u}{]}
, the subterm
{\tt u}
is not a redex.

We are now ready to provide the logical characterization of terms
having canonical form. This proof has been already given in
[
2
]
, based on the property that, if a term has a canonical
form, then there is a
head
reduction strategy reaching
this normal form. But in
order to make this paper self contained, we reformulate here the
proof, using (for the completeness proof) a more general approach,
namely that every reduction strategy choosing at least all the typed
redexes reaches a canonical form.

Theorem
\thethm

(Characterizing Canonicity)
.

A term
{\tt t}
is typable iff
{\tt t}
has a canonical form.

Proof.

•

(if) We reason by induction on the grammar defining the canonical forms.

We first consider
{\cal K}
-canonical forms, for which we prove a
stronger property, namely that for every
{\tt t}\in{\cal K}
, for
every
\sigma
there is an environment
\Gamma

such that
\Gamma\vdash{\tt t}:\sigma
. We reason by induction on the grammar defining
{\cal K}
.

If
{\tt t}={\tt x}
, the proof is straightforward. If
{\tt t}={\tt v}{\tt u}
, then by
the
i.h.
there is a typing derivation

\Gamma\vdash{\tt v}:[\,]\rightarrow\sigma
. Since to every term the
multiset
[\,]
can be assigned by rule (
\mathtt{many}
) with an empty
premise, the result follows by application of rule

({\tt app})
.

Let
{\tt t}={\tt u}[\langle{\tt p},{\tt q}\rangle/{\tt v}]

for every

{\tt A},{\tt B}
,

.
Let

,

.

(only if) Let

4.
Inhabitation for System
{\mathcal{P}}

Given
\mu
, a type or a multiset type, the inhabitation problem consists
in finding a closed term
{\tt t}
such that
\vdash{\tt t}:\mu
is derivable.
These notions will naturally be generalized later to non-closed terms.
Since system
{\mathcal{P}}
characterizes canonicity, it is natural to look for
inhabitants in
canonical form
. The next Lemma proves that the
problem can be simplified, namely that it is sufficient to look for
inhabitants in
pure canonical form

4.3.
Properties of the Inhabitation Algorithm

We prove several properties of the inhabitation algorithm,
namely, termination, soundness and completeness.

Termination

Being the inhabitation algorithm non
deterministic, proving its termination means to prove both that a
single run terminates and that every input generates a finite number
of runs. We will prove these two properties separately.

First, let us define the following
measure
on types and environments:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\#(\alpha)&:=&1\\
\#([\sigma_{i}]_{{i\in I}})&:=&1+_{{i\in I}}\#(\sigma_{i})\\
\#(\times({\tt A},{\tt B}))&:=&\#({\tt A})+\#({\tt B})+1\\
\#({\tt A}\to\sigma)&:=&\#({\tt A})+\#(\sigma)+1\\
\#(\Gamma)&:=&\sum_{{\tt x}\in{\tt dom}(\Gamma)}\#(\Gamma({\tt x}))\\
\end{array}

The measures are extended to predicates in the following way:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\#({\tt T}(\Gamma,\sigma))&:=&\#(\Gamma)+\#(\sigma)\\
\#({\tt H}_{{\tt a}}^{\Delta}(\Gamma,\sigma)\triangleright\tau)&:=&\#(\Gamma)+\#(\sigma)\\
\#({\tt M}(\Gamma,{\tt A}))&:=&\#(\Gamma)+\#({\tt A})\\
\#({\tt P}_{{\cal V}}({\tt A}))&:=&\#({\tt A})\\
\end{array}

Notice that
\#(\Gamma)\leq\#(\Gamma+\Delta)
, for
any
\Delta
. Also,
\#(\Gamma+\Delta)\leq\#(\Gamma)+\#(\Delta)
, thus
e.g.

\#({\tt x}:[\alpha]+{\tt x}:[\alpha])=\#({\tt x}:[\alpha,\alpha])=3\leq\#({\tt x}:[\alpha])+\#({\tt x}:[\alpha])=4
. Notice also that

\#(\Delta_{1})<\#(\Delta_{2})
does not imply

\#(\Delta_{1}+\Lambda)<\#(\Delta_{2}+\Lambda)
,
e.g.
when
\Delta_{1}={\tt x}:[\alpha]
,
\Delta_{2}={\tt y}:[\alpha,\alpha]

and
\Lambda={\tt y}:[\alpha]
. However, as a particular useful case,
if
\#(\Delta)+1<\#({\tt x}:{\tt A})
,
then
\#(\Delta+\Lambda)<\#({\tt x}:{\tt A}+\Lambda)
.
Indeed, if
{\tt x}\notin{\tt dom}(\Lambda)
,
then
\#(\Delta+\Lambda)\leq\#(\Delta)+\#(\Lambda)<\#({\tt x}:{\tt A})+\#(\Lambda)=\#({\tt x}:{\tt A}+\Lambda)
;
otherwise,
\#(\Delta+\Lambda)\leq\#(\Delta)+\#(\Lambda)<(\#({\tt x}:{\tt A})-1)+\#(\Lambda)=\#({\tt x}:{\tt A}+\Lambda)
.

The following property follows directly.

Property
\thethm
.

Let

.
Then
\#(\Gamma)\leq\#({\tt A})
.
Moreover,
{\tt p}=\langle{\tt p}_{1},{\tt p}_{2}\rangle

We can now prove:

Lemma
\thethm
.

Every run of the algorithm terminates.

Proof.

We associate a tree
{\cal T}
to each call of the algorithm, where the
nodes are labeled with elements in the set
\{{\tt T}(\_,\_),{\tt M}(\_,\_),{\tt H}_{\_}^{\_}(\_,\_)\triangleright\_,{\tt P}_{\_}(\_)\}

A node

is a son of

having
n

as premise. Thus, a run of the

, which turns to be

strictly decreases along all the branches of

and

•

Consider rule

5.
Characterizing Solvability

We are now able to state the main result of this paper,
i.e.
the
characterization of the solvability property for the pattern
calculus
{\Lambda_{\tt p}}
.

The logical characterization of canonical forms given in
Section
3
through the type assignment system
{\mathcal{P}}

is a first step in this direction. In fact, the system
{\mathcal{P}}
is
complete with respect to solvability, but it
is not sound, as shown in the next theorem.

Theorem
\thethm
.

The set of solvable terms is a proper subset of the set of terms having canonical forms.

Proof.

•

(Solvability implies canonicity) If
{\tt t}
is solvable, then
there is a head context
{\tt H}
such that
{\tt H}{[}{\tt t}{]}
is
closed and reduces to
\langle{\tt u},{\tt v}\rangle
, for some
{\tt u}
and
{\tt v}
.
Since all pairs are typable, the term
{\tt H}{[}{\tt t}{]}
is typable by
Lemma
3.2
(
2
), so that
{\tt t}
is typable
too by Lemma
3
. We conclude
that
{\tt t}
has canonical form by Theorem
3.2
.

•

(Canonicity does not imply solvability) Let

{\tt t}_{1}=\lambda{\tt x}.{\tt I}[\langle{\tt y},{\tt z}\rangle/{\tt x}][\langle{\tt y}^{\prime},{\tt z}^{\prime}\rangle/{\tt x}{\tt I}]
.
the term
{\tt t}_{1}

is canonical, hence typable by
Theorem
3.2
. However
{\tt t}_{1}
is not
solvable. In fact, it is easy to see that there is no term
{\tt u}

such that both
{\tt u}
and
{\tt u}{\tt I}
reduce to pairs. Indeed, let

{\tt u}\rightarrow^{*}\langle{\tt v}_{1},{\tt v}_{2}\rangle
; then

{\tt u}{\tt I}\rightarrow^{*}\langle{\tt v}_{1},{\tt v}_{2}\rangle{\tt I}
, which
will reduce to
{\tt fail}
. ∎

However, as explained in the introduction, we can use inhabitation of
system
{\mathcal{P}}
to completely characterize solvability. The following
lemma guarantees that the types reflect correctly the structure of the
data.

Lemma
\thethm
.

Let
{\tt t}
be a closed and typable term.

•

If
{\tt t}
has functional type, then
{\tt t}
reduces to an abstraction.

•

If
{\tt t}
has product type, then it reduces to a pair.

Proof.

Let
{\tt t}
be a closed and typable term. By Theorem
3.2

we know that

{\tt t}
reduces to a (closed) canonical form in
{\cal J}
. The proof is by induction on the
maximal length of such reduction sequences.
If
{\tt t}
is already a canonical form, we analyze all the cases.

•

If
{\tt t}
is a variable, then this gives a contradiction with
{\tt t}
closed.

•

If
{\tt t}
is an abstraction, then the property trivially holds.

•

If
{\tt t}
is a pair, then the property trivially holds.

•

If
{\tt t}
is an application, then
{\tt t}
necessarily
has a head (free) variable which
belongs to the set of free variables of
{\tt t}
, which leads to a
contradiction with
{\tt t}
closed.

•

If
{\tt t}={\tt u}[\langle{\tt p}_{1},{\tt p}_{2}\rangle/{\tt v}]
is closed,
then in particular

{\tt v}
is closed, which leads to a contradiction with

{\tt t}\in{\cal J}
implying
{\tt v}\in{\cal K}
. So this case is not possible.

Otherwise, there is a reduction sequence
{\tt t}\rightarrow{\tt t}^{\prime}\rightarrow^{*}{\tt u}
, where
{\tt u}
is in
{\cal J}
.
The term
{\tt t}^{\prime}
is also closed and typable
by Lemma
3.2
(
1
), then the
i.h.
gives the desired result for

{\tt t}^{\prime}

{\tt t}
.
∎

and

\emptyset\vdash{\tt v}_{j}:{\tt C}_{j}
(
1\leq i\leq k,1\leq j\leq m
). Let

{\tt H}=(\lambda{\tt x}_{k}\ldots((\lambda{\tt x}_{1}.\Box){\tt u}_{1})\ldots{\tt u}_{k}){\tt v}_{1}\ldots{\tt v}_{m}
be a head context. We have

{\tt H}{[}{\tt t}{]}
closed and
\emptyset\vdash{\tt H}{[}{\tt t}{]}:\sigma
, where

\sigma
is a product type. This in turn implies that
{\tt H}{[}{\tt t}{]}

reduces to a pair, by Lemma
5
. Then the term
{\tt t}

is solvable by definition. ∎

Our notion of solvability is conservative with respect to that of the
\lambda
-calculus.

Theorem
\thethm

(Conservativity)
.

A
\lambda
-term
{\tt t}
is solvable in the
\lambda
-calculus if and only if
{\tt t}

is solvable in the
{\Lambda_{\tt p}}
-calculus.

Proof.

•

(if) Let
{\tt t}
be a
\lambda
-term which is not solvable,
i.e.
which
does not have head normal-form. Then
{\tt t}
(seen as a term of our
calculus) has no canonical form, and thus
{\tt t}
is not typable by
Theorem
3.2
. It turns out that
{\tt t}

is not solvable in
{\Lambda_{\tt p}}
by Theorem
5
.

•

(only if) Let
{\tt t}
be a solvable
\lambda
-term so that there exist a
head context
{\tt H}
such that
{\tt H}{[}{\tt t}{]}
is closed and reduces to
{\tt I}
,
then it is easy to construct a head context
{\tt H}^{\prime}
such that

{\tt H}^{\prime}{[}{\tt t}{]}
reduces to a pair (just take
{\tt H}^{\prime}={\tt H}\ \langle{\tt t}_{1},{\tt t}_{2}\rangle
for some terms
{\tt t}_{1},{\tt t}_{2}
). ∎

6.
Conclusion and Further Work

We extend the classical notion of solvability, originally stated for
the
\lambda
-calculus, to a pair pattern calculus. We provide a logical
characterization of solvable terms by means of typability

and
inhabitation.

An interesting question concerns the consequences
of changing non-idempotent types to idempotent ones in our
typing system
{\mathcal{P}}
. Characterization
of solvability will still need the two
ingredients typability and inhabitation, however,
inhabitation will become undecidable, in contrast to our
decidable inhabitation problem for the non-idempotent
system
{\mathcal{P}}
. This is consistent with the fact that
the inhabitation problem for the
\lambda
-calculus
is undecidable for idempotent types
[
27
]
, but decidable for
the non-idempotent ones
[
10
]
.

Notice however that changing the meta-level substitution operator
to explicit substitutions would not change neither
the notion nor the characterization of solvability: all the
explicit substitutions are fully computed in normal forms.

Further work will be developed in different directions. As we already
discussed in Section
2
, different definitions of
solvability would be possible, as for example
in
[
17
]
. We explored the one based on a lazy
semantics, but it would be also interesting to obtain a full
characterization based on a strict semantics.

On the semantical side, it is well known that non-idempotent
intersection types can be used to supply a logical description of the
relational semantics of
\lambda
-calculus
[
15
,
23
]
. We
would like to start from our type assignment system for building a
denotational model of the pattern calculus. Last but not least, a
challenging question is related to the characterization of solvability
in a more general framework of pattern
\lambda
-calculi allowing the
patterns to be dynamic
[
19
]
.
