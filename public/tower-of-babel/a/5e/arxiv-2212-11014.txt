Rigidity of mapping class groups mod powers of twists
Giorgio Mangioni, Alessandro Sisto

Abstract
. We study quotients of mapping class groups of punctured spheres by suitable large powers of Dehn twists, showing an analogue of Ivanov’s theorem for the automorphisms of the corresponding quotients of curve graphs. Then we use this result to prove quasi-isometric rigidity of these quotients, answering a question of Behrstock, Hagen, Martin, and Sisto in the case of punctured spheres. Finally, we show that the automorphism groups of our quotients of mapping class groups are ”small”, as are their abstract commensurators. This is again an analogue of a theorem of Ivanov about the automorphism group of the mapping class group. In the process we develop techniques to extract combinatorial data from a quasi-isometry of a hierarchically hyperbolic space, and use them to give a different proof of a result of Bowditch about quasi-isometric rigidity of pants graphs of punctured spheres. Keywords : Mapping class group, quasi-isometric rigidity, Ivanov’s Theorem, Dehn twist quotients. MSC class: 20F65 (primary), 57K20 (secondary)



1.
Introduction

Dehn twist quotients

Given a finite-type surface
S
, let
MCG(S)
be its mapping class group. For every
K\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, let
DT_{K}
be the subgroup generated by the collection
\{T^{K}_{\alpha}\}
, where
\alpha
varies among all isotopy classes of essential simple closed curves on
S
, and
T_{\alpha}
denotes the Dehn Twist around
\alpha
. In this paper we study the properties of quotients of the form
MCG/DT_{K}
, where
K
is chosen to be “sufficiently large”.

There are plenty of reasons of interest in these quotients. Firstly, they can be regarded as ”Dehn filling quotients” of mapping class groups, as pointed out and explored in
[
DHS21
]
, where they are proven to be acylindrically hyperbolic. In fact, these and similar quotients are used in
[
BHMS24
]
(where they are proven to be hierarchically hyperbolic) to relate questions on finite quotients of mapping class groups to residual finiteness of certain hyperbolic groups; this should be compared with the use of Dehn fillings in the resolution of the virtual Haken conjecture
[
Ago13
]
.

Moreover, when the surface is closed and hyperbolic, these quotients are conjectured to be the outer automorphism group of the Burnside quotient
\Gamma/\Gamma^{n}
, where
\Gamma
is the fundamental group of the surface
[
CS21
]
. This fact would be the analogue of the celebrated Dehn-Nielsen-Baer theorem
[
Deh87
,
Nie27
]
.

These quotients of mapping class groups also appear naturally in a somewhat unrelated area of mathematics, namely the study of topological quantum field theories (see e.g.
[
Fun99
]
). As it turns out, if
G
is a compact Lie group, every homomorphism
MCG(S_{g})\to G
(with
g\geq 3
) factors through the quotient by large enough powers of Dehn twists
[
AS16
, Corollary 2.6]
, and this applies in particular to the so-called quantum representations of mapping class groups.

Yet another reason of interest in these quotients is simply that given a group
G
and a collection of elements
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
, it is natural to study the normal closure of the set, and the corresponding quotient. In the case of our quotients of mapping class groups, we are considering a collection of conjugacy representatives of Dehn twists, and we are ”stabilising” by taking powers.

Rigidity results

We have three main results, analogous to results for mapping class groups, that illustrate three different forms of rigidity of the groups we are considering. In all cases, we consider punctured spheres; we discuss below what would need to be done to cover surfaces with genus. We note that, up to commensurability, we could equivalently regard the quotient groups we consider as quotients of braid groups.

The first result we state is that our quotients are quasi-isometrically rigid, thus answering
[
BHMS24
, Question 3]
for the case of punctured spheres. We say that two groups
G
and
H
are
weakly commensurable
if there exist two finite normal subgroups
L\unlhd H
and
M\unlhd G
such that the quotients
H/L
and
G/M
have two finite index subgroups that are isomorphic.

Theorem 1

(Quasi-isometric rigidity)
.

Let
S=S_{0,b}
be a punctured sphere, with
b\geq 7
punctures. There exists
K_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
such that, if
K
is a non-trivial multiple of
K_{0}
, then
H=MCG(S)/DT_{K}
is quasi-isometrically rigid, meaning that if a finitely generated group
G
is quasi-isometric to
H
then
G
and
H
are weakly commensurable.

Quasi-isometric rigidity of mapping class groups was first proven in
[
BKMM12
]
, see also
[
Ham05
]
, but our argument is closer to the proof given in
[
BHS21
, Section 5]
. The key result from that paper is
[
BHS21
, Theorem 5.7]
, which roughly speaking allows one to extract an automorphism of a certain graph from a quasi-isometry of a hierarchically hyperbolic space satisfying suitable assumptions. We cannot use this result as stated, since our quotients do not satisfy its assumptions; instead, we will show that a similar result holds under different hypotheses that our quotient groups do satisfy. This is done in Theorem
8.7
, which we believe to be of independent interest since we enlarge the family of hierarchically hyperbolic spaces to which one can apply the arguments from
[
BHS21
, Section 5]
to study quasi-isometric rigidity.

In our case of interest, the graph from Theorem
8.7
can be related to a certain subgraph of the quotient of the curve graph. From there we are able to prove quasi-isometric rigidity of our quotient groups, adapting arguments due to Bowditch, especially from
[
Bow20
]
, where the author proved quasi-isometric rigidity of pants graphs.

Combining quasi-isometric rigidity and results on automorphisms of acylindrically hyperbolic groups from
[
AMS16
]
, we are able to deduce the following, which can be regarded as a form of algebraic rigidity. Here,
MCG^{\pm}
denotes the extended mapping class group, where orientation-reversing mapping classes are allowed.

Theorem 2

(Algebraic rigidity)
.

Let
S=S_{0,b}
be a punctured sphere, with
b\geq 7
punctures. There exists
K_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
such that, if
K
is a non-trivial multiple of
K_{0}
, then

(1)

\text{Aut}(MCG(S)/DT_{K})=MCG^{\pm}(S)/DT_{K}
;

(2)

\text{Out}(MCG(S)/DT_{K})\cong\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
;

(3)

The abstract commensurator of
MCG^{\pm}(S)/DT_{K}
is isomorphic to its inner automorphism group. In other words, any isomorphism between finite index subgroups of
MCG^{\pm}(S)/DT_{K}
is the restriction of an inner automorphism.

In the case of mapping class groups, the analogue of the result above was proven by Ivanov in his famous paper on automorphisms of curve graphs
[
Iva97
]
. Indeed, our quasi-isometric rigidity and in turn algebraic rigidity results rely on an analogue of Ivanov’s theorem for quotients of the
curve graph

\mathcal{C}(S)
of
S
, that is, the graph whose vertices are isotopy classes of essential simple closed curves and edges correspond to disjointness (up to isotopy). This is our final main result, and it can be thought of as a form of combinatorial rigidity:

Theorem 3

(Combinatorial rigidity)
.

Let
S=S_{0,b}
be a punctured sphere, with
b\geq 7
punctures, and let
\mathcal{C}(S)
be its curve graph. There exists
K_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
such that, if
K
is a non-trivial multiple of
K_{0}
, then the natural map
MCG^{\pm}(S)/DT_{K}\to\text{Aut}(\mathcal{C}(S)/DT_{K})
is an isomorphism.

In fact, the result applies to other quotients of mapping class groups of punctured spheres, see Theorem
5.11
and the discussion in the outline section. As explained below, the main tools used in the proof of Theorem
3
are the lifting techniques first used in
[
DHS21
]
and the finite rigid set of Aramayona-Leiniger
[
AL13
]
. Indeed, we actually obtain a finite rigidity result as well, see Corollary
5.14
. An analogue of the theorem also holds for
b=4
, but in that case the map has finite kernel, see Theorem
5.2
.

Remark 1
.

We believe that an integer
K_{0}
for which Theorems
1
,
2
, and
3
hold could in principle be explicitly computed. The starting point for this are explicit constants for various properties of subsurface projections, for example the bounded geodesic image theorem
[
Web15
]
. Then one would need to track all the constants at play in previous papers on the subject, in particular
[
Dah18
, Subsection 1.2.1, Section 3]
(which in turn depends on the construction from
[
BBF15
]
),
[
DHS21
, Proof of Theorem 2.1]
, and
[
BHMS24
, Notation 8.11]
. It would be interesting to have more direct arguments yielding an explicit (and hopefully low) constant, at least for low numbers of punctures in our setting.

1.1.
Open questions

Throughout the paper we only cover the case of punctured spheres/braid groups, so the first questions, which we believe to have positive answer, are the following:

Question 1
.

Does Theorem
3
generalise to arbitrary genus?

Question 2
.

Do Theorems
1
and
2
generalise to arbitrary genus?

It will be clear from the outline section that the key result to generalise is combinatorial rigidity, Theorem
3
, which is why we stated the first question separately.

It will be very important for us that the quotients we deal with are hierarchically hyperbolic. Proving hierarchical hyperbolicity of other quotients would be key to proving further quasi-isometric and algebraic rigidity results, and there is a natural class of quotients that contains the quotients we consider in this paper:

Question 3
.

Let
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
be elements of a mapping class group
MCG(S)
. Does there exist
K_{0}\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
such that for all non-trivial multiples
K_{i}
of
K_{0}
we have that
MCG(S)/\langle\langle\{g_{i}^{K_{i}}\}\rangle\rangle
is hierarchically hyperbolic?

In forthcoming work we will give a positive answer for the case where
S
is a five-holed sphere. Furthermore, if one allows
S
to be any finite-type surface, there are certain classes of quotients for which the answer is known to be affirmative, such as those covered in
[
BHMS24
]
, as discussed above, and
[
BHS17a
]
, where quotients by powers of pseudo-Anosovs are considered. Additional quotients covered by the question include quotients of mapping class groups by suitable powers of Dehn twists around non-separating curves only, for instance, as well as the quotients considered in
[
CM22
]
.

1.2.
Outline of proofs

We will first prove combinatorial rigidity, Theorem
3
. The main idea for doing so is the following. Fix a punctured sphere
S
, say with at least 7 punctures, let
\mathcal{C}
be its curve graph, and let
K
be a suitable large integer. While the map
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}/DT_{K}
is not a covering map (as it is quite far from being locally injective), there are still various subgraphs of
\mathcal{C}/DT_{K}
that can be lifted. This idea was first used in
[
DHS21
]
to show that
\mathcal{C}/DT_{K}
is hyperbolic by lifting geodesic triangles, and was developed further in
[
BHMS24
]
to show that
MCG(S)/DT_{K}
is in fact hierarchically hyperbolic. We push these techniques even further to show that the graphs constructed in
[
AL13
]
can be lifted. These are finite rigid sets for
\mathcal{C}
, that is, all isometric embeddings of them into
\mathcal{C}
differ by an element of the extended mapping class group. Consider now an automorphism
\overline{\phi}
of
\mathcal{C}/DT_{K}
and a copy
X
of one such graph in
\mathcal{C}/DT_{K}
. One can consider the following diagram, where the hats denote lifts and
\pi
is the projection map:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widehat{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\widehat{\phi(X)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi(X)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces g}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\overline{\phi}}

From the diagram we obtain a candidate element of the extended mapping class group that induces
\phi
, namely the element
g
mapping
\widehat{X}
to
\widehat{\phi(X)}
. Showing that this candidate is the desired element requires more care, and further lifts, but this is the basic idea. We note that we do not know of a graph that cannot be lifted from
\mathcal{C}/DT_{K}
to
\mathcal{C}
, but even a path of length 2 does not have a
unique

DT_{K}
-orbit of lifts, see Remark
4.2
, while uniqueness of orbits of lifts is crucial in many arguments.

In order to prove quasi-isometric rigidity we will also need combinatorial rigidity for some subgraphs of the quotient graphs. This is shown in Sections
6
and
7
, where we combine arguments due to Bowditch with further lifting techniques.

Remark 2
.

The first step towards generalising our results to the case of surfaces with genus, using the same outline, would be to show that some finite rigid sets admit lifts in those cases as well. The finite rigid sets from
[
AL13
]
are more complicated for surfaces with genus, but they can hopefully be used for this purpose.

1.2.1.
Quasi-isometric rigidity

To prove quasi-isometric rigidity, we will use a version of
[
BHS21
, Theorem 5.7]
which states, roughly, that any quasi-isometry
f
of a hierarchically hyperbolic group satisfying three assumptions induces an automorphism of a certain graph called the hinge graph. This is a graph that encodes the ”standard flats” of the group, as well as their intersection patterns. The three assumptions do not apply to our case, but we show that a similar statement still holds under different assumptions that do apply; this is Theorem
8.7
.
Afterwards, we use combinatorial arguments to show that an automorphism of the hinge graph induces automorphisms of the graphs from Sections
6
and
7
. Then, by combinatorial rigidity of such graphs, these automorphisms are induced by some element
g
of the mapping class group, and with some more effort one can show that
f
and
g
are at bounded distance.

1.2.2.
Algebraic rigidity

Since automorphisms of a group, and more generally isomorphisms between finite index subgroups, induce quasi-isometries, one can expect to obtain strong algebraic rigidity results from quasi-isometric rigidity. We do just that in Theorem
10.1
, using results from
[
AMS16
]
. Finite normal subgroups could cause the outer automorphism group to be finite rather than trivial, so the key technical results we need is that
MCG^{\pm}(S)/DT_{K}
does not contain non-trivial finite normal subgroups, Lemma
10.8
.

1.3.
Outline of sections

In Section
2
we introduce the basic lifting tools that we will use throughout the paper, and we also discuss finite rigid sets. Section

studies the projection maps from curve graphs to their relevant quotients, and a key result here is that our chosen finite rigid sets map injectively, see Theorem

. In Section

we show that (graphs isomorphic to our) finite rigid sets can be lifted from quotients of curve graphs to curve graphs. We then use this in Section

to show combinatorial rigidity, Theorem

, see Theorem

. Further combinatorial rigidity results, that will be needed in Section

, are proved in Sections

.

The goal of Section

is to show that quasi-isometries of suitable hierarchically hyperbolic spaces induce automorphisms of an associated graph, Theorem

we combine Theorem

and the combinatorial rigidity results from Sections

2.
Setting and main tools

2.1.
Mapping classes and curves

Let
S_{b}=S_{0,b}
be the sphere with
b
punctures, on which we fix some orientation. By a
curve
on
S_{b}
we will always mean the isotopy class of a simple closed curve which is essential, meaning that it does not bound a disk with less than two punctures. Let
\mathcal{C}_{b}=\mathcal{C}(S_{b})
be the curve graph of
S_{b}
, that is, the simplicial graph whose vertices are curves and two curves are adjacent if and only they admit disjoint representatives. We will often denote
S_{b}
by
S
and
\mathcal{C}_{b}
by
\mathcal{C}
whenever the dependence on
b
is not relevant. Given two curves
\alpha,\beta
, let
i(\alpha,\beta)
denote their
geometric intersection number
, that is, the minimum number of transverse intersection points between a representative for
\alpha
and a representative for
\beta
.

Let
MCG^{\pm}(S_{b})
be the extended mapping class group of
S_{b}
, where we allow orientation-reversing homeomorphisms. We are interested in (powers of) the following type of mapping classes:

Definition 2.1

(Dehn Twist)
.

Consider the Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with polar coordinates
(\theta,r)
, and consider the annulus
A=\{(\theta,r)|1\leq r\leq 2\}
. Choose the orientation on
A
such that a counterclockwise rotation is positive. Let
T:\,A\to A
be the twist map of
A
given by the formula
T(\theta,r)=(\theta+2\pi r,r)
. We call
T
the
left twist
of our annulus. See Figure
1
.

Figure 1.
The left twist
T
of an annulus.

Now let
S
be an oriented surface and let
\alpha
be a curve in S. Let
N
be a regular neighborhood of
\alpha
, which we call an
annulus
with
core curve

\alpha
, and choose an orientation-preserving homeomorphism
\phi:\,A\to N
. The
(left) Dehn twist
about
\alpha
, denoted
T_{\alpha}
, is the homeomorphism

x\to\begin{cases}\phi\circ T\circ\phi^{-1}(x)\mbox{ if }x\in N,\\
x\mbox{ if }x\in S\setminus N.\end{cases}

The isotopy class of the Dehn twist
T_{\alpha}
does not depend on the choice of
N
and
\phi
, nor on the choice of
\alpha
within its free isotopy class. Thus we will often abuse notation slightly and view
T_{\alpha}
as a mapping class.

For every
K\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, let
DT_{K}
be the subgroup generated by the collection
\{T_{\alpha}^{K}\}
, where
\alpha
varies among all curves on
S
. Furthermore, let
\pi:\,\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}/DT_{K}
be the quotient projection.

Definition 2.2

(
[
MM00
]
)
.

Let
\gamma
be a curve, and let
p_{\gamma}:\,S_{\gamma}\to S
be the
cyclic covering
associated to
\gamma
. There is a natural compactification of
S_{\gamma}
to a closed annulus

.

The

associated to the curve

is the simplicial graph
\mathcal{C}(\gamma)
whose vertices are the paths connecting the two boundary components of
\widehat{S_{\gamma}}
, modulo homotopies that fix the endpoints, and where two such paths are adjacent if they have representatives with disjoint interiors (but may share one or both endpoints).

If a curve
\alpha
intersects
\gamma
, we denote by
\pi_{\gamma}(\alpha)
the
annular projection
of
\alpha
onto
\gamma
, that is, a lift of any representative of
\alpha
to
\widehat{S_{\gamma}}
, which is an arc connecting the two boundary components. This is well-defined, as any two lifts of (any two representatives of)

are homotopic relative to the endpoints. If
\alpha
and
\beta
both intersect
\gamma
, we shall write
d_{\gamma}(\alpha,\beta)
as shorthand for
d_{\mathcal{C}(\gamma)}(\pi_{\gamma}(\alpha),\pi_{\gamma}(\beta))
.

Remark 2.6
.

Notice that a deep enough subgroup

is generated by powers of Dehn twists. This is proved by induction on the complexity

Lemma 2.8
.

Whenever
\mathcal{N}
is deep enough, every edge
\{\overline{x},\overline{y}\}\subset\mathcal{C}_{4}/\mathcal{N}
admits a unique
\mathcal{N}
-orbit of lifts.

Proof.

By definition of the quotient graph, there exist two adjacent vertices
x,y\in\mathcal{C}_{4}
whose projections are
\overline{x}
and
\overline{y}
, respectively. Therefore there exists at least one lift. Now let
\{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\}
be another lift of
\{\overline{x},\overline{y}\}
. Up to the action of
\mathcal{N}
we can assume that
y=y^{\prime}
. Therefore we have a path
\{x,y,x^{\prime}\}
inside
\mathcal{C}_{4}
, and there is an element
g\in\mathcal{N}
such that
g(x)=x^{\prime}
.

If
g
is the identity we are done, otherwise let
(s,\gamma_{s})
as in Proposition
2.3
. If
d_{\mathcal{C}}(x,s)\leq 1
, so that
\gamma_{s}
fixes
x
, we can apply
\gamma_{s}
to both edges. Now we have a path
\{\gamma_{s}(x),\gamma_{s}(y),\gamma_{s}(x^{\prime})\}
, and
x=\gamma_{s}(x)
is sent to
\gamma_{s}(x^{\prime})
by the element
\gamma_{s}g
. Since
\alpha(\gamma_{s}g)<\alpha(g)
we can proceed by induction on the complexity
\alpha(g)
.

Otherwise

. We claim that
d_{\mathcal{C}}(y,s)\leq 1
. If this is not the case then
\pi_{s}(y)
is well-defined, and by triangle inequality for annular projections either
d_{s}(x,y)>\Theta/2
or

. Without loss of generality, we can assume to be in the first case. If we choose
\Theta\geq 2B
, where

is the constant from the bounded geodesic image Theorem
2.4
, we get that every geodesic from

to

(that is, the edge between these vertices) should pass through the star of

, which is a contradiction. Therefore
\gamma_{s}
must fix

, and if we replace

with

we can again reduce the complexity of

, while preserving the fact that the two edges share an endpoint.

At the end of the inductive argument we have that

, thus proving uniqueness of the orbit of lifts of an edge.
∎

Lemma 2.9
.

Whenever
\mathcal{N}
is deep enough, every triangle

-orbit of lifts.

a lift of the triangle, which we see as a closed path

as in Proposition

2.3.

For the following definitions, let

Definition 2.11
.

A

such that

iff

In the curve graph, a chain is just a sequence of curves such that two of them intersect iff they are consecutive.

The following should be compared with the notion of
\mathcal{X}
-detectable intersection from
[

(Special intersection)

Let

•

\alpha
and

both complete

to maximal simplices;

•

there exist
\gamma,\,\delta
such that
\gamma,\,\alpha,\,\beta,\,\delta
is a chain;

•

there exists
\varepsilon\in P
such that
\text{Lk}(\gamma)\cap P=\text{Lk}(\delta)\cap P=P-\{\varepsilon\}
.

We say that
\gamma
and
\delta
are the auxiliary vertices that detect the intersection of

and
\beta
. The situation is illustrated in Figure

If

cuts out (see the remark following
[

Remark 2.14
.

Definition

For the rest of the paper, given a curve
\beta\in\mathcal{C}
which bounds a twice-punctured disk, let

be the

around
\beta

Lemma 2.17
.

Let

and let
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
be two curves which both have special intersection with the same
\beta
, with respect to the same

. Let

be the four-holed sphere that

cuts out, and let

be any of the two disks bounded by

inside

. Then there exists an integer

.

Proof.

P
cuts out a sphere

with four punctures, and by Remark

having special intersection implies that the intersection number is

. Thus we want to show that, if

are both adjacent to

in the Farey complex and

. By properties of the Farey complex there exists a sequence of triangles

such that each triangle contains

,

and every two consecutive triangles

. Thus it suffices to prove that if
\alpha,\alpha^{\prime},\beta
are the vertices of a triangle then
\alpha^{\prime}=H^{\pm 1}_{D}(\alpha)
. This is true, since the two curves

are adjacent to both
\alpha
and
\beta
, and
\alpha
and
\beta
belong to exactly two triangles.
∎

Remark 2.18
.

When

we have to be a little cautious. If

already bounds a twice-punctured disk

inside

2.4.

For the rest of the section let
b\geq 5
. We denote by

the full subgraph of

defined in

, Section 3]
. More precisely, we represent

be the subgraph spanned by such curves, as in Figure

A

Theorem 2.19

For every

, any locally injective simplicial map

, meaning

Another important fact about

because it can be detected using only vertices that belong to

(this fact should be compared with
[

\alpha
and

in Figure

of codimension two and the three curves

The next lemmas show how to construct some copy of

starting from a given copy of

Definition 2.20
.

If
b\geq 6
, we say that a vertex

\text{Lk}_{X_{b}}(\beta)\cong X_{b-1}
.

Lemma 2.21

(Existence of an extension of

)
.

Let

. Every copy of
X_{b-1}
inside
\mathcal{C}
which is in the link of a vertex

X_{b}
that contains
\beta
. Moreover, if
\alpha
has special intersection with
\beta

to contain

Proof.

First we show that
\beta
bounds a twice-punctured disk. To see this, notice that, by construction of the graph

, if two curves
x,y\in X_{b-1}
are at distance

then there exists a curve
z\in X_{b-1}
which is at distance 2 from both. This fact still holds if we replace distances in
X_{b-1}
with distances in the curve graph, since
X_{b-1}
is isometrically embedded, and it translates to the fact that whenever two curves in
X_{b-1}
are disjoint there is a third curve which intersects both. Hence the union of all curves of
X_{b-1}
must lie on the same connected component of

, which is a disk that we call

. But then

contains

must be a disk with at most

Now, since
\beta
bounds a twice-punctured disk it is easy to find some

which contains
\beta
as a minimal curve. Let
X_{b-1}^{\prime}=\text{Lk}_{X_{b}^{\prime}}(\beta)
, and consider the

obtained by shrinking

to a puncture

. By finite rigidity of

that completes

3.
Projection lemmas

In this section we gather various results about the nature of the projection map
\pi:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{N}
. We start by showing that the restriction of
\pi
to the curve graph of a subsurface is what one would expect.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
U
be a subsurface of
S
. Let
\mathcal{N}
be a deep enough subgroup, and let
\mathcal{N}(U)
be the subgroup of
\mathcal{N}
generated by the elements with support in
U
. If
x,x^{\prime}
are curves on
U
and there exists an element
g\in\mathcal{N}
mapping
x
to
x^{\prime}
then we may find another element
h\in\mathcal{N}(U)
mapping
x
to
x^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
\Delta
be a pants decomposition of the complement of
U
, including its boundary curves. We proceed by induction on the complexity of
g
. If
g
is the identity then we are done; otherwise let
(s,\gamma_{s})
be as in Proposition
2.3
.

If
d_{\mathcal{C}}(x,s)\leq 1
then we can apply
\gamma_{s}
to both curves, in order to reduce the complexity of
g
. Then by inductive hypothesis we can find some
h\in\mathcal{N}(\gamma_{s}(U))
mapping
\gamma_{s}(x)
to
\gamma_{s}(x^{\prime})
, and therefore
\gamma_{s}^{-1}h\gamma_{s}
maps
x
to
x^{\prime}
and belongs to
\mathcal{N}(U)
by properties of Dehn twists under conjugation.

Otherwise
d_{s}(x,x^{\prime})>\Theta
. Arguing exactly as in the proof of Lemma
2.8
we get that
\gamma_{s}
fixes
\Delta
pointwise whenever
\mathcal{N}
is deep enough. Now recall that, by construction,
\gamma_{s}\in\langle T^{N}_{s}\rangle
, where
T_{s}
is the Dehn twist around
s
. Then
s
must be disjoint from all curves in
\Delta
, which means that either
s\in\Delta
(which is impossible, since otherwise
\gamma_{s}
would fix
x
) or
s\subseteq U
. Therefore
\gamma_{s}\in\mathcal{N}(U)
, and if we apply it to
x^{\prime}
we can conclude by induction.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
U
be a connected subsurface of
S
of complexity at least
2
, and let
\mathcal{N}
be a deep enough subgroup. If
\pi:\,\mathcal{C}(S)\to\mathcal{C}(S)/\mathcal{N}
is the quotient projection then
\pi(\mathcal{C}(U))\cong\mathcal{C}(U)/\mathcal{N}(U)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.1
these graphs have the same vertices, and since
U
has complexity at least
2
the adjacency relation is having disjoint lifts in both cases.
∎

3.1.
Isometric projections

We move on to show that, whenever
\mathcal{N}
is deep enough, the projection is an isometry on a variety of subgraphs of the curve graph. All the result of this subsection rely on the following refinement of
[

, Lemma 4.4]
, which roughly speaking says that the projection
\pi:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{N}
preserves directions with small projections, in the link of some fixed simplex:

Lemma 3.3
.

Fix
\Theta>0
. Let
\mathcal{N}
be deep enough and let
\overline{\Delta}\subseteq\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{N}
be a simplex and
\Delta\subseteq X
be one of its lifts. Suppose that
x,y\in\text{Lk}(\Delta)
project to
\overline{x},\overline{y}\in\text{Lk}(\overline{\Delta})
and have the property that
d_{s}(x,y)<\Theta
for every

for which the quantity is defined. Then
\pi|_{[x,y]}
is an isometric embedding into
\text{Lk}(\overline{\Delta})
, for any geodesic
[x,y]\subseteq\text{Lk}(\Delta)
.

Proof.

The proof is very similar to that of
[
DHS21
, Lemma 4.4]
. Suppose by contradiction that there is a shorter path from
\overline{x}
to
\overline{y}
inside
\text{Lk}(\overline{\Delta})
. Lift this shorter path as a geodesic segment
[y,x^{\prime}]\subseteq\text{Lk}(\Delta)
, which can be done by Lemma
2.7
. There exists
\gamma\in\mathcal{N}
such that
\gamma x=x^{\prime}
. We proceed by induction on the complexity of
\gamma
, to prove that
d_{\text{Lk}(\Delta)}(y,x^{\prime})=d_{\text{Lk}(\Delta)}(y,x)
(thus contradicting that
d_{\text{Lk}(\overline{\Delta})}(\overline{x},\overline{y})<d_{\text{Lk}(\Delta)}(x,y)
). If
\gamma=1
we are done, otherwise let
(s,\gamma_{s})
be as in
2.3
. If
d_{\mathcal{C}}(x,s)\leq 1
then
\gamma_{s}x=x
, and we can apply
\gamma_{s}
to both geodesics and to
\Delta
and conclude by the induction hypothesis. Otherwise
d_{s}(x,\gamma x)>\Theta
, and arguing as in Lemma
2.8
we see that
\gamma_{s}
must fix
\Delta
pointwise. Moreover, either
d_{\mathcal{C}}(y,s)\leq 1
or
d_{s}(y,x^{\prime})\geq d_{s}(x,x^{\prime})-d_{s}(x,y)
must be large, since
d_{s}(x,y)
is assumed to be small. In both cases there must be some
s^{\prime}\in[y,x^{\prime}]
in the star of
s
. Thus, one can change the lift of
[y,x]
as
[y,\gamma_{s}x^{\prime}]
, while keeping it an isometric lift inside the link of
\Delta
. One concludes by induction hypothesis, which applies to
\gamma_{s}\gamma
.
∎

The previous lemma is particularly useful in the following form:

Corollary 3.4
.

For every finite set of vertices
V\subseteq\mathcal{C}
there exists
\Theta
such that, whenever
\mathcal{N}
is deep enough with respect to
\Theta
, the projection is an isometry on
V
.

Proof.

Since
V
is finite, we only need to show that for every
x,z\in\mathcal{C}
there is some constant
M(x,z)
such that
\sup_{s\in\mathcal{C}}d_{s}(x,z)<M(x,z)
, because then we can choose
\Theta>M=\max_{x,z\in V}M(x,z)
to ensure that the hypothesis of Lemma
3.3
applies. One way to see this is to complete
x,z
to complete clean markings
\mu,\nu
, in the sense of
[
MM00
, Section 2.5]
. By the Distance formula
[
MM00
, Theorem 6.12]
there exists a constant
M^{\prime}(S)
such that the sum
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{C},\,d_{s}(\mu,\nu)>M^{\prime}}d_{s}(\mu,\nu)
is bounded above in terms of the distance between
\mu
and
\nu
in the marking graph. In particular the sum is finite, and since every term is greater than a constant there must be a finite number of terms.
Moreover, for every
s\in\mathcal{C}
for which the quantity
d_{s}(x,y)
is defined (in particular, not a base curve of
\mu
or
\nu
), let
x^{\prime}\in\mu
and
y^{\prime}\in\nu
be curves which realise
d_{s}(\mu,\nu)
. Then by triangle inequality

d_{s}(x,y)\leq d_{s}(x,x^{\prime})+d_{s}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})+d_{s}(y^{\prime},y)=d_{s}(x,x^{\prime})+d_{s}(\mu,\nu)+d_{s}(y^{\prime},y)

and since annular projections are
2
-Lipschitz (see
[
MM00
, Lemma 2.3]
) we have that

d_{s}(x,y)\leq d_{s}(\mu,\nu)+4

Thus it suffices to choose
M(x,z)>\max_{s\in\mathcal{C},\,d_{s}(\mu,\nu)>M^{\prime}}d_{s}(\mu,\nu)+4
.
∎

Now we want to apply Corollary
3.4
to the rigid set.

Definition 3.5
.

Given a copy of
X_{b}\subset\mathcal{C}
for
b\geq 5
, set

Y_{b}=X_{b}\bigcup_{\beta\in X_{b}\,minimal}H_{\beta}^{\pm 1}(X_{b}).

Lemma 3.6
.

For every
b\geq 5
there exists
M\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
with the following property. Given a copy
X_{b}\subset\mathcal{C}
set
Y_{b}
as in Definition
3.5
. Then for every
x,z\in Y_{b}
and every
s\in\mathcal{C}
we have that
d_{s}(x,z)\leq M
.

Proof.

Fix a copy
X_{b}
and let
M=\max_{x,z\in Y_{b}}M(x,z)
, where
M(x,z)
is defined as in Corollary
3.4
. We are left to prove that, if
X_{b}^{\prime}
is another copy of the rigid set and
Y_{b}^{\prime}
is the corresponding union of half twists, then
M
works also for
Y_{b}^{\prime}
. Take an extended mapping class
f
that maps
X_{b}
to
X_{b}^{\prime}
, which exists by Theorem
2.19
. In particular
f
maps minimal curves to minimal curves. Therefore

Y_{b}^{\prime}=X_{b}^{\prime}\bigcup_{\beta^{\prime}\in X_{b}^{\prime}\,minimal}H_{\beta^{\prime}}^{\pm 1}(X_{b}^{\prime})=f(X_{b})\bigcup_{\beta\in X_{b}\,minimal}H_{f(\beta)}^{\pm 1}\circ f(X_{b})=

=f(X_{b})\bigcup_{\beta\in X_{b}\,minimal}f\circ H_{\beta}^{\pm 1}(X_{b})=f(Y_{b})

Now the conclusion follows, since for every
x,z\in Y_{b}^{\prime}
and
s\in\mathcal{C}
we have that

d_{s}(x,z)=d_{f^{-1}(s)}(f^{-1}(x),f^{-1}(z))\leq M.

∎

This corollary is crucial for us, so we state it as a theorem:

Theorem 3.7
.

For every
b\geq 5
the following holds whenever
\mathcal{N}
is deep enough. Let
X_{b}\subset\mathcal{C}
be a copy of the rigid set and let
Y_{b}
be as in Definition
3.5
. Then the restriction
\pi|_{Y_{b}}
is an isometry. In particular there exists an isometrically embedded copy of
X_{b}
inside
\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{N}
.

Proof.

Just combine Lemma
3.6
and Corollary
3.4
.
∎

There are other two consequences of Lemma
3.3
that will be quite useful later. The first one deals with the cardinality of the quotient:

Corollary 3.8
.

Whenever
\mathcal{N}
is deep enough, the quotient
\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{N}
is infinite.

Proof.

For every curve
x\in\mathcal{C}
and every pseudo-Anosov mapping class
g\in MCG(S)
there exists
\Theta>0
such that
\sup_{s\in\mathcal{C},n\in\mathbb{Z}}d_{s}(x,g^{n}(x))<\Theta
. This follows from an argument in the proof of
[
DHS21
, Theorem 2.1]
which just uses the Bounded geodesic image Theorem
2.4
and the fact that for every
x,z\in\mathcal{C}
then
\sup_{s\in\mathcal{C}}d_{s}(x,z)<\infty
, which we proved in Corollary
3.4
. Hence, if
\mathcal{N}
is deep enough with respect to
\Theta
, by Lemma
3.3
the projection is an isometry on the orbit
\{g^{n}(x)\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
, which is infinite since
g
is pseudo-Anosov.
∎

The second consequence deals with filling curves:

Corollary 3.9
.

Fix
\Theta>0
. Let
\mathcal{N}
be deep enough and let
\overline{\Delta}\subseteq\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{N}
be a simplex and
\Delta\subseteq X
be one of its lift. Suppose that
\Delta
cuts out a single subsurface
\Sigma
of complexity at least
1
, and let
x,y\subset\Sigma
be a pair of filling curves with the property that
d_{s}(x,y)<\Theta
whenever the quantity is defined. If
x,y
project to
\overline{x},\overline{y}\in\text{Lk}(\overline{\Delta})
then any two lifts
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\text{Lk}(\Delta)
again fill
\Sigma
.

Proof.

Recall that
x
and
y
fill
\Sigma
if and only if
d_{\text{Lk}(\Delta)}(x,y)\geq 3
. If
x
and
y
satisfy the hypothesis of Lemma
3.3
then the distance between
\overline{x}
and
\overline{y}
in
\text{Lk}(\overline{\Delta})
remains at least three. But then every pair of lifts must be at distance at least three, since the projection map is
1
-Lipschitz.
∎

3.2.
Puncture separations

Similarly to the previous subsection, we aim to establish some results of isometric projection, but this time they will arise from some observations on how curves separate punctures on a sphere. Choose an enumeration of the punctures of
S_{b}
. A curve
\alpha
on
S_{b}
is separating, therefore it splits the punctures into two sets
\mathcal{B}^{+}(\alpha)
and
\mathcal{B}^{-}(\alpha)
.

Definition 3.10
.

The
puncture separation
induced by
\alpha
is the unordered couple
\{\mathcal{B}^{+}(\alpha),\,\mathcal{B}^{-}(\alpha)\}
.

Remark 3.11
.

Notice that a Dehn twist fixes each puncture, therefore preserves puncture separations. In fact if two punctures are on the same side of
\alpha
they may be joined by an arc which is disjoint from
\alpha
, and the image of this arc via a Dehn twist will again join the same punctures while being disjoint from the image of
\alpha
. In other words, any two curves that induce different puncture separations cannot be identified in a quotient of the curve graph by a subgroup generated by powers of Dehn twists, such as all deep enough subgroups
\mathcal{N}
in the light of Remark
2.6
.

Definition 3.12
.

Two curves
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
induce
nested
puncture separations if
\mathcal{B}^{+}(\alpha)\subseteq\mathcal{B}^{\pm}(\alpha^{\prime})
. In other words, the splitting induced by
\alpha\cup\alpha^{\prime}
refines the splitting induced by
\alpha
.

Notice that two disjoint curves induce nested puncture separations, but the converse is not true (for example, choose some
\beta
intersecting
\alpha
and set
\alpha^{\prime}=T_{\beta}(\alpha)
, which induces the same puncture separation by Remark
3.11
). The following lemma shows a peculiar behaviour of the projection
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}/DT_{K}
which holds for all
K\in\mathbb{N}
:

Lemma 3.13
.

For every subgroup
H\leq MCG(S)
generated by powers of Dehn Twists, the following hold for the projection
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{C}/H
:

(1)

For
b=4
, if
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
are adjacent in the Farey complex then their projections remain at distance
1
.

(2)

For
b\geq 5
, if
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
are disjoint curves then their projections remain at distance
1
.

(3)

For
b\geq 5
, if
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
intersect and their puncture separations are not nested then their projections remain at distance at least
2
.

Proof.

In all cases we will use that the projection is
1
-Lipschitz, since the action of
H
over
\mathcal{C}
is simplicial.

(1)

Two adjacent curves in the Farey complex induce different puncture separations, therefore their projections must be at distance
1
since they cannot coincide.

(2)

If
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
are disjoint then they must induce different puncture separations, otherwise they would bound an unpunctured annulus and therefore they would be isotopic. Then their projections can only be at distance
1
, for the same reasons as before.

(3)

Suppose that
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
intersect and induce non-nested puncture separations. Firstly,
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
cannot have the same projection, since they induce different puncture separations. Furthermore, if by contradiction
\pi(\alpha)
and
\pi(\alpha^{\prime})
are the endpoints of an edge then we may lift it, since the action is simplicial. In other words there exists some
g\in H
such that
\alpha
is disjoint from
g(\alpha^{\prime})
. But then
\alpha
and
g(\alpha^{\prime})
must induce nested puncture separations, and so must do
\alpha
and
\alpha^{\prime}
since Dehn twists preserve puncture separations by Remark
3.11
.

∎

As an immediate consequence of Items (
1
) and (
2
) we get:

Corollary 3.14
.

For every
b\geq 4
, the projection map is an isometry on every simplex
\Delta\subset\mathcal{C}
.

Remark 3.15
.

Item (
1
) directly implies Theorem
3.7
for
b=4
. Moreover, Items (
2
) and (
3
) prove Theorem
3.7
for
b\geq 5
, since
Y_{b}
has diameter
2
and by construction each pair of intersecting curves induce non-nested puncture separations. This alternative proof works for every choice of
K
, though only if the surface is a punctured sphere.

4.
Lifting the rigid set

The aim of this section is to prove the following lifting result:

Theorem 4.1
.

For every
b\geq 5
and every
\mathcal{N}
deep enough, every copy of
X_{b}
in
\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{N}
has a lift inside
\mathcal{C}
, and any two lifts are conjugated by an element of
\mathcal{N}
.

Remark 4.2
.

At the moment, it is not known to the authors whether there exists a finite graph
\overline{G}
such that, whenever
\overline{G}
is immersed in some
\mathcal{C}/DT_{K}
, then
\overline{G}
does not admit a lift in
\mathcal{C}
. However there are easy examples where a graph
\overline{G}
admits two lifts
G,G^{\prime}
which belong to different
DT_{K}
-orbits; for applications it is often crucial for us that there is only one orbit of lifts of specific graphs. For example, for every
K>1
let
\overline{l}=\{\overline{\alpha},\overline{\beta},\overline{\gamma}\}
be a geodesic segment of length
2
in
\mathcal{C}_{4}/DT_{K}
, and let
l=\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma\}
be one of its lifts (whose existence is granted by Lemma
2.7
). Now let
\gamma^{\prime}=T^{K}_{\beta}(\gamma)
, so that
l^{\prime}=\{\alpha,\beta,\gamma^{\prime}\}
is another isometric lift of
\overline{l}
. If by contradiction
g\in DT_{K}\setminus\{1\}
maps
l
to
l^{\prime}
then it must fix
\alpha
and
\beta
. Notice moreover that, if an orientation preserving mapping class fixes an edge of the Farey complex then it fixes the whole complex. Thus
g
is a hyperelliptic involution, and since it must permute the punctures it cannot belong to any group generated by powers of Dehn twists, such as
DT_{K}
.

4.1.
Existence of a lift

Proof of existence.

We proceed by induction, using the case
b=5
as the base case since
X_{5}
is a pentagon, which lifts by Theorem
2.7
. Assume that
b\geq 6
and that every copy of
X_{b-1}
has a lift inside
\mathcal{C}_{b-1}
, and let
\overline{X}_{b}
be a copy of
X_{b}
inside
\mathcal{C}_{b}/\mathcal{N}
. Let
\overline{\beta}\in\overline{X}_{b}
be a minimal vertex, and let
\overline{X}_{b-1}:=\text{Lk}_{\overline{X}_{b}}(\overline{\beta})
.
In order to apply the inductive hypothesis we need to show that
\text{Lk}_{\mathcal{C}_{b}}(\beta)
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{C}_{b-1}
and that its projection is isomorphic to
\mathcal{C}_{b-1}/\mathcal{N}(S_{b-1})
. We start with the first assertion.

Lemma 4.3
.

Any lift
\beta
of a minimal vertex
\overline{\beta}\in\overline{X}_{b}
bounds a twice-punctured disk. In other words
\text{Lk}_{\mathcal{C}_{b}}(\beta)\cong\mathcal{C}_{b-1}
.

Proof.

Let
\overline{P}
be a facet inside
\overline{X}_{b-1}
. Since
\overline{\beta}\star\overline{P}
is a simplex we can find a lift
\beta^{\prime}\star P
by Lemma
2.7
, and up to applying some elements of
\mathcal{N}
we can assume that
\beta^{\prime}=\beta
and therefore
P\in\text{Lk}_{\mathcal{C}_{b}}(\beta)
. We want to show that all curves in
\text{Lk}_{\mathcal{C}_{b}}(\beta)
lie in the same subsurface of
S\setminus\beta
, or in other words that, given any two curves
\gamma,\delta\in\text{Lk}_{\mathcal{C}_{b}}(\beta)
, there is a chain
\gamma=\eta_{0},\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{k}=\delta\in\text{Lk}_{\mathcal{C}_{b}}(\beta)
. Since every curve in
\text{Lk}_{C_{b}}(\beta)
either coincides with or intersects a curve in
P
, which has maximal dimension in
\text{Lk}_{\mathcal{C}_{b}}(\beta)
, it is enough to show that for every
\gamma,\,\delta\in P
there is a curve
\eta
that intersects both. Now, since
\overline{X}_{b-1}
is isometrically embedded inside
\mathcal{C}_{b}/\mathcal{N}
we may pick some
\overline{\eta}\in\overline{X}_{b-1}
at distance
2
from both
\overline{\gamma}
and
\overline{\delta}
, and let
\eta
be one of its lifts in
\text{Lk}_{\mathcal{C}_{b}}(\beta)
. Since the projection map is
1
-Lipschitz,
\eta
must be at distance at least
2
from (hence intersect) both
\gamma
and
\delta
, and we are done.
∎

Now, Corollary
3.2
shows that the projection of
\text{Lk}_{\mathcal{C}_{b}}(\beta)
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{C}_{b-1}/\mathcal{N}(S_{b-1})
. Therefore we can use the inductive hypothesis to lift
\overline{X}_{b-1}
to some
X_{b-1}
inside
\text{Lk}_{\mathcal{C}_{b}}(\beta)
. Now the key point of the following construction will be this uniqueness lemma:

Lemma 4.4

(Unique lift for ”very” special intersections)
.

For
b\geq 5
the following holds whenever
\mathcal{N}
is deep enough. Let
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
be two curves with special intersection with the same
y
, with respect to the same facet
P
, and let
S
be any graph in the link of
y
. Suppose that
x,\,y,\,S
can be completed to a copy of
X_{b}
. Then if
x
and
x^{\prime}
project to the same element in the quotient there is some
g\in\mathcal{N}
which maps
x
to
x^{\prime}
and fixes
y
and
S
.

Proof.

Since both
x
and
x^{\prime}
have special intersection with
y
, with respect to the same facet
P
, by Lemma
2.17
there exists
k\in\mathbb{Z}
such that, in the four-holed sphere
S_{4}
that
P
cuts out,
x^{\prime}=H_{D}(x)
, where
D
is one of the disks bounded by
y
. Now we claim that
k=2m
must be even, and therefore
H^{2m}_{D}=T_{y}^{m}
extends to the whole surface
S
. To see this first notice that since
x
and
x^{\prime}
are in the same
\mathcal{N}
-orbit they must induce the same puncture separation by Remark
3.11
. Moreover
x
and
y
intersect twice, thus they separate the punctures of
S
into four sets
\mathcal{A}^{\pm},\mathcal{B}^{\pm}
, each of which corresponds to one of the punctures of
S_{4}
. Suppose that
x
induces the separation
\{\mathcal{A}^{+}\cup\mathcal{B}^{+},\mathcal{A}^{-}\cup\mathcal{B}^{-}\}
while
y
induces the separation
\{\mathcal{A}^{+}\cup\mathcal{A}^{-},\mathcal{B}^{+}\cup\mathcal{B}^{-}\}
, and that

contains the punctures corresponding to
\mathcal{A}^{\pm}
. Then
H_{D}
swaps the two punctures corresponding to
\mathcal{A}^{\pm}
, which shows that
H_{D}^{k}(x)
induces the same puncture separation as

if and only if

is even.

Now let
\gamma\in\mathcal{N}
such that
x^{\prime}=\gamma(x)
. If
\gamma=1
then we are done. Otherwise let
(s,\gamma_{s})
be as in Proposition
2.3
. If
d_{\mathcal{C}}(x,s)\leq 1
then we may apply
\gamma_{s}
to the whole data and proceed by induction on the complexity of
\gamma
. Otherwise

. Now we claim that any other

is in the star of

, so that we can replace

with
\gamma_{s}(x^{\prime})
and proceed by induction. If this is not the case then

is well-defined, and we have that

d_{s}(x,x^{\prime})\leq d_{s}(x,z)+d_{s}(x^{\prime},z)=d_{s}(x,z)+d_{s}(T^{m}_{y}(x),T^{m}_{y}(z))=d_{s}(x,z)+d_{T_{y}^{-m}(s)}(x,z)

where we used that
z
belongs to the star of

and is therefore fixed by

. But now

\Theta\leq d_{s}(x,x^{\prime})\leq d_{s}(x,z)+d_{T_{y}^{-m}(s)}(x,z)\leq 2\max_{p,q\in X_{b},\,s\in\mathcal{C}}d_{s}(p,q)\leq 2M

where

is the constant from Lemma
3.6
, which does not depend on

. This is a contradiction if
\mathcal{N}
is deep enough.
∎

Now, in order to complete our lift of
\overline{X}_{b}
we still need to lift the vertices
\overline{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,\overline{\alpha}_{b-3}\in\overline{X}_{b}
outside the link of
\overline{\beta}
. For every

we can find vertices
\overline{x},\overline{y}\in\overline{X}_{b-1}
and a codimension two simplex

such that
\overline{\beta},\,\overline{\alpha}_{1},\,\overline{\alpha}_{i},\,\overline{x},\,\overline{y}
and
\overline{R}
span a special pentagon
\overline{\mathfrak{P}}
. For example, one could look at Figure
6
, replace
z
with
\alpha_{i}
and then consider the corresponding vertices inside
\overline{X}_{b}
.

Lift
\overline{\mathfrak{P}}
to a special pentagon
\mathfrak{P}^{\prime}
, let

be the lift of the simplex
\overline{Q}=\overline{\beta}\star\overline{y}\star\overline{R}
inside
\mathfrak{P}^{\prime}

be the corresponding simplex inside

. Since there is a unique

there exists

mapping

to

. Thus we can apply

to
\mathfrak{P}^{\prime}
and assume that

. Moreover, let
x^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{P}^{\prime}
be the lift of
\overline{x}
, and

the corresponding lift inside

. If we apply Lemma

to
x,x^{\prime},y
and

we find some element

that maps
x^{\prime}
to
x
and fixes

. Thus we can apply

to
\mathfrak{P}^{\prime}
and assume that also
x=x^{\prime}
.

Now, let
G_{i}=\mathfrak{P}^{\prime}\cup X_{b-1}
. Let
\alpha_{1}^{i}\in\mathfrak{P}^{\prime}
be the lift of
\overline{\alpha}_{1}
inside the special pentagon. Lemma
2.21
provides a copy
X_{b}^{i}
which extends

and contains
\alpha_{1}^{i}
. By Lemma

,

, therefore

. Now, consider the copies
X_{b}^{2}
and

, for

. Therefore, if we apply Lemma

with

,

,

that maps

to

and fixes

. Then we conclude that

, which implies that the projection of

also on

is the lift we were looking for.
∎

4.2.
Uniqueness of the lift

Proof of uniqueness.

Again we proceed by induction. First we discuss the base case
b=5
.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
\mathcal{N}
is deep enough, any two lifts of
\overline{X}_{5}
inside
\mathcal{C}_{5}
differ by an element of
\mathcal{N}
.

Proof.

The proof is just a sequence of iterations of Lemma
4.4
. More precisely, let
\{\overline{\gamma}_{i}\}_{i=1,\ldots,5}
, be the vertices of
\overline{X}_{5}
, and let
\gamma_{i}
and
\gamma_{i}^{\prime}
be lifts that form two pentagons
X_{5}
and
X_{5}^{\prime}
. Up to applying some element of
\mathcal{N}
to
X_{5}^{\prime}
we may assume that
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{1}^{\prime}
and
\gamma_{2}=\gamma_{2}^{\prime}
, because the edge

Now assume that

\mathcal{C}_{b-1}
. The next step is the following:

If

Proof.

Let

. Up to an element of

. Now

Now we are finally able to prove the uniqueness part of Theorem

The aim of this section is to prove Theorem

5.1.

5.2.
Characterisation of half twists

From now on let
b\geq 5
. Let
\beta\in X_{b}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
be a minimal curve, and recall that we denote by

the half twist around
\beta
. Our first goal is to show that for any
\alpha\in X_{b}
its image

admits a graph-theoretic characterisation, which therefore is preserved by any automorphism of the curve complex. More precisely we want to prove the following:

Lemma 5.6
.

For any
\alpha\in X_{b}

•

H_{\beta}(\alpha)=\alpha
iff

;

•

otherwise there exists a facet

such that
H_{\beta}(\alpha)
is one of the two curves with special intersection with both
\alpha
and
\beta
, with respect to

.

Notice that we did not uniquely characterise
H_{\beta}(\alpha)
, as it is impossible to distinguish it from
H_{\beta}^{-1}(\alpha)
just from the curve graph (indeed, the orientation-reversing reflection

X_{b}
pointwise but swaps the half-twists).

Proof.

The first bullet is clearly true, so we focus on the second. Let
\alpha
be a curve in
X_{b}
that intersects
\beta
, and let
P
be a facet that both
\alpha
and
\beta
complete, which cuts out a four-holed sphere

. Now, clearly
H_{\beta}(\alpha)
has special intersection with both
\alpha
and
\beta
with respect to
P
(the curves
\gamma
and

are easy to find in both cases); therefore it suffices to notice that there are only two curves on

with intersection

with both
\alpha
and
\beta
, since in the curve graph of

the edge with endpoints
\alpha
and
\beta
belongs to only two triangles.
∎

5.3.
Definition of half twists in the quotient

For the rest of the section, let
\mathcal{N}
be a deep enough subgroup.

Definition 5.7
.

Let
\overline{X}_{b}
be a copy of
X_{b}
inside
\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{N}
, and let
\overline{\beta}\in\overline{X}_{b}
be a minimal vertex. Let

be a lift. Then we define the half twist around
\overline{\beta}
as the map

, defined as the composition
\pi\circ H_{s(\overline{\beta})}\circ s
.

In other words, we define the half twist by lifting
\overline{X}_{b}
, applying a half twist and projecting, so that the half twist commutes with the quotient projection. The definition is well-posed, since any two lifts differ by some
g\in\mathcal{N}
. Notice moreover that
H_{\overline{\beta}}(\overline{X}_{b})
is still a copy of
X_{b}
by Theorem
3.7
, since it is the projection of
H_{s(\overline{\beta})}\left(s(\overline{X}_{b})\right)

Our next goal is to show that the combinatorial characterisation of the half twist described in Theorem
5.6
still works in the quotient. In order to do so we need to show that:

(1)

H_{\overline{\beta}}(\overline{\alpha})
still satisfies the properties described in Theorem
5.6
;

(2)

\overline{\eta}
that satisfies the properties with
\overline{\alpha}
,
\overline{\beta}
and

lifts to a curve that satisfies the properties with some lifts
\alpha

. Therefore

must be the projection of

.

The following subsections will be dedicated to the proofs of these items.

5.3.1.

Let

Lemma 5.8

Both

.

consisting of elements that fix each puncture individually. But this subgroup is not deep enough if we choose a large enough constant

(for example, since the quotient
\mathcal{C}/PMCG
is finite, which contradicts Corollary

).
∎

5.5.
Finite rigidity of the quotient

As a by-product of the proofs of Theorems
5.11
and
5.2
we get an analogue of Theorem
2.19
for the quotient.

Corollary 5.14

(Finite rigidity of
\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{N}
)
.

For
b\geq 4
and
\mathcal{N}
deep enough, for every fixed copy
\overline{X}_{b}\subset\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{N}
(where by
\overline{X}_{4}
we mean a triangle), any isometric embedding
\phi:\overline{X}_{b}\to\mathcal{C}/\mathcal{N}
is induced by a mapping class
\overline{h}\in MCG/\mathcal{N}
. Moreover, two such
\overline{h}
differ by an element of the pointwise stabiliser
\text{PStab}(\overline{X}_{b})
, which is the projection of
\text{Pstab}(X_{b})
.

Proof.

For the existence part let
X_{b}
and
X_{b}^{\prime}
be lifts of
\overline{X}_{b}
and
\phi(\overline{X}_{b})
, respectively. Then by finite rigidity there is a mapping class
h\in MCG
mapping
X_{b}
to
X_{b}^{\prime}
, and the induced map
\overline{h}\in MCG/\mathcal{N}
maps
\overline{X}_{b}
to
\phi(\overline{X}_{b})
.

For the uniqueness part, if
h
and
h^{\prime}
both induce maps that map
\overline{X}_{b}
to
\phi(\overline{X}_{b})
then
h(X_{b})
and
h^{\prime}(X_{b})
are both lifts of
\phi(\overline{X}_{b})
, hence by uniqueness of the orbit of lifts there exists a

such that
g\circ h|_{X_{b}}=h^{\prime}|_{X_{b}}
. But then
g\circ h
and
h^{\prime}
must differ by an element of
\text{Pstab}(X_{b})
.
∎

For all

Definition 6.5

Let

The following definition is from

Definition 6.6

Let

By

Definition 6.7

Let
\Gamma

\mathcal{C}^{1}
or

(2)

\alpha^{\pm},\beta^{\pm}\in P^{\pm}

(3)

(4)

, and similarly for

Remark 6.8
.

When

, any division

in the sense of Bowditch admits a filling. For example, since the division is induced by some strongly-separating curve

could cut out some twice punctured disks, which are irrelevant to our argument since they cannot contain any

-separating curve. We will elaborate on this Remark in the proof of Lemma

Remark 6.9

We could state Definition

(1)

For all large multiples

The map

8.
Extracting combinatorial data from a quasi-isometry

The main goal of this section is, roughly speaking, to show that quasi-isometries of
MCG(S_{b})/DT_{K}
induce automorphisms of a certain graph, which we will later on relate to
\mathcal{C}/DT_{K}
. This strategy is inspired by
[
BHS21
, Section 5]
, where an approach to the quasi-isometric rigidity of mapping class groups is presented as a template to prove quasi-isometric rigidity of other
hierarchically hyperbolic spaces
(HHS),
MCG(S_{b})/DT_{K}
being a HHS (for suitable
K
)
[
BHMS24
]
. More precisely,
[
BHS21
, Theorem 5.7]
states that quasi-isometries of a HHS satisfying three additional assumptions induce automorphisms of a suitable graph. The assumptions are not satisfied by
MCG(S_{b})/DT_{K}
but nevertheless we will show that a very similar statement applies to that case, see Theorem
8.7
, which is the main result of this section. This theorem in fact applies to any HHS satisfying certain assumptions (different from those of
[
BHS21
]
), other examples of such HHSs being pants graphs.

We will begin by recalling all facts about HHSs that we will need.

8.1.
HHS background

A hierarchically hyperbolic space is a metric space
X
that comes with certain additional data, most importantly a family of uniformly hyperbolic spaces
\{\mathcal{C}(Y)\}_{Y\in\mathfrak{S}}
, called
coordinate spaces
, and uniformly coarsely Lipschitz maps
\pi_{Y}:X\to\mathcal{C}(Y)
. For mapping class groups, these are curve graphs of subsurfaces and maps coming from subsurface projections. Moreover, the index set
\mathfrak{S}
of the set of hyperbolic spaces has two relations,
orthogonality
and
nesting
, denoted
\bot
and
\sqsubseteq
respectively. For mapping class groups, these are containment and disjointness of subsurfaces (up to isotopy). When two elements
U,V
of
\mathfrak{S}
are not
\sqsubseteq
- nor
\bot
-compatible, one says that they are
transverse
. It is customary to denote a HHS simply by
(X,\mathfrak{S})
.

A crucial fact about HHSs is that they satisfy a
distance formula
. This means that for all sufficiently large threshold
s
there exists
D
such that for all
x,y\in X
we have

d_{X}(x,y)\asymp_{D,D}\sum\{\{d_{Y}(x,y)\}\}_{s},

where

•

\asymp_{D,D}
denotes equality up to multiplicative and additive error at most
D
,

•

\{\{A\}\}_{s}=A
if
A\geq s
and
\{\{A\}\}_{s}=0
otherwise,

•

d_{Y}(x,y)
is shorthand for
d_{\mathcal{C}(Y)}(\pi_{Y}(x),\pi_{Y}(y))
.

The idea of orthogonality is that it corresponds to products, in the following sense. Given any
U\in\mathfrak{S}
, there is a corresponding space
F_{U}
associated to it, which is quasi-isometrically embedded in
X
(
F_{U}
is a HHS itself with index set
\mathfrak{S}_{U}=\{Y\in\mathfrak{S}:Y\sqsubseteq U\}
, and in mapping class groups these essentially correspond to mapping class groups of subsurfaces). Given a maximal set
\{U_{i}\}
of pairwise orthogonal elements of
\mathfrak{S}
, there is a corresponding
standard product region

P_{\{U_{i}\}}
which is quasi-isometric to the product of the
F_{U_{i}}
(think of a Dehn twist flat as, coarsely, a product of annular curve graphs). The product region
P_{\{U_{i}\}}
has the property that for all
U
with
U_{i}\sqsubsetneq U
or
U\pitchfork U_{i}
for some
i
, we have that
\pi_{Y}(P_{\{U_{i}\}})
is a uniformly bounded set, that we denote
\rho^{\{U_{i}\}}_{V}
(usually, this notation is used for a different bounded set at finite Hausdorff distance from the one defined here; this will not matter for us). Product regions can also be associated to non-maximal sets of pairwise orthogonal elements of
\mathfrak{S}
, but in this case there is an additional factor than the
F_{U_{i}}
; this will not be important for us.

The following is
[
BHS21
, Corollary 1.28]
(which is stated for product regions corresponding to one element of the index set, but the proof does not use this):

Lemma 8.1
.

For all sufficiently large
s
there exists
D
such that, if
\{U_{i}\}
is a maximal collection of pairwise orthogonal indices and
x\in X
, then

d_{X}(x,P_{\{U_{i}\}})\asymp_{D,D}\sum_{W}\left\{\left\{d_{W}\left(x,\rho^{\{U_{i}\}}_{W}\right)\right\}\right\}_{s}

where the sum is made on all
W
such that
U_{i}\sqsubsetneq W
or
U_{i}\pitchfork W
for some
U_{i}
.

Corollary 8.2

(Intersection of different product regions)
.

If
\{U_{i}\}
and
\{V_{j}\}
are maximal collections of pairwise orthogonal indices, such that
\{U_{i}\}\cap\{V_{j}\}=\emptyset
and every
V_{j}
is
\sqsubseteq
-minimal, then
P_{\{U_{i}\}}\tilde{\cap}P_{\{V_{j}\}}
is coarsely a point.

Proof.

It is enough to apply Lemma
8.1
with
x\in P_{\{V_{j}\}}
. In fact, every
W\not\in\{V_{j}\}
contributes to the sum with a term that does not depend on
x
, but only on
\{U_{i},V_{j}\}
and
W
. Moreover, there is a finite number of terms that contribute to the sum, so if we choose a big enough threshold
s
we get that

d_{X}(x,P_{\{U_{i}\}})\asymp_{D,D}\sum_{j}\left\{\left\{d_{V_{j}}\left(x,\rho^{\{U_{i}\}}_{V_{j}}\right)\right\}\right\}_{s}

Therefore all points
x\in P_{\{V_{j}\}}
that lie within a given constant of
P_{\{U_{i}\}}
have nearby projections to all
\mathcal{C}V_{j}
, and hence they are close to each other, as required.
∎

Finally, we will be interested in quasiflats in HHSs and, related to quasiflats, orthants. In
X
and the
F_{U}
s there are special (uniform) quasi-geodesics, called
hierarchy paths
, which are those that project monotonically (with uniform constants) to all
\mathcal{C}(Y)
. Similarly, there are hierarchy rays and hierarchy lines (which are quasigeodesic rays and lines, respectively). Given a product region
P_{\{U_{i}\}}
and hierarchy lines (resp., rays) in some of the
F_{U_{i}}
, the product region contains a product of those (where for the indices
i
for which a line/ray has not been assigned one chooses a point in
F_{U_{i}}
instead). This is what we will refer to as
standard
k
-flats (resp., orthants)
, where
k
is the number of lines/rays. The
support
of a standard
k
-flat (resp., orthant) is the set of
U_{i}
for which a line/ray has been assigned. Related to this, a
complete support set
is a subset
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\nu}\subseteq\mathfrak{S}
of pairwise orthogonal indices with all
\mathcal{C}(U_{i})
unbounded, and with maximal possible cardinality
\nu
among sets with these properties. This definition is relevant here as
\nu
is then the maximal dimension of standard orthants (if the
\mathcal{C}(U_{i})
are not only unbounded, but also have non-empty Gromov boundary, as will be the case for us). Moreover, in this case points in the Gromov boundary
\partial C(U_{i})
each determine a hierarchy ray, and similarly pairs of points determine hierarchy lines. In particular, for every complete support set
\{U_{i}\}_{i}=1^{\nu}
and for every choice of distinct points
p_{i}\in\partial\mathcal{C}U_{i}
there exists an associated standard flat
\mathfrak{F}_{\{(U_{i},p_{i}^{\pm})\}}
, which is the product of the hierarchy lines in
F_{U_{i}}
whose projections to
\mathcal{C}U_{i}
have endpoints
p_{i}^{\pm}
.

8.2.
The hinge graph

In this section we discuss the graph that quasi-isometries will induce automorphisms of. We will call it the hinge graph, and it is best thought of, for the purposes of this section, as encoding standard orthants, as well as their coarse intersections. Here, recall that given subsets
A,B
of a metric space
X
, the coarse intersection
A\tilde{\cap}B
, if well-defined, is a subspace of
X
within bounded Hausdorff distance of all
N_{R}(A)\cap N_{R}(B)
for
R
sufficiently large.

Remark 8.3
.

The coarse intersection of any two standard flats
\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathfrak{F}_{2}
is well-defined by
[
BHS21
, Lemma 4.12]
. In fact, the lemma gives a description of the coarse intersection as a subspace
F
of the HHS whose projection to any hyperbolic space
U
is the coarse intersection of
\pi_{U}(\mathfrak{F}_{1})
and
\pi_{U}(\mathfrak{F}_{2})
. For example, the coarse intersection is a standard
1
-flat provided that all coarse intersections in the various hyperbolic spaces are bounded, except for one hyperbolic space where
\pi_{U}(\mathfrak{F}_{1})
and
\pi_{U}(\mathfrak{F}_{2})
are both quasilines with the same endpoints in the Gromov boundary.

Definition 8.4

(Hinge graph)
.

As in
[
BHS21
, Definition 5.2]
, let
\bf{Hinge}(\mathfrak{S})
be the set of
hinges
, that is, pairs
(U,p)
where
U
is contained in a complete support set and
p\in\partial CU
. We say that two hinges
(U,p)
and
(V,q)
are compatible if they are orthogonal and there exists some complete support set that contains both. Then we give
\bf{Hinge}(\mathfrak{S})
a metric graph structure by declaring two hinges to be adjacent if and only if they are compatible.

As in
[
BHS21
, Definition 5.3]
, one can associate to a hinge
\sigma=(U,p)
a standard
1
-orthant, denoted
h_{\sigma}
. This is a hierarchy ray whose projection to
\mathcal{C}(U)
is a quasigeodesic ray asymptotic to
p
, and whose projections to all other coordinate spaces are uniformly bounded. Another property of
h_{\sigma}
, stated in
[
BHS21
, Remark 5.4]
, is that if
\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}
are two different hinges then
d_{Haus}(h_{\sigma},h_{\sigma^{\prime}})=\infty
.

Lemma 8.5
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{S})
be a HHS. If for every

either the space
\mathcal{C}U
is bounded or
|\partial\mathcal{C}U|\geq 4
then:

(1)

For every hinge

there exist two standard flats
\mathfrak{F}_{1},\,\mathfrak{F}_{2}
whose coarse intersection is supported in
U
, is coarsely a standard

-flat, and contains

.

(2)

For any two compatible hinges
\sigma=(U,p)
and
\sigma^{\prime}=(V,q)
there exist two standard flats
\mathfrak{F}_{1},\,\mathfrak{F}_{2}
whose coarse intersection is a standard
2
-flat supported in
\{U,V\}
and containing
h_{\sigma}
and
h_{\sigma^{\prime}}
.

This lemma will replace the three assumptions from
[
BHS21
, Section 5]
.

Proof.

(1)

Choose another ideal point

, and let
\gamma
be a hierarchy path in

whose projection to

has limit points

and

. Then pick a complete support set
\{U_{i}\}
completing

, and for each

choose four distinct ideal points
p_{i}^{\pm},q_{i}^{\pm}\in\mathcal{C}U_{i}
. Let
\gamma_{i},\delta_{i}
be two bi-infinite hierarchy paths in each

whose projection to
\mathcal{C}U_{i}
have limit points, respectively,

and

. Then
\mathfrak{F}_{1}=\gamma\times\prod_{i}\gamma_{i}
and
\mathfrak{F}_{1}=\gamma\times\prod_{i}\delta_{i}
have the required coarse intersection in view of the discussion in Remark
8.3
.

(2)

The proof is very similar, in this case fixing two hierarchy lines rather than one.

∎

Remark 8.6

(Hidden hypothesis: asymphoricity)
.

. For our purposes, it suffices to know that asymphoric HHS include all hierarchically hyperbolic groups, such as our quotients of interest

Proof.

This is a very similar statement to

BHS21
, Theorem 5.7]
, which applies to HHSs satisfying three additional assumptions (not satisfied in our case). An inspection of the proof shows that each use of the three assumptions can be replaced by our Lemma

∎

The following is the analogue of
[

, Lemma 5.9]
, which is a corollary of
[

, Theorem 5.7]
and whose proof does not use the additional assumptions on the HHSs further. Given a quasi-isometry between HHSs, the lemma gives a condition for the image of a standard flat to lie within bounded Hausdorff distance of a standard flat in terms of the induced map

on hinge graphs. Crucially for applications, the bound is only in terms of the HHSs and the quasi-isometry constants.

Lemma 8.8

(Flats go to flats)
.

Let
(X,\mathfrak{S})
,
(Y,\mathfrak{T})
be asymphoric HHSs, such that for every

either the space

is bounded or

, and similarly for every

. Let

be a quasi-isometry. There exists a constant

, depending only on the quasi-isometry constants of

, with the following property. Let

be a complete support set, and let

9.
Quasi-isometric rigidity

This Section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1
. For the rest of the Section, let
S=S_{b}
be a sphere with
b\geq 7
. Let us start from the HHS structure of
MCG^{\pm}/DT_{K}
.

Definition 9.1
.

For
\Delta
,
\Delta^{\prime}
simplices of some graph
G
, we write
\Delta\sim\Delta^{\prime}

to mean
\text{Lk}(\Delta)=\text{Lk}(\Delta^{\prime})
. We denote by
[\Delta]
the
\sim
–equivalence class of
\Delta
, and by
\mathfrak{S}
the set of equivalence classes of non-maximal simplices. Finally, we define the saturation of
\Delta
as the set of vertices
v\in G
for which there exists a simplex
\Delta^{\prime}
such that
v\in\Delta^{\prime}
and
\Delta^{\prime}\sim\Delta
, i.e.

\text{Sat}(\Delta)=\left(\bigcup_{\Delta^{\prime}\in[\Delta]}\Delta^{\prime}\right)^{(0)}.

Remark 9.2
.

By
[
BHMS24
, Theorem 7.1]
,
MCG/DT_{K}
is a hierarchically hyperbolic
group
, that is, it has a ”nice” proper and cocompact action on some hierarchically hyperbolic space from which it inherits a HHS structure (see
[
BHS19
, Definition 1.21]
). Moreover, combining
[
BHMS24
, Theorem 7.1 and Proposition 8.13]
, we see that the HHS structure has the following properties:

(i)

The index set is the set
\mathfrak{S}
of
\sim
–equivalence classes of simplices inside
\mathcal{C}/DT_{K}
;

(ii)

There is a bijection
j:\,\mathfrak{S}\to\mathfrak{S}^{\geq 1}/DT_{K}
, where
\mathfrak{S}^{\geq 1}
is the set of essential, non-annular, possibly disconnected subsurfaces
U\subseteq S
.

(iii)

Two elements
[\overline{\Delta}],[\overline{\Sigma}]\in\mathfrak{S}
are orthogonal (resp. nested) if
j\left([\overline{\Delta}]\right)
and
j\left([\overline{\Delta}]\right)
admit representatives, which we also call
lifts
, that are disjoint (resp. nested);

(iv)

If
\overline{\Delta}
is not a facet, then the coordinate space
\mathcal{C}(\overline{\Delta}):=\mathcal{C}([\overline{\Delta}])
associated to
[\overline{\Delta}]
is quasi-isometric to
\text{Lk}(\overline{\Delta})
(see
[
BHMS24
, Claim 6.12]
). Otherwise, the pointwise stabiliser of the saturation
P(\overline{\Delta}):=\text{Pstab}(\text{Sat}(\overline{\Delta}))
is a hyperbolic group acting properly and cocompactly on
\mathcal{C}(\overline{\Delta})
.

We will use
convex-cocompact subgroups
to show that the
\mathcal{C}(\overline{\Delta})
have at least 4 points at infinity (or they are bounded). This notion was introduced in
[
FM02
]
, and it admits several characterisations. The properties that we will need are that there exist free convex-compact subgroups inside every finite index subgroup (see e.g.
[
FM02
, Theorem 1.4]
), and the following fact from
[
KL08
]
. If a subgroup
Q<MCG(S)
is convex-cocompact then there exists a constant
D
such that, for every element
h\in Q
and for every two curves
x,s\subset S
we have
d_{s}(x,h(x))\leq D
whenever the quantity is defined.

In our context, convex-cocompact subgroups survive in deep enough quotients:

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
S
be a connected surface of finite type and whose complexity is at least
2
. Given a convex-cocompact subgroup
Q<MCG(S)
, for all large multiples
K
the projection
\pi|_{Q}
is injective and the orbit maps of
Q
to
\mathcal{C}(S)/DT_{K}
are quasi-isometric embeddings.

Proof.

This is just
[
BHMS24
, Theorem 7.1.iii]
.
∎

Now, in order for our machinery from Section
8
to work, we must ensure that we are in the assumptions of Theorem
8.7
:

Lemma 9.4
.

For all large multiples
K
the following holds. For any
[\overline{\Delta}]\in\mathfrak{S}
, either the space
\mathcal{C}(\overline{\Delta})
is bounded or it has at least four points at infinity.

Proof.

First, assume that
\overline{\Delta}
has codimension
1
. Then
\mathcal{C}(\overline{\Delta})
is quasi-isometric to the hyperbolic group
P(\overline{\Delta})
, and we claim that there exists a copy of the free group on two generators
F_{2}
inside
P(\overline{\Delta})
. If this is the case then
P(\overline{\Delta})
is an infinite, non virtually cyclic hyperbolic group, hence it has at least four boundary points.

Let
\Delta\subset\mathcal{C}
be a lift of
\overline{\Delta}
. By
[
BHMS24
, Proposition 8.13.vii]
we have that
\pi(\text{Sat}(\Delta))=\text{Sat}(\overline{\Delta})
, therefore
P(\overline{\Delta})
contains the quotient projection of
P(\Delta):=\text{Pstab}(\text{Sat}(\Delta))
. Thus it is enough to show that there is a copy of
F_{2}
inside
P(\Delta)
whose projection to
MCG^{\pm}/DT_{K}
is injective. Now,
\Delta
is a facet in
\mathcal{C}
, thus it cuts out a four-holed sphere
U
. Notice that a curve
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}
is in the link of
\Delta
if and only if
\gamma
lies in
U
and is not one of its boundary curves. Hence, a simplex
\Delta^{\prime}
which has the same link of
\Delta
must be a pants decomposition for
S\setminus U
, including its boundary curves. This in turn means that, if we see
U
just as a four-punctured sphere
S_{4}
(that is, we forget about the difference between punctures and boundary curves), then
P(\Delta)
contains the pure mapping class group
PMCG(S_{4})
.

Inside
PMCG(S_{4})
, which is finite-index inside
MCG(S_{4})
, we may find a convex-cocompact copy of
F_{2}
, call it
H
. Suppose by contradiction that
\pi|_{H}
is not injective, which means that there is some
h\in H\cap DT_{K}\setminus\{1\}
. Now
h
can not fix every curve, since the only elements of
MCG(S_{4})
with this property are the hyperelliptic involutions, which permute the punctures. Thus let
x\subseteq U
be a curve such that
h(x)\neq x
and let
(s,\gamma_{s})
be as in Proposition
2.3
. Notice that
h(x)
still lies on
U
, therefore both
x
and
h(x)
complete
\Delta
to maximal simplices.

Now, suppose by contradiction that
d_{\mathcal{C}}(x,s)>1
, and therefore
d_{s}(x,h(x))>\Theta
. If we argue as in Lemma
2.8
we get that
\gamma_{s}
must fix
\Delta
pointwise. Hence
s
is disjoint from all curves in
\Delta
, but
s\not\in\Delta
since otherwise
\gamma_{s}
, which is a power of
T_{s}
, would fix
x
. Therefore
s
lies in
U
, and by convex-cocompactness of
H
there exists a constant
D
(which we can choose independently of
U
) such that
d_{s}(x,h(x))\leq D
. This is a contradiction if
\Theta>D
, which happens for all large multiples
K
.

Then we must have that
d_{\mathcal{C}}(x,s)\leq 1
, i.e.
x
must be fixed by
\gamma_{s}
, and we can apply
\gamma_{s}
to the whole data and proceed by induction on the complexity of
h
. In the end we must have that
h=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\gamma_{s_{i}}
and every
\gamma_{s_{i}}
fixes
x
. But then
h(x)=x
, which contradicts our hypothesis. Thus we proved the Lemma for
\mathcal{C}(\overline{\Delta})
whenever
\overline{\Delta}
has codimension
1
.

Now suppose that
\overline{\Delta}
is not a facet. Then
\mathcal{C}(\overline{\Delta})
is quasi-isometric to
\text{Lk}(\overline{\Delta})
, which is the projection of
\text{Lk}(\Delta)
for some lift
\Delta
. Notice that
\text{Lk}(\Delta)=\mathcal{C}(U)
is the curve graph of the subsurface
U
cut out by
\Delta
. If
U
has at least two connected components which are not pairs of pants, then
\mathcal{C}(U)
is bounded, and so is its projection. Otherwise
U
is some connected subsurface of complexity at least
2
, and
\mathcal{C}(\overline{\Delta})
is the image of the curve graph
\mathcal{C}(U)
under the quotient map, which is isomorphic to
\mathcal{C}(U)/DT_{K}(U)
by Corollary
3.2
.

Now our goal has become to show that, for every surface
U
of complexity at least
2
, the quotient of the curve graph
\mathcal{C}(U)/DT_{K}(U)
has at least four boundary points. But now we can just apply Lemma
9.3
: if we choose a convex-cocompact copy of
F_{2}
inside
MCG(U)
then its projection is still a copy of
F_{2}
whose orbit maps to
\mathcal{C}(U)/DT_{K}(U)
are quasi-isometric embeddings, and we are done.
∎

Thus we get:

Corollary 9.5

(of Theorem
8.7
)
.

Every quasi-isometry
f:\,MCG/DT_{K}\to MCG/DT_{K}
induces an automorphism

of the hinge graph, such that for all
\sigma\in\bf{Hinge}(\mathfrak{S})
we have

.

9.1.
Complete support sets for the quotient

Moving forward, our next goal is to understand the structure of complete support sets. For the following theorems we think of
\mathfrak{S}
as the set of classes of subsurfaces (thus omitting the bijection
j
whenever possible).

Lemma 9.6
.

The following holds for all large multiples
K
. Let
\left\{\overline{U}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{r}
be a collection of pairwise orthogonal indices. Then there exist pairwise disjoint representatives
\left\{U_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{r}

Notice that this lemma is not at all obvious: we just know that every two indices in
\left\{\overline{U}_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{r}

\overline{U}_{i}

9.6
.

We proceed by induction on

,

to

and

are disjoint, we have a join

C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{1}^{\prime}

to

Lemma 9.7
.

For all large multiples
K
the following holds. Let
\left\{\overline{U_{i}}\right\}_{i=1}^{r}

.

We proceed by induction on
r
, the base case

then, up to some element

, we can assume that

for

. Now let
h\in DT_{K}
be an element that maps
U_{r}
to

, and let

be sets of filling curves such that

. If
h
is not the identity, fix a point

. If

; otherwise

The main reason we established Lemmas

and

is that every complete support set for

lifts to a collection of pairwise orthogonal

and
S_{5}
of the maximal cardinality, which we call a

for the surface

; moreover, such a collection is unique up to the action of

.

Recall that a surface is

if its complexity is odd, otherwise it is

(in our case,
S_{b}
is odd iff

is even). Bowditch
[

, Section 6]
pointed out that if a subsurface

belongs to a complete support set for

, then

must have one of the following shapes, which ensure that every complete support set containing

can cut out at most one pair of pants.

•

If
S
is odd, then
U
is an
S_{4}
, and each connected component of the complement
S-U
is odd and meets
U
in exactly one curve.

•

If
S
is even then
U
is either an
S_{4}
with all but one of the complementary components odd, or an
S_{5}
with all complementary components odd.

Remark 9.8

(Cutting out a pair of pants)
.

Notice that the only case where
U
does not admit a complete support set whose union is the whole
S
is when
S
is even and the even connected component of
S-U
is a pair of pants. Indeed:

•

If
S
is odd, every connected component of
S-U
is an odd sphere, and can be cut into four-holed spheres. The same holds if
S
is even and
U
is an
S_{5}
.

•

If
S
is even and
U
is an
S_{4}
, we can fill the odd complementary components with four-holed spheres as above. Then, if the even component is not a pair of pants then it must be a sphere with at least five holes, and one can fill it with an
S_{5}
and some
S_{4}
.

9.2.
Unambiguous domains

Let
\sigma=(\overline{U},p)
be a hinge for
MCG/DT_{K}
, and let
\text{Compl}(\sigma)
be the set of all tuples of pairwise orthogonal hinges that
\sigma
completes to a complete support set. In other words,
\text{Compl}(\sigma)
is the set of all facets in
\bf{Hinge}(\mathfrak{S})
that
\sigma
completes to a maximal simplex.

Definition 9.9
.

Two hinges
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
are said to be
equally completable
, or to have the same completions, if
\text{Compl}(\sigma)=\text{Compl}(\sigma^{\prime})
.

This definition clearly induces an equivalence relation which is preserved by any automorphism of the hinge graph. One would like to think that if two hinges
(\overline{U},p)
and
(\overline{U}^{\prime},p^{\prime})
are equally completable then
\overline{U}=\overline{U}^{\prime}
. This is unfortunately not the case. Indeed, if
S
is even and
\overline{U}
is the class of a subsurface
U
which is an
S_{4}
whose even complementary component is a pair of pants
P
, then
\overline{U}
has the same completions as the class of the
S_{5}
given by the union of
U
and
P
. See Figure
28
to understand the situation. Luckily, we will see that this is the only problem that could arise.

Figure 28.
The classes of the
S_{4}
and the
S_{5}
in the Figure cannot be distinguished just by their completions, all of which lift inside the union of the odd complementary regions
\Sigma_{1}
,
\Sigma_{2}
, and
\Sigma_{3}
.

Definition 9.10
.

A hinge
\sigma
is
unambiguous
if any other equally completable hinge
\sigma^{\prime}
has the same support. A support
\overline{U}
is unambiguous is every hinge supported on
U
is unambiguous.

We need another definition that characterises (some) unambiguous domains, and can be recognised from the hinge graph.

Definition 9.11
.

A hinge
\sigma
is
minimal
if
\text{Compl}(\sigma)
is maximal by inclusion, among completions.

Lemma 9.12
.

For all large multiples
K
, a hinge
(\overline{U},p)
for
MCG/DT_{K}
is minimal if and only if it is unambiguous and its support is the class of a four-holed sphere.

Proof.

Let
U
be a lift of
\overline{U}
. We examine all possible shapes of
U
.

•

Suppose
U
is an
S_{5}
, and choose some subsurface
V\sqsubsetneq U
such that
V
is an
S_{4}
and
S-V
does not contain a pair of pants. Such a
V
exists, as one can choose a pair of pants
Q
whose boundary intersects the boundary of
S-U
, and set
V=U-Q
. Every completion for
\overline{U}
admits representatives that are disjoint from
U
by Lemma
9.6
, and therefore also from
V
. Thus every completion for
\overline{U}
is also a completion for
\overline{V}
. Moreover we claim that there exists a completion
\{\overline{V}_{i}\}
for
\overline{V}
but not for
\overline{U}
. To this purpose choose a completion
\{V_{i}\}
for
V
, such that some boundary curve
\delta
crosses one of the boundary components
\eta
of
U
, and let
\{\overline{V}_{i}\}
be their classes. Now recall that, as a consequence of Lemma
3.3
, if we fix some finite subset
F\subset\mathcal{C}
then for all large multiples
K
the projection map is an isometry on
g(F)
, for every mapping class
g\in MCG
. In our case, there is a finite number of
S_{5}
inside
S
, up to the action of the mapping class group, and for each of these we can find
\delta
and
\eta
as above. Then we set
F
as the union of these curves.
Now, any lift
\{V_{i}\}
of
\{\overline{V}_{i}\}
must intersect
U
. More precisely,
\eta
must cross the boundary curve
\delta^{\prime}
corresponding to
\delta
, since

d_{\mathcal{C}}(\delta^{\prime},\eta)\geq d_{\mathcal{C}/DT_{K}}(\overline{\delta^{\prime}},\overline{\eta})=d_{\mathcal{C}/DT_{K}}(\overline{\delta},\overline{\eta})=d_{\mathcal{C}}(\delta,\eta)\geq 2

where we used that the projection is
1
-Lipschitz and that its restriction to
F
is isometric. This shows that
\overline{U}
is not minimal.

•

Suppose
U
is an
S_{4}
which cuts out a pair of pants, as in Figure

. Let
V
be the
S_{5}
given by the union of
U
and the pair of pants cut out by

. Then, slightly abusing notation, we have that
\text{Compl}(\overline{U})=\text{Compl}(\overline{V})
. Indeed, every support
\overline{W}
which completes
\overline{V}
also completes
\overline{W}
; conversely, if
\overline{W}
completes
\overline{U}
then it admits a lift

inside one of the complementary components of

, which is therefore disjoint from
V
. Thus
\overline{U}
is ambiguous. Moreover, by the above argument
\overline{V}
is not minimal as it is an

, and therefore neither is
\overline{U}
which has the same completions as
\overline{V}
.

•

The only case left is when

is an
S_{4}
which does not cut out a pair of pants. Choose a completion
\{U_{i}\}
such that

, as in Remark

, and let

9.3.

Remark 9.13
.

Any automorphism of the hinge graph must map minimal hinges with the same completions to minimal hinges with the same completions, therefore it acts on the set of minimal supports. Thus, if

Corollary 9.14

(Products go to products)

9.5.
Quasi-isometric rigidity

We are finally ready to prove quasi-isometric rigidity of

, which is Theorem
1
. We subdivide the proof in two steps.

Theorem 9.20
.

Let
S=S_{0,b}
be a punctured sphere, with

the following holds. For every

there exists

-self-quasi-isometry

of the left multiplication by some element

Let

be a self-quasi-isometry of

, and let

be the induced map on minimal supports. With a slight abuse of notation, the restriction of

which comes from some element

tells us that there exists some constant

.

We are left to prove that every point

and

. Notice that it is enough to show that there is

we are left to prove that there exist two complete support sets

Firstly, fix a complete support set

If

.

If

to a complete support set.

be a lift of

such that every boundary curve of

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem

Theorem 10.1
.

For every

Definition 10.2

Two elements

, if there exist

We denote by

the set of loxodromic WPD elements.

The following result is a special case of

.

Lemma 10.5
.

Let
G=MCG^{\pm}(S_{b})/DT_{K}
for
b\geq 7
. For every isomorphism
\phi:\,H\to H^{\prime}
between finite index subgroups and every element
h\in H
of infinite order,

and
\phi(h)
are commensurable.

Proof.

Fix a finite generating set for
G
, and let

be the corresponding word metric. Since

has finite index there exists a quasi-isometry

, which we can choose to be the identity on

(for example, every

can be sent to one of the closest elements of

). Let
\Phi=\phi\circ f:\,G\to H^{\prime}
, which coincides with
\phi
on

and is a self-quasi-isometry of

because

is an isomorphism between finite index subgroups. Then by Theorem

there exist a constant

and an element

such that
\Phi
is
D

. In particular
d_{G}(g,1)=d_{G}(g,\Phi(1))\leq D
, and since the word metric is invariant under left multiplication we also have that

. Then
\Phi
is also

, since for every

(here we used that

). But now

, Proposition 9.4]
states that, whenever

are two subgroups, for every

Lemma 10.6
.

Let

be a group acting coboundedly, non-elementarily on a hyperbolic space

, and suppose the action admits loxodromic WPD elements. Let

For every element

The following statement is well-known to experts, but we provide a proof since we could not find a suitable reference.

Lemma 10.7
.

Let
S=S_{g,b}
be a surface of finite type with genus

and

punctures, with
(g,b)\not\in\{(0,2),(0,3),(0,4),(1,0),(1,1),(1,2),(2,0)\}

Let

be a finite normal subgroup and let

. If

fixes every isotopy class of simple closed curves then it must be the identity, for example by Ivanov’s Theorem
[
Iva97
, Theorem 1]
and its extension to lower genera
[
Kor99
, Theorem 1]
. Then suppose by contradiction that
f(\gamma)\neq\gamma
for some curve

, and let

be the corresponding Dehn twist. Since

is normal there exists

such that

. Thus, using how Dehn twists behave under conjugation, we have
T_{f(\gamma)}^{\pm 2}=fT_{\gamma}^{2}f^{-1}=T_{\gamma}^{2}gf^{-1}
, where the sign depends on whether

is orientation preserving or reversing. This can be rewritten as

(4)

T_{\gamma}^{-2}T_{f(\gamma)}^{\pm 2}=gf^{-1}.

Now, referring to the table at the end of

we see that

and

are the generators of a subgroup isomorphic to either

, hence the left-hand side of (

) has infinite order. This is impossible, since the right-hand side is an element of the finite subgroup

.

Appendix A
Quasi-isometric rigidity of pants graphs of spheres

This appendix, which is as self-contained as possible, contains a proof of the following result:

Theorem A.1

(Quasi-isometric rigidity of pants graphs of spheres)
.

Let
S_{b}
be a punctured sphere, with
b\geq 7
. Any self-quasi-isometry
f
of the pants graph
\mathbb{P}(S_{b})
is at uniformly bounded distance from an element of the extended mapping class group.

This partly recovers a result of Bowditch
[
Bow20
, Theorem 1.4]
with our machinery, though the proof will not be completely new since we will still rely on some results from
[
Bow20
, Sections 6 and 7]
and on the main theorem of
[
Bow16
]
. Indeed, the ”source” of rigidity here will be the fact that automorphisms of a certain graph, the graph of 1-separating curves, can only be extended mapping classes
[
Bow20
,
Bow16
]
. Hence, our goal is to show that automorphisms of the hinge graph induce automorphisms of this other graph, Corollary
A.12
, and in order to show this the key thing to do is roughly the following. Since hinges are not just subsurfaces, but rather pairs
(U,p)
where
U
is a subsurface and
p
is a point in the boundary of its curve graph, we have to be able to combinatorially determine which hinges have the same support subsurface. In fact, we will only do so for ”enough” hinges.

A.1.
Unambiguous subsurfaces

Let
\mathbb{P}(S_{b})
be the pants graph of
S_{b}
, whose HHS structure is given as follows (see
[
BHS17b
, Theorem G]
):

•

\mathfrak{S}
is the set of essential, non-annular subsurfaces;

•

\perp
is disjointness and
\sqsubseteq
is inclusion;

•

for every
U\in\mathfrak{S}
,
\mathcal{C}U
is the curve graph;

•

Projections are defined using subsurface projections.

Remark A.2
.

Notice that if
U\in\mathfrak{S}
is a connected subsurface then its curve graph has infinite diameter (see e.g.
[
MM99
, Proposition 3.6]
). Thus if
U\in\mathfrak{S}
has bounded curve graph then it is the disconnected union of subsurfaces of positive complexity, and its curve graph is a non-trivial join. Hence every bounded curve graph has diameter at most
2
, and therefore the HHS structure of the pants graph satisfies the mysterious asymphoricity assumption from Remark
8.6
.

Bowditch
[
Bow20
, Section 6]
pointed out that if
U
belongs to a complete support set then
U
must have one of the following shapes, which ensure that every complete support set containing
U
can cut out at most one pair of pants from
S
. Recall that a surface is
odd
if its complexity is odd, otherwise it is
even
(in our case,
S_{b}
is odd iff
b
is even). If
S
is odd then
U
is an
S_{4}
and each component of the complement is odd and meets
U
in exactly one curve. If
S
is even then
U
is either:

(1)

an
S_{4}
with all but one of the complementary components odd;

(2)

an
S_{5}
with all complementary components odd.

Notice that the curve graphs of both
S_{4}
and
S_{5}
have at least four points at infinity, so we are always in the assumptions of Theorem
8.7
. Thus, a quasi-isometry
f
of
\mathbb{P}(S_{b})
induces an automorphism
f_{hin}
of the hinge graph, and we want to show that
f_{hin}
maps hinges with the same underlying subsurface to hinges with the same underlying subsurface, at least in the vast majority of cases.

Given a hinge
\sigma=(U,p)
let
\text{Compl}(\sigma)
be the set of all tuples of pairwise orthogonal hinges that
\sigma
completes to a complete support set. In other words,
\text{Compl}(\sigma)
is the set of all facets in
\bf{Hinge}(\mathfrak{S})
that
\sigma
completes to a maximal simplex.

Definition A.3
.

Two hinges
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}
are said to be
equally completable
, or to have the same completions, if
\text{Compl}(\sigma)=\text{Compl}(\sigma^{\prime})
.

This definition clearly induces an equivalence relation which is preserved by any automorphism of the hinge graph. One would like to think that if two hinges are equally completable then they have the same underlying subsurface. However this is not always true: in the even case, an
S_{4}
whose even complementary component is a pair of pants
P
has the same completions as the
S_{5}
given by the union of
S_{4}
and
P
. See Figure
29
to understand the situation. Luckily we will see that this is the only problem that could arise.

Figure 29.
The
S_{4}
and the
S_{5}
in the Figure cannot be distinguished just by their completions, all of which must lie in the union of the
\Sigma_{i}

Definition A.4
.

A hinge
\sigma
is

if any other equally completable hinge
\sigma^{\prime}
has the same support. A support
U
is unambiguous is every hinge supported in
U
is unambiguous.

We need another definition that characterises (some) unambiguous surfaces and can be recognised from the hinge graph.

Definition A.5
.

A hinge

is

if
\text{Compl}(\sigma)
is maximal by inclusion, among completions.

Lemma A.6

Let

. In the odd case, every hinge is minimal and unambiguous. In the even case, a hinge is minimal if and only if it is unambiguous and its support is a four-holed sphere.

Let

(Products go to products)

.

∎

Lemma A.9
.

There exists
C_{3}\geq 0
such that whenever
U\in\mathfrak{S}
is a minimal domain and

, there exists a hierarchy line

such that

.

Proof.

Since
P_{\{U_{i}\}}=\prod F_{U_{i}}
it suffices to work in each coordinate, that is, we will show that there exists a constant
C_{3}
such that whenever
U\in\mathfrak{S}
is a minimal domain and
x\in F_{U}
, there exists a hierarchy line
\gamma\subset F_{U}
such that
d_{F_{U}}(x,\gamma)\leq C_{3}
. Notice that, by the distance formula and the fact that

is an

\sqsubseteq

, and hierarchy lines simply correspond to bi-infinite quasigeodesics (with certain constants). Moreover

acts on

∎

A.2.
Automorphism of terminal subsurfaces

Definition A.10
.

A support
U
is
terminal
if it has complexity
1
and it is cut out by a single curve. We say that the boundary curve of a terminal support is
1
-separating
, and we denote by
\mathcal{C}^{1}
the full subgraph of
\mathcal{C}
spanned by
1
-separating curves.

Lemma A.11
.

A hinge
\sigma=(U,p)
has terminal support if and only if it is minimal and there exists a hinge
(V,q)
, compatible with
\sigma
, such that any complete support set containing
(V,q)
must contain some
\sigma^{\prime}
which has the same completions as
\sigma
(i.e., it has the same support). In particular, having terminal support is preserved by automorphisms of the hinge graph.

Proof.

This is just a restatement in our context of
[
Bow20
, Lemma 6.3]
. We just have to notice that a terminal hinge is minimal, which follows from the discussion of Lemma
A.6
.
∎

Corollary A.12
.

If
b\geq 7
, any automorphism of the hinge graph induces an automorphism of
\mathcal{C}^{1}
.

Proof.

Any automorphism must map terminal supports to terminal supports and must preserve compatibility (which, for terminal subsurface, is equivalent to disjointness of their boundaries).
∎

Proof of Theorem
A.1
.

The previous discussion shows that
f
induces an automorphism of
\mathcal{C}^{1}
, which is the restriction of some extended mapping class
g\in MCG^{\pm}
when
b\geq 7
(see
[
Bow20
, Section 7]
and
[
Bow16
]
for a proof, which is ultimately an application of Ivanov’s Theorem). In other words,
f_{supp}
and
g
agree on terminal subsurfaces, and we want to show that they coincide on every minimal support
U
. We recall that, as showed in the proof of Lemma
A.6
, every complementary components
\Sigma
of
U
has complexity at least
1
, i.e.
U
does not cut out any pair of pants. Now, if
\Sigma
has complexity
1
then it is a terminal subsurface; otherwise
\Sigma
has complexity at least
2
, and therefore there exist two terminal subsurfaces inside
\Sigma
whose boundary curves fill
\Sigma

U
is the unique minimal support that is disjoint from all these terminal subsurfaces, and therefore
f_{supp}
and
g
must agree on

since they both preserve disjointness.

By Corollary
A.8
if
\{U_{i}\}
is a complete support set made of minimal supports then
f
maps the corresponding product region

within uniformly bounded Hausdorff distance from
g(P_{\{U_{i}\}})

P_{f_{supp}\{U_{i}\}}=P_{g\{U_{i}\}}
.

We are left to prove that every point

is the (uniform) coarse intersection of two standard product regions

, coming from minimal complete support sets. If this is the case then

and
f(P^{\prime})

g(P)
and
g(P^{\prime})
, which is coarsely

. In order to prove this, it is enough to show that there is some point

which is the coarse intersection of two standard product regions with minimal supports, since the mapping class group acts cocompactly on
\mathbb{P}(S_{b})
. In turn, by Corollary
8.2
we are left to prove that there exist two complete support sets
\{U_{i}\}
and
\{V_{i}\}
, with minimal supports, whose indices are pairwise distinct (notice that we can apply Corollary
8.2
since minimal indices are

\tilde{U}

∎
