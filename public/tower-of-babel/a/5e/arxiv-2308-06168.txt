Quantifying and estimating dependence via sensitivity of conditional distributions
Jonathan Ansari, Patrick B. Langthaler, Sebastian Fuchs, Wolfgang Trutschnig

Abstract
Recently established, directed dependence measures for pairs ( X , Y ) (X,Y) of random variables build upon the natural idea of comparing the conditional distributions of Y Y given X = x X=x with the marginal distribution of Y Y . They assign pairs ( X , Y ) (X,Y) values in [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] , the value is 0 0 if and only if X , Y X,Y are independent, and it is 1 1 exclusively for Y Y being a function of X X . Here we show that comparing randomly drawn conditional distributions with each other instead or, equivalently, analyzing how sensitive the conditional distribution of Y Y given X = x X=x is on x x , opens the door to constructing novel families of dependence measures Λ φ \Lambda_{\varphi} induced by general convex functions φ : ℝ → ℝ \varphi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R} , containing, e.g., Chatterjee’s coefficient of correlation as special case. After establishing additional useful properties of Λ φ \Lambda_{\varphi} we focus on continuous ( X , Y ) (X,Y) , translate Λ φ \Lambda_{\varphi} to the copula setting, consider the L p L^{p} -version and establish an estimator which is strongly consistent in full generality. A real data example and a simulation study illustrate the chosen approach and the performance of the estimator. Complementing the afore-mentioned results, we show how a slight modification of the construction underlying Λ φ \Lambda_{\varphi} can be used to define new measures of explainability generalizing the fraction of explained variance.



keywords
dependence measure, sensitivity, conditional distribution, Chatterjee’s correlation coefficient, explainability, copula

Introduction

In classical statistics comparing two groups/populations is usually done by
considering specific parameters like the mean or the variance of the two
distributions
\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{1}}
and
\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{2}}\,
. In other words, one studies expressions of the form
\Delta(r(x_{1}),r(x_{2}))
or
\Delta(v(x_{1}),v(x_{2}))
for some non-negative functional/distance
\Delta\,,
where
r(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y|X=x]
denotes the regression function (conditional expectation) and
v(x)=\mathbb{V}(Y|X=x)
the conditional variance
of
Y
given
X=x
, respectively.
Moving from the two-groups setting (modelled by a binary random variable
X
) to the general case
of an arbitrary conditioning variable
X\,,
it seems natural to examine how much the conditional
distribution of
Y
given
X
changes if
X
changes, i.e., how sensitive the conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x}
of
Y
given
X=x

is on
x
. Intuitively, knowing the extent of sensitivity should provide information on how much information on

Y
we gain by knowing
X
. Taking into account the distribution of the conditioning variable
X
,
one natural approach for quantifying sensitivity is to consider the average/expected value of
\Delta(\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{1}},\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{2}})
for a sample
x_{1},x_{2}
of
X
. Writing

\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X}
for the product measure of
\mathbb{P}^{X}
with itself one could therefore consider functionals of the form

\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X):=\alpha_{\Delta}^{-1}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Delta(\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{1}},\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{2}})\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X})(x_{1},x_{2}),

(1)

for some normalising constant
\alpha_{\Delta}>0\,.

As a matter of fact, various well-known measures can be represented in the form (
1
), including the portion of explained variance or Chatterjee’s famous coefficient of rank correlation, see Section
2
. Comparing conditional distributions is
certainly not a new idea, see, e.g.,

[
11
,
12
,
31
]
in the context of differential privacy.
However, to the best of our knowledge, the (recently fast growing) literature on dependence measures (also known as measures of predictability, i.e., measures attaining values in [0,1] and being minimal/maximal exclusively for the case of independence/perfect dependence) mainly focuses on comparing conditional and unconditional distributions,
so on expressions of the form

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Delta(\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x},\mathbb{P}^{Y})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}(x)\,,

(2)

where
\Delta
refers either to a
L^{2}
-distance (see, e.g.,
[
2
,
4
,
6
,
13
,
15
,
34
,
36
,
9
]
),
the
L^{1}
-distance (see, e.g.,
[
18
,
22
,
37
]
),
some optimal transport cost function (see, e.g.,
[
29
]
),
the maximum mean discrepancy (see, e.g.,
[
20
]
), or the Wasserstein distance (see, e.g.,
[
38
]
).

On the one hand, for
\Delta
denoting the

L^{2}
-distance between univariate distribution functions the quantities in (
1
) and (
2
) can be shown to coincide, see Example
1
in the next section. On the other hand, in general these expressions may
differ, so considering functionals of the form
\Lambda_{\Delta}
leads to novel measures of association and, in particular, to new dependence measures, which are the main focus of this contribution.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Section
2

first shows that Chatterjee’s dependence measure can be represented in the form
\Lambda_{\Delta}
and proposes a family of functionals
\Delta=\Delta_{\varphi}
based on
convex functions
\varphi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Before focusing on these measures
for the rest of the paper, we show that using the same construction but replacing the
conditional distributions by the regression functions leads to a large class of
so-called explainability measures which can be seen as generalization of the well-known
fraction of explained variance.
Section
3
proves that the afore-mentioned functionals
\Lambda_{\Delta_{\varphi}}=\Lambda_{\varphi}
are in fact dependence measures, studies
further (invariance and order) properties, then focuses on continuous
(X,Y)
, and translates the dependence measures to the copula setting.
Based on these results, in Section
4
a copula-based checkerboard
estimator is proposed and
shown to be strongly consistent in full generality, i.e., without any smoothness restrictions on the underlying bivariate copulas/distributions. A simulation study
illustrating the performance of the estimator complemented by a read data example in Section
5
round off the paper.

In what follows we always consider real-valued general random variables
X,Y
defined on a common probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})\,
. We will refer to

X
as the exogenous and to
Y
as the endogenous variable and assume that
the latter is non-degenerated, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}^{Y}
is not a one-point distribution.
Furthermore we will write
X^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}X
if, and only if
X^{\prime}
and
X
have the same distribution, i.e., if
\mathbb{P}^{X^{\prime}}=\mathbb{P}^{X}
holds.

2
General setting and motivating examples

Let
\mathcal{M}
denote the class of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}\,.

For studying the sensitivity of conditional distributions
we consider functionals
\Delta:\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
that may satisfy several desirable and natural properties such as symmetry, i.e.,
\Delta(\mu,\nu)=\Delta(\nu,\mu)
for all
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}\,,
or positive definiteness,
i.e.,
\Delta(\mu,\nu)\geq 0
for all
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{M}
with equality if and only
if
\mu=\nu\,.

Given a bivariate random vector
(X,Y)
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})\,,
the mapping
\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)
is defined according to (
1
),
whereby we implicitly assume that the mapping
(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto\Delta\big(\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{1}},\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{2}}\big)
is integrable. Moreoever, for the rest of this paper the normalising
constant
\alpha_{\Delta}
is defined by

\displaystyle\alpha_{\Delta}:=\sup\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Delta(\mathbb{P}^{Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{1}},\mathbb{P}^{Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{2}})\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X^{\prime}})(x_{1},x_{2}):\,(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}(Y)\right\}

(3)

where
\mathcal{R}(Y)
denotes the family of all random vectors
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
with

Y^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}Y
. Simple expressions for
\alpha_{\Delta}
will be derived later on.
Whenever the supremum is positive and finite we obviously have that

\Lambda_{\Delta}\in[0,1]
. By definition,
\Lambda_{\Delta}

assigns every bivariate random vector
(X,Y)
a non-negative number
which depends only on the distribution of
(X,Y).

In the sequel we study properties of the functional
\Lambda_{\Delta}
for several classes of
\Delta
and characterize in particular the maximal elements determining
the value of the constant
\alpha_{\Delta}\,.

Notice that for
\Delta
being positive definite obviously
\Lambda_{\Delta}
characterizes independence since independence of
X
and
Y
means that
the conditional distributions
(\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}}
do not depend on
x
,
which for such
\Delta
is equivalent to
\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)=0
.

Remark 2.1
.

Considering the special case of
\Delta
being a metric on
\mathcal{M}
, the functional

\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)
boils down to the average
\Delta
-distance of
two randomly (according to
\mathbb{P}^{X}
) drawn conditional distributions

\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{1}},\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{2}}
.
If, for example,
\Delta
is the Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W},
then the functional in (
1
) is given by

\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)=\alpha_{\Delta}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathcal{W}(\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{1}},\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{2}})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X}(x_{1},x_{2})
, see
[
38
]
for measures of association with Wasserstein distances of
the form (
2
). For a discussion of the case where
\Delta
corresponds to
L^{p}
-metrics we refer to Section
3.3
.

The subsequent two examples illustrate the broadness of the
\Lambda_{\Delta}
-approach according to (
1
) in the context of quantifying (directed) dependence
as well as explainability in terms of the sensitivity of conditional distributions.
They show that well-known statistical concepts can either
be expressed in terms of (
1
) with adequately chosen
\Delta
.

Example 1

(Chatterjee’s coefficient of correlation)
.

Consider
\Delta
given by

\displaystyle\Delta\big(\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{1}},\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{2}}\big)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\mathbb{P}(Y\geq y|X=x_{1})-\mathbb{P}(Y\geq y|X=x_{2})\Big)^{2}\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\,.

Then
\Lambda_{\Delta}
defined by (
1
) coincides with Chatterjee’s famous coefficient of correlation (see
[
6
,
9
]
)
since using change of coordinates we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{\Delta}^{-1}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\mathbb{P}(Y\geq y|X=x_{1})-\mathbb{P}(Y\geq y|X=x_{2})\Big)^{2}\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X})(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,\alpha_{\Delta}^{-1}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(Y\geq y|X=x)^{2}\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}(x)-\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(Y\geq y|X=x)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}(x)\right)^{2}\right)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{P}(Y\geq y|X))\,\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{V}(\mathds{1}_{\{Y\geq y\}})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)}.

Thereby according to the afore-mentioned references again using change of
coordinates and Fubini’s theorem, and letting
Y_{1},Y_{2},Y
be i.i.d., the normalizing constant
\alpha_{\Delta}

simplifies to (also see Theorem
3.2
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\alpha_{\Delta}&=\left(2\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{V}(\mathds{1}_{\{Y\geq y\}})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\right)\\
&=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\mathds{1}_{[y,\infty)}(Y_{1}(\omega))-\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\geq y)\Big)^{2}+\Big(\mathds{1}_{[y,\infty)}(Y_{2}(\omega))-\mathbb{P}(Y_{2}\geq y)\Big)^{2}\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\right)\\
&=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\Omega}\Big(\mathds{1}_{[y,\infty)}(Y_{1}(\omega))-\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}\geq y)-\mathds{1}_{[y,\infty)}(Y_{2}(\omega))+\mathbb{P}(Y_{2}\geq y)\Big)^{2}\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}(\omega)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\right)\\
&=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\Big(\mathds{1}_{[y,\infty)}(y_{1})-\mathds{1}_{[y,\infty)}(y_{2})\Big)^{2}\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Y}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y})(y_{1},y_{2})\right)\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\Delta\left(\delta_{y_{1}},\delta_{y_{2}}\right)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})\,,\end{split}

(4)

and where
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac measure at
x\in\mathbb{R}\,.

Hence, by the properties of Chatterjee’s coefficient of correlation, the above
choice of
\Delta
yields that the functional
\Lambda_{\Delta}
is a measure
of dependence, i.e.,

\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)\in[0,1]
,
\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)=0
if and only if
X
and
Y
are independent, and
\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)=1
exclusively
if
Y
is completely dependent on
X\,,
i.e., there exists some measurable function
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
with
Y=f(X)
almost surely.

Motivated by the above example, Section
3
introduces and investigates a
large family of functionals
\Lambda_{\Delta}
, where
\Delta
depends only on the difference of conditional distribution functions rescaled by some measurable
function
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\varphi}\big(\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{1}},\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{2}}\big)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\left(F_{Y|X=x_{1}}(y)-F_{Y|X=x_{2}}(y)\right)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)

(5)

We will show in Theorem
3.2
that for
\varphi
being convex and
strictly convex in
0=\varphi(0)
the functional
\Lambda_{\Delta_{\varphi}}=:\Lambda_{\varphi}

is a dependence measures (with normalizing constant
\alpha_{\Delta_{\varphi}}
according to (
4
)).

Example 2

(Cramér-von-Mises indices; fraction of explained variance)
.

Suppose that
Y
is square-integrable.
Denote by
r(x):=\mathbb{E}(Y|X=x)
the regression function
of
Y
given
X=x
, and consider

\displaystyle\Delta\big(\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{1}},\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{2}}\big)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}(Y|X=x_{1})-\mathbb{E}(Y|X=x_{2})\right)^{2}=(r(x_{1})-r(x_{2}))^{2}\,.

Then the normalizing constant
\alpha_{\Delta}
is given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{\Delta}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(y_{1}-y_{2}\right)^{2}\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Y}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y})(y_{1},y_{2})\right)=2\,\mathbb{V}(Y)

and the functional
\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)
in (
1
) coincides with the fraction of explained variance of
Y
given
X
,
also known as Sobol index or Cramér-von-Mises index (see
[
15
]
), i.e.,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{\Delta}^{-1}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(\mathbb{E}(Y|X=x_{1})-\mathbb{E}(Y|X=x_{2})\right)^{2}\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X})(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,\alpha_{\Delta}^{-1}\,\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}r^{2}(x)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}(x)-\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}r(x)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}(x)\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,\alpha_{\Delta}^{-1}\,\left(\mathbb{E}((r\circ X)^{2})-(\mathbb{E}(r\circ X))^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,\alpha_{\Delta}^{-1}\,\mathbb{V}(r\circ X)=\frac{\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{E}(Y|X))}{\mathbb{V}(Y)}=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mathbb{E}(Y|X=x)-\mathbb{E}(Y)\right)^{2}\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}(x)}{\mathbb{V}(Y)}\,.

Recall that in this case

\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)\in[0,1]
and we have

\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)=0
if, and only if
\mathbb{V}(r\circ X)=0
(which is the case if
X
and
Y
are independent but not vice versa), and

\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)=1
if, and only if
Y
is completely dependent on
X
.

In the case of an affine regression function
r(x)=ax+b
,
the quantity
\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)
then coincides with the squared Pearson
correlation
\rho
of
X
and
Y
, i.e.

\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)=\frac{(Cov(X,Y))^{2}}{\mathbb{V}(X)\mathbb{V}(Y)}=\rho^{2}(X,Y)\,.

As demonstrated in Example
2
,

\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)
coincides with the
L^{2}
-Cramér-von-Mises index,
i.e., it coincides with the expected squared distance between the conditional
and the unconditional expectation.
This motivates to investigate another subclass of functionals
\Lambda_{\Delta}

where
\Delta
now depends only on the difference of conditional expectations, weighted
again by some measurable function
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Delta\big(\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{1}},\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{2}}\big)

\displaystyle:=\psi\left(\mathbb{E}(Y|X=x_{1})-\mathbb{E}(Y|X=x_{2})\right).

(6)

This leads to a novel class of Cramer-von-Mises indices solely based on
sensitivity of the conditional expectations.
For some specific choices of
\psi
in (
6
), the associated functional
\Lambda_{\Delta}
in (
1
) relates the variability of the conditional distribution to the variability of the unconditional distribution as in (
2
), see, e.g.,
[
14
]
for several Gini-type measures of risk and variability.

For the special case where
\Delta=\Delta_{\psi}
is of the form (
6
), the functional
\Lambda_{\Delta_{\psi}}=:\Delta_{\psi}

is given by

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\psi}(Y|X):

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\psi}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi\left(\mathbb{E}(Y|X=x_{1})-\mathbb{E}(Y|X=x_{2})\right)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X})(x_{1},x_{2})

(7)

with normalizing constant
\alpha_{\psi}
fulfilling

\alpha_{\psi}=\sup\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi\left(\mathbb{E}(Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{1})-\mathbb{E}(Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{2})\right)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X^{\prime}})(x_{1},x_{2}):(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}(Y)\right\}\,,

whenever the supremum is finite and positive.

In order to assure that
\Lambda_{\psi}
according to (
7
) is a measure of explainability the following property will be key:
A convex function
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

is said to be strictly convex in
0
if
(\phi(-\varepsilon)+\phi(\varepsilon))/2>\phi(0)
holds for every
\varepsilon>0\,.

Notice that in the subsequent theorem
\psi
is also allowed to attain negative values.

Theorem 2.2

(Measures of explainability)
.

Suppose that
\psi(2Y)
and
\psi(-2Y)
are integrable and that

\psi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex and strictly convex in
0
with
\psi(0)=0
.
Then the constant
\alpha_{\psi}
is given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{\psi}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi\left(y_{1}-y_{2}\right)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Y}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y})(y_{1},y_{2})\,.

(8)

Furthermore,
\Lambda_{\psi}(Y|X)
defined according to (
7
) is a measure of explainability, i.e., it fulfills the following three properties:

(i)

\Lambda_{\psi}(Y|X)\in[0,1]\,.

(ii)

\Lambda_{\psi}(Y|X)=0
if, and only if
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{E}(Y|X))=0
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}(Y|X)=\mathbb{E}[Y]

almost surely.

(iii)

\Lambda_{\psi}(Y|X)=1
if, and only if

Y
is completely dependent on
X
, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}(Y|X)=Y

almost surely.

Proof.

We first prove property (
i
) and eq. (
8
) and
proceed as follows:
Let
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
be independent copies of
Y
. Then
using convexity of
\psi
and the integrability assumption the right-hand side of eq. (
8
) is finite since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\psi(Y_{1}-Y_{2})\right|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left|\psi\left(\frac{2Y_{1}+(-2Y_{2})}{2}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left|\psi(2Y_{1})+\psi(-2Y_{2})\right|\leq\mathbb{E}|\psi(2Y)|+\mathbb{E}|\psi(-2Y)|<\infty.

If
Y=f(X)
almost surely, then using change of coordinates it yields

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi\left(\mathbb{E}(Y|X=x_{1})-\mathbb{E}(Y|X=x_{2})\right)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X})(x_{1},x_{2})\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi\left(f(x_{1})-f(x_{2})\right)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X})(x_{1},x_{2})\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi\left(y_{1}-y_{2}\right)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{f(X)}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{f(X)})(y_{1},y_{2})\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi\left(y_{1}-y_{2}\right)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Y}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y})(y_{1},y_{2}).\end{split}

(9)

Hence, using disintegration and Jensen’s inequality it follows for all

(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}(Y)
that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\psi(0)=\psi\left(\mathbb{E}(Y^{\prime})-\mathbb{E}(Y^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\psi\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathbb{E}[Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{1}]-\mathbb{E}[Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{2}]\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X^{\prime}})(x_{1},x_{2})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi\left(\mathbb{E}[Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{1}]-\mathbb{E}[Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{2}]\right)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X^{\prime}})(x_{1},x_{2})

(10)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}y_{1}-y_{2}\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{2}})(y_{1},y_{2})\right)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X^{\prime}})(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi\left(y_{1}-y_{2}\right)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{2}})(y_{1},y_{2})\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X^{\prime}})(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi\left(y_{1}-y_{2}\right)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Y^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y^{\prime}})(y_{1},y_{2})

(11)

\displaystyle\leq\sup\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi\left(\mathbb{E}(Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{1})-\mathbb{E}(Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{2})\right)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X^{\prime}})(x_{1},x_{2}):(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}(Y)\right\}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\psi}.

Using eq. (
9
) and applying eq. (
10
) to eq. (
11
)
on the elements the supremum is taken over shows that the third inequality becomes an equality.
This proves eq. (
8
) and implies
\Lambda_{\psi}(Y|X)\in[0,1]
.

(
ii
): Obviously
\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{E}(Y|X))=0
if, and only if
\mathbb{E}(Y|X=x_{1})=\mathbb{E}(Y)=\mathbb{E}(Y|X=x_{2})
for
\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X}
-almost all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
.
The latter, however, is equivalent to the integrand in (
7
) being zero almost everywhere since
\psi
is strictly convex in
0
and
\psi(0)=0
.

(
iii
): If
Y
is completely dependent on
X
,
then (
9
) and (
8
) imply

\displaystyle\alpha_{\psi}\,\Lambda_{\psi}(Y|X)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi\left(\mathbb{E}(Y|X=x_{1})-\mathbb{E}(Y|X=x_{2})\right)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X})(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi\left(y_{1}-y_{2}\right)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Y}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y})(y_{1},y_{2})=\alpha_{\psi}.

Since
\mathbb{P}^{Y}
is non-degenerate, complete dependence of
Y
on
X
yields
\mathbb{E}(Y|X)=Y
and we do not have
Y=\mathbb{E}(Y)
almost surely. Hence, using (
i
) and (
ii
) the constant
\alpha_{\psi}
is
positive and
\Lambda_{\psi}(Y|X)=1
follows.

To show the reverse implication first recall that by Jensen’s inequality
and eq. (
8
) we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\alpha_{\psi}\Lambda_{\psi}(Y|X)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi\Big(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(y_{1}-y_{2})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{2}}(y_{1},y_{2})\Big)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X}(x_{1},x_{2})\\
&\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\psi(y_{1}-y_{2})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{2}}\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X}(x_{1},x_{2})=\alpha_{\psi}.\end{split}

(12)

For
\Lambda_{\psi}(Y|X)=1
the above inequality becomes an equality there exists some Borel set
G\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X}(G)=1
such that for
(x_{1},x_{2})\in G
and
Y_{i}\sim\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{i}}\,,

i\in\{1,2\}\,,

Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
independent,

\displaystyle\text{either}\quad Y_{1}\leq Y_{2}~\,\text{almost surely}\quad\text{or}\quad Y_{1}\geq Y_{2}~\,\text{almost surely.}

(13)

In fact, otherwise Jensen’s inequality would be strict due to strict convexity
of
\psi
at
0=\psi(0)
, which contradicts
\Lambda_{\psi}(Y|X)=1
.
Letting
G_{x}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}\colon(x,y)\in G\}
denote the
x
-cut of
G
,
defining
W:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon\mathbb{P}^{X}(G_{x})=1\}
and using disintegration obviously yields

\mathbb{P}^{X}(W)=1
. To simplify notation let
l(x)
and
u(x)
denote the infimum and
the supremum of the support of
\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x}\,,
i.e.,

\displaystyle l(x):=\inf(\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}(\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x}))\leq\sup(\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}(\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x}))=:u(x).

(14)

Then according to (
13
) for each
(x_{1},x_{2})\in G
the open intervals fulfil

(l(x_{1}),u(x_{1}))\cap(l(x_{2}),u(x_{2}))=\emptyset
.
Finally define

\displaystyle V:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x}\text{ is non-degenerate}\}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon\mathbb{V}(Y|X=x)>0\}\,.

(15)

For completing the proof it suffices to show

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{X}(V)=0\,

(16)

since it then follows that
\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x}
is degenerate for
\mathbb{P}^{X}
-almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}\,,
implying that
Y
is completely dependent on
X
.

Assume, on the contrary, that
\mathbb{P}^{X}(V)>0
holds. Then considering
\mathbb{P}^{X}(W)=1
it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{X}(V\cap W)=\mathbb{P}^{X}(V)>0\,.

(17)

Let
x\in V\cap W
be arbitrary but fixed. Then by contruction we have

\displaystyle(l(x),u(x))\cap(l(z),u(z))=\emptyset

(18)

for all
z\in\mathbb{R}
with
(x,z)\in G
, so in particular for all
z\in V\cap W\cap G_{x}
. Considering
\mathbb{P}^{X}(G_{x})=1=\mathbb{P}^{X}(W)
it follows that
0<\mathbb{P}^{X}(V)=\mathbb{P}^{X}(V\cap W\cap G_{x})
. Hence, (
18
) holds in particular for

\mathbb{P}^{X}
-almost every
z\in V\cap W
.

Finally, let
q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},\ldots
be an enumeration of the rational numbers in
\mathbb{R}
.
Then defining
M_{i}
by
M_{i}:=\{x\in V\cap W\colon q_{i}\in(l(x),u(x))\}
yields
\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}M_{i}=V\cap W
since the rationals are dense in
\mathbb{R}
.
Using (
17
) as well as sub-
\sigma
-additivity yields

\displaystyle 0<\mathbb{P}^{X}(V\cap W)=\mathbb{P}^{X}\Big(\bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty}M_{i}\Big)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}^{X}(M_{i}),

(19)

so there exists some
i_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
with
\mathbb{P}^{X}(M_{i_{0}})>0
.
The latter, however, contradicts (
18
), so
\mathbb{P}^{X}(V)>0
can not hold, and
the proof is complete.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

Our proof of assertion (
iii
) in Theorem
2.2

requires the assumption of strict convexity of
\psi
in
0
and uses the fact
that Jensen’s inequality applied to the difference of two non-degenerate i.i.d. random variables is strict if
\varphi
is strictly convex in
0
.
Hence, equality can only hold if the underlying random variables are degenerate.
Due to the integral with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X}\,,
it has to be shown that Jensen’s inequality holds for the inner integral
\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X}
-almost surely. For
\mathbb{P}^{X}
having at least one point mass the proof can be simplified
significantly, since then
\mathbb{P}^{X}(V)=0
follows immediately.

3
A new class of dependence measures

For the rest of the paper we now focus on the functionals
\Lambda_{\varphi}

and derive simple sufficient conditions for
\varphi
in (
5
) assuring that
\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)
defined by (
1
) is a
dependence measure.
Following the recent literature we refer to
\kappa
as a
(directed) dependence measure
(frequently also referred to as measure of predictability)
for bivariate random vectors
(X,Y)

if it satisfies the following axioms, see, e.g.,
[
2
,
5
,
6
,
8
,
19
,
35
,
37
]
and
the references therein:

(A1)

\kappa(Y|X)\in[0,1]
.

(A2)

\kappa(Y|X)=0
if, and only if
Y
and
X
are independent.

(A3)

\kappa(Y|X)=1
if and only if
Y
is completely dependent on
X
,
i.e., there exists some measurable transformation
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
Y=f(X)
holds almost surely.

Due to the axioms (A
2
) and (A
3
), the values
0
and
1
of a measure of predictability
\kappa
have a clear interpretation.
The meaning of
\kappa\,
attaining a specific value in the interval
(0,1)
, however, is not that unequivocal - nevertheless, considering that the values in

[0,1]
are naturally ordered, they offer a clear comparative interpretation:
the higher the value of
\kappa
the higher the extent of dependence of
Y

on
X
.
This aspect motivates the study of dependence orderings
\prec
that are compatible with
\kappa
in the following sense:

(P1)

Monotonicity
:
If
(X,Y)\prec(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
, then
\kappa(Y|X)\leq\kappa(Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime})
.

It is further desirable - particularly in the context of estimation - to have an understanding of the measures’ behavior in terms of convergence:

(P2)

Continuity
:
If
(X_{n},Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges (in some sense) to
(X,Y)
,
then
\lim_{n\to\infty}\kappa(Y_{n}|X_{n})=\kappa(Y|X)
.

Moreover the following invariance properties seem desirable and will be studied in the
sequel:

(P3)

Invariance of
X
:
For bijective
h
we have
\kappa(Y|X)=\kappa(Y|h(X))
.

(P4)

Invariance of
Y
:
If
g
is strictly increasing, then
\kappa(Y|X)=\kappa(g(Y)|X)
holds.

(P5)

Version-Invariance
:
If
(X,Y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\,,
then
\kappa(X,Y)=\kappa(X,Y)\,.

Note that property (P
5
) is also misleadingly referred to as law invariance in the context of risk measures.

3.1
The class
\Lambda_{\varphi}

For the special case where
\Delta
is of the form (
5
) the functional
\Lambda_{\Delta}
is given by

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X):

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\varphi}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(F_{Y|X=x_{1}}(y)-F_{Y|X=x_{2}}(y))\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X})(x_{1},x_{2})

(20)

with normalizing constant
\alpha_{\varphi}
according to (
3
) defined by

\displaystyle\alpha_{\varphi}

\displaystyle:=\sup\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(F_{Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{1}}(y)-F_{Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{2}}(y))\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y^{\prime}}(y)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Y^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y^{\prime}})(y_{1},y_{2}):(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}(Y)\right\}\,,

(21)

whenever the supremum exists and is positive.

For deriving sufficient conditions on
\varphi
in order for
\Lambda_{\varphi}
in (
20
) to characterize independence and perfect directed dependence, the following simple lemma is key.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
U
be a real-valued random variable on the probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
and let
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
be
\sigma
-algebras with
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{A}\,.
Then

\mathbb{E}[U|\mathcal{B}]\leq_{cx}\mathbb{E}[U|\mathcal{C}]\,,
where
\leq_{cx}
denotes the convex order.

Proof.

Let
\varphi
be a convex function. Then, whenever the following expectations exist, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\mathbb{E}[U|\mathcal{B}])]=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[U|\mathcal{C}]|\mathcal{B}])]\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\mathbb{E}[U|\mathcal{C}])|\mathcal{B}]]=\mathbb{E}[\varphi(\mathbb{E}[U|\mathcal{C}])]

(22)

applying the tower property and Jensen’s inequality for conditional expectation.
∎

Having this we can now formulate the following main result of this section.

Theorem 3.2

(Measures of directed dependence)
.

Assume that
\varphi:[-1,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is convex and strictly convex in
0
with
\varphi(0)=0
and that
Y
is non-degenerate. Then the normalizing constant

\alpha_{\varphi}
in (
21
) is given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{\varphi}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\left(\mathds{1}_{\{y_{1}\leq y\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{y_{2}\leq y\}}\right)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Y}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y})(y_{1},y_{2})

(23)

and
\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)
defined according to (
20
) is a
dependence measure, i.e.,

(i)

\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)\in[0,1]\,,

(ii)

\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)=0
if, and only if
X
and
Y
are independent,

(iii)

\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)=1
if, and only if
Y
is perfectly dependent on
X
.

Proof.

(
i
): For every
y\in\mathbb{R}
and every
c\in[0,1]\,,

applying Lemma
3.1
directly yields

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\varphi\left(\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}_{\{Y\leq y\}}|X]-c\right)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}\leq\int_{\Omega}\varphi\left(\mathbb{E}[\mathds{1}_{\{Y\leq y\}}|(X,Y)]-c\right)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}=\int_{\Omega}\varphi\left(\mathds{1}_{\{Y\leq y\}}-c\right)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}\,.

Hence, using disintegration, Jensen’s inequality and Fubini’s theorem it follows
that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\varphi(0)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y)-\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y)\,\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)

\displaystyle=\varphi\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(F_{Y|X=x_{1}}(y)-F_{Y|X=x_{2}}(y))\mathrm{\,d}(P^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X})(x_{1},x_{2})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\left(F_{Y|X=x_{1}}(y)-F_{Y|X=x_{2}}(y)\right)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}(x_{1})\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y})(x_{2},y)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\left(\mathds{1}_{\{y_{1}\leq y\}}-F_{Y|X=x_{2}}(y)\right)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y_{1})\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y})(x_{2},y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\left(\mathds{1}_{\{y_{1}\leq y\}}-F_{Y|X=x_{2}}(y)\right)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}(x_{2})\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Y}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y})(y_{1},y)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\left(\mathds{1}_{\{y_{1}\leq y\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{y_{2}\leq y\}}\right)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y_{2})\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Y}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y})(y_{1},y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\left(\mathds{1}_{\{y_{1}\leq y\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{y_{2}\leq y\}}\right)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Y}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y})(y_{1},y_{2})

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(F_{Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{1}}(y)-F_{Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{2}}(y))\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y^{\prime}}(y)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Y^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y^{\prime}})(y_{1},y_{2}):(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}(Y)\right\}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\varphi}\,,

where the supremum is attained whenever
Y=f(X)
almost surely
for some measurable
f
. This implies (
23
) and yields

\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)\in[0,1]\,.

(
ii
):
X
and
Y
are independent, if and only if
F_{Y|X=x}(\cdot)=F_{Y}(\cdot)
for
\mathbb{P}^{X}
-almost all
x
. The latter, however, is
equivalent to the integrand in (
20
) being zero
\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X}
-almost everywhere since

\varphi
is strictly convex in
0
and
\varphi(0)=0\,
holds.

(iii) Suppose that
Y=f(X)
almost surely for some measurable
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
Then obviously
F_{Y|X=x}(y)=\mathds{1}_{\{f(x)\leq y\}}
, so applying Fubini’s theorem
and change of coordinates yields

\displaystyle\alpha_{\varphi}\,\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(F_{Y|X=x_{1}}(y)-F_{Y|X=x_{2}}(y))\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X})(x_{1},x_{2})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)

(24)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(\mathds{1}_{\{f(x_{1})\leq y\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{f(x_{2})\leq y\}})\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X})(x_{1},x_{2})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(\mathds{1}_{\{y_{1}\leq y\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{y_{2}\leq y\}})\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{f\circ X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{f\circ X})(y_{1},y_{2})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{\varphi}.

Since
Y
is non-degenerate, (
ii
) and (
23
) imply that
\alpha_{\varphi}
is positive and finite, so
\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)=1
holds.

It remains to prove the reverse implication, i.e., that

\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)=1
implies
Y=f(X)
almost surely for some
f
, which can be done as follows: First, we show that the condition
\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)=1
implies the existence of some
\mathbb{P}^{Y}
-null set
N
such that for fixed
y\in N^{c}
there exists a
\mathbb{P}^{X}
-null set
N_{y}
with

\displaystyle F_{Y|X=x}(y)\in\{0,1\}\quad\text{for all }x\in N_{y}^{c}\,.

(25)

We then prove that there exists some
\mathbb{P}^{X}
-null set

N_{*}
fulfilling

\displaystyle F_{Y|X=x}(y)\in\{0,1\}\quad\text{for all }y\in\mathbb{R}\text{ and for all }x\in N_{*}^{c}\,.

(26)

Having this, proceeding like in the proofs of
[
4
, Theorem 9.2. (iii)]
or
[
37
, Lemma 12]
yields complete dependence of
Y
on
X
, which completes the proof.

In order to show (
25
),
fix
y\in\mathbb{R}
and consider

a:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, defined
by
a(x)=\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y\mid X=x)
.
We first establish an upper bound for the mapping

(x_{1},x_{2})\mapsto\varphi\big(a(x_{1})-a(x_{2})\big)

and procced as follows: Setting

\displaystyle c_{1}

\displaystyle:=a(x_{1})-\min\{a(x_{1}),a(x_{2})\}\in[0,1]

\displaystyle c_{-1}

\displaystyle:=a(x_{2})-\min\{a(x_{1}),a(x_{2})\}\in[0,1]

\displaystyle c_{0}

\displaystyle:=1-a(x_{1})-a(x_{2})+2\min\{a(x_{1}),a(x_{2})\}\in[0,1]

we obviously have
c_{1}+c_{-1}+c_{0}=1
, so using convexity yields

\displaystyle\varphi(a(x_{1})-a(x_{2}))

(27)

\displaystyle=\varphi\big(1\cdot(a(x_{1})-\min\{a(x_{1}),a(x_{2})\})-1\cdot(a(x_{2})-\min\{a(x_{1}),a(x_{2})\})+0\cdot c_{0}\big)

\displaystyle\leq\varphi(1)(a(x_{1})-\min\{a(x_{1}),a(x_{2})\})+\varphi(-1)(a(x_{2})-\min\{a(x_{1}),a(x_{2})\})+\underbrace{\varphi(0)}_{=0}\cdot c_{0}

\displaystyle=\varphi(1)\,a(x_{1})+\varphi(-1)\,a(x_{2})-\underbrace{(\varphi(1)+\varphi(-1))}_{>0}\,\underbrace{\min\{a(x_{1}),a(x_{2})\}}_{\geq a(x_{1})a(x_{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\varphi(1)\,a(x_{1})+\varphi(-1)\,a(x_{2})-(\varphi(1)+\varphi(-1))\,a(x_{1})a(x_{2})

\displaystyle=\varphi(1)\,a(x_{1})(1-a(x_{2}))+\varphi(-1)\,a(x_{2})(1-a(x_{1}))

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi\left(\mathds{1}_{\{y_{1}\leq y\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{y_{2}\leq y\}}\right)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{2}})(y_{1},y_{2}).

Integrating both sides over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with respect to
\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X}

and using disintegration it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(a(x_{1})-a(x_{2}))\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X}(x_{1},x_{2})

(28)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi\left(\mathds{1}_{\{y_{1}\leq y\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{y_{2}\leq y\}}\right)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{2}})(y_{1},y_{2})\,\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\left(\mathds{1}_{\{y_{1}\leq y\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{y_{2}\leq y\}}\right)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{1}}(y_{1})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}(x_{1})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x_{2}}(y_{2})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}(x_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(\mathds{1}_{\{y_{1}\leq y\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{y_{2}\leq y\}})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y_{1},y_{2}).

Consequently, if equality holds in (
28
), then for

\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X}
-almost all
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
inequality (
27
)
becomes an equality. In particular, there exists some Borel set
G\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}

with
\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X}(G)=1
such that

\displaystyle\min\{a(x_{1}),a(x_{2})\}=a(x_{1})a(x_{2})

holds for all
(x_{1},x_{2})\in G
.
Considering that the latter is equivalent to the fact that

a(x_{1})\in\{0,1\}
or
a(x_{2})\in\{0,1\}
for all
(x_{1},x_{2})\in G

we conclude that
a(x)\in\{0,1\}
for
\mathbb{P}^{X}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Summing up, for every fixed
y\in\mathbb{R}
we have shown that equality in (
28
)
implies that
a(x)=\mathbb{P}(Y\leq y\mid X=x)=F_{Y|X=x}(y)\in\{0,1\}
for

\mathbb{P}^{X}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Now, to prove (
25
), suppose that
\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)=1
, i.e., that we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(a(x_{1})-a(x_{2}))\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\varphi(\mathds{1}_{\{y_{1}\leq y\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{y_{2}\leq y\}}\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y_{1},y_{2})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\,.\end{split}

(29)

Then, due to (
28
),
there exists some
Borel set
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}
with
\mathbb{P}^{Y}(E)=1
such that for every

y\in E
we have equality in (
28
). Due to the previous step,
for
N:=E^{c}
(
25
) holds true for all
y\in E\,,
i.e., for every
y\in E
there exists a
\mathbb{P}^{X}
-null set
N_{y}
with
F_{Y|X=x}(y)\in\{0,1\}
for all
x\in N_{y}^{c}
.

In order to prove (
26
) first note that by disintegration we also have

\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x}(E)=1
for
\mathbb{P}^{X}
-almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}\,.
Hence we may
proceed as follows:
Define the set
N\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
by

\displaystyle N:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:F_{Y|X=x}(y)\in(0,1)\}

and suppose that
\mathbb{P}^{(X,Y)}(N)>0
. Setting
N_{x}:=\{y\in\mathbb{R}:(x,y)\in N\}

disintegration yields

\displaystyle 0<\mathbb{P}^{(X,Y)}(N)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x}(N_{x})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}(x),

so the set
O\subseteq\mathcal{\mathbb{R}}
, given by

O=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x}(N_{x})>0\textrm{ and }\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x}(E)=1\}
fulfills

\mathbb{P}^{X}(O)>0
. For every

\mathbb{P}^{(X,Y)}(N)=0

∎

3.2
Additional properties of
\Lambda_{\varphi}

Convexity of the function
\varphi
yields the so-called

data processing inequality
which states that applying a transformation
to the explanatory variable

can not increase the extent of dependence, see
[
7

for a detailed discussion. Note that strict convexity in

is not required for the following results.

Proposition 3.4

(Data processing inequality)
.

Assume that
\varphi
is convex on
[-1,1]
.
Then
\Lambda_{\varphi}
defined according to eq. (
20
) fulfills the data processing inequality,
i.e.,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|Z)\leq\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)

for all
X,Y,Z
such that
Y
and
Z
are conditionally independent given
X
.
In particular,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|h(X))\leq\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)

for all measurable functions
h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Assume that
Y
and
Z
are conditionally independent given
X
.
Proceeding as in the proof of the second assertion of Lemma 3.5 in
[
18

and using disintegration it follows that for every fixed

we have

\displaystyle F_{Y|Z=z}(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F_{Y|Z=z,X=x}(y)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X|Z=z}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F_{Y|X=x}(y)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X|Z=z}(x)

for
\mathbb{P}^{Z}
-almost all

. Hence, using
convexity of
\varphi
, Jensen’s inequality, and disintegration once more yields

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(F_{Y|Z=z_{1}}(y)-F_{Y|Z=z_{2}}(y))\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Z}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Z})(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}F_{Y|X=x_{1}}(y)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X|Z=z_{1}}(x_{1})-\int_{\mathbb{R}}F_{Y|X=x_{2}}(y)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X|Z=z_{2}}(x_{2})\right)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Z}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Z})(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F_{Y|X=x_{1}}(y)-F_{Y|X=x_{2}}(y)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X|Z=z_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X|Z=z_{2}})(x_{1},x_{2})\right)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Z}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Z})(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(F_{Y|X=x_{1}}(y)-F_{Y|X=x_{2}}(y))\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X|Z=z_{1}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X|Z=z_{2}})(x_{1},x_{2})\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{Z}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{Z})(z_{1},z_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(F_{Y|X=x_{1}}(y)-F_{Y|X=x_{2}}(y))\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{(X,Z)}(x_{1},z_{1})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{(X,Z)}(x_{2},z_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(F_{Y|X=x_{1}}(y)-F_{Y|X=x_{2}}(y))\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X})(x_{1},x_{2})\,,

which completes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)

Considering that
\Lambda(Y|X)
according to Theorem

(

, in order to establish some general ordering results
for
\Lambda_{\varphi}
we need to work with rearrangement invariant orders.
To this end, for integrable functions

with
\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\mathrm{\,d}\lambda(t)=\int_{0}^{1}g(t)\mathrm{\,d}\lambda(t)
consider the Schur order
\prec_{S}
, defined by

\displaystyle f\prec_{S}g\quad:\,\Longleftrightarrow\quad\int_{0}^{x}f^{*}(t)\mathrm{\,d}t\leq\int_{0}^{x}g^{*}(t)\mathrm{\,d}t\quad\text{for all }x\in(0,1)\,,

(30)

where
h^{*}
denotes decreasing rearrangement of a function
h\colon(0,1)\to\mathbb{R}\,,
i.e., the
\lambda
-almost everywhere unique decreasing function
h^{*}

such that
\lambda(\{h^{*}\leq t\})=\lambda(\{h\leq t\})
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}\,.

Denote by
q_{Z}(u):=\inf\{z\in\mathbb{R}:\mid F_{Z}(z)\geq u\}=F_{Z}^{-}(u)
the quasi-inverse of
the distribution function
F_{Z}
of
Z
.
Then, for
Y
and
Y^{\prime}
having continuous distribution functions, the Schur order
(Y|X)\leq_{S}(Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime})
for conditional distributions is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{Y\leq q_{Y}(v)\}}\mid X=q_{X}(\cdot)\right]\leq_{S}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{Y^{\prime}\leq q_{Y^{\prime}}(v)\}}\mid X^{\prime}=q_{X^{\prime}}(\cdot)\right]~~~\text{for all }v\in(0,1)\,,

(31)

see
[
2
]
as well as
[
1
,
3
,
33
,
35
]
.
Hence, the Schur-order for conditional distributions compares the variability of conditional distribution functions in the conditioning variable.

For bivariate random vectors
(U,V)
and
(U^{\prime},V^{\prime})\,,
denote by
(U,V)\leq_{lo}(U^{\prime},V^{\prime})
the lower orthant order, i.e., the pointwise comparison of the associated distribution functions. Further, the bivariate random vector
(U,V)
is said to be stochastically increasing (SI) if the conditional distribution
V|U=u
is stochastically increasing in
u\,,
i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[f(V)|U=u]
is increasing in
u
for all increasing functions
f
such that the expectations exist.
If
(U^{\prime},V^{\prime})
is SI and if
U\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}U^{\prime}
and
V\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{d}}}{{=}}V^{\prime}\,,
then
(V|U)\leq_{S}(V^{\prime}|U^{\prime})
implies
(U,V)\leq_{lo}(U^{\prime},V^{\prime})\,.
If additionally
(U,V)
is SI, then
(V|U)\leq_{S}(V^{\prime}|U^{\prime})
and
(U,V)\leq_{lo}(U^{\prime},V^{\prime})
are equivalent, see
[
3
, Lemma 3.16]
.

The following result provides simple conditions for monotonicity of
\Lambda_{\varphi}\,.

Proposition 3.7

(Monotonicity)
.

Suppose that
(Y|X)\leq_{S}(Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime})
and that
Y,Y^{\prime}
have continuous distribution function. Then
\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)\leq\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime})
for all convex functions
\varphi
such that the integrals exist.

Proof.

By the Hardy-Littlewood-Polya theorem (see, e.g.,
[
32
, Theorem 3.21]
we have that
(Y|X)\leq_{S}(Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime})
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\phi(F_{Y|X=q_{X}(u)}(q_{Y}(v)))\mathrm{\,d}\lambda(u)\leq\int_{0}^{1}\phi(F_{Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=q_{X^{\prime}}(u)}(q_{Y^{\prime}}(v)))\mathrm{\,d}\lambda(u)\quad

for all
v
and for all convex
\phi
such that the integrals exist. Since the functions
z\mapsto\varphi(z-c)
and
z\mapsto\varphi(c-z)
are convex whenever
\varphi
is convex, using Fubini’s theorem and change of coordinates
repeatedly it follows that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(F_{Y|X=x_{1}}(y)-F_{Y|X=x_{2}}(y))\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X})(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{(0,1)}\int_{(0,1)}\int_{(0,1)}\varphi\left(F_{Y|X=q_{X}(u_{1})}(q_{Y}(v))-F_{Y|X=q_{X}(u_{2})}(q_{Y}(v))\right)\mathrm{\,d}\lambda(u_{1})\mathrm{\,d}\lambda(v)\mathrm{\,d}\lambda(u_{2})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{(0,1)}\int_{(0,1)}\int_{(0,1)}\varphi\left(F_{Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=q_{X^{\prime}}(u_{1})}(q_{Y^{\prime}}(v))-F_{Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=q_{X^{\prime}}(u_{2})}(q_{Y^{\prime}}(v))\right)\mathrm{\,d}\lambda(v)\mathrm{\,d}\lambda(u_{1})\mathrm{\,d}\lambda(u_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(F_{Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{1}}(y)-F_{Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime}=x_{2}}(y))\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y^{\prime}}(y)\mathrm{\,d}(\mathbb{P}^{X^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X^{\prime}})(x_{1},x_{2})

which implies
\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)\leq\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y^{\prime}|X^{\prime})\,.

∎

Remark 3.8
.

(a)

Minimal and maximal elements with respect to the Schur order for conditional distributions characterize independence and complete dependence, respectively, see
[
2

. Hence, as a consequence of Theorem
3.7
, if
Y
is completely depend on

and if
\varphi
is convex, then
\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)=1\,,
using that
\alpha_{\varphi}\in(0,\infty)\,.
If additionally
\varphi
is strictly convex in

and if
\varphi(0)=0\,,
then Theorem

provides the reverse implication, i.e., that
\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)=1

almost surely.

(b)

Various well-known (sub-)families of bivariate distributions are stochastically increasing and increasing with respect to the lower orthant order (implying that
the associated conditional distributions are increasing with respect to the Schur order for conditional distributions). Examples are the bivariate normal distribution with respect to the non-negative correlation parameter, several Archimedean copula families such as the Clayton, Gumbel or Frank family, or various extreme value copula families, see, e.g.,
[
28
, Chapter 4]
,
[
27
]
, and
[
3
, Examples 3.18 and 3.19]
.

3.3
Continuous setting and
L^{p}
-version

From now on we will assume that
(X,Y)
has continuous bivariate
distribution function since continuity is necessary for having uniqueness in
Sklar’s theorem (see
[
28
]
) and both for deriving
continuity results of the functional
\Lambda_{\varphi}
and for the the estimation procedure studied in the next section.
Furthermore, in the sequel (following
[
22
]
) we will write conditional distributions as Markov kernels, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}^{Y|X=x}(\cdot)=K(x,\cdot)
, let
C
denote the (unique) copula underlying

(X,Y)
and
K_{C}(\cdot,\cdot)
as the Markov kernel of
C
.
Notice that according to
[
26
, Lemma 1]
(also see
[
3
, Theorem 2.2]
),
starting with a Markov kernel
K_{C}(\cdot,\cdot)
and setting

K(x,(-\infty,y]):=K_{C}(F_{X}(x),[0,F_{Y}(y)])

yields a (version of the) Markov kernel of
(X,Y)
.
In the continuous setting, the functional
\Lambda_{\varphi}
only depends on the
underlying copula
C
since for every continuous
\varphi\colon[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}

using change of coordinates we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\big(F_{Y|X=x_{1}}(y)-F_{Y|X=x_{2}}(y)\big)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi\big(K_{C}(F_{X}(x_{1}),[0,F_{Y}(y)])-K_{C}(F_{X}(x_{2}),[0,F_{Y}(x)])\big)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}\otimes\mathbb{P}^{X}(x_{1},x_{2})\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\varphi\big(K_{C}(u_{1},[0,v])-K_{C}(u_{2},[0,v]\big)\mathrm{\,d}\lambda(v)\mathrm{\,d}\lambda(u_{1})\mathrm{\,d}\lambda(u_{2}).

(32)

Following
[
23
]
we say that a sequence a sequence
(C_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of bivariate copulas
converges weakly conditional
to a bivariate copula
C
and
write
C_{n}\xrightarrow{wcc}C\,,

if there exists some Borel set
\Gamma\subseteq[0,1]

with
\lambda(\Gamma)=1
such that the probability measures

(K_{C_{n}}(u,\cdot))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly to the probability measure
K_{C}(u,\cdot)
for every
x\in\Gamma
.
The following result provides sufficient conditions for continuity of the functional
\Lambda_{\varphi}
and is straightforward to prove using eq. (
3.3
)
and dominated convergence.

Proposition 3.9

(Continuity)
.

Let
(X,Y),(X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{2},Y_{2}),\ldots
be bivariate random vectors with continuous distribution functions and corresponding copulas
A,A_{1},A_{2},\ldots
and
let
\varphi\colon[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous. Then

implies convergence of
(\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y_{n}|X_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to
\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)
.

Example 3
.

A particular class of dependence measures fulfilling all of the afore-mentioned properties
is obtained when considering

\varphi(x)=|x|^{p}
for some
p\geq 1
. In this case
\Lambda_{\varphi}
corresponds to the
L^{p}
-distance of conditional distribution functions. In fact, in this case
using eq. (

\displaystyle\Lambda_{p}(Y|X)

\displaystyle:=3\int_{[0,1]^{3}}|K_{A}(u_{1},[0,z])-K_{A}(u_{2},[0,z])|^{p}\,\mathrm{\,d}\lambda^{3}(u_{1},u_{2},z)=\Lambda_{p}(A)\,,

(33)

where the normalizing constant according is given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}:=\alpha_{\varphi}=\int_{(0,1)^{3}}|\mathds{1}_{\{u_{1}\leq v\}}-\mathds{1}_{\{u_{2}\leq v\}}|^{p}\mathrm{\,d}\lambda^{3}(u_{1},u_{2},v).

(34)

Since the normalizing constant does not depend on
p
it follows that the mapping

p\mapsto\Lambda_{p}(Y|X)
is decreasing in
p\in[1,\infty)
.

For
p=2
the functional
\Lambda_{p}
compares two conditional distributions as
in (
1
) and coincides with Chatterjee’s correlation coefficient
(see Example
1
) which compares conditional and unconditional distributions as in (
2
).
For
p=1\,,
however, the following examples illustrates that the comparison of two conditional distributions as in (
1
) only coincides for some specific families with the
L^{1}
-analogue of Chatterjee’s coefficient of correlation.

Example 4

(FGM and Fréchet copulas)
.

The functional
\zeta_{1}(Y|X)
defined by

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}(Y|X):=\frac{1}{3}\int_{0}^{1}|F_{Y|X=x}(y)-F_{Y}(y)|\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{Y}(y)\mathrm{\,d}\mathbb{P}^{X}(x)

(35)

is a dependence measure introduced and studied in
[
37
]
.

(a)

For
\alpha\in[0,1]
the copula

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}(u,v):=uv+\alpha\,u(1-u)v(1-v)

is a member of the Farlie-Gumbel-Morgenstern (FGM) family.
Let
(X,Y)
be a random vector with continuous distribution function and underlying
copula
C_{\alpha}
. Then straightforward calculations yield

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}(Y|X)=\frac{\alpha}{3}\qquad\textrm{ and }\qquad\zeta_{1}(Y|X)=\frac{\alpha}{4},

so
\Lambda_{1}(Y|X)=\tfrac{4}{3}\,\zeta_{1}(Y|X)\neq\zeta_{1}(Y|X)
.

(b)

For
\alpha\in[0,1]
the copula

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}(u,v):=\alpha\,M(u,v)+(1-\alpha)\,\Pi(u,v)

is a member of the Fréchet copula family. In this case
for continuous
(X,Y)
with underlying copula
C_{\alpha}
we get

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}(Y|X)=\alpha=\zeta_{1}(Y|X)\,.

4
Estimating the functionals

In this section, we propose a copula-based so-called checkerboard estimator
as plug-in estimator for the functionals
\Lambda_{\psi}
in (
7
) and
\Lambda_{\varphi}
in (
20
) and start with recalling some basic facts.

For every bivariate copula
A
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
the
N
-checkerboard
approximation of
A
(see
[
25
,
22
]
) will be denoted by
\mathfrak{Cb}_{N}(A)
.
As a consequence of
[
25
, Corollary 3.2]
, for every copula
A
the sequence
(\mathfrak{Cb}_{N}(A))_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
converges
weakly conditional to
A
.
To assure well-definedness we will refer to the bilinear interpolation of the
subcopula resulting from Sklar’s theorem by considering the empirical distribution functions as the empirical copula, see, e.g.,
[
16
,
18
]
.

Plugging in the empirical copula we get the following consistency result.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{2},Y_{2}),\ldots
be a random sample from
(X,Y)
and assume that
(X,Y)
has continuous
distribution function
H
, underlying copula
A
, and that
E_{n}
denotes the empirical copula
(of the first
n
observations). Then setting
N(n):=\lfloor{n}^{s}\rfloor
for some

s\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
the sequence
(\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)}(E_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly conditional to
A

with probability
1
.

Proof.

Choose
\tilde{\Gamma}\in\mathcal{B}([0,1])
with
\lambda(\tilde{\Gamma})=1
in such a way that for every

x\in\tilde{\Gamma}
we have
that
(K_{\mathfrak{Cb}_{N}(A)}(x,\cdot))_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly to
K_{A}(x,\cdot)
.
Setting
\Gamma:=\tilde{\Gamma}\setminus\bigcup_{N=2}^{\infty}\{0,\frac{1}{N},\frac{2}{N},\ldots,\frac{N-1}{N},1\}
obviously yields

\lambda(\Gamma)=1
.

Fix
x\in\Gamma
and consider some
y\in(0,1)
with

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}K_{\mathfrak{Cb}_{N}(A)}(x,[0,y])=K_{A}(x,[0,y]).

(36)

Plugging in the empirical copula, setting
I_{n}:=|K_{\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)}(E_{n})}(x,[0,y])-K_{A}(x,[0,y])|
,
and using the triangle inequality yields

\displaystyle I_{n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|K_{\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)}(E_{n})}(x,[0,y])-K_{\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)}(A)}(x,[0,y])|+|K_{\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)}(A)}(x,[0,y])-K_{A}(x,[0,y])|

The second summand converges to
0
for
n\rightarrow\infty
due to (
36
). In order to show that the first summand converges to
0
almost surely, denote by

i_{N}(x)\,,

N\geq 2\,,
the unique integer in
\{1,\ldots,N\}
fulfilling

x\in(\frac{i_{N}(x)-1}{N},\frac{i_{N}(x)}{N})
.

Since
\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)}(E_{n})
and

\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)}(A)
are checkerboard copulas, we have

\displaystyle K_{\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)}(E_{n})}(x,[0,y])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N(n)\left(E_{n}\left(\frac{i_{N(n)}(x)}{N(n)},y\right)-E_{n}\left(\frac{i_{N(n)}(x)-1}{N(n)},y\right)\right)

\displaystyle K_{\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)}(A)}(x,[0,y])

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle N(n)\left(A\left(\frac{i_{N(n)}(x)}{N(n)},y\right)-A\left(\frac{i_{N(n)}(x)-1}{N(n)},y\right)\right).

This, however, implies

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}|K_{\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)}(E_{n})}(x,[0,y])-K_{\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)}(A)}(x,[0,y])|\leq 2\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}N(n)\,d_{\infty}(E_{n},A)=0\,,

\mathbb{P}
-almost surely, whereby the last identity is a consequence of the definition of

N(n)
and the fact that according to
[
21
, Lemma 1]
the empirical copula
E_{n}
satisfies

d_{\infty}(E_{n},A)=O\left(\sqrt{\frac{\log(\log(n))}{n}}\right)

(37)

with probability
1
. Altogether it follows that
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I_{n}=0

almost surely.

Since all but at most countably many
y\in(0,1)
fulfill (
36
)
and weak convergence is equivalent to convergence on a dense set, we have shown
weak convergence of
(K_{CB_{N(n)}(E_{n})}(x,\cdot))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
to

(K_{A}(x,\cdot))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
for
every
x\in\Gamma
, which completes the proof.
∎

Having this, the following main result of this section is straightforward to prove.

Theorem 4.2

(Strongly consistent estimator for
\Lambda_{\varphi}(A)
)
.

Let
(X_{1},Y_{1}),(X_{2},Y_{2}),\ldots
be a random sample from
(X,Y)
, assume
that
(X,Y)
has continuous distribution function, and let
A
denote
the copula of
(X,Y)
. Furthermore choose
s
and the resolution
N(n)
as in Lemma
4.1
.
If
\varphi
is continuous, then
\Lambda_{\varphi}(\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)}(E_{n}))
is a strongly consistent estimator for
\Lambda_{\varphi}(A)
, i.e.,
with probability
1
, we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Lambda_{\varphi}(\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)}(E_{n}))=\Lambda_{\varphi}(A).

Proof.

According to (the proof of) Lemma
4.1
we have that

(K_{CB_{N(n)}}(u,\cdot))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

converges weakly to
K_{A}(u,\cdot)
for every
u\in\Gamma
.
As a direct consequence, for
u_{1},u_{2}\in\Gamma
it follows that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}|K_{CB_{N(n)(E_{n})}}(u_{1},[0,y]))-K_{CB_{N(n)(E_{n})}}(u_{2},[0,y]))|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|K_{A}(u_{1},[0,y]))-K_{A}(u_{2},[0,y]))|,

hence, considering
\lambda_{2}(\Gamma\times\Gamma)=1
, using eq. (
33
), and applying dominated convergence we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\int_{[0,1]}|K_{\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)(E_{n})}}(u_{1},[0,y]))-K_{\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)(E_{n})}}(u_{2},[0,y]))|^{p}d\lambda(y)\,d\lambda_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\int_{[0,1]}|K_{A}(u_{1},[0,y]))-K_{A}(u_{2},[0,y]))|^{p}d\lambda(y)\,d\lambda_{2}(u_{1},u_{2})=\Lambda_{p}(A).

∎

Remark 4.3
.

Notice that according to Theorem
4.2
strong consistency holds in full
generality, i.e., without any smoothness restrictions for the copula
A
.
We also conjecture that the estimator
\Lambda_{\varphi}(\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)}(E_{n}))

is asymptotically normal, a proof, however, seems still out of reach,
see, e.g.,
[
35
]
.

Remark 4.4
.

The checkerboard estimator can also be used for estimating
\Lambda_{\Delta}(Y|X)
in the more general case where the distance function
\Delta
is at least component-wise continuous with respect to weak convergence and invariant with respect to strictly increasing transformations of
Y
.

5
Simulation Study and Real Data Example

In this section we first study the performance of the checkerboard estimator
\Lambda_{\varphi}(\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)}(E_{n}))
from Theorem
4.2

and then quickly discuss a real data example.

5.1
Simulation study

We consider the continuous setting as studied in the previous section focus on the following choices for the convex function
\varphi
- notice that that
\varphi

is convex and strictly convex in
0=\varphi(0)
, so
\Lambda_{\varphi}

is a dependence measure:

(i)

\varphi(x)=|x|^{p}
for
p\in\{1,2,3\}\,,

(ii)

\varphi(x)=e^{cx}-1
for
c\in\{1/5,1,5\}\,,

(iii)

\varphi(x)=e^{|cx|}-1
for
c\in\{1/5,1,5\}\,.

As dependence structures we chose copulas
A
from the Marshall-Olkin (MO) family, defined by

M_{\alpha,\beta}(u,v):=\begin{cases}u^{1-\alpha}v\,,&\text{if}\quad u^{\alpha}\geq v^{\beta}\,,\\
uv^{1-\beta}\,,&\text{if}\quad u^{\alpha}<v^{\beta}\,,\end{cases}

with
\alpha,\beta\in[0,1]
and consider

(\alpha,\beta)\in\{(1,0),(1,1),(0.2,0.7),(0.3,1)\}.

Figures
1
to
4
illustrate the behavior of the estimator

\Lambda_{\varphi}(\mathfrak{Cb}_{N(n)}(E_{n}))
from Theorem
4.2

for each of the chosen copulas
A
and sample sizes given by

n\in\{10,50,100,500,1.000,5.000,10.000\},

where each scenario was simulated
1.000
times.
Estimation of the empirical checkerboard copula was performed using the command
ecbc
from the R package
qad
with the default of
s=1/2
(see
[
22
, Remark 3.14]
for a detailed discussion of this choice).
Figures
1
to
4
can be interpreted as follows:

•

the measures with convex function
\varphi
of type (
ii
) perform best in all cases in terms of speed of convergence.
One possible explanation of this observation is asymmetry of

\varphi(x)=e^{cx}-1\,:
The number of distinct values over which the integral runs is potentially twice as large and might therefore speed up convergence.
As a drawback, however, for these functions,
\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)
often attains small values, see Figures
3
and
4
, which,
however should not be confused with independence as considered in Figure
1
.

•

\Lambda_{\varphi}(Y|X)
is decreasing in the parameters
p
and
c
(compare with Example
3
). Consequently, in the case of complete dependence, the measures with parameter
p=1
and
c=1/5
perform best because deviations are less penalized by the shape of the function
\varphi
than
by the other cases. In contrast to that, if the parameters
p
and
c
are large, then the measure
\Lambda_{\varphi}
is more sensitive for detecting independence.

•

the
L^{2}
-version
\varphi(x)=x^{2}
as well as the asymmetric version
\varphi(x)=e^{x}-1
perform best over all considered cases.
