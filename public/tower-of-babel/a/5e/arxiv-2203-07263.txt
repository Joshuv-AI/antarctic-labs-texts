Logical shadow tomography: Efficient estimation of error-mitigated observables
Hong-Ye Hu, Ryan LaRose, Yi-Zhuang You, Eleanor Rieffel, Zhihui Wang

Abstract
We introduce a technique to estimate error-mitigated expectation values on noisy quantum computers. Our technique performs shadow tomography on a logical state to produce a memory-efficient classical reconstruction of the noisy density matrix. Using efficient classical post-processing, one can mitigate errors by projecting a general nonlinear function of the noisy density matrix into the codespace. The subspace expansion and virtual distillation can be viewed as special cases of the new framekwork. We show our method is favorable in the quantum and classical resources overhead. Relative to subspace expansion which requires O ⁡ ( 2 N ) O\left(2^{N}\right) samples to estimate a logical Pauli observable with [ [ N , k ] ] [[N,k]] error correction code, our technique requires only O ⁡ ( 4 k ) O\left(4^{k}\right) samples. Relative to virtual distillation, our technique can compute powers of the density matrix without additional copies of quantum states or quantum memory. We present numerical evidence using logical states encoded with up to sixty physical qubits and show fast convergence to error-free expectation values with only 10 5 10^{5} samples under 1% depolarizing noise.

. A common and crucial element of each experiment is mitigating the effect of errors, a task which is generally referred to as quantum error mitigation (QEM)

A relatively large number of QEM techniques have been proposed in recent literature

;

. At its core, any QEM technique uses additional quantum resources (qubits, gates, and/or samples) in a clever way to approximate what would happen in an ideal device.

In this work, we are interested in error mitigation in the region between noisy-intermediate scale quantum (NISQ) and fault-tolerant quantum computation. Especially, we present a conceptually simple and practical QEM technique. In our setup, we use quantum error correction code to distribute logical qubits information with multiple physical qubits. Without active quantum error correction which requires extra ancilla qubits and parity check measurements, the only step which happens on a quantum computer is repeatedly sampling from an encoded state to perform shadow tomography
aaronson_shadow_2018
;
huang_predicting_2020
. After this, one can use classical post-processing to project out errors as in subspace expansion
mcclean_decoding_2019
and calculate powers of the density matrix as in virtual distillation
huggins_virtual_2021
. In addition to enabling both of these methods, our technique requires significantly fewer resources. Especially, we show 1) the quantum gate overhead is independent of the number of logical qubits and we do not require multiple copies of the physical systems, 2) the sample complexity for estimating error mitigated Pauli observables only scales with the number of logical qubits instead of the total number of physical qubits, and 3) there exists an efficient classical algorithm that can post-process data with polynomial time. We show the new method is practical for relatively large systems with numerical evidence.

II
Logical shadow tomography

II.1
Motivation

Figure 1:
Graphic illustration of logical shadow tomography. (a) Red dots are logical qubits, and blue dots are physical qubits. Logical information is distributed to physical qubits by error correction code, then followed by noisy quantum computation on physical qubits. To get estimation of error mitigated observables, we perform classical shadow tomography on the noisy physical state. Particularly, we can apply random Clifford gates denoted as green blocks from some unitary ensemble
\mathcal{U}
, and take computational basis measurements. (b) A special case using
[[n,1]]
code for each logical qubit. In shadow tomography, we apply random unitary from tensor product of Clifford groups
{\mathcal{C}\ell}(2^{n})^{\otimes k}
. Additional gate depth will not scale with number of logical qubits
k
, and sample complexity for estimating error mitigated logical Pauli observables is the same as using global Clifford group
{\mathcal{C}\ell}(2^{nk})
.

One central task in quantum information processing is to estimate the expectation value of an observable
O
with respect to a pure state
\rho={|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|}
, i.e.
\langle O\rangle={\langle\psi|}O{|\psi\rangle}=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho O)
.
If the state is prepared by a quantum device, it can be noisy, and we instead evaluate
\langle O\rangle_{\text{noisy}}=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{\mathcal{E}}O)
by direct measurement, where
\rho_{\mathcal{E}}=\mathcal{E}(\rho)
denotes the noisy state that is corrupted from the target state
\rho
by an unknown noisy quantum channel
\mathcal{E}
.
Given the corrupted state
\rho_{\mathcal{E}}
, the goal of quantum error mitigation is to estimate a quantity
\langle O\rangle_{\text{QEM}}
that is closer to the target value
\langle O\rangle
compared to the direct measurement, such that

\left|\langle O\rangle_{\text{QEM}}-\langle O\rangle\right|<\left|\langle O\rangle_{\text{noisy}}-\langle O\rangle\right|.

(1)

QEM can be performed on either physical devices or during data post-processing.
In this work, we are interested in error mitigation in the region between noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) and fault-tolerant quantum computation, where the number of qubits is more than a few but cannot fulfill the requirement of fault-tolerance. We ask the question whether we can use those resources cleverly to achieve a more reliable quantum computation. QEM methods related to this idea are subspace expansion
mcclean_decoding_2019
and the virtual distillation
huggins_virtual_2021
. The key contribution of this work is to propose the application of classical shadow tomography
aaronson_shadow_2018
;
huang_predicting_2020
to reduce the quantum and classical resources needed to perform these QEM schemes, and provide rigorous analysis on its error mitigation capability.

The subspace expansion approach starts by encoding
k
logical qubits with
N
physical qubits via an error correction code
[[N,k]]
. Consider using a stabilizer code defined by a stabilizer group
\mathcal{S}=\langle S_{1},S_{2},\cdots,S_{N-k}\rangle
, the code subspace is specified by the projection operator

\Pi=\prod_{j=1}^{N-k}\frac{\mathds{1}+S_{j}}{2}=\frac{1}{2^{N-k}}\sum_{M\in\mathcal{S}}M,

(2)

where
M
denote group elements in
\mathcal{S}
as products of the stabilizers. Errors may occur to the physical state during the quantum information processing, which generally takes the physical state away from the code subspace. Suppose the goal is to estimate the logical observable
O
only, then even without active error correction, a simple projection of the the corrupted physical state
\rho_{\mathcal{E}}
back to the code subspace can already mitigate the error for the logical observable
mcclean_decoding_2019

\langle O\rangle_{\text{QEM}}=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi\rho_{\mathcal{E}}\Pi^{\dagger}O)}{\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi\rho_{\mathcal{E}}\Pi^{\dagger})}=\frac{1}{2^{n-k}c}\sum_{M\in\mathcal{S}}\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{\mathcal{E}}MO),

(3)

where
c=\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi\rho_{\mathcal{E}}\Pi^{\dagger})
. This amounts to measuring
\langle MO\rangle
on the corrupted physical state for all elements
M
in the stabilizer group
\mathcal{S}
(or for a majority of
M
sampled from
\mathcal{S}
). This approach can quickly become exponentially expensive when
N-k
becomes large.

Another approach for QEM goes under the name of virtual distillation. Assuming the target state is a pure state as the leading eigen state of
\rho_{\mathcal{E}}
. The sub-leading eigen states of
\rho_{\mathcal{E}}
(as errors orthogonal to the target state) can be suppressed by powering the density matrix
\rho_{\mathcal{E}}^{m}
, and we estimate
\langle O\rangle_{\text{QEM}}=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{\mathcal{E}}^{m}O)/\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{\mathcal{E}}^{m})
. Or more generally, a polynomial function
f(\rho_{\mathcal{E}})=c_{0}\mathds{1}+c_{1}\rho_{\mathcal{E}}+c_{2}\rho_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}+\cdots+c_{m}\rho_{\mathcal{E}}^{m}
of
\rho_{\mathcal{E}}
can be considered, with an optimal choice of the coefficients
c_{0},\cdots,c_{m}
to best mitigate the error, such that

\langle O\rangle_{\text{QEM}}=\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(f(\rho_{\mathcal{E}})O)}{\operatorname{Tr}(f(\rho_{\mathcal{E}}))}.

(4)

However, powering a density matrix on quantum devices typically involves making multiple copies of the quantum system, which can be challenging and expensive in quantum resources.

The key observation of this work is that both Eq. (
3
) and Eq. (
4
) (or their combination) can be efficiently evaluated in the classical post-processing phase after performing the classical shadow tomography
huang_predicting_2020
on the noisy physical state
\rho_{\mathcal{E}}
. The classical shadow tomography uses randomized measurements to extract information from an unknown quantum state, and predicts physical properties about the state efficiently by post-processing collected measurement outcomes on a classical computer. When the measurement basis are chosen to be Pauli or Clifford basis, the post-processing can be made efficient. The code subspace projection and the powering of density matrix can all be implemented efficiently in the post-processing phase by classical computation, given the Clifford nature of the classical shadows. In this way, we can implement the existing error mitigation schemes with significantly reduced quantum and classical resources.

II.3
Analysis

In the previous section, we have outlined the procedure of logical shadow tomography (LST). Here, we will analyze its performance from three perspectives: 1) error mitigation capacity, 2) quantum resources, and 3) classical resources. Particularly, in
error mitigation capacity
subsection, we show how error is suppressed with the code distance
d
of the error correction code and powers of density matrix. In
quantum resources
subsection, we show the gate overhead is similar to the original proposal of subspace expansion, except for a shallow depth Clifford circuit whose depth does not depend on the number of logical qubits. Compared to virtual distillation, our method only requires one copy of the physical system. In addition, we also show the sample complexity has an exponential reduction compared to the direct implementation of subspace expansion for estimating logical Pauli observables. In
classical resources
subsection, we outline the general classical algorithm for post-processing the data. Particularly, we show there exists fast algorithm for LST with
f(\rho_{\mathcal{E}})=\rho_{\mathcal{E}}
and its algorithm time complexity is
O(N^{3})
. This allows our method scale to large system size.

II.3.1
Error mitigation capability

\bullet

Code space projection
. Let
\mathcal{S}
be the stabilizer code used in LST. For any correctable error
E
, there exists a stabilizer generator
S
such that
SE=-ES
and so

\Pi E|\psi\rangle\propto(\mathds{1}+S)E|\psi\rangle=E(|\psi\rangle-|\psi\rangle)=0.

(8)

Analogously, if no error has occurred then
|\psi\rangle
is a codeword and

\Pi|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle.

(9)

Thus the projector
\Pi
discards results in which correctable errors have occurred
mcclean_decoding_2019
. The set of correctable errors is determined by the chosen code. Assume a simple noise model where each qubit is subjected to depolarizing noise with rate
p
. If a single error happened on one qubit. Then it can be projected out given the fact that single Pauli operator is not the stabilizer group. Those errors are non-logical errors.

The code space projection fails when more local error happens and they form a logical operator. The probability of having this failure is
p^{d}
, where
d
is the code distance of the error correction code. Mathematically, we can write down

\begin{split}\rho_{\mathcal{E}}=(1-p)^{N}\rho_{0}+p\rho_{1}+p^{2}\rho_{2}+\dots,\end{split}

(10)

where
\rho_{0}={|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{0}|}
is the ideal quantum state, and
N
is the system size.
\rho_{1}
is the quantum state subjected to error happened to one qubit, i.e.

\begin{split}\rho_{1}=&(XI\cdot\cdot I)\rho_{0}(XI\cdot\cdot I)+(IX\cdot\cdot I)\rho_{0}(IX\cdot\cdot I)+\dots\end{split}

(11)

Similarly,
\rho_{2}
is the quantum state subjected to two error happened, i.e.

\begin{split}\rho_{2}=&(XIY\cdot\cdot I)\rho_{0}(XIY\cdot\cdot I)\\
&+(IXX\cdot\cdot I)\rho_{0}(IXX\cdot\cdot I)+\dots\end{split}

(12)

Therefore, for any logical observable
O
, we have

\begin{split}&\dfrac{\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi\rho_{\mathcal{E}}\Pi O)}{\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi\rho_{\mathcal{E}}\Pi)}\\
&={\langle\psi_{0}|}O{|\psi_{0}\rangle}\left[1+O\left(p^{d}\dfrac{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{d}O)}{{\langle\psi_{0}|}O{|\psi_{0}\rangle}}\right)\right].\end{split}

(13)

More details can be found in Appendix
B
.

\bullet

Virtual distillation
. For completeness, we will review the virtual distillation theory here. With the spectral decomposition of the noisy density matrix, one can write

\rho_{\mathcal{E}}=p_{0}|\psi_{0}\rangle\langle\psi_{0}|+p_{1}|\psi_{1}\rangle\langle\psi_{1}|+\cdots+p_{n}|\psi_{n}\rangle\langle\psi_{n}|,

(14)

where
p_{0}>\cdots>p_{n}
and
\langle\psi_{i}|\psi_{j}\rangle=\delta_{ij}
. For shallow circuit, it is reasonable to assume
|\psi_{0}\rangle\equiv|\psi\rangle
is the noiseless state.

In this case, for any observable
O
and positive integer
m
we have
huggins_virtual_2021
;
yoshioka_generalized_2021

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{\mathcal{E}}^{m}O)}{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{\mathcal{E}}^{m})}

\displaystyle=\frac{p_{0}^{m}\langle\psi_{0}|O|\psi_{0}\rangle+\sum_{i}p_{i}^{m}\langle\psi_{i}|O|\psi_{i}\rangle}{p_{0}^{m}+\sum_{i}p_{i}^{m}}

(15)

\displaystyle=\langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle\left[1+O\left(\left(\dfrac{p_{1}}{p_{0}}\right)^{m}\dfrac{{\langle\psi_{1}|}O{|\psi_{1}\rangle}}{{\langle\psi|}O{|\psi\rangle}}\right)\right].

Thus, computing the expectation value with the
m
th power of the state suppresses errors exponentially in
m
, a phenomenon which can also be interpreted as artificially cooling the system
cotler_quantum_2019
. Here we allow for an arbitrary function
f
acting on the noisy state via its Taylor expansion
f(x)=\sum_{p=1}^{m}c_{p}x^{p}
. Including sums of powers up to
m
(instead of just the highest power
m
) was shown to improve results of numerical experiments in
yoshioka_generalized_2021
.

\bullet

LST (Combined approach)
. LST has the error mitigation capability of code space projection and virtual distillation. When we combine code space projection with virtual distillation, we expect both code distance
d
of the error correction code and
m
th power of the density matrix will suppress the error. Especially, when
m=2
in
\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi\rho_{\epsilon}^{m}\Pi O)
, i.e. one projects the squared density matrix to code space, the order of error suppression is improved from
\mathcal{O}(p^{d})
to
\mathcal{O}(p^{2d})
. In general, higher order of the power
m
will lead to stronger error mitigation effect. (See Appendix
B
for more details.)

II.3.2
Quantum resources

\bullet

Gate overhead
. LST requires additional qubits and gates to encode the logical state, the exact number of which depends on the chosen code. Restricting to stabilizer codes, the logical state preparation only requires implementing Clifford gates, which are presumably easier to implement on NISQ devices compared to universal quantum computation gate set. This encoding overhead is the same as the subspace expansion method. To perform the classical shadow tomography, an element from a unitary ensemble
\mathcal{U}
is appended to the quantum circuit before measuring all qubits in the computational basis. If
\mathcal{U}
is chosen to be a global Clifford ensemble, the added circuit depth is
\mathcal{O}(N)
with local random unitary gates
brandao_local_2016
. The gate overhead associated with this can be significant. If
\mathcal{U}
is chosen to be a tensor-product Pauli ensemble, the added circuit depth is
\mathcal{O}(1)

huang_predicting_2020
. The gate overhead is more affordable. However, the Pauli measurement will increase the sample complexity exponentially for non-local observables. Facing this dilemma between Clifford v.s. Pauli measurement, a recent work
hu_classical_2021
by part of the authors has developed an efficient classical shadow tomography approach for
finite-depth local
Clifford circuits, which can smoothly interpolate between the global Clifford and the local Pauli limit. Using shallow (finite-depth) Clifford circuits for shadow tomography, the gate overhead can be significantly reduced with only a mild increase of sample complexity.
hu_classical_2021
This approach can be combined with our error mitigation technique seamlessly to achieve an optimal balance between gate overhead and sample complexity. For this work, we will use a tensor-product Clifford group
{\mathcal{C}\ell}(2^{n})^{\otimes k}
as shown in Fig.
1
(b). The added circuit depth is
\mathcal{O}(n)
, where
n
is the physical qubits in
[[n,1]]
code for one logical qubit. It is important to notice that the depth of additional circuit is independent of the number of logical qubit
k
.

\bullet

Sample complexity
.
We now consider number of measurements needed for classical post-processing in LST.
If one uses a
[[n,1]]
code for each logical qubit and a tensor-product Clifford group
{\mathcal{C}\ell}(2^{n})^{\otimes k}
, where
k
is number of logical qubits, we have the following theorem which dictates the sample complexity of LST.

Theorem 1
(Details in Appendix
D
)

Let
\rho
be a
k
logical qubits quantum state, where each logical qubit is encoded with a
[[n,1]]
code, and
\Pi
be the associated projection operator to the code subspace. Then one needs
\mathcal{O}(\log(M)4^{k}/\epsilon\delta^{2})
samples to produce an estimation
\tilde{O}_{i}
of
\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi\rho\Pi O_{i})
with
\{i=1,\dots,M\}
of logical Pauli observables

such that

\text{Pr}\left(\left|\tilde{O}_{i}-\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi\rho\Pi O_{i})\right|\geq\epsilon\right)\leq\delta.

(16)

The result (
16
) applies to both the numerator and denominator of the LST estimate Eq. (
6
) separately. We emphasize that the number of samples does not depend on the total number of physical qubits
nk
, but only depends on the number of logical qubits
k
, and scales as
\mathcal{O}(4^{k})
. Compared to direct implementation of subspace expansion whose sample complexity is
\mathcal{O}(2^{nk})
, our method dramatically reduces the sample complexity.

In Ref.
mcclean_decoding_2019
, the authors also mentioned stochastic sampling of the stabilizer group elements to reduce the sample complexity at the price of losing the error mitigation capacity. Our approach bypasses the need to sample the all elements in the stabilizer group, as we can implement the projection operator directly and efficiently by data post-processing. Our advantage will be even more apparent if one uses larger
n
error correction code
[[n,1]]
, which provides larger code distance and better error mitigation capacity.

II.3.3
Classical resources

In this section, we show that there exists efficient algorithm for classical post-processing. Especially, for LST with
f(\rho_{\mathcal{E}})=\rho_{\mathcal{E}}
(no virtual distillation), the classical post-processing can be performed with polynomial classical memory and time.

After sampling, we have the classical shadow Eq. (
5
) consisting of
N
stabilizer states
U^{\dagger}{|b\rangle}{\langle b|}U
, the observable
O
, and the projector
\Pi
. We need to estimate the numerator and denominator of Eq. (
6
), both of which can be written

\operatorname{Tr}[f(\rho_{\mathcal{E}})\Gamma]

(17)

where
\Gamma=\Pi O
for the numerator and
\Gamma=\Pi
for the denominator. Let
m
be the highest degree of the Taylor expansion of
f
. As in
huang_predicting_2020
, we can evaluate the expectation of this term via

\mathbb{E}\operatorname{Tr}[\hat{\rho}_{i_{1}}\cdots\hat{\rho}_{i_{m}}\Gamma],

(18)

where
\{\hat{\rho}_{i_{1}}\dots\hat{\rho}_{i_{m}}
are independent samples. A single classical shadow
\hat{\rho}_{i}
requires
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
classical memory to store (where
N=nk
is the total number of qubits), so the argument inside the trace in (
18
) requires
\mathcal{O}(N^{2}m)
classical memory
aaronson_improved_2004
. In Appendix
A.1
, we showed that the evaluation of (
18
) boils down to evaluating the general form

\operatorname{Tr}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{l}(a_{j}\mathds{1}+b_{j}M_{j})\bigg),

(19)

where
M_{j}
are Pauli operators. This can be solved by finding the null space
\mathcal{N}_{A}
of a binary matrix representation of Pauli operators
\{M_{j}\}
. By simple counting argument (see Appendix
A.1
for more details), we see the evaluation of Eq. (
18
) has time complexity upper bounded by
\mathcal{O}\left(mN^{2}(mN+N-k+1)+m|\mathcal{N}_{A}|\right)
, when
m\geq 2
, where
k
is number of logical qubits,
N=nk
is the total number of physical qubits,
m
is power of density matrix, and
|\mathcal{N}_{A}|
is the volume of binary null space
\mathcal{N}_{A}
determined by a set of classical shadows
\{\hat{\rho}_{i_{1}},\dots,\hat{\rho}_{i_{m}}\}
. When
m
is large, the null space
\mathcal{N}_{A}
can be very large. In practice, the evaluation of (
18
) can be slow given the time complexity is proportional to
\mathcal{O}(m|\mathcal{N}_{A}|)
for
m\geq 2
.

We also find an improved classical algorithm for
m=1
with time complexity
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
. When
m=1
, we would like to evaluate
\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi\rho\Pi O)
, where
\rho
is proportional to a stabilizer state, which can be represented using stabilizer tableau. The intuition behind the efficient algorithm is that a stabilizer state can be efficiently projected by another stabilizer group projector by updating its stabilizer tableau. We leave the detail of the algorithm to Appendix
A.2
.

In conclusion, at least for the
m=1
(no virtual distillation) case, the post-processing complexity is polynomial
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
in the total qubit number
N
.

III
Numerical results

In Sec.
II.3
, we discussed the error mitigation capabilities, quantum resources, and classical resources of LST. One should notice that the discussion of sample complexity is mainly focused on estimation of
\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi\rho\Pi^{\dagger}O)
. While in practice, we would like to estimate the ratio of
\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi\rho\Pi^{\dagger}O)/\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi\rho\Pi^{\dagger})
. The sample complexity of the ratio does not have a closed form in general. This problem is not unique to our approach. It also exists for the subspace expansion
mcclean_decoding_2019
and virtual distillation

.

Nevertheless, in the following, we will use numerical experiments to investigate the sample complexity. We demonstrate the performance of LST through numerical simulation of large systems. In particular, we find LST outperforms the direct implementation of the subspace expansion and the sample complexity scaling is very close to our theoretical prediction in small noise region.

IV
Discussion

We have presented a procedure for estimating error-mitigated observables on noisy quantum computers. Our procedure is flexible enough to be performed on virtually any quantum computer: the only additional quantum resources needed are qubits and Clifford gates for encoding the logical state. After sampling from the logical state, a classical computer processes the obtained classical shadow to return the error-mitigated expectation value. In the analysis, we show if the error correction code has code distance
d
and
f(\rho_{\mathcal{E}})=\rho_{\mathcal{E}}
for LST, then error will be suppressed to
\mathcal{O}(p^{d})
, assuming independent depolarizing noise with rate
p
on each physical qubit. And we also show higher power of density matrix will further improve the performance. For sample complexity, we show it scales only with number of logical qubits
k
but not the number of encoding physical qubits
n
. This result is also supported with large scale numerics. In addition, we provide efficient classical algorithms to post-process the classical shadow data and remark that this post-processing can be easily parallelized for practical efficiency in real-world experiments.

With respect to error mitigation, our procedure provides an experimentally simple procedure to carry out proposed error mitigation techniques
mcclean_decoding_2019
;
huggins_virtual_2021
at scale. In particular, we have demonstrated subspace expansion with up to
n=60
physical qubits encoding a single logical qubit, i.e., a stabilizer group with
2^{59}
elements, an experiment which would be practically infeasible with the direct or stochastic sampling schemes of
mcclean_decoding_2019
;
yoshioka_generalized_2021
. We have also implemented virtual distillation
huggins_virtual_2021
without expensive swap operations to compute powers of the density matrix. Rather, our procedure uses the same quantum circuit to evaluate the error-mitigated expectation with any function
f(\rho_{\mathcal{E}})
of the noisy state
\rho_{\mathcal{E}}
; the only difference is in classical post-processing (and number of shadows needed). Note that virtual distillation without subspace expansion can be implemented in our protocol by using a trivial code (i.e., not encoding a logical state). Further, beyond making both of these techniques significantly more practical to implement, our procedure enables them to be composed with one another, and we have shown numerically the composition of both techniques results in further reduction of errors. Additional error mitigation techniques which act on the noisy state, e.g., those in
yoshioka_generalized_2021
, may also be implementable with our framework.

In our analysis, we assumed the Clifford circuit in the classical shadow part is noise-free. If there are noise in the Clifford circuit part, it can be mitigated if the noise is independent of the Clifford gates, as in
robust_shadow2021
;
2020arXiv201111580E
;
2022arXiv220203272B
, where similar idea was used for randomized benchmarking
PhysRevLett.106.180504
;
PRXQuantum.2.010351
.

Shadow tomography since proposed in
huang_predicting_2020
has found a number of applications in quantum information processing, including the recently proposed process tomography
levy_classical_2021
, and avoiding barren plateau in variational quantum circuits
2022arXiv220108194S
. This work constitutes an application in the error mitigation realm. We are optimistic our procedure will be effective on current and near-term quantum computers for a variety of experiments on relatively large systems.

In a recent literature
cliffordgatemitigation
, the authors discuss an error mitigation method to implement low noise logical gates for error correction codes. In comparison, our method focuses on the error mitigation of the final quantum states of the logical qubits.

While we were finishing the writing of the paper we learned of related work “
Shadow Distillation: Quantum Error Mitigation with Classical Shadows for Near-Term Quantum Processors
” by Alireza Seif, Ze-Pei Cian, Sisi Zhou, Senrui Chen, and
Liang Jiang
AlirezaQEM
.

Appendix A
Stabilizer algorithms

A.1
Evaluating the trace in Eq. (
7
)

In this appendix, we explain an efficient approach to evaluate the trace in Eq. (
7
). For random Clifford ensemble, the reconstruction map reads
\hat{\rho}=\mathcal{M}^{-1}(\hat{\sigma})=(2^{n}+1)\hat{\sigma}-\mathds{1}
, such that

\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\rho}}\operatorname{Tr}\bigg(\Pi\Big(\prod_{s=1}^{m}\hat{\rho}_{s}\Big)\Pi^{\dagger}O\bigg)=\sum_{q=0}^{m}\binom{m}{q}(2^{n}+1)^{q}(-1)^{m-q}\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\sigma}}\operatorname{Tr}\bigg(\Pi\Big(\prod_{s=1}^{q}\hat{\sigma}_{s}\Big)\Pi^{\dagger}O\bigg).

(20)

Notice that the projection operator
\Pi=\prod_{j=1}^{n-k}(\mathds{1}+S_{j})/2
and the snapshot state
\hat{\sigma}=U^{\dagger}{|b\rangle}{\langle b|}U=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(\mathds{1}+b_{i}U^{\dagger}Z_{i}U)/2
both take the from of stabilizer states. So the problem boils down to evaluating the trace of the following general form

\operatorname{Tr}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{l}(a_{j}\mathds{1}+b_{j}M_{j})\bigg),

(21)

where
M_{j}
are Pauli operators and
a_{j},b_{j}
are real coefficients. As we expand the product, the only terms that survive the trace are those terms with the Pauli operators multiplied to the identity operator. To find these combination of Pauli operators, we can first encode every Pauli operator
M_{j}
as a binary vector following

M_{j}=\mathrm{i}^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{ij}\zeta_{ij}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{\xi_{ij}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}^{\zeta_{ij}}\to\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\vdots\\
\xi_{ij}\\
\zeta_{ij}\\
\vdots\end{array}\right),

(22)

where
\xi_{ij},\zeta_{ij}\in\{0,1\}
are binary variables. Arranging all the binary vector representations of
M_{j}
as column vectors, together they form a
2n\times l
matrix, denoted as
A
. Each combination of Pauli operators
M_{j}
that multiply to identity corresponds to a binary null vector solution
x
of the binary matrix
A
, as
Ax=0
(modulo 2). The null vectors form the null space of
A
, denoted as
\mathcal{N}_{A}
. The null space of binary matrix
A
can be found using Gaussian elimination method, and its time complexity is
\mathcal{O}(nl\times\text{min}(2n,l))
. For
x\in\mathcal{N}_{A}
,

\prod_{j=1}^{l}(M_{j})^{x_{j}}=z(x)\mathds{1},

(23)

which defines the phase factor
z(x)
given
x
. Then the trace in Eq. (
21
) is given by

\operatorname{Tr}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{l}(a_{j}\mathds{1}+b_{j}M_{j})\bigg)=2^{n}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{N}_{A}}z(x)\prod_{j=1}^{l}a_{j}^{1-x_{j}}b_{j}^{x_{j}}.

(24)

Therefore the time complexity of evaluating the general trace Eq. (
24
) is
\mathcal{O}(nl\times\text{min}(2n,l)+|\mathcal{N}_{A}|)
, where
|\mathcal{N}_{A}|
is the volume of the null space
\mathcal{N}_{A}
that is determined by the set of Pauli operators
\{M_{i}\}
. Applying this result for Eq. (
20
), we get the time complexity for evaluating
\operatorname{Tr}\left(\Pi\left(\prod^{m}_{s=1}\hat{\rho}_{s}\right)\Pi^{\dagger}O\right)
is upper bounded by
\mathcal{O}(mnl\times\text{min}(2n,l)+m|\mathcal{N}_{A}|)
, with
l=mn+n-k+1
. For large
m
, the volume of null space
|\mathcal{N}_{A}|
can be troublesome, and scale exponentially with
m
. But luckily for
m=1
, there exists more efficient polynomial time algorithm, which is illustrated in Appendix
A.2
.

A.2
Efficient projection of a stabilizer state

As shown in Eq. (
22
), any Pauli string operator can be represented as a one-hot binary vector
x
and
z
, with
x_{i},z_{i}=0,1
for
i=1,\dots,N
, where
N
is the total number of qubits,

\begin{split}\sigma_{(x,z)}=i^{x\cdot z}\prod^{N}_{i=1}X_{i}^{x_{i}}Z_{i}^{y_{i}},\end{split}

(25)

where
X_{i},Z_{i}
are Pauli operators, and
x_{i},z_{i}
are binary values. The multiplication of two Pauli operators can be represented as

\begin{split}\sigma_{(x,z)}\sigma_{(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})}=i^{p(x,z;x^{\prime},z^{\prime})}\sigma_{(x+x^{\prime},z+z^{\prime})\%2},\end{split}

(26)

where the phase factor is

\begin{split}p(x,z;x^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(z_{i}x^{\prime}_{i}-x_{i}z^{\prime}_{i}+2(z_{i}+z^{\prime}_{i})\left\lfloor\frac{x_{i}+x^{\prime}_{i}}{2}\right\rfloor+2(x_{i}+x^{\prime}_{i})\left\lfloor\frac{z_{i}+z^{\prime}_{i}}{2}\right\rfloor\right)\mod 4.\end{split}

(27)

Any two Pauli strings either commute or anti-commute,

\sigma_{(x,z)}\sigma_{(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})}=(-)^{c(x,z;x^{\prime},z^{\prime})}\sigma_{(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})}\sigma_{(x,z)},

(28)

where the anticommutation indicator
c
has a simpler form

c(x,z;x^{\prime},z^{\prime})=\frac{p(x,z;x^{\prime},z^{\prime})-p(x^{\prime},z^{\prime};x,z)}{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(z_{i}x^{\prime}_{i}-x_{i}z^{\prime}_{i}\right)\mod 2.

(29)

Therefore, the complexity of calculating anticommutation indicator is
\mathcal{O}(N)
. The binary vectors
x
and
z
can be interweaved into a
2N
-component vector
g=(x_{0},z_{0},x_{1},z_{1},\cdots)
, which forms the binary representation of a Pauli operator
\sigma_{g}
.

Figure 4:
Data structure of a stabilizer state. Each Pauli string is represented as a binary vector. First
N
rows store the stabilizers of the state, and second
N
rows store the destabilizers of the state.

In Fig.
4
, each row is a binary representation of a Pauli string. For a Hilbert space with dimension
\mathbb{C}^{2^{N}}
, we can find at most
N
stabilizers
\{S_{i},i=1\dots N\}
and
N
destabilizers
\{D_{i},i=1\dots N\}
, with
[S_{i},S_{j}]=0
,
[D_{i},D_{j}]=0
and
\{S_{i},D_{j}\}=\delta_{i,j}
. Fig.
4
is called the stabilizer tableau of stabilizer state. We use
r
in Fig.
4
indicates the
\log-2
rank of the density matrix. For pure stabilizer state,
r=0
. And if
r>0
, then stabilizer tableau represents a mixed state and only partial Hilbert space is stabilized.

A stabilizer projector
\Pi=\prod_{k=1}^{l}\frac{\mathds{1}\pm G_{k}}{2}
can also be represented as a
l
-row tableau (only stabilizer). Now we are going to present an efficient algorithm to calculate
\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi\rho\Pi)
, and update the stabilizer tableau of a full rank stabilizer state
\rho
according to projection
\Pi
.

\bullet

Outline of the algorithm:
First, we set
\text{trace}=1
. Then we scan over every observable
G_{k}
in the generator of the operator. For each
G_{k}
, we continue to scan over all operators in the stabilizer tableau. If the observable
G_{k}
anticommute with:

1.

At least one active stabilizer (the first of them being
S_{p}
)
\to

G_{k}
is an
error
operator that take the state out of the code subspace
\to
stabilizer tableau need to be updated according to
\mathds{1}\pm G_{k}
. And trace will be multiplied by 1/2.

2.

Otherwise,
G_{k}
is in the stabilizer group generated by
\{S_{k}\}
. If the phase factor is compatible, then stabilizer state is eigenstate of
(\mathds{1}\pm G_{k})/2
with eigenvalue 1, i.e.
(\mathds{1}\pm G_{k}){|\psi\rangle}/2={|\psi\rangle}
; if the phase factor is incompatible, it means
(\mathds{1}\pm G_{k}){|\psi\rangle}/2=0
.

We see this algorithm has a double loop of
\mathcal{O}(N)
items, and each anticommutation check takes
\mathcal{O}(N)
time. Therefore, the time complexity of this algorithm is
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
, where
N
is the total number of qubits in the system.

Appendix B
Error mitigation capability

In Eq. (
10
), we show the density matrix subjected to depolarizing noise is naturally the spectral decomposition form. Here, we provide detailed proof of it. Let
{|\psi_{0}\rangle}
be the ideal quantum state encoded with
[[n,k,d]]
error correction code. And let
\{{|\bar{i}\rangle},i=1,\dots,2^{k}\}
be the orthonormal basis for the logical space. In general,
{|\psi_{0}\rangle}=\sum_{i=1}^{2^{k}}c_{i}{|\bar{i}\rangle}
. We assume the simple depolarizing error for each physical qubit. Then

\begin{split}\rho_{\epsilon}=(1-p)^{N}{|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{0}|}+p\rho_{1}+p^{2}\rho_{2}+\cdots,\end{split}

(30)

where
\rho_{i}
is the density matrix with
i
local error happened, and
N
is the system size. For example,

\begin{split}\rho_{1}=&(XII\cdots I){|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{0}|}(XII\cdots I)+(YII\cdots I){|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{0}|}(YII\cdots I)\\
&+(ZII\cdots I){|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{0}|}(ZII\cdots I)+(IXI\cdots I){|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{0}|}(IXI\cdots I)\\
&+\cdots+(II\cdots IZ){|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{0}|}(II\cdots IZ),\end{split}

(31)

and

\begin{split}\rho_{2}=&(XXI\cdots I){|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{0}|}(XXI\cdots I)+(XYI\cdots I){|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{0}|}(XYI\cdots I)\\
&+\cdots+(I\cdots ZZ){|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{0}|}(I\cdots ZZ).\end{split}

(32)

We define the support of a Pauli string as number of Pauli operators that is not the identity operator. Let
P_{l}
be a Pauli string operator with non-trivial support
l
. For example, the support of Pauli string
XIZYI
is three. Then any term in
\rho_{l}
can be written as
P_{l}{|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{0}|}P_{l}
with some
P_{l}
. It is easy to check
\Pi P_{l}{|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{0}|}P_{l}\Pi=0
for any
l<d
. By definition of the code distance
d
, any
P_{l}
with
l<d
is not in the stabilizer group. Therefore, it must anti-commute with some stabilizer generator
S
, such that
\{S,P_{l}\}=0
. We write
\Pi=\Pi^{{}^{\prime}}(\mathds{1}+S)
, where
\Pi^{{}^{\prime}}
includes all other stabilizer generators. Then

\Pi P_{l}{|\psi_{0}\rangle}=\Pi^{{}^{\prime}}(\mathds{1}+S)P_{l}{|\psi_{0}\rangle}=\Pi^{{}^{\prime}}P_{l}(\mathds{1}-S){|\psi_{0}\rangle}=\Pi^{{}^{\prime}}P_{l}({|\psi_{0}\rangle}-{|\psi_{0}\rangle})=0,

(33)

and

\Pi P_{l}{|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{0}|}P_{l}\Pi=0~~(l<d).

(34)

Therefore, we conclude
\Pi\rho_{l}\Pi=0
for any
l<d
, and

\begin{split}&\dfrac{\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi\rho_{\mathcal{E}}\Pi O)}{\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi\rho_{\mathcal{E}}\Pi)}\\
&={\langle\psi_{0}|}O{|\psi_{0}\rangle}\left[1+O\left(p^{d}\dfrac{\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_{d}O)}{{\langle\psi_{0}|}O{|\psi_{0}\rangle}}\right)\right].\end{split}

(35)

Now we want to prove the leading order correction of
\Pi\rho_{\epsilon}^{2}\Pi
is of order
\mathcal{O}(p^{2d})
with contradiction. Suppose the leading order correction is of order
\mathcal{O}(p^{s})
with
s<2d
, then there exist
P_{l}
and
P_{r}
with
l+r=s<2d
such that

\Pi(P_{l}{|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{0}|}P_{l})(P_{r}{|\psi_{0}\rangle}{\langle\psi_{0}|}P_{r})\Pi={\langle\psi_{0}|}P_{l}P_{r}{|\psi_{0}\rangle}(\Pi P_{l}{|\psi_{0}\rangle})({\langle\psi_{0}|}P_{r}\Pi)\neq 0.

(36)

This requires
\Pi P_{l}{|\psi_{0}\rangle}\neq 0
and
\Pi P_{r}{|\psi_{0}\rangle}\neq 0
. From Eq. (
34
), we know this requires
l\geq d
and
r\geq d
, and it contradicts with
l+r<2d
. Therefore, we conclude the leading order correction of
\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi\rho^{2}_{\epsilon}\Pi O)
is of order
\mathcal{O}(p^{2d})
.

For higher power of
\Pi\rho_{\epsilon}^{m}\Pi
, one may expect the leading order correction will be
\mathcal{O}(p^{md})
. Depending on the particular logical state
{|\psi_{0}\rangle}
and error correction code, the performance may or may not reach
\mathcal{O}(p^{md})
. This is because there can exist shortcuts that make the leading order correction larger than
\mathcal{O}(p^{md})
. In practice, we do witness the performance will be improved with larger
m
.

Infidelity in small error rate region. Theoretically we have shown the leading order correction to infidelity will be

with

. Here, we use

Appendix C
Mean and variance of a ratio of two random variables

Consider random variables
P
and
Q
and let
G=g(P,Q)=P/Q
. In general, there is no closed form expression for
\mathbb{E}[G(P,Q)]
, and
\operatorname{Var}[G(P,Q)]
. Here, we find approximations for the mean and variance using Taylor expansions of
g(P,Q)
.

The approximation for the mean value is

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[g(P,Q)]&=\mathbb{E}[g(\mu_{P},\mu_{Q})+g^{\prime}_{P}(\mu_{P},\mu_{Q})(P-\mu_{P})+g^{\prime}_{Q}(\mu_{P},\mu_{Q})(Q-\mu_{Q})+R]\\
&\approx\mathbb{E}[g(\mu_{P},\mu_{Q})]+g^{\prime}_{P}(\mu_{P},\mu_{Q})\mathbb{E}[(P-\mu_{P})]+g^{\prime}_{Q}(\mu_{P},\mu_{Q})\mathbb{E}[(Q-\mu_{Q})]\\
&=g(\mu_{P},\mu_{Q}),\end{split}

(37)

where
R
is the higher order reminders of the Taylor expansion. For keeping the Taylor expansion to the first order, we ignore higher order remainders.

For the variance, we have

\begin{split}\operatorname{Var}[g(P,Q)]&=\mathbb{E}\left\{[g(P,Q)-\mathbb{E}(g(P,Q))]^{2}\right\}\\
&\approx\mathbb{E}\left\{[g(P,Q)-g(\mu_{P},\mu_{Q})]^{2}\right\}\\
&\approx\mathbb{E}\left\{[g^{\prime}_{P}(\mu_{P},\mu_{Q})(P-\mu_{P})+g^{\prime}_{Q}(\mu_{P},\mu_{Q})(Q-\mu_{Q})]^{2}\right\}\\
&=g^{\prime 2}_{P}(\mu_{P},\mu_{Q})\operatorname{Var}(P)+g^{\prime 2}_{Q}(\mu_{P},\mu_{Q})\operatorname{Var}(Q)+2g^{\prime}_{P}(\mu_{P},\mu_{Q})g^{\prime}_{Q}(\mu_{P},\mu_{Q})\operatorname{Cov}(P,Q).\end{split}

(38)

For our case,
g(P,Q)=P/Q
, therefore
g^{\prime}_{P}(\mu_{P},\mu_{Q})=1/\mu_{Q}
,
g^{\prime}_{Q}(\mu_{P},\mu_{Q})=-\mu_{P}/\mu^{2}_{Q}
, and

Appendix D
Proof of sample complexities

Suppose we want to predict a linear property of the underlying quantum state,

\begin{split}o=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho O).\end{split}

(40)

We simply replace the unknown quantum state
\rho
with the classical shadows
\hat{\rho}=\mathcal{M}^{-1}(\hat{\sigma})
. This yields a stochastic number
\hat{o}=\operatorname{Tr}(\hat{\rho}O)
, and it will converge to correct answer with sufficient amount of classical shadows,

\mathbb{E}\hat{o}=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho O).

(41)

In practice, the expectation
\mathbb{E}\hat{o}_{i}
is replaced by a sample mean estimator,
o_{\operatorname{avg}}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\hat{o}_{i}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\operatorname{Tr}(O\hat{\rho}_{i})
. Based on Chebyshev’s inequality, the probability of the
estimation
o_{\operatorname{avg}}
to deviate from its expectation value
o
is
bounded by its variance
\operatorname{Var}(o_{\operatorname{avg}})
as
\operatorname{Pr}(|o_{\operatorname{avg}}-o|\geq\delta)\leq\operatorname{Var}(o_{\operatorname{avg}})/\delta^{2}
. To control the deviation within a desired statistial accuracy
\epsilon
, we require
\operatorname{Var}(o_{\operatorname{avg}})/\delta^{2}=\operatorname{Var}(\hat{o})/(M\delta^{2})\leq\epsilon
, where
M
is the number of classical shadows. In other words, the number of experiments needed to achieve the statistical error
\epsilon
is given by

M\geq\operatorname{Var}(\hat{o})/\epsilon\delta^{2}.

(42)

Therefore, the sample complexity is directly related to the variance of single-shot estimation
\operatorname{Var}(\hat{o})
. We can further bound the variance by

\begin{split}\operatorname{Var}(\hat{o})&=\mathbb{E}[\hat{o}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}[\hat{o}]^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[\hat{o}^{2}]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{U}}\underset{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}{\sum}{\langle b|}U\sigma U^{\dagger}{|b\rangle}{\langle b|}U\mathcal{M}^{-1}(O)U^{\dagger}{|b\rangle}^{2}\\
&\leq||O||^{2}_{\text{shadow}},\end{split}

(43)

where the shadow norm of an observable is defined as

\begin{split}||O||_{\text{shadow}}&=\max_{\sigma:\text{state}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{U}}\underset{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}{\sum}{\langle b|}U\sigma U^{\dagger}{|b\rangle}{\langle b|}U\mathcal{M}^{-1}(O)U^{\dagger}{|b\rangle}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\\
&=\max_{\sigma:\text{state}}\left(\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{U}}\underset{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}{\sum}\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma U^{\dagger}{|b\rangle}{\langle b|}U{\langle b|}U\mathcal{M}^{-1}(O)U^{\dagger}{|b\rangle}^{2})\right)^{1/2}\\
&=\max_{\sigma:\text{state}}\left(\operatorname{Tr}\sigma V_{\mathcal{U}}[O]\right)^{1/2},\end{split}

(44)

where we define a new operator
V_{\mathcal{U}}[O]=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{U}}\underset{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}{\sum}U^{\dagger}{|b\rangle}{\langle b|}U{\langle b|}U\mathcal{M}^{-1}(O)U^{\dagger}{|b\rangle}^{2}
that depends both on the unitary ensemble
\mathcal{U}
and observable
O

\mathcal{U}
satisfies unitary 3-design, it can be simplified as

\begin{split}V_{\mathcal{U}}[O]&=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{U}}\underset{b\in\{0,1\}^{n}}{\sum}U^{\dagger}{|b\rangle}{\langle b|}U{\langle b|}U\mathcal{M}^{-1}(O)U^{\dagger}{|b\rangle}^{2}\\

(45)

where
\sigma

\tau

S_{3}
,
\text{Wg}[g]

group element
g
,
g_{0}=(2,3)
is a fixed permutation to match the tensor network connection, and

In the following, we will mainly focus on the analysis of
V_{\mathcal{U}}[O]
operator and
||O||^{2}_{\text{shadow}}
. In the main text, we focus on the scheme of encoding each logical qubit with
[n,1]
stabilizer code, and doing quantum computation with total physical qubits
N=n\times l
, where
l
is the number of logical qubits. For the classical shadow tomography part, we will use random unitaries sampled from
{\mathcal{C}\ell}(2^{n})^{\otimes l}
. One reason of choosing this factorized random unitary group is global clifford group
{\mathcal{C}\ell}(2^{nl})
is harder to implement in experiments. And the difficulty of implementing this factorized scheme does not depend on number of logical qubits. In practice, it is possible to encode each logical qubit with a small error correction code, such as
[5,1]
code, and implement random circuits from
{\mathcal{C}\ell}(2^{n})
. If the random unitary ensemble is
{\mathcal{C}\ell}(2^{n})^{\otimes l}
, then it is easy to show the reconstruction map is

\begin{split}\mathcal{M}^{-1}[\sigma]=\otimes_{i=1}^{l}\left[(2^{n}+1)\sigma_{i}-\operatorname{Tr}(A_{i})\mathds{1}\right],\end{split}

(47)

where
\sigma_{i}
is the reduced classical shadow on part
i
. The logical Pauli observables will be factorized on each logical sectors, i.e.
O=O_{1}\otimes O_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes O_{l}
. And since the random untaries are sampled from ensemble
{\mathcal{C}\ell}(2^{n})^{\otimes l}
, they also have the tensor product structure, i.e.
U=U_{1}\otimes U_{2}\otimes\dots\otimes U_{l}
. By combining those two properties, we can show

\begin{split}V_{\mathcal{U}}[O]=\otimes_{i=1}^{l}V_{\mathcal{U}_{i}}[O_{i}].\end{split}

(48)

Therefore, we only need to focus on the property of
V_{\mathcal{U}_{i}}[O_{i}]
for each logical sector. In the following, we will use
d=2^{n}
for the Hilbert space dimension for one logical sector.

The calculation for
V_{\mathcal{U}_{i}}[P_{i}\mathds{1}_{i}]

: For projection operator
P_{i}
,
\mathcal{M}^{-1}(P_{i}\mathds{1}_{i})=(d+1)P_{i}-2\mathds{1}_{i}
. And Eq. (
45
) can be evaluated

\begin{split}V_{{\mathcal{C}\ell}(d)}[P_{i}\mathds{1}_{i}]=\dfrac{2d-2}{d+2}(P_{i}+\mathds{1}_{i}).\end{split}

(49)

The calculation for
V_{\mathcal{U}_{i}}[P_{i}O_{i}]

: For non-trivial Pauli string
O_{i}
,
\mathcal{M}^{-1}(P_{i}O_{i})=(d+1)P_{i}O_{i}
. And Eq. (
45
) can be evaluated

\begin{split}V_{{\mathcal{C}\ell}(d)}[P_{i}O_{i}]=\dfrac{2d+2}{d+2}(P_{i}+\mathds{1}_{i}).\end{split}

(50)

As we can see,
V_{{\mathcal{C}\ell}(d)}[P_{i}\mathds{1}_{i}]\lesssim V_{{\mathcal{C}\ell}(d)}[P_{i}O_{i}]=\dfrac{2d+2}{d+2}(P_{i}+\mathds{1}_{i})
. This result indicates the sample complexity for predicting logical Pauli operators
O=\otimes_{i=1}^{l}O_{i}
after projection by
P=\otimes_{i=1}^{l}P_{i}
does not depend on the locality of the logical Pauli operators,

\begin{split}||PO||_{\text{shadow}}^{2}\lessapprox\max_{\sigma:\text{state}}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\sigma\left(\dfrac{2d+2}{d+2}\right)^{l}\otimes_{i=1}^{l}(P_{i}+\mathds{1}_{i})\right)\lesssim 4^{l}.\end{split}

(51)

This result is different from the sample complexity from local Clifford group or tensored Clifford group
{\mathcal{C}\ell}(d)^{\otimes l}
, where the sample complexity will depends on the locality of Pauli string
O
. This difference is mainly introduced by the logical subspace projection
P
. Even the Pauli string
O
is trivial in some region, the subspace projection
P_{i}
will still introduce fluctuation.
