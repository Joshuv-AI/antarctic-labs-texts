Coded Many-User Multiple Access via Approximate Message Passing
Xiaoqi Liu Kuan Hsieh Ramji Venkataramanan

Abstract
We consider communication over the Gaussian multiple-access channel in the regime where the number of users grows linearly with the codelength. In this regime, schemes based on sparse superposition coding can achieve a near-optimal tradeoff between spectral efficiency and signal-to-noise ratio. However, these schemes are feasible only for small values of user payload. This paper investigates efficient schemes for larger user payloads, focusing on coded CDMA schemes where each user’s information is encoded via a linear code before being modulated with a signature sequence. We propose an efficient approximate message passing (AMP) decoder that can be tailored to the structure of the linear code, and provide an exact asymptotic characterization of its performance. Based on this result, we consider a decoder that integrates AMP and belief propagation and characterize its tradeoff between spectral efficiency and signal-to-noise ratio, for a given target error rate. Simulation results show that the decoder achieves state-of-the-art performance at finite lengths, with a coded CDMA scheme defined using LDPC codes and a spatially coupled matrix of signature sequences.

Introduction

We consider communication over an
L
-user Gaussian multiple access channel (GMAC), which has output of the form

\boldsymbol{y}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\boldsymbol{c}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\,,

(1)

over
n
channel uses. Here

\boldsymbol{c}_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is the codeword of the
\ell
-th user and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim{\mathcal{N}}_{n}(\boldsymbol{0},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{I})
is the channel noise. Motivated by modern applications in machine-type communications, a number of recent works have studied the GMAC in the
many-user
or
many-access
setting, where the number of users
L
grows with the block length
n

[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
]
.

In this paper, we study the many-user regime where
L,n\to\infty
with the user density
\mu:=L/n
converging to a constant.
Each user transmits a fixed number of bits
k
(payload) under a constant energy-per-information-bit constraint
\|\boldsymbol{c}_{\ell}\|^{2}_{2}/k\leq E_{b}
. The spectral efficiency is the total user payload per channel use, denoted by
\mathsf{S}=(Lk)/n=\mu k
. In this regime, a key question is to understand the tradeoff between user density (or spectral efficiency), the signal-to-noise ratio
E_{b}/N_{0}
, and the probability of decoding error. Here
N_{0}=2\sigma^{2}
is the noise spectral density.
A popular measure of decoding performance is the per-user probability of error (
\mathtt{PUPE}
), defined as

\mathtt{PUPE}:=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{c}_{\ell}\neq\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}_{\ell}),

(2)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{c}}_{\ell}
is the decoded codeword for user
\ell
.

Polyanskiy
[
2
]
and Zadik et al.
[
3
]
obtained converse and achievability bounds on the minimum
{E_{b}}/{N_{0}}
required to achieve
\mathtt{PUPE}\leq\epsilon
for a given
\epsilon>0
, when the user density
\mu
and user payload
k
are fixed. These bounds were extended to the multiple-access channels with Rayleigh fading in
[
5
,
6
]
.
The achievability bounds in these works are obtained using Gaussian random codebooks and joint maximum-likelihood decoding, which is computationally infeasible. In contrast, the focus of our work is computationally efficient schemes for which the tradeoff above can be precisely characterized.

Coding schemes based on sparse superposition coding

Sparse superposition codes were introduced by Barron and Joseph
[
7
,
8
]
for the

single-user
Gaussian channel, but also give a useful framework for efficient communication over the many-user GMAC
[
9
]
. We briefly review sparse superposition coding in the context of the GMAC. The codeword for user
\ell\in[L]
is constructed as
\boldsymbol{c}_{\ell}=\boldsymbol{A}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}
, where
\boldsymbol{A}_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times B}
is a random matrix and
\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{B}
is a message vector with exactly one nonzero entry. (The value of the nonzero entry is pre-specified, and chosen to satisfy the energy constraint.) Since there are
B
choices for the location of the nonzero, each user transmits
\log_{2}B
bits in
n
uses of the GMAC, and the spectral efficiency is
\mathsf{S}=\mu\log_{2}B
. For this construction, the combined channel model can be written as

\boldsymbol{y}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\boldsymbol{A}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},

(3)

where the design matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times LB}
is the horizontal concatenation of matrices
\boldsymbol{A}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{A}_{L}
,
and the message vector
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{LB}
is the concatenation of vectors
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{L}
.

The decoding problem is to recover the message vector
\boldsymbol{x}
from
(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{A})
. An efficient Approximate Message Passing (AMP) decoder for this sparse superposition GMAC scheme was analyzed in
[
9
]
. AMP is a family of iterative algorithms that has its origins in relaxations of belief propagation
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
. An attractive feature of AMP decoding is that it allows an exact asymptotic characterization of its error performance through a deterministic recursion called ‘state evolution’. For both i.i.d. and spatially coupled choices of the design
\boldsymbol{A}
, the asymptotic tradeoff achieved by the AMP decoder between the spectral efficiency and
E_{b}/N_{0}
was precisely characterized in
[
9
]
(for a target
\mathtt{PUPE}
). In fact,
[
9
]
analyzed a more general scheme where each user’s message vector
\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}
in (
3
) can be drawn from a general discrete prior on
{\mathbb{R}}^{B}
; sparse superposition coding is an important special case of this scheme.

\mathtt{PUPE}=10^{-4}

, and is uniformly better than the achievability bound from

[

This motivates the question studied in this paper: for large payloads, can we construct efficient coding schemes whose asymptotic performance is closer to the bounds?

Coding schemes based on CDMA

Our starting point is binary code-division multiple access (CDMA), where each user
\ell\in[L]
transmits one bit of information by modulating a signature sequence (or spreading sequence)
\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, i.e., the codeword
\boldsymbol{c}_{\ell}=\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}x_{\ell}
where
x_{\ell}\in\{\pm\sqrt{E}\}
. The
decoding problem is to recover the vector of information symbols
\boldsymbol{x}=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{L}]^{\top}
from the channel output vector

\boldsymbol{y}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}x_{\ell}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\,,

(4)

where
\boldsymbol{A}=[\boldsymbol{a}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{a}_{L}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times L}
is the matrix of signature sequences. We have used the same notation as
in (
3
) to highlight that binary CDMA is similar to the model in (
3
), with
B=1
.

The optimal spectral efficiency of CDMA in the large system limit (with random signature sequences) has been studied in a number of works, e.g.,

[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
. Assuming the signature sequences are i.i.d. sub-Gaussian, the best known technique for efficiently decoding
\boldsymbol{x}
from
\boldsymbol{y}
in (
4
) is AMP
[
12
,
18
]
. If each user wishes to transmit
k>1
bits in
n
channel uses, the binary CDMA scheme requires
k
blocks of transmission, with each block (and each signature sequence) having length
n/k
.
For large user payloads
k
, the binary CDMA with AMP decoding performs poorly, with a tradeoff curve that is significantly worse than the sparse superposition scheme.

1.1
Main contributions

The binary CDMA scheme described above transmits
uncoded
user information.
In this paper, we show how a significantly better performance tradeoff can be achieved for large user payloads (compared to the sparse superposition scheme and uncoded CDMA) using a coded CDMA scheme in which each user’s information sequence is first encoded using a linear code before being multiplied with the signature sequence.
Our scheme uses an efficient AMP decoder that is tailored to the decoder of the linear code. Among potential linear codes, Low-Density Parity Check (LDPC) codes are a particularly effective choice. They are known to be capacity-achieving for binary input symmetric channels with efficient Belief Propagation (BP) decoding
[
19
]
. In the coded CDMA setting, the soft-information available from the BP decoder can be leveraged within the AMP framework to iteratively refine the receiver’s estimate of the user information.

Coded CDMA schemes for multiple access (with different decoders) have been studied in earlier works
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
, which we discuss in the next subsection.
The novel contribution of our work is a flexible AMP decoder that can be tailored to the structure of the linear code, and an exact asymptotic characterization of its error performance in the many-user regime (Theorems
1
and
2
). Specifically, we show how a decoder for the underlying code, such as a maximum-likelihood or a belief propagation (BP) decoder, can be incorporated within the AMP algorithm with rigorous asymptotic guarantees (Corollary
1
).
Simulation results validate the theory and demonstrate the benefits of the concatenated scheme at finite lengths.

We will use a
spatially coupled
construction for the design matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
. Spatial coupling was originally proposed
[
25
]
as a way to improve the decoding threshold of LDPC codes. Spatially coupled LDPC codes with belief propagation have since been shown to be capacity-achieving decoding for a large class of binary-input channels
[
26
]
. Spatially coupled sparse superposition codes with AMP decoding have also been shown to be capacity-achieving, for both AWGN
[
27
,
28
]
and a broader class of channels
[
29
]
. Moreover, spatially coupled designs (with estimation via AMP) achieve Bayes-optimal error for both linear
[
30
,
31
,
32
]
and generalized linear models
[
33
]
. To keep the exposition simple, we first present the AMP decoder and analysis for the i.i.d. Gaussian design in Sections
3
and
4
before generalizing the results to spatially coupled designs in Section
5
.

We emphasize that our setting is distinct from unsourced random access over the GMAC
[
2
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
,
where all the users share the same codebook and only a subset of them are active. In our case, each user has a distinct signature sequence and all of them are active. While the latter is particularly relevant in designing grant-free communication systems, coding schemes for this setting often rely on dividing a common codebook into sections for different users.
Extending the ideas in this paper to unsourced random access is a promising direction for future work.

1.2
Related work

Coded CDMA for multiple access channels

Efficient decoders for coded CDMA schemes have been studied in a number of works
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
. These decoders are typically based on iterative soft interference cancellation, and have two components: a multiuser detector, and a single-user channel decoder for each user. The idea is to iteratively exchange soft-information between the two components to approximate the posterior probabilities on the information symbols. In the spirit of these decoders, one could use an AMP algorithm for multiuser detection followed by single-user channel decoding (e.g., via BP). Our results show that we can significantly improve on this approach by integrating the channel decoder within the AMP algorithm (see Fig.
5
).

Optimal power allocation across users for iterative schemes based on multiuser detection and single-user decoding was studied in
[
23
]
. We expect that the spatial coupling we use has a similar effect to optimal power allocation. This is based on a similar phenomenon observed for power-allocated vs. spatially coupled sparse superposition codes for single-user AWGN channels
[
37
]
. We note that our scheme can be viewed as an instance of non-orthogonal multiple access (NOMA)
[
38
,
39
]
, a class of schemes based on superposition coding that has been studied widely in recent years.

Approximate Message Passing

AMP algorithms were first proposed for compressed sensing
[
12
,
18
]
and its variants
[
40
]
. These algorithms have since been applied to a range of problems including estimation in generalized linear models and low-rank matrix estimation. We refer the interested reader to
[
41
]
for a survey. In the context of communication over AWGN channels, AMP has been used as a decoder for sparse superposition codes (SPARCs)
[
27
,
42
,
37
]
and for compressed coding
[
43
]
. For the GMAC, in addition to efficient schemes for smaller user payloads
[
9
]
, spatially coupled SPARCs have been used to obtain improved achievability bounds
[
44
,
45
]
. SPARC-based concatenated schemes with AMP decoding have been proposed for both single-user AWGN channels
[
46
,
47
,
48
]
and the GMAC where users have different codebooks
[
45
]
or share the same codebook
[
35
,
36
]
.

In most of these concatenated schemes
[
46
,
47
,
35
,
43
]
the AMP decoder for SPARCs does not explicitly use the structure of the outer code (which is decoded separately). Two key exceptions are the SPARC-LDPC concatenated schemes in
[
36
,
48
]
, which use an AMP decoding algorithm with an integrated BP denoiser. Drawing inspiration from these works, in Section
4.3
we propose an AMP decoder with a BP denoiser for our concatenated scheme. Our scheme and its decoder differ from those in
[
36
,
48
]
in a few important ways: i) we do not use the SPARC message structure, and ii) we treat each user’s codeword as a row of a signal matrix and devise an AMP algorithm with matrix iterates, a notable deviation from prior schemes where AMP operates on vectors.

The SPARC-LDPC scheme was recently extended to multiple access channels in
[
49
]
. Since the scheme is based on sparse superposition codes, for the reasons discussed on p.

, the AMP decoder in
[

can only handle a small number of users if the per-user payload is large (or a small payload with a large number of users). In contrast, our focus is to design an efficient scheme for a large number of users, each with a large fixed payload, e.g.,

Concatenated coding scheme

The

\ell

\boldsymbol{u}_{\ell}\in\{0,1\}^{k}

and each

to produce
\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}\in\{\pm\sqrt{E}\}^{d}

. In the second step of encoding, for each user

\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}

\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\tilde{n}}
, where
\tilde{n}:=n/d.
This yields a matrix
\boldsymbol{C}_{\ell}=\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}^{\top}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\tilde{n}\times d}
. The final length-

\ell
is simply
\boldsymbol{c}_{\ell}=\mathrm{vectorize}(\boldsymbol{C}_{\ell})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

2.1
Assumptions

We consider random signature sequences with independent entries and expected squared
\ell_{2}
-norm of one, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}=1
. For example, with i.i.d. Gaussian signature sequences, the entries of the design matrix are
A_{i\ell}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}{\mathcal{N}}(0,1/\tilde{n})
for
i\in[\tilde{n}],\,\ell\in[L]
.
Due to concentration of measure, this implies that
\|\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. Hence, the energy-per-bit constraint can be enforced asymptotically by choosing
E
so that
\|\boldsymbol{c}_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}/k=\|\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}^{\top}\|_{\text{F}}^{2}/k\to Ed/k\leq E_{b}
as
n\to\infty
. We make the natural assumption that the information bits
\boldsymbol{u}_{\ell}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
are uniformly random, for each user
\ell\in[L]
. We also assume that the noise variance
\sigma^{2}
in (
1
) is known, a mild assumption since
\sigma^{2}
can be consistently estimated as:

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{Y}\|^{2}_{\text{F}}}{n}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[\tilde{n}]}\sum_{j\in[d]}\mathbb{E}\left[\bigg(\sum_{\ell\in[L]}A_{i\ell}X_{\ell j}\bigg)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{Y}\|^{2}_{\text{F}}}{n}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in[\tilde{n}]}\sum_{j\in[d]}\sum_{\ell\in[L]}\mathbb{E}\left[A_{i\ell}^{2}\right]\cdot E

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\boldsymbol{Y}\|^{2}_{\text{F}}}{n}-d\mu E,

where the last equality uses
\sum_{i\in[\tilde{n}]}\mathbb{E}\left[A_{i\ell}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}\|\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}\|_{2}^{2}=1
, for
\ell\in[L]
.

We consider the asymptotic limit where
L/n\to\mu
as
n,L\to\infty
, for a user density
\mu>0
of constant order. We emphasize that
d
is fixed and does not scale as
n,L\to\infty
. Therefore
\tilde{n}/L=({n}/{d})/L\to{1}/{(d\mu)}
is also of constant order.

3
AMP decoder for i.i.d. design

Consider the i.i.d. Gaussian design matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
with entries
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}{\mathcal{N}}(0,1/\tilde{n})
.
The decoding task is to recover the signal matrix
\boldsymbol{X}
from the channel observation
\boldsymbol{Y}
in (
5
), given the design matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
and the channel noise variance
\sigma^{2}
(or its estimate). A good decoder must take advantage of the prior distribution on
\boldsymbol{X}
: recall that each row of
\boldsymbol{X}
is an independent codeword taking values in
\{\pm\sqrt{E}\}^{d}
, defined via the underlying rate
k/d
linear code. The prior distribution of each row of
\boldsymbol{X}
induced by the linear code is denoted by
P_{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}}
. Note that
P_{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}}
assigns equal probability to
2^{k}
vectors in
\{\pm\sqrt{E}\}^{d}
.

The AMP decoder recursively produces estimates
\boldsymbol{X}^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times d}
of
\boldsymbol{X}
for iteration
t\geq 0
. This is done via a sequence of denoising functions
\eta_{t}
that can be tailored to the prior
P_{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}}
. Starting from an initializer
\boldsymbol{X}^{0}=\boldsymbol{0}_{L\times d}
, for
t\geq 0
the AMP decoder computes:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}^{t}=\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{X}^{t}+\frac{1}{\tilde{n}}\boldsymbol{Z}^{t-1}\left[\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\eta_{t-1}^{\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{t-1}_{\ell}\right)\right]^{\top},

(6)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}^{t+1}=\eta_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{S}^{t}\right),\quad\boldsymbol{S}^{t}=\boldsymbol{X}^{t}+\boldsymbol{A}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}^{t},

(7)

where
\eta_{t}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
applies row-wise to matrix inputs, and
\eta_{t}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{s})=\frac{\textrm{d}\eta_{t}(\boldsymbol{s})}{\textrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
is the derivative (Jacobian) of
\eta_{t}
. Quantities with negative indices are set to all-zero matrices.
When
d=1
, (
6
)–(
7
) reduces to the classical AMP algorithm
[
18
]
for estimating a vector signal in a linear model.

State Evolution (SE)

As
n,L\to\infty
(with
\frac{L}{n}\to\mu
), the memory term
\frac{1}{\tilde{n}}\boldsymbol{Z}^{t-1}\left[\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\eta_{t-1}^{\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{t-1}_{\ell}\right)\right]^{\top}
term in (
6
)
ensures that the row-wise empirical distribution of
\boldsymbol{Z}^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\tilde{n}\times d}
converges to a Gaussian
{\mathcal{N}}_{d}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{t})
for
t\geq 1
. Furthermore, the row-wise empirical distribution of
(\boldsymbol{S}^{t}-\boldsymbol{X})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times d}
also converges to the same Gaussian
{\mathcal{N}}_{d}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{t})
. The covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
is iteratively defined via the following state evolution (SE) recursion, for
t\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{t+1}=\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{d}\,+\,d\mu\mathbb{E}\left\{[\eta_{t}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t})-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}][\eta_{t}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t})-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}]^{\top}\right\}.

(8)

Here
\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\sim{\mathcal{N}}_{d}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{t})
is independent of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\sim P_{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}}
, and
\boldsymbol{I}_{d}
is the
d\times d
identity matrix. The expectation in (
8
) is with respect to
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\boldsymbol{g}^{t}
, and the iteration is initialized with
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{0}=(\sigma^{2}+d\mu E)\boldsymbol{I}_{d}
. We shall refer to (
8
) as ‘iid-SE’ later on because it is associated with the i.i.d. Gaussian design.

The convergence of the row-wise empirical distribution of
\boldsymbol{S}^{t}
to the law of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t}
follows by applying standard results in AMP theory
[
50
,
41
]
. This distributional characterization of
\boldsymbol{S}^{t}
crucially informs the choice of the denoiser
\eta_{t}
. Specifically, for each row
\ell\in[L]
, the role of the denoiser
\eta_{t}
is to estimate the codeword
\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}
from an observation in zero-mean Gaussian noise with covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{t}
.
In the next section, we discuss the Bayes-optimal denoiser and two other sub-optimal but computationally efficient denoisers.

First, we provide a performance characterization of the AMP decoder with a generic Lipschitz-continuous denoiser in Theorem
1
. Decoding performance after
t

\mathtt{UER}=\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbbm{1}\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1}_{\ell}\neq\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}\}
,
or the bit-error rate
\mathtt{BER}=\frac{1}{Ld}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbbm{1}\{\hat{x}^{t+1}_{\ell,j}\neq x_{\ell,j}\}
. Here
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1}_{\ell}=h_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}^{t}_{\ell})
is a hard-decision estimate of the codeword
\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}
, produced using a suitable function
h_{t}
, and
\hat{x}^{t+1}_{\ell,j}
is the
j
th entry of
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1}_{\ell}
. For example,
h_{t}
may quantize each entry of
\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1}_{\ell}=\eta_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}^{t}_{\ell})
to a value in
\{\pm\sqrt{E}\}
.
We note that the
\mathtt{PUPE}
defined in (
2
) is the expected value of the
\mathtt{UER}
.

\eta_{t}

4.1
Bayes-optimal denoiser

Since the row-wise distribution of
\boldsymbol{S}^{t}
converges to the law of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t}
, the Bayes-optimal or minimum mean squared error (MMSE) denoiser
\eta_{t}^{\textrm{Bayes}}
estimates each row
\boldsymbol{X}
as the following conditional expectation. For
\ell\in[L]
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}^{t+1}_{\ell}=\eta_{t}^{\textrm{Bayes}}(\boldsymbol{s}^{t}_{\ell})=\mathbb{E}\left[\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\mid\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t}=\boldsymbol{s}^{t}_{\ell}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\cdot\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}-2\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{t})^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)}{\sum_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}-2\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{t})^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\right)}

(11)

where
\mathcal{X}\subset\{\pm\sqrt{E}\}^{d}
is the set of
2^{k}
codewords.
Since
\left\lvert\mathcal{X}\right\rvert=2^{k}
,
the cost of applying
\eta_{t}^{\textrm{Bayes}}
is
O(2^{k}d^{3})
which grows exponentially in
k
.
In each iteration
t
, the decoder can produce a hard-decision maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell}^{t}
from
\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}
via:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell}^{t+1}=h_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t})=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{P}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\mid\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t}=\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}\right).

(12)

In Section
4.4
(Fig.
4
) we present numerical results illustrating the performance of AMP with denoiser
\eta_{t}^{\textrm{Bayes}}
for a Hamming code with
d=7
and
k=4
.
In practical scenarios where
d
is of the order of several hundreds or thousands, applying
\eta_{t}^{\textrm{Bayes}}
is not feasible, motivating the use of sub-optimal denoisers with lower computational cost.

4.2
Marginal-MMSE denoiser

A computationally efficient alternative to the Bayes-optimal denoiser is the marginal-MMSE denoiser
[
46
,
35
]
which
acts entry-wise on
\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}
and
returns the entry-wise conditional expectation:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}^{t+1}=\eta_{t}^{\textrm{marginal}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t})

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{E}[\bar{x}_{1}\mid\bar{x}_{1}+g^{t}_{1}=s_{\ell,1}^{t}]\\
\vdots\\
\mathbb{E}[\bar{x}_{d}\mid\bar{x}_{d}+g^{t}_{d}=s_{\ell,d}^{t}]\end{bmatrix},\qquad\text{where}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\bar{x}_{j}\mid\bar{x}_{j}+g^{t}_{j}=s_{\ell,j}^{t}]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{(i)}}}{{=}}\sqrt{E}\tanh\left(\sqrt{E}s_{\ell,j}^{t}/\Sigma_{j,j}^{t}\right)\qquad\text{for }j\in[d].

(13)

The equality (i) follows from
g^{t}_{j}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,\Sigma^{t}_{j,j})
and
p(\bar{x}_{j}=\sqrt{E})=p(\bar{x}_{j}=-\sqrt{E})=\frac{1}{2}
due to the linearity of the outer code. A hard decision estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell}^{t+1}
can be obtained by quantizing each entry of
\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}^{t+1}
to
\{\pm\sqrt{E}\}
.

This marginal denoiser has an
O(d)
computational cost which is linear in
d
, but it ignores the parity structure of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
, which is useful prior knowledge that can help reconstruction. One way to address this is by using the output of the AMP decoder as input to a channel decoder for the outer code, as in
[
35
,
47
]
.
In the next subsection, we show how to improve on this approach. Considering an outer LDPC code, we use an AMP denoiser that fully integrates BP decoding.

4.3
Belief Propagation (BP) denoiser

Assume that the binary code used to define the concatenated scheme is a low-density parity check (LDPC) code.
An LDPC code is a linear code characterized by a sparse parity-check matrix, where each parity-check equation involves only a small number of code bits relative to the codelength. LDPC codes are typically decoded using Belief Propagation (BP), an iterative message passing algorithm that operates on the sparse factor graph corresponding to the parity check matrix. The factor graph is a bipartite graph with two sets of nodes: variable nodes (representing the code bits) and check nodes (representing the parity-check equations). The BP decoder iteratively passes messages between these two sets of nodes to compute updated probabilistic estimates on the value of each code bit. For a detailed discussion of LDPC codes and BP decoding, see
[
19
]
.

We propose a BP denoiser
\eta_{t}^{\textrm{BP}}
for the AMP decoder which exploits the parity structure of the LDPC code in each AMP iteration by performing a few rounds of BP on the associated factor graph. Like the other denoisers above,
\eta_{t}^{\textrm{BP}}
acts row-wise on the effective observation
\boldsymbol{S}^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times d}
. For
\ell\in[L]
, it produces the updated AMP estimate
\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}^{t+1}
from
\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}
as follows, using
\mathcal{R}
rounds of BP.

1) For each variable node
j\in[d]
and check node
i\in[d-k]
, initialize variable-to-check messages (in log-likelihood ratio format) as:

\displaystyle L_{j\to i}^{(0)}=\ln\left[\frac{p(s_{\ell,j}^{t}\mid x_{\ell,j}=+\sqrt{E})}{p(s_{\ell,j}^{t}\mid x_{\ell,j}=-\sqrt{E})}\right]=\frac{2\sqrt{E}s_{\ell,j}^{t}}{\Sigma^{t}_{j,j}}=:L(s_{\ell,j}^{t}).

(14)

This initialization follows the distributional assumption
s_{\ell,j}^{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{d}}}{{=}}\bar{x}_{j}+g^{t}_{j}
, where
g^{t}_{j}\sim{\mathcal{N}}(0,\Sigma^{t}_{j,j})
and
p(\bar{x}_{j}=\sqrt{E})=p(\bar{x}_{j}=-\sqrt{E})=\frac{1}{2}
. Note that, similar to the marginal-MMSE denoiser in (
13
), only the diagonal entries of the covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{t}
,
\Sigma^{t}_{j,j}
for
j\in[d]
, are used in (
14
).

2) Let
N(i)
denote the set of neighbouring nodes of node
i.
For round
1\leq r\leq\mathcal{R}
, compute the check-to-variable and variable-to-check messages, denoted by
L_{i\to j}^{(r)}
and
L_{j\to i}^{(r)}
, as:

\displaystyle L_{i\to j}^{(r)}

\displaystyle=2\tanh^{-1}\left[\prod_{j^{\prime}\in N(i)\setminus j}\tanh\left(\frac{1}{2}L_{j^{\prime}\to i}^{(r-1)}\right)\right]\,,

(15)

\displaystyle L_{j\to i}^{(r)}

\displaystyle=L(s_{\ell,j}^{t})+\sum_{i^{\prime}\in N(j)\setminus i}L_{i^{\prime}\to j}^{(r)}\,.

(16)

3) Terminate BP after
\mathcal{R}
rounds by computing the final log-likelihood ratio for each variable node
j\in[d]
:

\displaystyle L_{j}^{(\mathcal{R})}=L(s_{\ell,j}^{t})+\sum_{i\in N(j)}L_{i\to j}^{(\mathcal{R})}.

(17)

Equations (
15
)–(
17
) are the standard BP updates for an LDPC code
[
19
]
.

4) Compute the updated AMP estimate
\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}^{t+1}=\eta_{t}^{\textrm{BP}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t})
, where for
j\in[d]
,

\displaystyle\left[\eta_{t}^{\textrm{BP}}(\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t})\right]_{j}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{E}\ \frac{\exp\big(L_{j}^{(\mathcal{R})}\big)}{1+\exp\big(L_{j}^{(\mathcal{R})}\big)}-\sqrt{E}\ \frac{1}{1+\exp\big(L_{j}^{(\mathcal{R})}\big)}=\sqrt{E}\,\tanh\big(L_{j}^{(\mathcal{R})}/2\big).

(18)

The RHS above is obtained by converting the final log-likelihood ratio (
17
) to a conditional expectation, recalling that
x_{\ell,j}
takes values in
\{\pm\sqrt{E}\}
. Following the standard interpretation of BP as approximating the bit-wise marginal posterior probabilities
[
19
]
, the expression in (
18
) can be viewed as an approximation to

\mathbb{E}[\bar{x}_{j}\mid\bar{x}_{j}+g_{j}^{t}=s_{\ell,j}^{t}\,,\,\text{parities specified by $N(j)$
are satisfied}].

We highlight the contrast between the conditional expectation above and the one in (
13
), which does not use the parity-check constraints. As with the marginal-MMSE denoiser, a hard-decision estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\ell}^{t+1}
can be obtained by quantizing each entry of
\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}^{t+1}
to
\{\pm\sqrt{E}\}
. The computational cost of
\eta^{\textrm{BP}}_{t}
is
O(d\mathcal{R})
which is linear in
d
.

Computing the derivative of
\eta_{t}

5
Spatially coupled design and AMP decoder

In this section, we study the concatenated scheme with a spatially coupled Gaussian design matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
. We first define the spatial coupled design, and then describe the corresponding AMP decoder and characterize its asymptotic performance (Theorem
2
).

A spatially coupled (SC) design matrix
\boldsymbol{A}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\tilde{n}\times L}
is divided into
\textsf{R}\times\textsf{C}
equally sized blocks. The entries of
\boldsymbol{A}
within each block are i.i.d. Gaussian with zero-mean and variance prescribed by a
base matrix

\boldsymbol{W}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\textsf{R}\times\textsf{C}}
. Specifically,
the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
is obtained by replacing each entry
W_{\textsf{r},\textsf{c}}
of the base matrix
by an
\frac{\tilde{n}}{\textsf{R}}\times\frac{L}{\textsf{C}}
block with entries drawn
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{\tilde{n}/\textsf{R}}W_{\textsf{r},\textsf{c}})
,
for
\textsf{r}\in[\textsf{R}]
,
\textsf{c}\in[\textsf{C}]
. That is, we have

A_{i\ell}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}\bigg(0,\frac{1}{\tilde{n}/\textsf{R}}W_{\textsf{r}(i),\textsf{c}(\ell)}\bigg),\quad\text{for }i\in[\tilde{n}],\ \ell\in[L].

(29)

Here, the operators
\textsf{r}(\cdot):[\tilde{n}]\rightarrow[\textsf{R}]
and
\textsf{c}(\cdot):[L]\rightarrow[\textsf{C}]
map a particular row or column index of
\boldsymbol{A}
to its corresponding
row block
or
column block
index of
\boldsymbol{W}
. See Fig.
7
for an example.

Figure 7:
A spatially coupled design matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
constructed using a base matrix
\boldsymbol{W}
according to (
29
). The base matrix shown here is an
(\omega=3,\Lambda=7)
base matrix (defined in Definition
5.1
). The white parts of
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{W}
correspond to zeros.

As in
[
31
]
, the entries of the base matrix
\boldsymbol{W}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{\textsf{R}\times\textsf{C}}
are scaled to satisfy:

\sum_{\textsf{r}=1}^{\textsf{R}}W_{\textsf{r},\textsf{c}}=1\;\text{ for }\textsf{c}\in[\textsf{C}]
.
This is to ensure that the columns of
\boldsymbol{A}
(the signature sequences
\boldsymbol{a}_{\ell}
) have unit squared
\ell_{2}
-norm in expectation. Each block of
\boldsymbol{A}
can be viewed as an (uncoupled) i.i.d. design matrix with
{L}/{\textsf{C}}
users and
d{\tilde{n}}/{\textsf{R}}={n}/{\textsf{R}}
channel uses. Thus, we define the
inner
user density as

\displaystyle\mu_{\text{in}}:=\frac{L/\textsf{C}}{n/\textsf{R}}=\frac{\textsf{R}}{\textsf{C}}\mu.

(30)

The standard i.i.d. Gaussian design where
A_{i\ell}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,1/\tilde{n})
is a special case of the SC design, obtained by using a base matrix with a single entry (
\textsf{R}=\textsf{C}=1
).
We shall use a class of base matrices called
(\omega,\Lambda)
base matrices that have also been used for spatially coupled sparse superposition codes
[
28
,
9
]
.

Definition 5.1
.

An
(\omega,\Lambda)
base matrix
\boldsymbol{W}
is described by two parameters: the coupling width
\omega\geq 1
and the coupling length
\Lambda\geq 2\omega-1
. The matrix has
\textsf{R}=\Lambda+\omega-1
rows and
\textsf{C}=\Lambda
columns, with each column having
\omega
identical nonzero entries in the band-diagonal and zeros everywhere else. For
\textsf{r}\in[\textsf{R}]
and
\textsf{c}\in[\textsf{C}]
, the
(\textsf{r},\textsf{c})
th entry of the base matrix is given by

W_{\textsf{r},\textsf{c}}=\begin{cases}\ \frac{1}{\omega}\quad&\text{if}\ \textsf{c}\leq\textsf{r}\leq\textsf{c}+\omega-1\,,\\
\ 0\quad&\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

(31)

Fig.
7
shows an example of design matrix constructed using an
(\omega=3,\Lambda=7)
base matrix. From the GMAC perspective, the main difference between such a design and an i.i.d. Gaussian one is that only a small fraction of users are active during any given channel use. In Fig.
7
, only the first
L/\textsf{C}
users are active during the first
\tilde{n}/\textsf{R}
channel uses, and only the last
L/\textsf{C}
users are active for the last
\tilde{n}/\textsf{R}
channel uses. This allows these two sets of users to be decoded more easily than the others, thus helping the decoding of the adjacent blocks of users. This creates a decoding wave that propagate from the ends to the center. Examples and figures illustrating the decoding wave in spatially coupled designs can be found in
[
28
,
30
]
.

5.1
Spatially Coupled AMP

The AMP decoder for a spatially coupled Gaussian design matrix (SC-AMP) is a generalization of the AMP decoder for the i.i.d. Gaussian design presented in Section
3
, accounting for the fact that the SC design has an
\textsf{R}\times\textsf{C}
block-wise structure, with potentially different variances across blocks.
Starting with initialization
\boldsymbol{X}^{0}=\boldsymbol{0}_{L\times d}
and
\boldsymbol{\tilde{Z}}^{0}=\boldsymbol{0}_{\tilde{n}\times d}
,
the decoder computes for
t\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{Z}^{t}=\boldsymbol{Y}-\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{X}^{t}+\boldsymbol{\tilde{Z}}^{t},

(32)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{X}^{t+1}=\eta_{t}\left(\boldsymbol{S}^{t}\right),\quad\text{where}\quad\boldsymbol{S}^{t}=\boldsymbol{X}^{t}+\boldsymbol{V}^{t}.

(33)

Here the denoising function
\eta_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{L\times d}
is assumed to be Lipschitz and acts row-wise on matrix inputs. Denoting the rows of
\boldsymbol{S}^{t}
by
\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}
for
\ell\in[L]
, we have:

\displaystyle\eta_{t}(\boldsymbol{S}^{t})=\begin{bmatrix}\eta_{t,1}\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{t}_{1}\right)\\
\vdots\\
\eta_{t,1}\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{t}_{L/\textsf{C}}\right)\\
\vdots\\
\eta_{t,\textsf{C}}\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{t}_{(\textsf{C}-1)L/\textsf{C}+1}\right)\\
\vdots\\
\eta_{t,\textsf{C}}\left(\boldsymbol{s}^{t}_{L}\right)\end{bmatrix}\begin{array}[]{ c }\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{\begin{array}[]{ c }\eta_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\eta_{1}\\
\end{array}}\right\}\text{$\frac{L}{\textsf{C}}$ rows with $\textsf{c}=1$}\\
\\
\vphantom{\vdots}\left.\kern-1.2pt\vphantom{\begin{array}[]{ c }\eta_{\textsf{C}}\\
\vdots\\
\eta_{\textsf{C}}\\
\end{array}}\right\}\text{$\frac{L}{\textsf{C}}$ rows with $\textsf{c}=\textsf{C}$\,,}\end{array}

where
\eta_{t,\textsf{c}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
corresponds to the denoising function applied to the
\frac{L}{\textsf{C}}
rows in block
\textsf{c}\in[\textsf{C}]
. We have used the convention that the function returns a row vector when applied to a row vector.

For
t\geq 0
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{t}
and
\boldsymbol{V}^{t}
in (
32
)–(
33
) are defined through a matrix

\boldsymbol{Q}^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\textsf{R}\times d\textsf{C}}
, which consists of
\textsf{R}\times\textsf{C}
submatrices, each of size
d\times d
. For
\textsf{r}\in[\textsf{R}],\textsf{c}\in[\textsf{C}]
, the submatrix
\boldsymbol{Q}_{\textsf{r},\textsf{c}}^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
is defined as:

\boldsymbol{Q}_{\textsf{r},\textsf{c}}^{t}=\left[\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textsf{r}}^{t}\right]^{-1}\boldsymbol{T}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}\,,

(41)

where
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textsf{r}}^{t},\boldsymbol{T}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
are deterministic matrices defined later in (
44
)–(
46
), as part of the corresponding state evolution.
The
i
th row of matrix
\boldsymbol{\tilde{Z}}^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{\tilde{n}\times d}
then takes the form:

\boldsymbol{\tilde{z}}_{i}^{t}={d\mu_{\text{in}}}\,\boldsymbol{z}_{i}^{t-1}\,\sum_{\textsf{c}=1}^{\textsf{C}}W_{\textsf{r}(i),\textsf{c}}\,\boldsymbol{Q}^{t-1}_{\textsf{r}(i),\textsf{c}}\,\frac{1}{L/\textsf{C}}\cdot\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{\textsf{c}}}\left[\eta_{t-1,\textsf{c}(\ell)}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t-1})\right]^{\top},\quad\text{for}\;i\in[\tilde{n}],

(42)

where

\mathcal{L}_{\textsf{c}}=\{(\textsf{c}-1)L/\textsf{C}+1,\dots,\textsf{c}L/\textsf{C}\}
, and

\eta_{t,\textsf{c}}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{s})=\frac{\textrm{d}\eta_{t,\textsf{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})}{\textrm{d}\boldsymbol{s}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
is the Jacobian of
\eta_{t,\textsf{c}}
. Quantities with negative iteration index are set to all-zero matrices. The
\ell
th row of
\boldsymbol{V}^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times d}
takes the form:

\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}^{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{\tilde{n}}A_{i\ell}\,\boldsymbol{z}_{i}^{t}\,\boldsymbol{Q}_{\textsf{r}(i),\textsf{c}(\ell)}^{t}\,,\quad\text{for}\;\ell\in[L].

(43)

In (
42
) and (
43
), the vectors
\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}^{t},\boldsymbol{z}^{t}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{\ell}^{t}
are all row vectors.

Spatially Coupled State Evolution (SC-SE)

Similar to the i.i.d. case in Section
3
, we now state the asymptotic distributional guarantees for the AMP iterates with the SC design. For each iteration
t\geq 1,
in the limit as
L,n\to\infty
with
L/n\to\mu
, the memory term
\tilde{\boldsymbol{Z}}^{t}
in (
32
) ensures that
the empirical distribution of the rows of
\boldsymbol{Z}^{t}
in the block
\textsf{r}\in[\textsf{R}]
converges to a Gaussian
\mathcal{N}_{d}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textsf{r}}^{t})
. Furthermore, the empirical distribution of the rows of
(\boldsymbol{S}^{t}-\boldsymbol{X})
in block
\textsf{c}\in[\textsf{C}]
converges to another Gaussian
\mathcal{N}_{d}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{T}_{\textsf{c}}^{t})
. The covariance matrices
\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textsf{r}}^{t},\boldsymbol{T}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
are iteratively defined via the spatially coupled state evolution (SC-SE), a deterministic recursion defined as follows. Starting with initialization with
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\textsf{c}}^{0}=E\boldsymbol{I}_{d}
, for
t\geq 0
and
\textsf{r}\in[\textsf{R}]
,
\textsf{c}\in[\textsf{C}]
we define:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textsf{r}}^{t}=\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{d}\,+\,d\mu_{\text{in}}\sum_{c=1}^{\textsf{C}}W_{\textsf{r},\textsf{c}}\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}\,,

(44)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\textsf{c}}^{t+1}=\mathbb{E}\left\{\left[\eta_{t,\textsf{c}}\left(\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}\right)-\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}\right]\left[\eta_{t,\textsf{c}}\left(\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}\right)-\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}\right]^{\top}\right\},

(45)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\text{where }\boldsymbol{g}^{t}_{\textsf{c}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{d}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{T}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}),\quad\boldsymbol{T}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}={\left[\sum_{r=1}^{\textsf{R}}W_{\textsf{r},\textsf{c}}{[\boldsymbol{\Phi}_{\textsf{r}}^{t}]}^{-1}\right]^{-1}}.

(46)

Here
\boldsymbol{g}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}
is independent of
\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}\sim p_{\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}}
.
We can interpret
\boldsymbol{\Psi}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}
as the asymptotic covariance of the error in the estimated codeword
\boldsymbol{x}^{t}_{\ell}
relative to the true codeword
\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}
, for
\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{\textsf{c}}
, i.e., for the
c
-th block of users. The asymptotic error rates of AMP decoding with SC design are characterized by the following theorem.

Theorem 2

(Asymptotic
\mathtt{UER}
and
\mathtt{BER}
with SC design)
.

Consider the concatenated scheme with a spatially coupled Gaussian design, with the assumptions in Section
2.1
, and the AMP decoding algorithm in (
32
)–(
33
) with Lipschitz continuous denoisers
\eta_{t}:{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{L\times d}
, for
t\geq 1
. Let
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1}_{\ell}=h_{t,\textsf{c}(\ell)}(\boldsymbol{s}^{t}_{\ell})
be the hard-decision estimate of
\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}

in iteration
t
.
Then the asymptotic
\mathtt{UER}
and
\mathtt{BER}
in iteration
t
satisfy the following almost surely, for
t\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathtt{UER}

\displaystyle:=\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}^{t+1}_{\ell}\neq\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell}\right\}=\frac{1}{\textsf{C}}\sum_{\textsf{c}=1}^{\textsf{C}}\mathbb{P}\left(h_{t,\textsf{c}}\left(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}\right)\neq\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\right),

(47)

\displaystyle\lim_{L\to\infty}\mathtt{BER}

\displaystyle:=\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{Ld}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\hat{x}^{t+1}_{\ell,j}\neq x_{\ell,j}\right\}=\frac{1}{\textsf{C}}\sum_{\textsf{c}=1}^{\textsf{C}}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{P}\left(\left[h_{t,\textsf{c}}\left(\bar{x}+g_{\textsf{c}}^{t}\right)\right]_{j}\neq\bar{x}_{j}\right).

(48)

Here
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}\sim p_{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}}
and
\boldsymbol{g}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}\sim{\mathcal{N}}_{d}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{T}_{\textsf{c}}^{t})
are independent, with
\boldsymbol{T}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}
defined in (
46
). The limit is taken as
n,L\to\infty
with
L/n\to\mu
.

Proof.

See Section
7.2
.
∎

Choice of
\eta_{t}
and
h_{t}
in SC-AMP

The denoiser
\eta_{t}
in the AMP algorithm can be chosen analogously to the i.i.d. case in Section
4
, except that it now acts in a block-dependent manner as shown in (
5.1
). Take the Bayes-optimal denoiser
\eta_{t}^{\textrm{Bayes}}
as an example:
since the empirical distribution of the rows of
\boldsymbol{S}^{t}
in block
c
converges to the law of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}
, with
\boldsymbol{g}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}\sim{\mathcal{N}}_{d}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{T}_{\textsf{c}}^{t})
, we define for
\textsf{c}\in[\textsf{C}]
and
\boldsymbol{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\eta_{t,\textsf{c}}^{\textrm{Bayes}}(\boldsymbol{s})=\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}\ |\ \boldsymbol{\bar{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}=\boldsymbol{s}\right]=\sum_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\cdot\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}-2\boldsymbol{s})^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{T}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\right)}{\sum_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}-2\boldsymbol{s})^{\top}\left(\boldsymbol{T}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}\right)^{-1}\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\right)}\,.

(49)

The marginal-MMSE denoiser
\eta_{t}^{\textrm{marginal}}
and the BP denoiser
\eta_{t}^{\textrm{BP}}
can be defined similarly, with the only difference from the i.i.d. versions being that the covariance matrices are block-dependent for the spatially coupled case. The hard-decision estimator
h_{t}
is also be defined analogously, with block-dependent covariance. For example, the MAP hard-decision estimate takes the form

\displaystyle h_{t,\textsf{c}}(\boldsymbol{s})

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}\mathbb{P}\left({\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}}=\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\mid\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}=\boldsymbol{s}\right).

(50)

Observe that (
49
)–(
50
) are the counterparts of (
11
)–(
12
).

Implementation details

We discuss a few implementation details for the numerical results in Sections

.

Covariance estimation

In our iid-AMP simulations, the state evolution parameter
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
in (
8
), used by the Bayes-optimal denoiser
\eta_{t}^{\textrm{Bayes}}
in (
11
) and the MAP hard-decision estimator
h_{t}
in (
12
), is estimated using the modified residual term
\boldsymbol{Z}^{t}
in (
6
) via:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{t}=\frac{1}{\tilde{n}}\sum_{i\in[\tilde{n}]}{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}^{t}}({\boldsymbol{z}_{i}^{t}})^{\top},\qquad\text{for}\;t\geq 1.

(51)

State evolution results (not stated here) guarantee that for
t\geq 1
, we have
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{t}\to\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{t}
almost surely as
L\to\infty
with
L/n\to\mu
.
Recall that
d=n/\tilde{n}
does not grow with
n
and
L
. Since the AMP decoder uses
\eta_{t}^{\textrm{Bayes}}
and the MAP estimator
h_{t}
only for small
d
(e.g.,
d=7
for the Hamming code in Fig.
4
), a sufficiently accurate estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}^{t}
can be obtained with moderately large
\tilde{n}
and
L
.

For larger
d
(e.g.,
d=720
for the LDPC code used in Figs.
5
and
6
), the AMP decoder employs
\eta_{t}^{\textrm{marginal}}
or
\eta_{t}^{\textrm{BP}}
which do not require the full covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{t}
, but only the diagonal entries
\Sigma^{t}_{j,j}
for
j\in[d]
. We estimate these entries via:

\hat{\Sigma}^{t}_{j,j}=\frac{1}{\tilde{n}}\sum_{i\in[\tilde{n}]}\left(\boldsymbol{z}_{i}^{t}\right)_{j}^{2},\qquad\text{for}\;t\geq 1,\;j\in[d].

(52)

Estimating the diagonal entries alone is much less computationally expensive than the full
d\times d
covariance matrix, and ensures the computational cost of the AMP decoder with
\eta_{t}^{\textrm{marginal}}
or
\eta_{t}^{\textrm{BP}}
denoiser remain linear in
d
(and
k
).

Similarly, in our SC-AMP simulations, the SC-SE parameter
\boldsymbol{T}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d\times d}
in (
46
) for
\textsf{c}\in[\textsf{C}]
is estimated consistently via

\hat{\boldsymbol{T}}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}=\frac{1}{\tilde{n}/\textsf{R}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}_{\textsf{r}}}{\boldsymbol{z}_{i}^{t}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i}^{t})^{\top},\qquad\text{for}\;t\geq 1,

(53)

where
\mathcal{I}_{\textsf{r}}=\{(\textsf{r}-1)\tilde{n}/\textsf{R}+1,\dots,\textsf{r}\tilde{n}/\textsf{R}\}
.

Estimation of state evolution MSE

In each iteration of the iid-SE recursion (
8
), the MSE term
\mathbb{E}\{[\eta_{t}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t})-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}][\eta_{t}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t})-\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}]^{\top}\}
is estimated by generating Monte Carlo samples of
\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}{\mathcal{N}}_{d}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{t})
one randomly chosen sample of
\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}
, and applying
\eta_{t}
to each sample of
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t}
. Due to the linearity of the outer code, this MSE term is independent of the chosen sample of
\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}
, so we can fix
\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}
to be the all-zero codeword across all iterations. For consistently estimating the
d\times d
MSE matrix, the number of samples of
\boldsymbol{g}^{t}
needs to be much larger than
d
.
Similarly, in the spatially coupled case, for each
\textsf{c}\in[\textsf{C}]
, the MSE term
\mathbb{E}\{[\eta_{t,\textsf{c}}(\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}_{\textsf{c}}^{t})-\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}][\eta_{t,\textsf{c}}(\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}_{\textsf{c}}^{t})-\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}]^{\top}\}
in (
45
) is estimated using many Monte Carlo samples of
\boldsymbol{g}_{\textsf{c}}^{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}{\mathcal{N}}_{d}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{T}_{\textsf{c}}^{t})
and one randomly chosen sample of
\boldsymbol{\bar{x}}
.
With the
\eta_{t}^{\textrm{BP}}
denoiser, the computational and memory cost of MSE estimation could potentially be reduced using population dynamics
[
52
]
.

7.1
Proof of Theorem
1

The proof is similar to the proof of Theorem 1 in
[
9
]
. We begin with a state evolution characterization of the AMP iterates that follows from standard results in the AMP literature
[
18
]
,
[
41
, Section 6.7]
. For any Lipschitz test function
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
t\geq 1
, we almost surely have:

\displaystyle\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\varphi(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t})=\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t})\right].

(54)

The claims in (
9
) and (
10
) require a test function
\varphi
that is defined via indicator functions, which is not Lipschitz. We handle this by sandwiching
it between two Lipschitz functions that both converge to the required function in a suitable limit.

We prove (
10
), and omit the proof of (
9
) as it is simpler and follows along the same lines.
For
x_{\ell,j}\in\{\pm\sqrt{E}\}
, partition the space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
into two decision regions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\left(x_{\ell,j}\right):=\left\{\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}:\left[h_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t})\right]_{j}=x_{\ell,j}\right\},

(55)

and note that
\mathbbm{1}\{\hat{x}_{\ell,j}^{t+1}=x_{\ell,j}\}=\mathbbm{1}\{\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}\in\mathcal{D}\left(x_{\ell,j}\right)\}
. Let
d\left(\boldsymbol{v},\mathcal{D}\right):=\inf\{\|\boldsymbol{v}-\boldsymbol{u}\|_{2}:\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{D}\}
denote the distance between a vector
\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and a set
\mathcal{D}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.
For any
\epsilon>0
, define functions
\xi_{\epsilon,+},\xi_{\epsilon,-}:{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\xi_{\epsilon,+}\left(x_{\ell,j},\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}\right):=\begin{cases}1,\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{if }\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}\in\mathcal{D}\left(x_{\ell,j}\right)\\
0,\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\text{if }d\big[\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t},\mathcal{D}\left(x_{\ell,j}\right)\big]>\epsilon\\
1-d\big[\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t},\mathcal{D}\left(x_{\ell,j}\right)\big]/\epsilon,\;\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\xi_{\epsilon,-}\left(x_{\ell,j},\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}\right):=\begin{cases}1,\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\qquad\;\text{if }d\big[\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t},\mathcal{D}\left(x_{\ell,j}\right)^{c}\big]>\epsilon\\
0,\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\qquad\;\text{if }\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}\in\mathcal{D}\left(x_{\ell,j}\right)^{c}\\
d\big[\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t},\mathcal{D}\left(x_{\ell,j}\right)^{c}\big]/\epsilon,\,\ \quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Note that
\xi_{\epsilon,+},\xi_{\epsilon,-}
are Lipschitz-continuous with Lipschitz constant
1/\epsilon
. Moreover, define
\varphi_{\epsilon,+}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
as

\displaystyle\varphi_{\epsilon,+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}):=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\xi_{\epsilon,+}\left(x_{\ell,j},\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}\right),

(56)

and define
\varphi_{\epsilon,-}
analogously. Then
\varphi_{\epsilon,+}
and
\varphi_{\epsilon,-}
, being sums Lipschitz of functions, are also Lipschitz.

For any
\epsilon>0
and
\ell\in[L]
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{\epsilon,-}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t})\leq\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}\in\mathcal{D}\left(x_{\ell,j}\right)\right\}\leq\varphi_{\epsilon,+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}).

(57)

Applying (
54
) to
\varphi_{\epsilon,-}
and
\varphi_{\epsilon,-}
, we have

\begin{split}&\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\varphi_{\epsilon,-}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t})=\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi_{\epsilon,-}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t})\right],\\
&\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\varphi_{\epsilon,+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t})=\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi_{\epsilon,+}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t})\right].\end{split}

(58)

The functions
\varphi_{\epsilon,-}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t})
and
\varphi_{\epsilon,+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t})
both converge pointwise to
\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}\in\mathcal{D}\left(x_{\ell,j}\right)\right\}
as
\epsilon\to 0.
Thus by the Dominated Convergence Theorem, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi_{\epsilon,-}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t})\right]=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}\left\{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\in\mathcal{D}(\bar{x}_{j})\right\}\right]=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\varphi_{\epsilon,+}(\bar{\boldsymbol{x}},\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t})\right].

(59)

From (
57
), (
58
), and (
59
), we conclude that almost surely:

\displaystyle\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\varphi_{\epsilon,-}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t})=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\varphi_{\epsilon,+}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\ell},\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t})

\displaystyle=\lim_{L\to\infty}\frac{1}{Ld}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}\in\mathcal{D}\left(x_{\ell,j}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}\left\{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\in\mathcal{D}(\bar{x}_{j})\right\}\right].

(60)

By recalling
\mathbbm{1}\{\boldsymbol{s}_{\ell}^{t}\in\mathcal{D}(x_{\ell,j})\}=\mathbbm{1}\{\hat{x}_{\ell,j}^{t+1}=x_{\ell,j}\}
and noticing
\mathbbm{1}\{\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}+\boldsymbol{g}^{t}\in\mathcal{D}(\bar{x}_{j})\}=\mathbbm{1}\{[h_{t}(\bar{x}+g^{t})]_{j}=\bar{x}_{j}\}
, we see that
(
60
) is equivalent to (
10
). ∎

8
Discussion and future directions

This paper investigated communication over the GMAC in the many-user regime, where the number of users
L
scales linearly with the codelength
n
. We proposed a CDMA-type concatenated coding scheme with an efficient AMP decoder that can be tailored to the outer code. The asymptotic performance tradeoff was rigorously characterized, and it was shown that the scheme achieves state-of-the-art error performance with an outer LDPC code and AMP decoding with a belief propagation (BP) denoiser.

Although we considered Gaussian design matrices
\boldsymbol{A}
(i.i.d. or spatially coupled), using recent results on AMP universality
[
53
,
54
]
, the decoding algorithm and all the theoretical results remain valid for a much broader class of ‘generalized white noise’ matrices. This class includes i.i.d. sub-Gaussian matrices, so the results apply to the popular setting of random binary-valued signature sequences
[
14
,
17
]
.

The concatenated scheme and its analysis can also be extended to nonbinary CDMA constellations. Based on results for sparse superposition coding
[
55
,
9
]
, we expect that using PSK constellations instead of binary will improve the performance tradeoff for complex-valued channels.

Our theoretical results for the AMP decoder with a BP denoiser are valid only when BP is executed for fewer rounds than the girth of the LDPC factor graph (Lemma
1
). However, simulations indicate that the performance of AMP decoding may be improved if BP is run for additional rounds in each denoising step. Precisely characterizing the performance of AMP when the number of BP rounds is larger than the girth of the factor graph is a challenging open question. Another open question is to characterize the performance improvement achieved by executing several rounds of BP after AMP has converged.

Our asymptotic performance curves were obtained by running the state evolution recursion until convergence, with each iteration involving a computationally intensive MSE estimation step (see (
8
) and (
45
)). An alternative approach would be to directly characterize the fixed points of state evolution for sufficiently large base matrices using a potential function analysis, similar to
[
9
,
44
]
. The main challenge is that in our setting, such an analysis would require finding the extremal values of a potential function over (positive-definite)
d\times d
matrices
[
56
]
, which is infeasible even for moderately large
d
. One approach to simplify this optimization would be to assume symmetries in the structure of the optimal matrix.

This paper focused on the conventional GMAC setting where users have distinct codebooks. It would be very interesting to adapt the coding scheme to
unsourced
random access
[
2
,
34
,
35
,
36
]
, where the users share a common codebook and only a subset of users are active.
