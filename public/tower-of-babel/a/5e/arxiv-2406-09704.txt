Abstract
. In this paper, we introduce a data-driven framework for synthesis of provably-correct controllers for general nonlinear switched systems under complex specifications. The focus is on systems with unknown disturbances whose effects on the dynamics of the system is nonlinear. The specifications are assumed to be given as linear temporal logic over finite traces (LTL f ) formulas. Starting from observations of either the disturbance or the state of the system, we first learn an ambiguity set that contains the unknown distribution of the disturbances with a user-defined confidence. Next, we construct a robust Markov decision process (RMDP) as a finite abstraction of the system. By composing the RMDP with the automaton obtained from the LTL f formula and performing optimal robust value iteration on the composed RMDP, we synthesize a strategy that yields a high probability that the uncertain system satisfies the specifications. Our empirical evaluations on systems with a wide variety of disturbances show that the strategies synthesized with our approach lead to high satisfaction probabilities and validate the theoretical guarantees.



1.
Introduction

Stochastic dynamical systems are powerful mathematical models commonly employed to describe complex systems in many applications, ranging from autonomous systems
[
1
]
to biological systems
[
2
]
. Many of these applications are
safety-critical
, and formal correctness guarantees are required to avoid fatal or costly accidents. Another common feature of these applications is that they are characterized by nonlinear dynamics and that the characteristics of the noise affecting the system dynamics are often uncertain and need to be estimated from data
[
3
]
. That is, the system is both characterized by
aleatoric
uncertainty (i.e., the uncertainty intrinsic in the system dynamics) and by
epistemic
uncertainty (i.e., the uncertainty due to the lack of knowledge of the system dynamics).
A challenging research question that still remains open is
how to provide formal correctness guarantees for nonlinear stochastic systems whose noise distribution is unknown?
which is the focus of this work.

Formal guarantees of correctness for nonlinear stochastic systems are generally provided either via formal abstractions into a finite transition model, typically a variant of a Markov model
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
, or via stochastic barrier functions
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
]
. However, all those works assume a known model and known disturbances, i.e., no
epistemic uncertainty
.
To tackle this issue and account for epistemic uncertainty, data-driven formal approaches have been recently developed to estimate model dynamics from data
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. These works generally employ concentration inequalities to estimate the system dynamics from noisy measurements of the system and then rely on existing formal methods for verification and control of the estimated model. However, these approaches generally assume that the noise distribution of the system is known and only the vector field of the dynamics needs to be learnt. In addition, they often restrict themselves to linear disturbances. The complementary case where the noise distribution is unknown and needs to be estimated from data is considered in
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
, which however, are specific for linear systems or additive noise.

In this paper, we propose a formal data-driven approach for controller synthesis of nonlinear stochastic systems with unknown disturbances against linear temporal logic specifications over finite traces (LTLf)
[
17
]
. Our approach combines tools from optimal transport theory with abstractions methods. In particular, given a dataset, we first exploit results from
[
18
]
to build an
ambiguity set
for the disturbance distribution, that is, a set of distributions that contains the unknown law of the disturbance with high confidence. Then, we abstract the resulting uncertain model into a robust Markov decision process (RMDP)
[
19
]
, which formally accounts for both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty in the system. The resulting abstraction is employed to synthesize a controller that maximizes the probability that the system satisfies an LTL
f
property and is robust against the uncertainties, thus guaranteeing correctness.
The efficacy of our framework is illustrated on various benchmarks showing how our approach can successfully synthesize high-confidence formal controllers for uncertain nonlinear stochastic systems from data.

The key contributions of this paper are:

•

a novel strategy synthesis framework for nonlinear switched stochastic systems with general (e.g., non-additive) disturbances of unknown distributions under LTL
f
specifications,

•

an efficient procedure to obtain a finite and sound abstraction of said class of systems,

•

probabilistic satisfaction guarantees whose confidence is independent of the abstraction size,

•

extensive case studies and empirical evaluations involving five different systems, several choices of hyperparameters, and comparisons with the state-of-the-art approaches.

2.
Problem Formulation

In this work, we focus on discrete-time switched stochastic processes described by

(2.1)

\displaystyle\textbf{{x}}_{k+1}=f_{a_{k}}(\textbf{{x}}_{k},\textbf{{w}}_{k}),

where

\textbf{{x}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the state and
a_{k}\in A:=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{|A|}\}
is the control (mode or action) at time
k
. Noise term
\textbf{{w}}_{k}\in W
is an i.i.d. random variable taking values in a bounded set
W\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with probability distribution

P_{w}
identical at each time step
k
. Distribution
P_{w}
, however, is assumed to be
unknown
. For each
a\in A
,
f_{a}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times W\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the vector field, which is assumed to be
Lipschitz continuous in
w
with Lipschitz constant
L_{w}(x,a)\geq 0
, i.e.,

\|f_{a}(x,w)-f_{a}(x,w^{\prime})\|\leq L_{w}(x,a)\|w-w^{\prime}\|
for all
w,w^{\prime}\in W.

Consequently, Process (
2.1
) describes a general model of nonlinear switched stochastic processes, which encompasses many models commonly used in practice.

For
x_{0},\ldots,x_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
K\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\omega_{x}=x_{0}\xrightarrow{a_{0}}x_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}\ldots\xrightarrow{a_{K-1}}x_{K}
represents a finite
trajectory

of Process (
2.1
), and
\Omega_{x}
is the set of all finite trajectories (of any length).
We denote by
\omega_{x}(k)

the state of
\omega_{x}
at time
k\in\{0,\dots,K\}
.
A
switching strategy

\sigma_{x}:\Omega_{x}\to A
is a function that assigns an action
a\in A
to each finite trajectory.
For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
a\in A
and
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the
transition kernel

of Process (
2.1
) is

T^{a}(B\mid x)=\int_{W}\mathds{1}_{B}(f_{a}(x,v))P_{w}(dv)
.
Given a strategy
\sigma_{x}
and an initial condition
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the transition kernel uniquely defines a probability measure
P_{x_{0}}^{\sigma_{x}}
over the trajectories of Process (
2.1
)

[
20
]
such that
P_{x_{0}}^{\sigma_{x}}[\bm{\omega}_{x}(k)\in X]

represents the probability that, under strategy
\sigma_{x}
,
\textbf{{x}}_{k}
is in set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
starting from
x_{0}
.

Given a bounded set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we are interested in the temporal properties of Process (
2.1
)
w.r.t. a
set of regions of interest
R:=\{r_{1},\dots,r_{|R|-1},r_{u}\}
with
r_{i}\subseteq X
for all
i\leq|R|-1
, and
r_{u}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus X
. To each region
r_{i}\in R
, we associate an atomic proposition
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
, and say that
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\equiv\top
(i.e.,
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
is
true
) at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
iff
x\in r_{i}
. Denote by
AP:=\{\mathfrak{p}_{i},\dots,\mathfrak{p}_{|R|-1},\mathfrak{p}_{u}\}
the set of all atomic propositions and define the labeling function
L:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow 2^{AP}
as the function that maps each state
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to the set of atomic propositions that are true at that state, i.e.,
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\in L(x)
iff
x\in r_{i}
. Then, to each trajectory
\omega_{x}=x_{0}\xrightarrow{a_{0}}x_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}\ldots\xrightarrow{a_{K-1}}x_{K}
,
we can associate the (observation)
trace

\rho=\rho_{0}\rho_{1}\dots\rho_{K},

where
\rho_{k}:=L(x_{k})
for all
k\in\{0,\dots,K\}
.

To specify temporal properties of Process (
2.1
), we use LTL
f

[
17
]
, which
is a formal language commonly employed to express finite behaviors.
An LTL
f
formula
\varphi
is defined from a set of atomic propositions
AP
and is closed under the Boolean connectives “negation” (
\neg
) and “conjunction” (
\land
), and the temporal operators “until” (
\mathcal{U}
) and “next” (
\mathcal{X}
):

\displaystyle\varphi:=\top\mid\mathfrak{p}\mid\neg\varphi\mid\varphi_{1}\land\varphi_{2}\mid\mathcal{X}\varphi\mid\varphi_{1}\mathcal{U}\varphi_{2}

where
\mathfrak{p}\in AP
and
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}
are LTL
f
formulas themselves.
The temporal operators “eventually”,
\mathcal{F}
, and “globally”,
\mathcal{G}
, are defined as
\mathcal{F}\varphi:=\top\mathcal{U}\varphi
and
\mathcal{G}\varphi:=\neg\mathcal{F}(\neg\varphi)
.
The semantics (interpretation) of LTL
f
are defined over finite traces
[
17
]
. We say a trajectory
\omega_{x}
satisfies a formula
\varphi
, denoted by
\omega_{x}\models\varphi
, if a prefix of its trace
\rho
satisfies the formula.

We are interested in synthesizing a strategy that maximizes the probability that Process (
2.1
) satisfies a given LTL
f
formula, while accounting for the uncertainty coming from the fact that
P_{w}
is unknown and can only be estimated from data. A formal statement of the problem is as follows.

Problem 2.1
.

Consider the switched stochastic Process (
2.1
), a set
\{\textbf{{w}}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of
N
i.i.d. samples of
P_{w}
, a bounded set
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and an LTL
f
formula
\varphi
defined over the regions of interest
R
. Then, given a confidence level
1-\beta\in(0,1)
, synthesize a switching strategy
\sigma_{x}
such that, for every initial state
x_{0}\in X
, with confidence at least
1-\beta
,
\sigma_{x}
maximizes the probability that the paths
\omega_{x}\in\Omega_{x}
satisfy
\varphi
while remaining in
X
, i.e.,

\sigma_{x}\in\arg\max_{\sigma_{x}}P_{x_{0}}^{\sigma_{x}}[\omega_{x}\models\varphi\land\mathcal{G}\neg\mathfrak{p}_{u}]
.

Note that in Problem
2.1
the noise distribution
P_{w}
is unknown. Consequently,
P_{w}
must be estimated from data. This inevitably leads to a confidence probability on the resulting strategy, which irrespective of the method of quantification, depends on the data. Problem
2.1
requires this confidence to be at least
1-\beta
. We should also remark that we assume that a set of
N
samples from the noise is given.
This may seem like a strong assumption as, in general, only observations
\{\mathbf{y}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N+1}
of the state can be collected. However, in several cases, e.g., when successive full-state observations
\mathbf{y}^{(i)}=\mathbf{x}^{(i)}
are available, one can use the system’s dynamics
\mathbf{y}^{(i+1)}=f_{a^{(i)}}(\mathbf{y}^{(i)},\mathbf{w}^{(i)})
and knowledge of its actions
a^{(i)}
to obtain i.i.d. samples
\{\mathbf{w}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
of the noise distribution
P_{w}
.
For instance, this is possible when
f
is linear in
w
or, more generally, if the data are collected where
f_{a^{(i)}}(\mathbf{y}^{(i)},\cdot):W\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is injective. Such regions can be easily identified in many non-linear systems commonly employed in practice, as shown in the examples in Section
6
.

Overview of the approach

Since
P_{w}
is unknown and the state space of the process is uncountable, obtaining the exact solution to Problem
2.1
is generally infeasible. Hence, we follow an abstraction-based approach instead and synthesize an optimal strategy that is robust against the uncertainty in estimating
P_{w}
from data. First, in Section
4.1
,
we use the noise samples to learn an ambiguity set of probability distributions that contains
P_{w}
with confidence
1-\beta
. Next, in Section
4.3.1
, we obtain an interval MDP (IMDP) abstraction of Process (
2.1
) by considering that
w
is distributed according to a fixed distribution supported on the samples.
In Section
4.3.2
, to account for
all the distributions in the ambiguity set, we expand the set of transition probabilities of the IMDP, obtaining a robust MDP. Then, in Section
5
, we synthesize a (robust) optimal strategy for the robust MDP via robust dynamic programming. Finally, we refine this strategy to the original system, obtaining a guaranteed lower bound on the satisfaction of the LTL
f
specification for Process (
2.1
).

3.
Preliminaries on Robust Markov Decision Processes

A robust MDP (RMDP) is a generalization of a Markov decision process in which the transition probability distributions are uncertain and belong to an ambiguity set
[
19
,
21
]
.

Definition 3.1

(Robust MDP)
.

A labelled robust Markov decision process (RMDP)
\mathcal{M}
is a tuple
\mathcal{M}=(Q,A,\Gamma,q_{0},AP,L)
, where

•

Q
is a finite set of states

•

A
is a finite set of states and actions, and
A(q)
denotes the actions available at state
q\in Q
,

•

\Gamma=\{\Gamma_{q,a}:{q\in Q,a\in A(q)}\}
, where

\Gamma_{q,a}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(Q)
is the set of
transition probability distributions for state-action pair
(q,a)\in Q\times A(q)
,
1
1

1

Notice that for two distinct state-action pairs, the respective sets of transition probability distributions of
\mathcal{M}
are independent. This is the well-known
rectangular
property of robust MDPs
[
19
,
21
]
.

•

q_{0}\in Q
is initial state,

•

AP
is a finite set of atomic propositions

•

L:Q\longrightarrow 2^{AP}
is the labeling function.

A finite
path
of RMDP
\mathcal{M}
is a sequence of states
\omega=q_{0}\xrightarrow{a_{0}}q_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}\ldots\xrightarrow{a_{K-1}}q_{K}
such that
a_{k}\in A(q_{k})
and there
exists
\gamma\in\Gamma_{q_{k},a_{k}}
with
\gamma(q_{k+1})>0
for all
k\in\{0,\dots,K-1\}
. We denote the set of all paths of finite length by
\Omega
. Given a path
\omega\in\Omega
, we let
\omega(k)=q_{k}
be the state of
\omega
at time
k\in\{0,\dots,K\}
, and
\mathrm{last}(\omega)
be its last state.
A labelled interval-valued MDP (IMDP)
\mathcal{I}
, also called bounded-parameter MDP (BMDP)
[
22
]
, is an RMDP
\mathcal{I}=(Q,A,\Gamma,q_{0},AP,L)
where the transition probabilities lie within independent intervals. That is,

\Gamma_{q,a}=\{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}(Q):\underline{P}(q,a,q^{\prime})\leq\gamma(q^{\prime})\leq\overline{P}(q,a,q^{\prime})\>\text{for all}\>q,q^{\prime}\in Q,a\in A(q)\}.

Given a finite path of an RMDP or IMDP, a
strategy

\sigma
chooses the next action.
Formally,
\sigma:\Omega\rightarrow A
is a function that assigns an action
a\in A
to each finite path
\omega\in\Omega
of
\mathcal{M}
. Any given strategy
\sigma\in\Sigma
restricts the set of feasible transition probability distributions of the RMDP, thus yielding a set of Markov chains, or robust Markov chain. To further reduce this set to a single Markov chain, we define the adversary
function
[
22
]

that chooses the distribution of the next state.
Formally, an
adversary
is a function
\xi:\Omega\times A\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(Q)
that maps each finite path
\omega\in\Omega
and action
a\in A(\mathrm{last}(\omega))
to an admissible distribution
\gamma\in\Gamma_{q,a}
. The set of all adversaries is denoted by
\Xi
.
Given an initial condition
q_{0}\in Q
, a strategy
\sigma\in\Sigma
and an adversary
\xi\in\Xi
, the RMDP collapses to a Markov chain with a unique probability distribution
Pr_{\xi}^{q_{0},\sigma}
on its paths.

4.
Robust MDP Abstraction

Our approach is based on finite abstraction of Process (
2.1
) to an RMDP via ambiguity set learning. First, in Section
4.1
we show how we can build an ambiguity set that contains
P_{w}
with high confidence, and then, in the rest of the Section, we show how to build an RMDP abstraction

\mathcal{M}=(Q,A,\Gamma,AP,q_{0},L)
that formally accounts for this uncertainty.

4.1.
Learning the Unknown Probability Distribution

In this section, we make use of the samples of
w

to obtain a formal representation of the unknown distribution
P_{w}
. Specifically, we learn an ambiguity set, i.e., a set of probability distributions that is guaranteed to contain
P_{w}
with high confidence. In particular, Lemma
4.1
below allows us to build an ambiguity set of distributions centered on the empirical distribution generated by the samples.

Lemma 4.1

(Ambiguity set)
.

Given
s\in\mathbb{N}
,
l\in[1,\infty]
, and
N
i.i.d. samples
\textbf{{w}}^{(1)},\ldots,\textbf{{w}}^{(N)}
from
P_{w}
, define their empirical distribution as
\widehat{P}_{w}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{\textbf{{w}}^{(i)}}
. Further, let
\phi
be the diameter of
W
in the
\infty
-norm,
\beta\in(0,1)
, and

g(N,\phi,d,s,l)
be the upper bound on the expected transport cost
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}_{s}^{(l)}(P_{w},\widehat{P}_{w})]
between
P_{w}
and
\widehat{P}_{w}
given in
[
18
]
. Then the Wasserstein ball

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}:=\{P\in\mathcal{P}(W):\mathcal{W}_{s}(P,\widehat{P}_{w})\leq\varepsilon(N,\beta)\},

where

\displaystyle\varepsilon(N,\beta):=g(N,\phi,d,s,l)^{\frac{1}{s}}+\phi\sqrt{d}(2\log{1/\beta})^{\frac{1}{2s}}N^{-\frac{1}{2s}},

contains
P_{w}
with confidence
1-\beta
.

Proof.

In analogy to the proof of
[
23
, Proposition 24 of arXiv version]
,
we leverage the fact that
\mathcal{W}_{s}(P_{w},\widehat{P}_{w})
concentrates around its mean
[
24
]
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{W}_{s}^{(l)}(P_{w},\widehat{P}_{w})\geq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{W}_{s}^{(l)}(P_{w},\widehat{P}_{w})]+\tau\big)\leq e^{-N\tau^{2s}/(2\phi^{2s})},\>\forall\tau\geq 0.

By the definition of the
s
-Wasserstein distance and Jensen’s inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{W}_{s}^{(l)}(P_{w},\widehat{P}_{w})]:=\mathbb{E}[\big(\mathcal{T}_{s}^{(l)}(P_{w},\widehat{P}_{w})\big)^{1/s}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}_{s}^{(l)}(P_{w},\widehat{P}_{w})]^{1/s},

and therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(\mathcal{W}_{s}^{(l)}(P_{w},\widehat{P}_{w})\geq\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}_{s}^{(l)}(P_{w},\widehat{P}_{w})]^{1/s}+\tau\big)\leq e^{-N\tau^{2s}/(2\phi^{2s})},\>\forall\tau\geq 0.

Selecting
\tau
such that
\beta=e^{-N\tau^{2s}/(2\phi^{2s})}
and setting
\varepsilon:=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}_{s}^{(l)}(P_{w},\widehat{P}_{w})]^{1/s}+\tau
, we arrive at the desired result.
∎

It is important to stress that in all cases
g\to 0
as
N
increases. Thus, the radius of the ambiguity set
\mathcal{D}
shrinks with more data.

4.2.
States, Actions and Labeling Function of the Abstraction

The state-space of the abstraction is obtained as follows. First, we partition set
X
into the set of non-empty, non-overlapping regions
Q_{\mathrm{safe}}:=\{q_{1},\dots,q_{|Q_{\mathrm{safe}}|}\}
, i.e.,
q\cap q^{\prime}=\emptyset
for all
q\neq q^{\prime}\in Q_{\mathrm{safe}}
and
\cup_{q\in Q_{\mathrm{safe}}}q=X
. Furthermore, this partition must respect the regions of interest, i.e., for every
r\in R
and
q\in Q_{\mathrm{safe}}
,

q\cap r\in\{\emptyset,q\}
.
The state space is then defined by associating each state of the abstraction with a region in
Q_{\mathrm{safe}}
and the additional “unsafe" region
q_{u}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus X
, i.e.,
Q:=Q_{\mathrm{safe}}\cup\{q_{u}\}
. With a small abuse of notation, we let
q\in Q
denote at the same time a state of the abstraction and a region in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We also let the actions of the abstraction
A
be the same as those of Process (
2.1
), with
A(q):=A
for all
q\in Q
.
Furthermore, with an additional abuse of notation, we define the abstraction labeling function as
L:Q\rightarrow 2^{AP}
such that
L(q):=L(x)
if
x\in q
for all
q\in Q
.
Note that we do not fix the initial state, since our approach yields results for every
q_{0}\in Q
.

4.3.
Transition Probability Distributions of the Abstraction

4.3.1.
Accounting for the discretization error

To embed the discretization error into the abstraction, we first abstract Process (
2.1
) to an IMDP
\mathcal{I}:=(Q,A,\widehat{\Gamma},q_{0},AP,L)
, namely the “empirical IMDP”, under the assumption that
\textbf{{w}}_{k}
is distributed according to the empirical distribution
\widehat{P}_{w}
. Components
Q
,
A
,
q_{0}
,
AP
, and
L
of
\mathcal{I}
are as defined above. The following proposition defines the transition probability intervals
\widehat{\Gamma}
of
\mathcal{I}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
T_{\widehat{P}_{w}}^{u}
be the transition kernel of Process (
2.1
) when
\textbf{{w}}_{t}\sim\widehat{P}_{w}
, i.e.,
for
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
x\in X
, and
a\in A
,
T_{\widehat{P}_{w}}^{a}(B\mid x)=\int_{W}\mathds{1}_{B}(f_{a}(x,v))\widehat{P}_{w}(dv)
.
Further, define
\text{Reach}(q,a,\textbf{{w}}^{(i)}):=\{f_{a}(x,\textbf{{w}}^{(i)})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\in q\}
.
Then, for every
q\in Q_{\mathrm{safe}}
,
q^{\prime}\in Q
, and
a\in A
,

(4.2a)

\displaystyle\min_{x\in q}T_{\widehat{P}_{w}}^{a}(q^{\prime}\mid x)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N}|\{i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}:\text{Reach}(q,a,\textbf{{w}}^{(i)})\subseteq q^{\prime}\}|

(4.2b)

\displaystyle\max_{x\in q}T_{\widehat{P}_{w}}^{a}(q^{\prime}\mid x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}|\{i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}:\text{Reach}(q,a,\textbf{{w}}^{(i)})\cap q^{\prime}\neq\emptyset\}|.

Proof.

Let

Remark 4.5

(Clustering Samples)
.

As shown in Sections
4.1
and
4.3.1
, while the computational complexity of the abstraction is proportional to the number of samples, the ambiguity in the learned distribution and therefore the conservatism of the abstraction decrease with
N
. A straightforward way to deal with this trade-off

is to reduce the number of samples used to construct the abstraction via clustering. By doing so, we obtain another discrete distribution
\widetilde{P}_{w}
, supported on
N_{cluster}\ll N
points, and which is very close (in the Wasserstein sense) to
\widehat{P}_{w}
. By adding this small discrepancy to the original ambiguity radius and using the triangle inequality, we construct an ambiguity ball around
\widetilde{P}_{w}
that retains the exact same guarantees of containing
P_{w}
as our original ambiguity set.

Remark 4.6
.

Notice that the confidence on
\mathcal{M}
being a sound abstraction of Process (
2.1
) is the same as that of our ambiguity set containing
P_{w}
. This is an advantage of using an ambiguity set to represent learning uncertainty, instead of obtaining bounds on individual transition probabilities, as is commonly done in the literature, e.g.,
[
28
,
14
,
29
]
. In fact, these approaches lead the resulting confidence bounds to decrease with the abstraction size and to the paradox that large abstractions can be more uncertain than coarser ones.

5.
Strategy Synthesis

To obtain a strategy for Process (
2.1
), we compute a strategy
\sigma^{*}
for the abstraction
\mathcal{M}
that maximizes the probability of satisfying
\varphi
and is robust against all uncertainties in the abstraction. Since
\mathcal{M}
is a sound abstraction of (
2.1
),
\sigma^{*}
can be refined to a strategy on (
2.1
) with correctness guarantees.
To synthesize such
\sigma^{*}
, we first construct a deterministic finite automaton (DFA) that represents formula
\varphi

[
17
]
.

Definition 5.1

(DFA)
.

Given an LTL
f
formula
\varphi
defined over a set of atomic propositions
AP
, a deterministic finite automaton (DFA) constructed from
\varphi
is a tuple
\mathcal{A}=(Z,2^{AP},\delta,z_{0},Z_{F})
where

•

Z
is a finite set of states,

•

2^{AP}
is a finite set of input symbols,

•

\delta:Z\times 2^{AP}\longrightarrow Z
is the transition function,

•

z_{0}\in Z
is the initial state,

•

Z_{F}\subseteq Z
is the set of accepting states.

A trace
\rho=\rho_{0}\rho_{1}\dots\rho_{K}\in(2^{AP})^{*}
induces a run
z=z_{0}z_{1}\dots z_{K+1}
on
\mathcal{A}
, where
z_{k+1}=\delta(z_{k},\rho_{k})
for all
k\in\{0,\dots,K\}
. Per construction of
\mathcal{A}
introduced by
[
17
]
, trace
\rho
satisfies
\varphi
iff
z_{K+1}\in Z_{F}
, in which case run
z
is called
accepting
for
\mathcal{A}
.
Next, we generate the product of RMDP abstraction
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{A}
to capture the paths of
\mathcal{M}
that induce accepting runs in
\mathcal{A}
.

Definition 5.2

(Product RMDP)
.

Given RMDP
\mathcal{M}
and DFA
\mathcal{A}
, the product
\mathcal{M}^{\varphi}=\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{A}
is another RMDP
\mathcal{M}^{\varphi}=(Q^{\varphi},A^{\varphi},\Gamma^{\varphi},q_{0}^{\varphi},Q_{F}^{\varphi})
, where

•

Q^{\varphi}=Q\times Z
is the set of states,

•

A^{\varphi}=A
is the set of actions, and

denotes the actions available at state
(q,z)\in Q^{\varphi}

•

\Gamma^{\varphi}=\{\Gamma_{(q,z),a}^{\varphi}:{(q,z)\in Q^{\varphi},a\in A^{\varphi}((q,z))}

transition probability distributions for state-action pair
((q,z),a)\in Q^{\varphi}\times A((q,z))

\displaystyle\Gamma_{(q,z),a}^{\varphi}:=\{\gamma^{\varphi}\in\mathcal{D}(Q^{\varphi}):\exists\gamma\in\Gamma_{q,a}\>\text{s.t.}\>\forall q^{\prime}\in Q,\gamma^{\varphi}((q^{\prime},z^{\prime}))=\gamma(q^{\prime})\>\text{iff}\>z^{\prime}=\delta(z,L(q^{\prime}))\},

\mathcal{M}^{\varphi}

Conclusion and Future Work

We present a framework to perform formal control of switched stochastic systems with general nonlinear dynamics and unknown disturbances under LTL
f
specifications. The experimental results show the generality and effectiveness of our approach. Future research directions include increasing the tightness of our results and analyzing convergence of our solution to
the optimal one.
