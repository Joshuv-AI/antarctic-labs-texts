Abstract

Total variation(TV) regularization is applied to X-Ray computed tomography(CT) in an effort to reduce metal artifacts.
Tikhonov regularization with
L^{2}
data fidelity term and total variation regularization is augmented in this novel model by inequality constraints on sinogram data affected by metal to model errors caused by metal.
The formulated problem is discretized and solved using the Chambolle-Pock algorithm.
Faster convergence is achieved using preconditioning in a Douglas-Rachford spitting method as well as Advanced Direction Method of Multipliers(ADMM).
The methods are applied to real and synthetic data demonstrating feasibility of the model to reduce metal artifacts.
Technical details of CT data used and its processing are given in the appendix.

Keywords:
Computed tomography, metal artifact, total variation, regularization, preconditioning

Chapter 1
Introduction

Two sets of problems are treated in this thesis, the first is reconstruction that is to recover a cross-section or profile of an object given only projection data of X-rays passing through the object from different directions.
This classical computed tomography(CT) problem is compounded in the context of medical imaging by the fact that X-rays are harmful to living tissue and thus it is desirable to increase image quality of recovered images using as little projections as possible, thus making the problem more ill-posed.

The second related problem is that of metal artifacts, that is severe degradation of image quality due to metal objects present in the tissue which strongly attenuate or even block X-rays.
As many surgical procedures involve metal objects such as dental fillings, artificial hips or spine implants and the fact that magnetic resonance imaging is no alternative when ferromagnetic objects are present, efforts to improve image quality have been sought for some time and come in a variety of forms
[
Gje+16
]
.

The approach in this thesis employs variational regularization which enjoy widespread success in image processing.
Regularization can be seen as making a trade-off between data fidelity and assumed image properties, this is augmented by inequality constraints.
For the problem of removing metal artifacts sinogram data effected by metal is considered degraded and replaced by inequality constrains, reasoning that the data is unknown but expected to be greater then a certain threshold.
Many for metal artefact reduction are based around the idea of replacing this faulty sinogram data in various ways like inpainting by linearly fitting the gaps
[
Che+12
]
or expectation maximization
[
Wan+96
]
.
The model of this thesis focuses only on relative errors which are greater in projection affected by metal and as such is a general model that does not specifically take into account beam scattering or hardening.
While the data used in this thesis comes from a dual source CT scanner was used, for the reconstruction only data from only one of the two sources was used as if it was a regular single source scanner.
Indeed some methods for metal artifact reduction are based on the fact that two sources with different energy spectra are available
[
Gje+16
]

[
Bam+11
]
, but the purpose of this thesis is to evaluate the basic feasibility of removing artefacts with a general image processing techniques.

Theory of the Radon transform

This section gives the definition of the Radon transform, which is a simple model for X-ray tomography.
First the physical process is modelled according to
[
Eps08
]
, leading to the definition of the Radon transform
R
.
Next a way of inverting
R
, the filtered back projection, is discussed which will involve the adjoint
R^{\ast}
.
For a classical reference see
[
KS01
]
.

2.1
Modelling and definition of the Radon transform

First the physical process of a X-ray passing through material is modelled under the following assumptions:

1.

There is no refraction or diffraction so that beams travel along straight lines.

2.

The X-rays have a single energy level in the sense that all photons have the same energy, and thus the intensity
I
of the beam can be described with only one value.

3.

The loss of intensity a beam suffers from traveling through a material is the product of the intensity and a linear absorption coefficient
u(x)
specific to the traversed material, this is formulated as Beer’s law:

\frac{\mathrm{d}I}{\mathrm{d}t}=-u(x)I

where
t
refers to the arc length along a straight line segment.

Let
x_{0},x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
be linearly independent vectors, let
s\in\mathbb{R}
and consider the line
L(t)=sx_{0}+tx_{1}
, let the intensity along
L
be given by

i(t)=I(sx_{0}+tx_{1})

the linear absorption coefficient along
L
is

u_{L}(t)=u(sx_{0}+tx_{1}).

Then Beer’s law states that

i^{\prime}(t)=-u_{L}(t)i(t)\text{ or }-u_{L}(t)=\frac{i^{\prime}(t)}{i(t)}.

Integrating from
t=a
to
t=b
gives

-\int_{a}^{b}u_{L}(t)\;\mathrm{d}t=\log(i(b))-\log(i(a)).

(1.1)

Therefore, the process of an X-ray passing through an object, being weakened and received by a detector is modeled as integrating the absorption coefficient
u
over a straight line
L
.

In this model for computed tomography a radiation source, which emits parallel beams and an opposing line detector rotate around an object to be scanned in a plane.
The object to be scanned is modeled as a function
u\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
, that maps a point
(x,y)
to the linear absorption coefficient
u(x,y)
at that point, which can be thought of as a density distribution of the X-ray attenuation, referred to as radiodensity or simply density.
The function
u
is assumed to be zero outside a region of interest
D
.
Consider a ray traveling along the line
L=L(s,\theta)
where
\varphi
is the gradient angle of
L
and
s
its signed distance from the origin, the
offset
.
If the ray is emitted at the source with intensity
I(x_{S})
and its intensity is measured at the detector as
I(x_{D})
then by equation (
1.1
)

\int_{L}u(x)\;\mathrm{d}t=-\log(I(x_{D}))+\log(I(x_{S})).

(1.2)

The value of this line integral will be set as
(Ru)(\varphi,s)
.
The operator
R
which maps
u
to the function
Ru
, which is commonly interpreted as a function of angle and offset, is called the
Radon transform
, in a technical context the data
Ru
produced is called
sinogram
.
While the Radon transform can in principle be computed whenever the integrals over all such lines exist, it makes sense to define it for functions in
L^{2}
since then the result will also be in
L^{2}
as the following theorem shows; it also serves as a formal definition of
R
.

2.1 Theorem
.

Let
\rho>0,\,D=B_{\rho}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},\,\Omega=(-\rho,\rho)\times(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})
and
T=T(s)=\sqrt{\rho^{2}-s^{2}}
then the operator defined for
u\in L^{2}(D)
by

(Ru)(s,\theta)=\int_{L(s,\theta)}u\;\mathrm{d}s=\int_{-T}^{T}u(x_{0}(\theta)s+x_{1}(\theta)t)\;\mathrm{d}t

(1.3)

with

x_{0}(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta)\\
\sin(\theta)\end{pmatrix},\quad x_{1}(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}[r]-\sin(\theta)\\
\cos(\theta)\end{pmatrix},

defines a continuous linear operator
R\colon L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
with
\|{R}\|\leq\sqrt{2\pi\rho}
.
It is called the
Radon transform
.

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(D)\cap L^{2}(D)
, then

\displaystyle\|{Ru}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left|{\big(Ru\big)(s,\theta)}\right|^{2}\,\;\mathrm{d}(s,\theta)=\int_{-\rho}^{\rho}\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\left|{\big(Ru\big)(s,\theta)}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=\int_{-\rho}^{\rho}\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\left|{\int_{-T}^{T}u(x_{0}(\theta)s+x_{1}(\theta)t)\;\mathrm{d}t}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\rho}^{\rho}\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}2\rho\int_{-T}^{T}\left|{u(x_{0}(\theta)s+x_{1}(\theta)t)}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=2\rho\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\int_{-\rho}^{\rho}\int_{-T}^{T}\left|{u(x_{0}(\theta)s+x_{1}(\theta)t)}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{d}s\;\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=2\rho\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\int_{D}\left|{u(x)}\right|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}\theta=2\rho\pi\|{u}\|_{2}^{2}.

Here the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality was used in the third line and the transformation
\phi(t,s)=x_{0}(\theta)s+x_{1}(\theta)t
with
\det(\D\phi)=\det((x_{1},x_{0}))=1
in the forth line.
As
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(D)\cap L^{2}(D)
is dense in
L^{2}(D)
the operator
R
can be extended uniquely to
R\colon L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
and the inequality shows
\|{R}\|\leq\sqrt{2\pi\rho}
.
∎

2.2
Filtered Backprojection

The Radon transform is invertible, one way to show this is to use a connection to the Fourier transform.
Let
u\in L^{2}(D)
and
v=Ru
, define for fixed
\theta\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})
the function
v_{\theta}\colon s\mapsto v(s,\theta)
for
\left|{s}\right|<\rho
extended with
v_{\theta}(s)=0
for
\left|{s}\right|\geq\rho
.
Then the 1-D Fourier transform
\fourld
of
v_{\theta}
satisfies

\displaystyle\fourld(v_{\theta})(\omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}v_{\theta}(s)e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\omega s}\;\mathrm{d}s

(1.4)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\tilde{u}(x_{0}(\theta)s+x_{1}(\theta)t)\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\omega s}\;\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{d}s

(1.5)

where
\tilde{u}(x)=u(x)
for
x\in D
and
\tilde{u}(x)=0
otherwise.
Setting
x=x_{0}(\theta)s+x_{1}(\theta)t
then
s=x\cdot x_{0}(\theta)
as
(x_{0},x_{1})
is orthonormal, then equation (
1.4
) becomes

\fourld(v_{\theta})\left(\omega\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(x)\,e^{-\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\omega x\cdot x_{0}}\;\mathrm{d}x=\sqrt{2\pi}\four(\tilde{u})(\omega x_{0}),

(1.6)

where
\four
denotes the 2-D Fourier transform.
This fact is often referred to as the
Fourier slice theorem
and expressed in words as: projecting a function
u
to a line and then performing a 1-D Fourier transform is the same as taking the 2-D Fourier transform of
u
and restricting the values to that line, up to a factor of
\sqrt{2\pi}
.

Most importantly, from (
1.6
) it can be seen that
\four(u)
can be calculated from the projection data
v
, and thus using the inverse Fourier transform,
u
itself can be reconstructed.
For this purpose let
\four(\tilde{u})=\hat{u}
, then with the transformation
\xi=\omega x_{0}(\theta)
and
\;\mathrm{d}\xi=\left|{\omega}\right|\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}\omega
for any
x\in D

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=\tilde{u}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\hat{u}(\xi)e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\xi\cdot x}\;\mathrm{d}\xi

(1.7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|{\omega}\right|\hat{u}(\omega x_{0}(\theta))e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\omega x_{0}(\theta)\cdot x}\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}\omega

(1.8)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|{\omega}\right|\four(v_{\theta})\left(\omega\right)e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\omega x_{0}(\theta)\cdot x}\;\mathrm{d}\theta\;\mathrm{d}\omega

(1.9)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}w(x\cdot x_{0}(\theta),\theta)\;\mathrm{d}\theta

(1.10)

with

w(s,\theta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\left|{\omega}\right|}{2\pi}\fourld(v_{\theta})\left(\omega\right)e^{\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu\omega s}\;\mathrm{d}\omega=\fourld^{-1}\left(\frac{\left|{\cdot}\right|}{2\pi}\fourld(v_{\theta})\left(\cdot\right)\right)(s,\theta).

(1.11)

Thus a way to invert the Radon transform is found, which is called the
filtered back-projection
, where calculating
w
in (
1.11
) is called filtering, or applying the Ram-Lak filter and the right hand side of (
1.10
) the linear back-projection of
w
.
In fact this linear back-projection is the adjoint of the Radon transform as the following lemma shows.

2.2 Lemma
.

The adjoint of the Radon transform
R^{\ast}\colon L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(D)
maps
w\in L^{2}(\Omega)
to
R^{\ast}w
given by

(R^{\ast}w)(x)=\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}w(x\cdot x_{0}(\theta),\theta)\;\mathrm{d}\theta.

Proof.

Let
u\in L^{2}(D)\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(D)
and
w\in L^{2}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\Omega)
then

\displaystyle\langle{Ru},{w}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\int_{-\rho}^{\rho}w(s,\theta)\int_{-T}^{T}u(x_{0}(\theta)s+x_{1}(\theta)t)\;\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{d}s\;\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u(x)w(x\cdot x_{0}(\theta),\theta)\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}\theta=\langle{u},{R^{\ast}w}\rangle_{L^{2}(D)}.

Since
L^{2}(D)\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(D)
is dense in
L^{2}(D)
the same holds for
u,w\in L^{2}(D)
.
∎

The filtered back-projection is used is practice to numerically reconstruct the image, it has the advantage of being very fast but the disadvantage of amplifying any noise that might be present in the projection data.
One remedy for this is using a different filter e. g. instead of multiplying with
\left|{\omega}\right|
in (
1.11
) one can multiply by
\left|{\omega}\right|\sinc(\omega\frac{\pi}{2})
thus suppressing high frequency modes, which should mostly correspond to noise, this is called the Shepp-Logan filter.
Nonetheless, iterative reconstruction algorithms have recently gained more and more interest as the provide increased insensitivity to noise.
Additionally, these methods are more flexible and can be used when only a small number of projections are available – e. g. in an effort to save on radiation dose – or when additional assumptions are put into the model e. g. to reduce artifacts.

Performing the Radon transform followed by back projection gives the same result as convolution with the reciprocal of the norm, as formulated in the following lemma.

2.3 Lemma
.

Let
u\in L^{2}(D)
then for
z\in D
it holds that

(R^{\ast}Ru)(z)=\int_{D}u(\tilde{z})\frac{1}{\|{z-\tilde{z}}\|}\;\mathrm{d}\tilde{z}.

Proof.

Let
u\in L^{2}(D)\cap\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(D)
for fixed
z\in D
calculate

\displaystyle(R^{\ast}Ru)(z)

\displaystyle=\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}(Ru)(z\cdot x_{0}(\theta),\theta)\;\mathrm{d}\theta=\int_{-\frac{\pi}{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\int_{-T}^{T}u(z\cdot x_{0}(\theta)x_{0}(\theta)+tx_{1}(\theta))\;\mathrm{d}t\;\mathrm{d}\theta.

Use the transformation
\tilde{z}=z\cdot x_{0}(\theta)x_{0}(\theta)+tx_{1}(\theta)
.
Specifically let

M_{z}=\Bigl\{{(t,\theta)}\ \Bigl|\ {\theta\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}],0<\left|{t}\right|<\sqrt{\rho^{2}-(z\cdot x_{0}(\theta))^{2}}}\Bigr\}

(1.12)

then
\varphi\colon M_{z}\to D\setminus\{{z}\},(t,\theta)\mapsto\tilde{z}=(\tilde{x},\tilde{y})
with

\displaystyle\tilde{x}

\displaystyle=z\cdot x_{0}(\theta)\cos(\theta)-t\sin(\theta)

\displaystyle\tilde{y}

\displaystyle=z\cdot x_{0}(\theta)\sin(\theta)+t\cos(\theta)

is a coordinate transform.
Firstly
\varphi
does indeed map to
D\setminus\{{z}\}
as
\tilde{x}^{2}+\tilde{y}^{2}=(z\cdot x_{0}(\theta))^{2}+t^{2}<\rho^{2}
.
In order to show that
\varphi
is surjective let
\tilde{z}\in D\setminus\{{z}\}
be arbitrary then for any
\theta
the vectors
x_{0}(\theta),x_{1}(\theta)
are a orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and so
\tilde{z}=\tilde{z}\cdot x_{0}(\theta)x_{0}(\theta)+\tilde{z}\cdot x_{1}(\theta)x_{1}(\theta)
.
Choosing the unique
\theta\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]
such that
(\tilde{z}-z)\cdot x_{0}(\theta)=0
as well as
t=\tilde{z}\cdot x_{1}(\theta)
gives
\varphi((t,\theta))=\tilde{z}
with
(t,\theta)\in M_{z}
since
t^{2}=\left|{\tilde{z}-\tilde{z}\cdot x_{0}(\theta)x_{0}(\theta)}\right|^{2}=\left|{\tilde{z}}\right|^{2}-2(z\cdot x_{0}(\theta))^{2}+(z\cdot x_{0}(\theta))^{2}<\rho^{2}-z\cdot x_{0}(\theta)
.
Injectivity is clear as multiplying
\tilde{z}=z\cdot x_{0}(\theta)x_{0}(\theta)+tx_{1}(\theta)
by
x_{0}(\theta)
gives
z\cdot x_{0}(\theta)=\tilde{z}\cdot x_{0}(\theta)
which is true for a unique
\theta\in(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]
, multiplying by
x_{1}(\theta)
gives necessarily
t=\tilde{z}\cdot x_{1}(\theta)
.

Clearly
\varphi
is differentiable with the Jacobian

J=\begin{pmatrix}z\cdot x^{\prime}_{0}(\theta)\cos(\theta)-z\cdot x_{0}(\theta)\sin(\theta)-t\cos(\theta)&-\sin(\theta)\\
z\cdot x^{\prime}_{0}(\theta)\sin(\theta)+z\cdot x_{0}(\theta)\cos(\theta)-t\sin(\theta)&\cos(\theta)\\
\end{pmatrix}

(1.13)

which has the determinant

\det(J)=z\cdot x^{\prime}_{0}(\theta)-t=z\cdot x_{1}(\theta)-t.

(1.14)

Since
(\tilde{z}-z)\cdot x_{0}(\theta)=z\cdot x_{0}(\theta)-z\cdot x_{0}(\theta)=0
it holds that
\|{z-\tilde{z}}\|=\left|{(z-\tilde{z})\cdot x_{1}(\theta)}\right|=\left|{z\cdot x_{1}(\theta)-t}\right|
and therefore
\left|{\det(J)}\right|=\|{z-\tilde{z}}\|
.
Performing this coordinate transform yields

(R^{\ast}Ru)(z)=\int_{D}u(\tilde{z})\frac{1}{\|{z-\tilde{z}}\|}\;\mathrm{d}\tilde{z}=(u\ast\frac{1}{\|{\cdot}\|})(z).

(1.15)

∎

3
Mathematical Framework

This section presents the mathematical framework for the methods used in this thesis, it is mostly a summary of the content of
[
BL10
]
which is needed here.
The overall goal will be the solution of optimization problems which are of the form
\min_{u\in X}\Phi(u)+\lambda\Psi(u)
, where
\Phi
is the data fidelity or discrepancy term measuring how close
u
is to given data
u^{0}
and
\Psi
is the penalty or regularization term which favors desired properties of the solution.
The regularization parameter
\lambda>0
balances these two objectives.
Here
\Phi
and
\Psi
are assumed to be convex but possibly not smooth.
To give the context of the methods a brief introduction to convex analysis is given, this also covers the Fenchel conjugate, Rockafellar duality and saddle point problems.
Then a specific regularization term - the total variation semi-norm is presented and its role in image processing as a tool for edge preserving noise removal is discussed.
Finally the previous two sections are combined in a discussion of the Tikhonov regularization where the data fidelity term will not compare
u
directly to
u^{0}
, as would be the case e. g. with
\Phi(u)=\|{u-u^{0}}\|
, but rather after applying an operator
A
as in
\Phi(u)=\|{Au-u^{0}}\|
since this will be the situation in Section
2
.

3.1
Convex Analysis

3.1 Definition
.

Call the set
\mathbb{R}_{\infty}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
the
extended reals
.
Let
X
be a Banach space and
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
then

\epi(f)=\{{(x,t)\in X\times\mathbb{R}}\ |\ {f(x)\leq t}\}

(1.16)

is called the
epigraph
of
f
.
Note that
t=\infty
is not allowed and thus
f\equiv\infty
if and only if
\epi(f)=\emptyset
, if this is not the case
f
is called
proper
.
The function
f
is called
convex
if its epigraph is a convex set, and it is called
(weakly) lower semi-continuous
if its epigraph is (weakly) closed.
This definition of convexity is equivalent to
f(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y)\leq\lambda f(x)+(1-\lambda)f(y)
for all
x,y\in X
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
, additionally if the inequality is strict whenever
x\neq y
then
f
is called
strictly convex
.

3.2 Lemma
.

A function
f
is (weakly) lower semi-continuous if and only if for every sequence
(u_{n})
(weakly) converging to
u
in
X
it holds that
f(u)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}f(u_{n})
.

Proof.

Let
\epi(f)
be closed and let
u_{n}\to u
.
Let
\bar{f}=\liminf_{n\to\infty}f(u_{n})
and
(u_{n_{k}})
be a subsequence of
(u_{n})
such that
f(u_{n_{k}})\to\bar{f}
.
Since
(u_{n},f(u_{n}))\in\epi(f)
and
(u_{n_{k}},f(u_{n_{k}}))\to(u,\bar{f})
it follows that
(u,\bar{f})\in\epi(f)
, since
\epi(f)
is closed by assumption.
Thus
f(u)\leq\bar{f}
.

For the implication from left to right, let
(u_{n},t_{n})\in\epi(f)
with
(u_{n},t_{n})\to(u,t)
it needs to be shown that
(u,t)\in\epi(f)
.
Using the assumption and
f(u_{n})\leq t_{n}
it follows that
f(u)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}f(u_{n})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}t_{n}=t
and thus
(u,t)\in\epi(f)
.
The proof for weak convergence is completely analogous.
∎

A convex function
f
is weakly lower semi-continuous if and only if it is lower semi-continuous since its epigraph is a convex set, which is closed if and only if it is weakly closed.

The following standard argument guarantees the existence of a minimizer of the optimization problem
\min_{u\in X}F(u)
.

3.3 Lemma

(Direct method)
.

Let
X
be a reflexive Banach space and
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
be convex, proper, weakly lower semi-continuous and bounded from below.
Furthermore let
f
be radially unbounded, i. e.
\|{u_{n}}\|\to\infty
implies
f(u_{n})\to\infty
.
Then a solution
u^{\ast}
of
\min_{u\in X}f(u)
exists.

Proof.

Let
m=\inf_{u\in X}f(u)
then
m>-\infty
as
f
is bounded from below.
Choose a sequence
(u_{n})
with
f(u_{n})\to m
.
This sequence must be bounded as otherwise radially unboundedness of
f
would imply
f(u_{n})\to\infty
, which is not the case as
(u_{n})
is a minimizing sequence.
Since
X
is a reflexive Banach space the Eberlein-Šmulian theorem guarantees that this bounded sequence must have a weakly convergent subsequence
{u_{n_{k}}}\rightharpoonup u^{\ast}
.
Since
f
is weakly lower semi-continuous it holds that
f(u^{\ast})\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}f(u_{n_{k}})=m
and therefore
f(u^{\ast})=m=\min_{u\in X}f(u)
.
∎

3.2
Fenchel Duality

3.4 Definition
.

Let
X
be a normed vector space and
X^{\ast}
its dual and let
{\langle{\cdot},{\cdot}\rangle}_{X,X^{\ast}}=\langle{\cdot},{\cdot}\rangle
denote the duality pairing.
Let
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
be proper, then the function
f^{\ast}\colon X^{\ast}\to\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
defined by

f^{\ast}(\xi)=\sup_{x\in X}\,\langle{\xi},{x}\rangle-f(x)

is called the
convex conjugate
of
f
.
Note that the assumption of
f
being proper ensures that
f^{\ast}
is well defined, since then for fixed
\xi
the expression
\langle{\xi},{x}\rangle-f(x)
cannot be
-\infty
for all
x
, it can however be unbounded from above leading to
f^{\ast}(\xi)=\infty
.

3.5 Example
.

Calculate the conjugate of the function
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R},x\mapsto f(x)=\lambda\|{x}\|
for
\lambda>0
, for
\xi\in X^{\ast}
:

\displaystyle f^{\ast}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\sup_{x\in X}\,\langle{\xi},{x}\rangle-\lambda\|{x}\|=\sup_{\mu>0}\sup_{\|{x}\|=1}\,\langle{\xi},{\mu x}\rangle-\lambda\mu

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mu>0}\mu(\sup_{\|{x}\|=1}\,\langle{\xi},{x}\rangle-\lambda)=\sup_{\mu>0}\mu(\|{\xi}\|_{X^{\ast}}-\lambda).

Thus
f^{\ast}(\xi)=\infty
for
\|{\xi}\|_{X^{\ast}}>\lambda
and
f^{\ast}(\xi)=0
for
\|{\xi}\|_{X^{\ast}}\leq\lambda
.
This can be compactly written as
f^{\ast}=\indicator_{B_{\lambda}^{\ast}}
, where
\indicator_{M}
denotes the indicator function of a set
M
defined as

\indicator_{M}(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }x\in M\\
\infty&\text{if }x\notin M.\\
\end{cases}

3.6 Definition
.

Let
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
and
x\in X
then the set

\partial f(x)=\{{\xi\in X^{\ast}}\ |\ {f(x)+\langle{\xi},{y-x}\rangle\leq f(y),\forall y\in X}\}.

is called the
subdifferential
of
f
at
x
.

The subdifferential is indeed a generalization of the classical differential, since it can be shown that if
f
is differentiable at a point
x
with the derivative
f^{\prime}(x)
then
\partial f(x)=\{f^{\prime}(x)\}
.
The fact that for a convex functions
f
which is differentiable the tangent at a point
x
lies below the graph of
f
, i. e.
f(x)+\langle{f^{\prime}(x)},{y-x}\rangle\leq f(y)
for all
y
, is generalized by the subdifferential which is the collection of all slopes for which this is the case.
Furthermore the notion of critical points, that is
f^{\prime}(x)=0
as a necessary condition for
x
being a local minimum, is generalized by the subdifferential in the following way.
It holds that
u^{\ast}\in\argmin_{u\in X}f(u)
if and only if
0\in\partial f(u^{\ast})
, this follows immediately from the definition and is true also for non-convex functions.

The following lemma, known as Fenchel’s equality, provides a useful connection between the subdifferential of a function and its conjugate.

3.7 Lemma
.

Let
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
be proper, convex and lower semi-continuous then for
x\in X
,
\xi\in X^{\ast}
:

\xi\in\partial f(x)\Leftrightarrow f(x)+f^{\ast}(\xi)=\langle{\xi},{x}\rangle\Leftrightarrow x\in\partial f^{\ast}(\xi).

(1.17)

From now on let
X
be a Hilbert space and identify
X^{\ast}=X
via the Riesz representation.
In particular
\langle{\cdot},{\cdot}\rangle
will now denote both duality pairing and the scalar product on
X
.
The subdifferential can be thought of as a set valued operator denote by
2^{X}
the set of all subsets of
X
then
\partial f\colon X\to 2^{X},x\mapsto\partial f(x)
, sometimes also written as
\partial f\colon X\rightrightarrows X
.
Such a set valued operator
M\colon X\to 2^{X}
is called
singleton
if it maps every
x\in X
to a singleton set, the set valued inverse is defined by
M^{-1}\colon X\to 2^{X},u\mapsto\{x\in X|u\in Mx\}
.
Solving the optimization problem
\min_{u\in X}f(u)
is equivalent to solving the operator equation
0\in\partial f(x)
, or equivalently
x\in x+\sigma\partial f(x)
for any
\sigma>0
which means that
x
is a fixed point of the operator
\id+\sigma\partial f
.
It can be shown that under if
f
is proper, convex and lower semi-continuous then the operator
\id+\sigma\partial f
has a singleton inverse.

3.8 Definition
.

Let
X
be a Hilbert space and
f\colon X\to\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
be proper, convex and lower semi-continuous and
\sigma>0
then the mapping
(\id+\sigma\partial f)^{-1}
which maps
u^{0}
to the unique solution of

\min_{u\in X}\frac{1}{2}\|{u-u^{0}}\|_{X}^{2}+\sigma f(u)

is called the
resolvent
of
\partial f
to parameter
\sigma
.

Let
x,y\in X
and
\xi\in\partial f(x)
,
\eta\in\partial f(y)
then

\displaystyle f(x)+\langle{\xi},{y-x}\rangle\leq f(y)

(1.18)

\displaystyle f(y)+\langle{\eta},{x-y}\rangle\leq f(x)

(1.19)

adding both together gives

\langle{\xi-\eta},{x-y}\rangle\geq 0

(1.20)

in general a operator
M
with this property is called
monotone
.
If in addition to that
M+\id
is surjective the operator is called
maximal monotone
.
Generally in trying to solve
0\in M(u)
for a maximal monotone operator

one can show that for given

and
\sigma>0
there exists a unique

such that
z\in(\id+\sigma M)u
.
This means that the operator
P=(\id+\sigma M)^{-1}
is singleton,
P
is called the proximal mapping of
\sigma M
.
The fact that a solution of
0\in M(u)
is a fixed point of

leads to the algorithm of starting with a value
z^{0}\in X
and performing the iteration
z^{k+1}=P(z^{k})
, it is called the

, which under certain conditions generates a sequence weakly converging to a solution

of
0\in M(z^{\ast})
, if such a solution exists.

3.9 Lemma
.

Let
M
be a maximal monotone operator then

(\id+M)^{-1}+(\id+M^{-1})^{-1}=\id

Lemma
3.7
states that for
M=\partial f
the inverse is
(\partial f)^{-1}=\partial f^{\ast}
which combined with the previous lemma gives Moreau’s identity

(\id+\partial f)^{-1}+(\id+\partial f^{\ast})^{-1}=\id.

Let

and

be Hilbert spaces, consider the problem

\min_{u\in X}F(u)+G(Ku),

(1.21)

with

and

If the primal problem (

and there exists a

\min_{u\in X}F(u)+G(Ku)=\max_{w\in Y^{\ast}}-F^{\ast}(-K^{\ast}w)-G^{\ast}(w).

(1.22)

Where

\max_{w\in Y^{\ast}}-F^{\ast}(-K^{\ast}w)-G^{\ast}(w).

(1.23)

On the other hand let (

Solving the primal and dual equation simultaneously can be interpreted as finding saddle-points of the function

3.3
Total variation

The minimization problems discussed in this thesis will be of the form

\min_{u\in X}\Phi(u)+\lambda\Psi(u).

(1.25)

Where
\Phi
is the data fidelity term and
\Psi
is the regularization term.
One particularly successful penalty term
\Psi
is the total variation(TV) semi-norm.
It has been used by Rudin, Osher, Fatemi in the classical ROF model
[
ROF92
]
to remove noise from images, in that case
\Phi(u)=\|{u-u^{0}}\|_{2}^{2}
.
The main advantage of TV is that it penalizes the perimeter of the level sets of
u
thus penalizing many small jumps(noise) while allowing for sharp edges, therefore it is particularly well suited for the reconstruction of piecewise constant images.

Let
D
be a bounded Lipschitz domain subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For
u\in L^{1}_{loc}(D)
a measure
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}(D,\mathbb{R}^{d})
is called the
weak gradient
of
u
if for every
\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(D,\mathbb{R}^{d})

\int_{D}u\dive\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{D}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu.

If such a
\mu
exists call
\TV(u)=\|{\mu}\|_{\mathfrak{M}}=\|{\nabla u}\|_{\mathfrak{M}}
the total variation of
u
.
It then holds that

\TV(u)=\sup\Bigl\{{\int_{D}u\dive\varphi\,\;\mathrm{d}x}\ \Bigl|\ {\varphi\in\mathcal{D}(D,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\|{\varphi}\|_{\infty}\leq 1}\Bigr\}.

(1.26)

It can be shown that

\BV(D)=\bigl\{{u\in L^{1}(D)}\ \bigl|\ {\nabla u\in\mathfrak{M}(D,\mathbb{R}^{d})}\bigr\},

(1.27)

the space of functions of bounded variation, with the norm
\|{\cdot}\|_{BV}=\|{\cdot}\|_{1}+\TV(\cdot)
is a Banach space.

3.10 Lemma
.

Let
\varphi\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
be proper, convex, lower semi-continuous and increasing, then every function of the form

\Psi(u)=\begin{cases}\varphi(\TV(u)),&\text{if }u\in\BV(D)\\
\infty,&\text{else }\end{cases}

is proper, convex and lower semi-continuous on
L^{q}(D)
for
q\in[1,\infty)
.

3.11 Lemma
.

There exists a continuous embedding
\BV(D)\hookrightarrow L^{q}(D)
whenever
1\leq q\leq\frac{d}{d-1}
.
Furthermore a Poincaré-Wirtinger inequality holds, i. e. there exists a
c>0
such that

\|{u-\frac{1}{\left|{D}\right|}\int_{D}u\;\mathrm{d}x}\|_{q}\leq c\|{\nabla u}\|_{\mathfrak{M}}=c\TV(u).

(1.28)

3.12 Corollary
.

Let
X
be a reflexive Banach space and
\Phi\colon X\to\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
and
\Psi\colon X\to\mathbb{R}_{\infty}
be convex, weakly lower semi-continuous and bounded from below such that
F=\Phi+\lambda\Psi
is proper and radially unbounded then
\min_{u\in X}F(u)
has a minimizer.

Proof.

The direct method of Lemma
3.3
can be applied to
F
, which is convex as the sum of convex functions and lower semi-continuous as the sum of to functions with this property.

Tikhonov regularization

Consider an optimization problem of the form

\min_{u\in L^{q}(D)}\|{Au-u^{0}}\|_{Y}^{r}+\lambda\TV(u)

(1.29)

with
Y
a Banach space and
A\in\mathcal{L}(L^{q}(D),Y)
, i. e.
A
linear and bounded.
Then
\Phi\colon L^{q}(D)\to\mathbb{R}_{\infty},\Phi(u)=\|{Au-u^{0}}\|_{Y}^{r}
is proper, convex, weakly lower semi-continuous and bounded from below for any
r\in[1,\infty)
and finite for every
u
.
Let
\Psi\colon L^{q}(D)\to\mathbb{R}_{\infty},\Psi(u)=\TV(u)
in the sense of Lemma
3.10
, i. e.
\Psi(u)=\infty
if
u\notin\BV(D)
.
Then
F=\Phi+\lambda\Psi
is proper, convex, weakly lower semi-continuous and bounded from below on the reflexive Banach space
L^{q}(D)
.
In order to show the existence of a minimizer using Lemma
3.12
it still needs to be shown that the combined term is radially unbounded, this gives a restriction on
q
as well as a condition for the operator
A
.

3.13 Lemma
.

Let
1<q\leq\frac{d}{d-1}
and
A\in\mathcal{L}(L^{q}(D),Y)
be a linear mapping that does not map constant function to zero then with
\Psi(u)=\TV(u)
and
\Phi(u)=\|{Au-u^{0}}\|_{Y}^{r}
the combined term
F=\Phi+\lambda\Psi
is radially unbounded.
Under these assumption the problem
1.29
has as solution.

Proof.

For
u\in L^{q}(D)
set

Pu=\frac{1}{\left|{D}\right|}\int_{D}u\;\mathrm{d}x,

(1.30)

in the sense that
Pu
is the constant function then
u=u-Pu+Pu
.
Let
(u_{n})
be sequence with
\|{u_{n}}\|\to\infty
, since
\|{u_{n}}\|\leq\|{u_{n}-Pu_{n}}\|+\|{Pu_{n}}\|
either
\|{u_{n}-Pu_{n}}\|\to\infty
or
\|{u_{n}-Pu_{n}}\|
is bounded and
\|{Pu_{n}}\|\to\infty
must hold.
In the first case it follows from Lemma
3.11
that
\TV(u_{n})\to\infty
, and thus
F(u_{n})\to\infty
.
In the second case let
\mathds{1}
be the constant function with value
1
, then
Pu_{n}=c_{n}\mathds{1}
for a sequence of real values
(c_{n})
with
\left|{c_{n}}\right|\to\infty
.
Since it is assumed that
A
does not map constant functions to zero
A\mathds{1}\neq 0
it follows that
\|{APu_{n}}\|_{Y}=\|{c_{n}A\mathds{1}}\|_{Y}=\left|{c_{n}}\right|\|{A\mathds{1}}\|_{Y}\to\infty
and thus also
\|{APu_{n}-u^{0}}\|_{Y}\to\infty
.
Since
\|{Au_{n}-u^{0}}\|_{Y}\geq\|{APu_{n}-u^{0}}\|_{Y}-\|{A(u_{n}-Pu_{n})}\|_{Y}
and by assumption
\|{u_{n}-Pu_{n}}\|
is bounded it follows that
\|{Au_{n}-u^{0}}\|_{Y}\to\infty
which implies
\Phi(u_{n})\to\infty
.
Therefore

is radially unbounded in either case and the direct method of Lemma
3.3
can be applied to guarantee the existence of a minimizer of
1.29
.

Constrained Problem

Now a model for metal artifacts is devoloped and used to augment the regularization problem with inequality contraints.
If a ray passes through a very dense area, and is attenuated so strongly that the detector cannot differentiate its signal from noise, i. e. the received signal is unknown but lower than a certain threshold
\varepsilon

I(x_{D})<\varepsilon
.

I(x_{S})

(Rf)(\varphi,s)>-\log(\varepsilon)+\log(I(x_{S}))=\colon C

C

C

\Omega

7
Discretization

First the image domain is discretized as a regular grid of pixels.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
D_{h}=\{{(i,j)}\ |\ {1\leq i,j\leq n}\}
then for a given grid spacing
h>0
let the positions of the corresponding pixels be at

G_{h}=\Bigl\{{(x_{i},y_{j})}\ \Bigl|\ {x_{i}=h\left(i-\frac{n+1}{2}\right),y_{j}=h\left(j-\frac{m+1}{2}\right),(i,j)\in D_{h}}\Bigr\},

thus
G_{h}
is a regular grid centered around the origin.
Each pixel associated with
(i,j)\in D_{h}
is assigned a value in
\mathbb{R}
and thus the space of real valued functions on
D_{h}
is identified with
X=\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
This finite dimensional vector space is equipped with the scalar product

\langle{u},{v}\rangle_{X}=\sum_{i,j}u_{i,j}v_{i,j},\quad u,v\in X

(3.1)

where the sum is understood to be over all
(i,j)\in D_{h}
.
In order to discretize the gradient
\nabla
a finite difference scheme with spacing
h
is used, employing forward differences.
Neumann boundary condition are prescribed at the boundary of the image, due to the forward difference scheme this is only visible at the right and bottom boundary.
This results in the operator
\nabla_{h}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times 2}
,
u\mapsto(\partial_{x}u,\partial_{y}u)
with

\partial_{x}u=\begin{cases}\frac{u_{i+1,j}-u_{i,j}}{h}&\text{if }i<n\\
0&\text{if }i=n\end{cases}\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{y}u=\begin{cases}\frac{u_{i,j+1}-u_{i,j}}{h}&\text{if }j<m\\
0&\text{if }j=n.\end{cases}

The vector space
V=\mathbb{R}^{n\times n\times 2}
is equipped with the scalar product

\langle{p},{q}\rangle_{V}=\sum_{i,j}p_{i,j,1}q_{i,j,1}+\sum_{i,j}p_{i,j,2}q_{i,j,2},

(3.2)

which induces the norm
\|{p}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\langle{p},{p}\rangle_{V}}
.
The adjoint of
\nabla_{h}
is then a discretization of the negative divergence with backward differences and Dirichlet boundary conditions.
Specifically the divergence is the operator
\dive_{h}\colon V\to X

(1-\alpha(i,j,l))&\text{if }k=k(i,j,l)+1\\

Then the discrete Radon transform is an operator

To summarize the involved spaces and operators let an image

be mapped by

Putting these things together gives the discretized version of the unconstrained problem:

8.1 Lemma
.

The minimization problem

\min_{u\in X}\quad\frac{1}{2}\|{R_{h}u-u^{0}}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|{\nabla_{h}u}\|_{1}.

(
P_{h}
)

has a solution.

Proof.

The problem is of the form
\min_{u\in X}\Phi(u)+\lambda\Psi(u)
where both
\Phi(u)=\frac{1}{2}\|{R_{h}u-u^{0}}\|_{2}^{2}
and
\Psi(u)=\|{\nabla_{h}u}\|_{1}
are continuous and convex.
As noted the operator
R_{h}
does not map constant vectors to zero.
In this discrete setting a Poincaré type inequality holds
\|{u-\bar{u}}\|_{2}\leq C\|{\nabla_{h}u}\|_{1}
, for some
C>0
, where
\bar{u}=\frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i,j}u_{i,j}
is the mean value of

.
In order to see this define the function
f(u)=\frac{\|{u-\bar{u}}\|_{2}}{\|{\nabla_{h}u}\|_{1}}
, for
\|{\nabla_{h}u}\|_{1}\neq 0
and show that

is bounded.
Since scaling
u
does not change the value of

only consider
u\in S_{1}=\{{u\in X}\ |\ {\|{u}\|_{2}=1}\}
and also since
\nabla_{h}u=0
if and only if
u=\bar{u}
if and only if
Qu=u-\bar{u}=0
where

is a projection it is sufficient to find a bound for

on the subspace
W=\range(Q)\cap S_{1}
.
Since
W
is compact then
\inf_{W}\|{\nabla_{h}u}\|_{1}=c>0
thus
f
is bounded on
W
.
With this the same argument as in the continuous case in Lemma
3.13
can be made to show that the combined term in radially unbounded and thus a minimizer exists by the direct method.
Or indeed as the functional in the finite-dimensional case is continuous radially unboundedness gives the existence of a minimizer directly.
∎

Because
\|{\cdot}\|_{2}
is strictly convex the minimizer is unique if

is injective, which in turn depends on the dimensions of the involved spaces.

Now the constrained problem is discussed.
Let

To compare the projection data to the given data

Then

(

\displaystyle(\fzdh(\kappa_{\Delta}))_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l=0}^{n-1}x_{k,l}e^{-\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{n}(ik+jl)}

\displaystyle=4+x_{1,0}e^{-\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{n}i}+x_{n-1,0}e^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{n}i}x_{0,1}e^{-\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{n}j}+x_{0,n-1}e^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{n}j}

\displaystyle=-(e^{-\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{n}i}-2+e^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{n}i})+-(e^{-\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{n}j}-2+e^{\frac{2\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{n}j})

\displaystyle=-(e^{-\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{n}i}+e^{\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{n}i})^{2}+-(e^{-\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{n}j}+e^{\frac{\pi\mathrm{i}\mkern 1.0mu}{n}j})^{2}

\displaystyle=4\sin\left(\frac{\pi i}{n}\right)^{2}+4\sin\left(\frac{\pi i}{n}\right)^{2}

which is always non-negative.
Thus the denominator is not
0
, since
(\fzdh(\kappa_{\Delta}))_{i,j}=4\sin(i\pi/n)^{2}+4\sin(j\pi/n)^{2}=0
if and only if

this means that it needs to be ensured that

which equals
\sum_{i,j}\kappa_{i,j}
is not
0
.
This approach is used in
[
BS15a
]
for the deblurring case, where the operator which is blurring the image is indeed the convolution with a kernel.
It is not the case that

14.1
Inverse norm preconditioning

In Lemma
2.3
it was shown that
R^{\ast}Ru=\frac{1}{\|{\cdot}\|}\ast u
which by the convolution theorem implies that
\four(R^{\ast}Ru)=2\pi\four(\frac{1}{\|{\cdot}\|})\four(u)
, where
\four
denotes the 2-d Fourier transform.
In addition to that from the theory of the Radon transform it is known that

\frac{r}{2\pi}\four(R^{\ast}Ru)(r\omega)=\four(u)(r\omega)

(4.18)

see
[
Eps08
]
*p 142 where a different scaling of the Fourier transform and the Radon transform for angles
\varphi\in[0,\pi]
are used.
Both combined imply that

\four\left(\frac{1}{\|{x}\|}\right)=\frac{1}{\|{x}\|}.

(4.19)

This is also known from a connection of the Fourier transform of radially symmetric functions and the Hankel transform see
[
Pou10
, sec 9.3]
.
This means that solving
R^{\ast}Ru=y
can be achived by deconvolution with a kernel whose Fourier transform is known.

This idea is now transferred to the discrete setting, where it is generally not true that
R_{h}^{\ast}R_{h}
can be described by a discrete convolution but the idea from the continuous setting is used to efficiently find an approximation of the solution of the linear system
R_{h}^{\ast}R_{h}u=y
for given
y
.
Let
\|{x}\|_{\varepsilon}=\sqrt{\|{x}\|_{2}^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}
for some small
\varepsilon>0
be set as an approximate of norm of
x
this is done in order to guarantee that the reciprocal can be calculated at every point.
Let
\fzdh
be the 2-dimensional discrete Fourier transform and
\fzdh^{-1}
its inverse then then by the convolution theorem
\fzdh(u\ast\kappa)=\fzdh(u)\fzdh(\kappa)

Therefore the above idea to use deconvolution with a kernel whose Fourier transform equals
\frac{1}{\|{x}\|_{\varepsilon}}
leads to a preconditioner of the form
\tilde{M}u=\fzdh^{-1}(\frac{1}{\|x\|}\fzdh(u))
.
In order to guarantee the feasibility of the preconditioner set
M_{1}=c\tilde{M}
and choose
c>0
great enough to ensure that
M_{1}-R_{h}^{\ast}R_{h}\geq 0
.
In practice this can be achieved by performing power iteration on
\tilde{M}^{-1}R_{h}^{\ast}R_{h}
and then choosing
c
greater that the largest eigenvalue will ensure that
c\geq\tilde{M}^{-1}R_{h}^{\ast}R_{h}
, which gives
c\tilde{M}-R_{h}^{\ast}R_{h}\geq 0
.
Putting this together with the periodic Laplacian gives

\displaystyle Mu=c\alpha\fzdh^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\|{x}\|_{\varepsilon}}\fzdh(u)\right)+\beta\Delta_{p}u=y

(4.20)

\displaystyle\alpha c\frac{1}{\|{x}\|_{\varepsilon}}\fzdh(u)+\beta\fzdh(\kappa_{\Delta})\fzdh(u)=\fzdh(y)

(4.21)

\displaystyle\fzdh(u)=\fzdh(y)\cdot\left(\alpha c\frac{1}{\|{x}\|_{\varepsilon}}+\beta\fzdh(\kappa_{\Delta})\right)^{-1}

(4.22)

\displaystyle u=M^{-1}y=\fzdh^{-1}\left(\fzdh(y)\frac{\|{x}\|_{\varepsilon}}{\alpha c+\beta\fzdh(\kappa_{\Delta})\|{x}\|_{\varepsilon}}\right).

(4.23)

A ready-to-use algorithm for solving the discrete problem (

Note that in the algorithm the calculation of

is formulated such that only one application of

and

is used per iteration.

Impulse response preconditioners

Another idea for a preconditioner is to apply

to a discrete point function
\delta
.
Again if
R_{h}^{\ast}R_{h}
were a convolution with some kernel this impulse response

In the case where the image dimension

The obtained kernel

, in order to guarantee that the resulting preconditioner is symmetric.

K=\alpha k+\beta\kappa_{\Delta}
.
This gives the preconditioner

M^{-1}u=\fzdh^{-1}\left(\frac{\fzdh(u)}{\fzdh(K)}\right).

(4.24)

Again a constant

must be chosen such that

, again in practice this can be achieved with power iteration.

Another idea similar to the previous was used in

Again

Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers

In this section the method proposed in
[
RF12
]
of using the Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers(
admm
) is discussed and implemented for the problems in question.
Consider again the unconstrained problem:

\min_{u\in X}\quad\frac{1}{2}\|{R_{h}u-u^{0}}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|{\nabla_{h}u}\|_{1}.

(
P_{h}
)

This can be rewritten as a constrained problem by introducing the additional variables
v
and
w
:

\min_{u,v,w}\,\frac{1}{2}\|{w-u^{0}}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|{v}\|_{1}\\
,\,\text{s.t. }w=R_{h}u,\,v=\nabla_{h}u.

(4.41)

Since both of the constraints are linear this can be more compactly written with a single constraint matrix:

\min_{u,z}\,f(z)\text{ s.t. }z=Ku

(4.42)

with
z=\begin{bmatrix}w,v\end{bmatrix}^{T}
and

K=\begin{bmatrix}R_{h}\\
\nabla_{h}\end{bmatrix}
as well as
f(z)=f(\begin{bmatrix}w,v\end{bmatrix}^{T})=\frac{1}{2}\|{w-u^{0}}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|{v}\|_{1}
.

The associated augmented Lagrangian with Lagrange multiplier
\gamma
and parameter
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
is

\mathcal{L}(u,z,\gamma)=f(z)+\gamma^{T}(z-Ku)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|{z-Ku}\|_{\Lambda}^{2}

where
\Lambda
is a symmetric weighting matrix and
\|{x}\|_{\Lambda}=\|{\Lambda x}\|
.
Indeed if
\lambda
is chosen as

\Lambda=\begin{bmatrix}I_{1}&0\\
0&\tau I_{2}.\end{bmatrix}

where with
I_{1}
the identity on
\mathds{R}^{N\times M}
and
I_{2}
the identity on
\mathds{R}^{n\times n\times 2}
, this results in the same scaling of the gradient with a factor
\tau
as in the PDRQ and Primal-Dual algorithms, specifically
\|{[w,v]^{T}}\|_{\Lambda}=\|{w}\|_{2}+\tau\|{v}\|_{2}
.
Putting the terms containing
z-Ku
together and completing the square gives a term
c_{\eta}
that does not depend on
u
or
z

\mathcal{L}(u,z,\eta)=f(z)+\frac{\mu}{2}\|{z-Ku-\eta}\|_{\Lambda}^{2}+c_{\eta}

(4.43)

with
\eta=-\frac{1}{\mu}\Lambda^{-1}\gamma

and
c_{\eta}=-\frac{\mu}{2}\|{\eta}\|_{\Lambda}^{2}
independent of
u
and
z
.
The alternating direction method consists of minimizing in each component separately

\displaystyle u^{(j)}_{\ast}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{u}\mathcal{L}(u,z^{(j)},\eta^{(j)})

(4.44)

\displaystyle z^{(j)}_{\ast}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{z}\mathcal{L}(u^{(j+1)},z,\eta^{(j)})

(4.45)

and then updating
\eta
according to

\eta^{(j+1)}=\eta^{(j)}-\mu(z^{(j+1)}-Ku^{(j+1)}).

In
[
RF12
]
it is shown that if (
4.44
) and (
4.45
) are solved approximately, that is choosing

20
Conclusion

The model for artefact reduction by introducing inequality constraints on the sinogram was presented and significantly sped up by preconditioning.
Novel preconditioners were presented as well as existing ones.
The ability of the proposed technique to reduce metal artefacts in real data was demonstrated in a prototype setting.
Quality of the reconstructions surely could be improved by incorporating more technical knowledge of the raw data and its acquisition into the data processing.
Application to experimental data dedicated to testing for metal artifact reduction such as a real data of a phantom once with and once without metal inclusions under known calibration and with no out of view data could better determine the merit of the proposed method.
Obvious extensions of the model would be to use more general regularization such as the total generalized variation.
Both sources of a dual energy CT scanner could be used by comparing against data from both in the discrepancy term, although this would not make use of their different energy spectra.
Further modeling of the cause of artefacts might need to be integrated in the model.
It can be hoped that the results of this thesis can be improved upon to form viable technique for reducing metal artefacts or be integrated with existing models.

A point source and opposing detector – either flat or a spherical segment – rotate around the object to be scanned in a plane while moving along it, in what is called the z-direction, thus the source and detector follow a spiral trajectory.
A simple method of how real data from such a device can be preprocessed to be reconstructed into cross sections with 2D Radon transform is presented in

Appendix 6.A
Fan beam to parallel Beam(Fan2Para)

In Section
2
the Radon transform was presented as a model for tomography where with each exposure parallel beams are emitted in practice however a fan beam geometry is used where a point source emits beams.
In order to convert data obtained by fan beam geometry to parallel beam a re-sampling operation is performed:
Data at desired coordinates in parallel beam geometry is obtained by converting to equivalent fan beam coordinates and interpolating using the given data in fan beam geometry.
This process is now described in detail, refer to Figure
11
for an overview.
Considering the fan beam configuration of a CT machine where a point source
A
rotates around the origin of which it has a fixed distance
d
.
The position of the source
A
is measured by the angle
\varphi
between the line
L
from the origin to
A
and the horizontal x-Axis.
Then each beam emitted from
A
will be described by the angle
\alpha
between it and the line
L
.
As an example detector elements linearly spaced on a round detector, in the form of an arc of a circle, would correspond to beams whose angles
\alpha
are linearly spaced, this is the case in most CT machines.
As in the parallel geometry where the offset
s
was positive to the left of the center beam, i. e. in the direction of
x_{0}
, and negative to the right here too
\alpha
has a sign e. g. in Figure
11
the angle
\alpha
is counted as positive as it is to the left of the center line
L
.
For a given beam
B
in fan beam geometry with coordinates
(\alpha,\varphi)
find the coordinates
(s,\theta)
that this beam has in a parallel beam configuration.
From Figure
11
one can see that
\theta=\varphi-(\pi/2-\alpha)
as well as
s=d\sin(\alpha)
.
Thus the function that maps a beam from a fan beam configuration to a corresponding beam in a parallel configuration is given by
f\colon(\alpha,\varphi)\mapsto(s,\theta)=(d\sin(\alpha),\varphi-\pi/2+\alpha)
, this coordinate transform has the inverse given by
f^{-1}\colon(s,\theta)\mapsto(\alpha,\varphi)=(\arcsin(\frac{s}{d}),\theta+\pi/2-\arcsin(\frac{s}{d}))
.
To get an approximate value at a query point
P
with desired coordinates
(s,\theta)
in parallel beam space, the coordinates of
P
are transformed to fan beam coordinates
f^{-1}(s,\theta)
then using e. g. bilinear interpolation at this coordinates with the values of the collected data at positions
(\alpha,\varphi)
then gives an approximate value for data at
P
.
This method also known as rebinning is used in the MATLAB function
fan2para
, but has also been implemented for the project of this thesis to allow for more flexibility.

Appendix 6.B
Simple data extraction

Modern CT machines do not simply work in one plane but collect data for multiple planes simultaneously this is achieved by a cone beam configuration consisting of a point source emitting rays in a cone and an opposing detector whose sensors are arranged in a 2-dimensional array.
One popular configuration, that is used in the data for this project, is that of a cylindrical detector, in which each row of the detector consists of equiangular arranged detectors and the rows are a fixed distance apart.
Thus from the source each row gives a fan beam their distance is usually not measured at the detector but at the center of rotation where this equal distance is called the
slice width

w
as every fan beam represents a slice of the object to be scanned.
The data from every exposure is an array of values, each row having the data from one fan beam as described above, let this array be called a
frame
, as interpreting each frame as an image and playing them gives a video as if the source where a camera rotating around the object to be scanned in a spiral path.

A very simple way of extracting the data associated with a z-position is to – incorrectly – assume that the data for each frame consists of slices corresponding to
parallel
fan beams that are slice width apart and orthogonal to the z-axis.
This means it is assumed that there is a stack of parallel fan beams rather then the fan beams coming from a common source.
If the detector is in position
z
then row number
i
is in z-position
z+i\cdot w
, to get data associated with a
z
-position
z_{0}
all rows whose z-position is within
z_{0}\pm 0.5\cdot w
are collected, thus giving data associated with z-position
z_{0}
as if collected by a single row fan beam detector.
Then the data collected in this fashion can be converted to parallel beam data using the rebinning algorithm described above.
Note that this assumption is not true as the data is from a cone beam.
This very simple approach was also used in the data extraction in
[
BF11
]
as is explained in the appendix of the paper.

Appendix 7.A

Each frame uses the full range of the data type

Appendix 7.C
GPU Radon implementation

For a GPU-parallelization each thread is assigned one pixel of the image and calculates the value of the discrete gradient and divergence after reading in values at neighboring pixels.
In a similar fashion the linear back projection will only perform read operation on the sinogram data and thus has no write conflicts.
For the parallelization of the Radon transform again each thread is assigned one pixel and calculates its projection in the sinogram.
To resolve the resulting write conflict, different thread are assigned different angles to ensure that only one thread writes to any one column of the sinogram at a given time.
Cycling through the angles until every thread performed its operation on every angle - where threads pause if not enough angles are given - completes the operation.
Additionally each thread block is assigned its own sinogram to write to, and in a latter step summing these up to give the final result.
