Variance-Reduced Fast Krasnosel’skiǐ-Mann Methods for Finite-Sum Root-Finding Problems
Quoc Tran-Dinh

Abstract
We develop a new class of fast Krasnosel’skiǐ-Mann methods with variance reduction to solve a finite-sum cocoercive equation G ​ x = 0 Gx=0 . Our algorithm is single-loop and leverages a new family of unbiased variance-reduced estimators specifically designed for a wider class of root-finding algorithms. Our method achieves both 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / k 2 ) \mathcal{O}(1/k^{2}) and o ⁡ ( 1 / k 2 ) o(1/k^{2}) last-iterate convergence rates in terms of 𝔼 ⁡ [ ‖ G ​ x k ‖ 2 ] \mathbb{E}[\|{Gx^{k}}\|^{2}] , where k k is the iteration counter and 𝔼 ⁡ [ ⋅ ] \mathbb{E}[\cdot] is the total expectation. We also establish almost-sure o ⁡ ( 1 / k 2 ) o(1/k^{2}) convergence rates and the almost-sure convergence of iterates { x k } \{x^{k}\} to a solution of G ​ x = 0 Gx=0 . We instantiate our framework for two prominent estimators: SVRG and SAGA. By an appropriate choice of parameters, both variants attain an oracle complexity of 𝒪 ⁡ ( n + n 2 / 3 ​ ϵ − 1 ) \mathcal{O}\big({n+n^{2/3}\epsilon^{-1}}\big) to reach an ϵ \epsilon -solution, where n n denotes the number of summands in the finite-sum operator G G . Furthermore, under σ \sigma -strong quasi-monotonicity, our method achieves a linear convergence rate and an oracle complexity of 𝒪 ⁡ ( n + max ⁡ { n , n 2 / 3 ​ κ } ​ log ⁡ ( 1 ϵ ) ) \mathcal{O}\big({n+\max\{n,n^{2/3}\kappa\}\log(\frac{1}{\epsilon})}\big) , where κ := L / σ \kappa:=L/\sigma . We extend our approach to solve a class of finite-sum inclusions (possibly nonmonotone), demonstrating that our schemes retain the same theoretical guarantees as in the equation setting. Finally, numerical experiments demonstrate that our methods exhibit promising performance compared with state-of-the-art methods.

Introduction.

This paper develops and analyzes a new class of
stochastic fast
Krasnosel’skiǐ–Mann (KM) methods with
variance reduction
for approximating solutions of finite-sum “cocoercive” equations, as well as their application to finite-sum composite inclusions.

1.1
Problem statement.

Our first goal in this paper is to solve the following finite-sum “cocoercive” equation using novel stochastic variance reduction methods:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\textrm{Find $x^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$ such that:}~Gx^{\star}=0,\quad\textrm{where}\quad Gx:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}x.\end{array}\vskip-1.07639pt

(CE)

Here,
G_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
are single-valued mappings for all
i\in[n]:=\{1,\cdots,n\}
and
n\geq 1
.
Following the existing literature, such as
[
30
]
, we call (
CE
) a
root-finding problem
.

Equation (
CE
) can be rewritten equivalently as the following
fixed-point problem
:

\textrm{Find $x^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$ such that:}~x^{\star}=Fx^{\star},\vskip-1.07639pt

(FP)

where
Fx:=x-\lambda Gx
for any given
\lambda>0
.
Note that when
\lambda=\frac{2}{L}
,
G
in (
CE
) is
\frac{1}{L}
-cocoercive (see Assumption
1.1
below) if and only if
F
is nonexpansive, i.e.,
\|Fx-Fy\|\leq\|x-y\|
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

[
10
, Proposition 4.11]
.
Since the two formulations are equivalent, methods for solving (
FP
) can be applied to solve (
CE
) and vice versa. In this work, we focus on
the root-finding problem (
CE
)
.

Throughout this paper, we make the following assumption on (
CE
).

{assumption}

\mathrm{(a)}

Equation (
CE
) has a solution, i.e.,
\mathrm{zer}(G):=\{x^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:Gx^{\star}=0\}\neq\emptyset
.

\mathrm{(b)}

The mappings
G_{i}
,
i\in[n]
, satisfy the following “cocoercivity-type” condition:
There exists
L\in(0,+\infty)
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\langle G_{i}x-G_{i}y,x-y\rangle&\geq&\frac{1}{L}\big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|G_{i}x-G_{i}y\|^{2}\big],\end{array}\vskip-1.07639pt

(1)

Note that (
1
) implies the
\frac{1}{L}
-cocoercivity of
G
, i.e.,
\langle Gx-Gy,x-y\rangle\geq\frac{1}{L}\|Gx-Gy\|^{2}
.
Conversely, if
G
is
\frac{1}{L}
-cocoercive, then it may not satisfy (
1
).
However, if every
G_{i}
is
\frac{1}{L}
-cocoercive for
i\in[n]
, then
G
satisfies (
1
).
Thus (
1
) is stronger than the
\frac{1}{L}
-cocoercivity of
G
but weaker than the
\frac{1}{L}
-cocoercivity of all the summands
G_{i}
.

Assumption
1.1
(b) is not new.
It can be viewed as a generalization of the
\frac{1}{L}
-average cocoercive property in finite-sum convex optimization when
G_{i}=\nabla{f_{i}}
, which is equivalent to the
L
-average smoothness:
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\nabla{f}_{i}(x)-\nabla{f}_{i}(y)\|^{2}\leq L^{2}\|x-y\|^{2}
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
This average smoothness condition has been widely used in optimization and is slightly different from the expected smoothness condition considered in
[
40
,
58
]
.
If we fix
y:=x^{\star}\in\mathrm{zer}(G)
, then (
1
) reduces to a special case of
[
58
, Assumption 3.3]
for the finite-sum setting.

1.2
Proposed method.

The classical Krasnosel’skiǐ-Mann (KM) fixed-point iteration was independently developed by M. A. Krasnosel’skiǐ
[
54
]
and W. R. Mann
[
61
]
to approximately solve (
FP
) when
F
is nonexpansive.
More specifically, it can be expressed as follows:

Starting from an initial point
x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, at each iteration
k\geq 0
, it updates

x^{k+1}:=(1-\alpha_{k})x^{k}+\alpha_{k}Fx^{k},\vskip-1.07639pt

(KM)

where
\alpha_{k}\in(0,1)
is a given averaging parameter
(
e.g.,
\alpha=\frac{1}{2}
)
satisfying appropriate conditions.

If we substitute
F=\mathbb{I}-\frac{2}{L}G
into (
KM
) to solve (
CE
), we obtain
x^{k+1}=x^{k}-\frac{2\alpha_{k}}{L}Gx^{k}
, which can be viewed as a forward/gradient-type scheme, see, e.g.,
[
34
]
.
The best convergence rate for the squared residual norm
\|x^{k}-Fx^{k}\|^{2}
in (
KM
) (or, equivalently,
\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}
in the forward scheme) is
\mathcal{O}\big(1/k\big)
, see, e.g.,
[
26
]
.
This rate is suboptimal and significantly slower than the
\mathcal{O}\big(1/k^{2}\big)
rate, which was proven to be optimal in
[
67
]
for first-order methods to solve (
CE
) under Assumption
1.1
.

To boost convergence rates of (
KM
), one can either apply Halpern’s fixed-point iteration, as in
[
32
,
41
,
57
]
, or Nesterov’s acceleration, as in
[
8
,
15
,
77
]
.
The authors in
[
15
]
proposed discretizing a second-order ordinary differential equation (ODE) associated with
F
, leading to the following scheme:
Starting from
x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, at each iteration
k\geq 0
, it updates

x^{k+1}:=(1-\tfrac{s\tau_{k}}{2})x^{k}+(1-s)\tau_{k}(x^{k}-x^{k-1})+\tfrac{s\tau_{k}}{2}Fx^{k}+s\tau_{k}(Fx^{k}-Fx^{k-1}),\vskip-1.07639pt

(FKM)

where
s\in(0,1]
and
\tau_{k}:=\frac{r}{k+r}
with
r>2
are given parameters.

This scheme is called the
fast KM iteration.
Note that (
FKM
) is equivalent to Nesterov’s accelerated algorithm in
[
77
]
applied to
F=\mathbb{I}-\frac{2}{L}G
. Both
[
77
]
and
[
15
]
proved faster
\mathcal{O}\big(1/k^{2}\big)
and
o\big(1/k^{2}\big)
convergence rates for deterministic fast KM methods compared to the non-accelerated scheme (
KM
).

Our method in this paper builds on deterministic Nesterov’s accelerated KM methods from our recent work
[
77
]
for the cocoercive equation (
CE
) and (
FKM
) from
[
15
]
for the fixed-point problem (
FP
).
More specifically, the method in
[
77
, Theorem 2]
can be written in the following form after eliminating the iterate
y^{k}
:

Given
x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, set
x^{-1}:=x^{0}
, and at each iteration
k\geq 0
, it updates

x^{k+1}:=x^{k}+\theta_{k}(x^{k}-x^{k-1})-\eta_{k}S^{k},\quad\textrm{where}\quad S^{k}:=Gx^{k}-\gamma_{k}Gx^{k-1},\vskip-1.07639pt

(2)

where
\theta_{k}\in(0,1)
is an inertial or a momentum parameter,
\eta_{k}>0
is a given stepsize, and
\gamma_{k}\in(0,1)
is a given correction parameter.

Since we aim to solve large finite-sum instances of (
CE
) (i.e., when
n\gg 1
), for which evaluating
S^{k}
exactly is often costly or even infeasible, we approximate
S^{k}
using a stochastic unbiased estimator
\widetilde{S}^{k}
with variance reduction.

Our method.

Having
\widetilde{S}^{k}
, we propose the following method to solve (
CE
):

Given
x^{0}\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
, we set
x^{-1}:=x^{0}
and at each iteration
k\geq 0
, we construct an unbiased variance-reduced estimator
\widetilde{S}^{k}
of
S^{k}:=Gx^{k}-\gamma_{k}Gx^{k-1}
and then update

x^{k+1}:=x^{k}+\theta_{k}(x^{k}-x^{k-1})-\eta_{k}\widetilde{S}^{k},\vskip-1.07639pt

(VFKM)

where
\theta_{k}\in(0,1)
,
\eta_{k}>0
, and
\gamma_{k}\in(0,1)
are given, determined later, and
\widetilde{S}^{0}:=S^{0}
.

Our method (
VFKM
) uses three parameters
\theta_{k}
,
\eta_{k}
, and
\gamma_{k}
.
However, we do not explicitly specify their forms here or the construction of
\widetilde{S}^{k}
to avoid repetition.
Section
2
presents the construction of
\widetilde{S}^{k}
.
Sections
3
and
4
provide explicit update rules for
\theta_{k}
,
\eta_{k}
, and
\gamma_{k}
, and then analyze the convergence of (
VFKM
) for two different cases, respectively: without
strong quasi-monotonicity
and with
strong quasi-monotonicity
.

1.3
Applications to finite-sum inclusions.

Our second goal in this paper is to extend our method (
VFKM
) to solve the following finite-sum inclusion:

\textrm{Find $u^{\star}\in\mathrm{dom}(\Psi)$ such that:}~0\in\Psi{u^{\star}}:=Gu^{\star}+Tu^{\star},\vskip-1.07639pt

(NI)

where
Gu:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}G_{i}u
as in (
CE
) and each
G_{i}
is
\frac{1}{L_{g}}
-cocoercive for
i\in[n]
, and
T:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightrightarrows 2^{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
is a maximally
\nu
-co-hypomonotone operator, i.e., there exists
\nu\geq 0
such that
\langle x-y,u-v\rangle\geq-\nu\|x-y\|^{2}
for all
(u,x),(v,y)\in\mathrm{gra}(T)
, where
2^{\mathbb{R}^{p}}
is the set of all subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
\mathrm{gra}(T)
is the graph of
T
(see Section
5
for more details).
Note that the operator defining (
NI
) is not necessarily monotone as shown in Section
5
.

Special cases of (
NI
).

If
T
is monotone, then problem (
NI
) becomes a classical monotone inclusion, which has been widely studied in the literature; see, e.g.,
[
10
,
73
]
.
This formulation (
NI
) also covers the optimality conditions of finite-sum composite minimization and minimax optimization, as well as [mixed] variational inequality problems (VIPs), including Nash equilibrium models; see, e.g.,
[
34
]
.
We provide three examples below to illustrate the generality of (
NI
).

Example 1.
Consider the following finite-sum composite minimization problem, which commonly arises in machine learning applications such as linear and logistic regression (see, e.g.,
[
35
]
):

\begin{array}[]{l}{\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}}\big\{\phi(u):=f(u)+g(u)\equiv\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(u)+g(u)\big\},\end{array}\vskip-1.07639pt

(3)

where
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex and differentiable and
g
is proper, closed, and possibly nonconvex.
The optimality condition of (
3
) is
0\in\nabla{f}(u^{\star})+\partial{g}(u^{\star})
, which is a special case of (
NI
) with
G_{i}u=\nabla{f_{i}}(u)
and
Tu=\partial{g}(u)
, the “abstract” subdifferential of
g
(see
[
72
]
).

Example 2.

We consider the following finite-sum minimax optimization model, which often arises in robust and distributionally robust optimization, two-person zero-sum games, adversarial training, and generative adversarial networks; see, e.g.,
[
37
,
55
,
59
,
80
]
:

\begin{array}[]{l}{\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}}\max_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}}}\big\{\mathcal{L}(x,y):=f(x)+\Phi(x,y)-g(y)\equiv f(x)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Phi_{i}(x,y)-g(y)\big\},\end{array}\vskip-1.07639pt

(4)

where
f
and
g
are proper, closed, and possibly nonconvex functions, and
\Phi
is convex-concave and differentiable.
Let
u:=[x,y]
be a concatenation of
x
and
y
.
Then, the optimality condition of (
4
) is a special case of (
NI
) with
G_{i}u:=[\nabla_{x}\Phi_{i}(x,y),-\nabla_{y}{\Phi_{i}}(x,y)]
and
Tu:=[\partial{f}(x),\partial{g}(y)]
.

Example 3.

We consider the following mixed variational inequality problem (MVIP), which has a number of applications in mechanics, traffic networks, and Nash equilibria; see, e.g.,
[
34
]
:

\text{Find $u^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$ such that:}~\langle Gu^{\star},u-u^{\star}\rangle+g(u)-g(u^{\star})\geq 0,\vskip-1.07639pt

(5)

where
G
is given in (
NI
) and
g
is a proper, closed, and convex function.
In particular, if
g=\delta_{\mathcal{X}}
, the indicator of a convex set
\mathcal{X}
, then (
5
) reduces to the classical Stampacchia variational inequality (VIP), see
[
34
]
.
Clearly, (
5
) is a special case of (
NI
) with
Tu=\partial{g}(u)
.

Our approach for (
NI
).

Our approach to solve (
NI
) consists of the following two steps:

•

Step 1:
We reformulate (
NI
) as (
CE
) via a backward-forward splitting (BFS) equation, similar to
[
30
]
.
However, since
T
is co-hypomonotone, we need to establish the relationship between (
NI
) and this equation and verify Condition (
1
).

•

Step 2:
We apply (
VFKM
) to find an approximate solution of the BFS equation and then recover the corresponding approximate solution of (
NI
).

1.4
Our contribution.

Our main theoretical contributions, together with the proposed method (VFKM), are as follows:

(a)

In Section
2
, we introduce a new class of unbiased variance-reduced estimators
\widetilde{S}^{k}
for
S^{k}
in (
VFKM
), as defined in Definition
2.1
.
Within this class, we construct two specific estimators: one based on the loopless SVRG method from
[
45
,
53
]
and the other leveraging the SAGA estimator from
[
31
]
.
However, any estimator satisfying Definition
2.1
can be used in our method (
VFKM
).

(b)

In Section
3
, we establish that our method (
VFKM
) achieves an
\mathcal{O}\big(1/k^{2}\big)
last-iterate convergence rate for
\mathbb{E}\big[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}\big]
and
\mathbb{E}\big[\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}\big]
under Assumption
1.1
, where
k
is the iteration counter.
We further prove
o\big(1/k^{2}\big)
convergence rates for these metrics.
Additionally, we establish an almost-sure
o\left(1/k^{2}\right)
convergence rate for
\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}
and
\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}
, along with the almost-sure convergence of the iterate sequence
\{x^{k}\}
to a solution of (
CE
).

(c)

In Subsection
3.4
, we present two variants of (
VFKM
) using our SVRG and SAGA estimators.
We show that both variants require
\mathcal{O}(n+n^{2/3}\epsilon^{-1})
evaluations of
G_{i}
to achieve
\mathbb{E}\big[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}\big]\leq\epsilon^{2}
for a given tolerance
\epsilon>0
.
This yields an
n^{1/3}
factor improvement over deterministic accelerated methods and a
1/\epsilon
factor improvement over non-accelerated stochastic methods.

(d)

In Section
4
, we establish a linear convergence rate for (
VFKM
) under an additional
\sigma
-strong quasi-monotonicity on (
CE
).
When using our SVRG and SAGA estimators, the method attains an oracle complexity of
\mathcal{O}\big(n+\max\{n,n^{2/3}\kappa\}\log(\epsilon^{-1})\big)
, improving over deterministic counterparts by a factor of
n^{1/3}
whenever
\kappa\geq\mathcal{O}\big(n^{1/3}\big)
, where
\kappa:=\frac{L}{\sigma}
.

(e)

We apply our method (
VFKM
) to solve a class of finite-sum inclusions (
NI
), which may be nonmonotone, and derive a new variant with the same theoretical convergence rates and oracle complexity bounds as in Section
3
.

Comparison with prior works.

We now briefly compare our contributions with existing works to highlight key differences.

•

First, our unbiased variance-reduced estimator
\widetilde{S}^{k}
, including SVRG and SAGA, is specifically designed for
S^{k}
, not for
Gx^{k}
.
This differs from existing works such as
[
2
,
3
,
12
,
14
,
30
,
33
,
39
,
49
]
.
Note that
[
2
,
3
,
14
,
30
]
only consider specific SVRG and/or SAGA estimators to directly approximate
Gx^{k}
, while
[
12
,
33
,
39
,
49
]
cover a wide class of estimators, possibly including SVRG and SAGA as special instances.
In Subsection
2.1
below, we will further compare our class of estimators with these related works.

•

Second, our method (
VFKM
) is single-loop and uses simple operations, making it easier to implement than double-loop or catalyst-based algorithms in
[
48
,
81
]
.

•

Third, our convergence rates and oracle complexity estimates rely on the metric
\mathbb{E}\big[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}\big]
or
\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}
, which can be viewed as a type of Fukushima’s regularized gap function
[
36
]
for (
CE
).
This metric differs from recent results, e.g.,
[
2
,
3
]
, that use a Nesterov’s gap or restricted gap function from
[
64
]
.
Note that for (
CE
), the standard Auslender gap function is degenerate.

•

Fourth, our method achieves an
\mathcal{O}\left(1/k\right)
-factor improvement over non-accelerated methods in
[
2
,
3
,
30
]
.
Additionally, we also establish almost-sure
o\big(1/k^{2}\big)
convergence rates and show that the iterate sequence
\{x^{k}\}
converges almost surely to a solution of (
CE
).
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result on stochastic accelerated methods for root-finding problems.

•

Finally, our complexity matches the best-known results in convex optimization using SAGA or SVRG, without requiring enhancements such as restarting or nested strategies as in, e.g.,
[
4
,
86
]
.
This complexity is better than deterministic accelerated methods by a factor
n^{1/3}
.

Related work.

Both (
CE
) and (

) are well-studied in the literature (e.g.,
[
10
,
16
,
34
,
70
,

,

).
We focus on the most recent works relevant to our methods.

Accelerated methods.

Nesterov’s accelerated methods have been applied to solve both (
CE
) and (
NI
) in
[
42
,
51
]
and
[
8
]
, followed by
[
1
,
5
,
6
,
8
,
24
,
42
,
50
,
60
,
67
,
77
]
.
Unlike convex optimization, extending these methods to (
CE
) and (
NI
) faces a fundamental challenge in constructing a suitable Lyapunov function.
In convex optimization, the objective function serves this purpose, but it is absent in (
CE
) and (
NI
).
This necessitates a different approach for (
CE
) and (
NI
) (see, e.g.,
[
8
,
60
]
).
Our approach leverages similar ideas from dynamical systems as in
[
8
,
60
]
.

Alternatively, Halpern’s fixed-point iteration
[
41
]
has recently been proven to achieve better convergence rates (see
[
32
,
57
,
75
]
), matching Nesterov’s schemes.
Starting with the seminal work
[
82
]
, which extended Halpern’s iteration to the extragradient method, many subsequent works have exploited this idea for other methods (e.g.,
[
19
,
20
,
56
,
67
,
76
,
77
,
78
]
).
Recently,
[
77
]
established a connection between Nesterov’s and Halpern’s accelerations for solving (
CE
) with different schemes.
Nevertheless, all of these results are primarily developed for deterministic methods.

Stochastic approximation methods.

Stochastic methods for both (
CE
) and (
NI
) and their special cases have been extensively studied, e.g., in
[
46
,
52
,
69
]
.
Some methods exploit mirror-prox and averaging techniques such as
[
46
,
52
]
, while others rely on projection or extragradient-type schemes (e.g.,
[
13
,
27
,
44
,
47
,
62
,
69
,
83
]
).
Many algorithms use standard Robbins-Monro stochastic approximation with large or increasing batch sizes to reduce the variance.
Other works generalize the analysis to a broader class of algorithms (e.g.,
[
12
,
38
,
58
]
), covering both standard stochastic and variance reduction methods.
However, their complexity is generally worse than that of methods based on control variate techniques as in our work.

Variance reduction methods.
Control variate variance reduction techniques are widely used in optimization, leading to the development of various estimators, including SAGA
[
31
]
, SVRG
[
45
]
, SARAH
[
65
]
, and Hybrid-SGD
[
79
]
.
These estimators have been adopted in methods for solving (
CE
) and (
NI
).
For instance,
[
29
,
30
]
proposed a SAGA-type method for (
CE
) and (
NI
), under “star” cocoercivity and strong quasi-monotonicity — assumptions closely related to our work.
Nevertheless, our focus is on accelerated methods that achieve improved convergence rates and complexity.
Similarly,
[
2
,
3
]
employed SVRG estimators for methods related to (
NI
), but these algorithms are non-accelerated.
Other relevant works include
[
14
,
21
,
23
,
43
,
66
,
84
]
, some of which focused on minimax problems or bilinear matrix games.
More recently, variance-reduced methods based on Halpern’s fixed-point iteration have been explored (e.g.,
[

,
18

), leveraging SARAH to achieve improved complexity.
While this approach offers advantages in certain settings, our work takes a different path by focusing on Nesterov’s acceleration combined with variance reduction techniques.
In addition, we prove new and stronger convergence results compared to these papers.

1.6
Notation.

We work with a finite-dimensional space
\mathbb{R}^{p}
equipped with the standard inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and norm
\|\cdot\|
.
For a single-valued or multivalued operator
F
on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\mathrm{dom}(F):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:Fx\neq\emptyset\}
denotes its domain,
\mathrm{gra}(F):=\{(x,z)\in\mathrm{dom}(F)\times\mathbb{R}^{p}:z\in Fx\}
denotes its graph, and
J_{F}x:=\{u\in\mathrm{dom}(F):x\in u+Fu\}
denotes its resolvent.
For a symmetric matrix
\mathbf{X}
,
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{X})
and
\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{X})
stand for the smallest and largest eigenvalues of
\mathbf{X}
, respectively.

We use
\mathcal{F}_{k}
to denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by all the randomness arising from the algorithm, including
x^{0},\cdots,x^{k}
, up to the iteration
k
before constructing
\widetilde{S}^{k}
.

\mathbb{E}_{k}[\cdot]=\mathbb{E}[\cdot\mid\mathcal{F}_{k}]
denotes the conditional expectation with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, and
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
is the total expectation.
We also use
\mathcal{O}\left(\cdot\right)
,
o\left(\cdot\right)
, and
\Omega(\cdot)
to characterize convergence rates and oracle complexity as usual.

2
Unbiased Variance-Reduced Estimators for
S^{k}
.

Most existing variance reduction methods in the literature directly construct an estimator for
Gx^{k}
.
In this paper, we depart from those methods and construct a class of estimators
\widetilde{S}^{k}
for the intermediate quantity
S^{k}=Gx^{k}-\gamma_{k}Gx^{k-1}
defined in (
VFKM
).

2.1
A class of stochastic variance-reduced estimators for
S^{k}
.

We introduce the following class of unbiased variance-reduced estimators for
S^{k}
in (
VFKM
).

Definition 2.1

For given
\{\gamma_{k}\}
,
\{x^{k}\}
, and
S^{k}
in (
VFKM
), let
\mathcal{F}_{k}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra containing all the information generated by the algorithm up to iteration
k
before constructing
\widetilde{S}^{k}
.
A stochastic estimator
\widetilde{S}^{k}
of
S^{k}
, constructed using fresh randomness conditionally on
\mathcal{F}_{k}
, is called a variance-reduced unbiased estimator of
S^{k}
if there exist
\rho\in(0,1)
,
\Theta>0
,
\hat{\Theta}\geq 0
, and a nonnegative random sequence
\{\Delta_{k}\}
such that, for all
k\geq 1
, almost surely:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}_{k}[\widetilde{S}^{k}-S^{k}]&=&0,\\
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\|\widetilde{S}^{k}-S^{k}\|^{2}]&\leq&\mathbb{E}_{k}[\Delta_{k}],\\
\frac{1}{(1-\gamma_{k})^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{k}[\Delta_{k}]&\leq&\frac{1-\rho}{(1-\gamma_{k-1})^{2}}\Delta_{k-1}+\frac{\Theta}{(1-\gamma_{k})^{2}}U_{k}+\frac{\hat{\Theta}}{(1-\gamma_{k-1})^{2}}U_{k-1},\end{array}\right.\vskip-1.07639pt

(6)

where
U_{k}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|G_{i}x^{k}-G_{i}x^{k-1}\|^{2}
,
x^{-2}=x^{-1}=x^{0}
, and
\gamma_{-1}=\gamma_{0}\in[0,1)
.

Discussion.

Let us further discuss Definition
2.1
and compare it with related existing works.

1.

The first condition in (
6
) shows that
\widetilde{S}^{k}
is an unbiased estimator of
S^{k}
. The second line of (
6
) provides an upper bound on the variance of
\widetilde{S}^{k}
, which depends on both the parameter
\gamma_{k}
in
S^{k}
and the random variable
\Delta_{k}
. The last condition in (
6
) establishes a “quasi-Fejér property” for
(1-\gamma_{k})^{-2}\Delta_{k}
with contraction factor
1-\rho
and two vanishing terms
U_{k}
and
U_{k-1}
.
This condition involves evaluations of
G_{i}
at three consecutive iterates
x^{k-2}
,
x^{k-1}
, and
x^{k}
.

2.

Since Definition
2.1
defines a class of estimators
\widetilde{S}^{k}
for
S^{k}=Gx^{k}-\gamma_{k}Gx^{k-1}
, rather than for
Gx^{k}
alone, it differs from the definitions in
[
12
,
33
,
39
,
49
]
, which are formulated for
Gx^{k}
or
\nabla{f}(x^{k})
.
More specifically, both
[
39
]
and
[
49
]
use the same definition of variance-reduced unbiased estimators.
While their definition has a similar pattern to (
6
), it involves quantities different from
S^{k}
and
U_{k}
in Definition
2.1
.
In fact, our Definition
2.1
does not reduce to a special case of those in
[
39
]
and
[
49
]
when applied to finite-sum convex optimization.
Along similar lines,
[
33
]
introduced a class of estimators that covers both unbiased and biased estimators to develop accelerated methods.
Although it shares a similar pattern with (
6
), the quantities involved also differ when specialized to convex optimization.
The authors in
[
12
]
adopted the definition in
[
39
,
49
]
and extended it to VIPs, which is not the same as (
6
) since it measures
\|\widetilde{G}^{k}-Gx^{\star}\|^{2}
(where
\widetilde{G}^{k}
is an estimator of
Gx^{k}
) instead of
\|\widetilde{S}^{k}-S^{k}\|^{2}
and uses different terms on the right-hand sides of the corresponding inequalities.

In the following subsections, we will construct two estimators that satisfy Definition
2.1
using SVRG
[
45
,
53
]
and SAGA
[
31
]
, respectively.
However, any other estimator satisfying Definition
2.1
can be used in our method (
VFKM
).

2.2
The loopless SVRG estimator for
S^{k}
.

Consider an i.i.d. mini-batch
\mathcal{B}_{k}\subseteq[n]
with a fixed size
b:=|\mathcal{B}_{k}|
(sampling with replacement).
Let
G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}x:=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}G_{i}x
be a standard mini-batch estimator of
Gx
for a given
x\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
.
Given
\{x^{k}\}
from (
VFKM
), we define

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\widetilde{S}^{k}&:=&(1-\gamma_{k})\big(Gw^{k}-G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}w^{k}\big)+G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}x^{k}-\gamma_{k}G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}x^{k-1},\end{array}\vskip-1.07639pt

(7)

where, for all
k\geq 1
, the reference or snapshot point
w^{k}
is updated as follows:

w^{k}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}x^{k-1}&\text{with probability $\mathbf{p}$}\\
w^{k-1}&\text{with probability $1-\mathbf{p}$}.\end{array}\right.\vskip-1.07639pt

(8)

Here,
w^{0}:=x^{0}
, and the probability
\mathbf{p}\in(0,1)
is typically chosen to be small.
Similar to
[
53
]
, we call
\widetilde{S}^{k}
in (
7
) a loopless SVRG estimator, but it is still different from those in
[
30
,
45
,
53
]
.

Next, we state the following key property, whose proof is given in Appendix
B.1
.

Lemma 2.2

Let
\{x^{k}\}
and
S^{k}=Gx^{k}-\gamma_{k}Gx^{k-1}
be defined in (
VFKM
),
\widetilde{S}^{k}
be constructed by the loopless SVRG estimator (
7
), and
w^{k}
be given in (
8
).
Define

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\Delta_{k}&:=&\frac{1}{nb}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|G_{i}x^{k}-\gamma_{k}G_{i}x^{k-1}-(1-\gamma_{k})G_{i}w^{k}\|^{2}.\end{array}\vskip-1.07639pt

(9)

Then, for all
k\geq 1
, we have

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}_{k}[\widetilde{S}^{k}-S^{k}]&=&0,\\
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\|\widetilde{S}^{k}-S^{k}\|^{2}]&\leq&\mathbb{E}_{k}[\Delta_{k}],\\
\frac{1}{(1-\gamma_{k})^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{k}[\Delta_{k}]&\leq&\big(1-\frac{\mathbf{p}}{2}\big)\frac{1}{(1-\gamma_{k-1})^{2}}\Delta_{k-1}+\frac{4-6\mathbf{p}+3\mathbf{p}^{2}}{b\mathbf{p}(1-\gamma_{k})^{2}}U_{k}+\frac{2(2-3\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{p}^{2})\gamma_{k-1}^{2}}{b\mathbf{p}(1-\gamma_{k-1})^{2}}U_{k-1}.\end{array}\right.\hskip-15.0694pt\vskip-1.07639pt

(10)

Consequently, the estimator
\widetilde{S}^{k}
satisfies Definition
2.1
with
\rho:=\frac{\mathbf{p}}{2}\in(0,1)
,
\Theta:=\frac{4-6\mathbf{p}+3\mathbf{p}^{2}}{b\mathbf{p}}
,
\hat{\Theta}:=\frac{2(2-3\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{p}^{2})}{b\mathbf{p}}
, and
\Delta_{k}
in (
9
).

2.3
The SAGA estimator for
S^{k}
.

At each iteration
k\geq 1
, we first sample an i.i.d. mini-batch
\mathcal{B}_{k}\subseteq[n]
of a fixed size
b
with replacement.
Next, we sample a refresh mini-batch
\hat{B}_{k}\subseteq[n]
of size
b
without replacement and independent of
\mathcal{B}_{k}
.
Let
G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}x:=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}G_{i}x
be a mini-batch estimator of
Gx
for a given
x\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
and
\widehat{G}^{k}_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}:=\frac{1}{b}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}\widehat{G}^{k}_{i}
for given
\{\widehat{G}^{k}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
Given
\{x^{k}\}
and
S^{k}=Gx^{k}-\gamma_{k}Gx^{k-1}
in (
VFKM
), we define the following SAGA estimator for
S^{k}
:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\widetilde{S}^{k}&:=&\frac{1-\gamma_{k}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{G}^{k}_{i}+\big[G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}x^{k}-\gamma_{k}G_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}x^{k-1}-(1-\gamma_{k})\widehat{G}^{k}_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}\big],\end{array}\vskip-1.07639pt

(11)

where
\widehat{G}^{k}_{i}
is initialized at
\widehat{G}^{0}_{i}:=G_{i}x^{0}
for
i\in[n]
, and then, for
k\geq 1
, is updated by

\widehat{G}^{k}_{i}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\widehat{G}^{k-1}_{i}&\text{if}~i\notin\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{k},\\
G_{i}x^{k-1}&\text{if}~i\in\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{k}.\end{array}\right.\vskip-1.07639pt

(12)

For this SAGA estimator, we need to store all
n
components
\widehat{G}_{i}^{k}
computed so far for
i\in[n]
in a table
\mathcal{T}_{k}:=[\widehat{G}^{k}_{1},\widehat{G}^{k}_{2},\cdots,\widehat{G}^{k}_{n}]
.
At each iteration
k
, we will evaluate
G_{i}x^{k-1}
for
i\in\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{k}
, and update the table
\mathcal{T}_{k}
for all
i\in\hat{\mathcal{B}}_{k}
.
Hence, the SAGA estimator requires substantial memory to store
\mathcal{T}_{k}
if
n
and
p
are both large.

The following lemma shows that the estimator constructed in (
11
) satisfies Definition
2.1
.
Its proof is deferred to Appendix
B.2
.

Lemma 2.3

Let
\{x^{k}\}
and
S^{k}=Gx^{k}-\gamma_{k}Gx^{k-1}
be given in (
VFKM
) and
\widetilde{S}^{k}
be constructed by the SAGA estimator (
11
).
We define

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\Delta_{k}&:=&\frac{1}{nb}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|G_{i}x^{k}-\gamma_{k}G_{i}x^{k-1}-(1-\gamma_{k})\widehat{G}_{i}^{k}\|^{2}.\end{array}\vskip-1.07639pt

(13)

If we denote
\Theta:=\frac{2(n-b)(2n+b)+b^{2}}{nb^{2}}
and
\hat{\Theta}:=\frac{2(n-b)(2n+b)}{nb^{2}}
, then for all
k\geq 1
, we have

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}_{k}[\widetilde{S}^{k}-S^{k}]&=&0,\\
\mathbb{E}_{k}[\|\widetilde{S}^{k}-S^{k}\|^{2}]&\leq&\mathbb{E}_{k}[\Delta_{k}],\\
\frac{1}{(1-\gamma_{k})^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{k}[\Delta_{k}]&\leq&\big(1-\frac{b}{2n}\big)\frac{1}{(1-\gamma_{k-1})^{2}}\Delta_{k-1}+\frac{\Theta}{(1-\gamma_{k})^{2}}U_{k}+\frac{\hat{\Theta}}{(1-\gamma_{k-1})^{2}}U_{k-1}.\end{array}\right.\vskip-1.07639pt

(14)

Consequently,
\widetilde{S}^{k}
satisfies Definition
2.1
with
\rho:=\frac{b}{2n}\in(0,1)
,
\Theta
and
\hat{\Theta}
given above, and
\Delta_{k}
in (
13
).

3
Convergence and Complexity Analysis of (
VFKM
).

In this section, we first establish sublinear convergence rates of (
VFKM
).
Then, we prove its almost sure convergence rates and the almost sure convergence of
\{x^{k}\}
.
Finally, we estimate the complexity for two variants of (
VFKM
) using our SVRG and SAGA estimators, respectively.

3.1
Lyapunov function and descent lemma.

The following lemma serves as a key step in proving the convergence of (
VFKM
), whose proof is given in Appendix
C.1
.

Lemma 3.1

Suppose that Condition (
1
) holds for (
CE
).
Let
\{x^{k}\}
be generated by (
VFKM
) using a stochastic estimator
\widetilde{S}^{k}
of
S^{k}
satisfying Definition
2.1
and the following parameters:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\theta_{k}:=\frac{t_{k}-r-\mu}{t_{k+1}},\quad\gamma_{k}:=\frac{t_{k}-r-\mu}{t_{k}-\mu},\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{k}:=\frac{2\beta(t_{k}-\mu)}{t_{k+1}},\end{array}\vskip-1.07639pt

(15)

where
\beta>0
,
r>0
, and
\mu>0
are given and
t_{k}\geq r+\mu
for all
k\geq 0
.

Consider the following
Lyapunov function
:

\hskip-17.22217pt\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathcal{E}_{k}&:=&4r\beta(t_{k}-\mu)\big[\langle Gx^{k},x^{k}-x^{\star}\rangle-\beta\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}\big]+\|r(x^{k}-x^{\star})+t_{k+1}(x^{k+1}-x^{k})\|^{2}\\
&&+{~}\mu r\|x^{k}-x^{\star}\|^{2}+\frac{4\beta^{2}\hat{\Theta}(t_{k}-\mu)^{2}}{\rho}U_{k}+\frac{4\beta^{2}(1-\rho)(t_{k}-\mu)^{2}}{\rho}\Delta_{k},\end{array}\hskip-25.83325pt\vskip-1.07639pt

(16)

where
U_{k}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|G_{i}x^{k}-G_{i}x^{k-1}\|^{2}
.

Then, for all
k\geq 1
, we have

\hskip 0.0pt\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathcal{E}_{k-1}-\mathbb{E}_{k}[\mathcal{E}_{k}]&\geq&\Lambda_{k}(t_{k}-\mu)^{2}U_{k}+\mu(2t_{k}-r-\mu)\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|^{2}\\
&&+{~}4r\beta(t_{k-1}-t_{k}+r)\big[\langle Gx^{k-1},x^{k-1}-x^{\star}\rangle-\beta\|Gx^{k-1}\|^{2}\big],\end{array}\hskip-12.91663pt\vskip-1.07639pt

(17)

where
\Lambda_{k}:=4\beta\left[\frac{(t_{k}-r-\mu)}{(t_{k}-\mu)}\big(\frac{1}{L}-\beta\big)-\frac{\beta(\Theta+\hat{\Theta})}{\rho}\right]
.

3.2
Sublinear convergence rates of (
VFKM
).

For simplicity of presentation, we define the following constants:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcllcl}\bar{\beta}&:=&\min\left\{\frac{\rho}{[\rho+(r+1)(\Theta+\hat{\Theta})]L},\ \frac{1}{(r+1)L}\right\},&\Lambda&:=&\frac{4\beta\left(\rho-[\rho+(r+1)(\Theta+\hat{\Theta})]L\beta\right)}{L\rho(r+1)},\\
C_{0}&:=&r(1+3r+8rL^{2}\beta^{2}),&C_{1}&:=&\frac{4r^{2}(r-1)L^{2}\beta^{2}}{r+2}+\big(\frac{2}{r}+\psi\big)C_{0},\\
C_{2}&:=&4r^{2}L^{2}\beta^{2}+\big[\psi+\frac{2(r+2)^{2}}{r}\big]C_{0}+\frac{4(r+2)^{2}C_{1}}{r(r-2)},&C_{3}&:=&\frac{(r+2)^{2}(C_{1}+C_{2})}{2r^{2}},\end{array}\right.\vskip-1.07639pt

(18)

where
\psi:=\frac{4\beta^{2}(\Theta+\hat{\Theta})}{\rho\Lambda}
.
For a given
r>2
, we have
C_{s}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
for
s\in\{0,1,2,3\}
.

Note that we can always fix
r:=3
in our method without affecting the convergence results.
However, we keep
r
as a free parameter to examine how it influences the practical performance of our method.
For the constants in (
18
), only
\bar{\beta}
is used in our method (
VFKM
).
The other constants
\psi
,
\Lambda
, and
C_{i}
for
i=0,1,2,3
are used only in the theoretical convergence analysis in Theorem
3.2
.

Now, we establish sublinear convergence rates and iteration complexity of (
VFKM
).

Theorem 3.2

Suppose that
G
in (
CE
) satisfies Assumption
1.1
.
Let
\{x^{k}\}
be generated by our method (
VFKM
) to solve (
CE
) using a stochastic estimator
\widetilde{S}^{k}
of
S^{k}
satisfying Definition
2.1
with
\rho\in(0,1)
and
\Delta_{0}=0
almost surely.
For
\bar{\beta}
given in (
18
), assume that
r>2
and
\beta\in(0,\bar{\beta})
are given, and
\theta_{k}
,
\gamma_{k}
, and
\eta_{k}
are updated by

\theta_{k}:=\frac{k}{k+r+2},\quad\gamma_{k}:=\frac{k}{k+r},\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{k}:=\frac{2\beta(k+r)}{k+r+2}.\vskip-1.07639pt

(19)

Let
\Lambda
and
C_{s}
for
s\in\{0,1,2,3\}
be given in (
18
).
Then, the following statements hold.

\mathrm{(a)}

Summability bounds.

The following bounds hold:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2k+r+3)\mathbb{E}\big[\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}\big]&\leq&C_{0}\|x^{0}-x^{\star}\|^{2},\\
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(2k+r+3)\mathbb{E}\big[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}\big]&\leq&\frac{(r+2)^{2}}{2\beta^{2}r^{2}}\big(C_{0}+\frac{2C_{1}}{r-2}\big)\|x^{0}-x^{\star}\|^{2}.\end{array}\vskip-1.07639pt

(20)

\mathrm{(b)}

Big-O convergence rates.

For any
k\geq 0
, we have

\hskip-0.43057pt\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathbb{E}\big[\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}\big]&\leq&\dfrac{C_{2}\|x^{0}-x^{\star}\|^{2}}{(k+r+2)^{2}},\\
\mathbb{E}\big[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}\big]&\leq&\dfrac{C_{3}\|x^{0}-x^{\star}\|^{2}}{\beta^{2}(k+r-1)(k+r+2)}.\end{array}\hskip-17.22217pt\vskip-1.07639pt

(21)

\mathrm{(c)}

Small-o convergence rates.

The following
o\big(1/k^{2}\big)
rates also hold:

\lim_{k\to\infty}k^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}\big]=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{k\to\infty}k^{2}\mathbb{E}\big[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}\big]=0.\vskip-1.07639pt

(22)

For a given
\epsilon>0
, (
VFKM
) requires
\mathcal{O}\big(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\big)
iterations to achieve
\mathbb{E}[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}]\leq\epsilon^{2}
.

Discussion.
Before proving Theorem
3.2
, we make the following remarks on this result.

1.

Theorem
3.2
establishes both
\mathcal{O}\left(1/k^{2}\right)
and
o\left(1/k^{2}\right)
convergence rates for
\mathbb{E}\big[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}\big]
.
These rates improve upon non-accelerated methods, e.g.,
[
2
,
3
,
30
]
, by a factor of
\mathcal{O}\big(1/k\big)
, but they require Assumption
1.1
, which may be stronger than the assumptions in
[
2
,
3
]
.

2.

Our method (
VFKM
) uses three parameters
\theta_{k}
,
\gamma_{k}
, and
\eta_{k}
.
Both
\gamma_{k}
and
\theta_{k}
are given explicitly and do not depend on any parameter of
G
or of (
6
) in Definition
2.1
.
Only
\eta_{k}
depends on
\beta
, and thus
\bar{\beta}
defined in (
18
).
Since our method covers a class of estimators in Definition
2.1
, it is natural that
\bar{\beta}
depends on
L
in Assumption
1.1
, as well as
\rho
,
\Theta
, and
\hat{\Theta}
in Definition
2.1
.
However, once the estimator
\widetilde{S}^{k}
is specified,
\rho
,
\Theta
, and
\hat{\Theta}
are explicit, and thus
\bar{\beta}
depends only on
L
.

3.

The summability bounds in (
20
) are well-known for deterministic Nesterov acceleration methods in convex optimization and monotone operators; see, e.g.,
[
7
]
.
Since
\Delta{x}^{k}:=x^{k+1}-x^{k}
can be viewed as the “velocity” associated with the “state”
x^{k}
, the first summability bound in (
20
) describes the evolution of the corresponding “kinetic energy” over the iterations and shows that the “total weighted kinetic energy” is finite.
Likewise, viewing
\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}
as a “force magnitude”, the second summability bound in (
20
) tracks its evolution across iterations and shows that its total weighted sum is finite.

4.

The limits in (
22
) indicate a faster
o\big(1/k^{2}\big)
convergence rate of
\mathbb{E}\big[\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}\big]

\mathbb{E}\big[\|Gx^{k}\|^{2}\big]

4
Linear Convergence under Strong Quasi-monotonicity.

In this section, we establish a linear convergence rate and estimate the oracle complexity of (
VFKM
) under Assumption
1.1
and the following additional assumption:

{assumption}

G
in (
CE
) is
\sigma
-strongly quasi-monotone, i.e., there exist
x^{\star}\in\mathrm{zer}(G)
and
\sigma>0
such that
\langle Gx,x-x^{\star}\rangle\geq\sigma\|x-x^{\star}\|^{2}
for all
x\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
.

If
G
is strongly monotone with a strong monotonicity modulus
\sigma>0
, then it satisfies Assumption
4
.
However, the converse is not true in general.
For example, if we consider
G:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
Gx=x+\sin(x)
, then it is straightforward to verify that
x^{\star}=0\in\mathrm{zer}(G)
and
G
is
\sigma
-strongly quasi-monotone with
\sigma:=1-\frac{1}{\pi}>0
, but it is not strongly monotone.
Hence, this assumption is weaker than the strong monotonicity of
G
.
Note that we do not require each summand
G_{i}
to be strongly monotone.
Assumption
4
is not new.
It has been used in
[
68
]
and recently in
[
30
,
58
]
.

For simplicity of presentation, we first define the following constants:

\hskip-8.61108pt\begin{array}[]{lcl}\kappa:=\frac{L}{\sigma},\ \ \Gamma:=\rho+2(\Theta+\hat{\Theta}),\ \ M:=2(2\Gamma-1)\kappa,\ \ \text{and}\ \ N:=3\Gamma\kappa+2(1-2\rho).\end{array}\hskip-8.61108pt\vskip-1.07639pt

{assumption}

Then, we can view

Now, we take

is

-co-hypomonotone with

6.1
Minimax optimization model.

We consider the following general convex-concave minimax optimization problem involving quadratic objective functions:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}{\displaystyle\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}}\max_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}}}\Big\{\mathcal{L}(z,\xi):=f(z)+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{H}_{i}(z,\xi)-g(\xi)\Big\},\end{array}\vskip-1.07639pt

(44)

where
\mathcal{H}_{i}(z,\xi):=\frac{1}{2}z^{T}A_{i}z+z^{T}L_{i}\xi-\frac{1}{2}\xi^{T}B_{i}\xi+b_{i}^{\top}z-c_{i}^{\top}\xi
,
A_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{1}}
and
B_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}\times p_{2}}
are symmetric positive semidefinite matrices,
L_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}\times p_{2}}
,
b_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}
,
c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}
, and
f=\delta_{\Delta_{p_{1}}}
and
g=\delta_{\Delta_{p_{2}}}
are the indicator functions of the standard simplices in
\mathbb{R}^{p_{1}}
and
\mathbb{R}^{p_{2}}
, respectively.
Let us first define
x:=[z,\xi]\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, where
p:=p_{1}+p_{2}
.
Next, we define

G_{i}x=\mathbf{G}_{i}x+\mathbf{g}_{i}:=\begin{bmatrix}A_{i}&L_{i}\\
-L_{i}^{T}&B_{i}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}z\\
\xi\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}b_{i}\\

(

]

Lemma A.3

(Supermartingale theorem,
[
71
]
)

Let
\{X_{k}\}
,
\{\alpha_{k}\}
,
\{V_{k}\}
and
\{R_{k}\}
be sequences of nonnegative integrable random variables on some arbitrary probability space and adapted to the filtration
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{k}\right\}
with

and
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}R_{k}<+\infty
almost surely, and

Then,

converges almost surely and

almost surely.

A.2
Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

(a) The statement (a) was proven in
[
9
,
30
]
.

(b) Let

be the Moreau-Yosida approximation of
\lambda T
.
First, as shown in
[
11
]
,
A_{\lambda T}
is
(\lambda-\nu)
-cocoercive, provided that
\lambda>\nu
.
Next, since
J_{\lambda T}x=x-\lambda A_{\lambda T}x
and
J_{\lambda T}y=y-\lambda A_{\lambda T}y

(\lambda-\nu)
-cocoercivity of
A_{\lambda T}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\|J_{\lambda T}x-J_{\lambda T}y\|^{2}&=&\|x-y\|^{2}-2\lambda\langle A_{\lambda T}x-A_{\lambda T}y,x-y\rangle+\lambda^{2}\|A_{\lambda T}x-A_{\lambda T}y\|^{2}\\
&\leq&\|x-y\|^{2}-\lambda\big(\lambda-2\nu)\|A_{\lambda T}x-A_{\lambda T}y\|^{2}.\end{array}\vskip-1.07639pt

Thus if
\lambda\geq 2\nu
, then
\|J_{\lambda T}x-J_{\lambda T}y\|\leq\|x-y\|
, implying that
J_{\lambda T}
is nonexpansive.

(c) We denote
u:=J_{\lambda T}x
and
v:=J_{\lambda T}y
for given
x,y\in\mathrm{dom}(J_{\lambda T})
, where
\lambda>\nu
.
Note that
u
and
v
are well-defined due to Assumption
5
, see
[
11
]
.
Then, we have
A_{\lambda T}x=\frac{1}{\lambda}(x-J_{\lambda T}x)=\frac{1}{\lambda}(x-u)
.

Now, fix any
i\in[n]
.
From (
41
), we have
A_{\lambda T}x=\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}x-G_{i}u
.
Since
A_{\lambda T}
is
(\lambda-\nu)
-cocoercive and
A_{\lambda T}x=\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}x-G_{i}u
, we can show that

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\langle\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}x-\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}y-(G_{i}u-G_{i}v),x-y\rangle&\geq&(\lambda-\nu)\|\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}x-\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}y-(G_{i}u-G_{i}v)\|^{2}.\end{array}\vskip-1.07639pt

Using again
u-\lambda G_{i}u=x-\lambda\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}x
from (
41
), the last inequality leads to

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\langle\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}x-\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}y,x-y\rangle&\geq&(\lambda-\nu)\|\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}x-\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}y\|^{2}-(\lambda-2\nu)\langle\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}x-\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}y,G_{i}u-G_{i}v\rangle\\
&&+{~}(\lambda-\nu)\|G_{i}u-G_{i}v\|^{2}+\langle G_{i}u-G_{i}v,x-y-\lambda(\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}x-\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}y)\rangle\\
&=&(\lambda-\nu)\|\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}x-\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}y\|^{2}-(\lambda-2\nu)\langle\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}x-\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}y,G_{i}u-G_{i}v\rangle\\
&&+{~}\langle G_{i}u-G_{i}v,u-v\rangle-\nu\|G_{i}u-G_{i}v\|^{2}.\end{array}\vskip-1.07639pt

Utilizing
L_{g}\langle G_{i}u-G_{i}v,u-v\rangle\geq\|G_{i}u-G_{i}v\|^{2}
from the
\frac{1}{L_{g}}
-cocoercivity of
G_{i}
in Assumption
5
, we can derive that

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}_{[1]}&:=&\langle\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}x-\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}y,x-y\rangle\\
&\geq&(\lambda-\nu)\|\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}x-\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}y\|^{2}+\big(\frac{1}{L_{g}}-\nu\big)\|G_{i}u-G_{i}v\|^{2}-(\lambda-2\nu)\langle\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}x-\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}y,G_{i}u-G_{i}v\rangle\\
&=&\frac{4(1-L_{g}\nu)(\lambda-\nu)-(\lambda-2\nu)^{2}L_{g}}{4(1-L_{g}\nu)}\|\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}x-\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}y\|^{2}+\frac{(1-L_{g}\nu)}{L_{g}}\|G_{i}u-G_{i}v-\frac{(\lambda-2\nu)L_{g}}{2(1-L_{g}\nu)}\big(\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}x-\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}y)\|^{2}\\
&\geq&\frac{4\lambda-4\nu-L_{g}\lambda^{2}}{4(1-L_{g}\nu)}\|\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}x-\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}y\|^{2}.\end{array}\vskip-1.07639pt

This proves that
\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}
is
\frac{1}{L}
-cocoercive with

Note that the condition
\frac{2(1-\sqrt{1-L_{g}\nu})}{L_{g}}<\lambda<\frac{2(1+\sqrt{1-L_{g}\nu})}{L_{g}}
guarantees that

.
Finally, since
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{G}_{i,\lambda}
, it follows immediately that
\mathcal{G}_{\lambda}
satisfies Condition (
1
).

\square
