Abstract
. A formula for the Hausdorff dimension of typical limsup sets generated by randomly distributed isotropic rectangles in Heisenberg groups is derived in terms of directed singular value functions.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
28A80, 60D05.

1.
Introduction

In this paper we study the Hausdorff dimension of a typical limsup set generated by rectangles, where the centers of the rectangles are distributed randomly according to the Lebesgue measure. A motivation for our studies is to find counterparts in the Heisenberg setting for results known about random limsup sets in Euclidean spaces.

Recently, dimensional properties in Heisenberg groups have been studied widely. Usually the objective of these studies is to consider some results known in Euclidean spaces and find the correct analogues in the Heisenberg setting. Since the relation between Hausdorff dimension with respect to the Euclidean and the Heisenberg metrics is not a trivial one
[
4
,
5
]
, the Euclidean dimension results do not directly translate to results in Heisenberg groups and more careful consideration is required.

Hausdorff dimensions of self-similar and self-affine sets in the Heisenberg setting have been studied, for example, in
[
1
,
5
,
6
,
8
]
. In
[
2
,
3
,
16
,
18
]
, the authors study the Hausdorff dimensions of projections and slices in Heisenberg groups. The two types of natural projections and slices in Heisenberg groups are the horizontal and vertical ones. The vertical projections are not quite as well-behaved as the horizontal projections in the sense that they are, for example, not Lipschitz continuous. This means that methods employed in the Euclidean projection theorems cannot be used in the Heisenberg setting and the theorems themselves turn out to have a different character than their Euclidean counterparts.

Our focus is on the dimensional properties of random limsup sets in Heisenberg groups. Given a space
X
and a sequence
(A_{n})
of subsets of
X
, the limsup set generated by the sequence
(A_{n})
is the set of points in
X
which belong to infinitely many of the sets
A_{n}
, that is,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}A_{n}=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}\bigcup_{k=n}^{\infty}A_{k}.

The study of limsup sets has a long history, and can be traced back at least as far as the Borel-Cantelli lemma. These limsup sets are essential objects in the study of Besicovitch-Eggleston sets related to
k
-adic expansions of real numbers
[
7
,
10
]
, and in Diophantine approximation
[
19
,
22
]
. For developments in Diophantine approximation in Heisenberg groups, see
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
.

Dimensional properties of random limsup sets have been studied since
[
15
]
. There the authors considered limsup sets of arcs whose centers are randomly placed on the unit circle where the randomness is determined by the Lebesgue measure. They obtained a formula for the Hausdorff dimension of a typical limsup set generated by arcs.

Since
[
15
]
, there have been many developments in the study of dimensional properties of random limsup sets in metric spaces.
For various results, see
[
9
,
13
,
14
,
17
,
20
,
21
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
.
Together, the dimension results in these papers yield the almost-sure values of Hausdorff dimensions of random limsup sets in varying scenarios depending on properties of the ambient space, the sets generating the limsup sets and the measure according to which the sets are distributed.

If the underlying space is a Riemann manifold and the driving measure is the Lebesgue measure, then the generating sets can be any Lebesgue measurable sets satisfying some necessary density conditions. If the underlying space is the Euclidean or the symbolic space and the generating sets are balls, then the restrictions imposed on the measure can be relaxed a bit, although some special properties, like the measure being a Gibbs measure, are required. The underlying space can be taken to be any Ahlfors regular metric space provided that the generating sets are balls and the randomness is determined by the natural measure.

In
[
12
]
, the authors consider random limsup sets generated by rectangles in products of Ahlfors regular metric spaces. There, the Lipschitz continuity of projections plays an essential role, hence the same methods are not readily available when considering rectangles in Heisenberg groups and some new machinery is required.

In the paper
[
11
]
, the authors obtained an almost-sure formula for the Hausdorff dimension of a random limsup set generated by a certain type of rectangles in the first Heisenberg group. To accomplish this, they generalized certain Euclidean results to the context of unimodular groups (see Theorem
4.1
). They then carried out calculations specific to these types of rectangles and the first Heisenberg group to complete the proof.

With the definition of rectangles given in
[
11
]
, the dimension formula extends to higher-dimensional Heisenberg groups in a rather straightforward manner (see Remark
4.2
below). However, in Heisenberg groups there are also other natural ways of defining rectangles and the purpose of this paper is to obtain a dimension formula that is valid for limsup sets generated by these types of rectangles.

The paper is organized as follows:
First, we fix some notations and introduce the required concepts in order to present our main result (see Theorem
2.1
below). In Section
3
we establish upper bounds for the Hausdorff content and lower bounds for the capacity of rectangles. In Section
4
, we combine the results of Section
3
with a theorem proved in
[
11
]
to prove Theorem
2.1
. We end the paper with a brief comment on the main result in
[
11
]
and its generalization to higher dimensional Heisenberg groups.

2.
Notation and Main Result

In a metric space
(X,d)
, we write
B(x,r)
and
\overline{B}(x,r)
for the open and closed balls with radius
r
and center
x\in X
, respectively. Throughout this paper,
a\wedge b
denotes the minimum of two real numbers
a
and
b
.
For
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
we denote the Euclidean norm of
x
simply by
|x|
and we write
\langle x,y\rangle
for the Euclidean inner product of
x
and
y
. The dimension
m
of the Euclidean space in question will be clear from context.

Before stating our main result, we introduce the Heisenberg groups and discuss their properties which we will need in our analysis. For more details on general properties of Heisenberg groups we refer the reader to
[
3
]
and
[
23
]
.

The Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}:=\mathbb{H}^{n}
is the set
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
equipped with the group operation

\displaystyle p\ast p^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime},z+z^{\prime}+2\sigma((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))\right)

\displaystyle=\left(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime},z+z^{\prime}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_{i}y^{\prime}_{i}-y_{i}x^{\prime}_{i}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\left(x+x^{\prime},y+y^{\prime},z+z^{\prime}+2\left(\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle-\langle y,x^{\prime}\rangle\right)\right)

where
p=(x,y,z),p^{\prime}=(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma
is the standard symplectic form
on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
given by the formula

\displaystyle\sigma((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}))=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(x_{i}y^{\prime}_{i}-y_{i}x^{\prime}_{i}\right).

The neutral element of
\mathbb{H}
is
(0,0,0)
and the inverse of an element
p=(x,y,z)
is
p^{-1}=(-x,-y,-z)
. There is a norm on
\mathbb{H}
given by

\|p\|_{\mathbb{H}}=\left(|(x,y)|^{4}+z^{2}\right)^{1/4}

which determines a left-invariant metric via the formula

\displaystyle d_{\mathbb{H}}(p,p^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\|p^{-1}p^{\prime}\|_{\mathbb{H}}

\displaystyle=\Big(|(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})-(x,y)|^{4}+\big(z^{\prime}-z-2(\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle-\langle y,x^{\prime}\rangle)\big)^{2}\Big)^{1/4}.

The Haar measure on
\mathbb{H}
is the Lebesgue measure, which is invariant under both left and right translations so that
\mathbb{H}
is a unimodular group. In the metric
d_{\mathbb{H}}
, the Lebesgue measure of
B(p,r)
is proportional to
r^{2n+2}
and thus the metric space
(\mathbb{H},d_{\mathbb{H}})
has Hausdorff dimension
2n+2
.
The vertical line through the origin is

L(0)=\{p\in\mathbb{H}\mid x=y=0\},

and the vertical line through
p
is the set

L(p):=pL(0)=\{(x,y,z^{\prime})\in\mathbb{H}\mid z^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Moreover, the horizontal plane through the origin is

H(0)=\{p\in\mathbb{H}\mid z=0\}

and the horizontal plane through
p
is defined as

H(p):=pH(0)=\{p^{\prime}\in\mathbb{H}\mid z^{\prime}=z+2(\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle-\langle y,x^{\prime}\rangle)\}.

Note that
d_{\mathbb{H}}(p,p^{\prime})\geq|(x,y)-(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|
for every
p
and
p^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{H}
, with an equality if and only if
p^{\prime}\in H(p)
.

In the following we will work with homogeneous subgroups of
\mathbb{H}
. These are the closed subgroups of
\mathbb{H}
which are invariant under the intrinsic dilations
(x,y,z)\mapsto(sx,sy,s^{2}z)
,
s>0
. There are two kinds of homogeneous subgroups of
\mathbb{H}
, namely, the horizontal subgroups and vertical subgroups. A horizontal subgroup
\mathbb{V}
of
\mathbb{H}
is of the form
\mathbb{V}=V\times\{0\}
, where
V
is an isotropic subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, that is,
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is a linear subspace with the property that
\sigma_{|_{V}}=0
. A vertical subgroup
\mathbb{V}^{\perp}\subseteq\mathbb{H}
is a subgroup of the form
\mathbb{V}^{\perp}=V^{\perp}\times\mathbb{R}
, where
V^{\perp}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n}
is the orthogonal complement of an isotropic subspace
V
. The dimension (as a vector space) of a nontrivial isotropic subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n}
can be any value from the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

Given any linear subspace
W\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, we denote the orthogonal projection onto
W
by
P_{W}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2n}\to W
. Each isotropic subspace
V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n}
yields a semidirect group splitting of
\mathbb{H}
in two ways. Firstly, we obtain the Heisenberg group as the semidirect product
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{V}^{\perp}\rtimes\mathbb{V}
since each point
p\in\mathbb{H}
can be uniquely written as

p=\left(P_{V^{\perp}}(x,y),z-2\sigma(P_{V^{\perp}}(x,y),P_{V}(x,y))\right)\ast(P_{V}(x,y),0)

where
\left(P_{V^{\perp}}(x,y),z-2\sigma(P_{V^{\perp}}(x,y),P_{V}(x,y))\right)\in\mathbb{V}^{\perp}
and
(P_{V}(x,y),0)\in\mathbb{V}
.
This gives rise to well-defined horizontal and vertical projections

\displaystyle P_{\mathbb{V}}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{V},P_{\mathbb{V}}(p)=(P_{V}(x,y),0)

and

\displaystyle P_{\mathbb{V}^{\perp}}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{V}^{\perp},P_{\mathbb{V}^{\perp}}(p)=\left(P_{V^{\perp}}(x,y),z-2\sigma(P_{V^{\perp}}(x,y),P_{V}(x,y))\right).

On the other hand, we have that
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{V}\rtimes\mathbb{V}^{\perp}
, since each point
p
has a unique representation

p=(P_{V}(x,y),0)\ast\left(P_{V^{\perp}}(x,y),z+2\sigma(P_{V^{\perp}}(x,y),P_{V}(x,y))\right)

where

Q_{\mathbb{V}}(p):=(P_{V}(x,y),0)\in\mathbb{V}

and

Q_{\mathbb{V}^{\perp}}(p):=\left(P_{V^{\perp}}(x,y),z+2\sigma(P_{V^{\perp}}(x,y),P_{V}(x,y))\right)\in\mathbb{V}^{\perp}

determine the horizontal and vertical projections (respectively) with respect to this group splitting.
The horizontal projections are Lipschitz continuous and also group homomorphisms, whereas the vertical projections are neither.

For
1\leq d\leq n
we write

G_{h}(n,d)=\{V\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n}\mid V\text{ is a }d\text{-dimensional isotropic subspace of }\mathbb{R}^{2n}\}.

The set
G_{h}(n,d)
is called the isotropic Grassmannian and it is a submanifold of the usual Grassmannian manifold
G(2n,d)
consisting of all
d
-dimensional linear subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

A matrix
M\in M(2n,\mathbb{R})
is called symplectic, if it preserves the symplectic form
\sigma
. A matrix which is both symplectic and orthogonal is called orthosymplectic. Write

U(n)=\{M\in M(2n,\mathbb{R})\mid M\text{ is orthosymplectic}\}.

The set
U(n)
with matrix multiplication forms a group which acts transitively on
G_{h}(n,d)
(for the proof of this fact see
[
3
, Lemma 2.1]
). Note that since any
U\in U(n)
preserves both the form
\sigma
and the Euclidean inner product, the induced map
\widetilde{U}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
,
(x,y,z)\mapsto(U(x,y),z)

preserves both the Heisenberg norm and the metric.

Given
V\in G_{h}(n,d)
and
a pair of positive numbers
r=(r_{1},r_{2})
, we define the closed rectangle of type
1
centered at the origin to be the set

\displaystyle\overline{R}_{1,V}(0,r):=\left\{p^{\prime}\mid\|P_{\mathbb{V}}(p^{\prime})\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq r_{1},\ \|P_{\mathbb{V}^{\perp}}(p^{\prime})\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq r_{2}\right\},

and the closed rectangle of type
1
centred at
p
is defined by

\displaystyle\overline{R}_{1,V}(p,r)

\displaystyle:=p\overline{R}_{1,V}(0,r)

\displaystyle=\left\{p^{\prime}\mid\|P_{\mathbb{V}}(p^{-1}p^{\prime})\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq r_{1},\ \|P_{\mathbb{V}^{\perp}}(p^{-1}p^{\prime})\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq r_{2}\right\}.

We define rectangles with respect to the group splitting
\mathbb{H}=\mathbb{V}\rtimes\mathbb{V}^{\perp}
similarly with
Q
in place of
P
. More precisely, we let

\displaystyle\overline{R}_{2,V}(0,r):=\left\{p^{\prime}\mid\|Q_{\mathbb{V}}(p^{\prime})\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq r_{1},\ \|Q_{\mathbb{V}^{\perp}}(p^{\prime})\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq r_{2}\right\}

be the closed rectangle of type
2
centred at the origin and we define

\displaystyle\overline{R}_{2,V}(p,r)

\displaystyle:=p\overline{R}_{2,V}(0,r)

\displaystyle=\left\{p^{\prime}\mid\|Q_{\mathbb{V}}(p^{-1}p^{\prime})\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq r_{1},\ \|Q_{\mathbb{V}^{\perp}}(p^{-1}p^{\prime})\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq r_{2}\right\}

to be the closed rectangle of type
2
centred at
p
.

By the left-invariance of the metric and the relations

pP_{\mathbb{V}^{\perp}}(p^{-1}p^{\prime})P_{\mathbb{V}}(p^{-1}p^{\prime})=p^{\prime}=pQ_{\mathbb{V}}(p^{-1}p^{\prime})Q_{\mathbb{V}^{\perp}}(p^{-1}p^{\prime})

we obtain the following interpretations for the rectangles.
The rectangle
\overline{R}_{1,V}(p,r)
is the set of such points that can be reached from the center
p
by first moving "vertically" in
p\mathbb{V}^{\perp}
a distance at most
r_{2}
to end up at a point, say
q\in p\mathbb{V}^{\perp}
, and then moving "horizontally" in
q\mathbb{V}
a distance at most
r_{1}
. For the rectangle
\overline{R}_{2,V}(p,r)
we first move from the center "horizontally" in
p\mathbb{V}
a distance at most
r_{1}
and then "vertically" in
q\mathbb{V}^{\perp}
a distance at most
r_{2}
.

and

shed light into what the rectangles look like in the first Heisenberg group when
\mathbb{V}
is the

in some directions and much larger in some other directions provided that the

It is already evident from the rectangles above that the shape of a translated rectangle depends greatly on the center point
p
. When considering a rectangle centered at some arbitrary point, the following formulas are useful. For any

, it is true that

\displaystyle p\mathbb{V}

\displaystyle=\{p^{\prime}\mid p^{-1}p^{\prime}\in\mathbb{V}\}

\displaystyle=\{p^{\prime}\mid(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})-(x,y)\in V,z^{\prime}=z+2(\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle-\langle y,x^{\prime}\rangle)\}

and

\displaystyle p\mathbb{V}^{\perp}

\displaystyle=\{p^{\prime}\mid p^{-1}p^{\prime}\in\mathbb{V}^{\perp}\}=\{p^{\prime}\mid(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})-(x,y)\in V^{\perp}\}.

For an integer
d\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, a pair of positive numbers
r=(r_{1},r_{2})
and
t\in[0,2n+2]
we define the directed singular value functions
\Phi_{1}^{t}
and
\Phi_{2}^{t}
as follows: If
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
, let

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}^{t}(r,d)=\Phi_{2}^{t}(r,d)=\begin{cases}r_{2}^{t},&\text{ if }t\in[0,2n+2-d],\\
r_{1}^{t+d-2(n+1)}r_{2}^{2(n+1)-d},&\text{ if }t\in[2n+2-d,2n+2].\end{cases}

If
r_{1}\geq r_{2}
let

\displaystyle\Phi_{1}^{t}(r,d)=\begin{cases}r_{1}^{t},&\text{ if }t\in[0,d],\\
r_{1}^{\frac{t+d}{2}}r_{2}^{\frac{t-d}{2}},&\text{ if }t\in[d,d+2],\\
r_{1}^{d+1}r_{2}^{t-d-1},&\text{ if }t\in]d+2,2n+1[,\\
r_{1}^{2n+2+d-t}r_{2}^{2t-(2n+2+d)},&\text{ if }t\in[2n+1,2n+2],\end{cases}

and let

\displaystyle\Phi_{2}^{t}(r,d)=\begin{cases}r_{1}^{t},&\text{ if }t\in[0,d],\\
r_{1}^{d}r_{2}^{t-d},&\text{ if }t\in[d,2n+2].\end{cases}

Denote by
\lambda
the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{H}
and let
W\subseteq\mathbb{H}
be an open bounded subset of
\mathbb{H}
. Define the probability space
(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
by
\Omega=\mathbb{H}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathbb{P}=(\lambda_{W})^{\mathbb{N}}
, where
\lambda_{W}=\lambda(W)^{-1}\lambda_{|_{W}}
.

For a sequence
(d_{k})\in\{1,\ldots,n\}^{\mathbb{N}}
, a sequence
\underline{V}:=(V_{k})
of isotropic subspaces

, a sequence
\mathbf{j}:=(j_{k})\in\{1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}

of pairs of positive numbers and

, let

E_{\mathbf{j},\underline{V},\underline{r}}(\omega)=\limsup_{k\to\infty}\overline{R}_{j_{k},V_{k}}(\omega_{k},r_{k}).

for

-almost every

.

Remark 2.2
.

If

, then

for every

for some constant

for

3.
Hausdorff content and energy of rectangles

Our strategy in the proof of Theorem
2.1
will be as follows: In Proposition
3.2
, we obtain deterministic upper bounds for the Hausdorff content of rectangles, which we will later use to obtain an upper bound for the dimension of a limsup set generated by rectangles. In Proposition
3.3
, we obtain deterministic lower bounds for the capacity of rectangles. With the help of Theorem
4.1
, these lower bounds then turn into lower bounds for the dimension in Theorem
2.1
. Before establishing the upper and lower bounds, we recall the relevant definitions.

The
t
-dimensional Hausdorff content (with respect to the Heisenberg metric) of a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{H}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{t}(A)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\diam(C_{k})^{t}\ \Big|\ A\subseteq\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}C_{k}\right\},

where the diameter of a set
C\subseteq\mathbb{H}
in the Heisenberg metric is denoted by
\diam(C)
.
For
t>0
and a Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{H}
, the
t
-energy of
\mu
is defined by

\displaystyle I_{t}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\int\int d_{\mathbb{H}}(p,q)^{-t}\,d\mu(p)\,d\mu(q).

We define the
t
-energy of a Borel set
A\subseteq\mathbb{H}
to be the quantity
I_{t}(A)=I_{t}(\lambda_{|_{A}})
.

The
t
-capacity of a Borel set
A\subseteq\mathbb{H}
is defined by

\displaystyle\capa_{t}(A)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{I_{t}(\mu)^{-1}\mid\mu\in\mathcal{P}(A)\right\},

where
\mathcal{P}(A)
denotes the space of Borel probability measures on
\mathbb{H}
which are supported on
A
.

We have now defined the necessary concepts and we are ready to proceed with the proof.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
d\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and let
V,V^{\prime}\in G_{h}(n,d)
. Then for every
j
,
t
and
r
it is true that

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{t}\left(\overline{R}_{j,V}(0,r)\right)=\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{t}\left(\overline{R}_{j,V^{\prime}}(0,r)\right).

Proof.

We will first verify the claim for type
1
rectangles.
Since
U(n)
acts transitively on
G_{h}(n,d)
, there exists
U\in U(n)
such that
U(V^{\prime})=V
, which implies that also
U(V^{\prime\perp})=V^{\perp}
. Hence for any point
p=(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{H}
we have that

U(P_{V^{\prime}}(x,y))=P_{V}(U(x,y))\text{ and }U(P_{V^{\prime\perp}}(x,y))=P_{V^{\perp}}(U(x,y)).

Thus for the induced map
\widetilde{U}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
we obtain that

\displaystyle P_{\mathbb{V}}(\widetilde{U}(p))=(P_{V}(U(x,y)),0)=(U(P_{V^{\prime}}(x,y)),0)=\widetilde{U}(P_{\mathbb{V}^{\prime}}(p))

and (recalling that the form
\sigma
is invariant under
U
)

\displaystyle P_{\mathbb{V}^{\perp}}(\widetilde{U}(p))

\displaystyle=(P_{V^{\perp}}(U(x,y)),z-2\sigma(P_{V^{\perp}}(U(x,y)),P_{V}(U(x,y))))

\displaystyle=(U(P_{V^{\prime\perp}}(x,y)),z-2\sigma(U(P_{V^{\prime\perp}}(x,y)),U(P_{V^{\prime}}(x,y))))

\displaystyle=(U(P_{V^{\prime\perp}}(x,y)),z-2\sigma(P_{V^{\prime\perp}}(x,y),P_{V^{\prime}}(x,y)))

\displaystyle=\widetilde{U}(P_{\mathbb{V}^{\prime\perp}}(p)).

Thus
p\in\overline{R}_{1,V^{\prime}}(0,r)
if and only if
\widetilde{U}(p)\in\overline{R}_{1,V}(0,r)
. Since
\widetilde{U}
preserves the metric
d_{\mathbb{H}}
, it also leaves the Hausdorff content invariant and thus

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{t}\left(\overline{R}_{1,V^{\prime}}(0,r)\right)=\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{t}\left(\overline{R}_{1,V}(0,r)\right).

Repeating the same arguments for rectangles of type
2
completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 3.2
.

For every
j\in\{1,2\}
and
t\in[0,2n+2]
,

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{t}\left(\overline{R}_{j,V}(p,r)\right)\lesssim\Phi^{t}_{j}(r,d),

where the implicit constant depends on
n
,
t
,
d
and
j
, but not on the radius
r
, the center
p
or the subspace
V\in G_{h}(n,d)
determining the horizontal and vertical subgroups.

Proof.

We first consider the more delicate case of rectangles of type
1
. At the end of the proof we will see how similar arguments can be used to obtain the bounds also in the simpler case of type
2
rectangles.

Since the metric
d_{\mathbb{H}}
is left-invariant, the Hausdorff content
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{t}
is also left-invariant and we may assume that
p=(0,0,0)
.
By Lemma
3.1
, we may also assume that
V
is the isotropic subspace
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{0\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.
Write

R=\overline{R}_{1,V}(0,r)
.

For points
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we will from now on use the shorthand notations
u^{d}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d},0,\ldots,0)
and
u^{\perp}=(0,\ldots,0,u_{d+1},\ldots,u_{n})
. Then for
p=(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{H}
we have that

P_{V}(x,y)=(x^{d},0)\text{ and }P_{V^{\perp}}(x,y)=(x^{\perp},y).

The horizontal and vertical projections thus take the form

\displaystyle P_{\mathbb{V}}(p)=(P_{V}(x,y),0)=(x^{d},0,0)

and

\displaystyle P_{\mathbb{V}^{\perp}}(p)

\displaystyle=\left(P_{V^{\perp}}(x,y),z-2\sigma(P_{V^{\perp}}(x,y),P_{V}(x,y))\right)

\displaystyle=\left(x^{\perp},y,z-2\sigma((x^{\perp},y),(x^{d},0))\right)

\displaystyle=\left(x^{\perp},y,z+2\langle x^{d},y^{d}\rangle\right).

The definition of the rectangle now reads as

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\left\{p\mid\|P_{\mathbb{V}}(p)\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq r_{1},\ \|P_{\mathbb{V}^{\perp}}(p)\|_{\mathbb{H}}\leq r_{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{p\mid|x^{d}|\leq r_{1},\ |(x^{\perp},y)|^{4}+|z+2\langle x^{d},y^{d}\rangle|^{2}\leq r_{2}^{4}\right\}.

Observe that if
p\in R
, then
|(x,y)|\leq\sqrt{2}\max(r_{1},r_{2})
and

\displaystyle|z|\leq r_{2}^{2}+2|\langle x^{d},y^{d}\rangle|\leq r_{2}^{2}+2|x^{d}||y^{d}|\leq 3\max(r_{1}^{2},r_{2}^{2})

hence

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{t}(R)\leq(\diam(R))^{t}\lesssim\max(r_{1}^{t},r_{2}^{t}).

Thus, it suffices to show that if
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
then

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{t}(R)\lesssim r_{1}^{t+d-2(n+1)}r_{2}^{2(n+1)-d}

and if
r_{1}\geq r_{2}
then

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{t}(R)\lesssim\min\left(r_{1}^{\frac{t+d}{2}}r_{2}^{\frac{t-d}{2}},r_{1}^{d+1}r_{2}^{t-d-1},r_{1}^{2n+2+d-t}r_{2}^{2t-(2n+2+d)}\right).

Suppose first that
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
. Then given any points
p\in R
and
p^{\prime}\in\overline{B}(p,r_{1})
we have the estimates

|x^{\prime d}|\leq|x^{d}|+|x^{\prime d}-x^{d}|\leq 2r_{1},\ |(x^{\prime\perp},y^{\prime})|\leq r_{1}+r_{2}\leq 2r_{2}

and

\displaystyle|z^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\leq|z^{\prime}-z-2(\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle-\langle y,x^{\prime}\rangle)|+|z|+2(|x||y^{\prime}|+|y||x^{\prime}|)

\displaystyle\leq r_{1}^{2}+3r_{2}^{2}+2((|x^{d}|+|x^{\perp}|)|y^{\prime}|+|y|(|x^{\prime d}|+|x^{\prime\perp}|))\leq 20r_{2}^{2}.

Thus

\bigcup_{p\in R}\overline{B}(p,r_{1})\subseteq\{p^{\prime}\mid|x^{\prime d}|\leq 2r_{1},|(x^{\prime\perp},y)|\leq 2r_{2},|z^{\prime}|\leq 20r_{2}^{2}\}=:A.

Note that the Lebesgue measure of this set is
\lambda(A)\approx r_{2}^{2(n+1)-d}r_{1}^{d}
.

Let
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
be a maximal collection of points with the properties that
p_{i}\in R
for each
i
and
d_{\mathbb{H}}(p_{i},p_{j})\geq r_{1}
for
i\neq j
. Then the balls
B(p_{i},r_{1}/2)
are disjoint and

R\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}\overline{B}(p_{i},r_{1})\subseteq A.

Using the fact that
\lambda(B(p,\epsilon))=\lambda(B(0,\epsilon))\approx\epsilon^{2n+2}
for all
p\in\mathbb{H}
and
\epsilon>0
we obtain that

\displaystyle M\lambda(B(0,r_{1}/2))

\displaystyle=\lambda\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}B(p_{i},r_{1}/2)\right)\leq\lambda(A)\approx r_{2}^{2(n+1)-d}r_{1}^{d}

which in turn yields

M\lesssim\frac{r_{2}^{2(n+1)-d}r_{1}^{d}}{r_{1}^{2n+2}}=\left(\frac{r_{2}}{r_{1}}\right)^{2(n+1)-d}.

Since
R\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}\overline{B}(p_{i},r_{1})
we obtain the bound

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{t}(R)\lesssim\left(\frac{r_{2}}{r_{1}}\right)^{2(n+1)-d}r_{1}^{t}=r_{1}^{t+d-2(n+1)}r_{2}^{2(n+1)-d}

as desired.

We now consider the case
r_{1}\geq r_{2}
. We will first establish the bound

(1)

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{t}(R)\lesssim r_{1}^{\frac{t+d}{2}}r_{2}^{\frac{t-d}{2}}.

To this end, we estimate the distance between a point
p\in R
and its horizontal projection
(x^{d},0,0)=P_{\mathbb{V}}(p)\in\mathbb{V}
. Observe now that for
p\in R
it holds that

\displaystyle|z-2\langle x^{d},y^{d}\rangle|\leq|z+2\langle x^{d},y^{d}\rangle|+4|\langle x^{d},y^{d}\rangle|\leq r_{2}^{2}+4r_{1}r_{2}\leq 5r_{1}r_{2}.

Hence we obtain that

\displaystyle d_{\mathbb{H}}(P_{\mathbb{V}}(p),p)

\displaystyle=\left(|(x^{\perp},y)|^{4}+|z-2\langle x^{d},y^{d}\rangle|^{2}\right)^{1/4}\leq\left(r_{2}^{4}+(5r_{1}r_{2})^{2}\right)^{1/4}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt[4]{26}(r_{1}r_{2})^{1/2}\leq 3(r_{1}r_{2})^{1/2}.

Now cover the Euclidean ball
\overline{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,r_{1})
with

M\lesssim\left(r_{1}/\sqrt{r_{1}r_{2}}\right)^{d}=(r_{1}/r_{2})^{d/2}

Euclidean balls
B_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(p_{i}^{\prime},\sqrt{r_{1}r_{2}})
. Then the points
p_{i}:=(p_{i}^{\prime},0,0,0)\in\mathbb{V}.
Given
p\in R
there exists an index
j\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
such that

\sqrt{r_{1}r_{2}}\geq|x^{d}-(p_{j}^{\prime},0)|=d_{\mathbb{H}}(P_{\mathbb{V}}(p),p_{j})

hence

d_{\mathbb{H}}(p,p_{j})\leq 4(r_{1}r_{2})^{1/2}.
This yields the desired bound

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{t}(R)\lesssim(r_{1}/r_{2})^{d/2}(r_{1}r_{2})^{t/2}=r_{1}^{\frac{t+d}{2}}r_{2}^{\frac{t-d}{2}}.

To obtain the remaining upper bounds we will use similar "volume-based" arguments as we did in the previous case
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
. Consider a radius
\varrho\in[r_{2}^{2}/r_{1},r_{2}]
and suppose that
p\in R
and
p^{\prime}\in B(p,\varrho)
.
Then
|x^{\prime d}|\leq r_{1}+\varrho\leq 2r_{1}
,
|(x^{\prime\perp},y^{\prime})|\leq r_{2}+\varrho\leq 2r_{2}
and

\displaystyle|z^{\prime}+2\langle x^{\prime d},y^{\prime d}\rangle|\leq\

\displaystyle|z^{\prime}-z-2(\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle-\langle y,x^{\prime}\rangle)|+|z+2\langle x^{d},y^{d}\rangle|

\displaystyle+2|\langle x^{\prime d},y^{\prime d}\rangle-\langle x^{d},y^{d}\rangle+\langle x,y^{\prime}\rangle-\langle y,x^{\prime}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\varrho^{2}+r_{2}^{2}+2|\langle x^{\prime d},y^{\prime d}\rangle-\langle x^{d},y^{d}\rangle+\langle x^{d},y^{\prime d}\rangle-\langle y^{d},x^{\prime d}\rangle|

\displaystyle+2|\langle x^{\perp},y^{\prime\perp}\rangle-\langle y^{\perp},x^{\prime\perp}\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\varrho^{2}+r_{2}^{2}+2|\langle x^{\prime d}+x^{d},y^{\prime d}-y^{d}\rangle|

\displaystyle+2(|x^{\perp}||y^{\prime\perp}|+|y^{\perp}||x^{\prime\perp}|)

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\varrho^{2}+r_{2}^{2}+6r_{1}\varrho+8r_{2}^{2}\leq 16r_{1}\varrho.

Thus

\displaystyle\bigcup_{p\in R}\overline{B}(p,\varrho)

\displaystyle\subseteq\{p^{\prime}\mid|x^{\prime d}|\leq 2r_{1},|(x^{\prime\perp},y^{\prime})|\leq 2r_{2},|z^{\prime}+2\langle x^{\prime d},y^{\prime d}\rangle|\leq 16r_{1}\varrho\}

\displaystyle=:A_{\varrho}.

Note that
\lambda(A_{\varrho})\approx r_{1}^{d+1}r_{2}^{2n-d}\varrho
. The argument used above in the case
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
implies that we can cover
R
with
M
balls of radius
r_{2}
, or with
N
balls of radius
r_{2}^{2}/r_{1}
, where

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{r_{1}^{d+1}r_{2}^{2n-d}r_{2}}{r_{2}^{2n+2}}=\left(\frac{r_{1}}{r_{2}}\right)^{d+1}

and

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{r_{1}^{d+1}r_{2}^{2n-d}r_{2}^{2}/r_{1}}{(r_{2}^{2}/r_{1})^{2n+2}}=\left(\frac{r_{1}}{r_{2}}\right)^{2n+2+d}.

Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{t}(R)

\displaystyle\lesssim\min\left(\left(\frac{r_{1}}{r_{2}}\right)^{d+1}r_{2}^{t},\left(\frac{r_{1}}{r_{2}}\right)^{2n+2+d}r_{2}^{2t}r_{1}^{-t}\right)

\displaystyle=\min\left(r_{1}^{d+1}r_{2}^{t-d-1},r_{1}^{2n+2+d-t}r_{2}^{2t-(2n+2+d)}\right)

and the proof of the upper bound for rectangles of type
1
is complete.

We now consider the case of rectangles of type
2
. Proceeding as we did in the previous case we may once again assume that the center
p
is the origin and that
V=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{0\}
. The rectangle then takes the form

\displaystyle R^{\prime}:=\overline{R}_{2,V}(0,r)

\displaystyle=\left\{p\mid|x^{d}|\leq r_{1},\ |(x^{\perp},y)|^{4}+|z-2\langle x^{d},y^{d}\rangle|^{2}\leq r_{2}^{4}\right\}.

Note that by the argument given in the previous case we still have the same trivial bounds as before, that is,

\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{t}(R^{\prime})\leq|R^{\prime}|^{t}\lesssim\max(r_{1}^{t},r_{2}^{t}).
In the case
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
we proceed exactly as we did for the type
1
rectangles and we find that

\bigcup_{p\in R^{\prime}}\overline{B}(p,r_{1})\subseteq A,

where
A
is the set defined in the type
1
situation. Thus, we obtain the same upper bound as for the type
1
rectangles and the case
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
is proved. In the case
r_{1}\geq r_{2}
we follow similar arguments as we did to establish the upper bound (
1
). For a point
p\in R^{\prime}
we have from the definition of
R^{\prime}
that

\displaystyle d_{\mathbb{H}}(P_{\mathbb{V}}(p),p)

\displaystyle=\left(|(x^{\perp},y)|^{4}+|z-2\langle x^{d},y^{d}\rangle|^{2}\right)^{1/4}\leq r_{2}.

Thus by covering the Euclidean ball
\overline{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,r_{1})
with
M^{\prime}\lesssim\left(r_{1}/r_{2}\right)^{d}
Euclidean balls
\overline{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(p_{i}^{d},r_{2})
we see as before that for any
p\in R^{\prime}
there exists an index
i\in\{1,\ldots,M^{\prime}\}
such that
d_{\mathbb{H}}(p,p_{i})\leq 2r_{2}
, where
p_{i}
is defined in the same way as in the proof of (
1
). This yields the bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\infty}^{t}(R^{\prime})\lesssim r_{1}^{d}r_{2}^{t-d}

and the proposition is completely proved.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

For every isotropic subspace
V\in G_{h}(n,d)
, a pair of positive numbers
r=(r_{1},r_{2})
and for every
t\in]0,2n+2[\setminus\mathbb{Z}
we have the following:

If
r_{1}\geq r_{2}
,
d=1
and
t\in]3,2n+1[\setminus\mathbb{Z}
, then for every
s\in]\lfloor t\rfloor,t[
we have that

(2)

\displaystyle\capa_{s}(\overline{R}_{1,V}(0,r))\gtrsim\Phi^{t}_{1}(r,d)r_{1}^{s-t}.

For all other possible choices of
V\in G_{h}(n,d)
,
j\in\{1,2\}
,
r
and
t
we have that

(3)

\displaystyle\capa_{t}(\overline{R}_{j,V}(0,r))\gtrsim\Phi^{t}_{j}(r,d).

The implicit constants above depend on
n
,
t
,
s
,
j
and
d
, but not on
r
or
V\in G_{h}(n,d)
.

Proof.

As in the proof of Proposition
3.2
we begin by considering the more difficult case of type
1
rectangles. We will obtain the desired results for the rectangles of type
2
at the end of the proof.

Let
R=\overline{R}_{1,V}(0,r)
. Directly from the definition of capacity we obtain that

\capa_{t}(R)\geq\frac{\lambda(R)^{2}}{I_{t}(R)}.

Recall that
d_{\mathbb{H}}
and
\lambda
are invariant under the map
\widetilde{U}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
induced by
U\in U(n)
. Also, we recall from the proof of Proposition (
3.2
) that
U(n)
acts transitively on
G_{h}(n,d)
and if
U\in U(n)
maps
V^{\prime}
to
V
, then the induced map
\widetilde{U}
maps
\overline{R}_{1,V^{\prime}}(0,r)
to
\overline{R}_{1,V}(0,r)
. Thus, to estimate the quantity
\frac{\lambda(R)^{2}}{I_{t}(R)}
, we may again assume that
V=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{0\}
. The rectangle is then again the set

\displaystyle R

\displaystyle=\left\{p\mid|x^{d}|\leq r_{1},\ |(x^{\perp},y)|^{4}+|z+2\langle x^{d},y^{d}\rangle|^{2}\leq r_{2}^{4}\right\}.

Observe that for any fixed
x^{d}
with
|x^{d}|\leq r_{1}
, the slice

\{p^{\prime}\in R\mid x^{\prime d}=x^{d}\}

has Lebesgue measure comparable to
r_{2}^{2n+2-d}
. Hence, Fubini’s theorem implies that
\lambda(R)\approx r_{1}^{d}r_{2}^{2n+2-d}
.
We thus obtain the bound

(4)

\capa_{t}(R)\gtrsim\frac{r_{1}^{2d}r_{2}^{4n+4-2d}}{I_{t}(R)}.

In the following, we will show that if
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
, then

\displaystyle I_{t}(R)\lesssim\begin{cases}r_{1}^{2d}r_{2}^{4n+4-2d-t}&\text{ if }t\in]0,2n+2-d[\setminus\mathbb{Z},\\
r_{1}^{2n+2+d-t}r_{2}^{2n+2-d}&\text{ if }t\in]2n+2-d,2n+2[\setminus\mathbb{Z},\end{cases}

and if
r_{1}\geq r_{2}
, then

\displaystyle I_{t}(R)\lesssim\begin{cases}r_{1}^{2d-t}r_{2}^{4n+4-2d}&\text{ if }t\in]0,d[\setminus\mathbb{Z},\\
r_{1}^{\frac{3d-t}{2}}r_{2}^{\frac{8n+8-3d-t}{2}}&\text{ if }t\in]d,d+2[\setminus\mathbb{Z},\\
r_{1}^{d-1}r_{2}^{4n+5-d-t}&\text{ if }t\in]d+2,2n+1[\setminus\mathbb{Z}\text{ and }d\geq 2,\\
r_{2}^{4n+4-t}\log\left(\frac{2r_{1}}{r_{2}}\right)&\text{ if }t\in]3,2n+1[\setminus\mathbb{Z}\text{ and }d=1,\\
r_{1}^{t+d-2n-2}r_{2}^{6n+6-d-2t}&\text{ if }t\in]2n+1,2n+2[.\end{cases}

Apart from the bound involving the logarithm, the rest of the above bounds combined with (
4
) clearly imply (
3
). We show that (
2
) follows from the logarithm bound at the end of this proof.

Note that
d_{\mathbb{H}}
and
\lambda
are invariant under the map
\widetilde{U}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
induced by
U\in U(n)
. Recall from the proof of Proposition (
3.2
) that
U(n)
acts transitively on
G_{h}(n,d)
and if
U\in U(n)
maps
V^{\prime}
to
V
, then the induced map
\widetilde{U}
maps
\overline{R}_{1,V^{\prime}}(0,r)
to
\overline{R}_{1,V}(0,r)
. Thus, to estimate
I_{t}(R)
, we may again assume that
V=\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{0\}
.
Let

R_{t}(p)=\int_{R}d_{\mathbb{H}}(p,q)^{-t}\,\lambda(q)

so that

I_{t}(R)=\int_{R}R_{t}(p)\,d\lambda(p)
.
Let
p=(\rho,y_{0},z_{0})\in R
and for
q=(x,y,z)
let

\displaystyle g_{p}(q)

\displaystyle=\max\left(|x^{d}-\rho^{d}|,|(x^{\perp},y)-(\rho^{\perp},y_{0})|,|z-z_{0}-2(\langle\rho,y\rangle-\langle y_{0},x\rangle)|^{1/2}\right).

Note that

R\subseteq\{q\mid|x^{d}|\leq r_{1},|(x^{\perp},y)|\leq r_{2},|z+2\langle x^{d},y^{d}\rangle|\leq r_{2}^{2}\}=:A.

For
a\geq 0
define
B(a):=\{q\mid g_{p}(q)\leq a\}
. Since
g_{p}(q)\approx d_{\mathbb{H}}(p,q)
, we obtain that

\displaystyle R_{t}(p)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{A}g_{p}(q)^{-t}\,d\lambda(q)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda(A\cap\{q\mid g_{p}(q)^{-t}\geq a\})\,da

\displaystyle\approx\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda(A\cap B(a))a^{-(t+1)}da.

We must now estimate the Lebesgue measure of the set

\displaystyle A\cap B(a)

\displaystyle=\left\{q\,\bigg|\begin{array}[]{cl}&|x^{d}|\leq r_{1},|(x^{\perp},y)|\leq r_{2},|z+2\langle x^{d},y^{d}\rangle|\leq r_{2}^{2},|x^{d}-\rho^{d}|\leq a,\\
&|(x^{\perp},y)-(\rho^{\perp},y_{0})|\leq a,|z-z_{0}-2(\langle\rho,y\rangle-\langle y_{0},x\rangle)|\leq a^{2}\end{array}\right\}.

Suppose first that
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
and let
t\in]0,2n+2[\setminus\mathbb{Z}
.
Note that for any fixed
(x,y)
, the
z
-coordinate of a point
q\in A\cap B(a)
must come from an interval of length
\min(2r_{2}^{2},2a^{2})
. Taking into account the constraints on the first
2n
-coordinates, Fubini’s theorem yields

\displaystyle\lambda(A\cap B(a))

\displaystyle\lesssim\min\left(a^{2}a^{2n-d}a^{d},a^{2}a^{2n-d}r_{1}^{d},r_{2}^{2}r_{2}^{2n-d}r_{1}^{d}\right)

\displaystyle=\min\left(a^{2n+2},r_{1}^{d}a^{2n+2-d},r_{1}^{d}r_{2}^{2n+2-d}\right).

We thus obtain

\displaystyle R_{t}(p)

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{0}^{r_{1}}a^{2n+2}a^{-(t+1)}\,da+\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}r_{1}^{d}a^{2n+2-d}a^{-(t+1)}\,da

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{r_{2}}^{\infty}r_{1}^{d}r_{2}^{2n+2-d}a^{-(t+1)}\,da

\displaystyle\lesssim\max\left(r_{1}^{2n+2-t},r_{1}^{d}r_{2}^{2n+2-d-t}\right).

Hence

\displaystyle I_{t}(R)

\displaystyle\lesssim\lambda(R)\max\left(r_{1}^{2n+2-t},r_{1}^{d}r_{2}^{2n+2-d-t}\right)

\displaystyle\approx\max\left(r_{1}^{2n+2+d-t}r_{2}^{2n+2-d},r_{1}^{2d}r_{2}^{4n+4-2d-t}\right)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}r_{1}^{2d}r_{2}^{4n+4-2d-t}&\text{ if }t\leq 2n+2-d,\\
r_{1}^{2n+2+d-t}r_{2}^{2n+2-d}&\text{ if }t\geq 2n+2-d.\end{cases}

The case
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
is thus proved.

Suppose then that
r_{1}\geq r_{2}
and let
t\in]0,2n+2[
. In this case we need a more careful estimate for the Lebesgue measure of

A\cap B(a).
We collect the required upper bounds in the following sublemma.

Sublemma 1
.

For every
a\geq 0
we have that

\lambda(A\cap B(a))\lesssim\min\left(a^{2n+2},a^{d}r_{2}^{2n+2-d},r_{1}^{d}r_{2}^{2n+2-d}\right).

If
0<a\leq|\rho^{d}|
, then we also have the bound

\lambda(A\cap B(a))\lesssim\min\left(\frac{a^{2n+1}r_{2}^{2}}{|\rho^{d}|},\frac{a^{d+2}r_{2}^{2n+1-d}}{|\rho^{d}|}\right).

Proof.

In the proof of this lemma, to make reading less cumbersome, we deviate from our usual notation slightly and we write
x^{d}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
instead of
x^{d}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d},0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and similarly we write

x^{\perp}=(x_{d+1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n-d}
.

For every
a\geq 0
, we note as before that for any fixed first
2n
coordinates
(x,y)
, the
z
-coordinate of a point
q\in A\cap B(a)
must belong to the intersection of two intervals of lengths
2r_{2}^{2}
and
2a^{2}
, respectively. Noting also that then

x^{d}\in\overline{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(0,r_{1})\cap\overline{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\rho^{d},a)

and

(x^{\perp},y)\in\overline{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{2n-d}}(0,r_{2})\cap\overline{B}_{\mathbb{R}^{2n-d}}((\rho^{\perp},y_{0}),a),

Fubini’s theorem yields

\displaystyle\lambda(A\cap B(a))

\displaystyle r_{2}^{2}+a^{2}-z_{0}-2(\langle\rho,y\rangle-\langle y_{0},x\rangle+\langle\widehat{x},\widehat{y}\rangle).
