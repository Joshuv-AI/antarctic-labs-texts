Mean value formulas on surfaces in Grushin spaces
Valentina Franceschi ∗, Roberto Monti ∗, Alessandro Socionovo †

Abstract
. We prove (sub)mean value formulas at the point 0 ∈ Σ 0\in\Sigma for (sub)harmonic functions on a hypersurface Σ ⊂ ℝ n + 1 \Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1} where the differentiable structure and the surface measure depend on the ambient Grushin structure.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Main: 35B05. Secondary: 35H20, 49Q05.

1.
Introduction

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha>0
, we consider the vector fields on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

(1.1)

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle X_{i}=\frac{\partial}{\partial{x_{i}}},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,\quad\displaystyle X_{n+1}=|x|^{\alpha}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}.\end{array}

Here, a generic point in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is denoted by
\xi=(x,y)=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. We also consider the second order partial differential operator on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
given by

(1.2)

\mathscr{L}\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}X_{i}^{2}\varphi=\Delta_{x}\varphi+|x|^{2\alpha}\partial^{2}_{y}\varphi,

where
|x|=(x_{1}^{2}+\ldots+x_{n}^{2})^{1/2}
.
The operator
\mathscr{L}
in (
1.2
) is known as Baouendi-Grushin operator, see
[
9
]
for a historical account and see also
[
7
]
.

When
\alpha
is an even integer, this operator is hypoelliptic and
admits a fundamental solution with pole at any point
\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
(see
[
1
]
for an explicit representation). When
\xi_{0}=0
,
an explicit formula for this fundamental solution is in fact known for any
\alpha>0
(see
[
8
]
) and, up to a normalization constant, it is the function
\Gamma(\xi)=\varrho(\xi)^{1-n-\alpha}
,
\xi\neq 0
, where
\varrho:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}
is the gauge function

(1.3)

\varrho(\xi)=\big(|x|^{2(\alpha+1)}+(\alpha+1)^{2}y^{2}\big)^{\frac{1}{2(\alpha+1)}}.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a hypersurface of class
C^{2}
with
0\in\Sigma
.
We declare the vector-fields (
1.1
)
orthonormal and we project them onto the tangent space to
\Sigma
, getting tangential operators
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n+1}
.
We fix on
\Sigma
the hypersurface measure
\sigma
associated with (
1.1
) according to the theory of sub-elliptic perimeters and then we define the adjoint operators

\delta_{1}^{*},\ldots,\delta_{n+1}^{*}
with respect to
\sigma
. The natural restriction of
\mathscr{L}
to
\Sigma
is the differential operator

\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}=-\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\delta_{i}^{*}\delta_{i}.

In this paper, we investigate the validity of mean value formulas (sub-mean value formulas) at the point
0\in\Sigma
for functions
f\in C^{2}(\Sigma)
satisfying

\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}f=0
(
\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}f\geq 0
, respectively).

The operator
\mathscr{L}
is an example of “sub-Laplacian” or “sum of squares of vector fields”
satisfying the Hörmander condition
[
10
]
. When
\mathscr{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}^{2}
is such an operator in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, the validity of mean-value formulas for
\mathscr{L}
-harmonic functions
is established in
[
4
,
3
,
5
]
. Denoting
by

\Gamma(\cdot,\xi_{0})
the fundamental solution for
\mathscr{L}
with pole at
\xi_{0}
,
if a function
f
satisfies
\mathscr{L}f=0
then for any
r>0
and
\xi_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

(1.4)

f(\xi_{0})=\frac{1}{r}\int_{\Omega_{r}(\xi_{0})}f(\xi)K(\xi,\xi_{0})\;d\xi,

where
\Omega_{r}(\xi_{0})=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\Gamma(\xi,\xi_{0})>1/r\}
and

K(\xi,\xi_{0})={|X\Gamma(\xi,\xi_{0})|^{2}}/{\Gamma(\xi,\xi_{0})^{2}}
, with
|X\Gamma(\cdot,\xi_{0})|^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}(X_{i}\Gamma(\cdot,\xi_{0}))^{2}
.
The appearance of the kernel
K
is due to the different symmetry of Carnot-Carathéodory balls associated with the vector-fields building up
\mathscr{L}
and level sets of
\Gamma(\cdot,\xi_{0})
.

In the Riemannian case, the validity of mean-value formulas on metric balls for harmonic functions leads to the notion of “harmonic manifold”.
Starting probably with
[
11
]
, there exists a huge literature on the problem of characterizing harmonic manifolds and it is not possible to give a full account, here. In fact, our hypersurface
\Sigma
embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with the Grushin structure is not a Riemannian manifold but rather a weighted
Riemannian manifold that becomes singular at the point
0\in\Sigma
, see Remark
2.1
.

In the Grushin space, the harmonicity at
0\in\Sigma
is governed by the following structural function
q_{\Sigma}:\Sigma\setminus\{0\}\to\mathbb{R}

(1.5)

q_{\Sigma}(\xi)=\langle X\varrho,\nu\rangle\Big[(n+3\alpha)\langle X\log\varrho,\nu\rangle-2\alpha\langle\nabla_{x}\log|x|,\bar{\nu}\rangle+nH_{\Sigma}\Big].

Above,
X\varrho=(X_{1}\varrho,\ldots,X_{n+1}\varrho)
is the
X
-gradient of the gauge function
\varrho
,
\nu=(\bar{\nu},\nu_{n+1})
is the
\alpha
-normal to
\Sigma
,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
are standard scalar products in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
H_{\Sigma}
is the mean curvature of
\Sigma
associated with the Grushin structure.
We say that
\Sigma
is
\alpha
-harmonic if
q_{\Sigma}=0
. In particular, any homogeneous hypersurface,
\langle X\varrho,\nu\rangle=0
, is
\alpha
-harmonic, as we show in Section
5.1
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be an
\alpha
-harmonic hypersurface of class
C^{2}
with
0\in\Sigma
.
Any function
f\in C^{2}(\Sigma)
such that
\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}f=0

satisfies the mean-value formula at
0

(1.6)

f(0)=\frac{C_{\Sigma,n,\alpha}}{r^{n+\alpha}}\int_{B_{r}\cap\Sigma}f(\xi)\,|\delta\varrho(\xi)|^{2}d\sigma,

for all
r\in(0,r_{0})
and for some
r_{0}>0
depending on
\Sigma
.
The constant

0<C_{\Sigma,n,\alpha}<\infty
is defined by

(1.7)

\frac{1}{C_{\Sigma,n,\alpha}}=\frac{1}{r^{n+\alpha}}\int_{B_{r}\cap\Sigma}|\delta\varrho(\xi)|^{2}d\sigma,

where the right hand-side does not depend on
r\in(0,r_{0})
.

Above, the balls are

(1.8)

B_{r}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\varrho(\xi)<r\}.

and
|\delta\varrho|\leq 1
is the length of the tangential gradient of
\varrho
. When
\Sigma
is homogeneous, the kernel is
|\delta\varrho|^{2}=|x|^{2\alpha}/\varrho^{2\alpha}
.
In the case of
\alpha
-subharmonic hypersurfaces,
q_{\Sigma}\geq 0
, the statement is similar.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be an
\alpha
-subharmonic hypersurface of class
C^{2}
with
0\in\Sigma
.
Any function
f\in C^{2}(\Sigma)
such that
\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}f\geq 0

satisfies the following sub-mean-value formula at
0

(1.9)

f(0)\leq\frac{C_{\Sigma,n,\alpha}}{r^{n+\alpha}}\int_{B_{r}\cap\Sigma}f(\xi)\,|\delta\varrho(\xi)|^{2}d\sigma,

for all
r\in(0,r_{0})
and for some
r_{0}>0
depending on
\Sigma
. The constant

0<C_{\Sigma,n,\alpha}<\infty
is defined by

(1.10)

\frac{1}{C_{\Sigma,n,\alpha}}=\lim_{r\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{r^{n+\alpha}}\int_{B_{r}\cap\Sigma}|\delta\varrho(\xi)|^{2}d\sigma.

The operator
\mathscr{L}
in (
1.2
) and the hyper-surface measure
\sigma
are invariant with respect to the vertical translations
(x,y)\mapsto(x,y+y_{0})
, for any fixed
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. Theorem
1.2
can be therefore extended to get mean value formulas at points
(0,y_{0})\in\Sigma
also with
y_{0}\neq 0
.
Obtaining mean value formulas at points
(x_{0},y_{0})\in\Sigma
with
x_{0}\neq 0
is, instead, difficult because our knowledge of the fundamental solution of
\mathscr{L}
with pole at
(x_{0},y_{0})
with
x_{0}\neq 0
is not explicit enough.

Our interest in sub-mean value formulas on hypersurfaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
endowed with a system of Hörmander vector fields comes from the theory of minimal surfaces.
One of the key tools in
Bombieri-De Giorgi-Miranda’s proof
of the gradient estimate is the sub-mean value property for sub-harmonic functions on minimal surfaces of the Euclidean space, see
[
2
]
.
In our setting, a minimal surface is defined by
H_{\Sigma}=0
. This condition simplifies the structural function
q_{\Sigma}
, however, this is not sufficient to have
q_{\Sigma}\geq 0
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we recall the basic definitions of the measure
\sigma
, of the
\alpha
-normal
\nu
of
\Sigma
, and of mean curvature
H_{\Sigma}
.
In Section
3
, we introduce the various differential operators and we develop a calculus on radial functions.
The explicit computations of second order derivatives of
\varrho
is crucial, here. In Section
4
, we prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.
Finally, in Section
5
we study the structural function
q_{\Sigma}
.

2.
Perimeter and mean curvature of hypersurfaces

The
\alpha
-perimeter
of a Lebesgue measurable set

E\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
in an open set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is

(2.1)

P_{\alpha}(E;A)=\sup\left\{\int_{E}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}X_{i}\varphi_{i}(\xi)\,d\xi\,:\,\varphi\in C_{c}^{1}(A;\mathbb{R}^{n+1}),\,\displaystyle\max_{\xi\in A}|\varphi(\xi)|\leq 1\right\}.

We are using the Lebesgue measure
d\xi=d\mathscr{L}^{n+1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.
When the boundary of
E
is locally the graph of a Lipschitz function, its

\alpha
-perimeter
has the following integral representation
(see
[
6
, Proposition 2.1]
)

(2.2)

P_{\alpha}(E;A)=\int_{\partial E\cap A}\sqrt{|\bar{N}|^{2}+|x|^{2\alpha}|N^{\prime}|^{2}}\,d\mathscr{H}^{n},

where
N(\xi)=(\bar{N}(\xi),N^{\prime}(\xi))\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
is the Euclidean outer unit normal to
\partial E
at the point
\xi
, and

\mathscr{H}^{n}
is the standard
n
-dimensional Hausdorff measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. On top of its appearance as a sub-Riemannian perimeter, the relevance of the perimeter
P_{\alpha}
is due to its relation with the Heisenberg perimeter.
When
\alpha=1
and
n
is even, then the Heisenberg perimeter of a set with cylindrical symmetry coincides
with its
\alpha
-perimeter, see e.g.,
[
6
, Proposition 2.3]
.

Motivated by (
2.2
),
when
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is an orientable hypersurface that is locally a Lipschitz graph and
N=(\bar{N},N^{\prime})
is
its Euclidean normal, we call the Borel measure on
\Sigma

(2.3)

\sigma=\sqrt{|\bar{N}|^{2}+|x|^{2\alpha}|N^{\prime}|^{2}}\,\mathscr{H}^{n}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\Sigma.

the
\alpha
-perimeter measure of
\Sigma
.

The regular part of
\Sigma
is the set
\Sigma^{*}=\{\xi=(x,y)\in\Sigma:x\neq 0\}
.
At
\mathscr{H}^{n}
-a.e. point
\xi\in\Sigma^{*}
we can define the
\alpha
-
normal
of
\Sigma
as the vector field

\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\nu_{i}X_{i}
with

(2.4)

\begin{split}&\nu_{i}=\frac{N_{i}}{\sqrt{|\bar{N}|^{2}+|x|^{2\alpha}|N^{\prime}|^{2}}},\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,n,\\
&\nu_{n+1}=\frac{aN_{n+1}}{\sqrt{|\bar{N}|^{2}+|x|^{2\alpha}|N^{\prime}|^{2}}}.\end{split}

With abuse of notation, we identify
\nu
with the mapping
\nu:\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
given by the vector of its coordinates
\nu(\xi)=(\nu_{1}(\xi),\ldots,\nu_{n+1}(\xi))\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
for
\xi\in\Sigma^{*}
.

Remark 2.1

(Comparison with the Riemannian structure)
.

The hypersurface measure
\sigma
and the
\alpha
-normal
\nu
can be interpreted in the following Riemannian terms.
The tensor metric in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{x=0\}
making
X_{1},\dots,X_{n+1}
orthonormal is

(2.5)

g_{\alpha}(\xi)=\begin{pmatrix}I_{n}&0\\
0&|x|^{-2\alpha}\end{pmatrix},\quad x\neq 0.

When
x=0
the metric is not defined.
The Riemannian volume associated with
g_{\alpha}
is the measure
\mu=|x|^{-\alpha}\mathscr{L}^{n+1}
and is singular at
x=0
.
The Riemannian surface area associated with
g_{\alpha}
of a hypersurface
\Sigma
is the measure

\mu_{\Sigma}=|x|^{-\alpha}\sqrt{|\bar{N}|^{2}+|x|^{2\alpha}|N^{\prime}|^{2}}\mathscr{H}^{n}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\Sigma,

where
N=(\bar{N},N^{\prime})
is the Euclidean unit normal. We deduce that
Lebesgue measure and
\alpha
-perimeter are weighted
Riemannian volume and hypersurface measures with the same weight:

\mathscr{L}^{n+1}=|x|^{\alpha}\mu\quad\textrm{and}\quad\sigma=|x|^{\alpha}\mu_{\Sigma}.

We now focus on the case of graphs. Let

\Sigma=\Sigma_{u}=\{\xi=(x,u(x))\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:x\in\Omega\}
be the
y
-graph of a function
u\in C^{1}(\Omega)
for some open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We shall assume that
0\in\Omega
and let
\Omega^{*}=\Omega\setminus\{0\}
.
The
\alpha
-
unit normal
to
\Sigma_{u}
at points in
\Sigma_{u}^{*}
is the mapping
\nu=(\bar{\nu},\nu_{n+1}):\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

(2.6)

\bar{\nu}=\frac{-\nabla u}{\sqrt{|\nabla u|^{2}+|x|^{2\alpha}}},\quad\nu_{n+1}=\frac{|x|^{\alpha}}{{\sqrt{|\nabla u|^{2}+|x|^{2\alpha}}}}.

This normal is pointing upwards.
Notice that
\nu=\nu(\xi)
only depends on
x
and not on
y=u(x)
.

From (
2.3
) and from the area-formula, we deduce that
the
\sigma
-area of
\Sigma
has the integral representation

(2.7)

\sigma(\Sigma_{u})=\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{|\nabla u|^{2}+|x|^{2\alpha}}\;dx=\int_{\Omega}v(x)\;dx,

where
v
is the
\sigma
-area element

(2.8)

{v(x)=\sqrt{|\nabla u|^{2}+a^{2}},\qquad a=|x|^{\alpha}.}

If
\Sigma_{u}
minimizes the
\sigma
-area with respect to compact perturbations in
\Omega
and
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)
, then
u

satisfies the partial differential equation of the minimal surface-type

(2.9)

\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u(x)}{\sqrt{|\nabla u(x)|^{2}+|x|^{2\alpha}}}\right)=0,\quad x\in\Omega^{*}.

This
follows by a standard first variation procedure applied to (
2.7
).
This suggests the following definition.

Definition 2.2

(
\alpha
-mean curvature)
.

Let
\Sigma
be the
y
-graph of a function
u\in C^{2}(\Omega)
.
We define the
\alpha
-mean curvature
of
\Sigma
at the point
\xi=(x,u(x))\in\Sigma^{*}
as

(2.10)

H_{\Sigma}(\xi)=\frac{1}{n}\operatorname{div}\left(\frac{\nabla u(x)}{\sqrt{|\nabla u(x)|^{2}+|x|^{2\alpha}}}\right).

We say that
\Sigma
is an
\alpha
-minimal hypersurface if
H_{\Sigma}=0
on
\Sigma^{*}
.

A more geometric definition of
\alpha
-mean curvature will be presented in the next section.

3.
Tangential operators and Laplacians

We introduce tangential differential operators on hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
endowed with the Grushin structure.
Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be an embedded hypersurface of class
C^{2}
with
\alpha
-normal
\nu:\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

The
X
-gradient of a function
\varphi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
is the vector-field
X\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}X_{i}\varphi\,X_{i}
that we identify, with abuse of notation, with the vector of its coordinates
X\varphi=(X_{1}\varphi,\ldots,X_{n+1}\varphi)
.
We denote the standard scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.

Definition 3.1

(Tangential gradient)
.

Let
\nu:\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be the
\alpha
-normal of
\Sigma
.
The tangential gradient on
\Sigma
is the mapping

\delta:C^{1}(\Sigma)\to C(\Sigma^{*};\mathbb{R}^{n+1})

(3.1)

\delta\varphi=X\varphi-\langle X\varphi,\nu\rangle\nu.

For any
i=1,\ldots,n+1
we also let
\delta_{i}\varphi=X_{i}\varphi-\langle X\varphi,\nu\rangle\nu_{i}
.

In (
3.1
),
\varphi
is extended outside
\Sigma
in a
C^{1}
way, and the definition will be independent of this extension.
We are assuming that
\Sigma
is oriented and we are fixing a choice of
\alpha
-normal. The definition does not depend on this choice. When
\Sigma
is
a
y
-graph, we agree that

\nu
is pointing upwards.
In this case, the
\alpha
-normal
\nu
can be extended outside
\Sigma
in a way that is independent of the variable
y
.
In the rest of the paper the surface
\Sigma
will be always assumed to be a
y
-graph.

The definition of the tangential operator
\delta
in (
3.1
) is extrinsic. A different possibility could be to define the tangential gradient of functions on
\Sigma
using
the Riemannian metric
g_{\alpha}
. However, the choice in (
3.1
) is the correct one in order to recover the definition in (
2.10
) of
\alpha
-mean curvature.
Indeed, this definition reads

(3.2)

H_{\Sigma}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\nu_{i},

and it can be rephrased in the following way.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a
y
-graph of class
C^{2}
. Then on
\Sigma^{*}
we have the identity

H_{\Sigma}=-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\delta_{i}\nu_{i}.

Proof.

We use the definition of
\delta
and observe that
\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\nu_{i}X_{k}\nu_{i}=X_{k}(|\nu|^{2}/2)=0
, and
X_{n+1}\nu_{n+1}=0
, so that

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\delta_{i}\nu_{i}&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{i}\nu_{i}+\delta_{n+1}\nu_{n+1}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\nu_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\nu_{i}\nu_{k}X_{k}\nu_{i}+X_{n+1}\nu_{n+1}-\nu_{n+1}\nu_{k}X_{k}\nu_{n+1}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\nu_{i}-\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\left(\nu_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\nu_{i}X_{k}\nu_{i}\right)+X_{n+1}\nu_{n+1}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\nu_{i}=-nH_{\Sigma}.\qed\end{split}

Next we introduce the adjoint operators
\delta_{i}^{*}
, integrating by parts with respect to the measure
\sigma
.

Definition 3.3

(Adjoint tangential operators)
.

For each
i=1,\dots,n+1
, we define the
adjoint tangential operator

\delta_{i}^{*}:C^{1}(\Sigma)\to C(\Sigma^{*})

through the identity

(3.3)

\int_{\Sigma}\psi\,\delta_{i}\varphi\,d\sigma=-\int_{\Sigma}\varphi\,\delta_{i}^{*}\psi\,d\sigma,\quad\varphi,\psi\in C^{1}_{c}(\Sigma).

The explicit formula for adjoint operators is given in the next lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a hypersurface with
\alpha
-mean curvature
H_{\Sigma}
.
For every
\psi\in C^{1}_{c}(\Sigma)
and
i=1,\ldots,n+1
, we have on
\Sigma^{*}

(3.4)

\delta_{i}^{*}\psi=-\delta_{i}\psi-\psi\Big[\delta_{i}\log|x|^{\alpha}+\frac{1}{\nu_{n+1}}\Big(\delta_{n+1}\nu_{i}-\delta_{i}\nu_{n+1}\Big)+nH_{\Sigma}\nu_{i}\Big].

Proof.

Let
\varphi,\psi\in C^{1}_{c}(\Sigma^{*})
. Then by the area formula
(
2.7
) with
v
as in (
2.8
)

(3.5)

\begin{split}\int_{\Sigma}\varphi\,\delta_{i}^{*}\psi\,d\sigma&=\int_{\Sigma}\psi\,\delta_{i}\varphi\,d\sigma=\int_{\Omega}\psi(X_{i}\varphi-\langle X\varphi,\nu\rangle\nu_{i})vdx\\
&=-\int_{\Omega}\varphi\Big[X_{i}(v\psi)-\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}(\nu_{i}\nu_{k}v\psi)\Big]dx\\
&=-\int_{\Sigma}\varphi\frac{A}{v}d\sigma,\end{split}

where in the last identity we set
A=X_{i}(v\psi)-\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}(\nu_{i}\nu_{k}v\psi)
. We are left to prove that
A/v
is equal to the right-hand side in (
3.4
).

We have

(3.6)

\begin{split}-\frac{A}{v}&=-\frac{1}{v}\left[\psi X_{i}v+vX_{i}\psi-v\nu_{i}\langle X\psi,\nu\rangle-\psi\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}(\nu_{i}\nu_{k}v)\right]\\
&=-\delta_{i}\psi-\frac{\psi}{v}\left[X_{i}v-\nu_{i}\langle Xv,\nu\rangle-v\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}(\nu_{i}\nu_{k})\right].\end{split}

In the term within brackets above, we easily recognize
X_{i}v-\nu_{i}\langle Xv,\nu\rangle=\delta v
. On the other hand, using
X_{n+1}\nu=0
and (
3.2
), we get

(3.7)

\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}(\nu_{i}\nu_{k})=-n\nu_{i}H_{\Sigma}-\frac{1}{\nu_{n+1}}\Big[X_{n+1}\nu_{i}-\nu_{n+1}\langle X\nu_{i},\nu\rangle\Big]=-n\nu_{i}H_{\Sigma}-\frac{1}{\nu_{n+1}}\delta_{n+1}\nu_{i}.

Summarizing, we have

(3.8)

\frac{A}{v}=\delta_{i}\psi+\psi\left[\frac{\delta_{i}v}{v}+\frac{\delta_{n+1}\nu_{i}}{\nu_{n+1}}+nH_{\Sigma}\nu_{i}\right].

To prove our claim it then remains to check the following identity

(3.9)

\frac{\delta_{i}v}{v}=\frac{\delta_{i}a}{a}-\frac{\delta_{i}\nu_{n+1}}{\nu_{n+1}}=\delta_{i}(\log a)-\frac{\delta_{i}\nu_{n+1}}{\nu_{n+1}}.

Indeed, since
\nu_{n+1}=a/v
we have

(3.10)

\frac{\delta_{i}a}{a}-\frac{\delta_{i}\nu_{n+1}}{\nu_{n+1}}=\frac{\delta_{i}a}{a}-\frac{v\delta_{i}(\frac{a}{v})}{a}=\frac{\delta_{i}a}{a}-\frac{v}{a}\left(\frac{\delta_{i}a}{v}-\frac{a}{v^{2}}\delta_{i}v\right)=\frac{\delta_{i}v}{v}.

∎

Definition 3.5

(Tangential Laplacians)
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a hypersurface of class
C^{2}
.
The
tangential Laplacian
of
\Sigma
is the operator

\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}:C^{2}(\Sigma)\to C(\Sigma^{*})

(3.11)

\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}\varphi=-\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\delta_{i}^{*}\delta_{i}\varphi.

The relation between
\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}
and the non-adjoint Laplacian

(3.12)

\Delta_{\Sigma}\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\delta_{i}^{2}\varphi.

is described in the following proposition.

Lemma 3.6
.

For any
\varphi\in C^{2}(\Sigma)
we have the identity

(3.13)

\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}\varphi=\Delta_{\Sigma}\varphi+\nu_{n+1}^{2}\langle\delta\varphi,\delta\log a\rangle+\delta_{n+1}\varphi\,\delta_{n+1}\log a

Proof.

We have

(3.14)

\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}\varphi=-\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\delta_{i}^{*}\delta_{i}=\Delta_{\Sigma}\varphi+\langle\delta\varphi,\delta\log a\rangle-\frac{1}{\nu_{n+1}}\langle\delta\varphi,\delta\nu_{n+1}-\delta_{n+1}\nu\rangle.

By formula (
3.4
), for
i=1,\dots,n+1
we have

(3.15)

\delta\nu_{n+1}-\delta_{n+1}\nu=\nu_{n+1}\big[\delta\log a-\big(\bar{0},\delta_{n+1}\log a\big)\big]+\nu_{n+1}^{2}\big[\nu\delta_{n+1}\log a-\nu_{n+1}\delta\log a\big].

Since
\langle\delta(\cdot),\nu\rangle=0
, we deduce

(3.16)

\begin{split}-\frac{1}{\nu_{n+1}}\langle\delta\varphi,\delta\nu_{n+1}-\delta_{n+1}\nu\rangle=&-\langle\delta\varphi,\delta\log a\rangle+\delta_{n+1}\varphi\delta_{n+1}\log a\\
&+\nu_{n+1}^{2}\langle\delta\varphi,\delta\log a\rangle,\end{split}

proving the result.
∎

The formal Hessian of
\varphi
with respect to the vector-fields
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n+1}
is the
(n+1)\times(n+1)
matrix

X^{2}\varphi=\big(X_{i}X_{j}\varphi)_{i,j=1,\ldots,n+1}.

The non-adjoint Laplacian
\Delta_{\Sigma}
has a clear representation in terms of the Grushin operator
\mathscr{L}
in (
1.2
),
X^{2}
and
\alpha
-mean curvature of
\Sigma
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a hypersurface of class
C^{2}
with
\alpha
-mean curvature
H_{\Sigma}
.
For any
\varphi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
we have the identity on
\Sigma^{*}

(3.17)

\Delta_{\Sigma}\varphi=\mathscr{L}\varphi-\langle(X^{2}\varphi)\nu,\nu\rangle+nH_{\Sigma}\langle X\varphi,\nu\rangle.

Proof.

We first compute
\delta_{i}^{2}\varphi
, for
i\geq 1
. We have

(3.18)

\begin{split}\delta_{i}^{2}\varphi&=\delta_{i}(X_{i}\varphi-\langle X\varphi,\nu\rangle\nu_{i})\\
&=X_{i}X_{i}\varphi-\langle XX_{i}\varphi,\nu\rangle\nu_{i}-\langle X\varphi,\nu\rangle\delta_{i}\nu_{i}-\delta_{i}(\langle X\varphi,\nu\rangle)\nu_{i}.\end{split}

Summing over
i
, we obtain the identities

(3.19)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\langle XX_{i}\varphi,\nu\rangle\nu_{i}=\langle(X^{2}\varphi)\nu,\nu\rangle,\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\delta_{i}\nu_{i}=-nH_{\Sigma},

(3.20)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}\delta_{i}(\langle X\varphi,\nu\rangle)\nu_{i}=\langle\delta(\langle X\varphi,\nu\rangle),\nu\rangle=0,

and this completes the proof.
∎

We specialize the previous formulas to the case when
\varphi
is a radial function around
0\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The symmetry is governed by the gauge function
\varrho
in
(
1.3
).
Below, we collect the differential identities concerning first and second order derivatives of
\varrho
. With the notation
\xi=(x,y)
and
\varrho=\varrho(\xi)
we have

(3.21)

\displaystyle\nabla_{x}\varrho=x|x|^{2\alpha}\varrho^{-(2\alpha+1)}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\partial_{y}\varrho=(\alpha+1)y\varrho^{-(2\alpha+1)}.

Then the squared norm of the
X
-gradient of
\varrho
is

(3.22)

|X\varrho|^{2}=|\nabla_{x}\varrho|^{2}+|x|^{2\alpha}|\partial_{y}\varrho|^{2}=|x|^{2\alpha}\varrho^{-2\alpha}.

The second derivatives of
\varrho
are, with
i,j=1,\dots,n
and denoting by
\varepsilon_{ij}
the Kronecker symbol,

(3.23)

\begin{split}X_{i}X_{j}\varrho&=|x|^{2\alpha}\Big[\varepsilon_{ij}+2\alpha\frac{x_{i}x_{j}}{|x|^{2}}-(2\alpha+1)\frac{x_{i}x_{j}|x|^{2\alpha}}{\varrho^{2(\alpha+1)}}\Big],\\
X_{i}X_{n+1}\varrho&=(\alpha+1)|x|^{\texttrademark 2\alpha}x_{i}y\varrho^{-2\alpha-1}\Big[\frac{\alpha}{|x|^{\alpha+2}}-(2\alpha+1)\frac{|x|^{2\alpha}}{\varrho^{2(\alpha+1)}}\Big],\\
X_{n+1}X_{j}\varrho&=-(2\alpha+1)(\alpha+1)|x|^{3\alpha}x_{j}y\varrho^{-4\alpha-3},\\
X_{n+1}^{2}\varrho&=(\alpha+1)|x|^{2\alpha}\varrho(x)^{-2\alpha-1}\Big[1-(2\alpha+1)(\alpha+1)\frac{y^{2}}{\varrho^{2(\alpha+1)}}\Big].\end{split}

From (
3.23
), we get the following formulas for the Laplacian
\mathscr{L}
and for the quadratic form
\langle(X^{2}\varrho)\nu,\nu\rangle
:

(3.24)

\mathscr{L}\varrho=(n+\alpha)\frac{|X\varrho|^{2}}{\varrho},

and

(3.25)

\begin{split}\langle(X^{2}\varrho)\nu,\nu\rangle=|\bar{\nu}|^{2}&+2\alpha\frac{\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle^{2}}{|x|^{2}}-(2\alpha+1)\frac{|x|^{2\alpha}\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle^{2}}{\varrho^{2(\alpha+1)}}\\
&+(\alpha+1)\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle^{2}\nu_{n+1}y\left(\frac{\alpha}{|x|^{\alpha+2}}-2(2\alpha+1)\frac{|x|^{2\alpha}\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle^{2}}{\varrho^{2(\alpha+1)}}\right)\\
&+(\alpha+1)\nu_{n+1}^{2}\left(1-(\alpha+1)(2\alpha+1)\frac{y^{2}}{\varrho^{2(\alpha+1)}}\right).\end{split}

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a hypersurface with
\alpha
-normal
\nu
and
\alpha
-mean curvature
H_{\Sigma}
.
The structural function
q_{\Sigma}:\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
introduced in (
1.5
) governs the harmonicity of
\Sigma
at
0\in\Sigma
and appears in the following formula.

Theorem 3.8

(Tangential Laplacian of radial functions)
.

For any
\varphi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
, the function

\psi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\})
,
\psi=\varphi\circ\varrho
, satisfies the identity

(3.26)

\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}\psi=\Big\{\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\varrho)+\varphi^{\prime}(\varrho)\frac{n+\alpha-1}{\varrho}\Big\}|\delta\varrho|^{2}+q_{\Sigma}\,\varphi^{\prime}(\varrho).

Proof.

In a first step, we prove that formula (
3.26
) holds with the following expression for
q_{\Sigma}
:

(3.27)

\begin{split}q_{\Sigma}=-\frac{n+\alpha-1}{\varrho}|\delta\varrho|^{2}&+(n+\alpha)\frac{|X\varrho|^{2}}{\varrho}-\langle(X^{2}\varrho)\nu,\nu\rangle+nH_{\Sigma}\langle X\varrho,\nu\rangle\\
&+\alpha\nu_{n+1}^{2}\langle\delta\varrho,\delta\log|x|\rangle+\alpha\delta_{n+1}\varrho\delta_{n+1}\log|x|.\end{split}

The proof combines formula (
3.13
) of Lemma
3.6
and formula (
3.17
) in Lemma
3.7
:

(3.28)

\displaystyle\Delta_{\Sigma}\psi

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}(\psi\circ\varrho)-\langle X^{2}(\psi\circ\varrho)\nu,\nu\rangle+nH_{\Sigma}\langle X(\psi\circ\varrho),\nu\rangle

(3.29)

\displaystyle=\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\varrho)|\delta\varrho|^{2}+\varphi^{\prime}(\varrho)\big(\mathscr{L}\varrho-\langle(X^{2}\varrho)\nu,\nu\rangle+nH_{\Sigma}\langle X\varrho,\nu\rangle\big),

where the last identity is a simple computation with the chain rule. On the other hand, we have

(3.30)

\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}\psi=\Delta_{\Sigma}\psi+\alpha\nu_{n+1}^{2}\langle\delta\psi,\delta(\log|x|)\rangle+\alpha\delta_{n+1}\psi\delta_{n+1}(\log|x|).

Since
\delta\psi=\varphi^{\prime}(\varrho)\delta\varrho
, we deduce

(3.31)

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}\psi=\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\varrho)|\delta\varrho|^{2}+\varphi^{\prime}(\varrho)\big[

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}\varrho-\langle(X^{2}\varrho)\nu,\nu\rangle+nH_{\Sigma}\langle X\varrho,\nu\rangle

(3.32)

\displaystyle+\alpha\nu_{n+1}^{2}\langle\delta\varrho,\delta(\log|x|)\rangle+\alpha\delta_{n+1}\varrho\delta_{n+1}(\log|x|)\big]

The proof of (
3.26
) with
q_{\Sigma}
as in (
3.27
)
is then concluded by adding and subtracting the quantity
\frac{n+\alpha-1}{\varrho}|\delta\varrho|^{2}
within squared brackets and using
(
3.24
).

In the next step, we check that
q_{\Sigma}
in (
3.27
) is as in (
1.5
).
We start by observing that an elementary computation gives

(3.33)

\nu_{n+1}^{2}\langle\delta\varrho,\delta\log|x|\rangle+\delta_{n+1}\varrho\delta_{n+1}\log|x|=\frac{|x|^{2\alpha}}{\varrho^{2\alpha+1}}\Big(\nu_{n+1}^{2}-(\alpha+1)\nu_{n+1}|x|^{-2-\alpha}\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle y\Big),

and

(3.34)

-\frac{n+\alpha-1}{\varrho}|\delta\varrho|^{2}+(n+\alpha)\frac{|X\varrho|^{2}}{\varrho}=\frac{|X\varrho|^{2}}{\varrho}+\frac{n+\alpha-1}{\varrho}\langle X\varrho,\nu\rangle^{2}.

Inserting formulas (
3.23
)–(
3.25
) into (
3.27
), we obtain

(3.35)

\begin{split}q_{\Sigma}=&\frac{n+\alpha-1}{\varrho}\langle X\varrho,\nu\rangle^{2}+nH_{\Sigma}\langle X\varrho,\nu\rangle\\
&-\frac{|X\varrho|^{2}}{\varrho}\Big[2\alpha\frac{\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle^{2}}{|x|^{2}}-(2\alpha+1)\frac{|x|^{2\alpha}}{\varrho^{2\alpha+2}}\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle^{2}+2\alpha(\alpha+1)\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle y|x|^{-2-\alpha}\nu_{n+1}\\
&\qquad\qquad\;\;-2(\alpha+1)(2\alpha+1)\frac{|x|^{\alpha}}{\varrho^{2\alpha+2}}\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle y\nu_{n+1}\\
&\qquad\qquad\;\;-(\alpha+1)^{2}(2\alpha+1)y^{2}\nu_{n+1}^{2}\frac{1}{\varrho^{2\alpha+2}}\Big].\end{split}

Now we observe that

(3.36)

\langle X\varrho,\nu\rangle^{2}=\frac{|x|^{2\alpha}}{\varrho^{4\alpha+2}}\left(|x|^{\alpha}\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle+(\alpha+1)y\nu_{n+1}\right)^{2}

and so, since
|X\varrho|=(|x|/\varrho)^{\alpha}
,

(3.37)

\langle X\varrho,\nu\rangle=\frac{|X\varrho|}{\varrho^{\alpha+1}}\left(|x|^{\alpha}\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle+(\alpha+1)y\nu_{n+1}\right).

Replacing last identity into (
3.35
) and after some computations that are omitted, we obtain (
1.5
).
∎

4.
Mean value formulas

We are ready to prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.
Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a hypersurface of class
C^{2}
with
0\in\Sigma
. We say that:

i)

\Sigma
is
\alpha
-harmonic at
0
if
q_{\Sigma}=0
;

ii)

\Sigma
is
\alpha
-subharmonic at
0
if
q_{\Sigma}\geq 0
;

iii)

\Sigma
is
\alpha
-superharmonic at
0
if
q_{\Sigma}\leq 0
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

For any
\psi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Sigma)
, by the integration by parts formula (
3.3
) we have

(4.1)

0=\int_{\Sigma}\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}f\,\psi\,d\sigma=-\int_{\Sigma}\langle\delta f\,\delta\psi\rangle d\sigma=\int_{\Sigma}f\,\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}\psi\,d\sigma.

We shall use this identity for functions
\psi
with radial structure around
0
.
Let
\chi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
be a function such that
\chi(r)=1
for
0<r<1/2
and
\chi(r)=0
for
r>1
.
We may also assume that
\chi^{\prime}\leq 0
.
With the notation
m(r)=r^{n+\alpha}
, for
0<s<r
we define the function
\vartheta_{s}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})

(4.2)

\vartheta_{s}(\varrho)=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left(\frac{1}{m(s)}\chi\Big(\frac{m(\varrho)}{m(s)}\Big)\right),\quad\varrho>0.

Assume there exists a solution
\varphi_{s}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{+})
to the differential problem

(4.3)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\varphi_{s}^{\prime\prime}(\varrho)+\frac{m^{\prime\prime}(\varrho)}{m^{\prime}(\varrho)}\varphi_{s}^{\prime}(\varrho)=\vartheta_{s}(\varrho),\quad\varrho>0\\
\varphi_{s}(\varrho)=0,\quad\varrho>s.\end{array}\right.

Then we may consider the function
\psi_{s}(\xi)=\varphi_{s}(\varrho(\xi))
for
\xi\in\Sigma
.
By formula (
3.26
) with
q_{\Sigma}=0
and (
4.3
) we have

\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}\psi_{s}=\Big\{\varphi_{s}^{\prime\prime}(\varrho)+\varphi_{s}^{\prime}(\varrho)\frac{n+\alpha-1}{\varrho}\Big\}|\delta\varrho|^{2}=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\left(\frac{1}{m(s)}\chi\Big(\frac{m(\varrho)}{m(s)}\Big)\right)|\delta\varrho|^{2},

and from (
4.1
)
we deduce that

0=\int_{\Sigma}f\,\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}\psi_{s}\,d\sigma=\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\int_{\Sigma}f(\xi)\,\frac{1}{m(s)}\chi\Big(\frac{m(\varrho)}{m(s)}\Big)|\delta\varrho|^{2}\,d\sigma,

and thus for any
0<s<r

\frac{1}{m(s)}\int_{\Sigma}f(\xi)\,\chi\Big(\frac{m(\varrho)}{m(s)}\Big)|\delta\varrho|^{2}\,d\sigma=\frac{1}{m(r)}\int_{\Sigma}f(\xi)\,\chi\Big(\frac{m(\varrho)}{m(r)}\Big)|\delta\varrho|^{2}\,d\sigma.

We may approximate the characteristic function of the interval
(0,1)\subset\mathbb{R}
by a sequence of functions
\chi
as above.
Passing to the limit in the previous identity, we get

\frac{1}{m(s)}\int_{B_{s}\cap\Sigma}f(\xi)\,|\delta\varrho|^{2}\,d\sigma=\frac{1}{m(r)}\int_{B_{r}\cap\Sigma}f(\xi)\,|\delta\varrho|^{2}\,d\sigma.

This formula holds for
f=1
, proving that the right hand-side of (
1.7
) does not depend on
r>0
.
By continuity of
f
at
0
, we get (
1.6
) with
C_{\Sigma,n,\alpha}
as in (
1.7
).

We are left to show that problem (
4.3
) has a solution.
A straightforward computation shows that the function
\vartheta_{s}
in (
4.2
) reads

(4.4)

\begin{split}\vartheta_{s}(\varrho)&=-\frac{m^{\prime}(s)}{m(s)^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varrho}\left(m(\varrho)\chi\Big(\frac{m(\varrho)}{m(s)}\Big)\right),\end{split}

and so the differential equation in (
4.3
) is equivalent to

(4.5)

\frac{\partial}{\partial\varrho}\big(m^{\prime}(\varrho)\varphi_{s}^{\prime}(\varrho)\big)=-\frac{m^{\prime}(s)}{m(s)^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial\varrho}\left(m(\varrho)\chi\Big(\frac{m(\varrho)}{m(s)}\Big)\right).

Integrating with
\varphi^{\prime}_{s}(\varrho)=0
for
\varrho>s
we obtain:

(4.6)

\varphi_{s}^{\prime}(\varrho)=-\frac{m^{\prime}(s)m(\varrho)}{m(s)^{2}m^{\prime}(\varrho)}\chi\Big(\frac{m(\varrho)}{m(s)}\Big)=-\frac{\varrho}{s^{n+\alpha+1}}\chi\Big(\frac{\varrho^{n+\alpha}}{s^{n+\alpha}}\Big).

A final integration with
\varphi_{s}(\varrho)=0
for
\varrho>s
yields

(4.7)

\varphi_{s}(\varrho)=\int_{\varrho}^{\infty}\frac{r}{s^{n+\alpha+1}}\chi\Big(\frac{r^{n+\alpha}}{s^{n+\alpha}}\Big)dr,

showing that we find a function satisfying as a matter of fact
\varphi_{s}(\varrho)=0
for
\varrho>s
.

∎

Remark 4.1
.

Using the technique of Theorem
1.1
, with
m(r)=r^{n+\alpha}
replaced by
m(r)=r^{n+\alpha+1}
, one obtains a mean value formula for
\mathscr{L}
-harmonic functions at

, where
|\delta\varrho|
in (
1.1
) is replaced by
|X\varrho|
, and
\mathscr{L}
is the Grushin Laplacian (
1.2
).
The same technique works when
\mathscr{L}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{j}^{2}
is the sub-Laplacian of any family

X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
of smooth vector fields in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
satisfying the Hörmander condition, with
2\leq m\leq n+1
and admitting a global fundamental solution. The resulting mean-value formulas coincide with the formulas obtained in
[
5
]
.

We explain the relation between the two approaches in the case of a Carnot group of topological dimension
d>2
. Let
\Gamma
be the fundamental solution of the corresponding Carnot sub-Laplacian
\mathscr{L}
with pole at
0
. For a harmonic function
\mathscr{L}f=0
, the mean value formula (1.4) proved in
[
5
]

reads

(4.8)

f(0)=\frac{1}{r}\int_{\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\Gamma(\xi)>\frac{1}{r}\}}f(\xi)\left|X(\log\Gamma)\right|^{2}\varphi\left(\frac{1}{r\Gamma(\xi)}\right)\;d\xi,\quad r>0,

where
\varphi
is any continuous function on the interval
[0,1]
with unit integral.

Let

\varrho(\xi)=\Gamma(\xi)^{\frac{1}{Q-2}}
,
\xi\neq 0
, where
Q\in\mathbb{N}
is the homogeneous dimension of the group. The Lebesgue measure of the balls
B_{s}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\varrho(\xi)<s\}
satisfies
m(s)=\mathscr{L}^{d}(B_{s})=Cs^{Q}

depending on

and

.
Using the technique of Theorem

we get the mean value formula

f(0)=\frac{C_{d,Q}}{m(s)}\int_{B_{s}}f(\xi)\,|X\varrho(\xi)|^{2}d\xi,

with

fixed on choosing

. Formula (

is precisely formula (

the choice
\varphi(t)=\frac{Q}{Q-2}t^{\frac{2}{Q-2}}
. In fact, in this case we have

\left|X(\log\Gamma)\right|^{2}\varphi\left(\frac{1}{r\Gamma}\right)=Q(Q-2)r^{\frac{2}{2-Q}}|X\varrho|^{2},

and we obtain equivalence with (

) by setting

.

5.
Analysis of the structural function
q_{\Sigma}

5.1.
Homogeneous hypersurfaces are harmonic

In
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with the Grushin structure, we introduce the anisotropic dilations
d_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
,
\lambda>0
,

d_{\lambda}(\xi)=d_{\lambda}(x,y)=(\lambda x,\lambda^{\alpha+1}y),\quad\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.

We say that a set
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is
d_{\lambda}
-homogeneous if
d_{\lambda}(\Sigma)=\Sigma
for any
\lambda>0
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be a hypersurface of class
C^{2}
with
0\in\Sigma
.
If
\Sigma
is
d_{\lambda}
-homogeneous then
\Sigma
is
\alpha
-harmonic at
0
.

Proof.

We check the claim when
\Sigma
is a
y
-graph

\Sigma=\{(x,u(x))\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}
for some function
u\in\ C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfying the identity
u(\lambda x)=\lambda^{\alpha+1}u(x)
for any
\lambda>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Differentiating this identity at
\lambda=1
we get

(5.1)

\langle\nabla u(x),x\rangle=(\alpha+1)u(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Using formulas (
2.6
) for the
\alpha
-normal
\nu=(\bar{\nu},\nu_{n+1})
, (
5.1
) is equivalent to

|x|^{\alpha}\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle+(\alpha+1)y\nu_{n+1}=0,\quad(x,y)\in\Sigma^{*}.

By formula (
3.37
) this is in turn equivalent to
\langle X\varrho,\nu\rangle=0
on
\Sigma^{*}
, and this implies
q_{\Sigma}=0
, see (
1.5
).
∎

When
\Sigma
is
d_{\lambda}
-homogeneous we have
\langle X\varrho,\nu\rangle=0
and, using (
3.22
), the kernel
|\delta\varrho|^{2}
appearing in the mean value formula (
1.7
) reduces to

|\delta\varrho|^{2}=|X\varrho|^{2}=\frac{|x|^{2\alpha}}{\varrho^{2\alpha}}.

This kernel is
0
-homogeneous with respect to the dilations
d_{\lambda}
and satisfies
|\delta\varrho|^{2}\leq 1
.

5.2.
Flat case

The hyperplane
\Sigma=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:y=0\}
is
d_{\lambda}
homogeneous and it is therefore
\alpha
-harmonic.
The
\alpha
-normal is constant,
\nu=(0,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
, and it follows that
\Sigma
is also
\alpha
-minimal,
H_{\Sigma}=0
.

The tangential gradient
\delta

reduces to the standard gradient
\delta\varphi=(\nabla_{x}\varphi,0)
and the non-adjoint Laplacian
\Delta_{\Sigma}
in (
3.12
) reduces to the standard Laplacian
\Delta_{x}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, see (
3.17
). From formula (
3.13
)
we deduce that

\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}\varphi=\Delta_{x}\varphi+\alpha\langle\nabla_{x}\log|x|,\nabla_{x}\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}\mathrm{div}\Big(|x|^{\alpha}\nabla_{x}\varphi\Big).

Theorem
1.1
states that a function
\varphi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfying
\mathscr{L}_{\Sigma}\varphi=0
has the mean value property at
0

\varphi(0)=\frac{n+\alpha}{n\omega_{n}r^{n+\alpha}}\int_{\{|x|<r\}}\varphi(x)|x|^{\alpha}dx,

with
\omega_{n}=\mathscr{L}^{n}(\{|x|<1\})
.

5.3.
\alpha
-subharmonic surfaces

In this section, we look for sufficient conditions for a hypersurface
\Sigma
to be
\alpha
-subharmonic at
0\in\Sigma
.

Definition 5.2

(
\eta
-flatness)
.

Let
\eta>0
.
We say that a hypersurface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is
\eta
-flat at
0\in\Sigma
if there exists
r>0
such that its
\alpha
-normal
\nu=(\bar{\nu},\nu_{n+1})
satisfies

(5.2)

(\alpha+1)|y||\nu_{n+1}|\leq\eta|x|^{\alpha}|\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle|

for all points
\xi=(x,y)\in\Sigma^{*}\cap B_{r}
.

When
\Sigma
is the
y
-graph of a function
u
, condition (
5.2
) reads

(5.3)

(\alpha+1)|u|\leq\eta\big|\langle x,\nabla_{x}u\rangle\big|

holding for points a neighborhood of
0\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
u\in C^{1}(\{|x|\leq 1\})
be a function satisfying (
5.3
).
Then for any point
|x|\leq 1
we have

(5.4)

|u(x)|\leq\Big(\max_{|x|=1}|u|\Big)|x|^{\frac{\alpha+1}{\eta}}.

Proof.

Let
|x|=1
be fixed. We prove the claim along the segment
tx
, with
t\in[0,1]
.
Letting
\varphi:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
,
\varphi(t)=u(tx)
, assumption (
5.3
) reads

(5.5)

(\alpha+1)|\varphi(t)|\leq\eta t|\varphi^{\prime}(t)|.

If
\varphi=0
on
[0,1]
, the claim is trivial. Then we can assume that the open set
A=\{t\in(0,1):\varphi(t)\neq 0\}
is nonempty.
This set is a finite or countable disjoint union of intervals
(a,b)\subset A
. We always have
\varphi(a)=0
and from
(
5.5
) it follows that
\varphi^{\prime}\neq 0
on
(a,b)
, say
\varphi^{\prime}(t)>0
for any
t\in(a,b)
.
Then
\varphi
is strictly monotone increasing and thus
\varphi(b)>0
, and so
b=1
. It follows that
A=(a,1)
, for some
a\in[0,1)
, and
\varphi=0
on
[0,a]
.

We can without loss of generality assume that
a=0
and conclude the proof in the following way. We have
\varphi>0
and, say,
\varphi^{\prime}>0
on
(0,1)
. Then (
5.5
) reads

(5.6)

\frac{d}{dt}\log(t^{\alpha+1})\leq\eta\frac{d}{dt}\log\varphi(t),

and integrating this inequality from
t=s
to
t=1
,
0<s<1
, we get

\varphi(s)\leq\varphi(1)s^{\frac{\alpha+1}{\eta}}
. This implies (
5.4
) and the proof is concluded.
∎

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
by a hypersurface of class
C^{2}
with
\alpha
-normal
\nu=(\bar{\nu},\nu_{n+1})
and
\alpha
-mean curvature
H_{\Sigma}
.
Assume that:

i)

\Sigma
is
\eta
-flat at
0\in\Sigma
for some

(5.7)

0<\eta<\frac{n+\alpha}{n+3\alpha}.

ii)

We have, with limit restricted to
\xi=(x,y)\in\Sigma
,

(5.8)

\lim_{\xi\to 0}\frac{|x|^{2}H_{\Sigma}(\xi)}{\langle\bar{\nu}(\xi),x\rangle}=0.

Then there exists a
\delta>0
such that
q_{\Sigma}\geq 0
on
\Sigma^{*}\cap B_{\delta}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
\Sigma=\{(x,u(x))\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:|x|\leq 1\}
for some function
u\in C^{2}(\{|x|\leq 1\})
,
and that (
5.2
)
holds with
r=1
. Letting
C_{1}=\max_{|x|=1}|u(x)|
, points
(x,y)\in\Sigma
satisfy by (
5.4
)

(5.9)

|y|\leq C_{1}|x|^{\frac{\alpha+1}{\eta}}.

Formula (
1.5
) for
q_{\Sigma}
reads

q_{\Sigma}(\xi)=\langle X\varrho,\nu\rangle\Big[\frac{n+3\alpha}{\varrho}\langle X\varrho,\nu\rangle-\frac{2\alpha}{|x|^{2}}\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle+nH_{\Sigma}\Big],

and the inequality
q_{\Sigma}\geq 0
is thus implied by

(5.10)

\Big|\frac{2\alpha}{|x|^{2}}\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle-nH_{\Sigma}\Big|\leq\frac{n+3\alpha}{\varrho}|\langle X\varrho,\nu\rangle|.

Inserting the identity (
3.37
) into (
5.10
) and then using (
3.22
),
we see that
q_{\Sigma}\geq 0
is implied by

(5.11)

\Big|\frac{2\alpha}{|x|^{2}}\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle-nH_{\Sigma}\Big|\leq\frac{n+3\alpha}{\varrho^{2\alpha+2}}|x|^{\alpha}\big||x|^{\alpha}\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle+(\alpha+1)y\nu_{n+1}\big|.

By the
\eta
-flatness condition (
5.2
), (
5.11
) is in turn implied by

(5.12)

\Big|\frac{2\alpha}{|x|^{2}}\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle-nH_{\Sigma}\Big|\leq(1-\eta)\frac{n+3\alpha}{\varrho^{2\alpha+2}}|x|^{2\alpha}|\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle|,

and finally, using assumption (
5.8
), (
5.12
) is implied by

(5.13)

\Big(2\alpha+\frac{n|x|^{2}|H_{\Sigma}|}{|\langle x,\bar{\nu}\rangle|}\Big)\leq(1-\eta)(n+3\alpha)\frac{|x|^{2\alpha+2}}{\varrho^{2\alpha+2}}.

Now observe that, by (
5.9
),

(5.14)

\frac{\varrho^{2(\alpha+1)}}{|x|^{2(\alpha+1)}}=1+\frac{(\alpha+1)^{2}y^{2}}{|x|^{2(\alpha+1)}}\leq 1+C_{1}(\alpha+1)^{2}|x|^{2(\alpha+1)(1/\eta-1)}.

By (
5.14
) and (
5.8
),
inequality (
5.13
) is satisfied for
x=0
as a strict inequality, by (
5.7
).
Our claim follows by a limiting argument.

∎
