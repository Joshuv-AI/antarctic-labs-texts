Convergence Bounds for Sequential Monte Carlo on Multimodal Distributions using Soft Decomposition
Holden Lee, Matheau Santana-Gijzen

Abstract
We prove bounds on the variance of a function f f under the empirical measure of the samples obtained by the Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) algorithm, with time complexity depending on local rather than global Markov chain mixing dynamics. SMC is a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method, which starts by drawing N N particles from a known distribution, and then, through a sequence of distributions, re-weights and re-samples the particles, at each instance applying a Markov chain for smoothing. In principle, SMC tries to alleviate problems from multi-modality. However, most theoretical guarantees for SMC are obtained by assuming global mixing time bounds, which are only efficient in the uni-modal setting. We show that bounds can be obtained in the truly multi-modal setting, with mixing times that depend only on local MCMC dynamics. Prior works in the multi-modal setting [ 24 , 22 , 19 ] make the restrictive assumption that the Markov kernel in the SMC algorithm has no or limited movement between disjoint partitions of the state space. Instead, we allow for mixing between modes by only considering SMC with Markov processes whose generator decomposes as ⟨ f , ℒ k ​ f ⟩ μ k ≤ ∑ i = 1 m w i ​ ⟨ f , ℒ k ​ i ​ f ⟩ μ k ( i ) \langle f,\mathscr{L}_{k}f\rangle_{\mu_{k}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{ki}f\rangle_{\mu_{k}^{(i)}} . For mixture distributions, this covers common Markov chain approaches such as Langevin dynamics, Glauber dynamics and the Metropolis random walk. The main technical innovations in our proof are showing variance decay and hypercontractivity for mixture distributions. We apply our results to show efficient sampling from Gaussian mixtures.

Introduction

Obtaining samples from a given target distribution of the form
p(x)\propto e^{-V(x)}
is an important task across multiple disciplines.
In higher dimensions, Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods are a useful tool in obtaining samples from a specified target. The idea is that given a number of particles, running a Markov chain to equilibrium will yield samples from the chosen stationary distribution. This generally works well for uni-modal distributions. However, multi-modal distributions pose serious issues for mixing times of MCMC methods. Specifically, the long time needed to move between modes can yield very poor mixing times.

Sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) methods attempt to circumvent this issue by preserving the appropriate amount of samples in each mode. The general idea is that SMC obtains samples from a target distribution
\mu=\mu_{n}
by moving particles through a sequence of distributions
\mu_{0},\dots,\mu_{n}
. Particles are moved from
\mu_{k-1}
to
\mu_{k}
and then re-sampled according to their respective weights. After re-sampling at each level, a Markov chain is run so that the particles are more representative of the stationary distribution
\mu_{k}
.

SMC methods are well studied and there exists a body of work providing asymptotic and non-asymptotic guarantees
[
25
,
7
]
. However, much of the existing work only establishes bounds given global mixing times. In the multi-modal setting, these bounds are only relevant if the Markov chain is run for long times.
Moreover, any bound depending on global mixing times does not improve upon bounds for vanilla MCMC methods targeting a single distribution, and hence does not explain the observed advantage of
sequential
Monte Carlo for multi-modal distributions.
This issue is addressed by
[
24
,
19
]
; however, both papers require a restrictive assumption that MCMC kernels cannot move samples between disjoint sets of the state space. By leveraging meta-stable approximations,
[
22
]
obtain a bound using local MCMC dynamics that allows movement between components. However, this approach requires very specific run times to ensure that particles only mix to the interior of a mode, thus limiting its application.

Our work builds upon existing literature by providing non-asymptotic variance for a non-negative function
f
under the empirical measure
\mu_{n}^{N}
which only depend on local mixing, and allow for arbitrarily overlapping support of mixture components.
Our results apply to SMC where the chosen kernel has the property that
its generator satisfies the decomposition
\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{k}f\rangle_{\mu_{k}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{ki}f\rangle_{\mu_{k}^{(i)}}
, where
\mathscr{L}_{i}
is the generator over the ith mixture component. It is easy to find popular examples of Markov chains which exhibit this property, including Langevin Dynamics and Metropolis Random Walk (MRW).

There are various other methods to approach sampling from multi-modal distributions. Closely related to SMC, simulated tempering
[
18
]
moves particles between a sequence of distributions, allowing particles to move both directions through the sequence, and at each step applying a Markov kernel at each level. Guarantees for sampling with simulated tempering using local mixing of Langevin Dynamics are provided in
[
11
]
.
Other approaches include parallel tempering
[
28
]
and annealed importance sampling
[
21
]
.

A primary advantage of SMC compared to tempering methods is that we only have to move samples through distributions in one direction. However, this makes SMC more challenging to analyze, as the algorithm can no longer be understood as a single Markov chain, and so Markov chain decomposition theorems
[
17
]
do not apply. We will prove results for SMC under similar, albeit slightly stronger, assumptions on multimodal distributions that have been considered for simulated tempering
[
11
]
.

1.1
Overview of Sequential Monte Carlo

Given a measure
\mu_{n}
with density
p_{n}(x)\propto e^{-V(x)}
, Monte Carlo methods seeks to estimate
\mu_{n}(f)
by
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\xi_{n}^{(i)})
, where
\xi_{n}^{(i)}\sim\mu_{n}
for
1\leq i\leq N
.
SMC achieves this by first sampling
\{\xi_{1}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}\sim\mu_{1}
, where
\mu_{1}
in practice is chosen to be a measure for which samples are easily obtainable. The
N
particles are then transformed through a sequence of measures
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}
, resulting in (approximate) samples from
\mu_{n}
. In the transformation process, each particle is re-weighted according to the ratio
\frac{d\mu_{k}}{d\mu_{k-1}}(\xi_{k-1}^{(i)})
, which will be denoted as
\bar{g}_{k-1,k}(\xi_{k-1}^{(i)})
. The particles are then re-sampled with their respective weights and a Markov kernel is applied for smoothing.

Algorithm 1

Sequential Monte Carlo

1:

Sample
\{\xi_{1}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}\sim\mu_{1}
.

2:

for

k=1,\dots,n-1

do

3:

Re-sample
\{\hat{\xi}_{k+1}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
from a multinomial distribution with probabilities
\{w_{k+1}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
where

w_{k+1}^{(i)}=\frac{\bar{g}_{k,k+1}(\xi_{k}^{(i)})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\bar{g}_{k,k+1}(\xi_{k}^{(j)})}.

Let
\hat{\xi}_{k+1}^{(i)}=\xi_{k}^{(i^{\prime})}
if index
i^{\prime}
is chosen.

4:

Obtain
\{{\xi}_{k+1}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
by applying the transition kernel
P_{k+1}
to
\{\hat{\xi}_{k+1}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.

5:

end

for

6:

Output
\{\xi_{n}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
as approximate samples from
\mu_{n}
.

q_{k-1,k}(f)\vcentcolon=\bar{g}_{k-1,k}P_{k}(f).

The above can be interpreted as a single step of scaling the weights from

1.3
Discrete and Continuous-Time Generators

A central assumption of our paper (Assumption
1
) relies on properties of a Markov generator
\mathscr{L}
and its associated Dirichlet form
\mathcal{E}(f,f)=-\langle f,\mathscr{L}f\rangle_{\mu}
. The building blocks for our main result, Lemma
24

(bounding intra-mode variance) and Lemma
29

(hypercontractivity), are presented in a continuous-time framework.

To show that our main results can be applied to a variant of common discrete-time Markov chains, namely Glauber dynamics (Section
3.1.2
) and the Metropolis-Hastings chain (Section
3.1.3
), we formally connect the Markov generator of a discrete chain to its continuous time equivalent.

For any discrete-time, reversible Markov chain
P
with stationary distribution
\pi
, we can define its associated
continuous-time Markov semigroup

P_{t}
by:

P_{t}=e^{t(P-I)}

(2)

The generator
\mathscr{L}
of the continuous time equivalent is defined as:

\mathscr{L}f=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P_{t}f|_{t=0}=(P-I)f.

•

Discrete-Time:
The Dirichlet form for the chain
P
is what we have already defined in Equation (
1
):

\mathcal{E}_{P}(f,f):=\langle f,(I-P)f\rangle_{\pi}.

•

Continuous-Time:
The Dirichlet form for the generator
\mathscr{L}
is:

\mathcal{E}_{\mathscr{L}}(f,f):=-\langle f,\mathscr{L}f\rangle_{\pi}=-\langle f,(P-I)f\rangle_{\pi}=\langle f,(I-P)f\rangle_{\pi}.

As shown, the two Dirichlet forms are identical:

\mathcal{E}_{P}(f,f)=\mathcal{E}_{\mathscr{L}}(f,f).

(3)

This means that to show Assumptions
1
hold, it is equivalent to work with the generator
\mathscr{L}=P-I
.
Moreover, it should be noted that the continuous-time process
P_{t}
can be simulated algorithmically by running the discrete-time process “jump chain” and drawing the holding time between each transition according to a Poisson point process
[
16
]
.
This makes our analysis not only theoretically sound but algorithmically relevant as well.

, we include the main results from

Sections 6–8 contain our contributions to this problem. In Section

2
Main Result

Before giving our main theorem, we first state our assumptions which capture how the distributions in our sequence decompose into multiple modes. These assumptions allow the state space to be either continuous or discrete, as they work for any Markov process whose generator decomposes over the components, with each satisfying a log-Sobolev inequality. We further need to assume that the ratio between subsequent measures is bounded.

Assumption 1
.

Suppose SMC (Algorithm
1
) is run with the sequence of distributions
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}
with kernels
P_{k}=P_{k}^{t_{k}}
, where
P_{k}^{t}
is the Markov semigroup generated by
\mathscr{L}_{k}

with stationary distribution
\mu_{k}
and Dirchlet form
\mathscr{E}_{k}(f,f)=-\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{k}f\rangle_{\mu_{k}}
. In addition, assume the following hold:

1.

At each level
k
, assume
\mu_{k}
decomposes as the mixture
\mu_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{k}}}w_{k}^{(i)}\mu_{k}^{(i)}
, where
M_{k}
is the number of components and
w_{k}^{(i)}>0
are the weights satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{M_{k}}w_{k}^{(i)}=1
.

2.

There exists
\gamma
such that
\frac{d\mu_{k}}{d\mu_{k-1}}\leq\gamma
a.e. for all
k
(uniform upper bound on density ratios).

3.

There exists a decomposition

\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{k}f\rangle_{\mu_{k}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{k}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w_{k}^{(i)}}\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{ki}f\rangle_{\mu_{k}^{(i)}}

where
\mathscr{L}_{ki}
is the generator of some Markov process
P_{ki}
with stationary distribution
\mu_{k}^{(i)}
.

4.

Each distribution
\mu_{k}^{(i)}
satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality of the form

\operatorname{Ent}_{\mu_{k}^{(i)}}[{f^{2}}]\leq c_{ki}\cdot\mathscr{E}_{ki}(f,f),

and
C^{*}_{k}=\max_{i}c_{ki}
.

Remark 2
.

We note that our main theorem requires a bound on the minimum weight
w_{k}^{(i)}
, but does not assume any structure between the components; in particular, there is no requirement of closeness between components at different levels as we do not need any correspondence between them, and even the number of components can change. We leave as an open question how to derive finer bound when we take into account structure between the components. Intuitively, if a mode has small weight, this should only increase the complexity of SMC if this weight increases between levels. When the modes correspond across levels, then a natural conjecture is that the true complexity should depend not on the minimum weight but either a “bottleneck coefficient” or “total variation” in the weights, similarly to analyses of simulated tempering (see
[
28
, (7)]
).

We follow a common way of formalizing multimodality in the literature
[
10
,
13
]
through the mixture assumption (part 1 above) where each component
\mu_{k}^{(i)}
satisfies a functional inequality (as in part 4); that is, the natural Markov chain for the components mixes rapidly. We note that crucially, we only assume the existence of this decomposition, and its form is not known to the algorithm. As heuristic support for the decomposition hypothesis, we note it is polynomially equivalent to having at most
k
low-lying eigenvalues for discrete state space
[
15
]
or for Langevin
[
20
]
, and the latter holds for a distribution with
k
modes in the limit of low temperature under mild regularity assumptions
[
14
, Chapter 8, Propositions 2.1–2.2]
. We note that we do require the decomposition at each level
k
; this avoids cases where the target distribution may decompose well but bottlenecks are present at intermediate levels. An example where these bottlenecks arise is a mixture of Gaussians with different covariances (and perturbations thereof), for which the mixing time of parallel or simulated tempering can be exponentially large
[
27
]
, and in fact, any algorithm that samples from this family in a black-box setting requires exponentially many queries in general

[
10
]
. We will give concrete examples where the assumptions are satisfied in Section
3
.

Our main result states that under these assumptions, for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a number of samples
N
and a time
t
polynomial in all parameters, such that the SMC algorithm returns
N
samples for which the empirical variance for a bounded function
f
is within
\epsilon
of the true variance. As corollaries to our main result, we will present both a high probability bound on
|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}f(\xi_{n}^{(i)})-\mu_{n}(f)|
and a total variation bound on the empirical measure.

2.

t_{k}\geq 2C_{k}^{*}\gamma^{7}

yields

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}[(\eta_{n}^{N}(f)-\mu_{n}(f))^{2}]}\leq\epsilon.

Proof provided in Section

.

3
Applications

In this section we show that Assumption
1
is satisfied by a sequence of commonly occurring measures and frequently used Markov kernels, and hence Theorem
3
can be applied.
More specifically, we show that condition 3 is satisfied by Langevin diffusion, Glauber Dynamics and Metropolis-Hastings chains (e.g., Metropolis random walk). We show that conditions 1, 2, and 4 are satisfied for sequences of measures defined by power tempering for Gaussian mixtures and Gaussian convolution of arbitrary mixtures.

Functional inequalities including log-Sobolev inequalities for Glauber dynamics on various distributions have been the subject of intense study; see e.g.,
[
1
,
2
,
5
]
. For the Metropolis random walk, weaker inequalities, e.g., for
s
-conductance for log-concave distributions, are known
[
9
]
.
It should also be noted that our results are for the continuous version of Glauber dynamics and Metropolis-Hastings chains. This is not a problem for an algorithmic result, as the continuous chain can be simulated by drawing transition times according to a Poisson process.

Specifically, our bounds require that the Dirichlet form of the continuous process decomposes as
\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{k}f\rangle_{\mu_{k}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{k}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w_{k}^{(i)}}\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{ki}f\rangle_{\mu_{k}^{(i)}}
.
Later in Section
3
we connect our bounds back to the discretized process.

3.1.1
Langevin Diffusion

We first show that condition (2) of Assumption
1
is satisfied by Langevin diffusion, which is a Markov process with stationary distribution
p(x)\propto e^{-V(x)}
. Langevin diffusion is described by the stochastic differential equation

dX_{t}=-\gradient V(X_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,dW_{t},

(4)

where
W_{t}
is Brownian motion. The
time-discretized process with step size
h
is given by

X_{t+1}=X_{t}-h\gradient f(X_{t})+\sqrt{2h}\cdot\xi_{t},\;\;\;\;\xi_{t}\sim N(0,I).

(5)

This can be thought of as a random walk where, at each time step, there is a drift in the direction of the gradient.

Proposition 6
.

For each
k
, let
P_{k}=(\Omega,\mathscr{L}_{k})
be Langevin diffusion with stationary measure
\mu_{k}=\sum{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w_{k}^{(i)}}\mu_{k}^{(i)}
. Then condition (2) of Assumptions
1
holds with equality. Namely,

2.

There exists a decomposition

\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{k}f\rangle_{\mu_{k}}=\sum_{i=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{k}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w_{k}^{(i)}}\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{ki}f\rangle_{\mu_{k}^{(i)}}

where
\mathscr{L}_{ki}
is the generator of Langevin diffusion
P_{ki}
with stationary distribution
\mu_{k}^{(i)}
.

Given Langevin diffusion
\mathscr{L}_{k}
with stationary measure
\mu_{k}
and density
p_{k}(x)\propto e^{-g_{k}(x)}
, we consider the mixture measure
\mu_{k}=\sum{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w_{k}^{(i)}}\mu_{k}^{(i)}
, where the density of
\mu_{k}^{(i)}
is given by

p_{ki}(x)\propto\exp(-g_{ki}(x)).

For Langevin diffusion for a measure
\mu
with density
\propto e^{-g}
, we have that
\mathscr{L}=\Delta f-\langle\gradient g,\gradient f\rangle
. Moreover, by integration by parts we can express the generator as
\langle f,\mathscr{L}f\rangle_{\mu}=\int_{\Omega}\left\|{\gradient f}\right\|^{2}\mu(dx)
. Applying this definition we can show that condition (2) holds:

\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{k}f\rangle_{\mu_{k}}=-\int_{\Omega}\left\|{\gradient f}\right\|^{2}p_{k}(x)dx=-\int_{\Omega}\left\|{\gradient f}\right\|^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{k}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w_{k}^{(i)}}p_{ki}(x)dx=\sum_{i=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{k}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w_{k}^{(i)}}\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{ki}f\rangle_{\mu_{k}^{(i)}}.

∎

3.1.2
Glauber dynamics

We want to show that the decomposition in Assumptions
1
is satisfied for Glauber dynamics for a mixture distribution
p(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{M}w_{k}p_{k}(x)
on the hypercube
\Omega=\{0,1\}^{d}.
Glauber dynamics is the Markov chain whose transition kernel is given as follows: given
x\in\Omega
,

1.

Pick
i\in[1,d]
, with probability
\frac{1}{d}
.

2.

Flip coordinate
x_{i}
, with probability
p(X_{i}=1-x_{i}|X_{-i}=x_{-i}).

Here,
x_{-i}
denotes
(x_{j})_{j\neq i}\in\{0,1\}^{[n]\backslash\{i\}}
.
The above process yields the transition probabilities

P(y|x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}p(X_{i}=x_{i}|X_{-i}=x_{-i})&x=y\\
\frac{1}{d}p(X_{-i}=1-x_{i}|X_{-i}=x_{-i})&\|x-y\|=1,x_{i}\neq y_{i}\\
0&\|x-y\|>1\end{cases}

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the 1-norm (Hamming distance). The continuous-time Glauber dynamics is the continuous-time Markov process whose generator is given by
\mathscr{L}=P-I
.

Proposition 7
.

Let
P
be the transition kernel and
\mathscr{L}=P-I
be the generator of Glauber dynamics on
\{0,1\}^{d}
with stationary measure
p(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}p_{k}(x)
. Then condition (2) of Assumptions
1
holds with equality. Namely,

2.

There exists a decomposition

\langle f,\mathscr{L}f\rangle_{p}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{k}f\rangle_{p_{k}}

where
P_{k}
is the transition kernel and
\mathscr{L}_{k}=P_{k}-I
is the generator of
Glauber dynamics with stationary distribution
p_{k}(x)
.

Let
{x}^{\oplus i}
denote
x
with the
i
th coordinate flipped.
We consider the Dirichlet form for a reversible Markov chain,

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{P}(f,f)\vcentcolon

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}\bigg(f(x)-f(y)\bigg)^{2}p(x)P(y|x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|x-y\|=1\\
x_{i}\neq y_{i}\\
1\leq i\leq d\end{subarray}}\bigg(f(x)-f(y)\bigg)^{2}p(x)\frac{1}{d}P(X_{-i}=y_{i}|X_{-i}=x_{-i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigg(f(x)-f({x}^{\oplus i})\bigg)^{2}\sum_{k}w_{k}p_{k}(x)\frac{\sum_{k}w_{k}p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})}{\sum_{k}w_{k}\big(p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})+p_{k}(x)\big)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigg(f(x)-f({x}^{\oplus i})\bigg)^{2}\bigg(\frac{\sum_{k}w_{k}p_{k}(x)}{\sum w_{k}\big(p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})+p_{k}(x)\big)}\frac{\sum_{k}w_{k}p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})}{\sum_{k}w_{k}\big(p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})+p_{k}(x)\big)}\bigg)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\sum_{k}w_{k}\big(p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})+p_{k}(x)\big)

Defining sequences
a_{k}=\frac{w_{k}p_{k}(x)}{w_{k}\big(p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})+p_{k}(x)\big)}
,
b_{k}=\frac{w_{k}p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})}{w_{k}\big(p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})+p_{k}(x)\big)}
with weights
\alpha_{k}=\frac{w_{k}\big(p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})+p_{k}(x)\big)}{\sum_{k}w_{k}\big(p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})+p_{k}(x)\big)}
, noting that
a_{k}
,
b_{k}
are oppositely sorted sequences, we can apply the weighted version of Chebyshev’s sum inequality to get,

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigg(f(x)-f({x}^{\oplus i})\bigg)^{2}\sum_{k}\frac{w_{k}\big(p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})+p_{k}(x)\big)}{\sum_{k}w_{k}\big(p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})+p_{k}(x)\big)}\cdot\frac{p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})}{p_{k}(x)+p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\frac{p_{k}(x)}{p_{k}(x)+p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})}\cdot\sum_{k}w_{k}\big(p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})+p_{k}(x)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigg(f(x)-f({x}^{\oplus i})\bigg)^{2}\sum_{k}\frac{p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})}{p_{k}(x)+p_{k}({x}^{\oplus i})}\cdot w_{k}p_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}w_{k}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bigg(f(x)-f({x}^{\oplus i})\bigg)^{2}p_{k}(x)P_{k}(X_{i}=1-x_{i}|X_{-i}=x_{-i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}w_{k}\mathscr{E}_{P_{k}}(f,f).

By (
1
) we have that
\langle f,\mathscr{L}f\rangle_{p}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{k}f\rangle_{p_{k}}
as desired.
∎

3.1.3
Metropolis-Hastings chain

We will show that condition (2) of Assumption
1
is met by Markov chains of Metropolis-Hastings type. A Metropolis-Hastings Markov chain is a discrete-time Markov process that converge to a stationary distribution
\pi
by iterating two steps. First, given an initial point
x
, a step
y
is proposed according to the conditional distribution
P(y|x)
(called the proposal distribution). The proposal step
y
is accepted with probability

\alpha(x,y)\vcentcolon=\min\bigg(1,\frac{\pi(y)P(x|y)}{\pi(x)P(y|x)}\bigg).

The transition kernel
Q(y|x)
for
y\neq x
is given by

Q(y|x)\vcentcolon=P(y|x)\alpha(x,y).

For example, on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
P(y|x)
is often taken to be a Gaussian distribution centered at
x
, or uniform over a ball or ellipsoid (and the chain is called the Metropolis random walk).
Again, this can be converted to a continuous-time process which can be simulated algorithmically.

Proposition 8
.

Let
Q
be the transition kernel and
\mathscr{L}=Q-I
be the generator of the Metropolis-Hastings chain on
\Omega
with proposal
P
and stationary measure
\pi(x)=\sum_{k}w_{k}\pi_{k}(x)
. Then condition (2) of Assumptions
1
holds. Namely,

2.

There exists a decomposition

\langle f,\mathscr{L}f\rangle_{\pi}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{k}f\rangle_{\pi_{k}}

where
Q_{k}
is the transition kernel and
\mathscr{L}_{k}=Q_{k}-I
is the generator of the Metropolis-Hastings chain on
\Omega
with proposal
P
and stationary distribution
\pi_{k}(x)
.

For a reversible Markov chain we have that,

\displaystyle\mathscr{E}_{Q}(f,f)\vcentcolon

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}\bigg(f(x)-f(y)\bigg)^{2}\pi(x)Q(y|x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}\bigg(f(x)-f(y)\bigg)^{2}\pi(x)\min\bigg\{1,\frac{\pi(y)P(x|y)}{\pi(x)P(y|x)}\bigg\}P(y|x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}\bigg(f(x)-f(y)\bigg)^{2}\min\bigg\{\sum_{k}w_{k}\pi_{k}(x)P(y|x),\sum_{k}w_{k}\pi_{k}(y)P(x|y)\bigg\}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}\bigg(f(x)-f(y)\bigg)^{2}\sum_{k}w_{k}\min\bigg\{\pi_{k}(x)P(y|x),\pi_{k}(y)P(x|y)\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}w_{k}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Omega}\bigg(f(x)-f(y)\bigg)^{2}\min\bigg\{1,\frac{\pi_{k}(y)P(x|y)}{\pi_{k}(x)P(y|x)}\bigg\}\pi_{k}(x)P(y|x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}w_{k}\mathscr{E}_{Q_{k}}(f,f)

By definition
1
we have that
\langle f,\mathscr{L}f\rangle_{\pi}\leq\sum_{k}w_{k}\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{k}f\rangle_{\pi_{k}}.

∎

Target Distributions and Measure Sequences

In this subsection we show that our main Theorem

In each setting we show that its possible to finitely bound the density ratio

3.2.1
Gaussian Mixtures and Power Tempering

We now apply our results to a concrete setting where the target distribution
\pi(x)=\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}\cdot q(x;\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k})
is a mixture of Gaussians (with means
\mu_{k}
and variances
\Sigma_{k}
). This verifies that SMC is efficient in a setting where simulated tempering is known to succeed
[
11
]
, with a different proof. We will consider the sequence of measures
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{n}
defined by power tempering
\pi_{i}(x)\propto\pi(x)^{\beta_{i}}
for a chosen temperature ladder
\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n}

[
18
,
21
,
11
]
for defining a sequence of distributions and in this setting, the rest of our Assumptions
1
can be easily verified.

Proposition 9
.

Let the target distribution be a mixture of
M
Gaussians

\pi(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\alpha_{k}q(x;\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k}),\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{M}\alpha_{k}=1,\qquad\alpha_{k}>0,

where
q(x;\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k})
denotes the density of
\mathcal{N}(\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k}).
Let
0<\beta_{1}<\dots<\beta_{n}=1
be a temperature ladder and define the sequence of measures

\pi_{i}(x)\propto\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x)\vcentcolon=\pi(x)^{\beta_{i}}.

Then the following properties hold:

1.

(Density Ratio Bound) Assumption
1
(2) is satisfied with
\frac{d\pi_{i}}{d\pi_{i-1}}\leq\gamma_{i}
, where for
\Delta\beta=\beta_{i}-\beta_{i-1}

\gamma_{i}=\frac{1}{\min_{k}\alpha_{k}}\left(\frac{\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i-1}}\right)^{d/2}\left(\frac{\max_{k}|\Sigma_{k}|}{\min_{k}|\Sigma_{k}|}\right)^{\Delta\beta/2}.

2.

(Local LSI Constants) Each
\pi_{i}
is expressible as a mixture
\pi_{i}=\sum w_{k}^{(i)}\nu_{k}^{(i)}

\nu_{k}^{(i)}

\nu^{(i)}_{k}(x)\propto\frac{\pi(x)^{\beta_{i}}}{\sum_{j=1}^{M}\alpha_{j}q(x;\mu_{j},\Sigma_{j})^{\beta_{i}}}q(x;\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k})^{\beta_{i}}.

Then Assumption
1

3.

(Weight Lower Bound) The normalized mixture weights satisfy

\min_{i,k}w_{k}^{(i)}\geq\min_{i,k}\frac{\int q(x;\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k})^{\beta_{i}}dx}{\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}\int q(x;\mu_{k},\Sigma_{j})^{\beta_{i}}dx}\min_{k}\alpha_{k}^{2}.

If
\Sigma_{j}=\Sigma_{k}
, for all
j,k\in[1,M]
, then this reduces to
\min_{i,k}w_{k}^{(i)}\geq\min_{k}\alpha_{k}^{2}
.

Let
Z_{k}=\int\pi(x)^{\beta_{k}}dx
. To bound the ratio we consider

\frac{\pi_{i}(x)}{\pi_{i-1}(x)}=\frac{Z_{i-1}}{Z_{i}}\frac{\pi(x)^{\beta_{i}}}{\pi(x)^{\beta_{i-1}}}=\frac{Z_{i-1}}{Z_{i}}\pi(x)^{\Delta\beta}.

Then the density
\pi(x)
can be bounded by

\displaystyle\pi(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{M}\alpha_{k}q_{k}(x)\leq\max_{k}\left\|q_{k}\right\|_{\infty}=\max_{k}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}|\Sigma_{k}|^{1/2}}.

(6)

To bound the normalizing constants, using that
\beta_{i}\in(0,1)
, we have by the power mean inequality that

Z_{i}=\int\left(\sum_{k=1}^{M}\alpha_{k}q_{k}(x)\right)^{\beta_{i}}dx\geq\sum_{k=1}^{M}\alpha_{k}\int q_{k}(x)^{\beta_{i}}dx.

We find an upper bound by

\pi(x)^{\beta_{i}}\leq\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}^{\beta_{i}}q_{k}(x)^{\beta_{i}}=\sum_{k}\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\alpha_{k}^{1-\beta_{i}}}q_{k}(x)^{\beta_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{\min_{k}\alpha_{k}}\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}q_{k}(x)^{\beta_{i}}.

Then

Z_{i}=\int\pi(x)^{\beta_{i}}dx\leq\frac{1}{\min_{k}\alpha_{k}}\sum_{k=1}^{M}\alpha_{k}\int q_{k}(x)^{\beta_{i}}dx.

Therefore, using that
\int q_{k}(x)^{\beta_{i}}dx=\frac{(2\pi)^{d(1-\beta_{i})/2}}{\beta_{i}^{d/2}}|\Sigma_{k}|^{(1-\beta_{i})/2}
the ratio
\frac{Z_{i-1}}{Z_{i}}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\frac{Z_{i-1}}{Z_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\min_{k}\alpha_{k}}\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{M}\alpha_{k}\int q_{k}(x)^{\beta_{i-1}}dx}{\sum_{k=1}^{M}\alpha_{k}\int q_{k}(x)^{\beta_{i}}dx}\leq\frac{1}{\min_{k}\alpha_{k}}\cdot\max_{k}\frac{\int q_{k}(x)^{\beta_{i-1}}dx}{\int q_{k}(x)^{\beta_{i}}dx}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\min_{k}\alpha_{k}}\left(\frac{\beta_{i}}{\beta_{i-1}}\right)^{d/2}(2\pi)^{d\Delta\beta/2}\max_{k}|\Sigma_{k}|^{\Delta\beta/2}.

(7)

Combining (
6
) and (
7
) yields the first part.

Next we show an upper bound on the LSI constant. Consider the distribution
{\pi}_{\textup{mix},i}(x)\propto\sum_{k=1}^{M}\alpha_{k}q(x;\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k})^{\beta_{i}}=:\tilde{\pi}_{\textup{mix},i}
. It’s shown in
[
11
, Lemma 7.3]
that

\tilde{\pi}_{\textup{mix},i}(x)\leq\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x)\leq\frac{1}{\min_{k}\alpha_{k}}\tilde{\pi}_{\textup{mix},i}(x).

(8)

Next, we will consider a hypothetical mixture
\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x)=\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}^{(i)}\tilde{\nu}_{k}^{(i)}(x)
for some
\alpha_{k}^{(i)}
,
\tilde{\nu}_{k}^{(i)}
.
We choose
\alpha_{k}^{(i)}=\alpha_{k}
and
\tilde{\nu}_{k}^{(i)}=\frac{\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x)}{\tilde{\pi}_{\textup{mix},i}(x)}q(x;\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k})^{\beta_{i}}
.
Then considering
\tilde{\nu}_{k}^{(i)}
, by (
8

\displaystyle q(x;\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k})^{\beta_{i}}\leq\frac{\tilde{\pi}_{i}(x)}{\tilde{\pi}_{\textup{mix},i}(x)}q(x;\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k})^{\beta_{i}}\leq\frac{1}{\min_{k}\alpha_{k}}q(x;\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k})^{\beta_{i}}.

Then by Holley-Stroock (
[
6

) we have that

C_{\textup{LSI}}(\nu_{k}^{(i)})\leq\frac{1}{\min_{k}\alpha_{k}}C_{\textup{LSI}}(q(x;\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k})^{\beta_{i}}),

where we use

to denote the log-Sobolev constant of the normalized density

.
Since
q(x;\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k})^{\beta_{i}}=C\cdot q(x;\mu_{k},\frac{\Sigma_{k}}{\beta_{i}})
for some constant

C_{\textup{LSI}}(q(x;\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k})^{\beta_{i}})\leq\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\Sigma_{k})}{\beta_{i}}

We have that

\displaystyle w_{j}^{(i)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\alpha_{j}\int q(x;\mu_{j},\Sigma_{j})^{\beta_{i}}dx}{\frac{1}{\min_{k}\alpha_{k}}\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}\int q(x;\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k})^{\beta_{i}}dx}=\frac{\alpha_{j}\int q(x;\mu_{j},\Sigma_{j})^{\beta_{i}}dx}{\sum_{k}\alpha_{k}\int q(x;\mu_{k},\Sigma_{k})^{\beta_{i}}dx}\min_{k}\alpha_{k}

\Sigma_{k}=\Sigma_{j}

\min_{i,k}w_{k}^{(i)}\geq(\min_{k}\alpha_{k})^{2}

.

3.2.2
Gaussian Convolutions and Noise Contrastive Estimation

Noise Contrastive Estimation (NCE) is a estimation method for parametrized statistical models
[
12
]
which has recently been adapted in machine learning to train energy-based models. Given a set of points
\{x_{n}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
drawn from
\mu_{n}
, NCE produces an estimate of the data distribution
\mu_{n}
by comparing these data points to points
\{x_{1}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
generated from some known noise distribution
\mu_{1}
and estimating
\frac{d\mu_{n}}{d\mu_{1}}
.
When the sampled points
\{x_{1}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\{x_{n}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
have little overlap, NCE can provide poor estimates of
\mu_{n}
. Telescoping Ratio Estimation (TRE) is an extension of NCE that circumvents this issue by interpolating between the two sets of points to obtain the sets
\{x_{k}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
k=2,\dots,n-1

[
23
]
. NCE is applied between sets
\{x_{k}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
and
\{x_{k+1}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
to learn
\frac{d\mu_{k+1}}{d\mu_{k}}
and hence
\mu_{k+1}
inductively, for
k=1,\dots,n
.

We now apply our results in a concrete setting where our sequence of distributions
\mu_{k}
is constructed via Gaussian convolution. This is a common technique in diffusion models and score-based generative modeling and also fits naturally in the NCE/TRE framework—since intermediate point sets can be constructed by adding Gaussian noise.

We further specialize to the case where
\mu_{n}
is a mixture,
\mu_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}w_{i}\mu_{n}^{(i)}
. This provides a non-trivial example that satisfies our Assumptions
1
:

1.

It illustrates a special case of our framework where the mixture weights are constant, as
\mu_{k}=\mu_{n}*\varphi_{k}=\sum_{i}w_{i}(\mu_{n}^{(i)}*\varphi_{k})
.
While the density ratio
\bar{g}_{k,k+1}
is not analytically computable (a challenge which we address in remark
15
), it can be bounded by a uniform constant
\lambda
. This is shown explicitly in Proposition
12
.

2.

It provides a concrete setting where Assumptions
1
part 4 (LSI constants) can be explicitly verified. This is shown in a more general setting (Lemma
13
), but in our special setting, since the convolution of Gaussians is Gaussian, the LSI can be explicitly bounded.

The following proposition shows how intermediate point sets can be constructed by adding Gaussian noise.

Proposition 11
.

Let
\{x_{n}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
be from some data distribution with unknown measure
\pi_{n}
and define

x_{k-1}^{(i)}\vcentcolon=x_{k}^{(i)}+\sigma\xi,\;\xi\sim N(0,1),

for
k=2,\dots,n
. Then the measure
\mu_{k}
of
x_{k}^{(i)}
is given by

\mu_{k}=\mu_{n}*\varphi_{k},

where
\varphi_{k}\sim N(0,\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2})
.

Follows by additivity of variance for Gaussians.
∎

As defined in Proposition
11
,
\varphi_{1}
is a high variance Gaussian, which when convolved with a measure
\mu_{n}
yields a measure that is approximately Gaussian itself. Therefore, we can approximately sample from
\mu_{n}*\varphi_{1}
; see Remark
15
for details.
We now show that, given a target distribution
\mu_{n}
, our main result can be applied to the sequence of distributions
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}
where
\mu_{k}
, is defined as in Proposition
11
.

Proposition 12
.

Let
\mu_{n}=\sum_{i}w_{i}\mu_{n}^{(i)}
be the target measure and let

\varphi_{k}(x)\propto e^{-\beta_{k}\frac{\left\|{x}\right\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}},

with
0<\beta_{1}<\dots<\beta_{n-1}

Then condition (1) of Theorem
5

\frac{\mu_{n}*\varphi_{k}(x)}{\mu_{n}*\varphi_{k-1}(x)}\leq\gamma=\bigg(\frac{\beta_{k}}{\beta_{k-1}}\bigg)^{\frac{d}{2}}.

Consider the following:

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{n}*\varphi_{k}(x)}{\mu_{n}*\varphi_{k-1}(x)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\beta_{k}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\int d\mu_{n}^{(i)}(y)\exp(-\beta_k\frac{\norm{x-y}^2}{2\sigma^2})dy}{\sqrt{\beta_{k-1}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\int d\mu_{n}^{(i)}(y)\exp(-\beta_{k-1}\frac{\norm{x-y}^2}{2\sigma^2})dy}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\beta_{k}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\int d\mu_{n}^{(i)}(y)\exp(-(\beta_{k-1}+\beta_k-\beta_{k-1})\frac{\norm{x-y}^2}{2\sigma^2})dy}{\sqrt{\beta_{k-1}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\int d\mu_{n}^{(i)}(y)\exp(-\beta_{k-1}\frac{\norm{x-y}^2}{2\sigma^2})dy}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\beta_{k}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\int d\mu_{n}^{(i)}(y)\exp(-\beta_{k-1}\frac{\norm{x-y}^2}{2\sigma^2})\exp(-(\beta_k-\beta_{k-1})\frac{\norm{x-y}^2}{2\sigma^2})dy}{\sqrt{\beta_{k-1}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\int d\mu_{n}^{(i)}(y)\exp(-\beta_{k-1}\frac{\norm{x-y}^2}{2\sigma^2})dy}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{\beta_{k}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\int d\mu_{n}^{(i)}(y)\exp(-\beta_{k-1}\frac{\norm{x-y}^2}{2\sigma^2})dy}{\sqrt{\beta_{k-1}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\int d\mu_{n}^{(i)}(y)\exp(-\beta_{k-1}\frac{\norm{x-y}^2}{2\sigma^2})dy}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{\beta_{k}}{\beta_{k-1}}\bigg)^{\frac{d}{2}}.

∎

and Section

Lemma 13

Remark 15
.

It should be noted that the focus of our work is on analyzing sampling error. To get complete non-asymptotic error bounds for the TRE problem, one would also need to consider the error from learnt ratios
\hat{g}_{k,k+1}

\hat{\mu}^{\text{approx}}_{n}

4
Overview of Proof

In this section we first provide a conceptual overview of our main results and then follow this with a more detailed analytical summary.

Conceptually, each resampling step introduces error, which is alleviated by mixing of the Markov process. In their recursive framework to bound the error,
[
24
]
quantify the mixing through variance decay and hypercontractivity for the Markov process.
Specifically, they use the fact that under global functional inequalities (log-Sobolev constant
C
), the following hold:

1.

(Variance decay)
\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(P_{t}f)\leq e^{-\frac{2t}{C}}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(f)
.

2.

(Hypercontractivity)
\left\|{P_{t}f}\right\|_{q(t)}\leq\left\|{f}\right\|_{p}
, where
q(t)=1+(p-1)e^{\frac{2t}{C}}
.

In our case, global functional inequalities are not available.
We prove analogous bounds for mixture distributions under local functional inequalities, (that is,
\pi=\sum_{k}w_{k}\pi_{k}
where each
\pi_{k}
has satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with constant
C^{*}
):

1.

(Intra-mode variance decay)
\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(P_{t}f)\leq\frac{C^{*}}{2t}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(f)+\sum_{k}\frac{1}{w_{k}}\pi(f)^{2}
.

2.

(Hypercontractivity for mixture)
\frac{1}{(w^{*})^{1/q(t)}}\left\|{P_{t}f}\right\|_{L_{q(t)}(\pi)}\leq\frac{1}{(w^{*})^{1/p}}\left\|{f}\right\|_{L_{p}(\pi)}
, where
q(t)=1+(p-1)e^{\frac{2t}{C^{*}}}
.

Analytically, in Section
5
, we adapt the framework of
[
25
]
used to obtain variance bounds for the SMC estimate.

1.

We leverage that
\operatorname{Var}(\mu_{n}^{N}(f))=\mathbb{E}[(\mu_{n}^{N}(f)-\mu(f))^{2}]
can be bounded by
\operatorname{Var}(\nu_{n}^{N}(f))=\mathbb{E}[(\nu^{N}_{n}(f)-\mu(f))^{2}]
, where
\nu_{n}^{N}(f)
is an unbiased estimate of
\mu_{n}^{N}(f)
(Lemma
16
).

2.

We show that
\operatorname{Var}(\nu_{n}^{N}(f))
can be bounded in terms of two quantities
\hat{c}_{n}
and
\bar{v}_{n}
. These quantities can be bounded explicitly given two ingredients:

a)

A single-step bound of the form

\displaystyle\|\hat{q}_{k-1,k}(f)\|_{L_{2}(\mu_{k-1})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\alpha\|f\|_{L_{2}(\mu_{k})}^{2}+\beta\mu_{k}(f)^{2}.

(10)

for all
1<k\leq n
and some
\alpha<1
.
By making effective use of global mixing,
[
25
]
assumes
\beta<1
. We modify these results, allowing for
\beta\geq 1
.

b)

A hypercontractive inequality of the form

\displaystyle\left\|{P_{k}f}\right\|_{L_{p}(\mu_{k})}

\displaystyle\leq\theta(p,p/2)\left\|{f}\right\|_{L_{p/2}(\mu_{k})}

for all
k
.

In context,
\alpha
controls the variance bound on a single SMC step (
10
). Most notably,
\alpha<1
allows us to iterate over
n
steps of SMC without accruing error. To obtain a bound (
10
) using only local mixing we decompose the variance into the intra-mode and inter-mode variances of the mixture measures
\mu_{k}=\sum_{i}w_{i}\mu_{k}^{(i)}
(see (
13
)). By utilizing this variance decomposition we are able to bound the intra-mode variance using local Poincaré constants.

1.

In Section
6
, we obtain a bound for the intra-mode variance which depends only on local mixing (Lemma
24
). Similarly, we obtain a bound for the inter-mode variance. These bounds depend on the local Poincaré constants and the minimum mode weight
w^{*}=\min_{k}w_{k}
.

2.

In Section
7
, we obtain a bound of the form
\|\hat{q}_{k-1,k}(f)\|_{L_{2}(\mu_{k-1})}^{2}\leq\alpha_{k}\|f\|_{L_{2}(\mu_{k})}^{2}+\beta\mu_{k}(f)^{2}
(Lemma
27
), where
\alpha_{k}
depends on our local mixing results from Section
6
. By bounding
\alpha_{k}
for all
k
we are able to obtain the bound in equation (
10
)) above.

3.

In Section
8
, we obtain a bound of the form
\left\|{P_{k}f}\right\|_{L_{p}(\mu_{k})}\leq\theta(p,p/2)\left\|{f}\right\|_{L_{p/2}(\mu_{k})}
. We are able to determine
\theta(p,p/2)
as a function of
p
and the minimum mode weight
w^{*}=\min_{k}w_{k}
. This provides us with a closed form for
\hat{c}_{n}
in our final results.

Finally, to obtain better mixing time results we show that for any
\alpha<1
such that
\alpha_{k}<\alpha
for all
k
, Theorem
5
holds. This yields mixing times which depend only on the local mixing given by each
\alpha_{k}
.

First we note that the error of the SMC estimate can be bounded by the variance of

Lemma 16

6
Log-Sobolev Bounds & Local Smoothing

In this section we consider the inter- and intra-mode variance of a mixture distribution. By limiting SMC to Markov chains whose generators are of the form
\langle f,\mathscr{L}f\rangle_{\mu}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}w_{i}\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{i}f\rangle_{\mu_{i}}
we are able to obtain bounds for the intra-mode variance that depend only on the spectral gap of the decomposed chain. We also note that Assumption
1
provides us with log-Sobolev inequalities. Log-Sobolev bounds are stronger than Poincaré inequalities and in fact imply the Poincaré bounds
\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(f)\leq c_{\textup{LSI}}\mathscr{E}_{\pi}(f,f)
with the same constant. Therefore in this section we freely assume Poincaré inequalities. We will need to apply the stronger log-Sobolev bounds to give us hypercontractive results which depend only on local mixing.

6.1
Bounding the intra-mode variance

Lemma 23
.

If the Markov semigroup
P_{t}
is reversible with stationary distribution
\pi
, then

\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(P_{t}f)\geq 0.

Hence,
-\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(P_{t}f)=-2\left\langle{P_{t}f,\mathscr{L}P_{t}f}\right\rangle
is strictly decreasing.

We compute

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(P_{t}f)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\left({\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(P_{t}f)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}2\left\langle{P_{t}f,\mathscr{L}P_{t}f}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2(\left\langle{\mathscr{L}P_{t}f,\mathscr{L}P_{t}f}\right\rangle+\left\langle{P_{t}f,\mathscr{L}^{2}P_{t}f}\right\rangle)

Since
\mathscr{L}
is self-adjoint,

\displaystyle=4\left\langle{\mathscr{L}P_{t}f,\mathscr{L}P_{t}f}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=4\|\mathscr{L}P_{t}f\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}^{2}\geq 0.

∎

Lemma 24
.

Suppose that for a Markov generator
\mathscr{L}
with stationary distribution
\pi=\sum_{k}w_{k}\pi_{k}
there exists a decomposition
\langle f,\mathscr{L}f\rangle_{\pi}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{k}f\rangle_{\pi_{k}}
, where
\mathscr{L}_{k}
has stationary measure
\pi_{k}
and Poincaré constant
C_{k}
. Let
C^{*}=\max_{k}C_{k}
. Then for any function
f
,

\sum_{k=1}^{M}w_{k}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi_{k}}(P_{T}f)\leq\frac{C^{*}}{2T}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(f).

Since we have that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(P_{t}f)

\displaystyle=2\left\langle{P_{t}f,\mathscr{L}P_{t}f}\right\rangle,

we can consider

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(f)

\displaystyle\geq 2\int_{0}^{T}-\left\langle{P_{t}f,\mathscr{L}P_{t}f}\right\rangle_{\pi}dt

By Lemma
23
, since
-2\left\langle{P_{t}f,\mathscr{L}P_{t}f}\right\rangle
is a strictly decreasing function of
t
,

\displaystyle\geq-2T\left\langle{P_{T}f,\mathscr{L}P_{T}f}\right\rangle_{\pi}

\displaystyle\geq 2T\sum_{k=1}^{M}-w_{k}\left\langle{P_{T}f,\mathscr{L}_{k}P_{T}f}\right\rangle_{\pi_{k}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2T}{C^{*}}\sum_{k=1}^{M}w_{k}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi_{k}}(P_{T}f).

Hence
\sum_{k=1}^{M}w_{k}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi_{k}}(P_{T}f)\leq\frac{C^{*}}{2T}\operatorname{Var}_{\pi}(f)
.
∎

6.2
Bounding the total variance

We now apply Lemma

to obtain bounds on

which only depend on mixing within modes. First, note that

\operatorname{Var}_{\mu_{k-1}}(\hat{q}_{k-1,k}(f))=\sum_{i=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{k-1}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w_{k-1}^{(i)}}\operatorname{Var}_{\mu_{k-1}^{(i)}}(\hat{q}_{k-1,k}(f))+\sum_{i=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{k-1}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w_{k-1}^{(i)}}\bigg(\mu_{k-1}^{(i)}\big(P_{k-1}^{t_{k-1}}(\bar{g}_{k-1,k}f)\big)-\mu_{k-1}\big(\bar{g}_{k-1,k}f\big)\bigg)^{2}.

(13)

We prove and make use of the above decomposition in the following section (Section

Corollary 25
.

Suppose Assumption

\sum_{i=1}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}M_{k-1}}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}w_{k-1}^{(i)}}\operatorname{Var}_{\mu_{k-1}^{(i)}}(\hat{q}_{k-1,k}(f))\leq\frac{C_{k-1}^{*}\cdot\gamma}{2T}\mu_{k}(f^{2})

8
Hypercontractivity

Up to now we have only used the weaker Poincaré inequalities. In this section, we make use of the log-Sobolev inequalities to give us a hypercontractive result. Hypercontractivity allows us to exploit the smoothing properties of a Markov kernel to obtain bounds of the form

\displaystyle\|P_{t}f\|_{L^{q}}

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{L^{p}},

(14)

with
q>p>1
. The general idea is that
\|P_{t}f\|_{L^{q}}\rightarrow\|f\|_{L^{1}}
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.
Hence, given
p,q
, for some time
t
we should be able to obtain (
14
), and given
p,t
, we have
\|P_{t}f\|_{L^{q(t)}}\leq\|P_{t}f\|_{L^{p}}
, for some function
q(t)\to\infty
as
t\to\infty
.
Importantly, we show that we can obtain these bounds given only local mixing of
P_{t}
up to a multiplicative constant. This allows us to obtain bounds in Lemma
21
that only require local mixing.

Lemma 29
.

Let
P_{t}
be a reversible Markov process with stationary distribution
\pi=\sum_{k}w_{k}\pi_{k}
. Let

q(t)=1+(p-1)e^{2t/C^{*}}
where
C^{*}=\max_{k}c_{k}
.
Assume that the following hold.

1.

(Assumption
1
, part 2) There exists a decomposition of the form,

\langle f,\mathscr{L}f\rangle_{\pi}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{i}\langle f,\mathscr{L}_{k}f\rangle_{\pi_{k}}.

2.

For each
\pi_{k}
there exists a log-Sobolev inequality of the form,

\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi_{k}}[{f^{2}}]\leq 2c_{k}\cdot\mathscr{E}_{\pi_{k}}(f,f).

Let
f>0
and
w^{*}=\min_{k}w_{k}
.
Then
\frac{\left\|{P_{t}f}\right\|_{L}^{q(t)}(\pi)}{(w^{*})^{\frac{1}{q(t)}}}
is a non-increasing function in
t
:

\frac{\|P_{t}f\|_{L^{q(t)}(\pi)}}{(w^{*})^{\frac{1}{q(t)}}}\leq\frac{\|P_{0}f\|_{L^{q(0)}(\pi)}}{(w^{*})^{\frac{1}{q(0)}}}=\frac{\|f\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}}{(w^{*})^{\frac{1}{p}}}

and

\|P_{t}f\|_{L^{q(t)}(\pi)}\leq\theta(q(t),p)\|f\|_{L^{p}(\pi)}

where

\theta(q,p)=\left({\frac{1}{w^{*}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}-\frac{1}{q}}.

Note first that in our notation
P_{t}f^{q}=(P_{t}f)^{q}
. The result holds if
\frac{\|P_{t}f\|_{L^{q(t)}(\pi)}}{(w^{*})^{\frac{1}{q(t)}}}
is a decreasing function of
q(t)
, which is equivalent to showing,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\log(\frac{\Vert P_tf\Vert_{L^{q(t)}(\pi)}}{(w^*)^\frac{1}{q(t)}})\leq 0.

We will make use of the following results.

1.

We have a decomposition for entropy given by

\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi}[{f^{2}}]=\sum_{k=1}^{m}w_{k}\operatorname{Ent}_{\pi_{k}}[{f^{2}}]+\operatorname{Ent}[{\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{k}}[f^{2}]}].

(15)

2.

We have the following derivative (see below),

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\log(\mathbb{E}_\pi[P_tf^{q(t)}])=\frac{q^{\prime}(t)}{q(t)\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[P_{t}f^{q(t)}]}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\log(P_tf^{q(t)})P_{t}f^{q(t)}]+\frac{q(t)}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[P_{t}f^{q(t)}]}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[P_{t}f^{q(t)-1}\mathscr{L}P_{t}f]

(16)

3.

(
[
26
, Lemma 8.11]
) For
q>1
and a reversible Markov process
P_{t}
with generator
\mathscr{L}
we have

\langle f^{q-1},\mathscr{L}f\rangle_{\pi}\leq\frac{4(q-1)}{q^{2}}\langle f^{\frac{q}{2}},\mathscr{L}f^{\frac{q}{2}}\rangle_{\pi}.

(17)

We show the computations for the derivative (
16
)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\log(\mathbb{E}_\pi[P_tf^{q(t)}])

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[P_{t}f^{q(t)}]}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}P_{t}f^{q(t)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[P_{t}f^{q(t)}]}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}e^{q(t)\log(P_tf)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[P_{t}f^{q(t)}]}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\bigg(q(t)\log(P_tf)\bigg)P_{t}f^{q(t)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[P_{t}f^{q(t)}]}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\bigg(q^{\prime}(t)\log(P_tf)+\frac{q(t)}{P_{t}f}\mathscr{L}P_{t}f\bigg)P_{t}f^{q(t)}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{\prime}(t)}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[P_{t}f^{q(t)}]}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\log(P_tf)P_{t}f^{q(t)}]+\frac{q(t)}{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[P_{t}f^{q(t)}]}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[P_{t}f^{q(t)-1}\mathscr{L}P_{t}f]

Now we consider the following,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\log(\frac{\Vert P_tf\Vert_{L^{q(t)}(\pi)}}{(w^*)^\frac{1}{q(t)}})

By the first assumption,
