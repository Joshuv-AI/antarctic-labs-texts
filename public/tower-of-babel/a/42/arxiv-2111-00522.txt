The Politics of (No) Compromise: Information Acquisition, Policy Discretion, and Reputation
Liqun Liu

Abstract
Precise information is essential for making good policies, especially those regarding reform decisions. However, decision-makers may hesitate to gather such information if certain decisions could have negative impacts on their future careers. We model how decision-makers with career concerns may acquire policy-relevant information and carry out reform decisions when their policy discretion can be limited ex ante. Typically, decision-makers with career concerns have weaker incentives to acquire information compared to decision-makers without such concerns. In this context, we demonstrate that the public can encourage information acquisition by eliminating either the “moderate policy” or the status quo from decision-makers’ discretion. We also analyze when reform decisions should be strategically delegated to decision-makers with or without career concerns. Keywords: Delegation, Information Acquisition, Pandering

Introduction

Precise information is essential for making good policies, especially in decisions regarding reform. However, many decision-makers lack such information, making it difficult to identify innovative policies that may outperform the current status quo. In these situations, a common practice is to take a moderate and often experimental step forward. For instance, China created special economic zones (SEZs) to modernize its economy instead of implementing Russian-style shock therapy or maintaining the existing system.
More often than not, reform decisions are delegated to decision-makers who are granted partial authority instead of being given full discretion or limited to an intermediate option. For instance, when reforming the land system, Chinese local officials must choose between maintaining the existing collective land system and embracing the newly proposed Household Responsibility System (HRS).

Why are decision-makers often constrained in their discretion when making decisions about reforms?

In this paper, we highlight that constraining decision-makers’ discretion can often guarantee high-quality reform decisions when they care about future careers. In the examples above, decision-makers will build a reputation for being “adaptive” or “conservative” based on the policies they choose, which can have political or market-based consequences. These reputation concerns may discourage decision-makers from investing in policy expertise, as fully utilizing such expertise may require them to implement good policies that could be harmful to their reputation. As a result, when the reputation concerns become non-negligible, decision-makers are likely to pander to public opinion rather than focusing on policy-relevant information.
If decision-makers have limited options, their policy stakes tend to outweigh their career stakes. Therefore, constraining decision-makers’ discretion can alleviate their incentive problem caused by career concerns.

Substantively, our model squares with a variety of real-world delegation strategies in decision making regarding whether to change the status quo. In some cases, the constraint on decision-makers’ actions is derived from legal codes. We illustrate the basic logic of our results with an example in educational recruitment. Suppose a political science department is deciding between two candidates for the position of assistant professor. Candidate A has a proven record of decent publications, while Candidate B has no publications yet but shows potential to become a rising star in the field based on the unpublished papers. The department wants to hire the best candidate as long as their research capacity is above the department average. Ex ante, the safe decision seems to be hiring Candidate A. To make the best hiring decision, the department delegates the decision-making to a hiring committee chair who will conduct interviews and investigate the candidates’ capacity by reading their finished papers. The committee chair may share the same goal as the department, but she may also be the type that has conflicting interests with the new hire, particularly if she dislikes working with an ambitious rising star (e.g., the new hire may compete with the chair over research grants or the courses to teach in the new semester). We ask: If the committee chair is an aligned type who does not want to be perceived as misaligned, will she expend much effort interviewing the candidates?

Suppose the chair is delegated full discretion of the recruitment decision i.e., she has the option to either hire Candidate A, hire Candidate B, or not hire anyone at all. In such a scenario, the chair is likely to opt for the ex ante popular choice of hiring Candidate A, without putting in much effort into the interview process. This is because the chair may believe that conducting a serious interview but ultimately not hiring anyone may lead to the perception of potential misalignment within the department, which could harm her reputation. As a result, the chair may feel that the time and effort spent evaluating the candidates is not worthwhile due to the reputation concern.

Now let us consider two strategies that constrain the chair’s discretion. Firstly, suppose the chair can either hire Candidate B or not hire anyone. In this scenario, if the chair remains uninformed, she risks making one of two possible mistakes: missing out on the opportunity to work with a future superstar or hiring a market lemon and enduring a prolonged period of working with an underperforming employee. With the stakes so high, the chair has a strong incentive to conduct a thorough interview and make an informed hiring decision, even if it means risking a negative perception from the department. Secondly, suppose the chair must choose one candidate from the two. In this scenario, the chair also desires to conduct a serious interview, but for a slightly different reason. If the department anticipates that the chair should make well-informed hiring choices, selecting Candidate A over B may damage her reputation, as it could be viewed as the choice of a misaligned type. Therefore, the chair is incentivized to make correct decisions rather than stay uninformed and simply opt for the ex ante popular choice. Overall, the use of constrained delegation can prove to be a valuable tool in enhancing the quality of decisions.

We formalize this intuition by studying a model of policymaking with career concerns. A political agent (henceforth referred to as “he”) makes a decision regarding reform on behalf of a principal (henceforth referred to as “she”). Initially, neither the agent nor the principal observes the state of the world that determines which of three plausible policies is ideal: the status quo, a moderate reform, and a radical reform. The principal’s optimal uninformed policy is the moderate reform. The agent has a private type that dictates whether he shares the same policy goal as the principal. Aligned agents want to choose the ideal policies that match the state, just as the principal does. Misaligned agents avert any change from the status quo and suffer more from more radical reforms. Prior to choosing policies, the agent decides whether to acquire the information that reveals the true state of the world at a cost, and this decision is unobserved by the principal. After that, the agent chooses the policy within his discretion. Finally, the principal decides whether to reelect/promote the agent based on the agent’s reputation built up from the reform decision. The agent values choosing his favorite policies and being rewarded, whereas the principal is primarily concerned with maximizing her policy payoff.

Our main analysis focuses on whether the principal can encourage the agent to acquire information by limiting the agent’s policy discretion before decision making begins. If strategic delegation does not affect the agent’s decision to acquire information, the rationale for constrained delegation is to discipline the misaligned type, as well-known in delegation literature (for a review, see
Bendor et al. 2001
). We demonstrate that, by either eliminating the status quo or the moderate option, the agent generally places a higher value on information in his decision-making process, taking into account his career concerns. Therefore, constrained delegation has the potential to enhance the quality of reform decisions.

Although constrained delegation can improve the quality of reform decisions by addressing the information problem, it can also worsen the discipline problem. This is because while information allows the agent to choose the principal’s ideal policy, this can only happen if the policy is not eliminated beforehand. Furthermore, when an aligned agent selects well-informed policies, it becomes more likely that the principal will lose control over a misaligned agent who may “give up reelection” by consistently choosing to maintain the status quo. The key idea here is that the aligned type places a higher value on policy-relevant information compared to the misaligned type, which creates a separating equilibrium. Since it becomes unprofitable to mimic the aligned type’s behavior that allows for some reelection probability at the cost of policy, the misaligned type may opt for the status quo, which brings her the optimal policy payoff but foregoes the chance of reelection.

Putting together, we characterize the principal’s optimal delegation strategy. We propose that advocating for reform by eliminating the status quo can be surprisingly effective in improving the quality of decisions while maintaining discipline over decision-makers. If the status quo is not the principal’s ex ante optimal decision, eliminating it can weakly improve the likelihood of informed decisions without weakening the agent’s accountability. Thus, such an advocate will not harm the principal when it fails to motivate the agent to make informed decisions, but will strictly benefit the principal when it does.
We then examine whether the principal should eliminate the status quo or the moderate reform. We find that either strategy may be preferred depending on the specific parameters. Eliminating the moderate option is preferred when the agent is less likely to be aligned, the status quo is less likely to be ideal, and the potential downside of a failed radical reform is larger.

Our paper contributes to the delegation theory, particularly regarding how to incentivize decision-makers to acquire information. While previous studies, such as
Aghion and Tirole 1997
and
Gibbons et al. 2013
, have argued that granting decision-makers real authority is essential for promoting informed decision making, determining the optimal level of decision-making authority remains a challenging theoretical question.
Szalay 2005
proposes that eliminating intermediate options can provide the necessary incentive. However, when decision-makers have career concerns beyond making correct decisions, the problem becomes even more complex. We explore how career-minded decision-makers acquire information and make reform decisions under different levels of decision-making authority. Our novel finding, inspired by
Szalay 2005
, is that advocating for a reform (i.e., eliminating the status quo) is often a desirable solution. This approach not only improves decision quality but also disciplines potentially misaligned decision-makers. Our results suggest that “interval delegation” (i.e., eliminating the extreme options), which classic works proposed as the solution to the optimal delegation problem (e.g.,
Holmstrom 1980
;
Alonso and Matouschek 2008
;
Bendor and Meirowitz 2004
), is robust even when information is endogenous.

Our paper also contributes to the literature on ex post oversight and its role in improving decision-making quality. Ex post oversight, such as reelection, judicial review, or performance-based contracts, is a widely used mechanism to promote accountability in decision making. Previous studies have examined how principals can motivate informed but biased agents to make appropriate decisions by offering ex post incentives (e.g.,
Melumad and Shibano 1991
;
Gailmard 2002
;
Gailmard and Patty 2007
;
Gailmard and Patty 2017
;
Krishna and Morgan 2008
). However, there is a fundamental question as to whether ex post oversight can always promote accountability. In the context of judicial review,
Fox and Stephenson 2011
demonstrate that ex post review can encourage office-seeking politicians to prioritize posturing over utilizing their expertise. Additionally,
Patty and Turner 2021
show that ex post review can induce similar behaviors so long as the reversal of office-seeking politicians’ proposals can hurt them. Our study contributes to both strands of literature by examining how ex ante limits on decision-making authority can improve decision-making quality and discipline potentially misaligned decision-makers, in addition to the use of ex post oversight mechanisms. By incorporating the potential for ex ante discipline, we provide a more comprehensive understanding of how to promote accountability in decision making.

Finally, our paper contributes to the literature on pandering. Our theoretical framework is closely related to prior works that explore how decision-makers balance policy and career concerns in their policymaking. The received wisdom in this line of research is that decision-makers often sacrifice their information expertise and choose policies to impress their constituents, as discussed by
Canes-Wrone et al. 2001
,
Maskin and Tirole 2004
,
Prat 2005
,
Levy 2005
, and
Fox and Jordan 2011
. Previous studies have suggested remedies such as technical decisions made by officials not subject to reelections
(
Maskin and Tirole 2004
)
or limited transparency
(
Fox 2007
;
Fox and Van Weelden 2012
)
. Our model adds to these works by examining how the ex ante discipline of decision-makers’ policy discretion can also be a solution to improving decision-making quality, particularly those regarding reform decisions. Our results suggest that, in some cases, strategically limiting the authority of career-minded decision-makers is key to improving decision quality, rather than simply delegating to accountable or nonaccountable officials or fully embracing transparency or opacity.

2.1
Setup

Consider a model in which an agent (referred to as “he”) selects a policy on behalf of a principal (referred to as “she”). The set of available policies is
\{0,1,r\}
, where
0
represents the “status quo,”
1
represents a “moderate reform,” and

\omega

\{0,1,r\}
. A policy
x
that matches the state
\omega

\omega
. They share a common prior that assigns a probability
p_{\omega}\in(0,1)
to each state
\omega
.

The principal always desires the appropriate policy, but the agent’s policy interests may not align with hers. Specifically, the agent can be one of two types: with probability
\pi

t=c
) who shares the principal’s preference for the appropriate policy, while with probability
1-\pi
the agent is a “noncongruent type” (
t=n
) who has a state-independent preference for choosing the lowest possible action.

5

While modeling the noncongruent type as having state-independent preferences may be seen as a strong assumption, we demonstrate in the Supplementary Appendix that our results remain valid even with a more relaxed notion of noncongruence.
The type
t
is private information of the agent. Substantively, congruent agents can be policy entrepreneurs or “reformists” who strive to make good policies and leave a legacy, while noncongruent agents can be political conservatives who may be influenced by interest groups or hold ideological reasons for avoiding policy changes. Players’ policy payoffs are specified as standard quadratic loss functions: the principal and the congruent type of the agent share the policy payoff
v_{c}=-(x-\omega)^{2}
, while the noncongruent type’s policy payoff is
v_{n}=-x^{2}
.

Sequence of moves and payoffs

The game begins with Nature drawing the state

The agent then observes

The setting described in our model is relevant for both modern democracies and non-democratic regimes. In modern democracies, the model can be applied to a scenario where a representative voter decides whether to reelect the incumbent politician based on their performance. In non-democratic regimes, the model can be used to describe a situation where a political leader selects whether to promote a lower-ranked comrade based on their performance.

We have imposed these assumptions to ensure that the agent’s policy choice is the only salient factor in their performance evaluation. If the decision to acquire information is observed, then the agent has to signal congruence by acquiring information. This situation is uninteresting, as it essentially replicates the findings in

Solution Concept.
Our equilibrium notion is perfect Bayesian equilibrium (PBE), which requires (1) the principal to form beliefs about the agent’s type using the Bayes rule whenever possible, and (2) both players to act to maximize their expected payoffs. Since this game can have multiple equilibria, the divinity refinement is applied to eliminate equilibria with unreasonable off-path beliefs. The formal definitions of equilibria and refinement are provided in the Appendix. To simplify the algebra, we focus on equilibria in which the principal chooses a pure strategy of reelection. This treatment is also found in previous works such as
Maskin and Tirole 2004
and
Armstrong and Vickers 2010
. Finally, we refer to an equilibrium as “informative” if at least one type of the agent acquires and uses information along the equilibrium path.

Comments.
In line with classic political agency models, the principal in our model aims to screen for the right type of agent and discipline the agent to take appropriate actions, including both specializing and choosing appropriate policies. However, as noted by
Fearon 1999
, the principal generally cannot solve the problems of moral hazard and adverse selection altogether. Thus, our focus is on the discipline side: whether the principal can induce the agent to take desirable actions using a combination of ex ante control (designing the delegation set) and ex post oversight (reelection). In fact, while these two options are often analyzed separately in formal models of delegation (see
Bendor and Meirowitz 2004
, and
Gailmard and Patty 2012
for reviews) and accountability (see
Ashworth 2012
for a review), in many political agency relationships they are used together. For example, unelected judges are often career concerned
(
Levy 2005
)
and faced with extreme decisions to convict and acquit
(
Szalay 2005
)
.

3
Equilibrium policymaking

Ideally, the principal wants the agent to acquire and use policy-relevant information during the policymaking process. This leads to the following questions: can both types of agent acquire information? What factors might prevent the principal from achieving her desired outcome? To shed light on the properties of equilibria, we present two technical lemmas below.

Lemma 1
.

In any equilibrium, the noncongruent agent would not acquire information.

Proof.

All the proofs are in the Appendix.
∎

Lemma
1
states that, in any equilibrium, only the congruent type of agent may specialize to acquire information. This is because the noncongruent type derives zero value from information: policywise, his state-independent preference is for the lowest possible action, so the information about the state
\omega
does not enable him to make better policies. Politically, since the principal cannot observe information acquisition, it does not factor into the reelection decision. Therefore, if a noncongruent agent were to acquire information in any “conjectured equilibrium,” they could profitably deviate by remaining uninformed and acting as if they were informed.

Lemma 2
.

In any informative equilibrium, the noncongruent agent will be
removed surely; he chooses the lowest action within the delegation set.

Lemma
2
highlights the principal’s fundamental tradeoff in delegation: informed policymaking comes at the cost of losing control over a misaligned agent. To see why this dilemma arises in our theory, we suppose that the noncongruent type can be reelected along the equilibrium path. Given his policy preference, the noncongruent type should always choose the lowest possible action that secures reelection. However, the congruent type must choose other actions along the equilibrium path to utilize information. As a result, the lowest possible action becomes bad news for reelection, as it is more likely to be chosen by a noncongruent type. This leads to a contradiction.

Taken together, Lemmas
1
-
2
imply that if the principal’s delegation strategy cannot encourage the agent to acquire information before making policies, then it cannot improve upon the strategy
D^{*}=\{x=1\}
, where the principal orders the agent to choose the ex ante optimal policy. In other words, the only reason for the principal to adopt a delegation strategy that differs from
D^{*}
is to motivate informed policymaking.

In the following subsections, we examine which delegation strategy can achieve this goal. We analyze how a rational principal should choose her delegation strategy while anticipating the agent’s best response. According to Lemma
2
, the principal is sure to lose control over a noncongruent type in any informative equilibrium. Thus, her best hope is that the congruent type of the agent chooses the appropriate policies after specializing.

3.1
Benchmark: unconstrained delegation

We start with a benchmark setting where the principal does not limit the agent’s freedom of policy choices. From the principal’s perspective,
assuming
the agent always chooses the appropriate policies, the net policy gain compared to uninformed policymaking (i.e., always choose
x=1
) is
p((r-1)^{2}+1)
. Thus, an entirely policy-driven agent who shares the principal’s policy preference should acquire policy-relevant information whenever it costs less than
p((r-1)^{2}+1)
.

But matters are different when the agent cares about more than policies. Proposition
1
suggests that a career-concerned agent is reluctant to collect and use policy-relevant information when its cost exceeds
p(r-1)^{2}
, a threshold lower than
p((r-1)^{2}+1)
.

Proposition 1

(Unconstrained delegation discourages information acquisition)
.

In the subgame ensuing the principal adopts unconstrained delegation (
D^{U}=\{0,1,r\}
), an informative equilibrium does not exist. A pooling equilibrium exists where both types of the agent choose the ex ante popular action
x=1
. The principal reelects the agent when she observes
x\in\{1,r\}
, and removes when she observes
x=0
.

Proposition
1
describes the agent’s policy choices under unconstrained delegation. In this scenario, both types of the agent select the ex-ante popular policy of “moderate reform” to ensure reelection. The principal will reelect the agent whenever she observes actions that are weakly higher than the “moderate reform.”

The reason why career concerns can discourage informed policymaking is due to the agent’s distorted value of information. For a noncongruent agent, his value of information is zero by Lemma
1
, so only the congruent agent may find information useful. However, even the congruent agent cannot fully utilize information in policymaking if he is concerned about reelection. Specifically, when the state
\omega
calls for the status quo, the congruent agent avoids the “correct policy” (
x=0
) for fear of political removal and instead choose the nearest policy, “moderate reform” (
x=1
). Thus, policy-relevant information only helps the congruent agent make better decisions when the state
\omega
calls for a radical reform, which yields a total gain of utility of
p(r-1)^{2}
. If
k>p(r-1)^{2}
, the congruent agent will find information acquisition unattractive.

Importantly, Proposition
1
alludes to the possibility that career concerns can actually hinder the creation of good policies. If the agent panders to the ex ante popular policy of “moderate reform” without acquiring information, the principal receives the same payoff as she would have received had she directly ordered the implementation of the moderate reform (i.e.,
D^{*}=\{x=1\}
), which makes delegation pointless. Worse, if the cost of information acquisition is moderately high (
k\in(p(r-1)^{2},p((r-1)^{2}+1))
), then the principal could have achieved informed policymaking by committing to an incentive scheme that is independent from the policymaking details, such as term limiting the agent or backing away from the policy decisions before the reelection day. However, career concerns linked to the agent’s future career prospects make this approach unfeasible. This finding echoes
Maskin and Tirole 2004
that accountability might create perverse incentives for policymaking.

3.2
Constraining to motivate

As we have seen above, when the principal does not limit the agent’s freedom of choice, career concerns can distort the agent’s policymaking incentives. However, principals rarely use such a passive delegation strategy in practice.
In fact, two delegation strategies are salient in the real-world practice. The first is that
D^{M}=\{0,r\}
, meaning the principal removes the moderate policy from the agent’s discretion. For example, judges are sometimes required to choose between extreme decisions such as “acquittal” and “conviction.” The second is that
D^{S}=\{1,r\}
, meaning the
principal removes the status quo policy from the agent’s discretion. For example, in the 1980s, China’s local officials often encountered choices between a “moderate reform” and a “radical reform.” Sometimes, a delegation set
D^{E}=\{0,1\}
that prohibits the agent from engaging in excessively risky activities also appears. We will focus on whether these tailored delegation sets can motivate the agent to acquire and use policy-relevant information. If no delegation set can solve such an incentive problem, a policy-driven principal should simply order the ex ante optimal policy to be implemented.

In the following subsections, we derive the agent’s policymaking behaviors corresponding to different delegation strategies. Importantly,
the policy stakes of a radical reform (measured by
r
) matter in the agent’s decision to acquire information. Following the terminology of
Patty and Turner 2021
, we distinguish radical reforms between “low urgency” reforms (
r\leq 2
) and
“high urgency” reforms (
r>2
). For example, the abolition of China’s dual-pricing system in 1989 carried substantial political and economical stakes, which can be viewed as a “high urgency” reform. In comparison, China’s shareholding system reform of state-owned enterprise had much smaller potential downsides and can be viewed as a “low urgency” reform. We present the main results with respect to the low urgency reforms. Discussions about the high urgency reforms (
r>2
) are left in the supplementary appendix, as they deliver the same qualitative insights.

3.2.1
D=D^{M}=\{0,r\}

When the popular action “moderate reform” (
x=1
) is eliminated from the delegation set, the congruent agent’s optimal uninformed decision in our “low urgency ” case (

, instead of

4.3
Accountability or nonaccountability?

Moving beyond the discussion of delegation strategy, we now consider whether a policy-driven principal could improve her policymaking payoff by removing the agent’s accountability. Substantively, the constituents could delegate the reform decision to a lame duck politician. Compared to the classic solution, eliminating the status quo enables the principal to discipline a noncongruent agent, but it also results in a policy loss by being unable to implement the status quo when required. If the discipline effect more than offsets the policy loss, our solution would outperform the classic solution. This insight is valuable because it presents another possibility for studying accountability. Formal models of accountability often focus on ex post oversight as the primary means to motivate political agents to act in the right manner. However, our results suggest that the function of ex ante control may be understudied, as it can work equally well or even better than the exclusive use of ex post oversight.

5
Conclusion

One of the main challenges that undermines the quality of reform decisions is the lack of policy-relevant information. While political compromises, such as the “moderate option,” are often chosen to minimize policy risk in reform decisions, there are cases where decision-making authority is partially delegated to career-focused decision-makers instead of being fully delegated. In this paper, we develop a political agency model to explore the rationale behind different delegation strategies in the real world. We find that decision-makers, who are concerned with building a reputation for themselves through the decision-making process, usually lack the incentive to develop the expertise necessary to handle complex and difficult decision-making situations. In such cases, we demonstrate that restricting decision-makers’ discretion can be a practical way to encourage information acquisition. Importantly, when decision-makers are highly likely to hold divergent policy goals with the principal, we show that under benign conditions, advocacy for reform (i.e., eliminating the status quo) can help the principal resolve the incentive problem without losing control over decision-makers. This finding provides a rationale for various real-world phenomena. For instance, a political leader committed to reforming the status quo policy can motivate subordinates to find new solutions to existing problems. Similarly, many governments establish experimental zones to test novel ideas.

Our model comprehensively examines how ex ante control and ex post oversight can jointly influence decision-makers’ incentives to make sound policies. Through evaluating the welfare implications of various delegation strategies and accountability mechanisms, we revisit classic debates in policymaking, such as whether ex post oversight necessarily enhances accountability, whether accountable and non-accountable decision-makers should handle technical decisions, and whether delegation should take an interval form or not. Our findings suggest that when decision-makers are concerned about building their careers, a unified formal model that considers ex ante control and ex post oversight can yield novel insights. Our approach represents a crucial first step towards a more comprehensive understanding of delegation and accountability in policymaking.

C.3
Noncongruent type that values information

In the main text model, we assume that the “noncongruent type” is so politically conservative that he avoid any policy changes, and that information about the state of the world is worthless to him. In this subsection, we will relax this assumption and allow for a noncongruent agent who finds information valuable. Our aim is to examine the robustness of the model in light of this modification. To simplify the discussion of the various parameter cases, we will assume that
r
is within the range of
[\frac{3}{2},2]

Specifically, we relax this notion of “noncongruence” by assuming that the noncongruent type also has state-dependent preferences regarding policymaking, albeit with a bias term from the principal. To simplify matters, we specify a noncongruent agent’s policy payoff as
u_{n}(x,\omega,b)=-(x-x_{n}^{*}(\omega)^{2})
, where

\displaystyle x_{n}^{*}(\omega)=\begin{cases}0,\omega\in\{0,1\}\\
r,\omega=r\end{cases}.

Since the noncongruent agent’s ideal policy is lower than the principal’s (
x^{*}(\omega)=\omega
), he is more conservative than the principal regarding reform decisions. Specifically, the noncongruent agent agrees with the principal’s policy choices when the state calls for either the status quo or a radical reform, but disagrees when the state calls for a moderate reform.
Such an agent avoids policy changes unless there is an overwhelmingly strong signal that a reform is necessary. Although information still has value to a noncongruent type, his value of information is strictly lower than that of the congruent type, as the information only helps him make better policy decisions when
\omega=r
. To emphasize such a policy disagreement, we suppose
p\leq\frac{1}{2r}
that ensures the noncongruent agent’s ex ante preferred policy is
x=0
, which is lower than the congruent agent’s
x=1
.
Taken together, our specification implies that under clever delegation strategies, there may be a separating equilibrium where the congruent type acquires information and acts informatively, while the noncongruent agent remains uninformed and chooses the status quo.

Before proceeding to analysis, we continue to assume that the prior is symmetrically distributed around the moderate reform (Assumption
1^{*}
), the office rent is moderately strong (Assumption
2

3

Now, we will replicate Proposition
