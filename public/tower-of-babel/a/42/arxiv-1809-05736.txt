Abstract
In the present notes we explain the relationship between Calabi–Yau integrable systems and Hitchin systems based on work by Diaconescu–Donagi–Pantev and the author . Besides a review of these integrable systems, we highlight related topics, for example variations of Hodge structures, cameral curves and Slodowy slices, along the way.

Introduction

From a geometric viewpoint, complex integrable systems are holomorphic symplectic manifolds
(\mathbf{M},\omega)
that admit a holomorphic map
\pi\colon\mathbf{M}\to B
to another complex manifold
B
such that the generic fibers of
\pi
are compact connected Lagrangian submanifolds. These are isomorphic to complex tori, i.e., quotients
V/\Gamma
of a complex vector space
V
by a lattice
\Gamma\subset V
of full rank, by the complex version of the Arnold–Liouville theorem
[
23
, Chapter 2]
. A general holomorphic symplectic manifold does not admit such a structure. Therefore complex integrable systems play a special role in holomorphic symplectic geometry.

The first purpose of this article is to introduce two intricate infinite families of complex integrable systems, namely Calabi–Yau integrable systems and Hitchin systems. The former were constructed by Donagi–Markman
[
22
]
from any complete family
\pi\colon\mathcal{X}\to B
of compact Calabi–Yau threefolds. The generic fibers are Griffiths’ intermediate Jacobians
J^{2}(X_{b})

[
28
]
of the smooth fibers
X_{b}
of
\pi
. These complex tori are a generalization of the Jacobian of a compact Riemann surface. The geometry of
J^{2}(X)
for a compact Calabi–Yau threefold

is in general poorly understood unlike in the case of Fano threefolds
[
11
]
. In mathematics, Calabi–Yau integrable systems have close links to Deligne cohomology and algebraic cycles (see
[
14
,
22
]
,
[
26
, Section 7.8]
). In mathematical physics, they play an important role in
F

[
2
,
3
]
and geometric transitions
[
16

Hitchin systems were first discovered by Hitchin
[

,

and have been extensively studied since then. They are constructed from a compact connected Riemann surface

(of genus
\geq 2
) together with a semisimple complex Lie group

via the moduli space

of

The Hitchin system

are dual as abelian varieties to the generic fibers of

1.1
Structure of the notes

We begin by introducing complex integrable systems in Section
2
, and study them from a Hodge-theoretic viewpoint. We define non-degenerate complex tori and give the intermediate Jacobian
J^{2}(X)
of a compact Calabi–Yau threefold
X
as an example. The former are equivalent to certain Hodge structures of weight
1
. Generalizing to the relative setting, we arrive at variations of Hodge structures of weight
1
. These give rise to complex integrable systems if they admit a special section, namely an abstract Seiberg–Witten differential (Proposition
2.15
). With these methods we construct Calabi–Yau integrable systems from complete families of compact Calabi–Yau threefolds in a different way than originally done by Donagi–Markman
[
22
]
.

The second prime examples of integrable systems,
G
-Hitchin systems, are introduced in Section
3
for any general (semi)simple complex Lie group
G
. This requires some background material, in particular the introduction of the adjoint quotient of a semisimple complex Lie algebra and cameral curves. The latter are a necessary tool to determine the isogeny (and isomorphism) classes of generic fibers of
G
-Hitchin systems in general. When
G\subset{\rm GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
is a classical semisimple complex Lie group, they are closely related to the spectral curves introduced by Hitchin
[
30
,
31
]
for the same purpose. We give a detailed comparison between the two notions based on
[
18
]
.

In the final Section
4
, Calabi–Yau integrable systems are related to Hitchin systems. The corresponding families of (non-compact) Calabi–Yau threefolds are constructed via Slodowy slices. We review them together with their relation to so-called
\Delta
-singularities where
\Delta
is an irreducible Dynkin diagram. Finally, we state the precise relationship between (non-compact) Calabi–Yau integrable systems and
G
-Hitchin systems where the simple complex Lie group
G
has Dynkin diagram
\Delta

[
34

applied to the families of (non-compact) Calabi–Yau threefolds.

2
Hodge theory of integrable systems

The generic fibers of an algebraic integrable system are torsors for abelian varieties (see Definition
2.1
and Lemma
2.2
below). It follows that the smooth part of an algebraic integrable system is a family of abelian varieties if it admits a (Lagrangian) section. Such families are intimately related to Hodge theory, more precisely variations of Hodge structures. In the following we explain this relation and how holomorphic symplectic structures fit into the Hodge-theoretic framework.

2.1
Complex integrable systems

At first we generalize the notion of an algebraic integrable system by relaxing the condition on the generic fibers. This is necessary because Calabi–Yau integrable systems, one of our prime examples of integrable systems, are of this form. We begin by fixing the definition of an algebraic integrable system.

Definition 2.1

(algebraic integrable system)
.

Let
(\mathbf{M},\omega)
be a holomorphic symplectic manifold and
B
a connected complex manifold. A holomorphic map
\pi\colon\mathbf{M}\to B
is called an algebraic integrable system if

i)

\pi
is proper and surjective,

ii)

its smooth fibers are Lagrangian and connected,

iii)

its smooth part
\pi^{\circ}\colon\mathbf{M}^{\circ}\to B^{\circ}
admits a relative polarization, i.e., a line bundle
\mathcal{L}\to\mathbf{M}
such that
\mathcal{L}_{|\mathbf{M}_{b}}
is ample for all
b\in B^{\circ}
.

We call an algebraic integrable system
smooth
if
\pi\colon\mathbf{M}\to B
is a submersion so that
B^{\circ}=B
.

The definition implies the following well-known properties of algebraic integrable systems (see
[
23
]
).

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\pi\colon(\mathbf{M},\omega)\to B
be an algebraic integrable system. Then its smooth part
\pi^{\circ}\colon\mathbf{M}^{\circ}\to B^{\circ}
is a torsor for a family
\mathcal{A}(\pi^{\circ})\to B
of polarized abelian varieties. In particular,
(\mathbf{M}_{b},\mathcal{L}_{|\mathbf{M}_{b}})
is non-canonically isomorphic to a polarized abelian variety for every
b\in B^{\circ}
.

Sketch of proof.

Let
\mathcal{V}\to B^{\circ}
be the vertical bundle of
\pi^{\circ}
, i.e., the bundle of vector fields on
\mathbf{M}^{\circ}
which are tangent to the fibers. Let
v,w\colon U\to\mathcal{V}
be two local sections. If
U
is small enough, then
v
,
w
are Hamiltonian vector fields
v=X_{\pi^{*}f}
,
w=X_{\pi^{*}g}
for functions
f,g\colon U\to\mathbb{C}
. Since the fibers of
\pi^{\circ}
are Lagrangian, it follows that
[v,w]=X_{\omega(v,w)}=0
. Hence the flows of sections of
\mathcal{V}
define a fiber-preserving action of the abelian group
(\mathcal{V},+)
associated to the commutative Lie algebra
(\mathcal{V},[\bullet,\bullet])
on
\mathbf{M}^{\circ}
. The submanifold

\displaystyle\Gamma=\big\{v\in\mathcal{V}\,|\,\exists\,x\in\mathbf{M}^{\circ}\colon v\cdot x=x\big\}\subset\mathcal{V}

intersects each fiber in a full lattice. The relative polarization of
\pi^{\circ}\colon\mathbf{M}^{\circ}\to B^{\circ}
is transported to
\mathcal{A}(\pi^{\circ}):=\mathcal{V}/\Gamma\to B^{\circ}
which is therefore a family of abelian varieties acting simply transitively on
\pi^{\circ}\colon\mathbf{M}^{\circ}\to B^{\circ}
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

The holomorphic symplectic form

2.2
VHS and smooth complex integrable systems

From now on, we concentrate on

\pi\colon(\mathbf{M},\omega)\to B
which admit a (Lagrangian) section

\pi\colon\mathbf{M}\to B

Remark 2.9
.

The requirement that the smooth complex integrable system
\pi\colon\mathbf{M}\to B

\mathcal{A}(\pi)\to B

To see how Hodge theory is related to smooth complex integrable systems, we begin with a single complex torus

\displaystyle H^{n}(T,\mathbb{C})=\bigoplus_{p+q=n}H^{p,q}(T),\qquad H^{q,p}(T)=\overline{H^{p,q}(T)}.

(2.1)

The pair

\displaystyle H^{n}(T,\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{1}(T,\mathbb{Z})^{\otimes n},

\displaystyle H^{1}(T,\mathbb{C})=H^{1,0}(T)\oplus H^{0,1}(T).

It uniquely determines the complex torus

\omega_{\bm{\lambda}}
on

such that the zero section becomes Lagrangian and which induces

Here two

Sketch of proof.

The basic idea is to use

to prove that

Definition 2.16
.

Let
(\mathsf{V},Q)
be a polarized
\mathbb{Z}
-VHS of weight
1
over the complex manifold
B
. A section
\bm{\lambda}\in H^{0}(B,\mathsf{V}_{\mathcal{O}})
satisfying the condition (
2.5
) is called an abstract Seiberg–Witten differential.

Remark 2.17
.

The terminology is motivated by Seiberg–Witten theory in mathematical physics (see
[
20
]
for an introduction). A key ingredient in this theory are families
\mathcal{E}_{\rm SW}\to B
of Seiberg–Witten curves. Mathematically, these are families of (generically smooth) plane elliptic curves. As a concrete example, we take

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\rm SW}=\mathcal{E}\colon\ y^{2}z=(x-1)(x+1)(x-u),\qquad u\in\mathbb{C}.

Let
U=\mathbb{C}\setminus\{1,-1\}\subset\mathbb{C}
be the locus of smooth fibers and
\mathsf{V}
the corresponding
\mathbb{Z}
-VHS of weight
1
. For each
u\in U
,
\omega_{u}=\tfrac{{\rm d}x}{y}_{|E_{u}}
defines a basis of holomorphic
1
-forms, hence a frame of
F^{1}\mathsf{V}_{\mathcal{O}}
. The Seiberg–Witten differential is defined by
\tfrac{{\rm d}x}{y}(x-u)_{|E_{u}}
. It is a meromorphic
1
-form with a single pole at
\infty
. However, its residue vanishes so that

\displaystyle\lambda_{\rm SW}(u)=\left[\frac{{\rm d}x}{y}(x-u)_{|E_{u}}\right]\in H^{1}(E_{u},\mathbb{C})

is well-defined. The section
\lambda_{\rm SW}\in H^{0}(U,\mathsf{V}_{\mathcal{O}})
has the property of an abstract Seiberg–Witten differential, namely

\displaystyle T_{u}U\ni\partial_{u}\mapsto\nabla_{\partial_{u}}\lambda_{\rm SW}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\omega_{u}\in F^{1}\mathsf{V}_{\mathcal{O},u}

defines an isomorphism
TU\cong F^{1}\mathsf{V}_{\mathcal{O}}
. Abstracting this property motivated Definition
2.16
.

As the following Corllary shows, the cohomology class of the holomorphic symplectic form is an obstruction to the existence of an abstract Seiberg–Witten differential.

Corollary 2.18
.

Let
(\mathsf{V},Q)
be a polarized
\mathbb{Z}
-VHS of weight
1
over the complex manifold
B
which admits an abstract Seiberg–Witten differential
\lambda\in H^{0}(B,\mathsf{V}_{\mathcal{O}})
. Then the induced holomorphic symplectic structure on the total space of
\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{V})
is exact.

Proof.

Under the isomorphism
\iota\colon\mathcal{V}\to T^{*}B
,
\omega_{\bm{\lambda}}
(pulled back to
\mathcal{V}
) corresponds to the canonical symplectic structure
d\eta
on
T^{*}B
for the tautological
1
-form
\eta
. The latter corresponds to

\displaystyle T_{[s]}\mathcal{V}\ni w\mapsto Q(s,\nabla_{{\rm d}p(w)}\lambda),\qquad[s]\in\mathcal{V}\cong\mathsf{V}_{\mathcal{O}}/F^{1}\mathsf{V}_{\mathcal{O}},

where
p\colon\mathcal{V}\to B
is the projection. However, this
1
-form does not descend to
\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{V})
. Instead we define
f\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{C},

f([s])=Q(s,\lambda)
, and

\displaystyle\gamma(w):=Q(s,\nabla_{{\rm d}p(w)}\lambda)-{\rm d}f_{[s]}(w),\qquad w\in T_{[s]}\mathcal{V}.

It follows that
\gamma(w)=0
for
w\in T\mathsf{V}_{\mathbb{Z}}\hookrightarrow T\mathcal{V}
so that
\gamma
descends to a
1
-form on
\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{V})
with
{\rm d}\gamma=\omega_{\bm{\lambda}}
.
∎

The total space of the integrable is the smooth locus

A (topologically trivial)

In this section we give a brief introduction to

G

.
of a generic fiber of

If

However, there are some conceptual issues with spectral curves. For example, if

3.1
Adjoint quotient

Any semisimple complex Lie group
G
acts on its Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\operatorname{Lie}(G)
by the adjoint representation
\operatorname{Ad}\colon G\to{\rm GL}(\mathfrak{g})
. In particular,
G
acts on the algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{g}]
of polynomial functions on
\mathfrak{g}
and the inclusion
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{g}]^{G}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{g}]
defines the adjoint quotient

\displaystyle\chi\colon\ \mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}\sslash G:=\operatorname{Spec}\big(\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{g}]^{G}\big).

Chevalley (see
[
32
, Chapter 23]
) has proven that the pullback under the inclusion
\mathfrak{t}\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{g}
of a Cartan subalgebra restricts to the isomorphism
\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{g}]^{G}\cong\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{t}]^{W}
for the corresponding Weyl group
W
. This implies that
\mathfrak{g}\sslash G\cong\mathfrak{t}/W
. Since the invariant polynomials of a reflection group (e.g., the Weyl group
W
) form a free polynomial algebra, there exist
\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{r}\in\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{g}]^{G}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{g}]\cong\mathbb{C}[\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{r}]

and hence
\mathfrak{g}\sslash G\cong\mathbb{C}^{r}
non-canonically. Even though the free generators are not unique, their degrees
d_{j}:=\operatorname{deg}(\chi_{j})
are. The numbers
d_{j}-1
are called the exponents of
\mathfrak{g}
.

The adjoint quotient
\chi\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{t}/W
can be expressed more concretely. Let
v=v_{n}+v_{s}
be the Jordan decomposition of
v\in\mathfrak{g}
for nilpotent
v_{n}
and semisimple
v_{s}
so that
v_{s}\in\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}
for some Cartan subalgebra
\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}\subset\mathfrak{g}
. Let
g\in G
such that
g\cdot\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}=\mathfrak{t}
. Then the adjoint quotient is given by

\displaystyle\chi(v)=[g\cdot v_{s}]\in\mathfrak{t}/W.

From this formula, it is clear that
\chi\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{t}/W
is a generalization of the characteristic polynomial, see Exercise
3.13
.

The smooth locus of the adjoint quotient
\chi\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{t}/W
is given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{g}^{\rm reg}=\{v\in\mathfrak{g}\,|\,\dim\ker\operatorname{ad}(v)=r\}\subset\mathfrak{g},

so that
d\chi_{v}
is surjective iff
v\in\mathfrak{g}^{\rm reg}
. This result goes back to Kostant
[
35
]
.

3.2
\bm{G}
-Hitchin systems

A
G
-Higgs bundle over
C
is a pair
(P,\varphi)
consisting of a
G
-principal bundle
P\to C
and a section
\varphi\in H^{0}(C,K_{C}\otimes\operatorname{ad}(P))
, called a Higgs field, where
\operatorname{ad}(P)=P\times_{G}\mathfrak{g}
is the adjoint bundle. Any representation
\rho\colon G\to{\rm GL}(n,\mathbb{C})
induces a Higgs vector bundle
(\rho(P),\rho_{*}(\varphi))
. If
\rho=\operatorname{Ad}\colon G\to{\rm GL}(\mathfrak{g})
is the adjoint representation, then we call
(\operatorname{Ad}(P),\operatorname{Ad}_{*}(\varphi))
the adjoint Higgs (vector) bundle.

Definition 3.1
.

A
G
-Higgs bundle
(P,\varphi)
is semistable iff the adjoint Higgs bundle
(\operatorname{Ad}(P),

\operatorname{Ad}(\varphi))
is semistable, i.e.,

\displaystyle\frac{\operatorname{deg}(F)}{\operatorname{rk}(F)}\leq\frac{\operatorname{deg}(\operatorname{Ad}(P))}{\operatorname{rk}(\operatorname{Ad}(P))}

for any proper subbundle
0\subsetneq F\subsetneq\operatorname{Ad}(P)
which is preserved by
\operatorname{Ad}_{*}(\varphi)
.

The set of isomorphism classes of semistable and topologically trivial
G
-Higgs bundles over
C
carries the structure of a complex analytic space
\bm{Higgs}(C,G)
. The adjoint quotient
\chi\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{t}/W
globalizes to the Hitchin map

\displaystyle\bm{h}\colon\ \bm{Higgs}(C,G)\to\mathbf{B}(C,G):=H^{0}(C,(K_{C}\otimes\mathfrak{t})/W),\qquad[P,\varphi]\mapsto\chi\circ\varphi,

where the
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action on
\mathfrak{t}/W
is induced by the natural
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action on
\mathfrak{t}
. The target
\mathbf{B}(C,G)
of the Hitchin map is called the Hitchin base. Choosing generators
\chi_{1},\dots,\chi_{r}\in\mathbb{C}[\mathfrak{g}]^{G}
of degree
d_{i}=\deg(\chi_{i})
, we obtain the isomorphism

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}(C,G)\cong\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}H^{0}\big(C,K_{C}^{\otimes d_{i}}\big).

In particular,
\mathbf{B}(C,G)
is a vector space in a non-canonical way.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
G
be a semisimple complex Lie group and
C
a compact Riemann surface of genus
\geq 2
. Then the smooth locus
\mathbf{Higgs}(C,G)\subset\bm{Higgs}(C,G)
of the moduli space of semistable and topologically trivial
G
-Higgs bundles is a holomorphic symplectic manifold. Moreover, the Hitchin map
\bm{h}\colon\mathbf{Higgs}(C,G)\to\mathbf{B}(C,G)
is an algebraic integrable system. It admits sections, so-called Hitchin sections.

Therefore, once a Hitchin section is chosen, the Hitchin fibers
\bm{h}^{-1}(b)
, for generic
b\in\mathbf{B}(C,G)
, are canonically isomorphic to abelian varieties
\mathsf{P}_{b}
. We give the isogeny class of
\mathsf{P}_{b}
in terms of cameral curves, which we treat in the next subsection.

It is possible to determine the isomorphism class of

The first two statements of Theorem

3.3

Let

\Gamma=\Gamma^{\prime}
so that
\mathbf{C}=1
. The quotient orbifold
\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
has a unique singularity at
[0]\in\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma

\displaystyle\pi\colon\ \widehat{\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma.

Its resolution graph , i.e., the intersection graph of the irreducible components of its exceptional divisor, is dual to an irreducible
\mathrm{ADE}
-Dynkin diagram
\Delta_{h}(\Gamma)
. It turns out that the resolution graph uniquely determines
\Gamma

\Gamma\mapsto\Delta_{h}(\Gamma)
is a bijection onto irreducible
\mathrm{ADE}
-Dynkin diagrams.

Now let
\Gamma\trianglelefteq\Gamma^{\prime}
be two finite and distinct subgroups of
{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{C})
. Then the group
\mathbf{C}:=\Gamma^{\prime}/\Gamma
acts on
\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
. By the uniqueness of the minimal resolution,
\mathbf{C}
also acts on
\widehat{\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma}
, in particular on the dual
\Delta_{h}(\Gamma)
of its resolution graph. In that way we obtain the full bijection (
4.8
).
∎

4.2
Slodowy slices

Even though the quotient singularities
\mathbb{C}^{2}/\Gamma
with the group action of
\mathbf{C}\cong\Gamma^{\prime}/\Gamma
are classified by irreducible Dynkin diagrams
\Delta=\Delta_{h}(\Gamma)^{\mathbf{C}}
,
\mathbf{C}={\rm AS}(\Delta)
, it is a priori unclear how they are related to the simple complex Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}(\Delta)
corresponding to
\Delta
. This relation was elucidated by Brieskorn
[
10
]
and Slodowy
[
39
]
after a suggestion by Grothendieck.

Let
x\in\mathfrak{g}
be a nilpotent element which is subregular, i.e.,

\displaystyle\dim\ker\operatorname{ad}(x)=r+2,\qquad r=\operatorname{rk}(\mathfrak{g}).

By the Jacobson–Morozov theorem
[
12
]
, there exists a monomorphism
\rho\colon\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{g}
and
y,h\in\mathfrak{g}
such that

\displaystyle\rho\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=x,\qquad\rho\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}=y,\qquad\rho\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}=h.

The triple
(x,y,h)
is called an
\mathfrak{sl}_{2}
-triple for
x
. The Slodowy slice through
x
is defined by

\displaystyle S:=x+\ker\operatorname{ad}(y).

It carries a
\mathbb{C}^{*}\times\mathbf{C}
-action as we next explain. For the
\mathbb{C}^{*}
-action observe that
\rho\colon\mathfrak{sl}(2,\mathbb{C})\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{g}
exponentiates to yield the group homomorphism
\tilde{\rho}\colon{\rm SL}(2,\mathbb{C})\to G=G_{\rm ad}
. Letting
G
act on
\mathfrak{g}
by the adjoint action, we define

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}^{*}\times S\to S,\qquad(\zeta,v)\mapsto\zeta^{2}\tilde{\rho}\begin{pmatrix}\zeta^{-1}&0\\
0&-\zeta^{-1}\end{pmatrix}\cdot v.

This is a well-defined action, i.e., it preserves
S
. For the
\mathbf{C}
-action define the group

\displaystyle C(x,h):=\{g\in G_{\rm ad}\,|\,g\cdot x=x,\,g\cdot h=h\},

which acts on
S\subset\mathfrak{g}
via the adjoint action. Let
C(x,h)^{\circ}\subset C(x,h)
be the connected component of the identity. Then
C(x,h)/C(x,h)^{\circ}\cong\mathbf{C}
and the exact sequence

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C(x,h)^{\circ}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C(x,h)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{C}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 1}

splits
[
39

. In that way,
\mathbf{C}
acts on

as well. Since the

-action commutes with the
\mathbf{C}
-action, we obtain a
\mathbb{C}^{*}\times\mathbf{C}
-action on

. It interacts with the restriction

\displaystyle\sigma=\chi_{|S}\colon\ S\to\mathfrak{t}/W

of the adjoint quotient
\chi\colon\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{t}/W
as follows. If we let
\mathbb{C}^{*}
act on
\mathfrak{t}/W
by

\displaystyle\zeta\cdot[t]=\big[\zeta^{2}t\big],

i.e., by twice the standard weights, and
\mathbf{C}

is
\mathbb{C}^{*}\times\mathbf{C}
-equivariant. The importance of Slodowy slices for our purposes is summarized in the following.

Theorem 4.8

(Slodowy)
.

Let
\Delta=\Delta_{h}^{\mathbf{C}}
be an irreducible Dynkin diagram and
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}(\Delta)
the corresponding simple complex Lie algebra. If

is a subregular nilpotent element and
S=x+\ker\operatorname{ad}(y)
a Slodowy slice through

a)

(S_{[0]},x)
together with the induced
\mathbf{C}

\Delta
-singularity.

b)

The restriction
\sigma=\chi_{|S}\colon S\to\mathfrak{t}/W
of the adjoint quotient together with the
\mathbf{C}
-action is a semi-universal deformation of the
\Delta
-singularity
(S_{[0]},x)
.

4.4

\bm{G}
-Hitchin systems

After all this preparation, we finally come to the precise relation between Calabi–Yau integrable and Hitchin systems.

Theorem 4.12

(
[
5
]
)
.

Let
\Delta=\Delta_{h}^{\mathbf{C}}
be an irreducible Dynkin diagram and
G=G_{\rm ad}
the corresponding simple adjoint complex Lie group with Hitchin base
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{B}(C,G_{\rm ad})

\mathcal{J}^{2}_{\mathbf{C}}(\mathcal{X}^{\circ}/\mathbf{B}^{\circ})\to\mathbf{B}^{\circ}

\mathcal{J}^{2}_{\mathbf{C}}(\mathcal{X}^{\circ}/\mathbf{B}^{\circ})\cong\mathbf{Higgs}^{\circ}(C,G)
as algebraic integrable systems over
\mathbf{B}^{\circ}
.

If

is an irreducible
\mathrm{ADE}
-Dynkin diagram, then the fiberwise version of Theorem
4.12
appeared in
[
17
]
(see
[
16
]

Let

and
