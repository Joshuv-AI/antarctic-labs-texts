Abstract
Approximate functional dependencies (AFDs) are functional dependencies (FDs) that “almost” hold in a relation. While various measures have been proposed to quantify the level to which an FD holds approximately, they are difficult to compare and it is unclear which measure is preferable when one needs to discover FDs in real-world data, i.e., data that only approximately satisfies the FD. In response, this paper formally and qualitatively compares AFD measures. We obtain a formal comparison through a novel presentation of measures in terms of Shannon and logical entropy. Qualitatively, we perform a sensitivity analysis w.r.t. structural properties of input relations and quantitatively study the effectiveness of AFD measures for ranking AFDs on real world data. Based on this analysis, we give clear recommendations for the AFD measures to use in practice.

Introduction

There are many benefits to knowing the set of functional dependencies (FDs for
short) that are expected to hold in the instances of a database schema: FDs aid
in ensuring data consistency and help in data
cleaning
[
1

2
]
;
facilitate data integration
[
3
]
; and can be
exploited for query
optimization
[
4

5
]
,
among other tasks. In many data science scenarios, however, the set of design
FDs is unknown or incomplete
[
6
]
. As such, a
variety of techniques have been proposed to reverse engineer this set of design
FDs from a given relation instance
[
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
6
]
.

Reverse engineering is particularly challenging in the practical setting
where the relation instance itself does not satisfy the target set of design
FDs. This may happen, e.g.,
due to errors during data entry, resulting in a corrupt instance. To recover
the set of design FDs in such a setting, it clearly does not suffice to simply
enumerate the FDs that are satisfied in the relation instance. Instead, one must also consider
approximate functional dependencies

(AFDs), that is, FDs that “almost hold” in the relation.
A key decision to then make is when an FD “almost” holds. This decision is
reflected in the adoption of an
AFD measure
, which formally quantifies
the extent to which an FD holds approximately in a given relation by
attributing a score in the interval
[0,1]
. Higher values indicate a higher
degree of FD satisfaction. As such, an AFD measure provides a way of

ranking
the search space of all possible FDs where higher-scoring FDs are ranked before lower-scoring ones. A good AFD measure, then, is one that
ranks FDs in the relation’s target set of design FDs higher than those that
are not in the target set, and does this consistently for relation instances
that occur in practice. Given a good AFD measure one may discover the set of design FDs by ranking the search space, and returning all FDs smaller than a given threshold.

Many AFD measures have been proposed
in the literature over the past decades

(a)

How do the different AFD measures compare?

(b)

II
Related Work and Paper Scope

Relaxing FDs.

In the literature there are two distinct
ways to relax the notion of an FD
[
21
]
: relax
the constraint that an FD
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
needs to be fully satisfied; or replace
the way in which tuples are compared on their
\bm{X}
-values by a similarity
function rather than strict equality. We focus on the former.

AFD measures versus discovery algorithms.

We focus on understanding AFD measures and how they allow us to rank the search space
of possible FDs for AFD discovery. A related but orthogonal issue, that we do not consider in this paper, is the study
of AFD
discovery algorithms
. AFD discovery algorithms usually fix a way
to quantify the approximateness of an FD—typically through the choice of an
AFD measure that fixes a ranking of the search space—but then combine a
multitude of techniques to do the actual discovery. This includes pruning the
ranked search space for efficiency reasons (e.g.,

[
22
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
)
or complementing the measures’ ranking with heuristics for application-specific
purposes (e.g.,
[
17
,
23
]
).
Of course, improved knowledge of AFD measures may aid in improving discovery
algorithms in the future. For this reason, when we introduce measures in
Section
IV
we also indicate in which discovery algorithms they
are used. Because discovery algorithms do not directly expose the way in which
they rank the search space of FDs, we do not experimentally compare against
discovery algorithms.

Correlation.

When an FD holds in
a relation, there is clearly a statistical correlation among the FD’s
attributes. Conversely, correlated attributes may (but need not) indicate the
presence of an FD. The techniques that are typically used to test statistical
correlation, such as the
\chi^{2}
test or mean-square
contingency
[
17
]
, however, only measure the
strength of correlation (e.g.,
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
are correlated) but do not indicate
the direction in which functional dependence (
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
or
\bm{Y}\to\bm{X}
) is likely
to hold. As such, these techniques do not form appropriate AFD measures
[
16
]
and are not further considered here.

Exact FD discovery.

In the context of exact FD discovery, some works
consider the problem of ranking exact FDs according to relevance, where the
challenge lies in quantifying relevance
[
24
]
. We are not concerned with exact FD discovery, but with
measures for quantifying the extent to which FDs hold approximately.
Discovery of AFDs should also not be confused with the approximate discovery of
exact FDs as e.g., done in
[
10
]
. There,
only a subset of all FDs that are satisfied are computed in return for
performance improvements.

Existing comparisons of AFD measures.

Giannella and Robertson
[
12
]
compare a limited
number of measures on theoretical examples as
well as on 4 real world datasets. In their experiments, they report on average
differences between pairs of measures and do not compare with a ground truth set
of FDs. They therefore do not empirically compare the effectiveness of the
measures as done here. In their survey concerning FD relaxations, Caruccio
et al.
[
21
]
also survey some of the AFD measures that are considered here, but do not provide a qualitative comparison.

Discovery of Conditional FDs.

Conditional FDs (CFDs for short)
generalize FDs: they are FDs that only hold on a subset of the
data
[
25
]
. CFDs are widely used in data
cleaning
[
26
,
27
]
.
Discovery of (approximate) CFDs amounts to (i) selecting suitable subsets of the
data and (ii) discovering (A)FDs in these
subsets
[
28
]
. In particular, Geerts and
Rammelaere
[
28
]
propose a generic (A)CFD
discovery algorithm in which any (A)FD discovery algorithm can be plugged.
Insights into AFD measures, as is our focus here, can improve AFD discovery which in turn can be useful for the discovery of (A)CFDs.

Applications of AFDs.

Data management tasks may use AFDs to
different extents. For example, only semantically meaningful AFDs (i.e., FDs
that a human database administrator would include in a database schema design)
are useful for data cleaning tasks
[
22
]
while
for query optimisation one could try to exploit all AFDs present in a
relation, even if they are not semantically meaningful. Our real-world
benchmark focuses on the former kind of AFDs.
Thus, we present a study of
AFD measures applicable to discover semantically meaningful AFDs.

Probabilities.

Both notions of entropy are defined w.r.t. a given joint probability

is the probability of observing
\bm{w}
when

only well-defined when

is non-empty. Because the empty relation vacuously satisfies all FDs, we will implicitly assume without loss of generality
in the rest of this paper that relations are non-empty.

To simplify notation in what follows, we simply write

\displaystyle p_{R}(\bm{y})=\sum_{\bm{w}:\bm{W}\text{ s.t. }{\bm{w}}|_{\bm{Y}}=\bm{y}}p_{R}(\bm{w}),\;\;\;\;\;\;p_{R}(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x})=\frac{p_{R}(\bm{x}\bm{y})}{p_{R}(\bm{x})}.

equals the distribution induced by

IV
AFD Measures

In this section, we survey the literature on AFD measures.

AFD measures.

Formally, an
AFD measure
, short for

approximate
FD measure, is a function that maps pairs
(\varphi,R)
,
with
\varphi
an FD and
R
a relation, to a number in the interval
[0,1]

that indicates the level to which
\varphi
holds in
R
. Higher values are
intended to indicate that
R
makes fewer violations to
\varphi
, and we
require that
f(\varphi,R)=1
if
R
perfectly satisfies
\varphi
.
It is important to note that instead of defining AFD measures, some papers in
the literature define
error measures
where a high value indicates
a high number of errors against the FD. In what follows, we routinely re-define
such error measures
e
into an AFD measure
f_{e}
by setting

f_{e}(\varphi,R):=1-e(\varphi,R)
.

Every AFD measure
f
naturally gives rise to an associated AFD discovery
algorithm as follows. From an abstract viewpoint, an AFD discovery algorithm
simply consists of a fixed AFD measure
f
and a threshold

\epsilon\in[0,1]
. Given a relation
R(\bm{W})
the algorithm returns all FDs over
\bm{W}
whose
f
-value lies in the range
[\epsilon,1[
. (In particular, this excludes the FDs satisfied by
R
.)

Interpretation and baselines.

As with all threshold-based algorithms, a key difficulty for AFD discovery
algorithms lies in determining the correct threshold
\epsilon
to use. At its
core, this question boils down to how we should interpret the significance of
the values returned by
f
. It is tempting to see the values of
f
as a
percentage with
f(\varphi,R)=1
indicating that
R
perfectly satisfies

\varphi
and
f(\varphi,R)=0
indicating that
R
completely fails to
satisfy
\varphi
. This interpretation, however, is only valid if the measure
has a notion of
R
“completely failing to satisfy”
\varphi
. In particular,
this is only possible when there are relations for which
f(\varphi,R)=0
.
In what follows, we call a
relation
R
with
f(\varphi,R)=0
a
baseline of
f
for
\varphi
. If

f
has a baseline for every FD
\varphi
then we say that
f

has
baselines
, otherwise we call
f

without baselines
. Having baselines
is a necessary condition for interpreting measure scores as
percentages.

Conventions.

Throughout this section, assume that

R
is a
\bm{W}
-relation, let
\bm{X},\bm{Y}
be disjoint subsets of
\bm{W}
and let

\varphi=\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
. We convene that for all measures
f
that we describe, we
trivially set
f(\varphi,R):=1
if
R\models\varphi
. So, the definitions
that follow only apply when
R\not\models\varphi
. In that case, observe that

R
must be non-empty, that
|\!\dom_{R}(\bm{X})|\not=|R|
and that

|\!\dom_{R}(\bm{Y})|>1
since otherwise
R
trivially satisfies
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
. As a consequence,
H_{R}(\bm{Y})>0
and
h_{R}(\bm{Y})>0
. This ensures that the denominator of fractions in the formulas that follow are never zero.

IV-A

Co-occurrence ratio

Ilyas et al.
[
17
]
consider the derivation of
AFDs (called
soft
FDs in their paper) as well as general correlations
between attributes. To derive AFDs, they consider the
ratio between the number of distinct
\bm{X}
-tuples and the number of distinct

\bm{X}\bm{Y}
-tuples occurring in
R
. We denote this measure by
\rho
, formally
defined as:

\rho(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R):=\frac{|\dom_{\bm{X}}(R)|}{|\dom_{\bm{X}\bm{Y}}(R)|}.

This is
1
if
R
satisfies
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
and decreases when more
\bm{y}
-tuples
occur with the same
\bm{x}
-tuple. Note that
\rho
is a set-based measure, as it
ignores the multiplicities of the tuples in
R
. It is also
without baselines, as
|\!\dom_{\bm{X}}(R)|>0
for any non-empty relation
R

and as, by convention,
\rho(\varphi,R)=1
when
R
is empty.

IV-B

g-measures

Kivinen and Mannila
[
11
]
introduced three error measures on set-based relations. Generalized to bag-based relations, and converted to AFD measures, these are the following.

The measure
g_{1}
.

The measure
g_{1}
is based on logical
entropy. Specifically, Kivinen and Manila defined
g_{1}
to reflect the
(normalized) number of violating pairs in
R
. Here, a pair
(\bm{w},\bm{w}^{\prime})
of

R
-tuples is a
violating pair
if they are equal on
\bm{X}
but differ on

\bm{Y}
. Formally, if we denote the bag of violating pairs in
R\times R
by

G_{1}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)
then, converted to an AFD measure instead of an error measure

\displaystyle g_{1}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)

\displaystyle:=\frac{|R|^{2}-|G_{1}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)|}{|R|^{2}}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{|G_{1}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)|}{|R|^{2}}=1-h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X}).

In other words,
g_{1}
is maximized when the logical conditional entropy is minimized.

The measure
g_{1}
is without baselines.
Because pairs of the form
(\bm{w},\bm{w})
are never violating, it is straightforward to see that the total number of violating pairs is bounded from above by
|R|^{2}-\sum_{\bm{w}}R(\bm{w})^{2}
. We denote by
g^{\prime}_{1}
the normalized version of
g_{1}
,

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{1}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)

\displaystyle:=1-\frac{|G_{1}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)|}{|R|^{2}-\sum_{\bm{w}}R(\bm{w})^{2}}.

The baselines of
g^{\prime}_{1}
are hence those relations for which the set
G_{1}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)
consists of all possible violating pairs.

Both
g_{1}
and
g^{\prime}_{1}
have been used as the basis of AFD discovery
algorithms. In particular,
g_{1}
is the basis of

Fdx

[
23
]
while
g^{\prime}_{1}
is the basis of

Pyro

[
22
]
. Adaptations of
g^{\prime}_{1}
are also used in the context of denial constraints
[
29
]

and roll-up dependencies
[
30
]
.

The measure
g_{2}
.

Kivinen and Manila defined
g_{2}
to reflect the probability that a random tuple participates in a violating pair. We define
G_{2}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)
to be the set of all tuples in
R
that participate in a violating pair,

\displaystyle G_{2}(\bm{X}{\tiny\to}\bm{Y},R):=\left\{\bm{w}\in R\mid\exists\bm{w}^{\prime}\in R,(\bm{w},\bm{w}^{\prime})\in G_{1}(\bm{X}{\tiny\to}\bm{Y},R)\right\}.

Then,
g_{2}
, converted to an AFD measure instead of
an error measure as originally proposed,
computes the probability that a tuple, drawn randomly from
R
according to
p_{R}
, is not part of a violating pair,

g_{2}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R):=1-\sum_{\bm{w}\in G_{2}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)}p_{R}(\bm{w}).

The FD-compliance-ratio, that is used as one of the building blocks in UNI-DETECT
[
31
]
, is based on
g_{2}
.

The measure
g_{3}
.

The measure
g_{3}
computes the relative size of a maximal subrelation of
R
for which
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
holds. Specifically, define
R^{\prime}(\bm{W})
to be a subrelation of
R(\bm{W})
, denoted
R^{\prime}\subseteq R
, if
R^{\prime}(\bm{w})\leq R(\bm{w})

for all
\bm{w}\colon\bm{W}
. Let
G_{3}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)
denote the set of all subrelations of
R
that satisfy
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
,

G_{3}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R):=\left\{R^{\prime}\mid R^{\prime}\subseteq R,R^{\prime}\models\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}\right\}.

Then

g_{3}
is defined as the maximum relative size of a subrelation satisfying
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
:

\displaystyle g_{3}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)

\displaystyle:=\max_{R^{\prime}\in G_{3}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)}\frac{|R^{\prime}|}{|R|}.

Note that
1-g_{3}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)
can naturally be interpreted as the minimum fraction of tuples that need to be removed for
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
to hold in
R
.

The measure
g_{3}
is without baselines.or any non-empty
R
we can
always obtain a subrelation
R^{\prime}\in G_{3}(\varphi,R)
of size

|\!\dom_{\bm{X}}(R)|
by arbitrarily fixing one
\bm{y}
-value for each

\bm{x}
-value. As such,
g_{3}
is bounded from below by

\frac{|\dom_{\bm{X}}(R)|}{|R|}>0
. Gianella and
Robertson
[
12
]
proposed a normalized variant

g^{\prime}_{3}
of
g_{3}
, defined as follows:

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{3}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)

\displaystyle:=\max_{R^{\prime}\in G_{3}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)}\frac{|R^{\prime}|-|\dom_{R}(\bm{X})|}{|R|-|\dom_{R}(\bm{X})|}.

This variant has baselines, namely all relations
R
for which no
subrelation
R^{\prime}\in G_{3}(\varphi,R)
is larger than
|\dom_{R}(\bm{X})|
.

The unnormalized measure
g_{3}
is used in multiple AFD discovery algorithms
[
11
,
9
,
32
,
33
]
. Furthermore, the ‘per-tuple’ probability of an FD, as defined in
[
3
]
, is precisely
g_{3}
.
Berzal et al.
[
18
]
use it as the basis for relational decomposition based on AFDs instead of FDs.
Exact and approximate solutions for the computation of
g_{3}
in the context of non-crisp FDs are proposed in
[
34
]
.
We note that
g_{3}
has been generalized to other dependencies as well: e.g.,
conditional FDs
[
35
,
36
]
, inclusion dependencies
[
37
]
, and conditional matching dependencies
[
38
]
.
By contrast, the normalized version
g^{\prime}_{3}
only appears in
[
12
]
.

IV-C

Fraction of information

Fraction of Information.

Cavallo and Pittarelli
[
39
]
introduced

fraction of information
(
FI
) as a way to generalize FDs from
deterministic to probabilistic databases. Usage of
FI
as an AFD measure was later studied by Giannelli and Robertson
[
12
]
.

FI
is based on Shannon entropy and is formally defined as

\text{FI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R):=\frac{H_{R}(\bm{Y})-H_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X})}{H_{R}(\bm{Y})}.

The numerator
H_{R}(\bm{Y})-H_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X})
is known as
mutual information

[
19
]
, which we further denote by
I_{R}(\bm{X};\bm{Y})
.

We can understand
FI
as follows.
H_{R}(\bm{Y})
measures the uncertainty of
observing
\bm{Y}
, while
H_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X})
measures the uncertainty of
observing
\bm{Y}
after observing
\bm{X}
.
FI
hence represents the proportional
reduction of uncertainty about
\bm{Y}
that is achieved by knowing
\bm{X}
. When
R

satisfies
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
, there is no uncertainty about
\bm{Y}
after observing
\bm{X}

and hence
H_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X})=0
and so
FI
is
1
. Conversely, when
\bm{X}

and
\bm{Y}
are independent random variables in
p_{R}
, there is no
reduction in uncertainty, and hence
H_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X})=H_{R}(\bm{Y})
and so

FI
is
0
. Thus, the baselines of
FI
for
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
are
those relations
R
for which
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
are independent in
p_{R}
.

Bias.

Mandros et al.
[
13
,
14
]

and Pennerath et al.
[
15
]
proposed two refinements
to
FI
specifically for AFD discovery, called
reliable
FI

(
RFI
) and

smoothed
FI

(
SFI
), respectively. They are motivated in proposing these
refinements by the following observation. Consider a relation
S(\bm{W})
and assume
that we are given relation
R(\bm{W})
of size
n
that is obtained by sampling
n

tuples from
S
according to distribution
p_{S}
. Further assume that we do
not have access to
S
and wish to determine
\text{FI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},S)
based on

R
. Then a result by Roulston
[
40
]
states that the expected
value of
I_{R}(\bm{X};\bm{Y})
, taken over all
R
obtained in this manner, equals

I_{S}(\bm{X};\bm{Y})+\frac{|\dom_{S}(\bm{X}\bm{Y})|-|\dom_{S}(\bm{X})|-|\dom_{S}(\bm{Y})|+1}{2n}.

In other words, we may expect
I_{R}(\bm{X};\bm{Y})
to overestimate

I_{S}(\bm{X};\bm{Y})
and the magnitude of overestimation depends on the size of the
active domains of
\bm{X}\bm{Y}
,
\bm{X}
, and
\bm{Y}
in
S
, as well as on
n
. Additionally, because
H_{R}(\bm{Y})
underestimates
H_{S}(\bm{Y})

[
40
]
,
we may conclude that
\text{FI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)
is expected to overestimate

\text{FI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},S)
and the magnitude of overestimation depends on the active
domain sizes and the size of
S
. This overestimation is problematic since
\text{FI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)
will be quite large, even if
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
are independent in
p_{S}
, resulting in
\text{FI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},S)
being
0
.

Reliable
FI
.

Reliable
FI
corrects for this bias by subtracting the mutual information value
that is expected under random
(\bm{X};\bm{Y})
-permutations.

Definition 1
.

Relation
R^{\prime}
is an
(\bm{X};\bm{Y})
-permutation
of
R
, denoted

R\sim_{\bm{X};\bm{Y}}R^{\prime}

if (i)
|R|=|R^{\prime}|
; (ii)

\pi_{\bm{X}}(R)=\pi_{\bm{X}}(R^{\prime})
; (iii)
\pi_{\bm{Y}}(R)=\pi_{\bm{Y}}(R^{\prime})
; and (iv)
\pi_{\bm{Z}}(R)=\pi_{\bm{Z}}(R^{\prime})
where
\bm{Z}=\bm{W}\setminus\bm{X}\bm{Y}
.

In particular,
R^{\prime}
and
R
have the same marginal distributions on
\bm{X}
and on
\bm{Y}
,
p_{R^{\prime}}(\bm{X})=p_{R}(\bm{X})
and
p_{R^{\prime}}(\bm{Y})=p_{R}(\bm{Y})
. In what follows, for a measure
f
, we denote by
\expect_{R}[f(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R))]
the expected value of
f(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)
where the expectation is taken over all
(\bm{X};\bm{Y})
-permutations of
R
.

Reliable fraction of information is then defined as

\displaystyle\text{RFI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)

\displaystyle:=\text{FI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)-\expect_{R}[\text{FI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)].

Because the number of permutations of
R
is finite, we may compute
\expect_{R}[\text{FI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)]
, and hence also
\text{RFI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)
, by simply computing
\text{FI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R^{\prime})
for every permutation
R^{\prime}
of
R
and taking the average. More efficient algorithms are proposed in
[
14
,
13
]
. Even with these improved algorithms, computing
RFI
remains inefficient, as we show in Section
VI
.

Strictly speaking,
RFI
is not an AFD measure since it can become negative when
\text{FI}(\varphi,R)<\expect_{R}[\text{FI}(\varphi,R)]
. Because such negative
RFI
values indicate that there is weak evidence to conclude that
\varphi
is an AFD, we turn
RFI
into an actual AFD measure
\text{RFI}^{+}
by setting

\text{RFI}^{+}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R):=\max(\text{RFI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R),0).

The baselines of
\text{RFI}^{+}
for
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
are hence all relations whose
FI
value is smaller or equal than the expected value under random permutations.

Smoothed
FI
.

Smoothed
FI
uses
laplace smoothing
to reduce bias. Laplace smoothing is
a well-known statistical technique to reduce estimator variance. It is
parameterized by a value
\alpha>0
. Specifically, for a relation
S(\bm{X}\bm{Y})
, let
S^{(\alpha)}

denote the
\alpha
-smoothed version of
S
, defined by

S^{(\alpha)}(\bm{x}\bm{y}):=S(\bm{x}\bm{y})+\alpha
for every
\bm{x}\in\dom_{S}(\bm{X})
and
\bm{y}\in\dom_{S}(\bm{Y})
. Note in particular, that it is possible that
S(\bm{x}\bm{y})=0
, in which case
S^{(\alpha)}(\bm{x}\bm{y})=\alpha
. Then the smoothed
FI
of
R
is simply the normal
FI
of the
\alpha
-smoothed version of
\pi_{\bm{X}\bm{Y}}(R)
:

\displaystyle\text{SFI}_{\alpha}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)

\displaystyle:=\text{FI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},\pi_{\bm{X}\bm{Y}}^{(\alpha)}(R)).

We note that, because
\pi_{\bm{X}\bm{Y}}^{(\alpha)}(R)
contains a tuple
\bm{x}\bm{y}
for every possible combination of
\bm{x}\in\dom_{\bm{X}}(R)
and
\bm{y}\in\dom_{\bm{Y}}(R)
, it can be many times larger than
R
.
SFI
is therefore also relatively inefficient to compute, as we show in Section
VI
.

AFD discovery algorithms based on
RFI
and
SFI
are presented in
[
13
,
14
]

and
[
15
]
, respectively.

IV-D

Probabilistic dependency,
\tau
and
\mu

Piatetsky-Shapiro and Matheus
[
16
]
proposed
probabilistic
dependency
as another probabilistic generalization of a functional
dependency. They also introduced a normalized version of probabilistic
dependency, which is equivalent to the Goodman and Kruskal
\tau
measure of
association
[
41
]
. Finally, they also propose a rescaled
version of
\tau
. All three notions are defined as follows. It is worth noting,
that, apart from
[
16
]
, we are not aware of any work that
considers these measures for AFD discovery in the database context, let alone
designs AFD discovery algorithms for them.

Probabilistic dependency.

The
probabilistic dependency of
\bm{Y}
on
\bm{X}
in
R
, denoted by

\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)
, represents the conditional probability that two tuples
drawn randomly with replacement from
R
are equal on
\bm{Y}
, given that they are
equal on
\bm{X}
. Formally,

\displaystyle\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\bm{x}}p_{R}(\bm{x})\pdep(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x},R),

where
\pdep(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x},R)
is the probability that two random
\bm{Y}
-tuples
drawn with replacement from the conditional distribution
p_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x})

are equal:

\pdep(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x},R):=\sum_{\bm{y}}p_{R}(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x})^{2}=1-h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x}).

Probabilistic dependency is hence a measure based on logical entropy.
It can be understood as follows. Suppose that we are given two tuples that equal

\bm{x}
on
\bm{X}
. Then
\pdep(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x},R)
is the probability that these tuples are
also equal on
\bm{Y}
, and
\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)
is the expected value of

\pdep(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x},R)
over all
\bm{x}
.

We note that probabilistic dependency can also be seen as a generalization of the measure
g_{2}
. Whereas
g_{2}
computes the probability that a random tuple cannot be extended to a violating pair, probabilistic dependency computes the average conditional probability that a given
\bm{X}
-tuple
\bm{x}
cannot be extended to a violating pair, where the average is taken over all values of
\bm{X}
.

The measure
\tau
.

It is straightforward to see that
\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)>0
, always. As such,
\pdep
is a measure without baselines. In fact, Piatetsky-Shapiro and Matheus
[
16
]
show that we always have

\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)\geq\pdep(\bm{Y},R)

where
\pdep(\bm{Y},R)
, called
probabilistic self-dependency
, is defined
as the probability that two random tuples in
R
have equal
\bm{Y}
attributes,

\pdep(\bm{Y},R):=\sum_{\bm{y}}p_{R}(\bm{y})^{2}=1-h_{R}(\bm{Y}).

To account for the relationship between
\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)
and
\pdep(\bm{Y},R)
,
Piatetsky-Shapiro and Matheus propose to normalize
\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)
w.r.t.
\pdep(\bm{Y},R)
. The
resulting measure is equivalent to the Goodman and Kruskal
\tau
(tau) measure
of association
[
41
]
, which is defined as

\displaystyle\tau(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R):=\frac{\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)-\pdep(\bm{Y},R)}{1-\pdep(\bm{Y},R)}.

Piatetsky-Shapiro and Matheus
explain
\tau
in the following way
[
16
]
. Suppose we
are given a tuple drawn randomly from
R
according to
p_{R}
, and we need to
guess its
\bm{Y}
value. One strategy is to make guesses randomly according to the
marginal distribution of
\bm{Y}
, i.e. guess value
\bm{Y}=\bm{y}
with probability

p_{R}(\bm{y})
. Then the probability for a correct guess is
\pdep(\bm{Y},R)
. If we
also know that item has
\bm{X}=\bm{x}
, we can improve our guess using conditional
probabilities of
\bm{Y}
, given that
\bm{X}=\bm{x}
. Then our probability for success,
averaged over all values of
\bm{X}
, is
\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)
, and

\tau(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)
is the relative increase in our probability of successfully
guessing
\bm{Y}
, given
\bm{X}
. The baselines of
\tau
for
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
are hence those relations where this relative increase is zero.

The measure
\mu
.

Piatetsky-Shapiro and
Matheus
[
16
]
note that
\pdep
and
\tau
have the following undesirable property.

Theorem 1

(Piatetsky-Rotem-Shapiro
[
16
]
)
.

Given a random relation
R
of size
N\geq 2
containing attributes
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
, where
\bm{X}
has
K=|\dom_{R}(\bm{X})|
distinct values in its active domain, the expected values of
\pdep
and
\tau
under random permutations of
R
are

\displaystyle\expect_{R}[\pdep(\bm{X}{\tiny\to}\bm{Y},R)]

\displaystyle=\pdep(\bm{Y},R)+\frac{K-1}{N-1}(1-\pdep(\bm{Y},R)),

\displaystyle\expect_{R}[\tau(\bm{X}{\tiny\to}\bm{Y},R)]

\displaystyle=\frac{|\dom_{R}(\bm{X})|-1}{|R|-1}.

Thus, for a fixed distribution of
\bm{Y}
values,
\expect_{R}[\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)]

depends only on the number of distinct
\bm{X}
values and not on their relative
frequency. Moreover, the formula for
\expect_{R}[\tau(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)]
tells us that
if we have two candidate AFDs with the same right hand side,
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
and

\bm{Z}\to\bm{Y}
, then if
|\dom_{R}(\bm{Z})|>|\dom_{R}(\bm{X})|
, we may expect

\tau
to score
\bm{Z}\to\bm{Y}
better than
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
, regardless of any intrinsic
better relationship between
\bm{Z}
and
\bm{Y}
over
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
in
R
. In
response, Piatetsky-Shapiro and Matheus compensate for this effect by
introducing the measure
\mu
which normalizes
\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)
with respect
to
\expect_{R}[\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)]
instead of

\pdep(\bm{Y},R)
:
3
3

3

This fraction is ill-defined
if the denominator
1-\expect_{R}[\pdep(\varphi,R)]=0
. This only happens,
however, when
R\models\varphi
, which we have assumed not to be the case
throughout this section, since we have already convened to set

\mu(\varphi,R)=1
whenever
R\models\varphi
. See the Appendix for a
proof.

\displaystyle\mu(\bm{X}{\tiny\to}\bm{Y},R)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\pdep(\bm{X}{\tiny\to}\bm{Y},R)-\expect_{R}[\pdep(\bm{X}{\tiny\to}\bm{Y},R)]}{1-\expect_{R}[\pdep(\bm{X}{\tiny\to}\bm{Y},R)]}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1-\pdep(\bm{X}{\tiny\to}\bm{Y},R)}{1-\pdep(\bm{Y},R)}\frac{|R|-1}{|R|-|\dom_{R}(\bm{X})|}

Strictly speaking,
\mu
is not a measure since it returns negative values when
\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)>\expect_{R}[\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)]
. Because such negative
\mu
values indicate that there is weak evidence to conclude that
\varphi
is an AFD, we turn
\mu
into an actual AFD measure
\mu^{+}
by setting

\mu^{+}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R):=\max(\mu(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R),0).

The baselines of
\mu^{+}
for
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
are hence all relations where the
\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y})
value is smaller or equal to the expected value under random permuations.

IV-E

Classes of AFD measures.

Looking at the previous definitions, we observe three different notions that are used to formally define a measure. Consequently, we discern the following three classes (see also the second row in Table
III
):

(1)

The class of measures that have a notion of “violation” and quantify the number of violations, consisting of
\rho,g_{2},g_{3}
, and
g^{\prime}_{3}
. We denote this class by
Violation
(V).

(2)

The class of measures based on Shannon entropy, consisting of
\text{FI},\text{RFI}^{+}
, and
SFI
. We denote this class by
Shannon
(S).

(3)

The class of measures based on logical entropy, consisting of
g_{1},g^{\prime}_{1},\pdep,\tau
, and
\mu^{+}
and denoted by
Logical
(L).

In appendix
C
we observe that there are
striking similarities between measures in each of these classes, allowing
us to relate and link measures
across classes
. For example, we can
show that
FI
is the
Shannon
-based version of
\tau
, while
\pdep

is a
Logical
-based version of
g_{2}
. This gives a first insight into
how measures compare formally. By means of this comparison, we have
identified the following two new measures, which do not appear in the
literature but which are
Shannon
versions of existing measures. For
completeness, we include both of these measures in our study.

The new measure
g_{1}^{S}
.

The measure
g_{1}
is based on logical entropy. We may hence view
1-H_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X})
as the
Shannon equivalent of
g_{1}
, where logical entropy is replaced by Shannon entropy.
Giannella and Robertson
[
12
]
observed that

1-H_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X})
has the range
[-\infty,1]
instead of
[0,1]

and therefore disregard it as an AFD measure.
Nevertheless, we propose the following Shannon variant
for our comparison, obtained by limiting
1-H_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X})
to be positive:

g_{1}^{S}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R):=\max(1-H_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X}),0).

The new measure
\text{RFI}^{{}^{\prime}+}
.

We also observe a conceptual similarity between
\mu
and
RFI
:
\mu
corrects for the bias of
\tau
under random permutations while
RFI
corrects for the bias of
FI
under random permutations. Despite this conceptual similarity, note that the corrections are done differently:
\mu
corrects by taking the
normalized
difference between
\pdep
and
\expect_{R}[\pdep]
while
RFI
corrects by taking the
absolute
difference between
FI
and
\expect_{R}[\text{FI}]
. As such,
RFI
is not a normalized measure. Since it is natural to ask what the normalized variant of
RFI
is and how it behaves, we define

\text{RFI}^{{}^{\prime}+}(\varphi,R):=\max\left(\frac{\text{FI}(\varphi,R)-\expect_{R}[\text{FI}(\varphi,R)]}{1-\expect_{R}[\text{FI}(\varphi,R)]},0\right).

V
Sensitivity Analysis

In this section, we investigate the sensitivity of measures w.r.t. structural properties of the input relation. Specifically, we want to get insight into the measures’
ability to distinguish between an FD
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
in relation instances that were generated to satisfy the FD, but subsequently had errors introduced so that the FD no longer holds exactly, versus relation instances where
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
were randomly generated. A good FD measure should be able to consistently distinguish between these two cases, giving high scores to the former and low scores to the latter with a clear separation in scores between the two cases.

V-A

Methodology

There are various properties of the input relation
R
that may affect the measures’ power to distinguish between these two aforementioned cases. We study the effect on the distinguishing power for
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
w.r.t. the following.

(1) The
error rate
, i.e., the amount of errors introduced.
A reasonable requirement for a good AFD measure is that it should be inversely proportional to the error rate: an increase in the number of introduced errors should result in a decrease of the measure value. As discussed below, this is not the case for all considered measures.

(2) Statistics of the left-hand-side
\bm{X}
of the FD and the right-hand-side
\bm{Y}
of
the FD. Specifically, define
left-hand-side (LHS) uniqueness
of the
input relation
R
as the ratio
|\!\dom_{R}(\bm{X})|/|R|
, quantifying
the uniqueness of
\bm{X}
. Define the
right-hand-side (RHS) skew of
R
,
as the skewness of the distribution
p_{R}(\bm{Y})
. It has previously been observed in the
literature

[
16
,
15
,
13
,
14
]

that measures can be biased w.r.t. LHS-uniqueness and RHS-skew in the sense
that measures may give very high scores to relations with high LHS-uniqueness or
RHS-skew values. However, these statistics alone do
not
provide a good
signal for quantifying the approximateness of
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
because they look only
at
\bm{X}
or only at
\bm{Y}
but not at their correlation. We note that this is in
particular true for non-linear FDs: as the number of attributes in
\bm{X}
grows,
LHS uniqueness naturally tends to
1
. Thus, a measure that is biased
w.r.t. LHS-uniqueness risks returning too many non-linear FDs, especially on
tables with many attributes.

We have created three synthetic benchmarks, denoted
Err
,
Uniq
, and
Skew
to study the measures’ sensitivity to errors,
LHS uniqueness, and RHS-skew, respectively.
Each synthetic benchmark
\mathcal{B}
consists of relations
R(\bm{X}\bm{Y})
with

\bm{X}=\{X\}
and
\bm{Y}=\{Y\}
and is partitioned into two subsets: (1)

\mathcal{B}^{-}
containing relations
R
where
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}\not\in\Delta(R)
;
and (2)
\mathcal{B}^{+}
containing relations where
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}\in\Delta(R)
.
(Recall that
\Delta(R)
denotes the design schema of
R
.) Each subset employs a distinct random process to generate relations. For
relations in
\mathcal{B}^{-}
,
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
values are generated independently
at random, while relations in
\mathcal{B}^{+}
are generated by first constructing
a relation
R
such that
R\models\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
, and then passing
R
through a controlled noisy error channel.

Generation process.

The generation process of a relation
R
depends on a number of parameters
that are drawn uniformly at random from the following ranges:

|R|\in[100;10000]
;
|\!\dom_{R}(\bm{X})|\in[\frac{1}{5}|R|,\frac{3}{4}|R|]
,

|\!\dom_{R}(\bm{Y})|\in[5,\frac{1}{2}|\!\dom_{R}(\bm{X})|]
; and error rate
\eta\in[0.5\%,2\%]
.
Values for
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
are drawn according to the Beta
distribution,

B(\alpha,\beta)
, which is a family of continuous probability distributions
defined on the interval
[0,1]
in terms of two positive parameters
\alpha

and
\beta
that control the shape of the distribution. We consider the ranges

\alpha\in(0,1]
and
\beta\in[1,10]
. For
\alpha=\beta=1
the distribution
is uniform and for any other values it is reverse J-shaped with a right tail.
The
skewness
is defined as

\frac{2(\beta-\alpha)\sqrt{\alpha+\beta+1}}{(\alpha+\beta+2)\sqrt{\alpha\beta}}
and is known to measure the asymmetry of the probability
distribution about its mean. In particular, the skew is zero for the uniform
distribution and increasing values indicate longer tails with lower mass, that
is, a higher mass near the left end of the interval
[0,1]
. We sample values
for
\alpha
and
\beta
such that the skewness is at most one (except for

Skew
below where we consider skew values up to 10).

So, for every relation
R
the parameters
|R|
,
|\!\dom_{R}(\bm{X})|
,
|\!\dom_{R}(\bm{Y})|
,
\alpha_{\bm{X}}
,
\beta_{\bm{X}}
,
\alpha_{\bm{Y}}
,
\beta_{\bm{Y}}
,
\eta
are chosen uniformly at random under the conditions described above.
To generate a table
R
in
\mathcal{B}^{-}
, the following procedure is repeated
|R|
times: sample
\bm{x}\in\dom_{R}(\bm{X})
(resp.,
\bm{y}\in\dom_{R}(\bm{Y})
) according to
B(\alpha_{\bm{X}},\beta_{\bm{X}})
(resp.,
B(\alpha_{\bm{Y}},\beta_{\bm{Y}})
) and add
(\bm{x},\bm{y})
to
R
.
To generate a table
R
in
\mathcal{B}^{+}
,
we first construct a dictionary
D
by, for each value
\bm{x}\in dom_{R}(\bm{X})
,
assigning a value
D(\bm{x})\in dom_{R}(\bm{Y})
drawn at random according to

B(\alpha_{\bm{Y}},\beta_{\bm{Y}})
. Then, we populate
R
by adding
|R|
tuples

(\bm{x},D(\bm{x}))
where

\bm{x}\in dom_{R}(\bm{X})
is drawn at random according to
B(\alpha_{\bm{X}},\beta_{\bm{X}})
.
By construction,
R
satisfies the FD
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
. We then pass

R
through a controlled error channel such that, denoting by
R^{\prime}
the obtained
relation,
R^{\prime}
does not satisfy
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
anymore. Concretely, we modify

k=\lfloor\eta|R|\rfloor
tuples
\bm{w}=(\bm{x},D(\bm{x}))
, where
\eta
indicates the error
rate, by randomly picking any
\tilde{\bm{w}}\in R
with

\tilde{\bm{w}}|_{\bm{Y}}\neq\bm{w}|_{\bm{Y}}
and make
\tilde{\bm{w}}|_{\bm{Y}}
the new value for

\bm{w}|_{\bm{Y}}
. We point out that this does not introduce any new
\bm{Y}
-values and keeps
\dom_{R}(\bm{Y})
stable. We also experimented with other error channels that introduce new
\bm{Y}
values, but the results were similar and are therefore omitted.
Note that
\bm{X}
is not modified, and therefore
p_{R^{\prime}}(\bm{X})=p_{R}(\bm{X})
.
We note that the generation process is related to the one from
Zhang et al.
[
23
]
but with the addition of value
distributions for both
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
based on the Beta distribution.

The three synthetic benchmarks are created by controlling one of the parameters in the parameter set as follows. Every benchmark
\mathcal{B}
consists of 2500
\mathcal{B}^{-}
tables and 2500
\mathcal{B}^{+}
tables.

Benchmark
Err
.

We iteratively increase the error rate
\eta
from
0\%
to
10\%
in 50 steps and generate 50 relations in
\textsc{Err}^{+}
per step, varying all other parameters as described above.

is then extended with 2500 tables generated in
\textsc{Err}^{-}
.

Benchmark

.

We construct

by iteratively increasing LHS-uniqueness from
\frac{1}{5}|{R}|
to
10|{R}|
in 50 steps and by generating for every step 50 tables in
\textsc{Uniq}^{+}
and
\textsc{Uniq}^{-}
.

Benchmark
Skew
.

We construct
Skew
by iteratively increasing RHS-skew from 0 to 10 in 50 steps and by generating for every step 50 tables in
\textsc{Skew}^{+}
and
\textsc{Skew}^{-}
.

V-B

Results

We describe the results on the basis of Figure

, which, for each benchmark
\mathcal{B}
and measure
f
plots
\delta(f,\mathcal{B})
, the difference between average measure values on
\mathcal{B}^{+}
and average measure values on
\mathcal{B}^{-}
,

\delta(f,\mathcal{B}):=\text{avg}_{R\in\mathcal{B}^{+}}f(\bm{X}{\scriptsize\to}\bm{Y},R)\ -\ \text{avg}_{R\in\mathcal{B}^{-}}f(\bm{X}{\scriptsize\to}\bm{Y},R).

We also call

\delta(f,\mathcal{B})
the
separation
of
f
on

\mathcal{B}
. When it is small,
f
cannot distinguish between cases
where
\bm{X}
and
\bm{Y}
are sampled independently at random and where
data is generated according to our generation process for
\mathcal{B}^{+}
.
Values for
g_{1}
and
g^{\prime}_{1}
are grouped in Figure
1
as
their separations are identical. Our conclusions are summarized in
Table
III
.

Error rate.

The top row of Figure
1
plots the separation on
Err
as a function of error rate

\eta
. For
g_{1}
and
g^{\prime}_{1}
, the separation is zero, while for
SFI
it is nearly zero. This means that these measures have limited distinguishing power and are not well-suited as a yardstick
for assessing the amount of errors w.r.t. an FD.
For all other measures, there is a
clear separation, albeit less pronounced for
FI
and
\text{RFI}^{+}
.
As expected, when the error level increases the separation decreases, save for
g_{1}
,
g^{\prime}_{1}
, and
SFI
where it remains constant. The measures hence become less certain of having found an AFD as the error rate increases.
While
FI
and
\text{RFI}^{+}
also decrease as
\eta
increases, this decrease is less steep than for the other measures.

LHS-uniqueness.

The middle row of Figure
1
shows the separation on
Uniq
as a function of LHS-uniqueness.
For
g_{1}
,
g^{\prime}_{1}
and
SFI
, we see the same behavior as on
Err
: their separation is (nearly) zero; they hence lack distinguishing power.
Because it would be misleading to label
g_{1}
,
g^{\prime}_{1}
and
SFI
as being insensitive to LHS-uniqueness, we indicate in Table
III
that LHS-uniqueness is inapplicable with the symbol _.
The distinguishing power of
g^{\prime}_{3}
,
\text{RFI}^{{}^{\prime}+}
, and
\mu^{+}

is not affected by LHS-uniqueness as the separation remains large for all values of LHS-uniqueness. We do observe that the separation decreases slightly for very large LHS-uniqueness levels, indicating that these measures become less confident to have found an FD
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
in a relation
R
when
\pi_{\bm{X}}(R)
contains fewer duplicates.

For other measures the separation drops as LHS-uniqueness increases, tending to zero
at maximum LHS-uniqueness levels. These measures are hence biased w.r.t. LHS-uniqueness.
As discussed before,
it will therefore prove problematic to discover non-linear AFDs by means of these measures.

RHS-skew.

The bottom row of Figure
1
shows the separation on
Skew
as a function of
RHS-skew.
The measures
g_{1}
,
g^{\prime}_{1}

with the symbol _.
The distinguishing power of all
Violation
measures, as well as
g_{1}^{S}
, and
\pdep

These measures are thus biased w.r.t. RHS-skew. By contrast,
FI
,
\text{RFI}^{+}
,
\text{RFI}^{{}^{\prime}+}
,
\tau
, and
\mu^{+}
correct for this behavior and are insensitive to RHS-skew.

Conclusion.

The measures
g_{1}
,
g^{\prime}_{1}
and
SFI
are the least suitable AFD measures since, by contrast to the other measures, they do not clearly separate relations in
\mathcal{B}^{+}
from relations in
\mathcal{B}^{-}
for any of the three considered sensitivity parameters. The measures
g^{\prime}_{3}
,
\text{RFI}^{{}^{\prime}+}
, and
\mu^{+}
have a built-in mechanism that corrects for LHS-uniqueness, which is a most desirable property when discovering non-linear FDs. The
Shannon
measures (save
g_{1}^{S}
and
SFI
),
\tau
, and
\mu^{+}
correct for RHS-skew. The most desirable measures are therefore

\text{RFI}^{{}^{\prime}+}
and
\mu^{+}
as they both are insensitive to LHS-uniqueness and RHS-skew, and are inversely proportional to the error level.

VI
Evaluation on Real-World Data

In this section, we compare the effectiveness of the described AFD measures on
real-world tables which exhibit data distributions as well as data errors that
occur in practice. Because of space limitations, we focus on our most important
findings and refer to

complementary information and additional experiments in the Appendix.

VI-A

Overview

FDs in relations with
NULLs
.

The relations that we consider in this section come from practical domains and often also contain
NULL
values. Because it is unclear whether two distinct occurrences of a
NULL
should be considered the same value, or distinct values,
there is no clear semantics of FDs in the presence of
NULL
values. We therefore ignore
NULL
values when checking FD satisfaction and calculating measure scores. That is, if
R(\bm{W})
is a relation with
NULL
s and
\varphi=\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
an FD, then we consider
\varphi
to be satisfied if it is satisfied in the subrelation
R^{\prime}
of
R
consisting of all tuples
\bm{w}\in R
for which
\bm{w}(A)\not=\textsf{NULL}
for all
A\in\bm{X}\bm{Y}
. Similarly, the score of measure
f
on
(\varphi,R)
is computed by computing
f(\varphi,R^{\prime})
instead.

Real world data benchmark (
rwd
).

We created the
rwd
benchmark as follows. We started by considering
all relations mentioned in
[
42
]
, which collects the
real-world relations most commonly used in the dependency discovery
literature. This base set was extended with the relation Adult used, e.g., in

[
43
,
22
,
24
]
.
Since design schemas for these relations are unavailable, we manually created
them as follows. First, in order to ensure semantically sound design schemas, we
restricted our attention to the subset of relations that have a generally
interpretable domain. Further, to keep the manual annotation endeavor
manageable, we restricted ourselves to relations that have no more than 50
columns and to linear FDs. This results in 10 relations, listed in
Table
II
. For each relation
R
, we enumerate all candidate linear
FDs (i.e., pairs

(X,Y):\exists\bm{w}\in R,\bm{w}(X)\not=\textsf{NULL}\land\bm{w}(Y)\not=\textsf{NULL}
).
We manually validate whether a candidate FD is semantically
meaningful, and is hence part of the design schema or not, if its

g_{3}
-score is
\geq 0.5
. While we risk missing semantically meaningful FDs this way, note that
a
g_{3}
-score
<0.5
means that we need to remove more
than
50\%
of the tuples to obtain a subrelation that satisfies the candidate
FD, making it an improbable candidate for the design schema.
We identified 1170 candidate FDs to inspect. Two individuals manually inspected
each candidate. Non-matching decisions (i.e. one saw a candidate as valid
whereas the other did not) were discussed until a consensus was reached. We
observe that each validated semantically meaningful candidate FD has a

g_{3}
-score
\geq 0.99
, strengthening our impressions that it is unlikely that we have missed semantically meaningful FDs.

In this manner, we derive for
each benchmark relation

R
its design schema
\Delta(R)
. This set of FDs is
partitioned into two sets:
\perfectFD(R):=\{\varphi\in\Delta(R)\mid R\models\varphi\}
the set of
perfect
(design) FDs and
\designAFD(R):=\{\varphi\in\Delta(R)\mid R\not\models\varphi\}
the set of
approximate
(design) FDs. In particular,
\designAFD{R}
forms the ground truth of FDs to discover during AFD discovery on
R
.

shows statistics of the obtained benchmark. In total, we obtain 143 design FDs across all relations in

. Out of these, only a

Methodology.

We are interested in comparing the suitability of AFDs measures for the purpose
of AFD discovery.

Therefore, we compare AFD measures as follows. Remember from
Section
IV
that every AFD measure
f
and every threshold

\epsilon\in[0,1]
naturally induces a discovery algorithm
A_{f}^{\epsilon}

which, on input relation
R(\bm{W})
, returns all FDs
\varphi
over
\bm{W}
with
R\not\models\varphi
and
f(\varphi,R)\in[\epsilon,1[
. In this respect, every
measure hence defines a class
\class_{f}
of discovery algorithms, namely

\class_{f}=\{A_{f}^{\epsilon}\mid 0\leq\epsilon<1\}
. Given a subset
\mathcal{B}
of benchmark relations, we compare the
effectiveness of measures on
\mathcal{B}
by computing the area under the precision-recall
curve
(AUC-PR) of
\class_{f}
for each measure
f
, where the PR-curve is the set

\{(\recall(A,\mathcal{B}),\precision(A,\mathcal{B}))\mid A\in\class_{f}\}
. Here,
\recall(A,\mathcal{B})
and
\precision(A,\mathcal{B})
denote recall and precision of
A
on
\mathcal{B}
, respectively.
It is known that PR curves are well-suited to visualize the tradeoff between
precision and recall at various values of
\epsilon
when the prediction classes
are very imbalanced, which is the case here. So, the measure with the highest
AUC-PR score is the measure providing the best such tradeoff.

Furthermore, to obtain a more fine-grained view of measure performance on the
level of each Relation
R
individually we also report the
rank at max recall
:

\rankAtMaxRecall(f,R):=|A^{\epsilon}_{f}|
, with
\epsilon=min(f(\designAFD(R))

Intuitively,

indicates how many candidate AFDs need to be examined
when processing them in decreasing order of
f
-score to find all of
\designAFD(R)
.

values of

, namely
\alpha\in\{0.5,1,2\}
. Because the performance of

VII
Conclusions

An overview of our results is given in Table
III
. We find that well-ranking measures exist within each class:
g^{\prime}_{3}
in
Violation
,
\text{RFI}^{{}^{\prime}+}
in
Shannon
, and
\mu^{+}
in
Logical
.
We further observe that measures are only effective when correctly normalized—which is not always done in the literature.
Indeed,

g_{3}
is widely known and cited
[
11
,
9
,
32
,
12
,
33
,
34
,
18
]

but to the best of our knowledge only
[
12
]
considers the correctly normalized version
g^{\prime}_{3}
. The sensitivity of
g^{\prime}_{3}
to RHS-skew remains a structural weakness, hampering its effectiveness
as illustrated by its lower performance in practice on
R_{6}
(Fig
2
).

FI
is the defining measure of
Shannon
and suffers from sensitivity to LHS-uniqueness as illustrated by its behavior on
R_{3}
. Its corrections

SFI

[
15
]
and
RFI

[
13
,
14
]
were aimed at removing bias from
FI
, but our experiments on
SYN
reveal that their distinguishing power is greatly diminished; they especially overcompensate their correction of
FI
w.r.t. LHS-uniqueness. This is reflected by their behavior on
rwd
, where they are among the worst performing measures. Our novel correction
\text{RFI}^{{}^{\prime}+}
of
RFI
is the best performing measure on
rwd
and is insensitive to both LHS-uniqueness and RHS-skew. Its main drawback is the slow computation by current algorithms rendering it essentially useless in practice.

Our recommendation for most suitable AFD measure is therefore the little-known measure
\mu
. It has comparable performance to
\text{RFI}^{{}^{\prime}+}
as well as equal structural sensitivity properties, but can be efficiently computed.

When the number of attributes in the LHS increases, LHS-uniqueness is expected to increase naturally to
1
. Since
g^{\prime}_{3}
,
\text{RFI}^{{}^{\prime}+}
, and
\mu^{+}
are the only measures among the considered ones that are insensitive to LHS-uniqueness, they are the most promising measures for discovering non-linear AFDs.

Another finding worth noting is that we illustrated on
rwd
, perhaps contrary to popular belief, that by only inspecting a small number of top-ranked candidate FDs (according to
g^{\prime}_{3}
,
\text{RFI}^{{}^{\prime}+}
,
\mu
), one already succeeds in finding a large number of true design FDs that were obscured by errors. This means in particular, that a domain expert does not need to wade through hundreds of high-ranked candidate FDs but can restrict attention to a handful.

Inspired by Arocena et al.
[
45
]
,
we study in Appendix
G
the measures’ sensitivity to
different kinds and different levels of errors by passing the relations in
rwd

through a controlled error channel. We again find that
\text{RFI}^{{}^{\prime}+}
and
\mu^{+}

perform best, closely followed by
g^{\prime}_{3}
. We also find that the effectiveness
of all measures quickly deteriorates for increasing error levels making them
essentially useless for error levels above
5\%
.

Appendix A
Discussion on logical vs Shannon entropy

The notion of logical entropy arises in mathematical philosophy
[
20
]
, where it is observed to provide a theory of information based on logic. Importantly, formulas and equalities concerning logical entropy can be converted into corresponding formulas and equalities concerning Shannon entropy by the so-called dit-bit transform (see
[
20
]
). Logical and Shannon entropy are hence highly similar, but measure different things: logical entropy measures the probability of two random tuples to be distinguished, while Shannon entropy measures average uncertainty.

Appendix B
Proof of well-definedness of
\mu

In this section we prove the following result mentioned in Footnote
3
.

Lemma 1
.

If
\expect_{R}[\pdep(\varphi,R)]=1
then
R\models\varphi
.

Proof.

Assume that
\expect_{R}[\pdep(\varphi,R)]=1
. Let
R_{1},\dots,R_{n}
be an enumeration of all permuations of
R
. Then

\displaystyle\expect_{R}[\pdep(\varphi,R)]=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pdep(\varphi,R_{i})}{N}

Hence,
\expect_{R}[\pdep(\varphi,R)]=1
iff
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pdep(\varphi,R_{i})=N
. Because the range of
\pdep
is the interval
[0,1]
this sum can equal
N
if, and only if,
\pdep(\varphi,R_{i})=1
for every
R_{i}
, including
R
itself. Suppose, for the purpose of contradiction, that
R\not\models\varphi
. Then, the value of
\pdep
is given by the formula in Section
IV-D
, i.e.

\displaystyle\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{x}}p_{R}(\bm{x})[1-\pdep(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x},R)

\displaystyle=1-\expect_{\bm{x}}[h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x})]

where the second equality is due to Lemma
3
. Since
\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)=1
, this means in particular that
\expect_{\bm{x}}[h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x})]=0
, which by reasoning similar as above can only happen if
h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x})=0
for every
\bm{x}\in\pi_{\bm{X}}(R)
. This means, that for every
\bm{x}\in\pi_{\bm{X}}(R)
, the probability to draw two distinct
\bm{Y}
-tuples in
\pi_{\bm{Y}}(\sigma_{\bm{X}=\bm{x}}(R))
is zero. But that can only happen if there is only one
\bm{Y}
-value
\pi_{\bm{Y}}(\sigma_{\bm{X}=\bm{x}}(R))
, in which case
R\models\varphi
and we obtain our desired contradiction.

Appendix D
Proofs of equivalence

In this section we prove that the alternate measure formulations shown in
Table
IV
are correct, hence proving
Theorem
. The theorem is proved as a sequence of lemmas. Throughout this section, assume that
R\not\models\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
.

Lemma 2
.

g_{3}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)=\sum_{\bm{x}}p_{R}(\bm{x})\max_{\bm{y}}p_{R}(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x})
.

Proof.

We reason as follows.

\displaystyle g_{3}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)

\displaystyle=\max_{R^{\prime}\in G_{3}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)}\frac{|R^{\prime}|}{|R|}

\displaystyle=\max_{R^{\prime}\in G_{3}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)}\sum_{\bm{w}\in R^{\prime}}p_{R}(\bm{w})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{x}}\max_{\bm{y}}p_{R}(\bm{x}\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{x}}p_{R}(\bm{x})\max_{\bm{y}}p_{R}(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x}).

Here, the first equality is the definition of
g_{3}
. The second equality follows by definition of
p_{R}
. The third equality follows from the following observation: a relation
R^{\prime}\subseteq R
can only be maximal if
R^{\prime}(\bm{w})=R(\bm{w})
whenever
R^{\prime}(\bm{w})>0
for all
\bm{w}\in R
. That is, either we keep all occurrences of
\bm{w}
or we remove all of them. So, maximizing
\sum_{\bm{w}\in R^{\prime}}p_{R}(\bm{w})
corresponds to, for every
\bm{x}
, keeping that
\bm{y}
that maximizes
p_{R}(\bm{x}\bm{y})
. Thereby, effectively removing all other tuples
\bm{x}\bm{y}^{\prime}
with
\bm{y}\neq\bm{y}^{\prime}
. The last equality then follows from the definition of conditional probability.
∎

Lemma 3
.

\pdep(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x},R)=1-h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x})
and therefore

\displaystyle\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{x}}p_{R}(\bm{x})(1-h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid x))

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{\bm{x}}p_{R}(\bm{x})h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x})

\displaystyle=1-\expect_{\bm{x}}[h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x})]

Proof.

We first observe

\displaystyle\pdep(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x},R)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{y}}p_{R}(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x})^{2}

\displaystyle=1-(1-\sum_{\bm{y}}p_{R}(\bm{y}\mid\bm{x})^{2})

\displaystyle=1-h_{R}(Y\mid\bm{x}).

Hence,

\displaystyle\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{x}}p_{R}(\bm{x})\pdep(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x},R)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{x}}p_{R}(\bm{x})(1-h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid x))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{x}}p_{R}(\bm{x})-\sum_{\bm{x}}p_{R}(\bm{x})h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x})

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{\bm{x}}p_{R}(\bm{x})h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x}).\qed

Lemma 4
.

\tau(X\to Y,R)=1-\frac{\expect_{\bm{x}}[h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x})]}{h_{R}(\bm{Y})}

Proof.

We reason as follows.

\displaystyle\tau(X\to Y,R)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)-\pdep(\bm{Y},R)}{1-\pdep(\bm{Y},R)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\expect_{\bm{x}}[h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x})])-(1-h_{R}(\bm{Y}))}{1-(1-h_{R}(\bm{Y}))}

\displaystyle=\frac{h_{R}(\bm{Y})-\expect_{\bm{x}}[h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x})]}{h_{R}(\bm{Y})}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\expect_{\bm{x}}[h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x})]}{h_{R}(\bm{Y})}\qed

Here, the second equality is by Lemma
3
and the fact that
\pdep(\bm{Y},R)=1-h_{R}(\bm{Y})
by definition.

Relating this to logical entropy we observe

Lemma 5
.

\mu(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)=1-\frac{\expect_{\bm{x}}[h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x})]}{h_{R}(\bm{Y})}\frac{|R|-1}{|R|-|\dom(\bm{X},R)|}

Proof.

We reason as follows.

\displaystyle\mu(

\displaystyle\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)

\displaystyle:=\frac{\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)-\expect_{R}[\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)]}{1-\expect_{R}[\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)]}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1-\pdep(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)}{1-\pdep(\bm{Y},R)}\frac{|R|-1}{|R|-|\dom(\bm{X},R)|}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{1-(1-\expect_{\bm{x}}[h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x})])}{1-(1-h_{R}(\bm{Y}))}\frac{|R|-1}{|R|-|\dom(\bm{X},R)|}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\expect_{\bm{x}}[h_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{x})]}{h_{R}(\bm{Y})}\frac{|R|-1}{|R|-|\dom(\bm{X},R)|}.\qed

Lemma 6
.

\text{FI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)=1-\frac{H_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X})}{H_{R}(\bm{Y})}.

Proof.

To show the claimed equality, we reason as follows. Recall that we implicitly
assume throughout the paper that
R
is non-empty. By definition

\text{FI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R):=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }|\dom_{R}(\bm{Y})|=1,\\
\frac{H_{R}(\bm{Y})-H_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X})}{H_{R}(\bm{Y})}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

We now make a case analysis.

•

If
|\dom_{R}(\bm{Y})|=1
then
H_{R}(\bm{Y})=0
. Moreover, if

H_{R}(\bm{Y})=0
, also
H_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X})=0
. As such,

1-\frac{H_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X})}{H_{R}(\bm{X})}=1-\frac{0}{0}=1-0=1=\text{FI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R),

as desired.

•

If
|\dom_{R}(\bm{Y})|>1
then

\displaystyle\text{FI}(\bm{X}\to\bm{Y},R)

\displaystyle=\frac{H_{R}(Y)-H_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X})}{H_{R}(\bm{Y})}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{H_{R}(\bm{Y}\mid\bm{X})}{H(\bm{Y})}\qed

Appendix G
Evaluation on
rwd
with extra errors

To study the measures’ sensitivity to different kinds and different levels of
errors on real world data, we created the benchmark
\textsc{rwd}^{\text{e}}
. We obtain

\textsc{rwd}^{\text{e}}
by passing the relations
R\in\textsc{rwd}
through a controlled error channel such that, denoting by
R^{\prime}
the obtained relation, some FDs in

\perfectFD(R)
do not hold anymore in
R^{\prime}
and hence become part of

\designAFD(R^{\prime})
. Existing AFDs are always maintained, i.e.,

\designAFD(R)\subseteq\designAFD(R^{\prime})
.

We actually have multiple error channels, which are parameterized by an error
level
\eta\in[0,1]
and an error type. When passing
R
through the channel we
consider all
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}\in\perfectFD(R)
and modify
k=\lfloor\eta|R|\rfloor

\bm{Y}
-values. To avoid interference, we select at most one FD
\bm{X}\to\bm{Y}
for
every unique
\bm{Y}
per relation, ensuring that
\bm{Y}
does not appear in
\designAFD(R)
, and that no FD
\bm{Y}\to\bm{Z}
has previously been selected.
The procedure to modify the
\bm{Y}
values is determined by the chosen type of data error for which we consider three categories inspired by Arocena et al.
[
45
]
:
copy
error,
typo
and
bogus
value.
For a chosen tuple
\bm{w}\in R
, only
{\bm{w}}|_{\bm{Y}}
is changed, where the change depends on the data error type:

(i)

copy
: Randomly pick any
\tilde{\bm{w}}\in R
with
\tilde{\bm{w}}|_{\bm{Y}}\neq\bm{w}|_{\bm{Y}}
and
make
\tilde{\bm{w}}|_{\bm{Y}}
the new value for
\bm{w}|_{\bm{Y}}
.

(ii)

typo
: To every
\bm{y}\in\dom_{R}(\bm{Y})
, we associate three new values representing three common typos. From these, one is chosen each time at random as the new value for
{\bm{w}}|_{\bm{Y}}
.

(iii)

bogus
:
{\bm{w}}|_{\bm{y}}
is assigned a unique newly generated value.

We point out that
copy
does not introduce any new values and keeps
\dom_{R}(\bm{Y})
stable, while
typo
(resp.,
bogus
) introduces a number of new values independent of (resp., dependent on) the error level.
\bm{X}
is not modified, and therefore
p_{R^{\prime}}(\bm{X})=p_{R}(\bm{X})
.
To ensure that increasing error levels do not accidentally reduce errors, we
ensure that, for each
\bm{x}\colon\bm{X}
we pick at most
\lfloor N_{\bm{x}}/2\rfloor

tuples
\bm{w}
with
\bm{w}|_{\bm{X}}=\bm{x}
to modify, where
N_{\bm{X}}
is the number of times
that
\bm{x}
occurs in
\pi_{\bm{X}}(R)
.
\perfectFD
s for which this cannot be
guaranteed are omitted. The number of new AFDs that can be constructed
therefore depends on the error level.

We consider four error levels: 1%, 2%, 5% and 10%. For each type of data error
t
and each error level
\eta
, we obtain a new benchmark
\textsc{rwd}^{\text{e}}[t,\eta]
. Consequently, we generate 12
\textsc{rwd}^{\text{e}}
tables per
rwd
table
R
for which
|\perfectFD(R)|>0
(so, tables
R_{8}
and
R_{9}
are excluded). Overall the number of AFDs increases from
17 in
rwd
to 39 in

\textsc{rwd}^{\text{e}}[\textsf{copy},1\%]
. That number is the same for the other error types but can drop a little for higher noise levels as explained above. Similar to
\textsc{rwd}^{-}
, we retain only AFD candidates where each measure calculates a value in a reasonable amount of time.
A complete overview of the number of additional AFDs per relation and per error level is given in
[
44
]
.
Per combination of parameters, the ground truth then consists of the thus constructed AFDs together with the AFDs from
R
.
