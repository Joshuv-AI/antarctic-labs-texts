Characterizing Lipschitz images of injective metric spaces
Taras Banakh, Judyta Bąk, Joanna Garbulińska-Węgrzyn, Magdalena Nowak, Michał Popławski

Abstract
. A metric space X X is injective if every non-expanding map f : B → X f:B\to X defined on a subspace B B of a metric space A A can be extended to a non-expanding map f ¯ : A → X \bar{f}:A\to X . We prove that a metric space X X is a Lipschitz image of an injective metric space if and only if X X is Lipschitz connected in the sense that for every points x , y ∈ X x,y\in X , there exists a Lipschitz map f : [ 0 , 1 ] → X f:[0,1]\to X such that f ⁡ ( 0 ) = x f(0)=x and f ⁡ ( 1 ) = y f(1)=y . In this case the metric space X X carries a well-defined intrinsic metric. A metric space X X is a Lipschitz image of a compact injective metric space if and only if X X is compact, Lipschitz connected and its intrinsic metric is totally bounded. A metric space X X is a Lipschitz image of a separable injective metric space if and only if X X is a Lipschitz image of the Urysohn universal metric space if and only if X X is analytic, Lipschitz connected and its intrinsic metric is separable.

1.
Introduction

By the classical Hahn–Mazurkiewicz Theorem
[
11
,
16
]
, a Hausdorff space
X
is a continuous image of the unit segment
[0,1]
if and only if
X
is a Peano continuum, i.e.,
X
is compact, metrizable, connected and locally connected.
A metric counterpart of the Hahn–Mazurkiewicz Theorem is due to Ważewski
[
19
]
and Eilenberg, Harrold
[
7
]
who proved that a metric space
X
is the image of the closed interval
[0,1]
under a Lipschitz map if and only if
X
has finite length. The unit interval and the real line are the most important examples of injective metric spaces. So, it is natural to ask which metric spaces are Lipschitz images of injective metric spaces.
In this paper we address this problem and characterize metric spaces which are Lipschitz images of (separable or totally bounded) injective metric spaces.

For a metric space
X
, its metric will be denoted by
d_{X}
. For a map
f:X\to Y
, its
Lipschitz constant
is defined as a (finite or infinite) number

\operatorname{Lip}(f)\coloneqq\sup\limits_{{x,x^{\prime}\in X}\atop{x\neq x^{\prime}}}\frac{d_{Y}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))}{d_{X}(x,x^{\prime})}\in[0,\infty].

In this definition we assume that
\sup\varnothing=0
.

A map
f:X\to Y
between metric spaces
X,Y
is called

•

Lipschitz
if
\operatorname{Lip}(f)<\infty
;

•

L
-Lipschitz
for a real constant
L
, if
\operatorname{Lip}(f)\leq L
;

•

non-expanding
if it is 1-Lipschitz.

A metric space
X
is
Lipschitz connected
if for every points
x,y\in X
there exists a Lipschitz map
f:[0,1]\to X
such that
f(0)=x
and
f(1)=y
. Such a map
f
is called a
Lipschitz path
connecting the points
x,y
.
It is clear that every Lipschitz connected metric space is a path connected topological space. Every Lipschitz connected metric space
X
carries the
intrinsic metric

d_{IX}:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

d_{IX}(x,y)\coloneqq\inf\{\operatorname{Lip}(f):f\in X^{[0,1]}\;\wedge\;f(0)=x\;\wedge\;f(1)=y\}.

for any points
x,y\in X
. In the above formula,
X^{[0,1]}
stands for the set of all functions from
[0,1]
to
X
.

A metric space
X
is called
injective
if every
1
-Lipschitz map
f:B\to X
defined on a subspace
B
of a metric space
A
extends to a
1
-Lipschitz map
\bar{f}:A\to X
.

Injective metric spaces form a well-known and well-studied class of complete metric spaces,
[
4
]
,
[
10
, p. 47]
.
Such spaces are known under two alternative names: hyperconvex metric spaces
[
1
,
9
,
14
]
and
1
-Lipschitz Absolute Retracts
[
5
, Ch.I.1.]
.
The following (known) characterization of injective metric spaces can be deduced from
[
1
,
5
]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For a metric space
X
, the following conditions are equivalent:

(1)

X
is an injective metric space;

(2)

X
is
1
-Lipschitz Absolute Retract, which means that for every metric space
Y
containing
X
, there exists a
1
-Lipschitz map
f:Y\to X
such that
f(x)=x
for all
x\in X
;

(3)

X
is hyperconvex in the sense that for any indexed family of closed balls
\big(B[x_{i},r_{i}]\big)_{i\in I}
in
X
with
d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})\leq r_{i}+r_{j}
for all
i,j\in I
, the intersection
\bigcap_{i\in I}B[x_{i},r_{i}]
is not empty.

(4)

X
is metrically convex (for every
a,b\in X
and
t\in[0,1]
there exists
x\in X
such that
d_{X}(a,x)=t\cdot d_{X}(a,b)
and
d_{X}(x,b)=(1-t)\cdot d_{X}(a,b)
) and has a binary intersection property (every collection of mutually intersecting closed balls in
X
has a common point).

(5)

for every metric space
Y
containing
X
and for every metric space
Z
, every Lipschitz map
X\to Z
has an extension
Y\to Z
with the same Lipschitz constant.

Proof.

The implications
(5)\Leftrightarrow(1)\Leftrightarrow(2)
are proved in
[
5
, Proposition 1.2]
;
(3)\Rightarrow(2)
in
[
1
, Theorem 8]
, and
(2)\Leftrightarrow(4)
in
[
5
, Proposition 1.4]
. To see that
(4)\Rightarrow(3)
, take any indexed family
\big(B[x_{i},r_{i}]\big)_{i\in I}
of closed balls in
X
such that
d_{X}(x_{i},x_{j})\leq r_{i}+r_{j}
for every
i,j\in I
. The metric convexity of
X
ensures that for any distinct indices
i,j\in I
, the closed balls
B[x_{i},r_{i}]
and
B[x_{j},r_{j}]
have a common point. The binary intersection property guarantees that
\bigcap_{i\in I}B[x_{i},r_{i}]\neq\emptyset
.
∎

Another important source of injective metric spaces are complete
\mathbb{R}
-trees, see
[
6
,
14
]
.
A metric space
X
is called an
\mathbb{R}
-tree
if for every points
x,y\in X
the
metric segment

[x,y]\coloneqq\{z\in X:d_{X}(x,y)=d_{X}(x,z)+d_{X}(z,y)\}

is isometric to the interval
[0,d_{X}(x,y)]
on the real line, and for every points
x,y,z\in X
with
[x,y]\cap[y,z]=\{y\}
we have
d_{X}(x,z)=d_{X}(x,y)+d_{X}(y,z)
. By
[
14
]
, every complete
\mathbb{R}
-tree is an injective metric space. Nevertheless, classes of
\mathbb{R}
-trees and injective metric spaces are distinct. For instance
(0,1)
(with the euclidean metric) is
\mathbb{R}
-tree, which is not injective, since injective metric spaces are complete. On the other hand,
[0,1]^{2}
with the maximum metric is an injective metric space, which is not an
\mathbb{R}
-tree (the metric segment
[(0,0),(1,0)]
is not isometric to
[0,1]
).

A metric space
Y
is called a
Lipschitz image
(resp.
1
-Lipschitz image
) of a metric space
X
if there exists a surjective Lipschitz map
f:X\to Y
(with
\operatorname{Lip}(f)\leq 1
).

One can easily reformulate Hahn-Mazurkiewicz theorem
[
11
,
16
]
to the following version
\colon
a Hausdorff space
X
is a continuous image of the unit segment
[0,1]
if and only if
X
is a continuous image of some compact injective metric space
Y
. However, the Ważewski result in
[
19
]
implies that the class of Lipschitz images of the unit interval
[0,1]
is strictly smaller than the class of Lipschitz images of compact injective metric spaces: a counterexample is the injective metric space
[0,1]^{2}
which is not the Lipschitz image of the unit segment
[0,1]
.

The following three characterizations proved in Sections
5
,
6
,
7
are the main results of this paper.

Theorem 1.2
.

For a metric space
X
, the following conditions are equivalent:

1)

X
is a Lipschitz image of an injective metric space;

2)

X
is a Lipschitz image of a complete
\mathbb{R}
-tree;

3)

X
is Lipschitz connected.

Theorem 1.3
.

For a metric space
X
, the following conditions are equivalent:

1)

X
is a Lipschitz image of a totally bounded injective metric space;

2)

X
is a Lipschitz image of a compact
\mathbb{R}
-tree;

3)

X
is compact, Lipschitz connected, and its intrinsic metric is totally bounded.

Theorem 1.4
.

For a metric space
X
, the following conditions are equivalent:

1)

X
is a Lipschitz image of a separable injective metric space;

2)

X
is a Lipschitz image of a separable complete
\mathbb{R}
-tree;

3)

X
is a
1
-Lipschitz image of the Urysohn universal space;

4)

X
is analytic, Lipschitz connected, and its intrinsic metric is separable.

Let us recall that a topological space
X
is
analytic
if it is a continuous image of a separable complete metric space.
The
Urysohn universal metric space
is a complete separable metric space
\mathbb{U}
such that any isometric embedding of a subset
A
of a finite metric space
B
admits an extension to an isometric embedding of
B
in
\mathbb{U}
. It is well-known
[
18
]
that a Urysohn universal metric space exists and is unique up to an isometry.

2.
\mathbb{R}
-convex metric spaces and
\mathbb{R}
-trees

Definition 2.1
.

A metric space
X
is called
\mathbb{R}
-convex
if for every points
x,z\in X
, the metric segment

[x,z]\coloneqq\{y\in X:d_{X}(x,y)+d_{X}(y,z)=d_{X}(x,z)\}

is isometric to the segment
[0,d_{X}(x,z)]
of the real line.

Proposition 2.2
.

For a metric space
X
, the following conditions are equivalent:

1)

X
is
\mathbb{R}
-convex;

2)

for every points
x,y\in X
, there exists a unique set
I\subseteq X
such that
x,y\in I
and
I
is isometric to the segment
[0,d_{X}(x,y)]
on the real line;

3)

for every points
x,y\in X
there exists a unique
1
-Lipschitz map
f:[0,d_{X}(x,y)]\to X
such that
f(0)=x
and
f(d_{X}(x,y))=y
.

Proof.

The implications
(1)\Rightarrow(2)\Rightarrow(3)
are obvious. To prove that
(3)\Rightarrow(1)
, assume that a metric space
X
satisfies the condition (3). Given any points
x,y\in X
, we need to show that the metric segment
[x,y]
is isometric to the interval
[0,d_{X}(x,y)]
in the real line. By the condition (3), there exists a unique
1
-Lipschitz map
f:[0,d_{X}(x,y)]\to X
such that
f(0)=x
and
f(d_{X}(x,y))=y
. The triangle inequality implies that the image
I\coloneqq f\big[[0,d_{X}(x,y)]\big]
is a subset of the metric segment
[x,y]
. Assuming that
[x,y]\neq I
, we can find a point
z\in[x,y]\setminus I
. Then
d_{X}(x,y)=d_{X}(x,z)+d_{X}(z,y)
. By condition (3), there exist
1
-Lipschitz maps
\alpha:[0,d_{X}(x,z)]\to X
and
\beta:[0,d_{X}(z,y)]\to X
such that
\alpha(0)=x
,
\alpha(d_{X}(x,z))=z=\beta(0)
and
\beta(d_{X}(z,y))=y
. The
1
-Lipschitz maps
\alpha,\beta
compose a
1
-Lipschitz map
\gamma:[0,d_{X}(x,y)]\to X
defined by

\gamma(t)=\begin{cases}\alpha(t)&\mbox{if $t\in[0,d_{X}(x,z)]$};\\
\beta(t-d_{X}(x,z))&\mbox{if $t\in[d_{X}(x,z),d_{X}(x,y)]$}.\end{cases}

Since
\gamma(0)=x=f(0)
,
\gamma(d_{X}(x,y))=y=f(d_{X}(x,y))
, and
\gamma\neq f
, we obtain a contradiction with the uniqueness of the
1
-Lipschitz path
f
. This contradiction shows that
[x,y]=I
. The triangle inequality implies that the
1
-Lipschitz map
f:[0,d_{X}(x,y)]\to[x,y]
is an isometry, witnessing that the metric space
X
is
\mathbb{R}
-convex. Indeed, for every
u,z
such that
0\leq u\leq z\leq d_{X}(x,y)
we have
|u-z|\geq d_{X}(f(u),f(z))\geq d_{X}(x,y)-d_{X}(x,f(u))-d_{X}(f(z),y)\geq d_{X}(x,y)-|0-u|-|z-d_{X}(x,y)|=|u-z|.

∎

Proposition 2.3
.

If a metric space
X
is
\mathbb{R}
-convex, then for every points
x,y,z\in X
, the intersection
[x,y]\cap[x,z]
is equal to the metric segment
[x,u]
for a unique point
u\in[x,y]\cap[x,z]
.

Proof.

Since the metric space
X
is
\mathbb{R}
-convex, there exist isometries
\alpha:[0,d_{X}(x,y)]\to[x,y]
and
\beta:[0,d_{X}(x,z)]\to[x,z]
such that
\alpha(0)=x=\beta(0)
,
\alpha(d_{X}(x,y))=y
, and
\beta(d_{X}(x,z))=z
. By the compactness of
[x,z]
, the closed set
\alpha^{-1}[x,z]
in
[0,d_{X}(x,y)]
has the largest element
s
. Since
\alpha
is an isometry,
s=d_{X}(\alpha(0),\alpha(s))=d_{X}(x,\alpha(s))
. Let
u=\alpha(s)
and
t=\beta^{-1}(u)
. Since
\beta
is an isometry,
t=d_{X}(\beta(0),\beta(t))=d_{X}(x,u)=s
. Observe that
\alpha^{\prime}\coloneqq\alpha{\restriction}_{[0,s]}:[0,s]\to X
and
\beta^{\prime}\coloneqq\beta{\restriction}_{[0,t]}:[0,t]\to X
are two
1
-Lipschitz maps from the interval
[0,s]=[0,t]
to
X
such that such that
\alpha^{\prime}(0)=x=\beta^{\prime}(0)
and
\alpha^{\prime}(s)=u=\beta^{\prime}(t)
. Proposition
2.2
ensures that
\alpha^{\prime}=\beta^{\prime}
and hence
[x,y]\cap[x,z]=\alpha[[0,s]]=[x,u]
. The uniqueness of the point
u
implies from the uniqueness of the end-points of the segment
[x,u]=[x,y]\cap[x,z]
.
∎

We observe that a metric space
X
is
\mathbb{R}
-tree if
X
is an
\mathbb{R}
-convex metric space such that for every points
x,y,z\in X
with
[x,y]\cap[y,z]=\{y\}
, we have
d_{X}(x,z)=d_{X}(x,y)+d_{X}(y,z)
.

The following important fact can be found in
[
14
]
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Every complete
\mathbb{R}
-tree is an injective metric space.

3.
Lipschitz connected metric spaces

In this section we study Lipschitz connected and
L
-Lipschitz connected metric spaces in more details.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
L
be a real number. A metric space
X
is called
L
-Lipschitz connected
if for every points
x,y\in X
, there exists an
L
-Lipschitz path
f:[0,d_{X}(x,y)]\to X
such that
f(0)=x
and
f(d_{X}(x,y))=y
.

If an
L
-Lipschitz connected metric space contains more than one point, then
L\geq 1
. Therefore, the
1
-Lipschitz connectedness is the strongest notion among the
L
-Lipschitz connectedness properties.

Example 3.2
.

Every injective metric space
X
is
1
-Lipschitz connected.

Proof.

Given any point
x,y\in X
, consider the doubleton
\{0,d_{X}(x,y)\}
in the real line and the
1
-Lipschitz map
f:\{0,d_{X}(x,y)\}\to X
defined by
f(0)=x
and
f(d_{X}(x,y))=y
. By the injectivity of
X
, this
1
-Lipschitz map admits a
1
-Lipschitz extension
\bar{f}:[0,d_{X}(x,y)]\to X
, which is a
1
-Lipschitz path witnessing that
X
is
1
-Lipschitz connected.
∎

As we have mentioned in the introduction, every Lipschitz connected metric space
X
carries the intrinsic metric

d_{IX}:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle d_{IX}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\inf\{\operatorname{Lip}(f):f\in X^{[0,1]}\;\wedge\;f(0)=x\;\wedge\;f(1)=y\}

\displaystyle=\inf\{C\in[0,\infty):\exists f\in X^{[0,C]}\;(f(0)=x\;\wedge\;f(C)=y\;\wedge\;\operatorname{Lip}(f)\leq 1)\}

for any points
x,y\in X
. The second equality in the definition of the intrinsic metric holds because a function
f\in X^{[0,1]}
is
L
-Lipschitz if and only if the function
f_{L}:[0,L]\to X
,
f_{L}:t\mapsto f(t/L)
, is
1
-Lipschitz.

The definition of the intrinsic metric implies that
d_{X}\leq d_{IX}
for every Lipschitz connected metric space
X
.
Also any
1
-Lipschitz map
f:X\to Y
between Lipschitz connected metric spaces remains
1
-Lipschitz with respect to the intrinsic metrics on
X
and
Y
, which means that

d_{IY}(f(x),f(x^{\prime}))\leq d_{IX}(x,x^{\prime})

for every points
x,x^{\prime}\in X
.

The following lemma describes an interplay between the completions of a Lipschitz connected metric space with respect to the intrinsic metric and the original metric.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
X
be a Lipschitz connected metric space,
\overline{X}
be the completion of
X
,
\overline{X}_{I}
be the completion of the metric space
X_{I}\coloneqq(X,d_{IX})
, and
\overline{\operatorname{id}}:\overline{X}_{I}\to\overline{X}
be the unique continuous extension of the identity map
\operatorname{id}:X_{I}\to X
. Then
\overline{\operatorname{id}}^{-1}[X]=X_{I}
. In other words, every Cauchy sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
(X,d_{IX})
, which is convergent to a point
x
in
(X,d_{X})
is convergent to
x
in
(X,d_{IX}),
too.

Proof.

Given any point
x\in\overline{X}_{I}
with
\overline{\operatorname{id}}(x)\in X
, we need to prove that
x\in X_{I}
. Since
x\in\overline{X}_{I}
, there exists a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
X_{I}
such that
d_{IX}(x,x_{n})<\frac{1}{2^{n+2}}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

d_{IX}(x_{n},x_{n+1})\leq d_{IX}(x_{n},x)+d_{IX}(x,x_{n+1})<\tfrac{1}{2^{n+2}}+\tfrac{1}{2^{n+3}}<\tfrac{1}{2^{n+1}}.

So, we can find a 1-Lipschitz path
\gamma_{n}:[\tfrac{1}{2^{n+1}},\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}]\to X
such that
\gamma_{n}(\tfrac{1}{2^{n+1}})=x_{n+1}
and
\gamma_{n}(\tfrac{1}{2^{n}})=x_{n}
. The paths
\gamma_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, compose a 1-Lipschitz path
\gamma:[0,1]\to X
defined by
\gamma(0)=\overline{\operatorname{id}}(x)
and
\gamma{\restriction}_{[\tfrac{1}{2^{n+1}},\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}]}=\gamma_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. The definition of
\gamma
guarantees that

\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{IX}(x,x_{n})=0=\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{IX}(x_{n},\operatorname{id}^{-1}(\overline{\operatorname{id}}(x)))

and hence
x=\operatorname{id}^{-1}(\overline{\operatorname{id}}(x))\in\operatorname{id}^{-1}[X]=X_{I}
.
∎

A metric
d
on a set
X
will be called
compact
(resp.
complete
,
separable
,
analytic
) if so is the metric space
(X,d)
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(X,d_{X})
be a Lipschitz connected metric space.

(1)

If the metric
d_{X}
is complete, then so is the intrinsic metric
d_{IX}
.

(2)

The metric
d_{IX}
is analytic if and only if
d_{IX}
is separable and the metric
d_{X}
is analytic.

(3)

If the metric
d_{IX}
is separable (resp. totally bounded, compact), then so is the metric
d_{X}
.

Proof.

(1) Suppose that the metric
d_{X}
is complete and take a Cauchy sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in the metric space
(X,d_{IX}).
Since
d_{X}\leq d_{IX}
, the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
remains Cauchy in the metric space
(X,d_{X})
. Since
(X,d_{X})
is complete, this sequence converges to some point
x\in X
in the metric space
(X,d_{X})
. Lemma
3.3
guarantees that
\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}=x
in
(X,d_{IX})
too, witnessing that the intrinsic metric
d_{IX}
is complete.

(2) If the metric
d_{IX}
is analytic, then
d_{IX}
is separable and
d_{X}
is analytic, because of the continuity of the identity map
(X,d_{IX})\to(X,d_{X})
.

Next, assume that the metric
d_{IX}
is separable and
d_{X}
is analytic. Let
\overline{X}
be the completion of the metric space
(X,d_{X})
and
\overline{X}_{I}
be the completion of the metric space
X_{I}\coloneqq(X,d_{IX})
. Let
\bar{I}:\overline{X}_{I}\to\overline{X}
be the continuous extension of the (1-Lipschitz) identity map
I:X_{I}\to X
. By Lemma
3.3
,
X_{I}=\bar{I}^{-1}[X]
. Since the spaces
\overline{X}_{I}
and
\overline{X}
are Polish, the preimage
X_{I}=\bar{I}^{-1}[X]
is an analytic subspace of
\overline{X}_{I}
, by
[
13
, 14.4]
.

The statement (3) follows from the
1
-Lipschitz property of the identity map
(X,d_{IX})\to(X,d_{IX})
.
∎

The following simple example shows that the implications of Lemma
3.4
cannot be reversed in a general case.

Example 3.5
.

Let
L
be any line in the complex plane
\mathbb{C}
and
o\in\mathbb{C}\setminus L
be any point. For every uncountable compact zero-dimensional set
K\subseteq L
, the cone

X:=\bigcup_{x\in K}[o,x]

over
K
with vertex
o
is a Lipschitz connected compact (by
[
8
, 3.2.11]
) metric space with respect to the metric
d_{X}
inherited from the complex plane. Thus
(X,d_{X})
is also separable and totally bounded. Since
d_{IX}(x,y)\geq 2\cdot\operatorname{dist}(o,L)
for any distinct points
x,y\in K
, the metric space
(X,d_{IX})
is not separable (and hence not totally bounded and not compact).

If the compact space
K
has no isolated points, then for every countable dense set
D\subseteq K
, the Lipschitz connected metric subspace
Y=\bigcup_{x\in D}[o,x]
of
X
is not complete (by the Baire Theorem) whereas the intrinsic metric
d_{IY}
is
\sigma
-compact and complete.

Example 3.6
.

The metric subspace
X=\{(x,\sin(\frac{1}{x}))\colon x>0\}
of the Euclidean plane is Lipschitz connected and not complete whereas the intrinsic metric on
X
is complete.

4.
The
\mathbb{R}
-trees generated by weighted trees

In this section we describe a general construction of a (complete)
\mathbb{R}
-tree, determined by a weighted tree.

Definition 4.1
.

A
forest
is a partially ordered set
(T,\preceq)
such that for every
t\in T
the set

{\downarrow}t\coloneqq\{x\in T:x\preceq t\}

is finite and linearly ordered.

For any elements
s,t\in T
, the largest element of the linearly ordered set
{\downarrow}s\cap{\downarrow}t
is called the
infimum
of
s
and
t
and is denoted by
s\wedge t
.

For an element
t
of a forest
T
, the set
\mathrm{succ}(t)\coloneqq\min\{s\in T:t\prec s\}
is called the set of
immediate successors
of
t
in the forest.

A subset
B
of a forest
T
is called a
branch
of
T
if
B
is a maximal linearly ordered subset of
T
. The
boundary

\partial T
of a forest
T
is the set of all infinite branches in
T
.

For a forest
T
by
\min T
denote the set of minimal elements of
T
. For a nonempty forest
T
, the set
\min T
is not empty. A forest
T
is a
tree
if the set
\min T
is a singleton, consisting of the smallest element of
T
, called the
root
of the tree
T
and denoted by
r_{T}
.

Observe that for every element
r
of a forest
T
, the set

{\uparrow}r\coloneqq\{x\in T:r\preceq x\}

is a tree with root
r
.

Definition 4.2
.

A
weighted tree
is a pair
(T,w)
consisting of a tree
T
and a function
w:T\to[0,+\infty)
assigning to every vertex
t\in T
its
weight

w_{t}=w(t)
so that
w_{t}=0
if and only if
t
is the root of
T
.

Every weighted tree
(T,w)
determines an
\mathbb{R}
-tree

T_{w}=(\{r_{T}\}\times\{0\})\cup\bigcup_{t\in T}\{t\}\times(0,w_{t}],

endowed with the metric

d_{w}((t,t^{\prime}),(s,s^{\prime})):=\begin{cases}\;|t^{\prime}-s^{\prime}|&\mbox{if $s=t$};\\
t^{\prime}-w_{t}+s^{\prime}-w_{s}+\sum_{x\in{\downarrow}s\triangle{\downarrow}t}w_{x}&\mbox{otherwise},\\
\end{cases}

where
A\triangle B
stands for the symmetric difference
(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)
of the sets
A,B
. It is easy to check that the metric space
(T_{w},d_{w})
is indeed an
\mathbb{R}
-tree. The
\mathbb{R}
-tree
T_{w}
will be called the
\mathbb{R}
-tree of the weighted tree
(T,w)
.

The
\mathbb{R}
-tree
T_{w}\subset T\times\mathbb{R}
carries a natural lexicographic order

(s,s^{\prime})\leq_{L}(t,t^{\prime})\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad s\prec t\;\vee\;(s=t\;\wedge\;s^{\prime}\leq t^{\prime}).

So, for every element
t^{\prime\prime}\in T_{w}
we can consider its lower and upper sets

{\downarrow}t^{\prime\prime}\coloneqq\{s^{\prime\prime}\in T_{w}:s^{\prime\prime}\leq_{L}t^{\prime\prime}\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\uparrow}t^{\prime\prime}\coloneqq\{s^{\prime\prime}\in T_{w}:t^{\prime\prime}\leq_{L}s^{\prime\prime}\}

in the partially ordered set
(T_{w},\leq_{L})
. Since
T
is a tree, the lower set
{\downarrow}t^{\prime\prime}
of every element
t^{\prime\prime}\in T_{w}
is linearly ordered. For two elements
a^{\prime\prime},b^{\prime\prime}\in T_{w}
, let
a^{\prime\prime}\wedge b^{\prime\prime}
denote the largest element of the linearly ordered set
{\downarrow}a^{\prime\prime}\cap{\downarrow}b^{\prime\prime}
in the partially ordered set
(T_{w},\leq_{L})
.

Now we shall characterize weighted trees whose
\mathbb{R}
-trees are totally bounded or separable.

Definition 4.3
.

A weighted tree
(T,w)
is defined to be
precompact
if
for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a finite set
F\subseteq T
such that for every finite linearly ordered set
L\subseteq T\setminus F
we have
\sum_{x\in L}w_{x}<\varepsilon
.

Proposition 4.4
.

A weighted tree
(T,w)
is precompact if and only if the metric space
T_{w}
is totally bounded.

Proof.

To prove the “if” part, assume that the metric space
T_{w}
is totally bounded. Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a finite set
F^{\prime\prime}\subseteq T_{w}
such that for every
x^{\prime\prime}\in T_{w}
, there exists
y^{\prime\prime}\in F^{\prime\prime}
with
d_{w}(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})<\varepsilon
. Since
F^{\prime\prime}
is finite, there exists a finite set
F\subseteq T
such that
F^{\prime\prime}\subseteq\bigcup\{{\downarrow}(t,w_{t}):t\in F\}\subseteq T_{w}
. Replacing
F
by the finite set
{\downarrow}F\coloneqq\bigcup_{t\in F}{\downarrow}t
, we can assume that
F={\downarrow}F
. We claim that
\sum_{x\in L}w_{x}<\varepsilon
for every finite linearly ordered set
L\subseteq T\setminus F
. Since
L
is finite and linearly ordered, there exists an element
u\in L
such that
L\subseteq{\downarrow}u
. By the choice of the set
F^{\prime\prime}
, there exists a point
y^{\prime\prime}\in F^{\prime\prime}
such that
d_{w}((u,w_{u}),y^{\prime\prime})<\varepsilon
. Write the element
y^{\prime\prime}
as
(y,y^{\prime})
for some
y\in T
and
y^{\prime}\in[0,w_{y}]
. The choice of the set
F
ensures that
y\in F
and hence
L\subseteq{\downarrow}u\setminus F\subseteq{\downarrow}u\setminus{\downarrow}y
. Then

\sum_{x\in L}w_{x}\leq\sum_{x\in{\downarrow}u\setminus{\downarrow}y}w_{x}\leq d_{w}((u,w_{u}),y^{\prime\prime})<\varepsilon,

witnessing that the weighted tree
(T,w)
is precompact.

To prove the “only if” part, assume that the weighted tree
(T,w)
is precompact. To prove that the metric space
T_{w}
is totally bounded, fix any
\varepsilon>0
. By the precompactness of the weighted tree
(T,w)
, there exists a nonempty finite set
F\subseteq T
such that
\sum_{x\in L}w_{x}<\varepsilon
for every finite linearly ordered set
L\subseteq T\setminus F
. Replacing
F
with the finite set
{\downarrow}F\coloneqq\bigcup_{t\in F}{\downarrow}t
, we can assume that
F={\downarrow}F
. Since the set
F
is finite, the set

K\coloneqq{\textstyle\bigcup}\{{\downarrow}(t,w_{t}):t\in F\}=(\{r_{T}\}\times\{0\})\cup\bigcup_{t\in F}(\{t\}\times(0,w_{t}])

is compact and hence totally bounded in the metric space
T_{w}
. Then there exists a finite set
F^{\prime\prime}\subseteq K
such that for every
x^{\prime\prime}\in K
there exists
y^{\prime\prime}\in F^{\prime\prime}
with
d_{w}(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})<\varepsilon
. Replacing
F^{\prime\prime}
by the set
F^{\prime\prime}\cup\{(t,w_{t}):t\in F\}
, we can assume that
\{(t,w_{t}):t\in F\}\subseteq F^{\prime\prime}
. We claim that for every
x^{\prime\prime}\in T_{w}
, there exists
y^{\prime\prime}\in F^{\prime\prime}
such that
d_{w}(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})<\varepsilon
.

Indeed, if
x^{\prime\prime}\in K
, then the existence of a point
y^{\prime\prime}\in F^{\prime\prime}
with
d_{w}(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})<\varepsilon
follows from the choice of the set
F^{\prime\prime}
. So, we assume that
x^{\prime\prime}\notin K
.
In this case,
x^{\prime\prime}=(x,x^{\prime})
for some
x\in T\setminus F
and
x^{\prime}\in(0,w_{x}]
. Since
r_{T}\in{\downarrow}F=F
, there exists a point
y\in F\cap{\downarrow}x
such that
F\cap{\downarrow}x={\downarrow}y
. The choice of the set
F^{\prime\prime}
ensures that it contains the point
y^{\prime\prime}\coloneqq(y,w_{y})
. The choice of
F
guarantees that
\sum_{t\in{\downarrow}x\setminus{\downarrow}y}w_{t}=\sum_{t\in{\downarrow}x\setminus F}w_{t}<\varepsilon
and hence

d_{w}(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})=x^{\prime}-w_{x}+\sum_{t\in{\downarrow}x\setminus{\downarrow}y}w_{t}\leq\sum_{t\in{\downarrow}x\setminus{\downarrow}y}w_{t}<\varepsilon,
by the definition of the metric
d_{w}
. This shows that the metric space
T_{w}
is totally bounded.
∎

Proposition 4.5
.

For a weighted tree
(T,w)
, the
\mathbb{R}
-tree
T_{w}
is separable if and only if the tree
T
is countable.

Proof.

If the tree
T
is countable, then
T_{w}\cap(T\times\mathbb{Q})
is a countable dense set in the
\mathbb{R}
-tree, witnessing that the metric space
T_{w}
is separable.

Next, assume that the tree
T
is uncountable. The definition of metric
d_{w}
ensures that for every node
t\in T
, the set
\{t\}\times(0,w_{t})
is open in the metric space
T_{w}
. Therefore, the metric space
T_{w}
contains the uncountable family of pairwise disjoint open sets
\big\{\{t\}\times(0,w_{t}):t\in T\big\}
and hence
T_{w}
cannot be separable, according to
[
8
, Corollary 4.1.16]
.
∎

Let
(T,w)
be a weighted tree. In the set
\partial T
of all infinite branches of
T
, consider the subset

\partial T_{w}:=\{L\in\partial T:|L|=\infty,\;\sum_{t\in L}w_{t}<\infty\}.

On the set
\overline{T}_{w}:=T_{w}\cup\partial T_{w}
we define a metric
\bar{d}_{w}
,
that extends the metric
d_{w}
on
T_{w}
as follows:

\bar{d}_{w}(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime}):=\begin{cases}\;|x^{\prime}-y^{\prime}|&\mbox{if $x^{\prime\prime}=(x,x^{\prime}),y^{\prime\prime}=(y,y^{\prime})\in T_{w}$ and $x=y$};\\
x^{\prime}-w_{x}+y^{\prime}-w_{y}+\sum_{t\in{\downarrow}x\triangle{\downarrow}y}w_{t}&\mbox{if $x^{\prime\prime}=(x,x^{\prime}),y^{\prime\prime}=(y,y^{\prime})\in T_{w}$ and $x\neq y$};\\
x^{\prime}-w_{x}+\sum_{t\in{\downarrow}x\triangle y^{\prime\prime}}w_{t}&\mbox{if $x^{\prime\prime}=(x,x^{\prime})\in T_{w}$, $y^{\prime\prime}\in\partial T_{w}$ and $x\notin y^{\prime\prime}$ };\\
{w_{x}}-x^{\prime}+\sum_{t\in{y^{\prime\prime}\setminus{\downarrow}x}}w_{t}&\mbox{if $x^{\prime\prime}=(x,x^{\prime})\in T_{w}$, $y^{\prime\prime}\in\partial T_{w}$ and $x\in y^{\prime\prime}$ };\\
\;\sum_{t\in x^{\prime\prime}\triangle y^{\prime\prime}}w_{t}&\mbox{if $x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime}\in\partial T_{w}$}.\end{cases}

Proposition 4.6
.

For every weighted tree
(T,w)
, the metric space
\overline{T}_{w}
is a completion of the metric space
T_{w}
.

Proof.

The definition of the metric on
\overline{T}_{w}
implies that the metric space
T_{w}
is a dense subspace of the metric space
\overline{T}_{w}
.
To prove that
\overline{T}_{w}
is a completion of
T_{w}
, it suffices to show that every Cauchy sequence
(s^{\prime\prime}_{n})_{n\in\omega}
in the metric space
T_{w}
has a limit in the space
\overline{T}_{w}
. If for some element
t
of
T_{w}
, the set
\{n\in\omega:s^{\prime\prime}_{n}=t\}
is infinite, then
t
is the limit of the Cauchy sequence
(s^{\prime\prime}_{n})_{n\in\omega}
and we are done. So, we can assume that all elements of the Cauchy sequence
(s^{\prime\prime}_{n})_{n\in\omega}
are pairwise distinct and moreover all of them are distinct from the root
(r_{T},0)
of the
\mathbb{R}
-tree
T_{w}
. For every
n\in\omega
, write the element
s^{\prime\prime}_{n}
of
T_{w}
as a pair
(s_{n},s_{n}^{\prime})
where
s_{n}\in T
and
s_{n}^{\prime}\in(0,w_{s_{n}}]
. Let
\check{s}_{n}\coloneqq\max({\downarrow}s_{n}\setminus\{s_{n}\})
be the immediate predecessor of
s_{n}
in the tree
T
.

In the tree
T
, consider the set

\Lambda\coloneqq\{t\in T:|\{n\in\omega:t\preceq\check{s}_{n}\}|=\omega\}

and observe that it contains the root
r_{T}
of the tree
T
and hence is not empty.
Two cases are possible.

1. First assume that the set
\Lambda
has no maximal elements. In this case
\Lambda
contains an increasing sequence
(\lambda_{n})_{n\in\omega}
. For this sequence the set
b\coloneqq\bigcup_{n\in\omega}{\downarrow}\lambda_{n}
is an infinite branch of the tree
T
such that for every
t\in b
the set
\{n\in\omega:t\preceq\check{s}_{n}\}
is infinite. Write the branch
b
as
b=\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}
, where
b_{n}
is a unique element of
b
such that
|{\downarrow}b_{n}|=n+1
. We claim that
b\in\partial T_{w}
. Since the sequence
(s^{\prime\prime}_{n})_{n\in\omega}
is Cauchy, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
m\in\mathbb{N}
such that
d_{w}(s^{\prime\prime}_{n},s^{\prime\prime}_{m})<\varepsilon
for every
n\geq m
.

Since the sequence
(b_{n})_{n\in\omega}
is unbounded in the tree
T
, there exists a number
i\in\omega
such that
b_{i}\not\preceq\check{s}_{m}
. Given any number
j\geq i
, find a number
n\geq m
such that
b_{j}\preceq\check{s}_{n}
.
Observe that
b_{i},b_{j},\check{s}_{n}
and
\check{s}_{n}\wedge\check{s}_{m}
are elements of the linearly ordered set
{\downarrow}\check{s}_{n}
. Assuming that
\check{s}_{m}\wedge\check{s}_{n}\not\preceq b_{i}
, we conclude that
b_{i}\preceq\check{s}_{m}\wedge\check{s}_{n}\preceq\check{s}_{m}
, which contradicts the choice of the number
i
. This contradiction shows that
\check{s}_{m}\wedge\check{s}_{n}\preceq b_{i}
.

In this case, the definition of the metric
d_{w}
ensures that

\sum_{k=i+1}^{j}w_{b_{k}}\leq-w_{s_{n}}-w_{s_{m}}+\sum_{x\in{\downarrow}s_{n}\triangle{\downarrow}s_{m}}w_{x}\leq d_{w}(s^{\prime\prime}_{m},s^{\prime\prime}_{n})<\varepsilon

and hence
\sum_{k=i+1}^{\infty}w_{b_{k}}\leq\varepsilon<\infty
, witnessing that
b\in\partial T_{w}
.

Since the sequence
(s^{\prime\prime}_{n})_{n\in\omega}
is Cauchy and
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, the equality
b=\lim_{n\to\infty}s^{\prime\prime}_{n}
will follow as soon as we check that
\overline{d}_{w}(b,s^{\prime\prime}_{n})<2\varepsilon
for infinitely many numbers
n
. Indeed, for every number
n\geq m
in the infinite set
\{n\in\omega:b_{i}\preceq\check{s}_{n}\}
we have
\check{s}_{n}\wedge\check{s}_{m}\preceq b_{i}
and

\displaystyle\overline{d}_{w}(s^{\prime\prime}_{n},b)

\displaystyle=s_{n}^{\prime}-w_{s_{n}}+\sum_{x\in{\downarrow}s_{n}\triangle b}w_{x}=s_{n}^{\prime}-w_{s_{n}}+\sum_{x\in{\downarrow}s_{n}\setminus b}w_{x}+\sum_{x\in b\setminus{\downarrow}s_{n}}w_{x}

\displaystyle\leq s^{\prime}_{n}-w_{s_{n}}+\sum_{x\in{\downarrow}s_{n}\setminus{\downarrow}b_{i}}w_{x}+{\sum_{k=i+1}^{\infty}w_{b_{k}}}

\displaystyle\leq s^{\prime}_{n}-w_{s_{n}}+\sum_{x\in{\downarrow}s_{n}\setminus{\downarrow}s_{m}}w_{x}+\sum_{k=i+1}^{\infty}w_{b_{k}}\leq d_{w}(s^{\prime\prime}_{n},s^{\prime\prime}_{m})+\varepsilon<2\varepsilon.

2. Next, assume that the set
\Lambda
has a maximal element
\lambda
. Consider the infinite set
I\coloneqq\{n\in\omega:\lambda\preceq\check{s}_{n}\}
. If for some
s\in T
, the set
J\coloneqq\{n\in I:s=s_{n}\}
is infinite, then the maximality of
\lambda
ensures that
\lambda
is the immediate predecessor of
s
in the tree
T
. Then the subsequence
(s_{n}^{\prime\prime})_{n\in J}
of the sequence
(s^{\prime\prime}_{n})_{n\in\omega}
is contained in the compact set
\{(\lambda,w_{\lambda})\}\cup(\{s\}\times(0,w_{s}])
and hence the Cauchy sequence
(s_{n}^{\prime\prime})_{n\in\omega}
converges to some points of this compact set. So, assume that for every
s\in T
, the set
\{n\in I:s_{n}=s\}
is finite.
In this case we shall show that the Cauchy sequence
(s_{n}^{\prime\prime})_{n\in\omega}
converges to the element
\lambda^{\prime\prime}\coloneqq(\lambda,w_{\lambda})
of the
\mathbb{R}
-tree
T_{w}
. Since the sequence
(s_{n}^{\prime\prime})_{n\in\omega}
is Cauchy and consists of pairwise distinct elements, it suffices to show that for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
n\in\omega
such that
d_{w}(\lambda^{\prime\prime},s_{n}^{\prime\prime})<\varepsilon
. Since the sequence
(s^{\prime\prime}_{n})_{n\in\omega}
is Cauchy, there exists
m\in\omega
such that
d_{w}(s_{n}^{\prime\prime},s_{k}^{\prime\prime})<\varepsilon
for all
n,k\geq m
. We can assume that
\lambda\preceq\check{s}_{m}\prec s_{m}
. Thus the element
\lambda
has an immediate successor
\hat{\lambda}
in the tree
T
such that
\lambda\prec\hat{\lambda}\preceq s_{m}
. The maximality of
\lambda
ensures that
\hat{\lambda}\notin\Lambda
and hence the set
\{n\in\omega:\hat{\lambda}\preceq\check{s}_{n}\}
is finite. Then we can choose a number
n\in I
such that
n\geq m
and
\hat{\lambda}\not\preceq\check{s}_{n}
. Then
s_{n}\wedge s_{m}=\check{s}_{n}=\lambda
and

d_{w}(\lambda^{\prime\prime},s^{\prime\prime}_{n})\leq s^{\prime}_{n}-w_{s_{n}}+s^{\prime}_{m}-w_{s_{m}}+\sum_{x\in{\downarrow}s_{n}\triangle{\downarrow}s_{m}}w_{x}=d_{w}(s_{n}^{\prime\prime},s_{m}^{\prime\prime})<\varepsilon.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2

To prove Theorem
1.2
, we need to demonstrate the equivalence of the following conditions for every metric space
X
:

(1)

X
is a Lipschitz image of an injective metric space;

(2)

X
is a Lipschitz image of some complete
\mathbb{R}
-tree;

(3)

X
is Lipschitz connected.

The implication
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
follows from Proposition
2.4
(saying that every complete
\mathbb{R}
-tree is an injective metric space), and the implication
(1)\Rightarrow(3)
follows from the Lipschitz connectedness of injective metric spaces, proved in Example
3.2
.

To prove that
(3)\Rightarrow(2)
, assume that a metric space
X
is Lipschitz connected. Fix any point
x_{0}\in X
and for every point
a\in X
find a real number
w_{a}
and a
1
-Lipschitz path
\gamma_{a}\colon[0,w_{a}]\to X
such that
\gamma_{a}(0)=x_{0}
and
\gamma_{a}(w_{a})=a
. If
a=x_{0}
, then will assume that
w_{a}=0
.

Consider the tree
T\coloneqq X
endowed with the partial order

\preceq:=\{(x,y)\in X\times X:x=y\;\vee\;x=x_{0}\}

The function
w:X\to[0,\infty)
,
w:a\mapsto w_{a}
, turns
T
into a weighted tree. The weighted tree
(T,w)
generates the
\mathbb{R}
-tree
T_{w}
. Since the tree
T
contains no infinite branches, the
\mathbb{R}
-tree
T_{w}
coincides with its completion
\overline{T}_{w}=T_{w}\cup\partial T_{w}
and hence
T_{w}
is a complete
\mathbb{R}
-tree.

The
1
-Lipschitz maps
\gamma_{a}
,
a\in X
, compose a surjective
1
-Lipschitz map
\gamma:T_{w}\to X
such that
\gamma((r_{T},0))=x_{0}
and
\gamma((a,t))=\gamma_{a}(t)
for
(a,t)\in\bigcup_{a\in X}\{a\}\times(0,w_{a}]
. Indeed for any
(a,t),(b,s)\in T_{w}
if
a=b

d_{X}(\gamma((a,t)),\gamma((b,s)))=d_{X}(\gamma_{a}(t),\gamma_{a}(s))\leq|t-s|,

because
\gamma_{a}
is a 1-Lipschitz map. If
a\neq b

\displaystyle d_{X}(\gamma((a,t)),\gamma((b,s)))

\displaystyle=d_{X}(\gamma_{a}(t),\gamma_{b}(s))\leq d_{X}(\gamma_{a}(t),x_{0})+d_{X}(x_{0},\gamma_{b}(s))=

\displaystyle=d_{X}(\gamma_{a}(t),\gamma_{a}(0))+d_{X}(\gamma_{b}(0),\gamma_{b}(s))\leq t+s,

because
\gamma_{a}
,
\gamma_{b}
are 1-Lipschitz maps. The
1
-Lipschitz map
\gamma
witnesses that
X
is a
1
-Lipschitz image of the complete
\mathbb{R}
-tree
T_{w}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3

Theorem
1.3
will follow as soon as we check the equivalence of the following conditions for every metric space
X
:

(1)

X
is a Lipschitz image of some compact
\mathbb{R}
-tree;

(2)

X
is a Lipschitz image of a totally bounded injective metric space;

(3)

X
is a Lipschitz image of a compact
1
-Lipschitz connected metric space;

(4)

X
is a Lipschitz image of an
L
-Lipschitz connected compact metric space for some
L\geq 1
;

(5)

X
is compact, Lipschitz connected, and the intrinsic metric
d_{IX}
is totally bounded.

The implication
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
follows from Proposition
2.4
(saying that every complete
\mathbb{R}
-tree is an injective metric space), and the total boundedness of compact metric spaces.

The implication
(2)\Rightarrow(3)
follows from the completeness and
1
-Lipschitz connectedness of injective metric spaces, and the implication
(3)\Rightarrow(4)
is obvious.

(4)\Rightarrow(5)
Assume that a metric space
(X,d_{X})
is the image of some
L
-Lipschitz connected compact metric space
(Y,d_{Y})
under a Lipschitz map
f:Y\to X
. Then
X
is compact and Lipschitz connected, so the intrinsic metric
d_{IX}
on
X
is well-defined.
Scaling the metric of the metric space
Y
, we can assume that the Lipschitz map
f
is
1
-Lipschitz. In this case the definition of the intrinsic metric implies that the function
f
remains
1
-Lipschitz with respect to the intrinsic metrics on the metric spaces
X
and
Y
. The
L
-Lipschitz connectedness of the metric space
Y
implies that
d_{Y}\leq d_{IY}\leq L\cdot d_{Y}
, which implies that the identity map
(Y,d_{Y})\to(Y,d_{IY})
is Lipschitz and hence the metric space
(Y,d_{IY})
is totally bounded and so is its
1
-Lipschitz image
(X,d_{IX})
.

(5)\Rightarrow(1)
Assume that the metric space
(X,d_{X})
is compact, Lipschitz connected, and its intrinsic metric
d_{IX}
is totally bounded. Let
D\coloneqq\sup\{d_{IX}(x,y):x,y\in X\}
be the diameter of the (totally) bounded metric space
(X,d_{IX})
.

Since the metric space
(X,d_{IX})
is totally bounded, there exists a sequence
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\in\omega}
of finite partitions of
X
such that
\mathcal{F}_{0}=\{X\}
and for every
n\in\omega
, every set
F\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
has
d_{IX}
-diameter
\sup\{d_{IX}(x,y):x,y\in F\}<\frac{D+1}{2^{n}}
. Replacing each partition
\mathcal{F}_{n}
with
n\in\mathbb{N}
by the partition
\mathcal{F}_{n}\wedge\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\coloneqq\{A\cap B:A\in\mathcal{F}_{n-1},\;\;B\in\mathcal{F}_{n},\;\;A\cap B\neq\varnothing\}
, we can assume that every set
F\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
is contained in a unique set
\check{F}\in\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
.
For every
n\in\omega
and
F\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
, choose a point
c_{F}\in F
.

Consider the tree
T\coloneqq\bigcup_{n\in\omega}(\{n\}\times\mathcal{F}_{n})
endowed with the partial order
\preceq
, defined by
(n,F)\preceq(m,E)
iff
n\leq m
and
E\subseteq F
. Next, endow the tree
T
with the weight function
w:T\to[0,\infty)
defined by

w(n,F)=w_{(n,F)}=\begin{cases}\frac{D+1}{2^{n-1}}&\mbox{ if $n>0$};\\
0&\mbox{if $n=0$}.\end{cases}

.

Let
T_{w}
be the
\mathbb{R}
-tree of the weighted tree
(T,w)
. Since the families
\mathcal{F}_{n}
are finite and
\sum_{t\in b}w_{t}=2(D+1)
for every

, the weighted tree
(T,w)

, the metric space

\mathbb{R}
-tree.

It remains to construct a
1
-Lipschitz surjective map
\overline{\gamma}:\overline{T}_{w}\to X
. For every

and

, choose a

-Lipschitz function
\gamma_{n,F}:[0,w_{(n,F)}]\to X

,

-Lipschitz. Observe that the image

Remark 6.1
.

Although compactness of

7.
Proof of Theorem
1.4

Theorem
1.4
will follow as soon as we check the equivalence of the following conditions for every metric space
X
:

(1)

X
is a Lipschitz image of some separable complete
\mathbb{R}
-tree;

(2)

X
is a Lipschitz image of a separable injective metric space;

(3)

X
is a
1
-Lipschitz image of the Urysohn universal metric space;

(4)

X
is a
1
-Lipschitz image of a
1
-Lipschitz connected separable complete metric space;

(5)

X
is a Lipschitz image of an
L
-Lipschitz connected analytic metric space for some
L\geq 1
;

(6)

X
is analytic, Lipschitz connected, and the intrinsic metric
d_{IX}
is separable.

The implication
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
follows from Proposition
2.4
(saying that every complete
\mathbb{R}
-tree is an injective metric space).

To prove that
(2)\Rightarrow(3)
, assume that the metric space
X
is the image of a separable injective space
Y
under a Lipschitz map
f:Y\to X
. Scaling the metric of the metric space
Y
, we can assume that the Lipschitz map
f
is
1
-Lipschitz. By
[
18
]
, the Urysohn universal metric space
\mathbb{U}
contains an isometric copy of any separable metric space. So, there exists an isometric embedding
e:Y\to\mathbb{U}
. By the injectivity of
Y
, the
1
-Lipschitz map
e^{-1}:e[Y]\to Y
defined on the subset
e[Y]
of
\mathbb{U}
admits a
1
-Lipschitz extension
g:\mathbb{U}\to Y
. Then
f\circ g:\mathbb{U}\to X
is a surjective
1
-Lipschitz map, witnessing that
X
is a
1
-Lipschitz image of the Urysohn universal metric space
\mathbb{U}
.

To see the implication
(3)\Rightarrow(4)
it suffices to check that the Urysohn universal metric space
\mathbb{U}
is
1
-Lipschitz connected. Given any points
x,y\in\mathbb{U}
, consider the function
f:\{0,d(x,y)\}\to\{x,y\}\subseteq\mathbb{U}
such that
f(0)=x
and
f(d(x,y))=y
. By
[
3
,
12
]
(see also
[
2
,
15
,
17
]
), the isometric embedding
f:\{0,d(x,y)\}\to\mathbb{U}
extends to a
1
-Lipschitz map
f:[0,d(x,y)]\to\mathbb{U}
, witnessing that
\mathbb{U}
is
1
-Lipschitz connected.

The implication
(4)\Rightarrow(5)
is trivial.

(5)\Rightarrow(6)
Assume that for some
L\geq 1
, a metric space
(X,d_{X})
is the image of some
L
-Lipschitz connected analytic metric space
(Y,d_{Y})
under a Lipschitz map
f:Y\to X
. Then
X
is analytic and Lipschitz connected, so the intrinsic metric
d_{IX}
on
X
is well-defined.
Scaling the metric of the metric space
Y
, we can assume that the Lipschitz map
f
is
1
-Lipschitz. In this case the definition of the intrinsic metric implies that the function
f
remains
1
-Lipschitz with respect to the intrinsic metrics on the metric spaces
X
and
Y
. The
L
-Lipschitz connectedness of the metric space
Y
implies that
d_{Y}\leq d_{IY}\leq L\cdot d_{Y}
, which implies that the identity map
(Y,d_{Y})\to(Y,d_{IY})
is Lipschitz and hence the metric space
(Y,d_{IY})
is separable and so is its
1
-Lipschitz image
(X,d_{IX})
.

(6)\Rightarrow(1)
Assume that the metric space
(X,d_{X})
is analytic, Lipschitz connected, and its intrinsic metric
d_{IX}
is separable. By Proposition
3.4
, the metric space
X_{I}\coloneqq(X,d_{IX})
is analytic and hence it is the image of a separable complete metric space
(Z,d_{Z})
under a surjective continuous map
f:Z\to X_{I}
. Consider the metric
\hat{d}_{Z}
on
Z
, defined by the formula
\hat{d}_{Z}(z,z^{\prime})\coloneqq\max\{d_{Z}(z,z^{\prime}),d_{IX}(f(z),f(z^{\prime}))\}
. The continuity of the function
f
ensures that the metric
\hat{d}_{Z}
is topologically equivalent to the metric
d_{Z}
. Since
d_{Z}\leq\hat{d}_{Z}
, the completeness of the metric
d_{Z}
implies the completeness of the metric
\hat{d}_{Z}
. Replacing the metric
d_{Z}
by the metric
\hat{d}_{Z}
, we can assume that the function
f:Z\to X_{I}
is
1
-Lipschitz.

Since the metric space
Z
is separable, there exists a sequence
(\mathcal{F}_{n})_{n\in\omega}
of countable partitions of
Z
such that
\mathcal{F}_{0}=\{Z\}
and for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, every set
F\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
has diameter
\sup\{d_{Z}(x,y):x,y\in F\}<\frac{1}{2^{n}}
. Replacing each partition
\mathcal{F}_{n}
by the partition
\mathcal{F}_{n}\wedge\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\coloneqq\{A\cap B:A\in\mathcal{F}_{n-1},\;\;B\in\mathcal{F}_{n},\;\;A\cap B\neq\varnothing\}
, we can assume that every set
F\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
is contained in a unique set
\check{F}\in\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
.
For every
n\in\omega
and
F\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
, choose a point
c_{F}\in F
.

Consider the countable tree
T\coloneqq\bigcup_{n\in\omega}(\{n\}\times\mathcal{F}_{n})
endowed with the partial order
\preceq
, defined by
(n,F)\preceq(m,E)
iff
n\leq m
and
E\subseteq F
. Next, endow the tree
T
with the weight function
w:T\to[0,\infty)
defined by
w_{(0,Z)}=0
and
w_{(n,F)}=d_{IX}(f(c_{\check{F}}),f(c_{F}))+\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
F\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
(in this formula
\check{F}
denotes the unique set in
\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
that contains the set
F
).

Let
T_{w}
be the
\mathbb{R}
-tree of the weighted tree
(T,w)
and
\overline{T}_{w}=T_{w}\cup\partial T_{w}
be its completion. Proposition
4.5
ensures that the
\mathbb{R}
-tree
T_{w}
is separable and hence its completion
\overline{T}_{w}
is a separable complete
\mathbb{R}
-tree.

It remains to construct a
1
-Lipschitz surjective map
\overline{\gamma}:\overline{T}_{w}\to X
. For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
F\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
, choose a
1
-Lipschitz function
\gamma_{n,F}:[0,w_{(n,F)}]\to X
such that
\gamma_{n,F}(0)=f(c_{\check{F}})
and
\gamma_{n,F}(w_{(n,F)})=f(c_{F})
. The function
\gamma_{n,F}
exists because
d_{IX}(f(c_{\check{F}}),f(c_{F}))<w_{(n,F)}
. Next, consider the function

\gamma:T_{w}\to X
defined by

\gamma((0,X),0)=f(c_{Z})
and
\gamma((n,F),t)=\gamma_{n,F}(t)
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
F\in\mathcal{F}_{n}
and
t\in(0,w_{(n,F)}]
.
The definition of the metric
d_{w}
on the
\mathbb{R}
-tree
T_{w}
ensures that the map
\gamma:T_{w}\to X
is
1
-Lipschitz.

Consider the set
\partial T
of all infinite branches of the tree
T
and observe that every branch
b\in\partial T
is equal to the set
\{(n,F_{n}):n\in\omega\}
for some decreasing sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in\omega}\in\prod_{n\in\omega}\mathcal{F}_{n}
.
Observe that for every
n\geq 2
, the set
\{c_{\check{F}_{n}},c_{F_{n}}\}
is a subset of the set
\check{F}_{n}\in\mathcal{F}_{n-1}
and hence
d_{Z}(c_{F_{n-1}},c_{F_{n}})=d_{Z}(c_{\check{F}_{n}},c_{F_{n}})<\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}
.
Then the sequence
(c_{F_{n}})_{n\in\omega}
is Cauchy in the complete metric space
Z
and has a limit
\lambda(b)\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{F_{n}}\in Z
, which coincides with the unique point of the intersection
\bigcap_{n\in\omega}\overline{F_{n}}
.
The correspondence
\lambda:\partial T\mapsto\lambda(b)
, determines a function
\lambda:\partial T\to Z
. Observe that for every point
z\in Z
there exists a unique branch
b=\{(n,F_{n})\}_{n\in\omega}\in\partial T
such that
z\in\bigcap_{n\in\omega}F_{n}=\bigcap_{n\in\omega}\overline{F}_{n}=\{\lambda(b)\}
, witnessing that the function
\lambda:\partial T\to Z
is surjective. Taking into account that diameters of the sets in the partition
\mathcal{F}_{n}
tend to zero as
n
tends to infinity, one can show that the map
\lambda:\partial T\to Z
is continuous with respect to the topology on
\partial T
inherited from the Tychonoff product
\prod_{n\in\omega}(\{n\}\times\mathcal{F}_{n})
of discrete spaces
\{n\}\times\mathcal{F}_{n}
,
n\in\omega
.

Observe that for every branch
b=\{(n,F_{n})\}_{n\in\omega}
of the tree
T
and every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have
w_{(n,F_{n})}=d_{IX}(f(c_{F_{n-1}}),f(c_{F_{n}}))+\frac{1}{2^{n}}\leq d_{Z}(c_{F_{n-1}},c_{F_{n}})+\frac{1}{2^{n}}<\frac{1}{2^{n-1}}+\tfrac{1}{2^{n}}
and hence
\sum_{t\in b}w_{t}=\sum_{n\in\omega}w_{(n,F_{n})}<\infty
, witnessing that
\partial T_{w}=\partial T
.

Since the map
\gamma:T_{w}\to X
is
1
-Lipschitz, there exists a unique
1
-Lipschitz extension
\overline{\gamma}:\overline{T}_{w}\to\overline{X}
to the completion of the space
X
. We claim that
\overline{\gamma}[\overline{T}_{w}]=X
.

The inclusion
\overline{\gamma}[\overline{T}_{w}]\subseteq X
will follow as soon as we check that
\overline{\gamma}[\partial T]\subseteq X
. Fix any element
b\in\partial T
and find a unique decreasing sequence of sets
(F_{n})_{n\in\omega}\in\prod_{n\in\omega}\mathcal{F}_{n}
such that
b=\{(n,F_{n})\}_{n\in\omega}
.
Then
\lambda(b)=\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{F_{n}}
.
The definition of the metric on the
\mathbb{R}
-tree
\overline{T}_{w}
ensures that

b=\lim_{n\to\infty}((n,F_{n}),w_{(n,F_{n})})

in
\overline{T}_{w}
and hence
\overline{\gamma}(b)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma((n,F_{n}),w_{(n,F_{n})})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{n,F}(w_{(n,F_{n})})=\lim_{n\to\infty}f(c_{F_{n}})=f(\lim_{n\to\infty}c_{F_{n}})=f(\lambda(b))\in f[Z]=X
.

Next, we show that
X\subseteq\overline{\gamma}[\overline{T}_{w}]
. Given any point
x\in X
, choose any point

and find a unique branch
b=\{(n,F_{n})\}_{n\in\omega}\in\partial T
such that
z\in\bigcap_{n\in\omega}F_{n}

and

Therefore,
\overline{\gamma}:\overline{T}_{w}\to X
is a
1
-Lipschitz surjective map witnessing that

is a
1
-Lipschitz image of the separable complete
\mathbb{R}
-tree
\overline{T}_{w}
.
