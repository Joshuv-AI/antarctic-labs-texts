Feature Selection for Vertex Discriminant Analysis

Abstract
We revisit vertex discriminant analysis (VDA) from the perspective of proximal distance algorithms. By specifying sparsity sets as constraints that directly control the number of active features, VDA is able to fit multiclass classifiers with no more than k k active features. We combine our sparse VDA approach with repeated cross validation to fit classifiers across the full range of model sizes on a given dataset. Our numerical examples demonstrate that grappling with sparsity directly is an attractive approach to model building in high-dimensional settings. Applications to kernel-based VDA are also considered.



Support vector machines (SVM) stand out in the universe of classification algorithms for their simplicity, interpretability, speed, and theoretical guarantees
(
Smola and
Schölkopf 1998
;
Vapnik 1999
)
.
The circle of SVM algorithms have been bolstered by fundamental advances in statistical theory and computer hardware but also challenged by the growing size of modern datasets.
The Achilles heal of SVMs is their limitation to binary classification.
Strategies to overcome this limitation, most notably one-versus-rest and one-versus-one, rely on multiple SVMs to discriminate between classes.
This necessarily increases time to fit a model, obscures statistical interpretation, and violates the principle of parsimony.
Vertex discriminant analysis (VDA)
(
Lange and
Wu 2008
)
addresses this particular weakness of SVMs.
Our subsequent paper
(
Wu and
Lange 2010
)
derives a fast cyclic coordinate descent algorithm and attacks feature selection by a combination of Euclidean group and lasso penalties.
Our second followup paper
(
Wu and Wu 2012
)
generalizes VDA to nonlinear discriminant analysis by the kernel trick.

The current paper revisits the problem of feature selection in VDA from the perspective of distance to set penalties.
This allows us to incorporate a spectrum of constraints, with particular emphasis on sparsity sets.
The experiments recorded here demonstrate that the direct control of sparsity is highly effective in building parsimonious models on both simulated and real-world datasets.

1
Proposed Method

As a prelude to introducing a generic method for feature constraints, let us review VDA with
\epsilon
-insensitive pseudo-distances and derive a quadratic surrogate that both simplifies the associated empirical loss and converts minimization to iterative least squares.
Next, we present a distance penalized version of VDA that reduces constrained minimization to unconstrained minimization.
In particular, we focus on distance to sparsity sets as a mechanism for directly enforcing parsimony.
We then present two competitive MM algorithms designed with high-dimensional optimization in mind.
Finally, we comment on practical issues, including initialization strategies, convergence criteria, and hyperparameter tuning.

Before delving into specifics, let us clarify our notation. We restrict our attention to problems formulated over Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Vectors are written in lowercase boldface, for instance
\boldsymbol{y}
, and matrices are written in uppercase boldface, for instance
\boldsymbol{X}
.
The notation
\|\cdot\|
indicates the Euclidean norm on the ambient space.
In some cases we decorate a norm with a subscript, for example the Frobenius norm
\|\cdot\|_{F}
, to avoid ambiguity.
The superscript
\top
indicates a vector or matrix transpose.
The lowercase letter
k
denotes the number of selected parameters; equivalently, the lowercase letter
s=1-k/p
is reserved for the sparsity level.
The maximum number of parameters
p
is understood from context.
Finally, the letters
m
and
t
are reserved for inner iterations and outer iterations, respectively.
For example,
\boldsymbol{B}_{m}
is an estimate at iteration
m
to a problem
\min_{\boldsymbol{B}}~f_{\rho(t)}(\boldsymbol{B})
parameterized by a penalty coefficient
\rho(t)
, whereas
\boldsymbol{B}_{t}
denotes one of its solutions, either exact or approximate, at level
t
.

1.1
Majorizing
\epsilon
-Insensitive Empirical Risk

Given a dataset with
n
samples,
p
predictors, and
c
categories, the goal in VDA is to minimize the empirical risk

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}R(\boldsymbol{y}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{B}).

Here
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the feature vector for sample
i
,
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{c-1}
is the sample’s label encoded as a vertex of a regular simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{c-1}
, and the entries of
\boldsymbol{B}=(b_{jk})
are regression coefficients to be estimated.
When
c=2
,
3
, or
4
, the simplex is
[-1,1]
, an equilateral triangle, or a regular tetrahedron centered around the origin, respectively.
The predicted value for sample
i
’s class vertex
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
is
\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
.
We collect the rows
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}
into a feature matrix
\boldsymbol{X}
and the rows
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{\top}
into a class matrix
\boldsymbol{Y}
.
To fit
\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{B}
to
\boldsymbol{Y}
, we focus attention on the risk function

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max\{0,\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|_{2}-\epsilon\}^{2},

(1)

which is the square of
\epsilon
-insensitive pseudo-distance. The residual
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
for sample
i
incurs no loss when
\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
lies within a Euclidean
\epsilon
-ball around its assigned vertex
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
. Otherwise, the residual contributes the reduced loss
(\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|-\epsilon)^{2}
to the risk. If
\mathcal{Y}
denotes the collection of
c
vertices and
\hat{\boldsymbol{B}}
the estimated coefficient matrix, then an unassigned feature vector
\boldsymbol{x}
is assigned to a class through the map

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{x}

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits_{\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\|\boldsymbol{y}-\hat{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}\|,

singling out the closest vertex. Inclusion of an intercept can be beneficial when training class numbers are unbalanced, and easily is achieved by adding an intercept row to
\boldsymbol{B}
and a corresponding constant column
\boldsymbol{1}
to
\boldsymbol{X}
.

Squared
\epsilon
-insensitive pseudo-distances admit a quadratic surrogate that greatly simplifies minimization of the loss
f(\boldsymbol{B})
.
To identify the surrogate, consider the majorization

\displaystyle\inf_{\boldsymbol{v}}\{f_{1}(\boldsymbol{v})+f_{2}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v})\}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle f_{1}(\boldsymbol{v}_{m})+f_{2}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}_{m})

of the infimal convolution of two function
f_{1}(\cdot)
and
f_{2}(\cdot)
.
In addition to the indicated inequality for all
\boldsymbol{u}
, majorization requires the tangency condition of equality when
\boldsymbol{u}
equals the anchor point
\boldsymbol{v}_{m}
.
This is achieved by choosing
\boldsymbol{v}_{m}
to be the minimum point of the infimal convolution corresponding to
\boldsymbol{u}_{m}
.
For the closed set
C=[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
, the
0/\infty
indicator
f_{1}(\boldsymbol{v})=\delta_{C}(\|\boldsymbol{v}\|)
and the squared Euclidean distance
f_{2}(\boldsymbol{v})=\|\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}
we deduce the majorization

\displaystyle\max\{0,\|\boldsymbol{u}\|-\epsilon\}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{v}}\delta_{[-\epsilon,\epsilon]}(\|\boldsymbol{v}\|)+\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}\|^{2}\;\>\leq\;\>\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}_{m}\|^{2};

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{v}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{u}_{m},&\|\boldsymbol{u}_{m}\|\leq\epsilon\\
\frac{\epsilon}{\|\boldsymbol{u}_{m}\|}\boldsymbol{u}_{m},&\|\boldsymbol{u}_{m}\|>\epsilon\end{cases}

relevant to VDA.

Applying this majorization to model (
1
) term-by-term, we arrive at the overall majorization

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{B})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\boldsymbol{r}_{i}-\boldsymbol{r}_{mi}\|^{2},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}_{mi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i},&\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|\leq\epsilon\\
\frac{\epsilon}{\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),&\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|>\epsilon.\end{cases}

Here the estimate at iteration
m
, denoted
\boldsymbol{B}_{m}
, serves as an anchor for the surrogate function.
It is straightforward to verify that tangency occurs when
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{B}_{m}
.
To make further progress, consider two cases concerning the squared term.
When the residual is modest, we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}_{i}-\boldsymbol{r}_{mi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i})-(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\;\>=\;\>\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}.

In the opposite case, the penalty argument reads

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{r}_{i}-\boldsymbol{r}_{mi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(1-\frac{\epsilon}{\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{\top}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{m}\|}\right)\boldsymbol{y}_{i}+\frac{\epsilon}{\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{\top}-\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{m}\|}\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}.

In either case, we have successfully isolated the linear predictor
\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
.
This leads to the quadratic surrogate

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{B}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\boldsymbol{z}_{mi}-\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|^{2}\;\>=\;\>\frac{1}{2n}\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}-\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{B}\|_{F}^{2},

(2)

where
\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{B}
predicts
\boldsymbol{Y}
and
\boldsymbol{Y}
is replaced by
\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}
with rows

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}_{mi}^{\top}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{m},&\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|\leq\epsilon\\
w_{mi}\boldsymbol{y}_{i}^{\top}+(1-w_{m,i})\boldsymbol{x}_{i}^{\top}\boldsymbol{B}_{m},&\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|>\epsilon\end{cases}

\displaystyle w_{mi}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|-\epsilon}{\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|}.

In summary, our surrogate can be interpreted as an ordinary sum of squares with shifted responses. Observe that the tangency requirement
g(\boldsymbol{B}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})=f(\boldsymbol{B}_{m})
implies

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{B}_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\boldsymbol{z}_{mi}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|>\epsilon}\!\!w_{mi}^{2}\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\|^{2}.

Hence at iteration
m
, only samples with residual errors beyond
\epsilon
contribute to the loss.
These samples tend to drive the subsequent update
\boldsymbol{B}_{m+1}
.

1.2
Feature Selection via Sparsity Sets

Having derived a quadratic surrogate for the empirical risk (
1
), we now focus on feature selection.
Following our previous work on proximal distance algorithms
(
Keys
et al. 2019
)
, we penalize
f(\boldsymbol{B})
by the squared Euclidean distance of
\boldsymbol{B}
from the constraint set
S
.
This creates the objective

\displaystyle f_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{B})+\frac{\rho}{2}\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{B},S)^{2}

(3)

for
\rho>0
large.
In binary classification
c=2
, so a natural choice for
S
is

\displaystyle S_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Big\{\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}:\sum_{j=1}^{p}1_{\{b_{j}\neq 0\}}\leq k\Big\}

for
\boldsymbol{b}=(b_{j})
.
In words, the sparsity set
S_{k}
consists of those vectors with at most
k
nonzero components.
When an intercept
\boldsymbol{b}_{0}
is included in the model, the intercept is usually omitted in the sparsity set.
Minimizing the penalized objective
f_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B})
with
S=S_{k}
delivers a solution minimizing empirical risk with at most
k<p
distinguishing features.

When the number of classes
c>2
, the most plausible way to define sparsity is through the number of active features.
A feature corresponds to a row
\boldsymbol{b}_{j}
of
\boldsymbol{B}
, so we encode sparsity through the constraint set

\displaystyle S_{k}^{\mathrm{row}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigg\{\boldsymbol{B}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{b}_{1}\\
\cdots\\
\boldsymbol{b}_{p}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times(c-1)}:\sum_{j=1}^{p}1_{\{\|\boldsymbol{b}_{j}\|=0\}}\leq k\Bigg\}.

Thus, at most
k
active rows (features) are allowed in the model.
Note that
S_{k}^{\mathrm{row}}\equiv S_{k}
when
c=2
.
Regardless of the constraint set
S
, distance majorization replaces
\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{B},S)^{2}
with
\|\boldsymbol{B}-P_{S}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m})\|_{F}^{2}
, where
P_{S}(\boldsymbol{B})
denotes the Euclidean projection of
\boldsymbol{B}
onto
S
.
For a sparsity set, projection acts by ranking features according to their norms
\|\boldsymbol{b}_{j}\|
and then identifying the top
k
features via a partial sort.
Omitted rows are then set to
\boldsymbol{0}^{\top}
.
The intercept row is ignored in projection.
In summary, projection onto a sparsity set amounts to computing order statistics on estimates of model coefficients and thresholding to select the highest ranking features.

In the special case
S=S_{k}^{\mathrm{row}}
, combining majorization (
2
) with distance majorization leads to the overall quadratic surrogate

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2n}\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}-\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{B}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{P}_{m}-\boldsymbol{B}\|_{F}^{2}

(4)

with the shifted response matrix
\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}
and projection
P_{S}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m})
.
This convex quadratic serves as the main ingredient in deriving our novel proximal distance algorithms for VDA.

1.3
Algorithms

Let us briefly recall the MM principle
(
Lange 2016
)
applied to the penalized objective (
3
).
Given an anchor point
\boldsymbol{B}_{m}
, the surrogate (
4
) majorizes (
3
) so that
f_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B})\leq g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})
holds for every
\boldsymbol{B}
in the essential domain of the penalized objective.
At each iteration we select a potentially nonunique stationary point
\boldsymbol{B}_{m+1}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits_{\boldsymbol{B}}\,g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})
minimizing the surrogate (
4
) around the anchor point
\boldsymbol{B}_{m}
.
These observations combined with the tangency condition reveal the chain of inequalities

\displaystyle f_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m+1})\overset{\text{majorization}}{\leq}g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m+1}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})\overset{\text{definition}}{\leq}g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})\overset{\text{tangency}}{=}f_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m}).

Thus, minimizing surrogate (
4
) guarantees the descent property in minimizing the penalized loss (
3
).
The remainder of this section identifies specific iteration schemes based on this useful principle.
Additional details are available in Appendix
A
.

1.3.1
Direct Minimization

The directional derivative of the surrogate (
4
) in the direction
\boldsymbol{W}
is

\displaystyle d_{\boldsymbol{W}}g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-n^{-1}\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits[(\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}-\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{B})^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{W}]+\rho\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits[(\boldsymbol{B}-\boldsymbol{P}_{m})^{\top}\boldsymbol{W}].

At a stationary point
d_{\boldsymbol{W}}g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})
vanishes for all
\boldsymbol{W}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-n^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}-\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{B})+\rho(\boldsymbol{B}-\boldsymbol{P}_{m})\;\>=\;\>\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})

(5)

and that the
\boldsymbol{B}
update is

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}_{m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{X}+\rho\boldsymbol{I}_{p})^{-1}(n^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}+\rho\boldsymbol{P}_{m}).

To fit
\boldsymbol{B}
across a spectrum of feature subsets and values of
\rho
, we extract the thin singular value decomposition (SVD)
\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}
and invoke the Woodbury matrix identity

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{E}\boldsymbol{C}\boldsymbol{F})^{-1}=\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}-\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{E}(\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{E})^{-1}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}

with
\boldsymbol{A}=\rho\boldsymbol{I}_{p}
,
\boldsymbol{E}=\boldsymbol{V}
,
\boldsymbol{F}=\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}
, and
\boldsymbol{C}=n^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}
.
The substitutions derived in Appendix
A.1
then yield the preferred MM update

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}_{m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{m}+\boldsymbol{V}n^{-1}(n^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}+\rho\boldsymbol{I}_{r})^{-1}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}_{m}\right]

(6)

involving the inverse of an
r\times r
diagonal matrix, where
r
equals the rank of
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
.
While the required thin SVD is expensive to extract in high dimensions, it is done only once.
This strategy successfully decouples the annealing parameter
\rho
and model size
k
from the multitude of matrix operations.

1.3.2
Steepest Descent

Alternatively, one can take advantage of the quadratic form of (
4
) to derive a step size
\gamma_{m}
in gradient descent update
\boldsymbol{B}_{m+1}=\boldsymbol{B}_{m}-\gamma_{m}\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})
.
The Taylor expansion

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})+\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\left\{\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})^{\top}(\boldsymbol{B}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m})\right\}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathop{\rm tr}\nolimits\left\{(\boldsymbol{B}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m})^{\top}\nabla^{2}g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})(\boldsymbol{B}-\boldsymbol{B}_{m})\right\},

is exact.
In the particular case
\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{B}_{m}-\gamma\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})
, elementary calculus leads to the steepest descent update

\displaystyle\gamma_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|\boldsymbol{G}_{m}\|_{F}^{2}}{n^{-1}\|\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{G}_{m}\|_{F}^{2}+\rho\|\boldsymbol{G}_{m}\|_{F}^{2}}\qquad\boldsymbol{B}_{m+1}=\boldsymbol{B}_{m}+\gamma_{m}\boldsymbol{G}_{m},

(7)

where
\boldsymbol{G}_{m}=\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})
is defined by formula (
5
).

1.4
Practical Considerations and Implementation

We now address several issues in formulating a viable proximal distance algorithm based on our general framework.

1.4.1
Initialization

The penalized loss (
3
) is non-convex when
S
is a sparsity set.
Thus, solutions may be sensitive to the initial guess
\boldsymbol{B}_{0}
.
The special cases
k=p
and
k=0
are natural starting points for selecting a model via cross validation.
In these two cases the penalized loss is convex but unfortunately not strongly convex.
Choosing an initial point therefore requires some care.
We recommend minimizing the regularized loss

\displaystyle h_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{B})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\boldsymbol{B})+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}\|_{F}^{2},\quad\lambda\;\>>\;\>0,

(8)

via the surrogate
g_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{B}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})=\frac{1}{2n}\|\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}-\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{B}\|_{F}^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}\|_{F}^{2}
.
Strong convexity now holds, so minimization of
f_{\lambda}(\boldsymbol{B})
furnishes a neutral starting point.
Any random initial value
\boldsymbol{B}_{0}
should lead to the same solution.
Note that in the special case with
k=p
active features, the distance penalty vanishes and minimization of surrogate (
4
) reduces to a potentially ill-defined least squares problem.
We therefore recommend substituting the regularized surrogate (
8
) for surrogate (
4
) to stitch the solution path from
k=p
to
k=p-1
.

1.4.2
Convergence Criteria

Minimizing the penalized loss (
3
) with
\rho>0
large generally slows down convergence of iterative methods but is required to achieve optimality
(
Beltrami 1970
)
.
We surmount the poor convergence characteristics of penalty methods by solving a sequence of subproblems
(
3
)
parameterized by an annealing schedule
\{\rho(t)\}_{t\geq 0}
.
The idea is to choose an annealing schedule with
\rho(0)
small (for example,
\rho(0)=1
) and gradually increase the penalty coefficient.
Unfortunately, the choice of annealing schedule sending
\rho\to\infty
affects the quality of solutions.
It is therefore crucial to assess the quality of solutions in minimizing (
3
) for a particular value
\rho(t)
in the annealing schedule, the
inner iterations
, and in minimizing across all subproblems, the
outer iterations
.
These issues merit a brief discussion of convergence criteria.

Our previous work relied on the condition

\displaystyle|f_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m+1})-f_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m})|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\delta_{f}[1+f_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m})],

where
\delta_{f}
is a control parameter that sets a minimum level of progress per inner iteration on a relative scale
(
Keys
et al. 2019
;
Landeros
et al. 2022
)
.
In retrospect, the gradient condition

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m})\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\delta_{g},

is probably a safer criterion because convergence can be quite slow for large
\rho
even under Nesterov acceleration.
The tangency condition of the MM principle conveniently allows one to compute the gradient
\nabla f_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m})
through the equivalent expression
\nabla g_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m}\mid\boldsymbol{B}_{m})
.
Controlling the distance term across outer iterations
t
yields an approximate solution
\boldsymbol{B}_{t}=\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits~f_{\rho(t)}(\boldsymbol{B})
.
Thus, we impose conditions on the distance penalty

\displaystyle q_{t}\leq\delta_{d}\quad\mathrm{or}\quad|q_{t}-q_{t-1}|\leq\delta_{q},

where
q_{t}\equiv\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{B}_{t},S_{k}^{\mathrm{row}})
quantifies distance to a sparsity set.
Here the control parameter
\delta_{d}
defines a satisfactory level of closeness to
S_{k}^{\mathrm{row}}
, and the dual criteria involving
\delta_{q}
avoid excessive computing if progress slows dramatically. Our choices allow one to gradually guide solution estimates towards a constraint set while avoiding slow convergence due to large values of
\rho
.

1.4.3
Proximal Distance Iteration

Algorithm
1
summarizes the general minimization procedure in proximal distance iteration, including an early exit condition on the annealing path and Nesterov acceleration.
Warm starts are implicit in Algorithm
1
as one moves from a
\rho(t)
-penalized subproblem to the next subproblem.
The final projection step is justified when an approximate solution
\boldsymbol{B}
is close to a sparsity set
S_{k}^{\mathrm{row}}
in Euclidean distance; that is, when feature selection finally stabilizes.

.

i_{\mathrm{outer}}
and maximum inner iterations

.

Set threshold for Nesterov acceleration,
i_{\mathrm{Nesterov}}
.

\boldsymbol{B}
, and
q_{0}\leftarrow\mathop{\rm dist}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{B},S_{k}^{\mathrm{row}})
.

6:

Initialize counter for Nesterov acceleration
i\leftarrow 1
.

7:

for
outer iterations

do

8:

Initialize
\boldsymbol{B}_{1}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{B}
and
\boldsymbol{N}_{1}\leftarrow\boldsymbol{B}
.

9:

for
inner iterations

do

if

then

\triangleright

.

else

.

\triangleright

) or (

).

Let us now briefly address the theoretical convergence properties of Algorithm

The sparsity constraints described in Section
1.2
are defined by closed but nonconvex sets.
In principle, projection operators onto nonconvex can be multivalued.
For example, projection of the point

onto the sparsity set

is nonunique due to ties.
Fortunately, the theoretical results from
Keys
et al. 2019
buttress Algorithm

under Zangwill’s global convergence theorem when the penalty coefficient
\rho
is fixed
(

Luenberger 1984
)
.
First,
S_{k}^{\mathrm{row}}
is closed for any choice of
k
and nonempty.
Second, the
\epsilon
-insensitive loss
f(\boldsymbol{B})
is coercive whenever the predictor matrix
\boldsymbol{X}
has full rank.
This implies the penalized loss
f_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B})
is also coercive.
Finally, observe that the surrogate generated by distance majorization is related to the possibly multivalued proximal mapping
(
Parikh 2014
)
by

\displaystyle\bigcup_{\boldsymbol{P}_{m}\in P_{S_{k}^{\mathrm{row}}}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m})}~\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits_{\boldsymbol{B}}\left[f(\boldsymbol{B})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}-\boldsymbol{P}_{m}\|^{2}\right]=\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\rho^{-1}f}\left[P_{S_{k}^{\mathrm{row}}}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m})\right].

Because set projections preserve closedness, it suffices to show that the projection operator
P_{S_{k}^{\mathrm{row}}}(\boldsymbol{B})
is single-valued almost everywhere to satisfy the hypothesis of Proposition 8 from
Keys
et al. 2019
.
For fixed
\rho
and
k
this guarantees that all limit points of the proximal distance iterates are stationary points,
\{\boldsymbol{B}:\nabla f_{\rho}(\boldsymbol{B})=\boldsymbol{0}\}
.
In fact, convexity of the loss
f(\boldsymbol{B})
guarantees that every surrogate

is
\rho
-strongly convex.
The stronger conclusion of Proposition 11 from
Keys
et al. 2019
applies and allays concerns regarding selection of projected points when
P_{S_{k}^{\mathrm{row}}}(\boldsymbol{B})
is set-valued.

1.4.4
Tuning the Sparsity Level

Because a causal or optimal feature set is rarely known in advance, the effectiveness of VDA hinges on its ability to generate candidate feature sets and select an optimal model.
Cross validation is an obvious way to evaluate the classification error of candidate models.
Before discussing implementation specifics, we note that the sparsity set
S_{k}^{\mathrm{row}}
functions as a discrete hyperparameter determining model selection.
Changing the size of the target feature set
k
fundamentally changes the nature of the optimization problem.
In contrast, perturbing the penalty constant in a lasso penalized model merely shifts or perturbs parameter estimates along a solution path
(
Hastie
et al. 2001
)
.
Model size is only indirectly determined by the penalty constant.
The main benefits of the sparsity set approach are that it (a) allows us to formulate feature selection problems directly in terms of model size, (b) relies on simple projections rather than complex combinatorial searches to guide solutions towards an optimal or nearly optimal sparsity pattern, and (c) ultimately delivers models with excellent statistical properties.

For a given dataset
\{(\boldsymbol{y}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}

The former subset is further split into a

, used to estimate a solution path spanning several model sizes

, used to identify an optimal model size.
Samples in the latter

never appear in the cross validation procedure.
Given any set

\displaystyle\mathrm{CV}_{\mathcal{T}}(\boldsymbol{B},k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{F|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{f=1}^{F}\sum_{(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathcal{T}}1\{\boldsymbol{y}\neq\hat{\boldsymbol{y}}_{f}\}

Here the

This metric allows us to choose an optimal

For each

The optimal

Because estimates of cross validation error and therefore the selected model are sensitive to the choice of training and validation sets, we repeat the cross validation procedure several times by shuffling samples in the cross validation subset.

Extension to Nonlinear VDA

Linear VDA for feature selection easily generalizes to nonlinear VDA based on reproducing kernels.
Construction of decision boundaries in sparse nonlinear VDA operates by selecting representative samples called support vectors in the SVM literature
(
Wu and Wu 2012
)
.
Here we focus on defining and solving the optimization problem generated by nonlinear VDA.
The goal is to estimate a classification vector
\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})=[\psi_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}),\psi_{2}(\boldsymbol{x}),\ldots,\psi_{c-1}(\boldsymbol{x})]
by minimizing the objective
(2n)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\max\{\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\|-\epsilon,0\}^{2}
.
Given a reproducing kernel
W(\cdot,\cdot)
,
\boldsymbol{\psi}(\boldsymbol{x})
assumes the functional form

\displaystyle\psi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{ij}W(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{i})+b_{0j},\qquad j=1,2,\ldots,(c-1)

in predicting the vertex components from a feature vector
\boldsymbol{x}
.
Model parameters
\boldsymbol{B}=(b_{ij})
, including the intercepts
b_{0j}
, can be estimated by minimizing by criterion (
3
) with
\boldsymbol{W}
substituted for
\boldsymbol{X}
.
Nonlinear prediction is achieved through through the introduction of the reproducing kernel.
The underlying model is still linear in its parameters.

3.1
Sparse Signal Recovery

Model selection is likely to succeed only when (a) classes are sufficiently distinct, with between-class variation large compared to within-class variation, and (b) sample size is sufficiently large to overcome noise and masking.
We now demonstrate linear VDA’s viability in these ideal circumstances.
Based on the simulation model described in Section

We also vary the within-class variance

and indirectly modulate between-class variance through an intermediate parameter

The number of causal features

is kept fixed throughout each scenario.
Because the simulation procedure can generate highly correlated causal features, the number

should be viewed as an upper bound on estimated model size.

For a given dataset, we estimate an initial solution by minimizing the regularized model (

In varying

Thus, feature selection proceeds from a fully dense model

\boldsymbol{B}_{0}

In each case, estimating
\boldsymbol{B}_{k-1}

\boldsymbol{B}_{k}
as a warm-start.

4
Discussion and Concluding Remarks

The novelty of the current version of VDA stems from the application of
\epsilon
-insensitive loss, a new majorization, distance penalties, and projection onto sparsity sets.
Once we majorize the loss, our algorithm reduces to the classical proximal update

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm prox}\nolimits_{\rho^{-1}f}(\boldsymbol{P}_{m})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\rm argmin}\nolimits_{\boldsymbol{B}}\left[f(\boldsymbol{B})+\frac{\rho}{2}\|\boldsymbol{B}-\boldsymbol{P}_{m}\|^{2}\right];\quad\boldsymbol{P}_{m}\in P_{S_{k}^{\mathrm{row}}}(\boldsymbol{B}_{m}).

Computing this proximal update involves solving the nonlinear equations

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{W}_{\boldsymbol{B}}\boldsymbol{X}+\rho\boldsymbol{I})\boldsymbol{B}=\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{W}_{\boldsymbol{B}}\boldsymbol{Y}+\rho\boldsymbol{P}_{m},

where
\boldsymbol{W}_{\boldsymbol{B}}
is a diagonal matrix depending on the residuals
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
.
This structure suggests the standard iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS) strategy noticed by other authors in robust classification
(
Groenen
et al. 2008
;
van den Burg and
Groenen 2016
;
Nguyen and
McLachlan 2017
)
.
Sequential minimization via our surrogate (
4
) is effectively an IRLS algorithm for fitting VDA classifiers.

Our work has its limitations.
While our empirical results are satisfying, we have not characterized the statistical properties of our estimators. Notably, we still lack conditions implying the consistency of VDA
(
Lange and
Wu 2008
;
Wu and
Lange 2010
)
.
The simulation study in Section
3.1
makes the case that our model estimates successfully reduce mean squared error (Figure
1
).
Statistical theory would be especially helpful in understanding the stability of model selection under cross validation.
Figure
3
suggests that VDA is strongly biased towards selecting parsimonious models on datasets believed to have a small number of informative features.
Unfortunately, cross validation replicates often yield candidate models with different numbers of active features.
Dense models can be recovered, as illustrated by the prostate example summarized in Figure
3
.
Another limitation is that only one or a few driving features may be selected out of a cluster of highly correlated features.
This tendency may be beneficial in finding causal features in biological models.
Finally, our code implementation only relies on BLAS calls to exploit parallelism in linear algebra operations.
Our algorithms may have additional parallel structure gone unnoticed.

Our VDA benchmarks do not report computational times required for solving a single penalized problem or running cross validation.
Without supporting theory, it seems premature to pit VDA against the wide array of readily available software for classification.
Anecdotally, cumulative VDA fitting times (across every fold, model size, and cross validation replicate) range from about 5 seconds for the iris, zoo, lymphography data to several minutes for the splice, letters, colon, and lymphoma data.
The most time consuming dataset to fit was the HAR example, which required
>7
hours to generate 50 cross validation replicates with 5 folds each, or about
8.4
minutes per replicate on average.
These observations are encouraging, particularly in the more interesting case of sparse linear VDA.
While our model selection technique permits flexibility in nonlinear decision boundaries, we find that fitting nonlinear VDA classifiers is noticeably slower than linear VDA.
The additional computational cost and loss of interpretability in nonlinear VDA is unjustified in cases where a linear classifier is strictly better (see for example the zoo benchmarks in Tables
1
and
2
).
The SVD-based variant of our software slows noticeably with a large number of samples.
Additional computational tricks, beyond parallel linear algebra operations, are needed to scale our VDA algorithms to datasets with sample sizes
n>10^{5}
.
Mature classification packages such as LIBSVM
(
Chang and
Lin 2011
)
may provide insight in overcoming computing limitations.

Repeated cross validation is not the only technique for evaluating candidate models.
For instance, the method of
stability selection
described by
Meinshausen and
Bühlmann 2010
is attractive for its theoretical finite-sample guarantees in controlling family-wise errors.
Alternatively,
selecting features in groups
can help overcome multicollinearity
(
Alexander and
Lange 2011
)
.
Ideally group selection should also control candidate model sizes.
Without going into details, it is possible to project onto sets that limit the number of groups selected and the number of features selected per group.
The downside of doubly sparse cross validation is that involves searching over a 2-dimensional tuning-constant grid.
The
percentile lasso

(
Roberts and
Nowak 2014
)
is another attractive device for stabilizing model selection.
Our repeated cross validation procedure, similar to the percentile lasso, attempts to control variability by using the median of optimal model sizes across replicates.
The distributions reported in Figure
3
suggest that adapting feature selection to account for the variety of models generated by replicate cross validations may, in fact, improve credible intervals.
Incorporating a Bayesian perspective into sparse VDA might be based on the the peaks in Figure
3
, but again model selection is apt to become even more time consuming.

In conclusion, we have demonstrated both the prediction accuracy and the feature selection capabilities of our modified VDA algorithms across a range of simulated and real datasets.
Our numerical examples confirm the promise of incorporating feature selection into VDA with distance to constraint-set penalties.
Combining our estimation procedures with cross validation overcomes the need to specify a particular model size.
Replicating cross validation further improves the reliability of feature selection with sparse VDA and provides a straightforward mechanism for honest reporting.
In the case of kernel VDA, our proposed methods allow for the simultaneous selection of the support vectors defining the decision boundaries separating multiple classes.
Our future work on VDA will seek to address gaps in theory, the caveats of repeated cross validation, and the extension of VDA to clustering problems in analogy with SVM-based clustering.

A.1
Directly minimizing surrogate (

)

According to the orthogonality relation
\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}=\boldsymbol{I}_{r}
and Woodbury’s formula, the required inverse is

\displaystyle[n^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}+\rho\boldsymbol{I}_{p}]^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho^{-1}\left[\boldsymbol{I}_{p}-\rho^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}(n\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-2}+\rho^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V})^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho^{-1}\left[\boldsymbol{I}_{p}-\rho^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}(n\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-2}+\rho^{-1}\boldsymbol{I}_{r})^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho^{-1}\left[\boldsymbol{I}_{p}-n^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}(\rho\boldsymbol{I}+n^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}\right].

Multiplying the above inverse by
\rho\boldsymbol{P}_{m}
yields

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{m}-n^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}(n^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}+\rho\boldsymbol{I}_{r})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}_{m}.

Likewise multiplying the above inverse by
n^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}
gives

\displaystyle n^{-1}\rho^{-1}\left[\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}-n^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}(n^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}+\rho\boldsymbol{I}_{r})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}\boldsymbol{V}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\right]\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{-1}\rho^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}\left[\boldsymbol{I}_{r}-n^{-1}(n^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}+\rho\boldsymbol{I}_{r})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}\right]\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{-1}\rho^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}(n^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}+\rho\boldsymbol{I}_{r})^{-1}\left[(n^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}+\rho\boldsymbol{I}_{r})-n^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}\right]\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}(n^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}+\rho\boldsymbol{I}_{r})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}.

Summing these two results leads to the desired update

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{B}_{m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{P}_{m}+n^{-1}\boldsymbol{V}(n^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}+\rho\boldsymbol{I}_{r})^{-1}\left[\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\boldsymbol{U}^{\top}\boldsymbol{Z}_{m}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{2}\boldsymbol{V}^{\top}\boldsymbol{P}_{m}\right].

Appendix B
Dataset Descriptions and Provenance

B.1
Preprocessing

Throughout each example we standardize each feature vector
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
to have mean
\boldsymbol{0}
and unit variance by estimating sample means
\boldsymbol{\mu}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and variances
\boldsymbol{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^{2}
, where operations are applied element-wise.
We then apply a
Z
-score transformation
\boldsymbol{x}\mapsto(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})/\boldsymbol{\sigma}
.
Location and scale estimates are based on a
training
subset only, and the
Z
-score transformation is applied to all subsets in cross validation.
In the nonlinear case, we estimate the variance in distances between dissimilar samples (that is, when true class labels are distinct) using the median and set a scale parameter
\gamma=13/10\times\mathrm{median}
for radial basis functions (RBF) kernels.

For real-world datasets we take care to drop missing data.
In addition, we make the choice to drop any non-numeric features (e.g. categorical data)
that are not already coded
.
We note this in each description where applicable.

B.2
Gaussian Clouds (clouds)

As described by
Wu and Wu 2012
, we simulate six Gaussian clouds with centers
(x,y)
equally spaced along the unit circle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

\theta=\begin{cases}0\,\text{or}\,\pi&\text{for class 1},\\
\frac{\pi}{3}\,\text{or}\,\frac{4\pi}{3}&\text{for class 2},\\
\frac{2\pi}{3}\,\text{or}\,\frac{5\pi}{3}&\text{for class 3},\end{cases}\quad\text{with}\quad(x,y)=(\cos(\theta),\sin(\theta)).

Each class has equal probability and centers are sampled uniformly conditional on the chosen class.
Points about a given cloud are simulated with variance-covariance matrix
\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{2\times 2}
.
Thus, higher variability increases class overlap and hence classification difficulty.
We set
\sigma=0.25
and split the data into training, validation, and testing subsets of sizes 200, 50, and 1000, respectively, in 5-fold cross validation.

B.3
Nested Circles (circles)

The recipe described by
Wu and Wu 2012
samples two features from the circle
\{(x,y):x^{2}+y^{2}<c\}
and assigns them to one of
c
classes.
The intermediate values
r=\lceil x^{2}+y^{2}\rceil
and
r^{\prime}
are used to simulate class assignment via the rule

\text{class}=\begin{cases}r&\text{with probability}\,p\\
r^{\prime}&\text{with probability}\,1-p.\end{cases}

Here
\lceil\cdot\rceil
is the ceiling function,
r^{\prime}
is any class in
\{1,2,\ldots,c\}
other than
r
which is sampled uniformly, and
1-p
is the Bayes error controlling the difficulty of classification.
Our benchmark takes
c=3
and
p=0.8
to simulate a dataset with
1250
samples.
We split the data into training, validation, and testing subsets of sizes 200, 50, and 1000, respectively, in 5-fold cross validation.

B.4
Waveform (waveform)

This dataset is based on convex combinations of triangular waveforms that was originally presented by
Breiman
et al. 1984
and featured in Example 12.7.1 of
The Elements of Statistical Learning

(
Hastie
et al. 2001
)
.
We simulate
p=21
features across
c=3
classes using the recipe

\displaystyle X_{j}

\displaystyle=Uh_{1}(j)+(1-U)h_{2}(j)+\epsilon_{j}

\displaystyle\text{for class 1},

\displaystyle X_{j}

\displaystyle=Uh_{1}(j)+(1-U)h_{3}(j)+\epsilon_{j}

\displaystyle\text{for class 2},\qquad j=1,2,\ldots,21

\displaystyle X_{j}

\displaystyle=Uh_{2}(j)+(1-U)h_{3}(j)+\epsilon_{j}

\displaystyle\text{for class 3},

where
U
is a uniform deviate on
[0,1]
and the
\epsilon_{j}
are independent standard normal deviates.
The triangular waveforms
h_{1}(j)=\max\{6-|j-11|,0\}
,
h_{2}(j)=h_{1}(j-4)
, and
h_{3}(j)=h_{1}(j+4)
are centered at
j=11
,
j=15
, and
j=7
, respectively.
A total of 1375 samples are simulated with training, validation, and testing subsets of sizes 300, 75, and 1000, respectively, in
5
-fold cross validation.

B.5
Sparse Recovery Model (10clouds)

This recipe is adapted from the
clouds
example and a recipe from
Wang and
Shen 2007
(see Section 3.1).
Given a target number of features
p
and classes
c
, we simulate features by sampling from
\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{p\times p})
, where

\displaystyle\theta_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{3}+\frac{2(\ell-1)\pi}{c},

\displaystyle a_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\cos\theta_{\ell},&\ell~\text{is even}\\
\sin\theta_{\ell},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

\displaystyle\mu_{j}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}d\times\mathop{\rm sgn}\nolimits(a_{\ell}),&\text{for}~j=1,2,\ldots,c\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},~\text{and}

\displaystyle\Sigma_{ij}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sigma,&i=j~\text{and}~j=1,2,\ldots,c\\
1/\sigma,&i=j~\text{and}~j>c\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

For each sample
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
we simulate the response
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=\boldsymbol{B}^{\top}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
where each row
\boldsymbol{b}_{j}
of
\boldsymbol{B}
is assigned

\boldsymbol{b}_{j}=\begin{cases}c^{-1}\mathop{\rm sgn}\nolimits(a_{\ell})\boldsymbol{v}_{j},&j=1,2,\ldots,c\\
\boldsymbol{0},&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

and each
\boldsymbol{v}_{j}
is one of
c
vertices assigned to classes.
These choices increase the likelihood that the simulated dataset contains close to
c
informative features.
Large values of parameter
d
induce separation between classes whereas
\sigma
controls variability within each class.
