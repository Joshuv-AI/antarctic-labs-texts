Monomial identities in the Weyl algebra
Darij Grinberg, Tom Roby, Stephan Wagner, Mei Yin

Abstract
Abstract. Motivated by a question and some enumerative conjectures of Richard Stanley, we explore the equivalence classes of words in the Weyl algebra, 𝐤 ⟨ D , U ∣ D U − U D = 1 ⟩ \mathbf{k}\left<D,U\mid DU-UD=1\right> . We show that each class is generated by the swapping of adjacent balanced subwords , i.e., those which have the same number of D D ’s as U U ’s, and give several other characterizations, as well as a linear-time algorithm for equivalence checking. Armed with this, we deduce several enumerative results about such equivalence classes and their sizes. We extend these results to the class of c c -Dyck words, where every prefix has at least c c times as many U U ’s as D D ’s. We also connect these results to previous work on bond percolation and rook theory, and generalize them to some other algebras. Keywords: Weyl algebra, words, lattice paths, rook placements, Ferrers boards, Dyck words, monoid kernel, bond percolation, PBW bases, down-up algebra, noncommutative algebra, rings, combinatorics, finite fields. MSC 2020 Classifications: 12H05, 16S32, 05A15, 68R15.

For instance, from

if and only if

from

2.6
Main result: Equivalent descriptions of Weyl equivalence

Everything is now in place to state our main result, which gives several (necessary and sufficient) criteria for when two words
u,v\in\mathcal{M}
have the same image under
\phi
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
u
and
v
be two words in
\mathcal{M}
.
Then, the following seven statements are equivalent:

•

\mathcal{S}_{1}
: We have
\phi\left(u\right)=\phi\left(v\right)
.

•

\mathcal{S}_{2}
: The elements
\phi\left(u\right)
and

\phi\left(v\right)
act equally on the polynomial ring
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
.
(That is, we have

\left(\phi\left(u\right)\right)\left(p\right)=\left(\phi\left(v\right)\right)\left(p\right)

for each polynomial
p\in\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
.
Note that the action of
\mathcal{W}
on
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]

was defined in Subsection
2.1
.)

•

\mathcal{S}_{3}
: The words
u
and
v
have the same final
height and satisfy
H_{\operatorname{NE}}\left(u,z\right)=H_{\operatorname{NE}}\left(v,z\right)
.

•

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{3}
: The words
u
and
v
have the same final
height and satisfy
H_{\operatorname{SE}}\left(u,z\right)=H_{\operatorname{SE}}\left(v,z\right)
.

•

\mathcal{S}_{4}
: The words
u
and
v
have the same final
height and satisfy
H\left(u,z\right)=H\left(v,z\right)
.

•

\mathcal{S}_{5}
: We have
u\overset{\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}v
.

•

\mathcal{S}_{6}
: We have
u\overset{\operatorname*{flip}}{\sim}v
.

In particular, this shows that the two relations
\overset{\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}
and
\overset{\operatorname*{flip}}{\sim}
are the same.

Remark 2.2
.

Criterion
\mathcal{S}_{3}
(or
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{3}
) can also be turned into an efficient algorithm to decide whether or not
\phi\left(u\right)=\phi\left(v\right)
, which requires linear time and space in the length of
u
and
v
:

We use an array
A
with both positive and negative indices.
Start at
i=0
and read the word
u
. When a value of
i
is reached for the first time,
A[i]
is initialized as
0
. In particular,
A[0]
is initialized as
0
right at the beginning.
Each time the letter
U
is read, increase
A[i]
by
1
and increase
i
by
1
; else decrease
i
by
1
(and leave the values of the array unchanged). The final value of
i
is the final height of
u
, whereas the final entries of the array are the coefficients of
H_{\operatorname{NE}}\left(u,z\right)
.
Once
u
has been read completely, do the same with
v
(starting at
i=0
again), but decrease
A[i]
whenever the letter
U
occurs. Condition
\mathcal{S}_{3}
is clearly only satisfied if the final heights of
u
and
v
are the same and the array at the end consists entirely of zeros. If a value of
i
is reached in the second part that was not reached in the first part, or if an entry of the array becomes negative during the second stage of the algorithm, one can stop immediately:
\phi\left(u\right)\neq\phi\left(v\right)
in this case.
The length of the array is thus bounded by the length of
u
. Even more, since the sum of the entries of the array is bounded by the length of word
u
, a linear total number of bits is sufficient for all entries of the array.

In Section
8
, we will add some more equivalent statements to the list in Theorem
2.1
, albeit under the additional assumption that
u
and
v
have the same number of
U
’s and the same number of
D
’s.

The following result is a curious consequence of Theorem
2.1
(although we will prove it first and then use it in the proof of Theorem
2.1
).

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{M}
be a balanced word. Then,

\phi\left(u\right)=\phi\left(\omega\left(u\right)\right)
and

u\overset{\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}\omega\left(u\right)
.

3
Basic formulas for the Weyl algebra action

The proof of our main result requires significant build-up and preparation.
We begin with a closer look at the Weyl algebra
\mathcal{W}
and its action on the polynomial ring
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The action of the Weyl algebra
\mathcal{W}
on the
polynomial ring
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
is faithful: That is, if two
elements
a,b\in\mathcal{W}
satisfy
a\left(p\right)=b\left(p\right)
for all

p\in\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
, then
a=b
.

Proof 3.2
.

This is a folklore result, and can be easily derived from facts in the literature.
For instance,
[
Milici17
, Theorem 5.11]
(applied to
n=1
) shows that the
\mathbf{k}
-algebra
D\left(1\right)
of differential operators on the polynomial ring
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
is isomorphic to the Weyl algebra
\mathbf{k}\left<D,U\mid DU-UD=1\right>=\mathcal{W}
.
The actual proof of
[
Milici17
, Theorem 5.11]
shows that the
\mathbf{k}
-algebra morphism
\mathcal{W}\to D\left(1\right)
that sends
D
and
U
to
\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}
and the multiplication by
x
is injective.
But this morphism is precisely the action of
\mathcal{W}
on
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
(except that its target has been restricted to
D\left(1\right)
).
Thus, its injectivity means that the action is faithful.
This proves Lemma
3.1
.

Remark 3.3
.

The Weyl algebra
\mathcal{W}
acts not only on the polynomial ring
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
, but also on the rings of Laurent polynomials
\mathbf{k}\left[x,x^{-1}\right]
, of formal power series
\mathbf{k}\left[\left[x\right]\right]
, of formal Laurent series
\mathbf{k}\left(\left(x\right)\right)
, and (if
\mathbf{k}=\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbf{k}=\mathbb{C}
) of infinitely differentiable functions
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}\left(\mathbf{k}\right)
.
In each case, the action is faithful (since the polynomial ring
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
embeds into each of these rings),
and thus all our results still apply.

Each word
u\in\mathcal{M}
is mapped by
\phi
to an element of the Weyl
algebra
\mathcal{W}
, which in turn acts on the polynomial ring

\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
. Our next goal is to give an explicit formula
for this action in terms of a diagonal path
\mathbf{p}
that has
u
as its
reading word. For the sake of simplicity, we extend the action of

\mathcal{W}
from the polynomial ring
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
to the
Laurent polynomial ring
\mathbf{k}\left[x,x^{-1}\right]
(so that we
don’t have to worry about possible negative exponents on a power of
x
).

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\mathbf{p}=\left(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\right)

be a diagonal path. Let
h_{i}:=\operatorname{ht}\left(p_{i}\right)
for
each
i\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,k\right\}
. Then, for each
s\in\mathbb{Z}
,
we have

\left(\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)\right)\right)\left(x^{s}\right)=\left(\prod_{p_{i}\text{ is an SE-step of }\mathbf{p}}\left(s+h_{k}-h_{i+1}\right)\right)\cdot x^{s+h_{k}-h_{0}}.

(8)

Example 3.5
.

If the last steps of
\mathbf{p}
are

\cdots p_{k-4}\searrow p_{k-3}\nearrow p_{k-2}\nearrow p_{k-1}\searrow p_{k}
,
then
\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)=\cdots DUUD
and thus any
s\in\mathbb{N}
satisfies

\displaystyle\left(\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)\right)\right)\left(x^{s}\right)

\displaystyle=\cdots DUUDx^{s}=\cdots\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}xx\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}x^{s}}_{=sx^{s-1}}=s\cdots\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}x\underbrace{xx^{s-1}}_{=x^{s}}

\displaystyle=s\cdots\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\underbrace{xx^{s}}_{=x^{s+1}}=s\cdots\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}x^{s+1}}_{=\left(s+1\right)x^{s}}=s\left(s+1\right)\cdots x^{s}.

The two factors
s
and
s+1
that we have found correspond precisely to the
two SE-steps
p_{k-1}
and
p_{k-4}
of
\mathbf{p}
. Of course, further
SE-steps in
\mathbf{p}
will contribute more such factors.

The above example illustrates where Proposition
3.4
comes from:
In general, we can compute
\left(\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)\right)\right)\left(x^{s}\right)
in the same way,
decomposing
\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)\right)
into a product of
D
’s and
U
’s (which correspond, respectively, to
SE-steps and NE-steps of
\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)
), and
letting each of these
D
’s and
U
’s act on the monomial
x^{s}
sequentially
(starting with the last one). The letter
U
acts as multiplication by
x
and
thus sends
x^{k}
to
x^{k+1}
, whereas the letter
D
acts as
\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}
and thus sends
x^{k}
to
kx^{k-1}
. Thus, in total,
the exponent on our monomial
x^{s}
is incremented once for each
U
(that
is, for each NE-step of
\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)
) and
decremented once for each
D
(that is, for each SE-step of
\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)
), so that it
becomes
s+h_{k}-h_{0}
at the end (an easy consequence of (
2
)).
The factors accumulating in front of the monomial are precisely the
k
’s
coming from the
D
’s, and thus are precisely the
s+h_{k}-h_{i+1}

corresponding to the SE-steps
p_{i}
of
\mathbf{p}
, since it is these
SE-steps that turn into letters
D
in
\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)\right)
(and the
current degree of the monomial at the time when
D
is applied is exactly

s+h_{k}-h_{i+1}
). The final result is precisely the right hand side of
(
8
).

This argument can be easily translated into a rigorous proof of Proposition
3.4
, which can be found in the detailed version of this paper.

The following is a version of Proposition
3.4
in which the
reading word
\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)
additionally
undergoes the “toggle-and-reverse” anti-automorphism
\omega
:

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\mathbf{p}=\left(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\right)

be a diagonal path. Let
h_{i}:=\operatorname{ht}\left(p_{i}\right)
for
each
i\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,k\right\}
. Then, for each
s\in\mathbb{Z}
,
we have

\left(\omega\left(\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)\right)\right)\right)\left(x^{s}\right)=\left(\prod_{p_{i}\text{
is an NE-step of }\mathbf{p}}\left(s+h_{0}-h_{i}\right)\right)\cdot x^{s+h_{0}-h_{k}}.

The proof of this is similar to that of Proposition
3.4
; again, we refer to the detailed version for the details.

As a consequence of Proposition
3.6

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\mathbf{p}=\left(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\right)
and
\mathbf{q}=\left(q_{0},q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}\right)
be
two diagonal paths with the same initial height that satisfy
\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)\right)=\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{q}\right)\right)
. Then, the final heights
of
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
are equal, and we have

\displaystyle\left\{\operatorname{ht}\left(p_{i}\right)\ \mid\ p_{i}\text{ is an
NE-step of }\mathbf{p}\right\}_{\operatorname{multiset}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\operatorname{ht}\left(q_{i}\right)\ \mid\ q_{i}\text{ is an
NE-step of }\mathbf{q}\right\}_{\operatorname{multiset}}.

(9)

Proof 3.8
.

Let
h_{i}:=\operatorname{ht}\left(p_{i}\right)
for each
i\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,k\right\}
. Let
g_{i}:=\operatorname{ht}\left(q_{i}\right)

for each
i\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,m\right\}
. Note that
h_{0}=g_{0}
(since

\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
have the same initial height).

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
be high enough to be larger than all numbers
h_{i}-h_{0}
for all NE-steps
p_{i}
of
\mathbf{p}
and also larger than all
numbers
g_{i}-g_{0}
for all NE-steps
q_{i}
of
\mathbf{q}
.

Then, Proposition
3.6
yields

\left(\omega\left(\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)\right)\right)\right)\left(x^{s}\right)=\left(\prod_{p_{i}\text{
is an NE-step of }\mathbf{p}}\left(s+h_{0}-h_{i}\right)\right)\cdot x^{s+h_{0}-h_{k}}

and similarly

\left(\omega\left(\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{q}\right)\right)\right)\right)\left(x^{s}\right)=\left(\prod_{q_{i}\text{
is an NE-step of }\mathbf{q}}\left(s+g_{0}-g_{i}\right)\right)\cdot x^{s+g_{0}-g_{m}}.

But the left hand sides of these two equalities are equal, since
\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)\right)=\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{q}\right)\right)
. Thus, the right hand
sides are also equal. In other words, we have

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{p_{i}\text{ is an NE-step of }\mathbf{p}}\left(s+h_{0}-h_{i}\right)\right)\cdot x^{s+h_{0}-h_{k}}

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{q_{i}\text{ is an NE-step of }\mathbf{q}}\left(s+g_{0}-g_{i}\right)\right)\cdot x^{s+g_{0}-g_{m}}.

(10)

The products on both sides of this equality are nonzero (since
s
is larger
than all numbers
h_{i}-h_{0}
for all NE-steps
p_{i}
of
\mathbf{p}
and
also larger than all numbers
g_{i}-g_{0}
for all NE-steps
q_{i}
of

\mathbf{q}
). Thus, (
10
) entails that the exponents
s+h_{0}-h_{k}
and

s+g_{0}-g_{m}
are equal, and therefore we conclude that
h_{0}-h_{k}=g_{0}-g_{m}
. Since
h_{0}=g_{0}
, this entails
h_{k}=g_{m}
. In other
words, the final heights of
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
are equal.

Since the two exponents
s+h_{0}-h_{k}
and
s+g_{0}-g_{m}
in
(
10
) are equal, we obtain

\prod_{p_{i}\text{ is an NE-step of }\mathbf{p}}\left(s+h_{0}-h_{i}\right)=\prod_{q_{i}\text{ is an NE-step of }\mathbf{q}}\left(s+g_{0}-g_{i}\right)

(11)

by comparing coefficients in (
10
). Now forget that we fixed
s
.
We just proved the equality (
11
)
for each sufficiently high
s\in\mathbb{N}
. But this equality is a polynomial
identity in
s
, and thus must hold formally (since it holds for each
sufficiently high
s\in\mathbb{N}
). In other words, it must hold
in the polynomial ring
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
if we replace
s
by
x
.

Now recall that
h_{0}=g_{0}
. So (
11
) still holds
as a polynomial identity if we substitute
x
for
s+h_{0}=s+g_{0}

on both sides to transform the identity into

\prod_{p_{i}\text{ is an NE-step of }\mathbf{p}}\left(x-h_{i}\right)=\prod_{q_{i}\text{ is an NE-step of }\mathbf{q}}\left(x-g_{i}\right).

Since
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
is a unique factorization domain, this
yields that

\displaystyle\left\{h_{i}\ \mid\ p_{i}\text{ is an NE-step of }\mathbf{p}\right\}_{\operatorname{multiset}}

\displaystyle=\left\{g_{i}\ \mid\ q_{i}\text{ is an NE-step of }\mathbf{q}\right\}_{\operatorname{multiset}}.

This is exactly (
9
) since
h_{i}=\operatorname{ht}\left(p_{i}\right)
and
g_{i}=\operatorname{ht}\left(q_{i}\right)
for all respective

completes the proof of Proposition

shown that the final heights of
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
u
and
v
be two words in
\mathcal{M}
such
that
\phi\left(u\right)=\phi\left(v\right)
. Then,
u
and
v

contain the same number of
D
’s and the same number of
U
’s.

Proof 3.10
.

Let
\mathbf{p}=\left(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\right)
be the standard path of
u
, and let
\mathbf{q}=\left(q_{0},q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}\right)
be the standard path of
v
.
The paths
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
both start at
\left(0,0\right)
(by the definition of a standard path), and thus have the same initial height (namely,
0
).
Moreover, their reading words are
\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)=u
and
\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{q}\right)=v
(since
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
are the standard paths of
u
and
v
).
Thus, from
\phi\left(u\right)=\phi\left(v\right)
, we obtain

\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)\right)=\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{q}\right)\right)
.
Hence, Proposition
3.7
yields
that the final heights of
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
are equal, and that

\displaystyle\left\{\operatorname{ht}\left(p_{i}\right)\ \mid\ p_{i}\text{ is an
NE-step of }\mathbf{p}\right\}_{\operatorname{multiset}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\operatorname{ht}\left(q_{i}\right)\ \mid\ q_{i}\text{ is an
NE-step of }\mathbf{q}\right\}_{\operatorname{multiset}}.

The latter equality yields (in particular) that the number of NE-steps of

\mathbf{p}
equals the number of NE-steps of
\mathbf{q}
. Since the NE-steps
of
\mathbf{p}
are in bijection with the
U

\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)=u
, and similarly for
\mathbf{q}
, we can rewrite this as
follows: The number of
U
’s in
u
equals the number of
U
’s in
v
. In
other words,
u
and
v
contain the same number of
U
’s.

It remains to see that the words
u
and

contain the same number of
D
’s.
But (

) shows that

\left(\text{final height of }\mathbf{p}\right)-\left(\text{initial height of }\mathbf{p}\right)=\left(\text{\# of $U$'s in }\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)\right)-\left(\text{\# of $D$'s in }\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)\right)

and

\left(\text{final height of }\mathbf{q}\right)-\left(\text{initial height of }\mathbf{q}\right)=\left(\text{\# of $U$'s in }\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{q}\right)\right)-\left(\text{\# of $D$'s in }\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{q}\right)\right).

The left hand sides of these two equalities are equal (since the paths

\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
have the same initial height and the same final

\left(\text{\# of $U$'s in }\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)\right)-\left(\text{\# of $D$'s in }\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)\right)=\left(\text{\# of $U$'s in }\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{q}\right)\right)-\left(\text{\# of $D$'s in }\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{q}\right)\right).

In other words,

\left(\text{\# of $U$'s in }u\right)-\left(\text{\# of $D$'s in

(since

and

\left(\text{\# of
$U$'s in }u\right)=\left(\text{\# of $U$'s in }v\right)

and

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{M}
be a balanced word. Let

\left(\phi\left(u\right)\right)\left(x^{s}\right)=\lambda_{u,s}x^{s}

Proof 3.12
.

Let
\mathbf{p}=\left(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{k}\right)
be the standard path of

.
Both the initial height and the final height of

are

(since

Thus, the claim follows easily from Proposition

.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
a
and
b
be two balanced words in
\mathcal{M}
.
Then,
\phi\left(a\right)\phi\left(b\right)=\phi\left(b\right)\phi\left(a\right)

Proof 3.14

(First proof.)
.

Lemma
3.11
shows that the elements
\phi\left(a\right)
and
\phi\left(b\right)
of
\mathcal{W}
act on the
\mathcal{W}
-module
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
as diagonal matrices.
Since diagonal matrices commute, we thus conclude that the actions of
\phi\left(a\right)
and
\phi\left(b\right)
on
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
commute.
Hence, the elements
\phi\left(a\right)
and
\phi\left(b\right)
themselves commute (since the
\mathcal{W}
-module
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
is faithful).

Proof 3.15

(Second proof.)
.

The following alternative proof has been suggested to us by Jörgen Backelin.
It shows that Lemma
3.13
is a disguised form of a classical result known already to Dixmier
[

]
.

We equip the Weyl algebra

\mathcal{W}
with a
\mathbb{Z}
-grading by deciding that its generators
U

and
D
be homogeneous of degrees
1
and
-1
, respectively. The
0
-th
graded component
\mathcal{W}_{0}
of
\mathcal{W}
is then spanned by the

\phi
.
In particular, both
\phi\left(a\right)
and
\phi\left(b\right)
belong to
\mathcal{W}_{0}
(since
a
and
b
are balanced words).

However, a result of Dixmier (
[
Dixmie68
, last equation in §3.2]
) says that the
\mathbf{k}
-algebra

\mathcal{W}_{0}
is generated by the single element
\phi\left(DU\right)
.
The proof of this result in
[
Dixmie68
]
is fairly easy: We abbreviate

\phi\left(D\right)
and
\phi\left(U\right)
as
\overline{D}
and

\overline{U}
. First it is shown that
\mathcal{W}
is spanned by the
elements of the form
\overline{D}^{i}\overline{U}^{j}
with
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
, which are homogeneous of respective degrees
j-i
. Therefore the

0
-th graded component
\mathcal{W}_{0}
is spanned by the elements of the
form
\overline{D}^{i}\overline{U}^{i}
with
i\in\mathbb{N}
. But each of the
latter elements can be rewritten as

\overline{D}^{i}\overline{U}^{i}=\left(\overline{D}\overline{U}\right)\left(\overline{D}\overline{U}+1\right)\left(\overline{D}\overline{U}+2\right)\cdots\left(\overline{D}\overline{U}+i-1\right)

(as can be proved by induction on
i
), which is clearly a polynomial in

\overline{D}\overline{U}=\phi\left(DU\right)
. Thus the algebra

\mathcal{W}_{0}
is generated by the single element
\phi\left(DU\right)
.

Now, the algebra
\mathcal{W}_{0}
is commutative (since we have just shown that it is generated by a single element).
Therefore, any two of its elements commute.
In particular,
\phi\left(a\right)
and
\phi\left(b\right)
commute
(since both
\phi\left(a\right)
and
\phi\left(b\right)
belong to
\mathcal{W}_{0}
).
In other words,
\phi\left(a\right)\phi\left(b\right)=\phi\left(b\right)\phi\left(a\right)
.
This proves Lemma
3.13
again.

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
u,v\in\mathcal{M}
be two words such that

u\overset{\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}v
. Then,
\phi\left(u\right)=\phi\left(v\right)
.

Proof 3.17
.

It suffices to show that if
p,q\in\mathcal{M}
are two words, and if
x,y\in\mathcal{M}
are two balanced words, then
\phi\left(pxyq\right)=\phi\left(pyxq\right)
.
But this follows from Lemma
3.13
(which yields
\phi\left(x\right)\phi\left(y\right)=\phi\left(y\right)\phi\left(x\right)
), since
\phi
is a monoid morphism.

4
Some words on words

Next, we take a closer look at some properties of the monoid
\mathcal{M}
of words.
We introduce some more terminology:

•

A word
w\in\mathcal{M}
is said to be
rising
if it has at least
as many
U
’s as it has
D
’s.

•

A word
w\in\mathcal{M}
is said to be
falling
if it has at least
as many
D
’s as it has
U
’s.

Thus, each word
w\in\mathcal{M}
is rising or falling or both. Moreover, the
balanced words
w\in\mathcal{M}
are exactly the words
w\in\mathcal{M}
that
are both rising and falling.

•

A
down-zig
means a word of the form
UD^{k}U
for some
k\geq 2
.

•

A rising word
w\in\mathcal{M}
is said to be
up-normal
if it
contains no down-zig as a factor.

For example, the rising word
UUDUDUDD
is up-normal, whereas the rising word

UUDDUU
is not (since it has the down-zig
UDDU=UD^{2}U
as a factor).

We could similarly define “up-zigs” and “down-normal words” (by
toggling each letter in down-zigs and up-normal words, respectively), but we
will have no need for them.

For what follows, we need a simple property of products in a monoid:

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
a
and
b
be two elements of a monoid
M
.
Let
w\in M
be any product of
a
’s and
b
’s ending with a
b
.
Then,
w
can be written in the form
a^{r_{1}}b\,a^{r_{2}}b\,\cdots a^{r_{h}}b

for some nonnegative integers
h
and
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{h}
. (Note that these integers are allowed to be
0
.)

Proof 4.2
.

Almost immediate. (See the detailed version of this paper.)

Proposition 4.3
.

Every up-normal word has the form

D^{a}\,(UD)^{r_{1}}U\,(UD)^{r_{2}}U\,\cdots\,(UD)^{r_{h}}U\,D^{b}

for some nonnegative integers
a,b,h
and
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{h}

(that is, a power of
D
, followed by a product of several factors of the form
(UD)^{r}U
, followed by a further power of
D
, where all powers are allowed to be empty).

Proof 4.4
.

If the word consists entirely of
D
’s, then this is trivial.
Otherwise, we first remove the initial and the final run of
D
’s (of length
a
and
b
respectively, both of which can also be
0
) from our word. The remaining word is still up-normal, but starts and ends with a
U
.

This remaining word therefore has no two consecutive
D
s, since any run of
D
s longer than a single
D
would create a down-zig factor (when combined with the last
U
before the run and the first
U
after it).
Thus, each
D
in this remaining word has to be preceded
by a
U
(since the word starts with
U
).
This allows us to decompose this word into a product of
UD
’s and
U
’s (for instance, by reading it from right to left, and pairing each
D
with the
U
that necessarily precedes it); this product ends with a
U
(since our word ends with a
U
).
Thus, our word can be written as

(UD)^{r_{1}}U\,(UD)^{r_{2}}U\,\cdots\,(UD)^{r_{h}}U

for some nonnegative integers
h
and
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{h}
(by Lemma
4.1
, applied to
a=UD
and
b=U
).
This proves Proposition
4.3
.

The converse of Proposition
4.3
also holds: Each rising word of the form shown in Proposition
4.3
is up-normal. The proof is nearly trivial, but we will not use this fact in the following.

Next, we observe a near-trivial symmetry of balanced commutations:

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
u
and
v
be two words in
\mathcal{M}
.
Then,
u\overset{\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}v
if and only if
\omega\left(u\right)\overset{\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}\omega\left(v\right)
.

Proof 4.6
.

The map
\omega
transforms a word by reversing it and toggling each letter
7
7

7

We recall: To
toggle
a letter means to replace it by the opposite letter (i.e., to replace a
U
by a
D
or a
D
by a
U
).
.
Clearly, both of these operations turn balanced factors of our word into balanced factors of the resulting word.
Thus, if a word
a
is obtained from a word
b
by a balanced commutation, then
\omega\left(a\right)
is obtained from
\omega\left(b\right)
by a balanced commutation as well.
The same must therefore hold for multiple balanced commutations applied in sequence.
In other words, if
u\overset{\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}v
, then
\omega\left(u\right)\overset{\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}\omega\left(v\right)
.
The converse holds for similar reasons (or can also be obtained by applying the preceding sentence to
\omega\left(u\right)
and
\omega\left(v\right)
instead of
u
and
v
, since
\omega\circ\omega=\operatorname{id}
).
Thus, Proposition
4.5
is proved.

Our main goal in this section is to prove the following proposition:

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{M}
be a rising word. Then, there
exists a unique up-normal word
t\in\mathcal{M}
such that

t\overset{\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}w
.

This up-normal word

will be called the

of

In order to prove Proposition

First, we show some simple identities that allow us to convert between the three height polynomials (height, NE-height and SE-height) of a diagonal path:

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\mathbf{p}
be any diagonal path starting at height
a
and ending at height
b
. We have

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{p},z)=(1+z)H_{\operatorname{NE}}(\mathbf{p},z)+\sum_{j\geq b}z^{j}-\sum_{j\geq a+1}z^{j}

(12)

and

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{p},z)=(1+z^{-1})H_{\operatorname{SE}}(\mathbf{p},z)+\sum_{j\geq a}z^{j}-\sum_{j\geq b+1}z^{j}.

(13)

(The infinite sums are formal Laurent series, but only finitely many addends survive the cancellation.)

Proof 4.9
.

We only prove (
12
), since the proof of (
13
) is completely analogous.
If the path
\mathbf{p}
has length
0
, then
a=b
as well as
H(\mathbf{p},z)=z^{a}=z^{b}
and
H_{\operatorname{NE}}(\mathbf{p},z)=H_{\operatorname{SE}}(\mathbf{p},z)=0
.
The identity clearly holds in this case.
Now proceed by induction, and let
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
be the diagonal path obtained by removing the last vertex from
\mathbf{p}
.
Then
H(\mathbf{p},z)=H(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},z)+z^{b}
, and the final height of
\mathbf{p}^{\prime}
is

if the last step of
\mathbf{p}
is an NE-step, and
b+1
otherwise.
In the former case, we have
H_{\operatorname{NE}}(\mathbf{p},z)=H_{\operatorname{NE}}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},z)+z^{b-1}
, and the induction hypothesis gives us

H(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},z)=(1+z)H_{\operatorname{NE}}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},z)+\sum_{j\geq b-1}z^{j}-\sum_{j\geq a+1}z^{j},

from which the desired statement follows by adding
z^{b}
on both sides (since
z^{b-1}+z^{b}=\left(1+z\right)z^{b-1}
).
In the latter case, we have
H_{\operatorname{NE}}(\mathbf{p},z)=H_{\operatorname{NE}}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},z)
, and the induction hypothesis gives us

H(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},z)=(1+z)H_{\operatorname{NE}}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},z)+\sum_{j\geq b+1}z^{j}-\sum_{j\geq a+1}z^{j}.

Again, we add
z^{b}
on both sides to obtain the desired identity. This completes the induction and thus the proof.

Remark 4.10
.

Let
\mathbf{p}
and

By definition, we have

covers all SE-steps, and

the final vertex. This can also be used to derive (

Recall that the height polynomial

of a word

of its standard path

.
Thus, Lemma

can be applied to words:

Let

be a word with final height

\displaystyle H(w,z)=(1+z)H_{\operatorname{NE}}(w,z)+\sum_{j\geq b}z^{j}-\sum_{j\geq 1}z^{j}

\displaystyle H(w,z)=(1+z^{-1})H_{\operatorname{SE}}(w,z)+\sum_{j\geq 0}z^{j}-\sum_{j\geq b+1}z^{j}.

Let
\mathbf{p}
be the standard path of

Then, the initial height of

As we said, height polynomials of words are a particular case of height polynomials of diagonal paths.
But the general case can easily be reduced to this particular case:

Let

be a diagonal path. Let

.

Let
\mathbf{p}

Lemma 4.15
.

Let

(16)

\displaystyle H_{\operatorname{NE}}\left(uv,z\right)=H_{\operatorname{NE}}\left(u,z\right)+z^{k}H_{\operatorname{NE}}\left(v,z\right);

(17)

\displaystyle H_{\operatorname{SE}}\left(uv,z\right)=H_{\operatorname{SE}}\left(u,z\right)+z^{k}H_{\operatorname{SE}}\left(v,z\right).

The standard path of

The translation increases the heights of all vertices by

(See the detailed version for more details.)

Lemma 4.17
.

An up-normal word

is uniquely determined by its final height (i.e., the difference # of

’s

Let

By Proposition

Remark 4.19

Lemma 4.22

Lemma 6.5

The size of an equivalence class

Given an equivalence relation on a finite set, its equivalence classes are not

Theorem 6.23

Let

\displaystyle\prod_{i\geq 0}\binom{a_{i}+b_{i+2}-1}{b_{i+2}}\binom{b_{-i}+a_{-i-2}-1}{a_{-i-2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\begin{cases}\binom{a_{0}+b_{0}}{a_{0}},&\text{ if $w$ is balanced;}\\
\binom{a_{0}+b_{0}-1}{b_{0}},&\text{ if $w$ is rising and non-balanced;}\\
\binom{a_{0}+b_{0}-1}{a_{0}},&\text{ if $w$ is falling and non-balanced.}\\
\end{cases}

Proof 6.24

We prove the formula by considering the associated standard paths. Then

The mean size

Essentially the state vector of a given column is completely determined by that of the previous column and only one state vector need be held in the computer at any stage and all other state vectors overwritten, although some care is necessary for the execution.

to order

of all those reachable sites before or at column

=1+p+p+p^{2}+(2p^{2}-p^{4})+p^{2}=1+2p+4p^{2}-p^{4},

to order

\mathcal{R}_{1}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{1}
in Theorem

8.2
has been implicitly observed in
[
BCHR11
, bottom of p.
40]
. Note that this implication really requires the assumption about
equal numbers of
D
’s and of
U
’s in Theorem
8.2
, since (e.g.)
the Ferrers boards
B_{DUUDU}
and
B_{DDUU}
are rook-equivalent without

\phi\left(DUUDU\right)
equalling
\phi\left(DDUU\right)
. For an
even starker example, if
w\in\mathcal{M}
is any word, then the Ferrers
boards
B_{w}
and
B_{\omega\left(w\right)}
are rook-equivalent (being
each other’s reflection across a diagonal), but
\phi\left(w\right)
is
usually not
\phi\left(\omega\left(w\right)\right)
. We note that this reasoning leads to a new proof of Theorem
2.3
.

Theorem
8.2
connects our study of the kernel of
\phi
to a
classical question, namely: When are two Ferrers boards rook-equivalent? A
classical result of Foata and Schützenberger (
[
FoaSch70
, Theorem 6]

or
[
BCHR11
, Theorem 7]
) shows that each Ferrers board is rook-equivalent
to a unique “increasing Ferrers board”.
These “increasing Ferrers boards” are
somewhat similar to our up-normal words, but not quite in bijection, since (as
we said) the rook equivalence of
B_{u}
and
B_{v}
implies
\phi\left(u\right)=\phi\left(v\right)
only when we know that
u
and
v
have the
same number of
U
’s and the same number of
D
’s.

Interestingly, Foata and Schützenberger have their own kind of moves that
they use to normalize a Ferrers board modulo rook equivalence: the
“
\left(k,k^{\prime}\right)
-transforms” (see
[
FoaSch70
, Definition 8 bis on page 9]
). These appear to be
close relatives of our balanced flips.

A recent preprint by Cotardo, Gruica and Ravagnani
[
CoGrRa23
]
proves
another set of equivalent criteria for the rook-equivalence of two Ferrers
boards
[
CoGrRa23
, Corollary 3.2]
.
It lists six equivalent conditions, one of which (condition 6) is
rook-equivalence, whereas another (condition 1) is equivalent to our
statement
\mathcal{S}_{3}
(albeit this equivalence takes some work to
prove).
The other four conditions are not found in our lists so far.
In the following, we state two of these four conditions (4 and 5),
as they are rather surprising and reveal an unexpected connection to
the theory of finite fields. (Arguably, at least one of them has been
foreseen, to some extent, in Haglund’s
[
Haglun98
]
.)

First, we introduce the necessary notations.
For any finite field
F
, any nonnegative integers
n
and
k
, and any board

B\subseteq\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}^{2}
, we define
P_{k}\left(B/F\right)

to be the number of
n\times n
-matrices
A\in F^{n\times n}
of
rank
k
such that all entries of
A
in cells outside of
B
are zero.
This is called
P_{k}\left(B\right)
in
[
Haglun98
, Definition 1]
, where

F=\mathbb{F}_{q}
; but we include
F
in the notation since

P_{k}\left(B/F\right)
depends on
F
. It is easy to see, however, that

P_{k}\left(B/F\right)
does not depend on
n
,
as long as
n
is large enough that
B\subseteq\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}^{2}
.
In
[
CoGrRa23
]
, our
P_{k}\left(B/F\right)
is called

W_{k}\left(\operatorname{Mat}^{n\times m}_{q}\left(B\right)\right)
, where
F=\mathbb{F}_{q}
,
and where
n
and
m
are chosen large enough that

B\subseteq\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}\times\left\{1,2,\ldots,m\right\}
.
Now, we claim the following:

Theorem 8.4
.

Let
u
and
v
be two words in
\mathcal{M}
that have the
same number of
D
’s and the same number of
U
’s. Then, the following
statements are equivalent:
the statements
\mathcal{S}_{1}
,
\mathcal{S}_{2}
,
\mathcal{S}_{3}
,
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{3}
,
\mathcal{S}_{4}
,
\mathcal{S}_{5}
and
\mathcal{S}_{6}
from Theorem
2.1
,
the statement
\mathcal{R}_{1}
from Theorem
8.2
,
and the following two additional statements:

•

\mathcal{R}_{2}
: For any finite field
F
and any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we
have
P_{k}\left(B_{u}/F\right)=P_{k}\left(B_{v}/F\right)
.

•

\mathcal{R}_{3}
: For any finite field
F
, we have
P_{1}\left(B_{u}/F\right)=P_{1}\left(B_{v}/F\right)
.

Proof 8.5
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
be the Ferrers boards
B_{u}
and
B_{v}
.
Then, our statements
\mathcal{R}_{1}
,
\mathcal{R}_{2}
and
\mathcal{R}_{3}
are (respectively) the conditions 6, 5 and 4 of
[
CoGrRa23
, Corollary 3.2]
.
Thus, the former three statements are equivalent (since
[
CoGrRa23
, Corollary 3.2]
shows that the latter three conditions are equivalent).
Combined with Theorem
8.2
, this proves Theorem
8.4
.

Remark 8.6
.

Any word
w\in\mathcal{M}
satisfies
B_{w}=B_{Uw}=B_{wD}
.
That is, the Ferrers board
B_{w}
of a word
w\in\mathcal{M}
does not change if we insert a
U
at the beginning of
w
or a
D
at the end of
w
.
Thus, we can use Theorem
8.4
to tell whether two Ferrers boards are rook-equivalent:
Namely, we write the two Ferrers boards as
B_{u}
and
B_{v}
, where
u
and
v
are two words with the same number of
U
’s and the same number of
D
’s (this can be ensured by inserting an appropriate number of
U
’s at the beginning and an appropriate number of

’s at the end of either word), and then Theorem
8.4
provides us several equivalent criteria for the rook-equivalence of

and

.

9
Balanced commutations revisited: irreducible balanced words

In our definition of balanced commutations (which underlay the definition of the equivalence relation
\overset{\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}
), we allowed two arbitrary balanced factors of our word to trade places, as long as they were adjacent in the word.
Now, one may wonder whether we can get by with a smaller set of allowed swaps:
Is there a more restrictive subset of balanced commutations that generates the same equivalence relation
\overset{\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}
?

The answer is “yes”, and in fact there are likely several reasonable choices.
We here present one, which is not minimal but still far more parsimonious than the set of all balanced commutations.

To define it, we begin with a simple notion:
A balanced word
w
is said to be
irreducible
if it is nonempty and cannot be written as a concatenation
w=uv
of two nonempty balanced words
u
and
v
.
For instance, the balanced word
DDUDUU
is irreducible, whereas the balanced word
DUUUDD
is not (since it is the concatenation
DU\cdot UUDD
).
In terms of diagonal paths, this notion can be restated as follows:
Given a nontrivial diagonal path
\mathbf{p}
with initial height
0
and final height
0
, its reading word
\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)
is irreducible if and only if
\mathbf{p}
intersects the x-axis only in its first and last vertices.

Given two words
v,w\in\mathcal{M}
, we say that
v
is obtained from

w
by an
irreducible balanced commutation
if and only if we can write
v
and
w

as
v=pxyq
and
w=pyxq
, where
p,q\in\mathcal{M}
are two arbitrary words and where

x,y\in\mathcal{M}
are two irreducible balanced words with different first letters.
Clearly, this condition implies that
v
is obtained from
w
by a balanced commutation, but the converse is not true.
For example, the word
UUDDDUUD
is obtained from
UDDUUUDD
by an irreducible balanced commutation (swapping the
DDUU
with the
UD
in the middle), but the word
UUDDUDDU
is not (indeed, it is obtained by swapping the balanced factors
UDDU
and
UUDD
, but these factors don’t have different first letters, and the first of them is not irreducible).

We define an equivalence relation
\overset{\operatorname*{irr}}{\sim}
on

\mathcal{M}
by stipulating that two words
w,v\in\mathcal{M}
satisfy

w\overset{\operatorname*{irr}}{\sim}v
if and only if
v
can be obtained
from
w
by a sequence (possibly empty) of irreducible balanced commutations.
Even though not every balanced commutation is irreducible, we claim the following:

Theorem 9.1
.

The relations
\overset{\operatorname*{irr}}{\sim}
and
\overset{\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}
are the same.
That is, if a word
v
can be obtained from a word
w
by a sequence of balanced commutations, then we can also obtain
v
from
w
by a (possibly longer) sequence of irreducible balanced commutations.

The proof of this theorem needs a few lemmas. The first one is nearly obvious:

Lemma 9.2
.

Any balanced word
w\in\mathcal{M}
can be decomposed into
a product
w=v_{1}v_{2}\cdots v_{k}
of irreducible balanced words

v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}\in\mathcal{M}
. (If
w
is empty, we will have
k=0
here.)

Proof 9.3
.

This is shown in the same way as the existence of a factorization of a
positive integer into primes:

Let
w\in\mathcal{M}
be a balanced word. If
w
is irreducible or empty, then
we are done. If not, then
w
can be written as a product of two shorter
nonempty balanced words. If these two shorter words are irreducible, then we
are done. If not, then at least one of them can itself be written as a product
of two shorter nonempty balanced words, so that
w
becomes a product of three
nonempty balanced words. Thus, we obtain longer and longer factorizations of

w
into shorter and shorter nonempty balanced words. Obviously, this process
will eventually have to stop, and at that point we will have a factorization
of
w
into irreducible balanced words in front of us.

The decomposition in Lemma
9.2
is furthermore unique (and this
is easy to see using diagonal paths), but we do not need this.

Next we introduce some shorthand terminology: A
UIB word
will mean an
irreducible balanced word that begins with a
U
. A
DIB word
will mean
an irreducible balanced word that begins with a
D
. Note that any irreducible
balanced word is nonempty, and thus is either UIB or DIB (but not both). The
following is another easy observation:

Lemma 9.4
.

(a)
Any UIB word ends with a
D
.

(b)
Any DIB word ends with a
U
.

(c)
If
u\in\mathcal{M}
is a UIB word, then
\omega\left(u\right)
is a UIB word as well.

(d)
If
u\in\mathcal{M}
is a DIB word, then
\omega\left(u\right)
is a DIB word as well.

Proof 9.5
.

(a)
Let
w
be a UIB word. We need to prove that
w
ends with a
D
.

Assume the contrary. Thus,
w
ends with a
U
. But
w
also starts with a
U

(since
w
is UIB) and is balanced (for the same reason). Hence, Lemma

4.22
shows that we can write
w
as a concatenation
w=pq
,
where
p
and
q
are two nonempty balanced words. Hence,
w
is not
irreducible, despite being a UIB word.
This contradiction shows that our assumption was false.
Lemma
9.4

(a)
is thus proved.

(b)
This is analogous to part
(a)
; we just need to
interchange the roles of
U
and
D
.

(c)
Let
u\in\mathcal{M}
be a UIB word. Then,
u
ends with a
D

(by part
(a)
). Hence,
\omega\left(u\right)
starts with a
U

(since the anti-automorphism
\omega
reverses a word and replaces each
D
by
a
U
and each
U
by a
D
). Moreover,
u
is irreducible balanced (since
u

is UIB). Therefore,
\omega\left(u\right)
is irreducible balanced as well
(indeed, the irreducibility follows from the fact that any factorization of

\omega\left(u\right)
into two nonempty balanced factors could be turned
back into a factorization of
u
by applying
\omega
again).
Hence,
\omega\left(u\right)
is a UIB word (since
\omega\left(u\right)
starts with a
U
). This proves Lemma
9.4

(c)
.

(d)
This is analogous to part
(c)
; we just need to
interchange the roles of
U
and
D
.

It is not hard to see that each UIB word has the form
UsD
where
s
is a balanced
1
-Dyck word (i.e., a balanced word whose each prefix is rising).
Likewise, each DIB word has the form
DtU
where
t
is a balanced anti-
1
-Dyck word (i.e., a balanced word whose each prefix is falling).
Obviously, UIB words can be distinguished from DIB words by their first letter.

Now we claim the following variant of Lemma
4.22
:

Lemma 9.6
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{M}
be a balanced word that starts with a

U
and ends with a
U
. Then, we can write
w
as a concatenation
w=spqt
,
where
s,p,q,t\in\mathcal{M}
are four balanced words such that
p
is UIB and

q
is DIB.

Proof 9.7
.

Lemma
4.22
shows that we can write
w
as a concatenation

w=p^{\prime}q^{\prime}
, where
p^{\prime}
is a balanced word starting with
a
U
, and where
q^{\prime}
is a balanced word starting with a
D
. Consider
these
p^{\prime}
and
q^{\prime}
.

Lemma
9.2
shows that
p^{\prime}
can be decomposed into a
product
p^{\prime}=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}
of irreducible balanced words

p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k}\in\mathcal{M}
. Likewise,
q^{\prime}
can be
decomposed into a product
q^{\prime}=p_{k+1}p_{k+2}\cdots p_{\ell}
of
irreducible balanced words
p_{k+1},p_{k+2},\ldots,p_{\ell}\in\mathcal{M}
.
Consider these decompositions. Thus,
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{\ell}
are
\ell

irreducible balanced words such that
p^{\prime}=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}
and

q^{\prime}=p_{k+1}p_{k+2}\cdots p_{\ell}
. Hence,

p^{\prime}q^{\prime}=\left(p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}\right)\left(p_{k+1}p_{k+2}\cdots p_{\ell}\right)=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{\ell}.

The word
p_{1}
is nonempty (since it is irreducible) and is a prefix of

p^{\prime}
(since
p^{\prime}=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{k}
). Thus, it starts with
a
U
(since
p^{\prime}
starts with a
U
). Similarly,
p_{k+1}
starts with
a
D
.

Now, consider the
smallest
number
i\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,\ell\right\}
for which the word
p_{i}
starts with a
D
. (Such an
i

exists, since
p_{k+1}
starts with a
D
.) Then,
i
cannot be
1
(since

p_{1}
starts with a
U
, not with a
D
), and thus must be
\geq 2
. Hence,

i-1\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,\ell\right\}
. The word
p_{i-1}
cannot start
with a
D
(because then,
i
would not be the
smallest
number for
which
p_{i}
starts with a
D
), and thus must start with a
U
. Hence, the
word
p_{i-1}
is UIB (since it is irreducible balanced). Meanwhile, the word

p_{i}
is DIB (since it is irreducible balanced and starts with a
D
). All
the words
p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{\ell}
are balanced; hence, their
concatenations
p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{i-2}
and
p_{i+1}p_{i+2}\cdots p_{\ell}

are balanced as well (since any concatenation of balanced words is balanced).

Now,

w=p^{\prime}q^{\prime}=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{\ell}=\underbrace{\left(p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{i-2}\right)}_{\text{balanced word}}\underbrace{p_{i-1}}_{\text{UIB word}}\underbrace{p_{i}}_{\text{DIB word}}\underbrace{\left(p_{i+1}p_{i+2}\cdots p_{\ell}\right)}_{\text{balanced word}}.

Hence, we can write
w
as a concatenation
w=spqt
, where
s,p,q,t\in\mathcal{M}
are four balanced words such that
p
is UIB and
q
is DIB
(namely, we take
s=p_{1}p_{2}\cdots p_{i-2}
and
p=p_{i-1}
and
q=p_{i}

and
t=p_{i+1}p_{i+2}\cdots p_{\ell}
). This proves Lemma
9.6
.

This, in turn, allows us to improve Lemma
4.24
as follows:

Lemma 9.8
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{M}
be a rising word that is not up-normal.
Then, we can write
w
in the form
w=upqv
, where
u
and
v
are two words,
where
p
is a UIB word, and where
q
is a DIB word.

Proof 9.9
.

Proceed as in the above proof of Lemma
4.24
to find a factor
w^{\prime}
of
w
that is balanced and starts and ends with a
U
.
Then, apply Lemma
9.6
to
w^{\prime}
instead of
w
.
Thus,
w^{\prime}
is written as a concatenation
w^{\prime}=spqt
,
where
s,p,q,t\in\mathcal{M}
are four balanced words such that
p
is UIB and
q
is DIB.
Let
\widetilde{u}
be the prefix of
w
coming before the factor
w^{\prime}
, and
\widetilde{v}
be the suffix of
w
coming after
w^{\prime}
.
Then,
w=\widetilde{u}w^{\prime}\widetilde{v}=\underbrace{\widetilde{u}s}_{=:u}pq\underbrace{t\widetilde{v}}_{=:v}

is the factorization we are looking for.

Next, we prove an analogue of Proposition
4.7
:

Lemma 9.10
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{M}
be a rising word. Then, there exists
a unique up-normal word
t\in\mathcal{M}
such that
t\overset{\operatorname*{irr}}{\sim}w
.

Proof 9.11
.

The existence of
t
can be proved in the same way as the existence
part of Proposition
4.7
, but using Lemma
9.8

instead of Lemma
4.24
(since UIB words start with a
U
, while
DIB words start with a
D
).

The uniqueness of
t
follows from the uniqueness of
t
in Proposition

4.7
, since the relation
t\overset{\operatorname*{irr}}{\sim}w
implies
t\overset{\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}w
.

The following is an analogue of Proposition
4.5
:

Proposition 9.12
.

Let
u
and
v
be two words in
\mathcal{M}
. Then,

u\overset{\operatorname*{irr}}{\sim}v
if and only if
\omega\left(u\right)\overset{\operatorname*{irr}}{\sim}\omega\left(v\right)
.

Proof 9.13
.

This is similar to the proof of Proposition
4.5
, with a slight
twist: We need to show that if
a
and
b
are two irreducible balanced words
that have different first letters, then their images
\omega\left(a\right)
and
\omega\left(b\right)
are again two irreducible balanced words that
have different first letters. But this follows from parts
(c)
and

(d)
of Lemma
9.4
.

We have an analogue of Lemma
5.1
as well:

Lemma 9.14
.

Let
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
be two diagonal paths
with the same initial height and the same final height. Assume that
H\left(\mathbf{p},z\right)=H\left(\mathbf{q},z\right)
. Then,

\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)\overset{\operatorname*{irr}}{\sim}\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{q}\right)
.

Proof 9.15
.

Analogous to the proof of Lemma
5.1
, but using Proposition

9.12
and Lemma
9.10
instead of Proposition

4.5
and Proposition
4.7
. (Use the obvious
fact that
u\overset{\operatorname*{irr}}{\sim}v
implies
u\overset{\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}v
.)

We can now prove Theorem
9.1
:

Proof 9.16

(Proof of Theorem
9.1
.)
.

Let us modify Theorem
2.1

by adding the following extra statement:

•

\mathcal{S}_{5}^{\prime}
: We have
u\overset{\operatorname*{irr}}{\sim}v
.

Clearly, this statement
\mathcal{S}_{5}^{\prime}
implies
\mathcal{S}_{5}
,
since
u\overset{\operatorname*{irr}}{\sim}v
implies
u\overset{\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}v
. But the implication
\mathcal{S}_{4}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{5}^{\prime}
holds as well, and can be proved
just as the implication
\mathcal{S}_{4}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{5}
in
Theorem
2.1
was proved (but using Lemma
9.14
instead of
Lemma

). Hence, the statement
\mathcal{S}_{5}^{\prime}
is
equivalent to all the seven statements

\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2},\mathcal{S}_{3},\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{3},\mathcal{S}_{4},\mathcal{S}_{5},\mathcal{S}_{6}

from Theorem

.
In
particular,
\mathcal{S}_{5}^{\prime}
is equivalent to
\mathcal{S}_{5}
. In
other words,

is equivalent to

. In other words, the

\overset{\operatorname*{irr}}{\sim}
and

are the same. This proves Theorem

.

10
Other algebras

Everything we have done so far concerned the “rank-
1
” Weyl algebra

\mathcal{W}=\mathbf{k}\left\langle D,U\ \mid\ DU-UD=1\right\rangle.

But the main question we addressed – to
classify equal products of generators – can be posed for any
\mathbf{k}
-algebra given by generators and relations. In particular, several analogues
and variants of
\mathcal{W}
are natural candidates for a
similar study. In this section, we briefly discuss some of them, giving some
answers and posing some questions.
(There are many more – see, e.g.,
[
Gaddis23
]
for a recent survey.)

10.1
Multivariate Weyl algebras

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there is an “
n
-Weyl
algebra”
\mathcal{W}_{n}
, defined as the
\mathbf{k}
-algebra given by
2n
generators

D_{1},D_{2},\ldots,D_{n},U_{1},U_{2},\ldots,U_{n}
and relations

\displaystyle D_{i}U_{j}

\displaystyle=U_{j}D_{i}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for all }i\neq j;

\displaystyle D_{i}U_{i}

\displaystyle=U_{i}D_{i}+1\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for all }i;

\displaystyle D_{i}D_{j}

\displaystyle=D_{j}D_{i}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for all }i,j;

\displaystyle U_{i}U_{j}

\displaystyle=U_{j}U_{i}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{for all }i,j.

It is isomorphic to the
\mathbf{k}
-algebra of differential
operators on the polynomial ring
\mathbf{k}\left[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\right]
.

However, this algebra
\mathcal{W}_{n}
can also be seen as the
n
-fold
tensor power
13
13

13

All tensor products and tensor powers
in this paper are taken over the field
\mathbf{k}
.

\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n}
of the original Weyl algebra

\mathcal{W}
, via the
\mathbf{k}
-algebra isomorphism

\mathcal{W}_{n}\to\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n}
that sends each generator
D_{i}
to

\underbrace{1\otimes 1\otimes\cdots\otimes 1}_{i-1\text{ times}}\otimes\,D\otimes\underbrace{1\otimes 1\otimes\cdots\otimes 1}_{n-i\text{ times}}
and
each generator
U_{i}
to
\underbrace{1\otimes 1\otimes\cdots\otimes 1}_{i-1\text{ times}}\otimes\,U\otimes\underbrace{1\otimes 1\otimes\cdots\otimes 1}_{n-i\text{ times}}
. From this point of view, products of
generators of
\mathcal{W}_{n}
are just elements of the form
\phi\left(w_{1}\right)\otimes\phi\left(w_{2}\right)\otimes\cdots\otimes\phi\left(w_{n}\right)\in\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n}
, where
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots,w_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
are some words. Which of these products are equal? The answer
turns out to boil down to the answer for
n=1
(which we know from Theorems

2.1
,
8.2
and
8.4
):

Theorem 10.1
.

Let
u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n},v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}

be
2n
words in
\mathcal{M}
. Then,

\phi\left(u_{1}\right)\otimes\phi\left(u_{2}\right)\otimes\cdots\otimes\phi\left(u_{n}\right)=\phi\left(v_{1}\right)\otimes\phi\left(v_{2}\right)\otimes\cdots\otimes\phi\left(v_{n}\right)\text{
in }\mathcal{W}^{\otimes n}

if and only if

\text{each }i\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}\text{ satisfies }\phi\left(u_{i}\right)=\phi\left(v_{i}\right).

In other words, we don’t get any “new” equalities by tensoring
n
copies of
\mathcal{W}
.

The “if” part of Theorem
10.1
is obvious, while the “only if” part of the theorem follows immediately from two lemmas. The first is a
general fact from linear algebra (
[
Conrad24
, Theorem 5.15]
):

Lemma 10.2
.

Let
V_{1},V_{2},\ldots,V_{n}
be any
\mathbf{k}
-vector
spaces. For each
i\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
, let
x_{i}
and

y_{i}
be two nonzero vectors in
V_{i}
. Then,

x_{1}\otimes x_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes x_{n}=y_{1}\otimes y_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes y_{n}\text{ in }V_{1}\otimes V_{2}\otimes\cdots\otimes V_{n}

if and only if there exist some scalars
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbf{k}
such that
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\cdots\lambda_{n}=1
and such that

\text{each }i\in\left\{1,2,\ldots,n\right\}\text{ satisfies }x_{i}=\lambda_{i}y_{i}.

The next lemma ensures that the conditions of Lemma
10.2
are
met in the appropriate case:

Lemma 10.3
.

(a)
For any
w\in\mathcal{M}
, we have

\phi\left(w\right)\neq 0
.

(b)
If
u,v\in\mathcal{M}
and
\lambda\in\mathbf{k}
satisfy

\phi\left(u\right)=\lambda\phi\left(v\right)
, then
\lambda=1
and

\phi\left(u\right)=\phi\left(v\right)
.

Proof 10.4

(First proof.)
.

The family
\left(U^{j}D^{i}\right)_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
basis of
\mathcal{W}
(by
[
EGHetc11
, Proposition 2.7.1 (i)]
). Hence, each
element
a
of
\mathcal{W}
can be uniquely written as a
\mathbf{k}
-linear
combination
\sum\limits_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}}a_{i,j}U^{j}D^{i}
of the elements of this
family. When this
a
is nonzero, we define the
leading monomial
of
a

to be the lexicographically highest pair
\left(i,j\right)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
for which
a_{i,j}\neq 0
, and we define the
leading
coefficient
of
a
to be the coefficient
a_{i,j}
corresponding to this pair

\left(i,j\right)
.

(a)
Let
w\in\mathcal{M}
. Then, Theorem
8.1
yields

\phi\left(w\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{\min\left\{m,n\right\}}r_{k}\left(B_{w}\right)U^{m-k}D^{n-k}

(34)

for appropriate
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
. The elements
U^{m-k}D^{n-k}
on the right
hand side of this equality are
\mathbf{k}
-linearly independent (since the
family
\left(U^{j}D^{i}\right)_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a basis of

\mathcal{W}
), and at least one of the coefficients
r_{k}\left(B_{w}\right)
is nonzero (indeed, we have
r_{0}\left(B_{w}\right)=1
,
since any board
B
satisfies
r_{0}\left(B\right)=1
). Thus, the entire
right hand side is nonzero. Hence,
\phi\left(w\right)\neq 0
. This proves
Lemma
10.3

(a)
.

(b)
Let
u,v\in\mathcal{M}
and
\lambda\in\mathbf{k}
satisfy

\phi\left(u\right)=\lambda\phi\left(v\right)
. Recall that for each

w\in\mathcal{M}
, the element
\phi\left(w\right)\in\mathcal{W}
is
nonzero (by part
(a)
) and has leading coefficient
1
(indeed, the
equality (
34
) shows that the leading coefficient of

\phi\left(w\right)
is
r_{0}\left(B_{w}\right)=1
).
Thus, comparing leading coefficients in

\phi\left(u\right)=\lambda\phi\left(v\right)

shows that
\lambda=1
and
\phi\left(u\right)=\phi\left(v\right)
.

Proof 10.5

(Second proof.)
.

(b)
First, we generalize Proposition

3.7
by replacing the assumption “
\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)\right)=\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{q}\right)\right)
” by
“
\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{p}\right)\right)=\lambda\phi\left(\operatorname{w}\left(\mathbf{q}\right)\right)
” and adding the claim “
\lambda=1
” to the conclusion. The proof of Proposition

3.7
that we gave above still applies to this generalization,
once a few trivial changes are made. In particular, the polynomial identity

\prod_{p_{i}\text{ is an NE-step of }\mathbf{p}}\left(x-h_{i}\right)=\prod_{q_{i}\text{ is an NE-step of }\mathbf{q}}\left(x-g_{i}\right)

must be replaced by

\prod_{p_{i}\text{ is an NE-step of }\mathbf{p}}\left(x-h_{i}\right)=\lambda\prod_{q_{i}\text{ is an NE-step of }\mathbf{q}}\left(x-g_{i}\right),

which of course entails not only

\displaystyle\left\{h_{i}\ \mid\ p_{i}\text{ is an NE-step of }\mathbf{p}\right\}_{\operatorname{multiset}}

\displaystyle=\left\{g_{i}\ \mid\ q_{i}\text{ is an NE-step of }\mathbf{q}\right\}_{\operatorname{multiset}}

but also
\lambda=1
(by comparing leading coefficients).

Applying this generalized version of Proposition

3.7

to our situation (setting
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
to be the
standard paths of
u
and
v
), we obtain
\lambda=1
,
and thus
\phi\left(u\right)=\phi\left(v\right)
.
This proves Lemma
10.3

(b)
.

(a)
Let
w\in\mathcal{M}
. We must prove that
\phi\left(w\right)\neq 0
. Assume the contrary. Thus,
\phi\left(w\right)=0=0\phi\left(w\right)
. Hence, part
(b)
(applied to
u=w
and
v=w
and

\lambda=0
) yields
0=1
and
\phi\left(w\right)=\phi\left(w\right)
.
Obviously,
0=1
is absurd, so we found a contradiction. This proves Lemma

10.3

(a)
.

10.2
Characteristic
p

We have hitherto assumed that the field
\mathbf{k}
has characteristic
0
. If

\mathbf{k}
has characteristic
p\neq 0
instead, things change significantly:
Purely identity-type results such as Proposition
3.4
,
Proposition
3.6
and Lemma
3.11
remain valid
(indeed, they hold whenever
\mathbf{k}
is merely a commutative ring), but
the action of
\mathcal{W}
on
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
is no longer
faithful (i.e., Lemma
3.1
fails), and various other results
that build on the tacit identification of integers with elements of

\mathbf{k}
become false as well (e.g., Proposition
3.7
).
Lemma
3.13
remains true, but the first proof we gave above no longer
works (although it is not hard to derive it from the characteristic-
0
case,
since it is an identity in the free
\mathbf{k}
-module
\mathcal{W}
).
Thus, Lemma
3.16
remains true as well.

The proof of Proposition
3.9
also falls flat in
characteristic
p
, but the proposition itself survives. Indeed, it holds for
any nontrivial ring
\mathbf{k}
, and can be proved by comparing leading terms
in Theorem
8.1
.

The most interesting question is when two words
u,v\in\mathcal{M}
satisfy

\phi\left(u\right)=\phi\left(v\right)
for a field
\mathbf{k}
of
characteristic
p\neq 0
. The equivalence
\mathcal{S}_{1}\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{5}
in Theorem
2.1
no longer holds in this case, as
(e.g.) we have
\phi\left(U^{p+1}D\right)=\phi\left(UDU^{p}\right)

(indeed,
U^{p}
is a central element of
\mathcal{W}
when

\operatorname{char}\mathbf{k}=p
) but we don’t have
U^{p+1}D\overset{\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}UDU^{p}
.
One might try to salvage the
equivalence by loosening the notion of balanced commutations, e.g., by
allowing both
U^{p}
and
D^{p}
to be swapped with any (neighboring) factor
of the word; the resulting equivalence relation

\overset{p-\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}
might satisfy the equivalence
\left(\phi\left(u\right)=\phi\left(v\right)\right)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ \left(u\overset{p-\operatorname*{bal}}{\sim}v\right)
, but we don’t know
if it does.

Question 10.6
.

Does it?

The Weyl algebra
\mathcal{W}
in characteristic
p\neq 0
has some quotients
(unlike in characteristic
0
, where it is famously a simple algebra). Indeed,
both elements
U^{p}
and
D^{p}
are known to lie in its center, and thus
generate two-sided ideals
\mathcal{W}U^{p}\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{W}D^{p}\mathcal{W}
that can be quotiented out. We can thus form the three
quotients

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{-}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{W}/\left(\mathcal{W}U^{p}\mathcal{W}\right),\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \mathcal{W}_{-}:=\mathcal{W}/\left(\mathcal{W}D^{p}\mathcal{W}\right),

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{-}^{-}

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{W}/\left(\mathcal{W}U^{p}\mathcal{W}+\mathcal{W}D^{p}\mathcal{W}\right).

The third quotient,
\mathcal{W}_{-}^{-}
, is actually a finite-dimensional

\mathbf{k}
-vector space, of dimension
p^{2}
and with basis
\left(U^{j}D^{i}\right)_{i,j\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\right\}}
. It acts
faithfully and densely on the
\mathbf{k}
-algebra
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]/\left(x^{p}\right)
. The quotient
\mathcal{W}_{-}
acts faithfully on the full
polynomial ring
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
.
The quotients
\mathcal{W}_{-}
and
\mathcal{W}^{-}
are isomorphic via the isomorphism sending

U\mapsto D
and
D\mapsto-U
.

The question of when two words
u,v\in\mathcal{M}
give rise to equal
monomials can now be asked not only for
\mathcal{W}
, but also for any of its
quotient algebras
\mathcal{W}^{-}
,
\mathcal{W}_{-}
and
\mathcal{W}_{-}^{-}
.
For instance, the words
U^{p-1}DUD^{p-1}
and
U^{p-1}D^{p-1}
do
not yield equal monomials in
\mathcal{W}
(by Proposition

3.9
), but yield equal monomials in each of the quotients

\mathcal{W}^{-}
,
\mathcal{W}_{-}
and
\mathcal{W}_{-}^{-}
(since their
difference in
\mathcal{W}
is
U^{p-1}DUD^{p-1}-U^{p-1}D^{p-1}=U^{p-1}\underbrace{\left(DU-1\right)}_{=UD}D^{p-1}=U^{p-1}UDD^{p-1}=U^{p}D^{p}
,
which becomes
0
in each of the quotients). What combinatorial condition is
responsible for this equality? We don’t know; there are neither any balanced
commutations that can be applied to them to produce new words, nor any
U^{p}

or
D^{p}
factors that can be annihilated. Thus, we ask the following rather
open-ended question (actually three questions in disguise):

Question 10.7
.

Characterize equalities between monomials in
\mathcal{W}^{-}
,
\mathcal{W}_{-}
and
\mathcal{W}_{-}^{-}
combinatorially.

10.3
Down-up algebras

We now return to the case when
\mathbf{k}
is a field of characteristic
0
.

There are several deformations and other variations of the Weyl algebra

\mathcal{W}
, and our question about equal monomials can be asked for each of
them. We here discuss one of the most recent such variations: the

down-up algebra
, actually a family of algebras depending on three
scalar parameters
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
.

We fix three scalars
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbf{k}
. The
down-up
algebra

\mathcal{A}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)
is defined to be
the
\mathbf{k}
-algebra with generators
D
and
U
and the two relations

\displaystyle D^{2}U

\displaystyle=\alpha DUD+\beta UD^{2}+\gamma D\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{and}

\displaystyle DU^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha UDU+\beta U^{2}D+\gamma U.

Clearly, this algebra
\mathcal{A}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)
has an
algebra anti-automorphism
\omega
sending
D
and
U
to
U
and
D
. Also,
it is easy to check that the above two relations of
\mathcal{A}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)
are satisfied in the Weyl algebra
\mathcal{W}

whenever
\alpha+\beta=\gamma-\beta=1
; therefore, the Weyl algebra

\mathcal{W}
is a quotient of
\mathcal{A}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)
in this case. Down-up algebras originate in
[
BenRob98
, Proposition 3.5]

and have since found uses in noncommutative algebraic geometry and combinatorics.

We can define a map
\phi:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{A}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)
in the same way as we defined
\phi:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{W}
, but using the down-up algebra

\mathcal{A}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)
instead of
\mathcal{W}
.
(Thus,
\phi
is a morphism of multiplicative monoids and sends
D
and
U
to

D
and
U
.) Surprisingly, we have:

Theorem 10.8
.

Assume that
\alpha+\beta=\gamma-\beta=1
. Then, the
equivalence of the six statements
\mathcal{S}_{1}
,
\mathcal{S}_{3}
,

\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{3}
,
\mathcal{S}_{4}
,
\mathcal{S}_{5}
and
\mathcal{S}_{6}

in Theorem
2.1
still holds if we replace
\mathcal{W}
by

\mathcal{A}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)
.

Proof 10.9
.

The statements
\mathcal{S}_{3}
,
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}_{3}
,
\mathcal{S}_{4}
,

\mathcal{S}_{5}
and
\mathcal{S}_{6}

are unchanged from Theorem
2.1
, so they are
still equivalent. It thus remains to prove that
\mathcal{S}_{1}\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{5}
.

\mathcal{S}_{5}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{1}
: This relies on a version of
Lemma
3.16
for the algebra
\mathcal{A}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)
instead of
\mathcal{W}
.
This version, in turn, relies on a version of Lemma
3.13
for the algebra
\mathcal{A}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)
instead of
\mathcal{W}
.
But the latter has already been established in
[
BenRob98
]
.
In fact, the
\mathbf{k}
-algebra
\mathcal{A}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)
is graded (just like
\mathcal{W}
: the
generators
U
and
D
are homogeneous of degrees
1
and
-1
). Its
0
-th
graded component is commutative, by
[
BenRob98
, Proposition 3.5]
. As in
the second proof of Lemma
3.13
, this entails the validity of Lemma

3.13
for
\mathcal{A}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)
, and this in turn yields that Lemma
3.16
holds for
\mathcal{A}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)
as well.
Hence,
\mathcal{S}_{5}
implies

\mathcal{S}_{1}
.

\mathcal{S}_{1}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{5}
: The condition
\alpha+\beta=\gamma-\beta=1
ensures that the Weyl algebra
\mathcal{W}
is a
quotient of
\mathcal{A}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)
(with its
generators
D
and
U
being the projections of the generators
D
and
U
).
Thus,
\phi\left(u\right)=\phi\left(v\right)
in
\mathcal{A}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)
implies
\phi\left(u\right)=\phi\left(v\right)
in
\mathcal{W}
. In other words, our new statement
\mathcal{S}_{1}
implies the old statement
\mathcal{S}_{1}
from Theorem

2.1
. But that old statement implies
\mathcal{S}_{5}
, as we
already know.
Hence, our new
\mathcal{S}_{1}
also implies
\mathcal{S}_{5}
.

Example 10.10
.

One of the situations to which Theorem
10.8
applies is the case when
\alpha=2
and
\beta=-1
and
\gamma=0
.
In this case, the down-up algebra
\mathcal{A}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)=\mathcal{A}\left(2,-1,0\right)
is the
homogenized Weyl algebra
, since its two defining relations

\displaystyle D^{2}U=2DUD-UD^{2}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ DU^{2}=2UDU-U^{2}D

can be rewritten (in terms of commutators
\left[x,y\right]:=xy-yx
) as

\left[D,\ DU-UD\right]=0\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \left[U,\ DU-UD\right]=0

and thus mean that the commutator
DU-UD
is a central element.
This algebra appears in
[
BDSHP10
, §2.3]
under the name of
\mathcal{U}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{H}}\right)
, with the generators
a
,
a^{\dagger}
and
e
corresponding to our
D
,
U
and
DU-UD
.

Question 10.11
.

To what extent can the condition
\alpha+\beta=\gamma-\beta=1
be lifted in
Theorem
10.8
?

This condition is unnecessary for
\mathcal{S}_{5}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{1}
, but needed for
\mathcal{S}_{1}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{5}
. For example,
\mathcal{S}_{1}\Longrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{5}

would fail in the following five cases:

1.

the case
\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)=\left(0,1,0\right)
(here, we have
\phi\left(DU^{2}\right)=\phi\left(U^{2}D\right)
);

2.

more generally, the case
\alpha=\gamma=0
and arbitrary
\beta
(here we have
\phi\left(DU^{4}D\right)=\phi\left(U^{2}D^{2}U^{2}\right)
);

3.

the case
\left(\alpha,\beta\right)=\left(0,-1\right)
and arbitrary
\gamma
(here we have
\phi\left(DU^{4}\right)=\phi\left(U^{4}D\right)
);

4.

the case
\left(\alpha,\beta\right)=\left(-1,-1\right)
and arbitrary
\gamma
(here we have
\phi\left(DU^{3}\right)=\phi\left(U^{3}D\right)
);

5.

the case
\left(\alpha,\beta\right)=\left(1,-1\right)
and arbitrary
\gamma
(here we have
\phi\left(DU^{6}\right)=\phi\left(U^{6}D\right)
).

However, such failures seem to be the
exception, not the rule.
The last three are explained by
[
Zhao99
, Theorem 1.3 (f)]
, and correspond to the only roots of unity that are quadratic over
\mathbb{Q}
.
There are more exceptions with irrational
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
, for instance
\left(\alpha,\beta\right)=\left(0,i\right)
with
i=\sqrt{-1}
, which satisfies
\phi\left(DU^{8}\right)=\phi\left(U^{8}D\right)
.

With some additional work, we can adapt the above proof of Theorem
10.8
to replace the condition “
\alpha+\beta=\gamma-\beta=1
” by “
\alpha+\beta=1
and
\left(\gamma=0\right)\Longleftrightarrow\left(\beta=-1\right)
”.
Indeed, under this condition, we can find a nonzero scalar
\zeta\in\mathbf{k}
such that
\gamma=\left(1+\beta\right)\zeta
.
Then, there is a
\mathbf{k}
-algebra morphism from
\mathcal{A}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)
to
\mathcal{W}
that sends
D
and
U
to
D
and
\zeta U
.
This morphism sends equal monomials in
\mathcal{A}\left(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\right)
to proportional monomials in
\mathcal{W}
;
but Lemma
10.3

(b)
says that proportional monomials in
\mathcal{W}
must actually be equal, and so we can argue as in the proof of Theorem
10.8
.
However, the condition
\alpha+\beta=1
cannot be lifted in this way.
Instead, we suspect that the linear-recurrence highest weight modules of
[
BenRob98
, Proposition 2.2]
should be used in the general case in lieu of
\mathbf{k}\left[x\right]
(certainly, this would explain the above exceptions as coming from the periodic linearly recurrent sequences).
