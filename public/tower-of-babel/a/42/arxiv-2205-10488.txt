Cryptanalysis of Three Quantum Money Schemes
Andriyan Bilyk, Javad Doliskani, Zhiyong Gong

Abstract
We investigate the security assumptions behind three public-key quantum money schemes. Aaronson and Christiano proposed a scheme based on hidden subspaces of the vector space 𝔽 2 n \mathbb{F}_{2}^{n} in 2012. It was conjectured by Pena et al in 2015 that the hard problem underlying the scheme can be solved in quasi-polynomial time. We confirm this conjecture by giving a polynomial time quantum algorithm for the underlying problem. Our algorithm is based on computing the Zariski tangent space of a random point in the hidden subspace. Zhandry proposed a scheme based on multivariate hash functions in 2017. We give a polynomial time quantum algorithm for cloning a money state with high probability. Our algorithm uses the verification circuit of the scheme to produce a banknote from a given serial number. Kane, Sharif and Silverberg proposed a scheme based on quaternion algebras in 2021. The underlying hard problem in their scheme is cloning a quantum state that represents an eigenvector of a set of Hecke operators. We give a polynomial time quantum reduction from this hard problem to a linear algebra problem. The latter problem is much easier to understand, and we hope that our reduction opens new avenues to future cryptanalyses of this scheme.



1
Introduction

The first quantum money scheme was proposed by Wiesner
[
28
]
around 1970. The idea was to represent banknotes using quantum states which, by the laws of quantum mechanics, would be impossible to copy. Wiesner’s scheme, which is nowadays called a private-key quantum money scheme, worked as follows. The bank generates a pair
(s,\rho_{s})
consisting of a classical serial number
s
and a quantum state
\rho_{s}
, and stores
s
along with a classical description of
\rho_{s}
in a database. Without access to the database, no algorithm would be able to copy
\rho_{s}
with non-negligible probability. Therefore, only the issuing bank would be able to verify the banknotes. Although this scheme is information-theoretically secure, it suffers major drawbacks in terms of practicality: First, the issuing bank has to maintain a huge database containing classical descriptions of every banknote ever produced. Second, only the bank can verify a banknote, and therefore the users have to take the money back to the bank for every transaction.

Subsequent efforts to address the drawbacks of Wiesner’s scheme led to the idea of
public-key quantum money
. The first formal treatment of public-key quantum money was given by Aaronson
[
1
]
. Intuitively, in such a scheme, a banknote can be verified by everyone, using a publicly known algorithm, but cannot be copied by anyone. The first explicit construction of public-key quantum money, which was based on stabilizer states, was given in
[
1
]
. The construction was later proved to be insecure by Lutomirski et al.
[
19
]
.

Public-key quantum money cannot be information-theoretically secure, it has to be based on a computational assumption. There have been many attempts at building a secure public-key quantum money scheme. Farhi et al.
[
11
]
proposed a scheme based on knot theory. A banknote in their scheme consists of a quantum state
\rho_{s}
representing a superposition of certain grid diagrams, and serial number
s
which is the Alexander polynomial of the grids in
\rho_{s}
. A banknote is verified using a procedure based on a classical Markov chain.

Aaronson and Christiano
[
3
]
proposed a scheme based on hidden subspaces of the vector space
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. In their scheme, a banknote consists of a quantum state
\rho_{s}
that is a uniform superposition of elements in a random linear subspace
A_{s}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
of dimension
n/2
, and a serial number
s
that provides membership oracles for
A_{s}
and the orthogonal complement
A_{s}^{\perp}
. The verification of a banknote is done using a projection operator built from the membership oracles for
A_{s}
and
A_{s}^{\perp}
.

Zhandry
[
30
]
adapted the idea proposed in
[
19
]
to give a construction of public-key quantum money based on multivariate hash functions. In their scheme, even the issuing bank cannot reproduce the same banknote. A bank note in their scheme consists of a quantum state that is a uniform superposition of the preimages of a random output
y
of a hash function, and a serial number which is the point
y
. To be able to verify banknotes, a restricted class of hash functions are used. The verification is done using the quantum Fourier transform and computing the ranks of matrices in a certain superposition.

Kane, Sharif and Silverberg
[
14
]
proposed a completely different construction for public-key quantum money based on quaternion algebras. There exists a set of commuting operators, called Hecke operators, on the space of modular forms. The Hecke operators are Hermitian, and therefore the space of modular forms has a basis consisting of simultaneous eigenvectors for these operators. A banknote in this scheme consists of a quantum state
\rho_{s}
that is an eigenstate of the Hecke operators, and a serial number
s
that is a set of eigenvalues corresponding to
\rho_{s}
and a certain set of Hecke operators. The verification of a banknote is done using phase estimation.

1.1
Previous cryptanalysis

Unfortunately, the security of all proposed public-key quantum money schemes to this date have been based on ad hoc/nonstandard computational assumptions. However, there has been a limited number of attempts, many of which were not convincing, at cryptanalyzing these assumption. As mentioned above, the public-key quantum money scheme of
[
1
]
was broken by Lutomirski et al.
[
19
]
who presented two attacks that worked in different parameter regimes. Their first attack was classical and used only the description of the verification circuit, while their second attack was a quantum algorithm that could generate banknotes that were different from the intended banknotes but could pass verification with non-negligible probability.

Pena et al.
[
21
]
presented an attack on the scheme
[
3
]
, but their attack was not on the original parameters. In particular, the explicit construction proposed in
[
3
]
is defined over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
, but the attack presented in
[
21
]
is only efficient if the scheme was defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
for a larger prime
p
. Pena et al. conjectured that for
p=2
their algorithm would run in quasi-polynomial time. Their attack is entirely classical and uses only the serial number given in the banknote.

Roberts
[
23
]
proposed an attack on the assumption underlying the scheme of
[
30
]
for a certain parameter regime. However, as discussed in a later version of
[
30
]
, the attack does not make the system insecure. Moreover, the hardness assumption behind the scheme could be modified in a way that renders the attack useless.

1.2
This work

In this paper, we carry out cryptanalysis on the following three schemes:

•

Aaronson and Christiano
[
3
]
. As mentioned above, this scheme is based on hidden subspaces of the vector space
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. The hard problem behind this scheme is to recover a secret linear subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
from the the set of common roots of a set of random polynomials over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. The previous attack, carried out by Pena et al.
[
21
]
, against this scheme uses the Gröbner basis algorithm. Their attack is only effective if the scheme was defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p}
for larger
p
, instead of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. It was conjectured in
[
21
]
that there exists a quasi-polynomial time algorithm for the original scheme over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
.

We give a polynomial time quantum algorithm for the case
\mathbb{F}_{2}
. Our attack is geometric, it uses the fact that a linear variety is isomorphic to its Zariski tangent space at any point. In particular, the subspace hidden by a set of polynomials can be viewed as a linear variety
Y
included in an affine variety
X
. We show that one can recover the hidden subspace by simply computing the kernel of the Jacobian matrix of
X
at a random point of the subspace
Y
. Such a random point is obtained by measuring a money state.

•

Zhandry
[
30
]
. This scheme is based on Multivariate Hash Functions. There are different versions of the paper
[
30
]
that give slightly different formulations of the scheme, but the underlying hardness assumptions can all be stated using the idea of multi-collision of multivariate polynomial hash functions. We will focus on the version
[
29
]
, in which the hardness assumption is directly stated using quadratic forms.

We give a polynomial time quantum algorithm, using which one can obtain arbitrarily many copies of a given banknote. Our algorithm uses the verification circuit of the scheme to produce a certain superposition of “bolts”. A measurement on this superposition then produces a copy of the money state with high probability. The ability to produce arbitrarily many copies of the money state comes from the fact the measurement outcome
y^{\prime}
can be compared against the given serial number
y
. If
y^{\prime}=y
then desired money state has been produced, otherwise the whole process is repeated.

•

Kane, Sharif and Silverberg
[
14
]
. This scheme is based on quaternion algebras. There have not been any attempts at cryptanalyzing this scheme yet, partly because of the mathematics involved which is rather nontrivial. The underlying hard problem in this scheme is simply cloning the money state which is an eigenstate of the set of all Hecke operators.

Although we have not been able to break this scheme, we give a polynomial time quantum reduction from the underlying hard problem to the problem of inverting a certain matrix
A
. Our algorithm is based on a deep connection between ideal classes in a rational quaternion algebra and the space of modular forms of weight
2
over the complex numbers. The connection is provided by the theory of Theta series. The matrix
A
is highly structured, with entries the representation numbers of explicit quaternary quadratic forms. In other words,
A
can be described by a set of polynomials of degree two in four variables. To work with this matrix, one does not need to know much about the theory behind the scheme. We believe that this reduction is an important first step toward an effective cryptanalysis of this scheme, and opens interesting new avenues for future attacks.

2
Preliminaries

Quantum computation.

For a detailed treatment on quantum information we refer the reader to
[
27
]
. A register in this paper can contain a classical or a quantum state. The classical state of a register
\mathsf{X}
is described by a finite alphabet
\Sigma
. An example alphabet used in this paper is
\Sigma=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. The quantum state of
\mathsf{X}
is described by the set of density operators
\mathrm{D}(\mathcal{X})
where
\mathcal{X}
is the complex Euclidean space
\mathbb{C}^{\Sigma}
.

We will also represent quantum states using unit vectors in
\mathcal{X}
, which are called pure states. We will use the Dirac notation when describing unit vectors. In particular, a unit column vector
x\in\mathcal{X}
is written as
\lvert x\rangle
, and the row vector
x^{*}
, which is the conjugate transpose of
x
, is written as
\langle x\rvert
. The quantum state of the register
\mathsf{X}
can then be written as

\sum_{x\in\Sigma}\alpha_{x}\lvert x\rangle,\quad\sum_{x\in\Sigma}\lvert\alpha_{x}\rvert^{2}=1.

The trace distance between two quantum states
\rho
and
\sigma
is defined by
\lVert\rho-\sigma\rVert_{1}
where
\lVert A\rVert_{1}=\tr\sqrt{A^{*}A}
is the trace norm of linear operators. For pure states
\lvert\psi\rangle
and
\lvert\phi\rangle
the distance is computed using

\lVert\lvert\psi\rangle\langle\psi\rvert-\lvert\phi\rangle\langle\phi\rvert\rVert_{1}=2\sqrt{1-\lvert\langle\psi|\phi\rangle\rvert^{2}}.

For quantum state
\rho_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}
,
i=1,2

\lVert\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}-\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2}\rVert_{1}\leq\lVert\rho_{1}-\sigma_{1}\rVert_{1}+\lVert\rho_{2}-\sigma_{2}\rVert_{1}.

Public-key quantum money.

Following the definition in
[
3
]
, a public-key quantum money scheme consists of three algorithms:

•

\mathsf{Gen}
. Takes as input a security parameter
\kappa
, and generates a key pair
(k_{\text{pri}},k_{\text{pub}})
in probabilistic polynomial time (in
\kappa
). Here,
k_{\text{pri}}
is the private key and
k_{\text{pub}}
is the public key.

•

\mathsf{Bank}
. Takes as input a private key
k_{\text{pri}}
, and generates a banknote in probabilistic polynomial time.

•

\mathsf{Ver}
. Takes as input the public key
k_{\text{pub}}
and a banknote, and outputs either ‘accept’ or ‘reject’.

A banknote, denoted by
\lvert\$\rangle
, usually consists of a pair
(s,\rho_{s})
where
s
is a binary string and
\rho_{s}
is a mixed quantum state. The string
s
is called the
serial number
, and the state
\rho_{s}
is called the
money state
.

The notion of security for a quantum money scheme is best explained using an adversary-challenger game. The goal for an adversary
A
is to produce a copy of a valid banknote without having access to the private key. Consider the following game played by
A
and a challenger. The challenger calls
\mathsf{Bank}
to generate a banknote
\lvert\$\rangle=(s,\rho_{s})
, and sends the banknote to
A
.
A
generates two alleged banknotes
\lvert\$_{1}\rangle=(s_{1},\rho_{s_{1}})
and
\lvert\$_{2}\rangle=(s_{2},\rho_{s_{2}})
where
\rho_{s_{1}}
and
\rho_{s_{2}}
are allowed to be entangled. The challenger accepts if and only if
\mathsf{Ver}
accepts both
\lvert\$_{1}\rangle
and
\lvert\$_{2}\rangle
and
s_{1}=s_{2}=s
. A quantum money scheme
(\mathsf{Gen},\mathsf{Bank},\mathsf{Ver})
is said to be secure if for any polynomial time quantum adversary
A
, the challenger accepts in the above experiment with probability negligible in
\kappa
.

3
Quantum Money From Hidden Subspaces

In this section, we investigate the security of the quantum money scheme proposed by Aaronson and Christiano
[
3
]
, which is based on hidden linear subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.

3.1
The quantum money scheme

Let
n=\kappa
where
\kappa
is the security parameter. The
n
-qubit money state in this scheme is a uniform superposition

\lvert A\rangle=\frac{1}{2^{n/4}}\sum_{x\in A}\lvert x\rangle,

where
A\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is a random linear subspace of dimension
n/2
. The bank generates such a state by choosing a set of
n/2
random and linearly independent elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and building a superposition from their linear combinations. The state
\lvert A\rangle
is then verified by querying to classical oracles
U_{A}
and
U_{A^{\perp}}
where
A^{\perp}
is the orthogonal complement of
A
. These oracles are membership oracles. More precisely, for
x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}

U_{A}\lvert x\rangle=\begin{cases}-\lvert x\rangle&\text{if }x\in A,\\
\lvert x\rangle&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The oracle
U_{A^{\perp}}
is defined similarly. Using these oracles we can build projectors
\mathbb{P}_{A}
and
\mathbb{P}_{A^{\perp}}
onto the basis elements of
A
and
A^{\perp}
, respectively. The verification process works by first projecting onto
A
, then applying the
n
-qubit Hadamard transform
H^{\otimes n}
, then projecting on
A^{\perp}
and finally applying another
H^{\otimes n}
. So the verification algorithm can be written as
V_{A}:=H^{\otimes n}\mathbb{P}_{A^{\perp}}H^{\otimes n}\mathbb{P}_{A}
. It can be shown that
V_{A}=\lvert A\rangle\langle A\rvert
, i.e.,
V_{A}
is just a projector onto
A
. This means that the verification algorithm accepts a state
\lvert\psi\rangle
with probability
\lvert\langle\psi|A\rangle\rvert^{2}
.

In the above scheme, the following algorithms are publicly accessible.

\mathsf{G}(r)
:

takes a random
r\in\{0,1\}^{n}
as input. Outputs a set of random linearly independent elements
\{y_{1},\dots,y_{n/2}\}
for a subspace
A_{r}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, and a serial number
s_{r}
.

\mathsf{H}(s)
:

checks if
s
is a valid serial number.

\mathsf{T}(r)
:

takes an input of the form
\lvert s\rangle\lvert x\rangle
and applies
U_{A_{r}}
to
\lvert x\rangle
if
s
is a valid serial number.

\mathsf{T}^{\perp}(r)
:

similar to
\mathsf{T}(r)
but with
U_{A_{r}}
replaced by
U_{A_{r}^{\perp}}
.

The
\mathsf{Bank}
and
\mathsf{Ver}
algorithms are then defined as follows:

\mathsf{Bank}

calls
\mathsf{G}(r)
for a uniformly random
r\in\{0,1\}^{n}
to receive a pair
(s_{r},A_{r})
, and outputs a banknote
\lvert\$_{r}\rangle=\lvert s_{r}\rangle\lvert A_{r}\rangle
.

\mathsf{Ver}

takes as input a banknote
\cent
, and uses
\mathsf{H}
to check if
\cent
is of the form
(s,\rho)
for a valid serial number
s
. If
s
is valid then it uses
\mathsf{T}
and
\mathsf{T}^{\perp}
to compute
V_{A}(\rho)
, and outputs ‘accept’ if and only if
V_{A}
outputs ‘accepts’.

Assuming black box access to the oracles
U_{A}
and
U_{A^{\perp}}
, it was proved in
[
3
]
that a counterfeiter needs at least
\Omega(\sqrt{\epsilon}2^{n/4})
queries to prepare a state
\rho
such that
\langle A\rvert^{\otimes 2}\rho\lvert A\rangle^{\otimes 2}\geq\epsilon
. However, there has been no provably secure instantiation of these oracles to this date.

The first instantiation of
U_{A}
and
U_{A^{\perp}}
was proposed in the same paper
[
3
]
, which was based on multivariate polynomials. The idea is to “obfuscate” the subspace
A
using a set of polynomials
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. More precisely, let
I_{d,A}
be the set of polynomials of degree
d
in
\mathbb{F}_{2}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
that vanish on
A
. Then the polynomials
p_{i}
are selected uniformly at random from
I_{d,A}
. For a large enough
m
, the polynomials
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}
uniquely determine
A
with overwhelming probability. This means for all
v\in A
we will have
p_{1}(v)=\cdots=p_{m}(v)=0
, and for any
v\notin A
we will have
p_{k}(v)\neq 0
for at least one
1\leq k\leq m
with overwhelming probability. This is proved in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
3
, Lemma 29]
)
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a linear subspace and let
\beta>1
be a constant. For any set of polynomials
p_{1},\dots,p_{\beta n}\in I_{d,A}
selected uniformly at random, define
Z=\{v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}:p_{i}(v)=0\text{ for all }1\leq i\leq\beta n\}
. Then
A\subseteq Z
, and
\Pr[A=Z]=1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
.

One can sample uniformly at random from
I_{d,A}
in time
O(n^{d})
which is polynomial when
d
is a constant. An immediate consequence of Lemma
3.1
is that the set of random polynomials
p_{1},\dots,p_{\beta n}
can be effectively used as a membership oracle for
A
. Therefore, the banknote
(s_{r},\lvert A_{r}\rangle)
, which can be generated efficiently by the bank, consists of a superposition over the random subspace
A_{r}
and the list of polynomials
s_{r}=\{p_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq\beta n}
corresponding to
A_{r}
. The hardness assumption underlying the quantum money scheme of
[
3
]
is based on the following problem:

Problem 3.2
.

Let
d\geq 3
be a constant integer and
\beta>1
a real number. Let
A\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a random linear subspace of dimension
n/2
and let
p_{1},\dots,p_{\beta n}\in I_{d,A}
be selected uniformly at random. Given the banknote
(s,\lvert A\rangle)
, where
s=\{p_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq\beta n}
, recover a set of generators for
A
.

A noisy version of Problem
3.2
was also introduced in
[
3
]
, but was later shown to be quantum polynomial time equivalent to the noiseless version
[
2
, Section 9.6]
. In this paper, we give a quantum polynomial time algorithm for Problem
3.2
. Our idea is based on computing the Zariski tangent space of the variety described by the set of polynomials
s
.

3.2
Background

In this section, we review some basic facts about tangent spaces in algebraic geometry. Let
X
be a scheme, and let
x\in X
be any point. Let
\kappa(x)=\mathscr{O}_{X,x}/\mathfrak{m}_{x}
be the residue field at
x
, where
\mathscr{O}_{X,x}
is the local ring at
x
and
\mathfrak{m}_{x}
is the maximal ideal of
\mathscr{O}_{X,x}
. Then
\mathfrak{m}_{x}/\mathfrak{m}_{x}^{2}
is a vector space over
\kappa(x)
. The Zariski tangent space of
X
in
x
is defined as the dual vector space

T_{x}X=(\mathfrak{m}_{x}/\mathfrak{m}_{x}^{2})^{\vee}.

The following are standard in algebraic geometry, but include some explanations for the sake of completeness.

Fact 3.3
.

If
Y\subseteq X
is a closed subscheme, and
y\in Y
, then
T_{y}Y\subseteq T_{y}X
. This is because the morphism

\mathfrak{m}_{y}/\mathfrak{m}_{y}^{2}\rightarrow\mathfrak{m}_{x}/\mathfrak{m}_{x}^{2}

of
\kappa(y)
-vector spaces, where
\mathfrak{m}_{y}
is the maximal ideal of
\mathscr{O}_{Y,y}
, is surjective. Therefore, the dual morphism is injective.

Fact 3.4
.

For closed subschemes
Y,Z\subseteq X
and a point
y\in Y\cap Z
, we have
T_{y}(Y\cap Z)=T_{y}Y\cap T_{y}Z
. Here,
Y\cap Z
is the scheme-theoretic intersection. Locally, we can replace
X
with a
\spec(A)
, for a ring
A
, so that
Y
and
Z
are described by some ideals
I,J\subset A
. Then the intersection
Y\cap Z
is
\spec A/(I+J)
, and the above statement can be proved using straightforward commutative algebra.

Let
k
be a field. By a
k
-variety
X
we mean a reduced separated scheme of finite type over
k
. In this paper, we are interested in affine varieties, i.e., in schemes of the form
X=\spec k[T_{1},\dots,T_{n}]/I
where
I=(f_{1},\dots,f_{m})
is an ideal in the polynomial ring
k[T_{1},\dots,T_{n}]
. A
k
-variety is called linear if it is defined by the intersection of hyperplanes. Therefore,
X
is linear when the defining polynomials
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
all have degree one. A point
x\in X
is called a
k
-valued point if
\kappa(x)=k
. When
X
is affine, the Zariski tangent space at a
k
-valued point
x
can be computed using the Jacobian matrix at
x
. The Jacobian matrix of the set of polynomials
f_{1},\dots,f_{m}
at
x
is defined as

J_{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}}(x)=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial T_{1}}(x)&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial f_{1}}{\partial T_{n}}(x)\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\dfrac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial T_{1}}(x)&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial f_{m}}{\partial T_{n}}(x)\end{bmatrix},

where the derivatives
\partial f_{i}/\partial T_{j}
are formal derivatives.

For the affine variety
X
defined by the ideal
I
as above, the tangent space
T_{x}X
at the point
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in X
is the linear variety defined by the coordinates of the vector

J_{f_{1},\dots,f_{m}}(x)\begin{bmatrix}T_{1}-x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
T_{n}-x_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

(1)

In other words, letting

g_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f_{i}}{\partial T_{j}}(x)(T_{j}-x_{j}),

the space
T_{x}X
is defined as the zero set of the ideal
(g_{1},\dots,g_{m})
. It follows from the above that

Fact 3.5
.

If
X
is a linear
k
-variety and
x\in X
is a
k
-valued point, then
T_{x}X=X
.

3.3
Cryptanalysis

In this section, we give a polynomial time quantum algorithm for Problem
3.2
. We are given a banknote
(s,\lvert A\rangle)
where
A\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is a random linear subspace of dimension
n/2
, and
s
is a set of polynomials
p_{1},\dots,p_{m}\in I_{d,A}
, where
m=\beta n
,
\beta>1
. The first step is to measure the state
\lvert A\rangle
to obtain uniformly random element
x\in A
. So now we have two pieces of classical information: the set of polynomials
\{p_{i}\}
and a uniformly random
x\in A
.

Geometrically, the subspace
A
is the set of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-valued points in a linear variety
Y
given by an ideal
(H_{1},\dots,H_{n/2})
with
H_{i}
’s homogeneous linear polynomials. It follows from (
1
) and Fact
3.5
that
T_{x}Y=Y
. In fact, since the
H_{i}
are homogeneous, we have

Y=\ker J_{H_{1},\dots,H_{n/2}}(x).

Define the ideal
I=(p_{1},\dots,p_{m})
, and let
X=\spec\mathbb{F}_{2}[T_{1},\dots,T_{n}]/I
. By Lemma
3.1
, we can safely assume that the varieties
Y
and
X
have the same set of
\mathbb{F}_{2}
-valued points. If we define
X_{i}=\spec\mathbb{F}_{2}[T_{1},\dots,T_{n}]/(p_{i})
then
X=X_{1}\cap\cdots\cap X_{m}
.

Since
p_{i}\in I_{d,A}
, we have
Y\subseteq X_{i}
for all
i=1,\dots,m
, and therefore, by Fact
3.3
,

Y=T_{x}Y\subseteq T_{x}X_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,m.

From this and Fact
3.4
we obtain

Y\subseteq T_{x}X_{1}\cap\cdots\cap T_{x}X_{m}=T_{x}X.

(2)

We prove that equality holds in (
2
) with high probability.

Proposition 3.6
.

We have
\Pr[Y=T_{x}X]=1-2^{-\Omega(n)}
in (
2
).

Proof.

Note that the tangent space
T_{x}X_{i}
is given by the points on the hyperplane
h_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\partial p_{i}/\partial T_{j})(x)(T_{j}-x_{j})
. Since
Y\subseteq T_{x}X_{i}
, we have
h_{i}\in I_{1,A}
. We first prove the following claim.

Claim
. For any

, exactly half of the elements in

vanish at
v
.

The claim can be proved using the same trick as in the proof of Lemma
3.1
. More precisely, there exists a

such that

, so for every
h\in I_{1,A}
we also have
\tilde{h}=h+w_{1}T_{1}+\cdots+w_{n}T_{n}\in I_{1,A}
. The claim now follows from the fact that exactly one of the
h
and
\tilde{h}
vanish at
v
.

Suppose, for now, that the hyperplanes
T_{x}X_{i}
, or equivalently the
w_{i}
, are uniformly random among the hyperplanes containing
Y
. Let
v\notin A
. Then it follows from the above claim that
v\in T_{x}X_{1}\cap\cdots\cap T_{x}X_{m}
with probability
2^{-m}
, which prove the proposition. So it remains to prove that the hyperplanes
w_{i}\in I_{1,A}
are uniformly random when the
p_{i}\in I_{d,A}
are uniformly random. Define the mapping

\begin{array}[]{rrll}D_{x}:&I_{d,A}&\longrightarrow&I_{1,A}\\
&f&\longmapsto&\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\partial f}{\partial T_{j}}(x)(T_{j}-x_{j}).\end{array}

Since translation by a point of
A
preserves
I_{d,A}
and
I_{1,A}
, we may assume that
x
is the origin. In that case, the mapping
D_{x}
sends a polynomial
f\in I_{d,A}
to its linear part. That means if we write
f=g+h
where
\deg(h)\leq 1
then
D_{x}(f)=h\in I_{1,A}
. Now if
f
is uniformly random then every monomial of
f
is uniformly random (among the monomials vanishing on
A
) and therefore, the linear part of
f
is also uniformly random.
∎

To summarize, the hidden subspace can be recovered using the following algorithm.

1.

A banknote
(s,\lvert A\rangle)

2.

A set of generators for the subspace
A

3.

Measure the state
\lvert A\rangle
to obtain a uniformly random
x\in A
.

4.

Compute the Jacobian matrix
J_{s}(x)
of the set of polynomials
s
at
x
.

5.

Return a set of generators for the kernel of
J_{s}(x)
.

In this section, we investigate the security of the quantum money scheme proposed in
[

m

n

\mathcal{A}=\{\bm{A}_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq n}
where each
\bm{A}_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m\times m}

\begin{array}[]{rrll}f_{\mathcal{A}}:&\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\\

The function

is not collision resistant
[

are random upper triangular.

The hardness assumption underlying this scheme is instead based on the multi-collision resistance of

. Let us briefly define what that means. A set of

\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}
is called non-affine if they form a
k

is said to be
(k+1)
-non-affine multi-collision resistant (
(k+1)
-NAMCR) if it is hard to find
k+1

,

and random upper triangular
\bm{A}_{i}
, the function

is
2(k+1)
-NAMCR.

4.1
The quantum money scheme

In the following, we briefly explain the quantum money scheme of
[
29
]
. The public parameters of the scheme are:

•

The integers
m,n
. We set

where

•

The hash function

. The upper triangular matrices in

are generated uniformly at random.

A banknote in this scheme consists of a pair
(y,\lvert\text{\Lightning}_{y}\rangle)
where
\lvert\text{\Lightning}_{y}\rangle

, is the money state and

such that

. More precisely,

\lvert\text{\Lightning}_{y}\rangle\propto\lvert\text{\Lightning}_{y}^{\prime}\rangle^{\otimes(k+1)},\quad\text{where }\lvert\text{\Lightning}_{y}^{\prime}\rangle\propto\sum_{x:f_{\mathcal{A}}(x)=y}\lvert x\rangle.

We refer the reader to
[
29
]
for the details of how a bolt is generated. Since we will use the verification circuit in our attack, we include some details here. To verify a bolt, one needs to only verify each of the states
\lvert\text{\Lightning}_{y}^{\prime}\rangle
independently. The verification of an alleged banknote
\lvert\psi\rangle
proceeds in two steps: first, the state
\lvert\psi\rangle
is projected onto the span of the states
\{\lvert\text{\Lightning}_{z}^{\prime}\rangle\}_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}
. Then the function
f_{\mathcal{A}}
is computed into an auxiliary register and measured. The result of the measurement is then compared against
y
to determine the validity of the banknote.

The states
\{\lvert\text{\Lightning}_{z}^{\prime}\rangle\}_{z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}
are orthogonal and span a linear subspace
B\subset\mathbb{C}^{2^{m}}
of dimension
2^{n}
. To project onto
B
, a different set of basis states of

are used.

Fact 4.1

(
[
29
]
)
.

Define the set of states

\lvert\phi_{r}\rangle=\frac{1}{2^{m/2}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{m}}(-1)^{r\cdot f_{\mathcal{A}}(x)}\lvert x\rangle,\quad r\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}.

Then the states
\{\lvert\text{\Lightning}_{z}^{\prime}\rangle\}
and
\{\lvert\phi_{r}\rangle\}
span the same subspace

Therefore, to project onto

\lvert\phi_{r}\rangle
. It is easy to prepare
\lvert\phi_{r}\rangle

. Conversely, it was shown in
[
29
]

Theorem 4.2

(
[
29
]
)
.

There is a polynomial time quantum algorithm that, given the state

with overwhelming probability.

The verification procedure can now be summarized as follows. Given the input state

Cryptanalysis

In this section, we propose a polynomial time quantum algorithm that given a nonzero serial number
y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, generates a copy of the state

\lvert\text{\Lightning}_{y}^{\prime}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{C_{y}}}\sum_{x:f_{\mathcal{A}}(x)=y}\lvert x\rangle,

where

1

We start by noting that
[
29
]

C_{0}\lvert\text{\Lightning}_{0}^{\prime}\rangle+C_{y}\lvert\text{\Lightning}_{y}^{\prime}\rangle\propto\sum_{r:r\cdot y=0}\lvert\phi_{r}\rangle.

(3)

Let
\lvert\psi_{y}\rangle
be the normalized quantum state proportional to the state (
3

\lvert\psi_{y}\rangle
is a uniform superposition over the elements of

\lvert\psi_{y}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\#X_{y}}}\sum_{x\in X_{y}}\lvert x\rangle.

Suppose there is a quantum algorithm
Q
that, given

\lvert\psi_{y}\rangle
. To generate a copy of
\lvert\text{\Lightning}_{y}^{\prime}\rangle

\lvert\psi_{y}\rangle
. Compute

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\#X_{y}}}\sum_{x\in X_{y}}\lvert x\rangle\lvert f_{\mathcal{A}}(x)\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\#X_{y}}}(\sqrt{C_{0}}\lvert\text{\Lightning}_{0}^{\prime}\rangle\lvert 0\rangle+\sqrt{C_{y}}\lvert\text{\Lightning}_{y}^{\prime}\rangle\lvert y\rangle),

The probability of obtaining the state

\dim f_{\mathcal{A}}^{-1}(0)=\dim\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}^{m}-\dim\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}^{n}=m-n
1
1

1

It only remains to show that algorithm

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n-1}}}\sum_{r:r\cdot y=0}\lvert r\rangle.

(5)

Next, compute
\lvert\phi_{r}\rangle
into another register to obtain the state

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^{n-1}}}\sum_{r:r\cdot y=0}\lvert r\rangle\lvert\phi_{r}\rangle.

(6)

Using Theorem
4.2
, uncompute

and discard the first register. This produces the state

\lvert\psi_{y}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{C}}\sum_{r:r\cdot y=0}\lvert\phi_{r}\rangle,

for an appropriate normalization constant

. The above algorithm is summarized as follows.

1.

A serial number

2.

A bolt
\lvert\text{\Lightning}_{y}^{\prime}\rangle

3.

Compute a set

of generators for the kernel linear map

4.

Using

5.

Compute

into another register to obtain the state (

6.

Uncompute

into an extra register to obtain the state (

]
, which is based on quaternion algebras. The hard problem underlying this scheme is copying a quantum state that is encoding an eigenform of the Hecke operators. We give a reduction from this problem to a linear algebra problem, namely inverting a matrix with entries the representation numbers of quaternary quadratic forms. We believe that the latter problem is more suitable, and much more accessible, for future cryptanalysis.

Informally, the idea of our reduction is as follows. Suppose the quantum state to be copied is given by
\lvert\phi\rangle=\sum\alpha_{I}\lvert I\rangle
where the sum is over the set
\{I\}_{I\in\cl(\mathcal{O})}
of representatives of the left ideal classes of a maximal order
\mathcal{O}
in a quaternion algebra. Let
M
be the formal abelian group with basis
\cl(\mathcal{O})
. For a fixed
J\in\cl(\mathcal{O})
, based on a morphism from
M
to the space of modular forms we obtain the identity

f(q)=\sum_{I\in\cl(\mathcal{O})}\frac{\alpha_{I}}{\alpha_{J}}\Theta_{I,J}(q),

where the left hand side is the modular form corresponding to the formal sum
\sum_{I\in\cl(\mathcal{O})}\alpha_{I}[I]
, and the
\Theta_{I,J}
are theta series corresponding to the ideals
I,J
. By equating the coefficients of different powers of
q
in both sides of the above identity, we form a system of linear equations. The entries of the coefficient matrix
A
associated with this system are the representation numbers from the theta series
\Theta_{I,J}
. The dimension of the system is equal to the size of the set
\cl(\mathcal{O})
which is exponentially large. We show that if one is able to approximate the operation
A^{-1}
then one can make arbitrary many copies of
\lvert\phi\rangle
.

5.1.1
Modular forms.

Let
\mathbb{H}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\mathrm{Im}(z)>0\}
be the complex upper half plane, and let
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
be the special linear group over the integers. For
\gamma=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
z\in\mathbb{H}
define
\gamma z=(az+b)/(cz+d)
, which defines a group action on
\mathbb{H}
. For any integer
N>0
denote by
\Gamma(N)
the kernel of the projection
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/N\mathbb{Z})
. A congruence subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
of level
N
is any subgroup
\Gamma
containing
\Gamma(N)
. In this paper, we are particularly interested in the congruence subgroup

\Gamma_{0}(N)=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}\in\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}):\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}*&*\\
0&*\end{bmatrix}\pmod{N}\right\}.

The weight-
k
operator
[\gamma]_{k}
on the functions
f\in\hom(\mathbb{H},\mathbb{C})
is defined by
(f[\gamma]_{k})(\tau)=(cz+d)^{-k}f(\gamma z)
. This is also a group action on
\hom(\mathbb{H},\mathbb{C})
since
f[\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}]_{k}=(f[\gamma_{1}]_{k})[\gamma_{2}]_{k}
.

A weakly modular function of weight
k
with respect to a congruence subgroup
\Gamma
is a meromorphic function
f:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that
f[\gamma]_{k}=f
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Define the extended upper half plane
\mathbb{H}^{*}
by adding the set of rational points of the projective line to
\mathbb{H}
, i.e.,
\mathbb{H}^{*}=\mathbb{H}\cup\mathbb{P}^{1}(\mathbb{Q})
. A
modular form
of weight
k
with respect to
\Gamma
is a weakly modular function
f:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
that is holomorphic on
\mathbb{H}^{*}
. Every modular form
f
has a Fourier expansion

f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}q^{n},\quad q(z)=e^{2\pi iz}

called the
q
-expansion of
f
. If
a_{0}=0
in the
q
-expansion of
f
, we say that
f
is a
cuspform
.

5.1.2
Quaternion algebras.

Let
F
be a field of characteristic
\neq 2
. An
F
-algebra
B
is called a quaternion algebra if
B
has a basis
1,i,j,k
as an
F
-vector space such that
i^{2}=a,j^{2}=b
and
k=ij=-ji
for some
a,b\in F^{\times}
. There is an involution operation
{}^{-}:B\rightarrow B
defined by
t+xi+yj+zk\mapsto t-xi-yj-zk
. The reduced trace
\mathrm{trd}:B\rightarrow F
and the reduced norm
\mathrm{nrd}:B\rightarrow F
are defined by
\trd(\alpha)=\alpha+\overline{\alpha}
and
\nrd(\alpha)=\alpha\overline{\alpha}
. For
F=\mathbb{Q}
, a quaternion algebra
B
is said to be ramified at a prime
p
if the completion
B\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{Q}_{p}
is a division ring, otherwise
B
is unramified at
p
. In this paper, we are interested in the quaternion algebra
B
over
\mathbb{Q}
, denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}
, that is ramified at a single primes
p
and at
\infty
.

A
\mathbb{Z}
-lattice
I\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}
of rank
4
is called a fractional ideal. A
\mathbb{Z}
-order (or simply an order)
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}
is a fractional ideal that is also a ring. A maximal order is an order that is not properly contained in another order. For a fractional ideal
I\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}
define

\mathcal{O}_{L}(I)=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}:\alpha I\subseteq I\}.

Then
\mathcal{O}_{L}(I)
is also an order and is called the left order of
I
. The right order of
I
is defined similarly by
\mathcal{O}_{R}(I)=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}:I\alpha\subseteq I\}
. An ideal
I
is invertible if there is another ideal
I^{\prime}
such that
II^{\prime}=\mathcal{O}_{L}(I)=\mathcal{O}_{R}(I^{\prime})
and
I^{\prime}I=\mathcal{O}_{L}(I^{\prime})=\mathcal{O}_{R}(I)
. We denote the inverse of
I
, if it exists, by
I^{-1}
. The reduced norm of an ideal
I
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-submodule of
\mathbb{Q}
generated by the set
\{\nrd(\alpha):\alpha\in I\}
. The inverse of
I
can also be written as
I^{-1}=\overline{I}\nrd(I)^{-1}
where
\overline{I}=\{\overline{\alpha}:\alpha\in I\}
is the involution of
I
. For an order
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}
, A left fractional
\mathcal{O}
-ideal is a fractional ideal
I
such that
\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{L}(I)
. Right fractional
\mathcal{O}
-ideals are defined similarly.

Two fractional ideals
I,J
are said to be in the same right class if
I=\alpha J
for some
\alpha\in\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}^{\times}
, in which case we write
I\sim_{R}J
. The relation
\sim_{R}
defines an equivalence relation on the set of fractional ideals, and a class of an ideal
I
is denoted by
[I]_{R}
. Left equivalence classes are defined similarly. For an order
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}
, the right class set of
\mathcal{O}
is defined as the isomorphism classes of invertible right
\mathcal{O}
-ideals, i.e.,

\cl_{R}(\mathcal{O})=\{[I]_{R}:I\text{ an invertible }\mathcal{O}\text{-ideal}\}.

Since the mapping
I\mapsto\overline{I}
defines a bijection between left and right classes we often simply write
\cl(\mathcal{O})
instead of
\cl_{R}(\mathcal{O})
.

5.1.3
Hecke operators.

Denote by
M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
the space of modular forms of weight
k
with respect to the subgroup
\Gamma_{0}(N)
, and denote by
S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
the space of cuspforms of weight
k
with respect to
\Gamma_{0}(N)
. For an integer
n
with
(n,N)=1
, the
n
-th Hecke operator on
M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
is the linear operator

\begin{array}[]{rrll}T_{n}:&M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))&\longrightarrow&M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))\\
&f(z)&\longmapsto&\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{k-1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}ad=n\\
0\leq b<d\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{d^{k}}f\left(\frac{az+b}{d}\right)\end{array}

We will always assume that
(n,N)=1
, since this is what we are interested in, and also the theory is a bit simpler in this case. From the definition, we see that
T_{n}
preserves the subspace
S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
. The Hecke operators for
M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
satisfy

\displaystyle T_{mn}

\displaystyle=T_{m}T_{n}

\displaystyle(m,n)=1,

(8)

\displaystyle T_{p^{r+1}}

\displaystyle=T_{p^{r}}T_{p}-p^{k-1}T_{p^{r-1}}

\displaystyle p\text{ prime}.

For a modular form
f
, let
a_{n}(f)
be the
n
-th coefficient of the
q
-expantion of
f
. Then for
f\in M_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
and prime
p
we have

a_{n}(T_{p}f)=\begin{cases}a_{np}(f)+p^{k-1}a_{n/p}(f)&\text{if }p\mid n,\\
a_{np}(f)&\text{if }p\nmid n.\end{cases}

(9)

It follows that for coprime integers
m,n
we have
a_{m}(T_{n}f)=a_{mn}(f)
, and in particular,
a_{1}(T_{n}f)=a_{n}(f)
. Define
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{Z}[\{T_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}]
, the algebra generated by all the Hecke operators. Then
\mathbb{T}
is a commutative ring called the
Hecke algebra
. Since
\mathbb{T}
is commutative, there are elements
f\in S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
that are simultaneous eigenvectors for all element in
\mathbb{T}
. In particular, for any such
f
we have
T_{n}f=\lambda_{n}f
for some
\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. We refer to such
f
as an
eigenfunction
. If
f
is normalized, i.e.,
a_{1}(f)=1
, it is called an
eigenform
. The coefficient of the
q
-expansion of an eigenform satisfy important identities induced by (
8
). More precisely, let
f=\sum_{n}a_{n}q^{n}
be an eigenform, and assume
T_{n}f=\lambda_{n}f
. Then we have
a_{n}=\lambda_{n}
, and using (
8
) we obtain

\displaystyle a_{mn}

\displaystyle=a_{m}a_{n}

\displaystyle(m,n)=1,

(10)

\displaystyle a_{p^{r+1}}

\displaystyle=a_{p^{r}}a_{p}-p^{k-1}a_{p^{r-1}}

\displaystyle p\text{ prime}.

Let
d\mu(z)=dxdy/y^{2}
,
z=x+iy\in\mathbb{H}
be the hyperbolic measure, and let
V_{\Gamma_{0}(N)}
be volume of the modular curve
X(\Gamma_{0}(N)))
with respect to
d\mu(z)
. The space
S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
can be made into an inner product space using the Petersson inner product

\langle f,g\rangle=\frac{1}{V_{\Gamma_{0}(N)}}\int_{X(\Gamma_{0}(N))}f(z)\overline{g(z)}(\mathrm{Im}(z))^{k}d\mu(z).

The Hecke operators
T_{n}
are Hermitian with respect to this inner product, i.e.,
\langle T_{n}f,g\rangle=\langle f,T_{n}g\rangle
. This means there exists a basis of
S_{k}(\Gamma_{0}(N))
consisting of Hecke eigenforms.

One can also define a set of Hecke operators on ideal classes of a quaternion order as follows. Let
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}
be an order and let
\cl(\mathcal{O})
be the (right) class set of
\mathcal{O}
. Define the formal divisor group

M=\bigoplus_{I\in\cl(\mathcal{O})}\mathbb{Z}.[I]

(11)

For an integer
n>0
,
n\neq p
, the
n
-th Hecke operator on
M
is defined by

\begin{array}[]{rrll}T_{n}:&M&\longrightarrow&M\\
&[I]&\longmapsto&\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subseteq I\\
\nrd(JI^{-1})=n\end{subarray}}[J],\end{array}

where the sum is over all invertible right
\mathcal{O}
-ideals contained in
I
. In the basis of the ideal classes in
\cl(\mathcal{O})
, the Hecke operator
T_{n}
is represented by the
Brandt matrix

B(n)
which can be explicitly computed as follows. Let
h=\#\cl(\mathcal{O})
be the class number of
\mathcal{O}
, and fix a set
I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{h}
of representatives of the ideal classes in
\cl(\mathcal{O})
. Let
\mathcal{O}_{i}=\mathcal{O}_{L}(I_{i})
, and let
w_{i}
be the number of units in
\mathcal{O}_{i}
. The
n
-th Brandt matrix is defined by

B(n)_{ij}=\frac{1}{w_{i}}\#\{\alpha\in I_{j}I_{i}^{-1}:\nrd(\alpha)=n\nrd(I_{j}I_{i}^{-1})\}.

(12)

The Hecke operators
T_{n}
also satisfy the relations (
8
). Moreover, we can define an inner product on
M
with respect to which the
T_{n}
are Hermitian:
\langle[I_{i}],[I_{j}]\rangle=\delta_{i,j}w_{i}/2
where
\delta_{i,j}=1
if
i=j
, and
\delta_{i,j}=0
otherwise. We will also call this inner product the Petersson inner product. Similar to the case of modular forms, there is a set of elements of
M_{\mathbb{R}}=M\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{R}
that are simultaneous eigenvectors for all Hecke operators. We also call these element Hecke eigenforms. The element

\phi_{E}=\sum_{j=1}^{h}2w_{j}^{-1}[I_{j}]

of
M_{\mathbb{R}}
is called the Eisenstein eigenform. We have
T_{n}\phi_{E}=\sigma^{\prime}(n)
, where
\sigma^{\prime}(n)=\sum_{d\mid n,(p,d)=1}d
. The sub-
\mathbb{T}
-module of
M_{\mathbb{R}}
orthogonal to
\phi_{E}
is called the space of cuspforms. For any cuspform
\phi=\sum_{j=1}^{h}\alpha_{j}[I_{j}]
we have
\sum_{j=1}^{h}\alpha_{j}=0
.

5.2
The quantum money scheme

Let us briefly review the quantum money scheme presented in
[
14
]
. The parameters of the scheme are fixed as follows:

•

A prime
p\in O(2^{\kappa})
where
\kappa
is the security parameter.

•

The quaternion algebra
\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}
over
\mathbb{Q}
ramified at
p
and
\infty
.

•

A maximal order
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}
of discriminant
p
.

•

A set of Hecke operators
T_{n_{j}}
where
n_{j}=\poly(\kappa)
,
1\leq j\leq s
and
s=\poly(\kappa)
.

Let
I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{d}
be distinct representatives of the ideal classes in
\cl(\mathcal{O})
, where
d=\#\cl(\mathcal{O})
. In the above specific setup we have
d=\lfloor p/12\rfloor
. Let
M
be the formal divisor group on
\cl(\mathcal{O})
defined in (
11
). For simplicity, we will let
M_{\mathbb{C}}
denote
M\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\mathbb{C}
. Let
\{\phi_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq d}
be a set of eigenforms in
M_{\mathbb{C}}
that are a basis of
M_{\mathbb{C}}
, and let
\{\lvert\phi_{i}\rangle\}_{1\leq i\leq d}
be the corresponding quantum states. This means that for
\phi_{i}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}[I_{i}]
, we have

\lvert\phi_{i}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}\lvert I_{j}\rangle

where
\alpha^{2}=\sum_{j}\lvert\alpha_{j}\rvert^{2}
.
A banknote in the quantum money scheme consists of

1.

A money state of the form
\lvert\phi_{i}\rangle\lvert\phi_{i}\rangle
for some
1\leq i\leq d
,

2.

A serial number, which is a tuple
(b_{j})_{1\leq j\leq s}
of approximate eigenvalues of the
T_{n_{j}}
corresponding to the eigenform
\phi_{i}
.

3.

A classical digital signature of the serial number
(b_{j})_{1\leq j\leq s}
.

The verification of a banknote is done by

1.

Verifying the signature of the serial number

2.

Using phase estimation on the operators
e^{iT_{n_{j}}}
and the eigenstate
\lvert\phi_{i}\rangle
to recover an approximate serial number and compare against
(b_{j})_{1\leq j\leq s}
.

It takes more than a few pages to give a precise account on the implementation of the above scheme. Here, we briefly explain two main points.

Hamiltonian simulation.

To prepare an eigenstate
\lvert\phi_{i}\rangle
we start from the uniform superposition
\lvert\phi\rangle=\sum_{I\in\cl(\mathcal{O})}\lvert I\rangle
(normalization omitted). Then we use phase estimation on the operators
U_{j}=e^{iT_{n_{j}}}
to project onto an eigenstate
\lvert\phi_{i}\rangle
. Since the matrices of the operators
T_{n_{j}}
are sparse, standard Hamiltonian simulation techniques can be used to implement the unitaries
U_{j}
. However, for an integer
t>0
, the cost of simulating
U_{j}^{t}=e^{iT_{n_{j}}t}
depends linearly on
t
. More precisely, the simulation cost depends linearly on
st\lVert T_{n_{j}}\rVert_{\max}

[
4
]
, where
s
is the sparsity degree of
T_{n_{j}}
, i.e., the maximum number of nonzero entries in any row, and
\lVert T_{n_{j}}\rVert_{\max}
is the largest entry of
T_{n_{j}}
in absolute value. This means, using phase estimation, we can approximate the eigenvalues of the
T_{n_{j}}
only to accuracy
1/\poly(\kappa)
.

Projecting onto an eigenstate.

To be able to project onto a single eigenstate, we cannot use only one of the
T_{n_{j}}
, since in that case we would need an exponentially accurate phase estimation. However, if we run a polynomially accurate phase estimation for all the
T_{n_{j}}
, we might be able to project onto a random eigenstate
\lvert\phi_{i}\rangle
. For this to hold, one needs to make some heuristic assumptions on the distribution of the eigenvalues of the Heck operators. Such distributions are already studied in the literature
[
13
,
20
,
5
]
, but a formal treatment of the running time of the above projection based on these distributions is nontrivial.

5.3
Cryptanalysis

In this section, we analyze the assumption behind the scheme in Section
5.2
. We use the connection between quaternion orders and modular forms to translate the problem to the space of modular forms. The connection is provided by the theory of theta series.

5.3.1
From quaternions to modular forms.

Let
m=2k>0
be an integer and let
Q:\mathbb{Z}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
be an integral positive definite quadratic form. The
theta series
associated to
Q
is a function
\Theta_{Q}:\mathbb{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
defined by

\Theta_{Q}(z)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}}q^{Q(x)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r_{Q}(n)q^{n},\quad q=e^{2\pi iz}.

The integers
r_{Q}(n)=\#\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}:Q(x)=n\}
are called the
representation numbers
of
Q
. Now, let
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{B}_{p,\infty}
be a maximal order as in Section
5.2
. For any two ideals
I_{i},I_{j}\subseteq\mathcal{O}
define the mapping

\begin{array}[]{rrll}Q_{i,j}:&I_{j}I_{i}^{-1}&\longrightarrow&\mathbb{Z}\\
&\alpha&\longmapsto&\displaystyle\nrd(\alpha)\nrd(I_{i}I_{j}^{-1})\end{array}

(13)

This is a positive definite quadratic form. Using these quadratic forms we can define the morphism

\begin{array}[]{rrll}\Theta:&M_{\mathbb{C}}\times M_{\mathbb{C}}&\longrightarrow&M_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(p))\\
&([I_{i}],[I_{j}])&\longmapsto&\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in I_{j}I_{i}^{-1}}q^{Q_{i,j}(\alpha)}=1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle T_{n}[I_{i}],[I_{j}]\rangle q^{n},\end{array}

where
q=e^{2\pi iz}
,
z\in\mathbb{H}
. The last equality follows from the definition of Brandt matrices (
12
). Eichler
[
10
]
proved that the spaces
M_{\mathbb{C}}
and
M_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(p))
are isomorphic as Hecke modules, see also
[
15
,
16
]
. More precisely,

Theorem 5.1

(Eichler)
.

The map
T_{n}\mapsto T_{n}
of Hecke operators over
M_{\mathbb{C}}
and
M_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(p))
defines an isomorphism of Hecke algebras. Moreover, the morphism
\Theta
defines a nondegenrate Hecke bilinear map,

\Theta(T_{n}[I_{i}],[I_{j}])=\Theta([I_{i}],T_{n}[I_{j}])=T_{n}\Theta([I_{i}],[I_{j}]),

such that the traces of
T_{n}
on
M_{\mathbb{C}}
and
T_{n}
on
M_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(p))
agree.

If we fix one of the arguments of
\Theta
we get a homomorphism
M_{\mathbb{C}}\rightarrow M_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(p))
. The following lemma explain what the image of
\Theta
is for some specific arguments.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\phi=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}[I_{j}]
be an eigenform. Then for any integer
1\leq k\leq d
the modular form
\Theta([I_{k}],\phi)
is a Hecke eigenfunction. Moreover,
\Theta([I_{k}],\phi)\neq 0
if and only if
\alpha_{k}\neq 0
.

Proof.

The first part follows from Theorem
5.1
. More precisely, for any
k
and

T_{n}\Theta([I_{k}],\phi)=\Theta([I_{k}],T_{n}\phi)=\Theta([I_{k}],\lambda_{n}\phi)=\lambda_{n}\Theta([I_{k}],\phi).

For the second part, expanding
\Theta([I_{k}],\phi)
gives

\displaystyle\Theta([I_{k}],\phi)

\displaystyle=\Theta\bigg([I_{k}],\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}[I_{j}]\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}\Theta([I_{k}],[I_{j}])

\displaystyle(\text{by linearity of }\Theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}\Big(1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle T_{n}[I_{k}],[I_{j}]\rangle q^{n}\Big)

\displaystyle(\text{by definition})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\langle T_{n}[I_{k}],\phi\rangle q^{n}

\displaystyle(\text{by linearity of }\langle\,,\rangle)

(14)

If
\phi
is the Eisenstein eigenform
\phi_{E}
then
\alpha_{k}\neq 0
and
\langle T_{n}[I_{k}],\phi\rangle=\sigma^{\prime}(n)\alpha_{k}\neq 0
, so
\Theta([I_{k}],\phi)\neq 0
and the statement is true. So assume that
\phi
is a cuspform. If
\alpha_{k}=0
then

\langle T_{n}[I_{k}],\phi\rangle=\langle[I_{k}],T_{n}\phi\rangle=\lambda_{n}\langle[I_{k}],\phi\rangle=\lambda_{n}\alpha_{k}=0

(15)

for all
n\geq 1
. It follows from (
14
) and (
15
) that
\Theta([I_{k}],\phi)=0
. If
\alpha_{k}\neq 0
then
\langle[I_{k}],\phi\rangle\neq 0
and again we see from (

) that
\Theta([I_{k}],\phi)\neq 0
.
∎

A direct consequence of Lemma
5.2
is that the dimension of the image of the homomorphism
\Theta([I_{k}],-):M_{\mathbb{C}}\rightarrow M_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(p))
depends on the coefficients
\langle[I_{k}],\phi_{j}\rangle
being zero or not for different eigenforms

. In fact, using the technique in
[

]
, one can prove that

\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Theta([I_{k}],M_{\mathbb{C}})=\lvert\Sigma(k)\rvert

where
\Sigma(k)=\{j:\langle[I_{k}],\phi_{j}\rangle\neq 0\}
. Therefore, one would expect that for a random

,
\langle[I_{k}],\phi\rangle\neq 0
with high probability, or equivalently, that
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}\Theta([I_{k}],M_{\mathbb{C}})
is not too small. We now outline our reduction.

5.3.2
The reduction

Suppose we are given the quantum state

\lvert\phi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}\lvert I_{j}\rangle

which represents a random eigenform
\phi=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}[I_{j}]
. Since
\phi_{j}
is random, it is a cuspform with overwhelming probability. For a random
k
, we assume, by the above remarks, that
\alpha_{k}\neq 0
. Let
f(q)=\Theta([I_{k}],\phi)/\alpha_{k}\in M_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(p))
. Then, by Lemma
5.2
,
f(q)
is a Hecke eigenfunction. A closer look at (
14
) shows that
f(q)
is in fact an eigenform with eigenvalues the same as the eigenvalues of
\phi
. Therefore, we have the identity

f(q)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{k}}\Theta([I_{k}],[I_{j}])

(16)

of modular forms. Let
f(q)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}q^{n}
so that
a_{1},a_{2},\dots
are the eigenvalues of
f
. The idea is to build a system of linear equations, where
\alpha_{j}
are the unknowns, by equating the coefficients of
q^{n}
on both sides of (
16
) for different values of
n
. For any
n\geq 1
we get an equation

a_{n}=\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{k}}\langle T_{n}[I_{k}],[I_{1}]\rangle+\frac{\alpha_{2}}{\alpha_{k}}\langle T_{n}[I_{k}],[I_{2}]\rangle+\cdots+\frac{\alpha_{d}}{\alpha_{k}}\langle T_{n}[I_{k}],[I_{d}]\rangle.

(17)

Let
n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{d}
be a set of positive integers. Gathering linear equations of the form (
17
) for all the
n_{i}
we obtain a system

A\bm{\alpha}=\bm{a}

(18)

where
\bm{a}
is the column vector
[a_{n_{1}},a_{n_{2}},\dots,a_{n_{d}}]^{T}
,
\bm{\alpha}
is the column vector
[\alpha_{1}/\alpha_{k},\alpha_{2}/\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{d}/\alpha_{k}]^{T}
and the matrix
A=(\langle T_{n_{i}}[I_{k}],[I_{j}]\rangle)_{i,j}
. The quantum state representing the vectors
\bm{a}
and
\bm{\alpha}
are

\displaystyle\lvert\bm{a}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{n_{j}}^{2}}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{n_{j}}\lvert j\rangle\text{ and}

\displaystyle\lvert\bm{\alpha}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}^{2}/\alpha_{k}^{2}}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{k}}\lvert I_{j}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}\lvert I_{j}\rangle=\lvert\phi\rangle,

respectively. Suppose there is a quantum algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that can efficiently approximate the operation
A^{-1}
. Then one could compute an approximate copy of
\lvert\phi\rangle
as
\lvert\phi\rangle=A^{-1}\lvert\bm{a}\rangle
. Therefore, assuming access to
\mathcal{A}
, the problem is reduced to the following

Problem 5.3
.

Generate (an approximation of) the state
\lvert\bm{a}\rangle
for an appropriate choice of distinct integers
n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{d}
.

The main obstacle to solving Problem
5.3
is that since
d
is exponentially large, the
n_{i}
will be exponentially large as
i
becomes large regardless of which set of the
n_{i}
we choose. Recall that
a_{n}
is an eigenvalue of
\lvert\phi\rangle
for any
n\geq 1
. The best classical algorithm for computing
a_{n}
has complexity
\poly(p\log n)

[
6
]
, which is polynomial in
\log n
but exponential in
\log p=O(\kappa)
, where
\kappa
is the security parameter. Another possible approach is to use phase estimation to directly compute
a_{n}
, since we know that

e^{iT_{n}}\lvert\phi\rangle=e^{ia_{n}}\lvert\phi\rangle.

This would also fail because of the following reason. It is known that
\lvert a_{n}\rvert\leq\sigma_{0}(n)\sqrt{n}

[
7
]
, where
\sigma_{0}(n)
is the number of positive divisors of
n
. However, the value
\lvert a_{n}\rvert
can be exponentially large in
\kappa
. This means we need to perform phase estimation with exponential accuracy. But we can only simulate
e^{iT_{n}t}
for time
t
that is bounded by
\poly(\kappa)
.

To solve this problem, the idea is to use phase estimation but for a specific set of integers
n_{i}
such that we can exploit the relations (
10
) between the
a_{n_{i}}
’s. More precisely, let
s=\lceil\log d\rceil
and let
C=\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\dots,\ell_{s}\}
be a set
s
distinct primes of size
\poly(\kappa)
, say the first
s
primes
2,3,5,\dots,\ell_{s}
. Then we choose the
n_{i}
to be the set

\{n_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq d}:=\{\ell_{1}^{b_{1}}\ell_{2}^{b_{2}}\cdots\ell_{s}^{b_{s}}:(b_{1},\dots,b_{s})\in\{0,1\}^{s}\}.

(19)

Explicitly, if
j=b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{s}
is the binary representation of
j
then we set
n_{j}=\ell_{1}^{b_{1}}\ell_{2}^{b_{2}}\cdots\ell_{s}^{b_{s}}
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Given the quantum state
\lvert\phi\rangle
representing an eigenform
\phi
, let
f(q)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{n}q^{n}
be the modular form, defined by (
16
), that corresponds to
\phi
. Let
\{n_{i}\}_{1\leq i\leq d}
be the set of integers defined in (
19
), and let

\lvert\bm{a}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{n_{j}}^{2}}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{n_{j}}\lvert j\rangle.

For any constant
c>0
, there is a polynomial time quantum algorithm that can prepare a state
\lvert\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle
such that
\lVert\lvert\bm{a}\rangle\langle\bm{a}\rvert-\lvert\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle\langle\tilde{\bm{a}}\rvert\rVert_{1}\leq 1/\kappa^{c}
.

Proof.

For any prime
\ell\in C
we can use phase estimation on the operator
e^{iT_{\ell}}
and the eigenstate
\lvert\phi\rangle
to compute an approximate eigenvalue
\tilde{a}_{\ell}
such that
\lvert a_{\ell}-\tilde{a}_{\ell}\rvert\leq 1/s\kappa^{c}
. Now, using the single qubit operation

\tilde{R}_{\ell}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\smash[b]{\tilde{a}_{\ell}^{2}}}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-\tilde{a}_{\ell}\\
\tilde{a}_{\ell}&1\end{bmatrix}

we can generate the single qubit state

\lvert\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}\rangle:=\tilde{R}_{\ell}\lvert 0\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\smash[b]{\tilde{a}_{\ell}^{2}}}}(\lvert 0\rangle+\tilde{a}_{\ell}\lvert 1\rangle).

Tensoring these states together for all
\ell\in C
we obtain the state
\lvert\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle:=\bigotimes_{\ell\in C}\lvert\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}\rangle
. Now, for any
n_{j}
we can write
n_{j}=\ell_{1}^{b_{1}}\ell_{2}^{b_{2}}\cdots\ell_{s}^{b_{s}}
where
b_{1}b_{2}\cdots b_{s}
is the binary representation of
j
. Therefore, using the relations (
10
) we have
a_{n_{j}}=a_{\ell_{1}}^{b_{1}}\cdots a_{\ell_{s}}^{b_{s}}
. So we can rewrite the state
\lvert\bm{a}\rangle
as

\displaystyle\lvert\bm{a}\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{s}(1+a_{\ell}^{2})\bigg)^{-1/2}\sum_{(b_{1},\dots,b_{s})\in\{0,1\}^{s}}a_{\ell_{1}}^{b_{1}}\cdots a_{\ell_{s}}^{b_{s}}\lvert b_{1},\dots,b_{s}\rangle

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{\ell\in C}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\smash[b]{a_{\ell}^{2}}}}(\lvert 0\rangle+a_{\ell}\lvert 1\rangle)

Let
\lvert\psi_{\ell}\rangle=(\lvert 0\rangle+a_{\ell}\lvert 1\rangle)/\sqrt{1+\smash[b]{a_{\ell}^{2}}}
. Then

\lVert\lvert\psi_{\ell}\rangle\langle\psi_{\ell}\rvert-\lvert\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}\rangle\langle\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}\rvert\rVert_{1}=\sqrt{1-\lvert\langle\psi_{\ell}|\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}\rangle\rvert^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{s\kappa^{c}},

and we have

\displaystyle\lVert\lvert\bm{a}\rangle\langle\bm{a}\rvert-\lvert\tilde{\bm{a}}\rangle\langle\tilde{\bm{a}}\rvert\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle=\bigg\lVert\bigotimes_{\ell\in C}\lvert\psi_{\ell}\rangle\langle\psi_{\ell}\rvert-\bigotimes_{\ell\in C}\lvert\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}\rangle\langle\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}\rvert\bigg\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell\in C}\lVert\lvert\psi_{\ell}\rangle\langle\psi_{\ell}\rvert-\lvert\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}\rangle\langle\tilde{\psi}_{\ell}\rvert\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\kappa^{c}}\qed
