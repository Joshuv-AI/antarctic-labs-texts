Federated Learning under Importance Sampling
Elsa Rizk, Stefan Vlaski, and Ali H. Sayed

Abstract
Federated learning encapsulates distributed learning strategies that are managed by a central unit. Since it relies on using a selected number of agents at each iteration, and since each agent, in turn, taps into its local data, it is only natural to study optimal sampling policies for selecting agents and their data in federated learning implementations. Usually, only uniform sampling schemes are used. However, in this work, we examine the effect of importance sampling and devise schemes for sampling agents and data non-uniformly guided by a performance measure. We find that in schemes involving sampling without replacement, the performance of the resulting architecture is controlled by two factors related to data variability at each agent, and model variability across agents. We illustrate the theoretical findings with experiments on simulated and real data and show the improvement in performance that results from the proposed strategies.

I-B

Sampling and Inclusion Probabilities

Before describing the problem setting, we need to clarify the difference between two notions: (a) sampling probability and (b) inclusion probability. Consider the following illustrative example. Consider
N=4
balls of which we wish to choose
B=2
balls non-uniformly and without replacement. Let the sampling probabilities be
\pi_{n}=\{1/3,1/6,1/3,1/6\}
. This means that, initially, balls 1 and 3 are twice as likely to be selected compared to balls 2 and 4. For the first trial, all the inclusion probabilities are equal to the sampling probabilities, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(n\text{ chosen on $1^{st}$ trial})=\pi_{n}
. However, since we are sampling
without replacement
, the inclusion probabilities for the second trial depend on the outcome of the first trial, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}(n\text{ chosen on $2^{nd}$ trial}|m
chosen on
1^{st}
trial
)=\pi_{n}/(1-\pi_{m})
. Using the sampling probabilities, we can evaluate the likelihood that each ball will end up belonging to the selected set of
2
balls. In particular, the probability that ball
1
is chosen either in the first or second trial is given by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{1 chosen})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=2}^{4}\mathbb{P}\big(\text{$1$ chosen on $1^{st}$ trial \& $n$ chosen on $2^{nd}$ trial)}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}\big(n\text{ chosen on $1^{st}$ trial \& $1$ chosen on $2^{nd}$ trial})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=2}^{4}\pi_{1}\frac{\pi_{n}}{1-\pi_{1}}+\pi_{n}\frac{\pi_{1}}{1-\pi_{n}}.

(3)

Thus, the sampling probability is the working probability. It is the probability used to actually choose the samples, while the inclusion probability is a descriptive probability that indicates the likelihood of a ball being included in the final selected subset. Observe that the inclusion probabilities depend on the sampling scheme, while the sampling probabilities do not. When considering
uniform sampling without replacement
, the inclusion probability is a multiple of the sampling probability. For example, sampling
B
numbers from
\{1,2,\cdots,N\}
with sampling probabilities
1/N
, the inclusion probability is found to be
\mathbb{P}(n\in\mathcal{B})=B/N
. Note further that while the sampling probabilities sum to 1 over all the sampling space, the inclusion probabilities
\mathbb{P}(n\in\mathcal{B})
sum to
B
. In our derivations, we will be relying frequently on the inclusion probabilities.

Next, we consider a total number of
K
agents. At each iteration
i
of the algorithm, a subset of agents
\mathcal{L}_{i}
of size
L
is chosen randomly
without replacement
. We denote the probability that agent
k
is included in the sample by
Lp_{k}

[
43
]
, i.e.,

p_{k}\>\overset{\Delta}{=}\>\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(k\in\mathcal{L}_{i}\big)}{L}.

(4)

In addition, each sampled agent
k
will run a mini-batch SGD by sampling
B_{k}
data points
\mathcal{B}_{k,i}

without replacement
from its local data. We denote the probability of inclusion of data point
n
by
B_{k}p_{n}^{(k)}
, i.e.,

p_{n}^{(k)}\>\overset{\Delta}{=}\>\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(n\in\mathcal{B}_{k,i}\big)}{B_{k}}.

(5)

We refer to
p_{k}
and
p_{n}^{(k)}
as the
normalized inclusion probabilities
. They sum to
1
over the sampling space;
p_{k}
sums to
1
over all agents and
p_{n}^{(k)}
over the data at each agent.

II
Algorithm Derivation

The goal of the federated learning algorithm is to approximate the centralized solution
w^{o}
while dealing with the constraint of distributed data. The goal is achieved by using an unbiased estimate of the gradient of the cost function,
\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{k}(w).

As explained in
[
1
]
for the case of uniform sampling, if we assume each agent
k
runs
E_{k}
epochs per iteration
i
(with each epoch using
B_{k}
samples in
\mathcal{B}_{k,i,e}
), then we can construct an unbiased estimate for the true gradient by considering the following estimator:

\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{L}_{i}}\frac{1}{E_{k}B_{k}}\sum_{e=1}^{E_{k}}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{k,i,e}}\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(w;\bm{x}_{k,b}),

(6)

as opposed to the original estimator from
[
9
]
, where the main difference is the scaling by the epoch size
E_{k}
. This correction is important for the performance of the averaged model. Since the number of epochs
E_{k}
can be non-uniform across the agents, then, without correction, agents with large epoch sizes will bias the solution by driving it towards their local model and away from
w^{o}
.

Expression (
6
) is still not sufficient for our purposes in this article, since agents and data are allowed to be sampled
non-uniformly without replacement
. In this case, we need to adjust (
6
) by including the inclusion probabilities
[
38
]
. The inclusion probabilities are necessary to ensure the estimate is unbiased, as will later be seen in Lemma
1
. The local estimate of the gradient at agent
k
becomes
\frac{1}{Kp_{k}}\widehat{\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{k}}(w)
, with:

\widehat{\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{k}}(w)\>\overset{\Delta}{=}\>\frac{1}{E_{k}B_{k}}\sum_{e=1}^{E_{k}}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{k,i,e}}\frac{1}{N_{k}p_{b}^{(k)}}\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(w;\bm{x}_{k,b}).

(7)

Motivated by (
7
), we can write down a stochastic gradient update at each agent
k
at epoch
e
, and at the central processor at iteration
i
:

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{k,e}=

\displaystyle\>\bm{w}_{k,e-1}

\displaystyle-\frac{\mu}{Kp_{k}E_{k}B_{k}}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{k,i,e}}\frac{1}{N_{k}p_{b}^{(k)}}\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(\bm{w}_{k,e-1};\bm{x}_{k,b}),

(8)

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{i}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{L}_{i}}\bm{w}_{k,E_{k}},

(9)

where at each iteration
i
, step (
8
) is repeated for
e=1,2,\cdots,E_{k}
. We arrive at the Algorithm
1
, which we refer to as Importance Sampling Federated Averaging (ISFedAvg).

w_{0}

for
each iteration
i=1,2,\cdots

do

Select the set of participating agents
\mathcal{L}_{i}
by sampling

times from
\{1,\ldots,K\}

\pi_{k}
.

for

\bm{w}_{k,0}=\bm{w}_{i-1}

\mathcal{B}_{k,i,e}
by sampling

.
\bm{g}=\dfrac{1}{B_{k}}\sum\limits_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{k,i,e}}\dfrac{1}{N_{k}p_{b}^{(k)}}\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(\bm{w}_{k,e-1};\bm{x}_{k,b})

\bm{w}_{{k},e}=\bm{w}_{{k},e-1}-\mu\dfrac{1}{E_{k}Kp_{k}}\bm{g}

III
Convergence Analysis

III-A

Modeling Conditions

To facilitate the analysis of the algorithm, we list some common assumptions on the nature of the local risk functions and their respective minimizers. Specifically, we assume convex cost functions with smooth gradients.

Assumption 1
.

The functions
P_{k}(\cdot)
are
\nu-
strongly convex, and
Q_{k}(\cdot;x_{k,n})
are convex, namely:

\displaystyle P_{k}(w_{2})\geq P_{k}(w_{1})+\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{k}(w_{1})(w_{2}-w_{1})+\frac{\nu}{2}\|w_{2}-w_{1}\|^{2},

(10)

\displaystyle Q_{k}(w_{2};x_{k,n})\geq Q_{k}(w_{1};x_{k,n})+\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(w_{1};x_{k,n})(w_{2}-w_{1}).

(11)

Also, the functions
Q_{k}(\cdot;x_{k,n})
have
\delta-
Lipschitz gradients:

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(w_{2};x_{k,n})-\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(w_{1};x_{k,n})\|

\displaystyle\leq\delta\|w_{2}-w_{1}\|.

(12)

∎

We further assume that the individual minimizers
w_{k}^{o}=\argmin_{w\in\mathbb{R}^{M}}P_{k}(w)
do not drift too far away from
w^{o}
.

Assumption 2
.

The distance of each local model
w^{o}_{k}
to the global model
w^{o}
is uniformly bounded,
\|w^{o}_{k}-w^{o}\|\leq\xi.

∎

III-B

Error Recursion

Iterating the local update (
8
) over multiple epochs and combining according to (
9
), we obtain the following update for the central iterate:

\displaystyle\bm{w}_{i}=\>\bm{w}_{i-1}-\mu\frac{1}{L}\sum_{k\in\mathcal{L}_{i}}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Kp_{k}E_{k}B_{k}}\sum_{e=1}^{E_{k}}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{k,i,e}}\frac{1}{N_{k}p_{b}^{(k)}}

\displaystyle\times\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(\bm{w}_{k,e-1};\bm{x}_{k,b}).

(13)

To simplify the notation, we introduce the error terms:

\displaystyle\bm{s}_{i}

\displaystyle\>\overset{\Delta}{=}\>\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{i}}\frac{1}{Kp_{\ell}}\widehat{\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{\ell}}(\bm{w}_{i-1})-\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{k}(\bm{w}_{i-1}),

(14)

\displaystyle\bm{q}_{i}

\displaystyle\>\overset{\Delta}{=}\>\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{i}}\frac{1}{Kp_{\ell}E_{\ell}B_{\ell}}\sum_{e=1}^{E_{\ell}}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{\ell,i,e}}\frac{1}{N_{\ell}p_{b}^{(\ell)}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\big(\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(\bm{w}_{\ell,e-1};\bm{x}_{\ell,b})-\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(\bm{w}_{i-1};\bm{x}_{\ell,b})\big).

(15)

The first error term
\bm{s}_{i}
, which we call
gradient error
, captures the error from approximating the true gradient by using subsets of agents and data; while, the second error term
\bm{q}_{i}
, which we call
incremental error
, captures the error resulting from the incremental implementation, where at each epoch during one iteration, the gradient is calculated at the local iterate
w_{k,e-1}
. Note that this second error evaluates the loss function at the local and global iterates. As we will show later, the incremental error will fade away, and the dominant factor will be the gradient error. Before establishing the main result in Theorem
1
on the convergence of ISFedAvg algorithm, we present preliminary results that will lead to it. Thus, to show the convergence of the algorithm, we must assure the gradient noise
\bm{s}_{i}
has zero mean and bounded variance, and the incremental noise
\bm{q}_{i}
has bounded variance. Furthermore, since we split the noise due to the stochastic gradient into incremental and gradient noise, we can split the analysis into that of the centralized steps and the local epochs. By proving that both the centralized and local steps converge, we show the global algorithm converges too.

Replacing the two error terms (
14
) and (
15
) into recursion (
III-B
) and subtracting
w^{o}
from both sides of the equation, we get the following error recursion:

\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i}=\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i-1}+\mu\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{k}(\bm{w}_{i-1})+\mu\bm{s}_{i}+\mu\bm{q}_{i}.

(16)

To bound the
\ell_{2}-
norm of the error, we split it into two terms, centralized and incremental, using Jensen’s inequality with some constant
\alpha\in(0,1)
to be defined later:

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i}\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i-1}+\mu\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{k}(\bm{w}_{i-1})+\mu\bm{s}_{i}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mu^{2}\|\bm{q}_{i}\|^{2}.

(17)

We start with the first term that represents the centralized solution. We need to show that it converges. To do so, we start with the gradient noise, and we establish in Lemma
1
that it remains bounded. We bound the gradient noise
\bm{s}_{i}
under two constructions: sampling with replacement, and sampling without replacement.

Lemma 1

(
Estimation of first and second order moments of the gradient noise
)
.

The gradient noise defined in (
14
) has zero mean:

\mathbb{E}\{\bm{s}_{i}|\bm{w}_{i-1}\}=0,

(18)

with bounded variance, regardless of the sampling scheme. More specifically, sampling agents and data with replacement, results in the following bound:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{\|\bm{s}_{i}\|^{2}|\bm{w}_{i-1}\}\leq

\displaystyle\beta_{s}^{2}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i-1}\|^{2}+\sigma^{2}_{s},

(19)

where
\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i-1}=w^{o}-\bm{w}_{i-1}
and the constants:

\displaystyle\beta_{s}^{2}

\displaystyle\>\overset{\Delta}{=}\>\frac{3\delta^{2}}{L}+\frac{1}{LK^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{p_{k}}\left(\beta_{s,k}^{2}+3\delta^{2}\right),

(20)

\displaystyle\sigma_{s}^{2}

\displaystyle\>\overset{\Delta}{=}\>\frac{1}{LK^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{p_{k}}\left\{\sigma_{s,k}^{2}+\left(3+\frac{6}{E_{k}B_{k}}\right)\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{k}(w^{o})\|^{2}\right\},

(21)

\displaystyle\beta_{s,k}^{2}

\displaystyle\>\overset{\Delta}{=}\>\frac{3\delta^{2}}{E_{k}B_{k}}\left(1+\frac{1}{N_{k}^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\frac{1}{p_{n}^{(k)}}\right),

(22)

\displaystyle\sigma_{s,k}^{2}

\displaystyle\>\overset{\Delta}{=}\>\frac{6}{E_{k}B_{k}N_{k}^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\frac{1}{p_{n}^{(k)}}\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(w^{o};x_{k,n})\|^{2}.

(23)

On the other hand, sampling agents and data without replacement results in the same bound but without the scaling by
L
in the constants
\beta_{s}^{2}
and
\sigma_{s}^{2}
.

Proof.

See Appendix
B
.
∎

The term
\sigma_{s,k}^{2}
in the bound captures what we call
data variability
. It is controlled by the mini-batch size
B_{k}
; as the mini-batch increases the effect of this term is reduced. The
\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{k}(w^{o})\|

. It is reduced when the data and agents are more heterogeneous. From Assumption

, we can bound it uniformly.

Now that we have identified the mean and variance of the gradient noise, we can proceed to establish the important conclusion that the following centralized solution:

\bm{w}_{i}=\bm{w}_{i-1}-\mu\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{i}}\widehat{\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{\ell}(\bm{w}_{i-1})},

(24)

converges exponentially to an
O(\mu)-
neighbourhood of the optimizer. In this implementation, the center processor aggregates the approximate gradients of the selected agents. We will subsequently call upon this result to examine the convergence behavior of the proposed federated learning solution.

(
Mean-square-error convergence of the centralized solution

.

Consider the centralized recursion (

\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i}\|^{2}

\lambda=1-2\mu\nu+\mu^{2}(\delta^{2}+\beta_{s}^{2})\in[0,1).

\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i}\|^{2}\leq\lambda\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i-1}\|^{2}+\mu^{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}

(25)

It follows from (

) that, for sufficiently small step-sizes:

\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i}\|^{2}\leq\lambda^{i}\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{1-\lambda^{i}}{1-\lambda}\mu^{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}.

(26)

Proof.

We next bound the incremental noise
\bm{q}_{i}
. To do so, we introduce the local terms:

\displaystyle\bm{q}_{k,i,e}\>\overset{\Delta}{=}\>

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Kp_{k}}\Bigg(\frac{1}{B_{k}}\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{k,i,e}}\frac{1}{N_{k}p_{b}^{(k)}}\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(\bm{w}_{k,e-1};\bm{x}_{k,b})

\displaystyle-\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{k}(\bm{w}_{k,e-1})\Bigg).

(27)

We show in the next lemma that the local gradient noise
\bm{q}_{k,i,e}
has zero mean and bounded variance. This result is useful for showing that the local SGD steps converge in the mean-square-error sense towards their local models
w^{o}_{k}
.

Lemma 3

(
Estimation of first and second order moments of the local gradient noise
)
.

The local gradient noise defined in (
III-B
) has zero mean:

\mathbb{E}\left\{\bm{q}_{k,i,e}\big|\mathcal{F}_{e-1},\mathcal{L}_{i}\right\}=0,

(28)

and bounded variance, regardless of the sampling scheme:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\|\bm{q}_{k,i,e}\|^{2}\big|\mathcal{F}_{e-1},\mathcal{L}_{i}\right\}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{E_{k}}{K^{2}p_{k}^{2}}\beta_{s,k}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{k,e-1}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{K^{2}p_{k}^{2}}\sigma_{q,k}^{2},

(29)

where
\mathcal{F}_{e-1}=\{w_{k,0},w_{k,1},\cdots,w_{k,e-1}\}
is the filtration describing all sources of randomness due to the previous iterates,
\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{k,e}=w_{k}^{o}-\bm{w}_{k,e}
, and the constants are as defined in (

\sigma_{q,k}^{2}=\frac{3}{B_{k}N_{k}^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\frac{1}{p_{n}^{(k)}}\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(w_{k}^{o};x_{k,n})\|^{2}.

(30)

Lemma 4

For every agent

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{k,e}\|^{2}\leq\lambda_{k}^{e}\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{k,0}\|^{2}+\frac{1-\lambda_{k}^{e}}{1-\lambda_{k}}\mu^{2}\sigma_{q,k}^{2}.

The incremental noise defined in (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\bm{q}_{i}\|^{2}

III-C

Main Theorem

Now that we have bounded each term of (
III-B
) and using Lemma
2
and
5
, we find:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\lambda\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i-1}\|^{2}+\mu^{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}\right)+\frac{O(\mu^{3})}{1-\alpha}\left(\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i-1}\|^{2}+\xi^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{O(\mu^{4})}{1-\alpha}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma_{q,k}^{2},

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\prime}\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i-1}\|^{2}+\frac{\mu^{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{O(\mu^{3})}{1-\alpha}\xi^{2}+\frac{O(\mu^{4})}{1-\alpha}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma_{q,k}^{2},

(36)

where:

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}

\displaystyle\>\overset{\Delta}{=}\>\frac{\lambda}{\alpha}+\frac{O(\mu^{3})}{1-\alpha},

\displaystyle=\frac{1-2\mu\nu+\mu^{2}(\delta^{2}+\beta_{s}^{2})}{\alpha}+\frac{O(\mu^{3})}{1-\alpha}

\displaystyle=O\left(\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)+O\left(\frac{\mu}{\alpha}\right)+O\left(\frac{\mu^{2}}{\alpha}\right)+O\left(\frac{\mu^{3}}{1-\alpha}\right).

(37)

Applying this bound recursively we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\lambda^{\prime}\right)^{i}\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{0}\|^{2}+\frac{1-\left(\lambda^{\prime}\right)^{i}}{1-\lambda^{\prime}}\Bigg(\frac{\mu^{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{O(\mu^{3})}{1-\alpha}\xi^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{O(\mu^{4})}{1-\alpha}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma_{q,k}^{2}\Bigg),

(38)

and then taking the limit
i\to\infty
:

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-\lambda^{\prime}}\left(\frac{\mu^{2}\sigma_{s}^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{O(\mu^{3})}{1-\alpha}\xi^{2}+\frac{O(\mu^{4})}{1-\alpha}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma_{q,k}^{2}\right).

(39)

for
\lambda^{\prime}<1
, which for
\alpha=\sqrt{\lambda}
is achieved when:

\displaystyle\mu<\min\left\{\frac{2\nu}{\delta^{2}+\beta_{s}^{2}},\frac{2\nu}{\delta^{2}+\frac{E_{k}}{K^{2}p_{k}^{2}}\beta_{s,k}^{2}}\right\},

(40)

\displaystyle O(\mu^{3})<(1-\sqrt{\lambda})^{2}.

(41)

Thus, since
\alpha=O(1)
,
1-\alpha=O(\mu)
and
1-\lambda^{\prime}=O(\mu)
:

\displaystyle\lim_{i\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i}\|^{2}\leq O(\mu)\sigma_{s}^{2}+O(\mu)\xi^{2}+O(\mu^{2})\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma_{q,k}^{2}.

(42)

The result is summarized in the theorem.

Theorem 1

(
Mean-square-error convergence of federated learning under importance sampling
)
.

Consider the iterates
\bm{w}_{i}
generated by the importance sampling federated averaging algorithm. For sufficiently small step-size
\mu
, it holds that the mean-square-error converges exponentially fast:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i}\|^{2}\leq

\displaystyle O\left((\lambda^{\prime})^{i}\right)+O(\mu)\left(\sigma_{s}^{2}+\xi^{2}\right)+O(\mu^{2})\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sigma_{q,k}^{2},

(43)

where
\lambda^{\prime}=1-O(\mu)+O(\mu^{2})\in[0,1).

∎

IV
Importance Sampling

Due to the heterogeneity of nodes which arise from their data and computational capabilities, it is important to guide the algorithm based on the potential contribution that each agent can have on the overall performance. By allowing asynchronicity, i.e., different epoch sizes among agents, we can take advantage of the varying computational capabilities. From
[
38
]
, we know that the choice of samples at each iteration affects the solution. Therefore, instead of choosing the samples uniformly, we consider importance sampling where samples are chosen according to some distribution to be determined. A similar scheme can be enforced on the participating agents. In what follows, we show that using importance sampling enhances the overall performance.

IV-A

Agent Level: Importance Sampling of Data

Every agent
k
at each epoch must select a mini-batch of data based on the normalized inclusion probabilities
p_{n}^{(k)}
. To find the optimal probabilities, we minimize the bound on the variance of the local gradient noise
\sigma_{s,k}^{2}
.
We solve the problem for both cases when sampling is done with replacement and without replacement. The results are the same for both sampling schemes.

Lemma 6

(
Optimal local data inclusion probabilities
)
.

The optimal local data normalized inclusion probabilities are given by:

p_{n}^{(k),o}\>\overset{\Delta}{=}\>\frac{\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(w^{o};x_{k,n})\|}{\sum_{m=1}^{N_{k}}\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(w^{o};x_{k,m})\|}.

(44)

Proof.

By introducing a Lagrange multiplier
\lambda
, the optimization problem can be reformulated as:

\min_{p_{n}^{(k)},\lambda}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\frac{1}{p_{n}^{(k)}}\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(w^{o};x_{k,n})\|^{2}+\lambda\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}p_{n}^{(k)}-1\right).

(45)

Then, taking the derivative with respect to
p_{n}^{(k)}
and setting it to zero we get (
46
). Next, substituting
p_{n}^{(k),o}
into the condition, we find (
47
).

\displaystyle p_{n}^{(k),o}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(w^{o};x_{k,n})\|}{\sqrt{\lambda}}

(46)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{N_{k}}\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(w^{o};x_{k,m})\|

(47)

∎

As seen in Lemma
6
, more weight is given to a data point that has a greater gradient norm, thus increasing its chances of being sampled and resulting in a faster convergence rate. In addition, we observe that the more homogeneous the data is the more uniform the inclusion probability is.

IV-B

Cloud Level: Importance Sampling of Agents

At each iteration, the cloud must select a subset of agents to participate. The agents are selected in accordance with the normalized inclusion probabilities
p_{k}
. To find the optimal probabilities, we minimize the bound on the variance of the gradient noise
\sigma_{s}^{2}

The following result holds for sampling with and without replacement, since the gradient noise only differ by a multiplicative factor.

Lemma 7

(
Optimal agent inclusion probabilities for sampling with replacement
)
.

The optimal agent normalized inclusion probabilities are given by (
48
), where
\alpha_{k}=\left(3+\frac{6}{E_{k}B_{k}}\right)
:

p_{k}^{o}\>\overset{\Delta}{=}\>\frac{\sqrt{\sigma_{s,k}^{2}+\alpha_{k}\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{k}(w^{o})\|^{2}}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\sqrt{\sigma_{s,\ell}^{2}+\alpha_{\ell}\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{\ell}(w^{o})\|^{2}}}.

(48)

Proof.

The proof follows similarly to that of Lemma
6
.
∎

We observe that the normalized inclusion probabilities will be closer to a uniform distribution the more the data and model variability terms are similar across agents.

IV-C

Practical Issues

In the previous subsections, we focused on finding the optimal inclusion probabilities for the agents and data. However, several practical issues arise. The first is that all probabilities are calculated based on the optimal model
w^{o}
, which we do not have access to. To overcome this issue, we estimate the probabilities at each iteration by calculating them according to the current model
\bm{w}_{i-1}
. Thus,

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{n}^{(k),o}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(\bm{w}_{i-1};x_{k,n})\|}{\sum_{m=1}^{N_{k}}\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(\bm{w}_{i-1};x_{m})\|},

(49)

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{k}^{o}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}_{s,k}+\alpha_{k}\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{k}(\bm{w}_{i-1})\|^{2}}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\sqrt{\sigma^{2}_{s,\ell}+\alpha_{\ell}\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{\ell}(\bm{w}_{i-1})\|^{2}}}.

(50)

In addition, since calculating the true gradient of the local loss function is costly, we replace it with the mini-batch approximation when calculating
p_{k}^{o}
:

\widehat{p}_{k}^{o}=\frac{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}_{s,k}+\alpha_{k}\left\|\widehat{\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{k}}(\bm{w}_{i-1})\right\|^{2}}}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{K}\sqrt{\sigma^{2}_{s,\ell}+\alpha_{\ell}\left\|\widehat{\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{\ell}}(\bm{w}_{i-1})\right\|^{2}}}.

(51)

Furthermore, every agent has access to all of its data and consequently to all of the gradients. However, the cloud does not have access to the gradients of all agents, and in turn cannot calculate the denominator of
p_{k}
. Instead, we propose the following solution: at iteration 0, all probabilities are set to
p_{k}=\frac{1}{K}
; then, during the
i^{th}
iteration, after the participating agents
\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{i}
send the cloud their stochastic gradients
\widehat{\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{\ell}}(\bm{w}_{i-1})
, the probabilities are updated as follows:

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{k}^{o}=

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}_{s,k}+\alpha_{k}\left\|\widehat{\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{k}}(\bm{w}_{i-1})\right\|^{2}}}{\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{i}}\sqrt{\sigma^{2}_{s,\ell}+\alpha_{\ell}\left\|\widehat{\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}P_{\ell}}(\bm{w}_{i-1})\right\|^{2}}}\left(1-\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}_{i}^{c}}\widehat{p}_{\ell}^{o}\right),

(52)

where the multiplicative factor follows from ensuring all the probabilities
\widehat{p}_{k}^{o}
sum to
1
.
Similarly for the local probabilities, since we are implementing mini-batch SGD, we only update the probabilities of the data points that were sampled:

\displaystyle\widehat{p}_{n}^{(k),o}=

\displaystyle\frac{\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(\bm{w}_{i-1};x_{k,n})\|}{\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{k,i,e}}\|\nabla_{w^{{\sf T}}}Q_{k}(\bm{w}_{i-1};x_{b})\|}\left(1-\sum_{b\in\mathcal{B}_{k,i,e}^{c}}\widehat{p}_{b}^{(k),o}\right).

(53)

Finally, the last problem arises when sampling without replacement. We have found the optimal inclusion probabilities and not the optimal sampling probabilities, and moving from the former to the latter is not trivial. Thus, we rely on the literature under sampling without replacement with unequal probabilities. Multiple sampling schemes exist such that the sampling probabilities do not need to be calculated explicitly. In general, there are multiple non-uniform sampling without replacement schemes that guarantee the same inclusion probabilities. We choose to implement the sampling scheme proposed in
[
44
]
, which ensures the inclusion probabilities are
p_{k}^{o}
and
p_{n}^{(k),o}
for the agents and data, respectively. More explicitly, we first calculate the
progressive totals of the inclusion probabilities
\Pi_{k}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}Lp_{k}
, for
k=1,2,\cdots,K
, and we set
\Pi_{0}=0
. Then, we select uniformly at random a uniform variate
d\in[0,1)
. Then, we select the
L
agents that satisfy
\Pi_{k-1}\leq q+\ell<\Pi_{k}
, for some
\ell=0,1,\cdots,L-1
.

VI
Conclusion

This work incorporates two levels of importance sampling into the operation of federated learning: one for selecting agents and another for selecting data batches at the agents. Optimal dynamical choices for the sampling probabilities are derived, and a detailed convergence analysis is performed. We also provided approximate expressions for the optimal sampling policies and illustrate the theoretical findings and the performance enhancement by means of simulations.

Result on the Variance of the Mini-batch Estimate

We introduce the following auxiliary result that is a generalization of
[
46
]
. Let
\{\mathcal{S}=\bm{x}_{n}\in\mathds{R}^{M}\}_{n=1}^{N}
denote a set of

and variance
\sigma_{n}^{2}=\mathbb{E}{\|\bm{x}_{n}-\mathbb{E}\bm{x}_{n}\|}^{2}
. We consider the problem of estimating the expected value of the sample mean:

\overline{x}\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bm{x}_{n}\right)

(60)

We consider two estimators for
\overline{x}
, both constructed by considering a mini-batch of samples, where
\bm{x}_{b}^{\mathrm{r}}
is constructed by sampling from
\mathcal{S}

with replacement
, and
\bm{x}_{b}^{\mathrm{nr}}
is sampled from
\mathcal{S}

without replacement
. Let
p_{n}
be the normalized inclusion probability of
\bm{x}_{n}
. We then define the two estimators (

.

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{r}}

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{1}{Np_{b}}\bm{x}_{b}^{\mathrm{r}}

(61)

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{nr}}

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{1}{Np_{b}}\bm{x}_{b}^{\mathrm{nr}}

(62)

Lemma 8

(
Variance of the mini-batch mean with and without replacement
)
.

Both estimators are unbiased and it holds that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\widehat{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{r}}}=\mathbb{E}{\widehat{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{nr}}}

\displaystyle=\overline{x},

(63)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\|\widehat{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{r}}-\overline{x}\|}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{n=1}^{N}p_{n}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}p_{n}^{2}}\sigma_{n}^{2}+{\left\|\frac{1}{Np_{n}}\overline{x}_{n}-\overline{x}\right\|}^{2}\right),

(64)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\left\|\widehat{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{nr}}-\overline{x}\right\|}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{n=1}^{N}p_{n}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}p_{n}^{2}}\sigma_{n}^{2}+\left\|\frac{1}{Np_{n}}\overline{x}_{n}-\overline{x}\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{B^{2}}\sum_{n_{1}\neq n_{2}}\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{I}_{n_{1}}=1,\mathbb{I}_{n_{2}}=1)

\displaystyle\times\left(\frac{1}{Np_{n_{1}}}\overline{x}_{n_{1}}-\overline{x}\right)\left(\frac{1}{Np_{n_{2}}}\overline{x}_{n_{2}}-\overline{x}\right),

(65)

where the notation

Proof.

We begin with the

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\widehat{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{r}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{Np_{b}}\bm{x}_{b}^{\mathrm{r}}\right)=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathbb{E}\left\{\frac{1}{Np_{b}}\bm{x}_{b}^{\mathrm{r}}\bigg|\mathcal{S}\right\}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{N}p_{n}\frac{1}{Np_{n}}\bm{x}_{n}\right\}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\overline{x}=\overline{x}.

(66)

For the variance we find:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\left\|\widehat{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{r}}-\overline{x}\right\|}^{2}

\displaystyle=\>\mathbb{E}{\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\frac{1}{Np_{b}}\bm{x}_{b}^{\mathrm{r}}-\overline{x}\right\|}^{2},

\displaystyle=\>\mathbb{E}{\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left(\frac{1}{Np_{b}}\bm{x}_{b}^{\mathrm{r}}-\overline{x}\right)\right\|}^{2},

\displaystyle=\>\frac{1}{B^{2}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}{\left\|\frac{1}{Np_{b}}\bm{x}_{b}^{\mathrm{r}}-\overline{x}\right\|}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{B^{2}}\sum_{b_{1}\neq b_{2}}\mathbb{E}\left\{{\left(\frac{1}{Np_{b_{1}}}\bm{x}_{b_{1}}-\overline{x}\right)}{\left(\frac{1}{Np_{b_{2}}}\bm{x}_{b_{2}}-\overline{x}\right)}\right\},

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\>\frac{1}{B^{2}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}{\left\|\frac{1}{Np_{b}}\bm{x}_{b}^{\mathrm{r}}-\overline{x}\right\|}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{B^{2}}\sum_{b_{1}\neq b_{2}}\mathbb{E}{\left\{\frac{1}{Np_{b_{1}}}\bm{x}_{b_{1}}-\overline{x}\right\}}\mathbb{E}{\left\{\frac{1}{Np_{b_{2}}}\bm{x}_{b_{2}}-\overline{x}\right\}},

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\>\frac{1}{B^{2}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}{\left\|\frac{1}{Np_{b}}\bm{x}_{b}^{\mathrm{r}}-\overline{x}\right\|}^{2},

(67)

where
(a)

\mathcal{S}
are independent and

is sampled from
\mathcal{S}
independently, and hence

and

then follows from:

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{Np_{b}}\bm{x}_{b}\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bm{x}_{n}\right)=\overline{x}.

(68)

Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}{\left\|\widehat{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{r}}-\overline{x}\right\|}^{2}

\displaystyle{=}\>\frac{1}{B^{2}}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbb{E}{\left\|\frac{1}{Np_{b}}\bm{x}_{b}^{\mathrm{r}}-\overline{x}\right\|}^{2},

\displaystyle{=}\>\frac{1}{B}\sum_{n=1}^{N}p_{n}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}p_{n}^{2}}\sigma_{n}^{2}+{\left\|\frac{1}{Np_{n}}\overline{x}_{n}-\overline{x}\right\|}^{2}\right).

We now proceed to study the efficiency of the
without replacement
mini-batch mean. The fact that the

are sampled from

without replacement causes pairs

\mathcal{S}

without replacement

and introduce the activation function by:

0,\ \mathrm{if}\ \bm{x}_{n}\notin\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{nr}}.\end{cases}

(70)

(71)

For the mean, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\widehat{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{nr}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathbb{I}_{n}\frac{1}{Np_{n}}\bm{x}_{n}\right\}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\mathbb{I}_{n}\times\mathbb{E}\frac{1}{Np_{n}}\bm{x}_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{n=1}^{N}Bp_{n}\times\frac{1}{Np_{n}}\overline{x}_{n}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\overline{x}_{n}=\overline{x}.

For the variance, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\widehat{\bm{x}}^{\mathrm{nr}}-\overline{x}\right\|^{2}=

\displaystyle\>\mathbb{E}\left\|\frac{1}{B}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{I}_{n}\left(\frac{1}{Np_{n}}\bm{x}_{n}-\overline{x}\right)\right\|^{2},

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\>\frac{1}{B^{2}}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left\|\mathbb{I}_{n}\left(\frac{1}{Np_{n}}\bm{x}_{n}-\overline{x}\right)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{B^{2}}\sum_{n_{1}\neq n_{2}}\mathbb{E}\Bigg\{\mathbb{I}_{n_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{Np_{n_{1}}}\bm{x}_{n_{1}}-\overline{x}\right)\mathbb{I}_{n_{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left(\frac{1}{Np_{n_{2}}}\bm{x}_{n_{2}}-\overline{x}\right)\Bigg\}.

We begin with:

\displaystyle\>\mathbb{E}\left\{\left\|\mathbb{I}_{n}\left(\frac{1}{Np_{n}}\bm{x}_{n}-\overline{x}\right)\right\|^{2}\bigg|\mathbb{I}_{n}=1\right\}\times\mathds{P}\left(\mathbb{I}_{n}=1\right)

\displaystyle\>Bp_{n}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}p^{2}_{n}}\mathbb{E}\|\bm{x}_{n}-\overline{x}_{n}\|^{2}+\left\|\frac{1}{Np_{n}}\overline{x}_{n}-\overline{x}\right\|^{2}\right),

\displaystyle\>Bp_{n}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}p^{2}_{n}}\sigma_{n}^{2}+\left\|\frac{1}{Np_{n}}\overline{x}_{n}-\overline{x}\right\|^{2}\right).

(74)

For the cross-term we have:

\displaystyle\>\mathbb{E}\bigg\{\left(\frac{1}{Np_{n_{1}}}\bm{x}_{n_{1}}-\overline{x}\right)\left(\frac{1}{Np_{n_{2}}}\bm{x}_{n_{2}}-\overline{x}\right)\bigg|\mathbb{I}_{n_{1}}=1,\mathbb{I}_{n_{2}}=1\bigg\}

\displaystyle\>\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{I}_{n_{2}}=1,\mathbb{I}_{n_{1}}=1\right)\left(\frac{1}{Np_{n_{1}}}\mathbb{E}\bm{x}_{n_{1}}-\overline{x}\right)\left(\frac{1}{Np_{n_{2}}}\mathbb{E}\bm{x}_{n_{2}}-\overline{x}\right),

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\>\mathbb{P}\left(\mathbb{I}_{n_{2}}=1,\mathbb{I}_{n_{1}}=1\right)\left(\frac{1}{Np_{n_{1}}}\overline{x}_{n_{1}}-\overline{x}\right)\left(\frac{1}{Np_{n_{2}}}\overline{x}_{n_{2}}-\overline{x}\right).

(75)

We then get the desired result.
∎

\displaystyle\quad+a\mu^{2}\mathbb{E}\left\{\|w^{o}-w_{\ell}^{o}\|^{2}\big|\mathcal{L}_{i}\right\}+(b\mu^{4}+c\mu^{2})\sigma_{q,\ell}^{2}\Big)\sum_{n=1}^{N_{\ell}}\frac{1}{N_{\ell}^{2}p_{n}^{(\ell)}},

(108)

where we introduced constants
a,b,c
to make the notation simpler.
Then, taking again the expectation to remove the conditioning and using Assumption
2
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\bm{q}_{i}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{K^{2}p_{k}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\frac{1}{N_{k}^{2}p_{n}^{(k)}}\Big(a\mu^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i-1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+a\mu^{2}\|w^{o}-w_{k}^{o}\|^{2}+(b\mu^{4}+c\mu^{2})\sigma_{q,k}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\delta^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{1}{K^{2}p_{k}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{k}}\frac{1}{N_{k}^{2}p_{n}^{(k)}}\Big(a\mu^{2}\mathbb{E}\|\widetilde{\bm{w}}_{i-1}\|^{2}+a\mu^{2}\xi^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+(b\mu^{4}+c\mu^{2})\sigma_{q,k}^{2}\Big).

(109)

Further simplifying the notation gives us the desired result. Thus, since
a=O(\mu^{-1})
,
b=O(\mu^{-2})
and
c=O(1)
, we get
\mathbb{E}\|\bm{q}_{i}\|^{2}=O(\mu)
.
∎
