Bridging the Gap Between Practice and PAC-Bayes Theory in Few-Shot Meta-Learning
Nan Ding, Xi Chen, Tomer Levinboim, Sebastian Goodman, Radu Soricut

Abstract
Despite recent advances in its theoretical understanding, there still remains a significant gap in the ability of existing PAC-Bayesian theories on meta-learning to explain performance improvements in the few-shot learning setting, where the number of training examples in the target tasks is severely limited. This gap originates from an assumption in the existing theories which supposes that the number of training examples in the observed tasks and the number of training examples in the target tasks follow the same distribution, an assumption that rarely holds in practice. By relaxing this assumption, we develop two PAC-Bayesian bounds tailored for the few-shot learning setting and show that two existing meta-learning algorithms (MAML and Reptile) can be derived from our bounds, thereby bridging the gap between practice and PAC-Bayesian theories. Furthermore, we derive a new computationally-efficient PACMAML algorithm, and show it outperforms existing meta-learning algorithms on several few-shot benchmark datasets.



1
Introduction

Recent advances in machine learning and neural networks have resulted in effective but parameter-bloated, data-hungry models. When the training data for a target task of interest is insufficient, such overparameterized models may easily overfit to the training data and exhibit poor generalization abilities. To address this problem, several research efforts have focused on designing a learning strategy that can leverage the training data of other tasks for the sake of improving the performance of some specific target task(s). Specifically, in the meta-learning (also called learning-to-learn or lifelong-learning) setting
(
baxter1998theoretical
;
Ravi2017OptimizationAA
)
, a meta-learner first extracts knowledge from a set of observed (meta-training) tasks and subsequently, this knowledge enables a base-learner to better adapt to the new, possibly data-limited target (meta-testing) task.
The meta-learning framework has been successfully applied and made significant practical impact on computer vision
(
russakovsky2015imagenet
)
, language understanding
(
devlin2019bert
)
, reinforcement learning
(
finn2017model
)
and many other research fields.

In parallel to its impressive empirical success, a series of theoretical works
(
tripuraneni2020theory
;
pentina14
;
amit18a
;
rothfuss2020pacoh
)
study how meta-learning utilizes the knowledge obtained from the observed task data and how it generalizes to the unseen target task.
Among the generalization bounds, PAC-Bayes bounds
(
mcallester1999some
;
germain2009pac
)
are considered especially tight and have already been proposed for meta-learning
(
pentina14
;
amit18a
;
rothfuss2020pacoh
)
. However, there still remains a gap between these existing PAC-Bayesian bounds and their practical application (especially in the few-shot setting), which originates from the assumption that
the observed task environment
\tilde{T}
and the target task environment
T
are the same. In the PAC-Bayesian meta-learning setting, a task environment
T
is a distribution from which
(D,m)
is drawn from, where
D
is the data distribution and
m
is the number of training examples for the task. Although there is research work studying the case of general environment change (e.g.
(
pentina2015lifelong
)
) or data domain change (e.g.
germain2016new
), to the best of our knowledge, there is little work focusing on the case where only the number of training examples
\tilde{m}
in the observed tasks and
m
in the target task do not follow the same distribution.
In practice, such mismatch commonly happens, because there is usually significantly more data in observed tasks than the target tasks, especially in the few-shot case.
Without explicitly addressing this mismatch, the scope of the current theory is severely limited, and it prohibits a useful analysis on practical meta-learning algorithms such as MAML
finn2017model
. For example, when the number of training examples
m
in the target task is small, the existing bounds yield a large generalization gap which grows with
O(1/m)
.
In this paper, we bring the theory closer to practice by studying the setting where there are significantly more training examples in the observed task than in the target task (i.e.,
\tilde{m}\gg m
).
In Section
3.1
, we study two practical meta-training strategies and provide their PAC-Bayesian bounds in Theorem
3
and Theorem
4
. Both results are able to bring down the scaling coefficient of the bound from
O(1/m)
to
O(1/\tilde{m})
. However, Theorem
3
introduces a penalty term in the bound that captures the discrepancy between the observed and target task environment.
Motivated by MAML
finn2017model
, we show with Theorem
4
that we can eliminate the penalty term by utilizing a subsampling strategy, yielding a much tighter bound.

This theoretical work also bridges the gap from practice to theory, as we further show that the maximum-a-posteriori (MAP) estimates of our bounds (in which the base-learner and the hyper-posterior are both approximated by Dirac-measures) yield various popular meta-learning algorithms, including multi-task pretraining
(
russakovsky2015imagenet
)
, Reptile
(
nichol1803first
)
and MAML
(
finn2017model
)
.
In that sense, our PAC-Bayesian theories provide a different perspective for understanding and justifying these commonly used algorithms (Section
3.2
).

Lastly, in Section
4
, we propose PACMAML, a novel PAC-Bayesian meta-learning algorithm based on Theorem
4
. As opposed to MAML, our algorithm does not have higher-order derivatives in the gradient, and therefore represents a significant improvement in computational efficiency.
In Section
5
, we conduct numerical experiments that empirically support the correctness of our theorems, and report the effectiveness of the new PACMAML algorithm, which obtains superior results on several few-shot benchmark datasets.

2
Preliminaries

We begin by reviewing the background and settings of the existing PAC-Bayesian bounds for meta-learning. Our notation mainly follows that of
(
rothfuss2020pacoh
)
, which is itself adapted from
(
pentina14
;
amit18a
;
baxter1998theoretical
)
.

PAC-Bayesian for Supervised Learning

In supervised learning, a learning task is characterized by a data distribution
D
over a data domain
Z
where every example
z=(x,y)
. A hypothesis
h
from the hypothesis space
H
allows us to make predictions based on inputs
x
. The quality of the predictions is measured by a loss function
l(h,z)
, where the goal is to minimize the expected loss

L(h,D)=\EE_{z\sim D}l(h,z)
.
Typically,
D
is unknown and instead we are given a set of
m
observations

S\sim D^{m}=\{z_{i}\sim D\}_{i=1}^{m}
, in which case the empirical error on
S
is simply

\hat{L}(h,S)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}l(h,z_{i})
.

In the PAC-Bayesian setting,
we assume that the learner has prior knowledge of the hypothesis space
H
in the form of a prior distribution
P(h)
. When the learner observes a training dataset
S
, it updates the prior into a posterior distribution
Q
.
We formalize such a
base learner

Q(S,P)
that takes a dataset and a prior as input and outputs a posterior.

The expected error of the posterior
Q
is called the Gibbs error
L(Q,D)=\EE_{h\sim Q}L(h,D)
, and its empirical counterpart is
\hat{L}(Q,S)=\EE_{h\sim Q}\hat{L}(h,S)
.
The PAC-Bayesian framework provides the following bound over
L(Q,D)
based on its empirical estimate
\hat{L}(Q,S)
.

Theorem 1
(
(
alquier2016properties
;
germain2009pac
)
)

Given a data distribution
D
, a hypothesis space
H
, a prior
P
, a confidence level
\delta\in(0,1]
, and
\beta>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
over samples
S\sim D^{m}
, we have for all posterior
Q
,

\displaystyle L(Q,D)\leq\hat{L}(Q,S)+\frac{1}{\beta}\left(D_{KL}(Q\|P)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)+\frac{m}{\beta}\Psi(\frac{\beta}{m})

(1)

where
\Psi(\beta)=\log\EE_{h\sim P}\EE_{z\sim D}\exp(\beta(l(h,z)-L(h,D)))
.

PAC-Bayesian for Meta-Learning

In the meta-learning setting, the meta-learner observes different tasks
\tau_{i}=(D_{i},m_{i}
) during the meta-training stage, where all tasks share the same data domain
Z
, hypothesis space
H
and loss function
l(h,z)
. For each observed task
\tau_{i}
, the meta-learner observes a training set
S_{i}
of size
m_{i}
which is assumed to be sampled i.i.d. from its respective data distribution
D_{i}
(that is,
S_{i}\in D_{i}^{m_{i}}
).
We further assume that each task
\tau_{i}=(D_{i},m_{i})
is drawn i.i.d. from an environment
T
, which itself is a probability distribution over the data distributions and the sample sizes.
The goal of meta-learning is to extract knowledge from the observed tasks
\tau_{i}
, which can then be used as prior knowledge for learning on new (yet unobserved) target tasks
\tau=(D,m)\sim T
.
This prior knowledge is represented as a prior distribution
P(h)
over learning hypotheses
h
, and it is subsequently used by the base learner
Q(S,P)
for inference over the target tasks.

In the meta-learning PAC-Bayes framework, the meta-learner presumes a hyper-prior
\Pcal(P)
as a distribution over priors
P
.
Upon observing datasets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}
from multiple tasks, the meta-learner updates the hyper-prior to a hyper-posterior
\Qcal(P)
.
The performance of this hyper-posterior, also called the transfer-error, is measured as the expected Gibbs error when sampling priors
P
from
\Qcal
and applying the base learner:

\displaystyle R(\Qcal,T)

\displaystyle:=\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\EE_{(D,m)\sim T}\EE_{S\sim D^{m}}\left[L(Q(S,P),D)\right].

(2)

While
R(\Qcal,T)
is unknown in practice, it can be estimated using the empirical error,

\displaystyle\hat{R}(\Qcal,S_{i=1}^{n}):=\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{L}(Q(S_{i},P),S_{i})\right].

(3)

In
(
pentina14
;
rothfuss2020pacoh
)
, the following PAC-Bayesian meta-learning bound is provided:

Theorem 2
(
(
pentina14
;
rothfuss2020pacoh
)
)

Given a task environment
T
and a set of
n
observed tasks
(D_{i},m_{i})\sim T
, let
\Pcal
be a fixed hyper-prior and
\lambda>0
,
\beta>0
, with probability at least
1-\delta
over samples
S_{1}\in D_{1}^{m_{1}},\ldots,S_{n}\in D_{n}^{m_{n}}
, we have, for all base learner
Q
and all hyper-posterior
\Qcal
,

\displaystyle R(\Qcal,T)\leq

\displaystyle\hat{R}(\Qcal,S_{i=1}^{n})+\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{n\beta}\right)D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[D_{KL}(Q(S_{i},P)\|P)\right]+C(\delta,\lambda,\beta,n,m_{i}).

(4)

Here
C(\delta,\lambda,\beta,n,m_{i})
contains
\Psi
and
\frac{1}{\delta}
terms as in Eq.(
1
) (see Appendix
A.1
), and can be bounded by a function that is independent of
\Qcal
for both bounded and unbounded loss functions under moment constraints (see details in
(
rothfuss2020pacoh
)
).
From a Bayesian perspective, meta-learning attempts to learn a good hyper-posterior
\Qcal
such that for all tasks in the task environment
T
, the divergence terms
{D_{KL}(Q(S_{i},P)\|P)}
would be substantially smaller in expectation when
P\sim\Qcal
compared to when
P\sim\Pcal
, such as in the ordinary supervised learning setting of Eq.(
1
).

The hyperparameters
\lambda
and
\beta
can be adjusted to balance between the first three terms of the bound and the
C
function. Defining the harmonic mean of
m_{i}
as
\tilde{m}=(\sum_{i=1}^{n}1/nm_{i})^{-1}
, a common choice is
\lambda\propto n
and
\beta\propto\tilde{m}
*
*

*

Another common choice is
\lambda\propto\sqrt{n}
and
\beta\propto\sqrt{\tilde{m}}
, so that the bound is asymptotically consistent, and scales with
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tilde{m}}})
.
However, in practice the bound with
\beta\propto\tilde{m}
is usually tighter
(
germain2016pac
)
.
.
In this case, the generalization gap
R(\Qcal,T)-\hat{R}(\Qcal,S_{i=1}^{n})
becomes at least
O(\frac{1}{\tilde{m}})
(from the 3rd-term on the RHS of Eq.
4
).
In the next section, we examine an assumption in this bound which makes it impractical for the few-shot setting.

3
Bridging the Gap between Practice & Theory of Few-Shot Meta-Learning

The previous PAC-Bayesian meta-learning bound (Theorem
2
) assumes that the number of training examples
m_{i}
for the observed tasks
\tau_{i}
and the number of training examples
m
for the target task
\tau
are drawn from the same distribution (i.e.
\EE_{T}[m_{i}]=\EE_{T}[m]
).
However, practical applications of meta-learning such as
(
russakovsky2015imagenet
;
devlin2019bert
)
operate in a setting where there are far more training examples in the observed tasks than in the target task.
Moreover, focusing on the few-shot setting (where
m
is particularly small) exposes a gap between theory and practice – Theorem
2
is unable to use the large number of observed samples and can only produce a loose bound of
O(\frac{1}{m})
which is ineffective at explaining the impressive generalization performance of meta-learning as reported in practice.

In this section we attempt to close this gap by deriving an effective PAC-Bayesian bound (Theorem
4
) tailored for the few-shot setting.
Interestingly, the bounds derived in this section also provide PAC-Bayesian justifications for two practical algorithms, Reptile and MAML.

3.1
Practical PAC-Bayesian Bounds for Few-Shot Meta-Learning

A first attempt at leveraging the larger number of examples
m_{i}
in the observed tasks is to directly
follow the learning strategy of Theorem
2
,
by bounding
R(\Qcal,T)
using the empirical risk
\hat{R}(\Qcal,S_{i=1}^{n})
,
with
S_{i}\in D_{i}^{m_{i}}
and
(D_{i},m_{i})\sim\tilde{T}
, despite the change of task environment from
T
to
\tilde{T}
.
This slight generalization leads to the following bound (with proof in Appendix
A.2
):

Theorem 3

For a target task environment
T
and an observed task environment
\tilde{T}
where
\EE_{\tilde{T}}[D]=\EE_{T}[D]
and
\EE_{\tilde{T}}[m]\geq\EE_{T}[m]
, let
\Pcal
be a fixed hyper-prior and
\lambda>0
,
\beta>0
, then with probability at least
1-\delta
over samples
S_{1}\in D_{1}^{m_{1}},\ldots,S_{n}\in D_{n}^{m_{n}}
where
(D_{i},m_{i})\sim\tilde{T}
, we have, for all base learners
Q
and hyper-posterior
\Qcal
,

\displaystyle R(\Qcal,T)\leq

\displaystyle\hat{R}(\Qcal,S_{i=1}^{n})+\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{n\beta}\right)D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[D_{KL}(Q(S_{i},P)\|P)\right]+C(\delta,\lambda,\beta,n,m_{i})+\Delta_{\lambda}(\Pcal,T,\tilde{T}),

(5)

where
\Delta_{\lambda}(\Pcal,T,\tilde{T})=\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\EE_{P\in\Pcal}e^{\lambda(R(P,T)-R(P,\tilde{T}))}
.

When
\EE_{\tilde{T}}[m_{i}]\gg\EE_{T}[m]
, this decoupling of the task environments seems beneficial at first, because
O(\frac{1}{\tilde{m}})
is smaller compared to Eq.(
4
) when
\beta\propto\tilde{m}
.
Unfortunately however, Eq.(
5
) introduces an additional
penalty term
\Delta_{\lambda}
, which increases as
\EE_{\tilde{T}}[\tilde{m}]
gets larger.

To understand the influence of
\Delta_{\lambda}
, we plot the (blue) bound of Eq.(
5
) in Fig.
1
by using the synthetic Sinusoid regression task (see details in Section
5.1
and in Appendix
D.4
) where we fixed
m=5
and varied
m_{i}
from 5 to 100.
When
m_{i}=m=5
, Eq.(
5
) reduces to Eq.(
4
) and
\Delta_{\lambda}=0
. Contrary to intuition, increasing
m_{i}
does not reduce the bound, but instead makes it worse due to the rapid increase of
\Delta_{\lambda}
.

Figure 1:
The PAC-Bayesian bounds of Theorems
2
,
3
, &
4
as evaluated over the Sinusoid dataset. Some constant terms are neglected (see Appendix
D.4
for more details).

Can we utilize more training examples without introducing a penalty term such as
\Delta_{\lambda}
?
In the definition of
\hat{R}(\Qcal,S_{i=1}^{n})
(Eq.(
3
)), we note that the training dataset
S_{i}
of the observed task
\tau_{i}
is used twice: first in training the base-learner
Q(S_{i},P)
, and then, in evaluating the empirical risk
\hat{L}(Q,S_{i})
.
In analyzing the proof of the theorem (see Appendix
A.2
), it can be seen that the penalty term arises exactly because
Q(S_{i},P)
is trained over more samples compared to
Q(S,P)
of the target task, which results in the more powerful base-learners during meta-training than the one for the target task.

This motivates us to develop a MAML-inspired learning strategy, in which we maintain the same target-task training environment
T
for the base-learners of the observed tasks: we first sample a subset
S_{i}^{\prime}\in D_{i}^{m_{i}^{\prime}}
from
S_{i}
where
m^{\prime}_{i}
and
m
follow the same distribution and
m_{i}^{\prime}\leq m_{i}
. Then we use only the subset
S_{i}^{\prime}
to train the base-learner
Q(S_{i}^{\prime},P)
. At the same time, all examples of
S_{i}\in D_{i}^{m_{i}}
are used for evaluating the empirical risk
\hat{L}(Q,S_{i})
, so that the larger
m_{i}
in the empirical risk
\hat{L}(Q,S_{i})
help tightening the generalization gap.
This new strategy leads to the following bound (proof in Appendix
A.3
):

Theorem 4

For a target task environment
T
and an observed task environment
\tilde{T}
where
\EE_{\tilde{T}}[D]=\EE_{T}[D]
and
\EE_{\tilde{T}}[m]\geq\EE_{T}[m]
, let
\Pcal
be a fixed hyper-prior and
\lambda>0
,
\beta>0
, then with probability at least
1-\delta
over samples
S_{1}\in D_{1}^{m_{1}},\ldots,S_{n}\in D_{n}^{m_{n}}
where
(D_{i},m_{i})\sim\tilde{T}
, and subsamples
S_{1}^{\prime}\in D_{1}^{m_{1}^{\prime}}\subset S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}^{\prime}\in D_{n}^{m_{n}^{\prime}}\subset S_{n}
, where
\EE[m_{i}^{\prime}]=\EE_{T}[m]
, we have, for all base learner
Q
and all hyper-posterior
\Qcal
,

\displaystyle R(\Qcal,T)\leq

\displaystyle\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{L}(Q(S_{i}^{\prime},P),S_{i})\right]+\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{n\beta}\right)D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[D_{KL}(Q(S_{i}^{\prime},P)\|P)\right]+C(\delta,\lambda,\beta,n,m_{i}).

(6)

This bound is still
O(\frac{1}{\tilde{m}})
when choosing
\beta\propto\tilde{m}
, but unlike Eq.(
5
), it does not have an additional penalty term in Eq.(
6
), which is due to the shared training environment
T
of the base-learners in both observed and target tasks.
Importantly, the resulting bound is effective in the few-shot setting as an increase in the number of observed examples
m_{i}
monotonically tightens the generalization gap.
This is visually demonstrated in Fig.
1
in which the (orange) bound of Eq.(
6
) in Theorem
4
is monotonically decreasing as
m_{i}
increases, while the bound in Theorem
2
is limited only to
m_{i}=5
and the bound of Theorem
3
grows.

3.2
Justifying Reptile and MAML using PAC-Bayesian Bounds

It is worth noting that Theorems
3
and
4
not only address more practical scenarios in which observed (meta-training) examples are more abundant than the target examples, but they also serve as a justification for popular and practical meta-learning algorithms: Reptile
(
nichol1803first
)
and MAML
(
finn2017model
)
.

To show this, let us consider the maximum-a-posteriori (MAP) approximations on the hyper-posterior
\Qcal(P)
and base-leaner
Q_{i}(h),\forall i=1,\ldots,n
, with Dirac measures. In addition, we use the isotropic Gaussian priors with variance hyperparameter
\sigma_{0}^{2}
and
\sigma^{2}
for the hyper-prior
\Pcal(P)
and the prior
P(h)
.
The hypothesis
h
is parameterized by
\vb
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\Pcal(P)

\displaystyle=\Ncal(\pb|0,\sigma_{0}^{2}),\;\Qcal(P)=\delta(\pb=\pb_{0}),\;P(h_{\vb})=\Ncal(\vb|\pb,\sigma^{2}),\;Q_{i}(h_{\vb})=\delta(\vb=\qb_{i}),

and the goal of MAP approximation is to find the optimal meta-parameters
\pb_{0}
.
With the above assumptions, the PAC-Bayesian bound (denoted PacB) of Eq.(
5
) and Eq.(
6
) with respect to
\pb_{0}
becomes (up to a constant, see Appendix
B
),

\displaystyle PacB(\pb_{0})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{L}(\qb_{i},S_{i})+\frac{\tilde{\xi}\|\pb_{0}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\frac{1}{n\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|\pb_{0}-\qb_{i}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}},

(7)

where
\tilde{\xi}=\frac{1}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{n\beta}
.
Here,
\qb_{i}
can be any function of
\pb_{0}
and
S_{i}
for Eq.(
5
) (or
\pb_{0}
and
S_{i}^{\prime}
for Eq.(
6
)), such that the only free variable in Eq.(
7
) is
\pb_{0}
.
Indeed, by setting
\qb_{i}
according to the choices below, we can derive the gradients of several meta-learning algorithms.

When
\qb_{i}=\pb_{0}
, the gradient of Eq.(
7
) reduces to that of multi-task pretraining
(
russakovsky2015imagenet
;
devlin2019bert
)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\qb_{i}\to\pb_{0}}\frac{d(PacB)}{d\pb_{0}}=\frac{\tilde{\xi}\pb_{0}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{d}{d\pb_{0}}\hat{L}(\pb_{0},S_{i}).

On the other hand, if we use the optimal Dirac-base-learner
\qb_{i}^{*}
of
\pb_{0}
and
S_{i}
, such that

\displaystyle\qb_{i}^{*}=

\displaystyle\argmin_{\qb_{i}}\left[\hat{L}(\qb_{i},S_{i})+\frac{\|\pb_{0}-\qb_{i}\|^{2}}{2\beta\sigma^{2}}\right],

(8)

then the gradient of Eq.(
7
) becomes substantially simpler (see details in the Appendix
B
),

\displaystyle\frac{d(PacB)}{d\pb_{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{\xi}\pb_{0}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\pb_{0}-\qb_{i}^{*}}{\beta\sigma^{2}},

(9)

and in fact, Eq.(
9
) is equivalent to the meta-update rule
of the Reptile algorithm
(
nichol1803first
)
, whose inner-loop is an approximate algorithm for solving the optimal Dirac-base-learner
\qb_{i}^{*}
.

Lastly, when
\qb_{i}
is a few gradient descent steps of
\hat{L}(\qb_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})
with initial
\qb_{i}=\pb_{0}
, the gradient of Eq.(
7
) reduces to that of the MAML algorithm
†
†

†

A slight difference is that MAML usually assumes
S_{i}\cap S_{i}^{\prime}=\emptyset
; while in our setting, we assume
S_{i}^{\prime}\subset S_{i}
. However, Theorem
4
still holds when
S_{i}\cap S_{i}^{\prime}=\emptyset
.

(
finn2017model
)
as
\sigma^{2}\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma^{2}\to\infty}\frac{d(PacB)}{d\pb_{0}}=\frac{\tilde{\xi}\pb_{0}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{d}{d\pb_{0}}\hat{L}(\qb_{i},S_{i}).

One observation here is that, since
\qb_{i}
is function of the gradient of
\pb_{0}
,
d\qb_{i}/d\pb_{0}
involves high-order gradient w.r.t.
\pb_{0}
, which would result in a computationally intensive algorithm. In the next section we present a computationally efficient algorithm which relies only on first-order derivatives.

4
PAC-Bayesian Meta-Learning Algorithms in the Few-Shot Setting

In this section we present two PAC-Bayesian based Meta-Learning algorithms with non-Dirac base-learners.
We first derive their objective functions from the RHS of Eq.(
5
) and Eq.(
6
), and then derive low-variance gradient estimators for their optimization.

First, since Eq.(
4
) and Eq.(
5
) only differ by
\Delta_{\lambda}
, we follow
(
rothfuss2020pacoh
)
and
plug in their proposed Gibbs posterior based base-learner
Q^{*}(S_{i},P)(h)=P(h)\exp(-\beta\hat{L}(h,S_{i}))/Z_{\beta}(S_{i},P)
into Eq.(
5
), which minimizes Eq.(
5
) w.r.t.
Q
.
This yields that, with at least
1-\delta
probability,

\displaystyle R(\Qcal,T)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\underbrace{\left[-\frac{1}{\beta}\log Z_{\beta}(S_{i},P)\right]}_{W_{1}}+\tilde{\xi}D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)+\Delta_{\lambda}+C

(10)

where
\tilde{\xi}=\frac{1}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{n\beta}
and
C
is the same constant from the previous bounds. Since
\Delta_{\lambda}
is independent of
\Qcal
and can be neglected during inference or optimization of
\Qcal
, it reduces to the same PACOH objective as in
(
rothfuss2020pacoh
)
.

On the other hand, the same Gibbs posterior cannot be used as the base learner of Eq.(
6
), because the Gibbs posterior would depend on
S_{i}
, while the base learner in Eq.(
6
) should only be dependent on
S_{i}^{\prime}\subset S_{i}
. Therefore, we use the following posterior
Q_{i}^{\alpha}
with hyperparameter
\alpha
,

\displaystyle Q_{i}^{\alpha}(S_{i}^{\prime},P)(h)=\frac{P(h)\exp(-\alpha\hat{L}(h,S_{i}^{\prime}))}{Z_{\alpha}(S_{i}^{\prime},P)}.

Plugging into Eq.(
6
) (derivations in Appendix) yields that, with at least
1-\delta
probability,

\displaystyle R(\Qcal,T)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\underbrace{\left[-\frac{1}{\beta}\log Z_{\alpha}(S_{i}^{\prime},P)+\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(Q^{\alpha}_{i},S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})\right]}_{W_{2}}+\tilde{\xi}D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)+C.

(11)

where
\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(Q^{\alpha}_{i},S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})\triangleq\hat{L}(Q^{\alpha}_{i},S_{i})-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\hat{L}(Q_{i}^{\alpha},S_{i}^{\prime})
.
We refer to the RHS of this equation as the PACMAML objective, because Eq.(
11
) comes from the PAC-Bayesian bound of Eq.(
6
), which
is similar to MAML in subsampling the training data for base-learners.

Given these two objectives, the next step is to estimate the gradients of
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
, which can then be plugged into Monte-Carlo methods for estimating a hyper-posterior distribution of
\Qcal
(or optimization methods for finding an MAP solution).

Gradient Estimation

In
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
, the terms
Z_{\beta},Z_{\alpha},\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(Q^{\alpha}_{i},S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})
all involve integrations over
h
. When
P(h)
is Gaussian and
\hat{L}(h,S_{i})
is a squared loss, such integrations have closed form solutions and the gradients can be analytically obtained.
However, when
\hat{L}(h,S_{i})
is not a squared loss (such as the softmax loss), the integration does not have a closed form solution and we resort to approximations.
For example,
rothfuss2020pacoh
directly approximates the objective
W_{1}
with Monte-Carlo sampling, which however results in a biased gradient estimator.

Here, we follow an alternative approach from the REINFORCE algorithm
(
Williams92
)
, which instead approximates
the gradient of the objective
with Monte-Carlo methods, and has the benefit that the resulting gradient estimator is unbiased.
Assuming that the model hypothesis
h
is parameterized by
\vb
such that
\hat{L}(h,S_{i})\triangleq\hat{L}(\vb,S_{i})
, and
\vb
has prior
P(\vb)=\Ncal(\vb|\pb,\sigma^{2})
with meta-parameter
\pb
, then

\displaystyle\log Z_{\beta}(S_{i},\pb)=\log\int\Ncal(\vb|\pb,\sigma^{2})\exp(-\beta\hat{L}(\vb,S_{i}))d\vb.

Note that
\pb
appears in the probability distribution
\Ncal(\vb|\pb,\sigma^{2})
of the expectation, and the naive Monte-Carlo estimator of the gradient w.r.t.
\pb
is known to exhibit high variance. To reduce the variance, we apply the reparameterization trick
(
kingma2013auto
)
and rewrite
\vb=\pb+\wb
with
\wb\sim\Ncal(\wb|{\bf 0},\sigma^{2})
. This leads to the following gradient of
W_{1}
,

\displaystyle\frac{dW_{1}}{d\pb}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{d}{d\pb}\log Z_{\beta}(S_{i},\pb)=\int Q^{\beta}_{i}(\wb;S_{i})\frac{\partial\hat{L}(\pb+\wb,S_{i})}{\partial\pb}d\wb,

(12)

\displaystyle\text{where,}\;\;Q^{\beta}_{i}(\wb;S_{i})\propto\Ncal(\wb|{\bf 0},\sigma^{2})\exp(-\beta\hat{L}(\pb+\wb,S_{i})).

As for
W_{2}
, we also need to evaluate the gradient of
\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(Q^{\alpha}_{i},S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})
, where

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\pb}\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(Q^{\alpha}_{i},S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})\frac{\partial\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(\pb+\wb,S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})}{\partial\pb}d\wb+\int\frac{\partial Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S^{\prime}_{i})}{\partial\pb}\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(\pb+\wb,S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})d\wb.

(13)

The first term of Eq.(
13
) is similar to the gradient in Eq.(
12
).
The Monte-Carlo gradient estimator of the second term, however, exhibits the same high-variance problem as in the policy gradient method.
As a remedy, we approximate the gradient with the one from the Softmax Policy Gradient
(
ding2017cold
)
, which yields a low-variance approximate gradient of
W_{2}
(details in Appendix):

\displaystyle\frac{dW_{2}}{d\pb}

\displaystyle\simeq\int Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})\frac{\partial\hat{L}(\pb+\wb;S_{i})}{\partial\pb}d\wb+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\int\left(Q_{i}^{\beta}(\wb;S_{i})-Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})\right)\frac{\partial\hat{L}(\pb+\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})}{\partial\pb}d\wb.

(14)

The first-term in Eq.(
14
) is similar to the gradient of the First-order MAML (FOMAML,
finn2017model
). The second term involves
Q_{i}^{\beta}
and
Q_{i}^{\alpha}
, which are similar to the leader and the chaser in BMAML
(
yoon2018bayesian
)
. Intuitively, the second term provides additional information that plays a similar role to the high-order derivatives in MAML. However, unlike MAML and BMAML, Eq.(
14
) only involves partial derivatives over
\pb
(since
\wb
is not a function of
\pb
) and therefore relies only on first-order derivatives which contribute to its efficiency and stability.

To estimate Eq.(
12
) and Eq.(
14
) in practice, we first draw samples
\wb_{(n)}^{\alpha}\sim Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})
and
\wb_{(n)}^{\beta}\sim Q_{i}^{\beta}(\wb;S_{i})
using the Monte-Carlo sampling (e.g. SGLD
(
welling2011bayesian
)
or SVGD
(
liu2016stein
)
). After plugging the samples into
\hat{L}(\pb+\wb;S_{i})
and
\hat{L}(\pb+\wb;S^{\prime}_{i})
, we can apply automatic gradient computations (with Tensorflow
(
tensorflow2015-whitepaper
)
or Pytorch
(
NEURIPS2019_9015
)
) over
\pb
to get the stochastic gradient estimator of
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
.

6
Discussion

We studied two PAC-Bayesian bounds for meta-learning in the few-shot case, where the number of examples in the target task is significantly smaller than that in the observed tasks.
As opposed to previous bounds, our bound in Theorem
4
remains tight in this scenario.
We instantiated these new bounds and related them to the Reptile and MAML algorithms and furthermore derived the PACMAML algorithm, and showed its efficiency and effectiveness over several meta-learning benchmarks.
Broadly speaking, our work falls into the category of PAC-Bayesian theories of non-i.i.d data
(
pentina2015lifelong
)
; however, our study case is more specific and our bounds are based on practical strategies.
One major limitation of the work is that we do not take into account a data domain shift (e.g.
(
germain2016new
)
), which is often present in practice.
However, the study of domain shift from a theoretical perspective requires additional assumptions and knowledge about the target data, which do not always exist in practice.
We leave a deeper discussion and exploration on these topics to future work.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(Q(S_{i},P),D_{i})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{z_{i}\sim D_{i}}\EE_{h_{i}\sim Q(h_{i}|P,S_{i})}[L(h_{i},z_{i})],

where
S_{i}\sim D_{i}^{m_{i}}
and
S_{\tilde{T}}=\left\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}\right\}
.
Similarly, the generalization error on the target task environment
T
is

\displaystyle R(P,T)=\EE_{(D,m)\sim T}\EE_{S\sim D^{m}}\EE_{z\in D}\EE_{h\sim Q(h|P,S)}[L(h,z)].

Using the Markov Inequality, with at least
1-\delta
probability,

\displaystyle\EE_{P\sim\Pcal}\left[e^{\lambda(R(P,T)-R_{S_{\tilde{T}}}(P))}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}\EE_{P\sim\Pcal}\EE_{D_{i}\sim T,S_{i}\sim D_{i}^{m_{i}}}^{i=1,\ldots,n}\left[e^{\lambda(R(P,T)-R_{S_{\tilde{T}}}(P))}\right].

The left-hand side can be lower bounded by,

\displaystyle\log\EE_{P\sim\Pcal}\left[e^{\lambda(R(P,T)-R_{S_{\tilde{T}}}(P))}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\frac{\Pcal(P)}{\Qcal(P)}e^{\lambda(R(P,T)-R_{S_{\tilde{T}}}(P))}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\log\frac{\Pcal(P)}{\Qcal(P)}+\lambda\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}[R(P,T)-R_{S_{\tilde{T}}}(P)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)+\lambda(R(\Qcal,T)-\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}[R_{S_{\tilde{T}}}(P)]).

The right-hand side is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{\delta}\EE_{P\sim\Pcal}\EE_{D_{i}\sim T,S_{i}\sim D_{i}^{m_{i}}}^{i=1,\ldots,n}\left[e^{\lambda(R(P,T)-R_{S_{\tilde{T}}}(P))}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\EE_{P\sim\Pcal}\EE_{D_{i}\sim T,S_{i}\sim D_{i}^{m_{i}}}^{i=1,\ldots,n}\left[e^{\lambda(R(P,T)-R_{S_{\tilde{T}}}(P))}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\EE_{P\sim\Pcal}\left[e^{\lambda(R(P,T)-\EE_{S_{\tilde{T}}\sim\tilde{T}}[R_{S_{\tilde{T}}}(P)])}\right]

\displaystyle+\log\EE_{P\sim\Pcal}\EE_{D_{i}\sim T,S_{i}\sim D_{i}^{m_{i}}}^{i=1,\ldots,n}\left[e^{\lambda(\EE_{S_{\tilde{T}}}[R_{S_{\tilde{T}}}(P)]-R_{S_{\tilde{T}}}(P))}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\EE_{P\sim\Pcal}\left[e^{\lambda(R(P,T)-\EE_{S_{\tilde{T}}}[R_{S_{\tilde{T}}}(P)])}\right]+n\Psi_{2}(\frac{\lambda}{n}),

(18)

where,

\displaystyle\EE_{S_{\tilde{T}}}[R_{S_{\tilde{T}}}(P)]

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle\EE_{(D_{i},m_{i})\sim\tilde{T},S_{i}\sim D_{i}^{m_{i}}}^{i=1,\ldots,n}[R_{S_{\tilde{T}}}(P)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{(D_{i},m_{i})\sim\tilde{T}}\EE_{S_{i}\sim D_{i}^{m_{i}}}\EE_{z_{i}\in D_{i}}\EE_{h_{i}\sim Q(h_{i}|P,S_{i})}[L(h_{i},z_{i})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\EE_{(D,m)\sim\tilde{T}}\EE_{S\sim D^{m}}\EE_{z\in D}\EE_{h\sim Q(h|P,S)}[L(h,z)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R(P,\tilde{T}).

Combining the left-hand and right-hand bounds together, we have with at least probability
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle R(\Qcal,T)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[L(Q(S_{i},P),D_{i})\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}+n\Psi_{2}(\frac{\lambda}{n})\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\EE_{P\in\Pcal}e^{\lambda(R(P,T)-R(P,\tilde{T}))}.

(19)

Lastly, combining Eq.(
19
) with Eq.(
15
) yields Eq.(
5
).

Furthermore, from Theorem
3
, it is straightforward to obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 5

For a target task environment
T
and an observed task environment
\tilde{T}
where
\EE_{\tilde{T}}[D]=\EE_{T}[D]
and
\EE_{\tilde{T}}[m]\geq\EE_{T}[m]
, let
\Pcal
be a fixed hyper-prior and
\lambda>0
,
\beta>0
, then with probability at least
1-\delta
over samples
S_{1}\in D_{1}^{m_{1}},\ldots,S_{n}\in D_{n}^{m_{n}}
where
(D_{i},m_{i})\sim\tilde{T}
, we have, for all base learners
Q
and hyper-posterior
\Qcal
,

\displaystyle R(\Qcal,T)\leq

\displaystyle\hat{R}(\Qcal,S_{i=1}^{n})+\tilde{\xi}D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)+\frac{1}{n\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[D_{KL}(Q(S_{i},P)\|P)\right]

\displaystyle+C(\delta,\lambda,\beta,n,m_{i})+\Delta_{\lambda}(\Pcal,\Qcal,T,\tilde{T}),

(20)

where
\Delta_{\lambda}(\Pcal,\Qcal,T,\tilde{T})=\min\left\{\frac{1}{\lambda}\log\EE_{P\in\Pcal}e^{\lambda(R(P,T)-R(P,\tilde{T}))},R(\Qcal,T)-R(\Qcal,\tilde{T})\right\}
, and
\tilde{\xi}=\frac{1}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{n\beta}
.

Proof

Similar to (
16
), we have

\displaystyle R(\Qcal,\tilde{T})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[L(Q(S_{i},P),D_{i})\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}+n\Psi_{2}(\frac{\lambda}{n})\right).

A simple reorganization of the terms leads to,

\displaystyle R(\Qcal,T)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[L(Q(S_{i},P),D_{i})\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}+n\Psi_{2}(\frac{\lambda}{n})\right)+(R(\Qcal,T)-R(\Qcal,\tilde{T})).

(21)

Combining Eq.(
21
) with Eq.(
19
) and Eq.(
15
) gives the bound in Eq.(
20
).

Note that although Eq.(
20
) gives a potentially tighter bound than Eq.(
5
), empirically it makes little difference because
R(\Qcal,T)-R(\Qcal,\tilde{T})
is inestimable in practice and cannot be directly optimized as a function of
\Qcal
. We will only numerically estimate its value in synthetic datasets in order to estimate the bound.

A.3
Proof of Theorem
4

For a target task environment
T
and an observed task environment
\tilde{T}
where
\EE_{\tilde{T}}[D]=\EE_{T}[D]
and
\EE_{\tilde{T}}[m]\geq\EE_{T}[m]
, let
\Pcal
be a fixed hyper-prior and
\lambda>0
,
\beta>0
, then with probability at least
1-\delta
over samples
S_{1}\in D_{1}^{m_{1}},\ldots,S_{n}\in D_{n}^{m_{n}}
where
(D_{i},m_{i})\sim\tilde{T}
, and subsamples
S_{1}^{\prime}\in D_{1}^{m_{1}^{\prime}}\subset S_{1},\ldots,S_{n}^{\prime}\in D_{n}^{m_{n}^{\prime}}\subset S_{n}
, where
\EE[m_{i}^{\prime}]=\EE_{T}[m]
, we have, for all base learner
Q
and all hyper-posterior
\Qcal
,

\displaystyle R(\Qcal,T)\leq

\displaystyle\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{L}(Q(S_{i}^{\prime},P),S_{i})\right]+\tilde{\xi}D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)+\frac{1}{n\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[D_{KL}(Q(S_{i}^{\prime},P)\|P)\right]

\displaystyle+C(\delta,\lambda,\beta,n,m_{i}),

where
\tilde{\xi}=\frac{1}{\lambda}+\frac{1}{n\beta}
.

Proof

The "task environment generalization bound" is the same as the one in Theorem
2
, because the base-learner in observed and target task have the same task environment
T
. Therefore, we have

\displaystyle R(\Qcal,T)\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[L(Q(S^{\prime}_{i},P),D_{i})\right]+\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)+\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)+\frac{n}{\lambda}\Psi_{2}(\frac{\lambda}{n}).

(22)

As for the "task-specific generalization bound", define,

\displaystyle\hat{L}(\hb)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{m_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{m_{i}}l(h_{i},z_{ij}),\;\;L(\hb)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{z_{i}\sim D_{i}}l(h_{i},z_{i}),

where
z_{ij}\in S_{i}
which is sampled from
D_{i}
.
According to the Markov inequality, with at least
1-\delta
probability, we have

\displaystyle\EE_{P\sim\Pcal}\EE_{\hb\sim P^{n}}\left[e^{n\beta(L(\hb)-\hat{L}(\hb))}\right]\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\EE_{P\sim\Pcal}\EE_{\hb\sim P^{n}}\EE_{\Sbb\sim\Db^{\mb}}\left[e^{n\beta(L(\hb)-\hat{L}(\hb))}\right]

Now take the logarithm of both sides, and transform the expectation over
\Pcal,P
to
\Qcal,Q
, where we use base-learner
Q(S_{i}^{\prime},P)
with
S^{\prime}_{i}\in D_{i}^{m^{\prime}_{i}}
. Then the LHS becomes

\displaystyle\log\EE_{P\sim\Pcal}\EE_{\hb\sim P^{n}}\left[e^{n\beta(L(\hb)-\hat{L}(\hb))}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\EE_{\hb\sim\Qb(\Sbb^{\prime},P)}[\frac{\Pcal(P)\prod_{i=1}^{n}P(h_{i})}{\Qcal(P)\prod_{i=1}^{n}Q_{i}(h_{i}|S_{i}^{\prime},P)}e^{n\beta(L(\hb)-\hat{L}(\hb))}]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[D_{KL}(Q(S_{i}^{\prime},P)\|P)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\beta\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}[\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(Q(S_{i}^{\prime},P),D_{i})]-\beta\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{L}(Q(S_{i}^{\prime},P),S_{i})].

The first equation uses the fact that the hyper-prior
\Pcal
and hyper-posterior
\Qcal
as well as the prior
P
are shared across all
n
observed tasks.
The inequality uses Jensen’s inequality to move the logarithm inside expectation.

The RHS is

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\EE_{P\sim\Pcal}\EE_{\hb\sim P^{n}}\EE_{\Sbb\sim\Db^{\mb}}\left[e^{n\beta(L(h)-\hat{L}(h))}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\log\EE_{P\sim\Pcal}\EE_{\hb\sim P^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\prod_{j=1}^{m_{i}}\EE_{z_{ij}\sim D_{i}}\left[e^{\frac{\beta}{m_{i}}(\EE_{z_{i}\sim D_{i}}[l(h_{i},z_{i})]-l(h_{i},z_{ij}))}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}\Psi_{1}(\frac{\beta}{m_{i}}).

Now, combining the LHS and RHS together, we get that with at least
1-\delta
probability,

\displaystyle\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(Q(S^{\prime}_{i},P),D_{i})]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(Q(S^{\prime}_{i},P),S_{i})\right]+\frac{1}{n\beta}D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)+\frac{1}{n\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[D_{KL}(Q(S^{\prime}_{i},P)\|P)\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{n\beta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{m_{i}}{\beta}\Psi_{1}(\frac{\beta}{m_{i}}).

(23)

Combining Eq.(
22
) with Eq.(
23
) immediately yields Eq.(
6
).

Appendix B
Derivations of MAML and Reptile

In this section, we derive a couple of meta-learning algorithms based on the MAP estimation of PAC-Bayesian bounds. To this end, we assume that the distribution families of the hyper-posterior
\Qcal(P)
and posterior
Q_{i}(h)
are from delta functions. In addition, we use the isotrophic Gaussian priors for the hyper-prior
\Pcal(P)
and the prior
P(h)
on all model parameters,

\displaystyle\Pcal(P)

\displaystyle=\Ncal(\pb|0,\sigma_{0}^{2})

\displaystyle\Qcal(P)

\displaystyle=\delta(\pb=\pb_{0})

\displaystyle P(h)

\displaystyle=\Ncal(\hb|\pb,\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle Q_{i}(h)

\displaystyle=\delta(\hb=\qb_{i})\;\;\forall i=1,\ldots,n.

This way we have a closed form solution for the two KL terms, which are (up to a constant)

\displaystyle D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)=

\displaystyle\int d\pb\delta(\pb=\pb_{0})\cdot\left(\frac{\|\pb\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\frac{k}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma_{0}^{2})+\log\delta(\pb=\pb_{0})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|\pb_{0}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\frac{k}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma_{0}^{2})+c,

where
k
is the dimension of
\pb
and
c
is a constant. Similarly,

\displaystyle\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}[D_{KL}(Q_{i}|P)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d\pb\delta(\pb=\pb_{0})\int d\hb\delta(\hb=\qb_{i})\cdot\left(\frac{\|\hb-\pb\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+\frac{k}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})+\log\delta(\hb=\qb_{i})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d\pb\delta(\pb=\pb_{0})\cdot\frac{\|\pb-\qb_{i}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+\frac{k}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})+c

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|\pb_{0}-\qb_{i}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+\frac{k}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^{2})+c.

Plugging in the above results, the PAC-Bayesian bound (
PacB
) in Eq.(
5
) and Eq.(
6
) are both of the form of,

\displaystyle PacB=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}L(\qb_{i},S_{i})+\frac{\tilde{\xi}\|\pb_{0}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\frac{1}{n\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\|\pb_{0}-\qb_{i}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}+C^{\prime},

where the constant
C^{\prime}
corresponding to Eq.(
5
) and Eq.(
6
) are different by
\Delta_{\lambda}
. The only free variable of
PacB
is
\pb_{0}
. The base-learner
\qb_{i}
can be any function of
\pb_{0}
and
S_{i}
for Eq.(
5
) or
S_{i}^{\prime}
for Eq.(
6
). One could find the MAP estimation of
PacB
by gradient descent with respect to
\pb_{0}
.

Note that in Eq.(
5
), for a given
\pb_{0}
and
S_{i}
, there exists an optimal base-learner
\qb_{i}^{*}
in the form of,

\displaystyle\qb_{i}^{*}=\argmin_{\qb_{i}}(PacB)=\argmin_{\qb_{i}}\left[L(\qb_{i},S_{i})+\frac{\|\pb_{0}-\qb_{i}\|^{2}}{2\beta\sigma^{2}}\right].

Given the optimal
\qb_{i}^{*}
, the full derivative of
PacB
with respect to
\pb_{0}
is substantially simpler,

\displaystyle\frac{d(PacB)}{d\pb_{0}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial(PacB)}{\partial\pb_{0}}+\left\langle\frac{\partial\qb_{i}^{*}}{\partial\pb_{0}},\frac{\partial(PacB)}{\partial\qb_{i}^{*}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial(PacB)}{\partial\pb_{0}}=\frac{\tilde{\xi}\pb_{0}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\pb_{0}-\qb_{i}^{*}}{\beta\sigma^{2}},

(24)

where the 2nd equation is because
\frac{\partial(PacB)}{\partial\qb_{i}^{*}}=0
for the optimal base-leaner
\qb_{i}^{*}
. Eq.(
24
) is the equivalent to the meta-update of the Reptile algorithm
[
16
]
, except that Reptile does not solve for the optimal base learner
\qb_{i}^{*}
.

From the optimal condition, the base-learner
\qb_{i}^{*}
satisfies,

\displaystyle\frac{\pb_{0}-\qb_{i}^{*}}{\beta\sigma^{2}}=\nabla_{\qb_{i}^{*}}L(\qb_{i}^{*},S_{i}).

Therefore, we can rewrite Eq.(
24
) in the form of the
implicit gradient
,

\displaystyle\frac{d(PacB)}{d\pb_{0}}=\frac{\tilde{\xi}\pb_{0}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\qb^{*}_{i}}L(\qb_{i}^{*},S_{i}).

In contrast, the standard multi-task objective uses the
explicit gradient
, where
\qb_{i}=\pb_{0}
and

\displaystyle\frac{d(PacB)}{d\pb_{0}}=\frac{\tilde{\xi}\pb_{0}}{\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{\pb_{0}}L(\pb_{0},S_{i}).

Derivations of PACMAML

For Theorem
4
, we use the following posterior as the base-learner for observed task
\tau_{i}
,

\displaystyle Q_{i}(S_{i}^{\prime},P)(h)=\frac{P(h)\exp(-\alpha\hat{L}(h,S_{i}^{\prime}))}{Z_{\alpha}(S_{i}^{\prime},P)}.

Plugging this
Q_{i}
into Eq.(
6
), we have

\displaystyle R(\Qcal,T)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\hat{L}(Q_{i},S_{i})\right]+\tilde{\xi}D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)+\frac{1}{n\beta}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[D_{KL}(Q_{i}\|P)\right]+C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\left[\hat{L}(Q_{i},S_{i})+\frac{1}{\beta}D_{KL}(Q_{i}\|P)\right]+\tilde{\xi}D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)+C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\EE_{h\sim Q_{i}}\left[\hat{L}(h,S_{i})+\frac{1}{\beta}\log Q_{i}(h)-\frac{1}{\beta}\log P(h)\right]+\tilde{\xi}D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)+C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}\EE_{h\sim Q_{i}}\left[\hat{L}(h,S_{i})-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\hat{L}(h,S_{i}^{\prime})-\frac{1}{\beta}\log Z_{\alpha}(S_{i}^{\prime},P))\right]+\tilde{\xi}D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)+C

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\EE_{P\sim\Qcal}[-\frac{1}{\beta}\log Z_{\alpha}(S_{i}^{\prime},P)+\hat{L}(Q_{i},S_{i})-\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\hat{L}(Q_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})]+\tilde{\xi}D_{KL}(\Qcal\|\Pcal)+C.

where
C=\tilde{\xi}\log\frac{2}{\delta}+\frac{n}{\lambda}\Psi(\frac{\lambda}{n})+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{m_{i}}{\beta}\Psi(\frac{\beta}{m_{i}})
.

C.1
The Gradient Estimator of PACOH and PACMAML

Assuming that the model hypothesis
h
is parameterized by
\vb
such that
\hat{L}(h,S_{i})\triangleq\hat{L}(\vb,S_{i})
, and
\vb
has prior
P(\vb)=\Ncal(\vb|\pb,\sigma^{2})
with meta-parameter
\pb
, then

\displaystyle\log Z_{\beta}(S_{i},\pb)=\log\int\Ncal(\vb|\pb,\sigma^{2})\exp(-\beta\hat{L}(\vb,S_{i}))d\vb.

Note that the parameter
\pb
appears in the probability distribution of the expectation, and the naive Monte-Carlo gradient estimator of such gradient is known to exhibit high variance. To reduce the variance, we apply the reparameterization trick
[
13
]
and rewrite
\vb=\pb+\wb
with
\wb\sim\Ncal(\wb|{\bf 0},\sigma^{2})
, then

\displaystyle\log Z_{\beta}(S_{i},\pb)=\log\int\Ncal(\wb|{\bf 0},\sigma^{2})\exp(-\beta\hat{L}(\pb+\wb,S_{i}))d\wb.

This leads to the gradient of
W_{1}
in the following form,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\pb}W_{1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{d}{d\pb}\log Z_{\beta}(S_{i},\pb)=\int Q^{\beta}_{i}(\wb;S_{i})\frac{\partial\hat{L}(\pb+\wb,S_{i})}{\partial\pb}d\wb,

\displaystyle\text{where,}\;\;Q^{\beta}_{i}(\wb;S_{i})\propto\Ncal(\wb|{\bf 0},\sigma^{2})\exp(-\beta\hat{L}(\pb+\wb,S_{i})).

As for
W_{2}
, the first term is simlar to
W_{1}
, but we also need to evaluate the gradient of
\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(Q^{\alpha}_{i},S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})
, which is

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\pb}\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(Q^{\alpha}_{i},S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})\frac{\partial\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(\pb+\wb,S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})}{\partial\pb}d\wb+\int\frac{\partial Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S^{\prime}_{i})}{\partial\pb}\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(\pb+\wb,S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})d\wb.

(25)

The second term of Eq.(
25
) is equivalent to,

\displaystyle\int\frac{\partial Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})}{\partial\pb}\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(\pb+\wb,S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})d\wb

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\pb}\int Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})\text{stop\_grad}\left(-\beta\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(\pb+\wb,S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})\right)d\wb.

The Monte-Carlo gradient estimator of this has the same high-variance problem as in the policy gradient method, which causes unreliable inference without warm-start. Instead, we apply the cold-start policy gradient method by approximating the loss with the one from the softmax value function
[
8
]
as follows,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\int Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})\text{stop\_grad}\left(-\beta\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(\pb+\wb,S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})\right)d\wb

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\log\int Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})\exp\left(\text{stop\_grad}\left(-\beta\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(\pb+\wb,S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})\right)\right)d\wb.

Then we take the gradient of the softmax value function,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\pb}\log\int Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})\exp\left(\text{stop\_grad}\left(-\beta\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(\pb+\wb,S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})\right)\right)d\wb

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\int\frac{\partial Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})}{\partial\pb}\exp\left(\text{stop\_grad}\left(-\beta\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(\pb+\wb,S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})\right)\right)d\wb}{\int Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})\exp\left(\text{stop\_grad}\left(-\beta\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(\pb+\wb,S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})\right)\right)d\wb}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\int\frac{\partial\log Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})}{\partial\pb}Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})\exp\left(\text{stop\_grad}\left(-\beta\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(\pb+\wb,S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})\right)\right)d\wb}{\int Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})\exp\left(\text{stop\_grad}\left(-\beta\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(\pb+\wb,S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})\right)\right)d\wb}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{\int\frac{\partial\log Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})}{\partial\pb}\Ncal(\wb|{\bf 0},\sigma^{2})\exp(-\beta\hat{L}(\pb+\wb,S_{i}))d\wb}{\int\Ncal(\wb|{\bf 0},\sigma^{2})\exp(-\beta\hat{L}(\pb+\wb,S_{i}))d\wb}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\beta}\int Q_{i}^{\beta}(\wb;S_{i})\frac{\partial\log Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})}{\partial\pb}d\wb

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\int\left(Q_{i}^{\beta}(\wb;S_{i})-Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})\right)\frac{\partial\hat{L}(\pb+\wb,S^{\prime}_{i})}{\partial\pb}d\wb.

This yields the overall gradient of
W_{2}
to be,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\pb}W_{2}\simeq

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\int Q^{\alpha}_{i}(\wb;S^{\prime}_{i})\frac{\partial\hat{L}(\pb+\wb,S^{\prime}_{i})}{\partial\pb}d\wb+\int Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})\frac{\partial\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(\pb+\wb,S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})}{\partial\pb}d\wb

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\int\left(Q_{i}^{\beta}(\wb;S_{i})-Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})\right)\frac{\partial\hat{L}(\pb+\wb,S^{\prime}_{i})}{\partial\pb}d\wb

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\int Q_{i}^{\beta}(\wb;S_{i})\frac{\partial\hat{L}(\pb+\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})}{\partial\pb}d\wb+\int Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})\frac{\partial\hat{L}^{\Delta}_{\frac{\alpha}{\beta}}(\pb+\wb,S_{i},S_{i}^{\prime})}{\partial\pb}d\wb

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})\frac{\partial\hat{L}(\pb+\wb;S_{i})}{\partial\pb}d\wb+\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\int\left(Q_{i}^{\beta}(\wb;S_{i})-Q_{i}^{\alpha}(\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})\right)\frac{\partial\hat{L}(\pb+\wb;S_{i}^{\prime})}{\partial\pb}d\wb.

D.1
Gaussian Process Model Details

We use the Gaussian process prior, where
P_{\mathchar 28946\relax}(h)=\mathcal{GP}(h|m_{\mathchar 28946\relax}(x),k_{\mathchar 28946\relax}(x,x^{\prime}))
and
k_{\mathchar 28946\relax}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\exp{(-\|\phi_{\mathchar 28946\relax}(x)-\phi_{\mathchar 28946\relax}(x^{\prime})\|^{2}})
. Both
m_{\mathchar 28946\relax}(x)
and
\phi_{\mathchar 28946\relax}(x)
are instantiated to be neural networks. The networks are composed of an input layer of size
1\times 32
, a hidden layer of size
32\times 32
.
m_{\mathchar 28946\relax}
and
\phi_{\mathchar 28946\relax}
has an output layer of size
32\times 1
and
32\times 2
, respectively.

We focused on regression problems where for every example
z_{j}=(x_{j},y_{j})
and a hypothesis
h
, the
l_{2}
-loss function is used so that
l(h,z_{j})=\|h(x_{j})-y_{j}\|_{2}^{2}
. This leads to a Gaussian likelihood function. Assuming there are
m
examples in the dataset, we have

\displaystyle P(y|h,x)=

\displaystyle\Ncal(h,\frac{m}{2\alpha}I)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\pi m/\alpha)^{m/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\alpha}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(h(x_{j})-y_{j})^{2}\right).

As a result, the partition function
Z_{\alpha}(S,P)
is,

\displaystyle Z_{\alpha}(S,P)

\displaystyle=(\pi m/\alpha)^{m/2}\int_{h}dhP(y|h,x)P_{\mathchar 28946\relax}(h)

\displaystyle=(\pi m/\alpha)^{m/2}\Ncal(y|m_{\mathchar 28946\relax}(x),k_{\mathchar 28946\relax}(x,x^{\prime})+\frac{m}{2\alpha}I),

We apply the GP base-learner
Q
on the the observed data
S_{i}
of task
\tau_{i}
. For notation simplicity, let us denote
Q_{i}(h^{i}|S_{i},P)=\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i},K_{i})
, where
h^{i}
denotes the model hypothesis (predictions) of the
m_{i}
examples in
S_{i}
. Then we have,

\displaystyle\hat{L}(Q_{i},S_{i})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m_{i}}\int Q_{i}(h^{i})(y^{i}-h^{i})^{\top}(y^{i}-h^{i})dh^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m_{i}}\left(y^{i\top}y^{i}-2\mu_{i}^{\top}y^{i}+\mu_{i}^{\top}\mu_{i}+\text{tr}(K_{i})\right),

where
y^{i}
denotes the labels of the
m_{i}
examples in
S_{i}
.

The hyper-prior
\Pcal(P_{\mathchar 28946\relax}):=\Pcal(\mathchar 28946\relax)=\Ncal(\mathchar 28946\relax|0,\sigma_{0}^{2}I)
is an isotropic Gaussian defined over the network parameters
\mathchar 28946\relax
, where we take
\sigma_{0}^{2}=3
in our numerical experiments. The MAP approximated hyper-posterior takes the form of a delta function, where
\Qcal_{\mathchar 28946\relax_{0}}(P_{\mathchar 28946\relax}):=\Qcal_{\mathchar 28946\relax_{0}}(\mathchar 28946\relax)=\delta(\mathchar 28946\relax=\mathchar 28946\relax_{0})
. As a result, we have

\displaystyle D_{KL}(\Qcal_{\mathchar 28946\relax_{0}}\|\Pcal)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d\mathchar 28946\relax\delta(\mathchar 28946\relax=\mathchar 28946\relax_{0})\left(\frac{\|\mathchar 28946\relax\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\frac{k}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma_{0}^{2})+\log\delta(\mathchar 28946\relax=\mathchar 28946\relax_{0})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\|\mathchar 28946\relax_{0}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{0}^{2}}+\frac{k}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma_{0}^{2})+c,

which combined with
\tilde{\xi}
becomes the regularizer on the parameters
\mathchar 28946\relax_{0}
.
