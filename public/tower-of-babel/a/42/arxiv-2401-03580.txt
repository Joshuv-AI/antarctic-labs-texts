Damped Gauss–Newton Search for Multi-Metric Hyperparameter Optimization
Qinwu Xu, Zhanyu Ge, Yifan Jiang

Abstract
We study hyperparameter optimization (HPO) from a numerical-optimization perspective and propose a black-box, target-seeking damped Gauss–Newton method for multiple validation responses. In contrast to uninformed candidate search such as grid or random search, the method uses numerical sensitivity information inferred from previously observed changes in hyperparameters and validation performance. Successive full-vector observations are used to construct an iterative secant approximation of the local Jacobian, which relates changes in the performance vector to changes in all optimized hyperparameters; a damped Gauss–Newton system then produces a joint update of the complete hyperparameter vector rather than optimizing coordinates independently. The method does not differentiate through model training or require dedicated coordinate-wise perturbation runs: following the initial two evaluations used to establish the first secant, each subsequent iteration requires one new full-vector evaluation. Tikhonov regularization stabilizes the resulting local inverse problem when the number of optimized parameters exceeds the number of performance responses. Unlike conventional multi-objective HPO aimed at approximating a Pareto front, our formulation seeks a specified target operating point in the multi-response performance space. We evaluate the same optimization principle in three complementary settings: four-dimensional XGBoost training-time HPO on three public classification datasets, six-dimensional LoRA/SFT post-training of Qwen3-VL-8B on a binary VQAv2 subset, and eight-dimensional post-processing threshold optimization on fixed classifier outputs. On the XGBoost benchmarks, the proposed method achieves validation performance comparable to grid search, random search, and TPE under a smaller number of model evaluations, although it does not consistently outperform these black-box baselines. The VLM study demonstrates joint post-training hyperparameter updates using TPR/FPR feedback and reveals strongly non-monotonic response and sensitivity trajectories, including a high-TPR/high-FPR degenerate regime. The threshold study further demonstrates target seeking in an underdetermined two-response, eight-parameter system and shows sensitivity to initialization and the importance of retaining the best observed iterate. Across these settings, the results support trajectory-based secant sensitivity as an evaluation-efficient local optimization mechanism complementary to global black-box HPO.

Introduction

Hyperparameter optimization (HPO) is a central component of modern
machine learning because choices such as learning rate, regularization,
subsampling, model depth, and optimization schedules can substantially
affect predictive performance, training stability, and computational
cost
Bischl et al. (2023)
. Unlike model parameters learned
directly through training, hyperparameters are typically selected by an
outer optimization process in which candidate configurations are trained
or evaluated and their validation performance is observed. When each
candidate requires a complete training or post-training run, the number
of such evaluations can dominate the cost of HPO.

Classical search methods provide simple but largely direction-uninformed
strategies. Grid search evaluates a predefined Cartesian product of
candidate values, with cost growing rapidly with the number of
hyperparameters. Random search can be substantially more efficient by
exploring more distinct values along influential dimensions

Bergstra and Bengio (2012)
, but successive candidates do not explicitly
exploit an estimated local response direction derived from previous
performance changes.

Sequential model-based methods use previous evaluations more explicitly.
Bayesian optimization constructs a probabilistic surrogate of validation
performance and uses an acquisition function to select subsequent
configurations
Snoek et al. (2012)
. TPE uses density estimation for
conditional and mixed search spaces
Bergstra et al. (2013)
, while
SMAC provides a widely used sequential model-based framework for
algorithm configuration and HPO

Hutter et al. (2011)
;
Lindauer et al. (2022)
. These approaches exploit
information from previous trials through a learned surrogate or density
model. The approach studied here instead extracts a local numerical
sensitivity directly from changes observed along the optimization
trajectory and uses it to construct the next joint hyperparameter update.

Other HPO methods reduce cost through adaptive resource allocation or
population-based exploration. Hyperband terminates poorly performing
configurations early
Li et al. (2017)
, BOHB combines model-based
search with Hyperband
Falkner et al. (2018)
, and population-based
training jointly trains and adapts a population of models

Jaderberg et al. (2017)
. Evolutionary strategies provide another
derivative-free alternative, including covariance-adaptation methods that
update a search distribution from populations of evaluated solutions

Hansen and Ostermeier (2001)
. These approaches are particularly useful when
partial training trajectories or parallel evaluations are available,
but typically explore multiple candidates or maintain a population or
distribution over candidate configurations.

Gradient-based HPO provides a different source of directional
information. By expressing training and hyperparameter selection as a
bilevel optimization problem, hypergradients can be obtained by
differentiating through, approximating, or implicitly differentiating
the inner training procedure
Franceschi et al. (2018)
. Reversible
differentiation, for example, propagates gradients through the training
trajectory and can optimize many hyperparameters simultaneously

Maclaurin et al. (2015)
. Such methods provide rich gradient
information when the learning pipeline is differentiable and its
optimization dynamics are accessible. These requirements can limit
direct application to black-box learners, non-differentiable
hyperparameters, legacy training systems, or evaluation metrics for
which differentiation through the complete training procedure is
unavailable.

Our approach instead obtains directional information numerically while
keeping the learning procedure itself as a black box. It is therefore
related to derivative-free numerical optimization, where local models,
finite differences, or function-value observations are used when
analytical derivatives are unavailable

Conn et al. (2009)
;
Khare and Kumar (2021)
;
Fortin and Wolkowicz (2004)
. SPSA, for
example, estimates a search direction from simultaneous random
perturbations rather than coordinate-wise finite differences

Spall (1992)
. In contrast, our method introduces no
dedicated perturbation evaluations to estimate a scalar-objective
gradient. Successive
full-vector
HPO iterates instead provide
paired changes in the hyperparameter and validation-performance vectors.
These trajectory observations are reused as local secant sensitivity
information, from which a regularized Gauss–Newton system produces a
joint update of the complete hyperparameter vector. Thus, the method is
sensitivity-informed without requiring analytical gradients or
backpropagation through model training.

Multiple validation responses introduce an additional distinction.
Multi-objective HPO (MOHPO) is commonly formulated as the search for a
set of nondominated configurations representing tradeoffs among competing
objectives, using approaches such as Pareto-based evolutionary algorithms
or surrogate-based methods such as ParEGO

Deb et al. (2002)
;
Knowles (2006)
;
Morales-Hernández et al. (2023)
;
Karl et al. (2022)
.
We consider a related but different
target-seeking
problem. Let

\boldsymbol{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote the hyperparameter vector and

\mathbf{P}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
the corresponding vector
of measured validation responses.

Given a desired operating point

\mathbf{P}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the target-seeking problem is

\min_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{P}^{\star}-\mathbf{P}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(1)

The objective is therefore not to approximate a Pareto front, but to
move the measured response vector toward a specified operating point
while retaining the individual responses explicitly in the local
numerical system.

At iteration
k
, the black-box learning or evaluation procedure is
executed at the complete vector
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}
to obtain

\mathbf{P}_{k}
. Changes between successive hyperparameter and performance
vectors provide secant information used to update a local sensitivity
matrix. Importantly, this sensitivity should not be interpreted as an
analytical or infinitesimal derivative of the training pipeline.
Validation responses such as classification accuracy, TPR, and FPR can
be noisy, plateaued, or non-monotonic with respect to hyperparameters.
The method instead constructs a finite, trajectory-dependent local
response approximation from observed changes and repeatedly updates it
as new evaluations become available. Damping and best-iterate retention
are consequently important practical components of the procedure.

This construction also has an important evaluation-cost distinction.
A coordinate-wise forward-difference approximation of a

d
-dimensional sensitivity requires multiple dedicated perturbed
evaluations around a reference configuration. Our iterative construction
instead reuses the sequence of full-vector configurations generated by
the optimization itself. Following the initial two evaluations used to establish the first secant,
each subsequent update introduces one new full-vector evaluation and
jointly advances all optimized hyperparameters. We therefore distinguish the number of
expensive model evaluations from the inexpensive numerical work required
to update the local sensitivity and solve the small regularized linear
system. This distinction is particularly relevant when one evaluation
requires model training or large-model post-training.

When the number of optimized hyperparameters exceeds the number of
performance responses, the local inverse problem is underdetermined.
This occurs in all three studies considered here: four XGBoost
hyperparameters are guided by one primary validation response, six VLM
post-training hyperparameters by two responses, and eight decision
thresholds by two responses. We stabilize these local systems using
Tikhonov regularization in a damped Gauss–Newton update and enforce
feasible parameter ranges where applicable. Because the method is local
and trajectory-based, its behavior can depend on initialization,
damping, and the local response geometry; these properties are examined
empirically rather than assuming global smoothness or convergence.

We evaluate the framework in three complementary settings spanning
training, post-training, and post-processing. First, we jointly tune four
XGBoost training hyperparameters on three public classification datasets
and compare against grid search, random search, and TPE using explicit
candidate-evaluation budgets. Second, we optimize six LoRA/SFT
hyperparameters of Qwen3-VL-8B using TPR and FPR feedback, where each new
HPO evaluation requires VLM post-training and is therefore substantially
more expensive than the tabular case. Third, we optimize eight
class-specific decision thresholds against two target responses using
fixed classifier outputs, isolating the numerical optimization behavior
from model-training cost. Together, these settings test the same
full-vector update principle under substantially different evaluation
loops and response geometries.

Our main contribution is a black-box, target-seeking HPO framework that
reuses successive full-vector evaluations to obtain local secant
sensitivity information and jointly update multiple hyperparameters
through a Tikhonov-regularized damped Gauss–Newton system. The framework
occupies a complementary position between direction-uninformed search,
surrogate-based black-box HPO, and differentiable hypergradient methods:
it extracts numerical directional information from observed HPO
trajectories without differentiating through training, fitting a global
surrogate, or requiring dedicated coordinate-wise perturbation runs.
The experiments characterize both its evaluation efficiency and its
limitations, including non-monotonic responses and sensitivity to
initialization.

2
Method

2.1
Problem Formulation

Let

\boldsymbol{\phi}=[\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{d}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

denote the vector of parameters to be optimized. For a given

\boldsymbol{\phi}
, the learning or evaluation procedure produces a
vector of measured validation responses
\mathbf{P}(\boldsymbol{\phi})=[P_{1}(\boldsymbol{\phi}),\ldots,P_{m}(\boldsymbol{\phi})]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.
Here,
P_{q}
denotes a measured performance quantity rather than an internal training loss; its definition depends on the application.

We consider the black-box mapping
\boldsymbol{\phi}\longmapsto\mathbf{P}(\boldsymbol{\phi}),

without differentiating through the underlying training or evaluation
procedure. Given a desired operating point (performance metrics)

\mathbf{P}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the target-seeking problem is minimize the difference between it and the predicted performance metrics (equation 1). The individual responses remain explicit rather than being collapsed into a single application-specific scalar score.

The proposed method obtains directional information numerically from
changes in measured performance. In this sense, the search is

numerical-sensitivity informed
: unlike grid or random search,
which do not construct a local response direction from previous
evaluations, observed parameter–performance changes are used to inform
the next joint update. No analytical hypergradient or differentiation
through model training is required.

This formulation applies to all three settings considered in this paper.
For XGBoost,
\boldsymbol{\phi}
contains training hyperparameters; for
VLM post-training, it contains LoRA/SFT hyperparameters; and for the
post-processing experiment, it contains class-specific decision
thresholds.

2.2
Full-Vector Secant Sensitivity

At iteration
k
, the complete parameter vector

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}
is evaluated to obtain
\mathbf{P}_{k}=\mathbf{P}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}).

Two successive evaluations provide the full-vector secant pair

\Delta\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k-1},\qquad\Delta\mathbf{P}_{k}=\mathbf{P}_{k}-\mathbf{P}_{k-1}.

(2)

We represent the local response through a numerical sensitivity matrix

\mathbf{J}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d},\qquad\Delta\mathbf{P}_{k}\approx\mathbf{J}_{k}\Delta\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}.

(3)

The matrix
\mathbf{J}_{k}
plays the role of a local Jacobian relating
changes in all
d
optimized parameters to changes in the
m
measured
responses. It is not obtained by analytical differentiation through the
learning algorithm; instead, it is a trajectory-dependent numerical
sensitivity estimate constructed and updated from observed full-vector
parameter–performance changes.

Because a successive finite-difference sensitivity estimate requires two
observations, the numerical sensitivity is initialized using a small
simultaneous perturbation of the complete parameter vector. Starting
from the empirical or default initialization
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}
, we
first evaluate

\mathbf{P}_{0}=\mathbf{P}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0})
and then form
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}+\delta\mathbf{1}_{d},
where
\mathbf{1}_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the all-ones vector and

\delta
is a small perturbation magnitude (e.g.,
0.001
), such that
all
d
optimized coordinates are perturbed simultaneously. A second
full-vector evaluation gives

\mathbf{P}_{1}=\mathbf{P}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1})
, and the resulting
differences

\Delta\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}=\boldsymbol{\phi}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{0}

and

\Delta\mathbf{P}_{1}=\mathbf{P}_{1}-\mathbf{P}_{0}

provide the initial numerical sensitivity information. Subsequent
sensitivity estimates are obtained directly from consecutive HPO
iterates and therefore require no additional perturbation evaluations.
This initialization requires only one perturbed full-vector evaluation,
rather than separate perturbation evaluations for each optimized
coordinate.

After initialization, the parameter changes generated by the optimizer
itself provide subsequent secant observations. Thus, the numerical
sensitivity is repeatedly updated from the trajectory already produced
by the HPO procedure rather than by introducing dedicated
coordinate-wise perturbation runs at every iteration.

Importantly,
\mathbf{J}_{k}
should be interpreted as a finite local
response approximation rather than an analytical or infinitesimal
derivative of the complete learning pipeline. Validation quantities such
as accuracy, TPR, and FPR can be plateaued, noisy, or non-monotonic with
respect to hyperparameters. The sensitivity estimate therefore describes
the locally observed response along the optimization trajectory and is
updated as new observations become available.

For the VLM implementation, the optimized hyperparameters have
substantially different numerical scales. The corresponding secant
quantities are normalized before constructing the numerical sensitivity
used by the controller, preventing parameters with larger numerical
magnitudes from dominating the update solely because of their units.

2.3
Damped Gauss–Newton Joint Update

At iteration
k
, the local sensitivity matrix is estimated from the
performance and parameter changes between two consecutive iterates.
Specifically, for performance metric
p
and optimized parameter
q
,
we use the successive finite-difference approximation

[\mathbf{J}_{k}]_{pq}=\frac{P_{k,p}-P_{k-1,p}}{\phi_{k,q}-\phi_{k-1,q}},\qquad p=1,\ldots,m,\quad q=1,\ldots,d.

(4)

Because all parameters may change simultaneously between consecutive
iterates, Eq. (
4
) represents a
trajectory-based numerical sensitivity rather than an independently
perturbed coordinate-wise partial derivative. The first sensitivity
estimate is initialized from two full-vector evaluations: the initial
configuration and a small simultaneous perturbation of all optimized
parameters. Thereafter, each newly evaluated HPO iterate is paired with
the preceding iterate to update the secant sensitivity, requiring no
additional coordinate-wise perturbation evaluations.

At iteration
k
, define the discrepancy between the desired and
observed performance vectors as

\mathbf{r}_{k}=\mathbf{P}^{\star}-\mathbf{P}_{k}.

(5)

Using the resulting local sensitivity model, the desired joint parameter
change approximately satisfies

\mathbf{J}_{k}\Delta\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}\approx\mathbf{r}_{k}.

(6)

We compute the update using a Tikhonov-regularized damped
Gauss–Newton system,

\left(\mathbf{J}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{J}_{k}+\lambda_{d}\mathbf{I}\right)\Delta\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}=\mathbf{J}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{r}_{k},

(7)

or equivalently

\Delta\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}=\left(\mathbf{J}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{J}_{k}+\lambda_{d}\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{J}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{r}_{k},

(8)

where
\lambda_{d}>0
is the damping coefficient. The regularization
stabilizes the local inverse problem when
\mathbf{J}_{k}
is
ill-conditioned or when
d>m
, and suppresses excessively large updates
caused by uncertain local sensitivity estimates.

A key property of Eq. (
7
) is that it solves for

\Delta\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as a single vector. The
sensitivity matrix therefore couples the measured performance responses
to all optimized parameters, and all
d
coordinates are updated jointly
rather than through independent coordinate searches.

The next parameter vector is

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k+1}=\Pi_{\Omega}\left(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}+\alpha_{k}\Delta\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k}\right),

(9)

where
\alpha_{k}
is the update step length and
\Pi_{\Omega}
restricts
the parameters to their prescribed feasible ranges when bounds are
imposed. In implementations using the adaptive step-length schedule,

\alpha_{k}=a+b/(1+k),

(10)

where
a
controls the asymptotic step length and
b
controls the
additional early-iteration step size.

The complete updated vector
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k+1}
is then evaluated
once to obtain
\mathbf{P}_{k+1}
. Together with

(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k},\mathbf{P}_{k})
, this new observation provides the
successive finite-difference information used to construct

\mathbf{J}_{k+1}
through Eq. (
4
).

Consequently, following the two-evaluation full-vector sensitivity
initialization, each subsequent Gauss–Newton update introduces only one
new full-vector evaluation while updating all
d
optimized parameters
simultaneously. This differs from a coordinate-wise finite-difference
Jacobian construction, which would require dedicated perturbation
evaluations for individual coordinates. When an evaluation entails
model training or post-training, the small local linear-algebra system
in Eq. (
7
) is negligible compared with the model
evaluation cost.

3.2
Case II: VLM Post-Training Hyperparameter Optimization

Experimental setup:

We next evaluate the proposed method in a substantially more expensive
multimodal post-training setting using Qwen3-VL-8B. In contrast to
Case I, where each hyperparameter vector controls a complete XGBoost

post-training run. Importantly, every candidate evaluation is restarted
from the same initial model and LoRA state, so that differences in
validation performance can be attributed to the post-training
hyperparameters rather than accumulated training across HPO iterations.
The resulting model is evaluated on the validation split, and the
observed TPR and FPR responses are used to construct the local
sensitivity estimate and determine the next joint hyperparameter update.
The held-out evaluation split is not used to construct the sensitivity
estimates or HPO updates and is reported only for evaluation.
The implementation code is publicly available in an anonymous
repository.

3.3
Case III: Post-Processing Threshold Optimization

Experimental design:

We finally evaluate the proposed method in an eight-dimensional
post-processing problem requiring neither model training nor
post-training. A fixed proprietary CNN classifier produces probability
scores for eight anonymized classes. For class
i
, a prediction is
positive when
p_{i}\geq\phi_{i}
, with

\boldsymbol{\phi}=[\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{8}]^{\top}
and

4
Conclusion

We introduced a black-box, target-seeking HPO method that constructs
damped Gauss–Newton updates from successive full-vector evaluations,
without coordinate-wise perturbation runs or differentiation through
training. The method jointly updates all hyperparameters using local
secant sensitivities and Tikhonov regularization, while retaining the
best observed iterate to accommodate non-monotonic responses. Across
XGBoost training, Qwen3-VL-8B post-training, and classifier threshold
optimization, the experiments demonstrate the same update principle
under substantially different evaluation costs and response dimensions:
the method is competitive with standard HPO baselines on the tabular
benchmarks, supports joint six-dimensional VLM post-training updates
from TPR/FPR feedback, and approaches specified TP/FP operating points
in the eight-threshold problem. The observed non-monotonicity and
initialization dependence also highlight that the method is a local,
trajectory-dependent optimizer rather than a replacement for global
black-box HPO. Future work will investigate more robust sensitivity
estimation under noisy responses, larger model and search spaces, and
hybrid global–local optimization strategies.
