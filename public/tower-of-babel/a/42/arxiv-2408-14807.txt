Abstract
. We construct families of graphs from linear groups SL ⁡ ( 2 , q ) {\mathrm{SL}}(2,q) , GL ⁡ ( 2 , q ) {\mathrm{GL}}(2,q) and GU ⁡ ( 2 , q 2 ) {\mathrm{GU}}(2,q^{2}) , where q q is an odd prime power, with the property that the continuous-time quantum walks on the associated networks of qubits admit perfect state transfer.



1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a simple finite graph, with adjacency matrix
A
, for some
fixed ordering of the vertex set
V(X)
. In this paper we shall study a quantum mechanical model known as a
continuous-time quantum walk
on
X
, based on the
XX
-Hamiltonian
[
23
, IV.E]
. In this model, the evolution of states is governed by the unitary matrices
U(t)=\exp(-itA)
, for
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
(denoting time). For background on quantum walks we refer the reader to
[
10
]
and
[
12
]
.

A phenomenon of central importance in the theory is
perfect state transfer
(PST).
Let
{\mathbb{C}}^{V(X)}
denote the Hilbert space in which the characteristic vectors

e_{a}
,
a\in V(X)
, form an orthonormal basis.
Let
a
and
b
be vertices of
X
. We say that we have perfect state transfer from
a
to
b
at time
\tau
if
U(\tau)e_{a}=\gamma e_{b}

for some
\gamma\in{\mathbb{C}}
of absolute value 1; in other words, an initial state concentrated on the vertex
a
evolves at time
\tau
to one concentrated on
b
.
A spectral characterization (c.f Theorem 2.4.4
[
14
]
) of graphs admitting PST was obtained in Coutinho’s thesis
[
14
]
.
An early result of Godsil
[
19
, Corollary 10.2]
shows that for a given maximal degree there are only finitely many connected graphs that admit PST. It is therefore of interest to consider infinite families of connected graphs that admit PST. In this paper we construct several such families.

In order to provide context for our results we give a brief review, based partly on the list in
[
11
]
, of previous work in this direction.
Among paths, only those with two or three
vertices admit PST. More generally, it has recently been shown
[
13
]
that these paths are the only trees that admit PST. Complete graphs
K_{n}
do not admit PST for
n>2
.
Among strongly regular graphs, we have PST only in the trivial case of a
complement to a disjoint union of an even number of copies of
K_{2}
. However, bipartite
doubles of many
strongly regular graphs and distance regular graphs
[
15
]
do provide examples of PST.

For graphs belonging to the Johnson scheme
J(n,k)
of
k
-subsets in a set of size
n
, it was proved in
[
1
]
that PST can occur only if
n=2k
. In this case, the Kneser graph
K(2k,k)
is a disjoint union of
\frac{1}{2}\binom{2k}{k}

copies of
K_{2}
so, obviously, it admits PST. When
\binom{2k}{k}
is divisible by
4
, the complement also admits PST. Apart from these, no other graph belonging to the scheme has yet been found that admits PST, and it has been shown that
K(2k,k)
is the only generalized Johnson graph admitting PST.

There are some general results on the behavior of PST with respect to certain constructions such as products, covers and joins
[
2
]
,
[
11
]
, yielding further examples. There are also general results about graphs belonging to association schemes
[
15
]
. An important result proved at this level of generality is the fact that PST implies that one of the
0-1
matrices defining the association scheme must be a permutation matrix of order 2 having no fixed points.
Other examples arise from
Cayley graphs, mostly on abelian groups. For nonabelian groups, examples of PST have been found for some
normal
Cayley graphs, that is those in which the connection set is closed under conjugation. In both cases, the graphs belong to the conjugacy association scheme, and the aforementioned result implies that PST requires the existence of a central
involution in the group. Some general results on abelian Cayley graphs are proved in
[
26
]
. Hypercubes were among the first examples of PST studied

[
10
]
and, more generally, cubelike graphs have been studied in detail in

[
6
]
,
[
9
]
and
[
8
]
, and numerous
families admitting PST have been found. PST in Integral circulants has been classified

[
5
]
and this result has been generalized to Cayley graphs on abelian groups with cyclic Sylow
2
-subgroups
[
3
]
.
Nonabelian groups with normal Cayley graphs that admit PST include
Dihedral groups of order divisible by 4
[
7
]
, and extraspecial
2
-groups
[
25
]
.

Remark
.

As mentioned above,
if a normal Cayley graph on a group
G
admits PST, then
G
must necessarily contain a central involution. In the case
G
is a group containing a central involution
z
, the Cayley graph on
G
with
G\setminus\{1,z\}
as its “connection” set, is the complement of the disjoint union of
|G|/2
copies of
K_{2}
. As we discussed already, this graph admits PST, provided
|G|\equiv 0\pmod{4}
.

While this graph belongs to the conjugacy class scheme on
G
, it is a “trivially” occurring example. It is of interest to find examples of groups with central involutions
whose conjugacy class schemes contain nontrivial examples of graphs admitting PST.

It is also desirable for these nontrivial examples to be connected, as otherwise they are just disjoint unions of graphs
belonging to conjugacy class schemes on a subgroup of
G
.

In this paper we construct four families of connected graphs exhibiting PST from the linear groups

{\mathrm{SL}}(2,q)
,
{\mathrm{GL}}(2,q)
and
{\mathrm{GU}}(2,q)
, where
q
is an odd prime power.

Three of these are families of normal Cayley graphs and, as far as we know, are the first such examples in which the underlying groups are not solvable.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
q
be an odd prime power. If
G
is one of
\mathrm{GL}(2,q)
,
\mathrm{GU}(2,q)
, or
\mathrm{SL}(2,q)
, there exists a connected normal Cayley graph
\Gamma
on
G
admitting PST such that
\overline{\Gamma}
is not
the disjoint union of copies of
K_{2}
.

The connection sets of these Cayley graphs, which have a uniform definition for each family, will be specified later in §
4.1
, §
4.2
, and §
4.3
once the required notation has been established. At that point the reader may notice a parallelism in the definitions of the
\mathrm{GL}(2,q)
and
\mathrm{GU}(2,q)
graphs, as well as in the proofs of these cases of Theorem
1.1
, that is congruous with the spirit of ”Ennola duality”.

The fourth family is constructed in orbital (or Schurian) association schemes, defined in §
2.2
below.
(
[
4
]
and
[
20
]
are references for orbital schemes.) When
q
is a prime power, one of the main results of
[
21
]
shows that the double cosets of
{\mathrm{GL}}(2,q)
in
{\mathrm{GL}}(2,q^{2})
give rise to an orbital association scheme, which we denote by
\mathrm{GL}(2,q^{2})\sslash\mathrm{GL}(2,q)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
q
be a prime power with
q\equiv 3\pmod{4}
. Then there exists a connected graph

\Gamma
belonging to
\mathrm{GL}(2,q^{2})\sslash\mathrm{GL}(2,q)
admitting PST such that
\overline{\Gamma}
is not a disjoint union of copies of
K_{2}
.

We believe that these are the first nontrivial examples of perfect state transfer on graphs belonging to an orbital scheme. The Johnson scheme is also an orbital scheme but, as mentioned in the Introduction, the only known graphs belonging to a Johnson scheme admitting PST are the trivial
examples of the Kneser graphs
K(2k,k)
, and their complements when
\binom{2k}{k}
is divisible by 4.

To assist the reader we describe next the organization of this paper. There are two sections of preliminaries. §
2
deals with association schemes, recalling the definitions of the conjugacy class schemes and orbital schemes which provide the setting for our later calculations. We also include here the characterization of PST in graphs belonging to an association scheme, which is central to what follows.
In §
3
we recall the well-known descriptions
of the characters and conjugacy classes of the groups

{\mathrm{GL}}(2,q)
,
{\mathrm{GU}}(2,q)
and
{\mathrm{SL}}(2,q)
and establish notation.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is given in §
4
, with one subsection devoted to each
family. These subsections can be read independently. It is here
that the precise definitions of the Cayley graphs are to be found. As mentioned above, the constructions and proofs
for
{\mathrm{GL}}(2,q)
and
{\mathrm{GU}}(2,q)
are strongly parallel
with
{\mathrm{GU}}(2,q)
objects related to their
{\mathrm{GL}}(2,q)

counterparts by ”change of signs”.

The proof of Theorem
1.2
is carried out in the final section, which requires no material from §
4
.
The constructions of the orbital graphs are given and
the question of PST is reduced to a simple statement (Proposition
5.2
)
which is then established by a series of calculations, requiring
the careful evaluation of character sums over cosets.

2.
PST in association schemes

A
d
-class association scheme on
n
vertices, is a set
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{0},A_{1},\ldots A_{d}\}

of square
0
-
1
matrices of order
n
satisfying the following:

(1)

A_{0}=I
;

(2)

\sum\limits_{i=0}^{d}A_{i}=J
;

(3)

A_{i}^{T}\in\mathcal{A}
, for all
0\leq i\leq d
; and

(4)

A_{i}A_{j}=A_{j}A_{i}\in\mathrm{Span}\left(\mathcal{A}\right)
.

The Bose–Mesner algebra
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{A}]
of
\mathcal{A}
is the complex algebra generated
by the matrices in
\mathcal{A}
. By design, elements of
\mathcal{A}
form a basis for
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{A}]
. It is well known that
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{A}]
also has a basis
\mathcal{E}=\{E_{0},E_{1},\ldots E_{d}\}
of primitive idempotents.

Given
i,j\in\{0,1,\ldots,d\}
, let
\lambda_{i,j}
be such that
A_{i}E_{j}=\lambda_{i,j}E_{j}
. The
\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{E}
array whose
(i,j)
-th entry is
\lambda_{i,j}
, is called the character table of the scheme
\mathcal{A}
. Note that the
i
-th row of the character table gives the spectrum of
A_{i}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A graph
X
is said to belong to an association scheme
\mathcal{A}
if the adjacency matrix
A_{X}
of
X
is in
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{A}]
. Equivalently,
A_{X}
is the sum of a subset of the
A_{i}
,
i\neq 0
.

We note that, as we assume graphs to be undirected and without loops, the subset of the
A_{i}
in this definition must
be closed under transposition and not include
A_{0}
.

Coutinho has given necessary and sufficient conditions
[
14
, Theorems 2.4.4 and 3.2.1, Corollary 3.3.2]
for PST between two vertices of a graph belonging to an association scheme. We now reformulate his results in a way
which is more convenient for our purposes.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
X
be a graph
belonging to a
d
-class association scheme with primitive idempotents

E_{0}
, …,
E_{d}
, and corresponding eigenvalues
\deg(X)=\theta_{0}\geq\theta_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\theta_{d}
.
Let
g=\gcd\{\theta_{0}-\theta_{i}\ :\ i=0,\ldots,d\}
.
Then perfect state transfer occurs at a time
t
between vertices
a
and
b
if and only if all of the following conditions hold.

(a)

The eigenvalues of X are integers.

(b)

The relation of the scheme that contains
(a,b)
, say
T
, is a permutation matrix of order 2 with no fixed points.

(c)

Let
\Phi^{+}=\{\theta_{j}:TE_{j}=E_{j}\}
and
\Phi^{-}=\{\theta_{j}:TE_{j}=-E_{j}\}
. Then

v_{2}(\theta_{0}-\theta_{i})=v_{2}(g)
for all
\theta_{j}\in\Phi^{+}
and
v_{2}(\theta_{0}-\theta_{j})>v_{2}(g)

for all
\theta_{j}\in\Phi^{-}
. (Here
v_{2}(m)
is the
2
-adic valuation
of the integer
m
.)

Moreover,
t
is an odd multiple of
\frac{\pi}{g}

Remark
.

We note that the condition of
strong cospectrality
in
[
14
, Theorem 2.4.4(i)]
is implied by our condition (c) as the latter implies that
\Phi^{+}\cap\Phi^{-}=\emptyset
. Hence, if
\tilde{E}_{\theta}

is the idempotent projection for the eigenvalue
\theta
and

\tilde{E}_{\theta}=E_{i_{1}}+\cdots E_{i_{t}}
its decomposition into primitive
idempotents then we have
TE_{i_{j}}=\sigma_{\theta}E_{i_{j}}
for all
j
,
where
\sigma_{\theta}=\pm 1
is independent of
j
. Thus
\tilde{E}_{\theta}e_{b}=\tilde{E}_{\theta}Te_{a}=\sigma_{\theta}\tilde{E}_{\theta}e_{a}
.

In this paper, we are interested in two types of association schemes that arise from finite groups: (a) conjugacy class schemes and (b) orbital (or Schurian) schemes.
We first describe the conjugacy class scheme.

2.1.
Conjugacy Class Schemes

Let
G
be a finite group and let
\{C_{0},C_{1},\ldots C_{d}\}
be the set of conjugacy classes in
G
, with

C_{0}=\{1\}
Given
0\leq i\leq d
, let
A_{i}
be the
G\times G
matrix, with

A_{i}(g,h)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $hg^{-1}\in C_{i}$},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases},

for all
g,h\in G
.
It is well-known (see for e.g. § 3.3 of
[
20
]
) that the set
\mathcal{A}_{G}=\{A_{i}\ :\ i\in\{0,1,\ldots d\}\}
forms an association scheme and that its Bose-Mesner algebra is isomorphic to the centre of the group algebra
\mathbb{C}[G]
, that is,
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{A}_{G}]=Z\left(\mathrm{End}(\mathbb{C}[G])\right)
.

Let
\mathrm{Irr}(G)
denote the set of irreducible complex characters of
G
. Given
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
, let
E_{\psi}
be the
G\times G
matrix with
E_{\psi}(g,h)=\dfrac{\psi(hg^{-1})\psi(1)}{|G|}
. The set
\{E_{\psi}\ :\ \psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)\}
is the basis of primitive idempotents for
\mathbb{C}[\mathcal{A}_{G}]
. If
c
is an element in the class
C_{i}
, then we have

(1)

A_{i}E_{\psi}=\dfrac{|C_{i}|\psi(c^{-1})}{\psi(1)}E_{\psi}.

Using Theorem
2.2
, if there is a graph admitting PST that belongs to the conjugacy class scheme on
G
, then there is some
t\in G
such that
A_{t}
is a permutation matrix of order
2
. We note that
A_{t}
is a permutation matrix of order
2
if and only if
t
is a central involution. In other words, the existence of a graph admitting PST belonging to the conjugacy class scheme on
G
implies the existence of a central involution in
G
.

Let
G
be a group possessing a central involution
z
. Given a graph
\Gamma
belonging to the conjugacy class scheme on
G
, there exists an inverse-closed set
\mathfrak{S}\subset G
which is a union of conjugacy classes such that
\Gamma:=\mathrm{Cay}(G,\mathfrak{S})
. Let
A_{\Gamma}
denote the adjacency matrix of
\Gamma
. Given
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
, let
\theta_{\chi}
be the eigenvalue of
A_{\Gamma}
satisfying
A_{\Gamma}E_{\chi}=\theta_{\chi}E_{\chi}
. Using (
1
), we have

\theta_{\chi}:=\dfrac{\overline{\chi(\mathfrak{S})}}{\chi(1)}=\dfrac{{\chi(\mathfrak{S})}}{\chi(1)}.

The last equality follows since
\mathfrak{S}
is inverse-closed.

To check if
\Gamma
admits PST, we apply Theorem
2.2
. The role of
T
in the theorem is played by
A_{\{t\}}
. Using (
1
) and Theorem
2.2
, we have the following result.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
G
be a group possessing a central involution
t
, and let
\mathfrak{S}
be an inverse-closed set which is a union of conjugacy classes in
G
. Consider the Cayley graph
\Gamma:=\mathrm{Cay}(G,\mathfrak{S})
. Given
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
, define
\theta_{\chi}:=\dfrac{{\chi(\mathfrak{S})}}{\chi(1)}
,

\Phi^{+}=\{\theta_{\chi}\ :\ \chi(t)/\chi(1)=1\}

and

\Phi^{-}=\{\theta_{\chi}\ :\ \chi(t)/\chi(1)=-1\}.

If,

(i)

\theta_{\chi}\in\mathbb{Z}
, for all
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
; and

(ii)

there exists
a\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\theta_{\chi}\equiv\begin{cases}a\pmod{4}&\text{if $\chi\in\Phi^{+}$}\\
a+2\pmod{4}&\text{if $\chi\in\Phi^{-}$,}\end{cases}

then there is perfect state transfer between vertices
x
and
xt
of
\Gamma
, for all
x\in G
. ∎

2.2.
Orbital Schemes

Let
G
be a finite group and let
H\leq G
. By
G/H
, we denote the set of left cosets of
H
in
G
. Let
D
be a complete set of
(H,H)
–double coset representatives in
G
. Given
d\in D
, we define
A_{d}
to be the
G/H\times G/H
matrix satisfying

A_{d}(xH,yH)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $x^{-1}y\in HdH$},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases},

for all
xH,yH\in G/H
.

A complex character
\psi
of
G
is said to be multiplicity-free if

\{\left\langle\psi,\chi\right\rangle\ :\ \chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)\}\subseteq\{0,1\}.

It is a well-known result (see for e.g.,
[
20
, Corollary 13.5.1]
) that
G\sslash H:=\{A_{d}\ :d\in D\}
is an association scheme if and only if the permutation character
{1}_{H}^{G}:=\mathrm{Ind}_{H}^{G}\left({1}\right)
is multiplicity-free. An
orbital scheme
is an association scheme of the form
G\sslash H
, where
(G,H)
is such that
{1}_{H}^{G}
is multiplicity-free.

Consider an orbital scheme
G\sslash H
.
Let

(2)

\mathrm{Irr}({G\sslash H}):=\{\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)\ :\ \left\langle\psi,\chi\right\rangle=1\}.

Let
S\subset G
and
\psi
a complex character of
G
, then by
\chi(S)
, we denote the sum
\sum\limits_{s\in S}\chi(S)

Given
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}_{G\sslash H}(G)
, let
\mathbf{E}_{\chi}
be the
G/H\times G/H
matrix satisfying

\mathbf{E}_{\chi}(xH,yH)=\dfrac{\chi(1)}{|G|}\chi(x^{-1}yH).

It is a standard result (see for eg.
[
20
, Corollary 13.8.2]
) that
\{\mathbf{E}_{\chi}\ :\ \chi\in\mathrm{Irr}({G\sslash H})\}
is the basis of primitive idempotents for
\mathbb{C}[G\sslash H]
.

Given
d\in D
and
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G\sslash H)
, we also have (c.f
[
20
, Corollary 13.9.1]
)

(3)

A_{d}\mathbf{E}_{\chi}=\dfrac{\chi(d^{-1}H)}{|H\cap H^{d}|}\mathbf{E}_{\chi}.

By Theorem
2.2
, if there is a graph admitting PST in
G\sslash H
, then there is some
d\in G
such that
A_{d}
is a permutation matrix of order
2
. We now give a characterization of orbital schemes containing a permutation matrix of order
2
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G
be a finite group and let
H\leq G
be a subgroup such that
{1}_{H}^{G}
is multiplicity-free. Let
N_{G}(H)
be the normalizer of
H
in
G
. Given
d\in D
, the matrix
A_{d}\in G\sslash H
is a permutation matrix of order
2
if and only if
d\in N_{G}(H)\setminus H
and
d^{2}\in H
.

Proof.

Pick a
d\in G
, such that
A_{d}
is a permutation matrix. Since
A_{d}
is a permutation matrix and since
A_{d}(H,dH)=1
, it follows that
A_{d}(H,xH)=0
for all
xH\neq dH
,
which means that
HdH=dH
. Hence
d\in N_{G}(H)
. Conversely, if
d\in N_{G}(H)
, then
HdH=dH
, and thus
A_{d}
must be a permutation matrix. Thus
A_{d}
is a permutation matrix if and only if
d\in N_{G}(H)
.

We observe that given
r,s\in N_{G}(H)
, we have
A_{r}A_{s}=A_{rs}
. We also note that
A_{r}=I
if and only if
r\in H
. Thus
\{A_{d}\ :\ d\in N_{G}(H)\}
, forms a group isomorphic to
N_{G}(H)/H
. Using this, we conclude that given
d\in G
,
A_{d}
is a permutation matrix of order
2
if and only if
d\in N_{G}(H)\setminus H
and
d^{2}\in H
.
∎

Assume that
G\sslash H
is such that
N_{G}(H)\gneqq H
and that there is
t\in N_{G}(H)\setminus H
such that
t^{2}\in H
. From our discussion above,
A_{t}
is a permutation matrix of order
2
. Let
D\subset G
satisfying the following: (i) for all
x,y\in D
with
x\neq y
, we have
HxH\neq HyH
; (ii) given
x\in D
, there is an
\tilde{x}\in D
such that
x^{-1}\in H\tilde{x}H
; and (iii)
1\notin D
. The matrix
A_{D}:=\sum\limits_{d\in D}A_{d}
must be a symmetric
0
–
1
matrix. Let
\Gamma_{D}
be the graph in
G\sslash H
whose adjacency matrix if
A_{D}
. Given
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G\sslash H)
, let
\theta_{\chi}
be the eigenvalue satisfying
A_{D}\mathbf{E}_{\chi}=\theta_{\chi}\mathbf{E}_{\chi}
. Using (
3
), we have

\theta_{\chi}:=\sum\limits_{d\in D}\dfrac{\chi(d^{-1}H)}{|H\cap H^{d}|}=\sum\limits_{d\in D}\dfrac{\chi(dH)}{|H\cap H^{d}|}.

Application of Theorem
2.2
to
\Gamma_{D}
, by setting
T:=A_{t}
, yields the following result.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
G
,
H
,
t
,
D
, and
\Gamma_{D}
be as described in the previous paragraph. Given
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G\sslash H)
, define

\theta_{\chi}:=\sum\limits_{d\in D}\dfrac{\chi(dH)}{|H\cap H^{d}|},

\Phi^{+}=\left\{\theta_{\chi}\ :\ \dfrac{\chi(tH)}{|H|}=1\right\}

and

\Phi^{-}=\left\{\theta_{\chi}\ :\ \dfrac{\chi(tH)}{|H|}=-1\right\}.

If,

(i)

\theta_{\chi}\in\mathbb{Z}
, for all
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G\sslash H)
; and

(ii)

there exists
a\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\theta_{\chi}\equiv\begin{cases}a\pmod{4}&\text{if $\chi\in\Phi^{+}$}\\
a+2\pmod{4}&\text{if $\chi\in\Phi^{-}$,}\end{cases}

then there is perfect state transfer between vertices
xH
and
xtH
of
\Gamma_{D}
, for all
x\in G
. ∎

3.
Complex representations of
\mathrm{GL}(2,q)
,
{\mathrm{GU}}(2,q)
and
\mathrm{SL}(2,q)
.

In this section, we describe the irreducible characters of
\mathrm{GL}(2,q)
and
{\mathrm{GU}}(2,q)
and
\mathrm{SL}(2,q)
. The facts about
{\mathrm{GU}}(2,q)
and
\mathrm{SL}(2,q)
will be needed only for
our study of the Cayley graphs on the respective groups,
while those for
\mathrm{GL}(2,q)
will be needed for both the Cayley graphs and the orbital scheme graphs.
If
{\mathbb{F}}
is a field, we shall denote by
\widehat{{\mathbb{F}}^{\times}}
the group of multiplicative characters
{\mathbb{F}}^{\times}
with complex values.

3.1.
Characters of
\mathrm{GL}(2,q)

Let
q
be an odd prime power.
In this section, we describe conjugacy classes and complex irreducible representations of the finite general linear group
\mathrm{GL}(2,q)
.
The irreducible complex representations of this group were first classified by Schur
[
24
]
and Jordan
[
22
]
. For a modern exposition, we refer the reader to
[
18

We first start by describing the conjugacy classes of
\mathrm{GL}(2,q)
.
Let
q
be an odd prime power and let
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
and
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
denote the finite fields of sizes
q
and

respectively.
Fix
\Delta
to be a fixed non-square in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}

denote a fixed root of

. The set
\{1,\sqrt{\Delta}\}
is a basis for
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. Given

be such that

.

Given

c_{1}(x):=\begin{bmatrix}x&0\\
0&x\end{bmatrix}

c_{2}(x):=\begin{bmatrix}x&1\\
0&x\end{bmatrix}
,
c_{3}(x,y):=\begin{bmatrix}x&0\\
0&y\end{bmatrix}

z\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}

c_{4}(z):=\begin{bmatrix}x_{z}&\Delta y_{z}\\

. A central element in

is necessarily one of

is conjugate to

. A non-central element with two distinct eigenvalues

is conjugate to
c_{3}(x,y)
. Finally, an element with no eigenvalues in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}
is conjugate to one of
\{c_{4}(z)\ :\ z\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\}
.

Irreducible characters of
\mathrm{GL}(2,q)
can be split into four categories, by degree. We first describe the linear characters.

Let
\mathrm{det}:\mathrm{GL}(2,q)\to{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}
denote the determinant map. Every linear character of
\mathrm{GL}(2,q)
is of the form
\lambda\circ\mathrm{det}
,
where
\lambda
is a multiplicative character
of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}
. For ease of writing, we shall sometimes abuse notation to identify
\lambda\circ\mathrm{det}\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
with
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}}
.

Next, we describe the so called parabolically induced characters. Let
\mathrm{B}(q)
denote the subgroup of upper triangular matrices in
\mathrm{GL}(2,q)
. Given
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}\in\widehat{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}}
, we get a character
\chi:=[\chi_{1},\chi_{2}]
of

{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}\times{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}
and, by inflation,
a character of
\mathrm{B}(q)
, which we denote by the same symbol, given by

(4)

[\chi_{1},\chi_{2}]\left(\begin{bmatrix}x&z\\
0&y\end{bmatrix}\right)=\chi_{1}(x)\chi_{2}(y).

We obtain a character
I[\chi_{1},\chi_{2}]:=\mathrm{Ind}_{\mathrm{B}(q)}^{\mathrm{GL}(2,q)}(\chi)
of
\mathrm{GL}(2,q)
, via induction. It is well known that, provided
\chi_{1}\neq\chi_{2}
, the character
I[\chi_{1},\chi_{2}]
is an irreducible character of degree
q+1
. Such characters of degree
q+1
will be referred to as principal series characters.
It is also known that
for
\chi\in\widehat{{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}}
the associated linear character
\chi:=\chi\circ\mathrm{det}
of
G
is an irreducible summand of
I[\chi,\chi]
and that

is an irreducible character of degree
q
. We shall refer to such characters as Steinberg type characters.

A character

4.1.
PST in
\mathrm{GL}(2,q)

Let
q
be an odd prime power and let
G=\mathrm{GL}(2,q)
. Then
G
has a unique central involution, namely,
t:=\begin{bmatrix}-1&0\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix}
.
Let
S
denote the set of squares in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
and
N:=\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\setminus S
.

We consider the set
\mathfrak{S}
of elements which are conjugate to an element of the set

\{c_{3}(1,-1)\}\bigcup\left(\bigcup\limits_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}c_{2}(x)\right)\bigcup\left(\bigcup\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\\
z^{q+1}\in\{1\}\cup N\end{subarray}}c_{4}(z)\right).

Here,
c_{2}(x)
,
c_{3}(x,y)
, and
c_{4}(z)
are as defined in §
3.1
.
We will prove that the Cayley graph
\Gamma:=\mathrm{Cay}\left(G,\mathfrak{S}\right)
— a graph belonging to the conjugacy class scheme over
G
— possesses PST. This shall be done by proving that
\Gamma
satisfies the premise of Corollary
2.3
. We do this in a series of lemmas.

Lemma 4.1
.

The eigenvalues of
\Gamma
are integral.

Proof.

It is well known that, given a conjugacy class
C
and an irreducible character
\chi
,
\dfrac{\chi(C)}{\chi(1)}
is an algebraic integer. As
\mathfrak{S}
is a union of rational conjugacy classes, it follows that, for any
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
,
\theta_{\chi}={\chi(S)}/\chi(1)
is an integer.
∎

Our next step will be to show that all eigenvalues in
\Phi^{+}
are congruent to
2\pmod{4}
and the ones in
\Phi^{-}
are
0\pmod{4}
. We first prove some auxiliary lemmas. The following result is an immediate consequence of orthogonality of characters.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\lambda
be a linear character of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.

\sum\limits_{x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}}\lambda(x^{2})=2\lambda(S)=\begin{cases}q-1&\text{if $S\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\lambda)$}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

∎

The norm map
\mathrm{Nm}:\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\to\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
is a surjective homomorphsim satisfying
N(z)=z^{q+1}
, for all
z\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}
. The kernel
E
of this map is the unique subgroup of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}
, of order
q+1
. Given
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, the set
E_{x}:=\mathrm{Nm}^{-1}(x)
is a coset of
E
. For any
z\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, we note that
z^{q+1}=z^{2}
. Therefore,
E_{x}\cap\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
x
is a square in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. Moreover, we have

(5a)

\displaystyle|E_{x}\cap\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}|

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2&\text{if $x\in S$}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(5b)

\displaystyle E\cap\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}

\displaystyle=\{1,-1\}.

Therefore, we have

(6)

\{z\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\ :\ z^{q+1}\in\{1\}\cup N\}=\left(\bigcup\limits_{x\in N}E_{x}\right)\cup(E\setminus\{1,-1\}).

We now prove one last technical result before going back to our main result.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\lambda
be a non-trivial linear character of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
and let
\mu
be linear character of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}
with
\mu\neq\mu^{q}
.
Then, we have

(1)

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\\
z^{q+1}\in\{1\}\cup N\end{subarray}}\lambda(z^{q+1})=(q-1)-(q+1)\lambda(S)

(2)

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\\
z^{q+1}\in\{1\}\cup N\end{subarray}}\mu(z)=-(1+\mu(-1))

Proof.

Given
x\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, we recall that
E_{x}=\{z\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\ :\ z^{q+1}=x\}
is a coset of
E
, and thus a set of size
q+1
. Let
\lambda
be a non-trivial linear character of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.
Using (
5
) and (
6
), we have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\\
z^{q+1}\in\{1\}\cup N\end{subarray}}\lambda(z^{q+1})

\displaystyle=|E\setminus\{1,-1\}|\lambda(1)+\sum\limits_{x\in N}|E_{x}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}|\lambda(x)

\displaystyle=(q-1)\lambda(1)+(q+1)\lambda(N).

Since
\lambda
is non-trivial, by orthogonality of characters, we have
0=\lambda(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})=\lambda(N)+\lambda(S)
, and thus
\lambda(N)=-\lambda(S)
. This proves the first equality.

Let
\mu
be a non-trivial linear character of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}
, with
\mu\neq\mu^{q}
. Since
E
is a subgroup of index
q-1
in the cyclic group
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}
, we see that
E:=\{z^{q-1}\ :z\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\}
. Since
\mu\neq\mu^{q}
, there exists some

with
\mu(z^{q-1})=\mu^{q-1}(z)\neq 1
. Therefore,
\mu|_{E}
is a non-trivial character and thus
\mu(E)=0
. For every

,

is a coset of
E
, and thus
\mu(E_{x})=0
. Using (

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\\
z^{q+1}\in\{1\}\cup N\end{subarray}}\mu(z)

\displaystyle=\mu(E)-\mu(\{1,-1\})+\sum\limits_{x\in N}\mu(E_{x})

\displaystyle=-(1+\mu(-1)).

∎

. We begin with the eigenvalues indexed by linear characters of

.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
\lambda

\theta_{\lambda}=\begin{cases}q(q+1)\lambda(-1)+q\dfrac{(q-1)^{2}}{2}&\text{if $S\not\subseteq\mathrm{Ker}(\lambda)$,}\\

q(q+1)+(q^{2}-1)(q-1)+q\dfrac{(q-1)^{2}}{2}+q\dfrac{(q+1)(q-1)^{2}}{4}&\text{if $\lambda$ is trivial.}\end{cases}

Moreover,
\theta_{\lambda}\equiv 2\pmod{4}
and
\theta_{\lambda}\in\Phi^{+}
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda
be a complex linear character of
G
. Recalling our notation from §
3.1
, we can also treat
\lambda
as a complex linear character of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
. We recall that there are exactly
q(q+1)

; exactly

; and exactly
q(q-1)
elements conjugate to each

. We also recall that
c_{2}(x)=c_{2}(y)
if and only if

; and that
c_{4}(z)=c_{4}(w)
if and only if
\{z,\overline{z}\}=\{w,\overline{w}\}

We will show that

From (

Therefore, to show that
\Gamma_{q}
admits PST, we are reduced to proving the following proposition.

Proposition 5.2
.

Every eigenvalue of

is an integer and is divisible by

.

As

is in the Bose–Mesner algebra

.
To compute the eigenvalues of

5.1.
Character sums over cosets of

.

We recall that
B

G
.
Let

\theta\in\widehat{T}
, let
I[\theta]:=\mathrm{Ind}_{H}^{G}(\theta)
. We will now compute
I[\theta](gH)
.

We start by fixing a matrix representation of
G
that affords
I[\theta]
as its character.

Define

\omega_{\beta}:=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
\beta&1\end{bmatrix}
for
\beta\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}
and
\omega_{\infty}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix}
. Let
{\mathbb{P}}={\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}\cup\{\infty\}
be the projective line over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}

The set

\Omega:=\left\{\omega_{\beta}\ :\ \beta\in{\mathbb{P}}\right\}

is a complete set of left coset representatives for
B
in
G
.

Given
g\in G
and
\beta\in{\mathbb{P}}
, the elements

\sigma_{g}(\beta)\in{\mathbb{P}}
and

(11)

g\omega_{\beta}=\omega_{\sigma_{g}(\beta)}b(g,\beta).

The map
g\to\sigma_{g}
defines a transitive action of
G
on
{\mathbb{P}}
, with
B
as the stabilizer of
0
.

Now given
\theta\in\widehat{T}
, let
P_{\theta}(g)
to be the
|{\mathbb{P}}|\times|{\mathbb{P}}|

P_{\theta}(g)_{\beta,\alpha}=\delta_{\sigma_{g}(\alpha),\beta}\theta(b(g,\alpha)),

for all
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{P}}
. The map
P:G\to\mathrm{GL}(|{\mathbb{P}}|,\mathbb{C})
is a representation affording
I[\theta]
as its character.
We observe that

I[\theta](gH)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(P_{\theta}(g)P_{\theta}(H)\right).

We first start computing
M_{\theta}:=P_{\theta}(H)
. Given
\alpha
,
\beta\in{\mathbb{P}}
, define
H_{\alpha\to\beta}:=\{h\in H\ :\ \sigma_{h}(\alpha)=\beta\}
. Using the definition of

, we see that

(12)

M_{\theta}(\beta,\alpha)=\sum\limits_{h\in H_{\alpha\to\beta}}\theta(b(h,{\alpha})).

Thus, if
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{P}}
are not in the same
H

M_{\theta}(\beta,\alpha)=0
. We consider action of
H
on
{\mathbb{P}}
. Note that

\bigcup\limits_{\beta\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\omega_{\beta}B\cup\omega_{\infty}B\subset HB.

As
|HB|=\dfrac{|H||B|}{|\mathrm{B}(q)|}
, (where

\bigcup\limits_{\beta\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}}\omega_{\beta}B\cup\omega_{\infty}B=HB,

and thus the

is

{\mathbb{P}}_{1}:={\mathbb{F}}_{q}\cup\{\infty\}
. Given
\alpha
,
\beta\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, there is a unique

\beta=c_{\alpha,\beta}+d_{\alpha,\beta}\alpha
.
Let
h_{\alpha,\beta}:=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\

. Then we have

(13)

h_{\alpha,\beta}\omega_{\alpha}=\omega_{\beta}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&d_{\alpha,\beta}\end{bmatrix},

\sigma_{h_{\alpha,\beta}}(\alpha)=\beta

0&d_{\alpha,\beta}\end{bmatrix}

is transitive on
{\mathbb{P}}_{2}:={\mathbb{P}}\setminus{\mathbb{P}}_{1}
. We now derive formulae for entries of
M_{\theta}
. We define

.

Lemma 5.3
.

Given

The equality in the last case follows from (
12

\alpha,\beta

H

12

\displaystyle M_{\theta}(\beta,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{h\in H_{{\alpha}\to{\beta}}}\theta(b(h,{\alpha})).

Now consider the set
S:=\{b(h,{\alpha})\ :\ h\in H_{{\alpha}\to\beta}\}
. Given
x,y\in H_{\alpha\to\beta}
, we have
b(x,\alpha)={\omega_{\beta}}^{-1}x\omega_{\alpha}
and
b(y,\alpha)={\omega_{\beta}}^{-1}y\omega_{\alpha}

b(x,\alpha)^{-1}b(y,\alpha)={\omega_{\alpha}}^{-1}x^{-1}y\omega_{\alpha}\in(H_{\alpha\to\alpha})^{\omega_{\alpha}}.

Fix an element

14

S\subset b(z,\alpha)(H_{\alpha\to\alpha})^{\omega_{\alpha}}.

Again from (
14
), we have
b(x,\alpha)=b(y,\alpha)
if and only if
x=y
. Thus
|S|=|H_{{\alpha\to\alpha}}|
, and thus

S=b(z,\alpha)(H_{\alpha\to\alpha})^{\omega_{\alpha}}.

This shows that

\displaystyle M_{\theta}(\beta,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\theta(S)

(15)

\displaystyle=\theta(b(z,\alpha))\theta((H_{\alpha\to\alpha})^{\omega_{\alpha}})

Case 1: Assume that
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\cup\{\infty\}
. In this case,
\omega_{\alpha}
and
\omega_{\beta}
lie in
H
. Thus, for any

b(h,\alpha)={\omega_{\beta}}^{-1}h\omega_{\alpha}\in H
, and therefore
S\subset H
. We also have
(H_{\alpha\to\alpha})^{\omega_{\alpha}}=b(z,\alpha)^{-1}S\in H
. Since
(H_{\alpha\to\alpha})^{\omega_{\alpha}}\subset B

(H_{\alpha\to\alpha})^{\omega_{\alpha}}\subset B\cap H=\mathrm{B}(q)
. As

is the stabilizer of
\alpha

. This proves the first equality in the lemma.

Case 2: Assume that
\alpha,\beta\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}

in (
15

M_{\theta}(\beta,\alpha)=\theta\left(\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&d_{\alpha,\beta}\end{bmatrix}\right)\theta((H_{\alpha\to\alpha})^{\omega_{\alpha}})

Let

\alpha^{2}=s+t\alpha
. We note that
B
is the set of elements fixing the projective point

. Therefore, given

As
\{1,\alpha\}
is a basis for
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}

if and only if (i)
c=bs
, (ii)

, and (iii)
(a,b)\neq(0,0)

is an upper triangular matrix, and (iii) ensures that

(H_{\alpha\to\alpha})^{\omega_{\alpha}}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}a+b\alpha&b\\
0&(a+bt)-b\alpha\end{bmatrix}\ :\ a,b\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\ \&\ (a,b)\neq(0,0)\right\}.

We have

\theta\left((H_{\alpha\to\alpha})^{\omega_{\alpha}}\right)=\theta\left(\left\{(a+b\alpha,(a+bt)-b\alpha)\ :\ a,b\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\ \&\ (a,b)\neq(0,0)\right\}\right)

(a+b\alpha,(a+bt)-b\alpha)
forms the solution set of a degree

irreducible polynomial over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. Since roots of degree
2
irreducible polynomials are conjugate pairs, we can now conclude that

\left\{(a+b\alpha,(a+bt)-b\alpha)\ :\ a,b\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\ \&\ (a,b)\neq(0,0)\right\}=\{(z,z^{q})\ :\ z\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\},

and thus that

\theta\left((H_{\alpha\to\alpha})^{\omega_{\alpha}}\right)=\theta(C).

This concludes the proof.
∎

Corollary 5.4

I[{\theta}](gH)=\sum\limits_{\{\beta\in{\mathbb{P}}_{1}\ :\ \sigma_{g}(\beta)\in{\mathbb{P}}_{1}\}}\theta(b(g,\beta))\theta(\mathrm{B}(q))+\sum\limits_{\{\beta\in{\mathbb{P}}_{2}\ :\ \sigma_{g}(\beta)\in{\mathbb{P}}_{2}\}}\theta(b(g,\beta))\theta\left(\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\

Given

(recall that

and

or

\theta

\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\widehat{{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}}

\begin{split}I[\theta](m_{x,y}H)={}&(\theta_{1}(x)\theta_{2}(y)+\theta_{1}(y)\theta_{2}(x))\theta(\mathrm{B}(q))\\
{}&+\theta_{1}(x)\theta_{2}(y)\theta(C)\left[\sum\limits_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\setminus\left({\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}\cup y^{-1}x{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}\right)}\theta_{2}(d_{x^{-1}y\alpha,\alpha})\right].\end{split}

We now simplify this sum.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
x,y\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}
with
x^{-1}y\notin{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}
and let
\chi\in\widehat{{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}}
, then

\sum\limits_{\alpha\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\setminus(\left({\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}\cup y^{-1}x{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}\right)}\chi(d_{x^{-1}y\alpha,\alpha})=(q-1)\chi({\mathbb{F}}_{q^{\times}}).

Proof.

We first recall some notation: given
\epsilon,\gamma\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
,
(c_{\epsilon,\gamma},d_{\epsilon,\gamma})\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}\times\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
is the unique pair satisfying

\gamma=c_{\epsilon,\gamma}+d_{\epsilon,\gamma}\epsilon
. We can rewrite this equation as

(17)

\epsilon=-c_{\epsilon,\gamma}d_{\epsilon,\gamma}^{-1}+d_{\epsilon,\gamma}^{-1}\gamma,

and thus,

(18)

c_{\gamma,\epsilon}=-c_{\epsilon,\gamma}d_{\epsilon,\gamma}^{-1}\quad\text{and}\quad d_{\gamma,\epsilon}=d_{\epsilon,\gamma}^{-1}.

Set
\beta:=x^{-1}y
,
r:=c_{\beta,\beta^{2}}
, and
s:=d_{\beta,\beta^{2}}
. By design
\beta\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\setminus{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}
. Consider
\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\setminus\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\cup\beta^{-1}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\right)
. By (
17
), we have

(19)

\beta=-c_{\beta,\alpha}d_{\beta,\alpha}^{-1}+d_{\beta,\alpha}^{-1}\alpha.

Starting from
\alpha=c_{\beta,\alpha}+d_{\beta,\alpha}\beta
, we obtain

\displaystyle\beta\alpha

\displaystyle=c_{\beta,\alpha}\beta+d_{\beta,\alpha}\beta^{2}

\displaystyle=d_{\beta,\alpha}r+(c_{\beta,\alpha}+sd_{\beta,\alpha})\beta\qquad\text{(since $\beta^{2}=r+s\beta$)}

\displaystyle=(d_{\beta,\alpha}r-c^{2}_{\beta,\alpha}d_{\beta,\alpha}^{-1}+c_{\beta,\alpha}s)+(c_{\beta,\alpha}d_{\beta,\alpha}^{-1}+s)\alpha,

with the last step following from (
19

d_{\alpha,\beta\alpha}=(c_{\beta,\alpha}d_{\beta,\alpha}^{-1}+s)
. Since
\alpha\notin\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\cup\beta^{-1}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, we have
\beta\alpha,\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, and thus
d_{\alpha,\beta\alpha}\neq 0

18

d_{\beta\alpha,\alpha}=d_{\alpha,\beta\alpha}^{-1}=(c_{\beta,\alpha}d_{\beta,\alpha}^{-1}+s)^{-1}.

We claim that the function
\alpha\mapsto d_{\beta\alpha,\alpha}
from
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\setminus\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\cup\beta^{-1}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\right)

\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
, is (i) constant on left cosets (in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}

\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
that sit in the domain and (ii) takes distinct values on distinct
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
-left cosets in the domain. As the domain
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\setminus\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\cup\beta^{-1}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\right)
partitions into
(q-1)
distinct
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
-cosets in
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}
, the result follows from the claim.

Given

, we observe that
tc_{\beta,\alpha}=c_{\beta,t\alpha}
and
td_{\beta,\alpha}=d_{\beta,t\alpha}
. Thus, by (

\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\setminus\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\cup\beta^{-1}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\right)

d_{\beta\alpha,\alpha}=d_{\beta t\alpha,t\alpha}

\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\setminus\left(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\cup\beta^{-1}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}\right)
with
d_{\beta\alpha_{1},\alpha_{1}}=d_{\beta\alpha_{2},\alpha_{2}}
. Then, using (

), we have

c_{\beta,\alpha_{1}}d_{\beta,\alpha_{1}}^{-1}=c_{\beta,\alpha_{2}}d_{\beta,\alpha_{2}}^{-1}

Setting

\alpha_{1}=c_{\beta,\alpha_{1}}+d_{\beta,\alpha_{1}}\beta=tc_{\beta,\alpha_{2}}+td_{\beta,\alpha_{2}}\beta=t\alpha_{2},

and thus,
\alpha_{1}\in\alpha_{2}\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
.
This proves the claim in the above paragraph, which concludes our proof.

5.2.
Eigenvalues of

.

We now describe all the eigenvalues of
D
. Let
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G\sslash H)
. Using (
3
) and the definition of
D
, we see that

\mathcal{E}_{\chi}:=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{\{(x,y)\in\Delta\times\Delta\ :\ x\neq y\}}\dfrac{\chi(m_{x,y}H)}{|H\cap H^{m_{x,y}}|}

is the complete set of eigenvalues of
D
. For technical reasons, we extend the definition of

to all complex characters of
G

Given a complex character
\chi
of
G

(21)

\mathcal{E}_{\chi}:=\dfrac{1}{2}\sum\limits_{\{(x,y)\in\Delta\times\Delta\ :\ x\neq y\}}\dfrac{\chi(m_{x,y}H)}{|H\cap H^{m_{x,y}}|}

For each

, and

contains exactly

elements conjugate to

\left\langle\pi[\mu]|_{H},{1}\right\rangle=0

\pi[\mu]

\mathrm{Irr}(G\sslash H)
.

Now, we figure out which parabolic characters are in
\mathrm{Irr}(G\sslash H)

B

\theta=[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]

\displaystyle\left\langle{1},I[\theta]|_{H}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Trace}(M_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{(q+1)\theta(\mathrm{B}(q))}{|H|}+\dfrac{(q^{2}-q)\theta(C)}{|H|}\ \text{using Lemma~\ref{lem:charsumH}}).

Therefore,
\left\langle{1},I[\theta]|_{H}\right\rangle\neq 0
if and only if either
\mathrm{B}(q)\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\theta)
or
C\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\theta)
. We observe that

(i)
\mathrm{B}(q)\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\theta)
if and only if

(equivalently
\theta_{1}^{q+1}={1}
) and
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\theta_{2})
(equivalently
\theta_{2}^{q+1}={1}
); and

(ii)

, if and only if
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}^{-q}
.

We now conclude that

\left\langle{1},I[\theta]|_{H}\right\rangle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\theta_{1}\neq\theta_{2}$ and $\theta_{1}^{q+1}={1}=\theta_{2}^{q+1}$};\\
1&\text{if $\theta_{1}\neq\theta_{2}$ and $\theta_{1}={1}=\theta_{2}^{-q}$};\\
2&\text{if $\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}$ and $\theta_{1}^{q+1}={1}=\theta_{2}^{q+1}$};\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Let
\theta_{\lambda}:=[\lambda,\lambda]
, for some
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}}
such that
\lambda^{q+1}=1
. We recall that
I[\theta_{\lambda}]=\lambda+S_{\lambda}
is the decomposition of
I[\theta_{\lambda}]
into irreducible summands. Thus, given
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}}
, we have
\lambda\in\mathrm{Irr}(G\sslash H)
(respectively
S_{\lambda}\in\mathrm{Irr}(G\sslash H)
) if and only if
\lambda^{q+1}=1
. Recalling that
\{\mathcal{E}_{\chi}\ :\ \chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G\sslash H)\}
is the complete set of eigenvalues of
D
, we have now proved the following result.

Lemma 5.7
.

Any eigenvalue of
D
is an element of

\{\mathcal{E}_{I[\theta]}\ :\ \mathrm{B}(q)\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\theta)\ \text{or}\ C\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\theta)\}\cup\{\mathcal{E}_{\lambda},\mathcal{E}_{S_{\lambda}}\ :\ \lambda\in\widehat{{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}}\ \&\ \lambda^{q+1}={1}\}.

We now compute
\mathcal{E}_{I[\theta]}
. We observe that
|H\cap H^{m_{x,y}}|=(q-1)^{2}
. Using this observation and Corollary
5.6
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{I[\theta]}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2(q-1)^{2}}\sum\limits_{\{(x,y)\in\Delta\times\Delta\ :\ x\neq y\}}(\theta_{1}(x)\theta_{2}(y)+\theta_{1}(y)\theta_{2}(x))\theta(\mathrm{B}(q))+(q-1)\theta_{1}(x)\theta_{2}(y)\theta(C)\theta_{2}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times})

(22)

\displaystyle=\left[\dfrac{\theta(\mathrm{B}(q))}{(q-1)^{2}}+\dfrac{\theta(C)\theta_{2}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times})}{2(q-1)}\right]\times\sum\limits_{\{(x,y)\in\Delta\times\Delta\ :\ x\neq y\}}\theta_{1}(x)\theta_{2}(y)

We are now ready to prove Proposition
5.2
. We do this in a series of lemmas.

Lemma 5.8
.

For all
\theta\in\widehat{B}
,
\mathcal{E}_{I[\theta]}
is an integer divisible by
4
.

Proof.

Let
\theta=[\theta_{1},\theta_{2}]

We recall that
\theta
is a linear character of
\mathrm{B}(q)
and
C
and since
\theta_{2}
is a linear character of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}
. Therefore,
\left[\dfrac{\theta(\mathrm{B}(q))}{(q-1)^{2}}+\dfrac{\theta(C)\theta_{2}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times})}{2(q-1)}\right]
is an element in

\{0,q,\dfrac{(q^{2}-1)}{2},q+\dfrac{(q^{2}-1)}{2}\}
, and thus an integer.

Case 1: First, we consider the case in which
\mathrm{Ker}(\theta)\supset\mathrm{B}(q)
, that is,
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}
is in the kernel of both
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
. We have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{\{(x,y)\in\Delta\times\Delta\ :\ x\neq y\}}\theta(m_{x,y})

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\{(x,y)\in\Delta\times\Delta\}}\theta(m_{x,y})-\sum\limits_{x\in\Delta}\theta(x,x)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{\{(x,y)\in\Delta\times\Delta\}}\theta_{1}(x)\theta_{2}(y)-\sum\limits_{x\in\Delta}\theta_{1}(x)\theta_{2}(x)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{(q-1)^{2}}\left(\sum\limits_{\{w\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\times{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\}}\theta(w)\right)-\dfrac{1}{q-1}\left(\sum\limits_{x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}}\theta_{1}(x)\theta_{2}(x)\right),

with the last equality following from the fact that
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}
is in the kernel of both
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
. As
\theta
is an irreducible representation of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\times{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}
, the first sum is either
0
or
(q+1)^{2}
. As
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
are irreducible characters of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}
, using orthogonality of characters, the second sum is either
q+1
or
0
. Thus, if
\mathrm{Ker}(\theta)\supset\mathrm{B}(q)
, then
\sum\limits_{\{(x,y)\in\Delta\times\Delta\ :\ x\neq y\}}\theta(m_{x,y})
is an integer divisible by
q+1
. Thus, in this case,
q+1\mid\mathcal{E}_{I[\theta]}
. Since
q\equiv 3\pmod{4}
, this implies that
4\mid\mathcal{E}_{I[\theta]}
.

Case 2 :Now, we consider the case
C\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\theta)
. This condition is satisfied if and only if
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}^{-q}
. Thus, we have
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}^{-q}
.

Subcase 1: If
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}\not\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\theta_{2})
, then
\mathrm{B}(q)\not\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\theta)
. Thus, in this case,
\left[\dfrac{\theta(\mathrm{B}(q))}{(q-1)^{2}}+\dfrac{\theta(C)\theta_{2}({\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times})}{2(q-1)}\right]=0
, and thus,
\mathcal{E}_{I[\theta]}=0
.

Subcase 2: If
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\theta_{2})
, since
\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}^{-q}
, we must have
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\theta_{1})
. Therefore, in this case,
\mathrm{B}(q)\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\theta)
. From Case 1, it follows that

4\mid\mathcal{E}_{I[\theta]}
.
∎

We now turn our attention to linear characters in
\mathrm{Irr}(G\sslash H)
.

Lemma 5.9
.

For all
\lambda\in\widehat{G}\cong\widehat{{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}}
with
\lambda^{q+1}={1}
,
\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}
is an integer divisible by
4
.

Proof.

Using (
21
) and
|H\cap H^{m_{x,y}}|=(q-1)^{2}
, we have

\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}=\dfrac{\lambda(H)}{2(q-1)^{2}}\left(\sum\limits_{\{(x,y)\in\Delta\times\Delta\ :\ x\neq y\}}\lambda(m_{x,y})\right).

Since
\lambda^{q+1}={1}
, we have
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\lambda)
, and therefore,
\lambda|H={1}
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{|H|}{2(q-1)^{2}}\left(\sum\limits_{\{(x,y)\in\Delta\times\Delta\ :\ x\neq y\}}\lambda(m_{x,y})\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{q(q+1)}{2}\left(\sum\limits_{\{(x,y)\in\Delta\times\Delta\ :\ x\neq y\}}\lambda(m_{x,y})\right).

Since
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\lambda)
, we have

\lambda(m_{x,y})=\lambda_{m_{tx,ty}}
, for all
t\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{q(q+1)}{2}\left(\sum\limits_{\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\times{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\ :\ x{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}\neq y{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{\times}\}}\lambda(m_{x,y})\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{q(q+1)}{2}\left(\sum\limits_{\{(x,y)\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\times{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\}}[\lambda,\lambda](x,y)-\sum\limits_{x\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}}\lambda(x^{2})\right)

Let
S
denote the subgroup of squares in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}
, then we have

\mathcal{E}_{\lambda}=\dfrac{q(q+1)}{2}\left([\lambda,\lambda]\left({\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\times{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\right)-2\lambda(S)\right).

Since
[\lambda,\lambda]
is an irreducible character of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\times{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}
, and since
\lambda
restricts to an irreducible character of
S
, we have
2\lambda(S)\in\{0,q^{2}-1\}
and
[\lambda,\lambda]\left({\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\times{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}\right)\in\{0,(q^{2}-1)^{2}\}
. The result now follows.
∎

Lemma 5.10
.

For all
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathbb{F}}_{q^{2}}^{\times}}
with
\lambda^{q+1}={1}
,
\mathcal{E}_{S_{\lambda}}
is an integer divisible by
4
.

Proof.

Since
I[\lambda,\lambda]=\lambda+S_{\lambda}
, using (
21
), we see that

\mathcal{E}_{S_{\lambda}}=\mathcal{E}_{I[\lambda,\lambda]}-\mathcal{E}_{{\lambda}}.

Using the previous two results, we can conclude that the RHS of the above is
0\pmod{4}
, and thus the result follows.
∎

Proposition
5.2
now follows using Lemma
5.7
.
