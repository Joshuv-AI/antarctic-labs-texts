Buchsbaum-Eisenbud complexes of OI-modules Thanks: The second author was partially supported by Simons Foundation grant #636513.
Nathan Fieldsteel, Uwe Nagel

Abstract
. We extend the theory of Koszul and Buchsbaum-Eisenbud complexes to modules over commutative OI-algebras and show that they still have the familiar properties of the classical complexes. In particular, the OI-complexes are generically acyclic and often provide width-wise minimal free resolutions.

1.
Introduction

The desire to study spaces of different dimensions simultaneously has motivated many concepts and results. For example, the theory of
\FI
-modules (over a fixed ring) allows one to express and establish results in representation stability, where
\FI
is the category of finite sets with injections as morphisms. This theory has led to many further exciting developments (see, e.g.,
[
4
,
5
,
14
]
). In order to incorporate the study of Markov bases in algebraic statistics (see, e.g.,
[
9
]
),

\FI
-modules over an
\FI
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
were introduced in
[
11
]
. Establishing finiteness results required even further
generality by using the category
\OI
of ordered finite sets with order-preserving injections as morphisms. Gröbner bases for
\OI
-modules over a polynomial
\OI
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
were introduced in
[
11
]
. A theory of Hilbert functions of graded
\OI
-modules has been initiated in
[
10
,
12
]
(see also
[
13
]
). Over a noetherian polynomial
\OI
-algebra, the existence of resolutions by
finitely generated free
\mathbf{A}
-modules was established in
[
11
]
. However, very few examples of such resolutions or general constructions are known.
This is not too surprising because even in the classical setting of commutative algebra rather few widely applicable methods exist. Arguably, the most famous complex in commutative algebra is the Koszul complex associated to an
R
-module homomorphism
F\to R
. More generally, there is a family of complexes associated to an
R
-module homomorphism
F\to G
where both
F
and

G
are finitely generated free
R
-modules. We call these complexes Buchsbaum-Eisenbud complexes because of their appearance in
[
3
]
. They include the Eagon-Northcott complex
[
6
]
.

In this paper, we extend both constructions to the category of
\OI
-modules.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
\mathbf{A}
is a commutative
\OI
-algebra and
\varphi\colon\mathbf{F}\rightarrow\mathbf{A}
is a morphism from a finitely generated free
\mathbf{A}
-module to
\mathbf{A}
, then there exists a complex
K_{\bullet}(\varphi)
of finitely generated free
\mathbf{A}
-modules for which the module in homological degree
d
is the
d^{\text{th}}
exterior power of
\mathbf{F}
, and in each width
w
the complex
K_{\bullet}(\varphi)(w)
is the ordinary Koszul complex on
\varphi(w)\colon\mathbf{F}(w)\rightarrow\mathbf{A}(w)
. Furthermore, the complex
K_{\bullet}(\varphi)
is generically acyclic.

We use the term “generically acyclic” to indicate that there are circumstances in which
K_{\bullet}(\varphi)
is acyclic.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a commutative
\OI
-algebra and let
\varphi:\mathbf{F}\rightarrow\mathbf{G}
be a morphism of finitely-generated free
\mathbf{A}
-modules where
\mathbf{G}
is generated in width
0
. For each
i\geq 0
, there exists a complex
\BE^{i}_{\bullet}(\varphi)
of finitely generated free
\mathbf{A}
-modules
that in each width
w
is a Buchsbaum-Eisenbud complex on
\varphi(w)
. Furthermore,
\BE^{i}_{\bullet}(\varphi)
is generically acyclic.

Both of these constructions produce complexes of graded modules if
\varphi
is a morphism of graded
\mathbf{A}
-modules.

Minimality of graded free resolutions over a polynomial
\OI
-algebra was investigated in
[
8
]
. In particular, it was shown
that, if it exists, a minimal graded free resolution of an
\mathbf{A}
module is unique up to isomorphism and that, if
\mathbf{A}
is noetherian, any
\mathbf{A}
-module has such a minimal resolution.

Note that any free resolution
\mathbf{F}_{\bullet}
of an
\mathbf{A}
-module
\mathbf{M}
provides in each width
w
, a free resolution of
\mathbf{M}(w)
as an
\mathbf{A}(w)
-module, namely its width
w
component
\mathbf{F}_{\bullet}(w)
. If
\mathbf{F}_{\bullet}(w)
is a minimal free resolution for each
w
, the resolution
\mathbf{F}_{\bullet}
is called
width-wise minimal
. Such a resolution is necessarily minimal, but not every minimal resolution is width-wise minimal (see
[
8
]
). Using cellular resolutions, width-wise minimal free resolutions of some monomial ideals were constructed in
[
8
]
. The complexes above provide many more such examples. If
\varphi
is graded and
\BE^{i}_{\bullet}(\varphi)
is acyclic, it gives a width-wise minimal free resolution. It resolves the
i^{\text{th}}
symmetric power of
\coker\varphi
if
i\geq 1
.

Example 1.3
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a polynomial
\OI
-algebra with three width
1
variables, so that in width
w
we have
\mathbf{A}(w)=k[x_{i,j}\mid 1\leq i\leq 3,~1\leq j\leq w]
. Let
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{A}^{3}
be free
\mathbf{A}
-modules, where
\mathbf{F}
has rank
1
and is generated in width
1
, and consider the morphism
\varphi\colon\mathbf{F}\rightarrow\mathbf{G}
determined by sending the generator of
\mathbf{F}
to

\begin{bmatrix}x_{1,1}\\
x_{2,1}\\
x_{3,1}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbf{G}(1)
.
Thus, in each width
w
, the map
\varphi(w)
is represented by a generic
3\times w
matrix containing the variables of
\mathbf{A}(w)
. If we let
\mathbf{I}
denote the ideal in
\mathbf{A}
generated in width
3
by the determinant of
\varphi(3)
, then
\mathbf{I}(w)
is generated by the maximal minors of
\varphi(w)
for all
w
. In this situation,
\BE^{0}_{\bullet}(\varphi)
gives a width-wise minimal graded resolution of
\mathbf{I}
by free
\mathbf{A}
-modules. Any width
w\geq 3
component
\BE^{0}_{\bullet}(\varphi)(w)
is the classical Eagon-Northcott complex to
\varphi(w)
.

Our constructions of the above complexes mirror the classical constructions, interpreted suitably. They use multilinear algebra and duals of modules. Analogous complexes can be constructed in the category of
\FI
-modules. However, we are not aware of any (non-trivial) instance where these complexes provide width-wise minimal free resolutions. Thus, we focus on
\OI
-modules in this
paper.

In
Section

2
, we recall basic definitions, in particular, of polynomial
\OI
-algebras and free modules. We also discuss basic properties of tensor products and symmetric and exterior powers of modules over any commutative
\OI
-algebra. Furthermore, we introduce an internal hom functor that has the adjunction property (see
2.6
).
In
Section

3
, we review the classical complexes. In particular, we write the classical Buchsbaum-Eisenbud
complexes in a form so that they become amenable to extensions for
\mathbf{A}
-modules. To this end, we describe the differentials very explicitly.
We conclude the preparations in
Section

4
, where we show that tensor products and exterior and symmetric powers of finitely generated free
\mathbf{A}
-modules are free and finitely generated. Note that these statements witness identities
involving binomial coefficients (see, e.g,
Example

4.3
). We also characterize when a finitely generated free
\mathbf{A}
-module is isomorphic to its dual. Finally, our extensions of Koszul and Buchsbaum-Eisenbud complexes for
\OI
-modules are presented in Sections
5
and
6
. We illustrate them with several examples.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\OI
be the category whose objects are totally-ordered finite sets and whose maps are order-preserving injective functions. An
\OI
-module
(over
k
) is a functor from
\OI
to the category of
k
-modules.

The skeleton of
\OI
is the subcategory of
\OI
whose unique object of cardinality
w\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is
\{1,2,\ldots,w\}
. By abuse of notation we use
w
to refer to this object. We use the term
width
to refer to this parameter. So for an

\OI
-module
\mathbf{M}
, we call
\textbf{M}(w)
the
width

w

component of

M
. By an

element
of
\mathbf{M}
we mean an element
m
in some
\textbf{M}(w)
, which we call a
width

w

element of

M
.
\OI
-modules form a functor category, where the morphisms between two
\OI
modules
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{N}
are the natural transformations
\textbf{M}\to\textbf{N}
. The set of these is denoted
\operatorname{Nat}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N})
.

A
submodule
of an
\OI
-module
\mathbf{M}
is just a subfunctor of
\mathbf{M}
that is an
\OI
-module itself. Given a set
G
of elements of
\mathbf{M}
, the
submodule generated by

G
is the smallest submodule that contains
G
.

We are interested in a more general class of objects where, instead of looking at a functor from
\OI
to
k
-mod, we consider families of modules over different rings where both the modules and the rings are parametrized by
\OI
.

Definition 2.2
.

An
\OI
-algebra
is a covariant functor from
\OI
to the category of
k
-algebras. An
\OI
-algebra is called
commutative
,
graded
or
graded-commutative
if it is a functor to the category of
k
-algebras with the corresponding property. In this paper we will work with commutative
\OI
-algebras unless otherwise stated, though we will typically drop the adjective.

For an
\OI
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
, an
\mathbf{A}
-module is an
\OI
-module
\mathbf{M}
together with an action
\mathbf{A}\otimes\mathbf{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mu}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbf{M}
that satisfies the usual commuting diagrams required of an action map (see
[
11
, Definition 3.1]
).

Equivalently, an
\OI
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
is a commutative monoid in the category of
\OI
-modules over
k
, and an
\mathbf{A}
-module is a module in the category-theoretic sense. We will not make use of this perspective in this paper.

Note that the constant functor from
\OI
with image
k
defines not only an
\OI
-module, but also an
\OI
-algebra. Modules over this
\OI
-algebra are exactly the ordinary
\OI
-modules defined above. So the theory of modules over an
\OI
-algebra is a generalization of the theory of
\OI
-modules. From now on we will only consider modules over an
\OI
-algebra.

Example 2.3
.

Consider the functor
\mathbf{A}\colon\OI\rightarrow k\text{-alg}
for which, in any width
w
,
\mathbf{A}(w)
is the coordinate ring of the space of
3\times w
matrices over
k
, that is,

\mathbf{A}(w)=k\begin{bmatrix}x_{11}&x_{12}&&x_{1w}\\
x_{21}&x_{22}&\cdots&x_{2w}\\
x_{31}&x_{32}&&x_{3w}\end{bmatrix},

and where any
\OI
-morphism
\varepsilon\colon u\rightarrow v
induces an algebra map
\varepsilon_{*}=\mathbf{A}(\varepsilon)\colon\mathbf{A}(u)\rightarrow\mathbf{A}(v)
defined by
x_{ij}\mapsto x_{i\varepsilon(j)}
. In the language of
[
11
]
, this is the polynomial
\OI
-algebra
\left(\mathbf{X}^{\OI,1}\right)^{\otimes 3}

Define a functor
\mathbf{I}\colon\OI\rightarrow k
-mod so that
\mathbf{I}(w)\subseteq\mathbf{A}(w)
is the ideal generated by the
3\times 3
minors of the above
3\times w
matrix. The maps
\mathbf{A}(u)\rightarrow\mathbf{A}(v)
induce maps
\mathbf{I}(u)\rightarrow\mathbf{I}(v)
, and with these maps
\mathbf{I}
is an
\mathbf{A}
-module. As it is a submodule of
\mathbf{A}
, we call
\mathbf{I}
an ideal.
It is generated as an
\mathbf{A}
-module by a single element in width
3
, namely the determinant of the generic
3\times 3
matrix, as any
3\times 3
minor in any
\mathbf{I}(w)
with
w\geq 3
is the image of this determinant under an appropriately chosen
\OI
-morphism.

The most important
\mathbf{A}
-modules for our purposes are the free
\mathbf{A}
-modules, which we define now.

Definition 2.4
.

For any integer
n\geq 0
, define an
\mathbf{A}
-module
\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{A}}^{\OI,n}
as follows. For any
w\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, its width
w
component
\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{A}}^{\OI,n}(w)
is the free
\mathbf{A}(w)
-module with basis

\left\{e_{\nu}\mid\nu\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\OI}(n,w)\right\}
, and any
\OI
-morphism
\varepsilon\colon w\rightarrow w^{\prime}
acts on an element of
\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{A}}^{\OI,n}(w)
by

\sum\limits_{\nu\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\OI}(n,w)}a_{\nu}e_{\nu}\mapsto\sum\limits_{\nu\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\OI}(n,w)}\varepsilon_{*}(a_{\nu})e_{\varepsilon\circ\nu}\in{\mathbf{F}}^{\OI,n}_{\mathbf{A}}(w^{\prime}).

\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{A}}^{\OI,n}
is generated as an
\mathbf{A}
-module by the element
e_{\text{id}}
in width
n
.

A finitely generated
free
\mathbf{A}
-module of rank
r
is an
\mathbf{A}
-module that is isomorphic to a direct sum of the form

\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{A}}^{\OI,n_{1}}\oplus\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{A}}^{\OI,n_{2}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbf{F}_{\mathbf{A}}^{\OI,n_{r}}.

If
\mathbf{A}
is clear from context, we often simply write
{\mathbf{F}}^{\OI,w}
instead of
{\mathbf{F}}^{\OI,w}_{\mathbf{A}}
.
By
[
8
, Lemma 3.6]
, if
\mathbf{F}
is a finitely generated free
\mathbf{A}
-module, the number of elements in any basis and their widths are unique. In particular, the number of basis elements is equal to the rank of
\mathbf{F}
.

A
morphism of
\mathbf{A}
-modules
is a natural transformation
\varphi\colon\mathbf{M}\rightarrow\mathbf{N}
such that every map
\varphi(w)\colon\mathbf{M}(w)\rightarrow\mathbf{N}(w)
is a homomorphism of
\mathbf{A}(w)
-modules.
Note that any morphism out of a free
\mathbf{A}
-module
\mathbf{F}
is uniquely determined by the images of the generators of
\mathbf{F}
. This justifies the name “free.”

Example 2.5
.

The map
\varphi
in

\mathbf{A}

\mathbf{A}

\OI

For any
\OI
-algebra
\mathbf{A}
, the category of
\mathbf{A}
-modules has a pointwise direct sum and a pointwise tensor product defined by

\left(\mathbf{M}\oplus\mathbf{N}\right)(w)=\mathbf{M}(w)\oplus\mathbf{N}(w)
and

\left(\mathbf{M}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{N}\right)(w)=\mathbf{M}(w)\otimes_{\mathbf{A}(w)}\mathbf{N}(w)

One checks that this width-wise definition of the tensor product agrees with the categorical definition of the tensor product as the coequalizer of the diagram

Later, we want to consider the dual of an
\mathbf{A}
-module. This requires some care and preparation.
We use
\operatorname{Nat}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N})
to denote the set of
\mathbf{A}
-module morphisms
\mathbf{M}\to\mathbf{N}
. We note that while this set is a
k
-module, it does not have an
\mathbf{A}
-module structure or even an
\OI
-module structure. So it is not the internal
\operatorname{Hom}
in the category of
\mathbf{A}
-modules. To define the latter, we use a different approach.

By the adjoint functor theorem, the functor
{-}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{N}
has a right adjoint, which we will denote by
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{N},{-})
, a functor from
\mathbf{A}
-mod to
\mathbf{A}
-mod. The enriched Yoneda lemma implies that in every width
w
we have

\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{N},{-})(w)=\operatorname{Nat}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{F}^{\OI,w}_{\mathbf{A}}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{N},{-}).

This suggests the following definition.

Definition 2.6
.

For
\mathbf{A}
-modules
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{N}
, define a functor
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N})\colon\OI\to k
-mod:
For any
w\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, set

\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N})(w)=\operatorname{Nat}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{F}^{\OI,w}_{\mathbf{A}}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N}),

and, for any
\OI
-morphism
\varepsilon\colon w\rightarrow w^{\prime}
and any
\mathbf{A}
-module morphism
\varphi\colon\mathbf{F}^{\OI,w}_{\mathbf{A}}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{M}\rightarrow\mathbf{N}
, define a
k
-module homomorphism

\varepsilon_{*}\colon\operatorname{Nat}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{F}^{\OI,w}_{\mathbf{A}}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N})\to\operatorname{Nat}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{F}^{\OI,w^{\prime}}_{\mathbf{A}}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N})
by putting

\big(\varepsilon_{*}(\varphi)(v)\big)\left(e_{\eta}\otimes m\right)=\varphi(e_{\eta\circ\varepsilon}\otimes m)\in\mathbf{N}(v),

where
v\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
\eta\in\Hom_{\OI}(w^{\prime},v)
and
m\in\mathbf{M}(v)
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Consider
\mathbf{A}
-modules
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{N}
. For any integer
w\geq 0
, any
a\in\mathbf{A}(w)
and any
\mathbf{A}
-module morphism
\varphi\colon{\mathbf{F}}^{\OI,w}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{M}\to\mathbf{N}
, define an
\mathbf{A}
-module morphism
a\varphi=a\cdot\varphi\colon{\mathbf{F}}^{\OI,w}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{M}\to\mathbf{N}
by mapping
e_{\mu}\otimes m\in{\mathbf{F}}^{\OI,w}(v)\otimes_{\mathbf{A}(v)}\mathbf{M}(v)
with
v\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
(and so
\mu\in\Hom_{\OI}(w,v)
)
onto

\varphi\big(\mu_{*}(a)e_{\mu}\otimes m\big)\in\mathbf{N}(v)
. This gives
\operatorname{Nat}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{F}^{\OI,w}_{\mathbf{A}}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N})
the structure of an
\mathbf{A}(w)
-module and endows
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N})
with the structure of an
\mathbf{A}
-module.

Proof.

It is routine to check that the width-wise actions described above make
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N})(w)
an
\mathbf{A}(w)
-module. To verify that
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N})
is an
\mathbf{A}
-module, we also need to show that for any
\OI
-morphism
\eta\colon v\rightarrow v^{\prime}
, the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{A}(v)\otimes\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N})(v)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N})(v)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathbf{A}(v^{\prime})\otimes\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N})(v^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N})(v^{\prime})}

determined by
\eta
commutes. This means, for any
\mathbf{A}
-module morphism
\varphi\colon{\mathbf{F}}^{\OI,v}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{M}\to\mathbf{N}
and any
m\in\mathbf{A}(v)
, one has
\eta_{*}(a\cdot\varphi)=\eta_{*}(a)\cdot\eta_{*}(\varphi)
. To check this is in any width
w
, consider any
\mu\in\Hom_{\OI}(v^{\prime},w)
and any
m\in\mathbf{M}(w)
. One computes

\displaystyle\eta_{*}(a\cdot\varphi)(e_{\mu}\otimes m)

\displaystyle=\big(a\cdot\varphi\big)(e_{\mu\circ\eta}\otimes m)

\displaystyle=\varphi\big((\mu\circ\eta)_{*}(a)\cdot e_{\mu\circ\eta}\otimes m\big)

\displaystyle=\varphi\big(\mu_{*}(\eta_{*}(a))\cdot e_{\mu\circ\eta}\otimes m\big)

\displaystyle=\eta_{*}(\varphi)\big(\mu_{*}(\eta_{*}(a))\cdot e_{\mu}\otimes m\big)

\displaystyle=\big(\eta_{*}(a)\cdot\eta_{*}(\varphi)\big)(e_{\mu}\otimes m),

as desired.
∎

We always consider the
\mathbf{A}
-module structure on
\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{N})
as defined above and refer to it as the
internal hom
in the category of
\mathbf{A}
-modules. By its definition, it has the adjunction property

\operatorname{Nat}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{M}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{N},\mathbf{P})=\operatorname{Nat}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{M},\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{M},\mathbf{P})).

We also need analogs of multilinear constructions in the category of
\mathbf{A}
-modules.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a commutative OI-algebra over
k
, and let
\mathbf{M}
be an
\mathbf{A}
-module. Define the
tensor algebra
of
\mathbf{M}
over
\mathbf{A}
as

\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{M})=\mathbf{A}\oplus(\mathbf{M})\oplus(\mathbf{M}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{M})\oplus(\mathbf{M}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{M}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{M})\oplus\cdots.

Note that in any width,
\big(\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{M})\big)(w)
is simply the tensor algebra of
\mathbf{M}(w)
as an
\mathbf{A}(w)
-module. This means that
\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{M})
is itself an
\mathbf{A}
-module, though it also comes equipped with an
\mathbf{A}
-linear multiplication map
\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{M})\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{M})\longrightarrow\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{M})
determined by the width-wise algebra structures.

Definition 2.9
.

For any
\mathbf{A}
-module
\mathbf{M}
, consider the two-sided ideal of the tensor algebra
\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{M})
,

\mathbf{I}=\left\langle m\otimes n-n\otimes m\mid n,m~\text{in}~\mathbf{M}(w)~\text{for some width}~w\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\right\rangle.

The quotient of
\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{M})
by
\mathbf{I}
is called the
symmetric algebra
of
\mathbf{M}
, denoted
\operatorname{Sym}_{\bullet}(\mathbf{M})
.

The symmetric algebra of
\mathbf{M}
is graded, and we denote the components of its decomposition using the usual notation

\operatorname{Sym}_{\bullet}(\mathbf{M})=\bigoplus\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Sym}_{i}(\mathbf{M}),

where each
\operatorname{Sym}_{i}(\mathbf{M})
is an
\mathbf{A}
-module.

Definition 2.10
.

For any
\mathbf{A}
-module
\mathbf{M}
, consider the two-sided ideal of the tensor algebra
\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{M})
,

\mathbf{J}=\left\langle m\otimes m\mid m~\text{in}~\mathbf{M}(w)~\text{for some width}~w\right\rangle.

The quotient of
\mathbf{T}(\mathbf{M})
by
\mathbf{J}
is called the
exterior algebra
of
\mathbf{M}
, denoted

\bigwedge\nolimits^{\bullet}(\mathbf{M})
.

The exterior algebra of
\mathbf{M}
is also graded, and we denote the graded components using the usual notation

\bigwedge\nolimits^{\bullet}(\mathbf{M})=\bigoplus\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}(\mathbf{M}),

where each
\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}(\mathbf{M})
is an
\mathbf{A}
-module.

The exterior and the symmetric algebra of
\mathbf{M}
could be defined point-wise. In fact, one checks that the width
w
component of the exterior algebra of
\mathbf{M}
over
\mathbf{A}
is simply the exterior algebra of
\mathbf{M}(w)
over
\mathbf{A}(w)
,
that is,

\left(\bigwedge\nolimits^{\bullet}(\mathbf{M})\right)(w)=\bigwedge\nolimits^{\bullet}\left(\mathbf{M}(w)\right).

Similarly, one has

\left(\operatorname{Sym}_{\bullet}(\mathbf{M})\right)(w)=\bigoplus\limits_{i=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{Sym}_{i}(\mathbf{M}(w)).

3.
Classical Complexes Constructed via Multilinear Algebra

In this section we review classical complexes constructed by multilinear algebra, which we will generalize to the
\OI
setting in Sections
5
and
6
. In particular, we will discuss the Koszul complex and the Eagon-Northcott complex. The latter is part of a family of complexes we dub
Buchsbaum-Eisenbud complexes
because of their appearance in
[
3
]
.
It is important to note that our treatment of these complexes differs slightly from their presentations in other sources (see, e.g.,
[
2
,
7
]
). The differences in our presentation do not meaningfully change the complexes in the classical setting, but allow us to obtain analogs in the category of
\OI
-modules in subsequent sections. With this goal in mind, we describe all chain maps in detail.

We begin with the Koszul complex, which is a complex of free
R
-modules determined by the data of a map from a free
R
-module to
R
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring, let
F
be a finitely generated free
R
-module of rank
n
, and let
\varphi:F\rightarrow R
be an
R
-module map. We define
the Koszul complex

K_{\bullet}(\varphi)
to be the chain complex with modules
K_{d}(\varphi)=\bigwedge\nolimits^{d}(F)
for
d\geq 0
, where for
d\geq 1
the map
K_{d}(\varphi)\rightarrow K_{d-1}(\varphi)
is defined on any wedge of
d
elements of
F
by

e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{d}\mapsto\sum\limits_{j=1}^{d}(-1)^{j+1}\varphi(e_{j})e_{1}\wedge e_{2}\wedge\cdots\wedge\cancel{e_{j}}\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{n}.

The Koszul complex is exact if and only if the elements
\varphi(e_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(e_{n})
form a regular sequence. In this case, if
R
is a standard graded algebra over a field it provides a graded minimal free resolution of the ideal generated by the image of
\varphi
.
For example, if
R
is a noetherian polynomial ring, and
\varphi:F\rightarrow R
sends the basis of
F
to the variables of
R
, the Koszul complex
K_{\bullet}(\varphi)
provides a minimal free resolution of the graded irrelevant ideal of
R
.

The family of Buchsbaum-Eisenbud complexes provides a significant generalization of the Koszul complex. Their input is a homomorphism of free
R
-modules.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
F
and
G
be free
R
-modules with bases
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\}
and
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\}
, and let
\varphi\colon F\rightarrow G
be an
R
-module map, represented by a matrix
A=\left(a_{i,j}\right)\in R^{r\times n}
with respect to these bases.
Let
({-})^{*}
be the usual dualizing functor
\operatorname{Hom}_{R}({-},R)
and let
\{f_{1}^{*},\ldots,f_{n}^{*}\}
be the usual
dual basis for
F^{*}
.
For any integer
i\geq 0
, we define the
i^{\text{th}}
Buchsbaum-Eisenbud complex to
\varphi
as the chain complex
\text{BE}^{i}_{\bullet}(\varphi)
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\to\bigwedge\nolimits^{r}(G^{*})\otimes S_{n-r-i}(G^{*})\otimes\bigwedge\nolimits^{n}F\to\bigwedge\nolimits^{r}(G^{*})\otimes S_{n-r-i-1}(G^{*})\otimes\bigwedge\nolimits^{n-1}F\to\cdots

\displaystyle\to\bigwedge\nolimits^{r}(G^{*})\otimes S_{0}(G^{*})\otimes\bigwedge\nolimits^{r+i}F\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\alpha_{i}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}F\otimes S_{0}(G)\to\bigwedge\nolimits^{i-1}F\otimes S_{1}(G)\to\cdots

\displaystyle\to\bigwedge\nolimits^{1}F\otimes S_{i-1}(G)\to\bigwedge\nolimits^{0}F\otimes S_{i}(G)\to 0,

where
S_{i}
is short for
\operatorname{Sym}_{i}
and the map
\alpha_{i}\colon\bigwedge\nolimits^{r}(G^{*})\otimes S_{0}(G^{*})\otimes\bigwedge\nolimits^{r+i}F\rightarrow\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}F\otimes S_{0}(G)
is defined by

g_{1}^{*}\wedge\cdots\wedge g_{r}^{*}\otimes f_{j_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge f_{j_{r+i}}\mapsto\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq J\\
|I|=r\end{subarray}}\operatorname{sgn}(I\subseteq J)\cdot\operatorname{det}(A_{I})f_{J\setminus I}.

Here,
J=\{j_{1}<\ldots<j_{r+i}\}
,
f_{J}=f_{j_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge f_{j_{r+i}}
,
f_{J\setminus I}
is the wedge of elements with indices in
J\setminus I
,
A_{I}
is the
r
-minor of
A
with column indices in
I
and
\operatorname{sgn}(I\subseteq J)
is the sign of the permutation

\begin{pmatrix}j_{1}&\ldots&j_{r}&j_{r+1}&\ldots&j_{r+i}\\
i_{1}&\ldots&i_{r}&k_{1}&\ldots&k_{i}\end{pmatrix}

with
I=\{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\}
and
J\setminus I=\{k_{1}<\cdots<k_{i}\}
.
By definition, if
i+r>n
the length of
\BE^{i}_{\bullet}(\varphi)
is
\min\{i,n\}
.

The part of
\BE^{i}_{\bullet}(\varphi)
to the right of
\alpha_{i}
is a part of the Koszul complex determined by the
S(G)
-linear map

F\otimes S(G)\to S(G)
induced by
\varphi
. Concretely, the map

\bigwedge\nolimits^{p}(F)\otimes S_{q}(G)\rightarrow\bigwedge\nolimits^{p-1}(F)\otimes S_{q+1}(G)

is defined by

f_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge f_{i_{p}}\otimes g_{j_{1}}\cdots g_{j_{q}}\mapsto\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{p}\left(f_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\cancel{f_{i_{\ell}}}\wedge\cdots\wedge f_{i_{p}}\otimes\varphi(f_{i_{\ell}})g_{j_{1}}\cdots g_{j_{q}}\right).

Any map

\bigwedge\nolimits^{r}(G^{*})\otimes S_{p}(G^{*})\otimes\bigwedge\nolimits^{q}F\rightarrow\bigwedge\nolimits^{r}(G^{*})\otimes S_{p-1}(G^{*})\otimes\bigwedge\nolimits^{q-1}F

to the left of
\alpha_{i}
is defined by

g_{i_{1}}^{*}\cdots g_{i_{p}}^{*}\otimes f_{j_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge f_{j_{q}}\mapsto\sum_{i\in\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{p}\}}\Big(g_{i}(g_{i_{1}}^{*}\cdots g_{i_{p}}^{*})\otimes\sum\limits_{\ell=1}^{q}(-1)^{\ell+1}a_{i,j_{\ell}}f_{j_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge\cancel{f_{j_{\ell}}}\wedge\cdots\wedge f_{j_{q}}\Big).

Here,
g_{i}(g_{i_{1}}^{*}\cdots g_{i_{p}}^{*})
denotes the divided power action of
S_{\bullet}(G)
on
S_{\bullet}(G^{*}).

Often, the part of
\BE^{i}_{\bullet}(\varphi)
to the left of the map
\alpha_{i}
is given as the dual of a Koszul complex used for the beginning of
\text{BE}^{i}_{\bullet}(\varphi)
(see, e.g.,
[
2
]
). Applying the explicit isomorphisms given in
[
1
, Section 1.6]
, one checks that one obtains the maps as given above. The description of the above maps is not coordinate free, but turns out useful in what follows.

The factor
\bigwedge\nolimits^{r}(G^{*})
appearing in terms to the left of
\alpha_{i}
in
\BE^{i}_{\bullet}(\varphi)
is often omitted.
If
R
is a standard graded algebra, we assume that
\varphi
is a map of graded
R
-modules, i.e., it has degree zero. In this case, the factor
\bigwedge\nolimits^{r}(G^{*})
is needed to turn
\BE^{i}_{\bullet}(\varphi)
into a complex of graded free
R
-modules.

It is well-known that the complexes
\text{BE}^{i}_{\bullet}(\varphi)
are generically exact (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Theorem A2.10]
).
Note that the arguments also work if
i>n-r+1
or if
n<r
.

Example 3.3
.

For a generic
3\times w
matrix
B
, denote by
I_{3}
the ideal generated by the
3
-minors of
B
. Using the notation of

Example

1.3
, if
w\geq 3
then the quotient
\mathbf{A}(w)/\mathbf{I}_{3}(A)
is resolved as an
\mathbf{A}(w)
-module by
\text{BE}^{0}_{\bullet}(\varphi)
, where
\varphi\colon(\mathbf{A}(w))^{w}\to(\mathbf{A}(w))^{3}
is the map given by
B
. This was shown first by Eagon and Northcott. Often, the complex
\text{BE}^{0}_{\bullet}(\varphi)
is called an Eagon-Northcott complex because of
[
6
]
.

4.
Exterior and Symmetric Powers and Duals of Free
\OI
-modules

Our goal is to generalize the complexes from the previous section to the setting of modules over
\OI
-algebras. In this section, we establish some needed preparations. Most of the following statements are familiar classical results with commutative rings replaced by commutative
\OI
-algebras. However, these similarities can mask important differences. For example, the tensor product of two free
\mathbf{A}
-modules of rank
1
can be very complicated. Furthermore, duality behaves poorly in many cases.

We begin with a criterion for checking whether an
\mathbf{A}
-module is free.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a commutative OI-algebra, and let
\mathbf{M}
be a finitely-generated
\mathbf{A}
-module.
\mathbf{M}
is a free
\mathbf{A}
-module if and only if
\mathbf{M}
has a generating set
G=\{m_{1},\ldots,m_{n}\}
satisfying the following two conditions:

(i)

For each
m_{i}
in
G
, the
\mathbf{A}
-submodule of
\mathbf{M}
generated by
m_{i}
is a free
\mathbf{A}
-module of rank 1.

(ii)

For each
m_{i}
in
G
, the intersection of the
\mathbf{A}
-submodule of
\mathbf{M}
generated by
m_{i}
and the
\mathbf{A}
-submodule of
\mathbf{M}
generated by
G\setminus\{m_{i}\}
is
0
.

Proof.

It is immediate that for a free
\mathbf{A}
module
\mathbf{F}
, any basis will satisfy these two conditions. Conversely, let
\mathbf{M}
be a finitely generated
\mathbf{A}
-module, and let
G
be a generating set satisfying both conditions. We will prove that
\mathbf{M}
is free by induction on
|G|
.

If
|G|=1
, condition (i) implies that
\mathbf{M}
is free. If
|G|>1
, then let
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}
be the free submodule generated by
m_{1}
, and let
\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime}
be the submodule generated by
G\setminus\{m_{1}\}
. The set
G\setminus\{m_{1}\}
satisfies the two conditions from the lemma, so by induction
\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime}
is free. We can combine the submodule inclusion maps
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime}\hookrightarrow\mathbf{M}
into a map on the direct sum
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}\oplus\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime}\longrightarrow\mathbf{M}
, yielding a map which is surjective since
G
is a generating set for
\mathbf{M}
, and injective because
\mathbf{M}^{\prime}\cap\mathbf{M}^{\prime\prime}
is zero. This completes the proof.
∎

The first use we find for this lemma is to prove that the tensor product of free
\mathbf{A}
-modules is free.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a commutative
\OI
-algebra. Given two finitely generated free
\mathbf{A}
-modules
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{F}^{\prime}
, their tensor product
\mathbf{F}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{F}^{\prime}
is also free.

Proof.

Note that any tensor product of free
\mathbf{A}
-modules of finite rank can be written as a direct sum of tensor products of rank 1
\mathbf{A}
-modules. It thus suffices to prove the assertion in the case where
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{F}^{\prime}
have rank 1.

To prove the proposition in the rank
1
case, we explicitly describe a generating set for
\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{F}^{\text{OI},u}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{F}^{\text{OI},v}
in terms of
u
and
v
and show that it satisfies the two conditions in
4.1
. In a fixed width
w
, the module
\left(\mathbf{F}^{\text{OI},u}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{F}^{\text{OI},v}\right)(w)
is a free
\mathbf{A}(w)
-module with an
\mathbf{A}(w)
-module basis given by the set of pure tensors of basis elements of
\mathbf{F}^{\text{OI,u}}(w)
and
\mathbf{F}^{\text{OI,v}}(w)
, i.e. it has a basis of the form

\left\{e_{\mu}\otimes e_{\nu}\mid\mu\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\text{OI}}(u,w)~\text{and}~\nu\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\text{OI}}(v,w)\right\}.

For a particular pure tensor
e_{\mu}\otimes e_{\nu}
in this set, we will use
I_{\mu,\nu}
to denote the union of the images of the indexing maps

I_{\mu,\nu}=\operatorname{im}(\mu)\cup\operatorname{im}(\nu),

which we think of as a subset of
[w]=\{1,2,\ldots,w\}
. Let
G
be the set of pure tensors in
\mathbf{M}
for which the union of these images is the full set
[w]
, i.e.

G=\{e_{\mu}\otimes e_{\nu}\in\mathbf{M}(w)\mid w\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\text{ and }\ I_{\mu,\nu}=[w]\}.

We claim that
G
satisfies the conditions of
4.1
. To prove that
G
is a generating set, let
e_{\mu}\otimes e_{\nu}
be an arbitrary pure tensor in width
w
, with
|I_{\mu,\nu}|=w^{\prime}\leq w
. There is a unique OI morphism
\varepsilon\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\text{OI}}(w^{\prime},w)
whose image is
I_{\mu,\nu}
, namely the inclusion from
[w^{\prime}]
to
I_{\mu,\nu}
. If we define
\mu^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\text{OI}}(u,w^{\prime})
by
\mu^{\prime}(i)=\varepsilon^{-1}(\mu(i))
, and
\nu^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\text{OI}}(v,w^{\prime})
by
\nu^{\prime}(i)=\varepsilon^{-1}(\nu(i))
, we see that
e_{\mu^{\prime}}\otimes e_{\nu^{\prime}}
is in
G
, and
e_{\mu}\otimes e_{\nu}
is in the
\mathbf{A}
-submodule generated by
e_{\mu^{\prime}}\otimes e_{\nu^{\prime}}
.

To verify condition (i), let
e_{\mu}\otimes e_{\nu}
be any width
w
element of
G
. Define a morphism
\mathbf{F}^{\text{OI},w}\to\langle e_{\mu}\otimes e_{\nu}\rangle
by mapping
e_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbf{F}^{\text{OI},w}(w^{\prime})
onto
e_{\varepsilon\circ\mu}\otimes e_{\varepsilon\circ\nu}
. One checks that this map is an isomorphism of
\mathbf{A}
-modules.

To prove that condition (ii) holds, it is enough to show, for any two distinct elements
e_{\mu}\otimes e_{\nu}\in\mathbf{M}(w)
and

e_{\mu^{\prime}}\otimes e_{\nu^{\prime}}\in\mathbf{M}(w^{\prime})
in
G
and any two maps
\varepsilon\in\Hom_{\OI}(w,\tilde{w})
,
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in\Hom_{\OI}(w^{\prime},\tilde{w})
, one has
(\varepsilon\circ\mu,\varepsilon\circ\nu)\neq(\varepsilon^{\prime}\circ\mu^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime}\circ\nu^{\prime})
. Indeed, using that

w=|I_{\mu,\nu}|=|I_{\varepsilon\circ\mu,\varepsilon\circ\nu}|
and the analogous fact for the other element, this is not too difficult to show.
∎

Observe that the generators of
\mathbf{F}^{\text{OI},u}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{F}^{\text{OI},v}
have any width
w
with

\max\{u,v\}\leq w\leq u+v
.

Example 4.3
.

Consider the free rank 1
\mathbf{A}
-modules
=\mathbf{F}^{\OI,2}_{\mathbf{A}}
and
\mathbf{F}^{\OI,3}_{\mathbf{A}}
.
Their tensor product has a basis with 3, 12 and 10 elements of width 3, 4 and 5, respectively. Thus, there is an isomorphism

\mathbf{F}^{\OI,2}_{\mathbf{A}}\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{F}^{\OI,3}_{\mathbf{A}}\cong({\mathbf{F}}^{\OI,3})^{3}\oplus({\mathbf{F}}^{\OI,4})^{12}\oplus({\mathbf{F}}^{\OI,5})^{10}.

Comparing ranks in width
w
, we obtain for any
w\in\mathbb{N}
the identity,

\binom{w}{2}\cdot\binom{w}{3}=3\binom{w}{3}+12\binom{w}{4}+10\binom{w}{5}.

Turning to exterior powers, we show that a familiar formula in the classical setting remains true for
\mathbf{A}
-modules.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{N}
are
\mathbf{A}
-modules, then one has for every integer
i\geq 0
,

\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}\left(\mathbf{M}\oplus\mathbf{N}\right)\cong\bigoplus\limits_{j=0}^{i}\left(\bigwedge\nolimits^{i-j}\mathbf{M}\right)\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\left(\bigwedge\nolimits^{j}\mathbf{N}\right).

Proof.

By Equation (
2
),
we have in each width
w
a classical isomorphism of
\mathbf{A}(w)
-modules

\displaystyle\left(\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}\left(\mathbf{M}\oplus\mathbf{N}\right)\right)(w)=\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}\big(\mathbf{M}(w)\oplus\mathbf{N}(w)\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\varphi}}{{\rightarrow}}

\displaystyle\bigoplus\limits_{j=0}^{i}\bigwedge\nolimits^{i-j}(\mathbf{M}(w))\otimes_{\mathbf{A}(w)}\bigwedge\nolimits^{j}(\mathbf{N}(w))=

\displaystyle\Big(\bigoplus\limits_{j=0}^{i}\big(\bigwedge\nolimits^{i-j}\mathbf{M}\big)\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\big(\bigwedge\nolimits^{j}\mathbf{N}\big)\Big)(w).

Thus, it is enough to verify that for any
\OI
-morphism
\varepsilon\colon w\to w^{\prime}
the following diagram is commutative:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left(\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}(\mathbf{M}\oplus\mathbf{N})\right)(w)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Big(\bigoplus\limits_{j=0}^{i}\big(\bigwedge\nolimits^{i-j}\mathbf{M}\big)\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\big(\bigwedge\nolimits^{j}\mathbf{N}\big)\Big)(w)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left(\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}(\mathbf{M}\oplus\mathbf{N})\right)(w^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\Big(\bigoplus\limits_{j=0}^{i}\big(\bigwedge\nolimits^{i-j}\mathbf{M}\big)\otimes_{\mathbf{A}}\big(\bigwedge\nolimits^{j}\mathbf{N}\big)\Big)(w^{\prime})}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varepsilon_{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}

This is straightforward.
∎

Next, we show that the exterior power of a free
\mathbf{A}
-module is still free.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a commutative
\OI
-algebra, and let
\mathbf{F}
be a finitely-generated free
\mathbf{A}
-module. Then for every integer
i\geq 0
, the exterior power
\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}\mathbf{F}
is also a finitely-generated free
\mathbf{A}
-module.

Proof.

Because of
4.4
, it suffices to prove the statement in the case where
\mathbf{F}=\mathbf{F}^{\text{OI},d}
is a free
\mathbf{A}
-module of rank
1
. As for
4.2
, the proof proceeds by giving an explicit description of a generating set
G
for
\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}\mathbf{F}^{\text{OI},d}
which satisfies the conditions in
4.1
.

In any given width
w
, using the standard lexicographic ordering on the set of maps
\operatorname{Hom}_{\text{OI}}(d,w)
, we have a basis
B(w)
for the free
\mathbf{A}(w)
-module
\left(\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}\mathbf{F}^{\text{OI},d}\right)(w)
given by

B(w)=\left\{e_{\varepsilon_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{\varepsilon_{i}}~|~\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{i}\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\text{OI}}(d,w)~\text{and}~\varepsilon_{1}<\ldots<\varepsilon_{i}\right\}.

For such a basis element
e_{\varepsilon_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{\varepsilon_{i}}
, consider the union of the images of these indexing maps, which we denote by
I_{\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{i}}
. Let
G
be the following subset of the union of the bases

:

G=\{e_{\varepsilon_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{\varepsilon_{i}}\mid e_{\varepsilon_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{\varepsilon_{i}}\in B(w)~\text{for some}~w\in\mathbb{N}_{0}~\text{and}~I_{\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{i}}=[w]\}.

To show that
G
generates
\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}\mathbf{F}
, it is enough to establish that any
e_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{i}
in any
B(w)
is in the
\mathbf{A}
-submodule of
\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}\mathbf{F}
generated by

If
|I_{\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{i}}|=w
the element is in
G
, and we are done.
If
|I_{\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{i}}|=w^{\prime}<w
, let
\varepsilon
be the unique
\OI

\varepsilon
from
[w^{\prime}]
to
[w]
whose image is
I_{\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{i}}
.
Defining
\varepsilon_{j}^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\OI}(d,w^{\prime})

on any

by
\varepsilon^{-1}(\varepsilon_{j}(k))
,
we see that the element
e_{\varepsilon_{1}^{\prime}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{\varepsilon_{i}^{\prime}}
is in

and that the induced map

\varepsilon\colon\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}\mathbf{F}(w^{\prime})\rightarrow\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}\mathbf{F}(w)
sends this element to
e_{\varepsilon_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{\varepsilon_{i}}
. So
G
is a generating set for
\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}\mathbf{F}
as an
\mathbf{A}
-module.

To verify that
G
satisfies Condition (i) of
4.1
, consider any element
e_{\varepsilon_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{\varepsilon_{i}}\in B(w)
of
G
. Define a morphism
{\mathbf{F}}^{\OI,w}\to\langle e_{\varepsilon_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{\varepsilon_{i}}\rangle
by mapping
e_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbf{F}^{\text{OI},w}(w^{\prime})
onto
e_{\varepsilon\circ\varepsilon_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{\varepsilon\circ\varepsilon_{i}}
. One checks that this map is an isomorphism of
\mathbf{A}
-modules.

To prove that
G
satisfies Condition (ii) of

, it is enough to show for any two distinct elements

e_{\varepsilon_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{\varepsilon_{i}}\in B(w)
and

e_{\varepsilon^{\prime}_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{\varepsilon^{\prime}_{i}}\in B(w^{\prime})
of

and any two maps
\varepsilon\in\Hom_{\OI}(w,\tilde{w})

, one has

(\varepsilon\circ\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon\circ\varepsilon_{i})\neq(\varepsilon^{\prime}\circ\varepsilon^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon^{\prime}\circ\varepsilon^{\prime}_{i})
. As in the proof of

, this follows by using

∎

Observe that the generators of
\bigwedge\nolimits^{i}{\mathbf{F}}^{\OI,d}
have any width

with

, where

.

Example 4.6
.

The
\mathbf{A}
-module
\bigwedge^{2}{\mathbf{F}}^{\OI,3}
has rank 31. It
has a basis with 6, 15 and 10 elements of width 4, 5 and 6, respectively. Thus, there is an isomorphism

\bigwedge^{2}{\mathbf{F}}^{\OI,3}\cong({\mathbf{F}}^{\OI,4})^{6}\oplus({\mathbf{F}}^{\OI,5})^{15}\oplus({\mathbf{F}}^{\OI,6})^{10}.

Comparing ranks in width
w
, we obtain for any
w\in\mathbb{N}
the identity

\binom{\binom{w}{3}}{2}=6\binom{w}{4}+15\binom{w}{5}+10\binom{w}{6}.

Now we establish the analogous result for the symmetric power of a free
\mathbf{A}
-module.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
\mathbf{A}
be a commutative
\OI
-algebra, and let
\mathbf{F}
be a finitely-generated free
\mathbf{A}
-module. Then, for every integer
q\geq 0
, the symmetric power
\operatorname{Sym}_{q}(\mathbf{F})
is also a finitely-generated free
\mathbf{A}
-module.

\mathbf{I}

4.5
