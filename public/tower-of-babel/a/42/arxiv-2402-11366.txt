Abstract
. We study the large-time behavior of global energy class ( H 1 H^{1} ) solutions of the one-dimensional nonlinear Schrödinger equation with a general localized potential term and a defocusing nonlinear term. By using a new type of interaction Morawetz estimate localized to an exterior region, we prove that these solutions decompose into a free wave and a weakly localized part which is asymptotically orthogonal to any fixed free wave. We further show that the L 2 L^{2} norm of this weakly localized part is concentrated in the region | x | ≤ t 1 / 2 + |x|\leq t^{1/2+} , and that the energy ( H ˙ 1 \dot{H}^{1} ) norm is concentrated in | x | ≤ t 1 / 3 + |x|\leq t^{1/3+} . Our results hold for solutions with arbitrarily large initial data.



1.
Introduction

In this work, we study the large time behavior of solutions to the following nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLS) in dimension 1:

(1)

i\partial_{t}u=-\Delta u+V(x,t)u+|u|^{p-1}u

The interaction consists of two terms: a potential term localized near
x=0
and a defocusing nonlinearity. This type of equation occurs in many physical models from areas such as optics
[
2
]
and condensed matter physics
[
28
,
55
]
.
We will focus on solutions that are globally bounded in the
H^{1}
norm, with no additional smallness assumptions.

A fundamental goal of mathematical physics is to understand the asymptotic behavior of all solutions. This problem, known as asymptotic completeness, has been studied extensively in the case of linear equations with time-independent interactions, and very general results have been proved in both the two-body and
N
-body cases: see the texts
[
15
,
56
,
42
]
and references therein. For the time-independent problem, spectral theory tells us that the solution separates into some number of bound states (given by eigenfunctions in the discrete spectrum of
-\Delta+V
) and radiation (given by projection to the continuous spectrum). The time evolution of the bound states is explicit, and for potentials with sufficient decay at infinity the radiation scatters to a free wave solving the equation with potential
V=0
.

However, once the interaction terms are time-dependent or nonlinear, there is no asymptotic theory in anything approaching the generality of the linear case. Leaving aside the case of completely integrable equations, we cannot expect the evolution of the bound states and radiation to decouple like in the linear case. It is not even clear that the bound states and radiation decoupling asymptotically, since the solution could have components that spread slowly in time
[
47
]
. Despite these obstacles, a growing body of numerical and theoretic work has led to the widespread belief that (generic) solutions for a wide array of nonlinear dispersive equations resolve asymptotically into a number of bound states (called solitons) and radiation
[
46
]
. Although this soliton resolution conjecture has inspired a great deal of work on the existence and stability of solitons, it has only been proven to hold for a handful of equations
[
17
,
13
]
.

In fact, the standard scattering problem for linear localized time-dependent potentials has been largely open until very recently. There are fundamental reasons for this. The separation between the radiation and bound state components of the solutions cannot be determined by spectral theory and may be arbitrarily slow. A key problem is to prove microlocal propagation estimates to control the behavior of the solution in certain regions of phase-space. Although these types of estimate are well understood in the time-independent case, where they were decisive in proving
N
-body scattering
[
45
]
, the derivation of these estimates is significantly harder in the time-dependent case owing to the inapplicability of spectral theory methods, which prevent us from projecting away bound states and low-energy waves.

In our work, we will study the asymptotics of solutions
u(t)
to (
1
) that satisfy the condition

(2)

\sup_{t}\lVert u(t)\rVert_{H^{1}}<\infty

This assumption is standard in the field, going back to the work of Tao
[
51
]
. Moreover, for the defocusing equation (
1
), it is equivalent to assuming that the energy

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\mathcal{E}(t)=\int\frac{1}{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+V(x,t)|u(x,t)|^{2}+\frac{1}{p+1}|u(x,t)|^{p+1}\;dx}

is globally bounded, since by the triangle inequality

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\lVert u(t)\rVert_{H^{1}}^{2}\leq 2\mathcal{E}(t)+\lVert V(x,t)\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}}\lVert u(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}}

This is a realistic assumption, and can be shown to hold for a number of physically relevant models, including the cases where
V
is time-independent or satisfies the adiabatic condition
\partial_{t}V\in L^{1}_{t}L^{\infty}_{x}
.

Under the assumption (
2
), Tao has shown that in high dimension (
d\geq 5
) a weak form of soliton resolution holds, in the sense that all solutions to the nonlinear Schrödinger equation decompose in
H^{1}
as time goes to infinity as

(3)

u(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}u_{\textup{loc}}^{(i)}(x-x_{i}(t),t)+e^{it\Delta}u_{+}+o(1)

where each function
u_{\textup{loc}}^{(i)}
satisfies the uniform-in-time localization condition

(4)

\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{t}\int_{|x|\geq R}|u_{\textup{loc}}^{(i)}(x,t)|^{2}\;dx=0

and
|x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t)|\to\infty
for
i\neq j

[
53
]
. Under the additional assumption that
u
is radial, the decomposition (
3
) simplifies t

(5)

u(t)=u_{\textup{loc}}(x,t)+e^{it\Delta}u_{+}+o_{H^{1}}(1)

and
u_{\textup{loc}}
satisfies the quantitative localization bounds

(6)

\sup_{t}\int_{|x|\geq R}|u_{\textup{loc}}(x,t)|^{2}\;ds\lesssim_{u_{0}}R^{-\alpha}

for some
\alpha
depending only on the dimension
[
54
,
49
]
. In lower dimensions, it has not yet been possible to obtain such precise asymptotics. In dimension
3
, Tao’s work
[
51
]
shows that solutions to the radial focusing NLS decompose as

(7)

u(t)=u_{\textup{loc}}(x,t)+e^{it\Delta}u_{+}+o_{\dot{H}^{1}}(1)

where
|\nabla^{j}u_{\textup{loc}}(x,t)|\lesssim\langle x\rangle^{-3/2-j+}
. In particular, this implies that the kinetic energy of the bound state
u_{\textup{loc}}
decays in
x
:

\sup_{t}\int_{|x|\geq R}|\nabla u_{\textup{loc}}(x,t)|^{2}\;ds\lesssim_{u_{0}}R^{-2+}

which can be seen as a weaker version of quantitative the localization of mass (
6
) for
d\geq 5
. Although the mass is not known to be localized, the results of
[
35
,
48
]
show that it can only spread slowly over time. More precisely, all the mass of the nonscattering part of the solution must be localized in the region
|x|\leq t^{1/2+}
as
t\to\infty,
in the sense that

(8)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_{|x|\geq t^{1/2+}}|u_{\textup{loc}}(x,t)|^{2}\;dx=0

Within the class of equations

(9)

i\partial_{t}u+\Delta u=V(x,t)u

in dimension
3
with
\langle x\rangle^{2}V(x,t)\in L^{\infty}_{x,t}
(which includes radial cubic NLS), explicit self-similar solutions constructed in
[
47
]
show that the exponent
1/2
in (
8
) cannot be improved.

Extending these results to dimensions one and two is difficult because the linear dispersive decay
{\lVert e^{it\Delta}\rVert_{L^{1}\to L^{\infty}}\lesssim t^{-d/2}}
is no longer integrable in time, which causes serious difficulties in even defining the linear scattering state
u_{+}
. This problem was only recently addressed by
[
48
]
for the equation (
9
) by making use of the fact that

\lVert F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})e^{-it\Delta}V(x,t)\rVert_{L^{2}_{x}\to L^{2}_{x}}

is integrable in time for potentials
V
which decay pointwise at an appropriate rate.
Here,
F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})
and
F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})
denote smooth projections to the regions
|x|\leq t^{\alpha}
and
|D|\geq t^{-\delta}
; see
Section

2.1
for a precise definition.
Using this, it is possible to prove that (
8
) holds for (
9
) in dimensions one and two.

This method cannot be applied to (
1
) due to the presence of the nonlinear term
|u|^{p-1}u
, which does not decay pointwise in dimension one. To handle this term, we will use the defocusing character of the nonlinearity to prove Morawetz and interaction Morawetz estimates in the exterior region
|x|\geq t^{1/3+}
. We then apply these estimates to two cases of (
1
): equations with nonradiative solutions and mass-supercritical equations. If we assume that
u(t)
is nonradiative (which, roughly speaking, means
u_{+}=0:
see
1
for a precise definition), then the exterior Morawetz estimate can be used to show that

(10)

\int|x||u(x,t)|^{2}\;dx\lesssim\langle t\rangle^{1/2},

which in particular implies (
8
). For equations with
p<3
, nonlinear effects are strong enough to improve the right-hand side of (
10
) to
\langle t\rangle^{\frac{2p}{3(p+1)}}
. For mass-supercritical equations, the exterior interaction Morawetz estimate give us time-averaged decay for the nonlinearity, allowing us to prove the existence of the free channel wave operator
u_{0}\to u_{+}
, as well as the improved localization for the weakly localized part
u_{\textup{loc}}(t)
. To the best of our knowledge, the exterior interaction Morawetz estimate we give is new, and we believe it may be of independent interest.

1.1.
Related work

In addition to the work discussed above, there are a number of other perspectives on scattering and soliton resolution. We will briefly summarize a few areas and discuss how they relate to our work below:

1.1.1.
Equations with repulsive interactions

In the case where the attractive part of the interaction is absent or too weak to form a bound state, profile decomposition can be used to prove scattering of the solution. The general form of the argument, often known as the concentration compactness-rigidity method, was introduced in
[
29
]
for the focusing energy-critical nonlinear Schrödinger equation in dimension
3
, and has since been generalized and refined in a vast number of works: see
[
30
,
16
,
19
,
21
,
18
]
for a (very) incomplete sampling. In particular, it can be applied in the presence of a small or repulsive potential
[
27
,
32
,
1
,
12
]
. However, it is not applicable to (
1
) when
V
is allowed to be time-dependent or nonrepulsive, since the solution may exhibit bound states or other nonscattering behavior.

1.1.2.
NLS with potential and asymptotic stability of solitons

Beginning with the pioneering work of
[
24
]
, there have been a number of works on the asymptotic behavior of nonlinear Schrödinger equations with potential
[
26
,
39
,
40
,
4
,
5
,
50
]
culminating in proofs that solitons for a wide range of 1 dimension nonlinear Schrödinger equations are asymptotically stable under small, localized perturbations
[
3
,
9
,
34
]
. The argument proceeds by writing the solution
u
as the sum of a modulated soliton and a perturbation. In a reference frame moving with the soliton, the perturbation solves a nonlinear Schrödinger equation with potential. By adapting the method of space-time resonances introduced in
[
25
]
, it is possible to show that small perturbations exhibit modified scattering. A more complete summary of the method can be found in the recent survey article
[
23
]
. This method gives very precise asymptotics; however, it necessarily requires the initial data to be a small, localized perturbation of a soliton and thus cannot be applied to nonlinear equations with generic large initial data. Moreover, the method relies crucially on the linear spectral theory of Schrödinger operators with potential, so it is not clear how to adapt the argument to accommodate equations like (
1
) where the potential with general time dependence. Even in the case of time-independent potentials, one-dimensional models containing internal modes have been stubbornly resistant to this style of analysis
[
14
,
33
]
.

1.1.3.
Virial methods and local asymptotic stability of solitons

Beginning with the work of
[
31
]
, there have been a number of recent works that have established local asymptotic stability for a number of models containing solitons
[
37
,
36
,
43
,
44
,
11
,
10
]
. The key ingredient in this argument is to prove an appropriate virial identity, which gives decay of solutions in an appropriate (interior) region of space-time. This method has proved quite flexible, especially in applications to models that exhibit strongly nonlinear behavior. However, the information it gives is inherently local in character.

1.2.
Main results

We will assume that the potential and its derivative obey
uniform in time spatial
decay bounds

(11)

\sup_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}x},t}|\langle x\rangle^{\sigma}V(x,t)|\leq C

and

(12)

\sup_{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}x},t}|\langle x\rangle^{\sigma+1}\partial_{x}V(x,t)|\leq C

Our first result concerns the behavior of solutions that behave like a purely bound state, with no freely scattering radiation as
t\to\infty
:

Theorem 1
.

Suppose
\sigma\geq 2
, that
u
is a bounded energy solution to (
1
) and is nonradiative in the sense that

(13)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\langle F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\gamma F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\rangle_{t}=0

for
1/3<{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\beta}<1
(see
Section

3
for a definition of the notation
\langle A(t)\rangle_{t}
). If
|x|^{1/2}u_{0}\in L^{2}
, then
\langle|x|\rangle_{t}\lesssim 1+t^{1/2}
.

Furthermore, if
p<3
and
\sigma\geq\frac{p+3}{p}
, then we have the improved bound
\langle|x|\rangle_{t}\lesssim 1+t^{\frac{2p}{3(p+1)}}
.

For
u_{\text{lin}}(t)=e^{it\Delta}u_{0}
solving the linear Schrödinger equation,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\langle F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\gamma F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\rangle_{t}=\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1/2}}^{2}

can only vanish if
u_{0}=0
, which justifies the terminology nonradiative.

Remark 1
.

It might seem paradoxical that decreasing
p
, which increases the ability of the nonlinearity to disperse concentrated states, also decreases in the speed at which nonradiative states spread. This can be understood by noting that outgoing waves that are located far from the origin and have nonnegligible energy will be traveling fast enough to scatter freely. Since the defocusing nonlinearity aids in this scattering, any weakly bound part of the solution must stay closer to the origin for small
p
to avoid becoming radiation.

Next, we consider the problem (
1
) with mass supercritical nonlinearity
p>5
, and prove that the solutions split asymptotically into a free wave and a localized part which spreads slowly in time:

Theorem 2
.

Suppose
\sigma\geq 2
and
p>5
Then, any solution to (
1
) satisfying (
2
) can be written as

(14)

u(t)=e^{it\Delta}u_{+}+u_{\textup{loc}}(t)

where
u_{+}\in H^{1}
, and for any
\kappa>1/2,
and
\mu>\max(\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{\sigma})
, the weakly localized par
u_{\textup{loc}}
satisfies

(15)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\lVert F(|x|\geq t^{\kappa})u_{\textup{loc}}(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}=0

and

(16)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\lVert F(|x|\geq t^{\mu})\partial_{x}u_{\textup{loc}}(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}=0

We note that for a free solution
u_{\text{free}}=e^{it\Delta}u_{0}
, we have that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\lVert F(|x|\leq t^{\kappa})u_{\text{free}}(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\lVert F(|x|\leq t^{\mu})\partial_{x}u_{\text{free}}(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}=0

(this follows from
Lemma

5
in
Section

2.2
). In particular, this implies that the decomposition (
14
) reflects an increasing separation between the free part
e^{it\Delta}u_{+}
and the weakly localized part
u_{\textup{loc}}(t)
in physical space as
t\to\infty
.

Remark 2
.

In the interest of exposition, we have stated
1
and
2
with the short-range hypothesis
\sigma\geq 2
. With a slight modification to the argument, we could also derive similar results (but with worse localization) under the optimal short-range hypothesis
\sigma>1
.

Remark 3
.

The restriction
\mu>\frac{1}{3}
arises because in the interaction Morawetz estimates we must commute the Laplacian through a spatial cutoff, producing an additional effective potential term of size
\langle x\rangle^{-3}
. This estimate is only necessary to control the defocusing nonlinearity, so our arguments also show that the rate at which energy spreads in the time-dependent linear problem is determined by the decay of the potential.

Remark 4
.

The proofs of
1
and
2
ultimately rely on the exterior Morawetz estimates that we present in
Section

3
. Since Morawetz estimates hold for solutions in
H^{1/2}
, it is likely that (
2
) could be weakened to
\lVert u\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{s}_{x}}<\infty
for some
s>1/2
(and possibly
s=1/2
, although one would have to be careful about the failure of the endpoint Sobolev embedding). In the interest of expositional clarity, we do not pursue these improvements here.

1.3.
Difficulties and a sketch of the proof

In one dimension, the dispersive decay of
e^{it\Delta}
is too slow to apply the methods of
[
49
,
35
,
48
]
to (
1
) because the term
|u|^{p-1}u
does not belong to any weighted
L^{p}
space. We will overcome this difficulty by deriving decay estimates in the exterior region
|x|\geq t^{\beta}
, which provide sufficient control far from the origin to prove
1
and
2
.

We begin with
1
. To prove this result, we will show that
u(t)
satisfies a Morawetz-type estimate in the exterior region
|x|\geq t^{\beta}
for any
\beta\in(1/3,1)
. The advantage of this exterior cutoff is twofold. First, it allows us to turn the
spatial
decay of
V
into decay in
time
, making it negligible for the purposes of the estimate. Second, it eliminates the term

(17)

[{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}-i}\Delta,\gamma]={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\frac{1}{2}}[{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}-i}\Delta,[{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}-i}\Delta,|x|]]

which would otherwise prevent us from getting any estimate in dimension
1
. On the other hand, it also introduces the terms

[-\Delta,F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})]i\gamma F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})+F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})i\gamma[-\Delta,F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})]

which reflect the fact that the solution can flow across the boundary
|x|\sim t^{\beta}
. These ’quantum correction’ terms do not have a sign in general. However, after carefully symmetrizing, it can be shown that they have the right sign up to an error of
O(t^{-3\beta})
, and so can be treated perturbatively if
\beta>1/3
.

Using these Morawetz-estimates, we adapt the argument of
[
35
]
to show that
u(t)
is localized to the region
|x|\leq t^{1/2}
as
t\to\infty
. A key novelty here is the use of the additional term in the Morawetz estimate from the defocusing nonlinearity to obtain improved localization for equations with
p<3
.

To prove
1
, the key observation is that the exterior Morawetz estimate can be used to upgrade the
qualitative
convergence in the nonradiative hypothesis (
13
) into a
quantitative
decay rate in time for the ‘exterior Morawetz current’
\langle F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\gamma F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\rangle_{t}
. Since
\gamma
is the Heisenberg derivative of the observable
|x|
, this decay rate can be integrated to give a bound for
\langle|x|\rangle_{t}
, and optimizing in
\beta
gives the bound
\langle|x|\rangle_{t}\lesssim\langle t\rangle^{1/2}
. Moreover, by using the extra decay coming from the nonlinear portion of the Morawetz estimate, we can get better decay for
\langle F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\gamma F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\rangle_{t}
(and hence better localization of
u(t)
) in the case
p<3
. This agrees with the intuition that the nonlinearity in (
1
) should be long-range for
p\leq 3
(see
[
52
, Chapter 3]
).

To prove
2
, we first must show that the free channel wave operator
u_{0}\mapsto u_{+}
exists. The idea here is to introduce a projection
J_{\textup{free}}
such that

\slim_{t\to\infty}(I-J_{\textup{free}})e^{it\Delta}=0

which is supported near the propagation set for the free flow in phase space. A natural choice is to choose
J_{\textup{free}}
to localize inside the free flow region
x=2\xi t+o(t)
, where
\xi
is the frequency variable. Having done so, the existence of the free channel is equivalent to showing that the limit

(18)

u_{+}=\slim_{t\to\infty}e^{-it\Delta}J_{\textup{free}}u(t)

exists. As explained above, the methods of
[
48
]
do not apply here, since the nonlinear term does not obey weighted decay estimates. To overcome this obstacle, we introduce a new exterior interaction Morawetz estimate, which controls the nonlinearity away from the origin. Interaction Morawetz estimates were previously introduced in
[
6
]
to prove global well-posedness for defocusing nonlinear Schrödinger equations in dimension
3
, and have become a standard tool in proving global well-posedness and scattering. Obtaining these estimates in lower dimensions is more difficult, since ordinary Morawetz estimates fail due to the singular term (
17
). The first interaction Morawetz estimates in lower dimensions were obtained in
[
22
,
7
]
in dimension
2
, and hold only locally in time. Later, these results were improved in
[
8
,
41
]
, yielding the estimate

(19)

\lVert\partial_{x}|u|^{2}\rVert_{L^{2}_{t,x}}^{2}\lesssim\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}^{4}

We prove that this estimate holds even in the presence of a nonrepulsive potential provided we localize to
|x|\geq t^{\beta}
for
\beta\in(1/3,1)
. This estimate can be interpolated with mass conservation to obtain a pointwise decay estimate in the exterior region, allowing us to prove the existence of the strong limit (
18
) defining
u_{+}
.

To control the remainder
u_{\textup{rem}}=(I-J_{\textup{free}})u(t)
, we note that in addition to the regular Duhamel representation using
u_{0}
, we also have a representation ’from the future’ coming from the fact that
e^{it\Delta}u_{\textup{rem}}\rightharpoonup 0
. By combining an incoming/outgoing decomposition similar to the one used in
[
51
]
with some careful stationary phase arguments, we are able to prove that the mass of the solution is concentrated in the region
|x|\lesssim t^{1/2+}
and that the energy is concentrated in
|x|\lesssim t^{1/3+}
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Definitions

We use the notation
A\lesssim B
to mean that
A\leq CB
for some arbitrary but fixed constant
C>0
. If
C
is allowed to depend on the parameters
P_{1},P_{2},\cdots,P_{n}
, we write

A\lesssim_{P_{1},P_{2},\cdots,P_{n}}B
. Similarly,
A\sim B
means
\frac{1}{C}B\leq A\leq CB
for some arbitrary but fixed
C>0
.

In our arguments, we will frequently encounter products of differential operators and multiplication operators
G:\phi(x)\mapsto G(x)\phi(x)
. To fix notation, we denote by
P(D_{x})G(x)
the operator mapping
\phi
to
P(D_{x})(G(x)\phi(x))
, while
(P(D_{x})G(x))
is the application of
P(D_{x})
to
G(x)
. (In particular,
(P(D_{x})G(x))
is a multiplication operator.) Thus, for example,

\partial_{x}f(x)\phi=\partial_{x}(f(x)\phi)=(\partial_{x}f(x))\phi+f(x)\partial_{x}\phi

We now define the smooth cut-off functions
F(x\geq A)
and
F(x\leq A)
. Let

H(x):=\begin{cases}e^{-1/x}&x>0\\
0&x\leq 0\end{cases}

We define the function
F(x\leq 1)
by

F(x\leq 1):=\begin{cases}0&x\leq 1/2\\
\exp\left(-\frac{H(2-x)}{x-1/2}\right)&x>1/2\end{cases}

In particular,
F(x\leq 1)
is smooth. Moreover, since
G(x):=-\frac{H(2-x)}{x-1/2}
is strictly increasing for
1/2<x<2
, we see that
F
is strictly increasing on
[1/2,2]
. For every positive real number
A
, we define

(20)

\begin{split}F(x\geq A):=&F\left(\frac{x}{A}\geq 1\right)\\
F(x\leq A):=&1-F(x\geq A)\end{split}

when differentiating these function, we will use the notation
F^{(n)}(x\geq A)
to refer to the
n
th derivative of
F(X\geq 1
evaluated at
\frac{x}{A}
, and similarly for
F^{(n)}(x\leq A)
.

In the future, we will need to use the fact that
\sqrt{F(x\geq A)}
and
\sqrt{F^{\prime}(x\geq A)}
are smooth, bounded functions.

Lemma 3
.

For every
A>0
, the functions
\sqrt{F(x\geq A)}
and
\sqrt{F^{\prime}(x\geq A)}
are smooth.

Proof.

We note that it suffices to prove the result for
A=1
. We begin by proving that
\sqrt{F(x\geq 1)}
is smooth. From the definition, we see that

\begin{split}\partial_{x}\sqrt{F(x\geq 1)}=\partial_{x}\sqrt{1-F(x\leq 1)}=\frac{-F^{\prime}(x\leq 1)}{2\sqrt{1-F(x\leq 1)}}=\begin{cases}0&x<1/2\\
-\frac{G^{\prime}(x)\exp(G(x))}{2\sqrt{1-\exp(G(x))}}&x\geq 1/2\end{cases}\end{split}

This expression is smooth for
x<2
by the definition of
G
, and vanishes for
x>2
. Thus, the only possible singularity is
x=2
, and the function will be
C^{1}
provided that

\lim_{x\to 2^{-}}\partial_{x}\sqrt{F(x\geq 1)}=0

By Taylor expanding the exponentials and using that
G(x)\to 0
as
x\to 2
, we see that

\partial_{x}\sqrt{F(x\geq 1)}=-\frac{G^{\prime}(x)(1+o(1))}{\sqrt{G(x)(1+o(1))}}=\frac{G^{\prime}(x)}{\sqrt{G(x)}}(-1+o(1))

Now, for any
k\geq 0

(21)

G^{(k)}(x)=\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}\begin{cases}0&x\geq 2\\
\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2-x}\right)}{x-1/2}&x<2\end{cases}=\begin{cases}0&x\geq 2\\
P_{k}((2-x)^{-1},(x-1/2)^{-1})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2-x}\right)&x<2\\
\end{cases}

where
P_{k}(X,Y)
is a polynomial in
X
and
Y
. In particular, we see that

\lim_{x\to 2^{-}}\frac{G^{\prime}(x)}{\sqrt{G(x)}}=\lim_{x\to 2^{-}}P_{1}((2-x)^{-1},(x-1/2)^{-1})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2(2-x)}\right)=0

which shows that
\sqrt{F(x\geq 1)}
is
C^{1}
. In general, we have that

\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}\sqrt{F(x\geq 1)}=\sum_{n=1}^{k}\frac{\mathfrak{F}_{n,k}(x)}{(1-F(x\leq 1))^{n-1/2}}

where
\mathfrak{F}_{n,k}(x)
is of the form

\mathfrak{F}_{n,k}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{n,k}}C_{j,n,k}\prod_{i=1}^{n}F^{(a_{i,j,n,k})}(x\leq 1)

for some
N_{n,k}
,
C_{j,n,k}
, and
a_{i,j,n,k}
with
1\leq a_{i,j,n,k}\leq n
. As in the case
k=1
,
\sqrt{F(x\geq 1)}
will be
C^{k}
provided that
\lim_{x\to 2^{-}}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}\sqrt{F(x\geq 1)}=0
. By noting that

F^{(k)}(x\leq 1)=\mathfrak{G}_{k}(x)\exp(G(x))

where
\mathfrak{G}_{n,k}
is a polynomial in
G^{\prime}(x),G^{\prime\prime}(x),\cdots,G^{(k)}(x)
, we see by (
21
) that

\begin{split}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}\sqrt{F(x\geq 1)}=&\sum_{n=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{n,k}}\frac{C_{j,n,k}\prod_{i=1}^{n}F^{(a_{i,j,n,k})}(x\leq 1)}{(1-F(x\leq 1))^{n-1/2}}\\
=&\sum_{n=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{n,k}}(C_{n,j,k}+o(1))\mathfrak{P}_{j,n,k}(x)\sqrt{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2-x}\right)}\end{split}

where
\mathfrak{P}_{j,n,k}(x)
is a polynomial in
(2-x)^{-1}
and
(x-1/2)^{-1}
, and on the last line we have used the Taylor expansion
\exp(G(x))=1+O(G(x))
as
x\to 2
. It follows that

\lim_{x\to 2^{-}}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}\sqrt{F(x\geq 1)}=0

so
\sqrt{F(x\geq 1)}\in C^{k}
for all
k
.

Turning to
\sqrt{F^{\prime}(x\geq 1)}
, we observe that since
F^{\prime}(x\geq 1)
is nonzero on
(1/2,2)
, we will have shown that
\sqrt{F^{\prime}(x\geq 1)}\in C^{\infty}
once we show that for each
k\geq 1
,

\lim_{x\to 1/2^{+}}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}\sqrt{F^{\prime}(x\geq 1)}=\lim_{x\to 2^{-}}\frac{d^{k}}{dx^{k}}\sqrt{F^{\prime}(x\geq 1)}=0

The argument for
x\to 2^{-}
is a straightforward modification of the one we give above, while the argument for
x\to 1/2^{+}
is similar.
∎

We also denote by
\tilde{F}_{M}(x\geq A)
the function

\tilde{F}_{M}(x\geq A)=F\left(x+a_{M}\geq b_{M}A\right)

a_{M}
and
b_{M}
are chosen so that

\frac{M^{-1}-a_{M}}{b_{M}}=1/2,\qquad\frac{M-a_{M}}{b_{M}}=2

With these choices, we find that
\supp\tilde{F}_{M}(x\geq A)=[A/M,\infty)
and
\supp\tilde{F}^{\prime}(x\geq A)=[A/M,AM]
. In particular, by choosing
M
appropriately, we can guarantee that
\tilde{F}_{M}(x\geq A)
is nonzero in any region
|x|\gtrsim A
and
\tilde{F}^{\prime}(x\geq A)
is nonzero in any region
|x|\sim A
(with fixed but arbitrary implicit constants). In our work, we will write
\tilde{F}(x\geq A)
to denote
\tilde{F}_{M}(x\geq A)
, where
M
is chosen to guarantee these nonvanishing properties for an appropriate range of
x
values.

2.2.
Dispersive estimates

In our work, we will make frequent use of the
L^{1}\to L^{\infty}
dispersive estimate

(22)

\lVert e^{it\Delta}\rVert_{L^{1}\to L^{\infty}}\lesssim|t|^{-1/2}

As is well-known, this estimate implies the Strichartz estimates. In particular, we have the dual Strichartz estimates.

Theorem 4
.

For any
\Phi\in L^{q^{\prime}}_{t}L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}
with
q^{\prime},r^{\prime}\leq 2
satisfying

\frac{1}{r^{\prime}}+{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\frac{2}{q^{\prime}}}=\frac{5}{2}

we have that

\left\lVert\int e^{-is\Delta}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\Phi}(x,s)\;ds\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim\lVert{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\Phi}(x,t)\rVert_{L^{q^{\prime}}_{t}L^{r^{\prime}}_{x}}

Let us prove the stationary phase type estimates:

Lemma 5
.

For any positive exponents
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\delta
satisfying

\delta<\min(1/2,1-\alpha,1-\beta)

and any
N\geq 0
, the following inequality holds for
t\geq 1
:

\left\lVert F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})e^{it\Delta}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})F(|x|\leq t^{\beta})\right\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\lesssim_{N,\alpha,\beta,\delta}t^{-N}

Proof.

For
\phi\in L^{2}
, define the operator
I
by

\begin{split}I(\phi)=&F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})e^{it\Delta}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})F(|x|\leq t^{\beta})\phi(x)\\
=&\frac{F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-it\xi^{2}+ix\xi}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})\hat{\psi}(\xi)\;d\xi\end{split}

where
\psi=F(|x|\leq t^{\beta})\phi

By integrating by parts
K
times using the identity

\frac{i}{2t\xi-x}\partial_{\xi}e^{-it\xi^{2}+ix\xi}=e^{-it\xi^{2}+ix\xi}

we find that

\begin{split}I(\phi)=&\frac{F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-it\xi^{2}+ix\xi}\left(\partial_{\xi}\frac{i}{2t\xi-x}\right)^{K}\left(F(|{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\xi}|\geq t^{-\delta})\hat{\psi}\right)\;d\xi\\
=&\sum_{a+b+c={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}K}}C_{a,b,c}F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})\int e^{-it\xi^{2}}\frac{t^{a}}{(2t\xi-x)^{K+a}}t^{b\delta}F^{{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}(b)}}(|{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\xi}|\geq t^{-\delta})\partial_{\xi}^{c}\hat{\psi}\;d\xi\\
=:&\sum_{a+b+c=K}c_{a,b,c}F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})I_{a,b,c}(\phi)\end{split}

for some constants
C_{a,b,c}\in\mathbb{C}
. Now, for
|x|\lesssim t^{\alpha}
and
|\xi|\gtrsim t^{-\delta}
, our hypothesis that
\alpha<1-\delta
implies that

|2t\xi-x|\gtrsim t|\xi|

so

\begin{split}|I_{a,b,c}({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\phi})|\lesssim&t^{-K}t^{b\delta}\lVert|\xi|^{-K-a}F^{(b)}(|\xi|\geq t^{-\delta})\rVert_{L^{2}_{\xi}}\lVert x^{c}F(|x|\leq t^{\beta})\phi\rVert_{L^{2}_{x}}\\
\lesssim&t^{-K}t^{\delta(K+a+b-1/2)}t^{c\beta}\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{2}}\\
\lesssim&t^{-\delta/2}t^{-K(1-\max(2\delta,\delta+\beta))}\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{2}}\end{split}

Thus, by Hölder, we find that

(23)

\lVert F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})I_{a,b,c}(\phi)\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim t^{(\alpha-\delta)/2}t^{-K(1-\max(2\delta,\delta+\beta))}\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{2}}

Thus, provided
\max(2\delta,\delta+\beta)<1
, we can choose
K
sufficiently large that
I(\phi)
decays at a rate of at least
t^{-N}
in
L^{2}
.
∎

Lemma 6
.

For any positive exponents
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\delta
satisfying

\beta>\max(\alpha,\delta,1-\delta)

and any
N\geq 0
,
the following inequality holds for
t\geq 1
:

\left\lVert F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})e^{it\Delta}F(|D|\leq t^{-\delta})F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})\right\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\lesssim_{N,\alpha,\beta,\delta}t^{-N}

Proof.

As in the proof of
Lemma

5
, we define:

\begin{split}I(\phi)=&F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})e^{it\Delta}F(|D|\leq t^{-\delta})F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})\phi\\
=&\sum_{a+b+c=K}C_{a,b,c}F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\int e^{-it\xi^{2}+ix\xi}\frac{t^{a}}{(2t\xi-x)^{K+a}}t^{b\delta}\partial_{\xi}^{b}F(|D|\leq t^{-\delta})\partial_{\xi}^{c}\hat{\psi}\;d\xi\\
=:&\sum_{a+b+c=K}C_{a,b,c}F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})I_{a,b,c}(\phi)\end{split}

where here
\psi=F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})\phi
. By hypothesis,
\beta>1-\delta
, so under our microlocalization

|2t\xi-x|\gtrsim|x|

Thus, we find that

\begin{split}\lVert F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})I_{a,b,c}(\phi)\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim&\left\lVert\frac{t^{a+b\delta}}{|x|^{K+a}}F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\right\rVert_{L^{2}}\int|\partial_{\xi}^{b}F(|D|\leq t^{-\delta})\partial_{\xi}^{c}\hat{\psi}|\;d\xi\\
\lesssim&t^{a+(b-1/2)\delta-\beta(K+a-1/2)}\lVert\partial_{\xi}^{c}\hat{\psi}\rVert_{L^{2}}\\
\lesssim&t^{a+(b-1/2)\delta+c\alpha-\beta(K+a-1/2)}\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{2}}\end{split}

Substituting into the expression for
I(\phi)
, we find that

\lVert I(\phi)\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim_{K,\alpha,\beta,\delta}t^{-\beta(K-1/2)-\delta/2}t^{\max(1-\beta,\alpha,\delta)K}\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{2}}

Now, our hypotheses imply that
\beta>\max(\delta,1-\delta)\geq 1/2
, so
\beta>1/2>1-\beta
, and
the result follows.
∎

2.3.
Commutator estimates

Commutator estimates play an important role in proving propagation estimates. To begin, we record an estimate for commuting physical- and Fourier-space localization:

Lemma 7
.

If
F
and
G
are smooth cut-off functions supported near zero, then

(24)

[F(|x|\leq A),G(|D|\leq B)]\lesssim\frac{1}{AB}

Moreover, if
\tilde{G}
is a smooth cut-off function supported in
|x|\geq 1
, then

(25)

[F(|x|\leq A),\tilde{G}(|D|\geq B)]\lesssim\frac{1}{AB}

Proof.

We begin by proving (
24
). Observe that
G(|D|\leq 1)
can be represented as a convolution:

G(|D|\leq 1)\phi(x)=\int{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}k}(x-y)\phi(y)\;dy

where

k=\mathcal{F}^{-1}F(|\xi|\leq 1)
is smooth and rapidly decaying. By scaling,

G(|D|\leq B)\phi(x)=\int B{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}k}(B(x-y))\phi(y)\;dy

It follows that

\begin{split}[F(|x|\leq A),G(|D|\leq B)]\phi=&B\int(F(|x|\leq A)-F(|y|\leq A)){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}k}(B(x-y))\phi(y)\;dy\end{split}

Thus, by Schur’s test,

\lVert[F(|x|\leq A),G(|D|\leq B)]\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\lVert B(F(|x|\leq A)-F(|y|\leq A)){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}k}(B(x-y))\rVert_{L^{1}_{y}}

By the Mean Value Theorem,

|F(|x|\leq A)-F(|y|\leq A)|\lesssim\frac{|x-y|}{A}

uniformly in
x,y
, so

\begin{split}\lVert[F(|x|\leq A),G(|D|\leq B)]\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\lesssim&\frac{1}{A}\sup_{x}\lVert B|x-y|{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}k}(B(x-y){\color[rgb]{1,0,0})}\rVert_{L^{1}_{y}}\\
\lesssim&\frac{1}{AB}\end{split}

as required. Note that
1-\tilde{G}(|x|\geq B)=G(|x|\leq B)
, so (
25
) follows immediately.
∎

Using the convolution representation of
F(|D|\leq A)
, we see that for
AB\gg 1
,
F(x>B)F(|D|\leq A)
will essentially be supported on
F(x>\frac{1}{100}B)
, since
F(|D|\leq A)
represents a convolution with characteristic length scale
A^{-1}
. We make this heuristic precise in the following lemma:

Lemma 8
.

For any
N>0
,

(26a)

\displaystyle\lVert F(D>B)F(|x|\leq A)F(D\leq\frac{1}{100}B)\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\lesssim_{N}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(AB)^{N}}

(26b)

\displaystyle\lVert F(x>B)F(|D|\leq A)F(x\leq\frac{1}{100}B)\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\lesssim_{N}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(AB)^{N}}

Proof.

By the Plancherel theorem, it is enough to prove (
26b
).
Arguing as in the proof of
Lemma

7
, we see that this
operator has a kernel representation given by

K(x,y)=AF(x>B)k(A(x-y))F(y\leq\frac{1}{100}B)

Observe that the localization of
x
and
y
implies that
|x-y|\geq cB
for some constant
c
. Since
F
is smooth,
|k(z)|\leq|z|^{-N-1}
, so

\begin{split}\lVert F(x>B)F(|D|\leq A)F(x\leq\frac{1}{100}B)\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\leq&(\sup_{x}\lVert K(x,y)\rVert_{L^{1}_{y}})^{1/2}(\sup_{y}\lVert K(x,y)\rVert_{L^{1}_{z}})^{1/2}\\
\lesssim&\lVert Ak(Az)\rVert_{L^{1}(|z|\geq cB{\color[rgb]{1,0,0})}}\\
\lesssim&\lVert k(z)\rVert_{L^{1}(|z|\geq cAB{\color[rgb]{1,0,0})}}\\
\lesssim_{N}&(AB)^{-N}\end{split}

as required.
∎

We also record the following abstract commutator lemmas, which will often be useful.

Lemma 9
.

For operators
A,B,C
with
[A,C]=0
,

ABC-CBA=A[B,C]{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}-}C[B,A]

Lemma 10
.

A^{2}B+BA^{2}=2ABA+[A,[A,B]]

Lemma 11
.

Suppose
[A,C]=0
. Then,

(27)

A^{2}BC^{2}+C^{2}BA^{2}=2(AC)B(AC)+R(A,B,C)

where

R(A,B,C)=A[[A,B],C]C+C[[C,B],A]{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}A}+A[C,[C,B]]A+C[A,[A,B]]C

involves double commutators of
A
and
C
with
B
.

Proof.

To begin, we observe that

A^{2}BC^{2}=ABC^{2}A+A[A,B]C^{2}=(AC)B(AC)+A[A,B]C^{2}+A[B,C]CA

and reversing the roles of
A
and
C
gives

A^{2}BC^{2}=ABC^{2}A+A[A,B]C^{2}=(AC)B(AC)-C[B,C]A^{2}-C[A,B]CA

Thus,

\begin{split}A^{2}BC^{2}+C^{2}BA^{2}=2(AC)B(AC)+\left\{A[A,B]C-C[A,B]A\right\}C+\left\{A[B,C]C-C[B,C]A\right\}A\end{split}

Considering the first term in curly brackets, we see that

A[A,B]C-C[A,B]A=A[[A,B],C]+CA[A,B]-C[[A,B],A]-CA[A,B]=A[[A,B],C]-C[[A,B],A]

which gives the first and last term of
R(A,B,C)
. A similar calculation involving the other bracketed term gives the remaining double commutator terms.
∎

2.4.
Other preliminaries

In our later work, we will make use of the following lemma, which can be seen as a variant of the classical Ladyzhenskaya inequality:

Lemma 12
.

If
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}G}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is a bounded increasing function, then

\lVert|{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}G}(|x|)\phi|^{2}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\leq 2\lVert{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}G}(|x|)\partial_{x}|\phi|\rVert_{L^{2}}\lVert{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}G(|x|)|\phi|}\rVert_{L^{2}}

Proof.

Let us assume that
x\geq 0
, since the case of negative
x
is similar. We have that

\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}G}^{2}(x)|\phi(x)|^{2}=&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}G}^{2}(x)\int_{x}^{\infty}\partial_{y}|\phi(y)|^{2}\;dy\\
\leq&\int_{x}^{\infty}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}G}^{2}(x)|\partial_{y}|\phi(y)|^{2}|\;dy\\
\leq&2\int_{x}^{\infty}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}G}^{2}(y)|\partial_{y}\phi(y)||\phi(y)|\;dy\\
\leq&2\lVert{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}G(|x|)}\partial_{x}\phi\rVert_{L^{2}}\lVert{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}G(|x|)}\phi\rVert_{L^{2}}\end{split}

∎

3.
Propagation estimates

In order to control the spreading of the solution and take full advantage of the defocusing character of the nonlinearity, we will use several propagation estimates. For an operator
B
, we define the expectation of
B
with respect to
u
at time
t
to be

(28)

\langle B\rangle_{t,u}=\langle Bu,u\rangle_{L^{2}}

when the function
u
is clear from context, we will often drop it and simply write
\langle B\rangle_{t}
. For a time-dependent operator
B(t)
, the evolution of the expectation is given by

(29)

\partial_{t}\langle B(t)\rangle_{t}=\langle D_{H}B(t)\rangle_{t}

where
D_{H}B(t)
is the Heisenberg derivative given by

(30)

D_{H}B(t)=[-i\Delta+iV+i|u|^{p-1},B(t)]+\partial_{t}B(t)

Morawetz estimates are an important type of propagation estimate. Observe that for
B(t)=|x|
, we have

(31)

D_{H}|x|=[-i\Delta,|x|]=2\left(\frac{\sgn(x)\partial_{x}+\partial_{x}\sgn(x)}{2i}\right)=:2\gamma

where
\langle\gamma\rangle_{t}
is the Morawetz action.
If we formally commute the derivative through the nonsmooth function
\sgn(x)
, we have that

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\gamma=-i\sgn(x)\partial_{x}-i\delta(x)}

If
G(x)
is a function supported away from
x=0
, then the singularity at
x=0
does not contribute, and we have the identities

(32)

\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\gamma G=}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}-i\sgn(x)\partial_{x}G}\\
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}G\gamma=}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}-iG\sgn(x)\partial_{x}}\\
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}[\gamma,G]=}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}-i\sgn(x)G^{\prime}(x)}\end{split}

In particular,

(33)

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}[-i\Delta,G(|x|)]=-i\partial_{x}\sgn(x)G^{\prime}(|x|)-iG^{\prime}(|x|)\sgn(x)\partial_{x}=\gamma G^{\prime}(|x|)+G^{\prime}(|x|)\gamma}

For Schrödinger equations with purely repulsive interactions,
\langle D_{H}\gamma\rangle_{t}
has good positivity properties (at least in dimension greater than
3
), which allows us to control the solution in certain global space-time norms. Since we do not assume that the potential in (
1
) is repulsive, we cannot expect to derive estimates using
\langle D_{H}\gamma\rangle_{t}
. However, we are able to get estimates in the exterior of a time-dependent region, where the potential can be treated as an integrable perturbation.

3.1.
Basic estimates

Here, we collect some basic estimates, which we will refer to later in
Section

4
. We begin with two estimates on
\gamma u
in the region
|x|\sim t^{\beta}
:

Lemma 13
.

For
\beta\in(1/3,1)
, we have that

(34)

\left|\int_{0}^{T}\langle F^{\prime}(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\gamma F^{\prime}(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\rangle_{t}\;dt\right|\lesssim T^{\beta}

and

\sup_{T\in(t_{0},\infty]}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\left|\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{1}{t}\langle F^{\prime}(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\gamma F^{\prime}(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\rangle_{t}\;dt\right|\lesssim t_{0}^{\beta-1}}

Proof.

We begin by proving (
34
).
Let
G
be the bounded function given by
G(0)=0
and
G^{\prime}(x)=(F^{\prime}(x))^{2}
. Then, by (
30
),

(35)

\begin{split}\langle T^{\beta}G({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}T^{-\beta}}|x|{\color[rgb]{1,0,0})}\rangle_{T}=&\int_{0}^{T}\partial_{t}\langle t^{\beta}G({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}t^{-\beta}}|x|{\color[rgb]{1,0,0})}\rangle_{t}\;dt\\
{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}=}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\int_{0}^{T}\langle D_{H}\left(t^{\beta}G(t^{-\beta}|x|)\right)\rangle_{t}\;dt}\\
=&\int_{0}^{T}\langle\gamma G^{\prime}+G^{\prime}\gamma\rangle_{t}+\beta\langle t^{\beta-1}G\rangle_{t}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}-\beta}\langle\frac{|x|}{t}G^{\prime}\rangle_{t}\;dt\end{split}

where on the last line we have used (
33
). For notational simplicity, we will suppress the argument
(t^{-\beta}|x|)
of the functions
G
and
G^{\prime}
.
By the definition of
G^{\prime}
and
Lemma

10
,

(36)

\begin{split}\langle G^{\prime}\gamma+\gamma G^{\prime}\rangle_{t}=&\langle(F^{\prime})^{2}\gamma+\gamma(F^{\prime})^{2}\rangle_{t}\\
=&2\langle F^{\prime}\gamma F^{\prime}\rangle_{t}+\langle[F^{\prime},[F^{\prime},\gamma]]\rangle_{t}\\
=&2\langle F^{\prime}\gamma F^{\prime}\rangle_{t}\end{split}

where on the last line follows by using the third line of (
32
) to compute that

{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}[F^{\prime},\gamma]=-[\gamma,F^{\prime}]=i\sgn(x)t^{-\beta}F^{\prime\prime}}

is a function, and so commutes with
F^{\prime}
.
Inserting this into (
35
) and rearranging, we see that

\int_{0}^{T}2\langle F^{\prime}\gamma F^{\prime}\rangle_{t}\;dt=\langle T^{\beta}G(|x|\geq T^{\beta})\rangle_{T}+\int_{0}^{T}O(t^{\beta-1})\;dt

which gives
(
34
)
.

Turning to (
13
), we apply (
30
) to the propagation observable
t^{\beta-1}G(t^{-\beta}|x|)
to find that

\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\langle T^{\beta-1}G(T^{-\beta}|x|)\rangle_{T}-\langle t_{0}^{\beta-1}G(t_{0}^{-\beta}|x|)\rangle_{t_{0}}\lesssim}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{1}{t}\langle\gamma G^{\prime}(t^{-\beta}|x|)+G^{\prime}(t^{-\beta}|x|)\gamma\rangle_{t}\;dt}\\
&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}(\beta-1)t^{\beta-2}\langle G(t^{-\beta}|x|)\rangle_{t}+\beta t^{\beta-2}\left\langle\frac{|x|}{t^{\beta}}G^{\prime}(t^{-\beta}|x|)\right\rangle_{t}\;dt}\end{split}

Using (
36
) and rearranging, we find that

\begin{split}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\int_{t_{0}}^{T}\frac{2}{t}\langle F^{\prime}(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\gamma F^{\prime}(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\rangle_{t}\;dt=}&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\langle T^{\beta-1}G(T^{-\beta}|x|)\rangle_{T}-\langle t_{0}^{\beta-1}G(t_{0}^{-\beta}|x|)\rangle_{t_{0}}}\\
&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}(\beta-1)t^{\beta-2}\langle G(t^{-\beta}|x|)\rangle_{t}+\beta t^{\beta-2}\left\langle\frac{|x|}{t^{\beta}}G^{\prime}(t^{-\beta}|x|)\right\rangle_{t}\;dt}\\

4.
Bounds for localized nonradiative states

Now, we will prove
1
. Let us we will consider (
1
) with
|x|^{1/2}u_{0}\in L^{2}
, and suppose that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\langle F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\gamma F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\rangle_{t}=0

Recall that by
14
, we have that

(49)

\begin{split}\langle F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\gamma F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\rangle_{t}\lesssim&\int_{t}^{\infty}\Re\left\langle\frac{|x|}{s^{\beta+1}}F^{\prime}\gamma F\right\rangle_{s}+s^{-3\beta}\langle G(x/s^{\beta})\rangle_{s}\;ds\\
&-\int_{t}^{\infty}4s^{-\beta}\lVert\gamma\sqrt{F^{\prime}F}u(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}-\frac{s^{-\beta}}{p+1}\lVert\sqrt[p+1]{F^{\prime}F}u(s)\rVert_{L^{p+1}}^{p+1}\;ds\\
&+O(t^{1-\beta(\sigma+1)})\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\end{split}

where
G(x/s^{\beta})
is a bounded function supported in the region
|x|\sim s^{\beta}
.
Now,

F^{\prime}\gamma F=\sqrt{F^{\prime}F}\gamma\sqrt{F^{\prime}F}+\sqrt{F^{\prime}}\left([\gamma,\sqrt{F}]\sqrt{F^{\prime}}-[\gamma,\sqrt{F^{\prime}}]\sqrt{F}\right)\sqrt{F}

Since
[\gamma,f(x)]=-if^{\prime}(x)\sgn(x)
and
F,F^{\prime}>0
, we have that

\begin{split}\Re\left\langle\frac{|x|}{s^{\beta+1}}F^{\prime}\gamma F\right\rangle_{s}=&\Re\left\langle\frac{|x|}{s^{\beta+1}}\sqrt{F^{\prime}F}\gamma\sqrt{F^{\prime}F}\right\rangle_{s}\\
\leq&s^{-1}\lVert u(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}\lVert\gamma\sqrt{F^{\prime}F}u(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}\\
\leq&s^{-\beta}\lVert\gamma\sqrt{F^{\prime}F}u(s)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{C}{s^{2-\beta}}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\end{split}

For the second term, using the
L^{2}
conservation, we see that

s^{-3\beta}\langle G(x/s^{\beta})\rangle_{s}\lesssim s^{-3\beta}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

On the other hand,
G(x/s^{\beta})\lesssim\sqrt{\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{\prime}(x\geq s^{\beta})}
, so

\begin{split}s^{-3\beta}\langle G(x/s^{\beta})\rangle_{s}\lesssim&s^{\left(-3+\frac{2}{p+1}\right)\beta}\lVert\sqrt[p+1]{\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{\prime}(x\geq s^{\beta})}u\rVert_{L^{p+1}}^{2}\\
\lesssim&s^{-2\beta}\left(s^{-\beta}\int\tilde{F}\tilde{F}^{\prime}(x\geq s^{\beta})|u|^{p+1}\;dx\right)^{\frac{2}{p+1}}\end{split}

which is an improvement for
p<3,

\frac{p-1}{2(p+1)}<\beta<\frac{2}{p+1}
. Integrating the above inequalities and assuming that
\beta>\frac{p-1}{2(p+1)}
, we find that

(50)

\langle F\gamma F\rangle_{t}\lesssim t^{\beta-1}+\min(t^{\frac{p-1}{p+1}-2\beta},t^{1-3\beta})+t^{1-\beta(\sigma+1)}

Now, a quick calculation shows that

(51)

\partial_{t}\langle F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})|x|F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})\rangle_{t}=2\langle F\gamma\rangle_{t}+2\langle\sqrt{FF^{\prime}\frac{|x|}{t^{\beta}}}\gamma\sqrt{FF^{\prime}\frac{|x|}{t^{\beta}}}\rangle_{t}+\langle FF^{\prime}\frac{|x|^{2}}{t^{1+\beta}}\rangle_{t}

Thus, integrating in time and using
Lemma

13
, we see that

(52)

\langle F|x|F\rangle_{t}\lesssim t^{\beta}+\min(t^{\frac{2p}{p+1}-2\beta},t^{2-3\beta},t^{2-(\sigma+1)\beta})

For
p\geq 3
, this is optimized by choosing
\beta=\frac{1}{2}
, yielding

\langle F|x|F\rangle_{t}\lesssim t^{1/2}

On the other hand, if
p<3
and
\sigma\geq\frac{p+3}{p}

\beta=\frac{2p}{3(p+1)}<\frac{1}{2}
. Since

\langle|x|-F|x|F\rangle_{t}={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}\langle}(1-F^{2})|x|\rangle_{t}\lesssim t^{\beta}

this concludes the proof of
1

5.
Mass supercritical case: Construction of the free channel

Now, we will show how to construct the free channel wave operator

(53)

\Omega_{\text{free}}u_{0}:=\slim_{t\to\infty}e^{-it\Delta}J_{\textup{free}}(t)u(t)

for (
1

is defined to be

J_{\textup{free}}(t)=e^{it\Delta}F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})e^{-it\Delta}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})

In this section, we will prove the following result:

Theorem 18
.

For positive
\alpha
and
\delta
satisfying

(54)

\begin{split}\alpha<&\alpha_{0}:=\frac{(p-5)(p+2)}{4(p+1)}\\
\delta<&\min(1/2,\alpha)\end{split}

the limit defining the free channel wave operator (
53
) exists in the strong
H^{1}
sense.

Using Cook’s method, we can write

(55a)

\displaystyle\Omega_{\text{free}}u_{0}-e^{-i\Delta}J_{\textup{free}}(1)u(1)=

\displaystyle-i\slim\limits_{T\to\infty}\int_{1}^{T}\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}e^{-it\Delta}Vu(t)\;dt

(55b)

\displaystyle-i\slim\limits_{T\to\infty}\int_{1}^{T}\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}e^{-it\Delta}|u|^{p-1}u(t)\;dt

(55c)

\displaystyle+\slim\limits_{T\to\infty}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}(t)e^{-it\Delta}u(t)\;dt

where

\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}=F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})

To prove
18
, we must show that each of the integrals (
55a
)–(
55c
) converges as
T\to\infty
. We will show how to get convergence in
L^{2}
, and then sketch the modifications necessary to extend the convergence to
H^{1}
.

5.1.
The potential term

We begin by considering the potential term (
55a
). Let us write

\eqref{eqn:pot-err}=-i\slim_{T\to\infty}\int_{1}^{T}\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}e^{-it\Delta}F\left(|x|\geq t^{\beta}\right)Vu(t)\;dt\\
-i\slim_{T\to\infty}\int_{1}^{T}\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}e^{-it\Delta}F\left(|x|\leq t^{\beta}\right)Vu(t)\;dt

where

(56)

\max(\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{\sigma})<\beta<1-\delta

For the first term, the definition of
\beta
guarantees that
\lVert F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})V\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{x}}
is integrable in time, which allows us to control the integral. On the other hand, since
\delta<\min(1-\alpha,1/2)
, we can use
Lemma

5
to show that the integrand of the second term also decays at an integrable rate.

5.2.
The nonlinear term

We now turn to the nonlinear term (
55b
). As before, we decompose the interaction term into a piece close to the origin and a piece far from the origin:

\begin{split}e^{-it\Delta}J_{\textup{free}}|u|^{p-1}u=&\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}e^{-it\Delta}F(|x|\leq t^{\beta})|u|^{p-1}u\\
&+\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}e^{-it\Delta}F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})|u|^{p-1}u\end{split}

For the term localized near the origin, we can again use
Lemma

5
combined with the Sobolev embedding to conclude that

\lVert\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}e^{-it\Delta}F(|x|\leq t^{\beta})|u|^{p-1}u\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim t^{-2}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}^{p}

which is integrable in time. In the exterior region
|x|\geq t^{\beta}
, we have that

\begin{split}\lVert\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}e^{-it\Delta}F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})|u|^{p-1}u\rVert_{L^{2}}\lesssim&t^{-\frac{1}{2}(1-\alpha)}\lVert F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})|u|^{p-1}u\rVert_{L^{1}_{x}}\\
\lesssim&t^{-\frac{1}{2}(1-\alpha)}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\lVert\tilde{F}(|x|\geq t^{\beta})|u|^{2}\rVert_{L^{\infty}}^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\end{split}

where
\tilde{F}
is a cut-off function such that
\tilde{F}=1
on the support of
F
.

Replacing
F
by
\tilde{F}
in
17
, we have that
\tilde{F}|u|^{2}\in L^{\frac{4(p+1)}{p+3}}_{t}L^{\infty}_{x}
. Thus, the term in the exterior region will be integrable provided that

\frac{(p+3)(p-2)}{8(p+1)}+\frac{1-\alpha}{2}>1

which simplifies to

\alpha<\frac{(p-5)(p+2)}{4(p+1)}=\alpha_{0}

5.3.
The channel restriction error

Finally, we consider the term (
55c
) containing
\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}
. We can write

\displaystyle\eqref{eqn:J-err}=

\displaystyle\slim\limits_{T\to\infty}\int_{1}^{T}C_{1}(t)e^{-it\Delta}u(t)\;dt+\slim\limits_{T\to\infty}\int_{1}^{T}C_{2}(t)e^{-it\Delta}u(t)\;dt

\begin{split}C_{1}(t)=&\partial_{t}F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})\\

To start, we will show that the improper integral containing

exists by showing that it is Cauchy. Let

(57)

\begin{split}\biggl\lVert\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}C_{1}(t)e^{-it\Delta}u(t)\;dt\biggr\rVert_{L^{2}}=&\sup_{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{L^{2}}=1}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\left\langle\varphi,C_{1}(t)e^{-it\Delta}u(t)\right\rangle\;dt\\

Now, since

(58)

\begin{split}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\left\lVert\sqrt{\partial_{t}F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})}\varphi\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\;dt=&\left\langle\varphi,\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}\partial_{t}F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})\;dt\varphi\right\rangle\\

On the other hand, by considering the evolution of the propagation observable

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle B(t)\rangle_{t,\phi}=

\displaystyle\langle F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})\partial_{t}F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})\rangle_{t,\phi}

(59b)

\displaystyle+\langle\partial_{t}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})\rangle_{t,\phi}

(59c)

\displaystyle+\langle F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})\partial_{t}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})\rangle_{t,\phi}

(59d)

\displaystyle+\langle i[e^{-it\Delta}V(t)e^{it\Delta},B(t)]\rangle_{t,\phi}

(59e)

\displaystyle+\langle i[e^{-it\Delta}|u|^{p-1}e^{it\Delta},B(t)]\rangle_{t,\phi}

\begin{split}\langle e^{-it\Delta}V(t)e^{it\Delta}B(t)\rangle_{t,\phi}=&\langle e^{-it\Delta}V(t)e^{it\Delta}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})\phi(t),\phi(t)\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}\\

L^{1}_{t}(1,\infty)
. A similar argument also applies to
\langle B(t)e^{-it\Delta}V(t)e^{it\Delta}\rangle_{t,\phi}
, which shows that (
59d
) is time integrable. Similarly, for (
59e
), we write

\begin{split}\langle e^{-it\Delta}|u|^{p-1}e^{it\Delta}B(t)\rangle_{t,\phi}=&\langle e^{-it\Delta}|u|^{p-1}e^{it\Delta}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})\phi,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}\\
=&\langle F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})\phi,F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})e^{-it\Delta}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})|u|^{p-1}u\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}\\
\end{split}

and note that
F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})e^{-it\Delta}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})|u|^{p-1}u
was shown to be in
L^{1}_{t}L^{2}_{x}
in section
Section

5.2
, showing that (
59e
) is integrable. For (
59b
) and (
59c
), we observe that

\begin{split}\left\lVert\left[F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta}),F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})\right]\right\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\lesssim&t^{-\alpha+\delta}\\
\left\lVert\left[\partial_{t}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta}),F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})\right]\right\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\lesssim&t^{-1-\alpha+\delta}\\
\left\lVert\partial_{t}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})\right\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\lesssim t^{-1}\end{split}

Thus, when using the symmetrization formula, we find that

\eqref{eqn:crosstalk-1-b}+\eqref{eqn:crosstalk-1-c}=2\left\lVert\sqrt{F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})\partial_{t}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})}e^{-it\Delta}u(t)\right\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}+O(t^{-1-\alpha+\delta}\lVert u(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2})

is the sum of a positive term and an integrable term. Since (
59a
) is positive, combining these estimates shows that

(61)

\int_{1}^{\infty}\lVert\sqrt{\partial_{t}F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})e^{-it\Delta}u(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\;dt\lesssim\lVert u_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}

Thus, using Cauchy-Schwarz in (
57
) and using (
58
) and (
61
), we see that the integral containing
C_{1}(t)
is strongly Cauchy in time.

Since the argument for the integral containing
C_{2}(t)
is similar, we will sketch the necessary changes without going into detail. Using commutator estimates, we see that

C_{2}(t)=\partial_{t}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})+O_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}(t^{-1-\alpha+\delta})

The leading order term is essentially the same as
C_{1}(t)
, but with the roles of
F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})
and
F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})
swapped. A cursory examination shows that the previous argument goes through with only minor changes under this switch, so the integral containing
C_{2}(t)
also converges strongly in
L^{2}
.

5.4.
On the wave operator in
H^{1}

The above argument shows that the wave operator
\Omega_{\text{free}}
is well-defined in
L^{2}
whenever
u(t)
is bounded in
H^{1}
. Examining the proof, the
H^{1}
bound is only ever used to obtain the exterior
L^{\infty}
bound from
17
. As a result, we can show that
\partial_{x}\Omega_{\text{free}}u_{0}\in L^{2}
, since there are no problems with loss of regularity. We will sketch the changes necessary for this improvement. Applying Cook’s method, we obtain terms analogous to
eqs.

55a
,
55b
and
55c

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\Omega_{\text{free}}u_{0}=

\displaystyle e^{-i\Delta}J_{\textup{free}}(1)u(1)

(62a)

\displaystyle-i\slim\limits_{T\to\infty}\partial_{x}\int_{1}^{T}\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}e^{-it\Delta}Vu(t)\;dt

(62b)

\displaystyle-i\slim\limits_{T\to\infty}\partial_{x}\int_{1}^{T}\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}e^{-it\Delta}|u|^{p-1}u(t)\;dt

(62c)

\displaystyle+\slim\limits_{T\to\infty}\partial_{x}\int_{1}^{T}\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}(t)e^{-it\Delta}u(t)\;dt

It only remains to prove that these limits exist in
L^{2}
.

5.4.1.
The potential term

Examining the integrand of (
62a
), we find that

\begin{split}\partial_{x}(\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}e^{-it\Delta}Vu(t))=&[\partial_{x},\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}]e^{-it\Delta}Vu(t)\\
&+\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}e^{-it\Delta}V^{\prime}u(t)\\
&+\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}e^{-it\Delta}V\partial_{x}u(t)\end{split}

For the first term, we observe that the commutator
[\partial_{x},\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}]
has the same support properties as
\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}
and is smaller by a factor of
t^{-\alpha}
, so the argument from
Section

5.1
goes through with no changes. Since
V^{\prime}
has even better decay than
V
in the exterior region, the same is true for the second term. Finally, for the last term, we simply use the assumption of bounded energy instead of mass conservation when applying
Lemma

5
.

5.4.2.
The nonlinear term

Here, we have

\begin{split}\partial_{x}(\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}e^{-it\Delta}|u|^{p-1}u(t))=&[\partial_{x},\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}]e^{-it\Delta}|u|^{p-1}u(t)(t)\\
&+\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}e^{-it\Delta}\left(\frac{p+1}{2}|u|^{p-1}\partial_{x}u+\frac{p-1}{2}|u|^{p-3}u^{2}\partial_{x}\overline{u}\right)(t)\\
\end{split}

The commutator term can be handled using the same modifications discussed above, while for the second term, the argument from
Section

5.2
goes through provided we place the
\partial_{x}u
or
\partial_{x}\overline{u}
factor in
L^{2}
.

5.4.3.
The channel restriction term

Finally, we consider the channel restriction error term. We have

\begin{split}\eqref{eqn:J-err-deriv}=&[\partial_{x},\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}(t)]e^{-it\Delta}u(t)\\
&+F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})\partial_{t}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})\partial_{x}e^{-it\Delta}u(t)\\
&+\partial_{t}F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})e^{-it\Delta}\partial_{x}u(t)\\
\end{split}

For the first term, we observe that
\lVert[\partial_{x},\frac{d}{dt}\tilde{J}_{\text{free}}(t)]\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\lesssim t^{-1-\alpha}
, which is sufficient to place this term in
L^{1}_{t}
. For the second term, we note that
\partial_{t}F(|\xi|\geq t^{-\delta})
is supported in the region
|\xi|\sim t^{-\delta}
, so

\lVert\partial_{t}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})\partial_{x}\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\lesssim t^{-1-\delta}

which is integrable. Finally, for the third term, we can argue by duality to reduce the problem to showing that

\lVert\sqrt{\partial_{t}F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha}}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})\partial_{x}e^{-it\Delta}u(t)\rVert_{L^{2}_{x}}\in L^{2}_{t}

To show this, we perform a propagation estimate with respect to the observable

\partial_{x}B(t)\partial_{x}=\partial_{x}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})\partial_{x}

Since
\partial_{x}u
is bounded in
L^{2}
by assumption,
\langle\partial_{x}B(t)\partial_{x}\rangle_{t,\phi}
is bounded in time. Taking the Heisenberg derivative, we see that the argument for the terms
eqs.

59a
,
59b
and
59c
can be easily adapted by placing each of the derivatives on
\phi
. (Note that the nonperturbative terms will now be negative definite instead of positive definite due to the integration by parts.) For (
59d
), we see that

[\partial_{x}B(t)\partial_{x},e^{-it\Delta}Ve^{-it\Delta}]=[\partial_{x},e^{-it\Delta}Ve^{-it\Delta}]B(t)\partial_{x}+\partial_{x}B(t)[\partial_{x},e^{-it\Delta}Ve^{-it\Delta}]+\partial_{x}[B(t),e^{-it\Delta}Ve^{-it\Delta}]\partial_{x}

The last term requires no modification once we place the derivatives on the
\phi
’s, while the first two terms can also be handled similarly using the decay hypothesis on
\partial_{x}V
.

The term (
59e
) requires slightly more modification. As above, we have

\begin{split}[\partial_{x}B(t)\partial_{x},e^{-it\Delta}|u|^{p-1}e^{-it\Delta}]=&[\partial_{x},e^{-it\Delta}|u|^{p-1}e^{-it\Delta}]B(t)\partial_{x}+\partial_{x}B(t)[\partial_{x},e^{-it\Delta}|u|^{p-1}e^{-it\Delta}]\\
&+\partial_{x}[B(t),e^{-it\Delta}|u|^{p-1}e^{-it\Delta}]\partial_{x}\end{split}

We will focus on the first term, since the modifications for the other terms are either similar or easier. Here, we see that

[\partial_{x},-ie^{-it\Delta}|u|^{p-1}e^{-it\Delta}]=-ie^{-it\Delta}(\partial_{x}|u|^{p-1})e^{-it\Delta}

Thus, we calculate that

\begin{split}|\langle[\partial_{x},e^{-it\Delta}|u|^{p-1}e^{it\Delta}]B(t)\partial_{x}\rangle_{t,\phi}|=&|\langle e^{-it\Delta}(\partial_{x}|u|^{p-1})e^{-it\Delta}]B(t)\partial_{x}\phi,\phi\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}|\\
=&|\langle F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})\partial_{x}u(t),F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})e^{-it\Delta}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})(\partial_{x}|u|^{p-1})u\rangle_{L^{2}_{x}}|\end{split}

by the arguments used to control the nonlinear term,

\lVert F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})e^{-it\Delta}F(|D|\geq t^{-\delta})(\partial_{x}|u|^{p-1})u\rVert_{L^{1}_{t}L^{2}_{x}}<\infty

which completes the proof that the channel restriction error is integrable in
H^{1}
.

6.
Mass supercritical case: Behavior of the remainder

We now know that the part of the solution microlocalized to the support of
J_{\textup{free}}
behaves asymptotically like a free wave. In this section, we will show that the mass and energy of the rest of the solution can spread only slowly. In particular, we will show that:

Theorem 19
.

Let
\alpha
and
\delta
be the parameters introduced in
18
. Then, for any
\kappa
satisfying

(63)

\kappa>\max(1-\delta,\alpha,\beta)

we have

(64)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\lVert F(|x|\geq t^{\kappa})(I-J_{\textup{free}})u(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}=0

Similarly, for
\mu
satisfying

(65)

\mu>\beta

we have improved localization of energy given by

(66)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\lVert F(|x|\geq t^{\mu})(I-J_{\textup{free}})\partial_{x}u(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}=0

Remark 7
.

Note that (
63
) and (
65
) together with the bounds for the other parameters imply that
\kappa>1/2
and
\mu>\max(1/3,1/\sigma)
.

Fix
\kappa
and
\mu
satisfying (
63
) and (
65
). We introduce the decomposition
I-J_{\textup{free}}=J_{\text{low}}+J_{\text{rem}}
, where

(67)

\begin{split}J_{\text{low}}=&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}e^{it\Delta}}F(|{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}x}|\leq t^{\alpha}){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}e^{-it\Delta}}F(|D|\leq t^{-\delta})\\
J_{\text{rem}}=&{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}e^{it\Delta}}F(|{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}x}|\geq t^{\alpha}){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}e^{-it\Delta}}\end{split}

and write
u_{\text{low}}(t)=J_{\text{low}}(t)u(t)
,
u_{\text{rem}}=J_{\text{rem}}(t)u(t)
. Observe that the energy of
u_{\text{low}}(t)
goes to
0
as
t\to\infty
. Moreover, using
Lemma

6
and noting the restriction (
63
) on
\kappa
, we see that the mass of
u_{\text{low}}(t)
is concentrated in
|x|\leq t^{\kappa}
.
Thus, we have proved that

(68)

\lVert u_{\text{low}}(t)\rVert_{\dot{H}^{1}}+\lVert F(|x|\geq t^{1/2+})u_{\text{low}}(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}=o(1)

To complete the proof of
19
, it suffices to show that

(69)

\lVert F(|x|\geq t^{\kappa})u_{\text{rem}}(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}=o(1)

and

(70)

\lVert F(|x|\geq t^{\mu})\partial_{x}u_{\text{rem}}(t)\rVert_{L^{2}}={\color[rgb]{1,0,0}o(1)}

We will first show how to obtain the
L^{2}
bound, and then explain the argument for the
\dot{H}^{1}
bound.

6.1.
Slow spreading for
u_{\text{rem}}

6.1.1.
Preliminary decomposition

Recalling that
u_{\textup{rem}}=J_{\text{rem}}(t)u(t)
and using the Duhamel formula, we see that

(71)

u_{\text{rem}}(t)=e^{it\Delta}F(|x|\geq t^{\alpha})u_{0}-i\int_{0}^{t}e^{it\Delta}F(|x|\geq t^{\alpha})e^{-is\Delta}G(u(s))\;ds

where for notational convenience we define
G(u)=Vu+|u|^{p-1}u
. The term
e^{it\Delta}F(|x|\geq t^{\alpha})u_{0}
converges strongly to
0
in
H^{1}
, so it can be neglected. A similar argument shows that we can ignore the contribution to the Duhamel integral from
0<s<T
for any fixed parameter
T
. In addition, a quick calculation using the decay hypotheses for
V
and
17

shows that

F(|x|\geq t^{\beta})(Vu+|u|^{p-1}u)\in L^{4/3}_{t}W^{1,1}_{x}

so, by using the dual Strichartz estimates from
4

we find that

\lim_{T\to\infty}\sup_{t>T}\left\lVert\int_{T}^{t}e^{-is\Delta}{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}G}(u({\color[rgb]{1,0,0}s}))\;ds\right\rVert_{H^{1}}=0

Thus, we can write,

(72)

u_{\text{rem}}(t)=-i\int_{T}^{t}e^{it\Delta}F(|x|\geq t^{\alpha})e^{-is\Delta}F(|x|\leq s^{\beta}){\color[rgb]{1,0,0}G}(u(s))\;ds+R_{T}(t)

where
\lim_{T\to\infty}\sup_{t>T}\lVert R_{T}(t)\rVert_{H^{1}}=0
. On the other hand, for any
\psi\in H^{1}
,

\langle\psi,e^{-it\Delta}J_{\text{rem}}(t)u(t)\rangle=\langle e^{it\Delta}F(|x|\geq t^{\alpha})\psi,u(t)\rangle\to 0

so
\wlim_{t\to\infty}e^{-it\Delta}u_{\text{rem}}(t)=0
. It follows that we can write

(73)

\begin{split}u_{\text{rem}}(t)=&\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{it\Delta}\partial_{s}\left(e^{-is\Delta}J_{\text{rem}}(s)u(s)\right)\;ds\\
=&-\alpha\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{it\Delta}\frac{x}{s^{\alpha+1}}F^{\prime}(|x|\geq s^{\alpha})e^{-is\Delta}u(s)\;ds\\
&+i\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{it\Delta}F(|x|\geq s^{\alpha})e^{-is\Delta}(V+|u|^{p-1})u(s)\;ds\end{split}

where convergence for the integrals is understood in the weak sense. A straightforward adaptation of the arguments from
Section

5.3
shows that

\left\lVert\int_{t}^{\infty}\frac{x}{s^{\alpha+1}}F^{\prime}(|x|\geq s^{\alpha})e^{-is\Delta}u(s)\;ds\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}_{t}H^{1}_{x}}\lesssim\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}\left(1+\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1}}^{p-1}\right)

Thus, (
73
) can be rewritten as

(74)

\begin{split}u_{\text{rem}}(t)=&i\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{it\Delta}F(|x|\geq s^{\alpha})e^{-is\Delta}F(|x|\leq s^{\beta})G(u{\color[rgb]{1,0,0}(s)})\;ds+\tilde{R}(t)\end{split}

where
\tilde{R}(t)=o_{H^{1}}(1)
. The advantage of the representations (
72
) and (
74
) is that the remainders
R_{T}(t)
and
\tilde{R}(t)
are negligible in (
69
) and (
70
). Thus, in the discussion that follows we will drop the error terms in (
72
) and (
74
).

6.1.2.
An incoming/outgoing decomposition

To prove localization for
u_{\text{rem}}
, we will employ an incoming/outgoing decomposition. Let us define

(75)

\begin{split}P^{\text{out}}=&\mathds{1}_{x\geq 0}F(D\geq t^{-\lambda})+\mathds{1}_{x<0}F(-D\geq t^{-\lambda})\\
P^{\text{in}}=&\mathds{1}_{x\geq 0}F(-D\geq t^{-\lambda})+\mathds{1}_{x<0}F(+D\geq t^{-\lambda})\\
P^{\text{low}}=&F(|D|\leq t^{-\lambda})\end{split}

where

(76)

1-\kappa<\lambda<\min\left(\frac{1}{2},1-\alpha,1-\beta\right)

Remark 8
.

It is common in the literature to consider the alternative definitions

\displaystyle{P}^{\textup{out}}=

\displaystyle F(A)

\displaystyle{P}^{\textup{in}}=

\displaystyle 1-F(A)

where
A=\frac{1}{2}(x\cdot D+D\cdot x)
is the dilation operator and
F(x)
is (a possibly smoothed version of) a cut-off to
x>0

[
38
]
. At least formally, our incoming and outgoing operators can be written as

\displaystyle P^{\textup{out}}=

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{A>0}F(|D|\geq t^{-\lambda})

\displaystyle P^{\textup{in}}=

\displaystyle\mathds{1}_{A<0}F(|D|\geq t^{-\lambda})

Since we work in dimension one, the definition (
75
) allows us to avoid the technicalities associated with the operator calculus machinery. It is also essentially the same as the original definition given by Enss
[
20
]
.

By combining this decomposition with (
72
) and (
74
), we will establish the bounds (
69
).

Bounds for low frequencies

We begin by considering the
L^{2}
bound for
P^{\text{low}}u_{\textup{rem}}
. Using the representation (
72
), we see that

\begin{split}F(|x|\geq t^{\kappa})P^{\text{low}}u_{\textup{rem}}=&-i\int_{T}^{t}F(|x|\geq t^{\kappa})F(|D|\leq t^{-\lambda})e^{it\Delta}F(|x|\geq t^{\alpha})e^{-is\Delta}F(|x|\leq s^{\beta})G(u(s))\;ds\\
&+\{\textup{negligible terms}\}\end{split}

Note that we can write

e^{it\Delta}F(|x|\geq t^{\alpha})e^{-is\Delta}=e^{i(t-s)\Delta}-e^{it\Delta}F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})e^{-is\Delta}

so it suffices to establish the bounds

(77)

\begin{split}\int_{T}^{t}\lVert F(|x|\geq t^{\kappa})F(|D|\leq t^{-\lambda})e^{i(t-s)\Delta}F(|x|\leq s^{\beta})\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\;ds:=\mathrm{I}^{\text{low}}_{1}=&o(1)\\
\int_{T}^{t}\lVert F(|x|\geq t^{\kappa})F(|D|\leq t^{-\lambda})e^{it\Delta}F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\;ds:=\mathrm{I}^{\text{low}}_{2}=&o(1)\end{split}

We begin with
\mathrm{I}^{\text{low}}_{2}
. Observe that we can write

F(|x|\geq t^{\kappa})F(|D|\leq t^{-\lambda})e^{i(t-s)\Delta}F(|x|\leq s^{\beta})\phi=\int K^{\text{low}}_{2}(x,y)\phi(y)\;dy

where

K^{\text{low}}_{2}(x,y)=\frac{F(|x|\geq t^{\kappa})F(|y|\leq s^{\beta})}{2\pi}\int e^{-it\xi^{2}+(x-y)\xi}F(|\xi|\leq t^{-\lambda})\;d\xi

Integrating repeatedly by parts, we find that

\begin{split}K^{\text{low}}_{2}(x,y)=&\frac{F(|x|\geq t^{\kappa})F(|y|\leq s^{\beta})}{2\pi}\int e^{-it\xi^{2}+(x-y)\xi}\left(\partial_{\xi}\frac{-i}{2t\xi+(x-y)}\right)^{K}F(|\xi|\leq t^{-\lambda})\;d\xi\\
=&\sum_{a+b=K}C_{a,b}F(|x|\geq t^{\kappa})F(|y|\leq s^{\beta})\int e^{-i(t-s)\xi^{2}+(x-y)\xi}\frac{-i(s-t)^{a}}{\left(2t\xi+(x-y)\right)^{K+a}}t^{b\lambda}F^{(b)}(|\xi|\leq t^{-\lambda})\;d\xi\end{split}

Now, since
\kappa>\max(\beta,1-\lambda)
, we have that

|2t\xi+(x-y)|\sim|x|

so

\begin{split}|K^{\text{low}}_{2}(x,y)|\lesssim_{K}&\sum_{a+b=K}F(|x|\geq t^{\kappa})F(|y|\leq s^{\beta})\frac{t^{a}t^{(b-1)\lambda}}{|x|^{(K+a)}}\end{split}

It follows that

\int_{T}^{t}\lVert K^{\text{low}}_{2}(x,y)\rVert_{L^{2}_{x,y}}\;ds\lesssim_{K}\sum_{a,b=K}\frac{t^{a+1+(b-1)\lambda+\beta/2}}{t^{\kappa(K+a-1/2)}}

Since
\kappa>1/2>\lambda
, this expression vanishes as
t\to\infty
, establishing the second bound in (
77
). To prove the first bound, we observe that
\mathrm{I}^{\text{low}}_{1}
is associated to the integral kernel

\begin{split}K^{\text{low}}_{1}(x,y)=&\frac{F(|x|\geq t^{\kappa})F(|y|\leq t^{\alpha})}{2\pi}\int e^{-i(t-s)\xi^{2}+i(x-y)\xi}F(|\xi|\leq t^{-\lambda})\;d\xi\end{split}

Since
\kappa>\alpha
, a straightforward adaptation of the arguments used to control
K^{\text{low}}_{2}
shows that

\int_{T}^{t}\lVert K^{\text{low}}_{1}(x,y)\rVert_{L^{2}_{x,y}}\;ds=o(1)

as required.

Bounds for incoming waves

Now, we turn to the bounds for
P^{\text{in}}u_{\textup{rem}}
. Note that
P^{\text{in}}
projects to incoming waves. Heuristically, we should not expect to get incoming waves when evolving forward from data localized to near the origin, so we will again use the Duhamel representation from the past (
71
). Expanding out the relevant operators, we find that

\begin{split}F(|x|\geq t^{\kappa})P^{\text{in}}u_{\textup{rem}}=&-i\int_{T}^{t}F(x\geq t^{\kappa})F(-D\geq t^{-\lambda})e^{it\Delta}F(|x|\geq t^{\alpha})e^{-is\Delta}F(|x|\leq s^{\beta})G(u(s))\;ds\\
&+\{\text{symmetric and better terms}\}\\
&=-i\int_{T}^{t}F(x\geq t^{\kappa})F(-D\geq t^{-\lambda})e^{i(t-s)\Delta}F(|x|\leq s^{\beta})G(u(s))\;ds\\
&+i\int_{T}^{t}F(x\geq t^{\kappa})e^{it\Delta}F(-D\geq t^{-\lambda})F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})e^{-is\Delta}F(|x|\leq s^{\beta})G(u(s))\;ds\\
&+\{\text{symmetric and better terms}\}\\
&:=\mathrm{I}^{\text{in}}_{1}+\mathrm{I}^{\text{in}}_{2}+\{\text{symmetric and better terms}\}\\
\end{split}

The estimate for
\mathrm{I}^{\text{in}}_{1}
is similar to those used for
\mathrm{I}^{\text{low}}_{1}
and
\mathrm{I}^{\text{low}}_{2}
. Introducing the kernel
K^{\text{in}}_{1}
corresponding to the operator in
\mathrm{I}^{\text{in}}_{1}
, we see that

\begin{split}K^{\text{in}}_{1}(x,y)=&\frac{F(x\geq t^{\kappa})F(|y|\leq s^{\beta})}{2\pi}\int e^{-i(t-s)\xi^{2}+i(x-y)\xi}F(-\xi\geq t^{-\lambda})\;d\xi\\
=&\frac{F(x\geq t^{\kappa})F(|y|\leq s^{\beta})}{2\pi}\int e^{-i(t-s)\xi^{2}+i(x-y)\xi}\left(\partial_{\xi}\frac{i}{2(t-s)\xi-(x-y)}\right)^{K}F(-\xi\geq t^{-\lambda})\;d\xi\\
=&\sum_{a+b=K}C_{a,b}F(x\geq t^{\kappa})F(|y|\leq s^{\beta})\int e^{-i(t-s)\xi^{2}+i(x-y)\xi}\frac{i(t-s)^{a}}{(2(t-s)\xi-(x-y))^{K+a}}t^{b\lambda}F^{(b)}(-\xi\geq t^{-\lambda})\;d\xi\end{split}

Observing that
|2(t-s)\xi-(x-y)|\sim\max((t-s)|\xi|,|x|)
, we can calculate that

\begin{split}\lVert K^{\text{in}}_{1}(x,y)\rVert_{L^{2}_{x,y}}\lesssim&\sum_{a+b=K}C_{a,b}\lVert F(x\geq t^{\kappa})F(|y|\leq s^{\beta})\frac{(t-s)^{a-1}}{|2(t-s)t^{-\lambda}-(x-y)|^{K+a-1}}t^{b\lambda}\rVert_{L^{2}_{x,y}}\\
\lesssim&{}_{K}t^{(K-1)(\lambda-\kappa)}t^{\frac{\kappa+\beta}{2}}\end{split}

which can be made to decay arbitrarily fast in
t
, so
\mathrm{I}^{\text{in}}_{1}=o_{L^{2}}(1)
. To show
\mathrm{I}^{\text{in}}_{2}
goes to zero, we write the kernel for
F(x\geq t^{\kappa})e^{it\Delta}F(-D\geq t^{-\lambda})F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})
as

K^{\text{in}}_{2}=\frac{F(x\geq t^{\kappa})F(|y|\leq t^{\alpha})}{2\pi}\int e^{-it\xi^{2}+\xi(x-y)}F(-\xi\geq t^{-\lambda})\;d\xi

Since
\kappa>\alpha
, we see that
-2t\xi+(x-y)
is positive over the support of the integral, so integrating by parts as above, we find that
\lVert F(x\geq t^{\kappa})e^{it\Delta}F(-D\geq t^{-\lambda})F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}
decays rapidly in
t
. It follows that
\mathrm{I}^{\text{in}}_{2}\to 0
.

Bounds for outgoing waves

It only remains to control the outgoing part of
u_{\textup{rem}}
. Using the Duhamel representation from the future (
74
), we write

\begin{split}F(|x|\geq t^{\kappa})P^{\text{out}}u_{\textup{rem}}=&i\int_{t}^{\infty}F(x\geq t^{\kappa})F(D\geq t^{-\lambda})e^{it\Delta}F(|x|\geq s^{\alpha})e^{-is\Delta}F(|x|\leq s^{\beta})G(u)\;ds\\
&+\{\text{symmetric and better terms}\}\\
=&i\int_{t}^{\infty}F(x\geq t^{\kappa})F(D\geq t^{-\lambda})e^{i(t-s)\Delta}F(|x|\leq s^{\beta})G(u)\;ds\\
&-i\int_{t}^{\infty}F(x\geq t^{\kappa})F(D\geq t^{-\lambda})e^{it\Delta}F(|x|\leq s^{\alpha})e^{-is\Delta}F(|x|\leq s^{\beta})G(u)\;ds\\
&+\{\text{symmetric and better terms}\}\\
=:&\mathrm{I}^{\text{out}}_{1}+\mathrm{I}^{\text{out}}_{2}+\{\text{symmetric and better terms}\}\end{split}

For
\mathrm{I}^{\text{out}}_{1}
, we observe that the kernel for
F(x\geq t^{\kappa})F(D\geq t^{-\lambda})e^{i(t-s)\Delta}F(|x|\leq s^{\beta})
is

\begin{split}K^{\text{out}}_{1}(x,y)=&\frac{F(x\geq t^{\kappa})F(|y|\leq s^{\beta})}{2\pi}\int e^{-i(t-s)\xi^{2}+i\xi(x-y)}F(\xi\geq t^{-\lambda})\;d\xi\\
=&\sum_{a+b=K}C_{a,b}\frac{F(x\geq t^{\kappa})F(|y|\leq s^{\beta})}{2\pi}\int e^{-i(t-s)\xi^{2}+i\xi(x-y)}\frac{(t-s)^{a}t^{b\lambda}F^{(b)}(\xi\geq t^{-\lambda})}{2(s-t)\xi+(x-y))^{K+a}}\;d\xi\end{split}

Now, if
t\leq s\leq 2t
, then
s^{\beta}\ll t^{\kappa}
for
t\gg 1
, so

|2(s-t)\xi+(x-y)|\gtrsim t^{\kappa}

and by the same arguments as for
\mathrm{I}^{\text{in}}_{1}
, we find that

\int_{t}^{2t}\lVert F(x\geq t^{\kappa})F(D\geq t^{-\lambda})e^{i(t-s)\Delta}F(|x|\leq s^{\beta})\rVert_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}\;ds=o(1)

On the other hand, if
s>2t
, then
2(s-t)\xi\gtrsim st^{-\lambda}\gg s^{\beta}
since
1-\lambda>\beta
, and

|2(s-t)\xi+(x-y)|\gtrsim st^{-\lambda}

Hence, in this regime,

\begin{split}\lVert K^{\text{out}}_{1}(x,y)\rVert_{L^{2}_{x,y}}\lesssim_{K}\frac{s^{\beta/2}t^{(2K-3/2)\lambda}}{s^{K-1/2}}\end{split}

which is sufficient since
\lambda<1/2
. Turning to
\mathrm{I}^{\text{out}}_{2}
, we observe that by (
26a
),

\begin{split}F(D\geq t^{-\lambda})F(|x|\leq s^{\alpha})=F(D\geq t^{-\lambda})F(|x|\leq s^{\alpha})F(D\geq\frac{1}{100}t^{-\lambda})+O_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}(s^{-N})\end{split}

Thus, it suffices to obtain bounds on the operator
F(|x|\leq s^{\alpha})F(D\geq\frac{1}{100}t^{-\lambda})e^{-is\Delta}F(|x|\leq s^{\beta})
, which can be represented using the kernel

K^{\text{out}}_{2}(x,y)=\frac{F(|x|\leq s^{\alpha})F(|y|\leq s^{\beta})}{2\pi}\int e^{is\xi^{2}+i\xi(x-y)}F(\xi\geq\frac{1}{100}t^{-\lambda})\;d\xi

Since the derivative of the phase is bounded below by a multiple of
s\xi
under the assumptions that
\alpha+\lambda<1,\beta+\lambda<1
, we can integrate by parts to show that the operator decays rapidly in
s
.

6.1.3.
Improved
\dot{H}^{1}
control

We now sketch the modifications necessary to prove (
70
). Let us reintroduce the incoming/outgoing decomposition

\begin{split}\tilde{P}^{\text{out}}=&\mathds{1}_{x\geq 0}F(D\geq t^{-\nu})+\mathds{1}_{x<0}F(-D\geq t^{-\nu})\\
\tilde{P}^{\text{in}}=&\mathds{1}_{x\geq 0}F(-D\geq t^{-\nu})+\mathds{1}_{x<0}F(+D\geq t^{-\nu})\\
\tilde{P}^{\text{low}}=&F(|D|\leq t^{-\nu})\end{split}

where

(78)

0<\nu<\max(1/2,1-\alpha,\alpha,\beta,1-\beta,\mu)

Note that in general,
\nu\neq\lambda
. Evidently,

\lVert\tilde{P}_{\text{low}}\partial_{x}u_{\textup{rem}}\rVert_{L^{2}}\to 0

so we only need bounds on
\tilde{P}_{\text{in}}\partial_{x}u_{\textup{rem}}
and
\tilde{P}_{\text{out}}\partial_{x}u_{\textup{rem}}
. We first observe that the estimates for the incoming and outgoing waves continue to hold if we replace
\kappa
with
\mu
and
\lambda
with
\nu
. In fact, we only used the hypothesis that
\lambda>1-\kappa
to deal with low frequencies, and the hypothesis
\kappa>\alpha
was only used to control
\mathrm{I}_{2}^{\text{out}}
, where it can be replaced with the assumption
\nu<1-\alpha
. Moreover, when considering
\tilde{P}_{\text{in}}\partial_{x}u_{\textup{rem}}
, we obtain (1) terms where the
F(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})
multiplier is replaced by
F^{\prime}(|x|\leq t^{\alpha})t^{-\alpha}
and (2) terms where
G(u(s))
is replaced by
\partial_{x}G(u(s))
. Both types of terms have the same or better decay and localization (note that
\partial_{x}G(u(s))
and
G(u(s))
satisfy the same
L^{2}
bounds), so we only need to make minor modifications to the previous argument to prove (
70
).

7.
Proof of Theorem
2

We now optimize the parameters to show that the previous results imply
2
. Fix
\kappa>1/2
and
\mu>\max(\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{\sigma})
. Observe that we can choose
\alpha<\min(\alpha_{0},\kappa)
,
\delta<\min(1/2,\alpha)
and take

\min\left(\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{\sigma}\right)<\beta<\min(\kappa,\mu)

and the hypotheses for (
54
) and (
56
) are satisfied. Thus, by
18
, we can write

u(t)=e^{it\Delta}u_{+}+u_{\textup{loc}}(t)

where
u_{+}=\Omega u_{0}
and

(79)

u_{\textup{loc}}(t)=u(t)-e^{it\Delta}u_{+}=(I-J_{\textup{free}})u(t)+e^{it\Delta}(e^{-it\Delta}J_{\textup{free}}u(t)-u_{+})

By the definition of
u_{+}
, the second term in (
79
) goes to
0
in
H^{1}
, while the first term obeys the bounds (
64
) and (
66
) by
19
, which implies
2
.
