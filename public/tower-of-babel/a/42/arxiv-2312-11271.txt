Active flux for triangular meshes for compressible flows problems
Rémi Abgrall, Jianfang Lin, Yongle Liu

Abstract
In this article, we show how to construct a numerical method for solving hyperbolic problems, whether linear or non-linear, using a continuous representation of the variables and their mean value in each triangular element. This type of approach has already been introduced by Roe, and others, in the multi-dimensional framework under the name of Active Flux, see [ 11 , 12 , 10 , 16 , 7 ] . Here, the presentation is more general and follows [ 4 , 6 ] . Various examples show the good behavior of the method in both linear and non-linear cases, including non-convex problems. The expected order of precision is obtained in both the linear and non-linear cases. This work represents a step towards the development of methods in the spirit of virtual finite elements for linear or non-linear hyperbolic problems, including the case where the solution is not regular.



1
Introduction

A new numerical method for solving hyperbolic problems was first introduced in
[
11
,
12
,
10
]
. This new method uses the degrees of freedom (DoFs) of the quadratic polynomials, which are all on the boundary of the elements, and another degree of freedom that used is the cell average of the solution. This leads to a potentially third-order method, with a globally continuous approximation. The time integration relies on a back-tracing of characteristics. This has been extended to square elements, in a finite difference fashion, in
[
16
,
7
]
. In
[
4
]
, a different, though very close, point of view is followed. The time stepping relies on a standard Runge-Kutta time stepping, and several versions of the same problem can be used without preventing local conservation. More precisely, a conservative version of the studied problem is used to update the average value and the local conservation property is guaranteed. In addition, we have more flexibility for the DoFs on the boundary of the elements. Namely, we can use several versions of the same problem, such as the primitive formulation, to update these DoFs. This is benefit from the fact that the point values are free of the local conservation and we do not need to restricted by the conservation formulation. These routes have been followed in
[
6
]
which gives several versions of higher order approximation, in one dimension.

The purpose of this paper is to extend the work of
[
4
]
to several dimensions, using simplex elements. We first describe the approximation space, then propose a discretization method, with some justifications, and then show numerical examples. We also show how to handle discontinuous solutions.

The hyperbolic problems we consider are written in a conservative form as:

\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}+\text{ div }{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{u})=0,\quad\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},

(1)

with
d=2
, complemented by initial conditions

\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x},0)=\mathbf{u}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,

and boundary conditions that will be described case-by-case. The solution
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
must stay in an open subspace
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
so that the flux
{\mathbf{f}}
is defined. This is the invariant domain.

Two sets of problems are considered:

•

the case of scalar conservation law with
{\mathbf{f}}
linear taking the form of
{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{a}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{u}
or non-linear. In that case,
\mathbf{u}
is a function with values in
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathcal{D}=\mathbb{R}
but the solution must stay in
[\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{u}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\max\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{u}_{0}(\mathbf{x})]
, due to Kruzhkov’s theory: in these particular examples, we set
\mathcal{D}=[\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{u}_{0}(\mathbf{x}),\max\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbf{u}_{0}(\mathbf{x})]
.

•

the case of the Euler equations, where
\mathbf{u}=(\rho,\rho\mathbf{v},E)
,
\rho
is the density,
\mathbf{v}
is the velocity, and
E
is the total energy, i.e., the sum of the internal energy
e
and the kinetic energy
\tfrac{1}{2}\rho\mathbf{v}^{2}
. The flux is

{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{u})=\begin{pmatrix}\rho\mathbf{v}\\
\rho\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{v}+p\text{Id}_{d}\\
(E+p)\mathbf{v}\end{pmatrix}

with
\text{Id}_{d}
being a
d\times d
identity matrix.
We have introduced the pressure that is related to the internal energy and the density by an equation of state. Here we make the choice of a perfect gas,

p=(\gamma-1)e

with
\gamma
denoting the specific heat ratio. In all of the numerical examples tested in §
4.2
, we have taken
\gamma=1.4
.
In this example,
\mathcal{D}=\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{4},\rho>0,e>0\}
.

We note that for smooth solutions, (
1
) can be re-written in a non-conservative form,

\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}+\mathbf{J}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}=0,

where

\mathbf{J}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}=A\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial x}+B\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial y},

with
A
and
B
being the Jacobians of the flux in the
x
- and
y
- direction, respectively. The system is hyperbolic, i.e., for any vector
{\mathbf{n}}=(n_{x},n_{y})
, the matrix

\mathbf{J}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}=An_{x}+Bn_{y}

is diagonalisable in
\mathbb{R}
.

2
Approximation space

For now, we consider a triangle
E
and the corresponding quadratic polynomial approximation. The degrees of freedom are the vertices
\sigma_{i},i=1,\ldots 3
, the midpoints
\sigma_{i},i=4,\ldots 6
, and the average, see Figure
1
. The first question whether we can find a polynomial space
V
that contains at least the quadratic polynomials, and something more, in order to accommodate the average value as an independent variable, that is if we can find
7
polynomials (
\varphi_{i}
) such that

\varphi_{i}(\sigma_{j})=\delta_{i}^{j}~\text{ for }~1\leq i,j\leq 6,\quad\int_{E}\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})\;{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\rm d}}\mathbf{x}=0~\text{ for }~i=1,\ldots,6,

(2)

and

\varphi_{7}(\sigma_{j})=0,\quad\int_{E}\varphi_{7}(\mathbf{x})\;\rm d\mathbf{x}=|E|.

(3)

An obvious choice is
\varphi_{7}=60\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}
with
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3})
being the barycentric coordinates and
\varphi_{i}=
the Lagrange basis function
+\beta_{i}\varphi_{7}
with
\beta_{i}
well chosen.

More precisely, the quadratic Lagrange polynomials are

\varphi_{i}^{L}(\mathbf{x})=\lambda_{i}(2\lambda_{i}-1)
,
i=1,2,3
and
\varphi^{L}_{4}=4\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}
,
\varphi_{5}^{L}=4\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}
and
\varphi_{6}^{L}=4\lambda_{1}\lambda_{3}
. Since

\int_{E}\varphi_{i}^{L}\;{\rm d}\mathbf{x}=0,

for
i=1,2,3
, we set
\varphi_{i}=\varphi_{i}^{L}
. Similarly, we see that we can set

\varphi_{i}=\varphi_{i}^{L}-\frac{1}{3}\varphi_{7},

for
i=4,5,6.
It is trivial to verify that the above-mentioned polynomials
\varphi_{i}
satisfy the conditions (
2
) and (
3
). Therefore, these polynomials serve as the quadratic basis functions of the polynomial space
V
.

The same idea can be extended to tetrahedrons and higher than quadratic degree. For example, we can get a cubic approximation by taking

9
Lagrange points with barycentric coordinates
(\tfrac{i}{3},\tfrac{j}{3},\tfrac{k}{3})
with
i+j+k={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}3}
and at least one among the
\{i,j,k\}
being set to
0
so that we are on the boundary of the triangle
E
, and the average value. Hence the polynomials are:

\varphi_{10}=60\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3},

and

\varphi_{i}=\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{i}(3\lambda_{i}-1)(3\lambda_{i}-2)-2\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3},\quad\text{ for }\quad i=1,2,3,

then

\varphi_{4}=\frac{9}{2}\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}(3\lambda_{1}-1)-\frac{9}{2}\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3},

\varphi_{5}=\frac{9}{2}\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}(3\lambda_{2}-1)-\frac{9}{2}\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3},

and the others
\{\varphi_{6},\varphi_{7},\varphi_{8},\varphi_{9}\}
are obtained by symmetry.

Our notations will be
\mathbf{u}_{\sigma}
for the point values and
\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\mathbf{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{E}
for the cell average. Finally, the solution
\mathbf{u}
can be approximated in each element by

\mathbf{u}\approx\sum_{i=1}^{6}\mathbf{u}_{\sigma_{i}}\varphi_{i}+\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\mathbf{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{E}\varphi_{7},

(4)

for the third-order approximation and

\mathbf{u}\approx\sum_{i=1}^{9}\mathbf{u}_{\sigma_{i}}\varphi_{i}+\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\mathbf{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{E}\varphi_{10},

(5)

for the fourth-order approximation.

3
Numerical schemes

In this section, we describe how to evolve in time the degrees of freedom. We use the method of line, with a SSP Runge-Kutta (third-order) in time. In the following, we describe the spatial discretization.
We first describe a high-order version, and then a first-order one.

3.1
High order schemes

The update of the average values is done by using the conservative formulation, i.e.,

\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}+\text{div}{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{u})=0,

(6)

both the conservative and a non-conservative version of the same system

\dfrac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial t}+\mathbf{J}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}=0.

(7)

On a triangle
E
,
\mathbf{u}
is represented by point values at the Lagrange DoFs and its average on the element. The function
\mathbf{u}
is a quadratic or cubic polynomial on each element, so that
the update is done via:

|E|\dfrac{{\rm d}\bar{\mathbf{u}}_{E}}{{\rm d}t}+\oint_{\partial E}{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{u})\cdot{\mathbf{n}}\;\rm d\ell=0,

(8)

where the integral is obtained by using quadrature formula on each edge. In our simulations, we use a Gaussian quadrature formula with
3
Gaussian points on each edge, so that there is no loss of accuracy.

The update of the boundary values is more involved and we describe several solutions.
Inspired by Residual Distribution schemes, and in particular the LDA (Low Diffusion advection A) scheme
[
9
]
1
1

1

The LDA scheme (LDA standing for Low Diffusion advection A scheme, there were a B and C version) is a scheme specialized to steady advection problems on triangular meshes for scalar problems. Point value residual are defined to update the solution in a way similar as we do here. It is locally conservative, high-order and upwind but does not prevent spurious oscillations at discontinuities. It is the second order in its original version. The accuracy is obtained by defining the residual at this point as the product of the upwind parameter with
J\cdot\nabla u
. The upwind property is obtained by looking at the sign of the scalar product of local velocity (hence
J
for a scalar problem) against the normal opposite to the vertex for which we evaluate the residual. Finally local conservation is guaranteed by multiplying the upwind parameter by a scaling factor, the so-called
N
parameter, so that the sum or the fluctuations over the element is equal to
\int_{E}J\cdot\nabla u\,d\mathbf{x}
. The
J
Jacobians are evaluated so that this integral is equal to the integral over the boundary of the normal flux, by some generalization of the Roe average. An extension for systems was defined later in van der Weide’s PhD thesis
[
22
]
, see also
[
23
]
.
, one can define, for each of the polynomial DoFs
\sigma\in\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots\}
over the element
E
,

\Phi_{\sigma}^{E}(\mathbf{u})=\big(K^{E}_{\sigma}\big)^{+}N_{\sigma}\big(\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{u}_{\sigma})\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}\big),\quad N_{\sigma}^{-1}=\sum_{\sigma\in E}\big(K^{E}_{\sigma}\big)^{+},

where, since the problem is hyperbolic and whatever the vector
{\mathbf{n}}_{\sigma}^{E}
, one can take the positive and negative parts of
K_{\sigma}^{E}

K^{E}_{\sigma}=(K^{E}_{\sigma})^{-}+(K^{E}_{\sigma})^{+}=R\Lambda R^{-1}=R\Lambda^{-}R^{-1}+R\Lambda^{+}R^{-1},

with

\displaystyle\Lambda^{-}

\displaystyle=\diag(\min(\lambda_{1},0),\dots,\min(\lambda_{m},0)),

\displaystyle\Lambda^{+}

\displaystyle=\diag(\max(\lambda_{1},0),\dots,\max(\lambda_{m},0)),

\displaystyle K^{E}_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{u}_{\sigma})\cdot{\mathbf{n}}_{\sigma}^{E}.

This normal vector, see Figure
1
, is defined as follows:

•

for the quadratic approximation where we have used the DoFs of quadratic polynomials on the edges of the element, if
\sigma
is a vertex,
{\mathbf{n}}_{\sigma}
is the inward normal to the opposite edge of
\sigma
, if
\sigma
is a mid point, this is the outward normal to the edge where
\sigma
is sitting.

•

for the cubic approximation where we have used the DoFs of cubic polynomials on the edges of the element, we apply the same procedure, described in the above case of quadratic approximation, to the vertices and the other DoFs on edges.

These two procedures can be reduced to the following. We notice that a degree of freedom belongs to two edges, the one sitting on its left and the one sitting on its right. These two edges can be identical. We then define the normal by adding the scaled normals of the outward normals to these edges.

Once this is done, we update
\mathbf{u}
by the following

\dfrac{\rm d\mathbf{u}_{\sigma}}{\rm dt}+\sum\limits_{E,\sigma\in E}\Phi_{\sigma}^{E}=0.

The idea behind is to have some up-winding mechanism,
however, the consistency is not clear: if we have a linear
\mathbf{u}
, i.e., with
\nabla\mathbf{u}
constant, we would like to recover

\sum\limits_{E,\sigma\in E}\Phi_{\sigma}^{E}=\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{u}_{\sigma})\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}.

A priori, there is no reason for this. Hence a better idea is:

Figure 1:
Normals for (
9
). Left: quadratic; Right: cubic.

\dfrac{{\rm d}\mathbf{u}_{\sigma}}{{\rm d}t}+\sum\limits_{E,\sigma\in E}\Phi_{\sigma}^{E}(\mathbf{u})=0,

(9a)

with

\Phi_{\sigma}^{E}(\mathbf{u})=N_{\sigma}\big(K_{\sigma}^{E})^{+}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{u}_{\sigma})\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}(\sigma),

(9b)

K_{\sigma}^{E}=\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{u}_{\sigma})\cdot{\mathbf{n}}_{\sigma}^{E}\quad\text{and}\quad N_{\sigma}^{-1}=\sum\limits_{E,\sigma\in E}\big(K_{\sigma}^{E}\big)^{+}.

(9c)

Consistency is obvious. From
[
1
]
, one can see that the matrix
N_{\sigma}
exists for a symmetrizable system. A slightly different version is

\Phi_{\sigma}^{E}(\mathbf{u})=N_{\sigma}\text{ sign}\big(K_{\sigma}^{E})\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{u}_{\sigma})\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}(\sigma),\qquad N_{\sigma}^{-1}=\sum\limits_{E,\sigma\in E}\text{sign}\big(K_{\sigma}^{E}\big).

The numerical results are identical, and we will use the version (
9c
).

3.2
Low order schemes

For the average value, one can simply take a standard finite volume (so we do not use the boundary values). We have taken the Lax-Friedrichs numerical flux and the Roe scheme in our simulations.

The real problem is what to do with the point values.
A first-order version

Geometry for the first-order scheme. Left: quadratic; Right: cubic.

can be defined as follows. We first, and temporarily for the definition of sub-elements only, re-number the boundary nodes. We pick one degree of freedom, call it
b_{1}
, and list the others in a counter-clockwise manner: we get the list
\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{N-1}\}
of all boundary degrees of freedom. The centroid is denoted by
b_{N}
, with
N=7
for the quadratic approximation, and
N=10
for the cubic one. Then we define the sub-elements of vertices
\{b_{i},b_{N},b_{i+1}\}_{i=1,\dots,N-2}
and
\{b_{N-1},b_{N},b_{1}\}
with a numbering
b_{i}
modulo
N
. This defines
N-1
sub-elements that we denote by
T^{E}_{i}
, then we get inward normals
{\mathbf{n}}_{b_{i}}
. For quadratic elements, and using back the original numbering, this is illustrated in Figure
2
. In what follows, we use the original numbering with this geometrical definition of sub-elements.

The update of
\mathbf{u}_{\sigma}
is done with (
9a
) where
\Phi_{\sigma}^{E}
needs to be modified. We use

\Phi_{\sigma}^{E}=\frac{1}{|C_{\sigma}|}\sum_{T^{E}_{i},\sigma\in T^{E}_{i}}\Phi_{\sigma}^{T^{E}_{i}}(\mathbf{u}).

(10)

The residuals will be defined the following, which is done to get monotone first-order residual distribution schemes, see
[
9
,
2
]
for example. We will use two types of residuals:

•

a version that is inspired by the Local Lax-Friedrichs scheme

\Phi_{\sigma}^{T^{E}_{i}}=\frac{1}{3}\oint_{T^{E}_{i}}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}\;{\rm d}\mathbf{x}+\alpha_{T^{E}_{i}}\big(\mathbf{u}_{\sigma}-\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{T^{E}_{i}}\big)

with

\oint_{T^{E}_{i}}\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{u})\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}\;{\rm d}\mathbf{x}\approx|T^{E}_{i}|\mathbf{J}(\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{T^{E}_{i}})\nabla\mathbf{u}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\mathbf{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{T^{E}_{i}}}),

(11)

where we use the
\mathbb{P}^{1}
approximation on
T^{E}_{i}
and

\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{T^{E}_{i}}
is the arithmetic average of the
\mathbf{u}
’s at three vertices of
T^{E}_{i}
(hence we use the average value here). Last,

\alpha_{T^{E}_{i}}\geq\max\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sigma\in T^{E}_{i},}\\
{\mathbf{n}}_{b_{i}}\text{normals of~}T^{E}_{i}\end{subarray}}\rho\big(\mathbf{J}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbf{u}_{\sigma}})\cdot{\mathbf{n}}_{b_{i}}\big),

where
\rho(B)
is the spectral radius of the matrix
B
.

•

a version that is inspired by the finite volume scheme re-written in the residual distribution framework, see
[
3
]
for details. We have used the Roe scheme with Harten-Yee entropy fix.

We still need to define
|C_{\sigma}|
. It is

|C_{\sigma}|=\sum_{E,\sigma\in E}\sum_{T^{E}_{i},\sigma\in T^{E}_{i}}\frac{|T^{E}_{i}|}{3},

and after some simple calculations, we see that

|C_{\sigma}|=\sum_{E,\sigma\in E}\frac{2|E|}{3{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}(N-1)}}.

The low-order version can easily be shown to be invariant domain preserving under some CFL conditions. For example, the Local Lax-Friedrichs version needs that

3\frac{\Delta t}{|T^{E}_{i}|}\rho\big(\mathbf{J}(\mathbf{u}_{\sigma})\cdot{\mathbf{n}}_{b_{i}}\big)\leq 1,\qquad\forall E,i

and this condition turns out to be much more restrictive than the one we can use in practice.

3.3
Linear stability

Linear stability is not easy to study when the mesh has no particular symmetry, and when the scheme under consideration does not satisfy a variational principle. In the one dimensional case, it is possible to show that the scheme developed in
[
4
]
is linearly stable: this has been done by W. Barsukow and reported in
[
6
]
using
[
21
]
. However, the scheme (
9
), adapted in 1D, would not be exactly the same as in
[
4
]
. So we have conducted an experimental study on several types of meshes generated by GMSH
[
14
]
with several types of meshing option (frontal Delaunay, Delaunay). The all proved to be stable for the linear and non-linear problems tested above, with a CFL halved with respect to the standard value for the quadratic approximation and divided by 3 for the cubic approximation. This is more or less what is expected because for the quadratic (resp. cubic) approximation, the density of degrees of freedom is approximately doubled (resp. tripled).

For the first-order approximation, using the Rusanov scheme, it is easy to get a CFL condition under which the solution stays in its domain of dependence.

3.4
Non-linear stabilization

When the solution develops discontinuities, the high-order scheme will be prone to numerical oscillations. To overcome this, we have used a simplified version of the MOOD paradigm in
[
8
,
24
]
. Since point values and average are independent variables, we need to test both.

The idea is to work with several schemes ranging from order
p=3\text{ or }4
to
1
, with the lowest order one able to provide results staying in the invariance domain. For the element
E
, we write the scheme for
\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\mathbf{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{E}
as
S_{E}(k)
and for
\mathbf{u}_{\sigma}
as
S_{\sigma}(k)
.

We denote by
\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\mathbf{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}^{n}_{E}
and
\mathbf{u}_{\sigma}^{n}
the solution at the time
t_{n}
. After each Runge-Kutta cycle, the updated solution is denoted by
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{E}
,
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{\sigma}
.

We first run the full order scheme, for each Runge-Kutta cycle. Concerning the average
\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\mathbf{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{E}
, we store the flux
\int_{\Gamma}{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{u}^{h})\cdot{\mathbf{n}}
for reasons of conservation. Then, for the average,

1.

Computer Admissible Detector (CAD in short): We check if
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{E}
is a valid vector with real components: we check if each component is not NaN. Else, we flag the element and go to the next one in the list.

2.

Physically Admissible Detector (PAD in short): We check if
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{E}\in\mathcal{D}
. If this is false, the element is flagged, and go to the next one in the list.

3.

Then we check if at
t_{n}
, the solution is not a constant in the elements used in the numerical stencil (so we check and compare between the average
\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\mathbf{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{E}
and point values
\mathbf{u}_{\sigma}
in
E
with these in the elements sharing a face with
E
). This is done in order to avoid to detect a fake maximum principle. If the solution is declared locally constant, we move to the next element.

4.

Discrete Maximum Principle (DMP in short): we check if
\tilde{\mathbf{u}}_{E}
is a local extrema. if we are dealing with the Euler equations, we compute the density and the pressure and perform this test on these two values only, even though for a system this is not really meaningful. We denote by
\xi
the functional on which we perform the test (i.e.,
\mathbf{u}
itself for scalar problems, and the density/pressure obtained from
V(E)
: the set of elements that share a face or a vertex with
E
, excluding
E
itself).
We say we have a potential extrema if

\xi_{E}^{n+1}\not\in[\big(\min\limits_{F\in V(E)}\xi_{F}^{n}\big){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-}\varepsilon_{E}^{n},\big(\max\limits_{F\in V(E)}\xi_{F}^{n}\big){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}+}\varepsilon_{E}^{n}],

where
\varepsilon_{E}^{n}
is estimated as in
[
8
]
.
If the test is wrong, we go to the next element, else the element is flagged.

If an element is flagged, then each of its faces are flagged, and re-compute the flux of the flagged faces using the first-order scheme.

For the point values, the procedure is similar, and then for the flagged degrees of freedom, we re-compute

\sum\limits_{E,\sigma\in E}\Phi_{\sigma}^{E}(\mathbf{u}^{n})
with the first-order scheme.

4.2.1
Accuracy test

In the first example of non-linear system cases, we verify the order of convergence of the proposed scheme. To this end, we consider a smooth stationary isentropic vortex flow. The computational domain is
[-10,10]\times[-10,10]
with Dirichelet boundary conditions everywhere. The initial condition is given by

\rho=(1+\delta T)^{\frac{1}{\gamma-1}},\quad u=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}x_{2}e^{\frac{1-r^{2}}{2}},\quad v=\frac{\varepsilon}{2\pi}x_{1}e^{\frac{1-r^{2}}{2}},\quad p=\rho^{\gamma},

(15)

where the vortex strength is
\varepsilon=5
and
\delta T=-\frac{(\gamma-1)\varepsilon^{2}}{8\gamma\pi^{2}}e^{1-r^{2}}

. This is a stationary equilibrium of the system so the exact solution coincides with the initial condition at any time. The proposed scheme is not able to exactly preserve this equilibrium state and the truncation error is
\mathcal{O}(h^{p})
, where
h
is the characteristic size of the triangular mesh and
p
is the order of the scheme. Thus, it severs as a good numerical example to verify the order of convergence, see also, e.g. in
[
13
,
5
]
.

5
Conclusions

We have presented a computational method inspired by the so-called Active Flux method, following the ideas developed in

[
4
]
and
[
6
]
, and using triangular type meshes. The method is able to capture shocks. We have illustrated the behavior of this method on several cases with smooth and discontinuous problems and models ranging from scalar to systems. The behavior of the method is as expected.

Appendix A
Boundary conditions for the Euler equations

We have considered two type of boundary condition on
\partial\Omega
where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
: wall boundary conditions and inflow/outflow.
We describe how they are implemented for the update of the average values and the point values.

A.1
Average values

Inside the domain,
\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\mathbf{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{E}
will satisfy:

|E|\dfrac{{\rm d}\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\mathbf{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{E}}{{\rm d}t}+\oint_{\partial E}\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{u}^{h},\mathbf{u}^{-})\;{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\rm d}\ell}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}|E|\dfrac{{\rm d}\hbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.5pt\kern 1.72218pt\hbox{\kern-0.50003pt$\mathbf{u}$\kern 0.0pt}}}_{E}}{{\rm d}t}+}\sum_{\Gamma,\text{edge of }E}\int_{\Gamma}\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{u}^{h},\mathbf{u}^{-})\;{\rm d}\Gamma=0,

where the numerical flux is the continuous one for the high-order scheme and a standard numerical flux for the first-order case. Here,
\mathbf{u}^{h}
is the value of
\mathbf{u}
on the boundary of
\partial E
,
\mathbf{u}^{-}
is the value on the opposite side of the element, and
{\mathbf{n}}
is the local normal.

If one edge
\Gamma
is contained in
\partial\Omega
, we modify

\int_{\Gamma}\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{u}^{h},\mathbf{u}^{-})\;{\rm d}\ell\approx|\Gamma|\sum_{\text{ quadrature points }}\omega_{q}\hat{\mathbf{f}}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{u}^{h}(\mathbf{x}_{q}),\mathbf{u}^{-}(\mathbf{x}_{q}))

evaluated by a quadrature rule. We use a Gaussian quadrature with 3 points. The following cases are considered:

•

for wall, by setting the state
\mathbf{u}^{-}(\mathbf{x}_{q})
equal to the state obtained from
\mathbf{u}^{h}(\mathbf{x}_{q})
by symmetry with respect to
\Gamma
, i.e., same density, same pressure, but the velocity
\mathbf{v}
is

\mathbf{v}^{-}(\mathbf{x}_{q})=\mathbf{v}^{h}(\mathbf{x}_{q})-2\frac{\langle\mathbf{v}^{h}(\mathbf{x}_{q}),{\mathbf{n}}\rangle}{\|{\mathbf{n}}\|^{2}}{\mathbf{n}}.

•

for inflow/outflow, since we want to impose
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}_{\infty}
weakly, we take

\mathbf{u}^{-}=\mathbf{u}^{\infty}
, and the flux is some upwind flux (the Roe one for the numerical experiments).

•

for supersonic inflow/outflows or if the solution stays constant in a neighborhood of the boundary, we will impose Neumann condition, i.e., we force the solution to stay constant.

A.2
Point values

It is a bit more involved.
Let us remind that the update of the point values is made by (
9a
)

\dfrac{{\rm d}\mathbf{u}_{\sigma}}{{\rm d}t}+\sum\limits_{E,\sigma\in E}\Phi_{\sigma}^{E}(\mathbf{u})=0,

(17)

that we have to modify into

\dfrac{{\rm d}\mathbf{u}_{\sigma}}{{\rm d}t}+\sum\limits_{E,\sigma\in E}\Phi_{\sigma}^{E}(\mathbf{u})+\sum_{\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega,\sigma\in\Gamma}\Phi_{\sigma}^{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u})=0,

(18)

and we need to define
\Phi_{\sigma}^{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u})
.
We do it in two cases:

•

Neumann condition: again, we force the solution to stay the same, i.e.,

\sum\limits_{E,\sigma\in E}\Phi_{\sigma}^{E}(\mathbf{u})+\sum_{\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega,\sigma\in\Gamma}\Phi_{\sigma}^{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u})=0.

In practice, once
\sum\limits_{E,\sigma\in E}\Phi_{\sigma}^{E}(\mathbf{u})
in
(
17
) has been computed, we set this quantity to
0
.

•

wall. For each
\Gamma
in (
18
), with normal
{\mathbf{n}}
, there is one element
E
that has
\Gamma
as one of its edges. Then we consider
E^{-}
, the (virtual) element that is symmetric with respect to
{\mathbf{n}}
. We consider
\mathbf{u}^{-}
, the state symmetric to
\mathbf{u}^{h}
with respect to
{\mathbf{n}}
, i.e., same density and pressure, and symmetric velocity. By this we define
\Phi^{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u}^{h})
by
\Phi^{\Gamma}(\mathbf{u}^{h})=\Phi^{E^{-}}(\mathbf{u}^{-})
.
