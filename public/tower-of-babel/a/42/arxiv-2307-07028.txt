The Bohr Radius of the Weighted Bloch Spaces
Khasyanov R.Sh

Abstract
The concept of the Bohr radius of a pair of Banach spaces is introduced. The lower estimate for the value of the Bohr radius from the Bloch space to the space of bounded functions obtained by I. Kayumov, S. Ponnusamy and N. Shakirov is slightly improved. It is shown that for any weighted Bloch space the Bohr radius is not less than 1 / 2 1/\sqrt{2} . A criterion for the sharpness of the Bohr inequality in the weighted Bloch space with R = 1 / 2 R=1/\sqrt{2} is obtained. Using this criterion, the examples of the weights for which the inequality is sharp is given.

Introduction and main results

In 1914, H. Bohr, studying Dirichlet series, noticed
[
7
]
the following interesting fact in complex analysis, which is now called the Bohr phenomenon:

Theorem A
.

Let
f(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}
and
\|f\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}|f(z)|\leq 1
in the unit disc
\mathbb{D}=\{|z|<1\}
. Then

\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|r^{n}\leq 1,\quad 0\leq r\leq 1/3.

The constant
1/3
is sharp.

In fact, Bohr proved this for
r\leq 1/6
. The best constant
1/3
was obtained independently in the same year
by M. Riesz, I. Schur and F. Wiener (for different proofs see e.g. appendix in
[
11
]
).
This theorem is equivalent to the inequality known as the Bohr inequality:

\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|r^{n}\leq\|f\|_{\infty},\quad 0\leq r\leq 1/3.

An active study of various modifications and generalizations of the Bohr inequality began in the middle of 1990s since P. Dixon, using the Bohr inequality, solved a long-standing problem about the characterization of Banach algebras
[
11
]
. Part of the subsequent research in this area is directed towards extending the Bohr phenomenon in multidimensional framework and in more abstract settings (see e.g.
[

,
[

,

]
,
[

]
). In 2018 B. Bhowmik and N. Das applied the Bohr inequality to the question of comparing majorant series of subordinating functions
[

We consider the following questions:

Problem 1
.

Fix a holomorphic function
f(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}
and consider the class
\mathcal{F}
of all analytic functions of the form
\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{n}z^{n}
such that
|b_{n}|=|a_{n}|
for all
n\geq 0.
Which properties of
f
are inherited by all functions in the class
\mathcal{F}
? In other words, what properties of a holomorphic function can be detected from the moduli of its Maclaurin series coefficients?

For instance, the radius of convergence of the Maclaurin series and the Hardy space
H^{2}
norm depend only on the moduli of the coefficients. On the other hand, there are examples of properties that may hold for some members of
\mathcal{F}
but not for others: the series
\sum_{n\geq 1}\dfrac{\pm z^{n}}{n}
are bounded in the
\mathbb{D}
for almost every choice of plus and minus signs (
[
17
]
, Chapter V, Theorem 8.34), but if all plus signs are taken, the series is unbounded in the unit disk.
Bohr’s theorem implies that if
|f(z)|\leq 1
in the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
, then
|g(z)|\leq 1
in the disk of radius
1/3
for all functions
g\in\mathcal{F}
.

Problem 2
.

Let
X,Y
be Banach spaces of analytic functions in the disc
\mathbb{D}=\left\{|z|<1\right\}
. Let
f(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}
. We need to find the maximal
R
for which

\|f\|_{X}\leq 1\Longrightarrow\|\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|(Rz)^{n}\|_{Y}\leq 1.

We call such
R

the Bohr radius from
X
to
Y
and denote
R_{X\rightarrow Y}
. If
X
and
Y
coincide, we write
R_{X}.

The classical Bohr theorem states that
R_{H^{\infty}}=1/3.
We study Bohr radius in Bloch spaces.

Definition 1
.

We say that the analytical function in
\mathbb{D}

f(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}
belongs to the weighted Bloch space
\mathcal{B}(\omega)
with the weight
\omega(r)\geq 0
if

\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}(\omega)}:=|a_{0}|+\sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}\omega(|z|)|f^{\prime}(z)|<\infty.

For the standart weight
\omega(r)=1-r^{2}
we just write
\mathcal{B}
.

We start with the question of finding the value of
R_{\mathcal{B}\rightarrow H^{\infty}}
. This problem has already been studied in
[
13
]
, where the following lower and upper estimates are obtained:
0.624162\ldots\geq R_{\mathcal{B}\rightarrow H^{\infty}}\geq R
, where
R\approx 0.55356\ldots
is the solution of the equation
1-r+r\log(1-r)=0
. We noticed that the proof of the theorem can be slightly improved and we get a lower estimate about
0.01
better. Note that in
[
1
]
V. Allu and H. Halder considered this problem for functions defined on simply connected domains. As a consequence, they obtained a slightly less sharp lower estimate for the Bohr radius from the Bloch space to
H^{\infty}:R_{\mathcal{B}\rightarrow H^{\infty}}\geq 0.546679
.

Theorem 1
.

R_{\mathcal{B}\rightarrow H^{\infty}}\geq 0.563777.

Next, we consider the problem of finding the Bohr radius of the weighted Bloch spaces. The following theorem immediately follows from the Cauchy inequality.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathcal{B}(\omega)
be Bloch space with the weight
\omega(r)\geq 0
.
Then

R_{\mathcal{B}(\omega)}\geq 1/\sqrt{2}.

We ask a natural question: for a given weight, is the Bohr inequality with
R=1/\sqrt{2}
sharp? Using the method of E. Bombieri and J. Bourgain
[
8
]
, we give a criterion for the sharpness of such an inequality.

Theorem 3
.

The inequality

\Big|\Big|\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|\Big(\dfrac{z}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)^{n}\Big|\Big|_{\mathcal{B}(\omega)}\leq||f||_{\mathcal{B}(\omega)}

[formula]

is sharp, i.e. turns to equality for some nonzero
f\in\mathcal{B}(\omega)
, if and only if there exists
r_{0}\in\Big[\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}},1\Big]
, such that for all
r\in[0,1)
the inequality

\dfrac{\omega(r)}{\omega(r_{0})}\leq\min{\Big(2-\dfrac{r}{r_{0}};\dfrac{\sqrt{2}r_{0}+r}{\sqrt{2}r+r_{0}}\Big)}.

[formula]

holds. Extreme functions up to multiplication by a constant have the following form:

\int_{c}^{z}\dfrac{z/r_{0}-e^{i\phi}/\sqrt{2}}{1-e^{-i\phi}z/(\sqrt{2}r_{0})}dz+C,\quad\phi\in[0,2\pi),\>c\in\mathbb{D},\>C\in\mathbb{C}.

Using Theorem 3, we can find the examples of the weights for which the inequality (1) is sharp. We fix
r_{0}\in[1/\sqrt{2},1]
and denote
\omega_{1}(r):=2-\dfrac{r}{r_{0}},\>\omega_{2}(r):=\dfrac{\sqrt{2}r_{0}+r}{\sqrt{2}r+r_{0}}.
Note that
\omega_{2}(r)\leq\omega_{1}(r)
for
r\leq r_{0}
and
\omega_{1}(r)\leq\omega_{2}(r)
for
r\geq r_{0}
. Let
\tilde{\omega}(r):=\dfrac{\omega(r)}{\omega(r_{0})},
then
\tilde{\omega}(r)\leq h(r):=\min{(\omega_{1}(r),\omega_{2}(r))}
and
\tilde{\omega}(r_{0})=1.
It is easy to see, that
h(r)
is a decreasing and convex function for
r\in[0,r_{0}).
So we can drawn a plot of the function
h(r):

We see that there is no smooth function
\tilde{\omega}(r)
with the necessary properties and
r_{0}<1
. That is why any smooth weight does not satisfy the condition (2). However, we can give examples of weights that satisfy condition (2):

Examples.

1.
\omega(r)\equiv 1,\>r_{0}=1.

Fix
r_{0}\in[1/\sqrt{2},1)
and
\alpha\geq 1.

2. The weight

\omega(r)=\begin{cases}1,&r\leq r_{0}\\
\Big(\dfrac{1-r}{1-r_{0}}\Big)^{\alpha},&r\geq r_{0}\end{cases}

satisfies the condition (2).

3. The weight

\omega(r)=\Big(1-\Big|\dfrac{r-r_{0}}{1-r_{0}r}\Big|\Big)^{\alpha}

also satisfies the condition (2).

Problem 3
.

Let
X
be Banach space of analytic functions in the unit disc,
f(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}
. We need to find

m_{X}(r):=\sup_{f\in X}\dfrac{\Big\|\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|(rz)^{n}\Big\|_{X}}{\|f\|_{X}}.

In 1962 E. Bombieri showed (
[
9
]
or
[
12
, p. 170–178]
) that for
X=H^{\infty}

m_{\infty}(r):=m_{H^{\infty}}(r)=\dfrac{3-\sqrt{8(1-r^{2})}}{r},\quad 1/3\leq r\leq 1/\sqrt{2}.

This theorem was proved by I. Kayumov and S. Ponnusamy in a simpler way in the work
[
14
]
.

It immediately follows from the Cauchy inequality that
m_{{\infty}}(r)\leq\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-r^{2}}}:

\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|r^{n}\leq\Big({{\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|^{2}}}\Big)^{1/2}\Big({\sum_{n\geq 0}r^{2n}}\Big)^{1/2}=\dfrac{\|f\|_{2}}{\sqrt{1-r^{2}}}\leq\dfrac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{\sqrt{1-r^{2}}}.

In the 2004, E. Bombieri and J. Bourgain proved
[
8
]
the following two profound theorems related to the inequality above.

Theorem B
.

For all
r\in(0;1)
except
r=1/\sqrt{2}

m_{{\infty}}(r)<\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-r^{2}}},\>\>\>\>\>m_{{\infty}}(1/\sqrt{2})=\sqrt{2}.

Theorem C
.

For all
\varepsilon>0
there exists
C(\varepsilon)>0
, such that as
r\rightarrow 1
the lower bound

m_{{\infty}}(r)\geq\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-r^{2}}}-C(\varepsilon)\Big(\log\dfrac{1}{1-r}\Big)^{\varepsilon+3/2}

holds

Theorem C implies that
m_{{\infty}}(r)\rightarrow\infty
when
r\rightarrow 1
.
It is clear from the proof of Theorem 1 that
m_{\mathcal{B}}(r)\leq\dfrac{r}{\sqrt{1-r^{2}}}
. Using the construction already discussed in the proof of Theorem 2, we prove an analogue of Theorem B for the Bloch space:

Theorem 5
.

m_{\mathcal{B}}(R)<\dfrac{R}{\sqrt{1-R^{2}}},\quad 0<R<1.

Proofs

Proof of Theorem 1
.

If
\|f\|_{\mathcal{B}}\leq 1
, then
|f^{\prime}(z)|^{2}\leq\dfrac{1}{(1-|z|^{2})^{2}},\>z\in\mathbb{D}
.

We integrate this inequality over the circle
|z|=r
and obtain from Parceval formula

\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{2}|a_{n}|^{2}r^{2n}\leq\dfrac{r^{2}}{(1-r^{2})^{2}},\quad 0<r<1.

Hence

\sum_{n\geq 1}n^{2}|a_{n}|^{2}r^{n}\leq\dfrac{r}{(1-r)^{2}}.

We may now divide this inequality by r and integrate with respect to
r
with limit from
0
to
r
:

\sum_{n\geq 1}n|a_{n}|^{2}r^{n}\leq\dfrac{r}{1-r}.

Replace
r
with
r^{s}
:

\sum_{n\geq 1}n|a_{n}|^{2}r^{ns}\leq\dfrac{r^{s}}{1-r^{s}},\quad 0<r,s<1.

Thus,

\sum_{n\geq 1}|a_{n}|r^{n}\leq\Big({\sum_{n\geq 1}\dfrac{r^{2ns}}{n}}\Big)^{1/2}\Big({\sum_{n\geq 1}n|a_{n}|^{2}r^{2n(1-s)}}\Big)^{1/2}\leq\Big({\dfrac{r^{2(1-s)}\log(1-r^{2s})}{r^{2(1-s)}-1}}\Big)^{1/2}.

Consequently,
\sum_{n\geq 1}|a_{n}|r^{n}\leq 1
with
r\leq R
, where
R
is solution of the equation

\log(1-r^{2s})=\dfrac{r^{2(1-s)}-1}{r^{2(1-s)}}.

Using Wolfram Mathematica, we deduce that the best possible
r
, which can be achieved in this way is obtained at
s=0.333771
and is equal to
0.563777.

∎

Unfortunately, there is no extreme function for
R=0.563777
, since if such a function
f(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}a_{n}z^{n}
existed, then

|a_{n}|=C\dfrac{R^{n(2s-1)}}{n},\quad n\geq 1

Then the function
f(z)
is analytic in
\mathbb{D}
only for
s\geq 1/2
.

Proof of Theorem 2
.

In what follows we will solve an equivalent problem for weighted norms of functions in place of their derivatives. Note that
(f(Rz))^{\prime}=Rf^{\prime}(Rz)
. Thus, to find the Bohr radius in the Bloch space, it is sufficient to solve the following problem: find the maximal
R
at which

\sup_{{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in(0;1)\end{subarray}}}\omega(r)\|f(re^{i\theta})\|_{L^{\infty}[0,2\pi)}\leq 1\Longrightarrow\\
\sup_{r\in(0;1)}\omega(r)R\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|(Rr)^{n}\leq 1,

where
f(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}
.

Let us show that
R_{\mathcal{B}(\omega)}\geq\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}:

\omega(r)R\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|(Rr)^{n}\leq\omega(r)R\Big({\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|^{2}r^{2n}}\Big)^{1/2}\Big({\sum_{n\geq 0}R^{2n}}\Big)^{1/2}=\hskip 147.95433pt(4)\\
=\omega(r)\|f(re^{i\theta})\|_{L^{2}[0,2\pi]}\dfrac{R}{\sqrt{{1-R^{2}}}}\leq\omega(r)\|f(re^{i\theta})\|_{L^{\infty}[0,2\pi]}\dfrac{R}{\sqrt{{1-R^{2}}}}.

The inequality
\dfrac{R}{\sqrt{{1-R^{2}}}}\leq 1
is equivalent to
R\leq\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
, hence
R_{\mathcal{B}(\omega)}\geq\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}.

∎

Proof of Theorem 3
.

As before, instead of derivatives of functions, we consider the functions themselves. Thus, we need to show that a nonzero function
f(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}
such that

\sup_{r\in(0;1)}\dfrac{\omega(r)}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|\Big(\dfrac{r}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)^{n}=\sup_{{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in(0;1)\end{subarray}}}\omega(r)\|f(re^{i\theta})\|_{L^{\infty}[0,2\pi)}<\infty.\hskip 8.53581pt

[formula]

exist if and only if the weight
\omega
satisfies (2).

Necessity.
Assume that equality (5) holds for some
f
. Let us show that there exists
r_{0}\in[0,1]
with the following properties:

1.
r_{0}
is the point where the supremum of the left-side in (5) is attained:

\omega(r)\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|\Big(\dfrac{r}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)^{n}\leq\omega(r_{0})\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|\Big(\dfrac{r_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)^{n},\quad 0\leq r<1.

2. The norm of
f
is also attained at
r_{0}:

\omega(r)\|f(re^{i\theta})\|_{L^{\infty}[0,2\pi)}\leq\omega(r_{0})\|f(r_{0}e^{i\theta})\|_{L^{\infty}[0,2\pi)},\quad 0\leq r<1.

3. The Cauchy inequality in the chain (4) turns into equality which is equivalent to

|a_{n}|r_{0}^{n}=C(r_{0})\Big(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)^{n},\quad n\geq 0,

where
C(r_{0})
is a constant depending only on
r_{0}
.

4. The last inequality in the chain (4) is equality, that is

\|f(r_{0}e^{i\theta})\|_{L^{2}[0,2\pi)}=\|f(r_{0}e^{i\theta})\|_{L^{\infty}[0,2\pi)}.

Define
r_{0}
by the condition 1. We substitute
r=r_{0}
and
R=1/\sqrt{2}
in (4) and use the condition 1 and equality (5):

\omega(r_{0})\|f(r_{0}e^{i\theta})\|_{L^{\infty}[0,2\pi)}\geq\dfrac{\omega(r_{0})}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|\Big(\dfrac{r_{0}}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)^{n}\geq\\
\geq\sup_{r\in(0;1)}\dfrac{\omega(r)}{\sqrt{2}}\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|\Big(\dfrac{r}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)^{n}=\sup_{r\in(0;1)}\omega(r)\|f(re^{i\theta})\|_{L^{\infty}[0,2\pi)}.

Thus, condition 2 is also satisfied. Conditions 3 and 4 following from the fact that all inequalities in (4) are equalities for
r=r_{0}
and
R=1/\sqrt{2}.

It follows from the condition 3 that the radius of convergence for
f
is
\sqrt{2}r_{0}
, where
r_{0}\geq 1/\sqrt{2}.

It is known that the equality
\|f(r_{0}e^{i\theta})\|_{L^{2}[0,2\pi)}=\|f(r_{0}e^{i\theta})\|_{L^{\infty}[0,2\pi)}
holds only for functions

f(z)=K(r_{0})B\Big(\dfrac{z}{r_{0}}\Big),

where the function
B(z)
is a finite Blaschke product (see, for example,
[
12

). Without loss of generality, we can assume that
|K(r_{0})|=1
.

We find
C(r_{0})
. Let

B\Big(\dfrac{z}{r_{0}}\Big)=\prod_{i=1}^{K}\Big(\dfrac{z/r_{0}-\alpha_{i}}{1-\bar{\alpha_{i}}z/r_{0}}\Big)^{d_{i}},\hskip 19.91692pt\sum_{i=1}^{K}d_{i}=d,\hskip 14.22636pt\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{D}\>(1\leq i\leq K).

Denote by
dA(z)
the standard Lebesgue measure on the plane. From the formula for the area of the image of the finite Blaschke product, we can calculate its degree:

\displaystyle d=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\big|B^{\prime}(z)\big|^{2}dA(z)=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{D}}\big|(f(r_{0}z))^{\prime}\big|^{2}dA(z)=

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 1}n|a_{n}|^{2}r_{0}^{2n}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\dfrac{n}{2^{n}}C^{2}(r_{0})=2C^{2}(r_{0}).

Therefore
C(r_{0})=\sqrt{d/2}.

Note that

\sqrt{d/2}=C(r_{0})=|a_{0}|=|f(0)|=\Big|\prod_{i=1}^{K}\alpha_{i}^{d_{i}}\Big|<1,

[formula]

hence
d=1
.
If
d=1
, then from (6),
|\alpha_{1}|=1/\sqrt{2}
and

f(z)=\dfrac{z/r_{0}-e^{i\phi}/\sqrt{2}}{1-e^{-i\phi}z/(\sqrt{2}r_{0})}.

Without loss of generality, we consider

. In this case, the supremum
|f(re^{i\theta})|
is reached when

\|f(rz)\|_{L^{\infty}[0,2\pi)}=|f(-r)|=\dfrac{r/r_{0}+1/\sqrt{2}}{1+r/(\sqrt{2}r_{0})}

Proof of Theorem 4
.

We consider the function
f(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a_{n}z^{n}
from
[
3
]
:

f(z)=\dfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\dfrac{1-a^{2}}{(1-az)^{2}}\Big(\dfrac{z-a}{1-az}\Big),\quad 0<a<1/\sqrt{3}.

It is not difficult to show that
\sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}(1-|z|^{2})|f(z)|=1
. Obviously,
a_{0}=-\frac{3\sqrt{3}a(1-a^{2})}{2}<0
. Let us show that if
0<a<1/\sqrt{3}
, then
a_{n}>0,\>n\geq 1
. Denote
\tilde{a}=\dfrac{a^{2}}{1-a^{2}}
and let

g(w)=\sum_{n\geq 0}b_{n}w^{n}=\dfrac{1}{(1-w)^{2}}\Big(\dfrac{w}{1-w}-\tilde{a}\Big)=\sum_{n\geq 0}(n+1)(n/2-\tilde{a})w^{n}.

Since
f(z)=C(a)g(az),\>C(a)>0,
the condition
a_{n}>0
is equivalent to
b_{n}>0.
But
b_{n}=(n+1)(n/2-\tilde{a})>0
for all
n\geq 1
if and only if
0<a<1/\sqrt{3}.

Thus,

\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|z^{n}=\dfrac{3\sqrt{3}(1-a^{2})}{2}\Big(\dfrac{z-a}{(1-az)^{3}}+2a\Big)

and

R(1-r^{2})\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|(Rr)^{n}=R(1-r^{2})\dfrac{3\sqrt{3}(1-a^{2})}{2}\Big(\dfrac{Rr-a}{(1-aRr)^{3}}+2a\Big).

Using Wolfram Mathematica again, we find that the last expression is greater than one, for example, at
a=0.35
and
R=0.769
, so
R_{\mathcal{B}}\leq 0.769
.
∎

Proof of Theorem 5
.

Suppose that the expression
(1-r^{2})\sum_{n\geq 0}|a_{n}|\Big(\dfrac{r}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)^{n}
reaches the supremum at the point
r_{0}
. We replace all the inequalities in the chain (4) with equalities for
r=r_{0}
. Then, as in the proof of Theorem 3, it will follows from the sharpness condition of the Cauchy’s inequality that

|a_{n}|r_{0}^{n}=C(r_{0})R^{n},\quad n\geq 0,

so

r_{0}\geq R
. It follows from the equality of norms that
f(z)=B\Big(\dfrac{z}{r_{0}}\Big)
, where
B(z)
is finite Blaschke product that is

f(z)=B\Big(\dfrac{z}{r_{0}}\Big)=\prod_{i=1}^{K}\Big(\dfrac{z/r_{0}-\alpha_{i}}{1-\bar{\alpha_{i}}z/r_{0}}\Big)^{d_{i}},\>\>\>\sum_{i=1}^{K}d_{i}=d.

It follows from the formula for the area of the image of the finite Blaschke product that

C(r_{0})=\sqrt{d}\Big(\dfrac{1-R^{2}}{R}\Big).

Since we have replaced all the inequalities with equalities, then

(1-r^{2})|f(r)|\leq(1-r_{0}^{2})|f(r_{0})|=1-r_{0}^{2},\quad 0\leq r<1.

We substitute
r=0
into the left part of the inequality above:

|f(0)|=C(r_{0})=\sqrt{d}\Big(\dfrac{1-R^{2}}{R}\Big)\leq 1-r_{0}^{2}\leq 1-R^{2},

which is impossible for all
R\in(0;1)
.
∎
