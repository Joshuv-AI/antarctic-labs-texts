Matroid Products in Tropical Geometry
Nicholas Anderson

Abstract
. Symmetric powers of matroids were first introduced by Lovasz [ 9 ] and Mason [ 13 ] in the 1970s, where it was shown that not all matroids admit higher symmetric powers. Since these initial findings, the study of matroid symmetric powers has remained largely unexplored. In this paper, we establish an equivalence between valuated matroids with arbitrarily large symmetric powers and tropical linear spaces that appear as the variety of a tropical ideal. In establishing this equivalence, we additionally show that all tropical linear spaces are connected through codimension one. These results provide additional geometric and algebraic connections to the study of matroid symmetric powers, which we leverage to prove that the class of matroids with second symmetric power is minor closed and has infinitely many forbidden minors.

Introduction

In order to approach this problem, we revisit some early works of Mason

.

Let

The most simple examples of tropical ideals are

The underlying geometry of matroid symmetric powers and of tropical ideals is vital to establishing their connection. If

V(p)=\{x\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:p\text{ attains its minimum at least twice at }x\text{ or }p(x)=\infty\},

. The variety of a tropical ideal

which is also a balanced polyhedral complex, with weights defined as in

11
]
. In this paper we refer to any balanced polyhedral complex in

as a

, and we distinguish those tropical varieties that arise as the variety of a tropical ideal by referring to them as

To relate tropical geometry explicitly to valuated matroids, we consider that
\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\cong\overline{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{1}
under the map

. In particular, a valuated matroid

can be seen as a collection of polynomials in

Explicitly, given a vector

we have the associated vector-polynomial

The
tropical linear space
associated to
\mathcal{V}
is then given by

\trop(\mathcal{V}):=\bigcap_{v\in\mathcal{V}}V(p_{v}),

which is a balanced polyhedral complex in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with constant weights

.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a matroid. The tropical linear space
\trop(\mathcal{V})
is the variety of a tropical ideal if and only if a
d^{\text{th}}

exists for every

If the variety

is a tropically realizable variety by definition. We extend this terminology to the matroid

is a tropically realizable variety. Tropically realizable varieties behave nicely under coordinate projections and stable intersections with hyperplanes

Theorem 1.5
.

Tropically realizable matroids are a minor closed class. In particular, if
\mathcal{V}
is a matroid on

of

.

The geometric techniques used to prove

extend to other classes of matroids. We say that the matroid

is

We also consider the class of tropically algebraic matroids. Given a (tropical) ideal

Underlying these aforementioned proofs are several technical results that are quite useful, and in particular we’d highlight a special case of

2.
Matroid Products

In this section we will revisit some ideas of Mason
[
13
]
and Las Vergnas
[
7
]
particularly with respect to tensor and symmetric powers of matroids, working with matroids rather than valuated matroids.

Work with matroid products in the category of matroids with strong maps was explored by Crapo and Rota in
[
2
]
, and it was known at the time of these works that matroid products did not exist in this category. However, this is not the notion of matroid product that we are discussing in this paper. Instead, we interest ourselves in matroid products in a weaker sense, as defined by Las Vergnas for tensor products, and by Mason for symmetric powers.

2.1.
Tensor Products

Given matroids
M
and
N
, we aim to construct a new matroid, called a tensor product of
M
and
N
, which will be a matroid with ground set equal to the Cartesian product
E(M)\times E(N)
with rank
\rk(M)\rk(N)
and inheriting some structure from
M
and
N
. While this operation is always possible for matroids represented over a common field
k
, it is not necessarily unique, nor is it possible for matroids in general as shown by LasVergnas in
[
7
]
*Proposition 2.1.

Several results presented in this section have been known since LasVergnas’s original paper, however, we revisit this work and introduce a new proof of
[
7
]
*Proposition 2.1. Furthermore, our proof generalizes to an infinite class of non-algebraic matroids with no common non-realizable minors. This family of matroids is exactly the class of matroids that were initially studied by Lindström in
[
8
]
. We substantiate the claim that none of these matroids has a non-trivial matroid tensor product.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
M
and
N
be matroids on ground sets

and
E(N)
respectively. A matroid

(2.2)

respectively. If

is a loop in

and

then we have

(2.3)

\rk(P|_{\{(e,x)\mid x\in E(N)\}})=0

or

(2.4)

\rk(P|_{\{(x,f)\mid x\in E(M)\}})=0

respectively.

We will refer to the set
\{(e,x)\mid x\in E(N)\}
as the

associated to

and we will refer to the set
\{(x,f)\mid x\in E(M)\}
as the

associated to an element

.

Isomorphism
2.1

imply that, given bases

of

and

of

is a spanning set of any quasi product of

and

and

are

and

of

and

and

The motivating example for the definition of matroid tensor products is, as is often the case, linear spaces. Given two matroids

Outside of the realizable case, little is known about the existence of matroid tensor products beyond

2.6

In his first work on matroid tensor products, Las Vergnas proved that the Vámos matroid
V_{8}
has no second tensor power. However, in
[
7
]
this proof is simplified into two smaller results. First,
[

]
*Proposition 2.1 proves that no tensor product between

[

]

existed, it would have a minor isomorphic to a tensor product of

and

, a contradiction. We conclude that no second tensor power of

exists. In particular, the Vámos matroid is a forbidden minor of the class of matroids with second tensor powers. We make this connection between tensor products and their minors explicit.

We denote the collection of independent sets of a matroid

\mathbf{\mathcal{I}(N)}

Proposition 2.7

Let

and

and

It suffices to prove the result for single-element deletions and contractions. Moreover, by

,

, and

be independent in

is independent in

is independent in

. We conclude by

is a tensor product of

.

Now let

is a tensor product of

Now let

be independent in

2.2.
Matroid Symmetric Powers

We now turn our focus onto symmetric powers of matroids. Recall that, for any set

d^{\text{th}}
symmetric power of

is the set of unordered

. If we fix an element

\displaystyle*_{\alpha}:\Sym_{d}(E)

\displaystyle\to\Sym_{d+i}(E)

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\mapsto(\alpha,\beta).

Since the coordinates of elements of

are unordered, we will write

as
\alpha\beta
, taking after commutative multiplication notation.

We will often consider the subset of elements of
\Sym_{d+i}(E)
that are divisible by some

\Sym_{d+i}(E)|_{\alpha}:=\{e\in\Sym_{d+i}(E)\mid e=\alpha x\}.

Given a valuated matroid

on

we further extend this notation and write

instead of
\mathcal{V}|_{\Sym_{d+1}(E)|_{\alpha}},
where the restriction of

to the set

•

E(\mathcal{V}_{i})=\Sym_{i}(E),

•

induces the isomorphism

(2.5)

Linear Tropical Ideals

This section will be focused on what we will call

In the realizable case, linear ideals are perhaps the most simple and structured ideals of positive dimension, and any linear space can be realized as the variety of some linear ideal. However, when working with tropical ideals, valuated matroids, and their associated tropical linear spaces, it is already known that not all tropical linear spaces are tropically realizable varieties
[
3
]
. Despite this complication in the theory, tropical linear spaces remain a reasonably well-behaved and well-studied class of tropical varieties, see
[
15
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
for a few examples. In the remainder of this paper we will establish further combinatorial and geometric connections.

A homogeneous ideal

is called linear if it is generated by linear forms or, equivalently, it is the ideal of polynomials vanishing on a linear space. While tropical ideals are generally not finitely generated, they do have a finite
tropical basis

[
10
]
*Theorem 5.9, which is a set of polynomials in the ideal that cuts out the variety. This inspires the following definition.

Definition 3.1
.

A homogeneous tropical ideal

is a

if its linear forms form a tropical basis, that is

V(I)=V(I_{1}).

The variety of a linear tropical ideal is necessarily a tropical linear space balanced with constant weights
1
.

We say that an ideal

is

with respect to a polynomial

if
fp\in I
implies

. When

we will simply say that

is saturated.

Remark 3.2
.

Given a linear tropical ideal
I\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]

as a sequence of valuated matroids
(I_{1},I_{2},\dots,I_{d},\dots)

3.1.
Connectivity

In order to prove the main results of this section, we will need to show that a
d
-dimensional tropical linear space has no proper tropical subvarieties of dimension
d
. To show this, we will prove a stronger result, which extends a valuable result in matroid theory to all valuated matroids. Specifically, we will show that the space of top-dimensional Minkowski weights of a tropical linear space is one-dimensional. We refer the reader to
[
12
]
[Section 3.3] for relevant definitions and background for weighted polyhedral complexes, balancing, and the
\Star
operation for a polyhedral complex.

If
\Sigma
is a polyhedral complex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we write
|\Sigma|
to refer to the
support
of
\Sigma
, which is the set of points in
\Sigma
without the polyhedral structure. Recall that the facet-ridge graph of
\Sigma
is the graph whose vertices are given by the facets of
\Sigma
and where there are edges between two vertices
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
if
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
intersect along a ridge of
\Sigma
. We say that
\Sigma
is connected through codimension one if its facet-ridge graph
G(\Sigma)
is a connected graph. We additionally say that
\Sigma
is locally connected through codimension 1 if
G(\Star_{w}(\Sigma))
is connected for every
w\in|\Sigma|
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\Sigma
be a connected pure polyhedral complex that is locally connected through codimension one. Then
\Sigma
is connected through codimension one.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a connected, pure, polyhedral complex that is locally connected through codimension one. Further suppose towards a contradiction that
\Sigma
is not connected through codimension one. Then the facet-ridge graph
G(\Sigma)
has at least two connected components
C_{1},C_{2},\dots
. Since the vertices of
C_{i}
are cones of
\Sigma
we define the support of
C_{i}
as

|C_{i}|:=\bigcup_{\sigma\in V(C_{i})}\sigma\subset|\Sigma|.

Since
\Sigma
is connected, there must exist
C_{i}
and
C_{j}
such that
|C_{i}|\cap|C_{j}|\neq\emptyset
. Without loss of generality we suppose, suppose that
|C_{1}|\cap|C_{2}|\neq\emptyset
, and let
w\in|C_{1}|\cap|C_{2}|
. Since
w\in|C_{1}|
and
|C_{2}|
there is are facets
\sigma_{1}\in V(C_{1})
and
\sigma_{2}\in V(C_{2})
such that
w\in\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
. Now, since
\Sigma
is locally connected through codimension one,
G(\Star_{w}(\Sigma))
is a connected graph. Moreover, by construction,
G(\Star_{w}(\Sigma))
is a subgraph of
G(\Sigma)
. Since
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are vertices of
\Star_{w}(\Sigma)
, there is a path connecting
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
in
G(\Star_{w}(\Sigma))
, however this now implies there is a path between
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
in
G(\Sigma)
. Therefore
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are not distinct connected components, a contradiction. We conclude that
G(\Sigma)
is necessarily a connected graph and therefore
\Sigma
is connected through codimension one.
∎

The conditions of
Proposition

3.3
are met by a large class of objects studied in tropical Geometry called tropical manifolds, which are meant to be a tropical analogue to classical manifolds. A

is a polyhedral complex such that, for all

,
\Star_{w}(\Sigma)
is linearly isomorphic to the Bergman fan of some matroid that has dimension

. Of particular value to this paper is that every tropical linear space is a tropical manifold.

Corollary 3.4
.

Any connected tropical manifold is connected through codimension one.

Proof.

The Bergman fan of any matroid is connected in codimension one. Since a connected tropical manifold
M
is, by construction, locally the Bergman fan of a matroid, it is locally pure and locally connected in codimension one. This implies that
M
is both pure and, by
Proposition

3.3
, connected in codimension 1.
∎

Definition 3.5
.

Let
\Sigma
be a polyhedral complex. A

is an rational-valued function from the

\Sigma

\displaystyle m:\Sigma_{k}\to\mathbb{Q}

Corollary 3.6
.

The space of top-dimensional Minkowski-weights of a tropical manifold is a

-vector space and is generated by the constant weight function
w(\sigma)=1
.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma
be a

for some facet

for all other facets of

Connected tropical manifolds have no proper tropical subvarieties of equal dimension.

3.4.
Tropically Realizable Matroids

Given a linear tropical ideal
I
, the data of its variety is encoded entirely in
I_{1}
, which is itself a valuated matroid, and we have seen that
I
necessarily encodes infinitely large symmetric powers of
I_{1}
. We will now prove that these are in fact equivalent theories.

Recall that a tropical variety

is said to be tropically realizable if there is a tropical ideal
I
for which

. We extended this terminology to matroids by saying a valuated matroid
\mathcal{V}
on

is tropically realizable if there exists a linear tropical ideal

in
\overline{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
for which

, where

is the tropical linear space of

.

Theorem 3.17
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a valuated matroid. Then
\mathcal{V}

∎

Lemma 3.20
.

If
\mathcal{V}
is a valuated matroid on
[n]
and
\mathcal{V}_{d}
is a
d^{\text{th}}
symmetric power of
\mathcal{V}
. Then the valuated matroid

P:=\mathcal{V}_{d}\setminus(n\cdot\Sym_{d-1}([n]))

is a
d^{\text{th}}
symmetric quasi power of
\mathcal{V}\setminus n
and satisfies

V(P)=\pi_{[n-1]}(\trop(\mathcal{V})).

In particular,
P
is a
d^{\text{th}}
symmetric power of
\mathcal{V}\setminus n
.

Proof.

To show that
P
is a symmetric quasi power we proceed by induction on
d
. Let
d=2
. Let
x\in[n-1]
. Then

P|_{x}\cong\mathcal{V}|_{x}\setminus x\cdot n.

By
Equation

2.5
and
Remark

2.16
, we conclude
\mathcal{V}|_{x}\setminus x\cdot n\cong\mathcal{V}\setminus n
. Thus
P|_{x}\cong\mathcal{V}\setminus n
for all
x\in[n-1]
and
P
is a second symmetric quasi product. Suppose for all
i<d
that
P_{i}:=\mathcal{V}_{i}\setminus(n\cdot\Sym_{d-1}([n])
is an
i^{\text{th}}
symmetric quasi power of
\mathcal{V}\setminus n
. Now let
\mathbf{x}\in\Sym_{d-i}([n])
for
i\geq 1
. We have

\displaystyle P|_{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}|_{\mathbf{x}}\setminus(\mathbf{x}\cdot n\cdot\Sym_{i-1}(E))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}_{i}\setminus(n\cdot\Sym_{i-1}(E)

\displaystyle=P_{i}

where this latter inequality is well defined by
Remark

2.16
. In particular,
P
is a symmetric quasi power of
P_{1}\cong\mathcal{V}\setminus n
, as desired.

We now prove the latter claim.
Proposition

2.25
provides that
V(\mathcal{V}_{d})=\trop(\mathcal{V})
. Since
P
is a symmetric quasi power of
\mathcal{V},

V(P)\subset\trop(V\setminus n)
, which is equal to
\pi_{[n-1]}(\trop(\mathcal{V}))
. It remains to prove the reverse inclusion. Let
w\in\pi_{[n-1]}(V(\mathcal{V}_{d}))
. Then there is a
w^{\prime}\in V(\mathcal{V}_{d})
with
\pi_{[n-1]}(w^{\prime})=w
. In particular, every circuit polynomial of
\mathcal{V}_{d}
attains its minimum twice or is equal to
\infty
at
w^{\prime}
. However, we note a circuit polynomial
f
of
P
is also circuit polynomial of
\mathcal{V}_{d}
and that
f
attains its minimum twice or is equal to
\infty
at
w^{\prime}
if and only if
f
attains its minimum twice or is equal to
\infty
at
w
. We conclude that
w\in V(P)
so that
\pi_{[n-1]}(\trop(\mathcal{V}))\subset V(P)
, as desired.
∎

3.6.
Tropically Algebraic and Set-realizable Matroids

Matroids appear in many ways in the study of tropical ideals. In the previous sections we considered only linear tropical ideals, whose variety is a tropical linear space with weights
1
. However, we can just as easily ask about tropical linear spaces balanced with weights other than
1
. This remains largely an unexplored avenue of research. A valuated matroid
\mathcal{V}
is called
tropically set-realizable
if
\trop(\mathcal{V})
is the support of
V(I)
for some tropical ideal
I
. That is, we consider equality of
\trop(\mathcal{V})
with
V(I)
as sets with no polyhedral structure. See
[
16
]
for initial work in this direction.

Stemming from tropical set-realizability is also the study of
tropically algebraic matroids
— matroids that appear as the independence complex of a tropical ideal, as in
Definition

3.11
. Tropically algebraic matroids were first discussed in
[
3
]
but there is yet any work exploring this topic.

Restricting to the case of matroids rather than valuated matroids, we observe that the classes of tropically realizable, tropically set-realizable, and tropically algebraic matroids form nested classes of matroids in increasing order. There are yet any proofs that any of these containment relations is strict. However, in this section we conclude that all three classes are minor closed.

Proposition 3.23
.

The class of tropically set-realizable valuated matroids is minor closed.

Proof.

The exact proof of
Theorem

3.19
applies here without the requirement that
I_{1}=M
, as the weights associated to the maximal cones are no longer considered.
∎

While minor-closedness of tropically set-realisable matroids follows the same proof as used for tropically realizable matroids, tropically algebraic matroids require a slightly different approach.

If the independence complex of
I
forms the independent sets of a Boolean matroid
M
we say that
I
is a matroidal tropical ideal. Furthermore, if there is a tropical ideal with
\mathcal{I}(I)=M
then we say that
M
is tropically algebraic. Algebraic matroids are tropically algebraic
[
16
]
*Lemma 3, but it is still open whether or not there is a tropically algebraic matroid that is not algebraic.

Proposition 3.24
.

The class of tropically algebraic matroids is minor closed.

Proof.

Suppose
M
is a tropically algebraic matroid, and let
I
be a tropical ideal whose independence complex is
M
. Let without loss of generality we delete
n
. We claim that
J:=I\cap\overline{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n-1}]
has
\mathcal{I}(J)=M\setminus n
. To see this, we first note
[
11
]
*Theorem 4.7 provides that

V(J)=\pi_{[n-1]}(V(I)).

A set
T
is independent in
M\setminus n
if and only if
T
is independent in
M
and does not contain
n
. Thus we must show that
\pi_{T}(V(J))=\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{T}
if and only if
\pi_{T}(V(I))=\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{T}
. To see this, we need only observe that
\pi_{T}(V(J))=\pi_{T}(\pi_{[n-1]}(V(I)))=\pi_{T}(V(I))
, as desired.

To prove contraction, if
n
is a loop of
M
, then
M\contr n=M\setminus n
, so we choose to delete
n
instead and do the above. Otherwise
n
is not a loop and we do the following. Consider the specialization
J:=I|_{x_{n}=0}
. The independence complex of
J
is

\mathcal{I}(J)=\{T\subset[n-1]|J\cap\mathbb{R}[\{x_{i}\}_{i\in T}]=\{\infty\}\}.

We claim
\mathcal{I}(J)=\mathcal{I}(M\contr n)
. In particular, we must show that
T\in\mathcal{I}(J)
if and only if
T\cup i
is independent in
\mathcal{I}(I)
. Since
\mathcal{I}(J)
is a simplicial complex, it is sufficient to prove this for its bases.

First let
B
be a maximal subset of
\mathcal{I}(J)
. We claim
B\cup n
is in
\mathcal{I}(I)
. Suppose not. Then there is a nonzero polynomial
f
in
I\cap\overline{\mathbb{R}}[x_{i}:i\in B\cup n]
. This implies that
f|_{x_{n}=0}\neq\infty
, where
f|_{x_{n}=0}\in J
, a contradiction. Thus
\mathcal{I}(J)\subset\mathcal{I}(M\contr n)

That
\mathcal{I}(M\contr n)\subset\mathcal{I}(J)
is immediate, as if
B\in\mathcal{I}(M\contr n)
is a basis, we have that
B\cup\in\mathcal{I}(M)=\mathcal{I}(I)
, in particular,
I\cap\overline{\mathbb{R}}[x_{i}:i\in(B\cup n)]=\{\infty\}
, and thus
J\cap\overline{\mathbb{R}}[x_{i}:i\in B]=\{\infty\}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

In the proof of
Proposition

3.24
, we specialize our ideals at
x_{n}=0
, rather than
x_{n}=\infty
. However, in the case where
V(I)=\trop(\mathcal{V})
for some valuated matroid
\mathcal{V}
, both specialization at
x_{n}=0
and
x_{n}=\infty
yield tropical ideals that tropically algebraically represent
\mu\contr n
.
