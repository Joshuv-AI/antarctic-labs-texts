Semi-Lagrangian Finite-Element Exterior Calculus for Incompressible Flows
Wouter Tonnon, Ralf Hiptmair

Abstract
We develop a semi-Lagrangian discretization of the time-dependent incompressible Navier-Stokes equations with free boundary conditions on arbitrary simplicial meshes. We recast the equations as a non-linear transport problem for a momentum 1-form and discretize in space using methods from finite-element exterior calculus. Numerical experiments show that the linearly implicit fully discrete version of the scheme enjoys excellent stability properties in the vanishing viscosity limit and is applicable to inviscid incompressible Euler flows. We obtain second-order convergence and conservation of energy is achieved through a Lagrange multiplier.



keywords
Euler, Navier-Stokes, fluids, FEEC, structure-preserving, energy conservation

Incompressible Navier-Stokes Equations

We consider the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations—a standard hydrodynamic
model for the motion of an incompressible, potentially-viscous fluid—in a
container with rigid walls, where we impose so-called “free boundary conditions” in the
parlance of
(
Mitrea and Monniaux 2009

and
(
Temam and Ziane 1996

, see the latter
article for further references. We search the fluid velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}(t,\boldsymbol{x})
and
the pressure
p(t,\boldsymbol{x})
as functions of time

\boldsymbol{x}
on a bounded,
Lipschitz domain

such that they solve the evolution boundary-value
problem

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}-\epsilon\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{f},

\displaystyle\text{on }]0,T[\times\Omega,

(1a)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{on }]0,T[\times\Omega,

(1b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\text{on }]0,T[\times\partial\Omega,

(1c)

\displaystyle\epsilon\boldsymbol{n}\times\nabla\times\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0},

\displaystyle\text{on }]0,T[\times\partial\Omega,

(1d)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{u_{0}},

\displaystyle\text{on }\{0\}\times\Omega,

\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{u}}\omega+\epsilon\delta\text{d}\omega+\text{d}p

\displaystyle=f,

\displaystyle\text{on }]0,T[\times\Omega,

(2a)

\displaystyle\delta\omega

\displaystyle=0,

As shown in

)
, sufficiently-smooth solutions

\frac{dE}{dt}(t)\coloneqq\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\omega(t)\wedge\star\omega(t)=-\epsilon\int_{\Omega}\text{d}\omega(t)\wedge\star\text{d}\omega(t)+\int f(t)\wedge\star\omega(t).

(3)

This relation implies energy conservation for

and

\epsilon=0

Remark 1
.

We acknowledge that the boundary condition (
1d
) is
non-standard. This boundary condition was chosen because it is the natural boundary
condition associated to system (
2
). At first glance, an intuitive method to enforce the standard
no-slip boundary conditions is to replace (
1d
) by

\epsilon\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}
on
]0,T[\times\partial\Omega
. To obtain well-posedness, it is then also required to impose
p=0
on
]0,T[\times\partial\Omega
. However, in that case, we lose the natural boundary condition
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
. Instead,
\epsilon\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}
can be enforced using boundary penalty methods as will be shown in future work. In the case
\epsilon=0
, the only imposed boundary
condition (
1c
) is standard.

Remark 2
.

Boundary conditions (
1c
),(
1d
) can be interpreted as slip boundary conditions. However, on smooth domains
\Omega
, they are only equivalent to Navier’s slip boundary conditions if the Weingarten map related to
\partial\Omega

(
Mitrea and Monniaux 2009
, section 2)
.

Outline and Related Work

We propose a semi-Lagrangian approach to the discretization of the reformulated
Navier-Stokes boundary value problem (
2
). This method revolves
around the discretization of the material derivative
D_{\boldsymbol{u}}\omega
in the framework
of a finite-element Galerkin discretization on a fixed spatial mesh. The main idea is to
approximate
D_{\boldsymbol{u}}\omega
by backward difference quotients involving transported
snapshots of the 1-form
\omega
, which can be computed via the pullback induced by the
flow of the velocity vector field
\boldsymbol{u}
.

Semi-Lagrangian methods for transient transport equations like
(
2
) are well-established for the linear case when
\boldsymbol{u}

is a given Lipschitz-continuous velocity field. In particular, for
\omega
a 0-form, that
is, a plain scalar-valued function, plenty of semi-Lagrangian approaches have been
proposed and investigated, see, for instance,
(
Bercovier and Pironneau 1982
;
Bercovier et al 1983
;
Douglas and Russell 1982
;
Ewing et al 1984
;
Hasbani et al 1983
;
Pironneau 1982
;
Russell 1985
;
Süli 1988
;
Bause and Knabner 2002
;
Bermejo and Saavedra 2012
;
Wang and Wang 2010
)
. We
refer to
(
Heumann and Hiptmair 2013
, Chapter 5)
for a comprehensive pre-2013
literature review on the analysis of general semi-Lagrangian schemes. Most of these
methods focus on mapping point values under the flow, with the exception of a particularly
interesting class of semi-Lagrangian methods known as Lagrange-Galerkin
methods. Lagrange-Galerkin methods do not transport point values, but rather triangles (in
2D) or tetrahedra (in 3D). Refer to
(
Bermejo and Saavedra 2016
)
for a review
of those methods.

Meanwhile semi-Lagrangian methods for transport problems for differential forms of any
order have been developed

(
Heumann et al 2012
;
Heumann and Hiptmair 2011
;
Heumann and Hiptmair 2013
)
. The
next section will review these semi-Lagrangian methods for linear transport problems with
emphasis on 1-forms. We will also introduce a new scheme which is second-order in space
and time based on so-called “small edges”, see
Section

3.1.2
for details.

Semi-Lagrangian schemes for the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations are also
well-documented in literature, with emphasis on the Lagrange-Galerkin method

(
Bermejo and Saavedra 2016
;
Boukir et al 1994
;
Buscaglia and Dari 1992
;
Minev and Ross Ethier 1999
;
El-Amrani and Seaid 2011
;
Bermejo and Saavedra 2015
;
Bermejo et al 2012
)
. A
survey of the application of Lagrange-Galerkin methods to the incompressible Navier-Stokes
equations is given in
(
Bermejo and Saavedra 2016
)
. It is important to note
that these methods require the evaluation of integrals of transported quantities and, in
case these integrals cannot be computed exactly, instabilities can occur

(
Bermejo and Saavedra 2012
;
Morton et al 1988
)
. A possible remedy is to
add an additional stabilization term that includes artificial diffusion

(
Bermejo and Saavedra 2016
)
. Other semi-Lagrangian methods for incompressible
Navier-Stokes equations directly transport point values with the nodes of a mesh instead
of evaluating integrals of transported quantities, see

(
Patera 1984
;
Karniadakis and Sherwin 2005
;
Xiu and Karniadakis 2001
;
Xiu et al 2005
;
Bonaventura et al 2018
)

and
(
Celledoni et al 2016
)
, where the last work makes use of exponential
integrators
(
Celledoni et al 2003
)
. Most authors employ spectral
elements for the discretization in space

(
Patera 1984
;
Karniadakis and Sherwin 2005
)
, but any type of
finite-element space with degrees-of-freedom relying on point evaluations can be used. The
methods proposed in
(
Xiu and Karniadakis 2001
;
Xiu et al 2005
)
are also based on
finite-element spaces with degrees-of-freedom on nodes, but employ backward-difference
approximations for the material derivative. The work
(
Bonaventura et al 2018
)

proposes an explicit semi-Lagrangian method still built around the transport of point
values in the nodes of the mesh. The diffusion term is also taken into account in a
semi-Lagrangian fashion and the incompressibility constraint is enforced by means of a
Chorin projection. Also
(
Bonaventura et al 2018
)
proposes an explicit
semi-Lagrangian scheme using the same principles, but based on the
vorticity-streamfunction form of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations.

All the mentioned semi-Lagrangian schemes rely on the transport of point values of
continuous vector fields, which is the perspective embraced in formulation
(
1
). However, we believe that, in particular in the case of free
boundary conditions (
1c
) and (
1d
), the
semi-Lagrangian method based on (
2
) offers benefits similar to
the benefits bestowed by the use of discrete differential forms (finite-element exterior
calculus, FEEC
(
Arnold 2018
;
Arnold et al 2006
)
) for the
discretization of electromagnetic
fields.
Section

4
will convey that the
boundary conditions (
2c
), (
2d
), and the
incompressiblity constraint can very naturally be incorporated into a variational
formulation of (
2
) posed in spaces of 1-forms. This has been the
main motivation for pursuing the
new idea of a semi-Lagrangian method for
(
2
) that employs discrete 1-forms
. Another motivation has been
the expected excellent robustness of the semi-Lagrangian discretization in the limit

\epsilon\mapsto 0
. Numerical tests reported in
Section

5
will confirm
this.

Two more aspects of our method are worth noting: Firstly, a discrete 1-form
\omega_{h}

will not immediately spawn a continuous velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}=\omega_{h}^{\musFlat{}}
,
However, continuity is essential for defining a meaningful flow. We need an additional
averaging step, which we present in
Section

4.1
. Secondly, since
semi-Lagrangian methods fail to respect the decay/conservation law (
3
) exactly, we present a way how to enforce them in

Section

4.3
.

3.2
Semi-Lagrangian material
derivative

The method described in this section is largely based on

(
Heumann and Hiptmair 2013
;
Heumann et al 2012
)
. Throughout this section,
unless stated otherwise, we fix the stationary, Lipschitz-continuous velocity field

\boldsymbol{u}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
with
\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}
on
\partial\Omega
. This
means that we consider a linear transport problem and our main concern will be the
discretization of the material derivative
D_{\boldsymbol{u}}\omega
for a 1-form
\omega
. We can
define the flow
]0,T[\times\Omega\ni(\tau,\boldsymbol{x})\mapsto X_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as the
solution of the initial value problems

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}X_{t,t+\tau}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{u}(X_{t,t+\tau}(\boldsymbol{x})),\hskip 28.45274ptX_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{x}.

(14)

Given that flow we can define the material derivative for a time-dependent differential
1-form
\omega

D_{\boldsymbol{u}}\omega(t)\coloneqq\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}X_{t,t+\tau}^{*}\omega(t+\tau)\right|_{\tau=0}.

(15)

We employ a first- or second-order, backward-difference method to approximate the derivative. Writing
X_{t,t-\tau}^{*}
for the pullback of forms under the flow, we obtain for sufficiently-smooth
t\mapsto\omega(t)
and a timestep
0<\tau\rightarrow 0

\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{u}}\omega(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau}\left[\omega(t)-X_{t,t-\tau}^{*}\omega(t-\tau)\right]+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})

(16)

or

\displaystyle D_{\boldsymbol{u}}\omega(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\tau}\left[3\omega(t)-4X_{t,t-\tau}^{*}\omega(t-\tau)+X_{t,t-2\tau}^{*}\omega(t-2\tau)\right]+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{3}),

(17)

respectively. Note that both backward-difference methods are A-stable
(
Süli and Mayers 2003
)
. In the remainder of this section we restrict ourselves to (
16
), but exactly the same considerations apply to (
17
).

Given a temporal mesh
..<t^{n}<t^{n+1}<..
, we approximate
\omega(t^{n},\cdot)\in\Lambda^{1}(\Omega)
by a discrete differential form
\omega_{h}^{n}\in\Lambda^{1}_{h,p}(\Omega)
with
p\in\{1,2\}
. Using the backward-difference quotient (
16
), we can define the discrete material derivative for fixed timestep
\tau>0

(D_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\omega)(t^{n})\approx\frac{1}{\tau}\left[\omega_{h}^{n}-\mathcal{I}_{h,p}X_{t,t-\tau}^{*}\omega_{h}^{n-1}\right]\in\Lambda^{1}_{h,p}(\Omega),

(18)

where we need to use the projection operator
\mathcal{I}_{h,p}:\Lambda^{1}(\Omega)\mapsto\Lambda_{h,p}(\Omega)
since
X_{t,t-\tau}^{*}\omega^{n-1}_{h}\notin\Lambda^{1}_{h,p}(\Omega)
in general.
Recall from
Section

3.1
that the degrees of freedom for discrete 1-forms are associated to small (
p=2
) or big (
p=1
) edges. As discussed in
Section

3.1.3
, evaluating the interpolation operator entails integrating
X_{t,t-\tau}^{*}\omega^{n-1}_{h}
over small (
p=2
) or big (
p=1

\int_{e}X_{t,t-\tau}^{*}\omega_{h}^{n-1}=\int_{X_{t,t-\tau}(e)}\omega_{h}^{n-1}\approx\int_{\bar{X}_{t,t-\tau}(e)}\omega_{h}^{n-1},

(19)

where

\bar{X}_{t,t-\tau}(e)=\left\{(1-\xi)X_{t,t-\tau}(\boldsymbol{v}^{1})+\xi X_{t,t-\tau}(\boldsymbol{v}^{2});0\leq\xi\leq 1\right\}

(20)

4
Semi-Lagrangian Advection applied to the Incompressible Navier-Stokes Equations

Given a temporal mesh
t_{0}<t_{1}<...<t_{N-1}<t_{N}
, we elaborate a single timestep
t_{n-1}\mapsto t_{n}
of size
\tau\coloneqq t_{n}-t_{n-1}
,
n\leq N
. We assume that approximations
\omega^{k}_{h}\in\Lambda^{1}_{h,p}(\Omega)
of
\omega(t_{k},\cdot)\in\Lambda^{1}(\Omega)
are available for
k<n
with
[0,T]\mapsto\omega\in\Lambda^{1}(\Omega)
a solution of (
2
).

4.1
Approximation of the flow map

In the Navier-Stokes equations, the flow is induced by the unknown, time-dependent velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}(t,\boldsymbol{x})
. Therefore, (
14
) becomes

\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}X_{t,t+\tau}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{u}(t+\tau,X_{t,t+\tau}(\boldsymbol{x})),\hskip 28.45274ptX_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})=\boldsymbol{x}\;,\quad t\in(0,T).

(24)

The discretization of the material derivative requires us to approximate the flow map
X_{t,t-\tau}
in order to evaluate (
20
).

4.1.1
A first-order scheme

We use the explicit Euler method to approximate the (backward) flow according to

X_{t_{n},t_{n}-\tau}(\boldsymbol{x})\approx\boldsymbol{x}-\tau\boldsymbol{u}(t_{n}-\tau,\boldsymbol{x}),

(25)

where
t_{n}
is a node in the temporal mesh and
\tau
denotes the timestep size. We only have access to an approximation
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n-1}\coloneqq(\omega^{n-1}_{h})^{\musSharp{}}
of
\boldsymbol{u}
at time
t_{n-1}
, which gives

X_{t_{n},t_{n}-\tau}(\boldsymbol{x})\approx\boldsymbol{x}-\tau\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n-1}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(26)

Note that a direct application of the explicit Euler method would require an evaluation of
the velocity field at
t_{n}
. Instead, we perform a constant extrapolation and evaluate the
velocity field at
t_{n-1}
, that is, we use
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n-1}
in
(
26
).

The approximation
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n-1}
resides in the space of vector proxies for discrete
differential 1-forms as discussed in

Section

3.1
. This means that only tangential
continuity of
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n-1}
across faces of elements of the mesh is guaranteed, while
discontinuities may appear in the normal direction of the faces. Therefore,

\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n-1}
is not defined point-wise—even though
(
26
) requires point-wise evaluation. For that reason, we will
replace
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n-1}
by a globally-continuous, smoothened velocity field

\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{n-1}
that approximates
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n-1}
(see
Section

4.1.3
for the
construction). We then have

X_{t_{n},t_{n}-\tau}(\boldsymbol{x})\approx\boldsymbol{x}-\tau\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{n-1}(\boldsymbol{x})

(27)

which yields a first-order-in-time approximation of
X_{t_{n},t_{n}-\tau}(\boldsymbol{x})
, provided
that
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{n-1}
is a first-order approximation of
\boldsymbol{u}(t_{n-1},\cdot)
.

4.1.2
A second-order scheme

A second-order approximation can be achieved by using Heun’s method
(
Vuik et al 2015
)
instead of explicit Euler. We find the following second-order in time approximations

\displaystyle X_{t_{n},t_{n}-\tau}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\approx\boldsymbol{x}-\frac{\tau}{2}\left[\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\tau\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right],

(28)

\displaystyle X_{t_{n}t_{n}-2\tau}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\approx\boldsymbol{x}-\tau\left[\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n-2}(\boldsymbol{x}-2\tau\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right],

(29)

where we approximate the velocity field at
t_{n}
by the linear extrapolation
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{*}=2\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n-1}-\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{n-2}
. As described in
Section

4.1.1
, we replace the velocity fields by suitable smooth approximations. We obtain

\displaystyle X_{t_{n},t_{n}-\tau}(\boldsymbol{x})\approx\bar{X}_{t-\tau}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\boldsymbol{x}-\frac{\tau}{2}\left[\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})+\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{n-1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\tau\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right],

(30)

\displaystyle X_{t_{n},t_{n}-2\tau}(\boldsymbol{x})\approx\bar{X}_{t-2\tau}(\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\boldsymbol{x}-\tau\left[\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x})+\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{n-2}(\boldsymbol{x}-2\tau\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{*}(\boldsymbol{x}))\right],

(31)

where
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}^{\bullet}
with
\bullet=*,n-1,n-2
denotes the smoothened version
of
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{\bullet}
as it will be constructed in the next section.

4.1.3
Smooth reconstruction of the velocity field

Given a discrete velocity field
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}^{\musFlat{}}\in\Lambda_{h,p}^{1}(\Omega)
, we can
define a smoothened version
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}
of
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
that is

•

Lipschitz continuous to ensure stable evaluation of (
27
),

•

well-defined on every point of the meshed domain,

•

practically computable, and

•

second-order accurate.

We introduce
\bar{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h}
as follows. Let
h_{\min}
denote the length of the shortest edge of the mesh and
(u_{h}^{i})_{i=1,..,d}
the components of
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
. Then,

\bar{u}_{h}^{i}(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{1}{h_{\min}}\int_{x_{i}-\frac{1}{2}h_{\min}}^{x_{i}+\frac{1}{2}h_{\min}}u^{i}_{h}([x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},\xi,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{d}]^{T})d\xi

(32)

provides a second-order, Lipschitz-continuous approximation of
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
. In the above definition, we can also replace
h_{\text{min}}
by a localized parameter that scales as
\mathcal{O}(h)
with
h
the length of the edges ”close” to
\boldsymbol{x}
. Note that the above integral can be evaluated up to machine precision using the algorithm as described in
Section

3.2
for (
19
). The averaging (
32
) provides a second-order approximation of
\boldsymbol{u}_{h}
on every point in the mesh.

\displaystyle=\left(f^{n},\eta_{h}\right)_{\Omega},

(37a)

\displaystyle\left(\omega^{n}_{h,k},\text{d}\psi_{h}\right)_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=0,

(37b)

\displaystyle\left(\omega_{h,k-1}^{n},\omega_{h,k-1}^{n}\right)_{\Omega}+2\left(\omega_{h,k-1}^{n},\omega^{n}_{h,k}-\omega^{n}_{h,k-1}\right)

for all

. We can apply the same idea for energy-tracking to our second-order scheme as proposed in

6
Conclusion

We have developed a mesh-based semi-Lagrangian discretization of the time-dependent
incompressible Navier-Stokes equations with free boundary conditions recast as a
non-linear transport problem for a momentum 1-form. A linearly implicit fully discrete
version of the scheme enjoys excellent stability properties in the vanishing-viscosity
limit and is applicable to inviscid incompressible Euler flows. However, in this case
conservation of energy has to be enforced separately. Making the reasonable
choice of a time-step size proportional to the mesh width, the algorithm involves only
local computations. Yet, these are significantly more expensive compared to those required
for purely Eulerian finite-element and finite-volume methods. At this point the verdict on
the competitiveness of our semi-Lagrangian scheme is still open.

7
Two formulations of the momentum equation

Consider the momentum equation in (
1
)

\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}-\epsilon\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p=\boldsymbol{0}.

Note that we have by standard vector calculus identities

\displaystyle\Delta\boldsymbol{u}=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u})-\nabla\times\nabla\times\boldsymbol{u},

where we can use
\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{u}=0
to obtain

\displaystyle\Delta\boldsymbol{u}=-\nabla\times\nabla\times\boldsymbol{u}.

This allows us to rewrite the momentum equation as

\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+\epsilon\nabla\times\nabla\times\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p=\boldsymbol{0}.

Using the gradient of the dot-product, we find

\displaystyle\nabla(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{u})=2\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla\boldsymbol{u}+2\boldsymbol{u}\times(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{u}).

This identity allows us to rewrite the momentum equation to

\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{u})-\boldsymbol{u}\times(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{u})+\epsilon\nabla\times\nabla\times\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla\left(-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}+p\right)=\boldsymbol{0}.

From
(
Hiptmair 2002
;
Heumann and Hiptmair 2013
)
, we obtain the identity

\left(\text{L}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\omega\right)^{\musSharp{}}=\nabla(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{u})-\boldsymbol{u}\times(\nabla\times\boldsymbol{u})

where
\omega
is the differential 1-form such that
\boldsymbol{u}=\omega^{\musSharp{}}
. Since the material derivative for this 1-form is

D_{\boldsymbol{u}}\omega\coloneqq\partial_{t}\omega+\text{L}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\omega,

we find that the momentum equation can be written as

D_{\boldsymbol{u}}\omega+\epsilon\delta\text{d}\omega+\text{d}\tilde{p}=0,

where
\tilde{p}=-\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}+p
.
