Restricted slowly growing digits for infinite iterated function systems Thanks: Keywords : Iterated Function Systems (IFSs); continued fraction; Hausdorff dimension.
Gerardo González Robert, Mumtaz Hussain, Nikita Shulga, Hiroki Takahasi

Abstract
. For an infinite iterated function system 𝐟 \mathbf{f} on [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] with an attractor Λ ⁡ ( 𝐟 ) \Lambda(\mathbf{f}) and for an infinite subset D ⊆ ℕ D\subseteq\mathbb{N} , consider the set 𝔼 ⁡ ( 𝐟 , D ) = { x ∈ Λ ⁡ ( 𝐟 ) : a n ​ ( x ) ∈ D ​ for all ​ n ∈ ℕ ​ and ​ lim n → ∞ a n = ∞ } . \mathbb{E}(\mathbf{f},D)=\{x\in\Lambda(\mathbf{f}):a_{n}(x)\in D\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}=\infty\}. For a function φ : ℕ → [ min ⁡ D , ∞ ) \varphi:\mathbb{N}\to[\min D,\infty) such that φ ⁡ ( n ) → ∞ \varphi(n)\to\infty as n → ∞ n\to\infty , we compute the Hausdorff dimension of the set S ⁡ ( 𝐟 , D , φ ) = { x ∈ 𝔼 ⁡ ( 𝐟 , D ) : a n ​ ( x ) ≤ φ ⁡ ( n ) ​ for all ​ n ∈ ℕ } . S(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{f},D):a_{n}(x)\leq\varphi(n)\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}. We prove that the Hausdorff dimension stays the same no matter how slowly the function φ \varphi grows. One of the consequences of our result is the recent work of Takahasi (2023), which only dealt with regular continued fraction expansions. We further extend our result to slowly growing products of (not necessarily consecutive) digits.

1.
Introduction

The theory of infinite Iterated Function Systems on the unit interval includes important number systems such as the regular continued fractions, whose relevance cannot be understated. Recall that
each irrational
x\in(0,1)

has a unique
regular continued fraction expansion

x=1/(a_{1}+1/(a_{2}+\cdots))
, where each partial quotient
a_{n}=a_{n}(x)
,
n\geq 1
belongs to the set
\mathbb{N}
of positive integers.
A pioneering result in the fractal dimension theory of continued fraction is due to
Jarník who proved that the set of irrationals in
(0,1)
with bounded partial quotients
is of Hausdorff dimension
1
.
Good
[
6
]
proved that the set of irrationals in
(0,1)
whose partial quotients tend to infinity
is of Hausdorff dimension
1/2
.
Various extensions and refinements of Good’s theorem have been made which determine fractal dimensions of sets of irrationals whose partial quotients obey certain
restrictions or growth conditions, see
[
2
,
3
,
5
,
7
,
14
,
17
,
18
,
23
,
24
]

for example.

Hirst
[
8
]
proved results analogous to that of Good in the case when
a_{n}
’s belong to some subset of
\mathbb{N}
.
For every infinite subset
D\subseteq\mathbb{N}
,
Hirst considered the set

E(D):=\left\{x\in(0,1)\setminus\mathbb{Q}\colon a_{n}(x)\in D\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and }\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}(x)=\infty\right\}.

He introduced the exponent of convergence

\tau(D):=\inf\left\{s\geq 0:\sum_{n\in D}n^{-s}<\infty\right\},

and proved
that the Hausdorff dimension of
E(D)
does not exceed
\tau(D)/2
.
He also conjectured that
\tau(D)/2
should be the exact value of the Hausdorff dimension for this set. About thirty five years after the appearance of Hirst’s paper, this conjecture was resolved by Wang and Wu in
[
24
, Theorem 1.1]
.

Naturally, one might wonder that if additional restrictions are introduced on the growth rate of
a_{n}
, the Hausdorff dimension may drop. In this regard, there are two natural sets to consider,

\{x\in E(D)\colon a_{n}(x)\geq\varphi(n)\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\},

\left\{x\in E(D)\colon a_{n}(x)\leq\varphi(n)\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\},

where
\varphi\colon\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is a function satisfying
\lim_{n\to\infty}\varphi(n)=\infty
.
Interestingly, the dimension of the first set can drop from
\tau(D)/2
and even become
0
when
\varphi
grows very rapidly
[
2
,
3
,
5
,
14
,
17
,
18
,
25
]
while
the dimension of
the second set does not drop no matter how slowly
\varphi
grows, as precisely stated below. Let
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}
denote the Hausdorff dimension.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
23
]
, Theorem 1.1)
.

Let
D\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be an infinite subset and
\varphi\colon\mathbb{N}\to[\min D,\infty)
such that
\varphi(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. Then,

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}\left\{x\in E(D):a_{n}(x)\leq\varphi(n)\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}=\frac{\tau(D)}{2}.

This ‘no dimension drop’
seems to be a ubiquitous phenomenon, and generalizations of
the above theorem
to diverse settings are desired.
The first generalization of Theorem
1.1
was done by Nakajima and the last-named author in
[
19
]
to the semi-regular continued fractions, viewed as a non-autonomous iterated function system
[
20
]
.
In this paper, we generalize Theorem
1.1
in two directions while providing a much simpler proof.

First, we treat a question of the slow growth of each digit in an infinite Iterated Function System (iIFS) (Theorem
1.2
).
Second, we extend this result to the weighted products of an arbitrary finite number of digits in the given iIFS
(Theorem
1.3
).
Below we introduce a definition of an iIFS and state the main results.

1.1.
Infinite Iterated Function Systems

Consider a sequence
\mathbf{f}=(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of functions
f_{n}:[0,1]\to[0,1]
, satisfying

i.

Smoothness Property:

f_{n}\in C^{1}([0,1])
for each
n\geq 1
;

ii.

Contraction Property:
There exists an integer
m
and a real number
0<\rho<1
, such that for any
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
and
x\in[0,1]
,

0<|(f_{a_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{a_{m}})^{\prime}(x)|\leq\rho<1;

iii.

Open Set Condition:
For any
i\neq j\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
f_{i}((0,1))\cap f_{j}((0,1))=\emptyset.

The system
\mathbf{f}=(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is called an
infinite iterated function system
(iIFS).
By the smoothness and the contraction properties, we can define a natural projection
\Pi_{\mathbf{f}}:\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\to[0,1]
as

\Pi_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbf{a})=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{a_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{a_{n}}(1),

where
\mathbf{a}=(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Also, we denote by
\Lambda(\mathbf{f})
the attractor of the iIFS
(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, that is,

\Lambda(\mathbf{f})=\Pi_{\mathbf{f}}(\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}).

By the open set condition,
to all but countably many points
x
in
\Lambda

we can associate a unique sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of integers such that

x=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{a_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{a_{n}}(1).

For a given
x
we refer to the sequence
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}=(a_{n}(x))_{n\geq 1}
as a
sequence of digits
of
x
.

We say that an iIFS
\mathbf{f}=(f_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is
\boldsymbol{\xi}
-regular
if in addition it satisfies

iv.

Regularity Property
: There exists a sequence of positive numbers
\boldsymbol{\xi}=(\xi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
such that for every
\varepsilon>0
there are positive constants
c_{1},c_{2}
depending on
\varepsilon
such that

\frac{c_{1}}{\xi_{n}^{1+\varepsilon}}\leq|f^{\prime}_{n}(x)|\leq\frac{c_{2}}{\xi_{n}^{1-\varepsilon}}\quad\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and all }x\in[0,1].

If
\xi_{n}=n^{d}
, the
\boldsymbol{\xi}
-regular iIFS
\mathbf{f}
is called
d
-decaying system, as defined by Jordan and Rams in
[
14
]
. Finally, we assume that iIFS satisfies the following condition.

v.

Bounded Distortion Property
(BDP): There exists a constant
\kappa\geq 1
such that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\left|(f_{a_{1}}\circ\ldots\circ f_{a_{n}})^{\prime}(x)\right|\leq\kappa\left|(f_{a_{1}}\circ\ldots\circ f_{a_{n}})^{\prime}(y)\right|\text{ for all }x,y\in[0,1].

We note that all natural examples of IFS satisfy BDP, see Section
1.3
below.

1.2.
Statements of results

Let
\mathbf{f}
be a
\boldsymbol{\xi}
-regular iIFS.
For an infinite subset
D\subseteq\mathbb{N}

define

\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{f},D):=\left\{x\in\Lambda(\mathbf{f}):a_{n}(x)\in D\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N},\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}(x)=\infty\right\},

and

(1)

s_{0}(D;\boldsymbol{\xi}):=\inf\left\{s\geq 0:\sum_{n\in D}\frac{1}{\xi_{n}^{s}}<\infty\right\}.

For
2
-decaying systems such as the regular continued fractions, we have
\tau(D)=2s_{0}(D;\boldsymbol{\xi})
.
In
[
2
]
,
the solution of Hirst’s conjecture was generalized to digits in an iIFS in the following way:
If
\mathbf{f}
satisfies the BDP, then

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{f},D)=s_{0}(D;\boldsymbol{\xi}).

For a function
\varphi:\mathbb{N}\to[\min D,\infty)
,
define

S(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi):=\left\{x\in\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{f},D):a_{n}(x)\leq\varphi(n)\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

The first main result of the present paper is the following statement.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathbf{f}
be a
\boldsymbol{\xi}
-regular iIFS with the BDP. For every infinite subset
D\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and
any function
\varphi:\mathbb{N}\to[\min D,\infty)
such that
\varphi(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
,
we have

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}S(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi)=s_{0}(D;\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Theorem
1.2
concerns the behavior of a single digit.
Kleinbock and Wadleigh
[
15
]
proved that the product of consecutive partial quotients in regular continued fractions is closely related to improvements of Dirichlet’s Theorem in Diophantine approximation. Such sets have been further studied in
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
for example.
Hence, it is natural to generalise Theorem
1.2
to include products of digits.

Using Theorem
1.2
we derive a much more general result involving weighted products of a fixed number of digits.
For a fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}
consider
\overline{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
and a vector
\overline{g}=(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m})
of arbitrary functions
g_{i}\colon\mathbb{N}\ \to\mathbb{N},1\leq i\leq m
. Define a set

P_{m}(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi,\overline{t},\overline{g}):=\left\{x\in\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{f},D):a_{g_{1}(n)}^{t_{1}}(x)\cdots a_{g_{m}(n)}^{t_{m}}(x)\leq\varphi(n)\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

and denote
M(D):=\max\{\min D,(\min D)^{m\max_{i}t_{i}}\}
. We have the following theorem.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\mathbf{f}
,
D
be as in Theorem
1.2
and let
\varphi:\mathbb{N}\to[M(D),\infty)
be such that
\varphi(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
. For any
\overline{t}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
and for any functions
g_{i}\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N},1\leq i\leq m
, we have

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}P_{m}(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi,\overline{t},\overline{g})=\dim_{\mathrm{H}}S(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi)=s_{0}(D;\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Theorem
1.3
means that in fact
the Hausdorff dimension of
P_{m}(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi,\overline{t},\overline{g})
does not depend on
m,\overline{t}
and functions
g_{i}
’s no matter how they behave.
In particular, if we let
g_{i}(n)=n+i
and
\overline{t}=(1,\ldots,1)
, we get a set of slowly growing products of consecutive digits

\left\{x\in\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{f},D):a_{n+1}(x)\cdots a_{n+m}(x)\leq\varphi(n)\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

If we let
g_{i}(n)=ni
and
\overline{t}=(1,\ldots,1)
, we get a set of slowly growing products of digits, located in an arithmetic progression

\left\{x\in\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{f},D):a_{n}(x)\cdots a_{nm}(x)\leq\varphi(n)\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}.

The set of numbers with large products of digits in arithmetic progressions (that is, numbers
x\in[0,1]
with
a_{n}(x)\cdots a_{nm}(x)\geq\varphi(n)
for infinitely many
n
) for the case of the regular continued fractions was studied in
[
12
]
.

1.4.
Outline of proofs of the theorems

The rest of this paper consists of two sections, entirely dedicated to proofs of Theorems
1.2
and

1.3
. In the proofs, the upper bounds of the Hausdorff dimension are rather trivial as they can be obtained from the known results. The issue is to obtain the lower bounds.
A derivation of lower bounds on the Hausdorff dimension of fractal sets often relies on the mass distribution principle
[
4
]
,
and the construction of a mass usually becomes intricate depending on the structure of the fractal set under consideration. In the proof
[
23
, Theorem 1.1]
, the construction of masses on fractal sets heavily relies on the ergodic theory of the Gauss map generating the continued fraction.
Our idea is to dispense with the mass distribution principle altogether, and instead explicitly construct a well-organized Cantor set whose Hausdorff dimension can easily be bounded from below. Starting from a sufficiently large
n
, the compact sets in each layer of our Cantor set correspond to certain admissible digits. The lower estimate of its Hausdorff dimension will be a consequence of two properties: the grandchildren of any compact set appearing in any layer are well separated and the digits defining the layers tend to infinity at a very slow pace.

To show the lower bound in
Theorem
1.3
, we construct several subsets of the set under consideration, eventually showing that for some specific choice of a growth function, the set from Theorem
1.2
is a subset of a set from Theorem
1.3
. The upper bound follows trivially by lifting the restriction on the growth of products.

2.
Lower bound of Hausdorff dimension of certain Cantor subsets

In this section, we prove a lower bound for the Hausdorff dimension of certain Cantor sets. Similar arguments date back to Jarník in 1928
[
13
]

and they have been used in Cao-Wang-Wu
[
2
, Theorem 1.1]
among others much more recently. Our setup is inspired by Kleinbock-Weiss
[
16
, Section 2.4]
.

Denote the diameter of a non-empty set
A\subseteq[0,1]
by
|A|
. Let
\{\mathcal{A}_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
be a collection of finite families of pairwise disjoint compact subintervals of
[0,1]
with a positive diameter such that
\mathcal{A}_{0}
has exactly one element and the following properties hold:

•

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
B\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
, there is some
A\in\mathcal{A}_{n-1}
such that
B\subseteq A
.

•

For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
A\in\mathcal{A}_{n-1}
, there are at least two sets
B\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
such that
B\subseteq A
.

•

We have
\delta_{n}:=\max\{|A|:A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}\}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
, write
\Des_{n}(A):=\{B\in\mathcal{A}_{n+1}:B\subseteq A\}
. Under additional considerations, we provide a lower estimate for the Hausdorff dimension of the set

\mathbf{A}_{\infty}:=\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}\bigcup_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}A.

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume there is some
\varepsilon>0
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
, and every
Y,Z\in\Des_{n}(A)
with
Y\neq Z
we have

(3)

\min\left\{|y-z|:y\in\bigcup_{Y^{\prime}\in\Des_{n+1}(Y)}Y^{\prime},z\in\bigcup_{Z^{\prime}\in\Des_{n+1}(Z)}Z^{\prime}\right\}\geq|A|^{1+\varepsilon}.

If
s>0
satisfies

(4)

\sum_{B\in\Des_{n}(A)}|B|^{s(1+\varepsilon)}\geq|A|^{s(1+\varepsilon)}\quad\text{ for all }\quad A\in\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{A}_{n},

then
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{A}_{\infty}\geq s
.

Proof.

Take
\delta>0
and let
\mathcal{G}
be a
\delta
-covering of
\mathbf{A}_{\infty}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume
\mathcal{G}
is finite, consists of open intervals (see, for example,
[
4
, Section 3.4]
), and every element in
\mathcal{G}
intersects
\mathbf{A}_{\infty}
. For each
G\in\mathcal{G}
define

n(G):=\max\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\#\{A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}:G\cap A\neq\emptyset\}=1\right\}.

Let us explain why
n(G)
is well-defined. First, writing
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\{K_{0}\}
, we have
G\cap K_{0}\neq\emptyset
. Second, take
x\in G\cap\mathbf{A}_{\infty}
and
r>0
such that
(x-r,x+r)\subseteq G
. Since
\delta_{n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, for large
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
A_{n}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
contains
x
, then
A_{n}\subseteq G
, and thus
B\subseteq G
for all
B\in\Des_{n}(A_{n})
. Therefore,
n(G)
is the maximum of a non-empty finite set of integers. Let
A(G)
be the unique compact interval for which the maximum in the definition of
n(G)
is attained. Then there are two different sets
Y,Z\in\Des_{n(G)}(A(G))
intersecting
G
. Pick
Y^{\prime}\in\Des_{n(G)+1}(Y)
and
Z^{\prime}\in\Des_{n(G)+1}(Z)
intersecting
G
. Then, (
4
) yields

|G|\geq\min\{|y-z|:y\in Y^{\prime},z\in Z^{\prime}\}\geq|A(G)|^{1+\varepsilon}.

As a consequence, we have

\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}}|A(G)|^{s(1+\varepsilon)}\leq\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}}|G|^{s}.

Clearly,
\mathcal{G}_{1}:=\{A(G):G\in\mathcal{G}\}
also covers
\mathbf{A}_{\infty}
. Moreover, since every two members in
\mathcal{G}_{1}
are either disjoint or one is contained in the other, there is a subcover
\mathcal{G}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{1}
of
\mathbf{A}_{\infty}
consisting of pairwise disjoint sets. Let
N:=\max\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\mathcal{G}_{2}\cap\mathcal{A}_{n}\neq\emptyset\}
. Pick
B\in\mathcal{G}_{2}\cap\mathcal{A}_{N}
and
A\in\mathcal{A}_{N-1}
such that
B\subseteq A
. Since the members of
\mathcal{G}_{2}
are pairwise disjoint,
\mathcal{G}_{2}
covers
\mathbf{A}_{\infty}
, and we cannot go any level deeper than
N
, we must have
\Des_{N-1}(A)\subseteq\mathcal{G}_{2}
. Hence, if
\mathcal{G}_{3}
is the cover obtained from
\mathcal{G}_{2}
by replacing the sets in
\Des_{N-1}(A)
with
A
, we deduce from (
4
) that

\sum_{C\in\mathcal{G}_{3}}|C|^{s(1+\varepsilon)}\leq\sum_{C\in\mathcal{G}_{2}}|C|^{s(1+\varepsilon)}.

After finitely many replacements, we arrive at
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\{K_{0}\}
and thus

0<|K_{0}|^{s(1+\varepsilon)}\leq\sum_{G\in\mathcal{G}}|G|^{s}.

Therefore, we obtain
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}\mathbf{A}_{\infty}\geq s
.
∎

3.
Proofs of the main results

In this section, we prove Theorem
1.2
and Theorem
1.3
.

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

We separate the proof into two parts: the upper bound and the lower bound. The upper bound trivially follows from Theorem 1.1 in
[
2
]
, because
S(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi)\subseteq\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{f},D)
, and so

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}S(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi)\leq\dim_{\mathrm{H}}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{f},D)=s_{0}(D;\boldsymbol{\xi}).

Now we show the lower bound of
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}S(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi)
, that is

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}S(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi)\geq s_{0}(D;\boldsymbol{\xi}).

For this purpose, we will construct a suitable Cantor subset
K(\mathbf{f},D,\mathbf{l},\mathbf{u})\subseteq S(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi)
, where
\mathbf{l}=(l_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
\mathbf{u}=(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
are two slowly increasing sequences of natural numbers to be defined later.

There is nothing to prove if
s_{0}(D;\boldsymbol{\xi})=0
, as we always have the trivial lower bound

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}S(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi)\geq 0.

Hence, we further assume that
s_{0}(D;\boldsymbol{\xi})>0
. Given
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\overline{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we consider

I_{n}(\overline{a}):=\{x\in\Lambda:a_{k}(x)=a_{k}\text{ for all }k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\}.

We refer to sets of the form
I_{n}(\overline{a})
as
fundamental intervals
. For readability, let us additionally assume
m=1
in the Contraction Property. Then, for all
a\in\mathbb{N}
and all
x\in[0,1]
we have
|f_{a}^{\prime}(x)|\leq\rho
, so

|I_{n}(\overline{a})|\leq\rho^{n}\quad\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and all }\overline{a}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}.

At the end of the proof, we will explain how to proceed when
m\geq 2
.

Let
s,\varepsilon>0
satisfy
s(1+\varepsilon)^{2}<s_{0}(D;\boldsymbol{\xi})
and let
c_{1}=c_{1}(\varepsilon)>0
and
c_{2}=c_{2}(\varepsilon)>0
be as in the definition of the
\boldsymbol{\xi}
-regularity. Let
\kappa\geq 1
be the constant from the Bounded Distortion property. Write
D=\{d_{1}<d_{2}<d_{3}<\cdots\}
.

We now construct two sequences
\mathbf{l}
and
\mathbf{u}
in
\mathbb{N}
. First, in the view of
s(1+\varepsilon)<s_{0}(D,\boldsymbol{\xi})
, we may take a strictly increasing sequence
(r_{k})_{k\geq 1}
in
\mathbb{N}
such that

(5)

r_{k}-k\geq 4\quad\text{ and }\quad\sum_{n=k}^{r_{k}}\left(\frac{\kappa^{-1}c_{1}}{\xi_{d_{n}}^{1+\varepsilon}}\right)^{s(1+\varepsilon)}\geq 3\quad\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{N}.

Let
(N_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be a strictly increasing sequence of natural numbers such that for every
j\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(6)

\displaystyle\varphi(n)

\displaystyle\geq d_{r_{j}}+1\quad\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq N_{j}},\;\text{ and }

(7)

\displaystyle\max

\displaystyle\left\{(\xi_{d_{i}}\xi_{d_{k}})^{1+\varepsilon}:j\leq i\leq r_{j+1},\ j\leq k\leq r_{j+2}\right\}\leq\kappa^{-1}c_{1}^{2}\rho^{-\varepsilon N_{j}}.

Let
\mathbf{l}=(l_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
\mathbf{u}=(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be defined as follows: for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, take
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that

N_{j}-N_{1}+1\leq n\leq N_{j+1}-N_{1}

and put

l_{n}:=j\quad\text{ and }\quad u_{n}:=r_{j}.

Moreover, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
define the set

D_{n}:=\{d_{i}\in D\colon l_{n}\leq i<u_{n}\}.

We now describe the layers of a Cantor set contained in
S(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi)
. First, consider

K_{0}:=\overline{I}_{N_{1}-1}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{1})

Here and in the following, the bar over
I
denotes its closure. For all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
define

K_{n}(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}):=\overline{I}_{N_{1}-1+n}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{1},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}).

Define
B_{1}:=D_{1}
. For each
b\in B_{1}
, let
L(b)\in D_{2}
(resp.
R(b)\in D_{2}
) be such that
K_{2}(b,L(b))
(resp.
K_{2}(b,R(b))
) is the leftmost (resp. rightmost) interval in
\{K_{2}(b,b_{2}):b_{2}\in D_{2}\}
and write

B_{2}(b):=\left\{(b,b_{2}):b_{2}\in D_{1}\setminus\{L(b),R(b)\}\right\}.

Define

B_{2}:=\bigcup_{b_{1}\in B_{1}}B_{2}(b_{1}).

In what follows, for any
j,k\in\mathbb{N}
and any
\overline{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{j})\in\mathbb{N}^{j}
and
\overline{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
, we write

\overline{a}\overline{b}:=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{j},b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}).

Let us assume that for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have defined a non-empty finite set
B_{n}\subseteq D_{1}\times\cdots\times D_{n}
. For each
\overline{b}\in B_{n}
, let
L(\overline{b})\in D_{n+1}
(resp.
R(\overline{b})\in D_{n+1}
) be such that
K_{n+1}(\overline{b}L(\overline{b}))
(resp.
K_{n+1}(\overline{b}R(\overline{b}))
) is the leftmost (resp. rightmost) interval in
\left\{K_{n+1}(\overline{b}b):b\in D_{n+1}\right\}
and write

B_{n+1}(\overline{b}):=\left\{\overline{b}b:b\in D_{n+1}\setminus\{L(\overline{b}),R(\overline{b})\}\right\}.

Define

B_{n+1}:=\bigcup_{\overline{b}\in B_{n}}B_{n+1}(\overline{b}).

Note that each set
B_{n}
depends on
D_{n}
, which in turn is defined in terms of
\mathbf{l}
and
\mathbf{u}
, and hence of
(N_{j})_{j\geq 1}
. We will apply Lemma
2.1
to estimate the Hausdorff dimension of

K=K(\mathbf{f},D,\mathbf{l},\mathbf{u}):=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\bigcup_{\overline{b}\in B_{n}}K_{n}(\overline{b})\subseteq S(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi).

Let us check that
K
is indeed a subset of
S(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi)
. Pick any
x\in K
. It suffices to check the growth and the limit conditions, for the definition of
K
implies that every digit of
x
belongs to
D
. Take
n\in\mathbb{N}
. If
1\leq n\leq N_{1}-1
, then

a_{n}(x)=d_{1}\leq\min_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\varphi(k)\leq\varphi(n).

If
n\geq N_{1}
, let
j\in\mathbb{N}
be the single number for which
N_{j}\leq n\leq N_{j+1}-1
. Then we have
N_{j}-N_{1}+1\leq n-N_{1}+1\leq N_{j+1}-N_{1}
, and so
l_{n-N_{1}+1}=j
,
u_{n-N_{1}+1}=r_{j}
. This yields

a_{n}(x)\in D_{n-N_{1}+1}=\{d_{j}<\cdots<d_{r_{j}}\}.

As a result, by (
6
) and the choice of
j
we obtain

d_{j}\leq a_{n}(x)\leq d_{r_{j}}\leq\varphi(n).

Finally, as
n\to\infty
we have
j\to\infty
,
d_{j}\to\infty
and so
a_{n}(x)\to\infty
. We conclude
x\in S(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi)
.

Define
\mathcal{A}_{0}:=\{K_{0}\}
and
\mathcal{A}_{n}:=\{K_{n}(\overline{b}):\overline{b}\in B_{n}\}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Let us verify the assumptions on
\{\mathcal{A}_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
from Section
2
. Take
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Clearly,
\mathcal{A}_{n}
consists of finitely many pairwise disjoint compact intervals with positive diameter. The three points assumed on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
also hold:

•

The first point follows from the construction of
B_{n}\subseteq D_{1}\times\cdots\times D_{n}
.

•

By
r_{n}-n\geq 4
, for any
A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
the set
\Des_{n}(A)
has at least two elements.

•

Since we have assumed
m=1
in the Contraction Property, we have
\delta_{n}\leq\rho^{N_{1}-1}\rho^{n}
.

To check (
3
) from Lemma
2.1
, take
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that
N_{j}-N_{1}+1\leq n\leq N_{j+1}-N_{1}
, and
\overline{b}\in B_{n}
. First, note that if
b_{n+1}\in D_{n+1}
is such that
\overline{b}b_{n+1}\in B_{n+1}
then for all
b_{n+2}\in D_{n+2}
we have

\displaystyle|K_{n+2}(\overline{b}b_{n+1}b_{n+2})|

\displaystyle\geq|K_{n}(\overline{b})|\frac{\kappa^{-1}c_{1}^{2}}{(\xi_{b_{n+1}}\xi_{b_{n+2}})^{1+\varepsilon}}

\displaystyle\geq|K_{n}(\overline{b})|\rho^{\varepsilon N_{j}}

(by (
7
))

\displaystyle\geq|K_{n}(\overline{b})|\rho^{\varepsilon n}

\displaystyle\geq|K_{n}(\overline{b})|^{1+\varepsilon}.

Let
b,c\in D_{n+1}
and
b^{\prime},c^{\prime}\in D_{n+2}
be such that
\overline{b}bb^{\prime},\overline{b}cc^{\prime}\in B_{n+2}
. Then the construction of
B_{n+2}
implies that the distance between
K_{n+2}(\overline{b}bb^{\prime})
and
K_{n+2}(\overline{b}cc^{\prime})
is at least the length of one of the removed intervals:

\left\{K_{n+2}(\overline{d}):\overline{d}\in\{\overline{b}bL(\overline{b}b),\overline{b}bR(\overline{b}b),\overline{b}cL(\overline{b}c),\overline{b}cR(\overline{b}c)\}\right\}.

This length is larger than or equal to
|K_{n}(\overline{b})|^{1+\varepsilon}
, which shows (
3
) from Lemma
2.1
.

Condition (
4
) holds because the Bounded Distortion Property and (
5
) imply

\begin{split}\sum_{d\in B_{n+1}(\overline{b})}|K_{n+1}(\overline{b}d)|^{s(1+\varepsilon)}&\geq|K_{n}(\overline{b})|^{s(1+\varepsilon)}\left(\sum_{d\in D_{n+1}}\left(\frac{\kappa^{-1}c_{1}}{\xi_{d}^{1+\varepsilon}}\right)^{s(1+\varepsilon)}-2\right)\\
&\geq|K_{n}(\overline{b})|^{s(1+\varepsilon)}.\end{split}

Therefore, by Lemma
2.1
we obtain
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}S(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi)\geq\dim_{\mathrm{H}}K\geq s
as required.

When
m\geq 2
, we add
m
digits in each layer of our Cantor set construction and adjust the parameters accordingly. More precisely, the sequence
(r_{k})_{k\geq 1}
remains unchanged.
For
(N_{j})_{j\geq 1}
, instead of (
6
) we assume that for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\varphi(n)\geq d_{r_{j}}+1
when
n\geq mN_{j}
and instead of
(
7
) we impose

\max\left\{\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\xi_{d_{i_{k}}}^{1+\varepsilon}:j\leq i_{1}\leq r_{j+1},\ j\leq i_{k}\leq r_{j+2}\text{ for }2\leq k\leq 2m\right\}\leq\kappa^{-1}c_{1}^{2m}\rho^{-\varepsilon N_{j}}.

In order to define the sequences
\mathbf{l}=(l_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
\mathbf{u}=(u_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
take
j\in\mathbb{N}
such that

m(N_{j}-N_{1})+1\leq n\leq m(N_{j+1}-N_{1})

and put
l_{n}:=j
and
u_{n}:=r_{j}
. We start with
K_{0}:=\overline{I}_{mN_{1}-1}(d_{1},\ldots,d_{1})
. Next, for each
\overline{b}\in B_{1}:=D_{1}\times\cdots\times D_{m}
we let
L(\overline{b})
and
R(\overline{b})
be the vectors in
D_{m+1}\times\cdots\times D_{2m}
such that
K_{2m}(\overline{a}L(\overline{b}))
and
K_{2m}(\overline{b}R(\overline{b}))
are, respectively, the leftmost and rightmost intervals in the collection
\left\{K_{2m}(\overline{b}\overline{c}):\overline{c}\in D_{m+1}\times\cdots\times D_{2m}\right\}
. Afterwards, we write

B_{2}(\overline{b}):=\left\{\overline{b}\overline{c}:\overline{c}\in D_{m+1}\times\cdots\times D_{2m}\}\setminus\{L(\overline{b}),R(\overline{b})\right\}.

Finally, we define

B_{2}:=\bigcup_{\overline{a}\in B_{1}}B_{2}(\overline{b}).

The rest of the argument now closely follows the case
m=1
.
This completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
. ∎

3.2.
Proof of
Theorem
1.3

The proof is once again split into the upper bound and the lower bound.
The upper bound trivially follows from the definition of the set and

[
2
, Theorem 1.1]
, as we always have

P_{m}(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi,\overline{t},\overline{g})\subseteq\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{f},D),

and so

\dim_{\mathrm{H}}P_{m}(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi,\overline{t},\overline{g})\leq\dim_{\mathrm{H}}\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{f},D)=s_{0}(D;\boldsymbol{\xi}).

As for the lower bound, we will step-by-step construct subsets of the original set
P_{m}(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi,\overline{t},\overline{g})
and eventually come to a subset of the form

P_{m}(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi,\overline{t},\overline{g})\supseteq S(\mathbf{f},D,\zeta)

for a particular choice of a function
\zeta:\mathbb{N}\to[\min D,\infty).
By the result of Theorem
1.2
this will imply Theorem
1.3
.

First, without loss of generality we can assume that
\varphi
is strictly increasing, as for any function
\varphi:\mathbb{N}\to[\min D,\infty)
such that
\varphi(n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
we can always find another function
\phi
that is strictly increasing and satisfies
\phi(n)<\varphi(n)
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
This means we can always pass, if needed, to a subset

P_{m}(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi,\overline{t},\overline{g})\supseteq P_{m}(\mathbf{f},D,\phi,\overline{t},\overline{g})

with
\phi
being a strictly increasing function.

In what follows we assume that
\varphi
is strictly increasing. It is easy to see that if one has

a_{g_{i}(n)}(x)\leq\varphi(n)^{\frac{1}{mt_{i}}}\text{ for each }1\leq i\leq m,\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N},

then

a_{g_{1}(n)}^{t_{1}}(x)\cdots a_{g_{m}(n)}^{t_{m}}(x)\leq\varphi(n)\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

Hence the first subset we consider is

\begin{split}P_{m}(\mathbf{f},D,\varphi,\overline{t},\overline{g})&\supseteq\left\{x\in\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{f},D):a_{g_{i}(n)}(x)\leq\varphi(n)^{\frac{1}{mt_{i}}},1\leq i\leq m,\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\\
&=:G_{1}.\end{split}

Now let
I_{i}:=g_{i}(\mathbb{N})
.
For each
1\leq i\leq m
and for each
k\in I_{i}
,
let

M_{i,k}:=\min\{r\in\mathbb{N}\colon g_{i}(r)=k\}.

Note that
M_{i,k}
is well-defined as the minimum in the definition is taken over a non-empty set of natural numbers. The second subset we consider is

G_{1}\supseteq\left\{x\in\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{f},D):a_{r_{i}}(x)\leq\varphi(M_{i,r_{i}})^{\frac{1}{mt_{i}}},\text{ for all }r_{i}\in I_{i},1\leq i\leq m\right\}=:G_{2}.

For each
1\leq i\leq m
, define a set
C_{i}=\mathbb{N}\backslash I_{i}

For any
k\in C_{i}
, let

\displaystyle R_{i,k}:=\left\{\begin{split}&\min\{\{M_{i,j}\}_{j\in I_{i}}\cap\mathbb{N}_{>k}\},&&\quad\text{ if }\{M_{i,j}\}_{j\in I_{i}}\cap\mathbb{N}_{>k}\neq\emptyset,\\
&\max\{\{M_{i,j}\}_{j\in I_{i}}\cap\mathbb{N}_{<k}\}+k,&&\quad\text{ otherwise.}\end{split}\right.

Note that
R_{i,k}
is also well-defined as at least one of the sets
\{M_{i,j}\}_{j\in I_{i}}\cap\mathbb{N}_{>k}
or
\{M_{i,j}\}_{j\in I_{i}}\cap\mathbb{N}_{<k}
is non-empty, because
I_{i}
is always non-empty.

For each
1\leq i\leq m
, define a function
\zeta_{i}\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
by

\displaystyle\zeta_{i}(n):=\left\{\begin{split}&\varphi(M_{i,n})^{\frac{1}{mt_{i}}},&&\quad n\in I_{i},\\
&\varphi(R_{i,n})^{\frac{1}{mt_{i}}},&&\quad n\in C_{i}.\end{split}\right.

It is easy to see that each
\zeta_{i}(n)
is non-decreasing and tending to infinity as
n\to\infty.
Indeed, for each
1\leq i\leq m
either
I_{i}
or
C_{i}
is infinite. If
I_{i}
is infinite, then
\{M_{i,k}\}_{k\in I_{i}}
forms a non-decreasing sequence, tending to infinity as
k\to\infty
. If
C_{i}
is infinite, then
\{R_{i,k}\}_{k\in C_{i}}
forms a non-decreasing sequence, tending to infinity as
k\to\infty
. As
\varphi
is a strictly increasing function, we get the claim.

Finally, define a function
\zeta\colon\mathbb{N}\to[\min D,\infty)
by

\zeta(n):=\min\{\zeta_{1}(n),\ldots,\zeta_{m}(n)\}.

For any
1\leq i\leq m
and for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\zeta(n)\leq\zeta_{i}(n)
, and so we can pass to the final subset

G_{2}\supseteq\left\{x\in\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{f},D):a_{n}(x)\leq\zeta(n),\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}=:G_{3}=S(\mathbf{f},D,\zeta).

As
\zeta(n)
is non-decreasing and tending to infinity as
n\to\infty
, by Theorem
1.2
we get the desired conclusion. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.3
.∎
