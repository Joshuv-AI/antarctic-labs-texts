Weak solutions for the Stokes system for compressible fluids with general pressure
Maja Szlenk

Abstract
We prove existence and uniqueness of global in time weak solutions for the Stokes system for compressible fluids with a general, non-monotone pressure. We construct the solution at the level of Lagrangian formulation and then define the transformation to the original Eulerian coordinates. For nonnegative and bounded initial density the solution is also nonnegative for all t t and belongs to L ∞ ​ ( [ 0 , ∞ ) × 𝕋 d ) L^{\infty}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}) . A key point of our considerations is the uniqueness of such transformation. Since the velocity might not be Lipschitz continuous, we develop a method which relies on the results of Crippa & De Lellis, concerning regular Lagriangian flows. The uniqueness is obtained thanks to the application of a certain weighted flow and detail analysis based on the properties of the B ​ M ​ O BMO space.

Introduction

The Stokes system is an approximation of the Navier-Stokes equations for small Reynolds number. In such cases the advective intertial forces are relatively small and explicit dependence on time and convective term can be omitted. This is a typical situation for highly viscous fluids or when the velocities are very small. The flows satisfying these conditions are called Stokes or creeping, and they occur in numerous biological and physical problems, e.g. to describe dynamics of the blood in a process of sedimentation
[
31
]
, or to model swimming of microorganisms
[
11
,
17
,
20
]
. Other applications include also engineering, where the Stokes flow is used in the process of designing microfluids and microdevices
[
18
,
36
]
. The Stokes model is also connected to the Darcy law, which describes the flow of a fluid through porous media. Such phenomena are observed in biological tissues
[
2
,
10
]
and have many applications in petroleum engineering
[
21
,
27
]
. The other situation, where the fluid motion is governed by the Stokes equation is a laminar flow. In this case the fluid particles move in adjacent layers, with little mixing between them.

In this article we consider the compressible Stokes flow on the

1.1
Statement of the main theorem.

The mathematical theory of weak solutions to compressible fluid equations has been widely developing in the last twenty years, since the groundbreaking results of Lions in 1998
[
22
]
and Feireisl
[
12
,
16
]
in 2001. They proved the existence of weak solutions to compressible Navier-Stokes equations, provided that the pressure term is of the form
p(\varrho)=\varrho^{\gamma}
with
\gamma>\frac{9}{5}
and
\gamma>\frac{3}{2}
respectively. In
[
13
]
and
[
37
]
, this method was also adjusted to the pressure which is non-monotone on some finite interval. In particular, it allows to deal with the pressures expressed via equations of state, which are of more complex form than ideal gas, the model example being the van der Waals’ equation of state. The Lions & Feireisl approach can be also adjusted to more complex systems, for example Navier-Stokes-Fourier system
[
15
,
14
]
and other including entropy transport
[
23
]
or heat conductivity
[
3
]
.

The important results concerning non-monotone pressure laws are due to D. Bresch and P.-E. Jabin
[
4
,
5
]
. Their method, based on the Kolmogorov compactness criterion, allows to deal with the pressure satisfying

C^{-1}\varrho^{\gamma}-C\leq p(\varrho)\leq C\varrho^{\gamma}+C\quad\text{ and }\quad|p^{\prime}(\varrho)|\leq\bar{p}\varrho^{\gamma-1}

(1.4)

for
\gamma>\frac{9}{5}
. In context of our work, we refer the reader especially to
[
5
]
, where they presented their approach also on a modification of the Stokes system. In this case they proved the existence of global weak solutions for any
\gamma>1
with the same regularity as in the isentropic case, namely
\varrho\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\gamma})
.

Besides the relaxed conditions on the pressure, the Bresch & Jabin compactness criterion can be also applied to various classes of equations, where the Lions and Feireisl method was insufficient. One of the examples are the systems with the additional term in the continuity equation. Such models can be obtained from the multi-fluid systems
[
6
]
or appear in the mathematical modelling of tumor growth
[
39
]
. In this case the additional term results in lack of compensated compactness between
\textrm{div}u
and the pressure and therefore the classical method fails. However, the Bresch &
Jabin criterion allows to omit this problem. The problem with the convergence of effective viscous flux arise also in the anisotropic case. The recent result
[
1
]
, concerning anisotropic compressible Stokes system, omits this problem by controlling a certain defect measure associated to the pressure.

The other related topic are steady flows, where the behaviour of the fluid does not depend on time. The equations describing such flows are the classical equations of fluid mechanics with the time derivative set as zero, for example compressible Navier-Stokes
[
33
,
34
]
or Navier-Stokes-Fourier system
[
25
,
19
]
. Again, in the case of the more general pressure laws, the classical Lions-Feireisl method cannot be directly applied to obtain a weak solution. One of the ways to cope with that problem was introducing the variational entropy solutions
[
28
,
29
]
, where the total energy balance is replaced with entropy inequality. Another analyzed system is the steady Oseen flow
[
32
]
, which is a linearization of the Navier-Stokes system with partial consideration of the convective forces. Note that in our case the explicit dependence on time is removed only in the momentum equation, therefore this system can be consider as an intermediate step between steady and unsteady flows.

As the Stokes system has a simpler structure than the Navier-Stokes, the analysis can be carried out in the more general setting. If
\varrho_{0}
has higher regularity, then the solutions to the Stokes problem exist and are unique for the general

(see
[
22
]
, Remark 8.14). However, in case of
\varrho_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, the classical method requires the monotonicity condition on the pressure and the uniqueness was not established. In this paper we obtain the uniqueness of solutions to the Stokes system in case of the low regularity of the initial density and under very general pressure laws. Our main theorem states as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\varrho_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,
\varrho_{0}\geq 0
and the pressure satisfy (
1.3
). Then there exists a unique global in time weak solution to (
1.2

\displaystyle\varrho

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}),

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}),

\displaystyle\nabla u

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}([0,\infty);BMO),\;\mathrm{div}\,u\in L^{\infty}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}).

Our approach is based on the Lagrangian reformulation of the system, which allows us to obtain a global
L^{\infty}
estimate on the density. Having that estimate, we can straightforwardly apply Bresch and Jabin method to obtain compactness, and in consequence existence of solutions besides the relaxed conditions on the pressure. However, using the results from the theory of transport equations and classical harmonic analysis, we were able to establish also uniqueness of solutions.

The first step is to show the existence of a unique solution in the Lagrangian formulation. Then, it suffices to define a transformation back to the original Eulerian coordinates. However, there appear some difficulties in the construction, concerning low regularity of the solution of the Lagrangian reformulation. The obtained regularity of

provides that

\nabla u
may not be bounded in

generated by

may not be invertible on the whole torus. Nevertheless, the divergence of

is a regular Lagrangian flow and we can treat it using the properties from
[
7
]
and
[
8

The above reasoning, however, does not preserve uniqueness. We need to prove the latter by taking two solutions

Having the crucial

Discussion on the pressure

Observe that our condition in particular includes the assumptions from

Such class of possible pressures includes many physical situations. It contains the cases which were covered before, for example van der Waals’ fluid. Moreover, the admissible pressures also can be expressed via virial expansion, namely defined as a power series of the density:

The other case, where our result may be applicable, is the use in biological models, where the pressure term is responsible for interactions between different biological agents and therefore can take form other than resulting from physical constitutive laws.

Let us present some further properties of

•

Condition (

Let

•

•

\varrho_{t}+\mathrm{div}\,(\varrho u)=0,\quad\varrho(0,\cdot)=\varrho_{0},

(3.1)

then the function
\varrho(t,x(t,y))
is equal to

\varrho(t,x(t,y))=\varrho_{0}(y)\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\text{div}u(s,x(s,y))\mathrm{d}s\right)=\varrho_{0}(y)\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,y)\mathrm{d}s\right)

and therefore
\eta(t,y)=\varrho(t,x(t,y))
satisfies the first equation of (
2.2
) with
\sigma(t,y)=\mathrm{div}\,u(t,x(t,y))
. Furthermore, taking the second equation of (
2.2
) at a point
x(t,y)
, we obtain

\sigma(t,y)=p(\eta(t,y))-\frac{1}{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\int p(\varrho(t,x))\mathrm{d}x.

However, by Lemma 3.1. from
[
7
]
, for any
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
we have

\int f(x)\mathrm{d}x=\int f(x(t,y))e^{\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{div}\,u(s,x(s,y))\mathrm{d}s}\mathrm{d}y=\int f(x(t,y))e^{\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,y)\mathrm{d}s}\mathrm{d}y.

Thus

\int p(\varrho(t,x))\mathrm{d}x=\int p(\varrho(t,x(t,y))e^{\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,y)\mathrm{d}s}\mathrm{d}y=\int p(\eta(t,y))e^{\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,y)\mathrm{d}s}\mathrm{d}y

and
\sigma
satisfies the second equation of (
2.2
).

From the uniqueness of solutions in the Lagrangian formulation, we conclude that if
(\varrho_{i},u_{i})
,
i=1,2
are solutions to (
1.2
), then they are equal at the level of Lagrangian coordinates. In particular,

\mathrm{div}\,u_{1}(t,x_{1}(t,y))=\mathrm{div}\,u_{2}(t,x_{2}(t,y))=\sigma(t,y).

Therefore the uniqueness of the solutions to (
1.2
) is equivalent to uniqueness of solutions to the equation

\mathrm{div}\,u(t,x(t,y))=\sigma(t,y),

(3.2)

where
\sigma\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
is given and
x(t,y)
satisfies an ODE

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t,y)

\displaystyle=u(t,x),

(3.3)

\displaystyle x(0,y)

\displaystyle=y.

Having the unique
u
, the uniqueness of
\varrho
follows then again from the classical results from transport theory. The regularity of
u
provides that in particular
u\in L^{1}(0,T;W^{1,p})
for some
p\geq 1
and
\mathrm{div}\,u\in L^{1}(0,T;L^{\infty})
. Therefore there exists a unique
\varrho\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
, which is a solution to the continuity equation (
3.1
).

3.1
Definition of the flow
x_{s}

For
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
being the solutions to (
3.2
)-(
3.3
), we introduce the
weighted flow between
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
, that is a function
x_{s}(t,y),\;s\in[0,1]
such that
x_{s}
for
s=0
is the flow generated by
u_{2}
and for
s=1
the flow generated by
u_{1}
. Such
x_{s}
is defined by the ordinary differential equation

\displaystyle\dot{x_{s}}(t,y)

\displaystyle=su_{1}(t,x_{s})+(1-s)u_{2}(t,x_{s})

(3.4)

\displaystyle x_{s}(0,y)

\displaystyle=y

for
s\in[0,1]
. Note that the Jacobian
J_{s}
of
x_{s}
satisfies the same bounds as the Jacobian
J
of
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
, namely

e^{-L}\leq J_{s}(t,y)\leq e^{L}

(3.5)

with
\displaystyle L=\int_{0}^{T}\|s\mathrm{div}\,u_{1}(t,x_{s})+(1-s)\mathrm{div}\,u_{2}(t,x_{s})\|_{\infty}\mathrm{d}t\leq\int_{0}^{T}\|\sigma\|_{\infty}\mathrm{d}t
.

The first step to show uniqueness is to obtain certain
L^{p}
estimates for the derivative of
x_{s}
with respect to
s
.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
x_{s}
is defined by (
3.4
) for
u_{1},u_{2}\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
such that
\nabla u_{i}\in L^{\infty}(0,T;BMO)
,
i=1,2
, then for sufficiently small
t
,
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\left(t,\cdot\right)\in L^{p}
for some
p>4
.

Proof: differentiating
\dot{x}_{s}
with respect to
s
, we get

\frac{\mathrm{d}\dot{x}_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}=u_{1}(t,x_{s})-u_{2}(t,x_{s})+(s\nabla u_{1}(t,x_{s})+(1-s)\nabla u_{2}(t,x_{s}))\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s},

(3.6)

hence from the Gronwall’s lemma

\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right|\leq\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}|s\nabla u_{1}(\tau,x_{s})+(1-s)\nabla u_{2}(\tau,x_{s})|\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\int_{0}^{t}|u_{1}(\tau,x_{s})-u_{2}(\tau,x_{s})|\mathrm{d}\tau.

Let
\nabla v=s\nabla u_{1}+(1-s)\nabla u_{2}
. From the Hölder inequality,

\displaystyle\int\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right|^{p}\mathrm{d}y\leq

\displaystyle\left(\int\exp\left(2p\int_{0}^{t}|\nabla v(\tau,x_{s})|\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\mathrm{d}y\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\times\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int|u_{1}(\tau,x_{s})-u_{2}(\tau,x_{s})|^{2p}\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{1/2}.

As
u_{1},u_{2}\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
, the term

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{t}\int|u_{1}(\tau,x_{s})-u_{2}(\tau,x_{s})|^{2p}\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\tau\right)^{1/2}

is bounded. We will now estimate

I=\int\exp\left(2p\int_{0}^{t}|\nabla v(\tau,x_{s}(\tau,y))\mathrm{d}\tau\right)\mathrm{d}y.

By Jensen’s inequality and the bounds on Jacobian
J_{s}
, we have

\displaystyle\int e^{2p\int_{0}^{t}|\nabla v(\tau,x_{s}(\tau,y))|\mathrm{d}\tau}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\int\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{2pt|\nabla v(\tau,x_{s}(\tau,y))|}\;\mathrm{d}\tau\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\frac{1}{J_{s}}\right\|_{\infty,T}\int\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}e^{2pt|\nabla v(\tau,x_{s}(\tau,y))|}J_{s}(\tau,y)\;\mathrm{d}\tau\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq e^{L}\frac{1}{t}\int_{0}^{t}\int e^{2pt|\nabla v(\tau,x)|}\;\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}\tau.

From the fact that
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|\nabla u_{i}\|_{BMO}\leq C
for
i=0,1
, we know that
\nabla v\in L^{\infty}(0,T;BMO)
and from the Corollary
C.4

\int e^{2pt|\nabla v(\tau,x)|}\mathrm{d}x\leq C\;\;\text{ for all }\;\;p\leq\frac{C}{2t\|\nabla v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;BMO)}}.

In particular, for sufficiently small
t
we have
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
for some
p>4
. ∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
x_{s}
be as in Lemma
3.2
and let
T_{1}
be such that
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\in L^{p}
for
p>4
and
t\in[0,T_{1}]
. Then
\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\left(t,\cdot\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}
satisfies the inequality

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq C\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left(1+\left|\ln\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right|\right)+C\|u_{1}(t,\cdot)-u_{2}(t,\cdot)\|_{2}^{2}

(3.7)

for
t\in[0,T_{1}]
.

Proof: multiplying both sides of (
3.6
) by
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}
, we obtain

\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right|^{2}=\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\nabla v(t,x_{s})\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}+\big(u_{1}(t,x_{s})-u_{2}(t,x_{s})\big)\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}.

(3.8)

Integrating (
3.8
) over torus, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq 2\left|\int\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\nabla v(t,x_{s})\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathrm{d}y\right|+2\int|u_{1}(t,x_{s})-u_{2}(t,x_{s})|\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right|\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq 2\left|\int\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\nabla v(t,x_{s})\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathrm{d}y\right|+\int|u_{1}(t,x_{s})-u_{2}(t,x_{s})|^{2}\mathrm{d}y+\int\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}y.

From the bound (
3.5
) on
J_{s}
, we have

\int|u_{1}(t,x_{s})-u_{2}(t,x_{s})|^{2}\mathrm{d}y\leq C\|u_{1}(t,\cdot)-u_{2}(t,\cdot)\|_{2}^{2}.

By the regularity of
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}
from Lemma
3.2
,
\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right|^{2}\in L^{q}
for some
q>2
. Therefore we can apply Corollary
C.7
to estimate
\displaystyle\left|\int\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\nabla v(t,x_{s})\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathrm{d}y\right|
. In consequence we obtain the inequality (
3.7
), where
C
depends on
\|\nabla v\|_{BMO}
,
\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right\|_{q}
and
\|J\|_{\infty}
. ∎

3.2
The final argument

Having the results from the previous subsection, we can now prove the uniqueness:

Theorem 3.4
.

The solution to system (
3.2
)-(
3.3
) with regularity from Theorem
1.1
is unique.

Proof: Let
u_{1},u_{2}
be the solutions to (
3.2
)-(
3.3
),
u_{i}=\nabla\phi_{i}
and
x_{1},x_{2}
are the flows generated by
u_{1},u_{2}
respectively. We will show that
\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{2}=0
for all
t\in[0,T]
. By the weak formulation of
\mathrm{div}\,u(t,x(t,y))=\sigma(t,y)
, for any
\xi\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
we have

\displaystyle\int(u_{1}(t,x)-u_{2}(t,x))\nabla\xi(t,x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int(\mathrm{div}\,u_{1}(t,x)-\mathrm{div}\,u_{2}(t,x))\xi(t,x)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int\sigma(t,y)J(t,y)(\xi(t,x_{1}(t,y))-\xi(t,x_{2}(t,y)))\mathrm{d}y.

Using definition of the flow
x_{s}
, we can rewrite the last integral as

\int\sigma(t,y)J(t,y)\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\xi(t,x_{s}(t,y))\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}y=\int\sigma(t,y)J(t,y)\int_{0}^{1}\nabla\xi(t,x_{s}(t,y))\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}(t,y)}{\mathrm{d}s}\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}y.

By the density of smooth functions in
W^{1,2}
, we can choose
\xi=\phi_{1}-\phi_{2}
. Then
\nabla\xi=u_{1}-u_{2}
and we obtain

\displaystyle\int|u_{1}-u_{2}|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\int\sigma(t,y)J(t,y)\int_{0}^{1}(u_{1}(t,x_{s}(t,y))-u_{2}(t,x_{s}(t,y)))\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}(t,y)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq\|\sigma\|_{\infty}\|J\|_{\infty}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{2}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s.

Hence

\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{2}\leq C\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s.

(3.9)

Substituting (
3.9
) into (
3.7
) and integrating over
s
, we get

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right\|_{2}^{2}\;\mathrm{d}s\leq C\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right\|_{2}^{2}\left(1+\left|\ln\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right|\right)\;\mathrm{d}s+C\left(\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right\|_{2}\mathrm{d}s\right)^{2}

for
t\in[0,T_{1}]
for some
T_{1}\leq T
.

Let
\alpha(t)=\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right\|_{2}^{2}\mathrm{d}s
. As the function
x(1+|\ln x|)
is concave for
x<1
and
x^{2}
is convex, we can estimate both terms in the right hand side from Jensen’s inequality and obtain

\dot{\alpha}\leq C\alpha(1+|\ln\alpha|)+C\alpha.

From Osgood’s lemma, the problem

\dot{z}=Cz(1+|\ln z|),\quad z(0)=0

has a unique solution
z\equiv 0
. Therefore, as
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}_{|_{t=0}}=0
, we have

\alpha(t)\leq 0\textrm{ for all }t\in[0,T_{1}].

In conclusion,
\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\equiv 0
for all
t\in[0,T_{1}]
and so is
\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{2}
. Having that, we can perform analogous reasoning on the consecutive intervals
[nT_{1},(n+1)T_{1}]
to get
u_{1}=u_{2}
for all
t\in[0,T]
. ∎

4
The existence of solutions to (

)-(

In this section we prove that the transformation from equation (

3.3

1.1

(\eta,\sigma)
in the Lagrangian coordinates by Theorem

and

)-(

provides us also existence of the density

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\sigma\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
. There exists
u\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
such that
u
is a solution to system (
3.2
)-(
3.3
) and

\mathrm{div}\,u\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d}),\;\nabla u\in L^{\infty}(0,T;BMO).

First, we prove the existence for smoothened
\sigma
, by putting

\sigma_{\delta}=\sigma\ast\kappa_{\delta},

where
\kappa_{\delta}
is a standard mollifier.

Lemma 4.2
.

There exists a unique
u_{\delta}\in C(0,T;W^{1,\infty})
satisfying

\mathrm{div}\,u_{\delta}(t,x_{\delta}(t,y))=\sigma_{\delta}(t,y),

where
x_{\delta}(t,y)
is given by (
3.3
).

Proof: We define the suitable map, and then apply the Banach fixed point theorem. Let

\Phi\colon C(0,T;W^{1,\infty})\to C(0,T;W^{1,\infty})

be defined in the following way:

1.

If
\bar{u}\in C(0,T;W^{1,\infty})
, then
\bar{u}
is Lipschitz, so there exists a unique solution to system

\dot{x}=\bar{u}(t,x),\quad x(0)=y.

(4.1)

2.

We can now invert
x(t,y)
to get
y(t,x)
instead. After differentiation of (
4.1
) with respect to
y
, we get an ODE for the matrix
H(t,y)=\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}(t,y)
:

\partial_{t}H=\nabla_{x}\bar{u}(t,x(t,y))H,\;H(0,y)=I.

(4.2)

Moreover, the equation for
J(t,y)=\det H(t,y)
yields

\partial_{t}J(t,y)=\text{div}_{x}\bar{u}(t,x(t,y))J(t,y).

(4.3)

Therefore we have the estimates

\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla\bar{u}\|_{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\right)\leq\|H\|_{\infty}\leq\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|\nabla\bar{u}\|_{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\right),

and

\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{T}\|\text{div}\bar{u}\|_{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\right)\leq\|J\|_{\infty}\leq\exp\left(\int_{0}^{T}\|\text{div}\bar{u}\|_{\infty}\mathrm{d}s\right),

and
H
is invertible, which allows us to treat
y
as a function of
x
.

3.

Finally, we put
\Phi(\bar{u})=u
, where
u
is a unique solution to the system

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\nabla\phi(t,x),

(4.4)

\displaystyle\Delta\phi(t,x)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\delta}(t,y(t,x))

for
y(t,x)
being the inverse flow associated with
\bar{u}
.

The function
\sigma_{\delta}(t,y(t,\cdot))
is Lipschitz, as

\displaystyle|\sigma_{\delta}(t,y(t,x_{1}))-\sigma_{\delta}(t,y(t,x_{2}))|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\sigma_{\delta}\|_{\infty}|y(t,x_{1})-y(t,x_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla\sigma_{\delta}\|_{\infty}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right\|_{\infty}|x_{1}-x_{2}|.

Therefore
\phi(t,\cdot)\in C^{2,1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and in consequence
u(t,\cdot)\in C^{1,1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and we have the estimates

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|u\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\sigma_{\delta}\|_{\infty,T},

(4.5)

\displaystyle\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\nabla u\|_{C^{0,1}}

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\sigma_{\delta}(t,y(t,\cdot))\|_{C^{0,1}}.

Moreover, as
\sigma\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
, by the classical results for
L^{p}
regularity of strong solutions to Poisson equation (see e.g.
[
40
]
), for any
p\in(1,\infty)
we have the estimate

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|u\|_{\infty}+\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|u\|_{W^{1,p}}+\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\nabla u\|_{BMO}\leq C\|\sigma\|_{\infty,T},

(4.6)

which are uniform with respect to
\delta
.

4.1
Additional regularity of
u

We will now show that the fixed points of
\Phi
are uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{2,p})
. By use of an appropriate logarithmic inequality, it implies that
\Phi(K)\subseteq K
for some bounded and closed

Proposition 4.3
.

If
u_{\delta}
is a fixed point of

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\nabla u_{\delta}\|_{W^{1,p}}\leq C(p,\delta,T).

(4.7)

Proof: After differentiation of (

\Delta_{x}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x_{i}}=\nabla_{y}\sigma_{\delta}\cdot\frac{\partial y}{\partial x_{i}}.

Hence for any

\|\nabla\phi\|_{W^{2,p}}\leq C(p)\|\nabla\sigma_{\delta}\|_{\infty}\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right\|_{\infty},

which leads to

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\nabla u\|_{W^{1,p}}\leq C(p)\|\nabla\sigma_{\delta}\|_{\infty,T}e^{T\|\nabla\bar{u}\|_{\infty,T}}.

(4.8)

Now take
\bar{u}=u=u_{\delta}
in (

) and apply the following inequality for

\|\nabla f\|_{\infty}\leq C(p)\left(1+\|\nabla f\|_{BMO}\left(\ln^{+}(\|\nabla f\|_{W^{1,p}}+\|f\|_{\infty})\right)^{1/2}\right).

(4.9)

The proof of the above estimate one can find in
[

]
, Corollary 2.4. By the estimates (

) and Cauchy inequality, we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla u_{\delta}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,p})}

\displaystyle\leq C(p)\|\nabla\sigma_{\delta}\|_{\infty,T}\exp\left(T\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\nabla u_{\delta}\|_{\infty}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(CT\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\nabla u_{\delta}\|_{BMO}\left(\ln^{+}(\|\nabla u_{\delta}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,p})}+\|u_{\delta}\|_{\infty,T})\right)^{1/2}\right)

Therefore

\|\nabla u_{\delta}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,p})}^{2}-C\|\nabla u_{\delta}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,p})}-C\leq 0

and in consequence

\|\nabla u_{\delta}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{1,p})}\leq C

for some

depending on

,
\delta
and
T
. ∎

4.2
The fixed point argument.

Local existence.

We show that
\Phi\colon K\to K
is a contraction for sufficiently small

. From the elliptic estimates, we have

\displaystyle\|u_{1}(t,\cdot)-u_{2}(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\sigma_{\delta}(t,y_{1}(t,\cdot))-\sigma_{\delta}(t,y_{2}(t,\cdot))\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\nabla\sigma_{\delta}\|_{\infty,T}\|y_{1}(t,\cdot)-y_{2}(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}.

By Lemma

from the Appendix, for small

we have

\|y_{1}(t,\cdot)-y_{2}(t,\cdot)\|_{\infty}\leq Ct\|\bar{u}_{1}-\bar{u}_{2}\|_{\infty,T}

and due to the uniform bound on
\|\nabla u\|_{\infty}
for
u\in K
the constant
C
does not depend on
\bar{u}_{1}
and
\bar{u}_{2}
. Hence

\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{\infty,T_{1}}\leq C\|\nabla\sigma_{\delta}\|_{\infty,T}(T_{1}+o(T_{1}))\|\bar{u}_{1}-\bar{u}_{2}\|_{\infty,T_{1}}.

(4.10)

Choosing
T_{1}
such that
C\|\nabla\sigma_{\delta}\|_{\infty,T}T_{1}<1
, we get that
\Phi
is a contraction on
K
. Therefore there exists a unique fixed point
u_{\delta}
of
\Phi
on the interval
[0,T_{1}]
.

Extension to
[0,T]
.

Having the uniqueness on
[0,T_{1}]
, we are able to perform the same reasoning on
[T_{1},2T_{1}]
. Note that the estimate (
4.10
) on
[T_{1},2T_{1}]
would still depend only on the length of the interval. Therefore again
\Phi
is a contraction on
[T_{1},2T_{1}]
, which gives us the unique fixed point on
[0,2T_{1}]
. Performing this procedure on the consecutive intervals
[nT_{1},(n+1)T_{1}]
, we obtain the existence of a unique fixed point on the whole interval
[0,T]
, which completes the proof of Lemma
4.2
. ∎

4.3
Letting
\delta\to 0
.

In Lemma
4.2
we obtained the unique
u_{\delta}=\nabla\phi_{\delta}
, which satisfies the equation

\Delta\phi_{\delta}(t,x)=\sigma_{\delta}(t,y_{\delta}(t,x))

(4.11)

and the uniform estimates (
4.6
). Moreover, it turns out that
\partial_{t}u_{\delta}
is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}
. Indeed, from the weak formulation of (
4.11
), for any
\pi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
we have

\int\Delta\phi_{\delta}(t,x)\pi(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int\sigma_{\delta}(t,y_{\delta}(t,x))\pi(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int\sigma_{\delta}(t,y)\pi(x_{\delta}(t,y))J_{\delta}(t,y)\;\mathrm{d}y,

where
J_{\delta}(t,y)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{\delta}(s,y)\mathrm{d}s\right)
is the Jacobian of
x_{\delta}
. Differentiating this equality with respect to time, we obtain

\displaystyle\int\Delta\partial_{t}\phi_{\delta}(t,x)\pi(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=

\displaystyle\int\partial_{t}\sigma_{\delta}(t,y)\pi(x_{\delta}(t,y))J_{\delta}(t,y)\;\mathrm{d}y

(4.12)

\displaystyle+\int\sigma_{\delta}(t,y)\partial_{t}\pi(x_{\delta}(t,y))J_{\delta}(t,y)\;\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle+\int\sigma_{\delta}(t,y)\pi(x_{\delta}(t,y))\partial_{t}J_{\delta}(t,y)\;\mathrm{d}y.

Let us now estimate the terms on the right hand side of (
4.12
). First, observe that from equation (
2.2
)
\partial_{t}\sigma
is bounded, and therefore

|\partial_{t}\sigma_{\delta}|=|(\partial_{t}\sigma)\ast\kappa_{\delta}|\leq|\partial_{t}\sigma|\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d}).

For the second term, we have

\partial_{t}\pi(x_{\delta}(t,y))=\nabla\pi(x_{\delta}(t,y))\dot{x_{\delta}}(t,y)=\nabla\pi(x_{\delta}(t,y))u(t,x_{\delta}(t,y))

and then using uniform estimates on
u_{\delta}
, we get

\int|\nabla\pi(x_{\delta}(t,y))|^{2}|u_{\delta}(t,x_{\delta}(t,y))|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}y\leq\left\|\frac{1}{J_{\delta}}\right\|_{\infty}\int|\nabla\pi(t,x)|^{2}|u_{\delta}(t,x)|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\leq C,

therefore
\partial_{t}\pi(x_{\delta}(t,y))\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2})
. The third term is bounded as well, as
\partial_{t}J_{\delta}=\sigma_{\delta}J_{\delta}

and both
\sigma_{\delta}
and
J_{\delta}
are bounded by some
C(\|\sigma\|_{\infty,T})
.

The above estimates imply that
\Delta\partial_{t}\phi_{\delta}
is bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{-1,2})
uniformly in
\delta
and therefore

\|\partial_{t}u_{\delta}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2})}=\|\nabla\partial_{t}\phi_{\delta}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2})}\leq C.

(4.13)

We now let
\delta\to 0^{+}
and therefore obtain the solution to equation (
3.2
). The estimates (
4.6
) give

\|\phi_{\delta}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{2,p})}\leq C,

so
\phi_{\delta}\rightharpoonup^{*}\phi
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;W^{2,p})
up to a subsequence. From the uniform estimates (
4.6
) and (
4.13
), Aubin-Lions Lemma implies that in particular
u_{\delta}
is compact in
L^{1}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Moreover, using Theorem 2.9 from
[
8
]
, we get

\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|x(t,y)-x_{\delta}(t,y)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}\leq C\left|\ln\left(\|u-u_{\delta}\|_{L^{1}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})}\right)\right|^{-1},

where
x(t,y)
is the flow generated by this weak* limit
u=\nabla\phi
. Therefore
x_{\delta}\to x
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1})
.

The above convergence allows us to pass to the limit with
\delta\to 0
in a weak formulation of (
4.4
). For any
\xi\in C^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int\Delta\phi_{\delta}(t,x)\xi(t,x)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int(\sigma\ast\kappa_{\delta})(t,y_{\delta}(t,x))\xi(t,x)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int(\sigma\ast\kappa_{\delta})(t,y)\xi(t,x_{\delta}(t,y))J_{\delta}(t,y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t.

Letting
\delta\to 0
, we get

\int_{0}^{T}\int\Delta\phi(t,x)\xi(t,x)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\int\sigma(t,y)\xi(t,x(t,y))J(t,y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t,

(4.14)

where
J=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,y)\mathrm{d}s\right)
is the Jacobian of the limit flow
x(t,y)
.

To deduce that indeed we have
\mathrm{div}\,u(t,x(t,y))=\sigma(t,y)
, we need to change the variables in one of the sides in (
4.14
). Despite the fact that
x(t,\cdot)
is not a diffeomorphism, Lemma 3.1 from
[
7
]
allows us to perform the change of variables in the left hand side of (
4.14
) and obtain

\int_{0}^{T}\int\mathrm{div}\,u(t,x(t,y))\xi(t,x(t,y))J(t,y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t.

Therefore the equality (
4.14
) is transformed into

\int_{0}^{T}\int\big[\mathrm{div}\,u(t,x(t,y))-\sigma(t,y)\big]\xi(t,x(t,y))J(t,y)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}t=0.

As
\mathrm{div}\,u\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
, the Jacobian
J(t,y)
is strictly positive. Hence, from the arbitrary choice of
\xi
, we have
\mathrm{div}\,u(t,x(t,y))=\sigma(t,y)
in the sense of distributions, which ends the proof of Theorem
4.1
. ∎

As by Theorem
2.2
the norms
\|\eta\|_{\infty,T}
and
\|\sigma\|_{\infty,T}
does not depend on
T
, so are
\|\varrho\|_{\infty,T}
,
\|\mathrm{div}\,u\|_{\infty,T}
and the estimates given by (
4.6

we obtain the unique existence on the whole real line and hence the proof of Theorem
1.1
is completed.

Appendix A
Existence of solutions to (
2.2
)

We here prove that there exists a unique solution to (
2.2
), which completes the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Lemma A.1
.

For
\varrho_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and any
T>0
there exists a unique global in time solution

(\eta,\sigma)\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})\times L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})

to the equation (
2.2
) with the initial condition
\eta(0,y)=\varrho_{0}(y)
.

Proof: The proof relies on double application of the Banach fixed point theorem. First, observe that for a fixed
\eta\in L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
, there exists a unique
\sigma
, satisfying

\sigma=p(\eta)-\frac{1}{|\mathbb{T}^{d}|}\int p(\eta(t,y))\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,y)\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathrm{d}y.

(A.1)

To see that, take
\Psi\colon L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})\to L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
such that

\Psi(\sigma)=p(\eta)-\frac{1}{|\mathbb{T}^{d}|}\int p(\eta(t,y))\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma(s,y)\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathrm{d}y.

Then

\displaystyle|\Psi(\sigma_{1})-\Psi(\sigma_{2})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|\mathbb{T}^{d}|}\int|p(\eta(t,y))|\left|\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{1}(s,y)\mathrm{d}s\right)-\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{2}(s,y)\mathrm{d}s\right)\right|\mathrm{d}y.

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{|\mathbb{T}^{d}|}\int|p(\eta(t,y)|\int_{0}^{t}|\sigma_{1}(s,y)-\sigma_{2}(s,y)|\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq Ct\|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}\|_{\infty,\tau}.

Hence taking
\tau
such that
C\tau<1
, from the Banach fixed point theorem we get the existence of a unique solution on the interval
[0,\tau]
. However, as
\sigma
does not blow up on an interval
[0,T]
, we are able to extend the solution to (
A.1
) to the whole interval.

Now define
\Phi\colon L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})\to L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathbb{T}^{d})
as

\Phi(\eta)=\varrho_{0}(y)-\int_{0}^{t}\eta(s,y)\sigma(s,y)\mathrm{d}s,

where
\sigma
is given by (
A.1
). Similarly as before, we want to obtain the estimate

\|\Phi(\eta_{1})-\Phi(\eta_{2})\|_{\infty,\tau}\leq C\tau\|\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}\|_{\infty,\tau}

and choose
\tau
such that
C\tau<1
and
\Phi\colon\{\eta:\|\eta\|_{\infty,\tau}<r\}\to\{\eta:\|\eta\|_{\infty,\tau}<r\}
is a contraction. We have

|\Phi(\eta_{1})-\Phi(\eta_{2})|=|\int_{0}^{t}\eta_{1}\sigma_{1}-\eta_{2}\sigma_{2}\mathrm{d}s|\leq\int_{0}^{t}|\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}\|\sigma_{1}|\mathrm{d}s+\int_{0}^{t}\eta_{2}|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}|\mathrm{d}s.

The first integral can be bounded by
Ct\|\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}\|_{\infty,\tau}
, so to complete the desired estimate we need to estimate the difference of
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
. We have

|\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2}|\leq|p(\eta_{1})-p(\eta_{2})|+|\{p(\eta_{1})\}_{\sigma_{1}}-\{p(\eta_{2})\}_{\sigma_{2}}|.

As the derivative of
p
is bounded on an interval
[0,r]
, we can estimate the first element by
C\|\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}\|_{\infty}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle|\mathbb{T^{d}}|

\displaystyle|\{p(\eta_{1})\}_{\sigma_{1}}-\{p(\eta_{2})\}_{\sigma_{2}}|=\left|\int p(\eta_{1})\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{1}\mathrm{d}s\right)-p(\eta_{2})\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{2}\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathrm{d}y\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{1}\mathrm{d}s\right)|p(\eta_{1})-p(\eta_{2})|\mathrm{d}y+\int p(\eta_{2})\left|\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{1}\mathrm{d}s\right)-\exp\left(\int_{0}^{t}\sigma_{2}\mathrm{d}s\right)\right|\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq C\|\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}\|_{\infty}+C\int\left|\int_{0}^{t}(\sigma_{1}-\sigma_{2})\mathrm{d}s\right|\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\leq C\|\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}\|_{\infty}+Ct\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}|\{p(\eta_{1})\}_{\sigma_{1}}-\{p(\eta_{2})\}_{\sigma_{2}}|.

Hence for
\tau
small enough, we get

\sup_{0\leq t\leq\tau}|\{p(\eta_{1})\}_{\sigma_{1}}-\{p(\eta_{2})\}_{\sigma_{2}}|\leq C\sup_{0\leq t\leq\tau}\|\eta_{1}-\eta_{2}\|_{\infty},

which gives us the desired estimate and ends the proof. ∎

Appendix C
Properties of the
BMO
space

We present here the useful properties of the
BMO
functions, which can be found for example in
[
35
]
and
[
38
]
.

Definition C.1
.

A function
f\in L^{1}(\Omega)
belongs to space of bounded mean oscillation
BMO(\Omega)
iff

\|f\|_{BMO}=\sup_{Q\subset\Omega}\frac{1}{|Q|}\int_{Q}|f-\{f\}_{Q}|\mathrm{d}x<\infty,

where the supremum is taken over all cubes in
\Omega
.

Note that
\|\cdot\|_{BMO}
is not a norm, as
\|f\|_{BMO}=0
for
f
constant. However, we can equip the space
BMO(\Omega)
with the norm

\|\cdot\|_{L^{1}}+\|\cdot\|_{BMO}

and then it becomes the Banach space.

It is straightforward from the definition that the standard mollification is bounded in
BMO
:

Proposition C.2
.

For
f\in BMO
and
\kappa_{\delta}
the standard mollifier we have

\|f\ast\kappa_{\delta}\|_{BMO}\leq\|f\|_{BMO}.

One of the important tools concerning the
BMO
spaces is the John-Nirenberg inequality:

Lemma C.3

(John-Nirenberg)
.

There exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that for any cube
Q\subset\Omega
and
f\in BMO(\Omega)

|\{x\in Q:|f-\{f\}_{Q}|>\lambda\}|\leq c_{1}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{2}\lambda}{\|f\|_{BMO}}\right)|Q|.

The useful applications of the John-Nirenberg inequality are the following:

Corollary C.4
.

Let
f\in BMO(\Omega)
. Then

1.

f\in L_{loc}^{p}(\Omega)
for any
1\leq p<\infty
.

2.

\displaystyle\sup_{Q\subset\Omega}\int_{Q}\exp\left(\frac{|f-\{f\}_{Q}|}{\|f\|_{BMO}}\right)\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

C.1
The logarithmic inequality

We recall here the inequality from
[
26
]
:

Lemma C.5
.

Let
f\in BMO(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with compact support and
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}fg\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq C\|f\|_{BMO}\|g\|_{L^{1}}\big(|\ln\|g\|_{L^{1}}|+\ln(e+\|g\|_{L^{\infty}})\big).

It turns out that after slight modifications, the similar inequality holds for
g\in L^{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
for sufficiently large
q
.

Lemma C.6
.

Let
f\in BMO(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and
g\in L^{q}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
for some
q>2
. Then

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}fg\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq

\displaystyle C\|f\|_{BMO}\|g\|_{L^{1}}

(C.1)

\displaystyle\times\big(|\ln\|g\|_{L^{1}}|+\ln(e+\|g\|_{L^{q}})+(1+|\ln\|g\|_{L^{1}}|)\|g\|_{L^{q}}^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\big).

Proof: Assume
\int g\mathrm{d}x=0
. Then
g\in\mathcal{H}^{1}
and from duality of
\mathcal{H}^{1}
and
BMO
we have

\left|\int fg\;\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq\|f\|_{BMO}\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}.

By the characterization of
\mathcal{H}^{1}
(see e.g. paragraph III.4 in
[
35
]
) we can write
\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}
as

\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}}=\|g\|_{L^{1}}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\|R_{k}g\|_{L^{1}},

where
R_{k}
is the Riesz transform given as
\mathcal{F}(R_{k}g)=-i\frac{\xi_{k}}{|\xi|}\mathcal{F}(g)
. As the Riesz transform is an operator of weak-type
(1,1)
and strong-type
(p,p)
, we can apply Proposition V.3.2. from
[
38
]
and obtain

\|R_{k}g\|_{L^{1}}\leq C+C\int|g(x)|\ln^{+}|g(x)|\;\mathrm{d}x.

(C.2)

By scaling, we can rewrite (
C.2
) as

\|R_{k}g\|_{L^{1}}\leq\lambda+C\int|g(x)|\ln^{+}(|g(x)|/\lambda)\mathrm{d}x

for any
\lambda>0
. For
|g|\geq\lambda
, we have
\ln^{+}(|g|/\lambda)=\ln|g|-\ln\lambda
. Then

|\ln(|g|_{|_{|g|\geq\lambda}})|=\left|\ln\left(\frac{|g|_{|_{|g|\geq\lambda}}}{1+\|g\|_{L^{q}}}\right)+\ln(1+\|g\|_{L^{q}})\right|\leq\ln(1+\|g\|_{L^{q}})+\left|\ln\left(\frac{|g|_{|_{|g|\geq\lambda}}}{1+\|g\|_{L^{q}}}\right)\right|.

Now assume
\lambda<1+\|g\|_{L^{q}}
and take
x
such that
\lambda\leq|g(x)|\leq\frac{(1+\|g\|_{L^{q}})^{2}}{\lambda}
. Then

\left|\ln\left(\frac{|g(x)|}{1+\|g\|_{L^{q}}}\right)\right|\leq\left|\ln\left(\frac{\lambda}{1+\|g\|_{L^{q}}}\right)\right|

and in consequence

|\ln|g(x)|\leq 2\ln(1+\|g\|_{L^{q}})+|\ln\lambda|.

Choose
\lambda=\|g\|_{L^{1}}
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{\big\{|g|\leq\frac{(1+\|g\|_{L^{q}})^{2}}{\lambda}\big\}}|g|\ln^{+}(|g|/\lambda)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\big\{|g|\leq\frac{(1+\|g\|_{L^{q}})^{2}}{\lambda}\big\}}|g|(2\ln(1+\|g\|_{L^{q}})+|\ln\|g\|_{L^{1}}|)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\|g\|_{L^{1}}(2\ln(1+\|g\|_{L^{q}})+|\ln\|g\|_{L^{1}}|)

What is left is the case
|g(x)|>\frac{(1+\|g\|_{L^{q}})^{2}}{\lambda}
. From the Chebyshev inequality, we have

\left|\left\{x:|g(x)|>\frac{(1+\|g\|_{L^{q}})^{2}}{\|g\|_{L^{1}}}\right\}\right|\leq\left(\frac{\|g\|_{L^{1}}}{1+\|g\|_{L^{q}}}\right)^{2}.

Therefore from the Hölder inequality

\int_{\big\{|g|>\frac{(1+\|g\|_{L^{q}})^{2}}{\lambda}\big\}}|g|\ln^{+}(|g|/\lambda)\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{\|g\|_{L^{1}}}{1+\|g\|_{L^{q}}}\left(\int|g|^{2}\ln(|g|/\lambda)^{2}\mathrm{d}x\right)^{1/2}.

Using the fact that both
\int|g|^{2}\ln|g|\mathrm{d}x
and
\int|g|^{2}\ln^{2}|g|\mathrm{d}x
are bounded by

C\|g\|_{L^{q}}^{4-q}+\|g\|_{L^{q}}^{q}

for any
q>2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int|g|^{2}\ln(|g|/\lambda)^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int|g|^{2}\ln^{2}|g|\mathrm{d}x-2\ln\lambda\int|g|^{2}\ln|g|\mathrm{d}x+|\ln\lambda|^{2}\int|g|^{2}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq(C\|g\|_{L^{q}}^{4-q}+\|g\|_{L^{q}}^{q})(1+2|\ln\lambda|+|\ln\lambda|^{2})

\displaystyle=(C\|g\|_{L^{q}}^{4-q}+\|g\|_{L^{q}}^{q})(1+|\ln\lambda|)^{2}.

As
\frac{(Cs^{4-q}+s^{q})^{1/2}}{1+s}\sim s^{\frac{q-2}{2}}
, after combining the estimates we get

\int_{\big\{|g|>\frac{(1+\|g\|_{L^{q}})^{2}}{\lambda}\big\}}|g|\ln^{+}(|g|/\lambda)\mathrm{d}x\leq C\|g\|_{L^{1}}(1+|\ln\|g\|_{L^{1}})\|g\|_{L^{q}}^{\frac{q-2}{2}}.

Putting all terms together,

\|R_{k}g\|_{L^{1}}\leq C\|g\|_{L^{1}}\left(1+\ln(1+\|g\|_{L^{q}})+|\ln\|g\|_{L^{1}}|+(1+|\ln\|g\|_{L^{1}}|)\|g\|_{L^{q}}^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\right)

and therefore we obtain inequality (
C.1
).

If
\int g\;\mathrm{d}x\neq 0
, then we can apply this inequality to
\bar{g}(x)=g(x)-\frac{1}{|\mathbb{T}^{d}|}\int g\mathrm{d}x
and use the fact that the
L^{p}
norms of
\bar{g}
are bounded by norms of
g
up to a constant. ∎

The same result holds if we replace
f
by a composition of
f
and the flow
x(t,y)
:

Corollary C.7
.

If
f
and
g
satisfy assumptions of Lemma
C.6
and
x(t,y)
is the regular Lagrangian flow of some
u(t,x)
with bounded divergence, then

\displaystyle\left|\int f(x(t,y))g(y)\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq

\displaystyle C\|f\|_{BMO}\|g\|_{L^{1}}

\displaystyle\times\big(|\ln\|g\|_{L^{1}}|+\ln(e+\|g\|_{L^{q}})+(1+|\ln\|g\|_{L^{1}}|)\|g\|_{L^{q}}^{\frac{q-2}{2}}\big).

Proof: We will first approximate
u
with smooth vector fields, then make the change of variables and apply Lemma
C.6
, and at the end show the convergence to the non-smooth case.

Let
J(t,y)
be the Jacobian of
x
. By the properties of Lagrangian flows we have

e^{-L}\leq J(t,y)\leq e^{L},\text{ where }L=\int_{0}^{T}\|\textrm{div}u\|_{\infty}\mathrm{d}t.

Now let us approximate
u
by convolution, defining
u_{\varepsilon}
as a convolution with standard convolution kernels in time and space. Then if
x_{\varepsilon}
is a flow of
u_{\varepsilon}
, then
x_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)
is the diffeomorphism and the Jacobian
J_{\varepsilon}
of
x_{\varepsilon}
still satisfies the bounds

e^{-L}\leq J_{\varepsilon}(t,y)\leq e^{L}.

By the change of variables, we have

\int f(x_{\varepsilon}(t,y))g(y)\mathrm{d}y=\int f(x)\frac{g(y_{\varepsilon}(t,x))}{J_{\varepsilon}(t,y_{\varepsilon}(t,x))}\mathrm{d}x,

where
y_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)=x_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot)^{-1}
. Applying Lemma
C.6
, we obtain inequality (
C.1
) but with
L^{1}
and
L^{q}
norms of
\frac{g(y_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot))}{J_{\varepsilon}(t,y_{\varepsilon}(t,\cdot))}
instead of
g
. However, changing the variables again and using the bounds on
J_{\varepsilon}
, we obtain for any
p\geq 1

\int\frac{|g(y_{\varepsilon}(t,x)|^{p}}{J_{\varepsilon}(t,y_{\varepsilon}(t,x))^{p}}\mathrm{d}x=\int|g(y)|^{p}J_{\varepsilon}(t,y)^{1-p}\mathrm{d}y\leq e^{(p-1)L}\int|g(y)|^{p}\mathrm{d}y

and we are done.

Now we will show that indeed

\int f(x_{\varepsilon}(t,y))g(y)\mathrm{d}y\to\int f(x(t,y))g(y)\mathrm{d}y\;\text{ with }\;\varepsilon\to 0.

(C.3)

By the stability of the flow, we have the pointwise convergence
x_{\varepsilon}(t,y)\to x(t,y)
up to a subsequence. If
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, then (
C.3
) holds by the dominated convergence theorem. Let us approximate
f
by
f_{\delta}=f\ast\kappa_{\delta}
, where
\kappa_{\delta}
is again the standard mollifier. As
f\in L^{p}
for
p=q^{\prime}
, we have

\left|\int(f_{\delta}(x(t,y))-f(x(t,y))g(y)\mathrm{d}y\right|\leq\|f_{\delta}(x(t,\cdot))-f(x(t,\cdot))\|_{p}\|g\|_{q}

and by the bounds on
J(t,y)
,

\int|f_{\delta}(x(t,y))-f(x(t,y))|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x\leq e^{pL}\int|f_{\delta}(x)-f(x)|^{p}\;\mathrm{d}x\to 0,

therefore we have the desired convergence. Moreover, by the Proposition
C.2
the norms
\|f_{\delta}\|_{BMO}
in the right hand side of (
C.1
) are bounded by
\|f\|_{BMO}
, which ends the proof of the Corollary. ∎
