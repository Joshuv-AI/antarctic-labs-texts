Convergence condition of simulated quantum annealing for closed and open systems Preprint: APS/123-QED
Yusuke Kimura, Hidetoshi Nishimori

Abstract
Simulated quantum annealing is a generic classical protocol to simulate some aspects of quantum annealing and is sometimes regarded as a classical alternative to quantum annealing in finding the ground state of a classical Ising model. We derive a generic condition for simulated quantum annealing to converge to thermal equilibrium at a given, typically low, temperature. Both closed and open systems are treated. We rewrite the classical master equation for simulated quantum annealing into an imaginary-time Schrödinger equation, to which we apply the imaginary-time variant of asymptotic adiabatic condition to deduce the convergence condition. The result agrees qualitatively with a rigorous convergence condition of simulated quantum annealing for closed systems, which was derived from the theory of inhomogeneous Markov process. Also observed is qualitative agreement with a rigorous convergence condition of quantum annealing for closed systems under the real-time Schrödinger dynamics. This coincidence of convergence conditions for classical stochastic processes for simulated quantum annealing and the real-time quantum dynamics for quantum annealing is highly non-trivial and calls for further scrutiny.



I
Introduction

Quantum annealing (QA) is a metaheuristic, a generic approximate algorithm, for combinatorial optimization problems
Kadowaki and Nishimori 1998
;
Farhi et al. 2001
;
Santoro et al. 2002
;
Das and Chakrabarti 2008
;
Morita and Nishimori 2008
;
Albash and Lidar 2018
;
Hauke et al. 2019
;
Crosson and Lidar 2021
. Recent years have seen its developments toward applications to a broader class of problems beyond combinatorial optimization such as quantum simulations
Harris et al. 2018
;
King et al. 2018
;
Nishimura et al. 2020
;
Gardas et al. 2018
;
Bando et al. 2020
;
Weinberg et al. 2020
;
King et al. 2022a
;
King et al. 2022b
and optimization with continuous variables
Abel et al. 2021
;
Abel and Spannowsky 2021a
;
Abel and Spannowsky 2021b
;
Koh and Nishimori 2022
.

The classical algorithm of simulated quantum annealing (SQA) has often been used to simulate some properties of quantum annealing
Kadowaki 1999
;
Santoro et al. 2002
;
Heim et al. 2015
and shares the ultimate goal with QA to find the ground state of a classical Ising model. SQA uses stochastic processes to statistically sample the finite, typically very low (and ideally zero), temperature equilibrium state of the transverse-field Ising model written as a classical Ising model by the Suzuki-Trotter formula
Suzuki 1971
. SQA is not designed to reproduce dynamical properties of QA as the former follows classical stochastic processes whereas the latter is realized by the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, which are fundamentally different processes.
This point is clear also from the fact that the Suzuki-Trotter formula is derived by rewriting the equilibrium partition function of a quantum system into the equilibrium partition function of a corresponding classical Ising model.

Low-temperature equilibrium behaviors of the transverse-field Ising model are expected to be captured by SQA as long as the rate of change of time-dependent parameters in the Hamiltonian is sufficiently slow because then the system follows quasi-equilibrium state. It is therefore natural to ask how slow is slow enough for this expectation to stay valid. Equivalently, we may ask, under what conditions on the time dependence of parameters, SQA successfully reaches thermal equilibrium.

This question has indeed been answered for generic closed systems, i.e., systems isolated from the environment

1
1

1

As will be discussed later, the term “environment” here does not refer to classical thermal effects used in equilibrium simulations of SQA but means experimentally-relevant quantum-mechanical dissipation coming from interactions with a large number of degrees of freedom outside of qubit systems
Caldeira and Leggett 1983
, where the theory of inhomogeneous Markov process was used to analyze the stochastic process under SQA
Morita and Nishimori 2006
;
Morita and Nishimori 2008
. It has been shown that a generic condition for convergence to a finite-temperature equilibrium state looks qualitatively similar to the corresponding generic convergence condition of QA for the transverse-field Ising model with time-dependent coefficients evolving under the real-time Schrödinger dynamics. More precisely, in both cases of QA and SQA, the coefficient of the transverse-field term is to be decreased by a power law of time in the long-time limit with the power inversely proportionally to the system size. This apparent qualitative coincidence of convergence conditions for completely different dynamics is highly non-trivial, and it should be useful to provide a different perspective on this problem. Also from the technical point of view, the proof provided in Refs.
Morita and Nishimori 2006
;
Morita and Nishimori 2008
is rather abstract and it is not easy to extract relevant physics out of it.

To mention a few related studies, Refs.
Albash et al. 2012
;
Venuti et al. 2017
studied the quantum dynamics of open systems and Ref.
Crosson and Slezak 2020
discussed the relaxation time of time-independent models. Though not directly relevant to the present study, those papers represent interesting developments from the general perspective of dynamical properties of QA or SQA.

The present paper represents part of efforts toward the above-mentioned goal concerning convergence conditions by deriving a condition of convergence of SQA for closed and open systems, based on an approximate, but asymptotically accurate, adiabatic condition for the imaginary-time Schrödinger equation. The classical stochastic dynamics of SQA following the master equation is transformed to the imaginary-time Schrödinger equation through a well-established prescription
Nishimori et al. 2015
(see also Refs.
Henley 2004
;
Castelnovo et al. 2005
). Then the leading-order term of the asymptotic expansion of the adiabatic condition for the imaginary-time Schrödinger dynamics is used to derive our conclusion on the rate of time development of a coefficient. The logic is not completely rigorous mathematically because higher-order terms of the asymptotic expansion are ignored.
Nevertheless, it turns out that the resulting convergence condition is in qualitative agreement with the corresponding rigorous condition derived from the theory of inhomogeneous Markov process for closed systems
Morita and Nishimori 2006
.

This paper is structured as follows: In Sec.
II
, we derive the imaginary-time Schrödinger equation from the classical master equation for the time-dependent transverse-field Ising model. In Sec.
III
, we deduce a convergence condition for SQA to a thermal equilibrium state by applying the imaginary-time variant of adiabatic condition. Based on the result in Sec.
III
, we derive a condition on the coefficient of the transverse field that ensures the convergence to a thermal equilibrium state in Sec.
IV
. This is a central result of this paper. We discuss the result and conclude the paper in Sec.
V
.

II
Simulated quantum annealing and imaginary-time Schrödinger equation

In this section, we recapitulate the general process to rewrite the classical master equation for the Ising model into an imaginary-time Schrödinger equation under the context of SQA, closely following Ref.
Nishimori et al. 2015
.

II.1
Classical master equation

Our starting point is the master equation for the Markovian dynamics of the Ising model
Nishimori and Ortiz 2011
:

\frac{dP_{\sigma}(t)}{dt}=\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}}W_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}P_{\sigma^{\prime}}(t).

(1)

Here,
\sigma
represents a configuration of
N
Ising spins,
\sigma=\{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\ldots,\sigma_{N}\}
, and
P_{\sigma}(t)
is used to denote the probability of the system in configuration
\sigma
at time
t
. We use the notation
\hat{W}
to denote the transition matrix of size
2^{N}
whose entries are:
(\hat{W})_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}=W_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
. An off-diagonal entry of the matrix
\hat{W}
can be written as
Nishimori et al. 2015
:

W_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}=w_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta\left(H_{0}(\sigma)-H_{0}(\sigma^{\prime})\right)}\hskip 28.45274pt(\sigma\neq\sigma^{\prime}),

(2)

where
\beta
is the inverse temperature and
H_{0}(\sigma)
is the Hamiltonian of the Ising model under consideration.
We employ the heat bath method with single-spin flips in this paper, where
w_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
is given by

w_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{e^{\frac{1}{2}\beta\left(H_{0}(\sigma^{\prime})-H_{0}(\sigma)\right)}+e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta\left(H_{0}(\sigma^{\prime})-H_{0}(\sigma)\right)}},

(3)

where
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
differ only at a single spin. We set
W_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}=0
for other types of transitions such as multi-spin flips.
Owing to the probability conservation condition
\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}}W_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}=0
, a diagonal entry of the matrix
\hat{W}
,
W_{\sigma\sigma}
, is expressed as

W_{\sigma\sigma}=-\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}(\neq\sigma)}W_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}=-\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}(\neq\sigma)}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta\left(H_{0}(\sigma^{\prime})-H_{0}(\sigma)\right)}\,w_{\sigma^{\prime}\sigma}.

(4)

II.2
Imaginary-time Schrödinger equation for SQA

For simplicity of presentation, we first analyze the case of closed systems, i.e., systems described solely by the classical representation of the transverse-field Ising model at finite temperature to be defined below.
Generalization to open systems is relatively straightforward and will be discussed in a later section.

The Hamiltonian of the transverse-field Ising model for QA is

H(t)=H_{\rm Ising}+H_{\rm TF}(t),

(5)

where
H_{\rm Ising}
denotes the Hamiltonian of the Ising model

\displaystyle H_{\rm Ising}=-\sum_{\langle jj^{\prime}\rangle}J_{jj^{\prime}}\,\sigma_{j}^{z}\sigma_{j^{\prime}}^{z}

(6)

with
\langle jj^{\prime}\rangle
for pairs of interacting spins, and
H_{\rm TF}(t)
is for the transverse field with a time-dependent coefficient:

\displaystyle H_{\rm TF}(t)=-\Gamma(t)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{j}^{x}.

(7)

Here
\sigma_{j}^{z}
and
\sigma_{j}^{x}
are the
z
and
x
components of Pauli matrix at site
j
, respectively.

SQA runs Markov-chain Monte Carlo steps for the transverse-field Ising model rewritten as the corresponding classical Ising model derived by
the Suzuki-Trotter formula
Suzuki 1971
with the Hamiltonian:

\begin{split}&\beta H_{0}(\sigma)=\\
&-\sum_{k=1}^{M}\left(\sum_{\langle jj^{\prime}\rangle}\frac{\beta J_{jj^{\prime}}}{M}\sigma_{j}^{(k)}\sigma_{j^{\prime}}^{(k)}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\gamma(t)\sigma_{j}^{(k)}\sigma_{j}^{(k+1)}\right),\end{split}

(8)

where
M
is the total number of Trotter slices running from
k=1
to
M
, and the time dependent coefficient of the second term
\gamma(t)
is given by

\gamma(t)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left({\rm coth}\frac{\beta\Gamma(t)}{M}\right).

(9)

In Eq. (
8
),

\sigma_{j}^{(k)}(=\pm 1)
is used to denote a classical Ising spin at the
j
th site on the
k
th Trotter slice.
Notice that Eq. (
8
) has been derived with
\Gamma(t)
regarded as a fixed parameter as can be understood from the fact that the Suzuki-Trotter formula treats the equilibrium partition function.

In SQA,
\beta
and
M
are both kept constant at large values, typically choosing
\beta/M
to be of order unity, in order to reproduce the zero-temperature properties of the original transverse-field Ising model as faithfully as possible. Only the coefficient
\Gamma(t)
(and thus
\gamma(t)
) changes with time
t
.
It is important to notice here that the dynamics of SQA with time-dependent parameters is driven by the classical stochastic process of Monte Carlo simulation, which is completely different from the original (zero-temperature) quantum dynamics of the Schrödinger equation for the transverse-field Ising model of Eq. (
5
). One should not expect that the dynamical, i.e. transient, properties of the original quantum system of Eq. (
5
) can be reproduced by the classical method of SQA
Bando and Nishimori 2021
. Nevertheless, QA and SQA share the same goal to find, in the long-time limit
t\to\infty
, the ground state of the classical Ising model of Eq. (
6
) by tuning the parameter
\Gamma(t)
appropriately as a function of time
t
.
The following discussions are for a condition for SQA with a time-dependent parameter to converge to a thermal equilibrium state at given finite values of
\beta
and
M
in the long-time limit, which is different from a thermal relaxation process in a system with time-independent parameters.

The results deduced in this paper hold for any (real and positive) value of
\beta
. However, for practical applications of the results to a closed system, we are mainly interested in the limit at which
\beta
is chosen very large.

Let us rewrite the classical master equation for SQA in terms of an imaginary-time Schrödinger equation.
The quantum Hamiltonian dictating the latter equation,
\hat{H}
, can be constructed from the transition matrix
\hat{W}
as
Nishimori et al. 2015
:

\hat{H}:=-e^{\frac{\beta\hat{H}_{0}}{2}}\hat{W}e^{\frac{-\beta\hat{H}_{0}}{2}}.

(10)

In Eq. (
10
),
\hat{H}_{0}
is used to represent a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are:
(\hat{H}_{0})_{\sigma\sigma}=H_{0}(\sigma)
.

The quantum Hamiltonian has the following explicit expression:

\hat{H}=\sum_{\sigma}\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}}w_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\left(e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta\left(H_{0}(\sigma^{\prime})-H_{0}(\sigma)\right)}\ket{\sigma}\bra{\sigma}-\ket{\sigma^{\prime}}\bra{\sigma}\right).

(11)

Since the configurations
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
differ only at a single site, the
j
th site on the
k
th Trotter slice, the difference
\beta H_{0}(\sigma^{\prime})-\beta H_{0}(\sigma)
is given by the following expression, owing to the expression (
8
) of the Hamiltonian
H_{0}
:

\displaystyle\beta H_{0}(\sigma^{\prime})-\beta H_{0}(\sigma)=

\displaystyle\frac{2\beta}{M}\sum_{j^{\prime}\,({\rm n.n.}\,j)}J_{jj^{\prime}}\sigma_{j}^{(k)}\sigma_{j^{\prime}}^{(k)}

(12)

\displaystyle+2\gamma(t)\sigma_{j}^{(k)}\left(\sigma_{j}^{(k+1)}+\sigma_{j}^{(k-1)}\right).

The summation symbol on the right-hand side expresses the sum over sites
j^{\prime}
that are interacting with
j
. For notational simplicity, we denote the difference (
12
) by
-2\beta H_{j,k}
:

\displaystyle-\beta H_{j,k}:=

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{M}\sum_{j^{\prime}\,({\rm n.n.}\,j)}J_{jj^{\prime}}\sigma_{j}^{(k)}\sigma_{j^{\prime}}^{(k)}+\gamma(t)\sigma_{j}^{(k)}\left(\sigma_{j}^{(k+1)}+\sigma_{j}^{(k-1)}\right).

(13)

Utilizing the introduced notation, the diagonal coefficient in Eq. (
11
) can be rewritten as:

w_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta\left(H_{0}(\sigma^{\prime})-H_{0}(\sigma)\right)}=\frac{e^{\beta H_{j,k}}}{e^{\beta H_{j,k}}+e^{-\beta H_{j,k}}}.

(14)

Similarly, the off-diagonal coefficient in Eq. (
11
) is:

w_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{e^{\beta H_{j,k}}+e^{-\beta H_{j,k}}}.

(15)

Using a matrix-vector notation, the master equation (
1
) can be rewritten as follows:

\frac{d\hat{P}(t)}{dt}=\hat{W}(t)\hat{P}(t),

(16)

where
\hat{P}(t)
denotes a vector whose
\sigma
th entry
(\hat{P}(t))_{\sigma}
is
P_{\sigma}(t)
:
(\hat{P}(t))_{\sigma}=P_{\sigma}(t)
.

If we define

\displaystyle\ket{\phi(t)}:=e^{\frac{1}{2}\beta\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{P}(t),

(17)

it is straightforward to confirm that the master equation (
16
) is rewritten as the following imaginary-time Schrödinger equation
Nishimori et al. 2015
:

-\frac{d\ket{\phi(t)}}{dt}=\left(\hat{H}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}(\beta\hat{H}_{0})\right)\,\ket{\phi(t)}.

(18)

In the long-time limit, time dependence in the Hamiltonian
\hat{H_{0}}
becomes arbitrarily small as
\Gamma(t)
gradually approaches zero, and the expression on the outer parentheses in the above equation approaches
\hat{H}(t)
.
In this limit, the ground state of the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(t)
corresponds to the equilibrium state of the master equation (
16
) as shown in Ref.
Nishimori et al. 2015
.

We apply the adiabatic condition
Morita and Nishimori 2008
to the imaginary-time Schrödinger equation of Eq. (
18
) to deduce the convergence condition on SQA.

III
Imaginary-time variant of adiabatic condition

We next discuss the adiabatic condition for the imaginary-time Schrödinger equation.

III.1
General adiabatic condition for the imaginary-time Schödinger equation

To formulate the adiabatic condition, it is useful first to keep computation time to a finite value denoted as
\tau
. Then,
s
is used for a dimensionless time scaled by
\tau
, i.e.
s=t/\tau
. We write the Hamiltonian of a generic quantum system as
\mathcal{H}(t)
.
We have in mind the quantity in the large parentheses on the right-hand side of Eq. (
18
) for
\mathcal{H}(t)
as will be discussed later.
We assume that the instantaneous ground state of
\mathcal{H}(t)
is non-degenerate. This is justified in the present system, which is time-evolving by Eq. (
18
), because the quantity in the large parentheses on the right-hand side of Eq. (
18
) is the Hamiltonian of a transverse-field Ising model
with non-vanishing off-diagonal elements only between those states different by a single-spin flip (and thus expressed by
\sigma_{i}^{x}
), to which the Perron-Frobenius theorem
S. U. Pillai and Cha 2005
applies

2
2

2

Also in the long-time limit
t\to\infty
, the ground state is non-degenerate since, according to Eq. (
17
), the ground state of
\mathcal{H}(t)
corresponds to the equilibrium Boltzmann distribution of the original classical system
Nishimori et al. 2015
, which is naturally unique. This should not be confused with the
Z_{2}
degeneracy of the ground state of the classical Ising model of Eq. (
6
).
.
Notice that this transverse-field Ising model defined in terms of
\hat{H}
of Eq. (
11
) and
d(\beta\hat{H_{0}})/dt
is different from the original model Eq. (
5
).

We define
\overline{\mathcal{H}}(s)
as

\overline{\mathcal{H}}(s):=\mathcal{H}(t).

(19)

Let
\ket{j(s)}
denote the
j
th excited state,
j=0,1,\ldots
,

\overline{\mathcal{H}}(s)\ket{j(s)}=\epsilon_{j}(s)\ket{j(s)}.

(20)

\Delta_{j}(s)
is used to denote the instantaneous energy gap between the
j
th excited state and the ground state,
\Delta_{j}(s)=\epsilon_{j}(s)-\epsilon_{0}(s)
. The instantaneous energy gap between the first excited state and the ground state is particularly written as
\Delta(s)
, i.e.
\Delta(s)=\epsilon_{1}(s)-\epsilon_{0}(s)
.

A variant of the adiabatic condition for the imaginary-time Schrödinger equation obtained in Ref.
Morita and Nishimori 2008
states that: For the state vector

\displaystyle\ket{\psi(s)}=\sum_{j}c_{j}(s)\ket{j(s)}

(21)

of the imaginary-time Schrödinger equation,

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\tau}\frac{d\ket{\psi(s)}}{ds}=\overline{\mathcal{H}}(s)\ket{\psi(s)},

(22)

the coefficient
c_{j}(s)
with
j\neq 0
has the large-
\tau
asymptotic expansion as:

c_{j\neq 0}(s)\approx\frac{1}{\tau}\frac{\bra{j(s)}\frac{d\overline{\mathcal{H}}(s)}{ds}\ket{0(s)}}{\Delta_{j}(s)^{2}}+O(\tau^{-2}).

(23)

The first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
23
), the first-order term in the asymptotic expansion from the limit of large
\tau
, should be sufficiently small for the system to be close to the instantaneous ground state
\ket{0(s)}
.
Since we ignore the second- and higher-order terms, our theory is approximate but is nevertheless expected to be accurate in the asymptotic limit of large
\tau
.

In terms of the original time variable
t
, the adiabatic condition can be written as:

\frac{\lvert\bra{j(t)}\frac{d\mathcal{H}(t)}{dt}\ket{0(t)}\rvert}{\Delta_{j}(t)^{2}}\ll 1

(24)

for
j\neq 0
.
By bounding the numerator of the left-hand side of Eq. (
24
) by the operator norm as shown in Appendix A,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\bra{j(t)}\frac{d\mathcal{H}(t)}{dt}\ket{0(t)}\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert\frac{d\mathcal{H}(t)}{dt}\right\rVert,

(25)

we find that the condition is satisfied that the original system reaches thermal equilibrium (the ground state of the imaginary-time Schrödinger equation) in the long-time limit if the following relation is satisfied:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\left\lVert\frac{d\mathcal{H}(t)}{dt}\right\rVert}{\Delta(t)^{2}}\ll 1.

(26)

Notice that we apply the adiabatic condition only in the long-time limit since we are interested in minimizing the excitation probability only at the end of the process such that the system approaches the (low-temperature) equilibrium state in the long-time limit. It may therefore happen that the adiabatic condition may not necessarily be satisfied in the intermediate time region, which we do not care.

III.2
Adiabatic condition applied to SQA

Let us apply the condition of Eq. (
26
) to the problem we are interested in.

We apply the framework in the previous subsection to the case that
\mathcal{H}(t)
is the quantity in the large parentheses on right-hand side of the imaginary-time Schrödinger equation (
18
):

\mathcal{\hat{H}}(t)=\hat{H}(t)-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}(\beta\hat{H}_{0}).

(27)

Notice that
\mathcal{H}(t)
in the previous subsection is for a generic Hamiltonian, and
\hat{\mathcal{H}}(t)
here is the above specific one.
According to Eq. (
26
), convergence of SQA to the equilibrium state is assured in the long-time limit when the following condition is satisfied:

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\left\lVert\frac{d\mathcal{\hat{H}}(t)}{dt}\right\rVert}{\Delta(t)^{2}}\ll 1.

(28)

It is possible to yield an upper bound on the norm in the above numerator using Eq. (
11
). Equation (
14
) together with Eq. (
12
) implies that the diagonal term can be written as a finite number of terms of interacting spins. The off-diagonal term is just a transverse-field term with an appropriate coefficient evaluated by Eq. (
15
).
We thus find:

\begin{split}&\left\lVert\frac{d\mathcal{\hat{H}}(t)}{dt}\right\rVert\leq\\
&bMN\left(\Bigg\lVert\frac{d}{dt}(w_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta\left(H_{0}(\sigma^{\prime})-H_{0}(\sigma)\right)})\Bigg\rVert+\Bigg\lVert\frac{d}{dt}w_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\Bigg\rVert\right)\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\Bigg\lVert\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}(\beta\hat{H}_{0})\Bigg\rVert,\end{split}

(29)

where
b
is a constant independent of
M,N,\beta
, and
t
but is determined by the number of sites interacting with a given site.
Utilizing the relation

\Big\lVert\frac{d}{dt}(\beta H_{j,k})\Big\rVert=\Big\lVert\gamma^{\prime}(t)\sigma_{j}^{(k)}\left(\sigma_{j}^{(k+1)}+\sigma_{j}^{(k-1)}\right)\Big\rVert\leq 2\lvert\gamma^{\prime}(t)\rvert,

(30)

we have the following bound on the first term on the right-hand side of the inequality (
29
):

\displaystyle\Big\lVert\frac{d}{dt}(w_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\beta\left(H_{0}(\sigma^{\prime})-H_{0}(\sigma)\right)})\Big\rVert

\displaystyle=\Bigg\lVert\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{e^{\beta H_{j,k}}}{e^{\beta H_{j,k}}+e^{-\beta H_{j,k}}}\right)\Bigg\rVert

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg\lVert\frac{d}{dt}(\beta H_{j,k})\cdot{\rm sech}^{2}(\beta H_{j,k})\Bigg\rVert\leq\lvert\gamma^{\prime}(t)\rvert.

(31)

An analogous computation shows that

\displaystyle\Bigg\lVert\frac{d}{dt}w_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\Bigg\rVert=\Bigg\lVert\frac{d}{dt}\frac{1}{e^{\beta H_{j,k}}+e^{-\beta H_{j,k}}}\Bigg\rVert

(32)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg\lVert\frac{d}{dt}(\beta H_{j,k})\cdot{\rm tanh}(\beta H_{j,k}){\rm sech}(\beta H_{j,k})\Bigg\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\lvert\gamma^{\prime}(t)\rvert.

We also have

\Bigg\lVert\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}(\beta\hat{H}_{0})\Bigg\rVert\leq\sum_{k=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Bigg\lVert\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)\sigma_{j}^{(k)}\sigma_{j}^{(k+1)}\Bigg\rVert\leq MN\lvert\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)\rvert.

(33)

These computations applied to Eq. (
29
) yield the following bound:

\left\lVert\frac{d\mathcal{\hat{H}}(t)}{dt}\right\rVert\leq MN\left(\frac{3b}{2}\,\lvert\gamma^{\prime}(t)\rvert+\frac{1}{2}\,\lvert\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)\rvert\right).

(34)

Finding a lower bound of the energy gap
\Delta(t)
between the first excited state and the ground state is a nontrivial step. The coefficient of the transverse field in the quantum Hamiltonian (
11
) is
w_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
. We start from the following inequality for the coefficient
w_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
:

w_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{e^{\beta H_{j,k}}+e^{-\beta H_{j,k}}}\geq\frac{1}{2\,e^{\lvert\beta H_{j,k}\rvert}}.

(35)

\lvert\beta H_{j,k}\rvert
has the following upper bound owing to the definition of
H_{j,k}
in Eq. (
II.2
):

\lvert\beta H_{j,k}\rvert\leq\frac{\beta}{M}\bigg\lvert\sum_{j^{\prime}\,({\rm n.n.}\,j)}J_{jj^{\prime}}\sigma_{j}^{(k)}\sigma_{j^{\prime}}^{(k)}\bigg\rvert+2\gamma(t).

(36)

We adopt the notation

\displaystyle p(M):={\rm max}_{j,k}\,\frac{1}{M}\bigg\lvert\sum_{j^{\prime}\,({\rm n.n.}\,j)}J_{jj^{\prime}}\sigma_{j}^{(k)}\sigma_{j^{\prime}}^{(k)}\bigg\rvert,

(37)

then
\lvert\beta H_{j,k}\rvert
has an upper bound

\lvert\beta H_{j,k}\rvert\leq\beta p(M)+2\gamma(t).

(38)

From these relations, one learns that the coefficient
w_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
has a lower bound as

w_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\geq\frac{1}{2\,e^{\beta p(M)+2\gamma(t)}}.

(39)

For a generic transverse-field Ising model, a lower bound of the energy gap
\Delta(t)
between the ground state and the first excited stated was deduced in Refs.
Somma et al. 2007
;
Morita and Nishimori 2007
;
Morita and Nishimori 2008
. Applying the argument given in those references to the model under discussion, we obtain the following relation:

\Delta(t)\geq A(N)\,(w_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}})^{N},

(40)

where the
N
-dependence of
A(N)
is discussed below.
Making use of the lower bound for the coefficient
w_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}
in Eq. (
39
), we deduce the lower bound for
\Delta(t)

\Delta(t)\geq\frac{A(N)}{2^{N}}e^{-N(\beta p(M)+2\gamma(t))}.

(41)

The coefficient
A(N)
does not depend on time
t
but depends on
N
for
N\gg 1
as:

A(N)=a\sqrt{N}e^{-cN},

(42)

where
a
and
c
are positive constants independent of
N
in the asymptotic limit of large
N
. Thus, we obtain the lower bound of the energy gap
\Delta(t)
as follows:

\Delta(t)\geq\frac{a\sqrt{N}}{2^{N}}e^{-N(\beta p(M)+2\gamma(t)+c)}.

As a result of this equation and Eq. (

\begin{split}&\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\left\lVert\frac{d\mathcal{\hat{H}}(t)}{dt}\right\rVert}{\Delta(t)^{2}}\leq\\
&\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{2^{2N}M\left(3b\,\lvert\gamma^{\prime}(t)\rvert+\lvert\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)\rvert\right)}{2a^{2}}\,e^{2N(\beta p(M)+2\gamma(t)+c)}.\end{split}

(44)

Dropping constants of order one, we learn from this result that the condition

\begin{split}&\lim_{t\to\infty}\big(3b\,\lvert\gamma^{\prime}(t)\rvert+\lvert\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)\rvert\big)e^{4N\gamma(t)}\\
&\hskip 28.45274pt\times M\,2^{2N}e^{2N\beta p(M)+2Nc}\ll 1\end{split}

(45)

suffices to ensure the convergence of SQA for fixed values
N,M
, and
\beta
. Notice that the time-dependent part has been collected in the first line of the above equation.

IV
The result

We study in this section how the condition of Eq. (

IV.1
Convergence condition for closed systems

Here, we deduce a condition that ensures convergence of SQA from the bound (

It is natural to assume that
\Gamma(t)
is a monotonically decreasing function from a large value at

toward zero in the long-time limit. Thus

is monotonically increasing according to the definition of Eq. (

Suppose that the first term in the first line on the left-hand side of Eq. (

.
Solving the differential equation

we obtain the solution in terms of

\Gamma(t)=\frac{M}{\beta}{\rm tanh}^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{(4N)^{1/2N}(c_{1}t+c_{2})^{1/2N}}\right),

where

is an integral constant. With this form of

, one finds that
\lvert\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)\rvert e^{4N\gamma(t)}=c_{1}^{2}(c_{1}t+c_{2})^{-1}

This result inspires us to analyze a more general function of the following form for the coefficient of the transverse field

First, we show that the conditions (

For a similar reason, the second term on the right-hand side of Eq. (

When those conditions of Eqs. (

\displaystyle\lvert\gamma^{\prime}(t)\rvert e^{4N\gamma(t)}\leq\frac{c_{1}}{4N}+\frac{c^{\prime}}{2}.

(55)

Thus, we find that the term

are chosen sufficiently small under the conditions (

50

Next we explain that the condition (

With Eq. (

\begin{split}\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)e^{4N\gamma(t)}&=c_{1}g^{\prime}(t)\,(c_{1}t+c_{2})^{2Ng(t)-1}\\

(56)

For the third term on the right-hand side of Eq. (

51
).

One can confirm that the term
\lvert\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)\rvert e^{4N\gamma(t)}

under the conditions (

), (
50
), and (

is chosen sufficiently small.
In fact, one has the following bound under the stated conditions:

\begin{split}&\lvert\gamma^{\prime\prime}(t)\rvert e^{4N\gamma(t)}\leq c_{1}\lvert g^{\prime}(t)\rvert\,(c_{1}t+c_{2})^{2Ng(t)-1}\\
&+c_{1}^{2}\frac{g(t)}{2}(c_{1}t+c_{2})^{2Ng(t)-2}\\

In the last line of this equation, the first two terms tend to 0 as

V
Discussion

We have derived a condition that simulated quantum annealing (SQA) converges to thermal equilibrium for generic closed and open systems using the approximate, but asymptotically correct, adiabatic condition for the imaginary-time Schrödinger equation, to which the classical master equation governing SQA has been reduced. The result is in qualitative agreement with the rigorous version of convergence condition for closed systems derived from the theory of inhomogeneous Markov process
Morita and Nishimori 2006
;
Morita and Nishimori 2008
, where the condition of convergence has been proved to be:

\displaystyle\Gamma(t)\geq\frac{M}{\beta}\tanh^{-1}\frac{1}{(t+2)^{2/RL_{1}}}.

(71)

Here
R
is a constant of order
N
and
L_{1}
is an
N
-independent constant. Notice that the unit of time is different between those approaches: discrete steps for the above result in Refs.
Morita and Nishimori 2006
;
Morita and Nishimori 2008
and continuous time evolution in the present paper, resulting in the difference of coefficients of
t
, unity in Eq. (
71
) and
c_{1}
in Eq. (
48
). It is anyway encouraging that essentially the same conclusion has been reached by two completely different methods, partly because the present, physics-oriented, approach may be more flexible in applications to other problems than using the mathematically-solid, yet hard to generalize, theory of inhomogeneous Markov process.

It is worth attention that there is no essential difference in the convergence conditions for closed and open systems. The origin of this fact is that the additional term for environmental effects in Eq. (
60
) is time independent. We thus conclude that phase-flip errors caused by the environment as represented by Eq. (
IV.2
) does not essentially modify the generic bound on the rate of change of the time-dependent coefficient.

Another interesting fact is that the time dependence of the coefficient
\Gamma(t)
of Eq. (
47
) is quite similar to the corresponding convergence condition for real-time QA for closed systems as derived earlier under rigorous
Kimura and Nishimori 2022
and asymptotic
Somma et al. 2007
;
Morita and Nishimori 2008
;
Morita and Nishimori 2007
adiabatic conditions.
For example, the asymptotic version of convergence condition under the real-time Schrd̈inger dynamics is

\displaystyle\Gamma(t)=a(\delta t+c)^{-1/(2N-1)},

(72)

where
a,\delta
, and
c
are time-independent constants. On the side of the imaginary-time Schrödinger dynamics, when time
t
is large, Eq. (
47
) becomes

\Gamma(t)\sim\frac{M}{\beta(4N)^{1/2N}}(c_{1}t+c_{2})^{-1/2N}.

(73)

The forms of the two equations, (
72
) and (
73
), are quite analogous.
It is difficult to explain intuitively why those two completely different dynamical processes follow almost identical convergence conditions. This is particularly so, given that the real-time Schrödinger dynamics operates at zero temperature whereas the present results, Proposition 1 and Corollary 1, apply at any temperature, high or low, although one usually chooses very low temperatures to study the ground-state behavior of the system by SQA. We cannot exclude the possibility of an accidental coincidence, but also there may lie some deep physics behind this fact. We leave it to future work to try to answer this intriguing question.
