Towards a turnkey approach to unbiased Monte Carlo estimation of smooth functions of expectations
Nicolas Chopin, Francesca R. Crucinio, Sumeetpal S. Singh

Abstract
. Given a smooth function f f , we develop a general approach to turn Monte Carlo samples with expectation m m into an unbiased estimate of f ⁡ ( m ) f(m) . Specifically, we develop estimators that are based on randomly truncating the Taylor series expansion of f f and estimating the coefficients of the truncated series. We derive their properties and propose a strategy to set their tuning parameters – which depend on m m – automatically, with a view to make the whole approach simple to use. We develop our methods for the specific functions f ⁡ ( x ) = log ⁡ x f(x)=\log x and f ⁡ ( x ) = 1 / x f(x)=1/x , as they arise in several statistical applications such as maximum likelihood estimation of latent variable models and Bayesian inference for un-normalised models. Detailed numerical studies are performed for a range of applications to determine how competitive and reliable the proposed approach is.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
General setting

We consider the problem of unbiasedly estimating
f(m)
, for a smooth function

f
, using independent and identically distributed (IID) random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
with mean

m\in\mathbb{R}
.
In particular, using the Taylor expansion of
f
around a certain
x_{0}\neq 0
,

(1)

f(m)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{f^{(k)}(x_{0})}{k!}\left(m-x_{0}\right)^{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{k},\quad\gamma_{k}:=\frac{f^{(k)}(x_{0})}{k!}x_{0}^{k},

we will employ the so-called ‘sum’ estimator

(
McLeish, 2011
;
Glynn and Rhee, 2014
;
Rhee and Glynn, 2015
)

(2)

\hat{f}:=\sum_{k=0}^{R}\frac{\gamma_{k}U_{R,k}}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathds{1}\{R\geq k\}}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)}\gamma_{k}U_{R,k},

where
R
is an integer-valued random variable,

U_{R,k}=\sum_{r=k}^{\infty}U_{r,k}\mathds{1}\{R=r\},

and
U_{r,k}
is an unbiased estimate of
(m/x_{0}-1)^{k}
, as clarified in the following
assumption.

Assumption 1
.

Let
R
be a random variable taking values in
\{0,1,\ldots\}

such that
\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)>0
for all
k
. Let
\{U_{r,k}:k,r\in\mathbb{N};k\leq r\}
be a collection of random variables,
which are independent of
R
, and

(3)

\mathbb{E}\left[U_{r,k}\right]=\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{k}.

Let
U_{0,0}=U_{1,0}=\cdots=1.
Furthermore, we assume that
a_{k}:=\sup_{r\geq k}\mathbb{E}[|U_{r,k}|]<\infty
for
k>0
, and

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|\gamma_{k}|a_{k}<\infty
.

These conditions ensure that
\hat{f}
is an unbiased estimator,
see the following lemma and Appendix
A
for a proof.

Lemma 2
.

Under Assumption
1
,
\hat{f}
is integrable and
\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{f}\right]=f(m)
in (
1
).

A candidate for
U_{r,k}
is the following construction of
U_{r,k}
using

X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}
only,

(4)

U_{r,k}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(X_{i}/x_{0}-1),\quad 0<k\leq r.

Thus,
U_{r,k}
unbiasedly estimates
(m/x_{0}-1)^{k}
using the
first
k
univariates only. In Section
2.3
, we propose a
lower-variance estimator based on the whole sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}
. Including
the factor
x_{0}^{k}
in the definition of
\gamma_{k}
above will give simpler
expressions for functions of interests, such as
f(m)=\log m
and
f(m)=1/m
. We
shall also optimise this expansion point and henceforth, assume
x_{0}\neq 0
.

1.2.
Motivation and contributions

We are particularly interested in the functions
f(x)=\log x
and
f(x)=1/x
. The former arises in applications such as maximum likelihood estimation
where unbiased estimates of the likelihood are available, for example using
importance sampling. Using the sum estimator framework, these can then be
converted into unbiased estimates of the log-likelihood and its gradient. The
function
f(x)=1/x
arises in Bayesian and frequentist estimation of models
with un-normalised likelihoods, that is, when the likelihood involves an
intractable normalising constant.

Various unbiased estimators have already been proposed in the literature for
such functions; see Section
1.3
for a review. However, in our
experience, their performance tends to be very sensitive to ‘tuning’ parameters
such as the expansion point
x_{0}
and the distribution of
R
. This is
problematic for users, in particular in situations where one needs to routinely
compute unbiased estimates of
f(m)
at many different
m
values. This is the
case, for instance, in stochastic gradient methods, where at each iteration one
needs to estimate the gradient of a different term; see Section

2.6
for details.

Thus, our motivation is to propose a general ‘turnkey’ approach to unbiased
estimation which is both robust and easy to automate. Specifically, our contributions are as follows. We derive (Section
2
) some results on the general properties of the randomly truncated Taylor-series based estimator, and use them to inform the choice of the
truncation random variable
R
. We also discuss in detail the properties of the
standard unbiased estimate (
4
) of
(m/x_{0}-1)^{k}
and propose an
alternative with potentially much smaller variance. In particular, our new estimate uses the

whole
collection of samples
X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}
to construct the unbiased
estimate
U_{r,k}
of
(m/x_{0}-1)^{k}
for
0<k\leq r
. We show the overall variance
of the sum estimator will then decrease at a rate
\mathcal{O}\left((\log\mathbb{E}[R])/\mathbb{E}[R]\right)
. In contrast, the
variance of the sum estimate with the standard choice (
4
) for the
coefficient estimate does not diminish to zero as
\mathbb{E}[R]
increases. This difference can be significant for some applications.

The second part of our study (Section
3
) focuses on the
specific functions
f(x)=\log x
and
f(x)=1/x
. For these functions, we discuss
how to choose tuning parameters,
x_{0}
and the distribution of
R
, to ensure
that the resulting estimator has finite – and hopefully small – variance.

Section
4
presents numerical experiments that showcase the
proposed approach in applications such as maximum likelihood estimation in
latent variable models and Bayesian inference in un-normalised models.
Section
5
concludes with a discussion of the merits and scope
of the proposed approach. All the proofs appear in the Appendix.

1.3.
Related work

Sum estimators and related debiasing techniques have gained traction since their
introduction in
McLeish, 2011
;
Glynn and Rhee, 2014
;
Rhee and Glynn, 2015
; for example, see

Jacob and Thiéry, 2015
,

Lyne et al., 2015
,

Agapiou et al., 2018
and

Vihola, 2018
.

Some of these papers also consider single term estimators,
which rely on a single randomly selected term to unbiasedly estimate the
infinite series. In our particular context, single term estimators are less
appealing since a single term of a Taylor expansion of
f(m)
cannot give a
good approximation. Therefore, we do not consider them further. We refer to,
for example,
Vihola, 2018
for a more thorough discussion on the
connection between single term estimators and sum estimators, and furthermore,
their connection to multi-level Monte Carlo
(
Giles, 2008
)
in the context of
stochastic differential equations.

The idea of applying a debiasing technique to a Taylor expansion appeared before
in
Blanchet et al., 2015
, but without much discussion on how to make
the corresponding estimators applicable in practical scenarios. Our work helps fill this gap.

Debiasing techniques specific to the log function have been developed in

Luo et al., 2020
and
Shi and Cornish, 2021
. However, as is shown in our
numerical experiments, they generate estimates that may either have an infinite
variance, or they have a CPU cost with infinite variance.
In contrast, the CPU cost of our methods have a finite variance, and if properly
tuned, the variance of the estimates are also finite.

The function
f(x)=\exp(x)
, which also has many important applications, is outside
of the scope of this paper for reasons we explain later. We refer the reader to
Papaspiliopoulos, 2011
for a thorough treatment of debiasing techniques for this function.

1.4.
Notations and standing assumptions

The random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are independent and identically
distributed (IID) with expected value
m
and variance
\sigma^{2}
.

The expansion point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
in (
1
) is fixed initially,
and thus so are the
\gamma_{k}
’s, but we discuss how to choose
x_{0}
, for a
particular
m
, later on in Section
3
.

Our results will be articulated when
R
has the geometric distribution.
We denote by
\mathrm{Geometric}(p)
the geometric distribution such that
\mathbb{P}(R=k)=(1-p)^{k}p
,
k=0,1,\ldots
. Note that
\mathbb{E}[R]=(1-p)/p
and
\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)=(1-p)^{k}
.

We say that an estimator has
\mathcal{O}(R^{k})
complexity when its computation involves

\mathcal{O}(r^{k})
operations, conditional on
R=r
. The estimators considered in
this paper have all complexity at least
\mathcal{O}(R)
, since they require generating
R

random variables.

The notation
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{L}_{2}
means
\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}^{2}]<\infty
, which implies that
\hat{f}
has finite variance and is integrable,

\mathbb{E}[|\hat{f}|]<\infty
.

2.
General properties of Taylor-based sum estimators

2.1.
Variance of the general sum estimator

In this section, we consider the general estimator (
2
),
without considering a particular choice for the
U_{r,k}
’s, beyond
Assumption
1
. Using this assumption,

(5)

\mathbb{E}\left[\hat{f}\middle|R=r\right]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}\frac{\mathds{1}\{r\geq k\}}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)}\mathbb{E}[U_{r,k}|R=r]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}\frac{\mathds{1}\{r\geq k\}}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)}\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{k}.

See Appendix
A.2
for the verification.

One may decompose its variance, assuming it is finite, through the law of total variance,

(6)

\operatorname{var}[\hat{f}]=\operatorname{var}\left[\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}|R]\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{var}[\hat{f}|R]\right]

where the first term can be expressed as

(7)

\operatorname{var}\left[\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}|R]\right]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}^{2}\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{2k}\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)}-1\right)\\
+2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{l=k+1}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}\gamma_{l}\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{k+l}\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)}-1\right).

See Appendix
A.3
for the verification.

The term
\operatorname{var}\left[\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}|R]\right]
has a simple interpretation, namely, it is the variance of
\hat{f}
in the ideal case where the
X_{i}
’s and thus the
U_{r,k}
’s have zero variance, that is,

U_{r,k}=(m/x_{0}-1)^{k}
. Hence, this variance component is only due to the random truncation of the Taylor expansion.

To further analyse
\operatorname{var}\left[\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}|R]\right]
, we must make some assumptions on the
\gamma_{k}
’s.

Assumption 3
.

For the function
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, argument
m
and expansion point
x_{0}
, the series expansion in (
1
) holds. Furthermore,
(i) for some
c>0
,
|\gamma_{k}|\leq c
for all
k=0,1,\ldots
;
(ii)
|\gamma_{k}|\exp(\alpha k)\to\infty
for all
\alpha>0
;
(iii)
\beta_{0}:=|m/x_{0}-1|<1
.

Note that the series (
1
) cannot converge if we impose (i) and
(ii) without (iii).

Proposition 4
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
3
, if

R\sim\mathrm{Geometric}(p)
with
p\in(0,1-\beta_{0}^{2})
, then

(8)

\left(f(m)-\gamma_{0}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)\leq\operatorname{var}\left[\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}|R]\right]\leq\frac{c^{2}\beta_{0}^{2}}{(1-\beta_{0})^{2}}\times\frac{p}{1-p-\beta_{0}^{2}}.

See Appendix
B.1
for a proof. Assumption
1
is needed to ensure integrability and thus that
\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}|R]
is well defined. The condition
\beta_{0}<1
of Assumption
3
and corresponding restriction on
p
ensures the variance is finite.

The result above tells us that, under Assumption
3
, one can take

R\sim\mathrm{Geometric}(p)
and
\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}|R]
will have finite variance,
provided
p<1-\beta_{0}^{2}
.
In particular, the conditional variance behaves like
\mathcal{O}\left(\{\mathbb{E}[R]\}^{-1}\right)
since
\mathbb{E}[R]=1/p-1\rightarrow\infty
as
p\rightarrow 0
. We thus
recover the standard Monte Carlo error rate, which is a variance that decreases
as the inverse of the sample size.

Remark 5
.

We focus on the case
R\sim\mathrm{Geometric}(p)
, since it is an easy-to-sample-from
distribution; however, the upper bound established above holds for any
R

such that
\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)\geq(1-p)^{k}
. Conversely, one can see by inspecting the
proof that a distribution for
R
with sub-geometric tails should typically
lead to an infinite variance.

Of course, what remains to be seen is to what extent we can recover this ideal
behaviour when we use the practical estimates, namely
U_{r,k}
’s, for
(m/x_{0}-1)^{k}
. This will be the topic of the next two sections.

Remark 6
.

Both our functions of interest fulfil Assumption
3
:

\gamma_{k}=(-1)^{k-1}/k
for
f(x)=\log x
, and
\gamma_{k}=(-1)^{k-1}
for
f(x)=1/x
. A function that does
not
fulfil Assumption
3
is

f(x)=\exp(x)
as
\gamma_{k}
decreases at a super-geometric rate. This makes
it possible to consider for
R
some distribution with tails that are lighter
than geometric, for example, the Poisson distribution, which is why we do not
considered it further, but again see
Papaspiliopoulos, 2011
for a
thorough treatment of this function.

Before moving on, we state the expression for the second term of
decomposition (
6
), which we will study in the
following sections. See Appendix
A.4
for the derivation.

(9)

\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{var}[\hat{f}|R]\right]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma_{k}^{2}}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)^{2}}\left\{\sum_{r=k}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(R=r)\operatorname{var}(U_{r,k})\right\}\\
+2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{l=k+1}^{\infty}\frac{\gamma_{k}\gamma_{l}}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)\mathbb{P}(R\geq l)}\left\{\sum_{r=l}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(R=r)\operatorname{cov}\left(U_{r,k},U_{r,l}\right)\right\}.

2.2.
Simple estimator

A standard choice
(
Papaspiliopoulos, 2011
;
Blanchet and Glynn, 2015
)
for

U_{r,k}
is
U_{r,k}=U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{S}}
, where

U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{S}}:=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{X_{i}}{x_{0}}-1\right)

which actually does not depend on
r
. We denote by
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{S}}
the corresponding
sum estimator.

Computing
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{S}}
requires generating
R
random variables
X_{i}
,
but only the first
k
are used to compute the
k-
th term.
It is easy to see that for any
0\leq k\leq l
,

\operatorname{cov}\left(U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{S}},U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{S}}\right)=\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{x_{0}^{2}}+\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{2}\right)^{k}\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{l-k}-\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{k+l}.

This leads to the following condition to ensure the finiteness of the variance
of
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{S}}
.

Proposition 7
.

In addition to Assumption
3
,
suppose
\beta<1
where

(10)

\beta^{2}:=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{x_{0}^{2}}+{\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{2}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{x_{0}^{2}}+{\beta_{0}^{2}},

and
R\sim\mathrm{Geometric}(p)
with
p\in(0,1-\beta^{2})
. Then,
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{S}}\in\mathcal{L}_{2}
and

(11)

\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{var}(\hat{f}^{\mathrm{S}}|R)\right]\leq\frac{c^{2}(1+\beta)}{1-\beta}\times\frac{(1-p)}{1-p-\beta^{2}}.

See Appendix
B.2
for a proof.

Note that, given
m
and
\sigma^{2}
, it is always possible to find
x_{0}
such
that
\beta<1
; in fact the minimiser of
\beta
with respect to
x_{0}

verifies this condition.

What is unsatisfactory about this result is that the right hand side does not
vanish as
p\rightarrow 0
. This is not because the bound is not tight, as shown
by the following example whose derivation is given in Appendix
B.3
. Our numerical experiments suggest that this is true for other functions of
interest.

Example 8
.

Consider
f(x)=1/x
, and thus
\gamma_{k}=(-1)^{k-1}
, and assume that we want to estimate
f(m)
for
m>0
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{p\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{var}[\hat{f}^{\mathrm{S}}|R]\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{2}{m}-\frac{1}{x_{0}}\right)\left\{\frac{1}{1-\beta^{2}}-\frac{1}{1-\beta_{0}^{2}}\right\}.

If
\sigma^{2}>0
, then
\beta^{2}>\beta_{0}^{2}
, and since we need to select
x_{0}>m/2
to guarantee that the Taylor sum (
1
) converges, we have
\lim_{p\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{var}[\hat{f}^{\mathrm{S}}|R]\right]>0
, showing that the variance of the simple estimator does not converge to 0 in general.

2.3.
Cycling estimator

One obvious defect of the simple estimator is that computing
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{S}}

requires generating
R
random variables, but
U_{R,k}^{\mathrm{S}}
estimates
(m/x_{0}-1)^{k}
using only the first

k
of these univariates. Utilising the the full sample set
\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{R}\}
to
estimate
(m/x_{0}-1)^{k}
could give more precise estimates.

We propose the following estimate obtained by averaging over circular
shifts of the data, henceforth referred to as the ‘cycling’ estimate,

(12)

U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}:=\frac{1}{r}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{X_{i}}{x_{0}}-1\right)+\prod_{i=2}^{k+1}\left(\frac{X_{i}}{x_{0}}-1\right)+\ldots+\left(\frac{X_{r}}{x_{0}}-1\right)\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\left(\frac{X_{i}}{x_{0}}-1\right)\right].

Although it averages
r
non-independent estimates of
(m/x_{0}-1)^{k}
, it should
have lower variance than the simple estimator
U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{S}}
. We denote by
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{C}}

the corresponding sum estimator.

The following proposition shows that the cycling estimate, indeed, typically has lower
variance than the simple estimate.

Proposition 9
.

Assume the same conditions as
Proposition
7
, namely,
Assumption
3
, condition (
10
), and

R\sim\mathrm{Geometric}(p)
with
p\in(0,1-\beta^{2})
. Then
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{C}}\in\mathcal{L}_{2}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{var}(\hat{f}^{\mathrm{C}}|R)\right]\leq\frac{4c^{2}p\log(1/p)}{(1-p-\beta^{2})^{2}}\left[\beta^{2}+2\frac{\beta_{0}(1-p)}{(1-\beta_{0})^{2}}\right].

See Appendix
B.4
for a proof.

In other words, by taking
\mathbb{E}[R]\rightarrow+\infty
, the cycling estimate recovers, up to a log
factor, the standard Monte Carlo rate, i.e. the variance is
\mathcal{O}\left((\log\{\mathbb{E}[R]\})/\mathbb{E}[R]\right)
.
Thus, both terms of the variance in (
6
) now decrease as the mean of
R
increases.

A direct corollary, through Chebyshev’s inequality, of the above result is that
the cycling estimator converges in probability.

Corollary 10
.

Under the same assumptions as Proposition
9
,

\hat{f}^{\mathrm{C}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle P}}{{\rightarrow}}f(m)
as
p\rightarrow 0
.

2.4.
Minimum variance unbiased estimator

Instead of the cycling estimator, one could consider the following U-statistic:

(13)

U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{MVUE}}:=\frac{1}{\binom{r}{k}}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\leq r}\left(\frac{X_{i_{1}}}{x_{0}}-1\right)\cdot\dots\cdot\left(\frac{X_{i_{k}}}{x_{0}}-1\right).

which has minimimum variance among unbiased estimators of its expectation, see

Hoeffding, 1948
. In spite of the combinatorial number of terms, it is
still possible to compute the estimator in
\mathcal{O}(r^{2})
operations, using a
recursion, as pointed out by Sam Power in a personal communication, see
Appendix
D
. However, repeating our variance analysis for the MVUE
estimator appears challenging, indeed due to the combinatorial number of terms
in (
13
), and is left for further study. For the moment, we present a
numerical comparison in Appendix
D
, which happens to show cycling
and MVUE performing similarly.

2.5.
Relative merits of the simple and cycling estimators

The cycling estimator makes it possible to estimate
f(m)
as accurately as
required, and enjoys, up to a log factor, the same error rate as Monte Carlo
estimates. On the other hand, it is more expensive to compute than the simple
estimator. Although both estimators require generating
R
random variables, computing the
U_{R,k}
’s (
0\leq k\leq R
) for the cycling estimator requires
\mathcal{O}(R^{2})
operations.

That said, in many practical scenarios, the
X_{i}
’s can be expensive to generate. For instance, in our third numerical experiment we run a sequential Monte Carlo (SMC) sampler to get a
single
X_{i}
, which takes about 15 seconds. The CPU cost of the
\mathcal{O}(R^{2})
operations for the cycling estimate is negligible compared to the CPU cost of generating the
X_{i}
’s, unless
R
is
extremely large. In this scenario, the cycling estimate is clearly preferable owing to the potentially large variance reductions achieved.

Another way to see the relative merits of both estimators is to look at their
robustness relative to tuning parameters. This point will be discussed at
length in Section
3
. However, if our figure of merit
is ‘variance times CPU cost’, which is a common figure of merit in
Monte Carlo, then the cycling estimator
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{C}}
is clearly preferable since this
figure of merit diverges at a log rate as
p\rightarrow 0
, whereas it diverges at a
linear rate for the simple estimator.

2.6.
Unbiased estimators of gradients

In this section, we further develop the simple and cycling estimators to
unbiasedly estimate
\nabla_{\theta}f\left(m(\theta)\right)
, the gradient of

f\left(m(\theta)\right)
when
m=m(\theta)
depends on a parameter
\theta
. A
typical application that utilises unbiased gradient estimates is maximum
likelihood estimation; see Section
4
for an example.

Assuming
m(\theta)
is differentiable, using (
1
),

(14)

\nabla_{\theta}f(m(\theta))=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{m(\theta)}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{k-1}\frac{\nabla_{\theta}m(\theta)}{x_{0}},

and the corresponding ‘sum’ estimator, that is similar to (
2
) in construction, is

(15)

\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}f}:=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathds{1}\{R\geq k\}}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)}W_{R,k}\frac{k\gamma_{k}}{x_{0}},

where
R
is a random variable taking values in
\{1,\ldots\}
;
W_{R,k}=\sum_{r=k}^{\infty}W_{r,k}\mathds{1}\{R=r\}
;
W_{r,k}
is an unbiased estimate of
(m(\theta)/x_{0}-1)^{k-1}\nabla_{\theta}m(\theta)
for
r\geq k
; and each and every
W_{r,k}
is independent of
R
.

As was stated for
\hat{f}
in Lemma
2
, the gradient
estimate can be shown to be integrable and unbiased when the estimates
W_{r,k}

satisfy similar conditions of Assumption
1
, that is, with
univariates
U_{r,k}
therein replaced with
W_{r,k}
, and
\gamma_{k}
replaced with

k\gamma_{k}
. See Appendix
A.1
for the proof of
unbiasedness of
\hat{f}
which can be easily adapted to the present gradient
case.

We can define a simple estimator for (
14
),
denoted by
\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}f}^{\mathrm{S}}
, by replacing
W_{r,k}

in (
15
) with

(16)

W_{r,k}^{\mathrm{S}}:=G_{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\left(\frac{X_{i}}{x_{0}}-1\right)

where
(X_{1},G_{1}),(X_{2},G_{2}),\dots,(X_{r},G_{r})
is an IID collection of bivariates such that

\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=m(\theta)
and
\mathbb{E}[G_{i}]=\nabla_{\theta}m(\theta)
.

Instead of
W_{r,k}^{\mathrm{S}}
, the cycling estimator, denoted by
\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}f}^{\mathrm{C}}
, uses
W_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}
which is an average over ‘circular shifts’ of
W_{r,k}^{\mathrm{S}}
,

W_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}:=\frac{1}{r}\Bigg[G_{k}\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\left(\frac{X_{i}}{x_{0}}-1\right)+G_{k+1}\prod_{i=2}^{k}\left(\frac{X_{i}}{x_{0}}-1\right)+\ldots+G_{k-1}\left(\frac{X_{r}}{x_{0}}-1\right)\prod_{i=1}^{k-2}\left(\frac{X_{i}}{x_{0}}-1\right)\Bigg].

As was done in the previous section, we can analyse the variance of both
estimators using the law of total variance (
6
). To ensure that
the second term of the variance decomposition, namely
\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{var}(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}f}|R)\right]
, is finite we further need to assume that the second moment of

(X_{1},G_{1})
is finite.

Proposition 11
.

At the point
\theta
, assume
(X_{1},G_{1})
has finite second moments and let

\sigma^{2}(\theta)=\operatorname{var}(X_{1})
. Furthermore, let
Assumption
3
hold at
m=m(\theta)
; assume

\beta^{2}:=\frac{\sigma^{2}(\theta)}{x_{0}^{2}}+\left(\frac{m(\theta)}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{2}<1;

and let
R\sim\mathrm{Geometric}(p)
with
p\in(0,1-\beta^{2})
. Then both
\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}f}^{\mathrm{S}}
and
\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}f}^{\mathrm{C}}
are in
\mathcal{L}_{2}
. In addition,

(1)

\operatorname{var}\left[\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}f}|R]\right]=\mathcal{O}(p)
as
p\to 0
, for
\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}f}\in\{\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}f}^{\mathrm{S}},\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}f}^{\mathrm{C}}\}
.

(2)

As
p\to 0
,
\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{var}(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}f}^{\mathrm{S}}|R)\right]
converges to a finite, possibly non-zero, value.

(3)

\mathbb{E}\left[\operatorname{var}(\widehat{\nabla_{\theta}f}^{\mathrm{C}}|R)\right]=\mathcal{O}\left(p\log(1/p)\right)
as
p\to 0
.

The proof is in Appendix
B.5
.

The first statement implies the variance of the conditional expectation decreases as
\mathcal{O}(1/\mathbb{E}[R])
as
\mathbb{E}[R]\rightarrow\infty
. The second statement confirms the variance of the simple estimate does not diminish to zero as
\mathbb{E}[R]
increases; this is verified for
f(x)=\log(x).
In contrast, the final statement confirms the cycle method’s variance diminishes at rate
\log(\mathbb{E}[R])/\mathbb{E}[R]
.

3.
Tuning parameters

3.1.
Tuning
x_{0}

We now discuss how to set the tuning parameters
x_{0}
and
p
in practice. For
the sake of simplicity, we focus on the cycling estimator,
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{C}}
, and we
assume
m>0
, but adapting our discussion to either
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{S}}
or
m<0
should
be straightforward.

Propositions
7
and

9
of Section
2
reveal
the key considerations for selecting
x_{0}
and parameter
p
of
R\sim\mathrm{Geometric}(p)
,
appropriately. Firstly, to make sure that the variance of
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{C}}
is finite,

x_{0}
must be chosen to ensure that
\beta^{2}<1
; see (
10
).
Secondly, once
x_{0}
is chosen, then
p
has to be found from the interval
p\in(0,1-\beta^{2})
. This order cannot be inverted in general because, if
p
is too
close to one, there may not be an
x_{0}
that guarantees
\beta^{2}<1-p
as

\beta^{2}
has a unique minimiser with respect to
x_{0}
.

We now go through how to make these critical choices. From our experience, this discussion is largely neglected in the literature. We will further illustrate in Section
4
, with the aid of numerical examples, the importance of these tuning considerations, and the robustness of our tuning procedure below.

Recall, we focus the discussion for
m>0
. We see that
\beta^{2}<1\iff x_{0}>x_{0}^{\min}:=m/2+\sigma^{2}/(2m)
. Rather than targeting
x_{0}^{\min}
, we target the
x_{0}
that minimises
\beta^{2}
, namely

(17)

\displaystyle x_{0}^{\star}:=\arg\min_{x_{0}}\left\{\frac{\sigma^{2}}{x_{0}^{2}}+\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{2}\right\}=\frac{m^{2}+\sigma^{2}}{m}.

Being the minimiser,
x_{0}^{\star}
automatically fulfils the condition for
\beta^{2}<1
. Incidentally,
x_{0}^{\star}=2x_{0}^{\min}
. But there are also other reasons why
x_{0}^{\star}
might be a good choice. The bound in Proposition
9
grows
with
\beta^{2}
and even diverges as
\beta^{2}\rightarrow 1-p
. So, it makes sense to
minimise this quantity with respect to
x_{0}
. Also, minimising
\beta^{2}
permits a larger
p
, and this could save on the computational cost to generate the estimate of
f(m)
.

The catch is that we do not know the values of
m
and
\sigma^{2}
. The only general solution to this issue is to use a pilot run. That is, use an initial batch of
n_{0}
random variables
X_{i}
to obtain estimates

\widehat{m}
and

\widehat{\sigma^{2}}
, plug these estimates into (
17
) to obtain

\widehat{x_{0}^{\star}}
and use this estimate as the value for
x_{0}
.

Note however the following fundamental drawback of this approach. No matter how
large the pilot run is, the probability that
\widehat{x_{0}^{\star}}<x_{0}^{\min}
,
and equally
\beta^{2}>1
,
will be non-zero, and the variance of
\hat{f}^{\mathrm{C}}
cannot be guaranteed to be finite.

To mitigate this issue, we need to find a way to make the probability
x_{0}<x_{0}^{\min}
as small as possible for the chosen
x_{0}
. We propose the following

bootstrap

(
Efron and Tibshirani, 1993
)
approach. Generate a pilot
sample of size
n_{0}
and use it as follows:

i.

Estimate
x_{0}^{\star}=(m^{2}+\sigma^{2})/m
as

\widehat{x_{0}^{\star}}=(\widehat{m}^{2}+\widehat{\sigma^{2}})/\widehat{m}
where

\widehat{m}
and
\widehat{\sigma^{2}}
are the empirical mean and variance of
the pilot sample.

ii.

Find the upper limit of a lower one-sided bootstrap

(1-\alpha)
-confidence interval for
x_{0}^{\min}
, with
\alpha
very
small; note that
x_{0}^{\min}=x_{0}^{\star}/2
.

iii.

Set
x_{0}
to the larger of these two values.

In this way, not only will the

\mathbb{P}(x_{0}>x_{0}^{\min})
be close to one for the chosen
x_{0}
, the latter will
potentially also be close to
x_{0}^{\star}
when the sample mean and variance
are accurate.

In practice, we recommend using the percentile method to construct the bootstrap
confidence interval, that is, the upper bound is the
(1-\alpha)
-quantile of
the bootstrap estimates of
x_{0}^{\min}
, as this method may work well even when
the distribution of the
X_{i}
’s is not symmetric; for example, see

Efron and Tibshirani, 1993
for more background on bootstrap confidence
intervals. In our experiments we set
\alpha=0.01
. A second obvious
recommendation is to favour a large
n_{0}
, as the computational budget permits.

3.2.
Setting
p
and the CPU budget

Steps (i)-(iii) of Section
3.1
return an
x_{0}
value such that
\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{x_{0}^{\min}}<x_{0}\right)\geq 1-\alpha
,
where
\widehat{x_{0}^{\min}}
is the plug-in estimate and
\mathbb{P}(\cdot)

is computed with the empirical cumulative distribution function given by the pilot samples
\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n_{0}}
.
Let
\widehat{\beta^{2}}
be the corresponding plug-in estimate of

\beta^{2}
: in (
10
), use the found
x_{0}
from Step (iii) and
(\widehat{m},\widehat{\sigma^{2}})
from Step (i). Then – at this returned value for
x_{0}
– evidently,
we also have
\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{\beta^{2}}<1\right)\geq 1-\alpha
.
This motivates choosing
p
as follows:

iv.

Set

.

Numerical experiments

4.1.
Toy latent variable model

We consider the toy latent variable model (LVM) of
Rainforth et al., 2018
, also used in
Shi and Cornish, 2021
. Conditioned on
\theta\in\real
, the observations
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
are sampled independently as follows,

\displaystyle z_{i}|\theta

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\cdot;\theta\textsf{1}_{d},\textsf{Id}_{d}),\qquad\qquad y_{i}|z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(\cdot;z_{i},\textsf{Id}_{d}),

where
z_{i}
and
y_{i}\in\real^{d}
.

We set
\theta=1
and sample a data set of size
n=10^{3}
.
The goal is to unbiasedly estimate the log-likelihood
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p(y_{i}|\theta)
for

\theta=1
, a quantity which we are able to compute exactly for this model.

To do so, we set
f(m)=\log m
and consider each of the
n
data points separately. For
a given
y\in\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}
, we apply our approach as follows:
conditional on
R=r
, we sample
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{r}\sim p(z|\theta)
and set
X_{j}=p(y|Z_{j},\theta)
for
j=1,\dots,r
, which is evidently an unbiased estimate of

p(y|\theta)=\int p(y|z,\theta)p(z|\theta)dz
. In this particular case, we can exactly compute the
moments
m=\mathbb{E}[X_{j}]=p(y|\theta)
and
\sigma^{2}=\operatorname{var}[X_{j}]
; the optimal expansion point
x_{0}^{\star}=m+\sigma^{2}/m
; and the
corresponding value for
\beta^{2}
. See Appendix
E.1
for their
expressions.

We use this toy model to compare the performance of the simple and cycling
estimators of
\log p(y|\theta)
with that of the multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC)
estimator of
Shi and Cornish, 2021
. They use MLMC to modify the SUMO
approach of
Luo et al., 2020
to return an unbiased estimate of
\log p(y|\theta)

with
finite variance. Thus, we do not consider the SUMO
estimator here since its variance is infinite. Furthermore,
Shi and Cornish, 2021
show, empirically, that MLMC outperforms SUMO.

The estimator proposed by
Shi and Cornish, 2021
employs
MLMC
(
Blanchet et al., 2019
)
to debias the estimator of
\log p(y|\theta)
given by Importance Weighted Autoencoders (IWAE;

Burda et al., 2016
). The MLMC estimator is

(18)

\displaystyle\widehat{\log p(y|\theta)}^{\textrm{MLMC}}=I_{j}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Delta_{k}\mathds{1}\{\widetilde{R}\geq k\}}{\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{R}\geq k)},

where
\widetilde{R}\sim\mathrm{Geometric}(0.6)
,

I_{j}
,
j\geq 0
, is the IWAE estimate with proposal
q(\cdot;y)
and
2^{j}
samples:

\displaystyle I_{j}:=\log\frac{1}{2^{j}}\sum_{h=1}^{2^{j}}w(Z_{h};y,\theta),\quad\text{with }w(Z_{h};y,\theta):=\frac{p(Z_{h},y|\theta)}{q(Z_{h};y)},\quad Z_{1},\dots,Z_{2^{j}}\sim q(\cdot;y).

and
\Delta_{k}:=I_{j+k+1}-(I_{j+k}^{E}+I_{j+k}^{O})/2
; with
I_{j+k}^{E}
(resp.

I_{j+k}^{O}
) being the IWAE estimate that only uses the even-indexed (resp.
odd-indexed) samples. We consider the same proposal as in

Shi and Cornish, 2021
, i.e.

\mathcal{N}(\cdot;(y+\theta^{\star}\textsf{1}_{d})/2,\frac{2}{3}\textsf{Id}_{d})
, where
\theta^{\star}
denotes the maximum likelihood estimator for
\theta
, which is
shown to be optimal within a certain family in
Rainforth et al., 2018
.

The expected CPU cost, i.e. the number of generated
X_{j}
, is
\mathbb{E}[R]=1/p-1
for
our Taylor-based estimators, and
\mathbb{E}[2^{j+1+\widetilde{R}}]
for the MLMC
approach of
Shi and Cornish, 2021
. We adjust
p
and
j
so that both
expectations are equal to a certain pre-specified budget
C
.
We consider two values for
C
, namely
C=6
and
C=96
.

Figure

shows 100 estimates of each type (simple,
cycling, MLMC) for
d=2
(left column) and
d=5

C
of 6 (top line) and 96 (bottom

\mathbb{E}[R]=96

4.3.
Exponential random graphs

Exponential random graph (ERG) models are one of the classical examples of
models with a doubly intractable posterior distributions. For this model, the data is the observed edge connectivity of an
n
-node undirected graph. In particular, one
observes
y=(y_{ij})_{i<j}
, where
y_{ij}=1
(resp.
0
) if nodes

i
and
j
are connected (resp. are not). The likelihood is, given a
parameter
\theta
and canonical statistics
s(y)
, both in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
,

(20)

p(y|\theta)=\exp\left\{\theta^{T}s(y)\right\}/\mathcal{Z}(\theta).

The normalising constant
\mathcal{Z}(\theta)
is a sum over
2^{\binom{n}{2}}
terms,
and is therefore intractable.

We construct an unbiased estimate of

5.
Conclusion

We develop a general strategy for unbiasedly estimating
f(m)
, for a smooth
function
f
, from random variables
X_{i}
with expectation
m
. Inspired by
the simple estimator (Section
2.2
) – which is widely used in
the literature – we proposed a new cycling estimator
(Section
2.3
), and obtained conditions that guarantee finite
estimator and execution time (CPU cost) variance for both methods. This is an
improvement over previous approaches. In particular, the multi-level Monte
Carlo method of
Luo et al., 2020
returns estimates with infinite variance,
while the one of
Shi and Cornish, 2021
has a running time with infinite
variance. The latter issue may be particularly problematic in a parallel
implementation since the completion time is the maximum of all threads being
run.

As pointed out by a referee, the fact that the execution cost is random may be
a drawback in certain implementation scenarios, even if it has finite variance.
In such a case, one may prefer using a biased estimate whose execution
cost is deterministic. Such an approach has less theoretical support, but seems
to work well in certain practical applications, e.g., to approximate gradients
in variational auto-encoders
(
Burda et al., 2016
)
.

We show that the cycling estimator outperforms the simple one both
theoretically, see Proposition
9
and
Example
8
and empirically, see Section
4
,
with potentially only a little more computational cost. We also discussed the
sensitivity of these methods to their tuning parameters, an issue we found to
be largely overlooked in the literature. We gave precise guidelines for
selecting the expansion point
x_{0}
and the distribution of the random variable

R
that determines the random truncation (Sections
3.1

and
3.2
).

Our experiments show that, in order to build reliable unbiased estimates of

f(m)
or its gradient, one needs input variables
X_{i}
with low or moderate
variance. In fact, the minimal
\beta^{2}
in
Proposition
7
, achieved for
x_{0}^{\star}

in (
17
), is
(\beta^{2})^{\star}=\sigma^{2}/(\sigma^{2}+m^{2})
. Note
that
1-(\beta^{2})^{\star}
is indeed the inverse of relative variance of the

X_{i}
’s. As

\sigma^{2}/m^{2}\rightarrow\infty
, we need to select
p\in(0,1-(\beta^{2})^{\star})\to\{0\}
, hence requiring an infinite computational cost. In this case, we
recommend dedicating the computational effort towards building lower variance
estimates of
m
.

Even if the
X_{i}
’s have low variance, a poorly chosen
x_{0}
can cause the
estimators of
f(m)
to have high variance. While in low dimensional problems
one can get away with a poorly tuned
x_{0}
, we found that for high dimensional
applications, e.g., the ICA example in Section
4.2
, it is crucial
that the input variables
X_{i}
’s have low variance
and
to fine tune

x_{0}
, as recommended in Section
3.1
. Once
x_{0}
has been
selected, we describe a simple strategy to select
p
to guarantee that
\mathbb{E}[R]

is sufficiently large to have finite and small variance. This strategy works
well for the experiments we considered, however, in some cases it might be
possible to build optimal distributions for particular
f
’s (see,
e.g.
Rhee and Glynn, 2015
;
Cui et al., 2021
;
Zheng et al., 2023
).

As a by-product of our study, we provide a method to unbiasedly estimate the
model evidence in doubly intractable models. Previous attempts in the context
of ERG models have focused on pseudo-likelihood approaches in which the
intractable likelihood is replaced by a tractable approximation

(
Bouranis et al., 2018
)
. Also, using a combination of path sampling and kernel
density estimation, which provides biased estimators and are only suitable in
low-dimensional settings
(
Caimo and Friel, 2013
, Section 4)
.

Proof of Lemma

We will show

(22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathds{1}\{R\geq k\}}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)}|\gamma_{k}||U_{R,k}|\right]<\infty,

which implies the infinite series on the right-hand side of (

exists almost surely and is integrable. To show it has the desired mean, as (

(

to swap the expectation and the sum and obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}\{R\geq k\}U_{R,k}\right]}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)}\gamma_{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{k}.

To show (

(
Billingsley, 1995

allows the swap of the expectation and the sum,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathds{1}\{R\geq k\}}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)}|\gamma_{k}||U_{R,k}|\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}\{R\geq k\}|U_{R,k}|\right]}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)}|\gamma_{k}|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|\gamma_{k}|a_{k}<\infty,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}\{R\geq k\}|U_{R,k}|\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=k}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}\{R=r\}|U_{r,k}|\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=k}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(R=r)\mathbb{E}\left[|U_{r,k}|\right]

\displaystyle\leq a_{k}\sum_{r=k}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(R=r).

Note that the independence of
R
and

was invoked in the second line.

A.4.
Derivation of (

We start by computing
\mathbb{E}[(\hat{f})^{2}|R=r]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[(\hat{f})^{2}|R=r]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathds{1}\{R\geq k\}}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)}\gamma_{k}U_{R,k}\right)^{2}|R=r\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathds{1}\{R\geq k\}}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)^{2}}\gamma_{k}^{2}U_{R,k}^{2}|R=r\right]

\displaystyle+\mathbb{E}\left[2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{l=k+1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathds{1}\{R\geq k\}\mathds{1}\{R\geq l\}}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)\mathbb{P}(R\geq l)}\gamma_{k}\gamma_{l}U_{R,k}U_{R,l}|R=r\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathds{1}\{r\geq k\}}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)^{2}}\gamma_{k}^{2}U_{r,k}^{2}+2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{l=k+1}^{\infty}\frac{\mathds{1}\{r\geq l\}}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)\mathbb{P}(R\geq l)}\gamma_{k}\gamma_{l}U_{r,k}U_{r,l}.

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathds{1}\{r\geq k\}}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)^{2}}\gamma_{k}^{2}\left(U_{r,k}^{2}-\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{2k}\right)

B.1.
Proof of Proposition
4

Let
\tilde{f}=\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}|R]
.
The lower bound can be found by the law of total variance:
\operatorname{var}[\tilde{f}]\geq\operatorname{var}\left[\mathbb{E}[\tilde{f}|\mathds{1}\{R>0\}]\right]
and

\mathbb{E}[\tilde{f}|\mathds{1}\{R>0\}]=\gamma_{0}+\frac{\mathds{1}\{R>0\}}{\mathbb{P}(R>0)}(f(m)-\gamma_{0})
.
In the case of
R\sim\mathrm{Geometric}(p)
we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}[\tilde{f}]

\displaystyle\geq(f(m)-\gamma_{0})^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(R>0)}-1\right)

\displaystyle=(f(m)-\gamma_{0})^{2}\left(\frac{1}{1-p}-1\right).

For the upper bound, under Assumption
3
,
the first sum (
7
) can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}^{2}\beta_{0}^{2k}\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)}-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq c^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left[\left(\frac{\beta_{0}^{2}}{1-p}\right)^{k}-\beta_{0}^{2k}\right]

\displaystyle=c^{2}\left(\frac{1-p}{1-p-\beta_{0}^{2}}-\frac{1}{1-\beta_{0}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{c^{2}p\beta_{0}^{2}}{(1-\beta_{0}^{2})(1-p-\beta_{0}^{2})},

where the convergence of the infinite sum is guaranteed by the fact that
\beta_{0}<1
and
1-p>\beta_{0}^{2}
.

The absolute value of the second term may be bounded in a similar manner:

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{l=k+1}^{\infty}\left|\gamma_{k}\gamma_{l}\right|\beta_{0}^{k+l}\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)}-1\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2c^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)}-1\right)\sum_{l=k+1}^{\infty}\beta_{0}^{k+l}

\displaystyle=\frac{2c^{2}\beta_{0}}{1-\beta_{0}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\beta_{0}^{2k}\left(\frac{1}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)}-1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\beta_{0}}{1-\beta_{0}}\times\frac{c^{2}p\beta_{0}^{2}}{(1-\beta_{0}^{2})(1-p-\beta_{0}^{2})}.

Combining the two inequalities leads to:

\displaystyle\operatorname{var}\left[\mathbb{E}[\hat{f}|R]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(1+\frac{2\beta_{0}}{1-\beta_{0}}\right)\frac{c^{2}p\beta_{0}^{2}}{(1-\beta_{0}^{2})(1-p-\beta_{0}^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c^{2}\beta_{0}^{2}}{(1-\beta_{0})^{2}}\times\frac{p}{1-p-\beta_{0}^{2}}.

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{r=1}^{t}2\mathbb{P}\left(R=r\right)\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}\sum_{l=k+1}^{r}\frac{\gamma_{k}\gamma_{l}}{\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)\mathbb{P}(R\geq l)}\beta^{k+l}

-(i) assumes

Before proceeding with the proof of

9

It is convenient to define

The second moment of the cycling variables can be obtained by direct multiplication, distinguishing between three different cases:

Lemma 12
.

For any
r\geq l\geq k

(1)

If

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\right]=\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{l+k}\frac{1}{r}\left[(r-l-k+1)+2\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\rho^{j}+(l-k+1)\rho^{k}\right],

with the convention that
\sum_{j=n_{1}}^{n_{2}}=0
if
n_{1}<n_{2}
.

(2)

If

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\right]=\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{l+k}\frac{1}{r}\left[2\sum_{j=l+k-r+1}^{k-1}\rho^{j}+(l-k+1)\rho^{k}+(l+k-r+1)\rho^{l+k-r}\right],

with the convention that
\sum_{j=n_{1}}^{n_{2}}=0
if
n_{1}<n_{2}
.

(3)

If
r=l
, the cycling variable
U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}
coincides with
U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{S}}
and we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\right]=\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{x_{0}^{2}}+\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{2}\right)^{k}\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{l-k}=\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{l+k}\rho^{k}.

Proof.

We only prove case
1
. The proof for the second and third case follows the same lines.

Recall that
\widetilde{X}_{i}=(X_{i}/x_{0}-1)
, let

\widetilde{m}:=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{i}/x_{0}-1\right]
and

\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}:=\operatorname{var}\left(X_{i}/x_{0}-1\right)=\sigma^{2}/x_{0}^{2}
. Expanding

we find

\displaystyle r\times U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}=

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{l}\widetilde{X}_{i}+\dots+\prod_{i=k}^{l+k-1}\widetilde{X}_{i}

\displaystyle+\prod_{i=k+1}^{l+k}\widetilde{X}_{i}+\prod_{i=k+2}^{l+k+1}\widetilde{X}_{i}+\dots+\prod_{i=r-l+1}^{r}\widetilde{X}_{i}

\displaystyle+\left(\prod_{i=r-l+2}^{r}\widetilde{X}_{i}\right)\widetilde{X}_{1}+\dots+\widetilde{X}_{r}\prod_{i=1}^{l-1}\widetilde{X}_{i}.

The expansion has been organised in three separate rows for the following reason. Note that each row is non-empty since
r\geq k+l.
(i) We commence by calculating
\mathbb{E}\left[U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{X}_{j}\right]
. The first and third row involve non-independent products with the term
\prod_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{X}_{j}
, whereas for the middle row, the expected value is the product of the expectations. (ii) The previous calculation is sufficient since, by symmetry,
\mathbb{E}\left[U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{X}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle r\times U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\times\prod_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{X}_{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{X}_{j}^{2}\prod_{i=k+1}^{l}\widetilde{X}_{i}+\dots+\widetilde{X}_{k}^{2}\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\widetilde{X}_{j}\prod_{i=k+1}^{l+k-1}\widetilde{X}_{i}

\displaystyle+\prod_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{X}_{j}\prod_{i=k+1}^{l+k}\widetilde{X}_{i}+\dots+\prod_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{X}_{j}\prod_{i=r-l+1}^{r}\widetilde{X}_{i}

\displaystyle+\widetilde{X}_{1}^{2}\prod_{j=2}^{k}\widetilde{X}_{j}\prod_{i=r-l+2}^{r}\widetilde{X}_{i}+\dots+\prod_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{X}_{j}^{2}\prod_{i=r-l+k+1}^{r}\widetilde{X}_{i}+\dots+\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{X}_{j}^{2}\right)\widetilde{X}_{r}\prod_{i=k+1}^{l-1}\widetilde{X}_{i},

and, taking expectations,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[rU_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{X}_{j}\right]=

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}+\widetilde{m}^{2})^{k}\widetilde{m}^{l-k}+\dots+(\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}+\widetilde{m}^{2})\widetilde{m}^{l+k-2}

\displaystyle+\widetilde{m}^{l+k}+\ldots+\widetilde{m}^{l+k}

\displaystyle+(\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}+\widetilde{m}^{2})\widetilde{m}^{l+k-2}+\dots+(\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}+\widetilde{m}^{2})^{k}\widetilde{m}^{l-k}+\dots+(\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}+\widetilde{m}^{2})^{k}\widetilde{m}^{l-k}.

Counting how many times each term in the sum above appears we find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[rU_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\widetilde{X}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=\widetilde{m}^{l+k}(r-(l+k)+1)+2\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}(\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}+\widetilde{m}^{2})^{j}\widetilde{m}^{l+k-2j}+(\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}+\widetilde{m}^{2})^{k}\widetilde{m}^{l-k}(l-k+1)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{m}^{l+k}\left[(r-l-k+1)+2\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left(\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}}{\widetilde{m}^{2}}+1\right)^{j}+(l-k+1)\left(\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}}{\widetilde{m}^{2}}+1\right)^{k}\right].

We can then exploit the symmetry of
U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}
to conclude

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\widetilde{m}^{l+k}}{r}\left[(r-l-k+1)+2\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\left(\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}}{\widetilde{m}^{2}}+1\right)^{j}+(l-k+1)\left(\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}}{\widetilde{m}^{2}}+1\right)^{k}\right].

Using the fact that

\widetilde{\sigma}^{2}=\sigma^{2}/x_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\right]=\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{l+k}\frac{1}{r}\left[(r-l-k+1)+2\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\rho^{j}+(l-k+1)\rho^{k}\right].

B.4.2.
Bound on second moments of cycling variables

To characterise the behaviour of the cycling estimator as the number of terms in the Taylor expansion increases, we establish the following bound on
\operatorname{cov}\left[U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\right]
.

Lemma 13
.

Let

. Then,

\displaystyle\operatorname{cov}\left[U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\right]<\left\lvert\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right\rvert^{l+k}\times\begin{cases}\rho^{k}(l+k)/r,&\text{for }r\geq l+k\\
(\rho^{k}+1)&\text{for }r<l+k\\
(\rho^{k}-1)&\text{for }r=l.\end{cases}

Proof.

The proofs relies on bounding each one of the second moments obtained in Lemma
12
separately.
Recall that
\operatorname{cov}\left[U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\right]
.
Using the expression for
\mathbb{E}\left[U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\right]
in Lemma

12
–
1
, since
\rho>1
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{cov}\left[U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\right]

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{l+k}\frac{1}{r}\left[(r-l-k+1)+2\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\rho^{j}+(l-k+1)\rho^{k}-r\right]

\displaystyle<\left\lvert\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right\rvert^{l+k}\frac{1}{r}\left[1+2\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\rho^{j}+(l-k+1)\rho^{k}\right]

\displaystyle<\frac{\rho^{k}}{r}\left\lvert\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right\rvert^{l+k}\left[1+2\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}\rho^{j-k}+(l-k+1)\right]

\displaystyle<\frac{\rho^{k}}{r}\left\lvert\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right\rvert^{l+k}\left[1+2(k-1)+(l-k+1)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho^{k}}{r}(l+k)\left\lvert\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right\rvert^{l+k}.

Similarly, for case
2

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[U_{r,k}^{\mathrm{C}}U_{r,l}^{\mathrm{C}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{l+k}\rho^{k}\left[2\sum_{j=l+k-r+1}^{k-1}\rho^{j-k}+(l-k+1)+(l+k-r+1)\rho^{l-r}\right]

It follows that

Collecting all the stated, for cases

and

∎

B.4.3.
Proof of Proposition

Before proceeding to the proof of Proposition

Lemma 14

.

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p}{1-p}\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-p)^{h}}{h}

For a given

We compare our approach with one of the state of the art for Bayesian inference on ERG models, i.e. the exchange algorithm of

. We use the same setup described in Section 4.5 therein. The shape of the posterior distribution seems to be in good agreement with the obtained in our experiments (see Figure

Simple

Bivariate weighted histograms approximating the posterior distributions obtained with the simple estimator, the cycling estimator and the

package implementing the exchange algorithm of

.

As we now discuss, there are good reasons in favour of our method. First, the exchange algorithm of
Caimo and Friel, 2011
requires running nested MCMC chains where the inner one samples from the likelihood
p(y|\theta)
; in addition, in the version of the algorithm implemented in
bergm
, population Monte Carlo is employed to improve mixing. As a consequence, the resulting method is rather expensive and, due to the serial nature of MCMC, harder to parallelise than our proposed approach, in which each
\mathcal{Z}(\theta)
is obtained from different independent runs of SMC which can run in parallel.

Secondly, a fundamental task in the context of network model is the ability to select the model that best fits the data. This type of inference requires estimation of the model evidence
p(y)
.
To the best of our knowledge, given the output of the exchange algorithm one can compute the model evidence

p(y)=p(\theta)\frac{\exp\{\theta^{T}s(y)\}}{\mathcal{Z}(\theta)}\frac{1}{p(\theta|y)}

in two ways:

(1)

An approach similar to ours in which
\log\mathcal{Z}(\theta)
is estimated via path sampling, and
p(\theta|y)
via the exchange algorithm and a kernel density estimate (KDE)
(
Caimo and Friel, 2013
, Section 4)
.

(2)

Pseudo-likelihood approaches in which the intractable likelihood is replaced by a tractable approximation
(
Bouranis et al., 2018
)
.

The first approach requires running the exchange algorithm to sample from
p(\theta|y)
, from which a KDE is formed. Path sampling with auxiliary MCMC chains is used to sample from
p(y|\theta)
.
This approach is clearly only suitable for a small number of parameters due to the instability of the KDE in high dimensions. Also, as mentioned by the authors, this strategy incurs two sources of error when estimating
\log\mathcal{Z}(\theta)
: the Monte Carlo error to approximate the expectation w.r.t.
p(y|\theta)
, and the error due to discretising the path integral. The resulting approximation of the model evidence is naturally biased.
Our approach allows computation of the model evidence directly from the samples used to estimate the posterior, without requiring additional samples from
p(y|\theta)
and without the use of KDE. It also provides unbiased estimators which can be used within pseudo-marginal methods for model selection.

The second approach also cannot provide unbiased estimators of the model evidence and the quality of the approximation largely depends on the pseudo-likelihood used. To obtain a likelihood approximation close to the true one,
Bouranis et al., 2018
propose several improvements. We tested the evidence estimation in the
bergm
package on our toy example and found that the Monte Carlo MLE estimator needed to implement their proposed adjustments to the pseudo-likelihood could not converge since the underlying MCMC was not mixing.
