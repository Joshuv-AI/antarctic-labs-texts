Regularity results for free Lévy processes
Hao-Wei Huang and Jiun-Chau Wang

Abstract
. Given a free additive convolution semigroup ( μ t ) t ≥ 0 \left(\mu_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0} and a probability measure ν \nu on ℝ \mathbb{R} , we find the necessary and sufficient conditions for the process μ t ⊞ ν \mu_{t}\boxplus\nu to be Lebesgue absolutely continuous with a positive and analytic density throughout ℝ \mathbb{R} at all time t > 0 t>0 . For semigroups without this property, we find the necessary and sufficient conditions for the density of μ t ⊞ ν \mu_{t}\boxplus\nu to be analytic at its zeros. These results are quantified by the Lévy measure of the semigroup, making it fairly easy to construct many concrete examples. Finally, we show that μ t ⊞ ν \mu_{t}\boxplus\nu has a finite number of connected components in its support if both the Lévy measure of ( μ t ) t ≥ 0 \left(\mu_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0} and the initial law ν \nu do.

1.
Introduction

Recall from Biane’s work [
References
] that a free (additive) Lévy process
\{Z_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is a noncommutative stochastic process of self-adjoint random variables such that
Z_{0}=0
,
Z_{t}
tends weakly to
0
as
t\rightarrow 0
, and the increments
Z_{t}-Z_{s}
are freely independent and stationary. Thus, denoting by
\mu_{t}
the distribution of
Z_{t}
, the family
\left(\mu_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
satisfies the weak convergence
\mu_{t}\Rightarrow\mu_{0}=\delta_{0}
as
t\rightarrow 0
and forms a
\boxplus
-semigroup
under the free additive convolution
\boxplus
in the sense that

\mu_{t+s}=\mu_{t}\boxplus\mu_{s},\quad s,t\geq 0.

In particular, every
\mu_{t}
is
\boxplus
-infinitely divisible
; and conversely, every
\boxplus
-infinitely divisible measure
\mu
embeds in a unique
\boxplus
-semigroup
\left(\mu_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
such that
\mu_{1}=\mu
. Since its appearance in [
References
], there has been an intensive research on free Lévy processes. For examples, see [
References
] for a free analogue of the Lévy-Itô decomposition, [
References
] for a construction of stochastic integrals, and [
References
] for a formula of the infinitesimal generator of a free Lévy process.

The current paper contributes to this line of research by proving regularity results of the free convolution
\mu_{t}\boxplus\nu
where
\nu
belongs to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}}
, the set of all probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
. Our results are concerned with the regularizing effect of
\left(\mu_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
. Recall the
property
(H) for a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}}
as follows:

(H)

For all
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}}
, the measure
\mu\boxplus\nu
is Lebesgue absolutely continuous and has a strictly positive, analytic density everywhere on
\mathbb{R}
.

A process
\left(\mu_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
is said to have property (H) if every marginal distribution
\mu_{t}
has this property. Thus, such a
\left(\mu_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
can serve as a distributional mollifier in a rather strong sense. Property (H) was introduced and studied in the paper [
References
] where some sufficient conditions for
\left(\mu_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
to satisfy (H) were found, along with some examples. It was also shown in [
References
] that in order for
\left(\mu_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
to have property (H), each
\mu_{t}
cannot have finite second moment.

For
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}}
and an
\boxplus
-infinitely divisible measure
\mu
, a formula for the density
p_{\mu\boxplus\nu}
of the absolutely continuous part of
\mu\boxplus\nu
is found in Theorem
3.9
and we show later in Theorem
3.13
that
p_{\mu\boxplus\nu}
is everywhere positive and analytic on
\mathbb{R}
if and only if

\int_{\mathbb{R}}s^{2}\,d\sigma_{\mu}(s)=+\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+s^{2}}{(x-s)^{2}}\,d\sigma_{\mu}(s)>1

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Here the measure
\sigma_{\mu}
is the
Lévy measure
of
\mu
(see Section 2). This result yields the necessary and sufficient conditions for a free Lévy process to have property (H), as seen in Corollary
3.15
. Notice that the value of the second moment
m_{2}(\sigma_{\mu})
of
\sigma_{\mu}
being infinite is known to be equivalent to
m_{2}(\mu)=+\infty
. For a semigroup
\left(\mu_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
without (H), in Theorem
3.19
, we find the necessary and sufficient conditions for the density
p_{\mu_{t}\boxplus\nu}
to be analytic at its zeros. Examples are given to illustrate such an analyticity behavior, and our result subsumes all known cases of this phenomenon in the context of free Lévy process, including Biane’s classical example of free Brownian motion [
References
]. Finally, Theorem
3.10
shows that
\mu\boxplus\nu
has a finite number of connected components in its support if both the Lévy measure
\sigma_{\mu}
and
\nu
do.

The main tool in our proofs is a global inversion result which can be traced back to Proposition 5.12 in the original free convolution paper [
References
] of Bercovici and Voiculescu. Such a global inversion technique is the basis of Biane’s analysis for free Brownian motion in [
References
]. Later on, this technique was further studied in great detail and applied to the study of partially defined free convolution semigroups in [
References
]. While the arguments in [
References
] made a clever use of the Denjoy-Wolff fixed point theory to produce a powerful general theory of global inversion, our approach based on calculus and integration is tailor-made for free Lévy processes. This real-variable method is closely related to Biane’s work [
References
] and the analysis used in [
References
] for partially defined free convolution semigroups. In addition, our method makes it fairly easy to construct processes with specific regularity properties, as illustrated by the examples in this paper.

The multiplicative analogs of regularities properties will appear in a forthcoming paper.

This paper is organized as follows. After collecting some preliminary material in Section 2, we state our main results and examples in Section 3. The detailed proofs of these results are presented in Section 4. We shall now begin by reviewing the essence of free harmonic analysis.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Integral transforms

Denote by
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
the family of finite positive Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}
and recall that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}}=\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}:\mu(\mathbb{R})=1\}
. For any
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
, we consider its
Cauchy transform

G_{\nu}(z)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{z-s}\,d\nu(s),

an analytic map from the complex upper half-plane
\mathbb{C}^{+}
into the lower half-plane
\mathbb{C}^{-}=-\mathbb{C}^{+}
. The map
G_{\nu}
determines the measure
\nu
uniquely. The
F
-transform
of
\nu
is defined to be the reciprocal
F_{\nu}=1/G_{\nu}
, an analytic self-map of
\mathbb{C}^{+}
.

It is well-known that every analytic map
F:\mathbb{C}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{+}\cup\mathbb{R}
admits a unique
Nevanlinna representation
:

(2.1)

F(z)=a+bz+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+sz}{s-z}\,d\rho(s),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

where
a=\Re F(i)
,
b=\lim_{y\rightarrow\infty}F(iy)/iy\geq 0
, and
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
. The measure
\rho
in this case will be called the
F
-representing measure
of
F
. Thus, every
F
-transform
F_{\nu}
admits the integral form (
2.1
), with
b=1/\nu(\mathbb{R})
, and we write
\rho=\rho_{\nu}
in this case. By virtue of the identity
sz/(s-z)=z+z^{2}/(s-z)
, we sometimes write the integral form (
2.1
) in the following way:

F(z)=a+(b+\rho(\mathbb{R}))z-(1+z^{2})G_{\rho}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

It follows from (
2.1
) that the
F
-transform of a measure
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies
\Im F_{\nu}(z)\geq\Im z
for
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
. If equality occurs for some
z
in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
, then
\nu
is
degenerate
, that is,
\nu
is the point mass
\delta_{a}
at some
a\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, the limit
\lim_{y\rightarrow\infty}F_{\nu}(iy)/iy=1
implies that there exist positive numbers
\alpha
and
\beta
such that
F_{\nu}
has an analytic right inverse
F_{\nu}^{-1}
defined in the truncated cone
\Gamma_{\alpha,\beta}=\{x+iy:|x|<\alpha y,\,y>\beta\}
. The
Voiculescu transform

\varphi_{\nu}
is defined as
\varphi_{\nu}(z)=F_{\nu}^{-1}(z)-z
, and the identity

\varphi_{\nu_{1}\boxplus\nu_{2}}(z)=\varphi_{\nu_{1}}(z)+\varphi_{\nu_{2}}(z)

holds in a cone on which the three Voiculescu transforms are defined, see [
References
]. The Voiculescu transform
\varphi_{\nu}
is related to the
R
-transform

R_{\nu}
via the formula
R_{\nu}(z)=\varphi_{\nu}(1/z)
.

2.2.
Infinite divisibility and Lévy measure

Recall that a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}}
is said to be
\boxplus
-infinitely divisible
if for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a measure
\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\mu=\mu_{n}\boxplus\cdots\boxplus\mu_{n}
(
n
times). The set of all
\boxplus
-infinitely divisible measures is denoted by
\mathcal{ID}(\boxplus)
. It was shown in [
References
] that
\mu\in\mathcal{ID}(\boxplus)
if and only if its Voiculescu transform
\varphi_{\mu}
satisfies the
free Lévy-Hinčin formula
:

(2.2)

\varphi_{\mu}(z)=\gamma+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+sz}{z-s}\,d\sigma(s),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}
. The number
\gamma
and the measure
\sigma
in (
2.2
) are unique, and the measure
\sigma
will be called the
Lévy measure
of the infinitely divisible measure
\mu
. We write
\gamma=\gamma_{\mu}
and
\sigma=\sigma_{\mu}
to indicate the correspondence between these parameters and the measure
\mu
through (
2.2
).

The equivalence of classical, free, and Boolean central limit theorems [
References
,
References
] shows that
m_{2}(\mu)<+\infty
if and only if
m_{2}(\sigma_{\mu})<+\infty
if and only if
m_{2}(\rho_{\mu})<+\infty
, where
\rho_{\mu}
is the
F
-representing measure of
F_{\mu}
; in which case we also have the first moment
m_{1}(\mu)=\gamma_{\mu}+m_{1}(\sigma_{\mu})
and the variance
var(\mu)=m_{2}(\mu)-[m_{1}(\mu)]^{2}=\sigma_{\mu}(\mathbb{R})+m_{2}(\sigma_{\mu})
.

Note that
\varphi_{\mu_{t}}(z)=t\,\varphi_{\mu_{1}}(z)
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
and
t>0
in a
\boxplus
-semigroup
\left(\mu_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
. In particular, one has
\sigma_{\mu_{t}}=t\,\sigma_{\mu_{1}}
and we shall call
\sigma_{\mu_{1}}
the Lévy measure of the semigroup
\left(\mu_{t}\right)_{t\geq 0}
.

2.3.
Boundary behavior of analytic maps

Given a nonconstant map
f:\mathbb{C}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, its
vertical limit

f^{*}(\alpha)
at a point
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is defined as

f^{*}(\alpha)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}f(\alpha+i\epsilon),

and
f
is said to have an
angular derivative

f^{\prime}(\alpha)
at
\alpha
if for some
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the limit

f^{\prime}(\alpha)=\lim_{z\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}\alpha}\frac{f(z)-s}{z-\alpha}

exists in
\mathbb{C}
. Here the
non-tangential convergence

z\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}\alpha
means that the quantity
|\Re z-\alpha|/\Im z
remains bounded as
z
tends to the real number
\alpha
. We also say that
z\rightarrow\infty
non-tangentially if
|z|\rightarrow+\infty
and
|\Re z|/\Im z
is bounded. Note that if
f^{\prime}(\alpha)
exists then
\lim_{z\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}\alpha}f(z)
exists and is equal to the real number
s
.

We mention a few well-known results about non-tangential and vertical limits. The upper half-plane version presented here is summarized from Section 2 of [
References
]. First, Fatou Theorem shows that if
f:\mathbb{C}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{+}\cup\mathbb{R}
is analytic, then
f^{*}(\alpha)
exists in
\mathbb{C}
for almost all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
with respect to Lebesgue measure
\lambda
. Next, Lindelöf theorem for an analytic map
f:\mathbb{C}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
states that if
\mathbb{C}\setminus f(\mathbb{C}^{+})
contains at least three points and if
\gamma:[0,1)\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{+}
is a curve such that
\lim_{t\rightarrow 1^{-}}\gamma(t)=\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and the limit
L=\lim_{t\rightarrow 1^{-}}f(\gamma(t))
exists in the
extended complex plane

\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
, then the non-tangential limit
\lim_{z\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}\alpha}f(z)
exists in
\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
and
\lim_{z\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}\alpha}f(z)=L
. In particular, we have

\lim_{z\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}\alpha}f(z)=f^{*}(\alpha),

provided that any of the two limits exists in
\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
.

The Julia-Carathéodory theory for the angular derivatives is as follows (cf. Theorem 2.1 of [
References
]). Suppose that
f
is an analytic self-map of
\mathbb{C}^{+}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. One has

c=\liminf_{z\rightarrow\alpha}\frac{\Im f(z)}{\Im z}<+\infty

if and only if the angular derivative
f^{\prime}(\alpha)
exists; in which case we have
c>0
,
f^{*}(\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}
, and

(2.3)

f^{\prime}(\alpha)=c.

Alternatively, assuming
f^{*}(\alpha)\in\mathbb{R}
, one has
c=+\infty
if and only if

\lim_{z\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}\alpha}\left|\frac{f(z)-f^{*}(\alpha)}{z-\alpha}\right|=+\infty,

and we write
f^{\prime}(\alpha)=+\infty
in this case.

The boundary behavior of
F
-transform is closely related to the regularity of the underlying measure. We now review the relevant results from the books [
References
] and [
References
].
First, recall that a
support
of a Borel measure
\mu
is a Borel set whose complement is a
\mu
-measure zero set. The
topological support

\text{supp}(\mu)
is the intersection of all closed sets that support the measure
\mu
.

Let
\mu=\mu_{\text{ac}}+\mu_{\text{s}}
be the Lebesgue decomposition of
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}}
. The singular part
\mu_{\text{s}}
is supported on the
\lambda
-measure zero set

\{s\in\mathbb{R}:(-\Im G_{\mu})^{*}(s)=+\infty\},

and the atoms of
\mu
can be detected through the non-tangential limit [
References
]:

(2.4)

\lim_{z\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}s}(z-s)G_{\mu}(z)=\mu(\{s\}),\quad s\in\mathbb{R}.

So, a point
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
is an atom of
\mu
if and only if
F_{\mu}^{*}(\alpha)=0
and the angular derivative
F_{\mu}^{\prime}(\alpha)=1/\mu(\{\alpha\})<+\infty
. The absolutely continuous part
\mu_{\text{ac}}
may be given by

d\mu_{\text{ac}}(s)=\frac{1}{\pi}(-\Im G_{\mu})^{*}(s)\,d\lambda(s),

where the vertical limit function
(-\Im G_{\mu})^{*}
is well-defined almost everywhere on
\mathbb{R}
by the aforementioned result of Fatou.

We now present some useful consequences of the Julia-Carathéodory theory. Most of the results below are known already. We provide their proofs here only for reader’s convenience and for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\nu
be a non-zero Borel positive measure on
\mathbb{R}
such that

d\rho(s)=\frac{d\nu(s)}{1+s^{2}}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{R}}.

\qquad(1)

For every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we have the formula

(2.5)

\liminf_{z\rightarrow\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\nu(s)}{|z-s|^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\nu(s)}{(\alpha-s)^{2}}=\lim_{z\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\nu(s)}{|z-s|^{2}},

where these
equalities are considered in
(0,+\infty]
.

\qquad(2)

Suppose in addition that
\nu(\mathbb{R})<\infty
, and let
G_{\nu}
be its Cauchy transform. Then the common quantity in
(
2.5
) is finite at
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
if and only if the angular derivative
G_{\nu}^{\prime}(\alpha)
exists at
\alpha
. In this case we have
:

G_{\nu}^{\prime}(\alpha)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\nu(s)}{(\alpha-s)^{2}}

and

G_{\nu}(z)\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}G_{\nu}^{*}(\alpha)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\nu(s)}{\alpha-s}\quad\text{as}\quad z\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}\alpha.

\qquad(3)

Let
F(z)=a+(b+\rho(\mathbb{R}))z-(1+z^{2})G_{\rho}(z)
be a Nevanlinna form, and recall that
d\nu(s)=(1+s^{2})\,d\rho(s)
. Then (
2.5
) has a finite value at

\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
if and only if the angular derivative
F^{\prime}(\alpha)
exists at
\alpha
. In this case we have:

F(z)\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}F^{*}(\alpha)=a+b\alpha+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+s\alpha}{s-\alpha}\,d\rho(s)\quad\text{as}\quad z\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}\alpha,

and

F^{\prime}(\alpha)=b+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+s^{2}}{(s-\alpha)^{2}}\,d\rho(s).

Proof.

By replacing the measure
d\nu(s)
with
d\nu(s+\alpha)
, we may and do assume
\alpha=0
throughout the proof. We first prove (
2.5
). Fatou’s Lemma and the monotone convergence theorem show that

c=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\nu(s)}{s^{2}}\leq\liminf_{z\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\nu(s)}{|z-s|^{2}}\leq\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\nu(s)}{|i\epsilon-s|^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\nu(s)}{s^{2}},

from which we obtain the first equality in (
2.5
). The second equality in (
2.5
) holds if
c=+\infty
, because

\liminf_{z\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\nu(s)}{|z-s|^{2}}\leq\lim_{z\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\nu(s)}{|z-s|^{2}}.

If
c<\infty
, then for any

s\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
with

|x|\leq\delta y
for some
\delta>0
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{|z-s|^{2}}-\frac{1}{s^{2}}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s^{2}}\frac{|2(s-x)x+x^{2}-y^{2}|}{(x-s)^{2}+y^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{s^{2}}\left(\frac{2\delta|x-s|y}{(x-s)^{2}+y^{2}}+\frac{x^{2}+y^{2}}{y^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{s^{2}}\left(\delta+\delta^{2}+1\right)\in L^{1}(\nu).

The dominated convergence theorem then yields

\lim_{z\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\nu(s)}{|z-s|^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\nu(s)}{s^{2}},

finishing the proof of (
2.5
).

Next, we prove (2). Let
c\in(0,+\infty]
denote the common value of (
2.5
). In the case of
c<+\infty
, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality implies that the map
s\mapsto 1/s
belongs to
L^{1}(\nu)
. Then the identity

G_{\nu}(i\epsilon)+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\nu(s)}{s}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{i\epsilon}{(i\epsilon-s)s}\,d\nu(s)

and the dominated convergence theorem yield

G_{\nu}^{*}(0)=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\nu(s)}{s}.

Since

\frac{-\Im G_{\nu}(z)}{\Im z}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\nu(s)}{|z-s|^{2}},

the assumption
c<+\infty
and (
2.3
) imply
-G_{\nu}^{\prime}(0)=c
. Moreover, as
z\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}0
, say,
|\Re z|\leq\delta\Im z
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\Re G_{\nu}(z)-G_{\nu}^{*}(0)}{\Im G_{\nu}(z)}\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{|z|}{\Im z}\left|\frac{\Re G_{\nu}(z)-G_{\nu}^{*}(0)}{z}\right|\frac{\Im z}{-\Im G_{\nu}(z)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1+\delta^{2}}\left|\frac{G_{\nu}(z)-G_{\nu}^{*}(0)}{z}\right|\frac{2}{c}

\displaystyle\rightarrow 2\sqrt{1+\delta^{2}}.

Therefore, the convergence
G_{\nu}(z)\rightarrow G_{\nu}^{*}(0)
must be non-tangential as
z\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}0
. Conversely, if
G_{\nu}^{\prime}(0)
exists, then we have
c=-G_{\nu}^{\prime}(0)<+\infty
by (
2.3
). As seen earlier, the integral formula of
G_{\nu}^{*}(0)
follows immediately. The proof of (2) is completed.

The proof of (3) follows from the same arguments as in the proof of (2). We omit the details.
∎

Proposition 2.2
.

Given a map
f:\mathbb{C}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
, let
\{\gamma(t):t\in[0,1)\}\subset\mathbb{C}^{+}
be a curve such that
\gamma(t)\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
f(\gamma(t))\subset\mathbb{C}^{+}
as
t\rightarrow 1^{-}
. If

\lim_{t\rightarrow 1^{-}}\frac{f(\gamma(t))-L}{\gamma(t)-\alpha}>0

for some
L\in\mathbb{R}
, then
f(\gamma(t))\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}L
as
t\rightarrow 1^{-}
.

The next result describes the atoms of a measure.

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}}
be a nondegenerate measure whose
F
-representing measure is
\rho_{\nu}
. Given
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, we define

I_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+s^{2}}{(\alpha-s)^{2}}\,d\rho_{\nu}(s)\quad\text{and}\quad I_{2}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{d\nu(s)}{(\alpha-s)^{2}}.

\qquad(a)

\nu(\{\alpha\})>0
if and only if
F_{\nu}^{*}(\alpha)=0
and
I_{1}<+\infty
,
and in this case we have
(1+I_{1})\nu(\{\alpha\})=1
.

\qquad(b)

\rho_{\nu}(\{\alpha\})>0
if and only if
G_{\nu}^{*}(\alpha)=0
and
I_{2}<+\infty
,
in which case we have
I_{2}(1+\alpha^{2})\rho_{\nu}(\{\alpha\})=1
.

Proof.

Part (a) follows directly from (
2.4
) and Proposition
2.1
(3). We show (b). First, we write the Nevanlinna form

F_{\nu}(z)=\Re F_{\nu}(i)+(1+\rho_{\nu}(\mathbb{R}))z-(1+z^{2})G_{\rho_{\nu}}(z),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

If
\rho_{\nu}(\{\alpha\})>0
, then (
2.4
) shows that

\lim_{z\rightarrow_{\sphericalangle}\alpha}\frac{G_{\nu}(z)}{z-\alpha}=\frac{-1}{(1+\alpha^{2})\rho_{\nu}(\{\alpha\})}.

Hence, the derivative
G_{\nu}^{\prime}(\alpha)
exists and
G_{\nu}^{*}(\alpha)=0
. Proposition
2.1
(2) then shows that
I_{2}<+\infty
and
(1+\alpha^{2})\rho_{\nu}(\{\alpha\})=1/I_{2}
. The converse follows from the same consideration.
∎

2.4.
Analytic continuation

Throughout the paper, if a map
f
defined on
\mathbb{C}^{+}
can be extended continuously or analytically to points on
\mathbb{R}
, we will use the same notation
f
for the extension. The following result of Greenstein [
References
] gives the conditions under which an analytic map on
\mathbb{C}^{+}
can be continued analytically from
\mathbb{C}^{+}
into a subset of
\mathbb{C}^{-}
through a finite interval on
\mathbb{R}
.

Proposition 2.4

(Greenstein)
.

Let
a<b
be two real numbers.

(1)

Let
\rho
be the
F
-representing measure of an analytic map

F:\mathbb{C}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{+}\cup\mathbb{R}
. The map
F
extends analytically across the interval
(a,b)
into

(2)

In the next result, an atom

Corollary 2.5

Let

and

be so that

(

)

extends analytically to an open disk centered at

(2)

extends analytically to a punctured disk centered at

and the point

is a simple pole
of this extension.

In

, the point

has the residue

at

Proof.

Let

. The case of

Suppose first that the density

Conversely, assume that

extends analytically to

Let

Since

∎

3.1.

Fix

Proposition 3.1

(Global Inversion).

\qquad(1)

Example 3.4

(Regularity of
\boxplus
-infinitely divisible laws)

Given a nondegenerate

with free Lévy-Hinčin parameters

H(z)=\gamma_{\mu}+z+\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+sz}{z-s}\;d\sigma_{\mu}(s).

In this case we have
\omega=F_{\mu}
on
\mathbb{C}^{+}

\omega
serves as a continuous and injective extension of

to
\mathbb{C}^{+}\cup\mathbb{R}

(6). The image

\mathbb{R}

\mu
has a zero singularly continuous part and at most one atom, a fact that is already known in [
References
]. The existence of

amounts to

or, equivalently,
g(0)\leq 1
. By Proposition
3.2
, this means that the angular derivative

exists and is equal to
1-g(0)
. We also have a formula for

s_{\mu}=H(F_{\mu}(s_{\mu}))=H(0)=\gamma_{\mu}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{s}\,d\sigma_{\mu}(s).

Assume that

exists in
(0,+\infty)

3.2
shows that
F_{\mu}^{\prime}(s_{\mu})=1/H^{\prime}(0)

\mu(\{s_{\mu}\})=1-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+s^{2}}{s^{2}}\;d\sigma_{\mu}(s).

Conversely, if

exists and is non-zero. In summary, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

The zero

(ii)

and the angular derivative

(iii)

(See [Proposition 5.1,
References
] for a proof based on the results of [
References
].) If the point

does not exist, then

has no atomic part. The absolutely continuous part

d\mu_{\text{ac}}(s)=\frac{(-\Im G_{\mu})^{*}(s)}{\pi}\,d\lambda(s)=\frac{\Im F_{\mu}(s)}{\pi|F_{\mu}(s)|^{2}}\,d\lambda(s),\quad s\neq s_{\mu}.

Taking the push-forward of the measures

and

(Here we have used the inversion relationship
F_{\mu}(h(x))=x+if(x)
for

In particular, by writing the open set

as a countable disjoint union of open intervals, the support of
\mu
is a countable disjoint union of closed intervals; one of which is the degenerate interval

if the point

exists and is an atom lying outside of

if

has a version

defined by the formula:

p_{\mu}(h(x))=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{f(x)}{x^{2}+f(x)^{2}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}

and

. The density

is continuous on

and is positive on the open dense subset

yields the analyticity of

at any point

if and only if

is positive and analytic everywhere on

means

is uniformly bounded on
\mathbb{R}
if

∎

We single out the conclusion about the analyticity of the density

We now discuss the regularity of

Example 3.8
.

Recall that the set

S=\left\{s\in\mathbb{R}:(-\Im G_{\mu\boxplus\nu})^{*}(s)=+\infty\right\}

supports the singular part
(\mu\boxplus\nu)_{\text{s}}
. Let
(\mu\boxplus\nu)_{\mathrm{sc}}
denote the singularly continuous part of
\mu\boxplus\nu
. To each

, consider

, where
\omega
is
the global inverse of

. It is easy to see that the map
\omega
satisfies the subordination identity
G_{\mu\boxplus\nu}=G_{\nu}\circ\omega
in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
. This implies that the point
\alpha
cannot belong to
\mathbb{C}^{+}
; for if it does we would have

\displaystyle-\Im G_{\nu}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Im\left[G_{\nu}(\omega(s+i\epsilon))\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Im G_{\mu\boxplus\nu}(s+i\epsilon)=(-\Im G_{\mu\boxplus\nu})^{*}(s)=+\infty,

a contradiction.
So we must have
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Since

\left|F_{\nu}(\omega(s+i\epsilon))\right|=\frac{1}{\left|G_{\mu\boxplus\nu}(s+i\epsilon)\right|}\leq\frac{1}{-\Im G_{\mu\boxplus\nu}(s+i\epsilon)}\rightarrow 0\quad(\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}),

the map

has limit zero along the curve

and hence

must be a boundary point of type

\omega
is injective, the set

must be a finite set. Because a singularly continuous measure does not charge
any finite set, we conclude that
(\mu\boxplus\nu)_{\text{sc}}(S)=0
, proving that
(\mu\boxplus\nu)_{\text{sc}}
is in fact the zero measure.
∎

3.2.
Property (H)

Recall that a measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}}
has

\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{R}}
, the measure
\mu\boxplus\nu
is absolutely continuous with a positive and analytic density everywhere on
\mathbb{R}

Example 3.12

(Boolean stable laws)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{ID}(\boxplus)
be a Boolean stable law in the sense that for any constant
c>0
, there exist constants
c_{1}>0
and
c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

F_{\mu}(z)+cF_{\mu}(z/c)=z+c_{1}F_{\mu}((z-c_{2})/c_{1}),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}.

It
was shown in [
References
] that the
F
-transform of
\mu
has the form

F_{\mu}(z)=z+e^{i\pi ab}z^{1-a},\quad z\in\mathbb{C}^{+},

where (i)

and
0\leq b\leq 1

1/2<a\leq 2/3
and

2a-1\leq ab\leq 1-a
, or (iii)

and

\mu

in the last case the Cauchy distribution, and it is known that

for all

on
\mathbb{R}
. In case
(i) with

, the continuity of
F_{\mu}
on
\mathbb{C}^{+}\cup\mathbb{R}
yields
F_{\mu}(0)=\lim_{x\rightarrow 0^{+}}(x+e^{i\pi ab}x^{1-a})=0
,
F_{\mu}(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\})\subset\mathbb{C}^{+}
, and the angular derivative
F_{\mu}^{\prime}(0)=+\infty
by the explicit formula of
F_{\mu}
. It follows from Example
3.4
that
\mu
is atomless and

\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+s^{2})(x-s)^{-2}\,d\sigma_{\mu}(s)>1,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}.

The last inequality says that the set
B=\emptyset
.
Since
\mu(\{0\})=0
, we have
A=\emptyset
. Note that

y\Im[F_{\mu}(iy)-iy]=y\Im[e^{i\pi ab}(iy)^{1-a}]\to+\infty

in its Nevanlinna form with the representing measure

, which means that

. Thus, the set

is also empty. Theorem

then implies

argument shows that the same conclusion holds for all

and

\mu
considered above is positive and analytic

∎

The preceding example shows that the conditions for property (H) are hidden in the inequalities of Theorem

Theorem 3.13

Let

-\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{\Im\varphi_{\mu}(x+i\epsilon)}{\epsilon}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+s^{2}}{(x-s)^{2}}\,d\sigma_{\mu}(s)>1,\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

Proposition
2.4
implies a qualitative version of the preceding result.

Corollary 3.14
.

Given a nondegenerate measure
\mu\in\mathcal{ID}(\boxplus)

\qquad(1)

The measure
\mu
has property
(\mathrm{H})
.

\qquad(2)

The measure
\mu
has a positive density everywhere on

\mathbb{R}
and
m_{2}(\mu)=+\infty

\qquad(3)

The function
G_{\mu}

extends analytically to
\mathbb{R}
,

for all

\qquad(4)

The function

extends analytically to
\mathbb{R}

for all

, and
m_{2}(\rho_{\mu})=+\infty

Moreover, if

The next result follows easily from the fact

Corollary 3.15

A
\boxplus

Example 3.16

(Property (H) in domains of attraction)
.

Interestingly, all
\boxplus
-stable processes except the asymmetric ones and the free Brownian motion have property (H). This is because the Voiculescu transform
\varphi_{\nu_{a,\theta}}
of a freely stable law

is parameterized by its stability index

and the asymmetric coefficient
\theta\in[-1,1]
, and such an
\varphi_{\nu_{a,\theta}}
must belong to the following list up to dilation and translation [
References

(i)

\varphi_{\nu_{a,\theta}}(z)=-\left[i+\theta\tan(a\pi/2)\right]i^{a-1}z^{1-a}
when

(ii)

\varphi_{\nu_{a,\theta}}(z)=2\theta\log z-i\pi(1+\theta)
when
a=1
;

(iii)

\varphi_{\nu_{a,\theta}}(z)=1/z
when
a=2
.

Therefore, according to Theorem
3.13
and Corollary
3.15

and

\nu_{a,\theta}
and the
\boxplus
-semigroup generated by
\nu_{a,\theta}
both have property (H) from these explicit formulae. The next two examples show that the property (H) is not a topological property under weak convergence.

\qquad(1)

Let
\sigma_{\mu}
be an absolutely continuous measure defined by the density
f(s)=1
for
s\in[-1,1]
and
f(s)=|s|^{-3}
for
|s|>1
and let
\gamma_{\mu}=0
. The tail-sums of
\sigma_{\mu}
satisfy
\sigma_{\mu}\left(\{s\in\mathbb{R}:|s|>y\}\right)=y^{-2}
for

, so that
m_{2}(\sigma_{\mu})=+\infty
and the measures

\mu_{n}=D_{1/\sqrt{n\log n}}\,\mu^{\boxplus n}

converge weakly to the standard semicircular law
\nu_{2,0}
by the free central limit theorem [
References

\nu_{2,0}
does not have property (H), the attracted measure
\mu
does, because
m_{2}(\mu)=+\infty

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+s^{2}}{(x-s)^{2}}\,d\sigma_{\mu}(s)\geq f(|[x]|+1)\int_{[[x],[x]+1)}\frac{d\lambda(s)}{(x-s)^{2}}=+\infty

has property (H).

\qquad(2)

If
\nu_{a,\theta}
is a stable law with property
(H), then its domain
of attraction always contains an
\boxplus
-infinitely divisible law which does not have property (H). To show this, fix

be large enough so that

and
(2-a)m>1
. Choose the numbers
p=(1-\theta)/2
,

,

,

\gamma_{\mu}=0
. Define the Lévy measure
\sigma_{\mu}=\sigma_{\mu}^{+}+\sigma_{\mu}^{-}

by specifying

d\sigma_{\mu}^{+}(s)=pc\,s^{-(a+1)}\,d\lambda(s),\quad 1\leq s<+\infty,

and

d\sigma_{\mu}^{-}(s)=(q/p)\sum\nolimits_{k=1}^{\infty}\sigma_{\mu}^{+}\big([k^{m},(k+1)^{m})\big)\,\delta_{-k^{m}}.

Then the tail-sum
\sigma_{\mu}(\left\{s\in\mathbb{R}:|s|>y\right\})

is a regularly varying function of index
-a
. By the results
in [
References
], there are constants
a_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
and
b_{n}>0

such that
b_{n}\rightarrow 0
and the measures

\mu_{n}=D_{b_{n}}\mu^{\boxplus n}\boxplus\delta_{a_{n}}

converge weakly to the stable law

as

We also have
b_{n}=n^{-1/a}\ell(n)
where
\ell(n)
is a slowly
varying sequence in
\mathbb{N}
. Note that the Lévy measure of
\mu_{n}\in\mathcal{ID}(\boxplus)

is given by

d\sigma_{\mu_{n}}(s)=n\,\frac{b_{n}^{2}+s^{2}}{1+s^{2}}\,d\sigma_{\mu}(s/b_{n}),

so we have

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{1+s^{2}}{(x-s)^{2}}\,d\sigma_{\mu_{n}}(s)

\displaystyle=nb_{n}^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{1+s^{2}}{(x-b_{n}s)^{2}}\;d\sigma_{\mu}^{-}(s)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{-1}\frac{2ns^{2}}{(x/b_{n}-s)^{2}}\,d\sigma_{\mu}^{-}(s).

Consider
x_{n}=-b_{n}[(n+1)^{m}+n^{m}]/2
and
\widetilde{x}_{n}=x_{n}/b_{n}
for

. Since

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mu}^{+}([k^{m},(k+1)^{m}))

\displaystyle=pcm\int_{k}^{k+1}s^{-(am+1)}\;d\lambda(s)

\displaystyle\leq pcmk^{-(am+1)},\;\;\;\;\;k\in\mathbb{N},

and

\displaystyle\int_{[-n^{m},-1]}\frac{ns^{2}\,d\sigma_{\mu}^{-}(s)}{(\widetilde{x}_{n}-s)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{nq}{p}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{k^{2m}\sigma_{\mu}^{+}([k^{m},(k+1)^{m}))}{(\widetilde{x}_{n}+k^{m})^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4qc}{m\cdot n^{2m-3}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}k^{(2-a)m-1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4qc}{m\cdot n^{2m-3}}\int_{1}^{n+1}s^{(2-a)m-1}\;d\lambda(s)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{(2-a)m+2}qc}{(2-a)m^{2}}\cdot\frac{1}{n^{am-3}}.

The inequalities

then imply that

\displaystyle\int_{(-\infty,-n^{m})}\frac{ns^{2}\,d\sigma_{\mu}^{-}(s)}{(\widetilde{x}_{n}-s)^{2}}

\displaystyle=n\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{k^{m}}{\widetilde{x}_{n}+k^{m}}\right)^{2}\sigma_{\mu}^{-}(\{-k^{m}\})

\displaystyle\leq 16n^{3}\sigma_{\mu}^{-}((-\infty,-n^{m}))=\frac{16\widetilde{\ell}(n^{m})}{n^{am-3}}

for some slowly varying function
\widetilde{\ell}
. These findings yield that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{1+s^{2}}{(x_{n}-s)^{2}}\,d\sigma_{\mu_{n}}(s)=0.

On the other hand, the fact that

|x_{n}|\geq n^{m}b_{n}=n^{m-1/a}\ell(n)\geq n

for all sufficiently large

and Proposition 5.8 in [

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1+s^{2}}{(x_{n}-s)^{2}}\,d\sigma_{\mu_{n}}(s)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle nb_{n}^{2}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{1+s^{2}}{(x_{n}-b_{n}s)^{2}}\,d\sigma_{\mu}^{+}(s)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2nb_{n}^{2}\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{s^{2}}{n^{2}+(b_{n}s)^{2}}\,d\sigma_{\mu}^{+}(s)

\displaystyle\frac{a\pi}{\sin(a\pi/2)}n\sigma_{\mu}^{+}((nb_{n}^{-1},+\infty))

\displaystyle\frac{\pi pc}{\sin(a\pi/2)}\left[\frac{\ell(n)}{n}\right]^{a}\rightarrow 0

and the measure

3.3.

We now examine the analyticity
of the density

Proposition 3.18
.

The density
p_{\mu\boxplus\nu}
is not analytic at the zero

s_{0}
in the following two situations:

\qquad(1)

There is a sequence

such that

for all

and

\qquad(2)

The angular

.

Thus, we shall assume that

is an isolated zero of

and
\omega^{\prime}(s_{0})<+\infty
from now on. (The latter finiteness condition means that a strict inequality occurs in Theorem

Lemma 4.1
.

Let

Proof
of
\mathbf{Proposition\;\;\ref{Hprop1}}
: We begin with the properties of the map

. The definition of

implies that

\mathcal{I}_{x}(f(x))=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+s^{2}}{(x-s)^{2}+f(x)^{2}}\;d\rho(s)\leq b,\;\;\;\;\;x\in\mathbb{R}.

Note that the equality in (

) holds whenever
f(x)>0
. Indeed, if
f(x)>0
and yet
\mathcal{I}_{x}(f(x))<b
, then there is a
\delta>0
such that
\mathcal{I}_{x}(f(x))<b-\delta<b\leq\mathcal{I}_{x}(f(x)/2)

.

The preceding observation and the strict monotonicity of

imply that if

then

b=\mathcal{I}_{x}(f(x))<\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1+s^{2}}{(x-s)^{2}}\,d\rho(s)=g(x).

Conversely,
f(x)=0
implies
\mathcal{I}_{x}(y)<b
for all

g(x)=\sup_{y>0}\mathcal{I}_{x}(y)\leq b
. So, we have
V=\{x\in\mathbb{R}:g(x)>b\}
.

We already have
\Omega\supset\{x+iy\in\mathbb{C}^{+}:y>f(x)\}
, because the set
\Omega
contains the vertical array
\{z+iy:y>0\}
for any

. Conversely, if
x+iy\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{+}
and
f(x)>0
then
\mathcal{I}_{x}(y)<b=\mathcal{I}_{x}(f(x))
, showing that
f(x)<y
. It follows that
\Omega=\{x+iy\in\mathbb{C}^{+}:y>f(x)\}
.

Being a supremum of continuous maps,
g
is lower semi-continuous. We now verify the continuity of
f
. Consider the function
F(x,y)=\mathcal{I}_{x}(y)-b
on
\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty)

is continuous at

which is clearly a contradiction. Hence

The continuity of

Indeed, notice first that

\mathcal{I}_{x}(f(x)+\epsilon)<b<\mathcal{I}_{x}(f(x)-\epsilon),\quad x\in V,\;\epsilon\in(0,f(x)),

Conversely, every boundary point

Another consequence of the continuity of
