The large and moderate deviations approach in geometric functional analysis
Joscha Prochno

Abstract
The work of Gantert, Kim, and Ramanan [Large deviations for random projections of ℓ p \ell^{p} balls, Ann. Probab. 45 (6B), 2017] has initiated and inspired a new direction of research in the asymptotic theory of geometric functional analysis. The moderate deviations perspective, describing the asymptotic behavior between the scale of a central limit theorem and a large deviations principle, was later added by Kabluchko, Prochno, and Thäle in [High-dimensional limit theorems for random vectors in ℓ p n \ell_{p}^{n} balls. II, Commun. Contemp. Math. 23(3), 2021]. These two approaches nicely complement the classical study of central limit phenomena or non-asymptotic concentration bounds for high-dimensional random geometric quantities. Beyond studying large and moderate deviations principles for random geometric quantities that appear in geometric functional analysis, other ideas emerged from the theory of large deviations and the closely related field of statistical mechanics, and have provided new insight and become a source for new developments. Within less than a decade, a variety of results have appeared and formed this direction of research. Among others, a connection to the famous Kannan–Lovász–Simonovits conjecture and the study of moderate and large deviations for isotropic log-concave random vectors was discovered. In this manuscript, we introduce the basic principles, survey the work that has been done, and aim to manifest this direction of research, at the same time making it more accessible to a wider community of researchers. Keywords . Cramér’s theorem, Gibbs measure, moderate deviations principle, large deviations principle, Orlicz ball, random projection, Sanov’s theorem, Schatten class MSC . Introduction

Already the early years in the local theory of Banach spaces and geometric functional analysis have demonstrated a deep connection between the geometry of high-dimensional normed spaces, and thus of high-dimensional convex bodies, and probability theory. This fruitful interplay has already been nicely displayed in the wonderful and influential monographs
Asymptotic theory of finite-dimen-sional normed spaces
by V. D. Milman and G. Schechtman

Even though high-dimensional structures and randomness seem inevitably to lead to the existence of more or too many possibilities, resulting in an increased diversity and complexity as the dimension increases, the past decades have shown that this is often compensated and even patterns are created and concentration phenomena evoked, its source being convexity. Along the way, powerful methods have been developed by a large community of gifted mathematicians, both on the geometric and analytic, as well as on the probabilistic side. In fact, new fields at the crossroads between functional analysis, discrete and convex geometry, and probability theory have emerged, today known as asymptotic geometric analysis (also asymptotic convex geometry) and high-dimensional probability theory. Due to their common origin both fields overlap in many different ways, while still leaning towards the mathematical disciplines the names already indicate. The methodical diversity, drawing on ideas from analysis, geometry, and probability, is a common characteristic for both. Today the methods have become indispensable in numerous problems of (multivariate) statistics, computer science, theoretical numerical analysis, or compressed sensing, and quite often research activities are driven by questions that arose in the applied sciences, for instance, the famous Kannan–Lovász–Simonovits conjecture

1.1
The large and moderate deviations perspective

The beautiful universality that emerges from the central limit perspective unfortunately restricts the information that may be retrieved, for instance, from lower-dimensional projections of a high-dimensional convex body. But which aspects of, for instance, random projections can be used to distinguish between different convex bodies?

In order to answer this question, let us recall that a sequence
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies a large deviations principle (LDP) with lower-semicontinuous rate function
\rate
and speed
s_{n}\uparrow\infty
if and only if for all Borel sets
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathbb{P}[X_{n}\in A]\approx e^{-s_{n}\inf_{x\in A}\rate(x)}.

A moderate deviations principle (MDP) formally resembles an LDP but on a different scale and, typically, with significant differences in the behavior. More precisely, the scale for an MDP of a random quantity is between that of a weak limit theorem (like a central limit theorem) and that of a law of large numbers.
It is instructive to consider the case of independent and identically distributed (iid)
\mathbb{R}
-valued random variables
X_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, with finite exponential moments for which Cramér’s theorem
[
28
, Theorem I.4]
guarantees that, for all
a>\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}\Big[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{n}\geq a\Big]=-\rate(a),

with
\rate
being the Legendre transform of the log-moment generating function of
X_{1}
. While this LDP occurs at scale
n
of a law of large numbers, as seen from the expression
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
, an MDP occurs (under suitable conditions, e.g.,
[
31
]
) on a scale between
\sqrt{n}
and
n
, more precisely,
\frac{1}{b_{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
with
b_{n}/n\to 0
and
b_{n}/\sqrt{n}\to\infty
.

In contrast to a central limit theorem, large and moderate deviations principles are known to be typically non-universal and distribution-dependent, more precisely, they are parametric in speed and/or rate function; MDPs are usually less parametric in the rate (up to a variance dependence that may appear).
In this sense, the large and moderate deviations behavior of, for instance, a random projection of a convex body, depends in a subtle way on the geometry of the underlying body. In other words, LDPs and MDPs allow one to distinguish high-dimensional probability measures, like the uniform distributions on a high-dimensional convex body, via their lower-dimensional projections. We shall describe below the
development of this large and moderate deviations approach in the asymptotic theory of geometric functional analysis, but also the influence of large deviations (and in particular statistical mechanics) ideas and techniques, before we move on with a short introduction to large deviations theory and a more detailed presentation and discussion of some of the results presented now.

The contributions we have seen and the progress that has been made in the past seven years started with an inspiring work of N. Gantert, S. S. Kim, and K. Ramanan
[
39
]
who studied the question about the large deviations behavior of one-dimensional marginals of (scaled)
\ell_{p}^{d}
-balls defined as
{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{d}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\|x\|_{p}\leq 1\}
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, both in an annealed (i.e., marginal in a random direction) and quenched (i.e., marginal in a fixed deterministic direction) setting. In the annealed setting, their work reveals a stark difference in the behavior both speed- and rate-wise, depending on whether
1\leq p\leq 2
or
2<p\leq\infty
. The authors also identify a variational formula that relates the annealed and quenched LDPs.

This is where D. Alonso-Gutiérrez, J. Prochno, and C.Thäle started, looking at higher-dimensional marginals, studying LDPs for the Euclidean norm of random orthogonal
k_{d}
-dimensional projections of random vectors uniformly distributed on
\ell_{p}^{d}
-balls; here
k_{d}\in\mathbb{N}
may tend to infinity as
d\to\infty
. The observations are two-fold. On the one hand, we see again a significantly different large deviations behavior, depending on whether
1\leq p\leq 2
or
2<p\leq\infty
. On the other hand, the form of the rate function that governs the LDP depends on the asymptotic behavior of the random subspace dimensions
k_{d}
relative to
d
. One should particularly notice that for
1\leq p<2
, both the speed and the rate function differ from those for the
1
-dimensional random projections in
[
39
]
. Notably, when
p=2
the LDP does not feel the dimensions
k_{d}
of the random subspaces as long as they do not increase too fast with
d
and we obtain the same LDP as in
[
39
]
. The difference to the
1
-dimensional projections becomes visible only in the truly high-dimensional regime in which
k_{d}
is eventually proportional to the dimension
d
of the ambient space. When
2<p\leq\infty
the LDP occurs at the same speed as in
[
39
]
, but with a different rate function.

The case of general
\ell_{q}
-norms of random vectors uniformly distributed on
{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{d}
, i.e., without involving any projections, was studied by Z. Kabluchko, J. Prochno, and C. Thäle in
[
63
]
. The LDPs obtained in
[
63
]
significantly differ depending on the position of
p
relative to
q

q\leq p
, the speed is
d
and the rate function feels the radial part of the uniform distribution on
{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{d}
, while a lack of exponential moments makes the analysis for
q>p
more delicate, resulting in a slower speed
d^{p/q}
and a non-convex rate function. In particular, the LDP in this case is not susceptible to the radial component. In the follow-up paper
[
67
]
, the same authors studied
\ell_{q}
-norms of random vectors with distributions from a more general class related to
\ell_{p}
-balls. In particular, they considered MDPs for those random quantities in the case where
q<p
. A somehow related work, where the random points are chosen uniformly at random in a centered regular simplex in high dimensions, is
[
11
]
. There the authors obtained Berry–Esseen bounds in the regime
1\leq q<\infty
, complemented those by a non-central limit theorem together with moderate and large deviations in the case where
q=\infty
, and compared those results to the previously mentioned ones for the crosspolytope
{\mathbb{B}}_{1}^{d}
. We also refer to the work
[
47
]
of J. Grote, Z. Kabluchko, and C. Thäle for several types of limit theorems, including MDPs and LDPs, for high-dimensional random simplices.

The results mentioned so far are often referred to as level–1 large deviations. The level–2 large deviations or Sanov-type large deviations concern LDPs for random empirical measures. In
[
74
]
, S. S. Kim and K. Ramanan studied conditional limit theorems for
\ell_{p}
-spheres in the flavor of the classical and famous Poincaré–Maxwell–Borel Lemma (see
[
21
]
or
[
29
]
). Central to their analysis is an LDP that the authors establish for the sequence of empirical measures of the coordinates of a random vector uniformly distributed on the sphere
{\mathbb{S}}_{p}^{d-1}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\|x\|_{p}=1\}
(both, random vector and sphere are scaled with a factor
d^{1/p}
). Notably, the LDP not only holds in the weak topology (on the space of probability measures), but even in the stronger
q
-Wasserstein topology as long as
q<p
. A nice combination with another element of large deviations theory, the Gibbs conditioning principle, allows them to obtain the conditional limit theorem.

Let us briefly stop here and explain a central tool in all the work presented so far, which will then also serve as motivation for the research discussed in a moment. In the world of
\ell_{p}^{d}
-balls, there exists an inconspicuous
probabilistic representation of the uniform measure on
{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{d}
, relating it for any
1\leq p<\infty
to the so-called
p
-Gaussian distribution with Lebesgue density of the form

\mathbb{R}\ni x\mapsto C_{p}e^{-|x|^{p}/p},

where
C_{p}\in(0,\infty)
is a normalization constant.
More precisely, the work of G. Schechtman and J. Zinn
[
107
]
(see also the independent work
[
96
]
by S. T. Rachev and L. Rüschendorf) shows that if
X
is uniformly distributed on
{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{d}
, and
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d}
are independent
p
-generalized Gaussian random variables, then

X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{d}}}{{=}}U^{1/d}\frac{(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d})}{\|(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d})\|_{p}},

where
U
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
and independent of
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d}
. In view of a probabilistic approach to the geometry of
\ell_{p}^{d}
-balls, this representation is quite powerful, because it allows to make a transition from a random vector with dependent coordinates (like
X
) to one with independent coordinates (like
(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d})
); this makes many problems computationally feasible. Behind any of the large and moderate deviations results for
\ell_{p}^{d}
-balls stands this Schechtman–Zinn probabilistic representation, also in
[
39
]
.

Motivated by the work in
[
74
]
, Z. Kabluchko, J. Prochno, and C. Thäle investigated Sanov-type LDPs in the non-commutative setting of Schatten classes
[
66
]
, where no such Schechtman–Zinn representation had been available. We recall that the Schatten
p
-class is the space of
d\times d
real matrices where the norm of a matrix
A
is given by the
\ell_{p}^{d}
-norm of the vector of singular values of
A
. The authors established a non-commutative version of the Schechtman–Zinn representation and proved an LDP for the (properly scaled) empirical spectral measure of a matrix chosen uniformly at random from a unit ball in the Schatten
p
-class or from the boundary with respect to the cone measure (on the space of probability measures equipped with the weak topology). As a consequence they also obtained that the spectral measure converges weakly almost surely to a non-random limiting measure given by the Ullman distribution. It turns out that in this non-commutative setting, the analysis is considerably more delicate. This paper was the first going beyond the setting of
\ell_{p}
-balls. Moreover, the ideas and results developed in
[
66
]
and the related works
[
64
,
65
]
(see also
[
108
]
), which largely stem from large deviations theory and statistical mechanics, where crucial in
[
26
]
to establish a generalized version of the variance conjecture for the family of Schatten
p
-class unit balls of self-adjoint matrices.

Another work dealing at least in parts with the non-commutative setting is
[
72
]
. T. Kaufmann and C. Thäle derive a probabilistic representation for classes of weighted
p
-radial distributions, which, in essence, are based on mixtures of a weighted cone probability measure and a weighted uniform distribution on
{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{d}
. The authors then prove LDPs for the empirical measure of the coordinates of vectors chosen at random from
{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{d}
with respect to distributions from the weighted measure class. Those distributions are then extended to
p
-balls in classical matrix spaces, both for self-adjoint and non-self-adjoint matrices, and their eigenvalue and singular value distributions are identified for the respective random matrices. As an application, LDPs for the empirical spectral measures for the eigenvalues and the singular values are proved.

In
[
68
]
, Z. Kabluchko, J. Prochno, and C. Thäle proved a strong law of large numbers for
k
-dimensional random projections of the
d
-dimensional cube via the Artstein-Vitale law of large numbers for random compact sets. This result, which can also be derived from the celebrated Dvoretzky–Milman theorem, says that with respect to the Hausdorff distance, a uniform random projection of the
d
-dimensional cube onto
\mathbb{R}^{k}
converges almost surely to a centered
k
-dimensional Euclidean ball of radius
\sqrt{2/\pi}
, as
d\to\infty
. For every point inside this, the authors determined the asymptotic number of vertices and the volume of the part of the cube projected ‘close’ to this point. This result is a consequence of a more general large deviations result for random projections of arbitrary product measures obtained in this paper.

Related to the
d
-dimensional cube is also a work of S. Johnston, Z. Kabluchko, and J. Prochno, who studied the large deviations behavior of random one-dimensional projections of the uniform distribution on the cube
[-1,1]^{d}

[
57
]
. While a combination of Klartag’s central limit theorem for convex bodies with the Borel–Cantelli lemma implies the almost sure weak convergence to a centered Gaussian distribution of variance
1/3
, and thus shows that the typical behavior is universal, the authors establish an LDP showing that the atypical behavior is not universal. In fact, the speed of the LDP is
d
and the rate function is explicit, thus giving direct access to the dependence on the underlying distribution.

The previous results from
[
57
]
have been considerably generalized by Z. Kabluchko and J. Prochno in
[
61
]
, where LDPs for random matrices in the orthogonal group and Stiefel manifold are proved. The good convex rate functions are explicitly given in terms of certain log-determinants of trace-class operators and are finite on the set of Hilbert–Schmidt operators
M
satisfying
\|MM^{*}\|<1
. Those results are then applied to determine the precise large deviation behavior of
k
-dimensional random projections of high-dimensional product distributions using an appropriate interpretation in terms of point processes, also providing a characterizing of the space of all possible deviations. The case of uniform distributions on
{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{d}
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, is also considered and reduced to appropriate product measures.

A work partly generalizing the one of Z. Kabluchko and J. Prochno
[
61
]
is the recent paper
[
71
]
by T. Kaufmann, H. Sambale, and C. Thäle. Here the authors extend the set of projected distributions for which an LDP is shown (going from the uniform distribution to quite general
p
-radial distributions) and observe that the large deviations behavior obtained in
[
61

is universal for a large class of probability measures on
{\mathbb{B}}^{d}_{p}
. They also describe geometrically motivated distributions on the balls
{\mathbb{B}}^{d}_{p}
for which the LDP needs a suitable modification.

Another work dealing with large deviations induced by the Stiefel manifold is
[
75
]
. In this paper, S. S. Kim and K. Ramanan prove LDPs for
k
-dimensional, deterministic Stiefel projections of classes of random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
k<d

\ell_{p}^{d}
-balls with
1<p\leq\infty
and product measures with sufficiently light tails; the results hold under some convergence assumption on the empirical measures of the properly scaled Stiefel matrix. The results are then nicely applied to obtain large deviations for random projections in the quenched sense, where the Stiefel matrices are chosen at random with respect to the Haar measure on the Stiefel manifold and quenched refers to the fact that one conditions on almost sure realizations of the random Stiefel matrix. The authors also obtain a variational formula for the rate function governing the LDP for annealed projections. It is important to note that the key difference compared to
[
61
]
is in the object of study, which in
[
75
]
is the projection point itself, hence yielding an LDP on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, whereas in
[
61

it is the projected distribution on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
(and thus the main result yields an LDP on the space of probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
.

Having its origin in theoretical computer science, the Kannan–Lovász–Simonovits conjecture (see, e.g.,
[
1
]
) is arguably the most famous open problem in asymptotic geometric analysis and high-dimensional probability theory today; the currently best known result is due to B. Klartag
[
78
]
. As shown by D. Alonso-Gutiérrez, J. Prochno, and C. Thäle
[
5
]
, there is a connection between this conjecture and the study of large and moderate deviations for isotropic log-concave random vectors, therefore providing an alternative possibility to tackle the conjecture by presenting a potential route to disproving it. In their work, the authors study the moderate deviations for the Euclidean norm of random orthogonally projected random vectors in
{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{d}
, leading to a number of interesting observations, namely that the
\ell_{1}^{d}
-ball is already critical for the new approach, that for
p\geq 2
the rate function in the MDP undergoes a phase transition, depending on whether the scaling is below the square-root of the subspace dimensions or comparable, and finally that for
1\leq p<2
and comparable subspace dimensions, the rate function again displays a phase transition depending on its growth relative to
d^{p/2}
.

While the work on Sanov-type large deviations in the non-commutative setting of Schatten
p
-classes
[
66
]
was the first in which an LDP was derived without the classical Schechtman–Zinn probabilistic representation, it took a while to understand how to go beyond the framework of the (commutative) sequence spaces
\ell_{p}
. Quite recently, Z. Kabluchko and J. Prochno used a principle deeply manifested in large deviations theory and statistical mechanics, the so-called maximum entropy or Gibbs principle for non-interacting particles, to establish a probabilistic approach to the geometry of Orlicz balls
[
60
]
. More precisely, they extracted from this maximum entropy principle an asymptotic version of a probabilistic representation for the uniform distribution on an Orlicz ball. This allowed them, among other things, to compute the precise asymptotic volume of Orlicz balls (based on sharp large deviations techniques) and to determine the threshold behavior for the volume of intersections of Orlicz balls, a result extending the
\ell_{p}
-ball counterpart of G. Schechtman and M. Schmuckenschläger
[
106
]
. In particular, it became clear that the role of the Schechtman–Zinn probabilistic representation is replaced, in an asymptotic form, by appropriate Gibbs distributions whose potentials are given by Orlicz functions. Let us note that, in essence, those ideas have been used by F. Barthe and P. Wolff in
[
17
]
to show for a large family of Orlicz balls that they verify the R. Kannan, L. Lovász, and M. Simonovits spectral gap conjecture.

S. T. Rachev and L. Rüschendorf
[
96
]
and A. Naor and D. Romik
[
90
]
unified results obtained in the 1980s by P. Diaconis and D. Freedman
[
29
]
, establishing
a connection between
\ell_{p}^{d}
-balls and a
p
-generalized Gaussian distribution. In
[
59
]
, S. Johnston and J. Prochno studied similar
questions in a significantly broader setting, looking at low-dimensional projections of random
vectors uniformly distributed on sets of the form
{\mathbb{B}}_{\phi,t}^{d}:=\{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\sum_{i=1}^{d}\phi(s_{i})\leq td\}
, where

\phi:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty]
is a function satisfying some fairly mild conditions; this framework includes the simplex as well as the
\ell_{p}^{d}
-balls for
p>0
and the more general case
of Orlicz balls. Using an approach completely different from
[
96
]
and
[
90
]
, which is again based on ideas from the theory of large deviations and statistical mechanics, specifically those centered around Cramér’s theorem, the Gibbs conditioning principle, and Gibbs measures, the authors showed that there is a critical parameter
t_{\text{crit}}
at which there is a phase transition in the behavior of the low-dimensional projections. More precisely, for
t>t_{\text{crit}}
the coordinates of projected random vectors sampled from
{\mathbb{B}}_{\phi,t}^{d}
behave like independent uniform random variables, but for
t\leq t_{\text{crit}}
however the Gibbs conditioning principle comes into play, and here there is a parameter
\beta_{t}>0
(the so-called inverse temperature) such that the coordinates are approximately distributed according to a density proportional to
e^{-\beta\phi(s)}
. The general ideas of this paper have been developed in parallel with the ones put forward by Z. Kabluchko and J. Prochno in
[
60
]
.

In the work
[
2
]
, D. Alonso-Gutiérrez and J. Prochno studied the asymptotic thin-shell concentration for random vectors uniformly distributed on Orlicz balls. Their approach was inspired by the ideas developed in
[
60
]
and combined with moderate deviations techniques. Among other things, this allowed the authors to determine the precise asymptotic value of the isotropic constant of Orlicz balls and to provide both upper and lower bounds on the probability of such a random vector being in a thin shell whose radius is a constant multiple of the dimension
\sqrt{d}
. In fact, they manage to prove that in certain cases their bounds are best possible and improve upon the currently best known general bound when the deviation parameter goes down to zero as the dimension
n
of the ambient space increases.

The work
[
74
]
of S. S. Kim and K. Ramanan on Sanov-type large deviations and conditional limit theorems for
\ell_{p}
-spheres has recently been lifted to the setting of Orlicz spaces by L. Frühwirth and J. Prochno in
[
37
]
; the underlying idea using appropriate Gibbs distributions again stems from
[
60
]
. In the paper, a Sanov-type large deviations principle for the sequence of empirical measures of vectors chosen uniformly at random from an Orlicz ball is obtained and then, from this level-2 large deviations result, in a combination with Gibbs conditioning, entropy maximization, and the Orlicz version of the Poincaré–Maxwell–Borel lemma due to S. Johnston and J. Prochno
[
59
]
, the authors deduce a conditional limit theorem for high-dimensional Orlicz balls. In geometric parlance, the latter shows that if
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
are Orlicz functions, then random points in the
M_{1}
-Orlicz ball, conditioned on having a small
M_{2}
-Orlicz radius, look like an appropriately scaled
M_{2}
-Orlicz ball. In fact, it is shown that the limiting distribution in their Poincaré–Maxwell–Borel lemma, and thus the geometric interpretation, undergoes a phase transition depending on the magnitude of the
M_{2}
-Orlicz radius.

A few years ago there was actually a breakthrough on the commutative front. S. S. Kim, Y.-T. Liao, and K. Ramanan established both level–1 and level–2 large deviations principles for sequences of random vectors under asymptotic thin-shell-type conditions
[
73
]
. In its most elementary form, this condition requires the sequence of Euclidean norms of the random vectors, normalized by a factor
d^{-1/2}
, to satisfy an LDP at speed
d
and with rate function having a unique minimum. Notably, the authors manage to establish this asymptotic thin-shell condition in the case of random vectors uniformly distributed on Orlicz balls whose Orlicz functions are of super-quadratic growth. This is very nice for several reasons, but most prominently is maybe the following: the authors identify a similarity to Klartag’s famous central limit theorem which follows from the classical thin-shell estimate. The mentioned work is quite impressive and the goal in the near future will be to establish the asymptotic thin-shell condition for other classes of random vectors, for instance, for those chosen uniformly at random from unit balls of Lorentz sequence spaces (these spaces are, besides Orlicz sequence spaces, the most natural sequence space generalizations of
\ell_{p}
-spaces and also belong to the important class of
1
-symmetric Banach spaces); we refer to
[
62
]
for a recent and first probabilistic approach in this setting.

Last but not least, let us mention that in recent years sharp large deviations results in the flavor of R. R. Bahadur and R. R. Ranga Rao
[
12
]
or V. V. Petrov
[
92
]
have been obtained for several random geometric quantities. While LDPs identify the asymptotic exponential decay rate of probabilities in terms of speed and rate function, sharp large deviation estimates also provide the subexponential prefactor in front of the exponentially decaying term, which is not visible on the logarithmic scale of an LDP. In that sense, sharp large deviations provide more accurate quantitative estimates in finite dimensions, which is more in the spirit of the local theory of Banach spaces. This line of research was initiated by Y.-T. Liao and K. Ramanan
[
85
]
and picked up by T. Kaufmann in
[
70
]
and L. Frühwirth and J. Prochno in
[
38
]
. Since the techniques differ in various aspects from classical large deviations techniques, we shall not discuss those results here and rather refer the interested reader to the references above.

2
An outrageously short introduction to large deviations theory

Let us continue with a brief introduction to large deviations theory and some of its fundamental concepts. The systematic study and unified formalization of large deviations theory, which in essence concerns the asymptotic behavior of remote tails of sequences of probability distributions, was developed by S. Varadhan in 1966
[
111
]
. In fact, he was later awarded the prestigious Abel Prize “for his fundamental contributions to probability theory and in particular for creating a unified theory of large deviations” in 2007.

We shall state results here in their most elementary form (sketch some of their proofs) and provide references to more general ones where needed. However, the goal is to provide the uninitiated reader with a rough understanding of the underlying ideas and principles that are required later on. For a thorough introduction to this fascinating topic, we refer the reader to the survey of S. Varadhan
[
112
]
or the wonderful monographs by A. Dembo and O. Zeitouni
[
27
]
, R. S. Ellis
[
34
]
, F. den Hollander
[
28
]
, or by F. Rassoul-Agha and T. Seppäläinen
[
98
]
as well as the survey article by H. Touchette
[
110
]
.

2.1
Motivation

Assume we are given iid random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
, say for simplicity
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]=0
and
\mathrm{Var}[X_{1}]=\sigma^{2}>0
, and let
S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, denote the sequence of partial sums. The law of large numbers tells us that, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big[\Big|\frac{S_{n}}{n}\Big|\geq\varepsilon\Big|\Big]=0.

Chebyshev’s inequality yields a convergence rate of
1/n
, but can we, at least under sufficient moment conditions, improve upon that bound?

An event of the form
\{|S_{n}|\geq n\varepsilon\}
is referred to as a large deviation or rare event, compared to the normal scale
\{|S_{n}|\geq\sqrt{n}\varepsilon\}
considered in a weak limit theorem. By a direct computation one can check that, for instance, a sequence of independent standard Gaussians
g_{1},g_{2},\dots
satisfies, for any
a>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}\geq an\Big]=-\frac{a^{2}}{2},

and that a sequence of independent Rademacher random variables
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots
satisfies

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\geq an\Big]=-I(a),

where
I:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty]
is defined as

I(x):=\begin{cases}\frac{1+x}{2}\log(1+x)+\frac{1-x}{2}\log(1-x)&:\,x\in[-1,1]\\
\infty&:\,|x|>1.\end{cases}

If
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
denotes either the sequence of Gaussians or the one of Rademachers, then both examples indicate a similar behavior of the form

\mathbb{P}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\geq an\Big]\approx e^{-n\rate(a)}

for an appropriate function
\rate:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty]
and
a>0
. In particular, the rate of convergence is exponential and thus way faster than the rate
1/n
suggested by Chebyshev’s inequality.

This raises the question whether there is a more general principle behind this? Let us assume that the random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
are iid, centered, and have all exponential moments, i.e., for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the moment generating function satisfies

\lambda_{X}(t):=\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{tX_{1}}\Big]<\infty.

It follows from Markov’s inequality, together with the iid assumption, that, for any
a,t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\Big[S_{n}\geq an\Big]=\mathbb{P}\Big[e^{tS_{n}}\geq e^{tan}\Big]\leq e^{-tan}\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{tS_{n}}\Big]=e^{-atn}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{X}(t)=e^{-n[ta-\log\lambda_{X}(t)]},

and a simple optimization in the parameter
t>0
shows that, for any
a>0
,

\mathbb{P}\Big[S_{n}\geq an\Big]\leq e^{-n\sup_{t>0}[ta-\log\lambda_{X}(t)]}.

In particular,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}\Big[S_{n}\geq an\Big]\leq-n\sup_{t>0}[ta-\log\lambda_{X}(t)],

and the right-hand side is essentially the Legendre transform of the log-moment generating function of
X_{1}
.

2.2
Cramér type large deviations

It turns out that the so-called Chernoff bound with the Legendre transform presented above is actually optimal and this is the assertion of a classical theorem due to H. Cramér
[
25
]
. Let us state this result here in its simplest form.

Theorem 2.1

(H. Cramér, 1938)
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
be iid random variables such that, for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
,

\Lambda_{X}(t):=\log\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{tX_{1}}\Big]<\infty.

Then, for every
a>\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\log\mathbb{P}[S_{n}\geq an]=-\Lambda_{X}^{*}(a),

where
\Lambda_{X}^{*}(a):=\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}}[as-\Lambda_{X}(s)]
is the Legendre transform of
\Lambda_{X}
.

Idea of Proof.

First of all, it is easy to see that it is enough to consider the case
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]<0
and
a=0
, i.e., it is enough to prove that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\geq 0\Big]=-\Lambda^{*}_{Y}(0).

As we have seen, the upper bound follows essentially from the exponential Markov inequality. To argue that the supremum is actually attained on
(0,\infty)
one needs to take the distribution of mass of
X_{1}
into account, which means consider separately the following cases: (1)
\mathbb{P}[X_{1}<0]
, (2)
\mathbb{P}[X_{1}\leq 0]
and
\mathbb{P}[X_{1}=0]>0
as well as (3)
\mathbb{P}[X_{1}<0]>0
and
\mathbb{P}[X_{1}>0]>0
. Note that we used here the reduction to
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]<0
.

To explain the idea behind the proof the lower bound, let us assume that for a set of nice random variables
(Y_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
and
a>\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}]
,
\sigma^{2}=\mathrm{Var}[X_{1}]>0
, we are interested in
\mathbb{P}[\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\geq an]
. If we standarize the partial sum of random variables, then we obtain

\mathbb{P}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\geq an\Big]=\mathbb{P}\Big[\frac{S_{n}-n\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]}{\sigma\sqrt{n}}\geq\frac{\sqrt{n}(a-\mathbb{E}[X_{1}])}{\sigma}\Big].

Now, for large
n\in\mathbb{N}
, Gaussian approximation tells us that

\mathbb{P}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\geq an\Big]\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{\frac{\sqrt{n}(a-\mathbb{E}[X_{1}])}{\sigma}}^{\infty}e^{-y^{2}/2}\,\textup{d}y.

Because of the
\sqrt{n}
in the lower bound of the integration domain, this Gaussian approximation is not good if
a-\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]
is not small. The idea, which actually goes back to F. Esscher
[
35
]
, is to perform an exponential tilt of the original measure (today known as Esscher transform or Cramér transform) that adds more weight to larger values, thereby shifting the mean to the right, which eliminates the lower bound of the integration domain. As it turns out, the price that one pays in the integrand is acceptable and the central limit theorem can be properly applied to obtain the lower bound in Cramér’s theorem.

More formally, in our set-up of
\mu:=\mathbb{P}^{X_{1}}
, we define the exponentially tilted measure to be

\widetilde{\mu}(\textup{d}x):=\frac{e^{t_{\text{min}}x}}{\rho}\mu(\textup{d}x)

with
\rho:=e^{-\Lambda_{X}^{*}(0)}=\inf_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\lambda_{X}(t)
and
t_{\textrm{min}}>0
such that
\lambda_{X}(t_{\textrm{min}})=\rho
and
\lambda_{X}^{\prime}(t_{\textrm{min}})=0
. Given this measure, we consider independent random variables
\widetilde{X}_{1},\widetilde{X}_{2},\dots
with distribution
\widetilde{\mu}
. In particular, those random variables satisfy
\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}_{1}]=0=a
and thus deal with the problematic part mentioned before. Moreover, a change of measure from
\mu
to
\widetilde{\mu}
shows that

\mathbb{P}[S_{n}\geq 0]=\rho^{n}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[e^{t_{\text{min}}\widetilde{S}_{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\widetilde{S}_{n}\geq 0\}}\Bigg],

where
\widetilde{S}_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widetilde{X}_{i}
. Thus,

\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}[S_{n}\geq 0]=\underbrace{\log(\rho)}_{=-\Lambda_{X}^{*}(0)}+\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\Bigg[e^{t_{\text{min}}\widetilde{S}_{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\widetilde{S}_{n}\geq 0\}}\Bigg]

and it is therefore enough to show that the second summand on the right-hand side is non-negative. But this follows from the classical central limit theorem, since

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\Bigg[e^{t_{\text{min}}\widetilde{S}_{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\widetilde{S}_{n}\geq 0\}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\Bigg[e^{-t_{\text{min}}\widetilde{S}_{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq\widetilde{S}_{n}\leq 1\}}\Bigg]

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{t_{\text{min}}\sqrt{n}}{n}+\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{P}\Bigg[\frac{\widetilde{S}_{n}}{\sqrt{n}}\in[0,1]\Bigg]\,\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}0,

which then completes the proof.
∎

A natural question that arises is the one of what happens if we are not in the luxurious situation where we are working with random variables having all exponential moments. What happens, for instance, if we are faced with a sequence of heavy tailed random variables (those naturally appear, e.g., when studying large deviations related to
\ell_{p}
-balls)? As it turns out, we can still prove a version of Cramér’s theorem, also known as
Cramér’s theorem for random variables with stretched exponential tails
.

Theorem 2.2

(Cramér for stretched exponential tails)
.

Consider iid and integrable random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
. Assume that there exist constants
r\in(0,1)
,
c\in(0,\infty)
, and
0<C_{1}<C_{2}<\infty
such that, for all
t\geq 0
,

C_{1}e^{-ct^{r}}\leq\mathbb{P}[X_{1}\geq t]\leq C_{2}e^{-ct^{r}}.

(1)

Then, for any
a>\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]
, we have that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{r}}\log\mathbb{P}[S_{n}\geq an]=-c\big(a-\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]\big)^{r}.

The property of stretched exponential tails is expressed through (
1
). In particular, one can easily check that the lower bound in (
1
) guarantees that
\mathbb{E}[e^{tX_{1}}]=\infty
for all
t>0
. Note that the speed is
n^{r}
, which is much slower than the speed
n
in Cramér’s theorem (see Theorem
2.1
). Moreover, the rate function on the right-hand side is obviously non-convex, i.e., it does not arise as a Legendre transform. Since a proof is not contained in the standard literature, we provide at least a sketch here.

Idea of Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Let
a>0
. Without loss of generality we may assume that
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]=0
. We shall start with the lower bound, where one uses the philosophy that a sum of iid random variables with stretched exponential tails becomes large most easily if one of the random variables does, while all others have ‘normal’ size.

Lower bound:
Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain from

X_{1}\geq n(a+\varepsilon)\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\sum_{i=2}^{n}X_{i}\geq-(n-1)\varepsilon

that

S_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\geq n(a+\varepsilon)-(n-1)\varepsilon=an+\varepsilon.

Hence, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[S_{n}\geq an]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[X_{1}\geq n(a+\varepsilon)]\cdot\mathbb{P}\Big[\frac{S_{n-1}}{n-1}\geq-\varepsilon\Big],

where we used the iid property of the sequence
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
. As a consequence of the weak law of large numbers,

\mathbb{P}\Big[\frac{S_{n-1}}{n-1}\geq-\varepsilon\Big]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}1.

(2)

Thus, the lower bound in (
1
) yields that, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{r}}\log\mathbb{P}[S_{n}\geq an]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n^{r}}\log C_{1}-c(a+\varepsilon)^{r}+\frac{1}{n^{r}}\log\mathbb{P}\Big[\frac{S_{n-1}}{n-1}\geq-\varepsilon\Big]\,.

This together with (
2
) shows that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{r}}\log\mathbb{P}[S_{n}\geq an]\geq-c(a+\varepsilon)^{r},

which completes the proof of the lower bound for
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.

Upper bound:
The upper bound requires tedious computations, which we shall shorten considerably, leaving it to the reader to fill in the details. First, we note that by the very same philosophy already used in the lower bound, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[S_{n}\geq an]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big[S_{n}\geq an\,,\,\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}\geq an\Big]+\mathbb{P}\Big[S_{n}\geq an\,,\,\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}<an\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}\geq an\Big]+\mathbb{P}\Big[S_{n}\geq an\,,\,\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}<an\Big].

We will show that the second summand does not dominate the first on the scale
n^{r}
.

Because of the identical distribution of the random variables, for the first summand we have

\mathbb{P}\Big[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}\geq an\Big]\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}[X_{i}\geq an]=n\mathbb{P}[X_{1}\geq an].

In combination with the upper bound in (
1
) this yields, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n^{r}}\log\mathbb{P}\Big[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}\geq an\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n^{r}}\log n+\frac{1}{n^{r}}\log C_{2}-ca^{r}

and so see that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{r}}\log\mathbb{P}\Big[\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}\geq an\Big]\leq-ca^{r}.

(3)

Using the simple fact that, for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and every sequence
a_{1}(n),\dots,a_{N}(n)\geq 0
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\,\frac{1}{n^{r}}\,\log\sum_{i=1}^{N}a_{i}(n)=\max_{1\leq i\leq N}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{r}}\,\log a_{i}(n),

we obtain for
N=2
in combination with (
3
) that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{r}}\log\mathbb{P}[S_{n}\geq an]

\displaystyle\leq\max\bigg\{-ca^{r}\,,\,\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{r}}\log\mathbb{P}\Big[S_{n}\geq an\,,\,\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}<an\Big]\bigg\}\,.

Hence, it is enough to prove that the second entry in the maximum does not dominate the first. Using Markov’s inequality, we obtain, for every
\alpha>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{n}e^{\alpha X_{i}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{i}<na\}}\bigg]

\displaystyle\geq e^{\alpha na}\,\mathbb{P}\Big[S_{n}\geq an\,,\,\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}<an\Big].

Equivalently, for every
\alpha>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}\Big[S_{n}\geq an\,,\,\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}<an\Big]\leq e^{-\alpha na}\,\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{n}e^{\alpha X_{i}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{i}<na\}}\bigg]

and so the iid assumption yields that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big[S_{n}\geq an\,,\,\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}<an\Big]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\alpha na}\bigg(\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\alpha X_{1}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{1}<\alpha^{-1}\}}\Big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\alpha X_{1}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\alpha^{-1}\leq X_{1}<an\}}\Big]\bigg)^{n}.

We will now show that for appropriate choice of
\alpha
, the factor
e^{-\alpha na}
has the desired rate
-ca^{r}
.

The first expectation:
Using that for all
x<1
,
e^{x}\leq 1+x+x^{2}
, and that, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
1+x\leq e^{x}
, we obtain from a direct computation that (since
\mathbb{E}[X_{1}]=0
and
\sigma^{2}=\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}]
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\alpha X_{1}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X_{1}<\alpha^{-1}\}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq e^{\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}}\,.

(4)

The second expectation:
It follows from a tedious computation involving integration by parts for Lebesgue-Stieltjes integrals that

\mathbb{E}[e^{\alpha\xi}\mathbbm{1}_{\{a\leq\xi\leq b\}}]=e^{\alpha a}\mathbb{P}[\xi\geq a]-e^{\alpha b}\mathbb{P}[\xi>b]+\alpha\int_{a}^{b}e^{\alpha x}\mathbb{P}[\xi>x]\,\textup{d}x

holds for any random variable
\xi
and all
a<b
. In our setting, together with the upper bound in (
1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\alpha X_{1}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\alpha^{-1}\leq X_{1}<an\}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\alpha\int_{\alpha^{-1}}^{an}e^{\alpha x-cx^{r}}\,\textup{d}x+C_{2}e^{1-c\alpha^{-r}},

with absolute constant
C_{2}\in(0,\infty)
.
Now we choose
\alpha:=(c-\varepsilon)(an)^{r-1}
with
\varepsilon\in(0,c)
. Then, for all
x\in[\alpha^{-1},an]
,
\alpha x-cx^{r}\leq-\varepsilon x^{r}
(where we used
r\in(0,1)
). Thus, for sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\alpha X_{1}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\alpha^{-1}\leq X_{1}<an\}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\alpha\int_{\alpha^{-1}}^{an}e^{-\varepsilon x^{r}}\,\textup{d}x+e^{-Cn^{r(1-r)}},

where
C:=C(a,c,C_{2},\varepsilon,r)\in(0,\infty)
. This means that, for sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\alpha X_{1}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\alpha^{-1}\leq X_{1}<an\}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\alpha\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{1/r}r}\Gamma(r^{-1};\varepsilon\alpha^{-r})+e^{-Cn^{r(1-r)}},

where
\Gamma(\cdot;\cdot)
is the incomplete gamma function. Using the asymptotic
\Gamma(x;y)\sim y^{x-1}e^{-y}
for
y\to\infty
, we obtain for sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
(note that
\alpha=\alpha(n)
) that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\alpha X_{1}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\alpha^{-1}\leq X_{1}<an\}}\Big]\leq e^{-\widetilde{C}n^{r(1-r)}},

(5)

with
\widetilde{C}:=\widetilde{C}(a,c,C_{2},\varepsilon,r)\in(0,\infty)
.

Completion of the proof:

We will use the bounds (
4
) and (
5
). For the choice
\alpha=(c-\varepsilon)(an)^{r-1}
and sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big[S_{n}\geq an\,,\,\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}<an\Big]

\displaystyle\leq e^{-\alpha na}\bigg(e^{\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}}+e^{-\widetilde{C}n^{r(1-r)}}\bigg)^{n}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-(c-\varepsilon)(an)^{r}}e^{C_{1}n^{2r-1}}\bigg(1+e^{-\widetilde{C}n^{r(1-r)}}\bigg)^{n},

where
C_{1}:=C_{1}(\sigma,c,\varepsilon,a,r)\in(0,\infty)
. Since

\bigg(1+e^{-\widetilde{C}n^{r(1-r)}}\bigg)^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}1,

we have that, for large
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

e^{C_{1}n^{2r-1}}\bigg(1+e^{-\widetilde{C}n^{r(1-r)}}\bigg)^{n}\leq e^{2C_{1}n^{2r-1}}.

Noting that
2r-1<r
and so
2C_{1}n^{2r-1}=o(n^{r})
for
n\to\infty
, we see that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{r}}\log\mathbb{P}\Big[S_{n}\geq an\,,\,\max_{1\leq i\leq n}X_{i}<an\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n^{r}}\log\Big(e^{-(c-\varepsilon)(an)^{r}}e^{o(n^{r})}\Big)=-(c-\varepsilon)a^{r}.

Taking
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
completes the proof.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

We chose to state this theorem here, because later we shall use a more general version of such a result (where the constant can dependent on the parameter
t
), which is due to N. Gantert, K. Ramanan, and F. Rembart
[
40
]
. But the philosophy behind it is more easily explained in the situation considered in Theorem
2.2
.

2.3
Sanov’s theorem for the empirical measure

Another fundamental theorem in large deviations theory, aside from Cramér’s theorem, is Sanov’s theorem. This result concerns so-called level–
2
large deviations for the empirical measure of random variables and goes back to I. N. Sanov
[
101
]
. We shall present here a more general and today standard form, following the presentation in
[
98
]
.

Let
E
be a Polish space which will always be endowed with the Borel
\sigma
-field. Consider
\lambda\in\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)
, where
\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)
is the set of probability measures on
E
. Consider iid and
E
-valued random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
having distribution
\lambda
and define the corresponding empirical measure via

\mu_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}},\hskip 20.00003ptn\in\mathbb{N}.

This is a random probability measure on
E
, i.e., each
\mu_{n}
is an random element of
\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)
.

Remark 2.4
.

Let us recall some well known facts. Since
E
is a Polish space,
\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)
is a Polish space itself in the weak topology generated by
\mathscr{C}_{b}(E)
, the space of bounded continuous functions on
E
taking values in
\mathbb{R}
.

\mathscr{M}(E)
will denote the space of signed measures on
E
of which
\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)
is a subspace. Then
\mathscr{M}(E)
and
\mathscr{C}_{b}(E)
are in duality via

\langle\nu,f\rangle=\int_{E}f\,\textup{d}\mu

for
\nu\in\mathscr{M}(E)
,
f\in\mathscr{C}_{b}(E)
.

Convergence of probability measures in the weak topology
\sigma(\mathscr{M}(E),\mathscr{C}_{b}(E))
is the same as the weak convergence
\nu_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}\nu
, which holds by definition if and only if
\langle\nu_{n},f\rangle\rightarrow\langle\nu,f\rangle
for all
f\in\mathscr{C}_{b}(E)

1
1

1

Recall that the weak topology on
\mathscr{M}(E)
,
\sigma(\mathscr{M}(E),\mathscr{C}_{b}(E))
, is the minimal topology under which all functions
\nu\mapsto\langle\nu,f,\rangle
,
f\in\mathscr{C}_{b}(E)
are continuous.
.

Sanov’s theorem describes the large deviations behavior of the distributions

\rho_{n}:=\mathbb{P}\big[\mu_{n}\in B\big],\hskip 20.00003ptB\subset\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)\,\,\text{Borel set},

of
\mu_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Prior to proving an LDP it is good to develop a sense for a law of large numbers, which we shall do now before stating Sanov’s theorem. First, we note that by the classical strong law of large numbers, for all
f\in\mathscr{C}_{b}(E)
,

\displaystyle\mu_{n}(f)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{n}}[f]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(X_{i})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}-a.s.}\mathbb{E}[f(X_{1})]=\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[f].

(6)

Let us recall that a sequence
(g_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is said to determine weak convergence on
\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)
if and only if
\nu_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle w}}{{\longrightarrow}}\nu
is equivalent to the condition that, for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
\langle\nu_{n},g_{i}\rangle\rightarrow\langle\nu,g_{i}\rangle
. Now, since our Polish space is by definition separable, there exists a countable, convergence determining collection of
\mathscr{C}_{b}(E)
-functions. Let us denote those functions by
(f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
with
f_{i}\in\mathscr{C}_{b}(E)
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Because of (
6
), for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mu_{n}(f_{i})\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}-a.s.}\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[f_{i}].

In particular, because
(f_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
determines weak convergence on
\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)
, we obtain the following strong law of large numbers for
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
:

\mu_{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{\mathbb{P}-a.s.}\lambda.

Now that we have developed an understanding of the typical asymptotic behavior of the sequence of empirical measures corresponding to
X_{1},X_{2},\dots
, we shall look at the asymptotic probabilities of rare events treated in Sanov’s theorem.

Theorem 2.5

(Sanov’s theorem)
.

Let
E
be a Polish space and, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\rho_{n}
be the distribution of the empirical measure

\mu_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}},

corresponding to the iid sequence
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
having distribution
\lambda\in\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)
. Then
(\rho_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP on
\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)
at speed
n
with good convex rate function
H(\cdot|\lambda):\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)\to[0,\infty]
being the relative entropy

H(\nu|\lambda):=\begin{cases}\int_{E}f\log f\,\textup{d}\lambda&:\nu\ll\lambda\,\text{ and }\,f=\frac{\textup{d}\nu}{\textup{d}\lambda}\\
\infty&:\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

In words, Sanov’s theorem tells us that the probability that the empirical measures resemble some distribution
\nu
other than the underlying distribution
\lambda
behaves like
e^{-nH(\nu|\lambda)}
, i.e., the rate is determined by the entropy of
\nu
relative to
\lambda
. We shall not provide a proof of Sanov’s theorem here, but rather refer the interested reader to the nice exposition of the proof in
[
98
]
.

2.4
The Gärtner–Ellis theorem for dependent random variables

Before presenting the general theory of large deviations, let us describe a classical generalization of Cramér’s theorem due to J. Gärtner
[
42
]
and R. S. Ellis
[
33
]
, which shows that we may allow random sequences with a kind of moderate dependence, and still have LDP behavior. More precisely, let us consider random vectors
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
taking values in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and having moment generating functions

\lambda_{n}(t):=\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\langle t,Y_{n}\rangle}\Big],\hskip 10.00002ptt\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\,n\in\mathbb{N};

here
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the standard inner product on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We shall assume throughout this subsection that the following two conditions are satisfied:

(GE1)
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\big(\lambda_{n}(nt)\big)=\Lambda(t)\in[-\infty,\infty]
exists,

(GE2)
0\in\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda}^{\circ}
, with
\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda}:=\{t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\Lambda(t)<\infty\}
.

Under the previous assumptions, it is rather easy to show that
\Lambda
is convex and satisfies
\Lambda>-\infty
. Moreover, the Legendre transform
\Lambda^{*}(\cdot):=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}[\langle\cdot,t\rangle-\Lambda(t)]
is a convex good rate function (i.e., not everywhere equal to
\infty
with compact sublevel sets).

Let us recall that a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called an exposed point for
\Lambda^{*}
if and only if there exists a point
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that, for all
\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni y\neq x
,

\Lambda^{*}(y)-\Lambda^{*}(x)>\langle y-x,t\rangle.

This
t
is the normal vector to an exposing hyperplane for the point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Note that, since
\Lambda^{*}
is convex, for each
x\in\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda^{*}}
there exists
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\Lambda^{*}(y)-\Lambda^{*}(x)\geq\langle t,y-x\rangle
for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e., a supporting hyperplane at the point
(x,\Lambda^{*}(x))
of the graph of
\Lambda^{*}
. Thus, at an exposed point there exists a supporting hyperplane sharing no other point with the graph.

Theorem 2.6

(Gärtner–Ellis theorem)
.

Let
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that (GE1) and (GE2) are satisfied, and let
\mu_{n}:=\mathbb{P}^{Y_{n}}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, for all closed sets
C\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\big(\mu_{n}(C)\big)\leq-\inf_{x\in C}\Lambda^{*}(x),

and, for all open sets
O\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\big(\mu_{n}(O)\big)\geq-\inf_{x\in O\cap E}\Lambda^{*}(x),

where
E
is the collection of exposed points of
\Lambda^{*}
whose exposing hyperplane belongs to
\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda}^{\circ}
.

Suppose that, in addition,
\Lambda
is lower semi-continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, differentiable on
\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda}^{\circ}
, and that
\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
or
\Lambda
is steep on the boundary
\partial\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda}
of
\mathcal{D}_{\Lambda}
(meaning that
\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\partial D_{\Lambda}\colon t\in D_{\Lambda}}\lVert\nabla\Lambda(t)\rVert_{2}=\infty
). Then we may replace
O\cap E
by
O
. In large deviations parlance, the sequence
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with speed
n
and rate function
\Lambda^{*}
.

The steepness assumption may be removed in the iid setting
[
27
, Corollary 6.1.6]
, but is generally required for dependent random sequences. Moreover, one can generalize the result to topological vector spaces and we refer to
[
27
, Section 4.5]
. For a more detailed discussion, we refer the interested reader to
[
27
]
and
[
28
]
; it is also discussed there how the Gärtner–Ellis theorem implies both Cramér’s and Sanov’s theorem.

2.5
The abstract theory of large deviations

As already mentioned, a general theory of large deviations, going far beyond the ideas behind Cramér’s theorem, was developed by S. Varadhan in
[
111
]
. So let us explore on what principles this unified theory is build upon.

Definition 2.7

(Rate function and LDP)
.

Let
\mathscr{X}
be a topological Hausdorff space and
\mathscr{B}_{\mathscr{X}}
the Borel
\sigma
-field on
\mathscr{X}
. A rate function
\rate:\mathscr{X}\to[0,\infty]
is a lower-semicontinuous mapping with
\rate\not\equiv\infty
, which is said to be a good rate function if the level sets
\{x\in\mathscr{X}\,:\,\rate(x)\leq a\}
,
a\in[0,\infty)
are not only closed, but compact. The effective domain of the rate function is given by

\mathscr{D}_{\rate}=\big\{x\in\mathscr{X}\,:\,\rate(x)<\infty\big\}.

Let
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of probability measures on
\mathscr{B}_{\mathscr{X}}
. Further, let
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of positive reals
with
s_{n}\uparrow\infty
and
\rate:\mathscr{X}\to[0,\infty]
be a lower semi-continuous function.
We say that
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies a (full) large deviation principle (LDP) at speed
s_{n}
and with rate function
\rate
if and only if

\begin{split}-\inf_{x\in A^{\circ}}\rate(x)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}{1\over s_{n}}\log\nu_{n}(A)\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}{1\over s_{n}}\log\nu_{n}(A)\leq-\inf_{x\in\overline{A}}\rate(x)\end{split}

(7)

for all Borel sets
A\subset\mathscr{X}
, where
A^{\circ}
and
\overline{A}
denote the interior and closure of
A
, respectively.
We say that
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies a weak LDP with speed
s_{n}
and rate function
\rate
if the rightmost upper bound in (
7
) is valid only for compact sets
A\subset\mathscr{X}
.

Remark 2.8
.

The notion of an LDP for a sequence of random variables is obtained by considering the LDP for the corresponding sequence of distributions.

Remark 2.9
.

The estimates in (
7
), describing the LDP, can also be reformulated in terms of an upper and lower large deviations bound holding for closed and open sets respectively, which is very much in the spirit of the bounds in the Gärtner–Ellis theorem (see Theorem
2.6
above).

Remark 2.10
.

Let us remark that the rate function controlling the LDP is always unique.
A consequence of the rate function being good is that it attains its infimum on closed sets. Moreover, since
\nu_{n}(\mathscr{X})=1
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, for the upper bound in (
7
) to hold it is necessary that
\inf_{x\in\mathscr{X}}\rate(x)=0
. So when
\rate
is a good rate function, then there must exist an element
x\in\mathscr{X}
such that
\rate(x)=0
.

Note that on metric spaces
M
, one may check the lower-semicontinuity on sequences, i.e.,
\rate
is lower-semicontinuous if and only if, for all
x\in M
,

\liminf_{x_{n}\to x}\,\rate(x_{n})\geq\rate(x).

Remark 2.11
.

In view of the Portmanteau theorem, this definition reminds us of the weak convergence of measures. However, to the uninitiated reader this definition only mildly resembles what one might expect after having seen Cramér’s theorem. Naively one is tempted to think of a definition like

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{n}}\log\nu_{n}(A)=-\inf_{x\in A}\rate(x).

As it turns out, this is far to restrictive to allow us to build a rich and powerful theory. Indeed, note that if
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of non-atomic measures, then
\nu_{n}(\{x\})=0
for any
x\in\mathscr{X}
. But this means that
\rate\equiv\infty
, which is not allowed in the definition of a rate function. So we really need some topological restrictions in the definition of an LDP.

As we shall see later, when discussing the work on large deviation principles in the asymptotic theory of geometric functional analysis, one way of proving the large deviation estimates in (
7
) is to first prove the upper bound for compact sets or, in other words, to first prove a weak LDP. Heuristically, this can work if most of the mass in
\mathscr{X}
is concentrated (on an exponential scale) on compact sets.

Definition 2.12

(Exponential tightness)
.

Let
\mathscr{X}
be a Hausdorff topological space with Borel
\sigma
-field
\mathscr{B}_{\mathscr{X}}
and assume that
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of positive reals with
s_{n}\uparrow\infty
. We say that a sequence
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of probability measures on
\mathscr{B}_{\mathscr{X}}
is exponentially tight (at scale
s_{n}
) if and only if for every
C\in(0,\infty)
there exists a compact set
K_{C}\subset\mathscr{X}
such that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{n}}\log\nu_{n}(K_{C}^{c})<C.

Remark 2.13
.

Let us note that exponential tightness of a sequence of measures is not necessary for the sequence to satisfy an LDP with a good rate function. In nice situations however, if we are working with locally compact Hausdorff topological spaces
\mathscr{X}
or with Polish spaces (i.e., a separable completely metrizable topological space), then goodness of the rate function implies exponential tightness (see, e.g.,
[
27
]
).

As it turns out, the notion of exponential tightness is the key to lifting a weak LDP to a full one (see, e.g.,
[
27
, Lemma 1.2.18]
). At the same time, this guarantees us a good rate function in the LDP.

Lemma 2.14
.

Let
\mathscr{X}
be a Hausdorff topological space with Borel
\sigma
-field
\mathscr{B}_{\mathscr{X}}
and
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
a sequence of probability measures on
\mathscr{B}_{\mathscr{X}}
. Suppose that
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies a weak LDP at speed
s_{n}
and with rate function
\rate
. If
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is exponentially tight at scale
s_{n}
, then it satisfies a full LDP at speed
s_{n}
and with rate function
\rate
. In particular, in this case the rate function
\rate
is a good rate function.

2.5.1
Three fundamental principles

There are several useful tools in the theory of large deviations that allow one to obtain an LDP on one space by a transformation of a known LDP on some other. Such transformations seem rather innocent at first, but are not to be underestimated in their power. Note that we will refrain from providing any examples here, because we shall see all principles in action later when presenting some of the works on large deviations elaborated upon in the introduction.

The first stability result concerns the preservation of LDPs under continuous mappings and is commonly known as the contraction principle. Since the proof is quite short, we will present it here.

Lemma 2.15

(Contraction principle)
.

Let
\mathscr{X}
and
\mathscr{Y}
be Hausdorff topological spaces and let
T:\mathscr{X}\to\mathscr{Y}
be a continuous mapping. Denote their respective Borel
\sigma
-fields by
\mathscr{B}_{\mathscr{X}}
and
\mathscr{B}_{\mathscr{Y}}
. If
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of probability measures on
\mathscr{B}_{\mathscr{X}}
satisfying an LDP at speed
s_{n}
with a good rate function
\rate:\mathscr{X}\to[0,\infty]
, then the sequence of pushforwards

\mu_{n}:=\nu_{n}\circ T^{-1},\hskip 20.00003ptn\in\mathbb{N}

satisfies an LDP on the space
\mathscr{Y}
at the same speed
s_{n}
and with good rate function
\mathbb{J}:\mathscr{Y}\to[0,\infty]

\mathbb{J}(y):=\inf_{x\in\mathscr{X},T(x)=y}\rate(x),

where we agree that the infimum over the empty set is equal to
\infty
.

Proof.

Let us first argue that the function
\mathbb{J}
is indeed a good rate function.
We start by showing that
\mathbb{J}\not\equiv\infty
. Since
\rate
is a (good) rate function,

\mathscr{D}_{\rate}=\big\{x\in\mathscr{X}\,:\,\rate(x)<\infty\big\}\neq\emptyset.

And so it follows from the definition of
\mathbb{J}
that

\mathscr{D}_{\mathbb{J}}=\{y\in\mathscr{Y}\,:\,\mathbb{J}(y)<\infty\}\neq\emptyset,

hence
\mathbb{J}\not\equiv\infty
. We now show that the function
\mathbb{J}
is good. Clearly,
\mathbb{J}
is non-negative and, in view of Remark
2.10
and the continuity of
T
, attains its infimum for every
y\in T(\mathscr{X})
at some point in
\mathscr{X}
. We shall use this to show that the level sets are compact. Let
a\in[0,\infty)
. Since
\rate
is good,

\{x\in\mathscr{X}\,:\,\rate(x)\leq a\}

is a compact set in
\mathscr{X}
. Therefore, since
T
is continuous,

T\big(\{x\in E\,:\,\rate(x)\leq a\}\big)

is a compact set in
\mathscr{Y}
. But since

\displaystyle T\big(\big\{x\in\mathscr{X}\,:\,\rate(x)\leq a\big\}\big)

\displaystyle=\big\{T(x)\,:\,x\in\mathscr{X},\,\rate(x)\leq a\big\}=\big\{y\in\mathscr{y}\,:\,\exists x\in\mathscr{X}:T(x)=y\,\,\land\,\,\rate(x)\leq a\big\}

\displaystyle=\big\{y\in\mathscr{Y}\,:\,\inf_{x\in T^{-1}(y)}\rate(x)\leq a\big\}=\big\{y\in\mathscr{Y}\,:\,\mathbb{J}(y)\leq a\big\},

which shows the compactness of level sets. We used that
\mathbb{J}
attains its minimum on closed sets in the equality from line one to line two.
∎

The next principle frequently used, often in combination with the contraction principle, is the following one describing the large deviations behavior of pairs of independent random objects. We shall restrict ourselves to the case of Polish spaces, enough for the purpose of this paper. However, the result can be formulated and proved for regular topological spaces (see, e.g.,
[
27
, Exercise 4.2.7]
), in which case one needs to additionally assume the exponential tightness of measures; by Remark
2.13
this follows from the goodness of rate functions in Polish spaces.

Lemma 2.16
.

Let
E
be a Polish space. Assume that
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are sequences of probability measures on
E
. Suppose that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
(X_{n},Y_{n})
is distributed according to the product measure
\nu_{n}\otimes\mu_{n}
on
\mathscr{B}_{E}\otimes\mathscr{B}_{E}
(i.e.,
X_{n}
and
Y_{n}
are independent). Assume that
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP at speed
s_{n}
with good rate function
\rate_{X}
and
(\mu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
an LDP at speed
s_{n}
with good rate function
\rate_{Y}
. Then,

(a)

(X_{n},Y_{n})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, satisfies an LDP on
E\times E
at speed
s_{n}
with good rate function
\mathbb{J}:E\times E\to[0,\infty]
,

\mathbb{J}(x,y):=\rate_{X}(x)+\rate_{Y}(y).

(b) If
T:E\times E\to F
is a continuous mapping into another Polish space
F
, then the sequence
Z_{n}:=T(X_{n},Y_{n})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, satisfies an LDP on
F
at speed
s_{n}
with good rate function
\rate_{Z}:F\to[0,\infty]
,

\rate_{Z}(z)=\inf_{(x,y)\in E\times E\atop{T(x,y)=z}}\rate_{X}(x)+\rate_{Y}(y).

The last principle we shall present here concerns the possibility of transferring an LDP from one sequence of random variables to another as long as those sequences are close on an exponential scale. Let
M
be a metric space with distance
d
, equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-field
\mathscr{B}_{M}
. We say that two sequences of
M
-valued random variables
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are exponentially equivalent at speed
s_{n}
if and only if, for all
\delta\in(0,\infty)
,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{s_{n}}\log\mathbb{P}\big[\textup{d}(X_{n},Y_{n})>\delta\big]=-\infty.

The next result shows that, as far as the LDP is concerned, exponentially equivalent random variables cannot be distinguished (see, e.g.,
[
27
, Theorem 4.2.13]
).

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
M
be a metric space equipped with its Borel
\sigma
-field
\mathscr{B}_{M}
and assume that
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are sequences of
M
-valued random variables. If
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP at speed
s_{n}
with good rate function
\rate
and
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are exponentially equivalent, then
(Y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP at the same speed
s_{n}
with the same good rate function
\rate
.

3
Large deviations results and techniques in the world of
\ell_{p}^{n}
-spaces

In this section we shall present some of the large deviations results that have been obtained within the framework of
\ell_{p}^{n}
-balls, of course starting with the inspiring work of N. Gantert, S. S. Kim, and K. Ramanan
[
39
]
on random one-dimensional projections of
\ell_{p}^{n}
-balls. As we have already mentioned in the introduction, in this setting, computations are facilitated by a probabilistic representation of the uniform distribution on an
\ell_{p}^{n}
-ball. More precisely, G. Schechtman and J. Zinn proved in
[
107
]
that a random vector
X
uniformly distributed on
{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{n}
,
1\leq p<\infty
, has a representation

\displaystyle X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textup{d}}}{{=}}U^{1/n}\frac{(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})}{\|(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n})\|_{p}},

(8)

where
U
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
and independent of
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
, which are iid
p
-generalized Gaussians, i.e., they have Lebesgue density

f_{p}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty),\hskip 20.00003ptx\mapsto\frac{1}{2p^{1/p}\Gamma(1+1/p)}e^{-|x|^{p}/p}.

(9)

This representation is powerful as it allows one to work with a random vector having independent coordinates; in contrast to the coordinates of
X
, which are obviously dependent. We refer the reader to the survey
[
95
]
for a discussion of the probabilistic approach to the geometry of
\ell_{p}^{n}
-balls.

3.1
Large deviations for random projections of
\ell_{p}^{n}
-balls

In
[
39
]
, N. Gantert, S. S. Kim, and K. Ramanan started analyzing distributional properties of the projections of high-dimensional random vectors in
\ell_{p}^{n}
-balls on the scale of large deviations. On the normal deviations scale, a central limit theorem for projections of convex bodies, i.e., for
X^{(n)}
sampled from a log-concave measure (for instance the uniform measure on a convex body) that is also isotropic, had already been obtained by B. Klartag
[
76
]
, showing that for sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
and most
\theta^{(n)}\in{\mathbb{S}}_{2}^{n-1}
, the projection of
X^{(n)}
onto
\theta^{(n)}
satisfies
\langle X^{(n)},\theta^{(n)}\rangle\approx\mathscr{N}(0,1)
in some suitable sense.

We shall look at the results obtained in the annealed case, where a quite different large deviations behavior is revealed depending on whether
p<2
or
p\geq 2
. In particular, this shows an interesting sensitivity with respect to the underlying distribution of random vectors, which can be attributed to the stark difference in the geometry of
\ell_{p}^{n}
-balls in those two regimes. More precisely, the authors showed that if for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\theta^{(n)}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
is a uniform random direction and
X^{(n,p)}
is an independent random point uniformly distributed in the
\ell_{p}^{n}
-ball for some fixed
p\in[1,\infty]
, then the sequence of rescaled random variables (the normalized scalar random projections)

W^{(n,p)}:=n^{{1\over p}-{1\over 2}}\langle X^{(n,p)},\theta^{(n)}\rangle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}n^{1\over p}X_{i}^{(n,p)}n^{1\over 2}\theta^{(n)}_{i},\hskip 10.00002ptn\in\mathbb{N},

satisfies an LDP with speed
n^{2p\over 2+p}
if
p\in[1,2)
and speed
n
if
p\in[2,\infty]
, and with a certain rate function that also depends on the parameter
p
; for
p=\infty
, we use the convention
n^{1\over p}=1
. Below we only discuss the results for
p<\infty
and refer to
[
39
, Section 8]
for a compilation of results for general product measures satisfying certain tail conditions (including the uniform measure on
{\mathbb{B}}_{\infty}^{n}
).

3.1.1
Annealed LDP – the case
p\in[2,\infty)

For
p\in[2,\infty)
, denoting by
\mu_{p}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
the
p
-Gaussian measure having Lebesgue density given by (
9
), we consider the function

\Phi_{p}(t_{0},t_{1},t_{2}):=\log\Bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{t_{0}z^{2}+t_{1}zy+t_{2}|y|^{p}}\,\mu_{2}(\textup{d}z)\mu_{p}(\textup{d}y)\Bigg),\hskip 20.00003ptt_{0},t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R},

which satisfies
\Phi_{p}(t_{0},t_{1},t_{2})<\infty
whenever
t_{0}<{1\over 2}
,
t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
t_{2}<{1\over p}
. The rate function that describes the large deviations behavior is given by the Legendre transform of
\Phi_{p}
, i.e., we define

\rate_{p}(x):=\inf_{\tau_{0}>0,\tau_{1}\in\mathbb{R},\tau_{2}>0\atop{\tau_{0}^{-1/2}\tau_{1}\tau_{2}^{-1/p}=x}}\Phi^{*}(\tau_{0},\tau_{1},\tau_{2});

the infimum that appears already hints at an application of the contraction principle, i.e., the desired LDP will be contracted from another one. Let us now state the LDP for random one-dimensional projections corresponding to
[
39
, Theorem 2.2]
.

Theorem A
.

Let
p\in[2,\infty)
. Then the sequence of random variables
(W^{(n,p)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{R}
with speed
n
and quasi-convex, symmetric, good rate function
\rate_{p}
.

Idea of Proof.

First of all, for the simple and not quite so interesting case
p=2
, we refer to
[
39
, Theorem 2.12]
; the rate function is then given by
x\mapsto-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-x^{2})
for
x\in(-1,1)
and is
\infty
otherwise.

So assume now that
p\in(2,\infty)
. Using the specific form of the
p
-Gaussian measures (see (
9
)), one easily checks that for
t_{0}<{1\over 2}
,
t_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
t_{2}<{1\over p}
,

\Phi_{p}(t_{0},t_{1},t_{2})=-\frac{1}{p}\log(1-pt_{2})-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-2t_{0})+\log\Bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\frac{1}{2}t_{1}^{2}(1-pt_{2})^{-2/p}(1-2t_{0})^{-1}y^{2}}\,\mu_{p}(\textup{d}y)\Bigg),

which is finite, since
p>2
. Thus, the effective domain
\mathscr{D}_{\Phi_{p}}=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{3}\,:\,\Phi_{p}(z)<\infty\}
of
\Phi_{p}
satisfies

\mathscr{D}_{\Phi_{p}}^{\circ}=\Big(-\infty,\frac{1}{2}\Big)\times\mathbb{R}\times\Big(-\infty,\frac{1}{p}\Big)\ni 0.

It now follows from a general version of Cramér’s theorem
[
27
, Theorem 2.2.30]
(which applies to the multivariate setting if
0
is an interior point of the effective domain of the cumulant generating function of the considered random vectors) that the sequence

S^{(n,p)}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(|Z_{i}^{(n)}|^{2},Y_{i}^{(n,p)}Z_{i}^{(n)},|Y_{i}^{(n,p)}|^{p}\big),\hskip 10.00002ptn\in\mathbb{N},

with

Y^{(n,p)}=(Y^{(n,p)}_{1},\dots,Y^{(n,p)}_{n})\text{ having iid coordinates with common distribution $\mu_{p}$}

and

Z^{(n)}=(Z^{(n)}_{1},\dots,Z^{(n)}_{n})\text{ being a vector of independent standard Gaussians},

satisfies an LDP in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with speed
n
and good rate function given by the Legendre transform
\Phi^{*}
; note that
\Phi_{p}
is the log-moment generating function of the summand in the definition of
S^{(n,p)}
. The desired LDP will now be contracted from the latter one via the continuous mapping
T_{p}:(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
T_{p}(\tau_{0},\tau_{1},\tau_{2}):=\tau_{0}^{-1/2}\tau_{1}\tau_{2}^{-1/p}
. More precisely, one readily checks that

T(S^{(n,p)})=\frac{n^{1/p}}{n^{1/2}}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y^{(n,p)}_{i}Z_{i}^{(n)}}{\|Y^{(n,p)}\|_{p}\|Z^{(n)}\|_{2}},

and so one obtains an LDP for the sequence
T(S^{(n,p)})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, with speed
n
and rate function
\rate_{p}
. Now it is only left to show that the LDP for the latter sequence carries over (with identical speed and rate) to our sequence
W^{(n,p)}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. For this we refer to
[
39
, Lemma 3.4]
.
∎

3
, Theorem 1.1]
for the case of high-dimensional projections discussed in the introduction.

3.1.2
Annealed LDP – the case
p\in[1,2)

As we shall see now, when we are in the case
p\in[1,2)
, the random projections display a completely different large deviations behavior. For
p\in[1,2)
, we define the function
\mathbb{J}_{p}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
via

\mathbb{J}_{p}(x):=\frac{1}{r_{p}}|x|^{r_{p}},

with
r_{p}:=\frac{2p}{2+p}
. It is important to note that
r_{p}<1
for
p<2
and so the following LDP occurs at a different speed
n^{r_{p}}
, which is slower than the speed
n
in the LDP for
p\geq 2
. The following result corresponds to
[
39
, Theorem 2.3]
.

Theorem B
.

Let
p\in[1,2)
. Then the sequence of random variables
(W^{(n,p)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{R}
with speed
n^{r_{p}}
and quasi-convex, symmetric, good rate function
\mathbb{J}_{p}
.

It is immediately clear that this result cannot be proved in the same way as the LDP in Theorem
A
, because for
p<2
,
\Phi_{p}(t_{0},t_{1},t_{2})=\infty
for
t_{1}\neq 0
; this already suggests that the LDP, if it exists, occurs at a different speed slower than
n
.

Idea of Proof of Theorem
B
.

In a first step, one proves that if
Y\sim\mu_{p}
and
Z\sim\mu_{2}
, and
Y
is independent of

, then it holds that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t^{r_{p}}}\log\big(\mathbb{P}[YZ\geq t]\big)=-\frac{1}{r_{p}};

(10)

to prove this, one uses the
p
-Gaussian tail bounds saying that, for any

\frac{x}{x^{p}+1}e^{-x^{p}/p}\leq\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{-y^{p}/p}\,\textup{d}y\leq\frac{1}{x^{p-1}}e^{-x^{p}/p}.

(11)

In the second step, one uses (
10
) together wit a result of M. A. Arcones on large deviations of empirical processes with non-standard rates
[
8
, Theorem 2.1]
(alternatively one may use
[
40
, Theorem 1]
on LDPs for random variables with stretched exponential tails) to show that the random sequence

V^{(n,p)}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}^{(n,p)}Z_{i}^{(n)},\hskip 10.00002ptn\in\mathbb{N},

with
Y^{(n,p)}=(Y^{(n,p)}_{1},\dots,Y^{(n,p)}_{n})
having iid coordinates with distribution
\mu_{p}
and
Z^{(n)}=(Z^{(n)}_{1},\dots,Z^{(n)}_{n})
being a vector of independent standard Gaussians, satisfies an LDP

and quasi-convex good rate function

.

In a third step, one proves that the sequence

\frac{n^{1/p}}{n^{1/2}}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y^{(n,p)}_{i}Z_{i}^{(n)}}{\|Y^{(n,p)}\|_{p}\|Z^{(n)}\|_{2}},\hskip 10.00002ptn\in\mathbb{N},

which we considered above in the proof of Theorem
A
, and
V^{(n,p)}
,

, are exponentially equivalent with speed

; thus they satisfy the same LDP. Again, as mentioned in the proof of Theorem
A
, the LDP now carries over to
(W^{(n,p)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with speed

and quasi-convex, symmetric, good rate function
\mathbb{J}_{p}
(see

3.2
Large deviations for
\ell_{q}
-norms of random vectors in
\ell_{p}
-balls

We now present some LDPs obtained by Z. Kabluchko, J. Prochno, and C. Thäle for
\ell_{q}^{n}
-norms of random vectors chosen uniformly at random from
\ell_{p}^{n}
-balls
[
63
]
; we shall skip the central and non-central limit theorems the authors obtained and focus on the large deviations part. As a motivation, let us start with the classical deviation results of G. Schechtman and J. Zinn
[
107
, Theorem 3]
(see
[
107
, Corollary 4]
for the normalized Lebesgue measure) and of A. Naor in
[
89
, Theorem 2]
. The authors proved that if
1\leq p<q<\infty
, then

\mathbb{P}\big[n^{1/p-1/q}\|Z\|_{q}>z\big]\leq e^{-cn^{p/q}z^{p}}

(12)

for all
z>T(p,q)
, with
c:=1/T(p,q)
. Here
Z
can be either uniformly distributed in
{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{n}
or distributed according to the cone measure on the boundary of
{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{n}
.

The authors of
[
63
]
obtained an LDP counterpart, i.e., an asymptotic version of (
12
). In fact, they identify
n^{p/q}
in the exponent on the right-hand side of (
12
) as the speed of the LDP and
z^{p}
as the asymptotically leading term of the rate function as
z\to\infty
. This LDP is in a sense optimal, and one can, contrary to
[
107
]
, identify the exact constant
1/p
in the exponent. It is important to note, however, that in general neither one of the results implies the other. Only for deviation parameters
z
from a fixed compact interval, the result of
[
63
]
is optimal and indeed stronger. The following result is based on large deviations techniques for sums of random variables with stretched exponential tails obtained in
[
40
]
(i.e., a generalized version of Theorem
2.2
above) and corresponds to
[
63
, Theorem 1.3]
.

Theorem C
.

Assume that
1\leq p<\infty
and, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
Z^{(n,p)}
be uniformly distributed on
{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{n}
. If
p<q<\infty
, then the sequence
\|{\bf Z}\|:=(n^{1/p-1/q}\|Z^{(n,p)}\|_{q})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP in
\mathbb{R}
with speed
n^{p/q}
and good rate function

\rate_{\bf\|Z\|}(z):=\begin{cases}{1\over p}\big(z^{q}-M_{p}(q)\big)^{p/q}&:z\geq M_{p}(q)^{1/q}\\
\infty&:\text{otherwise}\,,\end{cases}

where
M_{p}(q):=\frac{p^{q/p}}{q+1}\,\frac{\Gamma(1+\frac{q+1}{p})}{\Gamma(1+\frac{1}{p})}
.

Idea of Proof.

The case
p<q
is more delicate than
q\leq p
because of the lack of finite exponential moments. Thus, instead of Cramér’s theorem, one uses a version for random variables with heavy tails. More precisely, the following result due to N. Gantert, K. Ramanan, and F. Rembart
[
40
, Theorem 1]
(which is actually reformulated in a suitable way here) is used: let
X,X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
be non-negative iid random variables, and assume that there are constants
r\in(0,1)
,
t_{0}>0
, and slowly varying functions
c_{1},c_{2},b:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that

c_{1}(t)e^{-b(t)t^{r}}\leq\mathbb{P}[X\geq t]\leq c_{2}(t)e^{-b(t)t^{r}}\,,\hskip 20.00003ptt\geq t_{0}\,.

Then, the sequence of random variables
{1\over n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{R}
with speed
b(n)n^{r}
and good rate function

\rate(z)=\begin{cases}(x-\mathbb{E}X)^{r}&:z\geq\mathbb{E}X\\
\infty&:\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\sim\mu_{p}
be independent
p
-generalized Gaussians and define

S_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}|^{q}\,.

In a first step, the authors apply Cramér’s theorem for stretched exponentials from
[
40
]
to prove an LDP for the random sequence
{\bf S}:=(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. Using the
p
-Gaussian tail bounds from (
11
),
one can check that, for sufficiently large
z\in\mathbb{R}
,

c_{1}(z)e^{-b(z)z^{p/q}}\leq\mathbb{P}[|Y_{1}|^{q}\geq z]\leq c_{2}(z)e^{-b(z)z^{p/q}},

with appropriate slowly varying functions
c_{1},c_{2},b:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
, where
b(z)
satisfies
b(z)\to{1\over p}
, as
z\to\infty
. Thus,
\bf S
satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{R}
with speed
n^{p/q}
and good rate function

\rate_{\bf S}(z)=\begin{cases}{1\over p}(z-M_{p}(q))^{p/q}&:z\geq M_{p}(q)\\
+\infty&:\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

In a second step, one applies the contraction principle of Lemma
2.15
with the continuous function
F(z)=z^{1/q}
,
z\in\mathbb{R}
, to conclude that
(F(S_{n}))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=((S_{n})^{1/q})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{R}
with speed
n^{p/q}
and good rate function
\rate_{\bf\|Z\|}
from the statement of the theorem.

In a third and last step, one shows that
((S_{n})^{1/q})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is exponentially equivalent to the sequence
(n^{1/p-1/q}\|Z^{(n,p)}\|_{q})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of interest. Here one uses the Schechtman–Zinn probabilistic representation from (
8
) to obtain the distributional identity

n^{1/p-1/q}\|Z^{(n,p)}\|_{q}\overset{d}{=}U^{1/n}{\Big({1\over n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}|^{q}\Big)^{1/q}\over\Big({1\over n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}}\,.

Now, for fixed
\delta>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, one can show that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left[\,\left|\Big({1\over n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}|^{q}\Big)^{1/q}-U^{1/n}{\Big({1\over n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}|^{q}\Big)^{1/q}\over\Big({1\over n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}}\right|>\delta\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\bigg[\Big({1\over n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}|^{q}\Big)^{1/q}>{\delta\over\varepsilon}\bigg]+\mathbb{P}\big[U^{1/n}<\sqrt{1-\varepsilon}\,\big]+\mathbb{P}\bigg[{1\over n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}|^{p}>(1-\varepsilon)^{-p/2}\bigg]

\displaystyle\hskip 130.88284pt+\mathbb{P}\big[U^{1/n}>\sqrt{1+\varepsilon}\,\big]+\mathbb{P}\bigg[{1\over n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}|^{p}<(1+\varepsilon)^{-p/2}\bigg]\,.

From Cramér’s theorem
[
27
, Theorem 2.2.30]
and
[
39
, Lemma 3.3]
one deduces that the four last terms decay exponentially with speed
n
,
while the exponential asymptotic of the first term has already been determined above. As a consequence, and since
p/q<1
, one has that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}{1\over n^{p/q}}\log\mathbb{P}\left[\,\left|\Big({1\over n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}|^{q}\Big)^{1/q}-U^{1/n}{\Big({1\over n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}|^{q}\Big)^{1/q}\over\Big({1\over n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}}\right|>\delta\right]

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}{1\over n^{p/q}}\log\mathbb{P}\bigg[\Big({1\over n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}|Y_{i}|^{q}\Big)^{1/q}>{\delta\over\varepsilon}\bigg]\,,

which is
-{1\over p}(({\delta\over\varepsilon})^{q}-M_{p}(q))^{p/q}
if
\delta/\varepsilon\geq M_{p}(q)^{1/q}
and
-\infty
otherwise. Letting
\varepsilon\to 0
, one concludes that the above limit is equal to
-\infty
. Thus, the two sequences
(n^{1/p-1/q}\|Z^{(n,p)}\|_{q})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
((S_{n})^{1/q})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are exponentially equivalent, thus obeying the same LDP (see Lemma
2.17
).
∎

Let us now present the LDP in the regime
1\leq q\leq p<\infty
, which corresponds to
[
63
, Theorem 1.2]
. As already pointed out, in this case exponential moments exist and make the large deviations analysis rather straight forward, but we will also see that the two regimes exhibit a different behavior from a rate function and speed point of view. In particular, the form of the rate function already hints at an application of the large deviations result for pairs of independent random objects presented in Lemma
2.16
. Let us note that the case
p=q
is rather immediate (see
[
63
, Theorem 1.4]
) and so below we work with
q<p
; the special case
p=\infty
is treated in (see
[
63
, Theorem 1.5]
).

Theorem D
.

Assume that
1\leq p<\infty
and, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
Z^{(n,p)}
be uniformly distributed on
{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{n}
. If
1\leq q<p
, the sequence
\|{\bf Z}\|:=(n^{1/p-1/q}\|Z^{(n,p)}\|_{q})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP with speed
n
and good rate function

\ratej_{\|\textbf{Z }\|}(z):=\begin{cases}\inf\limits_{z=z_{1}z_{2}\atop z_{1},z_{2}\geq 0}[{\rate}_{1}(z_{1})+{\rate}_{2}(z_{2})]&:z\geq 0\\
\infty&:\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

Here

{\rate}_{1}(z):=\begin{cases}-\log z&:z\in(0,1]\\
\infty&:\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt\rate_{2}(z):=\begin{cases}\inf\limits_{x\geq 0,y>0\atop{x^{1/q}y^{-1/p}}=z}\Lambda^{*}(x,y)&:z\geq 0\\
\infty&:z<0\,,\end{cases}

where
\Lambda^{*}
is the Legendre transform of the function

\Lambda(t_{1},t_{2}):=\log\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{t_{1}|s|^{q}+t_{2}|s|^{p}}\frac{e^{-|s|^{p}/p}}{2p^{1/p}\Gamma(1+1/p)}\,\mathrm{d}s\,.

Idea of Proof.

First of all, one employs again the Schechtmann–Zinn probabilistic representation from (
8
) to obtain, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

n^{1/p-1/q}\|Z^{(n,p)}\|_{q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{d}}}{{=}}n^{1/p-1/q}U^{1/n}\frac{\|Y^{(n,p)}\|_{q}}{\|Y^{(n,p)}\|_{p}},

where
Y^{(n,p)}=(Y^{(n,p)}_{1},\ldots,Y^{(n,p)}_{n})
is a vector of iid
p
-generalized Gaussians, and
U
is an independent random variable uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
. Then, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, one defines the random quantities

W_{n}:=n^{1/p-1/q}\frac{\|Y^{(n,p)}\|_{q}}{\|Y^{(n,p)}\|_{p}}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptS_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(|Y_{i}^{(n,p)}|^{q},|Y_{i}^{(n,p)}|^{p}\big)\,.

Now, for
t=(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
,

\Lambda(t_{1},t_{2})=\log\Big(\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{\langle t,(|Y_{1}^{(n,p)}|^{q},|Y_{1}^{(n,p)}|^{p})\rangle}\Big]\Big)=\log\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{t_{1}s^{q}+(t_{2}-1/p)s^{p}}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}s}{2p^{1/p}\Gamma(1+1/p)}

is the log-moment generating function of
S_{n}
. Its effective domain is
\mathbb{R}\times(-\infty,1/p)
if
q<p
and
\{(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,:\,t_{1}+t_{2}<1/p\}
for
q=p
. Thus, by Cramér’s theorem
[
27
, Theorem 2.2.30]
, the sequence
{\bf S}:=(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with speed
n
and good rate function
\Lambda^{*}
being the Legendre transform of
\Lambda
. One can check that
\Lambda^{*}(t_{1},t_{2})=+\infty
if
t_{1}\leq 0
or
t_{2}\leq 0
. This implies that the sequence
{\bf S}
also satisfies an LDP on
[0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)
with the same good rate function
\Lambda^{*}
.

In a next step, one defines the continuous function

F:[0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 10.00002pt(x,y)\mapsto x^{1/q}y^{-1/p},

and notes that, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
W_{n}\overset{d}{=}F(S_{n})
. The contraction principle of Lemma
2.15
implies that the random sequence
{\bf W}:=(W_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{R}
with speed
n
and good rate function

\rate_{\bf W}(z)=\begin{cases}\inf\limits_{x\geq 0,y>0\atop{x^{1/q}y^{-1/p}}=z}\Lambda^{*}(x,y)&:z\geq 0\\
+\infty&:z<0\,.\end{cases}

In the third and last step, one considers the random variables
V_{n}:=(U^{1/n},W_{n})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and uses again the fact (see
[
39
, Lemma 3.3]
) that the sequence
{\bf U}:=(U^{1/n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{R}
with speed
n
and rate function

\rate_{\bf U}(z)=\begin{cases}-\log z&:z\in(0,1]\\
\infty&:\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

Now, since
U^{1/n}
and
W_{n}
are independent, the sequence
{\bf V}:=(V_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with speed
n
and good rate function

\rate_{\bf V}(z_{1},z_{2}):=\rate_{\bf U}(z_{1})+\rate_{\bf W}(z_{2})\,,\hskip 20.00003pt(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\,,

by Lemma
2.16
(see also
[
8
, Lemma 2.2]
). Applying once more the contraction principle, but this time with the continuous function

F:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
,
(x,y)\mapsto xy
, one concludes that the sequence
U^{1/n}W_{n}\overset{d}{=}n^{1/p-1/q}\|Z^{(n,p)}\|_{q}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, satisfies an LDP on
\mathbb{R}
with speed
n
and good rate function

\ratej_{\|\textbf{Z }\|}(z)=\inf_{z=z_{1}z_{2}}{\rate}_{\bf V}(z_{1},z_{2})=\begin{cases}\inf\limits_{z=z_{1}z_{2}\atop z_{1},z_{2}\geq 0}{\rate}_{\bf V}(z_{1},z_{2})&:z\geq 0\\
\infty&:\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

This completes the argument.
∎

3.3
Moderate deviations for
\ell_{q}
-norms of random vectors in
\ell_{p}
-balls

In
[
67
]
, Z. Kabluchko, J. Prochno, and C. Thäle complemented the LDPs presented above by MDPs (which occur on scalings between the one of the CLT and LDP). More precisely, we refer the reader to
[
67
, Theorem C]
, where the result is actually obtained for more general distributions on the
\ell_{p}^{n}
-balls as introduced by F. Barthe, O. Guédon, S. Mendelson, and A. Naor in
[
16
]
. We shall not sketch the proof of the MDP and instead refer to
[
67
]
directly.

Let us recall that an MDP is formally nothing else than an LDP, but with important differences in the behavior of the two principles. For instance, while LDPs provide estimates on the scale of a law of large numbers, MDPs describe the probabilities at scales between a law of large numbers and a distributional limit theorem (like a CLT). Moreover, while the rate function in an LDP depends in a subtle way on the distribution of the underlying random variables, the rate function in an MDP, in typical situations, is only parametric in the variance that occurs and is inherited from a CLT.
Let us recall that a sequence
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d\in\mathbb{N}
) satisfies an LDP with
s
peed
s_{n}
and good rate function
\rate:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty]
if and only if

\begin{split}-\inf_{x\in A^{\circ}}\rate(x)&\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}s_{n}^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}[X_{n}\in A]\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}s_{n}^{-1}\log\mathbb{P}[X_{n}\in A]\leq-\inf_{x\in\overline{A}}\rate(x)\end{split}

for all measurable
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. One says that a sequence
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an MDP if the speed sequence
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is given by
s_{n}=b_{n}\sqrt{n}
with a positive sequence
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying
b_{n}=\omega(1)
and
b_{n}=o(\sqrt{n})
, where for two sequences
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(y_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
we use the Landau notation
x_{n}=o(y_{n})
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{x_{n}}{y_{n}}=0
and
x_{n}=\omega(y_{n})
if
\lim_{n\to\infty}|\frac{x_{n}}{y_{n}}|=\infty
. We refer the reader to
[
27
, Chapter 3.7]
and especially part (c) of the remark after Theorem 3.7.1 for further details on terminology.

Theorem E
.

Let
0<p<\infty
and
0<q<\infty
with
q<p
. Let
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of positive real numbers satisfying
b_{n}=\omega(1)
and
b_{n}=o(\sqrt{n})
.
For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
Z^{(n,p)}
be uniformly distributed on

Then the sequence of random variables

\displaystyle{\sqrt{n}\over b_{n}}\bigg({n^{{1/p}-{1/q}}\over M_{p}(q)^{1/q}}\|Z^{(n,p)}\|_{q}-1\bigg),\hskip 20.00003ptn\in\mathbb{N},

satisfies an MDP with speed

and good rate function

,

\sigma^{2}:={1\over q^{2}}\bigg({\Gamma({1\over p})\Gamma({2q+1\over p})\over\Gamma({q+1\over p})^{2}}-1\bigg)-{1\over p}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptM_{p}(q):=\frac{p^{q/p}}{q+1}\,\frac{\Gamma(1+\frac{q+1}{p})}{\Gamma(1+\frac{1}{p})}.

\lim_{n\to\infty}{1\over b_{n}^{2}}\log\mathbb{P}\Bigg[{\sqrt{n}\over b_{n}}\bigg({n^{{1/p}-{1/q}}\over M_{p}(q)^{1/q}}\|Z^{(n,p)}\|_{q}-1\bigg)\geq t\Bigg]=-{t^{2}\over 2\sigma^{2}}.

Remark 3.3

Note that already for

3.4
Large deviations, moderate deviations, and the KLS conjecture

Arguably, the major open problem in asymptotic geometric analysis is the Kannan–Lovász–Simonovits (KLS) conjecture. Its origin lies in theoretical computer science, where it arose in the study of sampling algorithms for high-dimensional convex bodies. A specific situation of interest is to design an algorithm efficiently computing the volume of a
d
-dimensional convex body. Such an algorithm is given a convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a quality parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,\infty)
. The body
K
is represented by a membership oracle, which, for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, can decide whether or not
x\in K
. The algorithm returns a number
V:=V(K,\varepsilon)\in\mathbb{R}
such that
(1-\varepsilon)\mathrm{vol}_{d}(K)\leq V\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mathrm{vol}_{d}(K)
. The efficiency of the designed algorithm is measured in terms of the number of arithmetic operations and calls to the membership oracle.

While it is known through the works of I. Bárány and Z. Füredi
[
14
]
and of G. Elekes
[
32
]
that deterministic algorithms are inefficient, there are reasonable randomized algorithms that can accurately compute the volume of convex bodies with high probability in polynomial time (see, e.g., the work of M. Dyer, A. Frieze, and R. Kannan
[
30
]
). The construction of this randomized algorithm is connected to an isoperimetric inequality for log-concave probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(see
[
1
]
for an introduction to the KLS conjecture and
[
113
]
for a detailed explanation on its connection with problems in computer science). The constant appearing in this isoperimetric inequality, referred to as Cheeger’s constant, is of particular interest and directly linked to the KLS conjecture, which can be stated as follows (see
[
20
, Theorem 1.1]
):

KLS Conjecture.

There exists an absolute constant
C\in(0,\infty)
such that for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
, every centered random vector
X
with log-concave distribution, and any locally Lipschitz function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
f(X)
is of finite variance,

\mathrm{Var}[f(X)]\leq C\,\lambda_{X}^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\big[\|\nabla f(X)\|_{2}^{2}\big],

where
\lambda_{X}^{2}:=\sup_{\theta\in{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}}\mathbb{E}\langle X,\theta\rangle^{2}
.

Using the localization lemma, R. Kannan, L. Lovász, and M. Simonovits proved the conjecture with a factor
(\mathbb{E}\|X\|_{2})^{2}
instead of
\lambda_{X}^{2}
(see
[
69
]
), and later S. Bobkov
[
19
]
improved this estimate. The currently best known bound has recently been obtained by B. Klartag
[
78
]
, who showed that the conjecture in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
holds true up to a factor of
\sqrt{\log d}
.

Even though the bound has been improved considerably in recent years, it is not clear whether the conjecture is indeed true. In
[
5
]
, D. Alonso-Gutiérrez, J. Prochno, and C. Thäle established a new connection between the KLS conjecture and the study of LDPs and MDPs for isotropic log-concave random vectors, providing a potential route to disproving the conjecture.

As mentioned before, an MDP is nothing else than an LDP for a differently normalized sequence of random variables, i.e., if a sequence
(t_{n}^{-1}X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of random variables satisfies an LDP with some speed and some rate function, an LDP for the sequence of random variables
\tilde{t}_{n}^{-1}X_{n}
with
\tilde{t}_{n}\to\infty
and
\tilde{t}_{n}=o(t_{n})
is referred to as an MDP.

The following result corresponds to
[
5
, Theorem A]
and establishes a connection between the KLS conjecture and moderate/large deviations principles. Essentially, it is a consequence of the fact, known from
[
1
,
46
,
83
]
, that the KLS conjecture would imply a sharp concentration bound for the Euclidean norm of isotropic log-concave random vectors. Below, for two positive sequences of quantities, we write
\approx
to denote equivalence up to absolute constants.

Theorem F
.

Let
1\leq k_{n}\leq n
be a sequence of integers such that
k_{n}=\omega(1)
and let
(\xi_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of isotropic log-concave random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{k_{n}}
. Consider a sequence of random variables
X_{n}=\frac{\|\xi_{n}\|_{2}}{\sqrt{k_{n}}}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, which satisfies (
7
) with speed
s_{n}
and rate function
\rate
. Assume that one of the following two conditions is satisfied:

(a)

s_{n}=o(\sqrt{k_{n}})
and
\rate
is non-singular, i.e.,
\rate(x)\neq\rate_{0}(x):=\begin{cases}0&:x=1\\
\infty&:\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(b)

s_{n}\approx\sqrt{k_{n}}
and
\inf\limits_{t>t_{0}}\frac{\inf_{x\in(t,\infty)}\rate(x)}{t}=0
for some absolute constant
t_{0}\in(1,\infty)
.

Then the KLS conjecture is false.

For a sequence of random vectors
\xi_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{n}}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, as given in Theorem
F
, it is well known that
\mathbb{E}\|\xi_{n}\|_{2}/\sqrt{k_{n}}\to 1
, as
n\to\infty
. Against this light, the natural scale for a law of large numbers, and hence an LDP, is
\sqrt{k_{n}}
, while scales with
t_{n}=o(\sqrt{k_{n}})
and
t_{n}=\omega(1)
correspond to an MDP. Thus, Theorem
F
establishes a connection between LDPs or MDPs for isotropic log-concave random vectors and the KLS conjecture, showing that by constructing a sequence of random vectors
\xi_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
X_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, follows an LDP or MDP with an appropriate speed and/or rate function would disprove the variance and hence the KLS conjecture. Moreover, the authors show that (rescaled) crosspolytopes constitute a critical case in Theorem
F
as they just fail to satisfy condition (b) there.

Remark 3.4
.

Beyond the connection to the KLS conjecture, the authors of
[
5
]
prove a number of other results (see
[
5
, Theorems B and C]
), more precisely, they study moderate deviations for the Euclidean norm of random orthogonally projected random vectors in
\ell_{p}^{d}
-balls. This leads to a number of interesting observations: (1) for
p\geq 2
the rate function in the MDP undergoes a phase transition, depending on whether the scaling is below the square-root of the subspace dimensions or comparable; (2) for

and comparable subspace dimensions, the rate function again displays a phase transition depending on its growth relative to

4
Large deviations results and techniques in the world of Schatten classes

The Schatten
p
-class
\mathcal{S}_{p}
(
0<p\leq\infty
) is the collection of all compact operators between Hilbert spaces for which the sequence of their singular values belongs to the sequence space
\ell_{p}
, including the important cases of the nuclear or trace class operators (
p=1
) and Hilbert–Schmidt operators (
p=2
). Having been introduced by R. Schatten in
[
103
, Chapter 6, pp. 71]
, back then referring to a compact operator as a completely continuous one, they are among the most prominent unitary operator ideals studied in functional analysis today. In fact, he worked in the more general setting of symmetric gauge functions and the unitarily invariant crossnorms on the subalgebra of finite rank operators generated by them.
The very origin of Schatten’s work can be traced back to his paper
[
102
]
and the subsequent works
[
104
,
105
]
with J. von Neumann, studying nuclear operators on Hilbert spaces; on Banach spaces this had later been considered by A. F. Ruston
[
100
]
and on locally convex spaces by A. Grothendieck
[
48
]
. Before Schatten’s monograph appeared,
spaces of completely continuous operators
had
received comparably little attention in the literature. Today, Schatten classes provide the mathematical framework for modern applied mathematics around low-rank matrix recovery (the non-commutative analogue to the classical compressed sensing approach) and completion (see, e.g.,
[
23
,
24
,
36
,
81
,
99
]
), which is only one reason for the increased interest in their structure in recent years. The Schatten class
\mathcal{S}_{p}
may be considered a non-commutative version of the classical
\ell_{p}
sequence space and both share various structural characteristics, but while there are several similarities on different levels, there are also many differences in their analytic, geometric, and probabilistic behavior. In fact, there are several situations in which the matrix spaces are harder to handle and where arguments are considerably more delicate and complicated.

From both the local and the global point of view the study of Schatten classes has a long tradition in geometric functional analysis and their structure has been investigated intensively in the past 50 years. For instance, Y. Gordon and D. R. Lewis proved that
\mathcal{S}_{p}
for
p\neq 2
fails to have local unconditional structure and therefore does not have an unconditional basis
[
45
]
. This answered a question of S. Kwapień and A. Pełczyński, who had previously shown in
[
82
]
that the Schatten trace class
\mathcal{S}_{1}
(naturally identified with
\ell_{2}\otimes_{\pi}\ell_{2}
) as well as
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}
are not isomorphic to subspaces with an unconditional basis.
In 1974, N. Tomczak-Jaegermann succeeded in
[
109
]
to prove that
\mathcal{S}_{1}
has Rademacher cotype
2
. More recently, H. König, M. Meyer, and A. Pajor obtained in
[
79
]
that the isotropic constants of the unit balls in
\mathcal{S}_{p}^{N}
are bounded above by absolute constants for all
1\leq p\leq\infty
. The concentration of mass properties of unit balls in the Schatten
p
-classes were studied by O. Guédon and G. Paouris in
[
49
]
and J. Radke and B.-H. Vritsiou were able to prove the thin-shell conjecture for
\mathcal{S}_{\infty}^{N}

[
97
]
. In
[
53
]
, A. Hinrichs, J. Prochno, and J. Vybíral succeeded in determining the asymptotic behavior of entropy numbers for natural identities
\mathcal{S}_{p}^{N}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{q}^{N}
up to absolute constants for all
0<p,q\leq\infty
and in
[
54
]
studied the sequences of Gelfand numbers for natural embeddings
\mathcal{S}_{p}^{N}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{q}^{N}
of Schatten classes, providing asymptotically sharp bounds (up to constants depending on
p
and
q
) for almost all regimes, thereby extending classical results for finite-dimensional
\ell_{p}
sequence spaces by E. D. Gluskin
[
43
,
44
]
and A. Y. Garnaev and E. D. Gluskin
[

to the non-commutative setting. Their results have then been complemented by J. Prochno and M. Strzelecki in
[

. In a series of papers, Z. Kabluchko, J. Prochno, and C. Thäle computed the precise asymptotic volume and the volume ratio for Schatten

-classes for
0<p\leq\infty

and proved a Schechtman–Schmuckenschläger type result for the volume of intersections of unit balls

Given that there had already been obtained a number of large deviations results for

4.3
Sanov-type LDPs for random matrices in Schatten unit balls

In
[
66
]
the authors study principles of large deviations in the non-commutative framework of self-adjoint and non self-adjoint Schatten
p
-classes. Let us note that an LDP for the law of the spectral measure of a Gaussian Wigner matrix had already been obtained by G. Ben Arous and A. Guionnet
[
18
, Theorem 1.1 and Theorem 1.3]
and a more general large deviations theorem for random measures (including the case of the empirical eigenvalue distribution of Wishart matrices) had been obtained by F. Hiai and D. Petz in
[
50
, Theorem 1]
(see also
[
52
]
), which followed their preceding ideas from
[
51
]
, where the empirical eigenvalue distribution of suitably distributed random unitary matrices was shown to satisfies an LDP as the matrix size goes to infinity. In the same spirit, Z. Kabluchko, J. Prochno, and C. Thäle obtained Sanov-type LDPs for the spectral measure of
n^{1/p}
multiples of random matrices chosen uniformly (or with respect to the cone measure on the boundary) from the unit balls of self-adjoint and non self-adjoint Schatten
p
-classes where
0<p\leq\infty
. While the proofs roughly follow a classical strategy in large deviations theory (see, e.g.,
[
6
,
18
,
50
,
51
]
), in the Schatten class case one needs to control simultaneously the deviations of the empirical measures
and
their
p
-th moments towards arbitrary small balls in the product topology of the weak topology on the space of probability measures and the standard topology on
\mathbb{R}
in the self-adjoint or
\mathbb{R}_{+}
in the non self-adjoint set-up, respectively, and then prove exponential tightness. A key element is the probabilistic representation for random points in the unit balls of classical matrix ensembles which we presented in the previous subsection (see (
4.2
) and (
4.2
)) and a non self-adjoint counterpart (see Proposition
4.2
). As we shall see, the good rate function governing the LDPs is essentially given by the logarithmic energy (which is remarkably the same as the negative of Voiculescu’s free entropy introduced in
[
115
]
) and, which is quite interesting, a perturbation by a constant, which is connected to the Ullman distribution. As a consequence of their LDPs, the authors obtained a law of large numbers and showed that the spectral measure converges weakly almost surely to the Ullman distribution.

4.3.3
Strategy of the proof

The proofs are quite technical and looking at any of the details is beyond the scope of this survey, but we shall take a glimpse at the general strategy of the proof of Theorem
G
(the non self-adjoint case follows the same strategy).

Step 1:

The authors prove an LDP for the sequence of pairs

\left(\nu_{n},\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|^{p}\nu_{n}(\textup{d}x)\right)=\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i,n}},\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i,n}|^{p}\right),\hskip 10.00002ptn\in\mathbb{N},

(15)

of empirical measures

\nu_{n}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i,n}}

of the vectors
X_{n}=(X_{1,n},\ldots,X_{n,n})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, introduced in (
13
), and empirical
p
th moments of these measures.
At first it seems natural to try to apply the contraction principle to the LDP for
\nu_{n}
(which is known to exist, e.g.,
[
52
, Theorem 5.4.3]
) with the mapping
\mu\mapsto(\mu,\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|^{p}\mu(\textup{d}x))
. However, this mapping is not continuous in the weak topology. While this may look like a mere technical issue, it is indeed not; one can check that the correct rate function for
(\nu_{n},\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|^{p}\nu_{n}(\textup{d}x))
does not coincide with what one would expect by a naive application of the contraction principle.

Step 2:
Using the contraction principle, the authors then derive an LDP for the sequence of random measures

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{n^{1/p}{X_{i,n}\over\|X\|_{p}}}.\hskip 20.00003ptn\in\mathbb{N},

This proves Theorem
G
in the case where
Z_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, are sampled according to the cone measure on
{\mathbb{S}}_{p,\beta}^{n-1}
.

Step 3:

For a uniform random variable
U
on
[0,1]
, the sequence
(U^{1/\ell})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
(recall that
\ell={\beta n(n-1)\over 2}+n
) satisfies an LDP with rate function

x\mapsto\begin{cases}-\frac{\beta}{2}\log x&:x\in(0,1]\cr\infty&:\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Applying the contraction principle, the authors then derive the LDP for the sequence of random measures

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{n^{1/p}U^{1/\ell}{X_{i,n}\over\|X_{n}\|_{p}}},\hskip 20.00003ptn\in\mathbb{N}.

This proves Theorem
G
in the case where
Z_{n}
,

, are distributed uniformly on the ball
{\mathbb{B}}_{p,\beta}^{n}
.

5
Large deviations results and techniques in the world of Orlicz spaces

Most of the large deviations results that have been obtained for quantities in geometric functional analysis belong to the framework of
\ell_{p}
-spaces (or their non-commutative Schatten
p
-class counterparts). As already explained in the introduction and in Section
3
, the reason for being able to obtain MDPs or LDPs in
\ell_{p}
-spaces is the existence of the Schechtman–Zinn probabilistic representation for random vectors chosen uniformly at random in
\ell_{p}^{n}
-balls (or chosen with respect to a generalized distribution as introduced by F. Barthe, O. Guédon, S. Mendelson, and A. Naor
[
16
]
).
It took quite a while to understand how to go beyond this setting and obtain large or moderate deviations results in the important class of Orlicz spaces, which form a natural generalization of
\ell_{p}
-spaces and belong to the class of
1
-symmetric Banach spaces; those spaces have first been introduced by W. Orlicz in 1932 and are of fundamental importance in real analysis, harmonic analysis, and functional analysis. The reason for difficulties in obtaining probabilistic results in Orlicz spaces was that in the world of Orlicz spaces there is no classical Schechtman–Zinn type probabilistic representation of the uniform distribution at our disposal. The key observation was that such a representation now involving appropriate Gibbs measures, is at least asymptotically available and this was derived by Z. Kabluchko and J. Prochno by means of maximum entropy considerations inspired by so-called energy constraints in statistical mechanics
[
60
]
. In particular, this sheds new light on the classical Schechtman–Zinn probabilistic representation. Before we dive into large deviations results for Orlicz spaces, we shall provide a formal definition and explain the heuristic idea as done in
[
60
, Section 1.2]
, which is of independent interest.

5.1
Orlicz spaces

A convex function
M:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be an Orlicz function if and only if
M(t)=M(-t)
,
M(0)=0
, and
M(t)>0
for
t\neq 0
.
The functional

\|(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\|_{M}:=\inf\left\{\rho>0\,:\,\sum_{i=1}^{d}M\Big(\frac{|x_{i}|}{\rho}\Big)\leq 1\right\}

is a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, known as Luxemburg norm, named after W. A. J. Luxemburg
[
86
]
. The Orlicz space
\ell_{M}^{d}
is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
equipped with the Luxemburg norm and we denote by

{\mathbb{B}}_{M}^{d}:=\Big\{x=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\|x\|_{M}\leq 1\Big\}

the unit ball in this space. Clearly those spaces generalize the classical
\ell_{p}^{d}
-spaces (just consider
M(t)=|t|^{p}
,
1\leq p<\infty
) and belong to the class of
1
-symmetric Banach spaces. One commonly just speaks of Orlicz functions, Orlicz norms, and Orlicz spaces; we refer to
[
80
]
for an introduction to the theory of Orlicz spaces.

It is a simple observation (see, e.g.,
[
60
, Lemma 2.1]
) that
{\mathbb{B}}_{M}^{d}
coincides with

B_{M}^{d}:=\Bigg\{x=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\sum_{i=1}^{d}M(x_{i})\leq 1\Bigg\},

which simplifies computations because one does not need to work with the infimum in the Luxemburg norm.

5.2
The maximum entropy principle & Gibbs measures

Let us explain here how the distributions that play a central role in the main results for Orlicz spaces obtained in
[
60
]
naturally appear through what is known as the maximum entropy principle. The idea behind this principle is that the probability distribution which best represents the current state of knowledge about a system is the one with largest entropy (in the physics sense). It seems that it has first been expounded by E. T. Jaynes in 1957
[
55
,
56
]
and he suggested a reinterpretation of statistical mechanics thereby connecting it to information theory.

Our argumentation below will not be mathematically rigorous, but provides a good heuristic to show how the desired Gibbs distributions appear in our setting. We follow
[
60
, Section 1.2]
and refer the interested reader to
[
98
]
,
[
27
, Section 7.3]
, and
[
34
, Section III]
for detailed expositions regarding micro-canonical and canonical ensembles.

Let us consider a sequence of iid random variables
Y_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, taking values in a Polish space
E
and having distribution
\lambda\in\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)
, where we recall that
\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)
is the space of probability measures on
E
equipped with the weak topology. For
d\in\mathbb{N}
, we shall denote by
L_{d}:=L_{d}^{Y}\in\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)
the empirical measure associated with the
Y_{i}
’s, i.e.,

L_{d}:=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{Y_{i}}.

This measure is thus a random probability measure. In the setting of Sanov’s theorem (see Section
2.3
above) we know that, as
d\to\infty
,
L_{d}\to\lambda
almost surely at an exponential rate. If we consider a set
C
of probability measures whose closure does not contain
\lambda
, then by the law of large numbers,
\mathbb{P}[L_{d}\in C]\to 0
as
d\to\infty
. The maximum entropy principle helps us to understand the case where we condition on the rare event that
L_{d}
remains in
C
. Roughly speaking and under certain assumptions,
L_{d}
converges to the element in the set
C
that minimizes the relative entropy
H(\cdot|\lambda)
with respect to the distribution
\lambda
, and so
maximizes
what is known as thermodynamic entropy.
Being a bit more precise, the maximum entropy principle states that if
C\subset\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)
is closed, convex, and satisfies

\inf_{\nu\in C}H(\nu|\lambda)=\inf_{\nu\in C^{\circ}}H(\nu|\lambda)<\infty,

where
C^{\circ}
denotes the interior of
C
, then there is a unique probability measure
\nu_{*}\in C
minimizing
H(\cdot|\lambda)
over the set
C
. Moreover, the conditional distributions of
L_{d}
converge weakly, as
d\to\infty
, to
\delta_{\nu_{*}}
, i.e.,

\lim_{d\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[L_{d}\in\cdot\,|\,L_{d}\in C]=\delta_{\nu_{*}}(\cdot)

in the weak topology on
\mathscr{M}_{1}(\mathscr{M}_{1}(E))
generated by
\mathscr{C}_{b}(\mathscr{M}_{1}(E))
. Furthermore, one can show that for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the conditional distribution of
Y_{k}
(conditioned on
L_{d}
being in
C
) converges weakly to the relative entropy minimizing measure
\nu_{*}
. An application of the maximum entropy principle now shows how a Gibbs measure arises as limiting distribution, which is exactly what happens in the case of Orlicz balls.

So let
\mathscr{H}:E\to\mathbb{R}
be a function, also referred to as Hamiltonian or energy, and consider the corresponding average energy
\overline{\mathscr{H}}_{d}:=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathscr{H}(Y_{i})
. Moreover, define for
R<\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[\mathscr{H}]
a set of probability measures

C:=\Big\{\nu\in\mathscr{M}_{1}(E)\,:\,\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[\mathscr{H}]\leq R\Big\}.

If the set
C
satisfies the assumptions of the maximum entropy principle, then there exists a unique probability measure
\mu_{*}\in C
minimizing the relative entropy
H(\cdot|\lambda)
over
C
. It is given as the following Gibbs measure at “inverse temperature”
\alpha_{*}>0
:

\mu_{*}(\textup{d}x)=\frac{e^{-\alpha_{*}\mathscr{H}(x)}}{\int_{E}e^{-\alpha_{*}\mathscr{H}(x)}\,\lambda(\textup{d}x)}\lambda(\textup{d}x),

where
\alpha_{*}
is such that
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{*}}[\mathscr{H}]=R
. So wrapping everything up, the maximum entropy principle says in this case that, for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
fixed,

\lim_{d\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big[Y_{k}\in\cdot\,\Big|\Big.\,\overline{\mathscr{H}}_{d}\leq R\Big]=\mu_{*}.

We shall now explain how this relates to our situation. As already pointed out, this derivation is not mathematically rigorous, one of the reasons being that in our setting
\lambda
is the Lebesgue measure, which is infinite. Let
M
be an Orlicz function and consider, for large
d
, “random variables”
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{d}
uniformly “distributed” according to the infinite Lebesgue measure
\lambda
. We are interested in the volume of the Orlicz ball

B_{M}^{d}(d):=\Bigg\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\sum_{i=1}^{d}M(x_{i})\leq d\Bigg\}.

Conditioning on
Y=(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{d})
being in
B_{M}^{d}(d)
yields the uniform distribution on
B_{M}^{d}(d)
, because for any measurable
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\mathbb{P}\Big[Y\in A\,\Big|\Big.\,Y\in B_{M}^{d}(d)\Big]=\frac{\mathbb{P}[Y\in A\cap B_{M}^{d}(d)]}{\mathbb{P}[Y\in B_{M}^{d}(d)]}=\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{d}(A\cap B_{M}^{d}(d))}{\mathrm{vol}_{d}(B_{M}^{d}(d))}.

Coming back to the maximum entropy principle, where
E=\mathbb{R}
, the Hamiltonian is given by the Orlicz function
M
, and
R=1
(which is smaller than
\mathbb{E}_{\lambda}[M]=\infty
), we have, for any fixed
k\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, that

\mathbb{P}\Big[Y_{k}\in\cdot\,\Big|\Big.\,\overline{\mathscr{H}}_{d}\leq 1\Big]=\mathbb{P}\Big[Y_{k}\in\cdot\,\Big|\Big.\,\sum_{i=1}^{d}M(Y_{i})\leq d\Big]\approx\mu_{*},

where
\alpha_{*}>0
is chosen such that
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{*}}[M]=1
. So, under the “energy constraint” that
Y
lies in an Orlicz ball, asymptotically the coordinates of
Y
follow a Gibbs distribution
\mu_{*}
. So when studying random vectors in Orlicz balls, then those Gibbs distributions provide the right probabilistic set-up for investigations; let us remark that a version of Sanov’s theorem with infinite underlying measure
\lambda
has been obtained in
[
13
]
.

5.3
Volumetric properties of Orlicz balls

As shown by Z. Kabluchko and J. Prochno in
[
60
]
, volumetric properties of unit balls in Orlicz spaces can be nicely accessed through large deviations techniques. We shall present some of the results obtained and present the idea behind their proof.

The first main result (see
[
60
, Theorem A]
) contains formulas for the asymptotic logarithmic volume of
B_{M}^{d}(dR)
and for its precise asymptotic volume. The reason is that the former result follows from an exponential tilting technique coupled with the classical central limit theorem (thus being in the spirit of the proof of Cramér’s theorem) and provides some structural insight, which is lost in the short proof of the precise asymptotic volume where ideas and results on sharp Cramér large deviations by V. V. Petrov
[
92
]
are employed; to be more precise, for determining the exact asymptotic volume, one use elements from the proof of
[
92
, Theorem 1]
.

Theorem J
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
R\in(0,\infty)
, and
M
be an Orlicz function. Then, as
d\to\infty
,

\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M}^{d}(dR)\big)^{1/d}\to e^{\varphi(\alpha_{*})-\alpha_{*}R},

i.e., on a logarithmic scale, we have

\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{1}{d}\log\,\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M}^{d}(dR)\big)=\varphi(\alpha_{*})-\alpha_{*}R,

and the precise asymptotic volume is given by

\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M}^{d}(dR)\big)\sim\frac{1}{|\alpha_{*}|\sqrt{2\pi d\,\sigma_{*}^{2}}}e^{d[\varphi(\alpha_{*})-\alpha_{*}R]},

where
\varphi:(-\infty,0)\to\mathbb{R}
is given by
\varphi(\alpha)=\log\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\alpha M(x)}\,\textup{d}x
,
\alpha_{*}<0
is chosen in such a way that
\varphi^{\prime}(\alpha_{*})=R
, and
\sigma_{*}^{2}:=\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\alpha_{*})
.

Idea of Proof.

1. Asymptotic volume radius.

The function
\varphi
from the statement is well-defined and twice continuously differentiable on
(-\infty,0)
with

\varphi^{\prime}(\alpha)=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}M(x)e^{\alpha M(x)}\,\textup{d}x}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\alpha M(x)}\,\textup{d}x}\,.

(16)

One can check that here exists a unique
\alpha_{*}:=\alpha_{*}(R)<0
such that
\varphi^{\prime}(\alpha_{*})=R
. Then one considers iid random variables
Z_{i}
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, with density

p(x)=e^{\alpha_{*}M(x)-\varphi(\alpha_{*})},\hskip 20.00003ptx\in\mathbb{R},

and a direct computation using (
16
) shows that
\mathbb{E}[M(Z_{1})]=R
and
\mathrm{Var}[M(Z_{1})]=\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\alpha_{*})>0
.
Then one considers a shifted version of those random variables, more precisely, the iid random variables
Y_{i}:=M(Z_{i})-R
,
i\in\mathbb{N}
, which satisfy
\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}]=0
and
\mathrm{Var}[Y_{1}]=\mathrm{Var}[M(Z_{1})]=\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\alpha_{*})=:\sigma_{*}^{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M}^{d}(dR)\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbbm{1}_{B_{M}^{d}(dR)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\,d\lambda^{d}(x_{1}\dots,x_{d})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbbm{1}_{B_{M}^{d}(dR)}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}M(x_{i})+d\varphi(\alpha_{*})}\prod_{i=1}^{d}p(x_{i})\,d\lambda(x_{1})\dots d\lambda(x_{d})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{B_{M}^{d}(dR)}(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d})e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}M(Z_{i})+d\varphi(\alpha_{*})}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}\leq 0\}}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}-d\alpha_{*}R+d\varphi(\alpha_{*})}\Big]

\displaystyle=e^{d\big(\varphi(\alpha_{*})-\alpha_{*}R\big)}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}\leq 0\}}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}}\Big].

This expression can be bounded from below and above using the central limit theorem. In fact, for every
c\in(0,\infty)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}\leq 0\big\}}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}}\Big]\geq\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{-c\sqrt{d}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}\leq 0\big\}}e^{c\alpha_{*}\sqrt{d}}\Big]=e^{c\alpha_{*}\sqrt{d}}\mathbb{P}\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}\in[-c,0]\Big],

where it was used that
-\alpha_{*}>0
, and so one has

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M}^{d}(dR)\big)\geq e^{d\big(\varphi(\alpha_{*})-\alpha_{*}R\big)}e^{c\alpha_{*}\sqrt{d}}\mathbb{P}\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}\in[-c,0]\Big].

By the central limit theorem,

\mathbb{P}\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}\in[-c,0]\Big]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathscr{N}(0,\sigma_{*}^{2})([0,c]).

Similar to the lower bound, one can obtain the upper bound, namely,

\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}\leq 0\big\}}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}}\Big]=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}\leq 0\big\}}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}}\Big]\leq\mathbb{P}\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}\in(-\infty,0]\Big]

and therefore,

\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M}^{d}(dR)\big)\leq e^{d\big(\varphi(\alpha_{*})-\alpha_{*}R\big)}\mathbb{P}\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}\in(-\infty,0]\Big].

The central limit theorem implies that

\mathbb{P}\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}\in(-\infty,0]\Big]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathscr{N}(0,\sigma_{*}^{2})((-\infty,0])=\frac{1}{2}.

Putting things together, for any
c\in(0,\infty)
,

e^{d\big(\varphi(\alpha_{*})-\alpha_{*}R\big)}e^{c\alpha_{*}\sqrt{d}}\mathbb{P}\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}\in[-c,0]\Big]\leq\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M}^{d}(dR)\big)\leq e^{d\big(\varphi(\alpha_{*})-\alpha_{*}R\big)}\mathbb{P}\Big[\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}\in(-\infty,0]\Big],

which implies the result taking the
d
-th root, letting
d\to\infty
, and using the central limit theorem.

2. Precise asymptotic volume.
As shown above, one has

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M}^{d}(dR)\big)

\displaystyle=e^{d\big(\varphi(\alpha_{*})-\alpha_{*}R\big)}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}\leq 0\}}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}}\Big],

(17)

where
Y_{i}:=M(Z_{i})-R
,
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
, with
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d}
independent and having Lebesgue density
p(x)=\exp(\alpha_{*}M(x)-\varphi(\alpha_{*}))
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, the distribution of
Y_{1}
is not concentrated on a lattice.
Recall also that
\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}]=0
and
\mathrm{Var}[Y_{1}]=\varphi^{\prime\prime}(\alpha_{*})=:\sigma_{*}^{2}
.
Let
\mu_{d}
be the distribution of
\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}
. Then one has

\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}\leq 0\}}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}}\Bigg]=\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-\alpha_{*}y}\,\mu_{d}(\textup{d}y).

The integral on the right-hand side is exactly the integral that appears in
[
92
, Equation 4.11]
, just with a different sign. As is demonstrated in Petrov’s proof using a Berry–Esseen argument, for
d\to\infty
, this integral can be evaluated (see
[
92
, Equation 4.19]
) as follows,

\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{-\alpha_{*}y}\,\mu_{d}(\textup{d}y)\sim\frac{1}{|\alpha_{*}|\sqrt{2\pi d\sigma_{*}^{2}}}.

Combining this with (
17
), one obtains that, as
d\to\infty
,

\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M}^{d}(dR)\big)\sim\frac{1}{|\alpha_{*}|\sqrt{2\pi d\,\sigma_{*}^{2}}}e^{d[\varphi(\alpha_{*})-\alpha_{*}R]},

which completes the proof.
∎

In their second main result (see
[
60
, Theorem B]
), the authors determine the asymptotic behavior of the volume of intersections of two Orlicz balls when the dimension tends to infinity. This generalizes a classical result of G. Schechtman and M. Schmuckenschläger
[
106
]
. For the mere fact that the quantities that appear in the following statement are all well-defined, we refer the reader directly to
[
60
]
.

Theorem K
.

Let
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
be two Orlicz functions and
R_{1},R_{2}\in(0,\infty)
. Consider

\varphi_{1}:=\varphi_{M_{1}}:(-\infty,0)\to\mathbb{R},\hskip 20.00003pt\varphi_{1}(\alpha)=\log\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\alpha M_{1}(x)}\,\textup{d}x

and choose
\alpha_{*}<0
such that
\varphi_{1}^{\prime}(\alpha_{*})=R_{1}
. Define the Gibbs density

p_{1}(x):=e^{\alpha_{*}M_{1}(x)-\varphi_{1}(\alpha_{*})},\hskip 20.00003ptx\in\mathbb{R}.

Then, we have

\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M_{1}}^{d}(dR_{1})\cap B_{M_{2}}^{d}(dR_{2})\big)}{\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M_{1}}^{d}(dR_{1})\big)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\begin{cases}0&:\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{2}(x)p_{1}(x)\,\textup{d}x>R_{2}\\
1&:\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{2}(x)p_{1}(x)\,\textup{d}x<R_{2}\,\,\text{and}\,\,\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{2}^{2}(x)p_{1}(x)\,\textup{d}x<\infty\end{cases}

and the speed of convergence to zero is exponential in
d
.

Idea of Proof.

Let
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d}
be iid random variables with distribution given by the density

p_{1}(x):=e^{\alpha_{*}M_{1}(x)-\varphi_{1}(\alpha_{*})},\hskip 20.00003ptx\in\mathbb{R},

where for
\alpha<0
, we have

\varphi_{1}(\alpha):=\log\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\alpha M_{1}(x)}\,\textup{d}x

and where
\alpha_{*}<0
is now chosen such that
\varphi_{1}^{\prime}(\alpha_{*})=R_{1}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M_{1}}^{d}(dR_{1})\cap B_{M_{2}}^{d}(dR_{2})\big)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbbm{1}_{B_{M_{1}}^{d}(dR_{1})}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\mathbbm{1}_{B_{M_{2}}^{d}(dR_{2})}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\,d\lambda^{d}(x_{1}\dots,x_{d})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbbm{1}_{B_{M_{1}}^{d}(dR_{1})}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\mathbbm{1}_{B_{M_{2}}^{d}(dR_{2})}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}M_{1}(x_{i})+d\varphi_{1}(\alpha_{*})}\prod_{i=1}^{d}p_{1}(x_{i})\,d\lambda(x_{1})\dots d\lambda(x_{d})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbbm{1}_{B_{M_{1}}^{d}(dR_{1})}(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d})\mathbbm{1}_{B_{M_{2}}^{d}(dR_{2})}(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{d})e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}M_{1}(Z_{i})+d\varphi_{1}(\alpha_{*})}\Big].

One now modifies the probabilistic argument seen before and defines random variables
Y_{1}^{(1)},\dots,Y_{d}^{(1)}
and
Y_{1}^{(2)},\dots,Y_{d}^{(2)}
letting

Y_{i}^{(1)}:=M_{1}(Z_{i})-R_{1}\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003ptY_{i}^{(2)}:=M_{2}(Z_{i})-\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{2}(x)p_{1}(x)\,\textup{d}x

for
i\in\{1,\dots,d\}
. Then
Y_{1}^{(1)},\dots,Y_{d}^{(1)}
are independent and also
Y_{1}^{(2)},\dots,Y_{d}^{(2)}
are independent. Moreover,
\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}^{(1)}]=0=\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}^{(2)}]
and
\mathrm{Var}[Y_{1}^{(1)}]=\varphi_{1}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha_{*})
while
\mathrm{Var}[Y_{1}^{(2)}]=\mathbb{E}[(Y_{1}^{(2)})^{2}]=\mathrm{Var}[M_{2}(Z_{1})]
. Using those transformations of the original random variables, one can write

\displaystyle\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M_{1}}^{d}(dR_{1})\cap B_{M_{2}}^{d}(dR_{2})\big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(1)}\leq 0\big\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(2)}\leq d[R_{2}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{2}(x)p_{1}(x)\,\textup{d}x]\big\}}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(1)}-d\alpha_{*}R_{1}+d\varphi_{1}(\alpha_{*})}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=e^{d[\varphi_{1}(\alpha_{*})-\alpha_{*}R_{1}]}\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(1)}\leq 0\big\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(2)}\leq d[R_{2}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{2}(x)p_{1}(x)\,\textup{d}x]\big\}}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(1)}}\Bigg].

The idea is that by the strong law of large numbers,

\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(2)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0,\hskip 20.00003pt\text{as }\,d\to\infty.

Thus, one can see that, as
d\to\infty
, the event

\Bigg\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(2)}\leq d\Big[R_{2}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{2}(x)p_{1}(x)\,\textup{d}x\Big]\Bigg\}

occurs with probability going to
0
(even exponentially fast) if
\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{2}(x)p_{1}(x)\,\textup{d}x>R_{2}
and probability going to
1
if

\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{2}(x)p_{1}(x)\,\textup{d}x<R_{2}
.

Consider the case
\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{2}(x)p_{1}(x)\,\textup{d}x>R_{2}
. To obtain an upper bound for the volume of the intersection one observes that

\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(1)}\leq 0\big\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(2)}\leq d[R_{2}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{2}(x)p_{1}(x)\,\textup{d}x]\big\}}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(1)}}\Bigg]\leq\mathbb{P}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(2)}\leq d[R_{2}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{2}(x)p_{1}(x)\,\textup{d}x]\Bigg],

which goes to
0
exponentially fast by Cramér’s theorem (see
[
27
]
) since
\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}^{(2)}]=0
and the negative exponential moments are finite for
Y_{1}^{(2)}
. It follows from the lower bound for
\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M_{1}}^{d}(dR_{1})\big)
in the proof of Theorem
J
that, as
d\to\infty
,

\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M_{1}}^{d}(dR_{1})\cap B_{M_{2}}^{d}(dR_{2})\big)}{\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M_{1}}^{d}(dR_{1})\big)}

(18)

goes to
0
at an exponential rate by Cramér’s theorem.

Next one considers the case
\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{2}(x)p_{1}(x)\,\textup{d}x<R_{2}
. Then

\eqref{eq:ratio intersection}=1-\frac{\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(1)}\leq 0\big\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(2)}>d[R_{2}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{2}(x)p_{1}(x)\,\textup{d}x]\big\}}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(1)}}\Bigg]}{\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(1)}\leq 0\big\}}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(1)}}\Bigg]}.

One needs to show that the quotient of expectations on the right-hand side goes to
0
. To this end, one observes that the expectation in the numerator can be estimated as follows,

\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(1)}\leq 0\big\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\big\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(2)}>d[R_{2}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{2}(x)p_{1}(x)\,\textup{d}x]\big\}}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(1)}}\Bigg]\leq\mathbb{P}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(2)}>d[R_{2}-\int_{\mathbb{R}}M_{2}(x)p_{1}(x)\,\textup{d}x]\Bigg],

which can be estimated above by
O(1/d)
via Chebyshev’s inequality. On the other hand, the proof of Theorem
J
shows that

\mathbb{E}\Bigg[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(1)}\leq 0\}}e^{-\alpha_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{d}Y_{i}^{(1)}}\Bigg]\sim\frac{1}{|\alpha_{*}|\sqrt{2\pi d\sigma_{*}^{2}}},

where
\sigma_{*}^{2}=\varphi_{1}^{\prime\prime}(\alpha_{*})
.
Therefore, one obtains that the ratio in (
18
) goes to
1
as
d\to\infty
, and thus one obtains the desired dichotomy.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

To obtain the phase transition in Theorem
K
, we have seen that it is enough to know the asymptotic logarithmic volume of Orlicz balls and their intersections. To deal with the critical case at the threshold, however, the precise asymptotics would have been needed. This was an open problem, which has recently been solved by L. Frühwirth and J. Prochno
[
38
]
. In fact, the authors proved (under some mild conditions on the Orlicz functions) a number of sharp concentration phenomena in Orlicz spaces, which imply that

\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M_{1}}^{d}(dR_{1})\cap B_{M_{2}}^{d}(dR_{2})\big)}{\mathrm{vol}_{d}\big(B_{M_{1}}^{d}(dR_{1})\big)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{1}{2}.

5.4
Sanov-type large deviations

We shall briefly discuss here another result concerning large deviations in the world of Orlicz spaces, namely a Sanov-type large deviations principle for the empirical measure of the coordinates of vectors chosen uniformly at random from Orlicz balls. This result has been obtained by L. Frühwirth and J. Prochno in
[
37
]
; for the conditional limit theorems that can be deduced from the level–2 large deviations result and also a more general version of the result presented below, we refer the reader directly to
[
37
]
.

Again, if
M:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is an Orlicz function and
\alpha\in(-\infty,0)
, we shall consider the corresponding Gibbs measure
\mu_{M,\alpha}
on the Borel sets of
\mathbb{R}
given by

\mu_{M,\alpha}(\textup{d}x):=\frac{e^{\alpha M(x)}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\alpha M(t)}\,\lambda(\textup{d}t)}\,\lambda(\textup{d}x).

For
\mu\in\mathscr{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
, we define the Orlicz-moment-mapping

\mathbb{M}_{M}(\mu):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}M(x)\mu(dx).

Theorem L
.

Let
M:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be an Orlicz function,
R\in(0,\infty)
, and consider a sequence of random vectors
X^{(d)}:=\big(X^{(d)}_{1},\ldots,X^{(d)}_{d}\big)\sim\mathrm{Unif}(B_{M}^{d}(dR))
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Then, there exists a unique
\alpha^{*}\in(-\infty,0)
such that the sequence of empirical measures

L_{d}:=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\delta_{X^{(d)}_{i}},\hskip 10.00002pt{}d\in\mathbb{N},

satisfies an LDP in
\mathscr{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
with the strictly convex good rate function
\mathbb{I}_{M}:\mathscr{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})\rightarrow[0,\infty]
, where

\mathbb{I}_{M}(\mu):=\begin{cases}H(\mu|\mu_{M,\alpha^{*}})+\alpha^{*}\big[\mathbb{M}_{M}(\mu)-R\big]&:\mathbb{M}_{M}(\mu)\leq R\\
\infty&:\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Remark 5.2
.

Very roughly speaking, in the proof of Theorem
L
one combines ideas from the proof of Theorem
J
with a general version of the Gärtner–Ellis theorem (we presented a classical version as Theorem
2.6
) that can be found, for instance, in
[
27
, Corollary 4.16.14]
. The latter reads as follows:
let
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of exponentially tight random variables on the locally convex Hausdorff topological vector space
\mathcal{E}
. Suppose the Gärtner–Ellis limit

\Lambda(\lambda):=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\log\mathbb{E}\Big[e^{n\lambda(\xi_{n})}\Big],\hskip 10.00002pt\lambda\in\mathcal{E}^{*},

exists in
\mathbb{R}
and is Gateaux-differentiable, where
\mathcal{E}^{*}
is the topological dual of
\mathcal{E}
. Then,
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP in
\mathcal{E}
with the convex good rate function
\Lambda^{*}:\mathcal{E}\rightarrow[0,\infty]
, given by

\Lambda^{*}(x):=\sup_{\lambda\in\mathcal{E}^{*}}\big[\lambda(x)-\Lambda(\lambda)\big].

Remark 5.3
.

Beyond the Sanov-type LDP on
\mathscr{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
endowed with the weak topology, the LDP can actually be obtained on generalized Wasserstein spaces, which is indeed essential for the proof of the conditional limit theorem in
[
37
]
.

6
Large deviations results under an asymptotic thin-shell condition

Let us close this survey with a wonderful paper by S. S. Kim, Y.-T. Liao, and K. Ramanan
[
73
]
that establishes LDPs under asymptotic thin-shell-type conditions. As already mentioned in the introduction, this can been seen as a large deviations counterpart to Klartags CLT for convex bodies. Whereas the CLT for convex bodies shows that fluctuations of most random projections of high-dimensional vectors satisfying a thin-shell condition can be characterized as almost Gaussian, the work
[
73
]
characterizes tail behavior (at
the level of annealed LDPs) for projections and their associated norms onto, possibly growing, random subspaces of high-dimensional random vectors satisfying an asymptotic thin-shell condition. In particular, the authors’ work unites disparate examples for LDPs in geometric functional analysis under a common framework, goeing beyond the specific setting of distributions on
\ell_{p}^{d}
-balls. Probably most important in this respect are the cases of LDPs for some general classes of Gibbs distribution with super-quadratic potentials and random vectors in super-quadratic Orlicz balls.

Before presenting some of the results in detail, let us be a bit more specific about the contributions of
[
73
]
. For any sequence of random vectors
(X^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
whose scaled Euclidean norms satisfy a certain LDP (denoted by Assumption
A
and its specific case Assumption
A^{*}
in
[
73
]
), the authors characterize
the tail behavior of the corresponding sequence of orthogonal projections of
X^{(n)}
onto a random

k_{n}
-dimensional basis,
k_{n}\leq n
, drawn with respect to the Haar measure on the Stiefel manifold
\mathbb{V}_{n,k_{n}}
of orthonormal
k_{n}
-frames in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, as the dimension
n\to\infty
with
k_{n}/n\to\lambda\in[0,1]
.
A slightly stronger version of the LDP Assumption A, where one uses that the good rate function in the LDP has a unique minimum, can be viewed as an asymptotic thin-shell condition, in the sense that it implies that, for all sufficiently large
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the random vector
X^{(n)}
satisfies the classical thin-shell condition
[
7
, Equation (1)]
(see the discussion in
[
73
, Section 2.1]
). It is important to note, however, that for growing subspaces, in contrast to CLT results where approximate Gaussian marginals (in bounded-Lipschitz distance) occur only for
k_{n}<2\log n/\log\log n

[
87
]
(or
k_{n}\sim n^{\alpha}
if one assumes additional regularity of
X^{(n)}
such as log-concavity
[
77
]
), the annealed LDPs indicate three crucial regimes for subspace dimensions
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, namely, constant, sublinear, and linear; the specific case of
\ell_{p}^{n}
-balls had been treated in
[
3
]
.

6.1
The crucial regimes and asymptotic thin-shell-type assumptions

Before presenting some of the main results of
[
73
]
, we introduce the mathematical set-up and the asymptotic-thin-shell-type conditions that are used. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider a random vector
X^{(n)}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, shall denote by
\id_{k}
the
k\times k
identity matrix. The Stiefel manifold of orthonormal
k
-frames in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
shall be denoted by
\mathbb{V}_{n,k}
and we recall that
\mathbb{V}_{n,k}=\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}\,:\,A^{T}A=\id_{k}\}
.

We are interested in orthogonal projections of vectors
X^{(n)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
onto random
k_{n}
-dimensional subspaces, where
1\leq k_{n}\leq n
. So for fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
1\leq k_{n}\leq n
, we let
A_{n,k_{n}}=(A_{n,k_{n}}(i,j))_{i,j=1}^{n,k_{n}}
be an
n\times k_{n}
random matrix drawn with respect to the Haar probability measure on
\mathbb{V}_{n,k_{n}}
; we assume this random matrix is independent of
X^{(n)}
and that the random objects are defined on the same probability space. The random matrix
A_{n,k_{n}}^{T}
then linearly projects
X^{(n)}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
onto a
k_{n}
-dimensional vector. The goal will be to analyze the large deviations behavior of the coordinates of
A_{n,k_{n}}^{T}X^{(n)}
(as
n\to\infty
) in the following three regimes: for a sequence
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
, we say

(1)
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is
constant
at
k\in\mathbb{N}
, denoted
k_{n}\equiv k
, if and only if
k_{n}=k
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
;

(2)
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

grows sub-linearly
, denoted
1\ll k_{n}\ll n
, if and only if
k_{n}\to\infty
and
\frac{k_{n}}{n}\to 0
;

(3)
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

grows linearly
with rate
\lambda\in(0,1]
, denoted
k_{n}\sim\lambda n
, if and only if
\frac{k_{n}}{n}\to\lambda
.

When the subspace dimensions
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are constant at
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then one clearly may investigate the large deviations behavior of the sequence
A_{n,k_{n}}^{T}X^{(n)}=A_{n,k}^{T}X^{(n)}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, of random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
. However, when the subspace dimensions
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
tend to infinity (as is the case in the other two regimes), then in order to be able to even pose a sensible LDP question, one must first embed the random sequence
(A_{n,k_{n}}^{T}X^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of vectors in changing dimensions into a common topological space. Now, since the law of
A_{n,k_{n}}
is invariant under permutation of its
k_{n}
columns (which means that the coordinates of
A_{n,k_{n}}^{T}X^{(n)}
are exchangeable), the essential distributional properties of the coordinates of the projection are encoded by the empirical measures of the coordinates of the projection. A suitable setting to prove LDPs is therefore the one of empirical measures of the coordinates of
A_{n,k_{n}}^{T}X^{(n)}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, more precisely,

L_{k_{n}}:=\frac{1}{k_{n}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{n}}\delta_{(A_{n,k_{n}}^{T}X^{(n)})_{j}},\hskip 20.00003ptn\in\mathbb{N}.

We can now present (some of) the thin-shell-type conditions the authors introduced:

Assumption A.
The random sequence
(\|X^{(n)}\|_{2}/\sqrt{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP in
\mathbb{R}
at speed
s_{n}
with good rate function
\mathbb{J}_{X}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty]
.

Assumption A
∗
.
The random sequence
(\|X^{(n)}\|_{2}/\sqrt{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP in
\mathbb{R}
at speed
n
with good rate function
\mathbb{J}_{X}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty]
.

Assumption B.
There exists a positive sequence
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}n/s_{n}=\infty
such that the random sequence
(\sqrt{s_{n}}\|X^{(n)}\|_{2}/n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP in
\mathbb{R}
at speed
s_{n}
with good rate function
\mathbb{J}_{X}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty]
.

Remark 6.1
.

Assumptions
A^{*}
and
B
are modifications of Assumption
A
, which are required to deal with different situations or regimes of subspace dimensions; in fact, the authors introduce another assumption, namely Assumption
C
, which is a refinement of Assumption
A
required in the sub-linear regime whenever Assumption
A^{*}
is not met, but we shall not discuss this here and refer to
[
73
, Subsection 2.3.1]
directly.

Remark 6.2
.

The specific case
s_{n}=n
, which is referred to as Assumption
A^{*}
, includes a number of distributions of interest in geometric functional analysis, e.g., certain types of product measures, uniform distributions on
n^{1/p}{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{n}
for
p\geq 2
or, more generally, uniform distributions on super-quadratic (scaled) Orlicz balls and classes of Gibbs distributions with super-quadratic potential.

This already hints at the fact that Assumption
A^{*}
is no longer met when looking at
n^{1/p}{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{n}
for
1\leq p<2
, which explains in part the appearance of the more general Assumption
A
; for more details we refer to
[
73
, Remark 2.3]
.

6.2
Main result in the constant regime

In the constant regime one requires the two assumptions Assumption
A^{*}
and Assumption
B
introduced above. The following theorem corresponds to
[
73
, Theorem 2.7]
.

Theorem M
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and assume that
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
satisfies
k_{n}\equiv k
. If either Assumption
A^{*}
or Assumption
B
with speed
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are met with a good rate function
\mathbb{J}_{X}
, then
(A^{T}_{n,k}X^{(n)}/\sqrt{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
at speed
(s_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with good rate function
\mathbb{I}_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to[0,\infty]
given by

\mathbb{I}_{k}(x):=\begin{cases}\inf_{c\in(0,1)}\Big[\mathbb{J}_{X}(\|x\|_{2}/c)-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-c^{2})\Big]&:\text{under Assumption $A^{*}$}\cr\inf_{c>0}\Big[\mathbb{J}_{X}(\|x\|_{2}/c)+\frac{c^{2}}{2}\Big]&:\text{under Assumption $B$}.\end{cases}

Let us continue with two remarks of which one sheds some light upon the specific form of the LDP just presented and the other presents an interesting consequence of the previous theorem.

Remark 6.3
.

The two parts in the rate functions essentially correspond to the radial component of the random sequence (which is what
\mathbb{J}_{X}
represents) and the angular component of the random sequence (which is what
-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-c^{2})
and
\frac{c^{2}}{2}
represent).

Remark 6.4
.

As a corollary to the previous theorem, the authors are also able to deduce LDPs for general
\ell_{q}
-(quasi)norms with
q\in(0,\infty)
and refer to
[
73
, Corollary 2.8]
, which is in fact a direct consequence of the contraction principle under the continuous mapping
\mathbb{R}^{k}\ni x\mapsto\|x\|_{q}
.

Idea of Proof of Theorem
M
under Assumption
A^{*}
.

In a first step, the authors show in
[
73
, Lemma 4.1]
that the top row of
A_{n,k}
,
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
k\leq n
, satisfies the distributional identity

A_{n,k}(1,\cdot):=\big(A_{n,k}(1,1),\ldots,A_{n,k}(1,k)\big)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\frac{(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{k})}{\|Z^{(n)}\|_{2}},

where
Z^{(n)}:=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for a sequence
(Z_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of iid standard Gaussians. This allows one to apply a result of F. Barthe, F. Gamboa, L. V. Lozada-Chang, and A. Rouault
[
15
, Theorem 3.4]
, which then shows that the random sequence
(A_{n,k}(1,\cdot))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP in
\mathbb{R}^{k}
at speed
n
with good rate function

J_{k}(y):=\begin{cases}-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\|y\|_{2}^{2})&:\|y\|_{2}\leq 1\cr\infty&:\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

From the independence of
(X^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(A_{n,k})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, and the assumption that
(\|X^{(n)}\|_{2}/\sqrt{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP at speed
n
with good rate function
\mathbb{J}_{X}
(Assumption
A^{*}
), the authors then deduce from
[
73
, Lemma 1.9]
(which is a more general version of Lemma
2.16
above, which under
A^{*}
is actually sufficient) that the random sequence of pairs
(A_{n,k}(1,\cdot),\|X^{(n)}\|_{2}/\sqrt{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP at speed
n
with good rate function
J_{k}(y)+\mathbb{J}_{X}(\alpha)
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
.

In a second step, one can now apply the contraction principle with the continuous mapping
\mathbb{R}^{k}\times[0,\infty)\ni(y,\alpha)\mapsto\alpha y\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
to show that the random sequence
(A^{T}_{n,k}X^{(n)}/\sqrt{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP at speed
n
with good rate function, which, for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
, is given by

\mathbb{I}_{k}(x):=\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{k},\,z\in\mathbb{R}}\Big\{-\frac{1}{2}\log(1-\|y\|^{2}_{2})+J_{X}(z)\,:\,x=yz,\,\|y\|_{2}\leq 1,\,z\geq 0\Big\}.

In a third and last step, it is only required to write this rate function in the appropriate form. Here one uses that without loss of generality the range of
z
in the infimum can be changed to
z>0
, the substitution
y=x/z
, and the fact that
\|y\|_{2}\leq 1
is equivalent to
\|x\|_{2}\leq z
, to show

\mathbb{I}_{k}(x)=\inf_{z\geq\|x\|_{2}}\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\log\Big(1-\frac{\|x\|^{2}_{2}}{z^{2}}\Big)+J_{X}(z)\Big]=\inf_{z>\|x\|_{2}}\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\log\Big(1-\frac{\|x\|^{2}_{2}}{z^{2}}\Big)+J_{X}(z)\Big].

Writing the latter in terms of
c=\|x\|_{2}/z
, one obtains the form of the good rate function as in the statement of the theorem.
∎

6.3
Main results in the sub-linear regime

In the sub-linear regime, when working with
(L_{k_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in spaces of probability measures, one requires Assumption
A
to obtain the desired LDP. When considering sequences of scaled Euclidean norms of the random projections, one requires Assumption
A^{*}
and what is referred to as Assumption
C
, but we shall neither introduce this assumption nor present the corresponding result
[
73
, Theorem 2.11]
here. The following theorem corresponds to
[
73
, Theorem 2.9]
and we shall write
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{q}(\mathbb{R})
for the subset of probability measures
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}
having finite
q
-th moments, i.e.,
\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|^{q}\,\nu(\textup{d}x)<\infty
. A sequence
(\nu_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{q}(\mathbb{R})
converges to a measure
\nu\in\mathcal{M}_{1}^{q}(\mathbb{R})
with respect to the
q
-Wasserstein topology if and only if the sequence converges weakly and the
q
-th moments converge as well; this topology can actually be metrized. For
\sigma>0
, we shall write
\mu_{2,\sigma}
for the Gaussian measure on
\mathbb{R}
with mean
0
and variance
\sigma^{2}
.

Theorem N
.

Let
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
be such that
1\ll k_{n}\ll n
and assume that Assumption
A
is satisfied with associated speed
s_{n}
and good rate function
\mathbb{J}_{X}
. In addition, suppose that
\mathbb{J}_{X}
has a unique minimum at
m>0
. Then, for every
q\in[1,2)
, the following hold:

(1) If
s_{n}\gg k_{n}
, then
(L_{k_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP in the space
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{q}(\mathbb{R})
at speed
k_{n}
with good rate function
\rate_{L,k_{n}}:\mathcal{M}_{1}^{q}(\mathbb{R})\to[0,\infty]
given by

\rate_{L,k_{n}}(\mu):=H(\mu|\mu_{2,m}).

(2) If
s_{n}=k_{n}
, then
(L_{k_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP in the space
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{q}(\mathbb{R})
at speed
k_{n}
with good rate function
\rate_{L,k_{n}}:\mathcal{M}_{1}^{q}(\mathbb{R})\to[0,\infty]
given by

\rate_{L,k_{n}}(\mu):=\inf_{c>0}\Big[H(\mu|\mu_{2,c})+\mathbb{J}_{X}(c)\Big].

(3) If
s_{n}\ll k_{n}
, then
(L_{k_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP in the space
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{q}(\mathbb{R})
at speed
k_{n}
with good rate function
\rate_{L,k_{n}}:\mathcal{M}_{1}^{q}(\mathbb{R})\to[0,\infty]
given by

\rate_{L,k_{n}}(\mu):=\begin{cases}\mathbb{J}_{X}(c)&:\mu=\mu_{2,c}\cr\infty&:\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We shall not present the idea of proof for this result and rather refer the reader to
[
73
, Subsection 4.3]
.

Remark 6.5
.

Let us note that, as in Theorem
M
, the rate functions that appear above can be decomposed into a radial component (represented by
\mathbb{J}_{X}
as a consequence of Assumption
A
), and an angular component from
A_{n,k_{n}}
(captured by the relative entropy term). Depending on the relative growth rate of
k_{n}
and
s_{n}
, different parts dominate the rate function and both terms are present only when
s_{n}=k_{n}
.

6.4
Main results in the linear regime

In the linear regime, when working with
(L_{k_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in spaces of probability measures or when considering sequences of scaled
\ell_{q}
-norms of the random projections, one requires Assumption
A
to obtain the desired LDPs.

Let us define the second-moment-mapping
\mathbb{M}_{2}:\mathcal{M}_{1}^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{M}_{2}(\nu):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|x|^{2}\,\nu(\textup{d}x)
. The following theorem corresponds
[
73
, Theorem 2.15]
.

Theorem O
.

Let
q\in[1,2)
,
(k_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
\lambda\in(0,1]
be such that
k_{n}\sim\lambda n
, and assume that Assumption
A
is satisfied with associated speed
s_{n}
and good rate function
\mathbb{J}_{X}
. Then
(L_{k_{n}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP in the space
\mathcal{M}_{1}^{q}(\mathbb{R})
at speed
s_{n}
and with the good rate functions
\rate_{L,\lambda}:\mathcal{M}_{1}^{q}(\mathbb{R})\to[0,\infty]
of the following form

(1) If
s_{n}=n
, then

\displaystyle\rate_{L,\lambda}(\mu)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{c>\sqrt{\lambda\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mu)}}\Bigg[\mathbb{J}_{X}(c)-\frac{1-\lambda}{2}\log\Big(1-\frac{\lambda\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mu)}{c^{2}}\Big)+\lambda\log(c)\Bigg]

\displaystyle-\lambda h(\nu)+\frac{\lambda}{2}\log(2\pi e)+\frac{1-\lambda}{2}\log(1-\lambda),

where
h(\nu):=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\log\Big(\frac{\textup{d}\nu}{\textup{d}x}\Big)\,\textup{d}\nu
denotes the entropy of
\nu
for those
\nu
with density, while
h(\nu):=-\infty
otherwise; we also use the convention
0\log 0:=0
.

(2) If
s_{n}\ll n
, then

\rate_{L,\lambda}(\mu):=\begin{cases}\mathbb{J}_{X}(c)&:\mu=\mu_{2,c}\cr\infty&:\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For the LDPs for sequences of
\ell_{q}
-norms of projected random vectors, we refer the reader to
[
73
, Theorem 2.16]
. Let us merely note here that for
q\in[1,2)
, the results follow directly from Theorem
O
via the contraction principle and that the cases for
q\geq 2
require a different approach (see
[
73
, Subsection 6.2]
).

6.5
Main examples satisfying asymptotic thin-shell-type assumptions

We shall now present some examples of sequences of random vectors
(X^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying some of the assumptions presented in Subsection
6.1
.

6.5.1
The case of product measures

Let us consider a sequence
(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of iid
\mathbb{R}
-valued random variables and
X^{(n)}:=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})
, where we suppose that

\Lambda_{X^{2}}(t):=\log\mathbb{E}\big[e^{tX_{1}^{2}}\big]<\infty

for all
t
in an open neighborhood around
0
. Then by Cramér’s theorem (e.g.,
[
27
, Theorem 2.2.1]
) the random sequence
(\|X^{(n)}\|_{2}^{2}/n)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP at speed
n
with good rate function
\Lambda_{X^{2}}^{*}
. Applying the contraction principle with the continuous mapping
x\mapsto\sqrt{x}
, we immediately see that Assumption
A^{*}
is satisfied with rate function

\mathbb{J}_{X}(x):=\begin{cases}\Lambda_{X^{2}}^{*}(x^{2})&:x\in[0,\infty)\cr\infty&:\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The existence of a unique minimizer
m=\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[|X_{1}|^{2}]}
of
\mathbb{J}_{X}
follows from the strong law of large numbers, namely
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}]=m^{2}
.

6.5.2
The case of
\ell_{p}^{n}
-balls

In what follows, we shall always denote by
X^{(n,p)}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
p>0
, a random vector chosen uniformly from
n^{1/p}{\mathbb{B}}_{p}^{n}
. A key element in computations and proofs is again the Schechtmann–Zinn probabilistic representation (see (
8
)) with an additional factor
n^{1/p}
because of the scaling of
\ell_{p}^{n}
-balls.

We start with
p\in[1,2)
. In this case, it already follows from Theorem
C
(i.e.,
[
63
, Theorem 1.3]
) that the random sequence
(\|X^{(n,p)}\|_{2}/\sqrt{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies an LDP at speed
n^{p/2}
and with respective rate function, i.e.,
(X^{(n,p)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies Assumption
A
with
s_{n}=n^{p/2}
and unique minimizer

m_{p}=\sqrt{M_{p}(2)}=\Bigg(\frac{p^{2/p}}{3}\,\frac{\Gamma(1+\frac{3}{p})}{\Gamma(1+\frac{1}{p})}\Bigg)^{1/2};

this means that Assumption
A^{*}
is not satisfied.

It is shown, however, in
[
73
, Proposition 3.3]
that Assumption
B
is satisfied. More precisely, the authors prove the following: for
p\in[1,2)
, the random sequence
(X^{(n,p)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies Assumption
B
with speed
s_{n}=n^{\frac{2p}{2+p}}
and the good rate function
\mathbb{J}_{X,p}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty]
given by

\mathbb{J}_{X,p}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{x^{p}}{p}&:x\geq 0\cr\infty&:x<0.\end{cases}

Let us note that a normalization with
1/p
in
p^{-1}x^{p}
is in a sense natural, since in that case we have Legendre-duality with
q^{-1}x^{q}
, where
q
is the Hölder-conjugate to
p
.

We refer to
[
73
, Subsection 3.2.2]
, specifically to
[
73
, Proposition 3.4]
and
[
73
, Theorem 3.5]
, for further examples in the case
p\in[1,2)
.

We now continue with the case
p\in[2,\infty)
, which is the rather easy one. In
[
73
, Proposition 3.7]
the authors prove the following: for
p\in[2,\infty)
, the random sequence
(X^{(n,p)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies Assumption
A^{*}
with speed
s_{n}=n
and good rate functions

\mathbb{J}_{X,2}(x):=\begin{cases}-\log(x)&:x\in(0,1]\cr\infty&:\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

for
p=2
and

\mathbb{J}_{X,p}(x):=\begin{cases}\inf_{y\geq x}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\log\Big(\frac{y^{2}}{x^{2}}\Big)+F^{*}_{p}(y)\Big]&:x\in(0,1)\cr\infty&:\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

for
p>2
, where

F^{*}_{p}(y)=\sup_{t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}}\Bigg[t_{1}y+t_{2}-\log\Bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{t_{1}x^{2}+t_{2}|x|^{p}}f_{p}(x)\,\textup{d}x\Bigg)\Bigg],\hskip 20.00003pty\in\mathbb{R}.

Recall that
f_{p}
denotes the
p
-Gaussian density as introduced in (
9
). The rate function
\mathbb{J}_{X,p}
in the LDP again has a unique minimizer at
m_{p}=\sqrt{M_{p}(2)}
. When
p=2
, then the LDP arises solely from the uniform distribution and follows from
[
39
, Lemma 3.3]
. For
p>2
it follows from the results of Z. Kabluchko, J. Prochno, and C. Thäle
[
63
, Theorems 1.1(a) and 1.2]
taking into account the different scaling.

There are a number of further results that can be obtained, for instance, for Euclidean norms of the random projections, and we refer the reader to
[
73
, Subsection 3.2.3]
directly, in particular to
[
73
, Remark 3.9]
.

6.5.3
The case of Orlicz balls

As a last example, we shall present the case of random vectors uniformly distributed on appropriately scaled Orlicz balls, where the defining Orlicz function
M
is assumed to be super-quadratic, meaning that

\frac{M(x)}{x^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle x\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\infty.

In what follows, we allow
M:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty]
and denote again by
\mathscr{D}_{M}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}\,:\,M(x)<\infty\}
the effective domain of
M
. We recall that

B_{M}^{n}(n):=\Bigg\{(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\,:\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}M(x_{i})\leq n\Bigg\}.

Further, let us use again the Orlicz-moment-mapping (which already appeared in the Sanov-type large deviations in Orlicz spaces)

\mathbb{M}_{M}:\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})\to[0,\infty),\hskip 10.00002pt\nu\mapsto\int_{\mathbb{R}}M(x)\,\nu(\textup{d}x)

for those probability measures
\nu
for which the integral is finite. For
\beta>0
, we work again with the probability measures

\mu_{M,\beta}\in\mathcal{M}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
given by

\mu_{M,\beta}(\textup{d}x):=\frac{e^{-\beta M(x)}}{\int_{\mathscr{D}_{M}}e^{-\beta M(y)}\,\lambda(\textup{d}y)}\lambda(\textup{d}x).

Moreover, we define the function

\mathcal{J}(u,v):=\sup_{s<0,\,t\in\mathbb{R}}\Bigg[su+tv-\log\Bigg(\int_{\mathscr{D}_{M}}e^{sM(x)+tx^{2}}\,\textup{d}x\Bigg)\Bigg],\hskip 10.00002ptu,v\in[0,\infty).

In
[
73
, Proposition 3.11]
, the authors prove the following result: if
M
is a super-quadratic Orlicz function and, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
X^{(n)}
is uniformly distributed in
B_{M}^{n}(n)
, then the random sequence
(X^{(n)})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies Assumption
A^{*}
with rate function

\mathbb{J}_{X,M}(z):=\mathcal{J}(1,z^{2})-\sup_{s<0}\Bigg[s-\log\Bigg(\int_{\mathscr{D}_{M}}e^{sM(x)}\,\textup{d}x\Bigg)\Bigg],\hskip 10.00002ptz\in[0,\infty),

which is
\infty
otherwise. Moreover, there exists a unique
\beta^{*}>0
such that
\mathbb{M}_{M}(\mu_{M,\beta^{*}})=1
and
\mathbb{J}_{X,M}
has unique minimizer at
m:=\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mu_{M,\beta^{*}})
, with
\mathbb{M}_{2}
being the second-moment-mapping.

Let us note that the result for super-quadratic Orlicz functions can also be used to deduce the result we presented for
\ell_{p}^{n}
-balls when
p>2
. To show this one chooses
M(x)=|x|^{p}
.
