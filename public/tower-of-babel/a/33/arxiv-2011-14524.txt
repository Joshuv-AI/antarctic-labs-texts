Weil-Chatelet Groups of Rational Elliptic Surfaces
Nadir Hajouji

Abstract
. We classify pairs ( S , γ ) (S,\gamma) , consisting of a rational elliptic surface S S and a Galois cover γ \gamma of the base, which satisfy a condition we call ℒ \mathcal{L} -stability. We explain how to use the theory of Mordell-Weil lattices to compute the kernel of the restriction maps of Weil-Chatelet groups for ℒ \mathcal{L} -stable pairs. We also prove results about the injectivity of restriction maps of Weil-Chatelet groups for some pairs which are not ℒ \mathcal{L} -stable.

Let

The Weil-Chatelet group

There are various reasons one may be interested in the Weil-Chatelet group of an elliptic curve:

•

The Weil-Chatelet group is usually introduced

as an “obstruction” to computing the Mordell-Weil group.

•

The Weil-Chatelet group is isomorphic to a quotient of the Brauer group of the elliptic curve, and the Weil-Chatelet group contains the Tate-Shafarevich group of the elliptic curve. Sometimes one is interested in
WC
because of its relationship to these groups.

•

However, the group is interesting in it’s own right - classes in the Weil-Chatelet group represent isomorphism classes of genus one curves without a rational point, and those genus one curves can be hard to study “directly”.

This work is motivated by the latter - we want to understand

because we want to be able to classify genus one fibrations without a section.

Unfortunately, the Weil-Chatelet group is
notoriously
difficult to compute.

2
2

2

In most cases, it is impossible to even describe the whole group, since it is not finitely generated.

In fact, the mere problem of computing a kernel

where

is a finite Galois extension,
can be hard to do in general, because it requires us to understand

as a

and there is no general algorithm for computing generators of the Mordell-Weil group.

However, there is one setting where Mordell-Weil groups can be computed without much trouble: when
E/K
is the generic fiber of a rational elliptic surfaces.

•

Right off the bat, we know that the Mordell-Weil group has rank at most 8, and in fact the torsion-free part of the Mordell-Weil group can be embedded into the
E_{8}
lattice.

•

We can determine the precise rank of

using the Shioda-Tate formula as soon as we know the isomorphism types
3

3

In fact, we only need to know the number of irreducible components in each fiber.
of the singular fibers in a minimal, smooth model of

.

•

In fact, it is easy to find an explicit generating set for the Mordell-Weil group of a rational elliptic surface - the group is generated by the sections of height at most 2, and one can classify all sections of height at most 2 using a computer algebra system.

The goal of this work is to exploit these ideas to better understand Weil-Chatelet groups of certain elliptic surfaces.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
K
be a field,
K^{\prime}/K
a cyclic Galois extension with Galois group
G=\left\langle\sigma\right\rangle
(where
|G|=n
, say),
E/K
be an elliptic curve and assume:

•

E(K)\cong\mathbb{Z}
as an abelian group.

•

E(K^{\prime})
is generated, as a

-module, by a single point

•

Tr(Q)
is a generator of

.

Then
WC(E/K)\to WC(E/K^{\prime})
is injective.

Proof.

Since
Q
generates

as a
\mathbb{Z}[G]

every element of

has the form:

\sum_{\sigma^{\ell}\in G}a_{\ell}(\sigma^{\ell}\cdot Q)\qquad(a_{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z})

Since

, we compute:

Tr\left(\sum_{\sigma^{\ell}\in G}a_{\ell}(\sigma^{\ell}\cdot Q)\right)=\sum a_{\ell}Tr(\sigma^{\ell}\cdot Q)=\sum a_{\ell}Tr(Q)=\left(\sum a_{\ell}\right)\cdot P

We know that
P
is not a torsion point., so
\sum_{\sigma^{\ell}\in G}a_{\ell}(\sigma^{\ell}\cdot Q)
has vanishing trace if and only if
\sum a_{\ell}=0
.

Now, fix a point

Let:

Q_{1}=\sum_{\sigma^{\ell}\in G}b_{\ell}(\sigma^{\ell}\cdot Q)

Then:

(\sigma-1)\cdot Q_{1}=\sum_{\sigma^{\ell}\in G}(b_{\ell+1}-b_{\ell})(\sigma^{\ell}\cdot Q)=\sum_{\sigma^{\ell}\in G}a_{\ell}(\sigma^{\ell}\cdot Q)=Q_{0}

This shows that every point

, which proves

This will allow us to determine when we have rank stability purely in terms of the fiber configuration and the the location of the ramification points in the next section.

Before discussing singular fibers of

3.1.

We want

5.1.
Semistable Surfaces

Let
S\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
be a semistable rational elliptic surface.
Note that
S
has at least 4 singular fibers.

Let
n_{0}
be the order of vanishing of
\Delta
at 0 and

n_{\infty}
the order of vanishing at
\infty
.
Let
n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{\ell}
be the order of vanishing of the discriminant over the remaining points in the discriminant locus.
Then:

n_{0}+n_{\infty}+\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}n_{i}=12\qquad n_{0},n_{\infty}\geq 0\qquad n_{1},n_{2},\ldots,n_{\ell}>0

Set
\varepsilon=2
if
n_{0},n_{\infty}
are both positive,
\varepsilon=1
if exactly one of
n_{0},n_{\infty}
is positive and
\varepsilon=0
if
n_{0}=n_{\infty}=0
.

•

The original elliptic surface has
\ell+\varepsilon
singular fibers.
After base changing, we have
p\ell+\varepsilon
singular fibers.

•

The rank of the trivial lattice before base changing is:

rankT=\sum m_{\nu}-1=12-(\ell+\varepsilon)

After base changing, the rank of the trivial lattice is:

rankT_{\gamma}=12p-(p\ell+\varepsilon)

•

Thus, we can compute the difference between
rankT_{\gamma}
and
rankT
:

\displaystyle rankT_{\gamma}-rankT

\displaystyle=(12p-(p\ell+\varepsilon))-(12-(\ell+\varepsilon))

\displaystyle=12(p-1)-(p-1)\ell-(p-1)\varepsilon

\displaystyle=(p-1)(12-\ell-\varepsilon)

Now, Shioda-Tate tells us:

\displaystyle rank\;E(K)+rankT=\rho(S)=10

\displaystyle rank\;E(K_{\gamma})+rankT_{\gamma}=\rho(S_{\gamma})\leq 10p

Subtracting the second equation from the first:

\displaystyle(rank\;E(K_{\gamma})-rankE(K))-(rankT_{\gamma}-rankT_{\gamma})=\rho(S_{\gamma})-\rho(S)\leq 10p-10

Rearranging, and using the previous computation, we obtain:

\displaystyle(rank\;E(K_{\gamma})-rankE(K))\leq 10(p-1)-(p-1)(12-\ell-\varepsilon)

Finally, we simplify the RHS to obtain:

(15)

(rank\;E(K_{\gamma})-rankE(K))\leq(p-1)(-2+\ell+\varepsilon)

Now, while we have a bound on
(rank\;E(K_{\gamma})-rankE(K))
,
the bound depends on
p
.
This means that we can’t prove rank stability by arguing that the jump in rank would exceed the bound on rank from Shioda-Tate.

We can, however, bound the dimension of the kernel
WC(E/K)\to WC(E/K_{\gamma})
as an
\mathbb{F}_{p}
vector space,
at least if the Mordell-Weil group after base changing is torsion-free.

Assume
E(K_{\gamma})
is torsion-free.
Let
\sigma
be a generator of the Galois group, and let
\mathcal{O}=\mathbb{Z}[e^{2\pi i/p}]
.
Then
E(K_{\gamma})=E(K)\oplus M
, for some regular
\mathbb{Z}[G]
-module
M
.
Let
\tau=\sum_{\sigma^{\ell}\in G}\sigma^{\ell}\in\mathbb{Z}[G]
, and
M_{\tau}\subset M
be the submodule of
M
annihilated by
\tau
.
Then
M_{\tau}
is an
\mathcal{O}
-module.

Let
M_{0}=(1-\sigma)M
. Note that
M_{0}\cap M^{G}=M_{0}\cap E(K)=0
, so the map
m\mapsto(1-\sigma)m
is injective. Thus,
rankM_{0}=rankM
as an abelian group.
Furthermore,
M_{0}\subset M_{\tau}
, so
M_{0}
is also a regular
\mathcal{O}
-module. The kernel of
WC(E/K)\to WC(E/K_{\gamma})
is isomorphic to
M_{\tau}/M_{0}
.
In order to bound the
\mathbb{F}_{p}
rank of the quotient, we need to bound the rank of
M_{\tau}
as an
\mathcal{O}
-module.

The rank of
M_{\tau}
as an
\mathcal{O}
-module is
(p-1)
times the rank of
M
as an abelian group. Furthermore, the rank of
M
as an abelian group is
rank(E(K_{\gamma}))-rank(E(K))
.
Thus, we can apply (
15
) to deduce that the rank of
M_{\tau}
as an
\mathcal{O}
-module is bounded above by
-2+\ell+\varepsilon
, with equality only if
\rho(S_{\gamma})=12p=h^{1,1}(S_{\gamma})
.

5.2.
Delsartes and Fastenberg Surfaces

To understand
WC
-kernels in general, we need a better understanding of Mordell-Weil groups of general elliptic surfaces.
At the very least, we need some way of bounding the rank of the Mordell-Weil group
after
base changing.

•

For rational elliptic surfaces, we really just needed to know the fiber configuration to determine the exact rank of the Mordell-Weil group.

•

Mordell-Weil groups of K3 surfaces are not as predictable as those of rational elliptic surfaces,
but they are much better understood than Mordell-Weil groups of general elliptic surfaces. We have a uniform bound on the rank, but the Mordell-Weil rank is no longer determined by the fiber configuration alone, and we have no analog of the Generator Theorem (8.33 in
[
8
]
).

For elliptic surfaces of Kodaira dimension 1,
we only have comparable results for special families.

A Delsartes elliptic surface over
k
is an elliptic surface over
\mathbb{P}^{1}
whose generic fiber
is given by an equation of the form:

y^{2}=x^{3}+at^{m}x+bt^{n}

where
a,b\in k
and
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
.
If
S\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
is a Delsartes surface and
\gamma:\mathbb{P}^{1}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
is a Galois cover branched over
0,\infty
, then the base
S_{\gamma}\to\mathbb{P}^{1}
is also a Delsartes surface.

Furthermore, if
E/k(t)
is the generic fiber of a Delsartes surface, then rank
E(k(t))
is bounded above by 68.
The bound,is sharp, e.g. the elliptic surface:

y^{2}=x^{3}+t^{360}x+1

has rank 68.
Altogether, this gives us an upper bound on the
change
in rank after we base change.
On the other hand,
we know that the change in rank is a multiple of
p-1
.
Thus, we have rank stability if we base change by a Galois extension of index
p>68
.
See (
[
4
]
,
[
9
]
) for more on these surfaces.

Similarly, the families of elliptic surfaces studied in
[
2
]
,
[
1
]

are stable under base change.
Furthermore, for each of those families, there is a bound on the rank of the Mordell-Weil group which is independent of the degree of the base extension. Thus, we can argue as we did with the Delsartes surfaces to obtain rank stability for cyclic extensions of sufficiently large prime degree.
