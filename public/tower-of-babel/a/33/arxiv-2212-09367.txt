Ladder Symmetries of Black Holes and de Sitter Space: Love Numbers and Quasinormal Modes
Roman Berens, Lam Hui, Zimo Sun

Abstract
In this note, we present a synopsis of geometric symmetries for (spin 0) perturbations around (4D) black holes and de Sitter space. For black holes, we focus on static perturbations, for which the (exact) geometric symmetries have the group structure of SO(1,3). The generators consist of three spatial rotations, and three conformal Killing vectors obeying a special melodic condition. The static perturbation solutions form a unitary (principal series) representation of the group. The recently uncovered ladder symmetries follow from this representation structure; they explain the well-known vanishing of the black hole Love numbers. For dynamical perturbations around de Sitter space, the geometric symmetries are less surprising, following from the SO(1,4) isometry. As is known, the quasinormal solutions form a non-unitary representation of the isometry group. We provide explicit expressions for the ladder operators associated with this representation. In both cases, the ladder structures help connect the boundary condition at the horizon with that at infinity (black hole) or origin (de Sitter space), and they manifest as contiguous relations of the hypergeometric solutions.

A common theme in both stories is the connection of boundary conditions. In both cases, the relevant radial equation (after separation of variables) is a second order ordinary differential equation. Close to any boundary, there are in general two asymptotic behaviors. For instance, for the black hole, the static

As we shall see, the ladder structures help explain this single-asymptote behavior, i.e., a single asymptotic behavior at one boundary is connected with another single asymptotic behavior at a different boundary. At the level of the radial solutions for
\Phi

It is worth stressing that much of this paper is a review of known results. Our goal is thus a modest one: to view both the black hole Love numbers and the de Sitter quasinormal modes through the same geometric/ representation-theoretic lens. For black holes, the surprise, if there is one, is the relevance of melodic CKVs. For de Sitter space, there is less of a surprise since the symmetries arise straightforwardly from KVs. In both cases, organizing the solutions in a representation of the symmetry algebra is a helpful way of thinking about the ladder structures. For de Sitter space, we provide expressions for operators (some of them new) for climbing up and down the myriad ladders. In both cases, the ladder operators provide a simple way to understand the single-asymptote behavior of the solutions.

\displaystyle ds^{2}=dr^{2}+\Delta(d\theta^{2}+{\,\rm sin}^{2}\theta\,d\varphi^{2}),

The inner/outer horizons are at

(C)KVs, forming an

Three of these are the familiar KVs for rotation:

\displaystyle J_{1}^{i}\partial_{i}

\displaystyle=-\sin\varphi\,\partial_{\theta}-\cos\varphi\cot\theta\,\partial_{\varphi}

\displaystyle J_{2}^{i}\partial_{i}

\displaystyle=\cos\varphi\,\partial_{\theta}-\cot\theta\sin\varphi\,\partial_{\varphi}

\displaystyle J_{3}^{i}\partial_{i}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\varphi}.

(2.2)

The remaining seven—CKVs—do not at first sight seem useful, since

Surprisingly, this naïve expectation is false. It turns out three CKVs are special:

\displaystyle K_{1}^{i}\partial_{i}

\displaystyle=\Delta(r)\sin\theta\cos\varphi\,\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{\prime}(r)\left(-\cos\theta\cos\varphi\,\partial_{\theta}+\csc\theta\sin\varphi\,\partial_{\varphi}\right)

\displaystyle K_{2}^{i}\partial_{i}

\displaystyle=\Delta(r)\sin\theta\sin\varphi\,\partial_{r}-\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{\prime}(r)\left(\cos\theta\sin\varphi\,\partial_{\theta}+\csc\theta\cos\varphi\,\partial_{\varphi}\right)

For the case at hand,
\nabla_{i}

It can be shown that a melodic CKV generates a symmetry for a massless scalar, even if the scalar
is not conformally coupled. The symmetry transformation it effects is:

\delta_{X}\Phi=X^{i}\nabla_{i}\Phi+\frac{d-2}{2d}\nabla_{i}X^{i}\Phi,

A proof is provided in the following inset.

Consider the following action for

\Phi

d

S=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{d}x\sqrt{|g|}\,\left(\Phi\Box\Phi-\xi R\Phi^{2}\right),

where the coupling
\xi

It can be shown that under the transformation

\delta_{X}\Phi=X^{i}\nabla_{i}\Phi+\frac{d-2}{2d}\nabla_{i}X^{i}\Phi,

(2.7)

where
X
is a CKV, the action transforms as

\displaystyle\delta_{X}S

\displaystyle=\int d^{d}x\sqrt{|g|}\left(\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{i}\left(X^{i}\Phi\Box\Phi-\xi X^{i}R\Phi^{2}\right)-\frac{d-1}{d}\left(\xi-\xi_{c}\right)\left(\Box\nabla_{i}X^{i}\right)\Phi^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{d-1}{d}\left(\xi-\xi_{c}\right)\int d^{d}x\sqrt{|g|}\,\left(\Box\nabla_{i}X^{i}\right)\Phi^{2},

(2.9)

where we have dropped the boundary term in the last line. Thus, the CKV

defines a symmetry of the action if
\xi=\xi_{c}
(conformal coupling), or if
\Box\nabla_{i}X^{i}=0

2.2
Ladder Structure and Love Numbers

To understand what the geometric symmetries have to say about the Love numbers (one for each
\ell
), we need to work in harmonic space, and
learn what the ladder structure tells us about the nature of the solutions
[
1
]
.

The equation of motion for the radial function
\phi_{\ell}
(
2.14
)
can be rewritten as

\displaystyle H_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}=0,\qquad H_{\ell}\equiv-\Delta\left(\partial_{r}\left(\Delta\partial_{r}\right)-\ell(\ell+1)\right).

(2.30)

Using
D^{+}_{\ell}
and
D^{-}_{\ell}
defined in (
2.19
), it can be shown that

H_{\ell+1}D_{\ell}^{+}=D_{\ell}^{+}H_{\ell},\qquad H_{\ell-1}D_{\ell}^{-}=D_{\ell}^{-}H_{\ell}.

(2.31)

Thus if
\phi_{\ell}
is a solution to the equation of motion for level
\ell
,
D^{\pm}_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}
is a
solution at level
\ell\pm 1
.
This reconfirms that
D^{\pm}_{\ell}
indeed act as raising and lowering operators.
Furthermore, these operators satisfy the relations:

\displaystyle D_{\ell+1}^{-}D_{\ell}^{+}-D_{\ell-1}^{+}D_{\ell}^{-}=\frac{2\ell+1}{4}\left(r_{S}^{2}-4r_{Q}^{2}\right)

(2.32)

\displaystyle H_{\ell}=D_{\ell+1}^{-}D_{\ell}^{+}-\frac{(\ell+1)^{2}}{4}\left(r_{S}^{2}-4r_{Q}^{2}\right)=D_{\ell-1}^{+}D_{\ell}^{-}-\frac{\ell^{2}}{4}\left(r_{S}^{2}-4r_{Q}^{2}\right).

(2.33)

The last expression implies that if
H_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}=0
, both
D^{-}_{\ell+1}D^{+}_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}
and
D^{+}_{\ell-1}D^{-}_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}
are proportional to
\phi_{\ell}
.
Note that the constant
r_{S}^{2}-4r_{Q}^{2}=(r_{+}-r_{-})^{2}
.

As explained above, the ladder structure originates from geometric symmetries.
There is another way to see that such a structure must be present: by recalling
contiguous relations of hypergeometric functions.
Observe that
\phi_{\ell}
obeys a second order ordinary differential equation with three regular singular points, at
r=r_{-}
,
r_{+}
, and
\infty
. Its solutions are hypergeometric functions
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b,c)
whose arguments are related to
\ell
. Contiguous relations relate hypergeometric functions with neighboring arguments, i.e., they take the form of some operator acting on
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b,c)
, giving another
{}_{2}F_{1}
with
a,b
, and/or
c
incremented by
1
, and by extension incrementing
\ell
by
1
also. Details can be found in
[
1
]
and summarized in Appendix
D
. From this point of view, it is not surprising that the ladder operators
D^{\pm}_{\ell}
exist.

How is the ladder structure helpful for the Love number problem? Let us recall what the
problem is. Consider what the equation of motion (
2.14
) tells us
about the asymptotic behavior of
\phi_{\ell}
at infinity and at the outer horizon.
As
r\rightarrow\infty
, it is straightforward to see that
\phi_{\ell}
goes as
r^{\ell}
or
1/r^{\ell+1}
. As
r\rightarrow r_{+}
,
\phi_{\ell}
goes as a constant or as
\ln[(r-r_{+})/r_{+}]
. The phenomenon of vanishing Love numbers has to do with the fact that the solution that is regular at the horizon (i.e., going as a constant as
r\rightarrow r_{+}
) has a purely
r^{\ell}
asymptotic at large
r
. The naïve expectation, that it has a mixture of “growing”
r^{\ell}
and “decaying”
1/r^{\ell+1}
at large
r
, does not hold. This is the surprise of the vanishing Love number, defined as the ratio of the decaying tail to the growing one. It is a problem of connecting asymptotic behaviors at two different boundaries: how come regularity at the horizon is connected with purely “growing” behavior at infinity?

Let us look at (
2.14
) and
(
2.33
). They tell us

H_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}=\left[D^{+}_{\ell-1}D^{-}_{\ell}-\frac{\ell^{2}}{4}(r_{S}^{2}-4r_{Q}^{2})\right]\phi_{\ell}=0

(2.34)

The constant term in
H_{\ell}
vanishes for
\ell=0
, i.e.,
H_{0}\phi_{0}=D^{+}_{-1}D^{-}_{0}\phi_{0}=0
. It suggests one possible solution is given by a
\phi_{0}
that is annihilated by
D^{-}_{0}
:

D^{-}_{0}\phi_{0}=0.

(2.35)

This is helpful, because we have turned a second order problem (i.e.,
H_{0}
involves two derivatives) into a first order one (i.e.,
D^{-}_{0}
involves only one derivative). Thus, imposing regularity at the horizon is no longer expected to yield generically an admixture of growing and decaying tail at infinity. Indeed,
D^{-}_{0}
is simply
\Delta\partial_{r}
, and
so
D^{-}_{0}\phi_{0}=0
implies
\phi_{0}
is a constant: this is clearly regular at the horizon and purely “growing” at infinity (i.e., no
1/r
tail). Moreover, starting from any solution
\phi_{0}
, it is simple to construct a solution at level
\ell
by climbing the ladder:

\phi_{\ell}\propto D^{+}_{\ell-1}\cdots D^{+}_{1}D^{+}_{0}\phi_{0}.

(2.36)

Plugging in the
\phi_{0}=
constant solution, and recalling the form of
D^{+}_{\ell}=(r^{2}-r_{S}r+r_{Q}^{2})\partial_{r}-(\ell+1)(r-r_{S}/2)
, it is
straightforward to see that the resulting
\phi_{\ell}
approaches a constant at the horizon and is purely growing, i.e., it has the
r^{\ell}
asymptotic behavior with no
1/r^{\ell+1}
tail (indeed, it cannot have any negative power tail).

Strictly speaking, the above argument for the vanishing of the Love numbers requires an extra comment. All we have shown is that there is a solution that is regular at the horizon and purely growing at infinity. How do we know there is not an independent solution that is also regular at the horizon but contains a decaying tail at infinity? We know that cannot happen, because an independent solution cannot also approach a constant at the horizon as its leading behavior, i.e., it must approach the other possible asymptotic behavior
\ln[(r-r_{+})/r_{+}]
and is irregular at the horizon.

\partial_{r}(\Delta\partial_{r}\phi_{0})=0

For any higher

to

(up to a constant). It is worth noting that actually carrying out the Noether procedure would yield

contribution.

3
de Sitter Ladders

We now turn our attention to de Sitter space. We shall work in the static patch, with the metric given by

ds^{2}=-\frac{\Delta}{r^{2}}\,dt^{2}+\frac{r^{2}}{\Delta}\,dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2},

(3.1)

where now
\Delta\equiv r^{2}\left(1-r^{2}/L^{2}\right)
. We consider a scalar field in de Sitter space with an arbitrary coupling
\xi
to the Ricci scalar. The equation of motion is

\left(\Box-\xi R\right)\Phi=0.

(3.2)

Since
R=\frac{d(d-1)}{L^{2}}
is a constant (with
d=4
in our case), one could also interpret
\xi R
as mass squared. The scalar is conformally coupled if
\xi=\xi_{c}\equiv\frac{d-2}{4(d-1)}
. It turns out to be more convenient to work with the parameter

\alpha\equiv\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}-\xi\,d(d-1).

(3.3)

Conformal coupling corresponds to
\alpha=\frac{1}{4}
, while masslessness corresponds to
\alpha=\frac{(d-1)^{2}}{4}
.

Let us emphasize again many of the results below are not new. The quasinormal spectrum was worked out in
[
26
]
, and its relation to the geometric symmetries was pointed out by
[
25
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
, though the expressions for some of the ladder operators, and the conserved charges (for the conformally coupled case), are new. The main motivation for including a discussion of de Sitter space is to emphasize the many similar features shared with the black hole problem. The way the ladder structures emerge out of representations is the same. And the way asymptotic behaviors at separate boundaries can be connected—relevant for Love numbers for black holes, and quasinormal modes for de Sitter space—is also the same.

2.4
) in a non-trivial way. Thus, we focus on the KVs. We shall comment on the extra symmetries that a conformally coupled scalar enjoys in Section
3.3

P_{3}
map solutions to solutions. Running an argument similar to the black hole case, we see that
E^{\pm}_{\gamma\ell}
effects
\ell\rightarrow\ell\pm 1
and

i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L\rightarrow i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L\pm 1
, and

F^{\pm}_{\gamma\ell}
effects
\ell\rightarrow\ell\mp 1
and

i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L\rightarrow i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L\pm 1
. Notice how the action of
E^{\pm}_{\gamma\ell}
can be alternatively described as effecting
\ell\rightarrow\ell\pm 1
while keeping
i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L-\ell
fixed.
This is what motivates the introduction of the symbol
\gamma
in (
3.7
), i.e.,
E^{\pm}_{\gamma\ell}
effects
\ell\rightarrow\ell\pm 1
without changing
\gamma

Is there a way to effect a shift in
i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L
, without changing
\ell
(or
m
)? The answer is yes, and unsurprisingly, it involves spherically symmetric combinations of the
K_{i}
and
P_{i}
:

\displaystyle\delta^{ij}\delta_{P_{i}}\delta_{P_{j}}\left(\phi_{\gamma\ell}Y_{\ell m}e^{-i\omega_{\gamma\ell}t}\right)

\displaystyle=-L^{2}G^{+}_{\gamma\ell}\phi_{\gamma\ell}Y_{\ell m}e^{-(i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L+2)t/L}

\displaystyle\delta^{ij}\delta_{K_{i}}\delta_{K_{j}}\left(\phi_{\gamma\ell}Y_{\ell m}e^{-i\omega_{\gamma\ell}t}\right)

\displaystyle=-L^{2}G^{-}_{\gamma\ell}\phi_{\gamma\ell}Y_{\ell m}e^{-(i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L-2)t/L},

(3.11)

where
G^{\pm}_{\gamma\ell}
is given by:

\displaystyle G^{+}_{\gamma\ell}\equiv E^{+}_{\gamma+2,\ell-1}F^{+}_{\gamma\ell},\qquad G^{-}_{\gamma\ell}\equiv E^{-}_{\gamma-2,\ell+1}F^{-}_{\gamma\ell}.

(3.12)

In other words,
\delta^{ij}\delta_{P_{i}}\delta_{P_{j}}

(or
G^{+}_{\gamma\ell})

effects
i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L\rightarrow i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L+2
, while
\delta^{ij}\delta_{K_{i}}\delta_{K_{j}}

(or
G^{-}_{\gamma\ell})
effects
i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L\rightarrow i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L-2
.
Equivalently, we can also say
G^{\pm}_{\gamma\ell}
effects
\gamma\rightarrow\gamma\pm 2
without changing
\ell
. As before, all of these ladders can also be seen as arising from contiguous relations for the hypergeometric solutions, which are shown in Appendix
D
.

With the above ladder operators defined, it is easy to see how one could construct a representation of
\mathfrak{so}(1,4)
.
Start from a solution labeled by
i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L
and
\ell
, or equivalently
\gamma
and
\ell
(we suppress
m
since incrementing it is straightforward using
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
); use
E^{\pm}_{\gamma\ell}
to increment
\ell
by
\pm 1
without changing
\gamma
, or use
G^{\pm}_{\gamma\ell}
to increment
\gamma
by
\pm 2
without changing
\ell
.
This way, we fill out a whole table of solutions, which we will also refer to as states.
So far, the only restriction is that
\ell\geq 0
, i.e., the table of
(\gamma,\ell)
has
\ell
taking all possible non-negative integer values, and
\gamma=\gamma_{0}+2n
for any integer
n
and some base value
\gamma_{0}
(with
\gamma_{0}
arbitrary for the moment). A further restriction on
\gamma
will come later when we discuss quasinormal modes. Incidentally, since
\gamma
in general has a non-zero real part, and therefore
i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L
as well, we have a non-unitary representation (e.g. the matrix representation for time-translation
D
is not anti-hermitian).

We close this subsection with a description of an economical method to build the entire representation of
\mathfrak{so}(1,4)
, given by
[

]

2.1
.) Taking
c^{\ell m}
to be the traceless symmetric tensor associated with

(

\displaystyle c^{\ell m}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{\ell}}\delta_{P_{i_{1}}}\cdots\delta_{P_{i_{\ell}}}\Phi_{\gamma 00}(t,r,\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle\propto\Phi_{\gamma\ell m}(t,r,\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle c^{\ell m}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{\ell}}\delta_{K_{i_{1}}}\cdots\delta_{K_{i_{\ell}}}\Phi_{\gamma 00}(t,r,\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle\propto\Phi_{\gamma-2\ell,\ell m}(t,r,\theta,\varphi),

(3.13)

up to some irrelevant normalization constant.
Proofs are once again in Appendix
I
.
In this language, (
3.11

\displaystyle\delta^{ij}\delta_{P_{i}}\delta_{P_{j}}\Phi_{\gamma\ell m}(t,r,\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle\propto\Phi_{\gamma+2,\ell m}(t,r,\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle\delta^{ij}\delta_{K_{i}}\delta_{K_{j}}\Phi_{\gamma\ell m}(t,r,\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle\propto\Phi_{\gamma-2,\ell m}(t,r,\theta,\varphi).

(3.14)

For further operators connecting different solutions, see
Appendix

.

3.2
Ladder Structures and Quasinormal Modes

The upshot of the last subsection is that de Sitter space has multiple ladders: besides a ladder in
\ell
, there is also a ladder in
\gamma
, defined as
i\omega L-\ell
.
(A ladder in
m
is present as well, but is no different from the familiar one of spherical harmonics.) Our next task is to learn what the ladder structures tell us about the nature of the quasinormal solutions.

After the separation of variables, the radial function
\phi_{\gamma\ell}(r)
obeys

\widetilde{H}_{\gamma\ell}\phi_{\gamma\ell}=0,\qquad\widetilde{H}_{\gamma\ell}\equiv-\frac{r^{2}}{L^{2}\Delta}\left(\partial_{r}\left(\Delta\partial_{r}\right)+\frac{\omega_{\gamma\ell}^{2}r^{4}}{\Delta}+\left(\alpha-\frac{9}{4}\right)\frac{r^{2}}{L^{2}}-\ell(\ell+1)\right).

(3.15)

The asymptotic behaviors of
\phi_{\gamma\ell}
are as follows:
\phi_{\gamma\ell}
goes as
r^{\ell}
or
1/r^{\ell+1}
as
r\rightarrow 0
;
\phi_{\gamma\ell}
goes as
\left(1-\frac{r^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)^{-i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L/2}
(outgoing at horizon) or
\left(1-\frac{r^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)^{i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L/2}
(incoming at horizon) as
r\rightarrow L
.
Linear combinations thereof are of course allowed. What is special about quasinormal modes is that they have such special frequencies that only a single asymptotic behavior is realized at each boundary. In particular, the behavior at the origin is purely regular
r^{\ell}
and the behavior at the horizon is purely outgoing
\left(1-\frac{r^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)^{-i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L/2}
.
This is reminiscent of the Love number problem in the case of the black hole: regularity at the horizon and purely growing behavior at infinity. Our task in this subsection is to understand this single-asymptote behavior for the quasinormal solutions.

Our strategy is similar to that in the black hole case (
2.33
): write the equation of motion operator
\widetilde{H}_{\gamma\ell}
as a product of raising and lowering operators plus a constant. We shall use
G^{\pm}_{\gamma\ell}
which raises or lowers
\gamma
by
2
while keeping
\ell
constant.
For other ways to rewrite the equation of motion, using
E^{\pm}_{\gamma\ell}
or
F^{\pm}_{\gamma\ell}
, see Appendix
G
.

The operators
G^{\pm}_{\gamma\ell}
defined in (
3.12
) are a bit cumbersome to use, because they involve second derivatives. They can be improved by subtracting off
\widetilde{H}_{\gamma\ell}
(or something proportional to it, keeping in mind that
the objective is to act on solutions, which are annihilated by
\widetilde{H}_{\gamma\ell}
):

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{+}_{\gamma\ell}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\gamma+\ell+1\right)^{-1}\left(G^{+}_{\gamma\ell}-\frac{L^{2}\Delta}{r^{4}}\widetilde{H}_{\gamma\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2r}{L^{2}}\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{L^{2}}\left(-\frac{2(\gamma+\ell)}{\Delta}r^{2}+\frac{(\gamma-1)(\gamma+2\ell)+\alpha-\frac{9}{4}}{(\gamma+\ell+1)}\right)

(3.16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\gamma\ell}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\gamma+\ell-1\right)^{-1}\left(G^{-}_{\gamma\ell}-\frac{L^{2}\Delta}{r^{4}}\widetilde{H}_{\gamma\ell}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2r}{L^{2}}\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{L^{2}}\left(-\frac{2(\gamma+\ell)}{\Delta}r^{2}+\frac{\gamma(\gamma+2\ell+1)+\alpha-\frac{9}{4}}{(\gamma+\ell-1)}\right).

(3.17)

It can be shown that

\widetilde{H}_{\gamma+2,\ell}\,\mathcal{G}^{+}_{\gamma\ell}=\mathcal{G}^{+}_{\gamma\ell}\,\widetilde{H}_{\gamma\ell},\qquad\widetilde{H}_{\gamma-2,\ell}\,\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\gamma\ell}=\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\gamma\ell}\,\widetilde{H}_{\gamma\ell}.

(3.18)

This reconfirms what we already know from geometric arguments, that given a solution
\phi_{\gamma\ell}
,
\mathcal{G}^{\pm}_{\gamma\ell}\phi_{\gamma\ell}
is a solution at level
\gamma\pm 2
and the same
\ell
.
20
20

20

It is also useful to note that

\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\gamma+2,\ell}\mathcal{G}^{+}_{\gamma\ell}-\mathcal{G}^{+}_{\gamma-2,\ell}\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\gamma\ell}=\frac{4}{L^{4}}\frac{(\gamma+\ell)\left(A_{\gamma\ell}+B_{\gamma\ell}\left(\alpha-\frac{9}{4}\right)-\left(\alpha-\frac{9}{4}\right)^{2}\right)}{(\gamma+\ell+1)^{2}(\gamma+\ell-1)^{2}},\\

\displaystyle A_{\gamma\ell}

\displaystyle\equiv\left(\gamma(\gamma+2\ell)-\ell+1\right)\left(\gamma(\gamma+2\ell)+(2\ell+3)(\ell-1)\right),

\displaystyle B_{\gamma\ell}

\displaystyle\equiv 2(\ell-1)(\ell+2).

(3.19)

The raising and lowering operators above are the same as those in Section 3.2 of
[
25
]
.

With this setup, we are ready to rewrite
\widetilde{H}_{\gamma\ell}
in terms of
\mathcal{G}^{\pm}_{\gamma\ell}
:

\widetilde{H}_{\gamma\ell}=\frac{L^{2}}{4}\left(\mathcal{G}^{+}_{\gamma-2,\ell}\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\gamma\ell}-\frac{1}{L^{4}}\frac{a_{\gamma\ell}b_{\gamma\ell}}{\left(\gamma+\ell-1\right)^{2}}\right)=\frac{L^{2}}{4}\left(\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\gamma+2,\ell}\mathcal{G}^{+}_{\gamma\ell}-\frac{1}{L^{4}}\frac{a_{\gamma+2,\ell}b_{\gamma+2,\ell}}{\left(\gamma+\ell+1\right)^{2}}\right),

(3.20)

where

\displaystyle a_{\gamma\ell}\equiv\left(\gamma-\gamma_{+}+2\ell+1\right)\left(\gamma-\gamma_{-}+2\ell+1\right),\qquad b_{\gamma\ell}\equiv\left(\gamma-\gamma_{+}\right)\left(\gamma-\gamma_{-}\right),

(3.21)

with

\gamma_{\pm}\equiv\frac{3}{2}\pm\sqrt{\alpha}.

(3.22)

Our strategy is similar to the one adopted for the black hole: observe that the equation of motion

\widetilde{H}_{\gamma\ell}\phi_{\gamma\ell}=0

simplifies if
\gamma=\gamma_{\pm}
or
\gamma=\gamma_{\pm}-2\ell-1
, such that the constant term in
\widetilde{H}_{\gamma\ell}

(the one
\propto a_{\gamma\ell}b_{\gamma\ell}
) vanishes.
With these choices for
\gamma
,
the equation of motion becomes

\mathcal{G}^{+}_{\gamma-2,\ell}\,\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\gamma\ell}\,\phi_{\gamma\ell}=0,

(3.23)

for which a possible solution is

\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\gamma\ell}\,\phi_{\gamma\ell}=0.

(3.24)

As before, this reduction of a second order differential equation to a first order one is what lies behind the single-asymptote behavior. Analysis of (
3.24
), for the choice
\gamma=\gamma_{\pm}
,
shows that the solution

\phi_{\gamma_{\pm},\ell}
approaches
r^{\ell}
at the origin, and
\left(1-\frac{r^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)^{-(\gamma_{\pm}+\ell)/2}
at the horizon. These match precisely the desired boundary conditions for a quasinormal mode, i.e. regularity at the origin and outgoing at the horizon.
21
21

21

We do not need it, but the exact quasinormal solution is

\phi_{\gamma_{\pm},\ell}(r)=\left(\frac{r}{L}\right)^{\ell}\left(1-\frac{r^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)^{-(\gamma_{\pm}+\ell)/2}.

(3.25)

Recalling (
3.7
), we see that the quasinormal frequency is given by

i\omega_{\gamma_{\pm}\ell}L=\gamma_{\pm}+\ell.

(3.26)

Thus, we have identified two quasinormal frequencies (for a given
\ell
), one for
\gamma_{+}
and one for
\gamma_{-}
. Each corresponding quasinormal mode serves as some sort of “ground state”. The idea is to build the “excited states” by successively applying
\mathcal{G}^{+}
. This is reminiscent of how the simple harmonic oscillator problem is solved algebraically.

Before doing so, let us remark upon the other possibility
\gamma=\gamma_{\pm}-2\ell-1
. In that case, it can be shown that (
3.24
) implies the irregular asymptotic behavior
r^{-{(\ell+1)}}
at the origin, which does not match the desired quasinormal boundary conditions.
22
22

22

We do not need this one either, but the exact irregular solution is

\phi_{\gamma_{\pm}-2\ell-1,\ell}(r)=\left(\frac{L}{r}\right)^{\ell+1}\left(1-\frac{r^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)^{-(\gamma_{\pm}-\ell-1)/2}.

(3.27)

23
23

23

To round out the discussion, one could go back to (
3.23
)

\mathcal{G}^{+}_{\gamma-2,\ell}\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\gamma\ell}\phi_{\gamma\ell}=0
, and ask what happens if instead of (
3.24
), one has

\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\gamma\ell}\phi_{\gamma\ell}\neq 0
. In that case, by studying what

\mathcal{G}^{+}_{\gamma-2,\ell}
annihilates,
it can be shown that the choice
\gamma=\gamma_{\pm}
yields
\phi_{\gamma\ell}
solutions irregular at the origin, whereas the choice

\gamma=\gamma_{\pm}-2\ell-1
yields solutions that are incoming at the horizon. Neither
satisfies the desired quasinormal boundary conditions.

Returning thus to the choice
\gamma=\gamma_{\pm}
, having found the “ground state”
\phi_{\gamma_{\pm},\ell}

(for given
\ell
), we can raise to a higher
\gamma
by successively applying the raising operator (recalling that each time,
\gamma
increments by
2
):

\phi_{\gamma_{\pm}+2n,\ell}\propto\mathcal{G}^{+}_{\gamma_{\pm}+2n-2,\ell}\mathcal{G}^{+}_{\gamma_{\pm}+2n-4,\ell}\cdots\mathcal{G}^{+}_{\gamma_{\pm}+2,\ell}\mathcal{G}^{+}_{\gamma_{\pm},\ell}\phi_{\gamma_{\pm},\ell},

(3.28)

where
n
an integer. It can be shown by inspecting the form of
\mathcal{G}^{\pm}_{\gamma\ell}
that the resulting
\phi_{\gamma_{\pm}+2n,\ell}
has the correct quasinormal boundary conditions, i.e.
\phi_{\gamma_{\pm}+2n,\ell}
approaches
r^{\ell}
at the origin, and approaches
\left(1-\frac{r^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)^{-(\gamma_{\pm}+2n+\ell)/2}
at the horizon.
This way, we can construct all the quasinormal modes, covering the whole spectrum:

i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L=\gamma+\ell=\gamma_{\pm}+2n+\ell,

(3.29)

where
n
is a non-negative integer. One can further show that the
E^{+}_{\gamma\ell}
operators
(which raises
\ell
) also preserve the quasinormal boundary conditions. So we can even combine
\mathcal{G}^{+}_{\gamma\ell}
and
E^{+}_{\gamma\ell}
to construct the entire spectrum from the quasinormal solution
\phi_{\gamma_{\pm},0}
, which can be regarded as
the
ground state.
24
24

24

There are some exceptional cases where the procedure—constructing the quasinormal modes by raising
\gamma
starting from
\phi_{\gamma_{\pm},\ell}
—requires a modification. This subtlety happens only for the
\gamma_{-}
branch: it turns out if
\gamma_{-}=-\ell
(or equivalently,
2\sqrt{\alpha}=2\ell+3
),
\mathcal{G}^{+}_{\gamma_{-},\ell}
is proportional to
\mathcal{G}^{-}_{\gamma_{-},\ell}
which means the former cannot be used to raise
\gamma
from
\phi_{\gamma_{-},\ell}
which is annihilated by the latter. To construct
\phi_{\gamma_{-}+2,\ell}
, one could alternatively use
E^{+}
instead. For instance, if
\alpha=9/4
(massless scalar) such that
\gamma_{-}=0
, the
\phi_{00}
solution is annihilated by both
\mathcal{G}^{+}_{00}
and
\mathcal{G}^{-}_{00}
. One could instead use
\phi_{20}
as the ground state from which to build out the quasinormal spectrum at
\ell=0
, by acting successively with
\mathcal{G}^{+}
. To find
\phi_{20}
, use the fact that
E^{-}_{20}\phi_{20}=0
.
In other words, forget about the state
\phi_{00}
(its corresponding frequency is zero anyway); instead, start from
\phi_{20}
which can be obtained by solving
E^{-}_{20}\phi_{20}=0
.

Or more precisely, there is a ground state for
\gamma_{+}
, and a ground state for
\gamma_{-}
. From each, a whole table of states labeled by
\gamma=\gamma_{\pm}+2n
and
\ell
can be constructed. In other words, they form two separate (non-unitary) representations of
\mathfrak{so}(1,4)
.

3.3
Conformally Coupled Case

A conformally coupled scalar (
\xi=\xi_{c}

or
\alpha=1/4
in (
3.2
)) enjoys additional symmetries effected by the CKVs.
The five CKVs of de Sitter space are

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{1}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta}{r^{2}}\sin\theta\cos\varphi\,\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\cos\theta\cos\varphi\,\partial_{\theta}-\frac{1}{r}\csc\theta\sin\varphi\,\partial_{\varphi}

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{2}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta}{r^{2}}\sin\theta\sin\varphi\,\partial_{r}+\frac{1}{r}\cos\theta\sin\varphi\,\partial_{\theta}+\frac{1}{r}\csc\theta\cos\varphi\,\partial_{\varphi}

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{3}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta}{r^{2}}\cos\theta\,\partial_{r}-\frac{1}{r}\sin\theta\,\partial_{\theta}

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{+}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}

\displaystyle=e^{-t/L}\left(\frac{r}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\partial_{t}-\frac{\sqrt{\Delta}}{L}\partial_{r}\right)

\displaystyle\widetilde{K}_{-}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}

\displaystyle=e^{t/L}\left(\frac{r}{\sqrt{\Delta}}\partial_{t}+\frac{\sqrt{\Delta}}{L}\partial_{r}\right).

(3.30)

The commutation relations among them and the KVs are given in Appendix
B
. Together, they form an
\mathfrak{so}(2,4)
algebra. From it, one can deduce how they act on solutions, labeled by
\gamma,\ell,m
as in the previous subsection. We can also roughly guess their effects based on their explicit form given in (
3.30
). For instance,
\widetilde{K}_{3}
does not involve
t
or
\varphi
, but involves

r
and
\theta
. Thus we expect
\delta_{\widetilde{K}_{3}}
acting on

\phi_{\gamma\ell}Y_{\ell m}e^{-i\omega_{\gamma\ell}t}
to effect
\ell\rightarrow\ell\pm 1
without changing
m
or the frequency.
But because the frequency is proportional to
\gamma+\ell
(
3.7
), keeping the frequency unchanged while incrementing
\ell
means

\gamma\rightarrow\gamma\mp 1
at the same time. Explicit expressions for the effect of
\delta_{\widetilde{K}_{i}}

B
.
Suffice to say the
\widetilde{K}_{i}
in general mix up solutions with different
\ell
and
m
(while keeping frequency fixed) in a way reminiscent of how the
K_{i}
act in the case of the black hole.

The two CKVs
\widetilde{K}_{\pm}
introduce something more novel. They involve a factor of
e^{\pm t/L}
and hence increment
i\omega L
by
1
. They do not involve
\theta,\varphi
and thus do not affect
\ell,m
. They are therefore the analogs of
\delta^{ij}\delta_{P_{i}}\delta_{P_{j}}
and
\delta^{ij}\delta_{K_{i}}\delta_{K_{j}}

\gamma
(by
2
) without changing
\ell,m
.
A crucial difference is that here there is no need to form quadratic combinations of generators, and so
\widetilde{K}_{\pm}
can increment
\gamma
by
1
:

\displaystyle\delta_{\widetilde{K}_{\pm}}\left(\phi_{\gamma\ell}Y_{\ell m}e^{-i\omega_{\gamma\ell}t}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L}\widetilde{G}^{\pm}_{\gamma\ell}\phi_{\gamma\ell}Y_{\ell m}e^{-(i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L\pm 1)t/L},

(3.31)

where

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}^{+}_{\gamma\ell}

\displaystyle\equiv-\sqrt{\Delta}\partial_{r}-\frac{\sqrt{\Delta}}{r}-i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L\frac{r}{\sqrt{\Delta}}

(3.32)

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}^{-}_{\gamma\ell}

\displaystyle\equiv\sqrt{\Delta}\partial_{r}+\frac{\sqrt{\Delta}}{r}-i\omega_{\gamma\ell}L\frac{r}{\sqrt{\Delta}},

(3.33)

with
\widetilde{G}^{\pm}_{\gamma\ell}
effecting
\gamma\pm 1
. These ladder operators are equivalent to those in Section 3.1 of
[
25
]
.
The various identities obeyed by
\widetilde{G}_{\gamma\ell}^{\pm}
, showing explicitly how they effect changes to the equation of motion operator, are in Appendix
G
.
As before, the corresponding contiguous relations for hypergeometric functions are shown in Appendix
D
.
And as before, an economical way to build the entire (non-unitary) representation of the
\mathfrak{so}(2,4)
is to use the traceless symmetric tensor from (

to recast one of our ladders:

\displaystyle c^{\ell m}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{\ell}}\delta_{\widetilde{K}_{i_{1}}}\cdots\delta_{\widetilde{K}_{i_{\ell}}}\Phi_{\gamma 00}(t,r,\theta,\varphi)

\displaystyle\propto\Phi_{\gamma-\ell,\ell m}(t,r,\theta,\varphi).

(3.34)

The proof is given in Appendix
I

\delta_{\widetilde{K}_{\pm}}\Phi_{\gamma\ell m}\propto\Phi_{\gamma\pm 1,\ell m}.

(3.35)

The derivation of the quasinormal spectrum given in the last subsection is general, and makes no assumption about the value of the coupling to Ricci. Thus (

4
Discussion

In this note we have adopted a geometric point of view that is applicable to spin
0
perturbations around both the black hole and de Sitter space. For the black hole, static, scalar perturbations enjoy an exact SO(1,3) symmetry, whose generators consist of 3 rotational KVs and 3 “boost” CKVs. The latter might come as a surprise since the scalar in question is not conformally coupled. But a special condition we call melodic (
2.4
) is responsible for the relevance of the 3 ‘‘boost’’ CKVs.
25
25

25

It is not surprising that the effective 3D metric (
2.1
) seen by the static scalar is conformally flat, and thus has CKVs. What is non-trivial is that some of its CKVs are melodic.
For de Sitter space, the dynamical scalar perturbations enjoy an exact SO(1,4) symmetry following from the isometry, which is extended to SO(2,4) if the scalar is conformally coupled.

In both the case of the black hole, and that of de Sitter space, the perturbation solutions form representations of the corresponding algebra. The non-trivial algebra means the matrix representations of some symmetry generators must have non-vanishing off-diagonal terms. This is the origin of ladder structures. The most familiar example is the algebra of rotation generators
J_{1},J_{2},J_{3}
: in a basis in which
J_{3}
is diagonal (i.e. with the solutions labeled by
m
),
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
are represented by non-diagonal matrices which means they give rise to ladder operators that can be used to raise or lower
m
. Static, scalar perturbations around the black hole can be labeled by
\ell,m
(the quantum numbers of rotations), forming a unitary (principal series) representation of SO(1,3): the boost CKVs, which have a non-trivial algebra with rotations, effect transformations that raise or lower
\ell
. Dynamical scalar perturbations around de Sitter space are labeled by
\ell,m
and frequency, forming a non-unitary representation of SO(1,4) (or SO(2,4) in the case of conformal coupling). Those symmetry generators that do not commute with the time-translation KV, effect transformations that raise or lower the frequency.

For both the black hole and de Sitter space, the nature of the perturbation solution in the “ground state”, from which the whole representation can be generated by applying ladder operators, is what lies behind the remarkable single-asymptote property: i.e., the black hole perturbation solution that is regular at the horizon has purely “growing” tidal behavior but no “decaying” (Love number) tail at infinity; the quasinormal solution around de Sitter space is regular at the origin and purely outgoing at the horizon.

There are a number of interesting follow-up questions. How could this geometric/ representation-theoretic understanding be extended to spin 1 and spin 2 perturbations? For the black hole, hints of this can be gleaned from the spin ladder discussed in
[
1
]
, where the spin ladder was used to explain the vanishing of spin 1 and 2 Love numbers for Kerr black holes. How could the geometric/representation-theoretic understanding be extended to
non-static
perturbations around a black hole? Near-zone approximations exist, which are applicable to low frequency black hole perturbations. One of them has a larger symmetry group than SO(1,3), in fact SO(2,4)
[
24
]
. This might be useful for understanding the dissipative tidal response of the black hole. More ambitiously, to understand black hole quasinormal modes, one must go beyond the low frequencies associated with near-zone approximations. An interesting recent development is the identification of symmetries associated with a near-light-ring approximation
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
. Could there be further hidden
exact
symmetries governing dynamical black hole perturbations? We hope to address some of these questions in the future.

Appendix E
Commutators and Lie Brackets

In this paper, when we write something like

[X,Y]=Z
where
X,Y,Z
are vectors, we implicitly mean three different, mutually consistent statements.
The first is interpreting
[X,Y]
as a Lie bracket (
E.2
), i.e.

[X,Y]^{\mu}_{\rm LB}=Z^{\mu}
.
The second is interpreting it as a statement about Lie derivatives:
[\mathcal{L}_{X},\mathcal{L}_{Y}]=\mathcal{L}_{Z}
(
E.3
).
The third is interpreting it as a a statement about symmetry
transformation (
E.1
):
[\delta_{X},\delta_{Y}]=\delta_{Z}
(
E.5
). The goal of this Appendix is to demonstrate their mutual consistency.

In this paper, we are interested in symmetry formation of the form

\delta_{X}=\mathcal{L}_{X}+c\left(\nabla_{\mu}X^{\mu}\right),

(E.1)

where
X
any vector,
c
is any constant, and
\mathcal{L}_{X}
denotes the Lie derivative along
X
, obey the same algebra as the Lie derivatives along the vectors themselves when acting on a general tensor. Here (and only here) we shall carefully distinguish between commutators of differential operators and Lie brackets of vectors, denoting the latter by

\left[X,Y\right]_{\text{LB}}^{\mu}\equiv X^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}Y^{\nu}-Y^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}X^{\nu}.

(E.2)

We then cite the well-known property that for any tensor
T
of rank
(r,s)
,

\left[\mathcal{L}_{X},\mathcal{L}_{Y}\right]{T^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{r}}}_{\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{s}}=\mathcal{L}_{\left[X,Y\right]_{\text{LB}}}{T^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{r}}}_{\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{s}},

(E.3)

and compute

\displaystyle\left[\delta_{X},\delta_{Y}\right]{T^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{r}}}_{\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{s}}

\displaystyle=\big(\left[\mathcal{L}_{X},\mathcal{L}_{Y}\right]+c\left(\mathcal{L}_{X}\left(\nabla_{\mu}Y^{\mu}\big)-\mathcal{L}_{Y}\left(\nabla_{\mu}X^{\mu}\right)\right)\right){T^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{r}}}_{\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{s}}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{L}_{\left[X,Y\right]_{\text{LB}}}+c\left(X^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}Y^{\mu}-Y^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}X^{\mu}\right)\right){T^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{r}}}_{\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{s}}~.

(E.4)

In the second line of (
E
), replacing the double covariant derivative
\nabla_{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}
by
\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}-R_{\mu\nu}
allows us to express
X^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}Y^{\mu}-Y^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}X^{\mu}
as the divergence of
[X,Y]_{\rm LB}
, and hence we find

\displaystyle\left[\delta_{X},\delta_{Y}\right]{T^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{r}}}_{\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{s}}

\displaystyle=\delta_{\left[X,Y\right]_{\text{LB}}}{T^{\mu_{1}\cdots\mu_{r}}}_{\nu_{1}\cdots\nu_{s}}.

(E.5)

Thus we see that any symmetry transformations of the form above obey the same algebra as the corresponding vectors.

An ordinary KV satisfies the above equation with

\displaystyle=-\frac{d-2}{d}\nabla^{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}X^{\nu}-{R^{\mu}}_{\nu}X^{\nu}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{d-1}\left(R\nabla_{\mu}X^{\mu}+\frac{d}{2}X^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}R\right)

(F.3)

also yields a symmetry of the action.
26
26

26

(d-1)\left(\xi-\xi_{c}\right)\Box\nabla_{\mu}X^{\mu}-m^{2}\nabla_{\mu}X^{\mu}=0

A few properties of melodic CKVs are quickly apparent from (

\begin{split}\Box\nabla_{\nu}\left(X^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}Y^{\nu}-Y^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}X^{\nu}\right)&=X^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}\Box\nabla_{\nu}Y^{\nu}-Y^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}\Box\nabla_{\nu}X^{\nu}\\

Thus if both

It follows that the melodic CKVs form a Lie algebra. Since the KVs form the isometry algebra of the manifold, and the CKVs form the conformal algebra, the algebra formed by the melodic CKVs must lie “in between” these two algebras. Denoting this algebra with

(Here we consider KVs to be trivially melodic CKVs.)

Returning to a scalar field theory, we are naturally led to consider the conserved currents of the symmetries associated with the melodic CKVs. The Noether procedure yields

where

(F.15)

Appendix H
Conditions for Horizontal Symmetries

In this appendix we shall establish the conditions for the existence of the so-called horizontal symmetries used throughout the main text. We consider an action of the form

S=\sum_{n}S_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n}\int dr\,\phi_{n}^{*}\mathcal{H}_{n}\phi_{n},

(H.1)

where

\mathcal{H}_{n}\equiv\partial_{r}\Delta\partial_{r}+V_{n}(r).

(H.2)

Here
\Delta(r)
and
V_{n}(r)
are arbitrary functions, and
n
is an abstract index that can represent multiple indices. The equation of motion is naturally

\mathcal{H}_{n}\phi_{n}=0.

(H.3)

We now assume that we have some ladder operators, i.e., some
\mathcal{D}^{\pm}_{n}
that raise and lower
n
. We must have

\mathcal{D}^{\pm}_{n}H_{n}\phi_{n}=H_{n\pm k}\mathcal{D}^{\pm}_{n}\phi_{n}.

(H.4)

where the equation of motion operator is given by

H_{n}\equiv-f(r)\mathcal{H}_{n}

(H.5)

for some function
f(r)
, and we have introduced a minus sign for later convenience. Like
n
,
k
is an abstract index that could represent the shifting of many different indices by different amounts. We may assume that the ladder operators are first order, since we may always remove higher derivatives by subtracting
H_{n}
or its derivatives.

Lastly, for any differential operator
\mathcal{O}
, we let
\widehat{\mathcal{O}}
be the operator that arises from integrating by parts, i.e.,
if

\mathcal{O}=\sum_{i}a_{i}(r)\partial_{r}^{i},

(H.6)

then

\widehat{\mathcal{O}}=\sum_{i}(-1)^{i}\partial_{r}^{i}a_{i}(r),

(H.7)

where derivatives act on everything to the right. We can then write

\int dr\,\phi\mathcal{O}\psi=\int dr\,\psi\widehat{\mathcal{O}}\phi

(H.8)

by dropping a boundary term.

We now take
n=0
(or any specific value, but 0 is convenient) and consider some first order differential operator
Q_{0}
which obeys
Q_{0}^{*}=Q_{0}
. We define the transformation
\delta\phi_{0}=Q_{0}\phi_{0}
and find that the action transforms as

\displaystyle\delta S_{0}

\displaystyle=\int dr\,\left(Q_{0}\phi_{0}^{*}\mathcal{H}_{0}\phi_{0}+\phi_{0}^{*}\mathcal{H}_{0}Q_{0}\phi_{0}\right)

(H.9)

\displaystyle=\int dr\,\left(\phi_{0}^{*}\left(\widehat{Q}_{0}\mathcal{H}_{0}+\mathcal{H}_{0}Q_{0}\right)\phi_{0}\right).

(H.10)

Thus we see that a sufficient condition for
\delta S_{0}
to vanish is

\widehat{Q}_{0}\mathcal{H}_{0}=-\mathcal{H}_{0}Q_{0}.

(H.11)

Since
Q_{0}
is a first order operator, it can be written in the form

Q_{0}=a(r)\partial_{r}b(r)

(H.12)

for some functions
a(r)
and
b(r)
. It follows that
\widehat{Q}_{0}
can be written

\widehat{Q}_{0}=-b(r)\partial_{r}a(r)=-\frac{1}{g(r)}Q_{0}g(r),

(H.13)

where
g(r)=a(r)/b(r)
. If

\left[Q_{0},g(r)\mathcal{H}_{0}\right]=0,

(H.14)

we can write

\widehat{Q}_{0}\mathcal{H}_{0}=-\frac{1}{g(r)}Q_{0}g(r)\mathcal{H}_{0}=-\mathcal{H}_{0}Q_{0},

(H.15)

so (
H.11
) is met. Thus if we can find some operator
Q_{0}
that commutes with
g(r)\mathcal{H}_{0}
for the same
g(r)
that appears in (
H.13
), then
\delta\phi_{0}=Q_{0}\phi_{0}
is a symmetry of the action
S_{0}
.

It is worth pausing to note that at this stage, the ladder structure has not been used at all, and as noted previously,
n
is set to zero simply for concreteness. If we somehow encounter any operator
Q_{n}
obeying (
H.13
) and (
H.14
), then we have an associated symmetry of the action
S_{n}
. Having a ladder structure may make it easier to find such a

but is in no way necessary.

If we have such an operator
Q_{0}

(H.16)

assuming

(H.17)

Of course, if

(H.18)

and

\left[Q_{n},g(r)\mathcal{H}_{n}\right]=0,

(H.19)

then (

) is satisfied, but since

is a higher order operator, we have

\widehat{Q}_{n}=\widehat{\mathcal{D}}^{-}_{n}\widehat{Q}_{n-k}\widehat{\mathcal{D}}^{+}_{n-k},

(H.20)

and there is no guarantee that

\widehat{Q}_{n}=-\frac{1}{g(r)}Q_{n}g(r)

(H.21)

(H.23)

Thus (

(H.24)

Appendix J
More Ways to Climb Ladders

Though the focus of this work has been on operators that act on the radial
\phi(r)
solutions, we have seen that we can act with various (C)KVs to move around the space of the full
\Phi(t,r,\theta,\varphi)
solutions. In this appendix, we shall explore this further.

We first need one more tool. For both the black hole and de Sitter space, we have a triplet of KVs
J_{i}
corresponding to spatial rotations. In the usual way, we can construct operators that raise and lower
m
. We take

J_{\pm}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}=\left(J_{1}^{\mu}\pm iJ_{2}^{\mu}\right)\partial_{\mu}=\pm ie^{\pm i\phi}\partial_{\theta}-e^{\pm i\phi}\cot\theta\,\partial_{\varphi}.

(J.1)

Then in the de Sitter case, we find

\delta_{J_{\pm}}\Phi_{\gamma\ell m}(t,r,\theta,\varphi)\propto\Phi_{\gamma\ell,m\pm 1}(t,r,\theta,\varphi).

(J.2)

(For the black hole case, simply drop the
\gamma
index and the
t
argument.)

The power of these new operators comes from the fact that
\Phi_{\gamma\ell m}=0
for
\ell<m
. Focusing on the black hole for now, we see that (
2.16
) shows that for
m=\ell
, the effect of
\delta_{K_{3}}
on
\Phi_{\ell\ell}
is solely to raise
\ell
, as the second term vanishes. We can thus map any
\Phi_{\ell m}
to
\Phi_{\ell+1,m}
, up to an overall constant, with the following sequence of operators

\left(\delta_{J_{-}}\right)^{\ell-m}\delta_{K_{3}}\left(\delta_{J_{+}}\right)^{\ell-m}\Phi_{\ell m}\propto\Phi_{\ell+1,m},

(J.3)

or perhaps more clearly,

(\ell,m)\xrightarrow{\left(\delta_{J_{+}}\right)^{\ell-m}}(\ell+1,\ell)\xrightarrow{\delta_{K_{3}}}(\ell+1,\ell)\xrightarrow{\left(\delta_{J_{-}}\right)^{\ell-m}}(\ell+1,m).

(J.4)

Unfortunately, in the black hole case there is no way to map
\Phi_{\ell m}
to
\Phi_{\ell-1,m}
using any combination of (C)KVs.

Moving to de Sitter space, we find from (
3.9
) that setting
\gamma=\gamma_{\pm}
makes the
F^{+}_{\gamma\ell}
term vanish, so the effect of
\delta_{K_{3}}
is to lower
\ell
alone. This gives us a way to map any quasinormal mode
\Phi_{\gamma\ell m}
to
\Phi_{\gamma,\ell-1,m}
. We lower
\gamma=\gamma_{\pm}+2n
to
\gamma_{\pm}
with
n
applications of
\delta^{ij}\delta_{K_{i}}\delta_{K_{j}}
, lower
\ell
with
\delta_{K_{3}}
, and then raise
\gamma
back up with
n
applications of
\delta^{ij}\delta_{P_{i}}\delta_{P_{j}}
:

\left(\delta^{ij}\delta_{P_{i}}\delta_{P_{j}}\right)^{n}\delta_{K_{3}}\left(\delta^{kl}\delta_{K_{k}}\delta_{K_{l}}\right)^{n}\Phi_{\gamma_{\pm}+2n,\ell m}\propto\Phi_{\gamma_{\pm}+2n,\ell-1,m},

(J.5)

or,

(\gamma_{\pm}+2n,\ell,m)\xrightarrow{\left(\delta^{kl}\delta_{K_{k}}\delta_{K_{l}}\right)^{n}}(\gamma_{\pm},\ell,m)\xrightarrow{\delta_{K_{3}}}(\gamma_{\pm},\ell-1,m)\xrightarrow{\left(\delta^{ij}\delta_{P_{i}}\delta_{P_{j}}\right)^{n}}(\gamma_{\pm}+2n,\ell-1,m).

(J.6)

Raising
\ell
is more involved but can be done, unlike in the Reissner–Nordström case. We must first lower
\gamma
to
\gamma_{\pm}
and
m
to zero with
\delta^{ij}\delta_{K_{i}}\delta_{K_{j}}
and
\delta_{J_{-}}
(in either order). We can then lower
\ell
to zero with
\delta_{K_{3}}
. Then we use

c^{\ell+1,m}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{\ell+1}}\delta_{P_{i_{1}}}\cdots\delta_{P_{i_{\ell+1}}}

(J.7)

to jump from
(\gamma_{\pm},0,0)
all the way to
(\gamma_{\pm},\ell,m)
. Lastly, we use
\delta^{ij}\delta_{P_{i}}\delta_{P_{j}}
to raise
\gamma
back to its original value. We can represent this as

\left(\delta^{ij}\delta_{P_{i}}\delta_{P_{j}}\right)^{n}\left(c^{\ell+1,m}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{\ell+1}}\delta_{P_{i_{1}}}\cdots\delta_{P_{i_{\ell+1}}}\right)\left(\delta_{K_{3}}\right)^{\ell}\left(\delta^{ij}\delta_{K_{i}}\delta_{K_{j}}\right)^{n}\left(\delta_{J_{-}}\right)^{m}\Phi_{\gamma_{\pm}+2n,\ell m}\propto\Phi_{\gamma_{\pm}+2n,\ell+1,m},

(J.8)

or

\begin{split}&(\gamma_{\pm}+2n,\ell,m)\xrightarrow{\left(\delta_{J_{-}}\right)^{m}}(\gamma_{\pm}+2n,\ell,0)\xrightarrow{\left(\delta^{ij}\delta_{K_{i}}\delta_{K_{j}}\right)^{n}}(\gamma_{\pm},\ell,0)\xrightarrow{\left(\delta_{K_{3}}\right)^{\ell}}\\
&(\gamma_{\pm},0,0)\xrightarrow{c^{\ell+1,m}_{i_{1}\cdots i_{\ell+1}}\delta_{P_{i_{1}}}\cdots\delta_{P_{i_{\ell+1}}}}(\gamma_{\pm},\ell+1,m)\xrightarrow{\left(\delta^{ij}\delta_{P_{i}}\delta_{P_{j}}\right)^{n}}(\gamma_{\pm}+2n,\ell+1,m).\end{split}

(J.9)

Thus in the de Sitter case, we can move from any quasinormal mode to any other via a series of operations, all built on KVs.
