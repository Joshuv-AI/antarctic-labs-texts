Tensor Renormalization Group for fermions
Ryo Sakai

Abstract
We review the basic ideas of the Tensor Renormalization Group method and show how they can be applied for lattice field theory models involving relativistic fermions and Grassmann variables in arbitrary dimensions. We discuss recent progress for entanglement filtering, loop optimization, bond-weighting techniques and matrix product decompositions for Grassmann tensor networks. The new methods are tested with two-dimensional Wilson–Majorana fermions and multi-flavor Gross–Neveu models. We show that the methods can also be applied to the fermionic Hubbard model in 1+1 and 2+1 dimensions.

Introduction

The renormalization group (RG) approach of lattice models has been crucial to identify their universal critical behavior, construct their phase diagram and understanding their continuum limits. The general idea
[
leo66
,
wilson73pr
,
wilson74rmp
]
consists in integrating over some of the microscopic degrees of freedom in order to obtain an effective theory with a larger lattice spacing and repeat the procedure until one reaches a macroscopic size. This leads to an RG map connecting effective theories at increasing lattice spacing. The fixed points and relevant directions of the RG maps have universal properties such as similar critical exponents observed in very different microscopic setups (e.g., magnets and solids).

The generic properties of RG maps are very well-understood
[
wilson73pr
,
wilson74rmp
,
cardy1996scaling
]
. However, the numerical implementation of the partial integration over some microscopic degrees of freedom (coarse-graining) can be challenging. This requires to parameterize some “space of theories” in terms of effective couplings and find numerical methods to calculate the “new” couplings in terms of the “old” couplings. This provides the RG map. Simplified RG maps such as various majority rules, bond moving
[
Migdal:1975zf
,
kadanoff76
]
approximate recursions
[
wilson73pr
]
, hierarchical approximations
[
dyson68
,
baker72
,
hmreview
]
, local potential approximations
[
berges2000
,
bervillier2007
,
bervillier2013
]
, can be easily implemented numerically and demonstrate the generic validity of the RG approach. However, these approximations provide critical exponents which are different from the exponents of the approximated models and it is difficult to systematically improve such approximations.

The basic RG ideas were incorporated in variational algorithms designed to construct the ground state wavefunction of Hamiltonians for one-dimensional spatial lattices
often called the density matrix renormalization group (DMRG) method
[
white92
,
schollwock2005
,
uli2011
]
.
This led to the development of tensor network ansatzes
(e.g. matrix product states (MPS))
for these wavefunctions. It became clear that the success of the method was linked to its ability to handle efficiently the entanglement entropy in one spatial dimension
[
schollwock2005
,
vidal2007
,

,
schollwock2011
,

,
2015PhRvL.115r0405E
,
silvi2017
,
hv2017
,

,
ran2020
,
RevModPhys.93.045003
]
.
The tensor technology was also used to reformulate and coarse-grain classical lattice models
[

,
Levin:2006jai
,
Gu:2009dr
,
PhysRevLett.103.160601
,
Gu:2010yh
,
Gu:2013gba
,

,

,

.
This new approach is called the tensor renormalization group (TRG).
The TRG approach allows us to interpret and quantitatively realize the traditional RG ideas in the language of the tensor network

In this Topical Review, we discuss recent TRG applications for fermionic models.
The starting point is a path integral involving Grassmann variables.
We will consider models in Euclidean spacetime and the statistical weights in the path integral will have the generic form
{\rm e}^{-S}
for some classical action
S
. The first step of the tensorial approach consists in expanding the exponential of the non-local parts of
S
(attached to links or plaquettes of the lattice)
in terms of discrete tensor indices and then perform the integration over the original variables. With this procedure, the path integral becomes a sum of product of local tensors with their indices contracted. For bosonic variables, the tensors are just ordinary functions of the coupling that can be collected in a straightforward manner. However, for Grassmann variables, one needs to keep track of the signs resulting from various orderings. As we will show in Sec.
2
, this can be handled by introducing auxiliary Grassmann variables which will be incorporated in the tensors. We then proceed to coarse-grain the reformulation of the path integral by performing approximately partial contractions following the early TRG method of Levin-Nave
(illustrated in Fig.
5
) and their HOTRG version (illustrated in Fig.
7
). In Sec.
, we review simple examples of numerical calculations for free Wilson-Majorana fermions (equivalent to the Ising model) and 1+1 QED (the Schwinger model).

An important aspect of the TRG coarse-graining is that it can in principle be performed exactly
[
prb87
,
Liu:2013nsa
]
. However, in practice, the computational cost still scales exponentially with the size of the system because despite the partial integration over some of the microscopic degrees of freedom, effective tensors with more indices are generated. The scaling is less severe, but nevertheless exponential. For this reason and also in order to get RG maps relating same size tensors, truncations are necessary. It has been argued that the inverse of the truncation size can be considered as a relevant direction which can interfere with the study of fixed points
[
Vanhecke:2019pez
]
.
It has also been known
[
Levin:2006jai
,
Gu:2009dr
]
that short-range entanglement can remain present during TRG iterations and generate unphysical fixed points.
Improvements of this situation for fermionic theories are discussed in Sec.
.
We introduce the corner double line (CDL) fixed point and then discuss methods to remove them.
This includes the tensor network renormalization (TNR)
[
2015PhRvL.115r0405E
]
, the loop-TNR
[

]

[

]

2
Formalism for the Grassmann TRG

We begin by explaining how to express fermionic path integrals in the language of tensor networks.
Because of the nilpotency of the Grassmann variables, they can be straightforwardly rewritten with finite-dimensional tensors.
We show how to construct the exact contraction between these fundamental tensors, which is necessary to reproduce original path integrals.
However, the exact contraction is usually prohibited in practice because it requires exponentially large computational resources.
One of the promising approaches is the TRG which carries out these contractions approximately.
Although the TRG algorithms are often used to compute the partition functions without the Grassmann variables, we will see that any TRG algorithm can be applied to fermionic path integrals.

2.1
Grassmann tensor network representation

For the time being in this section, we use the following simple quadratic model to explain how to derive the Grassmann tensor network representation.

\displaystyle S=-t\sum_{n\in\Lambda}\sum_{\nu=1}^{2}\left[\bar{\psi}(n+\hat{\nu})\psi(n)+\bar{\psi}(n)\psi(n+\hat{\nu})\right]+m\sum_{n}\bar{\psi}(n)\psi(n),

(1)

where we assume that
\psi(n)
and
\bar{\psi}(n)
are single-component Grassmann fields for simplicity.
We consider the model on a two-dimensional square lattice
\Lambda
with periodic boundary conditions.
The path integral on the lattice is defined via

\displaystyle Z=\int\prod_{n}{\rm d}\psi(n){\rm d}\bar{\psi}(n)~{\rm e}^{-S}.

(2)

Our goal is to rewrite Eq. (
2
) introducing local tensors.
There are multiple ways to define these local tensors so that the tensor network representation is in general not unique.
This situation is the same as in constructing tensor network representations for theories that do not include fermions.

One methodology was given by Shimizu and Kuramashi
[
Shimizu:2014uva
]
and subsequently refined by Takeda and Yoshimura by explicitly introducing auxiliary Grassmann variables
[
Takeda:2014vwa
]
.
Here, we review the formalism in Ref.
[
Takeda:2014vwa
]
.
Firstly, the hopping terms are decomposed introducing auxiliary Grassmann variables as

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{t\bar{\psi}(n+\hat{\nu})\psi(n)}=

\displaystyle\sum_{i_{\nu}(n)=0}^{1}\left(\int\sqrt{t}\bar{\psi}(n+\hat{\nu}){\rm d}\bar{\Phi}_{\nu}(n+\hat{\nu})\right)^{i_{\nu}(n)}\left(\int\sqrt{t}\psi(n){\rm d}\Phi_{\nu}(n)\right)^{i_{\nu}(n)}\left(\bar{\Phi}_{\nu}(n+\hat{\nu})\Phi_{\nu}(n)\right)^{i_{\nu}(n)},

(3)

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{t\bar{\psi}(n)\psi(n+\hat{\nu})}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{\nu}(n)=0}^{1}\left(\int\sqrt{t}\bar{\psi}(n){\rm d}\bar{\Psi}_{\nu}(n)\right)^{j_{\nu}(n)}\left(\int\sqrt{t}\psi(n+\hat{\nu}){\rm d}\Psi_{\nu}(n+\hat{\nu})\right)^{j_{\nu}(n)}\left(\bar{\Psi}_{\nu}(n)\Psi_{\nu}(n+\hat{\nu})\right)^{j_{\nu}(n)},

(4)

where
\Psi_{\nu}
,
\bar{\Psi}_{\nu}
,
\Phi_{\nu}
, and
\bar{\Phi}_{\nu}
are single-component Grassmann variables along the
\nu
-directional link.
The bit
i_{\nu}(n)
(
j_{\nu}(n)
) labels the Taylor expansion of the forward (backward) hopping term on the link
(n,n+\hat{\nu})
.
These bits are nothing but the occupation numbers.
We use capital Greek letters for the auxiliary Grassmann variables
(
\Psi_{\nu}
,
\bar{\Psi}_{\nu}
, etc.) as opposed to the original microscopic Grassman variables which are lowercase (
\psi
,
\bar{\psi}
).
In the rest of this section, in order to have more compact notations, we will drop the

2.1
) and (
2.1
).
In other words, when

In the right-hand sides of Eqs. (

) and (
2.1

Therefore,

\displaystyle=\int{\rm d}\psi{\rm d}\bar{\psi}~{\rm e}^{-m\bar{\psi}\psi}\prod_{\nu}\left(\sqrt{t}\psi{\rm d}\Phi_{\nu}(n)\right)^{i_{\nu}}\left(\sqrt{t}\bar{\psi}{\rm d}\bar{\Psi}_{\nu}(n)\right)^{j_{\nu}}\left(\sqrt{t}\bar{\psi}{\rm d}\bar{\Phi}_{\nu}(n)\right)^{i^{\prime}_{\nu}}\left(\sqrt{t}\psi{\rm d}\Psi_{\nu}(n)\right)^{j^{\prime}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\times\left(\bar{\Phi}_{\nu}(n+\hat{\nu})\Phi_{\nu}(n)\right)^{i_{\nu}}\left(\bar{\Psi}_{\nu}(n)\Psi_{\nu}(n+\hat{\nu})\right)^{j_{\nu}},

(5)

, and

.

2.1

\displaystyle i_{1}+j_{1}+i_{2}+j_{2}+i^{\prime}_{1}+j^{\prime}_{1}+i^{\prime}_{2}+j^{\prime}_{2}\mod 2=0.

Let us figure out how to carry out the contraction between the fundamental tensors derived above.
As an example, we consider the contraction between
\mathcal{T}_{n}
and
\mathcal{T}_{n+\hat{1}}
, which reproduces the hopping terms on the link

When we use the fundamental tensor given in Eq. (

\displaystyle\sum\int\mathcal{T}_{n+\hat{1}}\mathcal{T}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}}T_{n+\hat{1};(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2})}T_{n;(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1})(k_{2}l_{2})(k^{\prime}_{1}l^{\prime}_{1})(k^{\prime}_{2}l^{\prime}_{2})}

\displaystyle\times\int\mathcal{G}_{n+\hat{1};(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2})}\mathcal{G}_{n;(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1})(k_{2}l_{2})(k^{\prime}_{1}l^{\prime}_{1})(k^{\prime}_{2}l^{\prime}_{2})}.

(20)

Note that
\int
on the right-hand side means the integration over the auxiliary Grassmann variables labeled by repeated Greek indices.
Firstly, we integrate out
(\bar{\Phi}_{1}(n+\hat{1})\Phi_{1}(n))^{\alpha_{1}}
and
(\bar{\Psi}_{1}(n)\Psi_{1}(n+\hat{1}))^{\beta_{1}}
originating from
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, which results in

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{G}_{n+\hat{1};(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2})}\mathcal{G}_{n;(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1})(k_{2}l_{2})(k^{\prime}_{1}l^{\prime}_{1})(k^{\prime}_{2}l^{\prime}_{2})}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})(i^{\prime}_{2}+j^{\prime}_{2})+\alpha_{1}}\mathcal{Q}_{(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2})(k_{2}l_{2})(k^{\prime}_{1}l^{\prime}_{1})(k^{\prime}_{2}l^{\prime}_{2})},

(21)

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2})(k_{2}l_{2})(k^{\prime}_{1}l^{\prime}_{1})(k^{\prime}_{2}l^{\prime}_{2})}

\displaystyle={\rm d}\Phi_{1}^{i_{1}}{\rm d}\bar{\Phi}_{1}^{j_{1}}{\rm d}\Psi_{2}^{i_{2}}{\rm d}\bar{\Psi}_{2}^{j_{2}}{\rm d}\Psi_{2}^{j^{\prime}_{2}}{\rm d}\bar{\Phi}_{2}^{i^{\prime}_{2}}{\rm d}\Psi_{2}^{k_{2}}{\rm d}\bar{\Psi}_{2}^{l_{2}}{\rm d}\Psi_{1}^{l^{\prime}_{1}}{\rm d}\bar{\Phi}_{1}^{k^{\prime}_{1}}{\rm d}\Psi_{2}^{l^{\prime}_{2}}{\rm d}\bar{\Phi}_{2}^{k^{\prime}_{2}}

\displaystyle\times\left(\bar{\Phi}_{1}\Phi_{1}\right)^{i_{1}}\left(\bar{\Psi}_{1}\Psi_{1}\right)^{j_{1}}\left(\bar{\Phi}_{2}\Phi_{2}\right)^{i_{2}}\left(\bar{\Psi}_{2}\Psi_{2}\right)^{j_{2}}\left(\bar{\Phi}_{2}\Phi_{2}\right)^{k_{2}}\left(\bar{\Psi}_{2}\Psi_{2}\right)^{l_{2}}.

(22)

We have omitted the site dependence in the auxiliary Grassmann variables in Eq. (
2.2
) because it can be read from the bit indices immediately.
The sign in Eq. (
2.2
) is then taken into account by contracting the bosonic parts.
As a result of Eq. (
2.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\sum\int\mathcal{T}_{n+\hat{1}}\mathcal{T}_{n}

\displaystyle=M_{(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2})(k_{2}l_{2})(k^{\prime}_{1}l^{\prime}_{1})(k^{\prime}_{2}l^{\prime}_{2})}\mathcal{Q}_{(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2})(k_{2}l_{2})(k^{\prime}_{1}l^{\prime}_{1})(k^{\prime}_{2}l^{\prime}_{2})},

where

\displaystyle M_{(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2})(k_{2}l_{2})(k^{\prime}_{1}l^{\prime}_{1})(k^{\prime}_{2}l^{\prime}_{2})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}}(-1)^{(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})(i^{\prime}_{2}+j^{\prime}_{2})+\alpha_{1}}T_{n+\hat{1};(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2})}T_{n;(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1})(k_{2}l_{2})(k^{\prime}_{1}l^{\prime}_{1})(k^{\prime}_{2}l^{\prime}_{2})}.

(24)

It is important to realize that the auxiliary Grassmann variables should be integrated before the bosonic parts are contracted when we use the fundamental tensor given in Eq. (
6
).

illustrates the exact contraction in Eq. (

Instead of Eq. (

\displaystyle\sum\int\mathcal{T}_{n+\hat{1}}\mathcal{T}_{n}

\displaystyle=M^{\prime}_{(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(k_{2}l_{2})(k^{\prime}_{1}l^{\prime}_{1})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2})(k^{\prime}_{2}l^{\prime}_{2})}\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}_{(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(k_{2}l_{2})(k^{\prime}_{1}l^{\prime}_{1})(k^{\prime}_{2}l^{\prime}_{2})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2})},

\displaystyle M^{\prime}_{(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(k_{2}l_{2})(k^{\prime}_{1}l^{\prime}_{1})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2})(k^{\prime}_{2}l^{\prime}_{2})}=\sum_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}}(-1)^{\alpha_{1}}T_{n+\hat{1};(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2})}T_{n;(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1})(k_{2}l_{2})(k^{\prime}_{1}l^{\prime}_{1})(k^{\prime}_{2}l^{\prime}_{2})},

(26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}_{(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(k_{2}l_{2})(k^{\prime}_{1}l^{\prime}_{1})(k^{\prime}_{2}l^{\prime}_{2})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2})}

\displaystyle={\rm d}\Phi_{1}^{i_{1}}{\rm d}\bar{\Phi}_{1}^{j_{1}}{\rm d}\Psi_{2}^{i_{2}}{\rm d}\bar{\Psi}_{2}^{j_{2}}{\rm d}\Psi_{2}^{k_{2}}{\rm d}\bar{\Psi}_{2}^{l_{2}}{\rm d}\Psi_{1}^{l^{\prime}_{1}}{\rm d}\bar{\Phi}_{1}^{k^{\prime}_{1}}{\rm d}\Psi_{2}^{l^{\prime}_{2}}{\rm d}\bar{\Phi}_{2}^{k^{\prime}_{2}}{\rm d}\Psi_{2}^{j^{\prime}_{2}}{\rm d}\bar{\Phi}_{2}^{i^{\prime}_{2}}

\displaystyle(-1)^{(i^{\prime}_{2}+j^{\prime}_{2})(k_{2}+l_{2}+k^{\prime}_{1}+l^{\prime}_{1}+k^{\prime}_{2}+l^{\prime}_{2})}=(-1)^{(i^{\prime}_{2}+j^{\prime}_{2})(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})},

\displaystyle=\sum M_{(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2})(k_{2}l_{2})(k^{\prime}_{1}l^{\prime}_{1})(k^{\prime}_{2}l^{\prime}_{2})}\Phi_{1}^{i_{1}}\Psi_{1}^{j_{1}}\Phi_{2}^{i_{2}}\Psi_{2}^{j_{2}}\bar{\Psi}_{2}^{j^{\prime}_{2}}\bar{\Phi}_{2}^{i^{\prime}_{2}}\Phi_{2}^{k_{2}}\Psi_{2}^{l_{2}}\bar{\Psi}_{1}^{l^{\prime}_{1}}\bar{\Phi}_{1}^{k^{\prime}_{1}}\bar{\Psi}_{2}^{l^{\prime}_{2}}\bar{\Phi}_{2}^{k^{\prime}_{2}},

\displaystyle M_{(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2})(k_{2}l_{2})(k^{\prime}_{1}l^{\prime}_{1})(k^{\prime}_{2}l^{\prime}_{2})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1}}(-1)^{(\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1})(i^{\prime}_{2}+j^{\prime}_{2})+\alpha_{1}+\beta_{1}}T_{n+\hat{1};(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2})}T_{n;(\alpha_{1}\beta_{1})(k_{2}l_{2})(k^{\prime}_{1}l^{\prime}_{1})(k^{\prime}_{2}l^{\prime}_{2})}.

2.3
Model-independent notation

In this subsection, we introduce a new notation for the fundamental tensor

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n}=T_{n;xtx^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\mathcal{G}_{n;xtx^{\prime}t^{\prime}},

(35)

instead of Eq. (
6
). The variable

x
is defined by two bits via
x=(i_{1}j_{1})
and
t
,
x^{\prime}
, and
t^{\prime}
are defined similarly.

\mathcal{G}_{n;xtx^{\prime}t^{\prime}}
is defined by the right-hand side of Eq. (
2.1
).
The expression in Eq. (
35
) may seem fine because it does not depend on the details of our model, the number of components in the original fermionic field or the structure of hopping terms, and it only depends on the lattice geometry.
However, this notation can be problematic when considering their exact contractions.
As we have observed in Eq. (
2.2
), or Eq. (
26
), the sign factor from the auxiliary Grassmann integrals does distinguish the forward and backward hopping terms in the formulation in Ref.
[
Takeda:2014vwa
]
.
One of the ways to resolve this issue is to modify Eq. (
2.1
) as

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{t\bar{\psi}(n)\psi(n+\hat{\nu})}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{\nu}(n)=0}^{1}\left(\sqrt{t}\bar{\psi}(n){\rm d}\bar{\Psi}_{\nu}(n)\right)^{j_{\nu}(n)}\left(\sqrt{t}\psi(n+\hat{\nu}){\rm d}\Psi_{\nu}(n+\hat{\nu})\right)^{j_{\nu}(n)}\left(-\Psi_{\nu}(n+\hat{\nu})\bar{\Psi}_{\nu}(n)\right)^{j_{\nu}(n)},

(36)

and absorbing the extra factor
(-1)^{j_{\nu}(n)}
into the bosonic tensor in Eq. (
2.1
).
Note that this modification makes the bosonic tensor in Eq. (
2.1
) exactly the same as the coefficient tensor in Eq. (
2.1
).
From now on, we identify the expression in Eq. (
35
) with this modification.
The exact contraction demonstrated in Sec.
2.2
is then denoted by

\displaystyle\sum\int\mathcal{T}_{n+\hat{1}}\mathcal{T}_{n}=\sum_{\alpha}T_{n+\hat{1};xt_{1}\alpha t^{\prime}_{1}}T_{n;\alpha t_{2}x^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{2}}\int\mathcal{G}_{n+\hat{1};xt_{1}\alpha t^{\prime}_{1}}\mathcal{G}_{n;\alpha t_{2}x^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{2}}.

(37)

Now, we introduce the Grassmann parity function
f_{x}
for
x=(i_{1}j_{1})
such that

\displaystyle f_{x}=i_{1}+j_{1}~\mod 2,

(38)

and
f_{t}
,
f_{x^{\prime}}
, and
f_{t^{\prime}}
are done in the same way.
Using these parity functions, Eq. (
37
) is evaluated as

\displaystyle\sum\int\mathcal{T}_{n+\hat{1}}\mathcal{T}_{n}=M_{xt_{1}t_{2}x^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2}}\mathcal{Q}_{xt_{1}t_{2}x^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{2}t^{\prime}_{1}},

(39)

where

\displaystyle M_{xt_{1}t_{2}x^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2}}=\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{f_{\alpha}}T_{n+\hat{1};xt_{1}\alpha t^{\prime}_{1}}T_{n;\alpha t_{2}x^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{2}},

(40)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{xt_{1}t_{2}x^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{2}t^{\prime}_{1}}

\displaystyle={\rm d}\Phi_{1}^{i_{1}}{\rm d}\bar{\Phi}_{1}^{j_{1}}{\rm d}\Psi_{2}^{i_{2}}{\rm d}\bar{\Psi}_{2}^{j_{2}}{\rm d}\Psi_{2}^{k_{2}}{\rm d}\bar{\Psi}_{2}^{l_{2}}{\rm d}\Psi_{1}^{l^{\prime}_{1}}{\rm d}\bar{\Phi}_{1}^{k^{\prime}_{1}}{\rm d}\Psi_{2}^{l^{\prime}_{2}}{\rm d}\bar{\Phi}_{2}^{k^{\prime}_{2}}{\rm d}\Psi_{2}^{j^{\prime}_{2}}{\rm d}\bar{\Phi}_{2}^{i^{\prime}_{2}}

\displaystyle\times\left(\bar{\Phi}_{1}\Phi_{1}\right)^{i_{1}}\left(\Psi_{1}\bar{\Psi}_{1}\right)^{j_{1}}\left(\bar{\Phi}_{2}\Phi_{2}\right)^{i_{2}}\left(\Psi_{2}\bar{\Psi}_{2}\right)^{j_{2}}\left(\bar{\Phi}_{2}\Phi_{2}\right)^{k_{2}}\left(\Psi_{2}\bar{\Psi}_{2}\right)^{l_{2}}.

(41)

It should be emphasized that the exact contraction between the fundamental tensors expressed as in Eq. (
35
) is straightforwardly generalized to other models just modifying the definition of the Grassmann parity function in Eq. (
38
).

Similarly, we introduce the following expression for Eq. (
15
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n;XT\bar{X}\bar{T}}=\sum_{x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}}T_{n;xtx^{\prime}t^{\prime}}X^{x}T^{t}\bar{X}^{x^{\prime}}\bar{T}^{t^{\prime}}.

and

Eq. (

\displaystyle\int_{\bar{\Theta},\Theta}\mathcal{T}_{n+\hat{1};XT_{1}\bar{\Theta}\bar{T}_{1}}\mathcal{T}_{n;\Theta T_{2}\bar{X}\bar{T}_{2}}=\sum_{x,t_{1},t_{2},x^{\prime},t^{\prime}_{1},t^{\prime}_{2}}M_{xt_{1}t_{2}x^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2}}X^{x}T_{1}^{t^{\prime}_{1}}T_{2}^{t^{\prime}_{2}}\bar{X}^{x^{\prime}}\bar{T}_{2}^{t^{\prime}_{2}}\bar{T}_{1}^{t^{\prime}_{1}},

Note that

which is a natural extension of Eq. (

Extend the definition of the Grassmann parity function in Eq. (

as an example.
We assume the standard Wilson action,

\displaystyle S_{\rm g}=-\beta\sum_{n\in\Lambda}\Re\left[U_{1}(n)U_{2}(n+\hat{1})U^{*}_{1}(n+\hat{2})U^{*}_{2}(n)\right],

(46)

and the staggered fermion action,

\displaystyle S_{\rm f}=\sum_{n,\nu}\frac{\eta_{\nu}(n)}{2}\left[\bar{\chi}(n)U_{\nu}(n)\chi(n+\hat{\nu})-\bar{\chi}(n+\hat{\nu})U^{*}_{\nu}(n)\chi(n)\right]+m\sum_{n}\bar{\chi}(n)\chi(n).

(47)

\beta
and
m
denote the inverse gauge coupling and mass, respectively.
The staggered sign function
\eta_{\nu}(n)
is defined by
\eta_{1}(n)=1
and
\eta_{2}(n)=(-1)^{n_{1}}
at each site
n=(n_{1},n_{2})
.
Our goal is to derive the Grassmann tensor network representation for the following path integral,

\displaystyle Z=\int\prod_{n,\nu}{\rm d}U_{\nu}(n)\prod_{n}{\rm d}\chi(n){\rm d}\bar{\chi}(n)~{\rm e}^{-S}.

(48)

We parameterize
U_{\nu}(n)
by an integer
q_{\nu}(n)
mod
N
via
U_{\nu}(n)=\exp\left[2\pi{\rm i}q_{\nu}(n)/N\right]
.
Following Sec.
2.1
, one can immediately obtain a Grassmann tensor with some integer indices.
We begin with decomposing the hopping terms via

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{\eta_{\nu}(n)\bar{\chi}(n+\hat{\nu})U^{*}_{\nu}(n)\chi(n)/2}=\int{\rm d}\bar{\Phi}_{\nu}(n){\rm d}\Phi_{\nu}(n)~{\rm e}^{-\bar{\Phi}_{\nu}(n)\Phi_{\nu}(n)}~{\rm e}^{\bar{\chi}(n+\hat{\nu})\bar{\Phi}_{\nu}(n)/\sqrt{2}}~{\rm e}^{\eta_{\nu}(n)U^{*}_{\nu}(n)\chi(n)\Phi_{\nu}(n)/\sqrt{2}},

(49)

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{-\eta_{\nu}(n)\bar{\chi}(n)U_{\nu}(n)\chi(n+\hat{\nu})/2}=\int{\rm d}\bar{\Psi}_{\nu}(n){\rm d}\Psi_{\nu}(n)~{\rm e}^{-\bar{\Psi}_{\nu}(n)\Psi_{\nu}(n)}~{\rm e}^{\eta_{\nu}(n)U_{\nu}(n)\bar{\chi}(n)\Psi_{\nu}(n)/\sqrt{2}}~{\rm e}^{\chi(n+\hat{\nu})\bar{\Psi}_{\nu}(n)/\sqrt{2}}.

(50)

Integrating out the original staggered fields at each site, one obtains

\displaystyle\int{\rm d}\chi{\rm d}\bar{\chi}~{\rm e}^{-m\bar{\chi}\chi}\prod_{\nu}{\rm e}^{\bar{\chi}\bar{\Phi}_{\nu}(n-\hat{\nu})/\sqrt{2}}{\rm e}^{\eta_{\nu}(n)U_{\nu}(n)\bar{\chi}\Psi_{\nu}(n)/\sqrt{2}}{\rm e}^{\eta_{\nu}(n)U^{*}_{\nu}(n)\chi\Phi_{\nu}(n)/\sqrt{2}}{\rm e}^{\chi\bar{\Psi}_{\nu}(n-\hat{\nu})/\sqrt{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{\rm f},t_{\rm f},x^{\prime}_{\rm f},t^{\prime}_{\rm f}}T^{({\rm f})}_{x_{\rm f}t_{\rm f}x^{\prime}_{\rm f}t^{\prime}_{\rm f},q_{2}(n)q_{1}(n)}X^{x_{\rm f}}T^{t_{\rm f}}\bar{X}^{x^{\prime}_{\rm f}}\bar{T}^{t^{\prime}_{\rm f}},

(51)

where we have employed the model-independent notation in Sec.
2.3
and the coefficient tensor is given by

\displaystyle T^{({\rm f})}_{x_{\rm f}t_{\rm f}x^{\prime}_{\rm f}t^{\prime}_{\rm f},q_{2}(n)q_{1}(n)}

\displaystyle=T^{({\rm f})}_{(i_{1}j_{1})(i_{2}j_{2})(i^{\prime}_{1}j^{\prime}_{1})(i^{\prime}_{2}j^{\prime}_{2}),q_{2}(n)q_{1}(n)}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2}^{-\sum_{\nu}(i_{\nu}+j_{\nu}+i^{\prime}_{\nu}+j^{\prime}_{\nu})}(-1)^{j_{1}(i_{2}+j^{\prime}_{1}+j^{\prime}_{2})+j_{2}(j^{\prime}_{1}+j^{\prime}_{2})+i^{\prime}_{1}j^{\prime}_{2}+n_{1}(i_{2}+j_{2})}{\rm e}^{\frac{2\pi{\rm i}}{N}\sum_{\nu}q_{\nu}(n)\left(j_{\nu}-i_{\nu}\right)}

\displaystyle\times\left[-m\delta_{i_{1}+i_{2}+j^{\prime}_{1}+j^{\prime}_{2},0}\delta_{i^{\prime}_{1}+i^{\prime}_{2}+j_{1}+j_{2},0}+\delta_{i_{1}+i_{2}+j^{\prime}_{1}+j^{\prime}_{2},1}\delta_{i^{\prime}_{1}+i^{\prime}_{2}+j_{1}+j_{2},1}\right].

(52)

Note that
X^{x_{\rm f}}
,
T^{t_{\rm f}}
,
\bar{X}^{x^{\prime}_{\rm f}}
, and
\bar{T}^{t^{\prime}_{\rm f}}
in the right-hand side of Eq. (
2.4
) have been defined in the same way with Eq. (
42
).
We also introduce a four-leg tensor to describe the plaquette interaction term in
{\rm e}^{-S_{\rm g}}
via

\displaystyle T^{({\rm g})}_{q_{2}(n+\hat{1})q_{1}(n+\hat{2})q_{2}(n)q_{1}(n)}=\exp\left[\beta\cos\left\{\frac{2\pi}{N}\left(q_{1}(n)+q_{2}(n+\hat{1})-q_{1}(n+\hat{2})-q_{2}(n)\right)\right\}\right],

(53)

following Ref.
[
Kuramashi:2019cgs
]
.
Regarding
q_{\nu}
’s as tensor subscripts, we now define the fundamental tensor associated at the lattice site
n
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n;XT\bar{X}\bar{T},x_{\rm g}t_{\rm g}x^{\prime}_{\rm g}t^{\prime}_{\rm g}}=\sum_{x_{\rm f},t_{\rm f},x^{\prime}_{\rm f},t^{\prime}_{\rm f}}T_{n;x_{\rm f}t_{\rm f}x^{\prime}_{\rm f}t^{\prime}_{\rm f},x_{\rm g}t_{\rm g}x^{\prime}_{\rm g}t^{\prime}_{\rm g}}X^{x_{\rm f}}T^{t_{\rm f}}\bar{X}^{x^{\prime}_{\rm f}}\bar{T}^{t^{\prime}_{\rm f}},

(54)

where

\displaystyle T_{n;x_{\rm f}t_{\rm f}x^{\prime}_{\rm f}t^{\prime}_{\rm f},x_{\rm g}t_{\rm g}x^{\prime}_{\rm g}t^{\prime}_{\rm g}}=T^{({\rm f})}_{x_{\rm f}t_{\rm f}x^{\prime}_{\rm f}t^{\prime}_{\rm f},x_{\rm g}t_{\rm g}}\cdot T^{({\rm g})}_{x_{\rm g}t_{\rm g}x^{\prime}_{\rm g}t^{\prime}_{\rm g}}.

(55)

The path integral in Eq. (
48
) is now represented by

\displaystyle Z={\rm gTr}\left[\prod_{n}\mathcal{T}_{n}\right].

(56)

Here,
{\rm``gTr"}
stands not only for the integrations over all auxiliary Grassmann variables but also for the summations over all integers corresponding to the link variables.
Analogously, we refer
\mathcal{T}_{n}
and
T_{n}
in Eq. (
54
) to the Grassmann tensor and its coefficient tensor, respectively.
See Figure
4
for the diagrammatic explanation.

Let us briefly see how the exact contraction is carried out between these fundamental tensors.
As in Sec.
2.2
, we consider the contraction between the fundamental tensors at
n+\hat{1}
and
n
for demonstration.
Since a link variable is shared between these two fundamental tensors, the exact contraction should be

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha_{\rm g}}\int_{\bar{\Theta},\Theta}\mathcal{T}_{n+\hat{1};XT_{1}\bar{\Theta}\bar{T}_{1},x_{\rm g}{t_{\rm g}}_{1}\alpha_{\rm g}{t^{\prime}_{\rm g}}_{1}}\mathcal{T}_{n;\Theta T_{2}\bar{X}\bar{T}_{2},\alpha_{\rm g}{t_{\rm g}}_{2}x^{\prime}_{\rm g}{t^{\prime}_{\rm g}}_{2}}.

(57)

One will immediately find that the above contraction results in a new Grassmann tensor such that,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{XT_{1}T_{2}\bar{X}\bar{T}_{2}\bar{T}_{1},x_{\rm g}{t_{\rm g}}_{1}{t_{\rm g}}_{2}x^{\prime}_{\rm g}{t^{\prime}_{\rm g}}_{1}{t^{\prime}_{\rm g}}_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{\rm f},{t_{\rm f}}_{1},{t_{\rm f}}_{2},x^{\prime}_{\rm f},{t^{\prime}_{\rm f}}_{1},{t^{\prime}_{\rm f}}_{2}}M_{x_{\rm f}{t_{\rm f}}_{1}{t_{\rm f}}_{2}x^{\prime}_{\rm f}{t^{\prime}_{\rm f}}_{1},{t^{\prime}_{\rm f}}_{2},x_{\rm g}{t_{\rm g}}_{1}{t_{\rm g}}_{2}x^{\prime}_{\rm g}{t^{\prime}_{\rm g}}_{1}{t^{\prime}_{\rm g}}_{2}}X^{x_{\rm f}}T_{1}^{{t_{\rm f}}_{1}}T_{2}^{{t_{\rm f}}_{2}}\bar{X}^{x^{\prime}_{\rm f}}\bar{T}_{2}^{{t^{\prime}_{\rm f}}_{2}}\bar{T}_{1}^{{t^{\prime}_{\rm f}}_{1}},

(58)

whose coefficient tensor is defined by

\displaystyle M_{x_{\rm f}{t_{\rm f}}_{1}{t_{\rm f}}_{2}x^{\prime}_{\rm f}{t^{\prime}_{\rm f}}_{1},{t^{\prime}_{\rm f}}_{2},x_{\rm g}{t_{\rm g}}_{1}{t_{\rm g}}_{2}x^{\prime}_{\rm g}{t^{\prime}_{\rm g}}_{1}{t^{\prime}_{\rm g}}_{2}}=\sum_{\alpha_{\rm f},\alpha_{\rm g}}(-1)^{f_{\alpha_{\rm f}}}T_{n+\hat{1};x_{\rm f}{t_{\rm f}}_{1}\alpha_{\rm f}{t^{\prime}_{\rm f}}_{1},x_{\rm g}{t_{\rm g}}_{1}\alpha_{\rm g}{t^{\prime}_{\rm g}}_{1}}T_{n;\alpha_{\rm f}{t_{\rm f}}_{2}x^{\prime}_{\rm f}{t^{\prime}_{\rm f}}_{2},\alpha_{\rm g}{t_{\rm g}}_{2}x^{\prime}_{\rm g}{t^{\prime}_{\rm g}}_{2}}.

(59)

The Grassmann parity function
f_{\alpha_{\rm f}}
is given in the same way with Eq. (
38
).
Introducing a super index
p=(p_{\rm f},p_{\rm g})
for
p=x,t,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}
, Eq. (
59
) reads

\displaystyle M_{xt_{1}t_{2}x^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2}}=\sum_{\alpha}(-1)^{F_{\alpha}}T_{n+\hat{1};xt_{1}\alpha t^{\prime}_{1}}T_{n;\alpha t_{2}x^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{2}},

(60)

where we have defined a new parity function for the super index
\alpha=(\alpha_{\rm f},\alpha_{\rm g})
by

\displaystyle F_{\alpha}=f_{\alpha_{\rm f}}.

(61)

The function
F_{\alpha}
tells us the Grassmann parity of the super index
\alpha
: when
F_{\alpha}=0~(1)
, the super index
\alpha
is describing the Grassmann-even (odd) contribution.

Therefore, we reach the important conclusion that the structure of the Grassmann tensor network and the contraction rule are not affected by the gauge fields.
The resulting coefficient tensor in Eq. (
60
) has the same expression as Eq. (
40
), which was the coefficient tensor for the pure fermionic model in Eq. (
1
).
The exact contractions among the Grassmann tensors result in those among the coefficient tensors, with or without the lattice gauge fields.
One can use the tensor network diagram in Figure
3
(B) instead of Figure
4
(B) to represent Eq. (
56
).
Although we have assumed
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
as a gauge group for simplicity, the above construction can be combined with other tensor network formulations for various lattice gauge theories including the non-Abelian fields
[
Liu:2013nsa
,
Fukuma:2021cni
,
Hirasawa:2021qvh
,
Dittrich:2014mxa
,
Kuwahara:2022ubg
,
Luo:2022eje
,
Akiyama:2022eip
]
.

2.5
Approximate contraction by the Levin-Nave TRG

The usual TRG algorithms are intended to be applied to the classical systems without any Grassmann variables.
Still, any TRG algorithm can be utilized to evaluate the Grassmann path integrals.
As we have seen, the contractions between the Grassmann tensors always result in those between the corresponding coefficient tensors with some sign factors arising from the integrals over the auxiliary Grassmann variables.
Therefore, we can use the TRG algorithms to carry out the contractions among the coefficient tensors approximately.
In this subsection, we demonstrate how to extend the Levin-Nave TRG
[
Levin:2006jai
]
for the Grassmann tensor networks. The case of the HOTRG
[
2012PhRvB..86d5139X
]
is discussed in the next subsection.

Figure 5:

Schematic illustration of the Levin-Nave TRG algorithm.
Background dotted lines show a real-space square lattice.
(A) Initial tensor network on the lattice.
(B) Two kinds of SVD as shown in Eqs. (
63
) and (
64
).
(C) New tensor network by contracting four three-leg tensors.

Let us begin with reviewing the original Levin-Nave TRG.
The algorithm aims to evaluate the partition function or path integral represented by the tensor network

\displaystyle Z={\rm tTr}\left[\prod_{n\in\Lambda}T_{n}\right],

(62)

where we assume the two-dimensional periodic square lattice
\Lambda
and
T_{n}
is a four-leg tensor defined on the site
n
.
The algorithm employs the singular value decomposition (SVD) to decimate the four-leg tensor
T_{n}
into three-leg tensors:

\displaystyle T_{n;xtx^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\simeq\sum_{a=1}^{D_{\rm LNTRG}}A_{n;xta}B_{n;ax^{\prime}t^{\prime}},

(63)

\displaystyle T_{n;xtx^{\prime}t^{\prime}}\simeq\sum_{a=1}^{D_{\rm LNTRG}}C_{n;xt^{\prime}a}D_{n;ax^{\prime}t}.

(64)

Each three-leg tensor is defined as a unitary matrix multiplied by the square root of its singular value.
In the above expressions, we have assumed that the singular values are in descending order.
The truncation parameter
D_{\rm LNTRG}
is called the bond dimension.
Since we have used the SVD, Eqs. (
63
) and (
64
) give the best approximation in terms of the Frobenious norm of
T_{n}
under the fixed bond dimension.
Then, we define a new four-leg tensor via

\displaystyle T_{n^{\prime};xtx^{\prime}t^{\prime}}=\sum_{x_{1},x_{2},t_{1},t_{2}}C_{n+\hat{2};x_{2}t_{1}x}A_{n;x_{1}t_{1}t}D_{n+\hat{1};x^{\prime}x_{1}t_{2}}B_{n+\hat{1}+\hat{2};t^{\prime}x_{2}t_{2}},

(65)

which approximates the original
Z
as

\displaystyle Z\simeq{\rm tTr}\left[\prod_{n^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}T_{n^{\prime}}\right].

(66)

Therefore,
T_{n^{\prime}}
is a new fundamental tensor defined on a site
n^{\prime}
in the coarse-grained lattice
\Lambda^{\prime}
.
Since Eq. (
66
) describes the tensor network whose geometry is the same as that in Eq. (
62
), we can easily repeat the above decimation procedure.
Repeating this procedure
N
times,
2^{N}
original fundamental tensors are approximately contracted.
Thanks to this property, the algorithm allows us to evaluate the partition function in the thermodynamic limit.
The Levin-Nave TRG requires the
O\left(D_{\rm LNTRG}^{6}\right)
complexity and the
O\left(D_{\rm LNTRG}^{4}\right)
memory cost.
Note that the cost can further be reduced to the
O\left(D_{\rm LNTRG}^{5}\right)
complexity and the
O\left(D_{\rm LNTRG}^{3}\right)
memory using the randomized SVD
[
2018PhRvE..97c3310M
]
.
The algorithm is graphically summarized in Figure
5
.

We now need to define the SVD for the Grassmann tensor to extend the algorithm for the Grassmann tensor network.
Suppose
\mathcal{O}_{\Phi\Psi}
is a Grassmann tensor defined via

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\Phi\Psi}=\sum_{i,j}O_{ij}\Phi^{i}\Psi^{j},

(67)

where we have assumed the notation in Sec.
2.3
, say
i=(i_{1}\cdots i_{m})
and
j=(j_{1}\cdots j_{n})
with
\Phi^{i}=\Phi_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots\Phi_{m}^{i_{m}}
and
\Psi^{j}=\Psi_{1}^{j_{1}}\cdots\Psi_{n}^{j_{n}}
. We also assume that
\mathcal{O}
is Grassmann-even, in other words, the matrix elements are zero unless the sum of all the indices (
i
and
j
) is even.
Since the coefficient tensor is normal, we can apply the SVD for
O_{ij}
,

\displaystyle O_{ij}=\sum_{a=1}^{\min(2^{m},2^{n})}U_{ia}\sigma_{a}V^{{\dagger}}_{aj}=\sum_{a,b}A_{ia}\delta_{ab}B_{bj}.

(68)

In the second equality, we set
A=U\sqrt{\sigma}
and
B=\sqrt{\sigma}V^{{\dagger}}
.
Recalling the identity in Eq. (
32
), we can express
\delta_{ab}
by introducing new auxiliary Grassmann variables.
Plugging Eq. (
68
) into Eq. (
67
), we now have the SVD for the Grassmann tensor
\mathcal{O}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\Phi\Psi}=\int_{\bar{\Xi},\Xi}\mathcal{A}_{\Phi\Xi}\mathcal{B}_{\bar{\Xi}\Psi},

(69)

where
\Xi
and
\bar{\Xi}
are the
\min(m,n)
-component auxiliary Grassmann variables introduced via Eq. (
32
).
The Grassmann tensors
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
in the right-hand side have
A
and
B
in Eq. (
68
) as their coefficient tensors.
Since we have assumed that
\mathcal{O}
is Grassmann-even, the resulting
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are also Grassmann-even, as we will see in Sec.
2.7
.
It is no exaggeration to say that the SVD for a Grassmann tensor is the SVD for its coefficient tensor.
There is no difficulty in introducing the low-rank approximation based on the SVD.
In the following, we use the approximation

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}_{\Phi\Psi}\simeq\int_{\bar{\Xi},\Xi}^{D_{\rm LNTRG}}\mathcal{A}_{\Phi\Xi}\mathcal{B}_{\bar{\Xi}\Psi},

(70)

which means that the SVD of the coefficient tensor is truncated up to the bond dimension
D_{\rm LNTRG}
as

\displaystyle O_{ij}\simeq\sum_{a,b=1}^{D_{\rm LNTRG}}A_{ia}\delta_{ab}B_{bj}.

(71)

We will make some practical remarks on the SVD of the Grassmann tensor in Sec.
2.7

Diagrammatic representation of Eq. (
75
).

We now extend the algorithm for evaluating

\displaystyle Z={\rm gTr}\left[\prod_{n\in\Lambda}\mathcal{T}_{n}\right],

(72)

with the Grassmann tensor
\mathcal{T}_{n;XT\bar{X}\bar{T}}
.
We can formally write the SVD for the Grassmann tensor corresponding to Eqs. (
63
) and (
64
) as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n;XT\bar{X}\bar{T}}\simeq\int_{\bar{\Xi},\Xi}^{D_{\rm LNTRG}}\mathcal{A}_{n;XT\Xi}\mathcal{B}_{n;\bar{\Xi}\bar{X}\bar{T}},

(73)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n;XT\bar{X}\bar{T}}\simeq\int_{\bar{\Xi},\Xi}^{D_{\rm LNTRG}}\mathcal{C}_{n;X\bar{T}\Xi}\mathcal{D}_{n;\bar{\Xi}\bar{X}T}.

(74)

When
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is Grassmann-even, then
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{B}
,
\mathcal{C}
, and
\mathcal{D}
are also Grassmann-even.
Therefore, we can easily define a new Grassmann tensor from the contractions among them,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{n^{\prime};XT\bar{X}\bar{T}}=\int_{\bar{X}_{1},X_{1}}\int_{\bar{X}_{2},X_{2}}\int_{\bar{T}_{1},T_{1}}\int_{\bar{T}_{2},T_{2}}\mathcal{C}_{n+\hat{2};X_{2}\bar{T}_{1}X}\mathcal{A}_{n;X_{1}T_{1}T}\mathcal{D}_{n+\hat{1};\bar{X}\bar{X}_{1}T_{2}}\mathcal{B}_{n+\hat{1}+\hat{2};\bar{T}\bar{X}_{2}\bar{T}_{2}}.

(75)

The above equation is analogous to Eq. (
65
) in the usual Levin-Nave TRG.
Eq. (
75
) is diagrammatically shown in Figure
6
.
The new Grassmann tensor approximates the path integral by

\displaystyle Z\simeq{\rm gTr}\left[\prod_{n^{\prime}\in\Lambda^{\prime}}\mathcal{T}_{n^{\prime}}\right].

(76)

The resulting Grassmann tensor network in Eq. (
76
) is identical to the previous one in Eq. (
72
), including the ordering of the Grassmann measures in
{\rm gTr}
.
Therefore, one can easily repeat the decimation procedure toward the thermodynamic limit as in the usual Levin-Nave TRG.
We can understand that the SVD of the Grassmann tensor introduces new auxiliary Grassmann variables on the coarse-grained lattice
\Lambda^{\prime}
.
Therefore, the algorithm for the Grassmann tensor network perfectly corresponds to that for the normal tensor network as shown in Figure
5
.
In terms of coefficient tensors, several sign factors should be included in Eqs. (
74
) and (
75
) as we will see in Sec.
2.7
.

2.6
Approximate contraction by the Higher-Order TRG

Figure 7:

(A) Initial tensor network on a square lattice.

Red and blue symbols show

and

in Eq. (

), respectively.
(C) New tensor network by contracting adjacent two fundamental tensors with two projectors.

Let us next focus on the HOTRG algorithm that applies to any
d
-dimensional system.
The computational complexity scales with
O\left(D_{\rm HOTRG}^{4d-1}\right)
and the memory cost does with
O\left(D_{\rm HOTRG}^{2d}\right)
, where
D_{\rm HOTRG}
is the bond dimension in the HOTRG.
For simplicity, we consider the two-dimensional tensor network on a periodic square lattice again.
The original algorithm aims to evaluate Eq. (
62
) not decimating each local fundamental tensor but inserting the projectors that describe the coarse-graining transformation in the tensor-network language.
Unlike the Levin-Nave TRG, the HOTRG performs the contraction between two adjacent fundamental tensors along each direction sequentially.

We begin with reviewing the normal HOTRG.
As an example, we consider the contraction between
T_{n+\hat{1}}
and
T_{n}
.
Introducing

\displaystyle M_{xt_{1}t_{2}x^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2}}=\sum_{\alpha}T_{n+\hat{1};xt_{1}\alpha t^{\prime}_{1}}T_{n;\alpha t_{2}x^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{2}},

(77)

the HOTRG provides the coarse-graining transformation such that

\displaystyle T_{n^{\prime};xtx^{\prime}t^{\prime}}=\sum_{t_{1},t_{2},t^{\prime}_{1},t^{\prime}_{2}}P_{t_{1}t_{2}t}M_{xt_{1}t_{2}x^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2}}Q_{t^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2}},

(78)

where the three-leg tensors
P
and
Q
are projectors to decimate the degrees of freedom.
They play roles to map the original degrees of freedom
(t^{(^{\prime})}_{1}t^{(^{\prime})}_{2})
to the coarse-grained one
t^{(^{\prime})}
, whose size is restricted by
D_{\rm HOTRG}
, the bond dimension in the algorithm.
We can also regard that the HOTRG is inserting the following four-leg tensor,

\displaystyle W_{t_{1}t_{2}t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2}}=\sum_{t=1}^{D_{\rm HOTRG}}P_{t_{1}t_{2}t}Q_{tt^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2}},

(79)

into the tensor network.
With sufficiently large
D_{\rm HOTRG}
,
W_{t_{1}t_{2}t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2}}
should be equivalent to
\delta_{t_{1},t^{\prime}_{1}}\delta_{t_{2},t^{\prime}_{2}}
and the algorithm gives the exact contraction.

T_{n^{\prime}}
generates the approximated tensor network representation of the partition function.
The coarse-graining transformation in the HOTRG can be easily repeated.
As in the case of the Levin-Nave TRG, the HOTRG allows us to contract
2^{N}
original fundamental tensors approximately, just in
N
times of iteration.
Figure
7
graphically demonstrates the procedure of the algorithm.

There are several ways to determine
P
and
Q
.
Here, we just follow the original proposal in Ref.
[
2012PhRvB..86d5139X
]
assuming the translational symmetry for the tensor network in Eq. (
62
).
We consider the following two reduced density matrices,

\displaystyle\rho_{(t_{1}t_{2})(\tilde{t}_{1}\tilde{t}_{2})}=\sum_{x,x^{\prime},t^{\prime}_{1},t^{\prime}_{2}}M_{xt_{1}t_{2}x^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2}}M_{x\tilde{t}_{1}\tilde{t}_{2}x^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2}}^{*},

(80)

\displaystyle\rho_{(t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2})(\tilde{t}^{\prime}_{1}\tilde{t}^{\prime}_{2})}=\sum_{x,t_{1},t_{2},x^{\prime}}M_{xt_{1}t_{2}x^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2}}M_{xt_{1}t_{2}x^{\prime}\tilde{t}^{\prime}_{1}\tilde{t}^{\prime}_{2}}^{*}.

(81)

These matrices can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\rho_{(t_{1}t_{2})(\tilde{t}_{1}\tilde{t}_{2})}=\sum_{i}U_{(t_{1}t_{2})i}\lambda_{i}U^{{\dagger}}_{i(\tilde{t}_{1}\tilde{t}_{2})},

(82)

\displaystyle\rho^{\prime}_{(t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2})(\tilde{t}^{\prime}_{1}\tilde{t}^{\prime}_{2})}=\sum_{i}V_{(t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2})i}\lambda^{\prime}_{i}V^{{\dagger}}_{i(\tilde{t}^{\prime}_{1}\tilde{t}^{\prime}_{2})},

(83)

where
U
and
V
are unitary matrices and
\lambda
and
\lambda^{\prime}
denote the singular-value matrices with descending order.
Defining the following quantities,

\displaystyle\epsilon^{(^{\prime})}=\sum_{i>D_{\rm HOTRG}}\lambda^{(^{\prime})}_{i},

(84)

we choose
P_{t_{1}t_{2}t}=U^{*}_{(t_{1}t_{2})t}
and
Q_{t^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2}}=U_{(t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2})t^{\prime}}
if
\epsilon<\epsilon^{\prime}
and choose
P_{t_{1}t_{2}t}=V_{(t_{1}t_{2})t}
and
Q_{t^{\prime}t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2}}=V^{*}_{(t^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}_{2})t^{\prime}}
if
\epsilon>\epsilon^{\prime}
.
This procedure is equivalent to the higher-order SVD (HOSVD), which is a tensorial extension of the SVD as explained in Ref.
[
2012PhRvB..86d5139X
]
.
See Refs.
[
wang2011cluster
,
PhysRevLett.113.046402
,
PhysRevB.100.035449
]
for other ways to derive the optimal
P
and
Q
without assuming the translational invariance on the tensor network.

The HOTRG algorithm is straightforwardly extended to evaluate the Grassmann tensor network in Eq. (
72
).
Eq. (

We have reviewed two types of TRG algorithms so far and both of them employ the local approximation based on the (HO)SVD to define the coarse-graining transformations.
By constructing a coarse-graining transformation that includes not only the local tensor(s) but also the effects of the surrounding tensors (they are usually referred to as the environment), we can construct a more accurate transformation.
This kind of improvement is called the second renormalization group (SRG)
[
PhysRevLett.103.160601
,
PhysRevB.81.174411
,
2012PhRvB..86d5139X
]
.
One of the other ways to improve the TRG algorithms is to remove the redundant loop structure in the tensor network
[
Gu:2009dr
]
.
This can be achieved by the tensor network renormalization (TNR)
[
2015PhRvL.115r0405E
,
2015arXiv150907484E
]
.
We will see several TNR-type algorithms in Sec.
.

In addition to the TRG approach, many other algorithms perform approximate tensor contractions, and they are used according to their purpose and cost performance.
For example, corner transfer matrix renormalization group (CTMRG)
[
doi:10.1143/JPSJ.65.891
]
and time-evolving block decimation (TEBD)
[
PhysRevB.78.155117
,
PhysRevB.78.155117
]
are widely used in the tensor network computations based on the Hamiltonian formalism.
See Ref.
[
Okunishi:2021but
]
as a recent review.
