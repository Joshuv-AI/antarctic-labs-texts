Sufficient dimension reduction for regression with metric space-valued responses
Abdul-Nasah Soale

Abstract
Data visualization and dimension reduction for regression between a general metric space-valued response and Euclidean predictors is proposed. Current Fréchét dimension reduction methods require that the response metric space be continuously embeddable into a Hilbert space, which imposes restriction on the type of metric and kernel choice. We relax this assumption by proposing a Euclidean embedding technique which avoids the use of kernels. Under this framework, classical dimension reduction methods such as ordinary least squares and sliced inverse regression are extended. An extensive simulation experiment demonstrates the superior performance of the proposed method on synthetic data compared to existing methods where applicable. The real data analysis of factors influencing the distribution of COVID-19 transmission in the U.S. and the association between BMI and structural brain connectivity of healthy individuals are also investigated.



Keywords:
Fréchét regression, non-Euclidean responses, metric embedding, COVID-19 transmission, structural brain connectivity

1
Introduction

Regression analysis with Euclidean data
({\bf Y},{\bf X})
, where the response
{\bf Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
and predictor
{\bf X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, for some positive integers
p,q\geq 1
, is well-established. Lately, however, there has been a growing demand for regression with general metric space-valued objects that may belong to non-Euclidean spaces such as the space of positive definite matrices, probability distributions, networks, and spheres, to mention a few. Examples of regression with such random objects can be found in
Faraway 2014
;
Petersen & Müller 2019
, and
Petersen et al. 2019
. In some of these non-Euclidean metric spaces, standard properties of vector spaces such as inner products may not be applicable, which makes it difficult to implement classical regression methods directly. To address this problem, Fréchét regression was recently proposed by
Petersen & Müller 2019
.

Fréchét regression basically extends the classical regression in the Euclidean space to responses in general metric spaces.
Let
(\bm{\Omega}_{Y},\rho)
denote a metric space equipped with a metric
\rho
. For a random object
({\bf X},Y)\in(\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\bm{\Omega}_{Y})
, assume the conditional distributions
F_{X|Y}
and
F_{Y|X}
are well-defined. Then the conditional Fréchét mean of
Y|{\bf X}={\bm{x}}
is given by

\displaystyle m_{\oplus}({\bm{x}})=\argmin_{\omega\in\bm{\Omega}_{Y}}\E[\rho^{2}(Y,\omega)|{\bf X}={\bm{x}}].

(1)

From model (
1
), it is not hard to see that beyond the complex nature of
Y\in\bm{\Omega}_{Y}
, the problems associated with
{\bf X}
in the classical mean regression such as large
p
and outliers, easily carryover to Fréchét regression. Thus, sufficient dimension reduction still plays a crucial role in visualization and extracting relevant information in Fréchét regression with high dimensional
{\bf X}
.

For the regression between
Y\in\bm{\Omega}_{Y}
and
{\bf X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, a subspace
{\bf B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
is a Fréchet dimension reduction subspace if

\displaystyle Y\perp\!\!\!\perp{\bf X}|{\bf B}^{\top}{\bf X},

(2)

where
``\perp\!\!\!\perp"
means statistical independence and
{\bf B}^{\top}{\bf B}={\bf I}_{d}
, with
d<p
. While
{\bf B}
is not identifiable, it can be shown that the column space of
{\bf B}
, denoted as
\mathrm{span}({\bf B})
, is unique under some mild conditions (
Cook 1996
and
Yin et al. 2008
). If it exists, the intersection of all
{\bf B}
’s that satisfies (
2
) will have the smallest column space and is called the Fréchet central space for the regression of
Y
on
{\bf X}
. We denote the central space as
{\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}}=\mathrm{span}({\bf B})
and
dim({\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}})=d
as the structural dimension.

Sometimes we may only be interested in some part of the central space such as the mean space. In such a case, we restrict our attention to finding
\bm{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
such that

\displaystyle Y\perp\!\!\!\perp\E(Y|{\bf X})|\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}{\bf X},

(3)

where
\bm{\Gamma}^{\top}\bm{\Gamma}={\bf I}_{d}
. The smallest column space of
\bm{\Gamma}
for which (
3
) holds is called the central mean space denoted as
{\mathcal{S}}_{E(Y|{\bf X})}
. The central mean space is a subspace of the central space, i.e.,
{\mathcal{S}}_{E(Y|{\bf X})}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}}
. Hence,
\mathrm{span}(\bm{\Gamma})\subseteq\mathrm{span}({\bf B})
.

Recently proposed Fréchet dimension reduction methods can be found in the works of
Ying & Yu 2022
;
Dong & Wu 2022
, and
Zhang et al. 2023
. The central theme in these methods involves finding an equivalent representation of
Y
in the Hilbert space that preserves the distance between the original objects in
\bm{\Omega}_{Y}
. With the embedding, existing dimension reduction techniques such as ordinary least squares (OLS;
Li & Duan 1989
), sliced inverse regression (SIR;
Li 1991
), minimum average variance estimation (MAVE) and its variants (eg.
Xia et al. 2002
;
Xia et al. 2009
) are then applied. It is assumed that
(\bm{\Omega}_{Y},\rho)
is continuously embeddable in a Hilbert space to allow for the construction of a
universal kernel
on
\bm{\Omega}_{Y}
.

However, this comes with some restrictions on the metric space. For instance,
(\bm{\Omega}_{Y},\rho)
needs to be of the negative type to allow for embedding in a complete and separable Hilbert space as stated in Proposition 2 of
Zhang et al. 2023
. While this assumption may be satisfied in many applications, the requirement can be quite restrictive. Moreover, whether a given kernel is universal or not depends on the metric space under consideration, as not all popular kernels such as the Gaussian and Laplacian kernels are universal beyond the Euclidean space, see
Micchelli et al. 2006
. Therefore, choosing the appropriate kernel is crucial even if the metric space is continuously embeddable in a Hilbert space. Besides, constructing kernels come with the additional cost of choosing the optimal tuning parameters, which is not trivial in some cases. Lastly, using the kernel approach can be computationally intensive.

In this paper, we propose a Euclidean embedding approach which allows for slight distortions. By merely requiring
(\bm{\Omega}_{Y},\rho)
to be embeddable in the Euclidean space, we avoid the continuous embedding condition. Note that the metric
\rho
already maps any two objects in the response space to
\mathbb{R}
. Thus, we only need to find a low dimensional representation of the pairwise distance matrix, which preserves the distance between the objects in their original space. There is an extensive body of work on how to do this, see
Huber 1985
;
Bickel et al. 2018
;
Matoušek 2013
, and
Li et al. 2008
. We adopt the random projections technique, which is easier to implement and computationally faster. In addition, complications associated with the kernel approach including choosing the appropriate kernel for specific metrics and finding optimal tuning parameters are avoided.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we review some concepts on embedding between metric spaces. The ensemble estimates of the central space at the population level are discussed in Section 3. In the Section 4, we present a few metric choices for specific metric spaces and the estimation algorithm for the central space. The performance of our proposed surrogate-assisted techniques compared to existing methods are provided for synthetic and real data in Sections 5 and 6, respectively. The paper concludes with a discussion in Section 7. All proofs are relegated to the appendix.

2
Embedding metric spaces into Euclidean space

This section reviews some properties of metric spaces.

Definition 1

(Metric)
.

For a non-empty set
\bm{\Omega}_{Y}
, let the function
\rho:\bm{\Omega}_{Y}\times\bm{\Omega}_{Y}\to[0,\infty)
. Then
(\bm{\Omega}_{Y},\rho)
is a metric space equipped with a metric
\rho
if for all
y,y^{\prime},y^{*}\in\bm{\Omega}_{Y}
, the following properties are satisfied: (1) positivity:
\rho(y,y^{\prime})\geq 0
with equality if and only if
y=y^{\prime}
; (2) symmetry:
\rho(y,y^{\prime})=\rho(y^{\prime},y)
; and (3) triangle inequality:
\rho(y,y^{\prime})\leq\rho(y,y^{*})+\rho(y^{*},y^{\prime})
.

Therefore, for a finite subset of points
\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\}\in\bm{\Omega}_{Y},n\geq 2
, we can compute the pairwise distance matrix
{\bf D}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
with elements
({\bf D}_{n})_{ij}=\rho(Y_{i},Y_{j})
, for
i,j=1,\ldots,n
.

Definition 2

(Isometric embedding)
.

Let
(\bm{\Omega}_{Y},\rho_{Y})
and
(\bm{\Omega}_{z},\rho_{Z})
be metric spaces. A mapping
f:(\bm{\Omega}_{Y},\rho_{Y})\to(\bm{\Omega}_{Z},\rho_{Z})
is an isometric embedding if
\rho_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})=\rho_{Z}\big(f(y),f(y^{\prime})\big)
for all
y,y^{\prime}\in\bm{\Omega}_{Y}
.

In other words, an isometric embedding exists if
f(.)
is a bijection. For example, let
\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\}\in\bm{\Omega}_{Y}
and
\{{\bf a}_{1},\ldots,{\bf a}_{n}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{k},1\leq k\leq n
. Then
\{{\bf a}_{1},\ldots,{\bf a}_{n}\}
is an isometric embedding of
\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\}
if
\rho(Y_{i},Y_{j})=\lVert a_{i}-a_{j}\rVert_{2}
,
\forall i,j=1,\ldots,n
, where
\lVert.\rVert_{2}
denotes the Euclidean norm. By theorem 2.4 of
Wells & Lynn 1975
,
(\bm{\Omega}_{Y},\rho)
is isometrically embeddable in a Hilbert space if
(\bm{\Omega}_{Y},\rho)
is of a negative type. This means, for every finite subset
\{Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\}\in\bm{\Omega}_{Y},n\geq 2
, there exists
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}=0
such that the quadratic form

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}\rho(Y_{i},Y_{j})\leq 0.

(4)

While isometric embeddings may exist theoretically, it is often impossible to represent one metric space exactly into another. Moreover, for the purpose of visualization and recovering unbiased estimates of the central space or central mean space, an approximate embedding with slight
distortion
may suffice.

Definition 3

(
\epsilon
-almost isometry)
.

An
\epsilon
-almost isometry between the metric spaces
(\bm{\Omega}_{Y},\rho_{Y})
and
(\bm{\Omega}_{z},\rho_{Z})
exists if for some
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, a mapping
f:(\bm{\Omega}_{Y},\rho_{Y})\to(\bm{\Omega}_{z},\rho_{Z})
is such that

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)\rho_{Y}(y,y^{\prime})\leq\rho_{Z}\big(f(y),f(y^{\prime})\big)\leq(1+\epsilon)\rho_{Y}(y,y^{\prime}),\ \text{for all}\ y,y^{\prime}\in\bm{\Omega}_{Y}.

(5)

In the Euclidean space, such approximate embeddings can be obtained through linear maps.

Theorem 1

(Johnson–Lindenstrauss Lemma)
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and for any integer
n
, let
k
be a positive integer such that
k\geq C\epsilon^{-2}\log n
. Then for any set of
n
points

\{{\bf a}_{1},\ldots,{\bf a}_{n}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, there is a mapping

f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
such that

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)\lVert{\bf a}_{i}-{\bf a}_{j}\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert f({\bf a}_{i})-f({\bf a}_{j})\rVert_{2}\leq(1+\epsilon)\lVert{\bf a}_{i}-{\bf a}_{j}\rVert_{2},\forall i,j=1,2,\ldots,n.

Theorem
1
is popular in the dimension reduction literature. It implies that every high dimensional Euclidean space can be embedded in a low dimensional Euclidean space with dimension in
\log(n)
. In fact,
f
is a random linear map by Lemma 2.5.2 of
Matoušek 2013
. It has also been shown that projecting the high dimensional Euclidean space onto a large finite set of unit vectors on the hypershpere
\mathbb{S}^{p-1}
achieves an
\epsilon
-almost isometry. See
Matoušek 2013
.

3
Ensemble estimates of the central space

3.1
General background to the ensemble technique

By the definition of sufficient dimension reduction in (
2
), if we let

\widetilde{Y}
be a random copy of
Y
, then
Y\perp\!\!\!\perp{\bf X}|{\bf B}^{\top}{\bf X}\implies(Y,\widetilde{Y})\perp\!\!\!\perp{\bf X}|{\bf B}^{\top}{\bf X}
. Also, by the transformational properties of the central space (Theorem 2.3 of
Li 2018
),
(Y,\widetilde{Y})\perp\!\!\!\perp{\bf X}|{\bf B}^{\top}{\bf X}\implies\psi(Y,\widetilde{Y})\perp\!\!\!\perp{\bf X}|{\bf B}^{\top}{\bf X}
, for any measurable function
\psi
. Therefore, we can define a surrogate response
Y^{s}
as a mapping
f:\rho(y,y^{\prime})\to\mathbb{R}
, for all
y,y^{\prime}\in\bm{\Omega}_{Y}
, where
f(.)
is some square integrable linear function.

Proposition 1
.

\mathcal{S}_{Y^{s}|{\bf X}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{\E(Y^{s}|{\bf X})}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{E(Y|{\bf X})}
.

Proposition
1
implies that both
{\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}}
and
{\mathcal{S}}_{E(Y|{\bf X})}
can be characterized using a collection of subspaces for the regression of
Y^{s}
versus
{\bf X}
. As proposed by
Yin & Li 2011
, if we choose a rich collection of functions
Y^{s}
, we can estimate the central space by aggregating the mean subspaces for each function. Here, we extend this idea to include aggregating the central subspaces as well.

Definition 4
.

Let
\xi
be a family of functions
\psi:\Omega_{Y}\to\mathbb{R}
. Then
\xi
characterizes

(a)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{E(Y|{\bf X})}\text{ if }\mathrm{span}\big\{\mathcal{S}_{\E(Y^{s}|{\bf X})}:\psi\in\xi\big\}={\mathcal{S}}_{E(Y|{\bf X})}.

(6)

(b)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}}\text{ if }\mathrm{span}\big\{\mathcal{S}_{Y^{s}|{\bf X}}:\psi\in\xi\big\}={\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}}

(7)

Part (a) of Definition
4
is called the central mean space (CMS) ensemble while (b) is referred to as the central space (CS) ensemble. We will denote either the CMS or CS ensemble for
\psi\in\xi
as
\mathcal{S}(\xi)
.

Proposition 2
.

Let
L_{2}(F_{Y})
be the class of square-integrable functions of
Y^{s}
and
\mathcal{I}
be a class of measurable indicator functions of
Y^{s}
. Also assume
L_{2}(F_{Y})
is dense in
\mathcal{I}
. Then
\xi\subseteq L_{2}(F_{Y})
implies that
\mathcal{S}(\xi)\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{E(Y|{\bf X})}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}}
, for all
\psi\in\xi
.

Following Proposition
2
, our ensemble estimate of
{\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}}
can be obtained from the spectral decomposition of the candidate matrix
{\bf M}:P_{XY}\times\xi\to\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
such that
\mathrm{Span}({\bf M})\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}}
, where
P_{XY}=\{F_{XY}\}
is a collection of all distributions of
({\bf X},Y)
. Note that some conditions required for the original estimator also carryover to the ensemble case. For instance, the linear conditional mean (LCM) assumption is still required for the Fréchet central space estimates based on OLS and SIR ensembles as LCM is imposed on only the predictor.

3.2
Surrogate-assisted OLS and SIR estimators of
{\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}}

The original OLS and SIR both require the linear conditional mean (LCM) assumption and applies here as well.

Assumption 1

(LCM)
.

We assume
\E({\bf X}|{\bf B}^{\top}{\bf X})
is a linear function of
{\bf B}^{\top}{\bf X}
if
\mathrm{span}({\bf B})\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}}
.

We start with the surrogate-assisted OLS (sa-OLS) estimator. First, we assume
{\bf X}
satisfies the LCM assumption and that
\bm{\Sigma}=\text{Var}({\bf X})
is nonsingular. Next, for all
\psi\in\xi
, let

\displaystyle\beta(\psi)

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{XY^{s}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}},

(8)

\displaystyle{\bf M}_{ols}

\displaystyle=\E\big(\beta(\psi)\beta^{\top}(\psi)\big),

(9)

where
\bm{\Sigma}_{XY^{s}}
denotes the covariance between
{\bf X}
and
Y^{s}
and the expectation in (
9
) is taken over all
\psi\in\xi
. Thus,
\mathrm{span}({\bf M}_{ols})\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}}
. Notice that unlike the classical OLS, the sa-OLS estimator can recover more than one direction in the central space. Moreover, for Euclidean responses, sa-OLS can handle heteroscedastic error models with monotone links even when
\E(Y|{\bf X})=\{0\}
. The latter will be demonstrated in the simulation study.

We now turn our attention to the surrogate-assisted sliced inverse regression (sa-SIR) estimator. As in the sa-OLS case, we assume
{\bf X}
satisfies the LCM assumption and
\bm{\Sigma}=\text{Var}({\bf X})
is nonsingular. Then the candidate matrix for the sa-SIR estimator is given by

\displaystyle{\bf M}_{sir}(.,\psi)=\E\left\{\text{Var}\big[\E\big({\bf X}|Y^{s}\big)\big]\right\},

(10)

where the outer expectation
\E\{.\}
is taken over all
\psi\in\xi
.

4
Metric choice and estimation algorithm

Let
({\bm{x}}_{1},y_{1}),\ldots,({\bm{x}}_{n},y_{n})
be a random sample of
({\bf X},Y)
. Following Theorem
1
, we define
y^{s}=u^{\top}{\bf D}_{n}
, where
{\bf D}_{n}
is a matrix of pairwise distances and
u\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}:\lVert u\rVert_{2}=1
. It is worth noting that for any given metric space, there may be several choices for
\rho
. However, we will only focus on popular metrics used in the existing literature for the response metric spaces considered here.

4.1
Metric choice for different response spaces

4.1.1
Euclidean responses

While the plethora of SDR methods are design for the Euclidean response, it is still worth exploring how the proposed method fares in this space, at least for a start, as this space is more tractable.
Let
Y_{n}=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\}\in\bm{\Omega}_{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{q}
, where
q\geq 1
. For
q=1
, we estimate the pairwise distances as
({\bf D}_{n})_{ij}=|y_{i}-y_{j}|,\forall i,j=1,\ldots,n
. When
q>1
, we propose to use the
\ell_{2}
-norm:
({\bf D}_{n})_{ij}=\big(\sum_{k=1}^{q}(y_{ik}-y_{jk})^{2}\big)^{1/2}
. The Mahalanobis distance is arguably more appropriate for high dimensional responses if it is suspected that the correlation between the responses is associated with the predictor.

4.1.2
Distribution as response

If
\bm{\Omega}_{Y}
is a space of probability distributions, we propose to use the Wasserstein metric as used in
Petersen & Müller 2019
;
Dong & Wu 2022
, and
Zhang et al. 2023
). The Wasserstein metric for probability measures
\zeta
and
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{q}
with finite
k
th moment is given by

\displaystyle W_{k}(\zeta,\nu)=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim\zeta\\
y^{\prime}\sim\nu\end{subarray}}\big(\E\lVert y-y^{\prime}\rVert_{k}\big)^{1/k},\quad\quad k\geq 1,

(11)

where
\lVert.\rVert_{k}
is the
\ell_{k}
-norm,
y
and
y^{\prime}
marginally distributed as
\zeta
and
\nu
, respectively, and the infimum is taken over all pairs of
(y,y^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
(
Panaretos & Zemel 2019
)).

For univariate distributions,
W_{k}(.)
is explicitly defined as

\displaystyle W_{k}(y,y^{\prime})=\lVert F_{y}^{-1}-F_{y^{\prime}}^{-1}\rVert_{k}=\left(\int_{0}^{1}\big\lvert F_{y}^{-1}(s)-F_{y^{\prime}}^{-1}(s)\big\rvert^{k}ds\right)^{1/k},

(12)

where
F_{y}^{-1}
and
F_{y^{\prime}}^{-1}
are the respective quantile functions of
y
and
y^{\prime}
. We prefer
W_{1}(.)
over the quadratic Wasserstein (
W_{2}
) used in previous studies because the former is more robust to outliers.

4.1.3
Network as response

Consider a network or graph
G=(V,E)
, where
V
is the set of
vertices (nodes)
and
E
is the set of
edges (links)
. The
order
of
G
is
N_{V}=|V|
and its
size
is
N_{E}=|E|
. Two distinct nodes
v_{1},v_{2}\in V
are
connected
if there is an edge between them, denoted as the ordered pair
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
. The
neighborhood
of node
v_{1}
is given by
\Gamma(v_{1})=\big\{v_{j}\in V:\{v_{1},v_{j}\}\in\E,v_{1}\neq v_{j}\big\}
. The
degree
of
v_{1}
is
|\Gamma(v_{1})|
.

There are several ways to characterize the topological structure of a network. A common approach is to use the
centrality
measure. Centrality
C
is a function defined over all nodes that returns a positive value, i.e.,
C:(G,v)\to[0,\infty)
, for all
v\in V
. A centrality measure can be specific to the degree, closeness, betweenness, or eigenvectors of the adjacency matrix. Let
G_{1}=(V,E_{1})
and
G_{2}=(V,E_{2})
be two random networks of the same order. The centrality distance (CD) proposed by
Roy et al. 2014
is given by

\displaystyle d_{C}(G_{1},G_{2})=\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}|C(G_{1},v)-C(G_{2},v)|.

(13)

Our centrality measure of choice is the degree centrality (node degree), which in effect represents some notion of “importance” or popularity of the node. Degree centrality distance does not account for the weight of the link between the nodes, which is very important in weighted networks.

To account for edge weights, we may use the
graph diffusion distance (DD)
proposed by
Hammond et al. 2013
. This distance measure involves two steps. First, we find the graph Laplacian exponential kernel matrix of each graph and then take the Frobenius norm of the difference of kernels. Let
{\bf A}_{G_{1}}
and
{\bf A}_{G_{2}}
be the adjacency matrices of graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
, respectively. The corresponding degree matrix of graph
G_{i}
is given by
{\bf D}_{G_{i}}
, which is an
N_{V}\times N_{V}
diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are
({\bf D}_{G_{i}})_{jj}=\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N_{V}}({\bf A}_{G_{i}})_{jk}
. The corresponding Laplacians are given by
\L_{G_{i}}={\bf D}_{G_{i}}-{\bf A}_{G_{i}}
. Then, at any given time
t
, the diffusion distance is given by

\displaystyle d_{t}(G_{1},G_{2})=\max_{t}\big(\lVert e^{-t\L_{1}}-e^{-t\L_{2}}\rVert_{F}^{2}\big)^{1/2},

(14)

where
\lVert.\rVert_{F}
is the matrix Frobenius norm. For static networks, we fixed
t=1
.

4.1.4
Functional responses

Here, we consider responses measured over time but not necessarily at fixed time intervals. A good metric for this space is one that takes into account both the phase and amplitude variations. Based on this idea,
Faraway 2014
proposed to use the Fréchet distance. We choose to use the discrete Fourier distance of
Agrawal et al. 1993
. For the discrete Fourier distance, we use the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) to map the response sequence to a low dimensional space to reveal the periodicities and their relative strengths at the periodic components. Then we find the Euclidean distance between the modulus of the Fourier coefficients.

Let
y(t)
denote the functional response measured at time (sample)
t=1,\ldots,T
. The DFT of
y(t)
is a sequence of complex numbers of the same length
T
given by

Y(k)=\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}y(t)e^{-i2\pi kt/T},k=0,1,2,\ldots,T-1,

(15)

where
Y(k)
denotes the
k
th spectral sample and
i=\sqrt{-1}
is the imaginary unit. By Parseval’s theorem, the Fourier transform preserves the Euclidean distance in both the time and frequency domains. Moreover, the Fourier transform maps the sequence to a low-dimensional space using only the first few Fourier coefficients, which means we do not need all the coefficients to reconstruct the original sequence. Thus, we can find the distance between any two functional responses by calculating the Euclidean distance between the modulus of their
\lfloor{T/2}\rfloor+1
coefficients as used in the R package
TSdist
(
Mori et al. 2016
).

4.2
Estimation algorithms

For ease of computation, it is a common practice to estimate the candidate matrix based on a standardized predictor
{\bf Z}=\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}({\bf X}-\bm{\mu})
, where
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\Sigma}
denote the mean and covariance of
{\bf X}
, respectively. By the invariance property of the central space, if
{\bf M}
is the candidate matrix for
Y
versus
{\bf Z}
, then
\bm{\Sigma}^{-1/2}\mathrm{Span}({\bf M})\in{\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}}
. The sa-OLS and sa-SIR estimation algorithms are provided in 1 and 2.

The procedure in Algorithm 2 can be followed to estimate the candidate matrix for other inverse regression methods not covered here such as sliced average variance estimation (SAVE;
Cook & Weisberg 1991
) and directional regression (DR;
Li & Wang 2007
). While contour regression (
Li et al. 2005
) is not explored in this paper, the interested reader could use the computed distances directly to select the contour empirical directions or follow Algorithm 2.

sa-OLS

large integer (

4.3
Asymptotic properties of the surrogate-assisted estimator

The asymptotic behavior of the ensemble estimators depend on the terms of the number of projections
N
and the sample size
n
. First, for any given
u
,
u^{\top}{\bf D}_{n}
is can be viewed as a projection of
{\bf D}_{n}
in the direction of
u
, which represents the characteristic function of
{\bf D}_{n}
at
u
. When
n
is large and
u
is sparse,
Bickel et al. 2018
showed that
u^{\top}{\bf D}_{n}
will be approximately normal. Thus, asymptotically, we expect the sa-OLS to be close to the best linear unbiased estimator if the regression link is monotone and
{\bf D}
is sparse.

However, regardless of the asymptotic distribution of
u^{\top}{\bf D}_{n}
, the weak law of large numbers guarantees that
{\bf M}(U_{N})\to\E[{\bf M}(U)]
as
N\to\infty
, where
U
is a random vector uniformly distributed on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Also, since
{\bf M}(u_{k})
is positive semidefinite,
{\bf M}(U_{N})
takes each
{\bf M}(u_{k})
into account. Another thing to consider is that
{\bf M}_{n}(u)
is a function of the moments based on the sample
({\bm{x}},u^{\top}{\bf D}_{n})
, which can be expressed as a function of the empirical distribution
F_{n}(u)
. Hence,
{\bf M}_{n}(u)
can be expanded around
F_{0}(u)
, the true distribution function of
({\bf X},Y)
under some regularity conditions detailed in
Fernholz & Fernholz 1983
.

Suppose Hadarmard differentiability holds and that
{\bf M}_{n}(u)
can be expanded around
F_{0}(u)
for any
u\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. We further assume the following expression holds

\displaystyle{\bf M}_{n}(u)={\bf M}(u)+\E_{n}[\bm{\Psi}({\bf X},{\bf D}_{n},u)]+\bm{R}_{n}(u),

(16)

where
{\bf M}(u)
is the true value whose estimate is
{\bf M}_{n}(u)
,
\bm{\Psi}({\bf X},{\bf D}_{n},u)
is a square integrable function statisfying the following:

\displaystyle\E[\bm{\Psi}({\bf X},{\bf D}_{n},u)]

\displaystyle=\bm{0},

(17)

\displaystyle\sup_{u\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\lVert\bm{R}_{n}(u)\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle=o_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

(18)

where
\E_{n}[\bm{\Psi}({\bf X},{\bf D}_{n},u)]
denotes the sample estimate of
\E[\bm{\Psi}({\bf X},{\bf D}_{n},u)]
and
\bm{R}_{n}(u)
is a remainder term. When these conditions in (
17
) and (
18
) are satisfied as well as the regularity conditions,
{\bf M}_{n}(U_{N})
is guaranteed to be
\sqrt{n}
-consistent for
\E[{\bf M}(U)]
and asymptotically normal. See
Li et al. 2008
for more details.

Theorem 2
.

Let
U
be a random vector uniformly distributed on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Given a sample of size
n
and
\bm{U}_{N}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{N})
for some
N\in\mathcal{Z}^{+}
. If (
16
), (
17
), and (
18
) are satisfied and
n=O(N)
, then

\displaystyle{\bf M}_{n}(\bm{U}_{N})

\displaystyle=\E[{\bf M}(\bm{U})]+O_{p}(n^{-1/2}),

(19)

\displaystyle\sqrt{n}\left\{{\bf M}_{n}(\bm{U}_{N})-\E[{\bf M}(\bm{U})]\right\}

\displaystyle\overset{d}{\to}N(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{M}),\text{ if }\lim_{n\to\infty}(n/N)=0,

(20)

where
\bm{\Sigma}_{M}
is some positive definite matrix.

Theorem
2
implies that the sample estimator for
\mathcal{S}_{Y|{\bf X}}
is
\sqrt{n}
consistent for estimating the true central space from the aggregate of the marginal central spaces of
\mathcal{S}_{u^{\top}{\bf D}_{n}|{\bf X}}
if
N\to\infty
at a rate faster than
n
. Therefore, for any random response object
Y
in a compact metric space
(\Omega_{Y},d)
, Proposition
1
and Theorem
2
guarantees the recovery of a consistent unbiased estimator for
{\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{span}\big({\bf M}_{n}(\bm{U}_{N})\big)\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}}
.

5
Simulation Experiments

In this section, we evaluate the performance of our proposed surrogate-assisted methods compared to other competing methods in the literature where possible on synthetic data. For each method, we measure the estimation accuracy as the Frobenius norm of the difference in projection matrices of the true and estimated bases. This evaluation metric is common in the SDR literature.

Let
{\bf B}
be the true basis of
{\mathcal{S}}_{Y|{\bf X}}
and
\hat{\bf B}
be the estimate of
{\bf B}
. Define the accuracy measure
\Delta
as

\displaystyle\Delta=\lVert{\bf P}_{{\bf B}}-{\bf P}_{\hat{\bf B}}\rVert_{F},

(21)

where
{\bf P}_{\bf A}={\bf A}({\bf A}^{\top}{\bf A})^{-1}{\bf A}^{\top}
and
\lVert.\rVert_{F}
is the matrix Frobenius norm. Thus, smaller values of
\Delta
indicate more accurate estimates.

7
Discussion

This paper proposes a data visualization and dimension reduction technique for regressing metric space-valued responses on Euclidean predictors. Adopting the Euclidean embedding technique allows us to bypass the generation of high-dimensional universal kernels on the response space as proposed in
Zhang et al. 2023
. Although we only illustrated this idea for responses that are Euclidean, univariate distributions, networks, and functional trajectories, our proposal readily extends to response objects in other metric spaces. Regardless of the response type, the key is to use the appropriate metric to get the pairwise distances. In addition, the proposed method can be extended to other SDR methods such as the minimum average variance estimate (MAVE) and outer product of gradient (OPG) and their extensions (
Xia et al. 2002
;
Xia et al. 2009
).

As demonstrated in both the simulation experiments and real data analyses, sa-OLS and sa-SIR are very powerful preprocessing tools in regression settings with complex responses. These real analyses also make a strong case for dimension reduction methods that go beyond the Euclidean space as they give room to explore the relationship between different aspects of the response objects and predictors. For instance, here we were able to see how different measures of the central tendency of the COVID transmission distributions vary with the sufficient predictor. On the contrary, if we were using the classical SDR methods, we would only be able to model a summary statistic of the transmission rates, say, the mean as a function of the demographics. But then, we may not gain any useful insights into how other statistics are influenced by the same factors without fitting models with those statistics. Hence, with the distributional responses we can kill several birds with one stone.

Lastly, while being able to model a random response object as a function of Euclidean predictors expands our boundaries of applications, there is still room for more novel methods that can handle complex mix of responses and predictors in several metric spaces. Finally, the methods illustrated in this paper cover only linear sufficient dimension reduction. An extension to nonlinear SDR would be an interesting domain to explore in the future.
