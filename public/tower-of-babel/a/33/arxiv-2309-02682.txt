Curvatures of metric Jordan algebras Thanks: * Corresponding author
Hui Zhang, Zaili Yan, Zhiqi Chen ∗

Abstract
. In this paper, we study metric Jordan algebras, i.e., Jordan algebras with an inner product, and draw parallels to metric Lie algebras studied extensively for understanding left-invariant Riemannian metrics on Lie groups. We first define the Jordan-Levi-Civita connection on metric Jordan algebras which share analogous construction of invariant super-connections on Lie super-groups, then show its uniqueness. Utilizing this connection, we introduce three natural curvature tensors on metric Jordan algebras, and obtain the corresponding formulas. Based on the curvature formulas, we prove that every formally real Jordan algebra admits a metric of non-positive Jordan curvature and a Jordan-Einstein metric of negative Jordan scalar curvature. Moreover, for nilpotent Jordan algebras, we prove that they admit no Jordan-Einstein metrics.

Introduction

A
left symmetric algebra
(write LSA for short) is a vector space
V
with a binary operation
(x,y)\rightarrow xy
satisfying

\displaystyle(xy)z-x(yz)=(yx)z-y(xz),~~\forall x,y,z\in V.

(1.1)

Define the associator as
(x,y,z):=(xy)z-x(yz),~\forall x,y,z\in V.

Then equation (

(x,y,z)=(y,x,z),~\forall x,y,z\in V.

That is, the associator is symmetric in the left two variables. It is well-known that the commutator of a left symmetric algebra defines a natural Lie algebra structure, i.e.,

\displaystyle[x,y]:=xy-yx,~~\forall x,y\in V.

For this reason, left symmetric algebras are also referred to as

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some basic results of
the moment map and the Jordan algebras, respectively. The ambient field is always assumed
to be the real number field
\mathbb{R}
unless otherwise stated.

2.1.
The moment map in algebras

Let
\mathbb{R}^{n}
be the usual
n
-dimensional real vector space. Denote by
V_{n}=\otimes^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})^{*}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
the space of all bilinear maps (all binary algebras), and

\mathfrak{M}_{n}=\{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\text{ is an inner product on }\mathbb{R}^{n}\}

the moduli space of all inner products on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, respectively. Consider the natural action of
\textnormal{GL}(n)=\textnormal{GL}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
on
V_{n}
as follows

\displaystyle g.\mu(X,Y)=g\mu(g^{-1}X,g^{-1}Y),~~\forall g\in\textnormal{GL}(n),\mu\in V_{n},X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2.1)

Then one immediately sees that the orbit
\textnormal{GL}(n).\mu
is precisely the isomorphism class of
\mu
. Differentiating (
2.1
), we obtain the natural representation
\pi
of
\mathfrak{gl}(n)
on
V_{n}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle(\pi(A)\mu)(X,Y)=A\mu(X,Y)-\mu(\pi(A)X,Y)-\mu(X,\pi(A)Y),~~\forall A\in\mathfrak{gl}(n).

(2.2)

It follows that
\pi(A)\mu=0
if and only if
A\in\operatorname{Der}(\mu)
, that is, the derivation algebra of
\mu
. On the other hand, one knows that the linear group
\textnormal{GL}(n)
also naturally acts on
\mathfrak{M}_{n}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle g.\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle=\langle g^{-1}(\cdot),g^{-1}(\cdot)\rangle,~~g\in\textnormal{GL}(n),

and this action is obviously transitive.

Using the notations above, it is not hard to verify that the map

\displaystyle g^{-1}:(g.\mu,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)\rightarrow(\mu,g^{-1}.\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle),~~\forall g\in\textnormal{GL}(n),\mu\in V_{n},

is an
isometry
between the two metric algebras, that is, preserving the brackets and the inner products simultaneously.
This implies the subtle idea:
varying brackets instead of metrics for the study of metric
algebras
, which was introduced by Lauret in
[
33
]
, and has profoundly influenced contemporary research in homogeneous Riemannian geometry (see
[
8
,
36
,
37
]
).

In the sequel, we fix an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as a background metric. This makes each
\mu\in V_{n}

become a metric algebra. The fixed inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
also induces an
\textnormal{O}(n)
-invariant inner product on

V_{n}
as follows

\displaystyle\langle\mu,\lambda\rangle=\sum_{i,j,k}\langle\mu(E_{i},E_{j}),X_{k}\rangle\langle\lambda(E_{i},E_{j}),E_{k}\rangle,\quad~\mu,\lambda\in V_{n},

(2.3)

where
\left\{E_{1},E_{2},\cdots,E_{n}\right\}
is an arbitrary orthonormal basis of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
. Moreover, there is a natural inner product on
\mathfrak{gl}(n)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle(A,B)=\operatorname{Tr}AB^{t},~~A,B\in\mathfrak{gl}(n)

(2.4)

which is
\textnormal{Ad}(\textnormal{O}(n))
-invariant, and the symbol
\cdot^{t}
denotes the transpose relative to
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
. Decompose
\mathfrak{gl}(n)=\mathfrak{so}(n)+\operatorname{sym}(n)
into the direct sum of skew matrices and symmetric matrices, respectively, where

\displaystyle\mathfrak{so}(n)=\{A\in\mathfrak{gl}(n):A^{t}=-A\},\quad\operatorname{sym}(n)=\{A\in\mathfrak{gl}(n):A^{t}=A\}.

Then the function
m:V_{n}\backslash\{0\}\rightarrow\operatorname{sym}(n)
defined by

\displaystyle(m(\mu),A)=\frac{\langle\pi(A)\mu,\mu\rangle}{\|\mu\|^{2}},~~~~0\neq\mu\in V_{n},A\in\operatorname{sym}(n),

(2.5)

is called the
moment map
for the representation
V_{n}
of
\mathfrak{gl}(n)
. Notice that in the complex case, the function
m
is precisely the moment map from symplectic geometry, corresponding to the Hamiltonian action of
\textnormal{U}(n)
on the symplectic manifold
\mathbb{P}V_{n}
(see
[
41
]
).

Now, for each
\mu\in V_{n}
, we associate it a map
\textnormal{M}_{\mu}\in\operatorname{sym}(n)
as follows

\displaystyle\textnormal{M}_{\mu}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}L_{E_{i}}^{\mu}(L_{E_{i}}^{\mu})^{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}(L_{E_{i}}^{\mu})^{t}L_{E_{i}}^{\mu}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}(R_{E_{i}}^{\mu})^{t}R_{X_{i}}^{\mu},

(2.6)

where
\left\{E_{1},E_{2},\cdots,E_{n}\right\}
is an arbitrary orthonormal basis of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, and for any
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the operators
L_{X}^{\mu}
,
R_{X}^{\mu}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
are given by
L_{X}^{\mu}(Y)=\mu(X,Y)
and
R_{X}^{\mu}(Y)=\mu(Y,X),\forall Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, respectively.

Lemma 2.1

(
[
37
,
43
]
)
.

Let the notations be as above. Then
m(\mu)=\frac{\textnormal{M}_{\mu}}{\|\mu\|^{2}}
for any
0\neq\mu\in V_{n}
, and moreover,

\displaystyle\langle\textnormal{M}_{\mu}X,Y\rangle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\langle\mu(E_{i},E_{j}),X\rangle\langle\mu(E_{i},E_{j}),Y\rangle-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\langle\mu(E_{i},X),E_{j}\rangle\langle\mu(E_{i},Y),E_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle-\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\langle\mu(X,E_{i}),E_{j}\rangle\langle\mu(Y,E_{i}),E_{j}\rangle,~~\forall X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

(2.7)

where
\left\{E_{1},E_{2},\cdots,E_{n}\right\}
is an arbitrary orthonormal basis of
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
.

Remark 2.2
.

By Lemma
2.1
, we know that if
(\mu,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
is a metric Lie algebra, then the map
\textnormal{M}_{\mu}
in (
2.7
) coincides up to a scalar with the map
M
in (
A.3
) for
(\mu,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
. As we shall see later, this also holds for metric Jordan algebras (see Remark
3.19
).

2.2.
Jordan algebras

An algebra
\mathscr{A}
is said to be a Jordan algebra if, for all
X,Y
in
\mathscr{A}
:

\displaystyle XY

\displaystyle=YX,

(2.8)

\displaystyle X(X^{2}Y)

\displaystyle=X^{2}(XY).

(2.9)

Using the notation
L_{X}(Y)=XY
for all
X,Y\in\mathscr{A}
, and
[S,T]=ST-TS
for any two endomorphisms of
\mathscr{A}
, the property (
2.9
) can be written
[L_{X},L_{X^{2}}]=0
for all
X\in\mathscr{A}
.

Remark 2.3
.

If
(V,\diamond)
is an associative algebra, then one can naturally define on
V
a Lie algebra structure and a Jordan algebra structure, respectively, as follows

\displaystyle[X,Y]:=X\diamond Y-Y\diamond X,~~\forall X,Y\in V,\quad XY:=\frac{1}{2}(X\diamond Y+Y\diamond X),~~\forall X,Y\in V.

In general a Jordan algebra is not associative.

A Jordan algebra
\mathscr{A}
is called
simple
if
\mathscr{A}^{2}\neq 0
and
\mathscr{A}
has no nontrivial ideals, and it is called
semisimple
if it is a direct product of simple Jordan algebras.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a Jordan algebra. The Jordan algebra
\mathscr{A}
is called
solvable
if
\mathscr{A}^{(m)}=0
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathscr{A}^{(0)}=\mathscr{A},\mathscr{A}^{(k+1)}=\mathscr{A}^{(k)}\mathscr{A}^{(k)},k\geq 0
. The Jordan algebra
\mathscr{A}
is called
nilpotent
if
\mathscr{A}^{m}=0
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathscr{A}^{0}=\mathscr{A},\mathscr{A}^{k+1}=\mathscr{A}\mathscr{A}^{k},k\geq 0
.

Unlike in the context of Lie algebras, the concepts of solvability and nilpotency in Jordan algebras turn out to be equivalent (see
[
1
]
).

Lemma 2.5

(
[
20
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a Jordan algebra, then the following symmetric bilinear form

\displaystyle\tau(X,Y):=\operatorname{Tr}L_{XY},\forall X,Y\in\mathscr{A},

(2.10)

is associative, that is,
\tau(XY,Z)=\tau(X,YZ)
for all
X,Y,Z\in\mathscr{A}
.

For a Jordan algebra
\mathscr{A}
, we denote by
\mathscr{N}
the
radical
, i.e., the maximal nilpotent ideal of
\mathscr{A}
. It is proved by A. Albert that the radical
\mathscr{N}
of
\mathscr{A}
coincides with the kernel of the symmetric bilinear form
\tau
in (
2.10
).

Moreover, we have the following theorem

Theorem 2.6

(Wedderburn Principal Theorem)
.

Any real Jordan algebra
\mathscr{A}
can be written as a vector space direct sum
\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{S}+\mathscr{N}
, where
\mathscr{N}
is the radical of
\mathscr{A}
, and
\mathscr{S}
is a maximal semisimple subalgebra of
\mathscr{A}
isomorphic to
\mathscr{A}/\mathscr{N}
.

It follows Theorem
2.6
that
\mathscr{A}
is semisimple if and only if the bilinear form
\tau
is non-degenerate. By this result and Lemma
2.5
, one can easily prove that every semisimple Jordan algebra necessarily carries an identity element.

Now, let
\mathscr{A}
be a Jordan algebra with an identity element
E
, and
\mathbb{R}[Y]
denote the polynomials in the variable
Y\in\mathscr{A}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{R}
. Notice that
\mathbb{R}[Y]
is the subalgebra of
\mathscr{A}
generated by
Y
and the identity element, the number
\operatorname{dim}\mathbb{R}[Y]
is necessarily bounded. We define the
rank
of
\mathscr{A}
as follows

\displaystyle r=\max\{\operatorname{dim}\mathbb{R}[Y]:Y\in\mathscr{A}\}.

(2.11)

An element of
X\in\mathscr{A}
is called
regular
, if
\operatorname{dim}\mathbb{R}[X]
is maximal in
\{\operatorname{dim}\mathbb{R}[Y]:Y\in\mathscr{A}\}
. The
reduced trace
of a regular element
X\in\mathscr{A}
is defined by

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(X):=\operatorname{Tr}{L_{X}|_{\mathbb{R}[X]}}.

(2.12)

Since the set of regular elements is dense in
\mathscr{A},
the function (
2.12
) can be uniquely extended to a linear map
\operatorname{tr}:\mathscr{A}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
and in particular, we have
\operatorname{tr}(E)=r
(see
[
20
]
).

Lemma 2.7

(
[
20
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a Jordan algebra with an identity element. Then the symmetric bilinear form
\operatorname{tr}(XY)
is associative, that is,

\operatorname{tr}((XY)Z)=\operatorname{tr}(X(YZ))
for all
X,Y,Z\in\mathscr{A}
.

For a Jordan algebra
\mathscr{A}
, the Killing form on
\mathscr{A}
is defined as follows

\displaystyle B(X,Y):=\operatorname{Tr}L_{X}L_{Y},

(2.13)

for all
X,Y\in\mathscr{A}
. We note that the Killing form of a Jordan algebra is symmetric, but in general not associative.

3.
Connections and curvatures on Jordan algebras

In this section, we first introduce the concepts of connection and curvature on Jordan algebras, then obtain the corresponding formulas. Moreover, we get a Jordan version of Theorem
1.1
.

3.1.
Jordan-Levi-Civita Connection

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a Jordan algebra and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
be an inner product. For any bilinear map

\displaystyle\nabla:\mathscr{A}\times\mathscr{A}\rightarrow\mathscr{A},

write
\nabla_{X}Y:=\nabla(X,Y)
for any
X,Y\in\mathscr{A}
. We call such
\nabla
a
connection
on
\mathscr{A}
. If the connection
\nabla
satisfies

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}Y

\displaystyle+\nabla_{Y}X=XY,~~\forall X,Y\in\mathscr{A},

(3.1)

then we call
\nabla
a
torsion-free connection
on
\mathscr{A}
. If
\nabla
is torsion-free and satisfies

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{X}Y,Z

\displaystyle\rangle-\langle Y,\nabla_{X}Z\rangle=0,~~\forall X,Y\in\mathscr{A},

(3.2)

then we call
\nabla
a
Jordan-Levi-Civita connection
on
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
.

Remark 3.2
.

Invariant super-connections on Lie super-groups have two components: the even one and the odd one (
[
23
]
). The common connection on metric Lie algebras corresponds to the even one (see Appendix
A
), and our Definition
3.1
is formally adapted to the odd one of an invariant super-connection. Indeed, suppose that
G
be a Lie super-group with the Lie super-algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}={\mathfrak{g}}_{0}+{\mathfrak{g}}_{1}
consisting
of left-invariant vector fields, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
be a left-invariant super Riemannian metric on
G
. Then we may identify
(G,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
with
({\mathfrak{g}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
.
Let
\nabla
be the Levi-Civita connection of
({\mathfrak{g}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, and
X,Y,Z\in{\mathfrak{g}}
be homogeneous elements with
|X|\in\{0,1\}
denoting the parity of
X.
Then the torsion-free property and the
metric-preserving property of
\nabla
are respectively given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}Y-(-1)^{|X||Y|}\nabla_{Y}X-[X,Y]=0,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{X}Y,Z\rangle+(-1)^{|X||Y|}\langle Y,\nabla_{X}Z\rangle=0.

(3.4)

Similar to the standard theory in Riemannian geometry, the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla
is uniquely determined by
(
3.3
) and (
3.4
), i.e.,

\displaystyle 2\langle\nabla_{X}Y,

\displaystyle Z\rangle=\langle[X,Y],Z\rangle)-(-1)^{|X|(|Y|+|Z|)}\langle[Y,Z],X\rangle)+(-1)^{|Z|(|X|+|Y|)}\langle[Z,X],Y\rangle.

(3.5)

Moreover, associated with the Levi-Civita connection , the curvature
({\mathfrak{g}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
is given by

\displaystyle R(X,Y)Z=-\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}Z+(-1)^{|X||Y|}\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}Z+\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z.

(3.6)

We refer to
[
23
]
for more details about invariant structures on Lie super-groups,.

Remark 3.3
.

Clearly, for a metric Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, the property (
A.2
) is equivalent to
\nabla_{X}
being skew-symmetric for all
X\in\mathfrak{g}
, while for a metric Jordan algebra
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, the equation (
3.2
) is equivalent to
\nabla_{X}
being symmetric for all
X\in\mathscr{A}
.

The following theorem might be regarded as a special case of (
3.5
) within the context of invariant metrics on Lie super-groups.

Theorem 3.4
.

Every metric Jordan algebra
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
admits a unique Jordan-Levi-Civita connection
\nabla
, which is given by

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{X}Y,Z\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(\langle XY,Z\rangle-\langle YZ,X\rangle+\langle ZX,Y\rangle),

for all
X,Y,Z\in\mathscr{A}
.

Proof.

Assume that
\nabla
a Jordan-Levi-Civita connection on
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, then

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{X}Y,Z\rangle-\langle Y,\nabla_{X}Z\rangle=0,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{Y}Z,X\rangle-\langle Z,\nabla_{Y}X\rangle=0,

(3.8)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{Z}X,Y\rangle-\langle X,\nabla_{Z}Y\rangle=0,

(3.9)

for any
X,Y,Z\in\mathscr{A}
. Adding (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) and subtracting (
3.9
), we have

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{X}Y-\nabla_{Y}X,Z\rangle-\langle\nabla_{Z}X+\nabla_{X}Z,Y\rangle+\langle\nabla_{Y}Z+\nabla_{Z}Y,X\rangle=0.

Using the property (
3.1
), we have

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{X}Y,Z\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(\langle XY,Z\rangle-\langle YZ,X\rangle+\langle ZX,Y\rangle).

(3.10)

So the Jordan-Levi-Civita connection
\nabla
is uniquely determined by (
3.1
) and (
3.2
).

To prove the existence, we define
\nabla
by (
3.10
). It is easy to verify that
\nabla
is well-defined and that it satisfies the desired conditions (
3.1
) and (
3.2
).
∎

3.2.
The Jordan Curvature Tensor

Definition 3.5
.

Let
(\mathscr{A},\nabla)
be a Jordan algebra with a connection. The
curvature tensor
of
(\mathscr{A},\nabla)
is defined by

\displaystyle R(X,Y)Z=-\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}Z-\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}Z+\nabla_{XY}Z,~~X,Y,Z\in\mathscr{A},

or equivalently,
R(X,Y)=\nabla_{XY}-(\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}+\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X})
.
If moreover,
\nabla
comes from the Jordan-Levi-Civita connection of a metric Jordan algebra
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, then we call
R
the
Jordan curvature tensor
.

The Definition
3.5
is formally adapted to the curvature restricted to the odd component of super-connections (
3.6
).

Remark 3.6
.

It is easily seen that
R
is symmetric in the first two positions. Note that if
R=0
, also referred to as
flat
, then
\nabla
naturally induces a Jordan algebra representation of
\mathscr{A}
.

Remark 3.7
.

Consider the two-dimensional Jordan algebra

\mathscr{A}:e_{1}e_{1}=e_{1},e_{1}e_{2}=e_{2}.

Endow
\mathscr{A}
with the metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
so that
\{e_{1},e_{2}\}
is an orthonormal basis. Then by a straightforward calculation, we know that the Jordan-Levi-Civita connection satisfies
\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{1}=e_{1},\nabla_{e_{1}}e_{2}=e_{2}
and
\nabla_{e_{2}}e_{1}=0=\nabla_{e_{2}}e_{2}
. It follows that the only non-trivial term
R(e_{i},e_{j})e_{k}
is
R(e_{1},e_{1})e_{2}=-e_{2}
. So
R(e_{1},e_{1})e_{2}+R(e_{1},e_{2})e_{1}+R(e_{2},e_{1})e_{1}=-e_{2}
. This shows that the sum
R(X,Y)Z+

R(Y,Z)X+R(Z,X)Y
in general does not vanish.

Now, we explore Theorem
1.1
in the context of Jordan algebra. Suppose that
(\mathscr{A},\nabla)
is a Jordan algebra with a flat, torsion-free connection, that is,

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}Z+\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}Z-\nabla_{XY}Z=0,\quad\nabla_{X}Y+\nabla_{Y}X=XY

for all
X,Y,Z\in\mathscr{A}
. Define on
\mathscr{A}
a new binary operation

\displaystyle X\diamond Y:=\nabla_{X}Y,~~\forall X,Y\in\mathscr{A},

then it is easy to verify that
\diamond
satisfies

\displaystyle(X\diamond Y)\diamond Z-X\diamond(Y\diamond Z)=-(Y\diamond X)\diamond Z+Y\diamond(X\diamond Z),~~\forall X,Y,Z\in\mathscr{A}.

Note that this differs from the equation (
1.1
) by a sign. In an analogy of left symmetric algebra (
1.1
), we introduce the following definition

Definition 3.8
.

Let
(V,\diamond)
is an
arbitrary
binary algebra. If
(V,\diamond)
satisfies

\displaystyle(X\diamond Y)\diamond Z-X\diamond(Y\diamond Z)=-(Y\diamond X)\diamond Z+Y\diamond(X\diamond Z),~~\forall X,Y,Z\in V,

(3.11)

then we call
(V,\diamond)
is a
left skew-symmetric algebra
(write LSSA for short).

Clearly, an algebra is both a LSA (i.e., satisfying the equation (
1.1
)) and a LSSA if and only if it is an associative algebra.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
(V,\diamond)
is a left skew-symmetric algebra. Then the new binary operation

\displaystyle X\bullet Y:=X\diamond Y+Y\diamond X,~~\forall X,Y\in V

(3.12)

defines on
V
a Jordan structure.

Proof.

Note that for any
X,Y\in V

J

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}X\bullet((X\bullet X)\bullet Y)-\frac{1}{2}(X\bullet X)\bullet(X\bullet Y)

\displaystyle=X\bullet((X\diamond X)\bullet Y)-(X\diamond X)\bullet(X\bullet Y)

\displaystyle=X\bullet((X\diamond X)\diamond Y+Y\diamond(X\diamond X))-(X\diamond X)\bullet(X\diamond Y+Y\diamond X)

\displaystyle=X\diamond((X\diamond X)\diamond Y+Y\diamond(X\diamond X))+((X\diamond X)\diamond Y+Y\diamond(X\diamond X))\diamond X

\displaystyle\quad-(X\diamond X)\diamond(X\diamond Y+Y\diamond X)-(X\diamond Y+Y\diamond X)\diamond(X\diamond X)

Put

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=X\diamond((X\diamond X)\diamond Y),\quad b=X\diamond(Y\diamond(X\diamond X)),\quad c=((X\diamond X)\diamond Y)\diamond X,\quad d=(Y\diamond(X\diamond X))\diamond X,

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle=(X\diamond X)\diamond(X\diamond Y),\quad f=(X\diamond X)\diamond(Y\diamond X),\quad g=(X\diamond Y)\diamond(X\diamond X),\quad h=(Y\diamond X)\diamond(X\diamond X),

then
\textbf{J}=a+b+c+d-e-f-g-h.
Using (
3.11
), we have

\displaystyle b-g

\displaystyle=-Y\diamond(X\diamond(X\diamond X))+(Y\diamond X)\diamond(X\diamond X)=-Y\diamond(X\diamond(X\diamond X))+h,

\displaystyle c-f

\displaystyle=-(Y\diamond(X\diamond X))\diamond X+Y\diamond((X\diamond X)\diamond X)=Y\diamond((X\diamond X)\diamond X)-d.

Since
((X\diamond X)\diamond Z)-X\diamond(X\diamond Z)=-((X\diamond X)\diamond Z)+X\diamond(X\diamond Z)=0
, then
(X\diamond X)\diamond Z=X\diamond(X\diamond Z)
for any
X,Z\in V
. In particular,
X\diamond(X\diamond X)=(X\diamond X)\diamond X
, and

\displaystyle a-e

\displaystyle=X\diamond((X\diamond X)\diamond Y)-(X\diamond X)\diamond(X\diamond Y)=X\diamond(X\diamond(X\diamond Y))-(X\diamond X)\diamond(X\diamond Y)

\displaystyle=(X\diamond X)\diamond(X\diamond Y)-(X\diamond X)\diamond(X\diamond Y)=0.

Consequently,
\textbf{J}=a+b+c+d-e-f-g-h=0,
that is, (
3.12
) defines a Jordan structure.
∎

Corollary 3.10
.

For a Jordan algebra
\mathscr{A},
there is a one-to-one correspondence between flat torsion-free connections on
\mathscr{A}
and LSSA-structures on
\mathscr{A}
.

In the sequel, we always assume that
\nabla
,
R
are the Jordan-Levi-Civita connection and
the Jordan curvature tensor of the metric Jordan algerba
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, respectively. We also write
R(X,Y,Z,W)=\langle R(X,Y)Z,W\rangle
for any
X,Y,Z,W\in\mathscr{A}

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
be a metric Jordan algebra. Then

(a)

R(X,Y,Z,W)=R(Y,X,Z,W);

(b)

R(X,Y,Z,W)=R(X,Y,W,Z).

Proof.

Obviously, (a) holds. For (b), it follows from that

∎

We note that in general

Definition 3.12
.

The Jordan curvature of

is defined as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(X,Y)=\frac{\langle R(X,Y)X,Y\rangle}{\langle X,X\rangle\langle Y,Y\rangle-\langle X,Y\rangle^{2}}

for any linearly independent

Example 3.13
.

Consider the Jordan algebras

3.3.
The Jordan Ricci Curvature tensor

For a metric Jordan algebra
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, one may define the Jordan Ricci curvature tensor as follows

\displaystyle\operatorname{ric}(U,V)=\operatorname{Tr}{(X\mapsto R(U,X)V)},~~\forall U,V\in\mathscr{A}.

(3.13)

We point out that the tensor
\operatorname{ric}
is in general not symmetric. It is thus natural to introduce the following (symmetric) Jordan Ricci curvature tensor

Definition 3.14
.

The Jordan Ricci curvature tensor of
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
is defined as follows

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}(U,V)=\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{ric}(U,V)+\operatorname{ric}(V,U)),\forall U,V\in\mathscr{A}.

(3.14)

If moreover,
\operatorname{Ric}
is a constant multiple of
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, then we call
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
a Jordan-Einstein metric on
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
.

As we shall see later, ric and Ric are closely related to each other by the following so called
mean curvature vector
.

Definition 3.15
.

For a metric Jordan algebra
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, we define
H\in\mathscr{A}
by

\displaystyle H=\sum_{i=1}^{\dim\mathscr{A}}E_{i}^{2},

where
\{E_{i}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, and we call
H
the
mean curvature vector
of
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
.

It is easily seen that the mean curvature vector is independent of the choice of orthonormal bases.

Following
[
17
]
, for any
U\in\mathscr{A}
, we define two linear maps
\nabla_{U}
and
\nabla U
on
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, respectively, as follows

\displaystyle\nabla_{U}(X):=\nabla_{U}X,\quad\nabla U(X):=\nabla_{X}U,

for any
X\in\mathscr{A}
.

Lemma 3.16
.

The Jordan Ricci tensor of
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
satisfies

\displaystyle\textnormal{Ric}(U,U)=\operatorname{Tr}{(\nabla U)^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}{\nabla U^{2}},

for any
U\in\mathscr{A}
.

Proof.

Assume that
\{E_{i}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, then we have

\displaystyle\textnormal{Ric}(U,U)=\operatorname{Tr}{(X\mapsto R(U,X)U)}=\sum_{i}\langle R(U,E_{i})U,E_{i}\rangle=\sum_{i}\langle R(E_{i},U)U,E_{i}\rangle.

It follows that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}(U,U)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\langle-\nabla_{E_{i}}\nabla_{U}U-\nabla_{U}\nabla_{E_{i}}U+\nabla_{{E_{i}}U}U,E_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\langle-\nabla_{U}\nabla_{E_{i}}U+\nabla_{{E_{i}}U}U,E_{i}\rangle-\sum_{i}\langle\nabla_{E_{i}}\nabla_{U}U,E_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=:\textnormal{I}-\textnormal{II}.

Note that
E_{i}U=\nabla_{E_{i}}U+\nabla_{U}E_{i}
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{I}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\langle-\nabla_{U}\nabla_{E_{i}}U,E_{i}\rangle+\sum_{i}\langle\nabla_{\nabla_{U}E_{i}}U,E_{i}\rangle+\sum_{i}\langle\nabla_{\nabla_{E_{i}}U}U,E_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\operatorname{Tr}\nabla_{U}\circ\nabla U+\operatorname{Tr}\nabla U\circ\nabla_{U}+\operatorname{Tr}\nabla U\circ\nabla U

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}{(\nabla U)^{2}}.

Since
\nabla_{U}U+\nabla_{U}U=U^{2}
, then

\textnormal{II}=\sum_{i}(\nabla_{E_{i}}\nabla_{U}U,E_{i}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}(\nabla_{E_{i}}\nabla U^{2},E_{i}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}\nabla U^{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.17
.

It follows from a similar calculation that

\displaystyle\operatorname{ric}(U,V)=\operatorname{Tr}\nabla U\circ\nabla V-\operatorname{Tr}\nabla\nabla_{U}V,

for any
U,V\in\mathscr{A}
.

Theorem 3.18
.

Let
\left\{E_{i}\right\}
be an orthonormal basis of
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle).
Then

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}(X,Y)=-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\langle XE_{i},E_{j}\rangle\langle YE_{i},E_{j}\rangle+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j}\langle E_{i}E_{j},X\rangle\langle E_{i}E_{j},Y\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}B(X,Y)-\frac{1}{4}\langle H,XY\rangle,~~\forall X,Y\in\mathscr{A},

where
B
is the Killing form of
\mathscr{A},H
is the mean curvature vector of
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
.

Proof.

Let
\{E_{i}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
. Since by Theorem
3.4

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{E_{i}}U,E_{j}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(\langle UE_{i},E_{j}\rangle-\langle UE_{j},E_{i}\rangle+\langle E_{i}E_{j},U\rangle),

(3.15)

for all
i,j
, and
U\in\mathscr{A}
, then

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}{(\nabla U)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\langle\nabla_{\nabla_{E_{j}}U}U,E_{j}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\langle\nabla_{E_{i}}U,E_{j}\rangle\langle\nabla_{E_{j}}U,E_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j}\langle E_{i}E_{j},U\rangle^{2}-\underbrace{\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j}(\langle UE_{i},E_{j}\rangle-\langle UE_{j},E_{i}\rangle)^{2}}_{\textnormal{I}}.

It follows that

I

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j}\langle UE_{i},E_{j}\rangle^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\langle UE_{i},E_{j}\rangle\langle UE_{j},E_{i}\rangle+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j}\langle UE_{j},E_{i}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\langle UE_{i},E_{j}\rangle^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\langle U(UE_{i}),E_{i}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\langle UE_{i},E_{j}\rangle^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}L_{U}L_{U}.

So

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}{(\nabla U)^{2}}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j}\langle E_{i}E_{j},U\rangle^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\langle UE_{i},E_{j}\rangle^{2}+\frac{1}{2}B(U,U).

Note that

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}{\nabla U^{2}}=\sum_{i}\langle\nabla_{E_{i}}U^{2},E_{i}\rangle=\sum_{i}\langle U^{2},\nabla_{E_{i}}E_{i}\rangle=\sum_{i}\langle U^{2},\frac{1}{2}E_{i}^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\langle U^{2},H\rangle.

Then by Lemma
3.16
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}(U,U)=\operatorname{Tr}{(\nabla U)^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}{\nabla U^{2}}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\langle UE_{i},E_{j}\rangle^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j}\langle E_{i}E_{j},U\rangle^{2}+\frac{1}{2}B(U,U)-\frac{1}{4}\langle H,U^{2}\rangle.

This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.19
.

By Theorem
3.18
, the Ricci operator of
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
can be written as follows

\displaystyle\textnormal{Ric}=\textnormal{M}+\frac{1}{2}B-\frac{1}{4}S_{H},

(3.16)

where M coincides up to a scalar
\frac{1}{4}
with the moment map for the variety of Jordan algebras (see Lemma
2.1
),
B
is the symmetric operator defined by the Killing form of
\mathscr{A}
, and
S_{H}
is the symmetric operator defined by
\langle S_{H}X,Y\rangle=\langle H,XY\rangle,~~\forall X,Y\in\mathscr{A}.
Note that if
\mathscr{A}
is nilpotent, and
H\in(\mathscr{AA})^{\perp}
(for example
H=0
), then the Ricci operator coincides with M.

Remark 3.20
.

Similarly, by Remark
3.17
, one obtains the following result

\displaystyle\operatorname{ric}(X,Y)=-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\langle XE_{i},E_{j}\rangle\langle YE_{i},E_{j}\rangle+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j}\langle E_{i}E_{j},X\rangle\langle E_{i}E_{j},Y\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}B(X,Y)-\frac{1}{2}\langle H,\nabla_{X}Y\rangle,~~\forall X,Y\in\mathscr{A}.

Moreover,

if and only if the operator

is self-adjoint. In particular,

if

Remark 3.21
.

Suppose that

is of constant Jordan curvature, then

\displaystyle\langle R(X,Y)X,Y\rangle=c(\langle X,X\rangle\langle Y,Y\rangle-\langle X,Y\rangle^{2}),

for all

\displaystyle\operatorname{Ric}(U,U)=\sum_{i=1}^{\dim\mathscr{A}}\langle R(U,E_{i})U,E_{i}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{\dim\mathscr{A}}c(\langle U,U\rangle\langle E_{i},E_{i}\rangle-\langle U,E_{i}\rangle^{2})=c(\operatorname{dim}\mathscr{A}-1)\langle U,U\rangle,\forall U\in\mathscr{A}.

That is,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is a Jordan-Einstein metric. The converse is not true, see Remark
3.7
.

3.4.
Jordan scalar curvature

By Definition
3.14
, we introduce the Jordan scalar curvature for metric Jordan algebras, which is a trace of
Ric
, i.e.,

For a metric Jordan algebra

4.
The Jordan curvature of metric Jordan algebras

In this section, we study the Jordan curvature of formally real Jordan algebras, which are counterparts of compact real forms in complex semisimple Lie algebras (see Appendix
B
for a comparison). By Proposition
B.2
, a Jordan algebra
\mathscr{A}
with an identity element is formally real, if and only if
\mathscr{A}
admits an associative inner product.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
be a metric Jordan algebra. Assume that
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, is associative, then the Jordan-Levi-Civita connection is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}Y=\frac{1}{2}XY,~\forall X,Y\in\mathscr{A}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle R(X,Y)Z+R(Y,Z)X+R(Z,X)Y=0,

for all
X,Y,Z\in\mathscr{A}
.

Proof.

Since
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is associative, then

\displaystyle\langle XY,Z\rangle=\langle X,YZ\rangle,~~\forall X,Y,Z\in\mathscr{A}.

By Theorem
3.4
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{X}Y=\frac{1}{2}XY,~~\forall X,Y\in\mathscr{A}.

(4.1)

This proves the first statements. Moreover, it follows from (
4.1
) that

\displaystyle R(X,Y)Z

\displaystyle=\nabla_{XY}Z-\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}Z-\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}Z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(XY)Z-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{X}YZ-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{Y}XZ

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(XY)Z-\frac{1}{4}X(YZ)-\frac{1}{4}Y(XZ)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(XY)Z-\frac{1}{4}(YZ)X-\frac{1}{4}(ZX)Y.

Similarly,

\displaystyle R(Y,Z)X

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(YZ)X-\frac{1}{4}(ZX)Y-\frac{1}{4}(XY)Z,

\displaystyle R(Z,X)Y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(ZX)Y-\frac{1}{4}(XY)Z-\frac{1}{4}(YZ)X.

So

\displaystyle R(X,Y)Z+R(Y,Z)X+R(Z,X)Y=0,

for all
X,Y,Z\in\mathscr{A}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a formally real Jordan algebra, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
be an associative inner product on it. Then we have the following inequality

\displaystyle\langle XY,XY\rangle\leq\langle X^{2},Y^{2}\rangle,~~\forall X,Y\in\mathscr{A}.

(4.2)

Proof.

By Theorem
B.3
and Proposition
B.4
, it suffices to prove the lemma in the case that
\mathscr{A}
is simple. Now, let
\mathscr{A}
be a simple formally real Jordan algebra, and
r
be the rank of
\mathscr{A}
. By the associativity of the inner product and the commutativity of
\mathscr{A}

\displaystyle\langle XY,XY\rangle=\langle X(XY),Y\rangle=\langle L_{X}^{2}(Y),Y\rangle,

and

\displaystyle\langle X^{2},Y^{2}\rangle=\langle X^{2}Y,Y\rangle=\langle L_{X^{2}}(Y),Y\rangle.

Fix
X
, then both sides are quadratic forms in
Y
. Consider the spectral decomposition of
X
:

\displaystyle X=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}H_{i},

where
\{H_{1},\cdots,H_{r}\}
is a Jordan frame and
\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
. According to the Peirce decomposition of
\mathscr{A}
with respect to
\left\{H_{1},\cdots,H_{r}\right\}
(see Theorem
B.6
), we have the following orthogonal direct sum

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{R}H_{i}\bigoplus_{i<j}\mathscr{A}_{ij}.

Let

\displaystyle Y=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\mu_{i}H_{i}+\sum_{i<j}Y_{ij},

where
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
Y_{ij}\in\mathscr{A}_{ij}
. Then

\displaystyle L_{X}(Y)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\mu_{i}H_{i}+\sum_{i<j}\frac{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}{2}Y_{ij}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\langle L_{X}^{2}(Y),Y\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}^{2}\mu_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i<j}(\frac{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}{2})^{2}\|Y_{ij}\|^{2},

\displaystyle\langle L_{X^{2}}(Y),Y\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}^{2}\mu_{i}^{2}+\sum_{i<j}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}+\lambda_{j}^{2}}{2}\|Y_{ij}\|^{2}.

The inequality follows since

\displaystyle(\frac{\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}}{2})^{2}\leq\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}+\lambda_{j}^{2}}{2}.

This completes the proof.
∎

Example 4.3
.

Consider the formally real Jordan algebra
\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{R})
in Remark
2
, i.e., the space of
n\times n
real symmetric matrices with the usual Jordan multiplication
A\circ B:=\frac{1}{2}(AB+BA),\forall A,B\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{R}).
It is easily seen that

\langle A,B\rangle:=\operatorname{Tr}{AB^{t}}=\operatorname{Tr}{AB},~\forall A,B\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{R}),

is an associative inner product on
\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{R})
. In this case, Lemma
4.2
reads as follows

\operatorname{Tr}{(A\circ B)\circ(A\circ B)}\leq\operatorname{Tr}{(A\circ A)\circ(B\circ B)},~\forall A,B\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{R}),

which is also equivalent to the following known inequality

\operatorname{Tr}{ABAB}\leq\operatorname{Tr}{AABB},

for all
A,B\in\operatorname{Sym}(n,\mathbb{R}).

Remark 4.4
.

By the proof of Lemma
4.2
, we know that if
\langle XY,XY\rangle=\langle X^{2},Y^{2}\rangle
for all
X,Y\in\mathscr{A}
, then the formally real Jordan algebra
\mathscr{A}
is necessarily a direct sum of the one-dimensional simple Jordan algebra (i.e.,
e_{1}e_{1}=e_{1}
,
r=1
).

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a formally real Jordan algebra, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
be an associative inner product on it. Then the Jordan curvature of
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
is non-positive. Moreover, the Jordan curvature vanishes identically if and only
\mathscr{A}
is a direct sum of the one-dimensional simple Jordan algebra.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.1
, we know that

\displaystyle R(X,Y)X=\frac{1}{2}(XY)X-\frac{1}{4}(YX)X-\frac{1}{4}(X^{2})Y=\frac{1}{4}(XY)X-\frac{1}{4}(X^{2})Y,

for any
X,Y\in\mathscr{A}
. Since the inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is associative, we have

\displaystyle\langle R(X,Y)X,Y\rangle=\frac{1}{4}\langle(XY)X-(X^{2})Y,Y\rangle=\frac{1}{4}\langle XY,XY\rangle-\frac{1}{4}\langle X^{2},Y^{2}\rangle.

The theorem is completed by Lemma
4.2
and Remark
4.4
.
∎

5.
The Jordan Ricci curvature of metric Jordan algebras

In this section, we explore Jordan-Einstein metrics on Jordan algebras. We show that every simple formally
real Jordan algebra (of dimension at least two) admits a Jordan-Einstein metric of negative Jordan scalar curvature. For nontrivial nilpotent Jordan algebras, we prove that they admit no Jordan-Einstein metrics. Moreover, we construct some examples of Jordan-Einstein metrics.

5.1.
Jordan-Einstein metrics on formally real Jordan algebras

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a simple formally real Jordan algebra of rank
r
and
E
be the identity element. Then with respect to a Jordan frame
\{H_{1},H_{2},\cdots,H_{r}\},

\mathscr{A}
has the following Peirce decomposition (see Theorem
B.6
)

\displaystyle\mathscr{A}=\bigoplus_{i\leq j}\mathscr{A}_{ij}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{R}H_{i}\bigoplus_{i<j}\mathscr{A}_{ij}.

For
r\geq 3
, it is known that the number
d=\operatorname{dim}\mathscr{A}_{ij}
does not depend on
i,j
. So

\displaystyle n=\dim\mathscr{A}=\dim\bigoplus_{i\leq j}\mathscr{A}_{ij}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\dim\mathscr{A}_{ii}+\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq r}\dim\mathscr{A}_{ij}=r+\frac{r(r-1)}{2}d.

(5.1)

For
r=2
, we set
d=n-2
. It follows from the trace formula in
[
20
]
that

\displaystyle B(X,Y)=\operatorname{Tr}L_{X}L_{Y}=\alpha\operatorname{tr}(XY)+\frac{d}{4}\operatorname{tr}(X)\cdot\operatorname{tr}(Y),~~\forall X,Y\in\mathscr{A},

(5.2)

where
\alpha=1+\frac{(r-2)}{4}d
.

Now, we state the main result of this section.

Theorem 5.1
.

Every simple formally real Jordan algebra admits a Jordan-Einstein metric.

In order to prove Theorem
5.1
, we need some preparation work. For a simple formally real Jordan algebra
\mathscr{A}
, by Lemma
2.7
and Proposition
B.2
, we know that
\operatorname{tr}(XY)
a positive definite symmetric bilinear form which is associative. Using (
B.1
), we have the following result.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{0})
be a simple formally real algebra of fixed dimension

and rank
r
, where

\displaystyle\langle X,Y\rangle_{0}:=\operatorname{tr}(XY)=\frac{r}{n}\tau(X,Y)=\frac{r}{n}\operatorname{Tr}L_{XY},~~\forall X,Y\in\mathscr{A}.

Then the mean curvature vector of
(\mathscr{A},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{0})

\displaystyle H_{0}=\frac{n}{r}E=\left(1+\frac{(r-1)}{2}d\right)E.

Moreover, the Jordan Ricci tensor

\displaystyle\textnormal{Ric}_{0}(X,Y)=-\frac{rd}{16}\langle X,Y\rangle_{0}+\frac{d}{16}\textnormal{tr}(X)\cdot\textnormal{tr}(Y),

for all

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle H_{0}=\sum_{i}E_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle\langle X,H_{0}\rangle_{0}=\sum_{i}\langle X,E_{i}r\rangle_{0}=\sum_{i}\langle XE_{i},E_{i}\rangle_{0}=\operatorname{Tr}L_{X}=\frac{n}{r}\langle X,E\rangle_{0},~~\forall X\in\mathscr{A}.

In particular,

For the second statement. By Remark

\displaystyle\textnormal{Ric}_{0}(X,Y)=\langle\textnormal{M}_{0}X,Y\rangle_{0}+\frac{1}{2}B(X,Y)-\frac{1}{4}\langle H_{0},XY\rangle_{0},~~\forall X,Y\in\mathscr{A},

where

\displaystyle\langle\textnormal{M}_{0}X,Y\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\langle XE_{i},E_{j}\rangle_{0}\langle YE_{i},E_{j}\rangle_{0}+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j}\langle E_{i}E_{j},X\rangle_{0}\langle E_{i}E_{j},Y\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle\langle H_{0},XY\rangle_{0}=\frac{n}{r}\langle E,XY\rangle_{0}=\frac{n}{r}\langle X,Y\rangle_{0}=\left(1+\frac{(r-1)}{2}d\right)\langle X,Y\rangle_{0}.

\displaystyle\textnormal{Ric}_{0}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{4}B(X,Y)-\frac{1}{4}\left(1+\frac{(r-1)}{2}d\right)\langle X,Y\rangle_{0}.

On the other hand, by (

\displaystyle B(X,Y)=\operatorname{Tr}{L_{X}L_{Y}}=\left(1+\frac{(r-2)}{4}d\right)\operatorname{tr}(XY)+\frac{d}{4}\operatorname{tr}(X)\cdot\operatorname{tr}(Y).

It follows that

\displaystyle\textnormal{Ric}_{0}(X,Y)=-\frac{rd}{16}\langle X,Y\rangle_{0}+\frac{d}{16}\operatorname{tr}(X)\cdot\operatorname{tr}(Y).

This completes the proof of the Lemma.

3.18
we have

\displaystyle\textnormal{Ric}(X,Y)=\langle\textnormal{M}X,Y\rangle-\frac{1}{4}\langle H,XY\rangle,~~\forall X,Y\in\mathscr{N},

where
H=\sum_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}^{2}
is the mean curvature vector,
\{E_{i}\}
is an orthonormal basis of
(\mathscr{N},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
and

\displaystyle\langle\textnormal{M}X,Y\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\langle XE_{i},E_{j}\rangle\langle YE_{i},E_{j}\rangle+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j}\langle E_{i}E_{j},X\rangle\langle E_{i}E_{j},Y\rangle,~~\forall X,Y\in\mathscr{N}.

It is clear that we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}\textnormal{M}=-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j,k}\langle E_{i}E_{j},E_{k}\rangle^{2}\leq 0,

and the equality holds if and only
\mathscr{N}
is the trivial algebra. Moreover, if
0\neq Z
lies in the annihilator of
\mathscr{N}
, then

\displaystyle\langle\textnormal{M}Z,Z\rangle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j}\langle E_{i}E_{j},Z\rangle\langle E_{i}E_{j},Z\rangle\geq 0.

Theorem 5.9
.

A nontrivial nilpotent Jordan algebra admits no Jordan-Einstein metrics.

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary that
(\mathscr{N},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
is a nontrivial nilpotent metric Jordan algebra with

\displaystyle\textnormal{Ric}=c\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle,

for some constant
c\in\mathbb{R}
. Since the Killing form
B
of
\mathscr{N}
vanishes, then

\displaystyle c\cdot\operatorname{dim}\mathscr{N}=\operatorname{Tr}\textnormal{Ric}=\operatorname{Tr}\textnormal{M}+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Tr}B-\frac{1}{4}\langle H,H\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}\textnormal{M}-\frac{1}{4}\langle H,H\rangle<0.

Consequently,
c<0
. On the the hand, let
0\neq Z
be an element lying in the annihilator of
\mathscr{N}
. Then

\displaystyle c\langle Z,Z\rangle=\textnormal{Ric}(Z,Z)=\langle\textnormal{M}Z,Z\rangle-\frac{1}{4}\langle H,Z^{2}\rangle=\langle\textnormal{M}Z,Z\rangle\geq 0.

It follows that
c\geq 0
, which is contradiction. So a nontrivial nilpotent Jordan algebra admits no Jordan-Einstein metrics.
This proves the theorem.
∎

Example 5.10
.

Consider the
n
-dimensional Jordan algebra
\mathscr{A}

\displaystyle e_{1}e_{1}=e_{1},~e_{1}e_{2}=e_{2},~\cdots,~e_{1}e_{n}=e_{n}.

It is neither nilpotent nor semisimple.
Endow
\mathscr{A}
with a metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
so that
\{e_{1},e_{2},\cdots,e_{n}\}
is an orthonormal basis. Then by a straightforward calculation, we have

\displaystyle\textnormal{Ric}=0.

That is,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is a flat Jordan-Einstein metric on
\mathscr{A}
.

6.
Further study

For a metric Lie algebra
({\mathfrak{g}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, there is an important notion called
algebraic Ricci solitons
, i.e., the Ricci operator satisfies

\textnormal{Ric}=cI+D,~~~~\textnormal{for some}~c\in\mathbb{R},~D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g}).

The notion arises as limits under Ricci flow can be naturally seen as a generalization of Einstein metric (
[
27
,
28
,
33
]
).
It is well-known that nilpotent Lie algebras admit no Einstein metrics, but many of them admit
a nilsoliton metric
(i.e., algebraic Ricci soliton in the nilpotent case). This is also true for nilpotent Jordan algebras (see Theorem
5.9
and
[
24
]
). For nilpotent Lie algebras, nilsolitons can be characterized by a solvable Einstein extension, and we don’t know whether or not it holds for nilpotent metric Jordan algebras. Besides,
classifying Jordan-Einstein metrics on formally real Jordan algebras up to isometry and scaling
, is of interest, noting that the Lie version is still open (see
[
9
]
).

Generally speaking, the differential geometry of invariant metrics on Lie (super-)algebras is a classical topic, having important applications to homogeneous spaces. We try to build a similar theory for metric Jordan algebras in this paper, hoping to provide a way to understand homogeneous (super-)geometry by this Jordan theory.

Appendix A
Curvatures of metric Lie algebras

Let
G
be a connected Lie group with the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
consisting
of left-invariant vector fields, and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
be a left-invariant Riemannian metric on
G
. It is well-known
that we may identify
(G,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
with the metric Lie algebra
({\mathfrak{g}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
(see
[
7
,
25
]
). Let
\nabla
be the Levi-Civita connection associated with
({\mathfrak{g}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
and
X,Y,Z,U,V\in{\mathfrak{g}}
. Then the torsion-free property and the
metric-preserving property of
\nabla
are respectively given by

\displaystyle[X,Y]

\displaystyle=\nabla_{X}Y-\nabla_{Y}X,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{X}Y,Z

\displaystyle\rangle+\langle Y,\nabla_{X}Z\rangle=0.

(A.2)

By Koszul’s formula, one knows that the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla
of
({\mathfrak{g}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
is uniquely determined by
the equations (
A.1
) and (
A.2
), that is,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{X}Y,

\displaystyle Z\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(\langle[X,Y],Z\rangle)-\langle[Y,Z],X\rangle)+\langle[Z,X],Y\rangle).

Associated with the Levi-Civita connection
\nabla
, the Riemann curvature tensor of
({\mathfrak{g}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
is defined by

\displaystyle R(X,Y)Z=-\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}Z+\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}Z+\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z,

or equivalently,
R(X,Y)=\nabla_{[X,Y]}-[\nabla_{X},\nabla_{Y}]
. We remark that a frequently encountered definition of
Riemann curvature tensor in the literature differs from the above by a sign! The
(0,2)
-type Ricci tensor
Ric of
({\mathfrak{g}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
is a trace or contraction of
R
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\textnormal{Ric}=\operatorname{Tr}{(X\rightarrow R(U,X)V)}.

By the fundamental symmetry properties of
R
, one knows that Ric is a symmetric tensor. Furthermore, by taking the
trace of Ric, we obtain the scalar curvature sc of
({\mathfrak{g}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\textnormal{sc}=\operatorname{Tr}_{\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle}\textnormal{Ric}.

In the frame of metric Lie algebra
({\mathfrak{g}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, there is a
mean curvature vector

H\in{\mathfrak{g}}
, which is defined by

\displaystyle\langle H,X\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}\operatorname{ad}X,~\forall X\in{\mathfrak{g}}.

Note that the Lie algebra
{\mathfrak{g}}
is
unimodular
if and only if
H=0
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\{E_{i}\}
be an arbitrary orthonormal basis of
({\mathfrak{g}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle).
Then

\displaystyle\textnormal{Ric}(X,Y)=-

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\langle[X,E_{i}],E_{j}\rangle\langle[Y,E_{i}],E_{j}\rangle+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j}\langle[E_{i},E_{j}],X\rangle\langle[E_{i},E_{j}],Y\rangle

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}B(X,Y)-\frac{1}{2}(\langle[H,X],Y\rangle+\langle X,[H,Y]\rangle),~~\forall X,Y\in{\mathfrak{g}},

where
B
is the Killing form of
{\mathfrak{g}},
and
H
is the mean curvature vector of
({\mathfrak{g}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
.

The Ricci operator Ric (by abuse of notation) of
({\mathfrak{g}},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
is defined by
\langle\textnormal{Ric}(\cdot),(\cdot)\rangle=\textnormal{Ric}(\cdot,\cdot).
It follows from Lemma
A.1
that the Ricci operator is given by

\displaystyle\textnormal{Ric}=\textnormal{M}-\frac{1}{2}B-S(\operatorname{ad}H),

(A.3)

where
B
denotes the symmetric map defined by the Killing form relative to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle,

S(\operatorname{ad}H)=\frac{1}{2}(\operatorname{ad}H+(\operatorname{ad}H)^{t})
and M is the symmetric map defined by

\displaystyle\langle\textnormal{M}X,Y\rangle=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\langle[X,E_{i}],E_{j}\rangle\langle[Y,E_{i}],E_{j}\rangle+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{i,j}\langle[E_{i},E_{j}],X\rangle\langle[E_{i},E_{j}],Y\rangle,~~\forall X,Y\in{\mathfrak{g}}.

(A.4)

Note that for a nilpotent metric Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, the Ricci operator of
(\mathfrak{n},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
coincides with
\textnormal{M}.

Appendix B
Formally real Jordan algebras

Lemma B.1

(
[
20
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be an arbitrary Jordan algebra. Then the following identities hold:

(i)

[L_{X},L_{Y^{2}}]+2[L_{Y},L_{XY}]=0
,

(ii)

[L_{X},L_{YZ}]+[L_{Y},L_{ZX}]+[L_{Z},L_{XY}]=0
,

(iii)

L_{X^{2}Y}-L_{X^{2}}L_{Y}=2(L_{XY}-L_{X}L_{Y})L_{X}
,

for any
X,Y,Z\in\mathscr{A}
.

A Jordan algebra
\mathscr{A}
over
\mathbb{R}
with an identity element
E
is called a
formally real Jordan algebra
if
X^{2}+Y^{2}=0\Rightarrow X=0,Y=0
. This in particular implies that the mean curvature vector of metric formally real Jordan algebras never vanishes (see Definition
3.15
).

The following proposition characterizes formally real Jordan algebras.

Proposition B.2

(
[
20
]
)
.

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a Jordan algebra over
\mathbb{R}
with an identity element. The following statements are equivalent

(i)

\mathscr{A}
is a formally real Jordan algebra.

(ii)

There exists a positive definite symmetric bilinear form on
\mathscr{A}
which is associative.

(iii)

The symmetric bilinear form
\operatorname{tr}(XY)
is positive definite.

(iv)

The symmetric bilinear form
\tau(X,Y)=\operatorname{Tr}L_{XY}
is positive definite.

The proof of (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i) in Proposition
B.2
follows from the fact that if
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is an associative inner product on
\mathscr{A}
and
X^{2}+Y^{2}=0
, then
\langle X^{2}+Y^{2},E\rangle=\langle X,X\rangle+\langle Y,Y\rangle=0
, so
X=Y=0
.

Theorem B.3

(
[
20
]
)
.

Every formally real Jordan algebra is semisimple, which decomposes, in a unique way, a direct sum of simple ideals. Besides, every complex semisimple Jordan algebra is the complexification of some formally real Jordan algebra.

Moreover

Proposition B.4

(
[
20
]
)
.

In a simple formally real Jordan algebra, the associative symmetric bilinear form is unique up to a scalar. In particular, every associative symmetric bilinear form is a scalar multiple of
\operatorname{tr}(XY).

Remark B.5
.

Combining the results above, one may reasonably consider formally real Jordan algebras as the counterparts of compact real forms in complex semisimple Lie algebras.

In the sequel, we recall the Jordan frame and the corresponding Peirce decomposition. Assume that
\mathscr{A}
is a simple formally real Jordan algebra of fixed dimension
n

r\geq 2
. By Lemma
2.5

2.7

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}(XY)=\frac{r}{n}\tau(X,Y)=\frac{r}{n}\operatorname{Tr}L_{XY},~~\forall X,Y\in\mathscr{A}.

(B.1)

A non-zero element
C
of
\mathscr{A}
is called a

, if
C^{2}=C
and
C
cannot be written as the sum of two non-zero idempotents. It is known that there exists a

\mathscr{A}

\displaystyle X=\lambda_{1}H_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{r}H_{r},
