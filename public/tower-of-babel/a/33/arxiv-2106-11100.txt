Alternative rings whose associators are not zero-divisors
Erwin Kleinfeld Yoav Segev

Abstract
. The purpose of this short note is to prove that if R R is an alternative ring whose associators are not zero-divisors, then R R has no zero divisors. By a result of Bruck and Kleinfeld, if, in addition, the characteristic of R R is not 2 , 2, then the central quotient of R R is an octonion division algebra over some field.

1.
Introduction

In this paper we charaterize proper (i.e. not associative) alternative rings
whose central quotient (in the sense of
[
BK
]
) is an octonion division algebra over some field,
as in the Main Theorem.
This adds to another recent characterization that we proved in
[
KS
]
.
For historical information on the octonions see
[
Bl
,
E
]
,
and for their connection with algebraic groups see
[
SpV
]
.

Before we state our main result, we draw the
attention of the reader to Remark
3.2
with
regards to the notion of a zero divisor.

Main Theorem
.

Let
R
be an alternative, not associative ring.
Assume that
a non-zero associator in
R
is not a divisor of zero. Then

(a)

R
contains no divisors of zero.

(b)

If, in addition, the characteristic of
R
is not
2,

then the central quotient ring
R//C

is an
8
-dimensional octonion division algebra
over its center–the fraction field of the
center
C
of
R.

2.
Preliminaries on alternative rings

Our main reference for alternative rings is
[
K
]
.
Let
R
be a ring, not necessarily with
{\bf 1}
and not
necessarily associative.

Definitions 2.1
.

Let
x,y,z\in R.

(1)

The
associator

(x,y,z)
is defined to be

(x,y,z)=(xy)z-x(yz).

(2)

The
commutator

(x,y)
is defined to be

(x,y)=xy-yx.

(3)

R
is an
alternative ring
if

(x,y,y)=0=(y,y,x),

for all
x,y\in R.

(4)

The
nucleus
of
R
is denoted
N
and defined

N=\{n\in R\mid(n,R,R)=0\}.

Note that in an alternative ring the associator is skew symmetric in its
3

variables (
[
K
, Lemma 1]
). Hence
(R,n,R)=(R,R,n)=0,
for
n\in N.

(5)

The
center
of
R
is denoted
C
and defined

C=\{c\in N\mid(c,R)=0\}.

In the remainder of this paper
R
is an alternative ring

which is
not associative
.

N
denotes the nucleus of
R
and
C
its center.

The following lemma gives some properties of
R
which we will require later.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
w,x,y,z\in R,
and
n,n^{\prime}\in N,
then

(1)

(x,y,z)x=(x,xy,z)=(x,y,xz).

(2)

x(x,y,z)=(x,yx,z)=(x,y,zx)

(3)

(xn,y,z)=(nx,y,z)=(x,y,z)n=n(x,y,z).

(4)

[N,R]\subseteq N.

(5)

Nuclear elements commute with associators.

(6)

(w,n)(w,n)(x,y,z)=0.

Proof.

(1&2) This is in
[
K
, equation (4), p. 130]
, and
[
K
, equation (5), p. 130]
, respectively.

(3) By
[
K
, equation (2), p. 129]
,
(nx,y,z)=n(x,y,z),

and by the beginning of the proof of Lemma 4, p. 132 in
[
K
]
,

(nx,y,z)=(x,y,z)n
and
(xn,y,z)=n(x,y,z).

(4&5) By (3),
(nx,y,z)=(xn,y,z),
hence (4) holds, and also
n(x,y,z)=(x,y,z)n,
so (5) holds.

(6) This is
[
KS
, Lemma 2.4(5)]
.
∎

3.
Proof of the Main Theorem.

In this section
R
is an alternative ring which is not associative.
We assume that

non-zero associators are not zero-divisors in R.

Lemma 3.1
.

(1)

N
is contained in the center of
R.

So
N=C.

(2)

If
n\in N
is non-zero then
n
is not a zero divisor.

(3)

If
x\in R
and
x\notin N,
then
x
is not a zero divisor.

Proof.

(1) This follows from Lemma
2.2
parts (3), (4) and (6).
Indeed let
(x,y,z)\in R
be a non-zero associator,
and let
(w,n)
be a commutator, with
w\in R,
and
n\in N.

If
(w,n)\neq 0,

then
(w,n)(x,y,z)\neq 0,
but since
(w,n)\in N,
we have
(w,n)(x,y,z)=((w,n)x,y,z)\neq 0,

and
(w,n)((w,n)x,y,z)=0,
a contradiction.

(2) Let
n\in N
be non-zero. Suppose
tn=nt=0,
for some
t\in R.

Let
(x,y,z)
be a non-zero associator. Then
(x,y,z)n\neq 0,
and using Lemma
2.2
(3) we get

0=(x,y,z)(nt)=\big((x,y,z)n\big)t=(xn,y,z)t,

so
t=0.

(3) Let
x\in R
such that
x\notin N.
Assume that
xt=0,
for some
t\in R.

Since
x\notin N,
there are
y,z\in R
such that
u:=(x,y,z)\neq 0.

Consider
w:=(u,x,t)=(ux)t
(because
xt=0).

By Lemma
2.2
(1)
ux=(x,xy,z)\neq 0.

If
(u,x,t)=0,
we would get
(x,xy,z)t=0,
so
t=0.

Suppose
(u,x,t)\neq 0,
so that
t\neq 0.
Then, by Lemma
2.2
(1),

(u,x,t)x=(u,x,xt)=0,
this is a contradiction.

Similarly, if we assume that
tx=0,
then arguing as above
replacing
w
with
w^{\prime}=(t,x,u),
and using Lemma
2.2
(2),
we get that
t=0.

∎

Proof of the Main Theorem.

By Lemma
3.1
(2&3),
R
has no zero divisors, this shows part (a).
Part (b) follows from

[
BK
, Theorem A]
.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

As usual, a non-zero element
x\in R
is a
left zero divisor
if there
exists a non-zero
y\in R

such that
xy=0.

Right zero divisors
are similarly defined.
In this paper A non-zero element in
R
is a
zero divisor
if it is
either a left zero divisor or a right zero divisor.

Thus, when we say that an element is

not a zero divisor
we mean that it is both not a left zero
divisor and not a right zero divisor.

Note that our proof shows that if, in the Main Theorem, we would replace
“zero divisor” by “left zero divisor”, then part (a) of the theorem
would still hold. Similarly, part (a) would hold if we would replace
“zero divisor” by “right zero divisor”.
