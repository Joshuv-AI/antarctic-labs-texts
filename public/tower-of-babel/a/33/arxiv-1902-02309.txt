Conformal properties of soft-operators - 1 : Use of null-states
Shamik Banerjee, Pranjal Pandey, Partha Paul

Abstract
Soft-operators are (roughly speaking) zero energy massless particles which live on the celestial sphere in Minkowski space. The Lorentz group acts on the celestial sphere by conformal transformation and the soft-operators transform as conformal primary operators of various dimension and spin. Working in space-time dimensions D = 4 D=4 and 6 6 , in this paper, we study some properties of the conformal representations with (leading) soft photon and graviton as the highest weight operators. Typically these representations contain null-vectors. We argue, from the S S –matrix point of view, that infinite dimensional asymptotic symmetries and conformal invariance require us to set these null-vectors to zero. As a result, the corresponding soft-operator satisfies linear PDE on the celestial sphere. Curiously, these PDEs are equations of motion of Euclidean gauge theories on the celestial sphere with scalar gauge-invariance, i.e, the gauge parameter is a scalar field on the sphere. These are probably related to large U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) and supertranslation transformations at infinity. Now, the PDE satisfied by the soft-operator can be converted into PDE for the S S –matrix elements with the insertion of the soft-operator. These equations can then be solved subject to appropriate boundary conditions on the celestial sphere, provided by (Lorentz) conformal invariance. The resulting soft S S –matrix elements have an interesting ”pure-gauge” form and are determined in terms of a single scalar function. Heuristically speaking, the role of the null-state decoupling is to reduce the number of degrees of freedom or polarisation states of soft photon and graviton to one , given effectively by a single scalar function. This reduction in the number of degrees of freedom makes the Ward-identity for the asymptotic symmetry almost integrable. The result of the integration, which we are not able to perform completely, should of course be Weinberg’s soft-theorem. Finally, we comment on the resemblance of all of these things to quantization of fundamental strings.

There are an infinite number of operators denoted by

Here

The second part of the assumption which says that the primary operators and their descendants do not carry energy-momentum has the following consequence. Suppose
\bra{\{\omega_i,x_i,out\}}S^{1}_{a,in/out}(x)\ket{\{\omega_j,x_j,in\}}

The second assumption is that :

V.1

Let us denote the primary operator

where

is the local rotation matrix associated with the conformal transformation.

Now consider an infinitesimal special conformal transformation (SCT) :

where

R_{ab}(x)=\delta_{ab}+2(\epsilon_{a}x_{b}-\epsilon_{b}x_{a})=\delta_{ab}+2\,\Omega_{ab}(x),\qquad\Omega_{ab}=\epsilon_{a}x_{b}-\epsilon_{b}x_{a}

(33)

So the infinitesimal transformation of
S_{a}(x)
can be written as,

\displaystyle S^{\prime}_{a}(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial x^{\prime}}{\partial x}\right|^{-\frac{1}{4}}R_{ab}(x)S_{b}(x)

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+2\epsilon\cdot x)S_{a}(x)+2\,\Omega_{ab}(x)S_{b}(x)

O^{\prime}(x^{\prime})=\left(1+8\,\epsilon\cdot x\right)O(x)+\boxed{4\,\epsilon_{a}\,\partial_{b}F_{ab}(x)}

F_{ab}(x)=\partial_{a}S_{b}(x)-\partial_{b}S_{a}(x)

Now we impose the constraint that the operator

must transform like a
(\Delta=4,l=0)

(\Delta=4,l=0)

\partial_{a}F_{ab}(x)

\boxed{\partial_{a}F_{ab}=\partial_{a}(\partial_{a}S_{b}-\partial_{b}S_{a})=0}

V.2

Ward Identity for Supertranslation

The discussion of supertranslation is very similar to the case of the infinite dimensional
U(1)
symmetry. So let us only mention the changes that are required.

First of all in the free theory
Banerjee:2018fgd
we can construct the conserved charge
Q_{0}(f)
defined as,

Q_{0}(f)=\int d\mu(\omega,x)\omega f(x)a^{\dagger}(\omega,x)a(\omega,x)

(60)

where
f(x)
is an arbitrary function. This is a generalization of the Hamiltonian
H_{0}
, the generator of time-translation, for which
f(x)=1
. This can be shown to generate supertranslation in the free theory.

One can check that the commutator of
Q_{0}(f)
with the creation operator is given by,

[Q_{0}(f),a^{\dagger}(\omega,x)]=\omega f(x)a^{\dagger}(\omega,x)

(61)

Similarly under Lorentz transformation
\Lambda
,
Q_{0}(f)
transforms as,

U(\Lambda)Q_{0}(f)U^{-1}(\Lambda)=Q_{0}(f^{\prime}),\qquad f^{\prime}(x)=\bigg|\frac{\partial\Lambda^{-1}x}{\partial x}\bigg|^{-\frac{1}{n}}f(\Lambda^{-1}x)

(62)

The rest of the analysis is identical to that of the
U(1)
symmetry and so simply quote the final results.

The conserved charge
Q(f)
can be written as the sum of a hard and a soft charge, i.e,
Q(f)=Q_{H}(f)+Q_{S}(f)
. The hard charge is defined such that,

\begin{gathered}Q^{in}_{H}(f)\ket{\alpha, in}=\bigg(\sum_{k\in\alpha}\omega_{k}f(x_{k})\bigg)\ket{\alpha, in}\\
\bra{\beta, out}Q^{out}_{H}(f)=\bra{\beta, out}\bigg(\sum_{k\in\beta}\omega_{k}f(x_{k})\bigg)\end{gathered}

(63)

Since
Q(f)
is conserved,

Q^{in}_{H}(f)+Q^{in}_{S}(f)=Q^{out}_{H}(f)+Q^{out}_{S}(f)=Q(f)

(64)

This can be written as the Ward-identity
Strominger:2013jfa
;
He
;
Strominger:2014pwa
;
Kapec:2015vwa
;
Pate:2017fgt
;
Campiglia:2015kxa
;
Strominger:2017zoo
,

\boxed{\bra{\beta, out}Q^{out}_{S}(f)\ket{\alpha, in}-\bra{\beta, out}Q^{in}_{S}(f)\ket{\alpha, in}=\bigg(\sum_{i\in\alpha}\omega_{i}f(x_{i})-\sum_{i\in\beta}\omega_{i}f(x_{i})\bigg)\bra{\beta, out}\ket{\alpha, in}}

(65)

It follows from this Ward-identity that the soft charge
Q_{S}(f)
transforms under Lorentz transformation as,

\boxed{U(\Lambda)Q_{S}(f)U^{-1}(\Lambda)=Q_{S}(f^{\prime}),\quad f^{\prime}(x)=\bigg|\frac{\partial\Lambda^{-1}x}{\partial x}\bigg|^{-\frac{1}{n}}f(\Lambda^{-1}x)}

(66)

where we have used that
\Lambda\omega=|\partial x^{\prime}/\partial x|^{-1/n}\omega
.

Now we write
Q_{S}(f)
as an integral, i.e,

Q_{S}(f)=\int d^{n}x\,f(x)O(x)

(67)

where
O(x)
is a local operator. Then the transformation property of
Q_{S}(f)
leads to the following transformation law for
O(x)
under Lorentz transformation,

\boxed{U(\Lambda)O(x)U(\Lambda)^{-1}=\bigg|\frac{\partial x^{\prime}}{\partial x}\bigg|^{\frac{\Delta}{n}}O(x^{\prime}),\quad x^{\prime}=\Lambda x,\quad\Delta=n+1}

(68)

Therefore,
the Ward-identity (
65
) requires
O(x)
to be a scalar conformal primary of dimension

\Delta=n+1
. So this is a
necessary
condition.

VI.1
Potential candidates for
O(x)
in space-time dimension
D=6
or
n=D-2=4

Similar to the case of the
U(1)
symmetry, we now study different possibilities for
O(x)
. For the time being we only consider operators with
\Delta\geq 0
.
A potential candidate for
O(x)
is a (
\Delta=5,l=0
) primary constructed from

S^{\Delta}_{a_{1}a_{2}....a_{l}}
.

First let us write down all the
(\Delta=5,\,l=0)
operators starting from
S^{\Delta}_{a_{1}a_{2}....a_{l}}
. They are given by :

\displaystyle\left(\Delta=0\to\Delta=5,\,l=0\right)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274ptO(x)=(\partial^{2})^{2}\partial_{a}S^{0}_{a}(x),\hskip 8.53581pt\partial^{2}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\partial_{c}S^{0}_{abc}(x),\hskip 5.69054pt\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\partial_{c}\partial_{d}\partial_{e}S^{0}_{abcde}(x)

\displaystyle\left(\Delta=1\to\Delta=5,\,l=0\right)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274ptO(x)=(\partial^{2})^{2}S^{1}(x),\hskip 8.53581pt\partial^{2}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}S^{1}_{ab}(x),\hskip 5.69054pt\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\partial_{c}\partial_{d}S^{1}_{abcd}(x)

\displaystyle\left(\Delta=2\to\Delta=5,\,l=0\right)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274ptO(x)=\partial^{2}\partial_{a}S^{2}_{a}(x),\hskip 11.38109pt\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\partial_{c}S^{2}_{abc}(x)

\displaystyle\left(\Delta=3\to\Delta=5,\,l=0\right)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274ptO(x)=\partial^{2}S^{3}(x),\hskip 22.76219pt\partial_{a}\partial_{b}S^{3}_{ab}(x)

\displaystyle\left(\Delta=4\to\Delta=5,\,l=0\right)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274ptO(x)=\partial_{a}S^{4}_{a}(x)

Again the requirement that
O(x)
must be a
(\Delta=5,l=0)
primary rules out most of the above operators except the two,
\partial^{2}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}S^{1}_{ab}(x)
and
\partial_{a}\partial_{b}S^{3}_{ab}(x)
.

The transformation property of the operator
O(x)=\partial_{a}\partial_{b}S^{3}_{ab}(x)
under infinitesimal SCT (
31
) is

\partial^{\prime}_{a}\partial^{\prime}_{b}S^{\prime(3)}_{ab}(x^{\prime})=(1+10\,\epsilon\cdot x)\,\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\,S^{(3)}_{ab}(x)

(69)

So it is a (
\Delta=5,\,l=0
) primary without any constraint. Let us now study the operator
O(x)=\partial^{2}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}S^{1}_{ab}(x)
.

VI.2
Operator Decoupling

Let us rename the operator
S^{1}_{ab}(x)
as
h_{ab}(x)
. This can be the leading soft-graviton operator which also transforms like a
(\Delta=1,l=2)
primary.

Under an infinitesimal SCT (
31
),
h_{ab}(x)
transforms as

\displaystyle h^{\prime}_{ab}(x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\partial x^{\prime}}{\partial x}\right|^{-\frac{1}{4}}R_{ac}(x)R_{bd}(x)h_{cd}(x)

(70)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+2\,\epsilon\cdot x)\,h_{ab}(x)+2\,\Omega_{ac}(x)\,h_{bc}(x)+2\,\Omega_{bc}(x)h_{ac}(x)

This gives the transformation law of
O(x)=\partial^{2}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}h_{ab}(x)
as,

O^{\prime}(x^{\prime})=\left(1+10\,\epsilon\cdot x\right)O(x)+\boxed{8\,\epsilon_{a}\partial_{c}\left(\partial_{a}\partial_{b}h_{bc}(x)-\partial_{b}\partial_{c}h_{ab}(x)\right)}

(71)

If we define

h_{a}=\partial_{b}h_{ab}

(72)

and

F_{ab}=\partial_{a}h_{b}-\partial_{b}h_{a}

(73)

then we can write,

O^{\prime}(x^{\prime})=\left(1+10\,\epsilon\cdot x\right)O(x)+\boxed{8\,\epsilon_{a}\partial_{b}F_{ab}(x)}

(74)

So if we want
O(x)
to be a (
\Delta=5,l=0
) primary then we must set
\partial_{a}F_{ab}(x)=0
. But
\partial_{a}F_{ab}
is
not
a primary operator and so we cannot set this equal to zero at this stage.

Under an infinitesimal SCT
\partial_{a}F_{ab}(x)
transforms as,

\partial^{\prime}_{b}F^{\prime}_{ab}(x^{\prime})=(1+8\,\epsilon\cdot x)\,\partial_{b}F_{ab}(x)+2\,\Omega_{ac}\,\partial_{b}F_{cb}+\boxed{6\,\epsilon_{b}N_{ab}(x)}

(75)

where

N_{ab}=\partial^{2}h_{ab}-\frac{2}{3}\bigg(\partial_{a}\partial_{c}h_{cb}+\partial_{b}\partial_{c}h_{ca}\bigg)+\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ab}\partial_{c}\partial_{d}h_{cd}

(76)

One can now check that
N_{ab}
is a
(\Delta=3,l=2)
primary
and so we can consistently set
N_{ab}(x)=0
, i.e,

\boxed{N_{ab}=\partial^{2}h_{ab}-\frac{2}{3}\bigg(\partial_{a}\partial_{c}h_{cb}+\partial_{b}\partial_{c}h_{ca}\bigg)+\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ab}\partial_{c}\partial_{d}h_{cd}=0}

(77)

This is a (Lorentz) conformally invariant equation. Taking the divergence of this equation we get,

\boxed{\partial_{a}N_{ab}=\frac{1}{3}\partial_{a}F_{ab}=\frac{1}{3}\partial_{a}(\partial_{a}\partial_{c}h_{cb}-\partial_{b}\partial_{c}h_{ca})=0}

(78)

So
N_{ab}(x)=0
implies
\partial_{a}F_{ab}(x)=0
.

Therefore
O(x)=\partial^{2}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}h_{ab}(x)

will be a
(\Delta=5,l=0)
primary only if
N_{ab}=0
, i.e, (
77
) holds.

VI.3
Differential Equation For S-matrix Element

We proceed exactly as in the case of the
U(1)
.

We denote the S-matrix element with an insertion of
h_{ab}(x)
in the (incoming) outgoing channel by
\bra{\{\omega_i,x_i, out\}}h_{ab}(x)\ket{\{\omega_j,x_j,in\}}
. For simplicity we also assume that all the incoming and outgoing particles are scalars.

Let us also define,

H_{ab}(x,\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})=\bra{\{\omega_i,x_i, out\}}h_{ab}(x)\ket{\{\omega_j,x_j,in\}}

(79)

Now we insert the constraint equation (
77
) into an
S
-matrix element and obtain,

\boxed{\bra{\{\omega_i,x_i, out\}}\bigg(\partial^{2}h_{ab}(x)-\frac{2}{3}\bigg(\partial_{a}\partial_{c}h_{cb}(x)+\partial_{b}\partial_{c}h_{ca}(x)\bigg)+\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ab}\partial_{c}\partial_{d}h_{cd}(x)\bigg)\ket{\{\omega_j,x_j,in\}}=0}

(80)

Since there is no ordering between the
\{x,x_{i,out},x_{j,in}\}
coordinates we can pull the derivatives out of the
S
-matrix element. This leads to,

\partial^{2}H_{ab}(x)-\frac{2}{3}\bigg(\partial_{a}\partial_{c}H_{cb}(x)+\partial_{b}\partial_{c}H_{ca}(x)\bigg)+\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ab}\partial_{c}\partial_{d}H_{cd}(x)=0

(81)

Here we have suppressed the dependence of
H_{ab}
on the rest of the variables
\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\}
. As far as (
81
) is concerned these variables are just constants because all the derivatives act on the
x
coordinate.

If we take the divergence of this equation we get,

\boxed{\partial_{a}f_{ab}(x)=0}

(82)

where we have defined,

f_{ab}(x)=\partial_{a}H_{b}-\partial_{b}H_{a}=\partial_{a}\partial_{c}H_{bc}-\partial_{b}\partial_{c}H_{ac}

(83)

Let us note that we could have obtained (
82
) directly from the operator relation (
78
). Now in order to solve (
81
) and (
82
) we need boundary condition. To obtain the boundary condition we proceed exactly as in the case of
U(1)
and reproduce only the essential formulas.

From the Lorentz invariance of S-matrix we can write,

\bra{\{\omega_i,x_i, out\}}h_{ab}(x)\ket{\{\omega_j,x_j,in\}}=\bra{\{\omega'_i,x'_i, out\}}h^{\prime}_{ab}(x)\ket{\{\omega'_j,x'_j,in\}}

(84)

where

h^{\prime}_{ab}(x)=\bigg|\frac{\partial x^{\prime}}{\partial x}\bigg|^{\frac{1}{4}}R^{-1}_{ac}(x)R^{-1}_{bd}(x)h_{cd}(x^{\prime})=\bigg|\frac{\partial x^{\prime}}{\partial x}\bigg|^{\frac{1}{4}}R_{ca}(x)R_{db}(x)h_{cd}(x^{\prime})

(85)

Now studying the behavior of the
S
-matrix element under inversion
X_{a}\rightarrow X_{a}/X^{2}
we arrive at the following large
x
behavior of the
S
-matrix element,

\boxed{\bra{\{\omega_i,x_i, out\}}h_{ab}(x)\ket{\{\omega_j,x_j,in\}}\xrightarrow{\text{$x\rightarrow\infty$}}\frac{1}{x^{2}}I_{ac}(x)I_{bd}(x)M_{cd}(\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})+O(\frac{1}{x^{3}})}

(86)

where we have defined

M_{ab}(\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})=\bra{\{\omega'_i,x'_i, out\}}h_{ab}(0)\ket{\{\omega'_j,x'_j,in\}}

(87)

Again
M_{ab}
is expected to be finite because we have assumed that none of the
(x_{i,out},x_{j,in})
is at
\infty
.

Now in terms of
H_{ab}
the boundary condition (
86
) can be rewritten as,

\boxed{H_{ab}(x,\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})\xrightarrow{\text{$x\rightarrow\infty$}}\frac{1}{x^{2}}I_{ac}(x)I_{bd}(x)M_{cd}(\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})+O(\frac{1}{x^{3}})}

(88)

As a consistency check, we will show in section (
XI
) that the boundary condition (
88
), together with Weinberg’s soft-graviton theorem, is equivalent to the conservation of energy-momentum.

Let us now solve the equations (
81
) and (
82
). Equation (
82
) is the source-free
Euclidean
Maxwell equation on
R^{4}
with
H_{a}
as the vector potential. Now using the falloff condition on
H_{ab}
given by (
88
), we get
f_{ab}\sim O(\frac{1}{x^{4}})
as
x\rightarrow\infty
. So by the same argument as in the case of
U(1)
(after (
40
)) we get,

f_{ab}=\partial_{a}H_{b}-\partial_{b}H_{a}=0\Longleftrightarrow\boxed{H_{a}=\partial_{a}\psi(x,\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})=\partial_{b}H_{ab}}

(89)

where
\psi
is a scalar function. If we substitute this in (
81
) we get,

\boxed{\partial^{2}H_{ab}=\frac{4}{3}\bigg(\partial_{a}\partial_{b}-\frac{1}{4}\delta_{ab}\partial^{2}\bigg)\psi(x,\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})}

(90)

This is Poisson’s equation for each component of
H_{ab}
. The solution to this equation can be written as,

H_{ab}(x,\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})=\frac{4}{3}\bigg(\partial_{a}\partial_{b}-\frac{1}{4}\delta_{ab}\partial^{2}\bigg)f(x,\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})+\tilde{H}_{ab}(x,\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})

(91)

where,

\partial^{2}f=\psi,\quad\partial^{2}\tilde{H}_{ab}=0,\quad\partial_{a}\tilde{H}_{ab}=0

(92)

The last equation,
\partial_{a}\tilde{H}_{ab}=0
, can be obtained by taking divergence of (
91
) and taking into account equation (
89
) and the relation
\partial^{2}f=\psi
.

Now from the boundary condition (
88
) we know that
H_{ab}
goes to zero as
x\rightarrow\infty
. So, if the first term on the R.H.S of (
91
) goes to zero as
x\rightarrow\infty
then
\tilde{H}_{ab}
is must also go to zero as
x\rightarrow\infty
. This will imply that
\tilde{H}_{ab}
is identically zero because it is harmonic everywhere in space.

Now proving that the first term on the R.H.S of (
91
) indeed goes to zero at infinity will require a bit more analysis and so far we have not been able to convince ourselves that this indeed happens. So we keep the general solution for the
S
–matrix in this form, i.e,

\begin{gathered}H_{ab}(x,\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})=\\
\boxed{\bra{\{\omega_i,x_i, out\}}h_{ab}(x)\ket{\{\omega_j,x_j,in\}}=\frac{4}{3}\bigg(\partial_{a}\partial_{b}-\frac{1}{4}\delta_{ab}\partial^{2}\bigg)f(x,\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})+\tilde{H}_{ab}(x,\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})}\end{gathered}

(93)

where
\tilde{H}_{ab}
is harmonic everywhere and transverse, i.e,

\boxed{\partial^{2}\tilde{H}_{ab}(x,\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})=0,\quad\partial_{a}\tilde{H}_{ab}(x,\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})=0}

(94)

An important point to note is that the term
\tilde{H}_{ab}
does
not
contribute to the supertranslation Ward-identity (
65
). The reason is that the contribution of
h_{ab}
to the operator
O(x)
is given by
\partial^{2}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}h_{ab}
and so its insertion in a
S
–matrix element is given by,

\boxed{\bra{out}O_{in/out}(x)\ket{in}=(\partial^{2})^{3}f_{in/out}(x,\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})}

(95)

Now (
93
) is also consistent with Weinberg’s soft-graviton theorem. In
D=5+1
Weinberg’s soft-graviton theorem can be written as
Kapec:2017gsg
;
Kapec:2015vwa
,

\begin{gathered}\bra{\{\omega_i,x_i, out\}}h^{out}_{ab}(x)\ket{\{\omega_j,x_j,in\}}\\
=\gamma\bigg[\sum_{i\in out}\omega_{i}\bigg(\delta_{ab}-4\frac{(x-x_{i})_{a}(x-x_{i})_{b}}{(x-x_{i})^{2}}\bigg)-\sum_{j\in in}\omega_{j}\bigg(\delta_{ab}-4\frac{(x-x_{j})_{a}(x-x_{j})_{b}}{(x-x_{j})^{2}}\bigg)\bigg]\\
\times\bra{\{\omega_i,x_i, out\}}\ket{\{\omega_j,x_j,in\}}\end{gathered}

(96)

where
\gamma
is a numerical constant whose exact value is not necessary for our purpose. Now comparing (
96
) with (
93
) we get,

f(x,\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})=\gamma\bigg(\sum_{i\in out}\omega_{i}(x-x_{i})^{2}\ln(x-x_i)^{2}-\sum_{j\ in}\omega_{j}(x-x_{j})^{2}\ln(x-x_j)^{2}\bigg)\bra{\{\omega_i,x_i, out\}}\ket{\{\omega_j,x_j,in\}}

(97)

and

\tilde{H}_{ab}(x,\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})=0

(98)

So (
93
) is in fact a weaker result in the sense that the soft graviton theorem gives
\tilde{H}_{ab}=0
. It seems that one should be able to prove
\tilde{H}_{ab}=0
using (
94
) and the boundary conditions. We hope to return to this in future.

VI.4
Other Operators In The List

\displaystyle\left(\Delta=0\to\Delta=5,\,l=0\right)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274ptO(x)=(\partial^{2})^{2}\partial_{a}S^{0}_{a}(x),\hskip 8.53581pt\partial^{2}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\partial_{c}S^{0}_{abc}(x),\hskip 5.69054pt\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\partial_{c}\partial_{d}\partial_{e}S^{0}_{abcde}(x)

\displaystyle\left(\Delta=1\to\Delta=5,\,l=0\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274ptO(x)=(\partial^{2})^{2}S^{1}(x),\hskip 8.53581pt\partial^{2}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}S^{1}_{ab}(x),\hskip 5.69054pt\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\partial_{c}\partial_{d}S^{1}_{abcd}(x)

\displaystyle\left(\Delta=2\to\Delta=5,\,l=0\right)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274ptO(x)=\partial^{2}\partial_{a}S^{2}_{a}(x),\hskip 11.38109pt\partial_{a}\partial_{b}\partial_{c}S^{2}_{abc}(x)

\displaystyle:

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274ptO(x)=\partial^{2}S^{3}(x),\hskip 22.76219pt\partial_{a}\partial_{b}S^{3}_{ab}(x)

We have already considered the operator
\partial^{2}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}S^{1}_{ab}(x)
and
\partial_{a}\partial_{b}S^{3}_{ab}(x)
. The rest of the operators in the list can be studied in the same way. Let us, for example, consider the operator
O(x)=(\partial^{2})^{2}S^{1}(x)
. Under infinitesimal SCT (
31
) the operator transforms as,

(\partial^{\prime 2})^{2}S^{\prime 1}(x^{\prime})=(1+10\,\epsilon\cdot x)\,(\partial^{2})^{2}\,S^{1}(x)+\boxed{8\,\epsilon_{a}\partial_{a}\partial^{2}\,S^{1}(x)}

(99)

So if we want this operator to transform as a primary then we have to set the extra piece
\partial_{a}\partial^{2}S^{1}(x)
to zero. But this cannot be done because
\partial_{a}\partial^{2}S^{1}(x)
is not a primary operator. Under an infinitesimal SCT (
31

\partial^{\prime}_{a}\partial^{\prime 2}S^{\prime 1}(x^{\prime})=(1+8\,\epsilon\cdot x)\,\partial_{a}\partial^{2}\,S^{1}(x)+2\,\Omega_{ab}\partial_{b}\partial^{2}\,S^{1}(x)+\boxed{6\,\epsilon_{a}\partial^{2}\,S^{1}(x)}

(100)

So we have to set
\partial^{2}S^{1}(x)
to zero and this is possible because it is a primary operator, i.e, under infinitesimal SCT (
31
)

\partial^{\prime 2}S^{\prime 1}(x^{\prime})=(1+6\,\epsilon\cdot x)\,\partial^{2}\,S^{1}(x)

(101)

Now we can impose the constraint
\partial^{2}\,S^{1}(x)=0
consistently but this makes the operator
O(x)=(\partial^{2})^{2}S^{1}(x)
identically zero.

Therefore the potential candidates for
O(x)
in the
\Delta\geq 0
range are
O(x)=\partial^{2}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}S^{1}_{ab}(x)
and
\partial_{a}\partial_{b}S^{3}_{ab}(x)
.

Actually there is one more candidate for
O(x)
which is constructed out of a
(\Delta=-1,l=0)
primary
S^{-1}(x)
, given by
O(x)=(\partial^{2})^{3}S^{-1}(x)
. One can check that this indeed is a
(\Delta=5,l=0)
primary without any constraint on
S^{-1}(x)
. We have not mentioned this in this section because it has scaling dimension
\Delta=-1
.

Therefore the complete list of possibilities for
O(x)
is,

\boxed{O(x)=\partial^{2}\partial_{a}\partial_{b}S^{1}_{ab}(x),\quad(\partial^{2})^{3}S^{-1}(x),\quad\partial_{a}\partial_{b}S^{3}_{ab}(x)}

Later we will argue that we can actually get rid of the operator
(\partial^{2})^{3}S^{-1}(x)
.

VII
\Delta<0
primaries and more general possibilities for
O(x)

So far, in both the cases of
U(1)
and supertranslation, we have studied only primary operators
S^{\Delta}_{a_{1}a_{2}..a_{l}}(x)
with
\Delta\geq 0
. Now we want to relax the constraint on
\Delta
. In order to do this we need to systematise our observations in the last sections because for
\Delta<0
there are an infinite number of operators to check. Since the discussion for the
U(1)
and supertranslation are structurally identical, as far the representation of the conformal group is concerned, let us focus on
U(1)
:

In space-time dimensions
D=5+1
or
n=4
, the Ward-identity (
11
) requires the existence of a
(\Delta=4,l=0)

primary

O(x)
which we assume to be a descendant of the primaries
S^{\Delta}_{a_{1}a_{2}...a_{l}}(x)
. If we start with a primary
S^{\Delta}_{a_{1}a_{2}...a_{l}}(x)
then we can construct a unique
(\Delta=4,l=0)
descendant
O(x)
given by,

O(x)=(\partial^{2})^{\frac{4-\Delta-l}{2}}\partial_{a_{1}}....\partial_{a_{l}}S^{\Delta}_{a_{1}a_{2}...a_{l}}(x)

(103)

This requires
\frac{4-\Delta-l}{2}
to be a non-negative integer. Therefore not all primaries
S^{\Delta}_{a_{1}a_{2}...a_{l}}(x)
can contribute to
O(x)
. Now we impose the restriction that
O(x)
should also be a
primary
operator. This requires
S^{\Delta}_{a_{1}a_{2}...a_{l}}(x)
to have a
(\Delta=4,l=0)

primary descendant
. This is a non-trivial constraint. To solve this we study how
O(x)
in (
103
) transforms under infinitesimal special conformal transformations. At the first step this generates the operator
K_{b_{1}}O
where
K_{b_{1}}
is a generator of SCT. If
O(x)
is a primary then
K_{b_{1}}O=0
and the process stops. If not then we repeat it. At the next step we generate the operator
K_{b_{2}}K_{b_{1}}O
. If
K_{b_{1}}O
is a primary then
K_{b_{2}}K_{b_{1}}O=0
and the process stops. Since
K_{b_{1}}O
is a primary we can consistently set
K_{b_{1}}O=0
and this turns
O(x)
into a primary. The equation
K_{b_{1}}O=0
becomes a linear conformally invariant differential equation which is satisfied by the corresponding primary
S^{\Delta}_{a_{1}a_{2}...a_{l}}(x)
. Now a crucial point is that the constraint
K_{b}O=0
can make the operator
O
vanish unless the dimension and the spin of the primary
S^{\Delta}_{a_{1}a_{2}...a_{l}}(x)
take certain specific values. For example, we have seen that when
O(x)=\partial^{2}\partial_{a}S^{1}_{a}(x)
, the constraint equation is
K_{b}O=\partial_{a}(\partial_{a}S^{1}_{b}-\partial_{b}S^{1}_{a})=\partial_{a}F_{ab}=0
. Now
\partial_{a}F_{ab}
is a
(\Delta=3,l=1)
primary and it has a unique scalar descendant of dimension
4
, given by
\partial_{b}\partial_{a}F_{ab}
, which is identically zero because of the antisymmetry of
F_{ab}
. Now, if this were not zero, then this would have been proportional to the operator
O(x)
and so the constraint
\partial_{a}F_{ab}=0
would make
O(x)
zero, which is a trivial solution. In fact this is exactly the case for the primaries
S^{\Delta}_{a_{1}a_{2}...a_{l}}(x)
with
\Delta<0
.

Now suppose that instead of the second step, the process stops after
(p+1)
steps, i.e,
K_{b_{p+1}}\big(K_{b_{p}}K_{b_{p-1}}...K_{b_{1}}O\big)=0
. So the primary operator
K_{b_{p}}K_{b_{p-1}}...K_{b_{1}}O
is obtained at the
p
-th step. Since
O(x)
is a scalar and
K_{a}

VIII
”Gauge–structure” in differential equations and ”redundancy” of scalar primaries

The differential equations (

The case of

IX
Can the Ward-identity be solved?

For concreteness let us focus on the
U(1)
symmetry. We have seen that if we have some energetic charged particles and a conformal sector with primary operators
S^{\Delta}_{a_{1}a_{2}...a_{l}}
then the infinite dimensional
U(1)
symmetry and Lorentz invariance of the
S
–matrix dictates the form of the soft-charge to be (
115
),

Q^{out}_{S}(f)=\int d^{4}x\ f(x)O^{out}(x)=\int d^{4}xf(x)\bigg(\gamma\partial_{a}S^{3}_{a,out}(x)+\delta\partial^{2}\partial_{a}S^{1}_{a,out}(x)\bigg)

(122)

Q^{in}_{S}(f)=\int d^{4}x\ f(x)O^{in}(x)=\int d^{4}xf(x)\bigg(\gamma^{\prime}\partial_{a}S^{3}_{a,in}(x)+\delta^{\prime}\partial^{2}\partial_{a}S^{1}_{a,in}(x)\bigg)

(123)

where
(\gamma,\delta)
and
(\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})
are real numbers. Now let us write the Ward-identity (
11
) in the unintegrated form,

\bra{\beta, out}O^{out}(x)\ket{\alpha, in}-\bra{\beta, out}O^{in}(x)\ket{\alpha, in}=\bigg(\sum_{i\in\alpha}q_{i}\delta^{4}(x-x_{i})-\sum_{j\in\beta}q_{j}\delta^{4}(x-x_{j})\bigg)\bra{\beta, out}\ket{\alpha, in}

(124)

Now this is a differential equation for the
S
-matrix elements with the insertion of soft-operators. This may or may not be solvable depending on the structure of
O(x)
. For example, if we take
O(x)=\partial_{a}S^{3}_{a}(x)
then there is no way to solve this equation because there is one differential equation and four (or eight) unknown functions.

The
simplest
theory corresponds to the choice
O(x)=\partial^{2}\partial_{a}S_{a}(x)
. In this case we know, from the decoupling of primary descendants, that (
52
) holds, i.e,

\boxed{\bra{\beta, out}S_{a,out}(x)\ket{\alpha, in}=\partial_{a}\Lambda_{out}(x)}\quad\boxed{\bra{\beta, out}S_{a,in}(x)\ket{\alpha, in}=\partial_{a}\Lambda_{in}(x)}

(125)

Here we have omitted the dependence of
\Lambda_{in/out}
on the coordinates of in and out particles. Now Substituting these in the Ward-identity (
124
) we get,

(\partial^{2})^{2}\Lambda(x)=\bigg(\sum_{i\in\alpha}q_{i}\delta^{4}(x-x_{i})-\sum_{j\in\beta}q_{j}\delta^{4}(x-x_{j})\bigg)\bra{\beta, out}\ket{\alpha, in}

(126)

where we have defined,

\Lambda=\delta\Lambda_{out}-\delta^{\prime}\Lambda_{in}

(127)

This equation can be easily solved subject to the boundary condition (
48
), i.e,

\partial_{a}\Lambda(x)\xrightarrow{\text{$x\rightarrow\infty$}}\frac{1}{x^{2}}I_{ab}(x)M_{b}+O(\frac{1}{x^{3}})

(128)

where
M_{a}
is some constant vector dependent only on the coordinates of the hard particles.

Now one can solve this equation and check that
\partial_{a}\Lambda
is given by Weinberg’s soft-photon theorem, upto normalisation.

Now
\partial_{a}\Lambda
is given by,

\partial_{a}\Lambda=\delta\partial_{a}\Lambda_{out}-\delta^{\prime}\partial_{a}\Lambda_{in}=\delta\bra{\beta, out}S_{a,out}(x)\ket{\alpha, in}-\delta^{\prime}\bra{\beta, out}S_{a,in}(x)\ket{\alpha, in}

(129)

and so is a linear combination of
S
–matrix elements with an outgoing and an incoming soft-photon, respectively. These two amplitudes should be related by crossing but we are unable to determine the precise relation between them at this point. But, we believe that this can be done, and solution to the Ward-identity, with
O(x)=\partial^{2}\partial_{a}S_{a}(x)
, is given by Weinberg’s soft-photon theorem (upto normalization). It will be interesting to complete this proof.

Without going into detail let us simply mention that the same conclusion can be reached for the case of supertranslation in essentially the same way.

Global conservation laws from boundary conditions

This is a consistency check for the boundary conditions (
48
) and (
86
) that we have used for solving the differential equations. One may be skeptical about this this boundary because this was derived under the assumption that inversion is a symmetry. The following demonstration shows its reasonableness.

XI.1
U(1)
charge conservation

For
U(1)
we obtained the following boundary condition by using inversion and the fact that
S_{a}(x)
is a
(\Delta=1,l=1)

\bra{\{\omega_i,x_i,q_i,out\}}S^{out}_{a}(x)\ket{0,in}\xrightarrow{\text{$x\rightarrow\infty$}}\frac{1}{x^{2}}I_{ab}(x)M_{b}(\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha},q_{\alpha}\})+O(\frac{1}{x^{3}})

where
M_{a}
is a vector without
x
dependence. This boundary condition can be applied to the Weinberg soft-theorem because the leading soft–photon operator is also a
(\Delta=1,l=1)
primary. In terms of the
(\omega,x)
variables Weinberg’s soft photon theorem can be written as

,

\bra{\{\omega_i,x_i,q_i,out\}}S^{out}_{a}(x)\ket{0,in}\\
=\gamma\bigg(\sum_{i\in out}q_{i}\frac{2(x-x_{i})_{a}}{(x-x_{i})^{2}}\bigg)\bra{\{\omega_i,x_i,q_i,out\}}\ket{0,in}

(151)

where all the particles are assumed to be outgoing. The soft-factor is usually written in terms of the soft-photon polarization vector for which a particular choice has been made in this formula. Charge conservation is then derived from the fact
Weinberg:1964ew
that the Lorentz invariance of the
S
-matrix also requires it to be invariant under on-shell gauge transformation of the soft-photon polarization vector, i.e,
\epsilon_{\mu}(q)\rightarrow\epsilon_{\mu}(q)+\alpha q_{\mu}
, where
q_{\mu}
is the
4
-momentum of the soft-photon. Now, for the purpose of deriving global charge conservation, instead of the on-shell gauge-invariance one can use the boundary condition (
150
). This is also more natural in our context. Of course, on-shell gauge invariance and the falloff condition in (
150
) are both consequences of Lorentz invariance of the
S
-matrix. We would like to note that this is similar to the situation in 2-D CFT where the fall-off condition
T(z)\sim 1/z^{4}
gives the Ward identities for the global conformal group starting from the correlation function of
T(z)
and a set of primaries.

Let us now find out the large-
x
behavior of (
151
). Since only the soft-factor depends on
x
we simply expand the soft-factor for large-
x
at fixed
(\omega_{i},x_{i})
. The leading term is,

\big(2\sum_{i}q_{i}\big)\frac{x_{a}}{x^{2}}\bra{\{\omega_i,x_i,q_i,out\}}\ket{0,in}\sim O(\frac{1}{x})

(152)

But, (

) tells us that the leading term is of
O(1/x^{2})
because

O(1/x)
term in (

\boxed{\sum_{i}q_{i}=0}

(153)

XI.2
Energy-momentum conservation

In this case one has to use the leading soft-graviton theorem. The relevant boundary condition is given by (
86
)

\bra{\{\omega_i,x_i, out\}}h^{out}_{ab}(x)\ket{0,in}\xrightarrow{\text{$x\rightarrow\infty$}}\frac{1}{x^{2}}I_{ac}(x)I_{bd}(x)M_{cd}(\{\omega_{\alpha},x_{\alpha}\})+O(\frac{1}{x^{3}})

where
M_{ab}
is a traceless symmetric tensor without

is a
(\Delta=1,l=2)
primary which can be taken to be the leading soft-graviton operator. Now Weinberg’s soft-graviton theorem in
D
space-time dimensions can be written as

,

\bra{\{\omega_i,x_i, out\}}h^{out}_{ab}(x)\ket{0,in}=\gamma\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i}\omega_{i}\bigg(\delta_{ab}-n\frac{(x-x_{i})_{a}(x-x_{i})_{b}}{(x-x_{i})^{2}}\bigg)\bra{\{\omega_i,x_i, out\}}\ket{0,in}

(155)

where
n=D-2
. In this paper we have worked in
D=6
but this demonstration is valid for any dimension. The boundary condition does not depend on the dimension because in any dimension the leading soft-graviton operator is a
(\Delta=1,l=2)
primary. We now expand the soft-factor for large
x
but fixed and finite
(\omega_{i},x_{i})
. We also abbreviate the

-matrix element
without

. The results are the following :

1)
O(1)
: The

A_{0}=\big(\sum_{i}\omega_{i}\big)\bigg(\delta_{ab}-n\frac{x_{a}x_{b}}{x^{2}}\bigg)S_{0}

Since there is no

\boxed{\sum_{i}\omega_{i}=0}

(157)

2)
O(1/x)
: The order

A_{1}=\frac{1}{x^{2}}\sum_{i}\omega_{i}\bigg(x_{a}x_{ib}+x_{b}x_{ia}-2x_{a}x_{b}\frac{x_{i}\cdot x}{x^{2}}\bigg)S_{0}

(158)

Again since there is no
O(1/x)

) we get,

\sum_{i}\omega_{i}\bigg(x_{a}x_{ib}+x_{b}x_{ia}-2x_{a}x_{b}\frac{x_{i}\cdot x}{x^{2}}\bigg)=0

Now let us define,

Since the null momenta are parametrized as,

One of the very basic facts of string theory, which comes out of the quantization of fundamental strings, is the correspondence between null-states
