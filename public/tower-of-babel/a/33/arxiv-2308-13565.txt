DARWIN Series: Domain Specific Large Language Models for Natural Science
Tong Xie, Yuwei Wan, Wei Huang, Zhenyu Yin, Yixuan Liu, Shaozhou Wang, Qingyuan Linghu, Chunyu Kit, Clara Grazian, Wenjie Zhang, Imran Razzak, Bram Hoex

Abstract
Emerging tools bring forth fresh approaches to work, and the field of natural science is no different. In natural science, traditional manual, serial, and labour-intensive work is being augmented by automated, parallel, and iterative processes driven by artificial intelligence-based experimental automation and more. To add new capabilities in natural science, enabling the acceleration and enrichment of automation of the discovery process, we present DARWIN, a series of tailored LLMs for natural science, mainly in physics, chemistry, and material science. This series relies on open-source LLM, incorporating structured and unstructured scientific knowledge from public datasets and literature. We fine-tuned the models using over 60,000 instruction data points, emphasizing factual correctness. During the fine-tuning, we introduce the Scientific Instruction Generation (SIG) model, automating instruction generation from scientific texts. This eliminates the need for manual extraction or domain-specific knowledge graphs and efficiently injects scientific knowledge into the model. We also explore multi-task training strategies, revealing interconnections between scientific tasks. DARWIN series not only achieves state-of-the-art results on various scientific tasks but also diminishes reliance on closed-source AI models. Our research showcases the ability of LLM in the scientific domain, with the overarching goal of fostering prosperity within the broader AI for science community.

Related Work

2.1
Large language models

The development of LLM has been an exciting journey, fueled by innovation and a constant push to improve capabilities while addressing critical concerns such as robustness, safety, and ethical implications. It began with simpler models such as Word2Vec
[
14
]
and GloVe
[
15
]
, which fundamentally changed how machines understood and processed natural language by enabling them to capture semantic and syntactic relationships between words. The advent of transformer models like BERT
[
16
]
marked a key milestone in this journey, showcasing the power of attention mechanisms and the potential for pre-training on vast amounts of data. It set the stage for GPT, a series of models developed by OpenAI that pushed the boundaries of what LLMs could do, culminating in the groundbreaking GPT-3, a model with 175 billion parameters that showcased impressive capabilities in generating human-like text.

In the spirit of open research and a commitment to make
LLMs task-adaptable, instruction-tuning
[
3
]
is employed. The instructions are generated from human-annotated prompts and feedback
[
3
]
, or public benchmarks and datasets with manual or automatic augmentation
[
17
]
. Compared to the above approaches, self-instruct
[
11
]
generates new tasks (instructions along with instances) from scratch and has been quickly adopted for its superior performance and low cost. For instance, Stanford Alpaca
[
8
]
is trained on 52k instructions generated by GPT-3.5, and Vicunna
[
9
]
uses around 700k instructions from conversations gathered from ShareGPT.com. There is a direct correlation between the size and diversity of the instruction data and the generalizability of resulting models to unseen tasks
[
18
,
19
]
. While self-instruct tuning brings qualitative behaviours to LLMs, researchers find an effect of model collapse that the use of model-generated content in training may cause irreversible defects in the resulting models, where tails of the original content distribution disappear
[
20
]
.

Some studies train LLMs tailored to specific scientific domains, exemplified by endeavors such as PMC-LLaMA
[

in the medical field. Within the broader natural science domain, Galactica
[

However, a fundamental problem with Galactica is that it is not able to distinguish truth from falsehood, which is normally called halluciation. In response to this challenge, some models have opted to integrate external APIs
[

Simultaneously, scientific LLMs trained for specific tasks have demonstrated promising achievements. Notably, in the realm of biology, BioGPT
[

[

finetuned GPT-3 to solve tasks involving regression, classification, or inverse design and found finetuned GPT-3 often performs on par with, or even surpasses, conventional machine learning models while frequently requiring less training data. In addition, novel NLP tasks have been introduced to address domain-specific challenges. For instance, structured information inference (SII)
[
4

Methods

In this section, we will introduce the training process of the series of DARWIN model, including datasets and modelling.

3.1
Datasets

Various science-related datasets were used in tuning to align LLM to the natural science domain. These generally include science exam questions answering dataset, retrieved questions and corresponding answers from scientific papers, and FAIR datasets.

3.1.1
Scientific knowledge dataset

SciQ dataset.

Standardized tests drive progress in AI by challenging language understanding and pushing the boundaries of knowledge, modelling, and reasoning
[
31
]
. The SciQ dataset
[
32
]
is a large crowdsourced science Questions & Answers (QA) dataset comprising 13,679 science exam questions covering subjects like physics, chemistry, and biology. The questions are presented in multiple-choice format with four answer options, and most of them are accompanied by an additional paragraph containing supporting evidence for the correct answer. Since SciQ used science study textbooks as a base corpus, it provides broad and rather simple science knowledge.

3.2
Modeling

For our model DARWIN, we developed a comprehensive training pipeline (see Figure
9
) utilizing different large language models, LLaMA-7B and Vicuna-7B models, to balance performance and cost. The whole training pipeline involves three LLMs: DARWIN-SIG, DARWIN-BASE and DARWIN-MDP.

3.2.1
DARWIN-SIG

Since pretraining on domain texts may not mitigate hallucination issues, we propose a cost-effective approach for generating instruction data using open-source model. The main idea is to train a generator for converting full-text scientific papers into question-answer pairs. By integrating these knowledge-based content into the instructions, we enhance the reliability of the training dataset and avoid model collapse induced by the utilization of training data fully generated by LLMs. The process of SIG can be break down as following steps:

1.

Prompt design: Through a process of iterative manual examination and seeking advice from domain experts, we were able to design an effective prompt for LLM to generate question-answer pairs with detailed information extracted from scientific papers.

Figure 4:
DARWIN Series Model Structure and Training Pipeline

2.

Seed QA generation: We select seed papers from the scientific paper dataset in Section
3.1.1
and the rest are training papers. The seed papers were utilized as input to produce seed question-answer pairs by prompting GPT-4 (using prompt designed in step 1).

3.

DARWIN-SIG: Our SIG model was developed by fine-tuning the LLM using seed papers and their corresponding question-answer pairs. Given a prompt
p
and input text
x
, the goal of SIG is to generate a set of instructions
I
, each of which is a pair of question and corresponding answer
(q_{i},a_{i})
related to
x
:
SIG(p,x)=I
. The initial prompt was converted into an instruction, while the seed papers served as input to the model.

4.

Training QA generation: Utilizing the trained DARWIN-SIG, the large amount of training papers were subsequently used as input to automatically generate question-answer pairs with low cost. These QA pairs can be directly converted into instruction data.

During the SIG process, there are some details that merit discussion. The first is the choice of base model. Considering the potential inference cost associated with GPT-3.5/4, we turn to use open-source model for training generator. And after human evaluation of performance, we chose Vicuna-7B model as the base model instead of LLaMA or Alpaca. The second is the length limit. The full-text papers are usually longer than limit of 2,048 tokens and for those lengthy ones, we segmented texts and continuously input them by using the “[TBC]” token as a connector (see Figure 5). Thus, the model could output question-answer pairs aligned with the complete content of the papers.

Correspondingly, the answers generated are not overly generalized. 3.2.2
DARWIN-BASE

We generated knowledge-based instruction data for DARWIN based on SciQ dataset in section
3.1.1
and generated question-answer pairs in
3.2.1
. The answers in the SciQ dataset include correct options and provide support, which can be used as background knowledge input or as an explanation of the reasoning process. Based on this scenario, we generated a mixed set of instruction data consisting of the following patterns, arranged in increasing levels of difficulty: 1) Single-turn question-answering, where the model selects an answer based on the question and the provided background knowledge; 2) Single-turn question-answering, where the model selects an answer based on the question and also provides an explanation for the chosen answer; 3) Multi-turn dialogue, where the model selects an answer based on the question and provides an explanation for the chosen answer in response to user follow-up questions. Examples of the above three types are available in the Appendix (see Appendix
C
). Without any input, each question became an instruction, and its corresponding answer became an output. We fine-tuned the LLaMA-7B model using the two kinds of knowledge-based instruction data mentioned above, totalling over 20,000 instances. This process resulted in the development of DARWIN-BASE, a model equipped to engage in scientific question-answering.

In the context of material science, inverse design refers to a computational approach that aims to design new materials or optimize existing ones by working backward from desired properties or functionalities

Instead of starting with a specific material and analyzing its properties, inverse design begins with a desired set of properties or performance targets and seeks to identify the material structure or composition that would exhibit those properties. By utilizing inverse design techniques, researchers can potentially discover novel materials with tailored properties that are challenging or time-consuming through traditional experimental approaches. The inverse design has shown promise in various areas of material science, including photovoltaics, catalysts, batteries, thermoelectric materials, and more. Experiments

4.1
Baselines

To demonstrate the performance of DARWIN on different tasks, we compared the performance of DARWIN with 2 baseline models.

•

LLaMA
is an LLM released by Meta AI with a variety of model sizes from 7 billion to 65 billion parameters. LLaMA-7B version is used as our base model out of its ease of training.

•

GPT-3
is the third-generation language model with 175 billion parameters created by OpenAI.

In addition to the aforementioned two baselines, we included results from several dedicated machine learning models as references, like MODNet (v0.1.1), MolCLR and AMMExpress v2020. Those results were from matbench
[
35
]
. It should be noted that each model was individually trained on a specific FAIR dataset.

4.2
Fine-tuning procedure

We conducted fine-tuning of the LLaMA-7B model with instruction tuning on an 8-A100 GPU. The hyperparameters we used were similar to the ones used with Alpaca: the fine-tuning procedure included training for 3 epochs with a batch size of 4 for both training and evaluation. Gradients were accumulated over 8 steps, the learning rate was set to 2e-5 with no weight decay, and a warm-up ratio of 0.03. We utilized the Brain Floating Point 16 (BF16) data format to strike a balance between precision and computational cost. We fine-tuned LLaMA-7B on subsets or combinations of instruction data and named them differently as follows:

•

gptchem
was fine-tuned on several FAIR datasets separately, using GPT-3 as base model
[
36
]
.

•

LLaMA-single
was fine-tuned on instruction data from each dataset of 12 FAIR datasets, which means there are 12 LLaMA-single.

•

LLaMA-all
was fine-tuned on mixed instruction data of 9 FAIR datasets.

•

DARWIN
was fine-tuned on instruction data from SciQ dataset and question-answer pairs generated by SIG.

•

DARWIN-MDP
was further fine-tuned on DARWIN on mixed instruction data of 16 FAIR datasets.

Our final model DARWIN-MDP is a language model fine-tuned using supervised learning from a LLaMA-7B model on over 60,000 instruction-following demonstrations derived from scientific literature and datasets.

4.3
Metrics

We use accuracy of multiple choice of SciQ as metric to evaluate performance in fine-tuning on scientific knowledge. If the model can choose the correct option and generate the correct corresponding answer, then it is counted as correct:

\text{{Accuracy}}=\frac{{\text{{\# of correctly answered questions}}}}{{\text{{total \# of questions}}}}.

We also evaluated the performance of the fine-tuned DARWIN model on each MDP task separately. We only evaluate classification and regression task, but leave design task for future work due to its difficulty of evaluation.

For the classification task, we evaluted the performance of the model on each dataset using F1 score. When dealing with boolean-type labels (as exemplified in Section
3.2.3
), we compare the predictions, expressed as ’Yes’ or ’No’, with the ground truth values (’True’ or ’False’), and evaluate the F1-binary metric specifically for the class indicated by pos_label. For multi-category labels, we use macro-F1 score (unweighted mean of the F1 scores calculated per class).

For the regression task, we use Mean Absolute Error (MAE) to measure the difference between the predicted value and the true value:

\text{{MAE}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|y_{i}-\hat{y}_{i}|.

Conclusion

The emergence of foundational AI models and expansive language models offers the remarkable potential to redefine the advancement of scientific AI, making it more accessible, accurate, and cross-domain. However, the scientific domain, with its inherent complexities, presents unique challenges when it comes to implementing large language models.

Our research offers a glimpse into the possibilities and hurdles of employing these technologies in scientific applications. In this study, we incorporated existing knowledge bases effectively into LLM and created the DARWIN series which achieved SOTA results on multiple scientific tasks. We also introduced a SIG model that can automatically convert knowledge from a large corpus of papers. Furthermore, we explored different multi-task training strategies and found that training on knowledge-based questions and answers first can improve prediction performance on FAIR datasets, and combining multiple tasks proved to be more effective than individual training, highlighting the underlying connections between seemingly independent tasks.

7
Limitations

Data:
A key limitation of this study stems from the limited quantity of question-answer pairs and dependence on the somewhat confined FAIR datasets, potentially inhibiting the model’s applicability and effectiveness. To address this, future research could focus on amplifying the volume of tasks, generating more question-answer pairs via SIG, thereby enhancing dataset representativeness and improving model performance across various domains.

Model:
In striving for desired performance, this study utilizes multiple models instead of a unified one, introducing complexity and potential inefficiencies. To overcome this, future research should focus on scaling up the model using efficient tuning methods for comparable or superior performance with limited resources. Also, the impact of fine-tuning on large-scale scientific text as a means of injecting knowledge into the model remains untested. Future work will address this through comparative analysis, providing valuable insights into the model’s ability to leverage additional knowledge sources and improve performance in scientific fields.

Evaluation:
DARWIN-BASE is employed as the foundational model, with its performance evaluated in terms of multiple-choice question answering. A comprehensive evaluation mechanism for the question-answering task is warranted to appraise the efficacy of knowledge injection from diverse perspectives. We intend to formulate a model-graded evaluation mechanism that segregates answers into various categories contingent on their quality and compliance with specific criteria, such as misunderstanding, relevance, or correctness. This methodology would afford a more exhaustive assessment of the model’s performance, obviating the need for extensive manual intervention.
