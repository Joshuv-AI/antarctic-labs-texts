All-optical coherent quantum-noise cancellation in cascaded optomechanical systems
Jakob Schweer, Daniel Steinmeyer, Klemens Hammerer, Michèle Heurs

Abstract
Coherent quantum noise cancellation (CQNC) can be used in optomechanical sensors to surpass the standard quantum limit (SQL). In this paper, we investigate an optomechanical force sensor that uses the CQNC strategy by cascading the optomechanical system with an all-optical effective negative mass oscillator. Specifically, we analyze matching conditions, losses and compare the two possible arrangements in which either the optomechanical or the negative mass system couples first to light. While both of these orderings yield a sub-SQL performance, we find that placing the effective negative mass oscillator before the optomechanical sensor will always be advantageous for realistic parameters. The modular design of the cascaded scheme allows for better control of the sub-systems by avoiding undesirable coupling between system components, while maintaining similar performance to the integrated configuration proposed earlier. We conclude our work with a case study of a micro-optomechanical implementation.



I
Introduction

Achieving force measurements at the quantum limit has been a significant focus for several decades
Braginsky et al. 1980
;
Braginsky and Khalili 1995
and has fueled the development of optomechanics
Meystre 2013
;
Chen 2013
;
Aspelmeyer et al. 2014
. Optomechanical sensors exploit the interaction of a light field with the motion of a mechanical oscillator to measure its displacement with high precision. Force measurements based on these schemes are subject to shot noise and quantum radiation pressure backaction noise
Caves 1980
;
Clerk et al. 2010
. Shot noise is caused by the uncertainty in the number of photons over time and can be decreased relative to the signal by increasing the intensity of the optical field. Contrary, the backaction noise arises from the fluctuation in the radiation pressure of the optical field, which will increase with its intensity. The trade-off between these competing processes then sets a lower bound to the precision of the measurement, which is called the standard quantum limit (SQL)
Clerk et al. 2010
;
Bowen and Milburn 2020
;
Danilishin et al. 2019
.

The SQL is not a fundamental limit, and many different approaches have been suggested to achieve measurements with sub-SQL accuracy. These approaches include frequency-dependent squeezing
Unruh 1983
;
Bondurant and Shapiro 1984
;
Jaekel and Reynaud 1990
, variational measurements
Vyatchanin and Zubova 1995
;
Kimble et al. 2001
;
Khalili 2010
, dual mechanical resonators
Woolley and Clerk 2013
;
Briant et al. 2003
;
Caniard et al. 2007
;
de Lépinay et al. 2021
and optical spring effects
Buonanno and Chen 2001
;
Verlot et al. 2010
;
Chen et al. 2011
. In essence, these ideas go beyond the SQL by measuring a quantum non-demolition (QND) variable of the probe, that is, a variable that commutes with itself for different moments in time. In a QND measurement, the backaction is transmitted to the canonically conjugate observable and thus avoided. A more general approach to QND measurements is gained by introducing another system that acts like a reference frame with an effective negative mass
Polzik and Hammerer 2015
. By measuring with respect to this reference system, a QND measurement is realized. When the reference system is a harmonic oscillator, an effective negative mass amounts to a negative eigenfrequency. This idea was first experimentally utilized in demonstrating Einstein-Podolsky-Rosen (EPR) states of two atomic spin oscillators of positive and negative mass
Julsgaard et al. 2001
. Based on this, back action cancellation was demonstrated by Wasilewski et al.
Wasilewski et al. 2010
in the context of magnetometry. Extending this idea, several proposals have been made in a hybrid setting of a mechanical oscillator and atomic spin ensembles
Hammerer et al. 2009
;
Polzik and Hammerer 2015
, and the evasion of backaction noise in these spin ensembles experimentally verified in
Møller et al. 2017
.
Independently, Tsang and Caves
Tsang and Caves 2010
;
Tsang and Caves 2012
developed this idea in a more general context, called quantum-mechanics-free subsystems. In the context of optomechanics, the main idea is to introduce an ‘anti-noise’ path to the dynamics of the optomechanical sensor upon coupling to an ancillary resonator that acts as an effective negative mass. This way, the backaction noise can be cancelled coherently, and sub-SQL force sensing is achieved for all measurement frequencies. Appropriately, this approach is called coherent quantum noise cancellation (CQNC). Details and experimental feasibility of this all-optical effective negative mass oscillator were discussed in more detail by Wimmer et al.
Wimmer et al. 2014
.

Within the area of CQNC force sensing, many other possible negative mass oscillators and setups have been considered. Other setups include the use of ultra-cold atoms inside a separate cavity
Bariani et al. 2015
;
Gebremariam et al. 2019
, hybrid optomechanical cavity, i.e. implementing an atomic ensemble inside the optomechanical sensor
Motazedifard et al. 2016
;
Singh et al. 2022
and employing Bose-Einstein condensates
Zhang et al. 2013
. Even a new all-optical setup was suggested using two detuned optical modes inside the force sensor
Yan and Jing 2021
. These approaches can be categorized into integrated setups, where the effective negative mass is introduced directly into the optomechanical force sensor, and cascaded setups
Carmichael 1993
;
Gardiner 1993
, where the effective negative mass oscillator is a separate system. Recently, Zeuthen et al.
Zeuthen et al. 2022
considered a broad class of effective negative mass oscillators in a cascaded setting and even considered a possible coupling between the positive and negative mass oscillator in a parallel topology.

Inspired by this, we want to discuss a cascaded version of the original all-optical setup
Tsang and Caves 2010
;
Wimmer et al. 2014
. Instead of implementing the ‘anti-noise’ path directly into the optomechanical sensor, an all-optical effective negative mass oscillator is built as a separate system. The backaction is then cancelled by coupling the force sensor to the effective negative mass oscillator via a strong coherent field. This approach will give more freedom in the experimental design and simplify reaching the challenging conditions for a CQNC experiment. The main challenge before cancelling quantum backaction noise is to measure the backaction noise. Due to the modular nature of the cascaded approach, this can be tackled entirely separate from the effective negative mass oscillator. We will see that under some modifications to the matching conditions, our cascaded setup recovers the ideal CQNC performance described in
Wimmer et al. 2014
, and the additional degrees of freedom by expanding the dimension of the system lead to novel phenomena for sub-SQL force sensing. This includes the recovery of ideal CQNC around an off-resonant frequency and possible CQNC performance in the low- or high-frequency regime, even for unmatched CQNC conditions.

This paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we describe the model of our cascaded CQNC scheme and derive the quantum Langevin equations of motion. In Sec.
III
, we discuss force sensing in optomechanical sensors and derive the optimal parameters for ideal CQNC. In Sec.
IV
, we analyze possible deviations from the ideal conditions and their impact on the performance of coherent quantum noise reduction. Then, in Sec.
V
, a case study is provided. Finally, we summarize our findings in Sec.
VI
.

II
Model

Fig.
1
(a) illustrates a possible, schematic realization of our setup. We refer to
Steinmeyer 2019
for details on the experimental implementation of all-optical CQNC. An optomechanical sensor (OMS), subject to an external force and radiation pressure noise, is connected to an effective negative mass oscillator (NMO) by a coherent light field. The force is then measured by detecting outgoing light after the second system. The order of sub-systems can be chosen freely, and the two possible arrangements are depicted in Fig.
1
(b). In the first case, the light travels through the NMO, followed by the OMS. We will refer to this case as
\mathrm{NMO}\mapsto\mathrm{OMS}
. In the second case, the order is reversed; hence the light will travel through the OMS first, and we will refer to this case as
\mathrm{OMS}\mapsto\mathrm{NMO}
. The effects of these different arrangements on the performance of the backaction cancellation will be discussed later.

The OMS is modelled by an optical cavity with resonance frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{om}}
, containing a damped mechanical positive mass oscillator (PMO) with resonance frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{m}}
and linewidth
\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{OMS}}}=\Delta_{\mathrm{om}}c^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{om}}c_{\mathrm{om}}+\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{2}(x_{\mathrm{m}}^{2}+p_{\mathrm{m}}^{2})+\frac{gx_{\mathrm{m}}}{\sqrt{2}}(c_{\mathrm{om}}+c^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{om}}).\end{split}

(1)

Here,

(

) are the annihilation (creation) operators of the optical mode and
x_{\mathrm{m}}=X/x_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{ZPF}}}
,
p_{\mathrm{m}}=Px_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{ZPF}}}/\hbar
are the position and momentum operators of the mechanical oscillator normalized to the zero-point fluctuation
x_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{ZPF}}}=\sqrt{\hbar/m\omega_{\mathrm{m}}}
, such that
[x_{\mathrm{m}},p_{\mathrm{m}}]=i
. The last term in Eq. (
1
) describes the radiation pressure interaction of the cavity mode and the mechanical oscillator. Its strength is given by

, where
L
is the cavity length and
\alpha_{\mathrm{c}}\propto\sqrt{P}
is the field amplitude of the cavity mode, proportional to the input power
P
. Introducing dimensionless amplitude and phase quadratures

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{\mathrm{m}}

\displaystyle=\omega_{\mathrm{m}}p_{\mathrm{m}},

(2a)

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{\mathrm{m}}

\displaystyle=-\omega_{\mathrm{m}}x_{\mathrm{m}}-\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}p_{\mathrm{m}}-gx_{\mathrm{om}}+\sqrt{\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}F,

(2b)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{\mathrm{om}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{om}}}{2}x_{\mathrm{om}}+\Delta_{\mathrm{om}}p_{\mathrm{om}}+\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{om}}}x_{\mathrm{om}}^{\mathrm{in}},

(2c)

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{\mathrm{om}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{om}}}{2}p_{\mathrm{om}}-\Delta_{\mathrm{om}}x_{\mathrm{om}}-gx_{\mathrm{m}}+\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{om}}}p_{\mathrm{om}}^{\mathrm{in}}.

(2d)

Here,

is the decay rate of the cavity mode and

is its vacuum input noise. The noise process fulfills
\langle c_{\mathrm{om}}^{\mathrm{in}}(t)c_{\mathrm{om}}^{\mathrm{in}\dagger}(\tau)\rangle=\delta(t-\tau)

2

of the oscillator. The scaled thermal noise satisfies
\langle F_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{th}}}(t)F_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{th}}}(\tau)\rangle=n_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{th}}}\delta(t-\tau)
, where

is the average phonon number of the mechanical oscillator.

The NMO consists of two optical modes
c_{\mathrm{c}}
and

, with resonance frequencies
\omega_{\mathrm{c}}
and
\omega_{\mathrm{a}}
, coupled with a beam-splitter and down-conversion process. In analogy to

A

(3)

with the detunings
\Delta_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{a}}=\omega_{\mathrm{c},\mathrm{a}}-\omega_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}
, the beam-splitter coupling strength
g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{BS}}}
and the coupling strength of the down-conversion process
g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{DC}}}
. As above, we introduce amplitude and phase quadratures associated with the meter and ancilla cavity. Then the Hamiltonian (

) implies the following QLEs for the NMO,

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}x_{\mathrm{c}}+\Delta_{\mathrm{c}}p_{\mathrm{c}}+(g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{BS}}}-g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{DC}}})p_{\mathrm{a}}+\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}}x_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{in}},

(4a)

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}x_{\mathrm{c}}-\Delta_{\mathrm{c}}x_{\mathrm{c}}-(g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{BS}}}+g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{DC}}})x_{\mathrm{a}}+\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}}p_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{in}},

(4b)

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}}{2}x_{\mathrm{a}}+\Delta_{\mathrm{a}}p_{\mathrm{a}}+(g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{BS}}}-g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{DC}}})p_{\mathrm{c}}+\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}}x_{\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{in}},

(4c)

\displaystyle\dot{p}_{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}}{2}p_{\mathrm{a}}-\Delta_{\mathrm{a}}x_{\mathrm{a}}-(g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{BS}}}+g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{DC}}})x_{\mathrm{c}}+\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}}p_{\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{in}},

(4d)

with the cavity linewidth
\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}
and
\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}
and the input noise processes
\mathrm{a}^{\mathrm{in}}
and

III
Force sensing and ideal CQNC

To solve the dynamics of both systems, we turn to the frequency domain. Introducing the Fourier domain operators

O(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\mathrm{d}t\,O(t)e^{i\omega t},

equations (
2
)–(
4
) can be solved using the standard input-output formalism
Walls and Milburn 2007

\displaystyle x^{\mathrm{out}}=\sqrt{\kappa}x-x^{\mathrm{in}},

(5)

\displaystyle p^{\mathrm{out}}=\sqrt{\kappa}p-p^{\mathrm{in}}.

(6)

We consider the systems separately, first the OMS. On resonance,
\Delta_{\mathrm{om}}=0
, the output quadratures read

\displaystyle\begin{split}x^{\mathrm{out}}_{\mathrm{om}}&=e^{i\phi}x^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{om}},\\
p^{\mathrm{out}}_{\mathrm{om}}&=e^{i\phi}p^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{om}}-\chi_{\mathrm{m}}g^{2}\kappa_{\mathrm{om}}\chi_{\mathrm{om}}^{2}x^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{om}}\\
&\quad+\chi_{\mathrm{m}}\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{om}}}g\chi_{\mathrm{om}}\sqrt{\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}F,\end{split}

(7)

where
e^{i\phi}=(\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{om}}}{2}-i\omega)/(\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{om}}}{2}+i\omega)
. We have defined the susceptibilities for the optomechanical cavity and the mechanical oscillator, as

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathrm{om}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=[i\omega+\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{om}}}{2}]^{-1},

(8)

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathrm{m}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\omega_{\mathrm{m}}[(\omega^{2}-\omega^{2}_{\mathrm{m}})-i\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}\omega]^{-1}.

(9)

The mechanical oscillator is susceptible to the external force
F
, which contains the force signal
F_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{sig}}}
and thermal noise
F_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{th}}}
. By measuring with light, the force signal can be estimated via a phase measurement, which also introduces additional noise due to the radiation pressure. From the measured phase
p^{\mathrm{out}}_{\mathrm{om}}
in Eq. (
7
) we can give an unbiased estimator
\hat{F}
of the force
F
, as

\displaystyle\hat{F}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}\chi_{\mathrm{m}}\,g\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{om}}}\chi_{\mathrm{om}}}p_{\mathrm{om}}^{\mathrm{out}}=F+F_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{add}}},

(10)

where the additional force noise, added by the measurement light, is defined as

\displaystyle\begin{split}F_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{add}}}&=F_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{th}}}+\frac{e^{i\phi}}{\sqrt{\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}\chi_{\mathrm{m}}\,g\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{om}}}\chi_{\mathrm{om}}}p_{\mathrm{om}}^{\mathrm{in}}\\
&\quad-\frac{g\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{om}}}\chi_{\mathrm{om}}}{\sqrt{\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}}x_{\mathrm{om}}^{\mathrm{in}}.\end{split}

(11)

To characterize the sensitivity of the force measurement, we use the (power) spectral density of the added noise defined by

S_{F}(\omega)\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\Bigl\langle F_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{add}}}(\omega)F_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{add}}}(-\omega^{\prime})\Bigr\rangle+\mathrm{c}.\mathrm{c}.\right).

(12)

Assuming uncorrelated amplitude and phase quadratures, the added noise spectral density is

S_{F}=\frac{k_{B}T}{\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{m}}}+\frac{1}{2G_{\mathrm{om}}\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}\lvert\chi_{\mathrm{m}}\rvert^{2}}+\frac{G_{\mathrm{om}}}{2\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}},

(13)

where we defined the frequency dependent measurement strength as

\displaystyle\begin{split}G_{\mathrm{om}}(\omega)&=g^{2}\kappa_{\mathrm{om}}\lvert\chi_{\mathrm{om}}(\omega)\rvert^{2}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{om}}\;\frac{(\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{om}}}{2})^{2}}{\omega^{2}+(\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{om}}}{2})^{2}},\end{split}

(14)

with Lorentzian shape and maximum
\Gamma_{\mathrm{om}}=\frac{4\,g^{2}}{\kappa_{\mathrm{om}}}
. In this form, the noise spectral density (
13
) is dimensionless. To arrive at a force noise spectral density in units of
\mathrm{N}^{2}/\mathrm{Hz}
, one has to re-scale it, such that
S_{F}(\omega)=\hbar m\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}\omega_{\mathrm{m}}S_{F}(\omega)
for a given optomechanical force sensor
Wimmer et al. 2014
.
The terms in Eq. (
13
) are thermal noise due to Brownian motion of the mechanical oscillator (first term), shot noise in the phase quadrature (second term) and backaction noise from the amplitude quadrature (third term). The thermal noise adds a flat background to the force sensitivity, which is independent of the measurement rate
G_{\mathrm{om}}
and frequency.
Throughout this paper, we will assume that the thermal noise is either dominated by backaction noise
Wimmer et al. 2014
or is suppressed by cooling of the mechanical mode and therefore neglect this term.

The shot noise term scales inversely proportional to the measurement rate
G_{\mathrm{om}}
and thus inversely proportional to the power, as
G_{\mathrm{om}}\propto P
. Additionally, the backaction noise is proportional to
G_{\mathrm{om}}
. This implies that an optimal power value, which minimizes Eq. (
13
), exists for each frequency. Minimizing Eq. (
13
) with respect to
G_{\mathrm{om}}
for all frequencies gives the achievable lower bound

S_{F}(\omega)\geq\frac{1}{\gamma_{m}\lvert\chi_{\mathrm{m}}(\omega)\rvert}\equiv S_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{SQL}}}(\omega),

(15)

which is the standard quantum limit (SQL). The optimal measurement rate required is

G_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{SQL}}}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\lvert\chi_{\mathrm{m}}(\omega)\rvert}.

(16)

Next, we consider the NMO. On resonance,
\Delta_{\mathrm{c}}=0
, and for
g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{BS}}}=g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{DC}}}=\frac{1}{2}g_{\mathrm{a}}
the output quadratures read

\displaystyle\begin{split}x^{\mathrm{out}}_{\mathrm{c}}&=e^{i\theta}x^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{c}},\\
p^{\mathrm{out}}_{\mathrm{c}}&=e^{i\theta}p^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{c}}-\chi_{\mathrm{a}}g_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}\chi_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}x^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{c}}\\
&\quad+\chi_{\mathrm{a}}\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}}g_{\mathrm{a}}\chi_{\mathrm{c}}\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}}\left(\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}/2+i\omega}{\Delta_{\mathrm{a}}}x_{\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{in}}+p_{\mathrm{a}}^{\mathrm{in}}\right),\end{split}

(17)

where
e^{i\theta}=(\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}-i\omega)/(\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}+i\omega)
. We have defined susceptibilities of the meter cavity
\chi_{\mathrm{c}}
and the ancilla cavity
\chi_{\mathrm{a}}
as

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathrm{c}}

\displaystyle=[i\omega+\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}}{2}]^{-1},

(18)

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathrm{a}}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\mathrm{a}}[(\omega^{2}-\Delta_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}-\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}}{4})-i\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}\omega]^{-1}.

(19)

The aim of our setup is to couple the two systems in a manner that the backaction noise in the force spectrum will cancel, hence allowing for a sub-SQL performance. In our dual-cavity setup, this is done by cascading the two systems and matching the parameters of the NMO accordingly.

As seen in Fig.
1
(b), the whole scheme has two possible arrangements. Thus, to cascade the systems, we choose
x_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{out}}=x_{\mathrm{om}}^{\mathrm{in}}
and
p_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{out}}=p_{\mathrm{om}}^{\mathrm{in}}
for the case
\mathrm{NMO}\mapsto\mathrm{OMS}
and
x_{\mathrm{om}}^{\mathrm{out}}=x_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{in}}
and
p_{\mathrm{om}}^{\mathrm{out}}=p_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{in}}
for
\mathrm{OMS}\mapsto\mathrm{NMO}
. For ideal CQNC, the order will not matter. We will discuss cases that depend on the order further below. After cascading the two systems, we can again identify the additional force noise as in Eq. (
10
) and derive the added noise spectral density

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{F}&=\frac{1}{2G_{\mathrm{om}}\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}|\chi_{\mathrm{m}}|^{2}}\\
&\quad+\frac{G_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}\lvert\chi_{\mathrm{a}}\rvert^{2}+G_{\mathrm{om}}^{2}\lvert\chi_{\mathrm{m}}\rvert^{2}+2\,G_{\mathrm{a}}G_{\mathrm{om}}\Re(\chi_{\mathrm{m}}\chi_{\mathrm{a}}^{*})}{2G_{\mathrm{om}}\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}|\chi_{\mathrm{m}}|^{2}}\\
&\quad+\frac{G_{\mathrm{a}}\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}|\chi_{\mathrm{a}}|^{2}}{2G_{\mathrm{om}}\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}|\chi_{\mathrm{m}}|^{2}}\left(\frac{\omega^{2}+\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}/4}{\Delta_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}}+1\right),\end{split}

(20)

see Appendix
B
for details.
Analogous to the measurement strength of the OMS in Eq. (
14
), we have defined the frequency dependent measurement strength of the NMO,

\displaystyle\begin{split}G_{\mathrm{a}}(\omega)&=g_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}\lvert\chi_{\mathrm{c}}(\omega)\rvert^{2}=\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}\;\frac{(\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}}{2})^{2}}{\omega^{2}+(\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}}{2})^{2}},\end{split}

(21)

with
\Gamma_{\mathrm{a}}=\frac{4\,g_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}}{\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}}
.
The terms in Eq. (
20
) are shot-noise (first term), backaction noise (second term) and shot noise from the ancilla cavity (last term). The backaction noise is then cancelled if the conditions are such that

\displaystyle g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{BS}}}

\displaystyle=g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{DC}}}=\frac{1}{2}g_{\mathrm{a}},

(22)

\displaystyle G_{\mathrm{om}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=G_{\mathrm{a}}(\omega),

(23)

\displaystyle\chi_{\mathrm{m}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=-\chi_{\mathrm{a}}(\omega),

(24)

for all
\omega
. This means that the ancilla cavity should couple to the light with the same strength as the PMO, but the response to the force signal should be opposite to the PMO, hence it behaves as an effective negative mass. Considering the explicit form of Eqs. (
9
) and (
19
), condition (
24
) entails further restrictions:

1.

The detuning of the ancilla cavity to the meter cavity is

\Delta_{\mathrm{a}}=-\omega_{\mathrm{m}},

(24a)

which effectively moves the ancilla cavity to the negative mass frame.

2.

The linewidth of the ancilla cavity
\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}
should match the damping rate of the mechanical oscillator

\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}=\gamma_{\mathrm{m}},

(24b)

to mimic the oscillating behaviour of the PMO.

3.

The susceptibilities
\chi_{\mathrm{m}}
and
\chi_{\mathrm{a}}
differ by a factor
\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}/4
. To alleviate this, the detuning
\lvert\Delta_{\mathrm{a}}\rvert\gg\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}
, and together with forgoing points this implies the resolved sideband limit of the ancilla cavity

\omega_{\mathrm{m}}\gg\kappa_{\mathrm{a}},

(24c)

and a large quality factor of the mechanical oscillator,

Q_{\mathrm{m}}=\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{m}}}{\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}\gg 1.

(24d)

These conditions are similar to the integrated setup
Wimmer et al. 2014
, but instead of the coupling strength
g_{\mathrm{a}}
and
g
, the measurement strengths
G_{\mathrm{a}}
and
G_{\mathrm{om}}
need to match.

Assuming conditions (
22
)–(
24
) are met, the backaction term in Eq. (
20
) will vanish, and we arrive at

S_{F}=\frac{1}{2G_{\mathrm{om}}\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}|\chi_{\mathrm{m}}|^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\omega^{2}+\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}^{2}/4}{\omega_{\mathrm{m}}^{2}}+1\right),

(25)

which contains only shot-noise contributions of the measured phase quadrature and the ancilla cavity. The contribution of the OMS is sometimes referred to as the fundamental quantum limit (FQL)
Danilishin et al. 2019
, energetic quantum limit
Braginsky et al. 2001
or the quantum Cramér-Rao bound
Tsang et al. 2011
;
Miao et al. 2017
;
Khalili and Zeuthen 2021
. In the limit of large measurement strength, we arrive at the lower bound

\displaystyle S_{F}(\omega)\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\omega^{2}+\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}^{2}/4}{\omega_{\mathrm{m}}^{2}}+1\right)\equiv S_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{CQNC}}}(\omega).

(26)

Combining Eqs. (
15
) and (
26
) we find

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{CQNC}}}=S_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{SQL}}}\times\frac{1}{2Q_{\mathrm{m}}}\left(\frac{\omega^{2}+\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}^{2}/4+\omega_{\mathrm{m}}^{2}}{\sqrt{(\omega^{2}-\omega_{\mathrm{m}}^{2})^{2}+\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}^{2}\omega^{2}}}\right).

(27)

Thus, for
Q_{\mathrm{m}}\gg 1
, we can summarize

\displaystyle S_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{CQNC}}}=S_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{SQL}}}\times\begin{cases}\quad 1&\text{on resonance }\omega=\omega_{\mathrm{m}}\\
1/(2Q_{\mathrm{m}})&\text{off resonance }\omega\,\begin{subarray}{c}\ll\\
\gg\end{subarray}\,\omega_{\mathrm{m}}.\end{cases}

(28)

In conclusion, under the additional condition that
G_{\mathrm{om}}=G_{\mathrm{a}}
, the cascaded setup reproduces the same findings of
Wimmer et al. 2014
, leading to an enhancement in performance up to a factor of
2Q_{\mathrm{m}}
off-resonance and SQL performance on resonance.

IV.1
Order-independent imperfections

The parameters discussed in this subsection will impede the cancellation of backaction noise and, as a result, limit the CQNC performance but will not affect the force signal. Hence, the possible CQNC performance in the face of these imperfections will not depend on the system order.

IV.1.1
Non-ideal ancilla cavity linewidth
\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}\neq\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}

The strictest requirement for an all-optical CQNC setup is to match the ancilla cavity linewidth to the damping rate of the mechanical oscillator. Assuming all conditions for ideal CQNC are matched, except
\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}\neq\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}
. Since the measurement strengths,
G_{\mathrm{om}}=G_{\mathrm{a}}
are matched for all frequencies, and we assume no propagation losses between the system, this effectively reduces to the integrated CQNC setup

\displaystyle\begin{split}S_{F}&=\frac{1}{2G_{\mathrm{om}}\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}|\chi_{\mathrm{m}}|^{2}}+\frac{G_{\mathrm{om}}}{2\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}}\left\lvert\frac{\chi_{\mathrm{m}}+\chi_{\mathrm{a}}}{\chi_{m}}\right\rvert^{2}\\

(29)

For an optimal
G_{\mathrm{om}}

\displaystyle S_{F}=\frac{\lvert\chi_{\mathrm{m}}+\chi_{\mathrm{a}}\rvert}{\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}\lvert\chi_{\mathrm{m}}\rvert}+\frac{\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}\lvert\chi_{\mathrm{a}}\rvert^{2}}{2\gamma_{\mathrm{m}}\lvert\chi_{\mathrm{m}}\rvert^{2}}\left(\frac{\omega^{2}+\kappa_{\mathrm{a}}^{2}/4}{\omega_{\mathrm{m}}^{2}}+1\right)

IV.2
Order-dependent imperfections

The parameters discussed in this subsection, namely losses, not only hamper the cancellation of backaction noise but also affect the force signal directly.

IV.2.1
Losses

We first consider propagation losses, which occur between the first and the second system. The propagation losses are modelled via mixing the output signal of the first system with vacuum in a beam-splitter-like interaction. This leads to a modified output signal
Bowen and Milburn 2020
,

\displaystyle x_{\mathrm{out}}^{\prime}=\sqrt{\eta}\,x_{\mathrm{out}}+\sqrt{1-\eta}\,x_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{vac}}},

(37)

where
x_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{vac}}}
represents the vacuum field and
\eta\in[0,1]
is the efficiency of the process. Due to this additional noise, information about the backaction interaction of the first system is lost to the vacuum, hence perfect cancellation of backaction noise is not possible. As before, we can find an optimal coupling strength to minimize the additional noise. For the system order
\mathrm{NMO}\mapsto\mathrm{OMS}
, we achieve a minimal spectral density off-resonance

\displaystyle S_{F}=\sqrt{1-\eta}\times S_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{SQL}}}.

(38)

In the opposite order
\mathrm{OMS}\mapsto\mathrm{NMO}
, additionally to the loss of backaction information, some force signal will be lost due to propagation losses. Hence, the added noise will increase for this topology. We find

\displaystyle S_{F}=\frac{\sqrt{1-\eta}}{\eta}\times S_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{SQL}}},

(39)

for the minimal spectral density off-resonance. The spectral density is increased by
1/\eta
compared to the case
\mathrm{NMO}\mapsto\mathrm{OMS}
and hence directly proportional to the lost force signal.
Losses after the second system constitute the detection efficiency and can be modelled similarly. Since this will not affect the cancellation of backaction noise, we will omit detection losses for now.

Apart from propagation losses, we take intracavity losses into account. Introducing a Markovian bath for each cavity, with coupling rates
\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{bath}}}
and
\kappa_{\mathrm{om}}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{bath}}}
, the intracavity losses can be described in terms of the escape efficiencies

\displaystyle\eta_{\mathrm{om},\mathrm{c}}^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{esc}}}=\frac{\kappa^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{om},\mathrm{c}}}{\kappa^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{om},\mathrm{c}}+\kappa^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{bath}}}_{\mathrm{om},\mathrm{c}}}=\frac{\kappa^{\mathrm{in}}_{\mathrm{om},\mathrm{c}}}{\kappa_{\mathrm{om},\mathrm{c}}}.

(40)

Similar to propagation losses, introducing intracavity losses will always impede the cancellation of backaction noise, and depending on the order of the systems, the available force signal information will differ. For the case
\mathrm{NMO}\mapsto\mathrm{OMS}
, with optimal measurement strength, we find the minimal spectral density

\displaystyle S_{F}=\frac{\sqrt{\eta^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{esc}}}_{c}+\eta^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{esc}}}_{\mathrm{om}}-2\eta^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{esc}}}_{c}\eta^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{esc}}}_{\mathrm{om}}}}{\eta^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{esc}}}_{\mathrm{om}}}\times S_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{SQL}}}.

(41)

This encompasses both cases with propagation loss, for
\eta^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{esc}}}_{\mathrm{om}}\to 1
we retrieve Eq. (
38
) and for
\eta^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{esc}}}_{\mathrm{c}}\to 1
Eq. (
39
) respectively. Thus, for the configuration
\mathrm{NMO}\mapsto\mathrm{OMS}
, the intracavity loss can be handled similarly to propagation loss.

For the case
\mathrm{OMS}\mapsto\mathrm{NMO}
, we lose additional force signal due to intrinsic loss in the meter cavity. Moreover, the signal also picks up additional information of the phase quadrature. We arrive at the minimal spectral density,

\displaystyle S_{F}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\eta^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{esc}}}_{\mathrm{c}}+\eta^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{esc}}}_{\mathrm{om}}-2\eta^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{esc}}}_{\mathrm{c}}\eta^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{esc}}}_{\mathrm{om}}}}{\eta^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{esc}}}_{\mathrm{om}}\,|1-\eta^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{esc}}}_{\mathrm{c}}\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}\chi_{\mathrm{c}}|^{2}}\times S_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{SQL}}}.

(42)

The term
|1-\eta^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{esc}}}_{\mathrm{c}}\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}\chi_{\mathrm{c}}|^{2}
describes the meter cavity’s phase and noise contribution. Due to its dependence on the meter cavity susceptibility
\chi_{\mathrm{c}}
, this difference is frequency-dependent and will vanish for frequencies
\omega>\kappa_{c}
. For low frequencies, it will be at a maximal value of
|1-2\eta^{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{esc}}}_{\mathrm{c}}|^{2}
, making intracavity losses extra punishing for configuration
\mathrm{OMS}\mapsto\mathrm{NMO}
.
We see that introducing losses is detrimental to the possible noise reduction. As losses will never be avoidable, the system order
\mathrm{NMO}\mapsto\mathrm{OMS}
should always be preferable since higher levels of noise reduction are achieved.

g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{BS}}}

g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{DC}}}

g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{BS}}}=g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{DC}}}=1/2\,g_{\mathrm{a}}

g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{BS}}}+g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{DC}}}=g

\mathrm{NMO}\mapsto\mathrm{OMS}

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we discussed a cascaded version of the all-optical coherent quantum noise cancellation setup proposed by Tsang and Caves
Tsang and Caves 2010
;
Wimmer et al. 2014
. Instead of introducing the anti-noise path directly into the optomechanical cavity, we considered an all-optical effective negative mass oscillator as a standalone system and removed the backaction noise of the positive mass oscillator by coupling both systems coherently via a strong drive field. Under the conditions (
22
)–(
24
), we then rediscovered the perfect cancellation of backaction noise. Afterwards, we discussed deviations from the ideal conditions, including losses and the influence of the system order. We saw that for mismatched measurement strengths, by choosing the cavity linewidth and coupling strength in a specific way, CQNC can be recovered in the high- or low-frequency regime, or even at a specific frequency
\omega_{*}\neq\omega_{\mathrm{m}}
. For losses and a relative mismatch of beam-splitter and down-conversion coupling, the system order will also affect the noise cancellation performance. Finally, we discussed the performance of our setup for a set of realistic parameters and showed that a quantum noise reduction of 20% below the SQL is possible for the order
\mathrm{NMO}\mapsto\mathrm{OMS}
in the low-frequency regime.

Appendix A
Details on the Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{NMO}}}

Here, we give some more details on the Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{NMO}}}
in Eq. (
3
). The effective negative mass oscillator consists of two optical modes with different frequencies
\omega_{\mathrm{c}}
and
\omega_{\mathrm{a}}
, coupled by a beam-splitter and down-conversion process. The whole Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{NMO}}}=H_{0}+H_{\mathrm{drive}}+H_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{DC}}}+H_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{BS}}},

(45)

where the first term represents the free Hamiltonian of the optical modes,

\displaystyle H_{0}=\omega_{\mathrm{c}}c^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{c}}c_{\mathrm{c}}+\omega_{\mathrm{a}}a^{\dagger}a.

(46)

The second term describes the laser drive, which drives the meter cavity mode
c_{\mathrm{c}}
, and is given by

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{drive}}=iE(e^{-i\omega_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}t}c^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{c}}-e^{i\omega_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}t}c_{\mathrm{c}}).

(47)

Here,
\omega_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}
denotes the laser frequency and
E
describes the laser field amplitude, which is given by
\lvert E\rvert=\sqrt{\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}P/\hbar\omega_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{L}}}}
, with the laser power
P
and cavity linewidth
\kappa_{\mathrm{c}}
.
The third term describes a two-mode squeezing process. In this, a pump field impinges on a non-linear crystal, and a pump photon of frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{P}}
is converted into two photons of lower frequency. In the usual treatment of such processes, the pump is assumed to be a strong coherent field. In a rotating frame with respect to the pump frequency and linearized pump field
Ou et al. 1992
, the Hamiltonian is then

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{DC}}}=g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{DC}}}(ac_{\mathrm{c}}+a^{\dagger}c^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{c}}).

(48)

The coupling strength of the down-conversion process is given by
g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{DC}}}=\Gamma l\frac{c}{L}
, where
l
is the length of the crystal,
L
is the cavity length,
c
the speed of light and
\Gamma
the gain parameter. We refer to
Wimmer et al. 2014
;
Byer and Herbst 1977
for more details on the gain parameter.
The last term describes the beam-splitter interaction. It is given by

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{BS}}}=g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{BS}}}(a^{\dagger}c_{\mathrm{c}}+c^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{c}}a),

(49)

where
g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{BS}}}
denotes the coupling strength of this process. For a generic beam-splitter, the strength is defined by
g_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{BS}}}=rc/L
with
r
the reflectivity of the beam-splitter. Alternatively, for a setup considered in Fig.
1
(a), where the two modes are not spatially separated, the beam-splitter interaction can be achieved with a wave plate
Steinmeyer 2019
. The strength is then given by

Appendix B
Calculation of noise spectral densities

We consider a general linear quantum system consisting of
n
system variables,
k
in- and outputs and
m
bath variables. The input-output relations can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{out}}=K_{\mathrm{in}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{in}}

(51)

with a vector
\mathbf{x}
containing the
n
system variables, and
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{in}}
and
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{out}}
vectors for the
k
inputs and outputs. The whole system is governed by the equations of motion

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=M_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{sys}}}\mathbf{x}(t)+K_{\mathrm{in}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{in}}(t)+K_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{bath}}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{bath}}}(t),

(52)

where we have introduced the system matrix
M_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{sys}}}
and input matrices
K_{\mathrm{in}}
and
K_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{bath}}}
for the input and bath quadratures. The equations of motion (
52
) can be solved in the Fourier domain, where
\dot{\mathbf{x}}(t)=i\omega\mathbf{x}(\omega)
. It follows,

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}=(i\omega\mathbb{1}-M_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{sys}}})^{-1}\,(K_{\mathrm{in}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{in}}+K_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{bath}}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{bath}}}).

(53)

Together with Eq. (
51
), we derive the output quadratures as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{out}}&=K_{\mathrm{in}}^{\top}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{in}}\\
&=\left(K_{\mathrm{in}}^{\top}(i\omega\mathbb{1}-M_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{sys}}})^{-1}K_{\mathrm{in}}-\mathbb{1}\right)\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{in}}\\
&\quad+K_{\mathrm{in}}^{\top}(i\omega\mathbb{1}-M_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{sys}}})^{-1}K_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{bath}}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{bath}}}\\
&=T_{\mathrm{in}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{in}}+T_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{bath}}}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{bath}}}\\
&=\mathcal{T}\,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{in}},\end{split}

(54)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{in}}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{in}}\\
\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{bath}}}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr),

(55)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle=\left(T_{\mathrm{in}},T_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{bath}}}\right).

(56)

From this, we can calculate the (symmetrized) spectral density matrix as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})S_{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)&=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{out}}(\omega)\mathbf{x}^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{out}}(\omega^{\prime})\rangle+\mathrm{c}.\mathrm{c}.\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{T}(\omega)\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{in}}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\dagger}_{\mathrm{in}}\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}(-\omega^{\prime})\rangle+\mathrm{c}.\mathrm{c}.\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathcal{T}(\omega)S_{\mathrm{in}}\mathcal{T}^{\dagger}(-\omega^{\prime})\rangle+\mathrm{c}.\mathrm{c}.,\end{split}

(57)

with
S_{\mathrm{in}}
the input spectral density matrix.
Every sub-system in our setup has four system variables. Hence the system matrices
M_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{sys}}}
and bath input matrices
K_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{bath}}}
are all
4\times 4
-dimensional. The in- and output variables are the two quadratures of the laser light, making the input matrices
K_{\mathrm{in}}

4\times 2
-dimensional.

\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{\scriptscriptstyle{vac}}}\end{pmatrix}.
