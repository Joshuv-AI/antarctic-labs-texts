An optimization algorithm for nonsmooth nonconvex problems with upper- C 2 C^{2} objective Thanks: Submitted to the editors.
Jingyi Wang, Cosmin G. Petra

Abstract
An optimization algorithm for nonsmooth nonconvex constrained optimization problems with upper- C 2 C^{2} objective functions is proposed and analyzed. Upper- C 2 C^{2} is a weakly concave property that exists in difference of convex (DC) functions and arises naturally in many applications, particularly certain classes of solutions to parametric optimization problems [ 34 , 4 ] , e.g. , recourse of stochastic programming [ 36 ] and projection into closed sets [ 34 ] . The algorithm can be viewed as a bundle method specialized for upper- C 2 C^{2} problems and is globally convergent with bounded algorithm parameters. Compared to conventional bundle methods, the proposed method is both simpler and computationally more efficient. The algorithm handles general smooth constraints similarly to sequential quadratic programming (SQP) methods and uses a line search to ensure progress. The potential inconsistencies from the linearization of the constraints are addressed through a penalty method. The capabilities of the algorithm are demonstrated by solving both simple upper- C 2 C^{2} problems and real-world optimal power flow problems used in current power grid industry practices.

Introduction

In this paper, we consider the class of nonsmooth nonconvex optimization problems in the form of

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}x\end{subarray}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle f(x)+r(x)

(1)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle c(x)=0,\ \ \ d(x)\geq 0,

where the objective function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, and constraints
c:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{c}}
,
d:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{d}}
are continuously differentiable.
The function
r:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz continuous and
upper-
C^{2}
(see monograph
[
34
]
and Section
2
),
a property that is rarely exploited individually by numerical algorithms but, nevertheless, exists in many optimization problems
as we elaborate later in this section.

Nonsmooth optimization has been researched extensively for decades.
Most successful methods include subgradient methods
[
37
]
, bundle methods, and DC algorithms
[
1
]
.
Bundle methods are widely regarded as
one of the most efficient algorithms to address discontinuous first-order derivatives
[
26
,
19
]
.
The method develops an approximation model for the objective with the information from previous iterations,
referred to as a bundle, and solves optimization subproblems with it
[
24
,
18
]
.
Many bundle algorithms rely on the quadratic coefficient in the approximation model to avoid line searches
[
15
,
29
]
.
The solution to the subproblem is called a trial point/step, which through a rejection criterion is either taken or rejected, but included in the bundle to improve the trial point for the next iteration.
A trial point is called a serious point if it is accepted.
The approximation typically consists of piecewise-linear tangent planes at the bundle points
and can be adjusted to maintain positive linearization error that is lacking due to the nonconvexity of the objective.
A commonly used adjustment, the so-called down-shift mechanism, is introduced in
[
24
]
and investigated in
[
22
,
20
,
35
,
45
]
. Convergence analysis using this mechanism can be found in
[
2
,
28
]
.
Given better local convexity properties,
e.g.
, lower-
C^{2}
, the slope of the tangent planes can be tilted to generate locally convexified models
[
15
]
. The so-called redistributed bundle methods are shown to work in practice under less ideal conditions
[
14
]
.
Constrained nonsmooth nonconvex optimization adds another layer of complexity for bundle methods. Constraints that form a convex feasible set,
e.g.
, affine constraints, can be maintained as-is in the subproblems and convergence analysis would stand valid
[
14
,
13
]
.
For a nonconvex
c(\cdot)
, smooth or not, bundle methods typically incorporate it into the objective through penalty or filter methods so that the approximation is constructed for the new objective.

Outside of bundle methods,
[
10
]
proposed a sequential quadratic programming (SQP) method using gradient sampling for inequality-constrained optimization.
The global convergence result of the algorithm shows that accumulation points are stationary points of the exact penalty function, which can be reduced to the constraint violation depending on the penalty parameter.
A more efficient BFGS-SQP is proposed in
[
9
]
which shows promising convergence behaviors without requiring the existence of Hessian.
In
[
43
]
, a smoothing function of the objective that satisfies gradient consistency property and augmented Lagrangian constraint relaxation are used to solve the problem. Alternating direction method of
multipliers (ADMM) has also been applied to nonsmooth nonconvex problems
[
16
]
. In
[
41
]
the convergence analysis requires the objective to be locally prox-regular with affine constraints.
DC functions include both lower-
C^{2}
and upper-
C^{2}
functions, though the latter is seldom studied separately. For an overview of DC functions and the celebrated DC algorithms (DCA), we refer to
[
1
,
8
]
. In
[
23
]
, the recourse function of linearly bi-parameterized two-stage problems with quadratic recourse is shown to have convexity-concavity property. The authors proposed an iterative algorithm with a quadratic convex subproblem that converges subsequently to generalized critical points.

Upper-
C^{2}
functions exist in many optimization applications and can be viewed as DC functions.
Any finite, concave function is upper-
C^{2}

[
34
, Theorem 10.33]
, as are continuously differentiable functions
[
34
, Proposition 13.34]
.
Moreover, a squared distance function to a closed set, which is
the solution function of a minimization problem, is upper-
C^{2}

[
34
, Example 10.57]
.
The property extends to a large number of two-stage (stochastic) programming problems with recourse
[
36
,
3
,
17
]
,
whose objective includes an upper-
C^{2}
recourse function of the second-stage problem.
The application that motivated us to look into such concavity-related property is
the distributed security-constrained alternating current optimal power flow (SCACOPF) problems
[
6
,
33
,
32
,
31
,
30
]
, which can be formed as a two-stage recourse problem.
In this case, the nonsmooth part of the first-stage objective
r(\cdot)
becomes upper-
C^{2}
through regularization of the second-stage problems, if it is not already so
[
40
]
.
We point out that upper-
C^{2}
does not guarantee differentiability, lower-
C^{1}
or lower regularity (see Section
2
).

The existing algorithms unfortunately do not fully address the challenges in solving (
1
).
Without the explicit form of
r(\cdot)
, potential algorithm needs to rely on information at known points to
construct its approximation. Further, the cost of evaluating
r(\cdot)
and its subgradient could be substantial,
which incentivizes the algorithm to avoid line search on the objective. Bundle methods are good algorithm candidates.
However, in order to achieve convergence, existing bundle methods either rely on additional properties of
r(\cdot)
,
e.g.
, lower-
C^{2}

[
15
,
14
,
44
]
or allow the approximation model coefficient to be unbounded
[
28
,
29
,
12
]
. Neither is ideal for
r(\cdot)
that do not enjoy such properties.
The complex selection criteria of the bundle points and the cost of incorporating them in the
subproblem could also be a serious drawback. DCA, which in essence uses a linear function to approximate the concave function, bear some similarities to the proposed algorithm. The existence of both equality and inequality constraints makes the exact penalty method used in some DCAs to address constraints challenging. Given the absence of the convex part of the DC function, a flexible and robust quadratic term would help guarantee convergence and control the step size.
To provide theoretically sound and computationally efficient algorithms for (
1
),
we propose a simplified bundle method that capitalizes on the upper-
C^{2}
objective and the continuously differentiable, nonconvex constraints.
The proposed algorithms has been parallelized efficiently for SCACOPF problems
[
39
]
.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we describe the upper-type properties of
r(\cdot)
and mathematical notations.
In Section
3
, our algorithm is proposed and its global convergence analysis
is provided under assumptions drawn from Section
2
.
We discuss how the parameters of the algorithm are updated for the approximation models used in subproblems.
An algorithm to address possible inconsistency arising from the linearized constraints in the subproblems is then presented,
together with its convergence analysis.
Numerical experiments are shown in Section
4
that illustrate both the
theoretical and practical capabilities of the proposed algorithm.

2
Background and notations

In this section, we provide the concepts and notations necessary for the analysis in this paper.
The lower regular subdifferential of a function
r:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
at point
\bar{x}
,
denoted as
\hat{\partial}r(\bar{x})
, is defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\partial}r(\bar{x}):=\left\{g\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\to\bar{x}\\
x\neq\bar{x}\end{subarray}}\frac{r(x)-r(\bar{x})-\langle g,x-\bar{x}\rangle}{\left\lVert{x-\bar{x}}\right\rVert}\geq 0\right\},

(2)

where
\left\lVert{\cdot}\right\rVert
is the 2-norm and
\langle\cdot\rangle
is the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
If there exists a sequence
\{x^{\nu}\}
such that
x^{\nu}\xrightarrow[r]{}\bar{x}
and
g^{\nu}\in\hat{\partial}r(x^{\nu})
with
g^{\nu}\to\bar{g}
, then

\bar{g}
is a lower general subgradient of
r(\bar{x})
, where the f-attentive convergence
x^{\nu}\xrightarrow[r]{}\bar{x}
is trivial for
x^{\nu}\to\bar{x}
when
r
is Lipschitz.
The lower general subdifferential at
\bar{x}
is denoted as
\partial r(\bar{x})
.

A Lipschitz function
r
is lower regular (or subdifferentially regular) if and only if
\partial r(\bar{x})=\hat{\partial}r(\bar{x})

[
34
, Corollary 8.11]
.
Lower general subgradient is often simply called general subgradient,
and a lower regular function is called regular.
On the other hand,
upper regular subdifferential
[
34
,
25
]
is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{\partial}^{+}r(\bar{x}):=

\displaystyle-\hat{\partial}(-r)(\bar{x})=\left\{g\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\to\bar{x}\\
x\neq\bar{x}\end{subarray}}\frac{r(x)-r(\bar{x})-\langle g,x-\bar{x}\rangle}{\left\lVert{x-\bar{x}}\right\rVert}\leq 0\right\}.

(3)

Similarly, the upper general subdifferential is given by
\partial^{+}r(\bar{x}):=-\partial(-r)(\bar{x})
.
A function
r
is called upper regular if
-r
is lower regular.
Examples of upper regular functions include all continuous concave and continuously differentiable functions.

In nonsmooth nonconvex optimization literature, both Clarke subdifferential
[
7
]
, denoted as
\bar{\partial}r{(\bar{x})}
of
r
at
\bar{x}
, and
\partial r(\bar{x})
have been widely adopted.
If
r
is lower regular, given the convexity of
\hat{\partial}r(\bar{x})
,
\partial r(\bar{x})=\bar{\partial}r{(\bar{x})}

[
34
, Theorem 8.6, 8.49, 9.61]
.
This equivalence holds for an upper regular
r
as well (for proof see
[
40
]
) and allows us to use its Clarke subgradient and upper general subgradient interchangeably.
An important property of these subdifferential is the outer/upper-semicontinuity, necessary in establishing convergence
[
34
, Proposition 6.6]
.
In addition, for a Lipschitz
r
,
\bar{\partial}r(\bar{x})
is locally bounded
[
34
, Theorem 9.13]
.

A more restrictive but useful assumption than regularity is lower-
C^{k}
introduced in
[
38
,
34
]
,
with equivalent definitions in
[
11
]
.
A function
r:O\to\mathbb{R}
, where
O\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open, is said to be lower-
C^{k}
on
O
, if on some neighborhood
V
of each
\bar{x}\in O
there is a representation

\displaystyle r(x)=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in T\end{subarray}}{\text{max}}\ r_{t}(x),

(4)

where
r_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is of class
C^{k}
on
V
and the index set
T
is a compact space such that
r_{t}

and all of its partial derivatives through order
k
are jointly continuous on
(t,x)\in T\times V
.
A function is called upper-
C^{k}
if we replace the
max
with
min
in (
4
).
Let
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}
be compact, the function
r
is upper-
C^{2}
if it can be expressed as

\displaystyle r(x)=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in T\end{subarray}}{\text{min}}\ p(t,x)

(5)

for all
x\in O
, such that
p:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
and its first- and second-order partial derivatives in
x

depend continuously on
(t,x)
. Clearly, upper-/lower-
C^{k}
imply upper-/lower-regularity.
To expand on the upper-
C^{2}
examples given in Section
1
,
immediately from its definition (
5
), two-stage (stochastic) optimization problems that are coupled only in a smooth objective
have upper-
C^{2}
objective in the first-stage problem, regardless of how complex the feasible set for second-stage problem is. Many constraint-coupled second-stage problems can be relaxed to obtain an upper-
C^{2}
solution function, using for example, quadratic penalty regularization
[
40
]
.

Further, lower- and upper-type properties have equivalent definitions based on function and subgradient values that are more useful in analysis. Specifically, the finite-valued function
r
is lower-
C^{2}
on
O\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if there exists
\rho>0
such that

r(\cdot)+\frac{1}{2}\rho\left\lVert{\cdot}\right\rVert^{2}
is convex. Notice that this definition is already given on
O
with a uniform
\rho
. Through simple arithmetic and convexity with subgradients, for all
x\in O

\displaystyle r(x)-r(\bar{x})-\langle g,x-\bar{x}\rangle+\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert{x-\bar{x}}\right\rVert^{2}\geq 0,

(6)

for
g\in\bar{\partial}r(\bar{x})
.
By the symmetry property of Clarke subgradient
[
7
, Proposition 2.3.1]
, if
g\in\bar{\partial}r(\bar{x})
,
then
-g\in\bar{\partial}(-r)(\bar{x})
. Hence, if
r
is upper-
C^{2}
, by definition
-r
is lower-
C^{2}
,
and we have

\displaystyle r(x)-r(\bar{x})-\langle g,x-\bar{x}\rangle\leq\frac{\rho}{2}\left\lVert{x-\bar{x}}\right\rVert^{2},

(7)

where
g\in\bar{\partial}r(\bar{x})
.
We refer to (
7
) as the upper-
C^{2}
inequality.
Another common assumption for nonsmooth nonconvex objective is prox-regularity
[
41
]
. Given a Lipschitz
r
on an open set
O\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, if
r
is lower-
C^{2}
, it is prox-regular.

Next, we assume that the feasible set for
x
is bounded, in line with applications such as power-grid optimization
[
30
]
. To reflect this
assumption directly in problem formulation and to simplify notations, without loss of generality, (
1
) is recast as

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}x\end{subarray}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle r(x)

(8)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle c(x)=0,

\displaystyle 0\leq x\leq x_{u},

where
x_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
The dimension of the constraint is
m
,
i.e.
,
c(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

There are multiple optimality conditions in nonsmooth nonconvex analysis,
e.g.
,
stationary point, Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) point, Fritz-John critical point.
In this paper, we assume calmness for problem (
8
)
[
7
, Section 6.4]
at its local minimum
\bar{x}
.
Calmness can be viewed as a weak constraint qualification.
In particular, the widely adopted linear
independence constraint qualification (LICQ) ensures calmness. Calmness effectively validates the use of a KKT point for Clarke necessary optimality condition
[
7
]
, where the Lagrange multiplier for the objective function is nonzero in the Fritz-John critical point equation. Hence, for problem (
8
), a necessary first-order optimality condition at a local minimum
\bar{x}
is that there exists
\bar{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

\bar{\zeta}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\geq 0
and
\bar{\zeta_{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle 0\in\bar{\partial}r(\bar{x})+\nabla c(\bar{x})\bar{\lambda}-\bar{\zeta}_{l}+\bar{\zeta}_{u}

\displaystyle,

(9)

\displaystyle\bar{Z}_{u}(\bar{x}-x_{u})=0,\bar{Z}_{l}\bar{x}=0

\displaystyle,

\displaystyle c_{j}(\bar{x})=0,j=1,\dots,m

\displaystyle,

\displaystyle\bar{\lambda}_{j}c_{j}(\bar{x})=0,j=1,\dots,m

\displaystyle,

\displaystyle\bar{\zeta}_{l},\bar{\zeta}_{u},x_{u}-\bar{x},\bar{x}\geq 0

\displaystyle.

The matrix
\nabla c(\bar{x})
is of dimension
n\times m
.
The matrices
\bar{Z}_{u},\bar{Z}_{l}
are diagonal matrices whose diagonal values are
\bar{\zeta}_{u}
and
\bar{\zeta}_{l}
, respectively.
A point that satisfies (
9
) is called a KKT point of (
8
).
For upper regular functions, it is possible to establish a stronger form
of subgradient optimality condition as explained in
[
25
]
.

3
Simplified bundle algorithm

Given the nonsmooth nonconvex objective
r(\cdot)
in (
8
), in this section we consider bundle methods, which have
proven to be one of the most successful methods in solving such
problems
[
21
]
.
The proposed algorithm simplifies the bundle while retain many of the algorithm’s features.
Motivated by the discussion in Section
2
, we make the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

The Lipschitz continuous objective
r(\cdot)
in (
8
) is upper-
C^{2}
.

In particular, inequality (
7
) is satisfied.
We point out that
[
28
,
12
]
have explored bundle methods and to our knowledge were the first to prove convergence for upper-
C^{1}

objective and constraints. However, in their case the parameters of the approximation model are not guaranteed to be finite, in addition to the aforementioned challenges in applying bundle method to constrained optimization.
We assume uniform boundedness on the Hessian of the constraints, a common assumption in literature
[
10
]
.

Assumption 2
.

The function
c(\cdot)
is twice differentiable and there exists a constant
H_{u}^{c}\geq 0
such that
\frac{1}{2}|x^{T}\nabla^{2}c_{j}(x)x|\leq H_{u}^{c}\left\lVert{x}\right\rVert^{2}
for all feasible
x
and all
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
.

Assumption
2
can be relaxed to
c(\cdot)
being continuously differentiable with Lipschitz continuous first-order derivative
[
34
, Chapter 9]
. We also call
c(\cdot)
smooth if Assumption
2
is satisfied.

3.1
Algorithm description

The simplified bundle algorithm is an iterative method with locally approximated objective at each iteration.
It bears similarity to SQP in the treatment of constraints and can be viewed as its extension.
Compared to conventional bundle methods, the local convex quadratic approximation
\phi_{k}(\cdot)
to
r(\cdot)

is dependent only on the current serious point instead of a bundle of points.
More specifically, at iteration
k
and its serious step
x_{k}
,
\phi_{k}(\cdot)
is

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(x)=r(x_{k})+g_{k}^{T}(x-x_{k})+\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{x-x_{k}}\right\rVert^{2},

(10)

where
g_{k}\in\bar{\partial}r(x_{k})
, and
\alpha_{k}>0
is a scalar quadratic coefficient.
Equivalently, denoting
d=x-x_{k}
,
\phi_{k}(x)
can be reformulated as
\Phi_{k}(d)
such that

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}(d)

\displaystyle=r_{k}+g_{k}^{T}d+\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert d\right\rVert^{2},

(11)

where
r_{k}=r(x_{k})
. The function
value and subgradient at
x_{k}
are exact,
i.e.
,
\Phi_{k}(0)=r_{k},\nabla\Phi_{k}(0)=g_{k}
.
Furthermore, the constraints in (
8
) are linearized. The subproblem to be solved
at iteration
k
is

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}d\end{subarray}}{\text{minimize}}

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}(d)

(12)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}

\displaystyle c(x_{k})+\nabla c(x_{k})^{T}d=0,\ \ \ d_{l}^{k}\leq d\leq d_{u}^{k},

where
d_{l}^{k}=-x_{k},d_{u}^{k}=x_{u}-x_{k}
.
As in SQP methods, it is possible that the linearized constraints
in (
12
) are infeasible.
We address this possibility in Section
3.3
.
In this section, we assume that the solution
d_{k}
to (
12
) can be computed.
To measure progress in both the objective and constraints,
a
l_{1}
merit function is adopted in the form of

\displaystyle\phi_{1\theta_{k}}(x)=r(x)+\theta_{k}\left\lVert{c(x)}\right\rVert_{1},

(13)

where
\left\lVert{\cdot}\right\rVert_{1}
is the 1-norm and
\theta_{k}>0
is a penalty parameter.
A line search step on the constraints is needed in order to ensure progress
in the merit function (
13
).
The predicted change on the objective is defined as

\displaystyle\delta_{k}=

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}(0)-\Phi_{k}(d_{k})=-g_{k}^{T}d_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}.

(14)

To measure whether the approximation model
\Phi_{k}(\cdot)
of the objective formed at
x_{k}
is still valid at the trial step
x_{k}+d_{k}
,
we define the ratio
\rho_{k}
as

\displaystyle\rho_{k}=\begin{cases}r(x_{k})-r(x_{k}+d_{k})-\eta_{l}^{+}\delta_{k},\ &\delta_{k}\geq 0,\\
r(x_{k})-r(x_{k}+d_{k})-\eta_{l}^{-}\delta_{k},\ &\delta_{k}<0,\end{cases}

(15)

where
0<\eta_{l}^{+}\leq 1
and
\eta_{l}^{-}\geq 1
are two constant parameters of the algorithm.
If
\rho_{k}>0
, the model is still valid and the algorithm proceeds to line search.
Otherwise, the trial step
x_{k}+d_{k}
is
rejected and the parameter
\alpha_{k}
is updated to find a different trial step. This process
draws inspiration from trust-region methods and is a key element in many bundle methods.

The value of
\delta_{k}
is not necessarily positive. Therefore, the
corresponding threshold
\eta_{l}^{+}
and
\eta_{l}^{-}
differ based on the sign of
\delta_{k}
.
In both cases, the actual change in the objective
r(x_{k})-r(x_{k}+d_{k})
is allowed to
be slightly worse than the predicted one.
Namely, if
\delta_{k}\geq 0
, then the actual decrease can be smaller than the predicted decrease
\delta_{k}
,
though a fraction
\eta_{l}^{+}
of
\delta_{k}
is required;
and when
\delta_{k}<0
,
the actual increase in objective value can be slightly larger than the predicted increase value
-\delta_{k}
,
the degree to which is governed by
\eta_{l}^{-}\geq 1
.

Let the line search step size be
\beta_{k}\in(0,1]
. Then, the serious step taken is given as

x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\beta_{k}d_{k}
.
By letting
\delta_{k}^{\beta}=\Phi_{k}(0)-\Phi_{k}(\beta_{k}d_{k})
, we have

\displaystyle\delta_{k}^{\beta}=

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}(0)-\Phi_{k}(\beta_{k}d_{k})=-\beta_{k}g_{k}^{T}d_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\beta_{k}^{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}.

(16)

The predicted and actual change in objective at
x_{k+1}
is measured by
\rho_{k}^{\beta}
defined by

\displaystyle\rho_{k}^{\beta}=\begin{cases}r(x_{k})-r(x_{k+1})-\eta_{\gamma}^{+}\delta_{k}^{\beta},\ &\delta_{k}^{\beta}\geq 0,\\
r(x_{k})-r(x_{k+1})-\eta_{\gamma}^{-}\delta_{k}^{\beta},\ &\delta_{k}^{\beta}<0.\end{cases}

(17)

The parameter
\eta_{\gamma}^{+}\in(0,1]
and
\eta_{\gamma}^{-}\geq 1
can have different values than
\eta_{l}^{+}
and
\eta_{l}^{-}
to increase
flexibility of the algorithm.
The first-order optimality conditions of subproblem (
12
) are

\displaystyle g_{k}+\alpha_{k}d_{k}-\nabla c(x_{k})\lambda^{k+1}-\zeta_{l}^{k+1}+\zeta_{u}^{k+1}=0,

(18)

\displaystyle Z_{u}^{k+1}(d_{k}-d_{u}^{k})=0,Z_{l}^{k+1}(d_{k}-d_{l}^{k})=0,

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}^{k+1}\left[c_{j}(x_{k})+\nabla c_{j}(x_{k})^{T}d_{k}\right]=0,j=1,\dots,m,

\displaystyle\zeta_{u}^{k+1},\zeta_{l}^{k+1},d_{k}-d_{l}^{k},d_{u}^{k}-d_{k}\geq 0,

\displaystyle c(x_{k})+\nabla c(x_{k})^{T}d_{k}=0,

where
\lambda^{k+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the Lagrange multiplier for constraints
c
and
\zeta_{u}^{k+1}
and
\zeta_{l}^{k+1}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\zeta_{u}^{k+1},\zeta_{l}^{k+1},x_{k}+d_{k},x_{u}-x_{k}-d_{k}

\displaystyle\geq 0.

The line search conditions are given as follows

\displaystyle\theta_{k}\left\lVert{c(x_{k})}\right\rVert_{1}+\beta_{k}(\lambda^{k+1})^{T}c(x_{k})

\displaystyle\geq\theta_{k}\left\lVert{c(x_{k+1})}\right\rVert_{1}-\eta_{\beta}\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}\left\lVert{d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2},

\displaystyle\geq\theta_{k}\left\lVert{c(x_{k+1})}\right\rVert_{1}-\eta_{\beta}\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}\left\lVert{d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2},

\displaystyle\theta_{k}\left\lVert{c(x_{k})}\right\rVert_{1}+\eta_{\gamma}^{-}\beta_{k}(\lambda^{k+1})^{T}c(x_{k})

\displaystyle\geq\theta_{k}\left\lVert{c(x_{k+1})}\right\rVert_{1}-\eta_{\beta}\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}\left\lVert{d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}.

\delta_{k}^{\beta}

For simplicity in implementation and analysis, we use the following alternative

\displaystyle\theta_{k}\left\lVert{c(x_{k})}\right\rVert_{1}-\eta_{\gamma}^{-}\beta_{k}\left|(\lambda^{k+1})^{T}c(x_{k})\right|

\displaystyle\geq\theta_{k}\left\lVert{c(x_{k+1})}\right\rVert_{1}-\eta_{\beta}\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}\left\lVert{d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}.

Lemma 3
.

Given Assumption
1
, Algorithm
1
produces a finite
number of rejected steps.
As a consequence, the quadratic coefficient
\alpha_{k}
is bounded above and stays constant for
k
large enough.

Proof.

From the upper-
C^{2}
inequality (
7
), we have

\displaystyle r(x_{k}+d)-r_{k}-g_{k}^{T}d\leq C\left\lVert{d}\right\rVert^{2},

(23)

for a fixed constant
C>0
on the bounded domain of
x
.
In the first part of the proof we show that if at some iteration
k
,
\alpha_{k}
satisfies

\displaystyle\alpha_{k}>2C,

(24)

then no rejected steps can occur in Algorithm
1
after iteration
k
, or equivalently,
\rho_{t}>0
and
\rho_{t}^{\beta}>0
for all iterations
t\geq k
.
The inequalities (
23
) and (
24
) imply

\displaystyle r_{k}-r(x_{k}+d_{k})\geq

\displaystyle-g_{k}^{T}d_{k}-C\left\lVert{d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}

(25)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle-g_{k}^{T}d_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\left\lVert{d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}=\Phi_{k}(0)-\Phi_{k}(d_{k}).

As in the definition (
15
) of
\rho_{k}
, we distinguish between two cases based on the sign of
\delta_{k}
.
If
\delta_{k}=\Phi_{k}(0)-\Phi_{k}(d_{k})\geq 0
, then since
0<\eta_{l}^{+}\leq 1
, (
25
) gives

\displaystyle r_{k}-r(x_{k}+d_{k})>

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}(0)-\Phi_{k}(d_{k})\geq\eta_{l}^{+}\left[\Phi_{k}(0)-\Phi_{k}(d_{k})\right].

(26)

If
\delta_{k}=\Phi_{k}(0)-\Phi_{k}(d_{k})<0
, given
\eta_{l}^{-}\geq 1
, we can also write based on (
25
) that

\displaystyle r_{k}-r(x_{k}+d_{k})>

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}(0)-\Phi_{k}(d_{k})\geq\eta_{l}^{-}\left[\Phi_{k}(0)-\Phi_{k}(d_{k})\right].

(27)

As a consequence, by definition (
15
) we conclude

\rho_{k}>0
. Similar inequalities hold for
x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\beta_{k}d_{k}
since one can write based on (
23
) that

\displaystyle r(x_{k})-r(x_{k+1})\geq

\displaystyle-\beta_{k}g_{k}^{T}d_{k}-C\beta_{k}^{2}\left\lVert{d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}>-\beta_{k}g_{k}^{T}d_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{k}\beta_{k}^{2}\left\lVert{d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}

(28)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Phi_{k}(0)-\Phi_{k}(\beta_{k}d_{k}).

Same reasoning that leads to (
26
) and (
27
) for
\eta_{\gamma}^{+}
and
\eta_{\gamma}^{-}
implies that
\rho_{k}^{\beta}>0
. Therefore, for all
t\geq k
, one has
\rho_{t}>0,\rho_{t}^{\beta}>0
and thus
\alpha_{t}=\alpha_{k}
.
Since Algorithm
1
increases
\alpha_{k}
monotonically with a ratio
\eta_{\alpha}>1
whenever a rejected step is encountered,
only a finite number of rejected steps are needed to reach
\alpha_{k}>2C
and is followed by an infinite number of serious steps.

For the second part of the proof, suppose now
\alpha_{k}\leq 2C
for all
k
.
This is only possible when no or a finite number of rejected steps have been taken by the algorithm followed by all serious steps. This completes the proof.

Remark 4
.

For simplicity,
\alpha_{k}
is increased monotonically in the algorithm. In practice, we encourage that

\alpha_{k}
be reduced if
\rho_{k}>0
and
\eta_{l}^{+}>\eta_{u}^{+}
where
\eta_{u}^{+}
is an upper threshold for
\eta_{l}^{+}
.
In other words, if the actual decrease in objective is bigger than a certain ratio of the predicted decrease,
then
\Phi_{k}(\cdot)
is a good approximation and we reduce the quadratic coefficient to encourage larger step size.
This adaptation is for when the upper-
C^{2}
constant
C
is not uniform in the entire domain.
A decrease in
\alpha_{k}
allows the algorithm to adjust better to
the local upper-
C^{2}
constant that could be relatively small versus
C
, which could improve convergence.
In addition, one can use a diagonal matrix instead of a scalar
\alpha_{k}
to accelerate convergence without compromising the sparse structure of the linear system
[
40
]
.

Lemma 5

Given Assumption

Proof.

If

Since constraints

\displaystyle c_{j}(x_{k})+\beta_{k}\nabla c_{j}(x_{k})^{T}d_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\beta_{k}^{2}d_{k}^{T}H_{k\beta}^{j}d_{k},

where

and

Given

\displaystyle\left\lVert{c(x_{k+1})}\right\rVert_{1}\leq(1-\beta_{k})\left\lVert{c(x_{k})}\right\rVert_{1}+m\beta_{k}^{2}H^{c}_{u}\left\lVert{d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}.

\displaystyle\beta_{k}\left|(\lambda^{k+1})^{T}c(x_{k})\right|\leq\beta_{k}\left\lVert{\lambda^{k+1}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\left\lVert{c(x_{k})}\right\rVert_{1}.

\displaystyle\theta_{k}\left\lVert{c(x_{k})}\right\rVert_{1}-\eta_{\gamma}^{-}\beta_{k}\left|(\lambda^{k+1})^{T}c(x_{k})\right|-\theta_{k}\left\lVert{c(x_{k+1})}\right\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\geq(\theta_{k}-\eta_{\gamma}^{-}\beta_{k}\left\lVert{\lambda^{k+1}}\right\rVert_{\infty})\left\lVert{c(x_{k})}\right\rVert_{1}-\theta_{k}(1-\beta_{k})\left\lVert{c(x_{k})}\right\rVert_{1}-\theta_{k}m\beta_{k}^{2}H^{c}_{u}\left\lVert{d_{k}}\right\rVert^{2}

Therefore, if

\displaystyle\beta_{k}\geq\frac{1}{2}^{\lceil\log_{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\eta_{\beta}\alpha_{k}}{2H^{c}_{u}\theta_{k}m}\rceil}.

Further, by Lemma

\displaystyle>

3.3
Consistency restoration of linearized constraints

As mentioned previously, the linearized constraints of the subproblem (
12

8

12

consistent
linearized constraints.
For problems satisfying constraint qualification such as LICQ, the penalty problem with a well-designed penalty parameter could generate feasible accumulation points.
For problems that do not, it is possible to minimize the linearized constraint violation for infeasible accumulation points through the update rule of the penalty parameter.
For example, one can use the approach in
[
5
]
that requires solving a so-called feasibility problem.
We first formulate the following penalty subproblem :

\displaystyle\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}d\end{subarray}}{\text{min}}

Conclusions

In this paper, we have proposed and analyzed a two-algorithm methodology for nonsmooth, nonconvex optimization problems
with upper-
C^{2}
objectives, which exist in many applications, particularly two-stage optimization problems.
While both bundle methods and DC algorithms can potentially solve problems with upper-
C^{2}
objectives, attention is usually focused on the weakly convex objectives.
The proposed algorithm fills the gap of a robust and efficient algorithm for problems that lack weak convexity.
To that end, a convex quadratic programming (QP) subproblem is solved at each iteration and a trust-region update
rule is used to adjust the model parameters, which can stay bounded under reasonable assumptions. The line search
on the constraints is carefully designed to ensure overall progress.
The penalty subproblem in the consistency restoration algorithm remains a QP problem and is effective in addressing the
potential inconsistency of the linearized constraints.
Global convergence analysis of the proposed pair of algorithms is provided.

The algorithms are presented from a bundle method point of view,
but they can also can be viewed as an extension of SQP or a trust-region updated proximal variation of DCA with line search.
Further relaxation of the assumptions is possible, particularly for LICQ, which will be studied in the future. Another topic of interest is to extend the algorithms to more two-stage optimization problems using a combination of different bundle methods.
Finally, we note that the algorithm has been implemented on parallel computing platforms for our target application, power-grid optimization problems, and has shown significant potential for computational scalability
[
39
]
.
