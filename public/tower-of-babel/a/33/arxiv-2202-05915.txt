Collapsing Maps and Quasi-Isometries
Joshua Thompson, Davin Hemmila

Abstract
We introduce a generalization of the b-metric we call a (b,c)-metric. We show that if X X is a ( b , c ) (b,c) -metric space and ψ : X ⟶ Y \psi:X\longrightarrow Y is a quasi-isometry then Y Y is ( b , c ) (b,c) -metrizable. We also define a particular kind of collapsing map that can be applied to an arbitrary ( b , c ) (b,c) -metric space. We define a distance function on the image of this collapsing map and with this prove that the collapsing map is a quasi-isometry.

quasi-isometry,b-metric,collapsing

Introduction

An isometry is a distance-preserving transformation of a metric space and a quasi-isometry is a transformation that distorts distances by a bounded amount. Quasi-isometries are found in many places in mathematics and are ubiquitous in low-dimensional topology and geometric group theory
[
CM17
]
. The set of all isometries on a given metric space is a subject of classical study, see for example
[
FM11
]
. The set of all quasi-isometries on a given metric space is undoubtedly much more complex but also much less studied. Many examples of quasi-isometries are similar to self-maps that collapse or glue together distinct subsets. Collapsing maps are a subset of all possible transformations of this form. We show that if a map collapses a certain space in a certain way it will always be a quasi-isometry.

What we will mean by a certain space is that it be a globally Lipschitz
(b,c)
-metric space. The Lipschitz condition prevents the space from being stretched too far in the new metric. The
(b,c)
-metric space is a simple generalization of
b
-metric space studied by
[
Tom17
]
, which are almost metric spaces except the triangle inequality is relaxed by a multiplicative constant. A
(b,c)
-metric is almost a metric space except that its triangle inequality is relaxed by both an additive and multiplicative constant. Those familiar with quasi-isometries will note the obvious connection to
(b,c)
-metric spaces.

What we will mean for a map to collapse in a certain way is that do so in a bounded fashion. This means two things. First, our collapsing maps are self-maps that move points by at most a bounded distance. Second, the distortion is controlled by a Lipschitz constant. These two features of the collapsing map serve to control the distortion in the mapping and give rise to a quasi-isometry. Our main theorem is as follows.

Theorem
.

Let
(X,\rho)
be a
(b,c)-
metric space and let
\phi:X\longrightarrow\nicefrac{{X}}{{\sim}}
be a collapsing map that collapses subset
S
via equivalence relation
\sim
. If
\rho_{\phi}
is the collapsed metric on
\nicefrac{{X}}{{\sim}}
then there are constants
K
and
C
such that

\frac{1}{K}\rho(x,y)-C\leq\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])\leq K\rho(x,y)+C\mbox{ }\forall x,y\in X

Then
\phi
a quasi-isometry between
(X,\rho)
and
(\nicefrac{{X}}{{\sim}},\rho_{\phi})
.

1.1
Quasi-Isometry and
(b,c)
-metric Spaces

Here we define the basic terms and prove a Lemma which shows that quasi-isometries preserve

-metrics.

Definition 1.1
.

A quasi-isometry is a mapping between a
(b,c)
-metric space X and a semi-metric Y,
f:X\to Y
such that

\frac{1}{K}\rho(x,y)-C\leq\rho_{\phi}(f(x),f(y))\leq K\rho(x,y)+C

for some constants
C\in[0,\infty),K\in[1,\infty)
.

Example 1.1
.

The step function is a quasi-isometry from the reals to the integers. Let
f:\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}
by

be the least integer function. Then

is quasi-isometry from
\mathbb{R}
to
\mathbb{Z}
with

and
C=1
.

We are particularly interested in maps that collapse subsets, similar to what happens under quotient maps of topological spaces. It is well known that a bounded metric space is quasi-isometric to a point. Therefore, the following example should not be surprising.

Example 1.2
.

Let
X=\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let
D
be the closed unit disk. Let
f
be the quotient map that collapses the unit disk to a point. Then
f:X\longrightarrow Y
where

where
x\sim y
if both

and

lie in the closed unit disk. One can define a metric

where
\rho
is standard metric. It follows that

is quasi-isometric to
(X,\rho)
.

In this paper we collapse unbounded subsets, but they are unbounded in only one dimension. These subsets are collapsed to sub-manifolds that have bounded variation (actually Lipschitz continuous) so the unbounded nature of the collapsed subset is effectively controlled by the Lipschitz condition. So while general collapsing maps can kill the triangle inequality entirely we prove that our so-called Lipschitz collapsings give rise to
b
-metrics, metric spaces with a relaxed triangle inequality.

This puts in the category of
b
-metric spaces, a generalization of metric spaces introduced by Bourbaki, and recently considered by Tomonoari
[
Tom17
]
.

Definition 1.2
.

A

-metric space is a pair (X, d) where X is a set and

satisfying for all

1.

d(x,y)\geq 0
with
d(x,y)=0\iff x=y

2.

d(x,y)=d(y,x)

3.

There exists

d(x,z)\leq b(d(x,y)+d(y,z))

Example 1.3
.

We collapse the unit ball

\rho

be the relation
x\sim y
if both

and

and

\nicefrac{{X}}{{\sim}}
for

. The triangle inequality does not hold for all points in the collapsed space. For example in

The distortion of a metric caused by a quasi-isometry is of a bounded nature and we show that this bounded distortion merely relaxes the triangle inequality, instead of destroying it. Spaces that satisfy a ”relaxed triangle inequality” are
b-
metric spaces as defined by Czerwik (1998). That is, after multiplying by a constant

a triangle inequality holds. We expand this notion to a
(b,c)
-metric, where we multiply by
b
and add

. This is the appropriate setting to describe quasi-isometries.

By the term

we refer to a space with a non-degenerate symmetric bi-linear form; that is, a metric space without a triangle inequality.

Definition 1.3
.

A (b,c)-metric space is a semi-metric space with the extra condition that

d(x,z)\leq b(d(x,y)+d(y,z))+c

where
b\geq 1
and
c\geq 0
.

(b,c)-metrics arise from applying a quasi-isometry to a metric space without the restriction that the range must have a certain triangle inequality.

Example 1.4
.

The Euclidean plane is a
(1,0)
-metric space and Example
1.3
is a
(1,1)
-metric space.

Lemma 1.1
.

If
f:X\rightarrow Y
is a quasi-isometry where
X
is a
(b,c)
-metric space and
Y
is a semi-metric space, then
Y
is a
(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})
-metric space.

Proof.

Since
f
is a quasi-isometry and
X
has a relaxed triangle inequality, we know:

\rho_{\phi}(f(x),f(z))\leq K\rho(x,z)+C

\frac{1}{K}\rho(x,y)-C\leq\rho_{\phi}(f(x),f(y))

\frac{1}{K}\rho(y,z)-C\leq\rho_{\phi}(f(y),f(z))

\rho(x,z)\leq b(\rho(x,y)+\rho(y,z))+c

Multiplying (4) by K and adding C we get:

K\rho(x,z)+C\leq Kb(\rho(x,y)+\rho(y,z))+Kc+C

Bringing the inequality in (1) to (5) we get:

\rho_{\phi}(f(x),f(z))\leq K\rho(x,z)+C\leq Kb(\rho(x,y)+\rho(y,z))+Kc+C

Multiplying (2) and (3) by bK
2
, adding bCK
2
+
\frac{Kc+C}{2}
and bringing the sum of (2) and (3) to (6) we get:

Kb(\rho(x,y)+\rho(y,z))+Kc+C\leq

bK^{2}(\rho_{\phi}(f(x),f(y))+\rho_{\phi}(f(y),f(z)))+2bCK^{2}+Kc+C

so

\rho_{\phi}(f(x),f(z))\leq bK^{2}(\rho_{\phi}(f(x),f(y))+\rho_{\phi}(f(y),f(z)))+2bCK^{2}+Kc+C

and setting bK
2
=
b^{\prime}
and 2bCK
2
+ Kc + C =

we see Y is a
(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})
-metric.
∎

1.2
Collapsing Maps

In this section we define a collapsing map and show that it preserves the
(b,c)
-metric structure of a space.

Given a (b,c)-metric space
X
and a subset
S
we wish to define a mapping on
X
wherein we collapse
S
to a proper subset of itself and glue the complement of
S
in
X
together along this proper subset. This can be viewed as collapsing
S
and
bringing the complement along for the ride
. This is quite similar to the concept of a quotient map of topological spaces. Collapsing a set
S
can be thought of partitioning
S
into equivalence class and mapping every element of
S
to its equivalence class.

Quotient maps in metric spaces can be quite general and can destroy the metric completely. Since we are collapsing a To give ourselves some control we assume hereafter that the
(b,c)
-semi-metric space
(X,\rho)
is a (globally) Lipschitz manifold.

Definition 1.4

(Lipschitz)
.

A function
f:X\longrightarrow Y
is (globally) Lipschitz continuous if there exists a real constant
L\geq 0
such that for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in x

d_{Y}(f(x_{1}),f(x_{2}))\leq L\cdot d_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})

We say the function
f
is bi-Lipschitz if both
f
and its inverse are Lipschitz.

Example 1.5
.

If
S=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|y=\sin(x)+1
then
S
is the image of a (globally) Lipschitz function with Lipschitz constant
L=1.

Since the derivative of a Lipschitz function is bounded we can think of a (globally) Lipschitz manifold as one whose ”steepness” is bounded above by a constant. As we shall see, without this Lipschitz condition one can construct a collapsing of a smooth sub-manifolds that does not give quasi-isometry to our collapsed metric. There is a close relationship between quasi-conformal maps and Lipschitz homeomorphisms, see
[
Luu01
]
, but they do not consider quasi-isometries.

The sets we collapse come equipped with a fibering. Recall, a fibered manifold is a manifold
E
together with a projection
\pi:E\rightarrow c
that is a surjective submersion. Every point of
E
is then contained in some fiber
\pi^{-1}(x)
.

Definition 1.5

(Bounded fibered manifold)
.

A fibered manifold where the length of every fiber is bounded.

Example 1.6
.

In the example above, the unit disk is a bounded fibrered manifold. Note that the region of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
between the graphs of
y=x^{2}
and
y=x
may be fibered using vertical lines, but this fibering is not bounded.

For a given metric space
(X,\rho)
and subset
T\subset X
, there are two natural metric spaces we consider:
(T,\rho)
and
(T,\rho_{p})
where
\rho_{p}(a,b)
is defined to be the infimum of all paths
in T
from
a
to
b
. In our collapsing map
T
can be thought of as the image of a retraction and we need this image to not be too wild. Our collapsing map definition requires the existence of a set which is to be collapsed.

Definition 1.6

(Collapsing Set)
.

Let
X
be an
n-
manifold with metric
\rho
. The collapsing subset
S\subset X
has the following properties.

1.

S
is a bounded fibered
n
-dimensional sub-manifold of
X
with fibering
\pi:E\rightarrow X
.

2.

T\subset S
is a (n-1)-manifold transverse to each fiber
\pi^{-1}(x)
.

3.

The identity mapping
i:(T,\rho)\longrightarrow(T,\rho_{p})
is Lipschitz where
\rho_{p}
is the path-metric in
T
inherited from
\rho
.

If a curve
T
satisfies property (3) above we say that
T
is a Lipschitz curve in
X
.

Example 1.7
.

Let
S
be the region of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
between
y=-2
and
y=2
. This region can be fibered by vertical segments. Let
T=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mbox{ }|\mbox{ }y=\cos(2x)\}
. It is clear that
T
is transverse to all vertical segments in
S
and is the image of a Lipschitz function
f(x)=\cos(2x)
with Lipschitz constant
L=2
. Consequently the derivative is bounded at all
x
:

|f^{\prime}(x)|\leq 2

thus

\rho_{p}(a,b)=\int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{1+(f^{\prime}(t))^{2}}dt=\int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{1+4\sin^{2}(2t)}dt

\leq\int_{a}^{b}\sqrt{1+4(2)^{2}}dt=\sqrt{17}*|b-a|=\sqrt{17}\rho(a,b)

where
\rho
is the standard metric on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Thus the identity mapping
i:(T,\rho)\longrightarrow(T,\rho_{p})
is Lipschitz.

Let
S\subset X
be a collapsing set. We define a relation
\sim
called the
collapsing relation
, on
X
by

x\sim y\mbox{ if }x\in\pi^{-1}(z)\mbox{ and }y\in\pi^{-1}(z)

for some
z\in X
where
\pi^{-1}(z)
is a fiber of
S
. Since the subset
T\subset S
is transverse to each fiber each element

appears in exactly one equivalence class. For any

x^{\prime}=T\cap[x]
.

Lemma 1.2
.

The collapsing relation is an equivalence relation.

Proof.

Let
f=\pi^{-1}(w)
be fiber of the S. Reflexivity follows immediately because if
x\in f
then
x\in f
as well, and therefore
x\sim x
. The relation is symmetric because saying
x\in f
and
y\in f
is the same as saying
y\in f
and
x\in f
. Lastly transitivity holds because if
x\sim y
and
y\sim z
then
x\in f
and

y\in f
and
z\in f

x\in f
and

which is precisely

. ∎

Definition 1.7

(Collapsing Map)
.

Let
S
be a collapsing set in
X
. The map
\phi:X\to\nicefrac{{X}}{{\sim}}
is given by
\phi(x)=[x]
where
[x]
is the equivalence class of
x
under the relation
\sim
on
S
. We denote this map by the triple
(X,S,\phi)
and
write
\nicefrac{{X}}{{\sim}}=X^{*}
.

To obtain a semi-metric on the collapsed space we deform the distance between points that are closer to the collapsing set than they are to each other. We do this as follows. Let
r_{x}
denote the minimum distance from
x
to the collapsing set
S
. This is realized by taking balls of radius
r
about
x
and increasing the radius until the ball intersects
S
. This distance is the least such radius. If
d(x,y)>r_{x}+r_{y}
we say the pair
\{x,y\}
is in the
vicinity
of
S
. Note that if
x\in S
then
r_{x}=0
which implies
d(x,y)>r_{x}+r_{y}
for all
y\in X
. That is, if
x\in S
then
\{x,y\}
is in the vicinity of
S
for all
y\in X
.

Definition 1.8
.

Given a metric
\rho
on
X
and collapsing map
(X,S,\phi)
we define
\rho_{\phi}:X^{*}\times X^{*}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as follows:
Let
[x],[y]\in X^{*}
. If
\{x,y\}
is not in the vicinity of
S
we define

\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])=\rho(x,y)

and
if
\{x,y\}
is in the vicinity of
S
we define

\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])=\rho_{p}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})+r_{x}+r_{y}

where
x^{\prime}=T\cap[x]
and
y^{\prime}=T\cap[y]
and
\rho_{p}
is the path metric in
T
inherited from
\rho
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(X,\rho)
be a semi-metric space. The map
\rho_{\phi}:X^{*}\times X^{*}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by collapsing map
\phi
gives a semi-metric space
(X^{*},\rho_{\phi})
.

Proof.

Let
[x],[y]\in X^{*}
. If
\{x,y\}
is not in the vicinity of
S
then
\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])=\phi(x,y)
. In this case
\rho_{\phi}
satisfies the properties of a semi-metric since
\rho
does. Now assume
\{x,y\}
is in the vicinity of
S
.

1.

Since
\rho_{p}
is a semi-metric and
r_{x}\geq 0
for all
x\in X
we have

\rho_{p}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})+r_{x}+r_{y}\geq 0

from which it is immediate that
\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])\geq 0
.

2.

If
\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])=0
then
r_{x}=r_{y}=0
which implies
x\in S
and
y\in S
and
[x]=x^{\prime}
and
[y]=y^{\prime}
. So
\rho_{p}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=0
which implies
x^{\prime}=y^{\prime}
since
\rho_{p}
is a semi-metric. Therefore
[x]=[y]
.

∎

Collapsing Map is a Quasi-Isometry

We know that if we apply a quasi-isometry to a metric space we get a semi-metric space, so to shed light on the collapsing map we’d like to show that it is a quasi-isometry. In Lemma
2.1
we compare the length of points on
T
to their length of their image under the collapsing map. We show that the ratio of the new distance to the original distance is bounded above by a constant that depends on the Lipschitz constant of
T
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
T
be a Lipschitz curve in the
(b,c)-
metric space
(X,\rho)
. For all points
x,y
in
T\subset X
in the ratio of their distance in the collapsed metric
\rho_{\phi}
to that of the original metric
\rho
is bounded. That is,

\forall\{x,y\}\in T,\exists K_{L}\in\mathbb{R}\mbox{ such that }\frac{\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])}{\rho(x,y)}\leq K_{L}.

Proof.

Since
T
is a Lipschitz curve we know there is a map
f:(T,\rho)\longrightarrow(T,\rho_{p})
such that

\rho_{p}(x,y)\leq L\cdot\rho(x,y)

for a fixed
L\in\mathbb{R}
and any
x,y\in T
.

The collapsed metric essentially agrees with the path metric on
T
we now show. For
x,y\in T
the distance of each point to
S
is zero so

\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])=\rho_{p}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})+r_{x}+r_{y}=\rho_{p}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

and since
T
is a Lipschitz curve in
X
with Lipschitz constant
L
we have

\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])=\rho_{p}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq K\cdot\rho(x,y)

and the lemma follows.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
S
be a collapsing set and let
x,y\in S
and
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in T
as defined as in Definition

.
The
\rho

and

\rho(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq b^{2}\rho(x,y)+(b^{2}f+bc+c).

S
.

Proof.

If
\rho_{1}(x,y)\geq\rho_{1}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

and

, the case that
(X,\rho)
is a metric space. Note that the mapping moves a point

\rho(x,y^{\prime})\leq\rho(x,y)+\rho(y,y^{\prime})\leq\rho(x,y)+f

\rho(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq\rho(x^{\prime},x)+\rho(x,y^{\prime})\leq f+\rho(x,y)+f

\rho(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq\rho(x,y)+2f.

More generally, in a

\rho(x,y^{\prime})\leq b(\rho(x,y)+\rho(y,y^{\prime}))+c\leq b\rho(x,y)+bf+c

\rho(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq b(\rho(x^{\prime},x)+\rho(x,y^{\prime}))\leq b(f+\rho(x,y^{\prime}))+c\leq bf+b^{2}\rho(x,y)+b^{2}f+bc

\rho(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq b^{2}\rho(x,y)+(b^{2}f+bc+c).

x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime}\in T
and assume
\rho(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})=\rho(x,y)+2f
. Therefore

\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])=\rho_{\phi}([x^{\prime}],[y^{\prime}])\leq\rho_{\phi}([x^{\prime\prime}],[y^{\prime\prime}]).

Since
\rho(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})>f
, we apply Lemma
2.3
to
x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime}
so we have

\rho_{\phi}([x^{\prime\prime}],[y^{\prime\prime}])\leq b^{2}K_{L}\rho(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})+K_{L}(b^{2}f+bc+c)

and by construction

\rho(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime\prime})\leq(\rho(x,y)+2f)

so

\rho_{\phi}([x^{\prime\prime}],[y^{\prime\prime}])\leq b^{2}K_{L}((\rho(x,y)+2f))+K_{L}(b^{2}f+bc+c)

or

\rho_{\phi}([x^{\prime\prime}],[y^{\prime\prime}])\leq(b^{2}K_{L})\rho(x,y)+K_{L}(3b^{2}f+bc+c)

Thus

\rho_{\phi}([x],[x])\leq K\rho(x,y)+C.

where
K=b^{2}K_{L}
and
C=K_{L}(3b^{2}f+bc+c)
.
∎

Up to now for all points
x,y\in S
we have found a
K
and
C
that gives an upper bound for a quasi-isometry. We now extend this to all of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

There are constants
K,C
such that

\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])\leq K\rho(x,y)+C

for all
x,y\in X
.

Proof.

Let
r_{x}
denote the radius of the smallest ball (in the
\rho
metric) centered at
x\in X
that intersects
S
. Let
x_{S}
denote a point in
S
such that
\rho(x,x_{S})=r_{x}
.

For any
x\in S
Lemmas
2.3
and
2.4
imply that constants
K,C
exist. Now assume
x,y\notin S
. By definition
\rho(x,y)\leq r_{x}+r_{y}
then

\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])=\rho(x,y)

so the metric is unchanged. Now assume
\rho(x,y)>r_{x}+r_{y}
. This implies that is {x,y} is in the vicinity of
S
so again by definition,

\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])=r_{x}+r_{y}+d_{T}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})

where
x^{\prime}=T\cap[x]
.

The vicinity assumption implies

\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])\leq\rho(x,y)+d_{T}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}).

(1)

Since
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in T
, Lemma
2.1
implies

d_{T}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq K_{L}(\rho(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})).

(2)

and since
Lemma
2.2
implies

\rho(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq b^{2}\rho(x,y)+(b^{2}f+bc+c)

we have

d_{T}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq K_{L}(b^{2}\rho(x,y)+(b^{2}f+bc+c)).

(3)

Combining equations (
1
) and (
3
) gives

\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])\leq(b^{2}K_{L}+1)\rho(x,y)+(b^{2}f+bc+c))

By taking
K=b^{2}K_{L}+1
and
C=(b^{2}f+bc+c)
the Lemma holds.

∎

It is worth nothing that the inequality
3
above is the upper bound to proving that the
\rho
-geodesic between
x
and
y
is quasi-isometric to an arc of
T
bounded by
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
.

Lemma 2.6
.

There are constants
K,C
such that

\frac{\rho(x,y)}{K}-C\leq\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])\mbox{ }\forall x,y\in X

Proof.

Let
x,y\in X
with
x\neq y
and assume
\rho(x,x_{S})=r_{x}
and
\rho(y,y_{S})=r_{y}
. By Lemma
2.2
there exist constants
M,N
for which.

\displaystyle\rho(x_{S},x^{\prime})\leq Mf+N

(4)

\displaystyle\rho(y_{S},y^{\prime})\leq Mf+N.

(5)

Let
\rho(x,x^{\prime})=l
,
\rho(y,y^{\prime})=m
and
\rho(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=n
. By applying the relaxed triangle inequality twice we see there are constants
b\geq 1,c\geq 0
such that

\rho(x,y)\leq b^{2}(\rho(x,x^{\prime})+\rho(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})+\rho(y^{\prime},y))+b(c+1)=b^{2}(l+n+m)+b(c+1).

and dropping
n
gives

\frac{\rho(x,y)}{b^{2}}\leq l+m+\frac{c+1}{b}.

(6)

By the relaxed triangle inequality and inequality (
4
) and (
5
)

\displaystyle l\leq b(r_{x}+Mf+N)+c

(7)

\displaystyle m\leq b(r_{y}+Mf+N)+c.

(8)

Combining inequalities (
6
), (
7
) and (
8
) gives

\frac{\rho(x,y)}{b^{2}}\leq b(r_{x}+r_{y}+2b(Mf+N))+2c+\frac{c+1}{b}

so

\frac{\rho(x,y)}{b^{3}}-2(bMf+N)-\frac{c(2b+1)+1}{b^{2}}

(9)

By definition
\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])=r_{x}+r_{y}+d_{T}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
and
x\neq y
implies
d_{T}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})=\epsilon>0
so

\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])=r_{x}+r_{y}+\epsilon

. We set
K=b^{3}
and
C=2(bMf+N)-\frac{c(2b+1)+1}{b^{2}}
and inequality (
9
) gives

\frac{\rho(x,y)}{K}-C=\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])-\epsilon

and therefore

\frac{\rho(x,y)}{K}-C\leq\rho_{\phi}([x],[y]).

∎

Note that the lower bound
K
depends only on
b
coming from the
(b,c)-
metric, and not on the Lipschitz constant. In particular, for metric spaces the length of the longest fiber controls how much distances are decreased by the collapsing map.

Theorem 2
.

Let
(X,\rho)
be a
(b,c)-
metric space and let
\phi:X\longrightarrow\nicefrac{{X}}{{\sim}}
be a collapsing map that collapses subset
S
via equivalence relation
\sim
. If
\rho_{\phi}
is the collapsed metric on
\nicefrac{{X}}{{\sim}}
then there are constants
K
and
C
such that

\frac{1}{K}\rho(x,y)-C\leq\rho_{\phi}([x],[y])\leq K\rho(x,y)+C\mbox{ }\forall x,y\in X

Then
\phi
a quasi-isometry between
(X,\rho)
and
(\nicefrac{{X}}{{\sim}},\rho_{\phi})
.

Proof.

Let
K=b^{3}L+1
and Let

C=K(3b^{2}f+bc+c).

Since
L\geq 1
(
T
is a Lipschitz manifold) and
n\geq 1
and

these definitions of

and

satisfy both Lemma
2.5
and
2.6
.
Therefore
\phi
is a quasi-isometry between (X,

) and (
\nicefrac{{X}}{{\sim}}
,
\rho_{\phi}
).
∎

Example 2.1
.

Let
S=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}|\sin(x)-1<y<\sin(x)+1
be the region between two sine curves and let

for all

Corollary 2.1
.

If
X
is a (b,c)-metric space with collapsing map

, then

is a
(b^{\prime},c^{\prime})
-metric space.

Proof.

By Theorem 1 and Theorem 2, we see that

is a quasi-isometry and that Y =

is a semi-metric space. Since we have these conditions, Lemma 1.1 applies and we see Y is a

-metric space where

=

and

∎

Note: if c, C = 0
\implies

= 0 makes this a Lipschitz map between b-metrics.

3
Conclusion

In most literature, quasi-isometries have the domain and range be metric spaces. This does not necessarily need to be the case as seen in
[
GK13
]
(note the alternative definition for semi-metric). Assuming we have a metric we have shown that a quasi-isometry will preserve the (b,c)-triangle inequality by Lemma 1.1. The space of all quasi-isometries is vast and it seems to be that many canonical examples of quasi-isometries can be represented as a series of collapsing maps. In example
1.1
we see that floor function is a quasi-isometry. This can also be realized as countably many collapsing maps. Expanding on this a natural conjecture is:

Conjecture 2.1
.

Any quasi-isometry can be expressed as the composition of a collapsing map and Lipschitz map.

It is also possible to generalize both (b,c)-metrics as well as quasi-isometries by extending the bounds to functions instead of linear terms. The generalized quasi-isometry would not preserve a lot of structure, but it would preserve some depending on the function used. There is also analogous collapsing maps accompanying these new definitions.

Example 3.1
.

Here we define the generalized quasi-isometry to be:

\frac{1}{Q}\rho(x,y)^{2}+\frac{1}{K}\rho(x,y)-C\leq\rho_{\phi}(f(x),f(y))\leq Q\rho(x,y)^{2}+K\rho(x,y)+C

for some constants
C,Q\in[0,\infty),K\in[1,\infty)
.
The generalized (b,c)-metric to be:

1.

d(x,y)\geq 0
with
d(x,y)=0\iff x=y

2.

d(x,y)=d(y,x)

3.

d(x,z)\leq a(d(x,y)^{2}+d(y,z)^{2})+b(d(x,y)+d(y,z))+c

And the generalized collapsing map to be not have the restriction to lengths of fibers as well as instead of being Lipshitz it is 2-Hölder Continuous. Since the generalized collapsing map is 2-Hölder Continuous, the generalized quasi-isometry holds and by generalizing lemma 1.1 it produces an (a,b,c)-metric.

This example leads us to our second conjecture:

Conjecture 2.2
.

If we have a generalized quasi-isometry where our bounds are given by a function, a generalized (b,c)-metric and generalized collapsing map whose bounds are given my the same function then we have the generalized form of Theorem 2 and Corollary 2.1.
