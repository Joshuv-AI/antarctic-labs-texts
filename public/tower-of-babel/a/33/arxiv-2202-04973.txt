Floquet-Landau-Zener interferometry: Usefulness of the Floquet theory in pulse-laser-driven systems
Tatsuhiko N. Ikeda, Satoshi Tanaka, Yosuke Kayanuma

Abstract
We develop the Landau-Zener transfer matrix theory for the instantaneous Floquet states (IFSs) for quantum systems driven by strong pulse laser. Applying this theory to the pulse excitation probability in two-level quantum systems, we show unexpectedly good quantitative agreements for few-cycle pulses. This approach enables us to qualitatively understand the probability’s peculiar behaviors as quantum path interference between IFSs. We also study the pulse-width dependence, finding that this Floquet-state interpretation remains useful for shorter pulses down to 2-cycle ones in the present model. These results imply that the Floquet theory is meaningful in experimental few-cycle lasers if applied appropriately in the sense of IFSs.



I
Introduction

Intense few-cycle laser fields have opened opportunities for studying strong light-matter interactions and for optically controlling material properties
Brabec and Krausz 2000
.
Among various approaches, Floquet engineering is an emerging concept in optical material control, in which the time-oscillating nature of fields is utilized
Bukov et al. 2015
;
Oka and Kitamura 2019
;
Rudner and Lindner 2020
.
The guiding principle is Floquet theory
Floquet 1883
;
Shirley 1965
;
Sambe 1973
, which governs solutions of time-dependent Schrödinger equations (TDSE) under perfectly periodic external fields, i.e., infinitely-long pulses.

However, it has not been fully clarified yet when and how Floquet theory is justified under external pulse fields available in experiments.
In this direction, Holthaus and coworkers developed the instantaneous Floquet state (IFS) formalism in their pioneering works
Breuer and Holthaus 1989a
;
Breuer and Holthaus 1989b
;
Drese and Holthaus 1999
;
Holthaus 2015
.
Rather than approximating a pulse field crudely by a continuous wave, this formalism utilizes the Floquet states as instantaneous basis states, on which the actual quantum state evolves adiabatically or diabatically during the pulse.
While the theory was elegantly formulated and applied to some examples
Holthaus 2015
, its actual implementation is generically challenging, and its advantages have not been fully explored yet.

In this paper, we further develop the IFS formalism and find the situations where this formalism appropriately describes quantum dynamics under strong pulse fields.
In particular, we introduce the Landau-Zener-type transfer matrices in the Floquet extended Hilbert space, describing multiple transitions between the IFSs quantitatively correctly.
We apply our theory to two-level quantum systems driven by strong pulse fields, showing its applicability and usefulness.
Recent studies showed that the pulse excitation probability of two-level systems exhibits peculiar parameter dependence
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Kayanuma 2021
, but its mechanisms have remained uncovered yet.
Our theory explains even quantitatively that this peculiar behavior is due to quantum path interference between IFSs.
We also study the pulse-width dependence, finding that those Floquet-state interpretations remain valid shorter pulses down to 2-cycle ones in the present model.
These results imply that Floquet theory is meaningful in experimental few-cycle lasers if applied appropriately in the sense of IFSs.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
In Sec.
II
, we introduce the pulse excitation problem in a two-level quantum system and demonstrate that the excitation probability exhibits complex behaviors when we vary the pulse strength and the two levels’ energy difference.
To uncover the underlying mechanisms of these behaviors, we review the IFS formalism and develop the Floquet-Landau-Zener (FLZ) theory using transfer matrices for the Floquet Hamiltonian in Sec.
III
.
In Secs.
IV
and
V
, we implement the FLZ numerically, showing its quantitative success in calculating the pulse excitation probability.
We elucidate that the complex behaviors introduced in Sec.
II
originate from quantum path interference among IFSs.
We also show that these Floquet-based interpretations remain valid for unexpectedly short pulses, including 2-cycle pulse lasers.
Finally in Sec.
VI
we summarize our results and list some open problems for future study.

II
Formulation of the problem

For concreteness, we consider an abstract two-level quantum system in strong pulse fields.
Being an effective model in various physical systems, the driven two-level system may represent, e.g., lasing of N
2
molecules
Zhang et al. 2017
;
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Kayanuma 2021
, two-band electrons in semiconductors
Taya et al. 2021a
, nitrogen-vacancy centers in diamonds
Fuchs et al. 2009
, to name a few.
Throughout this work, we suppose that the Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{pulse}}(t)=\frac{b}{2}\sigma_{z}+a(t)V(t).

(1)

Here,
b

(>0)
is the energy difference between the two levels
\ket{\uparrow}
and
\ket{\downarrow}
.
The coupling to the external field consists of the periodic part
V(t+T)=V(t)
and the pulse envelope with peak height
a_{0}

(>0)

\displaystyle a(t)=a_{0}f(t),

(2)

where
T
is the period and we define its corresponding angular frequency as
\omega\equiv 2\pi/T
.
We assume that the envelope is positive and normalized so that
\max_{t}f(t)=1
and
f(t)\to 0

(t\to\pm\infty)
.
For concreteness, we focus on the following prototypical coupling term

\displaystyle V(t)=\cos(\omega t)\sigma_{x},

(3)

which naturally arises in, e.g., two-level atoms coupled to linearly-polarized lasers.
We discuss, in Appendix
C
, modified problems corresponding to circular and elliptic polarizations.
We also specify, for concreteness, the envelope to be Gaussian (generalization to other envelopes is straightforward)

\displaystyle f(t)=\exp\left[-\left(\frac{t}{\nu T}\right)^{2}\right],

(4)

where the dimensionless parameter
\nu

(>0)
represents the pulse width in units of
T
.
Namely, we consider a
\nu
-cycle pulse with the envelope (
4
).

Our problem to address is the following.
Suppose that our initial state at
t=-\infty
is the ground state

\ket{\Psi(-\infty)}=\ket{\downarrow}
,
which evolves in time according to the time-dependent Schrödinger equation (TDSE)

\displaystyle i\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\Psi(t)}=H_{\mathrm{pulse}}(t)\ket{\Psi(t)}

(5)

to become

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi(+\infty)}=\exp_{+}\left(-i\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}H_{\mathrm{pulse}}(\tau)d\tau\right)\ket{\downarrow}

(6)

after the pulse
(
\hbar=1
throughout this paper).
Then, we ask the final weight of the excited state

\displaystyle P_{\uparrow}=|\braket{\uparrow|\Psi(+\infty)}|^{2}.

(7)

Figure 1:
Excitation probability
P_{\uparrow}
plotted against the pulse peak height
a_{0}
and the energy level difference
b
with pulse width
\nu=6
.

Despite this simple setup,
P_{\uparrow}
variously changes depending on the energy level difference
b
, coupling energy
a
, driving frequency
\omega
, and pulse width
s
.
We show in Fig.
1
the results of
P_{\uparrow}
in terms of the numerical integration of TDSE (
5
) with frequency
\omega=1
and pulse width
\nu=6
.
At weak coupling
a_{0}
,
P_{\uparrow}
becomes significant only near resonance
b/\omega\simeq 1
.
This parameter regime is well described by the rotating-wave approximation, and the oscillating behavior of
P_{\uparrow}
is understood in relation to the Rabi oscillation
Rabi 1937
(see also Appendix
C.1
).

Furthermore, away from the resonance and at strong couplings, there is a complex pattern of the region for nonvanishing
P_{\uparrow}
.
For example, as
a_{0}
increases with
b=2.5
held fixed,
P_{\uparrow}
suddenly grows up at
a_{0}\sim 1.0
, shows clear oscillations up to
a_{0}\sim 3.0
, and then exhibits irregular behaviors
a_{0}\gtrsim 3.0
.
The sudden grow-up was shown, in the pioneering works by Holthaus and coworkers
Holthaus 2015
, to be due to avoided crossing structures of Floquet quasienergies as we will review below in Sec.
III.1
.
However, the oscillations and irregular behaviors have not been studied well.
In the following, we extend their theory by combining the Landau-Zener transfer matrix and elucidating those complex behaviors of
P_{\uparrow}
in the whole parameter region.

III
Landau-Zener-Stückelberg theory for Floquet states

III.1
Instantaneous Floquet states (IFS)

The key to understanding the complex dynamics is using the basis of the instantaneous Floquet states (ITS)
Breuer and Holthaus 1989b
;
Holthaus 2015
,
which we briefly review here.
We note that this formulation was generalized, in Ref.
Drese and Holthaus 1999
, to the case where
\omega
also varies slowly.

The Floquet states are defined by the solutions to the time-dependent Schrödinger equation for the continuous wave rather than the pulse.
Namely, according to Floquet theory
Shirley 1965
;
Sambe 1973
, the two independent solutions to

\displaystyle i\frac{d}{dt}\ket{\psi(t)}=H_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,t)\ket{\psi(t)}

(8)

with

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,t)=\frac{b}{2}\sigma_{z}+aV(t)

(9)

can be written in the following forms:

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{m}(a,t)}=e^{-i\epsilon_{m}(a)t}\ket{u_{m}(a,t)}\quad(m=1,2).

(10)

Note that
H_{\mathrm{cw}}(a,t)
is obtained by replacing the envelope
a(t)
in
H_{\mathrm{pulse}}(t)
by a constant
a
(i.e., replacing the Gaussian pulse by a continuous wave).
In Eq. (
10
),
\ket{u_{m}(a,t)}=\ket{u_{m}(a,t+T)}
are periodic and called the Floquet states, and the real numbers
\epsilon_{m}(a)
are quasienergies.
We explicitly put the dependence on the coupling strength
a
on the Floquet states and quasienergies that will play crucial roles.

We remark the famous replicas of Floquet states.
Note that Eq. (
10
) can also be written as

\ket{\psi_{m}(a,t)}=e^{-i[\epsilon_{m}(a)+l\omega]t}e^{il\omega t}\ket{u_{m}(a,t)}

for an arbitrary integer
l\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Being periodic,

\displaystyle\ket{u_{m,l}(a,t)}\equiv e^{il\omega t}\ket{u_{m}(a,t)}\quad(m=1,2)

(11)

are all Floquet states, and their quasienergies are given by

\displaystyle\epsilon_{m,l}(a)\equiv\epsilon_{m}(a)+l\omega.

(12)

In Fig.
2
, we plot the quasienergies with replicas numerically obtained for
b=2.5
.
They show avoided crossings near
a=1.0
and
3.0
, where two quasienergies repel each other.
This is a manifestation of strong hybridization between
\ket{\uparrow}
and
\ket{\downarrow}
, and we will discuss, in detail, how this hybridization leads to the complex pattern in Fig. (
1
).
We remark that the quasienergies of
m=1
and
2
do not repel but cross near
a=2.2
, which is due to a selection rule prohibiting hybridization (see Appendix
C.2
).

Figure 2:
Quasienergies for
b=2.5
plotted against coupling strength
a
. Solid (dashed) lines show those
for Floquet states of
m=2
(
m=1
) approaching
\ket{\downarrow}
(
\ket{\uparrow}
) as
a\to 0
.
Five Floquet replicas are presented.

Although these replicas lead to the same solution to the Schrödinger equaion (
8
),
they are all necessary when one expands an arbitrary periodic function
F(t)
with Floquet states.
In other words, the replicas satisfy the completeness relation

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1,2}\sum_{l=-\infty}^{\infty}\ket{u_{m,l}(a,t)}\bra{u_{m,l}(a,t^{\prime})}=T\delta_{T}(t-t^{\prime})I,

(13)

where
\delta_{T}(t)\equiv\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(t-nT)
and
I
is the identity operator.

The IFS formalism is to expand the solution for the pulse problem
\ket{\Psi(t)}
in terms of the Floquet states:

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi(t)}=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}(t)\ket{u_{\alpha}(a(t),t)},

(14)

where
\alpha=(m,l)
is a shorthand notation for the double indices.
Substituting Eq. (
14
) into Eq. (
5
), we have the time-evolution equation for the expansion coefficients (see Appendix
B
),

\displaystyle i\frac{dc_{\alpha}(t)}{dt}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta}\mathcal{H}_{\alpha\beta}(a(t))c_{\beta}(t),

(15)

where
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha\beta}(a(t))
is the infinite-dimensional “Hamiltonian” defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\alpha\beta}(a(t))

\displaystyle\equiv\delta_{\alpha\beta}\epsilon_{\alpha}(a(t))-i\frac{da}{dt}\mathcal{G}_{\alpha\beta}(a(t)),

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\alpha\beta}(a)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{T}\frac{dt}{T}\braket{u_{\alpha}(a,t)|\partial_{a}|u_{\beta}(a,t)}.

(17)

Here,
\partial_{a}\equiv\partial/\partial a
, and we have assumed that
\ket{u_{\alpha}(a,t)}
are differentiable for
a
by requiring the gauge-fixing condition
\braket{u_{\alpha}(a,t)|\partial_{a}|u_{\alpha}(a,t)}=0
.
Equation (
16
) means that the “Hamiltonian” in the extended (Sambe
Sambe 1973
) space has the quasienegies in its diagonal elements, and
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha\beta}
causes transitions between the Floquet states.

We remark an ambiguity in expanding the physical state
\ket{\Psi(t)}
in terms of the Floquet replica index
l
.
To work in the IFS, we fix the initial values of
c_{\alpha}(t)
by
\ket{\Psi(t_{\mathrm{ini}})}=\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}(t_{\mathrm{ini}})\ket{u(a(t_{\mathrm{ini}}),t_{\mathrm{ini}})}
, which has an infinite number of solutions due to the Floquet replicas.
However, when we calculate physical observables such as
|\braket{\uparrow|\Psi(t_{\mathrm{fin}})}|^{2}
,
the results do not depend on which initial condition is used
Drese and Holthaus 1999
.
Intuitively, this independence is based on the fact that the ambiguity happens only between the physically-equivalent states.
Thus, in the following, we assume that only one
l
is weighted in the initial condition.

This formalism helps us to interpret physical results.
Following Ref.
Holthaus 2015
,
let us interpret how
P_{\uparrow}
suddenly grows up at
a_{0}\sim 1.0
as
a_{0}
increases from zero at, e.g.,
b=2.5
.
Initially
(t\to-\infty)
, our state is
\ket{\downarrow}
, and the Floquet states there coincide with the energy eigenstates
\ket{\uparrow}
and
\ket{\downarrow}
since the coupling vanishes
a(t=-\infty)=0
.
Thus, we can set the initial state in the extended space as
c_{m=2,l=0}(-\infty)=1
and
c_{\alpha}(-\infty)=0
for
\alpha\neq(2,0)
.
Then, this state evolves according to Eq. (
15
), where the coupling envelope
a(t)
slowly varies.
Graphically, our initial state lies at a single left-end of a solid line in Fig.
2
, and it goes right as
a
increases with time.
This evolution is adiabatic and transitions between
\alpha
’s are unlikely as long as
-i\frac{da}{dt}\mathcal{G}_{\alpha\beta}(a(t))
in Eq. (
16
) is negligible.
For slowly varying
a(t)
, this condition breaks down at the first avoided crossing point
a=a_{\mathrm{AC},1}\sim 1.0
, where a part of the wave function is transferred to another state represented by a dashed line.
Therefore, for
a_{0}<a_{\mathrm{AC},1}
, the whole dynamics is adiabatic, the final state is almost the same as the initial state, and
P_{\uparrow}\sim 0
.
Meanwhile, for
a_{0}>a_{\mathrm{AC},1}
, the state experiences transitions twice, the final state is a superposition of the states on the solid and dashed lines in Fig.
2
, and
P_{\uparrow}
becomes nonvanishing.
This is the IFS interpretation for the sudden increase of
P_{\uparrow}
along e.g.
b=2.5
in Fig.
1
.

III.2
Transfer Matrices

One is naturally led to the question: Does the IFS viewpoint allow us to understand the whole complex structure in Fig.
1
?
To the authors’ knowledge, although the Landau-Zener-like transition probability at a single passage of an avoided crossing was analyzed
Drese and Holthaus 1999
, the interference pattern has not been well studied.
Our aim is to introduce the Landau-Zener-Stückelberg transfer matrix method in the extended space and to show that the IFS formalism is very powerful even quantitatively.

As we discussed at the end of Sec.
III.1
, the “Hamiltonian”
\mathcal{H}(a(t))
depends on time through the envelope
a(t)
, and its eigenvalues (quasienergies) form avoided crossings.
In applying the transfer matrix method to the IFS (see Appendix
A
for this method in the conventional sense), we need two generalizations: (i) there are quasienergy replicas of avoided crossings and (ii) the system passes avoided crossings multiple times in
-\infty<t<\infty
when the pulse peak
a_{0}
is large.

Suppose that there are
N

(>0)
avoided crossing points denoted by
\{a_{\mathrm{AC},n}\}_{n=1}^{N}
below the pulse peak height
a_{0}
and they are in ascending order:
0<a_{\mathrm{AC},1}<a_{\mathrm{AC},2}<\dots<a_{\mathrm{AC},N}<a_{0}
.
For example, we have
N=2
for
a_{0}=3.5
in Fig.
2
.
Correspondingly, we define the crossing times
t_{\mathrm{AC},n}

(>0)
by

\displaystyle a(t_{\mathrm{AC},n})=a_{0}f(t_{\mathrm{AC},n})=a_{\mathrm{AC},n}\quad(n=1,2,\dots,N).

(18)

For simplicity, we assume that the envelope is even,
f(-t)=f(t)
,
and monotonically decreasing in
t\geq 0
as the Gaussian envelope is.
Then, the crossings happen also at
t=-t_{\mathrm{AC},n}

(n=1,2,\dots,N)
, and we have

\displaystyle-t_{\mathrm{AC},1}<\dots<-t_{\mathrm{AC},N}<0<t_{\mathrm{AC},N}<\dots<t_{\mathrm{AC},1}.

(19)

At the
n
-th crossing point, the transfer matrix
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, which will be defined in Eq. (
21
), connects the state vectors before and after the crossing as

\displaystyle\vec{c}\,(t=t_{\mathrm{AC},n}^{+})=\mathcal{T}_{n}\vec{c}\,(t=t_{\mathrm{AC},n}^{-}).

(20)

Here
\vec{c}(t)
is the vector notation for
c_{\alpha}(t)
’s,
t_{\mathrm{AC},n}^{\pm}\equiv t_{\mathrm{AC},n}\pm 0
(we will also use
-t_{\mathrm{AC},n}^{\pm}\equiv-t_{\mathrm{AC},n}\pm 0
), and
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is an infinite-dimensional matrix given as follows.
The avoided crossing occurs between a pair of Floquet states, which we label as
(m_{U},l_{U})
and
(m_{L},l_{L})
.
Here, the subscript
U
(
L
) denotes the upper (lower) levels at the crossing.
For example,
(m_{U},m_{L})=(2,1)
and
(1,2)
at the first and second crossings, respectively, in Fig.
2
.
In this notation, the nonzero matrix elements of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
are given as

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{1-P_{n}}e^{-i\varphi^{S}_{n}}&-\sqrt{P_{n}}\\
\sqrt{P_{n}}&\sqrt{1-P_{n}}e^{i\varphi^{S}_{n}}\end{pmatrix}_{\alpha\beta},

(21)

where
\alpha=(m_{U},l_{U})
and
(m_{L},l_{L})
correspond to the first and second rows, respectively.
The two parameters
P_{n}
and
\varphi_{n}^{S}
are the Landau-Zener transition probability and the Stokes phase for the
n
-th avoided crossing (see Sec.
A
),

\displaystyle P_{n}

\displaystyle=\exp(-2\pi\delta_{n}),

(22)

\displaystyle\delta_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta_{n}^{2}}{4v_{n}},

(23)

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}^{S}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi}{4}+\delta_{n}\ln(\delta_{n}-1)+\arg\Gamma(1-i\delta_{n}).

(24)

Here,
\Delta_{n}
and
v_{n}
are the quasienergy gap and the passing speed at the
n
-th avoided crossing, respectively.
These parameters are defined in the approximate form of the pair quasienergies near
t=t_{\mathrm{AC},n}

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\alpha}(a(t))

\displaystyle\simeq\mathrm{const.}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{\Delta_{n}}{2}\right)^{2}+\left[\frac{v_{n}(t-t_{\mathrm{AC},n})}{2}\right]^{2}}

(25)

\displaystyle\simeq\mathrm{const.}\pm\left[\frac{\Delta_{n}}{2}+\frac{v_{n}^{2}(t-t_{\mathrm{AC},n})^{2}}{4\Delta_{n}}\right]

(26)

for
\alpha=(m_{U},l_{U})
and
(m_{L},l_{L})
.
Note that
\Delta_{n}
and
v_{n}
are well-defined in that they are the same for every Floquet replica.

To obtain
\Delta_{n}
and
v_{n}
in practice, we expand
\epsilon_{\alpha}(a)
around
a=a_{\mathrm{AC},n}
.
Since
d\epsilon_{\alpha}(a)/da
vanishes at the avoided crossing, we have the following second-order series expansion

\epsilon_{\alpha}(a(t))\simeq\epsilon_{\alpha}(a_{\mathrm{AC},n})+\frac{1}{2}(d^{2}\epsilon_{\alpha}(a)/da^{2})(da/dt)^{2}(t-t_{\mathrm{AC},n})^{2}
,
where
d^{2}\epsilon_{\alpha}(a)/da^{2}
and
da/dt
are evaluated at
a=a_{\mathrm{AC},n}
and
t=t_{\mathrm{AC},n}
, respectively.
Comparing this with Eq. (
26
), we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{(m_{U},l_{U})}(a_{\mathrm{AC},n})-\epsilon_{(m_{L},l_{L})}(a_{\mathrm{AC},n}),

(27)

\displaystyle v_{n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\Delta_{n}\left|\frac{d^{2}\epsilon_{\alpha}(a)}{da^{2}}\right|}\left|\frac{da}{dt}\right|,

(28)

where
\alpha
is either
(m_{U},l_{U})
or
(m_{L},l_{L})
that give the same
|d^{2}\epsilon_{\alpha}(a)/da^{2}|
.
One can obtain these parameters by numerical fitting as we will implement in Sec.
IV
or by analytical calculations for some special cases as we will demonstrate in Appendix
C
.

Except for the crossing points, the evolution is assumed to be mere phase acquisitions due to the first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
16
).
In the vector notation, we have

\displaystyle\vec{c}\,(t=t_{\mathrm{AC},n}^{-})=\mathcal{U}_{n,n+1}\vec{c}\,(t=t_{\mathrm{AC},n+1}^{+}),

(29)

where
\mathcal{U}_{n,n+1}
is diagonal and

\displaystyle(\mathcal{U}_{n,n+1})_{\alpha\alpha}=\exp\left[-i\int_{t_{\mathrm{AC},n+1}}^{t_{\mathrm{AC},n}}ds\epsilon_{\alpha}(a(s))\right].

(30)

For convenience, we define
(\mathcal{U}_{N,N+1})_{\alpha\alpha}=\exp\left[-i\int_{0}^{t_{\mathrm{AC},N}}ds\epsilon_{\alpha}(a(s))\right]
.

Since we are considering a symmetric envelope
a(-t)=a(t)
, time evolution is symmetric in
-\infty<t\leq 0
and
0\leq t<\infty
.
The transfer matrix
\mathcal{T}_{n}
describes the state transfer both at
t=\pm t_{\mathrm{AC},n}
, and

\mathcal{U}_{n+1,n}
represents the phase acquisition not only from
t_{\mathrm{AC},n+1}
to
t_{\mathrm{AC},n}
but also from
-t_{\mathrm{AC},n}
to
-t_{\mathrm{AC},n+1}
.
Thus, we obtain the state transfer between the first and final avoided crossings,

\displaystyle\vec{c}\,(t=t_{\mathrm{AC},1}^{+})=\left[\prod_{n=N}^{1}\mathcal{T}_{n}\mathcal{U}_{n,n+1}\right]\left[\prod_{n=1}^{N}\mathcal{U}_{n,n+1}\mathcal{T}_{n}\right]\vec{c}\,(t=-t_{\mathrm{AC},1}^{-}).

(31)

The entire dynamics is obtained by the phase acquisitions before (after) the first (final) avoided crossing:

\vec{c}(t=-t_{\mathrm{AC},1}^{-})=\mathcal{U}_{<}\vec{c}(t=t_{\mathrm{ini}})
and
\vec{c}(t=t_{\mathrm{fin}})=\mathcal{U}_{>}\vec{c}(t=t_{\mathrm{AC},1}^{+})
, where
(\mathcal{U}_{<})_{\alpha\alpha}=\exp\left[-i\int_{t_{\mathrm{ini}}}^{-t_{\mathrm{AC},1}}ds\epsilon_{\alpha}(a(s))\right]
and
(\mathcal{U}_{>})_{\alpha\alpha}=\exp\left[-i\int_{t_{\mathrm{AC},1}}^{t_{\mathrm{fin}}}ds\epsilon_{\alpha}(a(s))\right]
with
t_{\mathrm{ini}}=-\infty
and
t_{\mathrm{fin}}=+\infty
.

Thus, we have obtained the whole evolution of wave vector
\vec{c}(t)
in the IFS based on the transfer matrix method.
The physical interpretation is clear in Eq. (
31
).
The wave vector experiences adiabatic dynamics described by the phase factors
\mathcal{U}_{n,n+1}
and Landau-Zener-like diabatic dynamics described by the transfer matrices
\mathcal{T}_{n}
.
The phase factors due to the Stokes phase in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
and
\mathcal{U}_{n,n+1}
amount to the Stückelberg phase and cause interferences as we will see in the following.

Finally, we formulate how to calculate the physical observable
P_{\uparrow}
of interest from
\vec{c}
.
By using Eq. (
14
), we have

\displaystyle P_{\uparrow}

\displaystyle=|\braket{\uparrow|\Psi(t_{\mathrm{fin}})}|^{2}

(32)

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}(t_{\mathrm{fin}})\braket{\uparrow|u_{\alpha}(a(t_{\mathrm{fin}}),t_{\mathrm{fin}}}\right|^{2}.

(33)

We recall Eq. (
11
) and suppose that
t_{\mathrm{fin}}\to\infty
, in which
\ket{u_{m}(a(t_{\mathrm{fin}})),t_{\mathrm{fin}}}\to\ket{u_{m}(0,\infty)}=\delta_{m1}\ket{\uparrow}+\delta_{m2}\ket{\downarrow}
.
Thus, the sum over
\alpha=(m,l)
in Eq. (
33
) is trivially taken for
m
, and we have

\displaystyle P_{\uparrow}=\left|\sum_{l}c_{1,l}(t_{\mathrm{fin}})e^{il\omega t_{\mathrm{fin}}}\right|^{2}.

(34)

Note that
\vec{c}(t_{\mathrm{fin}})
is connected to
\vec{c}(t_{\mathrm{AC},1}^{+})
by

c_{m,l}(t_{\mathrm{fin}})=\exp[-i\int_{t_{\mathrm{AC},1}}^{t_{\mathrm{fin}}}\epsilon_{m,l}(a(s))ds]c_{m,l}(t_{\mathrm{AC},1}^{+})=\exp[-i\int_{t_{\mathrm{AC},1}}^{t_{\mathrm{fin}}}\epsilon_{m}(a(s))ds]e^{-il\omega(t_{\mathrm{fin}}-t_{\mathrm{AC},1})}c_{m,l}(t_{\mathrm{AC},1}^{+})
, where we have used Eq. (
12
).
Substituting this equation into Eq. (
34
), we obtain

\displaystyle P_{\uparrow}=\left|\sum_{l}c_{1,l}(t_{\mathrm{AC},1}^{+})e^{il\omega t_{\mathrm{AC},1}}\right|^{2}.

(35)

Equation (
35
) is useful since we can compute the excitation probability
P_{\uparrow}
just after the final passage of the avoided crossing, and
\vec{c}(t_{\mathrm{AC},1}^{+})
is given in Eq. (
31
).

We remark that, in Eq. (
31
),
we can set
c_{m=2,l=0}(-t_{\mathrm{AC},1}^{-})=1
and
c_{\alpha}(-t_{\mathrm{AC},1}^{-})=0
for
\alpha\neq(2,0)
.
These conditions are what we imposed for
t=t_{\mathrm{ini}}
at the end of Sec.
III.1
.
Nonetheless, the evolution between
t=t_{\mathrm{ini}}
and
-t_{\mathrm{AC},1}
merely gives an overall phase factor, which is irrelevant for
P_{\uparrow}
.

To summarize the transfer matrix method for the IFS, our recipe for obtaining
P_{\uparrow}
consists of using
\vec{c}(-t_{\mathrm{AC},1}^{-})
thus specified, transferring the state by Eq. (
31
), and invoking Eq. (
35
).
While this method is an approximation, its physical interpretation is clear in that the evolution is a close analog of the Landau-Zener-Stückelberg interferometry on the Floquet states.
In the following, we will implement this recipe and show that it works well even quantitatively.

V
Pulse-width dependence

In Sec.
IV
, we fixed the pulse width as
\nu=6
.
Meanwhile, experimentally, stronger peak amplitudes
a_{0}
tend to be realized for shorter pulse widths
Brabec and Krausz 2000
.
Thus, it is crucially important how small
\nu
can be for the FLZ theory remaining applicable.
Naively speaking, the FLZ theory is expected to become worse for shorter pulses because the envelope’s temporal change
da/dt
increases, and the assumption of adiabaticity eventually breaks down (see also Eq. (
16
)).
Note that this tendency also holds for increasing amplitude
a_{0}
as
da/dt
increases with
a_{0}
as well.

Excitation probability
P_{\uparrow}
for
b=2.5

. The solid and dashed curves show

) numerically and by invoking the FLZ theory (

).

To address this issue of pulse-width dependence, we examine the FLZ theory’s applicability with decreasing
\nu
.
Figure
6
shows
P_{\uparrow}
for
b=2.5
obtained by the exact numerical simulation of the TDSE (
5
) and by the FLZ method for different
\nu
’s.
For
\nu=2,3
, and 4, we observe that the FLZ theory captures quite well the exact results within the adiabatic-impulse approximation except for the regions near the avoided crossing points
a=a_{\mathrm{AC},1}=1.09
and
a_{\mathrm{AC},2}=3.05
.

We note that these regions of disagreement become wider for smaller
\nu
, which is consistent with the following intuitive argument on adiabaticity.
Our FLZ theory within the adiabatic-impulse approximation assumes that no transition occurs between IFSs except
a=a_{\mathrm{AC},n}

(n=1,2,\dots)
.
However, this assumption is valid when the quasienergy difference is much larger than the perturbation term (
\mathcal{G}
in Eq. (
16
)) proportional to
da/dt
.
Thus, this assumption is not satisfied near the avoided crossings where the quasienergy differences become small.
Also, this tendency is stronger for shorter pulses having larger
da/dt
.
Transitions between IFSs actually occur slightly away from the avoided crossings, while the adiabatic-impulse approximation neglects them entirely.
Nevertheless, it is remarkable that the FLZ theory works quantitatively well and has wide-enough applicability parameter regimes even if the pulse is as short as 2-cycle (
\nu=2
).

For the single-cycle pulse (
\nu=1
), the FLZ theory does not agree well with the numerically exact results as seen in the range of
a_{\mathrm{AC},1}<a_{0}<a_{\mathrm{AC},2}
, indicating that the FLZ theory does not provide a good physical interpretation.
There are two possible reasons for the breakdown of the FLZ theory in
\nu<2
:
(i) the adiabatic impulse approximation becomes inaccurate, and
(ii) transitions to distant Floquet replicas become nonnegligible.
As for (i), we notice that
\eta\equiv 1/\nu
serves as the adiabatic parameter (see Appendix
D.1
for details).
Since the adiabatic impulse approximation is justified in two-level systems by power-series arguments for
\eta
and is accurate for small
\eta

Drese and Holthaus 1999
;
Berry 1990
, it is natural that the approximation here starts to fail at
\eta\sim 1
, i.e.,
\nu\sim 1
, although their precise values should depend on models.
The other possibility (ii) is unlikely because the transition matrix elements
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha\beta}(a)
between IFSs are actually small compared to the quasienergy difference
\epsilon_{\alpha}(a)-\epsilon_{\beta}(a)
(see Appendix
D.2
for details).
Thus, the possibility (i) is likely to be the reason for the FLZ’s failure at the ultrashort pulse as short as
\nu\sim 1
in our model.

Despite this argument on the FLZ theory’s failure for ultrashort pulses, we leave for future work to determine the precise value of
\nu
for the breakdown and to fully understand why this theory works even down to
\nu=2
.
It is worth noting that the FLZ theory seems to work for
a_{0}>a_{\mathrm{AC},2}
at
\nu=1
, even with the disagreement in
a_{0}<a_{\mathrm{AC},2}
, for which the authors have not found its reason.
Systematically studying the FLZ in such an ultrashort-pulse regime is an open problem.

VI
Summary and Discussions

Considering the pulse excitation probability
P_{\uparrow}
in a two-level quantum system, we have studied the complex interference pattern in Fig.
1
in the two-dimensional space spanned by the pulse peak height
a_{0}
and the two levels’ energy difference
b
.
To understand these patterns, we have utilized the instantaneous Floquet states (IFS), rather than the original energy eigenstates, as a useful basis for understanding dynamics
Breuer and Holthaus 1989b
;
Drese and Holthaus 1999
.
The time evolution driven by strong pulse fields can then be regarded as adiabatic evolutions along the IFS and Landau-Zener-type (LZ-type) diabatic transitions between them
Holthaus 2015
.

We have developed this idea quantitatively by applying the transfer matrix method among Floquet states, formulated how to keep track of quantum states under multiple LZ-type transitions, and termed this formulation as the Floquet-Landau-Zener (FLZ) theory in Sec.
III
.
Implementing this theory numerically in Sec.
IV
(and analytically in Appendix.
C
), we have shown that the FLZ theory well reproduces
P_{\uparrow}
obtained by direct numerical calculations.
One advantage of the FLZ theory is that the physical interpretation of dynamics is transparent; The complex interference patterns in
P_{\uparrow}
originate from quantum path interference between IFSs as illustrated in Figs.
4
and
5
.

We have demonstrated that the FLZ theory is valid for longer pulses (i.e., larger
\nu
).
This is a natural tendency because the longer pulses mean slower changes of pulse envelopes
a(t)
, validating the adiabatic approximation.
Rather surprisingly, however, the FLZ theory has worked in appropriate parameter ranges if the pulse width is larger roughly than 2 cycles (
\nu\gtrsim 2
) as shown in Sec.
V
.
This should be relevant for experimental studies to address Floquet-related physics, which emerge ideally under strong continuous external fields while strong laser fields experimentally tend to be realized in short pulses.
Our findings imply that Floquet-related physics are present even in short-pulse experiments if we interpret appropriately in the sense of IFSs.
For the extension of the Floquet formalism to the case not strictly time-periodic external fields,
one of the present authors and Mizumoto
Kayanuma and Mizumoto 2000
studied the transition dynamics in the two-level system
under the level-crossing with a constant velocity plus time-periodic modulation in the relative energy.
An unexpected agreement has been observed between the calculation by the Floquet-Landau-Zener transfer matrix
method and the numerical solutions of the TDSE for a wide range of parameter values.
A deep understanding of this success is also an open question.

As concluding remarks, we list some future directions.
First, it is important to validate the transfer matrix methods in the Floquet extended space and to improve the adiabatic-impulse approximation systematically.
For the conventional Landau-Zener problem, the so-called Stokes phenomena are known to underlie
Davis and Pechukas 1976
, and WKB theories
Taya et al. 2021b
provide mathematical foundations.
One could generalize these insights to the Floquet extended space and validate the FLZ theory mathematically.
Second, it is intriguing to realize the FLZ interferometry in an experiment.
Our model should apply to any two-level systems, but there are two experimental challenges: (i) a long-enough coherence time and (ii) a strong-enough coupling to external fields with long-enough pulses.
In the present work, we neglected any decoherence/dissipation, which, if strong, may destroy the clear interference pattern.
It is intriguing to investigate if we can overcome those effects experimentally together with further theoretical investigations.
Finally, it is interesting to generalize the FLZ interferometry for other classes of systems with more than two levels
Holthaus and Just 1994
, including multiple two-level systems
Niranjan et al. 2020
.
Generally speaking, the denser the energy levels are, the worse the adiabatic approximation becomes.
Thus, we expect that the FLZ theory works in systems with not-so-many levels.
However, it could be possible to apply this theory for condensed-matter systems with many energy levels but an energy gap above the ground state.
We leave the above problems open for future studies.

Appendix A
Transfer matrix in the Landau-Zener problem

To supplement the discussions of transfer matrices for IFSs in the main text, we briefly review the transfer matrix for the seminal Landau-Zener problem for the linearly-time-dependent Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{LZ}}(t)=-(vt/2)\sigma_{z}+(\Delta/2)\sigma_{x}=\sum_{n=1}^{2}E_{n}(t)\ket{E_{n}(t)}\bra{E_{n}(t)}
with eigenenergies
E_{1}(t)\geq E_{2}(t)
.
As shown in the level diagram in Fig.
7
(a), from
t=-\infty
to
+\infty
, the system goes across, at
t=0
, the avoided crossing of gap
\Delta=E_{1}(0)-E_{2}(0)
at speed
v
.

Suppose that the initial state is given as
\ket{\Phi(t_{\mathrm{ini}})}
(
t_{\mathrm{ini}}<0
) and we are to solve its evolution
\ket{\Phi(t)}
and ask the population at the upper state
w(t)=|\braket{E_{1}(t)|\Phi(t)}|^{2}
.
This population can be obtained analytically
Berry 1990
;
Shevchenko et al. 2010
or numerically as illustrated in Fig.
7
(b), where
\Delta=5
,
v=10
,
t_{\mathrm{ini}}=-10
, and
\ket{\Phi(t_{\mathrm{ini}})}=\ket{E_{2}(t_{\mathrm{ini}})}
.

Figure 7:
(a) Eigenvalues of
H_{\mathrm{LZ}}(t)
(see also text). (b) Upper-state populations
w(t)
calculated by the exact numerics (solid) and by the transfer matrix method within the adiabatic-impulse approximation (dashed). In both panels, we set
v=10
and
\Delta=5
.

The transfer matrix method enables us to obtain an approximate solution with clear physical interpretation.
This method is based on the energy eigenbasis
\ket{\Phi(t)}=\sum_{n=1}^{2}b_{n}(t_{\mathrm{ini}})\ket{E_{n}(t_{\mathrm{ini}})}
and the assumption that no transition occurs away from the avoided crossing point
t=0
.
This assumption is known as the adiabatic-impulse approximation
Shevchenko et al. 2010
.
With this method, we have, for
n=1
and
2
,
b_{n}(t<0)=\exp[-i\int_{t_{\mathrm{ini}}}^{t}dsE_{n}(s)]b_{n}(t_{\mathrm{ini}})
and
b_{n}(t>0)=\exp[-i\int_{t_{\mathrm{ini}}}^{t}dsE_{n}(s)]b_{n}(0+)
.
At the avoided crossing, the state experiences transitions described by the following transfer matrix,

\displaystyle\bm{b}(0+)=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{1-P}e^{-i\varphi^{S}}&-\sqrt{P}\\
\sqrt{P}&\sqrt{1-P}e^{i\varphi^{S}}\end{pmatrix}\bm{b}(0-),

(40)

where

\displaystyle P\equiv\exp(-2\pi\delta)

(41)

is the prominent Landau-Zener transition probability with

\displaystyle\delta

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\Delta^{2}}{4v},

(42)

and

\displaystyle\varphi^{S}

\displaystyle\equiv-\frac{\pi}{4}+\delta\ln(\delta-1)+\arg\Gamma(1-i\delta),

(43)

is the Stokes phase with
\Gamma(z)
being the gamma function.
As illustrated in Fig.
7
(b), the transfer matrix method well captures the exact solution except in the vicinity of the avoided crossing.
While the actual dynamics is complicated within the avoided crossing region, it well describes, as a single matrix, the integrated evolution from the input to the output.

The transfer matrix method applies not only to
H_{\mathrm{LZ}}(t)
of linear time dependence but also to other Hamiltonians of more generic dependence
Shevchenko et al. 2010
.
For generic cases, we define
v
and
\Delta
by linearly approximating the energy eigenvalues near the avoided crossing (see, e.g., Ref.
Kayanuma and Mizumoto 2000
).
This generality enables us to apply this method for the IFS of our interest.

Appendix B
Derivation of Eq. (
15
)

Here we derive Eq. (
15
) by substituting Eq. (
14
) into the Schrödinger equation
id\ket{\Psi(t)}/dt=H_{\mathrm{pulse}}(t)\ket{\Psi(t)}
.
Note that
H_{\mathrm{pulse}}(t)=H_{\mathrm{cw}}(a(t),t)
by definition, and the Schrödinger equation reads

\displaystyle i\frac{d\ket{\Psi(t)}}{dt}=H_{\mathrm{cw}}(a(t),t)\ket{\Psi(t)}.

(44)

The left-hand of Eq. (
44
) side becomes

\displaystyle i\frac{d\ket{\Psi(t)}}{dt}=i\sum_{\alpha}\left[\frac{dc_{\alpha}(t)}{dt}\ket{u_{\alpha}(a(t),t)}+c_{\alpha}(t)\frac{da}{dt}\frac{\partial\ket{u_{\alpha}(a(t),t)}}{\partial a}+c_{\alpha}(t)\frac{\partial\ket{u_{\alpha}(a(t),t)}}{\partial t}\right].

(45)

Here we recall the definition of Floquet states,

i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}[e^{-i\epsilon_{\alpha}(a(t))t}\ket{u_{\alpha}(a(t),t)}]=H_{\mathrm{cw}}(a(t),t)[e^{-i\epsilon_{\alpha}(a(t))t}\ket{u_{\alpha}(a(t),t)}]
,
which implies

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial\ket{u_{\alpha}(a(t),t)}}{\partial t}=[-\epsilon_{\alpha}(a(t))+H_{\mathrm{cw}}(a(t),t)]\ket{u_{\alpha}(a(t),t)}.

(46)

Then we substitute Eq. (
45
) together with Eq. (
46
) into Eq. (
44
), obtaining

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha}\left[i\frac{dc_{\alpha}(t)}{dt}-\epsilon_{\alpha}(a(t))\right]\ket{u_{\alpha}(a(t),t)}

\displaystyle+i\sum_{\alpha}c_{\alpha}(t)\frac{da}{dt}\frac{\partial\ket{u_{\alpha}(a(t),t)}}{\partial a}=0,

(47)

where the terms with
H_{\mathrm{cw}}
canceled out between the left- and right-hand sides.

Finally, to rewrite the second term of Eq. (
47
), we note the following relations

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ket{u_{\alpha}(a(t),t)}}{\partial a}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}dt^{\prime}\delta_{T}(t-t^{\prime})\frac{\partial\ket{u_{\alpha}(a(t),t^{\prime})}}{\partial a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta}\int_{0}^{T}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{T}\ket{u_{\beta}(a(t),t)}\bra{u_{\beta}(a(t),t^{\prime})}\frac{\partial\ket{u_{\alpha}(a(t),t^{\prime})}}{\partial a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta}\ket{u_{\beta}(a(t),t)}\mathcal{G}_{\beta\alpha}(a(t)),

(48)

where we used Eqs. (
13
) and (
17
) to obtain the third and fourth lines, respectively.
Substituting Eq. (
48
) into Eq. (
47
) and considering the coefficients of each
\ket{u_{\alpha}(a(t),t)}
, we obtain

\displaystyle i\frac{dc_{\alpha}(t)}{dt}=\epsilon_{\alpha}(a(t))-i\frac{da}{dt}\sum_{\beta}\mathcal{G}_{\alpha\beta}(a(t))c_{\beta}(t),

(49)

which is equivalent to Eq. (
15
) in the matrix representation.

Appendix C

In this appendix, we consider the following coupling term

\displaystyle V(t)=\frac{1+\lambda}{2}\cos(\omega t)\sigma_{x}+\frac{1-\lambda}{2}\sin(\omega t)\sigma_{y},

(50)

which reduces to Eq. (
3
) for
\lambda=1
.
For a single spin-1/2, this term represents the Zeeman coupling
V(t)=\bm{B}(t)\cdot\bm{\sigma}
to an elliptically-polarized magnetic field
\bm{B}(t)=(\frac{1+\lambda}{2}\cos(\omega t),\frac{1-\lambda}{2}\sin(\omega t),0)

The dimensionless parameter

quantifies the ellipticity, and the special values
\lambda=0
and
1
correspond to the circular and linear polarizations, respectively.
Therefore, we call
\lambda=0
, 1, and the others as the linear, circular, and elliptic polarizations, respectively, even if the model does not necessarily suppose a single spin-1/2.

In the main text, we have shown that the FLZ theory works well for the linear polarization
(\lambda=1)
.
In those calculations, we implemented the transfer matrices
\mathcal{T}_{n}
and phase acquisition operators
\mathcal{U}_{n+1,n}
constructed from the quasienergies
\epsilon_{m}(a)
obtained numerically.
When
\lambda
is zero or small, however, we can analytically obtain the quasienergies approximately using the perturbation theory for
\lambda
.
In this appendix, using this analytical approach, we extend analyses and gain deeper insights from the limit of
\lambda=0
to small
\lambda
.

C.1
Circular polarization

We begin by considering the circular polarization (
\lambda=0
), for which the coupling term reads

\displaystyle V_{0}(t)=e^{-i\omega t}\sigma_{+}+e^{i\omega t}\sigma_{-}

(51)

with
\sigma_{\pm}=(\sigma_{x}\pm i\sigma_{y})/2
.
Note that this case corresponds to the rotating-wave approximation of Eq. (
3
).
In this special case, the continuous-wave problem (
8
) corresponds to the seminal Rabi model
Shirley 1965
.
We analytically obtain the two independent solutions as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{A}(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-i(\Omega+\frac{\omega}{2})t}}{\sqrt{2\Omega[\Omega+(\omega-b)/2]}}\begin{pmatrix}a/2\\
\left(\frac{\omega-b}{2}+\Omega\right)e^{i\omega t}\end{pmatrix},

(52)

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{B}(t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{i(\Omega+\frac{\omega}{2})t}}{\sqrt{2\Omega[\Omega-(\omega-b)/2]}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{a}{2}e^{-i\omega t}\\
\frac{\omega-b}{2}-\Omega\end{pmatrix},

(53)

where

\displaystyle\Omega=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{a^{2}+(b-\omega)^{2}}

(54)

is the Rabi frequency (energy).

We can read out the Floquet states and their quasienergies from Eqs. (
52
) and (
53
).
Recall that, in this work, we assign the Floquet states’ label
(m,l)
so that
(m,0)
approaches the undriven solutions
e^{-i(b/2)t}\ket{\uparrow}
(
e^{+i(b/2)t}\ket{\downarrow}
) for
m=1
(
m=2
).
To make this assignments, it is convenient to consider the two cases,
b>\omega
and
b<\omega
, separately.
For
b>\omega
,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{A}(t)}\to e^{-i(b/2)t}\ket{\uparrow},\ \ket{\psi_{B}(t)}\to e^{+i(b/2)t}\ket{\downarrow}

(55)

in the limit of
a\to 0
.
Thus, in this case, we see that

\displaystyle\ket{u_{1}(a;t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Omega[\Omega+(\omega-b)/2]}}\begin{pmatrix}a/2\\
\left(\frac{\omega-b}{2}+\Omega\right)e^{i\omega t}\end{pmatrix},

(56)

\displaystyle\ket{u_{2}(a;t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Omega[\Omega-(\omega-b)/2]}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{a}{2}e^{-i\omega t}\\
\frac{\omega-b}{2}-\Omega\end{pmatrix},

(57)

and all the quasienergies (
12
) are

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1,l}(a)

\displaystyle=+\Omega+\frac{\omega}{2}+l\omega,

(58)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2,l}(a)

\displaystyle=-\Omega-\frac{\omega}{2}+l\omega.

(59)

We plot the quasienergy for a representative off-resonant case
b=1.5
in Fig.
8
(a).
As the analytical expressions imply, there is no avoided crossing.

On the other hand, for
b<\omega
,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{A}(t)}\to e^{+i(b/2)t}\ket{\downarrow},\ \ket{\psi_{B}(t)}\to e^{i(\omega-b/2)t}\ket{\uparrow}

(60)

as
a\to 0
.
This means that

\displaystyle\ket{u_{1}(a;t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{i\omega t}}{\sqrt{2\Omega[\Omega-(\omega-b)/2]}}\begin{pmatrix}\frac{a}{2}e^{-i\omega t}\\
\frac{\omega-b}{2}-\Omega\end{pmatrix},

(61)

\displaystyle\ket{u_{2}(a;t)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Omega[\Omega+(\omega-b)/2]}}\begin{pmatrix}a/2\\
\left(\frac{\omega-b}{2}+\Omega\right)e^{i\omega t}\end{pmatrix},

(62)

and all the quasienergies (
12
) are

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1,l}(a)

\displaystyle=-\Omega+\frac{\omega}{2}+l\omega,

(63)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2,l}(a)

\displaystyle=+\Omega+\frac{\omega}{2}+l\omega.

(64)

On the resonance
b=\omega
,
\ket{\psi_{A}(t)}
and
\ket{\psi_{B}(t)}
do not converge to either
\ket{\uparrow}
or
\ket{\downarrow}
but to superpositions of them in the limit of
a\to 0
.
In fact, we have, for
b=\omega
,

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{A}(t)}

\displaystyle\to e^{-i(\omega/2)t}\frac{\ket{\uparrow}+\ket{\downarrow}}{\sqrt{2}},

(65)

\displaystyle\ket{\psi_{B}(t)}

\displaystyle\to e^{+i(\omega/2)t}\frac{\ket{\uparrow}-\ket{\downarrow}}{\sqrt{2}}

(66)

as
a\to 0
.
We will see that this special property on the resonance
b=\omega
leads to nontrivial behaviors of excitation probabilities
P_{\uparrow}
.
We plot the quasienergy for the resonant case
b=1
in Fig.
8
(b), where they are degenerate at
a=0
.

The property (i) is interpreted as follows.
Away from resonance, we have a quasienergy diagram like Fig.

as
a\to 0
.
In such a case, the IFS interpretation goes like at the end of Sec.

.
Our initial state for the pulse problem is
\ket{\downarrow}
, and it adiabatically moves along
\ket{u_{2}(a(t);t)}

\ket{\downarrow}
at

In the adiabatic move, there occurs no LZ-type transition to other branches of Floquet states, and finally the state comes back to
\ket{\downarrow}

P_{\uparrow}\approx 0
.

\lambda=0

P_{\uparrow}
for

in Fig.

(a).

The property (ii) is interpreted as follows.
On resonance, the Floquet states do not converge to either
\ket{\uparrow}
or
\ket{\downarrow}
but approach superpositions of them in
a\to 0
.
Thus, our initial state
\ket{\downarrow}
is a superposition of
\ket{u_{1}(a(t);t)}
and
\ket{u_{2}(a(t);t)}
at
t=-\infty
.
While these Floquet states move adiabatically without LZ-type transitions as there is no avoided crossing, they acquire relative phase factors due to the quasienergies.
Since the acquired phase from
t=-\infty
to
t=+\infty
is an increasing function of the pulse peak height
a_{0}
, the final excitation probability
P_{\uparrow}
oscillates with
a_{0}
.
This mechanism is FLZ interferometry discussed in Sec.
IV
although the superposition of Floquet states here is not created by LZ-type transitions but by particular limiting behaviors (
65
) and (
66
).

C.2
Elliptic polarization

In Appendix
C.1
, we have shown that
\lambda=0
is an ideal limit where the quasienergies are obtained analytically and no avoided crossing occurs.
For
\lambda\neq 0
,
V(t)
involves the counter-rotating component on top of Eq. (
51
):

\displaystyle V(t)

\displaystyle=V_{0}(t)+\lambda W(t),

(67)

\displaystyle W(t)

\displaystyle=e^{i\omega t}\sigma_{+}+e^{-i\omega t}\sigma_{-}.

(68)

This component hybridizes the independent solutions [Eqs. (
52
) and (
53
)] for
\lambda=0
, giving rise to avoided crossings of quasienergies.
To address this scenario analytically, we here consider the case of small
\lambda
’s, i.e., nearly-circular elliptic polarizations.
In these cases, we can use the Floquet states for
\lambda=0
(see Appendix
C.1
) as the unperturbed solutions and approximately obtain quasienergies with avoided crossings by perturbation theory in terms of
\lambda a_{0}
(see, e.g., Ref.
Ikeda et al. 2018
for technicalities).

We remark that this approach does not assume
a_{0}
is small.
In fact, the unperturbed solutions (Floquet states for
\lambda=0
) can involve nonperturbative effects of
a_{0}
.
Thus, this approach is particularly useful when

\displaystyle\lambda a_{0}\ll 1\ll a_{0}

(69)

since, for
a_{0}\ll 1
, we can apply the naive perturbation theory in terms of
a_{0}
for arbitrary
\lambda
.
We cannot find such parameters (
69
) for the linear polarization (
\lambda=1
) that we studied in the main text, where we needed to calculate quasienergies numerically.

.

, as

.

and

Let us now quantitatively apply the FLZ theory developed in Sec.
III
with its inputs, such as
a_{\mathrm{AC},n}
and
\epsilon_{m,l}(a)
, obtained analytically.
For concreteness, we first focus on
1<b<3
, for which the quasienergy diagram looks like Fig.

(a).
As shown in the figure, although the quasienegies
\epsilon_{1,l}(a)
and
\epsilon_{2,l+2}(a)
overlap at

, they do not repel.
This is a selection rule due to the fact that
W(t)
does not have matrix elements between these Floquet states,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\frac{dt}{T}\braket{u_{1,l}(a;t)|W(t)|u_{2,l+2}(a;t)}=0,

(70)

where
\ket{u_{m,l}(t)}=e^{il\omega t}\ket{u_{m}(t)}
with Eqs. (
56
) and (
57
).
More generally, similar selection rules follow from the fact that the matrix elements vanish between
(1,l)
and
(2,l+2k)
(
k\in\mathbb{Z}
), which means physically that
2k
-photon transitions are prohibited.
Therefore, the first avoided crossing occurs at the 3-photon resonance defined by
\epsilon_{1,l}(a)=\epsilon_{2,l+3}(a)
, which gives

\displaystyle a_{\mathrm{AC},1}=\sqrt{(\omega+b)(3\omega-b)}\qquad(\text{for}\ \omega<b<3\omega).

(71)

Note that we needed to calculate numerically
a_{\mathrm{AC},1}
for the linear polarization (
\lambda=1
) in Sec.

.

The quasienergies in the presence of small
\lambda
are obtained by considering the coupling by
\lambda W(t)
between the unperturbed Floquet states
\alpha=(1,l)
and
(2,l+3)
.
The Floquet Hamiltonian within the 2-dimensional subspace reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\alpha\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}\epsilon_{1,l}(a)&\lambda K\\
\lambda K&\epsilon_{2,l+3}(a)\end{pmatrix}_{\alpha\beta},

(72)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
denote either
(1,l)
or
(2,l+3)

and

\displaystyle K=\lambda\frac{a^{2}}{8\Omega}\sqrt{\frac{\Omega+\frac{\omega-b}{2}}{\Omega-\frac{\omega-b}{2}}}.

(73)

The eigenvalues of Eq. (
72
) lead to the quasienergies with avoided crossing:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{1,l}^{(\lambda)}(a;t)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\omega}{2}-\sqrt{\left(\frac{\epsilon_{1,l}(a)-\epsilon_{2,l+3}(a)}{2}\right)^{2}+(\lambda K)^{2}},

\displaystyle\epsilon_{2,l+3}^{(\lambda)}(a;t)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\omega}{2}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{\epsilon_{1,l}(a)-\epsilon_{2,l+3}(a)}{2}\right)^{2}+(\lambda K)^{2}}.

(74)

In the approximation made here, we have ignored couplings outside the 2-dimensional subspace, and Eq. (
74

\lambda

The quasienergy gap at the avoided crossing follows from Eq. (

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{2,l+3}^{(\lambda)}(a_{\mathrm{AC},1})-\epsilon_{1,l}^{(\lambda)}(a_{\mathrm{AC},1})

\displaystyle=\lambda\frac{\sqrt{(\omega+b)(3\omega-b)^{3}}}{4\omega},

(75)

where we used
\epsilon_{1,l}(a)=\epsilon_{2,l+3}(a)
and hence
\Omega=\omega
at
a=a_{\mathrm{AC},1}
.

Appendix D
Justification of the FLZ theory

D.1
Relation to the semiclassical limit

Here we show that the limit of
\nu\to 0
formally corresponds to the semiclassical limit
\hbar\to 0
.
For this purpose, we rewrite Eq. (
15
), highlighting the approach to adiabaticity.
Let us define
\hat{a}(t)=a_{0}\exp[-(t/T)^{2}]
, which coincides with
a(t)
at
\nu=1
and satisfies
a(t)=\hat{a}(t/\nu)=\hat{a}(\eta t)
.
Here,
\eta\equiv 1/\nu
is the adiabaticity parameter
Davis and Pechukas 1976
, and the
\eta\to 0
(
\nu\to\infty
) limit corresponds to the infinitely slowly varying envelope function.
Using
\hat{a}
and introducing the rescaled time
s\equiv\eta t
, we rewrite Eq. (
15
) as

\displaystyle i\eta\frac{d\hat{c}_{\alpha}(s)}{ds}=\sum_{\beta}\mathcal{H}_{\alpha\beta}(\hat{a}(s))\hat{c}_{\beta}(s)

(76)

with
\hat{c}_{\alpha}(s)\equiv c_{\alpha}(t/\eta)
.
Equation (
76
) shows that the adiabatic limit
\eta\to 0
is formally equivalent to the semiclassical limit
\hbar\to 0
.
Considering that the Landau-Zener-type transition derives from the series expansion for
\hbar
and its resummation
Drese and Holthaus 1999
;
Berry 1990
, we naturally expect that our FLZ theory works well when
\eta\ll 1
, i.e.,
\nu\gg 1
.

The above argument is, at least, consistent with our numerics in Fig.
6
, where the FLZ theory works well for
\nu\geq 2
but not for
\nu=1
.
However, characterizing the precise threshold, which should be model-dependent, and showing why it is about 2 in the present model remain open for future work.

D.2
Transitions to distant IFSs

The transfer matrix
\mathcal{T}_{n}
only mixes pairs of IFSs that have the nearest quasienergies
although the mixings can, in principle, happen between other IFS pairs that are more distant in the quasienergy.
These transitions neglected in our FLZ theory could be nonnegligible when the pulse width
\nu
becomes very small.
However, we show here that these transitions are not relevant in the present model.

To study this possibility quantitatively, we rewrite Eq. (
15
) so that the
\nu
-dependence is evident:

\displaystyle i\frac{d\tilde{c}_{\alpha}(\tau)}{d\tau}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\beta}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha\beta}(\tilde{a}(\tau))\tilde{c}_{\beta}(\tau),

(77)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{\alpha\beta}(\tilde{a}(\tau))

\displaystyle\equiv\nu T\delta_{\alpha\beta}\epsilon_{\alpha}(\tilde{a}(\tau))-i\frac{d\tilde{a}}{d\tau}\mathcal{G}_{\alpha\beta}(\tilde{a}(\tau)).

(78)

Here,
\tau\equiv\frac{t}{\nu T}
is a dimensionless time,
\tilde{c}_{\alpha}(\tau)=c_{\alpha}(\frac{t}{\nu T})
, and
\tilde{a}(\tau)=a(\frac{t}{\nu T})=a_{0}\exp(-\tau^{2})
.
In this representation,
\nu
effectively rescales the quasienergy as the first term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
78
).
For an IFS pair
(\alpha,\beta)
, their transition is negligible if their effective quasienergy difference
\nu T|\epsilon_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{\beta}|
is much larger than their coupling
|\frac{d\tilde{a}}{d\tau}\mathcal{G}_{\alpha\beta}(\tilde{a}(\tau))|
.
Our IFS theory neglects the pair transitions for
|\epsilon_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{\beta}|>\omega
, and this treatment is justified if

\displaystyle\nu\gg\nu_{c}\equiv\frac{1}{T}\max_{\tau}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta\\
|\epsilon_{\alpha}-\epsilon_{\beta}|>\omega\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{d\tilde{a}}{d\tau}\frac{\mathcal{G}_{\alpha\beta}(\tilde{a}(\tau))}{\epsilon_{\alpha}(a(\tau))-\epsilon_{\beta}(a(\tau))}\right|.

(79)

, we focus on the ratio

\displaystyle R_{\alpha\beta}(a)\equiv\frac{\mathcal{G}_{\alpha\beta}(a)}{[\epsilon_{\alpha}(a)-\epsilon_{\beta}(a)]T}

(80)

and verify

for

.
This ratio is shown in Fig.
