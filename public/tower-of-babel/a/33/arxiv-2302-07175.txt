What is the Seiberg–Witten map exactly?
Vladislav Kupriyanov, Alexey Sharapov

Abstract
We give a conceptual treatment of the Seiberg–Witten map as a quasi-isomorphism of A ∞ A_{\infty} -algebras.



1
Introduction

Based on several nontrivial examples, the authors of Ref.
[
1
]
suggested that
“there should be an underlying geometric reason for the Seiberg–Witten map”
. This paper resulted from our attempt to find such a reason. The answer we have arrived at, however, is purely algebraic rather than geometric.
Before going into details, let us recall the statement of the problem that the Seiberg–Witten map solves.

In the simplest situation, one considers a gauge vector field
\mathcal{A}
on an
n
-dimensional Minkowski space with coordinates
x^{i}
. The standard Maxwell’s equations for the gauge potential
\mathcal{A}
are obtained by varying the action functional

S[\mathcal{A}]=\int d^{n}x\mathcal{F}_{ij}\mathcal{F}^{ij}\,,

(1)

where
\mathcal{F}_{ij}=\partial_{i}\mathcal{A}_{j}-\partial_{j}\mathcal{A}_{i}
is the strength tensor of the electromagnetic field.
The action is invariant under the usual gauge transformations

\delta_{\lambda}\mathcal{A}_{i}=\partial_{i}\lambda\,,

(2)

\lambda
being the gauge parameter.

Suppose now that, in addition to the metric, the Minkowski space carries a constant bivector
\theta
. This allows one to endow the space of smooth functions of the
x
’s with the Weyl–Moyal
\ast
-product
1
1

1

As is customary for physical considerations we leave aside the question of convergence of the series.
:

(f\ast g)(x)=\lim_{y\rightarrow x}e^{\frac{1}{2}\theta^{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial y^{i}}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}}f(y)g(x)\,.

(3)

This product is known to be associative but not commutative unless
\theta=0
. In particular,

[x^{i},x^{j}]_{\ast}=\theta^{ij}
. Hence, a nonzero
\theta
brings in noncommutativity in the space-time that the gauge vector field lives on.
In order to distinguish between commutative and noncommutative cases, we will ‘put hats’ on fields living on the noncommutative Minkowski space.
Following the pattern of non-Abelian gauge theories, it is now quite natural to replace the infinitesimal gauge transformation (
2
) with

\hat{\delta}_{\hat{\lambda}}\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}=\partial_{i}\hat{\lambda}+[\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i},\hat{\lambda}]_{\ast}

(4)

and to define the gauge-covariant strength tensor by the familiar expression

\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{ij}=\partial_{i}\hat{\mathcal{A}_{j}}-\partial_{j}\hat{\mathcal{A}_{i}}+[\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i},\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{j}]_{\ast}\,.

(5)

Then,
\delta_{\hat{\lambda}}\hat{\mathcal{F}}=[\hat{\mathcal{F}},\hat{\lambda}]_{\ast}
. Unlike (
2
), the gauge transformations (
4
) do not commute to each other:

[\hat{\delta}_{\hat{\lambda}_{1}},\hat{\delta}_{\hat{\lambda}_{2}}]=\hat{\delta}_{[\hat{\lambda}_{1},\hat{\lambda}_{2}]_{\ast}}\,.

(6)

Finally, the
\ast
-product algebra of functions enjoys the trace functional

\mathrm{Tr}(f)=\int d^{n}xf(x)\,.

(7)

The main property of the trace reads

\mathrm{Tr}(f\ast g)=\mathrm{Tr}(g\ast f)=\int d^{n}xf(x)g(x)\,,

(8)

provided the functions
f
and
g
vanish at infinity fast enough.
With the trace, one can define the gauge invariant action
S[\hat{\mathcal{A}}]
by the same integral (
1
) with
\mathcal{F}
replaced by
\hat{\mathcal{F}}
. Because of the
\ast
-commutator in (
5
), the corresponding equations of motion are not linear anymore.

A remarkable fact established in
[
2
]
is that being dynamically different, the commutative and noncommutative field theories have essentially the same gauge structure. More specifically, there exists a bijective mapping between the spaces of fields
\mathcal{A}
and
\hat{\mathcal{A}}
that takes gauge equivalent configurations of fields to gauge equivalent ones. This by no means implies an isomorphism of the corresponding gauge algebras, regarded as infinite-dimensional Lie algebras. (Such an isomorphism is clearly impossible because one algebra is Abelian and the other is not.) It turns out that an appropriate geometric framework for understanding the above equivalence is that of Lie algebroids rather than Lie algebras. Here we will not dwell on this issue in detail, referring the interested reader to the papers
[
3
]
and
[
4
, Sec. 5]
. In a nutshell, one may allow the structure constants of the gauge algebra to depend on fields (though it is not our case). In this more general setting, it is legitimate to consider redefinitions of gauge fields that are accompanied by
field-dependent
redefinitions of gauge parameters. Such transformations form the natural automorphism group of a gauge theory in the Batalin–Vilkovisky (BV) formalism. Regarding now a linear representation of a Lie algebra as a particular case of Lie algebroids, one can consider the transformations

\hat{\mathcal{A}}=\hat{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{A})\,,\qquad\hat{\lambda}=\hat{\lambda}({\lambda,\mathcal{A}})\,.

(9)

The condition that gauge equivalent field configurations go into gauge equivalent ones leads to the equation
\delta_{{\lambda}}[\hat{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{A})]=\hat{\delta}_{\hat{\lambda}}\hat{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{A})
or, more explicitly,

\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}(\mathcal{A}+\partial\lambda)-\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}(\mathcal{A})=\partial_{i}\hat{\lambda}(\lambda,\mathcal{A})+[\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}(\mathcal{A}),\hat{\lambda}(\lambda,\mathcal{A})]_{\ast}\,,

(10)

with infinitesimal
\lambda
and
\hat{\lambda}
. Requiring further the gauge algebra to close off-shell, one also imposes the gauge consistency condition

\delta_{\lambda_{2}}\hat{\lambda}_{1}-\delta_{\lambda_{1}}\hat{\lambda}_{2}=[\hat{\lambda}_{1},\hat{\lambda}_{2}]_{\ast}-\hat{\lambda}([\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]_{\ast},\mathcal{A})\,,

(11)

where the variation
\delta_{\lambda_{2}}\hat{\lambda}_{1}
refers to the
\mathcal{A}
-dependence of
\hat{\lambda}(\lambda,\mathcal{A})
and the gauge transformation (
2
).
Geometrically, formulas (
10
,
11
) identify the transformation (
9
) as a morphism of two Lie algebroids associated with the action of the Abelian and non-Abelian Lie algebras on the space of fields
\mathcal{A}
. Any solution to Eqs. (
10
,
11
) with the initial condition

\hat{\mathcal{A}}=\mathcal{A}+O(\theta)\,,\qquad\hat{\lambda}=\lambda+O(\theta)

(12)

is called a Seiberg–Witten (SW) map.
The existence of SW maps is not obvious in advance. Original arguments in its support came from string-theoretic considerations
[
2
]
. It is easy to check that the following expressions satisfy the above equations up to the first order in
\theta
:

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{i}=\mathcal{A}_{i}-\frac{1}{2}\theta^{kj}\mathcal{A}_{k}(\partial_{j}\mathcal{A}_{i}+\mathcal{F}_{ji})+O(\theta^{2})\,,\\[8.53581pt]
\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}=\lambda+\frac{1}{2}\theta^{ij}\partial_{i}\lambda\mathcal{A}_{j}+O(\theta^{2})\,.\end{array}

(13)

In this paper, we give a refined algebraic explanation of the origin of the SW map, free from all inesential field-theoretic details. More concretely, we show that any SW map defines and is defined by a quasi-isomorphism of differential graded algebras (dg-algebras for short). The dg-algebras underlying the (non)commutative gauge theories above appear to be very simple and are discussed in the next section. Certainly, it is not the first time the term ‘quasi-isomorphism’ is mentioned in connection with SW-maps. Thus, in
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
, the problem of constructing SW maps was put in the framework of BV-BRST formalism. In that approach, each gauge theory is described by a BV master action on the ghost-extended space of fields and antifields. Expanding the master action in powers of antifields generates a sequence of multilinear operators, which can be regarded as the structure maps of a cyclic
L_{\infty}
-algebra. The defining relations of an
L_{\infty}
-algebra (a.k.a higher Jacobi identities) follow from the classical master equation. From this perspective, each SW map gives rise to a (quasi-)isomorphism of the corresponding
L_{\infty}
-algebras. In explicit terms, the relationship between SW maps and quasi-isomorphisms of
L_{\infty}
-algebras was explored in
[
9
]
.

Our setup is somewhat different. Instead of
L_{\infty}
-algebras, we work in the category of
A_{\infty}
-algebras, which seems more natural, as the
\ast
-product algebra (
3
) responsible for noncommutativity is associative. Our construction of quasi-isomorphism in no way involves the equations of motion, which are the main (and unavoidable) ingredient of the BV-BRST formalism. This leads to further conceptual simplification. The
L_{\infty}
-algebras, their quasi-isomorphisms and, eventually, SW maps are obtained by the standard antisymmetrization procedure from the corresponding
A_{\infty}
-algebras and their morphisms, much as the commutator in an associative algebra yields a Lie bracket. The quasi-isomorphism construction is recursive and results in explicit expressions for the field transformation (
9
). Since the recursion is on the degree of
\mathcal{A}
, the result appears to be nonperturbative in
\theta
, as opposed to the earlier approaches
[
2
,
1
,
10
,
11
]
. The importance of nonperturbative in
\theta
solutions (e.g. for studying instantons) was stressed in the original paper
[
2
]
. The algebraic treatment also provides the homotopy classification of ambiguities in SW maps, which we briefly address in Sec. 3.
All in all, we hope that the new conceptual understanding gained in this paper will be useful for further generalizations and applications of the SW map.
Some interesting possibilities are discussed in the concluding section.

Finally, a word about notation. Throughout the paper, we work over the field of reals
\mathbb{R}
,
though all our results are valid for any field of characteristic zero. All unadorned tensor products
\otimes
and
\mathrm{Hom}
’s are defined over
\mathbb{R}
.
When dealing with graded vector spaces and algebras, we tend to write down all sign factors explicitly. However, one can easily keep track of signs using the Koszul rule: whenever two graded objects
a
and
B
are swapped, the factor of
(-1)^{\deg a\cdot\deg B}
appears.

2
SW map as a quasi-isomorphism of dg-algebras

Let
A_{\theta}
denote a unital dg-algebra over
\mathbb{R}
generated by two sets of elements
x^{i}
and
\xi^{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, subject to the relations

x^{i}x^{j}-x^{j}x^{i}=\theta^{ij}\,,\qquad x^{i}\xi^{j}-\xi^{j}x^{i}=0\,,\qquad\xi^{i}\xi^{j}=0\,.

(14)

Here
\theta=(\theta^{ij})
is a real antisymmetric matrix. By definition,

\deg x^{i}=0\,,\qquad\deg\xi^{i}=1

(15)

and

dx^{i}=\xi^{i}\,,\qquad d\xi^{i}=0\,.

(16)

It follows from (
14
,
15
) that
A_{\theta}
is concentrated in degrees zero and one. In case
\theta=0
, the dg-algebra
(A_{0},d)
is obviously commutative. For any
\theta
the algebra
A_{\theta}
satisfies the Poincaré–Birkhoff–Witt (PBW) property: as an
\mathbb{R}
-vector space it is isomorphic to the space of polynomials
a(x,\xi)
that are at most linear in the
\xi
’s. So, the general element of
A_{\theta}
is represented by a polynomial

a=a_{0}(x)+a_{i}(x)\xi^{i}\,,

(17)

where
a_{0}(x)
and
a_{i}(x)
are polynomials in the
x
’s.
The product is then defined by the Weyl–Moyal type formula

(f\ast_{\theta}g)(x,\xi)=\mu\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\theta^{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}\otimes\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}\Big)^{n}f\otimes g\,,

(18)

where

\mu(f\otimes g)=p(fg)\,,

and
p
is the projection onto the subspace of polynomials at most linear in
\xi
’s. The polynomials (
17
) that are independent of the
\xi
’s form a subalgebra and those that are linear in
\xi
’s constitute a nilpotent ideal.

The cohomology algebra
H(A_{\theta})
is obviously
commutative and does not depend on
\theta
, i.e.,
H(A_{\theta})\simeq H(A_{\theta^{\prime}})
. In particular,
H^{0}(A_{\theta})=\mathbb{R}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For all antisymmetric matrices
\theta
and
\theta^{\prime}
the corresponding dg-algebras
(A_{\theta},d)
and
(A_{\theta^{\prime}},d)
are quasi-isomorphic.

Recall that two dg-algebras
A
and
B
are said to be quasi-isomorphic if there exists a zig-zag sequence of dg-algebra morphisms

A\leftarrow C_{1}\rightarrow C_{2}\leftarrow C_{3}\rightarrow\cdots\leftarrow C_{n}\rightarrow B

such that each morphism induces an isomorphism in cohomology. In particular, this means that
H(A)\simeq H(B)
. A dg-algebra
A
is called formal if it is quasi-isomorphic to its algebra of cohomology
H(A)
.

To prove Proposition
2.1
, let us define the operator
d^{\ast}:A_{\theta}\rightarrow A_{\theta}
of degree
-1
that acts on cochains (
17
) as

d^{\ast}a=x^{i}a_{i}(x)\,.

(19)

Then the algebra
H(A_{\theta})
is isomorphic to the subalgebra
Z=\ker d\cap\ker d^{\ast}\subset A_{\theta}
. The subalgebra
Z
, being commutative, does not depend on
\theta
. Hence, we have the zig-zag map

A_{\theta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\leftarrow}}Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i}}{{\rightarrow}}A_{\theta^{\prime}}\,,

(20)

where
i
stands for the natural embedding. This proves Proposition
2.1
. We also see that the algebra
A_{\theta}
is formal.

It is known that whenever two dg-algebras
A
and
B
are quasi-isomorphic they are quasi-isomorphic as
A_{\infty}
-algebras, meaning the existence of a single quasi-isomorphism
f:A\rightarrow B
. Let us recall some basic definitions.

Given a graded vector space
A=\bigoplus A_{n}
, define the shifted graded space
A[1]=\bigoplus A_{n+1}
, that is,
A[1]_{n}=A_{n+1}
. In order to avoid confusion we will denote the degree of a homogeneous element
a\in A[1]
by bar:

\bar{a}=\deg a-1\,.

(21)

(On the right,
a
is treated as an element of
A
.)

The bar coalgebra of
A
is given now by the direct sum

B(A)=\bigoplus_{l=1}^{\infty}B^{l}(A)\,,\qquad B^{l}(A)=A[1]^{\otimes l}\,,

(22)

endowed with the coproduct

\Delta(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{n})=\sum_{l=1}^{n-1}(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{l})\bigotimes(a_{l+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{n})\in B(A)\bigotimes B(A)\,.

(23)

Recall that a coderivation of a coalgebra
C
is a linear map
D:C\rightarrow C
such that

(D\otimes 1+1\otimes D)\Delta a=\Delta(Da)\qquad\forall a\in C\,.

(24)

The coderivations form a graded Lie algebra
\mathrm{Coder}(C)
w.r.t. the graded commutator.
As is well known, the space of coderivations of the coalgebra
B(A)
is isomorphic to the space

\mathrm{Hom}(B(A),A[1])=\bigoplus_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\mathrm{Hom}^{k}(B(A),A[1])\,,

so that to every
D\in\mathrm{Hom}(B^{l}(A),A[1])
there corresponds the coderivation of the form

\hat{D}(a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{n})=\sum_{i=0}^{n-l}\pm a_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{i}\otimes D(a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{i+l})\otimes\cdots\otimes a_{n}\,,

(25)

\pm
being the Koszul sign. A coderivation
\delta
of degree one is called a codifferential if
\delta^{2}=0
.
By definition, an
A_{\infty}
-algebra structure on a graded vector space
A
is a codifferential on
B(A)
.
In other words, an
A_{\infty}
-algebra is defined by an element
m\in\mathrm{Hom}^{1}(B(A),A[1])
such that
\hat{m}\hat{m}=0
. Expanding
m
in homogeneous components,
m=m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3}+\cdots
, we get a sequence of multilinear maps
m_{l}\in\mathrm{Hom}^{1}(B^{l}(A),A[1])
obeying the quadratic relations

\hat{m}_{1}\hat{m}_{1}=0\,,\qquad\hat{m}_{1}\hat{m}_{2}+\hat{m}_{2}\hat{m}_{1}=0\,,\qquad\ldots

(26)

In particular, for any dg-algebra
A
one can set
m_{1}(a)=da
,
m_{2}(a,b)=(-1)^{\bar{a}}ab
, and
m_{l}=0
for all
l>2
. Hence, each dg-algebra is an
A_{\infty}
-algebra for which only the two maps
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
may be different from zero.

If
(A,m)
and
(A^{\prime},m^{\prime})
are
A_{\infty}
-algebras, then a morphism between them is a morphism of differential graded coalgebras
F:B(A)\rightarrow B(A^{\prime})
. The morphism
F
is completely defined by a map
f\in\mathrm{Hom}^{0}(B(A),A^{\prime}[1])
, often called twisting cochain, obeying the relation

m^{\prime}_{1}(f)+m^{\prime}_{2}(f,f)+m^{\prime}_{3}(f,f,f)+\cdots=f\circ\hat{m}\,.

(27)

For dg-algebras
(A,d)
and
(A^{\prime},d^{\prime})
this amounts to the sequence of equations

\delta f_{l}=\Psi_{l}[f_{1},\ldots,f_{l-1}]\,,\qquad l=1,2,\ldots,

(28)

where

(\delta f_{l})(a_{1},\ldots,a_{l})=d^{\prime}f_{l}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{l})-\sum_{i=1}^{l}(-1)^{\bar{a}_{1}+\cdots+\bar{a}_{i-1}}f_{l}(a_{1},\ldots,da_{i},\ldots,a_{l})\,,

\begin{array}[]{c}\Psi_{l}[f_{1},\ldots,f_{l-1}](a_{1},\ldots,a_{l})=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}(-1)^{\bar{a}_{1}+\cdots+\bar{a}_{i}}f_{l-1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i}a_{i+1},\ldots a_{l})\\[14.22636pt]
-\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{l-1}(-1)^{\bar{a}_{1}+\cdots+\bar{a}_{i}}f_{i}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{i})f_{l-i}(a_{i+1},\ldots,a_{l})\,,\end{array}

(29)

and
f_{l}
stands for the restriction of
f
onto the subspace
B^{l}(A)
.
For
l=1
,
\Psi_{1}\equiv 0
and
f_{1}:A[1]\rightarrow A^{\prime}[1]

defines a cochain transformation, i.e.,
d^{\prime}f_{1}=f_{1}d
.
We say that a cochain transformation
f_{1}
is
unital
if it preserves the unit, that is,
f_{1}(1)=1
.

A morphism
F
is called quasi-isomorphism if
f_{1}
induces an isomorphism
H(f_{1}):H(A)\rightarrow H(A^{\prime})
in cohomology.

Notice that the operator
\delta
defines a differential that makes each space
\mathrm{Hom}(B^{l}(A),A^{\prime}[1])
into a cochain complex. By induction on
l
, one can see that
\delta\Psi_{l}=0
; and hence, all
\Psi_{l}
’s are
\delta
-cocycles. The quasi-isomorphism condition (
28
) requires all these cocycles to be trivial. We conclude that whenever the differential
\delta
is acyclic in degree
1
any morphism of complexes
f_{1}:A\rightarrow A^{\prime}
extends to a morphism
of
A_{\infty}
-algebras. Of course, the acyclicity of
\delta
is only a sufficient (not necessary) condition for the existence of an
A_{\infty}
-morphism
f
.

We say that a cochain
f\in\mathrm{Hom}(B^{l}(A),A^{\prime}[1])
is
normal
if it satisfies the normalization condition:
f(a_{1},\ldots,a_{l})=0
whenever any of the arguments is equal to
1\in A
. Obviously, the normal cochains constitute a subcomplex of the complex
\mathrm{Hom}(B^{l}(A),A^{\prime}[1])
.

Proposition 2.2
.

If
f_{1}
is unital and
f_{2},f_{3},\ldots,f_{l-1}
are normal, then the cochain
\Psi_{l}[f_{1},\ldots,f_{l-1}]
defined by Eq. (
29
) is normal as well.

The proof is by direct verification.

Let us specify the above formulas for the case of algebras
A=A_{\theta}
and
A^{\prime}=A_{\theta^{\prime}}
. As both the algebras are nested in degrees zero and one,
A_{\theta}=(A_{\theta})_{0}\oplus(A_{\theta})_{1}
, each map
f_{l}
splits into
l+1
components

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle f_{l}^{\prime}:\;(A_{\theta})_{1}^{\otimes l}\rightarrow(A_{\theta^{\prime}})_{1}\,,\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle f^{\prime\prime}_{l,k}:(A_{\theta})_{1}^{\otimes k}\otimes(A_{\theta})_{0}\otimes(A_{\theta})_{1}^{\otimes(l-k-1)}\rightarrow(A_{\theta^{\prime}})_{0},\qquad k=0,1,\ldots,l-1.\end{array}

(30)

In terms of these component maps, equations (
28
) decompose into two groups:

\begin{array}[]{c}f^{\prime}_{l}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},db,\ldots,a_{l-1})=df^{\prime\prime}_{l,k}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b,\ldots,a_{l-1})\\[8.53581pt]
-f^{\prime}_{l-1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\ast_{\theta}b,\ldots,a_{l-1})+f^{\prime}_{l-1}(a_{1},\ldots,b\ast_{\theta}a_{k+1},\ldots,a_{l-1})\\[8.53581pt]
+\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{k}f^{\prime}_{n}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\ast_{\theta^{\prime}}f^{\prime\prime}_{l-n,k-n}(a_{n+1},\ldots,a_{k},b,\ldots,a_{l-1})\\[14.22636pt]
-\displaystyle\sum_{n=k+1}^{l-1}f^{\prime\prime}_{n,k}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b,\ldots,a_{n-1})\ast_{\theta^{\prime}}f^{\prime}_{l-n}(a_{n},\ldots,a_{l-1})\end{array}

(31)

for all
0\leq k\leq l-1
and

\begin{array}[]{c}f^{\prime\prime}_{l,s}(a_{1},\ldots,db_{1},\ldots,a_{s-1},b_{2},\ldots,a_{l-2})-f^{\prime\prime}_{l,k}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b_{1},\ldots,db_{2},\ldots,a_{l-2})\\[8.53581pt]
=-f^{\prime\prime}_{l-1,s-1}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}\ast_{\theta}b_{1},\ldots,a_{s-1},b_{2},\ldots,a_{l-2})\\[8.53581pt]
+f^{\prime\prime}_{l-1,s-1}(a_{1},\ldots,b_{1}\ast_{\theta}a_{k+1},\ldots,a_{s-1},b_{2},\ldots,a_{l-2})\\[8.53581pt]
+f^{\prime\prime}_{l-1,k}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b_{1},\ldots,a_{s-1}\ast_{\theta}b_{2},\ldots,a_{l-2})\\[8.53581pt]
-f^{\prime\prime}_{l-1,k}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b_{1},\ldots,b_{2}\ast_{\theta}a_{s},\ldots,a_{l-2})\\[8.53581pt]
+\delta_{k,s-1}f^{\prime\prime}_{l-1,k}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b_{1}\ast_{\theta}b_{2},a_{s},\ldots,a_{l-2})\\[0.0pt]
\displaystyle-\sum_{n=k}^{s-1}f^{\prime\prime}_{n+1,k}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k},b_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\ast_{\theta^{\prime}}f^{\prime\prime}_{l-n-1,s-n-1}(a_{n+1},\ldots,b_{2},\ldots,a_{l-2})\end{array}

(32)

for all
0\leq k\leq s-1\leq l-2
. As is seen the second group of equations does not involve the
f^{\prime}
’s, giving thus a closed subsystem for determining unknown maps
f^{\prime\prime}_{k,l}
. We will present the general solution to Eqs. (
31
,
32
) in the next section. For now, let us explain the relevance of
A_{\infty}
-morphisms above to SW maps.
Given elements
a\in(A_{\theta})_{1}
and
b\in(A_{\theta})_{0}
, define the pair of chains

\varphi=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}a^{\otimes l}\,,\qquad\psi=\sum_{s,u=0}^{\infty}a^{\otimes s}\otimes b\otimes a^{\otimes u}

(33)

of the bar coalgebra
B(A_{\theta})
. Clearly,
\bar{\varphi}=0
and
\bar{\psi}=1
. Let
\hat{D}_{a}
denote the coderivation of
B(A_{\theta})
associated with the cochain
D_{a}\in\mathrm{Hom}(B^{1}(A_{\theta}),A_{\theta}[1])
defined by

D_{a}(c)=dc+a\ast_{\theta}c-c\ast_{\theta}a\qquad\forall c\in A_{\theta}[1]\,.

(34)

Then evaluating equation (
31
) on
\psi
and summing up over
l
, one can see that the cochains

f^{\prime}=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}f^{\prime}_{l}\,,\qquad f^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}f^{\prime\prime}_{l,k}

(35)

of
\mathrm{Hom}(B(A_{\theta}),A_{\theta^{\prime}}[1])
satisfy the equation

df^{\prime\prime}(\psi)+f^{\prime}(\varphi)\ast_{\theta^{\prime}}f^{\prime\prime}(\psi)-f^{\prime\prime}(\psi)\ast_{\theta^{\prime}}f^{\prime}(\varphi)=f^{\prime}(\hat{D}_{a}\psi)\,.

(36)

Now it remains to make the following identifications:

a\leftrightarrow\mathcal{A}\,,\qquad b\leftrightarrow\lambda\,,\qquad f^{\prime}(\varphi)\leftrightarrow\hat{\mathcal{A}}(\mathcal{A})\,,\qquad f^{\prime\prime}(\psi)\leftrightarrow\hat{\lambda}(\lambda,\mathcal{A})\,.

(37)

With such identifications, Eq.(
36
) reproduces the equivalence condition (
10
) if one set
\theta=0
and then change
\theta^{\prime}\rightarrow\theta
. Thus, we get the conclusion that the SW map is nothing but a quasi-isomorphism
f
of the dg-algebras
A_{0}
and
A_{\theta}
in the category of
A_{\infty}
-algebras. Similarly, evaluating equations (
32
) on the chain

\chi=\sum_{s,u,v=0}^{\infty}a^{\otimes s}\otimes\big(b_{1}\otimes a^{\otimes u}\otimes b_{2}-b_{2}\otimes a^{\otimes u}\otimes b_{1}\big)\otimes a^{\otimes v}\,,

(38)

one can deduce the identity

-f^{\prime\prime}(\hat{D}_{a}\chi)=f^{\prime\prime}(\psi_{1})\ast_{\theta^{\prime}}f^{\prime\prime}(\psi_{2})-f^{\prime\prime}(\psi_{2})\ast_{\theta^{\prime}}f^{\prime\prime}(\psi_{1})-f^{\prime\prime}(\psi_{12})\,,

(39)

where

\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\psi_{i}=\sum_{s,u=0}^{\infty}a^{\otimes s}\otimes b_{i}\otimes a^{\otimes u}\,,\quad i=1,2\,,\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle\psi_{12}=\sum_{s,u=0}^{\infty}a^{\otimes s}\otimes(b_{1}\ast_{\theta}b_{2}-b_{2}\ast_{\theta}b_{1})\otimes a^{\otimes u}\,.\end{array}

(40)

Upon identification
b_{i}\leftrightarrow\lambda_{i}
, Rel. (
39
) reproduces the gauge consistency condition (
11
).

Note that all the chains (
33
,
38
,
40
) are totally symmetric in permutations of tensor factors. This means that we evaluate the multilinear maps
f_{l}
on the symmetric diagonal. Upon symmetrization, the maps
f_{l}
define an
L_{\infty}
-morphism between
A_{\theta}
and
A_{\theta^{\prime}}
treated as dg-Lie algebras w.r.t. the commutators.

3
Explicit solutions and ambiguity

As a preparatory step, we introduce a complex that controls the solution space.
First, one can regard
B^{l}(A_{\theta})
as the
l
th tensor power of the complex
A_{\theta}[1]
with the differential induced by
d
.
Let
C^{l}
denote the corresponding cochain complex with coefficients in
A_{\theta^{\prime}}[1]
. By definition,

C^{l}=\mathrm{Hom}(B^{l}(A_{\theta}),A_{\theta^{\prime}}[1])=\bigoplus_{k=-1}^{l}\mathrm{Hom}^{k}(B^{l}(A_{\theta}),A_{\theta^{\prime}}[1])\,.

(41)

The second equality is due to the fact that the graded space
B^{l}(A_{\theta})
is concentrated in degrees
0,-1,\ldots,-l
, while
A_{\theta^{\prime}}[1]
is nested in degrees
0
and
-1
. We denote the differential in
C^{l}
by
d_{l}
, that is,
d_{l}(f)=f\circ d
for all
f\in C^{l}
.
The rationale behind the
introduction of this sequence of complexes is that the left- and right-hand sides of Eqs. (
31
,
32
) define, respectively, coboundaries and cocycles of the complex
C^{l}
in degree one. As above by
\bar{C}^{l}
we denote the subcomplex of normal cochains.

It is known that any operator in the space
of polynomials can be written as a differential operator (possibly of infinite order) with polynomial coefficients, see e.g.
[
12
, Lem. 5.1]
. This allows us to identify the cochains of
{C}^{l}
with polydifferential operators. Each polydifferential operator, in its turn, is completely determined by its symbol. The differential
d_{l}
makes then the space of symbols into a cochain complex, which we are going to describe in more detail below.

Considering the dg-algebra
A_{\theta}
just as a complex, denote by
\mathrm{B}
the complex dual to
A_{\theta}
.
By definition, the cochains of
\mathrm{B}=\mathrm{B}_{-1}\oplus\mathrm{B}_{0}
are given by the sums

\phi=\phi^{0}(p)+\phi^{i}(p)\zeta_{i}\,,

(42)

where
\phi^{0}
and
\phi^{i}
are formal power series in the commuting indeterminates
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
and we prescribe the following degrees to the formal variables:

\deg p_{i}=0\,,\qquad\deg\zeta_{i}=-1\,.

(43)

The dual differential
\partial:\mathrm{B}\rightarrow\mathrm{B}
increases the degree by one and acts on cochains (
42
) by the rule

\partial\phi=p_{i}\phi^{i}(p)\,.

(44)

The complex
(\mathrm{B},\partial)
enjoys the natural descending filtration
F^{k}B\supset F^{k+1}B
by powers of the
p
’s. Define the subspace
Z=Z_{-1}\oplus Z_{0}
of
\mathrm{B}
as

Z_{-1}=\big\{\phi=\phi^{i}(p)\zeta_{i}\;\big|\;p_{i}\phi^{i}(p)=0\big\}\,,\qquad Z_{0}=\big\{\phi=c\in\mathbb{R}\big\}\,.

(45)

The nonzero elements of
Z
are obviously nontrivial
\partial
-cocycles and
H(\mathrm{B})\simeq Z
.

Considering now the space
A_{\theta}
as a complex with zero differential, we define the tensor product of complexes

\mathrm{B}^{l}=A_{\theta}\otimes\mathrm{B}^{\otimes l}\,.

(46)

Let
\partial_{l}
denote the coboundary operator in
\mathrm{B}^{l}
. It is convenient to define the degree of a homogeneous cochain

\Phi=a\otimes\phi_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\phi_{l}\in\mathrm{B}^{l}

(47)

to be equal to

\deg\Phi=\deg\phi_{1}+\cdots+\deg\phi_{l}+\deg a+l-1\,.

For this grading, the complex
\mathrm{B}^{l}
is concentrated in degrees
-1,0,1,\ldots,l
.

Since we are working with complexes of
\mathbb{R}
-vector spaces, the Künneth formula yields immediately

H(\mathrm{B}^{l})=A_{\theta}\otimes H(\mathrm{B})^{\otimes l}\,.

(48)

For each cochain (
42
) one can assign the map
\sigma(\phi):A_{\theta}\rightarrow(A_{\theta})_{0}
that takes (
17
) to

\sigma(\phi)(a)=\phi^{0}(\partial_{i})a_{0}(x)+\phi^{j}(\partial_{i})a_{j}(x)\,,

where
\partial_{i}=\partial/\partial x^{i}
. Similarly, to each cochain (
47
)
one can assign the multilinear operator of
C^{k}
by setting

\Sigma(\Phi)(a_{1},\ldots,a_{l})=a\cdot\sigma(\phi_{1})(a_{1})\cdot\sigma(\phi_{2})(a_{2})\cdots\sigma(\phi_{l})(a_{l})\,.

(49)

Here the dot stands for the usual product of polynomials and the result is treated as an element of
A_{\theta^{\prime}}
.
This assignment extends to the whole of
\mathrm{B}^{l}
by linearity.

The filtration in
\mathrm{B}
induces that in the complex
\mathrm{B}^{l}
and we use the bold-font letter
\mathbf{B}^{l}
to denote the completion of
\mathrm{B}^{l}
w.r.t. this filtration. The map
\Phi\mapsto\Sigma(\Phi)
extends then to
{\mathbf{B}}^{l}
and we arrive at the following

Proposition 3.1
.

The map
\Sigma:\mathbf{B}^{l}\rightarrow C^{l}
is an isomorphism of complexes, i.e.,
d_{l}\Sigma=\Sigma\partial_{l}
.

It is the elements of
\mathbf{B}^{l}
which are identified with the symbols of operators.
The isomorphism
\Sigma
allows one to transfer the notion of normality from
C^{l}
to
\mathbf{B}^{l}
. Clearly,
the cochain (
49
) is normal iff
\phi_{1}^{0}(0)=\cdots=\phi^{0}_{l}(0)=0
. The normal cochains of
\mathbf{B}^{l}
form a subcomplex, which we denote by
\bar{\bf B}^{l}
. The formula

f(p)-f(0)=p_{i}\int_{0}^{1}dt\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_{i}}\right)(tp)\,,

(50)

valid for any formal power series
f(p)
, shows that each normal zero-chain
\phi=\phi^{0}(p)\in\mathrm{B}^{1}_{0}
is a coboundary of
\mathrm{B}^{1}
. It follows from the Künneth formula that

H^{n}(\bar{\mathrm{B}}^{l})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}(A_{\theta})_{0}\otimes Z_{-1}^{\otimes l}\,,\quad n=-1\,;\\[8.53581pt]
(A_{\theta})_{1}\otimes Z_{-1}^{\otimes l}\,,\quad n=0\,;\\[8.53581pt]
0\,,\quad n>0\,.\\[8.53581pt]
\end{array}\right.

(51)

Here
Z_{-1}
stands for the subspace of cocycles (
45
).
Since the differential
\partial_{l}
is acyclic in positive degrees when restricted to the subcomplex of normal cochains
\bar{\mathbf{B}}^{l}
, so is the differential
d_{l}
in the isomorphic complex
\bar{C}^{l}
. In particular,
H^{1}(\bar{C}^{l})=0
. This means the absence of obstructions to the solubility of Eqs. (
31
,
32
).

To write down an explicit expression for
f
we need to choose an appropriate homotopy operator that relates the identity map with a projection onto the subspace of nontrivial cocycles (
51
). First, we introduce the projector
\pi:B\rightarrow B
onto the subspace
Z\subset B
defined by (
45
). For every (
42
), we set

\pi\phi=\phi^{0}(0)+\phi^{i}(p)\zeta_{i}-\zeta_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{j}}\int_{0}^{1}p_{i}\phi^{i}(tp)dt\,.

One can see that
\pi^{2}=\pi
and
\mathrm{Im}\,\pi=Z
. Furthermore,
\pi
is homotopic to the identity map. The corresponding homotopy operator
h:B\rightarrow B
is given by

h\phi=\zeta_{i}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\frac{\partial\phi^{0}}{\partial p_{i}}\right)(tp)dt\,.

(52)

It is straightforward to check that

\partial h+h\partial=1-\pi

(53)

and

h^{2}=0\,,\qquad\pi h=0\,,\qquad h\pi=0\,.

(54)

Next, we extend the maps
\pi
and
h
from the complex
B
to the tensor product
B^{l}
by the standard formulas of homological algebra. For any cochain (
47
), we set

\pi_{l}(\Phi)=a_{0}\otimes\pi\phi_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\pi\phi_{l}\,,

(55)

h_{l}(\Phi)=\sum_{i=1}^{l}a_{0}\otimes\phi_{1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\phi_{i-1}\otimes h\phi_{i}\otimes\pi\phi_{i+1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\pi\phi_{l}\,.

(56)

Then

\partial_{l}h_{l}+h_{l}\partial_{l}=1-\pi_{l}\,,

(57)

and

h_{l}^{2}=0\,,\qquad\pi_{l}h_{l}=0\,,\qquad h_{l}\pi_{l}=0\,,\qquad\pi_{l}^{2}=\pi_{l}\,.

(58)

The isomorphism
\Sigma
allows one to push forward the operators
\pi_{l}
and
h_{l}
from
\mathbf{B}^{l}
to
C^{l}
. This results in
the operators
h^{\Sigma}_{l}=\Sigma h_{l}\Sigma^{-1}
and
\pi^{\Sigma}_{l}=\Sigma\pi_{l}\Sigma^{-1}
, which satisfy the same Rels. (
57
,
58
).

Let us set
f^{\prime\prime}_{l}=\sum_{k=0}^{l-1}f^{\prime\prime}_{l,k}
and rewrite Eqs. (
31
,
32
) in the form

d_{l}f^{\prime}_{l}=\Theta^{\prime}_{l}[f^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,f^{\prime}_{l-1};f^{\prime\prime}_{1},\ldots,f^{\prime\prime}_{l}]\,,\qquad d_{l}f^{\prime\prime}_{l}=\Theta^{\prime\prime}_{k}[f^{\prime\prime}_{1},\ldots,f_{l-1}^{\prime\prime}]\,.

(59)

Here the
\Theta
’s are determined by the r.h.s. of Eqs. (
31
,
32
).

Since
\pi_{l}^{\Sigma}\Theta_{l}^{\prime}=\pi_{l}^{\Sigma}\Theta_{l}^{\prime\prime}=0
, applying the homotopy operator
h^{\Sigma}_{l}
brings system (
59
) into the sequence of recurrence relations that determine the morphism
f
:

\begin{array}[]{l}f^{\prime\prime}_{l}=h^{\Sigma}_{l}\Theta^{\prime\prime}_{l}[f^{\prime\prime}_{1},\ldots,f_{l-1}^{\prime\prime}]\,,\qquad l=2,3,\ldots;\\[8.53581pt]
f^{\prime}_{m}=h^{\Sigma}_{l}\Theta^{\prime}_{l}[f^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,f^{\prime}_{m-1};f^{\prime\prime}_{1},\ldots,f^{\prime\prime}_{m}]\,,\qquad m=1,2,\ldots\end{array}

(60)

As the initial value for these recurrence relations, one takes a unital cochain
f^{\prime\prime}_{1}
.
It follows from Proposition
2.2
that the cochains
\Theta^{\prime\prime}_{l}
and
\Theta^{\prime}_{l}
are normal whenever so are the cochains
f^{\prime\prime}
’s and
f^{\prime}
’s they are built
from. Since the operator
h^{\Sigma}_{l}
respects normality, the resulting cochains
f^{\prime\prime}_{l}
and
f^{\prime}_{l}
are also normal. Thus, by induction, we see that relations (
60
) are well-defined provided
f^{\prime\prime}_{1}(1)=1
. Since
H^{0}(A_{\theta})\simeq\mathbb{R}
, the last condition implies that
f_{1}
induces an isomorphism
H(f_{1}):H^{0}(A_{\theta})\rightarrow H^{0}(A_{\theta^{\prime}})
of the zero cohomology groups. To be a quasi-isomorphism,
f
must also generate a cohomology isomorphism in degree one. The simplest way to satisfy the quasi-isomorphism condition is to take
f^{\prime\prime}_{1}
to be the identity map on the space of polynomials
a_{0}(x)
, see (
17
). Then
f^{\prime}_{1}=h_{1}^{\Sigma}\Theta_{1}^{\prime}=h_{1}^{\Sigma}df^{\prime\prime}_{1}
is again the identity map on the polynomials
a_{i}(x)\xi^{i}
. On the other hand, the identity map
f_{1}=(f_{1}^{\prime},f^{\prime\prime}_{1})=\mathrm{id}
, being an isomorphism of complexes, trivially satisfies Eq. (
31
) for
l=1
. Therefore,
f_{1}=\mathrm{id}
can always be chosen as an initial value for the recurrence relations (
60
).
Exactly this choice corresponds to the classical Seiberg–Witten map (
9
,
12
). With
f_{1}=\mathrm{id}
, the total
A_{\infty}
-morphism
f
is obviously invertible and we conclude that the algebras
(A_{\theta},d)
and
(A_{\theta^{\prime}},d)
, regarded as
A_{\infty}
-algebras, are isomorphic to each other.

In principle, one could try to construct an
A_{\infty}
-morphism whose component maps
f_{l}
with
l>1
are not normal or
f_{1}
is not unital. However, under such weak assumptions on the
f_{l}
’s, the solubility of Eqs. (
31
,
32
) gets out of control because all obstruction spaces
H^{1}(C^{l})\neq 0
. Therefore, the existence of such ‘abnormal’
A_{\infty}
-morphisms is not clear at the moment.

It remains to discuss the arbitrariness in the solutions of Eqs. (
31
,
32
) under the assumption of normality. Since, at each order
l
, we have a system of inhomogeneous linear equations
\delta f_{l}=\Psi_{l}
for the known
\Psi_{l}
and unknown
f_{l}
, the solution space is isomorphic to the space of normal
\delta
-cocycles in degree zero. In other words, it is possible to shift a solution
f_{l}=(f^{\prime}_{l},f^{\prime\prime}_{l})
by any
\delta
-cocycle
g_{l}=(g_{l}^{\prime},g_{l}^{\prime\prime})\in\bar{C}^{l}
. According to (
51
), one can always choose
g_{l}^{\prime}=z_{l}+dc_{l}
and
g_{l}^{\prime\prime}=d_{l}c_{l}
, where
\Sigma^{-1}(z_{l})
belongs to the completion of the space
(A_{\theta})_{1}\otimes Z_{-1}^{\otimes l}
, while
c_{l}
is an arbitrary homomorphism from
(A_{\theta})_{1}^{\otimes l}
to
(A_{\theta^{\prime}})_{0}
. In particular, two cochain transformations
f_{1},\tilde{f}_{1}:A_{\theta}\rightarrow A_{\theta^{\prime}}
, considered up to homotopy, differ from each other by an element
[z_{1}]\in H^{0}(\bar{C}^{1})
.
In the context of SW maps, these ambiguities have been discussed in many physical papers, see e.g.
[
10
,
8
,
13
,
11
,
14
,
15
]
and the references therein. Since there is some confusion in the literature about which ambiguities should be considered trivial and which are not, we will dwell on this issue in more detail.

Isolating all the terms that are linear in
f
’s on the left and summing up over
l
, we can write the sequence of equations (
28
) as a single Maurer–Cartan (MC) equation, namely,

Df=-\frac{1}{2}[f,f]\,,\qquad f=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}f_{l}\,.

(61)

Here the differential
D
on the left involves
\delta
and the product in
A
, while the commutator in
A^{\prime}
gives the Lie bracket on the right. The differential and the bracket make the space

\mathrm{Hom}(B(A),A^{\prime}[1])[1]
into a dg-Lie algebra
\mathcal{L}=\bigoplus\mathcal{L}_{n}
. The algebra
\mathcal{L}
has the descending filtration
F^{k}\mathcal{L}\supset F^{k+1}\mathcal{L}
by the number of arguments of homogeneous cochains. In particular,

\sum_{l=n}^{\infty}f_{l}\in F^{n}\mathcal{L}\,.

(62)

Due to the shift in degree, the morphism
f
gets degree one and defines an MC element of
\mathcal{L}_{1}
. Since
\mathcal{L}=F^{1}\mathcal{L}
, one can formally exponentiate the Lie algebra
\mathcal{L}_{0}
to produce the corresponding Lie group
G=\exp(\mathcal{L}_{0})
. This group acts on the space of MC elements by the following ‘gauge transformations’
[
16
]
:

f\quad\mapsto\quad f^{g}=gfg^{-1}-g^{-1}Dg\qquad\quad\forall g\in G\,.

(63)

Two MC elements (= morphisms)
f
and
\tilde{f}
are said to be gauge (= homotopy) equivalent if there exists an element
g\in G
such that
\tilde{f}=f^{g}
. In view of (
63
) it makes sense to consider the solutions to the MC equation (
61
) up to gauge equivalence.
Suppose now that two morphisms
f
and
\tilde{f}
coincide up to the order
l-1
in filtration degree, i.e.,
\tilde{f}-f\in F^{l}\mathcal{L}_{1}
. Then it follows from (
28
) that
\delta(\tilde{f}_{l}-f_{l})=0
. If the
\delta
-cocycle
g_{l}=\tilde{f}_{l}-f_{l}
happens to be trivial, that is
g_{l}=\delta c_{l}
, then one can perform the gauge transformation
f\mapsto f^{g}
with
g=\exp(c_{l})
. It is readily seen that
\tilde{f}-f^{g}\in F^{l+1}\mathcal{L}
; and hence, the morphisms
\tilde{f}
and
f^{g}
coincide up to the order
l
. We thus conclude that the first nontrivial class of
\delta
-cohomology
\big[\tilde{f}_{l}-f_{l}\big]
represents an obstruction to the homotopy equivalence of two
A_{\infty}
-morphisms and the corresponding SW maps. A more systematic exposition of the homotopy theory of
A_{\infty}
and other homotopy algebras can be found in
[
17
]
.

4
Discussion

We presented an algebraic treatment of the Seiberg–Witten map as a quasi-isomorphism of
A_{\infty}
-algebras. While in the original physical setting, the very existence of the SW map requires more or less nontrivial reasoning or calculations, it becomes a simple consequence of the dg-algebra quasi-isomorphism (
20
) in the algebraic interpretation. A simple homotopy operator (
52
) allows one to write down the corresponding SW map up to any given order in the gauge field
\mathcal{A}
. The simplicity of the quasi-isomorphism construction owes its existence to two facts:

(A)

The dg-algebras under consideration are isomorphic as cochain complexes and differ only in multiplication;

(B)

Every nontrivial cocycle has a central representative (homologically equivalent to an element from the centre
Z(A)
of the algebra
A
).

Together, these properties provide the quasi-isomorphism
A\leftarrow Z\rightarrow A^{\prime}
with
Z=\ker d\cap Z(A)
.

There are many dg-algebras satisfying conditions A and B. For instance, one can replace the last relation in (
14
) with the standard anticommutativity condition
\xi^{i}\xi^{j}-\xi^{j}\xi^{i}=0
for the odd generators. The result may be viewed as a noncommutative version of the de Rham complex with
dx^{i}=\xi^{i}
. Let us denote it by
\Omega_{\theta}
. The ordinary (commutative) de Rham complex corresponds to
\theta=0
. Any two dg-algebras
\Omega_{\theta}
and
\Omega_{\theta^{\prime}}
obviously satisfy the conditions above; hence, they are quasi-isomorphic. Furthermore, the dg-algebra
\Omega_{\theta}
admits the natural descending filtration
F^{k}\Omega_{\theta}\supset F^{k+1}\Omega_{\theta}
by the powers of the
\xi
’s. This allows one to define the sequence of dg-algebras
A^{{}_{(k)}}_{\theta}=\Omega_{\theta}/F^{k}\Omega_{\theta}
. The dg-algebra with relations (
14
) corresponds to
k=2
. It is clear that any pair of dg-algebras from the family
A^{{}_{(k)}}_{\theta}
satisfies conditions A and B whenever
k>1
. (For
k=1
,
H(A_{\theta}^{{}_{(1)}})\simeq A_{\theta}^{{}_{(1)}}
and condition B is violated unless
\theta=0
.)
The physical interpretation of the algebras
A_{\theta}^{{}_{(k)}}
with
k>2
is not clear at the moment, except for the case
k=3
. As was shown in
[
9
]
, one can identify the additional homogeneous subspace
(A_{\theta}^{{}_{(3)}})_{2}
in degree
2
with the space of equations of motion (e.g.
\mathcal{F}=0
as in Chern–Simons theory). Then the extra components of a quasi-isomorphism
f
with arguments in
(A_{\theta}^{{}_{(3)}})_{2}
allow one to cover the case of ‘open gauge algebra’ when the gauge consistency condition (
11
) involves an extra term vanishing on-shell.

All the above constructions extend immediately to the tensor product of
A^{{}_{(k)}}_{\theta}
with the matrix algebra
\mathrm{Mat}_{N}
. Although the cohomology algebra of
A^{{}_{(k)}}_{\theta}\otimes\mathrm{Mat}_{N}
is no longer Abelian, the subalgebra of cocycles
Z
is still independent of
\theta
, which is sufficient for the quasi-isomorphism. Upon replacing
\theta\rightarrow i\theta
, one can also reduce the matrix factor
\mathrm{Mat}_{N}
to the Lie subalgebra of anti-Hermitian matrices
u(N)
. More general matrix factors were considered in
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
.

Another class of relevant dg-algebras is provided by the Wess–Zumino (WZ) complexes
[
21
]
. The corresponding dg-algebras are generated by the same formal variables
x^{i}
and
\xi^{i}=dx^{i}
but the relations on them are essentially different. For the general discussion of WZ complexes, we refer the reader to
[
22
,
23
]
. By way of illustration, we present the following (not most general) commutation relations:

\begin{array}[]{ll}x^{i}x^{j}-q^{ji}x^{j}x^{i}=0\,,&\quad\xi^{i}x^{j}-q^{ji}x^{j}\xi^{i}=0\,,\\[8.53581pt]
\xi^{i}\xi^{j}+q^{ji}\xi^{j}\xi^{i}=0\,,&\quad(\xi^{i})^{2}=0\,.\end{array}

(64)

Here
q^{ij}
is a set of real numbers subject to the condition
q^{ji}q^{ij}=q^{ii}=1
.
The dg-algebra generated by these
x
’s and
\xi
’s, let us denote it by
\Omega_{q}
, enjoys the PBW property and is isomorphic, as a vector space, to the usual de Rham complex. Moreover, one can see that the Poincaré Lemma holds true for
\Omega_{q}
, meaning
H(\Omega_{q})\simeq\mathbb{R}\subset\Omega_{q}
. This gives the quasi-isomorphism
\Omega_{q}\leftarrow\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\Omega_{q^{\prime}}
of dg-algebras. Again, the natural grading on
\Omega_{q}
by powers of the
\xi
’s gives rise to the descending filtration
F^{k}\Omega_{q}\supset F^{k+1}\Omega_{q}
, so that one can define the sequence of dg-algebras
A^{{}_{(k)}}_{q}=\Omega_{q}/F^{k}\Omega_{q}
. The algebra
A^{{}_{(1)}}_{q}
, generated by the
x
’s, is often called the algebra of ‘quantum polynomials’. Accordingly, the vector space spanned by the
x
’s is referred to as the ‘quantum space’.
The next algebra,
A^{{}_{(2)}}_{q}
, is the quantum counterpart of the algebra (
14
) underlying the Weyl–Moyal noncommutativity. As with
A^{{}_{(2)}}_{\theta}
, the cocycles of
A^{{}_{(2)}}_{q}
form a commutative algebra; hence, all the dg-algebras of the family
A^{{}_{(2)}}_{q}
are quasi-isomorphic to each other. It should be mentioned that noncommutative field theory on quantum spaces was considered in
[
1
]
, albeit with completely different differential and commutation relations among the
x
’s and
\xi
’s. Some examples of noncommutative gauge theories related to WS complexes have been elaborated in the recent paper
[
24
]
.
