Fair Set Cover
Mohsen Dehghankar, Rahul Raychaudhury, Stavros Sintos, Abolfazl Asudeh

Abstract
. The potential harms of algorithmic decisions have ignited algorithmic fairness as a central topic in computer science. One of the fundamental problems in computer science is Set Cover, which has numerous applications with societal impacts, such as assembling a small team of individuals that collectively satisfy a range of expertise requirements. However, despite its broad application spectrum and significant potential impact, set cover has yet to be studied through the lens of fairness. Therefore, in this paper, we introduce Fair Set Cover , which aims not only to cover with a minimum-size set but also to satisfy demographic parity in its selection of sets. To this end, we develop multiple versions of fair set cover, study their hardness, and devise efficient approximation algorithms for each variant. Notably, under certain assumptions, our algorithms always guarantee zero-unfairness, with only a small increase in the approximation ratio compared to regular set cover. Furthermore, our experiments on various data sets and across different settings confirm the negligible price of fairness, as (a) the output size increases only slightly (if any) and (b) the time to compute the output does not significantly increase.

1.
Introduction

The abundance of data, coupled with AI and advanced algorithms, has revolutionized almost every aspect of human life.
As data-driven technologies take root in our lives, their drawbacks and potential harms become increasingly evident. Subsequently, algorithmic fairness has become central in computer science research to minimize machine bias.
However, despite substantial focus on fairness in predictive analysis and machine learning
(
6
,
36
,
39
)
,
traditional combinatorial optimization has received limited attention
(
49
)
. Nevertheless, combinatorial problems such as ranking
(
3
)
, matching
(
19
,
20
)
, resource allocation
(
17
,
9
,
27
)
, and graph problems of influence maximization
(
46
)
and edge recommendation
(
43
,
8
)
have massive societal impact.

In this paper, we study the Set Cover problem through the lens of fairness.
As one of Karp’s 21
NP
-complete problems with various forms, set cover models a large set of real-world problems

(
47
,
40
)
, ranging from airline crew scheduling
(
41
)
and facility location (such as placing cell towers) to computational biology
(
13
)
and network security
(
21
)
, to name only a few.
Given a ground set
U
of elements and a family of subsets of
U
, called
\mathcal{S}
, in the Set Cover problem, the final goal is to cover all the elements of
U
by picking the least number of sets from
\mathcal{S}
.
Beyond its classical application examples, in the real world, set cover is frequently used to model problems that (either directly or indirectly) impact societies and human beings.
In such settings, it is vital to ensure that the set cover optimization (a) does not cause bias and discrimination against some (demographic) groups, and (b) promotes social values such as equity and diversity.
We further motivate the problem with a real-world application in Example
1
for
Team of Experts Formation
.

Example 1.

Consider an institution that wants to assemble a team of experts (e.g., a data science company that wants to hire employees) that collectively satisfy a set of skills (e.g., {
python
,
sql
,
data-visualization
,
statistics
,
deep-learning
,
\cdots
}), i.e., for any skill, there must be at least one team member who has expertise in that skill. Naturally, the institution’s goal is to cover all the skills with the smallest possible team. However, due to historical biases in society, solely optimizing for the team size may cause discrimination by mostly selecting team members from privileged groups. Unfortunately, many such
biased selections
have happened in the real world.
For instance, investigating an incident where HP webcams failed to track
black
individuals
(
42
)
, revealed that HP engineers were mostly
white-male
and that caused the issue
(
45
)
.
On the flip side, promoting social values such as diversity are always desirable in problems such as team formation (e.g., the data science company would like to ensure diversity in their hiring).
In other words, not only do they want to satisfy all skills, but they also want to have an equal (or proportionate) representation of various demographic groups in the assembled team.

Motivated by Example
1
and other social applications of set cover, outlined in §
2
, in this paper we introduce
fair set cover
, where the objective is to find a smallest collection of sets to cover a universe of items, while ensuring (weighted) parity in the selection of sets from various groups. More specifically, given a set of groups
\mathcal{G}
, a set of elements
U
, a family of sets
\mathcal{S}
such that each set belongs to a group, and a coefficient
f_{h}
for each group
\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}
with
\sum_{\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}}f_{h}=1
, the goal is to select the minimum number of sets from
\mathcal{S}
to cover all elements in
U
, such that, for each group
\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}
the fraction of the number of sets from group
\mathbf{g}_{h}
in the final solution is
f_{h}
.
The formal definitions of our problems are given in §
3
.

In this paper, we revisit the set cover problem through the lens of group fairness. Our main contributions are as follows.

\bullet
(§
2
, §
3
)
Demonstrating some of its applications, we propose the (unweighted and weighted) fair set cover problem, and study its complexity and hardness of approximation.
We formulate fairness based on demographic parity in a general form that can be used to enforce count parity, as well as ratio parity, in selection among a set of binary or non-binary demographic groups.
While various related problems have been studied in the literature (see §
8
), to the best of our knowledge,
we are the first
to formulate and study the problem of
fair set cover
and its variations (such that the weighted case or the
\varepsilon
-unfairness) over multiple groups.

\bullet
(§
4
, §
6
)
In the unweighted setting, we first propose a naive algorithm that achieves zero-unfairness but at a (large) cost of increasing the approximation ratio by a factor of the number of groups. Next, we propose a greedy algorithm that, while guaranteeing fairness, has the same approximation ratio as of the regular set cover; however, its running time is exponential in the number of groups.
Therefore, we propose a faster (polynomial) algorithm that still guarantees fairness but slightly increases the approximation ratio by a fixed factor of
\frac{e}{e-1}
. Furthermore, we discuss a relaxed version of fairness constraints (called
\varepsilon
-unfairness) and the trade-offs in the approximation ratios achieved by our algorithms.

\bullet
(§
5
, §
6
)
In the weighted setting, we follow the same sequence by first proposing a naive algorithm that guarantees fairness but at a cost of a major increase in the output size (approximation ratio). We then propose a greedy algorithm that improves the approximation ratio but runs in exponential time relative to the number of groups. Finally, we propose a faster algorithm but at the expense of a minor increase in the approximation ratio.

\bullet
(§
7
)
To validate our theoretical findings, we conduct comprehensive experiments on real and synthetic datasets, across various settings, including binary vs. non-binary groups and fairness based on equal count vs. ratio parity. In summary, our experiments verify that our algorithms can achieve fairness with a negligible (if any) increase in the output size, and a small increase in the run time.

2.
Application Demonstration

In Example
1
, we demonstrated an application of fair set cover for
Team of Experts Formation
.
To demonstrate the application-span of our formulation, in the following, we briefly mention a few more applications.

Equal Base-rate in representative data sets

An equal base rate is defined as having an equal number of objects for different subgroups in the data set
(
30
)
. Satisfying equal base-rate is important for satisfying multiple fairness metrics in down-stream machine learning task.
Consider the schema of a dataset of individual records (e.g., Chicago breast-cancer examination data), with a set of attributes
\mathbf{x}=\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{\eta}\}
, where
\text{dom}(x_{i})>1
is the domain of
x_{i}
. In order to ensure representativeness in the data, suppose we would like to
collect a minimal set that contains at least one instance from all possible level-
\ell
value combinations.
A level-
\ell
combination is a value-combination containing
\ell
attributes in
\mathbf{x}
.
Each individual belongs to a demographic group (e.g.,
White
,
Black
).
In order to reduce potential unfairnesses for various groups (e.g., Chicago breast-cancer detection disparities
(
23
)
), it is required to satisfy equal base-rate in the dataset. This is an instance of
fair set cover
, where each row (individual record) in the data set, covers all level-
\ell
combinations it matches with.

Business license distribution

Issuing a minimum number of business licences (e.g., cannabis dispensary) while covering the population of a city is a challenging task for the City Office.
Each business location covers regions within a certain (travel time) range.
Besides coverage,
equity
is a critical constraint in these settings.
For example, disparity against
Black
-owned cannabis dispensaries in Chicago is a well-known issue
(
48
)
. Formulating and solving this problem as a fair set cover instance will resolve the disparity issues.

In Social Networks a similar application is
influencer selection
, where companies would like to select a small set of “innfluencers” to reach out to their target application. Specifically, once an influencer (aka content creator) post a content about a product, their followers view it.
However, the company might also want to send their products to a diverse set of influencers, to prevent visual biases in their advertisement
(
34
)
. Each influencer has a "cost" which is the amount of money that they should get to advertise a product. The company should minimize the advertising budget so the problem is mapped to the
fair weighted set cover problem
.

Fair Clustering

Clustering is a fundamental problem in Computer Science with many applications in as social network analysis, medical imaging, and anomaly detection
(
50
)
. We are given a set of
n
elements, where each element belongs to a group.
We are also given a clustering objective function that measures the clustering error.
The goal is to compute a set of
k
centers to minimize the clustering error, ensuring that a specific fraction of centers is selected from each group.
Interestingly some of the known clustering problems can be modeled as a
set cover
instance (for example
k
-center clustering).
While fair clustering has been studied over different clustering objective functions, such as
k
-center,
k
-median, and
k
-means
(
24
,
28
,
31
)
, there are still interesting open problems in the area. In the
sum of radii clustering
the goal is to choose a set of
k
balls (where the centers of the balls are elements in the input set) that cover all elements minimizing the sum of radii of the balls.
This problem is more useful than the
k
-center clustering because it reduces this dissection effect
(
22
,
37
)
.
The standard (unfair) version of this clustering problem has been thoroughly studied in the literature
(
16
,
11
)
.
Known constant approximation fixed parameter tractable algorithms are known for the fair sum of radii clustering
(
12
,
26
)
, however their running time depends exponentially on
k
.
The problem can be mapped to an instance of the
fair set cover
problem with
n
elements and
O(n^{2})
sets: The elements in
fair set cover
are exactly the elements in the clustering problem. For every element
e
we construct
O(n)
balls/sets with center
e
and radii all possible distances from
e
to any other element. Each set is assigned a weight which is equal to the radius of the ball. It is not hard to show that any solution of the weighted
fair set cover
instance is a valid solution for the fair sum of radii clustering and vice versa. Hence, our new algorithms for the (weighted)
fair set cover
problem can be used to derive an approximation algorithm for the fair sum of radii clustering in
O(\operatorname{poly}(n,k))
time.
In the technical report
(
14
)
, we provide additional applications of the
fair set cover
problem.

3.
Preliminaries

Problem definition.

We are given a universe of
n
elements
U=\{e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n}\}
, a family of
\mu
sets
\mathcal{S}=\{S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{\mu}\}
, where
\cup_{i=1}^{\mu}S_{i}=U
.
We are also given a set of
k
demographic groups
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathbf{g}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{g}_{k}\}
(e.g., {
white-male
,
black-female
,
...
}), while each set
S_{j}
in the family
\mathcal{S}
belongs to a specific demographic group
\mathbf{g}(S_{j})\in\mathcal{G}
. In the rest of this paper, we interchangeably refer to the group of a set as its
color
.
Let
\mathcal{S}_{h}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
be the family of sets from
\mathcal{S}
with color
\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}
. Let
m_{h}
be the number of sets of color
\mathbf{g}_{h}
in
\mathcal{S}
, i.e.,
m_{h}=|\mathcal{S}_{h}|
.
A
cover

X\subseteq\mathcal{S}
is a subset of
\mathcal{S}
such that for any
e\in U
there exists a set
S\in X
with
e\in S
.
In other words,
X
is a cover if
\bigcup_{s_{i}\in X}=U
.
To simplify the notation, for a family of sets
X\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, we define
X\cap U=(\bigcup_{S\in X}S)\cap U
.

Fairness definition

Following Example
1
and the applications demonstrated in §
2
, we adopt the group-fairness notion of
demographic parity
, as the (weighted) parity between the number of sets selected from each group.
Specifically, given the non-negative coefficients
\{f_{1},\cdots,f_{k}\}
such that
\sum\limits_{h=1}^{k}f_{h}=1
, a cover
X
is called a
fair cover
if, for all groups
\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}
:

\big|X\cap\mathcal{S}_{h}\big|=f_{h}\,\big|X\big|
.

Two special cases based on this definition are the following:

•

Count-parity
: when
f_{h}=\frac{1}{k}
,
\forall\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}
, a fair set cover contains equal number from each group.

•

Ratio-parity
: when
f_{h}=\frac{m_{h}}{\mu}
,
\forall\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}
, a fair set cover maintains the original ratios of groups in
\mathcal{S}
.

Note that our fairness definition is not limited to the above cases, and includes any fractions (represented as rational numbers) specified by non-negative coefficients
f_{h}
such that
\sum_{h=1}^{k}f_{h}=1
.

Next, we define the main problem studied in this paper.
Let
w:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a weighted function over the sets in
\mathcal{S}
. By slightly abusing the notation, for a family of sets
C\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, we define
w(C)=\sum_{S\in C}w(S)
. The goal is to find a fair cover such that the selected sets in the cover has the smallest sum of weights.

Definition 0 (Generalized Fair Weighted Set Cover problem (GFWSC)).

Given a universe of
n
elements
U
, a set of
k
groups
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathbf{g}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{g}_{k}\}
, a fraction
f_{h}
for each
\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}
such that their sum is equal to
1
, a family of
\mu
subsets
\mathcal{S}
, such that each
S\in\mathcal{S}
is associated with a group
\mathbf{g}(S)\in\mathcal{G}
, and a weighted function
w:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
the goal is to compute a fair cover
X_{w}
such that
\sum_{S\in X_{w}}w(S)
is minimized.

If all weights are equal to
1
, i.e.,
w(S)=1
, for every set
S\in\mathcal{S}
, then we call it the Generalized Fair Set Cover problem (GFSC).
In the next sections, we denote the optimum fair cover with
X^{*}
.

Under the
count-parity
fairness model,
we call our problems, the Fair Set Cover (FSC) problem, and the Fair Weighted Set Cover (FWSC) problem, respectively.
Even though the GFSC and GFWSC problems are more general than FSC and FWSC, respectively, the algorithmic techniques to solve FSC and FWSC can straightforwardly be extended to handle GFSC and GFWSC with (almost) the same guarantees. In order to simplify the analysis, in the next sections we focus on FSC and FWSC. Then in §
6
, we describe how our algorithms can be extended to handle GFSC and GFWSC.

Hardness and Assumption.
It is straightforward to show that FSC is
NP
-Complete.
Recently, Barnabò et al.
(
5
)
showed that FSC cannot be approximated with any sublinear approximation factor in polynomial time unless
\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}
.
However, if there are
enough
sets from each group, then in the next sections we design efficient algorithms with provable approximation guarantees.
More formally, for the FSC problem, we assume that every group contains at least
\Omega(\log n)\frac{|X^{*}|}{k}
sets.
For simplicity, in the next sections we also assume that every group has the same number of sets, i.e., for every group
\mathbf{g}_{j}
it holds that
m_{j}=m
. Hence,
\mu=mk
. This only helps us to analyze our algorithms with respect to only three variables,
m,k,n
. All our algorithms can be extended to more general cases (where each color does not contain the same number of sets). We further highlight it in §
7
, where we run experiments on datasets with
m_{i}\neq m_{j}
for
i\neq j
.

\varepsilon
-unfairness.
In some applications, the definition of fair cover might be too restrictive. Recall, that a cover
X
is fair if there are exactly
f_{h}|X|
elements from color
\mathbf{g}_{h}
in cover
X
(zero-unfairness).
Instead, we define the notion of
\varepsilon
-unfair cover
. Given a parameter
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, a cover
X
is called an
\varepsilon
-unfair cover if, for each group
\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}
, it holds that
(1-\varepsilon)f_{h}|X|\leq|X\cap\mathcal{S}_{h}|\leq(1+\varepsilon)f_{h}|X|
. In other words, instead of requesting exactly
f_{h}|X|
elements from each group
\mathbf{g}_{h}
, we request any number of elements in the range
[(1-\varepsilon)f_{h}|X|,(1+\varepsilon)f_{h}|X|]
. Notice that any fair cover is also an
\varepsilon
-unfair cover. All our defined problems can be studied in this setting. For example, we consider the
\varepsilon
-GFSC problem
that has the same objective with GFSC problem, but the returned cover should be an
\varepsilon
-unfair cover instead of a fair cover.
In §
6.1
, we show how our new approximation algorithms for the GFSC problem can be used to approximate the
\varepsilon
-GFSC problem.

4.
Fair Set Cover

4.1.
Naive Algorithm

We first describe a naive algorithm for the FSC problem with large approximation ratio. We execute the well-known greedy algorithm for the standard set cover problem (its pseudocode is shown in the technical report
(
14
)
) and let
X
be the set cover we get.
If
X
does not satisfy the fairness requirements, we arbitrarily add the minimal number of sets from each color to equalize the number of sets from each color in our final cover. The pseudocode is shown in the technical report
(
14
)
.

Lemma 0.

There exists a
\big(k(\ln n+1)\big)
-approximation algorithm for the FSC problem that runs in
O(m\cdot k\cdot n)
time.

4.2.
Greedy Algorithm

Figure 1
.
An illustration of a set system for the Fair Set Cover (FSC) problem, consisting of 16 points and 7 sets: 4 red sets and 3 blue sets. The Greedy Fair Set Cover algorithm (§
4.2
) selects
(S_{1},S_{5})
as the first pair of red and blue sets, followed by the pairs
(S_{3},S_{6})
and
(S_{4},S_{1})
. The standard greedy algorithm selects the sets
S_{1}
,
S_{2}
,
S_{3}
,
S_{4}
, and
S_{7}
, resulting in 4 red sets and 1 blue set, highlighting its lack of fairness.

In this subsection we propose an algorithm for the FSC problem whose approximation ratio
does not depend
on
k
.
For simplicity of explanation, we describe our algorithm for the case we have only two demographic groups, i.e.,
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2}\}
. In the end, our algorithm can be extended almost verbatim to
k>2
groups.

Let
X=\emptyset
be the cover we construct and let
U^{-}=U
be the set of uncovered elements.
We repeat the following steps until
U^{-}=\emptyset
.
In each iteration, we find the
pair
of sets
(S_{A},S_{B})
such that i) sets
S_{A},S_{B}
cover the maximum number of uncovered elements, i.e.,
U^{-}\cap(S_{A}\cup S_{B})
is maximized, and ii) set
S_{A}
has color
\mathbf{g}_{1}
and
S_{B}
has color
\mathbf{g}_{2}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{g}(S_{A})=\mathbf{g}_{1}
and
\mathbf{g}(S_{B})=\mathbf{g}_{2}
.
Once we find
S_{A},S_{B}
we update the set
U^{-}
removing the new covered element,
U^{-}\leftarrow U^{-}\setminus(S_{A}\cup S_{B})
, and we add
S_{A},S_{B}
in
X
. In the end, after covering all elements (
U^{-}=\emptyset
), we return
X
. The pseudocode of our algorithm is shown in the technical report
(
14
)
.

Analysis

First, it is straightforward to see that
X
is a fair cover. The algorithm stops when there is no uncovered element so
X
is a cover. In every iteration, we add exactly one set of color
\mathbf{g}_{1}
and one set of color
\mathbf{g}_{2}
, so
X
is a fair cover.

Lemma 0.

|X|\leq(\ln n+1)\cdot|X^{*}|
.

Proof:

For each pair
S_{A},S_{B}\in\mathcal{S}
, let
S_{A,B}=S_{A}\cup S_{B}
.
We consider the following instance of the set cover problem.
We define the set
U^{\prime}=U
and
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}=\{S_{i,j}\mid S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{1},S_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}\}
. Let
C^{*}
be the optimum solution of the set cover instance
(U^{\prime},\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
. Any fair cover returned by our algorithm can be straightforwardly mapped to a valid set cover for
(U^{\prime},\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
.
Hence, by definition, we have
|C^{*}|\leq\frac{1}{2}|X^{*}|
.

Recall that the standard greedy algorithm for the set cover problem returns a
(\ln n+1)
-approximation.
We show that our algorithm implements such a greedy algorithm and returns a
(\ln n+1)
-approximation in the set cover instance
(U^{\prime},\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
.
At the beginning of an iteration
i
, without loss of generality, assume that our algorithm has selected the pairs
(S_{A_{1}},S_{B_{1}}),\ldots,(S_{A_{i-1}},S_{B_{i-1}})
, so the sets

S_{A_{1},B_{1}},\ldots,S_{A_{i-1},B_{i-1}}
have been selected for the set cover instance
(U^{\prime},\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
. Let
U^{-}
be the set of uncovered elements in
U^{\prime}
at the beginning of the
i
-th iteration.
Let
S_{A_{j},B_{j}}
be the set in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}
that covers the maximum number of elements in
U^{-}
. We consider two cases.

If
S_{A_{j}}\notin\{S_{A_{1}},\ldots,S_{A_{i-1}}\}
and
S_{B_{j}}\notin\{S_{B_{1}},\ldots,S_{B_{i-1}}\}
, then by definition our greedy algorithm selects the pair
(S_{A_{j}},S_{B_{j}})
so the set
S_{A_{j},B_{j}}
is added in the cover of the set cover instance
(U^{\prime},\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
.

Next, consider the case where
S_{A_{j}}\notin\{S_{A_{1}},\ldots,S_{A_{i-1}}\}
and
S_{B_{j}}\in\{S_{B_{1}},\ldots,S_{B_{i-1}}\}
(the proof is equivalent if the set
S_{A_{j}}
has been selected before). Since there are enough sets from each group, let
S_{B_{h}}
be a set with color
\mathbf{g}_{2}
such that
S_{B_{h}}\notin\{S_{B_{1}},\ldots,S_{B_{i-1}}\}
. By definition, we have
|S_{A_{j},B_{h}}\cap U^{-}|\geq|S_{A_{j},B_{j}}\cap U^{-}|
. Our greedy algorithm selects the pair
S_{A^{*}},S_{B^{*}}
such that
|(S_{A^{*}}\cup S_{B^{*}})\cap U^{-}|
is maximized, and
S_{A^{*}}\notin\{S_{A_{1}},\ldots,S_{A_{i-1}}\}
,
S_{B^{*}}\notin\{S_{B_{1}},\ldots,S_{B_{i-1}}\}
. Hence,
|S_{A^{*},B^{*}}\cap U^{-}|=|(S_{A^{*}}\cup S_{B^{*}})\cap U^{-}|\geq|(S_{A_{j}}\cup S_{B_{h}})\cap U^{-}|\geq|(S_{A_{j}}\cup S_{B_{j}})\cap U^{-}|=|S_{A_{j},B_{j}}\cap U^{-}|
.

In every iteration, our greedy algorithm computes a set
S_{A,B}
that covers the most uncovered elements in
U^{\prime}
.
Let
C
be the family of sets returned by the greedy algorithm executed on
(U^{\prime},\mathcal{S}^{\prime})
.
Every selected set in
C
is a pair of sets in
\mathcal{S}
.
Hence,

|X|\leq 2(\ln n+1)|C^{*}|
.
Furthermore,

|C^{*}|\leq\frac{1}{2}|X^{*}|
as shown in the first paragraph of the proof. We conclude that
|X|\leq(\ln n+1)|X^{*}|
.

\square

Notice that there exists
O(m_{1}m_{2})=O(m^{2})
pairs of sets with different colors. At the beginning of the algorithm, for every pair of sets we compute the number of elements they cover in
O(m^{2}n)
time. Each time we cover a new element we update the counters in
O(m^{2})
time. In total, our algorithm runs in
O(m^{2}n)
time.
This algorithm can be extended to
k
groups, straightforwardly. In each iteration, we find the
k
sets of different color that cover the the most uncovered elements. The running time increases to
O(m^{k}n)
.

Theorem 3.

There exists an
(\ln n+1)
-approximation algorithm for the FSC problem that runs in
O(m^{k}n)
time.

While our algorithm returns an
O(\log n)
-approximation, which is the best that someone can hope for the FSC, the running time depends
exponentially
on the number of groups
k
. Next, we propose two faster
O(\operatorname{poly}(n,m,k))
time randomized algorithms with the same asymptotic approximation.

4.3.
Faster algorithms

The expensive part of the greedy algorithm is the selection of
k
sets of different color that cover the most uncovered elements, in every iteration.
We focus on one iteration of the greedy algorithm.
Intuitively,
we need to find
k
sets, each from a different color, that maximizes the coverage over the uncovered elements.
We call this problem, max
k
-color cover. Formally,

Definition 0 (max
k
-color cover).

Consider a set
U^{-}\subseteq U
, called the set of uncovered elements,
a family of sets
\mathcal{S}^{-}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, called the sets not selected so far, and
the value
k
showing the number colors (groups) that sets belong to.
Our goal is to select
k
sets
X\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{-}
, one from each color, such that
\big|X\cap U^{-}\big|
is maximized.

The max
k
-color cover problem is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete.
The proof is straight-forward using the reduction from the traditional max
k
-cover (MC) problem by creating
k
copies of each original set, while assigning each copy to a unique color. Now the max-cover problem has a solution with coverage
\ell
, if and only if the
k
-color MC has a solution with coverage
\ell
.
Since max
k
-color cover is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete, we propose constant-approximation polynomial algorithms for it.

\frac{1}{2}
- approximation algorithm

Inspired by
(
32
)
, where the authors also study a version of the max
k
-color cover problem (to design efficient algorithms for the
fair regret minimizing set
problem), we propose the following algorithm.
We run the standard greedy algorithm for the max
k
-cover problem. Let
Y=U^{-}
be the current set of uncovered elements in
U^{-}
and let
C=\emptyset
be the family of
k
sets we return. The next steps are repeated until
|C|=k
or
Y=\emptyset
. In each iteration, the algorithm chooses the set
S\in\mathcal{S}^{-}
such that
|S\cap Y|
is maximized. If
C
contains another set with color
\mathbf{g}(S)
, then we remove
S
and we continue with the next iteration. Otherwise, we add
S
in
C
and we update
Y=Y\setminus S
.
If
Y=\emptyset
and
C
does not contain sets from all groups, we add in
C
exactly one set per missing group.

Theorem 5.

There exists an
\frac{1}{2}
-approximation algorithm for the max
k
-color cover problem that runs in
O(n\mu)
time.

Proof:

By definition,
C
contains one set from each group in
\mathcal{G}
, so it is a valid
k
-color cover.
Let
S_{h}
be the
h
-th set added in
C
.
Without loss of generality, assume
\mathbf{g}(S_{h})=\mathbf{g}_{h}
, for
h=1,\ldots,k
. Let
O^{*}=\{O_{1},\ldots,O_{k}\}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{-}
be the optimum solution for the max
k
-color cover problem. Without loss of generality, assume that
\mathbf{g}(O_{h})=\mathbf{g}_{h}
, for
h=1,\ldots,k
. We have,

\displaystyle|C|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left|S_{i}\setminus(\cup_{h=1..i-1}S_{h}\right)|\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left|O_{i}\setminus\cup_{h=1}^{i-1}S_{h}\right|\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left|O_{i}\setminus C\right|

\displaystyle\geq|O^{*}|-|C|.

Hence
|C|\geq\frac{1}{2}|O^{*}|
. Each time we cover a new element, we update the number of uncovered elements in each set in
O(\mu)
time. Hence, the algorithm runs in
O(n\mu)
time.

\square

(1-\frac{1}{e})
-approximation algorithm

We design a randomized
LP
-based algorithm for the max
k
-color cover problem with better approximation ratio.
For every element
e_{j}\in U^{-}
we define the variable
y_{j}
. For every set
S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}
we define the variable
x_{i}
. Let
\mathcal{S}_{h}^{-}=\{S\in\mathcal{S}^{-}\mid S\in\mathcal{S}_{h}\}
.
The next Integer Program represents an instance of the max
k
-color cover problem.

\displaystyle\max\sum_{j}y_{j}

\displaystyle\sum_{i:S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}_{h}}x_{i}

\displaystyle=1,\quad\forall\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle\sum_{i:e_{j}\in S_{i}}x_{i}

\displaystyle\geq y_{j},\quad\forall e_{j}\in U^{-}

\displaystyle x_{i}\in\{0,1\},\forall S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{-},\quad

\displaystyle y_{j}\in\{0,1\},\forall e_{j}\in U^{-}

We then replace the integer variables to continuous (
x_{i}\in[0,1]
and
y_{j}\in[0,1]
) and use a polynomial time
LP
solver to compute the solution of the
LP
relaxation.
Let
x_{i}^{*}
,
y_{j}^{*}
be the values of the variables in the optimum solution.
For every group
\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}
, we sample exactly one set from
\mathcal{S}^{-}_{h}
, using the probabilities
\{x_{i}^{*}\mid S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}_{h}\}
.
Let
C
be the family of
k
sets we return.

Analysis

By definition
C
contains exactly one set from every group
\mathbf{g}_{i}
. Next, we show the approximation factor.

Lemma 0.

An element
e_{j}
is covered by a set in
C
with probability at least
(1-\frac{1}{e})y_{j}^{*}
.

Proof:

By definition,
e_{j}
is not covered by a set in
\mathcal{S}^{-}_{h}
with probability

1-\sum_{i:e_{j}\in S_{i},S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}_{h}}x_{i}^{*}\leq e^{-\sum_{i:e_{j}\in S_{i},S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}_{h}}x_{i}^{*}}.

The inequality holds because of the well know inequality
1+x\leq e^{x}
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle Pr[e_{j}\notin C]

\displaystyle=\prod_{\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}}(1-\sum_{i:e_{j}\in S_{i},S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}_{h}}x_{i}^{*})\leq\prod_{\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}}e^{-\sum_{i:e_{j}\in S_{i},S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}_{h}}x_{i}^{*}}

\displaystyle=e^{-\sum_{\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{i:e_{j}\in S_{i},S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}_{h}}x_{i}^{*}}=e^{-\sum_{i:e_{j}\in S_{i}}x_{i}^{*}}\leq e^{-y_{j}^{*}}.

We conclude that

Pr[e_{j}\in C]\geq 1-e^{-y_{j}^{*}}\geq(1-\frac{1}{e})y_{j}^{*}
.

\square

Lemma 0.

The expected number of elements covered by sets in
C
is at least
(1-\frac{1}{e})\mathsf{OPT}
, where
\mathsf{OPT}

is the number of covered elements in the optimum solution for the max
k
-color cover problem.

Proof:

Let
\mathsf{OPT}_{LP}=\sum_{j}y_{j}^{*}
be the optimum solution of the LP above.
Let
Z_{j}
be a random variable which is
1
if
e_{j}
is covered in
C
, and
0
otherwise.
We have

\displaystyle E\Big[\sum_{j}Z_{j}\Big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}E[Z_{j}]=\sum_{j}Pr[Z_{j}=1]=\sum_{j}Pr[e_{j}\in C]

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{j}(1-\frac{1}{e})y_{j}^{*}=(1-\frac{1}{e})\mathsf{OPT}_{LP}\geq(1-\frac{1}{e})\mathsf{OPT}.\vskip-5.69054pt

\square

Let
\mathcal{L}(a,b)
be the running time to solve an
LP
with
a
constraints and
b
variables. Our algorithm for the max
k
-color cover problem runs in
O(\mathcal{L}(n+k,2n+2\mu))
time.

Putting everything together, we conclude with the next theorem.

Theorem 8.

There exists a randomized
(1-\frac{1}{e})
-approximation algorithm for the max
k
-color cover problem that runs in
O(\mathcal{L}(n+k,2n+2\mu))
time. The approximation factor holds in expectation.

Faster algorithm for FSC

We combine the results from Theorem
3
and Theorem
8
(or Theorem
5
) to get a faster algorithm for the FSC problem.
In each iteration of the greedy algorithm from Theorem
3
we execute the algorithm from Theorem
8
(or Theorem
5
).
The pseudocode of the overall method is shown in the technical report
(
14
)
.

It is known
(
51
)
that the greedy algorithm for the standard Set Cover problem, where in each iteration it selects a set that covers a
\beta
-approximation (for
\beta<1
) of the maximum number of uncovered elements returns a
\frac{1}{\beta}(\ln n+1)
-approximation. From the proof of Lemma
2
we mapped the FSC problem to an instance of the standard set cover problem, so the approximation ratio of our new algorithm is
\frac{e}{e-1}(\ln n+1)
(or
2(\ln n+1)
).

The algorithm from Theorem
8
runs in
O(\mathcal{L}(n+k,2n+2m\cdot k))
. We execute this algorithm in every iteration of the greedy algorithm. In the worst case the greedy algorithm selects all sets in
\mathcal{S}
, so a loose upper bound on the number of iterations is
O(m)
.
The number of iterations can also be bounded as follows. If
I_{\mathsf{Greedy}}
is the number of iterations of the greedy algorithm, then by definition it holds that
k\cdot I_{\mathsf{Greedy}}\leq\frac{e}{e-1}(\ln n+1)|X^{*}|\Leftrightarrow I_{\mathsf{Greedy}}=O(\frac{|X^{*}|}{k}\log n)
.

Theorem 9.

There exists a randomized
\frac{e}{e-1}(\ln n+1)
-approximation algorithm for the FSC problem that runs in

O(\frac{|X^{*}|}{k}\mathcal{L}(n+k,2n+2m\cdot k)\log n)
time. The approximation factor holds in expectation. Furthermore, there exists a
2(\ln n+1)
-approximation algorithm that runs in

O(|X^{*}|\cdot n\cdot m\cdot\log n)
time.

5.
Weighted Fair Set Cover

Throughout this section we define
\Delta=\frac{w_{\mathsf{max}}}{w_{\mathsf{min}}}
, where
w_{\mathsf{max}}=\max\{w(S_{i})|S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}\}
and
w_{\mathsf{min}}=\min\{w(S_{i})|S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}\}
.
In this section we need the assumption that for every group
\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}
there are at least
\Omega(\Delta\log n)\cdot\frac{|X^{*}|}{k}
sets.
In Appendix
A
, we show that a naive algorithm for the FWSC, similar to the naive algorithm we proposed for the FSC returns a
k\Delta(\ln n+1)
-approximation algorithm that runs in
O(m\cdot k\cdot n)
time.

Theorem 1.

There exists a
k\Delta(\ln n+1)
-approximation algorithm for the FWSC problem that runs in
O(m\cdot k\cdot n)
time.

5.1.
Greedy Algorithm

For simplicity, we describe our algorithm for the case we have only two demographic groups, i.e.,
\mathcal{G}=\{\mathbf{g}_{1},\mathbf{g}_{2}\}
. In the end, our algorithm can be extended almost verbatim to
k>2
groups.
Let
X_{w}=\emptyset
be the cover we construct and let
U^{-}=U
be the set of uncovered elements.
We repeat the following steps until
U^{-}=\emptyset
.
In each iteration, we find the pair of sets
(S_{A},S_{B})
such that i) the ratio
\frac{w(S_{A})+w(S_{B})}{|(S_{A}\cup S_{B})\cap U^{-}|}
is minimized, and ii)
\mathbf{g}(S_{A})=\mathbf{g}_{1}
and
\mathbf{g}(S_{B})=\mathbf{g}_{2}
.
Once we find
S_{A},S_{B}
we update the set
U^{-}
removing the new covered element,
U^{-}\leftarrow U^{-}\setminus(S_{A}\cup S_{B})
, and we add
S_{A},S_{B}
in
X_{w}
. In the end, after covering all elements we return
X_{w}
.

The proof of the next Theorem can be found in Appendix
A
.

Theorem 2.

There exists a
\Delta(\ln n+1)
-approximation algorithm for the FWSC problem that runs in
O(m^{k}n)
time.

5.2.
Faster algorithm

The expensive part of the previous greedy algorithm is the selection of
k
sets of different color to minimize the ratio of the weight over the number of new covered elements, in every iteration.
We focus on one iteration of the greedy algorithm, solving an instance of the
weighted max
k
-color cover
problem.
We are given a set of
n
elements
U^{-}\subseteq U
, a family of
\mu
sets
\mathcal{S}^{-}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
, a weight function
w:\mathcal{S}^{-}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
, and a set of
k
groups
\mathcal{G}
such that every set belongs to a group. The goal is to select a family of exactly
k
sets
X_{w}\subseteq\mathcal{S}^{-}
such that i)
\frac{w(X_{w})}{|X_{w}\cap U^{-}|}
is minimized, and ii) there exists exactly one set from every color
\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}
.
An efficient constant approximation algorithm for the weighted max
k
-color cover problem could be used in every iteration of the greedy algorithm to get an
O(\Delta\log n)
-approximation in
O(\operatorname{poly}(n,m,k))
time.

Algorithm

We design a randomized
LP
-based algorithm for the weighted max
k
-color cover problem. We note that our new algorithm has significant changes compared to the
LP
-based algorithm we proposed for the unweighted case. The reason is that the objective of finding a fair cover
X_{w}
that minimizes
\frac{w(X_{w})}{|X_{w}\cap U^{-}|}
can not be represented as a linear constraint. Instead, we iteratively solve a constrained version of the weighted max
k
-color cover problem:
For a parameter
\tau\in[1,n]
, the goal is to select a family of exactly
k
sets
X_{w}\subset\mathcal{S}^{-}
such that i)
w(X_{w})
is minimized, ii)
|X_{w}\cap U^{-}|\geq\tau
, and iii) there exists exactly one set from every color
\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}
in
X_{w}
.
The main idea of our algorithm is that for each
\tau=1,\ldots,n
, we compute efficiently an approximate solution for the constrained weighted max
k
-color cover problem. In the end, we return the best solution over all
\tau
’s.

For every
\tau=1,\ldots,n
, we solve an instance of the constrained weighted max
k
-color cover problem.
The next Integer Program represents an instance of the constrained weighted max
k
-color cover problem with respect to
\tau
.

\displaystyle\min\sum_{S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}}w(S_{i})

\displaystyle x_{i}

\displaystyle\sum_{i:S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}_{h}}x_{i}

\displaystyle=1,\quad\forall\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle\sum_{i:e_{j}\in S_{i}}x_{i}

\displaystyle\geq y_{j},\quad\forall e_{j}\in U^{-}

\displaystyle\sum_{e_{j}\in U^{-}}y_{j}

\displaystyle\geq\tau

\displaystyle x_{i}\in\{0,1\},\forall S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{-},\quad

\displaystyle y_{j}\in\{0,1\},\forall e_{j}\in U^{-}

We then replace the integer variables to continuous (
x_{i}\in[0,1]
and
y_{j}\in[0,1]
), and use a polynomial time
LP
solver to compute the solution of the
LP
relaxation.

Let
x_{i}^{(\tau)}
,
y_{j}^{(\tau)}
be the values of the variables in the optimum solution.
For every group
\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}
, we sample exactly one set from
\mathcal{S}^{-}_{h}
, using the probabilities
x_{i}^{(\tau)}
.
Let
C^{(\tau)}
be the sampled family of
k
sets. If
|C^{(\tau)}\cap U^{-}|<\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{e})\tau
, then we skip the sampled family
C^{(\tau)}
and we re-sample from scratch. In the end, let
C^{(\tau)}
be a family of
k
sets returned by the sampling procedure such that
|C^{(\tau)}\cap U^{-}|\geq\frac{1}{2}(1-\frac{1}{e})\tau
.
Finally, we compute
r^{(\tau)}=\frac{\sum_{S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}}w(S_{i})x_{i}^{(\tau)}}{|C^{(\tau)}\cap U^{-}|}
.
We repeat the algorithm for every
\tau=1,\ldots,n
, and we set

\tau^{*}=\argmin_{\tau=1,\ldots,n}r^{(\tau)}
.
We return the family of
k
sets
C^{(\tau^{*})}
.

We show the proof of the next theorem in Appendix
A
.

Theorem 3.

There exists a randomized
\frac{e}{e-1}
-approximation algorithm for the weighted max
k
-color cover problem that runs in
O(n\mathcal{L}(n+k,2n+2\mu+1)+kn^{3})
time.
The approximation factor and the running time hold in expectation.

Faster algorithm for FWSC

and Theorem

6.
Generalized Fair Set Cover

Due to space limitations, we defer the details of our algorithms for GFSC and GFWSC problems to Appendix
B
.
Recall that in these problems, each color
\mathbf{g}_{h}
is associated with a fraction
f_{h}\in[0,1]
, such that
\sum_{\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}}f_{h}=1
, and the goal is to return a (fair) cover
X
such that
\frac{|X\cap\mathcal{S}_{h}|}{|X|}=f_{h}
, for every color
\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}
.
Our greedy (and faster greedy) algorithms from the previous sections (for the FSC and FWSC problems) easily extend to GFSC and GFWSC with similar theoretical guarantees. The high level idea is the following: In each iteration of the greedy algorithm, instead of selecting exactly one set from each group, the algorithm selects
p_{h}
sets for group
\mathbf{g}_{h}
. The selection is made such that
f_{h}=\frac{p_{h}}{\sum_{\mathbf{g}_{j}\in\mathcal{G}}p_{j}}
is satisfied for each group
\mathbf{g}_{h}
. As the greedy algorithm operates for integral rounds, the final set cover also satisfies the ratio constraints.

6.1.
The
\varepsilon
-GFSC problem

Our algorithms for the GFSC problem can be used
for the less constrained
\varepsilon
-GFSC problem, where, instead of zero unfairness, the goal is to ensure a maximum unfairness of
\varepsilon
. Particularly,
in Theorem
1
, we show that a solver for the GFSC problem provides a good approximation for the
\varepsilon
-GFSC problem.

Theorem 1.

Any
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for the GFSC problem is an
\left((1+\varepsilon)\alpha\right)
-approximation for the
\varepsilon
-GFSC problem.

Proof:

Let
X^{*}
be the optimum solution to the GFSC problem and let
X^{*}_{\varepsilon}
be the optimum solution to the
\varepsilon
-GFSC problem.
Starting from
X^{*}_{\varepsilon}
we find a feasible cover
X_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}
for the GFSC problem.
Initially,
X_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}=X^{*}_{\varepsilon}
.
Then, we add

(1+\varepsilon)f_{h}|X^{*}_{\varepsilon}|-|X^{*}_{\varepsilon}\cap\mathcal{S}_{h}|
sets from group
\mathbf{g}_{h}
, in
X^{*}_{\varepsilon}
.
In the end, notice that
|X_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{S}_{h}|=(1+\varepsilon)f_{h}|X^{*}_{\varepsilon}|
for every group
\mathbf{g}_{h}
, and
|X_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}|=(1+\varepsilon)|X^{*}_{\varepsilon}|
.
Hence,
\frac{|X_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{S}_{h}|}{|X_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}|}=f_{h}
, so
X_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}
is a solution for the GFSC problem and
|X^{*}|\leq|X_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}|
, by definition. We conclude that
|X^{*}|\leq|X_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}|=(1+\varepsilon)|X^{*}_{\varepsilon}|
.
Let
\mathcal{A}
be an
\alpha
-approximation algorithm for the GFSC and
X
be the output of
\mathcal{A}
. We have,

|X|\leq\alpha|X^{*}|\leq(1+\varepsilon)\alpha|X^{*}_{\varepsilon}|
.

\square

As a result, all the approximation algorithms proposed in the previous sections also approximate the
\varepsilon
-GFSC problem with asymptotically the same approximation ratio of
O(\ln n)
.

8.
Related Work

Algorithmic fairness has received a lot of attention in the last few years.
However, most of the recent work focuses on the intersection of fairness and Machine Learning
(
7
,
36
)
.

Despite its importance, limited work has been done on algorithmic fairness in
the context of combinatorial problems
(
49
)
. Satisfying fairness constraints while optimizing sub-modular functions is studied in
(
49
)
. Further examples include satisfying fairness in Coverage Maximization problems
(
2
,
4
)
and Facility Locations
(
29
)
.
In the red-blue set cover problem
(
10
)
, the goal is to select a family of sets to cover all red points, while minimizing the number of blue points covered.
In this body of work, the focus is on defining fairness on the point set (ground set). In other words, they assume the points belong to different demographic groups. In contrast, in this work, we define fairness on the family of sets.
Fairness is also studied in problems like Matching
(
20
,
19
)
,
Resource Allocation
(
35
)
, Ranking
(
3
,
52
)
, Queue Systems
(
15
)
, and Clustering
(
33
,
44
)
.

There has been recent work studying fairness in the Hitting Set problem
(
25
)
. Although hitting set is the dual of the set cover problem, the definition of fairness used is different from ours. We define and guarantee perfect fairness based on demographic parity (count and ratio parity).
In other words, we require an exact equality (e.g., equality of count equality or exact color ratio) between demographic groups.
By contrast, in
(
25
)
, a hitting set is defined to be fair if does not contain
many
points from a color. They have upper bounds on the number of points (equivalent to the subsets in the set cover problem) that can be picked from any particular color.
Furthermore, they design exact (fixed-parameter) algorithms with running time exponential in
k
and/or exponential on maximum number of sets that an element belongs to.

The Fair Team Formation Problem and the fair set cover problem with two groups are also discussed in
(
5
)
. The authors form a fair team of experts, covering a specific set of skills and, at the same time, being fair in terms of sensitive attributes. They focus on the binary case where individuals only belong to one of the two demographic groups. They present inapproximability results and some some heuristics. The practical experiments are limited to a dataset of around 6 skills and 1211 individuals.

9.
Conclusion

Set cover has an extended scope for solving real-world problems with societal impact. In this paper we revisited this problem through the lens of fairness.
We adopted the group fairness notion of demographic parity, and proposed a general formulation that extends to various cases such as count-parity and ratio-parity for binary and non-binary demographic groups.
We formulated set cover and weighted set cover under fairness constraint and proposed approximation algorithms that (a) always guarantee fairness, (b) have (almost) the same approximation ratio and similar time complexity with the the greedy algorithm for set cover (without fairness).
Our experiments on real and synthetic data across different settings demonstrated the high performance of our algorithms.

p_{h}
sets.
Similarly to the proof of Lemma
6
, we have,

\displaystyle Pr[e_{j}

\displaystyle\text{ not covered by a set in }C]\leq\prod_{\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}}\left(1-\sum_{i:e_{j}\in S_{i},S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}_{h}}\frac{x_{i}^{*}}{p_{h}}\right)^{p_{h}}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}}\left(\frac{1}{e^{\sum_{i:e_{j}\in S_{i},S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}_{h}}\frac{x_{i}^{*}}{p_{h}}}}\right)^{p_{h}}=\prod_{\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}}\frac{1}{e^{\sum_{i:e_{j}\in S_{i},S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}_{h}}x_{i}^{*}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{e^{\sum_{\mathbf{g}_{h}\in\mathcal{G}}\sum_{i:e_{j}\in S_{i},S_{i}\in\mathcal{S}^{-}_{h}}x_{i}^{*}}}=\frac{1}{e^{\sum_{i:e_{j}\in S_{i}}x_{i}^{*}}}\leq\frac{1}{e^{y_{j}^{*}}}.

Let
Z_{i}
be a random variable that returns
1
if
e_{j}\in C
, and
0
otherwise. We have,

E[\sum_{j}Z_{j}]\geq(1-\frac{1}{e})OPT_{LP}\geq(1-\frac{1}{e})OPT
.

After solving the
LP
in
\mathcal{L}(n+k,2n+2m\cdot k)
time, we sample
p_{h}
times from each color
\mathbf{g}_{h}
. Overall, we spend
O(\mathcal{L}(n+k\cdot 2n+2m\cdot k)+p)
time.
The randomized rounding algorithm is executed in every iteration of the greedy algorithm.
The greedy algorithm executes
O(m)
iterations. Using an analysis similar to §
4.3
, we bound the expected number of iterations to
O(\frac{|X^{*}|}{p}\log n)

Following the same arguments as in §
4.3
, we conclude with the next theorem.

Theorem 2.

There is a randomized
\frac{e}{e-1}(\ln n+1)
-approximation algorithm for the Generalized FSC problem that runs in
O(m(p+\mathcal{L}(n+k,2n+2m\cdot k)))
time. The approximation factor holds in expectation. The same algorithm also runs in
O(\frac{|X^{*}|}{p}\mathcal{L}(n+k,2n+2m\cdot k)\log n)
expected time.

B.3.
Extensions to GFWSC

Using the results in §
5
, all our algorithms for the weighted case can be extended to the generalized FSC. Skipping the details, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 3.

There exists a
\Delta(\ln n+1)
-approximation algorithm for the Generalized FWSC problem that runs in
O(m^{p}n)
time.

Furthermore, there exists a randomized
\frac{e}{e-1}\Delta(\ln n+1)
-approximation algorithm for the Generalized FWSC problem that runs in
O(mn\cdot\mathcal{L}(n+k,2n+2m\cdot k+1)+mpn^{3})
time. The approximation factor and the running time hold in expectation.
