Skewing the CMB × \times LSS: a Fast Method for Bispectrum Analysis
Priyesh Chakraborty, Shu-Fan Chen, Cora Dvorkin

Abstract
Upcoming cosmic microwave background (CMB) lensing measurements and tomographic galaxy surveys are expected to provide us with high-precision data sets in the coming years, thus paving the way for fruitful cross-correlation analyses. In this paper we study the information content of the weighted skew-spectrum, a nearly-optimal estimator of the angular bispectrum amplitude, as a means to extract non-Gaussian information on both bias and cosmological parameters from the bispectra of galaxies cross-correlated with CMB lensing, while gaining signiﬁcantly on speed. Our results show that for the combination of the Planck satellite and the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), the difference in the constraints on bias and cosmological parameters from the skew-spectrum and the bispectrum is at most 17%. We further compare and find agreement between our theoretical skew-spectra and those estimated from N-body simulations, for which it is important to include gravitational non-linearities beyond perturbation theory and the post-Born eﬀect for CMB lensing. We define an algorithm to apply the skew-spectrum estimator to the data and, as a preliminary step, we use the skew-spectra to constrain bias parameters and the amplitude of shot noise from the simulations through a Markov chain Monte Carlo likelihood analysis, finding that it may be possible to reach percent-level estimates for the linear bias parameter b 1 b_{1} .

Skew-Spectrum

To begin our discussion, we first note that any generic (local) quadratic field in harmonic space
D_{\ell m}
is defined as a convolution of fields

\end{array}}\right)K_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell}\,\delta_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}\delta_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}\,,

Given the minimum variance (MV) estimator of the bispectrum amplitude, one can ask for the choice of kernels which minimizes loss of information when cross-correlated with a single density fluctuation from the full bispectrum. Namely, we are interested in the quantity,

\displaystyle=\delta^{\rm K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{\rm K}_{mm^{\prime}}\tilde{C}_{\ell}\,,

(2.3)

where we call

the skew-spectrum and

\mathbf{P}_{\ell}
for
\ell=0,\,1,\,2

This is strictly true only for tree-level bispectra.

Power Spectrum and Bispectrum in Harmonic Space

We will begin by spelling out the power spectrum and bispectrum of the projected galaxy and CMB lensing convergence fields used in our analysis and in particular we will emphasize properties of the bispectrum that are crucial for a fast implementation of the skew-spectrum.

The CMB weak lensing convergence field

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}\Omega_{m0}H_{0}^{2}\frac{1+z(\chi)}{H(\chi)}\frac{\chi(\chi_{s}-\chi)}{\chi_{s}}\theta(\chi_{s}-\chi),

2.2
Skew-Spectrum Estimator

We will briefly review the derivation of the skew-spectrum in this section.
We follow a similar procedure as in
[
20
,
19
]
, but modified for more than one field and in harmonic space.

For the moment, let us consider a single observed field
\delta_{\ell m}
. Under the regime of weak non-Gaussianity, the MV estimator for the amplitude
f_{\rm NL}
of the bispectrum is given as
[
17
]

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\rm NL}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{(4\pi f_{\text{sky}})^{2}}\sum_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}g_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{2}\frac{b_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{\rm th}\hat{b}^{\rm obs}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}}{C_{\ell_{1}}C_{\ell_{2}}C_{\ell_{3}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{(4\pi f_{\text{sky}})^{2}}\sum_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}\sum_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}\sum_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}{\cal G}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{m_{1}m_{2}m_{3}}\,b_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{\rm th}\frac{\delta_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}\delta_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}\delta_{\ell_{3}m_{3}}}{C_{\ell_{1}}C_{\ell_{2}}C_{\ell_{3}}}\,,

(2.21)

where
\sigma^{2}
is the inverse variance of the estimator,
b^{\rm th}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}
is the theoretical template, and
\hat{b}^{\rm obs}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}
is the observed bispectrum. While we denote the estimator as
\hat{f}_{\rm NL}
, it can be applied to estimate the bispectrum amplitude of any non-Gaussian field. For example, when applied to the halo bispectrum, it may be used to infer the halo bias parameters.

Now we want to recast equation
2.21
as a sum over estimators of two-point statistics
\hat{\tilde{C}}_{\ell}
. Schematically, we want

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\rm NL}

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{\ell}\hat{\tilde{C}}_{\ell}\,.

(2.22)

It is straightforward to verify that this reorganization is possible by identifying the quadratic field:

\displaystyle D_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\sum_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}\sum_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}{\cal G}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell}^{m_{1}m_{2}(-m)}\,b^{\text{th}}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell}\frac{\delta_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}}{C_{\ell_{1}}}\frac{\delta_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}}{C_{\ell_{2}}}\,.

(2.23)

The odd factor
(-1)^{m}
is simply a choice of phase which allows the skew-spectrum estimator to assume the canonical form of an estimator of a two-point function. Thus, we see that the requisite kernel to obtain the quadratic field is simply the theoretical template. Upon cross-correlating this quadratic field with a filtered field we obtain

\displaystyle\left\langle D_{\ell m}\frac{\delta^{*}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}}{C_{\ell^{\prime}}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta^{\rm K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{\rm K}_{mm^{\prime}}\sum_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}\frac{g_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell}^{2}}{2\ell+1}\frac{b_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell}^{\text{th}}b_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell}^{\rm obs}}{C_{\ell_{1}}C_{\ell_{2}}C_{\ell}}=\delta^{\rm K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{\rm K}_{mm^{\prime}}\tilde{C}_{\ell},

(2.24)

and we call this statistic the skew-spectrum. Consequently, the estimator for the skew-spectrum can be directly given as

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{C}}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{m}D_{\ell m}\frac{\delta_{\ell m}^{*}}{C_{\ell}}\,.

(2.25)

For multiple fields, this statistic can be generalized straightforwardly. For simplicity, let us define
\delta_{\ell m}^{a}/C^{a}_{\ell}\equiv\tilde{\delta}_{\ell m}^{a}
. The quadratic field then becomes

\displaystyle D^{ab}_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\sum_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}\sum_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}{\cal G}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell}^{m_{1}m_{2}(-m)}\,b_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell}^{aab,\rm th}\tilde{\delta}^{a}_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}\tilde{\delta}^{a}_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}\,,

(2.26)

where we always assume that the quadratic field comes from only one field. We may cross-correlate this quadratic field with another field to obtain the skew-spectrum, which is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\left\langle D_{\ell m}^{ab}\tilde{\delta}^{b*}_{\ell^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta^{\rm K}_{\ell\ell^{\prime}}\delta^{\rm K}_{mm^{\prime}}\tilde{C}_{\ell}^{ab}

(2.27)

and

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{\ell}^{ab}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}}\frac{g_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell}^{2}}{2\ell+1}\frac{b_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell}^{aab,\text{th}}b_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell}^{aab,\rm obs}}{C^{a}_{\ell_{1}}C^{a}_{\ell_{2}}C^{b}_{\ell}}\,.

(2.28)

This quantity can also be readily estimated using standard power spectrum techniques,

\displaystyle\hat{\tilde{C}}^{ab}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\sum_{m}D^{ab}_{\ell m}\tilde{\delta}^{b*}_{\ell m}\,.

(2.29)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\ell+1}\frac{C_{\ell}^{D^{ab,i},D^{a^{\prime}b^{\prime},j}}C_{\ell}^{bb^{\prime}}}{C_{\ell}^{b}C_{\ell}^{b^{\prime}}}

2.4
Data Application Algorithm

While the bispectrum of Fourier modes benefits from factorization due to momentum conservation, the same is not true for the angular bispectrum. Namely, it is possible to write the bispectrum in momentum space into a separable form,

\displaystyle B(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}f_{i}(k_{1})g_{i}(k_{2})h_{i}(k_{3})

(2.41)

for some functions
f_{i}
,
g_{i}
and
h_{i}
, but not for the one in harmonic space because of the line-of-sight projection. This factorizability in momentum space is crucial to recast the quadratic field as a convolution and thus improve the computational speed of the skew-spectrum estimation using fast Fourier transform algorithms
[
20
,
19
]
. In order to achieve the same for angular bispectra, recall that the kernel can be expressed as

\displaystyle h^{abc}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{3}}c^{abc}_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}r\,r^{2}\tilde{I}^{ac}_{\ell_{1}}(r;n_{1})\tilde{I}^{bc}_{\ell_{2}}(r;n_{2})\tilde{I}^{c}_{\ell_{3}}(r;n_{3})\,.

(2.42)

Thus, we can organize the quadratic field as follows,

\displaystyle D_{\ell m}^{ab}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\sum_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{3}}c^{aab}_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{3}}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}r\,r^{2}\sum_{\ell_{1}m_{1}}\sum_{\ell_{2}m_{2}}\mathcal{G}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell}^{m_{1}m_{2}(-m)}(\tilde{I}_{\ell_{1}}^{ab}(r,n_{1})\tilde{\delta}^{a}_{\ell_{1}m_{1}})(\tilde{I}^{ab}_{\ell_{2}}(r,n_{2})\tilde{\delta}^{a}_{\ell_{2}m_{2}})\tilde{I}_{\ell}^{b}(r,n_{3})

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\sum_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{3}}c^{aab}_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\rm d}r\,r^{2}D_{\ell m}^{ab}(r;n_{1},n_{2})\tilde{I}_{\ell}^{b}(r,n_{3})\,,

(2.43)

where

D_{\ell m}^{ab}(r;n_{1},n_{2})=\int{\rm d}^{2}\hat{n}\,Y^{*}_{\ell m}(\hat{n})\Delta^{ab}(\hat{n};r,n_{1})\Delta^{ab}(\hat{n};r,n_{2})\,,

(2.44)

and
\Delta^{ab}
is defined as the field in position space with harmonic coefficients being

\Delta_{\ell m}^{ab}(r,n)=\tilde{I}_{\ell}^{ab}(r,n)\tilde{\delta}_{\ell m}^{a}\,.

(2.45)

Similar to the skew-spectrum in Fourier space, in order to get
D_{\ell m}^{ab}
, instead of summing over all the spherical harmonic coefficients as in equation
2.43
, we can directly multiply the two
\Delta^{ab}
fields in position space. Finally, we only need to cross-correlate the quadratic field
D_{\ell m}^{ab}
with
\tilde{\delta}^{b}_{\ell m}
in harmonic space. We summarize the roadmap as follows:

Skew-Spectrum Estimation Algorithm

1.

Obtain fields
\delta^{a}(\hat{n})
and
\delta^{b}(\hat{n})
.

2.

Compute the filtered harmonic coefficients,
\tilde{\delta}_{\ell m}^{a}
and
\tilde{\delta}_{\ell m}^{b}
.

3.

Pointwise product between
\tilde{\delta}_{\ell m}^{a}
and
\tilde{I}_{\ell}^{ab}(r,n_{i})
to obtain
\Delta^{ab}_{\ell m}(r,n_{i})
.

4.

Return to position space to obtain
\Delta^{ab}(\hat{n};r,n_{i})
.

5.

Compute harmonic transform of
\Delta^{ab}(\hat{n};r,n_{1})\Delta^{ab}(\hat{n};r,n_{2})
.

6.

Product with
\tilde{I}^{b}_{\ell}(r,n_{3})
and integrate over
r
to obtain the quadratic field.

7.

Cross-correlate with
\tilde{\delta}^{b}_{\ell m}
to obtain
\hat{\tilde{C}}^{ab}_{\ell}
.

3.1
Methodology

We perform a Fisher forecast to study the information content of skew-spectra and full bispectra. The data vector for the skew-spectrum consists of the three kernels and cross-correlations with CMB lensing, so it can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}

\displaystyle=\{\tilde{C}_{\ell}^{g_{j}g_{j},i},\,\tilde{C}_{\ell}^{g_{j}\kappa,i},\,\tilde{C}_{\ell}^{\kappa g_{j},i},\,\tilde{C}_{\ell}^{\kappa\kappa,i}\}\,,

(3.1)

where
i
indicates the order of Legendre polynomials and
j
labels the tomographic bins. Note that the data vector should include all
i
,
j
and
\ell

\mathbf{C}
, is computed using equations
2.35
and
2.36
. Therefore, given parameters
\bm{\lambda}
of interests, we can write down the Fisher matrix
\mathbf{F}^{\rm skew}
as

\displaystyle F^{\rm skew}_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{d}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}\mathbf{C}^{-1}\frac{\partial\mathbf{d}^{\rm T}}{\partial\lambda_{\beta}}\,.

(3.2)

For the bispectrum, we consider the correlations
g_{j}g_{j}g_{j}
,
g_{j}g_{j}\kappa

and
\kappa\kappa\kappa

[

44

\displaystyle F_{\alpha\beta}^{\rm bisp}=\frac{f_{\rm sky}}{6}\sum_{abc}\sum_{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}c^{\prime}}\sum_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}g_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{2}\frac{\partial b_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{abc}}{\partial\lambda_{\alpha}}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\ell_{1}}^{\prime-1}\right)_{aa^{\prime}}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\ell_{2}}^{\prime-1}\right)_{bb^{\prime}}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\ell_{3}}^{\prime-1}\right)_{cc^{\prime}}\frac{\partial b_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}^{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}c^{\prime}}}{\partial\lambda_{\beta}}\,,

(3.3)

where
\left(C_{\ell}^{\prime}\right)_{aa^{\prime}}=C_{\ell}^{aa^{\prime}}+\delta^{\rm K}_{aa^{\prime}}N_{\ell}^{aa^{\prime}}
,
N_{\ell}
is the noise power spectrum, and

is the geometric factor defined in equation

\displaystyle{\rm Var}(\lambda_{\alpha})\geq\left(\mathbf{F}^{-1}\right)_{\alpha\alpha}\,.

(3.4)

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\{H_{0},\,\omega_{b},\,\omega_{c},\,A_{s},\,n_{s},\,\tau,\,M_{\nu}\}\cup\bigcup\limits_{i=1}^{N_{\rm bin}}\{\bar{b}_{1}^{i},\,\bar{b}_{2}^{i},\,\bar{b}_{{\cal G}_{2}}^{i}\}\cup\{A_{\text{shot}}\}\,,

4.1
Flat-Sky Approximation

Since the sky coverage of our simulation data is small, it is sufficient to use the flat-sky approximation here, which allows gains in speed while minimizing loss of information. To proceed, we note the following mappings between spherical harmonic coefficients and flat-sky Fourier modes
[
60
]
:

\displaystyle\delta_{\ell m}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}}i^{m}\int\frac{{\rm d}\varphi_{\ell}}{2\pi}e^{-im\varphi_{\ell}}\delta(\bm{\ell})\,,

(4.1)

\displaystyle\delta(\bm{\ell})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}}\sum_{m}i^{-m}\delta_{\ell m}e^{im\varphi_{\ell}}\,.

(4.2)

Using these mappings we obtain the quadratic filtered field,

\displaystyle D_{i}^{ab}(\bm{\ell})

\displaystyle=\int_{\bm{\ell}_{1}}\int_{\bm{\ell}_{2}}(2\pi)^{2}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{\ell}-\bm{\ell}_{1}-\bm{\ell}_{2})h^{aab}_{i}(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell}_{2},\bm{\ell})\delta^{a}(\bm{\ell}_{1})\delta^{a}(\bm{\ell}_{2})\,.

(4.3)

Here
h_{i}^{aab}
is analogous to the convolution kernel in full sky. While the bispectrum above is strictly the three-point function of flat-sky modes, for scales under consideration it is identical within a few percent to the full-sky bispectrum. Thus, we can approximate the flat-sky theoretical bispectrum with the full-sky one:

\displaystyle\left\langle\delta^{a}(\bm{\ell}_{1})\delta^{b}(\bm{\ell}_{2})\delta^{c}(\bm{\ell}_{3})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{2}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{3})b^{abc}(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell}_{2},\bm{\ell}_{3})

\displaystyle\approx(2\pi)^{2}\delta_{\rm D}(\bm{\ell}_{1}+\bm{\ell}_{2}+\bm{\ell}_{3})b^{abc}_{\ell_{1}\ell_{2}\ell_{3}}\,.

(4.4)

The theoretical skew-spectrum then becomes

\displaystyle\tilde{C}^{ab}_{i,L}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M_{L}}\sum_{\bm{\ell}\in L}\int_{\bm{\ell}_{1}}\frac{h_{i}^{aab}(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell-\ell}_{1},\bm{-\ell})b^{aab}(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell-\ell}_{1},\bm{-\ell})}{C^{a}_{\ell_{1}}C^{a}_{|\bm{\ell-\ell}_{1}|}C^{b}_{\ell}}\,,

(4.5)

where
L
labels the bin and
M_{L}
is the number of modes in bin
L
. We can straightforwardly write the estimator as

\hat{\tilde{C}}^{ab}_{i,L}=\frac{1}{M_{L}}\sum_{\bm{\ell}\in L}D^{ab}_{i}(\bm{\ell})\tilde{\delta}^{b}(-\bm{\ell})\,.

(4.6)

We account for binning by replacing the angular integral in Fourier space as
\int\tfrac{{\rm d}\varphi_{\ell}}{2\pi}\rightarrow\tfrac{1}{M_{L}}\sum_{\bm{\ell}\in L}
. Note that we can still have a fast estimation of the skew-spectrum with the use of the FFTLog algorithm in equation
4.3
, where convolution becomes a product of pixels of two maps in position space (see Section
2.4
).
Finally, the theoretical covariance is

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\tilde{C}}^{ab}_{i,L},\,\hat{\tilde{C}}^{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}_{j,L^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{LL^{\prime}}I_{ij,L}^{ab,a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}+\frac{1}{4\pi f_{\rm sky}}J_{ij,LL^{\prime}}^{ab,a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}\,,

(4.7)

where
I_{ij,L}
and
J_{ij,LL^{\prime}}
are

\displaystyle I_{ij,L}^{ab,a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{2}{M_{L}^{2}}\sum_{\bm{\ell}\in L}\int_{\bm{\ell}_{1}}\frac{C^{bb^{\prime}}_{\ell}}{C^{b}_{\ell}C^{b^{\prime}}_{\ell}}\frac{C_{\ell_{1}}^{aa^{\prime}}C_{|\bm{\ell-\ell}_{1}|}^{aa^{\prime}}}{C_{\ell_{1}}^{a}C_{|\bm{\ell-\ell}_{1}|}^{a}C_{\ell_{1}}^{a^{\prime}}C_{|\bm{\ell-\ell}_{1}|}^{a^{\prime}}}h_{i}^{aab}(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell-\ell}_{1},-\bm{\ell})h_{j}^{a^{\prime}a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}(\bm{\ell}_{1},\bm{\ell-\ell}_{1},-\bm{\ell})\,,

(4.8)

\displaystyle J_{ij,LL^{\prime}}^{ab,a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{4}{M_{LL^{\prime}}}\sum_{\bm{\ell}\in L,\,\bm{\ell}^{\prime}\in L^{\prime}}\frac{C_{\ell^{\prime}}^{ab^{\prime}}C_{\ell}^{ba^{\prime}}}{C_{\ell^{\prime}}^{a}C_{\ell}^{b}C_{\ell}^{a^{\prime}}C_{\ell^{\prime}}^{b^{\prime}}}\frac{C_{|\bm{\ell-\ell}^{\prime}|}^{aa^{\prime}}}{C_{|\bm{\ell-\ell}^{\prime}|}^{a}C_{|\bm{\ell-\ell}^{\prime}|}^{a^{\prime}}}h_{i}^{aab}(\bm{\ell-\ell}^{\prime},\bm{\ell}^{\prime},-\bm{\ell})h_{j}^{a^{\prime}a^{\prime}b^{\prime}}(\bm{\ell-\ell}^{\prime},-\bm{\ell},\bm{\ell}^{\prime}).

(4.9)

Here,
M_{LL^{\prime}}
denotes the number of modes from the combination of bins
L
and
L^{\prime}
. We show a comparison of the skew-spectrum between theoretical predictions and simulations in Figure
4
and observe a general agreement within error bars. A comparison between the theoretical covariance and the estimated covariance from simulations is shown in Appendix
C
, where we also see a general agreement. However, it also suggests a more accurate modeling of small-scale spectra is required since the covariance coming from simulations shows a larger amplitude for the
\kappa g
,
g\kappa
and
\kappa\kappa
spectra. This implies that the error bars in our Fisher analysis are likely underestimated.

5
Conclusions

We show in this paper that we can efficiently extract the cosmological information encoded in the bispectra of the cross-correlation between CMB lensing and the galaxy/halo field. We do so by introducing the bispectrum-weighted skew-spectrum as a proxy statistic to compress the full cross-correlated bispectra in LSS
\times
CMB lensing analysis. The problem thereafter simply becomes the estimation of the cross-correlated power spectra between a filtered quadratic field and a filtered density fields.

This procedure utilizes the separable form of the angular bispectrum given in equation
2.17
and the fact that convolution in harmonic space can be done by simple products in position space.

To show the power of the skew-spectrum, we demonstrate through a Fisher forecast that the information content of the bias parameters from the skew-spectrum is the same as that from the full bispectrum within
7\%
. We then move a step further to include the seven
\nu\Lambda
CDM cosmological parameters in our analysis and still find satisfactory agreement. Based on these results, we perform a forecast for DESI ELG
\times

Planck
lensing and find that the agreement is even better when we have more tomographic bins and the cross-correlation with CMB lensing. The differences between skew-spectra and full bispectra are less than
17\%
for most of the parameters we consider in our analysis, reaching at most 12% for all bias parameters.

We provide a fast pipeline to estimate the skew-spectrum from real data and, as a first step before application to real data, we apply our formalism to the
MassiveNuS
simulation suite. We perform an MCMC likelihood analysis on the halo bias parameters and
A_{\rm shot}
with the use of cross-correlated skew-spectra for the projected halo density field and the CMB lensing convergence field. Due to limitations in the available modes from the simulations we obtain modest constraints, especially on
A_{\text{shot}}
, a detection of which is limited by the amplitude of shot noise for our range of halo masses. Note that we assume a simple scenario in this analysis. For instance, we consider only a Gaussian likelihood and covariance. We should keep in mind that more rigorous studies on impacts from these assumptions are required before actual data analysis.

Realistic data will be affected by RSD and at small scales will be sensitive to loop corrections due to gravitational non-linearities and higher-order terms in the local bias expansion. A careful treatment of these effects is in place before applying our technique to real data. It will also be interesting to look into the kurt-spectra
[
62
]
for higher-point correlation functions. We leave these for future work.

Appendix A
Modelling of tracers

In this appendix we lay out the mapping that we use between matter overdensities and halo/galaxy overdensities, namely the model for bias and shot noise.

A.1
Bias

In order to compute correlation functions of tracer fluctuations, it is necessary to invoke a bias model, i.e. a perturbative model that connects the tracer to the underlying matter fluctuations. We consider the basis of renormalized bias expansion in Eulerian space
[
66
]
, which only requires up to quadratic terms for the tree-level bispectrum, namely,

\displaystyle\delta_{g}

\displaystyle=b_{1}\delta_{cb}+\frac{b_{2}}{2}\delta_{cb}^{2}+b_{{\cal G}_{2}}{\cal G}_{2}[\Phi_{cb}]+{\cal O}(\Phi_{cb}^{3})\,,

(A.1)

where
{\cal G}_{2}[\Phi_{cb}]=(\partial_{i}\partial_{j}\Phi_{cb})^{2}-(\partial^{2}\Phi_{cb})^{2}
is the Galileon operator and
\Phi_{cb}
is the gravitational potential due to the CDM and baryons alone
[
67
,
68
]
. Assuming a local Lagrangian bias model
[
42
]
and using N-body simulations
[
69
]
, it is possible to obtain fitting formulas for
b_{2}
and
b_{{\cal G}_{2}}
given prior knowledge of
b_{1}
. The relations are given by

\displaystyle b_{1}(z)

\displaystyle=\bar{b}_{1}\,p(z)\,,

(A.2)

\displaystyle b_{2}(z)

\displaystyle=\bar{b}_{2}\left(0.412-2.143\,p(z)+0.929\,p(z)^{2}+0.008\,p(z)^{3}\right)\,,

(A.3)

\displaystyle b_{{\cal G}_{2}}(z)

\displaystyle=\bar{b}_{{\cal G}_{2}}\left(0.423-1.000\,p(z)+0.310\,p(z)^{2}+0.003\,p(z)^{3}\right)\,,

(A.4)

where
\bar{b}_{1}
,
\bar{b}_{2}
and
\bar{b}_{{\cal G}_{2}}
are parameters for the overall amplitudes with fiducial values being equal to
1
in our forecasts. Here
p(z)
denotes the redshift evolution of the linear bias.

A.2
Shot Noise

The assumption that the halo catalogue is a result of a discrete Poisson sampling of the matter density field leads to the well-known shot noise in the halo power spectrum and bispectrum
[
28
,
70
]
,

\displaystyle P_{\text{Poisson}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{h}}

(A.5)

\displaystyle B_{\text{Poisson}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{h}}\left(P_{hh}(k_{1})+P_{hh}(k_{2})+P_{hh}(k_{3})\right)+\frac{1}{\bar{n}_{h}^{2}}\,,

(A.6)

where
\bar{n}_{h}
is the average number density of halos (or galaxies) in a given survey volume. However, the Poisson prediction is not exact due to halo clustering and exclusion effects, as shown in
[
45
]
. These effects either raise or lower the Poisson shot noise at large scales (referred to as super- or sub-Poissonian) and can be modelled phenomenologically as in
[
19
,
27
,
46
]
. In these studies, a parameter
A_{\text{shot}}
was introduced to measure deviations from the Poisson prediction. The model we use is as follows,

\displaystyle P_{\text{shot}}(k)

\displaystyle=P_{\text{Poisson}}(1-A_{\text{shot}})\,,

(A.7)

\displaystyle B_{\text{shot}}(k)

\displaystyle=B_{\text{Poisson}}(1-A_{\text{shot}})\,,

(A.8)

with
\absolutevalue{\ashot}\leq 1
.
Here,
A_{\text{shot}}
simply measures whether the true shot noise is higher or lower than the Poisson prediction, and remains scale-independent.
