Transductive Few-Shot Learning: Clustering is All You Need?
Imtiaz Masud Ziko, Malik Boudiaf, Jose Dolz, Eric Granger and Ismail Ben Ayed

Abstract
We investigate a general formulation for clustering and transductive few-shot learning, which integrates prototype-based objectives, Laplacian regularization and supervision constraints from a few labeled data points. We propose a concave-convex relaxation of the problem, and derive a computationally efficient block-coordinate bound optimizer, with convergence guarantee. At each iteration, our optimizer computes independent (parallel) updates for each point-to-cluster assignment. Therefore, it could be trivially distributed for large-scale clustering and few-shot tasks. Furthermore, we provides a thorough convergence analysis based on point-to-set maps. We report comprehensive clustering and few-shot learning experiments over various data sets, showing that our method yields competitive performances, in term of accuracy and optimization quality, while scaling up to large problems. Using standard training on the base classes, without resorting to complex meta-learning and episodic-training strategies, our approach outperforms state-of-the-art few-shot methods by significant margins, across various models, settings and data sets. Surprisingly, we found that even standard clustering procedures (e.g., K-means), which correspond to particular, non-regularized cases of our general model, already achieve competitive performances in comparison to the state-of-the-art in few-shot learning. These surprising results point to the limitations of the current few-shot benchmarks, and question the viability of a large body of convoluted few-shot learning techniques in the recent literature. Our code is publicly available at https://github.com/imtiazziko/SLK-few-shot .



I
Introduction

Deep learning models achieve outstanding performances when trained on large-scale labeled data sets. However, they are seriously challenged when dealing with new classes that were not observed during training, even if a handful of labeled samples becomes available for these new classes. To address this challenge, few-shot learning methods have recently attracted wide interest within the machine learning and computer vision communities. In standard few-shot learning settings, we assume that we have access to training data containing labeled samples from an initial set of classes, commonly referred to as the
base
classes. A fully supervised model is first trained on this base data. Then, supervision for a new set of classes that are completely different from those seen in the base training is restricted to just one or a few labeled samples per class. Model generalization is assessed over few-shot
tasks
: Each task involves a
query
set, which contains unlabeled samples for evaluation, and is supervised by a
support
set, which contains a few labeled samples per new class.

The recent and abundant few-shot literature is widely dominated by convoluted meta-learning and episodic-training strategies, which create a set of few-shot tasks (or episodes) during base training, each containing support and query samples, so as to mimic the generalization challenges encountered at testing times. I-A

Contributions

We view transductive few-shot inference as a constrained clustering, and investigate a general multi-term formulation of the problem, which integrates: a prototype-based
objective (e.g, K-means or K-modes); a pairwise Laplacian regularizer; and supervision constraints from the support set. We propose a concave-convex relaxation of the objective, for which we derive a computationally efficient block-coordinate bound optimizer, with convergence guarantee. At each iteration, our optimizer computes independent (parallel) updates for each point-to-cluster assignment. Therefore, it could be trivially distributed for large-scale clustering and few-shot tasks. Furthermore, we provides a thorough convergence analysis based on point-to-set maps. We report comprehensive clustering and few-shot experiments over various data sets, showing that our method yields competitive performances, both in term of accuracy and optimization quality (i.e., the objectives obtained at convergence), while scaling up to large problems. Without resorting to complex meta-learning and episodic-training schemes, and using a standard training on the base classes, our approach outperforms state-of-the-art few-shot methods by significant margins across different models, settings, and data sets. Furthermore, and surprisingly, we found that even standard clustering procedures (e.g., K-means), which correspond to particular, non-regularized cases of our general model, already achieve competitive performances in comparison to the the state-of-the-art in few-shot learning (even though these are not the best-performing variants of our general model). These surprising results point to the
limitations of current few-shot benchmarks, and question the viability of a large body of convoluted few-shot learning techniques in the recent literature.

Related works

Our work integrates several powerful ideas in clustering: pairwise graph-Laplacian regularization, cluster-representative objectives and density-mode finding. Cluster-representative objectives, such as the standard K-means, are very popular because they provide prototype representations of the clusters, explicit cluster-assignment variables and straightforward out-of-sample extensions. Furthermore, typically, they are amenable to optimizers that scale up to large and high-dimensional data sets. II
Proposed Formulation

We advocate Laplacian K-prototypes for clustering and transductive few-shot inference, and propose a general bound- and relaxation-based solution of the problem, which can be used to regularize several prototype-based objective (e.g, K-means, K-median or K-modes). Importantly, our concave-convex relaxation yields a parallel-structure algorithm, with convergence guarantee, and can handle large-scale data sets. First, we discuss a general formulation for clustering. Then, we describe an adaptation to transductive few-shot learning, which has recently attracted significant interest. In this case, our model takes the form of a constrained clustering for a given few-shot task.

III
Concave-convex relaxation

It is easy to verify that, for binary simplex variables, the Laplacian term
{\cal L}({\mathbf{S}})
can be written as follows:

\displaystyle{\cal L}({\mathbf{S}})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}({\mathbf{S}}^{t}{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{S}})

(4)

\displaystyle=2\left(\sum_{p}{\mathbf{s}}_{p}^{t}{\mathbf{s}}_{p}d_{p}-\sum_{p,q}w_{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p},{\mathbf{x}}_{q}){\mathbf{s}}_{p}^{t}{\mathbf{s}}_{q}\right)

\displaystyle=2\left(\sum_{p}d_{p}-\sum_{p,q}w_{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p},{\mathbf{x}}_{q}){\mathbf{s}}_{p}^{t}{\mathbf{s}}_{q}\right)

where the last equality is valid only for binary (integer) variables, and

d_{p}=\sum_{q}w_{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p},{\mathbf{x}}_{q})

When we replace the integer constraints
{\mathbf{s}}_{p}\in\{0,1\}
by
{\mathbf{s}}_{p}\in[0,1]
, our relaxation becomes different from direct convex relaxations of the Laplacian
[
71
]
, which optimizes
\mathrm{tr}({\mathbf{S}}^{t}{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{S}})
subject to probabilistic simplex constraints. For relaxed variables,
\mathrm{tr}({\mathbf{S}}^{t}{\mathbf{L}}{\mathbf{S}})
is a convex function because the Laplacian is always positive semi-definite (PSD).
In contrast, our relaxation of the Laplacian term is concave for PSD affinity matrix
{\mathbf{W}}_{\mathcal{L}}=[w_{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p},{\mathbf{x}}_{q})]
. As we will see later, concavity yields a scalable (parallel) algorithm for large
N
, which computes independent updates for assignment variables
{\mathbf{s}}_{p}
. Our updates can be trivially distributed, and do not require storing a full
N\times N
affinity matrix. These are important computational and memory advantages over direct convex relaxations of the Laplacian
[
71
]
, which require solving for
N\times K
variables all together as well as expensive simplex projections, and over common spectral relaxations
[
69
]
, which require storing a full affinity matrix and computing its eigenvalue decomposition.

We propose the following concave-convex relaxation of the objective in (
II
) by relaxing the integer constraints and adding a convex negative-entropy barrier for constraints
{\mathbf{s}}_{p}\geq 0
:

{{\cal R}}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{M}})={\cal E}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{M}})+\mathrm{tr}({\mathbf{S}}\log{\mathbf{S}}^{t})

(5)

{\cal R}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{M}})
is minimized over each
{\mathbf{s}}_{p}\in\nabla_{K}

\nabla_{K}=\{{\mathbf{s}}\in[0,1]^{K}\;|\;{\mathbf{1}}^{t}{\mathbf{s}}=1\}

It is easy to check that, at the vertices of the simplex, our relaxation in (
5

The last term we introduced in Eq. (
5

{\mathbf{s}}_{p}
to non-negative values, which avoids extra dual variables for constraints
{\mathbf{s}}_{p}\geq 0

\mathbf{1}^{t}{\mathbf{s}}_{p}=1
. In fact, entropy-like barriers are commonly used in Bregman-proximal optimization
[
80
]
, and have well-known computational and memory advantages when dealing with the challenging simplex constraints
[
80

. Surprisingly, to our knowledge, they are not common in the context of clustering. In machine learning, such entropic barriers appear frequently in the context of conditional random fields (CRFs)
[
30

31
]
, but are not motivated from an optimization perspective; they result from standard probabilistic and mean-field approximations of CRFs
[

]
.

V
Convergence study

Putting together the
{\mathbf{S}}
- and
{\mathbf{M}}
-update steps, one could notice that sequence
\{\mathcal{R}({\mathbf{S}}^{i},{\mathbf{M}}^{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is non-increasing:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}({\mathbf{S}}^{i+1},{\mathbf{M}}^{i+1})\underbrace{\leq}_{{\mathbf{M}}\text{-update}}\mathcal{R}({\mathbf{S}}^{i+1},{\mathbf{M}}^{i})\underbrace{\leq}_{{\mathbf{S}}\text{-update}}\mathcal{R}({\mathbf{S}}^{i},{\mathbf{M}}^{i})

(21)

Furthermore, this sequence is bounded from below:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{M}})=\underbrace{\mathcal{F}({\mathbf{S}},{\mathbf{M}})}_{\resizebox{6783045}{}{$\begin{cases}\geq 0&\text{SLK-Means}\\
\geq-N&\text{SLK-MS}\end{cases}$}}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\underbrace{\mathcal{L}({\mathbf{S}})}_{\geq 0}+\underbrace{\mathrm{tr}({\mathbf{S}}\log{\mathbf{S}}^{t})}_{-N\log K}

(22)

This makes of
\{\mathcal{R}({\mathbf{S}}^{i},{\mathbf{M}}^{i})\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
a converging sequence, regardless of initialization
\{{\mathbf{S}}^{0},{\mathbf{M}}^{0}\}
.
However, without additional analysis, this does neither imply that sequence
\{{\mathbf{S}}^{i},{\mathbf{M}}^{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
itself converges, nor does it characterize its limit points.

In what follows, we analyze our proposed SLK algorithm through the lens of Zangwill’s global convergence theory, which provides a simple but general framework to study the convergence of iterative algorithms. Note that this theory was already used to prove the convergence of the concave-convex
[
58
]
and EM/GEM procedures
[
76
]
, both of which could be viewed as special cases of bound optimization. In particular, we show that all limits points of any
sequence
\{{\mathbf{S}}^{i},{\mathbf{M}}^{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
produced by our algorithm are stationary points.

V-A

Background

In this section, we introduce the minimal set of required elements to understand Zangwill’s theory, upon which our own convergence result is based.
We first introduce the central concept of a point-to-set map
\psi
, which maps a point
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta
to a set of points
\psi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\subset\Theta
.
Map
\psi
could be understood as the iteration of an optimization algorithm that, from a point
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{i}
in some space
\Theta

of the optimization variables, outputs a new point
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{i+1}\in\psi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{i})\subset\Theta
from a set of local candidate solutions.
We further recall the important notion of closedness, which generalizes the concept of continuity in standard point-to-point maps to point-to-set maps:

Definition 2
.

(Closedness) Let us consider two converging sequences:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}

\displaystyle\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{n}\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{*}

Let us further assume that:

\displaystyle\forall n\in\mathbb{N},~\widetilde{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{n}\in\psi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{n})

(23)

Then, the point-to-set map
\psi
is said to be closed at point
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
if relation (
23
) extends to the limit when
n\rightarrow\infty
, i.e:

\displaystyle\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}^{*}\in\psi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*})

The point-to-set map
\psi
is said to be closed on the set
\Theta
if it is closed at every point of
\Theta
.

We are now ready to enunciate Zangwill’s theorem:

Theorem 1
.

(p.29 in
[
83
]
)
Consider a compact set
\Theta
, and let

\psi:\Theta\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
a point-to-set map, i.e., an algorithm generating a sequence
\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}

from an initial point
\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{0}\}
, via iteration
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{i+1}\in\psi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{i})
.
Let
\Gamma\subset\Theta
denotes a given solution set, e.g., the set of fixed points of the algorithm:
\Gamma=\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta\,|\,\psi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})={\boldsymbol{\theta}}\}
.
Assume that:

(1)
\psi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is nonempty and
\psi
is closed at
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
,
\forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta

(2) There is a continuous function
\mathcal{L}:{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that:
\forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta
,
\hskip 10.00002pt\forall~{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\in\psi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
:

•

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\notin\Gamma\implies\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})<\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})

•

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\in\Gamma\implies\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})\leq\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})

Then any sequence
\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
defined by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{i+1}\in\psi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{i})
has all of its limit points in
\Gamma
.

As noted in
[
58
]
, the general idea to prove the convergence of an iterative algorithm is to properly set
\Gamma
and
\mathcal{L}
. The natural choice for
\Gamma
is to set it as the set of fixed points of the algorithm
\Gamma=\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta\,|\,\psi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})={\boldsymbol{\theta}}\}
.
\mathcal{L}
is the objective function that the algorithm minimizes. With that in mind, assumption (2) in
Theorem 1
simply ensures that, even though the algorithm has not yet reached stationary points
\Gamma
,
objective function
\mathcal{L}
is strictly decreasing.
Theorem 1
is, unfortunately, not straightforwardly applicable to our SLK algorithm, as it considers
a single algorithm update
\psi
, while SLK alternates between both
{\mathbf{S}}
- and
{\mathbf{M}}
-updates, which handle two completely different types of optimization variables (assignments and prototypes). A corollary proposed in
[
16
]
enables us to verify the assumptions on each of type of updates independently while preserving the final result:

Corollary 1
.

(Corollary 2. in
[
16
]
)
Consider
\Theta
a compact set,
\Gamma\subset\Theta
,
J
a finite set of indices, a set of point-to-set maps
\{\psi_{j}:\Theta\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\Theta),~j\in J\}
, and
\mathcal{L}:{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
a continuous function. Using the sequence of indices
I:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow J
, assume that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, for any
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in\Theta\setminus\Gamma
:

(1)
\psi_{I(n)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is nonempty and
\psi_{I(n)}
is closed at
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}

(2)
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime}\in\psi_{I(n)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\implies\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{\prime})<\mathcal{L}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})

Then any sequence
\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
defined by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{n+1}\in\psi_{I(n)}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{n})
has all of its limit points in
\Gamma
.

[

tiered

[

[

pixels.

CUB-200-2011

is a fine-grained image classification dataset with 200 categories. Following the setting in
[
11

for few-shot

84\times 84
pixels.

VI-B

Evaluation Protocol

We follow standard evaluation practices in few-shot learning, using 600 tasks (5-way classification) randomly sampled from the test classes.
The query set of each task contains 15 images per class, and the average accuracy over 600 few-shot tasks is reported, for both the 1-shot and 5-shot supervision scenarios.

VI-C

Architectures

We evaluate all clustering methods on two different network models as feature extractor function
f_{\theta}
, following the same settings used recently in
[
90
]
.
Particularly, we consider the following standard models for the few-shot learning experiments:

ResNet-18
is based on the standard deep residual network, ResNet
[
24
]
, whose first two down-sampling layers are removed, by setting the stride to 1 in the first convolutional layer and removing the first max-pooling layer. Furthermore, the kernel size of the first convolutional layer is set to
3\times 3
, instead of
7\times 7
in the original model. The used ResNet-18 model has 8 basic residual blocks, and the dimension of the extracted features is 512.

WRN

[
82
]
widens the residual blocks by adding more convolutional layers and feature planes. In our particular case, we used an architecture with 28 convolutional layers, along with a widening factor of 10. This network yields an extracted feature vector of dimension 640.

VI-D

Implementation Details

Base training:
We utilize the publicly available trained models from
[
90
]
6
6

6

https://github.com/imtiazziko/LaplacianShot
. The network models are
trained using the standard cross-entropy loss on the training set of
A
base classes, without resorting to meta-learning and episodic-training strategies. Training starts
from scratch and runs during 90 epochs. The models are optimized using SGD. Similarly to
[
90
,
72
]
, the initial learning rate is set to 0.1 and scaled by 0.1 at the
45th and 66th epoch for
mini
ImageNet and CUB, and after every 30 epochs for
tiered
ImageNet. The mini-batch size is set to 256 for ResNet-18 and 128 for
WRN.

Inference procedure:
Before running any clustering approach in the few-shot learning setting, we extract the features from both the support and query samples with the initial
f_{\theta}
trained on the base classes. Similarly to
[
72
,
90
]
, we perform CL2 normalization on the features. This amounts to computing the mean of
the base-class features, followed by centering the extracted support and query features via subtracting the mean. Then, we perform an L2 normalization on the centered features.
Similarly to
[
38
,
90
]
, we also perform a bias correction by computing the cross-class bias from the difference between the mean features of the support set
\mathcal{X}_{\text{s}}
and the query set
\mathcal{X}_{\text{q}}
. Then, we add this bias to each query features vector before performing the clustering for a given few-shot task.
For each task, the initial prototypes
\{{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{0}\}_{k=1}^{K}
for the clustering algorithms (see Alg.
1
) are either the support examples
{\mathbf{x}}_{p}\in\mathcal{X}_{\text{s}}^{k}
of each class in the 1-shot setting, or the mean of the support examples within each class in the 5-shot setting. Once the clustering of a task is performed, we label each query sample according to the class label of the support examples belonging to the same cluster.

SLK hyperparameters:
We define
\mathcal{N}_{p}^{\rho}
as the set of the
\rho
nearest neighbors of data point
{\mathbf{x}}_{p}
. Mode estimation is based on the Gaussian kernel as per (
14
), with
\sigma^{2}
estimated as:

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=\frac{1}{N\rho}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{p}\in\mathbf{X}}\sum_{\mathbf{x}_{q}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}^{\rho}}\|\mathbf{x}_{p}-\mathbf{x}_{q}\|^{2}

(29)

The Laplacian affinities
w_{\mathcal{L}}
are built using the
\rho
-nearest neighbors:

\displaystyle w_{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p},{\mathbf{x}}_{q})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\mathbf{x}_{q}\in\mathcal{N}_{p}^{\rho}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(30)

This yields a sparse affinity matrix, which is efficient in terms of memory and computational complexity.
In our experiments,
\rho
is simply chosen from three typical values (3, 5 or 10) tuned over training-data by sampling 500 few-shot tasks from the training set. Such procedure yielded a fixed
\rho=3
during inference. Since no test data is involved in choosing the value of
\rho
, this procedure is not biased towards the testing classes. Then, we tune parameter

\lambda
on the validation classes by sampling
500
few-shot tasks and evaluating a range of
\lambda
values from
[0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7,0.8,1.0]
. The best
\lambda

VIII
Conclusion

We presented a general formulation for clustering and transductive few-shot learning, integrating Laplacian regularization, prototype-based terms and
supervision constraints. Based on a concave-convex relaxation, our bound optimizer performs parallel point-wise updates, while guaranteeing convergence.
We reported comprehensive clustering and few-shot experiments over various data sets, showing that our methods yield competitive performances, in regard
to both accuracy and optimization quality. Using standard training on the base classes, and without resorting to complex meta-learning and episodic-training
strategies, our clustering methods yield state-of-the-art results in the transductive few-shot setting, outperforming significantly a large number of convoluted
methods in the recent literature, across various models, settings and data sets. In the 1-shot settings, our SLK-MS clustering outperforms the best-performing
state-of-the-art few-shot method by up to
3\%
. It also outperforms several convoluted meta-learning methods by substantial margins, up to
10\%
or more.
Surprisingly, we found that even the basic K-means already achieves competitive performances in comparison to the state-of-the-art in few-shot learning,
outperforming substantially several very recent methods. These surprising performances point to the limitations of the current few-shot benchmarks, question the
viability of a large body of complex few-shot learning works in the literature, and suggest simple clustering mechanisms as baselines.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition 1

Proposition 1
states that, given current solution
{\mathbf{S}}^{i,n}=[s_{p,k}^{i,n}]
at outer iteration
i
, we have the following auxiliary function (up to an additive constant) for concave-convex relaxation (
5
) and psd affinity matrix
{\mathbf{W}}_{\mathcal{L}}
:

{\cal A}^{i,n}({\mathbf{S}})=\sum_{p=1}^{N}{\mathbf{s}}_{p}^{t}(\log({\mathbf{s}}_{p})-{\mathbf{a}}_{p}^{i}-\lambda{{\mathbf{b}}}_{p}^{i,n})

(31)

where
{{\mathbf{a}}}_{p}^{i}
and
{{\mathbf{b}}}_{p}^{i}
are the following
K
dimensional vectors:

\displaystyle{{\mathbf{a}}}_{p}^{i}

\displaystyle=[a_{p,1}^{i},\dots,a_{p,K}^{i}]^{t},\,\mbox{with}\,\,a_{p,k}^{i}=w_{\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p},{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i})

(32a)

\displaystyle{{\mathbf{b}}}_{p}^{i}

\displaystyle=[b_{p,1}^{i},\dots,b_{p,K}^{i}]^{t},\,\mbox{with}\,\,b_{p,k}^{i}=\sum_{q}w_{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p},{\mathbf{x}}_{q})s_{q,k}^{i}

(32b)

Proof.

We want to study the sign of the following difference. Discarding the constant
2\sum_{p}d_{p}
in (
4
) for the Laplacian term
{\cal L}({\mathbf{S}})
, one can write:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{i}({\mathbf{S}})-{\cal A}^{i,n}({\mathbf{S}})

\displaystyle=\lambda~\sum_{p}{\mathbf{s}}_{p}^{t}({\mathbf{b}}_{p}^{i,n}-\sum_{q}w_{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p},{\mathbf{x}}_{q}){\mathbf{s}}_{q})

(33)

\displaystyle=\lambda~\sum_{p,q}{\mathbf{s}}_{p}^{t}w_{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p},{\mathbf{x}}_{q})({\mathbf{s}}_{q}^{i}-{\mathbf{s}}_{q})

(34)

First, instead of considering the
N\times K
matrix
{\mathbf{S}}
, let us represent our assignment variables by a flattened vector
{\mathbf{s}}\in[0,1]^{KN}
, that takes the form
{\mathbf{s}}=[{\mathbf{s}}_{1},{\mathbf{s}}_{2},\dots,{\mathbf{s}}_{N}]
.
Recall that each
{\mathbf{s}}_{p}
is a vector of dimension
K
containing the probability variables of all labels for point
p
:
{\mathbf{s}}_{p}=[s_{p,1},\dots,s_{p,K}]^{t}
. Let
\Psi=-{\mathbf{W}}_{\mathcal{L}}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{N}
, where
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product and
\mathbf{I}_{N}
the
N\times N
identity matrix.
One can rewrite (
5
) in the following convenient form:

-\sum_{p,q}w_{\mathcal{L}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p},{\mathbf{x}}_{q}){\mathbf{s}}_{p}^{t}{\mathbf{s}}_{q}={\mathbf{s}}^{t}\Psi{\mathbf{s}}

(35)

Which allows us to write the difference (
33
) as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{i}({\mathbf{S}})-{\cal A}^{i,n}({\mathbf{S}})=\lambda({\mathbf{s}}^{t}\Psi{\mathbf{s}}-{\mathbf{s}}^{t}\Psi{\mathbf{s}}^{i,n})

(36)

Now, notice that the Kronecker product
\Psi
is negative semi-definite when
{\mathbf{W}}_{\mathcal{L}}
is positive semi-definite. Hence, the function
{\mathbf{s}}\rightarrow{\mathbf{s}}^{t}\Psi{\mathbf{s}}
is concave and, therefore, is upper bounded by its first-order approximation at current solution
{\mathbf{S}}^{i,n}
(outer iteration
i
, inner iteration
n
). In fact, concavity arguments are standard in deriving auxiliary functions for bound-optimization algorithms
[
33
]
. With this condition, we have the following auxiliary function for the Laplacian-term relaxation:

{\mathbf{s}}^{t}\Psi{\mathbf{s}}\leq({\mathbf{s}}^{i,n})^{t}\Psi{\mathbf{s}}^{i,n}+(\Psi{\mathbf{s}}^{i,n})^{t}({\mathbf{s}}-{\mathbf{s}}^{i,n})

(37)

Equation (
37
) directly implies that:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{s}}^{t}\Psi{\mathbf{s}}-{\mathbf{s}}^{t}\Psi{\mathbf{s}}^{i,n}\leq 0

(38)

Which implies that:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{i}({\mathbf{S}})\leq{\cal A}^{i,n}({\mathbf{S}})

(39)

Finally note that for
{\mathbf{s}}={\mathbf{s}}^{i,n}
, equation (
37
) becomes an equality, and so do following equations (
38
) and (
39
), which finalizes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of Proposition
2

Let us consider outer iteration
i
, with the associated current assignements
{\mathbf{S}}_{i}
. For each class
k
, let us prove that
\{{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Cauchy sequence. Recall the recursive relation:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{p}s_{p,k}w_{\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p},{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}){\mathbf{x}}_{p}}{\sum_{p}s_{p,k}w_{\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p},{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n})}

(40)

with
w_{\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p},{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n})=\exp(-\lVert\frac{{\mathbf{x}}_{p}-{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}}{\sigma}\rVert^{2})
, for some
\sigma>0
. To simplify notations in what follows, we define:

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle=\exp(-x)

(42)

\displaystyle u^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}s_{p,k}w_{\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p},{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n})

(43)

\displaystyle v^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}s_{p,k}w_{\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p},{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}){\mathbf{x}}_{p}

(44)

Step 1:
First, let us prove that
\{u^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Cauchy sequence. Recall that in a metric space, a convergent sequence is necessarily a Cauchy sequence. Therefore, we only need to show that
u^{n}
is convergent (i.e bounded and strictly monotonic).

Notice that for
x>0
,
0\leq h(x)\leq 1
. Therefore:

\displaystyle u^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p}s_{p,k}h(\lVert\frac{{\mathbf{x}}_{p}-{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}}{\sigma}\rVert^{2})

(45)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p}s_{p,k}\leq N

(46)

Therefore,
u^{n}
is bounded between 0 and N. Now, let us study the consecutive differences
\Delta^{n}=u^{n+1}-u^{n}
:

\displaystyle\Delta^{n}=\sum_{p}s_{p,k}\left[h(\frac{\lVert{\mathbf{x}}_{p}-{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n+1}\rVert^{2}}{\sigma^{2}})-h(\frac{\lVert{\mathbf{x}}_{p}-{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}\rVert^{2}}{\sigma^{2}})\right]

(47)

Because
h
is convex, one can say that
\forall a,b\in\mathbb{R}
:

\displaystyle h(a)-h(b)\geq h^{\prime}(b)(a-b)

(48)

And because
h^{\prime}(b)=-h(b)
in our case, one ends up with:

\displaystyle h(a)-h(b)\geq h(b)(b-a)

(49)

Applied with
a=\frac{\lVert{\mathbf{x}}_{p}-{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n+1}\rVert^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}
and
b=\frac{\lVert{\mathbf{x}}_{p}-{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}\rVert^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}
, one can obtain:

\begin{split}\Delta^{n}\geq&\sum_{p}s_{p,k}w_{\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p},{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n})\left[\frac{\lVert{\mathbf{x}}_{p}-{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}\rVert^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\lVert{\mathbf{x}}_{p}-{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n+1}\rVert^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right]\\
=&\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{p}s_{p,k}w_{\mathcal{F}}({\mathbf{x}}_{p},{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n})\left[\lVert{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}\rVert^{2}-\lVert{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n+1}\rVert^{2}\right.\\
&\left.-2<{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n},{\mathbf{x}}_{p}>+2<{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n+1},{\mathbf{x}}_{p}>\right]\\
\end{split}

Now is time to recall recursive relation
{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n+1}=\displaystyle\frac{v^{n}}{u^{n}}
. By simply expanding, one can end up with:

\displaystyle\Delta^{n}\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\left[\lVert{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}\rVert^{2}u^{n}-\frac{\lVert v^{n}\rVert^{2}}{u^{n}}-2<{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n},v^{n}>+2\frac{\lVert v^{n}\rVert^{2}}{u^{n}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}u^{n}\left[\lVert{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}\rVert^{2}-2<{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n},{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n+1}>+\lVert{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n+1}\rVert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}u^{n}\lVert{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}-{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n+1}\rVert^{2}

(50)

Therefore,
\Delta^{n}>0
, which shows that
\{u^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is strictly increasing. This concludes the proof that
u^{n}
is a convergent sequence, and therefore a Cauchy one.

Step 2:
Now, on top of concluding the proof that
\{u^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a Cauchy sequence, Eq. (
50
) also offers an interesting relation between
\{\Delta^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and the sequence of interest
\{{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, which we can use.
Indeed, for any
n_{0},m\in\mathbb{N}
, we can sum Eq. (
50
):

\displaystyle\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{n_{0}+m}\Delta^{n}\geq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{n_{0}+m}u^{n}\lVert{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}-{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n+1}\rVert^{2}

(51)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{u_{0}}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{n_{0}+m}\lVert{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}-{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n+1}\rVert^{2}

(52)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\frac{u_{0}}{\sigma^{2}}\lVert{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n_{0}+m}-{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n_{0}}\rVert^{2}

(53)

Where Eq. (
52
) follows because
\{u^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is strictly increasing, and Eq. (
53
) follows from the triangle inequality. Now, the left-hand side of Eq. () can be reduced to
\sum_{n=n_{0}}^{n_{0}+m}\Delta^{n}=u^{n_{0}+M+1}-u^{n_{0}}
. But because we proved in Step 1 that
\{u^{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
was a Cauchy sequence, this difference is bounded by a constant. This concludes the proof that
\{{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is itself a Cauchy sequence in the Euclidean space.

Step 3:
We just proved that
\{{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
was a Cauchy sequence. Therefore
\{{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
can only converge to a single value
{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,*}
. We now use the continuity of function
g_{k}^{i}
to conclude that
{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,*}
has to be a solution of the initial equation (
15
):

\displaystyle{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,*}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n+1}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}g_{i}^{k}({\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n})

(54)

\displaystyle=g_{i}^{k}(\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,n})=g_{i}^{k}({\mathbf{m}}_{k}^{i,*})

(55)
