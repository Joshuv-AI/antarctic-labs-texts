Robust Bayesian approach for reliability prognosis of nondestructive one-shot devices under cumulative risk model
Shanya Baghel, Shuvashree Mondal

Abstract
The present study aims to determine the lifetime prognosis of highly durable nondestructive one-shot devices (NOSD) units under a step-stress accelerated life testing (SSALT) experiment applying a cumulative risk model (CRM). In an SSALT experiment, CRM retains the continuity of hazard function by allowing the lag period before the effects of stress change emerge. When prior information about the model parameters is available, Bayesian inference is crucial. In a Bayesian analysis of such lifetime data, conventional likelihood-based Bayesian estimation frequently fails in the presence of outliers in the dataset. This work incorporates a robust Bayesian approach utilizing a robustified posterior based on the density power divergence measure. The order restriction on shape parameters has been incorporated as a prior assumption to reflect the decreasing expected lifetime with increasing stress levels. In testing of hypothesis, a Bayes factor is implemented based on the robustified posterior. In Bayesian estimation, we exploit Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, which has certain advantages over the conventional Metropolis-Hastings algorithms. Further, the influence functions are examined to evaluate the robust behaviour of the estimators and the Bayes factor. Finally, the analytical development is validated through a simulation study and a real data analysis.

Introduction

In recent times, the reliability prognosis of nondestructive one-shot devices (NOSD) is drawing an increasing amount of attention because of their broad applicability in industrial and engineering domains. Metal fatigue, spare wheels and light bulbs are some examples of NOSD. Unlike one-shot devices, NOSD may survive multiple tests, offering additional data for reliability estimation. The observation for such devices is mostly restricted to recording if device failure occurs before or after a specified inspection time, which leads to the study of dichotomous data only. For highly durable products, accelerated life testing (ALT) experiments are frequently employed to get more failures within a short span of time

Furthermore, the influence functions are derived and examined thoroughly to assess the robust behaviour of the point estimators in the Bayesian framework. In the testing of hypotheses, the influence function reflects how outlying observation can influence the Bayes factor under the null hypothesis, potentially affecting decision-making.

2
Cumulative risk step-stress model

This section discusses the experimental setup for analyzing the nondestructive one-shot device (NOSD) testing data under the cumulative risk step-stress model.

2.1
The model

Consider
n
NOSD units are put to step-stress accelerated life testing (SSALT) experiment with
k
stress levels denoted by
x_{i}\,;\,i=1,2,\dots,k.
Starting from initial stress
x_{1}
, the stress level of NOSD units is increased from
x_{i}
to
x_{i+1}
at pre-specified time points
\tau_{i}\;;\;i=1,2,\dots,k
and
\tau_{0}=0.

At each stress level, the lifetime distribution of the NOSD is assumed to follow the Lehman family of distributions with different shape and scale parameters whose cumulative distribution function (cdf) and probability density function (pdf) are defined as

\displaystyle F_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=1-\exp\big\{-\lambda_{i}Q(t;\gamma_{i})\big\}.

(1)

\displaystyle f_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}Q^{{}^{\prime}}(t;\gamma_{i})\exp\big\{-\lambda_{i}Q(t;\gamma_{i})\big\}.

where
\lambda_{i}>0
and
\gamma_{i}>0
and
Q(t;\gamma_{i})
is strictly increasing function of
t
. Various lifetime distributions such as exponential, Weibull and Gompertz are members of this family for
Q(t;\gamma_{i})=t,t^{\gamma_{i}},(e^{\gamma_{i}t}-1)
respectively. Here, in equation (
1
), parameter
\lambda_{i}
is related to stress factor in log-linear form as

\lambda_{i}=\exp(c_{0}+c_{1}x_{i})\;;\;i=1,2,\dots,k.

(2)

Thus the set of model parameters to be estimated is denoted by

\bm{\theta}=\{(c_{j},\gamma_{i})^{T}\,;\,j=0,1\,;\,i=1,2,\dots,k\}.

2.2
Cumulative risk model

Introduced by Van Dorp and Mazzuchi
René Van Dorp and
Mazzuchi (2004)
, formalized by Kannan et al.
Kannan et al. (2010)
and generalized by Kannan and Kundu
Kannan and Kundu (2020)
, Cumulative risk model (CRM) addresses discontinuities in hazard rates occurring due to instantaneous stress changes. CRM ensures a smooth transition by inducting lag period
\delta
before the effect of stress change emerges. The hazard rate function is usually assumed to be linearly modelled in this lag period.

2.2.1
Lag period
\delta
known

In the development of CRM, we assume the lag period
\delta
is known. Figure

For NOSD testing data, the piece-wise hazard rate function under the Lehman family of distribution takes the following form.

h(t)=\begin{cases}\lambda_{1}Q^{{}^{\prime}}(t;\gamma_{1})&;\;0<t\leq\tau_{1}.\\
a_{i-1}+b_{i-1}t&;\;\tau_{i-1}<t\leq\tau_{i-1}+\delta\;,\;i=2,3,\dots,k-1.\\
\lambda_{i}Q^{{}^{\prime}}(t;\gamma_{i})&;\;\tau_{i-1}+\delta<t\leq\tau_{i}\;;\;i=2,3,\dots,k-1.\\
\lambda_{k}Q^{{}^{\prime}}(t;\gamma_{k})&;\;\tau_{k-1}<t<\infty.\end{cases}

(3)

To ensure continuity of
h(t)
in equation (
3
),
a_{i-1}
and
b_{i-1}
must satisfy

\displaystyle a_{i-1}+b_{i-1}\tau_{i-1}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i-1}Q^{{}^{\prime}}(\tau_{i-1};\gamma_{i-1}).

(4)

\displaystyle a_{i-1}+b_{i-1}(\tau_{i-1}+\delta)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}Q^{{}^{\prime}}(\tau_{i-1}+\delta;\gamma_{i-1}).

By solving equation (
4
), we obtain

\displaystyle a_{i-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta}\bigg\{(\delta+\tau_{i-1})\lambda_{i-1}Q^{{}^{\prime}}(\tau_{i-1};\gamma_{i-1})-\tau_{i-1}\lambda_{i}Q^{{}^{\prime}}(\tau_{i-1}+\delta;\gamma_{i})\bigg\}.

\displaystyle b_{i-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta}\bigg\{\lambda_{i}Q^{{}^{\prime}}(\tau_{i-1}+\delta;\gamma_{i})-\lambda_{i-1}Q^{{}^{\prime}}(\tau_{i-1};\gamma_{i-1})\bigg\}.

Therefore, Survival function
S(t)=e^{-\int_{0}^{t}h(x)dx}
is obtained as

S(t)=\begin{cases}\exp\Big\{-\lambda_{1}Q(t;\gamma_{1})\Big\}\quad;\;0<t\leq\tau_{1}.\\
\exp\Big\{-D^{(\delta)}(t;\gamma_{i-1,i})\Big\}\exp\bigg[-\Big\{\lambda_{i-1}Q(\tau_{i-1};\gamma_{i-1})+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{i-2}E^{(\delta)}(\tau_{l};\gamma_{l+1,l})\Big\}\bigg]\\
\hskip 110.96556pt;\;\tau_{i-1}<t\leq\tau_{i-1}+\delta\;;\;i=2,3,\dots,k-1.\\
\exp\Big\{-\lambda_{i}Q(t;\gamma_{i})\Big\}\exp\bigg\{-\sum\limits_{l=1}^{i-1}E^{(\delta)}(\tau_{l};\gamma_{l+1,l})\bigg\}\\
\hskip 110.96556pt;\;\tau_{i-1}+\delta<t\leq\tau_{i}\;;\;i=2,3,\dots,k-1.\\
\exp\Big\{-\lambda_{k}Q(t;\gamma_{k})\Big\}\exp\bigg[-\Big\{\lambda_{k-1}Q(\tau_{k-1};\gamma_{k-1})+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k-2}E^{(\delta)}(\tau_{i};\gamma_{i+1,i})\Big\}\bigg]\\
\hskip 110.96556pt;\;\tau_{k-1}<t<\infty.\end{cases},

(5)

where,

\displaystyle D^{(\delta)}(t;\gamma_{i-1,i})

\displaystyle=\frac{(t-\tau_{i-1})^{2}}{2\delta}\Bigg[\Big\{2\delta(t-\tau_{i-1})^{-1}-1\Big\}\lambda_{i-1}Q^{{}^{\prime}}(\tau_{i-1};\gamma_{i-1})+\lambda_{i}Q^{{}^{\prime}}(t;\gamma_{i})\Bigg].

\displaystyle E^{(\delta)}(\tau_{l};\gamma_{l+1,l})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{l}Q(\tau_{l};\gamma_{l})-\lambda_{l+1}Q(\tau_{l}+\delta;\gamma_{l+1})

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{\delta}{2}\Big\{\lambda_{l}Q^{{}^{\prime}}(\tau_{l};\gamma_{l})+\lambda_{l+1}Q^{{}^{\prime}}(\tau_{l}+\delta;\gamma_{l+1})\Big\}.

Throughout the theoretical development and simulation study presented in this article,
\delta
is assumed to be known. However, in practical applications,
\delta
is seldom available. To address this issue, we briefly outline an approach based on the recommendations of Kannan and Kundu
Kannan and Kundu (2020)
. Their method offers a straightforward yet effective framework for estimating
\delta,
ensuring the applicability of the theoretical results in real-world settings.

2.2.2
Lag period
\delta
unknown

In practical scenarios,
\delta
may not always be known. We estimate

, by maximizing profile likelihood function with respect to
\delta.
As this maximization cannot be performed analytically, Kannan and Kundu
Kannan and Kundu (2020)
suggested to employ grid search method. For each candidate

in the grid, MLE of the unknown parameters are computed as outlined in Subsection
2.3
and corresponding likelihood value is evaluated. The value of

yielding the maximum likelihood value is selected as the estimate for

\delta
is unknown in the lightbulb experiment conducted by Zhu

.

CRM under SSALT with interval monitoring

As the lag period consists of a very small amount of time, no failure is inspected during

n_{im}\,;\,i=1,2,\dots,k\,;\,m=1,2,\dots,q_{i}

. Hence,

is the number of survived units after time point

If
T
is lifetime of a NOSD, then failure and survival probabilities using equation (
5
) are given as

\displaystyle p_{i1}

\displaystyle=P(\tau_{i-1}<T\leq\tau_{i,1})=\int_{\tau_{i-1}}^{\tau_{i-1}+\delta}f(x)\,dx+\int_{\tau_{i-1}+\delta}^{\tau_{i,1}}f(x)\,dx.

\displaystyle=G^{(\delta)}(\tau_{i-1}+\delta;\gamma_{i-1,i})+\exp\left\{-\sum_{l=1}^{i-1}E^{(\delta)}(\tau_{l};\gamma_{l+1,l})\right\}G^{(1)}(\tau_{i-1,i};\gamma_{i}).

(6)

\displaystyle p_{im}

\displaystyle=P(\tau_{i,m-1}<T\leq\tau_{i,m})\;;\;m=2,3,\dots,q_{i}.

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{-\sum_{l=1}^{i-1}E^{(\delta)}(\tau_{l};\gamma_{l+1,l})\right\}G^{(m)}(\tau_{i};\gamma_{i}).

(7)

\displaystyle p_{s}

\displaystyle=P(T>\tau_{k})=\exp\left\{-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}E^{(\delta)}(\tau_{i};\gamma_{i+1,i})\right\}\exp\Big\{-\lambda_{k}Q(\tau_{k};\gamma_{k})\Big\},

(8)

where,

\displaystyle G^{(\delta)}(\tau_{i-1}+\delta;\gamma_{i-1,i})

\displaystyle=\exp\bigg[-\Big\{\lambda_{i-1}Q(\tau_{i-1};\gamma_{i-1})+\sum\limits_{l=1}^{i-2}E^{(\delta)}(\tau_{l};\gamma_{l+1,l})\Big\}\bigg]

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\bigg[1-\exp\Big\{-D^{(\delta)}(\tau_{i-1}+\delta;\gamma_{i-1,i})\Big\}\bigg].

\displaystyle G^{(m)}(\tau_{i};\gamma_{i})

\displaystyle=\exp\Big\{-\lambda_{i}Q(\tau_{i,m-1};\gamma_{i})\Big\}-\exp\Big\{-\lambda_{i}Q(\tau_{i,m};\gamma_{i})\Big\}.

\displaystyle G^{(1)}(\tau_{i-1,i};\gamma_{i})

\displaystyle=\exp\Big\{-\lambda_{i}Q(\tau_{i-1}+\delta;\gamma_{i})\Big\}-\exp\Big\{-\lambda_{i}Q(\tau_{i,1};\gamma_{i})\Big\}.

The log-likelihood function based on observed failure count data is given by

ln\,L(\bm{\theta})\propto\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}n_{im}\,ln\,p_{im}\right)+\bigg(n_{s}\,ln\,p_{s}\bigg).

Therefore, maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) can be obtained as

\hat{\bm{\theta}}=arg\mathop{max}_{\bm{\theta}}\,ln\,L(\bm{\theta})\;;\;\sum\limits_{i=1}^{k}\sum\limits_{m=1}^{q_{i}}n_{im}>0.

In the presence of outliers, MLE can give misinformation. Thus, a robust estimation method immune to small proportion of outliers is required. The density power divergence (DPD) proposed by Basu et al.
Basu et al. (1998)
is widely used for robust estimation
Baghel and Mondal (2024)
;
Balakrishnan and
Castilla (2024)
;
Baghel and Mondal (2024)
. Here, the DPD measure is computed as a divergence between empirical and theoretical failure (survival) probabilities for an NOSD testing unit. For CRM under SSALT with IMIIP, the empirical failure and survival probabilities are given as
\left(\frac{n_{im}}{n},\frac{n_{s}}{n}\right)
where
\,;\,i=1,2,\dots,k\,;\,m=1,2,\dots,q_{i}.
Then, the DPD measure can be obtained as

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})=\left\{p^{\alpha+1}_{s}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}p^{\alpha+1}_{im}\right\}

\displaystyle-\left(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\left\{\frac{n_{s}}{n}p^{\alpha}_{s}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}\frac{n_{im}}{n}p_{im}^{\alpha}\right\}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}\left(\frac{n_{s}}{n}\right)^{\alpha+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}\left(\frac{n_{im}}{n}\right)^{\alpha+1}\right\},

(9)

where
\alpha
is termed as the tuning parameter. As
\alpha\to 0
, DPD measure approaches likelihood equation. The minimum DPD estimator (MDE) can be obtained as

\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\alpha}=arg\mathop{min}_{\bm{\theta}}D_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}).

(10)

The asymptotic distribution of MDE is given in the appendix. Further, with availability of prior knowledge, the Bayesian approach comes into the picture. However, the conventional Bayesian approach relying on likelihood-based prior may yield unreliable estimates in the presence of outliers in data. Thus, robust Bayesian estimation becomes necessary.

3
Robust Bayes method of estimation

Ghosh and Basu
Ghosh and Basu (2016)
proposed to solve the non-robustness problem by replacing the likelihood function in the posterior with density power divergence (DPD)
Basu et al. (1998)
based loss function, where the derived posterior is called a pseudo posterior. We follow a similar approach for developing robust Bayesian inference for NOSD testing data.
The following subsection discusses the prior assumptions for the present study.

3.1
Prior assumptions

In Bayesian inference, the choice of prior governs the estimation. As considered by Fan et al.
Fan et al. (2009)
, we have taken prior information on
p_{im}
instead of model parameters
\bm{\theta}
for the first two prior selections. To avoid a zero-frequency situation, we follow the idea of Lee and Morris
Lee and Morris A. (1985)
and modify empirical probabilities as

(\tilde{p}_{s},\tilde{p}_{im})=\left(\frac{n_{s}+1}{n+k\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}+1},\frac{n_{im}+1}{n+k\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}+1}\right),

(11)

where,
i=1,2,\dots,k\;;\;m=1,2,\dots,q_{i}.

3.1.1
Normal prior based on data

Assume
e_{im}
is the error representing a difference between empirical and true failure probabilities. Therefore, it can be expressed that

\tilde{p}_{im}=p_{im}+e_{im}\;;\;i=1,2,\dots,k\;;\;m=1,2,\dots,q_{i},

(12)

where, the error
e_{im}
are assumed to be independent
N(0,\sigma^{2})
variables. The conditional likelihood function as prior distribution of
\bm{\theta}
given
\sigma^{2}
can be obtained by

L(\bm{\theta}|\sigma^{2})\propto\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{m=1}^{q_{i}}\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}(p_{im}-\tilde{p}_{im})^{2}\right\},

and
\pi(\sigma^{2})\propto\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}
is the non-informative prior of
\sigma^{2}.
The joint prior density of
\bm{\theta}
can be obtained as

\pi^{(Nor)}(\bm{\theta})\propto\int_{0}^{\infty}L(\bm{\theta}|\sigma^{2})\pi(\sigma^{2})\,d\bm{\theta}\propto\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}(p_{im}-\tilde{p}_{im})^{2}\right\}^{-\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}/2}.

(13)

3.1.2
Dirlichet prior based on data

Beta prior is a natural choice if a parameter can be interpreted as a probability. Extending this idea, a Dirichlet prior is considered for the failure and survival probabilities as

\pi^{(Dir)}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{p^{\beta_{s}-1}_{s}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{m=1}^{q_{i}}p^{\beta_{im}-1}_{im}}{Beta(\bm{\beta})},

(14)

where,
\beta_{s},\beta_{im}>0
for
i=1,2,\dots,k\;;\;m=1,2,\dots,q_{i}
and

Beta(\bm{\beta})=\frac{\Gamma\beta_{s}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{m=1}^{q_{i}}\Gamma\beta_{im}}{\Gamma(\beta_{s}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}\beta_{im})}.

The hyper-parameters
\bm{\beta}
are chosen such that

\displaystyle E(p_{im})

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{im}}{\beta_{s}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m^{{}^{\prime}}=1}^{q_{i}}\beta_{im^{{}^{\prime}}}}=\tilde{p}_{im}\;,\;E(p_{s})=\frac{\beta_{s}}{\beta_{s}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m^{{}^{\prime}}=1}^{q_{i}}\beta_{im^{{}^{\prime}}}}=\tilde{p}_{s}

(15)

\displaystyle Var(p_{s})

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{s}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{m^{{}^{\prime}}=1}^{q_{i}}\beta_{im^{{}^{\prime}}}}{\left(\beta_{s}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m^{{}^{\prime}}=1}^{q_{i}}\beta_{im^{{}^{\prime}}}\right)^{2}\left(\beta_{s}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m^{{}^{\prime}}=1}^{q_{i}}\beta_{im^{{}^{\prime}}}+1\right)}=\sigma_{(p)}^{2}.

(16)

where,
\sigma_{(p)}^{2}
is assumed to be a prefix quantity. The estimates of hyper-parameters can be obtained by equations (
15
) and (
16
) as

\displaystyle\hat{\beta}_{im}

\displaystyle=\tilde{p}_{im}\left\{\frac{\tilde{p}_{s}(1-\tilde{p})}{\sigma_{(p)}^{2}}-1\right\}\;;\;\hat{\beta}_{s}=\left\{\frac{\tilde{p}_{s}(1-\tilde{p})}{\sigma_{(p)}^{2}}-1\right\}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}\beta_{im}.

(17)

Therefore, the joint prior density is given as

\pi^{(Dir)}(\bm{\theta})\propto p^{\hat{\beta}_{s}-1}_{s}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{m=1}^{q_{i}}p^{\hat{\beta}_{im}-1}_{im}.

(18)

3.1.3
Order restricted prior assumption

The objective of an SSALT experiment is to accelerate the failure of the units by increasing the stress level. Therefore, it is quite reasonable to assume that the expected lifetime of the experimental units is lower at the higher stress level. Most of the inferences in the SSALT experiments ignore this assumption. We incorporate this information by developing an order-restricted approach to the shape parameters, assuming an ordered Dirichlet-Gamma distribution as the joint prior. For more information on order-restricted Bayes inference with this prior, refer to the studies
Mondal and Kundu (2020)
;
Pal et al. (2021)
;
Wiedner and Han (2021)
and references therein.

If there is order restriction on shape parameters as
\gamma_{i}<\gamma_{i+1}\;;\;i=1,2,\dots,k-1
and
\gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\gamma_{i}
, then we assume that

\gamma\sim GA(a_{0},b_{0})\;;\;\text{and}\;\bm{p}=\left(\frac{\gamma_{i}}{\gamma}\,,i=1,2,\dots,k\right)\sim DIR(\bm{a}),

where,
\bm{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k}).
Then prior assumption on shape parameter takes the form of Dirichlet-Gamma pdf as

\displaystyle\pi(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{k}|a_{0},b_{0},\bm{a})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma{(a_{1}+a_{2}+\dots+a_{k})}}{\Gamma a_{0}\Gamma a_{1}\dots\Gamma a_{k}}b_{0}^{a_{0}}\gamma^{a_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}e^{-b_{0}\gamma}}

\displaystyle\qquad\sum_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\gamma_{i_{1}}\gamma_{i_{2}}\dots\gamma_{i_{k}}\right)\;;\quad 0<\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}<\dots<\gamma_{k}<\infty.

(19)

Here,
\mathbb{P}
denote the set of all
k!
permutations on
\{1,2,\dots,k\}.
The parameters
c_{j}
assume Normal distribution as
c_{j}\sim N(\mu_{j},\sigma_{j}^{2})\;;\;j=0,1
. Thus, joint prior distribution is given as

\displaystyle\pi(c_{0},c_{1},\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\dots,\gamma_{k}|\bm{\theta}_{H})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_{0}\sigma_{1}}\exp\left\{-\sum_{j=0}^{1}\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{c_{j}-\mu_{j}}{\sigma_{j}}\right)^{2}\right\}\times

\displaystyle\quad\frac{\Gamma{(a_{1}+a_{2}+\dots+a_{k})}}{\Gamma a_{0}\Gamma a_{1}\dots\Gamma a_{k}}b_{0}^{a_{0}}\gamma^{a_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}}e^{-b_{0}\gamma}\times

\displaystyle\qquad\sum_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\gamma_{i_{1}}^{a_{1}-1}\gamma_{i_{2}}^{a_{2}-1}\dots\gamma_{i_{k}}^{a_{k}-1}\right)\;;\;0<\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}<\dots<\gamma_{k}<\infty,

(20)

where,
\bm{\theta}_{H}=\{\mu_{j},\sigma_{j},a_{0},b_{0},\bm{a}\}
are the hyper parameters.

3.2
Posterior Analysis

For robust Bayesian inference in the context of NOSD, following the suggestion of Ghosh and Basu
Ghosh and Basu (2016)
, a maximizer equation based on the DPD measure is presented as

B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\{\frac{n_{s}}{n}p^{\alpha}_{s}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}\frac{n_{im}}{n}p^{\alpha}_{im}\right\}-\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\left\{p_{s}^{\alpha+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}p^{\alpha+1}_{im}\right\},

(21)

where, MDE with
\alpha>0
is the maximizer of
B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})
. Therefore, robust posterior density, a pseudo posterior, can be defined as

\pi_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}|data)=\frac{\exp(B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}))\pi(\bm{\theta})}{\int\exp(B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}))\pi(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}}.

(22)

Here,
\pi_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}|data)
is the proper density for
\alpha>0.
For
\alpha\to 0
, robust pseudo posterior will converge to conventional likelihood-based posterior density. For NOSD testing data posterior densities are given as

•

Under Normal prior

\pi_{\alpha}^{(Nor)}(\bm{\theta}|data)\propto\exp(B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}))\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}(p_{im}-\tilde{p}_{im})^{2}\right\}^{-\sum_{i=1}^{k}q_{i}/2}.

(23)

•

Under Dirichlet prior

\pi_{\alpha}^{(Dir)}(\bm{\theta}|data)\propto\exp(B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}))\left\{p^{\hat{\beta}_{s}-1}_{s}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\prod_{m=1}^{q_{i}}p^{\hat{\beta}_{im}-1}_{im}\right\}.

(24)

•

Under ordered restricted prior assumption

\displaystyle\pi_{\alpha}^{(Ord)}(\bm{\theta}|data)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left\{B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})-\frac{1}{2}\Bigg(\left(\frac{c_{0}-\mu_{0}}{\sigma_{0}}\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{c_{1}-\mu_{1}}{\sigma_{1}}\right)^{2}\Bigg)\right\}\times\gamma^{a_{0}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}a_{i}}\times

\displaystyle\qquad e^{-b_{0}\gamma}\sum_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\gamma_{i_{1}}\gamma_{i_{2}}\dots\gamma_{i_{k}}\right)\;;\;0<\gamma_{1}<\gamma_{2}<\dots<\gamma_{k}<\infty.

(25)

For any loss function
Loss(.,.)
, robust Bayes estimator (RBE) can be obtained as

arg\min_{t}\int Loss(\bm{\theta},t)\pi_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}|data)d\bm{\theta}.

Particularly, for the squared error loss function, the robust Bayes estimator can be derived as

\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(b)}_{\alpha}=\int\bm{\theta}\pi_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}|data)d\bm{\theta}.

(26)

Under all three prior assumptions, Bayes estimate cannot be obtained in closed form. In such situations, Monte Carlo Markov Chain simulation methods can be used to approximate the Bayes estimates. Since widely used methods like Gibbs sampler and Metropolis-Hastings (MH) algorithm struggle with high dimensional or highly correlated variables, therefore there has been a growing interest in using the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) algorithm for Bayesian estimation recently
(
Thach and Bris, 2020
;
Thomas and Tu, 2021
;
Abba et al., 2024
)
. The HMC steps are given in the algorithm (

Algorithm 1

•

\bm{M}
, step size
\epsilon
, leapfrog step

and sample size

•

\bm{\Lambda}^{(0)}.

For

•

Sample

•

\epsilon

Let

\bm{\phi}_{\epsilon/2}=\bm{\phi}^{(t,t^{{}^{\prime}}-1)}+\left.\frac{\epsilon}{2}\frac{\partial\log\pi_{\alpha}(\bm{\Lambda}|t)}{\partial\bm{\Lambda}}\right|_{\bm{\Lambda}=\bm{\Lambda}^{(t-1,t^{{}^{\prime}}-1)}}

\bm{\theta}^{(t-1)}&;\;\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

4
Testing of hypothesis based on robust Bayes factor

Validating whether available data supports the hypothesis of interest is essential for inferential study. For datasets with outliers, robust testing of the hypothesis is pertinent. This section develops robust testing of the hypothesis based on the Bayes factor inspired by the procedure followed by Ghosh et al.
Ghosh et al. (2006)
. For parameter
\bm{\theta}=(c_{0},c_{1},\gamma_{i}\;;\;i=1,2,\dots,k),
consider the vector-valued function
fn:\mathbb{R}^{k+2}\xrightarrow{}\mathbb{R}^{w}.
The null and alternative hypotheses are given as

\bm{H}_{0}:\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{0}\quad\text{against}\quad\bm{H}_{1}:\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{1},

where,
\bm{\Theta}_{0}=\{\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{0}:fn(\bm{\theta})=\bm{0}_{w}\}
and
\bm{\Theta}_{1}=\{\bm{\theta}\notin\bm{\Theta}_{0}\}
. Let
\rho_{0}
and
1-\rho_{0}
be prior probabilities under
\bm{\Theta}_{0}
and
\bm{\Theta}_{1}
respectively. Let
\pi_{j}(\bm{\theta})
be prior density of
\bm{\theta}
under
\bm{\Theta}_{j}
such that,
\int_{\bm{\Theta}_{j}}\pi_{j}(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}=1\,;\,j=0,1
. Then, the prior can be expressed as

\pi(\bm{\theta})=\rho_{0}\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta})I\{\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{0}\}+(1-\rho_{0})\pi_{1}(\bm{\theta})I\{\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{1}\}.

Therefore, posterior probabilities under
\bm{\Theta}_{0}
and
\bm{\Theta}_{1}
are

\displaystyle P_{\pi_{\alpha}}(\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{0}|data)

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{0}}{M_{\alpha}(\pi)}\int_{\bm{\Theta}_{0}}\exp(B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}))\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta})\,d\bm{\theta}.

\displaystyle P_{\pi_{\alpha}}(\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{1}|data)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1-\rho_{0})}{M_{\alpha}(\pi)}\int_{\bm{\Theta}_{1}}\exp(B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}))\pi_{1}(\bm{\theta})\,d\bm{\theta},

where,
M_{\alpha}(\pi)
is the marginal density expressed as

M_{\alpha}(\pi)=\rho_{0}\int_{\bm{\Theta}_{0}}\exp(B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}))\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta})d\bm{\theta}+(1-\rho_{0})\int_{\bm{\Theta}_{1}}\exp(B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}))\pi_{1}(\bm{\theta})\,d\bm{\theta}.

The posterior odds ratio of
H_{0}
relative to
H_{1}
is given as

\frac{P_{\pi_{\alpha}}(\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{0}|data)}{P_{\pi_{\alpha}}(\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{1}|data)}=\left(\frac{\rho_{0}}{1-\rho_{0}}\right)BF_{01},

(27)

where,
BF_{01}
is the Bayes factor given as

BF_{01}=\frac{\int_{\bm{\Theta}_{0}}\exp(B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}))\,d\bm{\theta}}{\int_{\bm{\Theta}_{1}}\exp(B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}))\,d\bm{\theta}}.

(28)

The Bayes factor measures the strength of evidence the data offers supporting one hypothesis over another. Jeffreys
Jeffreys (1998)
suggested a scale to interpret the Bayes factor, and Kass and Raftery
Kass and Raftery (1995)
simplified it further, which is given in Table
1
.

Property of robustenss

This section includes robustness analysis through influence function (IF). Suppose, for a true distribution
F_{\bm{\theta}}
, functional of any estimator is denoted by
T_{\alpha}(F_{\bm{\theta}}).
Then, the influence function is defined as

IF(t;T_{\alpha},F_{\bm{\theta}})=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{T_{\alpha}(U_{\varepsilon})-T_{\alpha}(F_{\bm{\theta}})}{\epsilon}=\left.\frac{\partial(T_{\alpha}(U_{\epsilon}))}{\partial\epsilon}\right|_{\epsilon\to 0^{+}}.

(29)

Here,
U_{\epsilon}=(1-\epsilon)F_{\bm{\theta}}+\epsilon\Delta_{t}
is the contaminated model where
\epsilon,

(0<\epsilon<1)
is the proportion of contamination and
\Delta_{t}
denotes the degenerate distribution at point
t
.

Let
F_{\bm{\theta}}
be the true distribution from where data is generated. If
T_{\alpha}(F_{\bm{\theta}})
is statistical functional of MDE
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\alpha}
,
T_{\alpha}(F_{\bm{\theta}})
will be the value of
\bm{\theta}
which will minimize,

p^{\alpha+1}_{s}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}p^{\alpha+1}_{im}-\left(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)\left\{\left(\int_{I_{s}}dF_{\bm{\theta}}\right)p^{\alpha}_{s}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}\left(\int_{I_{im}}dF_{\bm{\theta}}\right)p^{\alpha}_{im}\right\},

(30)

where
I_{s}=\{t:t>\tau_{k}\}
and
I_{im}=\{t:\tau_{i,m-1}<t\leq\tau_{i,m}\}
. Replacing
F_{\bm{\theta}}
by contaminated model
U_{\epsilon}=(1-\epsilon)F_{\bm{\theta}}+\epsilon\Delta_{t}
in equation (
30
) and differentiating it with respect to
\epsilon
and putting
\epsilon\to 0^{+}
, we get The influence function of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}_{\alpha}
for NOSD testing units as

\displaystyle IF(t;T_{\alpha},F_{\bm{\theta}})=J^{-1}_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta})

\displaystyle\left[\left\{\Delta^{(I_{s})}_{t}-p_{s}\right\}p^{\alpha-1}_{s}\frac{\partial(p_{s})}{\partial\bm{\theta}}\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}\left\{\Delta^{(I_{im})}_{t}-p_{im}\right\}p^{\alpha-1}_{im}\frac{\partial(p_{im})}{\partial\bm{\theta}}\right].

(31)

Here,

\Delta^{(I)}_{t}=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if}\ t\in I\\
0\quad\text{otherwise}.\\
\end{cases}

5.1
Influence function of RBE

To study robustness through IF
(
Ghosh and Basu, 2016
)
, Bayes functional of
\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(b)}_{\alpha}
under squared error loss function is given as

T^{(b)}_{\alpha}(F_{\bm{\theta}})=\frac{\int\bm{\theta}\exp\big\{B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta};F_{\bm{\theta}})\big\}\pi(\bm{\theta})\,d\bm{\theta}}{\int\exp\big\{B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta};F_{\bm{\theta}})\big\}\pi(\bm{\theta})\,d\bm{\theta}},

(32)

\displaystyle\text{where, }B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta};F_{\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\{\left(\int_{I_{s}}dF_{\bm{\theta}}\right)p^{\alpha}_{s}(\bm{\theta})+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}\left(\int_{I_{im}}dF_{\bm{\theta}}\right)p^{\alpha}_{im}(\bm{\theta})\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\left\{p^{\alpha+1}_{s}(\bm{\theta})+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}p^{\alpha+1}_{im}(\bm{\theta})\right\}.

.

The influence function of Bayes estimator
\hat{\bm{\theta}}^{(b)}_{\alpha}

where,
Cov_{(p)}()
is the covariance for posterior distribution and

\displaystyle X_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\left\{\Delta^{(I_{s})}_{t}-p_{s}(\bm{\theta})\right\}p^{\alpha}_{s}(\bm{\theta})+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}\left\{\Delta^{(I_{im})}_{t}-p_{im}(\bm{\theta})\right\}p^{\alpha}_{im}(\bm{\theta})\right].

5.2
Influence function of Bayes factor

Here, the robustness property of the Bayes factor is examined by deriving its IF when the null hypothesis is true. Let
F_{\theta_{0}}
be the true distribution under the null hypothesis
H_{0}:\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{0}
and therefore functional related to the Bayes factor can be defined as

T^{(\alpha)}_{\bm{\Theta}}(F_{\bm{\theta}_{0}})=\frac{\int_{\bm{\Theta}_{0}}\exp\big\{B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{0};F_{\bm{\theta}_{0}})\big\}\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta})\,d\bm{\theta}}{\int_{\bm{\Theta}_{1}}\exp\big\{B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{1};F_{\bm{\theta}_{1}})\big\}\pi_{1}(\bm{\theta})\,d\bm{\theta}}.

(33)

Here,
B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{j};F_{\bm{\theta}_{0}})\,;\,j=0,1
, is expressed as

\displaystyle B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{j};F_{\bm{\theta}_{0}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\{\left(\int_{I_{s}}dF_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\right)p^{\alpha}_{s}(\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}\left(\int_{I_{im}}dF_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}\right)p^{\alpha}_{im}(\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{j})\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{1}{\alpha+1}\left\{p^{\alpha+1}_{s}(\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{j})+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{m=1}^{q_{i}}p^{\alpha+1}_{im}(\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{j})\right\}.

Let contamination in the true distribution
F_{\theta_{0}}
under
H_{0}:\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{0}
be given as
U_{\epsilon}=(1-\epsilon)F_{\bm{\theta}_{0}}+\epsilon\Delta_{t}
. Then, the following result provides an explicit expression of IF under the given setup.

Result 2
.

The influence function of Bayes factor BF
01
is obtained as

where,

Y_{\alpha}(\bm{\Theta})=\frac{\int_{\bm{\Theta}_{0}}\exp\big\{B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{0})\big\}\pi_{0}(\bm{\theta})\,d\bm{\theta}}{\int_{\bm{\Theta}_{1}}\exp\big\{B_{\alpha}(\bm{\theta}\in\bm{\Theta}_{1})\big\}\pi_{1}(\bm{\theta})\,d\bm{\theta}}.

Proof.

Given in the appendix.
∎

Table (

In pure data settings, MLE exhibits a lower magnitude of bias than MDE across all parameters, while BE generally outperforms RBE. Hence, BE performs well without contamination, though RBE offers bias values closer but slightly lower magnitude than BE. However, the advantage of RBE becomes more pronounced when contamination is present, where MLE suffers the most, displaying the highest magnitude of biases. The MDE showcase lower absolute bias than MLE, with its performance depending on tuning parameter

It is also observed that increase in bias from pure to contamination in MDE and RBE is comparatively lower than that of MLE and BE, proving the robustness of DPD based estimation methods.

6.1
Optimal choice of tuning parameter

As discussed in the introduction, the DPD measure-based estimation depends on the choice of tuning parameter
\alpha
. Hence, finding the optimal value for tuning the parameter concerning the interest criteria is required
Basak et al. (2021)
;
Castilla and Chocano (2022)
;
Sugasawa and
Yonekura (2021)
;
Yonekura and
Sugasawa (2023)
. Here, We suggest a non-iterative method based on the approach introduced by Warwick and Jones
Warwick and Jones (2005)
, which involves minimizing the objective function

\Phi_{\alpha}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})=C_{1}\,D_{\alpha}({\hat{\bm{\theta}}})+C_{2}\,tr\Big(J^{-1}_{\alpha}({\hat{\bm{\theta}}})K_{\alpha}({\hat{\bm{\theta}}})J^{-1}_{\alpha}({\hat{\bm{\theta}}})\Big),

(34)

where
D_{\alpha}(\hat{\bm{\theta}})
is the DPD measure indicating robustness,
tr(.)
is a trace of variance-covariance matrix handling precision of the estimates and
C_{1},C_{2}
are predefined positive weight values with
C_{1}+C_{2}=1
. Unlike Warwick and Jones
Warwick and Jones (2005)
and Basak et al.
Basak et al. (2021)
methods, this approach doesn’t need any pilot estimator. The expression for the variance-covariance matrix of the estimates is given in the appendix. The values of
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are situation-specific, whether more weight should be given to robustness or precision. When the dataset contains heavy outliers, maintaining high precision becomes difficult. In this situation, allowing more weight to robustness is quite reasonable. In this study, we have given equal weight values
C_{1}=C_{2}=0.5
for the data analysis.

Testing of hypothesis based on robust Bayes factor

For testing of hypothesis, the robust Bayes factor is used as a test statistic for any particular hypothesis for the given data. Let us define a simple null hypothesis against an alternative hypothesis as

\bm{H}_{0}:\bm{\theta}=\bm{\theta}_{0}\quad\text{against}\quad\bm{H}_{1}:\bm{\theta}\neq\bm{\theta}_{0}.

A continuous prior density would lead to zero prior probability to test
\bm{H}_{0}
. Therefore, it is suggestive to take an
\varepsilon
-neighborhood (spherical) around
\bm{\theta}_{0}
and assign prior probability
\rho_{0}
under
\bm{H}_{0}
. The empirical prior and posterior probabilities are calculated to obtain the empirical Bayes factor. From equation (
27

\text{Posterior odds}=\text{Prior odds}\times\text{Bayes factor}.

Here, the simple null hypothesis under Weibull lifetime distribution to be tested is

Conclusion

The present study has incorporated the cumulative risk model to determine the lifetime prognosis of the nondestructive one-shot device (NOSD) under a step-stress accelerated life testing experiment where the lifetime of NOSD comes from a standard Lehman family of distributions. The robust estimation procedure has been developed in the Bayesian framework, where the robustified posterior involved an exponential form of maximiser equation based on density power divergence. The Hamiltonian Monte Carlo algorithm is employed for the Bayes estimation. An intensive simulation study demonstrated the robustness of the minimum density power divergence and robust Bayes estimator over the conventional maximum likelihood estimator and Bayes estimator as the bias of robust estimators came out to be less than the conventional ones. Further, robust testing of hypotheses is conducted by exploiting the Bayes factor, and the influence function is derived to assess the robustness of the estimators analytically. Finally, a data analysis has been conducted to establish the utility of the theoretical results developed in this work.

This work can be extended to the non-parametric approach for inferential analysis. The step-stress model can be reanalysed under a competing risk set-up. The missing cause of failure analysis can also be conducted. Efforts in this direction are in the pipeline, and we will report these findings soon.
