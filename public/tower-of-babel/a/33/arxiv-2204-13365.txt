Running coupling and non-perturbative corrections for O ( N ) (N) free energy and for disk capacitor
Zoltán Bajnok, János Balog, Árpád Hegedűs and István Vona Holographic QFT Group, Institute for Particle and Nuclear Physics, Wigner Research Centre for Physics H-1525 Budapest 114, P.O.B. 49, Hungary

Abstract
We reconsider the complete solution of the linear TBA equation describing the energy density of finite density states in the O ⁡ ( N ) O(N) nonlinear sigma models by the Wiener-Hopf method. We keep all perturbative and non-perturbative contributions and introduce a running coupling in terms of which all asymptotic series appearing in the problem can be represented as pure power series without logs. We work out the first non-perturbative contribution in the O ⁡ ( 3 ) O(3) case and show that (presumably because of the instanton corrections) resurgence theory fails in this example. Using the relation of the O ⁡ ( 3 ) O(3) problem to the coaxial disks capacitor problem we work out the leading non-perturbative terms for the latter and show that (at least to this order) resurgence theory, in particular the median resummation prescription, gives the correct answer. We demonstrate this by comparing the Wiener-Hopf results to the high precision numerical solution of the original integral equation.

Introduction

Quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the theory of the strong interaction
is asymptotically free in perturbation theory and there is a dynamically
generated mass scale. The nature of the perturbative expansion is
asymptotic, which manifests itself in the factorially growing coefficients.
This factorial growth can be tamed by switching to the Borel plane,
i.e. by analysing the perturbative coefficients divided by the appropriate
factorials. The perturbative expansion of the various observables
on the Borel plane then has a finite radius of convergence and exhibits
singularities typically on the real line. Singularities on the positive
real line prevent Borel summability, leading to non-perturbative ambiguities.
These ambiguities can originate from instantons and/or renormalons

Beneke:1998ui
;
Bauer:2011ws
. The full knowledge of the singularities,
in principle, enables one to formulate an ambiguity free trans-series
for each observable. It would be ideal to proceed along these lines
for QCD, however, only a very limited number of perturbative terms
are at our disposal. Thus, exactly soluble models with properties similar
to QCD have been becoming more and more important.

There are various toy models in two dimensions that share many physically
relevant aspects with QCD, but are nevertheless exactly soluble. They
provide ideal testing grounds of non-perturbative physics. Particularly
interesting are the asymptotically free theories with a dynamically
generated mass gap. By coupling an external field to one of the conserved
charges the free energy can be calculated in two alternative ways:
from perturbation theory based on the UV Lagrangean and also from
the thermodynamic limit of the Bethe ansatz, which uses the IR degrees
of freedom, the masses and scatterings of particles.

In the UV description the singular point of the renormalized running
coupling defines a non-perturbative scale,
\Lambda
, which appears
in the perturbative expansion. On the IR side the expansion is based
on the Wiener-Hopf technique and includes the physical mass of the
particles. By matching the two expansions the highly non-trivial
m/\Lambda

mass gap relation can be established. This calculation was first performed
in the
O(N)
models
Hasenfratz:1990ab
;
Hasenfratz:1990zz

and later it was extended for various other two dimensional integrable
models: for the Gross-Neveu model
Forgacs:1991rs
;
Forgacs:1991ru
,
for the chiral
SU(N)\times SU(N)
model
Balog:1992cm
, for
their supersymmetric extensions
Evans:1994sv
;
Evans:1994sy

and also for the sine-Gordon model
Zamolodchikov:1995xk
. This
calculation involves only the first few perturbative coefficients.
It is notoriously difficult to expand the TBA equations to higher orders
and the next order result, decisive for the AdS/CFT correspondence,
was obtained in the
O(N)
models by direct perturbative calculations

Bajnok:2008it
.

A breakthrough in this field was achieved by Volin
Volin:2009wr
;
Volin:2010cq
,
who managed in the
O(N)
models to transform the expansion of TBA
into algebraic relations and to calculate the first
26 perturbative coefficients.
These were enough to see the factorial growth and to locate the leading
singularities on the Borel plane. His approach was extended to other
relativistic
Marino:2019eym
and non-relativistic models
Marino:2019fuy
;
Marino:2020dgc
;
Marino:2020ggm
;
Reichert:2020ymc
.
By focusing on the
O(3)
and
O(4)
models and performing the calculations
numerically, one can even obtain

perturbative terms, respectively

Abbott:2020mba
;
Abbott:2020qnl
;
Bajnok:2021zjm
. Alternatively,
one can make a systematic large

which can be matched to large
N
renormalon diagrams
DiPietro:2021yxb
;
Marino:2021six
.

Having enough perturbative coefficients fixes the leading factorial
and sub-factorial growths, which determine the nature and locations
of the closest Borel singularities. These singularities give rise
to non-perturbative corrections. The theory which connects the asymptotic
behaviour of the perturbative series to the non-perturbative corrections
is called resurgence, see
Aniceto:2018bis
;
Dorigoni:2014hea

for modern reviews and references therein. In its strongest version
it implies that the perturbative coefficients determine all the non-perturbative
corrections. By taking into account all the non-perturbative corrections
the physical observables can be written into trans-series forms. A
trans-series is a series containing all exponentially suppressed non-perturbative
corrections, where each term is multiplied with an (asymptotic) perturbative
series. It is understood to be Borel resummed laterally and the various
non-perturbative terms ensure that it is ambiguity free. The first
few terms of the trans-series were determined and matched to the numerical
solution of the TBA equation for the
O(4)
model in
Abbott:2020mba
;
Abbott:2020qnl
,
while the leading order results were analytically proven in
Bajnok:2021dri
.
Particularly important are the bridge equations which could relate
the perturbative expansions of the various non-perturbative terms
to each other. A big leap in this direction was the systematic expansion
of the TBA equation, which provided pertubative expansion of the
various non-perturbative contributions in parallel
Marino:2021dzn
.
The aim of our paper is to extend and to elaborate this approach for
the
O(N)
models in many respects.

on the TBA side in the

models, similarly to how it appeared for

The paper is organized as follows: in the rest of this section we
first recall the interesting history of the capacitor problem. We

As the

The Maxwell-Kirchhoff disk capacitor problem

The problem is to calculate the capacity of two, infinitely thin coaxial

1

The solution is not available in closed form but there are results in the limit
of small separation of the disks. If

\kappa=\frac{d}{a}

(1.1)

\frac{C_{\rm phys}}{a^{2}\pi}=\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{d},

(1.2)

If we want to go beyond this approximation, the effect of the edges of the disks

attracted the attention of many prominent physicists in the last one and a half

Integral equations and observables

2.3
Running coupling

Following the idea of
Forgacs:1991rs
, where a running coupling has been
introduced for fermionic models, we now fix the value of the rescaling parameter

v
, thereby promoting it to the status of a running coupling. First we note
that (see appendix
A
)

\beta(\xi)=e^{2\gamma_{1}\xi\ln 2\xi}A(\xi),\qquad\quad A(\xi)=\cos(\pi\gamma_{1}\xi)\,H_{p}(\xi),\qquad\quad\gamma_{1}=2\Delta-1.

(2.43)

It is easy to see that if we define
v
so that it satisfies the relation

2B=\frac{1}{v}+\gamma_{1}\ln v+L,

(2.44)

where
L
is some constant
2
2

2

This constant should not be
confused with the
logarithmic variable
\mathsf{L}
used in the disk capacitor case.
, then

e^{-2Bvx}\beta(vx/2)=e^{-x}{\cal A}(x),

(2.45)

where

{\cal A}(x)=e^{vx(\gamma_{1}\ln x-L)}A(vx/2).

(2.46)

The advantage of using the running coupling
v
instead of
B
itself is that

{\cal A}(x)
can be expanded around
v=0
in a power series:

{\cal A}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(vx)^{k}L_{k}(\ln x),

(2.47)

where
L_{k}
is a polynomial of degree
k
in its argument. Since

A(vx/2)=1+\alpha_{1}vx+O(v^{2}),

(2.48)

the leading expansion coefficients are

L_{0}(z)=1,\qquad,\qquad L_{1}(z)=\gamma_{1}z+\alpha_{1}-L.

(2.49)

Later we will see that (most of) the physical quantities can also be expanded
in
v
as a power series. The choice of the value of the constant
L
is a
matter of convenience and does not change the above conclusion. This is true
since a given running coupling can be power expanded in terms of a running
coupling defined by a different
L
value.

2.4
Exact equations

Using the perturbatively expandable function
{\cal A}(x)
, the equations of the
Wiener-Hopf method can be rewritten as follows.

Q(x)+\frac{\delta_{N,3}\nu}{e(1+vx)}+2i\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{H_{\ell}q_{\ell}}{vx+2\ell\xi_{o}}\nu^{2\ell\xi_{o}}+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\cal C}_{+}}\frac{e^{-y}{\cal A}(y)Q(y)}{x+y}dy=\frac{1}{1-vx},

(2.50)

q_{s}+\frac{\delta_{N,3}\nu}{e(1+2s\xi_{o})}+\frac{i}{\xi_{o}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{H_{\ell}q_{\ell}}{s+\ell}\nu^{2\ell\xi_{o}}+\frac{v}{\pi}\int_{{\cal C}_{+}}\frac{e^{-x}{\cal A}(x)Q(x)}{2s\xi_{o}+vx}dx=\frac{1}{1-2s\xi_{o}},

(2.51)

W=1+M\nu+4i\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{H_{\ell}q_{\ell}}{1-2\ell\xi_{o}}\nu^{2\ell\xi_{o}}+\frac{2v}{\pi}\int_{{\cal C}_{+}}\frac{e^{-x}{\cal A}(x)Q(x)}{1-vx}dx,

(2.52)

w=1+\frac{\delta_{N,3}}{e}\nu+2i\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}H_{\ell}q_{\ell}\nu^{2\ell\xi_{o}}+\frac{v}{\pi}\int_{{\cal C}_{+}}e^{-x}{\cal A}(x)Q(x)dx,

(2.53)

(N=3)\quad k_{+}(i)=-\frac{\nu}{2e}-2i\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{H_{\ell}q_{\ell}}{1+2\ell}\nu^{2\ell}-\frac{v}{\pi}\int_{{\cal C}_{+}}\frac{e^{-x}{\cal A}(x)Q(x)}{1+vx}dx.

(2.54)

Here

M=\left\{\begin{matrix}-2e\left(\frac{\Delta}{e}\right)^{2\Delta}\,\frac{\Gamma(1-\Delta)}{\Gamma(1+\Delta)}e^{i\pi\Delta}\qquad\quad&N\geq 4,\\
-\frac{i\pi}{e}+\frac{2}{e}[2B+1-\gamma_{E}-\ln 2+k_{+}(i)]\qquad\quad&N=3.\end{matrix}\right.

(2.55)

2.5
Trans-series and perturbative expansion,
N\geq 4

The form of the exact equation (
2.50
) suggests that the solution can be
written in a trans-series form

Q(x)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}Q_{m}(x)\nu^{2m\xi_{o}}.

(2.56)

Here the coefficient functions
Q_{m}(x)
still depend on
B
, but in the above
representation we temporarily treat
\nu
as an independent expansion
parameter and its value
\nu=e^{-2B}
will be used only at the end of the
calculation.

In the same spirit we write

q_{s}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}q_{s,m}\nu^{2m\xi_{o}},

(2.57)

W=W_{0}+M\nu+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}W_{m}\nu^{2m\xi_{o}},

(2.58)

w=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}w_{m}\nu^{2m\xi_{o}}.

(2.59)

The leading and next-to-leading coefficients satisfy

Q_{0}(x)+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\cal C}_{+}}\frac{e^{-y}{\cal A}(y)Q_{0}(y)}{x+y}dy=\frac{1}{1-vx},

(2.60)

Q_{1}(x)+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\cal C}_{+}}\frac{e^{-y}{\cal A}(y)Q_{1}(y)}{x+y}dy=-\frac{2iH_{1}q_{1,0}}{2\xi_{o}+vx},

(2.61)

where

q_{1,0}=\frac{1}{1-2\xi_{o}}-\frac{v}{\pi}\int_{{\cal C}_{+}}\frac{e^{-x}{\cal A}(x)Q_{0}(x)}{2\xi_{o}+vx}dx.

(2.62)

The leading and subleading coefficients of
W
and
w
are

W_{0}=1+\frac{2v}{\pi}\int_{{\cal C}_{+}}\frac{e^{-x}{\cal A}(x)Q_{0}(x)}{1-vx}dx,

(2.63)

W_{1}=\frac{4iH_{1}q_{1,0}}{1-2\xi_{o}}+\frac{2v}{\pi}\int_{{\cal C}_{+}}\frac{e^{-x}{\cal A}(x)Q_{1}(x)}{1-vx}dx,

(2.64)

w_{0}=1+\frac{v}{\pi}\int_{{\cal C}_{+}}e^{-x}{\cal A}(x)Q_{0}(x)dx,

(2.65)

w_{1}=2iH_{1}q_{1,0}+\frac{v}{\pi}\int_{{\cal C}_{+}}e^{-x}{\cal A}(x)Q_{1}(x)dx.

(2.66)

Next we expand the coefficient functions perturbatively in the running
coupling
v
. For the leading term
Q_{0}(x)
we define

{\cal A}(x)Q_{0}(x)=\psi(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}v^{k}\psi_{k}(x).

(2.67)

All
B
-dependence is given through the running coupling
v
, the component
functions
\psi_{k}
are
B
-independent.
\psi(x)
satisfies the integral
equation

\frac{\psi(x)}{{\cal A}(x)}+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\cal C}_{+}}\frac{e^{-y}\psi(y)}{x+y}dy=\frac{1}{1-vx}.

(2.68)

Here are the LO and NLO equations of the
perturbative expansion:

\psi_{0}(x)+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-y}\psi_{0}(y)}{x+y}dy=1,

(2.69)

\psi_{1}(x)+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-y}\psi_{1}(y)}{x+y}dy=x+xL_{1}(\ln x)\psi_{0}(x).

(2.70)

For the physical quantities we need the moments of the component functions
defined by

\Omega_{k,s}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x}x^{s}\psi_{k}(x)dx,\qquad\quad\Omega_{k}=\Omega_{k,0}.

(2.71)

Expressed in terms of these moments,

W_{0}=1+2\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\overline{\Omega}_{k}v^{k+1},\qquad\quad\overline{\Omega}_{k}=\sum_{r=0}^{k}\Omega_{k-r,r},

(2.72)

w_{0}=1+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Omega_{k}v^{k+1}.

(2.73)

In appendix
C
we give the solution of the LO and NLO perturbative
problems and calculate the corresponding moments by explicitly solving the
integral equations (
2.69
) and (
2.70
). It is extremely difficult
to go to higher orders with this method. Luckily, using Volin’s method

Volin:2009wr
;
Volin:2010cq
it is possible to obtain the “perturbative” coefficients
\overline{\Omega}_{k}
to very high order
algebraically. Although Volin’s algorithm was worked out originally for
the
1/B
expansion coefficients, it is possible to modify this
algorithm so that it gives directly the series (
2.72
). We can look
at (
2.72
) and (
2.73
) as structural results, proving that these
physical quantities have perturbative expansions in terms of the running
coupling
v
.

The LO and NLO results are

\overline{\Omega}_{0}=\Omega_{0}=\frac{1}{4},\qquad\quad\overline{\Omega}_{1}=\Omega_{1}+\Omega_{0,1}=\frac{3}{16}+\frac{3\Delta}{4}-\frac{r_{o}}{2}-\frac{L}{4},

(2.74)

where

r_{o}=\left(2\Delta-\frac{1}{2}\right)\ln 2-\Delta\ln\Delta.

(2.75)

If one is using the
L=-2r_{o}
scheme,
\ln 2
and
\ln\Delta
disappear from
the NLO coefficient. We found that this feature is preserved also at higher
orders.

We also introduce the moments of

\Sigma_{k,s}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x}x^{s}\phi_{k}(x)dx,\qquad\quad\Sigma_{k}=\Sigma_{k,0}.

(2.84)

It is easy to express

w_{1}=\frac{1}{e}\left(1-\frac{v}{\pi}\int_{{\cal C}_{+}}e^{-x}\phi(x)dx\right)=\frac{1}{e}\left(1-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Sigma_{k}v^{k+1}\right).

(2.86)

where

Z_{k}=\sum_{r=0}^{k}[\Sigma_{k-r,r}+(-1)^{r}\Omega_{k-r,r}].

(2.87)

We see that

The expression (

(2.88)

where

and

(2.89)

Z_{0x}=Z_{1x}=0,\qquad\quad Z_{2x}=\Sigma_{1,1}-\Omega_{1,1}+2\Omega_{0,2}.

(2.90)

Here

\Sigma_{1,1}-\Omega_{1,1}=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-x}xk_{1}(x)dx,\qquad\quad k_{1}(x)=\phi_{1}(x)-\psi_{1}(x).

(2.91)

k_{1}(x)+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-y}k_{1}(y)}{x+y}dy=-2x.

(2.92)

This is related to the NLO problem solved in appendix

Z_{2x}=-\frac{3}{64}.

(2.93)

(2.97)

Comparing (

2.8
The structure of the non-perturbative corrections

In this subsection we summarise the structure of the non-perturbative
corrections and comment on the leading behaviour.

The non-perturbative terms originate from various sources. They have
their seeds in the pole singularities of the integral kernel
\alpha(\omega)
,
in the source term of the integral equation,
\cosh\theta
, affecting
observables of the form

, as well as in the observable, i.e.
in

The integral kernel gives rise to non-perturbative corrections of
the form

2k-1\end{array}&\begin{array}[]{c}N=2k\\
N=2k+1\end{array}\end{cases}

(2.100)

Observe that we have “twice” as many terms in the even case than
in the odd case. This gives a different non-perturbative structure
for even and odd models, a fact, which was not pointed out explicitly
in

.

The source,
\cosh\theta
, produces an extra non-perturbative term

We thus first focus on the simpler

is absent.

Nevertheless, the observable

in

for

and disappears from

and from
\chi_{c}(B)

The non-perturbative corrections indicate a trans-series form of the
various observables. In particular for

Let us now investigate the term

•

For

•

For

which is not related to the asymptotics, was also tested for various

of the integral equation and the median Borel resummation of the truncated
perturbative series. In

This is similar to what happens in the sine-Gordon model

•

In the large
N

obtained from the asymptotics. This bulk energy constant can, however,
be tested against the contribution of the renormalon diagrams at large

Marino:2021six

For other observables such as for
O_{c1}
or for
\chi_{1}(B)

leading term in the
O(4)

Let us focus now on the exceptional

Conclusion

In this paper we investigated integral equations with kernels related
to the
O(N)
sigma models. We analyzed various observables including
the density
O_{c1}
and energy density
O_{cc}
of the groundstate
energy of the
O(N)
sigma models in a magnetic field and the capacitance
of the circular plate capacitor
O_{11}
. We also established relations
between these observables, see (
1.17
).
We advanced in the systematic, Wiener-Hopf
type solution of this class of models considerably. We first simplified
the Wiener-Hopf equations to ensure that only one integral operator
appears. Based on the analytical structure of its kernel we could
introduce a running coupling and show that all observables can be
expanded solely in its powers without any logarithms of it. We also
formulated a form of the equations which handled the non-perturbative
terms systematically showing also only power-like dependence on the
running coupling. These simplified equations are easier to handle
than their previous versions and we also developed a method based
on Laplace transformation how the various orders could be calculated
explicitly. Combining the relations between the observables and the
already known perturbative expansions we could advance in the calculations
of new non-perturbative terms. We investigated the structure of the
non-perturbative corrections and determined their leading terms.

As the
O(3)
kernel is exceptional, we elaborated on this case separately.
We studied the behaviour of the free energy in the
O(3)
sigma model,
where we compared the exactly determined non-perturbative terms to
the direct numerical solution of the integral equations and found
complete agreement. We also confronted the asymptotics of the perturbative
series with the leading non-perturbative corrections and confirmed
the previous findings that they do not match.

The introduction of our running coupling simplified the perturbative
expansion of the coaxial disk capacitance drastically. By expressing
its derivatives in terms of the density and groundstate energy of
the
O(3)
sigma model we were able to analyze also its leading non-perturbative
corrections. We then investigated how resurgence theory works and
we found that the asymptotics of the perturbative expansion contained
all information about the non-perturbative terms. We checked these
results against the numerical solution of the integral equation.

Our simplified equations can be used to calculate the trans-series
representation of the various observables. This trans-series is a
sum of all the exponentially suppressed terms

with

\nu=e^{-1/v-L}/v

multiplied by a typically asymptotic series in the running coupling

v

The Fourier transform of the kernel

\tilde{K}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\theta e^{i\omega\theta}K(\theta)=\frac{e^{-\pi|\omega|}+e^{-2\pi\Delta|\omega|}}{1+e^{-\pi|\omega|}}.

(A.1)

We will define the Fourier transform of other functions analogously and
also indicate it by a tilde. Note that for
O(3)
the Fourier transform of the
kernel simplifies to
e^{-\pi|\omega|}
.

In the Wiener-Hopf analysis an important role is played by the multiplicative
decomposition

G_{+}(\omega)G_{-}(\omega)=\frac{1}{1-\tilde{K}(\omega)},

(A.2)

where
G_{\pm}
are analytic in
H_{\pm}

respectively. Note that
\tilde{K}(0)=1
and
G_{\pm}(\omega)
are singular near

\omega=0
like
|\omega|^{-1/2}
. This is the typical behaviour
for bosonic models. (For fermionic models
G_{\pm}
are regular at
\omega=0
and
this makes their Wiener-Hopf analysis simpler.)
\tilde{K}(\omega)
is real and
symmetric and therefore

G_{-}(\omega)=G_{+}(-\omega).

(A.3)

G_{+}

G_{+}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{-i\Delta\omega}}\ \frac{\Gamma(1-i\omega\Delta)}{\Gamma(1/2-i\omega/2)}\exp\left\{-i\omega\Delta[1-\ln(-i\omega\Delta)]+i\omega[1-\ln(-i\omega/2)]/2\right\}.

(A.4)

For small real
\omega

G_{+}(\omega)\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\Delta|\omega|}}e^{\frac{i\pi}{4}{\rm sign}(\omega)}

(A.5)

and for asymptotically large argument in
H_{+}

G_{+}(i\kappa)=1+O(1/\kappa),\qquad\quad\kappa\to\infty.

(A.6)

We also need

G_{+}(i)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\Delta}}\Gamma(1+\Delta)e^{\gamma_{1}/2-\Delta\ln\Delta},\qquad\quad\gamma_{1}=2\Delta-1.

(A.7)

Functions

vanishing for

and

, analytic in
H_{+}
and
H_{-}
,

f^{(\pm)}(\omega)=\mp\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{f(\omega^{\prime})d\omega^{\prime}}{\omega-\omega^{\prime}}.

(A.8)

analytic in

(A.14)

{\cal F}(s)=\frac{\ln(s+1)\hat{\rho}_{o}+{\rm const.}}{2\sqrt{s+1}^{3}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s+1}^{5}}\right).

(C.33)

For the
\chi_{1}
problem the Laplace space functions are

{\cal G}_{0}
,
{\cal G}_{1}
,

{\cal G}^{(i)}_{1}
, corresponding to (repectively)
g_{0}
,
g_{1}
,
g^{(i)}_{1}
.
Again, it will turn out to be convenient to introduce the ad hoc notations

\rho(s)=\sqrt{s+1}-\sqrt{s},\qquad{\cal G}_{0}(s)=2\sqrt{\pi}(Y(s)-\sqrt{s+1}),\qquad Q(s)=Y(s)-\sqrt{s},

(C.34)

and

{\cal G}_{1}^{(a)}(s)=2\sqrt{\pi}Z(s),\qquad\quad{\cal G}_{1}^{(b)}(s)=2\sqrt{\pi}H(s).

(C.35)

We start by calculating the right hand side of the Laplace transformed LO
equation (
B.18
):

\int_{0}^{\infty}dxe^{-(s+1)x}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-y}-1}{(x+y)y\sqrt{y}}dy=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dp}{p+s+1}(\sqrt{p}-\sqrt{p+1}).

(C.36)

It is easy to see that in this notation the LO problem becomes

(1+K)Q=\rho

(C.37)

and the two partial NLO problems are

\begin{split}(1+K)Z&=-Y^{\prime},\\
(1+K)H&=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dp}{p}[Y^{\prime}(p+s)-e^{-p}Y^{\prime}(s)].\end{split}

(C.38)

C.4
Exchange relations

For the solution of the integral equations we will employ the following
identities, which are easily obtained by partial integration.

(K{\cal F})^{\prime}=-K[{\cal F}^{\prime}]-\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{{\cal F}(0)}{s+1},

(C.39)

(K{\cal F})^{\prime\prime}=K[{\cal F}^{\prime\prime}]+\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{{\cal F}^{\prime}(0)}{s+1}+\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{{\cal F}(0)}{(s+1)^{2}},

(C.40)

K[s(s+1){\cal F}^{\prime\prime}]=s(s+1)(K{\cal F})^{\prime\prime}+\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{{\cal F}(0)}{s+1},

(C.41)

K[(2s+1){\cal F}^{\prime}]=(2s+1)(K{\cal F})^{\prime}-\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{{\cal F}(0)}{s+1}.

(C.42)

Introducing the second order differential operator

{\cal D}_{a}=s(s+1)\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}+a(2s+1)\frac{d}{ds}

(C.43)

we can establish the exchange relation

K[{\cal D}_{a}{\cal F}]={\cal D}_{a}(K{\cal F})+\frac{(1-a){\cal F}(0)}{s+1}.

(C.44)

C.5
Solution of the LO problems

We start from the identity

\hat{\cal D}_{1}\rho=-\frac{1}{4}(1+K)\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\right],

(C.45)

where

\hat{\cal D}_{1}={\cal D}_{1}-\frac{3}{4}.

(C.46)

The operator
\hat{\cal D}_{1}
commutes with the integral operator
K
.

Applying
\hat{\cal D}_{1}
to the LO equation we get

\hat{\cal D}_{1}(1+K)Q=(1+K)\hat{\cal D}_{1}Q=-\frac{1}{4}(1+K)\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}\right],

(C.47)

which is equivalent to

\hat{\cal D}_{1}Q+\frac{1}{4\sqrt{s}}=\hat{\cal D}_{1}(Q+\sqrt{s})=\hat{\cal D}_{1}Y=0.

(C.48)

The last differential equation is a slightly modified hypergeometric equation
with parameters
-1/2,3/2;1
and argument
-s
. Since
Y(s)
is regular at

s=0
,
Y
must be proportional to the hypergeometric function:

Y(s)=Y(0){}_{2}F_{1}\left(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2};1;-s\right).

(C.49)

From the known properties of the hypergeometric function we establish that for
large
s

Y(s)=\frac{4Y(0)}{\pi}\sqrt{s+1}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s+1}}\right).

(C.50)

Since
{\cal G}_{0}(s)
must vanish for large
s
, we conclude that
Y(0)=\pi/4

and

Y(s)=\frac{\pi}{4}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2};1;-s\right).

(C.51)

For the leading moment we thus find

\bar{\gamma}_{0}=\frac{1}{\pi}{\cal G}_{0}(0)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}.

(C.52)

The solution of the LO
\chi_{c}
problem is completely analogous. Here we start
from

{\cal D}_{2}(1+K)U=(1+K)[{\cal D}_{2}U]+\frac{U(0)}{\pi(s+1)}=\frac{-2}{s+1},

(C.53)

from which it follows that

(1+K)\left\{{\cal D}_{2}U+\left[\frac{U(0)}{\pi}+2\right]U\right\}=0.

(C.54)

We again encounter a hypergeometric differential equation. In this case
the solution can be written in terms of a so far unknown parameter
\alpha
as

U(s)=\pi\left(\frac{1}{4}-\alpha^{2}\right){}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{3}{2}-\alpha,\frac{3}{2}+\alpha;2;-s\right).

(C.55)

The asymptotic behaviour of the hypergeometric function implies that
U(s)

for large
s
behaves as

U(s)\sim\frac{1}{(s+1)^{3/2-|\alpha|}}.

(C.56)

On the other hand we know from (
C.29
) that (up to logs)

U(s)\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{s+1}^{3}}.

(C.57)

This fixes
\alpha=0
and the final result is

U(s)=\frac{\pi}{4}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\frac{3}{2},\frac{3}{2};2;-s\right).

(C.58)

The actual large
s
behaviour of
U(s)
is

U(s)=\frac{\ln(s+1)+4\ln 2-2}{2\sqrt{s+1}^{3}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s+1}^{5}}\right),

(C.59)

which is consistent with (
C.33
) at LO.

The leading moment here is given by

\Omega_{0}=\frac{1}{\pi}U(0)=\frac{1}{4}

(C.60)

and for later purposes we also compute

\Omega_{0,1}=-\frac{1}{\pi}U^{\prime}(0)=\frac{9}{32},\qquad\quad\Omega_{0,2}=\frac{1}{\pi}U^{\prime\prime}(0)=\frac{75}{128}.

(C.61)

C.6
Solution of the NLO
\chi_{1}
problem

By taking the derivative of the LO equation (
C.37
) and using (
C.39
)
we obtain

K[Y^{\prime}]=Y^{\prime}-\frac{1}{4(s+1)},

(C.62)

which can be rewritten as

(1+K)[Y^{\prime}]=2Y^{\prime}-\frac{1}{4}(1+K)U.

(C.63)

Comparing this to the first equation in (
C.38
) we immediately see that

Z=-\frac{1}{2}\left(Y^{\prime}+\frac{U}{4}\right).

(C.64)

To solve the second NLO equation we introduce

\hat{\cal D}_{2}={\cal D}_{2}+\frac{5}{4},\qquad\qquad\hat{\cal D}_{2}Y^{\prime}=0.

(C.65)

Acting with
\hat{\cal D}_{2}
on the second NLO equation we get

\hat{\cal D}_{2}(1+K)H=(1+K)\left\{\hat{\cal D}_{2}H+\frac{H(0)}{\pi}U\right\}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dp}{p}\hat{\cal D}_{2}Y^{\prime}(p+s)=(2s+1)Y^{\prime\prime}+3Y^{\prime}.

(C.66)

Now it is easy to show that

(1+K)\left\{sY^{\prime\prime}-\frac{U}{4}\right\}=(2s+1)Y^{\prime\prime}

(C.67)

and this, together with (
C.64
) gives

\hat{\cal D}_{2}\left\{H+\left[\frac{1}{8}-\frac{H(0)}{\pi}\right]U\right\}=sY^{\prime\prime}+\frac{3}{2}Y^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\hat{\cal D}_{2}\left\{\ln(s+1)Y^{\prime}+\frac{Y}{s+1}\right\}.

(C.68)

Thus the solution for
H
is of the form

H=\left[\frac{H(0)}{\pi}-\frac{1}{8}\right]U+\frac{1}{2}\ln(s+1)Y^{\prime}+\frac{Y}{2(s+1)}+\xi Y^{\prime},

(C.69)

where we have added the regular (at
s=0
) solution of the homogeneous equation
with a so far undetermined coefficient
\xi
. Self-consistency requires

H(0)=\frac{\pi}{8}+\frac{\pi\xi}{4}

(C.70)

and finally we get

H=\xi\left(Y^{\prime}+\frac{U}{4}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ln(s+1)Y^{\prime}+\frac{Y}{2(s+1)}.

(C.71)

The constant
\xi
can be fixed as follows. Asymptotically

Z=-\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{s+1}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s+1}^{3}}\right),\qquad H=\frac{1}{4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{s+1}}\left\{\ln(s+1)+2\xi+2\right\}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{s+1}^{3}}\right)

(C.72)

and comparing this to (
C.19
) and (
C.27
) we can fix

\xi=\frac{\gamma_{E}}{2}+\ln 2-1.

(C.73)

This gives

\bar{\gamma}_{1}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}\left\{L-\alpha_{1}+\gamma_{1}(\gamma_{E}+2\ln 2-1)\right\}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{4}(L+2r_{o})

(C.74)

and using the definition (
B.29
) we reproduce (
B.30
).
Further we calculate

\bar{\gamma}_{0,1}=2\sqrt{\pi}\left(-Y^{\prime}(0)+\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{1}{\pi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}-\frac{3\sqrt{\pi}}{8},\qquad\quad I^{\prime}(1)=-\sqrt{\pi}

(C.75)

and find

R_{1}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left\{\bar{\gamma}_{1}+\bar{\gamma}_{0,1}+\frac{1}{\pi}I^{\prime}(1)\right\}=r_{1}-\frac{3}{4},\qquad\tilde{R}_{1}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left\{\bar{\gamma}_{1}-\bar{\gamma}_{0,1}-\frac{1}{\pi}I^{\prime}(1)\right\}=r_{1}+\frac{3}{4}.

(C.76)

For later use we also compute

\bar{\gamma}_{1,1}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left\{(L-\alpha_{1})Z^{\prime}(0)-\gamma_{1}H^{\prime}(0)\right\}=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\left(\frac{3}{4}r_{1}-\frac{5}{8}\gamma_{1}\right),

(C.77)

\bar{\gamma}_{0,2}=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\left(Y^{\prime\prime}(0)+\frac{1}{4}\right)=-\frac{15}{64}\sqrt{\pi}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}},\qquad\bar{\gamma}_{0,2}-\frac{1}{\pi}I^{\prime\prime}(1)=-\frac{15\sqrt{\pi}}{64}.

(C.78)

To determine

Acting with

Appendix D
Volin’s
O(3)
algorithm for general running coupling
v

In this section we briefly sketch how to calculate
\epsilon
and
\rho
in terms of
v
(in particular in terms of
the special coupling
\beta
(
3.7
)) from the result of the algorithm introduced in Appendix E.3 of
Volin:2010cq
.

Since from the ratio
\epsilon/\rho^{2}
all
\ln B
-s drop out,
this algorithm generates the densities themselves without such terms,
as a plain power series in
B^{-1}
. As explained in
Volin:2010cq
, this is done by formally treating
\ln B

as a variable independent of
B^{-1}
, both inside the above quantities
and in the definition of the
\alpha
running coupling:

\frac{1}{\alpha}=B+\frac{1}{2}\ln B+2\ln 2-\frac{1}{2}+\ln\hat{U}(B),\quad\hat{U}(B)\equiv\frac{4\pi e^{-\left(B+1/2\right)}}{\sqrt{B}}O_{1c}.

(D.1)

As
\ln B
drops out from the final result
\epsilon/\rho^{2}
,
it was admissible to fix it to any value,
\ln B=\ln B_{0}
. Here

B_{0}
is arbitrary, and it was chosen to be
B_{0}=e^{-1}
for
the
O(3)
model to simplify the algorithm. As we need both
\epsilon

and
\rho
to calculate the capacitance
C
, we must either restore
their
\ln B
dependence or try to reuse the data generated in the
above conventions. The idea is that since the densities
W_{0}
and

\hat{\rho}_{0}
are also a power series in the running coupling

v
, we might fix
\ln v
(as an independent variable) to a constant
as well, which must also correspond to the above
\ln B=\ln e^{-1}=-1

choice. Taking the logarithm of (
3.5
) gives
us

\ln v+\ln(2B)=\ln\left(1+v(\ln v+L)\right),

(D.2)

thus if we fix
\ln(B)=-1
,
L=\ln\left(\frac{2}{e}\right)
, and

\ln v=-L
simultaneously, we can satisfy the above equation. This
basically means, we may simply relate the
B^{-1}
expansion coming
from the algorithm to our
v
expansion by

\displaystyle 2B

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{v},

that is, what remains from (
3.5
). What
we get is the result in terms of a specific running coupling - let us denote this particular choice of the coupling as
\gamma
:

2B=\frac{1}{\gamma}+\ln\gamma+\ln\left(\frac{2}{e}\right).

(D.3)

Now the problem is that in the original algorithm we have also got
rid of all the
\ln 2
-s. However in our special running coupling
(
3.7
) the quantities are also free of
\ln 2
. It is
then admissible to switch from (
D.3
) to
(
3.7
) :

2B=\frac{1}{\gamma}+\ln\gamma+\ln 2-1\quad\Rightarrow\quad 2B=\frac{1}{\beta}+\ln\beta-3\ln 2+1,

(D.4)

while putting
\ln 2\to 0
everywhere in the above equations, i.e. omitting the
\ln 2
term in the definition of
\gamma
, and the
-3\ln 2
term in that of
\beta
. In the end, we may switch from the
\beta
coupling to any
v
coupling with arbitrary
L
value (while of course keeping the
\ln 2
-s in both the definition of
\beta
and the generic
v
).

To see an example, let us show this procedure step-by-step for
W_{0}
in (
2.72
). In the upper line, we start from the (incomplete) log-free result given by the algorithm in
B
and substitute
B^{-1}\to 2\gamma
simply:

\begin{array}[]{lll}1+\frac{1}{4}B^{-1}+\frac{19}{32}B^{-2}+\mathcal{O}(B^{-3})&&1+\frac{1}{2}\gamma+\frac{19}{8}\gamma^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{3})\\
&\;\Rightarrow&\\
1+\frac{1}{4}B^{-1}+\left[\frac{15}{32}-\frac{1}{8}\ln(16B)\right]B^{-2}+\mathcal{O}(B^{-3})&&1+\frac{1}{2}\gamma+\left(\frac{19}{8}-2\ln 2\right)\gamma^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{3}),\\
\end{array}

(D.5)

while in the line underneath we start from the correct result and use (
D.3
). Then, for the log-free case, we may replace
\gamma^{-1}+\ln\gamma-1\to\beta^{-1}+\ln\beta+1
, while for the exact case we must use (
D.4
) without dropping
\ln 2
-s. One can achieve the change of couplings by calculating the series expansion of
\gamma
in terms of
\beta
iteratively in both cases:

\begin{array}[]{rlll}\gamma(\beta)|_{\text{no logs}}&=\beta-2\beta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{3})&&\\
&&\;\Rightarrow&W_{0}=1+\frac{1}{2}\beta+\frac{11}{8}\beta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{3}),\\
\gamma(\beta)&=\beta-\left(2-4\ln 2\right)\beta^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\beta^{3})&&\\
\end{array}

(D.6)

and then realize that in the end, both methods give the same result in
\beta
.

To summarize, we can directly use the coefficients of the original algorithm, which drops
\ln B
and
\ln 2
from the start by a simple change of variables (
D.4
) - without
\ln(2)
-s, and get the perturbative coefficients of the densities in terms of any running coupling
v
.
