Geometry of Associated Quantum Vector Bundles and the Quantum Gauge Group
Gustavo Amilcar Saldaña Moncada

Abstract
. It is well–known that if one is given a principal G G –bundle with a principal connection, then for every unitary finite–dimensional linear representation of G G one can induce a linear connection and a Hermitian structure on the associated vector bundles which are compatible. Furthermore, the gauge group acts on the space of principal connections and on the space of linear connections defined on the associated vector bundles. This paper aims to present the non–commutative geometrical counterpart of all of these classical facts in the theory of quantum bundles and quantum connections. MSC 2010: 46L87, 58B99. Keywords: Quantum Connections, Hermitian Structures, Quantum Gauge Group.

A compact matrix quantum group (or a just a quantum group) will be denoted by

–corepresentation on a

\delta^{V}:V\longrightarrow V\otimes H

(2)

(\mathrm{id}_{V}\otimes\epsilon)\circ\delta^{V}\cong\mathrm{id}_{V}\qquad\mbox{ and }\qquad(\mathrm{id}_{V}\otimes\Delta)\circ\delta^{V}=(\delta^{V}\otimes\mathrm{id}_{H})\circ\delta^{V}.

We say that the corepresentation is

if
\dim_{\mathbb{C}}(V)<|\mathbb{N}|
. The map

is often referred to as
(right) coaction
of
\mathcal{G}
on

. It is worth mentioning that in the general theory (
[

]

Given two
\mathcal{G}
–corepresentations

T:V\longrightarrow W\qquad\mbox{ such that }\qquad(T\otimes\mathrm{id}_{H})\circ\delta^{V}=\delta^{W}\circ T.

The set of all corepresentation morphisms between two corepresentations

\delta^{W}
will be denoted as

(4)

\textsc{Mor}(\delta^{V},\delta^{W}),

and the set of all finite–dimensional

(5)

\textsc{Obj}(\mathbf{Rep}_{\mathcal{G}}).

A
\mathcal{G}
–corepresentation

is

if there exists a non–trivial subspace

(L\not=\{0\},V)
such that

–corepresentation

(not necessarily unique) that makes the corepresentation unitary. Henceforth, we will assume that every finite–dimensional

–corepresentation is unitary. A proof of the following theorem can be found in

]
.

.

Let

The Leibniz rule.

(2)

For every

If

\displaystyle\sum_{k}(dh^{\ast}_{k})g^{\ast}_{k}=0
, for

.

Under these assumptions, there is an antilinear involution

such that
(\vartheta g)^{\ast}=g^{\ast}\vartheta^{\ast}

(g\vartheta)^{\ast}=\vartheta^{\ast}g^{\ast}
and
(dg)^{\ast}=d(g^{\ast})

\in

\Gamma

\in

H
. It is worth mentioning that, in general, one does not need a quantum group or a

(2)

In a similar manner, one define a left covariant

by taking

\mathrm{Ad}:\Gamma^{\wedge}\longrightarrow\Gamma^{\wedge}\otimes\Gamma^{\wedge}

such that
\mathrm{Ad}(\vartheta)=(-1)^{\partial\vartheta^{(1)}\partial\vartheta^{(2)}}\vartheta^{(2)}\otimes S(\vartheta^{(1)})\vartheta^{(3)},

Clearly,

extends the right adjoint coaction

of

.

Let us define

(31)

\mathfrak{qg}^{\#\wedge}=\otimes^{\bullet}\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}/A^{\wedge},\quad\otimes^{\bullet}\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}:=\bigoplus_{k}(\otimes^{k}\mathfrak{qg}^{\#})\;\mbox{ with }\;\otimes^{k}\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}:=\underbrace{\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}}_{k\;times},

where

generated by elements

The space
\mathfrak{qg}^{\#\wedge}
is a graded differential
\ast
–subalgebra and it satisfies
\mathfrak{qg}^{\#\wedge}=\{\theta\in\Gamma^{\wedge}\mid\Phi_{\Gamma^{\wedge}}(\theta)=\mathbbm{1}\otimes\theta\}

[
4
]
. Furthermore, it is possible to extend the right
H

\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}
(see equation (

\mathfrak{qg}^{\#^{\wedge}}
by means of

(32)

1\circ g=\epsilon(g),\quad(\theta_{1}\theta_{2})\circ g=(\vartheta_{1}\circ g^{(1)})(\theta_{2}\circ g^{(2)}).

It is worth mentioning that

(33)

d\pi(g)=-\pi(g^{(1)})\pi(g^{(2)})

for all
g

\in

H
. According to
[
4
]
, the following identification holds:

(34)

\Gamma^{\wedge}=H\otimes\mathfrak{qg}^{\#\wedge}.

Let
G
be a compact matrix Lie group and
\mathcal{G}
its associated quantum group. If the bicovariant
\ast
–FODC of
\mathcal{G}
is defined by
\mathcal{R}=\mathrm{Ker}^{2}(\epsilon)=\displaystyle\{\sum^{n}_{i=1}a_{i}\,b_{i}\mid a_{i},\,b_{i}\,\in\,\mathrm{Ker}(\epsilon),\;n\,\in\,\mathbb{N}\}
, then, according to
[
8
,
29
]

(35)

\Gamma=H\otimes\mathfrak{g}^{\#}_{\mathbb{C}},\qquad\mbox{ where }\qquad{\mathrm{Ker}(\epsilon)\over\mathcal{R}}={\mathrm{Ker}(\epsilon)\over\mathrm{Ker}^{2}(\epsilon)}=\mathfrak{g}^{\#}_{\mathbb{C}}

is the complexification of the dual Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of

\mathfrak{qg}^{\#\wedge}=\bigwedge\mathfrak{g}^{\#}_{\mathbb{C}}
is the exterior algebra of
\mathfrak{g}^{\#}_{\mathbb{C}}
and by equation (

\Gamma^{\wedge}=H\otimes\bigwedge\mathfrak{g}^{\#}_{\mathbb{C}}

[
29
]
. In other words,

is a subalgebra of the algebra of
\mathbb{C}
–valued differential forms of

\ast
–calculus is a proper generalization of the algebra of
\mathbb{C}
–valued differential forms of

in non–commutative geometry. In this way, for a given quantum group
\mathcal{G}
and a bicovariant
\ast
–FODC
(\Gamma,d)
over
\mathcal{G}
, the triplet

will be interpreted as the algebra of
quantum differential forms
of
\mathcal{G}
. In this sense, the space

\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}={\mathrm{Ker}(\epsilon)\over\mathcal{R}}

plays the role of the

and the
\mathrm{ad}
corepresentation plays the role of the

of the right adjoint action of

on
\mathfrak{g}
.

Unfortunately, not all conditions on
H^{\infty}
can be extended to
\Gamma^{\wedge\,\infty}
. In fact, we have the following example.

Example 2.5
.

Let
\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\{0,1\}
be the group of integers modulo
2
and
\mathcal{G}
be its associated quantum group
[
7
]
. Then
H=\{\phi:S_{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\}
has a canonical linear basis given by

\phi_{0}(x)=\left\{0\;\;\mbox{ if }\;\;x=1\atop 1\;\;\mbox{ if }\;\;x=0,\right.\qquad\phi_{1}(x)=\left\{0\;\;\mbox{ if }\;\;x=0\atop 1\;\;\mbox{ if }\;\;x=1.\right.

Since
\mathrm{Ker}(\epsilon)=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\phi_{1}\}
, by Proposition
2.2
, the quantum group
\mathcal{G}
has only two bicovariant
\ast
–FODC’s: the trivial one defined by
\mathcal{R}=\mathrm{Ker}(\epsilon)
and the universal one defined by
\mathcal{R}=\{0\}
(see equation (
10
)). Let us choose the universal
\ast
–FODC
(\Gamma_{U},D)
. Then, for every
g

\in

\mathrm{Ker}(\epsilon)
we have

\pi(g)=\mathbbm{1}\otimes[g]_{\mathcal{R}}\cong[g]_{\mathcal{R}}=g,

where
[g]_{\mathcal{R}}
denotes the equivalence class of
g
in
\mathrm{Ker}(\epsilon)/\mathcal{R}=\mathrm{Ker}(\epsilon)
. On the other hand, by equation (
17
) we obtain
D\phi_{1}=-D\phi_{0}=(\phi_{0}-\phi_{1})\pi(\phi_{1})
. Thus, for every
g=\lambda^{0}\phi_{0}+\lambda^{1}\phi_{1}

\in

H
(
\lambda^{0}
,
\lambda^{1}

\in

\mathbb{C}
) we obtain

(36)

Dg=(\lambda^{1}-\lambda^{0})D\phi_{1}=(\lambda^{1}-\lambda^{0})(\phi_{0}-\phi_{1})\pi(\phi_{1}).

Let
(\Gamma^{\wedge},d,\ast)
be the universal differential envelope
\ast
–calculus. Then
\Gamma^{\wedge}=\otimes^{\bullet}_{H}\Gamma_{U}/\mathcal{Q}
, where
\mathcal{Q}
is the bilateral ideal generated by elements
\displaystyle\sum_{i}Dg_{i}\otimes_{H}Dh_{i}
such that
\displaystyle\sum_{i}g_{i}\,Dh_{i}=0.
By equation (
36
), together with the facts that
\{\pi(\phi_{1})\}
is a left
H
–basis of
\Gamma_{U}
(
[
27
]
) and
\phi_{0}-\phi_{1}
is invertible (with
(\phi_{0}-\phi_{1})^{-1}=\phi_{0}-\phi_{1}
), we get

\sum_{i}g_{i}\,Dh_{i}=0\;\Longrightarrow\;\sum_{i}Dg_{i}\otimes_{H}Dh_{i}=0.

Hence
\mathcal{Q}=\{0\}
, and therefore
(\Gamma^{\wedge},d,\ast)
is the universal graded differential
\ast
–algebra
[
27
,
30
]
.

Let
R:H\otimes H\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a linear map such
H
is coquasitriangular (
[
26
]
), and assume that
R
can be extended to a map
R:\Gamma^{\wedge}\otimes\Gamma^{\wedge}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
so that
\Gamma^{\wedge}
is coquasitriangular as well. Then

(37)

\phi_{0}\,\pi(\phi_{1})=R(\pi(\phi_{1})^{(1)}\otimes\phi^{(1)}_{0})\,\pi(\phi_{1})^{(2)}\,\phi^{(2)}_{0}\,\overline{R}(\pi(\phi_{1})^{(3)}\otimes\phi^{(3)}_{0}),

where
\overline{R}
is the convolution inverse of
R

[
26
]
. By equation (
12
) and the definition of
\Delta
on
\Gamma^{\wedge\,1}=\Gamma_{U}
(given above equation (
26
)) we have

(38)

\Delta(\pi(\phi_{1}))={{}_{\Gamma_{U}}}\Phi(\pi(\phi_{1}))+\Phi_{\Gamma_{U}}(\pi(\phi_{1}))=\pi(\phi_{1})\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbbm{1}\otimes\pi(\phi_{1}).

Thus
\pi(\phi_{1})^{(1)}\otimes\pi(\phi_{1})^{(2)}\otimes\pi(\phi_{1})^{(3)}=\pi(\phi_{1})\otimes\mathbbm{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbbm{1}\otimes\pi(\phi_{1})\otimes\mathbbm{1}+\mathbbm{1}\otimes\mathbbm{1}\otimes\pi(\phi_{1}).
On the other hand,
\phi^{(1)}_{0}\otimes\phi^{(2)}_{0}\otimes\phi^{(3)}_{0}=\phi_{0}\otimes\phi_{0}\otimes\phi_{0}+\phi_{1}\otimes\phi_{1}\otimes\phi_{0}+\phi_{0}\otimes\phi_{1}\otimes\phi_{1}+\phi_{1}\otimes\phi_{0}\otimes\phi_{1}
and due to the fact that
R(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\phi_{0})=\overline{R}(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\phi_{0})=\epsilon(\phi_{0})=1
and
R(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\phi_{1})=\overline{R}(\mathbbm{1}\otimes\phi_{1})=\epsilon(\phi_{1})=0
, equation (
37
) reduces to

\phi_{0}\,\pi(\phi_{1})=\phi_{0}(R(\pi(\phi_{1})\otimes\phi_{0})+\overline{R}(\pi(\phi_{1})\otimes\phi_{0}))+\phi_{1}(R(\pi(\phi_{1})\otimes\phi_{1})+\overline{R}(\pi(\phi_{1})\otimes\phi_{1}))+\pi(\phi_{1})\phi_{0}.

Since the sum
\phi_{0}(R(\pi(\phi_{1})\otimes\phi_{0})+\overline{R}(\pi(\phi_{1})\otimes\phi_{0}))+\phi_{1}(R(\pi(\phi_{1})\otimes\phi_{1})+\overline{R}(\pi(\phi_{1})\otimes\phi_{1}))
has degree
0
, it must vanish. Hence

\phi_{0}\,\pi(\phi_{1})=\pi(\phi_{1})\,\phi_{0}.

However,
\pi(\phi_{1})\,\phi_{0}=\phi_{1}\,\pi(\phi_{1})
and it follows that
\phi_{0}\,\pi(\phi_{1})=\phi_{1}\,\pi(\phi_{1}).
Since the element
\phi_{0}-\phi_{1}
is invertible, this implies
\pi(\phi_{1})=0
, which is a contradiction. Therefore, the coquasitriangular structure cannot be extended to
\Gamma^{\wedge}
.

The previous example illustrates that even for the simplest non–trivial quantum group equipped with its universal graded differential
\ast
–algebra, it is impossible to extend the coquasitriangular structure. For degrees

,
\Gamma^{\wedge}
is
highly non–commutative
, so no general scalar–valued formula can relate all elements in these degrees. Note that in this case, the universal graded differential
\ast
–algebra is the only non–trivial graded differential
\ast
–algebra generated by degree
0
elements.

2.2.
Basic Theory of Quantum Principal Bundles

At this point, we introduce the notions of quantum principal bundles and
quantum principal connections. As we have mentioned earlier, our work is based on the theory developed by M. Durdevich, although we modify the definition of quantum principal connections and adopt the notation used in
[
13
]
. For the purposes of this paper, we will present all the necessary basics of Durdevich’s formulation while explaining the motivation behind some of the definitions. Of course, there are similarities between Durdevich’s formulation and the one presented in
[
28
]
, especially in degrees
0
and
1
; however, there are also differences, particularly for degrees
2
and higher. The reader is encouraged to consult the original works
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
for further details.

Let
(B,\cdot,\mathbbm{1},\ast)
be a quantum space and let
\mathcal{G}
be a quantum group. A
quantum principal
\mathcal{G}
–bundle
over
B
(abbreviated “qpb”) is a quantum structure formally represented by the triplet

(39)

\zeta=(P,B,\Delta_{P}),

where
(P,\cdot,\mathbbm{1},\ast)
is called the
quantum total space
, and
(B,\cdot,\mathbbm{1},\ast)
is a quantum subspace, which receives the name
quantum base space
. Furthermore,

\Delta_{P}:P\longrightarrow P\otimes H

is a
\ast
–algebra morphism that satisfies

(1)

\Delta_{P}
is a
\mathcal{G}
–corepresentation.

(2)

\Delta_{P}(x)=x\otimes\mathbbm{1}
if and only if
x

\in

B
.

(3)

The linear map
\beta:P\otimes P\longrightarrow P\otimes H
given by

\beta(x\otimes y):=x\cdot\Delta_{P}(y):=(x\otimes\mathbbm{1})\cdot\Delta_{P}(y)

is surjective.

In general, there is no need to work with a quantum group; a
\ast
–Hopf algebra is sufficient
[
5
,
27
]
. Given
\zeta
a qpb over
B
, a
differential calculus
on it is:

(1)

A graded differential
\ast
–algebra
(\Omega^{\bullet}(P),d,\ast)
generated by
\Omega^{0}(P)=P
(
quantum differential forms of
P
).

(2)

A bicovariant
\ast
–FODC
(\Gamma,d)
over
\mathcal{G}
.

(3)

The map
\Delta_{P}
is extendible to a graded differential
\ast
–algebra morphism

\Delta_{\Omega^{\bullet}(P)}:\Omega^{\bullet}(P)\longrightarrow\Omega^{\bullet}(P)\otimes\Gamma^{\wedge}.

Here we have considered that
\otimes
is the tensor product of graded differential
\ast
–algebras.

Notice that if
\Delta_{\Omega^{\bullet}(P)}
exists, then it is unique because all our graded differential
\ast
–algebras are generated by their degree
0
elements. Furthermore,
\Delta_{\Omega^{\bullet}(P)}
is a graded differential
\Gamma^{\wedge}
–corepresentation on
\Omega^{\bullet}(P)

[
5
]
. In this way, the space of horizontal forms is defined as

(40)

\mathrm{Hor}^{\bullet}P\,:=\{\varphi\in\Omega^{\bullet}(P)\mid\Delta_{\Omega^{\bullet}(P)}(\varphi)\,\in\,\Omega^{\bullet}(P)\otimes H\},

and it is a graded
\ast
–subalgebra of
\Omega^{\bullet}(P)

[
27
]
. Since
\Delta_{\Omega^{\bullet}(P)}(\mathrm{Hor}^{\bullet}P)\subseteq\mathrm{Hor}^{\bullet}P\otimes H,
the map

(41)

\Delta_{\mathrm{Hor}}:=\Delta_{\Omega^{\bullet}(P)}|_{\mathrm{Hor}^{\bullet}P}:\mathrm{Hor}^{\bullet}P\longrightarrow\mathrm{Hor}^{\bullet}P\otimes H

is a
\mathcal{G}
–corepresentation on
\mathrm{Hor}^{\bullet}P
. Also, one can define the space of
base
forms (
quantum differential forms of
B
) as

(42)

\Omega^{\bullet}(B):=\{\mu\in\Omega^{\bullet}(P)\mid\Delta_{\Omega^{\bullet}(P)}(\mu)=\mu\otimes\mathbbm{1}\}.

The space of base forms is a graded differential
\ast
–subalgebra of
(\Omega^{\bullet}(P),d,\ast)
. In general, it is not generated by
\Omega^{0}(B)=B
. An explicit example of this fact can be found in
[
29
]
.

It is worth mentioning that we can define the graded differential

\ast
–algebra of vertical forms using the graded vector space

P\otimes\mathfrak{qg}^{\#\,\wedge}
. However, for the purposes of this paper,
it is unnecessary to cover this aspect of the theory. Interested readers are
encouraged to consult
[
5
,
27
]
.

By
dualizing
the notion of principal connections in differential geometry (
[
9
]
), we introduce the concept of quantum principal connection (abbreviated “qpc”) on a quantum principal
\mathcal{G}
–bundle
\zeta
with a differential calculus. It is defined as a linear map

(43)

\omega:\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}\longrightarrow\Omega^{1}(P)

satisfying

(44)

\Delta_{\Omega^{\bullet}(P)}(\omega(\theta))=(\omega\otimes\mathrm{id}_{H})\mathrm{ad}(\theta)+\mathbbm{1}\otimes\theta

for all
\theta

\in

\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}
, where
\mathrm{ad}
is the
\mathcal{G}
–corepresentation given in equation (
18
).

In analogy with the
classical
case, it can be proved that the set

(45)

\mathfrak{qpc}(\zeta):=\{\omega:\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}\longrightarrow\Omega^{1}(P)\mid\omega\mbox{ is a qpc on }\zeta\}

is not empty for any qpb
\zeta
and any differential calculus (
[
5
]
), and it is an affine space modeled by the vector space of
connection displacements
(
[
27
]
)

(46)

\overrightarrow{\mathfrak{qpc}(\zeta)}:=\textsc{Mor}^{1}(\mathrm{ad},\Delta_{\mathrm{Hor}}),

where

\textsc{Mor}^{1}(\mathrm{ad},\Delta_{\mathrm{Hor}})=\{\lambda:\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Hor}^{1}P\mid\lambda\mbox{ is linear and }(\lambda\otimes\mathrm{id}_{H})\circ\mathrm{ad}=\Delta_{\mathrm{Hor}}\circ\lambda\}.

There is a canonical involution on
\mathfrak{qpc}(\zeta)
given by

(47)

\widehat{\omega}:=\ast\circ\omega\circ\ast,

and we define the dual qpc of
\omega
as
\widehat{\omega}
. A qpc
\omega
is real if

(48)

\widehat{\omega}=\omega

and we say that it is imaginary if
\widehat{\omega}=-\omega
. It is worth mentioning that in order to embrace a more general theory, our definition of qpc’s is different that the standard one in the literature, for example in
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
27
]
, because in these papers qpc’s are always real.

A qpc is called
regular
if for all
\varphi

\in

\mathrm{Hor}^{k}P
and
\theta

\in

\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}
, we have

(49)

\omega(\theta)\,\varphi=(-1)^{k}\varphi^{(0)}\omega(\theta\circ\varphi^{(1)}),

where
\Delta_{\mathrm{Hor}}(\varphi)=\varphi^{(0)}\otimes\varphi^{(1)}.
A qpc
\omega
is called
multiplicative
if

(50)

\omega(\pi(g^{(1)}))\omega(\pi(g^{(2)}))=0

for all
g

\in

\mathcal{R}
.

It is worth noting that every qpc that comes from the
dualization
of a
classical
principal connection is real, regular and multiplicative.

By
dualizing
the notion of the covariant derivative of a principal connection in differential geometry (
[
9
]
), the covariant derivative of a qpc is defined as the projection of
d
onto the space of horizontal
forms. Notice that for all
\varphi

\in

\mathrm{Hor}^{k}P
, the difference
d\varphi-(-1)^{k}\varphi^{(0)}\omega(\pi(\varphi^{(1)}))
lies in
\mathrm{Hor}^{k+1}P
. In this way, for a given qpc
\omega
, we define its
covariant derivative
as the first–order linear map

(51)

D^{\omega}:\mathrm{Hor}^{\bullet}P\longrightarrow\mathrm{Hor}^{\bullet}P

such that for every
\varphi

\in

\mathrm{Hor}^{k}P

D^{\omega}(\varphi)=d\varphi-(-1)^{k}\varphi^{(0)}\omega(\pi(\varphi^{(1)})).

On the other hand, the first–order linear map

(52)

\widehat{D}^{\omega}:=\ast\circ D^{\omega}\circ\ast:\mathrm{Hor}^{\bullet}P\longrightarrow\mathrm{Hor}^{\bullet}P

is called the
dual covariant derivative
of
\omega
. Explicitly, we have

\widehat{D}^{\omega}(\varphi)=d\varphi+\widehat{\omega}(\pi(S^{-1}(\varphi^{(1)})))\varphi^{(0)}

for every
\varphi

\in

\mathrm{Hor}^{k}P
. It should be clear that, in general,
\widehat{D}^{\omega}\not=D^{\widehat{\omega}}
. In addition, we have

(53)

D^{\omega}\not=\widehat{D}^{\omega}.

In fact,

(54)

\widehat{D}^{\omega}(\varphi)=D^{\omega}(\varphi)+\ell^{\widehat{\omega}}(\pi(S^{-1}(\varphi^{(1)})),\varphi^{(0)})+(-1)^{k}\varphi^{(0)}(\omega-\widehat{\omega})(\pi(\varphi^{(1)})),

where

\displaystyle\ell^{\omega}:\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}\times\mathrm{Hor}^{\bullet}P

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathrm{Hor}^{\bullet}P

\displaystyle(\theta\;,\;\varphi)

\displaystyle\longmapsto\omega(\theta)\varphi-(-1)^{k}\varphi^{(0)}\omega(\theta\circ\varphi^{(1)}).

The map
\ell^{\omega}

measures the degree of non–regularity
of
\omega
, in the sense of
\ell^{\omega}=0
if and only if
\omega
is regular. In this way, for real and regular qpc’s we obtain
D^{\omega}=\widehat{D}^{\omega}=D^{\widehat{\omega}}
, which is the situation for qpc’s arising from
classical
principal connections. In other words,
D^{\omega}
and
\widehat{D}^{\omega}
are two different horizontal operators that generalize the covariant derivative of a principal connection in differential geometry. In the next section, we will work with both operators.

Direct calculations prove that (
[
5
,
6
]
)

(55)

D^{\omega},\,\widehat{D}^{\omega}\,\in\,\textsc{Mor}(\Delta_{\mathrm{Hor}},\Delta_{\mathrm{Hor}})\,,\quad D^{\omega}|_{\Omega^{\bullet}(B)}=\widehat{D}^{\omega}|_{\Omega^{\bullet}(B)}=d|_{\Omega^{\bullet}(B)}

and

(56)

D^{\omega}(\varphi\psi)=D^{\omega}(\varphi)\psi+(-1)^{k}\varphi D^{\omega}(\psi)+(-1)^{k}\varphi^{(0)}\ell^{\omega}(\pi(\varphi^{(1)}),\psi),

(57)

\widehat{D}^{\omega}(\varphi\psi)=\widehat{D}^{\omega}(\varphi)\psi+(-1)^{k}\varphi\widehat{D}^{\omega}(\psi)+\ell^{\widehat{\omega}}(\pi(S^{-1}(\psi^{(1)}))\circ S^{-1}(\varphi^{(1)}),\varphi^{(0)})\psi^{(0)},

for all
\varphi

\in

\mathrm{Hor}^{k}P
,
\psi

\in

\mathrm{Hor}^{\bullet}P
. Notice that
\omega
is regular if and only if
D^{\omega}
and
\widehat{D}^{\omega}
satisfy the graded Leibniz rule; this is the main reason to study regular qpc’s
[
5
]
. Moreover, for real qpc’s

(58)

D^{\omega}(\psi)^{\ast}=D^{\omega}(\psi^{\ast})+\ell^{\omega}(\pi(S(\psi^{(1)})^{\ast}),\psi^{(0)\ast})=\widehat{D}^{\omega}(\psi^{\ast})

Let us define the map
r^{\omega}
given by

(59)

\displaystyle r^{\omega}:H

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathrm{Hor}^{2}P

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle\longmapsto d\omega(\pi(g))+\omega(\pi(g^{(1)}))\omega(\pi(g^{(2)})).

Then we have

D^{\omega\,2}(\varphi)=-\varphi^{(0)}r^{\omega}(\varphi^{(1)})\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\widehat{D}^{\omega\,2}(\varphi)=-r^{\omega}(S^{-1}(\varphi^{(1)}))\varphi^{(0)}.

Let
\pi:P\longrightarrow B
be a
classical
principal
G
–bundle (
P
is the total space,
B
is the base space and
\pi
is the bundle projection) with a principal connection
\omega
. Then the curvature
R^{\omega}
of
\omega
is defined as the
\mathfrak{g}
–valued differential
2
–form of
P
given by
[
9
]

(60)

R^{\omega}=d\omega+{1\over 2}[\omega\wedge\omega],\quad R^{\omega}_{x}(X_{x},Y_{x})=d\omega_{x}(X_{x},Y_{x})+[\omega(X_{x}),\omega(Y_{x})]_{\mathfrak{g}},

where
[-,-]_{\mathfrak{g}}
denotes the Lie bracket of
\mathfrak{g}
, the Lie algebra of
G
, and
X_{x}
,
Y_{x}

\in

T_{x}P
,
x

\in

P
. In addition, the square of the covariant derivative is related to
R^{\omega}

[
9
]
. In this way, one could take equation (
59
) as the definition of the curvature for a qpc.

However, in differential geometry, the curvature is
\mathfrak{g}
–valued, so it is natural to expect that in the
non–commutative geometrical
setting the curvature be defined on
\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}
, as the
dualization
of the
classical
case indicates. Moreover, in differential geometry the curvature is a basic form of type
\mathrm{ad}

[
9
]
; therefore, for qpc’s, the curvature must be
an element of
\textsc{Mor}^{2}(\mathrm{ad},\Delta_{\mathrm{Hor}})=\{\lambda:\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}\longrightarrow\mathrm{Hor}^{2}P\mid\lambda\mbox{ is linear and }(\lambda\otimes\mathrm{id}_{H})\circ\mathrm{ad}=\Delta_{\mathrm{Hor}}\circ\lambda\}
.

Of course,
r^{\omega}
is well–defined on
\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}=\mathrm{Ker}(\epsilon)/\mathcal{R}
if and only if
r^{\omega}(\mathcal{R})=0
, and this relation holds if and only if
\omega
is multiplicative. This is problematic since, in general, there exist qpc’s that are not multiplicative
[
5
]
.

To overcome this problem, we define an embedded differential as a linear map

\Theta:\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}\longrightarrow\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}\otimes\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}

such that

(1)

\mathrm{ad}^{\otimes 2}\circ\Theta=(\Theta\otimes\mathrm{id}_{H})\circ\mathrm{ad},
where
\mathrm{ad}^{\otimes 2}:=M\circ(\mathrm{ad}\otimes\mathrm{ad})
, with

M:\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}\otimes H\otimes\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}\otimes H\longrightarrow\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}\otimes\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}\otimes H

given by
M(\theta_{1}\otimes g_{1},\theta_{2}\otimes g_{2})=\theta_{1}\otimes\theta_{2}\otimes g_{1}g_{2}
.

(2)

If
\Theta(\theta)=\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}\theta_{i}\otimes\theta_{j}
, then
d\theta=\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}\theta_{i}\theta_{j}
and
\Theta(\theta^{\ast})=-\displaystyle\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}\theta^{\ast}_{j}\otimes\theta^{\ast}_{i}

In general, an embedded differential can be constructed by choosing a
\ast
–
S
–invariant
\mathrm{ad}
–invariant complement
L\subset\mathrm{Ker}(\epsilon)
of
\mathcal{R}
and taking
\Theta=-(\pi\otimes\pi)\circ\Delta\circ\pi^{-1}|_{L}.
Notice that choosing
\Theta
is choosing a compatible way (with respect to the differential structure) to embed
\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}
into
\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}\otimes\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}
. Fix any such embedded differential
\Theta
. We define
the
curvature
of a qpc
\omega
as the linear map

(61)

d^{\widehat{\nabla}^{\omega}_{V}}=\widehat{\Upsilon}_{V}\circ\widehat{D}^{\omega}\circ\widehat{\Upsilon}^{-1}_{V}.

Since for any
\delta^{V}
,
\delta^{W}

\in

\mathcal{T}

\textsc{Mor}(\delta^{V}\oplus\delta^{W},\Delta_{\mathrm{Hor}})=\textsc{Mor}(\delta^{V},\Delta_{\mathrm{Hor}})\oplus\textsc{Mor}(\delta^{W},\Delta_{\mathrm{Hor}}),
and every finite-dimensional
\mathcal{G}
–corepresentation is a direct sum of a finite number of elements of
\mathcal{T}
, all these constructions extend naturally to any
\delta^{V}

\in

\textsc{Obj}(\mathbf{Rep}_{\mathcal{G}})
. It worth remarking that our formulation holds for every qpc
\omega
: it is not necessary to impose any condition on
\omega
(as
reality
or
regularity
) to define
\nabla^{\omega}_{V}
and
\widehat{\nabla}^{\omega}_{V}
.

The theory of connections on left/right quantum vector bundles, finitely generated projective left/right modules according to the Serre–Swan theorem, has been studied over many years, for example in
[
32
]
, and we will follow this line of research. In particular, in
[
32
]
a type of Bianchi identity is proved that all connections satisfy, but only when
B
is commutative.

In the
classical
case, given a principal
G
–bundle
\pi:P\longrightarrow B
and
\alpha
a linear representation of
G
on
V
, there is a canonical isomorphism between associated vector bundle–valued differential forms of
B
and basic differential forms of
P
of type
\alpha

[
9
]
. Moreover, this isomorphism allows to define the exterior derivative of the induced linear connection in terms of the covariant derivative of a principal connection
[
9
]
. The definitions of
E^{V}_{\mathrm{L}}
,
E^{V}_{\mathrm{R}}
, the fact that
\Upsilon_{V}
and
\widehat{\Upsilon}_{V}
are isomorphisms, and equations (

) and (

) are all
non–commutative geometrical
counterparts of these results in differential geometry.

In accordance with
[
31
]
,
P\,\square^{H}\,V^{\#}\cong E^{V}_{\mathrm{L}}
(for the natural left coaction on
V^{\#}
, the dual space of
V
). This construction is the commonly accepted one for associated qvb’s. Nevertheless, we have chosen to work with
E^{V}_{\mathrm{L}}
and
E^{V}_{\mathrm{R}}
because, in this way, the definitions of
\nabla^{\omega}_{V}
and
\widehat{\nabla}^{\omega}_{V}
(and their exterior covariant derivatives) become completely analogous to their
classical
counterparts (in differential geometry, both connections coincide). Furthermore, they are easier to work with, as they permit explicit calculations, as the reader will verify in the remainder of this paper. In addition, by using intertwining maps, the definition of the canonical Hermitian structure appears more natural.

3.2.
The Canonical Hermitian Structure

One of the purposes of this paper is to introduce a Hermitian structure on associated qvb’s compatible with induced qlc’s, and showing some of the properties of this structure. The main result will be Theorem
3.10
.

Definition 3.2

(Hermitian structures)
.

Let
(B,\cdot,\mathbbm{1},\ast)
be a quantum space and let
M
be a left quantum vector bundle on
B
. A Hermitian structure on
M
is defined as a
B
–valued sesquilinear map (antilinear in the second coordinate)

\langle-,-\rangle:M\times M\longrightarrow B

such that for all
x_{1}
,
x_{2}

\in

M

(1)

\langle x_{1},b\,x_{2}\rangle=\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle\,b^{\ast}
;

(2)

\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle^{\ast}=\langle x_{2},x_{1}\rangle
;

(3)

\langle x_{1},x_{1}\rangle

\in

B^{+}
, where
B^{+}
is the pointed convex cone generated by elements of the form
\{bb^{\ast}\}
.

In the same way, if
M
is a right quantum vector bundle on
B
, a Hermitian structure on
M
is a
B
–valued sesquilinear map (antilinear in the first coordinate)

\langle-,-\rangle:M\times M\longrightarrow B

such that for all
x_{1}
,
x_{2}

\in

M

(1)

\langle b\,x_{1},x_{2}\rangle=b^{\ast}\,\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle
;

(2)

\langle x_{1},x_{2}\rangle^{\ast}=\langle x_{2},x_{1}\rangle
;

(3)

\langle x_{1},x_{1}\rangle

\in

B^{+}
, where
B^{+}
is the pointed convex cone generated by elements of the form
\{b^{\ast}b\}
.

For example, let us take the free left
B
–module
B^{d_{V}}
(by the Serre–Swan theorem, it can be considered as a trivial left qvb). There is a canonical Hermitian structure on
B^{d_{V}}
defined by

(84)

\langle-,-\rangle^{d_{V}}_{\mathrm{L}}:B^{d_{V}}\times B^{d_{V}}\longrightarrow B,\qquad(\overline{b}\,,\,\overline{a})\longmapsto\sum^{d_{V}}_{i=1}b_{i}\,a^{\ast}_{i}

where
\overline{b}=(b_{1},...,b_{d_{V}})
and
\overline{a}=(a_{1},...,a_{d_{V}})
. It is worth mentioning that
\langle-,-\rangle^{d_{V}}_{\mathrm{L}}
is non–degenerate, i.e., there is a Riesz representation theorem in terms of left
B
–modules
[
32
]
. Similarly, there is a canonical Hermitian structure on
B^{d_{V}}
as a free right
B
–module (by the Serre–Swan theorem, it can be considered as a trivial right qvb) given by

(85)

\langle-,-\rangle^{d_{V}}_{\mathrm{R}}:B^{d_{V}}\times B^{d_{V}}\longrightarrow B,\qquad(\overline{b}\,,\,\overline{a})\longmapsto\sum^{d_{V}}_{i=1}b^{\ast}_{i}\,a_{i}

and it is non–degenerate, i.e., there is a Riesz representation theorem in terms of right
B
–modules
[
32
]
.

Let
\delta^{V}

\in

\mathcal{T}
and let

be a quantum principal

Proposition 3.3
.

(B^{d_{V}},\langle-,-\rangle^{d_{V}}_{\mathrm{L}})

Proof.

According to

on

provided that the matrix

is idempotent and self–adjoint (with the conjugation

is idempotent, and clearly

\langle-,-\rangle^{d_{V}}_{\mathrm{L}}\Big|_{B^{d_{V}}\cdot\varrho^{V}(\mathbbm{1})\times B^{d_{V}}\cdot\varrho^{V}(\mathbbm{1})}:B^{d_{V}}\cdot\varrho^{V}(\mathbbm{1})\times B^{d_{V}}\cdot\varrho^{V}(\mathbbm{1})\longrightarrow B

is non–degenerate. Finally, considering the isomorphism

\langle-,-\rangle_{\mathrm{L}}:E^{V}_{\mathrm{L}}\times E^{V}_{\mathrm{L}}\longrightarrow B

on

.
∎

Explicitly, by equation (

\widetilde{H}^{-1}(T)=\overline{b}^{{}_{T}}\cdot\varrho^{V}(\mathbbm{1})
with
\overline{b}^{{}_{T}}=(b^{{}_{T}}_{1},...,b^{{}_{T}}_{d_{V}})

\in

B^{d_{V}}

\langle T_{1},T_{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{L}}=\langle\overline{b}^{{}_{T_{1}}},\overline{b}^{{}_{T_{2}}}\rangle^{d_{V}}_{\mathrm{L}}=\sum^{d_{V}}_{k=1}b^{{}_{T_{1}}}_{k}\,b^{{}_{T_{2}}\ast}_{k}=\sum^{n_{V}}_{i=1}T_{1}(e_{i})T_{2}(e_{i})^{\ast}.

Furthermore, for all
b

\in

B
we have

(87)

\langle T_{1}\,b,T_{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{L}}=\langle T_{1},T_{2}\,b^{\ast}\rangle_{\mathrm{L}}.

It is worth mentioning that

does not depend on the orthonormal basis
\{e_{k}\}^{n_{V}}_{k=1}
used.

Let
\delta^{V}

\in

\textsc{Obj}(\mathbf{Rep}_{\mathcal{G}})
. Then there exists
\delta^{V_{i}}

\in

\mathcal{T}
such that
\delta^{V}\,\cong\,\bigoplus^{m}_{i=1}\delta^{V_{i}}.

[

]

(88)

A_{f}:\bigoplus^{m}_{i=1}E^{V_{i}}_{\mathrm{L}}\longrightarrow E^{V}_{\mathrm{L}},\qquad T\longmapsto T\circ f

\langle-,-\rangle_{\mathrm{L}}:E^{V}_{\mathrm{L}}\times E^{V}_{\mathrm{L}}\longrightarrow B,\qquad(T_{1}\,,\,T_{2})\longmapsto\sum(T_{1}\circ f^{-1})(v_{k})\,(T_{2}\circ f^{-1})(v_{k})^{\ast},

with

\in

Definition 3.4

(Canonical Hermitian structure)
.

For every
\delta^{V}

\in

\textsc{Obj}(\mathbf{Rep}_{\mathcal{G}})

\langle-,-\rangle_{\mathrm{L}}:E^{V}_{\mathrm{L}}\times E^{V}_{\mathrm{L}}\longrightarrow B,\qquad(T_{1}\,,\,T_{2})\longmapsto\sum^{n_{V}}_{k=1}T_{1}(e_{k})T_{2}(e_{k})^{\ast},

where
\{e_{i}\}^{n_{V}}_{i=1}
is any orthonormal basis of
V
.

It is worth mentioning that, despite the presence of the word

in its name,
\langle-,-\rangle_{\mathrm{L}}
depends on the inner product
\langle-|-\rangle
of
V
for which
\delta^{V}
is unitary, as in the

Let
\delta^{V}_{1}
,
\delta^{V}_{2}

\in

\mathcal{T}
. Take the canonical left
B

A_{V_{1},V_{2}}:E^{V_{1}}_{\mathrm{L}}\otimes_{B}E^{V_{2}}_{\mathrm{L}}\longrightarrow E^{V_{1}\otimes V_{2}}_{\mathrm{L}}

where

A_{V_{1},V_{2}}(T_{1}\otimes_{B}T_{2})(v_{1}\otimes v_{2})=T_{1}(v_{1})T_{2}(v_{2})

[

. The proof of the following proposition is a straightforward calculation.

Proposition 3.5
.

By considering the tensor product Hermitian structure on

\langle T_{1}\otimes_{B}T_{2},U_{1}\otimes_{B}U_{2}\rangle^{\otimes}_{\mathrm{L}}=\langle T_{1}\langle T_{2},U_{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{L}},U_{1}\rangle_{\mathrm{L}},
the map
A_{V_{1},V_{2}}
is an isometry.

Since

.

If

and

.

For every

\in

It is enough to prove the theorem for
\delta^{V}

\in

\mathcal{T}

\mathrm{End}(B^{d_{V}})=M_{d_{V}}(B)
and that every element of

\in

Let

, where
\overline{b}^{i}=(b^{i}_{1},...,b^{i}_{d_{V}})

\in

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
(\zeta,\omega)
be a qpb with a real qpc (which always exists
[

]
) and
\delta^{V}

\in

\textsc{Obj}(\mathbf{Rep}_{\mathcal{G}})

Proof.

Let
\delta^{V}

\in

\mathcal{T}
. According to equation (
70
), every
T

\in

E^{V}_{\mathrm{L}}
can be expressed as

T=\sum^{d_{V}}_{k=1}b^{{}_{T}}_{k}\,T^{\mathrm{L}}_{k}\quad\mbox{ with }\quad b^{{}_{T}}_{k}=\sum^{n_{V}}_{i=1}T(e_{i})\,x^{V\,\ast}_{ki}.

Moreover, by equations (
73
), (
74
) we get

\nabla^{\omega}_{V}(T)=\sum^{d_{V}}_{k=1}\mu^{D^{\omega}\circ T}_{k}\otimes_{B}T^{\mathrm{L}}_{k}\qquad\mbox{ with }\qquad\mu^{D^{\omega}\circ T}_{k}=\sum^{n_{V}}_{i=1}D^{\omega}(T(e_{i}))x^{V\ast}_{ki}.

Then, by equation (
64
), for all
T_{1}
,
T_{2}

\in

E^{V}_{\mathrm{L}}
we have

\displaystyle\langle\nabla^{\omega}_{V}(T_{1}),T_{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{L}}+\langle T_{1},\nabla^{\omega}_{V}(T_{2})\rangle_{\mathrm{L}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{d_{V},n_{V}}_{k,i=1}\mu^{D^{\omega}\circ T_{1}}_{k}\,T^{\mathrm{L}}_{k}(e_{i})\,T_{2}(e_{i})^{\ast}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle T_{1}(e_{i})\,T^{\mathrm{L}}_{k}(e_{i})^{\ast}\,(\mu^{D^{\omega}\circ T_{2}}_{k})^{\ast}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{d_{V},n_{V}}_{k,i,j=1}D^{\omega}(T_{1}(e_{j}))\,x^{V\,\ast}_{kj}x^{V}_{ki}\,T_{2}(e_{i})^{\ast}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle T_{1}(e_{i})\,x^{V\,\ast}_{ki}x^{V}_{kj}\,D^{\omega}(T_{2}(e_{j}))^{\ast}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{n_{V}}_{i=1}D^{\omega}(T_{1}(e_{i}))\,T_{2}(e_{i})^{\ast}+T_{1}(e_{i})\,D^{\omega}(T_{2}(e_{i}))^{\ast}.

Notice that for every
T

\in

E^{V}_{\mathrm{L}}

\Delta_{P}(T(e_{i}))=\displaystyle\sum^{n_{V}}_{j=1}T(e_{j})\otimes g^{V}_{ji},

where the elements
g^{V}_{ji}

\in

H
are given in Theorem
2.1
. Since
\omega
is real, in accordance with equation (
58
) and using the fact that
S(g^{V}_{ji})=g^{V\ast}_{ij}
(
[
4
]
), we obtain

D^{\omega}(T_{2}(e_{i}))^{\ast}=D^{\omega}(T_{2}(e_{i})^{\ast})+\sum^{n_{V}}_{j=1}\ell^{\omega}(\pi(S(g^{V}_{ji})^{\ast}),T_{2}(e_{j})^{\ast})=D^{\omega}(T_{2}(e_{i})^{\ast})+\sum^{n_{V}}_{j=1}\ell^{\omega}(\pi(g^{V}_{ij}),T_{2}(e_{j})^{\ast}).

Thus

\displaystyle\sum^{n_{V}}_{i=1}D^{\omega}(T_{1}(e_{i}))\,T_{2}(e_{i})^{\ast}+T_{1}(e_{i})\,D^{\omega}(T_{2}(e_{i}))^{\ast}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{n_{V}}_{i=1}D^{\omega}(T_{1}(e_{i}))\,T_{2}(e_{i})^{\ast}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle T_{1}(e_{i})D^{\omega}(T_{2}(e_{i})^{\ast})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum^{n_{V}}_{i,j=1}T_{1}(e_{i})\ell^{\omega}(\pi(g^{V}_{ij}),T_{2}(e_{j})^{\ast}).

Now according to equation (
56
) we have

\displaystyle D^{\omega}(T_{1}(e_{i})\,T_{2}(e_{i})^{\ast})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D^{\omega}(T_{1}(e_{i}))\,T_{2}(e_{i})^{\ast}+T_{1}(e_{i})\,D^{\omega}(T_{2}(e_{i})^{\ast})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum^{n_{V}}_{j=1}T_{1}(e_{j})\,\ell^{\omega}(\pi(g^{V}_{ji}),T_{2}(e_{i})^{\ast});

which implies

\displaystyle\sum^{n_{V}}_{i=1}D^{\omega}(T_{1}(e_{i}))\,T_{2}(e_{i})^{\ast}\;+\;T_{1}(e_{i})D^{\omega}(T_{2}(e_{i})^{\ast})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\sum^{n_{V}}_{i,j=1}T_{1}(e_{i})\ell^{\omega}(\pi(g^{V}_{ij}),T_{2}(e_{j})^{\ast})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{n_{V}}_{i=1}D^{\omega}(T_{1}(e_{i})\,T_{2}(e_{i})^{\ast})

\displaystyle-

4.

4.2.
Action on Quantum Connections

In differential geometry, one of the central ideas of gauge theory is to study classes of objects transformable from one to another by gauge transformations. Probably, one of the most important examples of this arises when the gauge group acts on the set of principal connections, since this reverberates in an action on associated connections. The purpose of this subsection is to develop the
non–commutative geometrical
counterpart of these actions.

The proof of the following theorem is straightforward. However, since this theorem introduces an action of

(see equation (

Proof.

It is enough to prove the theorem for
\delta^{V}

\in

\mathcal{T}
. Let us start noticing that by equation (

and also the map

\mathbf{A}_{\mathfrak{F}}:E^{V}_{\mathrm{L}}\longrightarrow E^{V}_{\mathrm{L}},\qquad T\longmapsto\mathfrak{F}\circ T

is well–defined. Furthermore, it is a
B
–bimodule isomorphism, and its inverse is given by

. In this way, for all

\in

we obtain

\displaystyle(\nabla^{\mathfrak{F}^{\circledast}\omega}_{\,V}\circ\mathbf{A}_{\mathfrak{F}})(T)\;=\;\nabla^{\mathfrak{F}^{\circledast}\omega}_{\,V}(\mathfrak{F}\circ T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{d_{V}}_{k=1}\mu^{D^{\mathfrak{F}^{\circledast}\omega}\circ\mathfrak{F}\circ T}\otimes_{B}T^{\mathrm{L}}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{d_{V}}_{k=1}\mu^{\mathfrak{F}\circ D^{\omega}\circ T}\otimes_{B}T^{\mathrm{L}}_{k};

so
(\Upsilon^{-1}_{V}\circ\nabla^{\mathfrak{F}^{\circledast}\omega}_{\,V}\circ\mathbf{A}_{\mathfrak{F}})(T)=\mathfrak{F}\circ D^{\omega}\circ T.
On the other hand,

\displaystyle((\mathrm{id}_{\Omega^{\bullet}(B)}\otimes_{B}\mathbf{A}_{\mathfrak{F}})\circ\nabla^{\omega}_{V})(T)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{d_{V}}_{k=1}(\mathrm{id}_{\Omega^{\bullet}(B)}\otimes_{B}\mathbf{A}_{\mathfrak{F}})(\mu^{D^{\omega}\circ T}\otimes_{B}T^{\mathrm{L}}_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum^{d_{V}}_{k=1}\mu^{D^{\omega}\circ T}\otimes_{B}\mathbf{A}_{\mathfrak{F}}(T^{\mathrm{L}}_{k})\;=\;\sum^{d_{V}}_{k=1}\mu^{D^{\omega}\circ T}\otimes_{B}\mathfrak{F}\circ T^{\mathrm{L}}_{k};

By using the fact that

is bijective, we conclude that
\mathbf{A}_{\mathfrak{F}}
satisfies the first part of the statement.

Let us take

\in

\Omega^{\bullet}(B)\otimes_{B}E^{V}_{\mathrm{L}}

we get

(\mathbf{A}_{\mathfrak{F}}\otimes_{B}\mathrm{id}_{\Omega^{\bullet}(B)})\sigma_{V}(\psi)=\displaystyle\sum_{k}\mathbf{A}_{\mathfrak{F}}(T^{\mathrm{R}}_{k})\otimes_{B}\widetilde{\mu}_{k}=\sum_{k}\mathfrak{F}\circ T^{\mathrm{R}}_{k}\otimes_{B}\widetilde{\mu}_{k},

\widehat{\Upsilon}^{-1}_{V}(\mathbf{A}_{\mathfrak{F}}\otimes_{B}\mathrm{id}_{\Omega^{\bullet}(B)})\sigma_{V}(\psi)=(\mathfrak{F}\circ\Upsilon^{-1}_{V})(\psi)

\sigma_{V}(\mathrm{id}_{\Omega^{\bullet}(B)}\otimes_{B}\mathbf{A}_{\mathfrak{F}})(\psi)=\displaystyle\sum_{k}T^{\mathrm{R}}_{k}\otimes_{B}\mu^{\prime}_{k}

if

because of
(\Upsilon^{-1}_{V}\circ(\mathrm{id}_{\Omega^{\bullet}(B)}\otimes_{B}\mathbf{A}_{\mathfrak{F}}))(\psi)=(\mathfrak{F}\circ\Upsilon^{-1}_{V})(\psi)

\widehat{\Upsilon}^{-1}_{V}\sigma_{V}(\mathrm{id}_{\Omega^{\bullet}(B)}\otimes_{B}\mathbf{A}_{\mathfrak{F}})(\psi)=(\mathfrak{F}\circ\Upsilon^{-1}_{V})(\psi)

Corollary 4.12
.

The following formula holds:

\nabla^{\mathfrak{F}^{\circledast}\omega}_{V}=(\mathrm{id}_{\Omega^{\bullet}(B)}\otimes_{B}\mathbf{A}_{\mathfrak{F}})\circ\nabla^{\omega}_{V}\circ\mathbf{A}^{-1}_{\mathfrak{F}}=(\mathrm{id}_{\Omega^{\bullet}(B)}\otimes_{B}\mathbf{A}_{\mathfrak{F}})\circ\nabla^{\omega}_{V}\circ\mathbf{A}_{\mathfrak{F}^{-1}}.

\mathbf{A}_{\mathfrak{F}}

\in

Proof.

\in

\mathcal{T}

6.
Concluding Comments

This paper extends the work presented in
[
13
]
by considering general qpc’s rather than only the real and regular ones, and by defining additional geometrical structures, which we will review.

First of all, we would like to highlight the importance of the universal differential envelope
\ast
–calculus
(\Gamma^{\wedge},d,\ast)
as quantum differential forms on
\mathcal{G}
. This space is one of the principal differences between Durdevich’s formulation and other formulations as in
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
, and it not only allows us to extend the
\ast
–Hopf algebra structure of
H^{\infty}
to
\Gamma^{\wedge\,\infty}
, but it is also maximal with this property
[
4
]
. Moreover,
(\Gamma^{\wedge},d,\ast)
allows us to define the quantum translation map at the level of differential calculus, which in turn leads to Proposition
4.2
. It is worth remembering that not all conditions in
H^{\infty}
can be extended to
\Gamma^{\wedge\,\infty}
(for example, see Corollary
4.6
). This opens the door to an exciting research project in which one could explore ways to generalize those properties that do not naturally extend, so that they encompass
\Gamma^{\wedge\,\infty}
in each situation.

Since
\textsc{Mor}(\delta^{V},\Delta_{P})
is a
B
–bimodule in a natural way, we decided to deal with the left and right structures, and Durdevich’s theory allows us to develop the theory for the left/right associated qvb’s. In
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
one can appreciate more explicitly the importance of taking into account both associated qvb’s and their induced qlc’s. For smooth compact manifolds, both associated qvb’s are the same, and since every qpc that comes from the
dualization
of a
classical
principal connection is regular and real, both induced qlc’s are the same.

It is worth mentioning that there are other papers dealing with Hermitian structures on quantum spaces, for example
[
38
]
, in which the author presented a notion of spin geometry on quantum groups. In
[
38
]
, quantum differential forms of
\mathcal{G}
are given by the braided
exterior calculus (
[
8
]
) instead of the universal differential envelope
\ast
–calculus that we used. Nevertheless, there is a surjectived morphism between these two spaces (
[
5
]
), and with that one could try to integrate both ideas in order to develop a theory for spinor quantum bundles.

We want to emphasize Theorems
3.7
,
3.9
. These theorems show a link between associated qvb’s and the well–known theory of Hilbert
C^{\ast}
–modules by the canonical Hermitian structure. The main two reasons to use the word
canonical
in Definitions
3.4
,
3.8
are the facts that at the end, these
B
–valued inner products do not depend on the choice of the generators
\{T^{\mathrm{L}}_{k}\}
and the result presented in Theorem
3.10
. As we have mentioned at the end of Section
3
, this theorem is the core of this paper since it recreates an important
classical
result in the most general framework of non–commutative geometry, which will allow to define formally adjoint operators of
d^{\nabla^{\omega}_{V}}
,
d^{\widehat{\nabla}^{\omega}_{V}}
and with that we will able to define Laplacians for associated qvb’s and study field theory on them, like the reader can check in
[
17
]
.

Now let focus on the quantum gauge group. As we have mentioned before, Definition
4.3
is the one presented in
[
25
]
but at the level of differential calculus, and of course, this definition does not recreate the
classical
case: it is to large.

In Durdevich’s theory there have been some attempts to get a definition of the quantum gauge group, for example in
[
34
,
39
]
. To accomplish the purpose of this paper, the definition of
\mathfrak{qGG}
presented in
[
34
]
is not useful because it does not create an action on the space of qpc’s. On the other hand, the formulation showed in
[
39
]
is only for the special case
B:=C^{\infty}_{\mathbb{C}}(M)
, where
M
is a compact smooth manifold, and for a special graded differential
\ast
–algebra on
\mathcal{G}
: the minimal admissible calculus. This is why we decided to use Definition
4.3
, despite the fact that it does not recreate the
classical
case.

One possible option to recover the
classical
case is to define
\mathfrak{qGG}
as the group of all graded differential
\ast
–algebra isomorphisms
\mathfrak{F}:\Omega^{\bullet}(P)\longrightarrow\Omega^{\bullet}(P)
that satisfy equation (
105
). However, depending on the qpb, the quantum gauge group would not have enough elements. This is a problem, for example, when we talk about Yang–Mills theory in non–commutative geometry, since the orbit of Yang–Mills qpc’s could be trivial
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
. From a physical point of view, this implies that there could be too many non–gauge–equivalent boson fields. This issue may also be present if we define
\mathfrak{qGG}
with only one more condition, for example, by requiring that the elements of
\mathfrak{qGG}
commute with the differential of
\Omega^{\bullet}(P)
. To prevent this from happening, we have decided to define
\mathfrak{qGG}
in the most general way, use equation (
119
) for the action on qpc’s and work with
ad hoc
subgroups of
\mathfrak{qGG}
in each situation.

In Subsection
4.2
, we have mentioned that in literature, for example
[
35
]
, the commonly accepted action of the (
0
–degree) quantum gauge group on qpc’s is given by

\mathfrak{F}^{\times}\omega=\mathfrak{F}_{\mathfrak{f}}\ast\omega\ast\mathfrak{F}_{\mathfrak{f}^{-1}}+\mathfrak{F}_{\mathfrak{f}}\ast(d\circ\mathfrak{F}_{\mathfrak{f}^{-1}}),

where
\omega

is a map from

. Nevertheless, equation (

, in Durdevich’s formulation the curvature is defined from the quantum dual Lie algebra
\mathfrak{qg}^{\#}
to
\Omega^{2}(P)
as in the

of the

On the other hand, although this work has been developed in the framework of non–commutative geometry, the quantum gauge group is a
classical

\mathfrak{qGG}
as a quantum group, although there would be a coaction on the space of qpc’s instead of an actions.
