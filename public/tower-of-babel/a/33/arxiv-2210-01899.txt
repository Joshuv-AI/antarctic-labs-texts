Lamb modes and Born approximation for small shape defects inversion in elastic plates
Éric Bonnetier, Angèle Niclas, Laurent Seppecher

Abstract
The aim of this work is to present theoretical tools to study wave propagation in elastic waveguides and perform multi-frequency scattering inversion to reconstruct small shape defects in a 2D and 3D elastic plate. Given surface multi-frequency wavefield measurements, we use a Born approximation to reconstruct localized defect in the geometry of the plate. To justify this approximation, we introduce a rigorous framework to study the propagation of elastic wavefield generated by arbitrary sources. By studying the decreasing rate of the series of inhomogeneous Lamb mode, we prove the well-posedness of the PDE that model elastic wave propagation in 2D and 3D planar waveguides. We also characterize the critical frequencies for which the Lamb decomposition is not valid. Using these results, we generalize the shape reconstruction method already developed for acoustic waveguide to 2D elastic waveguides and provide a stable reconstruction method based on a mode-by-mode spacial Fourier inversion given by the scattered field.



1
Introduction

This work is devoted to the reconstruction of small shape defects in a waveguide
using multi-frequency scattering data. It is an extension of the method exposed in
[
11
]

int the case of acoustic waveguide to the case of elastic plates.
If the scalar Helmholtz case is relevant to the non destructive testing of pipes or optical fibers
(see
[
17
]
), applications in the elastic case concern the monitoring of structural parts,
airplane, ship, offshore wind energy plants or bridges for instance (see
[
34
]
).

The main common point between acoustic and elastic waveguides is the existence of a
modal decomposition of the wavefield in a sum of explicit guided modes.
The acoustic modes form an orthonormal basis, a property not satisfied by their elastic counter-parts,
called Lamb modes.
Several authors have looked into this feature. The books
[
28
,
1
]
provide analytic expressions of Lamb modes as well as dispersion relations for their wavenumbers.
In
[
22
,
25
,
26
]
a new formulation is introduced, the
\displaystyle\bm{X}/\bm{Y}
formulation,
under which the family of Lamb mode turns out to be complete
[
3
,
18
,
8
]
.
The associated bi-orthogonality relations
[
14
]
thus allow the use of the Lamb basis to decompose any wavefield that propagates in an elastic waveguide as a sum of Lamb modes.

However, a rigorous mathematical framework is still missing
to study the propagation of an elastic wavefield generated by an arbitrary source term
(see however
[
5
,
6
]
in 2D).
One main goal of the present article is to prove well-posedness of the system of PDE’s,
that models 2D or 3D planar elastic waveguides with internal and boundary source terms.
To this end, we adapt the strategy developed for acoustic waveguides in
[
11
]
,
which differs from
[
5
]
.
Under stronger assumptions on the regularity of the source terms than those in
[
5
]
,
we present in Theorem
2
a constructive proof of existence and regularity of a wavefield propagating
in a two dimensional elastic waveguide.

As it turns out, this result is not valid at some particular frequencies,
which we call critical frequencies,
and that are characterized in the proof of Theorem
2
.
In particular, we establish in Corollary
1
that the critical frequencies,
for which the Lamb family is no longer complete, coincide with the vanishing of the bi-orthogonality relation established by
[
14
]
. This result, up to our knowledge, has not been proven
before and may help understanding the mathematical analysis of elastic waveguides.

Concerning the study of wave propagation in three-dimensional plates, most of the work
that we are aware of consists in adapting the 2D framework to situations with radial or
axial symmetry (see for instance
[
19
,
28
,
2
,
33
]
). In
[
31
]
,
arbitrary source terms are considered, without mathematical justification however.
Introducing the Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition of the wavefield
[
9
]
, we split the three dimensional system of elasticity into a system of two independent equations.
One of them fits into the scalar wave framework developed in
[
11
]
,
while the other can be rewritten using the
\displaystyle\bm{X}/\bm{Y}
formulation.
This provides a full expression for the decomposition of the wavefield generated by arbitrary source terms in dimension 3, see Theorem
3
.

Equipped with these results, we can generalize the shape reconstruction method presented in
[
11
]
to the case of elastic plates, so as to determine possible defects
(bumps or dips) in the geometry of a plate, from multi-frequency measurements.
We use the very same procedure as in the acoustic case : after mapping the perturbed plate to a
straight configuration, we simplify the resulting system of equations using the Born approximation.
The scattered wavefield generated by a known incident wavefield in the original geometry,
gives rise in the straightened plate to a boundary source term, that depends on the shape defect.
Using measurements of the scattered field on the surface of the plate at different frequencies,
we can reconstruct in a stable way the shape defect (provided the latter is small enough).
Numerical reconstructions are presented in the last part of the article, which show
the efficiency of the method.

The paper is organized as follows. In section 2, we study the forward source problem in a two dimensional waveguide and introduce all the tools needed to use Lamb waves as a modal
basis. In section 3, we generalize the results of section 2 to the forward source problem in three dimensional plates. Section 4 is devoted to the reconstruction of shape defects in two dimensional plates, generalizing the method presented in
[
11
]
. Finally, in section 5 we show
numerical illustrations of the propagation of waves in two and three dimensional plates
as well as reconstructions of different shape defects.

\displaystyle(u_{0},t_{0},-s_{0},v_{0})

0&0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{c}A_{1}\\
B_{2}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}2pq^{2}\sin(qh)\\
0\end{array}\right),

(30)

and has non trivial solutions (for instance

\displaystyle A_{1}=0

Or
\displaystyle k\neq 0

\displaystyle p\in\pi\mathbb{Z}

\displaystyle q^{2}-k^{2}=0

\displaystyle(b_{1},b_{2})=(0,0)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{S}=0

Conversely, if (

to (

This shows that
\displaystyle\omega\in\omega_{\text{crit}}
if an only if (

not satisfied, and concludes the proof of the Theorem.
∎

.

The three following definitions of the set of critical frequencies are equivalent:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\omega_{\text{crit}}&=&\{\omega\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\,|\,\exists n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}\quad\langle\bm{X}_{n},\bm{Y}_{n}\rangle=0\}\\

&=&\{\omega\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\,|\,\exists n\in\mathbb{N}^{\star}\quad\partial_{k_{n}}\omega=0\}.\end{array}

(35)

Proposition

(36)

We use the modal decomposition to provide an outgoing condition for elastic waveguides.
For acoustic waves, one may ask that each modal component

In the same spirit, we consider the following

Definition 5
.

A wavefield
\displaystyle\bm{u}\in\text{H}^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)
is said to be outgoing if
\displaystyle\bm{X}
and
\displaystyle\bm{Y}
defined in (

\left|\langle\bm{Y},\bm{X}_{n}\rangle^{\prime}(x)\frac{x}{|x|}-ik_{n}\langle\bm{Y},\bm{X}_{n}\rangle(x)\right|\,,\,\left|\langle\bm{X},\bm{Y}_{n}\rangle^{\prime}(x)\frac{x}{|x|}-ik_{n}\langle\bm{X},\bm{Y}_{n}\rangle(x)\right|\underset{|x|\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}.

(37)

This condition guarantees existence and uniqueness for the source problem (
1
)
as we prove below.
For every
\displaystyle r>0
, we set
\displaystyle\Omega_{r}:=(-r,r)\times(-h,h)
.
We consider that any source defined on
\displaystyle\Omega_{r}
(resp.
\displaystyle(-r,r)
) is extended
by
\displaystyle 0
on
\displaystyle\Omega
(resp.
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
).

Theorem 2
.

Let
\displaystyle r>0
. For every
\displaystyle\omega\notin\omega_{\text{crit}}
,
\displaystyle\bm{f}=(f_{1},f_{2})\in\text{H}^{1}(\Omega_{r})
and
{}^{\text{top}}=(b^{\text{top}}_{1},b^{\text{top}}_{2}),^{\text{bot}}=(b^{\text{bot}}_{1},b^{\text{bot}}_{2})\in\widetilde{\text{H}}^{3/2}(-r,r)
,
the system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}(\bm{u})+\omega^{2}\bm{u}=-\bm{f}&\text{ in }\Omega,\\
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{u})\cdot\nu=^{\text{top}/\text{bot}}&\text{ on }\Omega_{\text{top}/\text{bot}},\\
\bm{u}\text{ is outgoing,}&\end{array}\right.

(38)

has a unique solution
\displaystyle\bm{u}\in\text{H}^{3}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)
. This solution admits a Lamb-mode decomposition

u(x,z)=\sum_{n>0}a_{n}(x)u_{n}(z),\qquad v(x,z)=\sum_{n>0}b_{n}(x)v_{n}(z),

(39)

where
\displaystyle a_{n},b_{n}
are solutions to the decoupled Helmholtz system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}a_{n}^{\prime\prime}+k_{n}^{2}a_{n}=ik_{n}F_{1}^{n}-{F_{2}^{n}}^{\prime},\\
b_{n}^{\prime\prime}+k_{n}^{2}b_{n}={F_{1}^{n}}^{\prime}-ik_{n}F_{2},\end{array}\right.

(40)

\text{where}\qquad F_{i}^{n}(x)=\frac{1}{J_{n}}\left(\int_{-h}^{h}f_{i}(x,z)u_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z+b_{i}^{\text{top}}(x)u_{n}(h)+b_{i}^{\text{bot}}(x)u_{n}(-h)\right),\quad i\in\{1,2\}.\,\,\,

(41)

Equivalently,
\displaystyle a_{n}=G_{1}^{n}\ast F_{1}^{n}-G_{2}^{n}\ast F_{2}^{n}
and
\displaystyle b_{n}=G_{2}^{n}\ast F_{1}^{n}-G_{1}^{n}\ast F_{2}^{n}
with

G_{1}^{n}(x)=\frac{1}{2}e^{ik_{n}|x|},\qquad G_{2}^{n}(x)=\frac{x}{2|x|}e^{ik_{n}|x|}.

(42)

Moreover, there exists a constant
\displaystyle C>0
, which only depends on
\displaystyle h
,
\displaystyle\omega
and
\displaystyle r
,
such that

\|\bm{u}\|_{\text{H}^{3}(\Omega_{r})}\leq C\left(\|\bm{f}\|_{\text{H}^{1}(\Omega)}+\|^{\text{top}}\|_{\text{H}^{3/2}(\mathbb{R})}+\|^{\text{bot}}\|_{\text{H}^{3/2}(\mathbb{R})}\right).

(43)

Proof.

This proof is an adaptation of the proof presented in Appendix A of
[
11
]
.

Step 1.
We first show uniqueness of the solution.
Assume that
\displaystyle u
solves (
38
) with
\displaystyle\bm{f}=0
,
{}^{\text{top}}=^{\text{bot}}=0
.
The associated fields
\displaystyle\bm{X},\bm{Y}
defined in (
4
) can be decomposed as

\bm{X}(x,z)=\sum_{n>0}a_{n}(x)\bm{X}_{n}(z),\qquad\bm{Y}(x,z)=\sum_{n>0}b_{n}(x)\bm{Y}_{n}(z).

(44)

As proved in
[
26
]
, the operators
\displaystyle F
and
\displaystyle G
defined in (
5
) are self adjoint on
\displaystyle H_{0}
. By projecting (
4
) with the product
\displaystyle\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
\displaystyle\bm{X}_{n}
and
\displaystyle\bm{Y}_{n}
, we see that

a_{n}^{\prime}=ik_{n}b_{n},\qquad b_{n}^{\prime}=ik_{n}a_{n}.

(45)

Solving this system of ODE’s and using the outgoing condition
shows that
\displaystyle a_{n}=b_{n}=0
, leading to
\displaystyle\bm{u}=0
.

Step 2.
Assume that the functions
\displaystyle(u,v)
defined in (
39
) is well defined.
A quick computation shows that the associated fields
\displaystyle(\bm{X},\bm{Y})
satisfy (
5
)
for every mode since

{G^{n}_{1}}^{\prime}=ik_{n}G^{n}_{2},\qquad{G^{n}_{2}}^{\prime}=ik_{n}G^{n}_{1}+\delta_{0}.

(46)

Assuming that
\displaystyle|x|>r
, we also see that

\left|\langle\bm{X},\bm{Y}_{n}\rangle^{\prime}(x)\frac{x}{|x|}-ik_{n}\langle\bm{X},\bm{Y}_{n}\rangle(x)\right|\\
=\left|\left(\frac{x}{|x|}{G_{1}^{n}}^{\prime}-ik_{n}G_{1}^{n}\right)\ast F_{1}^{n}(x)-\left(\frac{x}{|x|}{G_{2}^{n}}^{\prime}-ik_{n}G_{2}^{n}\right)\ast F_{2}^{n}(x)\right|=0.

(47)

Repeating this computation for
\displaystyle\langle\bm{Y},\bm{X}_{n}\rangle
,
shows that
\displaystyle(u,v)
satisfies the outgoing condition.

Step 3.

We prove that the functions given by (
39
) are well-defined, in other words
that the series in (
39
) converge.
We know from
[
28
]
that the number of evanescent and propagative modes is finite,
so we only need to study the convergence of the inhomogeneous modes.
We begin by noticing that if
\displaystyle k_{n}
is an inhomogeneous mode, then
\displaystyle k_{m}=-\overline{k_{n}}

also satisfies the dispersion relation, and
\displaystyle X_{m}=\overline{X_{n}}
and
\displaystyle Y_{m}=\overline{Y_{n}}
.
We index the subset of inhomogeneous wavenumbers with positive real part by
\displaystyle j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
and all the asymptotic comparison are now meant when
\displaystyle j\to+\infty
.
Let
\displaystyle N=2j-1/2
(resp.
\displaystyle N=2j+1/2
) if
\displaystyle k_{j}
is associated to a symmetric
(resp. antisymmetric) mode.
Using
[
23
]
, we know that

hk_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi N)+i\frac{\pi N}{2}-i\frac{\ln(2\pi N)}{2\pi N}+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

(48)

Let
\displaystyle p_{j}
and
\displaystyle q_{j}
be the quantities defined in (
10
). We notice that
\displaystyle p_{j},q_{j}\sim-ik_{j}
. Since
\displaystyle(p_{j}-ik_{j})(p_{j}+ik_{j})=\omega^{2}/(\lambda+2\mu)
and
\displaystyle(q_{j}-ik_{j})(q_{j}-ik_{j})=\omega^{2}/\mu
it follows that

p_{j}=-ik_{j}+\underbrace{\frac{\omega^{2}}{(\lambda+2\mu)\pi}}_{c_{p}}\frac{1}{N}+o\left(\frac{1}{N}\right),\quad q_{j}=-ik_{j}+\underbrace{\frac{\omega^{2}}{\mu\pi}}_{c_{q}}\frac{1}{N}+o\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

(49)

We notice that

\sin(ik_{j}z)=\frac{iz}{2|z|}\exp\left(\frac{|z|}{h}\left(\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi N)+i\frac{\pi N}{2}-i\frac{\ln(2\pi N)}{2\pi N}\right)\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{1-|z|/2h}}\right),

(50)

\cos(ik_{j}z)=\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(\frac{|z|}{h}\left(\frac{1}{2}\ln(2\pi N)+i\frac{\pi N}{2}-i\frac{\ln(2\pi N)}{2\pi N}\right)\right)+O\left(\frac{1}{N^{1-|z|/2h}}\right),

(51)

and if
\displaystyle\alpha
stands for
\displaystyle p
or
\displaystyle q
,

\cos(\alpha_{j}z)\sim\cos(ik_{j}z)+\frac{c_{\alpha}}{N}z\sin(ik_{j}z),\quad\sin(\alpha_{j}z)\sim-\sin(ik_{j}z)+\frac{c_{\alpha}}{N}z\cos(ik_{j}z).

(52)

Using the definition of the symmetric modes (
12
), we find that
\displaystyle(\bm{X}_{j}(z);\bm{Y}_{j}(z))\sim

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}h-|z|\\
-i\mu\pi N(z-hz/|z|)\\
\mu\pi N(h-|z|)\\
i(z-hz/|z|)\end{array}\right)\frac{\pi^{3}N^{2}(c_{p}-c_{q})}{16}\exp\left(\left(\frac{|z|}{h}+1\right)\left(\frac{\ln(2\pi N)}{2}+\frac{i\pi N}{2}-\frac{i\ln(2\pi N)}{2\pi N}\right)\right).

and it follows that

\|u_{j}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(-h,h)},\|v_{j}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(-h,h)}\sim\frac{\pi^{4}h^{3/2}N^{3}|c_{p}-c_{q}|}{4\ln(2\pi N)^{3/2}},\quad\|u_{j}\|_{\text{L}^{\infty}(-h,h)},\|v_{j}\|_{\text{L}^{\infty}(-h,h)}\sim\frac{\pi^{5}hN^{2}|c_{p}-c_{q}|}{8},

\|t_{j}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(-h,h)},\|s_{j}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(-h,h)}\sim\frac{\pi^{5}h^{3/2}\mu N^{4}|c_{p}-c_{q}|}{4\ln(2\pi N)^{3/2}},\quad|J_{j}|\sim\frac{h^{2}\pi^{6}N^{5}\omega^{2}|c_{p}-c_{q}|}{8}.

Similar estimates can be derived for the antisymmetric modes, which yield the same asymptotic
behaviors.
Defining

a_{j}=G_{1}^{j}\ast F_{1}^{j}-G_{2}^{j}\ast F_{2}^{j},\qquad b_{j}=G_{2}^{j}\ast F_{1}^{j}-G_{1}^{j}\ast F_{2}^{j},

(53)

we see using Young’s inequality that

\|a_{j}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(-r,r)}\leq\|G_{1}^{j}\|_{\text{L}^{1}(-r,r)}\|F_{1}^{j}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(-r,r)}+\|G_{2}^{j}\|_{\text{L}^{1}(-r,r)}\|F_{2}^{j}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(-r,r)}.

(54)

From the asymptotics of
\displaystyle k_{j}
it follows that

\displaystyle\|G_{1}^{j}\|_{\text{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R})},\|G_{2}^{j}\|_{\text{L}^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\leq 1/N
.
Thus if
\displaystyle u^{p}_{j}
denotes a primitive of
\displaystyle u_{j}
, we see that

\int_{-h}^{h}f_{1}(x,z)u_{j}(z)\mathrm{d}y=\left[f_{1}(x,z)u_{j}^{p}(z)\right]^{z=h}_{z=-h}-\int_{-h}^{h}\partial_{z}f_{1}(x,z)u_{j}^{p1}(z)\mathrm{d}z.

(55)

Using the previous estimates, we find that
\displaystyle u^{p}_{j}(z)\sim\tfrac{2}{i\pi N}u_{j}(z)

and so there exists a constant
\displaystyle c_{1}>0
, that depends on
\displaystyle\omega
and
\displaystyle h
, such that

\|F_{1}^{j}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(-r,r)}\leq\frac{c_{1}}{N^{3}}\left(\|f_{1}\|_{\text{H}^{1}(\Omega)}+\|b_{1}^{\text{top}}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\|b_{1}^{\text{bot}}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\right).

(56)

We obtain a similar estimate for
\displaystyle F_{2}
. Finally, using the triangular inequality,

\|u\|_{\text{L}^{2}(\Omega_{r})}\leq\sum_{n|k_{n}\in\mathbb{R},i\mathbb{R}}\|a_{n}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(-r,r)}\|u_{n}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(-h,h)}+2\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\|a_{j}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(-r,r)}\|u_{j}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(-h,h)},

(57)

which leads to

\|u\|_{\text{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\Bigg[\sum_{n|k_{n}\in\mathbb{R},i\mathbb{R}}\frac{2r}{|J_{n}|}\left(\|u_{n}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(-h,h)}+\|v_{n}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(-h,h)}\right)\|u_{n}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(-h,h)}\\
+2c_{1}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}\frac{1}{(2j\pm 1/2)\ln(2j\pm 1/2)^{3/2}}\Bigg]\left(\|\bm{f}\|_{\text{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R})}+\|^{\text{top}}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})}+\|^{\text{bot}}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\right),

(58)

A similar control holds for
\displaystyle v
. Elliptic regularity results (see e.g.
[
15
]
)
show that there exists a constant
\displaystyle c_{3}
depending on
\displaystyle r
such that

\|\bm{u}\|_{\text{H}^{3}(\Omega_{r})}\leq c_{3}\left(\|\bm{u}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(\Omega_{r})}+\|\bm{f}\|_{\text{H}^{1}(\mathbb{R})}+\|^{\text{top}}\|_{\text{H}^{3/2}(\mathbb{R})}+\|^{\text{bot}}\|_{\text{H}^{3/2}(\mathbb{R})}\right),

(59)

which together with (
58
) conclude the proof.
∎

Remark 4
.

This result is probably not optimal: indeed, in the scalar case one can merely
assume that the source term lies in
\displaystyle\text{L}^{2}(\Omega_{r})
and obtain a solution
in
\displaystyle\text{H}^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)
(see
[
11
]
).
However, in the present case, the Lamb modes are not orthogonal and Parseval equality
does not hold, so that in the above proof, we controlled terms using the triangular inequality,
which may lead to a loss of accuracy.
We can see in the proof that the extra regularity of the source terms is needed to
derive (
56
), which in turn yields the convergence of the series (
57
). Providing adaptation to elastic waveguides, the theory developed in
[
24
]
may be better adapted to treat source terms with lower regularity.

To conclude, in this section we have constructed an explicit solution of the elasticity problem
in a regular waveguide, and have shown that its norm is controlled by that of the source terms.
Such estimates will be useful in the following to perform the Born approximation.

3
Forward source problem in a regular 3D plate

In this section, we are interested in the forward source problem in a three-dimensional regular waveguide with two infinite dimensions. Our motivation comes from the experiments reported in
[
4
]
, where the authors try to reconstruct width defects in thin elastic plates.
The propagation of waves in three-dimension waveguides with one infinite dimension such as pipes
or air ducts is a direct generalization of the two dimensional case presented in the previous
section, see for instance
[
6
]
.
Two main issues are at stake. First, as mentioned in
[
19
]
,
in addition to longitudinal and transverse modes, one needs to take into account
horizontal shear modes in order to form a complete modal basis.
Second, one would like to generalize the
\displaystyle(\bm{X},\bm{Y})
formulation
of Definition
1
to 3D.

Given an elastic wavefield
\displaystyle{\bf u}=(u,v,w)
that propagates in a three dimension plate,
our main contribution consists in introducing two auxiliary variables
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
in (
64
)
that only depend on
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle v
, which allow the decoupling of the equations
of elasticity. We show that
\displaystyle(\alpha,w)
can be decomposed using Lamb modes,
while
\displaystyle\beta
represents the horizontal shear modes.
This allows us to obtain a generalization of Theorem
2

to three dimensional plates.

3.1
Decoupling of the linear elastic equation

Let us consider a 3D infinite elastic plate
\displaystyle\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}\times(-h,h)
,
where
\displaystyle h>0
is half of the waveguide thickness.
For every
\displaystyle r>0
, we define
\displaystyle\Omega_{r}=B_{2}(0,r)\times(-h,h)
where

\displaystyle B_{2}(0,r)
is the ball in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{2}
centered at
\displaystyle(0,0)
with radius
\displaystyle r
.
A point
\displaystyle(x,y,z)\in\Omega
will be denoted by
\displaystyle(\bm{x},z)
,
and the elastic displacement by
\displaystyle\bm{u}=(u,v,w)
.
Given a frequency
\displaystyle\omega\in\mathbb{R}
and given
\displaystyle(\lambda,\mu)

3.2
Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition

Now that equations (
65
) and (
66
) are solved,
we return to equations (
60
) and (
62
).
We need to ensure that given the expressions of
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
,
we can recover a unique expression for
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle v
.
To this end, we use the Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition, which states that under certain conditions, a vector field can be decomposed in a unique way as the sum of a curl-free and divergence-free
fields.
This decomposition is mostly used in fluid mechanics to analyze three dimensional vector fields
(see for instance
[
9
,
13
]
). In our case, we apply it to two dimensional vector
fields, since we are only interested in finding a link between
\displaystyle(u,v)
and
\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta)
.
We give the corresponding statement below, the proof of which and be found in
[
27
]

concerning existence, while uniqueness is addressed in
[
32
]
.

Proposition 8
.

Every vector field
\displaystyle\bm{\xi}\in\text{H}^{1}_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{3},\mathbb{C}^{2})
, vanishing at infinity, can be uniquely decomposed as
\displaystyle\bm{\xi}=\bm{d}+\bm{c}
where
\displaystyle\text{curl}_{2}(\bm{d})=0
and
\displaystyle\text{div}_{2}(\bm{c})=0
. The couple
\displaystyle(\bm{d},\bm{c})
is called the Helmholtz-Hodge decomposition (HHD) of
\displaystyle\bm{\xi}
. Moreover,
\displaystyle\bm{\xi}
is uniquely determined by
\displaystyle\text{div}_{2}(\bm{\xi})
and

\displaystyle\text{curl}_{2}(\bm{\xi})
.

Providing enough regularity on
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
, one can compute
\displaystyle(u,v)
using the formula
[
27
]

(u,v)=-\nabla(\mathcal{G}(\alpha))+\nabla\times(\mathcal{G}(\beta)),

(82)

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}
represents the Newtonian potential operator which convolves each function with
\displaystyle x\mapsto\log(|\bm{x}|)/(2\pi)
. However, in the following, we will not need to use this formula. Indeed, we exhibit expressions of
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle v
that satisfy
\displaystyle\text{div}_{2}(\bm{u})=\alpha
and
\displaystyle\text{curl}_{2}(\bm{u})=\beta
and thus are the ones we look for thanks to the previous uniqueness result.

We introduce an outgoing radiation condition for 3D wavefields:

Definition 7
.

A wavefield
\displaystyle\bm{u}\in\text{H}^{2}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)
is said to be outgoing if it vanishes at infinity, and if
\displaystyle\bm{X},\bm{Y}
defined in (
73
) and
\displaystyle\beta
defined in (
64
) satisfy

\sqrt{R}\left[\partial_{r}-ik_{n}\right]\langle\bm{X},\bm{Y}_{n}\rangle(Re^{i\theta})\,,\,\sqrt{R}\left[\partial_{r}-ik_{n}\right]\langle\bm{Y},\bm{X}_{n}\rangle(Re^{i\theta})\underset{R\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0\quad\forall n>0\quad\forall\theta\in(0,2\pi),

(83)

\sqrt{R}\left[\partial_{r}-i\kappa_{n}\right]\left(\beta\,|\,\varphi_{n}\right)(Re^{i\theta})\underset{R\rightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0\quad\forall n\geq 0\quad\forall\theta\in(0,2\pi).

(84)

Under this condition, uniqueness of solutions to the source problem in 3D
will be guaranteed, as stated in the next Theorem.
We first introduce some notations. We define the scalar convolution by

\bm{g_{1}}\ast\cdot\bm{g_{2}}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bm{g_{1}}(\bm{x}-\bm{y})\cdot\bm{g_{2}}(\bm{y})\mathrm{d}\bm{y},

(85)

and introduce the Green functions

G_{1}^{n}(\bm{x})=-\frac{i}{4}\text{H}^{(1)}_{0}(k_{n}|\bm{x}|),\quad\bm{G_{2}}^{n}(\bm{x})=\nabla G_{1}^{n}(\bm{x}),\quad G_{3}^{n}(\bm{x})=-\frac{i}{4}\text{H}^{(1)}_{0}(\kappa_{n}|\bm{x}|).

(86)

Let
\displaystyle(\bm{f}^{L},\bm{f}^{sh})
,
\displaystyle(\bm{b_{\text{top}}}^{L},\bm{b_{\text{top}}}^{sh})
,
\displaystyle(\bm{b_{\text{bot}}}^{L},\bm{b_{\text{bot}}}^{sh})
denote the HHD of
\displaystyle(f_{1},f_{2})
,
\displaystyle(b_{1}^{\text{top}},b_{2}^{\text{top}})
,
\displaystyle(b_{1}^{\text{bot}},b_{2}^{\text{bot}})
respectively and set

g_{n}^{z}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{J_{n}}\left(\int_{-h}^{h}f_{3}(\bm{x},z)v_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z+b_{3}^{\text{top}}(\bm{x})v_{n}(h)+b_{3}^{\text{bot}}(\bm{x})v_{n}(-h)\right),

(87)

\bm{g}_{n}^{\ell}=\frac{1}{J_{n}}\left(\int_{-h}^{h}\bm{f}^{\ell}(\bm{x},z)u_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z+\bm{b}_{\text{top}}^{\ell}u_{n}(h)+\bm{b}_{\text{bot}}^{\ell}u_{n}(-h)\right),\quad\ell\in\{L,sh\}.

(88)

Theorem 3
.

Let
\displaystyle r>0
. For every
\displaystyle\omega\notin\omega_{\text{crit}}\cup\omega_{\text{crit}}^{sh}
,
\displaystyle\bm{f}\in\text{H}^{3}(\Omega_{r})
and
{}^{\text{top}},^{\text{bot}}\in\widetilde{H}^{3/2}(-r,r)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}(\bm{u})+\omega^{2}\bm{u}=-\bm{f}&\text{ in }\Omega,\\

(89)

has a unique solution
\displaystyle\bm{u}\in\text{H}^{3}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)
. This solution admits a decomposition
\displaystyle\bm{u}=\bm{u}^{L}+\bm{u}^{sh}
with

(90)

where

(91)

Equivalently,
\displaystyle\bm{A}_{n}=-g_{n}^{z}\ast\bm{G_{2}}^{n}+ik_{n}\bm{g}_{n}^{L}\ast G_{1}^{n}

\displaystyle C>0

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle r

(92)

Proof.

If
\displaystyle\bm{f}
,
{}^{\text{top}}
and

vanish, uniqueness in Propositions

and

show that

. Since
\displaystyle u
and
\displaystyle v
are uniquely determined by
\displaystyle\text{div}_{2}(u,v)
and
\displaystyle\text{curl}_{2}(u,v)

\displaystyle u=v=0

\displaystyle\alpha

Finally, the control of the wavefield with respect to source terms is obtained

(93)

In the present case, given current experimental setups

4.1
Born approximation

As incident wave, we use the function corresponding to
the first symmetric Lamb mode of a straight guide, which
we denote by
\displaystyle\bm{u}^{\text{inc}}(x,z):=\bm{u}_{1}(z)e^{ik_{1}x}
. The same analysis could be conducted with any other Lamb mode. However, note that the first symmetric Lamb mode has the advantage to propagate at any frequency, which is not the case for the other symmetric Lamb modes. The total wavefield
\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{u}}
solves the equations of elasticity in the waveguide:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{u}})+\omega^{2}\widetilde{\bm{u}}=0&\text{ in }\widetilde{\Omega},\\
\bm{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{u}})\cdot\nu=0&\text{ on }\partial\widetilde{\Omega}_{\text{top}/\text{bot}}.\end{array}\right.

(95)

Then, the scattered wavefield solves

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\nabla\cdot\bm{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{u}}^{s})+\omega^{2}\widetilde{\bm{u}}^{s}=0&\text{ in }\widetilde{\Omega},\\
\bm{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{u}}^{s})\cdot\nu=-\bm{\sigma}(\bm{u}^{\text{inc}})\cdot\nu&\text{ on }\partial\widetilde{\Omega}_{\text{top}/\text{bot}},\\
\bm{u}^{s}\text{ is outgoing.}\end{array}\right.

(96)

For every
\displaystyle(x,z)\in\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
, we know, using the definition of Lamb modes, that

\bm{\sigma}(\bm{u}^{\text{inc}})=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}s_{1}(z)&t_{1}(z)\\
t_{1}(z)&r_{1}(z)\end{array}\right)e^{ik_{1}x},

(97)

so if
\displaystyle(x,z)\in\partial\widetilde{\Omega}_{\text{top}}
then
\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}(\widetilde{\bm{u}}^{s})\cdot\nu
is equal to

-\bm{\sigma}({\widetilde{\bm{u}}}^{\text{inc}})\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+4h^{2}g_{1}^{\prime}(x)^{2}}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-2hg_{1}^{\prime}(x)\\
1\end{array}\right)=-\frac{e^{ik_{1}x}}{\sqrt{1+4h^{2}g_{1}^{\prime}(x)^{2}}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-2hg_{1}^{\prime}(x)s_{1}(z)+t_{1}(z)\\
-2hg_{1}^{\prime}(x)t_{1}(z)+r_{1}(z)\end{array}\right).

(98)

We can also do the same thing on

to explicit the equation of the scattered wavefield. Then, following the steps of

Using Theorem

u_{\omega}(x,h)=\sum_{n>0}a_{n}(x)u_{n,\omega}(h),\qquad v_{\omega}(x,h)=\sum_{n>0}b_{n}(x)v_{n,\omega}(h),

where
\displaystyle a_{n}=G_{1}^{n}\ast F^{n}_{1}-G_{2}^{n}\ast F_{2}^{n}
,
\displaystyle b_{n}=G_{2}^{n}\ast F_{1}^{n}-G_{1}^{n}\ast F_{2}^{n}
and

are defined in (

F^{n}_{1}(x)=\frac{e^{ik_{1}x}}{J_{n}}\left((2hg_{1}^{\prime}s_{1}(h)-(g_{1}^{\prime}-g_{2}^{\prime})\partial_{z}t_{1}(h))u_{n,\omega}(h)-(2hg_{2}^{\prime}s_{1}(h)+(g_{1}^{\prime}-g_{2}^{\prime})\partial_{z}t_{1}(h))u_{n,\omega}(-h)\right),

(113)

F^{n}_{2}=\frac{e^{ik_{1}x}}{J_{n}}(g_{1}^{\prime}-g_{2}^{\prime})\partial_{z}r_{1}(g)\left(-u_{n,\omega}(h)+u_{n,\omega}(-h)\right).

(114)

Assuming that
\displaystyle x
is located on the left of the support of the sources,

(u_{\omega},v_{\omega})(x,h)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n>0}(u_{n,\omega},-v_{n,\omega})(h)e^{-ik_{n}(\omega)x}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{ik_{n}(\omega)z}(F^{n}_{1}(z)-F^{n}_{2}(z))\mathrm{d}z,

(115)

As explained in
[
21
]
and illustrated in
[
19
]
, we can use a spatial Fourier transform along
\displaystyle x
to separate each term of the sum. We notice that up to a multiplicative coefficient, the fields
\displaystyle u_{\omega}
and
\displaystyle v_{\omega}
contain the same information about the source, so that only measurements of one component of the displacement are needed.

Further, since noise is likely to pollute the response of evanescent and inhomogeneous modes
in real-life experiments, we only consider the propagative modes and for these modes

\displaystyle n
we have access to

\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{i(k_{n}(\omega)+k_{1}(\omega))z}(g_{1}^{\prime}(z)c^{1}_{n}+g_{2}^{\prime}(z)c^{2}_{n})\mathrm{d}z\qquad\forall\omega\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

(116)

where
\displaystyle c^{1}_{n}
and
\displaystyle c^{2}_{n}
are known coefficients depending on the mode
\displaystyle n
. We use the following definition for the Fourier transform

\mathcal{F}(g)(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(z)e^{-i\xi z}\mathrm{d}z.

(117)

From now on, we consider that
\displaystyle n=1
is the first propagative symmetric Lamb mode and
\displaystyle n=2
is the first propagative antisymmetric Lamb mode.
Both modes exist at any frequency
\displaystyle\omega
, and
\displaystyle\omega\mapsto k_{1}(\omega)
or
\displaystyle\omega\mapsto k_{2}(\omega)
are increasing functions that map
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{+}
to
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{+}
(see an illustration in Figure
2
for the symmetric case, and for more details we refer to
[

]
).
In particular, if we set
\displaystyle\xi=2k_{1}
, the available information amounts to knowing

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(c_{1}^{1}g_{1}^{\prime}+c_{1}^{2}g_{2}^{\prime})(\xi)
for every
\displaystyle\xi\in(0,2k_{1}(\omega_{\max}))
.
Similarly, if
\displaystyle\xi=k_{1}+k_{2}
, we have knowledge of
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(c_{2}^{1}g_{1}^{\prime}+c_{2}^{2}g_{2}^{\prime})
for every

\displaystyle\xi\in(0,k_{1}(\omega_{\max})+k_{2}(\omega_{\max}))
.
We define

\xi_{\max}=\min\left(k_{1}(\omega_{\max})+k_{2}(\omega_{\max}),2k_{1}(\omega_{\max})\right).

(118)

Looking at expressions (
113
)-(
114
), we notice that the linear combinations
\displaystyle c_{1}^{1}g_{1}^{\prime}+c_{1}^{2}g_{2}^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle c_{2}^{1}g_{1}^{\prime}+c_{2}^{2}g_{2}^{\prime}
are independent so the functions
\displaystyle g_{1}^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle g_{2}^{\prime}
can be reconstructed using the inverse Fourier transform, in a stable way as the next Proposition shows
(its proof is the same as Proposition 12 in
[

Proposition 11
.

Let
\displaystyle g,g^{\text{app}}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(-r,r)
and their Fourier transform
\displaystyle d=\mathcal{F}(g)
and
\displaystyle d^{\text{app}}=\mathcal{F}(g^{\text{app}})
defined on
\displaystyle(0,\xi_{\max})
. Assume that there exists
\displaystyle M>0
such that
\displaystyle\|g\|_{\text{H}^{1}(-r,r)},\|g^{\text{app}}\|_{\text{H}^{1}(-r,r)}\leq M
, then

\|g-g^{\text{app}}\|^{2}_{\text{L}^{2}(-r,r)}\leq\frac{4}{\pi}\|\mathcal{F}(g)-\mathcal{F}(g^{\text{app}})\|_{\text{L}^{2}(0,\xi_{\max})}+\frac{2\pi}{\xi_{\max}^{2}}M^{2}.

(119)

Remark 5
.

We notice that the above estimate is actually better than the one presented in the acoustic case in Proposition 12 of
[
11
]
.
Indeed, in the acoustic case, there is only one propagative mode at every frequency, and the function
\displaystyle k_{1}+k_{2}
is not one-to-one from
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{+}
to
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
In the elastic case however, we take advantage of the existence of two different Lamb modes propagating at every frequency.

Given the reconstructions of
\displaystyle g_{1}^{\prime}
and
\displaystyle g_{2}^{\prime}
, we can integrate these functions,
using the fact that
\displaystyle g_{1}
and
\displaystyle g_{2}
have compact support, and obtain an approximation of
the shapes of the defects. In the previous estimate,
the error
\displaystyle\|d-d^{\text{app}}\|_{\text{L}^{2}(-r,r)}
contains both the measurement error,
as well as the error caused by the Born approximation (
107
).
It follows that the reconstruction error decreases when the size of the defects gets
smaller and when
\displaystyle\omega_{\max}
increases. We present examples of numerical reconstructions
in the next section.

We conclude this section by discussing possible extensions of this work.
First, the method presented here could be implemented in a similar fashion in 3D.
Indeed, using a Born approximation, one can show that the measurements are close to
those emanating from a wavefield generated by two boundary source terms
that depend on
\displaystyle\nabla g_{1}
and
\displaystyle\nabla g_{2}
in a regular waveguide.
In 3D, a Hankel transform plays the role of the Fourier transform,
and one obtains thus a reconstruction.

Second, by the same method, one can also reconstruct bends in an elastic waveguide,
in a similar manner as in the acoustic case described in section 3.2 of
[
11
]
.
However, the detection of homogeneities seems more difficult.
Following section 3.4 of
[
11
]
, one could use a Born approximation to approximate
the measurements by a wavefield generated by an internal source term
\displaystyle\bm{f}
,
that depends on a transformed inhomogeneity in a regular waveguide.
However, it does not seem easy to extract, from the measurements, something like the Fourier transform of a function, that would characterize the inhomogeneity,
as in (
116
).
