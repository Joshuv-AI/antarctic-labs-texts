Time-Fractional Porous Medium Type Equations. Sharp Time Decay and Regularization
Matteo Bonforte, Maria Gualdani, Peio Ibarrondo

Abstract
We consider a class of porous medium type of equations with Caputo time derivative. The prototype problem reads as D t α ​ u = − ℒ ​ u m D^{\alpha}_{t}u=-\mathcal{L}u^{m} and is posed on a bounded Euclidean domain Ω ⊂ ℝ N \Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N} with smooth boundary and zero Dirichlet boundary conditions. The linear operator ℒ \mathcal{L} falls within a wide class of either local or nonlocal operators, and the nonlinearity is allowed to be of degenerate or singular type, namely, 0 < m < 1 0<m<1 and m > 1 m>1 . Using the fractional gradient flow theory, we show existence of unique solution and prove new L p − L ∞ L^{p}-L^{\infty} smoothing effects by employing the Green function method. The comparison principle, which we provide in the most general setting, serves as a crucial tool in the proof and provides a novel monotonicity formula. To the best of our knowledge, this type of smoothing effects are novel in nonlinear PDEs involving Caputo derivative. Consequently, we establish that the regularizing effect due to the diffusion is stronger than the memory effect introduced by the fractional time derivative. We also prove that the solution satisfies the zero Dirichlet condition pointwise by means of sharp boundary estimates. Finally, we prove that our solution does not vanish in finite time if 0 < m < 1 0<m<1 , unlike the case with the classical time derivative. Indeed, we provide a sharp rate of decay for any L p L^{p} -norm of the solution for any m > 0 m>0 . Our findings indicate that the difference between slow and fast diffusion is mitigated by the memory effect, which dramatically slows the spatial diffusion.



Keywords:
porous medium equation; fast diffusion equation; nonlocal operators; Caputo fractional time derivative; subdiffusion; comparison principle; regularity estimates; long time behavior.

Introduction

We consider the Dirichlet problem for a general class of Porous Medium type of equations with Caputo fractional time derivative of order
\alpha\in(0,1)
:

D_{t}^{\alpha}u=-\mathcal{L}u^{m}\qquad m>0\,,

(CPME)

with
u(x,t)
defined on a smooth bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
The linear operator
\mathcal{L}
belongs to a general class of densely defined operators both of local and nonlocal type, which includes the classical Laplacian and fractional Laplacian operator. The fractional time operator
D_{t}^{\alpha}
is the Caputo derivative, introduced in
[
24
]
and defined for
\alpha\in(0,1)
by

D^{\alpha}_{t}f(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\frac{\rm d}{\,{\rm d}t}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{f(\tau)-f(0)}{(t-\tau)^{\alpha}}\,{\rm d}\tau\,.

(1)

In the last years, subdiffusive partial differential equations – equations that combine diffusion operators in the space variable with fractional time derivatives –
have attracted significant attention, as they are used to model anomalous diffusion, viscoelastic phenomena and processes with memory
[
44
,
45
,
46
]
. The prototype subdiffusive PDE is the “Heat Equation with memory”,

D^{\alpha}_{t}u=\Delta u\,.

(2)

For the well-posedness and regularity of (
2
) in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, see for instance the works of Allen, Caffarelli and Vasseur in
[
3
]
; Kemppainen, Siljander, Vergara and Zacher in
[
37
]
; and Zacher in
[
56
]
.
Optimal asymptotic decay for solutions of (
2
) are obtained by Cortazar, Quiros and Wolansky in
[
25
]
for the Cauchy problem in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. For the Dirichlet problem on bounded domains, Vergara and Zacher
[
53
]
show long time decay estimates, also allowing for general operators with variable coefficients in space and time. Recently, Chan, Gomez-Castro and Vázquez studied in
[
27
]
global well-posedness of (CPME) with
m=1
for singular solutions on bounded domains.

From a nonlinear perspective, Vergara and Zacher in
[
53
]
consider several fractional nonlinear models and obtain sharp decay estimates of
L^{q}
norms using fractional ODEs techniques. The Porous Medium with fractional pressure, associated to the so-called Caffarelli-Vázquez model first introduced in
[
23
]
, has been studied in its Caputo derivative version, by Allen, Caffarelli and Vasseur in
[
2
]
and by Daus, Xu, Zamponi, Zhang and the second author in
[
29
]
.

Akagi
[
1
]
, and Li and Liu
[
40
]
developed a theory of fractional gradient flows in Hilbert spaces, which is the analogous of the Brezis-Komura theory with fractional time derivative
[
21
,
39
]
. This theory provides well-posedness for both linear and for nonlinear problems, and it is the starting point of our paper.

Beside global well-posedness, the main contributions of this manuscript are threefold: (i) the study of comparison principle and time monotonicity formula, (ii)
L^{p}-L^{\infty}
smoothing effects, and (iii) optimal long time behaviour for (CPME). A surprising fact established with our analysis is the
non-extinction in finite time
for all solutions of (CPME) when
m\in(0,1)
.
This fact was previously observed in
[
53
]
for separate-variable solutions. In this work, we prove this property for any general solution.

The Caputo derivative adds the extra difficulty of the “memory effect”. Roughly speaking, this memory effect somehow destroys the semigroup structure, essential in the De Giorgi-Nash-Moser theory. Surprisingly enough, a nontrivial adaptation of the so-called Green function method, introduced in
[
18
]
, allows us to establish our main results, overcoming the memory effect. This is achieved by employing novel time monotonicity estimates, a feature that comes as a surprise in the context of the Caputo setting, as we shall further comment below.

To provide further insights about our results, some numerology is in order: our asymptotic estimates correspond to the known ones in the formal limit
m\rightarrow 1
. On the other hand, this does not happen in the –formal– limit
\alpha\rightarrow 1^{-}
. Indeed, neither the exponents of smoothing effects nor the ones in the long time behaviour estimates, correspond to the known ones when
\alpha=1
.

Next, we provide a more detailed exposition of our principal findings:

Comparison principle and time monotonicity.
We first recall the results about existence and uniqueness of solution for (CPME) from
[
1
,
40
]
. One of the approximations steps proposed in
[
40
]
is the starting point of our proof of the comparison principle. The comparison principle here corresponds to “well-ordering”: if initial data for (CPME) are ordered, then solutions will preserve the same order for any positive time.

In the present case, we have to overcome two difficulties: we do not have the standard semigroup property
S(t+h)f=S(h)S(t)f
because of the Caputo derivative, and we lack regularity to perform rigorously the computations. For these reasons, our proof involves several approximation steps. It is remarkable that we inherit comparison properties from the discretized problem, in such a general framework (see more comments on that after Theorem
1.2
). This comparison principle of Theorem
1.2
lays the cornerstone for several other results in this manuscript, most importantly for a
new time monotonicity estimates
“a la Benilan-Crandall”. Namely, we show that
t^{\frac{\alpha}{m-1}}u(t)
is monotone in time for every
t>0
, cf. Theorem
1.3
. The unexpected nature of this result arises from the memory effect, which complicates any form of time monotonicity.

Smoothing effects and boundary estimates.
We show
new
L^{p}-L^{\infty}
smoothing effects
, stated in Theorem
1.4
. To the best of our knowledge, these kind of
L^{p}-L^{\infty}
regularizing effects have been proved by Kemppainen, Siljander, Vergara and Zacher in
[
37
]
only for the linear Caputo Heat equation (
2
) on the whole space, whenever the initial data is integrable enough. Our regularizing estimates hold for
u_{0}\in L^{p}(\Omega)
with
p>\frac{N}{2s}
. There is a structural reason why one should not expect similar results for
p<\frac{N}{2s}
: the fundamental solution of (
2

\mathbb{R}^{N}
is unbounded and belongs to
L^{q}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
with
q<\frac{N}{N-2}
. Therefore, one should ask the initial datum to be in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
with
p>\frac{N}{2}
.

One question for the future is the proof of higher regularity, starting for example with the work of Allen, Caffarelli and Vasseur in
[
3
]
for Hölder continuity of the linear problem. We do not address this issue in the current manuscript.
The results present in the literature concerning nonlinear diffusions with Caputo time derivative are mostly of propagation of
L^{p}
-norms: for instance, in
[
55
]
the authors prove propagation of
L^{\infty}
-norm for (CPME) in the case when
\mathcal{L}=(-\Delta)
, meaning that solutions remains bounded whenever
u_{0}
is bounded.

We also study the behaviour of solutions near the boundary and show sharp estimates of the form
u(t,x)\lesssim{\rm dist}^{\gamma/m}(x,\partial\Omega)t^{-\alpha/m}
in Theorem
1.5
. Indeed, for “big initial data” we obtain lower estimates with matching powers, proving the so-called Global Harnack Principle, that is
u(t,x)\asymp{\rm dist}^{\gamma/m}(x,\partial\Omega)t^{-\alpha/m}
, see Theorem
1.6
. These results are optimal both in space and in time, the latter optimality is explained in what follows.

Optimal long time behaviour.
Our last contribution concerns a sharp analysis of the long time behaviour of the solution, cf. Theorem
1.7
: in contrast to the local-in-time results, in the fast diffusion regime
m\in(0,1)
, solutions to (CPME) do not extinguish in finite time. This is an important side effect of the “memory” intrinsic in the Caputo derivative. We compute the rates of decay of all
L^{p}
norms from above and below, with matching powers. This phenomenon of non-extinction was first observed by Vergara and Zacher in
[
53
]
for separate-variables solutions.
Here, by means of differential inequalities involving Caputo derivative

1.1
Main theorems

We study the following Cauchy-Dirichlet problem with Caputo time derivative

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}D_{t}^{\alpha}u(t,x)=-\mathcal{L}u^{m}(t,x)&\qquad\mbox{on }(0,+\infty)\times\Omega\,,\\
u(t,\cdot)=0&\qquad\mbox{on the lateral boundary, }\forall t>0\,,\\
u(0,\cdot)=u_{0}&\qquad\mbox{in }\Omega\,,\end{array}\right.

(CPME)

where
m>0
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is a bounded smooth domain, with boundary at least
C^{2,\alpha}
to avoid delicate questions about boundary regularity. We also assume
N>2s
. Note that Caputo derivative is defined as a classical time derivative of a convolution with the kernel
k\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}((0,+\infty))
:

D^{\alpha}_{t}f(t)=\partial_{t}[k*(f-f_{0})](t)=\frac{\rm d}{\,{\rm d}t}\int_{0}^{t}k(t-\tau)\,(f(\tau)-f_{0})\,{\rm d}\tau\,,

where the kernel is given by

k(t)=\frac{t^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\,.

This kernel
k
admits an inverse of the form

\ell(t)=\frac{t^{\alpha-1}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\,,

with respect to the convolution, i.e.,
k*\ell=1
.
It is worth mentioning that there are different types pf kernels that define other types of fractional derivative, and it would be interesting to analyze, see
[
1
,
47
,
55
]
.

The operator
\mathcal{L}
is a linear diffusion operator of local or nonlocal type. We refer to Section
1.2
for a precise description, and to Section
1.3
for some examples.
The solutions that we consider here are defined in the Hilbert space
H^{*}(\Omega)
, which is the topological dual space of
H(\Omega)
, the domain of the quadratic form associated to the operator
\mathcal{L}
:

H(\Omega)=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega)\,:\,\int_{\Omega}u\mathcal{L}u\,{\rm d}x<+\infty\right\}\,.

The space
H^{*}(\Omega)
is equipped with the following inner product and associated norm:

\langle u,v\rangle_{H^{*}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}u\,\mathcal{L}^{-1}u\,{\rm d}x\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\|u\|^{2}_{H^{*}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}u\,\mathcal{L}^{-1}u\,{\rm d}x\,.

Let us recall that when
\mathcal{L}=(-\Delta_{\Omega})
then
H(\Omega)=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
and
H^{*}(\Omega)=H^{-1}(\Omega)
. We refer to
[
14
,
16
]
for more details about the space
H^{*}(\Omega)
when
\mathcal{L}
is a general nonlocal operator.
Now, we introduce the class of solutions that we will consider in this manuscript.

Definition 1.1
.

(
H^{*}
-solutions). Let
u\in L^{2}((0,T):H^{*}(\Omega))
, then
u
is a

-solution of (

i)

We have

k*(u-u_{0})\in W^{1,2}((0,T):H^{*}(\Omega)),\qquad[k*(u-u_{0})](0)=0,\qquad|u|^{m-1}u\in L^{2}((0,T):H(\Omega)).

ii)

For a.e.
t\in(0,T)

\int_{\Omega}D^{\alpha}_{t}u(t,x)\mathcal{L}^{-1}\varphi(x)\,{\rm d}x=-\int_{\Omega}u^{m}(t,x)\varphi(x)\,{\rm d}x\qquad\quad\forall\varphi\in H^{*}(\Omega).

(3)

To ensure existence and uniqueness in this class of solutions, we use the theory of fractional gradient flows in Hilbert spaces developed in
[
1

40

For the Caputo derivative, in

it is proved that the solutions of fractional gradient flows associated to

-solutions according to the above definition.

-solutions, which has its own interest, and it is also a key ingredient for proving the rest of the main results presented in this paper. To the best of our knowledge, this comparison result is new and a complete proof can be found in Section

The proof of the comparison result is based on a suitable discretization in the spirit of

, together with a clever limiting process: we prove comparison first for the Euler implicit scheme associated to the

This method diverges from conventional PDE techniques. Typically, the limit solution from a discretization process is obtained through compactness arguments, and loses the well-ordering property. In our case, however, the uniqueness of gradient flow solution allows keep the desired comparison. Despite the fact that classical solutions and viscosity solutions are the natural classes of solutions where the comparison principle makes sense pointwise

42

A direct – and important – consequence of the comparison principle and of the time-scaling of the equation are the Benilan-Crandall time monotonicity formulae that we present below. Let us emphasize that achieving any time monotonicity in PDEs involving the Caputo time derivative is considerably more challenging than with the classical time derivative, due to the “memory effect”: when
D^{\alpha}_{t}f(t)\leq 0

4.4
, which plays the role of an “almost representation formula”. This pointwise bound is a direct consequence of the dual formulation

\mathcal{L}^{-1}D^{\alpha}_{t}u=-u^{m}\,,

and time monotonicity formula of Theorem
1.3
. This approach replaces the classical Moser iteration, which seems more challenging due to the memory effect of the time derivative.

Thanks to the Green function method, we are able to show stronger boundary estimates, and in particular we ensure that solutions satisfy the zero Dirichlet boundary condition in a precise and quantitative way. The estimate we provide involves a parameter
\gamma>0
, which characterizes the behaviour of the first eigenfunction of
\mathcal{L}
. The value of
\gamma
depends on the diffusion operator under consideration, see Section
1.2
for further explanations.

Theorem 1.5

(Upper boundary behaviour)
.

Let
m>0
,
m\neq 1
,
N>2s>\gamma
. Assume that (
G2
) and (
K
) hold. Given a
H^{*}
-solution
u
with initial datum
u_{0}\in L^{p}(\Omega)\cap H^{*}(\Omega)
with
p
satisfying

\begin{cases}p\geq 1+m,&\mbox{if }1+m>\frac{N}{2s-\gamma},\\
p>\frac{N}{2s-\gamma},&\mbox{if }\frac{N}{2s-\gamma}\geq 1+m,\end{cases}

then, we have

\left\|\frac{u^{m}(t)}{\mbox{\rm dist}(\cdot,\partial\Omega)^{\gamma}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\overline{\kappa}\frac{\|u_{0}\|_{p}}{t^{\alpha}},\qquad\quad\forall t>0\,,

(9)

with
\overline{\kappa}
depending only on
N,s,\alpha,p,m,\Omega
.

The upper bound (
9
) is sharp, since at least for “big initial data” we provide matching lower bounds:

Theorem 1.6

(Global Harnack Principle for “big” initial data)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.5
, assume moreover that
m>\frac{N-2s}{N+2s}
and that the initial data also satisfies
u_{0}(x)\geq\mathsf{c}\,\mbox{\rm dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{\frac{\gamma}{m}}
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
and some
\mathsf{c}>0
. Then, we have the following Global Harnack Principle

\frac{c_{0}}{1+t^{\alpha}}\leq\frac{u^{m}(t,x)}{\mbox{\rm dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{\gamma}}\leq\frac{c_{1}}{t^{\alpha}}\qquad\mbox{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega\quad\forall t>0\,.

(10)

with
c_{0},c_{1}>0
depending only on
\mathsf{c},N,s,\alpha,p,m,\Omega
and
\|u_{0}\|_{p}
.

The above Global Harnack Principle (
10
), follows by the comparison principle and from the form of the solution(s) of separation of variable.
The proof of the above results is contained in Section
4
.

The next natural question is whether the asymptotic behaviour of the solution is similar to the one with the classical time derivative. Surprisingly, the answer is negative. We have already commented on the discontinuity in
\alpha
of the time behaviour, we now state the precise result: all the
L^{p}
-norms decay in time with the same rate as
t
goes to infinity, for all
m>0
. In particular, we would like to focus on the fast diffusion regime
m\in(0,1)
, and compare the cases
\alpha=1
and
0<\alpha<1
. In the latter case, solutions do not extinguish in finite time, contrary to the case
\alpha=1
, cf.
[
14
]
, and this is one of the biggest differences between the classical and the Caputo time derivatives.

Theorem 1.7

(Sharp Time Decay of
L^{p}
-norms)
.

Assume (
G1
) and (
K
). Let
u
be a
H^{*}
-solution of (
CPME
) with nonnegative initial datum
u_{0}\in L^{p_{0}}(\Omega)\cap H^{*}(\Omega)
with
p_{0}>1
such that

\begin{cases}p_{0}\geq 1+m,&\mbox{if }1+m>\frac{N}{2s},\\
p_{0}>\frac{N}{2s},&\mbox{if }\frac{N}{2s}\geq 1+m.\end{cases}

Then, for every
1\leq p\leq\infty
it holds that

\frac{c_{0}}{1+t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}}\leq\|u(t)\|_{p}\leq\frac{c_{1}}{t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}}\qquad\forall t>0\,,

(11)

where
c_{0},c_{1}>0
depends on
N,s,\alpha,p,m,\Omega,\,\|u_{0}\|_{p_{0}}
.

The proof of (
11
) follows by the smoothing effects, abound from above the
L^{\infty}
-norm, and a lower bound for a weighted
L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}
-norm, inspired by
[
54
]
. More detailed comments and a complete proof can be found in Section
5
.

1.2
Assumptions and functional setting of the operator
\mathcal{L}

Let us present the main assumptions on the operator
\mathcal{L}
and its inverse operator
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
. These assumptions are the standard ones in the literature, see
[
11
,
12
,
14
,
16
,
18
,
19
]
.

Assumptions on
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
.
Our approach relies on the dual formulation of (
CPME
), so that we assume the existence of an inverse operator
\mathcal{L}^{-1}:L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
for
\mathcal{L}
represented through a kernel
\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}
:

\mathcal{L}^{-1}u(x):=\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}(x,y)u(y)\,{\rm d}y\,.

The main assumption on the Green function
\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}
is that there exists
c_{1,\Omega}>0
such that

0\leq\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}(x,y)\leq\frac{c_{1,\Omega}}{|x-y|^{N-2s}}\,,

(G1)

for some
s\in(0,1]
. Assumption (
G1
) guarantees the compactness of the operator
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, see
[
11
, Proposition 5.1]
. As a consequence, the operator
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
has a discrete spectrum and a
L^{2}(\Omega)
orthonormal basis of eigenfunctions that we denote by
\left(\lambda_{k}^{-1},\Phi_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1}
. Accordingly, we can express

\mathcal{L}^{-1}u(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{-1}\hat{u}_{k}\,\Phi_{k}(x)\,\qquad\mbox{with}\quad\hat{u}_{k}=\int_{\Omega}u(y)\Phi_{k}(y)\,{\rm d}y\,.

Furthermore, assumption (
G1
) also implies that
\mathcal{L}
has the discrete spectrum
\left(\lambda_{k},\Phi_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1}
and then

\mathcal{L}u(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}\hat{u}_{k}\,\Phi_{k}(x)\,.

(12)

Hence, assumption (
G1
) is enough to construct the Hilbert space
H^{*}(\Omega)
– the functional space where fractional gradient flow theory will be applied – as the topological dual space of

H(\Omega)=\left\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega)\quad:\quad\|v\|_{H(\Omega)}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}\,\left|\hat{v}_{k}\right|^{2}<+\infty\right\}\,.

Note that the scalar product and the norm of
H^{*}(\Omega)
now read as

\langle u,v\rangle_{H^{*}(\Omega)}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{-1}\hat{u}_{k}\,\hat{v}_{k}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\|u\|^{2}_{H^{*}(\Omega)}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}^{-1}|\hat{u}_{k}|^{2}\,.

We refer to
[
16
]
for a proper identification of the functional spaces
H(\Omega)
and
H^{*}(\Omega)
depending on the specific choice of the operator
\mathcal{L}
. See also
[
11
,
12
,
19
]
.

When dealing with boundary issues it is convenient to assume that the kernel
\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}
satisfies the following condition for some
s,\gamma\in(0,1]
: there exist
c_{0,\Omega},c_{1,\Omega}>0
such that for a.e.
x,y\in\Omega

c_{0,\Omega}{\delta_{\gamma}}(x){\delta_{\gamma}}(y)\leq\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}(x,y)\leq\frac{c_{1,\Omega}}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{N-2s}}\left(\frac{{\delta_{\gamma}}(x)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{\gamma}}\wedge 1\right)\left(\frac{{\delta_{\gamma}}(y)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{\gamma}}\wedge 1\right)\,,

(G2)

where
\delta_{\gamma}(x)=\mbox{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{\gamma}
. It was proven in
[
11
]
that (
G2
) implies that the first eigenfunction of
\mathcal{L}
, namely,
\mathcal{L}\Phi_{1}=\lambda_{1}\Phi_{1}
with
\Phi_{1}\geq 0
and some constant
\lambda_{1}>0
, satisfies
\Phi_{1}(x)\asymp\mbox{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{\gamma}
. Thus, we can rewrite assumption (
G2
) as follows

\mathsf{c}_{0,\Omega}\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{1}(y)\leq\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}(x,y)\leq\frac{\mathsf{c}_{1,\Omega}}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{N-2s}}\left(\frac{\Phi_{1}(x)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{\gamma}}\wedge 1\right)\left(\frac{\Phi_{1}(y)}{\lvert x-y\rvert^{\gamma}}\wedge 1\right)\,.

(G3)

Assumptions (
G2
) and (
G3
) will be used only in Theorem
1.5
and Theorem
1.6
. In the rest of the results we just assume (
G1
) on
\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}
.

Assumptions on
\mathcal{L}
.
Let
\mathcal{L}:\mbox{\rm dom}(\mathcal{L})\subset L^{2}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
be a positive, possibly unbounded and self-adjoint operator. From assumption (
G1
), we know that the operator
\mathcal{L}
can be expressed in its spectral form as in (
12
), and hence the integration by parts formula follows:

\int_{\Omega}u\mathcal{L}v\,{\rm d}x=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}^{\frac{1}{2}}u\mathcal{L}^{\frac{1}{2}}v\,{\rm d}x=\int_{\Omega}v\mathcal{L}u\,{\rm d}x\qquad\forall u,v\in H(\Omega)\,.

The main assumption that we need on the operator
\mathcal{L}
is the following weak form of Kato’s inequality: for any convex function
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with
h(0)=0
and any function
u\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\Omega)
with
h(u)\in H^{*}(\Omega)
, it holds that (recall
\Phi_{1}\geq 0
is the first eigenfunction of
\mathcal{L}
)

\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}\left[h(u)\right]\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}h^{\prime}(u)\mathcal{L}u\,\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\,.

(K)

Remark 1
.

We are not aware of any positive, linear operator satisfying (
G1
) which does not satisfy Kato’s inequality. Indeed, for the classical Dirichlet Laplacian,
\mathcal{L}=(-\Delta_{\Omega})
, Kato’s inequality holds pointwise since
h^{\prime\prime}(r)\geq 0
when
h
is a convex function. For nonlocal operators of the form

\mathcal{L}u(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left(u(x)-u(y)\right)\mathbb{K}(x,y)\,{\rm d}y+B(x)u(x)\,,

(13)

with
\mathbb{K}(x,y)\geq 0
and
B(x)\geq 0
, Kato’s inequality also holds in a stronger form than (
K
) as it can be seen in the Appendix of
[
14
]
.

Notations.

We will write the
L^{p}
norm in
\Omega
as
\|f\|_{p}:=(\int_{\Omega}|f|^{p}\,{\rm d}x)^{1/p}
, but for weighted norms with the first eigenvalue
\Phi_{1}
of the operator
\mathcal{L}
we use
\|f\|_{L^{p}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)}:=\left(\int_{\Omega}|f|^{p}\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\right)^{1/p}
in the space
L^{p}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega):=\{f\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\Omega):\|f\|_{L^{p}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)}<+\infty\}
. The Heaviside function will be denoted by
\mbox{sign}_{+}(f):=\max\{0,f\}
, and positive and negative part as
(f)_{+}:=\max\{0,f\}
and
(f)_{-}=\max\{0,-f\}
, respectively. We will use
f\asymp g
when there exists
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that
c_{1}g(x)\leq f(x)\leq c_{2}g(x)
for a.e.
x
.

1.3
Examples of
\mathcal{L}

The assumptions that we state above describe a wide class of operators of local and nonlocal type. Let us enumerate some of the most important ones here. For a more extended list of examples we refer to
[
14
]
and
[
19
, Section 3]
.

The Dirichlet Laplacian.
We can consider the classical Dirichlet Laplacian
\mathcal{L}=(-\Delta_{\Omega})
with
s=1
, where the Green function
\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}
is bounded by the classical Newtonian potential. For this operator (
G2
) is satisfied with
\gamma=1
.

The main examples of nonlocal operators on bounded domains are the three fractional Laplacians that we state below. Note that, on the whole space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the three definitions are equivalent, but on bounded domains they represent entirely different nonlocal operators, as highlighted by the behaviour of the first eigenfunction for each of them. Indeed, from a probabilistic standpoint, these operators are the infinitesimal generator of distinct Lévy processes.

The Restricted Fractional Laplacian (RFL).
We define this fractional Laplacian operator acting on a bounded domain by using the integral representation on the whole space in terms of a hypersingular kernel, namely,

(-\Delta_{|\Omega})^{s}f(x)=c_{N,s}\;\mbox{P.V. }\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,{\rm d}y\,,

(14)

where
s\in(0,1)
and we restrict the operator to functions that are zero outside
\Omega
. For this operator (
G2
) is satisfied with
\gamma=s
.

Spectral Fractional Laplacian (SFL).
Consider the so-called
spectral definition
of the fractional power
s\in(0,1)
of the classical Dirichlet Laplacian
\Delta_{\Omega}
on
\Omega
defined by a formula in terms of semigroups, namely

(-\Delta_{\Omega})^{s}f(x)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(-s)}\int_{0}^{\infty}(e^{t\Delta_{\Omega}}f(x)-f(x))\frac{\,{\rm d}t}{t^{1+s}}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{s}\hat{f}_{j}\phi_{j},

(15)

where
(\lambda_{k},\phi_{k})_{k\geq 1}
are the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the classical Dirichlet Laplacian on
\Omega
,
\hat{f}=\int_{\Omega}f(x)\phi_{k}(x)\,{\rm d}x
, and
\|\phi_{k}\|_{2}=1
. Since the eigenvalues are the same as in the Dirichlet Laplacian, (
G2
) is fulfilled with
\gamma=1
.

Censored Fractional Laplacian (CFL).

This third kind of Fractional Laplacian was introduced in
[
22
]
, in connection with censored stable processes. The operator takes the form:

\mathcal{L}f(x)=\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{\Omega}\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{|x-y|^{N+2s}}\,{\rm d}y\,,\qquad\mbox{with }\frac{1}{2}<s<1\,.

(16)

The restriction on the parameter
s
is due to the lack of trace inequality for
s\in(0,1/2]
. This operator satisfies (
G2
) with
\gamma=2s-1
.

Other examples:
As explained in
[
19
, Section 3]
, our theory may also be
applied to: i) the operators above with uniformly elliptic,
C^{1}
and symmetric coefficients; ii) sums of two fractional operators; iii) the sum of the classical Laplacian and a nonlocal operator kernel; iv) Schrödinger equations for non-symmetric diffusions; v) gradient perturbation of restricted
fractional Laplacians.

Functional spaces associated to
\mathcal{L}
We recall that the three fractional Laplacians on domains, (
14
), (
15
) and (
16
), can be expressed as in (
13
), see Section
1.3
and
[
19
]
for more details and examples. Moreover, for (
14
), (
15
) and (
16
), it is possible to identify
H(\Omega)
and
H^{*}(\Omega)
with the standard fractional Sobolev spaces as follows: Let
s\in(0,1]

H(\Omega)=\begin{cases}H_{0}^{s}(\Omega),&\mbox{if }s>1/2\\
H^{1/2}_{00}(\Omega),&\mbox{if }s=1/2\\
H^{s}(\Omega),&\mbox{if }s<1/2,\end{cases}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad H^{*}(\Omega)=\begin{cases}H^{-s}(\Omega),&\mbox{if }s>1/2\\
\left(H^{1/2}_{00}(\Omega)\right)^{*},&\mbox{if }s=1/2\\
H^{-s}(\Omega),&\mbox{if }s<1/2.\end{cases}

Recall that
H_{0}^{s}(\Omega)=H^{s}(\Omega)
when
s\leq 1/2
, and hence the dual coincide. If
s=1/2
, then
H^{s}_{00}(\Omega)\subsetneq H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
, which implies
H^{-s}(\Omega)\subsetneq\left(H^{s}_{00}(\Omega)\right)^{*}
. See
[
16
, Sec. 7.7]
for more details. This identification holds for any operator
\mathcal{L}
with discrete spectrum such that
0<\lambda_{k+1}/\lambda_{k}\leq\Lambda_{0}
for some
\Lambda_{0}>0
.

2
Discretized problem

In this section we will provide some properties of the discretization of (
CPME
) and then deduce the comparison principle. Several discretizations of the Caputo derivative have been proposed through the years, see for instance
[
29
,
40
,
41
]
. We choose the one in
[
40
]
based on a deconvolution scheme, since it is enough to obtain the convergence results needed in this work.

Definition 2.1
.

[
40
, Section 3.]

Let
\tau=T/N>0
be the time-step and
t_{k}=k\tau
with
0\leq k\leq N
points of the interval
(0,T)
.
Let us consider the Euler implicit scheme for (
CPME
):

\begin{cases}(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}U)_{k}=-\mathcal{L}U_{k}^{m}&\mbox{in}\quad\Omega,\quad\forall k\geq 0\,,\\
U_{0}=u_{0}&\mbox{in}\quad\Omega\,,\end{cases}

(17)

where

\left(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}U\right)_{k}:=\frac{1}{\tau^{\alpha}}\left(c_{k}^{k}(U_{k}-U_{0})+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}c_{i}(U_{k}-U_{k-i})\right)\,,

(18)

with
c_{k}^{k}:=\sum_{i=k}^{\infty}c_{i}>0
and
c_{i}>0
are defined recursively by

c_{i}:=\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\left((i+1-j)^{\alpha}-(i-j)^{\alpha}\right)c_{j}\qquad\mbox{for any }i\geq 1\,,

with
c_{0}:=\Gamma(1+\alpha)=c_{k}^{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}c_{i}
. Equivalently, we also will use

(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}X)_{k}=\frac{1}{\tau^{\alpha}}\left(c_{0}(X_{k+1}-X_{0})-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}c_{i}(X_{k-i}-X_{0})\right)\,.

(19)

One of the drawbacks of the Caputo derivative is the lack of a chain rule. However it is noted in
[
1
,
55
]
, that if the kernel
k
belongs to
H^{1}([0,T])
, then the “chain rule inequality” below holds for convex functions
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, see Lemma
A.4
. Namely, we have that

\partial_{t}[k*(h(f)-h(f_{0}))](t)\leq h^{\prime}(f(t))\partial_{t}[k*(f-f_{0})](t)\,.

Another remarkable difference with the classical time derivative is the fact that
D^{\alpha}_{t}f(t)\leq 0
only implies
f(t)\leq f(0)
, but not the monotonicity
f(t)\leq f(t_{0})
for all
t\geq t_{0}
.

Also the discrete Caputo derivative (
18
) satisfies the monotonicity formula and the chain rule inequality:

Proposition 2.2
.

[
40
, Theorem 3.1.]

i)

(Chain rule inequality). Let
h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a convex function in
C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. Then, for every sequence of functions
\{U_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}\subset L^{1}_{loc}(\Omega)
it holds that

(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}\,h(U))_{k}\leq h^{\prime}(U_{k})\,(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}U)_{k}\qquad\mbox{a.e. in }\Omega\,.

ii)

(Nonpositive discrete Caputo derivative). Assume that a sequence
\{X_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}\subset\mathbb{R}
satisfies
(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}X)_{k}\leq 0

for every
k\geq 1
, then

X_{k}\leq X_{0}\qquad\forall k\geq 1\,.

Proof.

i)

Since
h\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is convex , we have that
h^{\prime}(x)(x-y)\geq(h(x)-h(y))
for every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
. Then,

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(U_{k})(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}u)_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau^{\alpha}}\left(h^{\prime}(U_{k})(U_{k}-U_{0})c_{k}^{k}+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}h^{\prime}(U_{k})(U_{k}-U_{k-i})c_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\tau^{\alpha}}\left(c_{k}^{k}\left(h(U_{k})-h(U_{0})\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}c_{i}(h(U_{k})-h(U_{k-i}))\right).

ii)

We prove the result by induction. For the case
k=1
is trivial since
\tau,c_{0}>0
. Now, assume that
X_{k}\leq X_{0}
for every
k=1,\dots n
, and let us prove it for
k=n+1
. From (
19
) and
(D^{\alpha}_{c}X)_{k+1}\leq 0
we know that

(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}X)_{k+1}=\frac{1}{\tau^{\alpha}}\left((c_{0}(X_{k+1}-X_{0})-\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}(X_{k+1-i}-X_{0})\right)\leq 0\,,

which by the induction hypothesis give us

c_{0}(X_{k+1}-X_{0})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}(X_{k+1-i}-X_{0})\leq 0\,,

since
c_{i}>0
for every
i\geq 0
.

∎

2.1
Existence and uniqueness for the discretized problem

The method used to prove existence of solutions for (
17
) is a time-fractional version of the classical minimizing movements scheme
[
40
,
41
]
. As in the case of classical time derivative
[
14
]
, the energy used to construct the gradient flow solutions is

{\mathcal{E}}(u):=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{1+m}\int_{\Omega}|u(x)|^{1+m}\,{\rm d}x,&\mbox{if }u\in L^{1+m}(\Omega),\\
+\infty,&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Notice that this energy functional is convex and lower semicontinuous in the Hilbert space
H^{*}(\Omega)
, which is the (topological) dual space of
H(\Omega)
, the domain of the quadratic form associated to
\mathcal{L}
,

H(\Omega)=\left\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega)\;:\;\|u\|^{2}_{H(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}u\mathcal{L}u\,{\rm d}x<+\infty\right\}\,.

Let us recall that the space
H^{*}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space endowed with the scalar product and the norm

\langle u,v\rangle_{H^{*}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}u\mathcal{L}^{-1}v\,{\rm d}x\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\|u\|^{2}_{H^{*}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}u\mathcal{L}^{-1}u\,{\rm d}x\,.

The classical gradient flow theory in Hilbert spaces – in particular, in
H^{*}(\Omega)
– provides solutions of

-\partial_{t}u(t)\in\partial{\mathcal{E}}(u(t))\qquad\mbox{for a.e. }t>0\,,

(20)

where the subdifferential of
{\mathcal{E}}
is defined by

\xi\in\partial{\mathcal{E}}(u)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad{\mathcal{E}}(v)-{\mathcal{E}}(u)\geq\langle\xi,v-u\rangle_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\qquad\forall v\in H^{*}(\Omega)\,.

In our case, note that (
20
) is related to the classical PME on domains; note in fact that for any
u
such that

u\in D(\partial{\mathcal{E}})=\{v\in L^{1+m}(\Omega)\cap H^{*}(\Omega)\;:\;|v|^{m-1}v\in H(\Omega)\}\,,

we have, in the
H^{*}(\Omega)
sense,

\xi\in\partial{\mathcal{E}}(u)\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\mathcal{L}^{-1}\xi=|u|^{m-1}u\,.

In the fractional gradient flow theory, the classical time derivative
\partial_{t}u
in (
20
) is substituted by a fractional time derivative, such as the Caputo derivative,
D^{\alpha}_{t}u
. The well-posedness of fractional gradient flow solutions in Hilbert spaces has been proven in
[
1
,
40
,
41
]
. The first step in this approach is to provide the existence and uniqueness of the time discretized equation (
17
), that we stated below.

Theorem 2.3
.

[
40
,
41
]

Let
m>0
and assume (
G1
). For any
U_{0}\in L^{1+m}(\Omega)\cap H^{*}(\Omega)
fixed there exists a unique sequence of functions
\{U_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}\subset D(\partial{\mathcal{E}})
satisfying

-(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}U)_{k}\in\partial{\mathcal{E}}(U_{k})\qquad\forall k\geq 0\,,

(21)

which is (
17
) in the
H^{*}
-sense, namely, for every
k\geq 0

\int_{\Omega}(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}U)_{k}\mathcal{L}^{-1}\varphi\,{\rm d}x=-\int_{\Omega}U_{k}^{m}\varphi\,{\rm d}x\qquad\forall\varphi\in H^{*}(\Omega)\,.

(22)

Moreover,

{\mathcal{E}}(U_{k})\leq{\mathcal{E}}(U_{0})\qquad\forall k\geq 1\,.

(23)

The idea of the proof is the same of the nonlinear theory of Brezis for gradient flows
[
21
]
, see also Komura
[
39
]
and the excellent lecture notes
[
4
]
. Due to the convexity of
{\mathcal{E}}
we can construct recursively the next function
U_{k+1}
with
k\geq 0
by the minimizing movements scheme, namely, for any
k\geq 0

U_{k+1}:=\underset{u\in H^{*}(\Omega)}{\mbox{argmin}}\left[\frac{1}{2\tau^{\alpha}}\left(c_{k+1}^{k+1}\|u-U_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{*}(\Omega)}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\|u-U_{n-i}\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)+{\mathcal{E}}(u)\right]\,.

(24)

By the lower semicontinuity and convexity of the energy functional the sequence
U_{k}
is unique and satisfies (
21
). The decay of the energy (
23
) follows from Proposition
2.2
, namely,

\displaystyle(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}{\mathcal{E}}(U))_{k+1}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}U^{m}_{k+1}(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}U)_{k+1}\,{\rm d}x=-\int_{\Omega}U_{k+1}^{m}\,\mathcal{L}U^{m}_{k+1}\,{\rm d}x=-\|U_{k+1}^{m}\|^{2}_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\leq 0\,.

2.2
Discrete Comparison Principle

The main result of this section is the proof of the comparison principle for the discrete solutions obtained in Theorem
2.3
. For this purpose, we first prove a T-contractivity estimate in the Banach space
L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)
. Recall that assumption (
G2
) implies that the operator
\mathcal{L}
has a discrete spectrum
(\lambda_{k},\Phi_{k})_{k\geq 1}
with
\Phi_{k}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Indeed, eigenfunctions are classical in the interior, i.e.
C^{2s+\gamma}
, whenever the operator allows it, and have sharp boundary regularity
C^{\gamma}(\overline{\Omega})
. Moreover,

\underline{\kappa}\;\mbox{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{\gamma}\leq\Phi_{1}(x)\leq\overline{\kappa}\;\mbox{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{\gamma}\,,

see
[
11
, Theorem 7.1. and 7.2.]
for more details.

Let us remark that for nonnegative functions
u
the belong to
H^{*}(\Omega)
also belongs to
L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)
:

\|u\|_{L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)}=\int_{\Omega}u\,\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}^{-1/2}u\,\mathcal{L}^{1/2}\Phi_{1}\leq\lambda_{1}^{1/2}\|u\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)},

(25)

since
u\geq 0
and
\|\Phi_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=1
.

The theorem below follows the formal proof of the classical T-contractivity estimate in
L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}

[
14
, Theorem 2.4.]
and the rigorous techniques used to show the
L^{1}
-contractivity for the linear equation with Caputo derivative
[
55
, Theorem 7.1.]
. Both techniques require extra regularity on the time derivative, more precisely
\partial_{t}u\in L^{1}((0,T):L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega))
or
D^{\alpha}_{t}u\in L^{\infty}((0,T):L^{1}(\Omega))
, respectively. Since we only expect, a-priori, that
D^{\alpha}_{t}u\in L^{2}((0,T):H^{*}(\Omega))
, we first prove T-contractivity at the discrete level, and this allows to show comparison principle as
\tau\to 0
.

Theorem 2.4
.

(Discrete T-contractivity in
L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)
). Let
m>0
and assume (
G1
) and (
K
). Consider
\{U_{k}\}_{k\geq 0},\{V_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}\subset L^{1+m}(\Omega)\cap H^{*}(\Omega)
two sequence of solutions for (
17
). Then,

\|\left(U_{k}-V_{k}\right)_{+}\|_{L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)}\leq\|(U_{0}-V_{0})_{+}\|_{L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)}\qquad\forall k\geq 1\,.

Proof.

Notice that
U_{k},V_{k}\in L^{1+m}(\Omega)\subset L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)
for every
k\geq 0
, since
\Phi_{1}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Therefore, from the definition of discrete Caputo derivative, we obtain

(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}U)_{k},\;(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}V)_{k}\in L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)\qquad\forall k\geq 1\,.

We will show that
\left(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}\|(U-V)_{+}\|_{L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)}\right)_{k}\leq 0
for every
k\geq 1
and then apply Proposition
2.2
.
For this purpose, let us consider
h_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with
\varepsilon>0
definde by

h_{\varepsilon}(x)=\sqrt{(x_{+})^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}-\varepsilon,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}\,,

which is a convex and smooth approximation of
(x)_{+}
. Moreover,
|h_{\varepsilon}(x)|\leq|x|
and
|h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(x)|\leq 1
since

h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(x)=\frac{(x)_{+}}{\sqrt{x^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}}}.

Next, we test equation (
22
)
for
U_{k}
and
V_{k}
with
\mathcal{L}(h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(U_{k}^{m}-V_{k}^{m})\Phi_{1})\in H^{*}(\Omega)
; after subtracting the resulting equations, we get

\int_{\Omega}\left(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}(U-V)\right)_{k}h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(U^{m}_{k}-V^{m}_{k})\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}(U^{m}_{k}-V^{m}_{k})h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(U_{k}^{m}-V_{k}^{m})\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\,.

(26)

Using the convexity, the nonnegativity of
h_{\varepsilon}
, and Kato’s inequality (
K
), we bound the right hand side as

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}(U^{m}_{k}-V^{m}_{k})\,h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(U_{k}^{m}-V_{k}^{m})\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq-\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{L}\left[h_{\varepsilon}(U_{k}^{m}-V_{k}^{m})\right]\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{1}\int_{\Omega}\left(h_{\varepsilon}(U^{m}_{k}-V^{m}_{k})\right)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\leq 0\,,

since
\Phi_{1}
is the first eigenfunction and
\Phi_{1}\geq 0
. Thus, we deduce from (
26
) and Proposition
2.2
that

\displaystyle\left(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}\int_{\Omega}h_{\varepsilon}(U_{k}-V_{k})\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\right)_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}\left(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}(U-V)\right)_{k}h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(U_{k}-V_{k})\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}(U-V)\right)_{k}h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(U^{m}_{k}-V^{m}_{k})\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}(U-V))_{k}\left(h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(U_{k}-V_{k})-h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(U^{m}_{k}-V^{m}_{k})\right)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq I_{\varepsilon}\,,

where

I_{\varepsilon}:=\int_{\Omega}\left|(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}(U-V))_{k}\right|\left|h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(U_{k}-V_{k})-h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(U^{m}_{k}-V^{m}_{k})\right|\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\,.

To prove that
I_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow 0
when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, we use dominated convergence theorem: note that
(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}(U-V))_{k}\in L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)
for every
k\geq 1
,
|h_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(x)-h_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(y)|\leq 2
for every
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
, and
h^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}\rightarrow h^{\prime}
pointwise. On the other hand
|h_{\varepsilon}(x)|\leq|x|
, and therefore

\left|\left(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}h_{\varepsilon}(U-V)\right)_{k}\right|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k}\lambda_{j}\left(|U_{j}|+|V_{j}|\right)\in L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)\,

for some
\lambda_{j}>0
. Hence, the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
yields

\left(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}\int_{\Omega}h_{\varepsilon}(U_{k}-V_{k})\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\right)_{k}\rightarrow\left(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}\int_{\Omega}(U_{k}-V_{k})_{+}\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\right)_{k}\qquad\forall k\geq 1\,,

since
h_{\varepsilon}(x)\rightarrow(x)_{+}
. Therefore,

\left(D^{\alpha}_{\tau}\int_{\Omega}(U_{k}-V_{k})_{+}\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\right)_{k}\leq 0\qquad\forall k\geq 1\,,

and the result follows by Proposition
2.2
.
∎

Using the theorem above we prove the following discrete comparison principle:

Corollary 2.5
.

(Discrete Comparison Principle).
Let
m>0
and assume (
G1
) and (
K
). Consider two sequence of solutions of (
17
), namely,
\{U_{k}\}_{k\geq 0},\{V_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}\subset L^{1+m}(\Omega)\cap H^{*}(\Omega)
. Then, if
U_{0}(x)\leq V_{0}(x)
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
, it holds that for every
k\geq 1

U_{k}(x)\leq V_{k}(x)\qquad\mbox{for a.e. }x\in\Omega\,.

Moreover, if
V_{0}\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
then
V_{k}\geq 0
a.e. in
\Omega
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.4
we know that for every
k\geq 1

\|(U_{k}-V_{k})_{+}\|_{L^{1}_{\Phi 1}(\Omega)}\leq\|(U_{0}-V_{0})_{+}\|_{L^{1}_{\Phi 1}(\Omega)}\,.

Since
U_{0}\leq V_{0}
a.e. in
\Omega
, we have that
\|(U_{0}-V_{0})_{+}\|_{L^{1}_{\Phi 1}(\Omega)}=0
. Hence,

\|(U_{k}-V_{k})_{+}\|_{L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)}=0\,\qquad\mbox{ for all }k\geq 1\,.

The comparison principle follows since
\Phi_{1}(x)\geq\underline{\kappa}\;\mbox{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)
. For the nonnegativity of
V_{k}
we just take
U_{k}=0
for every
k\geq 0
, which is the trivial solution.
∎

3
Continuous problem

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
. For this purpose, we recall the result in
[
40
]
about the convergence of the discrete solutions of (
21
) to
H^{*}
-solutions of (
CPME
). In addition, we recollect some other results about existence, uniqueness and estimates for solutions exhibited in
[
1
]
. At the end of this section, we provide a proof of the comparison principle for the
H^{*}
-solutions by using the discrete comparison principle of Theorem
2.5
and the convergence of the discrete scheme. The statements in
[
1
]
and
[
40
]
are in the general framework of subdifferentials of
\lambda
-convex energy functionals in Hilbert spaces; here we adjust these statements for the Porous Medium equation.

3.1
Existence and uniqueness

In
[
40
]
, the authors show that solutions of the implicit Euler scheme (
21
) converge with certain rates to the unique
H^{*}
-solution, following the steps of the classical theory of gradient flow of Brezis
[
21
]
and Komura
[
39
]
. The theorem below will be crucial in the proof of the comparison principle.

Theorem 3.1
.

[
40
, Theorem 5.10]

Let
m>0
and assume (
G1
). Then, for every initial datum
u_{0}\in L^{1+m}(\Omega)\cap H^{-1}(\Omega)
there exits a unique
H^{*}
solution
u=u(t,x)
of (
CPME
). Moreover, it satisfies that

\displaystyle\|u(t+\tau)-u(t)\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\leq C|\tau|^{\alpha/2}\qquad\forall\tau>0\,,

(27)

and the following estimates for the error with respect to the discrete solution (
24
) holds,

\sup\limits_{k}\|U_{k}-u(t_{k})\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\leq C(u_{0},T)\;\tau^{\alpha/4}\qquad\forall\tau>0\,.

(28)

Convergence of the discrete scheme in a more general setting has been proven very recently by Li and Salgado in.
[
41
, Theorem 4.5.]
.

A different proof of existence and uniqueness of
H^{*}
-solution of (
CPME
) is provided by Akagi in
[
1
]
. Akagi approximates the problem by adding a classical time derivative
\varepsilon\,\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}
. After proving the existence for the equation
\varepsilon\partial_{t}u_{\varepsilon}+D^{\alpha}_{t}u_{\varepsilon}=-\mathcal{L}u_{\varepsilon}^{m}
using the theory of maximal monotone operators, he passes to the limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
thanks to uniform estimates. We will use some of these estimates in the proof of smoothing effects. Below we recall the most important ones:

Theorem 3.2
.

[
1
, Theorem 2.3. and 6.2]

Let
m>0
and assume (
G1
). Let
u_{0}\in L^{1+m}(\Omega)\cap H^{-1}(\Omega)
. Then, there exits a unique
H^{*}
solution
u=u(t,x)
of (
CPME
). This solution
u
satisfies:

\begin{split}u\in L^{\infty}((0,T):H^{*}(\Omega)),\quad|u|^{m-1}u\in L^{2}((0,T):H(\Omega))\,,\\
\ell*\left\|D^{\alpha}_{t}u\right\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}^{2}\in L^{\infty}((0,T))\qquad\qquad\,,\end{split}

(29)

and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}(u(t))\leq{\mathcal{E}}(u_{0})\qquad t>0\,.

(30)

Moreover, contractivity in
H^{*}(\Omega)
holds, that is, if
u
and
v
are
H^{*}
solutions with initial datum
u_{0},v_{0}\in L^{1+m}(\Omega)\cap H^{-1}(\Omega)
, respectively, then

\|u(t)-v(t)\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\leq\|u_{0}-v_{0}\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\qquad\;\mbox{for a.e. }\;t\in(0,T)\,.

(31)

Since there is uniqueness of
H^{*}
-solution, Theorem
3.1
and Theorem
3.2
describe the same solution for a fixed
u_{0}\in L^{1+m}(\Omega)\cap H^{*}(\Omega)
. Despite the difference in the approximation procedures, the limit solution coincide. Hence, we can use the estimates from both theorems simultaneously.

3.2
Comparison Principle

Now, we are in a position to prove the comparison principle for the
H^{*}
-solutions of (
CPME
). Let us recall the statement which holds for every
m>0
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
m>0
and assume (
G1
) and (
K
). Let
u
and
v
be two
H^{*}
-solutions of (
CPME
) with initial data
u_{0},v_{0}\in L^{1+m}(\Omega)\cap H^{*}(\Omega)
, respectively. If
u_{0}\leq v_{0}
a.e. in
\Omega
, then
\forall t>0

u(t)\leq v(t)\qquad\mbox{ a.e. in }\Omega\,.

Proof.
The idea is to inherit the comparison principle from the discrete comparison principle shown in Corollary
2.5
. Let us consider two solutions
u,v
of (
CPME
) with initial data
u_{0},v_{0}\in L^{1+m}(\Omega)\cap H^{*}(\Omega)
respectively, satisfying

u_{0}(x)\leq v_{0}(x)\qquad\mbox{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega\,.

(32)

We proceed by contradiction. Assume that there exist
t_{0}>0
and
\widetilde{\Omega}\subset\Omega
with
|\widetilde{\Omega}|>0
such that

u(t_{0},x)-v(t_{0},x)>0\qquad\mbox{ for a.e. }x\in\widetilde{\Omega}\,.

(33)

Then, we can define the sequence of time indexes
\{n_{\tau}\}_{\tau>0}
such that for all
\tau>0
we have
t_{n_{\tau}}=\tau\,n_{\tau}
and

t_{0}\in[t_{n_{\tau}},t_{n_{\tau}+1})\,.

Let us consider the respective discrete approximations of
u
and
v
as in (
24
), namely, the sequences
\{U_{k}\}_{k\geq 0},\,\{V_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}\subset L^{1+m}(\Omega)\cap H^{*}(\Omega)
with
U_{0}=u_{0}
and
V_{0}=v_{0}
. Notice that from (
32
) and the discrete comparison principle in Theorem
2.5
, it holds that for every
k\geq 0

U_{k}(x)-V_{k}(x)=-\left(U_{k}(x)-V_{k}(x)\right)_{-}\qquad\mbox{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega\,.

Hence, we deduce from (
28
) that

\displaystyle\|(u(t_{n_{\tau}})-v(t_{n_{\tau}}))+\left(U_{n_{\tau}}-V_{n_{\tau}}\right)_{-}\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\rightarrow 0\qquad\mbox{as }\tau\rightarrow 0\,.

(34)

Note that by (
27
), we also know that

\|(u(t_{n_{\tau}})-v(t_{n_{\tau}}))-(u(t_{0})-v(t_{0}))\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\rightarrow 0\qquad\mbox{as }\tau\rightarrow 0\,,

therefore each of the terms
u(t_{n_{\tau}})-v(t_{n_{\tau}})
and
U_{n_{\tau}}-V_{n_{\tau}}
in (
34
) converge (strongly in
H^{*}(\Omega)
) to
u(t_{0})-v(t_{0})\in L^{1+m}(\Omega)
.

In addition, the control of the energy in (
23
) and (
30
) implies the following uniform bound of the
L^{1+m}
-norm

\|(u(t_{n_{\tau}})-v(t_{n_{\tau}}))+\left(U_{n_{\tau}}-V_{n_{\tau}}\right)_{-}\|_{L^{1+m}}\leq 2\left(\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1+m}}+\|v_{0}\|_{L^{1+m}}\right)\qquad\forall\tau>0\,.

Thus, the sequence
\left\{(u(t_{n_{\tau}})-v(t_{n_{\tau}}))+\left(U_{n_{\tau}}-V_{n_{\tau}}\right)_{-}\right\}_{\tau>0}
is weakly compact in
L^{1+m}(\Omega)
and by (
34
) and uniqueness of the weak-strong limit, we obtain that up to a subsequence

(u(t_{n_{\tau}})-v(t_{n_{\tau}}))+\left(U_{n_{\tau}}-V_{n_{\tau}}\right)_{-}\rightharpoonup 0\qquad\mbox{ in }L^{1+m}(\Omega)\,,

(35)

or equivalently,

\int_{\Omega}\left[(u(t_{n_{\tau}})-v(t_{n_{\tau}}))+\left(U_{n_{\tau}}-V_{n_{\tau}}\right)_{-}\right]\psi\,{\rm d}x\rightarrow 0\qquad\forall\psi\in L^{\frac{1+m}{m}}(\Omega)\,.

(36)

We proceed similarly for the sequence
\left\{(u(t_{n_{\tau}})-v(t_{n_{\tau}}))-(u(t_{0})-v(t_{0}))\right\}_{\tau>0}
and thanks to

\|(u(t_{n_{\tau}})-v(t_{n_{\tau}}))-(u(t_{0})-v(t_{0}))\|_{1+m}\leq 2\left(\|u_{0}\|_{1+m}+\|v_{0}\|_{1+m}\right)\qquad\forall\tau>0\,,

we obtain

\int_{\Omega}\left[(u(t_{n_{\tau}})-v(t_{n_{\tau}}))-\left(u(t_{0})-v(t_{0})\right)\right]\psi\,{\rm d}x\rightarrow 0\qquad\forall\psi\in L^{\frac{1+m}{m}}(\Omega)\,.

(37)

Let us choose
\widetilde{\psi}(x):=\chi_{\widetilde{\Omega}}(x)\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
to be the characteristic function of
\widetilde{\Omega}
, in order to have

\displaystyle A_{\tau}:=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left[(u(t_{n_{\tau}})-v(t_{n_{\tau}}))+(U_{n_{\tau}}-V_{n_{\tau}})_{-}\right]\widetilde{\psi}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left(u(t_{n_{\tau}})-v(t_{n_{\tau}}))-(u(t_{0})-v(t_{0})\right)\widetilde{\psi}\,{\rm d}x+\int_{\Omega}\left(u(t_{0})-v(t_{0})\right)\widetilde{\psi}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}\left(u(t_{n_{\tau}})-v(t_{n_{\tau}}))-(u(t_{0})-v(t_{0})\right)\widetilde{\psi}\,{\rm d}x}\limits_{B_{\tau}\rightarrow 0}+\underbrace{\int_{\widetilde{\Omega}}\left(u(t_{0})-v(t_{0})\right)\,{\rm d}x}\limits_{C>0}\,.

Finally, we obtain a contradiction, since (
36
) and (
37
) implies
A_{\tau}\rightarrow 0
and
B_{\tau}\rightarrow 0
when
\tau\rightarrow 0
, but from the contradiction hypothesis (
33
) we know that
C>0
and it does not depend on
\tau
.

\square

4
Smoothing effects

The aim of this section is to prove the
L^{p}-L^{\infty}
smoothing effects in Theorem
1.4
. We divide the proof in several steps. First, in Proposition
4.2
we use the dual formulation of the problem and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus for Caputo derivative to obtain a fundamental pointwise formula. Then, in Theorem
4.3
we obtain a time monotonicity formulae for the function
t^{\frac{\alpha}{m-1}}u(t)
. The time monotonicity formulae we obtain are consistent with the case
\alpha=1
, since they depend only on the time scaling and the comparison principle. Next, we apply the time monotonicity formulae in the fundamental pointwise formula in order to obtain the upper bounds of
u^{m}(t,x)
, as described in Proposition
4.4
. We conclude the proof of Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
4.6
using assumptions (
G1
) and (
G2
), respectively, and Hölder’s inequality.

Before starting with the first proposition of this section, let us present a useful lemma about the differentiability of the time convolution.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
f\in L^{1}((0,T))
and
g\in W^{1,2}((0,T))
with
g(0)=0
, then
f*g\in W^{1,2}((0,T))
and

\frac{\rm d}{\,{\rm d}t}(f*g)=\left(f*\frac{{\rm d}g}{\,{\rm d}t}\right)\,.

Proof.

We obtain that
f*g\in L^{2}((0,T))
by Youngs inequality. For any
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T])
let us consider

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}(f*g)(t)\varphi^{\prime}(t)\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{t}f(t-\tau)g(\tau)\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)\,{\rm d}\tau\,{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\tau}^{T}f(t-\tau)\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)\,{\rm d}t\right)g(\tau)\,{\rm d}\tau.

(38)

Now, we show that for a.e.
\tau\in(0,T)

\int_{\tau}^{T}f(t-\tau)\varphi^{\prime}(\tau)\,{\rm d}t=\int_{0}^{T-\tau}f(t)\varphi^{\prime}(\tau+t)\,{\rm d}t=\left(\int_{0}^{T-\tau}f(t)\varphi(\tau+t)\,{\rm d}t\right)^{\prime}=\left(\int_{\tau}^{T}f(t-\tau)\varphi(\tau)\,{\rm d}t\right)^{\prime}.

For this purpose, let us study the incremental quotient

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}\left[\int_{0}^{T-\tau-h}f(t)\varphi(t+\tau+h)\,{\rm d}t-\int_{0}^{T-\tau}f(t)\varphi(t+\tau)\,{\rm d}t\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T-\tau}f(t)\chi_{[0,T-\tau-h]}(t)\frac{\varphi(t+\tau+h)-\varphi(t+\tau)}{h}\,{\rm d}t-\frac{1}{h}\int_{T-\tau-h}^{T-\tau}f(t)\varphi(t+\tau)\,{\rm d}t.

The first term converge by dominated convergence theorem when
h
goes to 0. For the second term we use Lebesgue differentiation theorem and the fact that
\varphi(T)=0
to prove that it converge to 0 as
h
goes to 0. Hence, we conclude from (
38
) and integration by parts that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}(f*g)(t)\varphi^{\prime}(t)\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\left(\int_{\tau}^{T}f(t-\tau)\varphi(t)\right)^{\prime}g(\tau)\,{\rm d}\tau=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\tau}^{T}f(t-\tau)\varphi(t)g^{\prime}(\tau)\,{\rm d}\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}(f*g^{\prime})(t)\varphi(t)\,{\rm d}t,

since
g(0)=0
.
∎

Taking in to account estimate (
29
) from the Existence Theorem
3.2
, we make the dual formulation of the problem rigourous and we prove the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus for Caputo in the following proposition.

Proposition 4.2

(Fundamental pointwise formula)
.

Let
u
be a
H^{*}
-solution of (
CPME
) with

\ell*\|D^{\alpha}_{t}u\|^{2}_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\in L^{\infty}((0,T))\,.

(39)

Then, for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
and a.e.
x_{0}\in\Omega
it holds that

\int_{\Omega}(u_{0}(x)-u(t,x))\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}(x_{0},x)\,{\rm d}x=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{u^{m}(\tau,x_{0})}{(t-\tau)^{1-\alpha}}\,{\rm d}\tau.

(40)

Proof.

The intuition behind the proof is to formally use the following test function in (
3
),

\psi(\tau,x):=\frac{\chi_{(0,t)}(\tau)}{(t-\tau)^{1-\alpha}}\;\delta_{x_{0}}(x),

and integrate on
[0,T]
. Formally, on the left hand side we would have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}D^{\alpha}_{t}u(\tau,x)\mathcal{L}^{-1}\psi(\tau,x)\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}\tau

\displaystyle=\ell*\left(\int_{\Omega}D^{\alpha}_{t}u\mathcal{L}^{-1}\delta_{x_{0}}\,{\rm d}x\right)=\ell*k*\left(\frac{\rm d}{\,{\rm d}t}\int_{\Omega}(u(t,x)\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}(x_{0},x)\,{\rm d}x\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(u(t,x)-u_{0}(x))\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}(x_{0},x)\,{\rm d}x\,,

and on the right hand side

-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}u^{m}(\tau,x)\psi(\tau,x)\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}\tau=-\int_{0}^{t}\frac{u^{m}(\tau,x_{0})}{(t-\tau)^{1-\alpha}}\,{\rm d}\tau\,.

In order to give a rigorous proof, we split the proof in two steps.

\bullet~
Step 1.
First, we show that for any
\varphi\in H^{*}(\Omega)
we have

\int_{\Omega}(u(t,x)-u_{0}(x))\mathcal{L}^{-1}\varphi(x)\,{\rm d}x=-\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{u^{m}(\tau,x)}{(t-\tau)^{1-\alpha}}\varphi(x)\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}\tau\,,

(41)

by testing the equation with

\tilde{\psi}(\tau,x)=\frac{\chi_{(0,t)}(\tau)}{(t-\tau)^{1-\alpha}}\,\varphi(x)\in L^{1}(0,T;H^{*}(\Omega))\,.

Note that condition (
39
) implies that
\tilde{\psi}
is an admissible test function, and
\ell*\left(D^{\alpha}_{t}u\mathcal{L}^{-1}\varphi\,{\rm d}x\right)\in L^{\infty}(0,T)
, since

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\frac{D^{\alpha}_{t}u(\tau,x)\mathcal{L}^{-1}\varphi(x)}{(t-\tau)^{1-\alpha}}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}\tau\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\varphi\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\|D^{\alpha}_{t}u(\tau)\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}}{(t-\tau)^{1-\alpha}}\,{\rm d}\tau

\displaystyle\leq\|\varphi\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\|D^{\alpha}_{t}u(\tau)\|^{2}_{H^{*}(\Omega)}}{(t-\tau)^{1-\alpha}}\,{\rm d}\tau\right)\left(\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\,{\rm d}\tau}{(t-\tau)^{1-\alpha}}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq t^{\alpha/2}\|\varphi\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\;\left(\ell*\|D^{\alpha}_{t}u\|^{2}_{H^{*}(\Omega)}(t)\right)^{1/2}<+\infty\,.

Hence, applying Fubini’s Theorem and Lemma
4.1
we obtain

\displaystyle\ell*\left(\int_{\Omega}D^{\alpha}_{t}u\mathcal{L}^{-1}\varphi\,{\rm d}x\right)\!(t)\!

\displaystyle=\!\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!\ell*\!\left(\frac{\rm d}{\,{\rm d}t}[k*(u-u_{0})]\right)\!\!(t)\mathcal{L}^{-1}\varphi\,{\rm d}x=\!\int_{\Omega}\!\!\frac{\rm d}{\,{\rm d}t}\left(\ell*[k*(u-u_{0})](t)\right)\mathcal{L}^{-1}\varphi\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\frac{\rm d}{\,{\rm d}t}(1*(u-u_{0})(t))\mathcal{L}^{-1}\varphi\,{\rm d}x=\int_{\Omega}(u(t)-u_{0})\mathcal{L}^{-1}\varphi\,{\rm d}x\,.

\bullet~
Step 2.
Now, we consider the sequence
\varphi_{r}(x)=\frac{\chi_{B_{r}(x_{0})}(x)}{|B_{r}|}\in H^{*}(\Omega)
with
x_{0}\in\Omega
being a Lebesgue point and we pass to the limit in (
41
) when
r\rightarrow 0
.
∎

Next, we want to bound from below the integral on the right hand side of (
40
), by some pointwise value of the solution of
u(t,x_{0})
. Therefore, we prove the two time monotonicity formulae of Theorem
1.3
, depending on whether
0<m<1
or
m>1
. This type of time monotonicity estimates are the analogous of the Benilan-Crandall estimates for the classical problem. In the context of Caputo derivatives, time monotonicity formulae are a surprising consequence of the comparison principle and the time scaling of (
CPME
).

Theorem 4.3

(Time monotonicity formulae)
.

Let
u
be a nonnegative
H^{*}
-solution for (
CPME
) under assumptions of Theorem
1.2
.

1.

If
0<m<1
, then the function

t\mapsto t^{\frac{-\alpha}{1-m}}u(t,x)

is non increasing for any
t>0
and a.e
x\in\Omega
.

2.

If
m>1
, then the function

t\mapsto t^{\frac{\alpha}{m-1}}u(t,x)

is non decreasing for any
t>0
and a.e
x\in\Omega

Proof.

The proof follows by time scaling and the comparison principle of Theorem
1.2
. For any
\lambda>0
, we define the rescaled solution

u_{\lambda}(t,x):=\lambda^{-\frac{\alpha}{1-m}}u(\lambda t,x)

which is a solution to (
CPME
) since

D^{\alpha}_{t}\left(\lambda^{-\frac{\alpha}{1-m}}u(\lambda t)\right)=\lambda^{\alpha-\frac{\alpha}{1-m}}\left(D^{\alpha}_{t}u\right)(\lambda t)=-\lambda^{-\frac{\alpha m}{1-m}}\mathcal{L}u^{m}(\lambda t)=-\mathcal{L}\left(\lambda^{-\frac{\alpha}{1-m}}u(\lambda t)\right)^{m}\,.

Case
0<m<1
:
Let us choose
0<t_{0}<t_{1}
and fix
\lambda=\frac{t_{1}}{t_{0}}\geq 1
, then it holds that

\displaystyle u_{\lambda}(0,x)=\left(\frac{t_{1}}{t_{0}}\right)^{-\frac{\alpha}{1-m}}u_{0}(x)\leq u_{0}(x)\qquad\mbox{a.e.}\quad x\in\Omega\,.

By comparison principle of Theorem
1.2
, we have that
u_{\lambda}(t,x)\leq u(t,x)
for any
t>0
and a.e.
x\in\Omega
. Hence, we can take
t=t_{0}
in order to conclude that

\left(\frac{t_{1}}{t_{0}}\right)^{-\frac{\alpha}{1-m}}u(t_{1},x)\leq u(t_{0},x)\qquad\mbox{for a.e.}\quad x\in\Omega\,.

Case
m>1
:
Let us consider any
0<t_{0}<t_{1}
and fix
\lambda=\frac{t_{0}}{t_{1}}\leq 1
, then

u_{\lambda}(0,x)=\left(\frac{t_{0}}{t_{1}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{m-1}}u_{0}(x)\leq u_{0}(x)\qquad\mbox{a.e.}\quad x\in\Omega\,.

As in the previuos case, we use comparison principle of Theorem
1.2
in order to obtain
u_{\lambda}(t,x)\leq u(t,x)
for any
t>0
and a.e.
x\in\Omega
. Therefore, we choose
t=t_{1}
to get

\left(\frac{t_{0}}{t_{1}}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{m-1}}u(t_{0},x)\leq u(t_{1},x)\qquad\mbox{for a.e.}\quad x\in\Omega\,.

∎

Afterwards, we implement the time monotonicity formulae of Theorem
4.3
in the equality (
40
) in order to obtain the following fundamental upper bounds.

Proposition 4.4

(Fundamental upper bounds)
.

Assume (
G1
) and (
K
) and let
u
be a bounded and nonnegative
H^{*}
-solution for (
CPME
). Then for a.e.
x_{0}\in\Omega
and a.e.
t\in(0,T)
it holds that

1.

If
0<m<1
, then

u^{m}(t,x_{0})\leq\frac{\mathsf{c}}{t^{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}(u_{0}(x)-u(t,x))\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}(x,x_{0})\,{\rm d}x,

(42)

with
\mathsf{c}=\mathsf{c}(m,\alpha)>0
.

2.

If
m>1
, then

u^{m}(t,x_{0})\leq\frac{\mathsf{c}}{t^{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}(u_{0}(x)-u(2t,x))\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}(x,x_{0})\,{\rm d}x,

(43)

with
\mathsf{c}=\mathsf{c}(m,\alpha)>0
.

Proof.

The idea of the proof is to use Benilan-Crandall estimates (
4.3
) on the right hand side of the fundamental integral formula (
40
). Namely, it sufices to prove the following inequality

\mathsf{c}\;t^{\alpha}\,u^{m}(t,x_{0})\leq\int_{0}^{t_{1}}\frac{u^{m}(\tau,x_{0})}{(t_{1}-\tau)^{\alpha}}\,{\rm d}\tau,

(44)

with
\mathsf{c}=\mathsf{c}(m,\alpha)>0
and
t_{1}=t
if
0<m<1
and
t_{1}=2t
for
m>1
.

\bullet~
Case
0<m<1
:
By Theorem
4.3
, we have that

u^{m}(\tau,x_{0})\geq\left(\frac{\tau}{t}\right)^{\frac{\alpha m}{1-m}}u^{m}(t,x_{0})\qquad\mbox{for}\quad 0<\tau<t\,.

Then,

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{u^{m}(\tau,x_{0})}{(t-\tau)^{1-\alpha}}\,{\rm d}\tau&\geq\frac{u^{m}(t,x_{0})}{t^{\frac{\alpha m}{1-m}}}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\tau^{\frac{\alpha m}{1-m}}}{(t-\tau)^{1-\alpha}}\,{\rm d}\tau\\
&=t^{\alpha}u^{m}(t,x_{0})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{r^{\frac{\alpha m}{1-m}}}{(1-r)^{1-\alpha}}{\rm d}r\\
&=B\left(\frac{\alpha m}{1-m}+1,\alpha\right)t^{\alpha}u^{m}(t,x_{0})\,,\end{split}

where we used the change of variable
tr=\tau
and the definition of the beta function
B(z_{1},z_{0})=\int_{0}^{1}\tau^{z_{1}-1}(1-\tau)^{z_{2}-1}{\rm d}\tau
, which is well finite for
z_{1},z_{2}>0
.

\bullet~
Case
m>1
:
By Theorem
4.3
, we have that

u^{m}(\tau)\geq\left(\frac{t}{\tau}\right)^{\frac{\alpha m}{m-1}}u^{m}(t,x_{0})\qquad\mbox{for}\quad 0<t<\tau<2t\,.

Then,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2t}\frac{u^{m}(\tau,x_{0})}{(2t-\tau)^{1-\alpha}}\,{\rm d}\tau

\displaystyle\geq\int_{t}^{2t}\frac{u^{m}(\tau,x_{0})}{(2t-\tau)^{1-\alpha}}\,{\rm d}\tau

\displaystyle\geq t^{\frac{\alpha(m)}{m-1}}u^{m}(t,x_{0})\int_{t}^{2t}\frac{\,{\rm d}\tau}{(2t-\tau)^{1-\alpha}\;\tau^{\frac{\alpha m}{m-1}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2^{\frac{\alpha m}{m-1}}}\;u^{m}(t,x_{0})\int_{t}^{2t}\frac{\,{\rm d}\tau}{(2t-\tau)^{1-\alpha}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha\,2^{\frac{\alpha m}{m-1}}}\;t^{\alpha}\,u^{m}(t,x_{0})\,.

The proof follows by combining (
40
) and (
44
).
∎

Next, we recall a result from
[
11
]
involving
L^{q}
estimates of the Green function.

Lemma 4.5

(Green function estimates,
[
11
]
)
.

Let
\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}
be the kernel of
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
, and assume that (
G1
) holds. Then, for all
0<q<{N}/(N-2s)
, there exist a constant
c_{2,\Omega}(q)>0
such that

\sup_{x_{0}\in\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}^{q}(x,x_{0})\,{\rm d}x\leq c_{2,\Omega}(q)\,.

(45)

The constant
c_{2,\Omega}
depends only on
s,N,q,\Omega
, and have an explicit expression, cf.
[
11
]
.

Finally, we are in the position to prove the
L^{p}-L^{\infty}
smoothing effects of Theorem
1.4
, namely

\|u(t)\|_{\infty}\leq\overline{\kappa}\frac{\|u_{0}\|^{\frac{1}{m}}_{p}}{t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}}\qquad\forall t>0\,.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.
First, we prove the estimate for nonnegative solutions and then generalize the result for sign-changing solutions.

\bullet~
Step 1.
(Smoothing effect for nonnegative solutions).
If we combine Proposition
4.4
and Lemma
4.5
, the boundedness of solutions
u(t)
follows for any
t>0
given that
0\leq u_{0}\in L^{q^{\prime}}(\Omega)
with
q^{\prime}>\frac{N}{2s}
:

\displaystyle\|u(t)\|_{\infty}^{m}

\displaystyle\leq\sup\limits_{x_{0}\in\Omega}\frac{\mathsf{c}}{t^{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}(u_{0}(x)-u(t_{1},x))\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}(x,x_{0})\,{\rm d}x\leq\sup\limits_{x_{0}\in\Omega}\frac{\mathsf{c}}{t^{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}u_{0}(x)\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}(x,x_{0})\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathsf{c}}{t^{\alpha}}\|u_{0}\|_{q^{\prime}}\sup\limits_{x_{0}\in\Omega}\|\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}(\cdot,x_{0})\|_{q}\leq C\frac{\|u_{0}\|_{q^{\prime}}}{t^{\alpha}},

where we have used the nonnegativity of
u(t_{1},x)
with
t_{1}=t
if
0<m<1
and
t_{1}=2t
if
m>1
, and Young’s inequality. The constant
C
depends on
\alpha,m,s,N
and
\Omega
.

\bullet~
Step 2.
(Smoothing effect for sign-changing solutions).
We reduce the problem to nonnegative initial data using the comparison principle. We split the signed initial datum,

u_{0}=(u_{0})_{+}-(u_{0})_{-}\,,

and consider the nonnegative
H^{*}
-solutions
\tilde{u}^{+}
and
\tilde{u}^{-}
with nonnegative initial data
(u_{0})_{+},(u_{0})_{-}
, respectively. First, we have to check that
(u_{0})_{+},(u_{0})_{-}\in L^{p}(\Omega)\cap H^{*}(\Omega)
with
p
as in (
5
). Since
u_{0}\in L^{p}(\Omega)\cap H^{*}(\Omega)
with
p
satisfying (
5
), then
(u_{0})_{+},(u_{0})_{-}\in L^{p}(\Omega)
with the same
p
. It remains to prove that they also belong to
H^{*}(\Omega)
. For this purpose, we have to use the Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality associated to the quadratic form of the operator: There exists
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}>0
such that

\|f\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\leq\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}\|f\|_{\frac{2N}{N+2s}}\,,

(46)

which holds under assumption (
G1
), see
[
16
, Theorem 7.5]
. Since
N>2s
then
\frac{2N}{N+2s}<\frac{N}{2s}
, and hence

\|(u_{0})_{\pm}\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\leq\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}\|(u_{0})_{\pm}\|_{\frac{2N}{N+2s}}\leq\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{L}}\left|\Omega\right|^{\frac{N-2s}{2N}}\|(u_{0})_{\pm}\|_{\frac{N}{2s}}\,.

Now, we use the smoothing effects for nonnegative initial data of Step 1 to obtain

\|\tilde{u}^{\pm}(t)\|_{\infty}\leq C\,\frac{\|(u_{0})_{\pm}\|_{p}^{\frac{1}{m}}}{t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}}\qquad\forall t>0\,.

By the comparison principle of Theorem
1.2
,
u_{0}\leq(u_{0})_{+}
a.e. in
\Omega
, therefore we have for all
t>0

u(t,x)\leq\tilde{u}^{+}(t,x)\leq C\,\frac{\|(u_{0})_{+}\|_{p}^{\frac{1}{m}}}{t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}}\qquad\mbox{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega\,.

For the lower bound, note that
-u_{0}\leq(u_{0})_{-}
a.e. in
\Omega
. Hence, by comparison principle it holds that for every
t>0

-u(t,x)\leq\tilde{u}^{-}(t,x)\leq C\,\frac{\|(u_{0})_{-}\|_{p}^{\frac{1}{m}}}{t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}}\qquad\mbox{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega\,,

and the result follows.

\square

On the other hand, if instead of using assumption (
G1
) we use (

), we are able to prove the following

estimate involving the distance to the boundary of

.

Theorem 4.6

(Upper boundary estimates)
.

Let
m>0
with
m\neq 1
and

. Assume (

) and (

). Then, for any

-solution with nonnegative initial datum

\left\|\frac{u(t)}{\mbox{\rm dist}(\cdot,\partial\Omega)^{\gamma/m}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\overline{\kappa}\frac{\|u_{0}\|_{p}^{1/m}}{t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}}\qquad\forall t>0\,.

Let us combine Proposition
4.4
with assumption (

) in order to obtain

\displaystyle u^{m}(t,x_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathsf{c}}{t^{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}\left(u_{0}(x)-u(t_{1},x)\right)\mathbb{G}_{\Omega}(x,x_{0})\,{\rm d}x\leq\frac{\mathsf{c}}{t^{\alpha}}\int_{\Omega}\frac{u_{0}(x)\,\mbox{\rm dist}(x_{0},\partial\Omega)^{\gamma}}{|x-x_{0}|^{N-2s+\gamma}}\,{\rm d}x

in
\Omega
.
∎

As it is stated in Theorem
1.5
, the upper boundary estimate above and the comparison principle allow us to provide a Global Harnack Principle for a class of

-solutions.

Theorem 4.7

(Global Harnack Principle for “big” initial data)
.

Let
m>\frac{N-2s}{N+2s}
with
m\neq 1
and
N>2s>\gamma
. Assume (
G2
) and (
K
). If the initial datum
u_{0}\in L^{p}(\Omega)\cap H^{*}(\Omega)
with
p>\frac{N}{2s-\gamma}
and
p\geq 1+m
satisfies

u_{0}(x)\geq c\,\mbox{\rm dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{\gamma/m}\qquad\mbox{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega\,,

for certain
c>0
, then it holds that for every
t>0

\frac{c_{0}}{1+t^{\alpha}}\leq\frac{u^{m}(t,x)}{\mbox{\rm dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{\gamma}}\leq\frac{c_{1}}{t^{\alpha}}\qquad\mbox{ for a.e. }x\in\Omega\,.

(48)

Proof.

The upper bound follows directly by Theorem
4.6
. For the lower bound we use the comparison principle of Theorem
1.2
with respect to the separate-variables solution, which exists if
m>\frac{N-2s}{N+2s}
. This solution has the form

v(t,x)=F(t)S(x)\,,

with
S
solving the elliptic problem
\mathcal{L}S^{m}=S
on
\Omega
and
F
solving the fractional ODE of the form
D^{\alpha}_{t}F=-F^{m}
for
t>0
with
F(0)=F_{0}>0
. We give a precise description of these solutions in Section
5
. As it was proved in
[
11
]
, under assumption (
G2
) the function

behaves like

S(x)\asymp\mbox{\rm dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{\frac{\gamma}{m}}\qquad\mbox{for a.e. }x\in\Omega\,,

and hence, from the assumption on the initial data we have

u_{0}(x)\geq c\,\mbox{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{\frac{\gamma}{m}}\geq C_{0}S(x)\qquad\mbox{ a.e. in }\Omega\,.

Now, choosing

we obtain

u_{0}(x)\geq F_{0}S(x)=v(0,x)\qquad\mbox{ a.e. in }\Omega\,.

Finally, by comparison principle in Theorem
1.2
, for a.e.
x\in\Omega
and every

it holds that

u(t,x)\geq v(t,x)=F(t)S(x)\geq c\,F(t)\,\mbox{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{\gamma/m}\geq c_{0}\,\frac{\mbox{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{\gamma/m}}{1+t^{\alpha}}\,,

since we have used the lower estimates for
F(t)
proved in
[
53
]
, see Theorem
A.3
.
∎

5
Sharp time decay of
L^{p}
-norms

This section contains the proof Theorem
1.7
; in particular, we show that every
H^{*}
-solution does not extinguish in finite time.

We first show, heuristically, why we should not expect extinction in finite time as in the case with classical time derivative. Let us consider solutions of for (
CPME
) of the form

v(t,x)=F(t)S(x).

Then,
F
and
S
satisfy the relation

\frac{\mathcal{L}S^{m}(x)}{S(x)}=-\frac{D^{\alpha}_{t}F(t)}{F^{m}(t)}=\lambda\,.

The equation satisfied by
S(x)
is the same as in the classical time derivative case. Defining
V(x):=S^{m}(x)
and
p:=\tfrac{1}{m}
we get the well-known semilinear equation of Lane-Emden-Fowler type:

\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}V(x)&=\lambda V^{p}(x)\qquad\mbox{in}\quad\Omega\,,\\
V(x)&=0\qquad\qquad\;\;\mbox{on}\quad\partial\Omega\,.\end{cases}

(49)

Due to the Pohozaev identity, in the local and the nonlocal setting, nontrivial solutions to (
49
) fail to exists for
p<\frac{N+2s}{N-2s}
, equivalently, for
m>m_{s}:=\frac{N-2s}{N+2s}
. As shown in
[
11
]
, under assumption (
G2
), solutions to (
49
) behave like

V(x)\asymp\mbox{\rm dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{\gamma}\asymp\Phi_{1}(x)\,,

with the parameter
\gamma>0
depending on the operator
\mathcal{L}
.

The time-dependent function
F(t)
, solution to

\begin{cases}D^{\alpha}_{t}F(t)=-\lambda F^{m}(t)\,,\\
F(0)=F_{0}>0\,,\end{cases}

(50)

has a completely different behaviour than in the classical case: by Theorem
A.3
, proved in
[
53
]
, we know that
F(t)
is bounded above and below by a function of the form
\frac{1}{1+t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}}
, more precisely

\frac{c_{1}}{1+t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}}\leq F(t)\leq\frac{c_{2}}{1+t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}}\qquad\forall t>0\,,

(51)

for some constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
that only depend on
F_{0},\lambda,\alpha
and
m
. The lower bound in (
51
) implies that
v(x,t)
does not vanish in finite time. Note also that the decay rate of any
L^{p}
-norm of
v(x,t)
coincides with the regularization rate shown in Theorem
1.4
.

An important question is whether this non-extinction phenomenon in finite time occurs for all nonnegative solution of (
CPME
). As we will prove in this section, the answer is affirmative and the time decay for the
L^{p}
-norms for all
1\leq p\leq\infty
is sharp.

The proof starts with a lower bound for the
L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}
-norm of the solution with a rate of
t^{-\alpha/m}
. Then, we conclude with an interpolation argument between the
L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}
-norm and the
L^{\infty}
-norm.

But first let us show the following estimate for the
L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}
-norm of the Caputo derivative.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
m>0
and
u
be a
H^{*}
-solution of (
CPME
) with
u_{0}\in L^{1+m}(\Omega)\cap H^{*}(\Omega)
. Then, estimate (
29
), which we recall here,

\ell*\|D^{\alpha}_{t}u(t)\|^{2}_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\in L^{\infty}((0,T))\,,

implies that

\ell*\|D^{\alpha}_{t}u(t)\|_{L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)}\in L^{\infty}((0,T))\,.

(52)

Proof.

Since the kernel
\ell
is nonnegative and
\Phi_{1}\in H(\Omega)
, we have that

\displaystyle\left|\ell*\left(\int_{\Omega}D^{\alpha}_{t}u(t,x)\Phi_{1}(x)\,{\rm d}x\right)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\ell(t-s)D^{\alpha}_{t}u(s,x)\Phi_{1}(x)\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}t\right|

\displaystyle\leq\|\Phi_{1}\|_{H(\Omega)}\int_{0}^{t}\ell(t-s)\|D^{\alpha}_{t}u(s)\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\Phi_{1}\|_{H(\Omega)}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\ell(t-s)\left(1+\|D^{\alpha}_{t}u(s)\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\Phi_{1}\|_{H(\Omega)}}{2}\left(\|\ell\|_{L^{1}(0,T)}+\ell*\|D^{\alpha}_{t}u(t)\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)\,,

where in the second inequality we have used Young’s inequality. We conclude by taking the essential supremum in
t\in(0,T)
and using estimate (
29
).
∎

The last lemma is used in the proof of a lower and upper bounds for the
L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}
-norm of the
H^{*}
-solutions.

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
0<m\leq 1
and u be a
H^{*}
-solution of (
CPME
) with
0\leq u_{0}\in L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)
. Then,

\frac{c}{1+t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}}\leq\|u(t)\|_{L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)}\qquad\quad\forall t>0\,,

(53)

with
c
depending on
\alpha,m,\lambda_{1},\|\Phi_{1}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}
and
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}}
.

Moreover, if
0\leq u_{0}\in L^{p}(\Omega)\cap H^{*}(\Omega)
with
p>\frac{N}{2s}\geq 1+m
or
p\geq 1+m>\frac{N}{2s}
, with
m>1
, then

\frac{c_{0}}{1+t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}}\leq\|u(t)\|_{L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)}\qquad\quad\forall t>0\,.

(54)

Proof.

Inspired by
[
54
]
, we look for a fractional differential inequality for the
L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}
-norm of the solution and then use a comparison argument.

\bullet~
Step 1.
Upper bound.
From the definition of the nonnegative
H^{*}
-solution
u
in (

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}[k*(u-u_{0})](t)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x=-\lambda_{1}\int_{\Omega}u^{m}(t)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\leq 0\qquad\mbox{ for a.e. }t>0\,,

using as a test function
0\leq\lambda_{1}\Phi_{1}\in H(\Omega)
. We now convolve both sides of the equation with the nonnegative kernel
\ell
and obtain

\displaystyle\ell*\left(\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}[k*(u-u_{0})](t)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\right)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\ell*\partial_{t}[k*(u-u_{0})]\right)(t)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\leq 0\,.

Since
\ell*k=1
and
\partial_{t}\left[1*(u(t,x)-u_{0}(x))\right]=u(t,x)-u_{0}(x)
for a.e.

, we conclude that

\int_{\Omega}\left(u(t)-u_{0})\right)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\leq 0\qquad\forall t>0\,.

\bullet~
Step 2.
Lower regularized differential inequality
. We first regularize the kernel
k
to apply a comparison principle for fractional ODEs. We know from (
29
) that
Y(t):=\|u(t)\|_{L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}}\in L^{\infty}((0,T))
and
k*(u-u_{0})\in H^{1}([0,T])
. Moreover, there exists a nonnegative kernel
h_{n}\in H^{1}([0,T])
(see for instance
[

,
55
]
) satisfying

\displaystyle h_{n}*g\rightarrow g\qquad\mbox{in }L^{p}(0,T)\quad\mbox{when }n\rightarrow\infty\,,

for any function
g\in L^{p}((0,T))
. The aim of this step is to prove the following inequality for the function
Y\in L^{\infty}((0,T))
:

\partial_{t}[k_{n}*(Y-Y_{0})](t)\geq-h_{n}*f(t,Y(t))\qquad\mbox{for a.e. }t\in(0,T)\,,

(55)

with
k_{n}:=h_{n}*k
and
f(t,x)
nondecreasing in
x
for a.e.
t>0
and
f(t,Y(t))\in L^{1}((0,T))
.

We start with the definition of the
H^{*}
-solution
u
in (
3
) with the admissible test function
\lambda_{1}\Phi_{1}\in H(\Omega)
,

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}[k*(u-u_{0}](t)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x=-\lambda_{1}\int_{\Omega}u^{m}(t)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\qquad\mbox{ for a.e. }t>0.

(56)

i)

Case
m=1
: We convolve both sides of (
56
) with the nonnegative kernel
h_{n}\in H^{1}([0,T])
and obtain for a.e.

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(t-\tau)\partial_{t}[k*(u-u_{0})](\tau)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}\tau=-\lambda_{1}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(t-s)u(\tau)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}\tau\,.

Then, choosing

) from Fubini-Tonelli’s Theorem and the regularity of

.

ii)

Case
0<m<1

\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}[k*(u-u_{0}](t)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\geq-\lambda_{1}\|\Phi_{1}\|_{1}^{1-m}\left(\int_{\Omega}u(t)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\right)^{m}\qquad\mbox{ for a.e. }t>0.

Then, we convolve the inequality above with the nonnegative kernel
h_{n}\in H^{1}([0,T])
,

\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}h_{n}(t-\tau)\partial_{t}[k*(u-u_{0})](\tau)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\,{\rm d}\tau\geq-\lambda_{1}\|\Phi_{1}\|_{1}^{1-m}\!\int_{0}^{t}h_{n}(t-s)\left(\int_{\Omega}u(\tau)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\right)^{m}\,{\rm d}\tau,

for a.e.

. We deduce (

) with
f(t,x)=\lambda_{1}\|\Phi_{1}\|_{1}^{1-m}|x|^{m-1}x
using Fubini-Tonelli’s Theorem and the regularity of the kernel
h_{n}\in H^{1}([0,T])
.

iii)

Case
m>1
: We use smoothing effects of Theorem
1.4
in (

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\partial_{t}[k*(u-u_{0}](t)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{1}\int_{\Omega}u^{m}(t)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\geq-\lambda_{1}\|u(t)\|_{\infty}^{m-1}\int_{\Omega}u(t)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\geq-\lambda_{1}\overline{\kappa}\frac{\|u_{0}\|_{p}^{\frac{m-1}{m}}}{t^{\frac{\alpha(m-1)}{m}}}\int_{\Omega}u(t)\Phi_{1}\,{\rm d}x\,.

Hence, convolving with the nonnegative kernel
h_{n}\in H^{1}([0,T])
we obtain (

f(t,x)=\lambda_{1}\overline{\kappa}\|u_{0}\|_{p}^{\frac{m-1}{m}}\,t^{-\frac{\alpha(m-1)}{m}}x
.

\bullet~
Step 3.
Lower regularized integral inequality
. Now, we combine the inequality for solution
Y
of (
55
) with the solution
Z
of the equation

\partial_{t}[k_{n}*(Z-Z_{0})](t)=-h_{n}*f(t,Z(t))\qquad\mbox{for a.e. }t\in(0,T)\,,

(57)

with
Z_{0}=Y_{0}
and we prove that

\frac{1}{2}[k_{n}*(Z-Y)_{+}^{2}](T)+\int_{0}^{T}(Z(t)-Y(t))_{+}h_{n}*(f(t,Z(t))-f(t,Y(t)))\,{\rm d}t\leq 0\,.

(58)

For this purpose, we subtract the equation of
Y
in (
55
)from (

) and we multiply the resulting equation by
(Z-Y)_{+}\in L^{\infty}(0,T)
. This yields

(Z-Y)_{+}(t)\partial_{t}\left[k_{n}*(Z-Y)\right](t)+(Z-Y)_{+}(t)\,h_{n}*(f(t,Z(t))-f(t,Y(t)))\leq 0\,.

By the fundamental identity of Lemma
A.4
with

, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\partial_{t}\left[k_{n}*(Z-Y)_{+}^{2}\right](t)+(Z-Y)_{+}(t)\,h_{n}*(f(t,Z(t))-f(t,Y(t)))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(Z-Y)_{+}(t)\partial_{t}\left[k_{n}*(Z-Y)\right](t)+(Z-Y)_{+}(t)\,h_{n}*(f(t,Z(t))-f(t,Y(t)))\leq 0.

Integrating in
[0,T]
and recalling that

a.e. in
\Omega
, we obtain (
58
) .

\bullet~
Step 4.
Comparison with
Z
.
We want to take the limit in (
58
) and obtain

k*(Z-Y)_{+}^{2}(T)\leq 0\,,

(59)

which implies
Z(t)\leq Y(t)
for a.e.

since
k(t)>0
for all

.

Recalling that
(Z-Y)_{+}^{2}\in L^{\infty}((0,T))

\displaystyle\left|k_{n}*(Z-Y)_{+}^{2}(T)-k*(Z-Y)_{+}^{2}(T)\right|\leq\|(Z-Y)_{+}^{2}\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}|(h_{n}*k)(t)-k(t)|\,{\rm d}t\rightarrow 0\,,

when

. On the other hand, since
f(t,Y(t)),f(t,Z(t))\in L^{1}((0,T))
we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{T}\!\!(Z(t)-Y(t))_{+}h_{n}*(f(t,Z(t))-f(t,Y(t)))\,{\rm d}t-\int_{0}^{T}(Z(t)-Y(t))_{+}(f(t,Z(t))-f(t,Y(t)))\,{\rm d}t\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|(Z-Y)_{+}\|_{\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\!\!|[h_{n}*(f(t,Z(t))-f(t,Y(t)))](t)-(f(t,Z(t))-f(t,Y(t)))(t)|\,{\rm d}t\rightarrow 0\,,

when

\frac{1}{2}[k*(Z-Y)_{+}^{2}](T)+\int_{0}^{T}(Z(t)-Y(t))_{+}(f(t,Z(t))-f(t,Y(t)))\,{\rm d}t\leq 0\,.

Notice that
(Z(t)-Y(t))_{+}(f(t,Z(t))-f(t,Y(t)))\geq 0
for a.e.

by the nondecreasing assumption on

) and the fact that
k(t)>0
for every

\bullet~
Step 5.
Lower bound.
Finally, we show that if
Z_{0}=Y_{0}
, then

\frac{c}{1+t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}}\leq Z(t)\leq Y(t)\qquad\forall t>0\,,

which give us the lower bound for

Let us recall the form of

for the three particular case of the fractional ODEs in (

i)

If

and

is the Mittag-Leffler function

ii)

If

iii)

Furthermore, by (

∎

Now that we have a lower bound for the decay of the

-norm of solutions of (

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.
The proof follows by an interpolation argument, using the lower bound of the

-norm of solutions and the

and Theorem

we obtain that for every

\displaystyle\frac{c}{1+t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|u(t)\|_{L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}}\leq\|u(t)\|_{p}\;\|\Phi_{1}\|_{p^{\prime}}\leq|\Omega|\|u(t)\|_{\infty}\,\|\Phi_{1}\|_{\infty}\leq|\Omega|\,\|\Phi_{1}\|_{\infty}\,\overline{\kappa}\,\frac{\|u_{0}\|^{\frac{1}{m}}_{p_{0}}}{t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}}\,,

Hence, for the case

6
Open questions

In the study of the problem for the Caputo Porous Medium equation many questions have arisen. We share the most important ones with the community.

i)

T-contractivity:
From the fractional gradient flow theory developed in
[
1
,
40
,
41
]
we know that the contractivity in the Hilbert space
H^{*}(\Omega)
holds, that is, if
u
and
v
are
H^{*}
-solutions with initial data
u_{0},v_{0}
, respectively, then

\|u(t)-v(t)\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\leq\|u_{0}-v_{0}\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\qquad\forall t>0\,.

In order to obtain the comparison principle of Theorem
1.2
, we try to prove a stronger version of property above called T-contractivity in
H^{*}(\Omega)
, namely,

\|(u(t)-v(t))_{+}\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\leq\|(u_{0}-v_{0})_{+}\|_{H^{*}(\Omega)}\qquad\forall t>0\,.

Since nonnegative functions of
H^{*}(\Omega)
are in
L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)
and
\Phi_{1}(x)\asymp\mbox{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)^{\gamma}
, this T-contractivity would imply the comparison principle of Theorem
1.2
directly. But, due to the lack of regularity estimates for
D^{\alpha}_{t}u
, we have to prove the T-contractivity in
L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)
at the discrete level in Theorem
2.4
and inherit the comparison principle from the discrete comparison principle.

We think that it would be enough to have
|D^{\alpha}_{t}u|\in L^{2}((0,T):H^{*}(\Omega))
or
|D^{\alpha}_{t}u|\in L^{2}((0,T):L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega))
in order to prove T-contractivity in
H^{*}(\Omega)
or
L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)
, respectively. Let us remark that T-contractivity in
L^{1}_{\Phi_{1}}(\Omega)
is also an open problem in the case of classical time derivative, when considering weak dual solutions of the Porous Medium and Fast Diffusion equation. In
[
14
,
19
]
it was proved only for
minimal
weak dual solutions.

ii)

Higher regularity:

The classical way to prove Hölder continuity is via some kind of Harnack inequalities for the solution. The problem with PDEs involving Caputo time derivative is that obtaining lower bounds for solutions is significantly more challenging.
In this manuscript we prove a Global Harnack Principle in Theorem
1.6
only for a class of solutions with “big” initial data, but we did not succeed to prove it for general initial data. To our knowledge, the only result in this direction appeared in
[
3
]
and
[
2
]
, where the authors apply the De Giorgi’s method to show Hölder regularity.

iii)

Asymptotic behaviour:
Theorem
1.7
shows that solutions of (
CPME
) does not vanish in finite time for any
m>0
. One may wonder about the behaviour of the solutions when
t\rightarrow\infty
. From the smoothing effects of Theorem
1.4
, we deduce that solution vanish at infinity, but we would like to know if there is any asymptotic profile
S(x)
such that

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}u(t)-S\|_{X}=0\,,

for certain norm
X
. From the classical theory, we expect that
S
is the solution of the elliptic problem described in Section
5
and that the solution behaves at infinity like the separate-variable solution.
In the classical approach, the results relies on a change of variables which with Caputo time derivative is more involved, see the fundamental identity for regular kernels in Lemma
A.4
.

Appendix A
Appendix. Fractional ODEs

In this appendix we collect some of the known results about the Caputo derivative and the associated fractional ODEs that are useful for our study. Let us start with the linear differential equation with
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
v_{0}>0
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

\begin{cases}D^{\alpha}_{t}v(t)=\lambda v(t)\quad\mbox{in }(0,+\infty)\\
v(0)=v_{0},\end{cases}

(61)

which solution is the so-called Mittag-Leffler function of the form
v(t)=v_{0}\,E_{\alpha}(\lambda t^{\alpha})
, where

E_{\alpha}(x)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^{k}}{\Gamma(k\alpha+1)}\,.

There is a large amount of literature about this function, cf
[
34
,
36
]
. But, let us remark that the behaviour at the infinity of
E_{\alpha}(-x)
is given by

E_{\alpha}(-x)\asymp\frac{1}{1+x}\,,

(62)

as it is shown in
[
43
]
.
Moreover, we can see the Mittag-Leffler function
E_{\alpha}(-x^{2})
with
\alpha\in(0,1)
as an interpolation between the Gaussian and the polynomial with quadratic decay

E_{1}(-x^{2})=e^{-x^{2}}\leq E_{\alpha_{1}}(-x^{2})\leq E_{\alpha_{2}}(-x^{2})\leq\frac{1}{1+x^{2}}\,,

with
0<\alpha_{1}\leq\alpha_{1}<1
.
Now, let us consider another linear fractional ODE which has an explicit solution.

Lemma A.1
.

[
38
, Example 4.11]

Let
0<\alpha<1
and
\beta>-\alpha
. Then, the solution of

\begin{cases}D^{\alpha}_{t}v(t)&=-\lambda t^{\beta}v(t)\\
v(0)&=v_{0},\end{cases}

(63)

is given by

v(t)=v_{0}\,E_{\alpha,1+\frac{\beta}{\alpha},\frac{\beta}{\alpha}}[-\lambda t^{\alpha+\beta}]\,.

This function is called the Kilbas-Saigo function and its explicit form can be found in
[
20
]
. However, for our study we only need the asymptotic behaviour that we state below.

Proposition A.2
.

[
20
, Proposition 6]

Let
0<\alpha<1
,
r>0
and
l>r-\frac{1}{\alpha}
, then

E_{\alpha,r,l}(-x)\asymp\frac{1}{1+c\,x}\qquad\quad\mbox{as}\quad x\rightarrow\infty\,.

In the nonlinear setting, fractional ODEs becomes very delicate. Let us present the following result due to Vergara and Zacher addressing the behaviour of a basic nonlinear fractional ODE.

Theorem A.3
.

[
53
, Theorem 7.1]

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
\lambda,m>0
and
v_{0}>0
. Let
v\in H^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
be the solution of

\begin{cases}D^{\alpha}_{t}v(t)=-\lambda v^{m}(t)&\mbox{in }(0,+\infty)\\
v(0)=v_{0}.\end{cases}

Then, there exist
c_{1},c_{2}>0
depending on
v_{0},m,\alpha
and
\lambda
such that

\frac{c_{1}}{1+t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}}\leq v(t)\leq\frac{c_{2}}{1+t^{\frac{\alpha}{m}}}\qquad\forall t>0\,.

(64)

The explicit form of the solution to this basic nonlinear fractional ODE is not known, but in
[
53
]
they construct subsolution and supersolution to estimate the lower and upper bounds. Note that the behaviour of
v
described in (
64
) is completely different from the solution of the analogous classical ODE. In the classical case, the exponent
m
plays a crucial role. Namely, for the classical ODE of the form
\partial_{t}v=-\lambda v^{m}
with
v(0)=v_{0}
we have that:

•

If
0<m<1
, the solution vanish in finite time since

v(t)=(v_{0}^{1-m}-\lambda(1-m)\,t)_{+}^{\frac{1}{1-m}}\,.

•

If
m=1
, the solution decays exponentially,
v(t)=v_{0}\exp(-\lambda t)
.

•

If
m>1
, the solution decays polynomially

v(t)=\frac{1}{\left(v_{0}^{-(m-1)}+\lambda(m-1)t\right)^{\frac{1}{m-1}}}\leq\frac{C}{t^{\frac{1}{m-1}}}\,.

This shows at the level of ODEs how different are the solutions when we consider Caputo time derivative. In particular, there is no continuity of results when
\alpha\rightarrow 1
.

To conclude this appendix, we present a fundamental identity for fractional derivatives with regular kernels. This formula plays the role of the chain rule
\partial_{t}h(u(t))=h^{\prime}(u(t))\partial_{t}u(t)
in the classical sense.

Lemma A.4
.

[
53
, Lemma 2.2]

Let
T>0
and
I
an open set of
\mathbb{R}
. Further, let
\tilde{k}\in W^{1,1}([0,T])
,
h\in C^{1}(I)
and
u\in L^{1}((0,T))
with
u(t)\in I
for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
. Suppose that the functions
h(u),h^{\prime}(u)u
and
h^{\prime}(u)(\tilde{k}^{\prime}*u)
belong to
L^{1}((0,T))
(which is the case if
u\in L^{\infty}((0,T))
). Then, we have for a.e.
t\in(0,T)

\begin{split}h^{\prime}(u(t))\frac{\rm d}{\,{\rm d}t}(\tilde{k}*u)(t)=&\frac{\rm d}{\,{\rm d}t}(\tilde{k}*h(u))(t)+\bigg(-h(u(t))+h^{\prime}(u(t))u(t)\bigg)\tilde{k}(t)\\
&+\int_{0}^{t}\bigg(h(u(t-\tau))-h(u(t))-h^{\prime}(u(t))\left[u(t-\tau)-u(t)\right]\bigg)(-\tilde{k}^{\prime}(\tau))\,{\rm d}\tau\,.\end{split}

Let us remark that when the function
h
is convex and the kernel
\tilde{k}
is nonnegative and nonincreasing, we obtain the inequality

\frac{\rm d}{\,{\rm d}t}(\tilde{k}*h(u))\leq h^{\prime}(u(t))\frac{\rm d}{\,{\rm d}t}(\tilde{k}*u)(t)\,.
