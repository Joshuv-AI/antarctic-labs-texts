Stochastic Linear-Quadratic Optimal Control Problems with Random Coefficients and Markovian Regime Switching System
Jiaqiang Wen, Xun Li, Jie Xiong, Xin Zhang

Abstract




Abstract.
This paper thoroughly investigates stochastic linear-quadratic optimal control problems with the Markovian regime switching system, where the coefficients of the state equation and the weighting matrices of the cost functional are random.
We prove the solvability of stochastic Riccati equation under the uniform convexity condition, and obtain the closed-loop representation of the open-loop optimal control using the unique solvability of the corresponding stochastic Riccati equation.
Moreover, by applying Itô’s formula with jumps, we get a representation of the cost functional on a Hilbert space, characterized as the adapted solutions of some forward-backward stochastic differential equations.
We show that the necessary condition of the open-loop optimal control is the convexity of the cost functional, and the sufficient condition of the open-loop optimal control is the uniform convexity of the cost functional. In addition, we study the properties of the stochastic value flow of the stochastic linear-quadratic optimal control problem.
Finally, as an application, we present a continuous-time mean-variance portfolio selection problem and prove its unique solvability.

Key words:
Stochastic linear-quadratic optimal control, Markovian regime switching, random coefficient, stochastic Riccati equation, mean-variance portfolio selection.

49N10, 93E20.

Introduction

Linear-quadratic (LQ, for short) optimal control problem plays a fundamental role in control theory, which appeared with the birth of stochastic analysis and developed rapidly in recent decades due to its wide range of applications. The study of stochastic linear-quadratic (SLQ, for short) optimal control problems can be traced back to the work of Kushner
[
12
]
and Wonham
[
25
]
.
In the classical setting, the SLQ optimal control problem can be solved elegantly via the Riccati equation under some mild conditions on the weighting coefficients (see Yong–Zhou
[
26
, Chapter 6]
).
Chen–Li–Zhou
[
3
]
investigated SLQ optimal control problems with indefinite weighting control matrix and their applications in solving continuous-time mean-variance portfolio selection problems in 1990s. From then on, there has been increasing interest in the so-called
indefinite
SLQ optimal control problems as well as addressing their applications (see Chen–Yong
[
4
]
, Ait Rami–Moore–Zhou
[
1
]
, and Li–Zhou–Lim
[
14
]
).

To tackle SLQ optimal control problems with random coefficients, Bismut
[
2
]
initially derived the existence and uniqueness of the solution of the stochastic Riccati equation (SRE, for short) using techniques of functional analysis. Peng
[
18
]
posted it as an open problem in the general setting in his collection of open problems for backward stochastic differential equations (BSDEs, for short). Kohlmann–Tang
[
11
]
, Tang
[
22
]
and Sun–Xiong–Yong
[
20
]
established the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the related SRE under different conditions using their methods, which partially addressed the open problem introduced by Peng
[
18
]
.
In detail, Kohlmann–Tang
[
11
]
studied the multidimensional backward stochastic Riccati equations and gave an application to the stochastic optimal control problem.
Tang
[
22
]
studied general SLQ optimal control problems with random coefficients and proved the existence and uniqueness of related backward stochastic Riccati equations.
Sun–Xiong–Yong
[
20
]
studied the SLQ optimal control problems with random coefficients, proved the solvability of the corresponding SRE, and obtained the closed-loop representation of the open-loop optimal control.
Li–Wu–Yu
[
13
]
analyzed a special type of indefinite SLQ problem with random coefficients. For more details about the efforts devoted to the stochastic Riccati equation and its connection with SLQ optimal control problems, we refer the interested readers to Kohlmann–Tang
[
10
]
, Tang
[
23
]

Recently, there has been a dramatically increasing interest in the SLQ optimal control problems with random jumps, such as Poisson jumps or the regime switching jumps, which are of practical importance in various fields such as economics, financial management, science, and engineering. In the past few years, researchers have focused on models of financial markets whose key parameters are described by Markov processes, such as stock returns, interest rates, and volatility.
In particular, one could face two market regimes in financial markets, one of which stands for a bull market with price rises, while the other for a bear market with price drops. We call such a formulation the regime switching model, where the market parameters depend on market modes that switch among a finite number of regimes.
More recently, applications of SLQ optimal control problems with regime switching models or Poisson jumps have been extensively developed. For instance, Ji–Chizeck
[
9
]
formulated a class of continuous-time LQ optimal control problems with Markovian jumps.
Zhou–Yin
[
29

studied a mean-variance portfolio selection with regime switching.
Liu–Yin–Zhou
[
16
]
studied the near-optimal controls of regime switching LQ control problems.
Hu–Oksendal
[
7
]
discussed SLQ optimal control problems with Poisson jumps and partial information using the technique of completing squares.
Song–Tang–Wu
[
19
]
established the maximum principle for progressive stochastic optimal control problems with random jumps.
Zhang–Li–Xiong
[
28
]
investigated open-loop and closed-loop solvabilities for SLQ optimal control problems with a Markovian regime switching system.
Hu–Shi–Xu
[
8
]
applied the constrained SLQ control with regime switching to a portfolio problem.
For some other important works, we refer the readers to

[
5
,
15
,
24
,
27
]
, and the references therein.

In a real market, besides the Markov chain, it is more reasonable allowing the market parameters to depend on the Brownian motion, due to the fact that the interest rates, stock rates, and volatilities are affected by the uncertainties caused by the Brownian motion.
However, up to now, few results have been obtained on this topic.
In this paper, inspired by the continuous-time mean-variance portfolio selection problems, we are interested in studying this topic, i.e., the SLQ optimal control problems under the Markovian regime switching system with random coefficients.
Further, by developing some ideas of Sun–Xiong–Yong
[
20
]
and Li–Zhou–Lim
[
14
]
, we establish the existence of an open-loop optimal control and prove the unique solvability of the associated SRE.
Also, we apply the theoretical results shown in this paper to treat a continuous-time mean-variance portfolio selection problem.
Next, we present our main results and difficulties in detail.

(i)

We first introduce the associated stochastic Riccati equation of Problem (M-SLQ) (see (
2.2
)), and prove that the optimal state process of Problem (M-SLQ) is invertible under the uniform convexity condition (
3.3
). Then, we show that a bounded process
\hat{P}(\cdot,\alpha(\cdot))
, together with two square-integrable processes
\hat{\Lambda}(\cdot)
and
\hat{\zeta}(\cdot)

3.1
), and thus establishing the unique solvability of the associated SRE (see

). Moreover, we derive a closed-loop representation for the open-loop optimal control of Problem (M-SLQ) using the unique solvability of the SRE (see
Theorem 3.2
).

(ii)

In order to prove the above theoretical results, we prove some auxiliary results. We establish the equivalence between Problem (M-SLQ) and Problem
{(\hbox{M-SLQ})}_{0}
, i.e., a control
u^{*}(\cdot)\in{\cal U}[t,T]

{(\hbox{M-SLQ})}

3.6
), and based on the equivalence, we analyze the time-consistency of the optimal control (see
3.8
).
Then, we obtain a quadratic representation of the stochastic value flow in terms of a bounded, left continuous, and

-valued process (see
Theorem 3.11
and
Theorem 3.12
).
In addition, by the technique of Itô’s formula with jumps, we represent the cost functional of Problem (M-SLQ) as a bilinear form, in terms of the adapted solutions of some forward-backward stochastic differential equations (FBSDEs, for short) in a suitable Hilbert space (see
5.4
).

(iii)

As a financial application, we present an example of the continuous-time mean-variance portfolio selection problem and prove the unique solvability of the related mean-variance problem. Also, we derive the representation of the unique optimal investment strategy (see
Theorem 6.1
), which further develops the work of Li–Zhou–Lim
[
14
]
to the Markovian regime switching system with random coefficients.

Compared with Sun–Xiong–Yong
[
20
]
, the difficulties of this paper mainly come from the solvability of the associated SRE, due to the presence of the Markovian regime switching jumps.

(iv)

Firstly, due to the presence of random coefficients, the Riccati equation associated with Problem (M-SLQ) becomes a nonlinear BSDE, usually referred to as the backward stochastic Riccati equation. Furthermore, the backward stochastic Riccati equation is driven by both the Brownian motion and the martingales
(\widetilde{N}_{kl}(\cdot))_{k,l\in{\cal S}}
generated by the Markov chain thanks to the occurrence of Markovian regime switching jumps in the model. Thus, the solvability of this BSDE is more complicated than that of the model with deterministic coefficients and without the Markovian regime switching jumps.

(v)

Secondly, let
(X_{j}(s),Y_{j}(s),Z_{j}(s),\Gamma^{j}(s))
be the solution of the FBSDE composed by the open-loop optimal state process and adjoint equation corresponding to initial state
(t,e_{j},\vartheta)
. It is worth to mention that the construction of the fourth component
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(s)
for the solution of matrix-valued FBSDE (
3.11
) is not directly extending the dimensional by combing
\Gamma^{j}(s)
as
(\Gamma^{1}(s),\cdots,\Gamma^{n}(s))
. Furthermore, the
\Gamma
-term of the solution to equation (
3.6
) associated with the initial state

is represented as
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}(s)\circ\xi
, which is different from that of
(Y(s),Z(s))=({\bf Y}(s)\xi,{\bf Z}(s)\xi)
. Please see more details in
3.10
.

(vi)

Thirdly, the existence of process
P(t)
appeared in the equation (42) of Sun–Xiong–Yong
[
20
]
for the representation of the value function follows directly by its definition
\mathbb{E}[{\bf M}(T)+\int_{t}^{T}{\bf N}(s)ds|\mathscr{F}_{t}]
. In our case, we have a similar representation for the value function but with
{\bf M}(T)
and
{\bf N}(s)
replaced by functions depend on the Markov chain
\alpha(\cdot)
and
\mathscr{F}_{t}
replaced by the
\sigma
-field generated by the Brownian motion and the Markov chain

.
To solve our problem, we need to prove that there exists a process

, which is

-adapted, such that

P(t,\alpha(t))=\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbf{M}(T,\alpha(T))+\int_{t}^{T}\boldsymbol{N}(s,\alpha(s))ds\Big|\mathscr{F}_{t}\Big].

This cannot be obtained directly from the definition of conditional expectation and we give proof for this (see the proof of

In

In

3
The main results

In this section, we state our main results, such as the solvability of the corresponding SRE, the closed-loop representation of the open-loop optimal control, and some auxiliary results.

3.1
Solvability of stochastic Riccati equation

As shown in Sun–Xiong–Yong
[
20
]
, the solvability of the corresponding SRE deduces the closed-loop representation of the open-loop optimal control, which is important to deal with Problem (M-SLQ).
Therefore, the SRE plays a crucial role in studying the SLQ problem, and our core goal is to establish the solvability of the corresponding SRE.
For this, we introduce the following SRE:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \displaystyle d\hat{P}(s,\alpha(s))&=-\big[\hat{Q}(s,\alpha(s))+\hat{S}(s,\alpha(s))^{\top}\Theta(s,\alpha(s))\big]ds\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle&\quad+\hat{\Lambda}(s)dW(s)+\hat{\zeta}(s)\bullet d\widetilde{N}(s),\quad s\in[0,T],\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\hat{P}(T,\alpha(T))&=G(\alpha(T)),\quad\alpha(0)=i_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

(3.1)

where
i_{0}\in{\cal S}
is the initial state of
\alpha(\cdot)
, and for any
(s,\omega,i)\in[0,T]\times\Omega\times{\cal S}
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{Q}(s,i)

\displaystyle\triangleq\hat{P}(s,i)A(s,i)+A(s,i)^{\top}\hat{P}(s,i)+C(s,i)^{\top}\hat{P}(s,i)C(s,i)

(3.2)

\displaystyle+\hat{\Lambda}(s)C(s,i)+C(s,i)^{\top}\hat{\Lambda}(s)+Q(s,i),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{S}(s,i)

\displaystyle\triangleq B(s,i)^{\top}\hat{P}(s,i)+D(s,i)^{\top}\hat{P}(s,i)C(s,i)+D(s,i)^{\top}\hat{\Lambda}(s)+S(s,i),

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{R}(s,i)

\displaystyle\triangleq R(s,i)+D(s,i)^{\top}\hat{P}(s,i)D(s,i),\quad\ \Theta(s,i)\triangleq-\hat{R}(s,i)^{-1}\hat{S}(s,i).

Suppose that the cost functional
J(0,0,i_{0};u(\cdot))

\displaystyle\bar{X}(s)={\cal X}(s){\cal X}^{-1}(t)\xi,\quad\bar{Y}(s)={\cal Y}(s){\cal X}^{-1}(t)\xi,\quad\bar{Z}(s)={\cal Z}(s){\cal X}^{-1}(t)\xi,\quad\bar{\Gamma}(s)=\breve{\Gamma}(s)\circ{\cal X}^{-1}(t)\xi.

Hence, for every
\xi\in L^{\infty}_{\mathscr{F}_{t}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the operator
{\cal L}_{t}\xi
could be represented in terms of the quadruple
({\cal X}(\cdot),{\cal Y}(\cdot),{\cal Z}(\cdot),\breve{\Gamma}(\cdot))
as follows,

[{\cal L}_{t}\xi](s,\alpha(s))=F_{0}(s,\alpha(s),{\cal X}(s),{\cal Y}(s),{\cal Z}(s)){\cal X}^{-1}(t)\xi,\quad s\in[t,T].

(5.14)

This relation is useful in proving
Theorem 3.12
.

Now, we are ready to show the representation to the cost functional
J_{0}(t,\xi,\vartheta;u(\cdot))
.
Note that
J_{0}(t,\xi,\vartheta;u(\cdot))
and
{J}(t,\xi,\vartheta;u(\cdot))
have the relation
J_{0}(t,\xi,\vartheta;u(\cdot))=\mathbb{E}{J}(t,\xi,\vartheta;u(\cdot))
, and from
2.3
, the first component
M(\cdot)
of BSDE (
2.4
) is bounded.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
{\cal N}_{t}
and
{\cal L}_{t}
be defined in (
5.9
) and
M(\cdot)
be the first component of the solution of BSDE (
2.4
). Then, under (
H
), the cost functional
J(t,\xi,\vartheta;u(\cdot))
has the following representation:

J(t,\xi,\vartheta;u(\cdot))=[[{\cal N}_{t}u,u]]_{t}+2[[{\cal L}_{t}\xi,u]]_{t}+\langle M(t)\xi,\xi\rangle,\ \hbox{ for all }(t,\xi,\vartheta)\in{\cal D}.

(5.15)

Furthermore, the cost functional
J_{0}(t,\xi,\vartheta;u(\cdot))
has the following representation:

J_{0}(t,\xi,\vartheta;u(\cdot))=[[{\cal N}_{t}u,u]]+2[[{\cal L}_{t}\xi,u]]+\mathbb{E}\langle M(t)\xi,\xi\rangle,\ \hbox{ for all }(t,\xi,\vartheta)\in{\cal D}.

(5.16)

Proof.

Fix any
(t,\xi,\vartheta)\in{\cal D}
and
u(\cdot)\in{\cal U}[t,T]
. Let
(\tilde{X}(\cdot),\tilde{Y}(\cdot),\tilde{Z}(\cdot),\tilde{\Gamma}(\cdot))
,
(\bar{X}(\cdot),\bar{Y}(\cdot),\bar{Z}(\cdot),\bar{\Gamma}(\cdot))
,
and
(X(\cdot),Y(\cdot),Z(\cdot),\Gamma(\cdot))

be the adapted solutions of FBSDE (
5
), FBSDE (
5
), and SDE (
2.1
) and BSDE (
3.6
), respectively.
Then

X(s)=\tilde{X}(s)+\bar{X}(s),\quad~Y(s)=\tilde{Y}(s)+\bar{Y}(s),\quad~Z(s)=\tilde{Z}(s)+\bar{Z}(s),\quad~\Gamma(s)=\tilde{\Gamma}(s)+\bar{\Gamma}(s),\quad s\in[t,T].

By
5.1
, the definitions of
{\cal N}_{t}
and
{\cal L}_{t}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle J(t,\xi,\vartheta;u(\cdot))=\langle\tilde{Y}(t),\xi\rangle+\langle\bar{Y}(t),\xi\rangle+\mathbb{E}_{t}\int_{t}^{T}\big\langle[{\cal N}_{t}u](s)+[{\cal L}_{t}\xi](s),u(s)\big\rangle ds.\end{array}

(5.17)

On one hand, applying Itô’s formula to
\langle\tilde{Y}(s),\bar{X}(s)\rangle
on
[t,T]
implies that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\langle G(\alpha(T))\tilde{X}(T),\bar{X}(T)\rangle-\langle\tilde{Y}(t),\xi\rangle=-\mathbb{E}_{t}\int_{t}^{T}\Big[\langle Q(s,\alpha(s))\tilde{X}(s),\bar{X}(s)\rangle+\langle S(s,\alpha(s))\bar{X}(s),u(s)\rangle\Big]ds.

On the other hand, applying Itô’s formula to
\langle\bar{Y}(s),\tilde{X}(s)\rangle
on
[t,T]
gives that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\langle G(\alpha(T))\bar{X}(T),\tilde{X}(T)\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{t}\int_{t}^{T}\Big[\langle B(s,\alpha(s))^{\top}\bar{Y}(s)+D(s,\alpha(s))^{\top}\bar{Z}(s),u(s)\rangle-\langle Q(s,\alpha(s))\bar{X}(s),\tilde{X}(s)\rangle\Big]ds.

So we have

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{Y}(t),\xi\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{t}\int_{t}^{T}\Big[\langle F_{0}(s,\alpha(s),\bar{X}(s),\bar{Y}(s),\bar{Z}(s)),u(s)\rangle\Big]ds=\mathbb{E}_{t}\int_{t}^{T}\langle[{\cal L}_{t}\xi](s),u(s)\rangle ds.

Moreover, applying Itô’s formula to
M(s)\bar{X}(s)
, we have

\displaystyle d[M(s)\bar{X}(s)]

\displaystyle=-\Big[A(s,\alpha(s))^{\top}M(s)\bar{X}(s)+C(s,\alpha(s))^{\top}\big(M(s)C(s,\alpha(s))+\Phi(s)\big)\bar{X}(s)+Q(s,\alpha(s))\bar{X}(s)\Big]ds

\displaystyle\quad\ +\big(M(s)C(s,\alpha(s))+\Phi(s)\big)\bar{X}(s)dW(s)+(\eta(s)\circ\bar{X}(s))\bullet d\widetilde{N}(s).

(5.18)

Since
M(T)\bar{X}(T)=\bar{Y}(T)
, we see that the triple
(M\bar{X},(MC+\Phi)\bar{X},\eta\circ\bar{X})
satisfies the same BSDE as
(\bar{Y}(\cdot),\bar{Z}(\cdot),\bar{\Gamma}(\cdot))
. Thus, by
comparing equations (
5
) and (
5.18
), we have

\displaystyle\bar{Y}(s)=M(s)\bar{X}(s),\quad\bar{Z}(s)=\big(M(s)C(s,\alpha(s))+\Phi(s)\big)\bar{X}(s),\quad\bar{\Gamma}(s)=\eta(s)\circ\bar{X}(s),\quad s\in[t,T].

It follows that
\langle\bar{Y}(t),\xi\rangle=\langle M(t)\xi,\xi\rangle
.
Substituting this relation into (
5.17
) implies result (
5.15
). By taking expectation to (
5.15
), we can easily obtain (
5.16
).
∎

Corollary 5.5
.

Under the condition (
H
), the following two assertions hold:

(i)

A control
u^{*}(\cdot)\in{\cal U}[t,T]
is optimal for Problem (M-SLQ)
0
w.r.t.
(t,\xi,\vartheta)\in{\cal D}
if and only if

\displaystyle{\cal N}_{t}\geqslant 0,\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\cal N}_{t}u^{*}+{\cal L}_{t}\xi=0.

(5.19)

(ii)

If
{\cal N}_{t}
is invertible and satisfies the positivity condition
{\cal N}_{t}\geqslant 0
, then Problem (M-SLQ)
0
is uniquely solvable at
t
, and the unique open-loop optimal control
u^{*}(\cdot)
w.r.t.
(t,\xi,\vartheta)\in{\cal D}
is given by

\displaystyle u^{*}(s)=-[{\cal N}^{-1}_{t}{\cal L}_{t}\xi](s),\quad s\in[t,T].

Proof.

By
2.2
, we see that
u^{*}(\cdot)\in{\cal U}[t,T]
is optimal for Problem (M-SLQ)
0
w.r.t. the initial triple
(t,\xi,\vartheta)
if and only if

J_{0}(t,\xi,\vartheta;u^{*}(\cdot)+\lambda v(\cdot))-J_{0}(t,\xi,\vartheta;u^{*}(\cdot))\geqslant 0,\quad\forall v(\cdot)\in{\cal U}[t,T],\ \lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

(5.20)

According to the representation (
5.16
), one has

\displaystyle J_{0}(t,\xi,\vartheta;u^{*}(\cdot)+\lambda v(\cdot))-J_{0}(t,\xi,\vartheta;u^{*}(\cdot))=

\displaystyle~\lambda^{2}[[{\cal N}_{t}v,v]]+2\lambda[[{\cal N}_{t}u^{*},v]]+2\lambda[[{\cal L}_{t}\xi,v]].

Hence, the equation (
5.20
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}[[{\cal N}_{t}v,v]]+2\lambda[[{\cal N}_{t}u^{*}+{\cal L}_{t}\xi,v]]\geqslant 0,\quad\forall v(\cdot)\in{\cal U}[t,T],\ \forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore, we must have

\displaystyle[[{\cal N}_{t}v,v]]\geqslant 0\quad\hbox{and}\quad[[{\cal N}_{t}u^{*}+{\cal L}_{t}\xi,v]]=0,\quad\forall v\in{\cal U}[t,T],

which implies that (
5.19
) holds. The converse assertion is obvious. The assertion (ii) is a direct consequence of the assertion (i). This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

It is noteworthy that in the assertion (ii) of
5.5
, the assumption that

{\cal N}_{t}\geqslant 0
and
{\cal N}_{t}
is invertible
are equivalent to that
{\cal N}_{t}
is uniformly positive, i.e., there exists a positive constant

such that

\displaystyle[[{\cal N}_{t}u,u]]\geqslant\varepsilon[[u,u]],\quad\forall u(\cdot)\in{\cal U}[t,T].

(5.21)

From the assertion (i) of
5.5
, we see that the condition
{\cal N}_{t}\geqslant 0

(or equivalently,
[[{\cal N}_{t}u,u]]\geqslant 0
for every
u(\cdot)\in{\cal U}[t,T]
) is necessary
for the existence of an open-loop optimal control of Problem (M-SLQ)
0
, and from the assertion (ii) of
5.5
,
we see that the condition (
5.21
), slightly stronger than
{\cal N}_{t}\geqslant 0
,
is sufficient for the existence of an open-loop optimal control of Problem (M-SLQ)
0
.
Moreover, by (
5.16
), we have

Based on the above discussions, we are in a position to prove the auxiliary results of

6
Example

As presented in the introduction, in a realistic market, it is better to allow the market parameters to depend on both the Markov chain and Brownian motion, due to the interest rates, stock rates, and volatilities being affected by the uncertainties caused by the Brownian motion.
In this section, as an application of our main results, we give an example of the continuous-time mean-variance portfolio selection problem, which partially develops the work of Li–Zhou–Lim
[
14
]
to the Markovian regime switching system with random coefficients.
For simplicity, we would like to let
m=n=1
.

Suppose there is a market in which two assets are traded over a finite horizon
[0,T]
. One of the assets is the bond whose asset price
S_{0}(\cdot)
is subject to the following ordinary differential equation:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle dS_{0}(s)=r(s)S_{0}(s)ds,\quad s\in[0,T],\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle S_{0}(0)=S_{0}>0,\end{array}\right.

where
r(\cdot)
is a positive and bounded function, which represents the bond’s interest rate.
The other one of the assets is stock whose asset price
S_{1}(\cdot)
satisfies the following stochastic differential equation:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle dS_{1}(s)=S_{1}(s)\Big\{b(s,\alpha(s))dt+\sigma(s,\alpha(s))dW(s)\Big\},\quad s\in[0,T],\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle S_{1}(0)=S_{1}>0,\quad\alpha(0)=i_{0},\end{array}\right.

where
b(s,\alpha(s))
is the appreciation rate and

\sigma(s,\alpha(s))
is the volatility or the dispersion of the stock.
Assume that for any choice of
i\in{\cal S}
, both
b(\cdot,i)
and
\sigma(\cdot,i)
are in the space
L_{\mathbb{F}^{W}}^{\infty}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
, and there is a positive constant
\delta
such that

\sigma(s,\alpha(s))^{2}\geqslant\delta,\quad\forall s\in[0,T].

Assume that the trading of shares takes place continuously and transaction costs and consumption are not considered. Then, a small investor’s self-financing wealth process
X(\cdot)
satisfies the following SDE:

\left\{\begin{aligned} dX(s)=&\Big\{r(s)X(s)+[b(s,\alpha(s))-r(s)]u(s)\Big\}ds+\sigma(s,\alpha(s))u(s)dW(s),\quad s\in[0,T],\\
X(0)=&x_{0}>0,\quad\alpha(0)=i_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

(6.1)

where
u(\cdot)\in L_{\mathbb{F}}^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R})
is a portfolio of the investor, which may change over time
s\in[0,T]
.
Note that
u(s)=0
implies that the investor invests his/her total wealth in the bond at time
s\in[0,T]
.

As shown in Li–Zhou–Lim
[
14
]
,
the mean-variance portfolio selection refers to the problem of finding an allowable investment policy (i.e., a dynamic portfolio satisfying all the constraints) such that the expected terminal wealth satisfies
\mathbb{E}[X(T)]=d
while the risk measured by the variance of the terminal wealth is

\operatorname{Var}(X(T))=\mathbb{E}\big[X(T)-\mathbb{E}[X(T)]\big]^{2}=\mathbb{E}\big[(X(T)-d)^{2}\big].

Then we consider the following dynamic stochastic optimization

\left\{\begin{aligned} \min~&\mathbb{E}\big[(X(T)-d)^{2}\big],\\
\mbox{s.t. }&\mathbb{E}[X(T)]=d,\\
&(X(\cdot),u(\cdot))\mbox{ satisfies (\ref{eq:wealth})}.\end{aligned}\right.

(6.2)

Since (
6.2
) is a convex optimization problem, the equality constraint
\mathbb{E}[X(T)]=d
can be dealt with by introducing a Lagrange multiplier
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, we have

\mathbb{E}\big[(X(T)-d)^{2}\big]-2\mu(\mathbb{E}[X(T)]-d)=\mathbb{E}\big[(X(T)-(d+\mu))^{2}\big]-\mu^{2}=\mathbb{E}[X(T)-\gamma]^{2}-\mu^{2},

where
\gamma=d+\mu
.
Now, if we set

\widetilde{X}(s)=X(s)-\gamma\exp\Big\{-\int_{s}^{T}r(s)ds\Big\},

then (
6.2
) can be transferred into the following problem

\left\{\begin{aligned} \displaystyle\min~&\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}(T)^{2}]\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\mbox{s.t. }&d\widetilde{X}(s)=\Big\{r(s)\widetilde{X}(s)+[b(s,\alpha(s))-r(s)]u(s)\Big\}ds+\sigma(s,\alpha(s))u(s)dW(s),\quad s\in[0,T],\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle&\ \widetilde{X}(0)=x_{0}-\gamma\exp\Big\{-\int_{0}^{T}r(s)ds\Big\},\quad\alpha(0)=i_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

(6.3)

It is easy to see that
Problem (
6.3
) is a special case of Problem (M-SLQ) with

G(\alpha(T))=1,\quad A(s,\alpha(s))=r(s),\quad B(s,\alpha(s))=b(s,\alpha(s))-r(s),\quad D(s,\alpha(s))=\sigma(s,\alpha(s)),\quad s\in[0,T],

and other coefficients are zero.
Then the SRE associated to Problem (
6.3
) is:

\left\{\begin{aligned} \displaystyle d\hat{P}(s,\alpha(s))&=-\big[\hat{Q}(s,\alpha(s))+\hat{S}(s,\alpha(s))^{\top}\Theta(s,\alpha(s))\big]ds\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle&\quad+\hat{\Lambda}(s)dW(s)+\hat{\zeta}(s)\bullet d\widetilde{N}(s),\quad s\in[0,T],\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\hat{P}(T,\alpha(T))&=1,\quad\alpha(0)=i_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

(6.4)

where
i_{0}\in{\cal S}
and for
s\in[0,T]
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{Q}(s,\alpha(s))

\displaystyle\triangleq 2r(s)\hat{P}(s,\alpha(s)),\qquad\hat{S}(s,\alpha(s))\triangleq[b(s,\alpha(s))-r(s)]\hat{P}(s,\alpha(s))+\sigma(s,\alpha(s))\hat{\Lambda}(s),

(6.5)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\hat{R}(s,\alpha(s))

\displaystyle\triangleq\sigma(s,\alpha(s))^{2}\hat{P}(s,\alpha(s)),\qquad\Theta(s,\alpha(s))\triangleq-\hat{R}(s,\alpha(s))^{-1}\hat{S}(s,\alpha(s)).

Now, we point out that conditions (
H
) and (
3.3
) hold for this case. In fact, it is trivial to verify condition (
H
). As for condition (
3.3
), by solving the linear SDE of (
6.3
), it is easy to verify that there is a positive constant
\varepsilon
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{X}(T)^{2}]\geqslant\varepsilon\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}|u(s)|^{2}ds,\quad\forall u(\cdot)\in\mathcal{U}[0,T].

Therefore,
Theorem 3.1
and
Theorem 3.2
deduce the following conclusion concerning the mean-variance portfolio selection problem.

Moreover, the unique optimal investment strategy

(6.6)

where

\left\{\begin{aligned} \displaystyle&dX^{*}(s)=\Big\{r(s)+[b(s,\alpha(s))-r(s)]\Theta(s,\alpha(s))\Big\}X^{*}(s)ds+\sigma(s,\alpha(s))\Theta(s,\alpha(s))X^{*}(s)dW(s),\ \ s\in[0,T],\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle&X^{*}(0)=x(0)-\gamma\exp\Big\{-\int_{0}^{T}r(s)ds\Big\},\quad\alpha(0)=i_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

Conclusion

This paper extends the work of Sun–Xiong–Yong

]
