Integrable and chaotic systems associated with fractal groups This article is accepted by Entropy and is pending publication. To comply with Entropy guidelines, the references are numbered in order of appearance in the text.
Rostislav Grigorchuk, Supun Samarakoon* \orcidS

Abstract
Fractal groups (also called self-similar groups) is the class of groups discovered by the first author in the 80-s of the last century with the purpose to solve some famous problems in mathematics, including the question raising to von Neumann about non-elementary amenability (in the association with studies around the Banach-Tarski Paradox) and John Milnor’s question on the existence of groups of intermediate growth between polynomial and exponential. Fractal groups arise in various fields of mathematics, including the theory of random walks, holomorphic dynamics, automata theory, operator algebras, etc. They have relations to the theory of chaos, quasi-crystals, fractals, and random Schrödinger operators. One of important developments is the relation of them to the multi-dimensional dynamics, theory of joint spectrum of pencil of operators, and spectral theory of Laplace operator on graphs. The paper gives a quick access to these topics, provide calculation and analysis of multi-dimensional rational maps arising via the Schur complement in some important examples, including the first group of intermediate growth and its overgroup, contains discussion of the dichotomy “integrable-chaotic” in the considered model, and suggests a possible probabilistic approach to the study of discussed problems.

Self-Similar Algebras

The definition of self-similar algebras resembles the definition of self-similar groups. It is based on the important property of infinite dimensional Hilbert space

to be isomorphic to the direct sum of
d

copies of it. A

There are many such isomorphisms and they are in a natural

-representations of the Cuntz algebra

as observed in

(17)

by generators and relations that we will call Cuntz relations.

{Theorem}

[Proposition 3.1 from

]
The relation putting into correspondence to a

-representation

of

into

-algebra

of bounded operators on a separable infinite dimensional Hibert space

, where

are generators of
\mathcal{O}_{d}

is a bijection between the set of representations of
\mathcal{O}_{d}
on

The inverse of this bijection puts into correspondence to a
d
-similarity
\psi\colon H\to H^{d}
the

-representation of
\mathcal{O}_{d}
given by
\rho(a_{k})=T_{k}

(18)

.

U_{x}f(\xi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}f(x\xi),\quad\xi\in\partial T_{i},\quad x\in X.

Spectra of Groups and Graphs

Let
\Gamma=(V,E)
be a
d
-regular (non-oriented) graph.
The
Markov operator
M
acts
on the
Hilbert space
\ell^{2}(V)
(which we denote by
\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
) and is defined as

(Mf)(x)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{y\sim x}f(y),

where
f\in\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
and
x\sim y
is the adjacency relation. The operator
L=I-M
where
I
is the identity operator is called
the
discrete Laplace operator. Operators
M
and
L
can be defined also for non-regular graphs as it is done for instance in
Mohar and Woess 1989
;
Chung 1997
. The Markov operator
M
is a self-adjoint operator with the norm
\norm{M}\leq 1
and the spectrum
\mathop{\rm Sp}\nolimits(M)\subset[-1,1]
. The name “Markov” comes from the fact that
M
is
the
Markov operator associated with
the
random walk on
\Gamma
in which
a
transition
u\to v
occurs with probability
p=\frac{1}{d}
, if
u
and
v
are adjacent vertices. Random walks on graphs are special case of Markov chains.

A graph
\Gamma
of uniformly bounded degree is called amenable if
1\in\mathop{\rm Sp}\nolimits(M)
(
\iff\norm{M}=1
). Such definition comes from the analogy with von-Neumann – Bogolyubov theory of amenable groups, i.e., groups with invariant mean
Greenleaf 1969
. By Kesten’s criterion
Kesten 1959
, amenable groups can be characterized as groups for which the spectral radius
r=\overline{\lim}_{n\to\infty}\sqrt[n]{P_{1,1}^{(n)}}

is equal to one
, where
P_{1,1}^{(n)}
is the probability of return to identity element in
n
steps of the simple random walk on the Cayley graph.

By a spectrum of a graph (or a group), we mean the spectrum of
M
. A more general concept is
used
when graph is
weighted
, in the sense that a weight function
w\colon E\to\mathbb{R}
on edges is
given
and the “weighted Markov” operator
M_{w}
is defined in
\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
as

(M_{w}f)(x)=\sum_{y\sim x}w(x,y)f(y).

A special case of such situation is given by a marked group
(G,A)
(i.e., group
G
together with its generating set
A
) and symmetric probability distribution
P(g)
on
A\cup A^{-1}
:
P(a)=P(a^{-1})
, for all
a\in A
and
\sum_{a\in A}P(a)=1/2
. Then Markov operator
M_{P}
acts as

(M_{P}f)(g)=\sum_{a\in A\cup A^{-1}}P(a)f(ag)

and
M_{P}
is the operator associated with a random walk on the (left) Cayley graph
\Gamma(G,A)
, where transition
g\to ag
holds with probability
P(a)
.

The case of uniform distribution on
A\cup A^{-1}
(i.e., of a simple random walk) is called isotropic case, while non-uniform distribution corresponds to the anisotropic case.

Basic questions about spectra of infinite graphs are:

[leftmargin=*,labelsep=4.9mm]

1.

What is the shape (up to homeomorphism) of
\mathop{\rm Sp}\nolimits(M)
?

2.

What can be said about spectral measures
\mu_{\varphi}
associated with functions
\varphi\in\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
, in particular, with delta functions
\delta_{v}
,
v\in V
?

As usual in mathematical physics, the gaps in the spectrum, discrete and singular continuous parts of the spectrum are of special interest. Also
an
important case is when graph
\Gamma
has a subgroup of
the
group of automorphisms acting on the set of vertices freely and co-compactly (i.e., with finitely many orbits). The case of vertex transitive graphs and especially of the Cayley graph is of special interest and is related to many topics in abstract harmonic analysis, operator algebras, asymptotic group theory and theory of random walks. Among open problems, let us mention the following.

{Problem}

Can the spectrum of a Cayley graph of a finitely generated group be a Cantor set? (i.e., homeomorphic to a Cantor set). The problem is open in both isotropic and anisotropic cases.

{Problem}

Can a torsion free group have a gap in spectrum?

If the answer to the last question is affirmative, then this would give a counterexample to the Kadison - Kaplanski conjecture on idempotents.

Spectral theory of graphs of algebraic origin is a part of spectral theory of convolution operators in
\ell^{2}(G)
or
\ell^{2}(G)\oplus\ldots\oplus\ell^{2}(G)
given by elements of a group algebra
\mathbb{C}[G]
or
n\times n
matrices with entries in
\mathbb{C}[G]
and is closely related to many problems on
L^{2}
-invariants, including
L^{2}
-Betti numbers, Novikov-Slubin invariants, etc.

The state of art of the above
problem is roughly
as follows. Spectra of Euclidean grids (lattices
\cong\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
)

Can One Hear the Shape of a Group?

One of interesting directions of studies in spectral theory of graphs is finding of iso-spectral but not isomorphic graphs. It is inspired by the famous question of M. Kac, “Can you hear the shape of a drum”
Kac 1966
. It attracted a lot of attention of researchers, and after several preliminary results, starting with the result of J. Milnor
Milnor 1964
, the negative answer was given in 1992 by C. Gordon, D. Webb and S. Walpert, who constructed a pair of plane regions that have different shapes but identical eigenvalues
Gordon and Webb 1996
. The regions are concave polygons, their construction uses group theoretical result of T. Sunada
Sunada 1985
.

In 1993, A. Valete
Valette 1994
raised the following question; “Can one hear the shape of a group?”, which means “Does the spectrum of the Cayley graph determines it up to isometry?”. The answer is immediate, and is
no
as the spectra of all grids
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

[-1,1]
. Still the question has some interest. The paper

{Theorem}
[

[leftmargin=*,labelsep=4.9mm]

\omega\in\Omega

\Sigma=[-\frac{1}{2},0]\cup[\frac{1}{2},1].

2.

Moreover, for each
\omega\in\Omega

\mathcal{G}_{\omega}

(i.e., there is a surjective homomorphism
\widetilde{G}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{G}_{\omega}
) generated by

Substitutional and Schreier Dynamical System

Given an alphabet
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}
and a substitution
\rho\colon A\to A^{*},\rho(a_{i})=A_{i}(a_{\mu})
, assuming that for some distinguished symbol
a\in A
,
a
is a prefix of
\rho(a)
, we can consider the sequence of iterates

a\longrightarrow\rho(a)\longrightarrow\rho^{2}(a)\longrightarrow\ldots\longrightarrow\rho^{n}(a)\longrightarrow\ldots,

where application of
\rho
to a word
W\in A^{*}
means the replacement of each symbol
a_{i}
in
A
by
\rho(a_{i})
. If we denote
W_{n}=\rho^{n}(a)
, then
W_{n}
is a prefix of
W_{n+1},n=1,2,\ldots
and there is a natural limit

W_{\infty}=\lim_{n\to\infty}W_{n}.

This limit

W_{\infty}
is an infinite word over
A
and the words
W_{n}
are prefixes of
W_{\infty}
. Also
W_{\infty}
is a fixed point of
\rho
:
\rho(W_{\infty})=W_{\infty}
. Using
W_{\infty}
, we can now define subshifts of the full shifts
(A^{\mathbb{N}},T)
and
(A^{\mathbb{Z}},T)
(where
T
is a shift map in the space of sequences). Let us do this for the bilateral shift.

Let
\mathcal{L}(\rho)=\{W\in A^{*}\mid W\text{ is a subword of }W_{\infty}\}
. Equivalently,
\mathcal{L}(\rho)
consists of words that appear as a subword of some
W_{n}
(and hence in all
W_{k},k\geq n
).

Now let
\Omega_{\rho}
be the set of sequences
\omega=(\omega_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
that are unions of words from
\mathcal{L}(\rho)
, where
\omega_{n}\in A
. In other words,
\omega\in\Omega_{\rho}
if and only if for all
m<0
,
n>0
there exist
M<m
,
N>n
such that the subword
\omega_{M}\ldots\omega_{N}
of
\omega
belongs to
\mathcal{L}(\rho)
. Obviously,
\Omega_{\rho}
is shift invariant closed subset of
A^{\mathbb{Z}}
. The dynamical system
(\Omega_{\rho},T)
with the shift map
T
restricted to
\Omega_{\rho}
is a substitutional dynamical system generated by
\rho
.

The most important case is when such system is minimal, i.e., for each
x\in\Omega_{\rho}
the orbit
\{T^{n}x\}_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}
is dense in
\Omega_{\rho}
.
For instance, this
is the case when the substitution
\rho
is primitive, which means that there exists
K
such that for each
i,j,1\leq i,j\leq m

the
symbol
a_{i}
occur in the word
\rho^{K}(a_{j})
.

By Krylov-Bogolyubov theorem, the system
(\Omega_{\tau},T)
has at least one
T
-invariant probability measure and the invariant ergodic measures (i.e., extreme points of the simplex of
T
-invariant probability measures) are of special interest. Another important case is when the system
(\Omega_{\rho},T)
is uniquely ergodic, i.e., there is only one invariant probability measure (necessarily ergodic).

A subshift
(\Omega_{\rho},T)
is called linearly repetitive (LR) if there exists a constant
C
such that any word
W\in\mathcal{L}(\rho)
occurs in any word
U\in\mathcal{L}(\rho)
of length
\geq C|W|
. This is a stronger condition than minimality. The following result
goes back to M. Boshernitzan
Boshernitzan 1984/85
(see also
Durand 2000
).

{Theorem}

Let
(\Omega,T)
be a linearly repetitive subshift. Then, the subshift is uniquely ergodic.

Here, it is not necessary for the subshift to be generated by a substitution.
It is known that subshifts associated with primitive substitutions are linearly repetitive
Damanik and Zare 2000
;
Durand et al. 1999
.
Theorem 1 of
Damanik and Lenz 2006
shows that linear repetitivity in fact holds for subshifts associated to any substitution provided minimality holds. Unique ergodicity is then a direct consequence of linear repetitivity due to Theorem
12
.

The classical example of
a
substitutional system is the Thue-Morse system determined by the substitution
0\longrightarrow 01,1\longrightarrow 10
over binary alphabet
Berstel et al. 2009
.

Following
Grigorchuk et al. 2017
;
Grigorchuk et al. 2017
;
Grigorchuk et al. 2018
we consider the substitution

over alphabet

and system

generated by it. Despite

not being

satisfies the linear repetivity property (in fact the same system can be generated by a primitive substitution

An additional property of the fixed point

is that it is a Toeplitz sequence. i.e., for each entry

of

there is period

Random Model and Concluding Remarks

As explained above, the spectral problem associated with groups and their Schreier graphs in many important examples could be converted into study of invariant sets and dynamical properties of multi-dimensional rational maps. Some of these maps, like (

Bartholdi and Grigorchuk 2000
;
Bartholdi and Grigorchuk 2000
;
Grigorchuk and Šuniḱ 2006
;
Grigorchuk and Šunić 2008
. The roots of their integrability are comprehensively investigated in

. The examples given by (
5

6

9
), (
10
), (
11
), (

an
invariant set of fractal nature, and computer simulations demonstrate their chaotic behavior, shown by dynamical pictures given by Figures

The families of groups
\mathcal{G}_{\omega},\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}_{\omega},

\omega\in\Omega=\{0,1,2\}^{\mathbb{N}}
(and many other similar families can be created) can be viewed as a random group if
\Omega
is supplied with a shift invariant probability measure (for instance, Bernoulli or more generally Markov measure). The first step in this direction is publication
Benli et al. 2014
where it is shown that for any ergodic shift invariant probability measure satisfying a mild extra condition (all Bernoulli measures satisfy it), there is a constant
\beta<1
such that the growth function
\gamma_{\mathcal{G}_{\omega}}(n)
is bounded by
e^{n^{\beta}}
.

More general model would be to supply the space
\mathcal{M}=\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{M}_{k}
of finitely generated groups or any of its subspaces
\mathcal{M}_{k}

with
a measure
\mu
(finite, or infinite, invariant or quasi-invariant with respect to any reasonable group or semigroup of transformations of the space) and study the typical properties of groups with respect to
\mu
. The system
(\mathcal{G}_{\omega},\Omega,T,\mu)
(where
T
denotes the shift) is just
one
example of this sort. As suggested in
Grigorchuk 2005
, it would be wonderful if one
could supply the space
\mathcal{M}
with
a measure that is invariant (or at least quasi-invariant) with respect to the group of finitary Nielsen transformations defined over infinite alphabet
\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots\}
.

Additionally to the randomness of groups,
one can associate with each particular group
a random family of Schreier graphs, like the family
\Gamma_{\xi}
,
\xi\in\partial T
for a group

using the uniform Bernoulli measure on the boundary (other choices for

;
Kravchenko 2010
;

Finally, even if we fix a group, say

,

Recall a classical construction of skew product in dynamical systems. Given two spaces
(X,\mu),(Y,\nu)
, the measure
\nu
preserving transformation
S\colon Y\to Y
, and for any
y\in Y
the measure
\mu
preserving transformation
T_{y}\colon X\to X
, under the assumption that the map
X\times Y\to X
,
(x,y)\mapsto T_{y}x
is measurable, one can consider the map
Q\colon X\times Y\to X\times Y
,
Q(x,y)=(T_{y}x,Sy)
, which preserves the measure
\mu\times\nu
. Natural conditions imply that
Q
is ergodic if
S
and
T_{y}
,
y\in Y
are ergodic. If for
k=1,2,\ldots
,
we
put

T_{y}^{(k)}=T_{S^{k}y}\circ T_{S^{k-1}y}\circ\ldots\circ T_{Sy}\circ T_{y},

then the random ergodic theorem of Halmos–Kakutani
Halmos 1956
;
Kakutani 1951
states that for
f\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
,
\nu
almost surely the averages
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}f(T_{y}^{(k)}x)
converge
\mu
almost surely to some function
f^{*}_{y}(x)\in L^{1}(X,\mu)
. In the simplest case when
Y=\{1,\ldots,m\}
and
\nu
is given by
a
probability vector
(p_{1},\ldots,p_{m})
,
p_{i}>0
,
i=1,\ldots,m
,
\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}=1
, we have
m
transformations
T_{1},\ldots,T_{m}
of
X
. The semigroup generated by them typically is a free semigroup and if
T_{1},\ldots,T_{m}
are invertible in a typical case, the group
\left<T_{1},\ldots,T_{m}\right>
is a free group of rank
m
.

The question of whether the pointwise ergodic theorem of Birkhoff holds for actions of a free group was raised by V. Arnold and A. Krylov
Arnol’d and Krylov 1963
and answered affirmatively by the first author in
Grigorchuk 1987
. A similar theorem is proven for the action of a free semigroup
Grigorchuk 2000
. The proofs are based on the use of the skew product when

is the Bernoulli shift on
\Lambda=\{1,2,\ldots,m\}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
\nu
is a uniform Bernoulli measure on

By a different method, a similar result for the free group actions was obtained by A. Nevo and E. Stein
Nevo and Stein 1994
. The method from
Grigorchuk 1987
;
Grigorchuk 2000
was used by A. Bufetov to get ergodic theorems for a large class of hyperbolic groups
Bufetov 2002
. Ergodic theorem for action of non-commutative groups
became popular

Bowen and Nevo 2015
;
Bowen and Nevo 2019

In fact, the product measure
\mu\times\nu

is
\nu

\mu=\int_{Y}(T_{y})_{*}\mu\differential{\nu(y)}.

In the case of

,
\nu=(p_{1},\ldots,p_{m})

\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_{i}(T_{i})_{*}\mu

(i.e., the
