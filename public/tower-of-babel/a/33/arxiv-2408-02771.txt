Symmetrizing polytopes and posets
Federico Castillo, Fu Liu

Abstract
. Motivated by the authors’ work on permuto-associahedra, which can be considered as a symmetrization of the associahedron using the symmetric group, we introduce and study the 𝔊 {\mathfrak{G}} -symmetrization of an arbitrary polytope P P for any reflection group 𝔊 {\mathfrak{G}} . We show that the combinatorics, and moreover, the normal fan of such a symmetrization can be recovered from its refined fundamental fan, a decorated poset describing how the normal fan of P P subdivides the fundamental chamber associated to the reflection group 𝔊 {\mathfrak{G}} . One important application of our results is providing a way to approach the realization problem of a 𝔊 {\mathfrak{G}} -symmetric poset ℱ {\mathcal{F}} , that is, the problem of constructing a polytope whose face poset is ℱ {\mathcal{F}} . Instead of working with the original poset ℱ , {\mathcal{F}}, we look at its dual poset 𝒯 \mathcal{T} (which is 𝔊 {\mathfrak{G}} -symmetric as well) and focus on a generating subposet 𝒵 \mathcal{Z} of 𝒯 \mathcal{T} , and reduce the problem to realizing 𝒵 \mathcal{Z} as a refined fundamental fan.



1.
Introduction

The five platonic solids have been known for thousands of years.
Ancient Greeks were fascinated by their symmetries, even though the precise notion of symmetry was not defined until much later.
In modern terms we can define the symmetric group of a polytope
P
as the group of linear transformations
T
such that
T(P)=P
;
see
[
4
]
for a general survey about computing symmetries.
Around a hundred years ago, Schiläfi studied regular polytopes, higher dimensional analogues of platonic solids, and classified them.
Formally, a regular polytope is a polytope such that its symmetric group acts transitively on its set of flags of faces.
In this paper we construct polytopes satisfying a weaker symmetric property.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
{\mathfrak{G}}\subset\mathfrak{Gl}(V)
be a group of linear transformations.
We define a
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetric polytope
to be a polytope
P\subset V
such that
Pg=P
for all
g\in{\mathfrak{G}}
.
Equivalently, a
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetric polytope is the convex hull of finitely many
{\mathfrak{G}}
-orbits.

Polytopes that are
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetric for particular groups
{\mathfrak{G}}
have been studied in the literature.
One example are
permutation polytopes

[
3
]
where
{\mathfrak{G}}
is the symmetric group acting on the set of matrices by matrix multiplication; the quintessential example is the Birkhoff polytope.
Another example is
{\mathfrak{G}}=\mathbb{Z}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
acting by negation (
x\mapsto-x
).
In this case a
\mathbb{Z}/2{\mathbb{Z}}
-symmetric polytope is known as a
centrally symmetric
polytope and this has been extensively studied in particular with respect to their f-vectors
[
13
]
.

In this paper we focus on a particular class of groups:
finite reflection groups
.
By definition, these are finite groups of linear transformations generated by reflections on a vector space.
Examples include the symmetric groups of the platonic solids, and more generally, of regular polytopes.
There is a specific family of examples we are interested in this paper: the symmetric group of the
(d-1)
-regular simplex which is known as the type-A Coxeter group and it is isomorphic to the symmetric group
{\mathfrak{S}}_{d}
.

In the case where all the vertices of a
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetric polytope
P
form a single
{\mathfrak{G}}
-orbit,
P
is called a
{\mathfrak{G}}
-permutohedron
(some sources called this orbit polytopes, weight polytopes, or
\Phi
-permutohedra when they want to emphasize in the root system rather than the reflection group).
See Figure
1B
for a three dimensional permutohedron.
This case has been widely studied, see for instance
[
15
]
; in particular, the face poset is well understood.
The general case, with multiple orbits, seems unexplored.
Recent examples of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{d}
-symmetric polytopes are the lineup polytopes of hypersimplices
[
6
]
and of product of simplices
[
5
]
.
In this paper we systematically construct
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetric polytopes by a process we call
symmetrization
.

Definition 1.2
.

For any polytope
P\subseteq V
, we define its
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetrization
to be the polytope

(1.1)

{\mathfrak{G}}(P)=\operatorname{Conv}\{{\boldsymbol{x}}g~:~g\in\mathfrak{G},{\boldsymbol{x}}\in P\}.

The combinatorics of the symmetrization are non-trivial.
In Figure

The one on the left, Figure

The one on the right, Figure

Symmetrizing combinatorially equivalent polytopes can lead to combinatorially different polytopes.

The combinatorics of a polytope is captured by its face poset, and the normal fan of a polytope contains not only its combinatorics but also some of its geometric information. Our first main result, Theorem
3.13
, describes the normal fan of
{\mathfrak{G}}(P)
in terms of the normal fan of
P
, as long as
P
satisfies certain properties.
The main feature of finite reflection groups that we exploit is the existence of a fundamental chamber
\Phi
.
This is a cone that controls the geometry of the group action.
To describe the combinatorics of the symmetrization we need to understand, very precisely, how the fundamental chamber is subdivided by the normal fan of
P
.
This lead us to the definition of the “fundamental fan” and its “refined” version, expanded in Section
4
.
The combinatorics of the refined fundamental fan is described by a decorated poset that gives enough information to construct the face poset of the resulting symmetrization.
Theorem
5.6
allows us to go from the refined fundamental fan of
P
to the normal fan of
{\mathfrak{G}}(P)
.

The main application of this article is an instance of the realization problem, that is, determine whether a given poset is the face poset of a polytope.
A
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetric poset
is a poset with a
{\mathfrak{G}}
-action on the elements that preserves the partial order.
Theorem
6.6
reduces the realization problem of a
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetric poset to a problem of realizing its generating subposet.
We give an example of how to take advantage of our result to solve such a realization problem in the last section.
We realize a poset that is a
hybrid
between the poset of ordered set partitions and the Boolean poset.

In the literature, there have been instances of polytopes where two combinatorial structures are combined to form a new one.
The face posets of some of them are symmetric posets.
Typically, this involves having polytopes
P
and
Q
where each face of
P
is modified using a structure derived from
Q
.
One recent example is the bipermutohedron
[
1
]
.
This polytope is constructed at the level of fans by subdividing the chambers of the normal fan of the diagonal simplex with carefully placed copies of the braid fan, and its face poset is
{\mathfrak{S}}_{d}
-symmetric.
Another example of a symmetric poset that can be realized as a polytope is the permuto-associahedron, first constructed by Kapranov
[
12
]
as a hybrid of the face poset of the permutohedron and that of an associahedron.
It was then realized as polytopes independently by Reiner-Ziegler
[
16
]
, Gaiffi
[
9
]
, and the authors
[
7
]
.
A different hybrid between permutohedron and associhedron is constructed by Baralić-Ivanović-Petrić
[
2
]
both in the poset setting and in the polytope setting.

The original motivation of this paper comes from the observation that all aforementioned constructions for permuto-associahedron can be viewed as the
{\mathfrak{S}}_{d}
-symmetrization of an associahedron.
However, the goal of this paper is twofold.
On one hand, we want to build a general theory on
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetrization of a polytope (satisfying some conditions) with an arbitrary reflection group
{\mathfrak{G}}
that unifies these constructions.
One the other hand, since “combining two structures” is not a formal definition, it would be interesting to establish formally when one can say a poset (a polytope, respectively) is a
hybrid
of two other posets (polytopes, respectively).
As the
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetrization of a polytope
P
can be considered as the hybrid between a
{\mathfrak{G}}
-permutohedron and the polytope
P
, studying the connection of the face poset of
{\mathfrak{G}}(P)
to the combinatorics of the fundamental chamber
\Phi
associated to
{\mathfrak{G}}
and the face poset of
P
will give us hints on what might be a correct definition of a
hybrid
poset, at least in this particular setting (see Definition
3.15
and Remark
3.15
).
We remark that although the face poset of the bipermutohedron
[
1
]
is
{\mathfrak{S}}_{d}
-symmetric, it does not fit in our framework of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{d}
-symmetrizations, and thus it is a not a
hybrid
in the sense we define in this paper.

In an upcoming paper, we will explore the connection between the type-A symmetrization of a polytope and its type-B symmetrization, and show how to construct its type-B symmetrization from its type-A symmetrization.

Organization of the paper

In Section
2

In Section
3
we develop the basic results about symmetrizing a polytope.
In Section
4
we focus on the combinatorics of fundamental fans.
In Section

we describe symmetrization in terms of posets.
Finally in Secion
6

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Polytopes and normal fans

We begin by reviewing basic notions of polyhedral geometry.
A general reference is
[
8
]
.
Let
V\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a subspace of the
d
-dimensional Euclidean space and
W
is the dual space of
V
that contains all linear functionals on
V
,
and suppose the perfect pairing between
V
and
W
is
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:W\times V\to{\mathbb{R}}
.

In this paper, we will assume that
[\cdot,\cdot]
is an inner product on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(which will be the dot product on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
for all the examples we have).
This naturally gives a way to define the dual space
W
of
V
as a quotient space of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

More precisely,
W={\mathbb{R}}^{d}/V^{\perp}
, where
V^{\perp}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\ :\ [{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{v}}]=0,\ \forall{\boldsymbol{v}}\in V\}
is the
orthogonal complement
of
V
,
and the perfect pairing between
V
and
W
is induced by the inner product
[\cdot,\cdot]
. In the case when
V\neq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and thus
V^{\perp}
is nontrivial, each vector
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in W
is an equivalent class of vectors in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Abusing the notation, we always use one of the vectors in this equivalent class to represent
{\boldsymbol{w}}
, called a
representative
of
{\boldsymbol{w}}
.

Let
U\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be an affine space in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
that is a translation of
V,
that is, there exists
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
U=V+{\boldsymbol{x}}.

One sees that there exists a unique
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in V^{\perp}
such that
U=V+{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0};
in this case, we will write
U=V({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}).
We may consider
U=V({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})
and
W
a pair of dual spaces with perfect pairing induced by the pairing between
V
and
W
, which is induced by the inner product on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Abusing the notation, we still use
\langle\ ,\ \rangle
to denote the pairing between
U
and
W
. Hence, for any
{\boldsymbol{u}}\in U
and
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in W
, we have

\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{u}}\rangle\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\rangle=[{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{u}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}].

A
polytope
in
U
is the convex hull of a finite set in
U
. We often call an
e
-dimensional polytope an
e
-polytope
.
A
polyhedron
is the intersection of finitely many closed halfspaces.
By Minskowsi-Weyl Theorem
[
8
, Theorem 1.4]
a polytope is a bounded polyhedron.
A
face
of a polyhedron
P
in
U
is a subset
F\subseteq P
such that there exists
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in W
such that

F=\left\{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in P~:~\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle\geq\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\rangle,\quad\forall{\boldsymbol{y}}\in P\right\}.

Notice that
P
is a face of itself, by considering
{\boldsymbol{w}}=\mathbf{0}
.
In addition we consider
\emptyset
to be a face of
P
(of dimension -1).
We denote by
f_{i}(P)
the number of
i
-dimensional faces of
P
, and call
(f_{0},f_{1},\dots,f_{e-1},f_{e})
the
f-vector
of
P
.
In this paper, when we discuss interior of a polyhedron
P
, denoted by
P^{\circ}
, we always mean the relative interior of
P
.

A
homogeneous cone
is a polyhedron defined by homogeneous linear inequalities. Alternatively, a homogeneous cone
K
can be defined as the
conic hull
of a set
R=\{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{r}}_{k}\}
of vectors:

K=\operatorname{Cone}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{r}}_{k})\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}{\boldsymbol{r}}_{i}\ :\ \lambda_{i}\geq 0\right\}.

We call any such a set
R
is a
generating set
for
K
, and an inclusion-minimal generating set is a
basis
for
K
.
A
(polyhedral) cone
is a translation of a homogeneous cone.
A cone is
pointed
if it contains a
0
-dimensional face.
It is well-known that for a pointed cone
K
, a set
\{{\boldsymbol{r}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{r}}_{k}\}
of vectors is a basis for
K
if and only if
\{\operatorname{Cone}({\boldsymbol{r}}_{i})\ :\ 1\leq i\leq k\}
is the set of rays, i.e., one-dimensional faces, of
K.
A
simplicial
cone is a pointed cone
K
generated by an independent set of vectors, which is equivalent to the number of rays of
K
equals to the dimension of
K
.

A
fan
is a collection
\Sigma
of cones that is a simplicial complex, i.e., if
\sigma\in\Sigma
, then any face of
\sigma
is in
\Sigma
, and if
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\in\Sigma,
then
\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2}
is a face of both.
The maximal dimensional cones of a fan are called
chambers
, and the
dimension
of a fan is the dimension of its maximal dimensional cones.
We denote by
f^{i}(\Sigma)
the number of codimension
i
cones in
\Sigma
.
The
support
of a fan is the union of its cones.
A fan is

if its support is the entire space.

A
conic dissection
of
W
is a collection of full-dimensional cones such that the union is
W
and different cones have disjoint interiors.
Note that the chambers of a complete fan form a conic dissection.
A fan or conic dissection is
pointed
(resp.
homogeneous
) if all of its cones are pointed (resp. homogeneous).
All the fans in
W
and conic dissections of
W
we consider in this paper are pointed and homogeneous, and all the fans in
U=V({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})
are pointed with
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
being their
0
-dimensional faces.

The set of all nonempty faces of
P
partially ordered by inclusion forms the
face poset

\mathcal{F}(P)
of

.
Note that the face poset of an

Likewise, the set of all cones in a fan

If two polytopes or fans have isomorphic face posets, we say they are

; otherwise, we say they are

.

Suppose

Since

The following lemma summarize a few results on the relation between normal cones and faces of a polytope that can be verified directly from the definition.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
P
be a polytope.
The
F\mapsto\operatorname{ncone}(F,P)
induces an order-reversing bijection from

to
{\mathcal{F}}(\Sigma(P))
. That is,
for any two faces

F\subseteq G\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\operatorname{ncone}(G,P)\subseteq\operatorname{ncone}(F,P).

Therefore, the poset
{\mathcal{F}}(P)
is isomorphic to the dual of the poset
{\mathcal{F}}(\Sigma(P))
.

Furthermore, if
F
is an
k

, then
\operatorname{ncone}(F,P)
is codimension
k
in
\Sigma(P)
. Hence, for any

f_{k}(P)=f^{k}(\Sigma(P)).

2.2.
Finite reflection groups

Recall that
V
is a subspace of

\langle g{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{u}}\rangle=\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{u}}g\rangle,\quad\text{for all $g\in{\mathfrak{G}}$, ${\boldsymbol{u}}\in U$, ${\boldsymbol{w}}\in W$}.

We define the hyperplane arrangement

Proof.

For any

\Phi g
, and hence is pointed as well.
We will verify that
{\mathscr{K}}_{P}
satisfies the properties of being a conic dissection.

We first show that for each vertex
{\boldsymbol{v}}
of
P
and each

we have that the cone
\kappa({\boldsymbol{v}},g)
is full-dimensional in

However, since
\kappa({\boldsymbol{v}},g)=g^{-1}[\Phi\cap\operatorname{ncone}({\boldsymbol{v}},P)]
,
it is enough to show that
\Phi\cap\operatorname{ncone}({\boldsymbol{v}},P)
is full-dimensional.
Because

is
\Phi
-placed, there exists

This together with the fact that both

implies that

is full-dimensional. Therefore,

is a set of full-dimensional cones in

Note that chambers in a normal fan form a conic dissection.

imply that

Theorem 3.13
.

Let

(1)

(2)

({\boldsymbol{v}},g)\in{\boldsymbol{V}}\times\mathfrak{G}
, we have
\operatorname{ncone}({\boldsymbol{v}}g,\mathfrak{G}(P))=\kappa({\boldsymbol{v}},g)

Proof.

Using Lemma 2.4 of

For any distinct pairs

\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{v}}g\rangle>\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}g^{\prime}\rangle\qquad\forall{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\kappa^{\circ}({\boldsymbol{v}},g).

We prove (
3.3
) by introducing an intermediate product and showing that for all
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\kappa^{\circ}({\boldsymbol{v}},g),

(3.4)

\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{v}}g\rangle\geq\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}g\rangle\geq\langle{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}g^{\prime}\rangle,

where the first equality holds if and only if
{\boldsymbol{v}}={\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}
and the second equality holds if and only
g=g^{\prime}
.
Let
{\boldsymbol{z}}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=g{\boldsymbol{w}}.
Then

(3.5)

{\boldsymbol{z}}=g{\boldsymbol{w}}\in g\kappa^{\circ}({\boldsymbol{v}},g)=\Phi^{\circ}\cap\operatorname{ncone}^{\circ}({\boldsymbol{v}},P).

Replacing
{\boldsymbol{w}}
with

and applying (
2.3
), we see that (

\langle{\boldsymbol{z}},{\boldsymbol{v}}\rangle\geq\langle{\boldsymbol{z}},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}\rangle\geq\langle{\boldsymbol{z}},{\boldsymbol{v}}^{\prime}g^{\prime}g^{-1}\rangle.

First, because

4.2.
Refined Fundamental Fan

In this part, we will separate cones in
\mathrm{FFan}(P)
into different sets according to which face of
\Phi

Recall that we use
\Sigma(\Phi)
to denote the fan that is induced by
\Phi

\Phi

Definition 4.3
.

Suppose
\tau\in\mathrm{FFan}(P)
or more generally
\tau
is convex subset of
\Phi
. Let
\Phi_{\tau}
be the inclusion-minimal face of
\Phi
that contains
\tau
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\tau\in\mathrm{FFan}(P)
or more generally
\tau
is a convex subset of
\Phi
. Then
\Phi_{\tau}
is the unique
\phi\in\Sigma(\Phi)
satisfying
\tau\subseteq\phi
and
\tau\cap\phi^{\circ}\neq\emptyset
.
Hence,
\Phi_{\tau}=\Phi
if and only if
\tau\cap\Phi^{\circ}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

We already know that
\tau\subseteq\Phi_{\tau}.

Suppose
\tau\cap\Phi_{\tau}^{\circ}=\emptyset
.
Since
\tau\subseteq\Phi_{\tau}
, we must have that
\tau
is contained in the boundary of
\Phi_{\tau}.

However, this implies that
\tau
is contained in one of the facet of
\tau,
contradicting the definition of
\Phi_{\tau}
.
Hence,
\Phi_{\tau}
satisfies the two required conditions.
Let
\phi
be a face of
\Phi
such that
\tau\subseteq\phi
but
\phi\neq\Phi_{\tau}
.
By the definition of
\Phi_{\tau}
, we know that
\Phi_{\tau}
is a proper face of
\phi
.
Thus,
\Phi_{\tau}
has an empty intersection with
\phi^{\circ}
.
Therefore,
\tau
, which is contained in
\Phi_{\tau}
, is disjoint from
\phi^{\circ}
as well.
Hence, the first conclusion of the lemma follows.
The second conclusion follows from the first conclusion together with the fact that
\tau\subseteq\Phi
.
∎

For each
\phi\in\Sigma(\Phi),
we define

(4.2)

\mathrm{FFan}(P,\phi)\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\{\tau\in\mathrm{FFan}(P)\ :\ \Phi_{\tau}=\phi\}.

Recall that
f^{i}(\Sigma)
is the number of codimension
i
cones in a fan
\Sigma
.
Although
\mathrm{FFan}(P,\phi)
is not necessarily a fan, we extend the operator
f^{i}
to them as well.

Clearly, we obtain a disjoint decomposition of
\mathrm{FFan}(P):

(4.3)

\mathrm{FFan}(P)=\bigsqcup_{\phi\in\Sigma(\Phi)}\mathrm{FFan}(P,\phi).

We call
\{\mathrm{FFan}(P,\phi)\ :\ \phi\in\Sigma(\Phi)\}
the
refined fundamental fan of
P
.

(b2)

\mathrm{FFan}(P,\Phi)

By the decompositions (

We now prove

We now give a more detailed description of the cones in
\mathrm{FFan}(P,\phi)

\Sigma(P)
and
\Sigma(\Phi)

For any fan

Clearly,

The map

Moreover, if

It follows from Lemma

and

(4.6)

\bigsqcup_{(\sigma,\phi)\in\Omega(P)}\sigma^{\circ}\cap\phi^{\circ}=\Phi=\bigsqcup_{\tau\in\mathrm{FFan}(P)}\tau^{\circ}.

(2) If

Finally, we prove (

is
\sigma

We can similarly show that

∎

\sigma\in\Sigma(P)

P^{\vee}\subset\partial\sigma
, hence
\sigma\cap\Phi^{\circ}\neq\emptyset
.
Then the claim follows Lemma
4.16
, and the conclusions of this lemma follow from the claim and Corollary
4.12
.
∎

Question 4.17
.

Is it true that
\mathcal{Z}(P,\phi)
is isomorphic to (the dual of) face poset of a face of
P
for all
\phi\in\Sigma(\Phi)
?

We finish this section by stating a consequence of Propositions
4.8
and
4.15
.

Proposition 4.18
.

Suppose
P
is a
\Phi
-placed and
\Psi
-appropriate polytope. Then
\mathcal{Z}(P)
(the combinatorics of
\mathrm{FFan}(P)
) determines
{\mathcal{F}}(P)
(the combinatorics of

5.
Symmetrizing posets

In the last section, we discussed that given a
\Phi
-placed and
\Psi
-appropriate polytope
P
, the poset
\mathcal{Z}(P)
(which captures the combinatorics of the fundamental fan
\mathrm{FFan}(P)
of
P
) does not determine the face poset
{\mathcal{F}}({\mathfrak{G}}(P))
of
{\mathfrak{G}}(P)
(which captures the combinatorics of
{\mathfrak{G}}(P)
). As a result, we introduced the refined fundamental fan of
P
.
In this section, we will introduce a special kind of poset denoted by
\mathcal{R}(P)
to capture the combinatorics of the refined fundamental fan of
P
, and show in Theorem
5.6
that the symmetrization of
\mathcal{R}(P)
recovers
{\mathcal{F}}({\mathfrak{G}}(P))
. We prove this result by analyzing the symmetrization process on each face of the fundamental chamber.
We finish this section with a discussion on the
f
-vector of
{\mathfrak{G}}(P)
.

5.1.
\Sigma
-posets and symmetrization

One way to capture the combinatorics of the refined fundamental fan is to associate each
\tau
in the face poset
\mathcal{Z}(P)
of the fundamental fan
\mathrm{FFan}(P)
with the cone
\phi=\Phi_{\tau}
.
We will make this idea more formal.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a fan.
We say a poset
\mathcal{R}
is a
\Sigma
-poset
if the underlying set is a subset of
{\mathcal{F}}\times\Sigma
for some poset
{\mathcal{F}}
. (Note that if
{\mathcal{F}}
is an antichain, then
{\mathcal{F}}
is just a set without ordering.)
Furthermore, we say
\mathcal{R}
is
order-consistent
, if for every
(\tau_{1},\sigma_{1}),(\tau_{2},\sigma_{2})\in\mathcal{R}
with
\tau_{1}\leq\tau_{2}
, we must have
\sigma_{1}\leq\sigma_{2}
.

We say two
\Sigma
-posets
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
are
isomorphic as
\Sigma
-posets
if there is a poset isomorphism
f
from
\mathcal{R}
to
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
that preserves the second entries, that is for every
(s,\sigma)\in\mathcal{R}
, if
f(\tau,\sigma)=(\tau^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})
then
\sigma=\sigma^{\prime}
.

Given a poset
{\mathcal{F}}
, and a map
\lambda:{\mathcal{F}}\to\Sigma
, we define
{\mathcal{F}}^{\lambda}
to be the
\Sigma
-poset on
\{\tau,\lambda(\tau)\}_{\tau\in{\mathcal{F}}}
ordered by
(\tau,\lambda(\tau))\leq(\tau^{\prime},\lambda(\tau^{\prime}))
if and only if
\tau\leq_{{\mathcal{F}}}\tau^{\prime}
.

It is clear that
{\mathcal{F}}^{\lambda}
is a
\Sigma
-poset that is isomorphic to
{\mathcal{F}}
as posets. Moreover,
{\mathcal{F}}^{\lambda}
is order-consistent if and only if
\lambda
is order-preserving.
The combinatorics of the refined fundamental fan of
P
can be expressed via a
\Sigma(\Phi)
-poset via the map
\tau\mapsto\Phi_{\tau}
.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
\kappa
be the map from all convex subsets of
\Phi
to
\Sigma(\Phi)
that maps
\tau
to
\Phi_{\tau}.
We call
\kappa
the
carrier map
for
\Phi
.

For convenience, we abuse notation and use
\kappa
to denote the restriction of
\kappa
to any set of convex subsets of
\Phi
, e.g., the restriction of
\kappa
to a fan supported on
\Phi
.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
P
be a
\Phi
-placed and
\Psi
-appropriate polytope.
We define the
face poset of the refined fundamental fan of
P
, denoted by
\mathcal{R}(P)
, to be the
\Sigma(\Phi)
-poset
\mathcal{Z}(P)^{\kappa}
.

Hence,
\mathcal{R}(P)
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{Z}(P)
as posets, but it contains more information encoded in the carrier map
\kappa
.

It is not true that every
\Sigma
-poset can be written as
{\mathcal{F}}^{\lambda}
.
However, almost all the
\Sigma(\Phi)
-posets considered in this paper can be expressed as
{\mathcal{F}}^{\kappa}
. Below we include a basic property of
\Sigma(\Phi)
-posets of this form.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}
be a poset on a set of convex subsets of
\Phi
ordered by inclusion. Then
\kappa
is an order-preserving map from
{\mathcal{F}}
to
\Sigma(\Phi)
. Hence,
{\mathcal{F}}^{\kappa}
is order-consistent.

In particular, for a
\Phi
-placed and
\Psi
-appropriate polytope
P
, the face poset
\mathcal{R}(P)
of its refined fundamental fan is order-consistent.

Proof.

The conclusion that
\kappa
is order-preserving following directly from the definition of
\kappa(\tau)
is the inclusion-minimal face of
\Phi
that contains
\tau
.
∎

We now define the
{\mathfrak{S}}
-symmetrization of an arbitrary
\Sigma(\Phi)
-poset.

Definition 5.5
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a
\Sigma(\Phi)
-poset. The
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetrization of
\mathcal{R}
is the
\Sigma(\mathcal{H})
-poset
{\mathfrak{G}}\mathcal{R}
whose underlying set is

\{(\tau,\eta)\ :\ (\tau,g^{-1}\eta)\in\mathcal{R},\text{ for some $g\in{\mathfrak{G}}$}\},

and with partial order defined by
(\tau_{1},\eta)\leq(\tau_{2},\eta)
if and only if there exists
g\in{\mathfrak{G}}
such that

(\tau_{1},g^{-1}\eta)\leq_{\mathcal{R}}(\tau_{2},g^{-1}\eta)
.

It is clear that if
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
are two isomorphic
\Sigma(\Phi)
-posets. Then their
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetrizations are isomorphic as posets or as
\Sigma(\mathcal{H})
-posets.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
P
be a
\Phi
-placed and
\Psi
-appropriate polytope.
Then the
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetrization
{\mathfrak{G}}\mathcal{R}(P)
of
\mathcal{R}(P)
is isomorphic to the face poset of
\Sigma({\mathfrak{G}}(P)).

Therefore, the face poset of
{\mathfrak{G}}(P)
is isomorphic to the dual of
{\mathfrak{G}}\mathcal{R}(P)
.

The following lemma is a more explicit description of
{\mathfrak{G}}\mathcal{R}(P)
.
It follows directly from the definitions.

Lemma 5.7
.

The
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetrization
{\mathfrak{G}}\mathcal{R}(P)
of
\mathcal{R}(P)
is the poset whose underlying set is

\{(\tau,g\kappa(\tau))\ :\ \tau\in\mathrm{FFan}(P),g\in{\mathfrak{G}}\},

and with partial order defined by
(\tau_{1},g_{1}\kappa(\tau_{1}))\leq(\tau_{2},g_{2}\kappa(\tau_{2}))
if and only if
\tau_{1}\subseteq\tau_{2}
and there exists
g\in{\mathfrak{G}}
such that

(5.1)

g^{-1}g_{1}\kappa(\tau_{1})=\kappa(\tau_{1})\text{ and }g^{-1}g_{2}\kappa(\tau_{2})=\kappa(\tau_{2}).

We need to develop more results before proving Theorem
5.6
.
Before that, we finish this subsection with a result that connecting
\mathcal{R}(P)
with
\Omega(P)
disscussed in the last section.

Lemma 5.8
.

We can consider
\Omega(P)
as a
\Sigma(\Phi)
-poset by defining

(5.2)

(\sigma,\phi)\leq(\sigma^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})\text{ in $\Omega(P)$ \hskip 10.22217ptif and only if \hskip 10.22217pt}\sigma\subseteq\sigma^{\prime}\text{ and }\phi\subseteq\phi^{\prime}.

The map
(\sigma,\phi)\mapsto(\sigma\cap\phi,\phi)
gives a
\Sigma(\Phi)
-poset isomorphism from
\Omega(P)
to
\mathcal{R}(P)
.

Proof.

It follows directly from Lemma
4.10
, Corollaries
4.11
and
4.12
.
∎

Remark 5.9
.

The poset
\Omega(P)
considered in the above lemma is an example of
\Sigma(\Phi)
-poset that cannot be expressed as
{\mathcal{F}}^{\lambda}
in general.
Indeed, for most of examples we consider such as Example
4.5
, the first entries
\sigma
appeaing in elements of
\Omega(P)
are
not
distinct, and hence do not form a poset.
Although it is sometimes handy to describe
\mathcal{R}(P)
using
\Omega(P)
, for the discussion of symmetrization of posets, the representation of
\mathcal{R}(P)
seems to be the right choice.

5.2.
Refined analysis

In order to prove Theorem
5.6
, we first will rewrite (
4.1
) as a disjoint sum of elements in order to understand which cones appearing in
\Sigma({\mathfrak{G}}(P))
better.
We start with reviewing some group-theoretic concepts and a result.

Definition 5.10
.

Let
\sigma
be a cone (or a subset) in
W
.
We say
g\in{\mathfrak{G}}

stabilizes

\sigma
if
g\sigma=\sigma.

The
stabilizer of of
\sigma
(with respect to
{\mathfrak{G}}
)
is
{\mathfrak{G}}_{\sigma}:=\{g\in{\mathfrak{G}}\ :\ g\sigma=\sigma\}
, which is the subgroup of
{\mathfrak{G}}
that consists all elements that stabilize
\sigma.

The
orbit of
\sigma
(in
{\mathfrak{G}}
)
is
{\mathfrak{G}}\sigma:=\{g\sigma\ :\ g\in{\mathfrak{G}}\}
.

It is a basic group theory result that there is a bijection between left cosets of the stabilizer
{\mathfrak{G}}_{\tau}
and the orbit
{\mathfrak{G}}\cdot\tau
. More precisely:

Lemma 5.11
.

Suppose
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{\ell}\}
is a set of representatives of left cosets of
{\mathfrak{G}}_{\tau},
that is,
\displaystyle{\mathfrak{G}}=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{\ell}g_{i}{\mathfrak{G}}_{\tau}
. Then
{\mathfrak{G}}\tau
consists of
g_{1}\tau,\dots,g_{\ell}\tau
.
Therefore,
|{\mathfrak{G}}_{\tau}|\cdot|{\mathfrak{G}}\tau|=|{\mathfrak{G}}|.

The following lemma summarizes results from
[
11
, Secion 1.12]
that will be useful for the discussion in this part.

Lemma 5.12
.

The followings are true:

(1)

For any
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in W
, there is a unique
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\Phi
such that
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in{\mathfrak{G}}{\boldsymbol{w}}
. Hence,

\displaystyle W=\bigsqcup_{{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\Phi}{\mathfrak{G}}{\boldsymbol{w}}.

(2)

For any
\eta\in\Sigma(\mathcal{H})
, there exists a unique subgroup of
{\mathfrak{G}}
that is the stabilizer of every point in
\eta^{\circ}.

Furthermore, if
\eta=\Phi
, this unique stabilizer is the trivial group of
{\mathfrak{G}}
.

(3)

Let
\eta,\eta^{\prime}\in\Sigma(\mathcal{H}),

{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\eta^{\circ}
and
{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}\in(\eta^{\prime})^{\circ}
. If
\eta^{\prime}\subseteq\eta
, then
{\mathfrak{G}}_{{\boldsymbol{w}}}\subseteq{\mathfrak{G}}_{{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}}.

The following corollary is an immediate consequence of the above lemma.

Corollary 5.13
.

Let
\tau,\tau^{\prime}\subseteq\Phi
and
\phi\in\Sigma(\Phi)
. Then the followings are true:

(1)

If
\tau\neq\tau^{\prime}
, then
{\mathfrak{G}}\tau
and
{\mathfrak{G}}\tau^{\prime}
are disjoint.

(2)

If
g\in{\mathfrak{G}}
stabilizes
\tau,
then
g
stabilizes every point of
\tau.
Hence,
\displaystyle{\mathfrak{G}}_{\tau}=\cap_{{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\tau}{\mathfrak{G}}_{\boldsymbol{w}}
is a pointwise stabilizer of
\tau
.

(3)

Let
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\phi^{\circ}
.
If
\tau\subseteq\phi
and
\tau\cap\phi^{\circ}\neq\emptyset,
then
{\mathfrak{G}}_{\tau}={\mathfrak{G}}_{\boldsymbol{w}}
is the unique stabilizer for all points in
\phi^{\circ}
.
Consequently, we have
{\mathfrak{G}}_{\phi}={\mathfrak{G}}_{\boldsymbol{w}}={\mathfrak{G}}_{\tau}
.

(4)

Suppose
\phi=\kappa(\tau)
. Then
{\mathfrak{G}}_{\phi}={\mathfrak{G}}_{\tau}.
Thus, for any
g,g^{\prime}\in{\mathfrak{G}}
, we have

g\phi=g^{\prime}\phi\text{ if and only if }g\tau=g^{\prime}\tau.

We remark that the above corollary holds if we replace
\Phi
with any chamber of
\Sigma(\mathcal{H})
.

Proof.

Conclusions (
1
) and (
2
) follow from Lemma
5.12
/(
1
).
We now prove (
3
).
The conclusion that
{\mathfrak{G}}_{\tau}={\mathfrak{G}}_{\boldsymbol{w}}
follows from (
2
), Lemma
5.12
/(
2
)(
3
), and the fact that for any point
{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}\in\phi,
there exists a (unique) face
\phi^{\prime}
of
\phi
such that
{\boldsymbol{w}}^{\prime}\in(\phi^{\prime})^{\circ}.
Finally, one notices that
\phi
itself is a
\tau
satisfying
\tau\cap\phi^{\circ}\neq\emptyset.
Thus,
{\mathfrak{G}}_{\phi}={\mathfrak{G}}_{\boldsymbol{w}}
.

Finally, (
4
) follows from (
3
) and Lemma
4.4
.
∎

Remark 5.14
.

The groups that arise as the stabilizers
{\mathfrak{G}}_{\phi}
of faces
\phi
of
\Phi
are the so-called
parabolic subgroups
of the reflection group
{\mathfrak{G}}

[
11
, Chapter 1.10]
.
They are in bijection with faces of
\Phi

[
11
, Chapter 1.15]
, and are all different, i.e.,
{\mathfrak{G}}_{\phi}\neq{\mathfrak{G}}_{\phi^{\prime}}
if
\phi\neq\phi^{\prime}\in\Sigma(\Phi)
.

Proof of Theorem
5.6
.

Using Corollary
5.13
/(
1
), we can rewrite (
4.1
) as

(5.3)

\Sigma({\mathfrak{G}}(P))={\mathfrak{G}}(\mathrm{FFan}(P))=\bigsqcup_{\tau\in\mathrm{FFan}(P)}{\mathfrak{G}}\tau=\bigsqcup_{\phi\in\Sigma(\Phi)}\bigsqcup_{\tau\in\mathrm{FFan}(P,\phi)}{\mathfrak{G}}\tau.

We define a map
f:\Sigma({\mathfrak{G}}(P))\to{\mathfrak{G}}\mathcal{R}(P)
in the following way: for each
\tau_{0}\in\Sigma({\mathfrak{G}}(P))
, by the above decomposition, there exists a unique
\tau\in\mathrm{FFan}(P)
such that
\tau_{0}=g\tau
for some
g\in{\mathfrak{G}}
.
We define

f(\tau_{0})=(\tau,g\kappa(\tau)).

Note that by Corollary
5.13
/(
4
), the definition of
f(\tau_{0})
is independent from the choice of
g
as long as it satisfies
\tau_{0}=g\tau
, and hence
f
is well-defined. For the same reason, given
(\tau,g\kappa(\tau))\in{\mathfrak{G}}\mathcal{R}(P)
, there exists a unique
t_{0}=gt
as its preimage. Hence,
f
is a bijection.

It remains to prove that
g_{1}\tau_{1}\subseteq g_{2}\tau_{2}
, as faces of
\Sigma({\mathfrak{G}}(P))
, if and only if
(\tau_{1},g_{1}\kappa(\tau_{1}))\leq(\tau_{2},g_{2}\kappa(\tau_{2}))
in the poset
{\mathfrak{G}}\mathcal{R}(P)
.
However, by Lemma
5.7
and Corollary
5.13
/(
4
), one sees that the latter is equivalent to

(5.4)

\tau_{1}\subseteq\tau_{2}\text{ and }\exists g\in{\mathfrak{G}}\text{ such that $g^{-1}g_{i}\in{\mathfrak{G}}_{\tau_{i}}$ for $i=1$ and $2.$}

By the Corollary
5.13
/(
1
), we must have
g^{-1}g_{i}\tau_{i}=\tau_{i}
for
i=1
and
2
.
It is then clear that (
5.4
) implies that
g_{1}\tau_{1}=g\tau_{1}\subseteq g\tau_{2}=g_{2}\tau_{2}
.
Now suppose
g_{1}\tau_{1}\subseteq g_{2}\tau_{2}
.
Then there exists
g\in{\mathfrak{G}}
such that
g_{1}\tau_{1}\subseteq g_{2}\tau_{2}\subseteq g\Phi.
Thus,
g^{-1}g_{1}\tau_{1}\subseteq g^{-1}g_{2}\tau_{2}\subseteq\Phi.

Hence, (
5.4
) follows, completing the proof.
∎

5.3.
Face vectors of symmetrizations

In this part, we derive a formula that computes the
f
-vector of
{\mathfrak{G}}(P)
from the combinatorics of the refined fundamental fan, as a consequence of results that have been presented in this section.
At the end we show that even though in general the combinatorics of the fundamental fan
\mathrm{FFan}(P)
does not determine the
f
-vector of
{\mathfrak{G}}(P)
as shown in Example
4.2
, it does determine the first two entries of the
f
-vector of
{\mathfrak{G}}(P)
(see Remark
5.20
).

Proposition 5.16
.

Suppose
P
is a
\Phi
-placed and
\Psi
-appropriate polytope. For
\phi\in\Sigma(\Phi)
, let
m(\phi)=|{\mathfrak{G}}_{\phi}|
. Then

(5.5)

f_{i}({\mathfrak{G}}(P))=\sum_{\text{$\phi:$ face of $\Phi$ of codimension at most $i$}}f^{i}(\mathrm{FFan}(P,\phi))\cdot\frac{|{\mathfrak{G}}|}{m(\phi)}.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.2
,
f_{i}({\mathfrak{G}}(P))=f^{i}(\Sigma({\mathfrak{G}}(P)))
. It follows from (
5.3
) and Lemma
5.11
that
f^{i}(\Sigma({\mathfrak{G}}(P)))
is computed by (
5.5
).

∎

.

to Example

.

Lemma 5.18

Suppose

determines the first two entries in the

-vector of
{\mathfrak{G}}(P)
.

The above corollary together with Euler’s formula has the following consequence. We leave it to the readers to check the details.

Corollary 5.21
.

Suppose
U
and
W
are of dimension at most
3,
and
P
is a
\Phi
-placed and
\Psi
-appropriate polytope.
Then
f^{0}(\mathrm{FFan}(P))
and

determine the

-vector of
{\mathfrak{G}}(P)
.

6.
Realizing a symmetric poset

In the last three sections, our discussion has been focused on deriving results of the symmetrization of a
\Phi
-placed and
\Psi
-appropriate polytope
P
.
Thus, the starting point is always a given polytope
P
.
Our results allow us to form a combinatorics structure to capture the combinatorics of the refined fundamental fan of
P
, and show that it contains enough information for us to construct the face poset of its symmetrization
{\mathfrak{G}}(P)
.
It follows from Theorem
5.6
that the face poset of
{\mathfrak{G}}(P)
is always
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetric.
In this section, we turn our attention to a related realization problem:

Question 6.1
.

Given a finite
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetric poset
{\mathcal{F}}
, can we construct a polytope
P
such that
{\mathcal{F}}
is the face poset of its symmetrization
{\mathfrak{G}}(P)
?

Remark 6.2
.

Following Definition
3.15
, the above question is equivalent to determining whether a
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetric poset
{\mathcal{F}}
is a hybrid of the face poset of of a
{\mathfrak{G}}
-permutohedron and another poset
\mathcal{G}
that is polytopal.
Hence, all the discussion below can be applied to this “hybrid poset” version question.
To save space, we will leave the details of the translation to the readers.

It turns out the theories we developed so far can reduce the realization problem of a symmetric poset to a smaller task of realizing its generating subposet.
We need one more concept before state the main theorem of this section.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
\mathcal{T}
be a
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetric poset with a generating subposet
\mathcal{Z}
.
We say
\mathcal{Z}
is
{\mathfrak{G}}
-compatible
if for every
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, there exists a
\phi\in\Sigma(\Phi)
such that
{\mathfrak{G}}_{z}={\mathfrak{G}}_{\phi}
. It follows from Remark
5.14
that if
\phi
exists, it is necessarily unique.

Hence, if
\mathcal{Z}
is
{\mathfrak{G}}
-compatible, we can associate it with a
unique

{\mathfrak{G}}
-stabilizer-preserving map
\lambda:\mathcal{Z}\to\Sigma(\Phi)
that maps
z\in\mathcal{Z}
to the unique
\phi\in\Sigma(\Phi)
such that
{\mathfrak{G}}_{z}={\mathfrak{G}}_{\phi}.

Moreover, for each
\phi\in\Sigma(\Phi)
, we define
\mathcal{Z}(\phi)
to be the subposet of
\mathcal{Z}
induced on
\lambda^{-1}(\phi)=\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}\ :\ {\mathfrak{G}}_{z}={\mathfrak{G}}_{\phi}\}.

Example 6.4
.

Consider the poset
\mathcal{S}_{d}
of set partitions of
[d]
.
This is similar to ordered set partitions
\mathcal{O}_{d}
(Definition
2.7
) but blocks are unordered.
The poset
\mathcal{S}_{d}
is not the face poset of any polytope when
d>2
.
Indeed, every interval of length 2 in a face poset must have four elements (this is called the
Diamond property

[
17
, Theorem 2.7 (iii)]
), and
\mathcal{S}_{d}
contains intervals of length 2 with more than four elements whenever
d>2
.

The poset
\mathcal{S}_{d}
is
{\mathfrak{S}}_{d}
-symmetric poset, but it is not
{\mathfrak{S}}_{d}
-compatible for any choice of genertaing poset when
d>2
.
Consider the poset
\mathcal{S}_{4}
of set partitions of
[4]
.
Any generating subposet must contain an element of the form
(\left\{a,b\right\},\left\{c,d\right\})
.
The stabilizer for the action of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{4}
of such an element is the subgroup generated by the transpositions
(ab),(cd)
and the elements
(ac)(bd),(ad)(bc)
.
This subgroup does not stabilize any
\phi\in\Phi_{3}
(it is not a parabolic subrgroup), and thus no generating subposet of
\mathcal{S}_{4}
is
{\mathfrak{G}}
-compatible.

Recall the carrier map
\kappa
for
\Phi
is defined in Definition
5.2
.

Example 6.5
.

Let
P
be a
\Phi
-placed and
\Psi
-appropriate polytope.
Let
\mathcal{T}(P)
be the face poset of
\Sigma({\mathfrak{G}}(P))
and
{\mathfrak{G}}
-action is the one we have been using.
Then
\mathcal{T}(P)
is
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetric with the generating subposet
\mathcal{Z}(P)
, the face poset of
\mathrm{FFan}(P)
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{Z}(P)
is
{\mathfrak{G}}
-compatible with the carrier map
\kappa
being its associated
{\mathfrak{G}}
-stabilizer-preserving map, and for each
\phi\in\Sigma(\Phi)
, we have
\mathcal{Z}(\phi)=\mathcal{Z}(P,\phi)
.

Theorem 6.6
.

Suppose
\mathcal{T}
is
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetric with a
{\mathfrak{G}}
-compatible generating subposet
\mathcal{Z}
, and suppose
\lambda:\mathcal{Z}\to\Sigma(\Phi)
is the
{\mathfrak{G}}
-stabilizer-preserving map associated with
\mathcal{Z}
.
Let
\mathcal{R}
be the
\Sigma(\Phi)
-poset
\mathcal{Z}^{\lambda}
.
Then the
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetrization
{\mathfrak{G}}\mathcal{R}
of
\mathcal{R}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{T}.

Proof.

The proof is basically the same as that of Theorem
5.6
by defining a map
f:gz\mapsto(z,g\lambda(z))
, and proving that it is a poset isomorphism from
\mathcal{T}
to
{\mathfrak{G}}\mathcal{R}
.
∎

Now we are ready to lay a general framework to realize symmetric posets as polytopes.
The most prominent examples of this kind of realization problems are connected: Reiner-Ziegler Coxeter permuto-associahedra
[
16
]
, Baralić-Ivanović-Petrić simple permuto-associahedron
[
2
]
, and Gaiffi’s permutonestohedra
[
9
]
.
Our results provide a unified way to approach these problems.

6.1.
A recipe for realizing a symmetric poset

Given a finite
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetric poset
{\mathcal{F}},
Proposition
4.15
, Theorem
5.6
and Theorem
6.6
provide a recipe for realizing
{\mathcal{F}}
, summarized in the following three steps:

I
.

Finding the generating set
\mathcal{Z}
and its associated map
\lambda
:
Let
\mathcal{T}
be the dual of
{\mathcal{F}}
. Find a generating subposet
\mathcal{Z}
of
\mathcal{T}
that is
{\mathfrak{G}}
-compatible, and let
\lambda:\mathcal{Z}\to\Sigma(\Phi)
be the
{\mathfrak{G}}
-stabilizer-preserving map associated with
\mathcal{Z}
.
Finally, compute
\mathcal{Z}(\phi)
for each
\phi\in\Sigma(\Phi)
or at least for
\phi=\Phi
.

II
.

Realizing
\mathcal{Z}^{\lambda}
as the refined fundamental fan of a polytope:

(a)

Realize the dual of
\mathcal{Z}(\Phi)
as a
\Phi
-placed and
\Psi
-appropriate polytope
P
, and described the normal fan of
P
, and describe the
\Sigma(\Phi)
-poset
\mathcal{Z}^{\lambda}
.

(b)

Verify that
\mathcal{R}(P)
, the face poset of the refined fundamental fan of
P
, is isomorphic to
\mathcal{Z}^{\lambda}
as
\Sigma(\Phi)
-posets.

III
.

Symmetrizing the polytope:
Symmetrize
P
to obtain its
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetrization
{\mathfrak{G}}(P)
, which is a desired realization of
{\mathcal{F}}
.

In general, Steps I and III in the recipe are straightforward.
Verifying whether the dual of a given poset
\mathcal{F}
that is
\mathfrak{G}
-symmetric possesses a
\mathfrak{G}
-compatible generating subposet may require effort. However, if
\mathcal{F}
is finite, this verification is feasible.
Therefore, the approach laid out in the recipe reduces the problem of realizing a
{\mathfrak{G}}
-symmetric poset
{\mathcal{F}}
to the problem of realizing a
{\mathfrak{G}}
-generator of its dual described in Step II.

Alternative steps for Step II

We note that Step
II.(a)
is the key and most difficult part of Step II, as we have to construct a
correct
polytope
P
so that Step (b) will be successful.
Therefore, one might want to split Step
II.(a)
into two steps:

(a.i)

Realize the dual of
\mathcal{Z}(\Phi)
as a polytope
Q
without worrying about whether it is
\Phi
-placed or
\Psi
-appropriate, and describe its normal fan.

(a.ii)

Find a suitable (invertible) affine transformation to convert
Q
to a
\Phi
-placed and
\Psi
-appropriate polytope
P
, and compute its normal fan.

Finally, for Step
II.(b)
of Step II, one can use various results we developed in this paper to complete this step. For example, it follows from Lemma
5.8
that Step (b) can be replaced with the following:

(b’)

Verify that
\Omega(P)=\{(\sigma,\phi)\in\Sigma(P)\times\Sigma(\Phi)\ :\ \sigma^{\circ}\cap\phi^{\circ}\neq\emptyset\}
ordered by (
5.2
) is isomorphic to
\mathcal{Z}^{\lambda}
as
\Sigma(\Phi)
-posets.

One benefit of using the alternative step
(b’)
is that we can use the following lemma to turn the problem of verifying
\Sigma(\Phi)
-poset isomorphism to an equivalent poset isomorphism problem. We say a map
f:\mathcal{Z}\to\Omega(P)
is
\lambda
-consistent
, if for every
z\in\mathcal{Z}
, we have
f(z)=(\sigma,\lambda(z))
for some
\sigma
.

Lemma 6.7
.

Suppose
\mathcal{Z}
,
\lambda
, and
P
are produced as in Step I and Step II/
II.(a)
of the recipe.
Then there exists a poset isomorphism
f
from
\mathcal{Z}
to
\Omega(P)
that is
\lambda
-consistent if and only if there exists a
\Sigma(\Phi)
-poset isomorphism
g
from
\mathcal{Z}^{\lambda}
to
\Omega(P)
.

Proof.

Let
h
be the poset isomorphism from
\mathcal{Z}
to
\mathcal{Z}^{\lambda}
defined by mapping
z
to
(z,\lambda(z))
. One checks that if
f
is a poset isomorphism from
\mathcal{Z}
to
\Omega(P)
that is
\lambda
-consistent, then
g:=f\circ h
is a desired
\Sigma(\Phi)
-poset isomorphism
g
. Conversely, if
g
is a
\Sigma(\Phi)
-poset isomorphism from
\mathcal{Z}^{\lambda}
to
\Omega(P)
, then
f:=g\circ h^{-1}
is a desired
\lambda
-consistent poset isomorphism.
∎

Necessary conditions

For the general problem of realizing a poset
{\mathcal{F}}
as a polytope, one often starts by checking whether
{\mathcal{F}}
satisfies some simple necessary conditions for it to be polytopal, e.g, whether
{\mathcal{F}}
has the Diamond property.
Similarly, after one completes Step I and before moves on to Step II of our recipe, it is a good idea to verify a few known necessary conditions for
\mathcal{Z}^{\lambda}
to be realized as the refined fundamental fan
\mathcal{R}(P)
of a poltyope
P
. We list a few necessary conditions below, as consequences of Lemma
5.4
, Corollary
4.7
, and Lemma
4.6
, respectively:

(N1)

\lambda
is order-preserving, or equivalently,
\mathcal{Z}^{\lambda}
is order-consistent.

(N2)

\lambda
is surjective, or equivalently,
\mathcal{Z}(\phi)
is nonempty for each
\phi\in\Sigma(\Phi)
.

(N3)

The maximal elements of
\mathcal{Z}(\phi)
are of rank
\dim(\phi)
, for each
\phi\in\Sigma(\Phi)
.

The necessary conditions listed above are all ones that can be easily verified before Step II. However, there are also necessary conditions that are harder to verify and are related to Step II:

(Na)

\mathcal{Z}(\Phi)
is polytopal, i.e., it is isomorphic to the face poset of a polytope.

(Nb)

There exists a fan
\Gamma
supported on the fundamental chamber
\Phi
such that
{\mathcal{F}}(\Gamma)^{\kappa}
and
\mathcal{Z}^{\lambda}
are isomorphic
\Sigma(\Phi)
-posets.

One sees that the difficulty of checking condition
(Na)
is basically the same as that of completing Step
(a.i)
.
Although condition
(Nb)
seems to be related to Step
II.(b)
, it really is a condition one should think of during Step
II.(a)
or Step
(a.ii)
, as we said above that we need to make sure that the polytope constructed in Step
II.(a)
is a
correct
one.
Furtheremore, since
\mathcal{Z}^{\lambda}
tells us all the combinatorics about the refined fundamental fan, constructing a
\Gamma
such that
{\mathcal{F}}(\Gamma)^{\kappa}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{Z}^{\lambda}
as
\Sigma(\Phi)
-posets could be very enlightening for completing Step
(a.ii)
.
Note that checking condition
(Nb)
could be completely independent from checking
(Na)
, as
{\mathcal{F}}(\Gamma)^{\kappa}
does not have to be equal to
\mathcal{Z}^{\lambda}
.
Therefore, we ask the following question:

Question 6.8
.

Are the necessary conditions
(Na)
and
(Nb)
sufficient to conclude that
\mathcal{Z}^{\lambda}
can be realized as a refined fundamental fan of a polytope?

The polytope

is
\Phi_{d-1}
-placed and
\Psi_{d-1}
-appropriate.

Proof.

The vertices of
P
are
\{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}+{\boldsymbol{a}}_{i}\ :\ 1\leq i\leq d-1\}
. It is easy to verify that all vertices have strictly increasing entries, and hence are in
\Psi_{d-1}^{\circ}
. Therefore,
P\subseteq\Psi_{d-1}^{\circ}
, and thus is
\Psi_{d-1}
-appropriate. Let
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=(1,2,\dots,d)
.
As the functional
\langle{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},\cdot\rangle
is constant on all the vertices of
P
, we conclude it is constant on
P.
Since
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\Phi_{d-1}^{\circ}
, we have that
P
is
\Phi_{d-1}
-placed.
∎

Remark 6.18
.

The particular choice of
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{0}
is not too important but notice that taking
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{0}={\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
does not work since the resulting vertices are not strictly increasing.

We can describe the normal cones of
T_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\Delta_{d-2})
(for any
{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
) via the following basic lemma.
Recall again from Example
2.6
that
W_{d-1}
is the dual space of
V_{d-1}
and has a corresponding dual basis
\left\{{\boldsymbol{f}}_{1}^{(d)},\dots,{\boldsymbol{f}}_{d-1}^{(d)}\right\}
defined as in (
2.4
).
Also, for convenience, for each
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in W_{d-1}
, we define its
first difference vector
of
{\boldsymbol{w}}
to be

(6.5)

{\boldsymbol{\delta}}({\boldsymbol{w}})=(w_{2}-w_{1},w_{3}-w_{2},\dots,w_{d}-w_{d-1})\in\mathbb{R}^{d-1},

and for each
1\leq i\leq d-1,
we denote its
i
th entry by
\delta_{i}({\boldsymbol{w}}):=w_{i+1}-w_{i}.

Lemma 6.19
.

Let
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
be a polytope and its normal fan is defined in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}
, which is identified with its own dual via the inner product
\left[\cdot,\cdot\right]
.
Then for any face
F\subseteq Q
we have that

(6.6)

\displaystyle\operatorname{ncone}(T_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(F),T_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(Q))=

\displaystyle\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}y_{i}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}^{(d)}\in W_{d-1}~:~{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\operatorname{ncone}(F,Q)\right\}

(6.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{{\boldsymbol{w}}\in W_{d-1}~:~{\boldsymbol{\delta}}({\boldsymbol{w}})\in\operatorname{ncone}(F,Q)\right\}.

Proof.

For any
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1},
one checks that
[{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{y}}]=\left\langle T_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{x}})-T_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}),\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}y_{i}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}^{(d)}\right\rangle.

Hence, we have
[{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}]\leq[{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime},{\boldsymbol{y}}]
if and only if
\left\langle T_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{x}}),\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}y_{i}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}^{(d)}\right\rangle\leq\left\langle T_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}),\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}y_{i}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}^{(d)}\right\rangle.

This, together with the definition of normal cones, implies Equality (
6.6
).

Letting
\sum_{i=1}^{d-1}y_{i}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}^{(d)}={\boldsymbol{w}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}w_{i}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i},
we obtain the relation that

y_{i}=w_{i+1}-w_{i}=\delta_{i}({\boldsymbol{w}}),
for each
1\leq i\leq d-1
. Hence, Equality (
6.7
) follows.
∎

We now describe the normal fan of
T_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\Delta_{d-2})
.

Lemma 6.20
.

For each
B\in\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{d-1}
, the normal cone
\operatorname{ncone}(T_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\Delta_{B}),T_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\Delta_{d-2}))
of
T_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\Delta_{d-2})
at the face
T_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\Delta_{B})
is given by

(6.8)

\nu_{B}\mathrel{\vbox{\hbox{\scriptsize.}\hbox{\scriptsize.}}}=\left\{{\boldsymbol{w}}\in W_{d-1}\ :\ \begin{array}[]{c}\delta_{i}({\boldsymbol{w}})=\delta_{j}({\boldsymbol{w}})\text{ if $i,j\in B$}\\
\delta_{i}({\boldsymbol{w}})\leq\delta_{j}({\boldsymbol{w}})\text{ if $i\notin B$ and $j\in B$}\end{array}\right\}.

Moreover, the map
B\mapsto\nu_{B}
is a poset isomorphism from
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{d-1}
to the dual of the face poset of
\Sigma(T_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}(\Delta_{d-2}))
.

Proof.

Applying Lemma
6.19
to Equation (
6.3
), we immediately obtain a description for the normal cones of
T_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}}(\Delta_{d-2})
as shown in (
6.8
).

The second conclusion the follows Lemma
2.2
, Example
2.4
, and the fact that
T_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
is an invertible affine transformation.
∎

Step
(b’)

Recall that
\Omega(P)
is the poset on
\{(\sigma,\phi)\in\Sigma(P)\times\Sigma(\Phi)\ :\ \sigma^{\circ}\cap\phi^{\circ}\neq\emptyset\}
with ordering relation

(6.9)

(\sigma,\phi)\leq(\sigma^{\prime},\phi^{\prime})\text{ in $\Omega(P)$ \quad if and only if \quad}\sigma\subseteq\sigma^{\prime}\text{ and }\phi\subseteq\phi^{\prime}.

We first figure out the set
\Omega(P)
when
P=T_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{0}}(\Delta_{d-2})
by giving a characterization for when
\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{\circ}\cap\nu_{B}^{\circ}\neq\emptyset
.

Proposition 6.21
.

For
\mathcal{S}\in\mathcal{O}_{d}^{\mathrm{std}}
and
B\in\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{d-1},
we have
\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{\circ}\cap\nu_{B}^{\circ}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
(\mathcal{S},B)\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}
, that is,
(\mathcal{S},B)
satisfies one of the followings conditions:

(1)

\mathcal{S}=([d])
and
B=[d-1].

(2)

\mathcal{S}\neq([d])
and
B
is a (nonempty) subset of
{\operatorname{Type}}(\mathcal{S})
.

As a consequence, for
P=T_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{0}}(\Delta_{d-2})
, we have

\Omega(P)=\{(\nu_{B},\sigma_{\mathcal{S}})\ :\ (\mathcal{S},B)\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}\}.

Proof of Proposition
6.21
.

The cones
\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}
and
\nu_{B}
are described in and Equations (
2.6
) and (
6.8
), respectively.
It can be verified that the interiors of these cones are obtained by replacing
\leq
with
<
in these descriptions. One sees that
\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{\circ}
and
\nu_{B}^{\circ}
can both be described using first difference vectors:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{\circ}=

\displaystyle\left\{{\boldsymbol{w}}\in W_{d-1}\ :\begin{array}[]{c}\delta_{i}({\boldsymbol{w}})=0\text{ if $i\notin{\operatorname{Type}}(\mathcal{S})$}\\
\delta_{i}({\boldsymbol{w}})>0\text{ if $i\in{\operatorname{Type}}(\mathcal{S})$}\end{array}\right\},

\displaystyle\nu_{B}^{\circ}=

\displaystyle\left\{{\boldsymbol{w}}\in W_{d-1}\ :\ \begin{array}[]{c}\delta_{i}({\boldsymbol{w}})=\delta_{j}({\boldsymbol{w}})\text{ if $i,j\in B$}\\
\delta_{i}({\boldsymbol{w}})<\delta_{j}({\boldsymbol{w}})\text{ if $i\notin B$ and $j\in B$}\end{array}\right\}.

If
\mathcal{S}=([d])
, then
\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{\circ}={\boldsymbol{0}}
, and thus
\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{\circ}\cap\nu_{B}^{\circ}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
0\in\nu_{B}^{\circ}
, which only happens when
B=[d-1].

Suppose
\mathcal{S}\neq([d]).
Then
{\operatorname{Type}}(\mathcal{S})
is nonempty. If
B
is a nonempty subset of
{\operatorname{Type}}(\mathcal{S})
, one checks
\displaystyle\sum_{i\in B}2{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}^{(d)}+\sum_{i\in{\operatorname{Type}}(\mathcal{S})\setminus B}{\boldsymbol{f}}_{i}
is in
\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{\circ}\cap\nu_{B}^{\circ},
and hence
\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{\circ}\cap\nu_{B}^{\circ}\neq\emptyset
. Conversely, assume
\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{\circ}\cap\nu_{B}^{\circ}\neq\emptyset
and let
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{\circ}\cap\nu_{B}^{\circ},
we need to show that
B
is a (nonempty) subset of
{\operatorname{Type}}(\mathcal{S})
. By the description (
6
) for
\sigma_{\mathcal{S}}^{\circ},
we have that

(6.14)

\delta_{i}({\boldsymbol{w}})<\delta_{j}({\boldsymbol{w}})\text{ for all $i\not\in{\operatorname{Type}}(\mathcal{S})$ and $j\in{\operatorname{Type}}(\mathcal{S})$}.

However, by the first condition in the description (
6
) and the fact that
B\in\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{d-1}
is not empty, we must have that
B
is either a subset of
{\operatorname{Type}}(\mathcal{S})
or has no intersection with
{\operatorname{Type}}(\mathcal{S}).
Suppose
B\cap{\operatorname{Type}}(\mathcal{S})=\emptyset.
We pick
a\in{\operatorname{Type}}(\mathcal{S})
and
b\in B
(this can be done because both sets are nonempty). Then
a\not\in B
and
b\not\in{\operatorname{Type}}(\mathcal{S})
. Thus, using the second condition in the description (
6
), we conclude
\delta_{a}({\boldsymbol{w}})<\delta_{b}({\boldsymbol{w}}).
However, this contradicts (
6.14
). Therefore, we conclude that
B
is a subset of
{\operatorname{Type}}(\mathcal{S})
, completing the proof.
∎

Corollary 6.22
.

Let
P=T_{{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{0}}(\Delta_{d-2})
.
The map
(\mathcal{S},B)\mapsto(\nu_{B},\sigma_{\mathcal{S}})
gives a poset isomorphism from
\mathcal{Z}_{d}
to
\Omega(P)
.
Hence, the map
((\mathcal{S},B),\sigma_{\mathcal{S}})\mapsto(\nu_{B},\sigma_{\mathcal{S}})
gives a
\Sigma(\Phi_{d-1})
-poset isomorphism from
\mathcal{Z}_{d}^{\lambda}
to
\Omega(P)
.

Proof.

It follows from Proposition
6.21
that the map
(\mathcal{S},B)\mapsto(\nu_{B},\sigma_{\mathcal{S}})
is a bijection from
\mathcal{Z}_{d}
to
\Omega(P)
.
Next, note that
\mathcal{Z}_{d}
is a subposet of
\mathcal{T}_{d}
which is dual to
{\mathcal{F}}_{d}.
Therefore, for any
(\mathcal{S},B),(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},B^{\prime})\in\mathcal{Z}_{d}
, we have

(\mathcal{S},B)\preceq(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},B^{\prime})\text{ in $\mathcal{Z}_{d}$}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\text{$\mathcal{S}^{\prime}\preceq\mathcal{S}$ in $\mathcal{O}_{d}$ and $B^{\prime}\preceq B$ in $\widehat{\mathcal{B}}_{d-1}$ }.

It then follows from Lemma
2.10
, Lemma
6.20
, and (
6.9
) that

(\mathcal{S},B)\preceq(\mathcal{S}^{\prime},B^{\prime})\text{ in $\mathcal{Z}_{d}$}\quad\text{if and only if}\quad(\nu_{B},\sigma_{\mathcal{S}})\leq(\nu_{B^{\prime}},\sigma_{\mathcal{S}^{\prime}})\text{ in $\Omega(P)$}.

Therefore, the first conclusion follows.

The second conclusion follows from the first conclusion and Lemma
6.7
.
∎
