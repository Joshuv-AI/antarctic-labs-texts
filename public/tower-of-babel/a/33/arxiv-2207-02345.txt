Random Variables with Measurability Constraints with Application to Opportunistic Scheduling
Michael J. Neely

Abstract
This paper proves a representation theorem regarding sequences of random elements that take values in a Borel space and are measurable with respect to the sigma algebra generated by an arbitrary union of sigma algebras. This, together with a related representation theorem of Kallenberg, is used to characterize the set of multidimensional decision vectors in a discrete time stochastic control problem with measurability and causality constraints, including opportunistic scheduling problems for time-varying communication networks. A network capacity theorem for these systems is refined, without requiring an implicit and arbitrarily complex extension of the state space, by introducing two measurability assumptions and using a theory of constructible sets. An example that makes use of well known pathologies in descriptive set theory is given to show a nonmeasurable scheduling scheme can outperform all measurable scheduling schemes.

X_{k}=h_{k}((Y_{j})_{j\in\tilde{J}_{k}})\quad\forall k\in K

I-A

Applications to stochastic control

The measurability constraints (
1
) have applications to stochastic control.
For example, consider a discrete time system that operates over
time slots
k\in\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
according to some probability triplet
(\Omega,{{\cal{F}}},P)
. Let
S_{k}:\Omega\rightarrow\Omega_{S}
be the
system state
that can be observed at time
k
, which is a random element associated with some measurable space
(\Omega_{S},{{\cal{F}}}_{S})
with arbitrary
structure. Every step
k
the system controller observes
S_{k}

and chooses a
decision
that affects a vector of attributes
X_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
where
m
is some fixed positive integer. The vector
X_{k}
is required to satisfy the following
system constraints

X_{k}\in C(S_{k})\quad\forall k\in\{1,2,3,\ldots\}

(3)

where
C:\Gamma\rightarrow Pow(\mathbb{R}^{m})
is a given set-valued function that maps the observed state
S_{k}
to a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
that consists of all decision options for
X_{k}
(where
Pow(\mathbb{R}^{m})
denotes the power set of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
). The next state
S_{k+1}
can be influenced by the prior states and decisions according to some model supported by the probability space, such as a Markov chain model. The
m
components of
X_{k}
can represent rewards, prices, power expenditures, and so on, associated with time slot
k
, and can also include values that affect the next state. This work is motivated by the application of
opportunistic scheduling
,
where
(S_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random channel states that are sequentially observed in a wireless communication system at the start of each slot
k
,
X_{k}
is a vector of transmission rates over
m
different channels, and
C(S_{k})
is the set of all possible transmission rate vectors that can be supported on slot
k
when the observed channel state is
S_{k}
.

Consider
causal and measurable
decision policies that are constrained to make decisions that yield valid random variables and are based only on observations of the past. Assume the decisions can be
stochastic
, so they can be informed by an external source of randomness that is represented by some
sigma algebra
{{\cal{G}}}\subseteq{{\cal{F}}}
on
\Omega
. For example,
{{\cal{G}}}
might be the sigma algebra generated by an infinite sequence of i.i.d. random elements in some arbitrary measurable space and whose values are selected by an independent computing device at time
0
(before any control decisions are made).
Then we require:

X_{k}\mbox{ is $\sigma(\sigma(S_{1})\cup\cdots\cup\sigma(S_{k})\cup{{\cal{G}}})$-measurable}\quad\forall k\in\{1,2,3,\ldots\}

(4)

where
\sigma(S_{i})
is the sigma algebra generated by the random element
S_{i}
. Under any such causal and measurable decision policy the result (
2
) implies

X_{k}=h_{k}(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{k},R)\quad\forall k\in\{1,2,3,\ldots\}

(5)

for some Borel measurable functions
h_{k}
, some
{{\cal{G}}}
-measurable random variable
R
that takes values in
[0,1]
, and some
\sigma(S_{i})
-measurable random variables

that take values in
[0,1]
. It is interesting that the
same
random variables
R,\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
can be used to construct
X_{k}
for all time steps
k
. In particular:

•

While the observed random elements
S_{i}
are associated with an arbitrarily complex measurable space
(\Omega_{S},{{\cal{F}}}_{S})
where
\Omega_{S}
has arbitrary cardinality, it suffices to boil these random elements down to real-valued random variables
Y_{i}:\Omega\rightarrow[0,1]
where each
Y_{i}
is a measurable function of
S_{i}
.

•

While the external source of randomness is from an arbitrarily complex sigma algebra
{{\cal{G}}}
on
\Omega
, it suffices to boil it down to a single draw of a random variable
R:\Omega\rightarrow[0,1]
that is
{{\cal{G}}}
-measurable.

The constraint
X_{k}\in C(S_{k})
seems to require knowledge of the

S_{k}
, while the form (
5

In particular, all policies that satisfy (
3
)-(
4

1.

Choose a single

-measurable random variable

.

2.

Choose Borel measurable functions

from which

k\in\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
.

3.

For each
k\in\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
, define a Borel measurable function

such that

(6)

If the constraint (
6
) is impossible to meet, then no causal decision policy that meets the required measurability constraints exists.

. In particular, the works

II-A

Terminology

Let
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
denote the natural numbers,
\mathbb{R}
the real numbers, and
{{\cal{B}}}(\mathbb{R})
the standard Borel sigma algebra on
\mathbb{R}
. For
A\in{{\cal{B}}}(\mathbb{R})
define
{{\cal{B}}}(A)=\{B\in{{\cal{B}}}(\mathbb{R}):B\subseteq A\}
.
A
measurable space
is a pair
(\Omega,{{\cal{F}}})
where
\Omega
is a nonempty set and
{{\cal{F}}}
is a sigma algebra on
\Omega
. Suppose
(\Omega_{1},{{\cal{F}}}_{1})
and
(\Omega_{2},{{\cal{F}}}_{2})
are two measurable spaces. Let
{{\cal{H}}}\subseteq\Omega_{1}
be another sigma algebra on
\Omega_{1}
. With respect to the measurable space

(\Omega_{2},{{\cal{F}}}_{2})
, a function
g:\Omega_{1}\rightarrow\Omega_{2}
is said to be
{{\cal{H}}}
-measurable

if

g^{-1}(A)\in{{\cal{H}}}\quad\forall A\in{{\cal{F}}}_{2}

where
g^{-1}(A)=\{\omega\in\Omega_{1}:g(\omega)\in A\}
.
With respect to the two measurable
spaces
(\Omega_{1},{{\cal{F}}}_{1})
and
(\Omega_{2},{{\cal{F}}}_{2})
, a
function
g:\Omega_{1}\rightarrow\Omega_{2}
is said to be
measurable
if it is
{{\cal{F}}}_{1}
-measurable.
Two measurable spaces
(\Omega_{1},{{\cal{F}}}_{1})
and
(\Omega_{2},{{\cal{F}}}_{2})
are
isomorphic
if there is a bijective function
b:\Omega_{1}\rightarrow\Omega_{2}
that is measurable and has a measurable inverse; such a function is called an
isomorphism
. A measurable space
(\Omega,{{\cal{F}}})
is called a
Borel space
if it is isomorphic to
(A,{{\cal{B}}}(A))
for some
A\in{{\cal{B}}}([0,1])
.
If
(\Omega_{2},{{\cal{F}}}_{2})
is a Borel space then a measurable function
g:\Omega_{1}\rightarrow\Omega_{2}
is sometimes referred to as a
Borel measurable function
as a reminder that the target space is a Borel space.

Fix
J
as a nonempty set (possibly uncountably infinite). Let
(\Omega_{j},{{\cal{F}}}_{j})
be measurable spaces for each
j\in J
. Define

\times_{j\in J}\Omega_{j}=\{(x_{j})_{j\in J}:x_{j}\in\Omega_{j}\quad\forall j\in J\}

Define
{{\cal{C}}}
as the collection of subsets of
\times_{j\in J}\Omega_{j}
of the form
\times_{j\in J}A_{j}
for some sets
A_{j}

that satisfy: (i)
A_{j}\in{{\cal{F}}}_{j}
for all
j\in J
; (ii)
A_{j}=\Omega_{j}
for all but at most one index
j\in J
. Define the product sigma algebra on
\times_{j\in J}\Omega_{j}
, also called the cylindrical sigma algebra, as

\otimes_{j\in J}{{\cal{F}}}_{j}=\sigma({{\cal{C}}})

where
\sigma({{\cal{C}}})
denotes the sigma algebra
generated by the collection of sets
{{\cal{C}}}
.
For a given measurable space
(\Omega,{{\cal{F}}})
define
\Omega^{J}=\times_{j\in J}\Omega
and define its product sigma algebra as
\otimes_{j\in J}{{\cal{F}}}
.
A special case of interest is
[0,1]^{J}
with product sigma algebra
\otimes_{j\in J}{{\cal{B}}}([0,1])

(this measurable space is a Borel space whenever
J
is a finite or countably infinite set).

A
probability space
is a triplet
(\Omega,{{\cal{F}}},P)
where
(\Omega,{{\cal{F}}})
is a measurable space and
P:{{\cal{F}}}\rightarrow[0,1]
is a probability measure. A

is a measurable function
X:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. A

is a measurable function
S:\Omega\rightarrow\Omega_{S}
where
(\Omega_{S},{{\cal{F}}}_{S})
is some given measurable space. By
U\sim{{\cal{U}}}[0,1]
we mean that
U:\Omega\rightarrow[0,1]
is a random variable that is uniformly distributed over
[0,1]
.
