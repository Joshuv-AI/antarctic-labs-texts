𝖯𝗈𝗅𝗒 \mathsf{Poly} : An abundant categorical setting for mode-dependent dynamics
David I. Spivak

Abstract
Dynamical systems—by which we mean machines that take time-varying input, change their state, and produce output—can be wired together to form more complex systems. Previous work has shown how to allow collections of machines to reconfigure their wiring diagram dynamically, based on their collective state. This notion was called “mode dependence”, and while the framework was compositional (forming an operad of re-wiring diagrams and algebra of mode-dependent dynamical systems on it), the formulation itself was more “creative” than it was natural. In this paper we show that the theory of mode-dependent dynamical systems can be more naturally recast within the category 𝖯𝗈𝗅𝗒 \mathsf{Poly} of polynomial functors. This category is almost superlatively abundant in its structure: for example, it has four interacting monoidal structures ( + , × , ⊗ , ∘ ) (+,\times,\otimes,\circ) , two of which ( × , ⊗ \times,\otimes ) are monoidal closed, and the comonoids for ∘ \circ are precisely categories in the usual sense. We discuss how the various structures in 𝖯𝗈𝗅𝗒 \mathsf{Poly} show up in the theory of dynamical systems. We also show that the usual coalgebraic formalism for dynamical systems takes place within 𝖯𝗈𝗅𝗒 \mathsf{Poly} . Indeed one can see coalgebras as special dynamical systems—ones that do not record their history—formally analogous to contractible groupoids as special categories.

A wiring diagram can be used to specify a fixed communication pattern between systems:

The real world is filled with instances of systems with time-varying input-output patterns.

When we say that
\mathsf{Poly}
is abundant, we mean that it is exceptionally rich in structure, and that structure is highly relevant to dynamical systems. Here are some of the features of this category:

1.

\mathsf{Poly}
has coproducts and products,

2.

\mathsf{Poly}

3.

\mathsf{Poly}

\mathsf{Poly}

.

5.

\mathsf{Poly}

6.

\mathsf{Poly}

7.

\mathsf{Poly}
admits a monoidal bifibration
\mathsf{Poly}\to\mathsf{Set}

\otimes\mapsto\times

8.

\mathsf{Poly}
admits an adjoint quadruple with
\mathsf{Set}

\mathsf{Set}^{\textnormal{op}}

9.

Comonoids in

Chapter 2
Introduction to
\mathsf{Poly}

3
Polynomial functors

Notation 2.1
.

We usually denote sets with upper-case letters
A,B
, etc.; the exception is ordinals: we denote the
n
th ordinal by
n=\{\mathrm{1},\ldots,\mathrm{n}\}
. We denote functions between sets—including elements of sets—using upright letters
\mathrm{f}\colon A\to B
and
\mathrm{a}\in A
.

All polynomials discussed here have a single variable, always
\mathcal{y}
; in particular
\mathcal{y}
itself is a polynomial. Coefficients and exponents of polynomials are arbitrary sets, e.g.
\mathbb{N}\mathcal{y}^{\mathbb{R}}+3
is a polynomial. Every set
A
will also be a polynomial, namely a constant. We denote generic polynomials with lower-case letters
p,q
, etc.

Recall that a
representable functor

\mathsf{Set}\to\mathsf{Set}
is one of the form
\mathsf{Set}(A,-)
for a set
A
.
We denote this functor by
\mathcal{y}^{A}\colon\mathsf{Set}\to\mathsf{Set}
and say it is
represented by

A\in\mathsf{Set}
. For example
\mathcal{y}^{3}
is represented by
3
and
\mathcal{y}^{3}(2)\cong 8
. As
A
varies we obtain the contravariant Yoneda embedding.

Classically, a polynomial
p
in one variable with set coefficients is a function
p(\mathcal{y})=A_{n}\mathcal{y}^{n}+\cdots+A_{1}\mathcal{y}^{1}+A_{0}\mathcal{y}^{0}
with each
A_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
. In category theory this is often generalized to allow for infinitely many terms and infinite exponents; e.g. we consider the following to be a polynomial

p(\mathcal{y})=\sum_{\mathrm{i}\in I}\mathcal{y}^{A_{\mathrm{i}}}

(5)

for arbitrary small sets
I
and
A
. We can think of such a
p
as a functor
\mathsf{Set}\to\mathsf{Set}
; it sends a set
X\in\ob(\mathsf{Set})
to the coproduct, over
\mathrm{i}\in I
, of the set
X^{A_{\mathrm{i}}}
of functions
A_{\mathrm{i}}\to X
, or equivalently the
A_{\mathrm{i}}
-fold product of
X
with itself. The result is covariantly functorial in
X
.

Considered this way,
p
is called a
polynomial functor
; polynomial functors sit inside of the category of all functors
\mathsf{Set}\to\mathsf{Set}
as a full subcategory, namely the one spanned by coproducts of representables.

Definition 2.2
.

The category
\mathsf{Poly}
has polynomial functors
p(\mathcal{y})
as in (
5
) as objects and natural transformations between them as morphisms.

In
\mathsf{Poly}
, products distribute over coproducts,
\Big(\sum_{i}p_{i}\Big)\times q\cong\sum_{i}\left(p_{i}\times q\right)
. More generally, for any discrete category
A
, functor
I\colon A\to\mathsf{Set}
, and functor
p\colon\sum_{a\in A}I(a)\to\mathsf{Poly}
(the above specific case being
A\coloneqq 2
,
I(2)\coloneqq 1
,
p_{1,i}\coloneqq p_{i}
, and
p_{2,1}\coloneqq q
) there is an isomorphism

\prod_{a\in A}\;\;\sum_{i\in I(a)}\;p_{(a,i)}\quad\cong\quad\sum_{i\in\prod_{a\in A}I(a)}\;\;\prod_{a\in A}\;p_{(a,i(a))}.

(6)

In fact,
\mathsf{Poly}
can be characterized as the free category that has coproducts, products, and satisfies Eq.
6
, (called
complete distributivity
or the Axiom of Choice; see e.g.
[
jacobs1999categorical
]
).
\mathsf{Poly}
is also equivalent to the Grothendieck construction of the canonical functor
\mathsf{Set}^{\textnormal{op}}\to\mathsf{Cat}
sending each object to the corresponding slice category (opposite)
A\mapsto(\mathsf{Set}/A)^{\textnormal{op}}
and sending
\mathrm{f}\colon B\to A
to pullback along
\mathrm{f}
.

Notation 2.3
.

We denote the product of polynomials by juxtaposition or sometimes
{\cdot}
, i.e.

pq\coloneqq p\times q=p{\cdot}q.

For any set
A
we denote the
A
-fold repeated product of
p
by
p^{A}\coloneqq\prod_{\mathrm{a}\in A}p
; in particular
p^{1}\cong p
and
p^{0}\cong 1
. The representable
\mathcal{y}^{n}
is indeed the
n
-fold repeated product of
\mathcal{y}
.

For any polynomial
p
, the set
p(1)
has particular importance; it can be identified with the set of representable summands (pure-power terms)
\mathcal{y}^{k}
in
p
. For example if
p=\mathcal{y}^{2}+3\mathcal{y}+2
then
p(1)=6
corresponding to the six representable summands in
p=\mathcal{y}^{2}+\mathcal{y}^{1}+\mathcal{y}^{1}+\mathcal{y}^{1}+\mathcal{y}^{0}+\mathcal{y}^{0}
. We will denote the representing object for the
\mathrm{i}
th representable summand of
p
by
\mathrm{p_{i}}
, i.e.

p=\sum_{\mathrm{i}\in p(1)}\mathcal{y}^{\mathrm{p_{i}}}.

Given a bundle
\pi\colon E\to B
, and element
\mathrm{b}\in B
, we denote the fiber
\pi^{-1}(\mathrm{b})
by
E_{\mathrm{b}}
. We will refer to elements of

and elements of
E_{\mathrm{b}}
as the
directions
in position
\mathrm{b}
. From the algebraic viewpoint, a position is a ‘pure-power’, or representable summand, and the associated direction-type is its ‘exponent’ or representing object; from the tree viewpoint, a position is a
root
and the associated directions are its
leaves
.
