Relaxation approach for learning neural network regularizers for a class of identification problems
Sébastien Court

Abstract
The present paper deals with the data-driven design of regularizers in the form of artificial neural networks, for solving certain inverse problems formulated as optimal control problems. These regularizers aim at improving accuracy, wellposedness or compensating uncertainties for a given class of optimal control problems (inner-problems). Parameterized as neural networks, their weights are chosen in order to reduce a misfit between data and observations of the state solution of the inner- optimal control problems. Learning these weights constitutes the outer-problem. Based on necessary first-order optimality conditions for the inner-problems, a relaxation approach is proposed in order to implement efficient solving of these inner-problems, namely the forward operator of the outer-problem. Optimality conditions are derived for the latter, and are implemented in numerical illustrations dealing with the inverse conductivity problem. The numerical tests show the feasibility of the relaxation approach, first for rediscovering standard L 2 L^{2} -regularizers, and next for designing regularizers that compensate unknown noise on the observed state of the inner-problem.



Keywords:
Optimal control theory, Regularization, Neural networks, Identification problem, Data-driven optimization, Learning problems.

Introduction

Designing regularizers for solving inverse problems is an important data-science topic for which data-driven approaches have been recently developed. In particular, the design of regularizers as artificial neural networks demonstrate interesting capacities for improving characteristics of inverse problems. Among these characteristics, one can aim at recovering uniqueness of solutions for initially ill-posed inverse problems, facilitating their solving, obtaining specific qualitative properties like sparsity, or even compensating unknown real-world noise that leads to uncertainties on the measured data. The design of such regularizers can be achieved towards an optimal control approach, consisting in optimizing parameters of a
machine
, namely an input-output map, in order to minimize a distance between real-world data and outputs of this machine.

1.1
General learning problem

Consider a series of
K
problems that belong to a given class of optimal control problems indexed by
1\leq k\leq K
, and assume that for each of them we know a solution. More precisely, we consider a set of tasks
\hat{y}_{k}
that we know to be achieved by the means of parameters
\hat{u}_{k}
(the
controls

K

\left\{(\hat{y}_{k},\hat{u}_{k}),1\leq k\leq K\right\}

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\min_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\quad\frac{1}{2K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\|u_{k}-\hat{u}_{k}\|_{\mathcal{U}}^{2},\\[15.0pt]
\text{subject to: $u_{k}$ is solution of Problem~\eqref{pbstandard} with $(\hat{y}_{k},r)$, for all $1\leq k\leq K$,}\end{array}\right.

(
\ast\ast
)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\min_{(y,u)\in\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{U}}\big(c(y,\hat{y})+\gamma\circ r(u)\big),\\[10.0pt]
\text{subject to: }\varphi(y,u)=0.\end{array}\right.

1.2
Motivation of data-driven regularization and state-of-the-art

In the formulation of the inner-problem (

∗

‣
1.1
), the additional term
r(u)
corresponds to a so-called
regularizer
. Its role consists in forcing the parameter/control function
u
to present certain properties, like regularity, boundedness, or sparsity for instance. Besides the qualitative properties aforementioned, designing a regularizer for a class of identification problems like (

∗

‣
1.1
) can address several issues. For example, inverse problems are well-known to present non-unique solutions, leading to describe them as being
ill-posed
. A regularizer designed with a data-driven approach could help to select a relevant solution. Another issue could be due to uncertainty on measurements: Data can be subject to a noise phenomenon whose nature is unknown, and that would lead to inaccuracy on the measured solution. An appropriate regularizer, implementing implicitly the effects of this noise, could help to compensate this lack of accuracy. We illustrate this specific application in our numerical realizations (see section
6
).

In order to design such a regularizer, one need to parameterize the set
\mathcal{R}
, that we choose as a set of artificial neural networks (ANNs) with a prescribed number of layers. A regularizer
(u\mapsto r(w,u))\in\mathcal{R}
is then a mapping parameterized by a set of weights

. We refer to section
3.1
for further details on the definitions and notation related to the ANNs we will consider. Beside the theoretical approximation capacities of ANNs (see
[
Elb+21
]
for example), data-driven methods training ANNs have lead to the design of regularizers that outperform classical solving methods for inverse problems.

For example, the data-driven modeling of regularizers with NETT (Network Tiknonov) was investigated in
[
Li+20
]
, in order to solve inverse problems subject to unknown noise, with applications to Computer Tomography. Let us also cite
[
OSH19
]
in the context of trained decoder networks for sparse reconstruction, and refer to
[
Li+20a
]
for a theoretical analysis of such an approach. Further developments were studied in
[
Obm+19
,
Obm+20
]
, considering aNETT (augmented Network Thikonov), designing a regularizer and also a penalty (misfit) function in the form of a neural network. Still on applications in medical imaging, the use of adversarial neural networks as regularizers was studied in
[
LOS18
]
. The use of deep neural networks for solving ill-posed inverse problems related to medical imaging was also studied in
[
AO17
]
. The special case of input-convex neural networks (ICNNs) for modeling regularizers with a data-driven approach was considered in
[

1.3
Approach and strategy

In
[
HKB18
]
the authors investigated the problem of learning the Thikhonov regularization parameter for a class of inverse problems, with a
bi-level
approach. This approach consists in adopting a hierarchy in the formulation of the regularizer learning problem: The identification problems (

∗

‣
1.1
) for which we want to design a regularizer are called
inner-problems
, and the main problem (

∗
⁣
∗

‣
1.1
) consists in optimizing the parameters of the regularizer for a given set of identification problems for which we know the ground-truth (the
data set
). The term
optimal control problem
will be employed for both inner and outer problems. At a meta-level, the outer-problem consists in optimizing parameters (here the parameters
w
of the regularizer) of the family of the inner- control problems, for which the parameters
u
to identify are also described as
controls
. Let us mention that the bi-level approach was initially developed for image denoising in
[
KP13
]
.

In the present paper, we adopt this bi-level approach, where the inner-problem (

∗

‣
1.1
) is considered as a constraint in the outer-problem. Compared with
[
HKB18
]
, in our case the range of possible regularizers is
a priori
wider than a Thikonov regularizer, as the regularizers are chosen in the form of artificial neural networks parameterized with weights. The price is to pay is a theoretical analysis (existence and uniqueness of minimizers) that becomes out-of-reach in the general case, due in particular to the purely nonlinear aspects of neural networks. Therefore, our main contribution is the development of a relaxation approach, consisting in replacing the optimality constraint of the inner-problems by the corresponding necessary Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions. In our case these conditions are equality constraints (vanishing gradient equalities) that transform the outer-problem into a standard optimal control problem of state constraint type.

This relaxation approach is inspired by optimal control problems with partial differential equations constraints. Indeed, partial differential equations modeling physical dynamical systems can often be derived from the least action principle. They are merely first-order optimality conditions of a minimization problem. Wellposedness questions for these partial differential equations are then related to existence and uniqueness of minimizers for functionals called
actions
.

The main theoretical result is given in Theorem
4.4
, where we provide the sensitivity of the objective function for the relaxed version of Problem (

∗
⁣
∗

‣
1.1
) with respect to the weights of the neural network. This allows us to implement the determination of these weights in Algorithm
1
, with gradient rules. Illustrations are proposed by considering as inner-problems the classical identification of coefficients in elliptic partial differential equations. Regularizers are designed, in particular for compensating the effects of a Gaussian noise introduced on the state variable.

2.1
Functional setting

We denote by
\mathcal{Y}
,
\mathcal{U}
,
\mathcal{Q}
real Banach spaces, and by
\varphi:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
the state mapping. We assume that
\mathcal{Q}
is reflexive.
The control space
\mathcal{U}
is assumed to be finite-dimensional, of dimension

, so that
\mathcal{U}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{in}}}
. Therefore in particular
\mathcal{U}
is a Hilbert space, and reflexive. The range space
\mathcal{V}
of the neural networks is finite-dimensional, of dimension
n_{\mathrm{out}}
, and
\mathcal{V}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}
. The space of weights is denoted by
\mathcal{W}
, and described in section
3.1
. Given weights
w\in\mathcal{W}
, we denote by
r(w,\cdot):\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathcal{V}
the associated neural network of
\mathcal{R}
, and by
\gamma:\mathcal{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
a smooth given mapping, possibly chosen as a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{out}}}
.

The data
\left\{(\hat{y}_{k},\hat{u}_{k}),1\leq k\leq K\right\}
are of finite number. The controls
(\hat{u}_{k})_{1\leq k\leq K}
are assumed to lie in
\mathcal{U}
, while the tasks
(\hat{y}_{k})_{1\leq k\leq K}
lie in a finite-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{Y}
.

The regularizer as realization of a neural network

Given

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\mathcal{U}\ni u\mapsto w_{L}\rho\left(w_{L-1}\rho\left(\left(\dots w_{2}\rho(w_{1}u)\right)\right)\right)\in\mathcal{V}\mid\ w=(w_{1},\dots,w_{L})\in\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathrm{Aff}(n_{\ell},n_{\ell+1},\mathbb{R})\right\}

\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell+1}}

We introduce

. We endow the space

with the Euclidean norm given by

. Given a smooth activation function

\displaystyle r(w,u)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}w_{2}(\rho(w_{1}(u)))&\text{if }L=2,\\[5.0pt]

is parameterized by the weights lying in

General assumptions

(\mathbf{A4})

In certain problems, like shape optimization for example, evaluating efficiently values of the parameter-to-state mapping
\mathbb{S}(u)
, and in particular
\mathbb{S}^{\prime}(u)^{\ast}
, can be challenging numerically, or expensive computationally. Therefore we prefer sometimes to adopt an optimal control approach, also called the
adjoint approach
, that we develop in our context in section
3.3
below. See remarks in
[
Hin+09
, section 1.6.1, page 59]
for further explanations. Further comments on the possible advantages of the optimal control approach are given in Appendix
A.2
.

3.3
Optimal control formalism for the inner-problem (

∗

‣
1.1
)

Let us recall the initial description of the inner-problem, when we keep the original state constraint:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle\min_{(y,u)\in\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{U}}\Big(J(y,u,w):=c(y,\hat{y})+\gamma\circ r(w,u)\Big),\\[10.0pt]
\text{subject to: }\varphi(y,u)=0\text{ in }\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}.\end{array}\right.

(
\ast
)

Unlike the direct approach which utilizes
\varphi(y,u)=0\Leftrightarrow y=\mathbb{S}(u)
, the optimal control approach – also called the adjoint approach – consists in taking into account the state constraint by introducing a Lagrange multiplier. We define the Lagrangian of problem (

∗

‣
3.3
) as follows

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rrcl}\mathscr{L}:&(\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{U})\times\mathcal{Q}&\rightarrow&\mathbb{R}\\
&((y,u),p)&\mapsto&c(y,\hat{y})+\displaystyle\gamma\circ r(w,u)+\left\langle p\,,\varphi(y,u)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}.\end{array}

Problem (

∗

‣
3.3
) can be solved by finding a saddle-point to the Lagrangian
\mathscr{L}
, minimized with respect to variables
(y,u)
and maximizing with respect to
p
. The sensitivity of
\mathscr{L}
with respect to variable
y
yields the adjoint equation, whose unknown is the adjoint state
p\in\mathcal{Q}
. It is given in
\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}
by

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}_{y}(y,u)^{\ast}.p+c^{\prime}_{y}(y,\hat{y})=0.

(8)

We define a solution of system (
8
) by transposition.

Definition 3.3
.

We say that
p
is a solution of system (
8
) if for all
f\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
we have

\displaystyle\langle p\,,f\rangle_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}+c^{\prime}_{y}(y,\hat{y}).\tilde{y}=0,

(9)

where
\tilde{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
denotes the solution of the following linear system in
\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}_{y}(y,u).\tilde{y}=f.

(10)

From Assumption
(\mathbf{A2})
, system (
10
) is well-posed. The existence of solutions for adjoint system (
8
), in the sense of Definition
3.3
, is stated as follows:

Proposition 3.4
.

Let be
(y,u)\in\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{U}
. There exists a unique solution
p\in\mathcal{Q}
to system (
8
), in the sense of Definition
3.3
, and there exists a constant
C(y,u)>0
(depending only on
(y,u)
) such that

\displaystyle\|p\|_{\mathcal{Q}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C(y,u)\|c^{\prime}_{y}(y,\hat{y})\|_{\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}}.

Proof.

From Assumption
(\mathbf{A2})^{\prime}
, it is easy to verify that the mapping
\varphi_{y}(y,u)^{\ast}
is injective, and so uniqueness holds. Let us prove existence. Denote by
\Lambda(y,u):\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{Y}
the linear operator which maps
f
to
\tilde{y}
, where
\tilde{y}
is the solution of (
10
). From Assumption
(\mathbf{A2})
, the operator
\Lambda(y,u)
is well-defined and bounded, and consequently
\Lambda(y,u)^{\ast}:\mathcal{Y}^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}\simeq\mathcal{Q}
too. Now define
p=-\Lambda(y,u)^{\ast}c^{\prime}_{y}(y,\hat{y})
. Then, for all
f\in\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}
, we verify that

\displaystyle\langle p\,,f\rangle_{\mathcal{Q}^{\prime},\mathcal{Q}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\langle c^{\prime}_{y}(y,\hat{y})\,,\Lambda(y,u)f\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}^{\prime},\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\langle c^{\prime}_{y}(y,\hat{y})\,,\tilde{y}\rangle_{\mathcal{Y}^{\prime},\mathcal{Y}}=-c^{\prime}_{y}(y,\hat{y}).\tilde{y},

and the announced estimate follows from boundedness of
\Lambda(y,u)^{\ast}
, which completes the proof.∎

Consequently, the adjoint approach provides another expression for the gradient associated with the inner-problem, namely the quantity
G(u,w,\hat{y})
introduced in (
6
).

Proposition 3.5
.

Let be
w\in\mathcal{W}
. If
u
is solution to Problem (

∗

‣
1.1
), then the gradient

introduced in Proposition
3.2
writes

G(u,w,\hat{y})=r^{\prime}_{u}(w,u)^{\ast}\gamma^{\prime}(r(w,u))+\varphi^{\prime}_{u}(y,u)^{\ast}.p,

(11)

where
y=\mathbb{S}(u)
,
p
is the solution of (
8
) corresponding to
(y,u)
, and necessarily

Proof.

From Proposition
2.1
, Problem (

∗

‣
1.1
) reduces to
\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathcal{U}}J(\mathbb{S}(u),u,w)
. Following the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions, if

is optimal to Problem (

∗

‣
1.1

\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left(J(\mathbb{S}(u),u,w)\right)=0

with for all

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left(J(\mathbb{S}(u),u,w)\right).\tilde{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J^{\prime}_{y}(\mathbb{S}(u),u,w).(\mathbb{S}^{\prime}(u).\tilde{u})+J^{\prime}_{u}(\mathbb{S}(u),u,w).\tilde{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle c^{\prime}_{y}(y,\hat{y})\,,\mathbb{S}^{\prime}(u).\tilde{u}\rangle_{\mathcal{U}}+\langle\gamma^{\prime}(r(w,u))\,,r^{\prime}_{u}(w,u).\tilde{u}\rangle_{\mathcal{V}}

\displaystyle\langle-\mathbb{S}^{\prime}(u)^{\ast}\varphi^{\prime}_{y}(y,u)^{\ast}.p+r^{\prime}_{u}(w,u)^{\ast}\gamma^{\prime}(r(w,u))\,,\tilde{u}\rangle_{\mathcal{U}},

where

Remark 3.6

4.2
Existence of minimizers for the relaxed problem

Existence of solutions for Problem (

Theorem 4.1

Assume that
\nu>0

~

∗
⁣
∗

‣

Proof.

The existence of feasible solutions for Problem (

~

∗
⁣
∗

‣
4.1

, combined with Proposition
3.2
(or also Proposition

\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{W}\ni(u,w)\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\|u-\hat{u}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{U}}+\frac{\nu}{2}\|w\|_{\mathcal{W}}^{2}

is bounded from below for

~

∗
⁣
∗

‣

are bounded in
\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{W}
, respectively. Remind that the spaces
\mathcal{U}
and

(u,w)\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{W}
such that

⁣

Remark 4.2
.

If we consider the case
\nu=0
, the boundedness of the sequence
(w_{n})_{n}
in the proof above is a priori not guaranteed. For proceeding like we did, in such a case we may need to assume further hypotheses on the neural network. For example, we could exploit the equality

, that is

\mathbb{S}^{\prime}(u^{(n)})^{\ast}c_{y}(\mathbb{S}(u^{(n)}),\hat{y})+r^{\prime}_{u}(w^{(n)},u^{(n)})^{\ast}\gamma^{\prime}(r(w^{(n)},u^{(n)}))=0,

still converges, which implies that the quantity

is uniformly bounded with respect to

. In the simple one-dimensional case where

, and

, this would lead us to consider that the boundedness of the gradient of the neural network implies the boundedness of its weights, which is not true in general. Therefore considering

4.4
Properties of the adjoint operator

The adjoint equation for Problem (

~

∗
⁣
∗

‣
4.1
) is obtained by differentiating the Lagrangian
\mathbf{L}
with respect to the variable

, which gives

G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y})^{\ast}.\mu=-(u-\hat{u}).

(24)

Let us take a look at the operator
G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y})^{\ast}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}^{\prime})
which arises in this equation. Since
\mathcal{U}\simeq\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{in}}}
is assumed to be of finite dimension, for each
(u,w)\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{W}
the operator
G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y})
can be represented by a matrix of
\mathbb{R}^{n_{\mathrm{in}}\times n_{\mathrm{in}}}
. Further, reminding that
G(u,w,\hat{y})=\frac{\partial}{\partial u}J(\mathbb{S}(u),u,w)
, it yields that
G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y})
is an Hessian matrix, and so it is represented by a symmetric matrix. Let us give the expression of
G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y})
, by denoting
y=\mathbb{S}(u)
:

G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y})=\mathbb{S}^{\prime}(u)^{\ast}c^{\prime\prime}_{yy}(y,\hat{y})\mathbb{S}^{\prime}(u)+c^{\prime}_{y}(y,\hat{y})\mathbb{S}^{\prime\prime}(u)+\gamma^{\prime}(r(w,u))r_{uu}^{\prime\prime}(w,u)+r^{\prime}(w,u)^{\ast}\gamma^{\prime\prime}(r(w,u))r^{\prime}(w,u).

More specifically, for all
v_{1}
,
v_{2}\in\mathcal{U}
we have:

\displaystyle\left\langle G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y}).v_{1};v_{2}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{U}^{\prime},\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c^{\prime\prime}_{yy}(y,\hat{y}).(\mathbb{S}^{\prime}(u).v_{1},\mathbb{S}^{\prime}(u).v_{2})+c^{\prime}_{y}(y,\hat{y}).(\mathbb{S}^{\prime\prime}(u).(v_{1},v_{2}))

\displaystyle+\gamma^{\prime\prime}(r(w,u)).(r^{\prime}(w,u).v_{1},r^{\prime}(w,u).v_{2})+\gamma^{\prime}(r(w,u)).(r_{uu}^{\prime\prime}(w,u).(v_{1},v_{2})).

Solving equation (
24
) determines the value of the variable
\mu
, and thus the question arises whether the operator
G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y})^{\ast}=G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y})
is invertible. Let us comment on the general case where
G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y})
is not necessarily invertible.

4.4.1
When the adjoint operator is invertible

Consider any
(u,w)\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{W}
such that
G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y})
is an invertible matrix. Analogously to the inner-problems, such a case corresponds to an assumption of type
(\mathbf{A2})
transcribed to Problem (

~

∗
⁣
∗

‣
4.1
), and following section
2.4
we can prove via the implicit function theorem the existence of a mapping
w\mapsto u
characterizing the equality
G(u,w,\hat{y})=0
. This is a mapping of type
control-to-state
, since for the outer-problem the variable
u
plays the role of a
state
and
w
is the
command
. This case corresponds to variables
w\in\mathcal{W}
such that the associated inner-problem admits a unique solution
u\in\mathcal{U}
. Since
G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y})
is a square matrix, the matrix
G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y})^{\ast}
is also invertible, and so the adjoint equation (
24
), namely
G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y})^{\ast}\mu+(u-\hat{u})=0
in the generic case, admits the unique solution

\mu=-G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y})^{-T}(u-\hat{u}).

Therefore in this case we are able to derive the necessary Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions for Problem (

~

∗
⁣
∗

‣
4.1
), as the associated qualification constraints are satisfied without ambiguity (see Theorem
4.4
).

4.4.2
When the adjoint operator is not invertible

In the case where
G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y})
is not invertible, in practice we introduce a perturbation of the latter, based on the following basic result:

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
A
be a square matrix. There exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
A-\varepsilon\mathrm{I}
is invertible.

Proof.

Denote by
\chi(\lambda):=\mathrm{det}(A-\lambda\mathrm{I})
the characteristic polynomial of
A
. If
\mathrm{Sp(A)}=\{0\}
, any
\varepsilon\neq 0
is suitable. Otherwise, choose any
\varepsilon
such that
0<\varepsilon<\inf\big\{|\lambda|,\ \lambda\in\mathrm{Sp}(A)\setminus\{0\}\big\}
. Then we have
\mathrm{det}(A-\varepsilon\mathrm{I})=\chi(\varepsilon)\neq 0
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Therefore, when
G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y})
is singular, we can find
\varepsilon(u,w,\hat{y})>0
small enough such that
G^{\prime}_{u}(u,w,\hat{y})-\varepsilon(u,w,\hat{y})\mathrm{I}
is invertible. Note that, up to adapting the proof above, we can also choose
\varepsilon(u,w,\hat{y})>0
small enough such that

7
Conclusion

We have proposed a method for addressing in practice the data-driven design of regularizers for improving a class of inverse problems formulated as optimal control problems. The class of regularizers is restricted to a set of artificial neural networks, which goes in the sense of the purely nonlinear nature of such a problem. Our approach is bi-level, and based on a relaxation exploiting the necessary optimality conditions for these problems. We saw that such a type of approach enables practical and efficient implementation, providing that the different objective functions and activation functions of the neural network are smooth. Developing a similar approach when the involved quantities are less smooth could present an interesting challenge. Further, investigating applicability of our approach for more complex problems (like image segmentation for example) would demonstrate even more its relevance. In such a context, one may need to take into account neural networks architectures that allow more sophisticated realizations. Finally, noticing that our approach necessitates the evaluation of second-order derivatives of the parameter-to-state mapping for the inner-problem, developing higher order adjoint-based methods for deriving the optimality conditions for the outer-problem could potentially improve the implementation and the applicability to large-scale tasks.

where we set
\|y\|_{\mathrm{H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}:=\|\nabla y\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}
.

Proof.

Substituting the first equations of (
28
) satisfied by
y_{1}
and
y_{2}
leads us to

-\divg(u_{1}\nabla y_{1})+\divg(u_{2}\nabla y_{2})=0\Rightarrow-\divg(u_{1}\nabla(y_{1}-y_{2}))+\divg((u_{2}-u_{1})\nabla y_{2})=0.

Note that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}u_{1}|y_{1}-y_{2}|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\Omega=\int_{\Omega}(u_{2}-u_{1})\nabla y_{2}\cdot\nabla(y_{1}-y_{2})\,\mathrm{d}\Omega

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle m\|\nabla(y_{1}-y_{2})\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|\nabla y_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}\|\nabla(y_{1}-y_{2})\|_{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle m\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}\|y_{2}\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}.

Using estimate (

, the previous one implies

Thus estimate (

A.2
On the optimal control approach for solving an inverse problem

Let us fix the ANN weights
w
for this subsection. A classical approach for solving inverse problems consists in taking into account the equality constraint
\varphi(y,u)=0
by incorporating it in the functional to minimize, as follows:

\displaystyle\min_{u\in\mathcal{U},y\in\mathcal{Y}}\Big(J(y,u,w):=\alpha\|\varphi(y,u)\|_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}+c(y,\hat{y})+\gamma\circ r(w,u)\Big),

where
\alpha>0
is a given parameter (and
w
too). The contribution of the state equation (
A
) in the functional to minimize is

\|\varphi(y,u)\|_{\mathcal{Q}}^{2}=\|-\divg(u\nabla y)-f\|_{\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}^{2}+\|y_{|\partial\Omega}-g\|_{\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}.

This approach would require differentiation of the data (represented here by
u
), more specifically the computation of
\nabla u
, and thus could lead to approximation biases, especially when the set of data is incomplete. Instead of that, we consider the state equation as a constraint, that we impose by introducing a Lagrange multiplier denoted by
(p,q)\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)=\mathcal{Q}\simeq\mathcal{Q}^{\prime\prime}
, namely the
adjoint
variable, leading to the search of a saddle-point of a Lagrangian functional, given as:

\mathscr{L}(y,u,p)=c(y,\hat{y})+\gamma\circ r(w,u)+\langle(p,q)\,,\varphi(y,u)\rangle_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}.

A saddle-point of
\mathscr{L}
is obtained as a critical point with respect to the variables
(y,u)
and
(p,q)
. The duality product
\langle(p,q)\,,\varphi(y,u)\rangle_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}
corresponds to a weak variational formulation, namely

\displaystyle\langle(p,q)\,,\varphi(y,u)\rangle_{\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{Q}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\langle p\,,-\divg(u\nabla y)-f\right\rangle_{\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}+\left\langle q\,,y_{|\partial\Omega}-g\right\rangle_{\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega),\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla p:(u\nabla y)\,\mathrm{d}\Omega-\left\langle p\,,f\right\rangle_{\mathrm{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega),\mathrm{H}^{-1}(\Omega)}+\left\langle q\,,y_{|\partial\Omega}-g\right\rangle_{\mathrm{H}^{1/2}(\partial\Omega),\mathrm{H}^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)},

where we have used the Green formula. Thus we see that the data represented by the function
u
does not need to be differentiated.
