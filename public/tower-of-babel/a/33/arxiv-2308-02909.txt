Kalai’s 𝟑 𝒅 \boldsymbol{3^{d}} conjecture for unconditional and locally anti-blocking polytopes
Raman Sanyal, Martin Winter

Abstract
. Kalai’s 3 d 3^{d} conjecture states that every centrally-symmetric d d -polytope has at least 3 d 3^{d} faces. We give short proofs for two special cases: if P P is unconditional (that is, invariant w.r.t. reflection in any coordinate hyperplane), and more generally, if P P is locally anti-blocking. In both cases we show that the minimum is attained exactly for the Hanner polytopes.

51M20, 52B05, 52B11, 52B12, 52B15

1.
Introduction

A convex polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
centrally-symmetric
if
-P=P
.
Central symmetry strongly affects the combinatorics. For example,
Figiel–Lindenstrass–Milman
[
4
]
proved that
P
cannot simultaneously
have few vertices and few facets. However, our understanding of extremal cases
is severely limited. This is best illustrated by the following basic conjecture
due to Kalai
[
5
]
. We write
s(P)
for the
number of non
-empty faces
of
P
and we denote by
C_{d}=[-1,+1]^{d}
the
d
-dimensional cube.

Conjecture 1

(
3^{d}
conjecture)
.

Every
d
-dimensional centrally-symmetric polytope
P
satisfies

s(P)\ \geq\ s(C_{d})\ =\ 3^{d}\,.

The conjecture is known to hold in dimensions
d\leq 4
;
see
[
11
]
. The
3^{d}
conjecture is likened to the
famous Mahler conjecture
[
7
]
in convex geometry;

cf.

[
12
]
. We write

P^{\circ}
for the polar dual of
P
and
M(P):=\vol_{d}(P)\vol_{d}(P^{\circ})

for the
Mahler volume
.

Conjecture 2

(Mahler conjecture)
.

Every
d
-dimensional centrally-symmetric polytope
P
satisfies

M(P)\ \geq\ M(C_{d})\ =\ \frac{4^{d}}{d!}\,.

Intuitively, the Mahler volume can be thought of as a
(
\mathrm{GL}_{d}
-invariant) measure of roundness and, by the Blaschke–Santaló inequality, is maximized on the Euclidean ball. The conjecture states that cubes are the “least round” centrally-symmetric polytopes.
Round polytopes require many faces and
s(P)
should be thought of as a combinatorial measure of roundness and hence should also be minimized on the cube.
It turns out that in both cases the conjectured minimizers are the
Hanner polytopes
: every
1
-dimensional centrally-symmetric polytope is a Hanner polytope; and a polytope of dimension
d\geq 2
is a Hanner polytope if
P
or
P^{\circ}
is linearly isomorphic to the Cartesian product of two lower-dimensional Hanner
polytopes.

A full-dimensional polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
unconditional
(or coordinate-symmetric) if
P
is
symmetric with respect to reflection in every coordinate hyperplane. The
Mahler conjecture holds for unconditional polytopes by the work of
Saint-Raymond
[
10
]
.
Hanner polytopes are unconditional, and
Reisner
[
9
]
showed that these are the only minimizers among all
unconditional polytopes.

The following main result of our paper is an analogue for the
3^{d}
conjecture:

Theorem 3
.

If
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an unconditional polytope, then
s(P)\geq 3^{d}
.
Moreover,
s(P)=3^{d}
if and only if
P
is a Hanner polytope.

This is a consequence of a stronger result. We need the following terminology: for
J\subseteq[d]:=\{1,...,d\}

we define the
coordinate subspace

\mathbb{R}^{d}_{J}:=\Span\{e_{i}:i\in J\}
, where
e_{i}
is the
i
-th unit basis vector.
Further let
\pi_{J}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}_{J}
be the
associated orthogonal projection (we write
\pi_{i}
if
J=\{i\}
).
A polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to be

locally anti-blocking
if
\pi_{J}(P)=P\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{J}
for all
J\subseteq[d]
.
Locally anti-blocking polytopes were introduced in
[
6
]
and generalize unconditional polytopes (see
Figure

1
for further illustration of these concepts).

Figure 1.
Visualization of two unconditional polytopes, a locally anti-blocking polytope that is not unconditional, and a polytope that is neither even though it is centrally-symmetric.

While locally anti-blocking polytopes are not necessarily centrally-symmetric,
it was shown in
[
1
]
that they still satisfy the Mahler
conjecture, including the conjectured equality cases (at least locally in each orthant).

We call a locally anti-blocking polytope
proper
if the origin is contained in its interior.

Theorem

3
is then a consequence of the following more general result:

Theorem 4
.

If
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a proper locally anti-blocking polytope, then
s(P)\geq 3^{d}
. Moreover,
s(P)=3^{d}
if and only if
P
is a (generalized) Hanner polytope.

Here, a
generalized Hanner polytope
is any polytope that is combinatorially equivalent to a usual Hanner polytope and is locally anti-blocking (but not necessarily centrally-symmetric).
In particular, generalized Hanner polytopes follow a recursive definition analogous to Hanner polytopes (see
Section

4
).

To prove
Theorem

4
, we show that there exist exactly
3^{d}
so-called
special points
in
P
and that each face contains at most one of them in its relative interior.
More precisely, for each
\sigma\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{d}
, we define a concave
function
f_{\sigma}
, whose maximum over
P
is attained exactly at one of the special points.
While our approach focuses on the combinatorics of
locally anti-blocking polytopes, it is quite similar to the original proof of
Mahler’s conjecture for unconditional
polytopes
[
10
,
9
]
and maybe suggests a
stronger relation between the two conjectures.

We also give a second short and more combinatorial proof of the inequality part of
Theorem

3
that pertains to the structure of face lattices of unconditional polytopes and that we consider to be of independent interest.
Another proof of the inequality part for unconditional polytopes due to Chambers and Portnoy has simultaneously appeared in
[
2
]
.

Remark 5

(Zonotopes)
.

Another class of polytopes for which both the Mahler conjecture and the
3^{d}
conjecture is known to hold are zonotopes.
The Mahler conjecture for zonotopes was verified in
[
8
]
.
The
3^{d}
conjecture for zonotopes is trivially true.
Indeed, the non-empty faces of a zonotope are
in one-to-one correspondence to the cones of the associated arrangement of
linear hyperplanes. Now any choice of
d
linearly independent hyperplanes
already gives rise to
3^{d}
cones. This also shows that the cube
C_{d}
is the
unique
d
-dimensional zonotope with
3^{d}
non-empty faces.

Remark 6

(Inductive approach)
.

If
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is unconditional and
H:=\{x:x_{i}=0\}
is a
coordinate hyperplane, then
P^{\prime}:=P\cap H
is unconditional in
H\cong\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
. A very appealing approach would be to show that
s(P)\geq 3s(P^{\prime})
and then to prove the
3^{d}
conjecture by induction. This, however,
does not work, as sections can become “too large”, already for unconditional polytopes.
The
3
-dimensional polytope with
8
vertices given by
(\pm 1,\pm 1,0)
,
(\pm 2,0,\pm 1)
has
f
-vector
(8,14,8)
and hence
s(P)=31
. The intersection with
H
however is a hexagon with
s(P^{\prime})=13
; see
Figure

2
.

Figure 2.
An unconditional polytope
P
and a section
P\cap H
(red) with a coordinate hyperplane that “exposes” more than a third of the faces.

2.
Basic facts about locally anti-blocking polytopes

A polytope
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
locally anti-blocking
if
\pi_{J}(x)\in P
holds for all
x\in P
and
J\subseteq[d]
.
In
Lemma

7
below we collect some well-known elementary consequences of this definition.

Recall that
N_{P}(x)
denotes the normal cone of
P
at a point
x\in P
,
i.e.,

N_{P}(x):=\{v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\langle y-x,v\rangle\leq 0\text{ for all $y\in P$}\}.

We also write
N_{P}(F)
for the normal cone at an interior point of the face
F\subseteq P
.

Lemma 7

(see
[
1
]
)
.

Let
P
be a locally anti-blocking polytope

()

Sections with and projections onto coordinate subspaces result in the same polytope
P\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{J}=\pi_{J}(P)=:P_{J}
.
P_{J}
is itself locally anti-blocking.

()

The polar dual
{P}^{\circ}
is again locally anti-blocking.
Moreover,
polar duality com
mutes with sections and projections:
({P}^{\circ})_{J}=(P_{J})^{\circ}\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{J}=:P_{J}^{\circ}
.

()

For
p\in P_{J}
holds

N_{P}(p)\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{J}=N_{P_{J}}(p)\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{J}
.

The following technical property of locally anti-blocking polytopes will be used repeatedly. Intuitively it states: if the interior of a face
F
is not entirely contained on one side of a coordinate
hyperplane
, then its normal cone
N_{P}(F)
must be contained in this hyperplane.

Proposition 8
.

If
F\subseteq P
is a face and
x,y\in\relint(F)
are points with
\sign(x_{i})\not=\sign(y_{i})
for some
i\in[d]
, then
v_{i}=0
for all
v\in N_{P}(F)
.

Proof.

Assume w.l.o.g.
x_{i}\leq 0<y_{i}
.
Since
x,y\in\relint(F)
, there is a
\lambda>1
with
x^{\prime}:=\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\in\relint(F)
and
x_{i}^{\prime}<0
.
Suppose that there exists a
v\in N_{P}(F)
with
v_{i}\not=0
, w.l.o.g.
v_{i}>0
. By the anti-blocking property of
P
we have
\pi_{i}(x^{\prime})=x^{\prime}-x^{\prime}_{i}e_{i}\in P
, and thus

\langle\pi_{i}(x^{\prime}),v\rangle=\langle x^{\prime},v\rangle-x^{\prime}_{i}v_{i}>\langle x^{\prime},v\rangle,

in contradiction to
x^{\prime}
being a maximizer of
\langle\kern 0.70007pt\cdot\kern 0.70007pt,v\rangle
. Thus
v_{i}=0
.
∎

3.
The inequality part of
Theorem

4

For this section let
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a proper locally anti-blocking polytope. We show that
s(P)\geq 3^{d}
, proving the inequality part of
Theorem

4
.

For a point
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we define
\widecheck{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

\widecheck{p}_{i}:=\begin{cases}p_{i}^{-1}&\text{if $p_{i}\not=0$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Note that
\sign(p)=\sign(\widecheck{p})
.
We say that a point
p\in P
is
special
if
\widecheck{p}\in N_{P}(p)
.
Our strategy for proving
s(P)\geq 3^{d}
is to show that
P
has exactly
3^{d}
special points and that each face of
P
can contain at most one of them in its relative interior.

For
\sigma\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{d}
define the
coordinate cone

\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\sigma}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\sign(x)=\sigma\}
. Those are relatively open cones with
\biguplus_{\sigma}\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\sigma}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Lemma 9
.

There are exactly
3^{d}
special points in
P
.
More precisely, for each
\sigma\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{d}
exists exactly one special point
p_{\sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\sigma}
.

Proof.

Define
f_{\sigma}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\sigma}\to\mathbb{R}
by

f_{\sigma}(x)=\sum_{\mathclap{i\in\supp(\sigma)}}\,\log(\sigma_{i}x_{i}).

Then
f_{\sigma}
is strictly concave in
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\sigma}
and has a unique maximizer on
P_{\sigma}:=P\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\sigma}
(where
P_{\sigma}\not=\varnothing
because
P
is proper).
Note also that
\nabla f_{\sigma}(p)=\widecheck{p}
for all
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\sigma}
. Below we show that
p_{\sigma}\in P_{\sigma}
is a special point of
P
if and only if it is the unique maximizer of
f_{\sigma}
in
P_{\sigma}
.
Since
\biguplus_{\sigma}P_{\sigma}=P
, this proves the claim.

A point
p_{\sigma}\in P_{\sigma}
maximizes
f_{\sigma}
if and only if for all
q\in P_{\sigma}
holds
\langle q-p_{\sigma},\nabla f_{\sigma}(p_{\sigma})\rangle

=\langle q-p_{\sigma},\widecheck{p}_{\sigma}\rangle\leq 0
, which is the definition of
\widecheck{p}_{\sigma}\in N_{P_{\sigma}}(p_{\sigma})\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\sigma}=N_{P}(p_{\sigma})\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\sigma}
. We used the anti-blocking property in form of
Lemma

7
(
iii
). ∎

We continue to write
p_{\sigma}
for the uniquely determined special point of
P
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\sigma}
. The inequality part of
Theorem

4
is now an immediate consequence of the following.

Lemma 10
.

A face of
P
contains at most one special point in its relative interior.

Proof.

Suppose
F\subseteq P
is a face of
P
with
p_{\sigma},p_{\tau}\in\relint(F)
.
Then
\widecheck{p}_{\sigma},\widecheck{p}_{\tau}\in N_{P}(F)
.
If
\sign((p_{\sigma})_{i})=\sigma_{i}\not=\tau_{i}=\sign((p_{\tau})_{i})
for some
i\in[d]
, then by
Proposition

8

(\widecheck{p}_{\sigma})_{i}=(\widecheck{p}_{\tau})_{i}=0

\Rightarrow

\sigma_{i}=0=\tau_{i}
, which is a contradiction. Thus
\sigma=\tau
.
∎

We shall call a face
special
if it contains a special point in its relative interior. By
Lemma

10
, there are exactly
3^{d}
special faces which we denote
by
F_{\sigma},\sigma\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{d}
, where
p_{\sigma}\in\relint(F_{\sigma})
and
\widecheck{p}_{\sigma}\in N_{P}(F_{\sigma})
.

4.
The minimizer part of
Theorem

4

For this section we assume that
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a proper locally anti-blocking polytope with exactly
s(P)=3^{d}
non-empty faces.
Then each face of
P
is a special face
F_{\sigma}
for some
\sigma\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{d}
.
Our goal is to show that
P
is a generalized Hanner polytope.
Recall that
generalized Hanner polytopes
are locally anti-blocking realizations of Hanner polytopes, and can be defined recursively as follows: for
d=1
a generalized Hanner polytope is a line segment that contains the origin in its interior.
For
d\geq 2
it is either the Cartesian product or the direct sum of two generalized Hanner polytopes of lower dimension, where by the direct sum of
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
we mean

P_{1}\oplus P_{2}:=\conv\{P_{1}\times\{0\}\cup\{0\}\times P_{2}\}.

For
J\subseteq[d]
we write
P_{J}:=P\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{J}
, which is itself proper locally anti-blocking of dimension
|J|
. In fact, it is a minimizer:

Proposition 11
.

For
J\subseteq[d]
we have
s(P_{J})=3^{|J|}
.

Proof.

We have
s(P_{J})\geq 3^{|J|}
by
Theorem

4
, and it suffices to show
s(P_{J})\leq 3^{|J|}
.

For each face
F
of
P_{J}
there exists a unique minimal face
F_{\sigma}
of
P
so that
F=F_{\sigma}\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{J}
.
We have
\widecheck{p}_{\sigma}\in N_{P}(F_{\sigma})\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\sigma}=N_{P_{J}}(F)\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{\sigma}
by
Lemma

7
(
iii
), and hence
\supp(\sigma)\subseteq J
.
There are exactly
3^{|J|}
sign-vectors with support in
J
.
∎

Applying
Proposition

11
to the case
|J|=2
, we find
s(P_{J})=9
and so
P_{J}
must be a quadrilateral.
Moreover, in order to be locally anti-blocking it must be either a Cartesian product
[a_{1},b_{1}]\times[a_{2},b_{2}]
(we say, it is
axis-aligned
) or a direct sum
[a_{1},b_{1}]\oplus[a_{2},b_{2}]
(we say, it is a
diamond
); see
Figure

3
.

Figure 3.
An axis-aligned quadrangle (left) and a diamond (right).

Based on this observation we build the graph
G_{P}
on the vertex set
[d]
with
J=\{i,j\}
forming an edge if and only if
P_{J}
is axis-aligned.
Some examples of this construction are shown in
Figure

4
.
We will show that the graph
G_{P}
captures the full combinatorial information about the minimizer
P
, and allows us to identify it as a generalized Hanner polytope.

Figure 4.
The 3-dimensional minimizers
P
and their associated graphs
G_{P}
.
The shaded areas visualize intersections of
P
with the 2-dimensional coordinate subspaces.
It holds the more general observation that for an axis-aligned cube the graph
G_{P}
is complete, and for an “axis-aligned” crosspolytope the graph
G_{P}
is edge-less.

We collect some properties of
G_{P}
:

Proposition 12
.

()

G_{{P}^{\circ}}
is the complement graph of
G_{P}
.

()

G_{P_{J}}
is the subgraph of
G_{P}
induced on
J\subseteq[d]
.

()

G_{P_{1}\oplus P_{2}}
is the disjoint union
G_{P_{1}}\uplus G_{P_{2}}
.

Proof.

(
i
)
:

This follows from
{P}^{\circ}\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{J}=P_{J}^{\circ}\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{J}
(using
Lemma

7
(
ii
)) and that the polar dual of an axis-aligned quadrangle is a diamond and vice versa.

(
ii
)
:
This follows from
P_{J}\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{K}=P_{K}
whenever
K\subseteq J
.

(
iii
)
:

Consider
P_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}}\times\{0\}
and
P_{2}\subset\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}}
.
If we have
J\subset\{0,...,d_{1}\}
then
P_{J}=P_{1}\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{J}
, and if
J\subset\{d_{1}+1,...,d_{1}+d_{2}\}
then
P_{J}=P_{2}\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{J}
.
In all other cases with
|J|=2
,
P_{J}
is a diamond.
∎

As seen in
Figure

3
, a minimizer is not necessarily a Hanner polytope in its unconditional form, but might be a generalized Hanner polytope.
It is convenient to consider polytopes only up to a particular equivalence relation which identifies generalized Hanner polytopes with the usual Hanner polytopes.

4.1.
Halfspace scaling

Given
i\in[d]
and
\alpha_{+},\alpha_{-}>0
, a
halfspace scaling
is a piecewise linear map that sends
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
onto
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with

y_{i}=\begin{cases}\alpha_{+}x_{i}&\text{if $x_{i}>0$}\\
\alpha_{-}x_{i}&\text{if $x_{i}\leq 0$}\end{cases}.

Halfspace scalings map proper locally anti-blocking polytopes onto proper locally anti-blocking polytopes and preserves their combinatorial type.
For this section we say that two polytopes are
halfspace-scaling equivalent
if they are related by a sequence of halfspace scalings.

Note that a polytope is a generalized Hanner polytope if and only if it is halfspace-scaling equivalent to a Hanner polytope (see also our definition of a
normal form
below).

4.2.
Reconstructing
\boldsymbol{P}
from
\boldsymbol{G_{P}}
.

Our next goal is to reduce the classification of minimizers to a combinatorial problem.
For that purpose, we show that
P
can be reconstructed from
G_{P}
up to equivalence.

For our discussion below it is sufficient and convenient to restrict to the positive orthant
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:x_{i}\geq 0\}
.
We introduce the following useful notation:
\sigma_{+}:=(+1,...,+1)
,
F_{+}:=F_{\sigma_{+}}
and
p_{+}:=p_{\sigma_{+}}
.
We also use the notation
F_{+}(P)
and
p_{+}(P)
to emphasize the implicit argument, which then allows us to write, for example,
F_{+}(P_{J})
or
F_{+}({P}^{\circ})
to refer to different setting if needed.

The following technical properties of
F_{+}
will be used extensively.

Proposition 13
.

For a face
F\subseteq P
the following are equivalent:

()

F=F_{+}
.

()

\relint(F)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
.

()

{F}^{\diamond}=F_{+}({P}^{\circ})
, where
{F}^{\diamond}\subseteq{P}^{\circ}
is the dual face to
F
.

Proof.

(
i
)

\Rightarrow

(
ii
)
:

Since
p_{+}\in\relint(F_{+})
and
\widecheck{p}_{+}\in N_{P}(F_{+})
are both in
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
, from
Proposition

8
follows that all other inner points of
F_{+}
must be in
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
as well.

(
ii
)

\Rightarrow

(
i
)
:

Since
F=F_{\sigma}
for some
\sigma\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{d}
, from
p_{\sigma}\in\relint(F_{\sigma})\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
follows
\sigma=\sigma_{+}
and
F_{\sigma}=F_{+}
.

(
ii
)

\Rightarrow

(
iii
)
:

We have
{F}^{\diamond}=F_{\sigma}({P}^{\circ})
for some
\sigma\in\{-1,0,+1\}^{d}
. Then
\widecheck{p}_{\sigma}({P}^{\circ})\in\relint(N_{{P}^{\circ}}({F}^{\diamond}))=\cone(\relint(F))\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
. Hence
\sigma=\sigma_{+}
and
{F}^{\diamond}=F_{+}({P}^{\circ})
.

(
iii
)

\Rightarrow

(
i
)
:
Swap
P
and
{P}^{\circ}
(and
F
and
{F}^{\diamond}
) and apply (
i
)
\Rightarrow
(
iii
).
∎

Proposition 14
.

If
F\subset F_{+}
is a proper face, then
F=F_{+}\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{J}
for some
J\subset[d]
.

Proof.

Since
F\subset F_{+}\subset\cl(\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+})
by
Proposition

13
(
i
)
\Rightarrow
(
ii
), either holds
\smash{F\subset\partial\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}
or
\smash{\relint(F)\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}}
.
By
Proposition

13
(
ii
)
\Rightarrow
(
i
), the latter is possible only if
F=F_{+}
.
Thus,
F\subset\partial\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}
, and

is of the claimed form.
∎

Proposition 15
.

If
F_{+}
is a facet, then it must be a simplex of the form

F_{+}=\conv\{\alpha_{1}e_{1},...,\alpha_{d}e_{d}\}\quad\text{with $\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{d}>0$}.

Proof.

By
Proposition

14
, the faces of
F_{+}
are of the form
F_{+}\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{J}
with
J\subseteq[d]
.
This can produce at most
2^{d}
faces (including the empty face).
Since
F_{+}
is of dimension
d-1
, it must be a simplex with exactly
2^{d}
faces.
Counting the faces in each dimension shows that the
\delta
-dimensional faces of
F_{+}
are of the form
F_{+}\cap\mathbb{R}^{d}_{J}
with
|J|=\delta+1
.
In particular, the vertices of
F_{+}
are in
\mathbb{R}^{d}_{J}=\Span\{e_{i}\}
where
J=\{i\}
, that is, they are of the form
\alpha_{i}e_{i}
.
The claim follows.
∎

Proposition 16
.

For
d\geq 3
holds

()

Characterizing Hanner polytopes

A graph is a

if

()

it is a single vertex.

()

it is the complement of a cograph.

()

Lemma 20

Proof.

Assume first that

We show that

If

If

()

is the complement of

(

()

with previously constructed Hanner polytopes

In conclusion,

5.
A combinatorial proof of
Theorem

3

In this section we present a second independent proof of the
3^{d}
conjecture specifically for unconditional polytopes that is more combinatorial in nature.

The following proof of
Theorem

3
is based on the fact that the face lattice
\mathcal{F}(P)
of a polytope is
relatively complemented
.
A bounded lattice
\mathcal{L}
with least element
\hat{0}
and greatest element
\hat{1}
is
complemented
, if each
F\in\mathcal{L}
has a (not necessarily unique)
complement

G\in\mathcal{L}
,
i.e.,

F\land G=\hat{0}\quad\text{and}\quad F\lor G=\hat{1},

where
\land
and
\lor
denote the meet and join in
\mathcal{L}
.
It is
relatively complemented
if every interval
[F,G]
is complemented.

For the face lattice
\mathcal{F}(P)
we have
\hat{0}=\varnothing
and
\hat{1}=P
.
It is well-known that face lattices are relatively complemented, but we include a short argument:

Lemma 23
.

The face lattice
\mathcal{F}(P)
of a polytope is relatively complemented

Proof.

Since intervals in face lattices are face lattices themselves, it suffices to show that
\mathcal{F}(P)
is complemented.

Clearly,
\varnothing
and
P
are complements of each other.
If
F
is a non-empty proper face, then project
P
“along”
F
, that is, so that
F
becomes a vertex
v
of the projection.
In the projection the vertex
v
has a complement, namely, any facet that does not contain the vertex.
The preimage of this facet under the projection is a complement of the original face
F
.
∎

Note also that in a centrally-symmetric polytope for each non-empty proper face
F\in\mathcal{F}(P)
the face
-F
is a complement.

Proof of
Theorem

3
(the inequality).

We proceed by induction on the dimension
d
of
P
. The cases
d\in\{0,1\}
are trivial.
For the induction step fix a coordinate hyperplane
H
and a proper face
F\in\mathcal{F}(P)
supported by a translate of
H
.
The following three sets
S_{+},S_{0},S_{-}\subset\mathcal{F}(P)
are disjoint:

\displaystyle S_{+}

\displaystyle:=\{\text{faces of $P$ that intersect $F$ but not $-F$}\},

\displaystyle\mathrlap{S_{0}}\phantom{S_{+}}

\displaystyle:=\{\text{faces of $P$ that intersect both of $F$ and $-F$, or neither}\},

\displaystyle S_{-}

\displaystyle:=\{\text{faces of $P$ that intersect $-F$ but not $F$}\}.

We show that each set contains
\geq 3^{d-1}
faces, which proves the claim.

Case
S_{0}
: the faces of
P\cap H
correspond to faces of
P
that are
H
-symmetric (
i.e.,
invariant under reflection in
H
). Each
H
-symmetric face is necessarily in
S_{0}
. Since
P\cap H
is unconditional of dimension
d-1
, we have
|S_{0}|\geq s(P\cap H)\geq 3^{d-1}
by induction hypothesis.

Case
S_{+}
/
S_{-}
: by symmetry it suffices to consider
S_{+}
.
For each
\check{F}\in(\varnothing,F]
and
\hat{F}\in[F,P)
the face
F
has a complement
G(\check{F},\hat{F})
in
[\check{F},\hat{F}]
(using
Lemma

23
), that is,
G(\check{F},\hat{F})\land F=\check{F}
and
G(\check{F},\hat{F})\lor F=\hat{F}
.
In particular, the
G(\check{F},\hat{F})
are distinct since they differ in meet and join with
F
. Below we will show that
G(\check{F},\hat{F})\in S_{+}
.
Assuming this claim, and observing that both
F
and the dual face
F^{\diamond}\in\mathcal{F}(P^{\circ})
are unconditional, the induction hypothesis applies and it already follows

\displaystyle|S_{+}|

\displaystyle\geq|\{G(\check{F},\hat{F}):\varnothing\subset\check{F}\subseteq F\subseteq\hat{F}\subset P\}|

\displaystyle=s\big((\varnothing,F]\big)\cdot s\big([F,P)\big)=s(F)\cdot s(F^{\diamond})\geq 3^{\dim(F)}\cdot 3^{d-1-\dim(F)}=3^{d-1},

It remains to show that indeed
G(\check{F},\hat{F})\in S_{+}
.
By construction
G(\check{F},\hat{F})
intersects
F
in
\check{F}\not=\varnothing
. It remains to show that
G(\check{F},\hat{F})
does
not
intersect
-F
.
But indeed, if
G^{\prime}:=G(\check{F},\hat{F})\land(-F)\not=\varnothing
, then both
G^{\prime}\subseteq G(\check{F},\hat{F})\subseteq\hat{F}
and
G^{\prime}\subseteq-F\Longrightarrow-G^{\prime}\subseteq F\subseteq\hat{F}
, hence
G^{\prime}\lor(-G^{\prime})\subseteq\hat{F}\subset P
, in contradiction to the fact that
G^{\prime}
and
-G^{\prime}
are complements in
P
and
G^{\prime}\lor(-G^{\prime})=P
.
∎

6.
Open questions

Below we collected some remaining questions and related conjectures.

6.1.
Combinatorially locally anti-blocking

Since Kalai’s
3^{d}
conjecture makes a statement only about the polytope’s combinatorics, our results apply to not only locally anti-blocking polytope, but to any polytope that is combinatorially equivalent to a locally anti-blocking polytope.
This raises the question: are there centrally-symmetric polytopes that are not combinatorially equivalent to a locally anti-blocking polytope?
While the answer is most certainly
yes
, we are not aware of any example.

Challenge 24
.

Construct (many) centrally-symmetric polytopes that are
not
combinatorially equivalent to a locally anti-blocking polytope.

A hope is that by understanding how to construct such polytopes, one will also understand why they might need to have many faces.

6.2.
The flag conjecture

The following conjecture was brought to our attention by Matthias Schymura and might be amenable to techniques similar to the ones we employed in this article.
Recall that a
flag
of
P
is an inclusion chain
\varnothing=F_{-1}\subseteq F_{0}

\subseteq F_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq F_{d}=P
, where
F_{i}
is an
i
-dimensional face of
P
.

Conjecture 25
.

Every
d
-dimensional centrally-symmetric polytope
P
has at least as many flags as the
d
-dimensional cube, i.e.,

\text{\#flags}(P)\ \geq\ \text{\#flags}(C_{d})\ =\ 2^{d}d!\,.

Like Kalai’s
3^{d}
conjecture,
25
is widely open, and it appers reasonable to first attack it for the class of locally anti-blocking polytopes.
