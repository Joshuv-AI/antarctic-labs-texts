A Galois connection between Turing jumps and limits
Vasco Brattka

Abstract
. Limit computable functions can be characterized by Turing jumps on the input side or limits on the output side. As a monad of this pair of adjoint operations we obtain a problem that characterizes the low functions and dually to this another problem that characterizes the functions that are computable relative to the halting problem. Correspondingly, these two classes are the largest classes of functions that can be pre or post composed to limit computable functions without leaving the class of limit computable functions. We transfer these observations to the lattice of represented spaces where it leads to a formal Galois connection. We also formulate a version of this result for computable metric spaces. Limit computability and computability relative to the halting problem are notions that coincide for points and sequences, but even restricted to continuous functions the former class is strictly larger than the latter. On computable metric spaces we can characterize the functions that are computable relative to the halting problem as those functions that are limit computable with a modulus of continuity that is computable relative to the halting problem. As a consequence of this result we obtain, for instance, that Lipschitz continuous functions that are limit computable are automatically computable relative to the halting problem. We also discuss 1–generic points as the canonical points of continuity of limit computable functions, and we prove that restricted to these points limit computable functions are computable relative to the halting problem. Finally, we demonstrate how these results can be applied in computable analysis.

Many of these results are scattered in the literature, and it remains somewhat unclear

Some clarity can be brought into the picture if one starts with the notion of a limit
computable function and develops it systematically.
It turns out that at the heart of such a development there is an adjoint situation
that is related to the fact that limit computations can either
be described with limits on the output side or with Turing jumps on the input side.
Shoenfield’s limit lemma is the non-uniform correlate of this observation.
From this perspective functions computable relative to the halting problem
and low functions are natural notions that should be studied alongside
with limit computable functions, since they constitute the largest classes
of functions that can be post or pre composed with limit computable functions
without leaving the class of limit computable functions.

2.
Limit computability

In this section we are going to characterize limit computable functions in different ways.
We start with recalling the definition. Limit computable functions are defined on Turing
machines that have a two-way output tape, and these machines are allowed to revise their output.

Definition
\thethm

(Limit computable functions)
.

A function
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is called
limit computable
,
if there exists a Turing machine
M
that upon any input
p\in{\mathrm{dom}}(F)
computes

F(p)
on its two-way output tape in the long run
such that on any output position
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
after a finite number of steps the output is

F(p)(n)
and will not be changed anymore.

If the Turing machine does only change the entire output (over all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
) finitely many times
for each fixed input
p
, then
F
is called
computable with finitely many mind changes
.
This is a strengthening of the notion of limit computability.

It is clear that all computable maps are limit computable and limit
computable maps are obviously closed under restriction.
The additional flexibility to revise the output tape gives
additional power to limit machines that ordinary Turing machines
do not have. As examples we mention some discontinuous functions that
are not computable, but limit computable.
For the definition we use tupling functions. We define

\langle n,k\rangle:=\frac{1}{2}(n+k)(n+k+1)+k
,

\langle p,q\rangle(2n):=p(n)
and
\langle p,q\rangle(2n+1):=q(n)
,

\langle p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},....\rangle\langle n,k\rangle:=p_{n}(k)
, and

\langle n,p\rangle:=np

for all
p,q,p_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
n,k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
By
\widehat{n}=nnn...\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
we denote the constant sequence with value
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Example
\thethm

(Limit computable maps)
.

The following maps are
limit computable but not continuous and hence not computable:

(1)

The
equality test for zero

{\mathsf{{E}}}:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}},p\mapsto\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\widehat{1}&\mbox{if $p=\widehat{0}$}\\
\widehat{0}&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

(2)

The
limit map

\lim:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}},\langle p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},...\rangle\mapsto\lim_{i\to\infty}p_{i}.

(3)

The
Turing jump

{\mathsf{{J}}}:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
by

{\mathsf{{J}}}(p)(i):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if Turing machine $i$ halts upon input $p$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

for all
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
and
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

The function
{\mathsf{{E}}}
is even computable with finitely many mind changes.

By
\lim_{\Delta}
we denote the restriction of
\lim
to convergent sequences with respect to the discrete topology on
{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
In some sense one can say that the limit map and
{\mathsf{{J}}}
are not only
some limit computable maps, but prototypes of limit computable maps.
Before we can prove a formal version of this statement, we first discuss compositions of computable
and limit computable maps that turn out to be limit computable.

Proposition
\thethm

(Composition)
.

If
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is limit computable and
G:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

is computable, then
F\circ G
and
G\circ F
are both limit computable.

Proof.

Let
M_{F}
be a limit machine computing
F
and
M_{G}
an ordinary Turing machine computing
G
.
Then a limit machine
M
computing
F\circ G
can just be obtained by composing the two machines
M_{F}
and
M_{G}

in the straightforward way.

A limit machine
M^{\prime}
for
G\circ F
can also be constructed by composing the machines
M_{F}
and
M_{G}
.
However, the composition has to be done such that if
M_{F}
changes the content of some output cell
n
,
then the computation of
M_{G}
has to be restarted at time step
n
of its program (which is sufficient to guarantee that
M_{G}
has
not yet seen the content of cell
n
). Since the output of
M_{F}
of any finite length eventually stabilizes and

M_{G}
uses a one-way output tape, it follows that
M^{\prime}
produces a converging output
in this way for any input
p\in{\mathrm{dom}}(G\circ F)
.
∎

It is easy to see that the first statement of the proposition can even be strengthened
in the following way.

Proposition
\thethm

(Composition with finitely many mind changes)
.

If
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is limit computable and
G:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

then

is limit computable.

For the composition

of functions as in Proposition
2

In fact, it is not too difficult to construct a counterexample.

Example
\thethm

(Composition)
.

The equality test
{\mathsf{{E}}}
is computable with finitely many mind changes, and the limit map
\lim
is limit computable,
but
{\mathsf{{E}}}\circ\lim
is not limit computable.

We leave the proof to the reader.
Now we provide a very useful characterization of limit computable functions.
This result is also implicit in the work of Wagner

Theorem
\thethm

(Limit normal form)
.

A function
G:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is limit computable if and only
if there exists a computable function
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
, such that

G=\lim\circ\;F
.

Proof.

By Example
2

\lim
is limit computable.
It follows from Proposition
2
that if
F
is computable,
then
\lim\circ\;F
is limit computable.

Conversely, if
G
is limit computable, then we construct a one-way
output Turing machine
M_{F}
that computes a function
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

This machine

internally simulates a limit machine

for
G

M_{F}
successively produces an output
\langle q_{0},q_{1},...\rangle
in
steps
\langle i,j\rangle=0,1,2,...

In step

simulates
M_{G}
for
i

M_{G}
has filled its
j

for the first time. Machine

writes the resulting content of the simulated output of

–th position into its own output

.
If the simulated

–th position eventually stabilizes, then
\lim_{i\to\infty}q_{i}(j)

exists and coincides with the final value of the
j
–th position

.
Thus the machine

computes a function
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

with
G=\lim\circ\;F
. Here

is restricted to
{\mathrm{dom}}(G)
.
∎

We note that the limit normal form theorem allows us to study limit computable
functions in terms of ordinary computable functions and without considering limit machines.
The particular output behavior of limit machines can be expressed directly using the limit map.
We also obtain the following characterization of limit computable functions as pointwise limits
of a sequence of computable functions.

Corollary
\thethm

(Pointwise limit)
.

A function
G:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is limit computable if and only if there is a computable sequence
(F_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

of computable functions
F_{n}:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
with
{\mathrm{dom}}(G)\subseteq{\mathrm{dom}}(F_{n})
and such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}F_{n}(p)=G(p)

for all
p\in{\mathrm{dom}}(G)
.

We note that there are limit computable functions (such as the limit map
\lim
) that do not have
any
1
–generics in their domain, simply because they are not continuous at any point.

It is easy to see that restricted to
1
–generics the Turing jump operator is continuous and computable relative to the halting problem.

Proposition
\thethm

(Jump on
1
–generics)
.

The Turing jump operator
{\mathsf{{J}}}:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}},p\mapsto p^{\prime}
restricted to the set
of
1
–generics is computable relative to the halting problem.

Proof.

The sets

(1)

A:=\{(w,i)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}\times{\mathbb{N}}:
the
i
–th Turing machine halts on all extensions of
w\}
,

(2)

B:=\{(w,i)\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}\times{\mathbb{N}}:
the
i
–th Turing machine halts on no extension of
w\}

are both computable relative to
0^{\prime}
. Given a
1
–generic
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
it is clear that for each
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
there
is a
w\sqsubseteq p
such that
(w,i)\in A
or
(w,i)\in B
. Depending on the answer we know that
{\mathsf{{J}}}(p)(i)=1
or
{\mathsf{{J}}}(p)(i)=0
.
Hence,
{\mathsf{{J}}}
restricted to
1
–generics is computable relative to the halting problem.
∎

As a consequence of the jump normal form (Theorem
2
) we obtain that every
limit computable function is computable relative to the halting problem, when restricted to the
1
–generic inputs.

Corollary
\thethm

(Limit computability on
1
–generics)
.

Restricted to
1
–generics every limit computable
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is computable relative to the halting problem.

As a non-uniform corollary of Proposition
2

we obtain that all
1
–generics are
generalized low
,
a property that is made more precise in the next well-known corollary
[
28
, Lemma 2]
.

Corollary
\thethm

(Jockusch 1977)
.

p^{\prime}\mathop{\equiv_{\mathrm{T}}}\langle p,0^{\prime}\rangle
for all
1
–generics
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Here the reduction
p^{\prime}\mathop{\leq_{\mathrm{T}}}\langle p,0^{\prime}\rangle
follows directly from Proposition
2
,
and the inverse reduction even holds for arbitrary
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

The points whose jump is below
0^{\prime}
also have a special name, they are called
low
.

Definition
\thethm

(Low points)
.

A point
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is called
low
, if its Turing jump
p^{\prime}
is limit computable.

It is clear that all computable points are low, and all limit computable
1
–generics are low by Corollary
2
.
One can use this observation to show that the class of low points also contains non-computable points.
Using the
low map

{\mathsf{{L}}}:={\mathsf{{J}}}^{-1}\circ\lim
we obtain the following characterization of low points
[
7
, Lemma 8.2]
.

Corollary
\thethm

(Low points)
.

A point
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is low if and only if there is a computable
q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

such that
{\mathsf{{L}}}(q)=p
.

It follows from Corollaries
2
and
2

that computable functions map low inputs to low outputs.
In
[
7
]
low functions were introduced that were further studied in
[
9
,
11
]
.

Definition
\thethm

(Low functions)
.

A function
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is called
low
if there is a computable
G:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

such that
F={\mathsf{{L}}}\circ G
.

This definition captures the idea that the result of
F
is computed as a low point.
It is clear that low functions map computable inputs to low outputs.
We obtain the following closure properties under composition.

Proposition
\thethm

(Composition with low functions)
.

Let
F,G:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
be functions.
If
F
and
G
are low then so is
G\circ F
. If
G
is limit computable
and
F
is low, then
G\circ F
is limit computable.

Proof.

Both observations are based on the jump normal form theorem (Theorem
2
) that
yields a computable
R:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
with
\lim=R\circ{\mathsf{{J}}}
.
By Corollary
2
we also have a computable
S:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
with
R\circ\lim=\lim\circ S
.
This implies
{\mathsf{{L}}}\circ{\mathsf{{L}}}={\mathsf{{J}}}^{-1}\circ\lim\circ{\mathsf{{J}}}^{-1}\circ\lim={\mathsf{{J}}}^{-1}\circ R\circ{\mathsf{{J}}}\circ{\mathsf{{J}}}^{-1}\circ\lim={\mathsf{{L}}}\circ S

and
\lim\circ{\mathsf{{L}}}=R\circ{\mathsf{{J}}}\circ{\mathsf{{J}}}^{-1}\circ\lim=R\circ\lim
. The cases of the composition of general low and limit computable functions
can easily be derived from these observations.
∎

In fact, the low functions form the largest class of functions that can be composed with limit computable functions from the left
without leaving the class of limit computable functions (see also
[
11
, Proposition 14.16]
for a related result).

Corollary
\thethm

(Low functions)
.

A function
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is low if and only if
G\circ F
is limit computable for every limit computable
G:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

The “only if” direction is a direct consequence of Proposition
2
.
The “if” direction follows from the limit normal form theorem if one applies the assumption to
G={\mathsf{{J}}}
.
As a consequence of Proposition
2
we get the following corollary.

Corollary
\thethm

(Finite mind changes are low)
.

Every function
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
that is computable with finitely many mind changes is low.

Hence, in contrast to low points
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
, we have a lot of natural examples of low functions,
such as the equality test
{\mathsf{{E}}}
.

We close this section by mentioning briefly that there is a characterization of functions that are computable
with finitely many mind changes that is analogous to Theorem
2
.

Theorem
\thethm

(Discrete limit normal form)
.

A function
G:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is computable with finitely many mind changes if and only
if there exists a computable function
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
, such that

G=\lim\nolimits_{\Delta}\circ\;F
.

We leave the simple proof to the reader (see
[
7
]
).

3.
Computability Relative to the Halting Problem

Now we consider functions that are computable relative to the halting problem
0^{\prime}
.
It turns out that they play a dual rôle to the low functions that are characterized in terms of
{\mathsf{{L}}}={\mathsf{{J}}}^{-1}\circ\lim
.
The functions that are computable relative to the halting problem can be characterized in terms of
{\mathsf{{H}}}:={\mathsf{{J}}}\circ\lim^{-1}:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

The first result shows that
{\mathsf{{H}}}
is computable relative to the halting problem.
In order to prove this we need to invert limits such that we obtain control on the jump of the resulting converging sequence.
Roughly speaking, this is possible since there are many sequences converging to a given point, and this
gives us enough freedom to choose a suitable one. We recall that the
composition
of
two multi-valued functions
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
and
g:\subseteq Y\rightrightarrows Z
is defined by
(g\circ f)(x):=\{z\in Z:(\exists y\in f(x))\;z\in g(y)\}

with
{\mathrm{dom}}(g\circ f):=\{x\in X:x\in{\mathrm{dom}}(f)
and
f(x)\subseteq{\mathrm{dom}}(g)\}
.
A multi-valued
f:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is called
computable (relative to some oracle
q
)
if there is
a single-valued
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
with the corresponding property and such that

F(p)\subseteq f(p)
for all
p\in{\mathrm{dom}}(f)
and
{\mathrm{dom}}(f)\subseteq{\mathrm{dom}}(F)
.
We recall that a function
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is called
computable relative to some oracle
q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
if there is some computable function
G:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that

F(p)=G\langle q,p\rangle
for all
p\in{\mathrm{dom}}(F)
.
In terms of computability theory, the next proof uses the
finite extension method
.

Theorem
\thethm

(Limit inversion)
.

{\mathsf{{H}}}={\mathsf{{J}}}\circ\lim^{-1}
is computable relative to the halting problem.

Proof.

We need to show that there exists a function

0^{\prime}
.
Given

we describe the computation of
I
relative to
0^{\prime}
by an inductive construction
in
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
We start with
w_{0}:=\varepsilon\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and
t_{0}:=0\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Given a finite sequence of words
w_{0},...,w_{t_{i}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}

together with numbers
t_{0}<t_{1}<...<t_{i}
and bits
b_{0},...,b_{i-1}\in\{0,1\}

we describe how we determine
t_{i+1}\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
w_{t_{\underline{i}}+1},...,w_{t_{\underline{i+1}}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and

For notational clarity, we have underlined the indexes of

.
Now we consider the question whether the

If the outcome of the decision is positive, then already a finite number

{\mathsf{{J}}}\circ I(p)=(b_{0},b_{1},b_{2},...)

We note that
{\mathsf{{H}}}
is, in particular, continuous, and
{\mathsf{{H}}}^{-1}=\lim\circ{\mathsf{{J}}}^{-1}
is computable by Theorem

.
Theorem
3
yields the following characterization of functions computable relative to the

{\mathsf{{H}}}
that characterizes
functions that are computable relative to the halting problem (the problem
\langle{\mathrm{id}}\times\chi_{0^{\prime}}\rangle
with the characteristic function
\chi_{0^{\prime}}
of the halting problem
would be a simpler such example), but the point is that our particular
{\mathsf{{H}}}
has this property.

Theorem
\thethm

(Computability relative to the halting problem)
.

A function
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is computable relative to the halting problem if and
only if there is a computable function
G:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
F=G\circ{\mathsf{{H}}}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3

{\mathsf{{H}}}
is computable relative to
0^{\prime}

is

F

F(p)=S\langle 0^{\prime},p\rangle
for all

.
By Theorem

. Hence
{\mathsf{{J}}}(\widehat{0})(i)={\mathsf{{J}}}(p)(r(i))
for all

and

.
Then
R:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
with
R(q)(i):=qr(i)
is computable and so is
G:=S\circ\langle R,T\rangle
.
We obtain

F(p)=S\langle{\mathsf{{J}}}(\widehat{0}),p\rangle=S\langle R\circ{\mathsf{{H}}}(p),T\circ{\mathsf{{H}}}(p)\rangle=G\circ{\mathsf{{H}}}(p)

for all

, hence

if

is restricted suitably (which is possible
since
{\mathsf{{H}}}(p)\cap{\mathsf{{H}}}(q)=\emptyset
for

.)
∎

We note that this result implies that
{\mathsf{{H}}}
is not computable (hence it is an interesting example
of a natural problem that is continuous and not computable).
Another interesting consequence of our results is
that the functions that are computable relative to the halting problem form the largest class of functions
that, when composed with a limit computable function from the right, yield a limit computable function.

Corollary
\thethm

(Computability relative to the halting problem)
.

A function
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
is computable relative to the halting problem if
and only if
F\circ G
is limit computable for every limit computable
G:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.

The “if” direction follows from Theorems
3
and
2
, when one chooses
G=\lim
.
The “only if” direction is a consequence of Theorems
3
and
2
.
A comparison of Corollaries
3
and
2
shows
that the notions of a low function is dual to the notion of a function that is computable relative to the halting
problem. Both classes should be naturally studied alongside the limit computable functions.

As another non-uniform side result of the limit inversion theorem (Theorem

from computability theory, the Friedberg jump inversion theorem

Corollary
\thethm

For every

4.
Limit computability on represented spaces

The purpose of this section is to transfer our results on limit computable maps to represented spaces.
We recall that a
representation
of a set
X
is a surjective
function
\delta:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to X
.
In this situation we say that
(X,\delta)
is a

represented space
.
Any point
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
with
\delta(p)=x
is called
a
name
of
x
, and the word “representation” is
reserved for the map
\delta
as such.
A
problem
is a multi-valued partial function

f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
on represented spaces
X
and
Y
.
If
(X,\delta_{X})
and
(Y,\delta_{Y})
are represented spaces
and
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
is a problem, then

F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

realizes

f
, in symbols
F\vdash f
, if
\delta_{Y}F(p)\in f\delta_{X}(p)

holds for all
p\in{\mathrm{dom}}(f\delta_{X})
.

Definition
\thethm

(Computable functions)
.

A problem
f
is called
continuous
,
computable
,
limit computable
,
low
or
computable with respect to the halting problem
,
if it has a realizer with the corresponding property.

Likewise, other properties can be transferred from realizers to problems.
By
{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)
we denote the
set of total continuous functions

f:X\to Y
.
It is clear that all properties related to closure under composition that have been discussed in the previous section can
be transferred to problems. This is because
F\vdash f
and
G\vdash g
implies
F\circ G\vdash f\circ g
.
We can also transfer properties of points to represented spaces using representations.

Definition
\thethm

(Computable points)
.

A point
x\in X
in a represented space
X
is called
computable
,
limit computable
or
low

if it has a name with the corresponding property.

We note that the notion of

via names since it is not invariant under equivalent representations (see Proposition

and the discussion afterwards).

Our main goal here is to transfer the limit normal form theorem and the
jump normal form theorem (Theorems

Since we can incorporate limits and jumps into the represented spaces, these

In fact, some of the discussed operations on represented spaces have further properties.
In particular the pair
(\delta\mapsto\delta_{\mathsf{{J}}},\delta\mapsto\delta^{\prime})
forms a
Galois connection
in the lattice of representations
of a fixed set. This is a consequence of Theorem
4
applied to the injection
{\mathrm{id}}:X\to Y
.

Corollary
\thethm

(Galois connection between Turing jumps and limits)
.

Let
(X,\delta_{X})
and
(Y,\delta_{Y})
be represented spaces with
X\subseteq Y
.
Then
\delta_{X_{\mathsf{{J}}}}\leq\delta_{Y}\iff\delta_{X}\leq\delta_{Y^{\prime}}
.

The operation
\delta\mapsto\delta^{\mathsf{{L}}}=(\delta_{\mathsf{{J}}})^{\prime}
is the
monad
of this Galois connection, which
implies that it is a closure operator. Likewise,
\delta\mapsto\delta_{\mathsf{{H}}}=(\delta^{\prime})_{\mathsf{{J}}}
is an interior operator
[
17
, Proposition 3(4)]
.

Proposition
\thethm

(Closure and interior operators)
.

\delta\mapsto\delta^{\mathsf{{L}}}
and
\delta\mapsto\delta^{\Delta}
are closure operators
and
\delta\mapsto\delta_{\mathsf{{H}}}
is an interior operator
on the lattice of representations of a fixed set
X
.

The fact that
\delta\mapsto\delta^{\Delta}
is also a closure operator can easily be proved directly.
Another consequence of Corollary
4
which can also easily be proved directly,
is that
\delta\mapsto\delta_{\mathsf{{J}}}
preserves suprema and
\delta\mapsto\delta^{\prime}
preserves infima
[
17
, Proposition 3(8)]
.
We recall for represented spaces
(X,\delta_{X})
and
(Y,\delta_{Y})
the
meet
or
infimum

\delta_{X}\wedge\delta_{Y}
,
which is a representation of
X\cap Y
, can be defined by
(\delta_{X}\wedge\delta_{Y})\langle p,q\rangle=z:\iff\delta_{X}(p)=\delta_{Y}(q)=z
.
If
X=Y
, then
\delta_{X}\wedge\delta_{Y}
is actually known to the the infimum in the lattice of representations of
X

[
52
, Lemma 3.3.8]
.
Hence we obtain the following.

Proposition
\thethm

(Infimum and jumps)
.

Let
(X,\delta_{X})
and
(Y,\delta_{Y})
be represented spaces. Then
(\delta_{X}\wedge\delta_{Y})^{\prime}\equiv\delta_{X}^{\prime}\wedge\delta_{Y}^{\prime}
.

Analogously,
\delta\mapsto\delta^{\prime}
commutes with products (see Theorem
4
),
which also implies Proposition
4
since
\delta_{X}\wedge\delta_{Y}=\Delta_{X\cap Y}^{-1}\circ\delta_{X\times Y}
,
where
\Delta_{X\cap Y}
denotes the
diagonal

\Delta_{X\cap Y}:X\cap Y\to(X\cap Y)\times(X\cap Y),x\mapsto(x,x)
.
Likewise,
\delta\mapsto\delta_{\mathsf{{J}}}
commutes with coproducts and suprema, but we do not formulate these results here.

5.
Limit computability on metric spaces

In this section we want to transfer some of our results to computable metric spaces.
We recall that
(X,d,\alpha)
is called a
computable metric space
, if

(X,d)
is a metric space with metric
d:X\times X\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
\alpha:{\mathbb{N}}\to X
is a sequence
that is dense in
X
such that
d\circ(\alpha\times\alpha):{\mathbb{N}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a computable sequence of real numbers.
Each computable metric space is equipped with its
Cauchy representation

\delta_{X}:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to X

that is defined by
\delta_{X}(p):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\alpha p(n)
with
{\mathrm{dom}}(\delta_{X}):=\{p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}:(\forall k)(\forall n\geq k)\;d(\alpha p(n),\alpha p(k))<2^{-k}

and
(\alpha p(n))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges
\}
. The real numbers
{\mathbb{R}}
are also equipped with a Cauchy representation
that is induced by a standard numbering
\alpha
of all rational numbers.
Besides the usual Cauchy representation there is also the so-called
naive Cauchy representation

\delta_{X}^{\mathrm{n}}

that is defined exactly as
\delta_{X}
but with
{\mathrm{dom}}(\delta_{X}^{\mathrm{n}}):=\{p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}:(\alpha p(n))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges
\}
.

Given a computable metric space
(X,d,\alpha)
we define the
open ball

B_{\langle c,r\rangle}:=B(\alpha(c),\overline{r}):=\{x\in X:d(x,\alpha(c))<\overline{r}\}

for every
c,r\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
where
\overline{\langle i,j,k\rangle}:=\frac{i-j}{k+1}
denotes the rational number encoded by
i,j,k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
By
W_{i}:=\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:
Turing machine
i
halts on input
n\}
we denote the usual numbering of c.e. subsets of
{\mathbb{N}}
,
and by
U_{i}:=\bigcup_{n\in W_{i}}B_{n}
we denote a numbering of all
c.e. open
subsets of
X
.
We can consider the set
{\mathcal{O}}(X)
of open subsets as a represented space with representation

\delta_{{\mathcal{O}}(X)}(p)=\bigcup_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}B_{p(i)}
. The c.e. open subsets are exactly the computable points in
{\mathcal{O}}(X)

with this representation.

We can now generalize the limit map and the Turing jump as defined in Example
2

to arbitrary computable metric spaces.

Definition
\thethm

(Limit and Turing jump)
.

Let
X
be a computable metric space. We define:

(1)

The
limit map
of
X
by

\lim\nolimits_{X}:\subseteq X^{\mathbb{N}}\to X,(x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mapsto\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}
.

(2)

The
Turing jump

{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}:X\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
of
X
by

{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}(x)(i):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if $x\in U_{i}$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

for all
x\in X
and
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

It is easy to see that both functions defined here are always limit computable.

Proposition
\thethm
.

\lim_{X}
and
{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}
are limit computable for every computable metric space
X
.

Proof.

Let
\delta_{X}
be the Cauchy representation of the computable metric space
(X,d,\alpha)
.
Given a
p=\langle p_{0},p_{1},p_{2},...\rangle\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
with a sequence
(p_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of names of points
x_{n}:=\delta_{X}(p_{n})
such that

x:=\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}
exists, we need to devise a limit computation that yields a
q\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\delta_{X})

with
\delta_{X}(q)=x
. For every
i,k\in{\mathbb{N}}
the property

(\exists n>i)\;d(\delta_{X}(p_{n}),\delta_{X}(p_{i}))>2^{-k-2}

is c.e. in
p
and
k,i
. Hence, with the help of
{\mathsf{{J}}}(p)
we can decide this property,
and hence for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
we can systematically search for a
i=i_{k}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that it fails. Such
an
i_{k}
must exists since
x_{n}
converges to
x
. We can assume that the sequence
(i_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is strictly
monotone increasing.
For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
we let
q(k):=p_{i_{k}}(k+2)
. Then for
n>k

\displaystyle d(\alpha q(n),\alpha q(k))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d(\alpha p_{i_{n}}(n+2),\delta_{X}(p_{i_{n}}))+d(\delta_{X}(p_{i_{n}}),\delta_{X}(p_{i_{k}}))+d(\delta_{X}(p_{i_{k}}),\alpha p_{i_{k}}(k+2))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2^{-n-2}+2^{-k-2}+2^{-k-2}<2^{-k}

and
\delta_{X}(q)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\alpha p_{i_{k}}(k+2)=\lim_{k\to\infty}x_{k}=x
.
By Theorem
2
it follows that
\lim_{X}
is limit computable.

{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}
can easily be computed on a limit Turing machine. Given a name of an input
x\in X
one produces for each
output position
{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}(x)(i)
the default value
0
and simultaneously one tries to verify
x\in U_{i}
. As soon
as
x\in U_{i}
can be confirmed, the
i
–th output value has to be changed to
1
, which happens at most once
for each position
i
.
∎

This allows us to transfer the limit normal form theorem (Theorem
2
) to computable metric spaces.
For
f,s:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
we write
s\sqsubseteq f
if
s(x)\subseteq f(x)
for all
x\in{\mathrm{dom}}(f)
and
{\mathrm{dom}}(f)\subseteq{\mathrm{dom}}(s)
.
In this situation we call
s
a
solution
or
selector
of
f
.

Theorem
\thethm

(Limit normal form)
.

Let
X
be a represented space,
Y
a computable metric space and

f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
a problem. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
is limit computable,

(2)

\lim_{Y}\circ g\sqsubseteq f
for some computable
g:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y^{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

That (2) implies (1) follows since
\lim_{Y}
is limit computable by Proposition
5
.
We need to prove that (1) implies (2). Let
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
be limit computable.
We use the Cauchy representation
\delta_{Y}
of
Y
.
Then
f
has a limit computable realizer
F:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
By the limit normal form theorem (Theorem
2
) there exists a computable
G:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

such that
F=\lim\circ G
. Now we define a function
H:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

by
H(p)=\langle r_{0},r_{1},r_{2},...\rangle
, provided that
G(p)=\langle q_{0},q_{1},q_{2},...\rangle
, where each
r_{i}

is determined as follows. We choose
r_{1}:=r_{0}:=(q_{0}(0),q_{0}(0),...)
and if
i>1
then
for each
k\in\{2,...,i\}
we check, which of the following two conditions is recognized first:

(1)

(\forall j\in\{1,...,k-1\})\;d(\alpha q_{i}(k),\alpha q_{i}(j))<2^{-j+1}
,

(2)

(\exists j\in\{1,...,k-1\})\;d(\alpha q_{i}(k),\alpha q_{i}(j))>2^{-j}
.

Depending on which condition is recognized first, we choose

(1)

r_{i}:=(q_{i}(1),q_{i}(2),...,q_{i}(i),q_{i}(i),....)
, if for all
k\leq i
the first condition is recognized first,

(2)

r_{i}:=(q_{i}(1),q_{i}(2),...,q_{i}(k-1),q_{i}(k-1),...)
, if
k\leq i
is minimal such that the second condition is recognized first.

We note that
r_{i}\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\delta_{Y})
for all
i
and if
q_{i}
has a prefix of length
k\leq i
that is a valid prefix
of a name in
q\in{\mathrm{dom}}(\delta_{Y})
, then
d(\delta_{Y}(r_{i}),\delta_{Y}(q))\leq 2^{-k+1}
.
Altogether,
H
is computable, and the definition ensures that
{\mathrm{range}}(H)\subseteq{\mathrm{dom}}(\delta_{Y}^{\mathbb{N}})

and
\lim_{Y}\circ\delta_{Y}^{\mathbb{N}}\circ H=\delta_{Y}\circ\lim\circ G=\delta_{Y}\circ F
.
This proves that
H
is a realizer of a computable multi-valued function
g:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y^{\mathbb{N}}
with
\lim_{Y}\circ g\sqsubseteq f
.
∎

For completeness we formulate the result also for single-valued
f
.
We note that the problem
g
still needs to be multi-valued in general.

Corollary
\thethm

(Limit normal form)
.

Let
X
be a represented space,
Y
a computable metric space and

f:\subseteq X\to Y
a map. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

f:\subseteq X\to Y
is limit computable,

(2)

f=\lim_{Y}\circ g
for some computable
g:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y^{\mathbb{N}}
.

As a corollary we obtain the following conclusion on pointwise limits of computable sequences of functions.

Corollary
\thethm

(Pointwise limit)
.

Let
X
be a represented space and
Y
a computable metric space.
Let
(g_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a computable sequence in
{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)
, i.e., a computable
sequence of computable functions
g_{n}:X\to Y
. Then
f:X\to Y
, defined by

f(x):=\lim_{n\to\infty}g_{n}(x)
for all
x\in X
, is limit computable.

We note that in general one cannot expect that all limit computable
f
can be obtained as the pointwise
limit of a computable sequence of single-valued
g
(as in the case of Baire space, see Corollary
2
).
We obtain, however, a characterization of limit computable points in computable metric spaces.

Corollary
\thethm

(Limit computable points)
.

Let
X
be a computable metric space. Then
x\in X
is limit computable (in the sense that it has a
limit computable name) if and only if there is a computable sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that

x=\lim_{n\to\infty}x_{n}
.

We recall that a computable metric space
(X,d)
is called
computably compact
if
\{X\}
is c.e. open in
{\mathcal{O}}(X)
.
We note that for computably compact metric spaces
(X,d)
the function space
{\mathcal{C}}(X)={\mathcal{C}}(X,{\mathbb{R}})
with the metric induced by the
uniform norm

||f||:=\sup_{x\in X}|f(x)|

is a computable metric space again. As a dense subset one can use, for instance, the set of rational polynomials
in the
distance functions

d_{x}:X\to{\mathbb{R}},y\mapsto d(x,y)
.
This space
{\mathcal{C}}(X)
is then computably isomorphic to the space obtained by using the function space representation
\delta_{{\mathcal{C}}(X,{\mathbb{R}})}
, see
[
3
]
.
If we apply Corollaries
4
and
5
to the space
{\mathcal{C}}(X)
, then we obtain the following result.

Corollary
\thethm

(Uniform limit)
.

Let
X
be a computably compact computable metric space.
Then
f:X\to{\mathbb{R}}
is computable relative to the halting problem if and only if there is a computable sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

of computable functions
f_{n}:X\to{\mathbb{R}}
that converges uniformly to
f
, i.e., such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}||f-f_{n}||=0
.

A characterization similar to Corollary
5
was obtained
for effectively
{{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{0}_{2}}
–measurable functions in
[
6
, Theorem 9.5]
(we refer the reader
to
[
6
]
for the definition of measurability, as we are not going to use these notions here any further).
This yields the following conclusion.

Corollary
\thethm

(Borel measurability)
.

Let
X,Y
be computable metric spaces. Then
a function
f:X\to Y
is limit computable if and only if it is effectively
{{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{0}_{2}}
–measurable.

Corollary
5
can also be applied to the naive Cauchy representation
since
\delta_{X}^{\mathrm{n}}=\lim_{X}\circ\widehat{\alpha}
, where
\widehat{\alpha}:{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to X^{\mathbb{N}},p\mapsto(\alpha p(0),\alpha p(1),\alpha p(2),...)

is the parallelization of
\alpha
that is computable with respect to the Cauchy representation
\delta_{X}
.
We obtain the following result that generalizes corresponding results for the spaces
X={\mathbb{R}}

and
X={\mathcal{C}}([0,1]^{n})
by Ziegler
[
56
, Propositions 2.5 and 2.7]
.

Corollary
\thethm

(Naive Cauchy representation as jump)
.

\delta_{X}^{\mathrm{n}}\equiv\lim_{X}\circ\delta_{X}^{\mathbb{N}}\equiv\delta_{X}^{\prime}
for every computable metric space
X
.

Proof.

With the help of Theorem
4
and Corollary
5

one obtains
\delta_{X}^{\mathrm{n}}\leq\delta_{X}^{\prime}
.
The reduction
\delta_{X}^{\prime}\leq\lim_{X}\circ\delta_{X}^{\mathbb{N}}
follows from the same theorems applied to
the identity
{\mathrm{id}}:X^{\prime}\to X
.
The remaining reduction
\lim_{X}\circ\delta_{X}^{\mathbb{N}}\leq\lim_{X}\circ\widehat{\alpha}
is easy to prove directly,
by choosing a diagonal sequence.
∎

Together with Theorem
4
we obtain a proof
of the following result from
[
12
, Theorem 22]
.

Corollary
\thethm

(B. and Hertling 2002)
.

Let
X,Y
be computable metric spaces.
The functions
f:\subseteq X\to Y
that are continuous with respect to the naive Cauchy representations
on
X
and
Y
are the usual continuous functions.

Now we study the jump
{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}
. We first prove that
{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}^{-1}
is computable in general.

Proposition
\thethm

(Inverse jump)
.

{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}^{-1}:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to X
is computable for every computable metric space
X
.

Proof.

We recall that a standard representation of
X
can be defined by

\delta(p)=x:\iff{\mathrm{range}}(p)=\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:x\in B_{n}\}
. It is easy to see that
\delta\leq\delta_{X}
,
where
\delta_{X}
denotes the Cauchy representation of
X

[
52
, Theorem 8.1.4]
.
Hence, it suffices to compute
{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}^{-1}
with respect to
\delta
.
Since
{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}(x)
is the characteristic function of
\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:x\in U_{n}\}
, it is straightforward
to generate a list of all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
x\in B_{n}
, given
{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}(x)
.
∎

Using this result we obtain the following form of the jump normal form theorem.
Surprisingly we need a variant of Schröder’s representation
[
43
]

of computable metric spaces that has compact fibers, which can be computed
with respect to positive information.
Such a representation was studied in
[
6
]
.

Theorem
\thethm

(Jump normal form)
.

Let
X,Y
be computable metric spaces and

f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
a problem. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
is limit computable,

(2)

g\circ{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}\sqsubseteq f
for some computable
g:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to Y
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5
it is clear that
g\circ{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}
is limit computable for every computable
g:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to Y
.
Let now
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
be limit computable. By Theorem
5
there is a computable

h:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lim_{Y}\circ h\sqsubseteq f
.
Let
\delta\equiv\delta_{X}
be a representation of
X
that is equivalent to the Cauchy representation
\delta_{X}

and that makes
\kappa_{X}:X\to{\mathcal{K}}_{+}({\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}),x\mapsto\delta^{-1}\{x\}
computable, where

{\mathcal{K}}_{+}({\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}})
denotes the space of compact subsets of
{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
endowed with the
representation
\delta_{{\mathcal{K}}_{+}({\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}})}(p)=K:\iff{\mathrm{range}}(p)=\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:K\cap B_{n}\not=\emptyset\}
.
Such a representation
\delta
with compact fibers
\delta^{-1}\{x\}
exists for every computable metric space
[
6
, Lemma 6.3 (5)]
.
Let
F
be a computable realizer of
h
, i.e.,
\lim_{Y}\delta_{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}F\sqsubseteq f\delta
.
That
\kappa_{X}
is computable implies that the set

U:=\{(x,n,k)\in X\times{\mathbb{N}}^{2}:(\exists p\in\delta^{-1}\{x\})(\exists i>n)\;d_{Y}(\delta_{Y}(F(p)(i)),\delta_{Y}(F(p)(n)))>2^{-k}\}

is c.e. open relative to
{\mathrm{dom}}(f)\times{\mathbb{N}}^{2}
, and hence there is a computable function
r:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
such that

U_{r\langle n,k\rangle}\cap{\mathrm{dom}}(f)=\{x\in X:(x,n,k)\in U\}\cap{\mathrm{dom}}(f)
.
Let
x\in{\mathrm{dom}}(f)
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Then for all
p\in\delta^{-1}\{x\}
there is some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
for all
i>n
we have
d_{Y}(\delta_{Y}(F(p)(i)),\delta_{Y}(F(p)(n)))\leq 2^{-k}
, because
(\delta_{Y}F(p)(n))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is convergent.
Since
\delta^{-1}\{x\}
is compact, the number
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
even exists uniformly for all
p\in\delta^{-1}\{x\}
.
That is, for every
x\in{\mathrm{dom}}(f)
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
there is some
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
x\not\in U_{r\langle n,k\rangle}
.
With the help of
{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}(x)(r\langle n,k\rangle)
we can actually check this condition, and hence we can compute an
s:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
such that
for each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
we obtain
x\not\in U_{r\langle s(k),k\rangle}
.
By Proposition
5
we can also obtain a
p\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}
with
\delta(p)=x

from
{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}(x)
. Hence there is a computable
g:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to Y
with
g\circ{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}(x)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\delta_{Y}(F(p)(s(k)))=\lim_{Y}\delta_{Y^{\mathbb{N}}}F(p)\sqsubseteq f(x)

for all
x\in{\mathrm{dom}}(f)
, where the first limit is computable since it is effective (with speed of convergence
2^{-k}
, see the proof of Proposition
5

for a detailed calculation).
∎

Since
{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}
is injective, we can assume that
{\mathrm{dom}}(g)
is such that
{\mathrm{dom}}(g\circ{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X})={\mathrm{dom}}(f)
.
We formulate the characterization for single-valued functions as a corollary.

Corollary
\thethm

(Jump normal form)
.

Let
X,Y
be computable metric spaces and

f:\subseteq X\to Y
a function. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

f:\subseteq X\to Y
is limit computable,

(2)

f=g\circ{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}
for some computable
g:\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}\to Y
.

Another consequence of Theorem
5
is that every multi-valued
limit computable problem has a single-valued limit computable selector.

Corollary
\thethm

(Selection)
.

Let
X,Y
be computable metric spaces.
For every limit computable
f:\subseteq X\rightrightarrows Y
there is a limit computable single-valued

g:\subseteq X\to Y
with
g\sqsubseteq f
.

Now we define
1
–genericity for computable metric spaces.

Definition
\thethm

(
1
–genericity)
.

Let
X
be a computable metric space. Then we call
x\in X

1
–generic
if it is a point
of continuity of
{\mathsf{{J}}}_{X}
.

It is easy to see that
1
–generic points can be characterized as those points that avoid
the boundaries of all c.e. open sets. By
\partial U
we denote the
boundary
of
U
.

Lemma
\thethm

(
1
–genericity)
.

Let
X
be a computable metric space. Then
x\in X
is
1
–generic if and only if

x\in X\setminus\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}\partial U_{i}
.

This characterization is equivalent to the definition of
1
–genericity as it is used in computability theory
and as it has been used in computable metric spaces before (see
[
30
, Corollary 2.3]
,
[
10
, Lemma 9.2]
or
[
27
, Definition 2.1]
).
By the Baire category theorem every complete metric space is comeager and since the set
\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}\partial U_{i}
is meager,
it follows that the
1
–generics form a comeager
G_{\delta}
–set in any complete computable metric space.
In particular, in a complete computable metric space, there are many
1
–generic points.
We obtain the following characterization of
1
–genericity.

Theorem
\thethm

(
1
–genericity)

Proposition

Corollary
\thethm

(Hereditary quotients)
.

Every admissible representation of a sequential topological space is a

6.
Limit computability and computability relative to the halting problem

It is clear that every function that is computable relative to the halting problem is also limit computable.
This observation even holds uniformly in the sense specified in the following corollary.
The corollary is a consequence of Theorem
4
(3) since
{\mathrm{id}}:X\to X^{\prime}
is computable.

Corollary
\thethm

(Limit computability of functions computable relative to the halting problem)
.

{\mathrm{id}}:{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)^{\prime}\to{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y^{\prime})
is computable for all represented spaces
X,Y
.

For some spaces, such as
X={\mathbb{N}}
, this identity is even a computable isomorphism (see Corollary
4
).
However, it is clear that this does not happen for many other spaces
X,Y
since often
{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)^{\prime}\subsetneqq{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y^{\prime})
.
The former set contains only continuous functions, the latter can contain discontinuous ones.
Even restricted to continuous functions the identity in Corollary
6
is not a computable isomorphism in general.

We can, however, characterize the functions that are computable relative to the halting problems in terms
of limit computable functions. For this purpose we need the notion of a modulus of continuity.
Let
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
be metric spaces and
f:X\to Y
a function.
Then
m:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
is called a
modulus of continuity
of
f
at
x\in X
if

d_{X}(x,y)<2^{-m(n)}\Longrightarrow d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))<2^{-n}

holds for all
y\in X
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
The following result is well-known (see, for instance,
[
3
, Lemma 4.4.25]
).

Proposition
\thethm

(Modulus of continuity)
.

Let
X,Y
be computable metric spaces. Then

\mathsf{Mod}:{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)\times X\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}},(f,x)\mapsto\{m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}:m\mbox{ is a modulus of continuity of $f$ at $x$}\}

is computable.

We call
M:X\rightrightarrows{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}},x\mapsto\{m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}:m
is a modulus of continuity of
f
at
x\}
the

global modulus of continuity
of
f
.
Using the previous proposition we can prove the following characterization.

Theorem
\thethm

(Computability relative to the halting problem)
.

Let
X,Y
be computable metric spaces and let
f:X\to Y
be a function. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

f
is computable relative to the halting problem,

(2)

f
is limit computable and continuous and its global modulus of continuity is computable relative to the halting problem.

Proof.

Let
f:X\to Y
be computable relative to the halting problem. Then
f:X^{\prime}\to Y^{\prime}
is computable by Theorem
4
,
and hence
f
is computable as a point in
{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)^{\prime}
by Theorem
4
. In particular,
f
is also limit computable (see Corollary
6
).
By Theorem
4
and Proposition
4
the problem
\mathsf{Mod}
from Proposition
6
is computable with type
\mathsf{Mod}:{\mathcal{C}}(X,Y)^{\prime}\times X^{\prime}\rightrightarrows({\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}})^{\prime}
,
and hence the global modulus of
f
is computable with type
M:X^{\prime}\rightrightarrows({\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}})^{\prime},x\mapsto\mathsf{Mod}(f,x)
.
By Theorem
4
this means that
M
is computable relative to the halting problem.

Let now
f
be limit computable and continuous with a global modulus of continuity
M
that is computable relative to the halting problem.
We consider the computable metric spaces
(X,d_{X},\alpha)
and
(Y,d_{Y})
.
If
f
is limit computable, then also
f\circ\alpha:{\mathbb{N}}\to Y
is limit computable and hence computable relative to the halting problem by Corollary
4
.
Given
x\in X
and
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we can compute with the help of the halting problem a modulus of continuity
m:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}
of

at
x
, and we can compute an
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
d_{X}(\alpha(n),x)<2^{-m(k)}
, which implies
d_{Y}(f\alpha(n),f(x))<2^{-k}
.
Since
f\alpha(n)
can be computed with the help of the halting problem, we have found an approximation of
f(x)
with precision
2^{-k}
with the help of the halting problem.
This means that
f
is computable relative to the halting problem.
∎

This result tells us exactly which additional condition beyond continuity a limit computable function
f:X\to Y
must satisfy in order to be computable relative to the halting problem.
If a function is limit computable and just continuous on metric spaces, then
a hyperarithmetical oracle is sufficient to compute it.
In fact, we can prove an even stronger result.

Proposition
\thethm
.

There exists a uniformly continuous function
f:2^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}

Proof.

We define

f(p):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2^{-n}&\mbox{if $0^{n}1^{|W_{n}|+1}0\sqsubseteq p$ for some $n\in{\mathsf{{Fin}}}$}\\

We use a fixed computable enumeration

The following algorithm shows that
f

(1)

We start producing the default output

(2)

As soon as a prefix of the input

k

2^{-n}
,
otherwise by
0
.

This algorithm describes a computation with finitely many mind changes, since for any fixed input
p
,
the output value changes at most two times.
In particular,
f
is low and limit computable.
Let us assume that
f
is computable relative to the halting problem. Then by Corollary
5

there exists a computable sequence
(f_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of computable functions

Since the sequence
(K_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

is a computable sequence of computably compact sets,
it follows that

]

Since the maximum is computable on computably compact sets by

it follows that

is a computable sequence, and it converges
for each

s_{n}:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\max f_{k}(K_{n})=\max f(0^{n}12^{\mathbb{N}})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2^{-n}&\mbox{if $n\in{\mathsf{{Fin}}}$}\\

But this would imply that

We can transfer this construction from Cantor space to the unit interval

We use the computable “triangle” function

\Delta:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}},x\mapsto\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1-|x|&\mbox{if $-1\leq x\leq 1$}\\

f(-1)=f(1)=0
and
f(0)=1
and interpolates linearly between them otherwise.
Outside of the interval
(-1,1)
the function
f
is identically zero.
We define a computable double sequence
(\Delta_{n,k})_{n,k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of triangle functions

\Delta_{n,k}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
scaled to the interval
I_{n,k}:=(2^{-n-1},2^{-n-1}+2^{-n-k-1})

\Delta_{n,k}(x):=\Delta(2^{n+k+2}(x-2^{-n-1})-1)

The function

With growing

is compressed further on the left hand side of
K_{n}
.
We define a continuous function
f:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}

Similarly as in the proof of Proposition

is computable with finitely many mind changes
and not computable relative to the halting problem. For the latter part one considers the values

s_{n}:=\max f(K_{n})

(1)

As long as we cannot exclude that

(2)

As soon as we detect that

.

(3)

Whenever we find a new number

as output.

At any point
x
at most a finite number of mind changes is required since either the value
k
stabilizes or
\Delta_{n,k}(x)=0

for all sufficiently large
k
.
∎

We note that Proposition
6
cannot be strengthened such that the function
f
is additionally continuously differentiable
(see Corollary
6
). However, this can be achieved by using smooth versions of the triangle function
\Delta

that are distributed all over the entire real line.

Proposition
\thethm
.

There exists a function
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
that is infinitely often differentiable, computable with finitely many mind changes and hence low and limit computable,
but that is not computable relative to the halting problem.

Proof.

We use the computable “bump” function
\Delta:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}

\Delta(x):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e^{\frac{1}{1-x^{2}}}&\mbox{if $|x|<1$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.,

which is infinitely often continuously differentiable, computable and zero outside of
(-1,1)
.
We define a computable double sequence
(\Delta_{n,k})_{n,k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of bump functions

\Delta_{n,k}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
scaled to the interval
I_{n,k}:=(n,n+2^{-k-1})
by

\Delta_{n,k}(x):=\Delta(2^{k+2}(x-n)-1)

The function
\Delta_{n,k}
is zero outside of
K_{n}:=[n,n+1]
, in fact even outside of the interval

I_{n,k}
.
With growing
k
the triangle of
\Delta_{n,k}

is compressed further on the left hand side of
K_{n}
.
We define
f:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

f(x):=\sum_{n\in{\mathsf{{Fin}}}}\Delta_{n,|W_{n}|}(x).

Similarly as in the proof of Proposition
6
one can show that
f
is computable with finitely many mind changes
and not computable relative to the halting problem. Additionally,
f
is infinitely often differentiable, since the
\Delta_{n,k}
are so.
∎

We can also produce a similar counterexample of type
f:2^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{S}}
, where

{\mathbb{S}}
denotes
Sierpiński space

{\mathbb{S}}=\{0,1\}
that is represented by
\delta_{\mathbb{S}}(p)=0:\iff p=\widehat{0}
.

Proposition
\thethm
.

There exists a continuous function
f:2^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{S}}
that is computable with finitely many mind changes and hence low and limit computable, but that is not computable relative to the halting problem.

Proof.

We define
f:2^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{S}}
by

f(p):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{if $p=\widehat{0}$}\\
0&\mbox{if $0^{n}1^{|W_{n}|+1}0\sqsubseteq p$ for some $n\in{\mathsf{{Fin}}}$}\\
1&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right..

The sets
A_{n}:=0^{n}1^{|W_{n}|+1}02^{\mathbb{N}}
are clopen for every
n\in{\mathsf{{Fin}}}
and
the set
A:=\{\widehat{0}\}\cup\bigcup_{n\in{\mathsf{{Fin}}}}A_{n}
is closed.
Hence
f
is the characteristic function
\chi_{U}
of the open set
U=2^{\mathbb{N}}\setminus A
and hence continuous.
The following algorithm shows that
f
is computable with finitely many mind changes:

(1)

We start producing the default output
0
as long as no other value is determined in the next step.

(2)

As soon as a prefix of the input
p
of form
0^{n}1^{k+1}0^{m+1}
is known, we check if there are exactly
k

different values among the numbers

e_{n}(0),...,e_{n}(m)
. If so, then the output is replaced by
0
,
otherwise by
1
.

This algorithm describes a computation with finitely many mind changes, since for any fixed input
p
,
the output value changes at most three times.
In particular,
f
is limit computable.

Let us assume that
f=\chi_{U}
is also computable relative to the halting problem.
We note that the map
{\mathcal{O}}(2^{\mathbb{N}})\to{\mathcal{C}}(2^{\mathbb{N}},{\mathbb{S}}),V\mapsto\chi_{V}
is a computable isomorphism,
and hence
U
is a computable point in
{\mathcal{O}}(2^{\mathbb{N}})^{\prime}
.
The map
\forall_{K}:{\mathcal{O}}(2^{\mathbb{N}})\to{\mathbb{S}}
with
\forall_{K}(V)=1\iff K\subseteq V
is computable for every
computably compact set
K\subseteq 2^{\mathbb{N}}
, and the map
K\mapsto\forall_{K}
is a computable isomorphism
between the space of compact subsets of
2^{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\mathcal{C}}({\mathcal{O}}(2^{\mathbb{N}}),{\mathbb{S}})

[
37
]
.
Since
(K_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with
K_{n}:=0^{n}12^{\mathbb{N}}
is a computable sequence
of computably compact sets, we obtain that
\forall_{K_{n}}
is computable uniformly in
n
.
Hence
n\mapsto\forall_{K_{n}}(U)
is computable relatively to the halting problem by Corollary
4
.
This is a contradiction since

\forall_{K_{n}}(U)=1\iff 0^{n}12^{\mathbb{N}}\subseteq U\iff n\in{\mathsf{{Inf}}}

and
{\mathsf{{Inf}}}
is not c.e. relative to the halting problem.
∎

Since a set
U\subseteq 2^{\mathbb{N}}
is c.e. open (relative to some oracle
q
) if and only if
\chi_{U}:2^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{S}}
is computable (relative to
q
) and
F\subseteq 2^{\mathbb{N}}

is a
computable
{\Sigma^{0}_{2}}
–set
in the Borel hierarchy (or, equivalently, a
computable

–set
) if and only
if
\chi_{F}:2^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{S}}^{\prime}
is computable
[
38

,
the previous proposition can also be rephrased in the following way.

Corollary
\thethm
.

There is a computable
{\Sigma^{0}_{2}}
–set
U\subseteq 2^{\mathbb{N}}
in the Borel hierarchy that is open, but not c.e. open relative to the halting problem.

A similar result for
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
was proved by Ziegler
[
54

.
There is even a much stronger counter example for
2^{\mathbb{N}}
.
There is a
{\Pi^{0}_{2}}
–set

that is a singleton (its only member being the

such that this set is closed but not a

–set relative to

for any

(see
[

[

Proposition
\thethm

(Kuznécov and Trahténbrot 1955)
.

There is a
{\Sigma^{0}_{2}}
–set
A\subseteq 2^{\mathbb{N}}
with a singleton complement that is not c.e. open relative to

for any
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Further results along these lines can be found in
[
14

.
If we re-translate this back to functions, then we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary
\thethm
.

There is a continuous and limit computable

that is not computable relative to
0^{(n)}
for any

.

This also shows that Corollary
6
cannot be generalized to arbitrary spaces
Y

and that the function

cannot have a realizer
F:2^{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}^{\mathbb{N}}

that is simultaneously continuous and limit computable, even though it has separate realizers with each of these

There are some classes of functions for which the condition on the modulus of continuity in Theorem
6
is
automatically satisfied. The first example is the class of Hölder continuous functions.
Let
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
be metric spaces. Then

is called

with constant

and exponent

d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))\leq Ld_{X}(x,y)^{\alpha}

holds for all
x,y\in X
.
A function is called
Lipschitz continuous
with constant
L>0
if it is
Hölder continuous with constant
L
and exponent
\alpha=1
.
Since every Hölder continuous function automatically has a computable constant
L
(perhaps slightly larger than necessary) and a computable exponent
\alpha
(perhaps slightly smaller than necessary and correct at least for small distances

),
it follows that it automatically has a computable global modulus of continuity.
Hence we obtain the following corollary of Theorem

.

Corollary
\thethm

(Hölder continuity)
.

Let

and

be computable metric spaces, and let

Then

is limit computable if and only if
f
is computable relative to the halting problem.

Restricted to the set of Hölder continuous functions with a fixed Lipschitz constant

and a fixed exponent

the map in Corollary

is even a computable
isomorphism, as the proof of “(2)

is uniform.
We note that even restricted to Lipschitz continuous functions the notions of a low function
and a function that is computable relative to a low oracle are incomparable by Proposition

We can also draw some conclusions on linear operators.
We recall that a computable normed space is just a separable normed
space such that the induced metric space is computable and makes the algebraic operations computable.
By the Borel graph theorem of Schwartz
[
45
]
(see also
[

) every Borel measurable
linear operator
T:X\to Y
from a Banach space
X
to a normed space

is continuous, i.e., bounded.
Using Theorem
6
we obtain the following
conclusion on effectively Borel measurable operators.

Corollary
\thethm

(Borel measurable linear functions)
.

Let
X
be a computable Banach space, let
Y
be computable normed spaces, and let
T:X\to Y
be linear. Then
T
is effectively Borel measurable if and only if
it is computable relative to a hyperarithmetical oracle (i.e., a
\Delta^{1}_{1}
oracle).

Every linear bounded operator is automatically Lipschitz continuous.
In the special case of limit computability Corollary
6
yields the following.

Corollary
\thethm

(Limit computable linear functions)
.

Let
X
be a computable Banach space,
Y
be computable normed spaces, and let
T:X\to Y
be linear.
Then
T
is limit computable if and only if
T
is computable relative to the halting problem.

7.
Applications

In this section we discuss a number of simple applications of the tools that we have
developed in the previous sections. Some of the results that we derive are known,
but our techniques provide very simple proofs. Other results are new.

We start with considering distance functions.
For every subset
A\subseteq X
of a metric space
(X,d)
we define the
distance function

d_{A}:X\to{\mathbb{R}},x\mapsto\inf_{y\in A}d(x,y)
.
Every distance function
d_{A}
satisfies
|d_{A}(x)-d_{A}(y)|\leq d(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
, which means that every distance function
is Lipschitz continuous with Lipschitz constant
1
.

Corollary
\thethm

(Distance functions)
.

Let
X
be a computable metric space and
A\subseteq X
.
The distance function
d_{A}:X\to{\mathbb{R}}
is limit computable if and only if it is computable relative to the halting problem.

We recall that a problem
f:X\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called
lower semi-computable
if it is computable as a function
f:X\to{\mathbb{R}}_{<}

with the real numbers
{\mathbb{R}}_{<}
equipped with the lower Dedekind cut representation, and likewise
f
is called
upper semi-computable

if it is computable as a function
f:X\to{\mathbb{R}}_{>}
, where the real numbers
{\mathbb{R}}_{>}
are equipped with the upper Dedekind cut representation.
Since it is clear that
{\mathrm{id}}:{\mathbb{R}}_{<}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
{\mathrm{id}}:{\mathbb{R}}_{>}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are limit computable
[
8
, Proposition 3.7]
, we obtain the following conclusion.

Corollary
\thethm

(Semi-computable functions)
.

Every lower or upper semi-computable function
f:X\to{\mathbb{R}}
is limit computable.

By
f^{\circ n}
we denote the
n
–fold composition of
f
with itself, i.e.,
f^{\circ 0}={\mathrm{id}}
,
f^{\circ 1}=f
,
f^{\circ 2}=f\circ f
, etc.
It is easy to see that the
Mandelbrot set

M:=\{c\in{\mathbb{C}}:(\forall n\in{\mathbb{N}})\;|f^{\circ n}_{c}(0)|\leq 2\}
, which is defined using
the iteration function
f_{c}:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}},z\mapsto z^{2}+c
, is co-c.e. closed (see
[
52
, Exercise 5.1.32 (c)]
and
[
24
]
),
and hence its distance function
d_{M}:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is lower semi-computable
[
13
, Corollary 3.14 (2)]
.
As a corollary of Theorem
6
and Corollaries
7
and
7
we obtain the following.

Corollary
\thethm

(Mandelbrot set)
.

The distance function
d_{M}:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
of the Mandelbrot set
M\subseteq{\mathbb{C}}
is computable relative to the halting problem.

Another application concerns the field of limit computable real numbers. By
({\mathbb{R}}^{(n)})_{\rm c}
we denote the set of points that are computable in
{\mathbb{R}}^{(n)}
, i.e., computable with respect to the
n
–th jump of the Cauchy representation.
We recall that a subfield of the reals is called
real algebraically closed
if the real-valued zeros of all non-constant polynomials
with coefficients from the subfield are again in the subfield.

Proposition
\thethm

(Field of real numbers)
.

The real numbers in
({\mathbb{R}}^{(n)})_{\rm c}
form a real algebraically closed subfield
of
{\mathbb{R}}
for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

Proof.

We note the following facts for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
a,b\in{\mathbb{Q}}
with
a<b
(see
[
52
]
):

(1)

The algebraic operations
+,-,\cdot:{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are computable.

(2)

Division
\div:\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is computable.

(3)

The constants
0,1
are computable real numbers.

(4)

The map
p:{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}\to{\mathcal{C}}[a,b],(a_{0},...,a_{n})\mapsto(x\mapsto\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}x^{i})
is computable.

(5)

There is a computable
Z_{[a,b]}:\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}[a,b]\to{\mathbb{R}}

such that
f(Z_{[a,b]}(f))=0
for every continuous
f:[a,b]\to{\mathbb{R}}
that has exactly one zero (see
[
52
, Corollary 6.3.5]
, where this was proved for
[a,b]=[0,1]
; it is straightforward to generalize the proof).

It follows from (1)–(3) that
{\mathbb{R}}_{\rm c}
is a computable subfield of
{\mathbb{R}}
and from (4) and (5) that this field is real algebraically closed
as the zeros of all non-constant polynomials are isolated and can be computed if the coefficients are all computable.
If we apply Proposition
4
, then we obtain that all the operations listed in (1)–(5) are also computable
when all spaces are replaced by their jumps.
This implies that
({\mathbb{R}}^{\prime})_{\rm c}
is also a real algebraically closed subfield of the real numbers.
Inductively, this property can be transferred to higher jumps.
∎

Zheng and Weihrauch
[
53
, Proposition 7.6]
also proved that
({\mathbb{R}}^{(n)})_{\rm c}
is a subfield of the reals.
The fact that
{\mathbb{R}}_{\rm c}
is a real algebraically closed field was proved by Rice
[
41
]
and Grzegorczyk
[
23
]
.
Freund and Staiger
[
20
]
proved that
({\mathbb{R}}^{\prime})_{\rm c}
is a real algebraically closed field.

Finally, we want to discuss some simple applications of our methods to differentiable functions.
By
f^{\prime}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
we denote the
derivative
of a differentiable function
f:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
With very little effort we obtain the following result.

Proposition
\thethm

(Operator of differentiation)
.

The following operation is computable:

d:\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathcal{C}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}^{\prime}),f\mapsto f^{\prime}
with

{\mathrm{dom}}(d):=\{f:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}:f
differentiable
\}
.

Proof.

If
f:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
is differentiable, then

f^{\prime}(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(x+(1-x)2^{-n})-f(x-x2^{-n})}{2^{-n}}

Using evaluation, currying and Theorem
5
we obtain that
d
is computable.
∎

Using Theorem
5
we obtain the following result
of Kuyper and Terwijn
[
30
, Theorem 4.3]
.

Corollary
\thethm

(Kuyper and Terwijn 2014)
.

The derivative
f^{\prime}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
of every differentiable computable function
f:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}

is limit computable and continuous at all
1
–generic points
x\in[0,1]
.

In fact, Kuyper and Terwijn obtained an even stronger result that characterizes
1
–generics in terms
of derivatives
[
30
, Theorem 5.2]
. Our point here is not to strengthen these results, but
to illustrate the applicability of the tools that we have provided in this article.

If
f:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
is even continuously differentiable then the limit in the proof of Proposition
7

can be seen to be a uniform limit. This leads to the following result of von Stein
[
49
]
.
By
{\mathcal{C}}^{1}
we denote the
set of continuously differentiable
functions
f:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proposition
\thethm

(von Stein 1989)
.

d|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{1}}:\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathcal{C}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}})^{\prime},f\mapsto f^{\prime}
is computable.

Proof.

Let
f:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
be continuously differentiable. Then
F:[0,1]^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, defined by

F(x,y):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f^{\prime}(x)-\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{x-y}&\mbox{if $x\not=y$}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

is continuous, and since
[0,1]^{2}
is compact,
F
is even uniformly continuous.
Let
\varepsilon>0
. Since
F
vanishes on the diagonal,
there exists
\delta>0
such that
|x-y|<\delta\Longrightarrow|F(x,y)|<\varepsilon
for all
x,y\in[0,1]^{2}
.
The values
x_{n}:=x+(1-x)2^{-n}
and
y_{n}:=x-x2^{-n}
satisfy
|x_{n}-y_{n}|=2^{-n}
.
Hence there is a
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
|x_{n}-y_{n}|<\delta
for all
n\geq k
.
Altogether, this shows that
f_{n}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
with

f_{n}(x)=\frac{f(x+(1-x)2^{-n})-f(x-x2^{-n})}{2^{-n}}.

satisfies
||f_{n}-f^{\prime}||<\varepsilon
for
n\geq k
.
Hence, we have the uniform limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{n}=f^{\prime}
.
The sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathcal{C}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}})
can be computed from
f
using evaluation and currying.
It follows that
d|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{1}}
is computable in the given way by Theorem
5
.
∎

This result can also be phrased such that
d|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{1}}:\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}})\to{\mathcal{C}}([0,1],{\mathbb{R}}),f\mapsto f^{\prime}
is limit computable.
In fact, von Stein even proved that
d|_{{\mathcal{C}}^{1}}
with this type is Weihrauch equivalent to
\lim
, which yields for instance
Myhill’s result
[
34
]
that there is a continuously differentiable computable function
f:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
with
a non-computable derivative
f^{\prime}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
(see
[
4
]
).
Here we rather aim for the following corollary that was originally proved by Ho (announced in
[
25
, Theorem 1.3]
and
proved in
[
26
, Theorem 19]
).

Corollary
\thethm

(Ho 1999)
.

The derivative
f^{\prime}:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
of every continuously differentiable computable function
f:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}

is computable relative to the halting problem.

With the following proposition we generalize the observation
[
10
, Corollary 9.10 (2)]
to
all computable metric spaces.

Proposition
\thethm

(C.e. comeager sets)
.

Let
X
be a computable metric space and let
(A_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a computable sequence
of co-c.e. closed subsets
A_{n}\subseteq X
that are all nowhere dense. Then

A:=X\setminus\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}A_{n}
is a comeager set that contains all

1
–generic points
x\in X
.

Proof.

If the
A_{n}\subseteq X
are nowhere dense (have non-empty interior) then
A_{n}=\partial A_{n}=\partial A_{n}^{\rm c}
. Hence

X\setminus\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\partial U_{n}\subseteq X\setminus\bigcup_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\partial A_{n}^{\rm c}=A
.
∎

We can also consider
1
–generic points in the space
{\mathcal{C}}[0,1]
of continuous functions.
In
[
5
]
it was proved that the set of somewhere differentiable functions is included in

\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}D_{n}
, where
(D_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a computable sequence of nowhere dense co-c.e. subsets
D_{n}\subseteq{\mathcal{C}}[0,1]
, defined by

D_{n}:=\left\{f\in{\mathcal{C}}[0,1]:(\exists t\in[0,1])(\forall h\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\})\left|\frac{f(t+h)-f(t)}{h}\right|\leq n\right\}
.
Hence, we obtain the following observation.

Corollary
\thethm

(Nowhere differentiability of
1
–generic functions)
.

Every continuous function
f:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
that is
1
–generic as a point in
{\mathcal{C}}[0,1]

is nowhere differentiable.

The mere property that every
1
–generic continuous
f:[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}
is not differentiable could also be deduced from
the fact that the operator of differentiation
d
is not continuous at any point
f
.

These examples just serve as illustrations that a careful analysis of the notions of limit computability and computability
with respect to the halting problem is useful for applications in analysis. In particular the concepts based on the
Galois connection between limit and Turing jumps yield
very transparent and simple proofs.
