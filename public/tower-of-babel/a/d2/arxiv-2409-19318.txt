Fairness Analysis with Shapley-Owen Effects
H arald Ruess

Abstract
We argue that relative importance and its equitable attribution in terms of Shapley-Owen effects is an appropriate one, and, if we accept a small number of reasonable imperatives for equitable attribution, the only way to measure fairness. On the other hand, the computation of Shapley-Owen effects can be very demanding. Our main technical result is a spectral decomposition of the Shapley-Owen effects, which decomposes the computation of these indices into a model-specific and a model-independent part. The model-independent part is precomputed once and for all, and the model-specific computation of Shapley-Owen effects is expressed analytically in terms of the coefficients of the model’s polynomial chaos expansion (PCE), which can now be reused to compute different Shapley-Owen effects. We also propose an algorithm for computing precise and sparse truncations of the PCE of the model and the spectral decomposition of the Shapley-Owen effects, together with upper bounds on the accumulated approximation errors. The approximations of both the PCE and the Shapley-Owen effects converge to their true values.

Introduction

Machines are widely used to make automated decisions about people, and it is important that these decisions be
fair
,
that is, without prejudice or favoritism toward any individual or group based on their inherent or acquired characteristics
Cheng et al. (2021)
.
Suppose a bank lends money to individuals based on certain characteristics, such as credit score, age group, zip code, and so on
Liu et al. (2018)
.
The bank wants to maximize its profit by lending money only to those who will repay the loan on time. There are sensitive characteristics such as ethnicity or gender that divide the population into groups.
The bank is considered fair (in lending money) if its
lending policy is largely agnostic with respect to
sensitive characteristics.
A canonical example of
unfair
algorithmic decision making comes from a software tool
to make pretrial detention and release decisions
that has been shown to be highly
racially biased
Angwin et al. (2022)
.
Similar real-world examples of algorithmic unfairness, along with an analysis of the root causes, have been analyzed in the context of machine learning
Mehrabi et al. (2021)
, since existing bias in real-world data is amplified by machine-learned algorithms.

However, a decision can appear fair if it is judged on the basis of individual characteristics without considering their interactions
Buolamwini and Gebru (2018)
.
Crenshaw
Crenshaw (2013)
, for example,
analyzes the case of a discrimination suit against black women that was dismissed because the plaintiff had hired women before the Civil Rights Act was passed - albeit white women - during the period when no black women were hired.
Crenshaw
Crenshaw (2013)
concludes
that a Black woman’s experience of discrimination differs from that of both women and Black people in general
Crenshaw (2013)
,
and the interaction along multiple dimensions of identity produces unique and different levels of discrimination for different subgroups.
The sum of human experiences of discrimination, in line with Simpson’s paradox
Blyth (1972)
,
must take into account the interactions between different characteristics and cannot be limited to considerations of fairness along individual characteristics alone
Gohar and Cheng (2023)
.

We are interested in measuring the "fairness" of the response of algorithmic decision making
with respect to certain sensitive subsets of inputs.
Fairness constraints are specified by (1) identifying sensitive subsets of input attributes, and (2) determining bounds on the
relative importance
of these subsets to the decision.
Of course, any such specification of
fairness
depends heavily on what is considered to be
acceptable
in a given social context.

Our proposal is a measure of fairness in terms of the
relative importance
of some inputs to the judgment of the decision function.
For example, if
gender
is a sensible attribute of negligible relative importance in a credit decision, then the bank’s decision could be said to be fair with respect to gender.
Similarly, fairness constraints are provided for sensitive subsets such as
\{\mathit{gender},\mathit{ethnicity}\}
.
We postulate that the variance of the expected output, conditioned on the sensitive inputs, is a natural measure of
relative importance
.
In this way, we can also specify a larger class of fairness constraints in terms of ratios and differences in the relative importance of different sensitive inputs,
including
disparate impact

Feldman et al. (2015)

and
differential fairness

Dwork et al. (2012)
.
The US Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, for example, advocates

disparate impact
in the sense
that the ratio of the chances of being hired if the sensitive trait is met to the chances of being hired if the sensitive trait is not met shall be bounded from above by
0.8
.

US Equal Employment Opportunity Commission.
Uniform guidelines on employee selection procedures
, March 2, 1979.

Relative importance, when normalized by the variance of the decision function, is consistent with the notion
of a
Sobol’ index
for subsets of inputs.
In
global sensitivity analysis
,
these indices are useful for quantifying which
inputs most influence response variability.
However, Sobol’ indices are difficult to interpret in the presence of statistical dependence between inputs
Iooss and Prieur (2019)
.

We are thus interested in an
importance attribution
of the relative importance of input characteristics such that
(1) the variance of the decision function is distributed among, and thus explained by, the attributed relative importance of all of the inputs,
(2) inputs of equal relative importance receive equal attribution,
(3) inputs with a zero marginal contribution to the relative importance of all supersets receive a zero attribution,
and (4)
each pair of inputs should share equally in the gain in relative importance for all possible shared interactions.
Since
Shapley values

Shapley et al. (1953)
;
Winter (2002)

are the only attribution that satisfies these constraints,
the scenario is as follows.
We set up a
cooperation game

Osborne and Rubinstein (1994)
;
Peleg and Sudhölter (2007)
;
Chalkiadakis et al. (2022)
with the inputs as the players of the game and
subsets of the inputs as possible
coalitions
of players.
The value of each coalition is measured by its relative importance to the variation in expected output.
Now, the Shapley value assigns the mutual contribution – due to correlation and interaction – of coalitions to each individual input within the coalition in a manner that is consistent with the above imperatives (1)-(4).

Shapley values for relative importance games are also called Shapley effects
Owen (2014)
;
Song et al. (2016)
.
A generalization of Shapley effects to the
Shapley-Owen
Owen (1972)
;
Owen (2014)
interaction effects attributes a fair share of relative importance not only to a single input but, more generally, to all possible subsets of the inputs.
Using this index, it is now also possible to gain insight into the
synergistic
or
antagonistic
nature of interactions within subsets of intputs.
For example, the Shapley-Owen effect for
\{\mathit{Gender},\mathit{Ethnicity}\}
attributes its marginal contribution to the relative importance of all its supersets.

In practice, we may not be able to determine the exact Shapley and Shapley-Owen interaction indices because there is an exponential number of possible coalitions of inputs, and computing Shapley values is already hard for some simple games
Deng and Papadimitriou (1994)
;
Conitzer and Sandholm (2006)
.
However, several algorithms have been proposed to approximate Shapley values
Mann and Shapley (1962)
;
Owen (1972)
;
Bachrach et al. (2010)
;
Fatima et al. (2008)
;
Maleki et al. (2013)
by considering only a
sample of coalition values, thus avoiding the need to consider an exponential number of such values.
Shapley effects can also be estimated
Song et al. (2016)
;
Broto et al. (2020a)
,
and Shapley effects are easily computable in
some special cases
Owen and Prieur (2017)
;
Iooss and Prieur (2019)
;
Broto et al. (2019)
;
Broto et al. (2020a)
;
Broto et al. (2020b)
.

Here we follow a different path in that we are separating
the computation of Shapley-Owen effects into model-independent and model-dependent computations.
Hereby, the model-independent computations are considered to be pre-computed, once-and-forall, and the model-dependent computation reduces to the construction of a sufficiently precise
polynomial chaos expansion
(PCE),
which relies on the
spectral decomposition
of the decision function.
In other words, Shapley-Owen effects are expressed analytically in terms of the PCE coefficients of the underlying decision model.
This PCE can be reused for different sensitivity analyses as long as the underlying decision function and the input distribution are unchanged.

This paper is structured as follows.
Section
2
reviews some basic mathematical notation and concepts.
Section
3
motivates and formalizes the central notion of
relative importance
as the basis for specifying fairness constraints. We also argue that the related notion of Sobol’ sensitivity indices are not sufficient for fairness analysis in the important case of dependent inputs.
Instead, Section
4
proposes Shapley-Owen effects, because of their "fair" attribution of relative importance, as adequate measures for specifying fairness constraints.
In order to make this paper as self-contained as possible we review basic concepts of PCE in Section
5
.
Based on these development we are proposing a simple algorithm for constructing precise and sparse truncations of PCE with known truncation errors.
Section
6
contains our main result, which is a spectral decomposition of Shapley-Owen effects based on the PCE of the underlying decision function.
We put these developments into perspective and compare our results with related work in Section
7
.
Finally, Section
8
concludes with some final remarks.

4
Shapley-Owen Effects and Fairness

Shapley effects overcome these conceptual problems of Sobol’ indices by attributing the mutual contribution - due to correlation and interaction - of a subset of inputs to each individual input within that subset
Owen (2014)
;
Song et al. (2016)
;
Owen and Prieur (2017)
.
Like ANOVA, they use variances, but unlike ANOVA for dependent data, a Shapley effect never goes negative, and it can be defined without making onerous assumptions about the input distribution.

4.1
Shapley-Owen Effects

4.3
Fair Attribution

The Shapley value is an instance of a larger class of

probabilistic values

Weber (1988)
,
but it is the only one satisfying the following four conditions
Shapley et al. (1953)
for all
possible values
\mathit{val}
:

1.

(Efficiency)
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathit{Sh}_{i}(\mathit{val})=\mathit{val}({[1,d]})
.

2.

(Symmetry) If
\mathit{val}({u+i})=\mathit{val}({u+j})
for all
u\subseteq\sim{\{i,j\}}
then
\mathit{Sh}_{i}(\mathit{val})=\mathit{Sh}_{j}(\mathit{val})
.

3.

(Dummy) If
\mathit{val}({u+i})=\mathit{val}({u})
for all
u\subseteq[1,d]
then
\mathit{Sh}_{i}(\mathit{val})=0
.

4.

(Additivity)
\mathit{Sh}_{i}(\mathit{val}_{1}+\mathit{val}_{2})=\mathit{Sh}_{i}(\mathit{val}_{1})+\mathit{Sh}_{i}(\mathit{val}_{2})
for all
i\in[1,d]
,

where the value
(\mathit{val}_{1}+\mathit{val}_{2})(u)
adds
the two values
\mathit{val}_{1}(u)
and
\mathit{val}_{2}(u)
.
Additivity also implies that multiplying
\mathit{val}
by a scalar multiplies all of the Shapley values by that same scalar; that is:
\mathit{Sh}_{i}(c\cdot\mathit{val})=c\cdot\mathit{Sh}_{i}(\mathit{val})
.
Informally,
efficiency
is interpreted as stating that the variance of the outcome can be explained in terms of attributed relative importance of each input.
Moreover, the
dummy
property states that attributions to inputs that do not add to the relative importance of other inputs should have attributed zero importance,
whereas
symmetry
demands that
two inputs who contribute equally to the relative importance of other inputs should be indistinguishable in that they have identical attribution.
Alternatives to Shapley’s original axiomatization
have been developed
Myerson (1980)
;
Hart and Mas-Colell (1989)
;
Young (1985)
.
For example, the axioms (Dummy), (Symmetry), and (Additivity) can be replaced by the equivalent single

(Balanced Contributions)
axiom
Myerson (1980)
;
Hart and Mas-Colell (1989)
:

\displaystyle\mathit{Sh}_{i}(\mathit{val})-\mathit{Sh}_{i}({\mathit{val}}\!\!\restriction_{\sim{j}})

\displaystyle=\mathit{Sh}_{j}(\mathit{val})-\mathit{Sh}_{j}({\mathit{val}}\!\!\restriction_{\sim{i}})\mbox{{},}

(13)

where
{\mathit{val}}\!\!\restriction_{\sim{i}}
is the restriction of the
game
\mathit{val}
to the inputs
X_{\sim{i}}
.
Balanced contribution is a principle
of fairness in cooperation, as it states that every pair of players should share equally in the gain of their cooperation.

More generally, Shapley-Owen values are based on game-theoretic axioms that differ slightly from the ones of the
Shapley value
Grabisch and Roubens (1999)
.

1.

(Additivity)
\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\mathit{val}_{1}+\mathit{val}_{2})=\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\mathit{val}_{1})+\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\mathit{val}_{2})
for all
u\subseteq[1,d]
.

2.

(Dummy) If
\mathit{val}({u+i})=\mathit{val}({u})
for all
u\subseteq\sim{i}
then
\mathit{Sh}_{i}(\mathit{val})=\mathit{val}({i})
and

\mathit{Sh}_{v+i}(\mathit{val})=0
for every
\emptyset\neq v\subseteq\sim{i}
.

3.

(Symmetry)
For all games
\mathit{val}
and permutations

\sigma\in\Pi([1,d])
:

\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\mathit{val})=\mathit{Sh}_{\sigma(u)}(\mathit{val}_{\sigma})
,
where the permutation game
\mathit{val}_{\sigma}
maps the direct image
\sigma(u)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\{\sigma(i)\,|\,i\in u\}
to

\mathit{val}({u})
.

4.

(Recursivity)
For every
\mathit{val}
,
any
u\subseteq[1,d]
such that
|u|>1
,
any
i\in u
,
and the games
M_{i}(v)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\mathit{val}({v\cup\{i\}})-\mathit{val}({v})
:

\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\mathit{val})=\mathit{Sh}_{u\setminus\{i\}}(M_{i})-\mathit{Sh}_{u\setminus\{i\}}({\mathit{val}}\!\!\restriction_{\sim{i}})
.

The Shapley-Owen interaction index (
9
) satisfies these axioms, and it is unique in that it is the only such attribution which restricts to singleton Shapley values
Grabisch and Roubens (1999)
.
Recursivity says that the interaction
of the players in
u
is equal to the interaction between the players in
u\setminus\{j\}
in
the omnipresence of
j
,
minus the interaction between the players of

u\setminus\{j\}
in the absence of
j
.
Overall, if we accept these "fairness" conditions on attributions, then variance-based Shapley-Owen effects are the only possible "fair" attribution of the relative importance of inputs and their interactions.

corresponds, for
q=1
, exactly to the total degree of
\alpha
.
For
q<1
, it includes all the univariate high-degree terms, but excludes high-degree terms with many interacting variables.
The construction of sparse and sufficiently
accurate PCE approximation is an art in itself

;
Dai and Milenkovic (2009)
;
Blatman (2009)
;
Blatman and Sudret (2010)
;
Blatman and Sudret (2011)

.
For example, sparse PCEs are constructed in the Bayesian framework using the Kashyab information criterion for model selection

.

5.4
Sparse PCE

The identity (
24
) on the
truncation error together with the
sparsity-of-effects
hypothesis
suggests a simple approximation algorithm with input parameters
q\in\mathbb{R}
and approximation bound
\epsilon>0
.
The algorithm starts with the initial approximation

\widehat{M}_{0}(X)\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}y_{0}(=\mathbb{E}({\mathcal{M}(X)}))
,
and iteratively adds new PCE terms
y_{\alpha}\Psi_{\alpha}(X)
such that
\alpha
satisfies the
q
-norm in the
p
-th but not in the
(p-1)
-th iteration step.
The algorithm stops when the norm of the error terms falls below
\epsilon
according to Proposition
5.1
.
This iteration terminates with
\lVert{\mathcal{M}(X)-\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{l}(X)}\rVert_{f}<\epsilon
, where
l
is the number of steps in the iteration.
Alternatively, the probabilistic stoppage criterion in Proposition
5.2
can be used, in which case we get a confidence interval for the error.
Now, the sparsity of the PCE approximation
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{l}(X)
is obtained by
adding the terms
y_{\alpha}\Psi_{\alpha}(X)
to the current PCE approximation
only if
y_{\alpha}
is sufficiently large.
Otherwise,
y_{\alpha}
is added to a
waiting
set,
and the PCE terms are chosen in every iteration as the maximum absolute value of the coefficients in the
waiting
set and the current
y_{\alpha}
.
If the maximum error
|y_{\alpha}-\widehat{y}_{\alpha}|
for the computed approximations
\widehat{y}_{\alpha}
of the PCE coefficients
y_{\alpha}
is bounded by some
\kappa>0
, then
that bound is used, for example, to decide whether
\widehat{y}_{\alpha}
is put into the

waiting
set or not.
This additional check prevents terms with a coefficient of
\widehat{y}_{\alpha}
, whose real coefficient
y_{\alpha}
is zero or close to zero, from being prematurely added to the truncated PCE.

\mathcal{M}(X)

Lemma 5.1

For the PCE

5.6
PCE for Dependent Inputs

The Rosenblatt transformation method involves mapping the original random variables
X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})
to the independent and uniformly distributed
U=(U_{1},\ldots U_{d})
on the hypercube
[-1,1]^{d}
.
A Rosenblatt
Rosenblatt (1952)
transform
\mathcal{T}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}(F_{k}(x_{k}\,|\,x_{[1,k-1]}))_{k\in[1,d]}\mbox{{},}

(34)

with
F_{k}
, for
k\in[1,d]
, the marginal cdf

F_{k}(x_{k}\,|\,x_{[1,k-1]})\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}{\mathbb{P}}({X_{k}\leq x_{k}\,|\,X_{1}=x_{1},\ldots,X_{k-1}=x_{k-1}})
.
These marginals are obtained analytically from the pdf
f
of
X
(Appendix
B
).
Note that the Rosenblatt transform is not unique, and that there are
d!
different ways to define such a transform, depending on which variable is chosen in each iteration.
Now, for
U=\mathcal{T}(X)
, the
U_{i}
are independent and uniformly distributed on
[-1,1]
.
In addition, if the inverses of the marginal cdfs exist,
then

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}^{-1}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}(F_{k}^{-1}(u_{k}))_{k\in[1,d]}

(35)

is the inverse of the Rosenblatt transform.
Therefore,
X=\mathcal{T}^{-1}(U)
and the pdf of
X
is
f
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mathcal{M}(X)
be square-integrable,

U=(U_{1},\ldots,U_{d})
be a
d
-dimensional vector of independent and uniformly distributed random variables, and
\mathcal{T}^{-1}
with
X=\mathcal{T}^{-1}(U)
.
Then the PCE of
\mathcal{M}(X)
is obtained as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(X)=(\mathcal{M}\circ\mathcal{T}^{-1})(U)=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}y_{\alpha}\widetilde{\mathit{L}_{\alpha}}(U)\mbox{{},}

(36)

where
\widetilde{\mathit{L}_{\alpha}}(U)
(
21
) is the normalized Legendre polynomial of multi-degree
\alpha
, and the coefficients
y_{\alpha}
are determined by the identity

\displaystyle y_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}({(\mathcal{M}\circ\mathcal{T}^{-1})(U)\,\widetilde{\mathit{L}_{\alpha}}(U)})\mbox{{}.}

(37)

We would get the Wiener-Hermite
Wiener (1938)
expansion if we had chosen an isoprobabilistic transformation into Gaussian instead of uniformly distributed independent variables.

6
Computing Shapley-Owen Effects

We describe the spectral decomposition of Shapley-Owen effects in terms of the PCE of the underlying decision
model
\mathcal{M}(X)
.
Let
\mathcal{T}
be a Rosenblatt transform with
U=\mathcal{T}(X)
and inverse
\mathcal{T}^{-1}
such that
U=\mathcal{T}^{-1}(X)
.
We also assume as given a PCE of
(\mathcal{M}\circ\mathcal{T}^{-1})(U)
as described in Lemma
5.2
.
The value functions of relative importance for the input vector
X
and the independently and uniformly distributed variables
U=\mathcal{T}(X)
are defined, for
u\subseteq[1,d]
as

\displaystyle\mathit{val}^{X}(u)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\mathbb{V}({\mathbb{E}({{\mathcal{M}(X)}\,|\,{X_{u}}})})

(38)

\displaystyle\mathit{val}^{U}(u)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\mathbb{V}({\mathbb{E}({{(\mathcal{M}\circ\mathcal{T}^{-1})(U)}\,|\,{U_{u}}})})\mbox{{}.}

(39)

These values are consistent with the Sobol’
indices, but only
\mathit{val}^{U}
can be decomposed with the identity (
7
), because the random variables in
U
are independent by construction.
From these definitions, Shapley-Owen effects are invariant under the inverse Rosenblatt transformation.

Lemma 6.1
.

For all
u\in[1,d]
:

\displaystyle\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\mathit{val}^{X})

\displaystyle=\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\mathit{val}^{U})\mbox{{}.}

(40)

The identity in Lemma
6.1
holds, because of the defining equality (
9
) of Shapley-Owen values and the identity

\mathbb{V}({\mathbb{E}({{\mathcal{M}(X)}\,|\,{X_{v}}})})=\mathbb{V}({\mathbb{E}({{\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{T}^{-1}(U))}\,|\,{U_{v}}})})
.
The statement in Lemma
6.1
also holds for other iso-probabilistic transforms
Lebrun and Dutfoy (2009)
.
The decision of which transform to use is problem-specific and depends strongly on the available information about the input distribution
Mara and Becker (2021)
.

Using the identities (
7
)
and (
29
),
the value
\mathit{val}^{U}(v)
is expressed in terms of the coefficients of the PCE for

\mathcal{M}(X)=(\mathcal{M}\circ\mathcal{T}^{-1})(U)
:

\displaystyle\mathit{val}^{U}(u)=\sum_{v:\,v\subseteq u}\sigma^{2}_{v}=\sum_{v:\,v\subseteq u}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\infty}y^{2}_{\alpha}\chi_{v}(\alpha)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\infty}y^{2}_{\alpha}\sum_{v:\,v\subseteq u}\chi_{v}(\alpha)\mbox{{},}

(41)

where
\sigma_{v}^{2}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\mathbb{V}({\mathcal{M}\circ\mathcal{T}^{-1})_{v}(U_{v})})
.
Identity (
41
) motivates the definition, for
\alpha\neq 0
,
of the value function

\displaystyle\mathit{val}^{\alpha}(u)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\sum_{v:\,v\subseteq u}\chi_{v}(\alpha)\mbox{{},}

(42)

such that
\mathit{val}^{U}(u)
, and by Lemma
6.1
also
\mathit{val}^{X}(u)
, is a linear combination of
\mathit{val}^{\alpha}(u)
.

\mathit{val}^{\alpha}
is a cooperation game, since, by definition,

\mathit{val}^{\alpha}(\emptyset)=0
.
It is also easy to see that
\mathit{val}^{\alpha}(u)\in\{0,1\}
.
Therefore, for any
v
, the absolute value of the inner sum in the definitional identity (
9
) of Shapley-Owen values for
\mathit{val}^{\alpha}
is bounded from above:

\displaystyle|\sum_{w\,:\,w\subseteq u}(-1)^{|u|-|w|}\mathit{val}^{\alpha}(v+w)|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{|u|-1}\mbox{{}.}

(43)

In case
|u|
is even, there are
2^{|u|-1}
possible
w
’s such that

(-1)^{|u|-|w|}=1
and also
2^{|u|-1}
possible
w
’s such that

(-1)^{|u|-|w|}=-1
. The case
|u|
is odd follows analogously.
The main point here is that we obtain an upper bound (
43
) which only depends on
u
.
Therefore, plugging the bound (
43
) into the definition (
9
) we obtain an upper bound for Shapley-Owen values with respect to
\mathit{val}^{\alpha}
.
For
\alpha\neq 0
and
\mathit{val}^{\alpha}
as defined
in (
42
):

\displaystyle|\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\mathit{val}^{\alpha})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{|u|-1}}{(d-|u|+1)}\sum_{v\,:\,v\subseteq\sim{u}}\binom{d-|u|}{|v|}^{-1}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\kappa_{u}\mbox{{}.}

(44)

This sum of inverse binomial coefficient can also be expressed analytically (e.g. Zeilberger’s algorithm).
For the linearity of Shapley-Owen values and the identity (
7
) we obtain a spectral decomposition of Shapley-Owen values from the inequality (
44
) and Lemma
6.1
.

Theorem 6.1
.

For all
u\in[1,d]
:

\displaystyle\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\mathit{val}^{X})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\infty}y^{2}_{\alpha}\,\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\mathit{val}^{\alpha})\mbox{{}.}

(45)

The infinite sum on the right-hand side is well-defined, since

\sum_{\alpha=1}^{\infty}y_{\alpha}^{2}
converges
and
|\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\mathit{val}^{\alpha})|
is bounded
by (
44
).
The significance of the identity (
45
)
is that the elementary Shapley values
\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\mathit{val}^{\alpha})

in the identity (
45
)
depend only on the normalized Legendre polynomials
\widetilde{\mathit{L}_{\alpha}}(X)

but not on
\mathcal{M}(X)
,
and can therefore be precomputed once and for all.
In addition, as long as
\mathcal{M}(X)
and the underlying distribution of
X
are unchanged, the PCE of
\mathcal{M}(X)

can be reused in a fairness analysis of
\mathcal{M}(X)
to compute other Shapley-Owen effects.
The price we pay is the outer infinite sum in (
45
), but we can at least approximate this infinite sum using a truncated PCE of
(\mathcal{M}\circ\mathcal{T}^{-1})(U)
with
l
summands:

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathit{Sh}_{u}}^{(l)}(\mathit{val}^{X})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{l}y^{2}_{\alpha}\,\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\mathit{val}^{\alpha})\mbox{{}.}

(46)

Therefore, from Theorem
6.1
and the identities in Section (
5.3
) on the truncation error, we obtain an upper bound on the approximation error of (
46
).

Corollary 6.1
.

For
\epsilon_{l}\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=\joinrel=}\sum_{\alpha=l+1}^{\infty}y_{\alpha}^{2}
as defined
in (
5.3
) and the upper bound
\kappa_{u}
as defined in Proposition
44
:

\displaystyle|\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\mathit{val}^{X})-\widehat{\mathit{Sh}_{u}}^{(l)}(\mathit{val}^{X})|

\displaystyle\leq\kappa_{u}\epsilon_{l}\mbox{{},}

(47)

and, a fortiori,

\lim_{l\to\infty}|\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\mathit{val}^{X})-\widehat{\mathit{Sh}_{u}}^{(l)}(\mathit{val}^{X})|=0
.

We assume the elementary
Shapley-Owen values
\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\chi_{v}(\alpha))=\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\chi_{v}(\mathit{L}_{\alpha}(X)))
to be tabulated for all

u\subseteq[1,d]
and sufficiently many (see below) multi-indices
\alpha
.
Now, the Shapley-Owen effects for
\mathcal{M}(X)
therefore are computed as follows:

1.

Compute a truncated and sparse PCE

\sum_{\alpha=0}^{l}y_{\alpha}\widehat{\mathit{L}_{\alpha}}(U)
, for
l\in\mathbb{N}
,
of
\mathcal{M}\circ\mathcal{T}^{-1}(U)
which is precise up to some given
\epsilon>0
(Lemma
5.1
using the algorithm suggested in Section (

).

2.

Compute the approximated Shapley-Owen effect

\widehat{\mathit{Sh}_{u}}^{(l)}(\mathit{val}^{X})
(
46
).

The total error of the approximated Shapley-Owen effect in Step (2) is bound by
\epsilon\kappa_{u}
(Corollary
6.1
).
Note that the coefficients
\kappa_{u}
are exponential in the

|u|
, but

is typically rather small in fairness analysis. For example,

is between

and

for the popular fairness datasets surveyed in

Related Work

Improving algorithmic fairness in machine learning
typically works by de-biasing the set of training data, by incentivizing learning to make fair decisions, or by removing bias from the output of the machine learning model
Bellamy et al. (2019)
;
Bird et al. (2020)
;
Dwork et al. (2012)
;
Hardt et al. (2016)
;
Jagielski et al. (2019)
;
Kearns et al. (2018)
;
Konstantinov and Lampert (2022)
;
Kusner et al. (2017)
;
Mehrabi et al. (2021)
;
Sharifi-Malvajerdi et al. (2019)
;
Wexler et al. (2019)
;
Zemel et al. (2013)
.
These are all interventions in the design of the machine learning model. A major drawback is that these methods typically embed a fixed notion of fairness into the decision making, whereas the concept of fairness is context-specific.
In contrast, measuring fairness in terms of Shapley-Owen effects allows explicitly specifying fairness constraints and verifying the underlying algorithm with respect to these constraints.

Techniques inspired by formal methods have been used to establish algorithmic fairness properties
by verifying
Albarghouthi et al. (2017)
;
Bastani et al. (2019)
;
Ghosh et al. (2021)
;
Meyer et al. (2021)
;
Sun et al. (2021)

or enforcing
Balunović et al. (2021)
;
Ghosh et al. (2022)
;
John et al. (2020)

a machine-learned model
on all runs of the system with high probability.
This requires certain knowledge about the system model, which may not always be available,
whereas our sensitivity analysis only requires the computability of a square-integrable model under analysis. In contrast to all this work, our fairness analysis treats the model as a black box.
Runtime verification, which is a lightweight solution for checking properties based on a single, possibly long execution trace of a given system, has recently been adapted for the problem of bias detection in decision functionality
Henzinger et al. (2023)
.
Like our approach, runtime monitoring is largely agnostic to the structure of the model, but it is limited to detecting bias and enforcing fairness from a single run.

Shapley values are a popular technique for explaining individual
input contribution in black-box predictive machine-learned models
Aas et al. (2021)
;
Datta et al. (2016)
;
Lundberg et al. (2020)
;
Lundberg and Lee (2017)
;
Molnar (2020)
;
Štrumbelj and Kononenko (2014)
;
Sundararajan and Najmi (2020)
;
Olsen et al. (2023)
;
Borgonovo et al. (2024)
.
These Shapley-based analyses are usually
local
in that they are based on the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}({{\mathcal{M}(X)}\,|\,{X_{u}=x_{u}^{*}}})

for a single observation
x_{u}^{*}
.
Corresponding global values also consider input uncertainty
by averaging the local value function over initial points
Strumbelj and Kononenko (2010)
;
Štrumbelj and Kononenko (2014)
.
Most importantly, the so-called SHAP values
Lundberg and Lee (2017)
,
which are widely used as
post hoc
explanations for the results of machine learning models,
are based on individual Shapley attributions for conditional expectation values.
Assuming that the inputs are independent simplifies computations such as Monte Carlo integration and regression
Lundberg and Lee (2017)
;
Aas et al. (2021)
,
but may also lead to
incorrect explanations
Aas et al. (2021)
;
Merrick and Taly (2020)
;
Fryer et al. (2021)
;
Olsen et al. (2022)
.
Such misinterpretations are unacceptable for a sensitive topic like fairness, and we therefore do not make simplifying assumptions such as input independence. In addition, the underlying fairness measures are computed up to known error bounds.

Unlike SHAP, our developments rely on Shapley-Owen effects, which use the variance-based value
\mathbb{V}({\mathbb{E}({{\mathcal{M}(X)}\,|\,{X_{u}}})})
to assign relative importance to subsets of inputs.
This variance-based value function
\mathit{S}_{u}
for quantifying the relative importance has originally been suggested by
Owen (2014)
.
For linear model with independent inputs the
Shapley regression value
R^{2}_{u}

Lipovetsky and Conklin (2001)
;
Grömping (2007)
;
Huettner and Sunder (2012)
;
Grömping (2015)
for feature importance equals
the normalized Sobol’ index
\nicefrac{{\mathit{S}_{u}}}{{\sigma^{2}}}

Saltelli et al. (2000)
.
This variance-based value has been applied to the sensitivity analysis of neural networks
Fock (2013)
;
Fernández-Navarro et al. (2016)
;
Kowalski and Kusy (2017)
;
Kowalski and Kusy (2018)
;
Li and Chen (2018)
;
Cheng et al. (2019)
.
The two-features Shapley-Owen value
has been applied for the explanation of tree-based machine learning models
Lundberg et al. (2020)
.
The variance-based value allows one to define variance-based sensitivity measures for models with
dependent inputs
Song et al. (2016)
;
Owen and Prieur (2017)
;
Benoumechiara and Elie-Dit-Cosaque (2019)
;
Iooss and Prieur (2019)
.

The defining identity (
9
) of Shapley-Owen effects
\mathit{Sh}_{u}(\mathit{val})
requires a summation over all supersets
v
of the complement
of
u
together with the computation of
the relative importance
\mathbb{V}({\mathbb{E}({{\mathcal{M}(X)}\,|\,{X_{v}}})})
.
This exponential number of summands in identity (
65
) severely limits applicability.
Alternatively, estimation algorithms, based on consistent estimators of the Shapley effects, have been proposed
Song et al. (2016)
.
The
exact permutation
algorithm is expensive, since it traverses the exponential space of permutations between the inputs,
whereas the
random permutation
algorithm is approximative in that it is based of randomly sampling some permutations of the inputs.
Experimental results with these estimators for Shapley effects are reported in
Mara and Becker (2021)
.
The characterization of the Shapley-Owen value in terms of its Möbius inverse (Appendix
J
) still requires an exponential number of summands
Plischke et al. (2021)
.
Our algorithm is novel in that it uses a spectral decomposition of Shapley-Owen effects to separate the computation into model-specific and model-independent computations. In this way, the computation of a Shapley-Owen effect is essentially reduced to the PCE of the underlying model, which is one of the central problems in
uncertainty quantification
.
Our algorithm is also unique in that it provides an upper bound on the accumulated errors in the computation of Shapley-Owen effects.
These error bounds are especially important
for the sensitive issue of fairness analysis.

8
Conclusions

We argue that the fairness of algorithmic decisions needs to be evaluated according to different dimensions of the inputs and how they interact.
If we accept a small number of conditions for equitable attributions, then Shapley-Owen effects are the only way to measure fairness.
There is no assumption of input independence in the calculation of Shapley-Owen effects, as this type of unrealistic assumption leads to incorrect fairness conclusions.

Variance-based measures for assigning relative importance to inputs form the basis of
global fairness analysis
,
which
uses variance decomposition to quantify which inputs and their interactions most influence unfair decisions.
In this way, for a given set of fairness constraints on sensitive inputs, an algorithmic decision can be validated to be fair with respect to those inputs.
If the Shapley-Owen allocation of the relative importance of some sensitive inputs is small enough to be considered fair, those inputs can be projected away or, alternatively, set to a fixed value.
Fairness analysis based on Shapley-Owen effects can also detect interactions among sensitive inputs that would not be detected by simply varying the inputs individually.
