Embeddedness of timelike maximal surfaces in ( 1 + 2 ) (1+2) -Minkowski space
E. Adam Paxton

Abstract
We prove that if ϕ : ℝ 2 → ℝ 1 + 2 \phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2} is a smooth proper timelike immersion with vanishing mean curvature, then necessarily ϕ \phi is an embedding, and every compact subset of ϕ ⁡ ( ℝ 2 ) \phi(\mathbb{R}^{2}) is a smooth graph. It follows that if one evolves any smooth self-intersecting spacelike curve (or any planar spacelike curve whose unit tangent vector spans a closed semi-circle) so as to trace a timelike surface of vanishing mean curvature in ℝ 1 + 2 \mathbb{R}^{1+2} , then the evolving surface will either fail to remain timelike, or it will fail to remain smooth. We show that, even allowing for null points, such a Cauchy evolution must undergo a scalar curvature blow-up—where the blow-up is with respect to an L 1 ​ L ∞ L^{1}\-L^{\infty} norm—and thus the evolving surface will be C 2 C^{2} inextendible beyond singular time. In addition we study the continuity of the unit tangent for the evolution of a self-intersecting curve in isothermal gauge, which defines a well-known evolution beyond singular time.



1
Introduction & statement of main results

The study of minimal surfaces in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
has a long history, and many interesting examples of complete minimal surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
are known. On the other hand, many beautiful theorems have demonstrated that minimal surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
exhibit a certain rigidity. For example, Bernstein’s theorem states that any complete minimal surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
which is a graph, must be a plane. In this article we consider the timelike maximal surfaces in Minkowski space
\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
, where the picture is quite different.

If
\phi\colon M^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
is a smooth proper timelike immersion then
\phi^{0}
is a Morse function and it follows that
M^{2}
is diffeomorphic to either
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or
S^{1}\times\mathbb{R}
. In appropriate coordinates, the mean curvature of
\phi(M^{2})
is hyperbolic, and by solving a Cauchy problem with sufficiently “small” initial data, it is possible to construct smooth proper timelike maximal immersions
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
such that
\phi(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a smooth graph close to a timelike plane (see Lindblad
[
14
]
). This clearly contrasts with Bernstein’s theorem in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(for more stability results in higher dimensions and higher codimensions see Allen, Anderson & Isenberg
[
1
]
, Brendle
[
5
]
, Donninger, Krieger, Szeftel & Wong
[
8
]
, as well as
[
14
]
).

On the other hand, given suitably “large” data, the Cauchy evolution for a timelike maximal surface will develop singularity in finite time (see e.g. Bellettini, Hoppe, Novaga & Orlandi
[
3
]
, Eggers & Hoppe
[
9
]
, Kibble & Turok
[
12
]
, and Nguyen & Tian
[
16
]
. See also Bahouri, Marachli & Perelman
[
2
]
, Eggers, Hoppe, Hynek & Suramlishvili
[
10
]
and Wong
[
19
]
for results in higher dimensions). Nguyen & Tian proved: there exists no smooth proper timelike immersion
\phi\colon S^{1}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
with vanishing mean curvature
[
16
]
. Thus the Cauchy evolution of any closed curve will form singularity in finite time, and every smooth proper timelike maximal immersion in
\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
is of the form
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
.

In this article we will be concerned with the geometry of timelike maximal immersions
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
and the corresponding Cauchy evolution for open curves. Our first result is:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
be a smooth, proper, timelike immersion with vanishing mean curvature. Then
\phi
is an embedding. Moreover, for each compact subset
K\subseteq\phi(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, there is a timelike plane
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
such that
K
is a smooth graph over
P
.

Remark 1.2
.

The restriction to compact subsets in Theorem
1.1
cannot be relaxed, and there exist examples (see Subsection
3.2
) of smooth proper timelike maximal embeddings
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
such that
\phi(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is not a graph.

Remark 1.3
.

If
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
is a smooth proper timelike maximal immersion, then in terms of a spacelike unit normal

N\colon\phi(\mathbb{R}^{2})\to S^{1+1}=\big\{(\sinh\varphi,\cos\vartheta\cosh\varphi,\sin\vartheta\cosh\varphi)\colon(\vartheta,\varphi)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\big\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{1+2}

Theorem
1.1
states that for every compact subset
K\subseteq\phi(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
N(K)
is contained in an open hemi-hyperboloid
S^{1+1}_{+}=\big\{(\sinh\varphi,\cos\vartheta\cosh\varphi,\sin\vartheta\cosh\varphi)\colon(\vartheta,\varphi)\in(\vartheta_{0}-\frac{\pi}{2},\vartheta_{0}+\frac{\pi}{2})\times\mathbb{R}\big\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
for some
\vartheta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
(which is a hemi-sphere with respect to the Minkowski metric). This may be compared with the counterpart in the Riemannian setting. For example, it is well-known that for a complete minimal surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
the image of the unit normal is either a single point, or it omits at most 4 points in the sphere
S^{2}
.

Remark 1.4
.

As a crucial step in the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we adapt an argument of Belletini, Hoppe, Novaga & Orlandi
[
3
]
to construct a global system of isothermal coordinates on an immersed timelike maximal surface (another such construction of global isothermal coordinates may be found in
[
17
, Chap. 7]
). This is a non-trivial result as, in stark contrast with the Riemannian setting, there exist infinitely many possible conformal structures of simply connected Lorentzian surfaces (Kulkarni
[
13
]
).

The coordinate
x^{0}
on
\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
is a time-function, and we now turn to a Cauchy problem for timelike maximal surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
. Let
\mathcal{C}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\{x^{0}=0\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
be a smooth proper immersion and let
V
be a smooth future-directed timelike vector field along
\mathcal{C}
. We say
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\times[-T,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
,
\phi(s,t)=(t,\gamma^{1}(s,t),\gamma^{2}(s,t))
, is a smooth timelike Cauchy evolution for
(\mathcal{C},V)
if
\phi
is a smooth proper timelike immersion with vanishing mean curvature such that
\phi(\cdot,0)=\mathcal{C}
and
V
is tangent to
\mathrm{Im}(\phi)
along
\mathcal{C}
. For a given smooth initial data
(\mathcal{C},V)
let

\displaystyle T_{*}=\sup\{T\geq 0\colon

there exists a smooth timelike Cauchy evolution

\displaystyle\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\times[-T,T]\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}\hskip 3.0pt\text{for}\thinspace(\mathcal{C},V)\}.

It may be shown that
T_{*}>0
under mild assumptions on the initial data
(\mathcal{C},V)
(see e.g. Corollary
5.12
) and from Theorem
1.1
it may be seen to follow that if the image
U_{0}(\mathcal{C})
of the unit tangent vector
U_{0}
along
\mathcal{C}
contains a closed semi-circle (for example, if
\mathcal{C}
is a self-intersecting curve) then
T_{*}<\infty
. However, our proof of Theorem
1.1
is by contradiction, and thus does not shed any light upon the nature of singularity at time
T_{*}
. It is natural to ask whether one can define a smooth, or
C^{k}
for some
k
, extension of the surface beyond singular time, possibly by allowing for null points.

It is well-known that singular behaviour necessarily involves the maximal surface failing to remain timelike at the time
T_{*}
(i.e. the hyperbolicity degenerates), see Jerrard, Novaga & Orlandi
[
11
, Theorem 3.1]
. Eggers & Hoppe
[
9
]
studied singularity formation in a self-similar regime, and observed a blow up of curvature of the spatial cross-sections at the singular time
T_{*}
. Nguyen & Tian observed that, provided the 2
nd
order term in a certain Taylor expansion is non-vanishing, then the limit curve at singular time
T_{*}
will look locally like a
C^{1,\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}
graph
[
16
, Remark 2.6]
. Since the 2
nd
order term is expected to be generically non-vanishing, one thus expects a blow up of curvature at the singular time generically. We prove:

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
,
(s_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and
\phi\colon(s_{0}-\varepsilon,s_{0}+\varepsilon)\times(t_{0}-\varepsilon,t_{0}]\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
be a
C^{1}
immersion of the form
\phi(s,t)=(t,\gamma^{1}(s,t),\gamma^{2}(s,t))
, such that
\phi|_{(s_{0}-\varepsilon,s_{0}+\varepsilon)\times(t_{0}-\varepsilon,t_{0})}
is
C^{2}
and timelike with bounded mean curvature. Suppose that
\phi
is null at the point
(s_{0},t_{0})
, i.e.
\mathrm{Im}(d\phi_{(s_{0},t_{0})})
is a null plane in
\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
. Then the curvature of the (planar) curves
\gamma(\cdot,t)
blows up as
t\nearrow t_{0}
, and
\phi
is not
C^{2}
.

Remark 1.6
.

In fact, we deduce Theorem
1.5
from a stronger result which gives the precise rate of curvature blow-up in an
L^{1}L^{\infty}
norm. Moreover, whilst Theorem
1.5
assumes the case that the limit curve at singularity formation is
C^{1}
, this blow-up rate holds without assuming any structure of the singularity. See Proposition
4.1
for details.

Theorem
1.5
rules out (in all cases) the possibility of a
C^{2}
causal extension of the Cauchy evolution beyond singular time. However, one may still ask whether there exists a
C^{1}
causal extension. A complete answer to this question, independent of gauge, is currently out of reach. Nonetheless, we will proceed to consider one well-known extension beyond singular time: by solving the maximal surface equations globally in isothermal gauge (a construction somewhat analogous to the Weierstrass representation for minimal surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
)
[
17
, Chap. 8]
,
[
20
, Chap. 7]
.

Let us briefly recall the method of isothermal gauge. Since we are now concerned with the prospect of less regular maximal surfaces, it is natural to consider less regular initial data
(\mathcal{C},V)
(other weak notions of solution have been considered by Belletini, Novaga & Orlandi
[
4
]
and Brenier
[
6
]
). Let
\mathcal{C}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\{x^{0}=0\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
be a
C^{k}
proper immersion,
k\geq 1
, and let
V
be a
C^{k-1}
future-directed timelike vector field along
\mathcal{C}
. One may construct a proper
C^{k}
map
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
of the form
\phi(s,t)=(t,\gamma(s,t))
, where
\gamma
satisfies (in the weak sense if
k=1
) the system of equations
\langle\gamma_{s},\gamma_{t}\rangle=0
,
|\gamma_{s}|^{2}+|\gamma_{t}|^{2}=1
,
\gamma_{tt}-\gamma_{ss}=0
, such that
\mathrm{Im}(\phi(\cdot,0))=\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{C})
and
\Sigma=\phi(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is tangent to
V
along
\mathcal{C}
.
\phi
defines a
C^{k}
timelike maximal immersion on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}
where
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}=\{(s,t)\colon\gamma_{s}(s,t)=0\}
and
\Sigma
gives a
C^{k}
timelike maximal surface away from
\Sigma_{\mathrm{sing}}=\phi(\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}})
. For every
p\in\Sigma_{\mathrm{sing}}
either
\Sigma
fails to be a
C^{1}
surface in a neighbourhood of
p
or
\Sigma
is a
C^{1}
surface in a neighbourhood of
p
but is null at
p
. See Section
5.1
for more details.

From Theorems
1.1
and
1.5
it follows that if
U_{0}(\mathcal{C})
contains a closed semi-circle, then
\Sigma
cannot be a
C^{2}
immersed surface (see Corollary
5.6
). There are (non-generic) cases however where
\Sigma
is
C^{1}
immersed. Indeed, in Example
5.13
we present a curve
\mathcal{C}
for which
U_{0}(\mathcal{C})
is exactly a closed semi-circle and show that an evolution by isothermal gauge of
\mathcal{C}
yields a
C^{1}
embedded surface which is a smooth timelike maximal surface away from a pair of null half-lines. This surface contains non-graphical compact sets (compare Theorem
1.1
). It turns out however that the situation of Example
5.13
is borderline. We prove:

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
,
\phi(s,t)=(t,\gamma(s,t))
, be a
C^{1}
evolution for a maximal surface by isothermal gauge, as described above, and write
U_{0}\colon\mathbb{R}\to S^{1}
for the unit tangent vector along the initial curve
\gamma(\cdot,0)
. Suppose that
\mathrm{Im}(U_{0})
contains an arc of length
>\pi
(for example if
\gamma(\cdot,0)
is self-intersecting). Then there exists a time
t_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that: either
\mathrm{Im}(\gamma(\cdot,t_{*}))
is not a
C^{1}
immersed curve; or
\mathrm{Im}(\gamma(\cdot,t_{*}))
is a
C^{1}
immersed curve, but the spatial unit tangent
U(\cdot,t_{*})=\nicefrac{{\gamma_{s}(\cdot,t_{*})}}{{|\gamma_{s}(\cdot,t_{*})|}}
(defined only on the set
\{s\colon\gamma_{s}(s,t_{*})\neq 0\}
) admits no extension to a continuous unit tangent vector field along
\gamma(\cdot,t_{*})
.

In most cases, the discontinuity of the spatial unit tangent corresponds to the curve
\gamma(\cdot,t_{*})
failing to be
C^{1}
. Eggers & Hoppe
[
9
]
introduced the swallowtail singularity, whereby the first singularity is a
C^{1,\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}}
curve which immediately splits off into a twin pair of travelling cusps. This picture was shown to be (in some sense) generic, for sufficiently regular initial data, by Nguyen & Tian
[
16
, Section 3]
. There exist, however, non-generic cases whereby the discontinuity of the unit tangent does not imply a regular cusp, and it is possible that the unit tangent admits no continuous extension along
\gamma(\cdot,t_{*})
, whilst
\mathrm{Im}(\gamma(\cdot,t_{*}))
is a
C^{1}
immersed curve, see Example
5.18
. Although we have no example where such a degenerate situation occurs whilst the surface
\phi(\mathbb{R}^{2})
remains
C^{1}
, we don’t rule this out.

Finally, we note that Theorem
1.1
fails for timelike maximal surfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{1+n}
for
n\geq 3
. Nguyen & Tian gave an example of a smooth, proper, timelike maximal immersion
\phi\colon S^{1}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+3}

[
16
, Appendix]
, and it was conjectured that generic closed curves do not evolve to singularities in higher codimension. This conjecture was confirmed by Jerrard, Novaga & Orlandi
[
11
]
who showed that when
n\geq 4
, generic closed curves with generic initial velocity will evolve to a globally regular surface, whilst in the borderline case
n=3
there are distinct, non-empty open sets of initial data leading to both regular surfaces and singular surfaces respectively. It is simple to see how the example of
[
16
, Appendix]
may be adapted to give a smooth proper self-intersecting timelike maximal immersion
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+3}
and it would be interesting to obtain similar results to
[
11
]
for open curves.

Preliminaries

In this section we will first give a brief recap of the maximal surface equations. We will then present an adaptation of the construction of global isothermal coordinates which was given by Belletini, Hoppe, Novaga & Orlandi in
[
3
]
, for a spatially compact timelike maximal surface, to the spatially non-compact case. We note that another construction of global isothermal coordinates is given in

2.1
Maximal surface equations

Let
x=(x^{0},x^{1},x^{2})
denote standard (i.e.
inertial
) coordinates on
\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
, so that the Minkowski metric is
\eta=-(dx^{0})^{2}+(dx^{1})^{2}+(dx^{2})^{2}
. Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
be an open subset and
\phi\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
be a
C^{1}
immersion. We write
\phi^{\alpha}=x^{\alpha}\circ\phi
for the expression of
\phi
in coordinates,
\alpha=0,1,2
, and denote the image of
\phi
by
\Sigma=\mathrm{Im}(\phi)
. The metric
g
induced by
\phi
is the bilinear form
g_{p}\colon T_{p}\mathbb{R}^{2}\times T_{p}\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
given by
g_{p}(X,Y)=\eta(d\phi_{p}(X),d\phi_{p}(Y))
.

For each
p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, recall that
\phi
is timelike at
p
if
\det(g_{p})<0
,
\phi
is null at
p
if
\det(g_{p})=0
,
\phi
is spacelike if
\det(g_{p})>0
, and
\phi
is causal at
p
if
\phi
is either timelike or null at
p
. We say that
\phi
is timelike (resp. causal) if it is timelike (resp. causal) at every point
p
. In the case that
\phi
is timelike at
p
, there exists a choice of unit spacelike normal vector
N(p)
, and we have a direct sum decomposition of the tangent space which is orthogonal with respect to
\eta
,

\displaystyle T_{\phi(p)}\mathbb{R}^{1+2}=\mathrm{span}\{N(p)\}\oplus T_{\phi(p)}\Sigma.

Let
(s,t)
denote coordinates on
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
. For every compact subset
V\subseteq\Omega
, define the area of
\phi(V)
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}\left[\phi;V\right]=\int_{V}\sqrt{|\det(g(s,t))|}dsdt.

The area of
\phi(V)
is independent of the choice of coordinates
(s,t)
on
V
. The Euler-Lagrange equations associated to the area functional
\mathcal{A}
are

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\det g|}}\partial_{i}\left(\sqrt{|\det g|}g^{ij}\partial_{j}\phi^{\alpha}\right)=0,

(1)

having adopted the summation convention. We say that a
C^{1}
immersion

is

if it satisfies (

) in the weak sense. When
\phi
is a
C^{2}
timelike immersion, (

) is equivalent to
{H}(\phi)=0
, where

is the mean-curvature vector of
\phi(\Omega)
.

(
1
) is independent of the choice of coordinates, so if
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
is a smooth solution to (

) and
\psi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a smooth diffeomorphism, then
\phi^{\prime}=\phi\circ\psi
also solves (
1
). (

) is also invariant under rescaling of
\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
, as well as the isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
. For a timelike immersion, with respect to a system of isothermal coordinates, (

) reduces to the wave equation

\displaystyle\phi_{tt}-\phi_{ss}=0.

.

Let

\displaystyle=0

(2)

\displaystyle|\gamma_{s}|^{2}+|\gamma_{t}|^{2}

Proof of Lemma
2.1
.

The proof is a standard argument exploiting the fact that
\phi^{0}
is a Morse function. Let
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
be a smooth, proper, timelike immersion. For each

write

\displaystyle C_{t}=\{(y^{1},y^{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon\phi^{0}(y^{1},y^{2})=t\}.

Since
\phi
is timelike,
\phi^{0}
can have no critical points. Thus
C_{t}
is a smooth submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
for all

by the implicit function theorem.

Let
g=\phi^{*}\eta
be the induced Lorentzian metric on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and let

, which is a smooth, nowhere-vanishing vector field on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
\phi(C_{t})=\mathrm{Im}(\phi)\cap\{x^{0}=t\}
is spacelike, so with respect to
g
, the submanifolds
C_{t}
are spacelike, and thus
X
is a timelike vector field orthogonal to the submanifolds
C_{t}
.

Define
T=\frac{1}{g(X,X)}X
, and consider the flow of
T
. Let
p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and let
\xi_{p}\colon(a,b)\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, be the smooth, inextendible integral curve of

through

, so
\frac{d\xi_{p}}{ds}(s)=T(\xi_{p}(s))
and
\xi_{p}(0)=p
. Then
\frac{d}{ds}\left(\phi^{0}(\xi_{p}(s))\right)=(d\phi^{0})_{\xi_{p}(s)}(T(\xi_{p}(s)))=1
and so

\displaystyle\phi^{0}(\xi_{p}(s))=\phi^{0}(p)+s.

(5)

We claim that

and

is timelike, and by (

\displaystyle(x^{1}-\phi^{1}(p))^{2}+(x^{2}-\phi^{2}(p))^{2}\leq(x^{0}-\phi^{0}(p))^{2}

\displaystyle\phi^{0}(p)\leq x^{0}\leq\phi^{0}(p)+b,

\displaystyle F(s_{0},t)

\displaystyle=\langle\gamma_{s}(s_{0},t),\gamma_{t}(s_{0},t)\rangle=0

\displaystyle G(s_{0},t)

\displaystyle=-1+|\gamma_{t}(s_{0},t)|^{2},

and the components of the second fundamental form
e(s,t)ds^{2}+2f(s,t)dsdt+g(s,t)dt^{2}
are

\displaystyle e(s_{0},t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\langle\gamma_{ss}(s_{0},t),n(s_{0},t)\rangle}{(1-|\gamma_{t}(s_{0},t)|^{2})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}

\displaystyle f(s_{0},t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\langle\gamma_{st}(s_{0},t),n(s_{0},t)\rangle}{(1-|\gamma_{t}(s_{0},t)|^{2})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}

\displaystyle g(s_{0},t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\langle\gamma_{tt}(s_{0},t),n(s_{0},t)\rangle}{(1-|\gamma_{t}(s_{0},t)|^{2})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}.

The mean curvature scalar is

\displaystyle\begin{split}h(s_{0},t)&=\frac{e(s_{0},t)}{E(s_{0},t)}+\frac{g(s_{0},t)}{G(s_{0},t)}\\
&=-\frac{\langle\gamma_{ss}(s_{0},t),n(s_{0},t)\rangle}{|\gamma_{s}(s_{0},t)|^{2}(1-|\gamma_{t}(s_{0},t)|^{2})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}+\frac{\langle\gamma_{tt}(s_{0},t),n(s_{0},t)\rangle}{(1-|\gamma_{t}(s_{0},t)|^{2})^{\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}}},\end{split}

(29)

and rearranging (
29
) gives the identity

\displaystyle(1-|\gamma_{t}(s_{0},t)|^{2})^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}h(s_{0},t)+k(s_{0},t)=\frac{\langle\gamma_{tt}(s_{0},t),n(s_{0},t)\rangle}{1-|\gamma_{t}(s_{0},t)|^{2}}.

(30)

Next we claim that

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}-\varepsilon}^{t_{0}}\frac{\langle\gamma_{tt}(s_{0},t),n(s_{0},t)\rangle}{1-|\gamma_{t}(s_{0},t)|^{2}}=\infty.

(31)

To show (
31
), write
\mu(t)=|\gamma_{t}(s_{0},t)|^{2}
, so that

\displaystyle\frac{\langle\gamma_{tt}(s_{0},t),n(s_{0},t)\rangle}{1-|\gamma_{t}(s_{0},t)|^{2}}=\frac{\langle\gamma_{tt}(s_{0},t),\gamma_{t}(s_{0},t)\rangle}{|\gamma_{t}(s_{0},t)|(1-|\gamma_{t}(s_{0},t)|^{2})}=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\dot{\mu}(t)}{\mu(t)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(1-\mu(t))}.

We have by assumption
\mu(t)\nearrow 1
as
t\nearrow t_{0}
, so

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}-\varepsilon}^{t_{0}}\frac{\dot{\mu}(t)}{1-\mu(t)}dt=\int^{t_{0}}_{t_{0}-\varepsilon}-\frac{d}{dt}(\log(1-\mu(t)))dt=\infty

from which (
31
) follows.

As
|h(s,t)|\leq C
, (
28
) then follows from (
30
) and (
31
) and the Proposition is proved.
∎

Example 4.2

(Shrinking circle)
.

Define
\phi\colon S^{1}\times(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
by
\phi(s,t)=(t,\gamma(s,t))
, where

\displaystyle\gamma(s,t)=(\cos t\cos s,\cos t\sin s).

Then one may compute
h(s,t)=0
, and
\phi
is a timelike maximal immersion. In addition,
\langle\gamma_{s},\gamma_{t}\rangle=0
(the parameterisation is orthogonal) and
|\gamma_{t}(s,t)|^{2}\nearrow 1
as

. Observe
|k(s,t)|=|\cos t|^{-1}
, and

for all

). For this example, we may study the rate of blow-up in more detail. The element of arclength along

\displaystyle\|k\|_{L^{q}((0,\frac{\pi}{2});L^{p}(S^{1}))}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{2\pi}|k(s,t)|^{p}d\sigma(s)\right)^{\frac{q}{p}}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{\frac{1}{p}}\left(\int_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}|\cos t|^{\frac{q(1-p)}{p}}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}

and

\displaystyle\|k\|_{L^{q}((0,\frac{\pi}{2});L^{p}(S^{1}))}<\infty\quad\text{if and only if}\quad\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}>1.

The shrinking circle is
C^{1}
inextendible beyond the singular time
\frac{\pi}{2}
(in fact, the maximal extension of

). In Subsection

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

:
Let
\phi\colon(s_{0}-\varepsilon,s_{0}+\varepsilon)\times(t_{0}-\varepsilon,t_{0}]\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
,
\phi(s,t)=(t,\gamma^{1}(s,t),\gamma^{2}(s,t))
, be a
C^{1}
immersion which is a
C^{2}
timelike immersion with bounded mean curvature on

, and which is null at the point
(s_{0},t_{0})
. For a sufficiently small
\varepsilon_{0}\in(0,\varepsilon)
, let

\displaystyle\dot{r}(t)=-\frac{\langle\gamma_{s}(r(t),t),\gamma_{t}(r(t),t)\rangle}{|\gamma_{s}(r(t),t)|^{2}};\quad r(t_{0})=s_{0},

which satisfies

for all

(such a solution exists by the Peano existence theorem). We have

.

Define

. Let

\phi^{\prime}(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})=(t^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime 1}(s^{\prime},t^{\prime}),\gamma^{\prime 2}(s^{\prime},t^{\prime}))

is a

immersion which is a

timelike immersion with bounded mean curvature on
\Omega
, and
\phi^{\prime}(0,t_{0})=\phi(s_{0},t_{0})
. By the chain rule,

\displaystyle\langle\gamma^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}}(s^{\prime},t^{\prime}),\gamma^{\prime}_{t^{\prime}}(s^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle=\dot{r}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle|\gamma_{s}(r(t^{\prime})+s^{\prime},t^{\prime})|^{2}

\displaystyle+\langle\gamma_{s}(r(t^{\prime})+s^{\prime},t^{\prime}),\gamma_{t}(r(t^{\prime})+s^{\prime},t^{\prime})\rangle

so by construction we have

\displaystyle\langle\gamma^{\prime}_{s^{\prime}}(0,t^{\prime}),\gamma^{\prime}_{t^{\prime}}(0,t^{\prime})\rangle=0

5
Evolution beyond singular time by isothermal gauge

As is well documented in the physics literature, see e.g.
[
20
, Chap. 7]
, one global notion of Cauchy evolution, which defines a timelike maximal surface away from some possible singular set, may be given for arbitrary initial data by solving the maximal surface equations in isothermal gauge. In fact, we have already encountered this construction in Examples
3.3
–
3.5
and
4.2
.

In Subsection
5.1
we will recall how to evolve by isothermal gauge. In Subsection
5.2
we will prove some results on bounds for the singular set, including a criterion (in terms of only the initial curve) for determining whether the singular set is non-empty in some localized patch, as well as a result of short-time existence. In Subsection
5.3
, we will present some examples whereby the evolution by isothermal gauge yields
C^{1}
embedded surfaces which are non-graphical (these examples are interesting in light of Theorem
1.1
). Finally, in Subsection
5.4
, we will address the question of for which initial data sets the isothermal gauge yields a
C^{1}
immersed surface, and prove Theorem
1.7
which demonstrates an obstruction to constructing
C^{1}
immersed surfaces by isothermal gauge which are not embedded.

5.1
Evolution by isothermal gauge

Let
\mathcal{C}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
, be a
C^{k}
,
k\geq 1
, proper immersion of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(s)=(0,c(s))

(33)

and let
V
be a
C^{k-1}
, future-directed, timelike vector field along
\mathcal{C}
. We refer to the pair
(\mathcal{C},V)
as the initial data.

We will construct a surface
\Sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
containing
\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{C})
, with
V
tangent to
\Sigma
along
\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{C})
, which is a
C^{k}
immersed timelike maximal surface away from some (possibly empty) singular set.

The prescription of the initial data
(\mathcal{C},V)
is equivalent to a prescription of a curve
\mathcal{C}
and a continuous distribution of timelike tangent planes along
\mathcal{C}
. By changing basis as necessary, we may thus assume
V
is of the form

\displaystyle V(s)=(1,v(s))

(34)

where

\displaystyle\langle\dot{c}(s),v(s)\rangle=0

(35)

(
c=(c^{1},c^{2})
,
v=(v^{1},v^{2})
). Since
V
is timelike implies
|v(s)|<1
, we may then reparametrize the curve
\mathcal{C}(s)
to ensure the additional constraint

\displaystyle|\dot{c}(s)|^{2}+|v(s)|^{2}=1

(36)

holds. The pair
(\dot{\mathcal{C}}(s),V(s))
gives an orthonormal frame along the initial data, and the timelike planes
T_{\mathcal{C}(s)}\Sigma=\mathrm{span}\{\dot{\mathcal{C}}(s),V(s)\}
are spanned by the null vectors

\displaystyle A_{\pm}(s)=V(s)\pm\dot{\mathcal{C}}(s)=\left(1,v(s)\pm\dot{c}(s)\right)=(1,a_{\pm}(s)).

(37)

Next, define a
C^{k}
map
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
by
\phi(s,t)=(t,\gamma^{1}(s,t),\gamma^{2}(s,t))
where
\gamma=(\gamma^{1},\gamma^{2})
is given by d’Alembert’s formula

\displaystyle\gamma(s,t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(c(s+t)+c(s-t)+\int_{s-t}^{s+t}v(\zeta)d\zeta\right).

(38)

(
38
) implies that

\displaystyle\gamma_{tt}-\gamma_{ss}

\displaystyle=0

(39)

\displaystyle\gamma(s,0)=c(s);\quad

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}(s,0)=v(s)

(40)

with (
39
) understood in the weak sense when
\gamma
is not
C^{2}
. The isothermal gauge conditions

\displaystyle\langle\gamma_{s}(s,t),\gamma_{t}(s,t)\rangle

\displaystyle=0

(41)

\displaystyle|\gamma_{s}(s,t)|^{2}+|\gamma_{t}(s,t)|^{2}

\displaystyle=1

(42)

are satisfied for all
(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
by (
38
). We will call
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
the evolution of
(\mathcal{C},V)
by isothermal gauge.

Write

\displaystyle\Sigma

\displaystyle=\phi(\mathbb{R}^{2})

and define the closed (possibly empty) singular set by

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}=\{(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon\gamma_{s}(s,t)=0\}

(43)

so that
\phi
gives a
C^{k}
immersion on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}
. Then from (
39
), (
41
), (
42
) we see that on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}
,
\phi
defines a timelike, maximal immersion. Write

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\mathrm{sing}}

\displaystyle=\phi(\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}).

(44)

By construction
\Sigma\setminus\Sigma_{\mathrm{sing}}
gives a
C^{k}
timelike maximal immersed surface containing
\mathcal{C}
and tangent to the velocity field
V
along
\mathcal{C}
.

The following simple topological result shows that this is indeed a global evolution.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
,
\phi(s,t)=(t,\gamma(s,t))
be an evolution by isothermal gauge for a
C^{1}\times C^{0}
initial data
(\mathcal{C},V)
, where
\mathcal{C}=\gamma(\cdot,0)
is a proper immersion, so that
|\gamma(s,0)|\to\infty
as
s\to\pm\infty
. Then
|\gamma(s,t)|\to\infty
as
s\to\infty
for all
t
, so that each map
\gamma(\cdot,t)
is proper, and thus
\phi
is proper.

Proof.

For each
t\in\mathbb{R}
, since
|\gamma_{t}|\leq 1
,
|\gamma(s,t)|\geq|\gamma(s,0)|-\int_{0}^{t}|\gamma_{t}(s,\tilde{t})|d\tilde{t}\geq|\gamma(s,0)|-t\to\infty
as
s\to\pm\infty
.
∎

Recalling that
a_{\pm}(s)=v(s)\pm\dot{c}(s)=\gamma_{t}(s,0)\pm\gamma_{s}(s,0)

give the spatial parts of the null vectors
A_{\pm}(s)=(1,a_{\pm}(s))
along the initial tangent planes, with
|a_{\pm}(s)|^{2}=1
, from (
38
) we see

\displaystyle\begin{split}\gamma_{s}(s,t)&=\frac{1}{2}\left(\dot{c}(s+t)+\dot{c}(s-t)+v(s+t)-v(s-t)\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\left(a_{+}(s+t)-a_{-}(s-t)\right)\end{split}

(45)

so we have the following characterisation of
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}=\left\{(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon a_{+}(s+t)=a_{-}(s-t)\right\}.

(46)

We will now show that
\Sigma_{\mathrm{sing}}
is singular, at least in the sense that it consists of null points. The following result was observed, as part of a broader context, in
[
11
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
be an evolution by isothermal gauge for a
C^{1}\times C^{0}
initial data
(\mathcal{C},V)
, and suppose
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}
as defined in (
43
) is non-empty. Suppose for some neighbourhood
U
of a point
q\in\partial\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}
that
\phi(U)
is a
C^{1}
embedded surface. Then
\phi(U)
is null at
\phi(q)
.

Proof.

Let
U
be a neighbourhood of
q\in\partial\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}
such that
\phi(U)
is a
C^{1}
embedded surface.
For each point
(s,t)\in U\setminus\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}
, the tangent space
T_{\phi(s,t)}\phi(U)
is a timelike plane which intersects the light cone along null directions spanned by the nowhere vanishing null vectors

\phi_{s}(s,t)+\phi_{t}(s,t)=\phi_{s}(s+t,0)+\phi_{t}(s+t,0)=A_{+}(s+t)=(1,a_{+}(s+t))

and

\phi_{s}(s,t)-\phi_{t}(s,t)=\phi_{s}(s-t,0)-\phi_{t}(s-t,0)=A_{-}(s-t)=(1,a_{-}(s-t)).

Choose a sequence of points
(s_{k},t_{k})\in U\setminus\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}
with
(s_{k},t_{k})\to q=(s_{*},t_{*})
. Since
a_{+}(s_{*}+t_{*})=a_{-}(s_{*}-t_{*})
, it follows that
\lim_{(s_{k},t_{k})\to(s_{*},t_{*})}a_{+}(s_{k}+t_{k})=\lim_{(s_{k},t_{k})\to(s_{*},t_{*})}a_{-}(s_{k}-t_{k})
, so the null lines along which
T_{\phi(s_{k},t_{k})}\phi(U)
intersects the light cone converge. So
T_{\phi(s_{*},t_{*})}\phi(U)=\lim_{(s_{k},t_{k})\to(s_{*},t_{*})}T_{\phi(s_{k},t_{k})}\phi(U)
must be a null plane.
∎

\mathcal{C}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}

k\geq 1
, of the form
\mathcal{C}(s)=(0,c(s))

\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle=\vartheta(s)+\arcsin(\mu(s))

\displaystyle\alpha_{-}(s)

Remark 5.3
.

The function
\mu

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
be an evolution by isothermal gauge for a

(\mathcal{C},V)
. Writing
U_{0}
for the unit tangent along
\mathcal{C}
as in (

), suppose that

contains a closed semi-circle, i.e. suppose there exist

\displaystyle|\vartheta(s_{2})-\vartheta(s_{1})|\geq\pi,

where

as in (

.

The same conclusion cannot be reached if

Proof:

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}

initial data
(\mathcal{C},V)
, and let
U_{0}
be the unit tangent along
\mathcal{C}
as in (
47
). Suppose that
\mathrm{Im}(U_{0})

is not a
C^{2}
immersed surface.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.4
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}\cap D(s_{1},s_{2})\neq\emptyset.

By continuity, for some
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}\cap D(s_{1},s_{2})\cap\{(s,t)\colon|t|<\varepsilon\}=\emptyset,

so let

In particular, Proposition

Lemma 5.7
.

Suppose
c\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a
C^{1}
immersion with a point of self-intersection, i.e. there exist
r_{1}<r_{2}
such that
c(r_{1})=c(r_{2})
. Let
U_{0}
denote the unit tangent along
c
as in (
47
). Then
U_{0}([r_{1},r_{2}])
contains an arc of length
>\pi
, i.e. there exist

such that

\displaystyle\vartheta(s_{2})-\vartheta(s_{1})>\pi

(57)

where
\vartheta
denotes the angle swept out between
U_{0}
and the
x^{1}
axis as in (
48
).

Proof.

Since
c(r_{1})=c(r_{2})
, for every
\omega\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\langle\dot{c}(s),\omega\rangle ds=0.

But if
U_{0}([r_{1},r_{2}])
is contained in a closed semi-circle, then there exists
\omega_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{2}}\langle\dot{c}(s),\omega_{0}\rangle ds>0,

a contradiction. We conclude that
U_{0}([r_{1},r_{2}])
contains an arc of length
>\pi
as claimed.
∎

Proposition

gives a sufficient condition in terms of

Lemma 5.8

\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}

Remark 5.11
.

If
\mathcal{C}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\{x^{0}=0\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
is a smooth proper immersion such that
\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{C})
is not a straight line, then it is easy to find a smooth vector field

along

for which the evolution
\phi
of

in isothermal gauge becomes singular in finite time (i.e.

\mathcal{C}(s)=(0,c(s))
and

, and choose

so that

. Let

, where

) and (

From Lemma

we may obtain the following short-time existence result, which does not require any decay of the initial data at infinity.

Corollary 5.12

(Short-time existence)
.

Let
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
be an evolution by isothermal gauge for a
C^{k}\times C^{k-1}
initial data
(\mathcal{C},V)
,
k\geq 1
, and let
U_{0}
denote the unit tangent vector along
\mathcal{C}
as in (
47
). Suppose
V
is uniformly timelike, i.e. with
V=(1,v)
we have
\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}}|v(s)|<1
, and suppose
U_{0}
is uniformly continuous. Then there exists
T>0
depending only on
\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{1-|v(s)|}
and the modulus of continuity of
U_{0}
such that
\mathrm{Im}(\phi)\cap\{(x^{0},x^{1},x^{2})\colon|x^{0}|\leq T\}
is a
C^{k}
immersed timelike maximal surface containing
\mathrm{Im}(\mathcal{C})
and tangent to
V
along
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Take
\varepsilon>0
so that
\sup_{s\in\mathbb{R}}|v(s)|^{2}\leq 1-\varepsilon
. Since
U_{0}
is uniformly continuous, there exists
\delta>0
, depending on
\varepsilon
and the modulus of continuity of
U_{0}
, such that
|\vartheta(r_{2})-\vartheta(r_{1})|^{2}<\varepsilon
provided
|r_{1}-r_{2}|\leq\delta
. Defining
s_{k}=\frac{\delta k}{2}
for all

gives

\displaystyle\sup_{r_{1},r_{2}\in[s_{k},s_{k+2}]}|\vartheta(r_{2})-\vartheta(r_{1})|^{2}+\sup_{r\in[s_{k},s_{k+2}]}|v(r)|^{2}<1,

so
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}\cap D(s_{k},s_{k+2})=\emptyset
for all

by Lemma
5.8
. With
T=\frac{\delta}{4}
, the set
\{(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon|t|\leq T\}
is contained in
\cup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}D(s_{k},s_{k+2})
, so
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}\cap\{(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon|t|\leq T\}=\emptyset
, and the claim follows.
∎

Example 5.13

(

5.4
Discontinuity of the spatial unit tangent: proof of Theorem
1.7

The surfaces constructed by isothermal gauge in Example
5.13
are
C^{1}
embedded, are smooth timelike maximal surfaces away from a pair of null lines, and contain compact subsets which are non-graphical (compare with Theorem
1.1
).
Note that in Examples
5.13
and
5.14
, the image of the tangent vector
U_{0}
along the initial curve
\mathcal{C}
is exactly a closed semi-circle.

In this section we will show that the behaviour observed in Example
5.13
is borderline. To be precise, we will prove Theorem
1.7
which states that: if
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
is an evolution by isothermal gauge for a
C^{1}\times C^{0}
initial data
(\mathcal{C},V)
, and if the image of the unit tangent vector along
\mathcal{C}
contains an arc of length
>\pi
, i.e. if there exist
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
so that

\displaystyle\vartheta(s_{2})-\vartheta(s_{1})>\pi,

(60)

where
\vartheta
is as in (
48
), then the spatial unit tangent (defined along
\phi|_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}}
) admits no extension to a continuous unit tangent vector field along
\phi
.

When
\mathcal{C}
is a closed curve, the discontinuity of the spatial unit tangent was proved by Nguyen & Tian
[
16
, Prop. 2.9 & Prop. 2.11]
(for smooth initial data) and by Jerrard, Novaga and Orlandi in
[
11
, Theorem 5.1]
(for
C^{1}\times C^{0}
initial data). The proof of Theorem
1.7
extends the argument of those authors to the spatially non-compact case (note that if
\mathcal{C}
is closed, then there exist
s_{1},s_{2}
so that (
60
) is satified by Lemma
5.7
).

Let
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
,
\phi(s,t)=(t,\gamma(s,t))
be an evolution by isothermal gauge. As in Section
5.2
, we write

\displaystyle a_{\pm}(s)=\gamma_{t}(s,0)\pm\gamma_{s}(s,0),

so that
|a_{\pm}(s)|^{2}=1
. Recall from (
51
), (
52
) that
a_{\pm}(s)=(\cos\alpha_{\pm}(s),\sin\alpha_{\pm}(s))
, where

\displaystyle\alpha_{+}(s)

\displaystyle=\vartheta(s)+\arcsin\left(\mu(s)\right)

\displaystyle\alpha_{-}(s)

\displaystyle=\vartheta(s)-\arcsin\left(\mu(s)\right)-\pi,

where
\vartheta
and
\mu
are defined by (
48
) and (
50
).

Let us now introduce

\displaystyle\beta(s,t)=\alpha_{+}(s+t)-\alpha_{-}(s-t).

(61)

We have

\displaystyle\beta(s,0)=\alpha_{+}(s)-\alpha_{-}(s)=2\arcsin(\tanh(\varphi(s)))+\pi\in(0,2\pi)\quad\text{for all}\thinspace s\in\mathbb{R}.

(62)

The proof of Theorem
1.7
is via a study of the spatial unit tangent map

\displaystyle U(s,t)=\frac{\gamma_{s}(s,t)}{|\gamma_{s}(s,t)|},

which is well defined for
(s,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}
. From (
38
) one may compute explicitly

\displaystyle\begin{split}U(s,t)=\sgn\left(\sin\frac{\beta(s,t)}{2}\right)e(s,t)\end{split}

(63)

where

\displaystyle e(s,t)=\left(-\sin\frac{\alpha_{+}(s+t)+\alpha_{-}(s-t)}{2},\cos\frac{\alpha_{+}(s+t)+\alpha_{-}(s-t)}{2}\right)

(64)

is a continuous unit vector field along
\gamma(s,t)
(note that
e(s,t)
does not necessarily define a unit tangent vector field along
\gamma(s,t)
).

We have
(s,t)\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{sing}}
precisely when
\beta(s,t)\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
. From formula (
63
), it is apparent that to study when
U
becomes discontinuous requires analysis of when
\sin\left(\frac{\beta}{2}\right)
changes sign.

Lemma 5.15
.

Let
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
be an evolution by isothermal gauge for a
C^{1}\times C^{0}
initial data
(\mathcal{C},V)
. With
U_{0}
denoting the unit tangent along
\mathcal{C}
as in (
47
), suppose that
\mathrm{Im}(U_{0})
contains an arc of length
>\pi
, i.e. suppose there exist
s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\vartheta(s_{2})-\vartheta(s_{1})>\pi,

where
\vartheta
is as in (
48
). Then, with
\beta
as in (
61
), there exists
(s_{0},t_{0})
such that
\beta(s_{0},t_{0})\notin[0,2\pi]
. Furthermore, if
\mathcal{C}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\{x^{0}=0\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
is a proper immersion, then there exists a time
t_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\sin\left(\frac{\beta(\cdot,t_{*})}{2}\right)
takes both positive and negative values.

Proof.

By identities (
51
) and (
52
), we have

\displaystyle\big(\alpha_{+}(s_{2})-\alpha_{-}(s_{1})\big)-\big(\alpha_{+}(s_{1})-\alpha_{-}(s_{2})\big)=2\big(\vartheta(s_{2})-\vartheta(s_{1})\big)>2\pi

and so, setting
s_{0}=\frac{1}{2}(s_{1}+s_{2})
and
t_{0}=\frac{1}{2}(s_{1}-s_{2})
gives

\displaystyle\beta(s_{0},-t_{0})-\beta(s_{0},t_{0})

\displaystyle=\big(\alpha_{+}(s_{0}-t_{0})-\alpha_{-}(s_{0}+t_{0})\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 25.0pt-\big(\alpha_{+}(s_{0}+t_{0})-\alpha_{-}(s_{0}-t_{0})\big)

\displaystyle>2\pi.

It follows that one of
\beta(s_{0},-t_{0})>2\pi
or
\beta(s_{0},t_{0})<0
holds, so
\beta(s_{0},t_{0})\notin[0,2\pi]
for some
(s_{0},t_{0})
as claimed.

Now suppose
\mathcal{C}
is proper, and suppose for a contradiction that there exists no time
t_{*}
such that
\sin\left(\frac{\beta(\cdot,t_{*})}{2}\right)
takes both positive and negative values. Write
A=\{t\in\mathbb{R}\colon\sin\frac{\beta(s,t)}{2}\geq 0\thinspace\text{for all}\thinspace s\in\mathbb{R}\}
,
B=\{t\in\mathbb{R}\colon\sin\frac{\beta(s,t)}{2}\leq 0\thinspace\text{for all}\thinspace s\in\mathbb{R}\}
. Then
A
and
B
are closed sets, and we are supposing that
A\cup B=\mathbb{R}
.

Note that
A
is non-empty by (
62
), whilst
\beta(s_{0},t_{0})\notin[0,2\pi]
implies that
B
is non-empty, and so by connectedness of
\mathbb{R}
,
A\cap B
must be non-empty. Taking
t_{1}\in A\cap B
gives
\beta(\cdot,t_{1})\equiv 2k\pi
, which implies
\gamma_{s}(\cdot,t_{1})\equiv 0
so
\mathrm{Im}(\gamma(\cdot,t_{1}))
consists of a single point. But since
\mathcal{C}
is proper, this contradicts Lemma
5.1
. Thus the lemma is proved.
∎

We will deduce Theorem
1.7
from Lemma
5.15
together with the following

Lemma 5.16
.

Let
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{1+2}
,
\phi(s,t)=(t,\gamma(s,t))
be an evolution by isothermal gauge for a

initial data
(\mathcal{C},V)
, and let
\beta

t_{*}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

. Then for any

, there is an open interval

, such that for all

, either
\gamma([s_{1},s_{2}],t)
is not a

immersed curve, or
\gamma([s_{1},s_{2}],t)

admits no continuous extension to a unit tangent vector field along

on

.

Proof.

We will follow the proof of
[
11
, Theorem 5.1(iii)]
. Let us assume that
[s_{1},s_{2}]
is such that
\beta(s_{1},t_{*})<0
and
\beta(s_{2},t_{*})>0
, since all other cases may be treated similarly. By continuity there exists
\delta_{0}\in(0,\zeta]
such that
\beta(s_{1},t)<0
and
\beta(s_{2},t)>0
for all
t\in(t_{*}-\delta_{0},t_{*}+\delta_{0})
.

Suppose for some
t_{0}\in(t_{*}-\delta_{0},t_{*}+\delta_{0})
, we have that
\gamma([s_{1},s_{2}],t_{0})
is a
C^{1}
immersed curve and
U(\cdot,t_{0})
extends to a continuous unit vector field
\hat{U}(\cdot,t_{0})
along
\gamma(\cdot,t_{0})
on the interval
[s_{1},s_{2}]
(we will see such a situation in Example
5.17
). Define

\displaystyle\begin{split}r_{2}&=\sup\{\hat{s}\in[s_{1},s_{2}]\colon\beta(s,t_{0})\leq 0\quad\text{for all}\thinspace s\in[s_{1},\hat{s}]\}\\
r_{1}&=\inf\{\hat{s}\in[s_{1},r_{2}]\colon\beta(s,t_{0})=0\quad\text{for all}\thinspace s\in[\hat{s},r_{2}]\},\end{split}

(65)

then

\displaystyle\beta(s,t_{0})=0\quad\text{for all}\thinspace s\in[r_{1},r_{2}]

(66)

and
\beta
takes both positive and negative values in every neighbourhood of
[r_{1},r_{2}]
.

5.16
.

We claim that

\displaystyle\alpha_{+}(r_{1}+t_{0})=\alpha_{+}(r_{2}+t_{0})+m\pi\quad\text{for some odd integer}\thinspace m.

(67)

To show (
67
), note that since
\gamma(s,t_{0})=\gamma(r_{1},t_{0})
for all
s\in[r_{1},r_{2}]
, it follows that
\hat{U}(r_{1},t_{0})=\hat{U}(r_{2},t_{0})
. Take sequences
\{x_{n}\}
and
\{y_{n}\}
with
x_{n}\to r_{1}
,
\beta(x_{n},t_{0})<0
, and
y_{n}\to r_{2}
,
\beta(y_{n},t_{0})>0
(which is possible from the definitions of
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
). Then from (
63
)

\displaystyle\hat{U}(r_{1},t_{0})

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{x_{n}\to r_{1}}\left\{\sgn\left(\sin\frac{\beta(x_{n},t_{0})}{2}\right)\right\}e(r_{1},t_{0})

\displaystyle\hat{U}(r_{2},t_{0})

\displaystyle=\lim\limits_{y_{n}\to r_{2}}\left\{\sgn\left(\sin\frac{\beta(y_{n},t_{0})}{2}\right)\right\}e(r_{2},t_{0})

\displaystyle=-\lim\limits_{x_{n}\to r_{1}}\left\{\sgn\left(\sin\frac{\beta(x_{n},t_{0})}{2}\right)\right\}e(r_{2},t_{0}),

so
e(r_{1},t_{*})=-e(r_{2},t_{*})
from which (
67
) follows from (
64
) and (
66
).

Geometrically, (
66
) and (
67
) amount to the statement that
\alpha_{+}(s+t_{0})
and
\alpha_{-}(s-t_{0})
(which we recall represent the null directions along the initial curve) are identically equal for
s\in[r_{1},r_{2}]
, and undergo a rotation by a non-trivial multiple of
\pi
as
s
varies from
r_{1}
to
r_{2}
. We will now show that this situation will be lost after a small perturbation of
t_{0}
. More precisely, we will show that for any
\varepsilon>0
, there is an open interval
I
, either of the form
I=(t_{0},t_{0}+\delta)
or
I=(t_{0}-\delta,t_{0})
for some
\delta>0
, such that for each
t\in I
, there is an interval
J=J(t)\subseteq[s_{1},s_{2}]
such that
\beta(\cdot,t)
takes both positive and negative values on
J
and
|\alpha_{+}(w_{1}+t)-\alpha_{+}(w_{2}+t)|<\varepsilon
for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in J
. Taking
\varepsilon
smaller than
\pi
, this will imply that condition (
67
) with
t_{0}
replaced by
t
cannot hold for any
r_{1},r_{2}\in J
, so we will conclude that for each
t\in I
, the unit tangent
U(\cdot,t)
admits no continuous extension to a unit tangent map, from which the conclusion of the lemma will follow.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. By (
65
) and continuity of
\alpha_{+}
there exists
r_{3}\in[s_{1},r_{1})
such that
\beta(r_{3},t_{0})<0
and

\displaystyle|\alpha_{+}(s+t_{0})-\alpha_{+}(r_{1}+t_{0})|<\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\quad\text{for}\hskip 3.0pts\in[r_{3},r_{1}].

(68)

Take
\delta>0
so that

\displaystyle\beta(r_{3},t)

\displaystyle<0\quad\text{for}\hskip 3.0ptt\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}+\delta].

(69)

By the uniform continuity of
\alpha_{+}
on compact sets, by refining
\delta>0
to a smaller number as necessary, we may ensure

\displaystyle|\alpha_{+}(s+t)-\alpha_{+}(s+t_{0})|

\displaystyle<\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\quad\text{for}\hskip 3.0pts\in[s_{1},s_{2}],t\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}+\delta].

(70)

By (
67
), we can define

\displaystyle r_{4}=\inf\left\{s\in[r_{1},r_{2}]\colon|\alpha_{+}(s+t_{0})-\alpha_{+}(r_{1}+t_{0})|=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\right\}.

(71)

We will first treat the case where
\alpha_{+}(r_{4}+t_{0})=\alpha_{+}(r_{1}+t_{0})+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
. By refining
\delta>0
to be smaller as neccesary, we may assume that
\alpha_{+}(w_{2}+t_{0})>\alpha_{+}(w_{1}+t_{0})
provided
w_{1}\in[r_{1},r_{1}+\delta]
and
w_{2}\in[r_{4}-\delta,r_{4}]
. Then for each
\tau\in(0,\delta]
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{r_{1}}^{r_{4}-\tau}(\alpha_{+}(s+\tau+t_{0})-\alpha_{+}(s+t_{0}))ds

\displaystyle=\int_{r_{4}-\tau}^{r_{4}}\alpha_{+}(s+t_{0})ds

\displaystyle\hskip 25.0pt-\int^{r_{1}+\tau}_{r_{1}}\alpha_{+}(s+t_{0})ds

\displaystyle>0

which shows that there exists an
s(\tau)\in[r_{1},r_{4}-\tau]
such that
\alpha_{+}(s(\tau)+\tau+t_{0})>\alpha_{+}(s(\tau)+t_{0})
. We then see

\displaystyle\begin{split}\beta\left(s(\tau)+\frac{\tau}{2},t_{0}+\frac{\tau}{2}\right)&=\alpha_{+}(s(\tau)+\tau+t_{0})-\alpha_{+}(s(\tau)-t_{0})\\
&>\alpha_{+}(s(\tau)+t_{0})-\alpha_{+}(s(\tau)-t_{0})=\beta(s(\tau),t_{0})\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{betazero}}}{{=}}0.\end{split}

(72)

Then for all
\tau\in(0,\delta]
, by (
69
) and (
72
)
\beta(\cdot,t_{0}+\frac{\tau}{2})
takes both positive and negative values on
J=J(t_{0}+\frac{\tau}{2}):=[r_{3},s(\tau)+\frac{\tau}{2}]
. On the other hand, for all
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in J
we have

\displaystyle|\alpha_{+}(\omega_{1}+t_{0}+\frac{\tau}{2})-\alpha_{+}(\omega_{2}+t_{0}+\frac{\tau}{2})|

\displaystyle\leq|\alpha_{+}(\omega_{1}+t_{0}+\frac{\tau}{2})-\alpha_{+}(\omega_{1}+t_{0})|

\displaystyle+|\alpha_{+}(\omega_{2}+t_{0}+\frac{\tau}{2})-\alpha_{+}(\omega_{2}+

\displaystyle t_{0})|+|\alpha_{+}(\omega_{1}+t_{0})-\alpha_{+}(r_{1}+t_{0})|

\displaystyle+|\alpha_{+}(\omega_{2}+t_{0})-\alpha_{+}(r_{1}+t_{0})|

and since the first two terms on the right hand side of the above inequality are bounded by (
70
) and each of the last two terms is bounded by (
68
) and (
71
), this gives
|\alpha_{+}(\omega_{1}+t_{0}+\frac{\tau}{2})-\alpha_{+}(\omega_{2}+t_{0}+\frac{\tau}{2})|<\varepsilon
which is what we set out to show.

Next we treat the case where
\alpha_{+}(r_{4}+t_{0})=\alpha_{+}(r_{1}+t_{0})-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}
by a similar argument. Choose
\delta>0
so that
\alpha_{+}(w_{1}+t_{0})>\alpha_{+}(w_{2}+t_{0})
provided
w_{1}\in[r_{1},r_{1}+\delta]
and
w_{2}\in[r_{4}-\delta,r_{4}]
. Then, for all
\tau\in(0,\delta]
there exists
s(\tau)\in[r_{1}+\tau,r_{4}]
such that
\alpha_{+}(s(\tau)-\tau+t_{0})>\alpha_{+}(s(\tau)+t_{0})
. In this case,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\beta\left(s(\tau)-\frac{\tau}{2},t_{0}-\frac{\tau}{2}\right)&=\alpha_{+}(s(\tau)-\tau+t_{0})-\alpha_{-}(s(\tau)-t_{0})\\
&>\alpha_{+}(s(\tau)+t_{0})-\alpha_{-}(s(\tau)-t_{0})=\beta(s(\tau),t_{0})\\
&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{betazero}}}{{=}}0.\end{split}

(73)

Then for all
\tau\in(0,\delta]
, by (
69
) and (
73
)
\beta(\cdot,t_{0}-\frac{\tau}{2})
takes both positive and negative values on
J=[r_{3},s(\tau)-\frac{\tau}{2}]
, whilst for all
w_{1},w_{2}\in J
, arguing as above by (
68
), (
70
) and (
71
) we have
|\alpha_{+}(w_{1}+t_{0}-\frac{\tau}{2})-\alpha_{+}(w_{2}+t_{0}-\frac{\tau}{2})|<\varepsilon
which is what we set out to show. This completes the proof.
∎

in Lemma

may be chosen to be contained in any neighbourhood of the time

, but it is not always possible to choose an interval

containing

Example 5.17

(Cusp reversal)
.

Consider the
