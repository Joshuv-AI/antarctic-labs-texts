Parabolic Anderson model in bounded domains of recurrent metric measure spaces
F. Baudoin, L. Chen, C.H. Huang, C. Ouyang, S. Tindel, J. Wang

Abstract
A metric measure space equipped with a Dirichlet form is called recurrent if its Hausdorff dimension is less than its walk dimension. In bounded domains of such spaces we study the parabolic Anderson models ∂ t u ⁡ ( t , x ) = Δ ​ u ​ ( t , x ) + β ​ u ​ ( t , x ) ​ W ˙ α ​ ( t , x ) \partial_{t}u(t,x)=\Delta u(t,x)+\beta u(t,x)\,\dot{W}_{\alpha}(t,x) where the noise W α W_{\alpha} is white in time and colored in space when α > 0 \alpha>0 while for α = 0 \alpha=0 it is also white in space. Both Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions are considered. Besides proving existence and uniqueness in the Itô sense we also get precise L p L^{p} estimates for the moments and intermittency properties of the solution as a consequence. Our study reveals new exponents which are intrinsically associated to the geometry of the underlying space and the results for instance apply in metric graphs or fractals like the Sierpiński gasket for which we prove scaling invariance properties of the models.

Introduction

This article originates in two different threads which have been very active in the last decades. Namely on the one hand models from mathematical physics involving fractals have drawn interest in the recent literature, see for instance
[
1
]
and references therein. On the other hand many fascinating connections between parabolic Anderson models
[
11
,
12
,
20
,
21
]
and directed polymer models
[
9
,
10
,
27
,
33
,
37
]
on spaces such as
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, or graphs have been established. Growth and weak/strong disorder properties for polymers have now been obtained either in discrete or continuous setting. Our aim in this paper is to bring those two worlds together, initiating a theory of parabolic Anderson and related models on general metric measure spaces equipped with a Dirichlet form.

There has been a recent spur in the study of stochastic heat equations and polymer measures on geometric structures. In a discrete setting one should probably go back to
[
10
]
, which focuses on disorder properties for polymers defined on infinite graphs. In continuous spaces, properties of the stochastic heat equation in sub-Riemannian spaces such as Heisenberg groups have been examined in
[
4
,
7
]
. One should also mention the recent preprint
[
19
]
dealing with regularity structures in non-flat settings. The current contribution fits in this global picture, our aim being to quantify the interaction between geometric and random environments for a rich class of models.

As mentioned above, we are focusing here on general metric measure spaces. In that class of spaces we are particularly interested in post critically finite (p.c.f.) fractals, such as the Sierpiński gasket and other nested fractals, which are limits of graphs. For an overview of the wealth of probabilistic and analytic techniques on p.c.f. fractals, we refer to the classical works
[
2
,
24
,
30
]
. Post critically finite fractals are compelling examples of geometric structures for two main reasons. First they lead to a wide variety of exponents for diffusions, heat equations and polymers. We will see some of these features below. Next they are limits of graphs, which means that corresponding limits of discrete objects can also be investigated for stochastic PDEs and polymers. We plan to pursue this line of investigation in future works.

Let us specify briefly our geometric setting (see Section
2
for more details). Namely, in the present paper, we study fractional parabolic Anderson models in bounded domains of recurrent metric measure spaces. A recurrent metric measure space
(X,d,\mu)
is a metric measure space that supports a Brownian motion whose heat kernel
p_{t}(x,y)
has sub-Gaussian estimates of the form

\displaystyle p_{t}(x,y)\simeq t^{-d_{h}/d_{w}}\exp\biggl(-C\Bigl(\frac{d(x,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\biggr)\,,

(1.1)

with
d_{h}<d_{w}
. The parameter
d_{h}
is the Hausdorff dimension of the metric space
(X,d)
and the parameter
d_{w}\geq 2
is the so-called walk dimension. Intuitively
d_{h}
controls the volume growth rate of balls of small radii (i.e.,
\mu(B(x,r))\simeq r^{d_{h}}
), while
d_{w}
controls the escape rate of such balls by the Brownian motion (i.e.,
\mathbf{E}_{x}(d(x,B_{t}))\simeq t^{1/d_{w}}
). For instance, in the Euclidean space
(\mathbb{R}^{n},d_{\mathrm{euc.}},dx)
one has
d_{h}=n
and
d_{w}=2
. In the Sierpiński gasket fractal, one has
d_{h}=\frac{\ln 3}{\ln 2}
and
d_{w}=\frac{\ln 5}{\ln 2}
, see
[
2
]
. The condition
d_{h}<d_{w}
that we assume throughout this paper implies that the Brownian motion is a strongly recurrent Markov process which admits (Hölder continuous) local times, see
[
2
]
and references therein.

We now turn to a brief description of our stochastic model of interest (see Section
3
for a more rigorous definition). In a bounded open domain
U
of a recurrent metric space
(X,d,\mu)
, we study the parabolic Anderson model

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\Delta u(t,x)+\beta u(t,x)\,\dot{W}_{\alpha}(t,x),

(1.2)

where
\Delta
is the Laplacian on
U
and where the family of Gaussian noises
W_{\alpha}
is white in time and colored in space when
\alpha>0
, while for
\alpha=0
it is also white in space. The parameter
\beta>0
controls the intensity of the noise in the equation. Solutions are understood in the Itô-Skorohod sense. Both Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions on the boundary of
U
for the Laplacian
\Delta
are considered. In order to describe our main findings, let us delve deeper into the structure of our noise. Namely the spatial covariance of our noise is similar to the one introduced in
[
3
,
6
]
, as well as the covariance on Heisenberg groups handled in
[
4
,
7
]
. Roughly speaking, the covariance function of
\dot{W}_{\alpha}
is given by

\mathbf{E}\left[\dot{W}_{\alpha}(t,x)\dot{W}_{\alpha}(s,y)\right]=\delta_{0}(t-s)\,G_{2\alpha}(x,y),

where
\delta_{0}
is the Dirac delta at
0\in\mathbb{R}
and
G_{2\alpha}
is the kernel of the operator
(-\Delta)^{-2\alpha}
(notice that the definition of the Laplace operator, its powers
(-\Delta)^{-2\alpha}
as well as estimates on
G_{2\alpha}
will be spelled out in Section
2
).
In particular, one can distinguish three regimes:

(i)

For
0<\alpha<d_{h}/2d_{w}
, the noise is distributional with spatial covariance diagonal singularity of order
\frac{1}{d(x,y)^{d_{h}-2\alpha d_{w}}}
.

(ii)

For
\alpha=d_{h}/2d_{w}
, we have a log-correlated field which is distributional with spatial covariance diagonal singularity of order
|\ln d(x,y)|
.

(iii)

For
\alpha>d_{h}/2d_{w}
, the noise
W_{\alpha}([0,t),x)
can be defined pointwise and is Hölder continuous.

Observe that in the sequel we will mostly focus on cases where
W_{\alpha}
is distributional, corresponding to (i) and (ii) above.

With this brief description of
W_{\alpha}
in hand, our first objective is to find conditions on
\alpha
so that equation (
1.2
) admits a unique solution. Our main theorem in this direction can be informally summarized as follows (the reader is referred to Theorem
3.10
in the Dirichlet case and Theorem
4.5
in the Neumann case for a more rigorous version including the regularity assumptions on the domain under consideration).

Theorem 1.1
.

On any bounded open domain
U
of
(X,d,\mu)
, with either Neumann or Dirichlet boundary conditions, equation (
1.2
) admits a unique solution in the Itô sense for any value
\alpha\in[0,\infty)
. In addition, for
\alpha>d_{h}/2d_{w}
, the solution admits a Feynman-Kac representation.

We point out and stress that
\alpha
can be
0
in Theorem
1.1
, which means that the space-time white noise is admissible for our equation. This is a consequence of our assumption
d_{h}<d_{w}
, which implies that the Brownian motion on
X
is strongly recurrent. Now observe that this condition can be rewritten in terms of the so-called spectral dimension
d_{s}
, which measures a growth rate for the eigenvalues of the Laplace operator
\Delta
on
(X,d,\mu)
. Specifically the relation
d_{h}<d_{w}
can also be stated as
d_{s}<2
, where we recall that
d_{s}=\frac{2d_{h}}{d_{w}}
. As one can see from Theorem
1.1
, the space-time white noise is a proper driving noise whenever
d_{s}<2
. However, for
d_{s}>2
we expect that some conditions on
\alpha
will have to be imposed in order to solve (
1.2
). In fact based on our previous considerations in
[
7
, Section 1]
, we expect the condition on
\alpha
to be

\alpha>\frac{d_{s}}{4}-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}-1\right).

This type of hypothesis will be analyzed in a future work.

Once existence and uniqueness are proved, we turn our attention to estimating Lyapounov exponents associated with the moments of the solution. In this context it is worth separating Dirichlet from Neumann boundary conditions, which lead to quite different asymptotic behaviors in (
1.2
).

(a)

Dirichlet boundary conditions.

Whenever equation (
1.2
) is specified with Dirichlet boundary conditions and bounded initial conditions, our main upper bound for the large time asymptotics of the
p
-moments of order
p\geq 2
can be read as

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})}{t}\leq\frac{p}{2}\Theta_{\alpha}(\beta\sqrt{p-1})-p\lambda_{1},\hskip 10.00002ptx\in U,

(1.3)

where
\lambda_{1}>0
is the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
U
. Furthermore, recalling that the spectral dimension
d_{s}
is defined by
d_{s}=\frac{2d_{h}}{d_{w}}
, the quantity
\Theta_{\alpha}
in (
1.3
) is a continuous increasing function such that:

(i)

If
0\leq\alpha<\frac{d_{s}}{4}
, then
\Theta_{\alpha}(\beta)\sim_{\beta\to 0}C\beta^{2}
and
\Theta_{\alpha}(\beta)\sim_{\beta\to+\infty}C\beta^{\frac{4}{2(1+2\alpha)-d_{s}}}
.

(ii)

If
\alpha=\frac{d_{s}}{4}
, then
\Theta_{\alpha}(\beta)\sim_{\beta\to 0}C\beta^{2}
and
\Theta_{\alpha}(\beta)\sim_{\beta\to+\infty}C\beta^{2}(\ln\beta)^{2}
.

(iii)

If
\alpha>\frac{d_{s}}{4}
, then
\Theta_{\alpha}(\beta)=C\beta^{2}
.

While a variety of conclusions can be drawn from (
1.3
), let us focus on weak/strong disorder type interpretations according to the parameter
\beta
in (
1.2
). Indeed, the upper bound (
1.3
) illustrates competing effects. The dissipative effect of the Dirichlet semigroup controlled by
\lambda_{1}
induces an exponential decay for
u(t,x)
, while the noise whose intensity is controlled by
\beta
induces an exponential growth. This competing effect is further illustrated by the following lower bound proved in Theorem
3.15
:

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})}{t}\geq C\beta^{2}p(p-1)-p\lambda_{1}(A),\hskip 10.00002ptx\in A,

(1.4)

where
A\subset U
is an open set that does not intersect the boundary of
U
and
\lambda_{1}(A)
is the first Dirichlet eigenvalue for
-\Delta_{A}
. In particular, for any such fixed domain
A
there exist critical parameters
\beta_{c,1}>0
and
\beta_{c,2}>0
depending on
A
,
p
and
\alpha
such that uniformly on
A
we have

\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})}{t}<0\text{ if }\beta<\beta_{c,1},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})}{t}>0\text{ if }\beta>\beta_{c,2}.

For the parabolic Anderson model on a bounded interval, this interesting competing effect between the driving noise
W_{\alpha}
and the Dirichlet semigroup was already pointed out in
[
15
]
. We also note that the rates we obtain recover results from
[
22
]
for bounded intervals in the white noise case, see also
[
14
]
.

Remarkably, the lower bound (
1.4
) can be substantially improved in the range
0<\alpha<\frac{d_{s}}{4}
for the large
\beta
regime. More precisely, we obtain the following lower bounds whose exponents in (i) below match the upper bound (
1.3
):

(i)

For
0<\alpha<\frac{d_{s}}{4}
, we have

\liminf\limits_{\beta\to+\infty}\liminf\limits_{t\to+\infty}\beta^{-\frac{4}{2(1+2\alpha)-d_{s}}}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})}{t}\geq Cp(p-1).

(ii)

For
\alpha=\frac{d_{s}}{4}
, we get

\liminf\limits_{\beta\to+\infty}\liminf\limits_{t\to+\infty}\,\,(\beta^{2}\ln\beta)^{-1}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})}{t}\geq Cp(p-1).

Those bounds quantify both the strong disorder effect and the intermittency phenomenon for large values of
\beta
.

(b)

Neumann boundary conditions.

For the solution
u(t,x)
associated with Neumann boundary conditions, the heat semigroup is conservative and there is therefore nothing to counteract the excitability effect induced by the noise. In that case we find that

\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})}{t}\leq\frac{p}{2}\widehat{\Theta}_{\alpha}(\beta\sqrt{p-1}),

where
\widehat{\Theta}_{\alpha}
is a continuous increasing function which is comparable to
\Theta_{\alpha}
when
\beta\to 0
or
\beta\to+\infty
. The corresponding lower bounds on
p
-th order moments are given by

\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})}{t}\geq C\beta^{2}p(p-1),

which seems to indicate a strong disorder regime as soon as
\beta>0
and is due again to the fact that we are considering spectral dimensions
d_{s}<2
. Observe that we have improvements on those lower bounds as before for the large
\beta
’s regime in the range
0<\alpha\leq\frac{d_{s}}{4}
.

Concerning the exponents in both the Dirichlet and Neumann boundary case, let us note that in the context of a locally compact Hausdorff Abelian group
G
a near-dichotomy for the (expected square) energy of the solution
u
was discovered in
[
23
]
when the driving noise is white in both the time and space variable. Roughly speaking, the dichotomy says that, for large
\beta
(equivalently for low temperatures), the energy of the solution behaves like
\exp\{C\beta^{2}\}
when
G
is discrete (e.g.
G=\mathbf{Z}^{d}
or
(\mathbf{Z}/n\mathbf{Z})^{d}
) and is bounded from below by
\exp\{C\beta^{4}\}
when
G
is connected (e.g.
G=\mathbf{R}
or
[0,1])
. Our results suggest that in our setting in the range
0\leq\alpha<\frac{d_{s}}{4}
the energy behaves for large
\beta
like
\exp\left\{C\beta^{\frac{4}{2(1+2\alpha)-d_{s}}}\right\}
which demonstrates a rich variety of exponents for the class of models we are considering and illustrates their dependency in terms of both the noise regularity and the geometric environment.

Chaos expansions, Feynman-Kac representations and martingale type arguments are widely used tools in the study of parabolic Anderson models in Euclidean spaces. We will use all three of them in the current contribution, according to the values of the parameters in (
1.2
) and the kind of bound we are dealing with. Namely
our strategy to prove the upper bound (
1.3
) is to make use of the solution chaos expansion, together with the equivalence of
L^{p}
norms on chaos of fixed orders.
For the lower bound (
1.4
), we need to divide our analysis in two cases. If
\alpha>0
we use a Feynman-Kac representation of the moments, while for
\alpha=0
in the case
p=2
we use a rather direct martingale type argument based on the mild formulation of (
1.2
). Those basic ingredients are then combined with thorough analytic estimates involving the heat kernel (
1.1
). Notice that when
\alpha=0
, the Feynman-Kac representation for moments would involve the construction and study of mutual intersection local times for independent Brownian motions in recurrent metric measure spaces. This is a task which is interesting in its own right, and deferred to a later work.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we first present the general framework of a recurrent metric measure space
(X,d,\mu)
, equipped with a Dirichlet form with generator
\Delta
whose heat kernel satisfies the sub-Gaussian estimates (
1.1
). Given a bounded open set
U\subset X
, one can restrict the Dirichlet form to
U
using either Dirichlet or Neumann boundary condition and under mild regularity assumptions on the domain the restricted Dirichlet form has a heat kernel with sub-Gaussian type estimates, see Lierl
[
28
]
for the Dirichlet boundary case and Murugan
[
34
]
for the Neumann boundary case. This allows us to define the fractional operator
(-\Delta_{U})^{-\alpha}
where
\Delta_{U}
is the Laplacian on
U
with either Dirichlet or Neumann boundary condition. Estimates for the kernel of this operator are then obtained. In that setting, the driving noise to equation (
1.2
) is then defined as

W_{\alpha}(t,x)=(-\Delta_{U})^{-\alpha}W_{0}(t,x),

where
W_{0}(t,x)
is a space-time white noise on
U
and the expression
(-\Delta_{U})^{-\alpha}W_{0}(t,x)
is understood in a weak
L^{2}
sense which is made precise in the text. In order to show the scope of our results, we then present in detail three examples of spaces to which our results apply: bounded intervals of the real line, metric graphs and the Sierpiński gasket. In the case of Sierpiński gasket we exhibit in Section
3.7
a scale invariance property of the parabolic Anderson models. In Section
3
, we study equation (
1.2
) with Dirichlet boundary condition and prove the above mentioned results. Section
4
deals with the Neumann boundary case.

2
Preliminaries

This section is devoted to recall basic facts about metric spaces equipped with a Dirichlet form. We also prove some crucial heat kernel estimates and give some details about powers of the Laplace operator and associated fractional Riesz kernels in this context, with different types of boundary conditions. Then we shall describe three examples of relevant applications of our abstract setting.

2.1
Recurrent metric measure spaces with sub-Gaussian heat kernel estimates

Our considerations will be based on a locally compact complete geodesic metric measure space
(X,d,\mu)
, where
\mu
is a Radon measure supported on
X
. We will denote
\mathrm{diam}(X)\in(0,+\infty]
the diameter of
X
, which can possibly be infinite.

The space
(X,d,\mu)
is equipped with a Dirichlet form. That is a densely defined closed symmetric form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}:=\rm{dom}\,\mathcal{E})
on
L^{2}(X,\mu)
. A function
v\colon X\to\mathbb{R}
is called a normal contraction of the function
u
if for
\mu
-almost every
x,y\in X
,

|v(x)-v(y)|\leq|u(x)-u(y)|,\hskip 20.00003pt\text{and}\hskip 20.00003pt|v(x)|\leq|u(x)|.

The form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
is called a Dirichlet form if it is Markovian, that is, it has the property that if
u\in\mathcal{F}
and
v
is a normal contraction of
u
then
v\in\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{E}(v,v)\leq\mathcal{E}(u,u)
.
Some basic properties of Dirichlet forms are collected in
[
16
, Theorem 1.4.2]
. In particular, we note that
\mathcal{F}\cap L^{\infty}(X,\mu)
is an algebra and
\mathcal{F}
is a Hilbert space equipped with the
\mathcal{E}_{1}
-norm defined as

\|f\|_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}:=\left(\|f\|_{L^{2}(X,\mu)}^{2}+\mathcal{E}(f,f)\right)^{1/2}.

(2.1)

In the sequel it will be convenient to express Dirichlet forms as integrals. This is usually true in the so-called
regular
setting that we proceed to recall now. Denoting by
C_{c}(X)
the space of continuous functions with compact support in
X
, we recall that a core for
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
is a subset
\mathcal{C}\subseteq C_{c}(X)\cap\mathcal{F}
which is dense in
C_{c}(X)
in the supremum norm and dense in
\mathcal{F}
in the
\mathcal{E}_{1}
-norm.

Definition 2.1
.

A Dirichlet form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
on
L^{2}(X,\mu)
is called regular if it admits a core.

In addition, given the regular Dirichlet form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
, for every
u,v\in\mathcal{F}\cap L^{\infty}(X)
one can define the energy measure
\Gamma(u,v)
through the formula

\displaystyle\int_{X}\phi\,d\Gamma(u,v)=\frac{1}{2}[\mathcal{E}(\phi u,v)+\mathcal{E}(\phi v,u)-\mathcal{E}(\phi,uv)],\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for\ all}\ \phi\in\mathcal{F}\cap C_{c}(X).

(2.2)

The latter definition of
\Gamma(u,v)
can be extended to any
u,v\in\mathcal{F}
by truncation. According to Beurling-Deny’s theorem
[
16
]
, for regular Dirichlet forms the quantity
\Gamma(u,v)
is a signed Radon measure for any
u,v\in\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{E}(u,v)
can be decomposed as

\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\int_{X}d\Gamma(u,v).

For
u\in\mathcal{F}
,
\Gamma(u,u)
is called the energy measure of
u
.
Another useful property of Dirichlet form, which will be invoked in this paper, is locality. We recall the definition below for further use.

Definition 2.2
.

(
[
16
, p.6]
)
A Dirichlet form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
is called
strongly local
if for any
u,v\in\mathcal{F}
with compact supports such that
u
is constant in a
neighborhood of the support of
v
, it holds that
\mathcal{E}(u,v)=0
.

Related to a Dirichlet form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
, there is a notion of generator and semigroup. The generator
\Delta
is defined using an integration by part formula, namely
\Delta
is defined on a dense domain
\mathrm{dom}(\Delta)
such that for every
f\in\mathcal{F}
and
g\in\mathrm{dom}(\Delta)
,

\mathcal{E}(f,g)=-\int_{X}f\Delta g\,d\mu.

We denote
\{P_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
the self-adjoint semigroup on
L^{2}(X,\mu)
associated with the Dirichlet space
(X,\mu,\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
. Using spectral theory this semigroup can be defined as
P_{t}=e^{t\Delta}
; see
[
16
, Section 1.4]
) for further details.

With those general notions in hand, let us summarize the assumptions on our metric space and its Dirichlet form that will prevail throughout the paper. Note that they are not independent (some may be derived from combinations of the others) and the list is chosen for comprehension rather than minimality.

Assumption 2.3
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be the locally compact complete geodesic metric measure space introduced above, together with its Dirichlet form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
. In the sequel we assume the following:

(i)

The measure
\mu
is Ahlfors
d_{h}
-regular, i.e., there exist
c_{1},c_{2},d_{h}\in(0,\infty)
such that for any
x\in X
and any
r\in\bigl(0,\mathrm{diam}(X)\bigr)
we have

c_{1}r^{d_{h}}\leq\mu\bigl(B(x,r)\bigr)\leq c_{2}r^{d_{h}}.

(ii)

\mathcal{E}
is a regular and strongly local Dirichlet form.

(iii)

The semigroup
\{P_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
is stochastically complete, i.e.,
P_{t}1=1
for every
t\geq 0
.

(iv)

The semigroup
\{P_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
has a continuous heat kernel
p_{t}(x,y)
satisfying sub-Gaussian estimates,
that is, for some
c_{3},c_{4},c_{5},c_{6}\in(0,\infty)
and
d_{w}\in[2,+\infty)
,

c_{5}t^{-d_{h}/d_{w}}\exp\biggl(-c_{6}\Bigl(\frac{d(x,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\biggr)\leq p_{t}(x,y)\leq c_{3}t^{-d_{h}/d_{w}}\exp\biggl(-c_{4}\Bigl(\frac{d(x,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\biggr)

(2.3)

for
\mu\!\times\!\mu
-a.e.
(x,y)\in X\times X
and each
t\in\bigl(0,\mathrm{diam}(X)^{1/d_{w}})
.

(v)

We have
d_{h}<d_{w}
.

Observe that in Assumption
2.3
, the parameter
d_{h}
is the Hausdorff dimension and the parameter
d_{w}
is called the walk dimension. Also note that when
d_{w}=2
, which is possible in our setting, we simply have Gaussian estimates.

A fundamental theorem in the theory of Dirichlet forms, see
[
16
, Theorem 7.2.1 and Theorem 7.2.2]
, is that in such a setting one can construct a Brownian motion
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
X
as the continuous Hunt process with semigroup
P_{t}
. Otherwise stated, for every bounded and Borel function
f
and
x\in X
we have

\mathbf{E}_{x}(f(B_{t}))=P_{t}f(x).

Notice that the condition
d_{h}<d_{w}
in Assumption
2.3
is known as the recurrence condition. It implies in particular (see Corollary 3.29 in
[
2
]
) that the Brownian motion
(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a strongly recurrent Markov process: for every
x,y\in X
,

\mathbf{P}_{x}(\exists\,t\geq 0\text{ such that }B_{t}=y)=1.

The recurrence condition also implies that functions in the domain
\mathcal{F}
have a continuous version. We note that our assumptions put us in the framework of Barlow’s fractional spaces, which were introduced and studied in
[
2
]
.

2.2
Dirichlet boundary conditions

This section specializes our setting to Dirichlet boundary conditions on a bounded set
U
. We first derive some bounds on the related semigroup. Then we will define fractional powers of the Laplacian on
U
.

2.2.1
Heat semigroup with Dirichlet boundary conditions

One of our goals in this note is to deal with stochastic heat equations on bounded domains of our metric space
X
. Let us thus consider a non-empty bounded open set
U
whose closure
\overline{U}
is compact. We will localize the notions of Dirichlet form and related objects to functions defined on
U
.
The domain of our localized Dirichlet form will be called
\mathcal{F}(U)
. It is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(U)=\left\{u\in L^{2}(U,\mu);\text{there\ exists}\ f\in\mathcal{F}\ \text{such\ that}\ f|_{U}=u\ \text{a.e.}\right\}.

(2.4)

Notice that for every
u\in\mathcal{F}(U)
the localized energy measure in (
2.2
) is simply given by
d\Gamma(u,u)=d\Gamma(f,f)
.

In order to introduce Dirichlet boundary conditions, we now restrict our Dirichlet form. That is, define

\mathcal{F}_{c}(U)=\left\{u\in\mathcal{F}(U);\text{ the essential support of }u\text{ is compact in }U\right\}.

Then the domain
\mathcal{F}^{D}(U)
will be the closure of
\mathcal{F}_{c}(U)
for the norm
\mathcal{E}_{1}
defined by (
2.1
). For
u,v\in\mathcal{F}^{D}(U)
we set

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{D}_{U}(u,v):=\mathcal{E}(u,v).

(2.5)

Notice that
(\mathcal{E}^{D}_{U},\mathcal{F}^{D}(U))
is a Dirichlet form and the functions in
\mathcal{F}^{D}(U)
satisfy the Dirichlet boundary condition. The generator
\Delta_{D,U}
of the Dirichlet form
(\mathcal{E}^{D}_{U},\mathcal{F}^{D}(U))
is called the Dirichlet Laplacian on
U
. We refer to
[
17
]
for more details on this construction, which we stress does not require any
smoothness
assumption on the boundary of
U
. In particular, see
[
16
, Theorem 4.4.2]
, the semigroup
P^{D,U}_{t}
with generator
\Delta_{D,U}
can be represented for
x\in U
as

\displaystyle P^{D,U}_{t}f(x)=\mathbf{E}_{x}(f(B_{t})1_{t<T_{U}})\,,

(2.6)

where
B_{t}
is a Brownian motion on
X
and
T_{U}
is the hitting time of the complement of
U
by
B_{t}
.

The construction of
P^{D,U}
in (
2.6
) is quite general. However, proper bounds on the heat kernel
p^{D,U}
corresponding to
P^{D,U}
are available when
U
is an inner uniform domain. We now define this type of domain. To begin with, we consider a connected set
U
. Then
U
admits an inner metric:

\displaystyle d_{U}(x,y)=\inf\left\{\mathrm{length}(\gamma);\,\gamma:[0,1]\to U\text{ rectifiable, }\gamma(0)=x\text{ and }\gamma(1)=y\right\}.

(2.7)

The following straightforward lemma will be useful.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
U\subset X
be an open set. Assume that
x\in U
and
r>0
are such that
B(x,r)\subset U
. Then we have
B_{U}(x,r)=B(x,r)
, where
B_{U}(x,r)
denotes the ball with center
x
and radius
r
for the distance
d_{U}
.

Proof.

Since
d\leq d_{U}
we have always have
B_{U}(x,r)\subset B(x,r)
. Now, assume that
B(x,r)\subset U
and let
y\in B(x,r)
. Since the space
(X,d)
is assumed to be geodesic, there exists a geodesic
\gamma
connecting
x
to
y
. This geodesic is included in
B(x,r)\subset U
so that
d_{U}(x,y)\leq\mathrm{length}(\gamma)=d(x,y)\leq r
.
∎

We denote by
\widetilde{U}
the completion of an open set
U
for
d_{U}
. With those preliminary notions in hand, we can now define inner uniform domains.

Definition 2.5
.

Consider an open, connected, bounded set
U
whose closure is compact. Let
c\in(0,1)
,
C>1
. Let
\gamma:[\alpha,\beta]\to U
be a rectifiable curve. We say that
\gamma
is a
(c,C)
inner uniform curve in
U
if the following two conditions are satisfied:

(i)

For all
t\in[\alpha,\beta]
,
d_{U}(\gamma(t),\widetilde{U}\setminus U)\geq c\min\{d_{U}(\gamma(\alpha),\gamma(t)),d_{U}(\gamma(t),\gamma(\beta))\}
,

(ii)

\mathrm{length}(\gamma)\leq Cd_{U}(\gamma(\alpha),\gamma(\beta))
.

Then the domain
U
is called a
(c,C)
inner uniform domain if any two points in
U
can be joined by a
(c,C)
inner uniform curve in
U
. The domain
U
is simply called inner uniform if there exist
c\in(0,1),C>1
such that
U
is
(c,C)
inner uniform.

For example, in Euclidean spaces, any bounded convex domain is inner uniform. Any bounded domain with piecewise smooth boundary with a finite number of singularities and non-zero interior angle at each of the singularities is also inner uniform, see Example 3.5 in
[
29
]
. The class of inner uniform domains is actually very wide and also includes sets with very rough boundaries like the Koch snowflake, see Section 6.5 in
[
17
]
. We refer to
[
17
,
28
,
29
]
for examples and further discussions about inner uniform domains. In our setting of recurrent metric measure spaces, further examples of (inner) uniform domains will be addressed in sections
2.4.1
,
2.4.2
and
2.4.3
.

Furthermore, for inner uniform domains we have the following upper and lower bounds on the heat kernel that follow from
[
28
, Theorem 3.3]
.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
U
be an inner uniform domain as introduced in Definition
2.5
. Then the Dirichlet form
(\mathcal{E}^{D}_{U},\mathcal{F}^{D}(U))
given by (
2.5
) is a strongly local regular Dirichlet form which admits a continuous symmetric heat kernel
p_{t}^{D,U}(x,y)
. Moreover, if
\Phi_{1}
is the first eigenfunction of
-\Delta_{D,U}
and
\lambda_{1}>0
is the corresponding principal eigenvalue, then the Dirichlet heat kernel
p_{t}^{D,U}(x,y)
satisfies the sub-Gaussian upper estimate

p_{t}^{D,U}(x,y)\leq\frac{c_{1}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}}{\min(1,t^{\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}})}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{2}\Bigl(\frac{d(x,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr),

(2.8)

as well as the sub-Gaussian lower estimate

p_{t}^{D,U}(x,y)\geq\frac{c_{3}\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{1}(y)e^{-\lambda_{1}t}}{\min(1,t^{\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}})}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{4}\Bigl(\frac{d_{U}(x,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr),

(2.9)

for every
(x,y)\in U\times U
and
t\in\bigl(0,+\infty)
, where we recall that
d_{U}
is defined by (
2.7
).

Remark 2.7
.

The proof of the upper bound (
2.8
) in small times comes from the upper bound in (
2.3
), combined with the estimate

p_{t}^{D,U}(x,y)\leq p_{t}(x,y).

Notice that this last relation is a straightforward consequence of (
2.6
). In large times, relation (
2.8
) follows from spectral theory, see (
2.26
) below. The lower bound (
2.9
) in small times follows from the stronger result

\displaystyle p_{t}^{D,U}(x,y)\geq\frac{c_{3}\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{1}(y)}{\mu(B_{U}(x,t^{1/d_{w}}))}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{4}\Bigl(\frac{d_{U}(x,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr)

(2.10)

which is stated in
[
28
, Theorem 3.3]
. Here
B_{U}
denotes the inner ball, i.e., the ball in
\tilde{U}
for the distance
d_{U}
. Since
d\leq d_{U}
we have
B_{U}(x,t^{1/d_{w}})\subset B(x,t^{1/d_{w}})
so that owing to our Assumption
2.3
, the Ahlfors regularity of
\mu
implies that
\mu(B_{U}(x,t^{1/d_{w}}))\leq ct^{d_{h}/d_{w}}
.
In large times, the lower bound (
2.9
) follows from spectral theory, see (
2.26
) below.

Remark 2.8
.

It follows from
[
29
, Lemma 7.13]
that the first eigenfunction
\Phi_{1}
is positive on
U
. Since it is moreover continuous, if
U
is an inner uniform domain and
A\subset U
is such that
\overline{A}\subset U
, then

\hat{m}_{A}:=\inf\left\{\Phi_{1}(y);y\in A\right\}>0.

(2.11)

This information will feature prominently in the application of (
2.9
).

For the remainder of this subsection, we assume that
U
is inner uniform so that the above heat kernel estimates (
2.8
) and (
2.9
) are valid. The following corollaries of Theorem
2.6
will be useful later.

Corollary 2.9
.

Let
U\subset X
be an inner uniform domain given as in Definition
2.5
. Let
x\in U
and
r>0
such that
B(x,2r)\subset U
. There exists a constant
c>0
(depending on
r
and
x
) such that for every
t\geq 0
and
y\in B(x,r/2)
, the semigroup
P^{D,U}
represented by (
2.6
) verifies

\displaystyle\left(P_{t}^{D,U}1_{B(x,r)}\right)\!(y)\geq ce^{-\lambda_{1}t}.

(2.12)

Proof.

We first notice that since we assume
B(x,2r)\subset U
, we have from Lemma
2.4
,
B(x,r)=B_{U}(x,r)
and
B(x,r/2)=B_{U}(x,r/2)
; this will be used without further notice in the proof below.
From the heat kernel lower bound in Theorem
2.6
and the strict positivity and continuity of
\Phi_{1}
, one has for
y\in B_{U}(x,r/2)
,

\displaystyle\left(P_{t}^{D,U}1_{B_{U}(x,r)}\right)\!(y)\geq\int_{B_{U}(x,r)}\frac{c_{3}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}}{\min(1,t^{\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}})}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{4}\Bigl(\frac{d_{U}(z,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr)d\mu(z).

(2.13)

Assume first
t^{1/d_{w}}\geq r/2
. Then it is readily checked that for every
y\in B_{U}(x,r/2)
and
z\in B_{U}(x,r)
we have

\left(\frac{d_{U}(z,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\right)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\leq c_{5}.

(2.14)

Plugging this inequality into (
2.13
) we obtain that for every
y\in B_{U}(x,r/2)
,

\displaystyle\left(P_{t}^{D,U}1_{B_{U}(x,r)}\right)(y)\geq c_{6}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}.

(2.15)

Assume now
t^{1/d_{w}}\leq r/2
. We have then from (
2.10
) for every
y\in B_{U}(x,r/2)

\begin{split}\left(P_{t}^{D,U}1_{B_{U}(x,r)}\right)(y)\geq&\int_{B_{U}(x,r)}\frac{c_{5}}{\mu(B_{U}(y,t^{1/d_{w}}))}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{4}\Bigl(\frac{d_{U}(z,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr)d\mu(z)\\
\geq&\int_{B_{U}(y,r/2)}\frac{c_{5}}{\mu(B_{U}(y,t^{1/d_{w}}))}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{4}\Bigl(\frac{d_{U}(z,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr)d\mu(z)\\
\geq&\int_{B_{U}(y,t^{1/d_{w}})}\frac{c_{5}}{\mu(B_{U}(y,t^{1/d_{w}}))}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{4}\Bigl(\frac{d_{U}(z,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr)d\mu(z).\end{split}

(2.16)

Now if
z\in B_{U}(y,t^{1/d_{w}})
, one can see that the exponential term in the right hand-side of (
2.16
) is lower bounded by a constant.
Reporting these considerations into (
2.16
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left(P_{t}^{D,U}1_{B_{U}(x,r)}\right)(y)\geq c_{8}.

(2.17)

Gathering (
2.15
) and (
2.17
), the proof of our claim (
2.12
) is achieved.
∎

The general bound (
2.12
) can be generalized to a wider class of domains satisfying a cone type condition. We state this property in a separate corollary.

Corollary 2.10
.

Under the same conditions as in Corollary
2.9
, let
A\subset U
be an open set such that
\overline{A}\subset U
. Assume that there exist
\delta>0
,
r_{0}>0
such that for all
y\in A
,

\displaystyle\mu(B_{U}(y,r)\cap A)\geq\delta r^{d_{h}},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for\ all}\ \ 0\leq r\leq r_{0}.

(2.18)

Then, there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
(depending on
\delta
and
r_{0}
) such that for every
t\geq 0
and
y\in A
,

\displaystyle\left(P_{t}^{D,U}1_{A}\right)(y)\geq c_{1}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}.

(2.19)

and

\displaystyle\int_{A}p_{t}^{D,U}(y,z)^{2}d\mu(z)\geq c_{2}\frac{e^{-2\lambda_{1}t}}{t^{d_{h}/d_{w}}}.

(2.20)

Proof.

Similarly to (
2.13
), from the heat kernel lower bound in Theorem
2.6
and Remark
2.8
, one has for
y\in A
,

\displaystyle(P_{t}^{D,U}1_{A})(y)\geq\int_{A}\frac{c_{1}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}}{\min(1,t^{\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}})}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{2}\Bigl(\frac{d_{U}(z,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr)d\mu(z).

Let us assume first that
0<t\leq r_{0}^{d_{w}}
, where
r_{0}
is the constant in relation (
2.18
). In that case we first get the trivial lower bound

\displaystyle(P_{t}^{D,U}1_{A})(y)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{B_{U}(y,t^{1/d_{w}})\cap A}\frac{c_{1}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}}{\min(1,t^{\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}})}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{2}\Bigl(\frac{d_{U}(z,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr)d\mu(z).

Next on
B_{U}(y,t^{1/d_{w}})
it is readily checked that
d_{U}(z,y)^{d_{w}}\leq t
. Hence one can bound the sub-Gaussian term above by
e^{-c_{2}}
. Therefore we obtain

\displaystyle(P_{t}^{D,U}1_{A})(y)\geq c_{3}\frac{\mu(B_{U}(y,t^{1/d_{w}})\cap A)}{\min(1,t^{\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}})}\,e^{-\lambda_{1}t}\geq c_{4}e^{-\lambda_{1}t},

where we have invoked condition (
2.18
) for the last inequality.
On the other hand, if
t\geq r_{0}^{d_{w}}
then our general bound (
2.15
) already implies
(P_{t}^{D,U}1_{A})(y)\geq c_{5}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}
so that the conclusion (
2.19
) easily follows. The proof of the estimate (
2.20
) is similar and left to the reader.
∎

Remark 2.11
.

The condition (
2.18
) can be checked in many examples and essentially means that the set
A
has a nice boundary (no
cusps
). For instance, if
X=\mathbb{R}
and
U=(0,1)
then any subinterval
A=(a,b)\subsetneq U
satisfies (
2.18
) with
\delta=1/2
. In the Sierpiński gasket example which is introduced with notations in Section
2.4.3
one can see that if
U=K\setminus V_{0}
, then any finite union of sets of the form
\mathfrak{f}_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathfrak{f}_{i_{n}}(U)
satisfies (
2.18
).

We now state a matching upper bound for (
2.20
), valid on inner uniform domains.

Corollary 2.12
.

Consider an inner uniform domain
U\subset X
, as given in Definition
2.5
. Let
p^{D,U}
be the kernel introduced in Theorem
2.6
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
x\in U
,

\displaystyle\int_{U}p_{t}^{D,U}(x,y)^{2}d\mu(y)\leq\left\{\begin{aligned} &Ct^{-\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}},&0<t<1,\\
&Ce^{-2\lambda_{1}t},&t\geq 1.\end{aligned}\right.

(2.21)

Therefore, if
0<\gamma<\lambda_{1}
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for every
t>0
,
x\in U
,

\int_{U}p_{t}^{D,U}(x,y)^{2}d\mu(y)\leq C\frac{e^{-2\gamma t}}{t^{\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}}}.

Proof.

As mentioned in Theorem
2.6
, the kernel
p_{t}^{D,U}
is symmetric. Hence the semigroup property yields

\displaystyle\int_{U}p_{t}^{D,U}(x,y)^{2}d\mu(y)=p_{2t}^{D,U}(x,x).

(2.22)

The result therefore easily follows from the upper bound (
2.8
).
∎

The upper bound in Corollary
2.6
implies an exponential decay for the semigroup
P^{D,U}_{t}
, which we label for further use.

Corollary 2.13
.

Let
P_{t}^{D,U}
be the semigroup defined by (
2.6
) for an inner uniform domain
U
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for every
t\geq 0
, and
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(U,\mu)
,

\left\|P^{D,U}_{t}u_{0}\right\|_{\infty}\leq Ce^{-\lambda_{1}t}\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}.

Proof.

We divide again the proof into a small time and large time estimate. That is for
0\leq t\leq 1
, one simply resorts to (
2.6
) in order to write

\displaystyle\left\|P^{D,U}_{t}u_{0}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}.

(2.23)

For
t>1
, we first apply the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to get

\displaystyle\left|P^{D,U}_{t}u_{0}(x)\right|^{2}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(U,\mu)}^{2}\int_{U}p^{D,U}_{t}(x,y)^{2}d\mu(y).

(2.24)

Next in order to estimate the right hand-side of (
2.24
) we recall (see Definition
2.5
) that
U
is bounded. Hence
\mu(U)<\infty
and
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(U,\mu)}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}
. Furthermore the integral
\int_{U}p^{D,U}(x,y)^{2}\mu(dy)
is upper bounded thanks to (
2.21
) for
t\geq 1
. Gathering this information into (
2.24
) we end up with

\displaystyle\left|P_{t}^{D,U}u_{0}(x)\right|^{2}\leq Ce^{-2\lambda_{1}t}\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}.

(2.25)

We now conclude our proof by putting together the estimate (
2.23
) for
0\leq t<1
and the bound (
2.25
) for
t\geq 1
.
∎

2.2.2
Fractional Laplacian

As mentioned in the introduction, the correlation of our noise
W_{\alpha}
is generated by the operator
(-\Delta)^{-2\alpha}
. We now recall how to define this operator through spectral theory. We first introduce a set of notation for the spectrum of
\Delta_{D,U}
.

Notation 2.14
.

Let
U
be an inner uniform domain and recall that
\Delta_{D,U}
is the generator of the Dirichlet form defined by (
2.5
). The operator
-\Delta_{D,U}
admits a discrete spectrum

\{\lambda_{j};j\geq 1\},\text{with}\ \lambda_{1}>0\ \text{and}\ \lambda_{j}\leq\lambda_{j+1},

together with an orthonormal basis
\{\Phi_{j};j\geq 1\}
of eigenfunctions such that
\Phi_{j}\in\mathcal{D}(\Delta_{D,U})
and
\Delta_{D,U}\Phi_{j}=-\lambda_{j}\Phi_{j}.

Let us also label a lemma which will ease our estimates on the covariance function.

Lemma 2.15
.

Under the condition of Notation
2.14
, the Dirichlet heat kernel
p_{t}^{D,U}
introduced in Theorem
2.6
admits the following convergent spectral expansion for all
t>0
and
x,y\in U
:

p_{t}^{D,U}(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}e^{-\lambda_{j}t}\Phi_{j}(x)\Phi_{j}(y).

(2.26)

Moreover, for every
\alpha>d_{h}/d_{w}
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{\alpha}}<+\infty.

(2.27)

Proof.

The decomposition (
2.26
) is classical for heat kernels on bounded spaces, see Theorem 3.44 in
[
2
]
. The upper bound (
2.27
) stems from diagonal estimates of
p_{t}^{D,U}
together with Tauberian type theorems (see
[
13
]
).
∎

The preliminaries above allow for a proper definition of negative power of the Laplace operator.

Definition 2.16

(Fractional Laplacians)
.

We still work under the setting of Notation
2.14
, and let
\alpha\geq 0
. For
f\in L^{2}(U,\mu)
, the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}
acting on
f
is defined as

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{\alpha}}\left(\int_{U}\Phi_{j}(y)f(y)d\mu(y)\right)\Phi_{j}(x).

(2.28)

Remark 2.17
.

From the definition (
2.28
), it is clear that
(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}:L^{2}(U,\mu)\to L^{2}(U,\mu)
is a bounded operator. More precisely, for a generic
f\in L^{2}(U,\mu)
one has

\|(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}f\|_{L^{2}(U,\mu)}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}^{-2\alpha}\langle f,\Phi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(U,\mu)}^{2}\leq\lambda_{1}^{-2\alpha}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\langle f,\Phi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(U,\mu)}^{2}=\lambda_{1}^{-2\alpha}\|f\|_{L^{2}(U,\mu)}^{2}.

Therefore we get the upper bound
\|(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(U,\mu)\to L^{2}(U,\mu)}\leq\lambda_{1}^{-\alpha}
.

The existence of a kernel for
(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}
is a consequence of functional calculus considerations. We now make this notion more precise following
[
3
]
. To begin with, we introduce the space of Schwartz functions on
U
.

Definition 2.18
.

Let
\{\Phi_{n};n\geq 1\}
be the set of eigenfunctions introduced in Notation
2.14
. Then we define the Schwartz space of test functions on
U
as

\mathcal{S}_{D}(U)=\left\{f\in L^{2}(U,\mu);\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{N},\lim_{n\to+\infty}n^{k}\left|\int_{U}\Phi_{n}(y)f(y)d\mu(y)\right|=0\right\}.

The following lemma, borrowed from
[
3
, Lemma 2.9]
and
[
3
, Corollary 2.11]
, identifies a kernel for the operator
(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}
.

Lemma 2.19
.

Under the setting of Notation
2.14
, let
(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}
be the operator given in Definition
2.16
. Then
(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}
admits a kernel
G^{D,U}_{\alpha}
which can be expressed as

\displaystyle G_{\alpha}^{D,U}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}t^{\alpha-1}p_{t}^{D,U}(x,y)dt,\hskip 10.00002ptx,y\in U,x\neq y.

(2.29)

where
p_{t}^{D,U}
is the heat kernel in Theorem
2.6
. More specifically, for
f\in\mathcal{S}_{D}(U)
and
y\in U
we have

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}f(y)=\int_{U}f(x)G^{D,U}_{\alpha}(x,y)d\mu(x).

(2.30)

For
f,g\in\mathcal{S}_{D}(U)
we can also write

\displaystyle\int_{U}(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}f(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}gd\mu=\int_{U}\int_{U}f(x)g(y)G^{D,U}_{2\alpha}(x,y)d\mu(x)d\mu(y).

(2.31)

Remark 2.20
.

Notice that owing to the sub-Gaussian upper estimate (
2.8
), the integral (
2.29
) defining
G_{\alpha}^{D,U}
is always convergent for
\alpha>0
and
x\not=y
.

In order to get optimal conditions for the existence-uniqueness of the stochastic PDE (
1.2
), it is important to have precise bounds on the kernel
G_{\alpha}^{D,U}
. The proposition below takes care of this step.

Proposition 2.21
.

For an inner uniform domain
U
, let
G^{D,U}_{\alpha}
be the kernel defined by (
2.29
). Then the following bounds hold true:

(i)

If
0<\alpha<d_{h}/d_{w}
, there exist constants
c,C>0
such that for every
x,y\in U
, with
x\neq y
,

\displaystyle c\frac{\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{1}(y)}{d_{U}(x,y)^{d_{h}-\alpha d_{w}}}\leq G_{\alpha}^{D,U}(x,y)\leq\frac{C}{d(x,y)^{d_{h}-\alpha d_{w}}}.

(2.32)

(ii)

If
\alpha=d_{h}/d_{w}
, there exist constants
c,C>0
such that for every
x,y\in U
, with
x\neq y

\displaystyle c\,\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{1}(y)\max(1,|\ln d_{U}(x,y)|)\leq G_{\alpha}^{D,U}(x,y)\leq C\max(1,|\ln d(x,y)|).

(2.33)

(iii)

If
\alpha>d_{h}/d_{w}
, there exist constants
c,C>0
such that for every
x,y\in U
,

\displaystyle c\,\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{1}(y)\leq G_{\alpha}^{D,U}(x,y)\leq C.

(2.34)

Proof.

The proof of the upper estimates is similar to the proof of
[
6
, Proposition 2.6]
for the Neumann fractional Riesz kernel and we omit the details here. We will thus handle the lower bounds in (
2.32
), (
2.33
) and (
2.34
), focusing on the three regimes for
\alpha
.

Case
0<\alpha<d_{h}/d_{w}
. We plug relation (
2.9
) into the definition (
2.29
) of
G^{D,U}_{\alpha}
. Lower bounding integrals over
(0,\infty)
by integrals over
(0,1)
and writing
\min(1,t^{d_{h}/d_{w}})=t^{d_{h}/d_{w}}
for
t\in(0,1)
, we get

G_{\alpha}^{D,U}(x,y)\geq c_{3}\,\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{1}(y)\int_{0}^{1}t^{\alpha-1-\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{4}\Bigl(\frac{d_{U}(x,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr)dt.

(2.35)

We then perform the elementary change of variable
r=t/d_{U}(x,y)^{d_{w}}
in the integral above. We end up with

G_{\alpha}^{D,U}(x,y)\geq c\frac{\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{1}(y)}{d_{U}(x,y)^{d_{h}-\alpha d_{w}}},

(2.36)

which is the lower bound in (
2.32
).

Case
\alpha>d_{h}/d_{w}
. We proceed as in the previous case. However, we now bound integrals on
(0,\infty)
by integrals on
(1,\infty)
and write
\min(1,t^{d_{h}/d_{w}})=1
for
t\in(1,\infty)
. We easily get

G_{\alpha}^{D,U}(x,y)\geq c_{3}\,\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{1}(y)\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}dt\geq c\,\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{1}(y).

(2.37)

That is the lower bound in (
2.34
) is achieved.

Case
\alpha=d_{h}/d_{w}
. As in the previous steps, we plug the lower bound (
2.9
) into (
2.29
). Restricting the integral to
(0,1)
and using the relation
\alpha=d_{h}/d_{w}
we discover that

\displaystyle G_{\alpha}^{D,U}(x,y)\geq c_{3}\,\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{1}(y)\int_{0}^{1}t^{-1}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{4}\Bigl(\frac{d_{U}(x,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr)dt.

Next the change of variable
u=t/d_{U}(x,y)^{d_{w}}
yields

\displaystyle G_{\alpha}^{D,U}(x,y)\geq c_{3}\,\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{1}(y)\int_{0}^{1/d_{U}(x,y)^{d_{w}}}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{4}\Bigl(\frac{1}{u}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr)\frac{du}{u}.

(2.38)

In order to avoid the singularity at
0
in the integral above, let us introduce the diameter
\widetilde{R}
of
U
with respect to the distance
d_{U}
. Then from (
2.38
) one sees that

\displaystyle G_{\alpha}^{D,U}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq c_{3}\,\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{1}(y)\int_{1/(2\widetilde{R})^{d_{w}}}^{1/d_{U}(x,y)^{d_{w}}}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{4}\Bigl(\frac{1}{u}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr)\frac{du}{u}

(2.39)

\displaystyle\geq c\,\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{1}(y)\int_{1/(2\widetilde{R})^{d_{w}}}^{1/d_{U}(x,y)^{d_{w}}}\frac{du}{u}

(2.40)

\displaystyle=c\,d_{w}\,\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{1}(y)(\ln(2\widetilde{R})-\ln d_{U}(x,y)).

(2.41)

When
x,y
are close to eah other, the dominant term in the right hand-side of (
2.41
) is
c\,d_{w}\,\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{1}(y)|\ln d_{U}(x,y)|
, while for
x,y
further apart a trivial lower bound is of the form
c\,\Phi_{1}(x)\Phi_{1}(y)
. We thus conclude the proof of (
2.33
).
∎

The following estimate is the main technical lemma we will be using to estimate solutions of the stochastic heat equation.

Lemma 2.22
.

Let
\alpha\geq 0
, and consider an inner uniform domain
U
. Recall that the operator
(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}
in Definition
2.16
admits the kernel
G_{\alpha}^{D,U}
given by (
2.29
). Then there exists a constant
C
such that for every
t>0

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in U}\|(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}p_{t}^{D,U}(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(U,\mu)}\leq\begin{cases}Ce^{-\lambda_{1}t}(1+t^{\alpha-\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}}),\quad 0\leq\alpha<\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}},\\
Ce^{-\lambda_{1}t}|\ln\left(\min(1/2,t)\right)|,\quad\alpha=\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}},\\
Ce^{-\lambda_{1}t},\quad\alpha>\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}.\end{cases}

(2.42)

Proof.

The case
\alpha=0
follows from Corollary
2.12
, so we assume
\alpha>0
. From relation (
2.29
) and the semigroup property for
p_{t}
, one has for every
t>0

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}p_{t}^{D,U}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{U}G_{\alpha}^{D,U}(y,z)p_{t}^{D,U}(x,z)d\mu(z)

(2.43)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{U}s^{\alpha-1}p_{s}^{D,U}(y,z)p_{t}^{D,U}(x,z)d\mu(z)ds

(2.44)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}s^{\alpha-1}p_{s+t}^{D,U}(x,y)ds.

(2.45)

Applying Minkowski’s inequality to the right hand-side of (
2.45
), we get

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}p_{t}^{D,U}(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(U,\mu)}\leq\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}s^{\alpha-1}\|p_{s+t}^{D,U}(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(U,\mu)}ds.

(2.46)

We now invoke our identity (
2.22
) and the bound (
2.8
) in order to estimate the right hand-side above. This easily yields

\begin{split}\|(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}p_{t}^{D,U}(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(U,\mu)}&\leq\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}s^{\alpha-1}p_{2(s+t)}^{D,U}(x,x)^{1/2}ds\\
&\leq Ce^{-\lambda_{1}t}\int_{0}^{+\infty}s^{\alpha-1}e^{-\lambda_{1}s}\max\left(1,\frac{1}{(t+s)^{d_{h}/2d_{w}}}\right)ds.\end{split}

(2.47)

Inequality (
2.42
) is then achieved thanks to some elementary calculus considerations.
∎

To conclude the section we give the definition of the Sobolev space with reproducing kernel
G_{2\alpha}^{D,U}
.

Definition 2.23

(Sobolev Space
\mathcal{W}_{D}^{-\alpha}(U)
)
.

For
\alpha>0
, we define the Sobolev space
\mathcal{W}_{D}^{-\alpha}(U)
as the completion of
\mathcal{S}_{D}(U)
with respect to the inner product

\displaystyle\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}_{D}^{-\alpha}(U)}=\int_{U}\int_{U}f(x)g(y)G^{D,U}_{2\alpha}(x,y)d\mu(x)d\mu(y)=\int_{U}\left[(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}f\right]\left[(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}g\right]d\mu,

(2.48)

where the kernel
G^{D,U}_{2\alpha}
is defined by (
2.29
).

2.3
Neumann boundary conditions

In this section we turn to the Neumann boundary case. For further details about the Neumann boundary conditions in the general setting of Dirichlet spaces, we refer to
[
8
]
and
[
17
]
. We will mimic the structure of Section
2.2
, by first studying a notion of Neumann semigroup and then defining fractional powers of the Laplacian.

2.3.1
Heat semigroup with Neumann boundary conditions

As in Section
2.2
, we consider a non-empty bounded open set
U\subset X
, whose closure
\overline{U}
is compact. The domain
\mathcal{F}(U)
is still defined by (
2.4
). However, instead of restricting the domain to
\mathcal{F}_{c}(U)
like in the Dirichlet case, we directly denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{N}_{U}(u,u)=\int_{U}d\Gamma(u,u),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\ \ u\in\mathcal{F}(U),

(2.49)

where
\Gamma
is the energy measure defined by (
2.2
). In the Euclidean case, if
U
is a domain with a smooth boundary,
\mathcal{E}_{U}^{N}
yields the usual Neumann Dirichlet form, see for instance
[
8
]
. In our general setting, as opposed to the Dirichlet case,
(\mathcal{E}^{N}_{U},\mathcal{F}(U))
is not necessarily a strongly local regular Dirichlet form. In order to ensure this property we shall restrict our class of domains as below.

Definition 2.24
.

As in Definition
2.5
, consider an open, connected bounded set
U
whose closure is compact. Let
c,C
be two constants with
c\in(0,1)
and
C>1
. We say that a rectifiable curve

\gamma:[\alpha,\beta]\to U
is a
(c,C)
uniform curve in
U
if the following two conditions are satisfied:

(i)

For all
t\in[\alpha,\beta]
,
d(\gamma(t),\partial U)\geq c\min\{d(\gamma(\alpha),\gamma(t)),d(\gamma(t),\gamma(\beta))\}
,

(ii)

\mathrm{length}(\gamma)\leq Cd(\gamma(\alpha),\gamma(\beta))
.

Then the domain
U
is called a
(c,C)
uniform domain if any two points in
U
can be joined by a
(c,C)
uniform curve in
U
.

Since
d\leq d_{U}
, it is easy to check that a uniform domain is always inner uniform, see for instance Section 3.1.2 in
[
17
]
. The converse is not true in general. For instance, a disc with one slit is an example of a inner uniform domain which is not uniform. In Euclidean space, any bounded convex domain is uniform.

The main advantage of uniform domains is that the Dirichlet form
\mathcal{E}^{N}_{U}
becomes strongly local regular and admits a continuous heat kernel with sub-Gaussian estimates. We spell out this theorem below.

Theorem 2.25

(
[
34
, Theorem 2.8]
)
.

Assume that
U
is a uniform domain as introduced in Definition
2.24
. Then the form
(\mathcal{E}^{N}_{U},\mathcal{F}(U))
defined by (
2.49
) is a strongly local regular Dirichlet form which admits a continuous heat kernel
p_{t}^{N,U}(x,y)
. Moreover, the Neumann heat kernel
p_{t}^{N,U}(x,y)
satisfies the upper estimate

p_{t}^{N,U}(x,y)\leq\frac{c_{1}}{\min(1,t^{\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}})}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{2}\Bigl(\frac{d(x,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr)\,,

(2.50)

and the lower estimate

p_{t}^{N,U}(x,y)\geq\frac{c_{3}}{\min(1,t^{\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}})}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{4}\Bigl(\frac{d(x,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr)\,,

(2.51)

for every
(x,y)\in U\times U
and
t\in\bigl(0,+\infty)
.

Notice that under the conditions of Theorem
2.25
, the form
(\mathcal{E}^{N}_{U},\mathcal{F}(U))
is referred to as Neumann Dirichlet form on
U
. The related diffusion process is the reflected Brownian motion.
We now close this section by stating a lemma about the
L^{2}
norm of the heat kernel, which is similar to Corollary
2.12
.

Lemma 2.26
.

Under the same conditions as for Theorem
2.25
, there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
x\in K
,

\int_{U}p_{t}^{N,U}(x,y)^{2}d\mu(y)\leq\left\{\begin{aligned} &Ct^{-\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}},&0<t<1,\\
&C,&t\geq 1.\end{aligned}\right.

2.3.2
Fractional Laplacian

In the case of Neumann boundary conditions, the fractional Laplacian is constructed very similarly to the Dirichlet case. We first summarize the spectral properties of the Laplacian in the proposition below.

Proposition 2.27
.

Consider a uniform domain
U
like in Definition
2.24
and the Dirichlet form
\mathcal{E}_{U}^{N}
defined by (
2.49
). The generator of
\mathcal{E}_{U}^{N}
is denoted by
\Delta_{N,U}
and called the Neumann Laplacian. Then:

(i)

The operator
-\Delta_{N,U}
admits a discrete spectrum
\{\nu_{j};j\geq 0\}
with
\nu_{0}=0
and
0<\nu_{1}\leq\nu_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\nu_{j}\leq\cdots
. The corresponding eigenfunctions are such that
\Psi_{0}=\mu(U)^{-1}
and
\{\Psi_{j};j\geq 1\}
form an orthonormal basis of

L^{2}_{0}(U,\mu)=\left\{f\in L^{2}(U,\mu);\ \int_{U}fd\mu=0\right\}.

(ii)

The Neumann heat kernel
p_{t}^{N,U}
of Theorem
2.25
admits a convergent series expansion for all
t>0
and
x,y\in U
:

p_{t}^{N,U}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\mu(U)}+\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}e^{-\nu_{j}t}\Psi_{j}(x)\Psi_{j}(y).

(2.52)

(iii)

For every
\alpha>d_{h}/d_{w}
, we have
\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\nu_{j}^{\alpha}}<+\infty.

We now define the fractional powers of the Laplacian along the same lines as Definition
2.16
. Notice however that this definition avoids the eigenvalue
\nu_{0}=0
, which would create some singularities.

Definition 2.28

(Neumann Fractional Laplacians)
.

Consider the same setting as for Proposition
2.27
, and let
\alpha
be a strictly positive parameter. For
f\in L^{2}(U,\mu)
the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}
on
f
is defined as

(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}f=\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}\frac{1}{\nu_{j}^{\alpha}}\Psi_{j}\int_{U}\Psi_{j}(y)f(y)d\mu(y).

Remark 2.29
.

Exactly as for Remark
2.17
, the fractional Laplacian
(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}:L^{2}(U,\mu)\to L_{0}^{2}(U,\mu)
is a bounded operator. More precisely, one has
\|(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(U,\mu)\to L^{2}(U,\mu)}\leq\nu_{1}^{-\alpha}
as in Remark
2.17
.

In order to discuss the kernels for the operators
(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}
, let us introduce a space of test functions adapted to the Neumann condition context.

Definition 2.30
.

Consider a uniform domain
U
. Let
\{\Psi_{n};n\geq 1\}
be the family of eigenfunctions introduced in Proposition
2.27
. We define the Neumann-Schwartz space of test functions on
U
as

\mathcal{S}_{N}(U)=\left\{f\in L^{2}(U,\mu),\text{for\ all}\ k\in\mathbb{N},\lim_{n\to+\infty}n^{k}\left|\int_{U}\Psi_{n}(y)f(y)d\mu(y)\right|=0\right\}.

We can now identify a kernel for
(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}
, similarly to Lemma
2.19
. The proof is similar to
[
3
]
and omitted.

Lemma 2.31
.

Let
U
be an inner domain and
\alpha>0
. Recall that the operator
(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}
is introduced in Definition
2.28
. Then
(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}
admits a kernel
G_{\alpha}^{N,U}
given by

\displaystyle G_{\alpha}^{N,U}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(s)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}t^{\alpha-1}\left(p_{t}^{N,U}(x,y)-\frac{1}{\mu(U)}\right)dt,\hskip 10.00002ptx,y\in U,x\neq y,

(2.53)

where
p_{t}^{N,U}
is the heat kernel in Theorem
2.25
. As in Lemma
2.19
, this means that for
f\in\mathcal{S}_{N}(U)
and
y\in U
we have

(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}f(y)=\int_{U}f(x)G^{N,U}_{\alpha}(x,y)d\mu(x).

For
f
and
g
in
\mathcal{S}_{N}(U)
, it also holds that

\int_{U}(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}f(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}gd\mu=\int_{U}\int_{U}f(x)g(y)G^{N,U}_{2\alpha}(x,y)d\mu(x)d\mu(y).

We now state some useful bounds for the fractional Laplacian kernel. We sketch the proof of the lower bound for lack of a proper reference.

Proposition 2.32
.

Consider a uniform domain
U
. For
\alpha>0
, let
G_{\alpha}^{N,U}
be the kernel given by (
2.53
). Then the following bounds hold true:

(i)

If
0<\alpha<d_{h}/d_{w}
, there exist three finite positive constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}
such that for any
x,y\in U
,
x\neq y
,

\displaystyle C_{1}d(x,y)^{\alpha d_{w}-d_{h}}-C_{2}\leq G^{N,U}_{\alpha}(x,y)\leq C_{3}d(x,y)^{\alpha d_{w}-d_{h}}.

(2.54)

(ii)

For
\alpha=d_{h}/d_{w}
, there exist three finite positive constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}
such that for any
x,y\in U
such that
x\neq y
,

\displaystyle-C_{1}\ln d(x,y)-C_{2}\leq G^{N,U}_{\alpha}(x,y)\leq C_{3}\max(\left|\ln d(x,y)\right|,1).

(2.55)

(iii)

If
\alpha>d_{h}/d_{w}
, there exist finite positive constants
C_{1},C_{2}
such that for any
x,y\in U
,

\displaystyle-C_{1}\leq G^{N,U}_{\alpha}(x,y)\leq C_{2}.

(2.56)

Proof.

The upper estimates are proved in
[
6
, Proposition 2.6]
and we omit the details here.
Similarly as in the proof of Proposition
2.32
, we show the lower estimates by using the Neumann heat kernel lower bound in Theorem
2.25
. To this aim, we decompose the integral defining
G_{\alpha}^{N,U}
as

\displaystyle G_{\alpha}^{N,U}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}t^{\alpha-1}\left(p_{t}^{N,U}(x,y)-\frac{1}{\mu(U)}\right)dt+\int_{1}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}\left(p_{t}^{N,U}(x,y)-\frac{1}{\mu(U)}\right)dt

(2.57)

\displaystyle:=\widetilde{G}_{\alpha}^{N,U}(x,y)+\hat{G}_{\alpha}^{N,U}(x,y).

(2.58)

In addition, it is proved in
[
6
, Lemma 2.3]
that there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\left|\hat{G}_{\alpha}^{N,U}(x,y)\right|<C,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for\ all}\ x,y\in U.

(2.59)

Our lower bounds in (
2.54
)-(
2.55
)-(
2.56
) will thus be obtained through the analysis of
\widetilde{G}_{\alpha}^{N,U}
. We now separate our considerations according to the value of
\alpha
.

Case
0<\alpha<d_{h}/d_{w}
.
We resort to relation (
2.51
) in order to write

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{\alpha}^{N,U}(x,y)\geq c_{3}\int_{0}^{1}t^{\alpha-1-\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{4}\Bigl(\frac{d(x,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr)dt-\int_{0}^{1}t^{\alpha-1}\frac{1}{\mu(U)}dt.

(2.60)

Then an elementary change of variable (along the same lines as (
2.35
)-(
2.36
)), plus the fact that
\int_{0}^{1}t^{\alpha-1}dt
is a convergent integral, show that

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{\alpha}^{N,U}(x,y)\geq C_{1}d(x,y)^{\alpha d_{w}-d_{h}}-C_{2}.

(2.61)

Case
\alpha=d_{h}/d_{w}
.
Just like in the previous case, we separate the exponential term from the constant term in the expression of
\widetilde{G}^{\alpha}_{N,U}
we get

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{\alpha}^{N,U}(x,y)\geq c_{3}\int_{0}^{1}t^{-1}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{4}\Bigl(\frac{d(x,y)^{d_{w}}}{t}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr)dt-C_{2}.

Next perform the change of variable
u=t/d_{U}(x,y)^{d_{w}}
and introduce the diameter
R=\mathrm{diam}(U)
, as we did in (
2.38
)-(
2.41
). We end up with

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{\alpha}^{N,U}(x,y)\geq c\,\int_{1/R^{d_{w}}}^{1/d(x,y)^{d_{w}}}\!\exp\biggl(\!-c_{4}\Bigl(\frac{1}{u}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}\!\biggr)\frac{du}{u}-C_{2}.

We now trivially bound the exponential term by
1
in order to get

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{\alpha}^{N,U}(x,y)\geq-C_{1}\ln d(x,y)-C_{2}.

(2.62)

Case
\alpha>d_{h}/d_{w}
.
If
\alpha>d_{h}/d_{w}
, we simply lower bound the exponential term in (
2.60
) by
0
. This allows to write

\widetilde{G}_{\alpha}^{N,U}(x,y)\geq-\frac{1}{\mu(U)}\int_{0}^{1}t^{\alpha-1}dt=-C_{2}.

(2.63)

Conclusion.

Combining (
2.57
), (
2.59
), (
2.61
), (
2.62
) and (
2.63
), we have achieved our claims.
∎

We now state a useful bound for the Neumann heat kernel which mirrors Lemma
2.22
.

Lemma 2.33
.

Let
\alpha\geq 0
and
0<\gamma<\nu_{1}
. Recall that the operator
(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}
is given in Definition
2.28
. Then there exists a constant
C
such that for every
t>0

\sup_{x\in U}\|(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}p_{t}^{N,U}(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(U,\mu)}\leq\begin{cases}C\max(1,t^{-\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}}),\quad\alpha=0,\\
Ce^{-\gamma t}t^{\alpha-\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}},\quad 0<\alpha<\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}},\\
Ce^{-\gamma t}(|\ln t|+1),\quad\alpha=\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}},\\
Ce^{-\gamma t},\quad\alpha>\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}.\end{cases}

Remark 2.34
.

It will be apparent from our proof that the parameter
\gamma<\nu_{1}
is used in order to produce some convergent integrals
\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-(\nu_{1}-\gamma)s}ds
. Hence our decay rate is not as sharp as the
e^{-\lambda_{1}t}
we obtained in (
2.41
) for the Dirichlet case. However, Lemma
2.33
is only applied in Theorem
4.5
, for which optimal decay rates are not necessary.

Proof.

This proof is an adaption of the proof of Lemma
2.22
to the Neumann context. First the case
\alpha=0
follows from Lemma
2.26
, so we assume
\alpha>0
. Next owing to (
2.53
) and applying the semigroup property similarly to (
2.45
) we deduce for every
t>0

\displaystyle(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}p_{t}^{N,U}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{U}G_{\alpha}^{N,U}(y,z)p_{t}^{N,U}(x,z)d\mu(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\int_{U}s^{\alpha-1}\left(p_{s}^{N,U}(y,z)-\frac{1}{\mu(U)}\right)p_{t}^{N,U}(x,z)d\mu(z)ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}s^{\alpha-1}\left(p_{s+t}^{N,U}(x,y)-\frac{1}{\mu(U)}\right)ds.

We now proceed to apply Minkowski’s inequality along the same lines as (
2.46
). This yields

\displaystyle\|(-\Delta_{D})^{-\alpha}p_{t}^{N,U}(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(U,\mu)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}s^{\alpha-1}\left\|p_{s+t}^{N,U}(x,\cdot)-\frac{1}{\mu(U)}\right\|_{L^{2}(U,\mu)}ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}s^{\alpha-1}\left(p_{2(s+t)}^{N,U}(x,x)-\frac{1}{\mu(U)}\right)^{1/2}ds,

(2.64)

where the equality (
2.3.2
) follows from the semigroup property and the fact that the Neumann heat semigroup is conservative.

The proof is finished thanks to elementary arguments (like for Lemma
2.22
). Let us just mention that the integral in (
2.3.2
) has to be split into two pieces, separately corresponding to integrals over
(0,1]
and
(1,\infty)
. For the integral over
(0,1]
one resorts to the heat kernel upper bound (
2.50
). For the integral over
(1,\infty)
we use identity (
2.52
), which is valid for
p^{N,U}_{2(s+t)}-\mu(U)^{-1}
and gives exponential decay. Notice however that the parameter
\gamma<\nu_{1}
is introduced for this step, in order to be left with a convergent integral
\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-(\nu_{1}-\gamma)s}ds
.∎

We close this section by giving a definition of Sobolev spaces adapting Definition
2.23
to Neumann boundary conditions.

Definition 2.35

(Sobolev Space
\mathcal{W}_{N}^{-\alpha}(U)
)
.

Let
C_{U}>0
be an arbitrary constant. For
\alpha>0
, we define the Sobolev space
\mathcal{W}_{N}^{-\alpha}(U)
as the completion of
\mathcal{S}_{N}(U)
with respect to the inner product

\displaystyle\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}_{N}^{-\alpha}(U)}=\int_{U}\int_{U}f(x)g(y)(G^{N,U}_{2\alpha}(x,y)+C_{U})d\mu(x)d\mu(y).

(2.65)

Remark 2.36
.

As mentioned in Definition
2.35
, the value of the constant
C_{U}
is irrelevant. We note however from Proposition
2.32
that it can be chosen in such a way that
G^{N,U}_{2\alpha}(x,y)+C_{U}
is bounded from below by a positive constant.

Remark 2.37
.

It is readily checked that an alternative way to express the Sobolev norm in (
2.65
) is

\displaystyle\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}_{N}^{-\alpha}(U)}=

\displaystyle\int_{U}\int_{U}f(x)g(y)G^{N,U}_{2\alpha}(x,y)d\mu(x)d\mu(y)+C_{U}\int_{U}fd\mu\int_{U}gd\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{U}(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}f(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}gd\mu+C_{U}\int_{U}fd\mu\int_{U}gd\mu.

2.4
Examples of application

This section is devoted to an overview of the potential applications of our general metric space setting. We will first look at intervals, then at metric graphs. We shall also mention a very typical application to fractal sets.

2.4.1
Example 1: Compact intervals

The simplest example which fits our framework is the unit interval
U=(0,1)\subset\mathbb{R}
(or any interval
(a,b)
). Here one starts from the metric space
X=\mathbb{R}
equipped with the Euclidean distance
d(x,y)=|x-y|
. The Brownian Dirichlet form on
\mathbb{R}
is

\mathcal{E}(f,g)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f^{\prime}(x)g^{\prime}(x)dx,\hskip 10.00002ptf,g\in W^{1,2}(\mathbb{R}).

The corresponding heat kernel is known to be

p_{t}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}e^{-\frac{(x-y)^{2}}{4t}}.

Referring to our formula (
2.3
), this means that
d_{h}=1
and
d_{w}=2
. Then the two boundary conditions we consider on
(0,1)
are spelled out as follows.

(a) Dirichlet boundary conditions.

The pair
(\mathcal{E}^{D},\mathcal{F}^{D}((0,1)))
in (
2.5
) is then given by

\mathcal{E}^{D}(f,g)=\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(x)g^{\prime}(x)dx,\hskip 10.00002ptf,g\in W^{1,2}_{0}([0,1]),

where
W^{1,2}_{0}([0,1])
designates the usual Sobolev space of functions vanishing at both
0
and
1
.
Functions
f
in the domain of the Dirichlet Laplacian
\Delta_{D}=\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}
satisfy the boundary condition
f(0)=f(1)=0
.
An explicit formula is available for the heat kernel
p^{D,(0,1)}
of Theorem
2.6
,

p_{t}^{D}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi t}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(e^{-\frac{(x-y+k)^{2}}{4t}}-e^{-\frac{(x+y+k)^{2}}{4t}}\right).

(b) Neumann boundary conditions.

The Neumann Dirichlet form (
2.49
) is given by

\mathcal{E}^{N}(f,g)=\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(x)g^{\prime}(x)dx,\hskip 10.00002ptf,g\in W^{1,2}([0,1]),

where
W^{1,2}([0,1])
is the usual Sobolev space on
[0,1]
.
Smooth functions
f
in the domain of the Neumann Laplacian
\Delta_{N}=\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}
satisfy the boundary condition
f^{\prime}(0)=f^{\prime}(1)=0
.
The Neumann heat kernel is given by

p_{t}^{N}(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi t}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(e^{-\frac{(x-y+k)^{2}}{4t}}+e^{-\frac{(x+y+k)^{2}}{4t}}\right).

We note that the parabolic Anderson models with Dirichlet and Neumann boundary condition have been studied in that case by
[
15
]
, see also
[
22
]
.

2.4.2
Example 2: Metric graphs

For a reference on the general theory of metric graphs, also referred to as cable systems in the literature, we refer to
[
36
]
, see also
[
5
]
and references therein. Intuitively, a metric graph is a finite collection of intervals (or edges), for which some of them are connected at their extremities. More precisely, we use
\mathbf{G}
to denote a connected metric graph, which is composed of a finite set of vertices
V
, a finite set of (internal) edges
\mathbf{E}
and a finite set of rays
\mathbf{R}
. For each edge
e\in\mathbf{E}
, there are two endpoints
e^{-}
and
e^{+}
in
V
as well as a length
r(e)>0
. Each ray has one associated endpoint
e^{-}
in
V
and the length is infinite. For
v\in V
, define the set of adjacent edges
\mathbf{E}_{v}=\left\{e\in\mathbf{E}\cup\mathbf{R}~|~v=e^{-}\text{ or }v=e^{+}\right\}
.

For
e\in\mathbf{E}
let
I_{e}=[0,r(e)]
and if
e\in\mathbf{R}
then
I_{e}=[0,\infty)
. In this case the metric graph
\mathbf{G}
is the set
\cup_{e\in\mathbf{E}\cup\mathbf{R}}I_{e}
modulo the equivalence relation which identifies endpoints of
I_{e_{1}}
and
I_{e_{2}}
if associated endpoints of
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are the same vertex.
We also define
\Phi_{e}:I_{e}\to\mathbf{G}
to be the projection onto the equivalence classes. For example
\Phi_{e_{1}}(0)=\Phi_{e_{2}}(r(e_{2}))
if
e^{-}_{1}=e^{+}_{2}
. We may think of
I_{e}
as subsets of
\mathbf{G}
and refer to
0\in I_{e}
as
e^{-}
and
r(e)\in I_{e}
as
e^{+}
.

Now, we shall introduce some notation concerning the function spaces on
\mathbf{G}
. Define the reference measure
\mu
on
\mathbf{G}
to be the Lebesgue measure when restricted to each
I_{e}
.
The space
L^{2}(\mathbf{G})=L^{2}(\mathbf{G},\mu)
is defined as

L^{2}(\mathbf{G})=\{f=(f_{e})_{e\in\mathbf{E}\cup\mathbf{R}}\,;f_{e}\in L^{2}(I_{e})\},

that is, we identify
L^{2}(\mathbf{G})
with
\oplus_{e}L^{2}(I_{e})
.
Other function spaces have similar vector decompositions, perhaps with boundary conditions. For example, we shall think of continuous functions
C(\mathbf{G})
to be the vectors with entries in
C(I_{e})
where, if
v
is an element of both
I_{e_{1}}
and
I_{e_{2}}
then
f_{e_{1}}(v)=f_{e_{2}}(v)
.
We also define the Sobolev space
H^{1}_{0}(\mathbf{G})
as

H^{1}_{0}(\mathbf{G})=\{f=(f_{e})_{e\in\mathbf{E}\cup\mathbf{R}}\,;\,f_{e}\in H^{1}(I_{e}),\mbox{ with $f$ continuous at vertices}\}.

Figure 1:
Examples of metric graphs (picture from Wikipedia)

When it is well defined, we consider
f(v)
to be the vector
(f_{e}(v))_{e\in\mathbf{E}_{v}}
of values of
f
(or traces of
f
) at the associated endpoint of
e
.
Let us now label a definition which will be useful for the definition of Laplacian on
\mathbf{G}
.

Definition 2.38
.

For all
v\in V
, we define
U_{v}:E\to\{-1,1\}
by setting
U_{v}(e)=1
if
v=e^{-}
and
U_{v}(e)=-1
if
v=e^{+}
. In this way, the inward facing normal derivative of
f
at
v\in V
along an edge
e
is
U_{v}(e)f_{e}^{\prime}(v)
.

Here and later,
f_{e}^{\prime}(0)
or
f^{\prime}_{e}(r(e))
is understood to be the trace of
f^{\prime}_{e}
onto the boundary of the interval
I_{e}
.

With those preliminary notions in hand, we are now ready to define Dirichlet forms on
G
. One defines first a derivative operator
d:H^{1}_{0}(\mathbf{G})\to L^{2}(\mathbf{G})
by
(df)_{e}(x)=f^{\prime}_{e}(x)
, which we will concisely denote by
df=f^{\prime}
. Note that, up to a sign,
d
depends on the orientation of the graph. Then the standard Dirichlet form on
G
corresponds to the so-called Kirchhoff boundary condition, see
[
5
]
. It is defined by

\mathcal{E}(f,g)=\int f^{\prime}g^{\prime}\ d\mu:=\sum_{e\in\mathbf{E}}\int_{0}^{r(e)}f^{\prime}_{e}(x)g^{\prime}_{e}(x)dx+\sum_{e\in\mathbf{R}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}f^{\prime}_{e}(x)g^{\prime}_{e}(x)dx,

the domain of
\mathcal{E}
being
H^{1}_{0}(\mathbf{G})
. The corresponding operator of
\mathcal{E}
is
\Delta f=-f^{\prime\prime}
, with domain

\mathrm{Dom}(\Delta)=\left\{f\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathbf{G})~|~\forall~e,f_{e}^{\prime}\in H^{1}(I_{e}),\text{ and }\forall~v\in V,~\sum_{v\in\mathbf{E}_{v}}U_{v}(e)f_{e}^{\prime}(v)=0\right\},

where we recall that the operator
U_{v}
is introduced in Definition
2.38
. Notice that
\mathcal{E}
and
\Delta
do not
depend on the orientation of the graph. Moreover, it is known, see
[
18
]
, that the heat kernel
p_{t}(x,y)
associated with the Dirichlet form
\mathcal{E}
satisfies the Gaussian estimates

c_{1}t^{-1/2}\exp\biggl(-c_{2}\Bigl(\frac{d(x,y)^{2}}{t}\Bigr)\biggr)\leq p_{t}(x,y)\leq c_{3}t^{-1/2}\exp\biggl(-c_{4}\Bigl(\frac{d(x,y)^{2}}{t}\Bigr)\biggr)\,,

(2.66)

for
(x,y)\in\mathbf{G}\times\mathbf{G}

t\in\bigl(0,\mathrm{diam}(\mathbf{G})^{1/2}]
, where the distance
d
is the geodesic distance. Therefore as in Section
2.4.1
and referring to formula (
2.3
), we have
d_{h}=1
and
d_{w}=2
.

In case of a metric graph
\mathbf{G}
, our main examples of subdomains are connected bounded open subsets. Those are easily seen to be uniform domains as in Definition
2.24
(and hence they are also inner uniform domains as in Definition
2.5
). Therefore our estimates from Sections
2.2
and
2.3
apply to this setting.

2.4.3
Example 3: Fractals, Sierpiński gasket

Besides metric graphs, a large class of examples that also fit in our setting is p.c.f. fractals as presented in
[
2
]
, see also
[
25
]
. For the sake of presentation we illustrate in detail the case of the Sierpiński gasket, which is one of the most popular examples of a p.c.f. fractal.

One of the classical ways to define the Sierpiński gasket is as follows:
let
V_{0}=\{p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\}
be a set of vertices of an equilateral triangle of side 1 in
\mathbb{C}
. Define

\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}_{i}(z)=\frac{z-p_{i}}{2}+p_{i},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\hskip 10.00002pti=1,2,3.

(2.67)

The Sierpiński gasket
K
(see Figure
2
) is the unique non-empty compact subset in
\mathbb{C}
such that

\displaystyle K=\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}\mathfrak{f}_{i}(K).

(2.68)

Figure 2:
Sierpiński gasket.

The set
V_{0}
is called the boundary of
K
, we will also denote it by
\partial K
. The Hausdorff dimension of
K
with respect to the Euclidean metric (denoted
d(x,y)=|x-y|
) is given by
d_{h}=\frac{\ln 3}{\ln 2}
. A (normalized) Hausdorff measure on
K
is given by the Borel measure
\mu
on
K
such that for every
i_{1},\dots,i_{n}\in\{1,2,3\}
,

\mu\left(\mathfrak{f}_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathfrak{f}_{i_{n}}(K)\right)=3^{-n}.

This measure
\mu
is
d_{h}
-Ahlfors regular, i.e., there exist constants
c,C>0
such that for every
x\in K
and
r\in[0,\mathrm{diam}(K)]
,

cr^{d_{h}}\leq\mu(B(x,r))\leq Cr^{d_{h}}.

(2.69)

It will be useful to approximate the gasket
K
by a sequence of discrete objects. Namely, starting from the set
V_{0}=\{p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\}
, we define a sequence of sets
\{V_{m}\}_{m\geq 0}
inductively by

\displaystyle V_{m+1}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{3}\mathfrak{f}_{i}(V_{m}).

(2.70)

Then we have a natural sequence of Sierpiński gasket graphs (or pre-gaskets)
\{G_{m}\}_{m\geq 0}
whose edges have length
2^{-m}
and whose set of vertices is
V_{m}
, see Figure
3
. Notice that
\#V_{m}=\frac{3(3^{m}+1)}{2}
. We will use the notations
V_{*}=\cup_{m\geq 0}V_{m}
and
V_{*}^{0}=\cup_{m\geq 0}V_{m}\setminus V_{0}
.

Figure 3:
Sierpiński gasket graphs
G_{0}
,
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
and
G_{3}

We are now ready to define Dirichlet forms on the metric space
X=K
by approximation.

(a) Neumann boundary conditions.

Let
m\geq 1
. For any
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V_{m}}
, we consider the quadratic form

\mathcal{E}_{m}(f,f)=\left(\frac{5}{3}\right)^{m}\sum_{p,q\in V_{m},p\sim q}(f(p)-f(q))^{2}.

We can then define a resistance form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{*})
on
V_{*}
by setting

\mathcal{F}_{*}=\{f\in\mathbb{R}^{V_{*}},\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{m}(f,f)<\infty\}

and for
f\in\mathcal{F}_{*}

\mathcal{E}(f,f)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\mathcal{E}_{m}(f,f).

Each function
f\in\mathcal{F}_{*}
can be uniquely extended into a continuous function defined on
K
. We denote by
\mathcal{F}
the set of functions with such extensions. It follows from the book of Kigami
[
25
]
that
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
is a local regular Dirichlet form on
L^{2}(K,\mu)
. The generator of the Dirichlet form
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F})
, denoted by
\Delta_{N}
, corresponds to the Laplacian with Neumann boundary condition with the domain given by

\mathrm{Dom}(\Delta_{N})=\Big\{u\in\mathcal{F};\,\exists\,f\in L^{2}(K,\mu)\text{ such that }\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\int_{K}fvd\mu,\,\forall v\in\mathcal{F}\Big\}.

(2.71)

In case
u\in\mathrm{Dom}(\Delta_{N})
, one sets
\Delta_{N}u=-f
, where
f
is the function featuring (
2.71
).
For more details, we refer to
[
25
, Theorem 3.4.6]
and
[
2
]
.

(b) Dirichlet boundary conditions.
Towards a definition of the Laplace operator with Dirichlet boundary conditions on
K
, define

\mathcal{F}_{0}=\left\{f\in\mathcal{F},f|_{V_{0}}=0\right\}.

Then by
[
25
, Corollary 3.4.7]
,
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{0})
is a local regular Dirichlet form on
L^{2}(K,\mu)
. The generator of
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{F}_{0})
, denoted by
\Delta_{D}
, is associated with the Dirichlet boundary condition with the domain

\mathrm{Dom}(\Delta_{D})=\Big\{u\in\mathcal{F}_{0};\,\exists\,f\in L^{2}(K,\mu)\text{ such that }\mathcal{E}(u,v)=\int_{K}fvd\mu,\,\forall v\in\mathcal{F}_{0}\Big\}.

(2.72)

Like in the Neumann case, whenever
u\in\mathrm{Dom}(\Delta_{D})
we set
\Delta_{D}u=-f
.

(c) Laplacian from approximations.

The Dirichlet and Neumann Laplacians can also be realized from the discrete approximation of the gasket, for which we need some notation. First recall that
V_{0}=\{p_{1},p_{2},p_{3}\}
, and that
V_{m}
is defined by (
2.70
).
For any
p\in V_{m}
, denote by
V_{m,p}
the collection of neighbors of
p
in
V_{m}
. Then

\#V_{m,p}=\begin{cases}4,&\text{ if }p\notin V_{0},\\
2,&\text{ if }p\in V_{0}.\end{cases}

For any
f\in\mathbb{R}^{V_{m}}
, consider the discrete Laplacian on
V_{m}
defined by

\displaystyle\Delta_{m}f(p)=\frac{3}{2}\,5^{m}\sum_{q\in V_{m,p}}(f(q)-f(p)),\hskip 10.00002ptp\in V_{m}\setminus V_{0}.

(2.73)

The Laplace operator is now obtained by approximation in the following way.
Let
C(K)
be the set of continuous functions on
K
. We define

\mathcal{D}=\left\{f\in C(K),\text{ there exists }g\in C(K)\text{ such that }\lim_{m\to\infty}\max_{p\in V_{m}\setminus V_{0}}|\Delta_{m}f(p)-g(p)|=0\right\}.

(2.74)

Then the Kigami Laplacian
\Delta
on the Sierpiński gasket
K
is defined by

\Delta f(p)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\Delta_{m}f(p),\hskip 10.00002ptf\in\mathcal{D}.

(2.75)

Notice that by definition in (
2.74
), for
f\in\mathcal{D}
the function
\Delta f
is continuous on
K
. For any
f\in\mathcal{D}
and
p\in V_{0}
, let us also define the Neumann derivative by

(df)_{p}=-\lim_{m\to\infty}\left(\frac{5}{3}\right)^{m}\sum_{q\in V_{m,p}}(f(q)-f(p))\,,

given that the limit converges.
The relation between Kigami and

Laplacians is the content of the following theorem.

Theorem 2.39

(
[
25

)
.

Recall that
\mathrm{Dom}(\Delta_{N})
,
\mathrm{Dom}(\Delta_{D})
and
\mathcal{D}
are respectively defined by (

), (
2.72
) and (

).

(i)

If
\mathcal{D}_{N}=\{f\in\mathcal{D},(df)_{p}=0\text{ for all }p\in V_{0}\}

\mathcal{D}_{N}=\mathrm{Dom}(\Delta_{N})\cap\mathcal{D}
,

3
Dirichlet parabolic Anderson model in inner uniform domains

In this section we focus on SPDE models with Dirichlet boundary conditions. We will first describe the type of noise we are considering. Then we shall get existence and uniqueness results for the equation and give estimates for the moments of the solution. We label the following hypothesis once for all in the section.

Assumption 3.1
.

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be a metric measure space satisfying Assumption
2.3
. We consider an inner uniform domain
U
as in Definition
2.5
.

Notice that since the domain is fixed for the whole section, we will drop the
U
from our notation on semigroups, Dirichlet forms, etc. For instance we shall write
\Delta_{D}
instead of
\Delta_{D,U}
.

3.1
Fractional noise

In this section we briefly describe the structure of the Gaussian noise that we consider as the driving noise for our stochastic heat equation. As the definition below shows, it will be a white noise in time with colored covariance in space.

Definition 3.2
.

Consider a regularity parameter
\alpha>0
and the following Hilbert space of space-time functions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{W}_{D}^{-\alpha}(U)),

(3.1)

where
\mathcal{W}_{D}^{-\alpha}(U)
is the Sobolev space introduced in Definition
2.23
. On a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbf{P})
we define a centered Gaussian family
\{W_{\alpha}^{D}(\phi);\phi\in\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}\}
, whose covariance is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[W_{\alpha}^{D}(\varphi)W_{\alpha}^{D}(\psi)\right]=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ \left\langle\varphi(t,\cdot),\psi(t,\cdot)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}_{D}^{-\alpha}(U)}dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{U^{2}}\varphi(t,x)\psi(t,y)G_{2\alpha}^{D,U}(x,y)d\mu(x)d\mu(y)dt\,,

(3.2)

for
\varphi
,
\psi
in
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
. This family is called Dirichlet fractional Gaussian fields.

We are working in situations where the stochastic heat equation accommodates a space-time white noise (corresponding to
\alpha=0
above). Let us label this definition separately.

Definition 3.3
.

On the same probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbf{P})
as in Definition
3.2
, the space-time white noise
\{W_{0}^{D}(\phi);\phi\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},L^{2}(U))\}
is a centered Gaussian family with covariance function

\mathbf{E}\left(W_{0}^{D}(\varphi)W_{0}^{D}(\psi)\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ \left\langle\varphi(t,\cdot),\psi(t,\cdot)\right\rangle_{L^{2}(U)}dt\ .

(3.3)

Notation 3.4
.

Since the parameter
\alpha
will be fixed for the entire section, we will abbreviate the notation
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
in (
3.1
) as
\mathcal{H}
.

Remark 3.5
.

Noises whose covariance functions are based on powers of the Laplacian are common in the literature. Let us mention the reference
[
31
]
for noises defined in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, as well as our studies
[
4
,
7
]
in Heisenberg groups. Fractional noises on fractals have also been considered in
[
3
]
. Therein the following two regimes are exhibited:

(i)

For
0\leq\alpha\leq\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
, the field
W^{D}_{\alpha}
given in Definition
3.2
or Definition
3.3
is a distribution in both time and space.

(ii)

For
\alpha>\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
, there exists a centered continuous Gaussian field
(X_{\alpha}^{D}(t,x))_{t\geq 0,x\in U}
with covariance

\mathbf{E}\left(X_{\alpha}^{D}(s,y)X_{\alpha}^{D}(t,x)\right)=\min(s,t)G^{D}_{2\alpha}(x,y),

such that for every
t>0
and
f\in\mathcal{S}_{D}(U)
we have

\displaystyle W_{\alpha}^{D}(1_{[0,t]}\otimes f)=\int_{U}X_{\alpha}^{D}(t,x)f(x)d\mu(x)\,.

(3.4)

3.2
Parabolic Anderson model and chaos expansion method

We are now ready to turn to the main object of study in this paper, that is, the stochastic heat equation (
1.2
). Specifically, consider two parameters
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
and the noise
W^{D}_{\alpha}
introduced in Definition
3.2
. We wish to solve the following parabolic Anderson model with Dirichlet Laplacian:

\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\Delta_{D}u(t,x)+\beta u(t,x)\,\dot{W}_{\alpha}^{D}(t,x).

(3.5)

In this section we focus on a proper definition of equation (
3.5
). Since most of our considerations here are standard, we might skip some details and refer to classical papers on the topic.

Systems like (
3.5
) are usually solved in a mild form. That is, one rewrites our equation of interest as

u(t,x)=J_{0}(t,x)+\beta I(t,x),

(3.6)

where
J_{0}(t,x)=P_{t}^{D}u_{0}(x)=\int_{U}p_{t}^{D}(x,y)u(0,y)d\mu(y)
is the solution to the homogeneous heat equation and
I(t,x)
is the stochastic integral given by

I(t,x)=\int_{[0,t]\times U}p_{t-s}^{D}(x,y)u(s,y)W_{\alpha}^{D}(ds,dy).

(3.7)

Notice that the stochastic integral
I(t,x)
above should be understood in the Itô sense. We refer to
[
20
,
38
]
for a proper definition of this integral, as well as
[
7
]
in a more geometric context. A solution to (
3.6
) is then defined as below.

Definition 3.6
.

A process
u=\{u(t,x);(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times U\}
is called a random field solution of (
3.5
) in the Itô-Skorohod sense if the following conditions are met:

(i)

u
is adapted;

(ii)

u
is jointly measurable with respect to
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times U)\otimes\mathcal{G}
;

(iii)

\mathbf{E}(I(t,x)^{2})<\infty
for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times U
;

(iv)

The function
(t,x)\to I(t,x)
is continuous in
L^{2}(\Omega)
;

(v)

u
satisfies (
3.6
) almost surely for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times U
.

Observe that equation (
3.6
) can classically be solved by Picard iterations schemes combined with standard Itô type estimates for moments of stochastic integrals. However we shall focus here on a chaos expansion methodology, which leads to sharper moment estimates.
We briefly recall the setting for chaos expansion now. The reader is sent to
[
4
,
20
]
for more detailed accounts. Let us first denote by
\mathcal{H}_{k}
the space

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{k}=\mathrm{Span}\left(\left\{H_{k}(W^{D}_{\alpha}(\phi));\phi\in\mathcal{H},\|\phi\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1\right\}\right),

(3.8)

where Span means closure of the linear span in
L^{2}(\Omega)
,
H_{k}
designates the
k
-th Hermite polynomial,
W^{D}_{\alpha}
is the noise in Definition
3.2
and
\mathcal{H}
is the Hilbert space from (
3.1
). The space
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is called the
k
-th Wiener chaos. There exists a linear isometry
I_{k}
between
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}
(with modified norm
\sqrt{k!}\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}}
) and
\mathcal{H}_{k}
given by

I_{k}\big(\phi^{\otimes k}\big)=k!\,H_{k}(W^{D}_{\alpha}(\phi)),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for any }\phi\in\mathcal{H}\text{ with }\|\phi\|_{\mathcal{H}}=1.

Let
u=\{u(t,x);t\geq 0,x\in U\}
be a random field such that
\mathbf{E}[u(t,x)^{2}]<\infty
for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in U
.
It is established in
[
35
]
that
u(t,x)
has a Wiener chaos expansion of the form

u(t,x)=\mathbf{E}[u(t,x)]+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{k}(f_{k}(\cdot,t,x)),

(3.9)

where
f_{k}
’s are symmetric elements of
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}
uniquely determined by
u
and where the series converges in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. When
u
is the solution to equation (
3.5
) according to Definition
3.6
, by a standard iteration procedure (borrowed from
[
21
,
20
]
)
u
admits a chaos expansion as in (
3.9
) with
f_{k}
given by

f_{k}(s_{1},y_{1},\ldots,s_{k},y_{k},t,x)=\frac{\beta^{k}}{k!}p^{D}_{t-s_{\sigma(k)}}(x,y_{\sigma(k)})\cdots p^{D}_{s_{\sigma(2)}-s_{\sigma(1)}}(y_{\sigma(2)},y_{\sigma(1)})P^{D}_{s_{\sigma(1)}}u_{0}(y_{\sigma(1)}),

(3.10)

where
\sigma
denotes the permutation of
\{1,2,\cdots,k\}
such that
0<s_{\sigma(1)}<\cdots<s_{\sigma(k)}<t
. In this setting we then have the following result.

Proposition 3.7
.

A process
\mathcal{U}=\{u(t,x);(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\times U\}
solves equation (
3.6
) in the sense of Definition
3.6
if and only if for every
(t,x)
the random variable
u(t,x)
admits a chaos decomposition (
3.9
)-(
3.10
), where the family
\{f_{k}(\cdot,t,x);k\geq 1\}
satisfies

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k!\|f_{k}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}}^{2}<\infty.

(3.11)

The next section is devoted to proving that (
3.11
) holds true for all
\alpha\geq 0
, which will lead to existence and uniqueness for (
3.6
).

Remark 3.8
.

In the range
\alpha>{d_{h}}/{2d_{w}}
, one can also resort to Feynman-Kac representations for the solutions. More specifically, if
\alpha>d_{h}/2d_{w}
we have seen in Remark
3.5
that the field
W^{D}_{\alpha}
is Hölder continuous in the space variable (see (
3.4
)).
Hence under mild conditions on the initial condition
u_{0}
, the unique solution of (
3.5
) is given by the following Feynman-Kac formula with Wick exponential:

u(t,x)=\mathbf{E}^{B}\left(u_{0}(B^{x}_{t})\exp\left(\beta\int_{0}^{t}\dot{W}_{\alpha}^{D}(s,B^{x}_{t-s})ds-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}G^{D}_{2\alpha}(B^{x}_{s},B^{x}_{s})ds\right){1}_{(t<T)}\right)\,,

(3.12)

where
B
is Brownian motion on
U
independent from
W^{D}_{\alpha}
started from
x
and
T
is its hitting time of the complement of
U
in
X
. In (
3.12
), also note that
\mathbf{E}^{B}
denotes the expectation computed with respect to
B
only (later
\mathbf{E}^{W}
will designate the expectation with respect to
W
only).
Moreover, thanks to (
3.4
) the integral
\int_{0}^{t}\dot{W}_{\alpha}^{D}(s,B^{x}_{t-s})ds
is understood as a Wiener integral which is well defined for a fixed realization of
B^{x}
. The proof of formula (
3.12
) follows from an approximation procedure explained in
[
20
, Section 3.2]
, so we omit details for conciseness (see also the proof of Theorem
3.13
).

3.3
Existence, uniqueness of solutions and
L^{2}
estimates

As we have seen in Proposition
3.7
, existence and uniqueness for equation (
3.6
) boils down to checking relation (
3.11
). We start this section by getting some upper bounds in that direction.

Lemma 3.9
.

Consider
\alpha\geq 0
and the noise
W^{D}_{\alpha}
in Definition
3.2
and
3.3
. The space
\mathcal{H}
is given by (
3.1
). For any
\beta>0
and
k\geq 1
the function
f_{k}
is defined by (
3.10
). Consider an additional parameter
\rho>0
, whose exact value will be calibrated later. Then the following holds true:

(i)

If
0\leq\alpha<\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
, then for
t\geq 0
,
x\in U
we have,

\displaystyle\|f_{k}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}}^{2}\leq C^{k}e^{-2\lambda_{1}t}\frac{\beta^{2k}}{k!}\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{1+2\alpha-\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}}}\right)^{k}e^{\rho t}.

(3.13)

(ii)

If
\alpha=\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
, then for
t\geq 0
,
x\in U
it holds that

\displaystyle\|f_{k}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}}^{2}\leq C^{k}e^{-2\lambda_{1}t}\frac{\beta^{2k}}{k!}\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}\frac{\ln\left(\max(3/2,\rho)\right)^{2k}}{\rho^{k}}e^{\rho t}.

(3.14)

(iii)

If
\alpha>\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
, then for
t\geq 0
,
x\in U
and
\rho>0
,

\displaystyle\|f_{k}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}}^{2}\leq C^{k}e^{-2\lambda_{1}t}\frac{\beta^{2k}}{k!}\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}\frac{e^{\rho t}}{\rho^{k}}.

(3.15)

The constants
C
in each estimate above depend on
\alpha
,
d_{h}
and
d_{w}
.

Proof.

We will perform most of our computations for the case
\alpha>0
. The case
\alpha=0
results from simpler considerations and is left to the reader for the sake of conciseness. We now divide the proof in several steps.

Step 1: Reduction to an integral recursion.
By symmetry, in order to get (
3.13
)-(
3.15
) we only need to evaluate the
L^{2}
-norm of
f_{k}(\cdot,t,x)
on a particular time simplex
[0,t]^{k}_{<}:=\{(s_{1},\dots,s_{k}):0<s_{1}<\cdots<s_{k}<t\}
. For
(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\in[0,t]^{k}_{<}
we introduce the following notation:

\displaystyle g^{D}_{k}(s,y,t,x)=p_{t-s_{k}}^{D}(x,y_{k})\cdots p_{s_{2}-s_{1}}^{D}(y_{2},y_{1}),

(3.16)

where
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{k})
and
s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
.
By comparing (
3.16
) and (
3.10
) it is clear that on the simplex
[0,t]^{k}_{<}
we have

\displaystyle f_{k}(s,y,t,x)=\frac{\beta^{k}}{k!}g_{k}^{D}(s,y,t,x)P_{s_{1}}^{D}u_{0}(y_{1}).

(3.17)

Hence owing to Corollary
2.13
and taking into account all possible orderings of
s_{1},\dots,s_{k}
we get

\|f_{k}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}}^{2}\leq C\frac{\beta^{2k}}{k!}M_{k}(t,x)\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2},

(3.18)

where
M_{k}(t,x)
is given by

M_{k}(t,x)=\int_{[0,t]^{k}_{<}}\int_{U^{2k}}g_{k}^{D}(s,y,t,x)\prod_{i=1}^{k}G_{2\alpha}^{D}(y_{i},y_{i}^{\prime})g_{k}^{D}(s,y^{\prime},t,x)e^{-2\lambda_{1}s_{1}}d\mu(y)d\mu(y^{\prime})ds.

(3.19)

We now upper bound
M_{k}
defined by (
3.19
) thanks to a recursive procedure. To this aim, taking the expression (
3.16
) of
g^{D}_{k}
into account, observe that one can write (
3.19
) as

e^{2\lambda_{1}t}M_{k+1}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}e^{2\lambda_{1}(t-s_{k+1})}\int_{U^{2}}p_{t-s_{k+1}}(x,y_{k+1})\\
\cdot G_{2\alpha}^{D}(y_{k+1},y^{\prime}_{k+1})\,p_{t-s_{k+1}}(x,y^{\prime}_{k+1})\,e^{2\lambda_{1}s_{k+1}}M_{k}(s_{k+1},y_{k+1})d\mu(y_{k+1})d\mu(y^{\prime}_{k+1})\,ds_{k+1},

(3.20)

where we start the recursion with

M_{0}(t,x):=e^{-2\lambda_{1}t}
.
In the identity above, we shall bound the term
e^{2\lambda_{1}s_{k+1}}M_{k}(s_{k+1},y_{k+1})
by
N_{k}(s_{k+1})
, where we define

N_{k}(t)=e^{2\lambda_{1}t}\sup_{x\in U}M_{k}(t,x).

(3.21)

Hence resorting to Lemma
2.19
, we end up with the following relation:

N_{k+1}(t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}\Psi(t-s)N_{k}(s)ds,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002pt\Psi(t)=e^{2\lambda_{1}t}\sup_{x\in U}\|(-\Delta_{D})^{-\alpha}p_{t}^{D}(x,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(U,\mu)}^{2}.

(3.22)

Notice that one can initiate the recursion (
3.22
) by observing that
N_{0}(t)=1
.

Step 2: A renewal procedure.
To study the renewal inequality (
3.22
) and get the estimates we want to prove, we now appeal to an idea we learned from the proof of Theorem 1.1 in
[
22
]
(see also
[
14
]
for a related circle of ideas). Namely for our additional parameter
\rho>0
let us denote

\mathcal{N}_{k}(\rho)=\sup_{t\geq 0}e^{-\rho t}N_{k}(t).

(3.23)

From (
3.22
) we infer that

e^{-\rho t}N_{k+1}(t)\leq\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\rho(t-s)}\Psi(t-s)e^{-\rho s}N_{k}(s)ds\leq\left(\int_{0}^{t}e^{-\rho(t-s)}\Psi(t-s)ds\right)\mathcal{N}_{k}(\rho).

Therefore we have

\mathcal{N}_{k+1}(\rho)\leq\hat{\Psi}(\rho)\,\mathcal{N}_{k}(\rho),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002pt\hat{\Psi}(\rho)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\rho s}\Psi(s)ds.

(3.24)

Since
\mathcal{N}_{0}(\rho)=1
we thus conclude by induction that
\mathcal{N}_{k}(\rho)\leq\hat{\Psi}(\rho)^{k}
.
Hence going back to the definition (
3.23
), for every
t\geq 0
we obtain

\displaystyle N_{k}(t)\leq\hat{\Psi}(\rho)^{k}e^{\rho t}.

(3.25)

Having relation (
3.21
) in mind, our bound on
M_{k}(t)
is now reduced to an estimate on
\hat{\Psi}
defined by (
3.24
). We now upper bound the function
\hat{\Psi}
according to the values of
\alpha
. It follows from Lemma
2.22

that the function
\Psi
defined by (
3.22
) satisfies

\displaystyle\Psi(t)\leq\begin{cases}C(1+t^{2\alpha-\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}}),\quad&0\leq\alpha<\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}},\\
C|\ln\left(\min(1/2,t)\right)|^{2},\quad&\alpha=\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}},\\
C,\quad&\alpha>\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}},\end{cases}

(3.26)

so that using calculus

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}(\rho)\leq\begin{cases}C\left(\frac{1}{\rho}+\frac{\Gamma\left(1+2\alpha-\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}\right)}{\rho^{1+2\alpha-\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}}}\right),\quad&0\leq\alpha<\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}},\\
C\frac{\ln\left(\max(3/2,\rho)\right)^{2}}{\rho},\quad&\alpha=\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}},\\
\frac{C}{\rho},\quad&\alpha>\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}.\end{cases}

(3.27)

Indeed, the cases
0\leq\alpha<\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
and
\alpha>\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
in (
3.27
) are easily justified by integrating (
3.26
) with an exponential weight and using basic identities for Gamma functions. Some details about the case
\alpha=\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
are provided at the end of the proof.

Step 3: Conclusion.
With (
3.27
) in hand, we plug this inequality into (
3.25
) and then (
3.21
) and (
3.18
). This proves (
3.13
) for the case
\alpha<\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
. Inequalities (
3.14
) and (
3.15
) are proved along the same lines.

Step 4: Some details for (
3.27
).

We now give some hints about deriving (
3.27
) in the case
\alpha=\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
. Namely if one integrates (
3.26
) with the exponential weight
e^{-\rho t}
we get

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}(\rho)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\rho t}|\ln\left(\min(1/2,t)\right)|^{2}dt=\int_{0}^{1/2}e^{-\rho t}(\ln t)^{2}dt+C\int_{1/2}^{+\infty}e^{-\rho t}dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\rho t}(\ln t)^{2}dt+C\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\rho t}dt\leq J_{01}+\frac{C}{\rho}\,,

(3.28)

where we have set
J_{01}=\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\rho t}(\ln t)^{2}dt
. We now take care of the term
J_{01}
by separating the small
\rho
from the large
\rho
regime. Namely consider an arbitrary
\rho_{0}>1
. Then for
\rho\leq\rho_{0}
we simply bound the term
e^{-\rho t}
by
1
in the integral defining
J_{01}
. We derive

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\rho t}(\ln t)^{2}dt\leq C\int_{0}^{1}(\ln t)^{2}dt\leq C.

(3.29)

Next for
\rho>\rho_{0}
we perform the elementary change of variable
u=\rho t
. We obtain

\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\rho t}(\ln t)^{2}dt=\frac{(\ln\rho)^{2}}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\rho}e^{-u}du-2\frac{\ln\rho}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\rho}e^{-u}\ln u\,du+\frac{1}{\rho}\int_{0}^{\rho}e^{-u}(\ln u)^{2}du.

Bounding all the integrals over
(0,\rho)
above by (convergent) integrals over
(0,\infty)
, we end up with

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}e^{-\rho t}(\ln t)^{2}dt\leq C\frac{(\ln\rho)^{2}}{\rho}.

(3.30)

Summarizing our computations, we gather (
3.29
) and (
3.30
) in order to obtain

J_{01}\leq C\frac{(\ln\rho)^{2}}{\rho}.

Reporting this inequality into (
3.3
), this yields (
3.27
). ∎

We now state the existence and uniqueness result for the solution together with an estimate of the second moment.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
\alpha\geq 0
be a positive parameter and
W_{\alpha}^{D}
be the Gaussian noise in Definitions
3.2
and
3.3
. For
\beta\geq 0
we consider equation (
3.5
), that is

\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\Delta_{D}u(t,x)+\beta u(t,x)\,\dot{W}_{\alpha}^{D}(t,x),

(3.31)

interpreted in the mild form (
3.6
). We assume that the initial condition
u(0,\cdot)=u_{0}
lies in
L^{\infty}(U,\mu)
. Then (
3.31
) admits a unique solution given by Definition
3.6
. This solution moreover satisfies the following
L^{2}
-upper bound estimates: for every
x\in U

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{2})}{t}\leq\Theta_{\alpha}(\beta)-2\lambda_{1},

(3.32)

where
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}(U)
is the first eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{D}
and
\Theta_{\alpha}
is a continuous increasing function such that

(i)

If
0\leq\alpha<\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
, then
\Theta_{\alpha}(\beta)\sim_{\beta\to 0}C\beta^{2}
and
\Theta_{\alpha}(\beta)\sim_{\beta\to+\infty}C\beta^{\frac{2d_{w}}{(1+2\alpha)d_{w}-d_{h}}}
.

(ii)

If
\alpha=\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
, then
\Theta_{\alpha}(\beta)\sim_{\beta\to 0}C\beta^{2}
and
\Theta_{\alpha}(\beta)\sim_{\beta\to+\infty}C\beta^{2}(\ln\beta)^{2}
.

(iii)

If
\alpha>\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
, then
\Theta_{\alpha}(\beta)=C\beta^{2}
.

The constant
C>0
in each estimate above is different and depends on
\alpha
,
d_{h}
and
d_{w}
.

Proof.

The proof is an easy consequence of Lemma
3.9
. We will only give details about the case
0\leq\alpha<\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
, the other one being treated similarly. Also recall that if
\{f_{k};k\geq 1\}
is the family of functions defined by (
3.10
), then Proposition
3.7
states that existence-uniqueness is reduced to relation (
3.11
):

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k!\|f_{k}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}}^{2}<\infty.

(3.33)

Now observe that for
0\leq\alpha<\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
, the function
f_{k}
satisfies (
3.13
) for an arbitrary parameter
\rho>0
. Next we consider the unique real number
\rho_{c}>0
such that

\displaystyle C\beta^{2}F(\rho_{c})=1,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{with}\hskip 10.00002ptF(\rho):=\frac{1}{\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{1+2\alpha-\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}}},

(3.34)

where
C
is the same constant as in (
3.13
). In the sequel we pick a parameter
\rho
such that
\rho>\rho_{c}
. Therefore such a
\rho
satisfies

\delta_{\rho}:=C\beta^{2}F(\rho)<1.

Hence relation (
3.13
) can be recast as

\displaystyle\|f_{k}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}}^{2}\leq\frac{e^{(\rho-2\lambda_{1})t}}{k!}\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}\delta^{k}_{\rho},

(3.35)

from which it is easily seen that relation (
3.33
) holds true. This finishes the proof of existence and uniqueness. We now proceed to prove the
L^{2}
bounds in our claim (
3.32
). To this aim, notice that for the unique solution
u
to (
3.31
) we have

\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{2})=(P_{t}u_{0})^{2}(x)+\sum_{k=1}^{+\infty}k!\|f_{k}(\cdot,t,x)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}}^{2}.

Plugging relation (
3.35
) above and using Corollary
2.13
we obtain

\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{2})\leq c\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}e^{(\rho-2\lambda_{1})t}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\delta_{\rho}^{k}\leq C_{\rho}\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}^{2}e^{(\rho-2\lambda_{1})t}.

(3.36)

This yields

\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{2})}{t}\leq\rho_{c}-2\lambda_{1}.

(3.37)

Now recall from (
3.34
) that
\rho_{c}
satisfies
C\beta^{2}F(\rho_{c})=1
, so that
\rho_{c}=F^{-1}((C\beta^{2})^{-1})
. Furthermore we have

\lim_{\rho\to 0}\rho F(\rho)=1,\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\lim_{\rho\to\infty}\rho^{1+2\alpha-d_{h}/d_{w}}F(\rho)=1.

Inverting those relations and plugging into (
3.37
), the asymptotic behavior
of
\Theta_{\alpha}
in (i) follows.

Finally we mention that for the case
\alpha=\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
, similar argument as above gives (
3.37
), with the parameter
\rho_{c}
satisfying

C\beta^{2}\frac{\ln\left(\max(3/2,\rho_{c})\right)^{2}}{\rho_{c}}=1,

where
C
is the same constant as in (
3.14
). Consider
F(\rho)=\frac{\ln\left(\max(3/2,\rho)\right)^{2}}{\rho}
. We have
F(\rho)\sim\frac{(\ln 3/2)^{2}}{\rho}
when
\rho\to 0
and
F(\rho)\sim\frac{(\ln\rho)^{2}}{\rho}
when
\rho\to+\infty
. Hence we conclude the proof of (ii).
∎

3.4
L^{p}
upper bounds on the moments

An analysis of the asymptotic behavior of
L^{p}
moments usually brings useful information on the parabolic Anderson model. We derive an upper bound on those moments below.

Theorem 3.11
.

Under the same conditions as in Theorem
3.10
, let
u
be the unique solution to (
3.31
). Consider an integer
p>2
and let
\Theta_{\alpha}
be the function in (
3.32
). Then for all
x\in U
the following
L^{p}
-upper bound holds true:

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})}{t}\leq\frac{p}{2}\Theta_{\alpha}\left(\beta\sqrt{p-1}\right)-p\lambda_{1}.

(3.38)

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
3.10
, we will focus on the case
0\leq\alpha<\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
. The other regime for
\alpha
is treated in a similar way and left to the reader for sake of conciseness.
Next we use the fact that in a fixed Wiener chaos the
L^{p}
-norm and the
L^{2}
-norm are equivalent, see the last line of
[
35
, page 62]
. Namely,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left(I_{k}(f_{k}(\cdot,t,x))^{p}\right)^{1/p}\leq(p-1)^{k/2}\mathbf{E}\left(I_{k}(f_{k}(\cdot,t,x))^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

(3.39)

Now in order to estimate the
L^{p}
-norm of
u(t,x)
we resort to the decomposition (
3.9
) and Corollary
2.13
. These allow to write

\|u(t,x)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\|I_{k}(f_{k}(\cdot,t,x))\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}.

Thus owing to (
3.39
) we have

\displaystyle\|u(t,x)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(p-1)^{k/2}\|I_{k}(f_{k}(\cdot,t,x))\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(3.40)

Plugging our estimate (
3.13
) into (
3.40
) this yields

\displaystyle\|u(t,x)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{1}t}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}C^{k/2}(p-1)^{k/2}e^{\frac{\rho t}{2}-\lambda_{1}t}\beta^{k}\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho^{1+2\alpha-\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}}}\right)^{k/2}.

(3.41)

Hence for
p>2
we define a constant
\rho_{c,p}
similarly to (
3.34
), as the unique positive real number satisfying

\displaystyle C(p-1)\beta^{2}\left(\frac{1}{\rho_{c,p}}+\frac{1}{\rho_{c,p}^{1+2\alpha-\frac{d_{h}}{d_{w}}}}\right)=1,

(3.42)

where
C
is the constant in (
3.13
). We now pick a parameter
\rho>\rho_{c,p}
. With this value of
\rho
, the proof follows very closely the lines of Theorem
3.10
. Namely for
\rho>\rho_{c,p}
and similarly to (
3.36
) we obtain

\|u(t,x)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leq C_{\rho}\|u_{0}\|_{\infty}e^{\frac{\rho t}{2}-\lambda_{1}t}.

Then we proceed as for the end of the proof for Theorem
3.10
.
∎

3.5
Feynman-Kac representation for the moments

Inspired by
[
7
,
20
]
, the lower bounds on moments for the solution
u
to the stochastic heat equation will be obtained through Feynman-Kac formulae for moments. The current section is devoted to prove this type of formula, through a
L^{2}(\Omega)
-limit procedure relying on the following elementary lemma.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of
L^{2}
-random variables defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P})
. We assume that the following double limit exists:

\lim_{m,n\to\infty}\mathbf{E}(X_{n}X_{m})=a.

Then
X_{n}
converges in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

We can now state our Feynman-Kac formula for moments, provided our noise
W_{\alpha}
is smoother than white noise.

Theorem 3.13
.

Consider a parameter
\alpha>0
and let the conditions of Theorem
3.10
prevail. Then the moments of the unique solution
u
to (
3.31
) admit the following representation for
t\geq 0
,
x\in U
and
p\geq 2
:

\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})=\mathbf{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{p}u_{0}(B^{x,j}_{t})\exp\left\{\beta^{2}\sum_{1\leq i,k\leq p,\,i\not=k}\int_{0}^{t}G_{2\alpha}^{D}(B^{x,i}_{s},B^{x,k}_{s})ds\right\}1_{(t<T^{j})}\right).

(3.43)

In formula (
3.43
), we have used a family
\{B^{x,j},1\leq j\leq p\}
of independent Brownian motions which are initiated from
x\in U
and are independent of the noise
W_{\alpha}
. For
1\leq j\leq p
we also denote by
T^{j}
the hitting time of
U^{c}
in
X
.

Proof.

The proof of our claim (
3.43
) follows from a standard procedure borrowed from
[
20
,
21
]
. We just highlight the main steps below. In the remainder of the proof we write
W
instead of
W_{\alpha}
for the sake of notation.

First consider a mollifier
p_{\varepsilon}(x)
in space. An example might be provided by
p_{\varepsilon}(x)=c\,p_{\varepsilon}^{D}(x)
, where
p_{\varepsilon}^{D}(x)
is the Dirichlet heat kernel on
U
and
c
a normalizing constant such that
c\int_{U}p_{\varepsilon}^{D}(x)dx=1
. Also consider a mollifier
\tilde{p}_{\delta}(t)
in time. Then a mollified noise can be defined for
\varepsilon,\delta>0
as

\displaystyle W^{\delta,\varepsilon}(t,x)=(\tilde{p}_{\delta}p_{\varepsilon})*{W}=W(\tilde{p}_{\delta}(t-\cdot)p_{\varepsilon}(x,\cdot)).

(3.44)

Now consider equation (
3.31
) driven by the smooth noise
W^{\delta,\varepsilon}
, interpreted in the Skorokhod sense. Similarly to
[
21
, Proposition 5.2]
, one can prove that this equation admits a unique solution
u^{\delta,\varepsilon}
. Moreover this solution can be written through a Feynman-Kac representation as

\displaystyle u^{\delta,\varepsilon}(t,x)=\mathbf{E}^{B}\left(u_{0}(B^{x}_{t})\exp\left(\beta Z_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{B}(t,x)-\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbf{E}^{W}[Z^{B}_{\delta,\varepsilon}(t,x)]^{2}\right)1_{(t<T)}\right).

(3.45)

In equation (
3.45
),
B
is a Brownian motion independent of
W
and
\mathbf{E}^{B}
(respectively
\mathbf{E}^{W}
) denotes the expected value with respect to
B
(respectively
W
) only. In addition, the random variable
Z_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{B}(t,x)
is defined by

\displaystyle Z_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{B}(t,x):=\int_{0}^{t}W^{\delta,\varepsilon}(t-s,B_{s}^{x})ds=W\left(\int_{0}^{t}\tilde{p}_{\delta}((t-s)-\cdot)p_{\varepsilon}(B^{x}_{s},\cdot)ds\right).

(3.46)

We shall now take limits in (
3.45
) in order to get our representation of moments. We will focus on the case
p=2
for sake of conciseness. The case
p>2
is similar.

We shall first take limits as
\delta\to 0
in the random variable
Z_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{B}(t,x)
. Namely for
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0
and
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}>0
and two independent Brownian motions
B^{1}
,
B^{2}
initiated from
x\in U
, let us compute
\mathbf{E}^{W}\big(Z_{\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{1}}^{B^{1}}(t,x)Z_{\delta_{2},\varepsilon_{2}}^{B^{2}}(t,x)\big)
(where we recall that
\mathbf{E}^{W}
designates the expected value with respect to
W
only). We get

\begin{split}\mathbf{E}^{W}\left(Z_{\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{1}}^{B^{1}}(t,x)Z_{\delta_{2},\varepsilon_{2}}^{B^{2}}(t,x)\right)=&\,\mathbf{E}^{W}\left(\int_{0}^{t}W^{\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{1}}(t-s,B_{s}^{1})ds\int_{0}^{t}W^{\delta_{2},\varepsilon_{2}}(t-s,{B}_{s}^{2})ds\right)\\
=&\int_{[0,t]^{2}}\int_{[0,\infty)\times U^{2}}\tilde{p}_{\delta_{1}}((t-s_{1})-v)\tilde{p}_{\delta_{2}}((t-s_{2})-v)\\
&\times p_{\varepsilon_{1}}({B}^{1}_{s_{1}},z_{1})p_{\varepsilon_{2}}({B}^{2}_{s_{2}},z_{2})G_{2\alpha}^{D}(z_{1},z_{2})dvdz_{1}dz_{2}ds_{1}ds_{2},\end{split}

(3.47)

where we have resorted to the definition (
3.44
) of
W^{\delta,\varepsilon}
and formula (
3.2
) for the covariance of
W
. Now by smoothness of
p_{\varepsilon}
one can easily take limits in (
3.47
) in order to obtain

\begin{split}&\lim_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\to 0}\mathbf{E}^{W}\left(Z_{\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{1}}^{B^{1}}(t,x)Z_{\delta_{2},\varepsilon_{2}}^{B^{2}}(t,x)\right)\\
&=\int_{[0,t]\times U^{2}}p_{\varepsilon_{1}}({B}^{1}_{v},z_{1})p_{\varepsilon_{2}}({B}^{2}_{v},z_{2})G_{2\alpha}^{D}(z_{1},z_{2})dvdz_{1}dz_{2}.\end{split}

(3.48)

Let us report the information about
Z_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{B}(t,x)
into the Feynman-Kac representation (
3.46
). To this aim we use the fact that
Z_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{B}(t,x)
is a centered Gaussian random variable conditionally on
B
. Moreover, for a centered Gaussian vector
(Y_{1},Y_{2})
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
we have
\mathbf{E}(e^{Y_{1}}e^{Y_{2}})=\exp(\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{E}(Y_{1}Y_{2}))
. Therefore one can compute

\begin{split}&\mathbf{E}\left(u^{\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{1}}(t,x)u^{\delta_{2},\varepsilon_{2}}(t,x)\right)\\
&=\mathbf{E}\left(u_{0}(B^{1}_{t})u_{0}({B}^{2}_{t})\exp\left(\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}\mathbf{E}^{W}[Z_{\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{1}}^{B^{1}}(t,x)Z_{\delta_{2},\varepsilon_{2}}^{B^{2}}(t,x)]\right)1_{(t<T_{1})}1_{(t<T_{2})}\right)\,,\end{split}

(3.49)

where we notice that the diagonal terms involving
Z_{\delta,\varepsilon}^{B^{i}}
cancel out with the martingale type term
\mathbf{E}^{W}[Z^{B}_{\delta,\varepsilon}(t,x)]^{2}
in (
3.45
).

One can now take limits in (
3.49
) as
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}\to 0
, like in (
3.48
). Then similar limits in
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}
can be taken, where the arguments follow from
[
20
]
. We end up with

\begin{split}&\lim_{\varepsilon_{1},\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{2},\delta_{2}\to 0}\mathbf{E}\left(u^{\delta_{1},\varepsilon_{1}}(t,x)u^{\delta_{2},\varepsilon_{2}}(t,x)\right)\\
&=\mathbf{E}\left(u_{0}(B^{1}_{t})u_{0}({B}^{2}_{t})\exp\left(\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}{\int_{0}^{t}G_{2\alpha}^{D}(B_{v}^{1},{B}_{v}^{2})dv}\right)1_{(t<T_{1})}1_{(t<T_{2})}\right).\end{split}

(3.50)

In the above, we have used our assumption
\alpha>0
so that
G_{2\alpha}^{D}(B^{1}_{v},B^{2}_{v})
is well-defined (even though it could blowup when
B^{1}_{v}=B^{2}_{v}
). Moreover, that the second line of (
3.50
) remains finite is a consequence of Lemma
3.9
(after Taylor-expanding the exponential).
Therefore owing to Lemma
3.12
the sequence
\{u^{\delta,\varepsilon}(t,x),\delta,\epsilon>0\}
converges in
L^{2}
. Formula (
3.43
) for
p=2
is then a matter of standard considerations for which we refer again to
[
20
]
.
∎

Remark 3.14
.

From the proof of Theorem
3.13
, we see that
u^{\delta,\varepsilon}
converges to
u
in
L^{2}
as
\delta,\varepsilon\to 0
. On the other hand, we clearly have
u^{\delta,\varepsilon}\geq 0
from the Feynman-Kac representation (
3.45
) if one assumes
u_{0}\geq 0.
Therefore, we conclude that
u\geq 0
if
u_{0}\geq 0
.

3.6
Lower bounds for the moments of the solution

With the Feynman-Kac representation (
3.43
) in hand, we are now ready to study the asymptotic behavior of
t\mapsto\mathbf{E}[u(t,x)^{p}]
for
p\geq 2
. More specifically, we are interested in lower bounds for the Lyapounov exponent
\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})}{t}
. Unlike the upper bound case, it will be important to separate the colored case
\alpha>0
from the white noise case
\alpha=0
due to differences in methodology.

3.6.1
The case
\alpha>0

We begin this section with a general estimate which gives the correct rate in terms of the temperature parameter
\beta
in the case of a noise
W^{D}_{\alpha}
which is more regular than white noise.

Theorem 3.15
.

Let
\alpha,\beta
be two strictly positive parameters. The noise
W^{D}_{\alpha}
is given in Definition
3.2
. Let
u
be the unique solution to equation (
3.31
). In addition, we consider an open subset
A\subset U
such that
\overline{A}\subset U
. The initial condition
u_{0}
of (
3.31
) is assumed to be in
L^{\infty}(U)
, nonnegative, and such that
u_{0}|_{A}\geq\varepsilon
for a given
\varepsilon>0
. Then for
x\in A
and
t\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})\geq\varepsilon^{p}\exp\left(C\beta^{2}p(p-1)t\right)(P^{D,A}_{t}1)(x)^{p},

(3.51)

where
C
is a positive constant that depends on
A
and
\alpha
but not
\beta
or
p
and
P^{D,A}_{t}
is the Dirichlet heat semigroup on
A
.
In particular, for
x
in
A

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})}{t}\geq C\beta^{2}p(p-1)-p\lambda_{1}(A),

(3.52)

where
\lambda_{1}(A)>0
is the first eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{D,A}
.

Proof.

Let us start from the representation (
3.43
) for
\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})
. Let us call
T_{A}^{i}
the hitting time of
A^{c}
by the Brownian motion
B^{i}
. Using the fact that
u_{0}
is positive and
1_{(t<T^{j})}\geq 1_{(t<T_{A}^{j})}
we have

\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})\geq\mathbf{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{p}u_{0}(B_{t}^{x,j})1_{(t<T_{A}^{j})}\exp\left\{\beta^{2}\sum_{1\leq i,k\leq p,i\not=k}\int_{0}^{t}G_{2\alpha}^{D}(B^{x,i}_{s},B^{x,k}_{s})ds\right\}\right).

(3.53)

Moreover since
A
is a bounded set, the lower bounds in (
2.32
), (
2.33
), (
2.34
) imply

\exp\left(\beta^{2}\sum_{1\leq i,k\leq p,\,i\not=k}\int_{0}^{t}G_{2\alpha}^{D}(B^{x,i}_{s},B^{x,k}_{s})ds\right)\geq\exp\left(\beta^{2}c\,\hat{m}^{2}p(p-1)t\right),

(3.54)

where
\phi_{1}
is the first eigenvector for
\Delta_{D,U}
and
\hat{m}
is given by
\hat{m}=\inf\{\phi_{1}(y);y\in A\}
.
Since
\hat{m}=\hat{m}_{A}>0
according to Remark
2.8
, we get that there exists
C=C_{A}
such that

\exp\left(\beta^{2}\sum_{1\leq i,k\leq p,\,i\not=k}\int_{0}^{t}G_{2\alpha}^{D}(B^{x,i}_{s},B^{x,k}_{s})ds\right)\prod_{j=1}^{p}1_{(t<T_{A}^{j})}\geq\exp\left(\beta^{2}Cp(p-1)t\right)\prod_{j=1}^{p}1_{(t<T_{A}^{j})}.

Plugging this inequality into (
3.53
) we end up with the following lower bound, valid for
x\in A
and
t
sufficiently large,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon^{p}\exp\left(C\beta^{2}p(p-1)t\right)\mathbf{E}\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{p}1_{t<T_{A}^{j}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{p}\exp\left(C\beta^{2}p(p-1)t\right)\left[P^{D,A}_{t}1(x)\right]^{p},

(3.55)

where we have invoked (
2.6
) for the second identity. This proves our claim (
3.51
).

In order to prove (
3.52
), we argue that since the first eigenfunction of
\Delta_{D,A}
is
positive on
A
(see Remark
2.8
again), it follows from the spectrum expansion that for
x\in A
and
t
sufficiently large

\displaystyle(P_{t}^{D,A}1)(x)\geq Ce^{-\lambda_{1}(A)t}.

(3.56)

Therefore gathering (
3.6.1
) and (
3.56
) we discover that

\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})\geq C\varepsilon^{p}\exp\left(C\beta^{2}p(p-1)t-p\lambda_{1}(A)t\right),

from which (
3.52
) is easily obtained. The proof is now complete.
∎

The estimate (
3.52
) gives the correct order in
\beta
for a noise
W_{\alpha}
in the more regular regime
\alpha>d_{h}/2d_{w}
. We can get the following improvement in the range
0<\alpha\leq\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
.

Theorem 3.16
.

As in Theorem
3.15
, we consider the noise
W^{D}_{\alpha}
in Definition
3.2
with
\alpha>0
. Let
\beta>0
and let also
u_{0}
be a bounded and nonnegative initial condition. Let
x\in U
, and assume that
u_{0}
that is bounded from below by a positive constant on a neighborhood of
x
. The process
u
is the unique solution to equation (
3.31
). Then one can improve the lower bounds on
\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})
from Theorem
3.15
in the following way:

•

For
0<\alpha<\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
,

\displaystyle\liminf_{\beta\to+\infty}\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\frac{1}{\beta^{\frac{2d_{w}}{(1+2\alpha)d_{w}-d_{h}}}}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})}{t}\geq Cp(p-1).

(3.57)

•

For
\alpha=\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
,

\liminf_{\beta\to+\infty}\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\frac{1}{\beta^{2}\ln\beta}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})}{t}\geq Cp(p-1).

Proof.

We will only deal with the case
0<\alpha<\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
. The case
\alpha=\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
is almost identical and left to the reader. As in (
3.53
) we start by using the Feynman-Kac representation (
3.43
). In this representation we lower bound the stopping time
T^{j}
by another stopping time
T^{j}_{\varepsilon}
defined in the following way: let
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}<r_{0}
, where
r_{0}
is such that
B(x,r_{0})\subset U
and
\varepsilon_{0}
is chosen so that
u_{0}(y)\geq c_{1}^{1/p}>0
on
B(x,\varepsilon_{0})
. Then we set

T_{\varepsilon}^{j}=\inf\{t\geq 0,B_{t}^{j}\notin B(x,\varepsilon)\}.

Since
T^{j}>T^{j}_{\varepsilon}
we deduce from (
3.43
) that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})

\displaystyle\geq c_{1}\mathbf{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{p}\exp\left\{\beta^{2}\sum_{1\leq i,k\leq p,i\not=k}\int_{0}^{t}G_{2\alpha}^{D}(B^{x,i}_{s},B^{x,k}_{s})ds\right\}1_{(t<T_{\varepsilon}^{j})}\right).

Furthermore one can argue as in (
3.54
) and use the lower bound in Proposition
2.21
(applied with
\alpha:=2\alpha
) in order to get the existence of a constant
c_{2}>0
such that

\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})\geq c_{1}\mathbf{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{p}\exp\left\{c_{2}\beta^{2}\sum_{1\leq i,k\leq p,i\not=k}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{ds}{d_{U}(B_{s}^{x,i},B_{s}^{x,k})^{d_{h}-2\alpha d_{w}}}\right\}1_{(t<T_{\varepsilon}^{j})}\right).

(3.58)

Next we resort to some explicit lower bounds in (
3.58
). Namely for
s\leq\min_{j}T^{j}_{\varepsilon}
all the Brownian motions
B^{j}
are confined in
B_{U}(x,\varepsilon)
. Hence we have
d_{U}(B_{s}^{x,i},B_{s}^{x,k})\leq 2\varepsilon
for all
i\not=k
. This yields

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})\geq c_{1}\exp\left(c_{3}p(p-1)\frac{\beta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{d_{h}-2\alpha d_{w}}}t\right)\mathbf{P}\left(T_{\varepsilon}^{1}>t\right)^{p}.

(3.59)

In addition, using again classical spectral theory arguments we get for
t
sufficiently large

\mathbf{P}\left(T_{\varepsilon}^{1}>t\right)\geq c_{4}e^{-\lambda_{1}(B(x,\varepsilon))t}.

Moreover, it is known that the sub-Gaussian bound on the heat kernel (
2.3
) implies that
\lambda_{1}(B(x,\varepsilon))
is bounded from above by
\frac{c}{\varepsilon^{d_{w}}}
(see
[
32
, Lemma 7.1]
). We therefore obtain

\mathbf{P}\left(T_{\varepsilon}^{1}>t\right)\geq c_{4}e^{-c_{5}\frac{t}{\varepsilon^{d_{w}}}}.

Plugging this inequality into (
3.59
) we end up with

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})\geq c_{6}\exp\left\{\left(c_{3}p(p-1)\frac{\beta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{d_{h}-2\alpha d_{w}}}-p\frac{c_{5}}{\varepsilon^{d_{w}}}\right)t\right\}.

(3.60)

We now optimize (
3.60
) over the values of
\varepsilon
. To this aim, recall from Assumption
2.3
that
d_{h}-d_{w}<0
. Hence we also have
d_{h}-d_{w}<2\alpha d_{w}
and thus
d_{h}-2\alpha d_{w}<d_{w}
. Therefore one can pick a constant
c_{7}>0
large enough so that

\frac{c_{3}}{c_{7}^{d_{h}-2\alpha d_{w}}}-\frac{c_{5}}{c_{7}^{d_{w}}}>0\,.

Next we choose
\varepsilon
of the form
c_{7}\beta^{\gamma}
with
\gamma>0
so that the two terms in the exponent in (
3.60
) are of the same magnitude. This is achieved whenever

\frac{\beta^{2}}{\varepsilon^{d_{h}-2\alpha d_{w}}}\simeq\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{d_{w}}}
which yields

\varepsilon\simeq\beta^{-\frac{2}{(1+2\alpha)d_{w}-d_{h}}}.

Summarizing those considerations we will pick
\varepsilon
of the form

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\frac{c_{7}}{\beta^{\frac{2}{(1+2\alpha)d_{w}-d_{h}}}},

(3.61)

and notice that for
\beta
large enough we indeed have
\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
. Plugging the value (
3.61
) into (
3.60
), this allows to conclude that for
\beta
large enough

\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})\geq c_{8}\exp\left\{c_{9}p(p-1)\beta^{\frac{2d_{w}}{(1+2\alpha)d_{w}-d_{h}}}t\right\},

which easily implies our claim (
3.57
).

Let us briefly give some hints about the case
\alpha=\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
. The only difference with (
3.57
) is that the lower bound in Proposition
2.21
yields

\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})\geq c_{6}\exp\left\{\left(-c_{3}p(p-1)\beta^{2}\ln\varepsilon-p\frac{c_{5}}{\varepsilon^{d_{w}}}\right)t\right\}\,.

Then in the optimization step we now need to choose
\varepsilon=\frac{1}{\beta^{2/d_{w}}}
.
∎

3.6.2
Lower bound for the moments in the case
\alpha=0

Our moment representation Theorem
3.13
was relying on the assumption
\alpha>0
. When
\alpha=0
, a similar representation would require a proper definition of intersection local times for independent Brownian motions on the uniform inner domain
U
. This is clearly out of the scope of the current article and is deferred to a subsequent publication. Our strategy to lower bound moments in the white noise case
\alpha=0
is thus inspired by a direct analytic method spelled out in
[
15
]
, with the caveat that it only works for
p=2
.

Theorem 3.17
.

Let
\beta>0
and consider an inner uniform domain
U
. Denote by
u
the unique solution to (
3.31
) driven by the white noise
W_{0}
in Definition
3.3
. We assume the initial condition
u_{0}
to be bounded and nonnegative on
U
. Let
x\in U
and assume that
u_{0}
is bounded from below by a positive constant in a neighborhood of
x
. Recall that
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{1}(U)
designates the first eigenvalue of
-\Delta_{D,U}
. Then
u
satisfies the following
L^{2}
-lower bound estimate:

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{2})}{t}\geq\Phi(\beta^{2})-2\lambda_{1},

(3.62)

where
\Phi
is the inverse function of
y\to c_{1}ye^{c_{2}y^{1/d_{w}}}
and
c_{1},c_{2}>0
are positive constants that do not depend on
\beta
but depend on
x
.

Proof.

Let
x\in U
and recall that we assume
u_{0}
is bounded from below by a positive constant in a neighborhood of
x
. Since
U
is specified in Definition
2.5
to be an open set, one can consider
r>0
such that
B(x,2r)\subset U
and such that
u_{0}(y)\geq c>0
for all
y\in B(x,r)
. We now split the proof in a few steps.

Step 1: Deriving a renewal equation.
Consider
y\in B(x,r)
with
r
as above. We compute the second moment of
u(t,y)
from the mild formulation (
3.6
) of the equation. Invoking the fact that the term
I(t,x)
is centered and applying Itô’s isometry with the inner product (
3.2
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left(u(t,y)^{2}\right)=\left(P_{t}^{D}u_{0}(y)\right)^{2}+\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}p_{t-s}^{D}(y,z)^{2}\,\mathbf{E}\left(u(s,z)^{2}\right)d\mu(z)ds.

(3.63)

Since
u_{0}(z)>c
on
B(x,r)
, we thus get

\mathbf{E}\left(u(t,y)^{2}\right)\geq c^{2}\left(P_{t}^{D}1_{B(x,r)}(y)\right)^{2}+\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B(x,r/2)}p_{t-s}^{D}(y,z)^{2}\,\mathbf{E}\left(u(s,z)^{2}\right)d\mu(z)ds.

From Lemma
2.9
, this gives that for every
y\in B(x,r/2)
,

\mathbf{E}\left(u(t,y)^{2}\right)\geq c^{2}e^{-2\lambda_{1}t}+\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B(x,r/2)}p_{t-s}^{D}(y,z)^{2}\mathbf{E}\left(u(s,z)^{2}\right)d\mu(z)ds.

By denoting
\phi(t)=\inf_{y\in B(x,r/2)}\mathbf{E}\left(u(t,y)^{2}\right)
, we get the following inequality

\displaystyle\phi(t)\geq c^{2}e^{-2\lambda_{1}t}+\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\inf_{y\in B(x,r/2)}\int_{B(x,r/2)}p_{t-s}^{D}(y,z)^{2}d\mu(z)\right)\phi(s)ds.

(3.64)

We now invoke the lower bound (
2.9
), together with the fact that the eigenfunction
\Phi_{1}
is strictly positive on
B(x,r/2)=B_{U}(x,r/2)
, in order to get

\displaystyle\inf_{y\in B(x,r/2)}\int_{B(x,r/2)}p_{t-s}^{D}(y,z)^{2}d\mu(z)

\displaystyle\geq c_{1}\exp\left(-2\lambda_{1}(t-s)-\frac{c_{2}}{(t-s)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}}\right).

(3.65)

Gathering (
3.64
) and (
3.65
), we thus get the renewal identity

\displaystyle\psi(t)\geq c_{1}+c_{2}\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{3}}{(t-s)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}}\right)\psi(s)ds,\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{where}\ \ \psi(t)=e^{2\lambda_{1}t}\phi(t).

(3.66)

It is then easily deduced from (
3.66
) that
\psi(t)\geq\zeta(t)
where
\zeta(t)
solves the renewal equation

\displaystyle\zeta(t)=c_{1}+c_{2}\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{3}}{(t-s)^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}}\right)\zeta(s)ds.

(3.67)

Step 2: Asymptotic behavior of
\zeta
in the renewal equation.
Our lower bound (
3.62
) is now reduced to a lower bound on the function
\zeta
defined by (
3.67
). Furthermore, the estimate for
\zeta
in (
3.67
) is based on Laplace transform techniques. That is, set
\hat{\zeta}(\rho)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}e^{-\rho t}\zeta(t)dt
for all
\rho>0
. Applying Laplace transform on both sides of (
3.67
) yields

\displaystyle\hat{\zeta}(\rho)=\frac{c_{1}}{\rho}+c_{2}\beta^{2}\hat{\mu}(\rho)\hat{\zeta}(\rho),\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{where}\hskip 10.00002pt\hat{\mu}(\rho)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\exp\left(-\rho t-\frac{c_{3}}{t^{\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}}\right)dt.

(3.68)

Equation (
3.68
) is now easily solved algebraically and we end up with

\displaystyle\hat{\zeta}(\rho)=\frac{c_{1}}{\rho(1-c_{2}\beta^{2}\hat{\mu}(\rho))}\,.

(3.69)

In order to ensure a proper definition for the right hand-side of (
3.69
), we just want to make sure that
c_{2}\beta^{2}\hat{\mu}(\rho)<1
. This is achieved by monotonicity arguments whenever
\rho>\rho_{c}
, where
\rho_{c}
is the unique positive number such that

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}(\rho_{c})=\frac{1}{c_{2}\beta^{2}}.

(3.70)

In addition, due to the fact that
\hat{\mu}
is a differentiable function on
\{\rho>0\}
, there exists a constant
c>0
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\downarrow\rho_{c}}(\rho-\rho_{c})\hat{\zeta}(\rho)=c.

(3.71)

As explained e.g. in
[
26
]
, the Wiener-Ikahara theorem for Laplace transforms with poles states that (
3.70
) implies

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\ln\zeta(t)}{t}=\rho_{c}.

(3.72)

Step 3: Estimating
\rho_{c}
.
In order to estimate
\rho_{c}
in (
3.72
) we go back to its definition (
3.70
). Then we estimate
\hat{\mu}(\rho)
as given in (
3.68
) in the following two regimes.

(1) Case of a large
\rho
.
In this situation we use Laplace’s method. Namely denote
f_{\rho}(t):=\rho t+{c_{3}}t^{-\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}
. Clearly it has unique global minimum at a point
t^{*}
of the form
t^{*}=(\frac{c_{3}}{(d_{w}-1)\rho})^{(d_{w}-1)/d_{w}}
. Moreover we have

\displaystyle f_{\rho}(t^{*})=C_{1}\rho^{\frac{1}{d_{w}}},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptf_{\rho}^{\prime\prime}(t^{*})=C_{2}\rho^{2-\frac{1}{d_{w}}}\,,

(3.73)

where
C_{1},C_{2}
are positive constants that depend on
c_{3}
and
d_{w}
. By Laplace’s method we know that
\hat{\mu}(\rho)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-f_{\rho}(t)}dt
is comparable to
e^{-f_{\rho}(t^{*})}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{|f_{\rho}^{\prime\prime}(t^{*})|}}.

Plugging (
3.73
) into the above expression we find that for
\rho
away from 0 we have some constants
c_{4},c_{5},c_{6},c_{7}>0
such that

\frac{c_{4}}{\rho^{1-\frac{1}{2d_{w}}}}e^{-c_{5}\rho^{1/d_{w}}}\leq\hat{\mu}(\rho)\leq\frac{c_{6}}{\rho^{1-\frac{1}{2d_{w}}}}e^{-c_{7}\rho^{1/d_{w}}}.

(2) Case
\rho
close to
0
.
For
\rho
close to
0
, note that

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}(\rho)\geq\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}e^{-c_{3}t^{-\frac{1}{d_{w}-1}}}dt\geq e^{-c_{3}}\frac{1}{\rho}e^{-\rho},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\hat{\mu}(\rho)\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\rho t}dt=\frac{1}{\rho}.

(3.74)

Therefore as
\rho\to 0
, we end up with
\hat{\mu}(\rho)\simeq C\rho^{-1}
.

Summarizing the estimates for small and large
\rho
, we have thus obtained that there exist some positive constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}
such that for all
\rho>0
we have

\frac{C_{1}}{\rho}e^{-C_{2}\rho^{1/d_{w}}}\leq\hat{\mu}(\rho)\leq\frac{C_{3}}{\rho}e^{-C_{4}\rho^{1/d_{w}}}.

Reporting this bound in (
3.70
) yields the existence of a constant
C_{5}
such that

\beta^{2}\leq C_{5}\rho_{c}e^{C_{2}\rho_{c}^{{1}/{d_{w}}}}\,\hskip 10.00002pt\Longleftrightarrow\hskip 10.00002pt\rho_{c}\geq\Phi(\beta^{2})\,,

where
\Phi
denotes the inverse function of
x\to C_{5}xe^{C_{2}x^{1/d_{w}}}
. Inserting this inequality in (
3.72
) we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\ln\zeta(t)}{t}=\rho_{c}\geq\Phi(\beta^{2}).

(3.75)

Step 4: Conclusion.
We now conclude in the following way: recall that
\psi(t)\geq\zeta(t)
and we have seen that
\zeta(t)
verifies (
3.75
). Therefore
\ln(\psi(t))/t
verifies (
3.75
) as well. In addition, owing to (
3.66
), we have

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{\phi(t)}{t}=\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{\psi(t)}{t}-2\lambda_{1}\geq\Phi(\beta^{2})-2\lambda_{1}.

Recalling that
\phi(t)=\inf_{y\in B(x,r/2)}\mathbf{E}(u(t,y)^{2})
, this proves our claim (
3.62
).
∎

When the boundary of a domain
A
satisfies a cone-type condition, one can substantially improve the lower bound (
3.62
). This is summarized in the theorem below.

Theorem 3.18
.

Under the same conditions as for Theorem
3.17
, let
A
be an open subset of
U
such that
\overline{A}\subset U
and such that
A
satisfies the hypothesis (
2.18
). We consider an initial condition
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(U)
such that
u_{0}
is non negative and
u_{0}\geq\varepsilon>0
on
A
. Then the solution
u
to (
3.31
) is such that for all
p\geq 2
and for all
x\in A
we have

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{2})}{t}\geq c\beta^{\frac{2d_{w}}{d_{w}-d_{h}}}-2\lambda_{1},

(3.76)

where
c>0
does not depend on
\beta
.

Proof.

The expression (
3.63
) for the second moment of
u(t,y)
is still valid. Furthermore, we have
u_{0}(z)\geq\varepsilon 1_{A}(z)
. Hence we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left(u(t,y)^{2}\right)\geq\varepsilon^{2}\left(P_{t}^{D}1_{A}(y)\right)^{2}+\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{A}p_{t-s}^{D}(y,z)^{2}\mathbf{E}\left(u(s,z)^{2}\right)d\mu(z)ds.

Similarly to what we did in the proof of Theorem
3.17
, set

\phi(t)=e^{2\lambda_{1}t}\inf_{x\in A}\mathbf{E}\left(u(t,x)^{2}\right).

Along the same lines as for (
3.64
)-(
3.65
)-(
3.66
) and resorting to relation (
2.20
), which is valid whenever (
2.18
) is fulfilled, we get that
\phi(t)\geq\zeta(t)
where
\zeta
solves the renewal equation

\displaystyle\zeta(t)=c_{1}+c_{2}\beta^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{(t-s)^{d_{h}/d_{w}}}\zeta(s)ds.

(3.77)

Next we still follow closely the argument of Theorem
3.17
, the main difference being that now we can compute explicitly the Laplace transform of
t\mapsto t^{-d_{h}/d_{w}}
. Namely applying Laplace transform on both sides of (
3.77
) we get

\hat{\zeta}(\rho)=\frac{c_{1}}{\rho}+c_{2}\beta^{2}\hat{\mu}(\rho)\hat{\zeta}(\rho),\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{where}\ \ \hat{\mu}(\rho)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{-\rho t}}{t^{d_{h}/d_{w}}}dt=c_{3}\rho^{-1+d_{h}/d_{w}}.

This equation can be solved algebraically and we get

\hat{\zeta}(\rho)=\frac{c_{1}}{\rho^{d_{h}/d_{w}}\left(\rho^{1-d_{h}/d_{w}}-\beta^{2}c_{2}c_{3}\right)}.

In particular, the positive pole for
\hat{\zeta}
is of the form

\rho_{c}=c_{4}\beta^{\frac{2d_{w}}{d_{w}-d_{h}}}.

The same steps as for Theorem
3.17
yield our claim (
3.76
).
∎

3.7
Some properties of the solutions, Intermittency

As mentioned in the introduction, when considering a Dirichlet type boundary condition in an inner uniform domain, the parabolic Anderson model exhibits an interesting phase transition for the behavior of moments. This is spelled out below.

Corollary 3.19
.

Let
U
be an inner uniform domain and
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(U)
a non negative initial condition. We consider a noise
W_{\alpha}
with
\alpha\geq 0
and the corresponding solution
u
to the equation (
3.31
). Let also
p\geq 2
and
x\in U
. Then there exists
\beta_{c,1}>0
such that for all
\beta\leq\beta_{c,1}
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})=0.

(3.78)

If we assume moreover that
\alpha>0
, and that
u_{0}
satisfies
u_{0}>\varepsilon>0
in a neighborhood of
x
, then there exists
\beta_{c,2}>0
such that for every
\beta\geq\beta_{c,2}

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})=+\infty.

(3.79)

Proof.

The first assertion (
3.78
) is derived from the upper bound (
3.38
) in Theorem
3.11
for
p\geq 2
(see (
3.32
) for the particular case
p=2
). Specifically, identity (
3.78
) is satisfied for a fixed
p
as long as the right hand side

h_{\alpha,\beta}:=\frac{p}{2}\Theta_{\alpha}(\beta\sqrt{p-1})-p\lambda_{1}

verifies
h_{\alpha,\beta}<0
for
\beta<\beta_{c,1}
. Now it is trivially checked from Theorem
3.10
that for all
\alpha\geq 0

\lim_{\beta\to 0}\Theta_{\alpha}(\beta\sqrt{p-1})=0.

We thus get our desired conclusion
h_{\alpha,\beta}<0
for
\beta
small enough. This ends the proof of (
3.78
).

We now turn to the claim (
3.79
). Since we assume that
u_{0}>\varepsilon
in a neighborhood of
x
and
U

is an open set, one can work under the conditions of Theorem
3.15
with
A=B(x,r)
for
r
small enough. Also recall that we are considering
\alpha>0
. Hence the lower bound (
3.51
) holds true. Since the right hand side

\displaystyle G(\beta):=C\beta^{2}p(p-1)-p\lambda_{1}(A)

(3.80)

of (
3.51
) verifies
\lim_{\beta\to\infty}G(\beta)=\infty
, we clearly have
G(\beta)>0
for
\beta>\beta_{c,2}
and a large enough
\beta_{c,2}
. This yields relation (
3.79
).
∎

We now get closer to the intermittency phenomenon, by proving that large moments of
u(t,x)
are divergent as
t\to\infty
.

Corollary 3.20
.

Let the conditions of Corollary
3.19
prevail. Consider
\alpha>0
,
\beta>0
and
x\in U
such that
u_{0}>\varepsilon>0
in a neighborhood of
x
. Then there exists
p_{0}
such that for all
p\geq p_{0}
we have

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})=+\infty.

Proof.

The proof is very similar to the proof of (
3.79
) above. It is based on Theorem
3.15
and the asymptotic behavior of the function
G
in (
3.80
), now seen as a function of
p
. Details are left to the reader.
∎

One way to quantify intermittency for the parabolic Anderson model is to show that in large time the
L^{p}(\Omega)
-norm of
u(t,x)
is much larger than the
L^{q}(\Omega)
-norm whenever
p>q
. We are now able to prove this for inner uniform domains.

Corollary 3.21
.

We still work under the conditions of Corollary
3.19
, with
\alpha,\beta>0
, an inner uniform domain
U
, and
x\in U
such that
u_{0}>\varepsilon
in a neighborhood of
x
. Let
q\geq 2
. Then, there exists
p_{0}>q
such that for
p\geq p_{0}
,

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})^{1/p}}{\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{q})^{1/q}}=+\infty.

(3.81)

Proof.

Denote by
Q_{p,q}(t,x)
the ratio
\|u(t,x)\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}/\|u(t,x)\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}
. Then

\frac{1}{t}\ln(Q_{p,q}(t,x))=\frac{1}{t}\left(\frac{1}{p}\ln(\mathbf{E}[u(t,x)^{p}])-\frac{1}{q}\ln(\mathbf{E}[u(t,x)^{q}])\right).

According to the lower bound in Theorem
3.15
(for a domain
A
of the form
B(x,r)
like in Corollary
3.21
) and the upper bound in Theorem
3.11
, we obtain

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln(Q_{p,q}(t,x))\geq C\beta^{2}(p-1)-\lambda_{1}(A)-\left(\frac{1}{2}\Theta_{\alpha}(\beta\sqrt{q-1})-\lambda_{1}\right).

(3.82)

As a function of
p
, the dominant term in (
3.82
) is
C\beta^{2}p
. Therefore if
p
is large enough we get

\liminf_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{t}\ln(Q_{p,q}(t,x))>0.

This is enough to prove (
3.81
).
∎

3.8
Scaling invariance in the Sierpiński gasket

Up to now we have stated results and bounds which are valid for a wide range of underlying spaces
X
. When particularizing our study to sets with convenient invariances, the stochastic heat equation also exhibits nice scaling properties. In this section we examine the case of the Sierpiński gasket with zero boundary condition at the vertices.

Let us thus go back to the setting of Section
2.4.3
, with the functions
\mathfrak{f}_{i}
given by (
2.67
) and the Sierpiński gasket
K
defined by (
2.68
). The inner uniform domain we consider here is
U=K\setminus V_{0}
. For a word
w=(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})
in
\{1,2,3\}^{n}
we define

\mathfrak{f}_{w}=\mathfrak{f}_{i_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ\mathfrak{f}_{i_{n}},\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptU_{w}:=\mathfrak{f}_{w}(K)\setminus\mathfrak{f}_{w}(V_{0})=\mathfrak{f}_{w}(U).

(3.83)

Since uniform curves in
U
are transformed by
\mathfrak{f}_{w}
into uniform curves in
U_{w}
, it follows that every
U_{w}
is a uniform and hence also a inner uniform domain. With this additional notation in mind, we can now state our invariance result.

Theorem 3.22
.

Let
\alpha,\beta
be two positive parameters. On the inner uniform domain
U=K\setminus V_{0}
we define the Dirichlet Laplace operator
\Delta_{D,U}
as in Section
2.2.1
and the noise
W^{D,U}_{\alpha}
through Definition
3.2
. We denote by
u
the unique solution of

\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\Delta_{D,U}u(t,x)+\beta u(t,x)\,\dot{W}_{\alpha}^{D,U}(t,x),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0,\,x\in U,

(3.84)

with initial condition
u(0,\cdot)=1
. For a word
w=(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})\in\{1,2,3\}^{n}
and
U_{w}
defined by (
3.83
), we similarly introduce
\Delta_{D,U_{w}}
and
W^{D,U_{w}}_{\alpha}
. Furthermore we define
v
as the unique solution to

\partial_{t}v(t,x)=\Delta_{D,U_{w}}v(t,x)+\beta\left(\frac{5^{\alpha+1/2}}{3}\right)^{n}v(t,x)\,\dot{W}_{\alpha}^{D,U_{w}}(t,x),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{for}\hskip 10.00002ptt\geq 0,\,x\in U,

(3.85)

with initial condition
v(0,\cdot)=1
. Then the following identity in the sense of finite dimensional distributions holds true:

\displaystyle(v(t,x))_{t\geq 0,x\in U_{w}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{(fdd)}}}{{=}}(u(5^{n}t,\mathfrak{f}^{-1}_{w}(x)))_{t\geq 0,x\in U_{w}}.

(3.86)

Proof.

We will prove the identity for one single value of
(t,x)
. The corresponding property for finite dimensional distributions is done in the same way and left to the reader.
We have seen in Theorem
2.39
that
\Delta_{D,U}
could be obtained as the extension of the Kigami Laplacian defined through the limiting procedure (
2.73
)-(
2.74
). A similar construction can be performed for
\Delta_{D,U_{w}}
. Hence it is easily seen from (
2.73
) that for a generic Schwartz function
g
(see Definition
2.18
for the notion of Schwartz function) we have

\displaystyle(\Delta_{D,U_{w}}g)\circ\mathfrak{f}_{w}=5^{n}\Delta_{D,U}(g\circ\mathfrak{f}_{w}).

(3.87)

Moreover, our representation (
2.26
) implies the corresponding relations for heat semigroups and heat kernels, valid for
t\geq 0
and
x,y\in U
:

\displaystyle(P_{t}^{D,U_{w}}g)\circ\mathfrak{f}_{w}=P_{5^{n}t}^{D,U}(g\circ\mathfrak{f}_{w}),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{and}\hskip 10.00002ptp_{t}^{D,U_{w}}(\mathfrak{f}_{w}(x),\mathfrak{f}_{w}(y))=3^{n}p^{D,U}_{5^{n}t}(x,y).

(3.88)

In the same way, one can invoke either (
2.28
) or (
2.29
) to see that

\displaystyle((-\Delta_{D,U_{w}})^{-\alpha}g)\circ\mathfrak{f}_{w}=5^{-n\alpha}(-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}(g\circ\mathfrak{f}_{w}).

(3.89)

Let us now specify the invariance for the noise
W_{\alpha}
. To begin with, from (
3.89
) one deduces that for
h\in L^{2}(U,\mu)
and
\alpha\geq 0

\displaystyle\int_{U_{w}}\left[(-\Delta_{D,U_{w}})^{-\alpha}(h\circ\mathfrak{f}^{-1}_{w})\right]^{2}d\mu

\displaystyle=5^{-2n\alpha}\int_{U_{w}}\left[((-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}h)\circ\mathfrak{f}^{-1}_{w}\right]^{2}d\mu.

Next we apply the scaling invariance of the Hausdorff measure on
K
, which stipulates that
\int_{U_{w}}f\circ f^{-1}_{w}d\mu=3^{-n}\int_{U}fd\mu
. We end up with

\displaystyle\int_{U_{w}}\left[(-\Delta_{D,U_{w}})^{-\alpha}(h\circ\mathfrak{f}^{-1}_{w})\right]^{2}d\mu=3^{-n}5^{-2n\alpha}\int_{U}((-\Delta_{D,U})^{-\alpha}h)^{2}d\mu,

(3.90)

and note that one can recast (
3.90
) as

\displaystyle\|h\circ\mathfrak{f}^{-1}_{w}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{W}_{D}^{-\alpha}(U_{w})}=3^{-n}5^{-2n\alpha}\|h\|^{2}_{\mathcal{W}_{D}^{-\alpha}(U)}.

(3.91)

Taking into account the covariance function (
2.29
) of
W_{\alpha}^{D,U}
and
W_{\alpha}^{D,U_{w}}
, we get the following equality in distribution for processes:

\{W_{\alpha}^{D,U_{w}}(g\circ\mathfrak{f}^{-1}_{w});\,g\in W_{D}^{-\alpha}(U)\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\{3^{-n/2}5^{-n\alpha}W_{\alpha}^{D,U}(g);\,g\in\mathcal{W}_{D}^{-\alpha}(U)\}.

(3.92)

We are ready to translate the previous observations into invariances for the stochastic heat equation. That is start from the mild formulation of (
3.84
):

u(t,x)=(P_{t}^{D,U}1)(x)+\beta\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}p^{D,U}_{t-s}(x,y)u(s,y)W^{D,U}_{\alpha}(ds,dy).

Composing the above relation with
\mathfrak{f}^{-1}_{w}
and applying a Brownian scaling in time we get

\displaystyle u(5^{n}t,\mathfrak{f}_{w}^{-1}(x))

\displaystyle=(P_{t}^{D,U_{w}}1)(x)+\beta\int_{0}^{5^{n}t}\int_{U}p^{D,U}_{5^{n}t-s}(\mathfrak{f}_{w}^{-1}(x),y)u(s,y)W^{D,U}_{\alpha}(ds,dy)

\displaystyle=(P_{t}^{D,U_{w}}1)(x)+\beta 5^{n/2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}p^{D,U}_{5^{n}t-5^{n}s}(\mathfrak{f}_{w}^{-1}(x),y)u(5^{n}s,y)\tilde{W}^{D,U}_{\alpha}(ds,dy),

where
\tilde{W}^{D,U}
is a field such that
\tilde{W}^{D,U}=W^{D,U}
(specifically
\tilde{W}^{D,U}(t,\cdot)
is obtained as
5^{n/2}W^{D,U}(t/5^{n},\cdot)
).
We now resort to (
3.88
) in order to write

\displaystyle u(5^{n}t,\mathfrak{f}_{w}^{-1}(x))

\displaystyle=(P_{t}^{D,U_{w}}1)(x)+\beta 5^{n/2}3^{-n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U}p^{D,U_{w}}_{t-s}(x,\mathfrak{f}_{w}(y))u(5^{n}s,y)\tilde{W}^{D,U}_{\alpha}(ds,dy).

Eventually, thanks to (
3.92
) we get the existence of a field
\tilde{W}^{D,U_{w}}

such that
\tilde{W}^{D,U_{w}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}{W}^{D,U_{w}}
and

\displaystyle u(5^{n}t,\mathfrak{f}_{w}^{-1}(x))=

\displaystyle\,(P_{t}^{D,U_{w}}1)(x)

\displaystyle+\beta\left(\frac{5^{\alpha+1/2}}{3}\right)^{n}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{U_{w}}p^{D,U_{w}}_{t-s}(x,y)u(5^{n}s,\mathfrak{f}_{w}^{-1}(y))\tilde{W}^{D,U_{w}}_{\alpha}(ds,dy).

Otherwise stated,
u(5^{n}t,\mathfrak{f}_{w}^{-1}(x))
solves equation (
3.85
). This shows identity (
3.86
) and finishes our proof.
∎

4
Neumann parabolic Anderson model in uniform domains

In this section we investigate the behavior of a parabolic Anderson model subject to Neumann boundary conditions. Since many of the computations are similar to the Dirichlet case, we sill just sketch the main differences. To begin with, let us label the hypothesis on the domain
U
considered below.

Assumption 4.1
.

The domain
U
is a uniform domain as specified in Definition
2.24
. On this domain we consider the Neumann Laplace operator from Proposition
2.27
.

We now proceed to a description of the noise in Section
4.1
, and we state the main results about the stochastic heat equation in Sections
4.2
to
4.4
.

4.1
Fractional noise

The family of noises we consider for the Neumann case are still described as white noises in time, with a spatial covariance based on the Neumann Laplacian.

Definition 4.2
.

For
\alpha>0
the noise
W^{N}_{\alpha}
is introduced similarly to Definition
3.2
, with a space
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}
given as

\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+},\mathcal{W}_{N}^{-\alpha}(U))
. Notice that the space
\mathcal{W}_{N}^{-\alpha}(U)
is introduced in Definition
2.35
, and we suppose that the constant
C_{U}
in (
2.65
) is such that
G^{N,U}_{2\alpha}(x,y)+C_{U}
is lower bounded by a positive constant (see Remark
2.36
). The Neumann fractional field
\{W_{\alpha}^{N}(\phi);\phi\in\mathcal{H}_{\alpha}\}
is then the centered isonormal Gaussian family with covariance

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[W_{\alpha}^{N}(\varphi)W_{\alpha}^{N}(\psi)\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\ \left\langle\varphi(t,\cdot),\psi(t,\cdot)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{W}_{N}^{-\alpha}(U)}dt,

(4.1)

It is assumed to be defined on a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbf{P})
.

Remark 4.3
.

For
\alpha=0
, the space-time white noise
W^{N}_{0}
is in fact defined exactly as in Definition
3.3
. Otherwise stated,
W^{N}_{0}=W^{D}_{0}
.

Remark 4.4
.

As we did in Remark
3.5
for the Dirichlet case, let us mention that Neumann noises on fractals have been introduced in
[
6
]
.
For
\alpha\leq{d_{h}}/{2d_{w}}
it had been obtained that
W^{N}_{\alpha}
is a space-time distribution, while for
\alpha>{d_{h}}/{2d_{w}}
a density property similar to (
3.4
) holds true.

4.2
Existence, uniqueness and
L^{p}
upper bounds

We are now in a position to state a result about the
L^{p}
moment of the parabolic Anderson model with Neumann Laplacian.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
and let
W^{N}_{\alpha}
be the noise from Definition
4.2
. We consider the equation

\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\Delta_{N}u(t,x)+\beta u(t,x)\,\dot{W}_{\alpha}^{N}(t,x)

(4.2)

interpreted in the mild sense as in (
3.6
). The initial condition
u_{0}
lies in
L^{\infty}(U,\mu)
. Then the equation admits a unique solution
u
according to Definition
3.6
. Moreover, for any integer
p\geq 2
, the solution
u
satisfies the following
L^{p}
-upper bound estimates:

\displaystyle\limsup_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})}{t}\leq\frac{p}{2}\widehat{\Theta}_{\alpha}\left(\beta\sqrt{p-1}\right),

(4.3)

where
\widehat{\Theta}_{\alpha}
is a continuous increasing function whose asymptotic behavior is exactly the same as
\Theta_{\alpha}
in Theorem
3.10
.

Proof.

We proceed using chaos expansions like in Theorem
3.10
. We thus have to check a bound like (
3.33
), with
p_{t}^{D}
replaced by
p_{t}^{N}
. In order to check (
3.33
) we then adopt the same strategy as in Lemma
3.9
. This leads to estimate a quantity like
M_{k}(t,x)
in (
3.19
). Let us just highlight a small difference between the definition of the current
M_{k}(t,x)
and the one we had in the Dirichlet case: relation (
3.19
) involved the positive kernel
G_{2\alpha}^{D}
, which should be replaced by
G^{N}_{2\alpha}
. Now the problem is that
G_{2\alpha}^{N}
is not positive anymore. Nevertheless as mentioned in Definition
4.2
, Remark
2.36
entails the existence of a constant
C_{U}
such that
G_{2\alpha}+C_{U}\geq 0
. With the definition (
4.1
) of inner product in
\mathcal{H}
and similarly to (
3.19
) one can express
M_{k}(t,x)
as

\begin{split}M_{k}(t,x)=&\int_{[0,t]^{k}_{<}}\int_{U^{2k}}g^{N}(s,y,t,x)\\
&\times\prod_{i=1}^{k}(G_{2\alpha}^{N}(y_{i},y_{i}^{\prime})+C_{U})g^{N}(s,y^{\prime},t,x)d\mu(y)d\mu(y^{\prime})e^{-2\lambda_{1}s_{1}}ds,\end{split}

(4.4)

where the function
g^{N}
is defined by

g^{N}(s,y,t,x)=p_{t-s_{k}}^{N}(x,y_{k})\cdots p_{s_{2}-s_{1}}^{N}(y_{2},y_{1}).

Starting from (
4.4
), the proof is completely similar to the proof of Lemma
3.9
. We just mention that the bound for
\|(-\Delta_{D})^{-\alpha}p_{t}^{D}\|_{L^{2}(U,\mu)}
now has to be changed into a bound for
\left\|\left[(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}+C_{U}\right]p_{t}^{N}\right\|_{L^{2}(U,\mu)}
. However, it is easy to see from the proof of Lemma
2.33
that

\sup_{x\in U}\left\|\left[(-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}+C_{U}\right]p_{t}^{N,U}(x,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{2}(U,\mu)}\leq\begin{cases}C(t^{\alpha-\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}}+1),\quad 0<\alpha<\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}\\
C|\ln(\max(t,1/2))|,\quad\alpha=\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}\\
C,\quad\alpha>\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}},\end{cases}

The bound (
4.3
) is thus obtained with the same estimates as in Lemma
3.9
and Theorem
3.10
. Details are left to the patient reader.
∎

4.3
L^{p}
lower bounds

In this section we turn to an analog of the lower bounds in Section
3.6
. We start with a general lower bound in the case
\alpha>0
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
\beta\geq 0
and
\alpha>0
, and consider the noise
W^{N}_{\alpha}
given in Definition
4.2
. Let
u
be the solution to equation (
4.2
), where the initial condition
u_{0}
is an element of
L^{\infty}(U,\mu)
such that
u_{0}\geq\varepsilon>0
on
U
. Let
p
be an integer with
p\geq 2
. Then for
x\in U
and
t\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})\geq\varepsilon^{p}\exp\left(C\beta^{2}p(p-1)t\right),

(4.5)

where
C
is a positive constant. In particular, for
x\in U

\displaystyle\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})}{t}\geq C\beta^{2}p(p-1).

(4.6)

Proof.

From the Feynman-Kac representation of the moments, which is established along the same lines as in the Dirichlet boundary case, one has

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})=\mathbf{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{p}u_{0}(\hat{B}_{t}^{x,j})\exp\left\{\beta^{2}\sum_{i\not=k}^{p}\int_{0}^{t}\left(G_{2\alpha}^{N}(\hat{B}^{x,i}_{s},\hat{B}^{x,k}_{s})+C_{U}\right)ds\right\}\right),

where
\hat{B}
is the reflected Brownian motion on
U
. Otherwise stated,
\hat{B}
is the diffusion with generator
\Delta_{N}
.
Replacing
G_{2\alpha}^{D}
by
G_{2\alpha}^{N}
in the computations going from (
3.54
) to (
3.6.1
), we easily get that

\mathbf{E}\left(u(t,x)^{p}\right)\geq\varepsilon^{p}\exp\left(C\beta^{2}p(p-1)t\right)\left(P_{t}^{N,U}1(x)\right)^{p}.

In addition, for Neumann boundary conditions we have
P^{N,U}_{t}1(x)=1
, from which (
4.5
) is easily deduced. Relation (
4.6
) is a trivial consequence of (
4.5
).
∎

Remark 4.7
.

In the Dirichlet case, we had observed a phase transition for moments in Corollary
3.19
. This phase transition is absent from the Neumann boundary picture. Indeed relation (
4.5
) implies that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbf{E}\left(u(t,x)^{p}\right)=\infty,\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{for\ all}\ \beta>0.

This difference in the asymptotic behavior stems from the fact that
\Delta_{D,U}
has a strictly negative principal eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
.

As in the Dirichlet case, one can improve the bounds of Theorem
4.6
when the noise is rough enough. This is summarized in the theorem below.

Theorem 4.8
.

Under the same conditions as in Theorem
4.6
, let
u
be the solution to equation (
4.2
). The initial condition
u_{0}
is bounded, non-negative on
U
and bounded from below by a positive constant on the neighborhood of a given
x\in U
. Then, for an integer
p\geq 2
, we have the following lower bounds.

•

For
0<\alpha<\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
,

\liminf_{\beta\to+\infty}\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\frac{1}{\beta^{\frac{2d_{w}}{(1+2\alpha)d_{w}-d_{h}}}}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})}{t}\geq Cp(p-1),

•

For
\alpha=\frac{d_{h}}{2d_{w}}
,

\liminf_{\beta\to+\infty}\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\frac{1}{\beta^{2}\ln\beta}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{p})}{t}\geq Cp(p-1).

Proof.

The proof is very similar to the proof of Theorem
3.16
. In particular, notice that the equivalent of (
3.59
) still involves the exit time
T^{1}_{\varepsilon}
. We thus end up with (
3.60
) as before. From there, the computations proceed as in Theorem
3.16
.
∎

We now label the result for the space-time white noise case. Its proof goes along the same lines as for Theorem
3.18
.

Theorem 4.9
.

Let the conditions of Theorem
4.6
and
4.8
prevail. We assume that we are in the white noise case
\alpha=0
. Moreover, the initial condition
u_{0}
is assumed to be in
L^{\infty}(U,\mu)
and lower bounded by
\varepsilon>0
. Then the solution
u
to (
4.2
) satisfies the following
L^{2}
-lower bound estimate:

\liminf_{t\to+\infty}\frac{\ln\mathbf{E}(u(t,x)^{2})}{t}\geq c\beta^{\frac{2d_{w}}{d_{w}-d_{h}}},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{for\ all}\ x\in U,

where
c>0
is a positive constant that does not depend on
\beta
.

4.4
Scaling invariance in the Sierpiński gasket

In this section we go back to the setting of Section
3.8
and we establish scaling factors in the Sierpinski gasket
K
. Our findings are summarized below.

Theorem 4.10
.

Let the notation and assumptions of Theorem
3.22
prevail. On the uniform domain
U=K\setminus V_{0}
, we consider the unique solution
u
to

\partial_{t}u(t,x)=\Delta_{N,U}u(t,x)+\beta u(t,x)\,\dot{W}_{\alpha}^{N,U}(t,x),\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{for}\ t\geq 0,x\in U,

with initial condition
u(0,\cdot)=1
. For the set
U_{w}
defined by (
3.83
), we also consider the solution
v
to

\partial_{t}v(t,x)=\Delta_{N,U_{w}}v(t,x)+\beta\left(\frac{5^{\alpha+1/2}}{3}\right)^{n}v(t,x)\,\dot{W}_{\alpha}^{N,U_{w}}(t,x),\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{for}\ t\geq 0,x\in U_{w},

(4.7)

with initial condition
v(0,\cdot)=1
.
Then the following identity in the sense of finite dimensional distribution holds true:

\displaystyle(v(t,x))_{t\geq 0,x\in U_{w}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(f\!dd)}}{{=}}(u(5^{n}t,\mathfrak{f}^{-1}_{w}(x)))_{t\geq 0,x\in U_{w}}.

Proof.

The proof goes along the same lines as for Theorem
3.22
. Details are spared for sake of conciseness. Let us just highlight a subtle change in the definition of the kernel
G_{2\alpha}^{N,U_{w}}
related to (
4.7
). Indeed, in the current situation we have

\displaystyle\int_{U_{w}}(-\Delta_{N,U_{w}})^{-\alpha}(g\circ\mathfrak{f}^{-1}_{w})^{2}d\mu=3^{-n}5^{-2n\alpha}\int_{U}((-\Delta_{N,U})^{-\alpha}g)^{2}d\mu.

Hence if one wishes (
3.90
) to hold in our context with the operator
\left(-\Delta_{N,U_{w}}\right)^{-\alpha}
replaced by

\left(-\Delta_{N,U_{w}}\right)^{-\alpha}+C_{U_{w}}
, we need to choose

C_{U_{w}}=3^{n}5^{-2n\alpha}C_{U}.

∎
