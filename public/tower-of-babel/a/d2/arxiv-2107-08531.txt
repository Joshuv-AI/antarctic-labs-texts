Singularities in L 1 L^{1} -supercritical Fokker–Planck equations: A qualitative analysis
Katharina Hopf

Abstract
. A class of nonlinear Fokker–Planck equations with superlinear drift is investigated in the L 1 L^{1} -supercritical regime, which exhibits a finite critical mass. The equations have a formal Wasserstein-like gradient-flow structure with a convex mobility and a free energy functional whose minimising measure has a singular component if above the critical mass. Singularities and concentrations also arise in the evolutionary problem and their finite-time appearance constitutes a primary technical difficulty. This paper aims at a global-in-time qualitative analysis with main focus on the isotropic case, where solutions will be shown to converge to the unique minimiser of the free energy as time tends to infinity. A key step in the analysis consists in properly controlling the singularity profiles during the evolution. Our study covers the 3D Kaniadakis–Quarati model for Bose–Einstein particles, and thus provides a first rigorous result on the continuation beyond blow-up and long-time asymptotic behaviour for this model.

Let us now put the analysis of the present work into context with existing literature and
discuss the main new difficulties.
Naturally, several aspects of our approach have their roots in the work
[
CHR20
]
.
This is particularly true for the fact that our fundamental a priori bound
consists in a space-uniform

Lipschitz estimate (of an integral quantity)
that is propagated in time. Both, in
[
CHR20
]
and in the present paper, such estimates are derived by means of suitable comparison principles.
However, the approach in
[
CHR20
]
relies on a Lagrangian reformulation of the problem in terms of the pseudo-inverse cumulative distribution function giving access to the powerful instrument of viscosity solution theory
[
CIL92
]
. While in higher dimensions such a reformulation is, in principle, still possible
[
CHW20
,
CRW16
,
ESG05
]
, the structural properties of the resulting problem greatly deteriorate. Indeed, in higher space dimensions, Lagrangian coordinates are vectorial and the corresponding reformulation leads to a quasilinear degenerate second-order
system
of equations (cf.
[
CHW20
, Section 2.1.3]
). In such situations, classical comparison techniques are rarely available.
Even in the isotropic case, where a 1D scalar problem can be obtained for the inverse of a rescaled radial cumulative distribution function
[
CHW20
, Section 2.1.2]
,
the comparison technique in
[
CHR20
]

does not directly generalise to higher dimensions, since for
d>1
the second-order differential operator in the new variables has an explicit dependence
on the unknown (see the comments following (7.3) in
[
Hop19
]
).

The new challenges we encounter in higher dimensions are thus mainly of a technical nature.
Especially the derivation of the universal space profile at
\{v=0\}
for unbounded densities in Section
3
(applying to isotropic flows) is significantly more delicate than in the 1D case and requires several intermediate steps.
Determining the profile at the
first
blow-up time is still quite feasible and, as in the 1D case, amounts to solving an ordinary differential equation—in higher dimensions to be combined with a bootstrap argument.
However, in (
fpγ

‘blow-ups’
and
‘blow-downs’
could in principle be highly oscillatory. Thus, for a global-in-time analysis a particular challenge lies in gaining information at general points in time.
We should emphasize that the space profile, while of interest in its own right, encodes a certain time-uniform continuity-at-infinity property that appears to be vital for proving relaxation to the minimiser

in the long-time limit. (Observe that when only looking at the equation (

) from a PDE point of view, other stationary

consisting of a smooth steady state

for some

plus a suitably weighted non-trivial Dirac measure at zero are conceivable, though unphysical.)
Let us finally point out that, in contrast to
[
CHR20
]
where the mass of the condensate component (i.e. of the singular part of the measure solution, which turns out to be supported in
\{v=0\}
) has only been shown to be a continuous function of time, the present approach allows us to infer Lipschitz continuity in the isotropic case and thus refines
[
CHR20
]
(cf.
[
Hop19
]
).
Some of the basic ideas of this manuscript have been sketched for the 1D model in the author’s PhD Thesis
[

, Chapter 5]
. As indicated in Chapter 5.3 of
[

]

2.
Approximation scheme

As pointed out in the introduction, local-in-time classical solutions of (
fpγ
) emanating from initial data that are large in a suitable sense may cease to exist in

after a finite time.
The main purpose of this section is to establish global existence for the approximation scheme (

) in spaces of suitable regularity as well as certain compactness and convergence properties for the corresponding approximate solutions.
In the isotropic case, our scheme obeys a monotonicity property and, as a consequence, gives rise to a unique limiting measure. Note that this feature may also be of interest from a numerics point of view. A key ingredient in the analysis is a uniform temporal Lipschitz bound for the partial mass function of isotropic solutions (see Proposition
2.6
).

\mathcal{T}[f]-\mathcal{T}[\tilde{f}]
, one may now follow
[
CLR09
]
to show the contraction mapping property of
\mathcal{T}
and deduce the existence of a fixed point
f\in C([0,T];X)
for small enough

as asserted in Lemma
2.2
. By construction, this fixed point is a mild solution of (
fpγ
).
The extra regularity
t\mapsto\nu(t)^{\frac{1}{2}}|\nabla f(t)|\in C_{b}((0,T);(L^{\infty}_{\ell}\cap L^{1}_{1})(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
follows from similar arguments (see
[
CLR09
, Section 2.2]
) combined with the uniqueness of mild solutions in

C([0,T];(L^{\infty}\cap L^{p}_{1})(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
for
p>d
shown in Lemma
2.1
.
(Of course, the contraction mapping property, whose proof we have not presented in full detail, also provides uniqueness.)

The properties
(i)
–
(vi)
can be deduced from classical arguments as in
[

, Sections 2.3 and 2.4]
(see also
[

,
QuS19
]
).

The analogous results for the regularised problem (

) are obtained along the same lines using in particular the bound
0\leq\eta_{\varepsilon}(f)\leq|f|^{\gamma}
, where

is defined in the line above (

Lemma 2.3

(Uniform moment bound)
.

Assume that
\ell\geq 1
,
n\geq 2
, and let
f_{\mathrm{in}}\in{X_{\ell,n}}
be non-negative.
Denote by
f_{\varepsilon}\in C([0,T^{*});{X_{\ell,n}})
the non-negative (local-in-time) mild solution of (
fpγ.reg
) as obtained in Lemma
2.2
.
Then, for all
t\in[0,T^{*})
,

(2.22)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{\varepsilon}(t,v)(1+|v|^{n})\,\mathrm{d}v\leq C(n,d)\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{n}}.

We emphasize that the constant
C(n,d)<\infty
in the above lemma is independent of
\varepsilon
and
T^{*}
. Moreover, the bound (
2.22
) equally holds for the local mild solution of (
fpγ
).

Proof.

Let us first provide the formal argument leading to the above estimate.
We abbreviate
f:=f_{\varepsilon}
and define for
k\in[0,\infty)

(2.23)

\displaystyle E_{k}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{k}f(t,v)\,\mathrm{d}v.

Then, by Lemma
2.2
,
E_{0}(t)\equiv\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=:B_{0}
.
Clearly,
B_{0}\leq\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{n}}
.

We now argue inductively and assume that
\sup_{t\in[0,T^{*})}E_{k-2}(t)\leq B_{k-2}
for some
k\in[2,n]
and a positive constant
B_{k-2}
obeying the bound
B_{k-2}\leq C\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{n}}
with
C=C(n,d)
.
Formally, we may then compute

(2.24)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{k}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E_{k}(t)&=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{k-2}v\cdot(\nabla f+vh_{\varepsilon}(f))\,\mathrm{d}v\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{div}(|v|^{k-2}v)f\,\mathrm{d}v-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{k}h_{\varepsilon}(f)\,\mathrm{d}v\\
&\leq(k-2+d)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{k-2}f\,\mathrm{d}v-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|v|^{k}f\,\mathrm{d}v\\
&=(k-2+d)E_{k-2}(t)-E_{k}(t),\end{aligned}

which implies that

(2.25)

\displaystyle E_{k}(t)\leq\max\{E_{k}(0),(k-2+d)B_{k-2}\}=:B_{k},\qquad t\in[0,T^{*}).

Since
k\leq n
, the new upper bound
B_{k}
again satisfies the estimate
B_{k}\leq C\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{n}}
for some possibly larger constant
C=C(n,d)
.

We now let
k=2
in the above step to find that
\sup_{t}E_{2}(t)\leq\max\{E_{2}(0),dB_{0}\}=B_{2}
.
By interpolation we infer that
E_{k}(t)\leq B_{2}^{\frac{k}{2}}B_{0}^{\frac{2-k}{2}}=:B_{k}
for all
k\in(0,2)
and all
t\in[0,T^{*}).

Observe that
B_{k}\leq C\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{n}}
for all
k\in(0,2)
.
We may now complete the induction argument: starting with
k-2=n-2\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor
(which lies in
[0,2)
) and iterating the above induction step
\left\lfloor n/2\right\rfloor
times, we arrive at the bound
\sup_{t}E_{n}(t)\leq C(n,d)\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{n}}
.

Finally, let us note that the computation (
2.24
) can be made rigorous by introducing a smooth, compactly supported cut-off function
\varphi_{R}
,
R\geq 1
, with
\varphi_{R}(v)=\varphi(R^{-1}v)
for some
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfying
0\leq\varphi\leq 1
and
\varphi\equiv 1
on
\{|v|\leq 1\}
. The time derivative of

t\mapsto\frac{1}{k}\int|v|^{k}f(t,v)\varphi_{R}(v)\,\mathrm{d}v
then satisfies an inequality which leads to (
2.24
)–(
2.25
) in the limit
R\to\infty
.
∎

Global existence for (
fpγ.reg
) in
{X_{\ell,n}}
will be obtained under the decay conditions

(2.26)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\ell\geq 1,\\
&n\geq\ell+d+1.\end{aligned}

Proposition 2.4

(Global existence for (
fpγ.reg
))
.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1]
.
Let
\ell,n
satisfy (
2.26
), and suppose that
f_{\mathrm{in}}\in{X_{\ell,n}}
is non-negative.
There exists a unique global-in-time mild solution
f_{\varepsilon}\in C([0,\infty);{X_{\ell,n}})
of the Cauchy problem (
fpγ.reg
).

Note that, as a consequence of Lemma
2.2
, the function
f_{\varepsilon}
in the above proposition enjoys the additional properties
(i)
–
(iv)
. In particular, it is a classical solution of (
fpγ.reg
) in
(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

Local-in-time wellposedness of (
fpγ.reg
) in
X:={X_{\ell,n}}
follows from Lemma
2.2
.
Thus, for proving global existence it suffices to show that, for
\varepsilon>0
fixed,
\|f_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{X}
cannot blow up in finite time. For this purpose, let
T<\infty
and suppose that

f_{\varepsilon}\in C([0,T);X)
is a mild solution of (
fpγ.reg
) on the interval
[0,T)
.
Since
n\geq 2
, we may invoke Lemma
2.3
to infer that
\|f_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{1}_{n}}
remains bounded uniformly in time:

(2.27)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T)}\|f_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{1}_{n}}\leq C\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{n}}<\infty.

Next, we let
p:=d+1
and estimate for
t\in[0,T)

(2.28)

\displaystyle\|f_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}_{\ell}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{T}\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\ell}}+C_{T}\int_{0}^{t}\nu(t-s)^{-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{d}{2p}}\|f_{\varepsilon}\eta_{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{p}_{\ell+1}}\,\mathrm{d}s,

where we used the fact that
\||\cdot|\tilde{f}(\cdot)\|_{L^{p}_{\ell}}\leq 2\|\tilde{f}\|_{L^{p}_{\ell+1}}
for
\tilde{f}\in L^{p}_{\ell+1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Since

(\ell+1)p-\ell(p-1)=\ell+d+1\leq n

and hence
(1+|w|^{\ell+1})^{p}\lesssim(1+|w|^{n})(1+|w|^{\ell})^{p-1}
,
we further have

(2.29)

\displaystyle\|f_{\varepsilon}\eta_{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{p}_{\ell+1}}^{p}

\displaystyle\leq C(\varepsilon)^{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(1+|w|^{\ell+1})^{p}|f_{\varepsilon}|^{p}\,\mathrm{d}w

(2.30)

\displaystyle\leq C(\varepsilon)^{p}\,\|f_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}_{n}}\|f_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\ell}}^{p-1}.

Hence, using the Young inequality
ab\leq\frac{1}{p}a^{p}+\frac{p-1}{p}b^{\frac{p}{p-1}}
, we deduce

(2.31)

\displaystyle\|f_{\varepsilon}\eta_{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon})\|_{L^{p}_{\ell+1}}

\displaystyle\leq C(\varepsilon)\,\|f_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{1}_{n}}+C(\varepsilon)\|f_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\ell}}.

Inserting this bound into (
2.28
), using (
2.27
), and applying the generalised Gronwall inequality
[
Ama95
, Theorem 3.3.1]
yields

(2.32)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,T)}\|f_{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}_{\ell}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{T,\varepsilon}(\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{\infty}_{\ell}}+\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{n}})\exp\left(C_{T,\varepsilon}\right),

where the constants
C_{T,\varepsilon}
depend on
\varepsilon,T
and fixed parameters.
This shows that the unique local-in-time mild solution can be extended beyond the time
T
, and since
T\in(0,\infty)
was arbitrary, the function
f_{\varepsilon}
extends to a unique global-in-time mild solution
f_{\varepsilon}\in C([0,\infty);X)
.
∎

For later reference, let us note the following consequence of the above theory.

.

with

Let

\displaystyle\text{(H2')}\begin{cases}&\text{\ref{it:HP.init} and }\text{the local regular solution $f$}\text{ of\penalty\ \eqref{eq:fpn} with }f(0)=f_{\mathrm{in}}\\

2.2.2.
Isotropic solutions

In this subsection we assume
f_{\mathrm{in}}
to be isotropic and write
g_{\mathrm{in}}(r)=f_{\mathrm{in}}(v)
,

.
By Proposition
2.4

(iv)
, the global mild solution
f_{\varepsilon}
of (
fpγ.reg
) is isotropic, allowing us to write
g_{\varepsilon}(t,r):=f_{\varepsilon}(t,v)
for
r=|v|\geq 0
.
Observe that
g_{\varepsilon}
satisfies the equation

(2.34)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=r^{-(d-1)}\partial_{r}\Big(r^{d-1}\partial_{r}g_{\varepsilon}+r^{d}h_{\varepsilon}(g_{\varepsilon})\Big)\text{ in }\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\mathbb{R}_{+},

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to 0}\Big(r^{d-1}\partial_{r}g_{\varepsilon}+r^{d}h_{\varepsilon}(g_{\varepsilon})\Big),

where the limit in the last line holds locally uniformly in
t\in[0,\infty)
.

Our fundamental a priori bound for (
fpγ
) relies on the fact that, in the isotropic case, equation (
fpγ.reg
) can be expressed as an evolution equation for the partial mass function

(2.35)

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}(t,r):=\int_{0}^{r}g_{\varepsilon}(t,\rho)\rho^{d-1}\mathrm{d}\rho=c_{d}^{-1}\int_{B_{r}}f_{\varepsilon}(t,v)\,\mathrm{d}v\leq c_{d}^{-1}\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

where
c_{d}
denotes the area of the unit sphere
\partial B_{1}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The equation for
M_{\varepsilon}
is obtained by multiplying (
2.34
) by
r^{d-1}
and integrating in

\displaystyle\partial_{t}M_{\varepsilon}=r^{d-1}\partial_{r}g_{\varepsilon}+r^{d}h_{\varepsilon}(g_{\varepsilon}).

Using the relations

\displaystyle\partial_{r}M_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=r^{d-1}g_{\varepsilon},

\displaystyle\partial_{r}^{2}M_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=r^{d-1}\partial_{r}g_{\varepsilon}+\tfrac{(d-1)}{r}\partial_{r}M_{\varepsilon},

one arrives at

(2.37)

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}M_{\varepsilon}&=\partial_{r}^{2}M_{\varepsilon}-\tfrac{(d-1)}{r}\partial_{r}M_{\varepsilon}+r^{d}h_{\varepsilon}(r^{1-d}\partial_{r}M_{\varepsilon}),\quad&&t>0,\;r\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\\
M_{\varepsilon}(t,0)&=0,&&t>0,\\
M_{\varepsilon}(0,r)&=M_{\mathrm{in}}(r),&&r\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

We note that, as a consequence of (
2.33
),

(2.38)

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}\equiv M\text{ in }[0,\tau_{*}]\times[0,\infty)\;\text{ for all }\varepsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{*}],

where
M(t,r)=c_{d}^{-1}\int_{B_{r}}f(t,v)\,\mathrm{d}v
with
f\in C([0,\tau_{*}];X_{d,2d+1})

denoting the local-in-time mild solution of (

).
Hence, thanks to the regularity established in Lemma
2.2
and hypothesis

we can ensure that

(2.39)

\displaystyle M\in C^{1,2}([0,\tau_{*}]\times[0,\infty))\;\text{ with }\;\sup_{\tau\in[0,\tau_{*}]}\|\partial_{t}M(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}([0,\infty))}\leq K<\infty,\qquad

where the last estimate follows from (
2.36
) and the regularity

f\in C([0,\tau_{*}];L^{\infty}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
,

\nabla f\in C([0,\tau_{*}];L^{\infty}_{d}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.

Proposition 2.6

(Global Lipschitz regularity in time)
.

Suppose that
f_{\mathrm{in}}
is isotropic and satisfies the hypotheses in
(H2’)
.
Denote by
M_{\varepsilon}
the partial mass function (
2.35
) of the global solution
f_{\varepsilon}
of (
fpγ.reg
) obtained in Proposition
2.4
. In particular,
M_{\varepsilon}
is a classical solution of equation (
2.37
) satisfying (
2.38
), (
2.39
) and is such that
f_{\varepsilon}
enjoys the uniform moment bound (
2.22
) for
n=2d+1
.
Then

(2.40)

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{*}]}\sup_{t,r>0}|\partial_{t}M_{\varepsilon}(t,r)|\leq K_{*},

where

(2.41)

\displaystyle K_{*}:=\max\{K,\tfrac{\tilde{m}}{\tau_{*}}\}<\infty

with
K
as in (
2.39
) and
\tilde{m}:=c_{d}^{-1}m=c_{d}^{-1}\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
.

Proof.

Let
K_{*}
be as in (
2.41
). We will show by contradiction that

\displaystyle\sup_{\varepsilon\in(0,\epsilon_{*}]}\big(M_{\varepsilon}(t,r)-M_{\varepsilon}(s,r)\big)\leq K_{*}|t-s|

for all
t,s,r>0
.

Suppose the last inequality is false for some
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exist
t_{1},s_{1},r_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}(t_{1},r_{1})-M_{\varepsilon}(s_{1},r_{1})-K_{*}|t_{1}-s_{1}|>0.

Pick some
T\geq\max\{t_{1},s_{1}\}\,{+}\,1
. Without loss of generality we further assume that
T>\tau_{*}
with
\tau_{*}
being as in (
2.38
), (
2.39
).
Then, for
\delta>0
small enough, we have

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon}(t_{1},r_{1})-\frac{\delta}{T-t_{1}}-\frac{\delta}{T-s_{1}}-M_{\varepsilon}(s_{1},r_{1})-K_{*}|t_{1}-s_{1}|>0

and hence

\displaystyle\sup_{(t,s,r)\in Q}\Big(M_{\varepsilon}(t,r)-M_{\varepsilon}(s,r)-K_{*}|t-s|-\frac{\delta}{T-t}-\frac{\delta}{T-s}\Big)>0,

where
Q=(0,T)\times(0,T)\times(0,\infty)
.

We assert that the function

(2.42)

\displaystyle P(t,s,r):=M_{\varepsilon}(t,r)-M_{\varepsilon}(s,r)-K_{*}|t-s|-\frac{\delta}{T-t}-\frac{\delta}{T-s}

attains its (positive) supremum in the interior of
Q
.
This can be seen as follows: by the uniform continuity of
M_{\varepsilon}
on
[0,T]\times[0,1]
and the fact that
M_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,0)\equiv 0
, there exists
r^{\prime}>0
such that
P<0
in
[0,T]\times[0,T]\times[0,r^{\prime}]
. Moreover, by (
2.38
) and (
2.39
)
one has
P<0
in
[0,\tau_{*}]\times[0,\tau_{*}]\times[0,\infty)
. The bound
M_{\varepsilon}\leq\tilde{m}
further shows that
P<0
in
[0,T]\times[T-\epsilon,T]\times[0,\infty)
and in

[T-\epsilon,T]\times[0,T]\times[0,\infty)
for some
\epsilon=\epsilon(\delta,\tilde{m})>0
.
Next, for all
\bar{s}\in[\tau_{*},T]
and
r\in[0,\infty)
, we have
P(0,\bar{s},r)\leq\tilde{m}-K_{*}\tau_{*}-\frac{2\delta}{T}<0
thanks to the choice of
K_{*}
. Likewise,
P(\bar{t},0,r)\leq-\frac{2\delta}{T}
for all
\bar{t}\in[t^{*},T]
and
r\in[0,\infty)
.
Hence, it remains to rule out the existence of a maximising sequence
(t_{n},s_{n},r_{n})
with
r_{n}\to\infty
. To this end, we take advantage of the bound (
2.22
) (for
n=2
) to estimate

\displaystyle P(t,s,r)

\displaystyle\leq c_{d}^{-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{r}}f_{\varepsilon}(s,v)\,\mathrm{d}v-\frac{2\delta}{T}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{c_{d}(1+r)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B_{r}}f_{\varepsilon}(s,v)\,(1+|v|)\,\mathrm{d}v-\frac{2\delta}{T}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{c_{d}(1+r)}\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}-\frac{2\delta}{T}.

Observe that the right-hand side is negative whenever
r\geq R_{*}
for a finite radius
R_{*}=R_{*}(\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{2}},T,\delta)
large enough. Hence, the same is true for
P(t,s,r)
.

Thus, the supremum of
P
must be attained at some interior point

p^{*}=(t,s,r)\in Q
. At the point
p^{*}
we have the optimality conditions

\displaystyle\partial_{t}M_{\varepsilon}(t,r)-K_{*}\frac{t-s}{|t-s|}=\frac{\delta}{(T-t)^{2}},

\displaystyle-\partial_{s}M_{\varepsilon}(s,r)+K_{*}\frac{t-s}{|t-s|}=\frac{\delta}{(T-s)^{2}},

and hence

\displaystyle\partial_{t}M_{\varepsilon}(t,r)-\partial_{s}M_{\varepsilon}(s,r)=\frac{\delta}{(T-t)^{2}}+\frac{\delta}{(T-s)^{2}}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\partial_{r}M_{\varepsilon}(t,r)=\partial_{r}M_{\varepsilon}(s,r)

and thus

(2.43)

\displaystyle h_{\varepsilon}(r^{1-d}\partial_{r}M_{\varepsilon}(t,r))-h_{\varepsilon}(r^{1-d}\partial_{r}M_{\varepsilon}(s,r))=0.

Further note that
0\geq\partial_{r}^{2}P(t,s,r)=\partial_{r}^{2}M_{\varepsilon}(t,r)-\partial_{r}^{2}M_{\varepsilon}(s,r)
.

In combination with equation (
2.37
) we deduce at the point
(t,s,r)=p^{*}:

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{t}M_{\varepsilon}(t,r)-\partial_{s}M_{\varepsilon}(s,r)-(\partial_{r}^{2}M_{\varepsilon}(t,r)-\partial_{r}^{2}M_{\varepsilon}(s,r))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\delta}{(T-t)^{2}}+\frac{\delta}{(T-s)^{2}}

\displaystyle>0,

which is a contradiction. This completes the proof of Proposition
2.6
.

Let us remark that, thanks to the smoothness of
M_{\varepsilon}
, estimate (
2.40
) may alternatively be proved by directly considering the equation satisfied by
N_{\varepsilon}:=\partial_{t}M_{\varepsilon}
, at least if one assumes a slightly stronger decay hypothesis on
f_{\mathrm{in}}
. Indeed, notice that positive constants
above
\sup N_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)

of the problem for
N_{\varepsilon}
are supersolutions, while negative constants below
\inf N_{\varepsilon}(0,\cdot)
are subsolutions. And if
f_{\varepsilon}\in C([0,T];L^{\infty}_{\ell}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and
\nabla f_{\varepsilon}\in C([0,T];L^{\infty}_{\ell-1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
for some
\ell>d
, we may use (
2.36
) to find that

N_{\varepsilon}(t,r)\to 0
as
r\to\infty
, uniformly in
t\in[0,T]
.
∎

The comparison principle underlying the proof of Proposition

can further be used to deduce monotonicity in
\varepsilon
of

.

Proposition 2.7

(Monotonicity of the scheme)
.

Let the hypotheses of Proposition
2.6
hold.
For any
0<\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq\varepsilon\leq\epsilon_{*}

(2.44)

\displaystyle M_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(t,r)\geq M_{\varepsilon}(t,r),\qquad t,r>0.

Proof.

To begin with, we recall that
h_{\varepsilon}\leq h_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
whenever
0<\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq\varepsilon
because of the non-increase of the function
(0,\infty)\ni s\mapsto s^{-\gamma}\eta(s)
.

The remaining reasoning is similar to the proof of Proposition
2.6
.
By contradiction, one assumes that there exist
t_{1},r_{1}>0
such that
M_{\varepsilon}(t_{1},r_{1})-M_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(t_{1},r_{1})
is positive.
Next, one fixes a finite time horizon
T\geq t_{1}+1
and picks
\delta>0
small enough such that
the function

\tilde{P}(t,r):=M_{\varepsilon}(t,r)-M_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}(t,r)-\tfrac{\delta}{T-t}

has a positive supremum on

4.
Renormalised form

4.1.
Variational structure

Our subsequent analysis relies on the following gradient-flow structure of
the regularised Fokker–Planck equation (
fpγ.reg
). Such a structure has previously been used in
[
CHR20
, Section 3.3]
for the proof of an energy dissipation identity.
To proceed, let us recall that
\Phi^{\prime}(s)=-\int_{s}^{\infty}\frac{1}{h(\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma
and
\Phi(0)=0
.

We define the approximate free energy functional by

(4.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}(f)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\frac{|v|^{2}}{2}f+\Phi_{\varepsilon}(f)\right)\mathrm{d}v,

where
\Phi_{\varepsilon}\in C([0,\infty))\cap C^{\infty}((0,\infty))
satisfies

(4.2)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varepsilon}(s)=\Phi(s)\text{ for }s\in[0,\varepsilon^{-1}]

and

(4.3)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{h_{\varepsilon}},\quad\Phi_{\varepsilon}\geq\Phi.

The function
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
with the above properties can be obtained by setting

\Phi_{\varepsilon}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(\sigma)\,\mathrm{d}\sigma,

where
\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(s)
is given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(s)=-\int_{s}^{B_{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma

with the constant
B_{\varepsilon}>\frac{1}{\varepsilon}
being such that

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}^{B_{\varepsilon}}\frac{1}{h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma=\int_{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{h(\sigma)}\,\mathrm{d}\sigma.

Identity (
4.2
) is a consequence of the fact that
h_{\varepsilon}(s)=h(s)
in
[0,\varepsilon^{-1}]
, while the second property in (
4.3
) follows from the inequality
h_{\varepsilon}\leq h
.

Notice that the functional derivative of
\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}
is given by

\delta\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}(f)=\frac{1}{2}|v|^{2}+\Phi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(f)
,
which allows us to rewrite (
fpγ.reg
) as

(4.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f_{\varepsilon}=\mathrm{div}\big(h_{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon})\nabla\delta\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon})\big).

Lemma 4.1

(Energy dissipation balance for (
fpγ.reg
))
.

Under the hypotheses of Proposition
2.4
,
the solutions
f_{\varepsilon}
of (
fpγ.reg
) obtained therein satisfy for all
0\leq s\leq t<\infty

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon}(t))+\int_{s}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{h_{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon})}|\nabla f_{\varepsilon}+vh_{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon})|^{2}\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}\tau=\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon}(s)).

Proof.

Recall that
f_{\varepsilon}\in C^{1,2}((0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
is a classical solution of (
fpγ.reg
).
Hence, the only task in deriving equation (
4.5
) lies in appropriately controlling the tails as
|v|\to\infty
.
This is a consequence of the moment control of the bounded function
f_{\varepsilon}
and follows from classical arguments, see e.g.
[
CLR09
]
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose
(H1)
,
(H2)
and use the notations in Proposition
1.1
.
For any
t\geq 0

\displaystyle\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon}(t))\geq\mathcal{H}(f(t)),

where the
\liminf
is taken along the sequence
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
selected in Proposition
1.1

(iii)
.

Proof.

Since
\Phi_{\varepsilon}\geq\Phi
(cf. (
4.3
)), we have

\int\Phi_{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon}(t))\geq\int\Phi(f_{\varepsilon}(t))
.
Next, given
\delta>0
, we let
L=L(\delta)>0
be large enough such that
|\Phi(s)|\leq\delta s
for
s\geq L
. Then

\displaystyle\int\Phi(f_{\varepsilon}(t,v))\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\geq\int\Phi(f_{\varepsilon}(t,v))\chi_{\{f_{\varepsilon}\leq L\}}\,\mathrm{d}v+\int\Phi(f_{\varepsilon}(t,v))\chi_{\{f_{\varepsilon}>L\}}\,\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle\geq\int\Phi(f_{\varepsilon}(t,v))\chi_{\{f_{\varepsilon}\leq L\}}\,\mathrm{d}v-\delta m,

where
m=\int f_{\mathrm{in}}
.
Sending first

(using dominated convergence) and then

, we infer

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int\Phi_{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon}(t))\geq\int\Phi(f(t))-\delta m

\displaystyle\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int\Phi_{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon}(t))

\displaystyle\geq\int\Phi(f(t)).

For the kinetic part, we let
\mathcal{A}_{\rho}:=\{\rho\leq|v|\leq\rho^{-1}\}
for

and estimate

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tfrac{1}{2}|v|^{2}f_{\varepsilon}(t)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tfrac{1}{2}|v|^{2}f(t)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int_{\mathcal{A}_{\rho}}\tfrac{1}{2}|v|^{2}(f_{\varepsilon}(t)-f(t))\,\mathrm{d}v\right|+\rho^{2}m+C\rho\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{3}},

where we used mass conservation and the bound

(cf. L.
2.3
).
We deduce

, where we used
the locally uniform convergence in Prop.
1.1

(iii)

and the fact that

|v|\geq 2\rho
as well as
|v\varphi_{\rho}^{\prime}(|v|)|=|\rho^{-1}v\varphi^{\prime}(|\rho^{-1}v|)|\lesssim 1
,
the dominated convergence theorem yields

(4.17)

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(f)v\cdot

\displaystyle[\xi^{\prime}(f)\psi\varphi_{\rho}^{\prime}(|v|)\cdot\tfrac{v}{|v|}]\,\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}t\Big|

(4.18)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|h(f)\xi^{\prime}(f)||\psi||v\varphi_{\rho}^{\prime}(|v|)|\,\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}t\;\longrightarrow\;0\text{ as }\rho\to 0.

The remaining part of the integral is more delicate. We estimate using the radial symmetry of
f(t,v)\;(=:g(t,|v|))

(4.19)

\displaystyle\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}

\displaystyle\nabla f\cdot[\xi^{\prime}(f)\psi\varphi_{\rho}^{\prime}(|v|)\cdot\tfrac{v}{|v|}]\,\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}t\Big|

(4.20)

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\!\!\int_{0}^{2\rho}|\xi^{\prime}(g)\partial_{r}g|\rho^{-1}|\varphi^{\prime}(\rho^{-1}r)|r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r\mathrm{d}t

(4.21)

\displaystyle=C\int_{0}^{T}A(t,\rho)\,\mathrm{d}t,

where we abbreviated

(4.22)

\displaystyle A(t,\rho)=\int_{0}^{2\rho}|\xi^{\prime}(g)\partial_{r}g|\rho^{-1}|\varphi^{\prime}(\rho^{-1}r)|r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r.

As a consequence of the bound (
2.40
), we have
|\xi^{\prime}(g)r^{d-1}\partial_{r}g|\leq CK_{*}+C(\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\xi^{\prime})r^{d}
. We hence infer the following
(t,\rho)
-uniform bound on
|A(t,\rho)|:

(4.23)

\displaystyle|A(t,\rho)|\leq C\int_{0}^{2\rho}\rho^{-1}|\varphi^{\prime}(\rho^{-1}r)|\,\mathrm{d}r=C\int_{0}^{2}|\varphi^{\prime}(\hat{r})|\,\mathrm{d}\hat{r}.

Thus, to show that
\lim_{\rho\to 0}\int_{0}^{T}A(t,\rho)\,\mathrm{d}t=0
it suffices to prove
the pointwise convergence
\lim_{\rho\to 0}A(t,\rho)=0
for (almost) all
t\in(0,T]
.
Thanks to Theorem
1.2
, only the following two cases may occur.

Case 1:
g(t,0+)=+\infty
.

In this case, there exists
r_{*}>0
such that
\xi^{\prime}(g(t,r))=0
for all
r\in(0,r_{*})
. Hence, we trivially have
\lim_{\rho\to 0}A(t,\rho)=0
.

Case 2:
g(t,\cdot)\in L^{\infty}
.

In this case, by Theorem
1.2
, there exists a neighbourhood
J
of
t
such that
f_{|J\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}
is smooth. In particular,
\partial_{r}g(t,\cdot)\in L^{\infty}(0,1)
.
If
d>1
, the conclusion
\lim_{\rho\to 0}A(t,\rho)=0
then directly follows from the definition of
A(t,\rho)
, while for
d=1
we resort to the fact that
\sup_{r\in(0,\rho)}|\partial_{r}g(t,r)|\to 0
as
\rho\to 0
.
∎

4.3.
Energy dissipation identity

An argument similar to that in the proof of Theorem
1.5
shows that isotropic solutions satisfy the energy dissipation balance. In the anisotropic case, we obtain an inequality.

Proof of Proposition
1.6
.

Combining Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
with the convergence properties of
f_{\varepsilon}
to
f
in Proposition
1.1

(iii)
, we readily infer for all
t>0
the inequality

(4.24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(f(t))+\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{D}(f(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau\leq\mathcal{H}(f_{\mathrm{in}}),

where

\mathcal{D}(f):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tfrac{1}{h(f)}|\nabla f+h(f)v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}v.

It remains to prove that in the isotropic case the above inequality holds with an equality.
Then, the asserted identity (
1.14
) follows by subtracting on both sides the quantity
\mathcal{H}(f(s))
, which is then known to equal
\mathcal{H}(f_{\mathrm{in}})-\int_{0}^{s}\mathcal{D}(f(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau
.
Thus, in the remainder, we assume that
f_{\mathrm{in}}
is isotropic.
Moreover, without loss of generality, we may assume hypotheses
(H2’)
. Otherwise we replace
f_{\mathrm{in}}
by
f(t_{0})
for small
t_{0}>0
. From the arguments below we will then obtain the identity

\mathcal{H}(f(t))+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathcal{D}(f(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau=\mathcal{H}(f(t_{0})),
and taking the limit
t_{0}\downarrow 0
, using monotone convergence for the term
\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathcal{D}(f(\tau))\,\mathrm{d}\tau
and dominated convergence for
\mathcal{H}(f(t_{0}))
we will arrive at the assertion.

As in the proof of Theorem
1.5
(cf. Section
4.2
), we pick a non-decreasing function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}([0,\infty);[0,1])
satisfying
\varphi(r)=0
for
r\in[0,1]
and
\varphi(r)=1
for
r\geq 2
, and abbreviate
\varphi_{\rho}(r)=\varphi(r/\rho)
for
\rho\in(0,1]
.
Then, defining

(4.25)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{(\rho)}(f):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}[\,\tfrac{1}{2}|v|^{2}f+\Phi(f)\,]\varphi_{\rho}(|v|)\,\mathrm{d}v,

one has

(4.26)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{(\rho)}(f(t))-\mathcal{H}^{(\rho)}(f_{\mathrm{in}})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}[\,\tfrac{1}{2}|v|^{2}+\Phi^{\prime}(f)\,]\,\mathrm{div}(\nabla f+h(f)v)\varphi_{\rho}(|v|)\,\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}\tau

(4.27)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t}\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\frac{1}{h(f)}|\nabla f+h(f)v|^{2}\varphi_{\rho}(|v|)\,\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}\tau

(4.28)

\displaystyle\qquad-\int_{0}^{t}\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}[\,\tfrac{1}{2}|v|^{2}+\Phi^{\prime}(f)\,]\,(\nabla f+h(f)v)\cdot\tfrac{v}{|v|}\varphi_{\rho}^{\prime}(|v|)\,\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}\tau.

We note that

(4.29)

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\to 0}\mathcal{H}^{(\rho)}(f(t))=\mathcal{H}(f(t))\text{ for all }t\geq 0.

Furthermore, monotone convergence gives

(4.30)

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\to 0}\int_{0}^{t}\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\frac{1}{h(f)}|\nabla f+h(f)v|^{2}\varphi_{\rho}(|v|)\,\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}\tau=\int_{0}^{t}\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}\frac{1}{h(f)}|\nabla f+h(f)v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}v\mathrm{d}\tau.

Hence, it remains to prove that the quantity

(4.31)

\displaystyle B(\tau,\rho):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}^{d}}}[\,\tfrac{1}{2}|v|^{2}+\Phi^{\prime}(f)\,]\,(\nabla f+h(f)v)\cdot\tfrac{v}{|v|}\varphi_{\rho}^{\prime}(|v|)\,\mathrm{d}v

satisfies

(4.32)

\displaystyle\lim_{\rho\to 0}\int_{0}^{t}B(\tau,\rho)\,\mathrm{d}\tau=0.

Using the isotropy of
f(\tau,\cdot)
we write

(4.33)

\displaystyle B(\tau,\rho)=c_{d}\int_{0}^{2\rho}[\,\tfrac{1}{2}r^{2}+\Phi^{\prime}(g)\,]\,(\partial_{r}g+h(g)r)\varphi_{\rho}^{\prime}(r)\,r^{d-1}\mathrm{d}r,

where
c_{d}
denotes the area of the unit sphere. We can now argue similarly as in the proof of Theorem
1.5
(see page
4.2
).
The function
|B(\tau,\rho)|
is uniformly bounded for
(\tau,\rho)\in[0,t]\times(0,1]
thanks to the estimate

|(\partial_{r}g+rh(g))r^{d-1}|\leq K_{*}
and the fact that,
by
(H2’)
and Proposition
1.1

(ii)
,
\inf_{[0,t]\times(0,1]}g=:\iota>0
.
Indeed, note that for
(\tau,\rho)\in[0,t]\times(0,1]

(4.34)

\displaystyle|B(\tau,\rho)|\leq C(\Phi^{\prime}(\iota))K_{*}\int_{0}^{2\rho}|\varphi^{\prime}(\rho^{-1}r)|\rho^{-1}\mathrm{d}r=C(\Phi^{\prime}(\iota))K_{*}\int_{0}^{2}|\varphi^{\prime}(\hat{r})|\mathrm{d}\hat{r}.

Identity (
4.32
) therefore follows from the dominated convergence theorem provided we can prove the pointwise convergence

\lim_{\rho\to 0}B(\tau,\rho)=0
for a.e.
\tau>0
.

Case 1:
g(\tau,0+)=+\infty
.

In this case, we estimate

(4.35)

\displaystyle|B(\tau,\rho)|\leq CK_{*}\int_{0}^{2}|\varphi^{\prime}(\hat{r})|\mathrm{d}\hat{r}\cdot\sup_{r\in(0,\rho)}|\tfrac{1}{2}r^{2}+\Phi^{\prime}(g(\tau,r))|

and note that the sublinearity of
\Phi
at infinity implies

(4.36)

\displaystyle\sup_{r\in(0,\rho)}|\tfrac{1}{2}r^{2}+\Phi^{\prime}(g(\tau,r))|\to 0\quad\text{ as }\rho\to 0.

Hence,
\lim_{\rho\to 0}|B(\tau,\rho)|=0
.

Case 2:
g(\tau,\cdot)\in L^{\infty}
.

Here, the assertion
\lim_{\rho\to 0}|B(\tau,\rho)|=0
is obtained similarly
as in Case 2 of the proof of Theorem
1.5
using the regularity of
g(\tau,\cdot)
shown in Theorem
1.2
.
∎

5.
Long-time behaviour

5.1.
Relaxation to equilibrium

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

Let

\mathcal{D}(f):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{h(f)}|\nabla f+h(f)v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}v=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(f)|\nabla\delta\mathcal{H}(f)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}v.

Proposition
1.6
implies that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{D}(f(t))\,\mathrm{d}t\leq\mathcal{H}(f_{\mathrm{in}})-\inf_{\mathcal{M}_{+}}\mathcal{H}<\infty
, and hence
there exists an increasing sequence
t_{k}\to\infty
such that

(5.1)

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{D}(f(t_{k}))=0.

The sequence
\{\mu_{t_{k}}\}_{k}\subset\mathcal{M}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of measures of mass
m
is tight since
\sup_{k}\int|v|^{3}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t_{k}}\leq C\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{3}}<\infty
. Prokhorov’s theorem thus ensures the existence of a measure

\mu_{\infty}\in\mathcal{M}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\infty}=m
such that, along a subsequence (not relabelled),

(5.2)

\displaystyle\mu_{t_{k}}

\displaystyle\overset{\ast}{\rightharpoonup}\mu_{\infty}\quad\text{ in }\mathcal{M}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(5.3)

\displaystyle\hskip-85.35826pt\text{ and moreover, }\qquad\int|v|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{t_{k}}

\displaystyle\to\int|v|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mu_{\infty}.

At the same time, by Proposition
1.1

(ii)
,
\mu_{t_{k}}=a(t_{k})\delta_{0}+f(t_{k})\mathcal{L}^{d}

where
f
satisfies a time-uniform bound of the form
|f(t,r)|\leq C(\rho)
for all
r\geq\rho>0
(cf. L.
2.8
resp. Cor.
2.9
).
Thus, the sequence
f_{k}(t):=f(t_{k}+t)
obeys an estimate
analogous to (
2.50
) for
G\subset\subset(-1,\infty)\times(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})
, where we assume without loss of generality that
t_{1}\geq 1
.
We may therefore argue similarly as in the proof of Proposition
1.1
and invoke the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem to infer the existence of
f_{\infty}\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\})\cap L^{1}_{3}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that, after possibly passing to another subsequence,

(5.4)

\displaystyle f(t_{k})\to f_{\infty}\quad\text{ in }C^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}).

Notice that
\mu_{\infty}^{\mathrm{reg}}=f_{\infty}\mathcal{L}^{d}
and
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\mu_{\infty}^{\mathrm{sing}}\subseteq\{0\}
, as a consequence of (
5.2
) and (
5.4
).

We will now show that
\mu_{\infty}
agrees with the minimiser of
\mathcal{H}
of mass
m
.
To this end, let

\lambda(s)=\exp(\Phi^{\prime}(s))=\frac{s}{(1+s^{\gamma})^{1/\gamma}}

and note that
\frac{h(s)}{\lambda^{2}(s)}=\frac{s(1+s^{\gamma})^{1+2/\gamma}}{s^{2}}\geq(1+s^{\gamma})^{1+\frac{1}{\gamma}}\geq 1
for all
s\in(0,\infty)
.
Hence,

(5.5)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(f)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}h(f)|\nabla\Phi^{\prime}(f)+v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}v

(5.6)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\tfrac{h(f)}{\lambda(f)^{2}}|\lambda(f)\nabla\Phi^{\prime}(f)+\lambda(f)v|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}v

(5.7)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla\lambda(f)+v\lambda(f)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}v.

Thanks to the convergence (
5.1
), the last estimate applied to
f:=f(t_{k})
implies that

(5.8)

\displaystyle\nabla\lambda(f(t_{k}))+v\lambda(f(t_{k}))\to 0\quad\text{ in }L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}).

At the same time, using (
5.4
), we may pass to the limit in the sense of distributions

(5.9)

\displaystyle\nabla\lambda(f(t_{k}))+v\lambda(f(t_{k}))\to\nabla\lambda(f_{\infty})+v\lambda(f_{\infty})\quad\text{ in }\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}{\setminus}\{0\}).

Thus,
\nabla\lambda(f_{\infty})+v\lambda(f_{\infty})=0
in
\mathscr{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}{\setminus}\{0\})
, and hence

\nabla\big(\mathrm{e}^{\frac{1}{2}|v|^{2}}\lambda(f_{\infty})\big)=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}{\setminus}\{0\}
.
This implies that
\Phi^{\prime}(f_{\infty})+\frac{1}{2}|v|^{2}\equiv-\theta
for a constant
\theta\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, where the sign of
\theta
follows from the fact that
\Phi^{\prime}\leq 0
.
Hence,
f_{\infty}(v)=(\Phi^{\prime})^{-1}(-\frac{1}{2}|v|^{2}-\theta)=f_{\infty,\theta}(v)
.
To determine
\theta
, recall that
\mu_{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=m
. Thus, if
\|f_{\infty,\theta}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}<m
, then
\mu_{\infty}(\{0\})>0
.
In this case, the convergence (
5.2
) combined with the time-uniform upper bound
\sup_{t>0}f(t,v)\leq C|v|^{-\frac{2}{\gamma}}\in L^{1}(B_{r_{*}})
, which follows from Theorem
1.2
(resp. Cor.
3.4
), implies the existence of
\underline{k}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mu_{t_{k}}(\{0\})>0
for all
k\geq\underline{k}
. Invoking once more Theorem
1.2
(now using the radial symmetry assumption), we find that (
1.10
) holds true for all such
t_{k}
and, owing to the convergence (
5.4
), we conclude that
\theta=0
.
If on the other hand
\|f_{\infty,\theta}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=m
, there is no excess mass and we must have
\mu_{\infty}=f_{\infty,\theta}\mathcal{L}^{d}
.
In conclusion, we have shown that the measure
\mu_{\infty}
coincides with the unique minimiser

\mu_{\min}=\mu_{\min}^{(m)}
of mass
m
.

From the convergence properties established so far we infer
\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{H}(\mu_{t_{k}})=\mathcal{H}(\mu_{\min})
. Since
t\mapsto\mathcal{H}(\mu_{t})
is non-increasing, this immediately yields

(5.10)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{H}(\mu_{t})=\mathcal{H}(\mu_{\min}).

Combining this result with the above compactness properties, mass conservation, and the uniqueness of the minimiser
\mu_{\min}^{(m)}
, one can easily deduce the remaining
convergence properties along any sequence
t\to\infty
as asserted in Theorem
1.7
. Here, also recall the bounds
\sup_{t>0}f(t,v)\lesssim|v|^{-\frac{2}{\gamma}}
for
|v|\leq r_{*}
,
f(t,v)\lesssim 1
for
|v|\geq r_{*}
and

\sup_{t>0}\|f(t)\|_{L^{1}_{3}}\lesssim\|f_{\mathrm{in}}\|_{L^{1}_{3}}
, which guarantee that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mu_{t}(\{0\})=\mu_{\infty}(\{0\})
and

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|f(t)-f_{\infty,\theta}\|_{L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}=0
for
p\in[1,\frac{\gamma d}{2})
.
∎

5.2.
Long-time and transient properties

Let us briefly point out some implications of the above analysis on further qualitative dynamical properties, restricting for consistency to the isotropic case.
If
m<m_{c}
, Theorem
1.7
along with Theorem
1.2
imply the eventual regularity of
\mu_{t}
after some sufficiently large time
T\gg 1
. However, using a contradiction argument, finite-time blow-up and the formation of a condensate (that is
\mu_{t}(\{0\})>0
for some
t>0
)
can be shown to occur for any size of the mass
m>0
by choosing the smooth initial data sufficiently concentrated near the origin (cf.
[
CHR20
,
Tos12
]
).
Hence, there exist flows exhibiting
transient condensates
with singular parts compactly supported in time.
On the other hand, whenever
m>m_{c}
, the above theory implies the eventual formation of a condensate:
\exists T\gg 1
such that
\mu_{t}(\{0\})>0
for all
t\geq T
. This is a consequence of the convergence
\lim_{t\to\infty}\mu_{t}(\{0\})=\mu_{\min}(\{0\})
.
It is also possible to infer information on the spatiotemporal features of singularity formation
and regularisation using rescaling methods. We refer to
[
Hop19
, Chapter 5.2]
, where such dynamics have been shown to be of
‘type II’
for the 1D case.

Finally, we note that finite-time condensation in the mass-supercritical case and convergence to the entropy minimiser can also be deduced in the anisotropic setting if
f_{\mathrm{in}}
admits a mass-supercritical isotropic lower barrier,
i.e.
f_{\mathrm{in}}\geq f_{\mathrm{in}}^{\#}
for some non-negative radially symmetric function
f_{\mathrm{in}}^{\#}
with
\int f_{\mathrm{in}}^{\#}>m_{c}
.
In this case, the density
f(t,\cdot)
is squeezed between two isotropic barriers which, in virtue of Theorem
1.7
, both converge to
f_{c}
as
t\to\infty
.

5.3.
Concluding remark

The comparison principle structure provides us with a priori bounds that allow for a detailed characterisation of the singularities which isotropic flows starting from regular data may exhibit (and even gives uniqueness in the 1D case
[
CHR20
,
Hop19
]
resp. convergence of the scheme to a unique limit in higher dimensions).
However, one may not expect such a structure to persist in more complex situations.
Particularly with regard to the study of uniqueness and stability properties in the presence of singularities, it would be interesting to see whether variational problems like (
fpγ
)
allow for more robust approaches.

Appendix A

Recall that
\mathcal{F}(t,v,w)=\mathrm{e}^{dt}G_{\nu(t)}(\mathrm{e}^{t}v-w)
,
\nu(t)=\mathrm{e}^{2t}-1
,

G_{\lambda}(\xi)=(2\pi\lambda)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{|\xi|^{2}}{2\lambda}}
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
T\leq 1
and let
\tilde{q}\in[1,\infty]
. There exists
C=C(\tilde{q},d)<\infty
such that for all
t\in(0,T]

(A.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bigg\|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla_{v}\mathcal{F}(t,v,w)||\mathrm{e}^{-t}w-v||f(w)|\,\mathrm{d}w\bigg\|_{L^{\tilde{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}&\leq C\|f\|_{L^{\tilde{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\\
&\leq C\nu(t)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|f\|_{L^{\tilde{q}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.\end{aligned}

Proof.

The second bound in (
A.1
) is trivial.

To verify the first inequality, we compute for
t\in(0,T]

(A.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\nabla_{v}

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(t,v,w)||\mathrm{e}^{-t}w-v||f(w)|\,\mathrm{d}w

(A.3)

\displaystyle=(2\pi\nu(t))^{-\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{dt}\mathrm{e}^{t}(2\nu(t))^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}2\tfrac{|\mathrm{e}^{t}v-w|}{\sqrt{2\nu(t)}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{|\mathrm{e}^{t}v-w|^{2}}{2\nu(t)}}|\mathrm{e}^{-t}w-v||f(w)|\,\mathrm{d}w

(A.4)

\displaystyle=(2\pi\nu(t))^{-\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{2dt}\mathrm{e}^{t}(2\nu(t))^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}2\frac{|\mathrm{e}^{t}(v-\tilde{w})|}{\sqrt{2\nu(t)}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{|\mathrm{e}^{t}(v-\tilde{w})|^{2}}{2\nu(t)}}|\tilde{w}-v||f(\mathrm{e}^{t}\tilde{w})|\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{w}

(A.5)

\displaystyle=(2\pi\nu(t))^{-\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{e}^{2dt}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}2\frac{|\mathrm{e}^{t}(v-\tilde{w})|^{2}}{2\nu(t)}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{|\mathrm{e}^{t}(v-\tilde{w})|^{2}}{2\nu(t)}}|f(\mathrm{e}^{t}\tilde{w})|\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{w}.

Now, the asserted inequality follows upon an application of Young’s convolution inequality,
