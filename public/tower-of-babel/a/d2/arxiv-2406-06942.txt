Optimal Matrix-Mimetic Tensor Algebras via Variable Projection Thanks: Submitted to the editors August 24, 2026.
Elizabeth Newman, Katherine Keegan

Abstract
Recent advances in matrix-mimetic tensor frameworks have made it possible to preserve linear algebraic properties for multilinear data analysis and, as a result, to obtain optimal representations of multiway data. Matrix mimeticity arises from interpreting tensors as operators that can be multiplied, factorized, and analyzed analogous to matrices. Underlying the tensor operation is an algebraic framework parameterized by an invertible linear transformation. The choice of linear mapping is crucial to representation quality and, in practice, is made heuristically based on expected correlations in the data. However, in many cases, these correlations are unknown and common heuristics lead to suboptimal performance. In this work, we simultaneously learn optimal linear mappings and corresponding tensor representations without relying on prior knowledge of the data. Our new framework explicitly captures the coupling between the transformation and representation using variable projection. We preserve the invertibility of the linear mapping by learning orthogonal transformations with Riemannian optimization. We provide original theory of uniqueness of the transformation and convergence analysis of our variable-projection-based algorithm. We demonstrate the generality of our framework through numerical experiments on a wide range of applications, including financial index tracking, image compression, and reduced order modeling. We have published all the code related to this work at https://github.com/elizabethnewman/star-M-opt .

1.2
Related Work

Matrix completion seeks to recover a matrix of the lowest possible rank that exactly matches partially-observed data, which is an NP-Hard problem. Practical algorithms form a convex relaxation of this problem by minimizing the matrix nuclear norm (i.e., the sum of the singular values) iteratively
[
50
]
. In
[
62
]
, the authors introduced a new tensor nuclear norm (TNN) based on the
t
-product and obtained quality low-tensor-rank approximations of partially-observed video data. Subsequent works extended tensor completion to the more general
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-product
[
35
,
52
]
. In
[
32
]
, the authors proposed learning the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-product as a subproblem of tensor completion and demonstrated success on a variety of imaging datasets. Due to the nonconvexity of the subproblem, the authors developed estimates via variance maximization and approximate Riemannian optimization to tractably update the linear transformation. Additional work in
[
58
]
introduced a Schatten-
p
quasi-norm regularization term to learn transformations that promote low-rank structure for TNN minimization. Further extensions have included learning nonlinear tensor-tensor products via neural networks for tensor completion
[
36
]
.

Learning the tensor-tensor product has thus far been restricted to tensor completion problems and the proposed algorithms have not fully made use of the optimality properties of the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-framework. Our paper takes a notably different perspective that places learning the transformation as the central goal, which enables a more general problem formulation, a wider range of potential applications, and, importantly, a strong algebraic foundation.

The mechanics of the

-product are powered by an underlying algebraic structure of tubal multiplication. The term “algebra” refers to square

under the familiar bilinear operation of matrix multiplication. As presented in

3

3

It is sometimes convenient to consider the action of

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}\star_{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{b}\equiv\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{M}}[\mathbf{a}]\myVec(\mathbf{b})\qquad\text{where}\qquad\mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{M}}[\mathbf{a}]:=\mathbf{M}^{-1}\diag(\mathbf{M}\myVec(\mathbf{a}))\mathbf{M}.

In words, tubal multiplication of

Lemma 2.1:

3.3
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-Optimization Algorithm: Riemannian Gradient Descent

The algebraic structure in (

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{O}_{n_{3}}:=\left\{\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}\times n_{3}}\mid\mathbf{Q}^{\top}\mathbf{Q}=\mathbf{I}_{n_{3}}\right\}.

(3.7)

Remark
.

\displaystyle\mathbf{G}=\nabla\overline{\Phi}(\mathbf{M}).

\displaystyle\myGrad\overline{\Phi}(\mathbf{M}):=\mathbf{M}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Omega}$}\qquad\text{where}\qquad\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Omega}$}=\frac{\mathbf{M}^{\top}\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{G}^{\top}\mathbf{M}}{2}.

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}\leftarrow\retraction_{\mathbf{M}}(-\alpha\myGrad\overline{\Phi}(\mathbf{M}))\qquad\text{with}\qquad\retraction_{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{M}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Omega}$})=\mathbf{M}\exp(\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Omega}$})

3.5
Computational Cost

For the prototype problems, the dominant cost at each
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-optimization iteration is a least squares solve or a matrix SVD per frontal slice, which, if
n_{1}=n_{2}=n
, costs
\mathcal{O}(n^{3}n_{3})
floating point operations. This is computationally demanding for large-scale problems. These operations can be performed in parallel, decreasing communication cost.
There are additional costs for applying the transformation along the third dimension and performing a line search. The former is the dominant communication cost in the algorithm. Additionally, the learned orthogonal transformation is often dense, requiring a storage cost of
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
floating point numbers. In practice, we consider
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-optimization to be a one-time, offline cost, and focus on the benefits of using a learned transformation in terms of representation quality, compressibility, and transferability in subsequent data analysis pipelines.

4.4
Compressing Snapshot Data of Dynamical Systems

Dynamical systems are central to simulating variety of real-world processes
[
21
,
2
,
34
]
; however, solving parametric dynamical systems is computationally expensive. In cases where one must solve repeatedly, such as for parameter uncertainty quantification or in optimization constrained by partial differential equations (PDEs), the expense can be prohibitive. To decrease the cost of each PDE solve, projection-based reduced order modeling techniques have gained widespread use
[
5
]
. At a high level, the goal is to approximate high-dimensional state measurements
\mathbf{x}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
in a low-dimensional subspace
\mathbf{x}_{k}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
k\ll n_{1}
. Mathematically, we seek a “good” basis matrix
\mathbf{U}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times k}
such that
\mathbf{x}(t)\approx\mathbf{U}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}(t)
for all relevant times; that is, the solution in the reduced space well-approximates the dynamics of the original model.

The proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) is an SVD-based approach to form a global basis across the parameters
[
51
]
. The heart of POD is the
method of snapshots
, which builds a basis that captures dominant trends from simulations. Procedurally, we construct a snapshot matrix for a fixed parameter at discrete time points

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}(c_{i})=\begin{bmatrix}|&|&&|\\
\mathbf{x}(t_{0};c_{i})&\mathbf{x}(t_{1};c_{i})&\cdots&\mathbf{x}(t_{n_{2}-1};c_{i})\\
|&|&&|\end{bmatrix}

(4.4)

where
\mathbf{x}(t_{j};c_{i})
is the discretized high-dimensional state for parameter
c_{i}
at time
t_{j}
. To capture dynamical behavior across the parameters, traditional matricized POD concatenates local snapshot matrices in a global snapshot matrix

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{X}(c_{1})&\mathbf{X}(c_{2})&\cdots&\mathbf{X}(c_{n_{3}})\end{bmatrix},

(4.5)

then forms a reduced basis via truncated matrix SVD
\mathbf{X}\approx\mathbf{U}_{k}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Sigma}$}_{k}\mathbf{V}_{k}^{\top}.

In practice, we may expect some relationship across the parameter dimension, and may lose these correlations when we construct the global snapshot matrix. In this experiment, we consider storing each
\mathbf{X}(c_{\ell})
as a frontal slice; that is,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}_{:,:,i}=\mathbf{X}(c_{i})\qquad\text{for $i=1,\dots,n_{3}$}.

(4.6)

The resulting snapshot tensor
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
has dimensions corresponding to
\text{space}\times\text{time}\times\text{parameters}
. Our goal is to generate a tensor POD basis

Conclusions

We presented a new
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-optimization paradigm that leverages the matrix mimeticity of the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-framework to simultaneously learn the underlying tensor algebra and obtain optimal representations of multilinear data. In
Section

2.5
, we proved invariant properties of the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-product under row permutations of the transformation.In
Section

3.1
, we introduced our variable-projection framework and highlighted the generality of our formulation through two prototype problems:
t
-linear regression and low-
t
-rank approximations. We derived the gradients needed to learn orthogonal transformations through Riemannian optimization, including differentiating through the
t
-SVDM (
Section

3.4.2
) and proved that
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-optimization will converge for
t
-linear regression (
Section

3.6
). In
Section

4
, we applied
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-optimization to a wide range of applications, including index tracking, image compression, and reduced order modeling.

This paper serves as the seminal work of the
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-optimization framework and opens the door to many new research directions. Currently, we are restricted to inner optimization problems that have analytic solutions. Following approaches in
[
44
]
, we will generalize to convex inner problems and apply
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-optimization to, e.g., tensor completion. In future work, we will extend
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-optimization to complex-valued and higher-order tensors, following similar extensions seen in
[
29
,
25
]
. To scale up to larger problems, we will reduce the computational and storage costs by exploring more compressible variants of the
t
-SVDM
[
29
]
, randomized versions of least squares and the
t
-SVDM
[
39
,
61
]
, and matrix-free implementations of the transformation. We will further explore second-order variants of Riemannian optimization to accelerate convergence and reduce training time
[
14
]
. Finally, we will apply
\star_{\mathbf{M}}
-optimization to new applications, such as X-ray spectroscopy
[

]
and hyperspectral image analysis

.

Appendix C
Riemannian Optimization Intuition

The following derivation of Riemannian gradient descent on the orthogonal group
\mathcal{O}_{n_{3}}
is based on
[
1
,
6
,
14
]
.
Given a Euclidean search direction
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}\times n_{3}}
(e.g., Euclidean gradient), we decompse the search direction into two components in Euclidean space
\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{S}_{T}+\mathbf{S}_{N}
where
\mathbf{S}_{T}
lies tangent to the manifold at the current iterate
\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{S}_{N}
lies in the normal direction.
For
\mathcal{O}_{n_{3}}
, the tangent space at the current iterate is

\displaystyle T_{\mathbf{M}}\mathcal{O}_{n_{3}}:=\left\{\mathbf{M}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Omega}$}\mid\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Omega}$}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}\times n_{3}}\text{ and }\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Omega}$}=-\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Omega}$}^{\top}\right\}.

(C.1)

One intuition comes from differentiating the constraint
\mathbf{M}^{\top}\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{I}
with respect to
\mathbf{M}
.
This yields the expression
(\delta\mathbf{M})^{\top}\mathbf{M}+\mathbf{M}^{\top}(\delta\mathbf{M})=\bf 0
where
\delta\mathbf{M}
lies in the tangent space at the point
\mathbf{M}
.
Because
\mathbf{M}
is invertible, we can safely set
\delta\mathbf{M}=\mathbf{M}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Omega}$}
for some matrix
\mathbf{\Omega}
.
Plugging this form for
\delta\mathbf{M}
into the differentiated constraint, we find that
\mathbf{\Omega}
is antisymmetric, as desired.

To find the tangent component of
\mathbf{S}
, we first represent the search direction
\mathbf{S}
as

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}=\mathbf{M}(\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Omega}$}+\mathbf{W})\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\mathbf{M}^{\top}\mathbf{S}=\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Omega}$}+\mathbf{W}

(C.2)

where
\mathbf{\Omega}
is antisymmetric and
\mathbf{W}
is symmetric.
We rewrite
\mathbf{M}^{\top}\mathbf{S}
as the sum of an antisymmetric and symmetric matrix via

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{\top}\mathbf{S}=\underbrace{\frac{\mathbf{M}^{\top}\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{M}}{2}}_{\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Omega}$}}+\underbrace{\frac{\mathbf{M}^{\top}\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{M}}{2}}_{\mathbf{W}}.

(C.3)

We define the Riemannian gradient by projecting
\mathbf{S}
onto the tangent space via

\displaystyle\myGrad\overline{\Phi}(\mathbf{M})

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{M}\left(\frac{\mathbf{M}^{\top}\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{M}}{2}\right)

(C.4)

We return to the manifold using a retraction, specifically the exponential mapping

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}\leftarrow\retraction_{\mathbf{M}}(-\alpha\myGrad\overline{\Phi}(\mathbf{M}))\qquad\text{where}\qquad\retraction_{\mathbf{M}}(\mathbf{M}\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Omega}$})=\mathbf{M}\exp(\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Omega}$})

(C.5)

where
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\Omega}$}\in T_{\mathbf{M}}\mathcal{O}_{n_{3}}
and
\exp:\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}\times n_{3}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{3}\times n_{3}}
is a matrix function
[
22
]
.
We note that many other retractions are possible, such as Cayley transformation and QR factorization
[
1
]
.
We focus on the exponential mapping in this work and leave other mappings for future research.

Figure

12
.

We will focus on low-rank implementations in future versions of the code as part of the push to improve efficiency.

Appendix J
Variable Projection with Unit Simplex Constraints

In the vector case, suppose we seek a minimizer of the following problem

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{*}(p)=\argmin_{\mathbf{x}\in\Delta^{n}}f(\mathbf{x},p)

(J.1)

where
\Delta^{n}
is the unit simplex and
p\in\mathbb{R}
is a scalar parameter for simplicity.
If
\mathbf{x}\in\Delta^{n}
, then
\mathbf{x}
is a vector of discrete probabilities; that is,
\mathbf{x}\geq\bf 0
entrywise and
\mathbf{e}^{\top}\mathbf{x}=1
where
\mathbf{e}
is the vector of all ones.
We assume
f
is convex in
\mathbf{x}
, and beause
\Delta^{n}
forms a convex set, the resulting optimization problem is convex and admits a global minimizer.
To solve (
J.1
), we set up the Lagrangian, following the conventions in
[
3
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\lambda}$},\mu)\equiv f(\mathbf{x},p)-\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\lambda}$}^{\top}\mathbf{x}+\mu(\mathbf{e}^{\top}\mathbf{x}-1)

(J.2)

where
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\lambda}$}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
and
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
are the dual variables.
The corresponding Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions are the following:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x},p)-\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\lambda}$}+\mu\mathbf{e}

\displaystyle={\bf 0}

stationarity

(J.3a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}^{\top}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=1

primal feasibility

(J.3b)

\mathbf{\lambda}

\displaystyle\geq{\bf 0}

dual feasibility

(J.3c)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}x_{i}

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{for $i=1,\dots,n$}

complementary slackness

(J.3d)

where stationarity can be equivalently written as
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x},\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\lambda}$},\mu)
.
Let
\mathbf{x}^{*}(p)
be a KKT point.
We are interested in computing the gradient at a KKT optimal point with respect to the parameters; that is,

\displaystyle\nabla_{p}f(\mathbf{x}^{*}(p),p)=\nabla_{p}\mathbf{x}^{*}(p)\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}^{*}(p),p)+\nabla_{p}f(\mathbf{x}^{*}(p),p)

(J.4)

The challenging term to compute is
\nabla_{p}\mathbf{x}^{*}(p)
, the sensitivity of
\mathbf{x}^{*}(p)
with respect to the parameters.
We explore this in two cases.

Figure 14:
Illustration of optimizing over the unit simplex.
The star is the unconstrained minimum of the objective function given by the gray contour lines.
The black line is the unit simplex, the

blue
and

magenta
points are current iterates that satisfy the constraint, and the blue point is optimal.
The vectors of correspond color are the (negative) gradient directions for the unconstrained problem.
The

red
vector in the left plot shows the direction the magenta point will move when updating with a projected gradient step.
The black vector in right plot is orthogonal to the simplex, demonstrating that blue point cannot move any closer to the minimum and thus is stationary.

Case 1: No Active Inequality Constraints (
\mathbf{x}^{*}(p)>0
)

From complementary slackness, we have
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\lambda}$}^{*}=\bf 0
.
Substituting
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\lambda}$}^{*}=\bf 0
into the stationarity condition, we have
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}^{*}(p),p)=-\mu^{*}\mathbf{e}
.
As expected, the gradient of the objective function at the stationary point is normal to the equality constraint (
Figure

14
).
The change in the optimal point,
\nabla_{p}\mathbf{x}^{*}(p)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, must ensure
\mathbf{x}^{*}(p)
to remains feasible; i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{e}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}^{*}(p)+\alpha\nabla_{p}\mathbf{x}^{*}(p)^{\top})

\displaystyle=1

(J.5a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{*}(p)+\alpha\nabla_{p}\mathbf{x}^{*}(p)^{\top}

\displaystyle\geq\bf 0

(J.5b)

where
\alpha\geq 0
is a step size.
Because
\mathbf{x}^{*}(p)
is already feasible, the first equality guarantees that that
\nabla_{p}\mathbf{x}^{*}(p)^{\top}
lies in the null space of
\mathbf{e}
.
Hence,
\nabla_{p}\mathbf{x}^{*}(p)^{\top}
is orthogonal to
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}^{*}(p),p)
and the first term in (
J.4
) vanishes.
Thus, we can ignore the sensitivity of the solution with respect to the parameters when the constraints are inactive.

Case 2: Active Constraints (
x_{i}=0\Longrightarrow\lambda_{i}\geq 0
on
\mathcal{A}
)

Let
\mathcal{A}
be the active set (i.e., the set of indices for which
x_{i}=0
) and let
\mathcal{A}^{C}
be the inactive set (i.e., the set of indices for which
x_{i}>0
).
In this case,
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}^{*}(p),p)=\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\lambda}$}-\mu\mathbf{e}
.
Let
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times|\mathcal{A}^{C}|}
be a subsampling matrix that selects entries in
\mathcal{A}^{C}
.
Then, we have
\mathbf{S}^{\top}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}^{*}(p),p)=-\mu\mathbf{S}^{\top}\mathbf{e}
.
Unsurprisingly, on the inactive set, the gradient behaves the same as in Case 1.

On active constraints, we have
x_{i}^{*}(p)=0
and the gradient
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}^{*}(p),p)
points in the steepest ascent direction, which is no longer guaranteed to be normal to the unit simplex (
Figure

14
).
This case does not guarantee the first term of (
J.4
) vanishes.
In practice, we optimize with an interior point method from
Matlab
to avoid hitting the constraints and thus only require Case 1 to hold.
