Bridging Language and Actions: A Survey of Language-Conditioned Robot Manipulation
Hongkuan Zhou, Xiangtong Yao, Oier Mees, Yuan Meng, Ted Xiao, Yonatan Bisk, Jean Oh, Edward Johns, Mohit Shridhar, Dhruv Shah, Jesse Thomason, Kai Huang, Joyce Chai, Zhenshan Bing, Alois Knoll, [3.0ex]

Abstract
Language-conditioned robot manipulation is an emerging field aimed at enabling seamless communication and cooperation between humans and robotic agents by teaching robots to comprehend and execute instructions conveyed in natural language. This interdisciplinary area integrates scene understanding, language processing, and policy learning to bridge the gap between human instructions and robotic actions. In this comprehensive survey, we systematically explore recent advancements in language-conditioned robotic manipulation. We categorize existing methods into language-conditioned reward shaping, language-conditioned policy learning, neuro-symbolic artificial intelligence, and the utilization of foundational models (FMs) such as large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs). Specifically, we analyze state-of-the-art techniques concerning semantic information extraction, environment and evaluation, auxiliary tasks, and task representation strategies. By conducting a comparative analysis, we highlight the strengths and limitations of current approaches in bridging language instructions with robot actions. Finally, we discuss open challenges and future research directions, focusing on potentially enhancing generalization capabilities and addressing safety issues in language-conditioned robot manipulators.

The agent comprises three key modules: the language module, the perception module, and the control module. These modules serve the functions of understanding instructions, perceiving the environment’s state, and acquiring skills, respectively. Markov Decision Process

Markov decision process (MDP) is a discrete-time stochastic control process. It provides a mathematical framework for modeling decision-making in situations where outcomes are partly random and partly under the control of a decision-maker. Formally, an MDP is defined by the tuple
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},\mathcal{R},\gamma)
, where:

•

\mathcal{S}
is the state space.

•

\mathcal{A}
is the action space.

•

\mathcal{P}

S\times A\times S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\mathcal{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)
indicates the probability that action
a

s

s^{\prime}
in the next time step.

•

\mathcal{R}:S\times A\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

\mathcal{R}(s,a)
represents the reward that an agent can get by taking action

•

\gamma
is a discount factor to ensure the convergence of the learning process.

II-B

Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning is a learning paradigm where an agent interacts with an MDP to learn an optimal policy that maximizes the cumulative expected reward over time. Reinforcement learning introduces the policy (
\pi
) into the MDP framework. A policy is mapping from states to actions (i.e.,
\pi(s)=a
) that guide the agent’s decision-making process.

The goal of reinforcement learning is to select a policy
\pi^{*}
which maximizes expected return
J(\pi)
when the agent acts according to it. The expected return can be written as

\displaystyle J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{a_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t})\atop s_{t+1}\sim P(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})}[\sum_{t}\gamma^{t}\mathcal{R}(s_{t},a_{t})]\text{.}

(1)

The central optimization problem in RL can be expressed by

\displaystyle\pi^{*}=\arg\max_{\pi}J(\pi)\text{,}

(2)

with
\pi^{*}
being the optimal policy. RL algorithms typically define value function

V^{\pi}(s)=\mathbb{E}_{a_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t})\atop s_{i+1}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})}[\sum_{t}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\mathcal{R}(s_{t},a_{t})|s_{0}=s]\text{,}

(3)

and action-value function

Q^{\pi}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}_{s^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot|s,a)}[\mathcal{R}(s,a)+\gamma V^{\pi}(s^{\prime})]

(4)

to guide the search for an optimal policy.

II-C

Imitation Learning

In imitation learning, agents directly model expert demonstrations without relying on direct task reward feedback. The goal remains to learn a policy,
\pi
, that emulates the behavior exhibited by experts in executing the desired tasks. These demonstrations consist of sequences of states and actions experienced by the experts, typically represented as
\tau=\{s_{0},a_{0},s_{1},a_{1},...\}
. The field of imitation learning can be primarily divided into two key categories: 1) Behavioral Cloning (BC) and 2) Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL).

II-C
1
Behavioral Cloning

In behavioral cloning
42
, the trajectory executed by expert agents is treated as the reference or ground truth trajectory. Through supervised learning, an imitation policy is acquired by minimizing the disparity between the anticipated actions and the actual actions observed in the ground truth trajectory. Considering a set of trajectories are collected from experts
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
, the optimization problem can be defined as:

\hat{\pi}^{*}=\arg\min_{\pi}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{T}}\sum_{s\in\tau}L(\pi(s),\pi^{*}(s))\text{.}

(5)

where
L
is the cost function,
\pi^{*}(s)
and
\pi(s)
are the expert’s and predicted actions at the state
s
, respectively.

II-C
2
Inverse Reinforcement Learning

An alternative approach to behavioral cloning is to reason about and try to learn a representation of the underlying reward function that the expert was using to generate the actions. The agent can potentially outperform the expert by learning the intent behind its behaviors. This approach, which reasons about the rewards to drive the expert’s behaviors, is called inverse reinforcement learning (IRL). Typical IRL approaches include Apprenticeship Learning
43
and Maximum Entropy IRL.
44

II-E

Language Model

Language models are designed to estimate the likelihood of sequences of words in human language. They learn patterns, structures, and semantics from vast amounts of textual data, enabling them to understand and predict language usage. In recent years, neural language models have advanced significantly, allowing for generating coherent and contextually relevant text. We provide a brief history of neural language models.

Neural Language/Lexical Models (NLMs):
NLMs leverage neural networks to estimate the probability of word sequences,
106
,
107
,
108
e.g., recurrent neural networks (RNNs), offering a more powerful alternative to traditional statistical methods. Collobert et al.
109
made a remarkable contribution by developing a unified neural network approach capable of handling various Natural Language Processing (NLP) tasks within a single framework, demonstrating the versatility of neural models in language understanding. Furthermore, the introduction of word2vec by Mikolov et al.
110
,
111
revolutionized word representations by employing a simple yet efficient shallow neural network to learn distributed word embeddings. These embeddings have proven highly effective across a wide range of NLP tasks and have been instrumental in advancing applications like language-conditioned robot manipulation by enhancing semantic understanding.

Pre-trained Language Models (PLMs):
PLM is an early attempt to extract semantic meaning from natural language. ELMo
112
aims to capture context-aware word representations by first pre-training a bidirectional LSTM (biLSTM) network and then fine-tuning the biLSTM network according to specific downstream tasks. Moreover, drawing inspiration from the Transformer architecture
113
and incorporating self-attention mechanisms, BERT
21
takes language model pre-training a step further. It accomplishes this by conducting bidirectional pre-training exercises on extensive unlabeled text corpora. These specially crafted pre-training tasks imbue BERT with contextual understanding.

Large Language Models (LLMs):
LLMs become popular as scaling of Pre-trained Language Models (PLMs) lead to an improved performance on downstream tasks. Many researchers attempt to study the performance limit of PLMs by scaling the size of models and datasets,
114
e.g., the comparatively small 1.5B parameter GPT-2,
115
versus larger 175B GPT-3,
116
and 540B PaLM.
117
Although these models share similar architectures, larger models exhibit enhanced capabilities such as few-shot learning, in-context learning, and improved performance on language understanding and generation benchmarks. Additionally, models like ChatGPT have adapted the GPT family for dialogue by incorporating techniques such as instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback.
118
This results in more coherent and contextually appropriate conversational abilities, which are crucial for interactive robot manipulation tasks where ongoing dialogue between the human and robot can enhance task execution and adaptability. By integrating LLMs into robotic systems, researchers aim to leverage their advanced language understanding to enable robots to handle challenging tasks by reasoning and inferring missing information.
119
,
84
This contributes to developing more robust and flexible manipulators that can operate effectively in unstructured real-world environments.

,
80

Vision-Language Models (VLMs):
Vision-Language Models play a key role in extracting and combining visual and textual content from large-scale web data. This paradigm equips the agent with an expansive understanding of the world. Seminal models in this domain, such as PaLM-E,
120
PaLI-X,

CLIP,

Vision-Language-Action Models (VLAs):
To fit both natural language responses and robotic actions into the same format, actions can be expressed as text tokens and incorporated directly into the training set of the model in the same way as natural language tokens. These models unify vision, language, and action by representing them as tokens and are trained end-to-end by generating action tokens for tasks. Gato

Foundation Models (FMs):
Foundation models, which are trained on massive data and can perform a wide range of tasks. Both LLMs and VLMs belong to FMs. Instead of training models from scratch, the prevalent approach nowadays involves utilizing pre-trained foundation models as a starting point to fine-tune new models. Well-known foundation models include language models like the GPT and PaLM family, Multi-modality models like DALL-E and Flamingo for images, and RT-1 and RT-2 for robot control.

Sparse Reward Shaping

Some methods

Despite the convenience of reward design, such approaches often grapple with the challenge of low sample efficiency, necessitating extended training periods or even failing to converge altogether.

III-A
2
Dense Reward Shaping

Some approaches leverage dense reward design to guide the exploration. Rewards are given not only upon finishing the specified instruction but also while progressing towards its completion. For instance, Yao et al.
56

8

Dense reward shaping significantly improves the sample efficiency of policy learning but introduces greater complexity, making reward design a major challenge.

Open-loop planning
:
In recent years, many researchers have designed LLM-based planners that integrate LLMs with different external components. These external components can provide LLMs additional input information or embodied feedback from the environment, thus ensuring the output actions adhere to the constraints and achieve the goal. Ahn et al.
79
leverage the affordance function to quantify the success rate of each action in the current state, and LLMs reorder the set of predicted actions and output the most accessible action. Ren et al.
80
combine LLMs with conformal prediction to measure and align uncertainty. LLM planning can be formulated as multiple-choice Q&A. It generates a series of candidate actions in the next step. Then, LLMs choose the correct options by a conformal prediction threshold calculated based on a user-specified success rate. If the robot cannot select the only correct option, it will ask humans for help, thus aligning the uncertainty, e.g., “Put a plastic bowl in the microwave” and “Put a metal bowl in the microwave” for the task “Heating up the food”. Moreover, Huang et al.
189
let two pre-trained LLMs play different roles in task planning. A pre-trained causal LLM decomposes the high-level task into a sensible mid-level action plan. The other pre-trained masked LLMs leverage semantic similarity to translate these mid-level actions into admissible learned actions, e.g., translating the action “squeeze out a glob of lotion” into “pour the lotion into right hand”. Wu et al.
190
utilize CLIP
29
and a masked RCNN as an open-vocabulary object detector to collect multiple RGB images and predict a list of objects existing in the scene, showcasing the open-loop model guide planning with auxiliary information and mitigating the issues of LLMs to generate multi-step actions involving unavailable objects in the specific environment.

Nevertheless, these prior approaches still cannot solve the long-horizon tasks successfully, which is caused by two major issues. Firstly, the prior approaches adopt short-term or open-loop execution strategies, trusting LLMs to generate the correct strategies without accounting for the geometric dependence over a skill sequence
81

that is an essential factor in solving the long-horizon task. Open-loop approaches like Saycan
79
,
80
,
189
,
190
have distinct planning and control components separately. LLMs, as offline planners, never received embodied feedback to reflect on previous executions. Therefore, most open-loop approaches must assume faultless skill execution in solving long-horizon tasks.
79
,
189
This assumption limits their scalability and success rate of solving tasks in a new environment. Additionally, many current approaches output a plan that can be seen as a one-shot plan,
191
i.e., the approaches have no replanning function. LLMs lacking state feedback do not replan the generated skill but only focus on reasoning over more accessible skills in the next step. Obviously, it is extremely difficult for these open-loop approaches to generate a flawless one-shot plan that can solve long-horizon tasks directly. On the one hand, various complex preconditions and unknown accidents will happen in the real world, which makes the one-shot plan non-executable easily. On the other hand, many challenging long-horizon tasks, such as household tasks or rearrangement tasks, involve multiple objects and a series of chronologically linked subgoals, making it difficult to cover all of them in a one-shot plan.

III-E

Empowered by Vision-Language Models

VLMs epitomize the fusion of visual and textual data, having a revolution in the realm of machine perception and understanding. Their proficiency in interpreting both modalities marks a significant milestone in the arena of robot manipulation. Two types of approaches have been identified in the literature: contrastive learning and autoregressive approaches.

III-E
1
Contrastive Learning Approaches

Contrastive Learning is widely used in VLMs. They learn representations where similar samples are brought closer together in the learned latent space while dissimilar samples are pushed farther apart.
The well-known approach CLIP

aligns the image embedding and the text embedding of its corresponding caption in the latent space.
In the field of robot manipulation, CLIP-based approaches are extensively applied.

IV-A

VIMAbench:
VIMAbench
247

217

The tasks cover a diverse range of skills, including goal-reaching, IL, novel concept grounding, constraint satisfaction, and reasoning. Each task is procedurally generated into thousands of instances by varying combinations of objects, textures, and initial configurations. VIMAbench generates training demonstrations using scripted Oracle agents with access to the full simulator state. The dataset contains nearly 650,000 successful expert trajectories for IL. A key feature of VIMAbench is the multi-level evaluation protocol to systematically test an agent’s generalization capabilities. It defines 4 levels of difficulty: 1) randomized object placement with seen prompts, 2) novel combinations of seen objects, 3) introduction of new objects not seen during training, and 4) completely new tasks and prompts. The benchmark aims to support the development of generalist robot learning agents that can handle a wide variety of tasks specified via intuitive multimodal interfaces. VIMAbench facilitates targeted probing of model capabilities on diverse skills beyond its training distribution.

IV-C

Auxiliary Tasks

In the realm of robotics, auxiliary tasks refer to secondary objectives incorporated alongside the primary manipulation task, aimed at enriching the robot’s learning process and enhancing its overall performance. These auxiliary tasks include visual attention, reconstruction, and predicting future states. These additional learning objectives have two main advantages: 1) Ensuring important information flow in the neural networks, which is critical for decision-making. 2) Guiding the direction of gradient descent during training can potentially lead the neural network to a more optimal location in the weight space. We aim to introduce these techniques within the language-conditioned domain and explore their utilization within language-conditioned approaches.

IV-C
1
Visual attention

To ensure proper manipulation of the intended objects, certain methodologies suggest incorporating a visual attention module into their neural networks. This module aims to deduce the relevant regions of objects described by the task. Stepputtis et al.
143
leverage the F-RCNN backbone to identify the specific object region to manipulate, guided by linguistic instructions. Abolghasemi et al.
142
utilize a GAN-based approach designed to create visual attention masks for objects intended to be manipulated. Mees et al.
65
employ visual affordance model VAPO
313
to learn a language-conditioned affordance and combine it with a 7-DoF low-level policy of agent to improve the success rate of manipulating right objects.

IV-C
2
Reconstruction

(Visual) reconstruction serves as a frequently employed strategy to ensure the essential information is extracted from observations. Popular models for reconstruction tasks encompass VAEs and GANs, which find extensive application as auxiliary tools in robot manipulations. Shridhar et al.
246
leverage VAEs for encoding and decoding voxel information, aiming to optimize the extraction of semantic details from observations. CC-VAE proposed by Nair et al.
314
encodes the observations into latent space. In contrast to original VAEs, CC-VAEs additionally incorporate the initial state of a trajectory as input. Rahmatizadeh et al.
132
and Abolghasemi et al.
142
use GAN autoencoder to reconstruct input images. Ebert et al.
315
reconstruct masks of the current frame and flow field between the current frame and the next frame in their architecture.

IV-C
3

Some approaches predict future states in order to help the agent better understand the dynamics of environments. Paxton et al.

train the agent by predicting not only the next actions based on present observations but also the next world states (including RGB, depth, and pose) as well as the next subgoals expressed in natural language. These auxiliary tasks aid the agent in comprehending its environment and breaking down tasks into multiple sequential steps. Ebert et al.
315
also forecast the image observations for the subsequent frame. It achieves this by predicting the motion of pixels from the current image observation to the next one.

Also, some other kinds of auxiliary tasks exist, like predicting the current frame’s depth information.

IV-D

Task representation

Enabling agents to perform a diverse range of tasks efficiently and seamlessly remains a challenge in the field of robot manipulation.
In this field, a crucial question emerges: How can a robot effectively acquire an understanding of similarities among diverse tasks, such as grasping tasks, while concurrently discerning finer differences, such as variations in colors and shapes?
In simpler words, how to represent different tasks?
In this part, we introduce two main concepts in this field, namely task-specific learning, and task-agnostic learning. Task-specific learning involves the agent focusing solely on learning from particular tasks, typically language-based instructions in the training data. Conversely, task-agnostic learning aims to train an agent capable of reaching any attainable state within the space of possible states. Given that language instructions can not iterate over all state space, these approaches often rely on visual data to enrich the goal space by treating visual states as goals.

IV-D
1
Task specific learning

Task-specific learning represents the most intuitive way to learn language-conditioned policy. The agent only learns to complete those predefined tasks described by language instructions and undergoes training on such a given task set, potentially acquiring the capability to generalize to additional language commands due to the inherent generalization capacity of the language encoding module. For instance, “grasp the red block” and “lift the red block” should have a similar task representation in the latent space after language encoding.
Sodhani et al.
294
introduce the CMDP
317
into the RL framework to enable the utilization of language-based side information as the task indicator. PerAct
246
encodes language goals and RGB-D voxel level observation with Transformer structure, and directly output action sequences. CLIPort
93
combines the broad semantic understanding of CLIP with the spatial precision of Transporter to map observation and languages to action sequences. Chen et al.
229
leverage task-specific prompts to obtain representations from a VLM to train policies that outperform equivalent policies trained on generic, non-promptable image embeddings.

Moreover, certain methods harness the robust generalization proficiency exhibited by LLMs, enabling the trained agent to extend its competence to a range of unfamiliar tasks and environments.
Ren et al.
295
introduce the feature representation of tools used in manipulation tasks, leveraging a pre-trained LLM. It enables the agent to adapt to new tools based on text descriptions during the meta-learning phase.
A similar approach is also introduced by Tang et al.
318
in task-oriented grasping, wherein the agent leverages a pre-trained LLM to extract features of objects to be grasped and demonstrates generalization capabilities when confronted with new objects in real-world grasping tasks.
Palo et al.
319
uses
FLAN-T5

320

Some approaches learn in an interactive way

,

2
Task agnostic learning

Contrary to task-specific learning, task-agnostic learning refers to the ability to reach any reachable goal state from any state within the environment.
325
The concept of “tasks” transforms from finite and discrete to infinite and continuous, with the form of
\langle
initial state
,
goal state
\rangle

Task-agnostic learning approaches aim to discover solutions that seamlessly traverse state-to-state transitions within the given boundaries of the environment.
Considering that tasks described by language instructions can not cover the whole continuous task space, these approaches usually combine the language instructions and goal images to build a continuous task space, since goal images can describe any state in the environment.
Some approaches

Vision-language-based multi-modal representation learning is frequently employed in such scenarios. Specifically, the CLIP-based approaches, which align the languages and images with contrastive loss in the latent space, are widely used in task-agnostic representation. Recent works use image frames

V
Future Directions

While vision and language models provide a strong foundation for language-conditioned manipulation, several challenges remain for future research. In this section, we discuss two key challenges:
generalization ability
and
safety
.

V-A

Generalization Ability

The generalization ability of language-conditioned robot manipulation systems is a critical aspect that needs to be addressed in future research. A trained agent will not be helpful in our daily work if it only works well in specific domains or pre-determined scenarios. There is a need for these systems to generalize their knowledge and skills to handle a wider range of scenarios. How can the agent understand and perform unseen language instructions? How to stabilize the performance of the trained agent in unfamiliar environments? How to make sure that the learned or designed skills can be transferred to a new domain with a few-shot or even zero-shot setting?
These three questions correspond to the generalization ability of language, visual, and control modules.
We notice that the current trend for acquiring better generalization ability is to leverage larger models and more data. “The more we see, the better we know” is the motivation behind that. FMs contain a large amount of knowledge in our daily lives, which is highly beneficial for robot manipulation tasks. Many studies
85
,
120
,
203
demonstrate the strong generalization ability, leveraging large models. They investigate the approaches to combine FMs with the control module. We anticipate the development of substantial vision-language-action models (VLAs) that are trained end-to-end to reliably address language-conditioned manipulation tasks encountered in everyday life. Such a model would address the limitations of current foundational models, which lack the ability to interact with the real-world environment. It is akin to repeatedly studying a food recipe and finally venturing into cooking a sunny-side-up egg or extensively reading martial arts manuals and eventually engaging in the first combat.
We continue our discussion regarding data scarcity, distribution shift, forgetting catastrophe, benchmarking, and etc. to further analyze the challenges in this field.

V-A
1
Data Scarcity

Training a large model requires a large amount of data. VLMs and LLMs are trained with web-scale data. Collecting such data in the robot manipulation field is not feasible and represents much effort. Recently, some approaches
136
,
3
,
4
,
66
,
330
leverages play data instead of expert labeled data to ease the effort to collect data. Unlabeled play data is often readily available in large quantities and can be acquired at a lower cost. It is also possible to gather play data by integrating computer gaming platforms, potentially providing access to data from millions of users. Some other approaches
92

92
utilize VLMs to augment the scenarios or instructions of video demonstration data.
Nevertheless, relying solely on play data and generative VLMs may not be sufficient to train a robust FM for robot manipulation. We foresee the need for alternative techniques to gather large-scale demonstration data to enhance robot manipulation capabilities.

V-A
2
Distribution Shift

The distribution shift between the training domain and the target domain could significantly impact the performance in the target domain. The distribution of perception data in a simulated environment may vary considerably from that in the real world. Consequently, a model trained solely in a simulated environment may not seamlessly transition to real-world applications due to a significant performance decrease
66
. To tackle this issue, transfer learning strategies can be utilized to fine-tune the model for a particular domain. However, fine-tuning might not entirely bridge the gap between the simulated and real-world environments. For one thing, the fine-tuned model could overfit the fine-tuning data, significantly diminishing its generalization capability. For the other thing, the fine-tuning procedure may lead to detrimental forgetting of the previously learned knowledge from the training domain. This issue will be discussed in the following session. Recently, many approaches have leaned toward leveraging FMs, which are pre-trained with data from various domains and fine-tuned into the targeting domain for specific tasks. In this approach, the model can assimilate relevant knowledge for the target domain while discarding irrelevant information. Additionally, the model is less prone to overfitting on the fine-tuning data due to its large size and containing a wealth of knowledge, which is much more than the targeting domain requires.

V-A
3
Catastrophic Forgetting

In the field of machine learning, forgetting refers to the phenomenon where previously acquired information or knowledge in a system degrades over time. This detrimental effect, often referred to as catastrophic forgetting, has been observed across several research domains, including foundation models (FMs), domain adaptation, meta-learning, generative models, and federated learning.
331
Training the model on a new dataset that contains new scenarios might make the model forget the knowledge that has been previously acquired. This phenomenon often causes a drop in performance on overall tasks. Well-known methods to prevent forgetting include elastic weight consolidation (EWC),
332
and Replay-buffer-based approaches,
333
,
334
,
335
which replay the old training data during training on new tasks, can help mitigate forgetting by allowing the model to retain knowledge of past experiences. Some distillation methods,
336
where the student model distills knowledge from the teacher model and retains crucial information while learning new tasks, can also be applied to address the forgetting problem.

V-A
4
Heterogeneous Embodied Agents

Language instructions are highly abstract messages with a clear purpose, independent of robot type and the process by which the robot performs tasks. While different embodiments share a common language space,
337
their varying physical properties mean that policies trained on one embodiment often require retraining before being transferred to another. For example, robotic manipulators such as the Franka and Kuka robots have different sizes, end-effectors, and workspace ranges. Using a Kuka robot to execute a language-conditioned policy trained on a Franka robot is likely to fail, even with the same instruction “pick the block”, because the policy does not account for embodiment differences. HTP addresses this issue in robot manipulation by simplifying the creation of reliable policies for new embodiments and tasks, reducing data requirements, and enhancing overall performance.
337
This approach may inspire the realization of an efficient universal policy.
The constraints of a target robot or embodiment and environment can cause serious gaps between a simulation based on the foundation models and the robot’s real execution, e.g., in the robot painting tasks.
338
Learning the dynamics model of brush actions using real robot data
339
and self-supervised fine-tuning
340
have shown promising results in reducing such gaps.

V-A
5
Benchmarking

The generalization ability in the real world is hard to quantify because the performance of the model in the real world is heavily dependent on real-world hardware specifications.
For this reason, most of the approaches evaluate their results in simulators, and no real-world benchmarks exist to evaluate the models.
However, high performance in a simulator does not guarantee good performance in the real world due to the sim-to-real gap between the simulator and real-world environments.
The benchmarks constructed on simulators either lack support from a physical engine
341
,
342
or are supported by physical engine
264
,
265
that exhibit a significant gap compared to the real world.
To address such limitations, we propose two possible directions. Firstly, constructing a simulator equipped with stronger physical engines to provide simulations that closely resemble the real world. The narrower the gap between the simulation and the real world, the less effort is required to apply the models trained in the simulator to real-world scenarios.
Secondly, real-world benchmarks are necessary. Organizing robot contests that provide platforms with realistic scenarios (such as household and tabletop environments) helps to evaluate the generalizability of models in real-world contexts.
343
,
344

V-A
6
Beyond web-scale data

Do we really need vast amounts of data to excel at manipulation and language understanding? Humans never have access to all web-scale data, yet we become proficient in communication and object manipulation through years of exposure to relatively limited data — videos, language, and actions. The data amount is significantly smaller than the web-scale datasets that train FMs.
Deep learning models are highly data-hungry, requiring massive amounts of information to generalize well. When there is limited data, deep learning models are more likely to overfit to spurious correlation patterns (short-cuts) that exist in the training set but do not generalize to real-world scenarios. This highlights a key limitation: relying solely on data-driven approaches may not be sufficient to solve manipulation and other complex tasks.
Neuro-symbolic approaches,
177
which combine neural networks with symbolic reasoning, offer a promising alternative.
These models can enhance data efficiency and reduce the need for massive datasets by integrating predefined human-readable knowledge, such as knowledge graph or knowledge base. This suggests that FMs trained on enormous data are not the only path to achieving intelligence. Exploring how to leverage limited data to train models with better generalization capabilities is an intriguing and important research direction.

V-B

Safety Issues

Safety issues are related to safety during human interaction. There exist three main concerns.

V-B
1
Ambiguity in language

One of the main safety concerns is the inherent ambiguity in human language. Natural language instructions often leave room for interpretation. For instance, given the instruction “
Remove the chemicals from the table
.”, the user may intend for the robot to carefully relocate the containers of chemicals from the table to a designated storage area, ensuring safety by avoiding spills or accidents. However, this instruction might be interpreted as physically removing chemicals by tipping over or mishandling the containers, resulting in a hazardous situation with chemical spills and potential harm.
One of the potential solutions is to interactively communicate with users to address the ambiguity in language. Robots should be equipped with the capability to ask clarifying questions when instructions are ambiguous. For instance, if instructed to “
Remove the chemicals from the table
,” the robot could respond with, “
Do you want me to move the chemicals to storage, or should I dispose of them
?”
Additionally, implementing feedback loops to confirm its interpretation before acting is essential. For example, the robot might state, “
I understood that I should carefully move the containers to the storage area. Is this correct
?” This ensures clarity and prevents potential errors.

V-B
2
Handling corner cases

Corner cases, which represent unusual, unexpected, or challenging scenarios, pose a significant challenge in language-conditioned robot manipulation. These cases seldom occur in real-world scenarios but can be extremely significant, demanding considerable attention. Handling these cases poses a significant challenge. When attempting to retrain the network with these gathered corner cases, it introduces a critical problem - model forgetfulness or catastrophic forgetting, as mentioned previously. Altering the frequency of corner cases may result in unforeseen model behavior for other cases. It is akin to the concept of “robbing Peter to pay Paul”. This challenge in managing corner cases also stands as the foremost issue in the realm of autonomous driving.
As observed in the autonomous driving domain, despite an enormous amount of data, the lack of safety-critical data remains a curse of rarity. An enhanced data utilization approach has been proposed in

V-B
4
Real-Time Performance

Real-time performance presents another hurdle for robot manipulation, especially when relying on large-scale models (LLMs, VLMs) that extend inference times. These models often have extended inference times, making it difficult to guarantee real-time responsiveness.
Robotic environments are rarely static, constantly changing due to object movements, human interactions, or environmental shifts. To maintain safety, robotic systems must adapt to these dynamic changes immediately. However, the slow inference times of large models delay decision-making, stretching the control loop and hindering the system’s ability to respond quickly, which compromises real-time safety.

To address this, several potential solutions can be explored. One approach is model optimization techniques, such as model pruning, quantization,
353
or knowledge distillation,
354
which reduce the size and complexity of models, improving inference speed without drastically compromising performance.
Additionally, hybrid systems combining smaller, faster models for real-time perception and response, with larger models reserved for offline or non-critical tasks, could strike a balance between real-time performance and advanced capabilities. These strategies could help mitigate the inference delays of large models, enhancing both responsiveness and safety.

VI
Conclusion

In summary, this survey presents an overview of the current approaches to integrating language instructions into robot manipulations. Our analysis focuses on various approaches with language-conditioned reward shaping, language-conditioned policy learning, neuro-symbolic AI, and also emphasizes state-of-the-art LLMs or VLMs-empowered models. We have conducted a comprehensive comparative analysis, evaluating these approaches based on factors such as semantic information extraction, environments and evaluations, auxiliary tasks, and task representations.

Furthermore, our discussion delves into the challenges present in this field and suggests potential research directions, aiming to inspire more researchers to engage in this area. We aspire for this paper to stimulate interest within the research community, encouraging further meaningful investigations that advance the synergy between language and robot manipulation.
