Testing Sumsets is Hard
Xi Chen, Shivam Nadimpalli, Tim Randolph, Rocco A. Servedio, Or Zamir

Abstract
A subset S S of the Boolean hypercube 𝔽 2 n \mathbb{F}_{2}^{n} is a sumset if S = { a + b : a , b ∈ A } S=\{a+b\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}a,b\in A\} for some A ⊆ 𝔽 2 n A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n} . Sumsets are central objects of study in additive combinatorics, featuring in several influential results. We prove a lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( 2 n / 2 ) \Omega(2^{n/2}) for the number of queries needed to test whether a Boolean function f : 𝔽 2 n → { 0 , 1 } f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\{0,1\} is the indicator function of a sumset. Our lower bound for testing sumsets follows from sharp bounds on the related problem of shift testing , which may be of independent interest. We also give a near-optimal 2 n / 2 ⋅ poly ⁡ ( n ) 2^{n/2}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n) -query algorithm for a smoothed analysis formulation of the sumset refutation problem.

Introduction

In recent years, theoretical computer science has increasingly been influenced by ideas and techniques from
additive combinatorics
, a field sitting at the intersection of combinatorics, number theory, Fourier analysis, and ergodic theory. Notable examples of this connection include communication complexity
[
CFL83
,
BSLRZ14
,
BKM23
]
, constructions of randomness extractors
[
Bou05
,
BIW06
,
Rao07
,
DS07
]
, and property testing
[
Gre05
,
Sam07
]
; we also refer the reader to various surveys on additive combinatorics from the vantage point of theoretical computer science
[
BTW07
,
Tre09
,
Vio11
,
Bib13
,
Lov17
]
.

Among the simplest objects of study in additive combinatorics are
sumsets
: A subset
S
of an abelian group
G
(with group operation “
+
”) is said to be a
sumset
if
S=A+A
for some
A\subseteq G
, where for sets
A,B\subseteq G
we write
A+B
to denote the set
\{a+b\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}a\in A,b\in B\}
.
Sumsets play a major role in additive combinatorics, where their study has led to many questions and insights about the additive structure of subsets of abelian groups. They are the subject of touchstone results in the field such as Freiman’s theorem
[
Fre73
]
, which (roughly speaking) says that if
|A+A|
is “not too much larger” than
|A|
then
A
must be contained in a generalized arithmetic progression which is “not too large.”

Our interest in this paper is in
algorithmic
questions related to sumsets. There has been some prior research on sumset-related problems in the context of exact and parameterized algorithms. In
[
FFV09
]
Fagnot, Fertin, and Vialette considered the
2-Sumset Cover
problem: given a set
S
of integers, does there exist a set
A
of cardinality at most
k
such that
S\subseteq A+A
? They proved
APX
-hardness for this problem and presented a
\mathrm{poly}(k)\cdot 5^{k^{2}(k+3)/2}
-time algorithm. The latter was improved to
\mathrm{poly}(k)\cdot 2^{(3\log k-1.4)k}
by Bulteau, Fertin, Rizzi, and Vialette
[
BFRV15
]
.
2-Sumset Cover
itself specializes
Generating Set
, in which the goal is to find a minimal set
A
such that
S\subseteq\{\sum_{i\in I}i\;;\;I\subseteq A\}
and which was studied in
[
CKSY07
]
. Given
S
and
k
, finding a set
A
of size
|A|\geq k
with
A+A\subseteq S
is equivalent to finding a
k
-Clique on the Cayley sum graph of
S
; this problem remains NP-hard, but can be solved with existing algorithms for
k
-clique
[
GR17
]
.

Apart from the works mentioned in the previous paragraph, computational questions pertaining to sumsets—and more broadly, to objects and properties arising naturally in additive combinatorics—do not appear to have been well studied to date. In the list
[
CL07
]
of open problems in additive combinatorics compiled by Croot and Lev fifteen years ago, only one of a dozen questions concerning sumsets deals with algorithmic aspects (Problem 4.11, “Recognizing sumsets algorithmically”), and the only subsequent reference to this problem that we have been able to identify is a 2020 MathOverflow post
[
Mat20
]
. In
[
AGU10
]
, Alon, Granville and Ubis ask whether it is possible to “quickly identify” subsets of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
which are of the form
A+B
with
|A|,|B|\geq k
, or which have “many representations” as
A+B
, but we are not aware of subsequent research addressing these questions. We find this lack of study to be surprising given the increasingly prominent role of additive combinatorics in theoretical computer science (as before, see the expositions and surveys by
[
Tre09
,
Vio11
,
Bib13
,
Lov17
]
) and the fact that many such questions are both simple and natural.

1.1
This Work

In this paper we restrict our attention to the case in which the ambient abelian group
G
is
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. We do this for several reasons: For one thing, our focus is on algorithmic problems, and
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is a very natural domain to consider from a theoretical computer science perspective. Another motivation is that
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is in some sense the simplest setting for many problems involving sumsets; as Green stated in
[
Gre05
]
, “the reason that finite field models are nice to work with is that one has the tools of linear algebra, including such notions as subspace and linear independence, which are unavailable in general abelian groups.” (Indeed, several of our arguments will make use of these linear-algebraic tools.)

Since
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is an exponentially large domain, it is natural to approach the study of sumsets over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
from the vantage point of
sublinear
algorithms. Thus, we will be interested in algorithms for which either the running time or the number calls to an oracle for the input set
S
(i.e. queries of the form “does element
x
belong to the set
S
?” is less than
2^{n}
. The recent work
[
DNS22
]
took such a sublinear-algorithms perspective; it studied a problem which was closely related to the problem of approximating the size of the sumset
A+A
, given access to an oracle for the unknown set
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. The main result of
[
DNS22
]
was that in fact
O_{\varepsilon}(1)
queries—in particular, with no dependence on the dimension parameter
n
—are sufficient for a
\pm\varepsilon\cdot 2^{n}
-accurate approximation of the quantity that they consider. This naturally motivates the following broad question: What other algorithmic problems involving sumsets may be solvable with “constant” (depending only on
\varepsilon
) or very low query complexity?

Motivated by this general question, in the current work we study a number of algorithmic questions related to sumsets. The main problems we consider are described below:

1.

We study (approximate) sumset recognition from a property testing perspective. In more detail, given access to a membership oracle for an unknown set
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, in the
sumset testing problem
the goal is to output “yes” with high probability (say, at least 9/10) if
S
is a sumset and “no” with high probability if
S
is
\varepsilon
-far from every sumset (i.e.
|S\,\triangle\,(A+A)|\geq\varepsilon 2^{n}
for every set
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
), while making as few queries to the oracle as possible.

2.

The above-described sumset testing problem turns out to be closely related to the problem of
shift testing
, which is defined as follows: A shift testing algorithm is given black-box access to two oracles
{\cal O}_{A},{\cal O}_{B}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
, which should be viewed as membership oracles for two subsets
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. The algorithm must output “yes” with probability at least
9/10
if
B=A+\{z\}
for some string
z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and must output “no” with probability at least
9/10
if the symmetric difference
B\,\triangle\,(A+\{z\})
has size at least
\varepsilon 2^{n}
for every
z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.

3.

For
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, let
\boldsymbol{N}_{\varepsilon}(S)
denote a random set which is an “
\varepsilon
-noisy” version of
S
, obtained by flipping the membership / non-membership of each
x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
in
S
with probability
\varepsilon
. It can be shown that for every
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and every constant
0<\varepsilon<1
, the noisy set
\boldsymbol{N}_{\varepsilon}(S)
is with high probability not a sumset. We study the problem of algorithmically
certifying
that
\boldsymbol{N}_{\varepsilon}(S)
is not a sumset; i.e. we are given access to a membership oracle for
\boldsymbol{N}_{\varepsilon}(S)
, where
S
is an arbitrary and unknown subset of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, and the goal is to output a set
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
of points such that there is no sumset
A+A
for which
(A+A)\cap C=\boldsymbol{N}_{\varepsilon}(S)\cap C.
We refer to this problem as the
smoothed sumset refutation problem,
since it aligns with the well-studied framework of smoothed analysis
[
ST01
]
in which an arbitrary worst-case instance is subjected to a mild perturbation.

Our main results for these problems are as follows.

Sumset Testing Lower Bound.

We give an
\Omega(2^{n/2})
lower bound on the query complexity of sumset testing:

Theorem 1
.

There is a constant
\varepsilon>0
(independent of
n
) such that any algorithm
{\cal A}
for the
\varepsilon
-sumset testing problem must make
\Omega(2^{n/2})
oracle calls.

We note that
Theorem

1
holds even for adaptive testers which may make two-sided error.

Tight Bounds for Shift Testing.
We show that the query complexity of shift testing is
{\Theta}^{*}(2^{n/2})
.

Theorem 2
.

(1)
There is an algorithm for the shift testing problem which makes
O(n2^{n/2}/\varepsilon)
oracle calls and runs in time
\mathrm{poly}(n)\cdot 2^{n}/\varepsilon
. Moreover,
(2) For any constant
0<c<1/2
, any algorithm for the shift testing problem must make
\Omega(2^{n/2})
oracle calls, even for
\varepsilon=(1/2-1/2^{cn})
.
This lower bound holds even for distinguishing the following two cases: (i)
A
is a uniform random subset of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and
B=A+\{z\}
for a uniform random
z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
; versus (ii)
A
and
B
are independent uniform random subsets of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.

Like
Theorem

1
, the lower bound (Part (2)) of
Theorem

2
holds even for adaptive testers which may make two-sided error.

A Near-Optimal Algorithm for “Smoothed” Sumset Refutation.
Our final result is a near-optimal algorithm which certifies that any noisy set
\boldsymbol{N}_{\varepsilon}(S)
is not a sumset:

Theorem 3
.

(1) There is an algorithm
{\cal A}
for the
\varepsilon
-smoothed sumset refutation problem that queries a set
\boldsymbol{B}
of
2^{n/2}\cdot O(n^{1.5}/\varepsilon^{1.5})
points with the property that
\boldsymbol{B}\cap\boldsymbol{N}_{\varepsilon}(S)
is inconsistent with every sumset with probability
1-o_{n}(1)
. Moreover, (2) Any algorithm for the
\varepsilon
-smoothed sumset refutation problem, for any
\varepsilon>0
must make
\Omega(2^{n/2}/\sqrt{n})
many oracle calls.

1.2
Technical Overview

The main idea of our algorithm for shift testing (Part (1) of
Theorem

2
) is to query one oracle with all shifts of a random point
r
by a subspace
V
, and query the other oracle by all shifts of the same point
r
by the orthogonal complement
V^{\perp}
. This requires only
O(2^{n/2})
queries, while providing information about the relationship between the two oracles vis-a-vis any possible shift
z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, since every possible shift has a decomposition into
z=z_{1}+z_{2}
for some
z_{1}\in V,z_{2}\in V^{\perp}.

The optimality of this general approach is witnessed by the lower bound in Part (2) of
Theorem

2
. The proof is by a “deferred decisions” argument which analyzes the knowledge transcript of a query algorithm which may be interacting either with the “yes”-pair of oracles or the “no”-pair of oracles. We describe a coupling of the knowledge transcripts between these two cases, and use it to argue that if fewer than
0.1\cdot 2^{n/2}
queries have been made, then with high probability the transcripts are identically distributed across these two cases. (See
[
GR02
]
for a similar high-level argument, though in an entirely different technical setting.)

The
\Omega(2^{n/2})
lower bound of
Theorem

1
for sumset testing is by a reduction to the lower bound for shift testing. We give a straightforward embedding of the “
A
is random,
B=A+\{z\}
”-versus-“
A,B
are independent random” shift testing problem over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
into the problem of sumset testing over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n+2}
. The most challenging part of the argument is to prove that in fact the “no” instances of shift testing (when
A,B
are independent random sets) give rise to instances which are far from sumsets over
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n+2}
. This requires us to argue that a subset of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n+2}
which is constant on two
n
-dimensional cosets and is uniform random on the other two
n
-dimensional cosets, is likely to be far from every sumset, which we prove using a linear algebraic argument.

For
Theorem

3
, a result due to Alon establishes that every subset of the Boolean cube of size
2^{n}-c2^{n/2}/\sqrt{n}
is a sumset for a small constant
c
, which implies that any sumset “
0
-certificate” has size
\Omega^{*}(2^{n/2})

[
Alo07
]
. To find such a certificate, we show that few noisy sumsets are likely to be consistent with an arbitrarily chosen subspace of dimension
n/2
, and then use a small random sample to rule out these sumsets with high probability.

1.3
Discussion

Our results suggest many questions and goals for future work; we record two such directions here.

The first direction is to obtain stronger results on sumset refutation.
Is it possible to strengthen our sumset refutation result by eliminating the “smoothed analysis” aspect, i.e. is it the case that any
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
that is
\varepsilon
-far from every sumset has a “
0
-certificate” of size
2^{n/2}\cdot\mathrm{poly}\left(n,\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)
? If so, can such certificates be found efficiently given query access to
S
?

The second, and perhaps most compelling, direction is to either strengthen our
\Omega(2^{n/2})
-query lower bound, or prove an upper bound, for the sumset testing problem. We are cautiously optimistic that the true query complexity of sumset testing may be closer to
2^{n/2}
queries than to
2^{n}
queries, but any nontrivial (
o(2^{n})
-query) algorithm would be an interesting result. One potentially relevant intermediate problem towards sumset testing is the problem of
k
-shift testing
, in which the goal is to determine whether oracles
\mathcal{O}_{A},\mathcal{O}_{B}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
correspond to
B=A+\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}\}
for some
k
“shift” vectors
(s_{i})_{i\in[k]}
versus
B
being
\varepsilon
-far from every union of
k
shifts of
A
.

Optimally Testing Shifts

Given
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, we say that
B
is a
shift
of

if there exists
z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
such that
A+z=B

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{O}_{A},\mathcal{O}_{B}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
be membership oracles for
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. Then:

1.

The algorithm
Shift-Tester
(
Algorithm

1
) makes
O(n2^{n/2}/\varepsilon)
oracle calls, runs in time
\mathrm{poly}(n)\cdot 2^{n}/\varepsilon
, and guarantees that:

(a)

If
B=A+z
for some
z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, the algorithm outputs “shift” with probability

;

(b)

If for every
z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}

\varepsilon

9/10
.

2.

Fix
c

3.1
Upper Bound

In this section, we prove Item 1 of
Theorem

4
. Note that since

\mathrm{dist}(B,A+z)=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left[\mathcal{O}_{A}(\boldsymbol{x})\neq\mathcal{O}_{B}(\boldsymbol{x}+z)\right],

if
B
is a shift of
A
(i.e.
B=A+z_{\ast}
for some
z_{\ast}
), we then have for that
z_{\ast}
that

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left[\mathcal{O}_{B}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{O}_{A}(\boldsymbol{x}+z_{\ast})\right]=1.

On the other hand, if
\mathrm{dist}(B,A+z)\geq\varepsilon
for every
z
, then for every
z
we have

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\left[\mathcal{O}_{B}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{O}_{A}(\boldsymbol{x}+z)\right]\leq 1-\varepsilon.

These simple observations suggest that in order to estimate
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\mathcal{O}_{B}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{O}_{A}(\boldsymbol{x}+z)]
for a particular
z
, we would like to make queries
\mathcal{O}_{B}(\boldsymbol{x}),\mathcal{O}_{A}(\boldsymbol{x}+z)
for uniform random
\boldsymbol{x}.
The fact that we need to do this for all
z
motivates the following approach; before proceeding, we introduce some notation.

Notation 5
.

We define the subsets
D_{1},D_{2}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, where
D_{1}
is the set of all
2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}
vectors whose last
\lfloor n/2\rfloor
coordinates are all-0 and
D_{2}\subset\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is the set of
2^{\lceil n/2\rceil}
vectors whose first
\lceil n/2\rceil
coordinates are all-0.
Note that every
z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
has a unique expression as

z\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=z^{(1)}+z^{(2)},\qquad\text{for}~z^{(1)}\in D_{1}~\text{and}~z^{(2)}\in D_{2}.

Fix a particular string
z=z^{(1)}+z^{(2)}
as above.
We write
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{r}+z^{(1)}
, and we observe that if
\boldsymbol{r}
is uniform random then so is
\boldsymbol{x}
.
As alluded to earlier we would like to query
B
on
\boldsymbol{x}
and
A
on
\boldsymbol{x}+z=\boldsymbol{r}+z^{(1)}+z=\boldsymbol{r}+z^{(2)}
.
The main observation is that if we query
B
on all strings in
D_{1}+\boldsymbol{r}
and query
A
on all strings in
D_{2}+\boldsymbol{r}
, then no matter what
z
is we will have made the queries
\mathcal{O}_{B}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{O}_{B}(\boldsymbol{r}+z^{(1)})
and
\mathcal{O}_{A}(\boldsymbol{x}+z)=\mathcal{O}_{A}(\boldsymbol{r}+z^{(2)})
, so we will have obtained a sample towards estimating
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}[\mathcal{O}_{B}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathcal{O}_{A}(\boldsymbol{x}+z)]
.
Since this is true for every
z
, we can reuse the above queries towards all possibilities for
z
.
(Of course one sample is not enough to estimate a probability, so we will repeat the above with
n/\varepsilon
different choices of
\boldsymbol{r}
.)

Input:
Oracles
\mathcal{O}_{A},\mathcal{O}_{B}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
and
\varepsilon>0

“Shift” or “
\varepsilon
-far from shift’’

Shift-Tester
(\mathcal{O}_{A},\mathcal{O}_{B},\varepsilon)
:

1.

Repeat the following
n/\varepsilon

(a)

Draw a uniformly random
\boldsymbol{r}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
.

(b)

Query
\mathcal{O}_{A}
on all
x\in D_{1}+\boldsymbol{r}
, and query
\mathcal{O}_{B}
on all
y\in D_{2}+\boldsymbol{r}
.

2.

For each
z=z^{(1)}+z^{(2)}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, let
p_{z}
be the fraction of the
n/\varepsilon
repetitions for which

\mathcal{O}_{A}(\boldsymbol{r}+z^{(1)})=\mathcal{O}_{B}(\boldsymbol{r}+z^{(2)}).

3.

If

for some
z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, output “shift”; otherwise output “
\varepsilon
-far from shift”.

Algorithm 1

An algorithm for shift testing.

Our algorithm,
Shift-Tester
, is presented in
Algorithm

1
.
Note that if
B

A
, i.e. if there exists a
z_{\ast}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}

\boldsymbol{r}+z^{(1)}_{\ast}\in A\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\boldsymbol{r}+z^{(1)}_{\ast}+z_{\ast}=\boldsymbol{r}+z^{(2)}_{\ast}\in B,

z^{(1)}_{\ast}+z_{\ast}=z^{(1)}_{\ast}+z^{(1)}_{\ast}+z^{(2)}_{\ast}=z^{(2)}_{\ast}
. In particular, we will have
p_{z_{\ast}}=1
and so the algorithm will return “shift” with probability
1
. On the other hand, suppose
B
is
\varepsilon
-far from
A+z
for every
z\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
; fix any such

A straightforward application of the Chernoff bound, combined with a union bound over the

19/20
a draw of
(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{no}}
is such that
\boldsymbol{B}

(1/2-1/2^{cn})
-far from every shift of
\boldsymbol{A}
(for any constant

). So to prove Item 2 of
Theorem

2
, it is enough to establish the following claim for deterministic algorithms. (By Yao’s minimax principle, this is sufficient to prove a lower bound for randomized algorithms as well.)

Claim 8
.

Let
{\tt Test}
be any deterministic,
adaptive algorithm that makes
N\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=0.1\cdot 2^{n/2}
oracle calls to
{\cal O}_{A}
and
{\cal O}_{B}
. Let
T_{\tt test}(A,B)
be the “transcript” of its queries to the oracles and received responses, i.e.
T_{\tt test}(A,B)
consists of

(first query to one of the oracles, response received)

\vdots

(
N
-th query to one of the oracles, response received).

Then we have

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{TV}}\left(T_{\tt test}(\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{yes}},\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{yes}}),T_{\tt test}(\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{no}},\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{no}})\right)\leq 0.02,

where
(\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{yes}},\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{yes}})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
and
(\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{no}},\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{no}})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{no}}
.

The claim follows by analyzing the behavior of the algorithm on an oracle constructed over the course of answering the queries posed by algorithm
Test
, i.e., “deferring” the decision of whether the oracle
(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B})
is drawn from
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{yes}}
or
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{no}}
. See for example Section 7.1 of
[
GR02
]
.

Proof.

For simplicity, we assume that in each round,
{\tt Test}
queries one point
q
and receives
both

\mathcal{O}_{A}(q)
and
\mathcal{O}_{B}(q)
; this can only make
{\tt Test}
more powerful.

Consider the following approach to answering queries posed by
Test
: before any queries are made, draw a uniform random
\boldsymbol{s}\sim\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. Let
q_{1},\dots,q_{t-1}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be the first
t-1
queries made by
Test
(we may suppose without loss of generality that all these
t-1
query strings are different from each other, since any algorithm that repeats a query string can easily be modified so as not to do so). When the
t
-th query string
q_{t}
is provided by
Test
, the answer is generated as follows:

1.

If
\boldsymbol{s}\neq q_{t}+q_{t^{\prime}}
for all
t^{\prime}\leq t
, then two independent uniform random bits
\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{A}},\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{B}}\in\{0,1\}
are drawn and returned as
\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{A}}(q_{t})
and
\mathcal{O}_{\boldsymbol{B}}(q_{t})
. (It may be helpful to think of this outcome as being “recorded”, i.e. when this happens the process “decides” that
\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{A}},\boldsymbol{b}_{\boldsymbol{B}}
are the values of
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
on the point
q_{t}

2.

If
\boldsymbol{s}=q_{t}+q_{t^{\prime}}

t

(\boldsymbol{A},\boldsymbol{B})
being
(\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{yes}},\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{yes}})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{yes}}

(\boldsymbol{A}_{\mathrm{no}},\boldsymbol{B}_{\mathrm{no}})\sim\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{no}}

This is because in either case, as long as no pair of queries

0.02

∎

5
Refuting Sumsets in the Smoothed Analysis Setting

In this section we study the smallest size of a
certificate
that a set is
not
a sumset.
Informally, for a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
a sumset
0
-certificate is a set
D\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
of points such that querying the oracle
\mathcal{O}_{S}
on every point in
D
suffices to prove that
S
is not a sumset. The formal definition follows:

Definition 15
.

A set
D\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is a
sumset
0
-certificate for

S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
if there is no sumset
S^{\prime}=A+A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
for which
S\cap D=S^{\prime}\cap D.

Small
0
-certificates are important objects of study for many property testing problems; for example, consider the classic problem of linearity testing. Since a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}
is linear if and only if
f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, the property of linearity is characterized by the non-existence of a “linearity 0-certificate” of size three. As is well known, in the seminal work
[
BLR93
]
Blum et al. showed that this is a
robust
characterization, in the sense that a simple sampling procedure which queries random triples
x,y,x+y
and checks whether they constitute a linearity 0-certificate suffices to distinguish linear functions from functions which are far from being linear. A similar framework of sampling 0-certificates is at the heart of many other important property testing results such as low degree testing (see e.g.
[
AKK+03
,
JPRZ04
]
and many other works) and testing triangle-freeness (see e.g.
[
AFKS00
,
Alo02
]
and many other works). Of course, testing results of this sort rely on, and motivate the discovery of, structural results showing that functions which are far from having the property in question must have “many” “small” 0-certificates.

With this motivation, it is natural to study the size of sumset 0-certificates. Our sumset testing lower bound from
Section

4
suggests that there are sets which are far from being sumsets but which do not have “many” “small” sumset 0-certificates. In fact, known results imply that for every non-sumset the smallest 0-certificate is of size
\Omega(2^{n/2}/\sqrt{n})
:

Lemma 16
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be any non-sumset. Then any sumset
0
-certificate
D
for
S
must have
|D|\geq\Omega(2^{n/2}/\sqrt{n})
.

Proof.

This is an immediate corollary of a result due to Alon (Section 4 of
[
Alo07
]
), which shows that any subset
T\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
of size
|T|\geq 2^{n}-{\frac{1}{4000}}{\frac{2^{n/2}}{\sqrt{n}}}
is a sumset. It follows that if
|D|<{\frac{1}{4000}}{\frac{2^{n/2}}{\sqrt{n}}}
, then any 0/1 labeling of the points in
D
is consistent with a sumset (by labeling all points in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus D
as belonging to the set).
∎

The previous lemma, which establishes than any
0
-certificate for sumset testing must have size
\Omega(2^{n/2}/\sqrt{n})
, establishes Part (2) of
Theorem

3
. In the remainder of this section, we prove a matching upper bound (up to a factor of
\mathrm{poly}(n,1/\varepsilon)
) for any set perturbed by a small amount of random noise, thereby establishing Part (1) of
Theorem

3
:

Theorem 17
.

For any set
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and any
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
, there exists a sumset
0
-certificate for
\boldsymbol{N}_{\varepsilon}(S)=S\triangle\boldsymbol{R}_{\varepsilon}
of size
2^{n/2}\cdot O(n^{1.5}/\varepsilon^{1.5})
, with probability
1-o_{n}(1)
over the random draw of
\boldsymbol{R}_{\varepsilon}
.
Moreover, such a
0
-certificate can be found efficiently and non-adaptively (by querying
2^{n/2}\cdot O(n^{1.5}/\varepsilon^{1.5})
points) given oracle access to
\boldsymbol{N}_{\varepsilon}(S).

The proof of
Theorem

17
is given at the end of this section, after several preliminary lemmas. We first observe that for any sumset
S=A+A
, the set
A
is an independent set in the Cayley graph on
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with respect to every generator
s\not\in S
:

Lemma 18
.

Fix a sumset
A+A=S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and a set
D\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
with
S\cap D=\emptyset
. Then
A
is an independent set in
\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(D)
.

Proof.

Assume for contradiction that
A
is not an independent set in
\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(D)
. This implies the existence of
x,y\in A
with
x+y=s
for some
s\in D
. This implies
s\in D\cap(A+A)=D\cap S
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Lemma 19
.

Let
V\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a linear subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, and let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{n-\dim(V)}}
be a choice of representatives from all cosets of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus V
.
Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be an arbitrary subset and denote by
A_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=A\cap\left(V+x_{i}\right)
the intersection of
A
with the
i
-th coset of
V
. Then,

(A+A)\cap V=\bigcup_{i=1}^{2^{n-\dim(V)}}(A_{i}+A_{i}).

Proof.

Any element of
A_{i}+A_{i}
belongs to
(A+A)\cap V
, as any two elements of
A_{i}
are in the same coset of
V
by definition.

It remains to show that any element of
(A+A)\cap V
is contained in
A_{i}+A_{i}
for some
i\in[2^{n-\dim(V)}]
. Fix an element

y=a_{i}+a_{j}
for some
a_{i}\in A_{i}
and
a_{j}\in A_{j}
. Note that
y\in V
implies
A_{i}+A_{j}=V
, which in turn implies
x_{i}+x_{j}\in V
; as
x_{i},x_{j}
are coset representatives of
V
, this implies that
A_{i}=x_{i}+V=x_{j}+V=A_{j}
. We thus have that
y\in A_{i}+A_{i}
for some
i
.
∎

Lemma 20
.

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]

s\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
that
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}\left[s\notin\boldsymbol{N}_{\varepsilon}(S)\right]\geq\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Fix
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
,
\varepsilon\in(0,1/2]
, and

\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[s\notin\boldsymbol{N}_{\varepsilon}(S)]=\varepsilon

s\not\in S

\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[s\notin\boldsymbol{N}_{\varepsilon}(S)]=1-\varepsilon\geq\varepsilon

∎

Lemma 23

For any family

5.1
On
1
-certificates

In light of
Definition

15
, it is tempting to consider “sumset 1-certificates,” where informally, a sumset 1-certificate for a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is a set
D\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
such that querying the oracle
{\cal O}_{S}
on every point in
D
suffices to prove that
S

is
a sumset. More precisely, let us say that a a set
D\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is a
sumset 1-certificate for

S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
if
S^{\prime}
is a sumset for every set
S^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
which satisfies
S\cap D=S^{\prime}\cap D.
While this may seem like a natural definition, it turns out that sumset 1-certificates must always be extremely large:

Observation 24
.

If
D
is a sumset 1-certificate for a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, then
|D|\geq 2^{n-1}-O(n^{2})
.

Proof.

There are
2^{2^{n}-|D|}
distinct sets
S^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
that have
S^{\prime}\cap D=S\cap D,
and as shown in
Proposition

25
(see
Appendix

A
) there are at most
2^{2^{n-1}+O(n^{2})}
sumsets in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
. So if
D<2^{n-1}-O(n^{2})
, then there are too many possibilities for
S^{\prime}
for all of them to be sumsets.
∎

Appendix A
The Number of Sumsets in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}

Proposition 25
.

The number of sumsets in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is at most

2^{2^{n-1}+O(n^{2})}.

Proof.

Consider a sumset
S=A+A
; we consider two cases depending on the linear rank of the set
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus S
.

Case 1:
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus S
does not have full rank. In other words, there exists a vector
v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
such that

{\left\langle x,v\right\rangle}=1\qquad\text{implies that}\qquad x\notin S.

In particular, we have that
S\subseteq\{x\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}{\left\langle x,v\right\rangle}=1\}
. As there are at most
2^{n}
choices for
v
, and for each choice of
v
there are at most
2^{2^{n-1}}
choices for
S
, we have that there are at most
2^{2^{n-1}+n}
many sumsets of this form.

Case 2:
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\setminus S
has full rank. In particular, there are
n
linearly independent vectors
not
in
S
. For
v\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, observe that the Cayley graph
\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(v)
is a perfect matching. Next, note that if
v\notin S=A+A
, then
A
must be an independent set in
\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(v)
. This is because, if
x,y\in A
with
x+v=y
then

A+A\ni x+y=x+x+v=v\notin A+A,

which is a contradiction. As we have
n
linearly independent vectors not in
S
, it follows that there exists an orthogonal transformation of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
such that
A
must be an independent set in the hypercube (where edges are incident to elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
that differ in a single coordinate). As the number of independent sets in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is at most
2^{2^{n-1}+O(1)}
(see for example
[
Gal19
]
), and as the number of orthogonal transformations of the hypercube is at most
2^{n^{2}}
, it follows that the total number of sumsets of this form is at most

2^{2^{n-1}+n^{2}+O(1)}.

Both cases together complete the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Proof of
Lemma

21

Definition 26
.

We say that a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
has
many sums
if
|A+A|\geq\frac{1}{4}|A|^{2}
.

Lemma 27
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
be a non-empty set, then there exists
A^{\prime}\subseteq A
such that
|A^{\prime}|\geq\sqrt{|A|}
and
A^{\prime}
has many sums.

Proof.

We construct
A^{\prime}
iteratively in a greedy manner.
Starting from
A^{\prime}=\{a\}
for an arbitrary
a\in A
, as long as there is any
x\in A\setminus A^{\prime}
such that
\left(A^{\prime}+x\right)\setminus\left(A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(|A^{\prime}|+1\right)
then we add
x
to
A^{\prime}
.

Let
a\in A^{\prime}
.
We observe that
|A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}|\leq\frac{|A^{\prime}|\cdot\left(|A^{\prime}|-1\right)}{2}
, and thus for a uniformly chosen
\boldsymbol{x}\sim A
we have

\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[a+\boldsymbol{x}\in A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}]\leq\frac{|A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}|}{|A|}\leq\frac{|A^{\prime}|\cdot\left(|A^{\prime}|-1\right)}{2|A|}.

Hence, for a uniformly chosen
\boldsymbol{x}\sim U(A)
the expected number of elements
a\in A^{\prime}
for which
a+\boldsymbol{x}\in A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}
is at most
|A^{\prime}|\cdot\frac{|A^{\prime}|\cdot\left(|A^{\prime}|-1\right)}{2|A|}=\frac{|A^{\prime}|-1}{2}\cdot\frac{|A^{\prime}|^{2}}{|A|}
.
In particular, there exists such an outcome of
x\in A
.
If
|A^{\prime}|\leq\sqrt{|A|}
then
x+a\in A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}
for at most
\frac{|A^{\prime}|-1}{2}
elements
a\in A^{\prime}
. Each of the sums
(x+a)
, for all
a\in A^{\prime}
, is unique as
x
is fixed. Thus,
\left(A^{\prime}+x\right)\setminus\left(A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(|A^{\prime}|+1\right)
.
We conclude that the greedy process would not halt before
A^{\prime}
is of size at least
\sqrt{|A|}
.

Finally, note that by definition of the greedy process we have

|A^{\prime}+A^{\prime}|\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(1+2+\ldots+|A^{\prime}|\right)\geq\frac{1}{4}|A^{\prime}|^{2}.

∎

The probability that
\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(\boldsymbol{R}_{\varepsilon})

Denote by

.
The number of sets in
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
of size

is
{2^{n}\choose k}\leq 2^{nk}
, and in particular this is an upper bound for the number of such sets that also have many sums.
Thus, the expected number of sets

of size
k
with many sums that are independent sets in
\Gamma_{\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}(\boldsymbol{R}_{\varepsilon})
, by union bound, is at most

2^{nk}\cdot(1-\varepsilon)^{\frac{1}{4}k^{2}}=2^{k\left(n+\frac{1}{4}\log\left(1-\varepsilon\right)k\right)}<2^{k\left(n-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}k\right)}<2^{\frac{5n}{\varepsilon}\cdot\left(-\frac{n}{4}\right)}=2^{-\frac{5}{4\varepsilon}n^{2}}.

∎

Proof of
