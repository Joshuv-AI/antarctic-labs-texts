Lipschitz Continuous Allocations for Optimization Games
Soh Kumabe, Yuichi Yoshida

Abstract
In cooperative game theory, the primary focus is the equitable allocation of payoffs or costs among agents. However, in the practical applications of cooperative games, accurately representing games is challenging. In such cases, using an allocation method sensitive to small perturbations in the game can lead to various problems, including dissatisfaction among agents and the potential for manipulation by agents seeking to maximize their own benefits. Therefore, the allocation method must be robust against game perturbations. In this study, we explore optimization games, in which the value of the characteristic function is provided as the optimal value of an optimization problem. To assess the robustness of the allocation methods, we use the Lipschitz constant, which quantifies the extent of change in the allocation vector in response to a unit perturbation in the weight vector of the underlying problem. Thereafter, we provide an algorithm for the matching game that returns an allocation belonging to the ( 1 2 − ϵ ) \left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right) -approximate core with Lipschitz constant O ⁡ ( ϵ − 1 ) O(\epsilon^{-1}) . Additionally, we provide an algorithm for a minimum spanning tree game that returns an allocation belonging to the 4 4 -approximate core with a constant Lipschitz constant. The Shapley value is a popular allocation that satisfies several desirable properties. Therefore, we investigate the robustness of the Shapley value. We demonstrate that the Lipschitz constant of the Shapley value for the minimum spanning tree is constant, whereas that for the matching game is Ω ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \Omega(\log n) , where n n denotes the number of vertices.



1
Introduction

1.1
Background and Motivation

Cooperative games model decision-making scenarios in which multiple agents can achieve greater benefits through cooperation. A primary concern in cooperative game theory is the allocation of payoffs or costs provided by the grand coalition in an acceptable manner to each agent. Among the cooperative games, those defined by optimization problems corresponding to the set of agents involved are known as
optimization games

[
8
]
.

Consider the following well-known examples formulated by the matching game (MG)
[
9
,
3
]
and the minimum spanning tree game (MSTG)
[
6
]
:

MG:

Members of a tennis club form pairs for a doubles tournament. Each pair of players has a predicted value for the prize money they would win if they teamed up. How should the total prize money won by all pairs be distributed among members?

MSTG:

Multiple facilities cooperate to construct a power grid to receive electricity from a power plant. Each potential power line has a predetermined installation cost. When constructing a power grid that ensures electricity distribution to all facilities, what cost should each facility bear?

In these examples, the characteristic function of the game often contains errors, or can be manipulated through deliberate misreporting. For instance,

MG:

It is challenging to accurately predict the compatibility of pairs who have never teamed up before. Moreover, pairs known to work well may hide this fact.

MSTG:

Costs for power line installation might be misestimated owing to unforeseen terrain or geological conditions caused by natural disasters or inadequate surveys, land rights, or landscape regulations. Facilities may conceal these issues.

In such uncertain situations, allocations that drastically change with slight perturbations in the game can lead to problems, such as

MG:

If minor estimation errors in predicted prize money significantly change the benefits for each player, players might not be satisfied with the allocation. In addition, when someone falsely reports a substantial increase in their gain, it becomes difficult to prove the intent of the manipulation if the degree of falsehood is small.

MSTG:

Facilities might not accept the allocation if trivial issues in power line construction significantly increase their cost burden. Moreover, if such issues are used to significantly reduce their own costs or increase those of competing facilities, several minor construction issues may be concealed, leading to risk management problems.

To avoid these issues, it is desirable to use allocations that are robust against perturbations in real-world cooperative games.

Before proceeding, we define some terms in cooperative game theory. The
cooperative game

(V,\nu)
is defined as a pair consisting of a set of
agents

V
and a
characteristic function

\nu\colon 2^{V}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
representing the payoff or cost obtained when subsets of agents cooperate.
An
optimization game
is defined by the optimization problem
\mathcal{P}
in a discrete structure. The
\mathcal{P}
-game

(G,w)
is defined from a pair of structures
G
consisting of
agent set

V
,
edge set

E
, and a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
. In several games,
G
is a graph
(V,E)
.
For each subset
S\subseteq V
, the characteristic function value
\nu(S)
is defined as the optimum value of
\mathcal{P}
on the substructure corresponding to
S
(e.g., the subgraph induced by
S
) with respect to weight vector
w
.
An optimization game is a
welfare allocation game
(resp.,
cost allocation game
) if each agent intends to maximize (resp., minimize) the value allocated to it.

Now, we formally introduce matching games and minimum spanning tree games, as discussed in previous examples.

Definition 1.1

(Matching game
[
3
,
8
]
)
.

Let

be an undirected graph. For a vertex set
S\subseteq V

of

(V,\nu)
, where
\nu(S)=\mathrm{OPT}(S,w)
.
The matching game is a welfare-allocation game.

Definition 1.2

(Minimum spanning tree game
[
4
,
6
]
)
.

Let
G=(V\cup\{r\},E)
be an undirected graph. For a vertex set
S\subseteq V
and edge weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
, let
\mathrm{OPT}(S,w)
denote the minimum weight of a spanning tree in
G[S\cup\{r\}]
with respect to weight

.
Notably, vertex

To ensure that the characteristic function has finite values, we assume that edge
(r,v)
exists for all

.
The
minimum spanning tree game
of

is defined as
(V,\nu)
, where
\nu(S)=\mathrm{OPT}(S,w)
.
The minimum spanning tree game is a cost-allocation game.

Let us return to the discussion of the robustness of the allocation.
To measure the robustness of the allocation, we introduce the concept of
Lipschitz continuity
in the allocation of these games, analogous to the introduction of Kumabe and Yoshida
[
23
]
for discrete optimization problems under the same name.
Algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that takes a weight vector
w\in\mathbb{R}^{E}
and returns an allocation
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{V}
is
L
-Lipschitz
or has
Lipschitz constant

L
if:

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}\\
w\neq w^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\frac{\|\mathcal{A}(w)-\mathcal{A}(w^{\prime})\|_{1}}{\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{1}}\leq L.

(1)

If the Lipschitz constant of the allocation method is small, the change in the allocation in response to a unit change in the weight vector is guaranteed to be small. Employing such an allocation method can resolve these problems as follows:

MG:

Minor mistakes in estimating the compatibility of pairs will not significantly affect the overall distribution of prize money, making it easier for agents to accept the allocation. Additionally, substantial misreporting is necessary to increase benefits significantly through declaration adjustments, making schemes more likely to be exposed.

MSTG:

Minor issues related to the installation of power lines will not cause significant fluctuations in the cost burden for each facility, making it easier for them to accept their share of the costs. Additionally, because the loss incurred by reporting such issues is small, the likelihood of these issues being concealed is reduced.

1.1.1
The core.

The
core
is the most fundamental solution concept in cooperative game theory. An allocation vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
is in the core of (welfare allocation) cooperative game
(V,\nu)
if:

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in S}x_{v}\geq\nu(S)\quad(S\subsetneq V),\quad\sum_{v\in V}x_{v}=\nu(V).

(2)

Similarly, an allocation vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
is in the core of (cost allocation) cooperative game
(V,\nu)
if:

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in S}x_{v}\leq\nu(S)\quad(S\subsetneq V),\quad\sum_{v\in V}x_{v}=\nu(V).

(3)

Because the core is one of the most fundamental solution concepts, it is natural to desire a Lipschitz continuous algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that takes a weight vector as the input and returns an allocation belonging to the core. However, this can only be achieved in a limited number of situations for two reasons. First, a non-empty core does not necessarily exist in all games. Second, even for games in which the core exists in all instances, there is no guarantee that a vector from the core can be selected such that the Lipschitz constant of
\mathcal{A}
remains small.

To examine the second reason, we consider the
assignment game
introduced by Shapley and Shubik
[
29
]
, which is a special case of the matching game in which the underlying graph is bipartite and known to have a non-empty core
[
8
,
29
]
.

Example 1.3
.

Let
n
be an odd number greater than or equal to
5
. Let us consider a path
G=(V,E)
consisting of
n
vertices labeled sequentially as
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
. The weight vectors
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle w_{(v_{i},v_{i+1})}=1\quad(i=1,\dots,n-1),\qquad w^{\prime}_{(v_{i},v_{i+1})}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }2\leq i\leq n-2\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

In this case, the allocations
x,x^{\prime}
belonging to the core of the assignment game for
w
and
w^{\prime}
are both unique and obtained as follows:

\displaystyle x_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $i$ is even}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad x^{\prime}_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $i$ is odd and $i\not\in\{1,n\}$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

If
\mathcal{A}
is an algorithm that takes a weight vector and returns a vector in the core of the game, then it must satisfy:

\displaystyle\frac{\|\mathcal{A}(w)-\mathcal{A}(w^{\prime})\|_{1}}{\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{1}}=\frac{n-2}{2}=\Omega(n)

Thus, algorithm
\mathcal{A}
must have a large Lipschitz constant,
\Omega(n)
.

Therefore, we consider providing a Lipschitz continuous algorithm that outputs allocations that satisfy some looser solution concepts. The least core
[
28
]
is one such solution concept that can be defined even for games in which a core does not exist. However, this does not resolve the problem of a large Lipschitz constant (for instance, in the game in Example
1.3
, the core and the least core coincide, resulting in a Lipschitz constant of
\Omega(n)
). Instead, we consider the approximate core, which is a solution concept that multiplicatively relaxes the constraints of partial coalitions.

1.1.2
Approximate Core.

The approximate core was introduced by Faigle and Kern
[
11
]
as a useful solution concept for games where the core is empty. Intuitively, the approximate core represents the core when the constraints for partial coalitions are relaxed by a factor
\alpha
.
For
\alpha\leq 1
, the allocation vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
is in the
\alpha
-(approximate) core
of the (welfare allocation) cooperative game
(V,\nu)
if:

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in S}x_{v}\geq\alpha\nu(S)\quad(S\subsetneq V),\quad\sum_{v\in V}x_{v}=\nu(V).

Similarly, for
\alpha\geq 1
, the allocation vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{V}
is in the
\alpha
-(approximate) core
of the (cost allocation) cooperative game
(V,\nu)
if:

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in S}x_{v}\leq\alpha\nu(S)\quad(S\subsetneq V),\quad\sum_{v\in V}x_{v}=\nu(V).

In this study, we provide algorithms with small Lipschitz constants that return an
\alpha
-approximate core for some constant
\alpha
in several optimization games.
For the matching game, we obtain the following:

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\epsilon\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
. For the matching game, there is a polynomial-time algorithm with a Lipschitz constant
O(\epsilon^{-1})
that returns
\left(\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon\right)
-approximate core allocation.

Note that Faigle and Kern
[
11
]
and Vazirani
[
32
]
showed that the
\frac{2}{3}
-approximate core of the matching game is non-empty, and that this is also a tight bound. However, their allocation, which is constructed using the optimal solution of the matching LP, is not Lipschitz continuous. Our allocation compromises the core approximability by
\frac{1}{6}+\epsilon
to ensure Lipschitz continuity.

Our allocation was inspired by the Lipschitz continuous algorithm for the maximum weight-matching problem proposed by Kumabe and Yoshida
[
23
]
. Similarly, the proposed algorithm is based on the greedy method. We emphasize our results because their algorithm reduces the approximation ratio to
\frac{1}{8}-\epsilon
to obtain a Lipschitz constant of
O(\epsilon^{-1})
. Although the definitions of Lipschitz continuity and the approximation ratio for discrete algorithms differ from those for allocations, making a simple comparison infeasible, our analysis is simpler and offers better approximation guarantees.

For the minimum spanning tree game, we have the following.

Theorem 1.5
.

For the minimum spanning tree game, there is a polynomial-time algorithm with Lipschitz constant
O(1)
that returns
4
-approximate core allocation.

The core of the minimum spanning tree game is non-empty; an allocation by Bird
[
4
]
is known to belong to the core and can be computed in polynomial time. However, it is not Lipschitz continuous. As in the matching game, our allocation compromises the core approximability to ensure Lipschitz continuity.

The original motivation for the approximate core was to provide useful solution concepts for games with non-empty cores. Therefore, no studies have been conducted on the approximate core of the minimum spanning tree game. In this regard, our setting demonstrates the usefulness of considering an approximate core, even for games with a non-empty core.

1.1.3
Shapley Value.

Let
\mathfrak{S}_{V}
be the set of all permutations over
V
.
For
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}
and
v\in V
, let
x_{\sigma,v}=\nu(\{\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(k)\})-\nu(\{\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(k-1)\})
, where
k
is the integer with
\sigma(k)=v
.
The
Shapley value

[
27
]
of game
(V,\nu)
is the vector
s
defined by:

\displaystyle s_{v}=\frac{1}{|V|!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}}x_{\sigma,v}.

The Shapley value does not necessarily belong to the core, even if it exists, and the computation is #
\mathsf{P}
-hard in most optimization games. However, they exhibit various desirable properties and have a wide range of applications
[
16
]
. Therefore, investigating the Lipschitz continuity of Shapley values is a natural task.

Considering its various properties, it is natural to expect the Shapley value to always have a small Lipschitz constant for general optimization games. Conversely, given the difficulty in computing it, it is natural to anticipate that it may not have a bounded Lipschitz constant. However, neither was accurate. Specifically, we demonstrate that whether the Shapley value in optimization games has a small Lipschitz constant depends on the game.

Theorem 1.6
.

There is a graph
G
such that the Shapley value of the matching game on
G
has a Lipschitz constant
\Omega(\log n)
.

Theorem 1.7
.

The Shapley value of the minimum spanning tree game has a Lipschitz constant of
2
.

Notably, the computation of the Shapley value for the minimum spanning tree game is #
\mathsf{P}
-hard
[
1
]
. The result of Theorem
1.7
is particularly interesting because it shows a value that is computationally difficult to calculate may still have a small Lipschitz constant.

Let

\mathcal{A}

Matching Game

In this section, we prove Theorem

We obtain the proof by constructing an algorithm that satisfies the assumptions in Lemma

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\textsc{MatchingGame}(G,w,b,\alpha)\mathrm{d}b

3.1
Core Approximability

The proofs of the following two lemmas for the core approximability analysis of Algorithm

are relatively straightforward.

Lemma 3.1
.

We have
\|z\|_{1}\leq 2\alpha\mathrm{OPT}(V,w)
.

Proof.

Because
M
is a matching of

, we have
\sum_{e\in M}w_{e}\leq\mathrm{OPT}(V,w)
. Because the modified weight

of each edge

in

contributes twice to

, we obtain

\|z\|_{1}=2\sum_{e\in M}\widehat{w}_{e}\leq 2\alpha\sum_{e\in M}w_{e}\leq 2\alpha\mathrm{OPT}(V,w).\qed

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
S\subseteq V
. Then, we have
\sum_{v\in S}z_{v}\geq\mathrm{OPT}(S,w)
.

Proof.

Let
e=(u,v)\in E
.
When edge

begins to be examined in the loop starting from Line
1
, if at least one of
u
or
v
(say,
u
) is already covered by
M
, then we have
\widehat{w}_{e}\leq z_{u}
. If neither
u
nor
v
are covered, then edge
e
is added to
M
, resulting in
\widehat{w}_{e}=z_{u}=z_{v}
.
Therefore,
\widehat{w}_{e}\leq\max\{z_{u},z_{v}\}\leq z_{u}+z_{v}
.
Let

be the maximum matching of

. Then, we have

\sum_{v\in S}z_{v}\geq\sum_{(u,v)\in M^{\prime}}(z_{u}+z_{v})\geq\sum_{e\in M^{\prime}}\widehat{w}_{e}\geq\sum_{e\in M^{\prime}}w_{e}=\mathrm{OPT}(S,w).\qed

3.2
Lipschitz Continuity

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph,
f\in E
,

We will bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\textsc{MatchingGame}(G,w,b,\alpha)-\textsc{MatchingGame}(G,w+\delta\bm{1}_{f},b,\alpha)\right\|_{1}\mathrm{d}b,

(6)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}\left\|\int_{0}^{1}\textsc{MatchingGame}(G,w,b,\alpha)\mathrm{d}b-\int_{0}^{1}\textsc{MatchingGame}(G,w+\delta\bm{1}_{f},b,\alpha)\mathrm{d}b\right\|_{1}.

From Lemma

or

is sufficient to prove Lipschitz continuity.
We denote the value of
\widehat{w}
,
M
, and

in
MatchingGame
(
G,w,b,\alpha
) (resp.,
MatchingGame
(
G,w+\delta\bm{1}_{f},b,\alpha
)) as
\widehat{w}
,
M
, and

(resp.,
\widehat{w}^{\prime}
,

, and

).

When
\widehat{w}_{f}=\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}
,
MatchingGame
(
G,w,b,\alpha
) and
MatchingGame
(
G,w+\delta\bm{1}_{f},b,\alpha

In
MatchingGame
(
G,w,b
), edge
e_{1}
coming

refers to

being considered before
e_{2}
in the loop starting from Line

In other words, either
\widehat{w}_{e_{1}}>\widehat{w}_{e_{2}}
or
\widehat{w}_{e_{1}}=\widehat{w}_{e_{2}}
and the index of

The following lemma forms the core of our Lipschitzness analysis:

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume
\widehat{w}_{f}\neq\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}
. Then, we have
\|z-z^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq 2\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}
.

Proof.

For each edge
e\neq f
such that
e\in M^{\prime}\setminus M
(resp.,
e\in M\setminus M^{\prime}
), edge
g
is a
witness
of
e
if it is adjacent to
e
in
M
(resp.,
M^{\prime}
) and
g\prec_{\widehat{w}}e
(resp.,
g\prec_{\widehat{w}^{\prime}}e
). Intuitively, a witness of
e
is the edge that directly causes
e
to be excluded from
M
or
M^{\prime}
.

From this definition, the witness of
e\in M^{\prime}\setminus M
belongs to
M\setminus M^{\prime}
and vice versa. Because
\prec
is an ordering on
E\setminus\{f\}
, by tracing the witnesses from any edge in
M\triangle M^{\prime}
, we will consequently arrive at
f
. This implies that as long as
M\neq M^{\prime}
, the edges in
M\triangle M^{\prime}
form a single path or cycle including
f
.
Moreover, in this case, we have
f\in M^{\prime}\setminus M
, because if we can add
f
to
M
in
MatchingGame
(
G,w,b,\alpha
), we could also add
f
to
M^{\prime}
in
MatchingGame
(
G,w+\delta\bm{1}_{f},b,\alpha
).

Let us now complete the proof. When
M=M^{\prime}
, we have
\|z-z^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq 2(\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}-\widehat{w}_{f})\leq 2\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}
.
Otherwise, we let
f=(u_{0},v_{0})
. Then, there exists a unique maximal sequence of vertices
(u_{0},\dots,u_{k})
such that
(u_{i},u_{i+1})\in M\triangle M^{\prime}
for all
i
and
f\prec_{\widehat{w}^{\prime}}(u_{0},u_{1})\prec(u_{1},u_{2})\prec\cdots\prec(u_{k-1},u_{k})
, and a unique maximal sequence of vertices
(v_{0},\dots,v_{l})
such that
(v_{i},v_{i+1})\in M\triangle M^{\prime}
for all
i
and
f\prec_{\widehat{w}^{\prime}}(v_{0},v_{1})\prec(v_{1},v_{2})\prec\cdots\prec(v_{l-1},v_{l})
(if
M\triangle M^{\prime}
forms a cycle, then
(u_{k-1},u_{k})=(v_{l},v_{l-1})
). Now, we have

\displaystyle\|z-z^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k}|z_{u_{i}}-z^{\prime}_{u_{i}}|+\sum_{i=0}^{l}|z_{v_{i}}-z^{\prime}_{v_{i}}|

\displaystyle=\left(|\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}-\widehat{w}_{(u_{0},u_{1})}|+\sum_{i=1}^{k}|\widehat{w}_{(u_{i-1},u_{i})}-\widehat{w}_{(u_{i},u_{i+1})}|\right)+\left(|\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}-\widehat{w}_{(v_{0},v_{1})}|+\sum_{i=1}^{l}|\widehat{w}_{(v_{i-1},v_{i})}-\widehat{w}_{(v_{i},v_{i+1})}|\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f},

where the last inequality is from the fact that the sequences defined by
\left(\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f},\widehat{w}_{(u_{0},u_{1})},\dots,\widehat{w}_{(u_{k-1},u_{k})}\right)
and
\left(\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f},\widehat{w}_{(v_{0},v_{1})},\dots,\widehat{w}_{(v_{l-1},v_{l})}\right)
are both decreasing.
∎

The following lemma analyzes the probability that
\widehat{w}_{f}\neq\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}
happens.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
b
is sampled uniformly from
[0,1]
,
\widehat{w}_{f}\neq\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}
happens with a probability of at most
\frac{\delta}{w_{f}\ln\alpha}
.

Proof.

\widehat{w}_{f}\neq\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}
happens when there exists an integer
i
with
w_{f}<\alpha^{i+b}\leq w_{f}+\delta
, indicating that
\left\lfloor{\log_{\alpha}w_{f}-b}\right\rfloor\neq\left\lfloor{\log_{\alpha}w_{f}+\delta-b}\right\rfloor
. This happens with probability

\log_{\alpha}(w_{f}+\delta)-\log_{\alpha}w_{f}=\log_{\alpha}\left(1+\frac{\delta}{w_{f}}\right)\leq\frac{1}{\ln\alpha}\cdot\frac{\delta}{w_{f}}=\frac{\delta}{w_{f}\ln\alpha}.\qed

Now, we complete our Lipschitzness analysis.

Lemma 3.5
.

We have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\textsc{MatchingGame}(G,w,b,\alpha)-\textsc{MatchingGame}(G,w+\delta\bm{1}_{f},b,\alpha)\right\|_{1}\mathrm{d}b\leq\frac{12}{\alpha-1}\delta.

Proof.

If
w_{f}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\textsc{MatchingGame}(G,w,b,\alpha)-\textsc{MatchingGame}(G,w+\delta\bm{1}_{f},b,\alpha)\right\|_{1}\mathrm{d}b

\displaystyle\leq 2\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}\leq 2\alpha(w_{f}+\delta)=2\alpha\delta\leq\frac{12}{\alpha-1}\delta,

where the last inequality is from
\alpha\leq 2
.
Otherwise, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\textsc{MatchingGame}(G,w,b,\alpha)-\textsc{MatchingGame}(G,w+\delta\bm{1}_{f},b,\alpha)\right\|_{1}\mathrm{d}b

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{w_{f}\ln\alpha}\cdot 2\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}\leq\frac{\delta}{w_{f}\ln\alpha}\cdot 2\alpha(w_{f}+\delta)\leq\frac{\delta}{w_{f}\ln\alpha}\cdot 4\alpha w_{f}=\frac{4\alpha\delta}{\ln\alpha}\leq\frac{12}{\alpha-1}\delta,

where the third inequality is from
\delta\leq w_{f}
and the last inequality is from the fact that
\frac{\alpha}{\ln\alpha}\leq\frac{3}{\alpha-1}
holds for
\alpha\in(1,2]
.
∎

4

In this section, we prove Theorem

The proof is obtained by constructing an algorithm that satisfies the assumption of Lemma
2.3
. Specifically, we prove that an algorithm that returns a vector represented by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\textsc{MSTGame}(G,w,b)\mathrm{d}b

satisfies the assumptions of Lemma

, where the procedure

To derive our allocation, we use an

that simulates Kruskal’s algorithm, constructed as in Algorithm

V\cup\{r\}
.
We provide an overview of Algorithm

and sets its

. The auxiliary tree is constructed by adding the edges of

in ascending order of weight to graph

into a single connected component

u_{C}
corresponding to
C
in the auxiliary tree. The height of

4.1
Core Approximability

We begin by analyzing the core approximability.
Let
T
be the auxiliary tree for
(G,\widehat{w})
.
For
X\subseteq V\cup\{r\}
, the

of

is the minimal connected subgraph of
T
that contains all leaves of
T
corresponding to
X
.
The following lemma bounds the value of the characteristic function for a subset
S
of
V
using values that can be computed from connector
\mathrm{conn}(S\cup\{r\})
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
S\subseteq V
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u,u^{\prime})\in E(\mathrm{conn}(S\cup\{r\}))\\
r\not\in T_{u^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}(h_{u}-h_{u^{\prime}})\leq\mathrm{OPT}(G[S\cup\{r\}],\widehat{w}).

When
V=S
, the equality holds.

Proof.

Let
R
be the minimum spanning tree of
G[S\cup\{r\}]
with respect to weight
\widehat{w}
.
For
x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, let
N_{<x}=\left|\{C\in\mathcal{C}_{\widehat{w},<x}\colon C\cap(S\cup\{r\})\neq\emptyset\}\right|
and
N_{\leq x}=\left|\{C\in\mathcal{C}_{\widehat{w},\leq x}\colon C\cap(S\cup\{r\})\neq\emptyset\}\right|
. Then,
T
contains at least
N_{<x}-1
edges
e
with
\widehat{w}_{e}\geq x
.
Specifically, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E(R)}\widehat{w}_{e}\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\\
\mathcal{C}_{\widehat{w},\leq x}\neq\mathcal{C}_{\widehat{w},<x}\end{subarray}}(N_{\leq x}-N_{<x})x=\sum_{u\in V(\mathrm{conn}(S\cup\{r\}))}(d_{\mathrm{conn}(S\cup\{r\})}(u)-1)h_{u},

where
d_{\mathrm{conn}(S\cup\{r\})}(u)
denotes the number of children of
u
in
\mathrm{conn}(S\cup\{r\})
. We observe that the equality holds for the first inequality for
V=S
by recalling Kruskal’s algorithm.
Let us prove

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in V(\mathrm{conn}(S\cup\{r\}))}(d_{\mathrm{conn}(S\cup\{r\})}(u)-1)h_{u}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u,u^{\prime})\in E(\mathrm{conn}(S\cup\{r\}))\\
r\not\in T_{u^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}(h_{u}-h_{u^{\prime}}).

to complete the proof. Because we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u,u^{\prime})\in E(\mathrm{conn}(S\cup\{r\}))\\
r\in T_{u^{\prime}}\end{subarray}}(h_{u}-h_{u^{\prime}})=h_{\mathrm{lca}(S\cup\{r\})},

where
\mathrm{lca}(C)
denotes the lowest common ancestor of
C
in the auxiliary tree, it is suffice to show

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in V(\mathrm{conn}(C))}(d_{\mathrm{conn}(C)}(u)-1)h_{u}=\sum_{(u,u^{\prime})\in E(\mathrm{conn}(C))}(h_{u}-h_{u^{\prime}})-h_{\mathrm{lca}(C)}

holds for all
C\subseteq V\cup\{r\}
.
We prove this by induction.
For
|C|=1
, both the left- and the right-hand sides are equal to zero. For
|C|>1
, let
u_{C_{1}},\dots,u_{C_{d_{\mathrm{conn}(C)}}}
be children of
\mathrm{lca}(C)
in
\mathrm{conn}(C)
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{u\in V(\mathrm{conn}(C))}(d_{\mathrm{conn}(C)}(u)-1)h_{u}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\mathrm{conn}(C)}}\sum_{u\in C_{i}}(d_{\mathrm{conn}(C_{i})}(u)-1)h_{u}+(d_{\mathrm{conn}(C)}-1)h_{\mathrm{lca}(C)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\mathrm{conn}(C)}}\left(\sum_{(u,u^{\prime})\in E(\mathrm{conn}(C_{i}))}(h_{u}-h_{u^{\prime}})-h_{u_{C_{i}}}\right)+(d_{\mathrm{conn}(C)}-1)h_{\mathrm{lca}(C)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d_{\mathrm{conn}(C)}}\left(\sum_{(u,u^{\prime})\in E(\mathrm{conn}(C_{i}))}(h_{u}-h_{u^{\prime}})+(h_{\mathrm{lca}(C)}-h_{u_{C_{i}}})\right)-h_{\mathrm{lca}(C)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(u,u^{\prime})\in E(\mathrm{conn}(C))}(h_{u}-h_{u^{\prime}})-h_{\mathrm{lca}(C)},

and the induction hypothesis is proved. Therefore, the lemma holds.
∎

Now, we have the following.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
S\subseteq V
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in S}\sum_{e\in E(T)}z_{e,v}\leq 4\mathrm{OPT}(G[S\cup\{r\}],w).

and

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in S}\sum_{e\in E(T)}z_{e,v}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E(\mathrm{conn}(S\cup\{r\}))\\
r\not\in T_{e}\end{subarray}}\sum_{v\in S}z_{e,v}\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E(\mathrm{conn}(S\cup\{r\}))\\
r\not\in T_{e}\end{subarray}}\sum_{v\in V}z_{e,v}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e\in E(\mathrm{conn}(S\cup\{r\}))\\
r\not\in T_{e}\end{subarray}}h_{e}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(u,u^{\prime})\in E(\mathrm{conn}(S\cup\{r\}))\\
r\not\in T_{e}\end{subarray}}2(h_{u}-h_{u^{\prime}})\leq 2\mathrm{OPT}(G[S\cup\{r\}],\widehat{w})

\displaystyle\leq 4\mathrm{OPT}(G[S\cup\{r\}],w),

where the second inequality is from

that follows from the fact that each
\widehat{w}_{e}
is rounded to a value proportional to the power of

and the third inequality is from Lemma

.
∎

We have the following bound for the grand coalition.

Lemma 4.3
.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{e\in E(T)}z_{e,v}\geq\mathrm{OPT}(G,w).

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}\sum_{e\in E(T)}z_{e,v}=\sum_{e\in E(T)}h_{e}\geq\sum_{(u,u^{\prime})\in E(T)}(h_{u}-h_{u^{\prime}})=\mathrm{OPT}(G,\widehat{w})\geq\mathrm{OPT}(G,w),

where the second equality is obtained from Lemma

.

4.2
Lipschitz Continuity

Let

. We bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\textsc{MSTGame}(G,w,b)-\textsc{MSTGame}(G,w+\delta\bm{1}_{f},b)\right\|_{1}\mathrm{d}b,

which is an upper bound on

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}\left\|\int_{0}^{1}\textsc{MSTGame}(G,w,b)\mathrm{d}b-\int_{0}^{1}\textsc{MSTGame}(G,w+\delta\bm{1}_{f},b)\mathrm{d}b\right\|_{1}.

Without loss of generality, we can assume
\delta\leq w_{f}
or
w_{f}=0
.
Now we fix

. We denote the value of

,

, and

(

.
Otherwise, let

be two connected components of

by the following operation:

(1.2)

Do exactly the same for

.

(2)

If

Lemma 4.4
.

Assume
\widehat{w}_{f}\neq\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\textsc{MSTGame}(G,w,b)-\textsc{MSTGame}(G,w+\delta\bm{1}_{f},b)\right\|_{1}\leq\widehat{w}_{f}+2\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle z_{1}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|C_{1}|}\bm{1}_{C_{1}}&\text{if $(u_{C},u_{C_{1}})$ is deleted in (1.1) and $r\not\in C_{1}$}\\
\bm{0}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

\displaystyle z_{2}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|C_{2}|}\bm{1}_{C_{2}}&\text{if $(u_{C},u_{C_{2}})$ is deleted in (1.2) and $r\not\in C_{2}$}\\
\bm{0}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

\displaystyle z_{3}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|C|}\bm{1}_{C}&\text{if $(u_{Y},u_{C})$ is deleted in (1.2) and $r\not\in C$}\\
\bm{0}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

\displaystyle z_{4}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|C_{1}|}\bm{1}_{C_{1}}&\text{if $r\not\in C_{1}$}\\
\bm{0}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

\displaystyle z_{5}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{|C_{2}|}\bm{1}_{C_{2}}&\text{if $r\not\in C_{2}$}\\
\bm{0}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\textsc{MSTGame}(G,w,b)-\textsc{MSTGame}(G,w+\delta\bm{1}_{f},b)\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(z_{1}+z_{2}\right)\widehat{w}_{f}+\left(z_{3}-z_{4}-z_{5}\right)\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|\left(z_{1}\widehat{w}_{f}+\left(z_{3}\circ\bm{1}_{C_{1}}-z_{4}\right)\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}\right)\right\|_{1}+\left\|\left(z_{2}\widehat{w}_{f}+\left(z_{3}\circ\bm{1}_{C_{2}}-z_{5}\right)\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}\right)\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left(\widehat{w}_{f}+\frac{|C_{1}|}{|C|}\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f},\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}\right)+\max\left(\widehat{w}_{f}+\frac{|C_{2}|}{|C|}\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f},\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}\right)\leq\widehat{w}_{f}+2\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}.\qed

The following lemma analyzes the probability that
\widehat{w}_{f}\neq\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}
happens.

Lemma 4.5
.

Assume
w^{\prime}_{f}\leq 2w_{f}
. If
b
is sampled uniformly from
[0,1]
,
\widehat{w}_{f}\neq\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}
happens with a probability of at most
\frac{\delta}{w_{f}\log 2}
.

Proof.

\widehat{w}_{f}\neq\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}
happens when there is an integer
i
with
w_{f}<2^{i+b}\leq w_{f}+\delta
, implying that
\left\lfloor{\log_{2}w_{f}-b}\right\rfloor\neq\left\lfloor{\log_{2}w_{f}+\delta-b}\right\rfloor
. This happens with probability

\log_{2}(w_{f}+\delta)-\log_{2}w_{f}=\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\delta}{w_{f}}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{w_{f}\log 2}.\qed

Now, we have the following:

Lemma 4.6
.

We have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\textsc{MSTGame}(G,w,b)-\textsc{MSTGame}(G,w+\delta\bm{1}_{f},b)\right\|_{1}\mathrm{d}b\leq\frac{10\delta}{\log 2}.

Proof.

If
w_{f}=0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\textsc{MSTGame}(G,w,b)-\textsc{MSTGame}(G,w+\delta\bm{1}_{f},b)\right\|_{1}\mathrm{d}b\leq 2\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}\leq 4w^{\prime}_{f}=4\delta.

Otherwise, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\textsc{MSTGame}(G,w,b)-\textsc{MSTGame}(G,w+\delta\bm{1}_{f},b)\right\|_{1}\mathrm{d}b

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{w_{f}\log 2}\left(\widehat{w}_{f}+2\widehat{w}^{\prime}_{f}\right)=\frac{\delta}{w_{f}\log 2}\cdot 5\widehat{w}_{f}\leq\frac{\delta}{w_{f}\log 2}\cdot 10w_{f}=\frac{10\delta}{\log 2}.

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5

Combining Lemmas
4.2
,
4.3
, and
4.6
and applying Lemma
2.3
yields Theorem
1.5
.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Combining Lemmas

,
4.3
,
4.6
, and
2.3

5
Shapley Value

In this section, we discuss the Lipschitz continuity of the Shapley values of the optimization games and prove Theorems
1.6
and
1.7
.

5.1
Matching Game

Let
n
be an odd integer greater than or equal to
5
. Consider a path
G=(V,E)
consisting of
n
vertices labeled sequentially as
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
.
Let
\mathrm{Shap}_{\mathrm{Match}}(w)
be the Shapley value for the matching game with respect to weight
w
.
The weight vectors
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{E}
are defined as

\displaystyle w_{(v_{i},v_{i+1})}

\displaystyle=1\quad(i=1,\dots,n-1),

\displaystyle w^{\prime}_{(v_{i},v_{i+1})}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1+\delta&\text{if }i=2\\
1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

For this instance, we prove

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{Shap}_{\mathrm{Match}}(w)-\mathrm{Shap}_{\mathrm{Match}}(w^{\prime})\|\geq\Omega(\delta\log n),

which directly implies Theorem
1.6
.

For
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}
and
v\in V
such that
\sigma(k)=v
, let

\displaystyle x_{\sigma,v}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{OPT}(\{\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(k)\},w)-\mathrm{OPT}(\{\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(k-1)\},w),

\displaystyle x^{\prime}_{\sigma,v}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{OPT}(\{\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(k)\},w^{\prime})-\mathrm{OPT}(\{\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(k-1)\},w^{\prime}).

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
i\geq 4
be an even number. If
\sigma
is uniformly sampled from
\mathfrak{S}_{V}
, then we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\mathbf{E}}\left[x_{\sigma,v_{i}}\right]-\mathop{\mathbf{E}}\left[x^{\prime}_{\sigma,v_{i}}\right]\right|\geq\frac{\delta}{i+1}.

Proof.

Let
k=\sigma^{-1}(v_{i})
and
S=\{v\in V\colon\sigma^{-1}(v)<k\}
.
Let
c_{1}
(resp.,
c_{2}
) be the size of the connected component of graph
G[S]
containing
v_{i-1}
(resp.,
v_{i+1}
). If
v_{i-1}\not\in S
(resp.,
v_{i+1}\not\in S
), we set
c_{1}=0
(resp.,
c_{2}=0
).

Clearly, when the connected component of
G[S]
containing
v_{i-1}
does not include edge
(v_{2},v_{3})
, that is, when
c_{1}\leq i-3
, it holds that
x_{\sigma,v_{i}}=x^{\prime}_{\sigma,v_{i}}
. When
c_{1}=i-2
, we have

\displaystyle x_{\sigma,v_{i}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $c_{2}$ is odd}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle x^{\prime}_{\sigma,v_{i}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $c_{2}$ is odd}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\\
\end{cases}

and thus
x_{\sigma,v_{i}}=x^{\prime}_{\sigma,v_{i}}
. When
c_{1}=i-1
, we have

\displaystyle x_{\sigma,v_{i}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $c_{2}$ is even}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle x^{\prime}_{\sigma,v_{i}}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1-\delta&\text{if $c_{2}$ is even}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\\
\end{cases}

Therefore,
x_{\sigma,v_{i}}\neq x^{\prime}_{\sigma,v_{i}}
happens only when
c_{1}=i-1
and
c_{2}
is even. In such cases, we have
x_{\sigma,v_{i}}-x^{\prime}_{\sigma,v_{i}}=\delta
.
Now, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\mathbf{E}}\left[x_{\sigma,v_{i}}\right]-\mathop{\mathbf{E}}\left[x^{\prime}_{\sigma,v_{i}}\right]\right|

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[x_{\sigma,v_{i}}\neq x^{\prime}_{\sigma,v_{i}}\right]\cdot\delta

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[c_{1}=i-1\land c_{2}\equiv 0\pmod{2}\right]\cdot\delta

\displaystyle\geq\Pr\left[c_{1}=i-1\land c_{2}=0\right]\cdot\delta

\displaystyle=\Pr\left[\max(\sigma^{-1}(v_{1}),\dots,\sigma^{-1}(v_{i+1}))=\sigma^{-1}(v_{i})\right]\cdot\delta

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{i+1}.\qed

Theorem
1.6
is derived directly from the following lemma:

Lemma 5.2
.

We have

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{Shap}_{\mathrm{Match}}(w)-\mathrm{Shap}_{\mathrm{Match}}(w^{\prime})\|\geq\Omega(\delta\log n),

Proof.

From Lemma
5.1
, we have

\displaystyle\|\mathrm{Shap}_{\mathrm{Match}}(w)-\mathrm{Shap}_{\mathrm{Match}}(w^{\prime})\|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\mathop{\mathbf{E}}\left[x_{\sigma,v_{i}}\right]-\mathop{\mathbf{E}}\left[x^{\prime}_{\sigma,v_{i}}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in\{4,6,8,\dots,n-1\}}\left|\mathop{\mathbf{E}}\left[x_{\sigma,v_{i}}\right]-\mathop{\mathbf{E}}\left[x^{\prime}_{\sigma,v_{i}}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in\{4,6,8,\dots,n-1\}}\frac{\delta}{i+1}\geq\Omega(\delta\log n).\qed

5.2
Minimum Spanning Tree Game

We begin with the following:

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
f\in E
,
S\subseteq V
, and
v\in V
such that
f\subseteq S\subseteq V\setminus\{v\}
.
Let
\delta>0
and
w^{\prime}=w+\delta\bm{1}_{f}
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{OPT}(S\cup\{v,r\},w^{\prime})-\mathrm{OPT}(S\cup\{v,r\},w)\leq\mathrm{OPT}(S\cup\{r\},w^{\prime})-\mathrm{OPT}(S\cup\{r\},w).

Proof.

Let
T
and
R
be the minimum spanning trees with respect to weight
w
for
G[S\cup\{r\}]
and
G[S\cup\{v,r\}]
, respectively.
Similarly, let
T^{\prime}
and
R^{\prime}
be the minimum spanning trees with respect to weight
w^{\prime}
for
G[S\cup\{r\}]
and
G[S\cup\{v,r\}]
, respectively.
If
f
is not an edge of
R
, the left-hand side is zero; thus, the lemma holds.
Assume otherwise.
Let the connected components of the graph obtained by removing
v
and its incident edges from
R
be
C_{1},\dots,C_{k}
. Because
v
is not an endpoint of
f
by the assumption of the lemma,
f
is the edge of one of the
C_{i}
’s. Without loss of generality, we assume that this is an edge of
C_{1}
. Let
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
be the two connected components formed by removing
f
from
C_{1}
, where
D_{1}
contains the neighbor of
v
on
R
, and
D_{2}
does not.

T
is formed by connecting trees
C_{1},\dots,C_{k}
with
k-1
edges. In particular,
f
is an edge of
T
.
T^{\prime}
is obtained by removing
f
from
T
and adding an edge
f^{\prime}
(which may be the same as
f
).
We show that an edge
f^{\prime\prime}
with
w^{\prime}_{f^{\prime\prime}}\leq w^{\prime}_{f^{\prime}}
exists such that
R\setminus\{f\}\cup\{f^{\prime\prime}\}
is a tree. If this is shown, then we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{OPT}(S\cup\{v,r\},w^{\prime})-\mathrm{OPT}(S\cup\{v,r\},w)

\displaystyle\leq w^{\prime}_{f^{\prime\prime}}-w_{f}\leq w^{\prime}_{f^{\prime}}-w_{f}=\mathrm{OPT}(S\cup\{r\},w^{\prime})-\mathrm{OPT}(S\cup\{r\},w)

and the lemma is proved.

If one endpoint of
f^{\prime}
belongs to
D_{2}
, then, by the connectivity of
T^{\prime}
, the other endpoint of
f^{\prime}
does not belong to
D_{2}
. Therefore, by setting
f^{\prime\prime}=f^{\prime}
,
v
and
D_{2}
are connected on
R\setminus\{f\}\cup\{f^{\prime\prime}\}
, which implies it forms a tree, and
w^{\prime}_{f^{\prime\prime}}=w^{\prime}_{f^{\prime}}
holds.
Otherwise, let
P
be the unique path on
T^{\prime}
such that it has two endpoints in
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
, and contains no other vertices in
D_{1}\cup D_{2}=C_{1}
. Let
f^{\prime\prime}
be the unique edge on
P
with an endpoint in
D_{2}
. Because
f^{\prime\prime}
is not contained in
C_{1}
, we have
f\neq f^{\prime\prime}
. As
v
and
D_{2}
are connected on
R\setminus\{f\}\cup\{f^{\prime\prime}\}
, it forms a tree.
Furthermore,
T\setminus\{f^{\prime\prime}\}\cup\{f^{\prime}\}
is a tree. Because
T
is the minimum spanning tree with respect to weight
w
, it follows that
w^{\prime}_{f^{\prime\prime}}=w_{f^{\prime\prime}}\leq w_{f^{\prime}}
. Hence, the lemma is proved.
∎

Let
\mathrm{Shap}_{\mathrm{MST}}(w)
be the Shapley value for the minimum spanning tree game with respect to weight
w
.
Theorem
1.7
is proved by combining the next lemma and Lemma
2.1
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
\delta>0
and
f\in E
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathrm{Shap}_{\mathrm{MST}}(w)-\mathrm{Shap}_{\mathrm{MST}}(w+\delta\bm{1}_{f})\right\|_{1}\leq 2\delta.

Proof.

For
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}
and
k\in\{1,\dots,|V|\}
, let

\displaystyle x_{\sigma,k}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{OPT}(\{r,\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(k)\},w)-\mathrm{OPT}(\{r,\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(k-1)\},w),

\displaystyle x^{\prime}_{\sigma,k}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{OPT}(\{r,\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(k)\},w+\delta\bm{1}_{f})-\mathrm{OPT}(\{r,\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(k-1)\},w+\delta\bm{1}_{f}).

Let
i
be the first index, such that
f\subseteq\{r,\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(i)\}
. Then, for
j<i
, we have
x_{\sigma,j}=x^{\prime}_{\sigma,j}
. We also have

\displaystyle x^{\prime}_{\sigma,i}-x_{\sigma,i}=\mathrm{OPT}(\{r,\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(i)\},w+\delta\bm{1}_{f})-\mathrm{OPT}(\{r,\sigma(1),\dots,\sigma(i)\},w)\leq\delta.

Furthermore, Lemma
5.3
implies that for
j>i
, we have
x^{\prime}_{\sigma,j}-x_{\sigma,j}\leq 0
.
Finally, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}\left(x^{\prime}_{\sigma,j}-x_{\sigma,j}\right)=\mathrm{OPT}(V\cup\{r\},w+\delta\bm{1}_{f})-\mathrm{OPT}(V\cup\{r\},w)\geq 0.

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}\left|x^{\prime}_{\sigma,j}-x_{\sigma,j}\right|\leq\delta+\left(\delta-\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}\left(x^{\prime}_{\sigma,j}-x_{\sigma,j}\right)\right)\leq 2\delta.

Therefore, we have

\left\|\mathrm{Shap}_{\mathrm{MST}}(w)-\mathrm{Shap}_{\mathrm{MST}}(w^{\prime})\right\|_{1}\leq\frac{1}{|V|!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{V}}\sum_{j=1}^{|V|}\left|x^{\prime}_{\sigma,j}-x_{\sigma,j}\right|\leq 2\delta.\qed
