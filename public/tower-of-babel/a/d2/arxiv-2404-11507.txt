Fluctuation of Ergodic Averages and Other Stochastic Processes
S. Mondal, J. Rosenblatt, M. Wierdl

Abstract
. For an ergodic map T T and a non-constant, real-valued f ∈ L 1 f\in L^{1} , the ergodic averages A N ​ f ​ ( x ) = 1 N ​ ∑ n = 1 N f ⁡ ( T n ​ x ) A_{N}f(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{n=1}^{N}f(T^{n}x) converge a.e., but the convergence is never monotone. Depending on particular properties of the function f f , the averages A N ​ f ​ ( x ) A_{N}f(x) may or may not actually fluctuate around the mean value infinitely often a.e. We will prove that a.e. fluctuation around the mean is the generic behavior. That is, for a fixed ergodic T T , the generic non-constant f ∈ L 1 f\in L^{1} has the averages A N ​ f ​ ( x ) A_{N}f(x) fluctuating around the mean infinitely often for almost every x x . We also consider fluctuation for other stochastic processes like subsequences of the ergodic averages, convolution operators, weighted averages, uniform distribution and martingales. We will show that in general, in these settings fluctuation around the limit infinitely often persists as the generic behavior.



1.
Introduction

The aim of this paper is to analyze the non-monotonicity and the fluctuation of a variety of convergent stochastic processes. Suppose that
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
is a standard probability space,
T_{N}:L^{1}\to L^{1}
is a sequence of bounded, linear operators such that for every real-valued, integrable function
f
,
(T_{N}f)
converges a.e. to
Tf
for some bounded linear operator
T:L^{1}\to L^{1}
.

Definition 1.1
.

With this setup, we say that
the convergence of
T_{N}f
is
non-monotone
if there does not exist any set
M\subset X
with
\mu(M)>0
satisfying the following:

For every
x\in M
,
T_{N}f(x)\geq T_{N+1}f(x)
eventually, or
T_{N}f(x)\leq T_{N+1}f(x)
eventually.

Given a sequence of bounded linear operators and its limit, we are interested in the following questions.

Question 1.2
.

[Non-monotonicity]
For any function
f\in L^{1}
, is the convergence of
T_{N}f
non-monotone?

When this question has an affirmative answer, we ask a more pertinent question about the behavior of convergence. Namely,

Question 1.3
.

[Fluctuation]
Can we say that for almost every
x\in X
,
T_{N}f(x)

fluctuates
around its limit infinitely often, that is, is
(T_{N}f(x)-Tf(x))>0
for infinitely many
N
, and
(T_{N}f(x)-Tf(x))<0
for infinitely many (different)
N
?

The study of non-monotonicity and fluctuation of convergent stochastic processes is a topic of intrinsic interest in probability theory. For a symmetric random walk, non-monotonicity was first investigated by Erdős and Rényi
[
10
]
. They proved that the length of the longest run of heads in
n
tosses of a fair coin will approximately be equal to
\log n
. The question of fluctuation for such stochastic processes was first studied by Chung and Hunt
[
8
]
, and later by Chung and Erdős
[
7
]
, and Csáki, Erdős and Révész
[
9
]
. They found bounds on the length of the longest excursion
1
1

1

There is a subtle difference between fluctuation and excursion. The former one refers to strict inequality, whereas the later one does not require the inequality to be strict.
in a symmetric random variable.

While our primary focus is on various ergodic averages, we will also study Lebesgue differentiation,
dyadic martingales, uniform distribution, and weighted averages. It turns out that for these stochastic processes, there are functions

T_{N}f

3.
Summary of results

In this section we state a variety of results about non-monotonicity and fluctuation. The proofs for these results are given in the upcoming sections as stated in Section
2
.

As a basic model case, take the classical ergodic averages. We have an ergodic, invertible, measure-preserving mapping
T
on a standard probability space
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
. For a function
f\in L^{1}=L^{1}(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
,
by the Pointwise Ergodic Theorem,
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
converges to
\int f
for a.e.
x
. (See (
4.1
) for the meaning of the averaging notation).

In the special case, if we consider the irrational rotation
({\mathbb{T}},{\Sigma},\lambda,R_{\alpha})
and a continuous function
f
, we can see that for almost every
x
, the convergence of
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(x+n\alpha)
to
\int f
is non-monotone. (See
Definition

1.1
for the definition of non-monotonicity.) Indeed, compare
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(x+n\alpha)
with
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(x+n\alpha)
, and use the uniform distribution of
(n\alpha)
mod 1 in
{\mathbb{T}}
. See Section
5
for details.

Our first proposition shows that this is, in fact, true for any ergodic transformation
T
and any integrable function
f
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose
f\in L^{1}
is real-valued and not a constant function. Then for a.e.
x
, the averages
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
are non-monotone.

We can also give a somewhat more detailed result about the gap that occurs between two consecutive averages.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose
f\in L^{1}
is real-valued and not a constant function. Then there exists
d>0
such that for all
T
ergodic, we have for a.e.
x
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)

\displaystyle>\frac{d}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)\text{ infinitely often, and }

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)

\displaystyle<-\frac{d}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)\text{ infinitely often.}

Different aspects of fluctuation for the classical ergodic averages have already been studied by several authors. For instance, Bishop
[
3
]
showed that one can give universal estimates for the
probability that there are many fluctuations in the ergodic averages of
L^{1}
functions. In fact, he proved an exact analogy of Doob’s upcrossing inequality for martingales, which was later improved by Kalikow and Weiss
[
15
]
. The most relevant result related to our paper is the following theorem
[
12
, Theorem 4]
.

Theorem 3.3
.

[Halász]
For any
f\in L^{1}
,
\big(\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)-\int f\big)
almost everywhere changes sign infinitely often in the weaker sense that it cannot be eventually positive or negative.

Halász’s theorem suggests that for every
f\in L^{1}
,
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
may fluctuate around the mean infinitely often for almost every
x
. In the following example, we see that it may not be the case.

Example 3.4
.

Take our probability space to be
[0,1)
with the usual Lebesgue measure
\lambda
. Take any ergodic map
T
and any Lebesgue measurable set
F\subset[0,1)
with
\lambda(F)>0.
Let
f
be the coboundary with the transfer function
\mathbbm{1}_{F}
, that is,
f=\mathbbm{1}_{F}-\mathbbm{1}_{F}\circ T
. Then the possible values of
\mathbbm{1}_{F}(Tx)-\mathbbm{1}_{F}\circ T^{N+1}(x)
for
x\in T^{-1}F
are
1-1=0
and
1-0=1
. So for these
x
, we get
\mathbbm{1}_{F}(Tx)-\mathbbm{1}_{F}\circ T^{N+1}(x)
changing value infinitely often, but the sign is never strictly negative. At the same time, the possible values of
\mathbbm{1}_{F}(Tx)-\mathbbm{1}_{F}\circ T^{N+1}(x)
for
x\not\in T^{-1}F
are
0-1=-1
and
0-0=0
. So, for these
x
, we get
\mathbbm{1}_{F}(Tx)-\mathbbm{1}_{F}\circ T^{N+1}(x)
, again changing value infinitely often, but the sign is never strictly positive. So, we do not have the averages
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
fluctuating around the mean in either case.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
F
be a real-valued, measurable function. We say that
F
has an
upper barrier
if there is a set
P
of positive measure on which
F
is constant

p
and such that
F(y)\leq p
for a.e.
y\in X
. We say that
F
has a
lower barrier
if there is a set
Q
of
positive measure on which
F
is constant
q
and such that
F(y)\geq q
for a.e.
y\in X
.

In the next theorem, we will characterize those functions for which
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
fails to fluctuate on a set of positive measure.

Theorem 3.6
.

Assume that
F
has either an
upper barrier or a lower barrier, and
f=F-F\circ T
is an integrable function such that
\int f=0
. Then there exists a set
E
of positive measure such that
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
fails to fluctuate on
E
, that is, for every
x\in E
, either
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)\geq 0
eventually or
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)\leq 0
eventually.

Conversely, assume that for some
f\in L^{1}
with
\int f=0
,
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
fails to fluctuate on a set of positive measure. Then
f
can be expressed as
F-F\circ T
for some measurable function
F
that has either an upper or a lower barrier.

For a given increasing sequence
(N_{i})_{i}
of positive integers, we initiate the study of similar types of questions for the averages
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i}]}f(T^{n}x)
. More precisely, we ask the following questions.

Question 3.7
.

For any real-valued function
f\in L^{1}
, is the convergence of
\left(\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i}]}^{T}f\right)_{i}
non-monotone?

Similarly,

Question 3.8
.

For almost every
x
, do the averages
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i}]}f(T^{n}x)
fluctuate almost everywhere around its mean
\int f
, that is, does
\big(\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i}]}f(T^{n}x)-\int f\big)_{i}
change sign infinitely often?

One would expect that the non-monotonicity and the fluctuation of the averages
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
is not something that persists when one takes a subsequence
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i}]}f(T^{n}x)
.
In
Proposition

5.6
, we will construct a system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
and a subsequence
(N_{i})
such that for every
x
,
\Big(\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i+1}]}f(T^{n}x)-\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i}]}f(T^{n}x)\Big)_{i}
will be eventually positive or negative depending only on
x
(not on
i
), thereby answering
Question

3.7
negatively. On the other hand, the following theorem shows that for any increasing sequence of positive integers
(N_{i})
and for the generic function
f
, the averages
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i}]}f(T^{n}x)
exhibit a fluctuating behavior around the mean infinitely often a.e.

Theorem 3.9
.

If
T
is ergodic, and
(N_{i})
is an unbounded sequence, then there is a dense
G_{\delta}
set
\mathcal{O}
in

such that for every
f\in\mathcal{O}
and for almost every

,
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i}]}f(T^{n}x)
fluctuates infinitely often around its mean. That is, for a.e.
x
, we have
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i}]}f(T^{n}x)>\int f
for infinitely many
i
and
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i}]}f(T^{n}x)<\int f
for infinitely many (different)

.

A salient feature of the proof of Theorem

is that it uses the

strong sweeping out property
of lacunary sequences.

The next natural question would be to describe those functions
f
for which the averages
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i}]}f(T^{n}x)
exhibit non-monotonicity or fluctuating behavior (around the mean) for a set of positive measure. Our results in this regard are far from complete.
It turns out that these questions are related to an intrinsic dynamical property of the sequence
(N_{i})
, which we will call the
complete recurrence property
. See Subsection
4.2
for the definition of the complete recurrence property.

In the special case when
(N_{i})
is a syndetic sequence (i.e., a sequence that has bounded gaps between the consecutive terms), for a ‘typical’
\alpha
, we can say something definitive about the non-monotonicity of the averages with respect to the rotation by
\alpha
.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
(N_{i})
be a syndetic sequence. Then there exists a countable subset
E
of
{\mathbb{T}}
such that the following holds.

If
\alpha\in{\mathbb{T}}\setminus E
and
f
is a non-constant, integrable function, then for a.e.
x
, the averages
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i}]}f(x+n\alpha)
are non-monotone.

The next topic of our discussion will be the fluctuation of convolution operators.

Definition 3.11
.

An
approximate identity
is a sequence
(\phi_{n})\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
such that for all
f\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
, we have
\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\phi_{n}\star f-f\|_{1}=0
. A sequence
(\phi_{n})
is
normalized
if
\|\phi_{n}\|_{1}=1
for all
n\geq 1
. A sequence is
eventually local
if the functions all have bounded support and for all
\delta>0
, there exists
N\geq 1
, such that for all
n\geq N
, we have
\phi_{n}(x)=0
a.e. on
{\mathbb{R}}\setminus[-\delta,\delta].
A
proper
approximate identity is a normalized, eventually local, approximate identity consisting of positive functions.

Suppose that
(\phi_{n})
is a proper approximate identity on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. While the convolutions
\phi_{n}\star f
converge in
L^{1}
-norm to

f
for all
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, these convolutions may or may
not converge almost everywhere. This is a topic for which there
is a great deal of literature of all types; see
[
21
]
for
some background and theorems about these issues.

In this paper, we are more
concerned with what could be considered the other side of the issue,
the rate of this convergence. Let
CB(\mathbb{R})

denote the continuous, bounded functions on
\mathbb{R}
in
the uniform norm topology. The main result concerning this topic is the following:

Theorem 3.12
.

Let
B=L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for some fixed
p,1\leq p\leq\infty
, or
B=CB(\mathbb{R})
. Suppose that
(\phi_{n})
is a proper approximate identity and

(\epsilon_{n})
is a
sequence of positive numbers with
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{n}=0
.
Then there is a dense
G_{\delta}
subset
\mathcal{A}
of

such that
for all
f\in\mathcal{A}
, we have both
\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\phi_{n}\star f(x)-f(x)}{\epsilon_{n}}=\infty
a.e. and
\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\phi_{n}\star f(x)-f(x)}{\epsilon_{n}}=-\infty
a.e.

The final topic of this paper is the fluctuation of convergent martingales. The classical dyadic martingale is probably the best example
to consider because of its connections to Lebesgue differentiation and, through that,
to dynamical systems.

The dyadic martingale is constructed as follows. Take
\mathcal{D}_{n}

to be the dyadic
\sigma
-algebra whose atoms are the intervals
D_{n,j}=[\frac{j}{2^{n}},\frac{j+1}{2^{n}}],j=0,\dots,2^{n}-1
. The finite
\sigma
-algebras

\mathcal{D}_{n}
are increasing, with the measure-theoretic
completion of
\bigcup\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mathcal{D}_{n}

being the Lebesgue
\sigma
-algebra in
[0,1]
.

Now for
G\in L^{1}[0,1]
, as
n\to\infty
, we have the conditional expectations
E(G|\mathcal{D}_{n})
converge
to
G
in
L^{1}
-norm and a.e. with respect to Lebesgue measure
\lambda
. We claim
that this convergence is generically fluctuating a.e.

Theorem 3.13
.

For a dense
G_{\delta}
set
\mathcal{O}
of
functions in
L^{1}[0,1]
, if
G\in\mathcal{O}
, for a.e.
x\in[0,1]
,
E(G|\mathcal{D}_{n})(x)>G(x)
for infinitely
many
n\geq 1
, and
E(G|\mathcal{D}_{n})(x)<G(x)
for infinitely many (other)
n\geq 1
.

Remark 3.14
.

It is clear that we have to consider the fluctuation of this strong type on
only a subset
\mathcal{O}
of
L^{1}[0,1]
. Indeed, if
G
were measurable
with respect to
\mathcal{D}_{m}
, then
E(G|\mathcal{D}_{n})=G
for all
n\geq m
.
So for such functions, there is no fluctuation. If we vary
m
, then such functions are dense
in
L^{1}[0,1]
in
L^{1}
-norm.

4.
Preliminaries

4.1.
Notation

Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
be a measure-preserving system and
f\in L^{1}(X)
. For a finite set of
S
of positive integers and a sequence
(a_{n})
of positive integers, we use the following averaging notation.

(4.1)

\mathbb{A}_{S}f(T^{a_{n}}x):=\dfrac{1}{|\#S|}\sum_{n\in S}f(T^{a_{n}}x).

Intervals in the averaging notation mean intervals of integers.

In particular,

(4.2)

\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x):=\dfrac{f(Tx)+f(T^{2}x)+\dots+f(T^{N}x)}{N}.

When we are concerned with the function space and not their functional value, we will use
\mathbb{A}_{S}^{T}
where
\mathbb{A}_{S}^{T}:X\to X
is defined by
\mathbb{A}_{S}^{T}f(x)=\mathbb{A}_{S}f(T^{n}x).

{\mathbb{T}}
stands for the one-dimensional torus, that is,
{\mathbb{R}}/{\mathbb{Z}}
, and
\lambda
is reserved for denoting the Lebesgue measure.

Sometimes we assume that
T
is totally ergodic to avoid the following obstacle regarding fluctuation. Suppose that
T
is not totally ergodic. Then there is a non-zero
f
such that
f\circ T=\zeta f
with
\zeta
a root of unity. Say
\zeta^{N}=1
. Then
\sum\limits_{n\in[N]}\zeta^{n}=0
. So the same thing occurs with any multiple of
N
, and hence, for example,
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)=\mathbb{A}_{[2N]}f(T^{n}x)=0
for almost every
x
. On the other hand, if we assume total ergodicity, then two averages cannot be equal.

Proposition 4.1
.

If
T
is ergodic and for (almost) every
x\in X
,
\mathbb{A}_{[M]}f(T^{n}x)=

\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
for some
M>N
and a non-zero
f\in L^{1}
, then
T
is not totally ergodic.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{A}_{[M]}^{T}f=\mathbb{A}_{[N]}^{T}f
, we have

f\circ T^{M}=\frac{M}{N}\sum\limits_{[N]}f\circ T^{n}-\sum\limits_{[M-1]}f\circ T^{n}.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be the linear span of
\{f\circ T,\dots,f\circ T^{M-1}\}
. We have
f\circ T^{M}\in\mathcal{S}
. That is,
\mathcal{S}\circ T\subset\mathcal{S}
. So, because
\mathcal{S}
is finite-dimensional,
\mathcal{S}
is invariant under
T
. Since
T
is ergodic, it follows that there is a non-zero
F\in\mathcal{S}
and
\zeta\in\mathbb{T},\zeta\not=1,
such that
F\circ T=\zeta F
. We claim that in fact
\zeta
is a root of unity and so
T
is not totally ergodic. To see this, we take
\displaystyle F=\sum\limits_{m\in[M-1]}c_{m}f\circ T^{m}
for some
c_{1},\dots,c_{M-1}\in\mathbb{C}
. So we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{[M]}^{T}F=\sum\limits_{m\in[M-1]}c_{m}

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{[M]}^{T}(f\circ T^{m})=\sum\limits_{m\in[M-1]}c_{m}\mathbb{A}_{[M]}^{T}f\circ T^{m}

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m\in[M-1]}c_{m}\mathbb{A}_{[N]}^{T}f\circ T^{m}=\sum\limits_{m\in[M-1]}c_{m}\mathbb{A}_{[N]}^{T}(f\circ T^{m})=\mathbb{A}_{[N]}^{T}F.

Because
F
is not zero and
F\circ T=\zeta F
, we have
\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\sum\limits_{m\in[M]}\zeta^{m}=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{m\in[N]}\zeta^{m}
. Using the geometric sum formula, it follows that
\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}(\zeta^{M}-1)=\frac{1}{N}(\zeta^{N}-1)
. That is,
M-N=M\zeta^{N}-N\zeta^{M}=(M-N\zeta^{M-N})\zeta^{N}
. Since
|\zeta|=1
, it follows that
M-N=|M-N\zeta^{M-N}|
. That is, in
\mathbb{C}
the distance from
N
to
M
is the distance of
N\zeta^{M-N}
to
M
. But this can only happen if
\zeta^{M-N}=1
. That is,
\zeta
is a root of unity.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

If
T
is totally ergodic, and
f
is a non-constant
L^{1}
function, then
\mathbb{A}_{[M]}^{T}f
is also non-constant for all
M\geq 1
.

Proof.

Suppose, to the contrary, there is a constant
c
such that
\mathbb{A}_{[M]}^{T}f(x)=c
for almost every
x\in X
. Applying this equality on
T^{M}x
, we get that
\mathbb{A}_{[M]}^{T}f(T^{M}x)=c
for almost every
x
. Adding these two equalities and dividing both sides by
2
, we have
\mathbb{A}_{[2M]}^{T}f(x)=c
for almost every
x
. Thus we have
\mathbb{A}_{[M]}^{T}f(x)=\mathbb{A}_{[2M]}^{T}f(x)=c
for almost every
x
. But this is a contradiction to Proposition
4.1
.
∎

4.2.
Complete recurrence property

In this subsection, we will introduce the notion of complete recurrence property. This has some consequences related to the non-monotonicity and fluctuation of the subsequence ergodic averages (see
Proposition

5.1
and
Theorem

5.13
). It is also a topic of independent interest.

Definition 4.3

(Complete recurrence property)
.

A sequence
(N_{i})
of positive integers is said to satisfy the complete recurrence property for the system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
if every set
E
of positive measure and every positive integer
L
satisfy the following condition:

(4.3)

\displaystyle\mu\Big(\cup_{i\geq L}T^{-N_{i}}E\Big)=1.

In particular, if a sequence
(N_{i})
satisfies (
4.3
) for the irrational rotation
({\mathbb{T}},\Sigma,\lambda,R_{\alpha})
, then we say that
(N_{i})
satisfies the complete recurrence property for
\alpha
. We say that a sequence
(N_{i})
satisfies the complete recurrence property if it satisfies the same for
every
ergodic system.

An equivalent formulation of the complete recurrence property is the following:

For every positive integer
L
and every set
E
and
F
of positive measures, there exists
i\geq L
such that

(4.4)

\mu\big(E\cap T^{-N_{i}}F\big)>0.

Remark 4.4
.

(1)

Recall that a set
R\subset{\mathbb{N}}
is said to be a
set of recurrence
if for any measure-preserving system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
and for all
A\in\mathcal{B}
with
\mu(A)>0
if we have
\mu(A\cap T^{r}A)>0
for some
r\in R
. This suggests to us the name complete recurrence property. Sets of recurrence have been studied extensively in ergodic theory. For a beautiful account on the set of recurrence, the reader is suggested to look at the book of Furstenberg
[
11
]
.

(2)

While the set of recurrence is defined for any measure-preserving system, to define the complete recurrence property, we must restrict ourselves to ergodic systems.

We will give some examples of complete recurrence sequences via the following lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
be an ergodic system and
(N_{i})
be an increasing sequence of positive numbers. Suppose that for every
f\in L^{\infty}

(4.5)

\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i\in[K]}f(T^{N_{i}}x)\to\int f\text{ in $L^{2}$ norm as }K\to\infty.

Then
(N_{i})
satisfies the complete recurrence property for
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
.

Proof.

To the contrary, suppose that
(N_{i})
does not satisfy the complete recurrence property for
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
. Then, by (
4.4
), there exist
E,F\in\mathcal{B}
with
\mu(E)\cdot\mu(F)>0
such that

(4.6)

\mu\big(E\cap T^{-N_{i}}F\big)=0\text{ for every large }i.

So, there exists a set
\tilde{E}\subset E
with
\mu(E)=\mu(\tilde{E})>0
and
\tilde{E}\cap T^{-N_{i}}F=\emptyset
for every large
i
.
This implies that for every
x\in\tilde{E}
,
\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i\in[K]}\mathbbm{1}_{F}(T^{N_{i}}x)\to 0
. On the other hand, using (
4.5
), we can find a subsequence
(K_{l})
such that for almost every
x\in X
,
\displaystyle\frac{1}{K_{l}}\sum_{i\in[K_{l}]}\mathbbm{1}_{F}(T^{N_{i}}x)\to\mu(F)
as
l\to\infty
. This is a contradiction because
\mu(\tilde{E})\cdot\mu(F)>0.

∎

A particular class of sequences that satisfies the above hypothesis is the class of
ergodic sequences
.

Definition 4.6
.

(Ergodic sequence)
Let
T
be an ergodic transformation on the probability space
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
. A sequence
(N_{i})
of positive increasing integers is said to be an ergodic sequence for the system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
if for every
f\in L^{2}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i\in[K]}f(T^{N_{i}}x)\to\int f\text{ in $L^{2}$ norm as }K\to\infty.

If the sequence
(N_{i})
is ergodic for every ergodic system, then it is said to be an ergodic sequence.

For a detailed discussion on ergodic sequences, one can look at the survey article of Rosenblatt and Wierdl
[
23
, Chapter II]
. Here we will list some examples of ergodic sequences, and hence sequences of complete recurrence by
Lemma

4.5
.

Example 4.7
.

Let
c>1
be a non-integer real number and
p_{n}
denote the
n
-th prime number.

(a)

Then from
[
5
, Theorem A]
, it follows that
(\lfloor n^{c}\rfloor)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is an ergodic sequence.

(b)

From
[
2
, Theorem 3.1]
, it follows that
(\lfloor p_{n}^{c}\rfloor)_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is an ergodic sequence.

Example 4.8
.

Let
p_{i}
denote the
i
-th prime number. It is well known that both
(i^{2})
and

are ergodic sequences for totally ergodic systems.

The following example shows that the complete recurrence property is indeed a special property.

Example 4.9
.

Let
I=[a,b]\subset{\mathbb{T}}
be an interval with
\lambda(I)<\frac{1}{4}
. Let
\alpha
be a fixed irrational number. Define
S=\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}:I\cap(I-n\alpha)\neq\emptyset\}
. We claim that
S
is a set of recurrence, but it does not satisfy the complete recurrence property. Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
be any measure-preserving system. Let
(\tilde{X},\tilde{\mathcal{B}},\tilde{\mu},\tilde{T})
be the product system of
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
and
({\mathbb{T}},\beta,\lambda,R_{\alpha}).
For an arbitrary
A\in\mathcal{B}
, let us consider the set
\tilde{A}=A\times I
. By the Poincaré recurrence theorem, there exists
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\tilde{\mu}\Big(\tilde{A}\cap\tilde{T}^{-n}\tilde{A}\Big)>0.
Such

must belong to

, and it also satisfies that
\mu(A\cap T^{-n}A)>0
. Thus

is a set of recurrence. On the other hand, there exists an interval

with

such that

. Hence, for any interval

with

So,

cannot satisfy the complete recurrence property.  ∎

5.
Fluctuation and non-monotonicity of the classical ergodic averages

5.1.
Proof of the fluctuation and non-monotonicity results

We begin by proving
Proposition

3.1
.

First observe that
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)\leq\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
implies that

\frac{1}{N+1}f(T^{N+1}x)\leq(\frac{1}{N}-\frac{1}{N+1})\sum\limits_{[N]}f(T^{n}x).

This means that
f(T^{N+1}x)\leq\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x).
Indeed,
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)\leq\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
holds if and only if
f(T^{N+1}x)\leq\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
holds. Similarly,
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)\geq\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
holds if and only if we have
f(T^{N+1}x)\geq\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
.
Now if either of these inequalities hold for all large enough values of
N
, then we can compare
f(T^{N+1}x)
to
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
to arrive at a contradiction.

Proof of
Proposition

3.1
.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
f
is mean-zero but not constant. Hence, there is some
\delta>0
, and there are sets
A
and
B
of positive measure, such that
f\geq\delta
on
A
and
f\leq-\delta
on
B
. By ergodicity, for a.e.
x
, there is some
N_{0}
, depending on
x
, such that
|\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)|\leq\delta/2
for all
N\geq N_{0}
. In addition, the ergodicity of
T
implies that for a.e.
x
, we have
T^{N+1}x\in A
for infinitely many
N
, and
T^{N+1}x\in B
for infinitely many
N
. Therefore, for a.e.
x
, we cannot have for all
N\geq N_{0}
,
f(T^{N+1}x)\leq\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
because using recurrence into
A
this would imply
\delta\leq\delta/2
. But at the same time, for a.e.
x
, we cannot have for all
N\geq N_{0}
,
f(T^{N+1}x)\geq\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
because using recurrence into
B
this would imply
-\delta\geq-\delta/2
. Using both of these computations, we see that for a.e.
x
, for infinitely many
N
, we do not have
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)\leq\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
, and for infinitely many
N
, we do not have
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)\geq\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
. Hence, for a.e.
x
, the ergodic averages are not monotonically decreasing, and they are not monotonically increasing.
∎

Now, we will prove
Proposition

3.2
.

Proof of
Proposition

3.2
.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
f
is mean-zero but not constant. Hence, there is some
\delta>0
, and there are sets
A
and
B
of positive measure, such that
f\geq\delta
on
A
and
f\leq-\delta
on
B
. Let
d=\frac{\delta}{6}
. If we have
\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)\leq\frac{d}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
for all sufficiently large

, then using the computation above, we have
\displaystyle f(T^{N+1}x)\leq\frac{(N+1)d}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
for all sufficiently large

. But for a.e.
x
,
T^{N+1}x\in A
for infinitely many

. Also, for a.e.
x
,
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)\to\int f\,d\mu=0
as

. Hence, for a.e.
x
, we have for infinitely many

,

\displaystyle\delta\leq 2d+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)\leq 2d+\frac{\delta}{2}=\frac{\delta}{3}+\frac{\delta}{2}
. This is impossible. So we must have for a.e.
x
,
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)>\frac{d}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
for infinitely many
N
.

Similarly, if we have
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)\geq-\frac{d}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
for all sufficiently large

, then using the computation above, we have
f(T^{N+1}x)\geq-\frac{(N+1)d}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
for all sufficiently large
N
. But for a.e.
x
,
T^{N+1}x\in B
for infinitely many
N
. Also, for a.e.
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)\to\int f\,d\mu=0
as

. Hence, for a.e.
x
, we have for infinitely many

-\delta\geq-2d+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)\geq-2d-\frac{\delta}{2}=-\frac{\delta}{3}-\frac{\delta}{2}
. This is impossible. So we must have for a.e.

,
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)<-\frac{d}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
for infinitely many

.
∎

We need the following results to prove
Theorem

3.6
. See
3.5
for the definitions of upper barrier and lower barrier.

Proposition 5.1
.

Suppose that
(N_{i})
satisfies the complete recurrence property for the system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
. Let
F
be a measurable function such that
F
has no
upper barrier and no lower barrier. Also, assume that
f=F-F\circ T
is an integrable function with
\int f=0
. Then for a.e.
x
, the averages
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i}]}f(T^{n}x)<0
infinitely often and
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i}]}f(T^{n}x)>0
infinitely often.

Proof.

Since
F
does not have an upper barrier,
F\circ T
also does not have an upper barrier. Hence, for a.e.
x
, there exists a set
B=B(x)
of positive measure such that
F(Ty)>F(Tx)
for all
y\in B
. Since
(N_{i})
satisfies the complete recurrence property, for every positive integer
L
, we have

\displaystyle\mu\Big(\cup_{i\geq L}T^{-N_{i}}B\Big)=1.
Thus, for infinitely many
i
,
T^{N_{i}}x
falls into
B
. For such an
i
,
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i}]}f(T^{n}x)=\frac{1}{N_{i}}\Big(F(Tx)-F(T^{N_{i}+1}x)\Big)<0
. Similarly, for infinitely many
j
,
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{j}]}f(T^{n}x)>0
.
∎

Since
{\mathbb{N}}
(with respect to the natural order) satisfies the complete recurrence property, we have the following corollary.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
F
be a measurable function such that
F
has no
upper barrier and no lower barrier. Also, assume that
f=F-F\circ T
is an integrable function with
\int f=0
. Then for a.e.
x
, the averages
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)<0
infinitely often and
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)>0
infinitely often.

Remark 5.3
.

One cannot expect that the conclusion of

will hold for
every

. In fact, in contrast to

, it was proved by Peres

,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n\in[N]}g(T^{n}y)\geq\int gd\mu\text{ for all }N\geq 1.

for

Proof of Theorem
3.6
.

Assume that
F
has an
upper barrier (the lower barrier case is similar). Then by definition, there is a set
E
of positive measure on which
F
is constant

p

F(x)\leq p
for a.e.

. Now consider the function
f=F-F\circ T
. Observe that
\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)\geq 0
for all
x\in E
.

Conversely, assume that there exists a set
E\in\mathcal{B}
such that
\mu(E)>0
and for all
x\in E
,
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)\geq 0
eventually (the
`\leq^{\prime}
-case is similar). This implies that
\mathbb{A}_{[0,N)}f(T^{n}x)\geq 0
eventually on
T^{-1}E.
Our goal is to show that
f=F-F\circ T
for some measurable function
F
that also has an upper or lower barrier. Consider the following function:

(5.2)

F(x)=\inf_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sum_{n\in[0,N)}f(T^{n}x).

Observe that
F
is bounded below on
T^{-1}E
; otherwise we cannot have
\mathbb{A}_{[0,N)}f(T^{n}x)\geq 0
eventually on
T^{-1}E
. Since the set

is invariant under

is ergodic,
\mu(G)=1
. So,
F
is a measurable function that is finite a.e. We have

(5.3)

\displaystyle F(Tx)=\inf_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sum_{n\in[N]}f(T^{n}x)

\displaystyle=\Big(\inf_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sum_{n\in[0,N]}f(T^{n}x)\Big)-f(x)

\displaystyle\geq\Big(\inf_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\sum_{n\in[0,N)}f(T^{n}x)\Big)-f(x)

\displaystyle=F(x)-f(x).

Define
K(x)=f(x)+F(Tx)-F(x)
.
By (
5.3
), we have
K(x)\geq 0
for almost every
x
, and by the pointwise ergodic theorem

(5.4)

\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{n\in[0,N)}K(T^{n}x)\to\int_{X}K(x)d\mu\text{ for a.e. }x.

(even if
\int_{X}K(x)d\mu=\infty
, it follows from the finite case).
The left-hand side is

(5.5)

\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{n\in[0,N)}f(T^{n}x)+\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{n\in[0,N)}\Big(F(T^{N}x)-F(x)\Big).

The first term tends to
\int f=0
a.e. by the pointwise ergodic theorem. Since
F(x)
is finite a.e., there exists
M>0
such that the set
S(M)=\{x:|F(x)|\leq M\}
is of positive measure, and hence for almost every
x
,
T^{N}x
falls into
S(M)
for infinitely many
N
. For such a

,
\big(F(T^{N}x)-F(x)\big)
is bounded by
2M
, and so for almost every

the second term also tends to

. Letting

, we conclude that

. Since

is non-negative, we must have

This proves that
f=F-F\circ T
almost everywhere. By assumption, we have
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)\geq 0
eventually on a set of positive measure
E
. Invoke

5.2
to conclude that

must have either an upper barrier or a lower barrier, finishing the proof of the theorem.

We need the following lemma of Halász
[

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)

.

Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
be a measure preserving system such that
T
is totally ergodic. Then for every
E\in\mathcal{B}
with

,

\mu(E)
a.e.

Suppose, to the contrary,
\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{[N]}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)

(5.6)

\Big(\sum_{n\in[N]}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)-N\mu(E)\Big)_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\text{ is bounded below on a set of positive measure}

The questions of non-monotonicity and fluctuation for the subsequence of ergodic averages are much more subtle than that of the classical ergodic averages. As the following example shows, one can construct a totally ergodic system

.

The following theorem provides a sufficient condition for non-monotonicity of the averages in terms of the complete recurrence property.

Theorem 5.13
.

Let
T
be a totally ergodic transformation and
(N_{i})
be an increasing sequence of positive integers that admits a subsequence
(N_{i_{j}})
such that

(1)

(N_{i_{j}+1}-N_{i_{j}})=K
for some fixed constant
K
.

(2)

(N_{i_{j}})
satisfies the complete recurrence property.

Then for any non-constant, integrable
f
,
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i}]}f(T^{n}x)
is non-monotone.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, let us assume that
f\in L^{1}_{0}
, that is,
\int f=0
.

One can check that

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i_{j}+1}]}f(T^{n}x)-\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i_{j}}]}f(T^{n}x)

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i_{j}}+1,N_{i_{j}+1}]}f(T^{n}x)-\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i_{j}}]}f(T^{n}x)\Big)\dfrac{(N_{i_{j}+1}-N_{i_{j}})}{N_{i_{j}+1}}.

Since
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i_{j}}]}f(T^{n}x)\to 0
for almost every
x
, it will be sufficient to show that there exists
\delta>0
such that for a.e.
x
,
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i_{j}}+1,N_{i_{j}+1}]}f(T^{n}x)>\delta
for infinitely many
j
and
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i_{l}}+1,N_{i_{l}+1}]}f(T^{n}x)<-\delta
for infinitely many
l
.

Define

g(x)=\mathbb{A}_{[K]}f(T^{n}x).

Since
(N_{i_{j}+1}-N_{i_{j}})=K
for every
j
,

\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i_{j}}+1,N_{i_{j}+1}]}f(T^{n}x)=g(T^{N_{i_{j}}}x).

This suggests that we should analyze the function
g
.

Since
f
is non-constant and
T
is totally ergodic, by
Corollary

4.2
,
g
is also a non-constant function. Since
f\in L^{1}_{0}
, we must have
g\in L^{1}_{0}
. Hence, there exists
\delta>0
such that both
\mu(S_{0}^{+})
and
\mu(S_{0}^{-})
are positive, where

\displaystyle S_{0}^{+}=\{x:g(x)>\delta\},\text{ and }\ S_{0}^{-}=\{x:g(x)<-\delta\}.

Since
(N_{i_{j}})
satisfies the complete recurrence property, for a.e.
x
, there are infinitely many
j
and

such that
g(T^{N_{i_{j}}}x)>\delta
and
g(T^{N_{i_{l}}}x)<-\delta
.
This finishes the proof.

Problem 5.20
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
be an ergodic system and
(N_{i})
be an increasing sequence of positive integers. Can we characterize those functions that fluctuate around their mean infinitely often?

5.3.
Some quantitative results

In
Proposition

3.2
we have seen a quantitative result between consecutive averages. Now we will investigate when the gap term
\frac{d}{N}
can be made larger. Take
w(N)
that increases to
\infty
as
N\to\infty
. For a mean-zero
f\in L^{1}
, we want to see what can be said about having
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)>\frac{w(N)}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
for a.e.
x
and for infinitely many
N
. This condition clearly forces
w(N)=o(N)
because a.e.
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)\to 0
as
N\to\infty
.

By our computation above, this inequality basically means that
f(T^{N+1}x)>2w(N)
for infinitely many
N
. So we cannot have a gap like this for
f\in L^{\infty}
. But if
f\in L^{1}\setminus L^{\infty}
, then it is possible to have this occur. Similarly, at the same time,
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)<-\frac{w(N)}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
infinitely often occurs if
f(T^{N+1}x)\leq-2w(N)
for infinitely many
N
. For unbounded
f\in L^{1}
this is possible.

The level
w(N)
for which this can occur depends on
f
and
T
. For example, suppose we have
w(N)=o(N)
. A related question is when can we have

\sum\limits_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu\{x:f(x)>w(N)\}=\infty?

It is likely that this occurs for a dense
G_{\delta}
of functions in
L^{1}
.
But then also suppose we can take a map
T
such that
f\circ T^{n}
are IID. By the Borel-Cantelli Lemma, this will allow us to conclude that for a.e.
x
,
f(T^{N+1}x)>w(N)
occurs for infinitely many
N
. Finding such a
T
may not work for
f
on
X
, but we can certainly get many instances of the above by changing our measure space
X
to
\prod\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\{0,1\}
, the usual product measure
\prod\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mu
, where
\mu(\{0\})=\mu(\{1\})=1/2
, and letting
T
be the shift map. Then we take any
f\in L^{1}
as above and replace it by
f_{0}=f\circ\pi_{0}
where
\pi_{0}
is the projection of
\prod\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\{0,1\}
onto the coordinate with
n=0
.

The conclusion above is that when
w(N)
increases to
\infty
but
w(N)/N\to 0
as
N\to\infty
, then there are many functions
f
and ergodic maps
T
such that for a.e.
x
, both
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)>\frac{w(N)}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
and

\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)<-\frac{w(N)}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
. Indeed, this is the typical case. We take here
\mathcal{M}
to be the invertible, measure-preserving maps of
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
given the usual weak topology.

Proposition 5.21
.

Suppose
w(N)
is increasing to
\infty
and
w(N)/N\to 0
as
N\to\infty
. Then there is a dense
G_{\delta}
set
\mathcal{G}\subset L^{1}
such that for all
f\in\mathcal{G}
, there is a dense
G_{\delta}
set
M(f)\subset\mathcal{M}
of ergodic maps
T
such that
for a.e.
x
, we have both
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)>\frac{w(N)}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
infinitely often and

\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)<-\frac{w(N)}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
infinitely often.

Proof.

We know that
f
is integrable on a probability space if and only if

\sum\limits_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu\big\{|f|\geq N\big\}<\infty.

It is not hard to see this is the best possible since if
\rho_{N}\to 0
, then for a dense
G_{\delta}
set of functions in
L^{1}
, we have
\displaystyle\sum_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu\big\{|f|>\rho_{N}\cdot N\big\}=\infty
. So if
w_{N}>0
and
w_{N}/N\to 0
, taking this as
\rho_{N}
, we see that for a dense
G_{\delta}
set of functions
\displaystyle\sum_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu\big\{|f|>w_{N}\big\}=\infty
.

A little more work will show even more, as stated in the proof: for a dense
G_{\delta}
set of functions, both
\displaystyle\sum_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu\big\{f>2w_{N}\big\}=\infty
and
\displaystyle\sum_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu\big\{f<-2w_{N}\big\}=\infty
.

We take such a class
\mathcal{F}
as above in
L^{1}
. We will use the argument of
[
20
, Theorem 4.3]
. However, here we will use
B_{N}=\{f>2w_{N}\}
and
B_{N}=\{f<-2w_{N}\}
, respectively, and use
T
to denote the transformation, not
\tau
.

By Theorem 4.3 of
[
20
]
, for all the
f\in\mathcal{F}
, we have a dense
G_{\delta}
set of maps
T
such that for a.e.
x
,
T^{N}x\in B_{N}
infinitely often, for both types of
B_{N}
.

Now we use the analysis before
Proposition

5.21
to both of the following inequalities:

\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)>\frac{w_{N}}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)\text{ and }\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(T^{n}x)<-\frac{w_{N}}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x).

Because we have used
2w_{N}
in defining the
B_{N}
, we have a little more room in the inequalities. So this gives us the divergent series results as stated in
Proposition

5.21
.
∎

Remark 5.22
.

It is worth noting that if
p>1
, then
L^{p}
is first category in
L^{1}
, and indeed the complement
L^{1}\backslash L^{p}
is a dense
G_{\delta}
. So if we ask for the generic behavior for

\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu\{|f|>w_{n}\}=\infty,\text{ and }w_{n}=n^{1/p},

we can use this.

However, for more general
w_{n}
with
w_{n}/n\to 0
, something more is needed. Probably, in this case there is always an increasing
\phi:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that
\phi(n)\geq w_{n}
and yet still
\phi(n)/n\to 0
. Then one argues that the
f\in L^{1}
such that
\phi^{-1}(|f|)
is not integrable is a generic set. So generically we would have

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu\big\{|f|\geq w_{n}\big\}\geq\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu\big\{|f|\geq\phi(n)\big\}=\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu\big\{\phi^{-1}(|f|)\geq n\big\}=\infty.

We use
\phi
and consider an Orlicz space of functions where
\phi^{-1}(|f|)
is integrable and show that it is first category in
L^{1}
, with a dense
G_{\delta}
complement, just as is done in the special cases where
\phi(n)=n^{1/p}
.

Now, we will deduce some quantitative results about the non-monotonicity of subsequences of averages by using some strong sweeping-out results of moving averages.

Write

(5.19)

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{[0,N_{i+1})}f(T^{n}x)

\displaystyle-\mathbb{A}_{[0,N_{i})}f(T^{n}x)

(5.20)

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i},N_{i+1})}f(T^{n}x)-\mathbb{A}_{[0,N_{i})}f(T^{n}x)\Big)\dfrac{N_{i+1}-N_{i}}{N_{i+1}}.

If we let
l_{i}=N_{i+1}-N_{i}
, then we get

\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i},N_{i+1})}f(T^{n}x)=\frac{1}{l_{i}}\sum_{j\in[0,l_{i}-1]}f(T^{N_{i}+j}x).

We introduce a sequence of operators
M_{i}:L^{1}\to L^{1}
as follows

(5.21)

M_{i}f(x)=\frac{1}{l_{i}}\sum_{j\in[0,l_{i}-1]}f(T^{N_{i}+j}x).

These operators are called the
moving averages
for the system
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu,T)
with parameters
(N_{i},l_{i})
.

Proposition 5.23
.

Assume that
T
is ergodic, and
(M_{i})
satisfies the strong sweeping out property. Then there is a residual set
\beta\subset\mathcal{B}
with the following property:

For any
E\in\beta
there exists a constant
d=d(E,N_{i})>0
such that for a.e.
x
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{[0,N_{i+1})}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)>\frac{d}{N_{i+1}}+\mathbb{A}_{[0,N_{i})}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)\text{ infinitely often,}

\displaystyle\text{ and for a.e. $x$, }\mathbb{A}_{[0,N_{i+1})}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)<-\frac{d}{N_{i+1}}+\mathbb{A}_{[0,N_{i})}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)\text{ infinitely often.}

The constant
d
can be taken to be
\frac{1}{2}\big(\min_{i}\{l_{i}\}\big)\cdot\big(\min\{\mu(E),1-\mu(E)\}\big).

Proof.

Let
d
be as mentioned in the proposition.
Since
T
is ergodic,

(5.22)

\mathbb{A}_{[0,N_{i})}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)\to\mu(E)\text{ for a.e. }x.

Suppose
(M_{i})
satisfies the strong sweeping out. Then, by using
[
14
, Theorem 1.2]
and observing that
\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i},N_{i+1})}f(T^{n}x)=M_{i}(x)
, we can find a residual set
\beta\subset\mathcal{B}
such that for every
E\in\beta
,

(5.23)

\limsup_{i}\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i},N_{i+1})}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)=1\text{ for a.e. }x

and

(5.24)

\liminf_{i}\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i},N_{i+1})}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)=0\text{ for a.e. }x.

Let
E\in\beta
. Let
\tilde{X}
be the common set of full measure where (
5.22
), (
5.23
) and (
5.24
) hold.
Hence, for
x\in\tilde{X}
, there are two subsequences
(N_{i_{j}})=(N_{i_{j}})(x)
and
(N_{i_{k}})=(N_{i_{k}})(x)
such that

(5.25)

\displaystyle\lim_{j}\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i_{j}}+l_{i_{j}})}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)=1

and

(5.26)

\displaystyle\lim_{k}\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i_{k}},N_{i_{k}}+l_{i_{k}})}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)=0.

Thus we have

(5.27)

\displaystyle\lim_{j}\Big(\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i_{j}},N_{i_{j}}+l_{i_{j}})}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)-\mathbb{A}_{N_{i_{j}}}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)\Big)

\displaystyle=(1-\mu(E))\text{ and }

(5.28)

\displaystyle\lim_{k}\Big(\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i_{k}},N_{i_{k}}+l_{i_{k}})}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)-\mathbb{A}_{N_{i_{k}}}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)\Big)

\displaystyle=(0-\mu(E)).

By (
5.19
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{N_{i_{j}+1}}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)-\mathbb{A}_{N_{i_{j}}}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)

\displaystyle=\Big(\mathbb{A}_{[N_{i_{j}},N_{i_{j}}+l_{i_{j}})}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)-\mathbb{A}_{N_{i_{j}}}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)\Big)\dfrac{l_{i_{j}}}{N_{i_{j}+1}}

By using (
5.27
), we get

\displaystyle\Big(\mathbb{A}_{N_{i_{j}+1}}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)-\mathbb{A}_{N_{i_{j}}}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)\Big)

\displaystyle\to(1-\mu(E))\dfrac{l_{i_{j}}}{N_{i_{j}+1}}\text{ as }j\to\infty.

Thus for large
j
,

\displaystyle\Big(\mathbb{A}_{N_{i_{j}+1}}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)-\mathbb{A}_{N_{i_{j}}}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)\Big)

\displaystyle\geq\dfrac{d}{N_{i_{j}+1}}.

Similarly, using (
5.19
) and (
5.28
), we get

\displaystyle\Big(\mathbb{A}_{N_{i_{k}+1}}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)-\mathbb{A}_{N_{i_{k}}}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)\Big)

\displaystyle\leq-\dfrac{d}{N_{i_{k}+1}}.

This finishes the proof.
∎

We say a sequence
(N_{i})
is sublacunary if it satisfies the following

\lim_{i\to\infty}\dfrac{N_{i+1}}{N_{i}}=1.

Corollary 5.24

Assume that

and

with the following property:

For any

there exists a constant

such that for a.e.
x
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{[0,N_{i+1})}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)

\displaystyle>\frac{d}{N_{i+1}}+\mathbb{A}_{[0,N_{i})}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)\text{ infinitely often,}

\displaystyle\text{ and for a.e. $x$, }\mathbb{A}_{[0,N_{i+1})}

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)<-\frac{d}{N_{i+1}}+\mathbb{A}_{[0,N_{i})}\mathbbm{1}_{E}(T^{n}x)\text{ infinitely often.}

Proof.

This result follows from
[
22
, Theorem 2.5]
and
Proposition

5.23
.

6.
Fluctuation of convolution operators

Given
\phi\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
and a suitable function
f
, the
convolution of
\phi
and
f
will be denoted by
\phi\star f(x)=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(y)f(x-y)d\lambda(y)
.
The first basic general principle is this one.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
B=L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
for some fixed

1\leq p\leq\infty
, or
B=CB(\mathbb{R})
.
Assume that
(\phi_{n})
is a
proper approximate identity. Let
(\epsilon_{n})
be a sequence of positive
numbers with
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{n}=0
. Then
there is a dense
G_{\delta}
subset
\mathcal{A}

of
B
such that for all
f\in\mathcal{A}
,
\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{|\phi_{n}\star f(x)-f(x)|}{\epsilon_{n}}=\infty
a.e.

Proof.

We use Theorem 1.1 of
[
14
]
. Because our approximate identity is
normalized and asymptotically local, we can use this theorem, which
is stated for probability spaces, in the context of convolutions on

\mathbb{R}
. Indeed, what we show here is that for any interval
I=(a,b)
, there is a dense
G_{\delta}
subset
\mathcal{A}_{I}
in
B

such that for
f\in\mathcal{A}_{I}
,
\sup\limits_{n\geq 1}\frac{|\phi_{n}\star f(x)-f(x)|}{\epsilon_{n}}=\infty
a.e. on
I
. Intersecting these sets over
all rational numbers
a
and
b
gives us a set

\mathcal{A}
as in the statement
of the theorem.

First, consider the case that
B=L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
. To use
Theorem 1.1 of
[
14
]
, we need to show
that for any
K
and
\epsilon<\frac{1}{2}
, there exists

f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with
\|f\|_{1}\leq 1
such that

\lambda\big\{x\in I:\sup\limits_{n\geq 1}\frac{|\phi_{n}\star f-f|}{\epsilon_{n}}\geq K\big\}\geq\lambda(I)-\epsilon.

For now also assume that
I=(0,1)
.
Then, taking
M
to be a whole number,
let
~{f(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda(I)}\frac{\sin(Mx)}{|\sin(Mx)|}\mathbbm{1}_{I}(x)}
except
when
\sin(Mx)=0
, and then let
f(x)=1
.
We see that
\|f\|_{1}\leq 1
.
Fix
N\geq 1
, and choose
M
sufficiently large such that the rapid
fluctuation of
f
causes
\|\phi_{n}\star f\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon
for
1\leq n\leq N
.
So for a fixed
n,1\leq n\leq N
, if
\epsilon_{n}\leq\frac{1}{2K}
,
this gives
\frac{|\phi_{n}\star f-f|}{\epsilon_{n}}\geq K
a.e.
on
I
because
|\phi_{n}\star f-f|\geq 1-\epsilon\geq\frac{1}{2}
.
So certainly we can construct
f,\|f\|_{1}\leq 1
, with
\lambda\big\{x\in I:\sup\limits_{n\geq 1}\frac{|\phi_{n}\star f-f|}{\epsilon_{n}}\geq K\big\}\geq\lambda(I)-\epsilon
.
By translating and dilating
the construction of
f
, we can achieve the
same result for any interval
I
, although the choice of
M
will
need to take into account the size of
I
.

The same argument works in any
L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
with some small
changes. If
1\leq p<\infty
, we take
f(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda(I)^{1/p}}\frac{\sin(Mx)}{|\sin(Mx)|}\mathbbm{1}_{I}(x)
, and proceed
as above. For

L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
the normalizing factor is taken to be
1
,
not
\frac{1}{\lambda(I)^{1/p}}
. A little
more care in the construction of
f
is needed when
B=CB(\mathbb{R})
, since we must make sure that the function is continuous. In this
case, again letting
I=(0,1)
at the outset, take

g\in CB(\mathbb{R})
to be a
piecewise linear function such that
g=1
on
(0,\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)
,
g=-1
on
(\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon,1)
, and
g=0
otherwise.
Then let
f(x)=g(Mx)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
.
As we decrease

\epsilon
and increase
M
, the increased fluctuation in
f
on
I

will allow us to have simultaneously
|\phi_{n}\star f|\leq\epsilon
for
all
n,1\leq n\leq N
, and
|f|=1
on some
J\subset I
with
\lambda(J)\geq 1-\epsilon
. Again, since
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{n}=0
,
this gives the desired estimate on the function on
(0,1)
. By
translating and dilating the construction of
f
, we can achieve the
the same result for any interval
I
.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

a)
Theorem
6.1
is really meant to address the
case where
(\phi_{n}\star f)
converges to
f
a.e. for all
f\in B
.
If not, having a ratio divergence does not have the same
value. But even in this situation, the fact that the divergence
could occur for different functions because of different
very sparse subsequences of
(\phi_{n})
suggests that even in this case
the theorem tells us something worthwhile.

b)
One can possibly think that dividing by a sequence
of constants
\epsilon_{n}
that tend to
0
is too strict, but rather
one should look for a sequence of positive functions
(\epsilon_{n}(x))
that
converges to
0
a.e. to gauge the rate of convergence of the convolutions.
However, if
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{n}(x)=0
a.e.,
then for any
\epsilon>0
and any bounded interval
I
, there
is
J\subset I
such that
\lambda(I\backslash J)<\epsilon
and

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\epsilon_{n}(x)=0
uniformly on
J
.
Using this, the argument in Theorem
6.1

shows that there is a dense
G_{\delta}
of functions
f
in
B

such that
\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{|\phi_{n}\star f(x)-f(x)|}{\epsilon_{n}(x)}=\infty
a.e.

c)
We could also develop other types of measures of the
rate of convergence of
(\phi_{n}\star f)
as was done in the ergodic
theory context in
[
14
, Theorem 1.1]
using the principle
formulated there. For example,
even in the best situation, where
\phi_{n}=n\mathbbm{1}_{[-1/n,0]},n\geq 1
,
as long as a sequence of positive numbers
(w_{n})
has

\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}w_{n}=\infty
, the series

\sum\limits_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}w_{n}(\phi_{n}\star f-f)
will diverge almost
everywhere for a dense
G_{\delta}
set of functions in
B
. The
proof of this and related variations on this theme are reasonably
straightforward adaptations of the ergodic theoretic results that are
given in
[
14
]
.

d)
It is clear that there are non-trivial functions,
even continuous functions, such that we do not have any such
divergence. Having a characterization of this, or at least some simple constructions, would be
worthwhile.

With a little more attention to the construction, we can improve
Theorem
6.1
to prove
Theorem

3.12
.

Proof of
Theorem

3.12
.

The mechanics of the proof are the same as for the proof of

6.1
.

B=L^{1}(\mathbb{R})

Take a set of positive numbers

Let

\displaystyle\frac{\phi_{n_{j}}\star f-f}{\epsilon_{n_{j}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\phi_{n_{j}}\star(a_{j}r_{m_{j}})-a_{j}r_{m_{j}}}{\epsilon_{n_{j}}}

\displaystyle-\sum\limits_{l\in[j-1]}\frac{\phi_{n_{j}}\star(a_{l}r_{m_{l}})-a_{l}r_{m_{l}}}{\epsilon_{n_{j}}}-\sum\limits_{l\in[j+1,L]}\frac{\phi_{n_{j}}\star(a_{l}r_{m_{l}})-a_{l}r_{m_{l}}}{\epsilon_{n_{j}}}.

\frac{\phi_{n_{j}}\star(a_{j}r_{m_{j}})-a_{j}r_{m_{j}}}{\epsilon_{n_{j}}}

on

E_{n_{j}}
.

. But now the sum

\sum\limits_{l\in[j+1,L]}\frac{\phi_{n_{j}}\star(a_{l}r_{m_{l}})-a_{l}r_{m_{l}}}{\epsilon_{n_{j}}}

comes from terms chosen later in the inductive construction, and it could
affect that
estimate that we have made already. However, we do have the freedom to
place restrictions on the values
of

making them
sufficiently small, without disturbing the
necessary fact that

\sum\limits_{l\in[L]}a_{l}\leq 1
, so we can arrange that

\sum\limits_{l\in[j+1,L]}\frac{\phi_{n_{j}}\star(a_{l}r_{m_{l}})-a_{l}r_{m_{l}}}{\epsilon_{n_{j}}}

is uniformly less than
\frac{K}{2}
on

. In summary, on
\widetilde{E_{m_{j}}}
,
we have

\frac{\phi_{n_{j}}\star f-f}{\epsilon_{n_{j}}}\geq\frac{3K}{2}
.
In this inductive step, we also assume, of course, that we have
chosen
a_{j}
so that
a_{j}>0
and

\sum\limits_{l\in[j]}a_{l}<1
.
This ends the inductive step. We notice that at the
j
th stage
of the induction we are revising the restrictions
on
(a_{l}:l=j+1,\dots,L)
that were imposed from earlier steps in
the induction to make the future choices of
a_{l}
even smaller than
before.
In any case, we can choose the sets
F_{n_{j}}
of sufficiently
small measure so that
the function
f
has
\|f\|_{1}\leq 1

and
\lambda\big\{x\in I:\sup\limits_{n\geq 1}\frac{\phi_{n}\star f(x)-f(x)}{\epsilon_{n}}\geq K\big\}\geq\lambda(I)-\epsilon
.

We now know that there is a dense

G_{\delta}

set
\mathcal{A}^{+}
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
such that for all

we have

\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\phi_{n}\star f-f}{\epsilon_{n}}=\infty

almost everywhere.
By working with the sets
\{x\in I:r_{m}(x)=1\}
, we could
similarly arrange
to have this companion lemma:
for all
K>0
and
\epsilon>0
, there
exists some function
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
with
\|f\|_{1}\leq 1
such that

\lambda\{x\in I:\inf\limits_{n\geq 1}\frac{\phi_{n}\star f(x)f(x)}{\epsilon_{n}}\leq-K\}\geq\lambda(I)-\epsilon
. Then we can conclude that there is a
dense
G_{\delta}

set
\mathcal{A}^{-}

in

such that for all

\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{\phi_{n}\star f-f}{\epsilon_{n}}=-\infty

almost everywhere. The intersection

gives the desired dense
G_{\delta}
set.

The case where
B=L^{p}(\mathbb{R})
is handled similarly except for the
normalization inherent in the choice of
(a_{l}:l=1,\dots,L)
. The case of

requires more care as before, because the functions we construct for our

be continuous. But there is not much difficulty in handling this, in the
same
way that it was dealt with in Theorem

, at the expense
of having a somewhat large set

to deal with, albeit one that can be

to have measure as small as we like at any stage of the construction.

Proof of
Theorem

3.13
.

We can prove the generic behavior occurs above in the following fashion.
First, fix
l\geq 1
and
N_{0}\geq 1
. Then let
\mathcal{O}_{l,N_{0}}
consist of all

G\in L^{1}[0,1]
such that
\lambda\{x:E(G|\mathcal{D}_{n})(x)>G(x)\,\text{for some}\,\,n,N_{0}\leq n\}>1-\frac{1}{l}
.
By using a similar argument as in
Theorem

3.9
, one can check that
\mathcal{O}_{l,N_{0}}
is open in the
L^{1}
-norm topology. Suppose we
show that it is dense in that topology too. Then
\mathcal{O}^{+}=\bigcap\limits_{l=1}^{\infty}\bigcap\limits_{N_{0}=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{O}_{l,N_{0}}
is a dense
G_{\delta}
set. Clearly, any

has the property that for a.e.

, there are infinitely many

with

. But now
let
\mathcal{O}^{-}=\{G:-G\in\mathcal{O}^{+}\}
. Then this is also a dense
G_{\delta}
set, and

G\in\mathcal{O}^{-}

has the property that for a.e.
x
, there is some
n
with
E(G|\mathcal{D}_{n})(x)<G(x)
.
Hence, any function
G
in the dense
G_{\delta}
set
\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}^{+}\cap\mathcal{O}^{-}
will have the fluctuation
property that we claimed.

So what we want to prove is that each
\mathcal{O}_{l,N_{0}}
is a dense set in the
L^{1}
-norm topology.
So fix a function
G\in L^{1}[0,1]
. Then, for any fixed
N_{0}
, we
want to construct a function
F\in L^{1}[0,1]
such that
\|G-F\|_{1}
is
small and such that the conditional expectations

fluctuate around the limit
F
on most of
[0,1]
, once we take a large number

and consider all
n,N_{0}\leq n\leq N
.

Here is what we do. First, for
any
\epsilon>0
, we can find

M
sufficiently large and
F_{0}
which is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{D}_{M}

and such that
\|G-F_{0}\|_{1}<\epsilon
. Now let
\delta>0
.
Then we consider the Rademacher functions
r_{k}
with
k\geq 0
. Let

\Sigma_{N}=\sum\limits_{k\in[0,N]}r_{k}
. We take

F=F_{0}+\delta\Sigma_{N}
. For
n\geq M
, we have

E(F|\mathcal{D}_{n})=F_{0}+\delta E(\Sigma_{N}|\mathcal{D}_{n})
. Now fix in addition

N_{0}
such that
M\leq N_{0}
without loss of generality.
For
N
sufficiently large, we will show that
E(\Sigma_{N}|\mathcal{D}_{n})
fluctuates
around its limit
\Sigma_{N}
on most
of
[0,1]
, with
N_{0}\leq n\leq N
. Since
M\leq N_{0}\leq n
, we have

E(F|\mathcal{D}_{n})=F_{0}+\delta E(\Sigma_{N}|\mathcal{D}_{n})
. So we have
also

fluctuating in the same manner around its limit
F
.
Since below we will be fixing
N
, even though it is large, we can now choose
\delta
sufficiently
small that
\|G-F\|_{1}\leq 2\epsilon
. This gives us the approximation of
G
by
suitably fluctuating conditional expectations that we need to prove the Baire category theorem - that
the dyadic martingale is fluctuating generically.

Let us now look more closely at what the Rademacher functions do for us here.
We have
r_{0}=\mathbbm{1}_{[0,1/2]}-\mathbbm{1}_{[1/2,1]}
, and
r_{k}(x)=r_{0}(\{2^{k}x\})=r_{0}(2^{k}x\mod 1)
for
all

, and
k\geq 0
. So
r_{k}
is measurable with respect to
\mathcal{D}_{k+1}
.
Also, for
1\leq n\leq k
, we have

. The Rademacher functions
are IID mean-zero functions taking the values of
\pm 1
. So the functions
n\to\Sigma_{n}=\sum\limits_{k\in[0,n]}r_{k}
are a model for the classical symmetric random walk on the integers.

So now consider
the conditional expectations
E(\Sigma_{N}|\mathcal{D}_{n})
. For
n\geq N+1
, we would
have all
r_{k},0\leq k\leq N
measurable with respect to
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, and hence

E(\Sigma_{N}|\mathcal{D}_{n})=\Sigma_{N}
. Our goal is to show that for all

N_{0}
and
\eta>0
, there is
N\geq N_{0}
large enough such
that for all
x\in[0,1]
except for a set of measure smaller than
\eta
, we have

(7.1)

E(\Sigma_{N}|\mathcal{D}_{n})(x)>\Sigma_{N}(x)\,\text{for some}\,\,n,N_{0}\leq n\leq N\text{ and }

(7.2)

E(\Sigma_{N}|\mathcal{D}_{n})(x)<\Sigma_{N}(x)\,\text{for some (other)}\,n,N_{0}\leq n\leq N.

By the definition of
r_{k}
and
\mathcal{D}_{n}
, we actually have

E(\Sigma_{N}|\mathcal{D}_{n})=\sum\limits_{k\in[0,n-1]}r_{k}
. So we are asking for
fluctuation of
\sum\limits_{k\in[0,n-1]}r_{k}
around
\sum\limits_{k\in[0,N]}r_{k}

using values of
n,N_{0}\leq n\leq N
. Subtracting
E(\Sigma_{N}|\mathcal{D}_{n})(x)=\sum\limits_{k\in[0,n-1]}r_{k}(x)
from
both sides of the inequalities in (
7.1
) and (
7.2
)
we are asking for the fluctuation of

\sum\limits_{k\in[n,N]}r_{k}
around
0
.
This is like
the fluctuation of the symmetric random walk around
0
. Here what we need to
use is that the terms
r_{k}
are IID mean-zero functions taking the values of
\pm 1
,
and so for large enough
N
, even though we will be restricting
n,N_{0}\leq n\leq N
,
we have
\sum\limits_{k\in[n,N]}r_{k}
fluctuating around
0
on
[0,1]
except for a set of measure less than
\eta
.  ∎

Remark 7.1
.

There is clearly an index, aka“time reversal”, occurring here. If we were dealing with

looking instead at where
\sum\limits_{k\in[n,N]}r_{k}
fluctuates on most of the measure space as

gets large,
if we take

with

.

8.
Uniformly distributed sequences

Next, we consider the non-monotonicity and fluctuation of averages of uniformly distributed sequences. We generally can take a sequence
\mathsf{x}:=(x_{n})
in
[0,1]
that is
uniformly distributed
. That is, for every continuous
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{C}
, the averages
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{n\in[N]}f(x_{n})\to\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\,dt
as
N\to\infty
. The classical example closest to ergodic averages is where we take
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
irrational and let
x_{n}
be given by the fractional parts
\{n\theta\}
for all
n\geq 1
. As usual, if we let
Tx=\{\theta+x\}
for
x\in[0,1]
, then
T
is ergodic and
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{n\in[N]}f(T^{n}x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{n\in[N]}f(\{n\theta+x\})
. For this average, we could take
f\in L^{1}([0,1],\lambda)
where
\lambda
is the normalized Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
. However, when we restrict
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)
to
x=0
, then we have to also restrict
f
, for example by assuming it is continuous, in order that we would have convergence of the averages
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{n\in[N]}f(\{n\theta\})
. For simplicity, let
I=\int_{0}^{1}f(t)\,dt
.

As in ergodic dynamical systems, we have some basic inequalities.
Suppose
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(\mathsf{x})\leq\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})
. Then

\frac{1}{N+1}f(x_{N+1})\leq\Big(\frac{1}{N}-\frac{1}{N+1}\Big)\sum\limits_{n\in[N]}f(x_{n})=\frac{1}{N(N+1)}\sum\limits_{n\in[N]}f(x_{n}).

This means that
f(x_{N+1})\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{n\in[N]}f(x_{n})=\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})
. Similarly, if
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(\mathsf{x})\geq\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})
then
f(x_{N+1})\geq\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})
.

These basic inequalities give the following analogue to
Proposition

3.1

Proposition 8.1
.

Suppose a continuous
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is not a constant function. Then for infinitely many
N
we have
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(\mathsf{x})>\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})
, and for infinitely many
N
we have
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(\mathsf{x})<\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(\mathsf{x})
. Hence, the averages
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})
are not eventually monotonically increasing or eventually monotonically decreasing.

Proof.

Since
f
is not a constant, there must be some
\delta>0
such that
f\geq\delta+I
on some non-empty open interval
I
, and
f\leq-\delta+I
on some non-empty open interval
J
.

Assume
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(\mathsf{x})\leq\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})
holds for all large enough values of
N
. This means
f(x_{N+1})\leq\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})
for all large enough
N
. But
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})\to I
as
N\to\infty
. Hence, for all large enough
N
,
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})\leq\delta/2+I
. However, we have
x_{N+1}\in I
for infinitely many
N
. So
\delta+I\leq f(x_{N+1})
for infinitely many
N
. If we put these inequalities together, we get values of
N
such that
\delta+I\leq f(x_{N+1})\leq\delta/2+I
. Then
\delta\leq\delta/2
, which is not possible.

Similarly, assume
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(\mathsf{x})\geq\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})
holds for all large enough values of
N
. This means that
f(x_{N+1})\geq\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})
for all large enough
N
. But
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})\to I
as
N\to\infty
. Hence, for all large enough
N
,
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})\geq-\delta/2+I
. However, we have
x_{N+1}\in J
for infinitely many
N
. So
-\delta+I\geq f(x_{N+1})
for infinitely many
N
. If we put these inequalities together, we get values of
N
such that
-\delta+I\geq f(x_{N+1})\geq-\delta/2+I
. Then
-\delta\geq-\delta/2
, which is not possible.
∎

As in
Proposition

3.2
, we can prove this more detailed result.

Proposition 8.2
.

Suppose
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is not a constant function. Then there exists
d>0
such that we have
\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(\mathsf{x})>\frac{d}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})
infinitely often, and

\mathbb{A}_{[N+1]}f(\mathsf{x})<-\frac{d}{N}+\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})
infinitely often.

Proposition

8.1
answers one possible question about how
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})
behaves as
N\to\infty
. But it does not address whether or not there are infinitely many sign changes for
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})-I
. In fact, as we will see, infinitely many sign changes may or may not occur, depending on the function
f
and the context of the dynamics. It is not clear how to characterize when it does occur in general.

We consider the special case of
\mathsf{x}=\{x_{n}\}_{n}=\{n\theta\}_{n}
for an irrational
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
. In addition, we take the special case of a continuous coboundary
f
. This is a continuous function
f(t)=F(t)-F(\{t+\theta\})
for some continuous

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{n\in[N]}f(\{n\theta\})=\frac{1}{N}\Bigl(F(\{\theta\})-F(\{(N+1)\theta\})\Bigr).

While this converges to

changes sign infinitely often.

As we argued for fluctuation in the Cesàro-average case, with suitable hypotheses on the transfer function we can argue for fluctuation in the case of averages of uniformly distributed sequences.

Proposition 8.3
.

Suppose a continuous, real-valued function
f
is a coboundary with the transfer function
F
that is continuous, real-valued, and not a constant.

(1)

Suppose that
F(\{\theta\})
is the maximum (or minimum) value of

\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})
converge to

as

, but
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})\geq 0
(or
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})\leq 0

.

(2)

Suppose that
F(\{\theta\})
is not a maximum (or minimum) value for

. Then the averages
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})
converge to
0
as
N\to\infty
and
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})
is infinitely often less (or greater) than
0
.

Remark 8.4
.

We have taken advantage of

being a coboundary to get a simpler expression for
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(\mathsf{x})
. This has allowed us to obtain

Suppose

is continuous and

On the other hand, as in

Proposition 8.6

9.
General weighted averages in ergodic theory

Here we take a dissipative sequence
\mu_{N}
of probability measures on
\mathbb{Z}
. Our operators
\mu_{N}^{T}f=\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\mu_{N}(n)f\circ T^{n}
. Now we ask when these operators always fluctuate. Because we are not assuming there is pointwise a.e. convergence, or even perhaps
L^{1}
-norm convergence, there is no clear limit about which we could consider fluctuation. However, in the case that there is norm convergence, fluctuation around the limit will be worthwhile to consider.

Conjecture 9.1
.

Let
\mu_{N}^{T}
be a dissipative sequence of operators such that
\mu_{N}^{T}f\to\int f
in
L^{1}
-norm for every
f\in L^{1}
. Then there is a dense
G_{\delta}
set
\mathcal{O}\subset L^{1}
such that for every
f\in\mathcal{O}
, the averages
\mu_{N}^{T}f(x)
fluctuate infinitely often around
\int f
for almost every
x\in X
.

The fluctuation behavior of the classical averages clearly depended on the fact that these are Cesàro averages. It may be worthwhile to ask when similar behavior holds for other methods of averaging like the ones above.

Example 9.2
.

Consider an ergodic map
T
that has
-1
as an eigenvalue. Let
f\in L^{1}
be mean-zero eigenvalue for
-1
, so that
f+f\circ T=0
. Hence,
\mathbb{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}f(Tx):=\frac{1}{2}\Big(f(Tx)+f(T^{2}x\Big)=0
. But this does not remain so, indeed for even
N
,
\mathbb{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)=0
but for odd
N
,
\mathbb{A}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{[N]}f(T^{n}x)=-\frac{1}{N}f
. So the averages converge to
0
, but not monotone, and they do not switch sign infinitely often.

However, if we use a different averaging method we can get a very different result. Take
\mathbb{A}^{b}_{[N]}f=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum\limits_{n\in[N]}{N\choose n}f\circ T^{n}
for all
N\geq 1
. These averages do converge in
L^{1}
-norm to
\int f\,d\lambda
for all
f\in L^{1}
, although they do not converge a.e. in general. See
[
18
]
. And we certainly can have monotonicity since we have
\mathbb{A}^{b}_{[N]}f=0
for all
N
when
f\circ T=-f
as above. Of course, we do not have fluctuation of the sign.
∎

This example makes it clear that whether, and when, we have monotonicity of the averages depends on the method of averaging that we use. Moreover, for which
f
and
T
we have fluctuation of the values about the mean will also depend on the averaging method used.

On the other hand, we can get some similar results to what happens using the classical ergodic averages, at least when using more general Cesàro averages. This is in line with what was done in Section
8
, where general uniformly distributed sequences were used instead of just the classical sequence
(\{n\theta\}:n\geq 1)
with
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
irrational.

Proposition 9.3
.

Consider an increasing sequence
(m_{n}:n\geq 1)
in
\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Assume that for all the
f\in L^{2}
, averages
\mathbb{A}_{[N]}f(T^{m_{n}}x):=\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{n\in[N]}f(T^{m_{n}}x)
converge to
\int f\,d\lambda
a.e. Then for a non-constant
f\in L^{2}
, for a.e.
x
