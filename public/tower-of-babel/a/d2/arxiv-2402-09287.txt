Abstract
. We study the real and imaginary parts of the powers of the Volterra operator on L 2 ​ [ 0 , 1 ] L^{2}[0,1] , specifically their eigenvalues, their norms and their numerical ranges.

Primary 47G10; Secondary 47A10, 47A12, 47A30

1.
Introduction

Let
V:L^{2}[0,1]\to L^{2}[0,1]
be the Volterra operator, defined by

Vf(x):=\int_{0}^{x}f(t)\,dt.

It is well known that
V
is a compact quasi-nilpotent operator,
whose is adjoint given by

V^{*}f(x)=\int_{x}^{1}f(t)\,dt\quad(f\in L^{2}[0,1]).

The powers
V^{n}
have been the object of numerous articles over the years. A simple proof by induction shows that, for all
n\geq 1
and all
f\in L^{2}[0,1]
, we have

(1)

V^{n}f(x)=\int_{0}^{x}\frac{(x-t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}f(t)\,dt\quad\text{and}\quad V^{*n}f(x)=\int_{x}^{1}\frac{(t-x)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}f(t)\,dt.

Rather less seems to be known about the real and imaginary parts of these powers,
namely

\operatorname{Re}V^{n}:=(V^{n}+V^{*n})/2
and

\operatorname{Im}V^{n}:=(V^{n}-V^{*n})/2i
.
It follows from (
1
) that, for all
f\in L^{2}[0,1]
,

(2)

\operatorname{Re}V^{n}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|x-t|^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}f(t)\,dt

and

(3)

\operatorname{Im}V^{n}f(x)=\frac{1}{2i}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|x-t|^{n-1}\sign(x-t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}f(t)\,dt.

The operators
\operatorname{Re}V^{n}
and
\operatorname{Im}V^{n}
form the subject of the present work,
in which we study their eigenvalues, their norms and their numerical ranges.

2.
Eigenvalues

As remarked in the introduction,
V
is a quasinilpotent operator,
so
V^{n}
can have no non-zero eigenvalues. In fact
V
is injective, so
zero is not an eigenvalue of
V^{n}
either.

The situation is quite different for
\operatorname{Re}V^{n}
and
\operatorname{Im}V^{n}
.
These are compact self-adjoint operators, so,
as they are non-zero, they certainly have non-zero eigenvalues.
Moreover, since
V
is a Hilbert–Schmidt operator, the
same is true for each of
\operatorname{Re}V^{n}
and
\operatorname{Im}V^{n}
,
so their sets of eigenvalues are square-summable sequences.
Our first result shows that, for certain values of
n
,
even more is true.

Theorem 2.1
.

If
n
is odd, then
\operatorname{Re}V^{n}
has at most
n
non-zero eigenvalues.
If
n
is even, then
\operatorname{Im}V^{n}
has at most
n
non-zero eigenvalues.

This result is an immediate consequence of the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
n\geq 1
.
The eigenvalues of
\operatorname{Re}(i^{n-1}V^{n})
are
0
, together with the solutions
\lambda
of
the equation
\det(A-\lambda B)=0
,
where
A=(a_{jk})_{j,k=0}^{n-1}
and
B=(b_{jk})_{j,k=0}^{n-1}
are the
n\times n
matrices given by

a_{jk}:=\frac{i^{n-1}}{(j+n-k)!}\quad\text{and}\quad b_{jk}:=\begin{cases}2/(j-k)!,&j\geq k,\\
0,&j<k.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
\lambda
be a non-zero eigenvalue of
\operatorname{Re}(i^{n-1}V^{n})
with eigenvector
f
.
Then

(4)

\frac{i^{n-1}}{2}(V^{n}+(-1)^{n-1}V^{*n})f=\lambda f.

This expresses
f
as the sum of integrals of
L^{2}
functions,
so, changing
f
on a set of measure zero, we may suppose that
f
is continuous.
Using (
4
) to ‘bootstrap’, we deduce that in fact
f\in C^{\infty}
.

Differentiating (
4
)
n
times, we obtain

\lambda f^{(n)}=\frac{i^{n-1}}{2}(V^{n}f+(-1)^{n-1}V^{*n}f)^{(n)}=\frac{i^{n-1}}{2}(f+(-1)^{n-1}(-1)^{n}f)=0.

As
\lambda\neq 0
, it follows that
f
satisfies the differential equation

(5)

f^{(n)}=0.

Also, differentiating (
4
)
k
times and then setting
x=1
, we obtain
the boundary conditions

(6)

i^{n-1}V^{n-k}f(1)=2\lambda f^{(k)}(1)\quad(k=0,1,\dots,n-1).

The general solution of (
5
) is

f(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}c_{j}x^{j},

where
c_{0},c_{1},\dots,c_{n-1}\in\mathbb{C}
.
Substituting this expression into (
6
), we obtain

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{i^{n-1}c_{j}}{(j+1)\cdots(j+n-k)}=2\lambda\sum_{j=k}^{n-1}c_{j}j(j-1)\cdots(j-k+1)\quad(k=0,\dots,n-1).

After simplification, this becomes

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\frac{i^{n-1}}{(j+n-k)!}j!c_{j}=\lambda\sum_{j=k}^{n-1}\frac{2}{(j-k)!}j!c_{j}\quad(k=0,\dots,n-1),

in other words,
(A-\lambda B)v=0
, where
A,B
are the matrices defined in the statement of the theorem,
and where
v
is the vector with entries
v_{j}:=j!c_{j}~(j=0,\dots,n-1)
. The existence of a non-zero vector
v

satisfying the equation
(A-\lambda B)v=0
is equivalent to the condition that
(A-\lambda B)
be a singular matrix,
in other words, that
\det(A-\lambda B)=0
.
We conclude that the non-zero eigenvalues of
\operatorname{Re}(i^{n-1}V^{n})
are precisely the solutions of the equation

\det(A-\lambda B)=0
.

Finally, we remark that, by the spectral theorem, a compact self-adjoint operator with only finitely many non-zero eigenvalues has a kernel of finite codimension. Thus
\operatorname{Re}(i^{n-1}V^{n})
has a kernel of finite codimension in
L^{2}[0,1]
.
In particular, the kernel is non-zero, and so zero is also an eigenvalue of
\operatorname{Re}(i^{n-1}V^{n})
.
∎

For small values of
n
, Lemma
2.2
permits us to calculate the eigenvalues explicitly. The results of these calculations are summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.3
.

The non-zero eigenvalues of

•

\operatorname{Re}V
are
1/2
;

•

\operatorname{Im}V^{2}
are
\pm\sqrt{3}/12
;

•

\operatorname{Re}V^{3}
are
-1/24
and
1/48\pm\sqrt{5}/80
;

•

\operatorname{Im}V^{4}
are
\pm\sqrt{1575\pm 84\sqrt{345}}/5040
.

What about the remaining cases, namely
\operatorname{Re}V^{n}
when
n
is even and
\operatorname{Im}V^{n}
when
n
is odd?
Even when
n=1,2
, these cases turn out to be more complicated.

Theorem 2.4
.

The eigenvalues of
\operatorname{Im}V
are
\displaystyle\Bigl\{\frac{1}{(2n+1)\pi}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\Bigr\}
.

Theorem 2.5
.

The eigenvalues of
\operatorname{Re}(V^{2})
are

\Bigl\{\frac{1}{4t^{2}}:t>0,\,\frac{\coth t}{t}=1\Bigr\}\cup\Bigl\{-\frac{1}{4t^{2}}:t>0,\,\frac{\cot t}{t}=-1\Bigr\}\cup\Bigl\{-\frac{1}{(2n+1)^{2}\pi^{2}}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\Bigr\}.

We carry out the proof just for Theorem
2.5
. Theorem
2.4
is similar, but simpler.

Proof.

Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of
\operatorname{Re}V^{2}
with eigenvector
f
.

We first show that
\lambda\neq 0
.
Suppose, on the contrary, that
\frac{1}{2}(V^{2}+V^{*2})f=0
.
Since
Vf
and
V^{*}f
are continuous functions,
differentiation gives
\frac{1}{2}(Vf-V^{*}f)=0
, in other words,
\int_{0}^{x}f=\int_{x}^{1}f
for all
x\in[0,1]
.
This implies that
f=0
a.e. on
[0,1]
, a contradiction. Thus
\lambda\neq 0
, as claimed.

Since
\lambda
is real and non-zero, it can be written as
\lambda=1/\omega^{2}
, where

\omega
is either real or pure imaginary. The eigenvalue condition then becomes

(7)

f=\frac{\omega^{2}}{2}(V^{2}f+V^{*2}f).

Using the bootstrap technique already mentioned in the proof of Theorem
2.3
,
we see that
f\in C^{\infty}
.

Differentiating (
7
) twice leads to the differential equation

(8)

f^{\prime\prime}=\omega^{2}f.

Also, differentiating (
7
) once, and setting
x=0,1
, and adding,
we obtain the boundary condition

(9)

f^{\prime}(0)+f^{\prime}(1)=0.

Lastly, setting
x=0,1
in (
7
), adding and simplifying,
we obtain the second boundary condition

(10)

f(0)+f(1)=(1/2)(f^{\prime}(1)-f^{\prime}(0)).

The general solution to the differential equation (
8
) can be written as

f(x)=A\Bigl(e^{\omega x}+e^{\omega(1-x)}\Bigr)+B\Bigl(e^{\omega x}-e^{\omega(1-x)}\Bigr),

where
A,B
are complex constants. Substituting this into the boundary condition (
9
) gives

2B(\omega+\omega e^{\omega})=0,

and into (
10
) yields

2A(1+e^{\omega})=-A(\omega-\omega e^{\omega}).

Since
f\not\equiv 0
, at least one of
A,B
is non-zero. If
B\neq 0
, then
e^{\omega}=-1
, so
\omega=(2n+1)\pi i

for some integer
n
. The corresponding eigenvalue is

\lambda=-\frac{1}{(2n+1)^{2}\pi^{2}}.

If
A\neq 0
, then
(1+e^{\omega})=-(1/2)\omega(1-e^{\omega})
, which can be rewritten as

\frac{\coth(\omega/2)}{\omega/2}=1.

If, further,
\omega
is real, say
\omega=2t
, then

\lambda=\frac{1}{4t^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\coth t}{t}=1.

If, on the other hand,
\omega
is purely imaginary, say
\omega=2it
, then

\lambda=-\frac{1}{4t^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\cot t}{t}=-1.

This completes the list of eigenvalues of
\operatorname{Re}V^{2}
.
∎

We conclude this section with
two general results about the distribution
of the eigenvalues of
\operatorname{Re}V^{n}
and
\operatorname{Im}V^{n}
.

Theorem 2.6
.

For
n\geq 1
, the eigenvalues of
\operatorname{Im}V^{n}
are symmetrically distributed about
0
.

Proof.

Let
U:L^{2}[0,1]\to L^{2}[0,1]
be the unitary operator
Uf(x):=f(1-x)
.
A simple calculation shows that
V^{*}=U^{-1}VU
, whence
\operatorname{Im}V^{n}=-U^{-1}(\operatorname{Im}V^{n})U
.
As
\operatorname{Im}V^{n}
and
-\operatorname{Im}V^{n}
are conjugate, they have the same eigenvalues.
This gives the result.
∎

The examples in Theorem
2.3
and Theorem
2.5
show that there is no such symmetry result for
\operatorname{Re}V^{n}
.
Instead, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 2.7
.

The eigenvalue of
\operatorname{Re}V^{n}
of largest modulus is positive.

Proof.

As remarked in (
2
), for
f\in L^{2}[0,1]
we have

\operatorname{Re}V^{n}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|x-t|^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}f(t)\,dt.

In particular,
\operatorname{Re}V^{n}
is an integral operator on
L^{2}[0,1]

with non-negative
kernel. By the Krein–Rutnam theorem (see e.g.
[
1
, Theorem 7.10]
),
the spectral radius of such an operator is an eigenvalue.
The result follows.
∎

3.
Norms

According to an old result of Halmos
[
3
, Problem 188]
,
the operator norm of
V
is

\|V\|=2/\pi
. The values of
\|V^{2}\|
and
\|V^{3}\|
are more complicated, but they
can be expressed in terms of solutions to certain equations
(see e.g.
[
2
, p.1058]
).
For larger values of
n
, one has to be content with
numerical computations or inequalities.
It is known that, for all
n\geq 1
,
the operator norm
\|V^{n}\|
and Hilbert–Schmidt
norm
\|V^{n}\|_{\HS}
satisfy

(11)

\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2n+1)(2n-1)}}\leq\|V^{n}\|\leq\|V^{n}\|_{\HS}=\frac{1}{(n-1)!}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n(2n-1)}}.

Here the right-hand equality has been known since
[
4
]
.
The left-hand inequality was proved in
[
2
, Theorem 1.2]
.
The latter article also contains an account of the history
of this topic.

Our goal in this section is to establish analogous result for
\operatorname{Re}V^{n}
and
\operatorname{Im}V^{n}
. Since each of these is a compact self-adjoint operator,
its operator norm is simply the maximum modulus of its eigenvalues.
The results about eigenvalues established in §
2

therefore lead immediately to the values listed in Table
1
.

It is possible to continue further, using Theorem

,
but only at the cost of obtaining just numerical values.

We now turn to the problem of finding the analogues of the
inequality (
11
) for
\operatorname{Re}V^{n}
and
\operatorname{Im}V^{n}
.
Our first result treats their Hilbert–Schmidt norms,
which we can evaluate exactly.

Theorem 3.1
.

For all
n\geq 1
, we have

\|\operatorname{Re}V^{n}\|_{\HS}=\|\operatorname{Im}V^{n}\|_{\HS}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}(n-1)!}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n(2n-1)}}.

Proof.

As already remarked in (
2
) and (
3
),
for
f\in L^{2}[0,1]
we have

\operatorname{Re}V^{n}f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|x-t|^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}f(t)\,dt

and

\operatorname{Im}V^{n}f(x)=\frac{1}{2i}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|x-t|^{n-1}\sign(x-t)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}f(t)\,dt.

Since the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of an integral operator on
L^{2}[0,1]
is just the
L^{2}
-norm of its kernel on
[0,1]^{2}
, it follows that

\|\operatorname{Re}V^{n}\|_{\HS}^{2}=\|\operatorname{Im}V^{n}\|_{\HS}^{2}=\frac{1}{4(n-1)!^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(x-t)^{2n-2}\,dt\,dx.

Using the binomial theorem, it is easy to check that

(12)

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(x-t)^{2n-2}\,dt\,dx=\frac{1}{n(2n-1)}.

The result follows.
∎

Remark
.

Comparing the result of Theorem
3.1
with (
11
),
we see that

(13)

\|\operatorname{Re}V^{n}\|_{\HS}=\|\operatorname{Im}V^{n}\|_{\HS}=\frac{\|V^{n}\|_{\HS}}{\sqrt{2}}.

This could also be seen more directly, as follows.
Since the Hilbert–Schmidt norm is a Hilbert-space norm,
it satisfies the parallelogram identity, so

\|V^{n}+V^{*n}\|_{\HS}^{2}+\|V^{n}-V^{*n}\|_{\HS}^{2}=2\|V^{n}\|_{\HS}^{2}+2\|V^{*n}\|_{\HS}^{2},

in other words,

2\|\operatorname{Re}V^{n}\|_{\HS}^{2}+2\|\operatorname{Im}V^{n}\|_{\HS}^{2}=\|V^{n}\|_{\HS}^{2}+\|V^{*n}\|_{\HS}^{2}.

Also, as the kernels of
\operatorname{Re}V^{n}
and
\operatorname{Im}V^{n}
have the same
absolute value a.e., we have
\|\operatorname{Re}V^{n}\|_{\HS}=\|\operatorname{Im}V^{n}\|_{\HS}
.
Lastly, since
V
and
V^{*}
are unitarily equivalent, we have
\|V^{n}\|_{\HS}=\|V^{*n}\|_{\HS}
.
The identity (
13
) follows immediately from these observations.

Now we turn our attention to the operator norms

\|\operatorname{Re}V^{n}\|
and
\|\operatorname{Im}V^{n}\|
.
Our goal is to establish the following theorem.

Theorem 3.2
.

For each
n\geq 1
, we have

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}(n-1)!}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n(2n+1)}}\leq\|\operatorname{Re}V^{n}\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}(n-1)!}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n(2n-1)}}

and

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}(n-1)!}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n(2n+1)}}\Bigl(1-\binom{2n-2}{n-1}^{-1}\Bigr)\leq\|\operatorname{Im}V^{n}\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}(n-1)!}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n(2n-1)}}.

To prove this result, we adopt a similar strategy
to that used in
[
2
]
to obtain the bounds in
(
11
). The idea is to estimate the operator
norm
\|S\|
of a self-adjoint Hilbert–Schmidt operator
S
using the
elementary general inequalities

(14)

\frac{\|S^{2}\|_{\HS}}{\|S\|_{\HS}}\leq\|S\|\leq\|S\|_{\HS}.

We have already evaluated

\|\operatorname{Re}V^{n}\|_{\HS}
and
\|\operatorname{Im}V^{n}\|_{\HS}
.
It remains to estimate

\|(\operatorname{Re}V^{n})^{2}\|_{\HS}
and
\|(\operatorname{Im}V^{n})^{2}\|_{\HS}
.
In view of (
14
), we seek lower bounds.
We start with
\|(\operatorname{Re}V^{n})^{2}\|_{\HS}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For all
n\geq 1
, we have

\|(\operatorname{Re}V^{n})^{2}\|_{\HS}\geq\frac{1}{2(n-1)!^{2}}\frac{1}{(2n-1)\sqrt{2n(2n+1)}}.

Proof.

Using Fubini’s theorem, we see that
(\operatorname{Re}V^{n})^{2}
is the
integral operator on
[0,1]
with kernel

K_{n}(x,y):=\frac{1}{4(n-1)!^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}|x-t|^{n-1}|t-y|^{n-1}\,dt.

The Hilbert–Schmidt norm of
(\operatorname{Re}V^{n})^{2}
is therefore the

L^{2}
-norm of this kernel on
[0,1]^{2}
. Suppose that
0\leq x\leq y\leq 1
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}|x-t|^{n-1}|t-y|^{n-1}\,dt

\displaystyle\geq\Bigl(\int_{0}^{x}+\int_{y}^{1}\Bigr)|x-t|^{n-1}|t-y|^{n-1}\,dt

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{x}(x-t)^{n-1}(y-t)^{n-1}\,dt

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1}(x-sx)^{n-1}(y-sx)^{n-1}x\,ds

\displaystyle\geq 2\int_{0}^{1}(x-sx)^{n-1}(y-sy)^{n-1}x\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{2x^{n}y^{n-1}}{2n-1}.

Hence

\displaystyle\|(\operatorname{Re}V^{n})^{2}\|_{HS}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}K_{n}(x,y)^{2}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=2\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{y}K_{n}(x,y)^{2}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{16(n-1)!^{4}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{y}\Bigl(\frac{2x^{n}y^{n-1}}{2n-1}\Bigr)^{2}\,dx\,dy

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{16(n-1)!^{4}}\frac{4}{(2n+1)4n(2n-1)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4(n-1)!^{4}}\frac{1}{(2n+1)2n(2n-1)^{2}}.\qed

We cannot quite repeat this proof for
(\operatorname{Im}V^{n})^{2}
,
since the corresponding kernel is given by an integral of
a function that is not positive everywhere. Nor can we assert
that
\|(\operatorname{Re}V^{n})^{2}\|_{\HS}=\|(\operatorname{Im}V^{n})^{2}\|_{\HS}
,
as was the case for the first powers of
\operatorname{Re}V^{n}
and
\operatorname{Im}V^{n}
.
However, it turns out that
(\operatorname{Re}V^{n})^{2}
is extremely close
to
(\operatorname{Im}V^{n})^{2}
in the Hilbert–Schmidt norm, and this is good enough for our needs.

Lemma 3.4
.

For all
n\geq 1
, we have

\|(\operatorname{Re}V^{n})^{2}-(\operatorname{Im}V^{n})^{2}\|_{\HS}=\frac{1}{2(2n-1)!}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n(4n-1)}}.

Proof.

Using elementary algebra, we have

(\operatorname{Re}V^{n})^{2}-(\operatorname{Im}V^{n})^{2}=\frac{1}{4}(2V^{2n}+2V^{*2n})=\operatorname{Re}V^{2n}.

The result therefore follows from Theorem
3.1
,
with
n
replaced by
2n
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

For all
n\geq 1
, we have

\|(\operatorname{Im}V^{n})^{2}\|_{\HS}\geq\frac{1}{2(n-1)!^{2}}\frac{1}{(2n-1)\sqrt{2n(2n+1)}}\Bigl(1-\binom{2n-2}{n-1}^{-1}\Bigr).

Proof.

By Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
and the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\|(\operatorname{Im}V^{n})^{2}\|_{\HS}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2(n-1)!^{2}}\frac{1}{(2n-1)\sqrt{2n(2n+1)}}-\frac{1}{2(2n-1)!}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n(4n-1)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(n-1)!^{2}}\frac{1}{(2n-1)\sqrt{2n(2n+1)}}\Bigl(1-\frac{(n-1)!^{2}}{(2n-2)!}\frac{\sqrt{2n+1}}{\sqrt{4n-1}}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2(n-1)!^{2}}\frac{1}{(2n-1)\sqrt{2n(2n+1)}}\Bigl(1-\binom{2n-2}{n-1}^{-1}\Bigr).\qed

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

All that is left to do is to insert the information from Theorem
3.1
and Lemmas
3.3
and
3.5

into (
14
). The result follows immediately.
∎

Remarks
.

(i) By Stirling’s formula

\binom{2n-2}{n-1}\sim\frac{4^{n-1}}{\sqrt{\pi n}}\quad(n\to\infty).

Thus although the estimate in Theorem
3.2

for
\|\operatorname{Im}V^{n}\|
is slightly weaker than
that for
\|\operatorname{Re}V^{n}\|
, the difference for large
n
is
extremely small.

(ii) Comparing the result of Theorem
3.2
with (
11
), we see that

\|\operatorname{Re}V^{n}\|\sim\|\operatorname{Im}V^{n}\|\sim\frac{\|V^{n}\|}{\sqrt{2}}\sim\frac{1}{(2\sqrt{2})n!}\quad(n\to\infty).

Thus, although the exact analogue of (
13
)
fails to hold for the operator norm, it does hold asymptotically.

4.
Numerical ranges

It is known that the numerical range
W(V)
of the Volterra
operator is the convex compact set bounded by the vertical segment
[-i/2\pi,\,i/2\pi]
and the curves

t\mapsto\Bigl(\frac{1-\cos t}{t^{2}}\Bigr)\pm i\Bigl(\frac{t-\sin t}{t^{2}}\Bigr)\quad(t\in[0,2\pi]).

This result is folklore. It appears in the middle of a discussion in
[
3
, p.113–114]
, where it is attributed to A. Brown.

As for higher powers of
V
, to the best of our knowledge their
numerical ranges have never been identified precisely. However, the projections of
W(V^{n})
onto the
x
- and
y
-axes
are just
W(\operatorname{Re}V^{n})
and
W(\operatorname{Im}V^{n})
respectively
and, in principle at least, computing these is a much easier task. Indeed,
each of
\operatorname{Re}V^{n}
and
\operatorname{Im}V^{n}
is a compact self-adjoint operator, so its numerical range is just the real interval obtained by taking the closed convex hull of the eigenvalues.

For small values of
n
, the results in §
2
lead to the intervals listed in Table
2
.

For larger values of

, we have the following result.

Theorem 4.1
.

For
n\geq 1
, we have

\Bigl[-\frac{n-1}{3(n+3)}\frac{1}{(n+1)!},\,\|\operatorname{Re}V^{n}\|\Bigr]\subset W(\operatorname{Re}V^{n})\subset\Bigl[-\|\operatorname{Re}V^{n}\|,\,\|\operatorname{Re}V^{n}\|\Bigr]

and

W(\operatorname{Im}V^{n})=\Bigl[-\|\operatorname{Im}V^{n}\|,\,\|\operatorname{Im}V^{n}\|\Bigr].

Proof.

The inclusions

W(\operatorname{Re}V^{n})\subset\Bigl[-\|\operatorname{Re}V^{n}\|,\,\|\operatorname{Re}V^{n}\|\Bigr]\quad\text{and}\quad W(\operatorname{Im}V^{n})\subset\Bigl[-\|\operatorname{Im}V^{n}\|,\,\|\operatorname{Im}V^{n}\|\Bigr]

are clear.
Moreover, the latter inclusion is actually an equality,
since, by Theorem
2.6
, the eigenvalues of
\operatorname{Im}V^{n}

are symmetrically distributed about the origin.

By Theorem
2.7
,
\|\operatorname{Re}V^{n}\|
is an eigenvalue of
\operatorname{Re}V^{n}
.
Also, if
f(x):=1-2x
, then
\|f\|_{2}=1/\sqrt{3}
and

\langle\operatorname{Re}V^{n}f,f\rangle=\operatorname{Re}\langle V^{n}f,\,f\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl(\frac{x^{n}}{n!}-2\frac{x^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\Bigr)(1-2x)\,dx=-\frac{n-1}{n+3}\frac{1}{(n+1)!}.

It follows that both

\|\operatorname{Re}V^{n}\|\quad\text{and}\quad-\frac{n-1}{3(n+3)}\frac{1}{(n+1)!}

belong to
W(\operatorname{Re}V^{n})
, and therefore so do all the points in between, by convexity.
∎

A Hilbert-space operator
T
is said to be
accretive
if its numerical range is contained in the half-plane

\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:\operatorname{Re}z\geq 0\}
, or equivalently, if its real part is a positive operator.
Also
T
is
dissipative
if
-T
is accretive.

Clearly the Volterra operator
V
is accretive. This is no longer true of higher powers of
V
.

Corollary 4.2
.

If
n\geq 2
, then
V^{n}
is neither accretive nor dissipative.

Proof.

As we have just shown in Theorem
4.1
,
if
n\geq 2
then
W(\operatorname{Re}V^{n})

contains both strictly positive and strictly negative values.
∎

A natural next step is to consider the accretivity of more general polynomials
p(V)
of the Volterra operator. In principle, this amounts to determining whether the eigenvalues of
\operatorname{Re}p(V)
are all non-negative. In practice this may be rather complicated. For quadratic polynomials, the calculations are similar to those for
\operatorname{Re}V^{2}
already carried out in the proof of Theorem
2.5
.

We record here one special case that is particularly simple, and for which eigenvalue calculations are not needed.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Then
aV+bV^{2}
is accretive if and only if

b\leq 0
and
2a+b\geq 0
.

Proof.

Note that
\operatorname{Re}V
is the rank-one operator given by

\operatorname{Re}Vf=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\int_{0}^{1}f\Bigr)1\quad(f\in L^{2}[0,1]).

Hence, for all
f\in L^{2}[0,1]
, we have

\operatorname{Re}\langle Vf,f\rangle=\langle\operatorname{Re}Vf,f\rangle=\frac{1}{2}\Big|\int_{0}^{1}f\Bigr|^{2}=2\|(\operatorname{Re}V)f\|_{2}^{2}.

Also

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\langle V^{2}f,f\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(\langle Vf,V^{*}f\rangle+\langle V^{*}f,Vf\rangle\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\Bigl(\|(V+V^{*})f\|_{2}^{2}-\|(V-V^{*})f\|_{2}^{2}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\|(\operatorname{Re}V)f\|_{2}^{2}-\|(\operatorname{Im}V)f\|_{2}^{2}.

Thus, if
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}
, then

\operatorname{Re}\Bigl\langle(aV+bV^{2})f,\,f\Bigr\rangle=(2a+b)\|(\operatorname{Re}V)f\|_{2}^{2}-b\|(\operatorname{Im}V)f\|_{2}^{2}\quad(f\in L^{2}[0,1]).

In particular,
aV+bV^{2}
is accretive if and only if

(15)

(2a+b)\|(\operatorname{Re}V)f\|_{2}^{2}-b\|(\operatorname{Im}V)f\|_{2}^{2}\geq 0\quad(f\in L^{2}[0,1]).

Taking
f(x):=(x-1/2)
, we have

\|(\operatorname{Re}V)f\|_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl|\int_{0}^{1}f\Bigr|=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\|(\operatorname{Im}V)f\|_{2}=(1/2)\|x^{2}-x\|_{2}>0.

Substituting this information into (
15
),
we see that (
15
) implies that

b\leq 0
.

Also, taking
f_{n}:=n1_{[0,\,1/n]}+n1_{[1-1/n,\,1]}
, we have

\|(\operatorname{Re}V)f_{n}\|_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Bigl|\int_{0}^{1}f_{n}\Bigr|=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bigl\|(\operatorname{Im}V)f_{n}\bigr\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2/n.

(To see the last inequality, observe that
|(\operatorname{Im}V)f_{n}(x)|\leq 1
for all
x\in[0,1]
and that

(\operatorname{Im}V)f_{n}(x)=0
if
x\in[1/n,1-1/n]
.) Substituting these
f_{n}
into (
15
), and letting
n\to\infty
, we see that (
15
) implies that

2a+b\geq 0
.

To summarize, we have shown that, if
aV^{2}+bV
is accretive, then
we have
b\leq 0
and
2a+b\geq 0
. Conversely, if both these inequalities hold, then (
15
) clearly implies that
aV+bV^{2}
is accretive.
∎
