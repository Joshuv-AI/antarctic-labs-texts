Continuous Turán numbers
Jesse Geneson geneson@gmail.com

Abstract
One of the most famous open problems in combinatorics is the Zarankiewicz problem, which asks for the maximum number of ones in an n × n n\times n matrix that has no s × t s\times t submatrix of all ones. The Kővari-Sós-Turán theorem provides an upper bound of O ⁡ ( s 1 t ​ n 2 − 1 t ) O(s^{\frac{1}{t}}n^{2-\frac{1}{t}}) for this problem for fixed t ≥ 2 t\geq 2 , which is known to be sharp in some cases. The Zarankiewicz problem is a subproblem of the more general problem of determining the maximum number of ones ex ⁡ ( n , M ) \operatorname{ex}(n,M) in an n × n n\times n 0-1 matrix that avoids the forbidden 0-1 matrix M M . In this paper, we define a notion of containment and avoidance for subsets of ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . Then we introduce a new, continuous and super-additive extremal function for subsets P ⊆ ℝ 2 P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2} called opx ⁡ ( n , P ) \operatorname{opx}(n,P) , which is the supremum of μ 2 ​ ( S ) \mu_{2}(S) over all open P P -free subsets S ⊆ [ 0 , n ] 2 S\subseteq[0,n]^{2} , where μ 2 ​ ( S ) \mu_{2}(S) denotes the Lebesgue measure of S S in ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . We show that opx ⁡ ( n , P ) \operatorname{opx}(n,P) fully encompasses ex ⁡ ( n , M ) \operatorname{ex}(n,M) up to a constant factor. More specifically, we define a natural correspondence between finite subsets P ⊆ ℝ 2 P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2} and 0-1 matrices M P M_{P} , and we prove that opx ⁡ ( n , P ) = Θ ⁡ ( ex ⁡ ( n , M P ) ) \operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Theta(\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P})) for all finite subsets P ⊆ ℝ 2 P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2} , where the constants in the bounds depend only on the distances between the points in P P . We also discuss bounded infinite subsets P P for which opx ⁡ ( n , P ) \operatorname{opx}(n,P) grows faster than ex ⁡ ( n , M ) \operatorname{ex}(n,M) for all fixed 0-1 matrices M M . In particular, we show that opx ⁡ ( n , P ) = Θ ⁡ ( n 2 ) \operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Theta(n^{2}) for any open subset P ⊆ ℝ 2 P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2} . We prove an even stronger result, that if Q P Q_{P} is the set of points with rational coordinates in any open subset P ⊆ ℝ 2 P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2} , then opx ⁡ ( n , Q P ) = Θ ⁡ ( n 2 ) \operatorname{opx}(n,Q_{P})=\Theta(n^{2}) . Finally, we obtain a strengthening of the Kővari-Sós-Turán theorem that applies to infinite subsets of ℝ 2 \mathbb{R}^{2} . Specifically, for subsets P s , t , c ⊆ ℝ 2 P_{s,t,c}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2} consisting of t t horizontal line segments of length s s with left endpoints on the same vertical line with consecutive segments a distance of c c apart, we prove that opx ⁡ ( n , P s , t , c ) = O ⁡ ( s 1 t ​ n 2 − 1 t ) \operatorname{opx}(n,P_{s,t,c})=O(s^{\frac{1}{t}}n^{2-\frac{1}{t}}) , where the constant in the bound depends on t t and c c . When t = 2 t=2 , we show that this bound is sharp up to a constant factor that depends on c c . We also extend opx ⁡ ( n , P ) \operatorname{opx}(n,P) to any number of dimensions, and we generalize most of our results including the strengthening of the Kővari-Sós-Turán theorem to any number of dimensions.

Introduction

We say that a 0-1 matrix

avoids

-free

-free

\operatorname{ex}(n,M)
for matrices

of all ones
[
43
]
. The famous Kővari-Sós-Turán theorem gives a partial solution to this problem.

In addition to the longstanding open Zarankiewicz problem, research on
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)
has also focused on other well-known classes of 0-1 matrices like permutation matrices. In particular, Marcus and Tardos
[
36
]
showed that every permutation matrix

has
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)=O(n)
, and used this fact to prove the Stanley-Wilf conjecture using results from
[
18
]
and
[
32

-gon
[
15
]

to bound the complexity of an algorithm for path minimization in a rectlinear grid with obstacles
[
37
]

In this paper, we present a new extremal function for subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, which fully encompasses
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)
up to a constant factor. We show that all past results about
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)

\operatorname{ex}(n,M)

\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
up to a constant factor for a set of points

that corresponds to

, we show that there is a matrix

for which
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Theta(\operatorname{ex}(n,M))
. We also consider infinite sets of points, like a single line segment, or two horizontal line segments with left endpoints in the same column and right endpoints in the same column, and more generally vertical stacks of any number of horizontal line segments. We show that these forbidden stacks of horizontal line segments have extremal functions that behave like the Turán numbers of complete bipartite graphs, and we obtain a strengthening of the Kővari-Sós-Turán theorem for these forbidden unions of segments, using the measure-theoretic form of Jensen’s inequality and Fubini’s theorem.

If

Properties of
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)

In this section, we prove basic facts about
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)

\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
is super-additive and continuous. We also investigate the effect of simple modifications to

on the value of the extremal function
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
. For subsets

with a rightmost column, we prove a sharp upper bound of

on the increase in

when we add a horizontal segment of length

to

with its left endpoint on some point in the rightmost column of

\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
for finite subsets

when we dilate

.

2.1
Basic observations about
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)

It is well-known that if
M^{\prime}
is obtained from the 0-1 matrix

by

rotation, or horizontal or vertical reflection, then we have
\operatorname{ex}(n,M^{\prime})=\operatorname{ex}(n,M)
. If

where

is the transformation that we apply to
M
to get
M^{\prime}
, and if
A
is an
n\times n
0-1 matrix that avoids
M
, then
R(A)
avoids
M^{\prime}
and

is still an
n\times n
0-1 matrix when

is a

rotation, horizontal reflection, or vertical reflection. Thus we have
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)\leq ex(n,M^{\prime})
. Similarly we obtain
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)\geq ex(n,M^{\prime})
since
M=R^{-1}(M^{\prime})
, so
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)=ex(n,M^{\prime})
. Below we observe an analogous fact for

Lemma 2.1
.

If

is obtained from

by

.

Proof.

Suppose that

where

is the transformation that we apply to

to get

, and suppose that

is a

-free open subset. Then

avoids

and

is still an open subset. Moreover

can be translated into a subset of

. Thus we have
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime})
. Similarly we obtain
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)\geq\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime})
, so
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime})
.
∎

Next we make another simple observation about
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
which does not have an exact analogue for
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)

Lemma 2.2
.

Suppose that
P^{\prime}
is obtained by translating

. Then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime})
.

Proof.

By definition of containment for subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, any subset that contains

will contain
P^{\prime}
, and any subset that contain
P^{\prime}
will contain
P
. Thus
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime})
.
∎

It is clear that
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)\leq\operatorname{ex}(n,N)
for any 0-1 matrix

that contains

0-1 matrix that avoids

.

Lemma 2.3
.

If

and

are subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
for which

contains

, then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,Q)

Proof.

Any open subset of
[0,n]^{2}
that avoids

will also avoid
Q
.
∎

Another immediate fact is that
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
is non-decreasing in

Lemma 2.4
.

For all

, we have
\operatorname{opx}(m,P)\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
.

Proof.

Any open subset of
[0,m]^{2}
that avoids

is also a subset of
[0,n]^{2}
.
∎

2.2
Super-additivity and continuity of
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)

In fact, it is possible to prove a much stronger result than the last remark. It is well-known that

of

in row

in row

\epsilon>0
there exist rows

and
r
of

with

and

in row

in row

Let

Next we show that

. In order to prove this, we start with a simple lemma.

Lemma 2.6
.

For all

and all

with

\operatorname{opx}(n,P)\leq\operatorname{opx}(n+\epsilon,P)<\operatorname{opx}(n,P)+3n\epsilon
.

Proof.

The first inequality is immediate by Lemma

. For the second inequality, observe that any open

-free subset

\operatorname{opx}(n+\epsilon,P)<\operatorname{opx}(n,P)+3n\epsilon

Proposition 2.7
.

The function
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)

for

.

Proof.

Fix

\operatorname{opx}(n,P)\leq\operatorname{opx}(n_{0}+\delta,P)<\operatorname{opx}(n_{0},P)+\epsilon
. Moreover if
n>n_{0}-\delta
, then we have
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)\geq\operatorname{opx}(n_{0}-\delta,P)>\operatorname{opx}(n_{0},P)-3(n_{0}-\delta)\delta>\operatorname{opx}(n_{0},P)-\epsilon
. Thus we have shown that for all
n_{0}>0
and for all
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
for which
|\operatorname{opx}(n,P)-\operatorname{opx}(n_{0},P)|<\epsilon
for all
n
such that
|n-n_{0}|<\delta
.
∎

2.3
Operations for
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)

Füredi and Hajnal
[
18
]
proved that if
M
is a 0-1 matrix with a one in the rightmost column in row
r
, and
M^{\prime}
is obtained from
M
by adding a new column on the right with a single one in row
r
, then
\operatorname{ex}(n,M^{\prime})\leq\operatorname{ex}(n,M)+n
. We prove a similar type of result for
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
. Before we prove this result, we prove a lemma that we will use a few times throughout the paper.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
P
be a horizontal line segment of length
c
with closed endpoints, and suppose that
S\subseteq[0,n]^{2}
is open and
P
-free. For each
y\in[0,n]
, let
S_{y}
denote the set of points
(a,b)\in S
such that
b=y
. Then
\mu_{1}(S_{y})\leq c
for all
y\in[0,n]
.

Proof.

Suppose for contradiction that there exists
y\in[0,n]
such that
\mu_{1}(S_{y})>c
. Since
S
is open, we have
0<y<n
and
S_{y}
is a countable disjoint union of intervals
I_{1},I_{2},\dots
with open endpoints. Then
\mu_{1}(S_{y})=\sum_{j\geq 1}|I_{j}|
. Thus for all
\epsilon>0
, there exists
N_{\epsilon}
such that
\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\epsilon}}|I_{j}|>\mu_{1}(S_{y})-\epsilon
.

Since we are supposing that
\mu_{1}(S_{y})>c
, we must have
\mu_{1}(S_{y})=c+q
for some
q>0
. Let
\epsilon=\frac{q}{2}
. Let
N_{\epsilon}
be sufficiently large so that
\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\epsilon}}|I_{j}|>\mu_{1}(S_{y})-\epsilon
. In each open interval
I_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,N_{\epsilon}
, we take a closed interval
C_{j}
of length
\max(0,|I_{j}|-\frac{\epsilon}{2^{j}})
. Then
\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\epsilon}}|C_{j}|=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\epsilon}}\max(0,|I_{j}|-\frac{\epsilon}{2^{j}})>(\mu_{1}(S_{y})-\epsilon)-\epsilon=\mu_{1}(S_{y})-q=c
. Thus the disjoint union of the
C_{j}
for
j=1,\dots,N_{\epsilon}
must contain
P
, so
S
contains
P
, which gives a contradiction. Thus
\mu_{1}(S_{y})\leq c
for all
y\in[0,n]
.
∎

Now we are ready to strengthen the result of Füredi and Hajnal from
[
18
]
.

Lemma 2.9
.

If
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
has a rightmost column, and
P^{\prime}
is obtained from
P
by adding a horizontal segment of length
c
to
P
with its left endpoint on a point in the rightmost column of
P
, then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime})\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,P)+cn
.

Proof.

Let
S^{\prime}_{t}\subseteq[0,n]^{2}
for
t\in\mathbb{N}
be a sequence of
P^{\prime}
-free open subsets with the property that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{2}(S^{\prime}_{t})=\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime})
. For each point
p
in row
r
of
S^{\prime}_{t}
, let
Z(t,p)
be the set of points in row
r
of
S^{\prime}_{t}
to the right of
p
. For each
t
, let
S_{t}
be obtained from
S^{\prime}_{t}
by removing from each row
r
of
S^{\prime}_{t}
any points
p
such that
\mu_{1}(Z(t,p))\leq c
. In other words,
S_{t}
only includes the points
p
from
S^{\prime}_{t}
for which
\mu_{1}(Z(t,p))>c
. Then
S_{t}
must avoid
P
, or else
S^{\prime}_{t}
would contain
P^{\prime}
by Lemma
2.8
.

Also
S_{t}
is open. To see this, fix any
p\in S_{t}
from some row
r
and column
g
of
S^{\prime}_{t}
. By definition of
S_{t}
,
\mu_{1}(Z(t,p))>c
, so
\mu_{1}(Z(t,p))=c+q
for some
q>0
. Since
S^{\prime}_{t}
is open, we can write
Z(t,p)
as a countable union of disjoint row-
r
intervals
I_{j}
for
j\geq 1
(which are open in the restriction to row
r
) such that
\sum_{j}|I_{j}|=c+q
. In each interval
I_{j}
, we choose a closed interval
C_{j}\subseteq I_{j}
such that
\sum_{j}|C_{j}|\geq c+\frac{q}{2}
. Note that we can do this, e.g., by choosing
|C_{j}|=\max(0,|I_{j}|-\frac{q}{2^{j+1}})
for each
j\geq 1
.

Since
S^{\prime}_{t}
is open, around every point
p^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}_{t}
there is an open ball
B(p^{\prime},\nu_{p^{\prime}})\subseteq S^{\prime}_{t}
of radius
\nu_{p^{\prime}}
centered at
p^{\prime}
for some
\nu_{p^{\prime}}>0
. For each closed interval
C_{j}
,
\cup_{p^{\prime}\in C_{j}}B(p^{\prime},\nu_{p^{\prime}})
is an open cover of
C_{j}
. Since
C_{j}
is closed and bounded, it is compact, so the open cover
\cup_{p^{\prime}\in C_{j}}B(p^{\prime},\nu_{p^{\prime}})
has a finite subcover
\cup_{\pi\in K}B(\pi,\nu_{\pi})
for some finite subset
K\subseteq C_{j}
. Since
C_{j}
is covered by a finite union of open balls
\cup_{\pi\in K}B(\pi,\nu_{\pi})
, there exists
h_{j}
such that
B(p^{\prime},h_{j})\subseteq S^{\prime}_{t}
for all
p^{\prime}\in C_{j}
.

Since
\sum_{j}|C_{j}|\geq c+\frac{q}{2}
, there exists a minimum
N
such that
\sum_{j=1}^{N}|C_{j}|>c+\frac{q}{4}
. Let
H=\min(\left\{h_{j}:1\leq j\leq N\right\})
. Then for every point
p^{\prime}\in C_{j}
for each
j=1,\dots,N
, we have
B(p^{\prime},H)\subseteq S^{\prime}_{t}
.

Since there is an open ball
B(p,\nu_{p})\subseteq S^{\prime}_{t}
, all points
\tau
in the same column
g
as
p
in
S^{\prime}_{t}
with
\operatorname{dist}(\tau,p)\leq\frac{\nu_{p}}{2}
must satisfy
\tau\in S^{\prime}_{t}
. Moreover since the open ball
B(p^{\prime},H)
is a subset of
S^{\prime}_{t}
for every point
p^{\prime}\in C_{j}
for each
j
between
1
and
N
inclusive, all points
\tau
in column
g
in
S^{\prime}_{t}
with
\operatorname{dist}(\tau,p)\leq\min(\frac{\nu_{p}}{2},\frac{H}{2})
must be in
S_{t}
. This is because if
\tau
is in row
r^{\prime}
and column
g
of
S^{\prime}_{t}
with
|r-r^{\prime}|\leq\min(\frac{\nu_{p}}{2},\frac{H}{2})
, then
\mu_{1}(Z(t,\tau))\geq\sum_{j=1}^{N}|C_{j}|>c+\frac{q}{4}
.

Let
R=\min(\frac{\nu_{p}}{2},\frac{H}{2},\frac{q}{4})
. Then the open ball
B(p,R)
is a subset of
S^{\prime}_{t}
. The points in the intersection of
g
with
B(p,R)
must be in
S_{t}
, as explained in the previous paragraph. Thus all points in
B(p,R)
to the left of column
g
must also be in
S_{t}
, by definition of
S_{t}
. All points
\tau
in the intersection of
g
with
B(p,R)
must be in some row
r^{\prime}
with
|r-r^{\prime}|<R
and satisfy
\mu_{1}(Z(t,\tau))>c+\frac{q}{4}
. Thus, all points
\tau^{\prime}
in
B(p,R)
to the right of column
g
in row
r^{\prime}
must satisfy
\mu_{1}(Z(t,\tau^{\prime}))>c
by definition of
R
. So all points in
B(p,R)
to the right of column
g
must also be in
S_{t}
, by definition of
S_{t}
. We showed that
B(p,R)\subseteq S_{t}
, so
S_{t}
is open.

Let
A=S^{\prime}_{t}-S_{t}
, and let
A_{y}
denote the set of elements of
A
in row
y
. By Fubini’s theorem and the definition of
S_{t}
,
\mu_{2}(A)=\int_{y\in[0,n]}\mu_{1}(A_{y})d\mu_{1}(y)\leq cn
. Thus
\mu_{2}(S_{t})\geq\mu_{2}(S^{\prime}_{t})-cn
, so
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{2}(S_{t})\geq\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime})-cn
, which implies that
\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime})\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,P)+cn
.
∎

Combining Lemmas
2.3
and
2.9
, we obtain the following corollary about adding a new point to the right of a set of points in the plane.

Corollary 2.10
.

If
P
is a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with a rightmost column, and
P^{\prime}
is obtained from
P
by adding a new point to
P
that is
c
to the right of a point
p
in the rightmost column of
P
, then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime})\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,P)+cn
.

Proof.

Let
P^{\prime\prime}
be obtained from
P
by adding a horizontal segment of length
c
to
P
with its left endpoint on point
p
in the rightmost column of
P
. Then
P^{\prime\prime}
contains
P^{\prime}
, so
\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime})\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime\prime})
by Lemma
2.3
. Thus by Lemma
2.9
, we have
\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime})\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime\prime})\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,P)+cn
.
∎

Both of the last two results are sharp. For example,
P
could be a single point.
P^{\prime}
would be a horizontal segment of length
c
in Lemma
2.9
or a pair of points in the same row at a distance of
c
in Corollary
2.10
. We discuss horizontal segments more in Section
4
.

If
M
is a 0-1 matrix, let
S(M,k)
be the 0-1 matrix obtained from
M
by inserting
k
rows of zeroes between every consecutive pair of rows of
M
and
k
columns of zeroes between every consecutive pair of columns of
M
. Tardos proved that
\operatorname{ex}(n,S(M,k))=O(ex(n,M)+n)
for all 0-1 matrices
M

[
41
]
, where the constant in the bound depends on
k
. Using the same proof, we show a stronger but more restricted bound that we use in our proof of the main result in Section
3
.

Lemma 2.11
.

For all finite subsets
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\operatorname{ex}(n,S(M_{P},k))\leq(k+1)^{2}\operatorname{ex}(\lceil\frac{n}{k+1}\rceil,M_{P})
.

Proof.

Suppose that
A
is an
n\times n
0-1 matrix with
\operatorname{ex}(n,S(M_{P},k))
ones that avoids
S(M_{P},k)
. Let
A^{\prime}
be the
(k+1)\lceil\frac{n}{k+1}\rceil\times(k+1)\lceil\frac{n}{k+1}\rceil
0-1 matrix obtained from
A
by adding at most
k
rows of zeroes to the bottom of
A
and at most
k
columns of zeroes to the right side of
A
. Note that
A^{\prime}
must still avoid
S(M_{P},k)
, since
A
avoids
S(M_{P},k)
. For each
1\leq x\leq k+1
and
1\leq y\leq k+1
, let
A_{x,y}
be the
\lceil\frac{n}{k+1}\rceil\times\lceil\frac{n}{k+1}\rceil
0-1 matrix obtained from
A^{\prime}
by taking the submatrix of rows
r
for which
(k+1)
divides
(r-x)
and columns
s
for which
(k+1)
divides
(s-y)
. For all
x
and
y
,
A_{x,y}
must avoid
M_{P}
or else
A
would contain
S(M_{P},k)
. Thus each 0-1 matrix
A_{x,y}
has at most
\operatorname{ex}(\lceil\frac{n}{k+1}\rceil,M_{P})
ones, so
A
has at most
(k+1)^{2}\operatorname{ex}(\lceil\frac{n}{k+1}\rceil,M_{P})
ones.
∎

The next result is analogous to the last one, but for finite subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
instead of 0-1 matrices. It shows that even if we add empty intervals of rows and columns to a finite subset
P
, it only changes
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
by at most a constant factor (which depends on the size of the added intervals and the distances between the points in
P
). We use this result multiple times in the remainder of the paper.

Lemma 2.12
.

Suppose that
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a finite subset in which the distances between all consecutive pairs of rows and columns are in
[c,d]
. Let
P^{\prime}
be a dilation of
P
by a factor of
q>1
, i.e. for every point
(x,y)\in P
, the point
(qx,qy)\in P^{\prime}
. Then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime})\leq(\lceil\frac{qd}{c}\rceil+1)^{2}\operatorname{opx}(\lceil\frac{\lceil\frac{n}{c}\rceil}{\lceil\frac{qd}{c}\rceil+1}\rceil c,P)
.

Proof.

Let
S_{t}\subseteq[0,n]^{2}
for
t\in\mathbb{N}
be a sequence of
P^{\prime}
-free open subsets with the property that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{2}(S_{t})=\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime})
. We partition
[0,\lceil\frac{n}{c}\rceil c]^{2}
into
c\times c
open squares, deleting any points at the boundaries, and we use the
c\times c
squares to define a family of subsets. Note that any deleted points at the boundaries have a total measure of
0
. For each
1\leq u\leq\lceil\frac{qd}{c}\rceil+1
and
1\leq v\leq\lceil\frac{qd}{c}\rceil+1
, let
[S_{t}]_{u,v}
be the subset of
[0,\lceil\frac{\lceil\frac{n}{c}\rceil}{\lceil\frac{qd}{c}\rceil+1}\rceil c]^{2}
obtained from
S_{t}
through the following operations.

For each
1\leq u\leq\lceil\frac{qd}{c}\rceil+1
, let
J_{u}
be the set of all
x
for which
\lceil\frac{qd}{c}\rceil+1
divides
\lceil\frac{x}{c}\rceil-u
excluding
x
that are multiples of
c
. In
[S_{t}]^{\prime}_{u,v}
, include the points in
S_{t}
with
x
-coordinates in
J_{u}
and
y
-coordinates in
J_{v}
(note that these points form open
c\times c
squares), do not include any points with
x
-coordinates not in
J_{u}
or
y
-coordinates not in
J_{v}
. We form
[S_{t}]_{u,v}
from
[S_{t}]^{\prime}_{u,v}
by completely contracting any rows or columns of
c\times c
squares that were not included in
[S_{t}]^{\prime}_{u,v}
and translating the resulting subset into the square
[0,\lceil\frac{\lceil\frac{n}{c}\rceil}{\lceil\frac{qd}{c}\rceil+1}\rceil c]^{2}
.

For all
u
and
v
,
[S_{t}]_{u,v}
is open since it is obtained by translating disconnected components of the intersection of an open set with an open set. Moreover
[S_{t}]_{u,v}
must avoid
P
or else
S_{t}
would contain
P^{\prime}
, since
P
is finite and has no consecutive columns or rows with distance less than
c
. Thus each subset
[S_{t}]_{u,v}
has
\mu_{2}([S_{t}]_{u,v})\leq\operatorname{opx}(\lceil\frac{\lceil\frac{n}{c}\rceil}{\lceil\frac{qd}{c}\rceil+1}\rceil c,P)
, so
\mu_{2}(S_{t})\leq(\lceil\frac{qd}{c}\rceil+1)^{2}\operatorname{opx}(\lceil\frac{\lceil\frac{n}{c}\rceil}{\lceil\frac{qd}{c}\rceil+1}\rceil c,P)
for all
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus
\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime})=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{2}(S_{t})\leq(\lceil\frac{qd}{c}\rceil+1)^{2}\operatorname{opx}(\lceil\frac{\lceil\frac{n}{c}\rceil}{\lceil\frac{qd}{c}\rceil+1}\rceil c,P)
.
∎

3
A connection to
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)

In this section, we show that
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
encompasses the extremal function
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)
up to a constant factor. We establish the theorem below by proving an upper bound and a lower bound.

Theorem 3.1
.

For all finite subsets
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Theta(\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P}))
, where the constants in the bound depend on the distances between the rows and columns in
P
.

Then we use this theorem to derive several corollaries about
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
from known results about
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)
.

3.1
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Theta(\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P}))
for all finite subsets
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}

In order to prove Theorem
3.1
, we first provide a construction which implies that
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Omega(\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P}))
. Then we show that
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=O(\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P}))
by turning
P
-free subsets into 0-1 matrices and applying Lemma
2.11
.

Theorem 3.2
.

For all finite subsets
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Omega(\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P}))
, where the constant in the bound depends on the distances between the rows and columns in
P
.

Proof.

Let
n
be a positive integer, let
d
be the minimum distance between any two consecutive rows of
P
, let
d^{\prime}
be the minimum distance between any two consecutive columns of
P
, and define
c^{\prime}=\min(d,d^{\prime})
.
Suppose that
c
is the maximum positive real number that is at most
\min(c^{\prime},1)
for which
\frac{n}{c}
is an integer. Note that
c\geq\frac{\min(c^{\prime},1)}{2}
, since
\frac{n}{x}
is an integer whenever
x=\frac{n}{2^{j}}
for some
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
S
be a subset of
[0,n]^{2}
obtained by starting with any
M_{P}
-free
\frac{n}{c}\times\frac{n}{c}
0-1 matrix
A
with the maximum possible number of ones, and for each pair of integers
1\leq i,j\leq\frac{n}{c}
such that
a_{i,j}=1
, adding the points in the open square
(c(j-1),cj)\times(n-ci,n-c(i-1))
to
S
. Note that
S
is open, since it is a union of open sets.

Suppose for contradiction that
S
contains
P
. Each point in the copy of
P
in
S
must be in a distinct open square of
S
, since the open squares have sidelength
c
, and
c
is at most both the minimum distance between any two consecutive rows of
P
and minimum distance between any two consecutive columns of
P
. For any points
p,p^{\prime}
in the copy of
P
in the same row, the open squares that contain
p
and
p^{\prime}
must have the same set of
y
-coordinates. For any points
p,p^{\prime}
in the copy of
P
in different rows, the open squares
U
and
U^{\prime}
that contain
p
and
p^{\prime}
must have disjoint sets of
y
-coordinates, since
c
is at most the minimum distance between any two consecutive rows of
P
and both
U
and
U^{\prime}
are open
c\times c
squares. Similarly, for any points
p,p^{\prime}
in the copy of
P
in the same column, the open squares that contain
p
and
p^{\prime}
must have the same set of
x
-coordinates. For any points
p,p^{\prime}
in the copy of
P
in different columns, the open squares
U
and
U^{\prime}
that contain
p
and
p^{\prime}
must have disjoint sets of
x
-coordinates, since
c
is at most the minimum distance between any two consecutive columns of
P
and both
U
and
U^{\prime}
are open
c\times c
squares.

For each point in the copy of
P
in
S
with coordinates
(x,y)
, we have
a_{\frac{n}{c}+1-\lceil\frac{y}{c}\rceil,\lceil\frac{x}{c}\rceil}=1
. Note that for any points
p,p^{\prime}
in the copy of
P
that are in the open squares
U,U^{\prime}
, the ones in
A
corresponding to
p
and
p^{\prime}
must be in the same row if
U
and
U^{\prime}
have the same set of
y
-coordinates, so the ones in
A
corresponding to
p
and
p^{\prime}
must be in the same row if
p
and
p^{\prime}
are in the same row. The ones in
A
corresponding to
p
and
p^{\prime}
must be in different rows if
U
and
U^{\prime}
have disjoint sets of
y
-coordinates, so the ones in
A
corresponding to
p
and
p^{\prime}
must be in different rows if
p
and
p^{\prime}
are in different rows. Similarly, the ones in
A
corresponding to
p
and
p^{\prime}
must be in the same column if
U
and
U^{\prime}
have the same set of
x
-coordinates, so the ones in
A
corresponding to
p
and
p^{\prime}
must be in the same column if
p
and
p^{\prime}
are in the same column. The ones in
A
corresponding to
p
and
p^{\prime}
must be in different columns if
U
and
U^{\prime}
have disjoint sets of
x
-coordinates, so the ones in
A
corresponding to
p
and
p^{\prime}
must be in different columns if
p
and
p^{\prime}
are in different columns.

Thus the ones in
A
form a copy of
M_{P}
in
A
, a contradiction of
A
being
M_{P}
-free. Thus
S
is
P
-free, and
\mu_{2}(S)=c^{2}\operatorname{ex}(\frac{n}{c},M_{P})\geq c^{2}\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P})=\Omega(\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P}))
, where the first inequality follows by super-additivity of the extremal function
\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P})
.
∎

Next we complete the proof of Theorem
3.1
to show that the construction in Theorem
3.2
is sharp up to a constant factor.

Theorem 3.3
.

For all finite subsets
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=O(\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P}))
, where the constant in the bound depends on the distances between the rows and columns in
P
.

Proof.

Let
n
be a positive integer. If
P
only has a single point, then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P})=0
. Thus, we may suppose that
P
has multiple points, so
M_{P}
has multiple ones. It is a well-known fact that any 0-1 matrix
M
with multiple ones has
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)\geq n
, so we can conclude that
\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P})\geq n
since
P
has multiple points.

By Lemma
2.12
, it suffices to prove this result for subsets
P
where the distance between consecutive rows and consecutive columns is equal to
1
. Let
S_{t}\subseteq[0,n]^{2}
for
t\in\mathbb{N}
be a sequence of
P
-free open sets with the property that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{2}(S_{t})=\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
. Since each
S_{t}
is open, we can define an infinite family of unions of open squares
Z_{t,r}\subseteq S_{t}
for
r\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
with Lebesgue measures converging to
\mu_{2}(S_{t})
.

Specifically let
Z_{t,r}
be obtained from
S_{t}
by drawing an
r\times r
grid of squares each of dimensions
\frac{n}{r}\times\frac{n}{r}
on
[0,n]^{2}
, and including the whole interior
U
of each square in
Z_{t,r}
if and only if
U\subseteq S_{t}
, and otherwise including no part of
U
in
S_{t}
. We do not include any points in
Z_{t,r}
from interiors
U
of squares for which
U\not\subseteq S_{t}
. We also do not include any points at the boundaries of the
\frac{n}{r}\times\frac{n}{r}
squares in
Z_{t,r}
.

For each point
p\in S_{t}
, let
w_{p}
be the maximum positive real number of the form
\frac{n}{x}
for any positive integer
x
such that the open ball
B(p,w_{p})
is a subset of
S_{t}
. Since
S_{t}
is open,
w_{p}
is defined for all
p\in S_{t}
. Inside each open ball
B(p,w_{p})
, we can draw an open square
T_{p}
of sidelength
w_{p}
with axis-parallel sides centered at
p
. Note that
S_{t}=\cup_{p\in S_{t}}T_{p}
.

Fix a positive integer
r
. We can classify the points in
S_{t}
that will not be included in
Z_{t,r}
into two sets
B_{t,r}
and
L_{t,r}
. If
p=(x,y)\in S_{t}
satisfies
x=i\frac{n}{r}
or
y=j\frac{n}{r}
for some integers
i
and
j
, then
p
is on the boundary of a
\frac{n}{r}\times\frac{n}{r}
square, so
p
is not included in
Z_{t,r}
. Let
B_{t,r}
be the set of points in
S_{t}
that are on the boundary of a
\frac{n}{r}\times\frac{n}{r}
square. If
p\in S_{t}
is not in
B_{t,r}
, then the only way that
p
will not be included in
Z_{t,r}
is if the open square
U
of sidelength
\frac{n}{r}
which contains
p
is not a subset of
S_{t}
. If
w_{p}\geq 2\frac{n}{r}
, then
U\subseteq T_{p}\subseteq S_{t}
. Thus if
U
is not a subset of
S_{t}
, then we must have
w_{p}<2\frac{n}{r}
. Let
L_{t,r}
be the set of points
p
in
S_{t}
that are not in
B_{t,r}
and are not in
Z_{t,r}
. Note that
S_{t}=B_{t,r}\cup L_{t,r}\cup Z_{t,r}
, and the sets
B_{t,r}
,
L_{t,r}
, and
Z_{t,r}
are disjoint.

Thus
\mu_{2}(S_{t})=\mu_{2}(B_{t,r}\cup L_{t,r}\cup Z_{t,r})=\mu_{2}(B_{t,r})+\mu_{2}(L_{t,r})+\mu_{2}(Z_{t,r})=\mu_{2}(L_{t,r})+\mu_{2}(Z_{t,r})
. Since
w_{p}<2\frac{n}{r}
for all
p\in L_{t,r}
, we have
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{2}(L_{t,r})=0
. Therefore
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{2}(Z_{t,r})=\mu_{2}(S_{t})
, so we have
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{2}(Z_{t,r})=\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
.

For each subset
Z_{t,r}
, observe that we can define an
r\times r
0-1 matrix
A_{t,r}
for which the
(i,j)
-entry equals
1
if and only if the square in row
i
and column
j
of the grid is included in
Z_{t,r}
. Our numbering of the squares in the grid goes from left to right and top to bottom, like a matrix. Note that
Z_{t,r}
avoids
P
, since
S_{t}
avoids
P
and
Z_{t,r}\subseteq S_{t}
. Thus
A_{t,r}
avoids
S(M_{P},\lceil\frac{r}{n}\rceil)
, or else the ones in
S(M_{P},\lceil\frac{r}{n}\rceil)
would correspond to a union of interiors of squares in
Z_{t,r}
that contain a copy of
P
.

Let
|A_{t,r}|
denote the number of ones in
A_{t,r}
. For each
t\geq 0
and
r\geq n^{2}
, we have
\mu_{2}(Z_{t,r})=(\frac{n}{r})^{2}|A_{t,r}|\leq(\frac{n}{r})^{2}\operatorname{ex}(r,S(M_{P},\lceil\frac{r}{n}\rceil))\leq(\frac{n}{r})^{2}(\lceil\frac{r}{n}\rceil+1)^{2}\operatorname{ex}(\lceil\frac{r}{\lceil\frac{r}{n}\rceil+1}\rceil,M_{P})=(1+o(1))\operatorname{ex}(\lceil\frac{r}{\lceil\frac{r}{n}\rceil+1}\rceil,M_{P})\leq(1+o(1))\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P})
, where the first inequality follows from the preceding paragraph and the second inequality follows from Lemma
2.11
. Thus
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{2}(Z_{t,r})=O(\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P}))
.
∎

3.2
Corollaries of results on
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)

Marcus and Tardos proved that
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)=O(n)
for every permutation matrix
M

[
36
]
, which was the missing piece to prove the Stanley-Wilf conjecture. Fox later sharpened the bound by proving that
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)=2^{O(k)}n
for every
k\times k
permutation matrix
M
. This implies the corollary below.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
|P|=k
for which all points in
P
have different
x
-coordinates and different
y
-coordinates, and the distances between all consecutive rows and all consecutive columns are at most
c
. Then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=2^{O(k)}n
, where the constant in the bound depends on
c
.

The Kővari-Sós-Turán theorem states that
\operatorname{ex}(n,J_{s,t})=O(n^{2-\frac{1}{t}})
, where
J_{s,t}
denotes the
s\times t
matrix of all ones. As a result, for every 0-1 matrix
M
there exists a constant
\epsilon>0
such that
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)=O(n^{2-\epsilon})
. This is because
M
is contained in the all-ones matrix of the same dimensions, so we can let
r
be the width of
M
and set
\epsilon=\frac{1}{r}
.

Corollary 3.5
.

For every subset
P
with
s
rows,
t
columns, and
st
points, we have
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=O(n^{2-\frac{1}{t}})
.

We prove a stronger version of this result in Section
4
for infinite sets of points that look like equal signs (
=
) when
t=2
and equivalence symbols (
\equiv
) when
t=3
.

Corollary 3.6
.

For every finite subset
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
there exists a constant
\epsilon>0
such that
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=O(n^{2-\epsilon})
.

We show that the last corollary is false for bounded infinite subsets in Section
4
. The Kővari-Sós-Turán theorem is known to be sharp for
s\geq 2
and
t=2
, as well as
s\geq 3
and
t=3
. Thus, we obtain the following corollary about
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
.

Corollary 3.7
.

For every subset
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
k\geq 2
rows,
2
columns, and
2k
points, we have
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Theta(n^{3/2})
. For every subset
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
k\geq 3
rows,
3
columns, and
3k
points, we have
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Theta(n^{5/3})
.

Keszegh
[
30
]
showed that any 0-1 matrix
M
that has no rows with multiple ones satisfies
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)=O(n2^{\alpha(n)^{t}})
for some constant
t
that depends on
M
, where
\alpha(n)
denotes the extremely slow-growing inverse Ackermann function. This implies a corresponding result for finite subsets
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Corollary 3.8
.

If
P
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that has no rows with multiple points, then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=O(n2^{\alpha(n)^{t}})
for some constant
t
that depends on
P
.

Call a 0-1 matrix
M

linear
if
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)=O(n)
and
nonlinear
otherwise. Similarly we call a subset
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
linear if
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=O(n)
and nonlinear otherwise. Several papers on
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)
have identified families of linear 0-1 matrices (e.g. permutation matrices, double permutation matrices, matrices corresponding to visibility graphs, and linear matrices with at most four ones in
[
13
,
22
,
24
,
36
,
41
]
), and each of these linear 0-1 matrices corresponds to linear subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Other papers have identified families of nonlinear 0-1 matrices (e.g. block permutation matrices, minimally nonlinear matrices, nonlinear matrices with applications to path minimization algorithms, and nonlinear matrices with at most four ones in
[
29
,
30
,
37
,
39
,
41
]
), and each of these nonlinear 0-1 matrices corresponds to nonlinear subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

4
Forbidden bounded infinite subsets

On the topic of linear subsets, we start with one of the most natural infinite forbidden subsets to consider, a line segment
P
.

Theorem 4.1
.

If
P
is a horizontal line segment of length
c
with closed endpoints, then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=cn
.

Proof.

Suppose that
S\subseteq[0,n]^{2}
is open and
P
-free. For each
y\in[0,n]
, let
S_{y}
denote the set of points
(a,b)\in S
such that
b=y
. By Lemma
2.8
, we have
\mu_{1}(S_{y})\leq c
for all
y\in[0,n]
.

Since
S
is open, the function
\mu_{1}(S_{y}):[0,n]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function by Fubini’s theorem, so it is Lebesgue integrable on
[0,n]
. Thus
\mu_{2}(S)=\int_{y\in[0,n]}\mu_{1}(S_{y})d\mu_{1}(y)\leq cn
, so
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)\leq cn
. On the other hand, the subset
T=(0,c)\times(0,n)
is open and
P
-free with
\mu_{2}(T)=cn
, so
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)\geq\mu_{2}(T)=cn
.
∎

Note that by symmetry, the last result also applies to vertical line segments. Also, the lower bound construction in the last result shows that Corollary
2.9
and Corollary
2.10
are sharp when applied to a single point. Next we consider line segments that are neither horizontal nor vertical.

Theorem 4.2
.

If
P
is a line segment with closed endpoints between
(0,0)
and
(a,b)
for some
a,b>0
, then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=(a+b)n-ab
.

Proof.

Suppose that
S\subseteq[0,n]^{2}
is open and
P
-free. Rotate
S
,
[0,n]^{2}
, and
P
clockwise all by the same angle, until
P
becomes a horizontal segment
R(P)
of length
\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}
with endpoints at
(0,0)
and
(\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}},0)
. The result of rotating
[0,n]^{2}
is denoted
R([0,n]^{2})
, and the result of rotating
S
is denoted
R(S)
. The rows of
R(S)
are a subset of the rows of
R([0,n]^{2})
, which has height
\frac{(a+b)n}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}
. Suppose that we also translate
R([0,n]^{2})
and
R(S)
so that all points in both sets lie between
y=0
and
y=\frac{(a+b)n}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}
. For each
y\in[0,\frac{(a+b)n}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}]
, let
R(S)_{y}
denote the set of points
(u,v)\in R(S)
with
v=y
, and let
R([0,n]^{2})_{y}
denote the set of points
(u,v)\in R([0,n]^{2})
with
v=y
. First, we claim that
R(S)
must avoid
R(P)
.

Suppose for contradiction that
R(S)
contains
R(P)
. Then there exists
y
such that
R(S)_{y}
contains
R(P)
. Let
L
be the set of
x
-coordinates in
R(S)_{y}
, so there is an injection
f:[0,\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}]\rightarrow L
such that
f(u)-f(v)\geq u-v
for all
u,v\in[0,\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}]
with
u>v
. Let
Q=R^{-1}(R(S)_{y})\subseteq S
be the preimage under
R
of
R(S)_{y}
. In particular let
(d,e)
be the preimage under
R
of
(f(0),y)
. Let
X_{Q}
denote the set of
x
-coordinates of the points in
Q
, and let
Y_{Q}
denote the set of
y
-coordinates of the points in
Q
.

Define
g_{X}:[0,a]\rightarrow X_{Q}
and
g_{Y}:[0,b]\rightarrow Y_{Q}
by
g_{X}(x)=d+\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}(f(\frac{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}{a}x)-f(0))
and
g_{Y}(y)=e+\frac{b}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}(f(\frac{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}{b}y)-f(0))
. For any
x_{1},x_{2}\in[0,a]
with
x_{1}>x_{2}
, we have
g_{X}(x_{1})-g_{X}(x_{2})=\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}(f(\frac{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}{a}x_{1})-f(\frac{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}{a}x_{2}))\geq\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}(\frac{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}{a}x_{1}-\frac{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}{a}x_{2})=x_{1}-x_{2}
. Similarly
g_{Y}(y_{1})-g_{Y}(y_{2})\geq y_{1}-y_{2}
for all
y_{1},y_{2}\in[0,b]
with
y_{1}>y_{2}
. Moreover for any point
(at,bt)
with
t\in[0,1]
, we can see that
(g_{X}(at),g_{Y}(bt))\in Q
. This is because
f(0)\in L
,
f(t\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}})\in L
and
R^{-1}
maps
(f(0),y)
to
(d,e)
, so
R^{-1}
maps
(f(t\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}),y)
to
(d+\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}(f(t\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}})-f(0)),e+\frac{b}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}(f(t\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}})-f(0)))=(g_{X}(at),g_{Y}(bt))
. Since
(f(t\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}),y)\in R(S)_{y}
and
Q=R^{-1}(R(S)_{y})
, we must have
(g_{X}(at),g_{Y}(bt))\in Q
. So
Q
contains
P
, implying
S
contains
P
, which is a contradiction. Thus
R(S)
must avoid
R(P)
.

Then
\mu_{1}(R(S)_{y})\leq\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}
for all
y\in[0,\frac{(a+b)n}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}]
, or else
R(S)
would contain
R(P)
by Lemma
2.8
with
c=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}
.

However for all
y
such that
\frac{(a+b)n}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}-\frac{ab}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}\leq y\leq\frac{(a+b)n}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}
, we have
\mu_{1}(R(S)_{y})\leq\mu_{1}(R([0,n]^{2})_{y})=(\frac{(a+b)n}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}-y)\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{ab}
. Similarly if
y\leq\frac{ab}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}
, then
\mu_{1}(R(S)_{y})\leq\mu_{1}(R([0,n]^{2})_{y})=y\frac{a^{2}+b^{2}}{ab}
. Since
R(S)
is open, the function
\mu_{1}(R(S)_{y}):[0,\frac{(a+b)n}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function by Fubini’s theorem, so it is Lebesgue integrable on
[0,\frac{(a+b)n}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}]
. Thus
\mu_{2}(S)=\mu_{2}(R(S))=\int_{y\in[0,\frac{(a+b)n}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}]}\mu_{1}(R(S)_{y})d\mu_{1}(y)\leq(\frac{(a+b)n}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}-\frac{2ab}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}})\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}+\frac{ab}{\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}=(a+b)n-ab
.

On the other hand, the subset
T=\left\{(0,a)\times(0,n)\right\}\cup\left\{(0,n)\times(0,b)\right\}
is open and
P
-free with
\mu_{2}(T)=an+bn-ab
, so
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)\geq\mu_{2}(T)=(a+b)n-ab
.
∎

We can use the last result to get a linear bound on
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
for a much more general family of subsets
P
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose that
f:[0,a]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is increasing, with
\frac{f(t)-f(s)}{t-s}\leq b
for all
s,t\in[0,a]
with
s<t
. If
P
is the set of points
\left\{(t,f(t)):t\in[0,a]\right\}
, then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Theta(n)
.

Proof.

Let
Q
be the set of points
\left\{(t,bt)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:t\in[0,a]\right\}
. Then
Q
contains
P
, as evidenced by the maps
g_{X}:[0,a]\rightarrow[0,a]
and
g_{Y}:f([0,a])\rightarrow[0,ba]
defined by
g_{X}(t)=t
and
g_{Y}(f(t))=bt
. So
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,Q)=O(n)
by Theorem
4.2
and Lemma
2.3
. On the other hand, we have
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)\geq an
since
(0,a)\times(0,n)
avoids
P
, so
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Theta(n)
.
∎

4.1
Bounded infinite
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Theta(n^{2})

In the last few results, we saw infinite subsets
P
with
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=O(n)
. In Section
3
we showed that
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Theta(\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P}))
for all finite subsets
P
, and it follows from the Kővari-Sós-Turán theorem that for every 0-1 matrix
M
there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)=O(n^{2-\epsilon})
, so for every finite subset
P
there exists
\epsilon>0
such that
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=O(n^{2-\epsilon})
. In the next result, we show that there exist bounded and countably infinite sets of points
P
for which
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Omega(n^{2})
. For the next proof, we use the well-known fact that
\operatorname{ex}(n,J_{r,r})=\Omega(n^{2-\frac{2}{r+1}})
for all
r\geq 2
, where the constant in the bound does not depend on
r
.

This fact is quick to prove using probabilistic methods as in
[
3
]
. Suppose that we choose a random
n\times n
0-1 matrix where each entry is
1
with probability
p
. The expected number of copies of
J_{r,r}
is
p^{r^{2}}\binom{n}{r}\binom{n}{r}
. We can delete a one from each copy, so the expected number of ones in the altered 0-1 matrix is at least
pn^{2}-p^{r^{2}}\binom{n}{r}\binom{n}{r}>\frac{pn^{2}}{2}
when
r\geq 2
and
p\leq n^{-\frac{2}{r+1}}
. Thus
\operatorname{ex}(n,J_{r,r})\geq\frac{1}{2}n^{2-\frac{2}{r+1}}
for all
r\geq 2
.

Theorem 4.4
.

If
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is open, then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Theta(n^{2})
, where the constant in the lower bound depends on
P
.

In order to prove this theorem, we will prove a stronger fact. Given an open subset
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, let
Q_{P}
be the set of points in
P
with rational coordinates. In the following proof, all logarithms are base 2.

Theorem 4.5
.

If
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is open, then
\operatorname{opx}(n,Q_{P})=\Theta(n^{2})
, where the constant in the lower bound depends on
P
.

Proof.

The upper bound is trivial. For the lower bound, it suffices to show that there exists a subset
Q\subseteq Q_{P}
for which
\operatorname{opx}(n,Q)=\Omega(n^{2})
, where the constant in the bound depends on
P
. Let
p
be an arbitrary element of
P
. Since
P
is open, there exists some
s>0
for which there is an open ball
B(p,s)
centered at
p
of radius
s
with
B(p,s)\subseteq P
. Since
s>0
, there exists some rational number
0<c<1
such that
B(p,s)
contains a closed square of sidelength
c
with vertices at rational coordinates. Without loss of generality, we may assume that this square is
[0,c]^{2}
.

Let
Q_{r}=\left\{\frac{ic}{r}:i=1,2,\dots,r\right\}^{2}
for
r\geq 2
, so that
|Q_{r}|=r^{2}
. If
H_{r}=\left\{1,2,\dots,r\right\}^{2}
for
r\geq 2
, then Lemma
2.12
implies that
\operatorname{opx}(m,H_{r})\leq(\lceil\frac{r}{c}\rceil+1)^{2}\operatorname{opx}(\lceil\frac{\lceil\frac{mr}{c}\rceil}{\lceil\frac{r}{c}\rceil+1}\rceil\frac{c}{r},Q_{r})
. By Theorem
3.1
and the cited lower bound on
\operatorname{ex}(n,J_{r,r})
in the paragraph before this proof,
\operatorname{opx}(m,H_{r})=\Omega(m^{2-\frac{2}{r+1}})
for all
r\geq 2
, where the constant in the bound does not depend on
r
. Thus
\operatorname{opx}(\lceil\frac{\lceil\frac{mr}{c}\rceil}{\lceil\frac{r}{c}\rceil+1}\rceil\frac{c}{r},Q_{r})=\Omega(\frac{m^{2-\frac{2}{r+1}}}{(\lceil\frac{r}{c}\rceil+1)^{2}})
for all
r\geq 2
. If
m\geq 4
is an integer and
r=\lceil\log{m}\rceil
, we obtain
\operatorname{opx}(\frac{m}{\log{m}},Q_{r})\geq\operatorname{opx}(\frac{mc}{r},Q_{r})\geq\operatorname{opx}(\lceil\frac{\lceil\frac{mr}{c}\rceil}{\lceil\frac{r}{c}\rceil+1}\rceil\frac{c}{r},Q_{r})=\Omega(\frac{m^{2-\frac{2}{r+1}}}{(\lceil\frac{r}{c}\rceil+1)^{2}})=\Omega(\frac{m^{2}}{(\log{m})^{2}})
. Let
n
satisfy
m=n\log{n}
for some integer
m\geq 4
. Thus we obtain
\operatorname{opx}(n,Q_{P})\geq\operatorname{opx}(\frac{n\log{n}}{\log(n\log{n})},Q_{P})\geq\operatorname{opx}(\frac{n\log{n}}{\log(n\log{n})},Q_{\lceil\log(n\log{n})\rceil})=\Omega(n^{2})
.
∎

4.2
Strengthening the Kővari-Sós-Turán theorem

For each
t\geq 2
, let
P_{s,t,c}
denote the set of points
\left\{(x,y):x\in[0,s]\text{ and }y\in\left\{c,2c,\dots,tc\right\}\right\}
. For example,
P_{s,2,c}
is a set of points that looks like an equal sign (
=
) and
P_{s,3,c}
is a set of points that looks like an equivalence symbol (
\equiv
).

We start by determining
\operatorname{opx}(n,P_{s,2,c})
up to a constant factor that depends on
c
before we prove a general upper bound on
\operatorname{opx}(n,P_{s,t,c})
. The integrals in the next two proofs are Lebesgue integrals. For the lower bound in the next proof, we use the result of Füredi
[
17
]
that
\operatorname{ex}(n,J_{s,2})=\Theta(s^{\frac{1}{2}}n^{\frac{3}{2}})
.

Theorem 4.6
.

For all
s>0
,
\operatorname{opx}(n,P_{s,2,c})=\Theta(s^{\frac{1}{2}}n^{\frac{3}{2}})
.

Proof.

The lower bound follows from Lemma
2.3
, Theorem
3.1
, and the result of Füredi cited in the paragraph before this proof. For the upper bound, let
S
be an open
P_{s,2,c}
-free subset of
[0,n]^{2}
. Let
S^{\prime}
be the
3
-dimensional set of points of the form
(x,y,z)
for which
(x,y)\in S
and
(x,z)\in S
and
y-z>c
. First, we note that
S^{\prime}
is open. To see why this is true, define
T_{1}=\left\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:(x,y)\in S\text{ and }(x,z)\in S\right\}
and define
T_{2}=\left\{(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:y-z>c\right\}
. Clearly
T_{2}
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Since
S
is open, for every point
(x,y)\in S
there exists
r>0
such that the open ball of radius
r
centered at
(x,y)
is a subset of
S
, i.e.
B((x,y),r)\subseteq S
. For each
(x,y,z)\in T_{1}
, we have both
(x,y)\in S
and
(x,z)\in S
, so there exist
r_{y},r_{z}>0
such that
B((x,y),r_{y})\subseteq S
and
B((x,z),r_{z})\subseteq S
. For every
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\in B((x,y,z),\min(r_{y},r_{z}))
, we have
(x^{\prime}-x)^{2}+(y^{\prime}-y)^{2}+(z^{\prime}-z)^{2}<\min(r_{y}^{2},r_{z}^{2})
, so
(x^{\prime}-x)^{2}+(y^{\prime}-y)^{2}<r_{y}^{2}
and
(x^{\prime}-x)^{2}+(z^{\prime}-z)^{2}<r_{z}^{2}
, which implies that
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in S
and
(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})\in S
, so
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime})\in T_{1}
. Thus
B((x,y,z),\min(r_{y},r_{z}))\subseteq T_{1}
, so
T_{1}
is open. Hence
S^{\prime}
is also open, since
S^{\prime}=T_{1}\cap T_{2}
.

The points
(x,y,z)\in S^{\prime}
must satisfy
0<z<n-c
and
z+c<y<n
, so the projection of these points onto the
y-z
plane is the interior of a triangle of area
\frac{(n-c)^{2}}{2}
. For each fixed
y
and
z
with
y,z\in(0,n)
, let
S^{\prime\prime}_{y,z}
denote the set of points
(a,b,d)\in S^{\prime}
with
b=y
and
d=z
. Then
\mu_{1}(S^{\prime\prime}_{yz})\leq s
by Lemma
2.8
for all
y,z\in(0,n)
, or else
S
would contain
P_{s,2,c}
. Since
S^{\prime}
is open, by Fubini’s theorem the function
\mu_{1}(S^{\prime\prime}_{yz}):[0,n]^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function, so it is Lebesgue integrable on
[0,n]^{2}
. Thus
\mu_{3}(S^{\prime})=\int_{z\in(0,n)}\int_{y\in(0,n)}\mu_{1}(S^{\prime\prime}_{yz})d\mu_{1}(y)d\mu_{1}(z)\leq\frac{s(n-c)^{2}}{2}
.

For all
x\in[0,n]
, let
S^{\prime}_{x}
denote the set of points
(a,b,d)\in S^{\prime}
with
a=x
, let
S_{x}
denote the set of points
(a,b)\in S
with
a=x
, and let
m_{x}=\mu_{1}(S_{x})
. Since
S
is an open set, by Fubini’s theorem the function
m_{x}:[0,n]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function. Therefore
\frac{\max(m_{x}-c,0)^{2}}{2}
is also a measurable function. Thus
\frac{\max(m_{x}-c,0)^{2}}{2}
is Lebesgue integrable on
[0,n]
.

For all
x
with
m_{x}\geq c
, we must have
\mu_{2}(S^{\prime}_{x})\geq\frac{(m_{x}-c)^{2}}{2}
. To see why this is true, suppose that
m_{x}\geq c
. For each
(x,y)\in S_{x}
, let
Q_{x,y}=\left\{(x,r):(x,r)\in S\text{ and }r\leq y\right\}
. Note that

\displaystyle\left\{(y,z):(x,y)\in S\text{ and }(x,z)\in S\text{ and }\mu_{1}(Q_{x,z})+c<\mu_{1}(Q_{x,y})\right\}\subseteq

\displaystyle\left\{(y,z):(x,y)\in S\text{ and }(x,z)\in S\text{ and }z+c<y\right\}.

Then we have
\mu_{2}(S^{\prime}_{x})=

\displaystyle\int_{z\in(0,n-c)}\int_{y\in(z+c,n)}1_{(x,z)\in S}1_{(x,y)\in S}d\mu_{1}(y)d\mu_{1}(z)\geq

\displaystyle\int_{z\in(0,n-c)}\int_{y\in(z+c,n)}1_{(x,z)\in S}1_{(x,y)\in S}1_{\mu_{1}(Q_{x,z})+c<\mu_{1}(Q_{x,y})}d\mu_{1}(y)d\mu_{1}(z).

Given any
(x,y)\in S
, note that
\mu_{1}(Q_{x,y})\in(0,m_{x})
. Since
S
is open, we can write
S_{x}
as a countable union of intervals
I_{j}
with open endpoints. Thus there are only countably many
b\in(0,m_{x})
for which there does not exist
r
such that
\mu_{1}(Q_{x,r})=b
. Since countable sets have measure zero, the last integral is equal to

\displaystyle\int_{z^{\prime}\in(0,m_{x}-c)}\int_{y^{\prime}\in(z^{\prime}+c,m_{x})}1d\mu_{1}(y^{\prime})d\mu_{1}(z^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{(m_{x}-c)^{2}}{2}

Thus by Fubini’s theorem we have
\mu_{3}(S^{\prime})=\int_{x\in[0,n]}\mu_{2}(S^{\prime}_{x})d\mu_{1}(x)\geq\int_{x\in[0,n]}\frac{\max(m_{x}-c,0)^{2}}{2}d\mu_{1}(x)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be defined by
f(t)=\frac{\max(t-c,0)^{2}}{2}
, so
f(m_{x})=\frac{\max(m_{x}-c,0)^{2}}{2}
and
f
is convex. Thus we can rewrite the last inequality in the last paragraph as
\mu_{3}(S^{\prime})\geq\int_{x\in[0,n]}f(m_{x})d\mu_{1}(x)
. Combining this with the inequality at the end of the second paragraph, we have
\int_{x\in[0,n]}f(m_{x})d\mu_{1}(x)\leq\frac{s(n-c)^{2}}{2}
. By Jensen’s inequality, we obtain
nf(\frac{\mu_{2}(S)}{n})\leq\frac{s(n-c)^{2}}{2}
, so
n\frac{(\frac{\mu_{2}(S)}{n}-c)^{2}}{2}\leq\frac{s(n-c)^{2}}{2}
or else
\frac{\mu_{2}(S)}{n}\leq c
. Thus
\mu_{2}(S)=O(s^{\frac{1}{2}}n^{\frac{3}{2}})
.
∎

In order to generalize the last theorem, we prove a lemma where we bound the volume of a
t
-dimensional solid that will be used in the main proof.

Lemma 4.7
.

The set
X
of points
(y_{1},\dots,y_{t})
for which
0<y_{t}<n-(t-1)c
and
y_{i+1}+c<y_{i}<n-(i-1)c
for all
i=1,\dots,t-1
has
\mu_{t}(X)=\Theta(n^{t})
, where the constants in the bound depend on
t
. In particular,
\mu_{t}(X)\geq(\frac{n}{t}-c)^{t}
for
n\geq ct
.

Proof.

X
is contained in the set of points
(y_{1},\dots,y_{t})
with
0\leq y_{i}\leq n
for all
i
, which has volume
n^{t}
, so
\mu_{t}(X)\leq n^{t}
. For a lower bound, suppose that
n>ct
and define a set of points
X^{\prime}
consisting of the points
(y_{1},\dots,y_{t})
such that
y_{t+1-i}\in(\frac{n}{t}(i-1),\frac{n}{t}i-c)
for each
i=1,\dots,t
. Then
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
, and
\mu_{t}(X^{\prime})=(\frac{n}{t}-c)^{t}
. Thus
\mu_{t}(X)\geq\mu_{t}(X^{\prime})=\Omega(n^{t})
.
∎

While the last theorem covered subsets that look like the equal sign (
=
), the next theorem covers subsets that look like equivalence symbols (
\equiv
) and more generally, vertical stacks of any finite number of horizontal segments of the same length with endpoints in the same left and right columns.

Theorem 4.8
.

For all
s>0
and fixed
t\geq 2
,
\operatorname{opx}(n,P_{s,t,c})=O(s^{\frac{1}{t}}n^{2-\frac{1}{t}})
, where the constants in the bound depend on
t
and
c
.

Proof.

Let
S
be an open
P_{s,t,c}
-free subset of
[0,n]^{2}
. Let
S^{\prime}
be the
(t+1)
-dimensional set of points of the form
(x,y_{1},y_{2},\dots y_{t})
for which
(x,y_{i})\in S
for each
i=1,2,\dots,t
and
y_{i}-y_{i+1}>c
for each
i=1,\dots,t-1
. First, we note that
S^{\prime}
is open. To see why this is true, as in Theorem
4.6
define
T_{1}=\left\{(x,y_{1},y_{2},\dots y_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{t+1}:(x,y_{i})\in S\text{ for all }i=1,2,\dots,t\right\}
. Define
T_{2}=\left\{(x,y_{1},y_{2},\dots y_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{t+1}:y_{i}-y_{i+1}>c\text{ for all }i=1,2,\dots,t-1\right\}
. Clearly
T_{2}
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{t+1}
. Since
S
is open, for every point
(x,y)\in S
there exists
r>0
such that the open ball of radius
r
centered at
(x,y)
is a subset of
S
, i.e.
B((x,y),r)\subseteq S
. For each
(x,y_{1},y_{2},\dots y_{t})\in T_{1}
, we have
(x,y_{i})\in S
for all
i=1,2,\dots,t
, so there exist
r_{i}>0
for each
i=1,2,\dots,t
such that
B((x,y_{i}),r_{i})\subseteq S
for all
i=1,2,\dots,t
. For every
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}_{1},y^{\prime}_{2},\dots y^{\prime}_{t})\in B((x,y_{1},y_{2},\dots y_{t}),\min(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{t}))
, we have
(x^{\prime}-x)^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}(y^{\prime}_{i}-y_{i})^{2}<\min(r_{1}^{2},r_{2}^{2},\dots,r_{t}^{2})
, so
(x^{\prime}-x)^{2}+(y^{\prime}_{i}-y_{i})^{2}<r_{i}^{2}
for all
i=1,2,\dots,t
, which implies that
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}_{i})\in S
for all
i=1,2,\dots,t
, so
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}_{1},y^{\prime}_{2},\dots y^{\prime}_{t})\in T_{1}
. Thus
B((x,y_{1},y_{2},\dots y_{t}),\min(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{t}))\subseteq T_{1}
, so
T_{1}
is open. Hence
S^{\prime}
is also open, since
S^{\prime}=T_{1}\cap T_{2}
.

The points
(x,y_{1},y_{2},\dots y_{t})\in S^{\prime}
must satisfy
0<y_{t}<n-(t-1)c
and
y_{i+1}+c<y_{i}<n-(i-1)c
for all
i=1,\dots,t-1
. Thus the projection of these points onto the last
t
coordinates is a
t
-dimensional solid of volume
\Theta(n^{t})
by Lemma
4.7
. For each fixed
y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{t}
with
y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{t}\in(0,n)
, let
S^{\prime\prime}_{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{t}}
denote the set of points
(a,b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{t})\in S^{\prime}
with
b_{i}=y_{i}
for each
i
. Then
\mu_{1}(S^{\prime\prime}_{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{t}})\leq s
by Lemma
2.8
or else
S
would contain
P_{s,t,c}
. Since
S^{\prime}
is open, by Fubini’s theorem the function
\mu_{1}(S^{\prime\prime}_{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{t}}):[0,n]^{t}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function, so it is Lebesgue integrable on
[0,n]^{t}
. Thus
\mu_{t+1}(S^{\prime})=\int_{y_{t}\in(0,n)}\int_{y_{t-1}\in(0,n)}\dots\int_{y_{1}\in(0,n)}\mu_{1}(S^{\prime\prime}_{y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{t}})d\mu_{1}(y_{1})d\mu_{1}(y_{2})\dots d\mu_{1}(y_{t})=O(sn^{t})
.

For all
x\in[0,n]
, let
S^{\prime}_{x}
denote the set of points
(a,b_{1},b_{2},\dots,b_{t})\in S^{\prime}
with
a=x
, let
S_{x}
denote the set of points
(a,b)\in S
with
a=x
, and let
m_{x}=\mu_{1}(S_{x})
. As in the last proof, since
S
is open, by Fubini’s theorem the function
m_{x}:[0,n]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function. Therefore
\frac{\max(m_{x}-ct,0)^{t}}{t^{t}}
is also a measurable function. Thus
\frac{\max(m_{x}-ct,0)^{t}}{t^{t}}
is Lebesgue integrable over
[0,n]
. For all
x
with
m_{x}\geq ct
, we have
\mu_{t}(S^{\prime}_{x})\geq\frac{\max(m_{x}-ct,0)^{t}}{t^{t}}
.

To see why this is true, suppose that
m_{x}\geq ct
. For each
(x,y)\in S_{x}
, as in Theorem
4.6
let
Q_{x,y}=\left\{(x,r):(x,r)\in S\text{ and }r\leq y\right\}
. Note that

\displaystyle\left\{(y_{1},\dots,y_{t}):(\forall i\leq t)((x,y_{i})\in S)\text{ and }(\forall i\leq t-1)(\mu_{1}(Q_{x,y_{i+1}})+c<\mu_{1}(Q_{x,y_{i}}))\right\}\subseteq

\displaystyle\left\{(y_{1},\dots,y_{t}):(\forall i\leq t)((x,y_{i})\in S)\text{ and }(\forall i\leq t-1)(y_{i+1}+c<y_{i})\right\}.

Then we have
\mu_{t}(S^{\prime}_{x})=

\displaystyle\int_{y_{t}\in(0,n-(t-1)c)}\int_{y_{t-1}\in(y_{t}+c,n-(t-2)c)}\dots\int_{y_{1}\in(y_{2}+c,n)}\prod_{i=1}^{t}1_{(x,y_{i})\in S}d\mu_{1}(y_{1})d\mu_{1}(y_{2})\dots d\mu_{1}(y_{t})\geq

\displaystyle\int_{y_{t}\in(0,n-(t-1)c)}\int_{y_{t-1}\in(y_{t}+c,n-(t-2)c)}\dots\int_{y_{1}\in(y_{2}+c,n)}(\prod_{i=1}^{t}1_{(x,y_{i})\in S})(\prod_{i=1}^{t-1}1_{\mu_{1}(Q_{x,y_{i+1}})+c<\mu_{1}(Q_{x,y_{i}})})d\mu_{1}(y_{1})d\mu_{1}(y_{2})\dots d\mu_{1}(y_{t}).

Given any
(y_{1},\dots,y_{t})
with
(x,y_{i})\in S
for each
i\leq t
, note that we have
\mu_{1}(Q_{x,y_{i}})\in(0,m_{x})
for each
i\leq t
. Since
S
is open, we can write
S_{x}
as a countable union of intervals
I_{j}
with open endpoints. Thus there are only countably many
b\in(0,m_{x})
for which there does not exist
r
such that
\mu_{1}(Q_{x,r})=b
. Since countable sets have measure zero, the last integral is equal to

\displaystyle\int_{z_{t}\in(0,m_{x}-(t-1)c)}\int_{z_{t-1}\in(z_{t}+c,m_{x}-(t-2)c)}\dots\int_{z_{1}\in(z_{2}+c,m_{x})}1d\mu_{1}(z_{1})d\mu_{1}(z_{2})\dots d\mu_{1}(z_{t})\geq

\displaystyle\frac{(m_{x}-ct)^{t}}{t^{t}},

where the last inequality follows by Lemma
4.7
. Thus by Fubini’s theorem, we obtain
\mu_{t+1}(S^{\prime})=\int_{x\in[0,n]}\mu_{t}(S^{\prime}_{x})d\mu_{1}(x)\geq\int_{x\in[0,n]}\frac{\max((m_{x}-ct,0))^{t}}{t^{t}}d\mu_{1}(x)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be defined by
f(z)=\frac{\max(z-ct,0)^{t}}{t^{t}}
, so
f(m_{x})=\frac{\max(m_{x}-ct,0)^{t}}{t^{t}}
and

is convex. Thus we can write the last inequality in the last paragraph as
\mu_{t+1}(S^{\prime})\geq\int_{x\in[0,n]}f(m_{x})d\mu_{1}(x)
. Combining this with the inequality at the end of the second paragraph, we have
\int_{x\in[0,n]}f(m_{x})d\mu_{1}(x)=O(sn^{t})
. By Jensen’s inequality, we obtain
nf(\frac{\mu_{2}(S)}{n})=O(sn^{t})
, so
n(\frac{\frac{\mu_{2}(S)}{n}}{t}-c)^{t}=O(sn^{t})
or else
\frac{\mu_{2}(S)}{n}\leq ct
. Thus
\mu_{2}(S)=O(s^{\frac{1}{t}}n^{2-\frac{1}{t}})
.
∎

4.3
Disjoint unions of horizontal segments

Our next result is for any disjoint union of a finite number of horizontal segments
P
with no two points having the same
x
-coordinate and no two segments having the same
y
-coordinate, for which we prove a linear upper bound on
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
. This is in analogue with the linear upper bound on
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)
when
M
is a double permutation matrix
[
22
]
.

Theorem 4.9
.

If
P
is a disjoint union of a finite number of horizontal segments with no two points having the same
x
-coordinate and no two segments having the same
y
-coordinate, then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=O(n)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.3
, it suffices to prove that
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=O(n)
with the following restrictions on
P
. We assume that the consecutive rows of
P
are a distance of
c
apart, each segment in
P
has closed endpoints and length
\ell
, and there is a distance of
c
between the vertical lines that contain the closest endpoints of consecutive segments from left to right.

Suppose that
S\subseteq[0,n]^{2}
is an open
P
-free subset. We draw a grid on
S
with a total of
\lceil\frac{n}{c}\rceil^{2}
squares each of dimensions
c\times c
. The squares in this proof are closed, so they intersect at the boundaries. Note that the rightmost column and topmost row of squares in the grid may cover some area outside of
[0,n]^{2}
if
\frac{n}{c}
is not an integer. We construct a 0-1 matrix
B
of dimensions
\lceil\frac{n}{c}\rceil\times\lceil\frac{n}{c}\rceil
so that the
c\times c
square
U_{ij}
in the
i^{th}
row and
j^{th}
column of squares in the grid corresponds to the
(i,j)
entry
b_{ij}
of
B
.

To construct
B
, we add entries row by row, left to right. Suppose that we are on row
i
of
B
. If
U_{ij}
contains no point of
S
, then we set
b_{ij}=0
. If
U_{ij}
is the first square in the
i^{th}
row of squares in the grid to contain any point of
S
, then we set
b_{ij}=1
. Finally if
U_{ij}
contains a point of
S
but some other square in the
i^{th}
row of squares to the left of
U_{ij}
contains a point of
S
, then let
j^{\prime}
be the greatest integer less than
j
for which
b_{ij^{\prime}}=1
and let
Q
denote the restriction of
S
to the
j-j^{\prime}+1
squares between
U_{ij^{\prime}}
and
U_{ij}
inclusive. We set
b_{ij}=1
if and only if there exists a row
r
of
Q
for which
\mu_{1}(Q_{r})>\ell
, where
Q_{r}
denotes the
1
-dimensional restriction of
Q
to row
r
.

Let
P^{\prime}
be the finite subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
obtained from
P
by only including the left and right endpoints of each segment, so the cardinality of
P^{\prime}
is twice the number of segments in
P
. Note that
M_{P^{\prime}}
is a double permutation matrix, so
\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P^{\prime}})=O(n)
by
[
22
]
.

The main observation is that
B
must avoid
S(M_{P^{\prime}},1)
. To see why this is true, suppose for contradiction that
B
contains
S(M_{P^{\prime}},1)
, and consider a copy of
S(M_{P^{\prime}},1)
in
B
. Suppose that there are entries
b_{ij_{1}}
and
b_{ij_{2}}
in row
i
of
B
that contain ones in the copy of
S(M_{P^{\prime}},1)
. Then in the
i^{th}
row of squares in the grid, if
Q
is the restriction of
S
to the squares between
U_{ij_{1}}
and
U_{ij_{2}}
inclusive, we can find some row
r
such that
\mu_{1}(Q_{r})>\ell
. By Lemma
2.8
with
c=\ell
,
Q
must contain a horizontal segment of length
\ell
with closed endpoints. Note that we can do the same process with any entries in the same row of
B
that contain ones in the copy of
S(M_{P^{\prime}},1)
. This yields a set of horizontal segments of length
\ell
with a distance of at least
c
between consecutive rows and a distance of at least
c
between columns containing the closest endpoints of consecutive segments, since
B
contains
S(M_{P^{\prime}},1)
and each entry of
B
corresponds to a
c\times c
square. However this means that
S
contains
P
, which is a contradiction.

Now we know that the number of ones in
B
is at most
\operatorname{ex}(\lceil\frac{n}{c}\rceil,S(M_{P^{\prime}},1))=O(n)
by Lemma
2.11
and
[
22
]
, where the constant in the bound depends on
c
. For every entry
b_{ij}=1
in
B
, we define chunks which cover the elements of
S
. If
b_{ij}
is not the rightmost entry in row
i
with a
1
, then let
b_{ik}
be the next entry in row
i
after
b_{ij}
for which
b_{ik}=1
. The chunk
C_{ij}
consists of all squares in the
i^{th}
row of the grid between
U_{ij}
and
U_{ik}
, including
U_{ij}
but not
U_{ik}
. If
b_{ij}
is the rightmost entry in row
i
of
B
with a
1
, then the chunk
C_{ij}
consists of all squares
U_{ik}
for
k\geq j
.

Consider any chunk
Q=C_{ij}
of
S
. By definition, for every row
r
in
Q
, we must have
\mu_{1}(Q_{r})\leq\ell
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
r\in[0,c]
. Thus by Fubini’s theorem,
\mu_{2}(Q)=\int_{r\in[0,c]}\mu_{1}(Q_{r})d\mu_{1}(r)\leq c\ell
. Thus
\mu_{2}(S)\leq c\ell\operatorname{ex}(\lceil\frac{n}{c}\rceil,S(M_{P^{\prime}},1))=O(n)
.
∎

5
Subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}

In this section, we generalize the extremal function
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
and our results to higher dimensional sets of points. We start by defining extremal functions of forbidden
d
-dimensional 0-1 matrices. We say that a
d
-dimensional 0-1 matrix
A

contains
a
d
-dimensional 0-1 matrix
B
if some submatrix of
A
is either equal to
B
or can be turned into
B
by changing some ones to zeroes. Otherwise we say that
A

avoids

B
, and that
A
is
B
-free
. We define the
d
-dimensional extremal function
\operatorname{ex}(n,M,d)
as the maximum possible number of ones in a
d
-dimensional
M
-free 0-1 matrix with all dimensions equal to
n
. Note that the extremal function
\operatorname{ex}(n,M,d)
is the same as
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)
when
d=2
and
M
is a
2
-dimensional 0-1 matrix. The vast majority of research on
\operatorname{ex}(n,M,d)
has been for the case
d=2
, but several papers have focused on higher dimensions including
[
33
,
25
,
23
,
19
,
44
]
.

As with matrix extremal functions, we can generalize
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
from
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose that
P
and
S
are both subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
C_{i}(P)
be the set of all
i^{th}
coordinates of points in
P
. Similarly let
C_{i}(S)
be the set of all
i^{th}
coordinates of points in
S
. We say that
S

contains

P
if there exist functions
f_{i}:C_{i}(P)\rightarrow C_{i}(S)
for each
i=1,\dots,d
such that all statements below are true:

1.

For each
i=1,\dots,d
, we have
\frac{f_{i}(x)-f_{i}(x^{\prime})}{x-x^{\prime}}\geq 1
for all
x,x^{\prime}\in C_{i}(P)
with
x>x^{\prime}
.

2.

For all
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in P
, we have
(f_{1}(x_{1}),\dots,f_{d}(x_{d}))\in S
.

If
S
does not contain
P
, then
S

avoids

P
, i.e.
S
is
P
-free
. For any subset
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
n\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, define
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)
as the supremum of
\mu_{d}(S)
over all open
P
-free subsets
S\subseteq[0,n]^{d}
.

We start with two simple observations about
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)
which generalize some earlier observations.

Lemma 5.1
.

If
P
and
Q
are subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which
Q
contains
P
, then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,Q,d)
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For all
m\leq n
, we have
\operatorname{opx}(m,P,d)\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)
.

As in the
2
-dimensional case, we have that
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)
is continuous in
n
for
n>0
.

Lemma 5.3
.

For all
n>0
and all
\epsilon
with
0<\epsilon<n
, we have
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)\leq\operatorname{opx}(n+\epsilon,P,d)<\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)+d(2n)^{d-1}\epsilon
.

Proposition 5.4
.

The function
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)
is continuous in
n
for
n>0
.

Proof.

Fix
n_{0}>0
and
\epsilon>0
. By Lemma
5.3
, for
\delta=\min(\frac{\epsilon}{d(2n_{0})^{d-1}},\frac{n_{0}}{3})
and
n<n_{0}+\delta
we have
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)\leq\operatorname{opx}(n_{0}+\delta,P,d)<\operatorname{opx}(n_{0},P,d)+\epsilon
. Moreover if
n>n_{0}-\delta
, then we have
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)\geq\operatorname{opx}(n_{0}-\delta,P,d)>\operatorname{opx}(n_{0},P,d)-d(2(n_{0}-\delta))^{d-1}\delta>\operatorname{opx}(n_{0},P,d)-\epsilon
. Thus we have shown that for all
n_{0}>0
and for all
\epsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
for which
|\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)-\operatorname{opx}(n_{0},P,d)|<\epsilon
for all
n
such that
|n-n_{0}|<\delta
.
∎

Given a subset
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, let
\Pr(P)
denote the subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in\Pr(P)
if and only if there exists
x_{d+1}
such that
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},x_{d+1})\in P
. The next observation is analogous to a fact about projections of
(d+1)
-dimensional 0-1 matrices into
d
dimensions.

Lemma 5.5
.

For all
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
, we have
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d+1)\geq n\operatorname{opx}(n,\Pr(P),d)
.

Proof.

Let
S_{t}\subseteq[0,n]^{d}
for
t\in\mathbb{N}
be a sequence of
\Pr(P)
-free open subsets with the property that
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{d}(S_{t})=\operatorname{opx}(n,\Pr(P),d)
. Note that since
S_{t}
is open for each
t
, we have
S_{t}\subseteq(0,n)^{d}
. For each
t\in\mathbb{N}
, let
Z_{t}\subseteq(0,n)^{d+1}
be the set such that for all
x_{1},\dots,x_{d+1}\in(0,n)
we have
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},x_{d+1})\in Z_{t}
if and only if
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in S_{t}
. Then
Z_{t}
is open, since it is the Cartesian product of open sets. Also
Z_{t}
is
P
-free, or else
S_{t}
would contain
\Pr(P)
. Thus
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d+1)\geq\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mu_{d+1}(Z_{t})=\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}n\mu_{d}(S_{t})=n\operatorname{opx}(n,\Pr(P),d)
.
∎

It is easy to find examples for which the last result is sharp. For example, let
P
be any subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
such that all elements of
P
have the same last coordinate.

Proposition 5.6
.

If all elements of
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
have the same last coordinate, then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d+1)=n\operatorname{opx}(n,\Pr(P),d)
.

Proof.

The lower bound
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d+1)\geq n\operatorname{opx}(n,\Pr(P),d)
follows from the last lemma. For the upper bound, let
S\subseteq[0,n]^{d+1}
be open and
P
-free. For each
z\in[0,n]
, let
S_{z}
be the subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in S_{z}
if and only if
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},z)\in S
.

Then
\mu_{d}(S_{z})\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,\Pr(P),d)
for all
z\in[0,n]
, or else
S
would contain
P
. Since
S
is open, the function
\mu_{d}(S_{z}):[0,n]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function by Fubini’s theorem, so it is Lebesgue integrable on
[0,n]
. Thus
\mu_{d+1}(S)=\int_{z\in[0,n]}\mu_{d}(S_{z})d\mu_{1}(z)\leq n\operatorname{opx}(n,\Pr(P),d)
.
∎

Using the last proposition, we can find many subsets
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
for which
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)=\Theta(n^{d})
for
d>2
.

Corollary 5.7
.

If
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is open, let
P^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d>2
be obtained from
P
by letting
(x,y,z_{1},\dots,z_{d-2})\in P^{\prime}
if and only if
(x,y)\in P
and
(z_{1},\dots,z_{d-2})=(0,\dots,0)
. If
Q_{P^{\prime}}
denotes the set of points in
P^{\prime}
with rational coordinates, then
\operatorname{opx}(n,Q_{P^{\prime}},d)=\Theta(n^{d})
.

Proof.

This follows from Theorem
4.5
by applying Proposition
5.6
a total of
d-2
times to
Q_{P}
.
∎

By the last corollary and Lemma
5.1
, we also obtain
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)=\Theta(n^{d})
for any open subset
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Corollary 5.8
.

If
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is open, then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)=\Theta(n^{d})
and
\operatorname{opx}(n,Q_{P},d)=\Theta(n^{d})
.

Given any finite subset
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define
M_{P}
to be the
d
-dimensional 0-1 matrix for which the length of the
i^{th}
dimension of
M_{P}
is the same as the number of distinct values for the
i^{th}
coordinate of the points in
P
for each
i
, the indices of the
i^{th}
dimension of
M_{P}
correspond to the values of the
i^{th}
coordinate of the points in
P
in order, and
M_{P}
has a one in each entry corresponding to a point of
P
and a zero in each other entry.

The next lemma is proved analogously to Lemma
2.11
. Note for
d>2
that
S(M_{P},k)
is defined analogously to the definition for
d=2
.

Lemma 5.9
.

For all finite subsets
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\operatorname{ex}(n,S(M_{P},k),d)\leq(k+1)^{d}\operatorname{ex}(\lceil\frac{n}{k+1}\rceil,M_{P},d)
.

As with the last lemma, the proof of the next lemma is analogous to the
2
-dimensional case in Lemma
2.12
.

Lemma 5.10
.

Suppose that
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a finite subset in which the differences between all consecutive values for each coordinate are in
[c_{1},c_{2}]
. Let
P^{\prime}
be a dilation of
P
by a factor of
q>1
, i.e. for every point
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in P
, the point
(qx_{1},\dots,qx_{d})\in P^{\prime}
. Then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime},d)\leq(\lceil\frac{qc_{2}}{c_{1}}\rceil+1)^{d}\operatorname{opx}(\lceil\frac{\lceil\frac{n}{c_{1}}\rceil}{\lceil\frac{qc_{2}}{c_{1}}\rceil+1}\rceil c_{1},P,d)
.

Using the last two lemmas, we obtain the next theorem with essentially the same proof method as in Theorem
3.1
. This lets us derive corollaries about
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)
from the literature on
\operatorname{ex}(n,M,d)
.

Theorem 5.11
.

For all finite subsets
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)=\Theta(\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P},d))
, where the constant in the bound depends on the differences between the consecutive values for each coordinate of
P
.

Klazar and Marcus proved that
\operatorname{ex}(n,M,d)=O(n^{d-1})
for every
d
-dimensional permutation matrix
M

[
36
]
. This bound was later sharpened in
[
25
]
where it was shown that
\operatorname{ex}(n,M,d)=2^{O(k)}n^{d-1}
for every
d
-dimensional permutation matrix
P
with
k
ones. This implies the corollary below.

Corollary 5.12
.

Let
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
|P|=k
for which no points in
P
have any common coordinates, and the differences between the consecutive values for each coordinate of
P
are all at most
c
. Then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)=2^{O(k)}n^{d-1}
, where the constant in the bound depends on
c
.

In the same paper
[
25
]
, the authors proved a generalization of the Kővari-Sós-Turán theorem for
d
-dimensional 0-1 matrices by showing that
\operatorname{ex}(n,J_{k_{1},\dots,k_{d}},d)=O(n^{d-\alpha(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})})
, where
J_{k_{1},\dots,k_{d}}
is the
d
-dimensional matrix of all ones with dimensions
k_{1}\times\dots\times k_{d}
and
\alpha(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})=\frac{\max(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})}{k_{1}\dots k_{d}}
. As a result, for every
d
-dimensional 0-1 matrix
M
there exists a constant
\epsilon>0
such that
\operatorname{ex}(n,M,d)=O(n^{d-\epsilon})
. This is because
M
is contained in the
d
-dimensional all-ones matrix of the same dimensions.

Corollary 5.13
.

For every finite subset
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
M_{P}=J_{k_{1},\dots,k_{d}}
, we have
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)=O(n^{d-\alpha(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})})
.

Corollary 5.14
.

For every finite subset
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
there exists a constant
\epsilon>0
such that
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)=O(n^{d-\epsilon})
.

If
M
is a
k\times k
permutation matrix for
k\geq 2
, let
M_{i}
be the
3
-dimensional 0-1 matrix of dimensions
k\times k\times i
for which entry
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})
of
M_{i}
is 1 if and only if entry
(x_{1},x_{2})
of
M
is 1. In
[
19
]
, we proved a more general result which implies that
\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{2},3)=\Theta(n^{\frac{5}{2}})
and
\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{3},3)=\Theta(n^{\frac{8}{3}})
. This implies the next corollary about
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)
.

Corollary 5.15
.

Let
P
be a set of
k
points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with all coordinates distinct. Let
P_{i}
be the set of
ik
points in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
which contains
(x,y,j)
for each point
(x,y)\in P
and each
j=1,2,\dots,i
. Then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P_{2},3)=\Theta(n^{\frac{5}{2}})
and
\operatorname{opx}(n,P_{3},3)=\Theta(n^{\frac{8}{3}})
.

We will next prove a much stronger result than the last corollary. It is a generalization of our strengthening of the Kővari-Sós-Turán theorem. For any
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and for each integer
t\geq 2
and real
c>0
, let
Q_{P,t,c}
denote the set of points
\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y):(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in P\text{ and }y\in\left\{c,2c,\dots,tc\right\}\right\}
. For example, if
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a segment, then
Q_{P,2,c}
is a
3
-dimensional set of points that looks like an equal sign (
=
) and
Q_{P,3,c}
is a
3
-dimensional set of points that looks like an equivalence symbol (
\equiv
).

The following proof is like the proof of Theorem
4.8
in Section
4
, but more general. Part of the idea for this result is based on the main result of our paper
[
19
]
for
d
-dimensional 0-1 matrices.

Theorem 5.16
.

For all
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and fixed
t\geq 2
,
\operatorname{opx}(n,Q_{P,t,c},d+1)=O(\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)^{\frac{1}{t}}n^{d+1-\frac{d}{t}}+n^{d})
, where the constants in the bound depend on
t
and
c
.

Proof.

Let
S
be an open
Q_{P,t,c}
-free subset of
[0,n]^{d+1}
. Let
S^{\prime}
be the
(t+d)
-dimensional set of points of the form
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{1},\dots y_{t})
for which
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{i})\in S
for each
i=1,2,\dots,t
and
y_{i}-y_{i+1}>c
for each
i=1,\dots,t-1
. First, we note that
S^{\prime}
is open. To see why this is true, define
T_{1}=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{1},\dots y_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{t+d}:(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{i})\in S\text{ for all }i=1,\dots,t\right\}
. Define
T_{2}=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{1},\dots y_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{t+d}:y_{i}-y_{i+1}>c\text{ for all }i=1,2,\dots,t-1\right\}
. Clearly
T_{2}
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{t+d}
. Since
S
is open, for every point
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y)\in S
there exists
r>0
such that the open ball of radius
r
centered at
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y)
is a subset of
S
, i.e.
B((x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y),r)\subseteq S
. For each
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{1},\dots y_{t})\in T_{1}
, we have
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{i})\in S
for all
i=1,2,\dots,t
, so there exist
r_{i}>0
for each
i=1,2,\dots,t
such that
B((x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{i}),r_{i})\subseteq S
for all
i=1,2,\dots,t
. For every
(x_{1}^{\prime},\dots,x_{d}^{\prime},y^{\prime}_{1},\dots y^{\prime}_{t})\in B((x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{1},\dots y_{t}),\min(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{t}))
, we have
\sum_{j=1}^{d}(x_{j}^{\prime}-x_{j})^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}(y^{\prime}_{i}-y_{i})^{2}<\min(r_{1}^{2},r_{2}^{2},\dots,r_{t}^{2})
, so
\sum_{j=1}^{d}(x_{j}^{\prime}-x_{j})^{2}+(y^{\prime}_{i}-y_{i})^{2}<r_{i}^{2}
for all
i=1,2,\dots,t
, which implies that
(x_{1}^{\prime},\dots,x_{d}^{\prime},y^{\prime}_{i})\in S
for all
i=1,2,\dots,t
, so
(x_{1}^{\prime},\dots,x_{d}^{\prime},y^{\prime}_{1},\dots y^{\prime}_{t})\in T_{1}
. Thus
B((x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{1},\dots y_{t}),\min(r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{t}))\subseteq T_{1}
, so
T_{1}
is open. Hence
S^{\prime}
is also open, since
S^{\prime}=T_{1}\cap T_{2}
.

The points
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{1},\dots y_{t})\in S^{\prime}
must satisfy
0<y_{t}<n-(t-1)c
and
y_{i+1}+c<y_{i}<n-(i-1)c
for all
i=1,\dots,t-1
. Thus the projection of these points onto the last
t
coordinates is a
t
-dimensional solid of volume
\Theta(n^{t})
by Lemma
4.7
. For each fixed
y_{1},\dots,y_{t}
with
y_{1},\dots,y_{t}\in(0,n)
, let
S^{\prime\prime}_{y_{1},\dots,y_{t}}
denote the set of points
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d},b_{1},\dots,b_{t})\in S^{\prime}
with
b_{i}=y_{i}
for each
i
. Then
\mu_{d}(S^{\prime\prime}_{y_{1},\dots,y_{t}})\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)
or else
S
would contain
Q_{P,t}
. Since
S^{\prime}
is open, by Fubini’s theorem the function
\mu_{d}(S^{\prime\prime}_{y_{1},\dots,y_{t}}):[0,n]^{t}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a measurable function, so it is Lebesgue integrable on
[0,n]^{t}
. Thus
\mu_{t+d}(S^{\prime})=\int_{(y_{1},\dots,y_{t})\in[0,n]^{t}}\mu_{d}(S^{\prime\prime}_{y_{1},\dots,y_{t}})d\mu_{t}(y_{1},\dots,y_{t})=O(\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)n^{t})
.

For all
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in[0,n]^{d}
, let
S^{\prime}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}}
denote the set of points
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d},b_{1},\dots,b_{t})\in S^{\prime}
with
a_{j}=x_{j}
for each
1\leq j\leq d
, let
S_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}}
denote the set of points
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d},b)\in S
with
a_{j}=x_{j}
for each
1\leq j\leq d
, and let
m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}}=\mu_{1}(S_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}})
. For
i<d
, let
S_{x_{1},\dots,x_{i}}
denote the set of points
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d},b)\in S
with
a_{j}=x_{j}
for each
1\leq j\leq i
, and let
m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{i}}=\mu_{d+1-i}(S_{x_{1},\dots,x_{i}})
.

For all
x_{1},\dots,x_{d}
with
m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}}\geq ct
, we must have
\mu_{t}(S^{\prime}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}})\geq\frac{\max(m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}}-ct,0)^{t}}{t^{t}}
. To see why this is true, suppose that
m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}}\geq ct
. For each
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y)\in S_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}}
, let
Q_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y}=\left\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},r):(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},r)\in S\text{ and }r\leq y\right\}
. Note that

\displaystyle\left\{(y_{1},\dots,y_{t}):(\forall i\leq t)((x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{i})\in S)\text{ and }(\forall i\leq t-1)(\mu_{1}(Q_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{i+1}})+c<\mu_{1}(Q_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{i}}))\right\}\subseteq

\displaystyle\left\{(y_{1},\dots,y_{t}):(\forall i\leq t)((x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{i})\in S)\text{ and }(\forall i\leq t-1)(y_{i+1}+c<y_{i})\right\}.

Then we have
\mu_{t}(S^{\prime}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}})=

\displaystyle\int_{y_{t}\in(0,n-(t-1)c)}\int_{y_{t-1}\in(y_{t}+c,n-(t-2)c)}\dots\int_{y_{1}\in(y_{2}+c,n)}\prod_{i=1}^{t}1_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{i})\in S}d\mu_{1}(y_{1})d\mu_{1}(y_{2})\dots d\mu_{1}(y_{t})\geq

\displaystyle\int_{y_{t}\in(0,n-(t-1)c)}\dots\int_{y_{1}\in(y_{2}+c,n)}(\prod_{i=1}^{t}1_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{i})\in S})(\prod_{i=1}^{t-1}1_{\mu_{1}(Q_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{i+1}})+c<\mu_{1}(Q_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{i}})})d\mu_{1}(y_{1})d\mu_{1}(y_{2})\dots d\mu_{1}(y_{t}).

Given any
(y_{1},\dots,y_{t})
with
(x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{i})\in S
for each

, note that
\mu_{1}(Q_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d},y_{i}})\in(0,m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}})
for each
i\leq t
. Since

is open, we can write

as a countable union of intervals

with open endpoints. Thus there are only countably many

for which there does not exist

\mu_{1}(Q_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d},r})=b
. Since countable sets have measure zero, the last integral is equal to

\displaystyle\int_{z_{t}\in(0,m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}}-(t-1)c)}\int_{z_{t-1}\in(z_{t}+c,m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}}-(t-2)c)}\dots\int_{z_{1}\in(z_{2}+c,m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}})}1d\mu_{1}(z_{1})d\mu_{1}(z_{2})\dots d\mu_{1}(z_{t})\geq

\displaystyle\frac{(m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}}-ct)^{t}}{t^{t}}.

where the last inequality is by Lemma

\mu_{t+d}(S^{\prime})=\int_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in[0,n]^{d}}\mu_{t}(S^{\prime}_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}})d\mu_{d}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\geq\int_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in[0,n]^{d}}\frac{\max((m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}}-ct,0))^{t}}{t^{t}}d\mu_{d}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
.

Let

be defined by

f(m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}})=\frac{\max(m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}}-ct,0)^{t}}{t^{t}}
and

\mu_{t+d}(S^{\prime})\geq\int_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in[0,n]^{d}}f(m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}})d\mu_{d}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})
. With the inequality at the end of the second paragraph, we have
\int_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})\in[0,n]^{d}}f(m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{d}})d\mu_{d}(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})=O(\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)n^{t})
.

For each

n^{d-j}\int_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{j})\in[0,n]^{j}}f(\frac{m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{j}}}{n^{d-j}})d\mu_{j}(x_{1},\dots,x_{j})\leq n^{d-j-1}\int_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{j})\in[0,n]^{j}}\int_{x_{j+1}\in[0,n]}f(\frac{m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{j+1}}}{n^{d-j-1}})d\mu_{1}(x_{j+1})d\mu_{j}(x_{1},\dots,x_{j})

n^{d-j-1}\int_{(x_{1},\dots,x_{j+1})\in[0,n]^{j+1}}f(\frac{m_{x_{1},\dots,x_{j+1}}}{n^{d-j-1}})d\mu_{j+1}(x_{1},\dots,x_{j+1})

for all

Thus by Jensen’s inequality combined with the preceding inequality, we have
n^{d}f(\frac{\mu_{d+1}(S)}{n^{d}})\leq n^{d-1}\int_{x_{1}\in[0,n]}f(\frac{m_{x_{1}}}{n^{d-1}})d\mu_{1}(x_{1})=O(\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)n^{t})

∎

As corollaries of Theorem

∎

6
Discussion

We proved that the extremal function
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
fully encompasses the Zarankiewicz problem up to a constant factor, and more generally
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
fully encompasses the matrix extremal function
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)
up to a constant factor. Any past results on the Zarankiewicz problem or more generally on
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)
imply corresponding results for
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
. While the inputs for
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)
are discrete (a positive integer
n
and a 0-1 matrix
M
), the inputs for
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
form a continuum (a real number
n
and any subset
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
).

We showed that
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Theta(n^{2})
for any open set
P
, where the constants in the lower bound depend on
P
. We proved the stronger result that
\operatorname{opx}(n,Q_{P})=\Theta(n^{2})
, where
Q_{P}
is the set of points with rational coordinates that are contained in
P
, but these bounds are only sharp up to a constant factor. A more general problem is to find sharper bounds on
\operatorname{opx}(n,P,d)
for open subsets
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Although our bounds for open subsets
P
are sharp up to a constant factor that depends on
P
, it is a natural problem to determine the exact value of
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
, particularly for certain open sets like open squares or open balls.

On the other hand, we found other bounded infinite subsets with extremal functions that behaved much like 0-1 matrix extremal functions. We showed that sets of points
E
that look like equal signs (
=
) have
\operatorname{opx}(n,E)=\Theta(n^{\frac{3}{2}})
, regardless of how long the horizontal segments are. On the other hand, sets of points
A
that look like plus signs (
+
) have
\operatorname{opx}(n,A)=\Theta(n)
by Theorem
4.1
and Lemma
2.9
. There are other natural sets of points that we have not considered, such as sets of points in the shape of
\sqcup
or H. One could also consider infinite sets of points in the shape of the boundary of a rectangle (
\square
) and other more complex grid-like sets of points.

All of the forbidden infinite subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in the previous paragraph are unions of horizontal or vertical line segments, but some extend naturally to sets formed by diagonal segments. For example, if
P
is the subset consisting of the segment with endpoints at
(0,0)
and
(1,0)
and the segment with endpoints at
(0,1)
and
(1,2)
, what is
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
? In this case, note that
P
contains the subset which consists of the four points
Q=\left\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,2)\right\}
, and
\operatorname{opx}(n,Q)=\Theta(n\log{n})
by Theorem
3.1
and
[
41
]
, so
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Omega(n\log{n})
.

Conjecture 6.1
.

If
P
is the subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
consisting of the segment with endpoints at
(0,0)
and
(1,0)
and the segment with endpoints at
(0,1)
and
(1,2)
, then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Theta(n\log{n})
.

We also have a conjecture for any disjoint union of horizontal segments
P
with no two points having the same
x
-coordinate, for which we conjecture a quasilinear upper bound on
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
. This is in analogue with the quasilinear upper bound on
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)
when
M
is a 0-1 matrix with no pair of ones in the same column
[
30
]
.

Conjecture 6.2
.

If
P
is a disjoint union of horizontal segments with no two points having the same
x
-coordinate, then there exists a constant
t
such that
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=O(n2^{\alpha(n)^{t}})
.

Similar questions could also be asked for sets of points that are formed by arcs instead of only segments. We also wonder what is the supremum of
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
over all subsets
P\subseteq[0,1]^{2}
for which all points in
P
have different
x
-coordinates and all points in
P
have different
y
-coordinates.

In Section
2
, we found operations that can be performed on sets of points
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
to obtain a new set of points
P^{\prime}
such that
\operatorname{opx}(n,P^{\prime})=\Theta(\operatorname{opx}(n,P))
. For example, if
P
has a rightmost column containing some point
p
, then you can add a horizontal segment of length
c
to
P
with its left endpoint on
p
to obtain a new subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and this only increases
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
by at most
cn
. Also we showed that dilating
finite
subsets
P
only changes
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
by at most a constant multiplicative factor. For a better understanding of
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
, it would be useful to find more operations. For example, Keszegh proved in
[
30
]
that if
J
is a 0-1 matrix that has a one in the bottom right corner and
K
is a 0-1 matrix that has a one in the top left corner, and
R
is obtained from translating
J
and
K
so that the bottom right corner of
J
overlaps the top left corner of
K
and filling the blank space with zeroes, then
\operatorname{ex}(n,R)\leq\operatorname{ex}(n,J)+\operatorname{ex}(n,K)
. Is an analogous operation possible for subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
?

Conjecture 6.3
.

Suppose that
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
contains the point
(c,d)
and all other points in
P
have
x
-coordinate at most
c
and
y
-coordinate at most
d
. Suppose that
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
contains the point
(c,d)
and all other points in
Q
have
x
-coordinate at least
c
and
y
-coordinate at least
d
. Then
\operatorname{opx}(n,P\cup Q)\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,P)+\operatorname{opx}(n,Q)
.

6.1
When the size of
P
grows with
n

All of the results in this paper focused on forbidden
bounded
sets of points. Several papers have investigated
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)
and corresponding extremal functions of forbidden sequences where the size of the forbidden 0-1 matrix
M
and the size of the forbidden sequence are allowed to grow with respect to
n

[
40
,
42
,
21
,
26
]
. For example, we showed in
[
21
]
that if
M_{s}
is any
2\times s
0-1 matrix that has ones in both the first and last columns, then
\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{s})=\Omega(n^{2-o(1)})
if and only if
s(n)=\Omega(n^{1-o(1)})
, answering a question from
[
42
]
.

Analogously, it is natural to investigate
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
where the size of the forbidden subset
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
is allowed to grow with respect to
n
. Let
Q_{s}
be any set of points with minimum
x
-coordinate
0
, maximum
x
-coordinate
s
, and all
y
-coordinates equal to
0
or
1
. Then
P_{s,2}
contains
Q_{s}
with
P_{s,2}
defined as in Theorem
4.6
, so
\operatorname{opx}(n,Q_{s})\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,P_{s,2})
.

Proposition 6.4
.

For all subsets
Q_{s}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
with minimum
x
-coordinate
0
, maximum
x
-coordinate
s
, and all
y
-coordinates equal to
0
or
1
, we have
\operatorname{opx}(n,Q_{s})=\Omega(n^{2-o(1)})
if and only if
s(n)=\Omega(n^{1-o(1)})
.

Proof.

If
s=\Omega(n^{1-o(1)})
, then the open set
(0,s)\times(0,n)
avoids
Q_{s}
, which shows that
\operatorname{opx}(n,Q_{s})=\Omega(n^{2-o(1)})
.

If
s\neq\Omega(n^{1-o(1)})
, then there is a constant
\alpha<1
and an infinite sequence
x_{1}<x_{2}<\dots
of positive reals such that
\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}x_{i}=\infty
and
s(x_{i})<x_{i}^{\alpha}
for all
i>0
. If
s(n)<n^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha<1
, then we have
\operatorname{opx}(n,Q_{s})\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,P_{s,2})=O(n^{\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{3}{2}})
by Theorem
4.6
. Note that
\frac{\alpha}{2}+\frac{3}{2}<2
, since
\alpha<1
. Thus if
s\neq\Omega(n^{1-o(1)})
, then
\operatorname{opx}(n,Q_{s})\neq\Omega(n^{2-o(1)})
. Thus we have shown that
\operatorname{opx}(n,Q_{s})=\Omega(n^{2-o(1)})
if and only if
s(n)=\Omega(n^{1-o(1)})
.
∎

6.2
Saturation functions for subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}

Another potential direction for future research is saturation functions. Saturation problems have been studied for decades, with much of the focus on graphs
[
6
,
8
,
10
,
34
]
, posets
[
12
,
31
]
, and set systems
[
11
,
28
]
. Recently a saturation function was introduced for forbidden 0-1 matrices
[
2
]
. We say 0-1 matrix
A
is
saturating
for 0-1 matrix
M
if
A
avoids
M
but any matrix obtained from
A
by changing a zero to a one must contain
M
. Define
\operatorname{sat}(n,M)
as the minimum number of ones in an
n\times n
0-1 matrix that is saturating for
M
. Fulek and Keszegh proved that
\operatorname{sat}(n,M)=O(1)
or
\operatorname{sat}(n,M)=\Theta(n)
for all 0-1 matrices
M

[
14
]
. The results in
[
14
]
showed that
\operatorname{sat}(n,M)=\Theta(n)
for almost all
k\times k
0-1 matrices
M
, but infinite families of 0-1 matrices
M
with
\operatorname{sat}(n,M)=O(1)
were found in
[
1
]
and
[
20
]
. In the same way that
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
encompasses
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)
, it seems natural to investigate if there is a saturation function for subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that encompasses
\operatorname{sat}(n,M)
.

Say that an open subset
S\subseteq[0,n]^{2}
is
saturating
for
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
in
[0,n]^{2}
if
S
avoids
P
but any open subset
S^{\prime}\subseteq[0,n]^{2}
that is a proper superset of
S
must contain
P
. So we can define
\operatorname{osat}(n,P)
as the infimum of
\mu_{2}(S)
over all open subsets
S\subseteq[0,n]^{2}
that are saturating for
P
in
[0,n]^{2}
. Note that
\operatorname{osat}(n,P)
is only defined if there exists an open subet of
[0,n]^{2}
that is saturating for
P
in
[0,n]^{2}
. Clearly
\operatorname{osat}(n,P)\leq\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
whenever
\operatorname{osat}(n,P)
is defined, and this is sharp. We can see that this is sharp for the simplest example of nonempty
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Lemma 6.5
.

If
P
consists of a single point, then
\operatorname{osat}(n,P)=0
.

Proof.

For all
n>0
, the empty set is saturating for
P
in
[0,n]^{2}
.
∎

Like the 0-1 matrix saturation function
\operatorname{sat}(n,M)
, we conjecture that the subset saturation function
\operatorname{osat}(n,P)
is either
O(1)
or
\Theta(n)
for all subsets
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
on which
\operatorname{osat}(n,P)
is defined.

Conjecture 6.6
.

For any subset
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\operatorname{osat}(n,P)
is defined, either
\operatorname{osat}(n,P)=O(1)
or
\operatorname{osat}(n,P)=\Theta(n)
, where the constants in the bounds depend on
P
.

We conjecture that
\operatorname{osat}(n,P)
is defined for every finite subset
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and that there is a relationship between
\operatorname{osat}(n,P)
and
\operatorname{sat}(n,M_{P})
for finite subsets
P
that is analogous to the relationship between
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
and
\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P})
in Theorem
3.1
.

Conjecture 6.7
.

For every finite subset
P\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the saturation function
\operatorname{osat}(n,P)
is defined and we have
\operatorname{osat}(n,P)=\Theta(\operatorname{sat}(n,M_{P}))
.

A different saturation function for 0-1 matrices was investigated in
[
7
]
, it would also be natural to see if there is some saturation function for subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
that encompasses the function in
[
7
]
. Another possible research direction is to investigate other natural variants of the definitions for
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
and
\operatorname{osat}(n,P)
. In our definition of
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
, we required the
P
-free subsets
S\subseteq[0,n]^{2}
to be open. This allowed us to show that
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)=\Theta(\operatorname{ex}(n,M_{P}))
, along with many other results. It would be interesting to see how the results in this paper change if we require the
P
-free subsets
S
to be closed instead. Alternatively we could require the sets
S
to be Borel sets, or to be Lebesgue measurable sets in the definitions of
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
and
\operatorname{osat}(n,P)
.

It would also make sense to investigate
\operatorname{opx}(n,P)
and
\operatorname{osat}(n,P)
over all open
P
-free subsets
S\subseteq[0,m]\times[0,n]
. This is in analogue with the 0-1 matrix extremal function
\operatorname{ex}(n,M)
, which has a more general variant
\operatorname{ex}(m,n,M)
that maximizes the number of ones among all
m\times n

M
-free 0-1 matrices. Other regions besides rectangles could also be considered for replacing
[0,n]^{2}
, such as balls of radius
n
.
