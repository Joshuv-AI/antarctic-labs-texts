Interior inverse problem for global conservative multipeakon solutions of the Camassa-Holm equation
Tao Liu, Kang Lyu

Abstract
. We consider the interior inverse problem associated with the global conservative multipeakon solution of the Camassa-Holm equation. Based on the inverse spectral theory on the half-line and the oscillation property of eigenfunctions, some (non)uniqueness results of the interior inverse problem are obtained. In addition, we give the trace formula, which connects the global conservative multipeakon solution with the corresponding eigenvalues and normalized eigenfunctions.

(1.2)

Here

is called the weight function,

Inverse spectral theory for (

The multipeakon solution of the CH equation is the solution of the form

(1.3)

which represents the interaction of

is definite, then the inverse problem is solvable for any

. Otherwise, the inverse problem is not always solvable. This causes the collision between some peaks, which leads to finite-time blow-up resembling wave breaking. Bressan and Constantin
[
5

introduced the pair

to describe the overall picture of the collision, where
\mu
is a non-negative Borel measure with the absolutely continuous part determined by

\mu_{ac}(B,t)=\int_{B}|u(x,t)|^{2}+|u_{x}(x,t)|^{2}dx,\ t\in\mathbb{R},

for each Borel set

The pair

[

,
19

30

(1.5)

In the indefinite case, the inverse problem is always solvable within the class of spectral problem (
1.5

The second main result of this paper is the trace formula, which connects the global conservative multipeakon solution

2.
The Generalized Spectral Problem

In this section, we give some preliminary assertions about the generalized spectral problem. We will closely follow the notation employed in
[
19
]
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed.
Suppose that
x_{1}<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{n}
. Define the measures
\omega
and
v
by

\omega=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}\delta_{x_{i}},\ v=\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i}\delta_{x_{i}},

(2.1)

where
\omega_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
,
v_{i}\geq 0
,
\delta_{x_{i}}
is the Dirac measure at
x_{i},\ i=1,\cdots,n
. We always assume that

|\omega_{i}|+v_{i}>0,\ i=1,\cdots,n.

(2.2)

Consider the generalized spectral problem

Hf=-f^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{4}f(x)=z\omega(x)f(x)+z^{2}v(x)f(x),\ x\in\mathbb{R},

(2.3)

where
\omega
and
v
are defined by (
2.1
).
Note that equation (
2.3
) has to be interpreted in the sense of distributions, namely, a function
f
satisfies (
2.3
) if and only if

-f^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{4}f(x)=0,x\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\},

(2.4)

and for any
i=1,\cdots,n,

\displaystyle f(x_{i}-)=f(x_{i}+),

(2.5)

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x_{i}-)=f^{\prime}(x_{i}

\displaystyle+)+z\omega_{i}f(x_{i}+)+z^{2}v_{i}f(x_{i}+).

(2.6)

We mention that
f
is differentiable on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}\}
. We define

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x_{i}):=f^{\prime}(x_{i}-),\ i=1,\cdots,n,

(2.7)

so that
f^{\prime}
is left-continuous on
\mathbb{R}
.

The values of the parameter
z
for which (
2.3
) has nontrivial bounded solutions are called eigenvalues of
H
, and the corresponding nontrivial bounded solutions are called eigenfunctions. All the eigenvalues are real (see for example Theorem
6.8
or
[
19
]
).
The set of eigenvalues is called the spectrum of
H
(denoted by
\sigma(H)
).

For any
z\in\mathbb{C}
, there exist solutions
\phi_{\pm}(z,\cdot)
of (
2.3
) satisfying

\phi_{\pm}(z,x)=e^{\mp\frac{x}{2}}

(2.8)

for all
x
near
\pm\infty
. The Wronskian of
\phi_{+}(z,x),\phi_{-}(z,x)
, which is independent of
x
, is given by

W(z)=\phi_{+}(z,x)\phi_{-}^{\prime}(z,x)-\phi_{+}^{\prime}(z,x)\phi_{-}(z,x),z\in\mathbb{C}.

(2.9)

Note that by (
2.3
), (
2.5
) and (
2.6
),
W(z)
is a polynomial with degree
\leq 2n
.
For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
W(\lambda)=0
if and only if
\phi_{+}(\lambda,x)
and
\phi_{-}(\lambda,x)
are linear dependent. Namely, there exists a nonzero constant
c_{\lambda}
such that

\phi_{-}(\lambda,x)=c_{\lambda}\phi_{+}(\lambda,x).

(2.10)

By (
2.8
), we know that the zeros of
W(z)
coincide with
\sigma(H)
. Therefore,
\sigma(H)
consists of finitely many eigenvalues. Notice that
0\notin\sigma(H)
because of

\displaystyle{W(0)=1}.

(2.11)

For any eigenvalue
\lambda
, denote by

\gamma_{\lambda}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\phi_{+}(\lambda,x)|^{2}d\omega(x)+2\lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\phi_{+}(\lambda,x)|^{2}dv(x).

(2.12)

Applying (
2.3
), by some calculations one can obtain

\lambda\gamma_{\lambda}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\phi_{+}(\lambda,x)|^{2}dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\phi_{+}^{\prime}(\lambda,x)|^{2}dx+\lambda^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\phi_{+}(\lambda,x)|^{2}dv(x)>0.

We call
\lambda\gamma_{\lambda}^{2}
the (modified) norming constant associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda
.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
19
, Proposition 3.1]
For any
\lambda\in\sigma(H)
,
\lambda
is real and

\dot{W}(\lambda)=-c_{\lambda}\gamma_{\lambda}^{2}\neq 0,

(2.13)

where

is defined by (
2.10
).

3.
Interior Inverse Problem I

In this section we consider the interior inverse problem with the case that
\varphi_{i}(a)\neq 0
(see (
3.1
) for the definition of
\varphi_{i}(a)
) for any
i=1,\cdots,N
.
Since
\sigma(H)
consists of finitely many eigenvalues, we suppose that

\displaystyle\sigma(H)=\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N},

where
N\leq 2n
,
\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots<\lambda_{N}
.
We mention that
0\notin\sigma(H).

Define

\varphi_{i}(x)=\frac{\phi_{+}(\lambda_{i},x)}{\sqrt{\lambda_{i}\gamma_{\lambda_{i}}^{2}}}

(3.1)

as the normalized eigenfunction associated with the eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}
.
We consider the following interior inverse problem: For given
a\in\mathbb{R}
, reconstruct
\omega
and
v
from

\{\lambda_{i},\varphi_{i}(a)\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
The set
\{\lambda_{i},\varphi_{i}(a)\}_{i=1}^{N}
is called the interior spectral data of
H=H(\omega,v)
. We mention that
\omega
and
v
are uniquely determined by the spectral data
\{\lambda_{i},\lambda_{i}\gamma_{\lambda_{i}}^{2}\}_{i=1}^{N}
(
[
19
, Theorem 4.3]
).

Define

G(z,a)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\lambda_{i}^{2}|\varphi_{i}(a)|^{2}}{\lambda_{i}-z}.

(3.2)

Obviously,
G(z,a)
is a Herglotz-Nevanlinna function. Since
0\notin\sigma(H)
,
G(z,a)
has the following Taylor series at
z=0
,

G(z,a)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}|\varphi_{i}(a)|^{2}+z\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\varphi_{i}(a)|^{2}+z^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{|\varphi_{i}(a)|^{2}}{\lambda_{i}}+O(z^{3}).

(3.3)

Let us introduce some basic lemmas. Denote by

\displaystyle\alpha:=1-\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\varphi_{i}(a)|^{2},\ \beta:=-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}|\varphi_{i}(a)|^{2}.

Lemma 3.1

Let

Now in a position to state the trace formula.

.

\displaystyle\dot{\phi}_{\pm}(0,x)

Lemma 6.7
.

For any
z\in\mathbb{R}
,
if
\phi_{+}(z,x_{j})\phi_{+}(z,x_{j+1})>0
, then
\phi_{+}(z,x)
has no zero in the interval
[x_{j},x_{j+1}]
. If
\phi_{+}(z,x_{j})\phi_{+}(z,x_{j+1})\leq 0
, then
\phi_{+}(z,x)
has exactly one zero in the interval
[x_{j},x_{j+i}]
.

Proof.

We first show that if
\phi_{+}(z,x_{j})\phi_{+}(z,x_{j+1})>0
, then
\phi_{+}(z,x)
has no zero in the interval
[x_{j},x_{j+1}]
. Otherwise, let
y_{1}\in[x_{j},x_{j+i}]
be the zero of
\phi_{+}(z,x)
.
Note that
for
x\in[x_{j},x_{j+1}]
, there exists
A,B\in\mathbb{R}
, so that

\phi_{+}(z,x)=Ae^{\frac{x}{2}}+Be^{-\frac{x}{2}}.

(6.70)

Then we have
Ae^{\frac{y_{1}}{2}}+Be^{-\frac{y_{1}}{2}}=0
. That is,
B=-Ae^{y_{1}}
.
Substituting this into (
6.70
) and letting
x=x_{j},x_{j+1}
, we have that

\displaystyle\phi_{+}(z,x_{j})=Ae^{\frac{x_{j}}{2}}-Ae^{y_{1}}e^{-\frac{x_{j}}{2}},\ \phi_{+}(z,x_{j+1})=Ae^{\frac{x_{j+1}}{2}}-Ae^{y_{1}}e^{-\frac{x_{j+1}}{2}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\phi_{+}(z,x_{j})\phi_{+}(z,x_{j+1})=A^{2}(e^{\frac{x_{j}}{2}}-e^{y_{1}-\frac{x_{j}}{2}})(e^{\frac{x_{j+1}}{2}}-e^{y_{1}-\frac{x_{j+1}}{2}})\leq 0.

This is in contradiction with the assumption that
\phi_{+}(z,x_{j})\phi_{+}(z,x_{j+1})>0
.

If
\phi_{+}(z,x_{j})\phi_{+}(z,x_{j+1})\leq 0
, then
\phi_{+}(z,x)
has at least one zero in the interval
[x_{j},x_{j+1}]
. We show
\phi_{+}(z,x)
has exactly one zero in the interval
[x_{j},x_{j+1}]
.
Otherwise, let
y_{1},y_{2}\in[x_{j},x_{j+1}]
be zeros of
\phi_{+}(z,x)
.
Hence by (
6.70
), one has

\displaystyle Ae^{\frac{y_{1}}{2}}+Be^{-\frac{y_{1}}{2}}=0,\ Ae^{\frac{y_{2}}{2}}+Be^{-\frac{y_{2}}{2}}=0.

Then one obtains that
A=B=0
. By (
2.5
) and (
2.6
), we have
\phi_{+}(z,x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. This contradicts (
2.8
).
∎

Theorem 6.8
.

H

From Lemma
6.2
and Lemma
6.5
, we obtain that

n+n_{v}
real and simple eigenvalues.

If
n_{v}+n_{+}=(n-1)_{v}+(n-1)_{+}

) and Lemma
