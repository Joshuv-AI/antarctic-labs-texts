Abstract
Consider the following partial “sorting algorithm” on permutations: take the first entry of the permutation in one-line notation and insert it into the position of its own value. Continue until the first entry is 1 1 . This process imposes a forest structure on the set of all permutations of size n n , where the roots are the permutations starting with 1 1 and the leaves are derangements. Viewing the process in the opposite direction towards the leaves, one picks a fixed point and moves it to the beginning. Despite its simplicity, this “fixed point forest” exhibits a rich structure. In this paper, we consider the fixed point forest in the limit n → ∞ n\to\infty and show using Stein’s method that at a random permutation the local structure weakly converges to a tree defined in terms of independent Poisson point processes. We also show that the distribution of the length of the longest path from a random permutation to a leaf converges to the geometric distribution with mean e − 1 e-1 , and the length of the shortest path converges to the Poisson distribution with mean 1 1 . In addition, the higher moments are bounded and hence the expectations converge as well.

Introduction

1.1
Fixed point forest

Consider a deck of
n
cards labeled
1,2,\ldots,n
given in an arbitrary order. Take the top card and reinsert it
into the pile at the position of its value. This gives rise to a partial sorting algorithm, where the algorithm stops
when card 1 is on the top. This can be formulated in terms of the set
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
of permutations of size

n
. Each permutation
\pi
represents an ordered deck of cards and can be written in one-line notation as

\pi(1)\pi(2)\ldots\pi(n)
, where
\pi(i)
is the value of the
i
-th card in the deck with
\pi(1)
being the topmost
card. Then
\pi(1)
is removed and reinserted into the position
\pi(1)
. For example, the permutation
3142\in\mathfrak{S}_{4}
goes to
1432
. This defines a graph with vertices being all permutations in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}

and vertex
\pi
being connected by an edge to vertex
\pi^{\prime}
, if
\pi^{\prime}
is obtained from
\pi
by moving the first entry

\pi(1)
to position
\pi(1)
. It was shown in
[
13
]
that the graph is in fact a rooted forest, which we call
F_{n}
.
A
rooted forest
is a union of
rooted trees
, where a tree is a graph that does not contain
any closed loops involving distinct vertices. Since later on we will consider trees with random permutations as roots,
we call each root of
F_{n}
a
base
. The bases of the forest are the permutations with
\pi(1)=1
,
and the leaves are the derangements, that is, permutations without fixed points. The partial sorting algorithm describes
a path from a permutation to a base.
Viewing the process in the opposite direction towards the leaves (i.e. vertices without incoming edge), one picks a fixed
point
\pi(i)=i
of the permutation and moves it to the beginning, which we call
bumping
the fixed point
i
. For this reason, we call this forest the
fixed point forest
.

n=3
and
n=4
are given in Figures

and

The goal of this paper is to understand the local structure of this forest as
n\to\infty
. Put differently,
we would like to describe the neighborhood of a “typical” permutation in the forest for large
n
.
We carry this out in Theorem
13
, where we find the limit of the forest as
n\to\infty

in the sense of
local weak convergence
,
as defined in Section
2.1
(see
[
2
]
for more background). The limit is a random tree
that we construct from an infinite collection of independent Poisson point processes. The
proof relies on Stein’s method for Poisson approximation.
The limit tree seems to have interesting properties; see
Section
1.4
for comparisons to other processes. As a corollary of this local
weak convergence,
we show that the distributions of the distance from a random permutation to the farthest and nearest
leaves descending from it converge, respectively, to a geometric distribution with
mean
e-1
and a Poisson distribution with mean
1
.
With some additional work, we prove that the higher moments
are bounded and hence the expectations converge as well (see Theorem
5.27
).

We were first made aware of the fixed point forest by Gwen McKinley who studied it in her undergraduate
thesis
[
13
]
, which she wrote under the guidance of the second author. McKinley was introduced to the
problem at the Missouri State University summer REU program by Les Reid to whom the process was suggested
by Gerhardt Hinkle. In her thesis, McKinley investigated several global properties of the forest, finding
for instance that the longest path to the base is of length
2^{n-1}-1
.
Despite the simplicity of the description of the fixed point forest, many basic questions about it
seem difficult and remain unanswered. We discuss some of them in more detail in Section
6
.
Both locally and globally, the structure of the fixed point forest seems quite rich.

In the remainder of the introduction, we provide a non-technical discussion of the local structure of the forest
F_{n}

in the limit
n\to\infty
. Let
\pi_{n}
be a uniformly random permutation in
F_{n}
.
The essential information for determining the neighborhood of
\pi_{n}
is the location
of its fixed and near-fixed points. Our idea for understanding the limiting structure of
F_{n}

is to construct a sort of limit of the fixed and near-fixed points of
\pi_{n}
. By rescaling by
a factor of
1/n
, these are represented as Poisson point processes on
[0,1]
.
Then, we define a tree from these point processes, which will turn out to be the local limit of
F_{n}

(see Section
3
).

1.3
Moving towards the base in the tree

1.4
Comparison to other known processes

It is a natural question to compare the fixed point forest or its subtrees to other known random trees, such
as the Galton–Watson tree
[
18
]
. The tree component of the fixed point forest containing the
identity permutation has approximately
(n-1)!
vertices. As we discussed earlier, the height of this
component is
2^{n-1}-1
as shown in
[
13
]
. The Galton–Watson tree on the other hand has
height
\sqrt{N}
if the tree has
N
vertices
[
1
]
, which is much larger than
2^{n-1}
when

N=(n-1)!
. This is consistent with the fact that the fixed point tree has offspring sizes that are correlated across
generations, whereas the Galton–Watson tree has independent siblings.
The fixed point forest locally also is quite different from a Galton–Watson tree. For example,
no leaf in the fixed point forest has a sibling that is also a leaf.

The process of picking a fixed point at random and moving it to the front (which corresponds to a walk to
a leaf) has some resemblance to the Tsetlin library
[
17
,
8
,
9
]
, which is a
model for the evolution of an arrangement of books in a library shelf over time. It is a Markov chain on
permutations, where the entry in the
i
-th position is moved to the front with probability
p_{i}
. However,
in the fixed point forest this process eventually stops when a derangement is reached, whereas in the Tsetlin
library the process can go on arbitrarily long.

2
The limiting objects

In this section, we provide the precise definition of the limiting tree.

2.1
Local weak convergence in general

The main result of this paper is the
local weak convergence
of the fixed point forest to
a certain limiting tree. This mode of convergence is sometimes called
Benjamini–Schramm convergence

after the paper
[
4
]
. We will give a short introduction to local weak convergence now,
but see
[
2
, Section 2]
for a more in depth discussion.

Let
G,G_{1},G_{2},\ldots
be a sequence of random rooted graphs.
For any rooted graph
H
,
H(r)
denotes the
r
-ball around the root of
H
; that is,

H(r)
is the subgraph of
H
induced by all vertices at distance
r
or less from
the root. We write
H\cong H^{\prime}
to signify that

H
and
H^{\prime}
are isomorphic as rooted graphs.
We say that
G
is the
local weak limit
of
G_{n}
if for every
r\geq 0
and every finite
graph
H
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[G_{n}(r)\cong H]\to\mathbf{P}[G(r)\cong H]

as
n\to\infty
. Roughly speaking, this says that the view from the root of
G_{n}
resembles the view from
the root of
G
in distribution more and more as
n\to\infty
.
Frequently,
G_{n}
is a finite, deterministic graph with its root chosen uniformly at random,
as will be the case in this paper.

2.2
Construction of the limit tree

The ingredients of our construction are a collection of Poisson point processes

(\xi_{k}^{\rho})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
of unit intensity on
[0,1]
and a sequence
U_{1},U_{2},\ldots
of
independent
\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
random variables.
Formally, a
point process
is an integer-valued random measure on the Borel sets of
\mathbb{R}
.
One should think of it as a random collection of points, represented as the atoms
of the measure.
A
Poisson point process

\xi
of unit intensity on
[0,1]
is characterized by two
properties: First, for any interval of length
x
, the number of points of
\xi
in
the interval is distributed as
\mathrm{Poi}(x)
. Second, the numbers of points of
\xi
in
disjoint intervals are independent.
We use the terminology
point process configuration
to mean a deterministic
collection of points, also represented formally as a measure.

The point process
\xi_{k}^{\rho}
represents the

k
-separated letters in the abstracted permutation
\rho
.
Let
\rho_{1}
be the parent of
\rho_{0}=\rho
, let
\rho_{2}
be the parent of
\rho_{1}
, and so on.
The random variable
U_{i}
represents
the
0
-separated letter in
\rho_{i}
that was bumped to create
\rho_{i-1}
.

To construct the tree, we first define maps corresponding to moving forwards and backwards
from a given vertex.

Definition 5

(The forward map
f
)
.

Let
\xi=(\xi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a collection of point process configurations on
[0,1]
.
For any atom
x
of
\xi_{0}
, let
f(\xi,x)=(\xi^{\prime}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
, where

\displaystyle\xi_{k}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\xi_{k+1}\big\rvert_{[0,x)}+\xi_{k}\big\rvert_{(x,1]}.

This is the down-shift operation depicted in Figure
6
, corresponding
to moving forwards towards a leaf in the tree from a permutation to one of its children by bumping
the abstracted fixed point
x
.

Definition 6

(The backward map
b
)
.

Let
\xi=(\xi_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a collection of point process configurations on
[0,1]
.
For any
u\in[0,1]
, let
b(\xi,u)=(\xi^{\prime}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
, where

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}_{0}

\displaystyle=\xi_{-1}\bigr\rvert_{[0,u)}+\xi_{0}\bigr\rvert_{(u,1]}+\delta_{u},

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}_{k}

\displaystyle=\xi_{k-1}\bigr\rvert_{[0,u)}+\xi_{k}\bigr\rvert_{(u,1]}\qquad\qquad\text{for $k\neq 0$}.

This is the reverse of the forward map, in the following sense:
if
x
is an atom of
\xi_{0}
and
f(\xi,x)=\xi^{\prime}
, then
b(\xi^{\prime},x)=\xi
.

Next, we define a tree by applying
f
and
b
to map out the abstracted permutations.

Definition 7
.

Given point process configurations
\xi^{\rho}=(\xi_{k}^{\rho})_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}
and a sequence

u=(u_{1},u_{2},\ldots)
of elements of

3
Local weak convergence of the fixed point tree

Recall that
i
is a
k
-separated position in a permutation
\pi
if
\pi(i)=i+k
.
The main idea in this section is that the neighborhood of a permutation
\pi
in
F_{n}
up to distance
r

can typically be reassembled from two pieces of information: the
k
-separated positions
in
\pi
for
-r<k<r
, and the values of
\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(r)
. The first piece lets us
work out the tree forwards (towards leaves) from
\pi
. When we rescale by
1/n
, these locations
converge to independent Poisson point processes on
[0,1]
.
The second piece of information lets us move backwards in the tree. With the same rescaling, these random
variables converge to independent points sampled uniformly from
[0,1]
.
The two pieces of information converge jointly, as shown in Proposition
12
,
and the weak local convergence of the fixed point tree

F_{n}
follows easily from this in Theorem
13
.

While this convergence is what one would expect from well known Poisson approximations
of fixed points of random permutations (see
[
6
, Theorem 11]
,
for example), it will take some technical work to prove our precise statement. We will use Stein’s method
via size-bias couplings using the framework from
[
3
]
, which we introduce now.
See also
[
14
, Section 4.3]
for a more detailed introduction to size-bias couplings.
The general idea for our purposes is that we have a collection of 0-1 random
variables, and we would like to show that they are well approximated by independent Poisson
random variables. If there exist certain couplings described below,
Stein’s method gives a quantitative version of this approximation.
The bound is given in terms of the covariances of the random variables and does not
depend on the couplings, once they are shown to exist.

{condition}

Let
\mathbf{I}=(I_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}
be a collection of 0-1 random variables.
For each
\alpha\in\mathcal{I}
, there is a random vector

J_{\bullet\alpha}=(J_{\beta\alpha})_{\beta\in\mathcal{I}}
coupled with
\mathbf{I}
such that

•

J_{\bullet\alpha}
is distributed as
\mathbf{I}
conditioned on
I_{\alpha}=1
;

•

we can partition
\mathcal{I}
into disjoint sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}^{+}\cup\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}^{-}\cup\{\alpha\}

such that with probability one,

\displaystyle J_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle\leqslant I_{\beta}\quad\text{if $\beta\in\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}^{-},$}

(1)

\displaystyle J_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle\geqslant I_{\beta}\quad\text{if $\beta\in\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}^{+}$.}

(2)

When this condition holds,
one can estimate the distance between
\mathbf{I}
and a vector of independent
Poisson random variables in terms of the covariances of the components of
\mathbf{I}
, with no
mention of the coupling.
Here,
as usual, the covariance between two random variables
X
and
Y
is

\mathop{\mathbf{Cov}}\nolimits(X,Y)=\mathbf{E}[XY]-\mathbf{E}[X]\mathbf{E}[Y].

The bound is on the
total variation distance
between the laws of the random vectors.
For random variables
X
and
Y
taking values in some space
\mathcal{S}
, this distance is defined as

\displaystyle d_{TV}(X,Y)=\sup_{S\subseteq\mathcal{S}}\lvert\mathbf{P}[X\in S]-\mathbf{P}[Y\in S]\rvert.

Another characterization of the total variation distance between the laws of
X
and
Y

is as the minimum of
\mathbf{P}[X\neq Y]
over all couplings of
X
and
Y
; see
[
3
, Section A.1]
.

Proposition 9

(Corollary 10.J.1 in
[
3
]
)
.

Assume the coupling condition.
Let
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}
be a vector of independent
Poisson random variables with
\mathbf{E}Y_{\alpha}=\mathbf{E}I_{\alpha}
. Then

\displaystyle d_{TV}(\mathbf{I},\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{E}I_{\alpha})^{2}+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{\smash{\beta\in\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}^{-}}}\lvert\mathop{\mathbf{Cov}}\nolimits(I_{\alpha},I_{\beta})\rvert+\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}\sum_{\smash{\beta\in\mathcal{I}_{\alpha}^{+}}}\mathop{\mathbf{Cov}}\nolimits(I_{\alpha},I_{\beta}).

(3)

We will also need the following technical lemma.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}
and let
\mathbf{Z}=(Z_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}
be vectors of independent
Poisson random variables. Then

\displaystyle d_{TV}(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{I}}\bigl\lvert\mathbf{E}Y_{\alpha}-\mathbf{E}Z_{\alpha}\bigr\rvert.

Suppose that

and

. Then

Applying this coupling to

and

.

Proposition 11
.

Let
\pi
be a uniformly random permutation on

\displaystyle\pi(1)

\displaystyle=a_{1},\;\ldots,\;\pi(r)=a_{r}

(4)

Let
\mathbf{I}=\bigl(I(i,k)\bigr)_{(i,k)\in\mathcal{I}}

The proof proceeds in three steps: First, we construct a coupling satisfying

of (

and a random vector

Constructing the coupling.

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}\{\text{$i$ is $k$-separated in $\pi^{\prime}$}\},

(5)

Limiting distributions and higher moments

In this section we compute the limiting distributions of two statistics of the fixed point
tree

using the local weak limit we have constructed in Section

As usual,

is a uniformly random permutation of length

from

We use

with probability

.

(i)

.

(ii)

. As

and

for all

.

.

We will prove that

and

in Propositions
5.31

and

, respectively. In Proposition

,

.
It is a standard result that this proves
the convergence of all moments (see

, for instance).

An interesting open problem is to determine the limiting distribution of
the number of steps from
\pi_{n}
to a leaf when moving randomly towards a leaf.

Sections
5.1
and
5.2
are devoted to proving
the convergence of
M_{n}
and

to their limiting distributions.
Both
M_{n}
and

are functionals of

satisfying the criteria of

. This corollary then proves that

and

converge in distribution to the corresponding functionals of the limit tree.
Thus, our task in these sections is to work out the distributions of the distances in

from the root to the nearest and farthest leaves descending from it.

Section
5.3
gives our proof that

and

are bounded in

, establishing the convergence of moments.
We emphasize that this result is not just a technicality. The reentry phenomenon
mentioned on Page

can cause

to be extremely large.
For example, if
\pi_{n}
begins with the string

, then

, with the same
small letters bumped repeatedly. The probability of such reentry is vanishingly small, making
it irrelevant to the distributional convergence of

. But these unlikely events nonetheless
contribute to the moments of

, and a priori
it is plausible that even the expectation of
L_{n}
tends to infinity.
To prove that this is not the case, we are forced to develop
several bounds that should be useful in future work on the fixed point forest.

M=0
. Otherwise, let
X_{1}
be the rightmost point
of
\xi_{0}^{\rho}
, and let
v_{1}
be the child of
\rho
corresponding to bumping it.
Now, if
\xi_{0}^{v_{1}}
has no points, then the walk is over and
M=1
.
Otherwise, let
X_{2}
be its rightmost point of
\xi_{0}^{v_{1}}
, which is necessarily to the left of
X_{1}
,
since
\xi_{0}^{v_{1}}=\xi_{1}^{\rho}\rvert_{[0,X_{1})}+\xi_{0}^{\rho}\rvert_{(X_{1},0]}
.
Continue in this way, making a sequence of vertices
\rho,v_{1},\ldots,v_{M}
and
a sequence of points corresponding to them,
X_{1},\ldots,X_{M}
.

In this procedure,
X_{1}
is the rightmost point of
\xi_{0}^{\rho}
, then

X_{2}
is the rightmost point of
\xi_{1}^{\rho}\rvert_{[0,X_{1})}
, then

X_{3}
is the rightmost point of
\xi_{2}^{\rho}\rvert_{[0,X_{2})}
, and so on.
We can interpret this as follows: We start at
1
, moving backwards in
a Poisson process until we encounter a point, which takes
\mathrm{Exp}(1)
time to arrive.
Then, looking at a different Poisson process,
we move backwards until we encounter a point, which again will arrive in
\mathrm{Exp}(1)
time, independent
of the first arrival. We then continue on in this way, and
M
is the total number of points encountered
before we make it backwards to time
0
.
This is the same as counting the number of arrivals in a
single

Poisson process between time
0
and
1
, which is distributed as
\mathrm{Poi}(1)
.

5.2
Farthest distance to a leaf

The next lemma is the limiting analogue of Proposition
4.20
.

Lemma 5.33
.

The unique longest path from the root of
T
to a descendent leaf is obtained by bumping the leftmost
abstracted fixed point at each step.

Proof 5.34
.

Suppose we have some sequence of points bumped in a path from the root of
T
to a descendent
leaf in which some point
y
was not the leftmost when it was bumped.
We will show the existence of a strictly longer path to a leaf.
Let
x<y
be a point that could have been bumped instead of
y
.
Decompose the original sequence of bumped points as

\displaystyle PyL_{1}a_{1}L_{2}a_{2}\cdots a_{r-1}L_{r}.

(14)

Capital letters in (
14
) denote (possibly empty) strings of points and lowercase
letters denote single points.
The string
P
consists of all bumped points prior to
y
. Next,
L_{1}
is made up of points
smaller than
x
, and
a_{1}
is the first point
larger than
x
. Then
L_{2}
consists of points smaller than
x
, and
a_{2}
is the next
subsequent point larger than
x
, and so on. Note that almost surely, no point of

\xi_{0}^{\rho},\xi_{1}^{\rho},\ldots
occurs more than once, so we never need to worry
about repeated values in these sequences.

We claim that we can instead bump the following sequence of points:

\displaystyle PxL_{1}yL_{2}a_{1}L_{3}a_{2}\cdots a_{r-2}L_{r}a_{r-1}.

(15)

As this sequence is one longer than (
14
), this claim completes the proof.
Thus, we just need to show that this sequence is in fact

Definition 4
.

bumpable, by which we mean
that when each point is bumped in turn, the next one is an abstracted fixed point.
Before we do this, we make a key observation: Suppose that
z_{1}\cdots z_{k}

and
z_{1}^{\prime}\cdots z_{k}^{\prime}
are two bumpable sequences, each of which contains the same number of points
larger than
z
. Then
z_{1}\cdots z_{k}z
is bumpable if and only if
z^{\prime}_{1}\cdots z_{k}^{\prime}z
is bumpable.

Clearly,
Px
is bumpable. Since all points in
L_{1}
are smaller than
x
and
x<y
, as each point of

L_{1}
is encountered in (
14
) and (
15
) the same number of larger points
has already been encountered in each sequence.
By our observation, the bumpability of
PyL_{1}
implies the bumpability of

PxL_{1}
. Next, since
Py
is bumpable and all points in
xL_{1}
are smaller than
y
, the sequence

PxL_{1}y
is also bumpable.
Repeating this reasoning, bumpability of
PyL_{1}a_{1}L_{2}
implies bumpability of

PxL_{1}yL_{2}
, and bumpability of
PyL_{1}a_{1}
implies bumpability
of
PxL_{1}yL_{2}a_{1}
. Continuing in this way, we arrive at the bumpability of (
15
).

The following proof involves a continuous-time Markov chain known as a

Definition 5
.

Yule process.
At state
k
, it jumps to
k+1
at rate
k
, with no other transitions allowed.
It is well known that if
(Y_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a Yule process starting at
1
, then

Y_{t}-1\sim\mathrm{Geo}\bigl(e^{-t}\bigr)
; see
[
11
, Section 4.1.D]
, for example.

Proposition 5.35
.

As
n\to\infty
, we have
L_{n}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,\mathrm{Geo}\bigl(e^{-1}\bigr)
.

Proof 5.36
.

Let
L
be the length of the longest path in the limit tree
T
from the root
to a descendent leaf.
By Corollary
3.15
, we have
L_{n}\,{\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt\longrightarrow}\limits^{d}}}\,L
.
By Lemma
5.33
the random variable
L
is the length of the path down
T
given by
bumping the leftmost fixed point until none remain.

First, we sketch one way to compute the distribution of
L
.
Suppose we start at the root
\rho
of
T
. With probability
e^{-1}
,
there are no points in
\xi_{0}^{\rho}
, and
L=0
. If not, we bump the leftmost point
X

of
\xi_{0}^{\rho}
to move to a vertex
v
. The next set of abstracted fixed points is

\xi_{0}^{v}
, which is equal to
\xi_{1}^{\rho}
on
[0,X)
and to
\xi_{0}^{\rho}
on
(X,1]
.
This is a new Poisson point process independent of the past, and so again we stop with probability

e^{-1}
and
L=1
. Continuing on in this way,
L
is the number of failures before
the first success in independent trials that succeed with probability
e^{-1}
.

The above argument works and is the most direct, but we give a different proof now
whose ideas will be useful in Section
5.3
.
Let
\mathcal{B}\subseteq[0,1]
be the set of points bumped in the longest path in
T

from the root to a descendent leaf.
Lemma
4.23
can be restated in this setting: A point
x
of
\xi_{k}^{\rho}

is an element of
\mathcal{B}
if and only if
0\leqslant k\leqslant\lvert\mathcal{B}\cap(x,1]\rvert
.
Thus, we can progressively build our set
\mathcal{B}
by the following procedure.
Start with
\mathcal{B}
empty. Scan
\xi_{0}^{\rho}
from right to left starting at time
1

until a point is encountered, and add it to
\mathcal{B}
.
Now, scan
\xi_{0}^{\rho}
and
\xi_{1}^{\rho}
from right to left from this point until
a point is encountered, and add it to
\mathcal{B}
. Now scan
\xi_{0}^{\rho}
,
\xi_{1}^{\rho}
,
and
\xi_{2}^{\rho}
, and so on, stopping when we reach time
0
.

In this procedure, the first point arrives at rate
1
,
since it is the first arrival time of a unit intensity Poisson process. The next point
arrives at rate
2
, since it is the first arrival time of two independent unit intensity
Poisson processes, and the next at rate
3
, and so on.
Thus, the size of
\mathcal{B}
is the number of increases of a Yule
process in time
1
, which is distributed as

\mathrm{Geo}\bigl(e^{-1}\bigr)
.

It follows from this proposition that the root of
T
has finitely many descendants almost surely.
In forthcoming work, this will be investigated further, and it will be shown that
the expected number of descendants is infinite.

5.3
Higher moments

To bound the moments of
L_{n}
and
M_{n}
, we must leave behind the limit tree
T
and work
directly with the finite fixed point forest. Since
M_{n}\leqslant L_{n}
and we are only looking for upper bounds,
we will deal exclusively with
L_{n}
. Our first result gives an upper bound on
L_{n}
in terms of

\mathcal{B}=\mathcal{B}(\pi_{n})=\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{k}\}
, the set of letters bumped in the longest path from
\pi_{n}
to a descendent leaf.
Since
b_{1}<b_{2}<\cdots<b_{k}
is increasing,
b_{i}-i
is weakly increasing. Fix some
x>0
and denote by
\mathcal{B}_{x}

the subset of
\mathcal{B}
such that
b_{i}-i<x
for
b_{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{x}
.

Lemma 5.37
.

Fix
x>0
. Then

L_{n}\leqslant 2^{|\mathcal{B}_{x}|}|\mathcal{B}|\left(1+\frac{1}{x}\right)^{|\mathcal{B}|}.

(16)

Proof 5.38
.

For
b_{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{x}
, we have
1+\frac{1}{b_{i}-i}\leqslant 2
and for
b_{i}\in\mathcal{B}\backslash\mathcal{B}_{x}
, we have

1+\frac{1}{b_{i}-i}<1+\frac{1}{x}.
With this, the bound in (
16
) follows directly from
Lemma
4.25
.

We will bound
\mathbf{E}L_{n}^{p}
using (
16
) by showing that
\lvert\mathcal{B}_{x}\rvert
and
\lvert\mathcal{B}\rvert

are unlikely to be large. Our first result in this direction, interesting in its own right,
is an exponential tail bound on
\lvert\mathcal{B}\rvert
.
Proposition
5.35
exactly computes the distribution of the
corresponding quantity in the limit case as
\mathrm{Geo}\bigl(e^{-1}\bigr)
.
Our proof here follows the same intuition, but it will be considerably more difficult.

Proposition 5.39
.

For some constant
C<1
, it holds for all
k,n\geqslant 0
that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\bigl[\lvert\mathcal{B}\rvert\geqslant k\bigr]\leqslant C^{k}.

We will need several preliminary lemmas first. Recall from Lemma
4.23
that
\mathcal{B}
can
be constructed by moving leftward through the random permutation
\pi_{n}
, successively revealing

\pi_{n}(n),\pi_{n}(n-1),\ldots,\pi_{n}(1)
and tracking the set
\mathcal{B}
as we go. Reversing the indexing
so that we can count up instead of down, define

\displaystyle X_{i}=\#\{j>n-i\mid\pi_{n}(j)\in\mathcal{B}\}.

This yields a

Definition 6
.

pure birth process (that is, one that either stays the same or increases by one at each step)
starting at
X_{0}=0
and ending at
X_{n}=\lvert\mathcal{B}\rvert
.
Our goal is to prove an exponential tail bound for
X_{n}
that does not
depend on
n
.
In Theorem
5.35
, we show that the analogous process for the limit tree is a Yule
process. The process
(X_{i})_{0\leqslant i\leqslant n}
is not as straightforward,
but the following lemma shows that it approximates a Yule
process in the sense that it increases with probability approximately
proportional to its current value.

Lemma 5.40
.

For
k<n/2
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[X_{i+1}=X_{i}+1\mid X_{i}=k]

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{1+k}{n-2k}.

(17)

Proof 5.41
.

For a given permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, let
x_{i}(\sigma)=\#\{j>n-i\mid\sigma(j)\in\mathcal{B}\}
,
so that
X_{i}=x_{i}(\pi_{n})
. Taking
i
and
k
to be fixed, define

\displaystyle\mathfrak{T}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\mid x_{i}(\sigma)=k,\,\sigma(n-i)=n-i+\ell\},

\displaystyle\mathfrak{U}

\displaystyle=\{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\mid x_{i}(\sigma)=k\}.

By Lemma
4.23
, the process
x_{i}(\sigma)
increases in its next step
if and only if
0\leqslant\sigma(n-i)-(n-i)\leqslant x_{i}(\sigma)
and
\sigma(n-i)\neq 1
, showing that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[X_{i+1}=X_{i}+1\mid X_{i}=k]\leqslant\frac{\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\lvert\mathfrak{T}_{\ell}\rvert}{\lvert\mathfrak{U}\rvert}.

(18)

Now, we compare the sizes of
\mathfrak{T}_{\ell}
and
\mathfrak{U}
by a combinatorial switching argument.
Suppose that
\sigma\in\mathfrak{T}_{\ell}
. For any
j\in[n]
, let

\sigma^{j}=(\sigma(n-i),\sigma(j))\circ\sigma
, the permutation given by swapping the values
at positions
n-i
and
j
in
\sigma
.
Given
\sigma^{j}
,
i
,
\ell
, and
n
but without
\sigma
or
j
, we can recover

\sigma
by the formula
\sigma=(n-i+\ell,\sigma^{j}(n-i))\circ\sigma^{j}
and
j
by

j=(\sigma^{j})^{-1}(n-i+\ell)
. This shows that the map from
\mathfrak{T}_{\ell}\times[n]\to\mathfrak{S}_{n}

given by
(\sigma,j)\mapsto\sigma^{j}
is injective.

We claim that for any
\sigma\in\mathfrak{T}_{\ell}
, the
permutation
\sigma^{j}
falls in
\mathfrak{U}
if either of the following holds:

(i)

j\leqslant n-i
;

(ii)

j>n-i+\ell
and
\sigma(j)\notin\mathcal{B}(\sigma)
.

Indeed, in the first case,
\sigma
and
\sigma^{j}
differ only at locations
n-i
and smaller.
Since
x_{i}(\sigma)
is determined by
\sigma(n-i+1),\ldots,\sigma(n)
,
we have
x_{i}(\sigma^{j})=x_{i}(\sigma)=k
, and hence
\sigma^{j}\in\mathfrak{U}
.
In the second case, the only way for
x_{i}(\sigma^{j})
to be different from
x_{i}(\sigma)
is if

\sigma^{j}(j)\in\mathcal{B}
. But this cannot occur, since

\sigma^{j}(j)=\sigma(n-i)\leq n-i+\ell
and
j>n-i+\ell
.

For
\sigma\in\mathfrak{T}_{\ell}
, there are
n-i
choices of
j
satisfying
(i)
.
There are another
i-\ell
choices
of
j
satisfying
j>n-i+\ell
; at most
x_{i-\ell}(\sigma)\leqslant x_{i}(\sigma)=k

of these have
\sigma(j)\in\mathcal{B}(\sigma)
,
giving us at least
i-\ell-k
choices of
j
satisfying
(ii)
. Thus, for each
\sigma\in\mathfrak{T}_{\ell}
,
there are at least
n-\ell-k
choices of
j
for which
\sigma^{j}\in\mathfrak{U}
. By injectivity
of the map
(\sigma,j)\mapsto\sigma^{j}
,

\displaystyle\lvert\mathfrak{U}\rvert\geq(n-\ell-k)\lvert\mathfrak{T}_{\ell}\rvert\geq(n-2k)\lvert\mathfrak{T}_{\ell}\rvert

for
\ell\leq k
. Substituting this into (
18
) gives (
17
).

Now, we couple
X_{i}
with a true Yule process,
whose marginal distributions we know to be exactly geometrically distributed.
The only complication is that we lose control of
(X_{i})
if it becomes too large,
which we deal with by considering it only up to a stopping time.

Lemma 5.42
.

Let
Y_{t}
be a Yule process starting from
1
.
Let
S=\min\{i\mid X_{i}\geqslant n/4\}
, taking
S=n
if the minimum is over the empty set.
The processes
(X_{i})_{0\leqslant i\leqslant n}
and
(Y_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 4}

can be coupled so that

\displaystyle 1+X_{i}\leqslant Y_{4i/n}

for all
i\leqslant S
.

Proof 5.43
.

Essentially, we just need to confirm that
X_{i}
is less likely to increase from time
i
to
i+1

than
Y
is from time
4i/n
to
4(i+1)/n
.
Formally, we start with
(X_{i},\,i\in[n])
and then build up the Yule process inductively on the same
probability space.
Since a Yule process is Markov, we can construct
(Y_{t})
up to some time
t_{0}
and then
extend it by attaching to its end a new Yule process starting at
Y_{t_{0}}
.

Assume for some
j\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
that we have already constructed
a Yule process
(Y_{t})_{0\leqslant t\leqslant 4j/n}
so that

1+X_{i}\leqslant Y_{4i/n}
for
i\leqslant\min(j,S)
. Note that this is trivial
in the starting case
j=0
. We want to show that if
S>j
, then we can extend
(Y_{t})
up to time

4(j+1)/n
so that
1+X_{j+1}\leqslant Y_{4(j+1)/n}
. (If
S\leqslant j
, then we can just extend
(Y_{t})
up to
time
4(j+1)/n
independently from
X_{j+1}
, since the relationship between the two is irrelevant.)

So, we assume
S>j
, which implies that
X_{j}<n/4
.
According to Lemma
5.40
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[X_{j+1}=X_{j}+1\mid X_{j}]\leqslant\frac{1+X_{j}}{n-2X_{j}}\leqslant\frac{2(1+X_{j})}{n}

under this assumption.
The next increase of a Yule process at
Y_{4j/n}
arrives at rate
Y_{4j/n}
.
Thus, conditional on
Y_{4j/n}
, the probability that a Yule process increases from
Y_{4j/n}

to
Y_{4j/n}+1
within time
4/n
is

\displaystyle 1-\exp\biggl(-\frac{4Y_{4j/n}}{n}\biggr)\geqslant 1-\exp\biggl(-\frac{4(1+X_{j})}{n}\biggr)\geqslant\frac{2(1+X_{j})}{n}.

The first inequality uses the inductive hypothesis that
1+X_{j}\leqslant Y_{4j/n}
, and the second
uses the inequality
1-e^{-x}\geqslant x/2
, which holds for
0\leqslant x\leqslant 1
.
Thus, conditional on
X_{j}
and
Y_{4j/n}
,
a Yule process starting at
Y_{4j/n}
is more likely to increase in time
4/n

than is the process
(X_{i})
from time
j
to
j+1
. Thus, conditionally on
X_{j}

and
Y_{4j/n}
, we can couple a Yule process
starting at
Y_{4j/n}
of duration
4/n
with
(X_{i})
so that it increases only if
(X_{i})

does from
j
to
j+1
. Tacking this Yule process onto the end of
(Y_{t})
, we have extended
our coupling as desired.

Proof 5.44

(Proof of Proposition
5.39
)
.

Recall that
X_{n}=\lvert\mathcal{B}\rvert
.
Couple
(X_{i})
and
(Y_{t})
according to Lemma
5.42
.
Fix
k\leqslant n/4
, and let
S^{\prime}=\min\{i\mid X_{i}\geqslant k\}
, taking
S^{\prime}=n
if the minimum is over
the empty set. Since
S^{\prime}\leqslant S
, we have
1+X_{S^{\prime}}\leqslant Y_{4S^{\prime}/n}
.

We claim that if
X_{n}\geqslant k
, then
Y_{4}\geqslant 1+k
. To see this, observe that if

X_{n}\geqslant k
, then
X_{S^{\prime}}\geqslant k
. Thus

\displaystyle Y_{4}\geqslant Y_{4S^{\prime}/n}\geqslant 1+X_{S^{\prime}}\geqslant 1+k.

This demonstrates that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[X_{n}\geqslant k]\leqslant\mathbf{P}[Y_{4}-1\geqslant k]=(1-e^{-4})^{k}\leqslant\exp\bigl(-e^{-4}k\bigr)

(19)

since
Y_{t}-1
is distributed as
\mathrm{Geo}\bigl(e^{-t}\bigr)
(see
[
11
, Section 4.1.D]
, for example).

For
n/4<k\leqslant n
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}[X_{n}\geqslant k]\leqslant\mathbf{P}[X_{n}\geqslant n/4]\leqslant\exp\biggl(-\frac{e^{-4}n}{4}\biggr)\leqslant\exp\biggl(-\frac{e^{-4}k}{4}\biggr).

(20)

The right hand side of (
20
) is larger than that of (
19
).
As
X_{n}
does not take values larger than
n
, this shows that
(
20
) holds for all
k\geqslant 0
.

Next, we prove a subexponential tail bound on the size of
\mathcal{B}_{x}
for any fixed
x
.

Lemma 5.45
.

For
x>0
and
t\leqslant n-x
,

\mathbf{P}[\lvert\mathcal{B}_{x}\rvert\geqslant t]\leqslant{t+x\choose t}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\left(\frac{x}{n-i}\right)\leqslant\biggl(\frac{ex}{t}\biggr)^{t}.

(21)

Proof 5.46
.

In order for each of the first
t
values in
\mathcal{B}
to satisfy
b_{i}-i<x
, we must have that all of the values
are less than
t+x
. For a specific choice of letters
1<c_{1}<\cdots<c_{t}
, let
E(c_{1},\ldots,c_{t})
denote the
event that the first
t
values of
\mathcal{B}
are
c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}.
If
|\mathcal{B}_{x}|\geqslant t
then
E(c_{1},\ldots,c_{2})

holds for one of at most
{t+x\choose t}
possible choices of
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}).

For a particular choice of letters
c_{1}<\cdots<c_{t}<t+x
,

\mathbf{P}[E(c_{1},\ldots,c_{t})]\leq\mathbf{P}\left[\bigcap_{i=1}^{t}\{\pi_{n}^{-1}(c_{i})<x+i\}\right]=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\frac{x}{n-i+1}.

To prove the final bound in (
21
), we apply the union bound and evaluate

\displaystyle\binom{t+x}{t}\prod_{i=1}^{t}\left(\frac{x}{n-i+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{x^{t}(x+t)\cdots(x+1)}{t!\;n\cdots(n-t+1)}\leqslant\frac{x^{t}}{t!},

since
x+t\leqslant n
under the assumptions of the lemma, and then we apply the bound

t!\geqslant(t/e)^{t}
.

Proposition
5.39
and Lemma
5.45
now combine to bound
\mathbf{E}L_{n}^{p}
.

Proposition 5.47
.

For any
p>0
, it holds that
\sup_{n}\mathbf{E}L_{n}^{p}<\infty
.

Proof 5.48
.

Fix
p>0
, and choose
x
large enough that
(1+\frac{1}{x})^{2p}<C^{-1}

for the constant
C
from Proposition
5.39
.
By (
16
) and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}L_{n}^{p}\leqslant\mathbf{E}\biggl[2^{2p\lvert\mathcal{B}_{x}\rvert}\biggr]^{1/2}\mathbf{E}\Biggl[\lvert\mathcal{B}\rvert^{2p}\biggl(1+\frac{1}{x}\biggr)^{2p\lvert\mathcal{B}\rvert}\Biggr]^{1/2}.

(22)

By Proposition
5.39
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\Biggl[\lvert\mathcal{B}\rvert^{2p}\biggl(1+\frac{1}{x}\biggr)^{2p\lvert\mathcal{B}\rvert}\Biggr]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}C^{t}t^{2p}\biggl(1+\frac{1}{x}\biggr)^{2pt},

(23)

which is summable.
Similarly, applying Lemma
5.45
and using
\lvert\mathcal{B}_{x}\rvert\leqslant n
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\Bigl[2^{2p\lvert\mathcal{B}_{x}\rvert}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{t=0}^{n-x}\mathbf{P}\bigl[\lvert\mathcal{B}_{x}\rvert=t\bigr]2^{2pt}+\mathbf{P}\bigl[\lvert\mathcal{B}_{x}\rvert\geqslant n-x\bigr]2^{2pn}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\biggl(\frac{ex}{t}\biggr)^{t}2^{2pt}+\biggl(\frac{ex}{n-x}\biggr)^{n-x}2^{2pn}.

The first term is finite, and the last term vanishes as
n\to\infty
.
Together with (
23
), this yields an upper bound on (
22
)
with no dependence on
n
.

Further Questions

Many of the open questions in
[
13
]
about the global properties of the fixed point forest
F_{n}

remain unanswered. Recall that each base of
F_{n}
is a permutation with
1
as a fixed
point. Let
T_{n}
denote the tree in
F_{n}
with the identity permutation as its base.
In fact,
T_{n}
is the largest tree in
F_{n}
, which can be seen as follows. Let
\pi
be any base with

\pi(1)=1.
Let
i
be the largest index of such that
\pi(i)\neq i.
Then
\pi(i)
is never bumped, so
switching
\pi(i)
and
\pi(1)
creates a new permutation
\pi^{\prime}
with subtree at least as big as the tree with base
\pi
.
Note that
\pi^{\prime}\in T_{n}
, since all letters after
1
are fixed points. Hence an isomorphic copy of the tree starting from

\pi
is also in
T_{n}
.

In
[
13
, Propositions 24 and 25]
, it is shown that for a uniformly random permutation
\pi_{n}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\leqslant\mathbf{P}[\pi_{n}\in T_{n}]\leqslant\frac{e}{n},

and it is conjectured that
\mathbf{P}[\pi_{n}\in T_{n}]\sim\frac{1}{n}
. We highlight this question here and tack
on a few more of our own:

Question 6.49
.

Prove that
n\mathbf{P}[\pi_{n}\in T_{n}]
converges as
n\to\infty
and determine its limit. Characterize all permutations
in
T_{n}
. How do the next largest components compare in size to it?

Another question posed in
[
13
]
is:

Question 6.50
.

Let
R_{n}
be the distance from
\pi_{n}
to the base of its tree in the fixed point forest.
What are the limiting asymptotics of
\mathbf{E}R_{n}
?

One could also ask about the limiting fluctuations of
R_{n}
from its mean.

Though some of our work in Section
4
could be helpful in addressing
these questions, our limit tree has nothing to say about them.
For example, the limiting tree has no base at all, reflecting that questions about the distance from

\pi_{n}
to a base are not local. One could instead look for
a different limit of the fixed point forest where edges
are scaled so that the diameter of each component of the forest stays bounded, along the lines of
the continuum random tree (see
[
1
]
).

The present work has created other avenues to explore. Continuing in the same
theme as Section
5
, what can we say about
paths from root to leaf in the limiting tree besides the longest and shortest ones? In particular,

Question 6.51
.

Walk from the root towards the leaves in the limiting tree by choosing randomly among all children
at each step. What is the distribution of the number of steps before reaching a leaf?

Other properties of the tree are interesting as well:

Question 6.52
.

Is simple random walk on the limiting tree transient or recurrent?
What is the branching number (see
[
12
, Section 1.2]
) of the tree?

A random walk on the nonlimit fixed point forest can be interpreted as a stochastic version of
the bumping process where we randomly bump and unbump letters of a permutation. A solution to the above
problem would likely give information on how quickly this process moves from the starting permutation.

Another problem is to determine what happens when the root of the fixed point forest is chosen
nonuniformly:

Question 6.53
.

Determine the local limit of the fixed point forest when the root is sampled from the
Ewens or Mallows distributions.

The fixed points of Mallows distributions are not distributed evenly over the permutation,
so we would expect convergence to a limiting tree defined by Poisson point processes
of nonuniform intensity.
