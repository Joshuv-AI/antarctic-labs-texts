A Montgomery-Hooley theorem for the k k -fold divisor function
Tomos Parry

Abstract
Let d k ​ ( n ) d_{k}(n) denote the k k -fold divisor function. For a wide range of large q q the expected bound ∑ n ≤ x n ≡ a ⁡ ( q ) d k ​ ( n ) − main term ≈ x q \sum_{n\leq x\atop{n\equiv a(q)}}d_{k}(n)-\text{ main term }\approx\sqrt{\frac{x}{q}} is shown to be true in an average sense - for all k k . 1
-   Introduction

For
k\geq 1
define the
k
-fold divisor function

d_{k}(n)=\sum_{u_{1}\cdot\cdot\cdot u_{k}=n\atop u_{1},...,u_{k}\in\mathbb{N}}1.

The question of the distribution of values of
d_{k}(n)
has a rich history, the values being linked to the Riemann zeta function through

\zeta(s)^{k}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{d_{k}(n)}{n^{s}}.

So too does the question of their distribution in arithmetic progressions, being in turn linked to Dirichlet
L
-functions and to, in particular, moments of Dirichlet
L
-functions, as indeed is said, for example, on page 3 of
[
17
]
. Consequently larger
k
often poses the more problems. The driving question seems to have been
the divisor problem for arithmetic progressions
, which asks when do we have a formula

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x\atop{n\equiv a(q)}}d_{k}(n)\approx\frac{xf_{x}(q,a)}{q}

(1)

or in other words when are the values of
d_{k}(n)
uniformly distributed across the residue classes; here
f_{x}(q,a)
is the residue at
s=1
of something that looks like
\zeta(s)^{k}x^{s-1}/s
and which we can think of therefore as having size no bigger than
(\log x)^{k-1}
. If (
1
) holds for all
q
up to essentially
x^{\theta}
then
d_{k}(n)
is said to have
exponent of distribution
\theta
. A critical value is
\theta=1/2
which has direct consequences for the prime numbers - see Theorem 4 of
[
5
]
or, of course, consider the breakthrough ideas of Zhang on bounded gaps. For
k=2
it is by now a classical result of Selberg and of Hooley that
\theta=2/3
is valid but further improvements have proven themselves elusive. For
k=3
important power-improvements on
\theta=1/2
were made in the 80s by Friedlander and Iwaniec and for
k=4
we have no better than
\theta=1/2
from the 60s due to Linnik - see pages 32-33 of
[
13
]
for references to these results. For larger
k
it is not known whether
\theta\geq 1/2
.

So as is usual in these circles of questions one is inclined to ask instead what happens if we average - either by considering the variance

V_{x}(q):=\sum_{a=1}^{q}\left|\sum_{n\leq x\atop{n\equiv a(q)}}d_{k}(n)-\frac{xf_{x}(q,a)}{q}\right|^{2}

or the averaged variance

V(x,Q):=\sum_{q\leq Q}V_{x}(q)

the point being that asymptotics for these of sizes around
x
and
xQ
respectively would confirm an error around
\sqrt{x/q}
on average. Starting with a result of Motohashi in the 70s there was a line of work (of which we mention
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
,
[
14
]
) on these variances culminating in 2012 with a very satisfactory result of Lau and Zhao
[
9
]
which provided an asymptotic for
V_{x}(q)
in a range essentially
\sqrt{x}\leq q\leq x
. For
k>2
however, an asymptotic formula for either variance isn’t known, and in
[
17
]
Rodgers and Soundararajan put forward conjectures as to the true sizes of these variances for general
k
, these based on comparison with the function field case, investigated in
[
8
]
. They then go on to provide evidence for these conjectures by approaching the problem with smooth weights. Allowing smooth weights usually simplifies things (consider the usual Dirichlet divisor problem, where improvements on an error
\ll x^{1/3}
are considered deep whilst with smoothing an error
\ll 1
is straightfoward) and indeed they achieve for general
k
an asymptotic formula for smooth
V(x,Q)
in a range essentially
Q\geq x^{k/(k+2)}
. More recently still, Nguyen
[
12
]
has found an asymptotic formula even for smooth
V_{x}(q)
in a range essentially
x^{1/(k-1)}\leq q\leq x^{1/k}
. A step in a different direction is to change the approximating main term to a more workable but less natural or probabilistic one - for an asymptotic formula in this case see
[
3
]
.

But as the authors in
[
17
]
allude to themselves, it is another challenge to prove such results with sharp cut-offs, their concern in the paper being confirming the nature of the main term in the integer case at all. But very recently progress has been done even in the sharp cut-off case, with Nguyen
[
13
]
finding an asymptotic formula for
V(x,x)
in the case
k=3
. In this article, we take things further by providing one for
V(x,Q)
for all
k
.

Let’s state our theorem. In Lemma
1
(C) we will prove that there is a quantity
f_{x}(q,a)
such that for fixed
q,a\in\mathbb{N}

\sum_{n\leq x\atop{n\equiv a(q)}}d_{k}(n)\sim\frac{xf_{x}(q,a)}{q}\hskip 28.45274ptx\rightarrow\infty.

(2)

(For a precise definition see that lemma, but essentially
f_{x}(q,a)
is a degree
\leq k-1
polynomial in
\log x
with coefficients
\ll_{\epsilon}q^{\epsilon}
). Suppose we know that for some
\mathfrak{c}\in(1/2,1)
we have

\int_{\pm\infty}\frac{\max_{0\leq n\leq 2k-2}|\zeta^{(n)}(\sigma+it)|^{k(k-2)}dt}{(1+|t|)^{3/2+\mathfrak{c}}}\ll 1\hskip 28.45274pt\text{for }\sigma\geq\mathfrak{c}.

A bound essentially
\zeta(s)\ll t^{(1-\sigma)/2}
is classical, and therefore from Cauchy’s integral formula so is one
\zeta^{(n)}(s)^{k}\ll t^{k(1-\sigma)/2}
, so we can certainly take
\mathfrak{c}\in(1-1/k(k-2),1)
for
k>2
or take
\mathfrak{c}\in(1/2,1)
for
k=2
.
Let
\mathfrak{d}\in(0,1)
be any value for which we know

\sum_{n\leq x}d_{k}(n)^{2}=xP(\log x)+\mathcal{O}_{\epsilon}\left(x^{1-\mathfrak{d}+\epsilon}\right)

(3)

for some polynomial
P
of degree
k^{2}-1
. By page 12 of
[
17
]
we have for
\sigma>1

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{d_{k}(n)^{2}}{n^{s}}=\zeta(s)^{k^{2}}f(s)

where
f
for
\sigma\geq 1/2+\epsilon
is holomorphic and
\ll_{\epsilon}1
, so a Perron’s formula argument shows
\mathfrak{d}=2/(k^{2}+4)
to be permissable for general
k
whilst a classical result of Wilson and Ramanujan says
\mathfrak{d}=1/2
is okay for
k=2
.

Then our conclusion is

Theorem 1
.

Let
f_{x}(q,a)
be as in (
2
) and define

\displaystyle E_{x}(q,a)=\sum_{n\leq x\atop{n\equiv a(q)}}d_{k}(n)-\frac{xf_{x}(q,a)}{q}\hskip 17.07164pt\text{ and }\hskip 17.07164ptV(x,Q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{q\leq Q}\sum_{a=1}^{q}|E_{x}(q,a)|^{2}.

Then there is a polynomial
P(\cdot,\cdot)
of degree
\leq k^{2}-1
such that for
1\leq Q=o(x)

\displaystyle V(x,Q)=xQP(\log x,\log Q)+\mathcal{O}_{k,\epsilon}\left(Q^{2}\left(\frac{x}{Q}\right)^{\mathfrak{c}}+\underbrace{x^{3/2+\epsilon}}_{k=2}+\underbrace{x^{2-4/(6k-3)+\epsilon}}_{k>2}+Qx^{1-\mathfrak{d}+\epsilon}\right).

We consider the main feature of our theorem to be its validity for general
k
.

With some work the polynomial could be explicitly worked out of course. We note that we have chosen to average over a complete residue set, and not over a reduced one, as in most of the works referenced above, in agreement with a remark of Motohashi on page 178 of
[
11
]
.

The study of the mean-square of a function averaging over both residue and modulus started in the 60s with the Barban-Davenport-Halberstam Theorem, which provided an upper bound of the right size for the variance of the primes. This was refined to an asymptotic formula by Montgomery and by Hooley and correspondingly results of our kind are sometimes referred to as Montgomery-Hooley theorems - see
[
19
]
for a survey. In
[
7
]
Goldston and Vaughan developed a circle method approach to establishing Montgomery-Hooley theorems and it is that which we follow here.

Sequences or functions for which there exist Montgomery-Hooley theorems seem to have so far been mostly assumed to satisfy

\sum_{n\leq x\atop{n\equiv a(q)}}F(n)\approx\frac{xf(q,a)}{q}

(4)

although recently thinner sequences have also been investigated - see
[
4
]
. The divisor function poses a different problem in that, as said on page 86 of
[
19
]
, the RHS of (
1
) doesn’t factorise in the simple way of (
4
). With this paper we are showing that the method of Goldston and Vaughan still succeeds.

Finally we remark that as far as only upper bounds are concerned results will already be known even for
d_{k}(n)
, even if nothing explicit is stated in the literature - a general principle is that, through the large sieve, a Siegel-Walfisz theorem gives a result for the variance. So just for the record we also formulate

Theorem 2
.

Let
V(x,Q)
be as in Theorem
1
. For
1\leq Q\leq x

V(x,Q)\ll_{\epsilon}x^{\epsilon}\left(xQ+\underbrace{x^{2-1/k}}_{k>2}\right).

and prove it following Lemma
2
. The proof of Theorem
1
starts on page 15.
