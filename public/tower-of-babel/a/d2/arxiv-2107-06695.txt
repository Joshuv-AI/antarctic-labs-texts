Solving discrete constrained problems on de Rham complex
Zhongjie Lu

Abstract
The poor and even ill conditions of the discrete constrained problems cause some difficulties in solving them with iterative methods. In this paper, we transform the discrete constrained problems on de Rham complex to equivalent Laplace-like problems. This transformation not only make these constrained problems solvable, but also make it easy to use many existing iterative methods and preconditioning techniques to solving large-scale discrete constrained problems. Keywords: constrained problem, divergence-free, ill condition, iterative method

Introduction

Many systems of partial differential equations
contain constraint conditions.
For example,
when solving the second-order Maxwell equation,
we want the solution to satisfy
the divergence-free condition
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0
.
To this end,
a Lagrange multiplier is inserted into the equation

\begin{split}\nabla\times\nabla\times\mathbf{u}+c\mathbf{u}+\nabla p&=\mathbf{f},\\
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}&=0.\end{split}

(1)

Then the component in the solution
that dose not satisfy the condition
\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u}=0

is eliminated.
The weak formulation for such problems usually
have the following form:

\begin{split}a(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})+b(\mathbf{v},p)&=(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{v})\,\quad\forall\mathbf{v}\in V,\\
b(\mathbf{u},q)&=0\qquad\quad\forall q\in Q.\end{split}

(2)

Here,
V
and
Q
are the function spaces for
\mathbf{u}
and
p
, respectively.
By constructing two proper finite element spaces
V_{h}
and
Q_{h}
for the two spaces,
we obtain its discrete problem

\begin{split}a(\mathbf{u}_{h},\mathbf{v})+b(\mathbf{v}_{h},p_{h})&=(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{v}_{h})\quad\forall\mathbf{v}_{h}\in V_{h},\\
b(\mathbf{u}_{h},q_{h})&=0\qquad\quad\;\,\forall q_{h}\in Q_{h}.\end{split}

(3)

Usually this kind of discrete problems has poor condition.
This results in that
many existing iterative methods and preconditioning techniques
that are efficient for Laplace-like problems
are inefficient and even invalid for such problems.
With some boundary conditions
and domains with some special shapes,
we can prove that
the terms
b(\mathbf{u},q)
in (
2
)
and
b(q_{h},\mathbf{u}_{h})
in (
3
)
no longer satisfy the inf-sup condition,
but
\mathbf{u}
in (
2
) and
\mathbf{u}_{h}
in (
3
)
are still unique.
But the algebraic system is non-invertible.
Even direct methods can not solve it.
Thing is worse
for the constrained grad-div problem

\begin{split}-\nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{u})+c\mathbf{u}+\nabla\times\mathbf{p}&=\mathbf{f},\\
\nabla\times\mathbf{u}&=0.\end{split}

There is an infinite-dimensional kernel contained in
its constraint condition
\nabla\times\mathbf{u}=0
.
Its corresponding algebraic system is highly ill.

A popular method to deal with such discrete problems is
penalty method
[
5
]
.
If the
\mathbf{u}_{h}
in (
3
) is unique,
the system of the penalty method is solvable.
With the penalty parameter becoming large,
the penalty solution tends to the exact solution.
If we want more precious solution,
the penalty parameter should be larger.
However, if the penalty parameter is too large,
the condition of the system of penalty method become terrible.
Besides this, because of the limited machine precision,
the penalty parameter can be arbitrarily large.
There are also researches on the preconditioned discretizations
for partial differential equations that can match such problems
[
8
]
.
There is a comprehensive survey about
the saddle point problems in
[
4
]

Matrix aspects of the constrained problem

Before we consider the discretization of the constrained problems on de Rham complex,
we study their matrix form:

\begin{split}(\mathcal{A}+c\mathcal{M})u+\mathcal{B}p&=\mathcal{F},\\
\mathcal{B}^{T}u&=\mathcal{G}.\end{split}

(5)

In this section, we definite this matrix system
and derive some theoretical results
that we shall use in the later sections.
In Section
5

we will show the relation between

In the system (

Theorem 2.5

Let

\mathbb{C}_{0}

\mathcal{M}\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}^{T}\mathcal{M}u^{(0)}=\mathcal{M}f^{(0)}

Proof.

As
\mathcal{A}

As

\begin{split}f^{(1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{\dim\mathbb{C}_{1}}f^{(1)}_{i}\mathfrak{u}^{(1)}_{i}.\end{split}

(17)

Here

is the coefficient of each eigenvector
in the base (
8

Similarly,
u^{(1)}\in\mathbb{C}_{1}
can be expanded as

\begin{split}u^{(1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{\dim\mathbb{C}_{1}}u^{(1)}_{i}\mathfrak{u}^{(1)}_{i},\end{split}

(18)

where

Putting the expansions

17

\begin{split}\left(\mathcal{A}+c\mathcal{M}\right)\sum_{i=1}^{\dim\mathbb{C}_{1}}u^{(1)}_{i}\mathfrak{u}^{(1)}_{i}=\mathcal{M}\sum_{i=1}^{\dim\mathbb{C}_{1}}f^{(1)}_{i}\mathfrak{u}^{(1)}_{i}.\end{split}

By the equation

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{\dim\mathbb{C}_{1}}\left(\lambda^{(1)}_{i}+c\right)u^{(1)}_{i}\mathcal{M}\mathfrak{u}^{(1)}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{\dim\mathbb{C}_{1}}f^{(1)}_{i}\mathcal{M}\mathfrak{u}^{(1)}_{i}.\end{split}

\begin{split}\left(\lambda^{(1)}_{i}+c\right)u^{(1)}_{i}=f^{(1)}_{i}\quad\text{for each }i.\end{split}

Then we have

\begin{split}u^{(1)}_{i}=\frac{f^{(1)}_{i}}{\lambda^{(1)}_{i}+c}\end{split}

and we obtain the unique solution

\mathcal{A}u^{(1)}+c\mathcal{M}u^{(1)}=\mathcal{M}f^{(1)}

Theorem 2.6
.

{\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}^{T}={\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{U}\mathcal{B}^{T}=\mathbb{C}_{0}\oplus_{\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{C}_{1}
.

Proof.

For
u\in{\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}^{T}
,
we have
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{U}\mathcal{B}^{T}u=\mathcal{B}\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{B}^{T}u)=0

and then
u\in{\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{U}\mathcal{B}^{T}
.

For
u\in{\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{U}\mathcal{B}^{T}
, we have

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{U}\mathcal{B}^{T}u=0\Longrightarrow u^{T}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{U}\mathcal{B}^{T}u=0\Longrightarrow(\mathcal{B}^{T}u)^{T}\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{B}^{T}u)=0.\end{split}

As
\mathcal{U}
is Hermitian positive definite,
we have
\mathcal{B}^{T}u=0
and then
u\in{\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}^{T}
.
∎

Theorem 2.7
.

\mathbb{C}^{M}={\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}\oplus{\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}
.

Proof.

There is an orthogonal decomposition for
\mathbb{C}^{M}
:

\begin{split}\mathbb{C}^{M}={\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}\oplus\left({\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}\right)^{\perp}.\end{split}

(19)

For
p\in\left({\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}\right)^{\perp}
,
we have

\begin{split}(\mathcal{B}p,v)=(p,\mathcal{B}^{T}v)=0\quad\text{for any }v\in\mathbb{C}^{N}.\end{split}

Here we use
\mathcal{B}^{T}v\in{\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}
for any
v\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
.
Then we have
\mathcal{B}p=0
and
\left({\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}\right)^{\perp}\subset{\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}
.

For
p\in{\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}
,
there is a
v\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
such that

p=\mathcal{B}^{T}v
.
For
q\in{\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}
,
we have

\begin{split}(p,q)=(\mathcal{B}^{T}v,p)=(v,\mathcal{B}q)=0.\end{split}

Then we have
{\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}\perp{\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}
.
By the decomposition (
19
),
we have
{\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}\subset\left({\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}\right)^{\perp}
.

Then we have
{\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}=\left({\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}\right)^{\perp}
.
By the decomposition (
19
),
we obtain the conclusion.
∎

With the similar proof, we have the next theorem.

Theorem 2.8
.

\mathbb{C}^{N}={\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}^{T}\oplus{\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}
.

Theorem 2.9
.

\mathbb{C}_{2}={\text{Im}}\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{M}\mathbb{C}_{2}={\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.8
, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{C}^{N}={\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}^{T}\oplus{\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}={\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}^{T}\oplus_{\mathcal{M}}\mathcal{M}^{-1}{\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}={\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}^{T}\oplus_{\mathcal{M}}{\text{Im}}\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{B}.\end{split}

(20)

By Theorem
2.6
, we have

\begin{split}{\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}^{T}={\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{U}\mathcal{B}^{T}=\mathbb{C}_{0}\oplus_{\mathcal{M}}\mathbb{C}_{1}.\end{split}

By the
\mathcal{M}
-decomposition for
\mathbb{C}^{N}
(
13
) and (
20
),
we obtain
\mathbb{C}_{2}={\text{Im}}\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{B}
.

The second result
\mathcal{M}\mathbb{C}_{2}={\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}
is equivalent to the first one.
∎

Theorem 2.10
.

For
\mathcal{G}\in{\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}
, if there is a
u_{g}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
such that
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{U}\mathcal{B}^{T}u_{g}=\mathcal{B}\mathcal{U}\mathcal{G}
,
then
\mathcal{B}^{T}u_{g}=\mathcal{G}
.

Proof.

Letting
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}=\mathcal{B}^{T}u_{g}
,
then we have
\tilde{\mathcal{G}}\in{\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}

and
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{U}\mathcal{B}^{T}u_{g}=\mathcal{B}\mathcal{U}\tilde{\mathcal{G}}
.
Consequently, we have
\mathcal{G}-\tilde{\mathcal{G}}\in{\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}

and
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{G}-\tilde{\mathcal{G}})=0
.
Then
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{G}-\tilde{\mathcal{G}})\in{\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}
.
As
{\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}\perp{\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}
by Theorem
2.7
,
we have
(\mathcal{G}-\tilde{\mathcal{G}})^{T}\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{G}-\tilde{\mathcal{G}})=0
.
As
\mathcal{U}
is a Hermitian positive definite matrix,
we have
\mathcal{G}-\tilde{\mathcal{G}}=0
.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

The subspace
\mathbb{C}_{0}
is an inherent characteristic of a system.
For a system, the eigenvalue problems (

needs to be computed only once.
The matrix
\mathcal{H}
can be fixed when the right hand side varies.

As the eigenvectors with zero eigenvalue of
the eigenvalue problem
are usually full vectors,
the term
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}^{T}\mathcal{M}
is probably a full matrix.
This results that if the dimension of the matrix is a litter large,
direct methods become inefficient and even impossible
when computing the equation (

An alternative choice is to use iterative methods,
where the matrix-vector multiplication

can be computed one by one.
Thus we can avoid dealing with the term
\mathcal{M}\mathcal{H}\mathcal{H}^{T}\mathcal{M}
in an explicit way.

∎

The measure of the errors

\begin{split}\frac{\|\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{B}p-\left(\mathcal{A}+c\mathcal{M}\right)u\|+\|\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{B}^{T}u\|}{\|\mathcal{F}\|+\|\mathcal{G}\|}\end{split}

As the settings in the equivalent problems

), (

in

where we also use the decomposition for
\mathcal{F}
(

Then we have

in the equivalent problems

and

6.3
The penalty method

Let
\mathcal{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{M\times M}
be a matrix that is easy to invert.
For the equation

\begin{split}\left(\mathcal{A}+c\mathcal{M}+{\varepsilon}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{S}^{-1}\mathcal{B}^{T}\right)u_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{F}+{\varepsilon}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{S}^{-1}\mathcal{G},\end{split}

(96)

its solution
u_{\varepsilon}
tend to the solution
u

on the system (
5
)
as the penalty parameter
\varepsilon
goes to infinity,
i.e.

\begin{split}u_{\varepsilon}\to u\text{ as }\varepsilon\to\infty.\end{split}

This method is widely used to solving the constrained problems.

The penalty parameter can not be arbitrarily large
in actual computations because of the limit of computer precision.
Consequently,
the error of the penalty solution
u_{\varepsilon}

can not be arbitrarily small.
Furthermore, the large parameters results that
the condition of the system (
96
) becomes bad.
In the numerical experiments,
we will compare the solutions of the penalty method and the exact solutions.

6.4
The inconsistent initial data
\mathcal{G}

In actual applications,
if the columns of
\mathcal{B}
are not full-rank,
it is possible that
the data
\mathcal{G}
does not satisfy
the basic requirement
\mathcal{G}\in{\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}
.
This results in that
there does not exist a
u\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
such that
\mathcal{B}^{T}u=\mathcal{G}
.
By Theorem
2.7
,
there is the orthogonal decomposition for
\mathbb{C}^{M}
:

\begin{split}\mathbb{C}^{M}={\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}\oplus{\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}.\end{split}

Then the
\mathcal{G}
can be divided into two orthogonal parts:

\begin{split}\mathcal{G}=\mathcal{G}_{c}+\mathcal{G}_{i},\end{split}

(97)

where
\mathcal{G}_{c}\in{\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}
and
\mathcal{G}_{i}\in{\text{Ker}}\mathcal{B}
.
We call
\mathcal{G}_{c}
the consistent part of
\mathcal{G}

and
\mathcal{G}_{i}
the inconsistent part of
\mathcal{G}
.
The inconsistent data can be introduced
through some inevitable ways,
for instance, numerical quadrature, data collection, machine precision and so on.
Of course,
there is no solution mathematically
when the inconsistent data
\mathcal{G}_{i}\not=0
.
However, the solution for the consistent part
\mathcal{G}_{c}

may be still meaningful,
especially when the consistent part
\mathcal{G}_{c}
is the dominant part of
\mathcal{G}
.

As the discussions in Section
3
and
4
,
the linear equations in the equivalent problems that we construct
are all well-posed.
What will happen if we still use these equivalent problems
to compute the solution
u\in\mathbb{C}^{N}

in the system (
61
)
in this case
\mathcal{G}\not\in{\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}
?

We choose

\begin{split}\mathcal{U}=\alpha\mathcal{I},\end{split}

(98)

where
\alpha
is a positive number
and
\mathcal{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
is the identity matrix.
The only place that involves
\mathcal{G}
in these equivalent problems
is the right hand side
\mathcal{B}\mathcal{U}\mathcal{G}
.
If
\mathcal{U}
is chosen as (
98
),
we have

\begin{split}\mathcal{B}\mathcal{U}\mathcal{G}=\alpha\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{G}_{c}+\mathcal{G}_{i}\right)=\alpha\mathcal{B}\mathcal{G}_{c}\equiv\mathcal{B}\mathcal{U}\mathcal{G}_{c}.\end{split}

(99)

By this result,
we know that only the consistent part
\mathcal{G}_{c}
in
\mathcal{G}

has effect on the solutions of these equivalent problems.
Then the solution
u\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
in these equivalent problems
with the choice (
98
) for
\mathcal{U}

is the solution of the system

\begin{split}(\mathcal{A}+c\mathcal{M})u+\mathcal{B}p&=\mathcal{F}\\
\mathcal{B}^{T}u&=\mathcal{G}_{c}.\end{split}

(100)

In this situation,
we can not use the error (
95
) to measure the convergence
of these equivalent problems
as the term
\|\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{B}^{T}u\|
in (
95
)
never converge to zero
because of the inconsistent component
\mathcal{G}_{i}\not=0
.
It seems that we should replace this term by
\|\mathcal{G}_{c}-\mathcal{B}^{T}u\|
.
However, we do not compute the explicit
\mathcal{G}_{c}
.
By Theorem
2.10
,
for
\mathcal{G}_{c}\in{\text{Im}}\mathcal{B}^{T}
,

\mathcal{B}^{T}u=\mathcal{G}_{c}
is equivalent to

\alpha\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}^{T}u=\alpha\mathcal{B}\mathcal{G}_{c}\equiv\alpha\mathcal{B}\mathcal{G}

with choice (
98
) for
\mathcal{U}
.
Then in this case,
the error
\|\mathcal{G}_{c}-\mathcal{B}^{T}u\|

can be replaced by

\begin{split}\alpha\|\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}^{T}u-\mathcal{B}\mathcal{G}_{c}\|\equiv\alpha\|\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}^{T}u-\mathcal{B}\mathcal{G}\|.\end{split}

Then we obtain an error to measure the convergence in this situation:

\begin{split}\frac{\|\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{B}p-\left(\mathcal{A}+c\mathcal{M}\right)u\|+\alpha\|\mathcal{B}\mathcal{G}-\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}^{T}u\|}{\|\mathcal{F}\|+\|\mathcal{G}\|}.\end{split}

For the penalty method (
96
),
we choose
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{I}
when the
\mathcal{G}
is inconsistent.
In this case, the penalty method (
96
) becomes

\begin{split}\left(\mathcal{A}+c\mathcal{M}+\varepsilon\mathcal{B}\mathcal{B}^{T}\right)u_{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{F}+\varepsilon\mathcal{B}\mathcal{G}\end{split}

(101)

The solution
u_{\varepsilon}
also tends to
the solution
u\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
in the system (
100
)
as the penalty parameter
\varepsilon
goes to infinity.

8
Conclusions

In this paper,
we consider solving the discrete systems
of the constrained problems on de Rham complex.
The first difficulty is the poor condition
of the discrete systems.
Many existing iterative methods and preconditioning techniques
do not work for such systems.
The second difficulty is that
the systems are non-invertible
in the case that
the constraint terms do not satisfy
inf-sup condition,
even if the desired component in the system is still unique.

We discretize the constrained problems
use the finite element complex.
We prove that the matrices in the algebraic system
satisfy the property
\mathcal{A}\mathcal{M}^{-1}\mathcal{B}=0
.
This property corresponds to the property
d^{2}=0
on complex.
This is the extra property of the matrix systems in this paper
compared with general constrained problems.
By this property,
we prove that the explicit Hodge decomposition of the right hand side
can be obtained through solving a Hodge Laplacian problem.
We construct several equivalent problems for
the discrete systems.
No mater whether the constraint term satisfy
inf-sup condition or not,
only if the component
\mathbf{u}_{h}
is unique,
we can solve it though some well-posed problems.
Furthermore,
the spectral distributions of the equations contained
in these equivalent problems are Laplace-like,
if the complex is Fredholm.
Then many existing iterative methods
and preconditioning techniques
can be applied to solving them.
In our paper
[
7
]
,
we have discussed how to solve
the linear equations and eigenvalue problems in these equivalent problems.
This make the large-scalar constrained discrete problem
become easy to solve.

We provide several numerical experiments on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
complex
to verify the equivalent problems.
The numerical results show the capability and efficiency
of the equivalent problems that we construct.
