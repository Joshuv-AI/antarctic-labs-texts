Accuracy and Architecture Studies of Residual Neural Network solving Ordinary Differential Equations
Changxin Qiu, Aaron Bendickson, Joshua Kalyanapu, Jue Yan

Abstract
In this paper we consider utilizing a residual neural network (ResNet) to solve ordinary differential equations. Stochastic gradient descent method is applied to obtain the optimal parameter set of weights and biases of the network. We apply forward Euler, Runge-Kutta2 and Runge-Kutta4 finite difference methods to generate three sets of targets training the ResNet and carry out the target study. The well trained ResNet behaves just as its counterpart of the corresponding one-step finite difference method. In particular, we carry out (1) the architecture study in terms of number of hidden layers and neurons per layer to find the optimal ResNet structure; (2) the target study to verify the ResNet solver behaves as accurate as its finite difference method counterpart; (3) solution trajectory simulation. Even the ResNet solver looks like and is implemented in a way similar to forward Euler scheme, its accuracy can be as high as any one step method. A sequence of numerical examples are presented to demonstrate the performance of the ResNet solver.

2
Problem setup, notations and residual neural network

2.1
Setup and notations

We consider solving following ordinary differential equation (ODE) system initial value problem

\frac{d\textbf{x}}{dt}=\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}(t)),~~\textbf{x}(t_{0})=\textbf{x}_{0},

(2.1)

where
\textbf{x}\in R^{n}
are the state variables.
We assume the right hand side vector function
\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}(t))
is Lipschitz continuous, with

\|\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}^{1})-\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}^{2})\|_{2}\leq L\|\textbf{x}^{1}-\textbf{x}^{2}\|_{2}.

(2.2)

Here
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the vector
L^{2}
norm.
Integrating the dynamic system (
2.1
) from
t_{0}
to
t_{0}+\Delta
, where
\Delta
is an increment or time lag, we obtain the following integral format of the ODE system

\textbf{x}(t_{0}+\Delta)-\textbf{x}(t_{0})=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta}\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}(t))~dt.

(2.3)

In this paper, we apply residual neural network as an solver to approximate (
2.3
).

The learning data are collected in the form of pairs. Each data pair refers to the solution states at two different time locations along one trajectory. The learning data set is given as

S=\left\{\left(\textbf{y}_{j}^{1},\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}\right)\,|\,\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}\in D\right\}_{j=1}^{J},

(2.4)

where
J
denotes the total number of data pairs and
D
is the domain of interest, from which the initial states of the learning data pairs are collected. For each pair, we have

\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}=\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}),~~~\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}\approx\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta),\quad j=1,\dots,J.

(2.5)

Again, we have
\Delta
denote the time lag between the two states, and taken as a constant for all
j
throughout this paper. Initial state of the learning data
\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}
can be randomly generated from the domain of interest
D
, i.e., generated with uniform distribution. We have
\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}
denoting the learning target, which is obtained from finite difference scheme approximation with time step size
\Delta
. In this article, we study three explicit methods of forward Euler, Runge-Kutta 2 and Runge-Kutta 4 to generate the target data.

2.2
One-step Residue Network (ResNet)

In this section, we present the residual neural network (ResNet) as a one-step method solving the ordinary differential equation system (
2.1
). The abstract goal of machine learning is to find a function
\mathcal{N}:R^{n}\to R^{n}
such that
\mathcal{N}(\cdot;\Theta)
accurately predicts the state
\textbf{x}(t_{0}+\Delta)
, given the initial state
\textbf{x}(t_{0})
and the time lag
\Delta
. The function
\mathcal{N}(\cdot;\Theta)
is parameterized by weight matrices and biases. The optimal parameter set
\Theta
will be obtained by training the network intensively over the given data set.

In this paper, we consider a standard fully connected feedforward neural network (FNN)
[
47
,
48
,
16
]
with
M
layers. We have
M\geq 3
, since the input and output vectors of the network are considered as the first and last layer. Among the total
M
layers, the interior
(M-2)
are the hidden layers. Thus the minimum structure of the neural network involves one hidden layer with
M=3
. We refer to Figure
2
for the structure of a standard feedForward neural network. Having
n_{i}
(
i=1,\cdots,M
) denote the number of neurons in each layer. The first layer is the input vector with
n_{1}
as its dimension and the last layer is the output vector with
n_{M}
as its dimension. With the input and output vectors as either the state or the approximation of the state
\textbf{x}(\cdot)
of the ODE system (
2.1
), we have
n_{1}=n_{M}=n
. Given the network input
\textbf{p}^{in}\in R^{n}
, the output of the FNN network is denoted as

\textbf{p}^{out}=\mathcal{N}(\textbf{p}^{in};\Theta),

where
\Theta
is the parameter set including all parameters in the network, i.e., weight matrices and biases connecting all layers.

(b)

Architecture of feedForward and residual neural network

The idea of residual neural network (ResNet) is to further introduce an identity operator and have the network effectively approximate the “residue” of the input-output map.
The structure of ResNet is also illustrated in Figure
2
to compare with feedFoward neural network. Now the ResNet consists of
M
fully connected layers and an identity operator that further "adds" the input
\textbf{p}^{in}
vector into the output
\textbf{p}^{out}
. With the identity operator introduced, the resulting ResNet produces the following neural network

\textbf{p}^{out}=\textbf{p}^{in}+\mathcal{N}(\textbf{p}^{in};\Theta),

(2.6)

where
\Theta
again denotes the collection of weight matrices and bias parameters.

The core residual neural network
\mathcal{N}(\cdot;\Theta)
still consists of
M
layers. Each two consecutive layers is connected with an affine linear transformation and a point-wise nonlinear activation function.
The function or the mapping
\mathcal{N}(\cdot;\Theta)
is a composition of following operators,

\mathcal{N}(\cdot;\Theta)=(\sigma_{M}\circ\textbf{W}_{M-1})\circ\cdots\circ(\sigma_{2}\circ\textbf{W}_{1}).

(2.7)

Here
\circ
stands for operator composition. We have
\textbf{W}_{i}
denoting the linear transformation operator or the weight matrix connecting the neurons from
i
-th layer to
(i+1)
-th layer. The parameter set is further augmented with the biases vectors. We have
\sigma_{i}:R\to R
denoting the activation function (
i\geq 2
), which is applied to each neuron of the
i
-th layer in a component-wise fashion. There are many activation functions available and widely used, e.g., the sigmoid functions, the ReLU (rectified linear unit), etc. In this paper we apply ReLU function as the activation function. Specifically
\sigma_{i}=max(0,x)
is applied between all layers, except to the output layer for which we have
\sigma_{M}(x)=x
. This is a common choice for deep learning neural network. Notice the activation function starts its application from the second layer
i=2
, since the input vector is regarded as the first layer.

The goal is to train the network to obtain an optimal parameter set
\Theta
, such that the ResNet can accurately approximate the
\Delta
-lag flow map
\textbf{x}(t)\to\textbf{x}(t+\Delta)
. This is achieved by applying (
2.6
)-(
2.7
) with
\textbf{p}^{in}_{j}=\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}
to obtain the network output
\textbf{p}_{j}^{out}
, comparing with the target
\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}
, and then looping among the data set
S
to minimize the error or the squared loss function

L(\Theta)=\|\textbf{p}_{j}^{out}-\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}\|_{2}^{2},\quad\forall j=1,\cdots,J.

(2.8)

The notation of (
2.6
) is abused here. We have
\textbf{p}_{j}^{out}
referring to the
j
-th output corresponding to its input
\textbf{p}^{in}_{j}=\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}
, where
j
is the index of the data pair in the learning data set
S
of (
2.4
). Usually the mean squared error

L(\Theta)=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\|\textbf{p}_{j}^{out}-\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}\|_{2}^{2},

is applied as the loss function, referred as the gradient descent method. To be more efficient, we apply (
2.8
) instead, which is referred as the stochastic or approximate gradient descent method. Finally, we minimize the error (
2.8
) iteratively to obtain the optimal parameter set
\Theta
, corresponding to a given tolerance
\epsilon

\Theta_{i}\longrightarrow\Theta_{i+1},\quad i=1,\cdots,K.

(2.9)

Here
K
is the total number of iterations involved in the training. Again, we go through the whole data set of (
2.2
) over each iteration to update the parameter set.

With the optimal parameter set
\Theta^{*}
determined and the well trained neural network
\mathcal{N}(\cdot;\Theta^{*})
available, the ResNet (
2.6
) can be viewed as an accurate ODE system (
2.1
) solver with time step size
\Delta
. In a word, we can apply
\textbf{p}^{0}=\textbf{x}(t_{0})
, and repeatedly call the ResNet (
2.6
) to generate a discrete set of points values approximating the ODE system

\textbf{p}^{k+1}=\textbf{p}^{k}+\mathcal{N}(\textbf{p}^{k};\Theta^{*}),\quad\textbf{p}^{0}=\textbf{x}(t_{0}).

(2.10)

The ResNet neural network (
2.10
) can be considered as an ODE solver to approximate the solution trajectory, which is similar to an explicit finite difference scheme. Comparing the one-step ResNet (
2.6
) and the integral format of the ODE system (
2.3
), it is easy to see that a well trained network operator
\mathcal{N}(\cdot;\Theta^{*})
is an approximation to the effective increment

\mathcal{N}(\textbf{x}(t_{0});\Theta^{*})\approx\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta}\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}(t))~dt.

(2.11)

The initial states in the learning data set
S
are randomly generated from the domain of interest. There is no error for the ResNet input
\textbf{p}_{j}^{in}=\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}
, since we have
\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}=\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0})
with
\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0})
being the initial condition of the dynamic system (
2.3
), where
j
is the integer index of the learning data set
S
. Thus the error of the ResNet ODE solver mainly comes from the target
\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}
, see (
2.8
).
In the following section, we carry out fundamental analysis and show the accuracy of the ResNet depends on the accuracy or the quality of the target from the data pair (
2.4
).

The relation between training data pair

Lemma 1

Given the ResNet training target
\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}
of (

) obtained from a one-step method with

\|\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}-\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)\|_{2}\leq C\Delta^{k+1},

Proof 1

Suppose
\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}
is obtained from a one-step method

\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}=\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}+\Delta\times\Phi\left(t_{0},\textbf{y}_{j}^{1},\textbf{F}(\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}),\Delta\right),

(3.6)

with

and step size of
\Delta

3.6

\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)=\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0})+\Delta\times\Phi\left(t_{0},\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}),\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0})),\Delta\right)+\Delta\times\tau_{j}(\Delta).

(3.7)

Subtract (
3.6

\|\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)-\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}\|_{2}\leq\Delta\|\Phi\left(t_{0},\textbf{y}_{j}^{1},\textbf{F}(\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}),\Delta\right)-\Phi\left(t_{0},\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}),\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0})),\Delta\right)\|_{2}+\Delta\times\tau_{j}(\Delta)\leq C\Delta^{k+1},

where

depends on the regularity of the system.

), we have

\displaystyle\|\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}-\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}+\Delta\times\textbf{F}(\textbf{y}_{j}^{1},t_{0})-\left(\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta}\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t),t)~dt\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta}\left(\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}),t_{0})-\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t),t)\right)~dt\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta}\frac{d}{dt}\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(\xi(t)),\xi(t))(t-t_{0})~dt\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta^{2}}{2}\left\|\frac{d}{dt}\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(\eta),\eta)\right\|_{2}\leq C\Delta^{2}.

(3.8)

Here
\frac{d}{dt}\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}(t),t)=\frac{\partial\textbf{F}}{\partial\textbf{x}}\textbf{F}+\frac{\partial\textbf{F}}{\partial t}
refers to the complete derivative to the
t
variable, with
\frac{\partial\textbf{F}}{\partial\textbf{x}}
denoting the Jacobian matrix of the vector function
F
on variable
\textbf{x}(t)
and
\frac{d\textbf{x}}{dt}=\textbf{F}
. Forward Euler method can be considered as a constant quadrature rule approximation to the integral of the ODE system (
3.1
). Weighted mean value theorem is applied to estimate the error term.

Case II:

\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}
obtained from 2nd order Runge-Kutta method (
3.3
)

Again we have
\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}=\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0})
. Subtract
\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)
of (
3.1
) from
\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}
of the second order Runge-Kutta method (
3.3
), we have

\displaystyle\|\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}-\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}+\Delta\times\left(\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}}{2}\right)-\left(\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta}\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t),t)~dt\right)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\Delta\times\left(\frac{k_{1}+k_{2}}{2}\right)-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta}\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t),t)~dt\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\Delta\times\left(\frac{k_{1}+\widetilde{k_{2}}}{2}\right)-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta}\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t),t)~dt\right\|_{2}+\frac{\Delta}{2}\|k_{2}-\widetilde{k_{2}}\|_{2},

where
k_{2}=\textbf{F}(\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}+\Delta k_{1},t_{0}+\Delta)
,
k_{1}=\textbf{F}(\textbf{y}_{j}^{1},t_{0})
and
\widetilde{k_{2}}=\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta),t_{0}+\Delta)
. With the
O(\Delta)
local truncation error of the forward Euler method approximating
\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)
and applying the Lipschitz continuity of
F
of the dynamic system, we have

\displaystyle\|k_{2}-\widetilde{k_{2}}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\textbf{F}(\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}+\Delta k_{1},t_{0}+\Delta)-\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta),t_{0}+\Delta)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq L\|\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}+\Delta k_{1}-\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)\|_{2}\leq C\Delta^{2}.

Here
C
represents a generic constant. The error from the two-points quadrature rule can be estimated as

\displaystyle\left\|\Delta\times\left(\frac{k_{1}+\widetilde{k_{2}}}{2}\right)-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta}\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t),t)~dt\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta}(\textbf{G}_{1}(t)-\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t),t))~dt\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(\eta),\eta)\right\|_{2}\left|\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta}(t-t_{0})\left(t-(t_{0}+\Delta)\right)~dt\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\Delta^{3}}{12}\left\|\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(\eta),\eta)\right\|_{2}\leq C\Delta^{3}.

Combine the above arguments, we have

\|\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}-\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)\|_{2}\leq C\Delta^{3}.

(3.9)

Here
\textbf{G}_{1}(t)
denotes the linear interpolation polynomial that interpolates
\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}(t),t)
at
t_{0}
and
t_{0}+\Delta
. And
\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}(\cdot),\cdot)
denotes the complete second derivative of
\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}(t),t)
to
t
. This 2-stage Runge-Kutta method can be considered as a trapezoidal quadrature rule approximating the integration.

Case III:

\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}
obtained from 4th order Runge-Kutta method (
3.4
)

With
\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}=\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0})
and subtract
\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)
of (
3.1
) from
\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}
of the fourth order Runge-Kutta method (
3.4
), we have

\displaystyle\,\|\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}-\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\Delta(k_{1}+3k_{2}+3k_{3}+k_{4})}{8}-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta}\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t),t)~dt\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\Delta(k_{1}+3\widetilde{k_{2}}+3\widetilde{k_{3}}+\widetilde{k_{4})}}{8}-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta}\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t),t)~dt\right\|_{2}+\left\|\frac{\Delta(k_{1}+3k_{2}+3k_{3}+k_{4})}{8}-\frac{\Delta(k_{1}+3\widetilde{k_{2}}+3\widetilde{k_{3}}+\widetilde{k_{4})}}{8}\right\|_{2}.

Terms of
k_{2},k_{3}
and
k_{4}
are from the Runge-Kutta4 method (
3.4
), with
k_{1}=\textbf{F}(\textbf{y}_{j}^{1},t_{0})=\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}),t_{0})
. We have
\widetilde{k_{2}}=\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\frac{\Delta}{3}),t_{0}+\frac{\Delta}{3})
,
\widetilde{k_{3}}=\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\frac{2\Delta}{3}),t_{0}+\frac{2\Delta}{3})
and

\widetilde{k_{4}}=\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta),t_{0}+\Delta)
introduced that
k_{2},k_{3}
and
k_{4}
approximate. Rewrite the Runge-Kutta4 method of (
3.4
) as a one-step method,
\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}=\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}+\Delta\Phi\left(t_{0},\textbf{y}_{j}^{1},\textbf{F}(\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}),\Delta\right)
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\Delta(k_{1}+3k_{2}+3k_{3}+k_{4})}{8}-\frac{\Delta(k_{1}+3\widetilde{k_{2}}+3\widetilde{k_{3}}+\widetilde{k_{4})}}{8}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Delta\left\|\Phi\left(t_{0},\textbf{y}_{j}^{1},\textbf{F}(\textbf{y}_{j}^{1}),\Delta\right)-\Phi\left(t_{0},\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}),\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0})),\Delta\right)\right\|_{2}\leq C\Delta^{5}.

Here
C
represents a generic constant. The error from the four-points quadrature rule can be estimated as

\displaystyle\left\|\Delta\times\left(\frac{k_{1}+3\widetilde{k_{2}}+3\widetilde{k_{3}}+\widetilde{k_{4}}}{8}\right)-\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta}\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t),t)~dt\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta}(\textbf{G}_{3}(t)-\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}_{j}(t),t))~dt\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\left\|\frac{d^{4}\textbf{F}}{dt^{4}}\right\|_{2}\left|\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta}(t-t_{0})(t-(t_{0}+\frac{\Delta}{3}))(t-(t_{0}+\frac{2\Delta}{3}))\left(t-(t_{0}+\Delta)\right)~dt\right|\leq C\Delta^{5}.

Again
C
represents a generic constant. Summarize the above arguments, we have

\|\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}-\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)\|_{2}\leq C\Delta^{5}.

(3.10)

Here
\textbf{G}_{3}(t)
denotes the cubic interpolation polynomial that interpolates
\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}(t),t)
at
t_{0}
,
t_{0}+\Delta/3
,
t_{0}+2\Delta/3
and
t_{0}+\Delta
. And
\frac{d^{4}\textbf{F}}{dt^{4}}
denotes the complete fourth derivative of
\textbf{F}(\textbf{x}(t),t)
to
t
variable at somewhere. This version of 4-stage Runge-Kutta method can be considered as the three-eighth Simpson quadrature rule approximating the integration.

Theorem 1

Have
\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)
denote the solution of the ODE system (
2.3
) with initial
\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0})
. Suppose the parameter set
\Theta
of the ResNet (
2.6
) is well trained such that the error between the output
\textbf{p}_{j}^{out}
and its target
\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}
is on machine round-off error level. Given the target
\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}
obtained from a
k
-th order one-step method, we have

\|\textbf{p}_{j}^{out}-\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)\|_{2}\leq C\Delta^{k+1},

(3.11)

where constant
C=C(\textbf{F})
depends on the regularity of the ODE system.

Proof 2

Apply triangle inequality and we have

\displaystyle\|\textbf{p}_{j}^{out}-\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\|\textbf{p}_{j}^{out}-\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}+\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}-\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\textbf{p}_{j}^{out}-\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}\|_{2}+\|\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}-\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\mbox{Training error}+\mbox{Target error}.

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta^{k+1}.

The ResNet (
2.6
) can be trained very well to obtain an
optimal
parameter set of weight matrices and biases. Numerical experiments show the training errors of
\|\textbf{p}_{j}^{out}-\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}\|_{2}
are small or trained to the tolerance error level and can be ignored when comparing to the target error of
\|\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}-\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)\|_{2}
. Tests show ResNet neural network does
a very good job
approximating the target
\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}
with stochastic gradient descent method and can be used as an accurate solver for the ODE system.

3.2
Objective and implementation setup

Now we describe the objective of studies for the rest of the article mainly through numerical tests. For each ODE system or in each numerical example, we conduct following three studies:

1.

Neural network architecture study in terms of numbers of layers and neurons;

2.

Training targets study in terms of error behavior;

3.

Solution trajectory approximation.

In the first architecture study, we vary the number of layers and neurons per layer and observe how the rectangular arrangements of neurons affects the performance of the network. The specific arrangement of neurons and layers is called the
architecture
of the network. For each neural network with an architecture setting, we train the network with a highly accurate training target
\textbf{z}_{i}^{2}
generated from a refined mesh Runge-Kutta4 method (mesh size
h=\Delta/1000
), and will be trained long enough for the error curve to be almost flat. Once the training is finished for this network, we then use a test set of
J
pairs of data
(\textbf{z}_{i}^{1},\textbf{z}_{i}^{2})^{J}_{i=1}
with
\textbf{p}_{i}^{in}=\textbf{z}^{1}_{i}
as the ResNet input and compare the ResNet output
\textbf{p}_{i}^{out}
to the reference solution
\textbf{z}_{i}^{2}
to calculate the error. The following two error norms are computed to measure the worst case and the average case of errors among the total
J
pairs of data.

Max(L_{\infty})=\max_{1\leq i\leq J}\|\textbf{p}_{i}^{out}-\textbf{z}_{i}^{2}\|_{\infty},

(3.12)

Mean(L_{2})=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{i=1}^{J}\|\textbf{p}_{i}^{out}-\textbf{z}_{i}^{2}\|_{2}.

(3.13)

To avoid the influence from the initialization of weights and biases, which are generated from a normal distribution around zero,
we apply a total ten runs of random initialization of the parameter set and average the output errors of (
3.12
) and (
3.13
) as the performance measurement of that specific ResNet. Notice that same learning data set and same number of iterations (i.e.
K=500
of (
2.9
)) as the stopping condition are applied for each neural network architecture study. Furthermore, we do not differentiate the learning data set and the test set, since no difference is observed. We simply apply the learning data set to calculate the errors of (
3.12
) and (
3.13
).

The goal of the second study is to verify the result of Theorem
1
that the ResNet solver error
\|\textbf{p}_{j}^{out}-\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)\|_{2}
is dominated by the target error
\|\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}-\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)\|_{2}
. Following the architecture study, we pick
one
efficient and accurate ResNet network architecture on which we run the subsequent tests. A deeper neural network with many hidden layers has many more parameters than a simpler network, so is computationally more expensive to train. The computational cost and the accuracy measured by (
3.12
) and (
3.13
) together are considered as metrics by which we select the
one
ResNet architecture that will be used for the target study and solution trajectory simulation.

In the second
target
study, we adopt three different target data
\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}
as learning data sets to obtain three independent ResNet networks. We then evaluate the performance of the three networks separately. This study involves the target
\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}
computed from the forward Euler method (
3.2
), Runge-Kutta2 method (
3.3
) and Runge-Kutta4 method (
3.4
) with mesh size
\Delta
. We measure the following mean
L_{2}
error between the target and the reference solution
\textbf{z}_{i}^{2}

\mbox{Target Mean}(L_{2})\,\mbox{error}=\frac{1}{J}\sum_{i=1}^{J}\|\textbf{y}_{i}^{2}-\textbf{z}_{i}^{2}\|_{2}.

(3.14)

For each target generated from one of the three finite difference methods, we also calculate the mean
L_{2}
error of (
3.13
) between the ResNet output and the reference solution after every iteration. Numerical tests show that the ResNet output error curve converges to the target error as the number of iterations increases. In a word, the ResNet solver error
\|\textbf{p}_{j}^{out}-\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)\|_{2}
is dominated by its target error
\|\textbf{y}_{j}^{2}-\textbf{x}_{j}(t_{0}+\Delta)\|_{2}
. Neural network can successfully learn and replicate the three finite difference methods.

Finally in the solution trajectory simulation, we call the ResNet solvers repeatedly to generate a discrete set of points approximating the continuous reference solution curve as a standard one step finite difference method.
All three ResNet solvers trained from forward Euler method, Runge-Kutta2 method and Runge-Kutta4 method are applied and compared to the reference solution to further check the accuracy and capability of the neural network solvers.

Throughout all numerical examples, constant time lag of
\Delta=0.1
or smaller, due to stability restriction of the finite difference methods, is applied. For the learning data set, a total number of
J=500
data pairs are applied to the linear ODE systems (Example 1) and a total number of
J=2000
data pairs are applied to nonlinear ODE systems (all other examples).

5
Conclusions

In this article, we consider the integral or weak formulation of ordinary differential equations and apply residual neural networks solving the ODEs. Specifically we investigate the optimal choice of hidden layers and neurons per layer as the ResNet architecture study. We also investigate the accuracy of ResNet solvers approximating the ODE solutions. Numerical tests show the accuracy of ResNet solver is dominated by the quality of the training target. Sequence of numerical examples verify the ResNet solver can be as accurate as any high order one step method, even the ResNet is implemented similarly to the first order forward Euler scheme.
