Hodge operators and groups of isometries of diagonalizable symmetric bilinear forms in characteristic two
Linus Kramer, Markus J. Stroppel

Abstract
We study groups of isometries on non-alternating symmetric bilinear forms on vector spaces of characteristic two, and actions of these groups on exterior powers of the space, viewed as modules over algebras generated by Hodge operators.

Introduction

In
[
9
]
, we have introduced Hodge operators using
diagonalizable
\sigma
-hermitian forms on vector spaces over a
field
{\boldsymbol{F}}
of arbitrary characteristic. If the dimension of that space
is an even number
n=2\ell
then these operators help to understand
exceptional homomorphisms between groups of semi-similitudes; these
homomorphisms can be interpreted as representations of the group of
semi-similitudes of the given form on the
\ell
-th exterior power of
the space, where the latter is turned into a module over an
algebra
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
generated by the Hodge operator
(see
[
9
, Sect. 2]
). That algebra turns out to be a
composition algebra if
\sigma\neq\mathrm{id}
or if
\operatorname{char}{\boldsymbol{F}}\neq 2
, but it will
be inseparable if
\sigma=\mathrm{id}
and
\operatorname{char}{\boldsymbol{F}}=2
: in that case, we
obtain
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}\cong{\boldsymbol{F}}[X]/(X^{2}-\delta)
, and the group of
{\boldsymbol{F}}
-linear
automorphisms is trivial.

In the case where
\sigma=\mathrm{id}
and the characteristic is two, the
forms in question are not the ones that lead to classical groups: we
then use a bilinear form that is symmetric but not alternating
(see
1.2
below); the definitions of classical
groups (i.e., symplectic, unitary, or orthogonal groups) in
characteristic two employ non-degenerate forms that are alternating,

\sigma
-hermitian with
\sigma\neq\mathrm{id}
, or quadratic forms.

Therefore, the inseparable case is treated in a cursory way only
in
[
9
]
. However, it leads to phenomena that appear
to be interesting, if only as a marked contrast to the results
in
[
9
]
. We treat this case in a more detailed manner
in the present notes, with a focus on
\ell=2
(and
n=4
) because
interesting phenomena are already apparent in this dimension (and the
Klein quadric provides some extra geometric intuition).

The Hodge operator in characteristic two

Fundamental definitions and results about Hodge operators have been

1.1 Notation
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{F}}
be any field of characteristic
2
, let
V
be a vector
space of dimension
n
over
{\boldsymbol{F}}
, and let
h\colon V\times V\to{\boldsymbol{F}}

be a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form. Moreover, assume that
there exists an orthogonal basis
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
with respect
to
h
(see
1.2
below).

As
\dim{V}
is assumed to be finite, our assumption that
h
be not
degenerate is equivalent to the fact that (by a slight abuse of
notation) we may view
h
as a
\sigma
-linear isomorphism onto the
dual space
V^{\vee}
,

h\colon V\to V^{\vee}\colon v\mapsto h(v,-)\,

see e.g.
[
8
, Ch. I, §2]
. Consider now the exterior
algebra
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\,}}{V}
, cf.
[
7
, VI 9]
. We note
that
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\,}}{}
is a functor on vector spaces and (semi)linear maps,
cp.
[
9
, 1.6]
. Moreover, there is a natural isomorphism

\textstyle({\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\,}}{V})^{\vee}\cong{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\,}}(V^{\vee})\,,
so we may write unambiguously
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\,}}{V}^{\vee}
. Explicitly, we have

\langle f_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge f_{\ell},w_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge w_{\ell}\rangle=\det(\langle f_{i},w_{k}\rangle)
for
f_{i}\in V^{\vee}
and
w_{k}\in V
, see
[
10
, I.5.6]

or
1

[

[
2
, § 8, Thme. 1, p. 102]
. Applying the functor
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\,}}
to
h\colon V\to V^{\vee}
, we obtain

{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\,}}{h}\colon{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\,}}{V}\to{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\,}}{V^{\vee}}
; we interpret this as a
bilinear form
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\,}}{h}
on the exterior algebra

\textstyle{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\,}}{h}(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{\ell},w_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge w_{\ell})={\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}{h}(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{\ell},w_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge w_{\ell})=\det(h(v_{i},w_{j})).

In particular, the form
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}h
is symmetric because
transposition does not change the determinant.

1.2 Lemma
.

Let
h\colon V\times V\to{\boldsymbol{F}}
be a non-zero symmetric bilinear form
on a vector space
V
of finite dimension over a field
{\boldsymbol{F}}
with

\operatorname{char}{\boldsymbol{F}}=2
. Then there exists an orthonormal basis for
V
with
respect to
h
if, and only if, the form
h
is not alternating
(i.e., if there exists
v\in V
with
h(v,v)\neq 0
).

Proof.

If there exists an orthonormal basis then the Gram matrix with
respect to that basis is diagonal, and will be zero if the form is
alternating. Conversely, assume that there exists
v\in V
with
h(v,v)\neq 0
. If
V^{\perp}\neq\{0\}
, we choose any basis for
V^{\perp}
, and any
vector space complement
W
to
V^{\perp}
with
v\in W
. The
restriction of
h
to
W
is not degenerate, and not alternating. It
suffices to show that there is an orthogonal basis for
W
. Assume
that
w_{1},\dots,w_{k}
are pairwise orthogonal vectors in
W
with

h(w_{i},w_{i})\neq 0
. Then these vectors are linearly independent, the
restriction of
h
to their span
W_{k}
is not
degenerate, and
W_{k}^{\perp}\cap W
is a complement to
W_{k}
in
W
.

We proceed by induction on
\dim W^{\perp}\cap W
: If the restriction of
h
to
W^{\perp}\cap W
is not alternating, we
apply the induction hypothesis. It remains to study the case
where there exist
x,y\in W^{\perp}\cap W
with
h(x,y)=1
and

h(x,x)=0=h(y,y)
. Put
w_{k+1}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={w_{k}+h(w_{k},w_{k})y}
,

w_{k+2}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=w_{k}+h(w_{k},w_{k})x+h(w_{k},w_{k})y
, and

\tilde{w}_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=w_{k}+x
. Straightforward computation yields

h(w_{k+1},w_{k+1})=h(w_{k+2},w_{k+2})=h(\tilde{w}_{k},\tilde{w}_{k})=h(w_{k},w_{k})\neq 0
, and that the vectors

\tilde{w}_{k},w_{k+1},w_{k+2}\in\{w_{1},\dots,w_{k-1}\}^{\perp}
are
pairwise orthogonal.

So

w_{1},\dots,w_{k-1},\tilde{w}_{k},w_{k+1},w_{k+2}
is an orthogonal
basis for
W+{\boldsymbol{F}}x+{\boldsymbol{F}}y
. Applying the induction hypothesis to

(W+{\boldsymbol{F}}x+{\boldsymbol{F}}y)^{\perp}\cap W
finishes the proof.
∎

1.3 The Pfaffian form
.

Recall that
\dim{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!n}}V=1
if
\dim V=n
. We fix an isomorphism

b\colon{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!n}}V\to{\boldsymbol{F}}
. For each positive integer
\ell\leq n
the
map
b
induces an isomorphism

\mathrm{Pf}\colon{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!n-\ell}}V\to{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}V^{\vee}
given by

\textstyle\mathrm{Pf}(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n-\ell})(w_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge w_{\ell})=b(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n-\ell}\wedge w_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge w_{\ell})\,.

This is the
Pfaffian form
, see
[
7
, VI 10 Problems 23–28,
VIII 12 Problem 42]
. As
\operatorname{char}{\boldsymbol{F}}=2
, the resulting bilinear map
\mathrm{Pf}

on
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\,}}{V}
is symmetric,

\textstyle\mathrm{Pf}(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n-\ell},w_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge w_{\ell})=\mathrm{Pf}(w_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge w_{\ell},v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n-\ell})\,.

If
n=2\ell
then

\mathrm{Pf}({v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{\ell}},{v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{\ell}})=0
holds for
{v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{\ell}}
.

1.4 Remark
.

For
n=4
and
\ell=2
we are dealing with the space
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!2}}{{\boldsymbol{F}}^{4}}

that carries the Klein quadric. The
quadratic form

\mathrm{Pq}

defining the Klein quadric is also referred to as a Pfaffian form
(cf.
[
6
]
and
[
13
]
where this form is
denoted by
q
), and
\mathrm{Pf}
is the polar form of that quadratic
form. Under the present assumption
\operatorname{char}{\boldsymbol{F}}=2
, the polar form
\mathrm{Pf}

carries less information than the quadratic form
\mathrm{Pq}
. Note that

\mathrm{Pf}
is alternating because it is the polar form of a quadratic
form in characteristic two.

If one interprets the elements of
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!2}}{{\boldsymbol{F}}^{4}}
as alternating
matrices then there exists a scalar
s\in{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\vphantom{x}\smash{\times}}
such that

\mathrm{Pq}(X)^{2}=s\,\det{X}
holds for each
X\in{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!2}}{{\boldsymbol{F}}^{4}}
,
cf.
[
3
, § 5 no. 2, Prop. 2, p. 84]
; the scalar
s

reflects the choice of basis underlying that interpretation.
See
[
14
, 12.14]
for an interpretation of
\mathrm{Pq}
in
terms of the exterior algebra.

1.6 Explicit computation
.

Suppose that
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
is an orthogonal basis of
V
. For
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}{V}
we use the basis vectors

v_{i_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{i_{\ell}}
with ascending

i_{1}<\dots<i_{\ell}\leq n
. Then
{{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}{h}(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{\ell},-)}
is a linear form on
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}{V}
which annihilates each one of
those basis vectors, except for
v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{\ell}
; in fact

\textstyle{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}{h}(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{\ell},v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{\ell})=h(v_{1},v_{1})\cdots h(v_{\ell},v_{\ell})\,.

In other words:
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}{h}
is again diagonalizable. It then also follows that
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}h
is not degenerate. The linear form
\mathrm{Pf}(v_{\ell+1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n})
annihilates
the same collection of basis
\ell
-vectors, and

\mathrm{Pf}(v_{\ell+1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n},v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{\ell})=b(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n})\,.

Therefore

\displaystyle J(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{\ell})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v_{\ell+1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n}\,\frac{h(v_{1},v_{1})\cdots h(v_{\ell},v_{\ell})}{b(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n})}\,.

Note that this last formula is correct only if
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
is an
orthogonal basis, and cannot be used if
v_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{\ell}

corresponds to a subspace
U
of
V
such that
h|_{U\times U}
is
degenerate.

1.7 The square of the Hodge operator
.

Let
H
be the Gram matrix of
h
with respect to the orthogonal
basis
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
. The square of
J
is a linear automorphism
of
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}V
, and we find that

\textstyle J^{2}=\delta_{\ell}\,\mathrm{id}\quad\text{ where }\quad\delta_{\ell}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\det(H)}{b(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n})^{2}}\,.

Recall that
\det(H)
depends on the choice of basis; the invariant
would be the square class
\operatorname{disc}(h)\in{{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\vphantom{x}\smash{\times}}/{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\mathchoice{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\hline\cr\cr\vrule\vphantom{x}\smash{\lower 0.21pt\hbox{$\kern-1.7pt\displaystyle{{\vphantom{x}\smash{\times}}}\kern-1.7pt$}}\vrule\crcr\cr\hline\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\hline\cr\cr\vrule\vphantom{x}\smash{\lower 0.25pt\hbox{$\kern-1.7pt\textstyle{{\vphantom{x}\smash{\times}}}\kern-1.7pt$}}\vrule\crcr\cr\hline\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\hline\cr\cr\vrule$\scriptstyle\vphantom{x}$\smash{\lower 0.25pt\hbox{$\kern-1.7pt\scriptstyle{{\vphantom{x}\smash{\times}}}\kern-1.6pt$}}\vrule\crcr\cr\hline\cr}}}{\vbox{\halign{#\cr\hline\cr\cr\vrule$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{x}$\smash{\lower 0.18pt\hbox{$\kern-1.6pt\scriptscriptstyle{{\vphantom{x}\smash{\times}}}\kern-1.5pt$}}\vrule\crcr\cr\hline\cr}}}}}
of

\det(h(v_{i},v_{j}))
. However, the whole expression depends only on
h
and
b
. Replacing
the isomorphism

changes

by a factor
and

by the square of that factor. In particular, the isomorphism

does not depend on the choice of

.

1.8 Lemma
.

For all
x,y\in{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}V
we have

a.

\mathrm{Pf}(J(x),y)={\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}{h}(x,y)
,

b.

\mathrm{Pf}(J(x),J(y))=\delta_{\ell}\,{\mathrm{Pf}(y,x)}
,

c.

{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}{h}(J(x),y)=\delta_{\ell}\,{\mathrm{Pf}(x,y)}
,

d.

{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}{h}(J(x),J(y))=\delta_{\ell}\,{{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}{h}(x,y)}
.

From now on, assume
n=2\ell
. Then
J
is an
{\boldsymbol{F}}
-linear endomorphism of
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}V
. We are going to use
J
to give
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}{V}
the structure of a
right module over an associative algebra of dimension
2
over
{\boldsymbol{F}}
.

1.9 The algebra
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
.

Take
\delta_{\ell}=\frac{\det(H)}{b(v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{n})^{2}}

as in
1.7
and let
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
denote the
{\boldsymbol{F}}
-algebra consisting of all matrices of the form

\textstyle x=\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}&\delta_{\ell}\,{x_{1}}\\
x_{1}&{x_{0}}\end{pmatrix}\in{\boldsymbol{F}}^{2\times 2}.

We identify
x_{0}\in{\boldsymbol{F}}
with the diagonal matrix

\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}&0\\
0&{x_{0}}\end{pmatrix}
and put

\boldsymbol{j}_{\ell}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\delta_{\ell}\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
. Thus

\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}&\delta_{\ell}\,{x_{1}}\\
x_{1}&{x_{0}}\end{pmatrix}=x_{0}+\boldsymbol{j}_{\ell}\,x_{1}\,.

Note that
\boldsymbol{j}_{\ell}^{2}=\delta_{\ell}
.

1.10 Definition
.

For
v\in{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}V
we put
v\,\boldsymbol{j}_{\ell}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=J(v)
.
In this way, the space
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}V
becomes an

\mathrm{O}{(V,h)}
-
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
-bimodule, i.e., it becomes a right module
over
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
and
\mathrm{O}{(V,h)}
acts
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
-linearly from the
left. Choosing an orthogonal basis
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
for
V
with a fixed
ordering, we obtain a basis
B
for
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}V
consisting of all

v_{j_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge v_{j_{\ell}}
where
(j_{1},\dots,j_{\ell})
is
an increasing sequence of length
\ell
in
\{1,\dots,n\}
. The
sequences with
j_{1}=1
form a subset
B_{1}
of
B
, and
J
maps each
element of
B_{1}
to one of
B\smallsetminus B_{1}
. Moreover, the
set
B_{1}
forms a basis for the
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
-module
{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}V
, showing that
the latter is a free module.

1.11 The bilinear form on the module
.

We define
g\colon{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}{V}\times{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}{V}\to{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
by

\textstyle g(u,v)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}{h}(u,v)+{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}{h}(u,v\boldsymbol{j}_{\ell})\,\boldsymbol{j}_{\ell}^{-1}={\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}{h}(u,v)+\boldsymbol{j}_{\ell}\,(-1)^{\ell}\,\mathrm{Pf}(u,v)\,;

see
1.8
for the description on the right hand
side. This expression is
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
-bilinear.

Note that
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
is not a field, in general: we need the more
general concept of bilinear forms over rings.

.

The form
g
is diagonalizable.

For a general proof (and a general formula)
see
[
9
, 2.7]
. Actually, if
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
is a field (of
characteristic two, and
\sigma=\mathrm{id}
by our assumptions) it suffices
to note that

is not alternating, see

. For the applications in Section

below, we give
a special statement explicitly:

1.13 Example

Let
v_{1},v_{2},v_{3},v_{4}
be an orthogonal basis for

, with respect

. Then
w_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=v_{1}\wedge v_{2}

From the definition of

1.14 Lemma
.

The kernel of
\eta_{\ell}
is trivial.

Proof.

That kernel consists of all scalar multiples
s\,\mathrm{id}
of the identity where
s^{2}=1
. As
\operatorname{char}{\boldsymbol{F}}=2
, this yields
s=1
.
∎

We will call
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}

split
whenever it contains divisors of
zero. This extends the established terminology for composition
algebras. Recall that
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
is split precisely if
\delta_{\ell}
is
a square:
\delta_{\ell}=s^{2}
for some
s\in{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\vphantom{x}\smash{\times}}
(and this happens precisely if
h
has
discriminant
1
).

In that case, we may assume
s=1
without loss of generality. In
fact, if we replace our isomorphism
b\colon{\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!n}}V\to{\boldsymbol{F}}
by
sb

then the Hodge operator
J
is replaced by

J\,s^{-1}\colon X\mapsto J(X)s^{-1}
, and we have
(J\,s^{-1})^{2}=\mathrm{id}

while the algebra
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
remains the same. If
s=1
then
z\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=1+\boldsymbol{j}_{\ell}\in{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
satisfies

z^{2}=2z=0
, and is nilpotent. Thus
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}\cong{\boldsymbol{F}}[X]/(X^{2})
is a local ring if
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
is
split.

1.15 Lemma
.

Let
W\mathrel{\mathop{:}}={\textstyle\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!\ell}}{V}
, and assume that
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
is
split.

a.

The maximal ideal in
{\boldsymbol{K}}_{\ell}
is generated by a nilpotent
element
