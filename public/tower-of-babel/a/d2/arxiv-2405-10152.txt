Braids, Twists, Trace and Duality in Combinatory Algebras
Masahito Hasegawa, Serge Lechenne

Abstract
. We investigate a class of combinatory algebras, called ribbon combinatory algebras , in which we can interpret both the braided untyped linear lambda calculus and framed oriented tangles. Any reflexive object in a ribbon category gives rise to a ribbon combinatory algebra. Conversely, From a ribbon combinatory algebra, we can construct a ribbon category with a reflexive object, from which the combinatory algebra can be recovered. To show this, and also to give the equational characterisation of ribbon combinatory algebras, we make use of the internal PRO construction developed in Hasegawa’s recent work. Interestingly, we can characterise ribbon combinatory algebras in two different ways: as balanced combinatory algebras with a trace combinator, and as balanced combinatory algebras with duality.

PROs

A
PRO
, meaning a "PROduct category", is a strict monoidal category generated by
a single object. We identify objects of a PRO with natural numbers
\mathbb{N}
; the tensor product of objects
m
and
n

is the addition
m+n
, while the tensor unit
I
is
0
. Thus a PRO
\mathbb{C}
can be defined
as an
\mathbb{N}^{2}
-indexed family
\mathbb{C}(m,n)
with the following constructs

•

the identity
\mathit{id}_{n}\in\mathbb{C}(n,n)

•

the composition
f;g\in\mathbb{C}(l,n)
for
f\in\mathbb{C}(l,m)
,
g\in\mathbb{C}(m,n)

•

the tensor
f_{1}+f_{2}\in\mathbb{C}(m_{1}+m_{2},n_{1}+n_{2})
for
f_{i}\in\mathbb{C}(m_{i},n_{i})

which are subject to the following axioms.

\begin{array}[]{lr}\lx@intercol\mathit{id}_{m};f=f=f;\mathit{id}_{n}\hskip 5.69054pt(f\in\mathbb{C}(m,n))\hfil\lx@intercol\\
\lx@intercol(f;g);h=f;(g;h)\hskip 5.69054pt(f\in\mathbb{C}(l,m),~g\in\mathbb{C}(m,n),~h\in\mathbb{C}(n,p))\hfil\lx@intercol\\
f+\mathit{id}_{0}=f=\mathit{id}_{0}+f\\
(f_{1}+f_{2})+f_{3}=f_{1}+(f_{2}+f_{3})\\
\mathit{id}_{m}+\mathit{id}_{n}=\mathit{id}_{m+n}\\
(f_{1}+f_{2});(g_{1}+g_{2})=(f_{1};g_{1})+(f_{2};g_{2})&(f_{i}\in\mathbb{C}(l_{i},m_{i}),~g_{i}\in\mathbb{C}(m_{i},n_{i}))\end{array}

From these axioms, we have the
interchange law

(f+\mathit{id}_{m^{\prime}});(\mathit{id}_{n}+g)=f+g=(\mathit{id}_{m}+g);(f+\mathit{id}_{n^{\prime}})

for
f\in\mathbb{C}(m,n)
and
g\in\mathbf{C}(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})
.
Indeed, instead of
f+g
for arrows
f
and
g
, we may use
f+m

(=f+\mathit{id}_{m})
and
m+f

(=\mathit{id}_{m}+f)
as primitive constructs,
with slightly simplified axioms
f+0=f=0+f
,
(m+f)+m^{\prime}=m+(f+m^{\prime})
,
(m+m^{\prime})+f=m+(m^{\prime}+f)
,
(f+m)+m^{\prime}=f+(m+m^{\prime})

and the interchange law

(f+m^{\prime});(n+g)=(m+g);(f+n^{\prime})

for defining PROs.

f+g
is then defined either as
(f+m^{\prime});(n+g)
or
(m+g);(f+n^{\prime})
.
This alternative characterisation will be useful in developing PROs in combinatory algebras.

A

(

(
Hasegawa, 2012
;
Turaev, 1994
;
Yetter, 2001
)
.
Compact closed categories are ribbon categories whose braid is symmetric and whose twist is the identity.

2.4.
Closed structure and reflexive objects

We say a monoidal category
\mathbb{C}
is
closed
when the functor
(\_)\otimes A\colon\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
has a right adjoint

A\multimap(\_)\colon\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, hence
\mathbb{C}(X\otimes A,B)\cong\mathbb{C}(X,A\multimap B)
.
In particular, left autonomous categories and ribbon categories are closed with
A\multimap B=B\otimes A^{*}
.

In a
monoidal category
\mathbb{C}

(which does not have to be closed), we say an object
A
is
(strictly) reflexive
when the
functor
\mathbb{C}(\_\otimes A,A)
is represented by
A
itself, thus there exists a natural isomorphism

\mathsf{cur}\colon\mathbb{C}(\_\otimes A,A)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cong}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathbb{C}(\_,A)
. When
\mathbb{C}
is closed,

A
is a reflexive object if and only if
A
is isomorphic to
A\multimap A
.
Using a reflexive object, we can model the abstraction and application in the untyped lambda calculus as follows.
For
f\colon X\otimes A\rightarrow A
, its currying
\mathsf{cur}(f)\colon X\rightarrow A
amounts to the lambda abstraction. For the application,
we have
\mathsf{app}=\mathsf{cur}^{-1}(\mathit{id}_{A})\colon A\otimes A\rightarrow A
. Then the
\beta
-rule
(\mathsf{cur}(f)\otimes X);\mathsf{app}=f

holds for

3.1.
Combinatory algebras

Let us recall some basics on applicative structures and combinatory algebras

(
Barendregt, 1985
;
Hindley and Seldin, 2008
;
Selinger, 2002
)
which will be used in the rest of this paper.
A
applicative structure

(\mathcal{A},\cdot)
is a set
\mathcal{A}
equipped with
a binary map
(\_)\cdot(\_):\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
called
application
.
As is customary, applications are assumed to be left associative, and the infix
\cdot
is often omitted.
Thus

(a\cdot b)\cdot c
while
a\,(b\,c)
is
a\cdot(b\cdot c)
.
In the literature, a
combinatory algebra
often refers to an
\mathbf{SK}
-algebra
, which is an applicative structure
equipped with elements
\mathit{\boldsymbol{S}}
and
\mathit{\boldsymbol{K}}
satisfying
\mathit{\boldsymbol{S}}\,a\,b\,c=(a\,c)\,(b\,c)
and
\mathit{\boldsymbol{K}}\,a\,b=a
.
However, combinatory algebras can also be characterised more conceptually by the
combinatory completeness

(
Curry, 1930a
;
Curry, 1930b
)
.
For an applicative structure
\mathcal{A}
, let
\mathcal{A}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be the set of expressions (polynomials)
generated by elements of
\mathcal{A}

and indeterminates
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
and application
\cdot
.
We say
\mathcal{A}
is combinatory complete if, for any

, there exists

\lambda^{*}x.p\in\mathcal{A}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
such that
(\lambda^{*}x.p)\cdot x=p
holds; and then a combinatory algebra can be defined
as a combinatory complete applicative structure. It is well-known that these two definitions of combinatory algebras coincide:
combinatory completeness implies existence of
\mathit{\boldsymbol{S}}
and
\mathit{\boldsymbol{K}}
, while it is standard to define
\lambda^{*}x.p
using
\mathit{\boldsymbol{S}}
and
\mathit{\boldsymbol{K}}

(
Barendregt, 1985
;
Hindley and Seldin, 2008
)
.

In this paper, we will deal with more general classes of combinatory algebras than those recalled above.
By focusing on combinatory completeness concerning
linear
polynomials (in which each indeterminate is used exactly once),
we have the linear variant of combinatory algebras, which are
\mathbf{BCI}
-algebras
with elements
\mathit{\boldsymbol{B}}
,
\mathit{\boldsymbol{C}}
and
\mathit{\boldsymbol{I}}

satisfying
\mathit{\boldsymbol{B}}\,a\,b\,c=a\,(b\,c)

\mathit{\boldsymbol{C}}\,a\,b\,c=a\,c\,b
and
\mathit{\boldsymbol{I}}\,a=a
. Similarly, by further restricting our attention to

planar
polynomials (in which each indeterminate is used exactly once in the prescribed order),
we have a planar variant of combinatory algebras, which will be recalled below.
Hasegawa
(
Hasegawa, 2023a
)
argues that the notion of operads (one-object multicategories) gives a uniform foundation handling
these variations and more (the braided case in particular).

4.
Ribbon categories with a reflexive object

Starting with combinatory algebras which give rise to reflexive PROs, we have enriched our algebras with braids
and twists.
The next step is to add trace and/or duality. But before doing that, it is useful to look at the situation from the categorical side.
Specifically, we consider ribbon categories with a reflexive object and figure out what the combinatory algebra arising from the
reflexive object looks like. Most interestingly, we will see that these combinatory algebras have an element whose application
sends elements of arity

to its (left) trace of arity

8.
Summary and future work

Summary

We have seen that a reflexive object in a ribbon category gives rise to
a ribbon combinatory algebra,
whose internal PROB is a ribbon category which is
almost
equivalent to
the appropriate subcategory of the original ribbon category.

This situation can be summarised as follows:
ribbon
combinatory
algebras
reflexive
ribbon
PROBs
take the internal PROB
[formula]
take the elements of
[formula]
[formula]

where a reflexive ribbon PROB means a PROB
\mathbb{C}

which is a ribbon category and
1
is reflexive (
\mathbb{C}((\_)+1,1)\cong\mathbb{C}(-,1)
).

One might expect that this would form an equivalence of categories. However,
while
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{A}}(0,1)=\mathcal{A}^{\bullet}\cong\mathcal{A}
holds for
a ribbon combinatory algebra
\mathcal{A}
, there is a nasty gap
between
\mathbb{C}
and
\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{C}(0,1)}
for a ribbon PROB
\mathbb{C}
as the functor

F\colon\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{C}(0,1)}
does not have to give an equivalence. Nevertheless,
under a mild condition
\bigcirc=\mathit{id}_{I}
,
F
indeed gives an equivalence
(Theorem
6.10
).

We have given an axiomatisation of ribbon combinatory algebras in terms of the trace combinator
\mathit{\boldsymbol{T\!r}}
, and shown that it is equivalent to
another axiomatisation using the combinators
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
for the duality.
Thus, in the untyped (reflexive) extensional setting, to give a trace combinator is
to give a ribbon structure.

Future work

We have studied combinatory algebras whose internal
PROB (PROP) is ribbon (compact closed when symmetric).
A related question is
to characterise combinatory algebras whose internal PROB (PROP) is
traced

(but not necessarily ribbon or compact closed).
For example, let
\mathbb{D}
be a cartesian closed category with
a Conway fixed-point operator and a reflexive object
D
— there are plenty of such cases found in domain theory, and a Conway operator
determines a trace
(
Hasegawa, 1999
;
Hasegawa, 2009
)
.
The global elements of
D

form an extensional
\mathbf{BCI}
-algebra (in fact an extensional
\mathbf{SK}
-algebra). Its internal PROP is a cartesian category with a Conway operator, hence traced; but it is not compact closed.

Another important question is to give finer examples of ribbon combinatory algebras. The crossed
G
-set models
(Example
5.7
) give ribbon combinatory algebras with non-trivial braids and twists, but they do not provide
interesting invariants of tangles. We expect to obtain better examples by applying techniques from knot theory.
For instance, the recent work relating knots and links to Thompson’s group
F

(
Jones, 2017
;
Jones, 2019
)
might be relevant, as
F
is the group of automorphisms on an object
A
with an isomorphism

A\otimes A\cong A

(
Fiore and Leinster, 2010
)
.
