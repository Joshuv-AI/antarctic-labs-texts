Generalized Monge-Ampère functionals and related variational problems
Freid Tong, Shing-Tung Yau

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce a family of real Monge-Ampère functionals and study their variational properties. We prove a Sobolev type inequality for these functionals and use this to study the existence and uniqueness of some associated Dirichlet problems. In particular, we prove the existence of solutions for a nonlinear eigenvalue problem associated to this family of functionals.



1
Introduction

The Monge-Ampère equation is one of the most important nonlinear PDEs in geometry. It arises very naturally in geometric problems in affine geometry
[
4
,
8
,
9
]
, the classical Minkowski problem
[
22
,
20
,
6
]
, and is also related to Kähler geometry via the complex Monge-Ampère equation
[
26
,
8
]
. The following Dirichlet problem for Monge-Ampère equation is a subject that has been widely studied.

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\det D^{2}u=F(x,u,\nabla u)&\text{ in }\Omega\\
u=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Here we assume the function
F(x,u,\nabla u)
is positive, and we would like to obtain convex solutions
u
. The equation (
1.1
) was first studied by Pogorelov
[
21
]
. Cheng and the second author solved (
1.1
) when
F
is independent of
\nabla u
and the sign of
F_{u}
is positive
[
7
]
, in that case the solutions are unique by the maximum principle. In that work,
F
is allowed to become singular near the boundary, and the solutions are obtained in
C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C(\overline{\Omega})
. Under further regularity conditions for
F
, Caffarelli-Nirenberg-Spruck
[
3
]
, and Krylov
[
16
]
independently obtained boundary estimates of all order for (
1.1
), and hence was able to obtain the smoothness of solutions up to the boundary.

When the sign of
F_{u}
is not necessarily positive, Caffarelli, Nirenberg and Spruck
[
3
]
solved the Dirichlet problem (
1.1
) under the assumption of the existence of a
subsolution
. Although their result is very natural from the PDE perspective, often constructing such a subsolution is a difficulty task, one that is almost as difficult as constructing a solution itself. A different approach without constructing a subsolution was taken by Tso
[
24
]
, he used a variational approach to study such problems using the Monge-Ampère functional introduced by Bakelman
[
1
,
2
]
. In particular, Tso studied the family of Dirichlet problems

(1.2)

\begin{cases}\det D^{2}u=(-u)^{p}&\text{ in }\Omega\\
u=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

and he proved the existence and uniqueness of solutions when
p<n
(subcritical case), and the existence of nontrivial solutions when
p>n
(supercritical case). The case
p=n
is the Monge-Ampère eigenvalue problem and has been studied in earlier work of Lions
[
19
]
. The variational approach turns out to be very natural and has been extended to the study of more general Hessian equations in
[
25
,
10
]
.

One feature of using the classical Monge-Ampère functional is that it works well when
F
depends on
u
and not on its gradient
\nabla u
. However, for more general
F
that depends on both
u
and
\nabla u
, one does not always expect to have a variational formulation. In this paper, we study the solvability of a class of Dirichlet problems whose right-hand side depend on
u
and on its gradient
\nabla u
in terms of its Legendre tranform
u^{\star}=\langle x,\nabla u\rangle-u
. Such equations arises naturally from the study of affine spheres
[
5
,
15
]
, and similar equations also arises in the recent work of Collins-Li
[
11
]
on a generalization of a construction of Tian-Yau for complete Calabi-Yau metrics. We discover a variational structure for this class of equations, and use this to undertake a variational study of such equations. In particular, we define a family of
generalized Monge-Ampère functionals

H_{n+k}
for any
k\in\mathbb{R}
, whose variations are given by

\delta H_{n+k}(u)[\phi]=-\int_{\Omega}\phi(u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u.

The functional themselves are of independent interest, and one of them in particular suggests a close relationship to the study of convex geometry (see Theorem
2.1
).

An important ingredient in our variational approach is a
Sobolev type inequality
(Theorem
2.2
) for
H_{n+k}
, which says that the functionals
H_{n+k}
bound the
L^{n+k+1}
norm of a convex function. The complex analogue of such Sobolev type inequalities and the limiting Trudinger type inequalities are of great importance in the study of complex Monge-Ampere equations, and sharp forms of the limiting Trudinger type inequalities for the complex Monge-Ampere equation have been established and used by Guo, Phong and the first author to prove sharp estimates for complex Monge-Ampere equations on compact Kähler manifolds
[
14
]
. In this paper, we will use the Sobolev inequality for
H_{n+k}
to study the solvability of the family of Dirichlet problems

(1.3)

\begin{cases}\det D^{2}u=\lambda(u^{\star})^{-k}(-u)^{p}&\text{ in }\Omega\\
u=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

The cases
p<n+k
(subcritical), and
p>n+k
(supercritical) are treated seperately, and the case
p=n+k
is treated as an eigenvalue problem. Our main theorem is the following

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
n+k>0
and consider the Dirichlet problem (
1.3
). Then

1.

If
0<p<n+k
, then for any
\lambda>0
, (
1.3
) admits a unique nontrivial solution
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

2.

If
p=n+k
, then there exists a unique
\lambda
for which (
1.3
) admits a non-trivial solution
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Moreover, the solution is unique up to scaling by a positive constant.

3.

If
p>n+k
, then for any
\lambda>0
, (
1.3
) admits a nontrivial solution
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we will define the functionals
H_{n+k}
and establish their basic properties. In Section
3
, we study a parabolic gradient flow of functionals associated with
H_{n+k}
and establish some basic apriori estimates for these equations. In section
4
, we will use the parabolic gradient flow to prove convergence results and establish existence and uniqueness for the associated Dirichlet problems. In particular, we will prove Theorem
1.1
. Finally in section
5
, we discuss a transformation which transforms the solution of the Dirichlet problem to a solution of an optimal transport problem and discuss some relations to other works.

2
The generalized Monge-Ampère functionals

Let us fix
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
to be an open, bounded, smooth, and strictly convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and we will always assume the origin is contained in
\Omega
. Let
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
be a strictly convex function on
\overline{\Omega}
which vanishes on the boundary, and we denote
\mathcal{C}_{0}
to be the space of all such functions,

\mathcal{C}_{0}=\{u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}):D^{2}u>0,u|_{\partial\Omega}=0\}.

For
u\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
, we denote
u^{\star}
to be the function

u^{\star}(x):=\langle x,\nabla u(x)\rangle-u(x),

which is the legendre transform of
u
evaluated at the point
\nabla u(x)
. We note that
u^{\star}
is nonnegative since
\inf_{x\in\Omega}u^{\star}(x)=u^{\star}(0)=-u(0)\geq 0
, and
u^{\star}
is strictly positive unless
u\equiv 0
.

For any number
k\in\mathbb{R}
, we can define a
generalized Monge-Ampère functional

H_{n+k}
by

(2.1)

H_{n+k}(u):=\frac{1}{n+k+1}\int_{\Omega}(u^{\star})^{k}(-u)\det D^{2}u.

Remark 1
.

Notice that
H_{n+k}(u)
is always positive for
u\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
since
u^{\star}\geq|u(0)|>0
for
u\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
, and it vanishes only when
u
is identically zero.

When
k=0
,
H_{n}
reduces to the classical Monge-Ampère functional first introduced by Bakelman
[
1
,
2
]
, and has been widely studied
[
7
,
24
]
. Other cases of interest include
k=-n-2
, which is related to the equation for an elliptic affine spheres
[
4
,
15
]
, and the case of
k\geq 0
is closely related to a class of equations studied in
[
5
]
, arising from the study of hyperbolic affine spheres. We shall see below that the scale-invariant case of
k=-n-1
will turn out to be very special.

2.1
Variational formulas

We compute the first and second variations of
H_{n+k}
. The first variation of
H_{n+k}
with respect to
\phi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
satisfying
\phi|_{\partial\Omega}=0
is given by

(2.2)

\displaystyle\delta H_{n+k}(u)[\phi]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n+k+1}\int_{\Omega}\phi(u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u+\frac{k}{n+k+1}\int_{\Omega}(\langle x,\nabla\phi\rangle-\phi)(u^{\star})^{k-1}(-u)\det D^{2}u

(2.3)

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{n+k+1}\int_{\Omega}(u^{\star})^{k}(-u)u^{ij}\phi_{ij}\det D^{2}u

(2.4)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n+k+1}\int_{\Omega}\phi(u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u+\frac{k}{n+k+1}\int_{\Omega}(\langle x,\nabla\phi\rangle-\phi)(u^{\star})^{k-1}(-u)\det D^{2}u

(2.5)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{k}{n+k+1}\int_{\Omega}(u^{\star})^{k-1}(-u)\langle x,\nabla\phi\rangle\det D^{2}u+\frac{1}{n+k+1}\int_{\Omega}(u^{\star})^{k}u^{ij}\phi_{i}u_{j}\det D^{2}u

(2.6)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{n+k+1}\int_{\Omega}\phi(u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u-\frac{k}{n+k+1}\int_{\Omega}\phi(u^{\star})^{k-1}(-u)\det D^{2}u

(2.7)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{n}{n+k+1}\int_{\Omega}\phi(u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u-\frac{k}{n+k+1}\int_{\Omega}\phi(u^{\star})^{k-1}\langle x,\nabla u\rangle\det D^{2}u

(2.8)

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\phi(u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u\,dx

The second variation with respect to
\phi,\psi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
, satisfying
\phi|_{\partial\Omega}=\psi|_{\partial\Omega}=0
is given by

(2.9)

\displaystyle\delta^{2}H_{n+k}(u)[\phi,\psi]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}u^{ij}\phi_{i}\psi_{j}(u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u+k\int_{\Omega}\phi\psi(u^{\star})^{k-1}\det D^{2}u.

From this, we can see that if
k\geq 0
, then this norm is always positive definite, hence
H_{n+k}
is convex on
\mathcal{C}_{0}
. Using the functionals
H_{n+k}
, we may define a norm
\|\cdot\|_{H_{n+k}}
on the space
\mathcal{C}_{0}
by setting

(2.10)

\|u\|_{H_{n+k}}:=[H_{n+k}(u)]^{\frac{1}{n+k+1}}

Proposition 2.1
.

If
k\geq 0
, then for
u,v\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
, the triangle inequality holds

\|u+v\|_{H_{n+k}}\leq\|u\|_{H_{n+k}}+\|v\|_{H_{n+k}}.

Proof.

By applying integration by parts to formulas (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), we obtain

H_{n+k}(u)=\frac{1}{(n+k)(n+k+1)}\left(\int_{\Omega}u^{ij}u_{i}u_{j}(u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u+k\int_{\Omega}(-u)^{2}(u^{\star})^{k-1}\det D^{2}u\right)

and

\delta H_{n+k}(u)[\phi]=\frac{1}{n+k}\left(\int_{\Omega}u^{ij}u^{i}\phi_{j}(u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u+k\int_{\Omega}(-\phi)(-u)(u^{\star})^{k-1}\det D^{2}u\right)

and we also have

\delta^{2}H_{n+k}(u)[\phi,\psi]=\int_{\Omega}u^{ij}\phi_{i}\psi_{j}(u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u\,dx+k\int_{\Omega}\phi\psi(u^{\star})^{k-1}\det D^{2}u\,dx.

Therefore by the Cauchy Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\delta^{2}H_{n+k}(u)[v,v]\cdot H_{n+k}(u)\geq\frac{n+k}{n+k+1}\left(\delta H(u)[v]\right)^{2},

from which the triangle inequality follows.
∎

2.2
Sobolev type inequalities

The functional
H_{-1}
(suitably normalized) turns out to be very special. In fact, it turns out to be independent of
u
.

Theorem 2.1
.

For any
u\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
, we have

\int_{\Omega}\frac{-u\det D^{2}u}{(u^{\star})^{n+1}}=|\Omega^{\circ}|,

where
\Omega^{\circ}
the
polar body
of
\Omega
, defined by

\Omega^{\circ}:=\{x:\langle x,y\rangle\leq 1\text{ for all }y\in\Omega\}.

Proof.

By the variational formula from above, we have

\delta\left(\int_{\Omega}\frac{-u\det D^{2}u}{(u^{\star})^{n+1}}\right)\equiv 0

which implies

\int_{\Omega}\frac{-u\det D^{2}u}{(u^{\star})^{n+1}}=const.

It suffices to show that this constant is equal to
|\Omega^{\circ}|
. Let
\hat{u}
be the function

\hat{u}(x)=\inf\left\{\frac{1-t}{t}:t>0,tx\in\Omega\right\},

then it’s easy to check that
\hat{u}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega\setminus\{0\})
and
\det D^{2}\hat{u}=0
on
\Omega\setminus\{0\}
. Let
\phi_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a smooth function satisfying
\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)=x
for
x\in[0,1-2\varepsilon]
,
\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)=1-\varepsilon
in a neighborhood of
1
,
\phi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}>0
and
\phi_{\varepsilon}^{\prime\prime}<0
. Then define
u_{\varepsilon}(x)=-\phi_{\varepsilon}(-\hat{u}(x))+\varepsilon\rho(x)
, where
\rho
is a strictly convex defining function for
\Omega
, then one can check that the following are true,

1.

\|u_{\varepsilon}^{\star}-1\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}=O(\varepsilon)
.

2.

\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)
is decreasing to
\Omega^{\circ}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.

3.

For
y\in\Omega^{\circ}
, let
x_{\varepsilon}\in\Omega
be the point such that
\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon})=y
, then
\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}u_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon})=-1
.

By a change of variables formula, we have

(2.11)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{-u_{\varepsilon}\det D^{2}u_{\varepsilon}}{(u_{\varepsilon}^{\star})^{n+1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)}\frac{-u_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon})}{(u_{\varepsilon}^{\star}(x_{\varepsilon}))^{n+1}}dy

(2.12)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega^{\circ}}\frac{-u_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon})}{(u_{\varepsilon}^{\star}(x_{\varepsilon}))^{n+1}}dy+\int_{\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)\setminus\Omega^{\circ}}\frac{-u_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon})}{(u_{\varepsilon}^{\star}(x_{\varepsilon}))^{n+1}}dy

where
y\in\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)
and
x_{\varepsilon}\in\Omega
are related by
\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon})=y
. The second integral converges to zero as
\varepsilon\to 0
since
\nabla u_{\varepsilon}(\Omega)
converges to
\Omega^{\circ}
. For the first term, note that both
-u_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon})
and
u_{\varepsilon}^{\star}(x_{\varepsilon})
converges to
1
for any
y\in\Omega^{\circ}
, therefore the first integral converges to
|\Omega^{\circ}|
and we’ve proven the claim.
∎

From Theorem
2.1
, we can deduce a Sobolev type inequality for
H_{n+k}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Suppose
n+k+1>0
, then there exist
c>0
such that for any
u\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
, we have

(2.13)

H_{n+k}(u)\geq c\int_{\Omega}|u|^{n+k+1}.

Moreover,
c
depends only on
n,k
and
\Omega
.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.1
and the fact that
u^{\star}(x)\geq|u(0)|
, we obtain the inequality,

(2.14)

H_{n+k}(u)\geq\frac{|\Omega^{\circ}|}{n+k+1}|u(0)|^{n+k+1}.

Since
0\in\Omega
, by the convexity of
u
, we have
|u(0)|\geq\frac{d(0,\partial\Omega)}{diam(\Omega))}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}
, and
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{n+k+1}\geq c\int_{\Omega}|u|^{n+k+1}
. Together with (
2.14
), this proves the claim.
∎

Using this, we can define an invariant of
\Omega
to be the maximum of all such
c
such that inequality (
2.13
) holds.

(2.15)

\underline{\lambda}_{n+k+1}(\Omega):=\inf_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}\left\{\frac{(n+k+1)H_{n+k}(u)}{\int_{\Omega}|u|^{n+k+1}}\right\}.

In the case
k=0
,
\underline{\lambda}_{n+1}
was studied by Tso
[
24
]
, who showed that it is essentially equal to the Monge-Ampère eigenvalue introduced by Lions
[
19
]
. We will show in section
4
, that each
\underline{\lambda}_{n+k+1}
will correspond to the solution of a nonlinear eigenvalue problem.

Remark 2
.

The discussion above can be generalized to allow for more general dependence on
u^{\star}
. For a positive function
h:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we denote
G(x):=\int_{0}^{x}s^{n}h(s)ds
, then we can consider the functional

H_{h}(u):=\int_{\Omega}G(u^{\star})\frac{(-u)\det D^{2}u}{(u^{\star})^{n+1}}.

Then its variation will be given by

\delta H_{h}(u)[\phi]=-\int_{\Omega}\phi h(u^{\star})\det D^{2}u,

and second variation is

\delta^{2}H_{h}(u)[\phi,\psi]=\int_{\Omega}\phi\psi h^{\prime}(u^{\star})\det D^{2}u+\int_{\Omega}u^{ij}\phi_{i}\psi_{j}h(u^{\star})\det D^{2}u.

Many of the results of this section holds for more general
h
.

3
A parabolic gradient flow

Let
F(x,u):\overline{\Omega}\times\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be a non-negative function. In this section, we will study a gradient flow of the functional

(3.1)

\mathcal{J}(u):=H_{n+k}(u)-\int_{\Omega}\left(\int_{u}^{0}F(x,s)ds\right).

whose variation is

(3.2)

\delta\mathcal{J}(u)[\phi]=-\int_{\Omega}\phi\left((u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u-F(x,u)\right).

First to establish some notation, we denote
Q_{T}:=\Omega\times(0,T]
to be the parabolic cylinder, and
\overline{Q}_{T}=\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T]
is the closure of
Q_{T}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Let
\partial^{\star}\Omega:=\overline{Q}_{T}\setminus Q_{T}
denote the parabolic boundary, and
\Sigma:=\partial\Omega\times(0,T)
be the spacial boundary. We will define the parabolic Hölder norms

\|u\|_{\tilde{C}^{k,\alpha}(\overline{Q}_{T})}:=\sum_{2m+l\leq k}\sup_{\Omega}|D_{t}^{m}D_{x}^{l}u|+\sum_{2m+l=k}\sup_{x\neq y}\frac{|D_{t}^{m}D_{x}^{l}u(x)-D_{t}^{m}D_{x}^{l}u(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}},

and denote
\tilde{C}^{k,\alpha}
to be space of all functions with finite
\tilde{C}^{k,\alpha}
-norm.

Let us assume for the rest of this section that
F(x,u)\geq\eta>0
is strictly positive and denote

g(x,u):=\log F(x,u),

and consider the following parabolic equation with initial condition
u_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
.

(3.3)

\begin{cases}u_{t}-\log\det D^{2}u-k\log(u^{\star})=-g(x,u)&\text{ in }Q_{T}\\
u=u_{0}&\text{ on }\overline{\Omega}\times\{0\}\\
u=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega\times(0,T).\end{cases}

By the first variation formula (
3.2
) of
\mathcal{J}
, we can see that
\mathcal{J}
is non-increasing along this flow.

In order to obtain the existence of solutions to (
3.3
), we will first consider the slightly more general boundary value problem

(3.4)

\begin{cases}u_{t}-\log\det D^{2}u-k\log(u^{\star})=-g(x,u)&\text{ in }Q_{T}\\
u=\Phi&\text{ on }\partial^{\star}Q_{T}\end{cases}

where we assume that the boundary data
\Phi
can be extended to
\Phi\in\tilde{C}^{2}(\overline{Q}_{T})
, such that
\Phi|_{\{0\}\times\overline{\Omega}}=u_{0}
and
u_{0}(0)<0
.
We assume moreover that
\Phi
satisfy the compatibility condition

(3.5)

\Phi_{t}=\log\det D^{2}u_{0}+k\log u_{0}^{\star}-g(x,u_{0})\text{ on }\{0\}\times\partial\Omega.

From now on, we will assume
n+k\geq 0
. The following theorem establishes a priori estimates for the parabolic equation (
3.4
).

Theorem 3.1
.

Assume
n+k\geq 0
. Let
u\in\tilde{C}^{4}(Q_{T})\cap\tilde{C}^{2}(\overline{Q}_{T})
satisfy equation (
3.4
), and suppose further that

u(t,0)\leq-\varepsilon<0\text{ and }\|u\|_{C^{0}}\leq K.

Then we have the estimate

\|u\|_{\tilde{C}^{2}(Q_{T})}\leq C

for
C
depending on
n,k,\Omega
, the initial condition
u_{0}
,
\varepsilon
,
K
,
\|g\|_{C^{2}(\overline{\Omega}\times[-K,0])}
,
\|\Phi\|_{C^{k}(\Sigma)}
, and
\|D_{t}\delta\Phi\|_{C^{0}(\Sigma)}

Proof.

The proof of these estimates are by now standard, and we only give a sketch of the proof here. The proof proceeds by first estimating
|u_{t}|
, then estimating
|\nabla u|
, and finally
|\nabla^{2}u|
. The estimate for
u_{t}
follows from the same arguments as in
[
24
, Appendix]
. The estimate for
\nabla u
follows from the construction of a subsolution from
[
3
, pg 391]
. The estimates for
\nabla^{2}u
follow from combining the arguments in
[
24
, Appendix]
with the arguments of
[
3
, Section 7]
to handle the extra gradient terms.
∎

Once we have this, we can obtain higher regularity for
u
on a forward interval of time by
[
17
]
and a standard bootstrap argument using Schauder estimates.

Corollary 3.1
.

For any
\delta>0
, we have higher order estimates for
u

\|u\|_{\tilde{C}^{l,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}\times[\delta,T])}\leq C,

where
C
depends on
n,k,l,\alpha,\delta
,
K
,
\varepsilon
and bounds for higher derivatives of
g
and higher derivatives of
\Phi
on
\partial\Omega\times[\delta,T]
, but is independent of
T
.

The estimates in Theorem
3.1
can be used to obtain short-time solution to the Dirichlet problems (
3.3
).

Proposition 3.1

(Short-time existence)
.

Assume
n+k\geq 0
. Then for any initial
u_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
, there exist a unique maximal time
T\in(0,\infty]
and a unique solution
u(t,x)\in\tilde{C}^{1,1}(\overline{Q}_{T})\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}\times(0,T])
of (
3.3
) in
Q_{T}
, such that

1.

Either
T=\infty
or
\limsup_{t\to T}\|u(t,\cdot)\|=\infty
.

2.

For any
t>0
, we have

\mathcal{J}(u(t,\cdot))\leq\mathcal{J}(u_{0}).

Proof.

Short-time existence follows from the estimates of Theorem
3.1
by standard arguments (see
[
24
, Theorem A]
). Suppose now that
T
is a finite time singularity, and let
r
be small enough such that
B_{r}(0)\subset\Omega
. Since
F
is strictly positive, there exist
\eta>0
such that
F(x,u)\geq\eta>0
. It follows that for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, the function

v=\varepsilon(|x|^{2}-r^{2})

is a supersolution of (
4.2
) on
B_{r}(0)
. By the comparison principle, we have
u(t,0)\leq v(0)<0
. Then the estimates of Theorem
3.1
show that if
T
is a singular time, we must have

\limsup_{t\to T}\|u(t,\cdot)\|=\infty.

The second part follows from the same argument as in
[
24
, Theorem A]
.
∎

4
Associated Dirichlet problems

In this section, we use a variational method to study the solvability of the family of Dirichlet problems

(4.1)

\begin{cases}(u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u=|u|^{p}&\text{ in }\Omega\\
u=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

The case
p<n+k
,
p>n+k
,

will be treated seperately. We will refer to the three different cases as the

More generally, let

We will need the following estimates for solutions of (

Theorem 4.1
.

Let

The estimate for

4.1
Subcritical case

Now we will turn our attention to the study the Dirichlet problem (
4.2
). The first result deals with the subcritical case.

Theorem 4.2

(Subcritical case)
.

Suppose that
F(x,u)\geq\eta>0
satisfies

1.

There exist
\lambda<\underline{\lambda}_{n+k+1}
, and
M>1
such that
\int_{u}^{0}F(x,s)ds\leq\lambda|u|^{n+k+1}
for
|u|\geq M
.

2.

\inf_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}\mathcal{J}(u)<\mathcal{J}(0)=0

then (
4.2
) admit a solution.

Proof.

By assumption 1 and the definition of
\underline{\lambda}_{n+k+1}
(
2.15
), we have

(4.3)

\mathcal{J}(u)\geq\underline{\lambda}_{n+k+1}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{n+k+1}-\int_{\Omega}\left|\int_{u}^{0}F(x,s)ds\right|\geq c\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{n+k+1}-CM,

which implies
\mathcal{J}(u)
is bounded from below. Since
\inf_{u\in\mathcal{C}_{0}}J(u)<\mathcal{J}(0)
, and
\mathcal{J}(u)
, we can pick
u_{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
so that
\mathcal{J}(u_{0})<0
. Let
u(t,x)
be the solution of the parabolic equation (
3.3
), then by Proposition
3.1
, we have
\mathcal{J}(u(t,\cdot))<\mathcal{J}(u_{0})<0
. We claim that there exist
K_{0},K_{1}>0
such that

K_{0}^{-1}\leq\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq K_{1}.

The upper bound is a consequence of (
4.3
) and the fact that
\mathcal{J}(u(t,\cdot))
is decreasing. The lower bound follows from the fact that
\mathcal{J}(u(t,\cdot))
is negative, therefore there is some
\varepsilon>0
such that
\mathcal{J}(u(t,\cdot))<-\varepsilon<0
, which means

\int_{X}\int_{u(t,x)}^{0}F(x,s)ds\geq\varepsilon.

and it follows from Chebychev’s that
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}
must be bounded from below. Once we have a uniform bound of
\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}}
from above and below, it follows by Proposition
3.1
that
u(t,x)
exists for all time, and the estimates in Theorem
3.1
gives us bounds of all order on
u(t,\cdot)
as
t\to\infty
. Since
\mathcal{J}(u(t,\cdot))
is decreasing and bounded below, we can extract a subsequence of times
t_{j}\to\infty
for which

\lim_{j\to\infty}\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{j}}\mathcal{J}(u(t,\cdot))=0.

If we denote
u_{j}(x)=u(t_{j},x)
, then we can rewrite this as

\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\left(\log((u_{j}^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u_{j})-\log F(x,u_{j})\right)\left((u_{j}^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u_{j}-F(x,u_{j})\right)=0.

From this and the estimates on
\|u(t_{j},\cdot)\|_{C^{k,\alpha}}
, we can extract a convergent subsequence
u(t_{j},\cdot)\to u_{\infty}
which must be a solution of the equation (
4.2
).
∎

Now we prove a uniqueness results. For the uniquess theorem, we do not need to assume
F
is strictly positive.

Theorem 4.3

(Uniqueness)
.

Suppose
n+k\geq 0
and
F(x,u)\geq 0
satisfies

1.

F(x,u)>0
for
u<0
.

2.

F(x,tu)\geq t^{n+k}F(x,u)
for all
0<t<1
.

3.

For
0<|u|\ll 1
, the inequality above is strict.

Then there exist at most one non-zero solution to (
4.2
) in
C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
.

Proof.

Assume there are two distict non-zero solutions
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
to (
4.2
). Without loss of generality we may assume that
|u_{1}|\nleq|u_{2}|
. Let

t_{0}:=\sup\{t>0:t|u_{1}|\leq|u_{2}|\}.

then
t_{0}<1
and let us set
\tilde{u}_{1}=t_{0}u_{1}
, then by assumption 2, we have

(\tilde{u}^{\star}_{1})^{k}\det D^{2}\tilde{u}_{1}=t_{0}^{n+k}F(x,u_{1})\leq F(x,\tilde{u}_{1}),

which implies

(4.4)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq(\det D^{2}u_{2})^{\frac{1}{n}}-(\det D^{2}\tilde{u}_{1})^{\frac{1}{n}}-\left(\frac{F(x,u_{2})^{\frac{1}{n}}}{(u_{2}^{\star})^{\frac{k}{n}}}-\frac{F(x,\tilde{u}_{1})^{\frac{1}{n}}}{(\tilde{u}_{1}^{\star})^{\frac{k}{n}}}\right)

(4.5)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}(\det D^{2}\tilde{u}_{1})^{\frac{1}{n}}\tilde{u}_{1}^{ij}(u_{2}-\tilde{u}_{1})_{ij}-\left(\frac{F(x,u_{2})^{\frac{1}{n}}}{(u_{2}^{\star})^{\frac{k}{n}}}-\frac{F(x,\tilde{u}_{1})^{\frac{1}{n}}}{(\tilde{u}_{1}^{\star})^{\frac{k}{n}}}\right)

where the second line follows from the concavity of
M\mapsto(\det M)^{\frac{1}{n}}
.
By our choice of
t_{0}
, we have
|\tilde{u}_{1}|\leq|u_{2}|
and one of the following must be true.

1.

\tilde{u}_{1}(x_{0})=u_{2}(x_{0})
for some
x_{0}\in\Omega

2.

\langle x_{0},D\tilde{u}_{1}(x_{0})\rangle=\langle x_{0},Du_{2}(x_{0})\rangle
for some
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
.

In the first case, we have
\tilde{u}_{1}\equiv u_{2}
by the strong maximum principle, and in the second case we need to apply the Hopf lemma. If
\det D^{2}\tilde{u}_{1}(x_{0})>0
, then we can apply the standard Hopf lemma
[
13
, Lemma 3.4]
. In the case
\det D^{2}\tilde{u}_{1}(x_{0})=0
, we will need the following elementary lemma in order to apply the Hopf lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Let
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
be a boundary point, and
B_{r}(y)\subset\Omega
be a interior ball that touches
\partial\Omega
at
x_{0}
. Then on
B_{r}(y)\setminus B_{r/2}(y)
, there exist a constant
c>0
such that

(4.6)

\tilde{u}_{1}^{ij}(x-y)_{i}(x-y)_{j}\geq c|(D^{2}\tilde{u}_{1})^{-1}|.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that
\tilde{u}_{1}\in C^{2,\alpha}
and
D^{2}\tilde{u}_{1}(x_{0})
is strictly positive in the tangential direction to
\partial\Omega
. Let us fix coordinate
(\tilde{x}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{x}_{n})
centered at
x_{0}
such that locally
\partial\Omega
is the graph of a function
\rho(\tilde{x}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{x}_{n-1})
and
\rho(\tilde{x}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\rho_{i}\tilde{x}_{i}^{2}+O(|\tilde{x}^{\prime}|^{3})
where
\rho_{i}>0
are the principal curvatures of
\partial\Omega
at
x_{0}
. By the boundary condition, we have for
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}

(\tilde{u}_{1})_{ij}(x_{0})=-(\tilde{u}_{1})_{n}(x_{0})\delta_{ij}\rho_{i},

and since
\tilde{u}_{1}\in C^{2,\alpha}
, we have

D^{2}\tilde{u}_{1}(x)=\begin{pmatrix}-(\tilde{u}_{1})_{n}(x_{0})\rho_{1}&0&0&(\tilde{u}_{1})_{n,1}(x_{0})\\
0&\ddots&0&\vdots\\
0&0&-(\tilde{u}_{1})_{n}(x_{0})\rho_{n-1}&(\tilde{u}_{1})_{n,n-1}(x_{0})\\
(\tilde{u}_{1})_{1,n}(x_{0})&\cdots&(\tilde{u}_{1})_{n-1,n}(x_{0})&(\tilde{u}_{1})_{n,n}(x_{0})\end{pmatrix}+O(|x-x_{0}|^{\alpha}).

Therefore for
|x-x_{0}|
sufficiently small, we get the inequality (
4.6
). When
|x-x_{0}|
is large, the inequality (
4.6
) follow trivially and the lemma is proved.
∎

This lemma will allow us to apply the Hopf lemma to the operator
L=\frac{1}{n}\tilde{u}_{1}^{ij}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}
. First we note that by (
4.5
), we have

(\det D^{2}\tilde{u}_{1})^{1/n}L(u_{2}-\tilde{u}_{1})\geq o_{|x-x_{0}|}(1)

where
o_{|x-x_{0}|}(1)
goes to
0
as
|x-x_{0}|
goes to
0
. If we fix coordinate system
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
such that
x_{0}=(0,\ldots,0)
and
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\times\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, then locally we can write
\partial\Omega
as the graph of a function
x_{n}=\rho(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})
and moreover

\rho(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1})=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\rho_{i}|x_{i}|^{2}+O(|x^{\prime}|^{3}),

where
\rho_{i}>0
are the principal curvature of
\partial\Omega
at the origin. Then we can pick
A
sufficiently large such that in a small neighborhood
U=B_{\delta}(0)\cap\Omega
of the origin, we have

u_{2}-\tilde{u}_{1}+(x_{n}^{2}-A\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}|x_{i}|^{3})<0

and using the lemma above, and by shrinking
U
if necessary, we have that in
U
,

(4.7)

\displaystyle(\det D^{2}\tilde{u}_{1})^{1/n}L(u_{2}-\tilde{u}_{1}+(x_{n}^{2}-A|x^{\prime}|^{3}))

\displaystyle\geq o_{|x|}(1)+(c+O(|x^{\prime}|))(\det D^{1}\tilde{u}_{1})^{\frac{1}{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}u^{ii}

(4.8)

\displaystyle\geq c+o_{|x|}(1)+O(|x^{\prime}|)

(4.9)

\displaystyle\geq 0

Now we fix a small interior ball
B_{r}(y)\subset U
that touches
\partial\Omega
at
x_{0}
and consider the standard Hopf barrier
w=e^{-\alpha|x-y|^{2}}-e^{-\alpha r^{2}}
. By the standard calculation
[
13
, Lemma 3.4]
, we have

Lw\geq\alpha e^{-\alpha\frac{r}{4}^{2}}(4\alpha u_{1}^{ij}(x-y)_{i}(x-y)_{j}-2\sum_{i}u_{1}^{ii}).

For
\alpha
sufficiently large, the lemma above gives us
Lw\geq c|(D^{2}\tilde{u}_{1})^{-1}|\geq c(\det D^{2}\tilde{u}_{1})^{\frac{-1}{n}}
, hence we have in
B_{r}(y)

L(u_{2}-\tilde{u}_{1}+(x_{n}^{2}-A|x^{\prime}|^{3})+\varepsilon w)\geq 0

and if we choose
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, then
u_{2}-\tilde{u}_{1}+(x_{n}^{2}-A|x^{\prime}|^{3})+\varepsilon w\leq 0
on
B_{r}(y)\setminus B_{r/2}(y)
, and applying the maximum principle gives
u_{2}-\tilde{u}_{1}+(x_{n}^{2}-A|x^{\prime}|^{3})\leq-\varepsilon w
. In particular

-\frac{\partial(u_{2}-\tilde{u}_{1})}{\partial x_{n}}(0)>\varepsilon\frac{\partial w}{\partial x_{n}}(0)>0

which is a contradiction. Hence we conclude
u_{2}=t_{0}u_{1}
, which implies
t_{0}^{n+k}F(x,u_{1})\equiv F(x,t_{0}u_{1})
, which contradicts our third assumption on
F
.
∎

Notice the assumptions in Theorem
4.3
are satisfied if
F(x,u)^{\frac{1}{n+k}}
is concave. In particular Theorem
4.3
applies for
F(x,u)=|u|^{p}
for
p<n+k
. As an corollary, we obtain the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the Dirichlet problem (
4.1
) in the subcritical case.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
0<p<n+k
, then the Dirichlet problems (
4.1
) admits a unique non-zero convex solution
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, which minimizes the functional

\mathcal{J}_{p}(u):=H_{n+k}(u)-\frac{1}{p+1}\int_{\Omega}(-u)^{p+1}.

Proof.

By Theorem
4.2
, there exist a unique
u_{\varepsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
that solves the Dirichlet problem

\begin{cases}(u_{\varepsilon}^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u_{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon-u_{\varepsilon})^{p}&\text{ in }\Omega\\
u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Moreover,
u_{\varepsilon}
is a global minimizer of

\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{p,\varepsilon}(w)=H_{n+k}(w)-\frac{1}{p+1}\int_{\Omega}(\varepsilon-w)^{p+1}.

It’s clear that
u_{\varepsilon}
is uniformly bounded above and below, therefore by Theorem
4.1
, we get interior estimates of all order and
u_{\varepsilon}
must converge along some subsequence to some non-zero
u\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
which is a solution of (
4.1
). By the arguments of
[
23
, Theorem 1.3]
and
[
18
, Theorem 1.2]
we get that
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
.
Moreover
u
minimzes
\mathcal{J}_{p}
because
u_{\varepsilon}
are minimzers of
\tilde{\mathcal{J}}_{\varepsilon,p}
, and the uniqueness follows from Theorem
4.3
.
∎

4.2
Supercritical case

Now we treat the supercritical case. In this case, we will obtain non-trivial solutions to (
4.1
) by a min-max method.

Theorem 4.4

(Supercritical case)
.

Assume that
n+k\geq 0
. Let
F(x,u)\geq\eta>0
be strictly positive and
\mathcal{J}
be the functional

\mathcal{J}(u)=H_{n+k}(u)-\int_{\Omega}\int_{u}^{0}F(x,s)\,ds.

Assume
F
satisfy the following,

1.

There exist
c,\sigma>0
such that

\mathcal{J}(u)\geq c

for
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}=\sigma
.

2.

There exist
u_{0}
,
u_{1}\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
such that
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}}<\sigma<\|u_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}}
, and
\mathcal{J}(u_{0})<c
, and
\mathcal{J}(u_{1})<c
.

3.

There exist
\theta\in(0,1)
,
M>0
, such that

\int_{u}^{0}F(x,s)ds\leq\frac{1-\theta}{n+k+1}|u|F(x,u)

for all
|u|>M
.

4.

\left|\frac{F_{u}(x,u)}{F(x,u)}\right|\leq C
.

then (
4.2
) admit a solution
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
and
\mathcal{J}(u)\geq c

Proof.

The proof follows from a mountain-pass lemma, and is proved in the same way as
[
24
, Theorem C]
with some small modifications. Let

\mathcal{P}=\{\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{C}_{0}:\|\gamma(0)\|_{L^{\infty}}<\sigma<\|\gamma(1)\|_{L^{\infty}},\mathcal{J}(\gamma(0))<c,\mathcal{J}(\gamma(1))<c\}

and

d=\inf_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}}\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\mathcal{J}(\gamma(s))\geq c>0.

We will show that
d
is a critical value of
\mathcal{J}
which is attained by some critical point
u\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
. Let us pick a path
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}
such that

\bar{\mathcal{J}}(\gamma):=\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\mathcal{J}(\gamma(s))<d+\varepsilon.

Then
u(s,t,x)
be the solution to the parabolic equation (
3.3
) with initial condition
u(s,0,\cdot)=\gamma(s)
.

Claim 1
.

u(s,t,x)\in\mathcal{P}
exists for all time
t
, and
\bar{\mathcal{J}}(u(t))
is decreasing in
t
.

Proof of Claim
1
.

Short-time existence and the fact that
u(s,t,x)\in\mathcal{P}
follows from Proposition
3.1
and the same arguments as in
[
24
, Theorem C]
. We will show that the solutions exist for all time. By Theorem
3.1
, it suffices to bound
|u(0,t)|
from above and below uniformly in
t
. Let
v:B_{r}(0)\to\mathbb{R}
solve
(v^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}v=\delta
for some
\delta<\eta
, and
v|_{\partial B_{r}(0)}=0
, then
v
is a supersolution of (
3.3
), and by the comparison principle, we have
|u(0,t)|>|v(0)|
.
Now we bound
u
from below. The evolution equation for
u_{t}
is

u_{tt}=u^{ij}(u_{t})_{ij}+k\frac{\langle x,\nabla u_{t}\rangle-u_{t}}{u^{\star}}-\frac{F_{u}(x,u)}{F(x,u)}u_{t}

and since
\frac{F_{u}}{F}
and
\frac{1}{u^{\star}}
are both bounded, it follows that
|u_{t}|\leq Ce^{At}
by the maximum principle. Hence
u
is bounded from below on all finite time interval. The fact that
\overline{\mathcal{J}}(u(t))
is decreasing in
t
is clear.
∎

Now let us define the closed sets
I_{t}\subset[0,1]
by

I_{t}:=\{s\in[0,1]:\mathcal{J}(u(s,t))\geq c\}\subset[0,1],

then by the monotonicity of
\mathcal{J}(u(t,\cdot))
along the parabolic equation (
3.3
), the sets
I_{t}
are a nested family of closed sets in
[0,1]
, which means the intersection

I=\cap_{t>0}I_{t}

is non-empty. It follows that there exist an long-time solution
u(t,x)
to the equation (
3.3
) such that

\mathcal{J}(u(t,\cdot))\geq d\text{ for all }t>0.

By the monotonicity of
\mathcal{J}
, we have that for any
\varepsilon>0
, we can pick
T
sufficiently large, such that

(4.10)

\int_{T}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{J}(u(t,\cdot))\right)\,dt=\int_{T}^{\infty}\int_{\Omega}\left((u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u-F(x,u)\right)\left(\log((u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u)-\log F(x,u)\right)\,dt\leq\varepsilon.

Claim 2
.

For sufficiently large
t
, we have an estimate

\|u(t)\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C

uniformly in
t
.

Proof of Claim
2
.

We will follow the same line of argument as in
[
24
, Theorem C]
. Let us denote

G:=(u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u,

then by (
4.10
) and the mean value theorem, we know that for every interval
[m,m+1]
sufficiently large, we can find
t_{m}\in[m,m+1]
such that

\int_{\Omega}(G(t_{m})-F(t_{m}))(\log G(t_{m})-\log F(t_{m}))\leq\varepsilon.

From now on we will fix such a
t_{m}
, and to simplify notation, we will assume all quantities are evaluated at chosen time
t_{m}
and supress the dependence on
t_{m}
.

Let
\alpha>0
be given by
e^{-\alpha}=1-\frac{\theta}{2}
, and define
S\subset\Omega
to be the set

S=\{|\log G-\log F|\leq\alpha\}

then

\varepsilon\geq\int_{\Omega\setminus S}(G-F)(\log G-\log F)\geq\alpha\int_{\Omega\setminus S}|G-F|=\alpha\int_{\Omega\setminus S}(e^{\log G-\log F}-1)F\geq\alpha(e^{\alpha}-1)\int_{\Omega\setminus S}F.

and since
F\geq\eta>0
, we have

|\Omega\setminus S|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha(e^{\alpha}-1)\eta}.

J(u)=\int_{S}\left(\frac{(-u)G}{n+k+1}-\int_{u}^{0}F(x,s)\,ds\right)+\int_{\Omega\setminus S}\left(\frac{(-u)G}{n+k+1}-\int_{u}^{0}F(x,s)\,ds\right)

For the first term, one has

(4.11)

\displaystyle\int_{S}\left(\frac{(-u)G}{n+k+1}-\int_{u}^{0}F(x,s)\,ds\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n+k+1}\int_{S}(-u)(G-F)+\frac{\theta}{n+k+1}\int_{S}(-u)F-C

(4.12)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\theta}{2(n+k+1)}\int_{S}(-u)F-C

(4.13)

\displaystyle\geq c\theta\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{n+k+1}-C

For the second term, one has

(4.14)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega\setminus S}\left(\frac{(-u)G}{n+k+1}-\int_{u}^{0}F(x,s)\,ds\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n+k+1}\int_{\Omega\setminus S}(-u)G-\frac{1-\theta}{n+k+1}\int_{\Omega\setminus S}(-u)F-C

(4.15)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha(e^{\alpha}-1)}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}-C

It follows that

C\geq\mathcal{J}(u)\geq c\theta\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{n+k+1}-\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha(e^{\alpha}-1)}\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}-C.

This shows that
\|u(t_{m})\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C
independent of
m
. To upgrade this bound to holds for all
t
sufficiently large, we can follow the same argument as in
[
24
, Theorem C]
. This proves the claim.
∎

With this claim, we know that
|u(t)|
is bounded for all time, therefore by the estimates of Theorem
3.1
, it follows that
u(t,\cdot)
is bounded uniformly in
C^{k,\alpha}
independent of
t
. Moreover, there exist
t_{i}\to\infty
and
\varepsilon_{i}\to 0
, such that

\left|\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{t=t_{i}}\mathcal{J}(u(t))\right|\leq\varepsilon_{i}.

Therefore taking a subsequence as
i\to\infty
, we obtain a limit
u_{\infty}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
which solves (
4.2
) and
d\leq\mathcal{J}(u_{\infty})(u)\leq d+\varepsilon
.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Let
p>n+k>0
, then the equations (
4.1
) admit a non-zero convex solution
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
.

Proof.

Let

\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(u)=H_{n+k}(u)-\frac{1}{p+1}\int_{\Omega}((\varepsilon-u)^{p+1}-\varepsilon^{p+1}),

then by the
2.13
, we have

(4.16)

\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(u)\geq a\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{n+k+1}-A\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}^{p+1}-O(\varepsilon)\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}

and therefore it’s easy to check that
F_{\varepsilon}(x,u)=(\varepsilon-u)^{p}
satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
4.4
with
\mathcal{J}(u)>\frac{a}{2}(\frac{p-n-k}{p+1})\left(\frac{(n+k+1)a}{(p+1)A}\right)^{\frac{n+k+1}{p-n-k}}
for
\|u\|_{L^{\infty}}=\left(\frac{(n+k+1)a}{(p+1)A}\right)^{\frac{1}{p-n-k}}
. Theorem
4.4
allows us to solve the equations

(u_{\varepsilon}^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u_{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon-u_{\varepsilon})^{p}

for
p>n+k
, and moreover by equation (
4.16
), we have

\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\geq c>0
, and since

\mathcal{J}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})=\int_{\Omega}\frac{(-u_{\varepsilon})(\varepsilon-u_{\varepsilon})^{p}}{n+k+1}-\frac{(\varepsilon-u_{\varepsilon})^{p+1}-\varepsilon^{p+1}}{p+1},

this gives us uniform bounds on
\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}}
both above and below. By the estimates in Theorem
4.1
,
u_{\varepsilon}
must converge along some subsequence to some non-zero
u\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})
which is a solution of (
4.1
). By the arguments of
[
23
, Theorem 1.3]
and
[
18
, Theorem 1.2]
we get
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
.
∎

4.3
An eigenvalue problem

Now we treat the scale-invariant equation as an eigenvalue problem. As before, we will assume that
n+k\geq 0
, and consider the equation,

(4.17)

\begin{cases}(u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u=\lambda|u|^{n+k}&\text{ in }\Omega\\
u=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

Theorem 4.5

(Existence and uniqueness of eigenfunctions)
.

There exist
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega)
which is a solution to (
4.17
) for
\lambda=\underline{\lambda}_{n+k+1}
. Moreover, if
(\lambda^{\prime},u^{\prime})
is another pair of such solution, then
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda
and there exist
c>0
such that
u=cu^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We first consider the case when
n+k>0
. Consider the family of equations depending on a parameter
s>0
.

(4.18)

\begin{cases}(u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u=(1-su)^{n+k}&\text{ in }\Omega\\
u=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

and define the set
I\subset\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
by

I:=\{s\geq 0:\eqref{eq: s-regularized}\text{ admits a solution}\}

For
s\in I
, we’ll denote
u_{s}
a solution of (
4.18
). It clear that if
s^{\prime}\in I
, then
u_{s^{\prime}}
is a subsolution of (
4.18
) for all
s<s^{\prime}
, therefore
I
must be an interval starting from
0
. Also if
s^{\prime}\in I
, then
2u_{s}
is a subsolution of (
4.18
) for all
s<s^{\prime}+\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
, therefore
I
is open. It follows that either
I=\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, or
I=[0,\tilde{s})
for some
\tilde{s}>0
.

Claim 3
.

I=[0,\tilde{s})
, and
\tilde{s}\leq\underline{\lambda}_{n+k+1}^{\frac{1}{n+k}}
.

Proof.

Given any
s<\tilde{s}
, let
u
be the solution to (
4.18
). Then for
p<n+k
, consider the equations

(4.19)

\begin{cases}(u^{\star})^{k}\det D^{2}u=(1-su)^{p}&\text{ in }\Omega\\
u=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

By Theorem
4.2
, (
4.19
) admits a unique solution
u_{p}
which moreover minimizes the functional

\mathcal{J}_{p}(u):=H_{n+k}(u)-\frac{s^{-1}}{p+1}\int_{\Omega}(1-su)^{p+1}.

Also since
u
is a subsolution to (
4.19
), by
[
3
, Theorem 7.1]
and Theorem
4.3
, we know that
|u_{p}|\leq|u|
is uniformly bounded, therefore by the estimates
3.1
, it follows that

\inf\mathcal{J}_{p}=\mathcal{J}_{p}(u_{p})\geq-C

is uniformly bounded away from
-\infty
. This implies that the functional

\mathcal{J}_{n+k}(u):=H_{n+k}(u)-\frac{s^{-1}}{n+k+1}\int_{\Omega}(1-su)^{n+k+1}

is bounded below as well. However, we know that

H_{n+k}(u)-\frac{s^{n+k}}{n+k+1}\int_{\Omega}|u|^{n+k+1}\geq\inf J_{n+k}>-\infty

which implies
s^{n+k}\leq\underline{\lambda}_{n+k+1}
, which proves the claim.
∎

As
s\to\tilde{s}
, we claim that
\sup_{\Omega}|u_{s}|
must tend to
\infty
. For otherwise by
3.4
, we can extract a subsequence that converges to a function
u_{\tilde{s}}
, which is a solution of (
4.18
), which contradicts the definition of
\tilde{s}
. Now we consider the sequence of rescaled solutions

v_{s}:=\frac{u_{s}}{\|u_{s}\|}.

Then
v_{s}
satisfy the equations

(4.20)

\begin{cases}\det D^{2}v_{s}=(\|u_{s}\|_{L^{\infty}}^{-1}-sv_{s})^{n+k}(u_{s}^{\star})^{-k}&\text{ in }\Omega\\
v_{s}=0&\text{ on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

and
\|u_{s}\|_{C^{0}}=1
. By the first part of Theorem
4.1
, we get a
C^{1}
bound
\|u_{s}\|_{C^{1}}\leq C
, and we can extract a subsequence of
u_{s}
that converges to uniformly a limit
u
, by the interior estimates in Theorem
4.1
,
u
solves the equation (
4.17
) in
\Omega
with
\lambda=\tilde{s}^{n+k}
. By the arguments of
[
23
, Theorem 1.3]
and
[
18
, Theorem 1.2]
we get
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
for some
\alpha>0
. This proves the existence claim when
k>-n
. By (
2.15
), we must have that
\lambda=\tilde{s}^{n+k}\geq\underline{\lambda}_{n+k+1}
which together with Claim
3
, gives that
\lambda=\underline{\lambda}_{n+k+1}
.

In the case when
k=-n
, let
u_{\varepsilon}
be the solution of the eigenvalue problem (
4.17
) for
k=-n+\varepsilon
, normalized so that
\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{0}}=1
. It’s clear that
\lambda_{\varepsilon}\leq C
uniformly in
\varepsilon
, hence
\det D^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\leq C(1+|\nabla u_{\varepsilon}|)^{n}
, and by Theorem
4.1
, we have a uniform gradient estimate
\|u_{\varepsilon}\|_{C^{1}}\leq C
. Hence we can extract a subsequence that converge uniformly to a convex function
u
, and the same arguments as above shows
u\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})\cap C^{\infty}(\Omega)
for some
\alpha>0
. In fact, in this case the Schauder estimates implies that
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
.

The only thing left to prove is uniqueness. Suppose that there exist two pairs
(\lambda_{1},u_{1})
and
(\lambda_{2},u_{2})
which solves (
4.17
). Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}
, hence

(4.21)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq(\det D^{2}u_{2})^{\frac{1}{n}}-(\det D^{2}u_{1})^{\frac{1}{n}}-\lambda_{1}^{1/n}\left(\frac{(-u_{2})^{\frac{n+k}{n}}}{(u_{2}^{\star})^{\frac{k}{n}}}-\frac{(-u_{1})^{\frac{n+k}{n}}}{(u_{1}^{\star})^{\frac{k}{n}}}\right)

(4.22)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}(\det D^{2}u_{1})^{\frac{1}{n}}u_{1}^{ij}(u_{2}-u_{1})_{ij}-\lambda_{1}^{1/n}\left(\frac{(-u_{2})^{\frac{n+k}{n}}}{(u_{2}^{\star})^{\frac{k}{n}}}-\frac{(-u_{1})^{\frac{n+k}{n}}}{(u_{1}^{\star})^{\frac{k}{n}}}\right).

where the second line follows from the concavity of
M\mapsto(\det M)^{\frac{1}{n}}
.
Notice that any scaling
(\lambda_{2},tu_{2})
for
t>0
also solves (
4.17
), therefore we can replace
u_{2}
by one of it’s scalings
t_{0}u_{2}
where

t_{0}=\sup\{t>0:tu_{2}<u_{1}\text{ in }\Omega\}.

After replacing

by
t_{0}u_{2}
, we have
u_{2}\leq u_{1}
and either there exist

for which

, or there exist

for which
\langle x,\nabla(u_{2}-u_{1})\rangle(x_{0})=0
. If the first case is true, then by the strong maximum principle

and we are done. In the second case, we can apply the Hopf lemma as in Theorem

. We’ve proven the Theorem.

.

5
Relationship to an optimal transport problem

In this section, we describe a transformation that transforms the solution of the Dirichlet problems (
1.3
) to that of a solution of a second boundary value problem, which has an interpretation of an optimal transport problem. This transformation arises from the duality of affine spheres
[
4
]
. (see also
[
15
, Section 5]
for a nice exposition.) We now describe the transformation below.

Suppose
u\in\mathcal{C}_{0}
, then let us denote RGraph
(u)
to be
radial graph
of the function
\frac{1}{-u}
,

\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}\supset\text{RGraph}(u):=\left\{\frac{1}{-u(x)}(x,1):x\in\Omega\right\}.

In this setting, RGraph
(u)
is actually the graph of a convex function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and moreover we have the relationship
\nabla\varphi(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\Omega^{\circ}
. (see
[
15
, Section 5]
) The following proposition describes the relationship between the equation satisfied by
u
and the equation satisfied by
\varphi
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
u
and
\varphi
be described by above, then we have

(5.1)

\frac{\det D^{2}u(x)}{(u^{\star})^{n+2}(x)}=\varphi^{n+2}\left(\frac{x}{-u(x)}\right)\det D^{2}\varphi\left(\frac{x}{-u(x)}\right).

Proof.

Let
y=\frac{x}{-u(x)}
be coordinates on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then by the definition of
\varphi
, we have
\varphi(y)=\frac{1}{-u(x)}
and
x=\frac{y}{\varphi(y)}
. Then by differentiating, we get

\frac{\partial x_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}=\frac{\delta_{ij}}{\varphi}-\frac{y_{i}}{\varphi^{2}}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y_{j}}=\frac{\delta_{ij}}{\varphi}-\frac{x_{i}}{\varphi}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y_{j}}=-u(\delta_{ij}-x_{i}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y_{j}}),

and by chain rule, we get

\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y_{j}}=\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_{i}}\frac{\partial x_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}=\frac{u_{i}}{u}(x_{i}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y_{j}}-\delta_{ij}),

which implies

\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial y_{j}}=-\frac{u_{j}}{u-\langle x,\nabla u\rangle}=\frac{u_{j}}{u^{\star}}.

taking another derivative and applying chain rule, we get

(5.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\varphi}{\partial y_{i}\partial y_{j}}=\frac{-u}{u^{\star}}\left(u_{jk}-\frac{u_{j}x_{m}u_{mk}}{u^{\star}}\right)\left(\delta_{ki}-x_{k}\frac{u_{i}}{u^{\star}}\right)

taking a determinant gives what we desired.
∎

Remark 3
.

It is known that the graph of a function
u
is an elliptic affine sphere with center at the origin if and only if
u
satisfies

\frac{\det D^{2}u}{(u^{\star})^{n+2}}=c

Therefore from Proposition
5.1
and this characterization of affine spheres, we see that Graph
(u)
is a elliptic affine sphere if and only if the graph of the Legendre tranform of
\varphi
is also an elliptic affine sphere. This is essentially the duality of affine sphere described in
[
4
]
.

More generally, we can see that if
u
is a solution of (
1.3
), then by (
5.1
),
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
solves the second boundary value problem.

(5.3)

\begin{cases}\det D^{2}\varphi=\lambda\frac{(-\varphi^{\star})^{n+2+k}}{\varphi^{n+2+p}}\\
\nabla\varphi(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\Omega^{\circ}.\end{cases}

Remark 4
.

In the case
k=-n-2
, the numerator on the right-hand-side of equation (
5.3
) does not appear. In this case, equation (
5.3
) was introduced and solved by Klartag in
[
15
]
, who proved the existence and uniqueness of solutions up to translations. It is in general not known whether Klartag’s solutions can be transformed to a solution of a Dirichlet problem (
1.3
).

Based on the above discussion, an immediate consequence of our existence results for the Dirichlet problem (
1.3
) is the following

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
n+k>0
, then the second boundary value problem (
5.3
) admits a nontrivial solution for all
p\neq n+k
. For
p=n+k
, (
5.3
) admits a solution for
\lambda=\underline{\lambda}_{n+k+1}
.

Proof.

This follows from Theorem
1.1
and the discussion above.
∎

Remark 5
.

More generally, (
5.3
) is equivalent to the condition

\nabla\varphi_{\star}\left(\frac{\lambda dx}{\varphi^{n+2+p}(x)}\right)=\frac{dy}{(-\varphi^{\star}(y))^{n+2+k}}.

From this we see that
\nabla\varphi
solves an optimal tranport problem from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to
\Omega^{\circ}
, where the domain is

d\mu=\frac{\lambda dx}{\varphi^{n+2+p}(x)}

and target measure is

d\nu=\frac{dy}{(-\varphi^{\star}(y))^{n+2+k}}.

Hence
\varphi
has an interesting interpretation as the solution of an optimal transport problem whose domain and target measure are coupled to the potential
\varphi
and its Legendre transform
\varphi^{\star}
.
