The electromagnetic symmetry sphere: a framework for energy, momentum, spin and other electromagnetic quantities
Sebastian Golat, Alex J. Vernon, Francisco J. Rodríguez-Fortuño

Abstract
Electromagnetic quantities such as energy density, momentum, spin, and helicity bring meaning and intuition to electromagnetism and possess intricate interrelations, particularly prominent in complex non-paraxial near-fields. These quantities are conventionally expressed using electric and magnetic field vectors, yet the electric-magnetic basis is one among other often overlooked alternatives, including parallel-antiparallel and right-left-handed helicity bases, related to the parity and duality symmetries of electromagnetism. Projecting time-harmonic electromagnetic fields into a variety of bases allows re-interpreting established quantities and reveals underlying mathematical structures: a Bloch sphere which describes asymmetries in electromagnetic energy, a systematic path to unify and uncover relations between electromagnetic quantities, and the unlocking of symmetry-driven equations in light-matter interaction.



Introduction

​​​​—​   Modern nanophotonics explores structured near fields in a non-paraxial regime, revealing phenomena like complex 3D polarizations, spin-orbit interactions, and oscillating energy flows, often leading to novel applications. Many physical quantities are defined and invoked in our attempts to gain intuition and give meaning to the field’s behavior, such as the field’s energy density, energy flow, linear momenta, spin and orbital angular momenta, helicity, and many others, which are often quadratic quantities in the fields. In-depth study of these quantities reveals intricate relations between them

Bispinor formalism

​​​​—​   Bispinors, combining electric and magnetic fields into a single ‘wavefunction’

[

5

\mathbb{C}^{3}\times\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

\mathbb{C}^{3}

{\bf\it E}

{\bf\it H}

\mathbb{C}^{2}

{\bf\it E}

{\bf\it H}

\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

\mathbb{C}^{2}
space
{\bf\it\psi}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}{\bf\it E},\sqrt{\mu}{\bf\it H})^{\intercal}
which separates electric and magnetic fields. But like any other
\mathbb{C}^{2}
space, isomorphic with the Jones vector that describes 2D plane wave polarisation, different choices of basis can be made (see
). Viewing an electromagnetic wave as composed of electric and magnetic fields (EM) is just as valid as considering it constituted by parallel and anti-parallel (PA), or right and left-handed (RL) fields. See Supplemental Material (SM) section A for a step-by-step justification

12

\lx@cleverref@save@label{eq:bases}\begin{aligned} {\bf\it\psi}_{\text{EM}}({\bf\it r})&=\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}&&=\frac{1}{2}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\,,\\

{\bf\it\psi}_{\text{RL}}({\bf\it r})&=\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}&&=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\,.\end{aligned}

The three bases EM, PA and RL relate to the fundamental electromagnetic symmetries: parity inversion
\hat{P}
:
{\bf\it r}\mapsto-{\bf\it r}
, time reversal
\hat{T}
:
t\mapsto-t
,
and discrete duality transformation
\hat{D}
. These act on the fields as

\begin{split}\hat{D}:\;\quantity(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\vc{E},\sqrt{\mu}\vc{H})^{\intercal}&\mapsto\quantity(\sqrt{\mu}\vc{H},-\sqrt{\varepsilon}\vc{E})^{\intercal},\\
\hat{P}:\;\quantity(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\vc{E},\sqrt{\mu}\vc{H})^{\intercal}&\mapsto\quantity(-\sqrt{\varepsilon}\vc{E},\sqrt{\mu}\vc{H})^{\intercal},\\
\hat{T}:\;\quantity(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\vc{E},\sqrt{\mu}\vc{H})^{\intercal}&\mapsto\quantity(\sqrt{\varepsilon}\vc{E}^*,-\sqrt{\mu}\vc{H}^*)^{\intercal},\end{split}

(2)

so
\hat{D}
is a 90 degree rotation in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
space, while
\hat{P}
is a mirror symmetry inverting the
{\bf\it E}
axis. The components of the EM basis are eigenvectors of the
\hat{P}
operator, RL basis of the
\hat{D}
operator, and PA basis of the combined
\hat{P}\hat{D}
operator.
Note that the fields
{\bf\it F}_{\text{R}}
and
{\bf\it F}_{\text{L}}
are similar to Riemann-Silberstein vectors

{\bf\it E}

Field projections

​​​​—​   Any electromagnetic field
({\bf\it E},{\bf\it H})
, including non-paraxial and structured near fields, can be decomposed into a sum of two components, based on the different bases in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
space:

\lx@cleverref@save@label{eq:projectionsE}\begin{aligned} {\bf\it E}&={\bf\it E}_{\text{e}}&&+{\bf\it E}_{\text{m}}&&=\quad\;\,{\bf\it E}&&+\quad\;\,{\bf\it 0}\\
&={\bf\it E}_{\text{p}}&&+{\bf\it E}_{\text{a}}&&=\frac{{\bf\it E}+\eta{\bf\it H}}{2}&&+\frac{{\bf\it E}-\eta{\bf\it H}}{2}\\
&={\bf\it E}_{\text{R}}&&+{\bf\it E}_{\text{L}}&&=\frac{{\bf\it E}+\text{i}\eta{\bf\it H}}{2}&&+\frac{{\bf\it E}-\text{i}\eta{\bf\it H}}{2}\,,\end{aligned}

(3)

with the corresponding
{\bf\it H}_{\text{e}}={\bf\it 0},{\bf\it H}_{\text{m}}={\bf\it H},{\bf\it H}_{\text{p,a}}=\pm{\bf\it E}_{\text{p,a}}/\eta
and
{\bf\it H}_{\text{R,L}}=\mp\text{i}{\bf\it E}_{\text{R,L}}/\eta
, and
\eta=\sqrt{\mu/\varepsilon}
. These pairs
({\bf\it E}_{i},{\bf\it H}_{i})
represent the projection of the total electromagnetic field into each basis vector in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
space, with
i=(\text{e},\text{m},\text{p},\text{a},\textsc{r},\textsc{l})
. For example, a linearly polarised field with
{\bf\it E}=E_{0}\hat{{\bf\it x}}
and
{\bf\it H}=E_{0}\hat{{\bf\it y}}/\eta
may be decomposed in the PA basis as the sum of parallel fields
({\bf\it E}_{\text{p}},{\bf\it H}_{\text{p}})=E_{0}(\frac{\hat{{\bf\it x}}+\hat{{\bf\it y}}}{2},\frac{\hat{{\bf\it x}}+\hat{{\bf\it y}}}{2\eta})
, which have
{\bf\it F}_{\text{a}}=0
, plus antiparallel ones
({\bf\it E}_{\text{a}},{\bf\it H}_{\text{a}})=E_{0}(\frac{\hat{{\bf\it x}}-\hat{{\bf\it y}}}{2},-\frac{\hat{{\bf\it x}}-\hat{{\bf\it y}}}{2\eta})
, which have
{\bf\it F}_{\text{p}}=0
. Each of these pairs of simultaneous electric and magnetic fields, given by
({\bf\it E}_{i},{\bf\it H}_{i})
, are mathematical projections, not physically realizable in vacuum, except for the pairs
({\bf\it E}_{\text{R}},{\bf\it H}_{\text{R}})
and
({\bf\it E}_{\text{L}},{\bf\it H}_{\text{L}})
which correspond to realizable pure helicity fields
[
16
,
3
]
. The total electromagnetic energy density
W
is given by the norm
\norm{\vc{\psi}}^{2}
. Thanks to orthogonality and Parseval’s identity, the energy density
W
is the sum of the squared norm of each of the two
{\bf\it\psi}
components in any of the three bases. Therefore,
W
, a quadratic quantity that is generally not linear with the fields, can be obtained
by linear addition
in three different ways
W=W_{\text{e}}+W_{\text{m}}=W_{\text{p}}+W_{\text{a}}=W_{\text{R}}+W_{\text{L}}
, of which the first is well-known. A similar argument applies for the total spin angular momentum
{\bf\it S}={\bf\it S}_{\text{e}}+{\bf\it S}_{\text{m}}={\bf\it S}_{\text{p}}+{\bf\it S}_{\text{a}}={\bf\it S}_{\text{L}}+{\bf\it S}_{\text{R}}
, and total stress tensor
\mathsfbfit{{T}}=\mathsfbfit{{T}}_{\text{e}}+\mathsfbfit{{T}}_{\text{m}}=\mathsfbfit{{T}}_{\text{p}}+\mathsfbfit{{T}}_{\text{a}}=\mathsfbfit{{T}}_{\text{L}}+\mathsfbfit{{T}}_{\text{R}}
, by changing the dot product implicit in
W_{i}=|{\bf\it F}_{i}|^{2}={\bf\it F}_{i}^{*}\dotproduct{\bf\it F}_{i}
into a cross or outer product. The individual components
W_{i}
,
{\bf\it S}_{i}
and
\mathsfbfit{{T}}_{i}
(with a subscript
i=(\text{e},\text{m},\text{p},\text{a},\textsc{r},\textsc{l})
corresponding to each of the six field projections) are the energy, spin, and stress tensor of each projection (using
{\bf\it E}_{i}
and
{\bf\it H}_{i}
from
), and can be defined laconically using the corresponding field coefficients
{\bf\it F}_{i}
from
:

\lx@cleverref@save@label{eq:projectionquadratics}\begin{aligned} W_{i}&={\bf\it F}^{*}_{i}\dotproduct{\bf\it F}_{i}&&=\tfrac{1}{4}\left(\varepsilon{\bf\it E}_{i}^{*}\dotproduct{\bf\it E}_{i}+\mu{\bf\it H}_{i}^{*}\dotproduct{\bf\it H}_{i}\right)\,,\\
i\omega{\bf\it S}_{i}&={\bf\it F}^{*}_{i}\!\crossproduct{\bf\it F}_{i}&&=\tfrac{1}{4}\left(\varepsilon{\bf\it E}_{i}^{*}\!\crossproduct\!{\bf\it E}_{i}+\mu{\bf\it H}_{i}^{*}\!\crossproduct{\bf\it H}_{i}\right)\,,\\

(4)

where

represents the symmetric outer product defined as
{\bf\it A}\odot{\bf\it B}={\bf\it A}\otimes{\bf\it B}+{\bf\it B}\otimes{\bf\it A}

Bloch sphere

​​​​—​   Given the mathematical analogy with Stokes parameters, it is natural to plot the real vector
{\bf\it W}=(W_{1},W_{2},W_{3})
in
W
-space. As with Stokes parameters, this vector is contained on a sphere of radius
W_{0}
, analogous to the Poincare or Bloch sphere. We term this the
energy symmetry sphere
, depicted in
, as it reveals how the energy is distributed among the symmetries. Time-reversal
\hat{T}
mirrors the sphere on the
(W_{1},W_{3})
plane, parity
\hat{P}
rotates it 180 degrees around the
W_{1}
axis, and duality
\hat{D}
rotates it around
W_{3}
. Unlike the Stokes parameters in the Poincare sphere, which only describe 2D polarisation, the symmetry sphere can describe
any
electromagnetic field
({\bf\it E},{\bf\it H})
, including non-paraxial, structured and near-fields, with arbitrary three-dimensional polarisation. The absolute spatial orientation of polarisation ellipses is not relevant to the energy symmetry sphere, but the relative orientation and phase between
{\bf\it E}
and
{\bf\it H}
plays a crucial role. Unlike the Poincare sphere, fully polarised electromagnetic fields can exist anywhere in the surface
or inside
the sphere. The distance to the origin indicates how asymmetrically-distributed the energy is. Linearly polarised plane waves lie at the origin of the symmetry sphere, with maximum symmetry
(W_{\text{e}}=W_{\text{m}}=W_{\text{p}}=W_{\text{a}}=W_{\text{R}}=W_{\text{L}}=W/2)
. All possible homogeneous plane waves and paraxial far fields exist along the
W_{3}
axis (
W_{1}=W_{2}=0
) with circular polarisation at the poles, while homogeneous evanescent waves can stray away from the
W_{3}
axis, more so when more confined. The states at the very surface of the symmetry sphere (where
W_{1}^{2}+W_{2}^{2}+W_{3}^{2}=W_{0}^{2}
) correspond to maximum-asymmetry between the energies in the two orthogonal states of some basis in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
space. As shown in the SM section B5
[
12
]
, this corresponds to
{\bf\it E}=\delta\eta{\bf\it H}
with
\delta
a complex scalar. This means that states in the surface have fewer degrees of freedom, the electric and magnetic fields are proportional and share the same polarisation structure. This was pointed out for pure helicity fields
[
14
,
15
]
, which is a particular case of being at the surface (north and south poles). In contrast, any states
inside
the sphere must have linearly independent fields.
depicts special points on the surface where
{\bf\it F}_{i}=0
and hence
W_{i}=0
for
i=(\text{e},\text{m},\text{p},\text{a},\textsc{r},\textsc{l})
, corresponding to the field projections from
. The polar angle with respect to the axis joining the origin to any
{\bf\it F}_{i}=0

[
12
]
).

{\bf\it r}

{\bf\it W}({\bf\it r})

An electromagnetic field
({\bf\it E},{\bf\it H})

{\bf\it H}

W_{3}\neq 0

W_{2}\neq 0

Conclusions

​​​​—​   By freeing ourselves from the electric-magnetic basis, and expressing electromagnetic fields in the parallel-antiparallel and right-left basis, a flood of insights and analogies is uncovered. By subtracting the energy and spin between field projections, a re-interpretation and systematization of known electromagnetic quadratic quantities and their relations results, in some cases leading to novel insights such as the wave-like behavior of the spin-like quantities. The symmetry sphere is a valuable resource to gain intuition and understanding of symmetry in electromagnetism and its interaction with matter, and we feel there is much room for its exploration. Our current analysis is limited to time-harmonic fields and quadratic quantities only, though we strongly believe the framework can be adapted to more general situations.

A.3
Microscopic Maxwell’s equations in different bases

The use of different bases to describe electromagnetic fields is theoretically on the same footing as the traditional
{\bf\it E}
and
{\bf\it H}
approach. In fact, one can rewrite Maxwell’s equations using them, and the well-known duality symmetry of Maxwell’s equations makes them particularly pleasant. Following standard notation with
\rho
being charge density and
{\bf\it J}
being current density, the dual symmetric (including magnetic charges and currents) microscopic Maxwell’s equations for time-harmonic fields are

\lx@cleverref@save@label{eq:maxwelldualnormalised}\begin{split}\divergence\sqrt{\varepsilon}{\bf\it E}&=\rho_{\text{e}}/\sqrt{\varepsilon}\,,\\
\divergence\sqrt{\mu}{\bf\it H}&=\rho_{\text{m}}/\sqrt{\mu}\,,\\
\curl\sqrt{\varepsilon}{\bf\it E}&=+\text{i}k\sqrt{\mu}{\bf\it H}-\sqrt{\varepsilon}{\bf\it J}_{\text{m}}\,,\\
\curl\sqrt{\mu}{\bf\it H}&=-\text{i}k\sqrt{\varepsilon}{\bf\it E}+\sqrt{\mu}{\bf\it J}_{\text{e}}\,.\end{split}

(17)

These can be rewritten in terms of the basis coefficients
{\bf\it F}_{i}
where
i\in\{\text{e,m,p,a,{r},{l}}\}
. In order to do that we need to find what charge and current densities look in those different bases. We may define the following scaled versions of charge and current densities:

\displaystyle f_{\text{e}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{\text{e}}}{2\sqrt{\varepsilon}},\quad\quad{\bf\it g}_{\text{e}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bf\it J}_{\text{e}}}{2c\sqrt{\varepsilon}},

(18)

\displaystyle f_{\text{m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\rho_{\text{m}}}{2\sqrt{\mu}},\quad\quad{\bf\it g}_{\text{m}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bf\it J}_{\text{m}}}{2c\sqrt{\mu}},

such that,
{\bf\it F}_{\text{e}}=\sqrt{\varepsilon}{\bf\it E}/2
and
{\bf\it F}_{\text{m}}=\sqrt{\mu}{\bf\it H}/2
constitute a simple re-scaling of Maxwell’s equations,

\begin{split}\lx@cleverref@save@label{eq:definedchargesbasis}\divergence{\bf\it F}_{\text{e/m}}&=f_{\text{e/m}}\,,\\
\curl{\bf\it F}_{\text{e/m}}&=\pm[\text{i}k{\bf\it F}_{\text{m/e}}-{\bf\it g}_{\text{m/e}}]\,.\end{split}

(19)

Next, to get the equations in the right-left basis we need to combine them to get fields
{\bf\it F}_{\text{R/L}}=({\bf\it F}_{\text{e}}\pm\text{i}{\bf\it F}_{\text{m}})/\sqrt{2}
, which will lead to charge and current density combinations:
f_{\text{R/L}}=(f_{\text{e}}\pm\text{i}f_{\text{m}})/\sqrt{2}
and
{\bf\it g}_{\text{R/L}}=({\bf\it g}_{\text{e}}\pm\text{i}{\bf\it g}_{\text{m}})/\sqrt{2}
, leading to

\begin{split}\divergence{\bf\it F}_{\text{R/L}}&=f_{\text{R/L}}\,,\\
\curl{\bf\it F}_{\text{R/L}}&=\pm[k{\bf\it F}_{\text{R/L}}+\text{i}{\bf\it g}_{\text{R/L}}]\,.\end{split}

(20)

Note that in this basis the right and left fields don’t mix, unlike in the usual electromagnetic basis.
To obtain Maxwell’s equations in the parallel-antiparallel basis, we need the fields to be
{\bf\it F}_{p/a}=({\bf\it F}_{\text{e}}\pm{\bf\it F}_{\text{m}})/\sqrt{2}
, charge densities to be
f_{\text{p/a}}=(f_{\text{e}}\pm f_{\text{m}})/\sqrt{2}
, and current densities
{\bf\it g}_{\text{p/a}}=({\bf\it g}_{\text{e}}\pm{\bf\it g}_{\text{m}})/\sqrt{2}
. These combinations yield

\begin{split}\divergence{\bf\it F}_{\text{p/a}}&={f_{\text{p/a}}}\,,\\
\curl{\bf\it F}_{\text{p/a}}&=\mp[\text{i}k{\bf\it F}_{\text{a/p}}-{{\bf\it g}_{\text{a/p}}}]\,.\end{split}

(21)

Of course, these are just some arbitrary choices of bases in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and we could make a different choice (take a different linear combination of Maxwell’s equations), however, one can define a charge density in the
\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
vector space as

\quad{\bf\it f}=f_{\text{e}}\hat{{\bf\it e}}+f_{\text{m}}\hat{{\bf\it m}}=f_{\text{p}}\hat{{\bf\it p}}+f_{\text{a}}\hat{{\bf\it a}}=f_{\text{R}}\hat{{\bf\it r}}+f_{\text{L}}\hat{{\bf\it l}}\quad

(22)

and a current bispinor in the
\mathbb{C}^{3}\times\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{L}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
vector space as

\quad{\bf\it g}={\bf\it g}_{\text{e}}\hat{{\bf\it e}}+{\bf\it g}_{\text{m}}\hat{{\bf\it m}}={\bf\it g}_{\text{p}}\hat{{\bf\it p}}+{\bf\it g}_{\text{a}}\hat{{\bf\it a}}={\bf\it g}_{\text{R}}\hat{{\bf\it r}}+{\bf\it g}_{\text{L}}\hat{{\bf\it l}}\quad

(23)

to write the time-harmonic microscopic Maxwell’s equations in a basis-independent manner as

\lx@cleverref@save@label{eq:microscopicmaxwell_{b}asisindependent}\begin{split}\divergence{\bf\it\psi}&={\bf\it f}\,,\\
\curl{\bf\it\psi}&=\hat{D}(\text{i}k{\bf\it\psi}-{\bf\it g})\,,\end{split}

(24)

where
\hat{D}
is the electromagnetic duality transformation

\hat{D}\hat{{\bf\it e}}=\hat{{\bf\it m}}
and
\hat{D}\hat{{\bf\it m}}=-\hat{{\bf\it e}}
.

B.2
Stokes parameters and the Poincaré sphere

As mentioned before the
\mathbb{C}^{2}
subspace of electromagnetic bispinors is analogous to the Jones vectors.
Any polarised paraxial light can be represented by a
\mathbb{C}^{2}
vector
{\bf\it J}
such that the electric field phasor can be written as

\sqrt{\varepsilon}{\bf\it E}=({\bf\it J}^{\intercal}\hat{{\bf\it e}})\text{e}^{\text{i}kz}=\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\text{e}^{\text{i}kz}=\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\text{e}^{\text{i}kz}=\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\text{e}^{\text{i}kz}\,,

(31)

where (
A_{\text{h}}
,
A_{\text{v}}
) are linear horizontal-vertical, (
A_{\text{d}}
,
A_{\text{a}}
) diagonal-antidiagonal and (
A_{\text{R}}
,
A_{\text{L}}
) right-left circular polarisation complex amplitudes. The outer product of the Jones vector with itself produces a tensor which, following
, may be written as a linear combination of Pauli matrices, whose coefficients are one half of the Stokes parameters as they are conventionally defined

\displaystyle{\bf\it J}{\bf\it J}^{\dagger}_{[\text{ps}]}

\displaystyle=\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\absolutevalue{A_\text{h}}^{2}&A_{\text{h}}A_{\text{v}}^{*}\\
A_{\text{v}}A_{\text{h}}^{*}&\absolutevalue{A_\text{v}}^{2}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{S}_{0}+\mathcal{S}_{1}&\mathcal{S}_{2}-\text{i}\mathcal{S}_{3}\\
\mathcal{S}_{2}+\text{i}\mathcal{S}_{3}&\mathcal{S}_{0}-\mathcal{S}_{1}\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad\quad

\displaystyle\text{(linear basis)}

(32)

and similarly in the diagonal-antidiagonal and right-left circular bases. Following
one can express the Stokes parameters as they are most well-known:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{0}=\absolutevalue{A_\text{h}}^{2}+\absolutevalue{A_\text{v}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{A_\text{d}}^{2}+\absolutevalue{A_\text{a}}^{2}=\absolutevalue{A_\text{R}}^{2}+\absolutevalue{A_\text{L}}^{2},

(33)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{A_\text{h}}^{2}-\absolutevalue{A_\text{v}}^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{A_\text{d}}^{2}-\absolutevalue{A_\text{a}}^{2},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{3}

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{A_\text{R}}^{2}-\absolutevalue{A_\text{L}}^{2}.

One can easily see that

\mathcal{S}_{0}

\mathbb{C}^{2}

B.4
Other quadratic quantites

There is one big difference between Jones vectors and bispinors: the
\mathbb{C}^{2}
components of bispinors are themselves
\mathbb{C}^{3}
-valued vectors. The way we dealt with this in the previous section was by taking a dot product

\displaystyle\mathsfbfit{{W}}_{[\text{EM}]}

\displaystyle=({\bf\it\psi}\dotproduct{\bf\it\psi}^{\dagger})_{[\text{EM}]}=\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}=\frac{1}{2}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\,.

(40)

However one might argue that this is just one of many possible products for
\mathbb{C}^{3}
vectors. Indeed it is the case that, for example, taking the vector cross-product leads to spin-like quantities

\displaystyle({\bf\it\psi}\crossproduct{\bf\it\psi}^{\dagger})_{[\text{EM}]}=\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}=\frac{\omega}{2\text{i}}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\,.

(41)

Sadly these spin-like quantities are
\mathbb{R}^{3}
vectors, therefore, there is no canonical way to define the determinant and hence prove the existence of a spin-sphere in this way. The next product that one can take is the symmetric tensor product which leads to stress-tensor-like quantities

\displaystyle({\bf\it\psi}\odot{\bf\it\psi}^{\dagger})_{[\text{EM}]}=\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}=\frac{1}{2}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}+\mathsfbfit{{W}}\mathsfbfit{{I}}_{3}\,,

(42)

where
\mathsfbfit{{I}}_{3}=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,1)
is the identity in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
space.
One might wonder why we used the symmetric tensor product rather than the regular one. Usually, the Maxwell stress tensor is defined as the real part of a complex quantity defined with the tensor product. However, the tensor product of any
\mathbb{C}^{3}
vector with its conjugate can be split into the symmetric and antisymmetric parts as follows

{\bf\it F}_{i}\!\otimes{\bf\it F}_{i}^{*}=\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}{\bf\it F}_{i}\odot{\bf\it F}_{i}^{*}}_{\real({\bf\it F}_{i}\otimes{\bf\it F}_{i}^{*})}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{2}{\bf\it F}_{i}\wedge{\bf\it F}_{i}^{*}}_{\text{i}\Im({\bf\it F}_{i}\otimes{\bf\it F}_{i}^{*})}

(43)

where we use symmetric and antisymmetric outer products which are defined as
{\bf\it F}\odot{\bf\it F}^{*}={\bf\it F}\otimes{\bf\it F}^{*}+{\bf\it F}^{*}\!\otimes{\bf\it F}
, and
{\bf\it F}\wedge{\bf\it F}^{*}={\bf\it F}\otimes{\bf\it F}^{*}-{\bf\it F}^{*}\!\otimes{\bf\it F}\!
respectively. The key observation is that a bivector
{\bf\it F}_{i}\wedge{\bf\it F}_{i}^{*}
(directed plane segment with a sense of rotation) is isomorphic to a vector (directed line segments with a sense of translation)
{\bf\it F}_{i}\crossproduct{\bf\it F}_{i}^{*}
, this isomorphism is called Hodge duality and it is denoted as
\star({\bf\it F}_{i}\crossproduct{\bf\it F}_{i}^{*})={\bf\it F}_{i}\wedge{\bf\it F}_{i}^{*}
(see, e.g.
[
1
, p. 38]
), or in other words any antisymmetric matrix in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
is isomorphic to a pseudovector

\star\!\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}=\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\quad\mbox{or}\quad\star\!\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}=\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}

(44)

Another useful fact is that
\tr(\vc{F}_i\odot\vc{F}_i^*)=\absolutevalue{\vc{F}_i}^{2}
so, together, we can write the tensor product as

{\bf\it F}_{i}\otimes{\bf\it F}_{i}^{*}=\frac{1}{2}[\mathsfbfit{{I}}_{3}\absolutevalue{\vc{F}_i}^{2}+({\bf\it F}_{i}\odot{\bf\it F}_{i}^{*}-\mathsfbfit{{I}}_{3}\absolutevalue{\vc{F}_i}^{2})+\star({\bf\it F}_{i}\crossproduct{\bf\it F}_{i}^{*})]

but this is exactly the stress tensor, spin and energy densities of field
{\bf\it F}_{i}
arranged in a single complex tensor

\lx@cleverref@save@label{eq:outer_{d}ecomposition}{\bf\it F}_{i}\otimes{\bf\it F}^{*}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}(W_{i}\mathsfbfit{{I}}_{3}+\mathsfbfit{{T}}_{i}-{\text{i}\omega}\,{\star{\bf\it S}_{i}})\,,

(45)

where
i\in\{\text{e,m,p,a,{r},{l}}\}
. Notice also, that the symmetric part is purely real

\real({\bf\it F}_{i}\otimes{\bf\it F}^{*}_{i})=\frac{1}{2}{\bf\it F}_{i}\odot{\bf\it F}_{i}^{*}=\frac{1}{2}(W_{i}\mathsfbfit{{I}}_{3}+\mathsfbfit{{T}}_{i})\,,

(46)

while the antisymmetric part is purely imaginary

\Im({\bf\it F}_{i}\otimes{\bf\it F}^{*}_{i})=\frac{1}{2\text{i}}{\bf\it F}_{i}\wedge{\bf\it F}_{i}^{*}=-\frac{\omega}{2}\,{\star{\bf\it S}_{i}}\,,

(47)

which justifies us replacing the real part of the regular tensor product with a symmetric product. This is a general behaviour of symmetric and antisymmetric products of a complex vector with its conjugate. We note that the reactive or imaginary stress tensor introduced in
[
2
]
can be written for the time-harmonic field simply as

\lx@cleverref@save@label{eq:TI}\mathsfbfit{{T}}_{I}=\Im({\bf\it F}_{\text{e}}\otimes{\bf\it F}^{*}_{\text{e}}+{\bf\it F}^{*}_{\text{m}}\otimes{\bf\it F}_{\text{m}})=-\frac{\omega}{2}\star\!(\underbrace{{\bf\it S}_{\text{e}}-{\bf\it S}_{\text{m}}}_{{\bf\it S}_{1}})=-\frac{\omega}{2}\star\!\!\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}=\frac{\omega}{2}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\,.

(48)

As a consequence of

\mathsfbfit{{T}}_{A}

\displaystyle\!\!\!({\bf\it\psi}\otimes{\bf\it\psi}^{\dagger})_{[\text{EM}]}=\frac{1}{2}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}\!\mathsfbfit{{I}}_{3}+\frac{1}{2}\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}+\frac{\omega\star}{2\text{i}}\!\pmqty{\lx@physics@matrix\endlx@physics@matrix}.

B.5
States on the surface of the energy-symmetry sphere

The main text Fig. 2 shows six special points on the surface of the symmetry sphere given by
{\bf\it F}_{i}=0
for
i=\{\text{e},\text{m},\text{p},\text{a},\textsc{r},\textsc{l}\}
. However, in principle, there is nothing special about those specific directions and the specific axes
W_{1}
,
W_{2}
,
W_{3}
defined in the main text.

In the well-known
\mathbb{C}^{2}
space of field polarisations, we typically write the electric field either in the horizontal-vertical basis,
(E_{\text{h}},E_{\text{v}})
, the diagonal-antidiagonal basis,
(E_{\text{d}},E_{\text{a}})
, or the circular basis,
(E_{\text{R}},E_{\text{L}})
, which gives rise to the three axes of the Poincare sphere
\mathcal{S}_{1}=|E_{\text{h}}|^{2}-|E_{\text{v}}|^{2}
,
\mathcal{S}_{2}=|E_{\text{d}}|^{2}-|E_{\text{a}}|^{2}
and
\mathcal{S}_{3}=|E_{\text{R}}|^{2}-|E_{\text{L}}|^{2}
, but these are not the only three possible bases. In fact, any two orthogonal polarisations can serve as a valid basis (with coefficients
E_{\alpha}
and
E_{\beta}
each being a linear combination of
E_{\text{h}}
and
E_{\text{v}}
) and correspond to an arbitrary axis
|E_{\alpha}|^{2}-|E_{\beta}|^{2}
on the Poincare sphere with some specific orientation. Similarly, our isomorphic
\mathbb{C}^{2}
space of bispinors admits infinitely many basis choices, apart from the EM, PA, RL basis, each with vector-valued coefficients
{\bf\it F}_{\alpha}
and
{\bf\it F}_{\beta}
that are each some linear combination of
\sqrt{\varepsilon}{\bf\it E}
and
\sqrt{\mu}{\bf\it H}
. In that basis one can write
{\bf\it\psi}_{[\alpha\beta]}=({\bf\it F}_{\alpha},{\bf\it F}_{\beta})
. This basis will represent an axis going through the origin of the symmetry sphere, with the axis value associated to the quantity
|{\bf\it F}_{\alpha}|^{2}-|{\bf\it F}_{\beta}|^{2}
. Reversing the argument, every axis crossing the origin of the symmetry sphere, regardless of orientation, will have some associated basis whose vector coefficients are
({\bf\it F}_{i\alpha},{\bf\it F}_{i\beta})
.

This means that every point lying exactly on the surface of the symmetry sphere (where
W_{1}^{2}+W_{2}^{2}+W_{3}^{2}=W_{0}^{2}
) corresponds to a point where a given
{\bf\it F}_{i\alpha}=0
. Because
{\bf\it F}_{i\alpha}
is always a linear combination of
\sqrt{\varepsilon}{\bf\it E}
and
\sqrt{\mu}{\bf\it H}
, the surface of the field-symmetry sphere necessarily corresponds to electromagnetic states where both quantities are linearly dependent, such that their linear combination can be zero. Hence, on the surface of the symmetry sphere,
\sqrt{\varepsilon}{\bf\it E}=\delta\sqrt{\mu}{\bf\it H}
with
\delta
a complex scalar. With some algebra, one can find that for any arbitrary location on the surface of the field-symmetry sphere, with spherical angle coordinates
(\theta_{i},\phi_{i})
measured with respect to the arbitrary axis corresponding to
|{\bf\it F}_{i\alpha}|^{2}-|{\bf\it F}_{i\beta}|^{2}
acting as polar axis, the linear dependence of the fields is given specifically by:

\lx@cleverref@save@label{eq:suppl:maximallyasymmetricfield}\sin(\theta_i/2){\bf\it F}_{i\alpha}=\text{e}^{i\phi_{i}}\cos(\theta_i/2){\bf\it F}_{i\beta},

(51)

such that the polar angle
\theta_{i}
determines the amplitude ratio, while the azimuthal angle
\phi_{i}
determines the relative phase between the basis coefficients
{\bf\it F}_{i\alpha}
and
{\bf\it F}_{i\beta}
. This relation is a well-known property of a Bloch sphere, often used in quantum superpositions of two states.

If we choose to express this in terms of
{\bf\it E}
and
{\bf\it H}
, this is equivalent to choosing the EM basis
{\bf\it\psi}=({\bf\it F}_{\text{e}},{\bf\it F}_{\text{m}})
to describe the fields and define the angles on the sphere
(\theta_{1},\phi_{1})
, such that the
W_{1}
axis plays the role of polar axis. In this case,
can be written as:

\lx@cleverref@save@label{eq:suppl:maximallyasymmetricfieldW1axis}\sin(\theta_1/2)\sqrt{\varepsilon}{\bf\it E}=\text{e}^{i\phi_{1}}\cos(\theta_1/2)\sqrt{\mu}{\bf\it H},

(52)

or, equivalently:

\lx@cleverref@save@label{eq:suppl:maximallyasymmetricfieldW1axis2}{\bf\it E}=\underbrace{\frac{\text{e}^{i\phi_{1}}}{\tan(\theta_1/2)}}_{\delta(\theta_{1},\phi_{1})}\eta{\bf\it H},

(53)

which provides the linear relationship between the
{\bf\it E}
and
{\bf\it H}
field corresponding to any point in the surface of the energy symmetry sphere. Six such special points (corresponding to the antipodes for the bases EM, PA, RL, where the sphere intersects the
W_{1}
,
W_{2}
and
W_{3}
axes) are shown in Fig. 2 of the main text.

B.6
Electromagnetic symmetry sphere for dipolar fields

Entire three-dimensional electromagnetic fields including near and far field regions can be mapped into the volume of the electromagnetic symmetry sphere, constructing complicated 3D patterns that measure the share of energy density among
W_{1,2,3}
.
In this section we map well-known dipolar fields to the symmetry sphere as well as ‘synthetic’ dipoles inspired by the vectors
{\bf\it F}_{A}
in the main article.
Each example is a superposition of an electric dipole with dipole moment
{\bf\it p}=(p_{x},p_{y},p_{z})
and a magnetic dipole with dipole moment
{\bf\it m}=(m_{x},m_{y},m_{z})
—this way, an
x
-polarised electric dipole is given by
(p_{x},p_{y},p_{z})=(1,0,0)
and
(m_{x},m_{y},m_{z})={\bf\it 0}
both superimposed at the origin. The full electromagnetic fields in free space created by such a magnetoelectric dipole are well known
[
3
]
and given in SI units by:

\lx@cleverref@save@label{eq:Efielddipole}{\bf\it E}({\bf\it r})=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}}\left\{k^{2}(\hat{{\bf\it r}}\times{\bf\it p})\times\hat{{\bf\it r}}\frac{\text{e}^{ikr}}{r}+\left[3(\hat{{\bf\it r}}\dotproduct{\bf\it p})\hat{{\bf\it r}}-{\bf\it p}\right]\left(\frac{1}{r^{3}}-\frac{ik}{r^{2}}\right)\text{e}^{ikr}\right\}-\frac{\eta k^{2}}{4\pi}(\hat{{\bf\it r}}\times{\bf\it m})\left(1-\frac{1}{ikr}\right)\frac{\text{e}^{ikr}}{r}

B.7
Maxwell-like equations with field quadratic quantities

The dual-symmetric (including magnetic charges) time-harmonic Maxwell’s equations in
, written in a more standard notation, are:

\lx@cleverref@save@label{eq:maxwelldualstandard}\begin{split}\divergence{\bf\it E}&=\rho_{\text{e}}/\varepsilon\,,\\
\divergence{\bf\it H}&=\rho_{\text{m}}/\mu\,,\\
\curl{\bf\it E}&=+\text{i}k\eta{\bf\it H}-{\bf\it J}_{\text{m}}\,,\\
\curl{\bf\it H}&=-\text{i}k{\bf\it E}/\eta+{\bf\it J}_{\text{e}}\,.\end{split}

(56)

In the main text we mention that certain combination of the spin-like quadratic quantities (
{\bf\it S}_{0},{\bf\it S}_{1},{\bf\it S}_{2},{\bf\it S}_{3}
) fulfil Maxwell-like equations. Indeed, from eq. (7) in the main text, one can check that the complex combinations
{\bf\it S}_{0}\pm\text{i}{\bf\it S}_{3}
satisfy the following equations:

\begin{split}\divergence(\vc{S}_0\pm\ii\vc{S}_3)&=0\,,\\
\curl(\vc{S}_0\pm\ii\vc{S}_3)&=\pm 2\text{i}k({\bf\it S}_{0}\mp\text{i}{\bf\it S}_{3})-2({\bf\it p}_{0}\pm\text{i}{\bf\it p}_{3})\,,\end{split}

(57)

making them exactly analogous to time harmonic electric and magnetic fields with twice the frequency of the fields and with currents but no charge densities. Also, the equivalent of duality symmetry of these quantities is simply complex conjugation or parity. One can combine these into a single Helmholtz equation

\lx@cleverref@save@label{eq:helmholtz}\!(\laplacian+4k^{2})({\bf\it S}_{0}\pm\text{i}{\bf\it S}_{3})=4k({\bf\it p}_{3}\pm\text{i}{\bf\it p}_{0})+2\gradient\crossproduct({\bf\it p}_{0}\pm\text{i}{\bf\it p}_{3})\,.\!

(58)

The remaining two spin-like quadratic quantities can also be combined into a complex quantity, however, this time they will be quasistatic fields (taking
k=0
in
and resembling electrostatic/magnetostatic fields) with both charge densities and currents

\begin{split}\divergence(\vc{S}_1\pm\ii\vc{S}_2)&=\frac{2}{c}(W_{2}\mp\text{i}W_{1})\,,\\
\curl(\vc{S}_1\pm\ii\vc{S}_2)&=-2({\bf\it p}_{1}\mp\text{i}{\bf\it p}_{2})\,,\end{split}

(59)

These can be combined into a single Poisson equation

\!(\laplacian+0k^{2})({\bf\it S}_{1}\pm\text{i}{\bf\it S}_{2})=\frac{2}{c}\gradient(W_2\mp\ii W_1)+2\curl(\vc{p}_1\mp\ii\vc{p}_2)\,.\!\!

(60)

In the same way in which
{\bf\it S}_{0}\pm\text{i}{\bf\it S}_{3}
fulfils the Helmholtz equation with twice the frequency of the fields
{\bf\it S}_{1}\pm\text{i}{\bf\it S}_{2}
fulfils the Poisson equation (Helmholtz equation with zero times the frequency).

For EM uncoupled equations,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}1+\chi_{0}=0\\

\text{free variables}\end{array}\right\}

For PA uncoupled equations,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}1+\chi_{0}=0\\
\chi_{2}=0\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\xLeftrightarrow{}\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\varepsilon_{r}+\mu_{r}=0\\

(82)

For RL uncoupled equations,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\chi_{1}=0\\
\chi_{2}=0\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\xLeftrightarrow{}\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\varepsilon_{r}-\mu_{r}=0\\

\displaystyle\xLeftrightarrow{}\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}\varepsilon_{r}({\bf\it r})=\mu_{r}({\bf\it r})\\

-and-

12

13

from
{\bf\it H}

\varepsilon_{r}({\bf\it r})=\mu_{r}({\bf\it r})
(free space being an example), which have been widely discussed as systems that do not perturb the helicity of incoming light, because the right and left-handed components of light are uncoupled
[

Inside every block of homogeneous material with constant material parameters, an electromagnetic basis in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
space can be found such that its two components are uncoupled. This could represent an interesting approach to solving electromagnetic problems.

C.5
On the choice of orthonormal basis in
\mathbb{C}^{2}

In a general material the time-averaged energy density can be written as follows
[
3
]

\lx@cleverref@save@label{eq:EMenergydensitywithEHDB}W=\frac{1}{4}\real({\bf\it D}^{\ast}\dotproduct{\bf\it E}+{\bf\it B}^{\ast}\dotproduct{\bf\it H}).

(84)

In a basis-independent way one can write this energy density using the two bispinors
{\bf\it\psi}
and
{\bf\it\gamma}
(see
) as

W=\real({\bf\it\gamma}^{\dagger}\dotproduct{\bf\it\psi}).

(85)

Note that this form should work for any arbitrary material (including chiral, non-reciprocal, anisotropic and even complex), which we will address in future work.
In the case of an arbitrary linear material
{\bf\it\gamma}=(\mathsfbfit{{I}}+\mathsfbfit{{\chi}}){\bf\it\psi}
, as a consequence we can understand this as an inner product

W=\real[{\bf\it\psi}^{\dagger}(\mathsfbfit{{I}}+\mathsfbfit{{\chi}})^{\dagger}\dotproduct{\bf\it\psi}]={\bf\it\psi}^{\dagger}\dotproduct{\bf\it\psi}+\tfrac{1}{2}({\bf\it\psi}^{\dagger}\mathsfbfit{{\chi}}^{\dagger}\dotproduct{\bf\it\psi}+{\bf\it\psi}^{\intercal}\mathsfbfit{{\chi}}^{\intercal}\dotproduct{\bf\it\psi}^{\ast})={\bf\it\psi}^{\dagger}\mathsfbfit{{g}}\dotproduct{\bf\it\psi}=\bar{{\bf\it\psi}}\dotproduct{\bf\it\psi}\equiv\norm{\vc\psi}^{2}

(86)

where the hermitian tensor
\mathsfbfit{{g}}=\mathsfbfit{{I}}+\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathsfbfit{{\chi}}+\mathsfbfit{{\chi}}^{\dagger})
can be understood as a metric tensor. At the same time the vector
\bar{{\bf\it\psi}}={\bf\it\psi}^{\dagger}\mathsfbfit{{g}}
plays role of a dual vector for
{\bf\it\psi}
and we denote it by a bar to point out similarity with the Dirac adjoint. The concept of the inner product or metric can be best understood with a simple example, consider two 2D real vectors
{\bf\it v}
and
{\bf\it w}
together with their inner product
{\bf\it v}\dotproduct{\bf\it w}={\bf\it v}^{\intercal}\mathsfbfit{{g}}{\bf\it w}
. Given a coordinate chart one can express vectors in a basis that is associated with these coordinates, however in general there are two different bases that are associated with a given coordinate system. The first one is called coordinate basis and it is made of vectors that represent the rate of change of the position with respect to the coordinate (we shall denote them
{\bf\it b}_{i}
) and the other is the orthonormal basis (we shall denote them
\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{i}={\bf\it b}_{i}/\absolutevalue{\vc{b}_i}
) and it is made of unit vectors that point in the same direction as
{\bf\it b}_{i}
. In a cartesian basis we have coordinates
(x,y)
and if the underlying manifold is not cureved then
{\bf\it b}_{x}=\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{x}=\hat{{\bf\it x}}
and same for
y
. Vectors can then be written as
{\bf\it v}=v_{x}\hat{{\bf\it x}}+v_{y}\hat{{\bf\it y}}
and similarly for
{\bf\it w}
. However this is not the case if we either choose a curvilinear coordinate system (for example polar one) or if the underlying space is curved. Consider coordinates
(r,\theta)
such that
{\bf\it r}=(x,y)=(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)
, then
{\bf\it b}_{r}=\partialderivative*{\vc{r}}{r}
and
{\bf\it b}_{\theta}=\partialderivative*{\vc{r}}{\theta}
, while
\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{r}={\bf\it b}_{r}=\hat{{\bf\it r}}
and
\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{\theta}={\bf\it b}_{\theta}/r=\hat{{\bf\it\theta}}
. One can then choose to express a vector in either of the two bases
{\bf\it v}=v_{r}{\bf\it b}_{r}+v_{\theta}{\bf\it b}_{\theta}=v_{\hat{r}}\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{r}+v_{\hat{\theta}}\hat{{\bf\it e}}_{\theta}
and similarly for
