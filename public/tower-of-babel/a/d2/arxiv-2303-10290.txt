Complete Asymptotic Expansions and the High-Dimensional Bingham Distributions
Armine Bagyan and Donald Richards

Abstract
For d ≥ 2 \displaystyle d\geq 2 , let X \displaystyle X be a random vector having a Bingham distribution on 𝒮 d − 1 \displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{d-1} , the unit sphere centered at the origin in ℝ d \displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d} , and let Σ \displaystyle\Sigma denote the symmetric matrix parameter of the distribution. Let Ψ ⁡ ( Σ ) \displaystyle\Psi(\Sigma) be the normalizing constant of the distribution and let ∇ Ψ d ​ ( Σ ) \displaystyle\nabla\Psi_{d}(\Sigma) be the matrix of first-order partial derivatives of Ψ ⁡ ( Σ ) \displaystyle\Psi(\Sigma) with respect to the entries of Σ \displaystyle\Sigma . We derive complete asymptotic expansions for Ψ ⁡ ( Σ ) \displaystyle\Psi(\Sigma) and ∇ Ψ d ​ ( Σ ) \displaystyle\nabla\Psi_{d}(\Sigma) , as d → ∞ \displaystyle d\to\infty ; these expansions are obtained subject to the growth condition that ‖ Σ ‖ \displaystyle\|\Sigma\| , the Frobenius norm of Σ \displaystyle\Sigma , satisfies ‖ Σ ‖ ≤ γ 0 ​ d r / 2 \displaystyle\|\Sigma\|\leq\gamma_{0}d^{r/2} for all d \displaystyle d , where γ 0 > 0 \displaystyle\gamma_{0}>0 and r ∈ [ 0 , 1 ) \displaystyle r\in[0,1) . Consequently, we obtain for the covariance matrix of X \displaystyle X an asymptotic expansion up to terms of arbitrary degree in Σ \displaystyle\Sigma . Using a range of values of d \displaystyle d that have appeared in a variety of applications of high-dimensional spherical data analysis we tabulate the bounds on the remainder terms in the expansions of Ψ ⁡ ( Σ ) \displaystyle\Psi(\Sigma) and ∇ Ψ d ​ ( Σ ) \displaystyle\nabla\Psi_{d}(\Sigma) and we demonstrate the rapid convergence of the bounds to zero as r \displaystyle r decreases. Keywords and phrases . Confluent hypergeometric function of matrix argument; Frobenius norm; Gradient operator; Power sum symmetric function, Zonal polynomial. 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification . 1
Introduction

Let
\displaystyle d
be a positive integer, where
\displaystyle d\geq 2
. Denote by
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
the unit sphere in
\displaystyle d
-dimensional Euclidean space,
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with center at the origin, and let
\displaystyle\thinspace\mathrm{d}x
be the normalized surface measure on
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
. For a symmetric
\displaystyle d\times d
matrix
\displaystyle\Sigma=(\sigma_{i,j})
, the
\displaystyle d\times 1
random vector
\displaystyle X=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{d})^{\prime}
is said to have a
Bingham distribution
on
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
if the probability density function of
\displaystyle X
, with respect to the surface measure
\displaystyle\thinspace\mathrm{d}x
, is

\phi(x;{\hskip-3.0pt}\Sigma)=[\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)]^{-1}\exp(x^{\prime}\Sigma x),

(1.1)

\displaystyle x\in\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
, with normalizing constant

\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)=\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\exp(x^{\prime}\Sigma x)\thinspace\mathrm{d}x.

(1.2)

These distributions were introduced by Bingham
[
4
]
, and they have become prominent since their appearance. We cite the work of Bagyan
[
2
]
, Bhattacharya and Bhattacharya
[
3
]
, Bingham, Chang, and Richards
[
5
]
, Chikuse
[
8
]
, Dryden
[
11
]
, Dryden and Mardia
[
12
]
, Fisher, Lewis, and Embleton
[
14
]
, Kume and Wood
[
19
]
, Mardia and Jupp
[
20
]
, and Patrangenaru and Ellingson
[
22
]
for various aspects of the theory of these distributions, their applications in random modulation, spherical data analysis, Procrustes analysis, shape analysis, and for many references to the literature.

One approach in the study of high-dimensional approximations for the Bingham distribution is to approximate the distribution of
\displaystyle X
and then to deduce approximations for, e.g.,
\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X)
, the covariance matrix of
\displaystyle X
; see, e.g., Chikuse
[
8
]
, Dryden
[
11
]
, and Kume and Wood
[
19
]
. In this article, starting from the zonal polynomial series for
\displaystyle\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)
, we obtain complete asymptotic expansions, i.e., series expansions in negative powers of
\displaystyle d
, for
\displaystyle\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X)
as
\displaystyle d\to\infty
. An advantage of our approach is that the resulting asymptotic expansions are obtained purely in terms of
\displaystyle\|\Sigma\|=[\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma^{2})]^{1/2}
, the Frobenius norm of
\displaystyle\Sigma
. Thus we make no assumptions about the structure of individual matrix entries of
\displaystyle\Sigma
; indeed, our only constraint on
\displaystyle\Sigma
is that
\displaystyle\|\Sigma\|\leq\gamma_{0}d^{r/2}
for all
\displaystyle d
, with universal constants
\displaystyle\gamma_{0}>0
and
\displaystyle r\in[0,1)
.

A key role is played in our derivations by masterful results of Reznick
[
23
]
, who obtained bounds on the ratios of power sum symmetric functions in several variables. Reznick’s results enable us to obtain, for series involving
\displaystyle\Sigma
, bounds which are valid for all
\displaystyle d
.

In Section
2
, we derive a complete asymptotic expansion for
\displaystyle\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)
. We obtain in Proposition
2.1
a
\displaystyle O(\cdot)
bound for the remainder of the zonal polynomial series expansion of
\displaystyle\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)
upon truncation after any given number of terms.

In Section
3
, we obtain a complete asymptotic expansion for the matrix

\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)\mathrm{Cov}(X)=\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}xx^{\prime}\exp(x^{\prime}\Sigma x)\thinspace\mathrm{d}x.

We derive in Proposition
3.2
a
\displaystyle O(\cdot)
bound for the Frobenius norm of the remainder of that series expansion after truncation at any number of terms. We obtain the asymptotic expansion for
\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X)
by multiplying together the series for
\displaystyle[\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)]^{-1}
and
\displaystyle\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)\mathrm{Cov}(X)
, and we provide in Theorem
3.3
the explicit expansion of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X)
up to terms of arbitrary degree in
\displaystyle\Sigma
. We remark that our methods can also be applied to obtain complete asymptotic expansions for higher moments of
\displaystyle X
.

In Section
4
, we describe the range of values of
\displaystyle d
that have appeared in the literature in a wide variety of applications of statistical analyses of spherical data. We compute in Tables
1
and
2
numerous values of the bounds for the remainder terms in the asymptotic expansions of
\displaystyle\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)
and
\displaystyle\nabla\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)
, the matrix gradient of
\displaystyle\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)
, and we elucidate some observed features of the tabulated values. Finally we provide all proofs in the appendix Section
A
.

2
Asymptotic expansion of the normalizing constant

Denote by
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{d\times d}
the vector space of
\displaystyle d\times d
real symmetric matrices. As with any norm on a vector space, the Frobenius norm,
\displaystyle\|\Sigma\|=[\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma^{2})]^{1/2}
, satisfies the subadditivity property,
\displaystyle\|\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2}\|\leq\|\Sigma_{1}\|+\|\Sigma_{2}\|
for all
\displaystyle\Sigma_{1},\Sigma_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{d\times d}
. The Frobenius norm is also submultiplicative:
\displaystyle\|\Sigma^{j}\|\leq\|\Sigma\|^{j}
for all
\displaystyle\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{d\times d}
and
\displaystyle j=1,2,3,\ldots

[
17
, p. 341]
.

Let
\displaystyle g_{1}
be a real-valued function of
\displaystyle d
and
\displaystyle g_{2}
be a nonnegative function of
\displaystyle d
. We use the terminology
\displaystyle g_{1}(d)=O(g_{2}(d))
as
\displaystyle d\to\infty
to signify that there exists a constant
\displaystyle\gamma>0
such that
\displaystyle|g_{1}(d)|\leq\gamma g_{2}(d)
for all sufficiently large
\displaystyle d
.

Consider the situation in which
\displaystyle\|\Sigma\|\leq\gamma_{0}
for all
\displaystyle d
. By expanding the exponential function in (
1.2
) in a Maclaurin expansion and integrating the first two terms of that series, we find that
\displaystyle\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)\approx 1+d^{-1}\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma)+O(d^{-2})
. However, if
\displaystyle\|\Sigma\|
is allowed to grow as
\displaystyle d
increases then a deeper analysis is required to derive the order of approximation of the remainder term.

Thus, our governing assumption throughout the article is that
\displaystyle\|\Sigma\|\leq\gamma_{0}d^{r/2}
for all
\displaystyle d
, with constants
\displaystyle\gamma_{0}>0
and
\displaystyle r\in[0,1)
.

For
\displaystyle a\in\mathbb{R}
, define the
rising factorial

\displaystyle(a)_{k}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+k-1)
,
\displaystyle k=0,1,2,\ldots
. The
zonal polynomial
corresponding to the partition
\displaystyle(k)
may be defined as

C_{(k)}(\Sigma)=\frac{k!}{(1/2)_{k}}\ \sum_{i_{1}+2i_{2}+3i_{3}+\cdots+ki_{k}=k}\ \prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{[\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma^{j})]^{i_{j}}}{i_{j}!\,(2j)^{i_{j}}},

(2.1)

where the multiple sum is over all nonnegative integers
\displaystyle i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}
such that
\displaystyle i_{1}+2i_{2}+3i_{3}+\cdots+ki_{k}=k
(James
[
18
, p. 493, Eq. (123)]
). In particular,

C_{(k)}(\Sigma)=\begin{cases}1,&k=0\\
\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma),&k=1\\
\frac{1}{3}\big[\big(\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma)\big)^{2}+2\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma^{2})\big],&k=2\end{cases}.

(2.2)

It is well-known that
\displaystyle\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)
, the normalizing constant in (
1.2
), can be expressed in terms of a confluent hypergeometric function of matrix argument. We refer to Bingham
[
4
]
for the case
\displaystyle d=3
and to Muirhead
[
21
, p. 288, Example 7.8]
for the general case. The confluent hypergeometric function of matrix argument can be expressed as an infinite series of zonal polynomials, leading to the result,

\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)={}_{1}F_{1}(1/2;d/2;\Sigma)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(1/2)_{k}}{(d/2)_{k}}\frac{C_{(k)}(\Sigma)}{k!};

(2.3)

see James
[
18
, p. 477]
, Muirhead
[
21
, Chapter 7]
. The series (
2.3
) converges absolutely for each
\displaystyle\Sigma
and uniformly on compact regions (Gross and Richards
[
15
, Theorem 6.3]
).

For
\displaystyle m\geq 1
we will use as an asymptotic approximation to
\displaystyle\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)
the sum of the first
\displaystyle m
terms of the series (
2.3
),

\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)\approx\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{(1/2)_{k}}{(d/2)_{k}}\frac{C_{(k)}(\Sigma)}{k!}.

(2.4)

In studying the growth rate of the remainder series,

R_{m}(\Sigma)=\sum_{k=m}^{\infty}\frac{(1/2)_{k}}{(d/2)_{k}}\frac{C_{(k)}(\Sigma)}{k!},

(2.5)

we shall encounter the constants

\gamma_{1}=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{3})\simeq 1.3660,\ \gamma_{2}=\gamma_{0}\gamma_{1},\ \hbox{and}\ \gamma_{3}=2^{3/2}e^{1/2}/\gamma_{1}\simeq 3.4138.

(2.6)

Then we shall establish the following result.

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle\|\Sigma\|\leq\gamma_{0}d^{r/2}
, where
\displaystyle\gamma_{0}>0
and
\displaystyle 0\leq r<1
. Then, for all
\displaystyle m\geq 1
and all
\displaystyle d\geq(2\gamma_{2}^{2})^{1/(1-r)}
,

|R_{m}(\Sigma)|\leq\frac{\gamma_{3}\gamma_{2}^{m}}{[(m+1)!]^{1/2}}d^{-m(1-r)/2}.

(2.7)

In particular,
\displaystyle R_{m}(\Sigma)=O\big(d^{-m(1-r)/2}\big)
as
\displaystyle d\to\infty
.

We note that a consequence of the inequality (
2.7
) is that
\displaystyle R_{m}(\Sigma)
converges to zero, i.e., the accuracy of the approximation (
2.4
) increases, as
\displaystyle d
increases (with
\displaystyle m
held fixed) or as
\displaystyle m
increases (for fixed
\displaystyle d
). The upper bound (
2.7
) also increases as
\displaystyle\gamma_{0}
or
\displaystyle r
increases; this is consistent with the fact that the condition
\displaystyle\|\Sigma\|\leq\gamma_{0}d^{r/2}
entails an increasingly larger matrix region as
\displaystyle\gamma_{0}
or
\displaystyle r
increases.

As a consequence of Proposition
2.1
, we obtain the following asymptotic expansion for
\displaystyle[\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)]^{-1}
.

Corollary 2.2
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle\|\Sigma\|\leq\gamma_{0}d^{r/2}
, where
\displaystyle\gamma_{0}>0
and
\displaystyle 0\leq r<1
. Then for all
\displaystyle l\geq 2
and all
\displaystyle d>(6\gamma_{2}^{2})^{1/(1-r)}
,

[\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)]^{-1}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}\frac{(1/2)_{j}}{(d/2)_{j}}\frac{C_{(j)}(\Sigma)}{j!}+O(d^{-(1-r)}).

(2.8)

3
Asymptotic expansion of the covariance matrix

It is well-known that
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{d\times d}
, the vector space of symmetric matrices, is an inner product space with the natural inner product
\displaystyle\langle H_{1},H_{2}\rangle=\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(H_{1}H_{2})
,
\displaystyle H_{1},H_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{d\times d}
. This inner product is also related to the Frobenius norm through the identity
\displaystyle\langle H,H\rangle=\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(H^{2})=\|H\|^{2}
,
\displaystyle H\in\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{d\times d}
.

Let
\displaystyle C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{d\times d})
be the set of all functions
\displaystyle f:\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{d\times d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that all first-order partial derivatives,
\displaystyle\partial f(\Sigma)/\partial\sigma_{i,j}
, are continuous, where
\displaystyle\Sigma=(\sigma_{i,j})
. Let
\displaystyle\delta_{i,j}
denote Kronecker’s delta, i.e.,
\displaystyle\delta_{i,j}=1
or
\displaystyle 0
according as
\displaystyle i=j
or
\displaystyle i\neq j
, respectively; then we define
\displaystyle\nabla
, the
gradient operator
, by

\nabla f(\Sigma)=\bigg(\tfrac{1}{2}(1+\delta_{i,j})\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma_{i,j}}f(\Sigma)\bigg).

The operator
\displaystyle\nabla
has appeared in various articles in multivariate analysis, e.g., Hadjicosta and Richards
[
16
]
, Richards
[
24
]
, Sebastiani
[
25
]
, and it arises in the theory of Taylor expansions on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{d\times d}
, as follows: For
\displaystyle\Sigma,H\in\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{d\times d}
and
\displaystyle f\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{d\times d})
, there holds the first-order Taylor expansion,

f(\Sigma+H)=f(\Sigma)+\langle H,\nabla f(\Sigma)\rangle+O(\|H\|^{2}),\qquad\|H\|\to 0.

(3.1)

Turning to the covariance matrix of the Bingham-distributed random vector
\displaystyle X
, it follows from (
1.1
) that
\displaystyle X
has the same distribution as
\displaystyle-X
; therefore, by symmetry,
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(X)=0
. Hence
\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X)
, the covariance matrix of
\displaystyle X
, equals
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(XX^{\prime})
. By (
3.2
) and a straightforward justification of the interchange of derivatives and integral, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=[\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)]^{-1}\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}xx^{\prime}\exp(x^{\prime}\Sigma x)\thinspace\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\displaystyle=[\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)]^{-1}\nabla\int_{\mathcal{S}^{d-1}}\exp\big(\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma xx^{\prime})\big)\thinspace\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\displaystyle=[\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)]^{-1}\nabla\Psi_{d}(\Sigma).

Our asymptotic approximations to the matrix
\displaystyle\nabla\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)
through term-by-term differentiation of the zonal polynomial series (
2.3
) will be justified by means of bounds for
\displaystyle\|\nabla C_{(k)}(\Sigma)\|
, the Frobenius norm of the matrix
\displaystyle\nabla C_{(k)}(\Sigma)
. In order to calculate bounds for each
\displaystyle\nabla C_{(k)}(\Sigma)
, we will need the following properties of the gradient operator.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle H
be a
\displaystyle d\times d
symmetric matrix. Then

\nabla\exp\big(\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma H)\big)=H\exp\big(\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma H)\big).

(3.2)

Also, for
\displaystyle k=0,1,2,\ldots
,

\nabla[\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma)]^{k}=k[\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma)]^{k-1}I_{d}

(3.3)

and

\nabla\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma^{k})=k\Sigma^{k-1}.

(3.4)

In the sequel, we will obtain asymptotic approximations of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X)
involving terms of arbitrary degree in
\displaystyle\Sigma
. In deriving those results we note that, as a consequence of (
2.1
), (
3.3
), and (
3.4
), the matrix
\displaystyle\nabla C_{(k)}(\Sigma)
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
\displaystyle k-1
for
\displaystyle k\geq 1
.

For
\displaystyle m\geq 2
, we use the asymptotic approximation

\nabla\Psi_{d}(\Sigma)\approx\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\frac{(1/2)_{k}}{(d/2)_{k}}\frac{\nabla C_{(k)}(\Sigma)}{k!}.

(3.5)

Note that we necessarily impose the condition
\displaystyle m\geq 2
since
\displaystyle\nabla C_{(0)}(\Sigma)=0
, the zero matrix.

Following on the results in Section
2
, we naturally define

\nabla R_{m}(\Sigma)=\sum_{k=m}^{\infty}\frac{(1/2)_{k}}{(d/2)_{k}}\frac{\nabla C_{(k)}(\Sigma)}{k!}.

To establish the accuracy of the matrix approximation (
3.5
), we shall derive the convergence properties as
\displaystyle d\to\infty
(and also as
\displaystyle m\to\infty
) of
\displaystyle\nabla R_{m}(\Sigma)
. As a consequence of those results, we deduce that
\displaystyle\nabla R_{m}(\Sigma)
is well-defined.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose that
\displaystyle\|\Sigma\|\leq\gamma_{0}d^{r/2}
, where
\displaystyle\gamma_{0}>0
and
\displaystyle 0\leq r<1
. Then, for all
\displaystyle m\geq 2
and all
\displaystyle d\geq(2\gamma_{2}^{2})^{1/(1-r)}
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\|\nabla R_{m}(\Sigma)\|\leq\frac{(2e)^{1/2}\,\gamma_{2}^{m-1}}{[(m-1)!]^{1/2}}d^{-[1+(m-1)(1-r)]/2}.

(3.6)

In particular,

\displaystyle\|\nabla R_{m}(\Sigma)\|=O\big(d^{-[1+(m-1)(1-r)]/2}\big)
as
\displaystyle d\to\infty
.

As a consequence of (
3.6
), the accuracy of the approximation (
3.5
) increases as
\displaystyle d\to\infty
(with
\displaystyle m
held fixed) or as
\displaystyle m
increases (with
\displaystyle d
held fixed). Also, as with (
2.7
), the bound in (
3.6
) increases as
\displaystyle\gamma_{0}
or
\displaystyle r
increases.

We now obtain an asymptotic expansion of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X)
up to terms of arbitrary degree in
\displaystyle\Sigma
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose that the random vector
\displaystyle X
has a Bingham distribution with the probability density function (
1.1
). Suppose also that
\displaystyle\|\Sigma\|\leq\gamma_{0}d^{r/2}
, where
\displaystyle\gamma_{0}>0
and
\displaystyle 0\leq r<1
. Then for all
\displaystyle l,m\geq 2
and all
\displaystyle d>(6\gamma_{2}^{2})^{1/(1-r)}
,

\mathrm{Cov}(X)=\bigg(1-\sum_{j=1}^{l-1}\frac{(1/2)_{j}}{(d/2)_{j}}\frac{C_{(j)}(\Sigma)}{j!}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\frac{(1/2)_{k}}{(d/2)_{k}}\frac{\nabla C_{(k)}(\Sigma)}{k!}\bigg)+O(d^{-\alpha}),

(3.7)

where

\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}\min\{3-2r,1+(m-1)(1-r)\}=\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{2}(2-r),&m=2\\
\tfrac{1}{2}(3-2r),&m\geq 3\end{cases}.

As we showed before,
\displaystyle C_{(j)}(\Sigma)
and
\displaystyle\nabla C_{(k)}(\Sigma)
are homogeneous polynomials of degrees
\displaystyle j-1
and
\displaystyle k-1
, respectively. Therefore the highest degree of
\displaystyle\Sigma
appearing in (
3.7
) equals
\displaystyle l-1+m-2=l+m-3
.

Consider the asymptotic expansion of
\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X)
arising from (
3.7
) with
\displaystyle l=2
and
\displaystyle m=3
. Applying (
3.3
), the rules for derivatives of polynomials in
\displaystyle\Sigma
, to (
2.2
), the formulas for the zonal polynomials
\displaystyle C_{(1)}(\Sigma)
and
\displaystyle C_{(2)}(\Sigma)
, we have

\nabla C_{(1)}(\Sigma)=\nabla\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma=I_{d}

and

\nabla C_{(2)}(\Sigma)=\frac{1}{3}\nabla[(\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma)^{2}+2\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma^{2})]=\frac{2}{3}[(\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma)I_{d}+2\Sigma].

Substituting these expressions into (
3.7
) and collecting terms we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}(X)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\Big(1-\frac{1}{d}\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma)\Big)\Big(\frac{1}{d}I_{d}+\frac{1}{d(d+2)}[(\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma)I_{d}+2\Sigma]\Big)+O(d^{-(3-2r)/2})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}I_{d}-\frac{2}{(d+2)}(\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma)I_{d}+\frac{2}{d(d+2)}\Sigma

\displaystyle\displaystyle\quad-\frac{1}{d^{2}(d+2)}(\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma)^{2}I_{d}-\frac{2}{d^{2}(d+2)}(\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma)\Sigma+O(d^{-(3-2r)/2}).

In some articles it was assumed, without loss of generality, that
\displaystyle\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma)=0
; cf.,Kume and Walker
[
19
]
. In that case, the above approximation reduces to

\mathrm{Cov}(X)=\frac{1}{d}I_{d}+\frac{2}{d(d+2)}\Sigma+O(d^{-(3-2r)/2}).

4
Implications for applications

The results derived in Sections
2
and
3
provide explicit bounds on the remainder terms in asymptotic expansions, for large values of
\displaystyle d
, of the normalizing constant and the covariance matrix of the Bingham distribution on
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{d-1}
. These results are, to the best of our knowledge, the first such derivations in the literature, so we now comment on the implications of those bounds for real-world applications.

To assess the implications of our results for applications, we surveyed the literature on the Bingham distributions in a search for the range of reported values of
\displaystyle d
. Dryden
[
11
]
applied the high-dimensional Bingham distribution with
\displaystyle d=62,501
to model cortical surfaces using magnetic resonance images of the human brain. A related study by Brignell,
et al.

[
6
]
applied principal components analysis to reduce the data to dimensions up to
\displaystyle 20
.

Sra
[
26
]
reviewed applications of high-dimensional spherical data analyses in machine learning. Those areas include text clustering, gene expression data analysis, feature extraction, and wireless communications, with dimensions up to
\displaystyle d=1,000
. Brombin, Pesarin, and Salmaso
[
7
]
discussed examples in the study of shape modeling, and simulations for which the dimension of the data is as large as
\displaystyle d=50
.

Dai, Dorman, Dutta, and Maitra
[
10
]
applied spherical data analysis to the characterization of changes in cerebral blood-flow when the human brain is not subject to any stimuli or tasks, to the assessment of variability in handwritten numerical digits, and to the identification of genetic pathways that underlie cancer. Those topics involve dimensions ranging from
\displaystyle d=20
to
\displaystyle d=100
.

To summarize, the articles that we reviewed described applications with dimensions ranging from small,
\displaystyle d=20
, to very large,
\displaystyle d=62,501
.

Proof of Proposition

:
By (

),

|R_{m}(\Sigma)|\leq\sum_{k=m}^{\infty}\frac{(1/2)_{k}}{(d/2)_{k}}\frac{|C_{(k)}(\Sigma)|}{k!},

(A.1)

and by (
2.1
),

|C_{(k)}(\Sigma)|\leq\frac{k!}{(1/2)_{k}}\ \sum_{i_{1}+2i_{2}+3i_{3}+\cdots+ki_{k}=k}\ \prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{|\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma^{j})|^{i_{j}}}{i_{j}!\,(2j)^{i_{j}}}.

(A.2)

For each vector of indices
\displaystyle(i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k})
such that
\displaystyle i_{1}+2i_{2}+\cdots+ki_{k}=k
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{k}|\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma^{j})|^{i_{j}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\|\Sigma\|^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{|\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma^{j})|^{i_{j}}}{\|\Sigma\|^{ji_{j}}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\|\Sigma\|^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{|\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma^{j})|^{i_{j}}}{(\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma^{2}))^{ji_{j}/2}}.

(A.3)

Denote by
\displaystyle\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
the eigenvalues of
\displaystyle\Sigma
. Then for each
\displaystyle j=1,\ldots,k
,

|\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma^{j})|^{i_{j}}=\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}^{j}\bigg|^{i_{j}}\leq\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\lambda_{i}|^{j}\bigg)^{i_{j}},

and by substituting this bound into (
A.1
) we obtain

\prod_{j=1}^{k}|\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma^{j})|^{i_{j}}\leq\|\Sigma\|^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\lambda_{i}|^{j}\right)^{i_{j}}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\lambda_{i}|^{2}\right)^{ji_{j}/2}}.

(A.4)

By a remarkable result of Reznick
[
23
, p. 447, eq. (2.12)]
we obtain, for each
\displaystyle j=1,\ldots,k
and all
\displaystyle d
,

\frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\lambda_{i}|^{j}\right)^{i_{j}}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\lambda_{i}|^{2}\right)^{ji_{j}/2}}\leq d^{\max\{0,(2-j)i_{j}/2\}}=\begin{cases}d^{i_{1}/2},&j=1\\
1,&j\geq 2\end{cases}.

(A.5)

Substituting the bound in (
A.5
) into (
A.4
), we obtain

\prod_{j=1}^{k}|\hskip 1.0pt\mathrm{tr}\,(\Sigma^{j})|^{i_{j}}\leq d^{i_{1}/2}\|\Sigma\|^{k}\equiv d^{i_{1}/2}\|\Sigma\|^{i_{1}+2i_{2}+3i_{3}+\cdots+ki_{k}}.

(A.6)

On applying (
A.6
) to (
A.2
) we obtain

|C_{(k)}(\Sigma)|\leq\frac{k!}{(1/2)_{k}}a_{k}\|\Sigma\|^{k},

(A.7)

(A.8)

We will derive in Lemma
A.1
a single-sum expression for the multiple sum
\displaystyle a_{k}
, and then we will deduce from that single-sum the inequality

a_{k}\leq\frac{(d^{1/2}/2)_{k}}{k!}.

(A.9)

Substituting (
A.9

A.7
) and recalling that
\displaystyle\|\Sigma\|\leq\gamma_{0}d^{r/2}
for all
\displaystyle d
, we obtain

(A.10)

Inserting (
A.10
) into (
A.1
) we obtain

|R_{m}(\Sigma)|\leq\sum_{k=m}^{\infty}\frac{(d^{1/2}/2)_{k}}{(d/2)_{k}}\frac{\gamma_{0}^{k}d^{rk/2}}{k!}.

(A.11)

As we shall show in Lemma
A.2
, with
\displaystyle\gamma_{1}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\sqrt{3}+1)\simeq 1.366025

\frac{(d^{1/2}/2)_{k}}{(d/2)_{k}}\leq\gamma_{1}^{k-1}((k-1)!)^{1/2}d^{-k/2}

(A.12)

for all
\displaystyle d\geq 1
,
\displaystyle k\geq 1
. Letting
\displaystyle\gamma_{2}=\gamma_{1}\gamma_{0}
and substituting (
A.12

A.11

\displaystyle d

|R_{m}(\Sigma)|\leq\gamma_{1}^{-1}\sum_{k=m}^{\infty}\frac{((k-1)!)^{1/2}}{k!}\,\gamma_{2}^{k}d^{-k(1-r)/2}.

(A.13)

By applying the ratio test, we find that the series (

) converges absolutely for all fixed
\displaystyle d
.

Note that although the
\displaystyle k
th term in (

\displaystyle O(d^{-k(1-r)/2})
, it is not evident that the sum of the resulting series is
\displaystyle O(d^{-m(1-r)/2})
as
\displaystyle d\to\infty
. To establish that stated convergence rate we apply to (

\displaystyle\displaystyle|R_{m}(\Sigma)|

\displaystyle\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{1}^{-1}\Big(\sum_{k=m}^{\infty}\frac{(k-1)!}{(k!)^{2}}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{k=m}^{\infty}\gamma_{2}^{2k}d^{-k(1-r)}\Big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\gamma_{1}^{-1}\Big(\sum_{k=m}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k\cdot k!}\Big)^{1/2}\cdot\gamma_{2}^{m}d^{-m(1-r)/2}\big(1-\gamma_{2}^{2}d^{-(1-r)}\big)^{-1/2}.

(A.14)

For
\displaystyle k\geq m
, it is straightforward that

\frac{m}{k}\leq 1\leq\binom{k}{m}\equiv\frac{k!}{m!(k-m)!},

equivalently,

\frac{1}{k\cdot k!}\leq\frac{1}{m\cdot m!}\cdot\frac{1}{(k-m)!},

also that

\frac{1}{m}\leq\frac{2}{m+1}.

Therefore

\frac{1}{k\cdot k!}\leq\frac{1}{m\cdot m!}\cdot\frac{1}{(k-m)!}\leq\frac{2}{(m+1)!}\cdot\frac{1}{(k-m)!}.

Summing this inequality over all
\displaystyle k\geq m
we obtain

\sum_{k=m}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k\cdot k!}\leq\frac{2}{(m+1)!}\sum_{k=m}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(k-m)!}=\frac{2e}{(m+1)!}.

(A.15)

\big(1-\gamma_{2}^{2}d^{-(1-r)}\big)^{-1}\leq 2,

(A.16)

and applying (
A.15
) and (
A.16
) to (

) we obtain, for all

(A.17)

This establishes (

) and proves that
\displaystyle R_{m}(\Sigma)=O(d^{-m(1-r)/2})
as
\displaystyle d\to\infty
, and the proof of the proposition now is complete.

First, we establish the inequality (

Lemma A.1
.

For
\displaystyle k=0,1,2,\ldots
,

a_{k}=\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{\big((d^{1/2}-1)/2\big)^{l}}{l!}\frac{(1/2)_{k-l}}{(k-l)!}\leq\frac{(d^{1/2}/2)_{k}}{k!}.

(A.31)

Proof.
First, we follow the approach of Comtet
[
9
, p. 97, Eq. 2d]
to derive an explicit formula for
\displaystyle a_{k}
.

Let
\displaystyle t=(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3},\ldots)
be a vector of indeterminates, and define

a_{k}(t)=\sum_{i_{1}+2i_{2}+3i_{3}+\cdots+ki_{k}=k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{t_{j}^{i_{j}}}{i_{j}!\,(2j)^{i_{j}}},

\displaystyle k\geq 0
. For an indeterminate
\displaystyle u
, a formal generating-function for the sequence
\displaystyle\{a_{k}(t),k=0,1,2,\ldots\}
is

\displaystyle\displaystyle G_{t}(u)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}(t)u^{k}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}u^{k}\sum_{i_{1}+2i_{2}+3i_{3}+\cdots+ki_{k}=k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{t_{j}^{i_{j}}}{i_{j}!\,(2j)^{i_{j}}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{i_{1}+2i_{2}+3i_{3}+\cdots+ki_{k}=k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{t_{j}^{i_{j}}u^{ji_{j}}}{i_{j}!\,(2j)^{i_{j}}}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\bigg(\sum_{i_{j}=0}^{\infty}\frac{t_{j}^{i_{j}}u^{ji_{j}}}{i_{j}!\,(2j)^{i_{j}}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\exp\Big(\frac{t_{j}u^{j}}{2j}\Big)=\exp\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{t_{j}u^{j}}{2j}\bigg).

Now set
\displaystyle t_{1}=d^{1/2}
and
\displaystyle t_{j}=1
for all
\displaystyle j\geq 2
. Then
\displaystyle a_{k}(t)
reduces to
\displaystyle a_{k}
;
\displaystyle G_{t}(u)
reduces to
\displaystyle G(u)
, the formal generating-function for the sequence
\displaystyle\{a_{k}\}
; and we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle G(u)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}u^{k}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\exp\bigg(\tfrac{1}{2}d^{1/2}u+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=2}^{\infty}\frac{u^{j}}{j}\bigg)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\exp\bigg(\tfrac{1}{2}(d^{1/2}-1)u+\tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{u^{j}}{j}\bigg)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\exp\big(\tfrac{1}{2}(d^{1/2}-1)u-\tfrac{1}{2}\log(1-u)\big)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\exp\big((d^{1/2}-1)u/2\big)\cdot(1-u)^{-1/2}.

Expanding both of the latter functions in infinite series, we obtain

\displaystyle\displaystyle G(u)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{\big((d^{1/2}-1)/2\big)^{l}}{l!}u^{l}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(1/2)_{m}}{m!}u^{m}\bigg)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}u^{k}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{\big((d^{1/2}-1)/2\big)^{l}}{l!}\frac{(1/2)_{k-l}}{(k-l)!}.

By comparing the coefficients of
\displaystyle u^{k}
in
\displaystyle G_{t}(u)
and
\displaystyle G(u)
, we obtain the equality in (
A.31
).

Since
\displaystyle\big((d^{1/2}-1)/2\big)^{l}\leq\big((d^{1/2}-1)/2\big)_{l}
then, by applying the well-known convolution identity,

\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{(v_{1})_{l}}{l!}\frac{(v_{2})_{k-l}}{(k-l)!}=\frac{(v_{1}+v_{2})_{k}}{k!},

\displaystyle v_{1},v_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, we obtain

a_{k}\leq\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{\big((d^{1/2}-1)/2\big)_{l}}{l!}\frac{(1/2)_{k-l}}{(k-l)!}=\frac{(d^{1/2}/2)_{k}}{k!}.

The proof of (
A.31
) now is complete.
