Abstract
In this expository paper, we illustrate two explicit methods which lead to special L L -values of certain modular forms admitting complex multiplication (CM), motivated in part by properties of L L -functions obtained from Calabi–Yau manifolds defined over ℚ \mathbb{Q} .

Introduction

In arithmetic geometry, the study of
L
-functions plays an important role as demonstrated by the well-known Birch and Swinnerton–Dyer (BSD) conjecture which connects the purely algebraic object, the algebraic rank of an elliptic curve
E
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, with a purely analytic object, the analytic rank of the Hasse–Weil
L
-function
L(E,s)
of
E
at the center of the critical strip. It further provides a detailed description of the leading coefficient of
L(E,s)
expanded at this point. In this expository paper, we will explore how to use the theory of modular forms and hypergeometric functions to compute special
L
-values of modular forms. The cases we compute all have a common background of Calabi–Yau manifolds admitting complex multiplication (CM).

A Calabi–Yau manifold
X
of dimension
d
is a simply connected complex algebraic variety with trivial canonical bundle and vanishing
i
th cohomology group
H^{i}(X,{\mathcal{O}}_{X})
for
0<i<d
. When
d=1
,
X
is an elliptic curve; when
d=2
,
X
is a K3 surface. A few papers of Noriko Yui, such as
[
12
,
44
]
, are devoted to the modularity of Calabi–Yau manifolds
X
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
. More precisely, the action of the absolute Galois group
G_{\mathbb{Q}}
of
\mathbb{Q}
on the transcendental part of the middle étale cohomology
H^{d}_{\rm et}(X,\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})
should correspond to an automorphic representation according to Langlands’ philosophy. In particular, when the Galois representation is
2
-dimensional, it should correspond to a classical modular form of weight
d+1
so that both have the same associated
L
-functions. Recently, in
[
23
]
the second and third authors with Noriko Yui and Wadim Zudilin studied 14 truncated hypergeometric series that are related to weight-4 cuspidal eigenforms arising from rigid Calabi–Yau threefolds defined over
\mathbb{Q}
by (super)congruences. One of the 14 modular forms in
[
23
]
has CM, that is,
f
is invariant under the twist by a quadratic character associated to an imaginary quadratic extension
K
of
\mathbb{Q}
. In this case there is an idèle class character
\chi=\chi(f)
of
K
with algebraic type
d+1
such that
L(f,s)=L\big(\chi,s-\frac{d}{2}\big)
. See
[
22
, Chapter 7, Section 4]
by the first author for more detail.

As explained in
[
10
]
by Deligne, for a weight-
k
modular form
f(\tau)
, the values of the attached
L
-function
L(f,s)
at the integers within its critical strip, namely
L(f,n+1)
for
0\leq n\leq k-2
, are of special interest, called the periods of
f
. Precisely, these values can be expressed as (cf.
[
20
]
)

\displaystyle L(f,n+1)=\frac{(2\pi)^{n+1}}{n!}\int_{0}^{\infty}f({\rm i}t)t^{n}{\rm d}t,\qquad 0\leq n\leq k-2.

(1.1)

Though literally
L(f,n+1)
can be computed as a line integral, the computation can be unraveled as an iterated integral. We will illustrate this idea through examples. As these are periods of modular forms, the theory of modular forms plays a central role. Additionally, the theory of hypergeometric functions provides helpful perspectives for obtaining exact values. Our main results below are motivated in part
by the Clausen formula for hypergeometric functions (see (
2.9
)), which expresses the square of a one-parameter family of integrals in terms of a one-parameter family of iterated double integrals.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\eta(\tau)
be the Dedekind eta function. Let
\psi
be the idèle class character of
\mathbb{Q}\big(\sqrt{-1}\big)
such that
L(\psi,s-1/2)
is the Hasse–Weil
L
-function of the CM elliptic curve
E_{1}\colon y^{4}+x^{2}=1
of conductor
32
. Then

\displaystyle 2L(\psi,{1/2})^{2}=L\big(\psi^{2},{1}\big).

In terms of cusp forms with CM by
\mathbb{Q}\big(\sqrt{-1}\big)
, the above identity can be restated as

\displaystyle 2L\big(\eta(4\tau)^{2}\eta(8\tau)^{2},1\big)^{2}=L\big(\eta(4\tau)^{6},2\big),

where
\eta(4\tau)^{2}\eta(8\tau)^{2}
is the weight-
2
level
32
cuspidal eigenform corresponding to
\psi
, and
\eta(4\tau)^{6}
is the weight-
3
level
16
cuspidal eigenform corresponding to
\psi^{2}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\chi
be the idèle class character of
\mathbb{Q}\big(\sqrt{-3}\big)
such that
L(\chi,s-1/2)
is the Hasse–Weil
L
-function of the CM elliptic curve
E_{2}\colon x^{3}+y^{3}=1/4
of conductor
36
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}L(\chi,1/2)^{2}=L\big(\chi^{2},1\big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\frac{8}{3}}L(\chi,1/2)^{3}=L\big(\chi^{3},3/2\big).

These identities can be reformulated in terms of cusp forms with CM by
\mathbb{Q}\big(\sqrt{-3}\big)
as

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}L\big(\eta(6\tau)^{4},1\big)^{2}=L\big(\eta(2\tau)^{3}\eta(6\tau)^{3},2\big)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{8}{3}L\big(\eta(6\tau)^{4},1\big)^{3}=L\big(\eta(3\tau)^{8},3\big).

Here
\eta(6\tau)^{4}
is the level
36
weight-
2
cuspidal Hecke eigenform corresponding to
\chi
,
\eta(2\tau)^{3}\eta(6\tau)^{3}
is the level
12
weight-
3
Hecke eigenform corresponding to
\chi^{2}
, and
\eta(3\tau)^{8}
is the weight-
4
Hecke eigenform of level
9
corresponding to
\chi^{3}
.

In connection with Calabi–Yau manifolds explained above, the two weight-2 forms
\eta(4\tau)^{2}\eta(8\tau)^{2}
and
\eta(6\tau)^{4}
come from the elliptic curves
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, respectively. The weight-3 form
\eta(4\tau)^{6}
arises from any one of the elliptic K3 surfaces labeled by
\mathscr{A}
in
[
37
]
by Stienstra and Beukers. One of the defining equations is
y^{2}+\big(1-t^{2}\big)xy-t^{2}y=x^{3}-t^{2}x^{2}
. The relation between such a K3 surface and the elliptic curve
y^{4}+x^{2}=1
is through the Shioda–Inose structure described in
[
35
]
. In
[
37
]
Stienstra and Beukers showed that the monodromy group
\Gamma_{2}
of the elliptic fiberation of
\mathscr{A}
is isomorphic to an index-2 subgroup of the congruence group
\Gamma_{1}(5)
. The group
\Gamma_{2}
itself is a noncongruence group. See
[
2
]
by Atkin and the first two authors for explicit congruence relations between the unique normalized weight-3 cusp form for
\Gamma_{2}
and the congruence cusp form
\eta(4\tau)^{6}
.

Similarly, one of the algebraic varieties corresponding to the weight-3 form
\eta(2\tau)^{3}\eta(6\tau)^{3}
is the elliptic K3 surface
y^{2}+\big(1-3t^{2}\big)xy-t^{4}\big(t^{2}-1\big)y=x^{3}
(labeled by
\mathscr{C}
in
[
37
]
). This K3 surface is related to
E_{2}
also via the Shioda–Inose structure. The symmetric square
L
-function of
\eta(6\tau)^{4}
is
L\big(\eta(2\tau)^{3}\eta(6\tau)^{3},s\big)L(\chi_{-3},s-1)
and similarly the symmetric square
L
-function of
\eta(4\tau)^{2}\eta(8\tau)^{2}
is
L\big(\eta(4\tau)^{6},s\big)L(\chi_{-1},s-1)
. Here
\chi_{-d}
denotes the quadratic character attached to the imaginary quadratic extension
\mathbb{Q}\big(\sqrt{-d}\big)
of
\mathbb{Q}
. A Calabi–Yau threefold corresponding to the weight-4 modular form
\eta(3\tau)^{8}
via modularity is defined by
X_{1}^{3}+X_{2}^{3}+X_{3}^{3}+X_{4}^{3}-4X_{5}X_{6}=0
,
X_{5}^{4}+2X_{6}^{2}-3X_{1}X_{2}X_{3}X_{4}=0
, see
[
23
]
. The symmetric cube
L
-function of
\eta(6\tau)^{4}
is
L\big(\eta(3\tau)^{8},s\big)L\big(\eta(6\tau)^{4},s-1\big)
.

Our guiding philosophy is in line with the special values of the Riemann zeta function:

\displaystyle\zeta(2n)={\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{1}{m^{2n}}=(-1)^{n+1}\frac{B_{2n}\cdot(2\pi)^{2n}}{2(2n)!},

where
B_{n}
denotes the
n
th Bernoulli number. The Bernoullli numbers satisfy Kummer congruences which are important for the development of
p
-adic modular forms. This was extended to the ring of Gaussian integers by Hurwitz who showed that for any positive integer
k
, the numbers

\displaystyle N(k):=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}}\frac{1}{\big(m+n\sqrt{-1}\big)^{4k}}=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}}\frac{\big(m-n\sqrt{-1}\big)^{4k}}{\big(m^{2}+n^{2}\big)^{4k}}

satisfy

\displaystyle N(k)=N(1)^{k}\cdot(\text{a rational number}).

See Hurwitz’s paper
[
17
]
or the excellent expository paper
[
21
]
by Lee, R. Murty, and Park in which they reproved Hurwitz’s result using the fact that for any quadratic imaginary number
\tau
, there is a transcendental number
b_{\mathbb{Q}(\tau)}
, depending only on the field
\mathbb{Q}(\tau)
(see (
2.1
), the Chowla–Selberg formula), such that for any integral weight-
k
modular form
f
with algebraic coefficients,

\displaystyle f(\tau)/b_{\mathbb{Q}(\tau)}^{k}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}},

(see
[
45
, Proposition 26]
by Zagier).

In Hurwitz theorem,
N(k)
is the Eisenstein series (see
[
45
, Section 2.2]
by Zagier)

\displaystyle G_{4k}(\tau):={\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}}\frac{1}{(m+n\tau)^{4k}}=\zeta(4k)E_{4k},\qquad E_{4k}(\tau)=1-\frac{8k}{B_{4k}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^{4k-1}q^{n}}{1-q^{n}},

where
q={\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}\tau}
, evaluated at
\tau=\sqrt{-1}
. Along the same vein, in
[
40
]
Rodriguez-Villegas and Zagier consider the Hecke character
\varphi
corresponding to an elliptic curve admitting CM by the imaginary quadratic field
\mathbb{Q}\big(\sqrt{-7}\big)
and they obtain a very nice formula relating the central values of the
L
-functions of
\varphi^{2k+1}
.

A special case of Damerell’s result (see
[
8
,
9
,
42
]
) says that given a CM elliptic curve defined over
\mathbb{Q}
with the corresponding idèle class character
\rho
, for each positive integer
n
there is a rational number
C_{\rho,n}
such that

\displaystyle L\big(\rho^{n},n/2\big)=C_{\rho,n}L(\rho,1/2)^{n}.

It has been found that these numbers are generalizations of Bernoulli numbers with important arithmetic meanings (
[
5
]
by Coates and Wiles and
[
42
]
by Yager). See
[
24
]
by Manin,
[
33
]
by Shimura,
[
10
]
by Deligne,
[
20
]
by Kohnen and Zagier for some classical discussions on periods of modular forms,
[
14
,
15
,
16
]
by Haberland and
[
27
]
by Paşol and Popa for periods and inner products,
[
18
]
by Ono, Rolen and Sprung and
[
28
]
by Rogers, Wan, and Zucker for more recent developments. Specifically, in
[
28
]
, from a different perspective the authors computed the periods
L\big(\eta(4\tau)^{6},1\big)
and
L\big(\eta(2\tau)^{3}\eta(6\tau)^{3},2\big)
. Our two theorems determine the rational number
C_{\rho,n}
explicitly for special choices of elliptic curves and positive integers
n
. In the end of the paper, using CM values of Eisenstein series and modular polynomials, we compute a few more
C_{\chi,n}
values, listed in Table
1
. Our approach can be adapted to compute
C_{\rho,n}
for other idèle class characters
\rho
associated to CM elliptic curves defined over
\mathbb{Q}
; see
[
36
, p. 483]
by Silverman for the full list of such elliptic curves. Note that in each case the class number of the corresponding CM field is 1. Also we focus on the periods whose corresponding Eisenstein series are holomorphic, but other periods can be handled similarly.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
The Chowla–Selberg formula

The Chowla–Selberg formula says that if
E
is an elliptic curve whose endomorphism ring over
\mathbb{C}
is an order of an imaginary quadratic field
K=\mathbb{Q}\big(\sqrt{-d}\big)
with fundamental discriminant
-d
, then all periods of
E
are algebraic multiples of a particular transcendental number

\displaystyle b_{K}:=\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\prod_{0<a<d}\Gamma\left(\frac{a}{d}\right)^{\frac{n\epsilon(a)}{4h_{K}}},

(2.1)

where
\Gamma\left(\cdot\right)
stands for the Gamma function,
n
is the number of torsion elements in
K
,
\epsilon
is the primitive quadratic Dirichlet character modulo
d
, that is, the quadratic character attached to
K
over
\mathbb{Q}
, and
h_{K}
is the class number of
K
, see
[
31
]
by Selberg and Chowla,
[
13
]
by Gross, or
[
45
, equation (97)]
. For example

\displaystyle b_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-4})}=\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)}\qquad\text{and}\qquad b_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})}=\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)}\right)^{3/2}.

2.2
Gamma and beta functions

The Gamma function satisfies two important properties in addition to the functional equation
\Gamma(x+1)/\Gamma(x)=x
when
x
is not a non-positive integer. See
[
1
]
by Andrews, Askey and Roy for details. The first one is the reflection formula: for
a\in\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(1-a)}=\frac{\sin(a\pi)}{\pi}.

(2.2)

For example,
\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}=\pi
,
\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)=\sqrt{2}\pi
so that

\displaystyle b_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-4})}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{2},\qquad\text{and}\qquad b_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})}=\left(\frac{3}{4}\right)^{3/4}\frac{1}{\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{3}.

The second one is the multiplication formula for
\Gamma
: for integer
m\geq 1
and
a\in\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\Gamma(a)\Gamma\left(a+\frac{1}{m}\right)\cdots\Gamma\left(a+\frac{m-1}{m}\right)=\Gamma(ma)\cdot(2\pi)^{(m-1)/2}m^{\frac{1}{2}-ma}.

(2.3)

Now recall the beta function. For
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
with positive real parts,

\displaystyle B(a,b):=\int_{0}^{1}x^{a-1}(1-x)^{b-1}{\rm d}x.

(2.4)

It is known that

\displaystyle B(a,b)=\frac{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)}.

(2.5)

For example,
B(1/3,1/3)=\frac{3^{1/2}}{2\pi}\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)^{3}
is an algebraic multiple of
b_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})}
.

2.3
Hypergeometric functions

The (generalized) hypergeometric function with parameters
a_{i}
,
b_{j}
and argument
x
is defined by

\,{}_{n+1}F_{n}\left[\begin{matrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\cdots&a_{n+1}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
&b_{1}&\cdots&b_{n}\end{matrix}\;;\;x\right]:=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(a_{1})_{k}\cdots(a_{n+1})_{k}}{(b_{1})_{k}\cdots(b_{n})_{k}}\frac{x^{k}}{k!},

where
(a)_{k}:=a(a+1)\cdots(a+k-1)
is the Pochhammer symbol with the convention
(a)_{0}=1
, and can be written as
(a)_{k}=\Gamma(a+k)/\Gamma(a)
.
For
a_{i}
,
b_{j}
such that the formal power series is well-defined, the radius of convergence for
x
is typically 1. In particular,

{}_{1}F_{0}[a_{1}\,;\,x]=(1-x)^{-a_{1}}
. These functions can be defined recursively as follows
[
1
, equation (2.2.2)]
: when
\operatorname{Re}(b_{n})>\operatorname{Re}(a_{n+1})>0
,

\displaystyle\,_{n+1}F_{n}\left[\begin{matrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\cdots&a_{n+1}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
&b_{1}&\cdots&b_{n}\end{matrix}\;;\;x\right]

(2.6)

\displaystyle{}=\frac{1}{B(a_{n+1},b_{n}-a_{n+1})}\int_{0}^{1}y^{a_{n+1}-1}(1-y)^{b_{n}-a_{n+1}-1}\,_{n}F_{n-1}\left[\begin{matrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&\cdots&a_{n}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
&b_{1}&\cdots&b_{n-1}\end{matrix}\;;\;xy\right]{\rm d}y.

In the classic developments, the
{}_{2}F_{1}
functions play a vital role. They can be written using the above recipe in the following Euler integral formula

\displaystyle\,_{2}F_{1}\left[\begin{matrix}a&b\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
&c\end{matrix}\;;\;x\right]=\frac{1}{B(b,c-b)}\int_{0}^{1}y^{b-1}(1-y)^{c-b-1}(1-xy)^{-a}{\rm d}y.

(2.7)

In this sense, we say the
{}_{2}F_{1}
value corresponds to a 1-integral, or 1-period.

The Gauss summation formula
[
1
, Theorem 2.2.2]
says that for
a,b,c\!\in\!\mathbb{C}
with
\operatorname{Re}(c\!-\!a\!-\!b){>}0
,

\displaystyle\,_{2}F_{1}\left[\begin{matrix}a&b\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
&c\end{matrix}\;;\;1\right]=\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(c-a-b)}{\Gamma(c-a)\Gamma(c-b)}.

(2.8)

A virtue of such a formula is to give the precise value of the integral (
2.7
) in terms of Gamma values.

Here is a version of the Clausen formula
[
1
, p. 116]
: for
a,b,x\in\mathbb{C}

\displaystyle\,_{2}F_{1}\left[\begin{matrix}a&b\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
&a+b+\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix}\;;\;x\right]^{2}=\,_{3}F_{2}\left[\begin{matrix}2a&2b&a+b\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
&2a+2b&a+b+\frac{1}{2}\end{matrix}\;;\;x\right]

(2.9)

as long as both sides converge.
If we write both hand sides using the integral forms via (
2.6
), then the Clausen formula expresses the square of a certain 1-integral as an iterated 2-integral. From the classic result of Schwarz, hypergeometric functions are tied to automorphic forms of triangle groups. See
[
43
]
by Yang for using hypergeometric functions to compute automorphic forms for genus 0 Shimura curves with three elliptic points and see
[
39
]
by the third author and Yang for some applications.

2.4
Modular forms

There are many texts on modular forms, for instance
[
7
,
11
,
22
,
26
,
29
,
34
,
45
]
. Let
q={\rm e}^{2\pi{\rm i}\tau}
, where
\tau
lies in the upper half complex plane
\mathfrak{H}
. One of the most well-known modular forms is the discriminant modular form
\Delta(z)=\eta(\tau)^{24}
where
\eta(\tau)=q^{1/24}\prod\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{n})
is the weight-1/2 Dedekind eta function. Many modular forms can be written as eta products or quotients.

Among all modular forms, theta functions and Eisenstein series (see
[
29
,
45
]
) play important roles. We first recall below the classic weight-1/2 Jacobi theta functions following mainly
[
45
]
by Zagier:

\displaystyle\theta_{2}(\tau):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{(2n+1)^{2}/8},\qquad\theta_{3}(\tau):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}q^{n^{2}/2},\qquad\theta_{4}(\tau):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(-1)^{n}q^{n^{2}/2},

which satisfy the relation

\displaystyle\theta_{3}^{4}=\theta_{2}^{4}+\theta_{4}^{4}.

They can be expressed in terms of the Dedekind eta function as

\displaystyle\theta_{2}(\tau)=2\frac{\eta(2\tau)^{2}}{\eta(\tau)},\qquad\theta_{3}(\tau)=\frac{\eta(\tau)^{5}}{\eta(\tau/2)^{2}\eta(2\tau)^{2}},\qquad\theta_{4}(\tau)=\frac{\eta(\tau/2)^{2}}{\eta(\tau)}.

(2.10)

Then the modular
\lambda
-function is

\displaystyle\lambda=\left(\frac{\theta_{2}}{\theta_{3}}\right)^{4}=\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}\eta(\tau/2){\eta(2\tau)^{2}}}{\eta(\tau)^{3}}\right)^{8}=16\big(q^{1/2}-8q+44q^{3/2}-192q^{2}+718q^{5/2}+\cdots\big),

and

\displaystyle 1-\lambda=\left(\frac{\theta_{4}}{\theta_{3}}\right)^{4}=\left(\frac{\eta(\tau/2)^{2}\eta(2\tau)}{\eta(\tau)^{3}}\right)^{8}.

(2.11)

The lambda function
\lambda(\tau)
generates the field of all meromorphic weight-0 modular forms for
\Gamma(2)
, the principal level-2 congruence subgroup. The group
\Gamma(2)
has 3 cusps, 0, 1,
{\rm i}\infty
at which the values of
\lambda
are
1
,
\infty
,
0
respectively.

Another relevant classical result is (see Borweins
[
3
]
)

\displaystyle\theta_{3}^{2}=\,_{2}F_{1}\left[\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
&1\end{matrix}\;;\;\frac{\theta_{2}^{4}}{\theta_{3}^{4}}\right].

(2.12)

Thus

\displaystyle\theta_{4}^{2}=(1-\lambda)^{1/2}\,_{2}F_{1}\left[\begin{matrix}\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\\
&1\end{matrix}\;;\;\lambda\right].

(2.13)

From
[
45
, Proposition 7]
, the logarithmic derivative of the discriminant modular form
\Delta(\tau)=\eta(\tau)^{24}
is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\tau}\log\Delta(\tau)=E_{2}(\tau),

where

\displaystyle E_{2}(\tau):=1-24\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{nq^{n}}{1-q^{n}}

is the weight-2 holomorphic quasi-modular form for
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Lemma 2.1
.

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}(\tau)=\frac{{\rm d}\lambda(\tau)}{{\rm d}\tau}=2\pi{\rm i}\cdot 8\cdot\frac{\eta(\tau/2)^{16}\eta(2\tau)^{16}}{\eta(\tau)^{28}}=\pi{\rm i}\cdot\lambda(\tau)\cdot\theta_{4}^{4}(\tau).

A finite index subgroup
\Gamma
of
{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
acts on the upper half plane
\mathfrak{H}
via fractional linear transformations. Its fundamental domain can be compactified by adding a few missing points, called the cusps, to get the compact modular curve
X_{\Gamma}
for
\Gamma
. The meromorphic modular functions for
\Gamma
form a field and we will denote it by
\mathbb{C}(X_{\Gamma})
. If
X_{\Gamma}
has genus 0,
\mathbb{C}(X_{\Gamma})
has a generator
t
over
\mathbb{C}
which plays a crucial role. For example, its derivative
t^{\prime}=\frac{{\rm d}t}{{\rm d}\tau}
is a weight-2 meromorphic modular form for
\Gamma
. When
t=\lambda(\tau)
, the explicit form of
t^{\prime}
is given in Lemma
2.1
. When
-I\notin\Gamma
, by the Galois theory in the context of modular curves, a finite extension of
\mathbb{C}(X_{\Gamma})
corresponds to a unique finite index subgroup
\Gamma^{\prime}
of
\Gamma
. Note that
\mathbb{C}(X_{\Gamma^{\prime}})
is a simple field extension of
\mathbb{C}(X_{\Gamma})
. The ramifications of a generator can occur either along the cusps or along the elliptic points with specified degrees. Nevertheless, finding such a generator is equivalent to determining the group
\Gamma^{\prime}
. In some cases below, we start with a genus 0 group
\Gamma
with
\mathbb{C}(X_{\Gamma})
generated by
t
and compute an algebraic function on
\Gamma
which leads to a genus 1 subgroup
\Gamma^{\prime}
. In this case the invariant differential for
X_{\Gamma^{\prime}}
naturally corresponds to the unique normalized weight-2 cusp form
f
for
\Gamma^{\prime}
. This process may lead to an expression of
f(\tau){\rm d}\tau
as an algebraic function
R(t)
times
{\rm d}t
. Combined with formula (
1.1

can be computed using

\displaystyle\int_{t(0)}^{t({\rm i}\infty)}R(t){\rm d}t.

When

has CM by an imaginary quadratic field

2.1

is an algebraic multiple of
b_{K}
. This approach is closely related to
[
28

by Rogers, Wan, and Zucker where they used elliptic integrals instead of modular forms.

We illustrate the above idea by the following example. The cubic equation (
2.15
) leads to one natural way to parametrize the degree-3 Fermat curve
X^{3}+Y^{3}=1
, which is isomorphic to the modular curve
X_{0}(27)
. Here we use the standard notation that
X_{0}(N)
is the modular curve for

\displaystyle\Gamma_{0}(N)=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in{{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}\colon c\equiv 0\mod N\right\}.

The genus 0 congruence subgroup
\Gamma_{0}(9)

{\rm i}\infty

X_{0}(27)
is a 3-fold cover of

\Gamma_{0}(9)

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{c||c|c|c|c}\Gamma_{0}(9)&{\rm i}\infty&0&\frac{1}{3}&\frac{2}{3}\\
\hline\cr\Gamma_{0}(27)&{\rm i}\infty,\frac{1}{9},\frac{2}{9}&0&\frac{1}{3}&\frac{2}{3}\end{array}

This means that, as a cover of
X_{0}(9)

X_{0}(27)

0
,
\frac{1}{3}
,
\frac{2}{3}

\infty

\displaystyle\frac{a(3\tau)}{c(3\tau)}=\frac{1}{3}\frac{{\eta(\tau)^{3}}}{\eta(9\tau)^{3}}+1=\frac{1}{3}\big(q^{-1}+5q^{2}-7q^{5}+3q^{8}+15q^{11}+\cdots\big)

\Gamma_{0}(9)

\infty

1
,
\zeta_{3}
,
\zeta_{3}^{2}
at the cusps

2/3

X(\tau)=c(3\tau)/a(3\tau)

matches exactly the ramification information of the covering map
X_{0}(27)\to X_{0}(9)
. Therefore,

is a modular function for
\Gamma_{0}(27)
which also generates the cubic extension
\mathbb{C}(X_{0}(27))/\mathbb{C}(X_{0}(9))
. As
X^{3}+Y^{3}=1
, this is a natural realization of the degree-3 Fermat group alluded to above. Therefore, up to scalar,

is the unique holomorphic differential
1
-form on the curve

. On the other hand, the Hecke eigenform
f_{27}(\tau):=\eta(3\tau)^{2}\eta(9\tau)^{2}
defines a holomorphic differential
1
-form,

, on

and

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}X}{Y^{2}}(q)=3f_{27}(q)\frac{{\rm d}q}{q}.

Or equivalently,

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}X}{Y^{2}}(\tau)=2\pi{\rm i}\cdot 3f_{27}(\tau){{\rm d}\tau}.

Set
s(\tau)=X(\tau/3)=c(\tau)/a(\tau)
and

It follows that

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}s}{t^{2}}(\tau)=2\pi{\rm i}\cdot f_{27}(\tau/3){{\rm d}\tau}.

In other words,

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}s}{{\rm d}\tau}=2\pi{\rm i}\cdot s\big(1-s^{3}\big)a^{2}/3.

2.5
Idèle class characters and modular forms

Given a number field
K
, denote by
\Sigma(K)
the set of places of
K
, and for each
v\in\Sigma(K)
, let
K_{v}
denote the completion of
K
at
v
. When
v
is a nonarchimedean place, let
\mathcal{O}_{v}
be the ring of integers of
K_{v}
,
\mathcal{M}_{v}=\pi_{v}\mathcal{O}_{v}
its unique maximal ideal, and
\mathcal{U}_{v}
the group of units in
\mathcal{O}_{v}
. The group of idèles
I_{K}
of
K
is the restricted product
\mathop{\prod^{\prime}}\limits_{v\in\Sigma(K)}K_{v}^{\times}
with respect to
\mathcal{U}_{v}
at the nonarchimedean places
v
of
K
. It is a locally compact topological group in which the multiplicative group
K^{\times}
is diagonally embedded.

A character
\xi
of
I_{K}
is a continuous homomorphism from
I_{K}
to
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
. Denote by
\xi_{v}
its restriction to
K_{v}^{\times}
. Then we can write
\xi=\prod\limits_{v\in\Sigma(K)}\xi_{v}
. Furthermore, owing to the topology on
I_{K}
, one can show that, for almost all nonarchimedean places
v
of
K
, the character
\xi_{v}
is trivial on
\mathcal{U}_{v}
, hence it is determined by its value at any uniformizer
\pi_{v}
at
v
since
K_{v}^{\times}=\mathcal{U}_{v}\times\langle\pi_{v}\rangle
. In this case we say that
\xi
and
\xi_{v}
are unramified at
v
. At a nonarchimedean place
v
where
\xi_{v}
is not trivial on
\mathcal{U}_{v}
, again by the topology on
K_{v}^{\times}
, one can show that there is a smallest positive integer
\mathfrak{f}_{v}
so that
\xi_{v}
is trivial on the subgroup
1+\mathcal{M}_{v}^{\mathfrak{f}_{v}}
of
\mathcal{U}_{v}
. In this case we say that
\xi
and
\xi_{v}
are ramified at
v
and the product of
\mathcal{M}_{v}^{\mathfrak{f}_{v}}
over ramified places
v
is called the
conductor
of
\xi
.

When a character
\xi
of
I_{K}
is trivial on
K^{\times}
, it is called an
idèle class character
of
K
. The weak approximation theorem (cf.
[
25
, p. 117]
) implies that an idèle class character
\xi=\prod_{v}\xi_{v}
with a given conductor is determined by the local components
\xi_{v}
for all but finitely many places
v
.

The nonarchimedean places
v
of
K
are in one-to-one correspondence with the maximal ideals
\mathfrak{P}_{v}
of the ring of integers of
K
. Given an idèle class character
\xi=\prod\limits_{v\in\Sigma(K)}\xi_{v}
, the formula

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}(\mathfrak{P}_{v})=\xi_{v}(\pi_{v})

defines a character
{\xi^{\prime}}
on the free abelian group generated by all
\mathfrak{P}_{v}
with
\xi_{v}
unramified, in other words, the group of fractional ideals of
K
coprime to the conductor of
\xi
. In the literature
{\xi^{\prime}}
is called a
Hecke Grossencharacter
. Upon checking the behavior of
{\xi^{\prime}}
on the integral principal ideals, one finds that
{\xi^{\prime}}
has the same conductor as
\xi
. Conversely, given a Hecke Grossencharacter
{\xi^{\prime}}
, the above formula defines an unramified character
\xi_{v}
of
K_{v}^{\times}
for all but finitely many places
v
of
K
, which can be uniquely extended to an idèle class character
\xi=\prod_{v}\xi_{v}
with the same conductor as
{\xi^{\prime}}
by the weak approximation theorem. So the two kinds of characters are the same. The reader is referred to Section 6, Chapter VII of the book
[
25
]
by Neukirch for more detail.

A typical example of an unramified idèle class character is
\xi=|~|_{K}=\prod\limits_{v\in\Sigma(K)}|~|_{v}
, the absolute value of
I_{K}
. Here
|~|_{v}
is the standard valuation at
v
. More precisely, if
v
is a real place, it is the usual absolute value on
\mathbb{R}
; if
v
is a complex place, it is the square of the usual absolute value on
\mathbb{C}
; if
v
is a nonarchimedean place, it is given by

\displaystyle|\pi_{v}|_{v}=\frac{1}{Nv}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad|u_{v}|_{v}=1\qquad{\rm for}\ \ u_{v}\in\mathcal{U}_{v}

with
Nv
the cardinality of the residue field
\mathcal{O}_{v}/\mathcal{M}_{v}
.

Remark 2.2
.

In
[
22
, Chapter 5, Proposition 1]
it is shown that any character
\xi
of
I_{K}
can be written as the product of
|~|_{K}^{s^{\prime}}
for some complex number
s^{\prime}
times a unitary idèle class character
\xi_{1}
of
I_{K}
which takes values in the unit circle
S^{1}\subset\mathbb{C}^{\times}
.

To an idèle class character
\xi
of
K
, we associate an
L
-function defined as

\displaystyle L(\xi,s)=\prod_{v~{\rm nonarchimedean}\atop{\xi_{v}~{\rm unramified}}}\frac{1}{1-\xi_{v}(\pi_{v})(Nv)^{-s}}.

(2.18)

Note that the
L
-function attached to the trivial character is nothing but the Dedekind zeta function of
K
, which converges absolutely for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
. The same holds for
\xi
unitary. In general, by Remark
2.2
, we can write
\xi=|~|_{K}^{s^{\prime}}\xi_{1}
with
s^{\prime}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\xi_{1}
a unitary idèle class character. Since
L(\xi,s)=L(\xi_{1},s+s^{\prime})
, we conclude that the
L
-function attached to
\xi
converges absolutely to a holomorphic function on the right half-plane
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1-\operatorname{Re}(s^{\prime})
. It suffices to understand the analytic behavior of
L
-functions attached to unitary idèle class characters of
K
. This was studied by Hecke for Grossencharacters. Hecke’s result was reproved in Tate’s thesis
[
38
]
using adelic language, summarized below.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
K
be a number field with different
\mathfrak{d}
. Let
\xi
be a unitary idèle class character of
K
with conductor
\mathfrak{f}
. The associated
L
-function
L(\xi,s)
defined above is holomorphic on
\operatorname{Re}(s)>1
. It can be analytically continued to a meromorphic function on the whole
s
-plane, bounded at infinity in each vertical strip of finite width, and holomorphic everywhere except for a simple pole at
s=1
when
\xi
is the trivial character. Further, there is a suitable
\Gamma
-product
L_{\infty}(\xi_{\infty},s)
, depending on
\xi_{v}
at the archimedean places
v
of
K
, such that

\displaystyle\Lambda(\xi,s):=L_{\infty}(\xi_{\infty},s)L(\xi,s)

satisfies the functional equation

\displaystyle\Lambda(\xi,s)=W(\xi)N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\mathfrak{df})^{\frac{1}{2}-s}\Lambda\big(\xi^{-1},1-s\big),

(2.19)

where
W(\xi)
is a constant of absolute value
1
and
\xi^{-1}
is the inverse of
\xi
.

Here
W(\xi)
, called the root number of
\xi
, is equal to the Gauss sum of
\xi
divided by its absolute value. See
[
38
]
for details.

In particular, when
K
is an imaginary quadratic extension of
\mathbb{Q}
, it has one infinite place
\infty
with
K_{\infty}=\mathbb{C}
. The
\Gamma
-factor
L_{\infty}(\xi_{\infty},s)
is equal to
(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)
for all unitary
\xi
. Such a character
\xi
is said to have
algebraic type

k
if
\xi_{\infty}
maps
z\in\mathbb{C}^{\times}
to
\xi_{\infty}(z)=(z/|z|)^{n}
with
|n|=k-1
. The above theorem for
\xi
algebraic of type
k\geq 1
combined with the converse theorem for modular forms proved by Weil
[
41
]
implies the existence of a modular form
f_{\xi}=\sum\limits_{n\geq 0}a_{n}q^{n}
of weight
k
such that the associated
L
-function
L(f_{\xi},s):=\sum\limits_{n\geq 1}a_{n}n^{-s}
satisfies the relation

\displaystyle L(f_{\xi},s)=L\left(\xi,s-\frac{k-1}{2}\right).

The form
f_{\xi}
is cuspidal if
\xi
is nontrivial. See
[
22
, Chapter 7, Section 4]
for details. Observe that, since the
L
-function attached to
f
is Eulerian at all primes, the form
f
is a Hecke eigenfunction.

We end this subsection by noting that for an elliptic curve
E
defined over
\mathbb{Q}
of conductor
N
with CM by
K
, it was known to Deuring that the Hasse–Weil
L
-function
L(E,s)
attached to
E
obtained by counting
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-rational points on the reduction of
E
modulo the prime
p
is equal to
L\big(|~|_{K}^{-1/2}\xi,s\big)=L\big(\xi,s-\frac{1}{2}\big)
for some nontrivial unitary idèle class character
\xi
of
K
, algebraic of type
2
. The above discussion says that all positive powers of
\xi
also correspond to modular forms.

2.6
Eisenstein series

We now recall some useful facts of Eisenstein series of general level.
Define the level
N
holomorphic Eisenstein series as follows. For details see
[
7
,
11
]
. For a fixed pair of integers
(a_{1},a_{2})
, for
k\geq 3
, we let

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k,(a_{1},a_{2};N)}(\tau)=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\atop(m,n)\equiv(a_{1},a_{2})\mod N}\frac{1}{(m\tau+n)^{k}}.

The series is a weight
k
holomorphic modular form on

\Gamma(N)=\left\{\gamma\in{{\rm SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}\colon\gamma\equiv I_{2}\mod N\right\}.
When
N\geq 3
,
\Gamma(N)
does not contain
-I_{2}
. For
k=1
or
2
, the series does not converge absolutely so we adopt the following approach using the more general non-holomorphic Eisenstein series

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k,(a_{1},a_{2};N)}^{\ast}(s,\tau)=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\atop(m,n)\equiv(a_{1},a_{2})\mod N}\frac{1}{(m\tau+n)^{k}}\frac{\operatorname{Im}(\tau)^{s}}{|m\tau+n|^{2s}}.

We now recall some basic properties of this function. For details see
[
7
, Proposition 5.2.2]
by Cohen and Strömberg. Firstly the series
\mathcal{G}_{k,(a_{1},a_{2};N)}^{\ast}(s,\tau)
converges absolutely and uniformly on any compact subset of the upper half complex plane when
\operatorname{Re}(2s+k)>2
thus it is continuous at
s=0
when
k\geq 3
. Also for a fixed
\tau
, there exists a meromorphic continuation of
\mathcal{G}_{k,(a_{1},a_{2};N)}^{\ast}(s,\tau)
to the whole
s
-plane which is parallel to the Fourier series stated in Proposition
2.4
below. In our later application, we are interested in the following series

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\ast}_{k,(a_{1},a_{2};N)}(\tau):=\mathcal{G}_{k,(a_{1},a_{2};N)}^{\ast}(0,\tau)=\lim_{\operatorname{Re}(s)>0\atop s\rightarrow 0}\mathcal{G}_{k,(a_{1},a_{2};N)}^{\ast}(s,\tau).

Thus when
k\geq 3
, the series
\mathcal{G}^{\ast}_{k,(a_{1},a_{2};N)}(\tau)
and
\mathcal{G}_{k,(a_{1},a_{2};N)}(\tau)
coincide.
For integers
a\geq 0
,
N\geq 1
and
k\geq 1

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{k,(a;N)}^{\ast}(\tau):=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\mathcal{G}_{k,(a,i;N)}^{\ast}(\tau)=\sum_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{\left((Nm+a)\tau+n\right)^{k}}

formally. To give the meromorphic continuation of

When

Theorem 2.6
.

We have the Fourier expansions

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{1,(a;N)}^{\ast}(\tau)=-{\rm i}\pi\left(1-\frac{2a}{N}\right)-2\pi{\rm i}\sum_{n>0}\frac{q^{na}-q^{n(N-a)}}{1-q^{Nn}},
