Asymptotic-preserving neural networks for the semiconductor Boltzmann equation and its application on inverse problems
Liu Liu, Yating Wang, Xueyu Zhu, Zhenyi Zhu

Abstract
In this paper, we develop the Asymptotic-Preserving Neural Networks (APNNs) approach to study the forward and inverse problem for the semiconductor Boltzmann equation. The goal of the neural network is to resolve the computational challenges of conventional numerical methods and multiple scales of the model. To guarantee the network can operate uniformly in different regimes, it is desirable to carry the Asymptotic-Preservation (AP) property in the learning process. In a micro-macro decomposition framework, we design such an AP formulation of loss function. The convergence analysis of both the loss function and its neural network is shown, based on the Universal Approximation Theorem and hypocoercivity theory of the model equation. We show a series of numerical tests for forward and inverse problems of both the semiconductor Boltzmann and the Boltzmann-Poisson system to validate the effectiveness of our proposed method, which addresses the significance of the AP property when dealing with inverse problems of multiscale Boltzmann equations especially when only sparse or partially observed data are available.



1
Introduction

Kinetic equations have been widely used in many areas such as rarefied gas, plasma physics, astrophysics, semiconductor device modeling, social and biological sciences
[
30
]
. They describe the non-equilibrium dynamics of a system composed of a large number of particles and bridge atomistic and continuum models in the hierarchy of multiscale modeling. The Boltzmann-type equation, as one of the most representative models in kinetic theory, provides a power tool to describe molecular gas dynamics, radiative transfer, plasma physics and polymer flow
[
2
]
. They have significant impacts in designing, optimization, control and inverse problems. For example, it can be used in the design of semiconductor devices, topology optimization of gas flow channel, or risk management in quantitative finance
[
10
]
. Many of these applications often require finding unknown or optimal parameters in the Boltzmann-type equations or mean-field models
[
1
,
5
,
6
,
9
,
25
]
.

The linearized Boltzmann equation, in particular the semi-conductor Boltzmann equation that we will study in this work, has significant applications in the semiconductor device modeling
[
22
]
.
In this work, we will study the forward problem of approximating the solution of the model and the inverse problem which aims to infer the collision cross-section in the semiconductor Boltzmann equation, with available full or partial observation data given as measurable density quantity.

Modeling and predicting the evolution of multiscale systems such as the Boltzmann-type equations have always been challenging, which often requires sophisticated knowledge of numerical methods and labor-intensive implementation, in addition to the prohibitive costs due to the well-known curse of dimensionality. In real-life applications that usually involve the scarcity of available data, multiple scales and uncertainties, solving physical problems with missing or incomplete initial or boundary conditions via traditional numerical approaches thus becomes highly impractical. This motivates researchers to develop data-driven models and methods
[
13
]
in the recent decade.

Machine Learning, or Deep neural networks (DNNs) in particular, have gained increasing interest in approximating the solutions partial differential equations (PDE) due to its universal approximation property and its ability to handle high-dimensional problems. Many studies have been done to employ DNN for solving deterministic or parameterized PDEs, and have shown remarkable promise in various applications. Among those, Physics informed neural network (PINN)
[
32
,
33
]
is one of the most famous approach which incorporates the available physical laws and limited data, such as boundary or initial conditions and the source term, to approximate the solution of the underlying PDE. The idea has been successfully applied in the simulation of many forward and inverse problems
[
7
,
27
,
28
]
.

For the kinetic problems under study, another challenge is the multiple scale lying in the models, which makes it computational difficult to resolve small scales especially when the physical scaling parameter is small. Asymptotic-preserving (AP) schemes, which preserve the asymptotic transition from one scale to another at the discrete level, have been a popular and robust computational method in the past two decades
[
16
]
for many kinetic and hyperbolic problems. The main advantage of AP schemes is that they are very efficient in all regimes–especially the hydrodynamic or diffusive regime, since they do not need to resolve the small physical parameters numerically yet can still capture the macroscopic behavior.

However, if one directly applies the standard PINNs when dealing with these multiscale model, it may lead to incorrect inferences and predictions
[
19
]
. This is due to the presence of small scales need to be enforced consistently during the learning process, but a standard PINN formulation only captures the solution at the leading order of the Knudsen number, thereby losing accuracy in the asymptotic limit regimes.
To overcome this difficulty, the authors in
[
17
]
propose the Asymptotic-preserving neural networks (APNNs) to enhance the performance of standard PINN for solving multiscale linear transport equations. The loss function of APNN contains the micro-macro decomposition of the original problem and some conservation conditions, which has shown to be efficient and accurate in capturing the limiting macroscopic behavior of the solution when the scaling parameter approaches zero. For the hyperbolic type linear kinetic equations with multiple scales, there are some existing work that studied the APNN method, for example, see
[
3
,
18
,
19
]
.

In this work, we will show how to construct APNNs for the semiconductor Boltzmann equation and show its effectiveness in solving both forward and inverse problems of interests. To our best knowledge, this is the
first
work we employ the APNN method under the micro-macro decomposition framework for the semiconductor Boltzmann equation.

Besides, inspired by existing work
[
8
,
15
]
that have studied the convergence of the loss function and the neural network solution for the linear transport equation and Fokker-Planck equation.
We show the convergence result based on the hypercoercivity analysis, that is
uniform
in the Knudsen number and owns an exponential decay in time. We remark that our analysis framework on the APNNs can be extended similarly to a class of linear and nonlinear kinetic models.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we first introduce the model equations including the semiconductor Boltzmann and the Boltzmann-Poisson system, then derive a coupled system in the micro-macro decomposition framework with a formal analysis of asymptotic limit.
In Section
3
, we review the PINNs and introduce APNNs method for our model by constructing the loss function that preserves the AP property in the training of neural networks.
In Section
4
, we provide the convergence analysis of both the loss function and its neural network approximated solution, based on the universal approximation theorem and hypercoercivity theory for kinetic models.
In Section
5
, a series of numerical experiments are shown to validate the effectiveness of our proposed APNN method, where both forward and inverse problems using synthetic data generated by the kinetic model. Finally, conclusions and future work will be discussed in the last section.

2
The models and Micro-macro decomposition method

The semiconductor-Boltzmann.

We consider the semiconductor Boltzmann equation
[
20
]
given by

\varepsilon\partial_{t}f+v\partial_{x}f+\partial_{x}\phi\,\partial_{v}f=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{Q}(f),

(1)

where
f(t,x,v)
is the probability density distribution for particles located at
x\in\mathcal{D}
with velocity
v\in\mathbb{R}
. In our model,
\varepsilon
is the Knudsen number defined as the ratio of the mean free path and the typical length scale. In our application,
\varepsilon
varies from
O(1)
, the kinetic regime, to
\varepsilon\ll 1
, the diffusion regime. the
\phi(t,x)
is external electric potential.

The anisotropic collision operator
\mathcal{Q}
describes a linear approximation of the electron-phonon interaction, given by

\mathcal{Q}(f)(t,x,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sigma(v,w)\left(M(v)f(t,x,w)-M(w)f(t,x,v)\right)dw,

with
M
the normalized Maxwellian

M(v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-v^{2}}
.
Here
\sigma(v,w)
denotes the scattering coefficient for the electron-phonon collisions. The collision frequency is defined as

\lambda(v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sigma(v,w)M(w)dw.

We refer the readers to
[
12
,
22
,
35
]
for more physical background.

Define
n_{x}
the unit outward normal vector on the spatial boundary

\partial\mathcal{D}
. Let
\gamma=\partial\mathcal{D}\times\Omega
, then the phase boundary can be split into an outgoing boundary
\gamma_{+}
, incoming boundary
\gamma_{-}
and a singular boundary
\gamma_{0}
, which are defined by

\displaystyle\gamma_{+}:=\left\{(x,v)\in\partial\mathcal{D}\times\Omega:v\cdot n_{x}>0\right\},

(2)

\displaystyle\gamma_{-}:=\left\{(x,v)\in\partial\mathcal{D}\times\Omega:v\cdot n_{x}<0\right\},

\displaystyle\gamma_{0}:=\left\{(x,v)\in\partial\mathcal{D}\times\Omega:v\cdot n_{x}=0\right\}.

The inflow boundary condition is given by

f(t,x,v)=f_{\text{BC}}(t,x,v),\qquad\text{for }\,(t,x,v)\in[0,T]\times\gamma_{-}.

(3)

We assume the initial condition that

f(t=0,x,v)=f_{\text{IC}}(x,v).

The Boltzmann-Poisson system.

In this work, we also study the
nonlinear
Boltzmann-Poisson system, where the electric potential
\phi(t,x)
is solved by the Poisson equation as below:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\varepsilon\partial_{t}f+v\partial_{x}f+\partial_{x}\phi\,\partial_{v}f=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{Q}(f),\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\beta\partial_{xx}\phi=\int_{\mathbb{R}}fdv-c(x),\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\phi(t,0)=0,\qquad\phi(t,1)=V,\end{array}\right.

(4)

with
\beta
the scaled Debye length,
V
the applied bias voltage and
c(x)
the doping profile.

2.1
The micro-macro decomposition method

We employ the micro-macro decomposition technique
[
26
]
and derive the micro-macro system for the semiconductor Boltzmann equation (
1
).
Assume the ansatz

f={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\Pi f}+\varepsilon g,

(5)

where
g:=g(t,x,v)
and the notation
\Pi f:=\langle f\rangle\,M(v)
is defined by

\langle f\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(t,x,v)\,dv=\rho(t,x).

Inserting the ansatz (
5
) into (
1
), one gets

\varepsilon\partial_{t}(\Pi f)+\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}g+v\,\partial_{x}(\Pi f)+\varepsilon\partial_{x}(vg){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}-2v\rho\,\partial_{x}\phi\,M(v)+\varepsilon\partial_{x}\phi\,\partial_{v}g}=\mathcal{Q}(g),

(6)

where
\partial_{v}(\Pi f)=\rho\,\partial_{v}M(v)=-2\rho\,vM(v)
is used.

Take the projection operator
\Pi
on both sides of (
6
), then

\varepsilon\partial_{t}(\Pi f)+\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}(\Pi g)+\partial_{x}\rho\left(\int vM(v)dv\right)M(v)+\varepsilon\Pi\partial_{x}(vg)+\varepsilon\partial_{x}\phi\,\Pi(\partial_{v}g)=0,

which is equivalent to

\partial_{t}(\Pi f)+\Pi\partial_{x}(vg)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\partial_{x}\phi\,\Pi(\partial_{v}g)}=0,

(7)

where
\Pi g=0
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}vM(v)dv=0
is used.
Now subtract (
6
) by (
7
), then

\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}g+v\partial_{x}(\Pi f)+\varepsilon(\mathbb{I}-\Pi)\partial_{x}(vg)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon\partial_{x}\phi\,(\mathbb{I}-\Pi)\partial_{v}g-2v\rho\,\partial_{x}\phi\,M(v)}=\mathcal{Q}(g),

that is

\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}g+v\partial_{x}\rho\,M(v)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon(\mathbb{I}-\Pi)\left(v\,\partial_{x}g+\partial_{x}\phi\,\partial_{v}g\right)-2v\rho\,\partial_{x}\phi\,M(v)}=\mathcal{Q}(g).

(8)

As
\varepsilon\to 0
, equation (
7
) stays unchanged, and the microscopic equation (
8
) gives

g=\mathcal{Q}^{-1}\left(v\,\partial_{x}\rho\,M(v)-2v\rho\,\partial_{x}\phi\,M(v)\right)=\left(\partial_{x}\rho-2\rho\,\partial_{x}\phi\right)\mathcal{Q}^{-1}(vM(v)).

Plug
g
into (
7
) and integrate over
v
, we derive the drift-diffusion limit (
[
31
]
) which reads

\partial_{t}\rho={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}T\left(\partial_{xx}\rho-2\partial_{x}(\rho\,\partial_{x}\phi)\right)},

(9)

where in the 1D slab geometry we have
T=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{v^{2}}{\lambda(v)}\,M(v)dv
.

To summarize, the coupled equations (
7
)–(
8
) for
\rho
and
g
solve the following system in our micro-macro decomposition framework:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}\langle vg\rangle+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\partial_{x}\phi\,\langle\partial_{v}g\rangle}=0,\\[6.0pt]
\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}g+v\partial_{x}\rho\,M(v)+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\varepsilon(\mathbb{I}-\Pi)\left(v\,\partial_{x}g+\partial_{x}\phi\,\partial_{v}g\right)-2v\rho\,\partial_{x}\phi\,M(v)}=\mathcal{Q}(g),\\[6.0pt]
\Pi g=0.\end{array}\right.

(10)

where the third equation is for the mass conservation for
g
, which is crucial to the training of neural networks
[
18
]
and will be mentioned in section
3.2
.

As
\varepsilon\to 0
, according to (
10
), the limiting system for
\rho
and
g
satisfies

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{x}\langle vg\rangle+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\partial_{x}\phi\,\langle\partial_{v}g\rangle}=0,\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle v\partial_{x}\rho\,M(v)-2v\rho\partial_{x}\phi\,M(v)=\mathcal{Q}(g),\\[6.0pt]
\displaystyle\Pi g=0,\end{array}\right.

(11)

which is
equivalent
to the diffusion limit given in (
9
).

3
Neural Network Approaches

3.1
Physics Informed Neural Networks

The standard Physics-informed Neural Networks (PINNs)
[
32
,
33
]
incorporate residual associated with the underlying PDE, along with initial and boundary conditions or other physical properties (when applicable) into the loss function.
It adopts automatic differentiation and embeds the physical laws in addition to the data-driven terms in the loss function at a given set of points in a computational domain.

We first review the standard PINN approach in our problem setting. For forward problems, our goal is to approximate the solution of the semiconductor Boltzmann equation (
1
). Suppose the neutral network has
L
layers; the input layer takes
(t,x,v)
and the final layer gives
f^{NN}(t,x,v;m,w,b)
or simply
f^{NN}_{\theta}
with
\theta
representing the neural network parameters,
as the output. The relation between the
l
-th and
(l+1)
-th layer (
l=1,2,\cdots L-1)
is
given by

n_{j}^{(l+1)}=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{l}}w_{ji}^{(l+1)}\sigma_{l}(n_{i}^{l})+b_{j}^{(l+1)},

(12)

where
m=\left(m_{0},m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{L-1}\right)
,
w=\left\{w_{ji}^{(k)}\right\}_{i,j,k=1}^{m_{k-1},m_{k},L}
and
b=\left\{b_{j}^{(k)}\right\}_{j=1,k=1}^{m_{k},L}
. More specifically,

1.

n_{i}^{l}
: the
i
-th neuron in the
l
-th layer

2.

\sigma_{l}
: the activation function in the
l
-th layer

3.

w_{ji}^{(l+1)}
: the weight between the
i
-th neuron in the
l
-th layer and the
j
-th neuron in the
(l+1)
-th layer

4.

b_{j}^{(l+1)}
: the bias of the
j
-th neuron in the
(l+1)
-th layer

5.

m_{l}
: the number of neurons in the
l
-th layer.

For forward problems, we desire to approximate the solution of the semiconductor Boltzmann equation (
1
) with given initial and boundary conditions. To find the optimal values for the network parameters
\theta
that are composed of all the weights
w_{ji}
and bias
b_{j}
, the neural network is trained by minimizing the following loss function

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{PINN}}^{\varepsilon}(\theta)=Loss_{\text{GE}}(\theta)+\lambda_{1}Loss_{\text{BC}}(\theta)+\lambda_{2}Loss_{\text{IC}}(\theta),

(13)

where the loss for the residual of the governing equation (
1
) given by:

Loss_{\text{GE}}(\theta):=\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\varepsilon\partial_{t}f_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}+v\partial_{x}f_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}+\partial_{x}\phi\,\partial_{v}f_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{Q}(f_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}})|^{2}\,d\bm{v}d\bm{x}dt.

(14)

The loss for the boundary condition is

Loss_{\text{BC}}(\theta):=\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\gamma_{-}}\left|\mathcal{B}\left(f_{\theta}^{NN}\right)-f_{\text{BC}}\right|^{2}\,dsdt,

(15)

and the loss for the initial condition:

Loss_{\text{IC}}(\theta):=\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathcal{I}\left(f_{\theta}^{NN}\right)-f_{\text{IC}}\right|^{2}\,d\bm{v}d\bm{x},

(16)

where the initial and boundary conditions are incorporated into the loss function as a regularization or penalty term,
with penalty parameters
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
chosen for good performance. The most popular method to minimize loss function over the parameter space are stochastic gradient descent and advanced optimizers such as Adam
[
23
]
. After the training process and achieving the optimal set of parameter values
\theta^{\star}
by minimizing the PINN loss
(
13
), i.e.,

\theta^{\star}=\text{argmin}\ \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{PINN}}^{\varepsilon}(\theta),

the neural network surrogate
f^{NN}_{\theta^{\star}}(t,x,v)
can be evaluated at any given point in the temporal and phase space to obtain the solution of (
1
).

In the context of inverse problems, the structure of the network is almost the same as the forward problem setting, except that the unknown physical parameters of interests
\xi
are considered as learnable parameters. In this case, we aim to optimize network parameters
\theta
and
\xi
jointly, namely

(\theta^{\star},\xi^{\star})=\text{argmin}\ \mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{PINN}}^{\varepsilon}(\theta,\xi),

with

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{PINN}}^{\varepsilon}(\theta,\xi)=Loss_{\text{GE}}(\theta,\xi)+\lambda_{1}Loss_{\text{BC}}(\theta,\xi)+\lambda_{2}Loss_{\text{IC}}(\theta,\xi)+\lambda_{3}Loss_{\text{Data}}(\theta,\xi),

where
Loss_{\text{GE}}
,
Loss_{\text{BC}}
and
Loss_{\text{IC}}
are the same as (
14
), (
15
) and (
16
), and the data mismatch loss
Loss_{\text{Data}}
is involved when additional measurement data are available in the inverse problems. In our problem setting, our targeted parameter of interest is the collision kernel
\sigma
. Both available observation data will be employed in our numerical experiments for inverse problems. Details of available observation data will be explained in Section
5
.

3.2
The APNN framework

Due to the Knudsen number
\varepsilon
in our model equations, the traditional PINNs ignore the asymptotic property and cannot capture the macroscopic behavior
uniformly
with respect to
\varepsilon
, thus leading to wrong approximations.
In contrast, the strategy of designing Asymptotic-preserving neural networks (APNNs), first brought up by
[
18
]
, is to define a new loss function that can capture the correct asymptotic behavior of the model equations, especially when the physical scaling parameter is small; in other words, the loss function has the AP property.
The diagram
1
below illustrates the idea of APNNs
[
18
]
:

Figure 1:
Illustration of APNNs.

Let
\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}
be a multiscale model that depends on the scaling parameter
\varepsilon
. As
\varepsilon\to 0
, it converges to a reduced order limit

\mathcal{F}^{0}
. The solution of
\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}
is approximated by the neural network through the imposition of residual term
\mathcal{R}_{NN}(\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon})
,
whose asymptotic limit is denoted by
\mathcal{R}_{NN}(\mathcal{F}^{0})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. The neural network is called AP if
\mathcal{R}_{NN}(\mathcal{F}^{0})
is consistent
with the residual for the system
\mathcal{F}^{0}
.

Solution Representation.
We employ two deep neural networks to parameterize the two functions
\rho(t,\bm{x})
and
g(t,\bm{x},\bm{v})
in system (
10
), where the notations here are extended to the multi-dimensional spatial and velocity space. Let
\tilde{\rho}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}
be the output of a neural network with inputs
t
and
\bm{x}
. Since the density
\rho
is non-negative, we put an exponential function at the last output layer as a post-processing procedure, i.e., let

\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\bm{x}):=\exp\left(-\tilde{\rho}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\bm{x})\right)\approx\rho(t,\bm{x})

(17)

be the approximation for
\rho
.

We remark that the velocity discretization for
g(t,\bm{x},\bm{v})
, where the Hermite quadrature rule is used since
\bm{v}\in(-\infty,\infty)
. This is standard and the same as traditional AP method studied in
[
20
]
. Let
g(t,\bm{x},\bm{v})=\psi(t,\bm{x},\bm{v})M(\bm{v})
, with
M(\bm{v})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-\bm{v}^{2}}
and

\psi(t,\bm{x},\bm{v})=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\psi_{k}(t,\bm{x})H_{k}(\bm{v})

(18)

is the Hermite expansion with
N
being the order.
For simplicity, we omit the
t
and
\bm{x}
dependence of functions below. Denote
H_{k}
as the renormalized Hermite polynomials with
H_{-1}=0
,
H_{0}=1/\pi^{1/4}
, and

H_{k+1}=v\sqrt{\frac{2}{k+1}}H_{k}-\sqrt{\frac{k}{k+1}}H_{k-1}\quad\text{for }k\geq 0.

The inverse Hermite expansion is defined by

\psi_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\bm{v}}}\psi(v_{j})\,H_{k}(v_{j})\,w_{j},

(19)

where
(v_{j},w_{j})
are the points and corresponding weights of the Gauss-Hermite quadrature rule.
We then compute the collision operator
Q
in (
1
) as follows

Q(g)(\bm{v})=M(\bm{v})\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\bm{v}}}\sigma(\bm{v},v_{j})\,\psi(v_{j})\,w_{j}-\lambda(\bm{v})g(\bm{v}),

with
\lambda(\bm{v})=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\bm{v}}}\sigma(\bm{v},v_{j})\,w_{j}
.
The derivative in
\bm{v}
for
\psi
is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{\bm{v}}\psi

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\psi_{k}\,\partial_{\bm{v}}H_{k}(\bm{v})=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\psi_{k}\sqrt{2k}\,H_{k-1}(\bm{v})

(20)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\bm{v}}}\psi(v_{j})\,H_{k}(v_{j})w_{j}\sqrt{2k}\,H_{k-1}(\bm{v})=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{v}}\psi(v_{j})\,C_{j}(\bm{v}),

where
C_{j}(\bm{v})=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\sqrt{2k}\,H_{k}(v_{j})H_{k-1}(\bm{v})w_{j}
.

In order to obtain approximation for
g
, we let
\tilde{\psi}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\bm{x},\bm{v})
be the output of a fully-connected neural network with input
t
,
\bm{x}
and
\bm{v}
, then impose a post-processing step to guarantee the mass conservation in (
10
). Define

g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\bm{x},\bm{v})=\psi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\bm{x},\bm{v})M(\bm{v}):=\tilde{\psi}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\bm{x},\bm{v})M(\bm{v})-\Pi\left(\tilde{\psi}_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\bm{x},\bm{v})M(\bm{v})\right),

(21)

as an approximation for
g
, now

\Pi g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}=0
is automatically satisfied. Moreover,
\nabla_{\bm{v}}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}
is computed by

\nabla_{\bm{v}}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\bm{v}}}\psi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\bm{x},v_{j})C_{j}(\bm{v})M(\bm{v})-2\bm{v}M(\bm{v})\psi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\bm{x},\bm{v})+2\bm{v}M(\bm{v})\langle\psi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\bm{x},\bm{v})M(\bm{v})\rangle.

(22)

where
\nabla_{\bm{v}}\psi_{\theta}^{NN}=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{\bm{v}}}\psi_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}(t,\bm{x},v_{j})C_{j}(\bm{v})
.

APNN Loss.
For our APNN method, we propose the physics-informed loss based on the residual for the macro-micro system (
7
)–(
8
) as the loss function:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{APNN}}^{\varepsilon}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{D}|}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\left|\partial_{t}\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}+\nabla_{\bm{x}}\cdot\left\langle\bm{v}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right\rangle+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\phi\cdot\left\langle\nabla_{\bm{v}}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right\rangle}\right|^{2}\,d\bm{x}dt

(23)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{D}\times\Omega|}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\Omega}\Big|\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}+\varepsilon(I-\Pi)\left(\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\phi\cdot\nabla_{\bm{v}}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}}\right)

\displaystyle-2\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\phi\,\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\,M(\bm{v})+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}M(\bm{v})-\mathcal{Q}(g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}})\Big|^{2}\,d\bm{v}d\bm{x}dt

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda_{1}}{|\mathcal{T}\times\partial\mathcal{D}\times\Omega|}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\gamma_{-}}\left|\mathcal{B}\left(\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}M(\bm{v})+\varepsilon g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right)-f_{\text{BC}}\right|^{2}\,dsdt

\displaystyle+\frac{\lambda_{2}}{|\mathcal{D}\times\Omega|}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\Omega}\left|\mathcal{I}\left(\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}M(\bm{v})+\varepsilon g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right)-f_{\text{IC}}\right|^{2}\,d\bm{v}d\bm{x}.

As
\varepsilon\to 0
, the above first two terms arisen from the model equation lead to

\begin{split}&\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{APNN}}\text{, residual}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{D}|}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\left|\partial_{t}\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}+\nabla_{x}\cdot\left\langle\bm{v}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right\rangle+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla_{\bm{x}}\phi\cdot\left\langle\nabla_{\bm{v}}g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\right\rangle}\right|^{2}\,d\bm{x}dt\\[6.0pt]
&+\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}\times\mathcal{D}\times\Omega|}\int_{\mathcal{T}}\int_{\mathcal{D}}\int_{\Omega}\left|-2\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\phi\,\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\,M(v)+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{x}}\rho_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}}\,M(v)-\mathcal{Q}(g_{\theta}^{\mathrm{NN}})\right|^{2}\,d\bm{v}d\bm{x}dt,\end{split}

which is exactly the loss for the limiting equations (
11
). Thus our designed loss function for APNN satisfies the AP property that the traditional PINN fails to capture.

4.1
Preliminaries

We first review an important theorem on the existence of the approximated neural network solution, namely the Universal Approximation Theorem (UAT)
[
11
]
which uses a two-level neural network. We adapt it to discuss our model (
1
) in the framework of APNN as shown in Figure
1
, and use the notations introduced in subsection
3.1
.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose the solution to (
1
) satisfies
f\in C^{1}([0,T])\cap C^{1}(\mathcal{D})\cap C^{1}(\Omega)
. Let the activation function
\bar{\sigma}
be any non-polynomial function in
C^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, then for any
\delta>0
, there exists a two-layer neural network

f^{NN}(t,x,v)=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{1}}w_{1i}^{(2)}\bar{\sigma}\left(\left(w_{i1}^{(1)},w_{i2}^{(1)},w_{i3}^{(1)}\right)\cdot(t,x,v)+b_{i}^{(1)}\right)+b_{1}^{(2)},

such that

\displaystyle\left\|f-f^{NN}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}<\delta,\qquad\left\|\partial_{t}(f-f^{NN})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}<\delta,

(24)

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{x}(f-f^{NN})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}<\delta,\quad\left\|\nabla_{v}(f-f^{NN})\right\|_{L^{\infty}(K)}<\delta,

where the domain
K
denotes
[0,T]\times\mathcal{D}\times\Omega
.

We remark that the above result can be generalized to neural network with several hidden layers
[
24
]
.

4.2
Convergence of the loss function

Recall our APNN framework introduced in section
3.2
, we build the loss function based on the residual of the macro-micro system (
7
)–(
8
), thus our neural network approximated solutions are
\rho
and
g
, instead of
f
if applying PINN to the model (
1
).
We first show that a sequence of neural network solutions to
(
7
)–(
8
) exists such that the total loss function converges to zero, if assuming the analytic solution

\rho\in C^{1}([0,T])\cap C^{1}(\mathcal{D}),\qquad g\in C^{1}([0,T])\cap C^{1}(\mathcal{D})\cap C^{1}(\Omega).

(25)

Theorem 1
.

Consider the semiconductor Boltzmann equation (
1
) with the given electric potential
\phi(t,x)
and satisfies

||\nabla_{x}\phi||_{L^{\infty}([0,T]\times\mathcal{D})}\leq C_{\phi}
. Let
\rho
,
g
be the analytic solution to the macro-micro system (
7
)–(
8
), which are sufficiently smooth in its physical domain,
i.e., they satisfy the condition (
25
).
Then there exists a sequence of neural network parameters
\{m_{[j]},w_{[j]},b_{[j]}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
such that the sequence of APNN solutions with
m_{[j]}
nodes, denoted by

\{\rho_{j}(t,x)=\rho^{nn}(t,x;m_{[j]},w_{[j]},b_{[j]})\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
and
\{g_{j}(t,x,v)=g^{nn}(t,x,v;m_{[j]},w_{[j]},b_{[j]})\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
satisfy that

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{APNN}}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{j},g_{j})\to 0,\quad\text{as }j\to\infty.

Proof.

Define a sequence of small numbers
\delta_{j}=\frac{1}{j}
.
By the UAT above, for any
\delta_{j}
, there exists a DNN solution
\rho_{j}(t,x)
,
g_{j}(t,x,v)

such that

||\rho_{j}-\rho||_{L^{\infty}(K_{1})}<\delta_{j},\qquad||g_{j}-g||_{L^{\infty}(K_{2})}<\delta_{j},

and similarly for their first-order derivatives in
t,x
for
\rho_{j}
and
t,x,v
for
g_{j}
. Here
K_{1}=[0,T]\times\mathcal{D},\,K_{2}=[0,T]\times\mathcal{D}\times\Omega
. For simplicity, we denote the volume of
x
and
v
spaces by
\mathcal{D}
and
\Omega
below. Define

d_{ge,j}^{(1)}(t,x)=\partial_{t}\rho_{j}+\nabla_{x}\cdot\langle vg_{j}\rangle+\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\langle\nabla_{v}g_{j}\rangle.

Since

\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla_{x}\cdot\langle vg\rangle+\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\langle\nabla_{v}g\rangle=0,

subtract
d_{ge,j}^{(1)}
by the above equation, then

d_{ge,j}^{(1)}(t,x)=\partial_{t}(\rho_{j}-\rho)+\nabla_{x}\cdot\langle v(g_{j}-g)\rangle+\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\langle\nabla_{v}(g_{j}-g)\rangle.

(26)

Integrating
|d_{ge,j}^{(1)}|^{2}
over
K_{1}
, one gets

Loss^{(1)}_{\text{ge}}:=||\partial_{t}(\rho_{j}-\rho)+\nabla_{x}\cdot\langle v(g_{j}-g)\rangle+\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\langle\nabla_{v}(g_{j}-g)\rangle||_{L^{2}(K_{1})}^{2},

(27)

which corresponds to the first term in the loss function

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{APNN}}^{\varepsilon}
shown in (
23
).

Thanks to the boundedness of the physical domains, we have

\displaystyle||\partial_{t}(\rho_{j}-\rho)||_{L^{2}(K_{1})}^{2}\leq||\partial_{t}(\rho_{j}-\rho)||_{L^{\infty}(K_{1})}^{2}\cdot T\cdot\mathcal{D}\lesssim\delta_{j}^{2},

\displaystyle||\nabla_{x}\cdot\langle v(g_{j}-g)\rangle||_{L^{2}(K_{1})}^{2}\leq||\nabla_{x}\cdot\langle v(g_{j}-g)\rangle||_{L^{\infty}(K_{1})}^{2}\cdot C_{\Omega}\cdot T\cdot\mathcal{D}\lesssim\delta_{j}^{2},

\displaystyle||\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\langle\nabla_{v}(g_{j}-g)\rangle||_{L^{2}(K_{1})}^{2}\leq C_{\phi}^{2}\,||\nabla_{v}(g_{j}-g)\rangle||_{L^{\infty}(K_{1})}^{2}\cdot\Omega\cdot T\cdot\mathcal{D}\lesssim\delta_{j}^{2},

where we used the fact that
L^{2}
norm is bounded by
L^{\infty}
norm, due to
(t,x,v)
are bounded domains.
Therefore,
Loss^{(1)}_{\text{ge}}\leq O(\frac{1}{j^{2}})
.

Similarly, define

d_{ge,j}^{(2)}=\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}g_{j}+\varepsilon(I-\Pi)(v\cdot\nabla_{x}g_{j}+\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\nabla_{v}g_{j})-2v\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi\,\rho_{j}M(v)+v\cdot\nabla_{x}\rho_{j}M(v)-Q(g_{j}).

Since
\rho
,
g
solves the equation (
8
) which is linear, subtract
d_{ge,j}^{(2)}
by (
8
), we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle d_{ge,j}^{(2)}=

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}(g_{j}-g)+\varepsilon(I-\Pi)\left\{v\cdot\nabla_{x}(g_{j}-g)+\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\nabla_{v}(g_{j}-g)\right\}

(28)

\displaystyle-2v\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi\,(\rho_{j}-\rho)M(v)+v\cdot\nabla_{x}(\rho_{j}-\rho)M(v)-Q(g_{j})+Q(g).

Integrating
|d_{ge,j}^{(2)}|^{2}
over
K_{2}
, one gets

\displaystyle\displaystyle Loss^{(2)}_{\text{ge}}

\displaystyle:=||\varepsilon^{2}\partial_{t}(g_{j}-g)+\varepsilon(I-\Pi)\left\{v\cdot\nabla_{x}(g_{j}-g)+\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\nabla_{v}(g_{j}-g)\right\}

(29)

\displaystyle-2v\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi\,(\rho_{j}-\rho)M(v)+v\cdot\nabla_{x}(\rho_{j}-\rho)M(v)-Q(g_{j})+Q(g)||_{L^{2}(K_{2})}^{2}.

For the first four terms, we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{4}||\partial_{t}(g_{j}-g)||_{L^{2}(K_{2})}^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}||\partial_{t}(g_{j}-g)||_{L^{\infty}(K_{2})}^{2}\cdot\Omega\cdot T\cdot\mathcal{D}\lesssim\varepsilon^{4}\delta_{j}^{2},

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}||(I-\Pi)\left\{v\cdot\nabla_{x}(g_{j}-g)+\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\nabla_{v}(g_{j}-g)\right\}||_{L^{2}(K_{2})}^{2}\leq\varepsilon^{2}||v\cdot\nabla_{x}(g_{j}-g)+\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\nabla_{v}(g_{j}-g)||_{L^{2}(K_{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{2}\left(||\nabla_{x}(g_{j}-g)||_{L^{\infty}(K_{2})}^{2}\cdot C_{\Omega}\cdot T\cdot\mathcal{D}+C_{\phi}^{2}\,||\nabla_{v}(g_{j}-g)||_{L^{\infty}(K_{2})}^{2}\cdot\Omega\cdot T\cdot\mathcal{D}\right)\lesssim\varepsilon^{2}\delta_{j}^{2},

\displaystyle||2v\cdot\nabla_{x}\phi\,(\rho_{j}-\rho)M(v)||_{L^{2}(K_{2})}^{2}\leq 4C_{\phi}^{2}\,||\rho_{j}-\rho||_{L^{\infty}(K_{2})}^{2}\cdot\tilde{C}_{\Omega}\cdot T\cdot\mathcal{D}\lesssim\delta_{j}^{2},

\displaystyle||v\cdot\nabla_{x}(\rho_{j}-\rho)M(v)||_{L^{2}(K_{2})}^{2}\leq||\nabla_{x}(\rho_{j}-\rho)||_{L^{\infty}(K_{2})}^{2}\cdot\tilde{C}_{\Omega}\cdot T\cdot\mathcal{D}\lesssim\delta_{j}^{2}.

For the last two terms in (
29
), thanks to the bounded and linear operator
Q
, one can derive

||Q(g_{j})-Q(g)||_{L^{2}(K_{2})}^{2}=||Q(g_{j}-g)||_{L^{2}(K_{2})}^{2}\lesssim||g_{j}-g||_{L^{2}(K_{2})}^{2}\leq||g_{j}-g||_{L^{\infty}(K_{2})}^{2}\cdot\Omega\cdot T\cdot\mathcal{D}\lesssim\delta_{j}^{2}.

To sum up the above terms, note that
\delta_{j}=\frac{1}{j}
, we have

Loss^{(2)}_{\text{ge}}\lesssim\max\{1,\varepsilon^{2},\varepsilon^{4}\}\delta_{j}^{2}\leq O(\delta_{j}^{2}).

Thus the loss arisen from the governing equations is bounded by
O(\delta_{j}^{2})
, namely

Loss^{(1)}_{\text{ge}}+Loss^{(2)}_{\text{ge}}\leq O(\delta_{j}^{2})=O(\frac{1}{j^{2}}).

(30)

For the inflow boundary data, denote
f_{j}=\rho_{j}M+\varepsilon g_{j}
,
then
Loss_{\text{BC}}
is bounded by

Loss_{\text{BC}}:=||f_{j}-f||_{L^{2}(\gamma_{T}^{-})}^{2}\leq||f_{j}-f||_{L^{\infty}(\gamma_{T}^{-})}^{2}\cdot\Omega\cdot T\cdot|\partial\mathcal{D}|\lesssim\delta_{j}^{2},

(31)

where
\gamma_{-}
is defined in (
2
) and
\gamma_{T}^{-}=[0,T]\times\gamma_{-}
. For the case of specular
reflection BC, the proof is similar and omitted here.

For the initial data, let the APNN approximation be

f_{j}(0,x,v)=\rho_{j}(0,x)M(v)+\varepsilon g_{j}(0,x,v)
, then

\displaystyle\displaystyle Loss_{\text{IC}}

\displaystyle:=||f_{j}(0,x,v)-f_{\text{IC}}(x,v)||_{L^{2}(\mathcal{D}\times\Omega)^{2}}^{2}

(32)

\displaystyle\leq||f_{j}(0,x,v)-f_{\text{IC}}(x,v)||_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{D}\times\Omega)^{2}}^{2}\cdot\Omega\cdot\mathcal{D}\lesssim\delta_{j}^{2}.

Combine (
30
), (
31
) and (
32
), we conclude that the total loss function for the APNN method

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{APNN}}^{\varepsilon}=Loss_{\text{ge}}^{(1)}+Loss_{\text{ge}}^{(2)}+Loss_{\text{BC}}+Loss_{\text{IC}}\leq O(\frac{1}{j^{2}}).

Therefore,
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{APNN}}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{j},g_{j})\to 0
as
j\to\infty
.

4.3
Convergence of the APNN solution

Now we prove that with the parameters
\{m_{[j]},w_{[j]},b_{[j]}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
equipped, the asymptotic-preserving neural network in Theorem
1
indeed converges to the analytic solution of the semiconductor Boltzmann model (
1
).

Theorem 2
.

Let
\{m_{[j]},w_{[j]},b_{[j]}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence given in Theorem
1
, and
f
be the analytic solution to the linear Boltzmann equation (
1
). Denote
f_{j}=\rho_{j}M+\varepsilon g_{j}
, with the sequence of APNN solutions
\{\rho_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
,
\{g_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
defined in Theorem
1
.
Then,
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{APNN}}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{j},g_{j})\to 0
implies that for any
t\in(0,T]
,

||f_{j}(t,\cdot,\cdot,m_{[j]},w_{[j]},b_{[j]})-f(t,\cdot,\cdot)||_{H^{1}(\mathcal{D}\times\Omega)}\leq\frac{C}{j^{2}}\,e^{-Kt}\,.

(33)

Moreover, we conclude that

||f_{j}(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot,m_{[j]},w_{[j]},b_{[j]})-f||_{L^{\infty}\left([0,T];H^{1}(\mathcal{D}\times\Omega)\right)}\to 0,\,\text{ as }j\to\infty.

Proof.

Multiply (
26
) by
M(v)
and
divide (
28
) by
\varepsilon
on both sides, then add them up we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\partial_{t}(f_{j}-f)+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left\{v\cdot\nabla_{x}(f_{j}-f)+\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\nabla_{v}(f_{j}-f)\right\}

(34)

\displaystyle=d_{ge,j}^{(1)}M-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}d_{ge,j}^{(2)}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}Q(f_{j}-f),

where
f=\rho M+\varepsilon g
and
f_{j}=\rho_{j}M+\varepsilon g_{j}
are used.

Inspired by
[
29
, section 3.2]
where the model without scaling is discussed, we consider the following fluctuations around equilibrium for distribution functions
f
and
f_{j}
,

f=f_{\infty}+\varepsilon\sqrt{f_{\infty}}\,h,\qquad f_{j}=f_{\infty}+\varepsilon\sqrt{f_{\infty}}\,h_{j},

(35)

where the stationary solution is given by

f_{\infty}=e^{\phi}\frac{\rho_{\infty}}{||e^{\phi}||_{L^{1}_{x}}}\mathcal{M},

with
\rho_{\infty}
the total mass of
f
that is conserved as the initial data, and
\mathcal{M}
is the normalized Maxwellian

\mathcal{M}(v)=\frac{e^{-|v|^{2}/2}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}
.
In addition, we assume that the potential function
\phi
satisfies

\displaystyle||\phi||_{C^{2}(\mathcal{D})}\leq C_{\phi}
.

Inserting (
35
) into (
34
), one gets the linearized equation for

\tilde{h}:=h-h_{j}
as follows:

\partial_{t}\tilde{h}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(v\cdot\nabla_{x}\tilde{h}+\nabla_{x}\phi\cdot\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}\right)=\underbrace{-d_{ge,j}^{(1)}M+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}d_{ge,j}^{(2)}}_{\text{Term A}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}Q(\tilde{h}).

(36)

Define the operator

G_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}Q-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}v\cdot\nabla_{x},

and we take
\tilde{h}_{\text{IC}}\in H_{x,v}^{1}\cap\text{Ker}(G_{\varepsilon})^{\perp}
.
For simplicity, in the following energy estimates we only consider the periodic BC. Studying the cases of incoming and specular reflection BC are challenging and will be considered a future work.

Multiply (
36
) by
\tilde{h}
and integrate it on
\mathcal{D}\times\Omega
, then

\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}||\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}^{2}=\langle\text{Term A},\tilde{h}\rangle_{L^{2}}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\langle Q(\tilde{h}),\,\tilde{h}\rangle_{L^{2}},

(37)

where we assumed vanishing boundary condition in velocity for
\tilde{h}
.

Thus, for the time evolution for the
L^{2}
norm of
\tilde{h}
, we have

\frac{d}{dt}||\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq-\frac{2\lambda}{\varepsilon^{2}}||\tilde{h}-\Pi\tilde{h}||_{\Lambda}^{2}+\frac{C_{1}}{\varepsilon}\left(\varepsilon||d_{\text{ge},j}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}||\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}^{2}\right),

where the Young’s inequality is used in the last term. Similarly, the time evolution for the
L^{2}
norm of
\nabla_{x}\tilde{h}
is given by

\frac{d}{dt}||\nabla_{x}\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq-\frac{2\lambda}{\varepsilon^{2}}||\nabla_{x}\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{\Lambda}^{2}+\frac{2C_{\phi}}{\varepsilon}||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}\cdot||\nabla_{x}\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}\,.

For the time evolution for the
L^{2}
norm of
\tilde{h}^{\perp}:=\tilde{h}-\Pi\tilde{h}
, one has

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{2}}||\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{\Lambda}^{2}-\frac{C^{{}^{\prime}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{\Lambda}^{2}+C^{{}^{\prime\prime}}||\nabla_{x}\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\tilde{C}^{{}^{\prime}}}{\varepsilon}\left(\varepsilon||d_{\text{ge},j}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)

(38)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+||\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}\cdot||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{L^{2}}+||\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}\cdot||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}\right).

where some properties of
\nabla_{v}Q
are used, see
[
29
, section 1.2 and Appendix B.2.6]
for details.

For the time evolution of the mixed term, one has

\frac{d}{dt}\langle\nabla_{x}\tilde{h},\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}\rangle_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{3}}||\nabla_{x}\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{\Lambda}^{2}+\frac{C^{{}^{\prime}}}{\varepsilon}||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{\Lambda}^{2}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}||\nabla_{x}\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\tilde{C}_{\phi}}{\varepsilon}||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}^{2}\,,

(39)

where we used properties of
Q
and Young’s inequality as follows

\displaystyle\langle Q(\nabla_{x}\tilde{h}),\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}\rangle=\langle Q(\nabla_{x}\tilde{h}-\Pi(\nabla_{x}\tilde{h})),\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq C||\nabla_{x}\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{\Lambda}\cdot||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}||_{\Lambda}\leq C\eta||\nabla_{x}\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{\Lambda}^{2}+\frac{C}{\eta}||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}||_{\Lambda}^{2},

together with the relation

||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}||_{\Lambda}^{2}\leq 2||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{\Lambda}^{2}+2||\nabla_{v}\Pi(\tilde{h})||_{\Lambda}^{2}\leq 2||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{\Lambda}^{2}+2C_{L}||\nabla_{x}h||_{L^{2}}^{2}.

See
[
4
, Appendix B.2.7]
for details. Note that different from
[
4
]
, we have the source term on the RHS and the potential in the convection on the LHS of equation (
36
).

As suggested by
[
4
]
, we define the norm

||\tilde{h}||_{\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon^{\perp}}^{1}}^{2}:=a_{1}||\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+a_{2}||\nabla_{x}\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+a_{3}||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+a_{4}\,\varepsilon\langle\nabla_{x}\tilde{h},\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}\rangle_{L^{2}},

(40)

with
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
,
a_{3}
and
a_{4}
strictly positive and

||\cdot||_{\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon^{\perp}}^{1}}\sim||\cdot||_{H^{1}_{x,v}}
.
Here all the positive constants
C
,
C^{{}^{\prime}}
,
C^{{}^{\prime\prime}}
are generic.
For our linear semiconductor model (
1
), the collision operator
Q
satisfies all the assumptions in
[
29
, section 1.2]
by taking
||\cdot||_{\Lambda}=||\cdot||_{L^{2}_{x,v}}
.

Then adding up (
4.3
), (
4.3
), (
38
) and (
39
) multiplied by
\varepsilon
, one has

\displaystyle\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}||\tilde{h}||_{\mathcal{H}_{\varepsilon^{\perp}}^{1}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{K_{1}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(||\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{\Lambda}^{2}+||\nabla_{x}\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{\Lambda}^{2}+||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{\Lambda}^{2}\right)-K_{2}||\nabla_{x}\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+K_{3}||d_{\text{ge},j}||_{L^{2}}^{2}

(41)

\displaystyle+\frac{C_{\phi}^{{}^{\prime}}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\left(||\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}^{2}+||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{\Lambda}^{2}+||\nabla_{x}\tilde{h}||_{L^{2}}^{2}\right)+C_{\phi}^{{}^{\prime\prime}}||\nabla_{v}\tilde{h}^{\perp}||_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq-K_{0}||\tilde{h}||_{H_{\Lambda}^{1}}^{2}+K_{3}||d_{\text{ge},j}||_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq-K_{0}||\tilde{h}||_{H^{1}}^{2}+K_{3}||d_{\text{ge},j}||_{L^{2}}^{2}\,,

under the reasonable assumption that
||\phi||_{C^{2}(\mathcal{D})}
is bounded and small. The Poincaré inequality is used and we refer to
[
4
, Section 7.2]
for details on the second before last inequality.

Therefore by the Grönwall’s inequality and

\int_{0}^{t}||d_{\text{ge},j}(\tau,\cdot,\cdot,\cdot)||_{L^{2}}^{2}\,d\tau=Loss_{\text{GE}}
, then

\displaystyle\displaystyle||f_{j}(t,\cdot,\cdot)-f(t,\cdot,\cdot)||_{H^{1}(\mathcal{D}\times\Omega)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\,e^{-Kt}\left\{Loss_{IC}+Loss_{\text{GE}}+Loss_{\text{BC}}\right\}

(42)

\displaystyle\leq C\,e^{-Kt}\,\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{APNN}}^{\varepsilon}\leq\frac{C}{j^{2}}\,e^{-Kt}\,,

for any
t\in(0,T]
and positive constants
C
,
K
that are independent of
\varepsilon
. Notice that this convergence owns an
exponentially
decay in time.

Taking
L^{\infty}
norm in
t\in[0,T]
, we conclude that

||f_{j}-f||_{L^{\infty}\left([0,T];H^{1}(\mathcal{D}\times\Omega)\right)}\to 0,\qquad\text{as }j\to\infty.

This indicates that when the total loss function
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{APNN}}^{\varepsilon}(\rho_{j},g_{j})\to 0
as
j\to\infty
, the sequence of APNN solutions
\rho_{j}
,
g_{j}
leading to
f_{j}
also converges to the true solution
f
of the semi-conductor Boltzmann model (
1
). Our convergence result is
uniform
in
\varepsilon
, and can be extended to higher Sobolev space
H^{k}_{x,v}
by using the strategy in
[
4
]
.

We also remark that one could adapt our analysis in Section
4
similarly to other kinetic models, such as linear transport model, the Fokker-Planck equation and the nonlinear Boltzmann or Landau equations, thanks to
[
29
]
where the properties of collision operators in those models were carefully studied. In our future work, we will further improve our analysis result by studying the Barron-type functions
[
14
]
and posterior estimates of the solution error.

5
Numerical Examples

In our numerical experiments, we will show several examples to illustrate effectiveness of our designed APNN method. For the first two numerical examples, we employ the APNN for the deterministic semiconductor Boltzmann model (
1
) and the Boltzmann-Poisson system (
4
). We compare the performance of PINN and APNN methods in different regimes, ranging from the kinetic regime (
\varepsilon\approx O(1)
) to the diffusive regime (
\varepsilon\ll 1

Conclusions and future work

In this paper, we construct an AP neural network in the micro-macro decomposition framework for the semiconductor Boltzmann and the Boltzmann-Poisson system with multiple scales. Based on the Universal Approximation Theorem and the hypocoercivity tools, convergence analysis for both the loss function and NN approximated solution are conducted. Several numerical tests are carried out to demonstrate the performance of the APNNs, both for inverse and forward problems with multiple scaling.

In the future work, we will further improve our analysis result by studying the Barron-type functions
[
14
]
and posterior estimates of the solution error. In addition, we shall study higher dimensional problems and extend to the uncertainty quantification problems with more practical applications.

Velocity discretizations.

For completeness, we briefly mention the velocity discretization similar to what has been studied in
[
20
]
. Set
f(t,x,v)=\psi(t,x,v)M(v)
, where
M(v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-v^{2}}
, with

\psi(t,x,v)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\psi_{k}(t,x)\tilde{H}_{k}(v),

(48)

being the Hermite expansion.
For notation simplicity, we omit the

and
x
dependence of functions below.
Here
\tilde{H}_{k}
are the renormalized Hermite polynomials
defined as
\tilde{H}_{-1}=0
,
\tilde{H}_{0}=1/\pi^{1/4}

\tilde{H}_{j+1}=v\sqrt{\frac{2}{j+1}}\tilde{H}_{j}-\sqrt{\frac{j}{j+1}}\tilde{H}_{j-1}\quad\text{for }j\geq 0,

satisfying
\partial_{v}\tilde{H}_{j}=\sqrt{2j}\,\tilde{H}_{j-1}
.
The inverse Hermite expansion is given by

\psi_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\psi(v_{j})\,\tilde{H}_{k}(v_{j})\,w_{j},

(49)

where
(v_{j},w_{j})
are the points and corresponding weights of the Gauss-Hermite quadrature rule.
Thus the collision operator

in (
1
) can be computed by

Q(f)(v)=M(v)\sum_{j=0}^{N}\sigma(v,v_{j})\,\psi(v_{j})\,w_{j}-\lambda(v)f(v),

with
\lambda(v)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\sigma(v,v_{j})\,w_{j}
. From (
48
) and (
49
), one computes the derivative in
v
by

\displaystyle\partial_{v}\psi

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\psi_{k}\,\partial_{v}\tilde{H}_{k}(v)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\psi_{k}\sqrt{2k}\,\tilde{H}_{k-1}(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\psi(v_{j})\,\tilde{H}_{k}(v_{j})w_{j}\sqrt{2k}\,\tilde{H}_{k-1}(v)=\sum_{j=0}^{N}\psi(v_{j})\,C_{j}(v),

where
C_{j}(v)=\sum_{k=0}^{N}\sqrt{2k}\,\tilde{H}_{k}(v_{j})\tilde{H}_{k-1}(v)w_{j}
,
and can be precomputed before the time iteration.
