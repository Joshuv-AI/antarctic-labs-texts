Efficient Gradient Estimation of Variational Quantum Circuits with Lie Algebraic Symmetries
Mohsen Heidari mheidar@iu.edu, Masih Mozakka mmozakka@iu.edu, Wojciech Szpankowski szpan@purdue.edu

Abstract
Hybrid quantum-classical optimization and learning strategies are among the most promising approaches to harnessing quantum information or gaining a quantum advantage over classical methods. However, efficient estimation of the gradient of the objective function in such models remains a challenge due to several factors including the exponential dimensionality of the Hilbert spaces, and information loss of quantum measurements. In this work, we developed an efficient framework that makes the Hadamard test efficiently applicable to gradient estimation for a broad range of quantum systems, an advance that had been wanting from the outset. Under certain mild structural assumptions, the gradient is estimated with the measurement shots that scale logarithmically with the number of parameters and with polynomial classical and quantum time. This is an exponential reduction in the measurement cost and polynomial speed up in time compared to existing works. The structural assumptions are (1) the dimension of the dynamical Lie algebra is polynomial in the number of qubits, and (2) the observable has a bounded Hilbert-Schmidt norm.

1.2
Overview of the techniques

Our theoretical results rely on the interplay among various representations captured by Lie algebra, and group theoretic structures. We first make a connection between the partial derivatives of
\mathcal{L}(\overrightarrow{a})
to the
adjoint
operator which characterizes the derivative of a differentiable operator exponential. Then, we present a binary encoding technique to study the products of various Pauli string terms. This technique has been used before in the context of the stabilizer formulation in quantum error correction
CRSS 96
;
Got 97
. This representation enables us to make a connection between the matrix differential in Lie algebra and specific group actions involving binary vectors. These vectors are associated to a series of Hadamard tests to be approximated. As a result, the partial derivatives of
\mathcal{L}(\overrightarrow{a})
are written as an infinite-length linear combination of expectation values of Hadamard tests on the output of the ansatz. Then, we show that the number of Hadamard tests in this decomposition is bounded by the dimensionality of the Hamiltonian algebra. Moreover, we show that such an infinite summation can be written as a classical matrix exponential with a size equal to the dimensionality of the Hamiltonian Lie algebra. Hence, when the DLA dimensionality is polynomial in
d
, the number of qubits, the partial derivative can be computed efficiently with
d
Hadamard tests and
\mathsf{poly}(d)
classical time. The Hadamard tests are similar to those developed for single-gate ansätze.

Moreover, we utilize the existing results on statistical estimations of quantum systems, such as shadow tomography to further reduce the complexity of the gradient estimation. Particularly, we develop a novel accelerated method for classical shadow tomography with reduced computational complexity.

Shadow tomography.

Turning the gradient estimation to a series of Hadamard tests has another benefit that can further reduce the number of shots to
O(\log p)

Our approach is based on the
CST

HKP 20
which is a procedure to estimate several observables with minimal sample complexity. We deviate from the classical route and present a new method that accelerates CST using quantum computers. For that, we take a quantum to classical, then to quantum then to classical (QCQC) approach where a quantum algorithm is used to perform CST estimations. Typically, for non-local
O
with bounded norm, one needs to use CST with Clifford measurements. This approach has an almost optimal sample complexity but it has an exponential computational complexity of
2^{\Theta(d)}

2^{d}

matrices. To reduce the running time we modify CST and present a method with an exponential speed-up.

3.1
Binary Encoding of Pauli Strings

We start with a series of notations and definitions and present a binary encoding technique to study the products of various Pauli string terms.
This binary representation allows us to write the partial derivatives of
\mathcal{L}(\overrightarrow{a})
as linear combination of terms related to the Hadamard tests applied to the ansatz output.

Consider the binary vector group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\left\{(0|0),(0|1),(1|0),(1|1)\right\}
with the element-wise modulo two addition, where
(\cdot|\cdot)
is used for notational convenience. We associate the identity and each Pauli operator with elements of this set as

\displaystyle\sigma^{0}\rightarrow(0|0),\qquad\sigma^{1}\rightarrow(0|1),\qquad\sigma^{2}\rightarrow(1|0),\qquad\sigma^{3}\rightarrow(1|1).

Extending this association, the
d
-qubit Pauli strings are characterized with the binary vector space
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2d}
, where each element is denoted by
(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{b}),\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}_{2}
. Any Pauli string
\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}
with
\mathbf{s}\in\left\{0,1,2,3\right\}^{d}
is associated with the tensor product
\tensor_{j}\sigma^{s_{j}}
. From the above formulation, each
\sigma^{s_{j}}
has a binary representation
(s_{j}^{0}|s_{j}^{1})
. Therefore,
\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}
is associated with two binary vectors
\mathbf{s}^{0}=(s^{0}_{1},\cdots,s^{0}_{d}),\mathbf{s}^{1}=(s^{1}_{1},\cdots,s^{1}_{d})
as

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}\rightarrow(\mathbf{s}^{0}|\mathbf{s}^{1}),\qquad\forall\mathbf{s}\in\{0,1,2,3\}^{d}.

For example, the Pauli string
X\tensor Y\tensor X
which is associated with
\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}
with
\mathbf{s}=(1,2,1)
has the following binary encoding:
\quantity((0,1,0) | (1, 0, 1)).

Moreover, we define a symplectic inner product given by

\displaystyle\langle(\mathbf{a}|\mathbf{b}),(\mathbf{a}^{\prime}|\mathbf{b}^{\prime})\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}b_{j}^{\prime}+b_{j}a_{j}^{\prime},

for all
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{a}^{\prime},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{d}
. For instance,
\langle\quantity((0,1) | (1, 0)),\quantity((1,1) | (0, 1))\rangle=2.
Taking modulo two of this product is the key to our representation of products of Pauli strings. For that define:

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}\odot\mathbf{r}:=\mod_{2}(\langle(\mathbf{s}^{0}|\mathbf{s}^{1}),(\mathbf{r}^{0}|\mathbf{r}^{1})\rangle).

(5)

We need to define another group action.

Klein four-group representation.

For distinct elements
a,b,c\in\left\{1,2,3\right\}
defined

\displaystyle 0\circ a=a\qquad a\circ a=0,\qquad a\circ b=b\circ a=c.

(6)

This is the group action used to define the
Klein four-group
over the set
\left\{0,1,2,3\right\}
with
0
being the group’s identity. Extend this notation for vectors
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r}\in\left\{0,1,2,3\right\}^{d}
with the element-wise operation

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}\circ\mathbf{r}:=\quantity(\bfs_1\circ\bfr_1, \cdots, \bfs_d\circ\bfr_d).

Signed product.

Let

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{i,j}:=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if}~(i,j)=(2,1),(3,1),(3,2)\\
1&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

The signed product of any pair
\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r}
is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}\circledast\mathbf{r}:=(\mathbf{s}\odot\mathbf{r})\prod_{j=1}^{d}\varepsilon_{s_{j},r_{j}}.

(7)

Note that
\mathbf{s}\circledast\mathbf{r}\in\left\{-1,0,1\right\}
for any

.

With the above formulations, we present the connection to the Lie bracket and product of Pauli strings. First, we show how the above notations characterize the products of Pauli operators:

Lemma 7
.

The product of any pair of Pauli strings equals the following

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}\sigma^{\mathbf{r}}=i^{\mathbf{s}\circledast\mathbf{r}}\sigma^{\mathbf{s}\circ\mathbf{r}}.

Proof.

Starting from single qubit products, let
a\neq b\neq c
be distinct elements of
\left\{1,2,3\right\}
. Without loss of generality suppose
a<b
. Then, it is not difficult to verify the following relations

\displaystyle 0\circledast a=0,\qquad 0\circ a=a\Rightarrow\sigma^{0}\sigma^{a}=\sigma^{a}

\displaystyle a\circledast a=0,\qquad a\circ a=0\Rightarrow\sigma^{a}\sigma^{a}=\sigma^{0}

\displaystyle a\circledast b=1,\qquad a\circ b=c\Rightarrow\sigma^{a}\sigma^{a}=i\sigma^{c}

\displaystyle b\circledast a=-1,\qquad a\circ b=c\Rightarrow\sigma^{a}\sigma^{a}=-i\sigma^{c},

where we used the fact that
i^{-1}=-i
. Hence, we established the lemma for
d=1
. For general
d
, with the tensor product, we have that

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}\sigma^{\mathbf{r}}

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{j}\sigma^{s_{j}}\sigma^{r_{j}}=\bigotimes_{j}i^{s_{j}\circledast r_{j}}\sigma^{s_{j}\circ r_{j}}

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{j}\quantity(\varepsilon_{s_j, r_j} i^{s_j \odot r_j} \sigma^{s_j \circ r_j}),

where we used (
7
) and the fact that
i^{x}=xi
for
x=\pm 1
. Since
\odot
and
\circ
are element-wise operations, then the above term simplifies to the following

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}\sigma^{\mathbf{r}}

\displaystyle=(\prod_{j}\varepsilon_{s_{j},r_{j}})i^{\sum_{j}s_{j}\odot r_{j}}\sigma^{\mathbf{s}\circ\mathbf{r}}=(\prod_{j}\varepsilon_{s_{j},r_{j}})i^{\mathbf{s}\odot\mathbf{r}}\sigma^{\mathbf{s}\circ\mathbf{r}}.

Since products of
\varepsilon_{s_{j},r_{j}}
belongs to
\{-1,1\}
, then we can push it to the exponent of
i
to get the desired expression:
\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}\sigma^{\mathbf{r}}=i^{\mathbf{s}\circledast\mathbf{r}}\sigma^{\mathbf{s}\circ\mathbf{r}}
.
∎

This lemma leads to the next result that is used to characterize the commutator of Pauli strings.

Lemma 8
.

The Lie bracket (commutator) of any pair of Pauli strings
\sigma^{\mathbf{s}},\sigma^{\mathbf{r}}
is given by

\displaystyle[\sigma^{\mathbf{s}},\sigma^{\mathbf{r}}]=2i(\mathbf{s}\circledast\mathbf{r})\sigma^{\mathbf{s}\circ\mathbf{r}},

Proof.

The proof is based on Lemma
7
:

\displaystyle[\sigma^{\mathbf{s}},\sigma^{\mathbf{r}}]

\displaystyle=\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}\sigma^{\mathbf{r}}-\sigma^{\mathbf{r}}\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}

\displaystyle=i^{\mathbf{s}\circledast\mathbf{r}}\sigma^{\mathbf{s}\circ\mathbf{r}}-i^{\mathbf{r}\circledast\mathbf{s}}\sigma^{\mathbf{r}\circ\mathbf{s}}.

Note that
\mathbf{r}\circ\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{s}\circ\mathbf{r}
. Also note that
\mathbf{s}\circledast\mathbf{r}=0
if
\mathbf{s}\odot\mathbf{r}=0
. Since,
\odot
is symmetric, then
\mathbf{s}\odot\mathbf{r}=0
implying that
\mathbf{s}\circledast\mathbf{r}=0
. In this case, the commutator is equal to zero. When,
\mathbf{s}\circledast\mathbf{r}=1
, then
\mathbf{r}\circledast\mathbf{s}=-1
; implying that, the commutator equals to
[\sigma^{\mathbf{s}},\sigma^{\mathbf{r}}]=i\sigma^{\mathbf{s}\circ\mathbf{r}}-i^{-1}\sigma^{\mathbf{r}\circ\mathbf{s}}=2i\sigma^{\mathbf{s}\circ\mathbf{r}}
. Lastly, when
\mathbf{s}\circledast\mathbf{r}=-1
, then
\mathbf{r}\circledast\mathbf{s}=1
and therefore,
[\sigma^{\mathbf{s}},\sigma^{\mathbf{r}}]=-2i\sigma^{\mathbf{s}\circ\mathbf{r}}
. Combining the three cases completes the proof.
∎

Now, given the definitions for
\circ,\circledast
, and
\odot
, we are ready to present our main results.

Turning the gradient estimation to a series of Hadamard tests has another benefit that can further reduce the number of shots to

CST

HKP 20

correspond to

without any reconfigurations. In that case, having several copies of

Based on Theorem

\displaystyle H_{\mathbf{s}}=R_{\mathbf{s},+}^{\dagger}OR_{\mathbf{s},+}-R_{\mathbf{s},-}^{\dagger}OR_{\mathbf{s},-},

We use CST to exponentially reduce the measurement shots for the gradient estimation when

Accilerated Shadow Tomography.

We build upon CST with random Clifford measurements and present a new method that exponentially accelerates CST using quantum computers. For that, we take a quantum to classical, then to quantum then to classical (QCQC) approach where a quantum algorithm is used to perform CST estimations. Note that the goal is to estimate

for all

using several copies of the output state
\rho^{out}
.

The first stage of our approach is the same as CST. We generate
N=nK
copies of
\rho^{out}
by passing copies of
\rho
through the ansatz, where
n
and
K
will be determined later. For the
i
th copy we choose a unitary
V_{i}
randomly and uniformly from the Clifford group
CL(2^{n})
. We apply the circuit of

on the
i
th copy
\rho^{out}
and measurement in the computational basis. This results in a collection of binary strings
\hat{b}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{d},i\in[N]
. Normally in CST, the measurement outcomes together with

are used to generate classical

as the shadows. Then one directly computes
\tr{H_{\bfs_j} \hat{\rho}_i}
for each

and

We deviate from the original CST and avoid this exponential classical computation. We use the following lemma to proceed.

Lemma 13

For any

and shadow

resulting from

and

\displaystyle\tr{H_{\bfs_j}\hat{\rho}_i}=(2^{d}+1)\expectationvalue{V_i^\dagger H_{\bfs_j} V_i}{b_i}.

(11)

Proof.

Note that the shadow matrix for CST with Clifford measurements is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{i}:=(2^{d}+1)V_{i}^{\dagger}\outerproduct{\hat{b}_i}{\hat{b}_i}V_{i}-I.

It remains to show that
H_{\mathbf{s}_{j}}
is traceless which follows (
10
) and the cyclic property of the trace.
∎

Therefore, in the second stage, we prepare the state

and apply the Clifford circuit

to obtain

as a quantum state. Then, we measure each state with each

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{j,k}:=\frac{2^{d}+1}{n}\sum_{i=n(k-1)+1}^{nk}x_{j,i},\qquad k\in[K],j\in[p].

Then, we compute the median of the averages as the final estimation:

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{j}=\text{median}\{\hat{h}_{j,k}:k\in[K]\}.

Once estimates of
D_{\mathbf{s}_{j}}
are obtained, we can use the subroutine
Subgroup Gradient Estimation

Extensions and Approximations

5.1
Extension to Polynomial Size DLA

Next, we extend the result of the previous section to general PQCs for which DLA has
\mathsf{poly}(d)
dimensionality.

Definition 17
.

For the unitary of the form
e^{iA(\overrightarrow{a})}
where
A(\overrightarrow{a})=\sum_{i}a_{i}G_{i}
with
G_{i}
being traceless Hermitian operators, the set
\mathcal{G}=\{G_{1},\cdots,G_{p}\}
is called the generators of the PQC.

Definition 18

(Dynamical Lie Algebra)
.

Given a PQC with generators
\mathcal{G}
as in Definition
17
, the Dynamical Lie Algebra (DLA)
\mathsf{g}_{A}
is the subalgebra of

\mathfrak{su}(2^{d})
spanned by the repeated nested commutators of the elements in
\mathcal{G}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle i\mathsf{g}_{A}:=\emph{Span}_{\mathbb{R}}\langle iG_{1},\cdots,iG_{p}\rangle_{\text{Lie}}\subseteq\mathfrak{su}(2^{d}),

where
\langle\cdot\rangle_{\text{Lie}}
denotes the Lie closure, obtained by repeatedly taking the nested commutators, and the span is over the real numbers. We say that
\mathsf{g}_{A}
has polynomial size/dimensionality if
\dim(\mathsf{g}_{A})=\mathsf{poly}(d)
as a vector space.

In general, computing the DLA is computationally expensive. One viable approach involves direct construction starting from a set of generators and iteratively commuting them to discover new elements until a basis of the DLA is attained (for example see Algorithm 1 in
LCS+ 22
). The complexity of this approach in general is
O(2^{d})
. However, this is not an issue if the dimension of the DLA to be constructed is promised to by
\mathsf{poly}(d)
.

Theorem 19
.

The partial derivative of
\mathcal{L}
decomposes for a PQC with DLA
\mathsf{g}_{A}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a_{\mathbf{r}}}=\sum_{G\in\mathsf{g}_{A}}\alpha_{G}(r)i\tr{O[G, \rho^{out}]},

where
\alpha_{G}(r)\in\mathbb{R}
are the coefficients. Moreover, if
\mathsf{g}_{A}
has
\mathsf{poly}(d)
dimensionality and its generators have a
\mathsf{poly}(d)
size decomposition in Pauli strings, then there is an algorithm that estimates
\nabla\mathcal{L}(\overrightarrow{a})
with
\mathsf{poly}(d)
Hadamard tests and additional
\mathsf{poly}(d)
classical time.

Proof.

The proof of the Theorem follows from a similar argument as in Theorem
11
. With such an argument we can show that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a_{j}}=i\tr{ O \qty[\rho^{out}, \frac{1-\exp{i\ad_{A}}}{\ad_{A}}(\pdv{A}{a_j})]}.

From the definition of
\frac{1-e^{i\mathsf{ad}_{A}}}{\mathsf{ad}_{A}}
and the fact that
\partialderivative{A}{a_j}=G_{j}
, the above quantity decomposes as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a_{j}}=-i\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i)^{k}}{(k+1)!}\tr{ O \qty[\rho^{out}, (\ad_A)^k(G_j)]},

Note that
\mathsf{ad}_{A}
maps
\mathsf{g}_{A}
unto itself as its
commutator ideal
. That is

\mathsf{ad}_{A}(G)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{j}[X_{j},Y_{j}],

where
X_{j},Y_{j}\in\mathsf{g}_{A}
. Since
\mathsf{g}_{A}
is closed under the Lie bracket, then
[X_{j},Y_{j}]\in\mathsf{g}_{A}
. Hence,
\mathsf{ad}_{A}(G)\in\mathsf{g}_{A}
and it equals to a finite sum of the basis elements
E_{l}
of
\mathsf{g}_{A}
as

\displaystyle\mathsf{ad}_{A}(G_{j})=\sum_{l=1}^{d_{\mathsf{g}}}\alpha_{l,j}E_{l}.

Therefore, the above summation can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a_{j}}=-i\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i)^{k}}{(k+1)!}\sum_{l=1}^{d_{\mathsf{g}_{A}}}\alpha_{l,j}\tr{ O \qty[\rho^{out}, E_l]}.

Note that
\mathsf{ad}_{A}(G_{j})
can be viewed as a vector
(\alpha_{1,j},\cdots,\alpha_{d_{\mathsf{g}_{A}},j})
in the
E_{l}
basis. Moreover,
\mathsf{ad}_{A}
can be viewed as a linear transformation (matrix)
T_{A}
in the
E_{l}
basis. This matrix is
d_{\mathsf{g}_{A}}\times d_{\mathsf{g}_{A}}
hence is
\mathsf{poly}(d)
in size. With that taken into account, the partial derivative can be written as

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\Loss}{a_{j}}\equiv\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{(-i)^{k}}{(k+1)!}T_{A}^{k}G_{j},\qquad\forall j\in[p].

Where
G_{j}
is understood as a vector in the
E_{l}
basis. Now let

B_{\mathsf{g}_{A}}=(1-e^{-iT_{A}})T_{A}^{-1}.

This matrix can be computed classically in
\mathsf{poly}(d_{\mathsf{g}_{A}})=\mathsf{poly}(d)
time. To compute the gradient from this vector representation, one first needs to estimate all
R_{l}:=i\tr{ O \qty[\rho^{out}, E_l]}
and compute the following

\displaystyle\nabla\mathcal{L}=B_{\mathsf{g}_{A}}\overrightarrow{R}.

(12)

With that the proof is complete.
∎

5.2.2
Unbiased Estimation via Randomization

The infinite sum can be estimated via a randomization technique. We show that the partial derivative in Theorem
9
can be written as an expectation value of a function of the Poisson random variable. Let
K
be the Poisson random variable with rate
\lambda=2
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big\{K=k\Big\}=\frac{2^{k}}{k!}e^{-2},\qquad k=0,1,\cdots

By sampling
K
from this distribution we can estimate the partial derivative expression of Theorem
9
. Note that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a_{\mathbf{r}}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(2)^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}X(k)

\displaystyle X(k):=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{2}\sum_{\mathbf{s}_{1}\in\mathcal{S}}\cdots\sum_{\mathbf{s}_{k}\in\mathcal{S}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}\quantity( a_{\bfs_j} \qty( \bfs_j \circledast(\bfs_1 \circ\cdots\circ\bfs_{j-1}\circ\bfr)))D_{\mathbf{s}_{1}\circ\cdots\circ\mathbf{s}_{k}\circ\mathbf{r}}.

Hence, by sampling from this distribution we can compute an unbiased estimate of the partial derivative.

5.3
Short-term Hamiltonian

The above procedure can be made significantly more efficient if one guarantees that the parameter range is small, that is
|a_{\mathbf{s}}|\leq\epsilon

is the time step. In that case, each partial derivative is approximated in

classical computation with one Hadamard test of Figure
1

Corollary 22
.

If
\max_{\mathbf{s}\in\mathcal{S}}|a_{\mathbf{s}}|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{p(1+2\epsilon)}

\epsilon

\partialderivative{\Loss}{\qas}\approx D_{\mathbf{s}}

Concluding Remarks

This paper provides a framework to estimate the gradient of generic PQCs via Hadamard tests for Pauli operators followed by classical post-processing. It is shown that the proposed approach is polynomial in classical and quantum resources when the DLA of the associated Hamiltonian of the PQC has a dimensionality polynomial in the number of qubits. Moreover, this method does not change the ansatz structure and can be used to reduce the measurement shot complexity to scale logarithmically with the number of parameters. The results would be beneficial in various optimization or learning quantum algorithms that rely on the estimation of the gradient.

Our results are steps toward a unified framework for efficiently estimating the gradient of an arbitrary parameterized circuit without making changes to its unitary. As future work, one can extend the proposed framework to the estimation of higher-order derivatives such as the Hessian or the Fubini-Study metric defined for a parameterized quantum system. For that, one needs to define a second-order Hadamard test strategy for measuring the double derivatives. Another future work is to extend this strategy to multi-layered PQCs where each layer might be a black box unitary. Finally, deriving lower bounds on the classical and quantum resources needed to estimate the gradient or higher-order derivatives is another important direction.

\displaystyle\left.\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_{1}}\right|_{a_{1}=0}

\displaystyle=D_{1}\left(1-\frac{4}{6}a_{2}^{2}+\dots\right)+D_{3}\left(a_{2}-\frac{8}{24}a_{2}^{3}+\dots\right)

\displaystyle=D_{1}\frac{1}{a_{2}}\left(\sum_{p}\frac{(-1)^{p}}{(2p+1)!}(2a_{2})^{2p+1}\right)+D_{3}\frac{1}{a_{2}}\left(\sum_{p}\frac{(-1)^{p}}{(2p+2)!}(2a_{2})^{2p+2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2a_{2}}\left(D_{1}\sin{2a_2}+D_{3}(1-\cos{2a_2})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin{a_2}}{a_{2}}\left(\cos{a_2}D_{1}+\sin{a_2}D_{3}\right).

Which is exactly the expression we got analytically. In this example, we can see how our method is akin to approximating a function with a series expansion. The power of this approach lies in the group structure of the Pauli matrices, which produces predictable patterns. Thus, we are dealing with a repeating structure that makes use of a subset of
D_{i}
s. Another point is that
D_{i}
only makes use of
\rho_{out}
. We can calculate these
D_{i}
and approximate the derivative to any arbitrary precision.

Appendix C
Summary of Related Works

Parameter Shift Rule.

There have been several approaches to estimating the gradient
[
13
,
32
,
41
,
17
,
19
,
36
,
31
,
48
]
. The zeroth-order approach (e.g., finite differences) evaluates the objective function in the neighborhood of the parameters. Although it is a generic approach, recent studies showed their drawbacks in terms of convergence rate
[
19
]
. First-order methods (e.g., parameter shift rule) directly calculate the partial derivatives
[
36
]
. When the ansatz is of the form
U(\overrightarrow{a})=\prod_{j=1}^{p}e^{-ia_{j}\sigma^{\mathbf{s}_{j}}}
, the parameter shift rule implies that:

\partialderivative{\Loss}{a_j}=\langle\mathcal{L}(\overrightarrow{a}+\frac{\pi}{4}e_{j})\rangle-\langle\mathcal{L}(\overrightarrow{a}-\frac{\pi}{4}e_{j})\rangle,

where
e_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the
j
th canonical vector, that is
e_{j,j}=1
and
e_{j,r}=0
for all
r\neq j
.

Stochastic
PSR
:

Stochastic parameter shift rule was proposed in
[
3
]
to measure the derivative for unitary operators of the form
e^{i(\theta\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}+B)}
, where
\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}
is a Pauli string and
B
is an arbitrary Hermitian operator. The authors showed that the derivative is equal to

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\Loss}{\theta}=\int_{0}^{1}C_{+}(\theta,t)-C_{-}(\theta,t)dt,

where
C_{\pm}(\theta,t):=\tr{O V_\pm(\theta, t) \rho V_\pm^\dagger(\theta, t)}
, with

\displaystyle V_{\pm}(\theta,t):=e^{it(\theta\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}+B)}e^{\pm i\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}}e^{i(1-t)(\theta\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}+B)}.

Then the authors present a Monte Carlo strategy to estimate this integral. A more general variant of the parameter shift rule has been introduced
[
46
]
. The considered general ansatz of the form
U(\theta)=e^{i(\theta A+B)}
for generic Hermitian
A
and
B
. Given the spectral decomposition of
A
the paper provides an explicit formula for the derivative of the objective function. This is done via a Discrete Fourier series approach.

Such methods for generic ansatz
e^{i(\theta A+B)}
require not only to perturb the parameters but also to change the unitaries involved. As a result,
to modify not just the parameters of the
PQC
, but also change the unitaries that appear. In practice, such modifications require a re-evaluation of the schedule of the underlying quantum-control system and hence are at a disadvantage. Moreover, the stochastic
PSR
has a high estimation variance. This is because the above integral is estimated by sampling values of
s
uniformly in the interval
(0,1)
and then
calculating the costs with a finite-shot estimate. In addition, this method leads to a bigger number of unique circuits to compute the derivative, increasing the compilation overhead for both hardware and simulator implementations.

Nyquist
PSR
:

Recently
[
42
]
proposed a “proper” shift rule for
PQC
of the form
e^{i(\theta A+B)}
where only the parameters are shifted without any other modifications of the ansatz. The method was called
Nyquist parameter shift rule
and relies on a beautiful connection between the Nyquist-Shannon Sampling theorem and the Fourier series that was observed earlier in
[
46
,
43
]
. This paper shows that if
f(x)=tr\{OU\rho U^{\dagger}\}
with
U=e^{ixH+B}
and
K
being the difference between the maximum and minimum eigenvalues of
A
, then the Fourier spectrum of
f
is contained in
[-K,K]
. Hence, the Nyquist-Shannon Sampling theorem can be used to estimate the derivative of the objective function. The proposed method, however, has a low approximation error when the parameter value is large enough. More precisely, the approximation error is
O(\frac{1}{c^{2}})
as long as
\theta=(1-\Omega(1))c
, where
c
is the maximum magnitude of a parameter value. Our method is suitable when the parameter value is small.

Trotterization.

In this approach the non-product unitary is approximated via the Suzuki-Trotter transformation
[
40
]
which states that for any operators
A_{1},A_{2},\cdots,A_{k}
, that do not necessarily commute with each other, the following holds

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\exp{A_j/n }\Big)^{n}=\exp\{\sum_{j=1}^{k}A_{j}\big\}.

The Trotter formula has been used in literature to derive approximations for generic PQCs
[
49
,
29
,
33
]
. However, implementing the above approximation may lead to a high gate complexity and is not preferable in scenarios where direct implementation is available.

[A,B]:=AB-BA,

\displaystyle\derivative{\exp{X(\tau)}}{\tau}=\exp{X(\tau)}\frac{1-\exp{-\ad_X}}{\mathsf{ad}_{X}}\derivative{X(\tau)}{\tau}.

(18)

where it is assumed that

Next, we prove the following lemma which is in a different ordering of the above equation.

Lemma 23
.

Suppose

for some Hermitian operator valued function

\displaystyle\derivative{U}{\tau}=-\frac{1-\exp{i\ad_H}}{\mathsf{ad}_{H}}(\derivative{H(\tau)}{\tau})U,

where it is assumed that
H(\tau)
is differentiable.

Proof.

First note that for any operator
X\in\mathsf{g}

(\mathsf{ad}_{X})^{\dagger}=\mathsf{ad}_{X^{\dagger}}.

We can write
U=(e^{-iH(\tau)})^{\dagger}
. Therefore, given that
\derivative{X^\dagger}{\tau}=(\derivative{X}{\tau})^{\dagger}
, from (
18

\displaystyle\derivative{U}{\tau}

\displaystyle=\quantity(\dv{e^{-iH(\tau)}}{\tau})^{\dagger}=\quantity( \exp{-iH(\tau)} \frac{1-\exp{-\ad_{-iH}}}{\ad_{-iH}}\dv{(-iH(\tau))}{\tau})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle\quantity( \exp{-iH(\tau)} \frac{1-\exp{+i\ad_H}}{\ad_H}\dv{H(\tau)}{\tau})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\quantity( \frac{1-\exp{+i\ad_H}}{\ad_H}\dv{H(\tau)}{\tau})^{\dagger}U.

Note that for any
H\in\mathsf{g}
we have the following equality by its convergent power series:

\displaystyle\frac{1-\exp{-\ad_H}}{\mathsf{ad}_{H}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{(k+1)!}(\mathsf{ad}_{H})^{k}.

(19)

Therefore, applying this equation for the operator
\frac{1-e^{i\mathsf{ad}_{H}}}{\mathsf{ad}_{H}}
, the derivative equals to the following

\displaystyle\quantity(-i\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(i)^k}{(k+1)!} (\ad_H)^k (\dv{H}{\tau}))^{\dagger}U=i\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-i)^{k}}{(k+1)!}\quantity((\ad_H)^k (\dv{H}{\tau}))^{\dagger}U.

Note that for any

the following identity holds

(\mathsf{ad}_{X}(Y))^{\dagger}=-\mathsf{ad}_{X}^{\dagger}(Y^{\dagger}).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\derivative{U}{\tau}=i\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i)^{k}}{(k+1)!}(\mathsf{ad}_{H})^{k}(\derivative{H}{\tau})U=-\frac{1-\exp{+i\ad_H}}{\mathsf{ad}_{H}}(\derivative{H}{\tau})U.

This is the desired expression.
∎

From this lemma, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial U}{\partial a_{\mathbf{s}}}=-\frac{1-\exp{i\ad_{A}}}{\mathsf{ad}_{A}}(\partialderivative{A}{\qas})U.

This gives the first part of (
17
).
Next, from (
18

\displaystyle\frac{\partial U^{\dagger}}{\partial a_{\mathbf{s}}}=U^{\dagger}\frac{1-\exp{+i\ad_{A}}}{\mathsf{ad}_{A}}(\partialderivative{A}{\qas}),

where we used the fact that
A
is Hermitian. Therefore, the partial derivative of

equals to

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\rho^{out}}{\qas}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1-\exp{i\ad_{A}}}{\mathsf{ad}_{A}}(\partialderivative{A}{\qas})U\rho U^{\dagger}+U\rho U^{\dagger}\frac{1-\exp{i\ad_{A}}}{\mathsf{ad}_{A}}(\partialderivative{A}{\qas})

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\exp{-i\ad_{A}}}{\mathsf{ad}_{A}}(\partialderivative{A}{\qas})\rho^{out}+\rho^{out}\frac{1-\exp{+i\ad_{A}}}{\mathsf{ad}_{A}}(\partialderivative{A}{\qas}).

By simplifying the terms in the right-hand side, the partial derivative can be written as the commutator:

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\rho^{out}}{\qas}=\quantity[\rho^{out}, \frac{1-\exp{i\ad_{A}}}{\ad_{A}}(\pdv{A}{\qas})].

Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a_{\mathbf{s}}}=\tr{ O \frac{\partial}{\partial\qas}\rho^{out}}=\tr{ O \qty[\rho^{out}, \frac{1-\exp{i\ad_{A}}}{\ad_{A}}(\pdv{A}{\qas})]}.

Next, we use the definition of

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial a_{\mathbf{s}}}=-i\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i)^{k}}{(k+1)!}\tr{ O \qty[\rho^{out}, (\ad_A)^k(\qps)]},

In this section we present some of the technical lemmas regarding the Hadamard test.

Lemma 26
.

For each
D_{\mathbf{s}}
in (

\displaystyle H_{\mathbf{s}}=R_{\mathbf{s},+}^{\dagger}OR_{\mathbf{s},+}-R_{\mathbf{s},-}^{\dagger}OR_{\mathbf{s},-},

Recall that

\displaystyle D_{\mathbf{s}}=i\tr{O[\qps, \rho^{out}]}.

Moreover, note from

\displaystyle[\sigma^{\mathbf{s}},B]=i\quantity(R_\bfs(\frac{\pi}{2}) B R_\bfs(\frac{\pi}{2})^\dagger- R_\bfs(-\frac{\pi}{2}) B R_\bfs(-\frac{\pi}{2})^\dagger),

\displaystyle D_{\mathbf{s}}

\displaystyle=-\tr{O \qty(R_\bfs(\frac{\pi}{2}) \rho^{out} R_\bfs(\frac{\pi}{2})^\dagger- R_\bfs(-\frac{\pi}{2}) \rho^{out} R_\bfs(-\frac{\pi}{2})^\dagger)}

Lemma 27
.

The partial derivative observables
H_{\mathbf{s}}
satisfy
\tr{H_\bfs^2}\leq 4\tr{O^2}
for any
\mathbf{s}\in\left\{0,1,2,3\right\}^{d}

Proof.

For the proof of the second claim note that

\displaystyle H_{\mathbf{s}}^{2}=R_{\mathbf{s},+}^{\dagger}O^{2}R_{\mathbf{s},+}+R_{\mathbf{s},-}^{\dagger}O^{2}R_{\mathbf{s},-}-R_{\mathbf{s},+}^{\dagger}OR_{\mathbf{s}}(\pi)OR_{\mathbf{s},-}-R_{\mathbf{s},-}^{\dagger}OR_{\mathbf{s}}(-\pi)OR_{\mathbf{s},+}

Taking the trace of this operator gives

\displaystyle\tr{H_\bfs^2}=2\tr{O^2}-\tr{ O R_{\bfs}(\pi) O R_{\bfs}(-\pi)}-\tr{O R_{\bfs}(-\pi) O R_{\bfs}(\pi)}.

Note that
R_{\mathbf{s}}(\pi)=-i\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}
and
R_{\mathbf{s}}(-\pi)=i\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}

\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}=\sum_{b}\lambda_{b}\outerproduct{b}{b}

\ket{b}
is an eigenstate of
\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}
and
\lambda_{b}\in\{-1,1\}
is the corresponding eigenvalue. As a result,

\displaystyle R_{\mathbf{s}}(\pi)=e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}}=\sum_{b}e^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}\lambda_{b}}\outerproduct{b}{b}=\sum_{b}(i)^{-\lambda_{b}}\outerproduct{b}{b}=-i\sigma^{\mathbf{s}}

where we used the fact that

\displaystyle\tr{ O R_{\bfs}(\pi) O R_{\bfs}(-\pi)}=\tr{ O R_{\bfs}(-\pi) O R_{\bfs}(\pi)}=\tr{ O \qps O \qps}.

The observable

has a Pauli decomposition of the form

with

\displaystyle\tr{ O \qps O \qps}=\sum_{\mathbf{t}}\sum_{\mathbf{r}}a_{\mathbf{t}}a_{\mathbf{r}}\tr{\sigma^\bft\qps\sigma^\bfr\qps}.

\displaystyle\tr{\sigma^i\sigma^j\sigma^i\sigma^j}=\begin{cases}-2&\text{if}~i\neq j\neq 0\\
2&\text{otherwise.}\\

\displaystyle\tr{ O \qps O \qps}=2^{d}\sum_{\mathbf{t}}(a_{\mathbf{t}})^{2}(-1)^{\absolutevalue{\set{i: t_i\neq s_i\neq 0}}}.

Appendix F
Classical Shadow Tomography

For completeness, in this section we briefly describe the classical shadow tomography procedure. For more details see
[
18
]
. Classical shadow tomography is a technique used in quantum computing to efficiently learn properties of a quantum state using only a few measurements. It was introduced to extract useful information from quantum states without requiring a full quantum state tomography, which is costly in terms of the number of measurements and computational resources.

More precisely, let
O_{j},j\in[M]
be a set of observables. The goal is to estimate the expectation value of these observable for measuring an unknown state
\rho
in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. For that, several copies of
\rho
are provided. CST is a procedure with minimal sample (copy) complexity.

Theorem 28

(
[
18
]
)
.

Suppose the observables
O_{j},j\in M
are traceless, then the expectation values
\tr{O_j\rho},j\in[M]
can be approximated up to an additive error
\epsilon
with probability
(1-\delta)
given

O\quantity(\frac{1}{\epsilon^2} \log\frac{M}{\delta} \max_j \norm{O}_{\text{shadow}}^2)

copies of
\rho
.

The shadow norm is a measure that resembles the variance in the worst case state.

In what follows we describe the steps in this procedure for a generic state
\rho
in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
..

First, generate a unitary operator
U
randomly from a class of choices
\mathcal{U}
to be determined. Apply
U
on the input state resulting in the state
U^{\dagger}\rho U
. Measure the resulted state in the canonical basis
\ket{j},j\in[\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}]
. From Born’s rule the probability of getting the output
j
is
p_{j}=\matrixelement{j}{U^{\dagger}\rho U}{j}
.
Given an outcome
j
, define
\omega_{j}=U\outerproduct{j}{j}U^{\dagger}.
The expectation
\mathbb{E}_{\sim(J,U)}[\omega_{J}]
over the measurement randomness (
p_{j}
) and the choice of
U
equals to
\mathcal{M}[\rho],
where
\mathcal{M}
is a mapping defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}[O]:=\mathbb{E}_{U}\Big[\sum_{j\in[\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}]}\matrixelement{j}{U^{\dagger}O U}{j}~U\outerproduct{j}{j}U^{\dagger}\Big],

(20)

for any operator
O
on
\mathcal{H}
.
Observe that
\mathcal{M}
is a linear mapping on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and hence has an inverse denoted by
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
. We note that
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
is the shadow channel
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
introduced in
[
18
]
. We apply
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
on
\omega_{j}
resulting in the so called shadow

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}:=\mathcal{M}^{-1}\big[U\outerproduct{j}{j}U^{\dagger}\big].

(21)

Note that
\hat{\rho}
is a classical matrix and hence can be copied several times. Moreover,
\hat{\rho}
is not a valid density operator as it is not necessarily a positive semi-definite matrix. However, it is an unbiased estimate of the original state.

When
\mathcal{U}
is tomographically complete, the classical shadow
\hat{\rho}
is unbiased, that is
\mathbb{E}_{U,J}[\hat{\rho}]=\rho
.

Definition 29
.

The shadow norm of any operator
O
on
\mathcal{H}
is

\displaystyle\norm{O}_{\emph{shadow}}:=\max_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}[H]}\Big(\sum_{j\in[\text{dim}_{\mathcal{H}}]}\hskip-10.0pt\matrixelement{j}{U^{\dagger}\sigma U}{j}~\expectationvalue{U\CM^{-1}[O]U^\dagger}{j}^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

F.1
CST with Pauli Measurements

Shadow tomography with Pauli measurements.
Suppose the observable
O_{j}
act non trivially on at most
k
qubits. For that
V
in CST is the tensor product of randomly chosen Pauli operators:

\displaystyle V=V_{1}\tensor\cdots\tensor V_{d}\in CL(2)^{\tensor d},

where each
V_{j}
is chosen randomly and uniformly from the Clifford group
CL(2)
. In this case,
\mathcal{U}=CL(2)^{\tensor d}
. Moreover, the shadow matrix is computed as

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}:=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{d}\quantity(3V_j^\dagger\ketbra{\hat{b}_j}V_j - I).

In that case, the shadow norm is bounded by the locality of the observables as

\displaystyle\norm{O_j}_{\emph{shadow}}\leq 2^{k}\norm{O_j}_{\infty}

As a result the sample complexity of CST is given by

n=O\quantity(\frac{4^k}{\epsilon^2}\log m \max_j \norm{O_j}^2_\infty).

The CST algorithm runs in
\tilde{O}\quantity(2^{\Theta(k)}m\log m)
classical time.

F.2
CST with Clifford Measurements

The
Clifford group
is a set of unitary operations that map Pauli operators to other Pauli operators under conjugation. For a system of
d
qubits, the Clifford group
\mathcal{C}_{d}
consists of unitaries
U
such that for any Pauli string
P
:

UPU^{\dagger}=P^{\prime},

where
P^{\prime}
is another Pauli operator.

Clifford circuits are particularly useful because they can be efficiently simulated classically, and their structure allows for easy manipulation of Pauli observables, making them useful for shadow tomography. In that case,
V
is chosen randomly from the Clifford group. The shadow norm is bounded as

\displaystyle\norm{O_j}_{\emph{shadow}}\leq\sqrt{3\tr{O^2}},

when
\tr{O^2}<\infty
. The shadow matrix is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}=(2^{d}+1)V^{\dagger}\outerproduct{\hat{b}}{\hat{b}}V-I.

As a result the sample complexity of CST is bounded as

n=O\quantity(\frac{1}{\epsilon^2}\log m \max_j \tr{O^2_j}),

and the CST algorithm runs
\tilde{O}\quantity(2^{\Theta(d)}m \log m)
classical time.
