A Theory of Quantum Jumps and Its Applications to Lyman and Balmer Series
Z.E. Musielak

Abstract
A theory of quantum jumps is developed by using a new asymmetric equation, which is complementary to the Schrödinger equation. The new equation displays Bohr’s rules for quantum jumps, and its solutions demonstrate that once a quantum jump takes place at a random time, then its evolution is continuous and coherent. The temporal solutions are used to determine time-scales of the quantum jumps, which are very short but finite. The spatial solutions are used to find the radial probability density of finding the electron after its jump. The obtained results are applied to the Lyman and Balmer series absorption, and their experimental verification is discussed.



1
Introduction

The randomness and abruptness of quantum jumps between two
discrete energy levels in an atom was the main feature of quantum
theory originally formulated by Bohr [1] in 1913. His main argument
was that electrons change their allowed energy levels by absorbing
or emitting photons of very specific energies, and that the quantum
jumps corresponding to absorption and emission are instantaneous.
This notion of random and instantaneous quantum jumps became
one of the pillars foundation of the Copenhagen interpretation of
quantum mechanics (QM) (e.g., [2-5]). However, Schrödinger
[6] argued against such instantaneous quantum jumps by pointing
out that no process in the real world happens instantenously or
in zero time.

The first direct observations of quantum jumps in individual atoms
exposed to electromagnetic (EM) fields took place in 1986 and
were reported in [7-9]. The collected data showed that the
atoms flipped between a state that allowed them to emit a
photon to a state that did not, and that the atoms remain in
one state, or the other, for periods ranging from a few tenths
of a second to a few seconds before jumping again. Other
indepedent experiments confirmed the results and demonstrated
that the jumps were random and abrupt (e.g., [10-12], and
references therein). A different view has emerged from the
experimental results obtained by Minev et al [13], who
confirmed that quantum jumps come at random times, but
once they come, the evolution of each completed jump is
a continuous and coherent physical process that unfolds in
a finite time. It was suggested that a quantum trajectories
theory could account for the experimental data [14-16].

The theoretical approach described in this paper is different
from any previous attempt to account for quantum jumps,
including the quantum trajectories theory. The new theory
of quantum jumps developed herein is based on a new
asymmetric equation (NAE) that was derived from the
irreducible representations (irreps) of the extended Galilean
group [17]. Since the same irreps can be used to derive
the Schrödinger equation (SE), and since there are other
similarities between the equations, the SE and NAE are
considered to be complementary to each other. The
main physical implication of this complementarity is
that quantum phenomena, like quantum jumps or
quantum measurements, are not described by the SE,
but they may be represented by the NAE.

The NAE naturally describes interactions of quantum systems
with environments that are subjected to monitoring by measuring
devices. Specifically, the NAE can directly describe interactions
of a single quantum particle with a measuring apparatus, or the
process of absorption or emission of electromagnetic (EM) waves
by quantum systems. Thus, while the SE is used to formulate
unitary quantum theories, the NAE can also be used to formulate
non-unitary theories because of its second-order derivatives
in time (see Section 2, for details). Random and instantaneous
quantum jumps violate the unitary evolution (e.g., [2-13]),
thus, an attempt herein to describe them by using the NAE.
Similarly, the quantum measurement problem is a non-unitary
evolution of a quantum system during its measurement (e.g.,
[18-21]) and, therefore, the NAE was used to solve it [22].

It was recently demonstrated that a non-unitary theory of
quantum measurements can be formulated using the NAE,
and that its predictions are consistent with Born’s position
measurement postulates [22]. Moreover, since the origin
and nature of dark matter remains unknown (e.g., [23-26]),
and since all previous attempts to formulate a theory of dark
matter (DM) based on the SE (e.g., [27,28]) failed (e.g., 29,30]),
a theory of DM using the NAE was developed [22], and applied
to construct models of galactic cold DM halos [31,32], whose
theoretical predictions are proposed to be verifed by specific
astronomical observations [33]. The NAE was also used
to describe propagation of classical (acoustic) waves in
isothermal and stratified media [34].

The main aim of this paper is to develop a theory of quantum
jumps using the NAE. The theory is applied to a hydrogen
atom and used to describe absorption Lyman and Balmer
series. It is shown that Bohr’s rule for quantum jumps
(e.g., [3-5]) emerges naturally from the NAE, and that the
NAE can be solved analytically by separation of variables.
To preserve the coherence of the wavefunction, the solutions
are restricted to the EM frequencies corresponding directly to
the jumps; in other words, measurements that destroy the
coherence are not accounted for by the solutions. The
temporal solutions of the NAE show that time-scales for
the quantum jumps are very short but finite. The spatial
solutions are used to obtain the radial probability densities,
which are normal distributions with the location of their
centers in agreement with Bohr’s rule for quantum jumps.
Experimental verification of the obtained results is also
discussed.

The paper is organized as follows: the basic equations resulting
from the Galilean group are given and discussed in Section 2;
the governing equation and its solutions are presented in
Section 3; the solutions are applied to the Lyman and Balmer
absorption processes in Section 4; experimental verification
of the obtained results is discussed in Section 5; and Conclusions
are given in Section 7.

2
Schrödinger and new asymmetric equations

In Galilean relativity, space and time are separated and described
by different metrics, which are invariant with respect to rotations,
translations and boosts that form the Galilean group (e.g., [35,36]).
The irreducible representations (irreps) of the Galilean group were
originally obtained and classified by Bergmann [37], who demonstrated
that only scalar and spinor irreps are physical in Galilean space and
time. The spinor irreps were used by Lévy-Leblond [38,39] to
obtain his spinor wave equation that describes elementary particles
with spins in nonrelativistic QM. In this paper, only the scalar irreps
are considered.

In the extended Galilean group (e.g., [38,39]), the space and
time translations are combined into one subgroup,
T(3+1)
,
whose properties are the same as the
T(3+1)
subgroup of
the Poincaré group [35,36]. Since
T(3+1)
is an invariant
subgroup, its irreps are the irreps of the entire extended
Galilean group, and they can be used to derive equations
of nonrelativistic and relativistic QM [40]. According to
Wigner [41], an elementary particle is uniquely identified
if its wavefunction transforms as one of the irreps of the
extended Galilean group. For a scalar wavefunction
\phi(t,\mathbf{x})
, the Wigner condition is given by the
following eigenvalue equations
i\partial_{t}\phi=\omega\phi
and
-i\nabla\phi=\mathbf{k}\phi
, where

\partial_{t}=\partial/\partial t
, and
\omega
and

\mathbf{k}
are the eigenvalues that label the irreps
[42,43].

Using the eigenvalue equations and the de Broglie relationship
(e.g., [3-5]), the following two asymmetric equations are
derived [17,32]:

\left[i{{\partial}\over{\partial t}}+\frac{\hbar}{2m}\nabla^{2}\right]\phi_{S}(t,\mathbf{x})=0\ ,

(1)

which is the Schrödinger equation (e.g., [3-5]), and

\left[\frac{i}{\omega}{{\partial^{2}}\over{\partial t^{2}}}+\frac{\hbar}{2m}{\mathbf{k}}\cdot\nabla\right]\phi_{A}(t,\mathbf{x})=0\ ,

(2)

which is called the new asymmetric equation or NAE [31-33].
Since both equations are derived from the same irreps,
they may be considered as complementary to each other.
However, the main difference between them is that the
SE is the first-order in its time derivative, thus allows
for unitary theories, while the NAE can also be used to
construct non-unitary theories because of its second-order
time derivative. In addition, the NAE requires specifying
the eigenvalues
\omega
and
\mathbf{k}
that are
explicitly present in the equation.

There are processes in nonrelativistic QM, such as the
quantum measurement problem and quantum jumps,
that are considered to be non-unitary [3-5]. In these
processes, the external input/output of EM radiation
plays important role, and the NAE directly allows
accounting for this radiation by specifying its

\omega
and
\mathbf{k}
. A possible solution
of the quantum measurement problem by using the
NAE is presented in [22]. In this paper, the NAE is
used to formulate a theory of quantum jumps.

3
Basic equations and their solutions

3.1
The governing equation

To apply the NAE to quantum jumps, it is required that
the labels of the irreps
\omega
and
\mathbf{k}
are
specified. Let
\omega=\omega_{o}
,
E_{o}=\hbar\omega_{o}
, and
{\mathbf{k}}={\mathbf{k_{o}}}
,
where
\omega_{o}
and
{\mathbf{k_{o}}}
represent
frequency and wavevector of EM radiation absorbed or
emitted by a hydrogen atom. Then, Eq. (
2
) can
be written in the following form

\left[\frac{i\hbar}{\omega_{o}}\left({{\partial^{2}}\over{\partial t^{2}}}\right)+\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}({\mathbf{k}_{o}}\cdot\nabla)+V(r)\right]\phi_{A}(t,\mathbf{r})=0\ ,

(3)

where
V(r)=-e^{2}/(4\pi\epsilon r)
is the Coulomb potential.

To determine
\omega_{o}
and
{\mathbf{k_{o}}}
,
one may use

\Delta E=R_{E}\left(\frac{n_{f}^{2}-n_{i}^{2}}{n_{f}^{2}n_{i}^{2}}\right)\ ,

(4)

where
R_{E}=\hbar^{2}/2ma_{B}^{2}
is the Rydberg constant,

a_{B}=(4\pi\epsilon_{o}\hbar^{2})/me^{2}
is the Bohr radius,
and
n_{i}
and
n_{f}
are the prinicipal quantum numbers
corresponding to the initial and final states, respectively.
For absorption,
n_{f}>n_{i}
and
\Delta E>0
; however,
for emission,
n_{f}<n_{i}
and
\Delta E<0
. Taking

\Delta E=\hbar\omega_{o}
, one obtains

\omega_{o}=\frac{R_{E}}{\hbar}\left(\frac{n_{f}^{2}-n_{i}^{2}}{n_{f}^{2}n_{i}^{2}}\right)\ ,

(5)

and

{\mathbf{k_{o}}}=k_{o}{\mathbf{\hat{k}}_{o}}=\frac{\omega_{o}}{c}{\mathbf{\hat{k}}_{o}}=\frac{R_{E}}{c\hbar}\left(\frac{n_{f}^{2}-n_{i}^{2}}{n_{f}^{2}n_{i}^{2}}\right){\mathbf{\hat{k}}_{o}}\ .

(6)

Then, Eq. (
3
) can be written as

\left[\frac{i\hbar}{\omega_{o}}\left({{\partial^{2}}\over{\partial t^{2}}}\right)+\left(\frac{\lambda_{C}}{4\pi}\right)(\hbar\omega_{o})({\mathbf{\hat{k}}_{o}}\cdot\nabla)-\left(\frac{\hbar^{2}}{ma_{B}}\right)\frac{1}{r}\right]\phi_{A}(t,\mathbf{r})=0\ ,

(7)

which is the governing equation for the presented theory, with

\lambda_{C}=h/mc
denoting the Compton wavelength.

3.2
Temporal and spatial solutions

To solve the governing equation, the spherical variables are separated
into the temporal and spatial (radial only) components,
\phi_{A}(t,\mathbf{r})=\chi(t)\ \eta(\mathbf{r})
, and the separation
constant
-\mu^{2}=E_{n}=-(\hbar^{2}/2m)(1/na_{B})^{2}
.
Then, Eq. (
7
) becomes

{{d^{2}\chi}\over{dt^{2}}}+i\left(\frac{\hbar\omega_{o}}{mn^{2}a_{B}^{2}}\right)\chi=0\ ,

(8)

and

{{d\eta}\over{dr}}+\frac{2}{(\mathbf{\hat{k}_{o}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{r}})k_{o}a_{B}}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}a_{B}}-\frac{1}{r}\right)\eta=0\ ,

(9)

where
n
is the principle quantum number, and
n=n_{f}

for absorption and
n=n_{i}
for emission; since the solutions
also depend on
n
, one gets
\chi(t)=\chi_{n}(t)
and
\eta(r)=\eta_{n}(r)
.

Taking
i=(1/\sqrt{2}+i/\sqrt{2})^{2}
, Eq. (
8
)
can be integrated giving solutions

\chi_{n}(t)=C_{\pm}\exp{\left[\pm\ i\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{i}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\Omega_{n}t\right]}\ ,

(10)

where
C_{\pm}
are the integration constants corresponding
to the
\pm
solutions, and the characteristic frequency

\Omega_{n}
is given by

\Omega_{n}=\frac{1}{na_{B}}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega_{o}}{m}}\ .

(11)

Both solutions with
C_{+}
and
C_{-}
are physical and they
correspond to
t\rightarrow+\infty
and
t\rightarrow-\infty
,
respectively. In the following, only the solution with
C_{+}
is
considered because quantum jumps occur when
t>0
. The
real part of the solution is

{\cal{R}\it e}[\chi_{n}(t)]=C_{+}\cos{(\Omega_{n}t)}\exp{(-\Omega_{n}t)}\ ,

(12)

and its physical meaning and applications to a hydrogen atom are
presented and discussed in Setion 3.

Before solving Eq. (
9
), it must be noted that in spherical
symmetry
\mathbf{\hat{r}}
can always be aligned with
\mathbf{\hat{k}_{o}}
, which means that
\mathbf{\hat{k}_{o}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{r}}=1
. It is also convenient to transform
Eq. (
9
) to the new independent variable
r_{a}=r/a_{B}
,
which gives

\frac{d\eta_{n}}{dr_{a}}+\beta_{B}\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}-\frac{1}{r_{a}}\right)\eta_{n}=0\ ,

(13)

where

\beta_{B}=8\pi\left(\frac{a_{B}}{\lambda_{c}}\right)\left(\frac{n_{f}^{2}n_{i}^{2}}{n_{f}^{2}-n_{i}^{2}}\right)\ .

(14)

According to Eq. (
13
),
\eta_{n}(r)
reaches its maximum value
when
r_{a}=n^{2}
or
r=n^{2}a_{B}
, which is Bohr’s rule for quantum
jumps (e.g., [3-5]). In other words, Eq. (
13
) directly displays the
Bohr rule that gives the most probable radius
r_{a}
, where the maximum
value of
\eta_{n}(r)
occurs.

The solution to Eq. (
13
) is

\eta_{n}(r)=\eta_{o,n}\ r_{a}^{\beta_{o}}\ \exp{\left[-\left(\frac{\beta_{o}}{n^{2}}\right)r_{a}\right]}\ ,

(15)

where the integration constant
\eta_{o,n}
is obtained from
the following normalization condition

4\pi a_{B}^{3}\int_{0}^{\infty}r_{a}^{2}|\eta_{n}(r_{a})|^{2}dr_{a}=1\ .

(16)

For
\eta_{n}(r)
given by Eq. (
15
), the integral can be evaluated
and the result is

\eta_{o,n}^{2}(\beta_{B})=\frac{1}{4\pi a_{B}^{3}\Gamma(2\beta_{B}+3)}\left(\frac{2\beta_{B}}{n^{2}}\right)^{2\beta_{B}+3}\ ,

(17)

which is valid if
{\cal{R}\it e}(2\beta_{o})>0
and

{\cal{R}\it e}(4\beta_{o}+2)>-1
; note that both
conditions are satisfied in the theory presented herein.

3.3
Radial probability density

Having obtained the normalized spatial wavefunction
\eta_{n}(r)
,
the radial probability density in a spherical shell volume element
can be evaluated, and it is given by

dP_{n}(r_{a})=4\pi a_{B}^{3}r_{a}^{2}|\eta_{n}(r_{a})|^{2}dr_{a}\ ,

(18)

or, after using Eqs (
15
) and (
17
), one obtains

{\cal{P}}_{n}(r_{a})\equiv\frac{dP_{n}(r_{a})}{dr_{a}}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(2\beta_{B}+3)}\left(\frac{2\beta_{B}}{n^{2}}\right)^{2\beta_{ap}+3}\ r_{a}^{2(\beta_{ap}+1)}\ e^{-(2\beta_{ap}/n^{2})r_{a}}\ ,

(19)

which gives
\int_{o}^{\infty}{\cal{P}}_{n}(r_{a})dr_{a}=1
.

3.4
Physical meaning of the solutions

Having obtained the temporal and spatial solutions to the governing
equation, the physical meaning of these solutions is now discussed.
According to Eq. (
12
), the temporal component
\chi_{n}(t)

of the wavefunction
\phi_{A}(t,\mathbf{r})
decays exponentialy
in time, and the rate of this exponential decay depends on the
value of the characteristic frequency
\Omega_{n}
given by Eq.
(
11
). Let the time
T_{n}=2\pi/\Omega_{n}
be the
duration time of a single quantum jump; the results presented
below show that the duration time is very short but finite. By
calculating this time for a considered quantum jump, and knowing
the distance travelled by the electron during the jump, the electron
speed can be determined for each jump.

The spatial solution and its normalization factor given by Eqs
(
15
) and (
17
), respectively, were used to obtain
the radial probability density for any quantum jump (see Eq.

19
). The theory predicts that after an electron makes
a quantum jump, the probablility density of finding it is very
narrow and centered at
r=n^{2}a_{B}
, which is consistent
with Bohr’s rule for quantum jumps. Experimental results
[6-8] demonstrated that the electron remains in the excited
state for periods ranging from a few tenths of a second to a
few seconds before jumping again, and returns to its original
state. The developed theory describes such physical situations,
and its solutions preserve the coherence of the wavefunction.
To achieve it, the solutions are restricted only to the EM
frequencies corresponding directly to the quantum jumps
(see Sec. 4). In other words, the solutions do not account
for measurements because they destroy the coherence of
the wavefunction.

In the following, the obtained theoretical results are applied
to a hydrogen atom and its Lyman and Balmer series absorption.
The theory also describes emission of EM radiation when the
initial,
n_{i}
, and final,
n_{f}
, are properly defined for such
transitions.

5
Experimental verification

According to the results presented in Tables 1 and 2, the duration time,

T_{n}
, for the electron’s transitions corresponding to the Lyman
and Balmer series absorptions is finite and of the order of
10^{-16}

s
and
10^{-15}

s
, respectively. The fact that the duration time
is finite, not instantenous as originally postulated (e.g., [1-5]), is
consistent with the recent experimental results [13]. However, the
predicted values of
T_{n}
for the hydrogen atom cannot be directly
compared to the times of quantum jumps measured by [13]. The
reason is that the measurements in [13] were performed on the
so-called artificial atoms, which are much larger in size than atoms,
but they have discrete energy leveles. The times
T_{n}
given in
Tables 1 and 2 are theoretical predictions based on the developed
theory and they still remain to be verfied experimentally.

The measurements performed by three different teams [7-9]
demonstrated that individual atoms, after absorbing EM radiation,
stay in their excited states for periods ranging from a few tenths
of a second to a few seconds before jumping again. Several
other indepedent experiments [10-12] confirmed this range.
The theoretical results presented in this paper give the radial
probablility densities in spherical shell volume elements for the
Lyman and Balmer series absorptions, and these probability
densities may last for time periods determined by the
experiments. It is proposed that the predicted radial
probability densities for a single quantum jump can be
verified by using a quantum microscope similar to that
designed by researchers [44], who used it to measure the
orbital structure of Stark states in an excited hydrogen atom.
There are other methods to measure quantum phenomena
[45-48], but some of them may not be suitable to observe
single orbitals in a hydrogen atom [47].

6
Conclusions

In this paper, a new asymmetric equation, which is
complementary to the Schrödinger equation, is used
to develop a theory of quantum jumps. The main
advantage of this new theory is that it explicitly displays
the Bohr rules for quantum jumps and it allows for the
theory to be non-unitary. The solutions to the new
equation are used to determine the time-scales of
quantum jumps, and to calculate the radial probability
density of finding the electron after its quantum jump.
The obtained solutions are applied to the Lyman and
Balmer series.

The theoretical results obtained in this paper are qualitatively
in agreement with the experimental results given by Minev et
al. [13], who showed that quantum jumps come at random
times, but once they come, the evolution of each completed
jump is a continuous and coherent physical process that
takes place in a finite time. The specific values of the duration
times of the considered quantum jumps in the Lyman and
Balmer series are predicted to be in the range of
10^{-16}-10^{-15}

s
. Since the measurements in [13] were
performed on the so-called artificial atoms, which are much
larger than atoms, the theoretically predicted times cannot
be directly compared to the experimental results; thus, they
they still remain to be verfied experimentally.

In accordance with the experiment results [6-9], the
theoretically predicted radial probability densities are
considered to last for periods ranging from a few tenths
of a second to a few seconds; the experiments showed
that after such times, the electron returns to its original
orbital. Thus, it is proposed that the presented radial
probability densities be observed experimentally by
using a quantum microscope similar to that designed
in [44], or other methods [45-48] suitable to observe
single orbitals in a hydrogen atom.
