Help Me Help You: The Aggregate Value of Source and Target Data in Transfer Learning
Steve Hanneke, Samory Kpotufe

Abstract
Transfer learning, in regression or classification, concerns problems where one aims to use imperfect data from a source distribution P P to improve prediction w.r.t. a target distribution Q Q . While there is now much theoretical understanding of the unsupervised setting of this problem (where no labeled target data is available), the supervised setting with labeled target data remains under-studied. We identify and characterize an interesting dichotomy in the rates achievable in the supervised setting: there is a weak transfer regime where optimal rates are determined by information in either the source or target dataset but not both, and a strong transfer regime where much faster target rates are achievable, accounting for complementary information in source and target data. Furthermore, while previous theoretical tools for the unsupervised setting can be extended to capture the weak transfer regime, we argue that they cannot capture the strong regime due to limitations inherent in the various notions of relatedness they rely on. Instead, we provide an appropriate refinement of relatedness notions—termed weak and strong moduli of transfer —that reveal the two regimes and lead to a nontrivial characterization of which pairs of distributions P P and Q Q adhere to which regime. Finally, the above dichotomy reveals a new categorization of existing algorithmic approaches in terms of their optimality or lack-thereof across supervised regimes, and we provide a generic approach that remains nearly optimal in all regimes without prior distributional knowledge.

1
Introduction

Transfer Learning
, in regression or classification, refers to the problem of improving prediction performance w.r.t. a target distribution
Q
by relying on imperfect data from a related
source
distribution
P
. The
unsupervised
setting of this problem, where no labeled data from the target
Q
is available, has received much theoretical attention for over a decade, and is now relatively well understood. However, the
supervised
setting, where the learner also has access to some amount of labeled data from
Q
, remains under-studied. As it turns out, the supervised setting displays unexpected regimes of rates in terms of nuanced relationships between
P
and
Q
, not well captured by previous works.

For initial intuition on achievable rates in supervised transfer, we may consider as a baseline, the rates achievable by an oracle that relies on either unsupervised transfer from
P
(and ignores the
Q
data) or supervised learning from
Q
(and ignores the
P
data), whichever yields the best performance under
Q
. One may hope to be able to do better than such an oracle by properly aggregating
P
and
Q
data. In this work we show that there is in fact an interesting dichotomy with two regimes of rates. First, there is a
weak transfer regime
where optimal rates are no better than that of such an oracle as above which ignores one of the datasets; interestingly, the weak regime appears to capture the limit of what is expressible by the bulk of traditional notions of relatedness that have appeared in the literature on transfer. Second, there is a
strong transfer regime
where much faster rates are achievable, accounting for complementary information in both datasets. As an example of such complementary information,
it may be that a small amount of
Q
data helps prune away predictors that are good under
P
but are quite suboptimal under
Q
(e.g., predictors based on spurious features), while a large amount of
P
data helps converge to a remaining predictor that is good under both
P
and
Q
.

The two regimes are determined by how excess risks (over a hypothesis class
\mathcal{H}
) measured under
P
relate to the same as measured under
Q
. In particular, our formal results are expressed in terms of so-called
weak and strong moduli of transfer
, two new concepts which allow for a generic treatment of the relatedness between source and target. The weak modulus describes the extent to which low excess risk on the source
P
translates to low excess risk on the target
Q
.
As we will see, it (tautologically) captures the best performance achievable in unsupervised transfer learning, while also capturing the best rates achievable in the weak transfer regime.
Furthermore, it also lower-bounds many established measures of relatedness appearing in the literature on transfer learning, and thus describes the limits of transfer performance expressible by such measures.
On the other hand, the strong modulus
localizes
the weak modulus over predictors with low target excess risk, and thus more explicitly integrates the contribution of target data with that of the source data.
The strong modulus captures both the strong and weak transfer regimes: it expresses rates in supervised transfer always no worse than those expressible by the weak modulus, and sometimes strictly better.

Now, the strong transfer regime corresponds to situations—i.e., source-target pairs
P,Q
—where the rates expressible by the strong modulus can be strictly faster than those expressible by the weak modulus.
A main contribution of this work is to completely characterize such situations, and therefore also situations where the weak and strong modulus express the same rate, i.e., where optimal rates are no better than that of the naive oracle that ignores one of the two datasets.
For instance, certain classification problems with spurious features will fall into the strong transfer regime, while interestingly, any setting with a convex loss over a convex hypothesis class (such as in linear regression) will always fall in the weak transfer regime.

The above dichotomy leads to a new categorization of existing heuristics in terms of their optimality in either regime: for instance common heuristics such as
naive pooling
(i.e. ERM on the combined datasets) can be suboptimal in either regime in the presence of noise, while a procedure akin to
fine-tuning
(which trains first on the
P
data then fine-tunes on the
Q
data) is optimal in the weak regime when
P
and
Q
share optimal predictors, but can be sub-optimal otherwise in both regimes. It is then natural to ask whether it is possible to achieve optimal oracle rates in both regimes adaptively. We show that this is indeed possible, and derive new algorithmic principles—rooted in a reduction to certain notions of
confidence sets
—that can guarantee optimal rates in both regimes,
adaptively
, i.e., with no prior distributional knowledge of the relation between
P
and
Q
.

Formal Overview of Results.

We consider a generic prediction setting with distributions
P
and
Q
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, a fixed hypothesis class

\mathcal{H}
of functions
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
, and loss function
\ell:\mathcal{Y}^{2}\to\real_{+}
. We allow general risks of the form
R_{\mu}(h)=\Expectation_{\mu}\ell(h(X),Y)
for
\mu\equiv P
or
Q
; of special interest is the
excess risk

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h)\doteq R_{\mu}(h)-\inf_{h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}}R_{\mu}(h^{\prime})
. Risk minimizers for
P
and
Q
need not exist, or be the same. The learner has access to
n_{P}>0
labeled data from
P
and
n_{Q}\geq 0
labeled data from
Q
.

\bullet
The
weak modulus of transfer
is given as the function

\delta(\epsilon)\doteq\sup\left\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h):h\in\mathcal{H},\ \mathcal{E}_{P}(h)\leq\epsilon\right\}.

Clearly, in the unsupervised case, we have the tautology that if a learner
\hat{h}_{P}
trained on source sample achieves
\mathcal{E}_{P}(\hat{h}_{P})\leq\epsilon_{P}
, for some rate
\epsilon_{P}
, then it achieves
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h}_{Q})\leq\delta(\epsilon_{P})
. It is therefore not surprising that it is implicitly tied to many existing notions of relatedness designed for unsupervised transfer: popular examples are
refinements of
total-variation
for domain adaptation in classification
[
Mansour et al., 2009
,
Ben-David et al., 2010
]
, Wasserstein distances in regression and classification
[
Redko et al., 2017
,
Shen et al., 2018
]
, or measures relating covariance structures across
P
and
Q
in linear regression
[
Mousavi Kalan et al., 2020
,
Zhang et al., 2022b
,
Ge et al., 2023
]
. Exact relations are discussed in Section
3.1
.
What is unclear however is the extent to which the weak modulus captures the best risks achievable in supervised transfer
, if at all, since it does not account for target data.

We first show via minimax results in classification and regression that the best rate expressible (for supervised transfer) in terms of the weak modulus takes the form

\displaystyle\min\left\{\epsilon_{Q},\delta(\epsilon_{P})\right\},

(2)

where
\epsilon_{\mu}
, for
\mu
denoting
Q
or
P
, is roughly the best rate achievable under
\mu
given
n_{\mu}
samples from
\mu
;
for example in classification, typically
\epsilon_{\mu}\approx n_{\mu}^{-1/2}
or faster, while in linear regression with squared loss,
\epsilon_{\mu}\approx n_{\mu}^{-1}
. In other words, the best expressible rate in terms of
\delta(\cdot)
(or in terms of notions of relatedness that it lower-bounds) is
that of a naive oracle that ignores the worst of the two datasets from
P
and
Q
.

We can now ask whether the rates in (
2
) are improvable, which leads to the strong modulus.

\bullet
The
strong modulus of transfer

follows as a natural refinement of the weak modulus, and aims to better capture situations where the aggregate of source and target data might yield strictly better rates than (
2
), i.e., the best of source or target data alone. For intuition, suppose, e.g., that the source distribution
P
admits two distinct risk minimizers
{h^{\!*}_{P,1}},{h^{\!*}_{P,2}}
, the first with
\mathcal{E}_{Q}({h^{\!*}_{P,1}})\approx 0
, while the second has large
\mathcal{E}_{Q}({h^{\!*}_{P,2}})\geq\frac{1}{4}
.
For example,
{h^{\!*}_{P,2}}
might focus on spurious features (e.g. background color in object classification) that are predictive for data from
P
, but are harmful for prediction under
Q
. Then, with enough
Q
data, this might be evident, so that we may avoid such suboptimal solutions,
while the value of the weak-modulus is limited by
h^{\!*}_{P,2}
. With this intuition in mind, a simplified version of the strong modulus roughly takes the form

\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\doteq\sup\left\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h):h\in\mathcal{H},\ \mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)\leq\epsilon_{1}\text{ and }\mathcal{E}_{P}(h)\leq\epsilon_{2}\right\},

for a range of values of
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}
(the precise definition, stated in Section
2.2
, is expressed more-rigorously as localization under
Q
of the weak modulus).
We can express tighter rates for supervised transfer, in terms of the strong modulus, namely, oracle rates are of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h})\lesssim\delta(\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P})\leq\min\left\{\epsilon_{Q},\delta(\epsilon_{P})\right\}

(3)

As shown in Theorem
6
, these rates are tight even in situations where
\delta(\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P})\ll\min\left\{\epsilon_{Q},\delta(\epsilon_{P})\right\}
, i.e., situations where there are gaps between moduli.

This makes precise the notions of weak and strong transfer regimes discussed above:
namely, the weak transfer regime denotes pairs
P
,
Q
for which
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\min\{\epsilon_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\}

\forall\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}
, whereas the strong transfer regime denotes pairs
P
,
Q
for which there exist
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}
for which
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})<\min\{\epsilon_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\}
.

\bullet
We provide a
full characterization of such gaps
between weak and strong moduli (Theorem
4
), i.e., a description of transfer problems where the strong modulus results in strictly better rates. Namely, we show that such gaps exist iff certain
monotonicity
relations between source and target risks hold. This characterization yields new insights into the implicit assumptions underlying many existing relatedness measures, revealing that they only provide tight rates for
(P,Q)
pairs that satisfy monotonicity conditions.

We observe several interesting implications of these results.
On one hand, common convex settings—with convex loss and convex hypothesis class—always yield
monotone
transfer problems, with no gap between weak and strong moduli, and therefore are well captured by the weak modulus. On the other hand, some common feature selection settings in regression or classification, as often motivated via causal assumptions
[
Simon, 1954
,
Sagawa et al., 2020
]
, are non-monotonic, and thus display strict gaps.

\bullet
Finally, new
algorithmic principles
emerge from the analysis as so far alluded to.
Since in general the relatedness measure
\delta(\cdot,\cdot)
is a priori unknown and is impractical to estimate,
we are particularly interested in the existence of
adaptive procedures
that can achieve the oracle rates
\delta(\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P})
with no prior distributional knowledge. We show that such adaptive procedures exist, via a reduction to so-called
strong confidence sets
, namely, estimated subsets of the hypothesis class that retain good predictors, up to the variability in the data. Accounting for such variability in both source and target data turns out crucial as we can also show that many existing algorithmic approaches fail to adaptively achieve the same low risks. For instance, perhaps surprisingly, (a) selecting between estimated source and target predictors by cross-validation with target data, or (b) ERM on the combined source and target data, both already fail to be adaptive to the weak modulus rates of (
2
), let alone the improved rates in (
3
). Procedures akin to
fine-tuning
can achieve (
2
) sometimes, e.g., if source and target share an optimal predictor—, but as is well known, may run into
negative transfer
otherwise (which is not possible if (
2
) is achieved). Some recently proposed robust validation technique (à la Lepski) of
[
Hanneke and Kpotufe, 2019
]
can always achieve (
2
), but fails to achieve (
3
).
We discuss all of these procedures in more detail in Section
3.5
.

While the adaptive procedures derived in the analysis are for the most part of a theoretical nature, we hope that the resulting design principles, i.e., our reduction to confidence sets, may yield insights into practical procedures that can automatically leverage the combined information in the source and target data while always avoiding
negative transfer
(situations where the source should not be relied upon
[
Zhang et al., 2022a
]
).
For instance, it is easily seen that in linear regression with squared loss, our generic approaches result in efficiently solvable convex programs (see Remark
17
).
This is potentially the case in more general settings with tractable losses, but requires further investigation.

Other Related Works.

As alluded to so far, the strong modulus appears related in spirit to recent works on robustness against spurious features and invariant risk minimization
[
Baktashmotlagh et al., 2013
,
Gong et al., 2016
,
Arjovsky et al., 2019
,
Wu et al., 2023
,
Heinze-Deml and Meinshausen, 2021
,
Kostin et al., 2024
, see e.g.,]
, as it similarly aims to distinguish between good hypotheses under the source in terms of how much their risks vary under the target distribution. However, a key distinction is that, by considering a continuum of possible excess risks, the strong modulus allows us to integrate in the statistical uncertainty inherent in finite-sample regimes.

Our notions of
moduli of transfer
focus on relations between source and target
risks
, and thus, do not capture the full spectrum of notions of relatedness that have been considered in the context of transfer learning and related tasks. For instance, some notions not captured are those tied to a specific problem setting or algorithms considered—this is often the case in nonparametric settings; others are notions that describe more structural relationships between regression functions. Some relevant works are discussed next.

Starting with nonparametric regression or classification settings, various works
[
Kpotufe and Martinet, 2021
,
Cai and Wei, 2021
,
Scott, 2019
,
Reeve et al., 2021
,
Pathak et al., 2022
,
Galbraith and Kpotufe, 2023
, see, e.g., ]
, consider structural conditions on the change in regression function and or covariate distributions, that appear only indirectly related to changes between source and target risks.

A different line of work considers more general structural assumptions relating source and target
[
Blitzer et al., 2011
,
Balcan et al., 2019
,
Du et al., 2020
,
Tripuraneni et al., 2020
,
Ben-David and Schuller, 2003
,
Ando and Zhang, 2005
,
Baxter, 2000
,
Baxter, 1997
,
Thrun and Pratt, 1998
,
Yang et al., 2013
,
Aliakbarpour et al., 2024
,
Alon et al., 2024
,
Meunier et al., 2023
, see, e.g.]
, e.g., regression functions might share the same underlying index subspace (as related to
representation learning
), leading to decreases in problem complexity which are not captured by this work.

On the algorithmic side, various theoretical works are related in spirit to the present work in that they also address the problem of
negative transfer
, which as discussed above, is not our main goal but is automatically addressed here as a consequence of our adaptivity results.
For instance,
[
Klivans et al., 2024
]
introduces a testing paradigm where one is to reject the source if it leads to large target error; however they do not aim to adapt to every situation where the source is useful, as their tests rely on unlabeled target data and therefore do not leverage label information when available. As another example, for the problem of mean estimation,
[
Blanchard et al., 2024
]
tests for source distributions whose first and second moments are sufficiently close to that of the target to help improve performance. As such they aim to be adaptive to a given discrepancy measure encoded by difference in moments. It remains unclear when such discrepancies are appropriate, beyond the mean estimation problem.

Basic Definitions

Assumption 1

We assume throughout that
R_{\mu}(h)<\infty,\forall h\in\mathcal{H}

\mu

Note that the above is a mild assumption, as it does not require the loss to be uniformly bounded.

For example, the case of binary classification corresponds to
\mathcal{Y}=\{\pm 1\}
, where we often choose

\ell(y,y^{\prime})={\mathbbold 1}\!\left\{y\neq y^{\prime}\right\}
,
and results below will depend on the VC dimension of the class

(see, e.g.,
[

Koltchinskii, 2006
]
).
As another example, the case of regression corresponds to
\mathcal{Y}\subset\real
,
and we may choose
\ell(y,y^{\prime})=(y-y^{\prime})^{2}
,
and results below may depend, for instance, on the covering numbers or pseudo-dimension of the class
\mathcal{H}
(see, e.g.
[
Anthony and Bartlett, 1999
]
).

Remark 1
.

Our general results will in fact capture quite abstract dependences on
\mathcal{H}
,
via an abstractly-defined notion of confidence sets,
and any notion of complexity that allows for such confidence sets are therefore admissible.

Definition 1
.

The

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h;\mathcal{H}_{0})\doteq R_{\mu}(h)-\inf_{h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}}R_{\mu}(h^{\prime}).

In particular, we will just refer to

, and often write

We will need the following useful definition
[

]
. Let
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\real\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{+}=(0,\infty]

Definition 2

(

For any distribution

Clearly,

The Weak Modulus of Transfer.

The first notion considered below serves to capture the reduction in target

Definition 3

(Weak Modulus)
.

Let

Define the modulus

\delta(\epsilon)\doteq\sup\left\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h):h\in\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon)\right\},

or written as
\delta_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}(\epsilon)

In the results below involving the weak modulus,

).
As such, the weak modulus essentially describes,

As we will argue in Section

While the weak modulus sharply captures the rates of

It aims to capture the reduction in

In particular, the intuition is that,

-risk functions, it is sometimes possible that

(i.e., smaller weak modulus).
This idea is not reflected in any of the previous notions of discrepancy in the literature.
Formally, we have the following definition.

2.3
Properties and Relation between Moduli

Our main aim in this section is to establish certain relationships between weak and strong moduli, namely as expressed in Corollary
2
below, that are relevant in comparing achievable rates expressed in terms of moduli. The following quantity turns out useful in establishing this result; it will in fact also appear later in Section
3.3
when discussing adaptive rates in terms of the strong modulus. It can loosely be viewed as the best
Q
-excess risk of
P
-risk minimizers (see Remark
2
).

Definition 5

(Lower Pivot)
.

The following captures the infimum excess
Q
-risk of good classifiers under
P
:

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}\doteq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}~\inf\!\left\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h):h\in\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon)\right\}.

We also consider an
upper pivot
counterpart to Definition
5
.
Although stated here for contrast, it will turn out useful later in Section
3.1
when considering relations to measures of discrepancy, and will also be crucial in Section
4
where we characterize the gap between weak and strong strong moduli.

Definition 6

(Upper Pivot)
.

We denote by
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}\doteq\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}~\sup\!\left\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h):h\in\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon)\right\}=\lim\limits_{\epsilon\to 0}\delta(\epsilon)
.

This quantity may be viewed loosely as the worst
Q
excess-risk of
P
-risk minimizers if they exist.
Note that, by definition,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}\geq\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
.

Remark 2

(Intuition on pivots)
.

Consider the simple case of a finite
\mathcal{H}
, and denote the set of
P
-risk minimzers as
\mathcal{H}_{P}^{*}\doteq\argmin_{h\in\mathcal{H}}R_{P}(h)

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}=\min_{h^{\!*}_{P}\in\mathcal{H}_{P}^{*}}\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h^{\!*}_{P})\text{ while }\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}\doteq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\delta(\epsilon)=\max_{h^{\!*}_{P}\in\mathcal{H}_{P}^{*}}\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h^{\!*}_{P}).

The pivots capture the above intuition which is given in terms of risk minimizers, but their definitions allow for general problem settings that might not admit risk minimizers.

Next we consider an alternative expression of
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
, which is easier to use at times. For intuition, again consider the simplest case of a finite
\mathcal{H}

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}

for which
\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})
contains an

(whereas

every

). The proposition below generalizes this intuition.

Proposition 1

(Equivalent Form of the Pivot)
.

We have

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}=\inf\left\{\epsilon_{1}\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\real\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{+}\!\!:\forall\epsilon>0,\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})\cap\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon)\neq\emptyset\right\}.

Proof.

For ease of exposition, let

{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
denote the set
\left\{\epsilon_{1}\in\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\real\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu_{+}:\forall\epsilon>0,\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})\cap\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon)\neq\emptyset\right\}.

For any
\epsilon_{1}>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
, we have that

\epsilon_{1}>\inf\!\left\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h):h\in\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon)\right\}
for all
\epsilon>0
, since this last infimum only increases as
\epsilon\to 0
; in other words, for all
\epsilon>0
,
\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})\cap\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon)\neq\emptyset
, i.e.,
\epsilon_{1}\in{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
. We therefore have that
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}\geq\inf{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
. Notice that, this last inequality also holds when
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}=\infty
.

Now for any
\epsilon_{1}\in{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
, by definition, we have that for all
\epsilon>0
,

\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon)}\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)\leq\epsilon_{1}
, in other words,
\epsilon_{1}\geq\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
. We therefore also have that
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}\leq\inf{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
.
∎

Remark 3
.

We note that we may have
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
very large, in fact matching
\sup_{a,b\in\mathcal{Y}}\ell(a,b)
(admitting
\infty
for unbounded losses). This describes
P

having little information on

Q
, i.e., where all
h^{\prime}
s with low
P
-risk have large
Q
-risk.

The above proposition leads to a useful simpler expression of the strong modulus for arguments
\epsilon_{1}
above
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}>0
.

•

If
\epsilon_{1}>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
,
every
h\in\mathcal{H}
has

\mathcal{E}_{P}(h;\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}))=\mathcal{E}_{P}(h)
. It follows that, for
\epsilon_{1}>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}

\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\sup\left\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h):h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})\cap\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{2})\right\}.

•

If
\epsilon_{1}\leq\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
, then
\epsilon_{1}\leq\delta(\epsilon_{2})
. Equivalently, we always have
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}\leq\delta(\epsilon_{2})
.

Proof.

For the first claim,
for
\epsilon_{1}>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
,
we have
\epsilon_{1}\in{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}

(where
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
is defined in the proof of Proposition
1
). Now by definition,
{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
is the set of
\epsilon_{1}
such that for all
\epsilon
,
\exists h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})
satisfying
\mathcal{E}_{P}(h)\leq\epsilon
.
Therefore,
\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})}R_{P}(h)=\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}R_{P}(h)
, and the claim follows.

For the second claim,
we note that
\delta(\epsilon_{2})\in{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{E}}}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
,
since any
\epsilon>0
has

\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon)\cap\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{2})=\mathcal{H}_{P}(\min\{\epsilon,\epsilon_{2}\})\neq\emptyset
,
and is a subset of
\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{2})\subset\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\delta(\epsilon_{2}))
,
where the last inclusion is from
the definition of
\delta(\epsilon_{2})
.
Hence
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}\leq\delta(\epsilon_{2})
.
∎

The following corollary states that the strong modulus represents a refinement of the weak modulus.

Corollary 2

(Relation Between Strong and Weak Moduli)
.

For
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}>0
,

\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\leq\min\left\{\epsilon_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\right\}.

Proof.

If
\epsilon_{1}>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
,
then Corollary
1
implies

\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\sup\left\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h):h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})\cap\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{2})\right\}
,
which is bounded by
\min\left\{\epsilon_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\right\}

by definition of
\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})
and
\delta(\epsilon_{2})
.

If
\epsilon_{1}\leq\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
,
then since Corollary
1
implies

\epsilon_{1}\leq\delta(\epsilon_{2})
in this case,
we have
\min\left\{\epsilon_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\right\}=\epsilon_{1}\geq\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
, where the final inequality is immediate from the definition of
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
.
∎

In Section
4
,
we characterize situations with
gaps
, i.e., where where we may have
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\ll\min\left\{\epsilon_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\right\}
.

Monotonicity of Moduli.

We conclude by stating basic monotonicity
properties of the weak and strong moduli.
The following proposition holds trivially since
\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon)\subset\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon^{\prime})
, for
\epsilon\leq\epsilon^{\prime}
, but is given for emphasis.

Proposition 2
.

The weak modulus
\delta(\epsilon)
is non-decreasing in
\epsilon
, i.e., for all
\epsilon\leq\epsilon^{\prime}
it holds that
\delta(\epsilon)\leq\delta(\epsilon^{\prime})
.

It turns out the strong modulus is also non-decreasing in both arguments. While monotonicity was immediate by definition for the weak modulus
\delta(\cdot)
, it is a bit more involved for the strong modulus
\delta(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Proposition 3
.

The strong modulus
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
is non-decreasing, i.e.,

\forall\epsilon_{1}\leq\epsilon^{\prime}_{1}
,
\forall\epsilon_{2}\leq\epsilon^{\prime}_{2}

we have
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\leq\delta(\epsilon^{\prime}_{1},\epsilon^{\prime}_{2})
.

Proof.

The fact that
\delta(\epsilon^{\prime}_{1},\epsilon_{2})\leq\delta(\epsilon^{\prime}_{1},\epsilon^{\prime}_{2})
is immediate from the definition.
It remains to show
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\leq\delta(\epsilon^{\prime}_{1},\epsilon_{2})
.
We first note the main difficulty in this argument arises
when
\epsilon_{1}\leq\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
.
In particular, the conditions on
\mathcal{E}_{P}(h;\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}))
and
\mathcal{E}_{P}(h;\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}))
may involve different
P
-infimum errors, hence the resulting sets become hard to compare.

So, first, assume

\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})}R_{P}(h)=\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}^{\prime})}R_{P}(h)
(this is the case, e.g., when
\epsilon_{1}>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
, see Corollary
1
). It then follows that the set
\{h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}):\mathcal{E}_{P}(h;\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}))\leq\epsilon_{2}\}
is contained in
\{h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}):\mathcal{E}_{P}(h;\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}))\leq\epsilon_{2}\}
, hence
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\leq\delta(\epsilon^{\prime}_{1},\epsilon_{2})
.

Now, suppose the contrary. Then

\exists h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}^{\prime})
such that

\inf_{h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}^{\prime})}R_{P}(h^{\prime})\leq R_{P}(h)<\min\left\{\inf_{h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})}R_{P}(h^{\prime}),\inf_{h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}^{\prime})}R_{P}(h^{\prime})+\epsilon_{2}\right\}.

By the second inequality, we have that
h\notin\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})
, hence

\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\leq\epsilon_{1}\leq\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)\leq\delta(\epsilon_{1}^{\prime},\epsilon_{2}).

∎

3
Rates in terms of Moduli of Transfer

Our aim in this section is to show that it is possible to (adaptively) achieve rates sometimes faster than the best rates achievable in terms of popular measures of discrepancy.

As such, we start in Section
3.1
below by showing that the weak modulus indeed provides a unified language for popular notions of discrepancy in domain adaptation.
Next, in order to understand the gains inherent in adapting to the strong modulus rather than to the weak modulus, we outline in Section
3.2
the statistical limitations of the weak modulus—and therefore of the many discrepancies it captures.
This is then followed in Section
3.3
by adaptive rates in terms of the strong modulus via an algorithmic reduction to the existence of certain confidence sets. For completeness, examples of such confidence sets are derived in Section
3.4
.
Finally we end in Section
3.5
with a discussion of how previous algorithmic techniques fail to adapt to the strong modulus.

3.1
Weak Modulus Captures Many Existing Discrepancies

Here we consider a few examples of existing notions of discrepancy between source and target
P,Q
, and illustrate the types of bounds they imply on
\delta(\epsilon)
. These bounds were already implicit in past work, even while the weak modulus
\delta(\epsilon)
was never explicitly defined as the main object of study, or as the implied notion of discrepancy. For intuition, whenever we can establish for a measure
\text{dist}(P,Q)
that
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)\leq F(\mathcal{E}_{P}(h),\text{dist}(P,Q))
, for some non-decreasing
F
, it certainly follows that

\delta(\epsilon)\leq F(\epsilon,\text{dist}(P,Q))
. Details are given in Appendix
A.1
along with discussions of situations where the inequalities are strict.

Some Discrepancies in Classification.

We first remark that some of the notions below can be stated generally beyond classification, e.g., the
\cal Y
-discrepancy
and the
transfer-exponent
, however they usually appear in works on classification. In what follows assume

\ell(a,b)={\mathbbold 1}\!\left\{a\neq b\right\}
and suppose
\mathcal{Y}=\{-1,1\}
.

\bullet

\cal Y
-discrepancy

[
Mohri and Medina, 2012
]
:
\mathrm{disc}_{\cal Y}(P,Q)\doteq\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\left|R_{P}(h)-R_{Q}(h)\right|
. For any
\epsilon\in(0,1]
,

\displaystyle\delta(\epsilon)\leq\epsilon+\mathrm{disc}_{\cal Y}(P,Q)+\left(\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}R_{P}(h)-\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}}R_{Q}(h)\right).

(5)

\bullet

Localized
\cal Y
-discrepancy
:
Hanneke and Kpotufe [2019]
instead defines
\mathrm{disc}_{\cal Y}^{\prime}(P,Q)=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}}|\mathcal{E}_{P}(h)-\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)|,

which can be further
localized
as

\mathrm{disc}_{\cal Y}^{*}(P,Q;\epsilon_{0})=\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{0})}|\mathcal{E}_{P}(h)-\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)|,

for any fixed
\epsilon_{0}>0
(see Example 4 of
Hanneke and Kpotufe [2019]
, which was later rediscovered in
[
Zhang et al., 2021
, Proposition 5]
). Clearly,

\delta(\epsilon)\leq\epsilon+\mathrm{disc}_{\cal Y}^{*}(P,Q;\epsilon_{0})\text{ for any }\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{0}.

\bullet

\cal A
-discrepancy

[
Ben-David et al., 2010
]
:
\mathrm{disc}_{\cal A}(P,Q)\doteq\sup_{h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}}\left|P_{X}(h\neq h^{\prime})-Q_{X}(h\neq h^{\prime})\right|
.
For simplicity, as in
[
Mansour et al., 2009
]
, suppose there exist
h^{\!*}_{Q},h^{\!*}_{P}\in\mathcal{H}
that minimize the respective risks, and also let
h^{\!*}=\argmin_{h\in\mathcal{H}}R_{Q}(h)+R_{P}(h)
.
For
\epsilon\in(0,1]
,

\displaystyle\delta(\epsilon)\leq\epsilon+\mathrm{disc}_{\cal A}(P,Q)+(R_{P}(h^{\!*}_{P})-R_{Q}(h^{\!*}_{Q}))+R_{Q}(h^{\!*})+R_{P}(h^{\!*}).

(6)

\bullet

Transfer exponent

[
Hanneke and Kpotufe, 2019
]
: a value
\rho\geq 0
is called a transfer exponent if
\exists C_{\rho}>0
satisfying:

\forall h\in\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)\leq C_{\rho}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{P}^{1/\rho}(h)+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
.
For any
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, it’s immediate that

\delta(\epsilon)\leq C_{\rho}\cdot\epsilon^{1/\rho}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}.

We also discuss TV,
KL divergence, and bounded density ratios
[
Sugiyama et al., 2008
]
in Appendix
A.1
.

Some Discrepancies in Regression.

The discussion here may be extended to classification by viewing
\ell(a,b)
as a
surrogate loss
(and the classifier as
\text{sign}(h)
for example). In what follows, let
\ell(a,b)=(a-b)^{2}
denote the squared loss, and
\cal Y\subset\real
. The discussion inherently assumes that
\mathbb{E}\ Y^{2}<\infty
under
P
or
Q
.

\bullet

Wasserstein Distance

[
Redko et al., 2017
,
Shen et al., 2018
]
: Let
\cal L
denote the set of
1
-Lipschitz functions on
\mathcal{X}
w.r.t some metric. Then consider the
integral probability metric

W_{1}(P,Q)\doteq\sup_{f\in\cal L}\left|\Expectation_{P_{X}}(f)-\Expectation_{Q_{X}}(f)\right|
.
Suppose
\mathcal{H}
is a set of bounded functions
\mathcal{X}\to[-M,M]

with
\mathcal{H}\subset\lambda\cdot{\cal L},\lambda>0
.
We consider a weak covariate shift
scenario, where
\Expectation_{P}[Y|X]=\Expectation_{Q}[Y|X]=h^{\!*}(X)
for some
h^{\!*}\in\mathcal{H}
.
Then
\forall\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\delta(\epsilon)\leq\epsilon+8M\lambda\cdot W_{1}(P,Q).

(7)

\bullet

Covariance Ratios

[
Zhang et al., 2022b
,
Ge et al., 2023
]
: consider linear regression, i.e.,
X\in\real^{d},Y\in\real
, and where

\mathcal{H}\doteq\left\{h_{w}(x)\doteq w^{\top}x:w\in\real^{d}\right\}
, and let
\Sigma_{\mu}\doteq\mathbb{E}_{\mu}XX^{\top}
, for
\mu\equiv

P
or
Q
. Assume
\Sigma_{P}
is full rank, and let
h^{\!*}_{P}=\argmin_{h}R_{P}(h)
. Then, letting

\lambda_{\text{max}}
denote the maximum eigenvalue,
we have for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\text{if }\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h^{\!*}_{P})=0

\displaystyle\text{ (relaxed covariate-shift condition) }\ \delta(\epsilon)\leq\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\Sigma_{P}^{-1}\Sigma_{Q}\right)\cdot\epsilon.

(8)

\displaystyle\text{otherwise, }\ \ \delta(\epsilon)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\Sigma_{P}^{-1}\Sigma_{Q}\right)\cdot\epsilon+\sqrt{\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\Sigma_{P}^{-1}\Sigma_{Q}\right)\cdot\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h^{\!*}_{P})}\cdot\sqrt{\epsilon}+\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h^{\!*}_{P})

(9)

\displaystyle\leq 2\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\Sigma_{P}^{-1}\Sigma_{Q}\right)\cdot\epsilon+2\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h^{\!*}_{P}).

(10)

Similarly, certain variants of the
MMD distance
[
Huang et al., 2007
]
, another so-called
integral probability metric
,
may be viewed as special cases of
{\cal Y}
-discrepancy or extensions of the
{\cal A}
-discrepancy to the regression setting;
see e.g.,
Redko et al. [2017]
. The implied bounds on
\delta(\epsilon)
are analogous to those given for
\mathrm{disc}_{\mathcal{Y}}
and
\mathrm{disc}_{\mathcal{A}}
above.

Upper-bounds similar to the above have been shown in various recent results of
[
Mousavi Kalan et al., 2020
,
Zhang et al., 2022b
,
Ge et al., 2023
]
.
We note that a number of recent results, e.g.
[
Zhang et al., 2022b
,
Pathak et al., 2023
,
Chen et al., 2024
]
, consider more general linear cases with different risk minimizers under
P
and
Q
, along with refinements on
\lambda_{\text{max}}\left(\Sigma_{P}^{-1}\Sigma_{Q}\right)
in their upper-bounds.

3.2
Rates Expressible in Terms of the Weak Modulus

In this section, we show via classification and regression examples that the best possible rates expressible in terms of the weak modulus take the form
\min\{\epsilon_{Q},\delta(\epsilon_{P})\}
where
\epsilon_{\mu}
,
\mu=P
or
Q
, denotes the best achievable rates (up to log factors) using data from
\mu
alone. This is done via minimax lower-bounds on classes of problems parametrized by generic functions
f
that upper-bound
\delta(\cdot)
.

Working Examples of Prediction Tasks.

To illustrate our results we will often consider the two classical examples of
classification with bounded VC dimension
, and linear regression (viewed generally over any finite-dimensional linear space, i.e., including for instance polynomial regression).

The exact settings are formalized below.

(VC-CLS)

We let
\mathcal{Y}=\{-1,1\}
,
\ell(y,y^{\prime})={\mathbbold 1}\!\left\{y\neq y^{\prime}\right\}
, and we assume
\mathcal{H}
has finite VC dimension
d_{\mathcal{H}}

[
Vapnik and Chervonenkis, 1971
]
.

(LIN-REG)

We let
X\in\real^{d},Y\in\real
, we let

\mathcal{H}\doteq\left\{h_{w}(x)\doteq w^{\top}x:w\in\real^{d}\right\}
,
and we consider the squared loss
\ell(y,y^{\prime})=(y-y^{\prime})^{2}
.
We assume
\Expectation_{\mu}Y^{2}<\infty
and
\Sigma_{\mu}\doteq\mathbb{E}_{\mu}XX^{\top}
is well defined for
\mu=P
or
Q
.

The proofs of the lower-bound results below can be found in Appendix
A.3
.

Classification
(VC-CLS)
.

We adopt the following classical noise conditions (see e.g.
[
Mammen and Tsybakov, 1999
,
Audibert and Tsybakov, 2007
,
Massart and Nédélec, 2006
,
Koltchinskii, 2006
,
Bartlett et al., 2006
]
).

Definition 7

(BCC)
.

In what follows, we use the short-hand
\mu(h\neq h^{\prime})\doteq\mu_{X}\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{X}:h(x)\neq h^{\prime}(x)\right\}\right)
, and we define the
diameter
of any
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{H}
, as

\text{diam}_{\mu}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})\doteq\sup_{h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}\mu(h\neq h^{\prime}).

We say that
\mathcal{H}
satisfies a
Bernstein Class Condition
(BCC), as measured under
\mu
, with parameters
(C_{\mu},\beta_{\mu}
),
C_{\mu}>0
and
\beta_{\mu}\in[0,1]
,
if
\forall 0<\epsilon<1/2
,

\text{diam}_{\mu}(\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\epsilon))\leq C_{\mu}\cdot\epsilon^{\beta_{\mu}}.

(11)

Note that the condition trivially holds for
\beta_{\mu}=0
,
C_{\mu}=1
. Also, the constraint
\epsilon<1/2
is superfluous in the case
C_{\mu}\geq 2
, and merely serves to simplify our analysis in some places.
The condition captures the hardness of the learning problem: when
\beta_{\mu}=1
, which formalizes
low noise
regimes, we expect fast rates of the form
n^{-1}
, in terms of sample size
n
, while for
\beta_{\mu}=0
, rates are of the more common form
n^{-1/2}
.
In general, tightest rates are of the form
n^{-\frac{1}{2-\beta_{\mu}}}

(up to logs)
and are achievable by ERM learners
[
Massart and Nédélec, 2006
,
Koltchinskii, 2006
]
.

We note however that, while
\beta_{\mu}=0
imposes no restriction on the class, any
\beta_{\mu}>0
by definition can be restrictive: suppose for instance that
\inf_{h}R_{\mu}(h)
is achieved, then by (
11
), all risk minimizers can only differ on a set of measure
0
. Nonetheless, BCC yields useful insights into the continuum of achievable rates in classification across noise regimes.

Definition 8

(Weak Modulus Class)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
denote a class of functions
\mathcal{X}\mapsto\left\{-1,1\right\}
.
Consider any non-decreasing function
f
on
(0,1]\mapsto[0,1]
. For any such
f
, and
0\leq\beta_{P},\beta_{Q}\leq 1
, let

\Sigma_{\mathcal{H}}(f,\beta_{P},\beta_{Q})
denote all pairs of distributions
P,Q
such that, w.r.t.
\mathcal{H}
we have
(i)
\delta_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}(\epsilon)\leq f(\epsilon),\ \forall\ 0<\epsilon\leq 1/4
, and (ii)
P
and
Q
satisfy BCC (Definition
7
) with parameters
(1,\beta_{P})
and
(1,\beta_{Q})
respectively.

Theorem 1

(Classification Lower-Bounds)
.

In what follows, let
\hat{h}
denote any learner (possibly improper) having access to independent datasets
S_{P}
and
S_{Q}
of size
n_{P},n_{Q}
respectively. The following holds for any
\mathcal{H}
with
|\mathcal{H}|\geq 3
.

(i)
Suppose the VC dimension of
\mathcal{H}
satisfies
d_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\max\{n_{Q},n_{P}\}
, and that for some
\kappa\geq 1
,
f
is nondecreasing and satisfies
\alpha f(\epsilon)\leq\kappa f(\alpha\epsilon)
for all
0<\epsilon,\alpha\leq 1
. We have that, for universal constants
c,c_{0}
:

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{h}}\sup_{(P,Q)\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{H}}(f,\beta_{P},\beta_{Q})}\Expectation\ \mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h})\geq c\cdot{\frac{1}{\kappa}}\cdot\min\left\{\left(\frac{d_{\mathcal{H}}}{n_{Q}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2-\beta_{Q}}},f\!\left(c_{0}\left(\frac{d_{\mathcal{H}}}{n_{P}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2-\beta_{P}}}\right)\right\}.

(12)

(ii)
For general nondecreasing
f
we have that, for universal constants
c,c_{0}
:

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{h}}\sup_{(P,Q)\in\Sigma_{\mathcal{H}}(f,\beta_{P},\beta_{Q})}\Expectation\ \mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h})\geq c\cdot\min\left\{\left(\frac{1}{n_{Q}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2-\beta_{Q}}},f\!\left(c_{0}\left(\frac{1}{n_{P}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2-\beta_{P}}}\right)\right\}.

(13)

(iii)
For general nondecreasing
f
, for any
2\leq d\leq\max\{n_{Q},n_{P}\}
there exists
\mathcal{H}
with
d_{\mathcal{H}}=\Theta(d)
such that (
12
) holds.

Notice that the condition on
f
admits any nondecreasing concave function
f\geq 0
, with corresponding
\kappa=1
(i.e.,
f(\alpha\epsilon)\geq\alpha f(\epsilon)+(1-\alpha)f(0)
), and allows some convex
f
for
\kappa\geq 1
.
For instance, this admits functions of the form
C_{1}\epsilon^{1/\rho}+C_{2}
for
\rho\geq 1
.
As it turns out, the condition is needed in our construction in order to satisfy

\delta(\epsilon)\leq f(\epsilon)

for all
0<\epsilon\leq 1/4
.
The proof of Theorem
1
is included in Appendix
A.3
.

Remark 4

(
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
as a Learning Limit)
.

One may also wonder how
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}\doteq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\delta(\epsilon)
may lower-bound achievable rates in regimes with small
n_{Q}
. This is somewhat opaque since the distributions witnessing the above lower-bounds allow for
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}=0

3.3
Adaptive Rates in Terms of the Strong Modulus

We show in this section that it is possible to achieve rates
adaptive
to the strong modulus (and hence to the weak modulus), i.e., with no a priori knowledge of such relation between
P
and
Q
. In particular, adaptive rates would take the form
\delta(\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P})\leq\min\left\{\epsilon_{Q},\delta(\epsilon_{P})\right\}
while the modulus
\delta(\cdot,\cdot)
is unknown to the learner.

Such adaptivity can be reduced to access to
strong
confidence sets
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}
, where
\mu
stands for
P
and
Q
: these are sets estimated from data which, with high probability, (a) only contain good predictors, i.e.,
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}
is contained in some
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\epsilon_{\mu})
, for small
\epsilon_{\mu}
, and (b) also contain
all
predictors with very low excess risk, i.e., it contains
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\epsilon_{\mu}/C)
for some
C>1
. Adaptive transfer rates will then be inherited from such confidence sets, i.e., as determined by
\epsilon_{\mu}
and
C
, for
\mu\equiv P
or
Q
.
Examples of such sets for various problems of interest are given later in Appendix
B.1
.
In particular, for most cases of interest,
the achievable value of
\epsilon_{\mu}
is of essentially
the same order as the minimax rate for excess risk.

A confidence set
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}
is illustrated here with solid gray boundary; the
ball
of radius
\epsilon_{\mu}
centered at
h^{\!*}_{\mu}
represents
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\epsilon_{\mu})
, while the inner ball represents
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\epsilon_{\mu}/C)

Definition 10
.

Let
\mu
denote either
P
or
Q
.
Let
0<\tau\leq 1
and

. We call a random set
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}\doteq\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}(S_{\mu})
an
(\epsilon,\tau,C)
-strong confidence set
(under
\mu
), for an
\epsilon>0
,
if the following holds with probability at least
1-\tau
:

\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\epsilon/C)\ \subset\ \hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}\ \subset\ \mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\epsilon).

Examples of strong confidence sets are deferred for now till Section
3.4
.
We also discuss relaxations of the notion of strong confidence set in Appendix
A.2
,
but which are only able to achieve the weaker guarantee in Corollary
3
in terms of the weak modulus.

For the rest of this section, we require the following definition of ERM which admits situations where the infimum empirical risk might not be attained (as we admit general
\ell
and
\mathcal{H}
).

Definition 11
.

Let
\mu
denote either
P
or
Q
, and let
\hat{R}_{\mu}(h)\doteq\frac{1}{n_{\mu}}\sum_{(x,y)\in S_{\mu}}\ell(h(x),y)
denote empirical risk over
S_{\mu}
.
For any
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{H}
,
we let
\hat{h}_{\mu}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
denote the function returned by an
empirical risk minimization
function

\mathrm{ERM}_{\mu}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
:
namely, a function mapping the data set
S_{\mu}
to an
h\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}

satisfying
\hat{R}_{\mu}(h)<\inf_{h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}\hat{R}_{\mu}(h^{\prime})+e^{-n_{\mu}}
.
In particular, when
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}=\mathcal{H}
,
we simply write
\mathrm{ERM}_{\mu}=\mathrm{ERM}_{\mu}(\mathcal{H})
, and
\hat{h}_{\mu}\doteq\hat{h}_{\mu}(\mathcal{H})
.

Remark 5
.

In common settings such as classification with
0
-
1
loss, or linear regression with squared loss, we can let
\mathrm{ERM}_{\mu}(\mathcal{H})
denote any
h\in\mathcal{H}
achieving
\inf_{h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}}\hat{R}_{\mu}(h^{\prime})
.

The following procedure, expressed in terms of strong confidence sets, will be shown to adaptively achieve a rate in terms of the strong modulus.

Algorithm 1
.

Input:
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\sharp}\supset\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\flat}
, and
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}
, all subsets of
\mathcal{H}
.

If
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\sharp}\cap\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}\neq\emptyset
, return any
h\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\sharp}\cap\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}
,

otherwise return
\hat{h}_{P,Q}\doteq\mathrm{ERM}_{P}(\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\sharp})
.

We require the following local definition of achievable rate (under
P
) over a given subset
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{H}
.
This is because, as we consider general scenarios, it is possible that
\hat{h}_{P}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
may admit a different generalization rate than
\hat{h}_{P}(\mathcal{H})
: for instance in classification, the Bernstein Class Condition on
\mathcal{H}
(Definition
7
) may allow for fast rates
\ll n_{P}^{-1/2}
for
\hat{h}_{P}(\mathcal{H})
, but if the same BCC parameters do not hold for
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
we may have
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{P}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})\approx n_{P}^{-1/2}
.

Definition 12
.

Fix
0<\tau<1
.
Given a subset
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{H}
,
we let
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{P}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
,
depending on
n_{P}
, denote the following:

\min\left\{{\epsilon>0}:\mathbb{P}_{S_{P}}\left(R_{P}(\hat{h}_{P}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime}))-\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}R_{P}(h)\leq\epsilon\right)\geq 1-\tau\right\}.

Note that the infimum is achieved in the set by continuity of probability measures.

Theorem 3
.

Let
0<\tau\leq 1
,
C^{\sharp},C^{\flat},C>1
. Suppose
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\sharp}
,
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\flat}
are respectively
(\epsilon_{Q},\tau,C^{\sharp})
and
(\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp},\tau,C^{\flat})
-strong confidence sets under
Q
, and
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}
is an
(\epsilon_{P},\tau,C)
-strong confidence set under
P
, for some
\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P}>0
depending respectively on
n_{Q}
and
n_{P}
. Let
\hat{h}
denote the hypothesis returned by Algorithm
1
.
Letting
\tilde{\varepsilon}_{P}=\tilde{\varepsilon}_{P}(\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\flat})
,
we then have with probability at least
1-4\tau
:

\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h})\leq\begin{cases}\delta(\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P})&\text{ if }\epsilon_{Q}>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}\text{ or }\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\sharp}\cap\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}\neq\emptyset,\\
C^{\flat}\cdot\delta(\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp},\tilde{\varepsilon}_{P})&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that any
C>1
, i.e., arbitrarily large, may be chosen for
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}
above as it does not affect the rate.

Proof.

Assume the events of Definition
10
(for
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\sharp}
,
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\flat}
,
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}
)
hold (this occurs with probability at least
1-3\tau
by the union bound).
By Definition
10
, every
h\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\sharp}\cap\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}
is in

\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q})\cap\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{P})
. Now notice that for any such
h
in this last intersection, we have
\mathcal{E}_{P}(h;\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q}))\leq\mathcal{E}_{P}(h)\leq\epsilon_{P}
.
Thus, by definition, we have that any
h\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\sharp}\cap\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}
satisfies
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)\leq\delta(\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P})
.

Now suppose
\epsilon_{Q}>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
, and
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\sharp}\cap\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}=\emptyset
. Recall the equivalent definition of
\delta(\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P})
of Corollary
1
for the case
\epsilon_{Q}>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
.
By Proposition
1
, for any
\epsilon>0
,
\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q})\cap\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon)\neq\emptyset
; therefore,
pick any

h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q})\cap\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{P}/C)
.
Since
\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{P}/C)\subset\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}\subset\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{P})
,
it follows that
h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q})\cap\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{P})
, hence,
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)\leq\delta(\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P})
. Furthermore, since
\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp})\cap\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{P}/C)\subset\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\sharp}\cap\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}=\emptyset
, we know
h\notin\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp})
, i.e.,
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)>\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp}
.
In other words, for the choice
\hat{h}_{P,Q}\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\flat}\subset\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp})
, we must have that

\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h}_{P,Q})\leq\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp}<\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)\leq\delta(\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P}).

For the final case, suppose
\epsilon_{Q}\leq\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}
, while
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\sharp}\cap\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}=\emptyset
. We have the following two subcases.

– First, suppose that
\displaystyle\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp})}R_{P}(h)=\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q}/(C^{\flat}C^{\sharp}))}R_{P}(h)
. Then it follows that

\mathcal{E}_{P}(\hat{h}_{P,Q};\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp}))=\mathcal{E}_{P}(\hat{h}_{P,Q};\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}^{\flat})\leq\tilde{\varepsilon}_{P}\text{ (with probability at least $1-\tau$) }.

Since we also have that
\hat{h}_{P,Q}\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp})
, it follows by definition that it satisfies
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h}_{P,Q})\leq\delta(\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp},\tilde{\varepsilon}_{P})
.

– Otherwise, let
0<\epsilon\leq\tilde{\varepsilon}_{P}
, and pick
\tilde{h}\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp})
such that

\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp})}R_{P}(h)\leq R_{P}(\tilde{h})<\min\left\{\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q}/(C^{\flat}C^{\sharp}))}R_{P}(h),\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp})}R_{P}(h)+\epsilon\right\}.

By the second inequality, namely, the first term in the
\min
, such an
\tilde{h}\notin\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q}/(C^{\flat}C^{\sharp}))
. Using the second term in the
\min
, we have that
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\tilde{h})\leq\delta(\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp},\epsilon)
.
We therefore have that

\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h}_{P,Q})\leq\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp}<C^{\flat}\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\tilde{h})\leq C^{\flat}\delta(\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp},\epsilon)\leq C^{\flat}\delta(\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp},\tilde{\varepsilon}_{P}).

We remark that each of these final two subcases yields a bound slightly better than the one stated in the theorem, which is given as such for simplicity.
∎

Corollary 3

(Improving on Weak Modulus Rate)
.

Under the conditions of Theorem
3
,
if
C^{\flat}\leq C^{\sharp}
, we have
with probability at least
1-4\tau
, that Algorithm
1
achieves a rate never larger than

\min\!\left(\epsilon_{Q},\delta(\epsilon_{P})\right)
.

Proof.

Whenever
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h})\leq\delta(\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P})

. Otherwise we have

\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h})\leq C^{\flat}\cdot\delta(\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp},\tilde{\varepsilon}_{P})\leq C^{\flat}\cdot\epsilon_{Q}/C^{\sharp}\leq\epsilon_{Q}.

Thus, for
\epsilon_{Q}\leq\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}\leq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\delta(\epsilon)
, the above implies
that the bound on
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h})
is at most
\min\left(\epsilon_{Q},\delta(\epsilon_{P})\right)
.
∎

3.5
Sub-Optimiality of Common Algorithmic Techniques

In this section, we discuss various common techniques for transfer learning; we will argue that each fails to achieve the excess risk guarantee of Theorem
3
, although they can be successful in some scenarios. The examples focus on classification but can be extended to regression with bounded loss.

In all that follows, let
\hat{h}_{P}=\mathrm{ERM}_{P}(\mathcal{H})
,
\hat{h}_{Q}=\mathrm{ERM}_{Q}(\mathcal{H})

denote empirical risk minimizers under
P
and
Q
.

Example 1

(
Validation).

Let
\tilde{R}_{Q}(h)
denote empirical risk on an independent target sample of size
n_{Q}
.

\text{Return}\quad\hat{h}\doteq\argmin_{h\in\left\{\hat{h}_{P},\hat{h}_{Q}\right\}}\tilde{R}_{Q}(h).

Favorable Settings:
it is hard to construct a situation where
\hat{h}
is guaranteed to take advantage of the
P
sample when useful. This requires that
\tilde{R}_{Q}(\hat{h}_{P})<\tilde{R}_{Q}(\hat{h}_{Q})
, which is hard to guarantee, but can happen sometimes.

Sub-Optimality:
Validation can only achieve an excess risk depending on
n_{Q}
, as it is subject to the variance inherent in the validation sample. That is, it might fail to take advantage of the
P
sample.
Consider the following simple classification example. Let
{\cal X}=\left\{x_{0}\right\}
, and
\mathcal{H}=\left\{h_{\sigma}\right\}_{\sigma\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}}
where
h_{\sigma}(x_{0})=\sigma
. Now suppose
\mathbb{P}\left(Y=1|x_{0}\right)=1/2+\tau
, so that
h^{\!*}_{P}=h^{\!*}_{Q}=h_{+}
. Now let
n_{P}=\infty
so that
\hat{h}_{P}=h_{+}
; by usual anti-concentration arguments, if
n_{Q}\leq c_{1}/\tau^{2}
, then with constant probability
c_{2}>0
, the majority of
Y
labels in both the training and validation samples is
-1
, i.e.,
we’d have
\hat{h}_{Q}=h_{-}
, and also that
\hat{h}=h_{-}
. In other words, the best excess risk
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h})
achievable is of order
\tau=\Omega(n_{Q}^{-1/2})
.

In contrast, Theorem
3
guarantees a rate of
0
in the same scenario (with high probability).

Example 2

(
Pooling).

\text{Return}\quad\hat{h}\doteq\argmin_{h\in\mathcal{H}}\quad n_{P}\cdot\hat{R}_{P}(h)+n_{Q}\cdot\hat{R}_{Q}(h).

Favorable Settings:
If there is a common risk minimizer under
Q
and
P
(i.e.,
h^{\!*}_{P}=h^{\!*}_{Q}
),

Hanneke and Kpotufe [2022]
establish positive results for
pooling in some regimes (e.g.,
\beta_{P}=\beta_{Q}=1
in Definition
7
),
though still with some sub-optimality in other regimes.

Sub-Optimality:
The approach is not robust to
negative transfer
, i.e., to situations where the source data can be hurtful and should be ignored.

Consider again a simple scenario

{\cal X}=\left\{x_{0}\right\}
, and
\mathcal{H}=\left\{h_{\sigma}\right\}_{\sigma\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}}
where
h_{\sigma}(x_{0})=\sigma
.
Suppose
\mathbb{P}_{Q}(Y=1|x_{0})=1
while
\mathbb{P}_{P}(Y=1|x_{0})=0
.
If
n_{P}>n_{Q}
, the pooling
\hat{h}
equals
h_{-}

hence has
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h})=1
.
In contrast, simply training ERM on the
Q
data
would return
h_{+}
with
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h_{+})=0
.
Thus, this is an example of
negative transfer
,
where including the data from
P
is harmful to this
method’s risk.

In contrast, Theorem
3
would guarantee a rate of
0
in the same scenario (with high probability).

We also note that such negative transfer for pooling can even occur with
h^{\!*}_{P}=h^{\!*}_{Q}
when
n_{P}\gg n_{Q}
, provided
\hat{R}_{P}
has higher variance than
\hat{R}_{Q}

[
Hanneke and Kpotufe, 2022
, see]
. We emphasize that Theorem
3
always guarantees a rate no worse than that of using the
Q
data alone, i.e., no negative transfer.

Example 3

(
Fine-Tuning).
1
1

1

is a term employed with neural networks, where the source
P

Let
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}
be an

-strong confidence set under

\text{Return}\quad\hat{h}\doteq\argmin_{h\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}}\hat{R}_{Q}(h).

Favorable Settings:
This procedure can be shown to adapt to the weak modulus in special cases, particularly when there is a minimizer

of
both

In such cases, it can be shown that

are the achievable rates described in Section

.
The proof of this can be adapted from that of Proposition 3 of

Sub-Optimality:
Negative transfer can happen in this case. If all

eliminates

.

Consider again the same scenario described for pooling above.
In this case, if

, then with probability

,

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}=\{h_{-}\}
, so that the
\hat{h}

.

Example 4

(
Lepski/Robust Validation).

Let
\tilde{R}_{Q}(h)
be the empirical validation risk defined in Example
1
.

\text{Return}\quad\hat{h}\doteq\begin{cases}\hat{h}_{P}&\text{if }\tilde{R}_{Q}(\hat{h}_{P})\leq\tilde{R}_{Q}(\hat{h}_{Q})+\epsilon_{Q}\\
\hat{h}_{Q}&\text{otherwise }\end{cases},

for appropriate
\epsilon_{Q}=\epsilon_{Q}(n_{Q})
, potentially data-dependent (see e.g.
[
Hanneke and Kpotufe, 2019
]
).

Favorable Settings:
This procedure can be shown to adapt to the weak modulus, that is

\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h})\lesssim\min\left(\epsilon_{Q},\delta(\epsilon_{P})\right)
, where
\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P}
are the achievable rates described in Section

3.4
.
The proof of this can be adapted from that of Proposition 2 of
Hanneke and Kpotufe [2019]
. A related method is explained in detail in Appendix
A.2
.

Sub-Optimality:

It cannot adapt to the strong modulus:
consider the following classification example.
Let
\mathcal{H}=\left\{h_{1},h_{2},h_{3}\right\}
,
where
\argmin_{h\in\mathcal{H}}R_{P}(h)=\left\{h_{1},h_{2}\right\}
, and
\argmin_{h\in\mathcal{H}}R_{Q}(h)=\left\{h_{1}\right\}
. Again suppose that
n_{P}=\infty
and that
\hat{h}_{P}=h_{2}
(which can be constructed to happen with probability
1/2
). Now we can construct the
Q
distribution such that if
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h_{3})=\Theta(n_{Q}^{-1/2})
, then we have with constant probability that
\hat{h}_{Q}=h_{3}
(see, e.g. the above Example
1
case). Therefore, let
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h_{2})\doteq\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h}_{P})\gg\epsilon_{Q}
(where, e.g.,
\epsilon_{Q}=O(n_{Q}^{-1/2})
) then
\hat{h}=\hat{h}_{Q}
with high probability, and we can therefore only guarantee
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h})=\Omega(n_{Q}^{-1/2})
.

In contrast, for the same setting, Theorem
3
guarantees a
Q
-excess risk of
\delta(\epsilon_{Q},0)=0
with high probability.

A detailed construction for Example
4
is given in Appendix
B.2
.

4
The Gap Between Strong and Weak Moduli

We now consider the question of when bounds in terms of the strong modulus are strictly tighter than those in terms of the weak modulus, i.e., whether we have
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})<\min\left\{\epsilon_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\right\}
for at least some values of
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}>0
.
In other words, when does the strong modulus capture a strictly more optimistic relation between
P
and
Q
?

We will show in Theorem
4
that this is fully characterized by a notion of
monotonity
between achievable excess risks
\mathcal{E}_{P}
and
\mathcal{E}_{Q}
: roughly,
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\min\left\{\epsilon_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\right\}
for all
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}
, iff
\mathcal{E}_{Q}
decreases together with
\mathcal{E}_{P}
, for
given values
of
\mathcal{E}_{Q}
, in a sense to soon be made formal. Interestingly, as a consequence, no gap exists in common situations when both the class
\mathcal{H}
and the loss
\ell
are convex (Theorem
5
), e.g., in ordinary least-squares linear regression. On the other hand, natural examples of gaps
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})<\min\left\{\epsilon_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\right\}
arise in classification and regression, e.g., in feature selection (Example
5
of Section
4.3
).
Of particular importance, whenever such gaps exist, the weak modulus
\delta(\cdot)
and all the notions of discrepancies it lower-bounds (examples of Section
3.1
), do not fully capture the transferable information in the aggregate data from
P
and
Q
.

4.1
Preliminaries

We start with the following definitions. The first definition below simply restricts attention to values of
\epsilon_{1}
achievable under the target
Q
; the reason for this restriction will soon become clear.

Definition 13
.

Define
\mathcal{\ddot{E}}_{Q}(\mathcal{H})\doteq\mathrm{closure}\!\left(\left\{\epsilon\geq 0:\exists h\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime},\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)=\epsilon\right\}\right)
as the closure of
achievable

Q
-excess-risks.
Furthermore, we consider the mapping

\epsilon\mapsto\ddot{\epsilon}\doteq\max\left\{\epsilon^{\prime}\in\mathcal{\ddot{E}}_{Q}(\mathcal{H}):\epsilon^{\prime}\leq\epsilon\right\}
.

Remark that we have
\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon)=\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\ddot{\epsilon})
. The following proposition then follows by definition and is given for emphasis:

Proposition 4
.

We always have,
\forall\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}>0
,

\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\leq\min\left\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\right\}\leq\min\left\{\epsilon_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\right\}
.

In fact the reader may have noticed that the result of Corollary
3
based on the weak-modulus could have been given in terms of achievable
Q
-excess errors. In particular, Algorithm
1
(among others such as the
robust validation
of Example
4
) in fact achieves the rate

\min\left\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{Q},\delta(\epsilon_{P})\right\}
. It is thus the effective rate achievable under the weak modulus, whenever
\mathcal{\ddot{E}}_{Q}(\mathcal{H})
has discontinuities (e.g., if
\mathcal{H}
is finite).
Hence, we will be interested in characterizing situations with a gap
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})<\min\left\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\right\}
, as opposed to a gap to
\min\left\{\epsilon_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\right\}
which is less meaningful. The existence of such gaps is shown to be characterized via notions of
monotonicity
between
P
and
Q
excess risks. Also, as it turns out,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}\doteq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\delta(\epsilon)
plays a
crucial role.

We start by considering
h
’s with
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)
above
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
.

Definition 14
.

We say that a
transfer problem
, as defined by
(P,Q,\mathcal{H},\ell)
, is
monotonic above
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
, if
the following holds:
\forall\epsilon_{1}\in\mathcal{\ddot{E}}_{Q}(\mathcal{H})
with
\epsilon_{1}>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
, and
\forall\epsilon_{2}>0
with
\delta(\epsilon_{2})>\epsilon_{1}
, we have
\forall\tau>0
:

\displaystyle\exists h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}\text{ with }\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)\in[\epsilon_{1}-\tau,\epsilon_{1}]\text{ and }\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h^{\prime})\in[\delta(\epsilon_{2})-\tau,\delta(\epsilon_{2})],\text{ satisfying }\mathcal{E}_{P}(h)\leq\mathcal{E}_{P}(h^{\prime})\leq\epsilon_{2}.

(17)

Figure 4:
Suppose
\mathcal{H}
consists of one-sided thresholds on the line,
Y=h^{\!*}_{\mu}(x)
for
\mu\equiv P
or
Q
. It’s easy to see that
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)
(mass of disagreement regions) goes up as
h
approaches
h^{\!*}_{P}
from the left, and goes down otherwise. The problem therefore has no gap between moduli.

The above definition while somewhat opaque, turns out to be equivalent to
pseudo-denseness
(appropriately defined) of
monotone
subsets
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{H}
, i.e., subsets where
\mathcal{E}_{Q}
goes down with
\mathcal{E}_{P}
(see Appendix
C
).

Next we consider the structure of
h
’s with
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)
at most
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
.

Definition 15
.

We say that the transfer problem
(P,Q,\mathcal{H},\ell)
is
anti-monotonic below
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
if either
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}=0
or the following holds true for all
0<\epsilon_{1}{\leq}\ \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
:

\lim_{\epsilon_{2}\to 0}\sup\left\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h):h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}),\mathcal{E}_{P}(h;\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}))\leq\epsilon_{2}\right\}=\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}.

In other words, the best
h
’s in terms of
P
-risk on the set
\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})
are the worst in terms of
Q
risk.

Remark 9
.

In some sense, anti-monotonicity means that the information
P
has on
Q
saturates for large enough
Q
data: if the
Q
data alone can decrease risk below some
\epsilon_{Q}\leq\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
, then even an infinite amount of
P
data no longer helps; in fact, the
P
data may even be harmful as it directs us toward worse predictors at the boundary of
\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q})
, while we would rather pick a predictor on its interior.

4.2
Characterization of Gaps

Theorem 4

(Gap Characterization)
.

For any transfer problem
(P,Q,\mathcal{H},\ell)
,
we have that
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\min\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\}

(for all
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}>0
)
if and only if
(P,Q,\mathcal{H},\ell)
is monotonic above
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
and anti-monotonic below
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
.

The analysis is given in Appendix
C
. Anti-monotonicity above
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
follows most easily by definition (Proposition
11
). The proof of monotonicity is most involved: we show in Proposition
12
that if Definition
14
holds, then there is no gap above
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
; on the other hand,
we will show in Proposition
13
that, if
a transfer problem has no gap above
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
it must hold that monotone sets are pseudo-dense in
\mathcal{H}
. This last statement is constructive, and relies on a mapping from
\epsilon_{1}\in\mathcal{\ddot{E}}_{Q}(\mathcal{H})
to
\epsilon_{2}=\delta^{\scalebox{0.8}{-}\scalebox{0.6}{1}}(\epsilon_{1})
—the pseudo-inverse of the weak modulus, which is then shown to maintain monotonicity.

4.3
Examples

This section presents examples witnessing
both non-trivial gaps between the strong and weak modulus, and also a general case in which there can be no such gap.

Figure 5:
Suppose
P
is supported in the gray region so that
h^{\!*}_{P,1},h^{\!*}_{P,2}
are optimal under
P
for classifying
square vs circle
. Notice that
h^{\!*}_{P}
however relies on spurious features; a small amount of data from
Q
, some falling in the upper-left quadrant, allows rejecting
h^{\!*}_{P,2}
.

We start
with a simple example of gaps, which the reader may find easy to generalize.

Example 5

(Gap In Feature Selection)
.

First we consider a basic regression problem for
X\in\real^{2}
where
Q,P
are given as follows. Let
e_{1},e_{2}
denote coordinate vectors.

\bullet

Q_{X}\sim{\cal N}(0,I_{2})
while
Y\doteq e_{1}^{\top}X+\xi
, for
\xi\sim{\cal N}(0,1)
.

\bullet

P_{X}
is supported on the subspace

\left\{x:x^{\top}e_{1}=x^{\top}e_{2}\right\}
, i.e., equal coordinates, with marginals
{\cal N}(0,1)
, while
Y\doteq e_{1}^{\top}X+\xi=e_{2}^{\top}X+\xi
, for
\xi\sim{\cal N}(0,1)
.

We let
\mathcal{H}_{i}\doteq\left\{h_{c}(x)=c\cdot e_{i}^{\top}x:c\in\real\right\}
, and
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\cup\mathcal{H}_{2}
, i.e.,
sparse vectors
.

Notice now that, for any
h_{c}\in\mathcal{H}_{1}

,
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h_{c})=\mathcal{E}_{P}(h_{c})=(1-c)^{2}
.

On the other hand, for any
h_{c}\in\mathcal{H}_{2}

,
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h_{c})=1+c^{2}
while
\mathcal{E}_{P}(h_{c})=(1-c)^{2}
.

In other words, we always that (1)
\forall\epsilon_{2}>0,\ \delta(\epsilon_{2})\geq 1=\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
, and (2) for
\epsilon_{1}<1
,
\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})
only retains hypotheses in
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. It follows that, for any
\epsilon_{2}<\epsilon_{2}<1
, we have

\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\epsilon_{2}
as witnessed by some
h_{c}\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})\cap\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{2})
with
(1-c)^{2}=\epsilon_{2}
. In particular, we have a gap, i.e.,
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})<\epsilon_{1}<\delta(\epsilon_{2})
.

Notice that the above example is easily generalized to higher dimensions as the main ingredient was to uncover multiple good predictors under
P
—by having
\text{supp}(P_{X})
close to an appropriate subspace or manifold relating features of
X
— some of which are bad under
Q
. Similar examples can be constructed in classification as illustrated in Figure
5
.

Next, we consider a surprisingly general set of situations where no gap is possible.

Theorem 5

(Convex classes and convex losses)
.

Suppose the loss function
\ell(\cdot,\cdot)
satisfies that, for any
y
,
\ell(\cdot,y)
is a continuous convex function of its first argument (for instance, the squared loss).
Suppose
\mathcal{H}
is a convex set of functions
\mathcal{X}\to\real
, that is,
\forall h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
,
\forall\alpha\in[0,1]
, the function
\alpha h+(1-\alpha)h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
(for instance, linear functions).
Then
\forall\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}>0
,

\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\min\{\epsilon_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\}
.

Proof.

If
\epsilon_{1}\geq\delta(\epsilon_{2})
,
then we have

\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})\supset\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{2})
,
and it follows that

\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\delta(\epsilon_{2})=\min\{\epsilon_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\}
.

Otherwise, suppose
\epsilon_{1}<\delta(\epsilon_{2})
.
Then there exists
h\in\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{2})\setminus\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})
.
Note that we also have

\mathcal{E}_{P}(h;\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}))\leq\epsilon_{2}
.
Let
h^{\prime}
be any function in
\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})
with
\mathcal{E}_{P}(h^{\prime};\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}))\leq\epsilon_{2}
.

Since both

\mathcal{E}_{P}(h;\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}))\leq\epsilon_{2}

and

\mathcal{E}_{P}(h^{\prime};\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}))\leq\epsilon_{2}
,
and
\ell
is convex in its first argument,
Jensen’s inequality implies any
\alpha\in[0,1]
has

\mathcal{E}_{P}(\alpha h+(1-\alpha)h^{\prime};\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}))\leq\alpha\mathcal{E}_{P}(h;\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}))+(1-\alpha)\mathcal{E}_{P}(h^{\prime};\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}))\leq\epsilon_{2}
.

Moreover, since
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)>\epsilon_{1}\geq\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h^{\prime})
,
by continuity of
\ell
in its first argument (and the dominated convergence theorem to extend continuity of
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\alpha h+(1-\alpha)h^{\prime})
in
\alpha
, using
\ell(h(x),y)+\ell(h^{\prime}(x),y)
as the dominating function, recalling that all risks are finite by Assumption
1
)
we have that there exists
\alpha\in[0,1]
such that

\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\alpha h+(1-\alpha)h^{\prime})=\epsilon_{1}
.
By convexity of
\mathcal{H}
, we know that
\alpha h+(1-\alpha)h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
.
Therefore, the supremum

\sup\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h^{\prime\prime}):\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h^{\prime\prime})\leq\epsilon_{1},\mathcal{E}_{P}(h^{\prime\prime};\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}))\leq\epsilon_{2},h^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{H}\}=\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})

is
achieved
by the hypothesis
\alpha h+(1-\alpha)h
,
with a value
\epsilon_{1}
.
Hence,
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\min\{\epsilon_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\}

in this case as well.
∎

5
Lower Bounds for the Strong Modulus

In this section we ask whether the upper-bounds of Theorem
3
are tight
even in the case where there might be gaps between strong and weak moduli
, i.e., for classes of distributions
(P,Q)
admitting
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})<\min\left\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\right\}
for some values of
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}
. Certainly, since for some transfer problems we always have
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\min\left\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\right\}
, our lower-bounds of Section
3.2
would apply when there is no gap.

As it turns out, the lower-bounds constructions of this section have to adhere to further structure inherent to
admissible
strong moduli
\delta(\cdot,\cdot)
, i.e., not all functions can be the strong moduli of a pair
(P,Q)
.
As in Theorem
1
,
to approach a minimax lower bound analysis, we must first
define a family of
(P,Q)
pairs in terms of their strong moduli:
namely,
\Sigma_{g}=\{(P,Q):\delta_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}(\cdot,\cdot)\leq g(\cdot,\cdot)\}
for some function
g
.
In light of the fact that
\delta_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}(\cdot,\cdot)
has an
inherent structure, it only makes sense to restrict to
functions
g(\cdot,\cdot)
which also share these same
structural properties,
which we describe in detail in the following results.

Lemma 1

(Gaps are Intervals)
.

Fix any
\epsilon_{2}>0
. If for some
\epsilon_{1}>0
, we have
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})<\epsilon_{1}
, then it must hold that,

\forall\epsilon\text{ satisfying }\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\leq\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{1}
we also have

\delta(\epsilon,\epsilon_{2})=\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
.

Proof.

Define

\mathcal{H}(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\doteq\left\{h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}):\mathcal{E}_{P}(h;\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}))\leq\epsilon_{2}\right\},

and recall that
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\sup\left\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h):h\in\mathcal{H}(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\right\}
. In other words, if
\epsilon\geq\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
we have that
\mathcal{H}(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\subset\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon)
, and since
\epsilon\leq\epsilon_{1}
, we also have that
\inf\left\{R_{P}(h):h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon)\right\}=\inf\left\{R_{P}(h):h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})\right\}
so we conclude that

\mathcal{H}(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\mathcal{H}(\epsilon,\epsilon_{2})
.
∎

Remark 10

(Implications for
\mathcal{\ddot{E}}_{Q}(\mathcal{H})
)
.

In particular, note that Lemma
1
further implies that if for some

\epsilon_{1}>0
we have

\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})<\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}
, then
\forall\epsilon
satisfying
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\leq\epsilon\leq\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}
,
we also have
\delta(\epsilon,\epsilon_{2})=\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
,
since
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\delta(\ddot{\epsilon}_{1},\epsilon_{2})
.

Lemma
1

implies
that the
strong modulus is in fact very structured
as summarized in the corollary below and illustrated in Figure
6
.

Corollary 4

(Graph of the Strong Modulus)
.

For any transfer problem, the strong modulus
\delta(\cdot,\cdot)
satifies the following conditions. Fix any
\epsilon_{2}>\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}>0
:

•

\forall\epsilon_{1}>0
,
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
matches the function
\min\left\{\epsilon_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\right\}
except on a collection
{\cal I}(\epsilon_{2})
of

(0,\delta(\epsilon_{2})]
.
Furthermore, on any such

\delta(\cdot,\epsilon_{2})
is constant, equal to
\epsilon_{I}=\inf I
,

\delta(\epsilon_{I},\epsilon_{2})=\epsilon_{I}
.

•

Each

{\cal I}(\epsilon_{2})
is contained in an interval from
{\cal I}(\epsilon^{\prime}_{2})
.

Proof.

For
\epsilon_{1}\leq\delta(\epsilon_{2})

if
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})<\epsilon_{1}
,

implies

\delta(\cdot,\epsilon_{2})
is constant
between

\delta(\epsilon^{\prime}_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\epsilon^{\prime}_{1}

\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{1}

\delta(\epsilon^{\prime\prime}_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\epsilon^{\prime}_{1}

Since the above holds for every

\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})<\epsilon_{1}
,
every such
\epsilon_{1}
is in some such interval

.
The second claim follows immediately from this interval structure, together with the fact that
\delta(\cdot,\cdot)

).

.

General admissible
\boldsymbol{g}

•

\delta_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}(\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P})<\min\!\left\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{Q},\delta_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}(\epsilon_{P})\right\}
,

•

\Expectation\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h})\geq\frac{C}{\kappa}g(\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P})
.

The proof is given in Appendix
D
,
and follows similar arguments as that of Theorem
1
.
The main distinction is that of
ensuring the lower bound is witnessed by a pair
(P,Q)
exhibiting a gap
\delta_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}(\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P})<\min\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{Q},\delta_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}(\epsilon_{P})\}
,
which is the reason for requiring
g
to be admissible, as defined in Definition
16
.

6
Final Remarks

We conclude below with some open questions which may be of general interest.

Mixed Losses.
While we have focused attention in this work to a general but
single
loss
\ell
for both source and target tasks, there are many situations in transfer learning with mixed losses: for instance in classification, we are often ultimately interested in 0-1 loss under the target, however, for computational reasons, we instead optimize a surrogate objective for the source and or the target tasks. A more refined theory will be needed to properly capture such settings.

Benign Moduli.
Our various lower-bounds are established for general classes encoding general moduli. However, in order to match all dependencies on
\mathcal{H}
’s complexity present in upper-bounds, our analysis required we impose some restriction on such moduli. This leaves the possibility that transfer-learning is in fact easier outside such restrictions, i.e., faster rates might be attained.

Nonparametric Settings.
While our upper-bounds yield fairly general insights, provided access to particular confidence sets, we only provided examples of such confidence sets for parametric classification and regression. It is quite possible that different approaches might be needed in nonparametric settings, i.e. if rate optimal confidence sets are hard to construct. Furthermore, since our lower-bounds are all dimension dependent, it is unclear whether the proposed moduli in fact yield a tight characterization of transfer rates in nonparametric settings.

MultiTask and MultiSource.
Settings with
N\gg 1
sources
P_{i},i\in[N]
remain largely open. There are certainly many works addressing these settings by extending divergence measures designed for the
N=1
settings (e.g., any of the divergences discussed above), but it remains unclear which measure of the aggregate information
P_{i}
’s have on a target
Q
can be adapted to (e.g., each
P_{i}
may be far from
Q
, while an
unknown
mixture of them might be close to
Q
).

A different assumption, considered by many recent analyses (as discussed in the introduction) is that all
P_{i}
’s and
Q
share some common low-dimensional structure that can lower the complexity of the target problem (even if no good source predictor transfers well to the target problem). This is not captured by our notions of moduli.

Example 6

(
\cal Y

Let

Remark 11

Consider

\displaystyle\delta(\epsilon)\leq\epsilon+\mathrm{disc}_{\cal A}(P,Q)+(R_{P}(h^{\!*}_{P})-R_{Q}(h^{\!*}_{Q}))+R_{Q}(h^{\!*})+R_{P}(h^{\!*}).

Example 8

(Transfer Exponent;
Hanneke and Kpotufe, 2019
,
Hanneke, Kpotufe and Mahdaviyeh, 2023
)
.

Supposing there exist

\mathcal{E}_{P}(h^{\!*}_{P})=0

R_{Q}(h)-R_{Q}(h^{\!*}_{P})\leq C_{\rho}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{P}^{1/\rho}(h)

(equivalently
\forall h\in\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{0})

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)=R_{Q}(h)-R_{Q}(h^{\!*}_{P})+\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h^{\!*}_{P})\leq C_{\rho}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{P}^{1/\rho}(h)+\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h^{\!*}_{P}),

(25)

implying that, for all
\epsilon\in(0,1]

\delta(\epsilon)\leq C_{\rho}\cdot\epsilon^{1/\rho}+\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h^{\!*}_{P}).

A more general definition:
we can extend the above definition to the general case with or without
P
-risk minimizers.
Define
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}\doteq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\delta(\epsilon)
.
A transfer exponent
(C_{\rho},\rho)
is defined as satisfying the condition:

\forall h\in\mathcal{H}
,
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)\leq C_{\rho}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{P}^{1/\rho}(h)+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
.
This immediately implies the following bound:

\delta(\epsilon)\leq C_{\rho}\cdot\epsilon^{1/\rho}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}.

Remark 12

(Density Ratios)
.

Assuming

Remark 13

(Tightness)
.

While the weak modulus was introduced mainly as a way to capture existing notions of discrepancy and understand their limitations w.r.t. the strong modulus, it is nonetheless interesting on its own. Importantly, the fact that it can be adapted to (in the supervised transfer setting) despite depending on unknown risk minimums, brings up the question of the tightness of known rates expressed in terms of existing discrepancies in comparison to the rates of Corollary
3
expressed in terms of the weak modulus.

Moreover, investigating such tightness w.r.t. to the weak modulus, yields some qualitative point of comparison between existing notions of discrepancy, which are otherwise not directly comparable to each other.

Starting with the transfer exponent,
we first note that this notion attempts to capture some desired properties of
\delta(\epsilon)
. For one, it is
asymmetric
just as
\delta
is, and thus does not deteriorate in situations where transfer is asymmetric—for instance

covers the decision boundary under

but not vice versa. Second, the resulting upper-bound yields the same limit

as
\delta(\epsilon)
, which captures large source sample regimes.
However, given the arbitrariness of the polynomial form of the transfer-exponent,
\delta(\epsilon)
can naturally be smaller. Similarly, the
\cal Y
-discrepancy and
\cal A
-discrepancy can be loose: we may have, e.g., that

while these discrepancies are not

are loose for small
\epsilon
.

The localized
{\cal Y}

avoids this problem (taking

), asymptoting to

similarly to the transfer exponent.
In fact, notice that

\mathrm{disc}_{\cal Y}^{*}(P,Q;\epsilon_{0})\leq\sup_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{0})}\max\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h),\mathcal{E}_{P}(h)\}\leq\max\{\delta(\epsilon_{0}),\epsilon_{0}\}
.
Thus, dropping
\epsilon_{0}
for

\delta(\epsilon)
in situations where

\delta(\epsilon)\geq\epsilon
for all
\epsilon

Cases where

(e.g., cases with

)
are referred to as

by
Hanneke and Kpotufe [2019]
, who also argue that
\mathrm{disc}_{\cal Y}^{*}(P,Q;\epsilon)
does not capture such cases.

A.2
Alternative Procedures for the Weak Modulus

We saw in Section
3.5
that the rates of Corollary
3
may be attained by alternative procedures such as the
robust validation
(Lepski) approach of Example
4
. We present a similar approach here, that adapts to the weak modulus, and sometimes to the strong modulus, while relying on a relaxed notion of
confidence set
.

Definition 17
.

Let
\mu
denote either
P
or
Q
.
Let
0<\tau\leq 1
and
\epsilon_{\mu}>0
. We call a random set
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}\doteq\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}(S_{\mu})
an
(\epsilon_{\mu},\tau)
-weak confidence set
(under
\mu
) if the following conditions are met with probability at least
1-\tau
:

\ {\rm(i)}\ \exists\hat{\epsilon}_{\mu}>\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(\hat{h}_{\mu})\text{ such that }\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\hat{\epsilon}_{\mu})\subset\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu},\quad\text{ and }\quad{\rm(ii)}\ \hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}\subset\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\epsilon_{\mu}).

We discuss a construction of such a weak confidence set in Appendix
A.2.1
,
which is simpler to construct compared to the analogous strong confidence set in Appendix
B.1.1
.
Note that the relaxation in this definition (compared to Definition
10
)
is that
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}
needs to contain a set
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\hat{\epsilon}_{\mu})
, rather than
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\epsilon/C)
, in other words, the ratio
\epsilon_{\mu}/\hat{\epsilon}_{\mu}
is allowed to be random.

Notice that strong confidence are generally weak confidence sets: if we pick
\epsilon

such that,
with probability at least
1-\tau
,

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(\hat{h}_{\mu})<\epsilon/C
,
then an
(\epsilon,\tau,C)
-strong confidence set
is also an
(\epsilon,2\tau)
-weak confidence set.

In the following algorithm, the intent is that the input sets

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}
,
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}
are weak confidence sets
based on the
Q
-data
S_{Q}
and
P
-data
S_{P}
, respectively. Relevant confidence parameters are stated in the theorem below.

Algorithm 2
.

Input:
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}
,
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}\subset\mathcal{H}
, and
\hat{h}_{Q}=\mathrm{ERM}_{Q}

If
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}\cap\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}\neq\emptyset
, return any
h\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}\cap\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}
, otherwise return
\hat{h}_{Q}
.

The following result easily follows.

Theorem 7
.

Let
0<\tau\leq 1
, and suppose
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}
is an
(\epsilon_{Q},\tau)
-weak confidence set under
Q
, and
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}
is an
(\epsilon_{P},\tau)
-weak confidence set under P, for some
\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P}>0
depending respectively on
n_{Q}
and
n_{P}
. Let
\hat{h}
denote the hypothesis returned by Algorithm
2
. We then have that, with probability at least
1-2\tau
:

\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h})\leq\min\left\{\epsilon_{Q},\delta(\epsilon_{P})\right\}.

Furthermore, if
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}\cap\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}\neq\emptyset
, we have under the same event that
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h})\leq\delta(\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P})
.

Proof.

Assume the events of Definition
17
hold for
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}
and
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}
(with probability at least
1-2\tau
). By Definition
17
, condition (ii), we have that any
h\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}\cap\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}
is in
\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q})\cap\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{P})
implying

\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)\leq\min\left\{\epsilon_{Q},\delta(\epsilon_{P})\right\}
.

So suppose
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}\cap\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}=\emptyset
. By both conditions of Definition
17
, we have that

\hat{h}_{Q}\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}
and satisfies
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h}_{Q})\leq\epsilon_{Q}
. Now, let
\hat{\epsilon}_{P}
and

\hat{\epsilon}_{Q}
as in Definition
17
. Consider any

h\in\mathcal{H}_{P}(\hat{\epsilon}_{P})
. By condition (i) we have that

h\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{P}
so is therefore not in
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{Q}
, which contains

\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\hat{\epsilon}_{Q})
. Consequently, we have that

\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h}_{Q})<\hat{\epsilon}_{Q}<\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)\leq\delta(\epsilon_{P})
where the last inequality follows from that
h\in\mathcal{H}_{P}(\hat{\epsilon}_{P})\subset\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{P})
.

The last part of the theorem repeats a similar argument as for Theorem
3
: namely, if we have
\hat{h}\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q})\cap\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{P})
, then it follows that

\mathcal{E}_{P}(\hat{h};\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{Q}))\leq\mathcal{E}_{P}(\hat{h})\leq\epsilon_{P}
, implying by definition that
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h})\leq\delta(\epsilon_{Q},\epsilon_{P})
.

A.2.1
Example of a Weak Confidence Set

Here we present a well-known construction of a weak confidence set for classification
(VC-CLS)
(as used, for instance, in the work of
Hanneke and Kpotufe, 2019
),
designed to be adaptive to the noise parameters
(C_{\mu},\beta_{\mu})
from Definition
7
. Importantly we will see that, as a relaxed notion of confidence set, it is naturally easier to state than the strong confidence set described in Section
B.1
.

Proposition 5
.

For
\mu
either
P
or
Q
, consider the following definition of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}
.
Define

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)\doteq\frac{d_{\mathcal{H}}\log(n_{\mu}/d_{\mathcal{H}})+\log(1/\tau)}{n_{\mu}},

where
d_{\mathcal{H}}
denotes the VC dimension of
\mathcal{H}

[
Vapnik and Chervonenkis, 1971
]
.
For any
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
,
define the empirical distance

\hat{\mu}(h\neq h^{\prime})=\frac{1}{n_{\mu}}\sum_{(x,y)\in S_{\mu}}{\mathbbold 1}\!\left\{h(x)\neq h^{\prime}(x)\right\}
.
There exist universal constants
C,C^{\prime}
such that
the following

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}\doteq\left\{h\in\mathcal{H}:\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}(h)\leq C\sqrt{\hat{\mu}(h\neq\hat{h}_{\mu})\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)}+C\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)\right\}

is an
(\epsilon_{\mu},\tau)
-weak confidence set, for

\epsilon_{\mu}=C^{\prime}\left(C_{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)\right)^{\frac{1}{2-\beta_{\mu}}},

where
\beta_{\mu}\in[0,1]
and
C_{\mu}\geq 2
are
as in the Bernstein Class Condition (Definition
7
).

The proof of Proposition
5
is based on the following lemma,
which
is a corollary of results of
[
Vapnik and Chervonenkis, 1974
]

(see
[
Hanneke and Kpotufe, 2022
]
for a formal proof).

Lemma 2

(Uniform Bernstein inequality)
.

For
\mu
either
P
or
Q
, there exists a universal constant
C_{0}>1
such that, with probability at least
1-\tau
,
every
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
satisfy

\left|(R_{\mu}(h)-R_{\mu}(h^{\prime}))-(\hat{R}_{\mu}(h)-\hat{R}_{\mu}(h^{\prime}))\right|\leq C_{0}\sqrt{\hat{\mu}(h\neq h^{\prime})\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)}+C_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)

(30)

and

\frac{1}{2}\mu(h\neq h^{\prime})-C_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)\leq\hat{\mu}(h\neq h^{\prime})\leq 2\mu(h\neq h^{\prime})+C_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau).

(31)

We now present the proof of Proposition
5
.

Proof of Proposition
5
.

In Lemma 2 of
[
Hanneke and Kpotufe, 2022
]
, property (ii) of Definition
17
is established, on the event of probability at least
1-\tau
from Lemma
2
.
Moreover, note that on this same event from Lemma
2
,
for
\hat{\epsilon}_{\mu}=\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(\hat{h}_{\mu})+\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)
,
any
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\hat{\epsilon}_{\mu})
has

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}(h)\leq R_{\mu}(h)-R_{\mu}(\hat{h}_{\mu})+C_{0}\sqrt{\hat{\mu}(h\neq\hat{h}_{\mu})\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)}+C_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)\leq C_{0}\sqrt{\hat{\mu}(h\neq\hat{h}_{\mu})\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)}+(C_{0}+1)\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau),

where the second inequality is due to
h\in\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\hat{\epsilon}_{\mu})
,
implying

.
Therefore, any such

is also in
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}
(for

in the definition of

and hence property (i) of Definition
17

is also satisfied on this event.

S_{P}

S_{Q}

A.3.2
Regression Lower Bound

Theorem
2
will follow immediately from the following two lemmas.

Lemma 4
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2
, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{h}}\sup_{(P,Q)\in\Lambda\left(\sigma^{2}_{Y},\lambda_{0},\epsilon_{0}\right)}\Expectation\ \mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h})\geq c\cdot\min\left\{\frac{d\cdot\sigma^{2}_{Y}}{n_{Q}},\lambda_{0}\frac{d\cdot\sigma^{2}_{Y}}{n_{P}}\right\}.

(45)

Proof.

We consider the following construction with fixed marginals
P_{X},Q_{X}
. Let
e_{i},i\in[d]
denote the
i
-th coordinate vector in
d
. We also let
\epsilon\doteq\min\left\{\frac{d\cdot\sigma_{Y}^{2}}{n_{Q}},\lambda_{0}\frac{d\cdot\sigma_{Y}^{2}}{n_{P}}\right\}
and let
\tau>0
denote a free variable to be specified later in terms of
\epsilon
. Finally, let
\omega\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{d}
denote a vector of Rademacher r.v’s
\omega_{i}
.

\bullet

Q_{X}
is supported
\left\{e_{i}\right\}
, and satisfies
Q_{X}(e_{i})={1}/{d}
.
We then define the joint
Q_{\omega}=Q_{X}\times Q^{\omega}_{Y|X}
, where the conditional
Q^{\omega}_{Y|X}
is given by the relation

Y=\tau\cdot\omega^{\top}X+{\cal{N}}(0,\sigma^{2}_{Y})
where the noise variable is independent of
X
.

\bullet

P_{X}
is supported
\left\{0\right\}\cup\left\{e_{i}\right\}
, and satisfies
P_{X}(e_{i})={1}/(d\cdot\lambda_{0})
, and
P_{X}(0)=1-1/\lambda_{0}
.
We then define the joint
P_{\omega}=P_{X}\times Q^{\omega}_{Y|X}
.

First we verify that for every instance
\omega\in\left\{\pm 1\right\}^{d}
,

(P_{\omega},Q_{\omega})\in\Lambda(\lambda_{0},\epsilon_{0})
. By definition, the two distributions shares the same risk minimizer

h_{\omega}(x)\doteq\tau\cdot\omega^{\top}x
. Next, we have

\Sigma_{Q}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i}e_{i}e_{i}^{\top}=\frac{1}{d}I_{d}
, while

\Sigma_{P}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}}\Sigma_{Q}
with inverse
\lambda_{0}\cdot\Sigma_{Q}^{-1}
, and we therefore have
\lambda_{\max}\left(\Sigma_{P}^{-1}\Sigma_{Q}\right)=\lambda_{0}
.

Now, for any
h:\real^{d}\mapsto\real
, define the excess risk w.r.t.
Q_{\omega}
as
\mathcal{E}_{Q_{\omega}}(h)\doteq\mathbb{E}_{Q_{X}}(h(X)-h_{\omega}(X))^{2}
.

Next, let
\hat{h}:S_{P}\times S_{Q}\mapsto{\cal L}_{2,Q_{X}}
denote any learner, where
S_{P},S_{Q}
denote independent datasets of respective sizes
n_{P},n_{Q}
. For simplicity we also let
\hat{h}
denote the learner’s output. Then for any
i\in[d]
, define
\hat{\omega}_{i}=\text{sign}(h(e_{i}))
.

We have that

\mathcal{E}_{Q_{\omega}}(\hat{h})\geq\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\tau^{2}/d)\cdot{\mathbbold 1}\!\left\{\hat{\omega}_{i}\neq\omega_{i}\right\}
. Letting
\Pi_{\omega}\doteq P_{\omega}^{n_{P}}\times Q_{\omega}^{n_{Q}}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\sup_{\omega}\Expectation_{(S_{P},S_{Q})\sim\Pi_{\omega}}\mathcal{E}_{Q_{\omega}}(\hat{h})\geq\Expectation_{\omega}\Expectation_{(S_{P},S_{Q})\sim\Pi_{\omega}}\mathcal{E}_{Q_{\omega}}(\hat{h})\geq\tau^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{d}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Expectation_{\omega}\Expectation_{(S_{P},S_{Q})\sim\Pi_{\omega}}{\mathbbold 1}\!\left\{\hat{\omega}_{i}\neq\omega_{i}\right\}.

(46)

Since the integrand in the summation in (
46
) is bounded, we can change the order of integration as
In particular, for any fixed sample
S_{P},S_{Q}
, let
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}
denote respectively the combined
X
and
Y
values in both samples, and let let
\mathbf{X}_{i}
denote
X
values falling at
e_{i}
(from both
P,Q
sampled combined), and let
\mathbf{Y}_{i}
denote the corresponding
Y
values.
We then consider the following integration order

\displaystyle\Expectation_{\omega}\Expectation_{(S_{P},S_{Q})\sim\Pi_{\omega}}{\mathbbold 1}\!\left\{\hat{\omega}_{i}\neq\omega_{i}\right\}=\Expectation_{\mathbf{X}}\ \Expectation_{\omega\setminus\omega_{i}}\ \Expectation_{\mathbf{Y}\setminus\mathbf{Y}_{i}\ \mid\ \omega\setminus\omega_{i},\mathbf{X}}\ \mathbb{E}_{i}\ {\mathbbold 1}\!\left\{\hat{\omega}_{i}\neq\omega_{i}\right\},

(47)

where we let
\mathbb{E}_{i}(\cdot)
denote integration over
\mathbf{Y}_{i},\omega_{i}
, conditioned on all other random variables in
S_{P},S_{Q},\omega
.

Let
\hat{n}_{i}\doteq\left|\mathbf{X}_{i}\right|
. Suppose
\hat{n}_{i}>0
and define the constant vector
a\doteq[\tau,\ldots,\tau]\in\real^{\hat{n}_{i}}
. Notice that, viewed as a vector,

\mathbf{Y}_{i}=\omega_{i}\cdot a+{\cal N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{Y}\cdot I_{\hat{n}_{i}})
, i.e., is drawn according to the mixture
\frac{1}{2}{\cal N}(-a,\sigma^{2}_{Y}\cdot I_{\hat{n}_{i}})+\frac{1}{2}{\cal N}(a,\sigma^{2}_{Y}\cdot I_{\hat{n}_{i}})
. The Bayes classifier for
\omega_{i}
, namely,
h^{\!*}_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{i})\doteq\text{sign}\left(a^{\top}\mathbf{Y}_{i}\right)
, has 0-1 error

\mathbb{E}_{i}\ {\mathbbold 1}\!\left\{h^{\!*}_{i}(\mathbf{Y}_{i})\neq\omega_{i}\right\}=\phi(-\left\|a\right\|/\sigma_{Y})
, where
\phi
denotes the standard-normal CDF. Hence we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i}\ {\mathbbold 1}\!\left\{\hat{\omega}_{i}\neq\omega_{i}\right\}\geq\phi(-\left\|a\right\|/\sigma_{Y})=\phi\!\left(-{\tau}\sqrt{\hat{n}_{i}/\sigma^{2}_{Y}}\right).

(48)

If
\hat{n}_{i}=0
then

\mathbb{E}_{i}\ {\mathbbold 1}\!\left\{\hat{\omega}_{i}\neq\omega_{i}\right\}=\frac{1}{2}=\phi(0)
,
since the only randomness left is then in
\omega_{i}
. Now let
\mathbf{X}\sim P_{X}^{n_{P}}\times Q_{X}^{n_{Q}}
denote all
X
values in
S_{P},S_{Q}
. Integrating over
\mathbf{X}
, and by convexity of
z\mapsto\phi(-\tau\sqrt{z/\sigma^{2}_{Y}}),z\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle\Expectation_{\omega}\Expectation_{(S_{P},S_{Q})\sim\Pi_{\omega}}{\mathbbold 1}\!\left\{\hat{\omega}_{i}\neq\omega_{i}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}\ \phi\!\left(-\tau\sqrt{\hat{n}_{i}/\sigma^{2}_{Y}}\right)\geq\phi\!\left(-\tau\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{Y}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}\ \hat{n}_{i}}\right)

(49)

\displaystyle=\phi\!\left(-\tau\sqrt{\frac{1}{d\sigma^{2}_{Y}}(n_{P}/\lambda_{0}+n_{Q})}\right).

(50)

Setting
\tau^{2}=\epsilon\leq 2\cdot\frac{d\sigma^{2}_{Y}}{n_{P}/\lambda_{0}+n_{Q}}
,
we see that the r.h.s. above is at least
\phi(-\sqrt{2})
. Thus, to finish, we write

\displaystyle\sup_{\omega}\Expectation_{(S_{P},S_{Q})\sim\Pi_{\omega}}\mathcal{E}_{Q_{\omega}}(\hat{h})\geq\tau^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{d}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{d}\phi(-\sqrt{2})=\phi(-\sqrt{2})\cdot\epsilon.

(51)

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
2
, we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{h}}\sup_{(P,Q)\in\Lambda\left(\sigma^{2}_{Y},\lambda_{0},\epsilon_{0}\right)}\Expectation\ \mathcal{E}_{Q}(\hat{h})\geq c\cdot\min\left\{\frac{d\sigma^{2}_{Y}}{n_{Q}},\epsilon_{0}\right\},

for a universal constant

.

Proof.

The lemma trivially holds if
\epsilon_{0}=0
, so w.l.o.g. suppose
\epsilon_{0}>0

We consider the same setup and notation as in the proof of Lemma
4
,
with the following exceptions:

•

\tau^{2}=\min\!\left\{\frac{d\sigma^{2}_{Y}}{n_{Q}},\epsilon_{0}\right\}
,

•

we fix

for all

and

.

In particular, note that

-risk minimizer is given by

(i.e.,

whereas (as in the proof of Lemma

-risk minimizer is given by the weight vector

Also note that,

is

In particular,

\Expectation_{\omega}\Expectation_{(S_{P},S_{Q})\sim P^{n_{P}}\times Q_{\omega}^{n_{Q}}}{\mathbbold 1}\!\left\{\hat{\omega}_{i}\neq\omega_{i}\right\}\geq\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}\ \phi\!\left(-\tau\sqrt{\hat{n}_{i,Q}/\sigma^{2}_{Y}}\right)\geq\phi\!\left(-\tau\sqrt{\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{Y}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}\ \hat{n}_{i,Q}}\right)=\phi\!\left(-\tau\sqrt{\frac{n_{Q}}{d\sigma^{2}_{Y}}}\right)\geq\phi(-1),

∎

Appendix B
Strong Modulus

B.1
Examples of Strong Confidence Sets

We present some examples of strong confidence sets for classical problems in classification and regression. We note in particular that, while Theorem
3
requires two such confidence sets for different values of

\epsilon
, the constructions below are for a single
\epsilon
but are stated in a way to make it clear that different values of
\epsilon
are admissible by varying constant factors in the definition of these sets.

B.1.1
Classification Example

We consider classification with a VC class
\mathcal{H}
. In what follows assume
\ell(a,b)={\mathbbold 1}\!\left\{a\neq b\right\}
and for the simplest case suppose
\mathcal{Y}=\{-1,1\}
. We first provide a simple construction of a strong confidence set with
\epsilon\approx n^{-1/2}
, based on the classic uniform convergence bound of
Talagrand [1994]
.
Subsequently, we will refine these sets via a more-sophisticated construction, to provide strong confidence sets adaptively achieving an
\epsilon\lesssim n^{-\frac{1}{2-\beta_{\mu}}}
,
for
\beta_{\mu}
from the noise condition of Definition
7
,
which are therefore smaller when the (unknown) value

\beta_{\mu}>0
.

Proposition 8

(
\sqrt{n}
-confidence sets)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}^{\prime}(\tau)\doteq n^{-1}(d_{\mathcal{H}}+\log(1/\tau))
.
For any sufficiently large universal constant
C
,
the set

\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}\doteq\left\{h\in\mathcal{H}:\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}(h)\leq C\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime}_{\mu}(\tau)}\right\}

(55)

is an
(\epsilon,\tau,2)
-strong confidence set (under
\mu
),
for
\epsilon=(4/3)C\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{\prime}_{\mu}(\tau)}
.

Proof.

The classic result of

[
Talagrand, 1994
]
provides that,
for any sufficiently large universal constant
C
,
with probability at least
1-\tau
,

\forall h\in\mathcal{H},\left|\hat{R}_{\mu}(h)-R_{\mu}(h)\right|\leq\frac{C}{6}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}^{\prime}(\tau)}.

(56)

On this event, every
h\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}

satisfies

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h)=R_{\mu}(h)-\inf_{h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}}R_{\mu}(h^{\prime})\leq\hat{R}_{\mu}(h)-\inf_{h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}}\hat{R}_{\mu}(h^{\prime})+\frac{C}{3}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}^{\prime}(\tau)}=\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}(h)+\frac{C}{3}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}^{\prime}(\tau)}\leq\frac{4}{3}C\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}^{\prime}(\tau)},

(57)

where the first inequality is by (
56
)
and the last inequality follows from the definition of
\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}
.
Moreover, on this same event,
any
h\in\mathcal{H}
with

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h)\leq\frac{2C}{3}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}^{\prime}(\tau)}

has

\hat{\mathcal{E}}_{\mu}(h)\leq\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h)+\frac{C}{3}\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}^{\prime}(\tau)}\leq C\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}^{\prime}(\tau)},

(58)

and hence
h\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}
.
Thus, for
\epsilon=\frac{4}{3}C\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}^{\prime}(\tau)}
,
we have

\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\epsilon/2)\subset\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}\subset\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\epsilon)
.
∎

) from Lemma
2
,
we have

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h)\leq\epsilon+C_{0}\cdot\sqrt{\text{diam}_{\hat{\mu}}(\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h)))\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)}+C_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau),

(63)

and moreover (also by Lemma
2
),

\text{diam}_{\hat{\mu}}(\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h)))\leq 2\text{diam}_{\mu}(\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h)))+C_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau).

(64)

By the Bernstein Class Condition (Definition
7
),

\text{diam}_{\mu}(\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h)))\leq C_{\mu}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h)^{\beta_{\mu}}
.
Altogether, we have

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h)\leq\max\!\left\{2C_{0}\cdot\sqrt{2C_{\mu}\cdot\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h)^{\beta_{\mu}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)},2\epsilon+4C_{0}^{3/2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)\right\}.

(65)

Solving for
\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h)
yields

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h)\leq\max\!\left\{\left(2C_{0}\right)^{\frac{2}{2-\beta_{\mu}}}\cdot\left(2C_{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)\right)^{\frac{1}{2-\beta_{\mu}}},2\epsilon+4C_{0}^{3/2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)\right\}\leq 4\epsilon,

(66)

for an appropriately large choice of universal constant
C^{\prime}
.

We are now ready to argue any
\epsilon\in 2^{-\mathbb{N}_{0}}
with
\epsilon\geq\epsilon_{\mu}
satisfies (
59
).
By the above, we have

\mathcal{H}_{\hat{\mu}}(2\epsilon)\subset\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(8\epsilon)
.
By Lemma
2
,

\text{diam}_{\hat{\mu}}(\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(8\epsilon))\leq 2\text{diam}_{\mu}(\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(8\epsilon))+C_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)
.
Together,

\text{diam}_{\hat{\mu}}(\mathcal{H}_{\hat{\mu}}(2\epsilon))\leq 2\text{diam}_{\mu}(\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(8\epsilon))+C_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)
.
Finally, by the Bernstein Class Condition,

\text{diam}_{\mu}(\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(8\epsilon))\leq C_{\mu}\cdot(8\epsilon)^{\beta_{\mu}}
.
Together, we have

\displaystyle C_{0}\sqrt{\text{diam}_{\hat{\mu}}(\mathcal{H}_{\hat{\mu}}(2\epsilon))\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)}+C_{0}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)\leq C_{0}\sqrt{2(8\epsilon)^{\beta_{\mu}}\cdot C_{\mu}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)}+2C_{0}^{3/2}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\mu}(\tau)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2},

(67)

for an appropriately large choice of the universal constant
C^{\prime}
.
We have thus established that
\hat{\epsilon}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{loc}}\leq 2\epsilon_{\mu}
.
∎

B.1.2
Regression Example

We consider the linear regression setting of

(LIN-REG)
.
Assume throughout this section that
\left\|x\right\|\leq 1\ \forall_{\mu}x
, and
Y-\mathbb{E}[Y|x]
is uniformly subGaussian with parameter
\sigma^{2}_{Y}\geq 1
; see Condition 3 of
[
Hsu et al., 2012
]
.

Abbreviate
\Sigma=\Sigma_{\mu}
and
define
\hat{\Sigma}\doteq\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\mu}}XX^{\top}
, where
\hat{\mu}
is the empirical version of
\mu
on
S_{\mu}\sim\mu^{n_{\mu}}
. We make use of the following corollary to classical concentration results.

Lemma 6

(Matrix concentration)
.

For any
0<\tau<1
, the following holds with probability at least
1-\tau
. There exists
C=C(\tau,d,\text{eigs}(\Sigma))
such that, for
n_{\mu}\geq C
, we have (writing
A\preceq B
for
A-B
is negative semidefinite
):

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Sigma\preceq\hat{\Sigma}\preceq\frac{3}{2}\Sigma.\quad\text{ Equivalently }\forall v\in\real^{d},\quad\frac{1}{2}\left\|v\right\|_{\Sigma}^{2}\leq\left\|v\right\|_{\hat{\Sigma}}^{2}\leq\frac{3}{2}\left\|v\right\|_{\Sigma}^{2}.

(68)

Proof.

Let
\hat{A}\doteq\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}\hat{\Sigma}\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}
so that
\mathbb{E}\hat{A}=I_{d}
. Then standard matrix Bernstein bounds (see e.g.,
[
Tropp, 2015
, Theorem 1.6.2]
) imply that, with probability at least
1-\tau
,

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{A}-I_{d}\right\|_{\text{op}}\leq\alpha_{\mu},\text{ for }\alpha_{\mu}\doteq\sqrt{c\frac{\log(d/\tau)}{n_{\mu}}}+c\frac{\log(d/\tau)}{n_{\mu}}\text{ where }c=c\left(\sup_{X}\left\|\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}}X\right\|^{2}\right)=c\left(\lambda_{\text{min}}(\Sigma)\right).

(69)

In other words, we have

(1-\alpha_{\mu})I_{d}\preceq\hat{A}\preceq(1+\alpha_{\mu})I_{d}
. We obtain the result by multiplying either side of each term by
\Sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}
, and setting
n_{\mu}
sufficiently large so that
\alpha_{u}\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
∎

We have the following proposition. Notice that, the proposition assumes knowledge of upper-bounds on distributional parameters, namely the subGaussian parameter
\sigma_{Y}
, and a parameter
c_{\mu}
which we assume to be an upper bound on

R_{\mu}(h_{w_{*}})-R_{\mu}^{*}
, where we let
R_{\mu}^{*}
denotes Bayes risk, and
w_{*}
minimizes
R_{\mu}(h_{w})
over the linear class. Thus, if the regression function
\mathbb{E}[Y|x]
is linear as often assumed, we have that
c_{\mu}=0
; more generally,
if
Y
itself is bounded, then
c_{\mu}
can be taken as the square of this bound.

Proposition 10
.

Let
\hat{w}
minimize
\hat{R}_{\mu}(h_{w})
over
w\in\real^{d}
, and for any such
w
, and symmetric and positive
A\in\real^{d\times d}
, define

\left\|w\right\|_{A}^{2}=w^{\top}Aw
. Assume
n_{\mu}\geq C(\tau,d,\text{eigs}(\Sigma))
as in Lemma
6
.

In what follows,
c_{0}
is a universal constant, and
c_{\mu}
depends on
\mu
as explained above. We have that, the following set is a
(26\epsilon,2\tau,26)
-strong confidence set, for
\epsilon=c_{0}\cdot\sigma^{2}_{Y}\frac{d+c_{\mu}+\log(1/\tau)}{n_{\mu}}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}\doteq\left\{h_{w}:\left\|w-\hat{w}\right\|_{\hat{\Sigma}}^{2}\leq 6\epsilon\right\}.

(70)

Proof.

We rely on the following two facts (see e.g.
[
Hsu et al., 2012
]
). First, with probability at least
1-\tau
, we have that

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h_{\hat{w}})\leq\epsilon
, for some universal
c_{0}
. Second, it is a classical fact that, for any
w\in\real^{d}
,

\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h_{\hat{w}})=\left\|w-w_{*}\right\|^{2}_{\Sigma}
, where
w_{*}
minimizes
R_{\mu}(h_{w})
. Using Lemma
6
, we then have that, with probability at least
1-2\tau
, the following holds.

- For any
h_{w}\in\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\mu}(h_{w})=\left\|w-w_{*}\right\|_{\Sigma}^{2}\leq 2\left\|w-\hat{w}\right\|_{\Sigma}^{2}+2\left\|\hat{w}-w_{*}\right\|_{\Sigma}^{2}\leq 4\left\|w-\hat{w}\right\|_{\hat{\Sigma}}^{2}+2\epsilon\leq 26\epsilon.

(71)

- For any
h_{w}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mu}(\epsilon)
, we have that

\displaystyle\left\|w-\hat{w}\right\|_{\hat{\Sigma}}^{2}\leq\frac{3}{2}\left\|w-\hat{w}\right\|_{\Sigma}^{2}\leq 3\left(\left\|w-w_{*}\right\|_{\Sigma}^{2}+\left\|\hat{w}-w_{*}\right\|_{\Sigma}^{2}\right)\leq 6\epsilon.

(72)

∎

-strong confidence set, for

simply by plugging in

\hat{w}
guarantees some excess risk

of any

Appendix C
Gap Between Strong and Weak Moduli

C.1
Further Definitions

The following condition on the
pseudo-denseness
of
monotone
subsets of
\mathcal{H}
will be shown to be equivalent to the
monotonicity
condition of Definition
19
and can yield better insight on the problem.

Definition 19
.

We call a subset
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{H}

monotone
if it satisfies:

\forall h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
we have that

\mathcal{E}_{P}(h^{\prime})\leq\mathcal{E}_{P}(h)\implies\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h^{\prime})\leq\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h).

Definition 20

(Pseudo-dense Monotone Sets)
.

We say that
monotone sets are pseudo-dense
if
there exists a sequence of
monotone
subsets
\mathcal{H}_{k}\subset\mathcal{H},k\in\mathbb{N}
, satisfying the following
pseudo-denseness
condition.

(i)

\forall\epsilon_{1}\in\mathcal{\ddot{E}}_{Q}(\mathcal{H})\text{ s.t. }\epsilon_{1}>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
,

\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup\left\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h):h\in\mathcal{H}_{k},\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)\leq\epsilon_{1}\right\}=\epsilon_{1}
.

(ii)

\forall\epsilon_{2}>0
s.t.
\delta(\epsilon_{2})>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
,
\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup\left\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h):h\in\mathcal{H}_{k},\mathcal{E}_{P}(h)\leq\epsilon_{2}\right\}=\delta(\epsilon_{2})
.

It should be immediately clear that the above pseudo-denseness of monotones sets imply the conditions of Definition
14
, i.e., that the transfer problem is monotonic above
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
. The other direction is not obvious:
we show in Proposition
12
that if Definition
14
holds, then there is no gap above
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
. On the other hand,
we will show in Proposition
13
that, if
a transfer problem has no gap above
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
it must hold that monotone sets are pseudo-dense in
\mathcal{H}
.

Remark 19
.

Pseudo-denseness roughly states that the union of these
\mathcal{H}_{k}
’s is
nearly indistinguishable
from
\mathcal{H}

in the achievable
\mathcal{E}_{Q}
values and the induced modulus,
down to
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
, while
monotonicity
roughly states that
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)
is nonincreasing as
\mathcal{E}_{P}(h)
decreases over
h
’s in
\mathcal{H}_{k}
. In particular,
all these conditions are met
if
\mathcal{H}
itself satisfies monotonicity, i.e., we can take
\mathcal{H}_{k}=\mathcal{H}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

C.2
Proof of Gap Characterization Theorem
4

We start with the following simple lemma, which allows us to restrict attention to situations where
\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}<\delta(\epsilon_{2})
.

Lemma 7
.

For any
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}>0
, we have that

\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})<\min\left\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\right\}
only if

\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}<\delta(\epsilon_{2})
.

Proof.

Suppose
\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}\geq\delta(\epsilon_{2})\geq\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
. That is,

\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\delta(\epsilon_{2}))\subset\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})
so that by Corollary
1
,
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\delta(\epsilon_{2})
.
∎

The anti-monotonic part of the theorem almost follows by definition as established in the following proposition.

Proposition 11

(Below
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
, No-Gap
\equiv
Anti-Monotonicity below)
.

We have that
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\min\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\}

for every
0<\epsilon_{1}\leq\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}

and
\epsilon_{2}>0
,
if and only if

(P,Q,\mathcal{H},\ell)
is anti-monotonic below
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
.

Proof.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}>0
.
If
(P,Q,\mathcal{H},\ell)
is anti-monotonic below
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
,
then for all
0<\epsilon_{1}<\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
and
\epsilon_{2}>0

we have

\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}\geq\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\geq\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}))\doteq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\sup\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h):h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}),\mathcal{E}_{P}(h;\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}))\leq\epsilon\}=\ddot{\epsilon}_{1},

implying equality. Noting that
\epsilon_{1}\leq\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
implies
\min\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\}=\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}

completes this half of the claim.

For the other direction,
if
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\min\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\}

for every
0<\epsilon_{1}\leq\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
and
\epsilon_{2}>0
,
then
again by the fact that any such
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}
have

\min\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\}=\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}
,
we have

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}(\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}))\doteq\lim_{\epsilon_{2}\to 0}\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\lim_{\epsilon_{2}\to 0}\min\!\left\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\right\}=\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}.

Thus,
(P,Q,\mathcal{H},\ell)
is anti-monotonic below
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
.
∎

Proposition 12

(Monotonicity
\implies
No-Gap Above
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
)
.

If the transfer problem
(P,Q,\mathcal{H},\ell)
is monotonic above
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
, then
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\min\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\}

for every
\epsilon_{1}>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}

and
\epsilon_{2}>0
.

Proof.

We will argue by contradiction. Suppose there is a gap: fix some
\epsilon_{2}>0
and suppose that for some
\epsilon_{1}>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
, we have
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})<\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}<\delta(\epsilon_{2})
, which by Lemma
7
is exhaustive of situations where
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\neq\min\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\}
.

First, note that
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\geq\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
for
\epsilon_{1}>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
:
since we have
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}\doteq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\delta(\epsilon)
, pick
\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}<\epsilon_{2}
sufficiently small so that
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}\leq\delta(\epsilon_{2}^{\prime})\leq\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}
; by Lemma
7
, we have that
\delta(\epsilon_{2}^{\prime})=\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}^{\prime})\leq\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
(by Corollary
1
). It follows that, under the assumption of a gap, we also have
\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
.

Pick any
\tau<\left(\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}-\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\right)\land\left(\delta(\epsilon_{2})-\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}\right)
. By monotonicity (Definition
14
), consider any
h,h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}
satisfying (
17
)

\displaystyle\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})<\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}-\tau\leq\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)\leq\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}\text{ and }

(73)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{P}(h^{\prime})\leq\epsilon_{2}\text{ and }\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h^{\prime})\geq\delta(\epsilon_{2})-\tau>\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}.

(74)

Notice that we must have that

\mathcal{E}_{P}(h)>\epsilon_{2}
since otherwise, we would have

h\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})\cap\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{2})
, implying by Proposition
1
that

\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)\leq\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\sup\left\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h^{\prime}):h^{\prime}\in\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})\cap\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{2})\right\}.

Altogether, we have that

\mathcal{E}_{P}(h^{\prime})\leq\epsilon_{2}<\mathcal{E}_{P}(h)
while
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h^{\prime})>\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}\geq\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)
. In other words, for all such values of
\tau
, there exist no
h,h^{\prime}
satisfying
(
17
). It follows that
(P,Q,\mathcal{H},\ell)
cannot be monotonic above
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
.
∎

We complete the equivalence in the following proposition.

Proposition 13

(No-Gap Above
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}

\implies
Monotonicity)
.

If
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\min\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\}
for every
\epsilon_{1}{>}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}

and
\epsilon_{2}>0
, then
monotone
subsets are pseudo-dense in
\mathcal{H}
(as per Definition
20
).

The proof of Proposition
13
is most involved, as it requires exhibiting monotone subsets of
\mathcal{H}
satisfying Definition
14
.

C.2.1
Proof of Proposition
13

The following is assumed throughout this proof section.

Assumption 2

(No Gap Above
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
)
.

Suppose
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\min\{\ddot{\epsilon}_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\}
for all
\epsilon_{1}{>}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}

and
\epsilon_{2}>0
.

Our construction of monotone subsets relies of the following crucial notion.

Definition 21
.

For every
\epsilon_{1}\in\mathcal{\ddot{E}}_{Q}(\mathcal{H})
, define the
inverse modulus
as

\delta^{\scalebox{0.8}{-}\scalebox{0.6}{1}}(\epsilon_{1})=\inf\left\{\epsilon_{2}>0:\delta(\epsilon_{2})\geq\epsilon_{1}\right\},

and we let
\delta^{\scalebox{0.8}{-}\scalebox{0.6}{1}}(\epsilon_{1})=\infty
if the set is empty.

The above is always finite for
\epsilon_{1}<\sup{\mathcal{\ddot{E}}_{Q}(\mathcal{H})}

h\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)\geq\epsilon_{1}
and let
\epsilon_{2}=\mathcal{E}_{P}(h)
; by definition,
\delta(\epsilon_{2})\geq\epsilon_{1}

\sup{\mathcal{\ddot{E}}_{Q}(\mathcal{H})}
is achieved by some

.
Notice that a necessary condition for the set
\left\{\epsilon_{2}>0:\delta(\epsilon_{2})\geq\epsilon_{1}\right\}
to be empty
is that

\epsilon_{1}=\sup\mathcal{\ddot{E}}_{Q}(\mathcal{H})<\infty
and
\sup\{\mathcal{E}_{P}(h):h\in\mathcal{H}\}=\infty
.
Thus, in most scenarios of interest (e.g., classification, or linear regression)
this will never occur.
Nevertheless, our results also allows for this case.

Also, notice that
\delta^{\scalebox{0.8}{-}\scalebox{0.6}{1}}(\epsilon_{1})
is a non-decreasing function since for
\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}>\epsilon_{1}
, the set

\left\{\epsilon_{2}:\delta(\epsilon_{2})\geq\epsilon_{1}^{\prime}\right\}\subset\left\{\epsilon_{2}:\delta(\epsilon_{2})\geq\epsilon_{1}\right\}
.

Lemma 8

(Link to Strong Modulus)
.

We have:

\forall\epsilon_{1}\in\mathcal{\ddot{E}}_{Q}(\mathcal{H})\setminus[0,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}],\quad\delta^{\scalebox{0.8}{-}\scalebox{0.6}{1}}(\epsilon_{1})=\inf\left\{\epsilon_{2}>0:\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\epsilon_{1}\right\}.

Proof.

This follows since we have the tautology

\left\{\delta(\epsilon_{2})\geq\epsilon_{1}\right\}\equiv\left\{\min\left\{\epsilon_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\right\}=\epsilon_{1}\right\}
, and
\epsilon_{1}=\ddot{\epsilon}_{1}
.
∎

\bullet

For each
l\in\mathbb{N}
,
for
\alpha=2^{-l}
,
define a finite set

\inf\!\left(\left[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}\!+\!i2^{-l},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}\!+\!(i\!+\!1)2^{-l}\right)\!\cap\mathcal{\ddot{E}}_{Q}(\mathcal{H})\right)
,
among

for which this set is non-empty.

Properties:

1.

For every
l,l^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}

with

.

2.

For every
\epsilon_{1}\in\mathcal{\ddot{E}}_{Q}(\mathcal{H})\cap[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}+\alpha,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}+\frac{1}{\alpha})

there exists
\epsilon^{\prime}_{1}\in{\cal A}_{\alpha}\cap[\epsilon_{1}-\alpha,\epsilon_{1}]
.

\bullet

Approximation parameters.
For every fixed non-empty
\cal A_{\alpha}
, pick
\zeta,\beta>0

Remark 20

(Proof Structure)
.

Note that if such an assignment of

We start with the following key lemma.

Lemma 9
.

Consider any
\epsilon_{1}\in\mathcal{\ddot{E}}_{Q}(\mathcal{H}),\epsilon_{1}>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}
, and let
\epsilon_{2}
satisfy

\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\epsilon_{1}
. Then for any
\zeta,\eta>0
,
\exists h
such that

\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h)\in[\epsilon_{1}-\zeta,\epsilon_{1}]\text{ and }\mathcal{E}_{P}(h)\in[\delta^{\scalebox{0.8}{-}\scalebox{0.6}{1}}(\epsilon_{1})-\eta,\epsilon_{2}].

Proof.

First notice that

\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{2})\cap\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})\setminus\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}-\zeta)
is non-empty:
if it were empty, then
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})\leq\epsilon_{1}-\zeta
(contradicting
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\epsilon_{1}
) since by Corollary
1
the strong modulus equals
\sup\left\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h):h\in\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{2})\cap\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})\right\}
. Hence, we can define:

\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}=\sup\left\{\mathcal{E}_{P}(h):h\in\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{2})\cap\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})\setminus\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}-\zeta)\right\}.

It holds that
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}^{\prime})=\epsilon_{1}
, since
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}^{\prime})\leq\epsilon_{1}
by definition, whereas

\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{2}^{\prime})\cap\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})\text{ contains }\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{2})\cap\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})\setminus\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}-\zeta),

implying
\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}^{\prime})\geq\sup\left\{\mathcal{E}_{Q}(h):h\in\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{2})\cap\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})\setminus\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}-\zeta)\right\}=\epsilon_{1}
.

We therefore have
\delta^{\scalebox{0.8}{-}\scalebox{0.6}{1}}(\epsilon_{1})\leq\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}\leq\epsilon_{2}
, since by Lemma
8
,
\delta^{\scalebox{0.8}{-}\scalebox{0.6}{1}}(\epsilon_{1})
is the infimum of

\left\{\epsilon:\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon)=\epsilon_{1}\right\}
.

It follows that
\exists h\in\mathcal{H}_{P}(\epsilon_{2})\cap\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1})\setminus\mathcal{H}_{Q}(\epsilon_{1}-\zeta)

with
\mathcal{E}_{P}(h)\geq\epsilon_{2}^{\prime}-\eta
, establishing the claim.
∎

Corollary 5
.

For any
\epsilon_{1}\in\mathcal{\ddot{E}}_{Q}(\mathcal{H})
with
\epsilon_{1}>\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\scalebox{0.6}{P,Q}}^{\sharp}

,
\exists h\in\mathcal{H}

Pick
\epsilon_{2}\in(\delta^{\scalebox{0.8}{-}\scalebox{0.6}{1}}(\epsilon_{1}),\delta^{\scalebox{0.8}{-}\scalebox{0.6}{1}}(\epsilon_{1})+\eta)

By definition of
\delta^{\scalebox{0.8}{-}\scalebox{0.6}{1}}(\epsilon_{1})

\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})=\min\{\epsilon_{1},\delta(\epsilon_{2})\}=\epsilon_{1}

9
.
∎

The above immediately implies a first simple recursive condition for choices of

Corollary 6

(Conditional choice of
h_{i}

Consider the construction of
\mathcal{H}_{\alpha,\beta}

Lemma 10

Let

\epsilon_{1}<\epsilon_{Q}

Next, consider case (ii).

\mathcal{E}_{Q_{\sigma}}(h_{\sigma^{\prime}})\leq\frac{c_{1}}{\kappa_{0}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{dist}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})}{d}\cdot f(\epsilon_{P})\leq f\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{dist}(\sigma,\sigma^{\prime})}{d}\epsilon_{P}\right)=f\!\left(\mathcal{E}_{P_{\sigma}}(h_{\sigma^{\prime}})\right).

(83)

Therefore, for any

\displaystyle\delta(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})

(84)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{1}\land\max\!\left\{f(\mathcal{E}_{P_{\sigma}}(h_{\sigma^{\prime}}):\mathcal{E}_{P_{\sigma}}(h_{\sigma^{\prime}})\leq\epsilon_{2}\right\}=\min\{\epsilon_{1},f(\epsilon_{2})\}=g(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}).

(85)

Next we argue the claim that each of these
