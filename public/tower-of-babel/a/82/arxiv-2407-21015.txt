Comparative Analyses of the Type D ASEP: Stochastic Fusion and Crystal Bases
Erik BRODSKY a, Eva ENGEL b, Connor PANISH c and Lillian STOLBERG d

Abstract
The Type D asymmetric simple exclusion process (ASEP), a particle system involving two classes of particles, was studied from both probabilistic and algebraic perspectives by Kuan et al. (2022). From a probabilistic perspective, we perform stochastic fusion on the Type D ASEP introduced by Kuan (2019) and analyze the outcome on the generator matrix, limits of drift speed, stationary distributions, and Markov self-duality. From an algebraic perspective, we construct a fused Type D ASEP system from a Casimir element of 𝒰 q ​ ( 𝔰 ​ 𝔬 6 ) \mathcal{U}_{q}(\mathfrak{so}_{6}) , using crystal bases to analyze and manipulate various representations of 𝒰 q ​ ( 𝔰 ​ 𝔬 6 ) \mathcal{U}_{q}(\mathfrak{so}_{6}) . We know that the same generators for the normal ASEP are produced by stochastic fusion and by a suitable ground state transformation on a central element of 𝒰 q ​ ( 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 2 ) \mathcal{U}_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}) in a symmetric tensor product representation. However, in the case of the Type D ASEP, we find that the probabilistic and algebraic generator matrices are not the same and thus represent different processes. We conclude that the relationship between stochastic fusion and a ground state transformation (specifically, what we term the Type A ground state transformation) established by Kuan (2019) does not generalize to all finite-dimensional simple Lie algebras.

To approach this problem, we apply observations about crystal bases to probability. The Type A and Type D crystal graphs differ in that the Type A crystal graph is simply connected, while the Type D crystal graph is not. This suggests that the algebraic constructions of normal ASEP and Type D ASEP will differ, which leads us to believe that the equivalence between the algebraic and probabilistic constructions of normal ASEP will not extend to the Type D ASEP. Of course, proving this fact rigorously takes more work, and is the content of this paper. To our knowledge, this is the first use of crystal bases in a stochastic process.

1.1.1
Type D ASEP

1.1.2
Stochastic fusion

Taking a lattice with an even number of sites, we define stochastic fusion as the merging of each set of two consecutive lattice sites such that the particles in each original site of the set are stacked at the new site. Note that two particles of the same class can exist at one new site, and that the total number of sites involved in the process is decreased by one half through stochastic fusion. See
[
16
]
for a thorough treatment of stochastic fusion.
We proceed to define notation for the fused Type D ASEP, using

32
to denote one particle of class 1 and two particles of class 2. We will use angle brackets to denote
\gamma

2.1
Producing the stochastic fusion generator

We begin by defining the generator for stochastic fusion on
K

\Gamma
-lattice sites for the Type D ASEP, focusing on a general simplification in the construction process. We will assume that
K
is an even positive integer.

In the spirit of
[
16
, Section 3]
, we define the transition matrix of the stochastically fused Type D ASEP as

Q^{(K)}_{t}\coloneqq\Lambda^{(K)}P^{(K)}_{t}\Phi^{(K)}
.
Taking the derivative of both sides at time
t=0
, we express the same equation in terms of generators

L^{(K)}_{Q}=\Lambda^{(K)}L^{(K)}_{p}\Phi^{(K)}
.

Now, we will construct
\Lambda^{(2)}
, the matrix representing fission on one
\gamma
-lattice site, and
\Phi^{(2)}
, the matrix representing fusion on two
\Gamma
-lattice sites. We will use these two matrices to construct general
\Lambda^{(K)}
and
\Phi^{(K)}
, which appear in the formula for
L_{Q}^{(K)}
.

To proceed, we must introduce some new notation. Any particle configuration on two lattice sites can be represented as a function
\eta\colon\{1,2\}\rightarrow\{0,1,2,3\}
, which maps the index of a lattice site to the particle configuration on that lattice site. Furthermore, we can let
A_{1}(\eta)\subset\{1,2\}
denote the set of lattice sites that class 1 particles occupy and define
A_{2}(\eta)
similarly. Now we can state the function from
[
20

G^{2}(\eta):=\prod_{x\in A_{1}(\eta)}q^{-2x}\prod_{x\in A_{2}(\eta)}q^{-2x}.

For simplicity, we will represent the function
\eta
as an ordered pair
(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
, where
\eta_{1}:=\eta(1)
and
{\eta_{2}:=\eta(2)}
. For example, one can verify that

G^{2}((2,1))=\prod_{x\in\{2\}}q^{-2x}\prod_{x\in\{1\}}q^{-2x}=q^{-6}.

In fact,
G^{2}
is a reversible measure on particle configurations on pairs of
\Gamma
-lattice sites, which implies that the Type D ASEP does not prefer class 1 or class 2 particles
[
20
]
.

In Section
1.1.2

Definition 2.16

\displaystyle G^{-1}(\pi_{W\otimes W}(C))G-a\,\mathrm{Id}=aL,\qquad G^{-1}(\pi_{W\otimes W}(C))G=aL+a\,\mathrm{Id}

The construction of the process of stochastically fused Type D ASEP on two

3.1
Properties of the generator

We show in the following lemma that the generator matrix

can be expressed as a permutation of

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists a permutation matrix

L_{m}=J\bigl(\mathrm{Id}_{16\times 16}\otimes L_{p}^{(2)}\bigr)J^{-1}

Proof.

For a short proof by existence, consider the diagonal ordering of the possible states of four
\Gamma
-lattice sites as listed in Appendix
A.3
, and rearrange

according to these states to get
\textrm{Id}_{16\times 16}\otimes L_{p}^{(2)}
.

For a more intuitive proof, note that any state in the Type D ASEP on four
\Gamma
-lattice sites can be represented as
(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})
where
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\in\{0,1,2,3\}
. Letting only the middle
\Gamma
-sites interact corresponds to
x_{1}
and
x_{4}
being held constant, while the interaction of
x_{2}
and
x_{3}
is governed by
L_{p}^{(2)}
. Now consider the bijective function
h\colon(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4})\mapsto((x_{1},x_{4}),(x_{2},x_{3}))
. Since
(x_{1},x_{4})
is held constant while
(x_{2},x_{3})
interact, it follows that

can be permuted into
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{16}L_{p}^{(2)}
, since there are 16 possibilities for

. Finally, it follows that
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{16}L_{p}^{(2)}=\textrm{Id}_{16\times 16}\otimes L_{p}^{(2)}

can be permuted into

3.2
Taking drift speed to infinity

In the following theorem, we examine the limiting behavior of stochastically fused Type D ASEP with respect to drift speed. Recall that
n
affects drift speed, with larger values of

causing faster drift in the Type D ASEP.

Theorem 3.2
.

The following are finite:
\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigl(q^{-2n}L_{Q}\bigr)
for
q>1
, and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigl(q^{2n}L_{Q}\bigr)
for
0<q<1
.

The theorem above makes the intuitive statement that, as the drift speed

of the Type D ASEP on four
\Gamma
-lattice sites increases, so does the drift speed of the stochastically fused process on two
\gamma
-sites. Now we list two lemmas necessary to prove the theorem.

First, we prove that the limit of drift speed commutes with the products that produce
L_{Q}
. In particular, the limit of
L_{Q}
depends only on that of
L_{m}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

The limit as
n\to\infty
is commutative on
L_{Q}
. Precisely,

\lim_{n\to\infty}L_{Q}=\lim_{n\to\infty}(\Lambda L_{m}\Phi)=\Lambda(\lim_{n\to\infty}L_{m})\Phi.

Proof.

We remind the reader that
\Lambda
and
\Phi
do not depend on
n
. Therefore,

\lim_{n\to\infty}L_{Q}=\lim_{n\to\infty}(\Lambda L_{m}\Phi)=\Lambda(\lim_{n\to\infty}L_{m})\Phi.

Now we must show that the limit of
L_{m}
is finite.

Lemma 3.4
.

The following are finite:
\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigl(q^{-2n}L_{m}\bigr)
for
q>1
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigl(q^{2n}L_{m}\bigr)
for
0<q<1
.

Proof.

Take

. Using the notation of Section
1.1.1
, we have that

q^{-2n}L_{1}=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}*&\frac{(-q^{1-2n}+q^{-1})^{2}}{q^{2}}&-q^{-4}+q^{-2}+\frac{2}{q^{2+2n}}&-q^{-4}+q^{-2}+\frac{2}{q^{2+2n}}\\
1-q^{-2}+2q^{-2n}&*&-q^{2-4n}+2q^{-2n}+q^{-4n}&\left(-q^{1-2n}+q^{-1}\right)^{2}\\
q^{2}\left(-q^{1-2n}+q^{-1}\right)^{2}&2q^{2-2n}+q^{2-4n}-q^{4-4n}&*&2q^{2-2n}+q^{2-4n}-q^{4-4n}\\
1-q^{-2}+2q^{-2n}&\left(-q^{1-2n}+q^{-1}\right)^{2}&-q^{2-4n}+2q^{-2n}+q^{-4n}&*\end{smallmatrix}\right],

so

\begin{aligned} \lim_{n\to\infty}q^{-2n}L_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}*&q^{-4}&-q^{-4}+q^{-2}&-q^{-4}+q^{-2}\\
1-q^{-2}&*&0&q^{-2}\\
1&0&*&0\\
1-q^{-2}&q^{-2}&0&*\end{bmatrix}\end{aligned}.

Since the rows of generator matrices sum to zero,

in row

is the negation of the sum of the rest of the elements in row

, so it is finite. This is true for all rows
i
of
L_{1}
.

Similarly,

\begin{aligned} q^{-2n}L_{2}&=\begin{bmatrix}*&\frac{q^{1-4n}+q^{-1}}{q}\\
q\left(q^{1-4n}+q^{-1}\right)&*\end{bmatrix}\end{aligned},

so
\lim_{n\to\infty}q^{-2n}L_{2}=\big[\begin{smallmatrix}*&q^{-2}\\
1&*\end{smallmatrix}\big]

and each
*
is finite since the rows of a generator matrix sum to zero.

Now by Section
1.1.1
, we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}q^{-2n}L_{p}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}q^{-2n}\left(L_{1}\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{4}L_{2}\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{4}[0]\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(q^{-2n}L_{1}\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{4}q^{-2n}L_{2}\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{4}[0]\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigl(q^{-2n}L_{1}\bigr)\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{4}\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigl(q^{-2n}L_{2}\bigr)\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{4}[0],

which is finite by the computations above. A similar argument shows that
\lim_{n\to\infty}q^{2n}L^{(2)}_{p}
is finite for
0<q<1
.

Finally, using Lemma
3.1
, we obtain

q^{\pm 2n}L_{m}=q^{\pm 2n}J\bigl(\textrm{Id}_{16\times 16}\otimes L^{(2)}_{p}\bigr)J^{-1}=J\bigl(\textrm{Id}_{16\times 16}\otimes\bigl(q^{\pm 2n}L^{(2)}_{p}\bigr)\bigr)J^{-1}

for respective boundaries on
q
, by the properties of the Kronecker product and standard matrix multiplication. Noting that
J
and
J^{-1}
do not depend on
n
, we take the limit as
n\to\infty
of both sides and see that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigl(q^{\pm 2n}L_{m}\bigr)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigl(J\bigl(\textrm{Id}_{16\times 16}\otimes\bigl(q^{\pm 2n}L^{(2)}_{p}\bigr)\bigr)J^{-1}\bigr)=J\bigl(\textrm{Id}_{16\times 16}\otimes\bigl(\lim_{n\to\infty}q^{\pm 2n}L^{(2)}_{p}\bigr)\bigr)J^{-1},

where the right (and therefore left) side of the equation is finite since
\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigl(q^{\pm 2n}L^{(2)}_{p}\bigr)
is finite for respective boundaries on
q
. This implies that the rate matrix grows on the order of
q^{2n}
irrespective of drift direction.
∎

Now we can prove the primary theorem of this section.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Note that Lemma
3.4
suggests that multiplying
q^{\pm 2n}L_{m}
(for respective boundaries on
q
) by any constant preserves the finite limit, since the constant can be moved outside the limit. Note that

q^{\pm 2n}L_{Q}=q^{\pm 2n}\Lambda L_{m}\Phi=\Lambda q^{\pm 2n}L_{m}\Phi

by the commutativity of scalar multiplication. Taking the limit of both sides of the equation, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigl(q^{\pm 2n}L_{Q}\bigr)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigl(\Lambda q^{\pm 2n}L_{m}\Phi\bigr)=\Lambda\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigl(q^{\pm 2n}L_{m}\bigr)\Phi

by Lemma
3.3
. We know that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\bigl(q^{\pm 2n}\!L_{m}\bigr)
is finite for respective values of
q
, so
\lim_{n\to\infty}\!q^{\pm 2n}\allowbreak\times L_{Q}
must also be finite for such values of
q
.
∎

3.3
Spectral gaps

Recall that the spectral gap of a Markov process is the absolute value of the second-largest (i.e., least negative) eigenvalue of the generator matrix, where all eigenvalues of generator matrices are less than or equal to zero. From our calculations, the spectral gap corresponding to the generator
\mathcal{L}_{2}
simplifies to a reasonable expression, which we state below. However, the spectral gaps for
\mathcal{L}_{3}
,
\mathcal{L}_{4}
,
\mathcal{L}_{6}
, and
\mathcal{L}_{9}
do not simplify to expressions of a reasonable length.

Proposition 3.5
.

The spectral gap for the
\mathcal{L}_{2}
communicating class, denoted as
\lvert\lambda_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}\rvert
is

\lvert\lambda_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}\rvert=\frac{\bigl(q^{4n-2}+1\bigr)\bigl(q^{4}+1\bigr)}{q^{2n}\bigl(q^{2}+1\bigr)}.

Proof.

We use the code in Appendix
A
to directly calculate the eigenvalues for
\mathcal{L}_{2}
. We then take the absolute value of the second largest eigenvalue of each generator to obtain the spectral gap for
\mathcal{L}_{2}
.
∎

For
q>1
and
0<q<1
, notice that the spectral gap
|\lambda_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}|
goes to infinity as
n
goes to infinity, meaning the relaxation time
1/\lambda_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}
goes to zero. This implies that as the drift speed of the Type D ASEP goes to infinity, the time to convergence to the stationary distribution approaches zero for the 2-state communicating classes. The authors suspect the same is true for the 3, 4, 6, and 9-state communicating classes.

Also observe that the eigenvalues of
q^{\pm 2n}\mathcal{L}_{2}
are those of
\mathcal{L}_{2}
multiplied by
q^{\pm 2n}
. Thus, the limit as
n
goes to infinity of the spectral gap of
q^{-2n}\mathcal{L}_{2}
for
q>1
is 1, so its relaxation time is 1. Moreover, the limit as
n
goes to infinity of the spectral gap of
q^{2n}\mathcal{L}_{2}
for
0<q<1
is
{\bigl(q^{4}+1\bigr)/\bigl(q^{2}+1\bigr)}
, so its relaxation time is
\bigl(q^{2}+1\bigr)/\bigl(q^{4}+1\bigr)
. These results suggest that the time to convergence to the stationary distribution increases (relative to the time to convergence in the previous paragraph) as
n
goes to infinity for the 2-state communicating class. The authors suspect the same is true for the 3, 4, 6, and 9-state communicating classes.

Remark 3.6
.

As
n
goes to infinity, some of the jump rates in the (time-rescaled) fused Type D ASEP converge to
0
, which can be seen by taking the limit as
n
goes to infinity of entries in the fusion generator. Thus, as
n
approaches infinity, the fused Type D ASEP (where
\delta=0
and time is rescaled by a factor of
q^{\pm 2n}
for respective values of
q
) degenerates to the usual ASEP.

3.4
Reversible measures

First, notice that in our particle system the non-normalized versions of the stationary distributions are reversible measures for the communicating classes. This is intuitive since the fused Type D ASEP is positive recurrent and irreducible on its communicating classes, so the stationary distributions for the generators on its communicating classes are left eigenvectors of those generators. As the left eigenvectors corresponding to eigenvalue 0 are nonzero across all communicating classes, they are candidates for reversible measures. One can verify that these eigenvectors, evaluated at
n=2
, are reversible measures for arbitrary
n
.

Now we state the reversible measures explicitly, using the communicating classes listed in Appendix
A.2
and
q
-deformed integer notation
[n]_{q}=\frac{q^{n}-q^{-n}}{q-q^{-1}}
.

Lemma 3.7
.

The following is true for all
q>0
, where
q\neq 1
.
For the
9
-state communicating class, a reversible measure is

q^{8}[2]^{4}_{q}\left[1,\frac{q^{-8}}{[2]^{4}_{q}},\frac{q^{8}}{[2]^{4}_{q}},\frac{q^{-4}}{[2]^{2}_{q}},\frac{q^{4}}{[2]^{2}_{q}},\frac{q^{-4}}{[2]^{2}_{q}},\frac{q^{4}}{[2]^{2}_{q}},\frac{1}{[2]^{4}_{q}},\frac{1}{[2]^{4}_{q}}\right].

For the
6
-state communicating class, a reversible measure is

q^{6}[2]^{2}_{q}\left[q^{-2},q^{2},\frac{q^{-6}}{[2]^{2}_{q}},\frac{q^{6}}{[2]^{2}_{q}},\frac{q^{-2}}{[2]^{2}_{q}},\frac{q^{2}}{[2]^{2}_{q}}\right].

For the
4
-state communicating class, a reversible measure is
q^{4}\left[q^{4},1,1,q^{-4}\right]
.

For the

-state communicating class, a reversible measure is

For the

Now we generalize our reversible measures to arbitrary system sizes. In the following proposition,
\langle a_{i},a_{j}\rangle
denotes a particle configuration on two
\gamma
-lattice sites, where

Proposition 3.8
.

There exists a reversible measure
\pi^{(N)}
for the generator

on

\gamma

Proof.

Notice that the interacting particle system does not differentiate amongst the two classes of particles, so we can use occupation variables to write a particle configuration

\gamma

By casework, one sees that

3.5
Markov duality

Definition 3.9
.

Let
X_{t}
be a time-homogeneous Markov process on a discrete state space
\mathcal{X}
. Label the generator matrix of the Markov process as
L_{X}
. Let
D
be a matrix with rows and columns indexed by
\mathcal{X}
. Then
X_{t}
is self-dual with respect to the matrix
D
if
L_{X}D=DL_{X}^{\mathsf{T}}

[
8
]
.

The first possible approach to duality would be through
q
-Krawtchouk polynomials. In
[
4
, Theorem 3.1]
, the authors propose a self-duality function for the non-fused Type D ASEP with parameters
(q,n=2,\delta=0)
and
(q,n=3,\delta=0)
. Using the parameters
L=2
and
n=2
, we find a potential self-duality function (given by
[
4
, Theorem 3.1]
) for this two-site Type D ASEP, where
D
is the matrix of this proposed self-duality function. However, using the matrix
D
that we computed,
L_{Q}^{D}D[1,2]\neq D\bigl(L_{Q}^{D}\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}[1,2]
, so
L_{Q}^{D}D\neq D\bigl(L_{Q}^{D}\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}
and thus
D
is not the matrix of the self-duality function of the Type D ASEP with two
\gamma
-lattice sites. In our case (unlike in
[
4
]
),
constructing a particle system via probabilistic and algebraic processes results in different duality functions, so we need to consider different methods of constructing duals.

We were able to construct a nontrivial dual for
L_{Q}^{D}
when
n=2
using the eigenvectors of the operator and a set of (arbitrary) nonzero complex weights
\alpha_{i}
. We let
n=2
because the eigenvalues of
L_{Q}^{D}
(which are the eigenvalues of the block generators
\mathcal{L}_{i}
, where
i
ranges over communicating classes) simplify nicely in this case. Furthermore, we extend this dual to one on
L_{Q}
using a symmetry
S
that commutes with
L_{p}
and works nicely with fission and fusion.

Recall the fact that the generator of a reversible Markov process is diagonalizable. Now let
\mathcal{A}_{i}
be the diagonal matrix of the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}_{i}
such that
\mathcal{L}_{i}=\mathcal{P}_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}\mathcal{P}^{-1}_{i}
. Define
\alpha\coloneqq\{\alpha_{i}|\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}\}
. Also define

\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}:=\bigoplus_{i}\alpha_{i}\mathcal{P}_{i},\qquad\mathcal{A}:=\bigoplus_{i}\mathcal{A}_{i}.

Observe that, by definition,
L_{Q}^{D}
equals
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{-1}\oplus\mathcal{Z}
, where
\mathcal{Z}:=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{4}[0]
.

We can now relate
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}
to our operator defining Markov self-duality.

Theorem 3.10
.

The operator
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}:=\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\oplus\mathcal{Z}
defines a nontrivial dual for
L_{Q}^{D}
, in the sense that
L_{Q}^{D}\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}\bigl(L_{Q}^{D}\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}
.

Proof.

This is true because

\displaystyle L_{Q}^{D}\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{-1}\oplus\mathcal{Z}\bigr)\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\oplus\mathcal{Z}\bigr)=\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)\oplus\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\oplus\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)^{-1}\bigr)\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\oplus\mathcal{Z}=\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)\bigl(\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{-1}\bigr)^{T}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)\oplus\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{-1}\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}\oplus\mathcal{Z}=\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\oplus\mathcal{Z}\bigr)\bigl(\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{-1}\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}\oplus\mathcal{Z}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}\oplus\mathcal{Z}\bigr)\bigl(\bigl(\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\mathcal{A}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{-1}\bigr)\oplus\mathcal{Z}\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}\bigl(L_{Q}^{D}\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}.

∎

Note that since
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}
is a block diagonal matrix, so is
\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}\mathcal{P}_{\alpha}^{\mathsf{T}}
, which implies that
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}
is also block diagonal.

Now we can extend the results above to provide a duality function for
L_{Q}
when
n=2
.

Lemma 3.11
.

If
C
is the permutation matrix described in Proposition
2.7
, then
C\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}C^{\mathsf{T}}
is a dual for
L_{Q}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.7
, we have that
C^{-1}L_{Q}C=L_{Q}^{D}
. By the definition of duality and by substitution,

C^{-1}L_{Q}C\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}\bigl(C^{-1}L_{Q}C\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}}
.
The right side of the equality is equivalent to
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}C^{\mathsf{T}}L_{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}\bigl(C^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)^{-1}
, so we can multiply both sides of the equality by
C
and
C^{\mathsf{T}}
to obtain
L_{Q}C\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}C^{\mathsf{T}}=C\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}C^{\mathsf{T}}L_{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Now we use symmetry and the result above to describe additional duals for
L_{Q}
.

Lemma 3.12
.

If the matrix
S_{Q}
satisfies
[S_{Q},L_{Q}]=0
, then
S_{Q}\bigl(C\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}C^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)
is a dual for
L_{Q}
.

Proof.

We have by assumption that
S_{Q}L_{Q}=L_{Q}S_{Q}
. Since
L_{Q}\bigl(C\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}C^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)=\bigl(C\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}C^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)L_{Q}^{\mathsf{T}}
by Lemma
3.11
, we have by substitution that

L_{Q}\bigl(S_{Q}C\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}C^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)=S_{Q}L_{Q}\bigl(C\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}C^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)=\bigl(S_{Q}C\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}C^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)L_{Q}^{\mathsf{T}},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Finally, we describe a sufficient condition for a dual of
L_{Q}
.

Theorem 3.13
.

If the matrix
S
satisfies
SL_{p}=L_{p}S
,
\Phi\Lambda S\Phi=S\Phi
, and
\Lambda S\Phi\Lambda=\Lambda S
, then
(\Lambda S\Phi)\bigl(C\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}C^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)
is a dual for
L_{Q}
.

Proof.

Define
S_{Q}:=\Lambda S\Phi
. We want to show that
[S_{Q},L_{Q}]=0
.

It follows from our assumptions and the definitions of
L_{Q}
and
S_{Q}
that

L_{Q}S_{Q}=(\Lambda L_{p}\Phi)(\Lambda S\Phi)=\Lambda L_{p}S\Phi

and

S_{Q}L_{Q}=(\Lambda S\Phi)(\Lambda L_{p}\Phi)=\Lambda SL_{p}\Phi=\Lambda L_{p}S\Phi

so
[S_{Q},L_{Q}]=0
.

Now Lemma
3.12
implies that
S_{Q}\bigl(C\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}C^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)=(\Lambda S\Phi)\bigl(C\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}C^{\mathsf{T}}\bigr)
is a dual for
L_{Q}
.
∎

Next, we explain (intuitively, in words) why there are natural nontrivial examples of

that can satisfy the conditions of Theorem

. In the context of the Type D ASEP, initial conditions of the form

are called

for more details. Certain symmetries

More explicitly, we can leverage previous methods to find such an

Proposition 3.14

Given a particle configuration

From here, the dual function in Theorem

A.7
Type A ground state transformation

The following notes were provided to us by Dr. Jeffrey Kuan to aid in our calculation of the ground state transformation.

Suppose
C
is a central element and
H=(\pi_{V}\otimes\pi_{V})(\Delta(C))
. Let
v\in V
be a highest-weight vector of a highest-weight representation
V
. Then,

H(v\otimes v)=av\otimes v

for some constant
a
. By the definition of a central element,

\Delta\bigl(F_{j}^{k}\bigr)C=C\Delta\bigl(F_{j}^{k}\bigr)
.
Given unit basis vectors
u_{i}\otimes u_{j}\in V\otimes V
, let
(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{3}
such that

\bigl\langle u_{i}\otimes u_{j}|\Delta\bigl(F_{3}^{k_{3}}\bigr)\cdot\Delta\bigl(F_{2}^{k_{2}}\bigr)\cdot\Delta\bigl(F_{1}^{k_{1}}\bigr)|v\otimes v\bigr\rangle\neq 0.

F_{3}
,
F_{2}
,
F_{1}
may need to be in a different order, but the values of
k
=(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3})
should be the same.

Set
G\colon V\otimes V\to V\otimes V
by

G(u_{i}\otimes u_{j})=\bigl\langle u_{i}\otimes u_{j}|\Delta\bigl(F_{3}^{k_{3}}\bigr)\cdot\Delta\bigl(F_{2}^{k_{2}}\bigr)\cdot\Delta\bigl(F_{1}^{k_{1}}\bigr)|v\otimes v\bigr\rangle u_{i}\otimes u_{j},

where
k
is the above term.

Now, set
S=G^{-1}HG
.

Theorem A.4
.

a^{-1}S-\mathrm{Id}
has rows that sum to
0
.

Proof.

For fixed
u_{i}\otimes u_{j}
, we have that the sum of the row indexed by
u_{i}\otimes u_{j}
equals

\sum_{w_{1},w_{2}}\frac{\langle u_{i}\otimes u_{j}|H|w_{1}\otimes w_{2}\rangle\langle w_{1}\otimes w_{2}|G|w_{1}\otimes w_{2}\rangle}{\langle u_{i}\otimes u_{j}|G|u_{i}\otimes u_{j}\rangle},

which then equals

\sum_{w_{1},w_{2}}\left[\frac{\langle u_{i}\otimes u_{j}|H|w_{1}\otimes w_{2}\rangle}{\langle u_{i}\otimes u_{j}|G|u_{i}\otimes u_{j}\rangle}\sum_{\text{{k}}}\langle w_{1}\otimes w_{2}|\Delta\bigl(F_{3}^{k_{3}}\bigr)\cdot\Delta\bigl(F_{2}^{k_{2}}\bigr)\cdot\Delta\bigl(F_{1}^{k_{1}}\bigr)|v\otimes v\rangle\right].

By commutation between the coproduct and
H
, this must then equal

\sum_{\text{{k}}}\frac{\bigl\langle u_{i}\otimes u_{j}|\Delta\bigl(F_{3}^{k_{3}}\bigr)\cdot\Delta\bigl(F_{2}^{k_{2}}\bigr)\cdot\Delta\bigl(F_{1}^{k_{1}}\bigr)H|v\otimes v\bigr\rangle}{\langle u_{i}\otimes u_{j}|G|u_{i}\otimes u_{j}\rangle}.

Finally, this equals

\sum_{\text{{k}}}\frac{a\bigl\langle u_{i}\otimes u_{j}|\Delta\bigl(F_{3}^{k_{3}}\bigr)\cdot\Delta\bigl(F_{2}^{k_{2}}\bigr)\cdot\Delta\bigl(F_{1}^{k_{1}}\bigr)|v\otimes v\bigr\rangle}{\langle u_{i}\otimes u_{j}|G|u_{i}\otimes u_{j}\rangle}=a.

Multiplying by
a^{-1}
and subtracting the identity, we obtain a matrix whose rows sum to
0
.
∎
