Self-adjoint Jacobi operators in the limit circle case Thanks: Supported by project Russian Science Foundation 17-11-01126
D. R. Yafaev

Abstract
. We consider symmetric Jacobi operators with recurrence coefficients such that the corresponding difference equation is in the limit circle case. Equivalently, this means that the associated moment problem is indeterminate. Our main goal is to find a representation for the resolvents of self-adjoint realizations J J of such Jacobi operators. This representation implies the classical Nevanlinna formula for the Cauchy-Stieltjes transforms of the spectral measures of the operators J J . We also efficiently describe domains of the operators J J in terms of boundary conditions at infinity.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Basic definitions

Given two sequences
a_{n}>0
and
b_{n}=\bar{b}_{n}
where
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}=\{0,1,\ldots\}
,
a
Jacobi matrix is defined by an equality

{\mathcal{J}}=\begin{pmatrix}b_{0}&a_{0}&0&0&0&\cdots\\
a_{0}&b_{1}&a_{1}&0&0&\cdots\\
0&a_{1}&b_{2}&a_{2}&0&\cdots\\
0&0&a_{2}&b_{3}&a_{3}&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}.

(1.1)

Let
u=(u_{0},u_{1},\ldots)^{\top}=:(u_{n})
be a column.
Then

({\mathcal{J}}u)_{0}=b_{0}u_{0}+a_{0}u_{1}\quad\mbox{and}\quad({\mathcal{J}}u)_{n}=a_{n-1}u_{n-1}+b_{n}u_{n}+a_{n}u_{n+1}\quad\mbox{for}\quad n\geq 1.

(1.2)

Let us consider a second-order difference equation

a_{n-1}u_{n-1}+b_{n}u_{n}+a_{n}u_{n+1}=zu_{n},\quad n\geq 1,

(1.3)

associated with the operator
\mathcal{J}
. Clearly, values
u_{0}
and
u_{1}
determine uniquely a solution
u_{n}
of equation (
1.3
). In particular,
the solutions
p_{n}(z)
and
q_{n}(z)
are distinguished by boundary conditions

p_{0}(z)=1,\quad q_{0}(z)=0,

(1.4)

and

p_{1}(z)=(z-b_{0})/a_{0},\quad q_{1}(z)=1/a_{0}.

(1.5)

It is easy to see that
p_{n}(z)
is a polynomial of degree
n
and
q_{n}(z)
is a polynomial of degree
n-1
. Let us set
p(z)=(p_{0}(z),p_{1}(z),\ldots)^{\top}
,
q(z)=(q_{0}(z),q_{1}(z),\ldots)^{\top}

and
e_{0}=(1,0,\ldots)^{\top}
. Then

{\mathcal{J}}p(z)=zp(z)\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\mathcal{J}}q(z)=zq(z)+e_{0}.

(1.6)

Let
u=(u_{n})
and
v=(v_{n})
be two solutions of equation (
1.3
). A direct calculation shows that their Wronskian

\{u,v\}:=a_{n}(u_{n}v_{n+1}-u_{n+1}v_{n})

(1.7)

does not depend on
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
. Clearly, the Wronskian
\{u,v\}=0
if and only if the solutions
u
and
v
are proportional. Note that
\{p,q\}=1
so that

p_{n}(z)q_{n+1}(z)-p_{n+1}(z)q_{n}(z)=a_{n}^{-1},\quad\forall n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}.

(1.8)

We consider Jacobi operators
J
acting in
the space
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
with the scalar product
\langle u,v\rangle=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}u_{n}\bar{v}_{n}
.
Let
\mathcal{D}
be a dense in
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
set of vectors
u
with only a finite number of non-zero components
u_{n}
.
The minimal Jacobi operator
J_{\rm min}
is defined by the equality
J_{\rm min}u={\mathcal{J}}u
on domain

{\mathcal{D}}(J_{\rm min})=\mathcal{D}
.
It extends to a bounded operator defined on the whole space
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})

if and only if the corresponding recurrence coefficients
(a_{n})\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
and
(b_{n})\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
. In the general case, we introduce also
the maximal operator
J_{\rm max}
by the same formula
J_{\rm max}u={\mathcal{J}}u
on a set
{\mathcal{D}}(J_{\rm max})
of all vectors
u\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
such that
{\mathcal{J}}u\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
.
The operator
J_{\rm min}
is
symmetric in the space
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
, and its adjoint operator
J^{*}_{\rm min}=J_{\rm max}
.

The operator
J_{\rm max}
is not of course symmetric.
For all
u,v\in{\mathcal{D}}(J_{\rm max})
, we have an identity (the Green formula)

\langle{\mathcal{J}}u,v\rangle-\langle u,{\mathcal{J}}v\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}(u_{n+1}\bar{v}_{n}-u_{n}\bar{v}_{n+1})

(1.9)

where the limit in the right-hand side exists.
Indeed, a direct calculation shows that

\sum_{m=0}^{n}({\mathcal{J}}u)_{m}\bar{v}_{m}-\sum_{m=0}^{n}u_{m}({\mathcal{J}}\bar{v})_{m}=a_{n}(u_{n+1}\bar{v}_{n}-u_{n}\bar{v}_{n+1}).

Passing here to the limit
n\to\infty
and using that
{\mathcal{J}}u\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
for
u\in{\mathcal{D}}(J_{\rm max})
, we obtain (
1.9
).

The comprehensive presentation of the theory of self-adjoint Jacobi operators can be found in the books
[
1
]
(Chapters I and II),
[
2
]
(Chapter VII),
[
5
]
(Part I) and the survey
[
6
]
.

1.2.
Determinacy versus indeterminacy

Since the operator
J_{\rm min}
commutes with the complex conjugation, its deficiency indices

d_{\pm}:=\dim\ker(J_{\rm max}-zI),\quad\pm\operatorname{Im}z>0,

are equal, i.e.
d_{+}=d_{-}=:d
, and, so,
J_{\rm min}
admits self-adjoint extensions. Here and below
I
is the identity operator. For an arbitrary
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
let us consider the equation
{\mathcal{J}}u=zu
. By definition (
1.2
), its solutions are given by the formula
u=u_{0}p(z)
. They belong to
{\mathcal{D}}(J_{\rm max})
if and only if
p(z)\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
. Therefore
d=0
if
p(z)\not\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
for
\operatorname{Im}z\neq 0
; otherwise
d=1
.

This dichotomy can also be stated in terms of the Weyl limit point/circle theory and in terms of the determinacy/indeterminacy of the associated moment problem. Recall that,
similarly to differential equations, difference equation (
1.3
) always has a non-trivial solution in
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
for
\operatorname{Im}z\neq 0
. This solution is either unique (up to a constant factor) or all solutions of (
1.3
) belong to
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
.
The first instance is known as the limit point (LP) case and the second one – as the limit circle (LC) case. In the LP case
p(z)\not\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
and in the LC case
p(z)\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
for
\operatorname{Im}z\neq 0
. Thus, the operator
J_{\rm min}
is essentially self-adjoint if and only if the LP case occurs.

As is well known (see, for example, Theorem 6.16 in
[
5
]
), in the LC case
inclusions

p(z)\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})\quad\mbox{and}\quad q(z)\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})

(1.10)

hold true for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
. It now follows from (
1.8
) that in the LC case necessarily

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}^{-1}<\infty.

(1.11)

Equivalently, this fact can be stated
as a sufficient condition (known as the Carleman condition)

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}^{-1}=\infty

(1.12)

for the essential self-adjointness of the operator
J_{\rm min}
.

In general, the essential self-adjointness of operators
J_{\rm min}
is determined by both sets of coefficients
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
. This is briefly discussed in Sect. 3. Here we only note that, even in the case
(b_{n})=0
,
condition (
1.12
) is not necessary for the essential self-adjointness of
J_{\rm min}
. However Theorem 1.5 in Chaper VII of the book
[
2
]
shows that this is true under an additional assumption

a_{n-1}a_{n+1}\leq a_{n}^{2}

(for large
n
). Without this assumption, the operators
J_{\rm min}
may be essentially self-adjoint even for the coefficients
(a_{n})
satisfying conditions (
1.11
) and
(b_{n})=0
.
(see
[
3
]
).

We also note a link with the Hamburger moment problem

s_{n}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\lambda^{n}d\rho(\lambda).

(1.13)

Recall that these equations for a nonnegative measure
d\rho(\lambda)
with infinite support have a solution if and only if the sequence
(s_{n})
is positive definite, i.e.,

\sum_{n,m\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}s_{n+m}\xi_{m}\bar{\xi_{n}}>0\quad\forall\xi=(\xi_{n})\in{\mathcal{D}},\;\xi\neq 0.

(1.14)

This problem is called determinate if the measure satisfying
(
1.13
) is unique. Otherwise it is called indeterminate.

For a Jacobi matrix (
1.1
), we set

s_{n}=({\mathcal{J}}^{n}e_{0},e_{0})
.
Then condition (
1.14
) is satisfied. If
J
is an arbitrary self-adjoint extension of the operator
J_{\rm min}
and
E_{J}(\lambda)
is its spectral family, then the measure
d\rho_{J}(\lambda)=d(E_{J}(\lambda)e_{0},e_{0})
satisfies equation (
1.13
).
It turns out (see Theorem 2 in
[
6
]
) that moment problem (
1.13
) is determinate if and only if the operator
J_{\rm min}
is essentially self-adjoint.

We also note that the essential self-adjointness of an operator
J_{\rm min}
is determined by a behavior of its coefficients
a_{n}
and
b_{n}
for large
n
only. Indeed, if
(\tilde{a}_{n}-a_{n})\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
and
(\tilde{b}_{n}-b_{n})\in\ell^{\infty}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
, then the operator
\widetilde{J}_{\rm min}-J_{\rm min}
is bounded so that the deficiency indices of the operators
\widetilde{J}_{\rm min}
and
J_{\rm min}
are the same.

1.3.
Self-adjoint extensions

Recall that

\clos J_{\min}=J_{\min}^{**}=J_{\max}^{*}.

In this paper we are interested
in the LC case where the minimal Jacobi operators
J_{\rm min}
are not essentially self-adjoint so that

\clos J_{\rm min}\neq J_{\rm max}=J_{\rm min}^{*}.

(1.15)

Let us set

{\sf D}:={\mathcal{D}}(\clos J_{\rm min}).

(1.16)

For a vector
h\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
, we denote by
\{h\}
the one dimensional subspace of
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
spanned by the vector
h
. The symbol
\dotplus
denotes the direct sum of subspaces. The following description (see Lemma 6.22 and Theorem 6.23 in
[
5
]
or Theorem 2.6 in
[
6
]
) of self-adjoint extensions of the operators
J_{\rm min}
is a modification of von Neumann formulas adapted to Jacobi operators.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let relation (
1.15
) be satisfied. Choose some
\zeta\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
Then

{\mathcal{D}}(J_{\rm max})={\sf D}\dotplus\{p(\zeta)\}\dotplus\{q(\zeta)\}.

(1.17)

All self-adjoint extensions
J_{t}
of the operator
J_{\rm min}
are parametrized by numbers
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
and

t=\infty
. The domains of the operators
J_{t}
are determined by the equalities

{\mathcal{D}}(J_{t})={\sf D}\dotplus\{tp(\zeta)+q(\zeta)\},\quad t\in{\mathbb{R}},

(1.18)

and

{\mathcal{D}}(J_{\infty})={\sf D}\dotplus\{p(\zeta)\}.

(1.19)

Below we use Proposition
1.1
for
\zeta=0
only.

Our main goal is to find an explicit formula for the resolvents
R_{t}(z)=(J_{t}-zI)^{-1}
of self-adjoint operators
J_{t}
. To that end, we define, for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, a bounded operator
{\mathcal{R}}(z)
in the space
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
by an equality

({\mathcal{R}}(z)h)_{n}=q_{n}(z)\sum_{m=0}^{n}p_{m}(z)h_{m}+p_{n}(z)\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}q_{m}(z)h_{m}.

(1.20)

Actually, the operator
{\mathcal{R}}(z)
belongs to the Hilbert-Schmidt class.
We prove (see Theorem
2.1
) that, in a natural sense,
{\mathcal{R}}(z)
can be considered as a quasiresolvent of the operator
J_{\rm max}
. A representation for the resolvents
R_{t}(z)
is obtained in Theorem
2.7
.

Another goal of the paper is to find an alternative description of self-adjoint extensions of the operator
J_{\rm min}
in terms of an asymptotic behavior of solutions
u_{n}
of the Jacobi equation (
1.3
) for
n\to\infty
. This is discussed in Section 3.

2.
Resolvents of self-adjoint Jacobi operators

In this section we only suppose that a minimal Jacobi operator
J_{\rm min}
is not essentially self-adjoint so that we are in the LC case.

2.1.
Quasiresolvent

Recall that in the LC case
inclusions (
1.10
) are satisfied for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Let us define an operator
{\mathcal{R}}(z)
playing the role of the resolvent of the operator
J_{\rm max}
by equality (
1.20
). In particular, it follows from (
1.4
), (
1.5
) that

({\mathcal{R}}(z)h)_{0}=\langle h,q(\bar{z})\rangle

(2.1)

and

({\mathcal{R}}(z)h)_{1}=h_{0}a_{0}^{-1}+\langle h,q(\bar{z})\rangle(z-b_{0})a_{0}^{-1}

(2.2)

for all
h\in{\ell}^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
.
In view of inclusions (
1.10
) the operators
{\mathcal{R}}(z)
are bounded in the space
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Note also that
{\mathcal{R}}(z)^{*}={\mathcal{R}}(\bar{z})
.

A proof of the following statement is close to the construction (see, e.g., Lemma 5.1 in
[
7
]
) of the resolvents for essentially self-adjoint Jacobi operators.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let relation (
1.15
) be satisfied.
For all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, we have

{\mathcal{R}}(z):\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})\to{\mathcal{D}}(J_{\rm max})

(2.3)

and

(J_{\rm max}-zI){\mathcal{R}}(z)=I.

(2.4)

Proof.

Recall that an operator
{\mathcal{J}}
was defined by equalities (
1.2
). We will check that

(({\mathcal{J}}-zI){\mathcal{R}}(z)h)_{n}=h_{n}

(2.5)

for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
and
h=\{h_{n}\}\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
. For
n=0
, we have

(({\mathcal{J}}-zI){\mathcal{R}}(z)h)_{0}=(b_{0}-z)({\mathcal{R}}(z)h)_{0}+a_{0}({\mathcal{R}}(z)h)_{1}=h_{0}

according to formulas (
2.1
) and (
2.2
). For
n\geq 1
, we rewrite definition (
1.20
) as

({\mathcal{R}}(z)h)_{n}=q_{n}(z)x_{n}(z)+p_{n}(z)y_{n}(z)

where

x_{n}(z)=\sum_{m=0}^{n}p_{m}(z)h_{m},\quad y_{n}(z)=\sum_{m=n+1}^{\infty}q_{m}(z)h_{m}.

(2.6)

It now follows from definition (
1.2
) that

(({\mathcal{J}}-zI){\mathcal{R}}(z)h)_{n}=a_{n-1}\big(q_{n-1}x_{n-1}+p_{n-1}y_{n-1}\big)\\
+(b_{n}-z)\big(q_{n}x_{n}+p_{n}y_{n}\big)+a_{n}\big(q_{n+1}x_{n+1}+p_{n+1}y_{n+1}\big),\quad n\geq 1.

(2.7)

According to (
2.6
) we have

q_{n-1}x_{n-1}+p_{n-1}y_{n-1}=q_{n-1}(x_{n}-p_{n}h_{n})+p_{n-1}(y_{n}+q_{n}h_{n})

and

q_{n+1}x_{n+1}+p_{n+1}y_{n+1}=q_{n+1}(x_{n}+p_{n+1}h_{n+1})\\
+p_{n+1}(y_{n}-q_{n+1}h_{n+1})=q_{n+1}x_{n}+p_{n+1}y_{n}.

Substituting these expressions into the right-hand side of (
2.7
), we see that

\big(({\mathcal{J}}-zI){\mathcal{R}}(z)h\big)_{n}=a_{n-1}\Big(q_{n-1}(x_{n}-p_{n}h_{n})+p_{n-1}(y_{n}+q_{n}h_{n})\Big)\\
+(b_{n}-z)\big(q_{n}x_{n}+p_{n}y_{n}\big)+a_{n}\big(q_{n+1}x_{n}+p_{n+1}y_{n}\big).

Let us now collect together all terms containing
x_{n}
,
y_{n}
and
h_{n}
. Then

\big(({\mathcal{J}}-zI){\mathcal{R}}(z)h\big)_{n}=\Big(a_{n-1}q_{n-1}+(b_{n}-z)q_{n}+a_{n}q_{n+1}\Big)x_{n}\\
+\Big(a_{n-1}p_{n-1}+(b_{n}-z)p_{n}+a_{n}p_{n+1}\Big)y_{n}+a_{n-1}\big(-p_{n}q_{n-1}+p_{n-1}q_{n}\big)h_{n}.

(2.8)

The coefficients at
x_{n}
and
y_{n}
equal zero by virtue of Jacobi equation (
1.3
) for
(q_{n})
and
(p_{n})
, respectively. Since
\{p,q\}=1
, the right-hand side of
(
2.8
) equals
h_{n}
.
This proves (
2.5
) whence
({\mathcal{J}}-zI){\mathcal{R}}(z)h=h
. In particular, we see that
{\mathcal{R}}(z)h\in{\mathcal{D}}(J_{\rm max})
so that
{\mathcal{J}}
can be replaced here by
J_{\rm max}
.
This yields both (
2.3
) and (
2.4
).
∎

Remark 2.2
.

In definition (
1.20
),
one can replace
q_{n}(z)
by the polynomials
\widetilde{q}_{n}(z)=q_{n}(z)+cp_{n}(z)
for an arbitrary
c\in{\mathbb{C}}
. Then
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}(z)={\mathcal{R}}(z)+c\langle\cdot,p(\bar{z})\rangle p(z)
and formulas (
2.3
), (
2.4
) remain true.

Since
p(z)
is a unique (up to a constant factor) solution of the homogeneous equation
(J_{\rm max}-zI)u=0
, we can also state

Corollary 2.3
.

All solutions of the equation

(J_{\rm max}-zI)u=h\quad\mbox{where}\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad h\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})

for
u\in{\mathcal{D}}(J_{\rm max})
are given by the formula

u=\Gamma p(z)+{\mathcal{R}}(z)h\quad\mbox{for some}\quad\Gamma=\Gamma(z;h)\in{\mathbb{C}}.

(2.9)

An asymptotic relation for
({\mathcal{R}}(z)h)_{n}
is a direct consequence of definition (
1.20
)
and condition (
1.10
):

({\mathcal{R}}(z)h)_{n}=q_{n}(z)\langle h,p(\bar{z})\rangle+o(|p_{n}(z)|+|q_{n}(z)|)\quad{\rm as}\quad n\to\infty.

(2.10)

This relation can be supplemented by the following result.

Proposition 2.4
.

For all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
and all
h\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
, we have

u:={\mathcal{R}}(z)h-q(z)\langle h,p(\bar{z})\rangle\in{\sf D}.

(2.11)

Proof.

Let first
h\in{\mathcal{D}}
. Then
({\mathcal{R}}(z)h)_{n}=q_{n}(z)\langle h,p(\bar{z})\rangle
for sufficiently large
n
so that
u\in{\mathcal{D}}\subset{\sf D}
.

Let now
h
be an arbitrary vector in
\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
. Observe that
u\in{\sf D}
if and only if there exists a sequence
u^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{D}}
such that

u^{(k)}\to u\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\mathcal{J}}u^{(k)}\to{\mathcal{J}}u\quad\mbox{as}\quad k\to\infty.

(2.12)

Let us take any sequence
h^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{D}}
such that
h^{(k)}\to h
and set

u^{(k)}={\mathcal{R}}(z)h^{(k)}-q(z)\langle h^{(k)},p(\bar{z})\rangle.

Then
u^{(k)}\in{\mathcal{D}}
and
u^{(k)}\to u
as
k\to\infty
because the operator
{\mathcal{R}}(z)
is bounded. It follows from equalities (
1.6
) and (
2.4
) that

({\mathcal{J}}-z)u^{(k)}=h^{(k)}-e_{0}\langle h^{(k)},p(\bar{z})\rangle\to h-e_{0}\langle h,p(\bar{z})\rangle=({\mathcal{J}}-z)u

as
k\to\infty
. This proves relations (
2.12
) whence
u\in{\sf D}
.
∎

In view of Proposition
2.4
representation (
2.9
) is consistent with formula (
1.17
).

2.2.
Main result

Let us first find a link between the polynomials
p(z)
,
q(z)
for an arbitrary
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
and the polynomials
p(0)
,
q(0)
. We always suppose that
condition (
1.15
) is satisfied which implies inclusions (
1.10
) for all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

For all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, we have

p(z)=\big(1-z\langle p(z),q(0)\rangle\big)p(0)+z{\mathcal{R}}(0)p(z)

(2.13)

and

q(z)=-z\langle q(z),q(0)\rangle p(0)+q(0)+z{\mathcal{R}}(0)q(z).

(2.14)

Proof.

To prove (
2.13
), we set

u=p(z)-z{\mathcal{R}}(0)p(z)

(2.15)

so that

u_{0}=1-z\langle p(z),q(0)\rangle

(2.16)

according to equality (
2.1
) (for
z=0
).
It follows from relation (
2.4
) that
{\mathcal{J}}u={\mathcal{J}}p(z)-zp(z)=0
whence
u=u_{0}p(0)
.
Now definition (
2.15
) implies that
p(z)=u_{0}p(0)+z{\mathcal{R}}(0)p(z)
. It remains to use (
2.16
).

The proof of (
2.14
) is quite similar. We now set

v=q(z)-q(0)-z{\mathcal{R}}(0)q(z)

(2.17)

so that
v_{0}=-z\langle q(z),q(0)\rangle
. It again follows from relations (
1.6
) and (
2.4
)
that
{\mathcal{J}}v={\mathcal{J}}q(z)-{\mathcal{J}}q(0)-zq(z)=0
whence
v=v_{0}p(0)=-z\langle q(z),q(0)\rangle p(0)
. Therefore
(
2.14
) is a direct consequence of definition (
2.17
).
∎

Putting together Lemma
2.5
with Proposition
2.4
(for
z=0
), we can also state the following result.

Lemma 2.6
.

For all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, we have

p(z)-\big(1-z\langle p(z),q(0)\rangle\big)p(0)-z\langle p(z),p(0)\rangle q(0)\in{\sf D}

(2.18)

and

q(z)+z\langle q(z),q(0)\rangle p(0)-\big(1+z\langle q(z),p(0)\rangle\big)q(0)\in{\sf D}.

(2.19)

Now it easy to construct resolvents of self-adjoint extensions
J_{t}
of the operators
J_{\rm min}
. Recall that domains of the operators
J_{t}
are defined by relations (
1.18
) and (
1.19
).

Theorem 2.7
.

Let assumption (
1.15
) hold.
For all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
\operatorname{Im}z\neq 0
and all
h\in\ell^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}_{+})
, the resolvent

3.
Asymptotic behavior of solutions of the Jacobi equation

In this section we consider a specific class of Jacobi operators
J_{\rm min}
that are not essentially essentially self-adjoint. We describe all their self-adjoint extensions
J
in terms of asymptotic behavior of solutions
u_{n}
of the Jacobi equation (
1.3
) for
n\to\infty
. Then we find a representation for the resolvents of the operators
J
in terms of asymptotic coefficients in formulas for
u_{n}
. Our construction here is independent of Proposition
1.1
(or the von Neumann formulas) and of the results of Sect. 2.2.

3.1.
Setting the problem

To motivate the construction below, let us compare descriptions of self-adjoint extensions of minimal symmetric operators for differential and Jacobi operators. Take, for example, an operator
H_{\rm min}=-d^{2}/dx^{2}
with domain
{\mathcal{D}}(H_{\rm min})=C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
in the space
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
. Of course, its adjoint operator
H_{\rm min}^{*}=H_{\rm max}
as well as self-adjoint extensions can be described by the von Neumann formulas. It is, however, essentially more convenient to define these operators in terms of boundary conditions at the point
x=0
. Indeed, the set
{\mathcal{D}}(H_{\rm max})
coincides with the Sobolev class
{\sf H}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
. Functions
f\in{\sf H}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
have boundary values
f(0)
and
f^{\prime}(0)
.
The domain
{\mathcal{D}}(\clos H_{\rm min})
consists of
f\in{\sf H}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
such that
f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=0
, and all self-adjoint extensions of
H_{\rm min}
are determined by boundary conditions

f^{\prime}(0)=hf(0)\;\mbox{where}\;h\in{\mathbb{R}}\quad\mbox{or}\quad f(0)=0.

(3.1)

We are looking for a similar description for self-adjoint extensions of the Jacobi operator
J_{\rm min}
.
The starting point of our construction is an asymptotic formula

u_{n}=a_{n}^{-1/2}\big(s_{+}e^{i\varphi_{n}}+s_{-}e^{-i\varphi_{n}}+o(1)\big),\quad n\to\infty,

(3.2)

for vectors
u\in\{u_{n}\}\in{\mathcal{D}}(J_{\rm max})
.
Here the phases
\varphi_{n}
are given by an explicit formula (see (
3.10
), below). The asymptotic coefficients
s_{+}=s_{+}(u)
and
s_{-}=s_{-}(u)
in (
3.2
) depend on
u
. They play the role of the boundary values
f(0)
and
f^{\prime}(0)
for functions
f\in{\sf H}^{2}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
, and we are looking for an analogue of the mapping

f\mapsto(f(0),f^{\prime}(0))

(3.3)

of the set
{\mathcal{D}}(H_{\rm max})
onto
{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
.

Our first goal is to describe all self-adjoint extensions of operators
J_{\rm min}
in terms of asymptotic coefficients
s_{\pm}
in formula (
3.2
). It turns out that such extensions
{\bf J}_{\omega}
can be parametrized by complex numbers
\omega\in{\mathbb{T}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
, and the domain
{\mathcal{D}}({\bf J}_{\omega})
of
{\bf J}_{\omega}
consists of elements
u\in{\mathcal{D}}(J_{\max})
satisfying the condition

s_{+}(u)=\omega s_{-}(u),\quad|\omega|=1.

(3.4)

This formula plays the role of (
3.1
).

Another goal of this section is to find an explicit formula for the resolvents
{\bf R}_{\omega}(z)=({\bf J}_{\omega}-zI)^{-1}
of the operators
{\bf J}_{\omega}
. Of course this formula has the same structure as (
2.20
),

{\bf R}_{\omega}(z)h=\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\omega}(z)\langle h,p(\bar{z})\rangle p(z)+{\mathcal{R}}(z)h,

(3.5)

but the coefficient

\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\omega}(z)=-\frac{s_{+}(q(z))-\omega s_{-}(q(z))}{s_{+}(p(z))-\omega s_{-}(p(z))}

(3.6)

is expressed in terms of the asymptotic coefficients in (
3.2
).

3.2.
Jost solutions

We here suppose that the coefficients

as

sufficiently rapidly so that condition (

From analytic point of view, we rely on the results of paper
