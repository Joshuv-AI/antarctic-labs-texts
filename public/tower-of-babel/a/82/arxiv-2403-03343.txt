Introduction to the monodromy conjecture
Willem Veys

Abstract
The monodromy conjecture is a mysterious open problem in singularity theory. Its original version relates arithmetic and topological/geometric properties of a multivariate polynomial f f over the integers, more precisely, poles of the p p -adic Igusa zeta function of f f should induce monodromy eigenvalues of f f . The case of interest is when the zero set of f f has singular points. We first present some history and motivation. Then we expose a proof in the case of two variables, and partial results in higher dimension, together with geometric theorems of independent interest inspired by the conjecture. We conclude with several possible generalizations.

1
Introduction

The monodromy conjecture is an intriguing and mysterious problem in singularity theory, on the crossroads of number theory, algebra, analysis, geometry and topology.
Its original statement predicts a remarkable relation between arithmetic and geometric/topological properties of a polynomial with integer coefficients.
More precisely, let
f\in{\mathbb{Z}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
and
p
a prime number. What can we say about the numbers of solutions of
f=0
over the finite residue rings
{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{i}{\mathbb{Z}}
for varying
i
?
These data turn out to be equivalent to the knowledge of a certain
p
-adic integral involving a complex parameter
s
, nowadays called the (
p
-adic) Igusa zeta function
Z_{p}(f;s)
. Igusa showed that it is in fact a rational function in
p^{-s}
. In particular, its poles govern the asymptotic behaviour of the numbers of solutions above when
i
tends to infinity.

The conjecture predicts that these poles induce monodromy eigenvalues of
f
: when
s_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}
is a pole of
Z_{p}(f;s)
, then
e^{2\pi i\Re(s_{0})}
should be a monodromy eigenvalue of
f
at some complex point of
\{f=0\}
. These eigenvalues are certain invariants of
f
in the context of differential topology, where
f
is viewed as a function from
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
to
{\mathbb{C}}
.
A priori there seems to be no reason to expect such an implication; it is somewhat motivated by a similar statement, which is a theorem for analogous real or complex integrals, nowadays called ‘archimedean zeta functions’.
When the hypersurface
\{f=0\}
has no singularities, both its Igusa zeta function and monodromy eigenvalues are quite simple and the conjecture is obvious. It is really a problem within different aspects of singularity theory.

Later Denef and Loeser constructed two related, more geometric, ‘zeta functions of Igusa type’: the topological and motivic zeta function, inspired by a formula for
Z_{p}(f;s)
in terms of an embedded resolution of
\{f=0\}
and by the context of motivic integration, respectively. Similar monodromy conjectures can be stated for these zeta functions.

Somewhat frustratingly, a conceptual reason for the conjecture to hold is still missing. All proofs of all known cases are ad hoc; one essentially derives enough information on both sides (poles and eigenvalues), often using resolution of singularities and requiring hard work, and then the conclusion follows by inspection of that information.
On the other hand, attempts of proofs or effective proofs, as well as quests for counterexamples, regularly produce geometric results of independent interest.

We sketch the ‘archimedean history’, leading to the original
p
-adic formulation of the conjecture, and introduce the other zeta functions of Igusa type.
We explain a proof for polynomials in two variables (the original proof is due to Loeser in 1988), which is up to now the only dimension where the conjecture is known to hold in general, and we present some significant results for polynomials in three variables, focussing for simplicity of notation on the topological zeta function. Here some geometric results of independent interest pop up.
We summarize various special cases when the conjecture was proven, and we conclude with possible generalizations of the conjecture in different directions.

Another introductory text with focus on the motivic zeta function is
Nicaise
.
The survey paper
VS
is a substantial introduction to
p
-adic and motivic integration, featuring also zeta functions of Igusa type and applications to singularities.

2
History

The original monodromy conjecture connects number theory and geometry/topology; however, it arose from a problem in analysis, posed at the International Congress of Mathematicians in 1954.

2.1
Archimedean zeta functions

We start with a basic example from calculus. Consider the real integral

I(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{s}\varphi(x)dx,

where
\varphi
is a complex
C^{\infty}
function with compact support and
s
is a complex parameter. One easily verifies that
I(s)
converges for
s\in{\mathbb{C}},\Re(s)>-1
, and is holomorphic in that region of the complex plane. In order to investigate its possible continuation, we decompose
I(s)
as

\int_{0}^{1}x^{s}\big(\varphi(x)-\varphi(0)\big)dx+\int_{0}^{1}x^{s}\varphi(0)dx+\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{s}\varphi(x)dx\,.

Since
\varphi(x)-\varphi(0)=x\tilde{\varphi}(x)
for another such function
\tilde{\varphi}
, the first summand converges for
\Re(s)>-2
. The second summand equals
\frac{\varphi(0)}{s+1}
, and the third one converges and is holomorphic on
{\mathbb{C}}
. Hence
I(s)
can be meromorphically continued to the region where
\Re(s)>-2
, with
-1
as possible pole.
Repeating this procedure yields that
I(s)
has a meromorphic continuation to
{\mathbb{C}}
with possible (simple) poles in
{\mathbb{Z}}_{<0}
.

What happens if we generalize the univariate polynomial
x
to a general multivariate
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
? We consider again a so-called ‘test function’
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
, i.e., a
C^{\infty}
function with compact support, and we associate to these data the (real)

Z(s)=Z(f,\varphi;s):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|f(x)|^{s}\varphi(x)dx.

Strictly speaking, the integrand is not defined on
\{f=0\}
, but we will always neglect such subsets of measure zero, in order to simplify notation. (These integrals were sometimes called ‘complex powers’ and denoted as
f^{s}
, and they were rather considered as distributions on the space of test functions, see e.g.
Igusa4
.)
Now one easily verifies that
Z(s)
converges for
s\in{\mathbb{C}},\Re(s)>0
, and is holomorphic in that region of the complex plane. During a talk at the ICM in 1954, I. Gel’fand posed the meromorphic continuation of

Z(s)
as an open problem, motivated by applications to the existence of fundamental solutions of linear PDE’s with constant coefficients. Around 1970, Bernstein and S. Gel’fand

and Atiyah

proved this using resolution of singularities, and Bernstein gave another proof

,
using the so-called b-function (note that both techniques were not available in 1954).
We explain the main ideas in both proofs; for a more elaborate overview on this problem, we refer to
Le

(
Igusa4
, Chapter 5)
. We will see that each proof provides also a complete list of possible candidate poles.

First proof.

Let

To each

In local coordinates

(f\circ h)(y)=u_{P}(y)\prod_{i\in S_{P}}y_{i}^{N_{i}}\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{Jac}_{h}(y)=v_{P}(y)\prod_{i\in S_{P}}y_{i}^{\nu_{i}-1},

(1)

respectively, where

Globally this means that the divisors of zeroes of

Using

Since

\sum_{P}\int_{U_{P}}\rho_{P}(y)|u_{P}(y)|^{s}|v_{P}(y)|(\varphi\circ h)(y)\prod_{i\in S_{P}}|y_{i}|^{N_{i}s+\nu_{i}-1}dy_{i}.

Theorem 2.1

.
Moreover, if

Second proof.

We first briefly introduce the b-function. For details and more information, we refer to e.g.
(
Igusa4
, Chapter 4)
Bernstein
. Let

be a field of characteristic zero and

is the (non-commutative) algebra of algebraic differential operators

, where

Theorem 2.2
(

)

Let

P(s)\in\mathcal{D}_{n}[s]
and a nonzero polynomial

P(s)\cdot f^{s+1}=b(s)f^{s}.

(2)

This identity can be viewed as either ‘formal’ or for integer values of

2
) is called the

Bernstein-Sato polynomial
or

of

, denoted

.
For

; see e.g.
(

For ease of exposition, we assume now that

\varphi

Using (

) for

, we compute

\displaystyle b_{f}(s)Z(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}b_{f}(s)f(x)^{s}\varphi(x)dx=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}P(x,\partial/\partial x,s)\cdot f(x)^{s+1}\varphi(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f(x)^{s+1}P^{*}(x,\partial/\partial x,s)\cdot\varphi(x)dx,

where the last equality is an instance of integration by parts, via the adjoint operator
P^{*}
of

.
Hence

Z(s)=\frac{1}{b_{f}(s)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f(x)^{s+1}\varphi_{1}(x,s)dx,

for some other test function
\varphi_{1}
, depending polynomially on
s
. We can continue this process, and obtain for arbitrary

that

Z(s)=\frac{1}{b_{f}(s+r-1)\dots b_{f}(s+1)b_{f}(s)}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}f(x)^{s+r}\varphi_{r}(x,s)dx,

for some test function

, and conclude finally the following.

Theorem 2.3

Let
f\in{\mathbb{R}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\setminus{\mathbb{R}}
and
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
a
C^{\infty}
function with compact support. Then
Z(f,\varphi;s)
has a meromorphic continuation to
{\mathbb{C}}
.
Moreover, its poles are of the form

\lambda-k
, with
\lambda

b_{f}
and

.

Note that this list is consistent with Theorem
2.1
, see Theorem

.

A somewhat finer analysis, see e.g.
(

, Theorem 5.3.1)

. Then

, where

Relation with monodromy

At first sight the test functions

2.4

Conjecture 1

3
More ‘Igusa type’ zeta functions and formulas via resolution

Unlike for the archimedean zeta functions, there is an explicit formula for the
p
-adic zeta function in terms of a given embedded resolution. That formula leads to a kind of geometric specialization of the
p
-adic zeta function, the
topological zeta function
. On the other hand we have the
motivic zeta function
, which is some geometric upgrade of the
p
-adic one. One can formulate an analogous monodromy conjecture for these zeta functions.

From now on we will use repeatedly the algebro-geometric version of embedded resolution, over various fields
Hironaka
. We fix associated notation and terminology for the rest of the paper.

Note 1

Let
K
be a field of characteristic zero and
f\in K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\setminus K
. Let
h:Y\to{\mathbb{A}}_{K}^{n}
be an embedded resolution of
\{f=0\}
, defined over
K
. So
Y
is an
n
-dimensional smooth algebraic variety,
h^{-1}\{f=0\}=\cup_{j\in S}E_{j}
is a simple normal crossings divisor, that is, its irreducible components
E_{j}
are nonsingular hypersurfaces that intersect transversally, and the restriction of
h
to
Y\setminus h^{-1}\{f=0\}
is an isomorphism.
(One could require that
h
is moreover an isomorphism outside the inverse image of the singular locus of
\{f=0\}
.)
We write
S=S_{e}\sqcup S_{s}
, where
S_{e}
ranges over the exceptional components and
S_{s}
over the components of the strict transform of
\{f=0\}
.

Moreover,
Y
,
h
and all
E_{j}
are defined over
K
. To each
E_{j}
one associates its numerical data
(N_{j},\nu_{j})
, where
N_{j}
and
\nu_{j}-1
are the multiplicities of
E_{j}
in the divisor of zeroes of
f\circ h
and of the
n
-form
h^{*}(d_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{n})
, respectively. In other words:

\operatorname{div}(f\circ h)=\sum_{j\in S}N_{j}E_{j}\quad\text{ and }\quad\operatorname{div}\big(h^{*}(dx_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{n})\big)=\sum_{j\in S}(\nu_{j}-1)E_{j}.

Also, for
I\subset S
, we denote
E_{I}=\cap_{i\in I}E_{i}
and
E_{I}^{\circ}=E_{I}\setminus\cup_{k\not\in I}E_{k}
. Hence

equals the disjoint union of all
E_{I}^{\circ}
.
Note that, in particular,
E_{\emptyset}^{\circ}=Y\setminus\cup_{j\in S}E_{j}
and that it is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{A}}_{K}^{n}\setminus\{f=0\}
.

To simplify notation, when

, we put

and
E_{i}^{\circ}:=E_{\{i\}}^{\circ}
.

Remark 6

According to e.g.

3.1

Consider an embedded resolution

We will need the notion of reduction mod

3.2
The topological zeta function

We now indicate a funny heuristic argument to compute the limit of the expression (
3
), when
p
tends to
1
(whatever that means …).
First, using l’Hôpital’s rule,
\frac{p-1}{p^{\nu_{i}+N_{i}s}-1}
tends to
\frac{1}{\nu_{i}+N_{i}s}
. More challenging, by Grothendieck’s trace formula,
\sharp(\overline{E_{I}}^{\circ}({\mathbb{F}}_{p}))
can be written as an alternating sum of traces of the
p
th power Frobenius operator, acting on adequate
\ell
-adic cohomology groups of
\overline{E_{I}}^{\circ}

Denef2
. The limit when
p
tends to
1
of this operator is morally the identity operator, and then that alternating sum tends to the alternating sum of the dimensions of those cohomology groups, that is, to an
\ell
-adic Euler characteristic. Finally, a comparison theorem motivates to consider the usual topological Euler characteristic
\chi(E_{I}^{\circ})
of the complex points of
E_{I}^{\circ}
as the limit of
\sharp(\overline{E_{I}}^{\circ}({\mathbb{F}}_{p}))
.
We conclude that heuristically

\lim_{p\rightarrow 1}Z(f;s)=\sum_{I\subset S}\chi(E_{I}^{\circ})\prod_{i\in I}\frac{1}{\nu_{i}+N_{i}s}.

Denef and Loeser were inspired by this heuristic argument to define a new singularity invariant for any
f\in{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\setminus{\mathbb{C}}

DenefLoeser1
.

Definition 2

Let

and choose an embedded resolution

of
\{f=0\}
, for which we use notation from Note

.
The (global) topological zeta function of

is

Z_{\operatorname{top}}(f;s):=\sum_{I\subset S}\chi(E_{I}^{\circ})\prod_{i\in I}\frac{1}{\nu_{i}+N_{i}s}.

(4)

The local topological zeta function of

at

is

Z_{\operatorname{top},a}(f;s):=\sum_{I\subset S}\chi(E_{I}^{\circ}\cap h^{-1}\{a\})\prod_{i\in I}\frac{1}{\nu_{i}+N_{i}s}.

(5)

Remark 9

It is not clear that the topological zeta function is well-defined, i.e., that it is independent of the chosen embedded resolution. Denef and Loeser proved this in

3.3
The motivic zeta function

Kontsevich suggested the idea of motivic integration (a geometric analogue of
p
-adic integration) in a lecture at Orsay in 1995. This theory has been further developed by Denef and Loeser, amongst others; we refer to the wonderful book
CNS
and the references therein.
The motivic zeta function is defined as an analogue of the Igusa zeta function, using motivic integration instead of
p
-adic integration.

Definition 3

Let
k
be any field. The Grothendieck group of
k
-varieties
K_{0}(\mathrm{Var}_{k})
is the quotient of the free abelian group generated by the symbols
[X]
, where
X
runs over all varieties over
k
, by the relations

\displaystyle[X]=[Y]

\displaystyle\mbox{ if }X\cong Y,

\displaystyle[X\setminus Y]+[Y]=[X]

\displaystyle\mbox{ if }Y\mbox{ is a closed subvariety of }X.

Multiplication determined by
[X]\cdot[Y]:=[X\times_{k}Y]

turns
K_{0}(\mathrm{Var}_{k})
into a commutative ring, called the Grothendieck ring of
k
-varieties.
We denote by
\mathbb{L}
the class of the affine line
{\mathbb{A}}_{k}^{1}
and by
K_{0}(\mathrm{Var}_{k})_{\mathbb{L}}
the localization of
K_{0}(\mathrm{Var}_{k})
with respect to
\mathbb{L}
.

The motivic zeta function
DenefLoeser2
is defined as an integral over the ‘arc space’
k\llbracket t\rrbracket^{n}
, which is the analogue of the space of
p
-adic integers
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{n}
. It is a power series in the formal variable
T
, which is sometimes written as
\mathbb{L}^{-s}
(with a formal
s
), to stress the analogy with
p^{-s}
.
That arc space carries a natural
motivic measure
d\mu
, with values in
K_{0}(\mathrm{Var}_{k})_{\mathbb{L}}
, which is the analogue of the Haar measure on
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{n}
. For a detailed explanation, we refer e.g. to the original papers
DenefLoeser2
and
DenefLoeser3
, to the introductory survey
Veys Sapporo
or to
CNS
.

Definition 4

Let
k
be a field of characteristic zero and
f\in k[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\setminus k
. The motivic zeta function of
f
is a formal power series over
K_{0}(\mathrm{Var}_{k})_{\mathbb{L}}
in the variable
T=\mathbb{L}^{-s}
, given by the (converging) motivic integral

Z_{\operatorname{mot}}(f;s)=Z_{\operatorname{mot}}(f;T):=\int_{k\llbracket t\rrbracket^{n}}(\mathbb{L}^{-s})^{\mathrm{ord}_{t}f(x)}d\mu.

More concretely, define

\mathcal{X}_{i}=\left\{\gamma\in\left(\frac{k\llbracket t\rrbracket}{(t^{i+1})}\right)^{n}\mid\mathrm{ord}_{t}f_{i}(\gamma)=i\right\}

for
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
, where
f_{i}:\left(\frac{k\llbracket t\rrbracket}{(t^{i+1})}\right)^{n}\rightarrow\frac{k\llbracket t\rrbracket}{(t^{i+1})}
is the natural extension of
f:k^{n}\rightarrow k
, and for
\lambda\in\frac{k\llbracket t\rrbracket}{(t^{i+1})}
we denote by
\mathrm{ord}_{t}(\lambda)\in\{0,1,\dots,i,+\infty\}
the highest power of
t
that divides
\lambda
. Then we have

Z_{\operatorname{mot}}(f;s)=\frac{1}{\mathbb{L}^{n}}\sum_{i\geq 0}[\mathcal{X}_{i}]\mathbb{L}^{-in-is}.

In order to see also the analogy of this expression with the Igusa zeta function, one easily verifies that, for
f\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\setminus{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
, we have

Z(f;s)=\frac{1}{p^{n}}\sum_{i\geq 0}\sharp\left\{a\in\left(\frac{{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}}{(p^{i+1})}\right)^{n}\mid\mathrm{ord}_{p}f(a)=i\right\}p^{-in-is}.

Denef’s formula can be upgraded to a similar formula for
Z_{\operatorname{mot}}(f;s)
in terms of an embedded resolution of
\{f=0\}

DenefLoeser2
.
The various versions of the monodromy conjecture can also be formulated for the motivic zeta function. In this setting there is a subtlety in the notion of pole because
K_{0}(\mathrm{Var}_{k})
is not a domain

(even

is a zero divisor

For precise statements we refer to

The motivic zeta function specializes to the
p
-adic one for all but finitely many

and to the topological one

. Hence the monodromy conjecture in the motivic setting implies it in the two other settings, but not necessarily in the other direction.
(There exist explicit examples, like

However, crucial arguments in proofs are often of geometric nature and typically ‘universal’. In that case proofs for the topological zeta function can immediately be upgraded to proofs for the motivic zeta function. In particular, this holds for the arguments in §

and §

3.4

We now also describe the ‘other side’ of the monodromy conjecture, monodromy eigenvalues and roots of the b-function, in terms of an embedded resolution.

Definition 5

Let

\displaystyle P_{n-1}(t)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(t-1)\zeta_{a}(t)}=\frac{1}{t-1}\prod_{j\in S_{e}}(t^{N_{j}}-1)^{\chi(E_{j}^{\circ})}\qquad(n\text{ odd}).

Lemma 1

Let the exceptional component
E_{0}
intersect exactly

Proof

In the Picard group

\sum_{j\in S}N_{j}E_{j}=h^{*}\mathrm{div}(f)=\mathrm{div}(h^{*}(f))=0\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i\in S}(\nu_{j}-1)E_{j}=K_{Y}-h^{*}K_{{\mathbb{A}}^{2}}=K_{Y}.

5.1
Strategy for surfaces (
n=3
)

Recall that, for
n=2
, one could expect that (generically) an exceptional curve
E_{j}
does not contribute to the poles of
Z_{\operatorname{top}}(f;s)
or
Z_{\operatorname{top},0}(f;s)
as soon as
\chi(E_{j}^{\circ})\geq 0
.
We proved this in Proposition
3
, which was an important step in the proof of the monodromy conjecture for curves.

For
n=3
, the corresponding expectation is that (generically) an exceptional surface
E_{j}
does not contribute to the poles of
Z_{\operatorname{top}}(f;s)
or
Z_{\operatorname{top},0}(f;s)
as soon as
\chi(E_{j}^{\circ})\leq 0
.
We proved this expectation almost in complete generality
Veys pv integrals
; in this subsection we outline the main ideas in the proof, including some new geometric results.
(We formulate this outline for
Z_{\operatorname{top}}(f;s)
, but it is also valid for
Z_{\operatorname{top},0}(f;s)
.)

Let
f\in{\mathbb{C}}[x,y,z]\setminus{\mathbb{C}}
. We fix an embedded resolution
h:Y\to{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
of
\{f=0\}
, for which we use notation from Note
1
. Further, we may and will assume that
h
is constructed as a composition of admissible blow-ups as in
Hironaka
, where each centre of blow-up is contained in the consecutive strict transform of
\{f=0\}
.
We focus on exceptional surfaces
E_{j}
that are mapped to a point by
h
, and hence are projective (in the case of isolated singularities we may assume this for all exceptional
E_{j}
).

When is
\chi(E_{j}^{\circ})\leq 0
? In order to investigate this, it is important to realize the difference with the case of curves. When
n=2
, an exceptional curve
E_{j}\subset Y
is created as a
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
by blowing up some point during
h
, and its strict transform by the further blow-ups in
h
stays isomorphic to
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. Furthermore, the number of intersection points of that exceptional curve with other components does not change during the resolution process. When
n=3
, an exceptional surface
E_{j}\subset Y
is either created, by blowing up a point, as
E_{j}^{*}\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
, or, by blowing up a nonsingular curve
Z
, as a ruled surface
E_{j}^{*}
over
Z
(by which we mean a
{\mathbb{P}}^{1}
-bundle over
Z
). During further blow-ups of
h
, this
E_{j}^{*}
often ‘changes’. Indeed, assume that the next centre of blow-up is a point
P
in
E_{j}^{*}
, or a curve intersecting
E_{j}^{*}
transversally in
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}
. Then the strict transform of
E_{j}^{*}
after this blow-up is isomorphic to
E_{j}^{*}
, blown up in
P
, or in
P_{1},\dots,P_{m}
, respectively. The remaining case, a blow-up with centre a curve contained in
E_{j}^{*}
, does not change
E_{j}^{*}
. Finally, at the end of the resolution process,
E_{j}\subset Y
is isomorphic to the projective plane or ruled surface
E_{j}^{*}
, blown up finitely many (say
r
) times.
Moreover, then
E_{j}
has
r
more intersecting (irreducible) curves with other components than
E_{j}^{*}
at the moment of its creation.

However, this change does not influence the ‘open part’
E_{j}^{\circ}
of that exceptional surface
E_{j}
. Indeed, let
C
denote the union of all intersections of
E_{j}^{*}
with other components after creation. Then, in the process of constructing
E_{j}
out of
E_{j}^{*}
by point blow-ups, all changes occur within
C
and its further consecutive total transform in that exceptional surface. As a conclusion,

E_{j}^{*}\setminus C\cong E_{j}\setminus\{\text{intersection of }E_{j}\text{ with other components in }Y\}=E_{j}^{\circ},

and hence
\chi(E_{j}^{*}\setminus C)=\chi(E_{j}^{\circ})
.

When the surface
E_{j}
(or, equivalently,
E_{j}^{*}
) is not rational, it turns out that the condition
\chi(E_{j}^{\circ})\leq 0
is quite special, and that in these cases
E_{j}
does not contribute to the poles of
Z_{\operatorname{top}}(f;s)
, see
Veys BullSMF
and
Veys pv integrals
.
But already in the case
E_{j}^{*}\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
, there is a whole zoo of configurations
C\subset{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
with
\chi({\mathbb{P}}^{2}\setminus C)=0
, and an even larger zoo with
\chi({\mathbb{P}}^{2}\setminus C)\leq 0
. For instance, there exist such
C
with any possible number of irreducible components, and of arbitrarily high degree and multiplicity of the singularities of
C
.

Theorems
5.1
and

We return now to our fixed embedded resolution

of
\{f=0\}
, and an exceptional surface

, that is created during

either as a projective plane or as a (projective) rational ruled surface

Let

-\frac{\nu_{j}}{N_{j}}

\frac{\nu_{j}}{N_{j}}\neq\frac{\nu_{i}}{N_{i}}

In order to use Theorem
5.2

is a ruled surface. Indeed, otherwise for instance
no fibres
of the ruled surface

With Theorem
5.2
as main input, and using the relations between numerical data from

and some subtle arguments, we obtained the following very general vanishing result for candidate poles of order

(

, 3.7)

Theorem 5.3

Let

be a rational projective exceptional surface, inducing

as candidate pole of order

.

(1) If

is created by blowing up a point, then

does not contribute to the poles of

.

(2) If
E_{j}
is created by blowing up a (projective) rational curve and
\cup_{i\in S_{j}}(E_{j}\cap E_{i})
is connected, then
E_{j}
does not contribute to the poles of
Z_{\operatorname{top}}(f;s)
.

We illustrate with the above example how Theorem
5.2
can be used to show that the contribution of

to the residue of the candidate pole
-\frac{\nu_{j}}{N_{j}}
is zero.

\mathcal{R}_{E_{j}}=0+\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}+\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime}_{0}}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}+\frac{0}{\alpha_{2}}+\frac{-1}{\alpha_{3}}+\frac{1}{\alpha^{\prime}_{0}\alpha_{2}}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{3}}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}\alpha_{3}}.

From
Veys Relations
, we have the basic relation
3\alpha_{0}+\alpha^{\prime}_{0}-1=0
, associated to the original configuration on
E_{j}^{*}\cong{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
, and the additional relations

\alpha_{1}=2\alpha_{0}+\alpha^{\prime}_{0}-1,\quad\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{0}+\alpha^{\prime}_{0}+\alpha_{1}-1\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha_{3}=\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}-1,

associated to each blow-up leading to
E_{j}
.

In a concrete case, as here, plugging in such relations in the expression
\mathcal{R}_{E_{j}}
would yield zero when expected. But this does not allow proving a general theorem.
We illustrate further the strategy that does allow it. One can associate a similar expression to any normal crossings curve
\cup_{i}C_{i}
on a smooth projective surface
A
, where each
C_{i}
is equipped with a nonzero rational number
\alpha_{i}
, satisfying
K_{A}=\sum_{i}(\alpha_{i}-1)C_{i}
. For instance in the example we have such an expression
\mathcal{R}
for the intermediate surface obtained after contracting
C^{\prime}_{0}
in
E_{j}
and an expression
\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}
for
\Sigma
.

Now the relations between the
\alpha
’s above yield in particular that

\alpha^{\prime}_{0}=\alpha_{2}+1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\alpha_{2}=\alpha_{3}+1,

and it is easy to verify that such ‘blow down relations’ are exactly what is needed to obtain that
\mathcal{R}_{E_{j}}=\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}
.
The expression
\mathcal{R}_{\Sigma}
is

0+\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}+\frac{0}{\alpha_{3}}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}\alpha_{3}}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{3}}=\Big(\frac{1}{\alpha_{0}}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{1}}\Big)\Big(1+\frac{1}{\alpha_{3}}\Big),

and this is zero because

\alpha_{0}+\alpha_{1}=0.

This last relation also follows from the relations between the
\alpha
’s above. An important point in general is that we already knew that such a relation must hold. It follows from the fact that
K_{\Sigma}=(\alpha_{0}-1)C_{0}+(\alpha_{1}-1)C_{1}+(\alpha_{3}-1)C_{3}
and the adjunction formula, applied to a generic fibre of
\Sigma
.

5.2
Special polynomials

Here we present an overview of the many special families of polynomials, for which the monodromy conjecture was proven, mostly for the standard version, sometimes for the more difficult stronger version featuring the b-function. Early results concern only the
p
-adic zeta function, but are mostly also valid in the context of the motivic and (a fortiori) the topological zeta function.
Later results are often stated in the setting of the motivic or topological zeta function.

In most cases, proofs in one setting (topological,
p
-adic, motivic) imply or can be upgraded to proofs in the other settings. But in some particular cases a proof in the
p
-adic or motivic setting is really more difficult than for the topological zeta function (typically when complicated combinatorial formulas are involved).

Everything started with Igusa’s calculations of the
p
-adic zeta function for polynomials
f
that are relative invariants of prehomogeneous vector spaces. (We refer to e.g.
SS
for these notions. Igusa uses the symmetry of the associated group; resolution of singularities does not appear here.) This study can be reduced to 29 types; for most of them Igusa derived in several papers explicit expressions for the zeta function, showing that the real parts of its poles are roots of
b_{f}
. This is summarized in
Ig
. Kimura, Sato and Zhu treated all types using microlocal analysis
KSZ
.

For surface singularities (
n=3
), the standard version is proved for several families of polynomials. Rodrigues and Veys
RV
showed it for ‘almost all’ homogeneous polynomials. Artal, Cassou-Noguès, Melle and Luengo proved it for all superisolated singularities, and also for the remaining case of homogeneous polynomials
ACLM1
. Polynomials that are general with respect to a threedimensional toric idealistic cluster were handled by Lemahieu and Veys
LV
.

As a side remark, we mention the exploration of a possible generalization of the monodromy conjecture to functions on normal surface singularities
Ro
RV2
.

Polynomials that are nondegenerate with respect to their local or global Newton polyhedron attracted considerable attention. All proofs use (mutually related) combinatorial formulas for the respective zeta functions:
p
-adic
(
DenefHoornaert
, Theorem 4.2)
, topological
(
DenefLoeser1
, Théorème 5.3)
, and motivic
(
BV
, Theorem 10.5)
.
(Another such formula, in the context of the generalizations in the next section, is derived in
Bo1
.)

Loeser showed an instance of the strong version of the conjecture for arbitrary
n
, assuming however several technical conditions
Lo3
. There are many contributions to the standard version.
For
n=3
, it was shown unconditionally by Lemahieu and Van Proeyen
LVP
for the topological zeta function, and by Bories and Veys for the
p
-adic and motivic zeta function
BV
. (This is a case where proofs in the
p
-adic and motivic setting are considerably more difficult.)
More recently Esterov, Lemahieu and Takeuchi
ELT
introduced new arguments for both existence of eigenvalues and cancellation
of candidate poles for the topological zeta function, especially for
n=4
, establishing in particular the conjecture unconditionally for
n=4
.

In arbitrary dimension, Larson, Payne and Stapledon proved the conjecture for the motivic zeta function, assuming the nondegenerate polynomials have
simplicial
Newton polyhedra
LPS
.
Also in arbitrary dimension, Quek showed cancellation of candidate poles for the motivic zeta function, by constructing a stack-theoretic embedded resolution
Q
. This yields in particular a new geometric proof of the main result of
BV
.

Finally, we list families of polynomials in an arbitrary number of variables
n
, for which the conjecture is proved.

Artal, Cassou-Noguès, Melle and Luengo showed the standard version for quasi-ordinary polynomials on the motivic level
ACLM2
.

For hyperplane arrangements, Budur, Mustaţǎ and Teitler showed the standard version for the topological zeta function
BMT
. In that paper, they also reduce the stronger version to showing that, for an indecomposable essential central hyperplane arrangement of degree d, we have that
-n/d
is a root of its b-function. The latter is shown in some cases by Budur, Saito and Yuzvinski in
BSYV
, featuring also an appendix by Veys containing unexpected examples where that value
-n/d
is
not
a pole of the topological zeta function.

Blanco, Budur and van der Veer proved the strong version for semi-quasihomogeneous polynomials with an
isolated singularity
at the origin
BBvdV
.

Budur and van der Veer showed the standard and strong version for polynomials
fg
, where
g
is a
log generic
and
log very-generic
polynomial with respect to
f
, respectively

BvdV
.

6
Generalizations

For ease of presentation, and in order to focus on the ‘essentials’, we exposed everything in the most basic setting. In this final section we present the original formulation of the conjecture, in the context of more general
p
-adic fields and also involving a multiplicative character. We also mention subsequent generalizations: mappings/ideals instead of only one polynomial, zeta functions involving a non-standard differential form, and zeta functions in several variables
s_{\ell}
.

One can combine several generalizations simultaneously, but here we present each generalizing aspect separately in order to focus on that aspect.

6.1
Original formulation

Let
K
be an arbitrary
p
-adic field, that is, a finite extension of
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
. Let
\mathcal{O}_{K}
be its valuation ring with maximal ideal
m_{K}
, generated by a uniformizing parameter
\pi
, and residue field
\mathcal{O}_{K}/m_{K}
of cardinality
q
(which is a power of
p
).

Any
z\in K^{\times}
can be written uniquely in the form
z=\pi^{\operatorname{ord}_{K}(z)}\cdot\operatorname{ac}(z)
, where
\operatorname{ord}_{K}(z)\in{\mathbb{Z}}
is the
\pi
-order
of
z
and
\operatorname{ac}(z)\in\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
is the
angular component
of
z
.
The standard
norm
of
z
is
|z|_{K}:=q^{-\operatorname{ord}_{K}(z)}
.

Definition 7

Let
K
be a
p
-adic field as above,
f\in K[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\setminus K
and
\varphi
a locally constant function
K^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
with compact support. Let furthermore
\varkappa:\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
be a (multiplicative) character, that is, a group homomorphism with finite image. To these data one associates the
p
-adic Igusa zeta function

Z_{K}(f,\varkappa,\varphi;s):=\int_{K^{n}}|f(x)|_{K}^{s}\varkappa\big(\operatorname{ac}f(x)\big)\varphi(x)dx,

where
dx
denotes the standard Haar measure on
K^{n}
(such that
\mathcal{O}_{K}^{n}
has measure
1
), and
s\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
\Re(s)>0
.

Remark 18

This zeta function can be defined more conceptually without choosing a uniformizing parameter and the associated angular component. A quasi-character of
K^{\times}
is a continuous homomorphism
\omega:K^{\times}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{\times}
(extended with
\omega(0)=0
), to which one associates the zeta function
Z_{K}(f,\varphi,\omega):=\int_{K^{n}}\omega(f(x))\varphi(x)dx
.
Choosing a uniformizing parameter
\pi
and
s\in{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying
\omega(\pi)=q^{-s}
, we have that
\omega(z)=|z|_{K}^{s}\varkappa(\operatorname{ac}z)
, where
\varkappa:=\omega|_{\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}}
.
Then
Z_{K}(f,\varphi,\omega)=Z_{K}(f,\varkappa,\varphi;s)
.

Generalizing Theorem
2.5
,
Z_{K}(f,\varkappa,\varphi;s)
is a rational function in

, and thus has a meromorphic continuation to
{\mathbb{C}}
.
Also Denef’s formula (Theorem

) generalizes, see
(
Denef2
, Theorem 2.2)
. Here we just mention the adapted list of candidate poles.

Proposition 4

Let
h:Y\rightarrow{\mathbb{A}}_{K}^{n}
be an embedded resolution of
\{f=0\}
, for which we use notation from Note
1
. Then, denoting by

the order of the character
\varkappa

-\frac{\nu_{j}}{N_{j}}+\frac{2\pi k}{(\ln p)N_{j}}i,\text{ with }\ j\in S\text{ such that }d\mid N_{j}\text{, and }k\in{\mathbb{Z}}\,.

The original formulation of the monodromy conjecture is in this setting.

Conjecture 3
(Monodromy conjecture)

Let
F
be an arbitrary number field and
f\in F[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\setminus F
. Then, for all but a finite number of (non-archimedean) completions
K
of

\varkappa

\mathcal{O}_{K}^{\times}
and all test functions
\varphi

There is an analogous local version and a stronger version with the b-function
b_{f}
. The general form of the topological zeta function is as follows, where the number

in the ‘limit procedure’ of §

Definition 8
(

Let

and

be an embedded resolution of

, for which we use notation from Note

The (global) topological zeta function associated to

and

Z_{\operatorname{top}}(f,d;s):=\sum_{I\subset S,\forall i\in I:d|N_{i}}\chi(E_{I}^{\circ})\prod_{i\in I}\frac{1}{\nu_{i}+N_{i}s}.

Also for this variant one can generalize the monodromy conjecture in the obvious way.
For the motivic generalization, and the formulation of the monodromy conjecture in that setting, we refer to
(
DenefLoeser2
, 2.4)
. Note that, with respect to this more general motivic zeta function, the ‘basic’ motivic zeta function from §

is sometimes called the

.

For an upgrade of the proof for

(

According to Proposition

6.2
Mappings/ideals

For simplicity we take as test function
\varphi
the standard choice: the characteristic function of
{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{n}

Definition 9

Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, such that the ideal generated by
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}
is not trivial.
The
p
-adic Igusa zeta function associated to the mapping

Z(\mathbf{f};s):=\int_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{n}}||\mathbf{f}(x)||_{p}^{s}dx,

where
||\mathbf{f}(x)||_{p}=||(f_{1}(x),\dots,f_{r}(x))||_{p}:=\max\{|f_{1}(x)|_{p},\dots,|f_{r}(x)|_{p}\}
.

Many aspects of the one polynomial case generalize in a natural way. Fixing
p
and taking all

in
{\mathbb{Z}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\setminus{\mathbb{Z}}

is equivalent to the knowledge of the numbers of solutions of
f_{1}=\ldots=f_{r}=0
over the finite rings
{\mathbb{Z}}/p^{i}{\mathbb{Z}}
.
There is a similar ‘Denef type’ formula, in terms of a principalization of the ideal generated by
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}
in
{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. When the
f_{j}
are defined over
{\mathbb{C}}
or over any field

of characteristic zero, we have a similar notion of topological and motivic zeta function, respectively, associated to the ideal
I=(f_{1},\dots,f_{r})

Veys-Zuniga1

On the other hand, there is no clear generalization of the notion of local Milnor fibre of a function
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
to an arbitrary polynomial map
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{r}
. But there is a notion of ‘Verdier monodromy’

I
in
{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
(and even in a more general setting of subschemes of schemes), and also a notion of b-function

for such an ideal

2.4
holds in this setting: if
s_{0}
is a root of

, then
e^{2\pi is_{0}}
is a Verdier monodromy eigenvalue of
I
, and every Verdier monodromy eigenvalue is obtained this way

. We initiated the question whether the various versions of the monodromy conjecture for one polynomial could generalize to this setting of ideals, that is, do poles of zeta functions of ideals
I
induce roots of
b_{I}(s)
or Verdier monodromy eigenvalues of
I

The strong version was verified for monomial ideals in
HMY
. The (Verdier) monodromy version was proven for arbitrary ideals in two variables in
VPV2
, and for all families of space monomial curves with a plane semigroup in
MVV
(see also
MMVV
).
Of independent interest is an A’Campo type formula for ‘Verdier monodromy zeta functions’ in arbitrary dimension
(
VPV2
, Theorem 3.2)
.

A variant of the holomorphy conjecture in this setting was proved for
n=2

.

Most importantly, Mustaţǎ

to the b-function of

Theorem 6.1
(
Mu2
)

(1) Let
I=(f_{1},\dots,f_{r})
be a nontrivial ideal in
{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]

g:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}f_{i}y_{i}\in{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{r}]
.
Then
b_{g}(s)=(s+1)b_{I}(s)
.

(2) Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
generate a nontrivial ideal. Consider the polynomial
g:=\sum_{i=1}^{r}f_{i}y_{i}\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{r}]

Z(g;s)=\frac{1-p^{-1}}{1-p^{-s-1}}Z(\mathbf{f};s).

As a corollary, the monodromy conjecture for one polynomial

6.3
Zeta functions with differential form

Recall Corollary
1
in the context of the real zeta functions of §
2.1
: if
s_{0}
is a pole of
Z(f,\varphi;s)
, then
e^{2\pi is_{0}}
is a monodromy eigenvalue of
f
at some point of
\{f=0\}
. This is also true for complex zeta functions associated to a complex polynomial; and in that case also the converse holds:
all
monodromy eigenvalues of

are obtained this way

We now shift our point of view; for a test function
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}
, we consider
\varphi dx
together as the measure associated to the

differential

can be obtained from a pole of an archimedean zeta function associated to

and a form

.

Is there some analogue of this more general result in the

-adic case? For sure, locally constant functions are much less flexible than

functions;
typically ‘most’ monodromy eigenvalues of

are
not
induced by poles of

-adic zeta functions.
We proposed the following setting, taking here for simplicity as test function the characteristic function on
(p{\mathbb{Z}}_{p})^{n}
.

We view the Haar measure

on

as the measure associated to the standard differential

Definition 10

Let

Z_{0}(f,\omega;s):=\int_{(p{\mathbb{Z}}_{p})^{n}}|f(x)|_{p}^{s}|g(x)|_{p}dx.

Definition 11

Let

6.4
Multivariate zeta functions

There are obvious multivariate generalizations of the archimedean and
p
-adic Igusa zeta functions associated to several polynomials, see e.g.
Lo2
, and of the induced topological and motivic zeta functions. We mention the basic
p
-adic and topological versions.

Definition 12

(1) Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\in{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\setminus{\mathbb{Q}}_{p}
and put
F=(f_{1},\dots,f_{k})
. The multivariate
p
-adic Igusa zeta function associated to
F
is

Z(F;\mathbf{s}):=\int_{{\mathbb{Z}}_{p}^{n}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}|f_{\ell}(x)|_{p}^{s_{\ell}}dx,

where all
s_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{C}}
with
\Re(s_{\ell})>0
, and
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
.

(2)
Let
f_{1},\dots,f_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\setminus{\mathbb{C}}
; we put
F=(f_{1},\dots,f_{k})
and
f=\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}f_{\ell}
. Choose an embedded resolution
h:Y\rightarrow{\mathbb{A}}_{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
of
\{f=0\}
, for which we use notation from Note
1
, with moreover
\operatorname{div}(f_{\ell}\circ h)=\sum_{j\in S}N_{j}^{(\ell)}E_{j}
for
\ell=1,\dots,k
.
The (global) topological zeta function of
F
is

Z_{\operatorname{top}}(F;\mathbf{s}):=\sum_{I\subset S}\chi(E_{I}^{\circ})\prod_{i\in I}\frac{1}{\nu_{i}+N_{i}^{(1)}s_{1}+\dots+N_{i}^{(k)}s_{k}}.

(12)

We focus here on the topological one. We denote its
polar locus
, that is, the support of the divisor of poles, by
\mathcal{P}(Z_{\operatorname{top}}(F;\mathbf{s}))
. It is a union of hyperplanes, among the
candidate polar hyperplanes
\{\nu_{j}+N_{j}^{(1)}s_{1}+\dots+N_{j}^{(k)}s_{k}=0\},j\in S
.

The generalization of the monodromy eigenvalues (when
k=1
) is the monodromy support of
F
, defined
as follows. For each
\alpha\in({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{k}
, one has a rank one local system on
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{k}
with monodromy
\alpha_{i}
around the
i
th missing coordinate hyperplane. Let
\mathcal{L}_{\alpha}
denote its pullback via
F|_{{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus\{f=0\}}:{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\setminus\{f=0\}\to({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{k}
. For
x\in\{f=0\}
, let
U_{x}
be the complement of
\{f=0\}
in a small open ball around
x
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Then the
monodromy support
\mathcal{S}_{F}
of
F
consists of those
\alpha\in({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{k}
for which there exists a point
x\in\{f=0\}
with

H^{*}(U_{x},\mathcal{L}_{\alpha})\neq 0
.
(It is a finite union of torsion-translated codimension one affine algebraic subtori of
({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{k}

BLSW
.)

Conjecture 5

Let
F
be a
k
-tuple of nonconstant polynomials in
{\mathbb{C}}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. Then

\operatorname{Exp}\Big(\mathcal{P}\big(Z_{\operatorname{top}}(F;\mathbf{s})\big)\Big)\subset\mathcal{S}_{F},

where
\operatorname{Exp}:{\mathbb{C}}^{k}\to({\mathbb{C}}^{*})^{k}
is the coordinate wise map
\beta\mapsto e^{2\pi i\beta}
.

For
k>1
, this generalized conjecture was posed by Loeser, see
Ni
and
Lo2
.
It is known for tuples of plane curves
Ni
, and tuples of hyperplane arrangements
B-ls
.

A stronger version involves a generalization of the Bernstein-Sato polynomial, the
Bernstein-Sato ideal
B_{F}
of
F
, which is the ideal generated by polynomials
b\in{\mathbb{C}}[s_{1},\dots,s_{k}]
satisfying

P\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}f_{\ell}^{s_{\ell}+1}=b\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}f_{\ell}^{s_{\ell}}

for some
P\in\mathcal{D}_{n}[s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}]
.
Let
Z(B_{F})
denote the zero locus of
B_{F}
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{k}
. (It was proven in
BVWZ
that
\operatorname{Exp}(Z(B_{F}))=\mathcal{S}_{F}
, extending Theorem
2.4
.)
The stronger version then states that
\mathcal{P}(Z_{\operatorname{top}}(F;\mathbf{s}))\subset Z(B_{F})
.

This stronger version was posed in
B-ls
. It is known for tuples factorizing a tame hyperplane arrangement
Bath
, and for tuples of linear polynomials
Wu
.
In
BvdV
instances of both standard and stronger version are proved in the context of so-called
log generic
and
log very-generic
polynomials, respectively.
