From a conjecture of Collatz to Thompson’s group ℱ \mathcal{F} , via a conjunction of Girard
Peter M. Hines

Abstract
The famous 3 ​ x + 1 3x+1 problem of L. Collatz needs no introduction; however, this paper concerns a lesser-known, but similarly unresolved, precursor problem : the Original Collatz Conjecture, or OCC [ 1 ] . We demonstrate that the core arithmetic operator from the OCC, when combined with a conjunction of J.-Y. Girard from his Geometry of Interaction system [ 2 , 3 ] , leads to a realisation of R. Thompson’s group ℱ \mathcal{F} as congruential functions, in the sense of J. Conway [ 4 ] . We also give the underlying category theory that accounts for this, and describe the core operator from the OCC as a canonical coherence isomorphism.



Keywords :
Collatz Conjectures, Thompson’s Group
\mathcal{F}
, Congruential Functions, Geometry of Interaction, Categorical Coherence, Associahedra

1
Basic Definitions : the OCC, and Girard’s conjunction

We start with the Original Collatz Conjecture, as described in
[
1
]
.

Definition 1
.

We denote the symmetric group on
\mathbb{N}
by
{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
, and define the
original Collatz bijection

\rho\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
by
\rho(n)=\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{lcr}\vskip 3.00003pt\frac{2n}{3}&&n\ (mod\ 3)=0,\\
\vskip 3.00003pt\frac{4n-1}{3}&&n\ (mod\ 3)=1,\\
\vskip 3.00003pt\frac{4n+1}{3}&&n\ (mod\ 3)=2.\end{array}\right.

Lemma 2
.

The unique fixed points of
\rho
are
\{0,1\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, and the orbits of all
\{0,\ldots,9\}\setminus\{8\}
are finite.

Proof.

Elementary algebra gives
\{0,1\}
as unique solutions to
\rho(n)=n
. By direct calculation,
2\rightarrow 3\rightarrow 2
and
4\rightarrow 5\rightarrow 7\rightarrow 9\rightarrow 6\rightarrow 4
are finite orbits.
∎

As described in
[
1
]
, the above bijection appeared in unpublished notebooks of Collatz, dated
1^{st}
May, 1932, as the core of the following (currently unresolved) conjecture :

Conjecture

(The O.C.C.)
.

The orbit of
8
under
\rho
is infinite.

We combine the core operator of the OCC with the model of a logical conjunction used by J.-Y. Girard
[
2
,
3
]
. As pointed out in
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, Girard’s system was based around the inverse monoid
\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{N})
of partial injections on
\mathbb{N}
; we restrict ourselves to the subgroup
{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})\subseteq\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{N})
.

Definition 3
.

Girard’s conjunction
is the function
\_\star\_:{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})\times{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})\rightarrow{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
given by

(a\star b)(n)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcr}\vskip 3.00003pt2.a\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)&&n\ \mbox{even,}\\
2.b\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)+1&&n\ \mbox{odd.}\\
\end{array}\right.

It is well-established (e.g.
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
) that this is an injective homomorphism.

1.1
A slight simplification

The OCC considers orbits of the natural numbers under the bijection
\rho
. We conjugate
\rho
by the successor function / its (partially defined) inverse to eliminate the triviality
\rho(0)=0
, but preserve the behaviour of all other orbits :

Definition 4
.

We define the
reduced Collatz bijection

\lambda\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
by
\lambda(n)=\rho(n+1)-1
. This is a well-defined bijection on
\mathbb{N}
, since
\rho(0)=0
. Expanding out the definition gives an explicit formula :

\lambda(n)=\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{lcr}\vskip 3.00003pt\frac{4n}{3}&&n\ (mod\ 3)=0,\\
\vskip 3.00003pt\frac{4n+2}{3}&&n\ (mod\ 3)=1,\\
\vskip 3.00003pt\frac{2n-1}{3}&&n\ (mod\ 3)=2.\end{array}\right.

By construction,
\lambda^{K}(n)=\rho^{K}(n+1)-1
, so questions about the non-trivial orbits of
\rho
and those of
\lambda
are interchangeable; the OCC then becomes the conjecture that the orbit of
7
under
\lambda
is infinite.

Lemma 5
.

\lambda\neq\rho
, and
\lambda(n)=\rho(n)\ \Leftrightarrow\ n=0
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Trivially,
\lambda\neq\rho
. Writing the three cases separately gives

\lambda(n)\ =\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{lcr}4n/3&\ \ \ \ n=3M&2n/3\\
(4n+2)/3&\ \ \ \ n=3M+1&(4n-1)/3\\
(2n-1)/3&\ \ \ \ n=3M+2&(4n+1)/3\\
\end{array}\right\}\ =\ \rho(n)

giving, respectively,
n=0
, a contradiction, and
n=-1\notin{\mathbb{N}}
.
∎

1.2
Combining the original and reduced Collatz bijections

The inverses of
\lambda,\rho\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
may be given explicitly as :

\lambda^{-1}(n)\ =\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\vskip 3.00003pt\frac{3n}{4}&n\ (mod\ 4)=0,\\
\vskip 3.00003pt\frac{3n-2}{4}&n\ (mod\ 4)=2,\\
\frac{3n+1}{2}&n\ \mbox{ odd,}\end{array}\right.\ \mbox{ and }\ \rho^{-1}(n)=\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{lcr}\vskip 3.00003pt\frac{3n}{2}&&n\ (mod\ 2)=0,\\
\vskip 3.00003pt\frac{3n+1}{4}&&n\ (mod\ 4)=1,\\
\frac{3n-1}{4}&&n\ (mod\ 4)=3.\end{array}\right.

Composing the reduced Collatz bijection with the inverse of the original Collatz bijection results in a bijection previously seen in category theory & logic :

Definition 6
.

We define the
associator

\alpha\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
by
\alpha=\lambda\rho^{-1}\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
; equivalently,

\alpha(n)=\rho(\rho^{-1}(n)+1)-1
. Expanding out the definition gives :

\alpha(n)\ =\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\vskip 3.00003pt2n&n\ (mod\ 2)=0,\\
\vskip 3.00003ptn+1&n\ (mod\ 4)=1,\\
\frac{n-1}{2}&n\ (mod\ 4)=3,\end{array}\right.\ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ \alpha^{-1}(n)\ =\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\vskip 3.00003pt\frac{n}{2}&n\ (mod\ 4)=0,\\
\vskip 3.00003ptn-1&n\ (mod\ 4)=2,\\
2n+1&n\ (mod\ 2)=1.\end{array}\right.

We may think of the associator as,
“The shifted Collatz bijection, composed with the inverse of the original bijection”
.
By contrast with either Collatz bijection, orbits of natural numbers under the associator are very easy to characterise.

Lemma 7
.

The associator has a unique fixed point,
\alpha(0)=0
. The orbit of every
n>0\in\mathbb{N}
has a single local minimum iff
n>0
is even; no orbit has a local maximum, and thus the orbit of every
n>0
is infinite.

Proof.

It is easier (but equivalent) to prove this for the inverse,
\alpha^{-1}=\rho\lambda^{-1}
.

Trivially,
\alpha^{-1}(0)=0
; this is unique, since
\alpha^{-1}(n)=n\ \Leftrightarrow\ r(n)=l(n)
, contradicting Lemma
5
for all
n\neq 0
. When
n\in\mathbb{N}
is odd,
\alpha^{-1}(n)=2n+1>n
; which is similarly odd. Thus,
n<\alpha^{-1}(n)
for any odd
n
. When
n>0
is even,
\alpha^{-1}(n)<n
may be even or odd, but cannot be zero since
\alpha^{-1}
is a bijection. Thus, any trajectory starting with a non-zero even number decreases until it arrives at an odd number (the local minimum), at which point it increases without limit.

Every finite orbit attains a maximum and a minimum; thus the only finite orbit under
\alpha^{-1}
(and hence under
\alpha
) is the fixed point
\alpha^{-1}(0)=0=\alpha(0)
.
∎

Girard’s conjunction and the associator (& hence the original Collatz bijecton) are linked by a property more familiar from category theory :

Lemma 8

(Naturality)
.

\alpha(f\star(g\star h))\ =\ ((f\star g)\star h)\alpha
, for all
f,g,h\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
.

Proof.

Direct calculation gives, for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\alpha(f\star(g\star h))(n)\ =\ ((f\star g)\star h)\alpha(n)\ =\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{lcr}4f\left(\frac{n}{2}\right)&&n\ (mod\ 2)=0\\
4g\left(\frac{n-1}{4}\right)+2&&n\ (mod\ 4)=1\\
2h\left(\frac{n-3}{4}\right)-1&&n\ (mod\ 4)=3\\
\end{array}\right.

∎

2
Thompson’s
\mathcal{F}
from Collatz and Girard

A significant object of study in combinatorial group theory is Richard Thompson’s group
\mathcal{F}
; we refer to
[
8
,
9
]
for an overview. Many concrete realisations are known; we start with an abstract definition as generators and relations.

Definition 9
.

Thompson’s group
\mathcal{F}
is generated by the set
\{x_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
subject to the relations

x_{i}^{-1}x_{j}x_{i}=x_{j+1}
, for all
i<j
. It is well-known (e.g.
[
8
]
) that this is not a minimal generating set; the subset
\{x_{0},x_{1}\}
generates the entirety of
\mathcal{F}
, but the required relations are less intuitive.

Theorem 10
.

The group generated by
\{\alpha\ ,\ Id\star\alpha\}
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

We define
\{X_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
by
X_{0}=\alpha
and
X_{j+1}=Id\star X_{j}
, for all
j>0
.
As
(\_\star\_)
is a group homomorphism, Lemma
8
(naturality) implies

\alpha(Id\star(Id\star f))\alpha^{-1}\ =\ (Id\star Id)\star f\ =\ Id\star f\ \ \ \ \forall f\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})

Applying this to the inductive definition of
X_{j}
gives
X_{j}=X_{i}X_{j+1}X_{i}^{-1}
, for all
i<j
, and so the subgroup of
{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
generated by
\{X_{j}\}_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
is a homomorphic image of
\mathcal{F}
, given by
x_{j}\mapsto X_{j}
, for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
. We then appeal to the well-known property (.e.g.
[
8
]
) that every such homomorphic image is either abelian or isomorphic to
\mathcal{F}
itself, to deduce that this subgroup is isomorphic to
\mathcal{F}
.

Finally, the presentation of Definition
9
is not minimal and
\{x_{0},x_{1}\}
suffices to generate the whole of
\mathcal{F}
; our result follows.
∎

Definition 11
.

We refer to the subgroup of
{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
generated by

\alpha(n)\ =\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\vskip 3.00003pt{2n}&{n}\ (mod\ 2)=0\\
\vskip 3.00003ptn+1&n\ (mod\ 4)=1\\
\frac{n-1}{2}&n\ (mod\ 4)=3\end{array}\right.\ \ \ \ (Id\star\alpha)(n)\ =\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\vskip 3.00003ptn&n\ (mod\ 2)=0\\
\vskip 3.00003pt2n-1&n\ (mod\ 4)=1\\
\vskip 3.00003ptn+2&n\ (mod\ 8)=3\\
\frac{n-1}{2}&n\ (mod\ 8)=7\end{array}\right.

as the
Congruential realisation of
\mathcal{F}
.

We may think of
(Id\star\alpha)
as, ‘replicating
\alpha=\rho\left(\rho^{-1}(n)+1\right)-1
on the odd numbers only’. By construction, we may write the generators of this realisation of
\mathcal{F}
in terms of the original Collatz bijection, as :

n\ \mapsto\ \rho\left(\rho^{-1}(n)+1\right)-1\ \ \mbox{and}\ \ n\mapsto\ \left\{\begin{array}[]{lcr}\vskip 3.00003ptn&&n\ \mbox{even,}\\
2.\rho\left(\rho^{-1}\left(\frac{n-1}{2}\right)+1\right)-1&&n\ \mbox{ odd.}\end{array}\right.

We observe that the above realisation of
\mathcal{F}
may also be derived from that given by M.V. Lawson in
[
10
]
in terms of the polycyclic monoids of inverse semigroup theory
[
11
]
, by relying on a common & well-known arithmetic representation of these inverse monoids
[
12
]
.

Remark 12

(Girard’s conjunction on
\mathcal{F}
)
.

In
[
13
]
, K. Brown describes an injective group homomorphism
\mu:\mathcal{F}\times\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}
that is “associative up to conjugation by [the generator]
x_{0}
”, so

\mu(\mu(a,b),c)\ =\ x_{0}\mu(a,\mu(b,c))x_{0}^{-1}\ \ \ \forall\ a,b,c\in\mathcal{F}

This is of course the naturality property of Lemma
8
, and the generator
x_{0}
in the congruential realisation is precisely the associator, derived from the original Collatz bijection. We may therefore identify Girard’s conjunction (when restricted to this realisation of
\mathcal{F}
) with Brown’s homomorphism, and conclude that the congruential realisation of
\mathcal{F}
is closed under Girard’s conjunction.

2.1
\mathcal{F}
as congruential functions

The above terminology is based on the following definition of J. Conway :

Definition 13
.

A function
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
is
congruential
when there exists some indexed set
\{(x_{j},y_{j}):x_{j},y_{j}<K\}_{j=0,\ldots,K-1}
such that
f(Km+i)\ =\ x_{j}m+y_{j}
.

Conway’s key result
[
4
]
(see also
[
14
]
) – that universal computation may be expressed via simple iterative problems on congruential functions – was later refined & simplified by S. Burckel
[
15
]
, who used a strict subset of Conway’s functions.

It may readily be verified that the bijections
\rho,\lambda
, and
\alpha
are congruential, and this property is preserved under composition, inverses, and Girard’s conjunction. The above realisation of
\mathcal{F}
is indeed then a realisation as congruential functions.

.

It remains to describe the structure of the group of congruential bijections generated by
\{\lambda,\rho,(Id\star\lambda),(Id\star\rho)\}
. This is presumably non-trivial, but worthwhile; it arises directly from the O.C.C. and contains Thompson’s
\mathcal{F}
in a natural way. Some identities this group satisfies may be derived categorically, from Figure

An explanation

The explanation why such seemingly distinct topics (Collatz’s conjecture(s), Girard’s linear logic, Thompson’s
\mathcal{F}

canonical isomorphisms
, in the sense of categorical coherence. Further, Girard’s conjunction (or Brown’s homomorphism) is a categorical tensor, and Thompson’s
\mathcal{F}
has been known since its introduction to have close connections with MacLane’s celebrated coherence theorem for associativity

Definition 15

(\mathcal{C},\otimes)

3.1
Interpreting the pentagram

The interpretation of MacLane’s pentagon (the
red
paths) as the 1-skeleton of Stasheff’s fourth associahedron is well-known; nodes correspond to vertices (i.e. binary well-bracketings of four symbols), and edges correspond to mappings between them. In Figure
1
, the labels on blue paths (i.e. the inner pentagon) are the unique components of natural transformations between the following injective homomorphisms from
{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})^{\times 4}
to
{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
:

\{\ \ (\_\star(\_\star\_\star\_))\ ,\ ((\_\star\_\star\_)\star\_)\ ,\ ((\_\star\_)\star\_\star\_)\ ,\ (\_\star(\_\star\_)\star\_)\ ,\ (\_\star\_\star(\_\star\_))\ \ \}

We may of course interpret these homomorphisms as edges of
\mathcal{K}_{4}
, and the blue paths as mappings between edges of the fourth associahedron.

Finally, we need to give an account of the
green
paths. Again interpreting via the fourth associahedron
\mathcal{K}_{4}
, we should understand these as,
mappings between edges and vertices
; they are the components of natural transformations such as

(\_\star(\_\star(\_\star\_)))\ \Rightarrow(\_\star(\_\star\_\star\_))\ \ \ \ \mbox{ with unique component }\ \ Id\star\rho^{-1}

Equivalently, they correspond to deleting / inserting a matching pair of brackets in a string of four symbols. This then provides us with the interpretation of Collatz’s bijection : it inserts a matching (right-associated) pair of brackets into such a string; the reduced Collatz bijection performs the same task for a left-associated pair. Their inverses delete such matching pairs of brackets.

3.2
The OCC, and natural transformations between bracketings

The Original Collatz Conjecture concerns fixed-points of powers of
\rho
— implicitly, it is based on the homomorphism from
(\mathbb{N},+)
to
{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
given by
n\mapsto\rho^{n}
. We consider
{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
as simply a distinguished subgroup of the symmetric inverse monoid
{\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{N})}
and make the following definition :

Definition 24
.

We define the
left-
and
right- Collatz homomorphisms
to be the monoid homomorphisms from
({\mathbb{N}},+)
to
{\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{N})}
given by

{\mathcal{C}\mathfrak{z}}_{L}(n)=\lambda^{n}\ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ {\mathcal{C}\mathfrak{z}}_{R}(n)=\rho^{n}

Proposition 25
.

There exists a natural transformation
{\mathcal{C}\mathfrak{z}}_{L}\Rightarrow{\mathcal{C}\mathfrak{z}}_{R}
whose unique component is the successor function
succ\in{\mathcal{I}(\mathbb{N})}
.

Proof.

Let us re-write the key identity of Definition
4
as
1+\left(\lambda\right)(n)=\rho(n+1)
, for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Then
1+\left(\lambda^{K}\right)(n)=\rho^{K}(n+1)
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
giving, for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

succ.{\mathcal{C}\mathfrak{z}}_{R}(k)\ =\ {\mathcal{C}\mathfrak{z}}_{L}(k).succ\ \ \ \forall n\in(\mathbb{N},+)

as required.
∎

Thus there exists a natural transformation between the left- and right- Collatz homomorphisms, whose unique component is simply the successor function.

3.3
A general setting, and future directions

It will not have escaped the reader’s attention that the identity isomorphism, Girard’s conjunction, and Definition
19
generalise to a countably infinite
{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
-indexed family of group homomorphisms.

Definition 26
.

For all
k>0
, we define
\mu_{(k)}:{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})^{\times k}\rightarrow{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
to be the injective group homomorphism given by, for all
F=(f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{k-1})\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})^{\times k}
,

\mu_{(k)}\left(F\right)(n)\ =\ k.f_{n\ (mod\ k)}\left(\frac{n-(n\ (mod\ k))}{k}\right)+(n\ (mod\ k))

giving the identity as
Id_{N}=\mu_{(1)}:{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})\rightarrow{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
, Girard’s conjunction as
(\_\star\_)=\mu_{(2)}:{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})\times{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})\rightarrow{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
, and
(\_\star\_\star\_)=\mu_{(3)}:{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})^{\times 3}\rightarrow{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
, …

Informally,
\mu_{(k)}\left(F\right)
replicates the action of each member of
\{f_{0},f_{1},\ldots,f_{k-1}\}
on the corresponding member of the exact covering system

\{k\mathbb{N}+j\}_{j=0..k-1}
.

Our claim – to be justified in
[
27
,
28
]
– is that this indexed family of operations generates a sub-operad of
Endo({\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}}))
isomorphic to the (free, formal) operad
RPT
of ‘rooted planar trees’, and this observation allows us to give commuting diagrams of congruential functions between arbitrary facets of associahedra of all dimensions.

In this setting, the original and reduced Collatz bijections
\rho,\lambda\in{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
are special, in that they label the
third
associahedron – which simply consists of two vertices and an edge – as follows :

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces((\bullet\bullet)\bullet)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\bullet\bullet\bullet)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\bullet(\bullet\bullet))}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\lambda}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\rho}

As we are also able to establish label-preserving embeddings
\mathcal{K}_{a}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{K}_{a+b}
, for all
a,b\in\mathbb{N}
, they may therefore be found in commuting diagrams over
{\mathcal{S}}({\mathbb{N}})
derived from arbitrary-dimensional associahedra.

A fuller description of this is found in
[
27
,
28
]
, based on the underlying algebra described in
[
29
]
.
