Well posedness for systems of self-propelled particles
Marc Briant, Nicolas Meunier

Abstract
This paper deals with the existence and uniqueness of solutions to kinetic equations describing alignment of self-propelled particles. The particularity of these models is that the velocity variable is not on the euclidean space but constrained on the unit sphere (the self-propulsion constraint). Two related equations are considered : the first one in which the alignment mechanism is nonlocal, using an observation kernel depending on the space variable, and a second form which is purely local, corresponding to the principal order of a scaling limit of the first one. We prove local existence and uniqueness of weak solutions in both cases for bounded initial conditions (in space and velocity) with finite total mass. The solution is proven to depend continuously on the initial data in L p L^{p} spaces with finite p. In the case of a bounded kernel of observation, we obtain that the solution is global in time. Finally by exploiting the fact that the second equation corresponds to the principal order of a scaling limit of the first one we deduce a Cauchy theory for an approximate problem approaching the second one.



Key words.
Vicsek model, Vicsek-Kolmogorov equation, collective dynamics, nonlinear Fokker-Planck equation on the sphere, normalized interaction kernels, well-posedness

35A01, 92B05

Introduction

In this work, we are interested in the study of two partial differential equations, which differ only by a spatial integral in the nonlinearity. We consider the following kinetic equation for the distribution function
f=f(t,x,\omega)
for
(t,x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
:

(1.1)

\partial_{t}f+\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(c\omega f\right)=\sigma\Delta_{\omega}f+\nu\nabla_{\omega}\cdot\left(f\mathbf{P}_{\omega^{\bot}}(\mathbf{F_{0}}[f])\right),

where
\nabla_{\omega}
,
\nabla_{\omega}\cdot
are the gradient and divergence operators on the sphere;
c>0
,
\nu>0
and
\sigma>0
are positive constants and
\mathbf{F_{0}}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbf{F_{0}}[f](t,x,v)

(1.2)

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbf{K}\left(\left|x-x_{*}\right|,(x_{*}-x)\cdot\omega,(x-x_{*})\cdot\omega_{*}\right)f(t,x_{*},\omega_{*})\>dx_{*}d\omega_{*},

and the interaction kernel
K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfies

(1.3)

K\in L^{1}_{x}W^{1,\infty}_{\omega}L^{1}_{\omega_{*}}.

Denote

(1.4)

\mathbf{k}(\omega,\omega_{*})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{K}\left(\left|x_{*}\right|,x_{*}\cdot\omega,-x_{*}\cdot\omega_{*}\right)\>dx_{*}\in W^{1,\infty}_{\omega}L^{1}_{\omega_{*}}.

The second kinetic equation for the distribution function
f=f(t,x,\omega)
for
(t,x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is:

(1.5)

\partial_{t}f+\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(c\omega f\right)=\sigma\Delta_{\omega}f+\nu\nabla_{\omega}\cdot\left(f\mathbf{P}_{\omega^{\bot}}(\mathbf{F_{1}}[f])\right),

with
\mathbf{F_{1}}
given by

(1.6)

\mathbf{F_{1}}[f](t,x,\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mathbf{k}(\omega,\omega_{*})f(t,x,\omega_{*})d\omega_{*}.

Note that the two kinetic equations (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) and (

)-(
1.6
) are related: in the first one, the alignment mechanism is nonlocal, using an observation kernel depending on the space variable, and the second form which is purely local, corresponds to the principal order of a scaling limit of the first one by using a rescaling
x^{\prime}=\varepsilon x
and changing
\sigma
and
\nu
in
\sigma/\varepsilon
and
\nu/\varepsilon
, where
\varepsilon
is a small space-time scaling parameter.

The typical example we have in mind for

and

(1.7)

\mathbf{k}(\omega,\omega_{*})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{K}\left(\left|x_{*}\right|,x_{*}\cdot\omega,-x_{*}\cdot\omega_{*}\right)\>dx_{*}=a\omega_{*}+b\omega_{*}\otimes\omega_{*}\>\omega,

where
a\geq 0
and
b\geq 0
.

This type of equations has been studied for different kinds of kernels
\mathbf{K}
and
\mathbf{k}
. As examples, we mention the Doi-Onsager seminal equation
[
19
,
9

, where

,
which is used in the study of rod-like polymer suspensions. There are also variations of the continuous Vicsek model, where

, which lead to the dipolar phase transition
[
12
]
or to more involved phase transitions
[
6
,
7
]
with the coefficients
\sigma
and

depending on the average velocity of the solution
\int_{\mathbb{S}}\omega_{*}f(\omega_{*})\>d\omega_{*}
. We also refer to
[

More recently, in
[

, a model showing nematic alignement properties has been informally studied.
Our problem therefore relates to the first moment issue
[
12
]
and the recent second moments
[
8
]
.

A rigorous Cauchy theory has been proven in the case
\mathbf{k}(\omega,\omega_{*})=\omega_{*}
in every Sobolev space

in the spatially homogeneous framework
[

]
. It appears that the proofs given in

.

2
The setting

In this part we justify the equations (
1.1
) and (
1.5
) we study here and we recall some formulas for the computation on the sphere.

2.1
Justification of the kinetic equations

The past fifteen years have seen an upsurge of studies, especially in physics, on active particles that have resulted in the apparition of the so-called field of active matter.

Active (or self-propelled) particle systems have the ability to explain the emergence of collective phenomena that occur, for example, in the collective behavior of animals, flocks of birds
[
15
]
, schools of fish
[
14
]
, pedestrian dynamics
[
18
]
, and microswimmers
[
4
]
.

The simplest interactions between self-propelled particles can have striking consequences at the collective level. For example, a simple pairwise alignment interaction between self-propelled particles, as introduced by Vicsek et al.
[
3
,
22
]
, can lead to large-scale collective clustering and motion in settings where long-range ordering and phase separation would be forbidden for systems in equilibrium
[
21
,
17
]
.

Let us now give more details about Vicsek’s model.
Consider
N
particles: particle
k
has position
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and velocity
v_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
. They satisfy the following stochastic differential equations:

(2.1)

\displaystyle dx_{k}=c\,\omega_{k}\,dt,

(2.2)

\displaystyle d\omega_{k}=\mathbf{P}_{\omega_{k}^{\perp}}\circ\big(\nu\,(\bar{\omega}_{k}-\omega_{k})\,dt+\sqrt{2\sigma}\,dB_{t}^{k}\big),

where
\mathbf{P}_{\omega^{\perp}}=(\text{Id}-\omega\otimes\omega)
is the orthogonal projection onto the orthogonal plane to
\omega
.

Let us briefly explain the meaning of each term in this system of equations. Equation (
2.1
) describes the transport of the particles: particle
k
moves in the orientation
\omega_{k}
at speed
c>0
. Equation (
2.2
) gives the evolution of the orientations over time. It includes two competing forces. On one hand the first term represents organized motion: particles try to adopt the same orientation. The constant
\nu>0
gives the relaxation speed. On the other hand
\left(B_{t}^{k}\right)_{k}
are N independent Brownian motions and they introduce noise in the dynamics, driving particles away from organized motion. The symbol “
\circ
” is used to specify that (
2.2
) has to be understood in the Stratonovich sense.

The k-th particle tends to align itself with the direction
\bar{\omega}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
defined by:

(2.3)

\displaystyle\bar{\omega}_{k}

\displaystyle=\alpha\sum_{\begin{subarray}{l}j,|x_{j}-x_{k}|<R\end{subarray}}\omega_{j},

The k-th particle thus aligns its velocity with neighboring cells.

Let us now give informally the mean-field limit as the number of agents
N\to\infty
of the system (
2.1
) - (
2.2
). To do so, we construct the empirical particle measure
f^{N}(t)
, given by

f^{N}(t)(x,\omega)=f^{N}(x,\omega,t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{(X_{i}(t),\omega_{i}(t))}(x,v),

such that
f^{N}(0)=f_{0}^{N}
. Then
f^{N}(t)
is a random measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
for all
t\geq 0
. For certain types of particle systems, see
[
1
]
e.g., it can be shown that, when
N\to\infty
,
f^{N}
converges to a deterministic function
f(x,\omega,t)
with
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
and
t>0
that satisfies a partial differential equation. In the case of (
2.3
), the same result is conjectured: the function
f(x,\omega,t)
satisfies the following mean field kinetic equation:

(2.4)

\partial_{t}f+\nabla_{x}\cdot(c\,\omega f)=\sigma\,\Delta_{\omega}f+\nu\nabla_{\omega}\cdot\Big(P_{\omega^{\perp}}\mathbf{F}[f]f\Big),

where
F
denotes the interaction force:

\mathbf{F}[f](x,\omega,t)=\int_{y\in B(x,R),w\in\mathbb{S}^{1}}w\,f(y,w,t)\,dydw.

Here, we consider the situation where the interaction can be described by a more general kernel than (
2.3
).

2.2
Useful formulas for the computation on the sphere

We recall here some formulas on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, which will be used in the sequel.

Let

f,g:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
be scalar-valued function. The following formula for integration by parts hold true, see
[
20
]
for details:

(2.5)

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f\nabla_{\omega}g\>d\omega=-\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}g\nabla_{\omega}f\>d\omega+(d-1)\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\omega fg\>d\omega.

Moreover, since
\nabla_{\omega}f
is a tangent vector-field, from the definition of the projection

\mathbf{P}_{\omega^{\perp}}
, we deduce that

(2.6)

\begin{cases}\mathbf{P}_{\omega^{\perp}}\omega&=0\\
\mathbf{P}_{\omega^{\perp}}\nabla_{\omega}f&=\nabla_{\omega}f.\end{cases}

Furthermore, for any constant vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

(2.7)

\begin{cases}\nabla_{\omega}(\omega\cdot v)&=\mathbf{P}_{\omega^{\perp}}v\\
\nabla_{\omega}\cdot\left(\mathbf{P}_{\omega^{\perp}}v\right)&=-(d-1)\omega\cdot v.\end{cases}

3
Cauchy theory for a linear Fokker-Planck equation

In order to deal with the non-linearity
\mathbf{F_{0}}[f]
or
\mathbf{F_{1}}[f]
we first establish a
L^{\infty}_{x,\omega}
Cauchy theory to an associated linear problem.
To that purpose we give the following existence result. It is standard in evolution parabolic equation (see for instance
[
10
, Section 7.1]
) and we refer specifically here to a result by Degond
[
5
, Appendix A]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
u_{0}
be in
L^{2}_{x,\omega}
and
\mathbf{F}
in
L^{\infty}_{t,x}W^{1,\infty}_{\omega}
. There
exists a unique
u
in the space

Y=\left\{u\in L^{2}_{t,x}H^{1}_{\omega},\quad\partial_{t}u+\omega\cdot\nabla_{x}u\in L^{2}_{t,x}H^{-1}_{\omega}\right\},

to the linear diffential equation, for all
(t,x,\omega)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
:

(3.1)

\partial_{t}u+\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(c\omega u\right)=\sigma\Delta_{\omega}u+\nu\nabla_{\omega}\cdot\left(u\mathbf{P}_{\omega^{\bot}}\mathbf{F}\right).

Moreover, if
u_{0}
is non-negative then
u
is non-negative.

Note that in
[
5
, Appendix A]
, the velocity space is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the Fokker-Planck equation is not written with zeroth order terms. However, their proof relies on an abstract result by Lions
[
16
]
and on a modification of the equation that includes a zeroth order term, that are directly applicable in our context. Indeed the only difference would come from the integration by parts with the tangential derivative
\nabla_{\omega}
that generates additional terms, see (
2.5
),
in the weak formulation. However, the latter terms vanish as we are looking at the orthogonal part of
\mathbf{F}
compared to
\omega
.

As we discussed above, we would like a
L^{\infty}_{x,\omega}
version of the theorem above. We shall thus prove the following result that is a generalized version of
[
13
, Lemma 3.1]
(note that the
L^{1}
-norm is not accessible). The Lemma
3.2
could be indeed adapted for any
p>1
(up to changing the constants), as the choice of 3/2 is arbitrary (between 1 and 2).

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
2\leq p<\infty
,
u_{0}
be in
L^{p}_{x,\omega}
and
\mathbf{F}
in
L^{\infty}_{t,x}W^{1,\infty}_{\omega}
. The solution
u
to the linear equation (
3.1
) constructed in Theorem
3.1
is in
L^{\infty}_{t}L^{p}_{x,\omega}
and gains regularity in
\omega
since it satisfies

\forall t\geq 0,\quad\left\|u(t)\right\|_{L^{p}_{x,\omega}}\leq\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{p}_{x,\omega}}\exp\left(C_{\infty}(\mathbf{F})t\right),

and

\int_{0}^{t}\left\|u^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\nabla_{\omega}u(s)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x,\omega}}ds\leq\frac{1}{p-\frac{3}{2}}\left\|u_{0}\right\|^{p}_{L^{p}_{x,\omega}}\exp\left(p\,C_{\infty}(\mathbf{F})t\right),

where
C_{\infty}(\mathbf{F})=\nu\left\|\mathbf{F}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}W^{1,\infty}_{v}}\left(1+\frac{\nu}{\sigma}\left\|\mathbf{F}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x,\omega}}\right)
.

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

Let
u_{0}
be in
L^{p}_{x,\omega}
for
p\geq 2
, thus
u_{0}
belongs
L^{2}_{x,\omega}
so we can use Theorem
3.1
and construct
u
solution to (
3.1
). We compute by integrating by parts

(3.2)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{p}\frac{d}{dt}\left\|u(t)\right\|^{p}_{L^{p}_{x,\omega}}=&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mbox{sgn}(u(t,x,v))\left|u(t,x,v)\right|^{p-1}\partial_{t}u(t,x,\omega)\>dxd\omega\\
=&-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mbox{sgn}(u)\left|u\right|^{p-1}\nabla_{x}(c\omega u)\>dxd\omega\\
&\quad-\sigma\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\nabla_{\omega}\left(\mbox{sgn}(u)\left|u\right|^{p-1}\right)\cdot\nabla_{\omega}u\>dxd\omega\\
&\qquad+\nu\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\mbox{sgn}(u)\left|u\right|^{p-1}\nabla_{\omega}\cdot\left(u\mathbf{P}_{\omega^{\bot}}\mathbf{F}\right)\>dxd\omega.\end{split}

The integration by parts does not generate additional terms since the integrand,
\nabla_{\omega}
,
is orthogonal to
\omega
.

Since
\mbox{sgn}(u)\left|u\right|^{p-1}\nabla_{x}(c\omega u)=\nabla_{x}(c\omega\left|u\right|^{p})
, the first term on the right-hand side in (
3.2
) is zero. Also, we see that

\nabla_{\omega}\left(\mbox{sgn}(u)\left|u\right|^{p-1}\right)\cdot\nabla_{\omega}u=(p-1)\left|u\right|^{p-2}\left(\nabla_{\omega}u\right)^{2},

and

\left|\left|u\right|^{p-1}\nabla_{\omega}\cdot\left(u\mathbf{P}_{\omega^{\bot}}\mathbf{F}\right)\right|\leq\left|u\right|^{p}\left\|\mathbf{F}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}W^{1,\infty}_{\omega}}+\left|u\right|^{p-1}\left|\nabla_{\omega}u\right|\left\|\mathbf{F}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x,\omega}}.

Using Cauchy-Schwarz on the third term on the right-hand side in (
3.2
), we end up with

(3.3)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{p}\frac{d}{dt}\left\|u(t)\right\|^{p}_{L^{p}_{x,\omega}}&\leq-(p-1)\sigma\left\|u^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\nabla_{\omega}u\right\|_{L^{2}_{x,\omega}}^{2}+\nu\left\|\mathbf{F}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}W^{1,\infty}_{\omega}}\left\|u(t)\right\|^{p}_{L^{p}_{x,\omega}}\\
&\qquad+\nu\left\|\mathbf{F}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x,\omega}}\left\|u\right\|_{L^{p}_{x,\omega}}^{\frac{p}{2}}\left\|u^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\nabla_{\omega}u\right\|_{L^{2}_{x,\omega}}\\
&\leq-\left((p-1)\sigma-\frac{\eta\nu\left\|\mathbf{F}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x,v}}}{2}\right)\left\|u^{\frac{p-2}{2}}\nabla_{\omega}u\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x,\omega}}\\
&\qquad+\nu\left\|\mathbf{F}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}W^{1,\infty}_{\omega}}\left(1+\eta^{-1}\right)\left\|u(t)\right\|^{p}_{L^{p}_{x,\omega}},\end{split}

for any
\eta>0
.

Choosing
\eta
sufficiently small such that
\frac{\sigma}{2}-\frac{\eta}{2}\nu\left\|\mathbf{F}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x,v}}\geq 0
we can apply Grönwall lemma and for all

get

\begin{split}\left\|u(t)\right\|^{p}_{L^{p}_{x,\omega}}+&\left(p-\frac{3}{2}\right)\sigma\int_{0}^{t}\left\|u^{\frac{p-2}{2}}(s)\nabla_{\omega}u(s)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}_{x,\omega}}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\exp\left(p\nu\left\|\mathbf{F}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}W^{1,\infty}_{v}}(1+\eta^{-1})(t-s)\right)ds\\
&\leq\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{p}_{x,\omega}}^{p}\exp\left(p\nu\left\|\mathbf{F}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}W^{1,\infty}_{v}}(1+\eta^{-1})t\right).\end{split}

The latter is exactly Proposition
3.3
for
p
in
[2,+\infty)
.
∎

\begin{split}&\left|\mathbf{P_{\omega^{\bot}}}\left(\mathbf{F_{0}}[u^{(n)}]\right)(s,x,\omega)\right|\\
&\qquad\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left|\mathbf{P_{\omega^{\bot}}}\left(\mathbf{K}\right)(x,\omega,x_{*},\omega_{*})\right|u^{(n)}(s,x_{*},\omega_{*})\>dx_{*}d\omega_{*}\\
&\qquad\leq\left\|\mathbf{K}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,\omega,x_{*},\omega_{*}}}M_{1}\end{split}

which is time independent. Hence, there exists
C
independent of time and
R
such that

\begin{split}&\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}u^{(n+1)}(t,x,\omega)\chi_{R}(x,\omega)dxd\omega-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}u_{0}(x,\omega)\chi_{R}(x,\omega)dxd\omega\right|\\
&\quad\quad\quad\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B(0,R+1)\backslash B(0,R)}\left|u^{(n+1)}\right|dxd\omega.\end{split}

Thanks to the weak-* convergence of
\left(u^{(n)}\right)_{n\in\mathbf{N}}
towards
u
on a given
[0,T]
(up to a subsequence, see Step 2 of the proof of Theorem
1.1
) we can take the limit as
n
goes to
+\infty
and obtain

\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}u\>\chi_{R}\>dxd\omega-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}u_{0}\>\chi_{R}\>dxd\omega\right|\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\int_{B(0,R+1)\backslash B(0,R)}u\>dxd\omega.

To conclude we first invoke the dominated convergence theorem (
u
and
u_{0}
are positive and integrable and
\chi_{R}
is bounded by one) to take the limit
R
goes to
+\infty
and see that the left-hand side converges to
\left|\int u-\int u_{0}\right|
. For the right-hand side we also invoke the dominated convergence theorem to see that it tends to
0
as
R
tends to
+\infty
, because
u(t)
is integrable. At last we showed that

\forall t\in[0,T],\>\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}u(t,x,\omega)dxd\omega=M_{1}.

Step 2: Global solution and estimate.
In Step 1 we built a solution to (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) on
[0,T_{\max,0})
and thanks to the uniqueness of Proposition
4.1
it follows that the solution associated to
u_{0}
on
[0,T]
preserves the mass. Let us now prove that
T_{\max,0}=+\infty
.

Using Proposition
3.3
we have the following implicit control over
u
:

\forall t\in[0,T_{\max,0}),\quad\left\|u(t)\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,\omega}}\leq\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,\omega}}e^{C_{\infty}\left(\mathbf{P_{\omega^{\bot}}}(\mathbf{F_{0}(u)})\right)t},

where

\displaystyle C_{\infty}\left(\mathbf{P_{\omega^{\bot}}}(\mathbf{F_{0}(u)})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nu\left\|\mathbf{P_{\omega^{\bot}}}(\mathbf{F_{0}(u)})\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x}W^{1,\infty}_{\omega}}\left(1+\frac{\nu}{\sigma}\left\|\mathbf{P_{\omega^{\bot}}}(\mathbf{F_{0}(u)})\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x,\omega}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\nu\left\|\mathbf{K}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,x_{*},\omega_{*}}W^{1,\infty}_{\omega}}M_{1}\left(1+\frac{\nu}{\sigma}\left\|\mathbf{K}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,x_{*},\omega_{*},\omega}}M_{1}\right).

The estimate directly comes from (
5.1
) applied to
\mathbf{K}
and
\nabla_{\omega}\mathbf{K}
. We thus see that we have a constant
C_{\infty}>0
only depending on the parameter of the subject :
\nu
,
\sigma
,
\mathbf{K}
and
M_{1}
such that

\forall t\in[0,T_{\max,0}),\quad\left\|u(t)\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,\omega}}\leq\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,\omega}}e^{C_{\infty}t}.

This prevents
u
from
\lim\limits_{t\to T_{\max,0}^{-}}\left\|u(t)\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x,\omega}}=+\infty
unless
T_{\max,0}=+\infty
. The solution
u
is therefore globally defined in time.

6
Proof of Proposition
1.3

First of all, since
u
is a weak solution to (
1.1
)-(
1.2
),
u^{(\varepsilon)}
is a weak solution to

\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}+\nabla_{x}\cdot\left(c\omega u^{\varepsilon}\right)=\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\Delta_{\omega}u^{\varepsilon}+\frac{\nu}{\varepsilon}\nabla_{\omega}\cdot\left[u^{\varepsilon}\mathbf{P}_{\omega^{\bot}}(\mathbf{F_{0}}[u])\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon},\frac{x}{\varepsilon},\omega\right)\right].

The result will hold true if

R_{\varepsilon}(t,x,\omega)=\nabla_{\omega}\cdot\left[u^{\varepsilon}\mathbf{P}_{\omega^{\bot}}(\mathbf{F_{0}}[u])\left(\frac{t}{\varepsilon},\frac{x}{\varepsilon},\omega\right)\right]-\nabla_{\omega}\cdot\left[u^{\varepsilon}\mathbf{P}_{\omega^{\bot}}(\mathbf{F_{1}}[u^{\varepsilon}])\right]

satisfies the desired estimate.

Take a function
\phi
in
C^{\infty}_{c}([0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and, using integration by parts, compute

\begin{split}&\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}R_{\varepsilon}(s,x,\omega)\phi(s,x,\omega)dsdxd\omega\right|\\
&\quad\quad\quad=\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}u^{\varepsilon}\nabla_{\omega}\phi\cdot\left[\mathbf{P}_{\omega^{\bot}}(\mathbf{F_{0}}[u])\left(\frac{s}{\varepsilon},\frac{x}{\varepsilon},\omega\right)-\mathbf{P}_{\omega^{\bot}}(\mathbf{F_{1}}[u^{\varepsilon}])\right]dsdxd\omega\right|\\
&\quad\quad\quad\leq\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}u^{\varepsilon}\left|\nabla_{\omega}\phi\right|\left|\mathbf{F_{0}}[u]\left(\frac{s}{\varepsilon},\frac{x}{\varepsilon},\omega\right)-\mathbf{F_{1}}[u^{\varepsilon}]\right|dsdxd\omega.\end{split}

We thus study

\displaystyle\left|\mathbf{F_{0}}[u]\left(\frac{s}{\varepsilon},\frac{x}{\varepsilon},\omega\right)-\mathbf{F_{1}}[u^{\varepsilon}])\right|

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left[\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{K}\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon}-x_{*},\omega,\omega_{*}\right)u^{\varepsilon}\left(s,\varepsilon x_{*},\omega_{*}\right)dx_{*}\right)-\mathbf{k}(\omega,\omega_{*})u^{\varepsilon}(s,x,\omega_{*})\right]d\omega_{*}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left[\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{K}\left(x_{*},\omega,\omega_{*}\right)u^{\varepsilon}\left(s,x-\varepsilon x_{*},\omega_{*}\right)dx_{*}\right)-\mathbf{k}(\omega,\omega_{*})u^{\varepsilon}(s,x,\omega_{*})\right]d\omega_{*}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbf{K}\left(x_{*},\omega,\omega_{*}\right)\left[u^{\varepsilon}\left(s,x-\varepsilon x_{*},\omega_{*}\right)-u^{\varepsilon}(s,x,\omega_{*})\right]dx_{*}.

We emphasize that this is the first time where we actually use the property that
\mathbf{F_{0}}
is a convolution operator in the spatial variable, see (
1.2
).

Now,
\phi(t,x,\omega)
and
\mathbf{K}(x_{*},\omega,\omega_{*})
are of compact support in respectively
x
and
x_{*}
, independently of
t
,
\omega
and
\omega_{*}
. Let us so fix
R>0
such that

\mbox{Supp}(\phi(t,\cdot,\omega))\subset B_{x}(0,R)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mbox{Supp}(\mathbf{K}(\cdot,\omega,\omega_{*}))\subset B_{x_{*}}(0,R).

Thanks to the previous computations we obtain

\begin{split}&\left|\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}R_{\varepsilon}(s,x,\omega)\phi(s,x,\omega)dsdxd\omega\right|\\
&\leq C_{\phi,u^{\varepsilon}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times B_{x}(0,R)\times B_{x_{*}}(0,R)}\left|u^{\varepsilon}(s,x-\varepsilon x_{*},\omega_{*})-u^{\varepsilon}(s,x,\omega_{*})\right|dsdxdx_{*}d\omega d\omega_{*}.\end{split}

where
C_{\phi,u^{\varepsilon}}=\left|S^{d-1}\right|\left\|\mathbf{K}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{x_{*},\omega,\omega_{*}}}\left\|u^{\varepsilon}\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x,\omega}}\left\|\phi\right\|_{L^{\infty}_{t,x,\omega}}=O(\varepsilon)
since
u^{\varepsilon}
is
C^{1}
by using the finite increments.
