\Abstract
Gaussian processes (GPs) are well-known tools for modeling dependent data with applications in spatial statistics, time series analysis, or econometrics. In this article, we present the
\proglang
R package
\pkg
varycoef that implements estimation, prediction, and variable selection of linear models with spatially varying coefficients (SVC) defined by GPs, so called GP-based SVC models. Such models offer a high degree of flexibility while being relatively easy to interpret. Using
\pkg
varycoef, we show versatile applications of (spatially) varying coefficient models on spatial and time series data. This includes model and coefficient estimation with predictions and variable selection. The package uses state-of-the-art computational statistics techniques like parallelization, model-based optimization, and covariance tapering. This allows the user to work with (S)VC models in a computationally efficient manner, i.e., model estimation on large data sets is possible in a feasible amount of time.

\Keywords
covariance tapering, dependent data, model-based optimization, spatial statistics, (penalized) maximum likelihood estimation, variable selection

\Plainkeywords
covariance tapering, dependent data, model-based optimization, spatial statistics, (penalized) maximum likelihood estimation, variable selection

Introduction

Spatially varying coefficients (SVC) provide a flexible and interpretable approach to extend linear models to spatial data. There are various methodologies on how to estimate and make predictions for SVC models. To name a few, geographically weighted regression (GWR) introduced by
Brunsdon et al. 1998
or Bayesian SVC processes by
Gelfand et al. 2003
are popular examples. Today, there are several
\proglang
R packages and other software implementations for SVC modeling available; each with individual focuses in their respective framework. A thorough comparison between all of them is beyond the scope of this work. Instead, we provide a rough outline of existing software implementations.

In
\proglang
R
(
\proglangR Core Team 2021
)
, GWR has been implemented in packages like
\pkg
GWmodel
(
Gollini et al. 2015
)
,
\pkg
spgwr
(
Bivand and Yu 2017
)
, and
\pkg
gwrr
(
Wheeler 2013
)
. A detailed comparison between those packages can be found in
Gollini et al. 2015
. Bayesian SVC modeling is implemented in the
\proglang
R packages
\pkg
spTDyn
(
Bakar et al. 2016
)
and
\pkg
spBayes
(
Finley et al. 2015
;
Finley and Banerjee 2020
)
. Both packages use Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling algorithms and are rather restricted in the number of observation locations. Another Bayesian method to estimate SVC models uses the explicit stochastic partial differential equation (SPDE,
Lindgren et al. 2011
) link between Gaussian fields and Gaussian Markov random fields (GMRF,
Rue and Held 2005
). Using integrated nested Laplace approximation (INLA) implemented in the
\proglang
R package
\pkg
INLA
(
Lindgren and Rue 2015
)
, one can estimate SVC models for data sets with a large number of observations. However, the number of hyper parameters and therefore varying coefficients is limited
(
Rue et al. 2017
)
. Finally, spatially varying coefficients can be modeled using splines. Available options include the packages
\pkg
mgcv
(
Wood 2017
)
and
\pkg
mboost
(
Hothorn et al. 2021
)
. In other programming languages some of the above mentioned methodologies are available, too. For instance, the
\proglang
Python spatial analysis library
\pkg
PySAL
(
Rey and Anselin 2010
)
implements Bayesian SVC processes by
Gelfand et al. 2003
. GWR is also available in
\pkg
PySAL and geographic information system (GIS) software like
\proglang
ArcGIS or
\proglang
GRASS.

This article discusses SVC models where each coefficient is defined by a Gaussian process (GP,
Rasmussen and Williams 2005
), so called GP-based SVC models. The proposed model is similar to Bayesian SVC processes by
Gelfand et al. 2003
, but with some specific assumptions on the model, we can provide a computationally efficient way of estimating GP-based SVC models using a classical maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) approach
(
Dambon et al. 2021b
)
. Therefore, in contrast to all of the methodologies and software implementations above, the
\proglang
R package
\pkg
varycoef implements a frequentist approach for SVC modeling using Gaussian processes. Additionally, while all of the above SVC modeling implementations from above are either limited in the number of observations or spatially varying coefficients,
\pkg
varycoef has been developed for to work well with large data sets or a moderate number of varying coefficients. Over time, the methodology as well as the corresponding
\proglang
R package
\pkg
varycoef have been augmented continuously. For instance, the package now implements a joint variable selection procedure for GP-based SVC models
(
Dambon et al. 2021a
)
using penalized maximum likelihood estimation (PMLE). Another new feature of
\pkg
varycoef is the support of different types of covariance functions and the idea of SVC models has been generalized to work different types of dependent data such as time series. The goal of this article is to present the current state of the package
\pkg
varycoef with its versatile applications.

The rest of this article is structured as follows. In Section
2
we introduce GP-based SVC models in their original form to be used by
\pkg
varycoef. Section
3
covers the MLE of GP-based SVC models including prediction methods. The variable selection using PMLE is discussed and showcased in Section
4
. In particular, we move from a classical application using spatial data and show an application on time series data. Section
5
summarizes this work.

2
GP-based SVC Models

We commence with a formal introduction of Gaussian processes before extending the linear regression models to GP-based SVC models.

2.1
Gaussian Processes

Gaussian processes are widely used for modeling dependency structures. Applications can be found in – but are not limited to – spatial statistics
(
Gelfand and Schliep 2016
;
Banerjee et al. 2008
;
Datta et al. 2016
)
, econometrics
(
Wu et al. 2014
)
, and time series modeling
(
Roberts et al. 2013
)
. Similarly to a normal distribution, a GP is defined as an infinite-dimensional process with a mean function
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
and a covariance function
c
,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mu}}(\cdot)

\displaystyle:D\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle c(\cdot,\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{})

\displaystyle:D\times D\rightarrow\left[0,\infty\right),

for some domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d},d\geq 1
, and covariance parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}
. In this work, we restrict ourselves to constant mean functions and isotropic covariance functions. That is, the covariance function is only depending on the distance of its arguments
u=|\!|{\textbf{{s}}}-{\textbf{{s}}}^{\prime}|\!|
, where
|\!|\cdot|\!|
denotes the Euclidean distance and
{\textbf{{s}}},{\textbf{{s}}}^{\prime}\in D
.

Popular examples of covariance functions are given by the Matérn or generalized Wendland covariance class. In the isotropic case, former one is defined as

\displaystyle c:\left[0,\infty\right)

\displaystyle\to\left[0,\infty\right),

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle\mapsto\sigma^{2}\frac{2^{1-\nu}}{\Gamma(\nu)}\left(\sqrt{2\nu}\frac{u}{\rho}\right)^{\nu}K_{\nu}\left(\sqrt{2\nu}\frac{u}{\rho}\right),

(1)

where
\sigma^{2}\geq 0
is the variance,
\rho>0
is the range,
\nu>0
is the smoothness, and
K_{\nu}
is the modified Bessel function of second kind and order
\nu
. Setting the smoothness
\nu
to some specific values simplifies the bulky formula of (
1
) to simple functions like the exponential
c(u)=\sigma^{2}\exp(-u/\rho)
for
\nu=1/2
or the squared exponential
c(u)=\sigma^{2}\exp(-u^{2}/\rho)
for
\nu=\infty
. We let the definition of the covariance function up to the user and only assume that the covariance parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}:=(\rho,\sigma^{2})
are unknown. Both are essential to interpret the estimated Gaussian process as the range
\rho
provides a measure of spatial dependence and the variance
\sigma^{2}
gives the volatility of the Gaussian process. We provide examples of Gaussian processes defined by zero-means and different covariance functions of Matérn class. They are given on
[0,10]\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, i.e., in
d=1
dimension. Throughout this article as well as in
\pkg
varycoef, we use the package
\pkg
RandomFields
(
Schlather et al. 2015
)
to sample Gaussian processes. The two sampled processes are depicted in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
Two zero-mean Gaussian processes with Matérn covariance functions and distinct parametrizations. The observation location
{\textbf{{s}}}_{i}
are defined equidistantly on
[0,10]\subset{\mathbb{R}}
with distance 0.01, i.e.,
n=1001
. One can clearly see the effects of the parameters
\nu,\rho
, and
\sigma^{2}
on the smoothness, the range of dependence, and the variance of the curves, respectively.

2.2
Spatially Varying Coefficient Models

Let
n
be the number of observations and let
p
be the number covariates given by
{\textbf{{x}}}^{(j)}:=(x_{1}^{(j)},...,x_{n}^{(j)})\in\mathbb{R}^{n},j=1,...,p
. With the responses
{\textbf{{y}}}=(y_{1},...,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, errors
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}:=(\varepsilon_{1},...,\varepsilon_{n})\sim{\cal{N}}_{n}({\mathbf{0}},\tau^{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{n})
where
\tau>0
, and coefficients
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=(\beta_{1},...,\beta_{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
the linear model is given by:

\displaystyle y_{i}=\beta_{1}x_{i}^{(1)}+...+\beta_{p}x_{i}^{(p)}+\varepsilon_{i}.

Spatially varying coefficient models generalize the linear model. In a classical context of regressing spatial data sets, one considers the observation locations
{\textbf{{s}}}_{i}\in D
that are associated to each observed sample
i
. Here, one usually assumes
d=2
and that the observation locations
{\textbf{{s}}}_{i}
do not necessarily have to be distinct. A general SVC model is then given by:

\displaystyle y_{i}=\beta_{1}({\textbf{{s}}}_{i})x_{i}^{(1)}+...+\beta_{p}({\textbf{{s}}}_{i})x_{i}^{(p)}+\varepsilon_{i}.

It is a this point where the above mentioned methodologies to estimate the SVC model differ depending on the assumption of the coefficients
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}(\cdot)
. In our case, we assume fixed effects
\mu_{j}
and random effects
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{j}(\cdot)
defined by zero-mean Gaussian processes with an isotropic covariance function
c_{j}(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}_{j})
to model the spatial structures of the coefficients, i.e, we have
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}(\cdot)\approx\mu_{j}+{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{j}(\cdot)
. Additionally, we assume prior mutual independence between all
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{j}(\cdot)
.

For a finite set of observations
{\textbf{{s}}}=({\textbf{{s}}}_{1},...,{\textbf{{s}}}_{n})
the Gaussian processes
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{j}(\cdot)
from above reduce to zero-mean normal distributions. Therefore, we can write the GP-based SVC model as a linear mixed model:

\displaystyle{\textbf{{y}}}={\mathbf{X}}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}+{\mathbf{W}}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}({\textbf{{s}}})+{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}.

(2)

The full derivation of (
2
) is given in
Dambon et al. 2021b
. In the resulting model, the first term on the right hand side is the data matrix
{\mathbf{X}}=\bigl({\textbf{{x}}}^{(1)}|...|{\textbf{{x}}}^{(p)}\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
associated with the fixed effects
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=(\mu_{1},...,\mu_{p})
. The random effects and its corresponding covariates are given in the second term. Not every fixed effect covariate
{\textbf{{x}}}^{(j)}
has to be associated with a random effect or vice versa. Therefore, we denote the
q
random effect covariates by
{\textbf{{w}}}^{(k)}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
for
k=1,...,q
and the data matrix
{\mathbf{W}}=\bigl(\diag{\textbf{{w}}}^{(1)}|...|\diag{\textbf{{w}}}^{(q)}\bigr)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{q\times nq}
. The random effects are contained in
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}({\textbf{{s}}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{nq}
which is the sole component modeling the spatially varying relationship depending on the locations
s
. Individual zero-mean spatially varying coefficients are defined as
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{k}(\cdot)\sim{\cal{G}}{\cal{P}}\bigl(0,c_{k}(\cdot;{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}_{k})\bigr)
. For observation locations
s
, they reduce to a normal distribution with
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{k}({\textbf{{s}}})\sim{\cal{N}}_{n}({\mathbf{0}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}_{k})
, where
\bigl({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}_{k}\bigr)_{lm}:=c_{k}(|\!|{\textbf{{s}}}_{l}-{\textbf{{s}}}_{m}|\!|;{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}_{k})
. The random effect
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}({\textbf{{s}}})
is the joint effect over all individual Gaussian processes, i.e.,
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}({\textbf{{s}}})=\bigl({\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{1}({\textbf{{s}}}),...,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}_{q}({\textbf{{s}}})\bigr)\sim{\cal{N}}_{nq}({\mathbf{0}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{})
with joint block covariance matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}=\diag({\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}_{1},...,{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}_{q})
. Finally, we add the errors
{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
, also called the nugget in spatial modeling.

Inspired by the example in Figure
1
, we sample data under the assumption of an GP-based SVC model. The package
\pkg
varycoef provides the function
\code
sample_fullSVC() to sample data from a GP-based SVC model with observations on the real line, i.e.,
d=1
. In total we consider
n=300
observations with i.i.d. locations
s_{i}\sim{\cal{U}}\bigl([0,10]\bigr)
. It is called a full SVC model since each covariate is associated with a spatially varying coefficient, i.e.,
p=q
and
{\textbf{{x}}}^{(j)}={\textbf{{w}}}^{(j)}
. Here,
\code
sample_fullSVC() gives an intercept
{\textbf{{x}}}^{(1)}={\mathbf{1}}_{n}
and
{\textbf{{x}}}^{(2)}\sim{\cal{N}}_{n}({\mathbf{0}}_{n},{\mathbf{I}}_{n\times n})
. The mean and covariance parameters are provided in the code below. The resulting data, i.e., the response
y
and the covariate
{\textbf{{x}}}^{(2)}
are depicted in Figure
2
.

2.3
Optimization of Likelihood

Our goal is to estimate the parameters of interest
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}:=({\boldsymbol{\theta}}{},{\boldsymbol{\mu}})
with all covariance parameters given by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}=(\rho_{1},\sigma_{1}^{2},...,\rho_{q},\sigma^{2}_{q},\tau^{2})
. We rely on maximum likelihood estimation, where the multivariate normal distribution and corresponding log-likelihood of the GP-based SVC model (
2
) is given by:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Y}}

\displaystyle\sim{\cal{N}}_{n}\left({\mathbf{X}}{\boldsymbol{\mu}},{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}_{\mathbf{Y}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}):=\sum_{k=1}^{q}\left({\textbf{{w}}}^{(k)}{{\textbf{{w}}}^{(k)}}^{\top}\right)\odot{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}_{k}+\tau^{2}{\mathbf{I}}_{n\times n}\right),

(3)

\displaystyle\ell({\boldsymbol{\omega}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(n\log(2\pi)+\log\det{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}_{{\mathbf{Y}}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}{})+\left({\textbf{{y}}}-{\mathbf{X}}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right)^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}_{{\mathbf{Y}}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}{})^{-1}\left({\textbf{{y}}}-{\mathbf{X}}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\right)\right).

(4)

Control Parameters

\code

c_{k}
used to define

\code

c_{k}

and only has the range

3.1.2
Profile Likelihood

The parameter of interest
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
is of dimension
p+2q+1
. For numeric optimization, such a high dimensional parameter space tends to have numerical instabilities. In order to reduce the computational burden, one can concentrate the log likelihood and optimize on the profile likelihood. From (
3
), we can see that
{\mathbf{Y}}
has the form of a generalized linear model. The mean effect parameter
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
is a nuisance parameter, which can explicitly be defined by the covariance parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}
, i.e.:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mu}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}{})=\left({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}_{{\mathbf{Y}}}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}){\mathbf{X}}\right)^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}_{{\mathbf{Y}}}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}){\textbf{{y}}}.

Therefore, the optimization can be expressed as only depending on the covariance parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}
or, in other words, being expressed by an isometric profile of the full likelihood. The binary argument
\code
profileLik of
\code
SVC_mle_control() toggles if the optimization is to be computed on the profile likelihood, or not.

3.1.3
Initial Values and Boundary Conditions

The numeric optimization – both over the profile likelihood and the full likelihood – requires providing initial values
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{(init)}\in{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
. If not otherwise specified, we provide the following initial values. With respect to the mean parameters, we use the results from an ordinary least squares
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}(\text{OLS})
of the linear model
{\textbf{{y}}}={\mathbf{X}}{\boldsymbol{\mu}}+{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
. Concerning the covariance parameters, we use the median recorded distance between observations
\delta
and the empirical variance of the response
y
denoted by
s_{\textbf{{y}}}^{2}
. In particular, we set the initial values for the range to
\delta/4
and for the variance of the Gaussian processes (including the nugget) to
s_{\textbf{{y}}}^{2}/(q+1)
. Another possibility to set the initial values of the latent Gaussian processes is to first apply a GWR. With the estimated coefficients we are able to compute the semivariograms and to deduct the respective covariance parameters. Latter method is currently not implemented.

To ensure that the covariance matrix
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}_{\mathbf{Y}}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}{})
is well-defined and positive-definite, we require and check that
\sigma^{2}_{k}\geq 0,\rho_{k}>0
, and
\tau^{2}>0
for all
k=1,...,q
. Additionally, the “L-BFGS-B” optimization by
Byrd et al. 1995
allows lower bounds
{\textbf{{l}}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
and upper bounds
{\textbf{{u}}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
on the parameter of interest
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\in{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
, i.e.,
{\textbf{{l}}}\leq{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\leq{\textbf{{u}}}
. These bounds can benefit the stability of the numeric optimization, but have to be chosen carefully. For instance, if the range parameter
\rho_{k}
of a covariance function is much larger than the diameter of the (observed) domain
D
, there is little to say about the corresponding Gaussian process. In fact, one could consider the coefficient to be non varying at all. On the other hand, if such upper bound is set too low, it hinders the optimization to obtain the best estimate. We can argue similarly for the lower bound. If a range parameter
\rho_{k}
becomes too small, the estimated Gaussian process starts to behave like a nugget and an appropriately chosen lower bound
l
could prevent this. To summarize, we give the default initial values and boundaries for each type of parameter in Table

3.1.4
Covariance Tapering

Introduced by
Furrer et al. 2006
, covariance tapering is a technique to tackle the “big
n
problem”, which arises from a large number of observations
n

(
Heaton et al. 2019
;
Lasinio et al. 2013
)
. In particular, for
n>
10’000 computers struggle to calculate the determinant or solving a linear system of
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}_{{\mathbf{Y}}}
in a feasible time. Here, covariance tapering is a pragmatic approach which makes the evaluation of such calculations – and therefore of the log likelihood (
4
) – time efficient by inducing a sparse matrix structure.

Therefore, the original covariance function
c
is multiplied with another, suitable covariance function
c^{\star}
with a sufficiently small support, i.e.,
c^{\star}(u)=0
for all
u
greater than some taper distance
\rho_{\star}>0
. The covariance function
c^{\star}
has to be chosen in accordance with the original covariance function
c
, see
Furrer et al. 2006
for details. The tapered covariance function
c^{(tap)}(u):=c(u)c^{\star}(u)
maintains most of its original characteristics, like the order of differentiability at the origin. Due to the small support of
c^{(tap)}(u)
, the induced covariance matrix becomes sparse and can be stored efficiently. Further, there exist optimized algorithms to take advantage of the sparse matrix structure, e.g., the Cholesky decomposition by
Ng and Peyton 1993
implemented in the
\proglang
R package
\pkg
spam
(
Furrer and Sain 2010
)
. The optimization of the likelihood is either executed on matrices without covariance tapering or on sparse matrices of class ‘
\code
spam’, where covariance tapering has been applied. Here, the
\code
SVC_mle_control() argument
\code
tapering triggers covariance tapering. By default, it is set to
\code
NULL and no covariance tapering is applied. If a positive scalar is provided, it defines the taper range
\rho_{\star}
and therefore the sparsity structure of the covariance matrix.

{Schunk}
{Sinput}

R> # setting the taper range to distance 5 in the units of the locations
R> SVC_mle_control(tapering = 5)

3.1.5
Parallelized Optimization

The optimization of the log likelihood function (
4
) is implemented with the
\proglang
R function
\code
optim(), specifically the “L-BFGS-B” quasi-Newton gradient method
(
Byrd et al. 1995
)
. In each iteration step
\iota
,
\code
optim() sequentially evaluates the objective function
fn
several times. Let
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{(\iota)}\in{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
be the current parameter value. First
\code
optim() evaluates
fn\bigl({\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{(\iota)}\bigr)
and then approximates its gradient
gr
at
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{(\iota)}
by evaluating
fn\bigl({\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{(\iota)}+{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{p}\bigr)
for some
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}_{p}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{|{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}|},p=1,...,P
. The number of evaluations
P
to approximate the gradient generally increases with the dimension of the parameter space
{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
, i.e.,
|{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}|:=\dim{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}
. However, all of the above mentioned evaluations of
fn
are independent of each other and we can take advantage of a parallel computing. It is implemented with the package
\pkg
optimParallel
(
Gerber and Furrer 2019
)

4
Variable Selection

4.1
Introduction

Due to the flexible nature of a GP-based SVC models, some natural questions arise when defining the model or interpreting the results of an estimated model:
Which covariates should be defined with spatially varying coefficients? Is a constant coefficient sufficient? Did the estimated model overfit the data due to its high flexibility?
To address these questions, we introduced a joint variable selection method for the fixed and random effects of the GP-based SVC model
(
Dambon et al. 2021a
)
.

4.2
Optimization of the Penalized Likelihood

The penalized likelihood is defined by the likelihood
\ell
(
4
) and
L_{1}
penalties
(
Tibshirani 1996
)
on the fixed effects
\mu_{j}
and the variance
\sigma_{k}^{2}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle p\ell({\boldsymbol{\omega}})=\ell({\boldsymbol{\omega}})+n\sum_{j=1}^{p}\lambda_{j}|\mu_{j}|+\sum_{k=1}^{q}\lambda_{p+k}|\sigma^{2}_{k}|

(5)

The optimization problem is related to variable selection of linear mixed models (for an overview, see
Müller et al. 2013
) and, in particular, the works of
Bondell et al. 2010
and
Ibrahim et al. 2011
. We assume that the shrinkage parameters of the penalized likelihood (
5
) are defined like in an adaptive Lasso
(
Zou 2006
)
. However, we account for their inherit difference as the parameters shrink the fixed and random effects, respectively. For the unknown shrinkage parameters
\bigl(\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}\bigr)\in\bigl({\mathbb{R}}_{>0}\bigr)^{2}
, we have:

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}:=\frac{\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\mu}}}{\hat{\mu}_{j}},\quad\lambda_{p+k}:=\frac{\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}}{\hat{\sigma}^{2}_{k}}.

(6)

For now, we assume that
\bigl(\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}\bigr)
are fixed and known. The objective function of the optimization is defined as
-2p\ell({\boldsymbol{\omega}})
. Its optimization is achieved by a coordinate descent where we cyclically iterate between the optimization of the fixed effects
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
and the covariance parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{\mu}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{p}}-2p\ell({\boldsymbol{\mu}}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}^{(t)}),

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\argmin_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}\in{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}-2p\ell({\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}|{\boldsymbol{\mu}}^{(t+1)}),

for
t\geq 0
, where the initial value is given by
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}^{(0)}=\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}}(\text{MLE})
. While the first step is implemented by the
\pkg
glmnet package
(
Friedman et al. 2010
)
, the second step requires more effort. We refer to
Dambon et al. 2021a
for more details. Under convergence, the coordinate descent approach returns the penalized maximum likelihood estimates denoted
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}(\text{PMLE})
.

4.3
Selection of Shrinkage Parameters

In the last section, we assumed the shrinkage parameters
\bigl(\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}\bigr)
to be known. Here, we focus on their selection by optimizing an information criterion. For some shrinkage parameters
\bigl(\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}\bigr)
, we call the coordinate descent which computes
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}\bigl(\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\mu}},\lambda_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}\bigr)
for respective shrinkage parameters, which we abbreviate with
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}
. For the estimated model with parameters
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}
we can calculate an information criterion.

Currently, there are two information criteria implemented in
\pkg
varycoef: a conditional Akaike (cAIC) introduced by
Vaida and Blanchard 2005
and a Bayesian (BIC) information criterion. For parameter estimates
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}
, both information criteria are defined as the sum of the goodness of fit given with
-2\ell\bigl(\hat{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}\bigr)
(also called deviance) and a specific model complexity penalty
\alpha\bigl(\hat{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}\bigr)
. For the cAIC’s penalty we require the effective degrees of freedom
df(\cdot)
which are defined as the trace of the hat matrix
{\mathbf{H}}
:

\displaystyle df\bigl(\hat{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\trace{\mathbf{H}}\bigl(\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\hat{\tau}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{2}\trace\left[\bigl({\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}\bigr)^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}^{-1}{\mathbf{X}}\right]+n-\hat{\tau}^{2}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\trace\left[{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}^{-1}\right],

where
{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}^{-1}=\left[{\boldsymbol{\Sigma}}{}\bigl(\hat{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}{}}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}\bigr)\right]^{-1}
. The respective definitions of
\alpha\bigl(\hat{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}_{{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}\bigr)
are given in Table
5
.

{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}

{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}

\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}

•

The first method is a brute force grid approach by providing pairs of shrinkage parameters and computing it for all given combinations. Once the information criterion has been evaluated for each shrinkage parameter, the stopping condition is met.

•

The second method is a much more sophisticated model-based optimization (MBO) which we briefly describe in the next section.

4.3.1
Model-based Optimization

Model-based optimization
(
Jones 2001
;
Koch et al. 2012
;
Horn and Bischl 2016
)
offers compelling means to find a minimum of the objective function
IC(\cdot)
by using a so called surrogate model. It relies on
n_{\text{init}}
initial values
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(1)},...,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(n_{\text{init}})}
that span the predefined parameter space
\Lambda
. By evaluating
IC
for these values, we receive
n_{init}
tuples
\bigl({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)},\xi^{(i)}\bigr)
with
\xi^{(i)}:=IC({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)})
, which we use to krige. More specifically, we assume a Gaussian surrogate model with constant mean and a Matérn covariance function of smoothness
\nu=3/2
and estimated covariance parameters, c.f. equation (
1
) and Table
1
. In this case, the random variable
\Xi({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
expressing the distribution at
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
conditional on the
n_{\text{init}}
tuples is given by a normal distribution
\Xi({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\sim{\cal{N}}\left(\widehat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),\widehat{s}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\right)
. Here,
\widehat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
and
\widehat{s}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
are the kriging surface and kriging variance, respectively, for which we use the plug-in estimates of the surrogate model. The parameters of the surrogate model are then iteratively updated for
n_{\text{iter}}
steps. In each step
\iota=1,...,n_{\text{iter}}
:

1.

Define the current information criterion minimum
\xi_{\text{min}}:=\min\{\xi^{(1)},...,\xi^{(n_{\text{init}}+\iota-1)}\}
.

2.

Compute an infill criterion from the current posterior distribution
\Xi({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
. We use the expected improvement (EI, see equation (
7
) below) infill criterion which can be expressed analytically for a Gaussian process surrogate model (see equation (
8
) below):

\displaystyle\textnormal{EI}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})

\displaystyle=E_{\Xi}\left(\max\{\xi_{\text{min}}-\Xi({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),0\}\right)

(7)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\bigl(\xi_{\text{min}}-\widehat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\bigr)\Phi\left(\frac{\xi_{\text{min}}-\widehat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}{\widehat{s}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}\right)+\widehat{s}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\phi\left(\frac{\xi_{\text{min}}-\widehat{\mu}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}{\widehat{s}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}\right),&\quad\textnormal{if }\widehat{s}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})>0,\\
0,&\quad\textnormal{if }\widehat{s}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=0.\end{cases}

(8)

In the equation above
\Phi
and
\phi
denote the cumulative distribution function and the probability density function of the standard normal distribution, respectively. The next, best shrinkage parameter
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(n_{\text{init}}+\iota)}
is found by maximizing (
8
).

3.

Evaluate the information criterion at respective location, i.e.,
\xi^{(n_{\text{init}}+\iota)}:=IC({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(n_{\text{init}}+\iota)})
, and add the tuple
\bigl(\xi^{(n_{\text{init}}+\iota)},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(n_{\text{init}}+\iota)}\bigr)
to the existing set of tuples.

4.

Update the surrogate model’s parameters and the distribution of
\Xi({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
.

The
n_{\text{init}}
initial shrinkage parameters
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{(i)}
are drawn as Latin hypercube sample (LHS) from a predefined subset of the parameter space
\Lambda
using the
\proglang
R packages
\pkg
lhs
(
Carnell 2020
)
and
\pkg
ParamHelpers
(
Bischl et al. 2020
)
. The surrogate model is defined using the
\proglang
R package
\pkg
mlr
(
Bischl et al. 2016
)
. The MBO is implemented with the
\proglang
R package
\pkg
mlrMBO
(
Bischl et al. 2017
)
.

and

10\times 10

5
Summary

The
\pkg
varycoef package offers user-friendly tools to model and regress dependent data using (spatially) varying coefficient models. Though the package has been developed with an application to spatial data (
d=2
) in mind, it now supports the modeling of other types of dependent data, as long as a suitable distance measure between observations exists. The models are flexible with respect to the definition of fixed or random effects and, in particular, with respect to the covariance matrices. Several statistical and computational techniques like parallel computing, covariance tapering including sparse matrix algorithms, and optimization over the profile likelihood have been implemented to foster the applicability to large data sets. Besides model estimation and prediction, our
\proglang
R package offers a variable selection method. Model-based optimization, a powerful computational statistics algorithm, is used in
\pkg
varycoef to make the shrinkage parameter selection computationally efficient.

In this article, we gave a variety of examples that showcase the user-friendly application of
\pkg
varycoef on synthetic and real data. The gain of new insights from the estimated models is substantial and immediate. Here, our package offers a wide variety of
\proglang
R methods such that the usage of Gaussian process-based spatially varying coefficient models is similar to, say, linear models with
\code
lm() or general additive models with
\code
gam() from the package
\pkg
mgcv.

Computational details

Most of the presented examples in this paper were generated on a MacBook Pro laptop (macOS 11.4) equipped with an Intel(R) Core(TM) i5-8259U CPU (8 logical processing cores @ 2.3 GHz) and 8 GB of RAM. Results for the Lucas County data set were generated on a Ubuntu server (Ubuntu version 16.04.7) equipped with 8 Intel Xeons E7-2850 (a total of 80 logical processing cores @ 2.0 GHz) and 2 TB of RAM. The intermediate results can be found in the GIT repository
https://git.math.uzh.ch/jdambo/jss-paper-open-access
.

The results in this paper were obtained using

\proglang
R 4.1.0 with the

\pkg
varycoef 0.3.1 package.
\proglang
R itself and all packages mentioned or used (except for
\pkg
INLA) are available from the Comprehensive
\proglang
R Archive Network (CRAN) at
https://CRAN.R-project.org/
. For

INLA, please consult

.
