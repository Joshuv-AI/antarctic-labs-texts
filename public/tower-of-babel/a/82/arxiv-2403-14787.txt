Traces left by random walks on large random graphs: local limits
Steffen Dereich

Abstract




Abstract:

In this article, we develop a theory for understanding the traces left by a random walk in the vicinity of a randomly chosen reference vertex. The analysis is related to interlacements but goes beyond previous research by showing weak limit theorems for the vicinity around the reference vertex together with the path segments of the random walk in this vicinity.
Roughly speaking, our limit theorem requires appropriate mixing properties for the random walk together with a stronger variant of Benjamini-Schramm convergence for the underlying graph model. If these assumptions are satisfied, the limiting object can be explicitly given in terms of the Benjamini-Schramm limit of the graph model.

MSc Classification:
Primary 05C81 Secondary 05C82, 60C05, 90B15.

Keywords:
Random walk on random graph, Benjamini-Schramm convergence, local convergence, configuration model, interlacements

1.
Introduction

In this article, we analyse reversible random walks on large random or deterministic finite, rooted graphs. The aim is to understand the traces left by the random walk in the neighbourhood of the root, where typically the root vertex is a uniformly chosen vertex of the underlying graph.
In our analysis, we assume a strengthened version of Benjamini-Schramm convergence
[
BS01
]
, see also
[
AS04
]
, and we impose an assumption on the mixing times of the random walks.

Local convergence techniques are the main tool for understanding the asymptotic behaviour of large random graphs. For instance, the relative size of the largest cluster typically converges to the probability of the limitting graph (appearing as local limit) being infinite, see

[
ER61
]
for the Erdős-Rényi model and
[
MR98
]
and
[
JL09
]
for results concerning random graphs with fixed degree sequence (also known as configuration model). Similar results hold when applying percolation on the graphs.
The theory is nowadays well established and it is the topic of several monographs, see
[
AS00
,
JLuR00
,
Dur07
,
vdH17
]
.

More recently,
[
vdH21
]
showed that the relationship between the relative size of the largest component and the local limit becomes more transparent when replacing local convergence by a stronger type of convergence that is coined
local convergence in probability
in the article. We face a similar problem in this article since the quantities that we need to control in the graph are not continuous in the topology of local convergence. A remedy for this will be a stronger type of convergence.

Since we consider a random walk on the largest component the respective percolation problem is
vacant set percolation
, where one removes all sites seen by a random walk and then asks whether a large component remains. As observed by Sznitman in
[
Szn10
,
SS10
]
so called interlacements are central towards the understanding of such problems.

When considering vacant set percolation on the configuration model one obtains analogies of the results known for classical percolation, where the relative size of the largest component for the vacant set equals the probability that in the limiting random rooted graph the root is in an infinite component after removal of all the sites seen by an interlacement,
see
[
vTW11
,
vT13
,
vH22
]
for results concerning the configuration model. We stress that from a technical point of view the analysis of vacant set percolation often make strong use of particularities of the configuration model as established in
[
JL09
]
.

Our primary focus does not lie on percolation (although we will explain how one can extend the approach taken in
[
vdH21
]
to vacant set percolation). Instead we record information on the behaviour of the random walk around a possibly randomly picked root. We take the role of an observer placed in the root that tracks the movement of the random walk once it comes into a certain distance to the observer for a certain amount of time. We prove limit theorems in total variation distance for the traces seen by the observer. From a statistical point of view the distribution of the limiting object can be used as a prior distribution for filtering applications. An easy example for a statistic that then will converge in distribution is a function of the time spent in the vertices around the root. Significantly more elaborate statistics are included.

From a mathematical point of view the problem is that incoming new paths may come from various directions and the probabilities governing this process are
not
local quantities. To cope with this we demand to have control about the goodness of the local approximation (of the graph) which has to increase sufficiently fast as the size of the graph tends to infinity. We formalise the meaning of the latter and we will relate this to the mixing times of the random walks. Roughly speaking, one needs to be able to couple an exploration of the graph along the random walk started in the root for at least the mixing time. The analysis relies on new estimates for the total variation distance of certain entrance locations and times to an idealised decoupled random variable. The estimates are in spirit similar to the ones derived in
[
AB92
]
, but unknown to the best of our knowledge.

1.4.
Exploration convergence

Classical Benjamini-Schramm convergence is a form of weak convergence for random rooted graphs. More explicitly, a sequence of random rooted graphs
G_{n}^{o_{n}}
converges to a random rooted graph
G^{o}
in the sense of Benjamini-Schramm
[
BS01
]
if for every radius
R\in\mathbb{N}
the rooted graphs can be coupled such that (after relabeling) the
R
-neighbourhoods of
G_{n}^{o_{n}}
agree with
G^{o}
in the limit with probability one.
In this article we work with a different notion of local convergence, one which incorporates a control of the quality of the latter approximation depending on the index
n
. For this we will use the concept of an exploration process.

We start with the formal definition of an
exploration rule
.
Recall that
\bar{\operatorname{\mathbb{G}}}^{\mathrm{finite,\,root}}
is the set of finite, rooted, generalised graphs. An
exploration
is a tuple

\mathfrak{E}=(\mathcal{G},(e_{1},\dots,e_{n}))\in\bar{\operatorname{\mathbb{G}}}^{\mathrm{finite,\,root}}\times\mathcal{E}^{*}

so that for each
i=1,\dots,n
the edge
e_{i}
is in
\mathcal{G}
and the edge
e_{i}
has both of its endpoints in the set of vertices in
\mathcal{G}
.

An
exploration rule

K
is a probability kernel that assigns an exploration
(\mathcal{G},(e_{1},\dots,e_{n}))
to a distribution on the edges of
\mathcal{G}
that is invariant under isomorphisms. More explicitly, for an exploration
\mathfrak{E}=(\mathcal{G},(e_{1},\dots,e_{n}))
and a graph isomorphism
\phi
taking the exploration
\mathfrak{E}
to
\mathfrak{E}^{\prime}=\phi(\mathfrak{E})
(meaning that
\mathfrak{E}^{\prime}=(\phi(\mathcal{G}),(\phi(e_{1}),\dots,\phi(e_{n})))
) we have

K(\mathfrak{E},\varphi\in\,\cdot\,)=K(\mathfrak{E}^{\prime},\,\cdot\,).

For a graph
G
and a finite subset
V_{0}\subset V
of vertices we build the
V_{0}
-disclosed subgraph

G_{|V_{0}}
of
G
as follows: it is the generalised weighted finite graph that

•

has vertices
V_{0}
and edges

\{e\in E(G):\{e^{\prime},e^{\prime\prime}\}\cap V_{0}\not=\emptyset\}

(all edges with one of the endpoints lying in
V_{0}
)

•

where every edge connecting to a vertex outside of
V_{0}
is redirected to
\partial
and

•

all vertex and edge weights are as in
G
.

An
exploration process
is now a stochastic process that is defined for an arbitrary rooted graph
G^{o}
and an exploration rule
K
as follows:
\mathfrak{E}_{0}=(\mathcal{G}_{0}^{o},\emptyset)
where
\mathcal{G}^{o}_{0}
is the
V(0)=\{o\}
-disclosed subgraph of
G
with root
o
and for every
k=1,\dots
we iteratively

•

choose an
K(\mathfrak{E}_{k-1};\,\cdot\,)
-distributed edge
e_{k}
,

•

form
V(k)
by adding to
V(k-1)
the endpoints of
e_{k}
in the graph
G
(if
e_{k}=\partial
no vertices are added) and

•

let
\mathfrak{E}_{k}=(\mathcal{G}_{k}^{o},(e_{1},\dots,e_{k}))
where
\mathcal{G}_{k}^{o}
denotes the
V(k)
-disclosed subgraph of
G
with root
o
.

Note that during the exploration the disclosed subgraph changes whenever the exploration rule picks an edge that leaves the set of previously disclosed vertices.
In the case that
G^{o}
is itself random we run the exploration with the respective conditional distributions.

Definition 1.5
.

We call an exploration rule
K

proper
if for every
\operatorname{\mathcal{E}}\in\operatorname{\mathbb{G}}^{\mathrm{expl}}
with at least one edge connecting to
\partial
the distribution

.

Choosing

with minimal distance to the root, leads to the

2.
Markov process estimates

In this section we derive estimates for entrance times that are in the spirit of Aldous and Brown
[
AB92
]
.
We denote by
G\in\operatorname{\mathbb{G}}
a fixed
connected, deterministic, weighted
graph and we denote by
(P^{v}:v\in V)
the reversible continuous time Markov family with generator
L=L_{G}
being defined as in (
1
). Proovided that
\sum_{w\in V}\alpha(w)
is finite, the distribution

\pi=\pi^{G}=(\pi^{G}_{v})_{v\in V}
given by

\pi_{v}^{G}=\frac{\alpha(v)}{\sum_{w\in V}\alpha(w)}\qquad(v\in V)

is the unique invariant distribution.
We need to introduce some further quantities.
For a subset
B\subset V
and
\tau\geq 0
we call the measure
e^{\tau}_{B,G}
given by

e_{B,G}^{\tau}(y)=\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{B}(y)\alpha_{y}\sum_{z\in B^{c}}L(y,z)\,P_{G}^{z}(T_{B}>\tau)\qquad(y\in V)

the
\tau
-equilibrium measure of
B
and let

\capa_{G}^{\tau}(B)=\|e_{B,G}^{\tau}\|=\sum_{y\in B}\alpha_{y}\sum_{z\in B^{c}}L(y,z)\,P_{G}^{z}(T_{B}>\tau)

the
\tau
-capacity of
B
.
In the particular case that
\tau=0
, we have

\capa_{G}^{0}(B)=\sum_{y\in B}\alpha_{y}\sum_{z\in B^{c}}L(y,z).

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G\in\mathbb{G}
be a finite graph,
B\subset V
and
y\in V
. One has the following:

(a)

The distribution
P^{\pi}(T_{B}\in\cdot,X_{T_{B}}=y)
has on
(0,\infty)
the continuous Lebesgue density

t\mapsto\pi_{y}\sum_{z\in B^{c}}L(y,z)\,P^{z}(T_{B}>t).

(b)

For every
t\geq 2\tau>0
, we have

|P^{y}(T_{B}>t)-P^{y}(T_{B}>\tau)\,P^{\pi}(T_{B}>t)|\leq 2d_{G}(\tau)+\mbox{$\frac{2\tau}{\alpha(V)}$}\capa_{G}^{0}(B).

Proof.

(a)
We note that for
y\in B
as
\delta\downarrow 0

\displaystyle P^{\pi}(T_{B}\in(t,t+\delta],X_{T_{B}}=y)

\displaystyle=P^{\pi}(T_{B}\in(t,t+\delta],X_{t+\delta}=y)+o(\delta)

\displaystyle=\pi_{y}\,P^{y}((X_{s})_{s\in[\delta,t+\delta]}\text{ does not hit $B$})+o(\delta)

\displaystyle=\delta\pi_{y}\sum_{z\in B^{c}}L(y,z)\,P^{z}(T_{B}>t)+o(\delta)

where the
o(\delta)
term can be controlled uniformly for all
t\geq 0
since
\max_{x\in V}L_{x}<\infty
by finiteness of
V
.
Hence,

\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\sup_{t\geq 0}\Bigl|\frac{1}{\delta}P^{\pi}(T_{B}\in(t,t+\delta],X_{T_{B}}=y)-\pi_{y}\sum_{z\in B^{c}}L(y,z)\,P^{z}(T_{B}>t)\Bigr|=0.

The term on the right of the minus sign is continuous in
t
and thus one gets that
the measure
P^{\pi}(T_{B}\in\cdot,X_{T_{B}}=y)
is continuously differentiable on
(0,\infty)
with the respective differential.

(b)
We note that under
P^{\pi}
the expected number of transitions along edges from
B^{c}
to
B
on an interval
I
is equal to
\frac{1}{\alpha(V)}\capa_{G}^{0}(B)
times the length of the interval. Hence,

\displaystyle P^{\pi}(T_{B}\in(t-2\tau,t])\leq P^{\pi}\bigl(\{(X_{t})_{t\in[t-2\tau,t]}

has a transition along

\displaystyle\text{ an edge from $B^{c}$ to $B$}\}\bigr)\leq\mbox{$\frac{2\tau}{\alpha(V)}$}\capa_{G}^{0}(B).

Now using the Markov property we get with

\mu_{z,\tau}=P^{z}(X_{\tau}\in\,\cdot\,|T_{B}>\tau)

that

\displaystyle P^{z}(T_{B}>t)

\displaystyle=P^{z}(T_{B}>\tau)\,P^{\mu_{z,\tau}}(T_{B}>t-\tau)

\displaystyle\leq P^{z}(T_{B}>\tau)\,P^{\mu_{z,\tau}}\bigl((X_{t})_{t\in[\tau,t-\tau]}\text{ does not hit }B\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq P^{z}(T_{B}>\tau)(P^{\pi}(T_{B}>t-2\tau)+d(\tau))

\displaystyle\leq P^{z}(T_{B}>\tau)\Bigl(P^{\pi}(T_{B}>t)+\mbox{$\frac{2\tau}{\alpha(V)}$}\capa_{G}^{0}(B)+d(\tau)\Bigr).

Conversely,

P^{z}(T_{B}>t)\geq P^{z}((X_{t})_{t\in[0,\tau]\cup[2\tau,t]}\text{ does not hit }B)-P^{z}(X_{\tau}\not\in B,(X_{t})_{t\in[\tau,2\tau]}\text{ hits }B)

and with
P^{z}(X_{\tau}\not\in B,(X_{t})_{t\in[\tau,2\tau)}\text{ hits }B)\leq d(\tau)+\frac{\tau}{\alpha(V)}\capa_{G}^{0}(B)
and

P^{z}((X_{t})_{t\in[0,\tau]\cup[2\tau,t]}\text{ does not hit }B)\geq P^{z}(T_{B}>\tau)(P^{\pi}(T_{B}>t-2\tau)-d(\tau))

we get that

\displaystyle P^{z}(T_{B}>t)

\displaystyle\geq P^{z}(T_{B}>\tau)(P^{\pi}(T_{B}>t-2\tau)-d(\tau))-d(\tau)-\mbox{$\frac{\tau}{\alpha(V)}$}\capa_{G}^{0}(B)

\displaystyle\geq P^{z}(T_{B}>\tau)\,P^{\pi}(T_{B}>t)-2d(\tau)-\mbox{$\frac{\tau}{\alpha(V)}$}\capa_{G}^{0}(B).

∎

We will use the previous lemma to show that under appropriate assumptions the distribution of the first entrance time and its location are close in total variation norm to a product distribution of the exponential and an appropriate discrete distribution.

(10)

Theorem A.4

(Local convergence for the configuration model)
.

Let
(p_{r})_{r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a sequence of probability weights with
p_{0}\not=1
and suppose that for a
\tau\in(2,\infty)

p_{r}=\mathcal{O}(r^{-\tau})\text{ \ as \ }r\to\infty.

Let
G_{n}
be a configuration model with weights
(p_{r})_{r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
meaning that
we take a sequence of i.i.d.
(p_{r})
-distributed random variables
(q_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, let

d_{k}^{(n)}=q_{k}+\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{k=n\}}\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{\sum_{v=1}^{n}q_{v}\text{ is odd}\}}

and define
(G_{n})
in such a way that given
\mathbf{d}^{(n)}=(d^{(n)}_{k})_{k=1,\dots,n}
,
G_{n}
is a random graph with fixed degree sequence
\mathbf{d}^{(n)}
.
We suppose that
D
and
D^{*}
are random variables with weights
(p_{r})_{r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and
(p^{*}_{r})_{r\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
given by

p^{*}_{r}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[D]}rp_{r}.

If

\ell_{n}=\begin{cases}o(n^{\frac{\tau-2}{\tau}}),&\text{ if }\tau<4,\\
o(n^{\frac{1}{2}}(\log n)^{-1}),&\text{ if }\tau=4,\\
o(n^{\frac{1}{2}}),&\text{ if }\tau>4,\end{cases}

then
(G_{n}^{o_{n}})
with an independent uniformly chosen root converges
(\ell_{n})
-locally to a
\mathrm{GWP}(D,D^{*})
random tree
G^{o}
.

Proof.

We will apply Theorem
A.1
and Remark
A.3
(a). with the choice
a_{n}=n^{1/\tau}
.
We analyse the analogues of
\mathbb{E}[D_{n}]
,
\mathbb{P}(D_{n}^{*}\geq a_{n})
,
\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{D_{n}^{*}<a_{n}\}}D_{n}^{*}]
and
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(D_{n}^{*},D^{*})
which are now random and which depend on the degree sequence
\mathbf{d}^{(n)}
. We need to consider

\mathfrak{m}_{1}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k}^{(n)},\ \mathfrak{p}_{n}=\frac{1}{n\mathfrak{m}_{1}^{(n)}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{k}^{(n)}\geq a_{n}\}}d_{k}^{(n)},\ \mathfrak{m}_{2}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{n\mathfrak{m}_{1}^{(n)}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{k}^{(n)}<a_{n}\}}(d_{k}^{(n)})^{2}

and

\mathfrak{d}^{(n)}=\ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Bigl|\frac{1}{n\mathfrak{m}_{1}^{(n)}}k\sum_{r=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{r}^{(n)}=k\}}-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[D]}kp_{k}\Bigr|

First note that by the strong law of large numbers

\mathfrak{m}_{1}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}d_{k}^{(n)}\to\mathbb{E}[D]>0,\text{ almost surely}.

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{k}^{(n)}\geq a_{n}\}}d_{k}^{(n)}\Bigr]\leq\frac{1}{n}+\mathbb{E}[\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{q\geq a_{n}-1\}}q]=\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{\tau-2}{\tau}})

so that
\mathfrak{p}_{n}=\mathcal{O}_{P}(n^{-\frac{\tau-2}{\tau}})
.
Next, analyse the third term:

\mathfrak{m}_{2}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{n\,\mathfrak{m}_{1}^{(n)}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{k}^{(n)}<a_{n}\}}(d_{k}^{(n)})^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n\,\mathfrak{m}_{1}^{(n)}}\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{q_{k}<a_{n}\}}q_{k}^{2}+2q_{n}+1\Bigr).

Standard arguments yield that

\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{k=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{q_{k}<a_{n}\}}q_{k}^{2}\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{q<a_{n}\}}q^{2}\bigr]=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}(n^{\frac{3-\tau}{\tau}}),&\text{ if }\tau<3,\\
\mathcal{O}(\log n),&\text{ if }\tau=3,\\
\mathcal{O}(1),&\text{ if }\tau>3,\end{cases}

so that

\mathfrak{m}_{2}^{(n)}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}_{P}(n^{\frac{3-\tau}{\tau}}),&\text{ if }\tau<3,\\
\mathcal{O}_{P}(\log n),&\text{ if }\tau=3,\\
\mathcal{O}_{P}(1),&\text{ if }\tau>3.\end{cases}

In the case that
\sum_{r=1}^{n}q_{r}\geq 1
we further have that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}^{(n)}=

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\Bigl|\frac{1}{n\mathfrak{m}_{1}^{(n)}}k\sum_{r=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{r}^{(n)}=k\}}-\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[D]}kp_{k}\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[D]}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k\Bigl|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{d_{r}^{(n)}=k\}}-p_{k}\Bigr|+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl|1-\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{1}^{(n)}}{\mathbb{E}[D]}\Bigr|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ \frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[D]}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k\Bigl|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{q_{r}=k\}}-p_{k}\Bigr|+\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}[D]\,n}(q_{n}+1)+\frac{1}{2}\Bigl|1-\frac{\mathfrak{m}_{1}^{(n)}}{\mathbb{E}[D]}\Bigr|

Letting
\bar{S}^{(n)}_{k}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r=1}^{n}\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{q_{r}=k\}}
we get that

\mathbb{E}[|\bar{S}^{(n)}_{k}-p_{k}|]\leq\var(\bar{S}^{(n)})^{1/2}\wedge(2p_{k})\leq\sqrt{\frac{p_{k}}{n}}\wedge(2p_{k}).

Hence,

\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k|\bar{S}_{k}^{(n)}-p_{k}|\Bigr]\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k\sqrt{\frac{p_{k}}{n}}\wedge(2p_{k})=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{\tau-2}{\tau}}),&\text{ if }\tau<4,\\
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2}\log n),&\text{ if }\tau=4,\\
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/2}),&\text{ if }\tau>4,\end{cases}

where the
\mathcal{O}
estimates follow with standard computations.
In the case that
\tau>3
the random variables
q_{1},\dots
have a finite second moment and we get that

|\mathfrak{m}_{1}^{(n)}-\mathbb{E}[D]|=\mathcal{O}_{P}(n^{-1/2}).

If
\tau<3
, we let
\bar{\mathfrak{m}}_{1}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}q_{k}\operatorname{\mathbbm{1}}_{\{q_{k}\leq\lceil n^{1/(\tau-1)}\rceil\}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\mathfrak{m}_{1}^{(n)}-\mathbb{E}[D]|]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[\mathfrak{m}_{1}^{(n)}-\bar{\mathfrak{m}}_{1}^{(n)}\bigr]+\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl|\bar{\mathfrak{m}}_{1}^{(n)}-\mathbb{E}[\bar{\mathfrak{m}}_{1}^{(n)}]\bigr|\bigr]

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}[D]-\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bar{\mathfrak{m}}_{1}^{(n)}\bigr].

We bound the term in the middle by

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\bigl|\bar{\mathfrak{m}}_{1}^{(n)}-\mathbb{E}[\bar{\mathfrak{m}}_{1}^{(n)}]\bigr|\bigr]\leq\var(\bar{\mathfrak{m}}_{1}^{(n)})^{1/2}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{\lceil n^{1/(\tau-1)}\rceil}k^{2}p_{k}\Bigr)^{1/2}

and get with standard computations that all three terms are of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{-\frac{\tau-2}{\tau-1}})
which is of lower order than
n^{-\frac{\tau-2}{\tau}}
.
Consequently,

\mathfrak{d}^{(n)}=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}_{P}(n^{-\frac{\tau-2}{\tau}}),&\text{ if }\tau<4,\\
\mathcal{O}_{P}(n^{-1/2}\log n),&\text{ if }\tau=4,\\
\mathcal{O}_{P}(n^{-1/2}),&\text{ if }\tau>4.\end{cases}

We can couple
\mathbf{d}^{(1)},\mathbf{d}^{(2)},\dots
in such a way that the above
\mathcal{O}_{P}
estimates hold actually almost surely as
\mathcal{O}
-estimates (in the sense that the constant is allowed to be random). For the appropriately coupled sequences one gets that, almost surely,

\ell_{n}\mathfrak{p}_{n}\to 0,\ \ell_{n}\frac{\mathfrak{m}^{(n)}_{2}}{\sqrt{n}}\to 0\text{ \ and \ }\ell_{n}\mathfrak{d}^{(n)}\to 0.

Choosing
G_{n}^{o_{n}}
as random graph with fixed degree sequence
\mathbf{d}^{(n)}
we can apply Theorem
A.1

\omega
-wise and get the result.
∎
