Integrating Explanations in Learning LTL Specifications from Demonstrations
Ashutosh Gupta, John Komp, Abhay Singh Rajput, Krishna Shankaranarayanan, Ashutosh Trivedi, Namrita Varshney

Abstract
This paper investigates whether recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) can assist in translating human explanations into a format that can robustly support learning Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) from demonstrations. Both LLMs and optimization-based methods can extract LTL specifications from demonstrations; however, they have distinct limitations. LLMs can quickly generate solutions and incorporate human explanations, but their lack of consistency and reliability hampers their applicability in safety-critical domains. On the other hand, optimization-based methods do provide formal guarantees but cannot process natural language explanations and face scalability challenges. We present a principled approach to combining LLMs and optimization-based methods to faithfully translate human explanations and demonstrations into LTL specifications. We have implemented a tool called Janaka based on our approach. Our experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of combining explanations with demonstrations in learning LTL specifications through several case studies.

While both LLMs and optimization-based approaches can be used to extract LTL specifications, they have complementary strengths.

Our Approach.

We employ quantitative semantics for LTL formulas over finite traces as a quantitative measure of a formula’s fitness within the set of demonstrations.
This fitness measure is used not only to assess the quality of hypotheses generated by an LLM but also to improve the fitness of the resulting formulas through formula repair.
Our high-level approach is outlined in Figure
1
.
We input demonstrations in the form of system traces involving a set of atomic propositions, along with natural language explanations provided by the designer.
We assume that salient atomic propositions have already been incorporated in the explanations.
After presenting some general prompts regarding LTL syntax, the LLM generates a set of candidate formulas, denoted as
\langle\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{k}\rangle
.
We evaluate their fitness using our quantitative semantics, and if any of the formulas pass a specified threshold, we accept the corresponding hypothesis.
However, if none of the generated formulas are deemed suitable, we extract LTL subformulas from this family, which serve as templates
\langle\tau_{1}\ldots,\tau_{k}\rangle
.
These formulas are subsequently input into our fitness repair algorithm, in conjunction with the demonstrations, to synthesize a formula that offers a more satisfactory explanation of the provided demonstrations.

2.1
Qualitative Semantics for LTL

be

Formulas in LTL over

Let

is defined over words in

The notation

•

2.2
Quantitative semantics for LTL

Given an LTL formula
\varphi
and a finite trace, we design two valuation functions
[\![{\varphi,w}]\!]

\varphi

[

and

[
4
]

We support both semantics to cater to two different intuitions behind the synthesis problem that needs to be solved.
The robust semantics returns a positive value when a trace satisfies a formula and a negative value otherwise. Moreover, higher positive and negative values convey the degree to which a perturbed trace satisfies the formula.
The discounted semantics returns a value in the range

The complexity of an LTL formula can result from two aspects: the complexity of the temporal structure (

is simpler than
p\wedge\textsf{X}p\wedge\textsf{X}\textsf{X}p

\langle\left\{p\right\}\left\{p\right\}{\left\{p\right\}}\rangle

p

for explaining the sample
\langle\left\{p\right\}\rangle

We use the temporal discounting parameter

We modify the semantics of
[

]
and

4
]

.

2.3
Mining LTL from Demonstrations and Explanations

A
sample
, or set of demonstrations,
\mathcal{S}
is a finite set of traces
P\subseteq(2^{\mathcal{P}})^{*}
.
In this work, depending on the context, we use the term trace and demonstration interchangeably.
For an LTL formula
\varphi
, we say
\mathcal{S}\models\varphi
iff for every
\tau\in P

\tau\models\varphi
.
Given a sample
\mathcal{S}
, we are interested in learning an LTL formula
\varphi
such that
\mathcal{S}\models\varphi
.
While the qualitative criterion for fitness may not be selective enough, as there may be multiple LTL hypotheses that satisfy a given sample, we argue that instead of making decisions based on Occam’s razor principles, it is arguably better to integrate designer’s insight in selecting competing hypotheses. For this reason, we study the following problem.

Definition 1
(Qualitative Mining for LTL)

Given a set of demonstrations
\mathcal{S}
, and a natural language explanation
\mathcal{E}
of the system capturing designer’s insights, synthesize an LTL formula
\varphi
such that
\mathcal{S}\models\varphi
.

Constraint solvers such as
\mathsf{Gurobi}
can be used to synthesize LTL formulas from demonstrations; however, they are unable to process natural language explanations. LLMs, on the other hand, can work with both explanations and demonstrations, but do not provide formal guarantees. We posit that LLMs can often generate a set of competing hypotheses such that while none of the proposed formulas may capture the specification in its entirety, together they capture different salient features from the explanation. To utilize this feature of LLMs, while providing formal guarantees, we use constraint solvers to mix-and-match features from this set of formulas to generate the final formula.

To measure the partial fitness of a formula over demonstrations and to aid in combining the best-performing formulas, we use quantitative semantics defined in the previous section as a fitness measure. For a given semantics

is defined as the average of all valuations over all traces in the sample, i.e.,

. We will refer to
[\![{\varphi,\mathcal{S}}]\!]
and
[\![{\varphi,w}]\!]
as the
fitness
of
\varphi

\mathcal{S}
and

Definition 2
(Quantitative Mining for LTL)

Given a set of demonstrations
\mathcal{S}
, and a natural language explanation

.

4.2
Experimental Results

We synthesize templates from the LLM-produced formula with the best fitness using three different strategies. In the first strategy, we implemented a general randomization strategy, which involved modifying the tree structure by selecting random nodes and replacing them with a tree of height
\leq d
as mentioned in Section 4. The second entailed the specific fixation of the operators
G
and
F
in addition to the first. This adjustment was integrated as a direct response to the LLM’s tendency to generate outputs that closely correspond with the English descriptions but lacking the necessary temporal operators. The third strategy focused on adding random quantifiers at the root node, a measure to maintain the inherent structure of the formulas generated by the LLM, and then synthesizing the template by the first strategy. Among these three strategies, the second yielded the least effective results, contributing minimally to enhancing formula fitness. Conversely, the third strategy proved to be beneficial in elevating formula fitness. Additionally, the randomization approach demonstrated superior performance.
In our experiments, we used mixed of the above strategies and repeatedly ran the tool until it yielded formulas with high fitness.

Janaka
implements formula synthesis using both of our described semantics using fitness-based encoding for both of the semantics to find the best formula using
\mathsf{Gurobi}
.
We add additional constraints to ensure that
\mathsf{Gurobi}
did not return a trivial formula by limiting its search space, e.g., formulas like
p\lor\lnot p
(tautology) or
FFp
or equivalent sub-formulas like
F(p)\land F(p)
and various other optimization strategies to reduce the search space.
We ran
\mathsf{Gurobi}
with a time limit and if the time limit was hit, it returned the best solution found so far.

To exercise our tool we required more complicated formulas or a conjunction of many smaller formulas (such as our motivating example).

4.3
Ablation Study

As an ablation study, we tested the impact of the natural language description in the LLM’s performance by providing only traces for the formula generation step of our process. We found that the LLMs struggled to produce any useful formula. Due to this poor performance we did not include them in our experimental results. We repeated the ablation testing by removing the traces from the process to examine the impact of the descriptions alone. Table
4
contrasts the learned formulas and their scores for the cases when we provided traces and when we did not. We observed that the LLM’s success was dependent on several factors, the given detail of the original descriptions, their degree of specificity, and the LLM’s ability to understand how the temporal operators F and G define acceptance of future parts of the trace. Finally, to test the contribution of the LLM and
Janaka
to the overall results,
Janaka
was given the formula traces and an empty template, testing if it could generate the formulas from scratch. Within the given processing timeout, no useful formulas were obtained. This result answers research question 3 positively.

We trained the LLM models in two modes: one-shot learning and multishot learning. In one shot learning, we trained the LLM using examples and ground rules to give the LLM an understanding of the LTL and the synthesis problem. The chat is presented in Appendix
0.D
. In multishot learning, we divide the problem into sub-tasks and ask the LLM to generate a formula. Initially, we provided the English description plus one trace. Subsequently, we increased the subset size to three traces and then to six traces. After each increase in traces, we ask the LLM to analyze and verify the given traces alongside the English description. We observed the LLM give an average formula at the first step, and with each addition of traces, it keeps adding small modifications to the formula using LTL operators, which results in poor performance. We found the first method more effective than the latter one.
Our reported results are using one-shot learning with
\mathsf{ChatGPT4.0}
.

Grammatical inference

[
26

18

L^{\star}
algorithm
[
5
]
, whereas the latter generally relies on state-merging procedures and can be used to learn probabilistic automata
[
13
]
, MDPs in the context of model checking
[
35
]
, timed automata
[
34
]
, and regular decision processes
[
1
]
.

The problem of learning LTL formulae from traces is a form of grammatical inference that has been well-studied. Several of the methods most related to our own are presented in
[
30
,
37
,
42
,
48
]
. The focus of
[
37
]
is to produce the minimal formula which is consistent with a rational sample represented as a lasso. The problem of matching formulae with traces is encoded as a constraint system and a satisfying assignment yields the learned property. However, requiring the inputs to be lassos significantly restricts the application to real scenarios. The method in
[
30
]
requires a user-defined input template of the LTL formula which they would like to satisfy and outputs all possible propositional substitutions consistent with the sample. Kim
[
28
]
select the best candidate LTL templates that satisfy the given positive and negative traces based on Bayesian probability.

Unlike these works, we use positive traces generated without restrictions for our learning. While
[
23
]
has shown the difficulty of this approach, recent work has focused on method to overcome the restriction. Roy
[
42
]
explored a method that progressively builds a DFA from a trivial single satisfying state to the smallest non-trivial DFA that still maintains satisfaction of the provided traces. Another form of progressively building a to final formula was explored in
[
48
]
. Their approach started with a known good formula and a set of demonstrations that defined a new, related specification. Using a trellis structure, they progressively update the formula, pruning the sections that do not extend the formula in support of the additional specification.
Our approach works in a progressive manner but, unlike these approaches, we start with candidate LLM generated LTL formulas that are expected to contain errors and uses small templates of LTL syntax to improve the overall satisfaction of formulas against positive traces.

Extracting formal requirements from natural language text has been a focus of extensive research
[
11
]
. While there has been progress using unrestricted natural language specifications
[
27
,
21
]
, many techniques require conformance of the requirements language to specific ontologies
[
44
]
, sentence structure
[

]
or unique requirements languages
[

,
14
,
45
]
.
Recently
[

,

,

]
, large language models have been utilized to translate LTL from natural language.
Liu et al.
[

]
propose Lang2LTL, a modular system that leverages pretrained LLMs to extract referring expressions, corresponding real-world landmarks, and finally translate the command into an LTL task specification for robotic motion planning.
Hahn et al.
[
24
]
study generalization abilities of LLMs when translating natural language into formal specifications with complex semantics.
They report that LLMs maintain their generalization capabilities from pre-trained knowledge of natural language to generalize to variable names and operator descriptions.
However none of these approaches study the application of LLMs in extracting LTL from demonstrations or descriptions.
Mavrogiannis
[

uses an LLM trained with task examples and allowed instructions for translating cooking instructions from random internet recipes into LTL formulas.
Closer to our approach is that of Rahmani et al.

In contrast, our work is focused on using an LLM not to generate exact LTL formulas but synthesizing candidate ones from positive, finite traces that could then be repaired to maximize the best qualities of both the LLM and existing LTL synthesis tools.

Appendix 0.A
Rationale behind Robustness Semantics

In the robust semantics
[
3
]
, given an LTL formula
\varphi
and a trace
T
, a
valuation function
[\![{\varphi,T}]\!]
is defined as follows.

•

[\![{(p,T)}]\!]=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }p\in T(0),\\
-1&\text{if }p\notin T(0).\end{cases}

•

[\![{(\neg p,T)}]\!]=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }p\notin T(0),\\
-1&\text{if }p\in T(0).\end{cases}

•

[\![{(\varphi\land\psi,T)}]\!]=\begin{cases}\beta\cdot[\![{(\varphi,T)}]\!]\cdot[\![{(\psi,T)}]\!]&\text{if~}[\![{(\varphi,T)}]\!]\geq 0\land[\![{(\psi,T)}]\!]\geq 0,\\
-1&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

•

[\![{(\varphi\lor\psi,T)}]\!]=\beta\cdot\begin{cases}\frac{([\![{(\varphi,T)}]\!]+[\![{(\psi,T)}]\!])}{2}&\text{if~}[\![{(\varphi,T)}]\!]\geq 0\wedge[\![{(\psi,T)}]\!]\geq 0,\\
\max([\![{(\varphi,T)}]\!],[\![{(\psi,T)}]\!])&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

•

[\![{(G\varphi,T)}]\!]=\beta\cdot\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{|T|}\alpha^{i}[\![{(\varphi,T(i))}]\!]&\text{if }[\![{(\neg\varphi,T(t))}]\!]<0\hskip 2.84544pt\forall t,\\
-1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

•

[\![{(F\varphi,T)}]\!]=\beta\cdot\begin{cases}\alpha^{t}[\![{(\varphi,T(t))}]\!]&\text{where }t=\min\{j|[\![{(\varphi,T(j))}]\!]\geq 0\},\\
\gamma*\alpha^{|T|}&\text{if }[\![{(\varphi,T(t))}]\!]=0~\text{for all}~t.\end{cases}

•

[\![{(X\varphi,T)}]\!]=\begin{cases}[\![{(\varphi,T(1))}]\!]&\text{if }[\![{(\varphi,T(1))}]\!]\geq 0\land|T|>1\\
\gamma&\text{if~}|T|<2\\
-1&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

•

[\![{(\varphi U\psi,T)}]\!]=\begin{cases}\alpha^{t}\cdot[\![{(\psi,T(j))}]\!]&\text{where}~t=\min\{j|[\![{(\psi,T(j))}]\!]\geq 0\}\text{~and}\\
&\forall i<t,~[\![{(\varphi,T(i))}]\!]\geq 0\\
\alpha^{|T|}\cdot\gamma&\text{if }\forall i[\![{(\psi,T(i))}]\!]\geq 0\\
-1&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

For the literals, we assign valuation -1 or 1 depending on whether
the trace satisfies it or not. We interpret conjunction by taking the product of the valuation of the individual conjuncts. This implies that both subformulae must do well on the trace. This is better than assigning
[\![{(\varphi{\land}\psi,T)}]\!]
as the minimum of
[\![{(\varphi,T)}]\!]
and
[\![{(\psi,T)}]\!]

[
46
]
. This valuation ensures that both
\varphi
and
\psi
must score high for
\varphi\land\psi
to score high. However, the
\min
function is not sensitive to the formula that has a higher value. Therefore, the learning algorithm becomes unguided for one part of the formula. This suggests a modification to the valuation function that takes both the subformulae into account symmetrically, without flattening one of the subformulae. Our valuation function for the conjunction, defined as their product
[\![{(\varphi,T)}]\!]\cdot[\![{(\psi,T)}]\!]
, is based on this idea.

We interpret disjunction as an average over the disjuncts when the valuations of individual disjuncts
are
\geq 0
. Likewise, if one of the disjuncts is
\geq 0
and the other is
<0
, then we choose the maximum value.
This implies that a disjunction scores well
if any of the two disjuncts does well on the trace.

Our interpretation of
\textsf{G}\varphi
computes the discounted sum of the value of
\varphi

at each position of the word. To give less weightage to a literal appearing further down in the trace as opposed to earlier,
we apply the temporal discount of
0<\alpha<1
.
Our interpretation of
\textsf{F}\varphi
computes the discounted score of
\varphi

at the earliest position where
\varphi
has a non-negative score.

The discount value
\alpha
is a parameter defined by the user to control the temporal complexity.
Consider the
formula
\varphi=\textsf{F}q
for
p,q\in\mathcal{P}
and the trace

T=\{p\}\{p,r\}\{p\}\{p,s\}\{p\}\{p\}\{p,q\}(\{r\}\{q\})^{*}

Then
q
holds for
the first time at
T(7)
. Thus,
V(q,T[7:|T|])=1
, making
V(\varphi,T)=\alpha^{7}
.
Our valuation assigns non-zero scores only for satisfiable formulae.

Rationale for Discounted Semantics

Let
0{\leq}\alpha{\leq}1
and
0{\leq}\beta{\leq}1
be temporal and nesting discount factors.
For a finite trace
w
and LTL formula
\varphi
, we define the valuation of
\varphi
wrt
w

[\![{\varphi,w}]\!]
as follows:

•

[\![{\mathsf{true},w}]\!]=1
.

•

[\![{a,w}]\!]=1
if and only if
w(0)\ni a
.

•

[\![{\neg\varphi,w}]\!]=1-[\![{\varphi,w}]\!]
.

•

[\![{\varphi\vee\psi,w}]\!]=\beta\cdot\max\left\{[\![{\varphi,w}]\!],[\![{\psi,w}]\!]\right\}
.

•

[\![{\textsf{X}\varphi,w}]\!]=\alpha[\![{\varphi,w[1]}]\!]
.

•

[\![{\varphi\until\psi,w}]\!]=\max\limits_{0\leq i<|w|}\left\{\min\left\{\alpha^{i}\cdot[\![{\psi,w[i]}]\!],\min\limits_{0\leq j<i}\left\{\alpha^{j}\cdot[\![{\varphi,w[i]}]\!]\right\}\right\}\right\}.

For
G
and
\eventually
operators, these semantics follow from those of
\until
and given here for completeness:

•

[\![{\eventually\varphi,w}]\!]=\max\limits_{0\leq i<|w|}\left\{\alpha^{i}\cdot[\![{\varphi,w[i]}]\!]\right\}.

•

[\![{\textsf{G}\varphi,w}]\!]=1-\max\limits_{0\leq i<|w|}\left\{\alpha^{i}\cdot[\![{\neg\varphi,w[i]}]\!]\right\}=1-\max\limits_{0\leq i<|w|}\left\{\alpha^{i}\cdot(1-[\![{\varphi,w[i]}]\!])\right\}.

evaluates to

Similarly, for a trace

[\![{\textsf{G}p,w}]\!]
evaluates to

n
is the first index in which
p
becomes false.
In summary,
[\![{\eventually p,w}]\!]
is higher when
p
is found earlier, capturing the quantitative semantics for “eventually”.
On the other hand,
[\![{\textsf{G}p,w}]\!]
is higher for a longer contiguous initial sequence that the trace exhibits, capturing the quantitative semantics of “globally.”
