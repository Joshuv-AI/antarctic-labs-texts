Poincaré invariance of spinning binary dynamics in the post-Minkowskian Hamiltonian approach
Hojin Lee, Sangmin Lee

Abstract
We initiate the construction of the global Poincaré algebra generators in the context of the post-Minkowskian Hamiltonian formulation of gravitating binary dynamics in isotropic coordinates that is partly inspired by scattering amplitudes. At the first post-Minkowskian (1PM) order, we write down the Hamiltonian in a form valid in an arbitrary inertial frame. Then we construct the boost generator at the same order which uniquely solves all the equations required by the Poincaré algebra. Our results are linear in Newton’s constant but exact in velocities and spins, including all spin multiple moments. We also compute the generators of canonical transformations that proves the equivalence between our new generators and the corresponding generators in the ADM coordinates up to the second post-Newtonian (2PN) order.



1
Introduction

A two-body problem in general relativity is often addressed by an effective theory.
The fundamental theory is the Einstein-Hilbert action coupled to the two bodies.
The effective theory is obtained by “integrating out” the gravitational field
mediating the interaction.
To be specific, we restrict our attention to the Hamiltonian formulation and focus on the conservative sector where the gravitational radiation is not yet taken into account.

There are two widely used perturbative expansions to build up the effective theory. One is the post-Newtonian (PN) expansion
in powers of
v^{2}\sim GM/r
. The other is the post-Minkowskian (PM) expansion in powers of
GM/r

with no assumption on
v^{2}
.
See
e.g.

Blanchet:2013haa
;
Porto:2016pyg
;
Schafer:2018kuf
;
Levi:2018nxp
;
Bjerrum-Bohr:2022blt
;
Kosower:2022yvp
;
Buonanno:2022pgc
;
Goldberger:2022ebt
for comprehensive reviews and further references.

Regardless of the perturbative scheme, the two-body effective theory
is expected to be covariant under the global Poincaré symmetry
of the underlying asymptotically flat spacetime.
In the traditional ADM formalism, the structure of the Poincaré generators was elucidated in
Regge:1974zd
.
The generators are of great practical values too.
As shown in
Damour:2000kk
, whenever one reaches a new order of the (PN or PM) perturbative Hamiltonian,
constructing the Poincaré generators to the same order provides a stringent consistency check
and can sometimes fix undetermined coefficients.
The boost generator of a spinning binary was first constructed in
Damour:2007nc
up to the next-to-leading order (NLO) spin-orbit (SO) coupling.
Its extension to higher order can be found
e.g.
in
Steinhoff:2008zr
;
Hergt:2008jn
;
Hartung:2013dza
;
Levi:2016ofk
.
Recently, the construction was completed up to the overwhelming 5PN order
in
Levi:2022dqm
;
Levi:2022rrq
.

A recent trend in this subject area is that ideas and tools from (quantum) scattering amplitudes
are being used to produce new results in the (classical) PM Hamiltonian;
see
e.g.

Bjerrum-Bohr:2022blt
;
Kosower:2022yvp
;
Buonanno:2022pgc
for reviews.
Scattering amplitudes are well known to be Lorentz scalars.
As such, one may expect that the PM Hamiltonian based on amplitudes
should be well-suited for the discussion of the global Poincaré symmetry.
Curiously, however, there has been little to no attempts
to construct the Poincaré generators in the amplitude-based PM context.
The main goal of the present paper is to fill this gap.

For simplicity, let us begin with a non-spinning binary system.
The first order post-Minkowskian (1PM) Hamiltonian in the center of momentum (COM) frame
is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}H^{[1]}|_{\mathrm{COM}}&=-\frac{Gm_{1}^{2}m_{2}^{2}(2\gamma^{2}-1)}{E_{1}E_{2}r}\,,\\
E_{a}&=\sqrt{\vec{p}_{a}^{2}+m_{a}^{2}}\,,\quad\gamma=-\frac{p_{1}\cdot p_{2}}{m_{1}m_{2}}=\frac{1}{m_{1}m_{2}}(E_{1}E_{2}-\vec{p}_{1}\cdot\vec{p}_{2})\,.\end{split}

(1)

Here, the superscript of
H^{[1]}
denotes the 1PM order that is linear in Newton’s constant
G
but
exact in
\vec{v}=\vec{p}/m
.
Among the Poincaré generators, the most interesting and delicate is the boost generator,
as we will review shortly.
A boost generator connects distinct inertial frames.
In order to construct the boost generator associated with the Hamiltonian (
1
),
we have to generalize it to a form valid in an arbitrary inertial frame.
The desired generalization will be the first new result of this paper in section
2.1
.

Given the general form of the Hamiltonian,
the closure of the Poincaré algebra uniquely determines the boost generator,
as we will show in section
2.2
.
The computation is based on a mild ansatz to be explained later in this section.
In short, our ansatz is motivated by a similar ansatz in
Damour:2000kk

and the fact that the boost generator defines a sort of “center coordinate” of the binary system.

In section
3
, we repeat the same exercise for a spinning binary system.
Our work for spinning binaries remains at the 1PM order but is exact not only in velocities but also in spins.
Building upon the general spin-multipole expansion of
Levi:2014gsa
;
Levi:2015msa

and its on-shell interpretation of
Arkani-Hamed:2017jhn
in terms of the massive spinor helicity variables, the complete spinning 1PM Hamiltonian was first obtained in the COM frame in
Chung:2020rrz
.
Again, we generalize the Hamiltonian to a form valid in an arbitrary frame
and then construct the boost generator.
A proper understanding of the “spin-gauge symmetry” in the Hamiltonian framework
plays a critical role throughout our computations. See
Steinhoff:2015ksa
;
Kim:2021rda

and appendix
A
of this paper
for a modern treatment of the spin-gauge symmetry.

PN and PM computations should agree whenever they overlap.
In section
4
, we verify explicitly that our new results are consistent with
PN results in the literature.
In the non-spinning case,
we establish the equivalence between our Hamiltonian and the ADM Hamiltonian
at the intersection of the 1PM and 2PN expansions.
In the spinning case,
we establish the equivalence up to the NLO SO coupling linear in
G
.
The boost generators are also compared and shown to agree.
In practice, showing the equivalence means
finding an explicit form of the generator of a canonical transformation
which accounts for the difference between the two coordinate choices.

Our work naturally suggests many directions for generalizations and applications. Some of them will be discussed in section
5
.

1.1
Poincaré algebra

In this technical appendix to the Introduction, we briefly review the general structure of the Poincaré algebra generators following
Damour:2000kk
,
and motivate our ansatz that will play a key role in the main text.

For a 2-body dynamics with spin, the translation and rotation generators are simply

\displaystyle\vec{P}=\vec{p}_{1}+\vec{p}_{2}\,,\quad\vec{J}=\sum_{a=1}^{2}\left(\vec{x}_{a}\times\vec{p}_{a}+\vec{S}_{a}\right)\,.

(2)

Our convention for the Poisson bracket is such that

\displaystyle\{x_{i},p_{j}\}=\delta_{ij}\,,\quad\{S_{i},S_{j}\}=\epsilon_{ijk}S_{k}\,.

(3)

It is customary to trade the boost generator
\vec{K}
for
\vec{G}

through the relation
\vec{K}=\vec{G}-t\vec{P}
.
The algebra then reads

\displaystyle\left\{P_{i},P_{j}\right\}=0\,,\quad\qquad\left\{P_{i},H\right\}=0\,,\quad\qquad\left\{J_{i},H\right\}=0\,,

(4)

\displaystyle\left\{J_{i},J_{j}\right\}=\epsilon_{ijk}J_{k}\,,\quad\left\{J_{i},P_{j}\right\}=\epsilon_{ijk}P_{k}\,,\quad\left\{J_{i},G_{j}\right\}=\epsilon_{ijk}G_{k}\,,

(5)

\displaystyle\left\{G_{i},P_{j}\right\}=\delta_{ij}H\,,\quad\left\{G_{i},H\right\}=P_{i}\,,\quad\qquad\left\{G_{i},G_{j}\right\}=-\epsilon_{ijk}J_{k}\,.

(6)

Translation and rotation invariance of
H
in (
4
) dictates that
H
is a 3d scalar
function that depend on the inner products among
\vec{p}_{1}
,
\vec{p}_{2}
and
\vec{r}=\vec{x}_{1}-\vec{x}_{2}
.
The third relation in (
5
) means that
\vec{G}
is a 3d-vector.

The non-trivial checks lie in (
6
). When
H
has been computed up to some order in a perturbation series,
we should construct, to a commensurate order,
\vec{G}
satisfying all three conditions in (
6
).
Let us call the three conditions “
H
/
P
/
J
-conditions”, respectively.
We will see repeatedly in this paper that
the three conditions are so constraining that the unique solution for
\vec{G}
is forced upon us
as long as we begin with a reasonable ansatz.

The
H/P
-conditions give some hints on how to write down an ansatz for
\vec{G}
at each order.
Since
\vec{P}
and
H
commutes with each other,
we can rewrite the
H/P
-conditions as

\displaystyle\vec{X}\equiv\frac{\vec{G}}{H}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\{X_{i},P_{j}\}=\delta_{ij}\,,\quad\{\vec{X},H\}=\frac{\vec{P}}{H}\,.

(7)

The first relation on the RHS states that
\vec{X}
acts like a position coordinate canonically conjugate to
\vec{P}
.
The second relation states that the velocity of
\vec{X}
stays constant:

\displaystyle\frac{d\vec{X}}{dt}=\{\vec{X},H\}=\frac{\vec{P}}{H}\,.

(8)

These observations are well known and appealing, but in practice when we rely on a perturbation theory, the non-perturbative relations may not be directly useful.
With hindsight, we suggest that we introduce the “coordinate” vector
\vec{X}
order by order.
Specifically, for the PM expansion,

\displaystyle H=H^{[0]}+H^{[1]}+H^{[2]}+\cdots\,,\qquad\vec{G}^{[1]}=\vec{G}^{[0]}+\vec{G}^{[1]}+\vec{G}^{[2]}+\cdots\,,

(9)

we demand that, in the non-spinning case,

\displaystyle\vec{G}^{[n]}=H^{[n]}\vec{X}^{[n]}\,,\quad\vec{X}^{[n]}=\alpha^{[n]}_{1}\vec{x}_{1}+\alpha^{[n]}_{2}\vec{x}_{2}\,.

(10)

The quantities
\alpha^{[1]}_{1,2}
are assumed to be dimensionless functions of momenta,
independent of the position vectors.
At 0PM (free theory), it holds trivially.

\displaystyle\vec{G}^{[0]}=E_{1}\vec{x}_{1}+E_{2}\vec{x}_{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\vec{X}^{[0]}=\left(\frac{E_{1}}{E_{1}+E_{2}}\right)\vec{x}_{1}+\left(\frac{E_{2}}{E_{1}+E_{2}}\right)\vec{x}_{2}\,.

(11)

At each order, the
H
-condition requires that

\displaystyle\alpha^{[n]}_{1}+\alpha^{[n]}_{2}=1\,.

(12)

The
P
and
J
conditions are non-trivial because
H
and
\vec{G}
receive perturbative corrections.

In the main text of this paper, we will apply the ansatz (
10
) and its extension to the spinning case
to the 1PM order and successfully solve the
P
and
J
conditions, thereby completing the
construction of
\vec{G}^{[1]}
.

2
Non-spinning 1PM

Our metric convention is
(-+++)
. Momentum 4-vectors
are denoted by
p_{a}=(E_{a},\vec{p}_{a})

(a=1,2)
.
Once we choose an inertial frame,
most equations are written in the 3d vector notation.
Frequently used abbreviations include,
in addition to (
1
),

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\vec{r}=\vec{x}_{1}-\vec{x}_{2}\,,\quad\hat{n}=\frac{\vec{r}}{r}\,,\quad\vec{u}_{a}=\frac{\vec{p}_{a}}{E_{a}}\,,\quad E=E_{1}+E_{2}\,,\quad z_{a}=\frac{E_{a}}{E_{1}+E_{2}}\,.\end{split}

(13)

2.1
Hamiltonian

We recall the process of turning a tree-level amplitude to a potential.
In the 2-to-2 elastic scattering, the momentum transfer 4-vector
(\hbar q)
is defined such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}p_{1}^{\mathrm{(in)}}=p_{1}-\hbar q/2\,,&\quad p_{2}^{\mathrm{(in)}}=p_{2}+\hbar q/2\,,\\
p_{1}^{\mathrm{(out)}}=p_{1}+\hbar q/2\,,&\quad p_{2}^{\mathrm{(out)}}=p_{2}-\hbar q/2\,,\end{split}

(14)

Requiring the mass-shell condition for all external momenta,
we find two conditions:

\displaystyle(p_{1}+p_{2})\cdot q=0\,,\quad(p_{1}-p_{2})\cdot q=0\,.

(15)

The space of
q
satisfying
both
conditions
is 2-dimensional. But, as is well known
for the Newton or the Coulomb potential, we have to perform a 3d Fourier transform to obtain the potential.
There is no real tension here; the scattering amplitude is on-shell whereas the Hamiltonian is off-shell.
The standard procedure is to impose only the first condition in (
15
) and relax the second one.

For a given inertial frame (call it the lab frame),
we can solve the condition for
q^{0}
:

\displaystyle q^{0}=\frac{(\vec{p}_{1}+\vec{p}_{2})\cdot\vec{q}}{E_{1}+E_{2}}\equiv\vec{u}_{c}\cdot\vec{q}\,.

(16)

Here,
\vec{u}_{c}
is the velocity of the
center of momentum degree of freedom
as observed by the lab frame.
In the COM frame, the famous Fourier transform
that turns a tree amplitude to a potential is

\displaystyle 4\pi\int_{\vec{q}}\,\,\frac{e^{i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{r}}}{\vec{q}^{2}}=\frac{1}{r}\,,\qquad\int_{\vec{q}}\equiv\int\frac{d^{3}\vec{q}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,.

(17)

To work in a non-COM frame, we simply take

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=4\pi\int_{\vec{q}}\,\,\frac{e^{i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{r}}}{\vec{q}^{2}-(q^{0})^{2}}=4\pi\int_{\vec{q}}\,\,\frac{e^{i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{r}}}{\vec{q}^{2}-(\vec{u}_{c}\cdot\vec{q})^{2}}\,.

(18)

(This integral was considered earlier in
Jones:2022aji
.)
One can perform the integral by noting that

\displaystyle\vec{q}^{2}-(\vec{u}_{c}\cdot\vec{q})^{2}=q^{i}A_{ij}q^{j}\,,

(19)

where
A_{ij}
is an effective “metric” which depends on
\vec{p}_{1,2}
but is constant on the
\vec{q}
-space.
Since
\vec{u}_{c}^{2}<1
, the metric
A_{ij}
is non-generate.
In the end, the integral simply gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\frac{1}{r\sqrt{1-(\vec{u}_{c})^{2}_{\perp}}}\equiv\frac{\gamma_{c}}{r}\,,\quad(\vec{u}_{c})^{2}_{\perp}=\vec{u}_{c}^{2}-(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{u}_{c})^{2}\,.

(20)

We may say that the potential acquires a Lorentz factor
associated with the transverse part of
\vec{u}_{c}
.
In conclusion, the 1PM Hamiltonian in the lab frame is given by

\displaystyle H^{[1]}=\gamma_{c}\,H^{[1]}_{\mathrm{bare}}=\gamma_{c}\left[-\frac{Gm_{1}^{2}m_{2}^{2}(2\gamma^{2}-1)}{E_{1}E_{2}r}\right]\,.

(21)

2.2
Boost

Our next goal is to construct the 1PM boost generator
\vec{G}^{[1]}
compatible with our new Hamiltonian
H^{[1]}
(
21
),
starting from the general ansatz (
10
) and
solving the
P
/
J
conditions:

\displaystyle\vec{G}^{[1]}=H^{[1]}\vec{X}^{[1]}=H^{[1]}(\alpha^{[1]}_{1}\vec{x}_{1}+\alpha^{[1]}_{2}\vec{x}_{2})\,,\quad\alpha^{[1]}_{1}+\alpha^{[1]}_{2}=1\,.

(22)

To avoid clutter, we will omit the superscript from
\alpha^{[1]}_{a}
in what follows.

P first

At first sight, it is not clear whether we should try to solve the
P
-condition first or the
J
-condition first.
Let us explore both possibilities,
starting from the
P
-condition:

\displaystyle\{\vec{G}^{[0]},H^{[1]}\}+\{\vec{G}^{[1]},H^{[0]}\}=0\,.

(23)

The first term in (
23
) can be computed explicitly:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\{\vec{G}^{[0]},H^{[1]}\}&=-\left(\vec{u}_{1}+\vec{u}_{2}\right)H^{[1]}+(\vec{u}_{c}-\gamma_{c}^{2}(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{u}_{c})\hat{n})H^{[1]}\\
&\quad+\left(\frac{\vec{x}_{1}}{r}(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{u}_{1})-\frac{\vec{x}_{2}}{r}(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{u}_{2})\right)H^{[1]}\\
&\quad+\gamma_{c}^{2}(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{u}_{c})\left(\frac{\vec{x}_{1}}{r}(\vec{u}_{c}\cdot\vec{u}_{1})_{\perp}-\frac{\vec{x}_{2}}{r}(\vec{u}_{c}\cdot\vec{u}_{2})_{\perp}\right)H^{[1]}\,.\end{split}

(24)

The Lorentz-scalar
(2\gamma^{2}-1)
commutes with
\vec{G}^{[0]}
as a consequence of

\displaystyle\{\vec{G}^{[0]},\gamma\}=0\,.

(25)

From here on, any function of
\gamma
will be treated as a constant.

As for the second term in (
23
), the ansatz (
22
) give

\displaystyle\begin{split}\{M_{1}\vec{x}_{1}+M_{2}\vec{x}_{2},H^{[0]}\}&=M_{1}\vec{u}_{1}+M_{2}\vec{u}_{2}+\{M_{1},H^{[0]}\}\vec{x}_{1}+\{M_{2},H^{[0]}\}\vec{x}_{2}\,,\end{split}

(26)

where
M_{a}=H^{[1]}\alpha_{a}

(a=1,2)
.
The next step is to match the coefficients of the four vectors
\vec{u}_{1}
,
\vec{u}_{2}
,
\vec{x}_{1}
,
\vec{x}_{2}
from (
23
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}M_{1}-\frac{E_{2}}{E_{1}+E_{2}}H^{[1]}&=0\,,\\
M_{2}-\frac{E_{1}}{E_{1}+E_{2}}H^{[1]}&=0\,,\\
\{M_{1},H^{[0]}\}-\frac{1}{r}\left[\gamma_{c}^{2}(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{u}_{c})[1-(\vec{u}_{c}\cdot\vec{u}_{1})_{\perp}]-(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{u}_{1})\right]H^{[1]}&=0\,,\\
\{M_{2},H^{[0]}\}+\frac{1}{r}\left[\gamma_{c}^{2}(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{u}_{c})[1-(\vec{u}_{c}\cdot\vec{u}_{2})_{\perp}]-(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{u}_{2})\right]H^{[1]}&=0\,.\end{split}

(27)

Remarkably, the first two conditions already fix
the sought-for coefficients
\alpha_{1,2}
uniquely,

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=z_{2}\,,\quad\alpha_{2}=z_{1}\,,\quad z_{a}\equiv\frac{E_{a}}{E_{1}+E_{2}}\,.

(28)

Moreover, a bit of algebra shows that this solution also passes the other two, seemingly over-constraining, conditions. To recap, we have shown with mild effort that

\displaystyle\vec{G}^{[1]}=H^{[1]}\vec{X}^{[1]}\,,\quad\vec{X}^{[1]}=z_{2}\vec{x}_{1}+z_{1}\vec{x}_{2}\,.

(29)

Once the
P
-condition is established,
it takes not too much work to verify the
J
-condition. We rewrite the
J
-condition with a short-hand notation as

\displaystyle\{\vec{G}^{[0]},\vec{G}^{[1]}\}_{\times}=0\,,\qquad\{\vec{A},\vec{B}\}_{\times}|_{i}\equiv\epsilon_{ijk}\{A_{j},B_{k}\}\,.

(30)

We can simplify the
J
-condition using the
P
-condition as follows.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\{\vec{G}^{[0]},\vec{G}^{[1]}\}_{\times}&=\{\vec{G}^{[0]},H^{[1]}\vec{X}\}_{\times}\\
&=\{\vec{G}^{[0]},H^{[1]}\}\times\vec{X}+H^{[1]}\{\vec{G}^{[0]},\vec{X}\}_{\times}\\
&=-\{\vec{G}^{(1)},H^{[0]}\}\times\vec{X}+H^{[1]}\{\vec{G}^{[0]},\vec{X}\}_{\times}\\
&=-\{H^{[1]}\vec{X},H^{[0]}\}\times\vec{X}+H^{[1]}\{\vec{G}^{[0]},\vec{X}\}_{\times}\\
&=H^{[1]}\left[\{H^{[0]},\vec{X}\}\times\vec{X}+\{\vec{G}^{[0]},\vec{X}\}_{\times}\right]\,.\end{split}

(31)

The
\{H^{[1]},H^{[0]}\}
term drops out because
\vec{X}\times\vec{X}=0
.
Evaluating the brackets, we find

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\qquad\{H^{[0]},\vec{X}\}\times\vec{X}+\{\vec{G}^{[0]},\vec{X}\}_{\times}\\
&=\left[\alpha_{1}^{2}-\alpha_{1}-E_{1}\partial_{E_{1}}\alpha_{1}\right](\vec{x}_{1}\times\vec{u}_{1})+\left[\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}-E_{2}\partial_{E_{2}}\alpha_{1}\right](\vec{x}_{1}\times\vec{u}_{2})\\
&\quad+\left[\alpha_{2}^{2}-\alpha_{2}-E_{2}\partial_{E_{2}}\alpha_{2}\right](\vec{x}_{2}\times\vec{u}_{2})+\left[\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}-E_{1}\partial_{E_{1}}\alpha_{2}\right](\vec{x}_{2}\times\vec{u}_{1})\,.\end{split}

(32)

So, the
J
-condition gives four partial differential equations for the two functions
\alpha_{1,2}
.
Since
\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}=1
, we may focus on the two equations for
\alpha_{1}
.
When we already have a candidate solution (
28
),
it is trivial to check that they indeed satisfy the equations.

J first and P-condition in q-space

We can try to solve the
J
-condition first and then verify the
P
-condition.
But, to minimize extra work, we take (
31
) as our starting point,
where it is
assumed
that the
P
-condition holds.
This may sound tautological, but as long as we confirm the
P
-condition
after we solve the
J
-condition, there will be no logical flaw.

Getting back to (
32
) and combining the two equations, we can show that
\alpha_{1}
depends only on the ratio
E_{1}/E_{2}
.
Each equation can be solved up to an integration constant, giving

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}=\frac{1}{1+\kappa_{1}(E_{1}/E_{2})}=1-\frac{1}{1+\kappa_{2}(E_{2}/E_{1})}\,.

(33)

The exchange symmetry of the two particles sets
\kappa_{1}=1=\kappa_{2}
, confirming that (
28
)
is the unique solution to the
J
-condition.
We may now proceed to verify the
P
-condition.

In our derivation of the 1PM Hamiltonian (
21
),
the momentum space expression of the amplitude played a crucial role.
It will become even more important when we add spins to the binary system in the next section.
In that regard, it is instructive to reexamine the
P
-condition in the
\vec{q}
-space,
where the “Hamiltonian” is

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}^{[1]}=\left(\frac{k}{E_{1}E_{2}}\right)\frac{e^{i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{r}}}{\vec{q}^{2}-(q^{0})^{2}}\,.

(34)

Recall that the
P
-condition can be written as

\displaystyle\{H^{[0]}\vec{X}^{[0]},H^{[1]}\}+\{H^{[1]}\vec{X}^{[1]},H^{[0]}\}=0\,.

(35)

Rearranging a little bit, we obtain

\displaystyle(\vec{X}^{[0]}-\vec{X}^{[1]})\{H^{[0]},H^{[1]}\}+H^{[0]}\{\vec{X}^{[0]},H^{[1]}\}+H^{[1]}\{\vec{X}^{[1]},H^{[0]}\}=0\,.

(36)

In the
\vec{q}
-space, a short computation shows,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\{H^{[0]},\widetilde{H}^{[1]}\}&=-i\vec{q}\cdot(\vec{u}_{1}-\vec{u}_{2})\widetilde{H}^{[1]}\,,\\
\{\vec{X}^{[0]},\widetilde{H}^{[1]}\}&=\left[-i\vec{q}\cdot(z_{2}\vec{u}_{1}+z_{1}\vec{u}_{2})\vec{r}+\frac{2(\vec{u}_{c}\cdot\vec{q})\left(\vec{q}-(\vec{u}_{c}\cdot\vec{q})\vec{u}_{c}\right)}{\vec{q}^{2}-(q^{0})^{2}}\right]\frac{\widetilde{H}^{[1]}}{H^{[0]}}\\
&\qquad-(\vec{u}_{1}+\vec{u}_{2})\frac{\widetilde{H}^{[1]}}{H^{[0]}}\,,\\
\{\vec{X}^{[1]},H^{[0]}\}&=z_{2}\vec{u}_{1}+z_{1}\vec{u}_{2}=(\vec{u}_{1}+\vec{u}_{2})-\vec{u}_{c}\,.\end{split}

(37)

We combine these to find

\displaystyle\begin{split}&(\vec{X}^{[0]}-\vec{X}^{[1]})\{H^{[0]},\widetilde{H}^{[1]}\}+H^{[0]}\{\vec{X}^{[0]},\widetilde{H}^{[1]}\}+\widetilde{H}^{(1)}\{\vec{X}^{[1]},H^{[0]}\}\\
&\hskip 113.81102pt=\nabla_{\vec{q}}\left[-(\vec{u}_{c}\cdot\vec{q})\widetilde{H}^{[1]}\right]\,.\end{split}

(38)

Being a total derivative, it vanishes upon integration over
the
\vec{q}
-space.
Thus, we have successfully reconfirmed the
P
-condition
from the
\vec{q}
-space point of view.

3
Spinning 1PM

We generalize our construction of the boost generator to include spins of the two bodies.
The fact that the amplitude admits a Fourier integral representation will greatly simplify the computations.

3.1
Hamiltonian

Review of the COM Hamiltonian

In the COM frame, the complete 1PM Hamiltonian for a spinning binary was given in
Chung:2020rrz
.

\displaystyle H^{[1]}|_{\mathrm{COM}}=-\frac{4\pi Gm_{1}^{2}m_{2}^{2}}{E_{1}E_{2}}\int_{\vec{q}}\frac{e^{i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{r}}}{\vec{q}^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=\pm 1}e^{2s\rho}W_{1}\left(s\tau_{1}\right)W_{2}\left(s\tau_{2}\right)\right]U_{1}U_{2}\,.

(39)

The variable
\rho
is the relative rapidity:
\gamma=\cosh\rho
.
The arguments of the
W
functions are

\displaystyle\tau_{a}=i\frac{\varepsilon\left(q,v_{1},v_{2},a_{a}\right)}{\sinh\rho},\quad\varepsilon(a,b,c,d)=\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}a^{\mu}b^{\nu}c^{\rho}d^{\sigma}\,,\quad v_{a}=p_{a}/m_{a}\,.

(40)

The
W
functions encode the general 3-point vertex with two massive legs and a single graviton,
following the spin-multipole expansion of
Levi:2015msa
.

Thomas-Wigner rotation in a lab frame

The derivation of the rotation factor
U_{a}

(a=1,2)
in
Chung:2020rrz

is based on the relative orientation of three time-like vectors:
incoming particle
a
, outgoing particle
a
, time axis of the reference frame common to both particles.
The COM frame is defined such that the time axis is defined by the 4-vector

\displaystyle w=\frac{p_{1}+p_{2}}{\sqrt{-(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}}}\,.

(43)

To work in an arbitrary lab frame, we simply choose a frame and declare that the time axis is
l=(1,\vec{0})
in that frame.

We follow
Chung:2020rrz
to derive the rotation factor in the lab frame. After accounting for
the difference in the metric signature convention,
the formula for the rotation angle reads

\displaystyle 1-\cos\alpha=\frac{\left(\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}u^{\nu}v^{\rho}\omega^{\sigma}\right)^{2}}{(1-u\cdot v)(1-v\cdot w)(1-w\cdot u)}\,.

(44)

To be specific, let us focus on particle 1. Particle 2 can be treated similarly.
We choose the three time-like unit vectors to be

\displaystyle u=\frac{p_{1}-\hbar q/2}{m_{1}}\,,\quad v=\frac{p_{1}+\hbar q/2}{m_{1}}\,,\quad w=l=(1,\vec{0})\,.

(45)

For the denominator, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}1-u\cdot v&=2+(\hbar q)^{2}/4m_{1}^{2}\,,\\
1-v\cdot w&=1+E_{1}/m_{1}+\hbar q^{0}/2m_{1}\,,\\
1-u\cdot w&=1+E_{1}/m_{1}-\hbar q^{0}/2m_{1}\,.\end{split}

(46)

In the classical limit, the
(\hbar q)
terms are all suppressed.
Moreover, the
q^{2}
term in the first line, when expanded, will cancel against the propagator
and produce delta-function localized terms that are irrelevant to the long-distance interaction
between the two bodies.

For the numerator, we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mu}(u,v,w)=\pm\frac{\hbar}{m_{1}^{2}}(0,\vec{p}_{1}\times\vec{q})\,.

(47)

Combining all the factors, and keeping only the leading term in the classical limit, we find

\displaystyle 2(1-\cos\alpha_{1})=4\sin^{2}(\alpha_{1}/2)=\frac{\hbar^{2}(\vec{p}_{1}\times\vec{q})^{2}}{m_{1}^{2}(E_{1}+m_{1})^{2}}\,.

(48)

The Thomas-Wigner rotation factor is nothing but
U=\exp(i\alpha\vec{S}\cdot\hat{m}/\hbar)
,
where
\hat{m}
is the unit vector denoting the rotation axis given in (
47
).
Combining all elements and taking the classical (
\hbar\rightarrow 0
) limit, we obtain

\displaystyle U_{1}=\exp\left[+if_{1}\vec{a}_{1}\cdot(\vec{u}_{1}\times\vec{q})\right]\,,\quad f_{a}=\frac{E_{a}}{E_{a}+m_{a}}\,,\quad\vec{a}_{a}=\frac{\vec{S}_{a}}{m_{a}}\,.

(49)

The sign can be fixed by requiring consistency with the COM frame Hamiltonian.

Vertex parameter

Let us reanalyze the
\tau

Writing the Lorentz scalar in a 3d notation, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varepsilon\left(q,p_{1},p_{2},a\right)&=(E_{2}\vec{p}_{1}-E_{1}\vec{p}_{2})\cdot(\vec{a}\times\vec{q})+q^{0}\vec{a}\cdot(\vec{p}_{1}\times\vec{p}_{2})-a^{0}\vec{q}\cdot(\vec{p}_{1}\times\vec{p}_{2})\\
&=E_{1}E_{2}\left[(\vec{u}_{1}-\vec{u}_{2})\cdot(\vec{a}\times\vec{q})+q^{0}\vec{a}\cdot(\vec{u}_{1}\times\vec{u}_{2})-a^{0}\vec{q}\cdot(\vec{u}_{1}\times\vec{u}_{2})\right]\,.\end{split}

In the COM frame,

But in general, both of them are important.
We observed in (

As for

\displaystyle a^{0}_{a}=\frac{\vec{p}_{a}\cdot\vec{S}_{a}}{m_{a}(E_{a}+m_{a})}=f_{a}(\vec{u}_{a}\cdot\vec{a}_{a})\qquad(a=1,2)\,.

We can understand this prescription
by carefully studying the “spin-gauge symmetry”
as is reviewed in some detail in appendix

Here, we content ourselves with a short summary.
A Lorentz covariant description of a spinning body
begins with a spin tensor
S_{\mu\nu}
. Among its six components, only three are physical.
There are infinitely many possible gauge choices.
The two most popular choices are the “covariant gauge”
associated with the co-moving frame of the body
and the (Pryce-Newton-Wigner

;
Pryce:1948pf
;
Newton:1949cq

We define the spin-length vector

such that its spatial components are
\vec{a}=\vec{S}/m
, where
\vec{S}
is
essentially the spatial component of the canonical spin tensor.
Using the known map between the two gauges and the Pauli-Lubanski map from a spin tensor to a spin vector,
we can indeed define the spin-length vector with all the desired properties, eventually leading to (

In conclusion, the all-spin 1PM Hamiltonian valid in an arbitrary inertial frame is

\displaystyle H^{[1]}=-\frac{4\pi Gm_{1}^{2}m_{2}^{2}}{E_{1}E_{2}}\int_{\vec{q}}\frac{e^{i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{r}}}{\vec{q}^{2}-(q^{0})^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{s=\pm 1}e^{2s\rho}W_{1}\left(s\tau_{1}\right)W_{2}\left(s\tau_{2}\right)\right]U_{1}U_{2}\,,

(52)

where (

Spin-orbit coupling

Writing down the Hamiltonian in the position space is conceptually straightforward but practically cumbersome.
We illustrate the idea by presenting the explicit form of the spin-orbit Hamiltonian.
It consists of two groups of terms. One comes from the generating function and is linear in
\gamma
. The other one comes from the Thomas-Wigner rotation factor and is proportional to
(2\gamma^{2}-1)
.
Each group is split into two pieces based on their dependence
on the velocity vectors.

Here are some useful relations for computing the SO Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle\nabla\left(\frac{\gamma_{c}}{r}\right)=-\frac{\gamma_{c}^{3}}{r^{2}}\left[(1-\vec{u}_{c}^{2})\hat{n}+(\vec{u}_{c}\cdot\hat{n})\vec{u}_{c}\right]\,,\quad\vec{u}_{c}\times(\vec{u}_{1}-\vec{u}_{2})=-(\vec{u}_{1}\times\vec{u}_{2})\,.

(53)

All in all, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}H^{[1]}_{\mathrm{SO}}&=H^{[1a]}_{\mathrm{SO}}+H^{[1b]}_{\mathrm{SO}}+H^{[1c]}_{\mathrm{SO}}+H^{[1d]}_{\mathrm{SO}}\,,\\
H^{[1a]}_{\mathrm{SO}}&=-2\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{r^{2}}\gamma\gamma_{c}^{3}(1-\vec{u}_{c}^{2})(\vec{u}_{1}-\vec{u}_{2})\cdot\left[\hat{n}\times(\vec{a}_{1}+\vec{a}_{2})\right]\,,\\
H^{[1b]}_{\mathrm{SO}}&=-2\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{r^{2}}\gamma\gamma_{c}^{3}(1-\vec{u}_{c}^{2})(\hat{n}\cdot(\vec{u}_{1}\times\vec{u}_{2}))\left[f_{1}(\vec{u}_{1}\cdot\vec{a}_{1})+f_{2}(\vec{u}_{2}\cdot\vec{a}_{2})\right]\,,\\
H^{[1c]}_{\mathrm{SO}}&=-\frac{Gm_{1}^{2}m_{2}^{2}(2\gamma^{2}-1)}{E_{1}E_{2}r^{2}}\gamma_{c}^{3}(1-\vec{u}_{c}^{2})\left[f_{1}(\vec{u}_{1}\times\vec{a}_{1})-f_{2}(\vec{u}_{2}\times\vec{a}_{2})\right]\cdot\hat{n}\,,\\
H^{[1d]}_{\mathrm{SO}}&=-\frac{Gm_{1}^{2}m_{2}^{2}(2\gamma^{2}-1)}{E_{1}E_{2}r^{2}}\gamma_{c}^{3}(\vec{u}_{c}\cdot\hat{n})\left[f_{1}(\vec{u}_{1}\times\vec{a}_{1})-f_{2}(\vec{u}_{2}\times\vec{a}_{2})\right]\cdot\vec{u}_{c}\,.\end{split}

(54)

Here, the

term comes from the first two terms of (

term comes from the last term of (

and

come from the rotation factor (

Specifically, we compare our results with the ADM results up to a “formal” 2PN order.

Our formal PN counting is closely related to, but not exactly equivalent to, the standard PN order coun

4.3
Spinning 1PN

1PN with spin

The translation generator
remains unchanged.
The rotation generator
\vec{J}
now includes spins
as already indicated in (
2
). It implies that
the spin vector has zero weight in the formal PN counting.
Nevertheless, additional powers of spin increase the PN weight of the Hamiltonian, as the spin vectors enter through
the combination
\vec{a}_{1,2}/r
, where
\vec{a}_{1,2}
are the spin-length vectors,
\vec{a}=\vec{S}/m
.

The leading spin-dependent term in the Hamiltonian is the spin-orbit coupling,

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{SO}}^{(2)}=\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{2r^{2}}\left[(3(\vec{v}_{1}\times\vec{a}_{1}-\vec{v}_{2}\times\vec{a}_{2})+4(\vec{v}_{1}\times\vec{a}_{2}-\vec{v}_{2}\times\vec{a}_{1})\right]\cdot\hat{n}\,.

(128)

Turning to the boost generators, and taking hints from
Hanson:1974qy
;
Bel:1980ahp
, we learn that
\vec{G}^{(-1/2)}
remains unchanged
while
\vec{G}^{(+1/2)}
changes slightly.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\vec{G}^{(-1/2)}&=m_{1}\vec{x}_{1}+m_{2}\vec{x}_{2}\,,\\
\vec{G}^{(+1/2)}&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a=1,2}\left(m_{a}\vec{v}_{a}^{2}\vec{x}_{a}+\left(-\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{r}\right)\vec{x}_{a}-\vec{S}_{a}\times\vec{v}_{a}\right)\,.\end{split}

(129)

The
J
-condition holds including the spin contribution,

\displaystyle\{G^{(-1/2)}_{i},G^{(1/2)}_{j}\}+\{G^{(1/2)}_{i},G^{(-1/2)}_{j}\}=-\epsilon_{ijk}J_{k}\,.

(130)

The spin contributions to the
P
-condition all cancel out:

\displaystyle\{\vec{G}^{(1/2)}_{\mathrm{spin}},H^{(1)}\}+\{\vec{G}^{(-1/2)},H^{(2)}_{\mathrm{SO}}\}=0\,.

(131)

So, no further change is needed for
\vec{G}^{(1/2)}
.

Canonical transformation at 1PN with spin

At the leading order, the possibilities for canonical transformation allowed by the PN power-counting and
\Delta H^{(2)}_{\mathrm{SO}}\propto r^{-2}
are

\displaystyle C^{(1)}_{\mathrm{SO}}=\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{r}\left[b_{1}\vec{a}_{1}-\bar{b}_{1}\vec{a}_{2}\right]\cdot\hat{n}+M\left(\vec{v}_{1}\times\vec{v}_{2}\right)\cdot(b_{2}\vec{a}_{1}-\bar{b}_{2}\vec{a}_{2})\,,

(132)

where (
b_{k}
,
\bar{b}_{k}
) are dimensionless functions of the mass ratio.
The resulting shift of the Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta H^{(2)}_{\mathrm{SO}}&=\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{r^{2}}\left[(b_{1}\vec{a}_{1}-\bar{b}_{1}\vec{a}_{2})\cdot\hat{n}\right]\left[(\vec{v}_{1}-\vec{v}_{2})\cdot\hat{n}\right]+\frac{GM^{2}}{r^{2}}(b_{2}\vec{a}_{1}-\bar{b}_{2}\vec{a}_{2})\cdot(\hat{n}\times\vec{v}_{c})\,.\end{split}

(133)

PN expansion of 1PM-SO

When we expand the spin-linear Hamiltonian (
54
) in the PN way, only
H^{[1a]}
and
H^{[1c]}
contribute at the 1PN order. The result is

\displaystyle H^{(2)}_{\mathrm{SO-PM}}

\displaystyle=\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{r^{2}}\left[2(\vec{v}_{1}-\vec{v}_{2})\times(\vec{a}_{1}+\vec{a}_{2})-\frac{1}{2}(\vec{v}_{1}\times\vec{a}_{1})+\frac{1}{2}(\vec{v}_{2}\times\vec{a}_{2})\right]\cdot\hat{n}\,.

(134)

It agrees perfectly with (
128
). There is no need for a canonical transformation.
If
C^{(1)}_{\mathrm{SO}}
were non-vanishing,
it would induce the shift
\Delta\vec{G}^{(1/2)}=\{\vec{G}^{(-1/2)},C^{(1)}_{\mathrm{SO}}\}
,
which would contradict the
free
nature of
\vec{G}^{(1/2)}
.

Appendix A
Spin gauge symmetry

We review the spin gauge symmetry mainly following
Kim:2021rda

and use it to explain how the canonical spin vector
\vec{S}
follows from the spin tensor
S_{\mu\nu}

after a gauge-fixing.

The dynamical variables of the spherical top model consists of
x^{\mu}
,
p_{\mu}
,
S_{\mu\nu}
and
the “body-attached orthonormal frame”,

\displaystyle\eta_{\mu\nu}\Lambda^{\mu}{}_{A}\Lambda^{\nu}{}_{B}=\eta_{AB}\,,\qquad\Lambda^{\mu}{}_{A}\Lambda^{\nu}{}_{B}\eta^{AB}=\eta^{\mu\nu}\,.

(152)

The non-vanishing Poisson brackets are

\displaystyle\begin{split}\{x^{\mu},p_{\nu}\}&=\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu}\,,\\
\{\Lambda^{\rho}{}_{A},S_{\mu\nu}\}&=-(\delta^{\rho}{}_{\mu}\Lambda_{\nu A}-\delta^{\rho}{}_{\nu}\Lambda_{\mu A})\,,\\
\{S_{\mu\nu},S_{\rho\sigma}\}&=-(\eta_{\nu\rho}S_{\mu\sigma}-\eta_{\mu\rho}S_{\nu\sigma}-\eta_{\nu\sigma}S_{\mu\rho}+\eta_{\mu\sigma}S_{\nu\rho})\,.\end{split}

(153)

The mass-shell constraint and the spin-gauge generators are

\displaystyle\phi_{0}=\frac{1}{2}(p^{2}+m^{2})\,,\quad\phi_{a}=\frac{1}{2}(\hat{p}^{\mu}+\Lambda^{\mu}{}_{0})S_{\mu\nu}\Lambda^{\nu}{}_{a}\,,\quad\hat{p}^{\mu}=p^{\mu}/|p|\,.

(154)

Gauge-fixing constraints

Ref.
Kim:2021rda
studied the covariant gauge-fixing constraints,

\displaystyle(\chi_{\mathrm{cov}})^{0}=\frac{1}{p^{2}}x^{\mu}p_{\mu},\quad(\chi_{\mathrm{cov}})^{a}=\hat{p}_{\mu}\Lambda^{\mu a}\,.

(155)

These constraints
imply
the covariant spin condition:

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mu}{}_{0}=\hat{p}^{\mu}\,,\quad S_{\mu\nu}p^{\nu}=0\,.

(156)

To analyze a binary system, it is often convenient to work in a fixed lab frame,
which will lead us to the “canonical” Pryce-Newton-Wigner gauge
Pryce:1935ibt
;
Pryce:1948pf
;
Newton:1949cq
.
Given the lab frame 4-vector,
l=(1,\vec{0})
, we may consider

\displaystyle\chi^{0}=-\frac{x\cdot l}{m}\,,\quad\chi^{a}=l_{\mu}\Lambda^{\mu a}\,.

(157)

We normalized the new constraints such that
they become equal to the covariant ones when the lab frame coincide with the co-moving frame.
The new constraints imply that

\displaystyle\Lambda^{\mu}{}_{0}=l^{\mu}\,,\quad(p^{\mu}+ml^{\mu})S_{\mu\nu}\Lambda^{\nu}{}_{a}=0\,.

(158)

In the lab frame, the second equation leads to

\displaystyle(E+m)S_{0i}+p^{j}S_{ji}=0\quad\mbox{or}\quad S_{0i}=\frac{S_{ij}p^{j}}{E+m}=-\frac{(\vec{S}\times\vec{p})_{i}}{E+m}\,,\quad S_{i}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{ijk}S^{jk}\,.

(159)

We will shortly see how this explains the spin-part of
\vec{G}^{[0]}
.

Dirac brackets in the canonical gauge

Next, we compute the Poisson brackets between
\chi^{A}
and
\phi_{B}
and use them to compute the Dirac brackets.
It is easy to check that

\displaystyle\{\chi^{A},\chi^{B}\}=0=\{\phi_{A},\phi_{B}\}\,.

(160)

Computing
C^{A}{}_{B}\equiv\{\chi^{A},\phi_{B}\}

up to terms that vanish under the constraints, we find

\displaystyle\begin{split}\{\chi^{0},\phi_{0}\}=\frac{E}{m}\,,&\quad\{\chi^{0},\phi_{b}\}=\frac{E-m}{2m^{3}}(l^{\mu}S_{\mu\nu}\Lambda^{\nu}{}_{b})\,,\\
\{\chi^{a},\phi_{0}\}=0\,,&\quad\{\chi^{a},\phi_{b}\}=\frac{E+m}{2m}\delta^{a}{}_{b}\,.\end{split}

(161)

The Dirac bracket is defined in the standard manner,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\{f,g\}_{*}&=\{f,g\}-(C^{-1})^{A}{}_{B}\left[\left\{f,\phi_{A}\right\}\left\{\chi^{B},g\right\}-\left\{f,\chi^{B}\right\}\left\{\phi_{A},g\right\}\right]\,.\end{split}

(162)

Computing the Dirac brackets
and using the constraints to simplify the results at the end,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\{x^{\mu},x^{\nu}\}_{*}&=0\,,\\
\{x^{\mu},p_{\nu}\}_{*}&=\delta^{\mu}{}_{\nu}+\frac{1}{E}p^{\mu}l_{\nu}\,,\\
\{x^{\rho},S_{\mu\nu}\}_{*}&=-\frac{1}{E(E+m)}\left(p^{\rho}l^{\sigma}+E\eta^{\rho\sigma}\right)(S_{\sigma\mu}l_{\nu}-S_{\sigma\nu}l_{\mu})\,,\\
\{S_{\mu\nu},S_{\rho\sigma}\}_{*}&=\{S_{\mu\nu},S_{\rho\sigma}\}-\frac{4}{E+m}\left(p_{[\mu}S_{\nu][\rho}l_{\sigma]}-p_{[\rho}S_{\sigma][\mu}l_{\nu]}\right)\,.\end{split}

(163)

We are ready to show that the Dirac bracket here agrees
with the Poisson bracket in the main text,
with
(\vec{x},\vec{p},\vec{S})
as independent variables.
Let us first look at the
(x,p)
sector in the lab frame

l^{\mu}=(1,\vec{0})
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\{x^{0},p_{0}\}_{*}=0\,,\qquad\{x^{0},p_{i}\}_{*}=0\,,\\
&\{x^{i},p_{0}\}_{*}=-\frac{p_{i}}{E}\,,\quad\{x^{i},p_{j}\}_{*}=\delta^{i}{}_{j}\,.\end{split}

(164)

Next, we look at the
(x,S)
sector.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\{x^{0},S_{\mu\nu}\}_{*}&=0\,,\quad\{x^{\rho},S_{ij}\}_{*}=0\,.\\
\{x_{i},S_{0j}\}_{*}&=-\frac{1}{E+m}\left(\frac{1}{E}p_{i}S_{jk}p_{k}+S_{ij}\right)=\left\{x_{i},\frac{S_{jk}p_{k}}{E+m}\right\}_{*}\,.\end{split}

(165)

Finally, the
(S,S)
sector. The
\{S_{ij},S_{kl}\}
brackets remain unchanged. The other ones are

\displaystyle\begin{split}\{S_{ij},S_{0k}\}_{*}&=\frac{1}{E+m}\left(\delta_{ik}S_{jl}p^{l}-\delta_{jk}S_{il}p^{l}-p_{i}S_{jk}-p_{j}S_{ik}\right)=\left\{S_{ij},\frac{S_{kl}p^{l}}{E+m}\right\}_{*}\,,\\
\{S_{0i},S_{0j}\}_{*}&=\frac{E-m}{E+m}S_{ij}+\frac{p_{i}S_{0j}-p_{j}S_{0i}}{E+m}=\left\{\frac{S_{ik}p^{k}}{E+m},\frac{S_{jl}p^{l}}{E+m}\right\}_{*}\,.\end{split}

(166)

Covariant vs Canonical

In general, the spin-gauge transformation generates shifts

\displaystyle(x^{\prime})^{\mu}=x^{\mu}+\Delta x^{\mu}\,,\quad(S^{\prime})_{\mu\nu}=S_{\mu\nu}+\Delta S_{\mu\nu}\,,

(167)

such that both
p_{\mu}
and

J_{\mu\nu}=x_{\mu}p_{\nu}-x_{\nu}p_{\mu}+S_{\mu\nu}

remain invariant. In other words,

\displaystyle\Delta S_{\mu\nu}+(\Delta x_{\mu})p_{\nu}-(\Delta x_{\nu})p_{\mu}=0\,.

(168)

In this paper, we need both of the two famous gauge choices: the covariant one for the co-moving frame and the canonical one for the lab frame.
To avoid confusion, let us denote the covariant spin tensor by
\Sigma_{\mu\nu}
. Consider the spatial parts:

\displaystyle\Sigma_{k}\equiv\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{ijk}\Sigma_{jk}\,,\quad S_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon_{ijk}S_{jk}\,.

(169)

The time components (
S_{0i}
and
\Sigma_{0i}
) are determined by the space components (
S_{ij}
and
\Sigma_{ij}
) through the respective gauge conditions. Explicitly,

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0i}=-\frac{(\vec{\Sigma}\times\vec{p})_{i}}{E}\,,\quad S_{0i}=-\frac{(\vec{S}\times\vec{p})_{i}}{E+m}\,.

(170)

Eq.(3.60b) of
Hanson:1974qy
gave the relation between the two, which was reproduced in the spin-gauge language in eq.(2.16) of
Kim:2021rda
:

\displaystyle\vec{\Sigma}=\frac{E}{m}\vec{S}-\frac{\vec{p}(\vec{p}\cdot\vec{S})}{m(E+m)}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\vec{S}=\frac{m}{E}\vec{\Sigma}+\frac{\vec{p}(\vec{p}\cdot\vec{\Sigma})}{E(E+m)}\,.

(171)

It is useful to note that

\displaystyle\vec{p}\cdot\vec{\Sigma}=\vec{p}\cdot\vec{S}\,,\quad\frac{\vec{p}\times\vec{\Sigma}}{E}=\frac{\vec{p}\times\vec{S}}{m}\equiv\vec{p}\times\vec{a}\,,\quad\frac{\vec{\Sigma}}{E}=\vec{a}-f\vec{u}(\vec{u}\cdot\vec{a})\,,\quad f=\frac{E}{E+m}\,.

(172)

Another important fact explained in
Hanson:1974qy
is that

\displaystyle\vec{S}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{\mu\nu}\Sigma_{\mu\nu}\,.

(173)

The RHS is both Lorentz invariant and spin-gauge invariant,
while the LHS appears to be Lorentz non-invariant.
The spin-gauge symmetry accounts for the apparent mismatch.

We are ready to explain the origin of the “
a^{0}
prescription” used in the main text.
The original spin variables (
S_{\mu\nu}
or
\Sigma_{\mu\nu}
)
are tensors while
a^{\mu}
is a vector.
The standard procedure to switch between a spin tensor
and a spin vector is the Pauli-Lubanski map, which
makes sense only in the covariant gauge. In our convention, the map is

\displaystyle W_{\mu}=-\frac{1}{2m}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\Sigma^{\nu\rho}p^{\sigma}\,.

(174)

Since we prefer
S
to
\Sigma
in the main text, we use (
172
) to relate
W^{\mu}
to
\vec{S}
to obtain

\displaystyle W^{0}=\frac{\vec{p}\cdot\vec{S}}{m}\,,\quad\vec{W}

\displaystyle=\frac{\vec{S}}{m}+\frac{\vec{p}(\vec{p}\cdot\vec{S})}{m^{2}(E+m)}\,.

(175)

We could define
a^{\mu}
as
W^{\mu}/m
, but using the fact that

\varepsilon(q,p_{1},p_{2},a_{1})=\varepsilon(q,p_{1},p_{2},a_{1}+\kappa p_{1})

for any
\kappa
, we modify the definition of
a^{\mu}
such that

\displaystyle a^{\mu}\equiv\frac{W^{\mu}}{m}-\frac{(\vec{p}\cdot\vec{S})p^{\mu}}{m^{2}(E+m)}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\vec{a}=\frac{\vec{S}}{m}\,,\quad a^{0}=\frac{\vec{p}\cdot\vec{S}}{m(E+m)}\,.

(176)

Appendix B
PN expansion of PM Hamiltonian

We give an explicit expression for the 2PN expansion of the SO Hamiltonian given in (
54
).
The
[1b]
part is the easiest.
It is already cubic in velocities,
so we simply make the replacements,

\displaystyle\gamma\rightarrow 1\,,\quad\gamma_{c}\rightarrow 1\,,\quad 1-\gamma_{c}^{2}(\vec{u}_{c}\cdot\hat{n})^{2}\rightarrow 1\,,\quad\vec{u}_{1,2}\rightarrow\vec{v}_{1,2}\,,\quad f_{1,2}\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}\,,

(177)

and run the 3d Schouten identity to find

\displaystyle\begin{split}H^{[1b](3)}_{\mathrm{SO}}&=-\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{r^{2}}\hat{n}\cdot(\vec{v}_{1}\times\vec{v}_{2})\left(\vec{v}_{1}\cdot\vec{a}_{1}+\vec{v}_{2}\cdot\vec{a}_{2}\right)\\
&=-\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{r^{2}}\left[(a_{1},v_{1},v_{2})(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{v}_{1})+(v_{1},a_{1},n)(\vec{v}_{1}\cdot\vec{v}_{2})-(v_{2},a_{1},n)\vec{v}_{1}^{2}\right]\\
&\quad+\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{r^{2}}\left[(a_{2},v_{2},v_{1})(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{v}_{2})+(v_{2},a_{2},n)(\vec{v}_{1}\cdot\vec{v}_{2})-(v_{1},a_{2},n)\vec{v}_{2}^{2}\right]\,.\end{split}

(178)

The
[1a]
part requires a bit more work:

\displaystyle\begin{split}H^{[1a](3)}_{\mathrm{SO}}&=-\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{r^{2}}\left[(\vec{v}_{c}^{2}-3(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{v}_{c})^{2})(\vec{v}_{1}-\vec{v}_{2})\right.\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt\left.+(\vec{v}_{1}-\vec{v}_{2})^{2}(\vec{v}_{1}-\vec{v}_{2})-(\vec{v}_{1}^{2}\vec{v}_{1}-\vec{v}_{2}^{2}\vec{v}_{2})\right]\cdot[\hat{n}\times(\vec{a}_{1}+\vec{a}_{2})]\\
&=\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{r^{2}}\left[(\vec{v}_{c}^{2}-3(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{v}_{c})^{2})(\vec{v}_{1}-\vec{v}_{2})\right.\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt\left.+(\vec{v}_{2}^{2}-2\vec{v}_{1}\cdot\vec{v}_{2})\vec{v}_{1}-(\vec{v}_{1}^{2}-2\vec{v}_{1}\cdot\vec{v}_{2})\vec{v}_{2}\right]\cdot[(\vec{a}_{1}+\vec{a}_{2})\times\hat{n}]\,.\end{split}

(179)

The
[1c]
part is the most complicated:

\displaystyle\begin{split}H^{[1c](3)}_{\mathrm{SO}}&=-\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{8r^{2}}\left[-\vec{v}_{1}^{2}(\vec{v}_{1}\times\vec{a}_{1})\cdot\hat{n}+\vec{v}_{2}^{2}(\vec{v}_{2}\times\vec{a}_{2})\cdot\hat{n}\right.\\
&\hskip 71.13188pt+2(\vec{v}_{c}^{2}-3(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{v}_{c})^{2})(\vec{v}_{1}\times\vec{a}_{1}-\vec{v}_{2}\times\vec{a}_{2})\cdot\hat{n}\\
&\hskip 71.13188pt\left.+2(3\vec{v}_{1}^{2}+3\vec{v}_{2}^{2}-8(\vec{v}_{1}\cdot\vec{v}_{2}))(\vec{v}_{1}\times\vec{a}_{1}-\vec{v}_{2}\times\vec{a}_{2})\cdot\hat{n}\right]\,.\end{split}

(180)

The
[1d]
part is just as easy as the
[1b]
part:

\displaystyle\begin{split}H^{[1d](3)}_{\mathrm{SO}}&=-\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{2r^{2}}(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{v}_{c})(\vec{v}_{1}\times\vec{a}_{1}-\vec{v}_{2}\times\vec{a}_{2})\cdot\vec{v}_{c}\\
&=\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{2r^{2}}(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{v}_{c})(\zeta_{2}\vec{a}_{1}+\zeta_{1}\vec{a}_{2})\cdot(\vec{v}_{1}\times\vec{v}_{2})\,.\end{split}

(181)

Adding up all four groups, we finally obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}H^{[1](3)}_{\mathrm{SO}}&=\frac{Gm_{1}m_{2}}{8r^{2}}\sum_{a=1,2}A^{(3)}_{a}\,,\\
A^{(3)}_{1}&=(a_{1},v_{1},n)\left[5\vec{v}_{1}^{2}+8\vec{v}_{1}\cdot\vec{v}_{2}-2\vec{v}_{2}^{2}-6\vec{v}_{c}^{2}+18(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{v}_{c})^{2}\right]\\
&\quad+(a_{1},v_{2},n)\left[-16\vec{v}_{1}\cdot\vec{v}_{2}+8\vec{v}_{c}^{2}-24(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{v}_{c})^{2}\right]\\
&\qquad+(a_{1},v_{1},v_{2})\left[(4\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}-8)(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{v}_{1})+4\zeta_{2}^{2}(\hat{n}\cdot\vec{v}_{2})\right]\,.\end{split}

(182)

The expression for
A^{(3)}_{2}
is obtained by the exchange symmetry.
