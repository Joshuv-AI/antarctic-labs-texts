Bounded reduction of orthogonal matrices over polynomial rings
Pavel Gvozdevsky

Abstract
. We prove that a matrix from the split orthogonal group over a polynomial ring with coefficients in a small-dimensional ring can be reduced to a smaller matrix by a bounded number of elementary orthogonal transformations. The bound is given explicitly. This result is an effective version of the early stabilisation of the orthogonal K 1 K_{1} functor proven by Suslin and Kopeiko in [ 39 ] . Since the similar effective results for special linear and symplectic groups are obtained by Vaserstein in [ 44 ] , the present paper closes the problem for split classical groups.

Introduction

This paper deals with the last remaining case of bounded reduction in split classical groups over polynomial rings. Namely we study the case of orthogonal groups.

Chevalley groups over certain rings have bounded width with respect to the elementary generators. For example this holds for Dedekind domains of arithmetic type, see

[

A given Chevalley group
G
over a given ring is said to have bounded reduction if any element of
G
can be decomposed as a product of bounded number of elementary generators and one (not necessarily elementary) element from a certain subsystem subgroup. In other words, it means that one can reduce any element to the subsystem subgroup by bounded number of elementary transformations. Without requirement for the number of elementary transformations to be bounded this property is called the surjective stability of the
K_{1}
-functor. In papers
[

Actually, from the proofs of the theorems in these papers one can recover the bound on the required number of elementary transformations, despite the fact that this bound is not stated in papers explicitly. Therefore, these are results on bounded reduction.

However, conditions on stable rank are still very strong. Even though small Jacobson dimension implies small stable rank, rings with large Jacobson dimension usually fail to have small stable rank. In the present paper, we consider another important class of rings. Namely we take a polynomial ring in arbitrary number of variables with coefficients in a small-dimensional ring. Here we use Krull dimension because the techniques require for dimension to behave well with respect to adding an independent variable.

Without the bound on the number of elementary transformations similar result for classical groups is known. This is so called early surjective stability of the

From this result he deduced that the elementary subgroup of the general linear group over an arbitrary finitely generated commutative ring has Kazhdan’s property (T).

2.
Preliminaries and notation

Rings, ideals and dimensions

By a ring we always mean associative and commutative ring with unity.

If
R
is a ring, then by
R^{*}
we denote the set of invertible elements in
R
.

For the elements
r_{1}
,
\ldots
,
r_{k}\in R
, we denote by
\langle r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}\rangle
the ideal in
R
generated by these elements.

In the present paper we use three different notions of a ring dimension.

•

By
\dim R
we denote Krull dimension of the ring
R
. That is the supremum of the lengths of all chains of prime ideals.

•

By
\Jdim R=\dim\Max(R)
we denote the dimension of the maximal spectrum
\Max(R)
of the ring
R
. It is equal to the supremum of the lengths of all chains of such prime ideals that coincide with its Jacobson radical.

•

By
\BSdim R
we denote the Bass–Serre dimension of a ring
R
. That is the minimal
\delta
such that
\Max(R)
is a finite union of irreducible Noetherian subspaces of dimension not greater than
\delta
.

Obviously, for a Noetherian ring

we have

\BSdim R\leqslant\Jdim R\leqslant\dim R\text{.}

The following property of Bass–Serre dimension is well known; see Lemma 4.17 in
[
2
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
R
be a ring with
\BSdim R=\delta<\infty
. Then it has a finite collection
P_{1}
,
\ldots
,
P_{m}
of maximal ideals, such that for any element
s\in R\setminus\bigcup_{i}P_{i}
we have
\BSdim R/(s)<\delta
. In case where
\delta=0
, this means that

.

Even split orthogonal group

Let
R
be a commutative ring and
V
be a free
R
-module of rank
2r
with basis
e_{1}
,
\ldots
,
e_{r}
,
e_{-r}
,
\ldots
,
e_{-1}
; this ordering of the basis elements will be used throughout the paper. Consider the quadratic form

q(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}x_{-i}\text{,}

where

are the coordinates of

The group of automorphism of

preserving the form

is called the even split orthogonal group and denoted by

. We identify its elements with their matrices in the basis
e_{1}
,
\ldots
,
e_{r}
,
e_{-r}
,
\ldots
,
e_{-1}

For an element

T_{i,j}(\xi)=T_{-j,-i}(-\xi)=e+\xi e_{i,j}-\xi e_{-j,-i}\text{.}

Such matrices are called elementary orthogonal transvections. It is easy to see that
T_{i,j}(\xi)\in O(2r,R)
.

The subgroup of
O(2r,R)
, generated by all elementary orthogonal transvections is called the elementary subgroup and denoted by
EO(2r,R)
.

For any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
EO(2r,R)^{\leqslant N}
the subset of
EO(2r,R)
consisting of matrices that can be expressed as the product of no more than
N
elementary orthogonal transvections.

Odd split orthogonal group

Now let
V
be a free
R
-module of rank
2r+1
with basis
e_{1}
,
\ldots
,
e_{r}
,
e_{0}
,
e_{-r}
,
\ldots
,
e_{-1}
, and the quadratic form

on the module

defined as follows

q(x)=x_{0}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}x_{i}x_{-i}\text{.}

The group of automorphism of
V
preserving the form
q
is called the odd split orthogonal group and denoted by
O(2r+1,R)
.

In the odd case, we have two types of elementary orthogonal transvections. First type is the same as in the even case:

T_{i,j}(\xi)=T_{-j,-i}(-\xi)=e+\xi e_{i,j}-\xi e_{-j,-i}\qquad i,j=1,\ldots,-1\text{,}\;i\neq\pm j\text{,}

and we refer to them as long root transvections. Second type is called short root transvections, and those are the matrices

T_{i,0}(\xi)=e+2\xi e_{i,0}-\xi e_{0,-i}-\xi^{2}e_{i,-i}\qquad i=\pm 1,\ldots\pm r\text{.}

Remark
.

The terms "long root" and "short root" come from the terminology for Chevalley groups, which are defined by root systems; see, for example,
[
15
]
.

The even split orthogonal group corresponds to the root system

D_{n}=\{e_{i}\pm e_{j}\colon i\neq j,1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n\}\text{,}

in which all the roots have the same length.

However, the odd split orthogonal group corresponds to the root system

B_{n}=\{e_{i}\pm e_{j}\colon i\neq j,1\leqslant i,j\leqslant n\}\cup\{e_{i}\colon 1\leqslant i\leqslant n\}\text{,}

in which there are long roots (of length
\sqrt{2}
) and short roots (of length 1).

As in the even case, we denote by
EO(2r+1,R)
the subgroup of
O(2r+1,R)
, generated by all elementary orthogonal transvections, and by
EO(2r+1,R)^{\leqslant N}
, the subset of
EO(2r+1,R)
consisting of matrices that can be expressed as product of no more than
N
elementary orthogonal transvections.

Obviously, the group
O(2r,R)
embeds into
O(2r+1,R)
as the stabiliser of
e_{0}
.

3.
Absolute flexible stable rank

In this section, we introduce a new type of stable rank condition called the
absolute flexible stable rank
. Here is the definition.

Definition 3.1
.

A commutative ring
A
satisfies the absolute flexible stable rank condition
\mathrm{AFSR}_{d}
if for any row
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{d})
with coordinates in
A
, there exists an element
c_{1}\in A
such that for any invertible element
\varepsilon_{1}\in A^{*}
, there exists
c_{2}\in A
such that for any
\varepsilon_{2}\in A^{*}
,
\ldots
, there exists
c_{d-1}\in A
such that for any
\varepsilon_{d-1}\in A^{*}
, every maximal ideal of
A
containing the ideal
\langle b_{1}+\varepsilon_{1}c_{1}b_{d},\ldots,b_{d-1}+\varepsilon_{d-1}c_{d-1}b_{d}\rangle
contains already the ideal
\langle b_{1},\ldots,b_{d}\rangle
.

One can think of it as follows. Two players are playing a game. Player 1 chooses a row
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{d})
with coordinates in
A
. Then they take turns starting with Player 2. Player 2 in his
i
-th turn chooses element
c_{i}\in A
; after that Player 1 in his turn chooses invertible element
\varepsilon_{i}\in A^{*}
. Player 2 wins if after
d
turns every maximal ideal of
A
containing the ideal
\langle b_{1}+\varepsilon_{1}c_{1}b_{d},\ldots,b_{d-1}+\varepsilon_{d-1}c_{d-1}b_{d}\rangle
contains already the ideal
\langle b_{1},\ldots,b_{d}\rangle
. A commutative ring
A
satisfies the absolute flexible stable rank condition
\mathrm{AFSR}_{d}
if Player 2 has a winning strategy.

Now we show that the condition
\mathrm{AFSR}_{d}
holds for small-dimensional rings. That generalises the result of
[
11
]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
A
be a commutative ring. Assume that
\Max(A)
is Noetherian and
\Jdim A\leqslant d-2
. Then
A
satisfies
\mathrm{AFSR}_{d}
.

Proof.

Following
[
11
]
, for any ideal
I\unlhd A
we denote by
J(I)
the intersection of all the maximal ideals containing
I
, and we set
J=\{I\unlhd A\mid J(I)=I\}
. The ideals from
J
are in correspondence with the closed subsets of
\Max(A)
.

For an ideal
I\unlhd A
, a prime ideal
P\in J
is called a
component
of
I
if
P
is minimal among the primes of
J
that contains
I
. The assumption for
\Max(A)
to be Noetherian implies that any ideal has only finitely many components.

Now we show that making his
i
-th turn Player 2 can guarantee that any component of ideal
I=\langle b_{1}+\varepsilon_{1}c_{1}b_{d},\ldots,b_{i-1}+\varepsilon_{i-1}c_{i-1}b_{d}\rangle
(for
i=1
set
I=0
) that contains
b_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}c_{i}b_{d}
also contains
b_{d}
, and that doing so is a winning strategy.

When the
i
-th turn of Player 2 starts, the ideal
I
is already fixed and has finitely many component. Let
X
be the set of components of
I
that does not contain
b_{d}
. All Player 2 has to do is to guarantee that
b_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}c_{i}b_{d}
is not contained in the union of ideals from
X
.

Set
Y=\{P\in X\mid b_{i}\in P\}
. Since the components of
I
cannot contain each other, we can choose for any
\mathfrak{A}
,
\mathfrak{B}\in X
an element
z(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})\in\mathfrak{A}\setminus\mathfrak{B}
. Then to achive his goal Player 2 can set

c_{i}=\sum_{\mathfrak{B}\in Y}\prod_{\mathfrak{A}\in X\setminus\{\mathfrak{B}\}}z(\mathfrak{A},\mathfrak{B})\text{.}

Let us prove that Player 2 won. Let
b_{i}^{\prime}=b_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}c_{i}b_{d}
,
i=1,\ldots,d-1
. Assume that some maximal ideal
\mathfrak{M}
contains
\langle b_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,b_{d-1}^{\prime}\rangle
, but does not contain
b_{d}
. We claim that in this case there exists a chain of primes in
J

P_{0}\subsetneq P_{1}\subsetneq\ldots\subsetneq P_{d-1}=\mathfrak{M}\text{,}

which contradicts the assumption on
\Jdim A
.

We build this chain from the end in such a way that
\langle b_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,b_{i}^{\prime}\rangle\leqslant P_{i}
but
b_{i+1}^{\prime}\notin P_{i}
. We set
P_{d-1}=\mathfrak{M}
. Then for
i<d-1
we choose
P_{i}
to be the component of
\langle b_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,b_{i}^{\prime}\rangle
that is contained in
P_{i+1}
, such a component exists by Zorn’s lemma. Since
P_{i}\subseteq\mathfrak{M}
, it follows that
b_{d}\notin P_{i}
, and by construction of
b_{i+1}^{\prime}
, this implies that
b_{i+1}^{\prime}\notin P_{i}
.

The fact that
b_{i+1}^{\prime}\notin P_{i}
guarantees that the inclusions in the chain are proper.
∎

Proof.

such that
b^{-}=s^{\widetilde{k}}(s^{l}(b^{-})^{\prime})=Mb^{+}

∎

Lemma 6.6
