Mean Curvature Flow with Boundary

Brian White

Stanford University

Communicated by Carlo Sinestrari

Abstract.

We develop a theory of surfaces with boundary moving by mean curvature flow.
In particular, we prove a general existence theorem using elliptic regularization,
and we prove boundary regularity
at all positive times under very mild hypotheses.

Keywords.
Mean curvature flow, boundary, regularity

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study mean curvature flow for surfaces with boundary:
each point moves so that the normal component of its velocity is equal to the mean
curvature, and the boundary remains fixed. (More generally, the boundary can
be time-dependent, but prescribed.)
In particular,

(1)

We define
integral Brakke flows with boundary
and prove the basic properties.
This is a rather general class that includes network flows. See §
5
.

(2)

We define the subclass of
standard Brakke flows with boundary
.
These are flows as in (
1
) with additional nice properties. In particular, for almost all
times, the moving surface has the prescribed boundary in the sense
of mod
2
homology. This condition excludes, for example, surfaces
with triple junctions (or, more generally, with odd-order junctions). See §
13
.

(3)

Following Ilmanen
[
11
]
, we use elliptic regularization to prove existence
of standard Brakke flows with boundary for any prescribed initial surface. See §
14
.

(4)

We prove a strong boundary regularity theorem for standard Brakke flows
with boundary. See §
17
.

As a special case of some of the results
(Theorems
14.1
and
17.1
), we have

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
N
be a smooth, compact,
(m+1)
-dimensional Riemannian manifold with smooth, strictly mean-convex boundary.
Let
M_{0}
be a smoothly embedded
m
-dimensional submanifold of
N
whose boundary is
a smooth submanifold
\Gamma
of
\partial N
.
(More generally,
M_{0}
can be any
m
-rectifiable set of finite
m
-dimensional measure whose boundary,
in the sense of mod
2
flat chains, is a smoothly embedded submanifold
\Gamma
of
\partial N
.)
Then there is a standard Brakke flow

t\in[0,\infty)\mapsto M(t)

with boundary
\Gamma
such that
M(0)=M_{0}
.
Furthermore, if
M(\cdot)
is any standard Brakke flow with boundary
\Gamma
, then the flow is smooth
(with multiplicity one) in a spacetime neighborhood of each point
(p,t)
with
p\in\Gamma
and
t>0
.

Thus (under the hypotheses of the theorem) we have boundary regularity at
all
positive times, even after
interior singularities may have occurred.

The regularity in Theorem
1.1
is uniform as
t\to\infty
.
For given any sequence of times
t_{i}\to\infty
, there is a subsequence
t_{i(j)}
such that the time-translated
flows

t\in[-t_{i(j)},\infty)\mapsto M_{i(j)}(t):=M(t_{i(j)}+t)

converge to a standard eternal limit flow
M^{\prime}(\cdot)

by §
10
and Theorem
13.1
. Since the area of
M(t)
is a decreasing function of
t
,
the area of
M^{\prime}(t)
is constant (it is equal to the limit as
t\to\infty
of the area of
M(t)
). It follows that the
M^{\prime}(t)
are stationary integral varifolds
and therefore non-moving (i.e., independent of
t
).
The limit flow is regular
at the boundary by Theorem
17.1
,
and thus the convergence
M_{i(j)}(\cdot)\to M^{\prime}(\cdot)
is smooth
near the boundary by the local regularity theory in
[
20
]
.

The notion of
standard
Brakke flow with boundary is crucial in Theorem
1.1
:
the regularity assertion of Theorem
1.1
is false for general integral Brakke flows
with boundary, because interior singularities can move into the boundary.
Consider, for example, three points
A
,
B
, and
C
on the unit circle in
\mathbf{R}^{2}

and consider a configuration consisting of three curves in the interior of the triangle
ABC

such that the three curves meet at equal angles at a point
P
in the interior of the disk and such
the other endpoints of the curves are the three points
A
,
B
, and
C
. The configuration evolves so that the three points on the unit circle are fixed,
and so that interior points move with normal velocity equal to the curvature. This implies
that the triple junction
P(t)
moves in such a way that the curves continue to meet at equal
angles at the junction. If each interior angle of the triangle
ABC
is less than
120^{\circ}
,
then the triple junction remains in the interior, and we have boundary regularity at all times.
However, if one of the angles is greater than
120^{\circ}
, then
P(t)
will bump into the corresponding
vertex in finite time, thus creating a boundary singularity.

The flow described in the previous paragraph is an integral Brakke flow with boundary
\{A,B,C\}
.
However, it is not a standard Brakke flow with boundary
\{A,B,C\}
, because if we think
of the network as a mod
2
chain, then the boundary contains
P(t)
in addition to
A
,
B
, and
C
.

It is natural to wonder whether such a boundary singularity could occur if the original surface
is smooth and embedded. In the case of curves, the answer is “no": the flow would remain smooth everywhere for all time
by the analog of Grayson’s Theorem. However, although I do not yet have a proof, I believe
that there is an integral Brakke flow

t\in[0,\infty)\mapsto M(t)

with boundary
\Gamma
, where
\Gamma
consists of smooth embedded curves in the unit
sphere in
\mathbf{R}^{3}
, such that
M(0)
is a smoothly embedded surface in the unit ball
and such that later the moving surface develops a triple junction curve that eventually
bumps into the boundary.
Note that this could only happen if we had non-uniqueness, since by
Theorem
1.1
there is a standard Brakke flow
M^{\prime}(\cdot)
with the same
initial surface and the same boundary, and that flow never develops boundary singularities.
Of course the two flows are equal at least until singularities occur, but they must differ as soon as
M(\cdot)

has a triple junction curve.

In Theorem
1.1
, the condition that
\Gamma
lie in the boundary of
N

is also crucial. In another paper
[
23
]
, we show that there is a standard mean curvature flow with boundary that starts
with a smoothly
embedded Möbius strip in
\mathbf{R}^{3}
and that develops a boundary singularity
at which the tangent flow is given by a smoothly embedded, non-orientable shrinker with straight line boundary.
For oriented surfaces, the situation is very different: we prove
[
23
]
*Theorem 1 that
if
M
is an
m
-dimensional, smoothly embedded shrinker in
\mathbf{R}^{m+1}
with an
(m-1)
-dimensional
linear subspace as boundary, then
M
is a flat halfspace.

The regularity part of Theorem
1.1
is a consequence of the
following general theorem (see Theorem
16.2
):

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose that
t\in[0,T]\mapsto M(t)
is an
m
-dimensional standard mean curvature
flow with boundary
\Gamma
in a smooth,
(m+1)
-dimensional Riemannian manifold.
If a tangent flow at
(p,t)
is contained in a wedge, where
p\in\Gamma
and
t>0
,
then
(p,t)
is a regular point of the flow
M(\cdot)
.

Two features of this paper seem to be new even for Brakke flows without boundary.
First, when taking limits of Brakke flows, we get improved subsequential convergence
of the mean curvature for almost all times; see Remark
10.3
.
Second,
to prove Huisken’s monotonicity formula, one needs to know that the mean curvature vector is orthogonal
to the variety almost everywhere. Brakke
[
3
]
*§5 proved such orthogonality
for arbitrary integral varifolds of bounded first variation. However, the proof is rather
long (40 pages). In this paper, we give a much easier proof that such orthogonality is preserved when
taking weak limits of mean curvature flows. Thus, in particular, orthogonality holds in
flows coming from elliptic regularization.
For this reason, we have chosen to include orthogonality of mean curvature as part of the definition
of Brakke flow.

For simplicity, in most of the paper we consider flows in which the boundary is fixed.
In §
18
, we indicate
how to modify the theory for moving boundaries.

In §
19
, we show that a certain weak notion of orientability
is preserved when taking weak limits of flows.

Although mean curvature flow has been extensively studied, there have been
only a few investigations of mean curvature flow of surfaces with boundary.
The papers
[
19
]
and
[
20
]
dealt with mean curvature flow of surfaces
both with and without boundary.
In
[
17
]
, Stone proved a theorem analogous to the boundary regularity part
of Theorem
1.1
, but only
at the first singular time and under additional, rather restrictive hypotheses. In particular, the moving surface
was assumed to be mean convex and to satisfy a Type I estimate.
In
[
10
]
, mean curvature flow with boundary was used to prove
sharp lower density bounds for area-minimizing hypercones.

2.
Notation

In this paper,
U
is a smooth Riemannian manifold (possibly with smooth boundary).
We do not assume that
U
is complete: it may be an open subset
of a larger Riemannian manifold. We let
G_{m}(U)
denote the Grassman bundle of
pairs
(x,P)
where

and

is an
m
-dimensional linear subspace of
\operatorname{Tan}(U,x)
.
We let
\mathcal{X}(U)
denote the space of continuous, compactly supported vectorfields on
U
.
We let
\mathcal{X}_{m}(U)
denote the space of continuous, compactly supported functions on
G_{m}(U)

that assign to each
(x,P)
in
G_{m}(U)
a vector in
\operatorname{Tan}(U,x)
.

If
M
is a Radon Measure on
U
and if
f
is a function on
U
, we let

Mf=\int f\,dM.

If
\Gamma
is a
k
-dimensional submanifold of
U
(or, more generally, a
k
-rectifiable
set of locally finite
k
-dimensional measure), then (by slight abuse of notation) we
will also use

to denote the associated Radon measure.
Thus

\int f\,d\Gamma=\int_{\Gamma}f\,d\mathcal{H}^{k}

and

\Gamma(K)=\mathcal{H}^{k}(\Gamma\cap K).

3.
L^{p}
vectorfields

In the following theorem,
1_{K}
denotes the characteristic function of the set
K
.
Thus if
M
is a Radon measure on
U
and if

\|Y1_{K}\|_{L^{p}(M)}=\left(\int_{K}|Y|^{p}\,dM\right)^{1/p}.

Similarly,

\|Y1_{K}\|_{L^{\infty}(M)}

is the essential supremum of
|Y|
on the set

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
V_{i}
and
V
be rectifiable
m
-varifolds in
U

such that
V_{i}\rightharpoonup V
. Let
M_{i}
and
M
be the associated Radon measures on
U
.
Suppose that
p\in(1,\infty]
and that
Y_{i}
is a Borel vectorfield on
U
such that

c_{K}:=\sup_{i}\|Y_{i}1_{K}\|_{L^{p}(M_{i})}<\infty

for every
K\subset\subset U
.
Then, after passing to a subsequence, there is a Borel vectorfield
Y
on
U
in
L^{p}_{\textnormal{loc}}(M)

such that

\int X(x,\operatorname{Tan}(M_{i}(x))\cdot Y_{i}(x)\,dM_{i}(x)\to\int X(x,\operatorname{Tan}(M,x))\cdot Y(x)\,dM(x)

for every
X\in\mathcal{X}_{m}(U)
.

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle L_{i}:\mathcal{X}_{m}(U)\to\mathbf{R},

\displaystyle L_{i}(X)=\int X(x,\operatorname{Tan}(M_{i}(x))\cdot Y_{i}(x)\,dM_{i}(x).

If
K\subset\subset U
and if
X\in\mathcal{X}_{m}(U)
is supported in
\{(x,P):x\in K\}
, then
by Hölder’s Inequality,

(3.1)

|L_{i}(X)|\leqslant c_{K}\left(\int|X(x,\operatorname{Tan}(M_{i},x))|^{q}\,dM_{i}(x)\right)^{1/q},

where
q=p/(p-1)
, or, equivalently,

(3.2)

|L_{i}(X)|\leqslant c_{K}\left(\int|X(x,P)|^{q}\,dV_{i}(x,P)\right)^{1/q}.

From (
3.1
), we see that

(3.3)

|L_{i}(X)|\leqslant c_{K}d_{K}{}^{1/q}\sup|X|

where
d_{K}:=\sup_{i}M_{i}(K)
. Note that
d_{K}<\infty
by weak convergence of
M_{i}
to
M
.

By (
3.3
) and Banach-Alaoglu, we can assume,
after passing to a subsequence, that there is an
L:\mathcal{X}_{m}(U)\to\mathbf{R}
such that

L_{i}(X)\to L(X)\quad\text{for every $X\in\mathcal{X}_{m}(U)$}.

Letting
i\to\infty
in (
3.2
) gives

(3.4)

|L(X)|\leqslant c_{K}\left(\int|X(x,P)|^{q}\,dV(x,P)\right)^{1/q}.

By the Riesz Representation Theorem, there is an
M
-measurable vectorfield
\tilde{Y}(x,P)

such that

(3.5)

L(X)=\int X(x,P)\cdot\tilde{Y}(x,P)\,dV(x,P)

for every
X\in\mathcal{X}_{m}(U)
. Since
V
is rectifiable and since
M
is the associated Radon measure on
U
,
we can rewrite (
3.5
) as

L(X)=\int X(x,\operatorname{Tan}(M,x))\cdot\tilde{Y}(x,\operatorname{Tan}(M,x))\,dM(x).

Thus if we set
Y(x)=\tilde{Y}(x,\operatorname{Tan}(M,x))
, then we have

L(X)=\int X(x,\operatorname{Tan}(M,x))\cdot Y(x)\,dM(x),

as desired.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

In Theorem
3.1
, if
Y_{i}(x)
is perpendicular to
\operatorname{Tan}(M_{i},x)
for

-almost every

\operatorname{Tan}(M,x)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}|H_{N}|^{2}-H_{N}\cdot\nabla\hat{\rho}-H\cdot\nabla\hat{\rho}+\frac{|\nabla^{\perp}\hat{\rho}|^{2}}{\hat{\rho}}

7.
Monotonicity with Boundary in Euclidean Space

The monotonicity inequality becomes simpler for mean curvature flow with fixed boundary in a Euclidean space.
(The material in this section and in Sections
8
and
9
is not used in the rest of the paper.)

Let
\Gamma
be a smooth, properly embedded
(m-1)
-dimensional manifold in
\mathbf{R}^{n}

For

over

with vertex

\{v+s(x-v):x\in\Gamma,\,s\geqslant 1\}.

The

The exterior cone (counting multiplicity) determines a Radon measure

on

dE_{\Gamma,v}=\theta_{\Gamma,v}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m}.

Theorem 7.1
.

Suppose

is a
m
-dimensional Brakke flow in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
with boundary
\Gamma

Let

\displaystyle(M(a)+E_{\Gamma,v})\psi(\cdot,a)-(M(b)+E_{\Gamma,v})\psi(\cdot,b)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\int\left|H-\frac{\nabla^{\perp}\psi}{\psi}\right|^{2}\psi\,dM(t)\,dt+\int_{a}^{b}\int|(\nu_{M}+\nu_{E})\cdot\nabla\psi|\,d\Gamma\,dt.

Proof.

By translating and parabolically dilating, it suffices to consider the case when

\displaystyle(M\rho)(a)-(M\rho)(b)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}\int\left|H-\frac{(\nabla^{\perp}\rho)}{\rho}\right|^{2}\rho\,dM(t)\,dt

\displaystyle+\int_{a}^{b}\int\nu_{M}\cdot\nabla\rho\,d\Gamma\,dt.

) follows from (
7.1
)
since
\psi(\cdot,a)
attains its maximum value
(4\pi(t_{0}-a))^{-m/2}

at the point

.
The inequality (

) follows since

and
since a straightforward calculation shows that

(Alternatively, (

8.
Entropy

In this section, we adapt the concept of entropy in mean curvature flow to mean curvature flow with boundary.

Suppose that
M
is a properly embedded
m
-dimensional manifold (without boundary) in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
Recall that the
entropy
of
M
is

(8.1)

\displaystyle e(M)

\displaystyle=\sup_{a\in\mathbf{R}^{n},\,\lambda>0}(4\pi\lambda)^{-m/2}\int_{x\in M}e^{-|x-a|^{2}/(4\lambda)}\,dx

\displaystyle=\sup_{\tilde{M}}\,(4\pi)^{-m/2}\int_{x\in\tilde{M}}e^{-|x|^{2}/4}\,dx,

where the second supremum is over all surfaces
\tilde{M}
obtained from
M
by translating and dilating.

More generally, suppose
M
is a Radon measure on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. The
m
-dimensional entropy of
M
is

(8.2)

\displaystyle e(M)

\displaystyle=\sup_{a\in\mathbf{R}^{n},\,\lambda>0}(4\pi\lambda)^{-m/2}\int e^{-|x-a|^{2}/(4\lambda)}\,dM

\displaystyle=\sup_{\tilde{M}}\int(4\pi)^{-m/2}\int_{x\in\tilde{M}}e^{-|x|^{2}/4}\,d\tilde{M}.

where the supremum is over all Radon measures
\tilde{M}
obtained from
M
by translation and
m
-dimensional
scaling.
(If
M
is a Radon measure and
\lambda>0
, the
m
-dimensional rescaling of
M
by
\lambda
is the measure

\lambda_{\#}M
obtained by pushing
M
forward by
x\mapsto\lambda x
and then multiplying by
\lambda^{m}
.)

If
t\in I\mapsto M(t)
is a smooth mean curvature flow of properly immersed
m
-manifolds in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
or, more generally, if it is an
m
-dimensional Brakke flow in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, then, by Huisken’s monotonicity formula, the entropy

e(M(t))
is a decreasing function of
t\in I
.

Now suppose that
\Gamma
is a smooth, properly embedded
(m-1)
-dimensional manifold in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, and suppose that
M
is an
m
-manifold with boundary
\Gamma
.
If
v\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
, we let
[M,\Gamma,v]
be the piecewise-smooth manfold obtained by attaching the exterior cone

\{v+s(x-v):v\geqslant 1\}

to
M
.
More generally, if
M
is any Radon measure on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, we let
[M,\Gamma,v]
be the Radon measure

M+E_{\Gamma,v}.

We define

(8.3)

\Theta_{\textnormal{gauss}}(M,\Gamma,v,r)=\int\frac{\exp(-|x-v|^{2}/(4r^{2}))}{(4\pi r^{2})^{m/2}}\,d[M,\Gamma,v]x+\frac{1}{2}1_{\Gamma}(x),

where

1_{\Gamma}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $x\in\Gamma$, and}\\
0&\text{if $x\notin\Gamma$}.\end{cases}

The term
\frac{1}{2}1_{\Gamma}(x)
is necessary to make
\Theta_{\textnormal{gauss}}(M,\Gamma,v,r)
continuous as a function of
v
.
(To see that
\Theta_{\textnormal{gauss}}(M,\Gamma,v,r)
depends continuously on
v
, note that if
v_{i}\notin\Gamma
converges to
v\in\Gamma
,
then, after passing to a subsequence,
E_{\Gamma,v_{i}}
converges to
E_{\Gamma,v}
together with a halfplane bounded by
\operatorname{Tan}(\Gamma,v)
.)

We define the
m
-dimensional entropy
e(M;\Gamma)
of the pair
(M,\Gamma)
to be

(8.4)

\displaystyle e(M;\Gamma)

\displaystyle=\sup_{a\in\mathbf{R}^{n},r>0}\Theta_{\textnormal{gauss}}(M,\Gamma,a,r)

\displaystyle=\sup_{(\tilde{M},\tilde{\Gamma})}\Theta_{\textnormal{gauss}}(\tilde{M},\tilde{\Gamma},0,1),

where the second supremum is over all pairs
(\tilde{M},\tilde{\Gamma})
obtained from
(M,\Gamma)
by
translation and dilation. Note that
e(M;\emptyset)=e(M)
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
t\in I\mapsto M(t)
be an
m
-dimensional Brakke flow in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
with boundary
\Gamma
. Then

t\in I\mapsto e(M(t);\Gamma)

is a decreasing function of
t
.

Proof.

This is an immediate consequence of the Monotonicity Theorem
7.1
.
∎

Theorem 8.2
.

Suppose that
M
is a shrinker, i.e., that

is a mean curvature flow. Then, in the definition (
8.1
) of entropy, the supremum is attained for
a=0
and
\lambda=1
.
Furthermore, the density of
M
at every point

is
\leqslant e(M)
, and if equality holds at any point,
then
M
is a cone about that point.

See for example
[
5
]
*lemma 7.01, or, for the first assertion, the proof of Theorem
8.3
below.
(In
[
5
]
*lemma 7.01, the shrinker is assumed to have polynomial area growth, but that assumption is not necessary since every shrinker has polynomial
area growth. See for example
[
4
]
*Proposition 10.)

Theorem 8.3
.

Suppose that
M
is an
m
-dimensional shrinker in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
whose boundary is an
(m-1)
-dimensional
linear subspace

.
Then in the definition (
8.4
) of

, the supremum is attained for
a=0
and
r=1
.
Furthermore, the extended density of
\Sigma
at each point is
\leqslant e(M,L)
, and if equality holds at any point, then

Proof.

The proof is essentially the same as in the boundaryless case.
For the reader’s convenience, we give the proof of the first assertion: that the supremum in (

)
is attained for
a=0
and
r=1
.
Let

M(t)=\begin{cases}|t|^{1/2}_{\#}M&(t\leqslant 0),\\
0&(t>0).\end{cases}

Thus

is a mean curvature flow with boundary

.
Let

\Theta=\Theta_{\textnormal{gauss}}(M,\Gamma,0,1)=\Theta_{\textnormal{gauss}}(M(-1),\Gamma,0,1)

Trivially

By self-similarity,

for all

.
By monotonicity (Theorem

),

. From polynomial area growth of

]
*Proposition 10,

.

,

∎

9.
Entropy and Maximal Density Ratio

Entropy is closely related to a more geometrically intuitive notion, namely the maximal density ratio. In particular,
for any surface (or Radon measure) the ratio of the two quantities is bounded above and below.

The (
m
-dimensional)
maximal density ratio

\operatorname{mdr}(M)
of a surface
M

in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
is defined to be

\operatorname{mdr}(M):=\sup_{x\in\mathbf{R}^{n},\,r>0}\,\frac{\mathcal{H}^{m}(M\cap\mathbf{B}(x,r))}{\omega_{m}r^{m}},

where
\omega_{m}
is the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbf{R}^{m}
.
Of course
\operatorname{mdr}(M)
is invariant under rigid motions and scaling.
More generally, if
M
is a Radon measure on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, the the
m
-dimensional maximal density ratio of
M

is

(9.1)

\operatorname{mdr}(M):=\sup_{a\in\mathbf{R}^{n},\,r>0}\,\frac{M\mathbf{B}(x,r)}{\omega_{m}r^{m}}.

Theorem 9.1
.

\operatorname{mdr}(M)\geqslant e(M)\geqslant c_{m}\operatorname{mdr}(M)
for some
c_{m}>0
.

Proof.

Let us assume that
M
is an
m
-manifold; except for notation, the same proof works for Radon measures. Let
\tilde{M}
be a surface obtained from
M
by translating and scaling. Let

V(r)=\mathcal{H}^{m}(\tilde{M}\cap\mathbf{B}(0,r))

and
\theta=\operatorname{mdr}(M)
. Thus
V(r)\leqslant\omega_{m}\theta r^{m}
, so

(9.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{M}\cap\{|x|\leqslant R\}}e^{-|x|^{2}/4}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{r=0}^{R}e^{-r^{2}/4}V^{\prime}(r)\,dr

\displaystyle=e^{-R^{2}/4}V(R)-\int_{r=0}^{R}\frac{d}{dr}\left(e^{-r^{2}/4}\right)V(r)\,dr

\displaystyle=e^{-R^{2}/4}V(R)+\int_{r=0}^{R}\frac{1}{2}re^{-r^{2}/4}V(r)\,dr

\displaystyle\geqslant e^{-R^{2}/4}V(R).

To prove the lower bound for
e(M)
, let
0<\eta<1
We may assume by translating and scaling that
V(1)\geqslant\eta\omega_{m}\operatorname{mdr}(M)
.
Thus by (
9.2
) with
R=1
,

(4\pi)^{m/2}e(M)\geqslant e^{-1/4}V(1)\geqslant e^{-1/4}(\eta\,\omega_{m})\operatorname{mdr}(M).

Since this holds for all
\eta\in(0,1)
it also holds for
\eta=1
. Thus

e(M)\geqslant(4\pi)^{-m/2}e^{-1/4}\omega_{m}\operatorname{mdr}(M).

To prove the upper bound for
e(M)
, we may assume that
\theta:=\operatorname{mdr}(M)
is finite.
Now
V(r)\leqslant\omega_{m}\theta r^{m}
, so from (
9.2
) we see that

(9.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{M}\cap\{|x|\leqslant R\}}e^{-|x|^{2}/4}\,dx

\displaystyle=e^{-R^{2}/4}V(R)+\int_{r=0}^{R}\frac{1}{2}re^{-r^{2}/4}V(r)\,dr

\displaystyle\leqslant e^{-R^{2}/4}\omega_{m}\theta R^{m}+\int_{r=0}^{R}\frac{1}{2}re^{-r^{2}/4}\omega_{m}\theta r^{m}\,dr.

Letting
R\to\infty
and multiplying by
(4\pi)^{-m/2}
gives

(9.4)

\displaystyle(4\pi)^{-m/2}\int_{\tilde{M}}e^{-|x|^{2}/4}\,dx

\displaystyle\leqslant\theta\int_{r=0}^{R}(4\pi)^{-m/2}(\omega_{m}/2)r^{1+m}e^{-r^{2}/4}\,dr

\displaystyle=\theta.

Taking the supremum over all
\tilde{M}
gives
e(M)\leqslant\theta=\operatorname{mdr}(M)
.

(Here is one way to see that the definite integral on the right side of (
9.4
) is
1
. Note that if
\tilde{M}
is an
m
-plane through the origin, then
V(r)\equiv\omega_{m}r^{m}
, so the inequalities in (
9.3
)
and in (
9.4
) become
equalities. Furthermore, in this case, the left side of (
9.4
) is
1
and
\theta=1
, so
the definite integral on the right side of (
9.4
) must be equal to
1
.)
∎

Remark 9.2
.

The proof shows that if
M
is a Radon measure on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, then
M\rho

is a weighted average of the density ratios
(M\mathbf{B}(0,r))/(\omega_{m}r^{m})
over
r\in(0,\infty)
. More generally,

M\psi_{v,a}
is a weighted average of
(M\mathbf{B}(v,r))/(\omega_{m}r^{m})
over
r\in(0,\infty)
.

Now we turn to manifolds (or varieties) with boundary.
Suppose
\Gamma
is a smooth
(m-1)
-manifold without boundary in
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
If
M
is an
m
-dimensional manifold with boundary
\Gamma
, we let

\Theta(M,\Gamma,a,r)=\frac{\operatorname{area}((M\cup E_{\Gamma,a})\cap\mathbf{B}(a,r))}{\omega_{m}r^{m}}+\frac{1}{2}1_{\Gamma}(x),

where
E_{\Gamma,v}
is the exterior cone over
\Gamma
with vertex
v

(as in Section
7
).

More generally, if
M
is a Radon measure on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
, we let

\Theta(M,\Gamma,a,r)=\frac{(M+E_{\Gamma,a})\mathbf{B}(a,r)}{\omega_{m}r^{m}}+\frac{1}{2}1_{\Gamma}(x),

where
E_{\Gamma,a}
is the Radon measure associated to the exterior cone.

Given a point
a\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
, note that
(M+E_{\Gamma,a})\partial\mathbf{B}(a,r)=0
for almost all
r
, and that for such
r
,
the function
\Theta(M,\Gamma,\cdot,r)
is continuous at
a
.

We also let

\operatorname{mdr}(M;\Gamma)=\sup_{a\in\mathbf{R}^{n},\,r>0}\Theta(M,\Gamma,a,r).

Theorem 9.3
.

Suppose that
\Gamma
is a smooth, property embedded
(m-1)
-dimensional submanifold of
\mathbf{R}^{n}
and that

M
is a Radon measure on
\mathbf{R}^{n}
. Then

\operatorname{mdr}(M;\Gamma)\geqslant e(M;\Gamma)\geqslant c_{m}\operatorname{mdr}(M;\Gamma).

The proof is essentially identical to the proof of Theorem
9.1
.

Remark 9.4
.

In the definition (
8.4
) of
e(M;\Gamma)
, if we fix the point
v
and take the supremum over
r>0
, we get an entropy-like quantity

that depends on

:

Likewise, we can define a fixed-center-point version of the maximal density ratio:

\operatorname{mdr}(M;\Gamma)(a):=\sup_{r>0}\Theta(M,\Gamma,a,r).

Of course

and
\operatorname{mdr}(M;\Gamma)=\sup_{a}\operatorname{mdr}(M;\Gamma)(a)
.
Furthermore, for each
v\in\mathbf{R}^{n}
,

\operatorname{mdr}(M;\Gamma)(v)\geqslant e(M;\Gamma)(v)\geqslant c_{m}\operatorname{mdr}(M;\Gamma)(v),

and if

is an
m
-dimensional Brakke flow with boundary
\Gamma
, then

is a
decreasing function of
t
. The proofs are identical to the proofs of Theorems
9.3

and
8.1
.

In the following theorem, we write

By making a spacetime translation, it suffices to consider the case

For

Definition 11.1
.

We say that an integral Brakke flow
t\in I\mapsto M(t)
with boundary
\Gamma

is

provided the following holds:

.

Equivalently,

.

.

(2)

\partial[M(t)]=[\Gamma(t)]
for almost every

.

Proof.

[

]
*Theorem 4.2 for the proof of

]

, for almost every

[M_{i(j)}(t)]\to[M(t)]

.

Existence

Theorem 14.1
.

Let
N
be a smooth Riemannian manifold.
If
N
has nonempty boundary, we assume that the boundary is smooth and
m
-convex.
Let
\Gamma
be smooth, properly embedded
(m-1)
-dimensional manifold in
N
.
Let
M_{0}
be a smoothly embedded
m
-dimensional manifold in
N
with boundary
\Gamma

and with finite area,
or, more generally, let
M_{0}
be an
m
-rectifiable set of finite
m
-dimensional
measure such that
\partial[M_{0}]=[\Gamma]
.
Then there exists a standard Brakke flow

t\in[0,\infty)\mapsto M(t)

with boundary
\Gamma
such that

M(t)\rightharpoonup\mathcal{H}^{m}\llcorner M_{0}

as
t\to 0
.

If
p\notin\Gamma
and if
M_{0}
is smooth in a neighborhood of
p
, then the flow is smooth
in a spacetime neighborhood of
(p,0)
. If
p\in\Gamma
and if
M_{0}
is
C^{1,\alpha}
in a neighborhood
of
p
, then the flow is parabolically
C^{1}
in a spacetime neighborhood of
(p,0)
.

Recall that
m
-convexity of
\partial N
at a point
p
is the condition that the sum of the smallest
m
principal curvatures of
\partial N

at
p
with respect to the inward unit normal is nonnegative. (Thus
1
-convexity is convexity, and if
\dim N=m+1
, then
m
-convexity is mean-convexity.)
We say that
\partial N
is strictly
m
-convex at
p\in\partial N
if the sum of the smallest
m
-principal curvatures at
p
is
greater than
0
.

Proof.

Let us first prove the theorem assuming that
\partial N
is strictly
m
-convex.

Let
\Gamma^{*}=(M_{0}\times\{0\})\cup(\Gamma\times[0,\infty)
.
Let
\lambda>0
.
Consider a mod
2
flat chain
\Sigma^{\lambda}
in
N\times\mathbf{R}
that minimizes

\int_{(x,z)\in N\times\mathbf{R}}e^{-\lambda z}d\mu_{\Sigma^{\lambda}}(x,z)

subject to
\partial\Sigma^{\lambda}=[\Gamma^{*}]
.
Let
M^{\lambda}
be the associated Radon measure; thus
\Sigma^{\lambda}=[M^{\lambda}]
.

Note that
\Sigma^{\lambda}
is mass-minimizing with respect to the Ilmanen metric
e^{-2\lambda z/m}(g+dz^{2})

(where
g
is the metric on
N
.)
Thus
\operatorname{spt}(M^{\lambda})\setminus\Gamma^{*}
is smooth (with multiplicity
1
) away from a closed set
of Hausdorff dimension
\leqslant m-1

[
8
]
.

(Here is where strict
m
-convexity of
\partial N
is used. Note that strict
m
-convexity of
\partial N
implies strict
(m+1)
-convexity
of
(\partial N)\times\mathbf{R}
with respect to the Ilmanen metric. By the maximum principle in
[
22
]
,
\Sigma^{\lambda}
cannot
touch
(\partial N)\times\mathbf{R}
at any interior point of
\Sigma^{\lambda}
.)

For
t\geqslant 0
, let
M^{\lambda}(t)
be the portion of
M^{\lambda}-\lambda t(0,1)
in
\tilde{N}=N\times(0,\infty)
.
Then

t\in[0,\infty)\mapsto M^{\lambda}(t)

is an integral Brakke flow with boundary
\tilde{\Gamma}:=\Gamma\times(0,\infty)
in
\tilde{N}
.
In fact, it is standard:

(1)

Because
M^{\lambda}
is smooth almost everywhere, the mean curvature vector is orthogonal
to the surface almost everywhere.

(2)

Unit regularity follows from Allard’s Regularity Theorem
[
1
]
*§8
and Boundary Regularity Theorem
[
2
]
*§4
applied to
M^{\lambda}
.

(3)

The mod
2
boundary condition holds by construction.

By
[
11
]
*5.1, 3.2(ii), the areas of the
\mathcal{M}^{\lambda}(t)
have a uniform upper bound on area
as
\lambda\to\infty
:

\operatorname{area}(\mathcal{M}^{\lambda}_{0}\cap\{a<z<a+b\})\leqslant(b+\lambda^{-1})\operatorname{area}(M_{0})

for any
a,b>0
, and thus

\operatorname{area}(\mathcal{M}^{\lambda}(t)\llcorner\{0<z<b\})\leqslant(b+\lambda^{-1})\operatorname{area}(M_{0}).

Consequently
(by Theorems
10.1
,
10.2
, and
13.1
),
the flows
t\in[0,\infty)\mapsto\mathcal{M}^{\lambda}(t)
converge
as
\lambda\to\infty
(after passing to a subsequence)
to a standard Brakke flow
\mathcal{M}(\cdot)
in
\tilde{N}
with boundary
\tilde{\Gamma}
.

Furthermore, as in
[
11
]
,

\mathcal{M}(0)=M_{0}\times(0,\infty)

and

\mathcal{M}(t)=M(t)\times(0,\infty)

(except possibly for countably many
t
), where
M(t)

is a Radon measure in
N
.

Since
\mathcal{M}(\cdot)
is a standard Brakke flow with boundary
\Gamma^{*}
in
N^{*}
,
it follows that
t\in[0,\infty)\mapsto M(t)
is a standard Brakke flow in
N
with boundary
\Gamma

and with

M(0)=M_{0}.

(See
[
11
]
*8.9.)

If
M_{0}
is
C^{1,\alpha}
in a neighborhood of a point
p
, then the flow is parabolically
C^{1}

in a spacetime neighborhood of
(p,0)
by
[
20
]
.
If
M_{0}
is smooth in a neighborhood of a point

,
then the flow is smooth in a spacetime neighborhood of
(p,0)
by
[
13
]
*Corollary A.3.

This completes the proof assuming strict
m
-convexity. For the general case, let
g_{i}
be a sequence of smooth
metrics on
N
such that the
g_{i}
converge smoothly to the original metric and such that
\partial N
is strictly
m
-convex
with respect to each
g_{i}
. Then we get a suitable Brakke flow for each metric
g_{i}
.
By Theorems
10.1
,
10.2
, and
13.1
,
a subsequence of those Brakke flows will converge to a suitable Brakke flow for the metric
g
.

(Theorems

,
10.2
, and

are
stated for a fixed metric, but they hold, with the same proofs, for sequences of metrics.)

Theorem 15.1

(Shrinker Theorem)
.

Let
\Gamma
be an
(m-1)
-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathbf{R}^{m+1}

Suppose that

t\in(-\infty,0)\mapsto S(t)

is an
m

with
\lambda>0

M:=S(-1)

\operatorname{spt}(M)\setminus\Gamma
is
disjoint from some
m

\Gamma
.
Then

is a sum of half-planes (each with boundary

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the set of halfplanes with boundary
\Gamma

that are disjoint from
(\operatorname{spt}M)\setminus\Gamma
.
We claim that
\mathcal{P}
is open. To see this, suppose
P\in\mathcal{P}
.
By rotating, we can assume that

is the halfplane

Let
M^{+}

Let

>0
at some points.
By multiplying by a small positive constant, we can assume that the graph of
f
lies below
M^{+}
.
Extend

to
\mathbf{B}^{m}(0,1)
so that it is odd in

f(-x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m})=-f(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{m}).

Now let

be the solution of the nonparametric MCF equation with

\displaystyle u(\cdot,-1)=f,

\displaystyle u(\cdot,t)|\partial\mathbf{B}^{m}\equiv 0.

By the boundary maximum principle,
c:=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}u(0,0)>0
.
Let
G(t,\epsilon)
be the graph of
u(\cdot,t)-\epsilon
.
Let
\epsilon>0
. By the maximum principle (Theorem
20.1
),

\operatorname{spt}(S^{+}(t))
lies above

for all

.
Hence
\operatorname{spt}(S^{+}(t))
lies in the closed region above

for

.
Equivalently,
\operatorname{spt}(M)
lies in the closed region above

for all
t\in[-1,0)
. At
t\to 0
,

converges
to the plane
\{x:x_{2}=cx_{1}\}
. Thus we see that the halfplane
P_{\lambda}:=\{x:x_{2}=\lambda x_{1},\,x_{1}>0\}

is in
\mathcal{P}
for all
\lambda\in[0,c)
.
Likewise there is a
c^{\prime}<0
such that the halfplane
P_{\lambda}
is disjoint from
M
for all
\lambda\in(c^{\prime},0]

This completes the proof of openess of
\mathcal{P}
.

Now let
P
be a halfplane in the boundary of
\mathcal{P}
. Then
\operatorname{spt}(M)
touches
P
, so, by the strong maximum
principle,
M
contains
P
. (Note that

and the varifold associated to

are both stationary with respect to the shrinker metric,
so
M
contains
P
by the strong maximum principle in
[
16
]
.) Now repeat the process with

replaced by

The process must stop in finitely many steps, since otherwise

would contain infinitely many

\mathbf{R}^{m+1}\setminus\Gamma
.
∎

The Wedge Theorem and Boundary Regularity

Theorem 16.1

(Wedge Theorem)
.

Suppose
W
is a wedge (see Definition
15.2
) in
\mathbf{R}^{m+1}
with edge
\Gamma
.
Suppose

t\in(-\infty,0)\mapsto S(t)

is a self-similar, standard Brakke flow in
W
with boundary
\Gamma
.
Then
S(\cdot)
is a non-moving halfplane with multiplicity
1
.

Proof.

Let
M=S(-1)
.
By Theorem
15.1
,
M=\sum_{i=1}^{k}P_{i}

where each
P_{i}
is a multiplicity-one halfplane with boundary
\Gamma
.
(The
P_{i}
need not be distinct, since a halfplane in the support of
M
is allowed to have any positive integer multiplicity.)
Since
\partial[M]=[\Gamma]
,
k
is odd.

For each
i
, let
\nu_{i}
be the unit vector in the plane of
P_{i}
that is normal to
\Gamma

and that points out from
P_{i}
. For any smooth, compactly supported vectorfield
X
,

\int\operatorname{Div}_{P_{i}}X\,dP_{i}=\int_{\Gamma}X\cdot\nu_{i}\,d\mathcal{H}^{m-1},

so

\displaystyle\int\operatorname{Div}_{M}X\,dM

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{i}\right)\cdot X\,d\mathcal{H}^{m-1}.

Thus

\nu(M,\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{k}.

By definition of mean curvature flow with boundary,
|\nu(M,\cdot)|\leqslant 1
.
Now we use the following elementary fact:
if
\nu^{\prime}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nu_{i}
where the
\nu_{i}=(\cos\theta_{i},\sin\theta_{i})
are unit vectors
with
|\theta_{i}|\leqslant\theta<\pi/2
, then

(16.1)

|\nu^{\prime}|\geqslant\begin{cases}k\cos\theta&\text{if $k$ is even},\\
\sqrt{1+(k^{2}-1)\cos^{2}\theta}&\text{if $k$ is odd}.\end{cases}

(The inequalities (
16.1
) can be proved as follows.
Given
k
and
\theta
, it is easy to show that at the mimimum of
|\nu^{\prime}|
, each
\theta_{i}
is
\pm\theta
.
If
k
is even, the minimum is attained by having half of the
\theta_{i}
equal to
\theta
and the other
half equal to
-\theta
. If
k=2j+1
is odd, the minimum is attained when
j
of the
\theta_{i}
are equal
to
\theta
and
j+1
are equal to
-\theta
.)

In our case, the number of planes (counting multiplicity) in

Theorem 17.1

Suppose

is a smooth,
(m+1)
-dimensional Riemannian manifold with smooth, weakly mean-convex boundary.

is a smooth, properly embedded
(m-1)
-dimensional submanifold of

Suppose

\Gamma

\operatorname{spt}(M(0))\cap\partial N=\Gamma\cap\partial N

Proof.

Note that if

*10.5 or

under Hypothesis (

Since the result is local, it suffices to work in a small neighborhood of the point

Such a neighborhood is diffeomorphic to a halfspace, so we may assume that

In the rest of the proof,

\mathbf{B}^{m+1}(a,r)

is the Euclidean ball of radius

about

\mathbf{R}^{m}
to

(so that the graph lies in

Lemma 17.2
.

For every
\epsilon>0

\displaystyle F(\cdot,0)=f(\cdot),

\displaystyle F(\cdot,t)|\partial\mathbf{B}^{m}(a,r)=f|\partial\mathbf{B}^{m}(a,r),

Proof of lemma.

Let

.
If there were no suitable

for some

Let

Let

over

.
Choose

20.
Appendix: A Strong Maximum Principle

Theorem 20.1
.

Suppose that
t\in[0,T]\mapsto M(t)
is an integral Brakke flow with moving boundary
\Gamma

in a smooth Riemannian manifold
N
.
Suppose that
t\in[0,T]\mapsto D(t)
is a smooth, one-parameter family of compact, smoothly embedded,

m
-dimensional manifolds with boundary in
N
that are moving by mean curvature.
(In other words, the normal velocity at each point is equal to the mean curvature vector at that point.)
If

\displaystyle\text{$D(0)$ is disjoint from $\operatorname{spt}(M(0))$},

\partial D(t)
is disjoint from
\operatorname{spt}M(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
, and

\Gamma(t)
is disjoint from
D(t)
for all
t\in[0,T]
,

then
D(t)
is disjoint from
\operatorname{spt}(M(t))
for all
t\in[0,T]
.

Proof.

Choose
\delta>0
small enough so that

Q:=\cup_{t\in[0,T]}\{x\in N:\operatorname{dist}(x,D(t))\leqslant\delta\}

is compact, and so that for
t\in[0,T]
,

(20.1)

\operatorname{dist}(p,q)>\delta

for all
p\in\Gamma(t)
and
q\in D(t)
, and for all
p\in\operatorname{spt}M(t)
and
q\in\partial D(t)
.

Let
\lambda<0
be a strict lower bound for the Ricci curvature of
N
in the set
Q
.
We claim that

e^{-\lambda t}\operatorname{dist}(D(t),\operatorname{spt}M(t))>\delta

for all
t\in[0,T]
.
For if not, there would be first time
t>0
such that

(20.2)

e^{-\lambda t}\operatorname{dist}(D(t),\operatorname{spt}M(t))=\delta.

At that time, there would be an arc-length parametrized geodesic

\gamma:[0,L]\to N

such that

\displaystyle\gamma(0)\in D(t),

\displaystyle\gamma(L)\in\operatorname{spt}M(t),\,\text{and}

\displaystyle L=\operatorname{dist}(D(t),\operatorname{spt}M(t))

Note that
\gamma(0)\notin\partial D(t)
and
\gamma(L)\notin\Gamma(t)

by (
20.1
) and (
20.2
). (Recall that
\lambda<0
).
By
[
9
]
*Lemma 11 and
[
9
]
*Theorem 28,
at the point
\gamma(0)\in D(t)
, the surface

D(\cdot)
moves in the direction
\gamma^{\prime}(0)
faster than the mean curvature in that
direction, a contradiction.
∎
