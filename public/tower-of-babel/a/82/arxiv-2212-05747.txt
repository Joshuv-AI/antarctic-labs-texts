Optimal periodic L 2 L_{2} -discrepancy and diaphony bounds for higher order digital sequences
Friedrich Pillichshammer

Abstract
We present an explicit construction of infinite sequences of points ( 𝒙 0 , 𝒙 1 , 𝒙 2 , … ) (\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\ldots) in the d d -dimensional unit-cube whose periodic L 2 L_{2} -discrepancy satisfies L 2 , N per ​ ( { 𝒙 0 , 𝒙 1 , … , 𝒙 N − 1 } ) ≤ C d ​ N − 1 ​ ( log ⁡ N ) d / 2 for all ​ N ≥ 2 , L_{2,N}^{{\rm per}}(\{\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}\})\leq C_{d}N^{-1}(\log N)^{d/2}\quad\mbox{for all }N\geq 2, where the factor C d > 0 C_{d}>0 depends only on the dimension d d . The construction is based on higher order digital sequences as introduced by J. Dick in the year 2008. The result is best possible in the order of magnitude in N N according to a Roth-type lower bound shown first by P.D. Proinov. Since the periodic L 2 L_{2} -discrepancy is equivalent to P. Zinterhof’s diaphony the result also applies to this alternative quantitative measure for the irregularity of distribution.

-discrepancy, diaphony, explicit constructions, digital sequence, higher order sequence

1
Introduction and Statement of the Main Result

We study distribution properties of point sequences in the
d
-dimensional unit-cube
[0,1)^{d}
by means of periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy and of diaphony.

Periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy.

The periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy is the
L_{2}
mean of the local discrepancy function with respect to test sets from the class of “periodic intervals”. This is in contrast to the usual
L_{2}
-discrepancy which uses as test sets exclusively subintervals of the unit-cube that are anchored in the origin, i.e., intervals of the form
[\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{t}):=[0,t_{1})\times\ldots\times[0,t_{d})
, where
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{d})
in
[0,1]^{d}
. See
[
1
,
7
,
9
,
22
,
25
]
for general information about (standard)
L_{2}
-discrepancy.

For
x,y\in[0,1]
set

I(x,y)=\begin{cases}[x,y)&\text{if $x\leq y$},\\
[0,y)\cup[x,1)&\text{if $x>y$,}\end{cases}

and for
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in[0,1]^{d}
with
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
and
\boldsymbol{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d})
we set
B(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=I(x_{1},y_{1})\times\dots\times I(x_{d},y_{d})
. We call
B(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
a “periodic interval”.

For a finite set
\mathcal{P}_{N,d}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}\}
of points in the
d
-dimensional unit-cube
[0,1)^{d}
the periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy is defined as

L_{2,N}^{\mathrm{per}}(\mathcal{P}):=\left(\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\left|\frac{A(B(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}),\mathcal{P}_{N,d})}{N}-\lambda_{d}(B(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}))\right|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}\right)^{1/2},

where the counting function
A(B(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}),\mathcal{P}_{N,d})
denotes the number of indices
n
with
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\in B(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
and where
\lambda_{d}(B(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}))
denotes the volume of
B(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
. The term
A(B(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}),\mathcal{P}_{N,d})/N-\lambda_{d}(B(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}))
is sometimes referred to as the “local discrepancy” of
\mathcal{P}_{N,d}
for the test set
B(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
. The local discrepancy measures the difference of the portion of points in a periodic interval and the volume of this periodic interval. Hence it is a measure for the irregularity of distribution of a point set in
[0,1)^{d}
.

For an infinite sequence
\mathcal{S}_{d}=(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\ldots)
in
[0,1)^{d}
the periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy
L_{2,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{S}_{d})
is the periodic
L_{2}
discrepancy of the point set consisting of the first
N
elements of
\mathcal{S}_{d}
, i.e.,

L_{2,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{S}_{d}):=L_{2,N}^{{\rm per}}(\{\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}\})\quad\mbox{for $N\in\mathbb{N}$.}

The periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy has been studied recently in several papers. See, for example,
[
6
,
14
,
16
,
17
,
20
,
21
,
23
]
.

Diaphony.

The diaphony, as introduced by Zinterhof
[
37
]
in the year 1976 (see also
[
9
]
), is just another quantitative measure for the irregularity of distribution of point sets and sequences in the unit-cube
[0,1)^{d}
. For a finite set
\mathcal{P}_{N,d}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}\}
like above the diaphony is defined as

F_{N}(\mathcal{P}_{N,d}):=\left(\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}\frac{1}{\rho(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\exp(2\pi\mathtt{i}\boldsymbol{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where
\mathtt{i}=\sqrt{-1}
, where
\rho(\boldsymbol{h}):=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\max(1,|h_{j}|)
for
\boldsymbol{h}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and where “
\cdot
” denotes the usual Euclidean inner product. For an infinite sequence
\mathcal{S}_{d}
the diaphony
F_{N}(\mathcal{S}_{d})
is the diaphony of the initial
N
elements of
\mathcal{S}_{d}
, i.e.,

F_{N}(\mathcal{S}_{d}):=F_{N}(\{\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}\})\quad\mbox{for $N\in\mathbb{N}$.}

The diaphony is a popular quantitative measure for the irregularity of distribution in the sense that a sequence
\mathcal{S}_{d}
is uniformly distributed modulo one (in the sense of Weyl
[
36
]
), if and only if
\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}F_{N}(\mathcal{S}_{d})=0
; see, for example,
[
9
, Theorem 1.33]
. Diaphony of point sets and sequences is studied in a multitude of papers, see, for example,
[
2
,
10
,
12
,
24
,
29
,
30
,
32
,
33
]
and the references therein. Relations between the number-theoretic concept of diaphony and crystallographic concepts are discussed in
[
18
]
.

Relations and known results.

It is a known fact that the periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy can be expressed in terms of exponential sums. For
\mathcal{P}_{N,d}=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N-1}\}
in
[0,1)^{d}
we have

L_{2,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P}_{N,d})=\left(\frac{1}{3^{d}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}\frac{1}{r(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\exp(2\pi\mathtt{i}\boldsymbol{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{x}_{n})\right|^{2}\right)^{1/2},

(1)

where for
\boldsymbol{h}=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we set

r(\boldsymbol{h})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}r(h_{j})\ \ \ \mbox{ and }\ \ r(h_{j})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{ if $h_{j}=0$},\\
\frac{2\pi|h_{j}|}{\sqrt{6}}&\mbox{ if $h_{j}\not=0$.}\end{array}\right.

For a proof of this relation see
[
23
, Theorem 1]
or
[
16
, p. 390]
. Formula (
1
) shows that the periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy is – up to a multiplicative factor depending on
d
– exactly the diaphony.

We briefly explain some notation: For quantities
A(N,d)
and
B(N,d)
depending on
N
and
d
we write
A(N,d)\lesssim_{d}B(N,d)
if there is a factor
c_{d}>0
which depends only on
d
(and not on
N
) such that
A(N,d)\leq c_{d}B(N,d)
. If we have
A(N,d)\lesssim_{d}B(N,d)
and
B(N,d)\lesssim_{d}A(N,d)
simultaneously, then we write
A(N,d)\asymp_{d}B(N,d)
.

Using this notation we can express the fact that periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy and diaphony are the same up to a factor depending only on
d
in the form

L_{2,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P}_{N,d})\asymp_{d}F_{N}(\mathcal{P}_{N,d}).

So the periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy can be understood as a geometrical interpretation of the diaphony, which is of analytic nature. From this point of view, the results about periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy presented in this paper apply directly also to the diaphony. Both measures can be also interpreted as worst-case errors of quasi-Monte Carlo integration rules in suitable function classes. For discussions in this direction we refer to
[
14
,
16
,
29
]
.

In classical discrepancy theory (see
[
1
,
9
,
22
,
25
]
) one is interested in how small the discrepancy can be in the best case and in constructions of point sets and sequences whose discrepancy is of the optimal order of magnitude. For the periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy and diaphony, respectively, the following is known:

Finite point sets.

For every dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a quantity
c_{d}>0
which only depends on
d
, such that for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
and every
N
-element point set
\mathcal{P}_{N,d}
in
[0,1)^{d}
we have

L_{2,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P}_{N,d})\geq c_{d}\frac{(\log N)^{(d-1)/2}}{N}.

(2)

See, for example,
[
14
, Corollary 2]
. This is the “periodic” counterpart of Roth’s famous lower bound for the usual
L_{2}
-discrepancy from
[
34
]
. The lower bound (
2
) is known to be best possible in dimension
d=2
, see, for example,
[
13
,
14
]
. For arbitrary dimension
d
, it follows from
[
15
, Theorem 5.3]
(see the discussion in
[
21
, Remark 18]
) that the periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy of order 2 digital
(t,m,d)
-nets over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
is of order of magnitude
(\log N)^{(d-1)/2}/N
, where
N=2^{m}
, which matches the lower bound (
2
). Another “semi” construction, based on digitally shifted digital nets in prime base
b
is presented in
[
21
, Section 4]
(but unfortunately here the digital shifts leading to the optimal order of magnitude are unknown, which is indicated by the term “semi construction”).

Remark 1
.

Just to avoid misunderstandings we mention that Lev
[
24
]
studied a slightly more general notion of diaphony which involves certain weights and which he called generalized diaphony. For this kind of generalized diaphony he showed the existence of certain nets (where Lev means point sets together with suitable positive weights for every point) yielding optimal order of magnitude (see
[
24
, Main Theorem]
). However, these upper bounds are only achieved for certain weights which do not comprise the classical setting considered in the present paper.

Infinite sequences.

For every dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a quantity
c^{\prime}_{d}>0
which only depends on
d
, such that for every infinite sequence
\mathcal{S}_{d}
of points in
[0,1)^{d}
we have

L_{2,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{S}_{d})\geq c^{\prime}_{d}\frac{(\log N)^{d/2}}{N}\quad\mbox{for infinitely many $N\in\mathbb{N}$.}

(3)

This has been shown first in the context of diaphony by Proĭnov
[
31
]
(this work is only available in Bulgarian language). A discussion of Proĭnov’s work can be found in
[
19
]
. Later, a direct proof in terms of periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy has been provided in
[
20
]
. The lower bound (
3
) is known to be best possible in dimension
d=1
. One-dimensional infinite sequences whose periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy satisfies a bound of order of magnitude
\sqrt{\log N}/N
for every
N\geq 2
were given in, e.g.,
[
2
,
12
,
29
,
32
,
33
]
. These constructions comprise the fundamental van der Corput sequence as shown by Proĭnov and Grozdanov
[
33
]
(for information about the van der Corput sequence see
[
11
]
). On the other hand, although it suggests itself to believe that the lower bound (
3
) is also best possible for arbitrary dimensions
d\geq 2
, so far there was no proof for this assertion.

The main result.

In this paper we give for the first time an explicit construction of
infinite
sequences in
[0,1)^{d}
for arbitrary dimension
d
, whose periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy is of order of magnitude
(\log N)^{d/2}/N
in
N
which is best possible according to (
3
). In fact, our result even gives a slightly more refined description of the whole situation. Our main result is the following:

Theorem 2
.

For every
d\in\mathbb{N}
one can explicitly construct an infinite sequence
\mathcal{S}_{d}
of points in
[0,1)^{d}
such that for all
N\geq 2
we have

L_{2,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{S}_{d})\lesssim_{d}\frac{(\log N)^{(d-1)/2}}{N}\sqrt{S(N)}\lesssim_{d}\frac{(\log N)^{d/2}}{N},

where
S(N)
is the sum-of-digits function of
N
in base 2 representation, i.e., if
N=2^{n_{1}}+2^{n_{2}}+\cdots+2^{n_{r}}
with
n_{1}>n_{2}>\cdots>n_{r}\geq 0
, then
S(N)=r
.

Remark 3
.

Obviously, we have
S(N)\lesssim\log N
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and
S(N)=(\log(N+1))/\log 2
if
N
is of the form
N=2^{m}-1
with
m\in\mathbb{N}
. However, for certain
N
the sum-of-digits function can be much smaller. For example, if
N
is a power of 2, then
S(N)=1
only. Hence the main theorem describes an infinite class of integers
N\in\mathbb{N}
, for which the periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy of
\mathcal{S}_{d}
is much smaller than the worse lower bound from (
3
) which holds for infinitely many
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

The explicit construction of infinite sequences from Theorem
2
will be presented in Section
2
. Theorem
2
will then be a consequence of Theorem
9
that will be stated at the end of Section
2
.

2
Explicit Constructions of Sequences

We now present explicit constructions of sequences whose periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy satisfies the bound from Theorem
2
. We use the same construction like in
[
8
]
for sequences with optimal order of the standard
L_{2}
-discrepancy. The general construction principle was introduced by Dick in
[
3
,
4
]
and is based on linear algebra over the finite field
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
of order
2
(we identify
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
with the set
\{0,1\}
equipped with the arithmetic operations modulo 2). Dick’s construction in turn is based on digital sequences according to Niederreiter
[
26
,
27
]
.

Digital sequences.

Basic module for the construction of a digital sequence in
[0,1)^{d}
are
d
(one for each coordinate direction)
\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}
matrices
C_{1},\ldots,C_{d}
with entries from
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. In order that the points of a digital sequence belong to
[0,1)^{d}
it is commonly assumed that for
C_{j}=(c_{j,k,l})_{k,l\in\mathbb{N}}
for each
l\in\mathbb{N}
the elements of column
l
become zero eventually, i.e., there exists a number
K(l)\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
c_{j,k,l}=0
for all
k>K(l)
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, where
\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, the
n
-th point of the digital sequence is constructed in the following way. Consider the binary digit expansion of
n
which is of the form
n=n_{0}+n_{1}2+\cdots+n_{m-1}2^{m-1}
with
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
n_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for every
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,m-1\}
, and define the binary digit vector of
n
as
\vec{n}:=(n_{0},n_{1},\ldots,n_{m-1},0,0,\ldots)^{\top}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Then, for
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, define

\vec{x}_{j,n}=(x_{j,n,1},x_{j,n,2},\ldots)^{\top}:=C_{j}\vec{n},

where the matrix vector product is evaluated over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, and

x_{j,n}:=\frac{x_{j,n,1}}{2}+\frac{x_{j,n,2}}{2^{2}}+\frac{x_{j,n,3}}{2^{3}}+\cdots.

Then the
n
-th point
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
of the sequence
\mathcal{S}_{d}
is given by
\boldsymbol{x}_{n}=(x_{1,n},\ldots,x_{d,n})
. A sequence
\mathcal{S}_{d}
constructed this way is called a
digital sequence
(over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
) with
generating matrices

C_{1},\ldots,C_{d}
.

Remark 4
.

A finite version of digital sequences are so-called
digital nets
, which consist of
2^{m}
elements in
[0,1)^{d}
and which are constructed in exactly the same way like digital sequence with the restriction that
C_{1},\ldots,C_{d}
are (finite)
p\times m
matrices over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
with fixed
m,p\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\geq m
, and
n\in\{0,1,\ldots,2^{m}-1\}
. For more information see
[
7
,
26
,
27
]
.

Explicit constructions of suitable generating matrices
C_{1},\ldots,C_{d}
for digital sequences were obtained by Sobol’
[
35
]
, Niederreiter
[
26
,
27
]
, Niederreiter-Xing
[
28
]
and others (see also
[
7
, Chapter 8]
).

A concrete construction.

We briefly describe a special case of Sobol’s and Niederreiter’s construction of the generating matrices. Let
C_{j}=(c_{j,k,l})_{k,l\geq 1}
with
c_{j,k,l}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. Let
p_{1}=x
and
p_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}[x]
for
j\in\{2,\ldots,d-1\}
be the
(j-1)
-st primitive polynomial in a list of primitive polynomials over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
that is sorted in increasing order according to their degree
e_{j}=\deg(p_{j})
, that is,
e_{2}\leq e_{3}\leq\cdots\leq e_{d-1}
(the ordering of polynomials with the same degree is irrelevant). We also put
e_{1}=\deg(x)=1
.

Let
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Take
i-1
and
z
to be respectively the main term and remainder when we divide
k-1
by
e_{j}
, so that
k-1=(i-1)e_{j}+z
, with
z\in\{0,1,\ldots,e_{j}-1\}
. Now consider the Laurent series expansion

\frac{x^{e_{j}-z-1}}{p_{j}(x)^{i}}=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{l}(i,j,z)}{x^{l}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}((x^{-1}))

and write, for
l\in\mathbb{N}
,

c_{j,k,l}=a_{l}(i,j,z).

(4)

Now a digital sequence with generating matrices
C_{j}=(c_{j,k,l})_{k,l\geq 1}
constructed this way is a special instance of a Sobol’ sequence (which itself is a special instance of a generalized Niederreiter sequence). For more information on these constructions we refer to
[
7
, Chapter 8]
. Observe that for the presented construction of generating matrices we have
c_{j,k,l}=0
for all
k>l
.

The general construction principle.

The present construction of sequences with the optimal order of periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy is based on higher order digital sequences as introduced by Dick in
[
3
,
4
]
. We state here simplified versions of their definitions that are sufficient for the present purpose.

The distribution quality of digital sequences depends on the choice of the respective generating matrices. In the following definitions we put some restrictions on
C_{1},\ldots,C_{d}
with the aim to quantify the quality of equidistribution of the digital sequence.

First we give the definition for the finite case as introduced in Remark
4
.

Definition 5
.

Let
m,p,\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
with
p\geq\alpha m
and let
t
be an integer such that
0\leq t\leq\alpha m
. Let
C_{1},\ldots,C_{d}
be
p\times m
matrices with entries from
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. For
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,p\}
let
\vec{c}_{j,i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m}
be the
i
-th row vector of the matrix
C_{j}
. If for all
1\leq i_{j,\nu_{j}}<\cdots<i_{j,1}\leq p
with

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{l=1}^{\min(\nu_{j},\alpha)}i_{j,l}\leq\alpha m-t

the vectors

\vec{c}_{1,i_{1,\nu_{1}}},\ldots,\vec{c}_{1,i_{1,1}},\ldots,\vec{c}_{d,i_{d,\nu_{d}}},\ldots,\vec{c}_{d,i_{d,1}}

are linearly independent
over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, then the digital net constructed from the generating matrices
C_{1},\ldots,C_{d}
is called an
order
\alpha
digital
(t,m,d)
-net over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. We then say that the matrices
C_{1},\ldots,C_{d}
satisfy the
order
\alpha
digital
(t,m,d)
-net property
.

Definition 6
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
and let
t\geq 0
be an integer. Let
C_{1},\ldots,C_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}
and let
C_{j,\alpha m\times m}
denote the left upper
\alpha m\times m
submatrix of
C_{j}
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. If for all
m>t/\alpha
the matrices
C_{1,\alpha m\times m},\ldots,C_{d,\alpha m\times m}
satisfy the order
\alpha
digital
(t,m,d)
-net property, then the digital sequence with generating matrices
C_{1},\ldots,C_{d}
is called an
order
\alpha
digital
(t,d)
-sequence over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

From Definition
6
it is clear that if
\mathcal{S}_{d}
is an order
\alpha
digital
(t,d)
-sequence, then for any
t\leq t^{\prime}
, it is also an order
\alpha
digital
(t^{\prime},d)
-sequence. If
\alpha=1
, then the concept of order 1 digital
(t,d)
-sequences is exactly the same like digital
(t,d)
-sequences as introduced by Niederreiter in
[
26
,
27
]
. For a geometrical interpretation of the order
\alpha
digital net property we refer to
[
7
, Chapter 15]
or to
[
8
, Section 1.4]
.

Note that a digital sequence can be an order
\alpha
digital
(t,d)
-sequence over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
and at the same time an order
\alpha^{\prime}
digital
(t^{\prime},d)
-net over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
for
\alpha^{\prime}\not=\alpha
. This means that the quality parameter
t
may depend on
\alpha
. If necessary we write
t(\alpha)
instead of
t
for the quality parameter of an order
\alpha
digital
(t(\alpha),d)
-sequence. In particular
[
4
, Theorem 4.10]
implies that an order
\alpha
digital
(t,d)
-sequence is an order
\alpha^{\prime}
digital
(t^{\prime},d)
-sequence for all
1\leq\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha
with

t^{\prime}=\lceil t\alpha^{\prime}/\alpha\rceil\leq t.

(5)

In other words,
t(\alpha^{\prime})=\lceil t(\alpha)\alpha^{\prime}/\alpha\rceil
for all
1\leq\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha
. More information can be found in
[
7
, Chapter 15]
.

Next we present a concrete construction of an order
\alpha
digital
(t,d)
-sequence.

A concrete construction of order
\alpha
digital sequences.

In order to construct order
\alpha
digital sequences we need the following composition principle.

Definition 7
.

For
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
the
digit interlacing composition
(with interlacing factor
\alpha

Obviously, Theorem

Walsh functions.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
the
k
-th Walsh function (in base 2)
{\rm wal}_{k}:[0,1)\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
is defined in the following way: let
k
have binary representation

k=\kappa_{a-1}2^{a-1}+\cdots+\kappa_{1}2+\kappa_{0},

with
\kappa_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, and let
x\in[0,1)
have binary expansion

x=\frac{\xi_{1}}{2}+\frac{\xi_{2}}{2^{2}}+\cdots

with
\xi_{i}\in\{0,1\}
(unique in the sense that
infinitely many of the
\xi_{i}
must be zero), then

{\rm wal}_{k}(x):=(-1)^{\xi_{1}\kappa_{0}+\cdots+\xi_{a}\kappa_{a-1}}.

For dimension
d\geq 2
, vectors
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in[0,1)^{d}
we write

{\rm wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\prod_{j=1}^{d}{\rm wal}_{k_{j}}(x_{j}).

A collection of useful properties of Walsh functions can be found in
[
7
, Appendix A]
.

We call
x\in[0,1)
a dyadic rational if it can be written in a finite binary expansion. By
\oplus
we denote the digit-wise addition modulo
2
. In order to avoid ambiguities and since it suffices for our purpose we define
\oplus
just for dyadic rationals. For dyadic rationals
x=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\xi_{i}}{2^{i}}
and
y=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{\eta_{i}}{2^{i}}
with
\xi_{i},\eta_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, where both digit sequences
(\xi_{i})_{i\geq 1}
and
(\eta_{i})_{i\geq 1}
become eventually zero, we put

x\oplus y:=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\frac{\zeta_{i}}{2^{i}},\;\;\;{\rm where}\;\;\;\zeta_{i}:=\xi_{i}+\eta_{i}\,(\operatorname{mod}\,2).

Obviously
x\oplus y
is then also a dyadic rational. For vectors
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in[0,1)^{d}
whose components are dyadic rationals we set
\boldsymbol{x}\oplus\boldsymbol{y}:=(x_{1}\oplus y_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\oplus y_{d})
.

For any
\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
and
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in[0,1)^{d}
whose components are dyadic rationals we have

{\rm wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x}\oplus\boldsymbol{y})={\rm wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x}){\rm wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{y})

(7)

It can be shown (see
[
7
, Lemma 4.72]
) that any digital net
\mathcal{P}_{2^{m},d}
(see Remark
4
) is a subgroup of
([0,1)^{d},\oplus)
and furthermore, all components of every point of
\mathcal{P}_{2^{m},d}
are dyadic rationals. From (
7
) it follows that
{\rm wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}
is a character of the group
(\mathcal{P}_{2^{m},d},\oplus)
. Hence, for any digital net
\mathcal{P}_{2^{m},d}
with generating matrices
C_{1},\ldots,C_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{m\times m}
and any
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
it follows that

\frac{1}{2^{m}}\sum_{h=0}^{2^{m}-1}{\rm wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{h})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{ if }C_{1}^{\top}\vec{k_{1}}+\cdots+C_{d}^{\top}\vec{k_{d}}=\vec{0},\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

(8)

For a proof of this fact we refer to
[
7
, Lemma 4.75]
. This property is called the
character property of digital nets
.

Walsh series expansion of the periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy.

The starting point is the representation of the periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy in terms of exponential sums. Re-writing (
1
) we obtain

(L_{2,N}^{{\rm per}}(\mathcal{P}))^{2}=-\frac{1}{3^{d}}+\frac{1}{3^{d}N^{2}}\sum_{n,p=0}^{N-1}K_{d}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n},\boldsymbol{x}_{p}),

where

K_{d}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):=\sum_{\boldsymbol{h}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\frac{1}{r(\boldsymbol{h})^{2}}\exp(2\pi\mathtt{i}\boldsymbol{h}\cdot(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}))=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{h=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r(h)^{2}}\exp(2\pi\mathtt{i}h(x_{j}-y_{j})\right).

Now we expand
K_{d}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
into a Walsh series. We have

K_{d}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}}\rho(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l}){\rm wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x}){\rm wal}_{\boldsymbol{l}}(\boldsymbol{y}),

where

\rho(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l})=\int_{[0,1]^{2d}}K_{d}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}){\rm wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x}){\rm wal}_{\boldsymbol{l}}(\boldsymbol{y})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{y}.

Due to the multiplicative structure of
K_{d}
and of the multi-dimensional Walsh functions, for
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})
and
\boldsymbol{l}=(l_{1},\ldots,l_{d})
in
\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
we have

\rho(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l})=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\rho(k_{j},l_{j}),

where, for
d=1
and
k,l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\displaystyle\rho(k,l)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}K_{1}(x,y){\rm wal}_{k_{j}}(x){\rm wal}_{l_{j}}(y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{h=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r(h)^{2}}\exp(2\pi\mathtt{i}h(x-y)){\rm wal}_{k}(x){\rm wal}_{l}(y)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{h=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r(h)^{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\exp(2\pi\mathtt{i}hx){\rm wal}_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\left(\int_{0}^{1}\exp(-2\pi\mathtt{i}hy){\rm wal}_{l}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{h=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r(h)^{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\exp(2\pi\mathtt{i}hx){\rm wal}_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\right)\overline{\left(\int_{0}^{1}\exp(2\pi\mathtt{i}hy){\rm wal}_{l}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y\right)}.

Put

\beta_{h,k}:=\int_{0}^{1}\exp(2\pi\mathtt{i}hx){\rm wal}_{k}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Note that
\beta_{0,0}=1
,
\beta_{0,k}=0
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\beta_{h,0}=0
for
h\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
. Then we can write

\rho(k,l)=\sum_{h=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\beta_{h,k}\overline{\beta}_{h,l}}{r(h)^{2}}

and, in particular,
\rho(0,0)=1
and
\rho(0,l)=\rho(k,0)=0
for
k,l\in\mathbb{N}
. For
k,l\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\rho(k,l)=\sum_{h=-\infty\atop h\not=0}^{\infty}\frac{\beta_{h,k}\overline{\beta}_{h,l}}{r(h)^{2}}=\frac{6}{4\pi^{2}}\sum_{h=-\infty\atop h\not=0}^{\infty}\frac{\beta_{h,k}\overline{\beta}_{h,l}}{h^{2}}=6\gamma_{2}(k,l),

where
\gamma_{2}(k,l)
are defined in
[
7
, Eq. (14.17) on p. 453]
as the Walsh coefficients of the normalized and periodically extended second Bernoulli polynomial. These coefficients are computed in
[
7
, Lemma 14.17]
and from there we obtain:

Lemma 10
.

We have
\rho(0,0)=1
and
\rho(k,0)=\rho(0,l)=0
for all
k,l\in\mathbb{N}
. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
write

k=2^{a_{1}-1}+2^{a_{2}-1}+\cdots+2^{a_{v}-1}
with

and
a_{1}>a_{2}>\dots>a_{v}\geq 1
, and

and
k^{\prime\prime}=k-2^{a_{1}-1}-2^{a_{2}-1}=k^{\prime}-2^{a_{2}-1}
. Likewise, for

write
l=2^{b_{1}-1}+2^{b_{2}-1}+\cdots+2^{b_{w}-1}
with
w\in\mathbb{N}
and
b_{1}>b_{2}>\dots>b_{w}\geq 1
, and
l^{\prime}=l-2^{b_{1}-1}
and
l^{\prime\prime}=l-2^{b_{1}-1}-2^{b_{2}-1}=l^{\prime}-2^{b_{2}-1}
. Then for all

we have

\rho(k,l)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2^{-2a_{1}}&\mbox{if }k=l,\\
2^{-a_{1}-b_{1}-2}&\mbox{if }k^{\prime}=l^{\prime}>0\mbox{ and }k\neq l,\\
-2^{-a_{1}-a_{2}-2}&\mbox{if }k^{\prime\prime}=l,\\
-2^{-b_{1}-b_{2}-2}&\mbox{if }k=l^{\prime\prime},\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

\alpha m_{i}\times m_{i}
submatrices of the generating matrices
C_{1},\ldots,C_{d}
of the digital sequence. We denote the digital shift, which is given by (
10
), by
\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}
. Note that all the coordinates of the digital shift are dyadic rationals since the components of
\vec{\sigma}_{i,j}
become eventually zero.

Let
\mathcal{D}_{i}
denote the dual net corresponding to the digital net with generating matrices (
11
), i.e.,

\mathcal{D}_{i}=\{\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}\ :\ C_{1,\alpha m_{i}\times m_{i}}^{\top}\vec{k}_{1}+\cdots+C_{d,\alpha m_{i}\times m_{i}}^{\top}\vec{k}_{d}=\vec{0}\},

where for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with binary expansion
k=\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{1}2+\kappa_{2}2^{2}+\cdots
we set
\vec{k}=(\kappa_{0},\kappa_{1},\ldots,\kappa_{\alpha m_{i}-1})^{\top}
. Set
\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\ast}=\mathcal{D}_{i}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}{\rm wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sum_{n=2^{m_{1}}+\cdots+2^{m_{i-1}}}^{-1+2^{m_{1}}+\cdots+2^{m_{i}}}{\rm wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n}),

where again for
i=1
we set
2^{m_{1}}+\cdots+2^{m_{i-1}}=0
, and by the character property (
8
)

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2^{m_{1}}+\cdots+2^{m_{i-1}}}^{-1+2^{m_{1}}+\cdots+2^{m_{i}}}{\rm wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{n})=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\rm wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i})2^{m_{i}}&\mbox{if }\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{D}_{i},\\
0&\mbox{if }\boldsymbol{k}\notin\mathcal{D}_{i}.\end{array}\right.

Therefore

\displaystyle(L^{{\rm per}}_{2,N}(\mathcal{S}_{d}))^{2}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3^{d}N^{2}}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}=1}^{r}2^{m_{i}+m_{i^{\prime}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{D}^{\ast}_{i},\boldsymbol{l}\in\mathcal{D}^{\ast}_{i^{\prime}}}\rho(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l}){\rm wal}_{\boldsymbol{k}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i}){\rm wal}_{\boldsymbol{l}}(\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{i^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3^{d}N^{2}}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}=1}^{r}2^{m_{i}+m_{i^{\prime}}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}\in\mathcal{D}^{\ast}_{i},\boldsymbol{l}\in\mathcal{D}^{\ast}_{i^{\prime}}}|\rho(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l})|.

Now define

\mathcal{E}_{i,i^{\prime}}=\{(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l})\in\mathcal{D}^{\ast}_{i}\times\mathcal{D}^{\ast}_{i^{\prime}}\ :\ \rho(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l})\neq 0\}

and

\mathcal{E}_{i,i^{\prime}}(z)=\{(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l})\in\mathcal{E}_{i,i^{\prime}}\ :\ \mu(\boldsymbol{k})+\mu(\boldsymbol{l})=z\},

where the function
\mu:\mathbb{N}_{0}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
is defined by
\mu(0):=0
and for
k=\kappa_{0}+\kappa_{1}2+\cdots+\kappa_{a-2}2^{a-2}+2^{a-1}
with
\kappa_{j}\in\{0,1\}
by
\mu(k):=a
and, for
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
, we put
\mu(\boldsymbol{k}):=\mu(k_{1})+\cdots+\mu(k_{d})
.

From Lemma
10
it follows that for
(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l})\in\mathcal{E}_{i,i^{\prime}}
we have

|\rho(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l})|\leq\frac{1}{2^{\mu(\boldsymbol{k})+\mu(\boldsymbol{l})}}.

Thus we have

(L^{{\rm per}}_{2,N}(\mathcal{S}_{d}))^{2}\leq\frac{1}{3^{d}N^{2}}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}=1}^{r}2^{m_{i}+m_{i^{\prime}}}\sum_{(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l})\in\mathcal{E}_{i,i^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{2^{\mu(\boldsymbol{k})+\mu(\boldsymbol{l})}}.

(12)

Now we re-order the sum over all
(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l})\in\mathcal{E}_{i,i^{\prime}}
according to the value of
\mu(\boldsymbol{k})+\mu(\boldsymbol{l})
. Assume that
\boldsymbol{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{d})\in\mathcal{D}_{i}^{\ast}
. Let
k_{j}=\kappa_{j,0}+\kappa_{j,1}2+\cdots+\kappa_{j,a_{j}-2}2^{a_{j}-2}+2^{a_{j}-1}
with
a_{j}=\mu(k_{j})
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. Let further
\vec{c}_{j,u}
denote the
u
-th row vector of the matrix
C_{j,\alpha m_{i}\times m_{i}}
. Then

C_{1,\alpha m_{i}\times m_{i}}^{\top}\vec{k}_{1}+\cdots+C_{d,\alpha m_{i}\times m_{i}}^{\top}\vec{k}_{d}=\vec{0}

is equivalent to

\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\sum_{u=0}^{a_{j}-2}\vec{c}_{j,u+1}^{\ \top}\kappa_{j,u}+\vec{c}_{j,a_{j}-1}^{\ \top}\right)=\vec{0}.

Hence it follows from the linear independence property for the row vectors of generating matrices of digital nets in Definition
5
that

\mu(\boldsymbol{k})=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{d}>m_{i}-t.

In the same way
\boldsymbol{l}\in\mathcal{D}_{i^{\prime}}^{\ast}
implies that
\mu(\boldsymbol{l})>m_{i^{\prime}}-t
. Hence
(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l})\in\mathcal{D}^{\ast}_{i}\times\mathcal{D}^{\ast}_{i^{\prime}}
implies
\mu(\boldsymbol{k})+\mu(\boldsymbol{l})\geq m_{i}+m_{i^{\prime}}-2t+2
.

Thus for the innermost sum in (
12
) we have

\sum_{(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{l})\in\mathcal{E}_{i,i^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{2^{\mu(\boldsymbol{k})+\mu(\boldsymbol{l})}}=\sum_{z=m_{i}+m_{i^{\prime}}-2t+2}^{\infty}\frac{|\mathcal{E}_{i,i^{\prime}}(z)|}{2^{z}}

and this implies

(L^{{\rm per}}_{2,N}(\mathcal{S}_{d}))^{2}\leq\frac{1}{3^{d}}\sum_{i,i^{\prime}=1}^{r}\frac{2^{m_{i}}}{N}\frac{2^{m_{i^{\prime}}}}{N}\sum_{z=m_{i}+m_{i^{\prime}}-2t+2}^{\infty}\frac{|\mathcal{E}_{i,i^{\prime}}(z)|}{2^{z}}.

(13)

Now the right hand side of the estimate (
13
) for the squared periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy is – up to the factor
1/3^{d}
– the same as the upper bound on the standard
L_{2}
-discrepancy in
[
8
, Lemma 3.1, Eq. (16)]
. Note that the sets
\mathcal{E}_{i,i^{\prime}}(z)
are subsets of the corresponding sets
\mathcal{J}_{i,i^{\prime}}(z)
used in
[
8
, Lemma 3.1, Eq. (15)]
. This means that from this stage on we can proceed in exactly the same way as in
[
8
]
in order to obtain the proposed upper bound in Theorem
9
.
∎

Remark 13
.

In
[
5
]
it is shown by a different proof method that even order
\alpha=2
digital sequences suffice in order to obtain the optimal order of magnitude for the standard
L_{2}
-discrepancy. We conjecture that the same holds true for the periodic
L_{2}
-discrepancy.
