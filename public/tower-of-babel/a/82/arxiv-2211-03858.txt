Disordered arcs and Harer stability
Oscar Harr, Max Vistrup, and Nathalie Wahl

Abstract
. We give a new proof of homological stability with the best known isomorphism range for mapping class groups of surfaces with respect to genus. The proof uses the framework of Randal-Williams–Wahl and Krannich applied to disk stabilization in the category of bidecorated surfaces, using the Euler characteristic instead of the genus as a grading. The monoidal category of bidecorated surfaces does not admit a braiding, distinguishing it from previously known settings for homological stability. Nevertheless, we find that it admits a suitable Yang–Baxter element, which we show is sufficient structure for homological stability arguments.

Braided action and Yang–Baxter operators

We want to obtain Theorems

[

]

Let

The sum of bidecorated disks

As explained in Section

,
we get an associated strong monoidal functor

\mathbf{B}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow{{\mathbf{M}}_{2}}

]

associated to the chain of curves

where the

th cylinder

The strong monoidal functor
\mathbf{B}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow{{\mathbf{M}}_{2}}

with the structure of an

Remark 1.1
.

Homological stability frameworks such as
[

Remark 2.5
.

The connectivity estimate above can be shown to be optimal
in certain low-genus examples, corresponding to known computations
of the unstable homology of mapping class groups.
Indeed,
\mathscr{D}^{2}(S_{1,r})
is disconnected.
To see this, consider the spectral sequence associated
to the action of the mapping class group
\Gamma(S_{1,r})
on
the simplicial complex
\mathscr{D}^{2}(S_{1,r})
.
This is the spectral sequence arising from the vertical filtration
of the double complex
\mathbb{Z}\mathscr{D}^{2}(S_{1,r})_{\bullet}\otimes_{\Gamma(S_{1,r})}F_{\bullet}

By a standard argument using Shapiro’s lemma (see e.g.
[

\widetilde{H}_{q}(\Gamma(S_{1,r-1}))&\text{if }p=0,\\
\widetilde{H}_{q}(\Gamma(S_{0,r}))&\text{if }p=1,\\
\widetilde{H}_{q}(\Gamma(S_{0,r-1}))&\text{if }p=2,\\

3.
The monoidal category of bidecorated surfaces

In this section, we describe a monoidal groupoid
({{\mathbf{M}}_{2}},\mathbin{\text{{\#}}})
of surfaces
decorated by two intervals in their boundary, where the monoidal
structure glues the intervals in pairs.
We show that this groupoid is a module over the braided monoidal
groupoid
\mathbf{B}
of braid groups, giving, on classifying spaces,
the structure of an
E_{1}
-module over an
E_{2}
-algebra in the sense of
[
Kra19
]
.

3.1.
Bidecorated surfaces and the monoidal structure

The groupoid
{{\mathbf{M}}_{2}}
has objects
bidecorated surfaces
, that are, informally, surfaces with two intervals marked in their boundary. To give a precise definition of the objects that is convenient for the
monoidal structure, we start by
constructing a special sequence of bidecorated
surfaces
X_{n}
, built out of disks, and defined inductively.

Let
X_{1}=D^{2}\subset\mathbb{C}
denote the unit disk in the complex plane,
and define the embeddings
\iota_{1}^{0},\iota_{1}^{1}\colon I\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow X_{1}
by

\iota_{1}^{0}(t)=e^{i(\pi/4+t\pi/2)}\quad\text{and}\quad\iota_{1}^{1}(t)=e^{i(5\pi/4+t\pi/2)}.

We denote by
\overline{\iota_{1}^{i}}\colon I\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow X_{1}
the reversed map

t\longmapsto\iota_{1}^{i}(1-t)
for
i=0,1
.

Recursively, suppose we have defined
(X_{m},\iota^{0}_{m},\iota_{m}^{1})
for some
m\geq 1
.
We construct
X_{m+1}
from
X_{m}
by gluing an additional disk along
two half intervals, with new markings
\iota_{m+1}^{0},\iota_{m+1}^{1}

coming from the first half of the markings of
X_{m}
and
the second half of the markings of the attached disk:

X_{m+1}\vcentcolon=\frac{X_{m}\sqcup X_{1}}{\begin{subarray}{c}\iota^{i}_{m}(t)\sim\overline{\iota^{i}_{1}}(t),\ t\in{[{}^{1}{\mskip-5.0mu/\mskip-3.0mu}_{2},1]},\end{subarray}}\ \ \ \textrm{with}\ \ \ \iota_{m+1}^{i}(t)=\begin{cases}\iota_{m}^{i}(t),&\text{if }t\leq 1/2,\\
\iota_{1}^{i}(t),&\text{else.}\end{cases}

for
i=0,1
.
Note that the marked intervals in the boundary of
X_{m}
might live in different boundary components (in fact this will happen every other time). Figure
3
shows what happens when a disk is glued to a surface in the above described manner, in each of these two possible cases.

X_{1}=D^{2}

to a bidecorated surface

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
m\geq 1
.
Then
X_{m}\cong S_{g,r}
is a surface of genus
g
with
r
boundary components, where

(g,r)=\begin{cases}(\frac{m}{2}-1,2),&\text{if $m$ is even},\\
(\frac{m-1}{2},1),&\text{if $m$ is odd}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Note first that
X_{m}
is a connected surface for each
m
, since
X_{1}
is a disk and
X_{m}
is obtained from
X_{1}
by successively adding disks (or strips), attached along two disjoint intervals in the boundary.
For the same reason, we get that the Euler characteristic of
X_{m}
is

\chi(X_{m+1})=\chi(X_{m})-1=\dots=1-m.

By the classification of surfaces, we are left to compute the number of boundary components of
X_{m}
.
For this, observing Figure
3
, we notice that if we glue a disk along two intervals of
S
that lie in the same boundary component, the new marked intervals given by the above procedure will give new intervals in different boundary components and vice versa, and no boundary component without marked intervals are ever created. It follows that the number of boundary components of
X_{m}
alternates between
1
and
2
. The result follows.

\square

We are now ready to define the objects of the groupoid
{{\mathbf{M}}_{2}}
. We will use the boundary of the above defined surfaces
X_{m}
to parametrize the boundary components of the surfaces that contain the marked intervals, to allow us to work with parametrized boundary components instead of parametrized arcs, in order to simplify some definitions.

Definition 3.2
.

A
bidecorated surface
is a tuple
(S,m,\varphi)

where
S
is a surface,

m\geq 1
is an integer, and

\varphi\colon\partial X_{m}\sqcup(\sqcup_{k}S^{1})\xrightarrow{\sim}\partial S

is a homeomorphism, giving a parametrization of the boundary of
S
.
We think of
(S,m,\varphi)
as a surface with two parametrized arcs

I_{0}\vcentcolon=\varphi\circ\iota_{m}^{0}\quad\text{and}\quad I_{1}\vcentcolon=\varphi\circ\iota_{m}^{1}

in its boundary, and
k
additional parametrized boundaries. The
surface
S
may also have punctures.

The monoidal groupoid

({{\mathbf{M}}_{2}},\mathbin{\text{{\#}}},U)
has objects the bidecorated surfaces together with
a formal unit
U
.
There are no morphisms between two bidecorated surfaces

(S,m,\varphi)
and
(S^{\prime},m^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})
unless
S
and
S^{\prime}

are homeomorphic and
m=m^{\prime}
,
in which case we define the set of morphisms to be all the mapping
classes of homeomorphisms
that preserve the boundary parametrizations

{\operatorname{Hom}}_{{\mathbf{M}}_{2}}((S,m,\varphi),(S^{\prime},m,\varphi^{\prime}))\vcentcolon=\pi_{0}{\operatorname{Homeo}}_{\partial}(S,S^{\prime})=\pi_{0}\{f\in{\operatorname{Homeo}}(S,S^{\prime})\mid f\circ\varphi=\varphi^{\prime}\},

where
{\operatorname{Homeo}}(S,S^{\prime})
is endowed with the compact-open topology,
and
{\operatorname{Homeo}}_{\partial}(S,S^{\prime})
with the subspace topology.
The only morphism involving the unit
U

is the identity
\text{id}_{U}
.

Remark 3.3
.

Our definition of the morphisms in the category
{{\mathbf{M}}_{2}}
is such that
punctures in a surfaces
S
can be permuted by automorphisms of
S
in

{{\mathbf{M}}_{2}}
. Our argument works just as well with labeled punctures, that are
not permutable by homeomorphims, or both labeled and unlabeled
punctures, just like we could also have additional boundary components
that are only marked up to a permutation. The only changes this would
cause to the
argument would be that it would make the notations and conventions more
cumbersome.

The monoidal structure
\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}
is defined as follows.
The object
U
is by definition a unit for
\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}
.
For the remaining objects, the monoidal product
\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}

is defined by

\displaystyle(S,m,\varphi)\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}(S^{\prime},m^{\prime},\varphi^{\prime})\vcentcolon=\left(\frac{S\sqcup S^{\prime}}{\begin{subarray}{c}I_{i}(t)\sim\overline{I_{i}^{\prime}}(t),t\in{[{}^{1}{\mskip-5.0mu/\mskip-3.0mu}_{2},1]},\end{subarray}},m+m^{\prime},\varphi\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}\varphi^{\prime}\right),

where
i=0,1
, and where

\varphi\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}\varphi^{\prime}\colon\partial X_{m+m^{\prime}}\sqcup(\sqcup_{k+k^{\prime}}S^{1})\ \hookrightarrow\ \partial(S\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}S^{\prime}),

is obtained using the canonical identification
\partial X_{m+m^{\prime}}\cong(\partial X_{n}\backslash\iota_{m}(\frac{1}{2},1))\cup(\partial X_{m^{\prime}}\backslash\iota_{m^{\prime}}(0,\frac{1}{2}))
.
On morphisms, the monoidal product is given by juxtaposition.

The monoidal category
{{\mathbf{M}}_{2}}
has the following
injectivity
property
with respect to gluing a disk, that will be useful in the
proof of our stability result.

Proposition 3.4
.

For any object
S=(S,m,\varphi)
of
{{\mathbf{M}}_{2}}
, and any
p\geq 0
,
the map

{\operatorname{Aut}}_{{\mathbf{M}}_{2}}(S)\xrightarrow{\ {\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}D^{\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}{p+1}}}\ }{\operatorname{Aut}}_{{\mathbf{M}}_{2}}(S\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}D^{\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}{p+1}})

D=(X_{1},1,\operatorname{id})
is our chosen disk.

Proof.

Recall that the underlying surface of
D^{\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}p+1}
is the surface
X_{p+1}
defined above.
Picking a smooth representative of the underlying surface of
S\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}X_{p+1}
, with
S
a smooth subsurface in its interior, we can model
the map in the statement using the description of the mapping class
group of surfaces in terms of isotopy classes of diffeomorphisms
rather than homeomorphisms. (See e.g.,
[
Bol09
, Thm 1.2]

for a detailed account of the classical isomorphism

\pi_{0}{\operatorname{Homeo}}_{\partial}(S)\cong\pi_{0}{\operatorname{Diff}}_{\partial}(S)

Now the result follows by essentially the same argument as
the case of attaching of surface along a single arc instead of two, as treated in
[

, using the fibration

\displaystyle{\operatorname{Diff}}(S\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}X_{p+1}\ \textrm{rel}\ \partial S\cup X_{p+1})

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow{\operatorname{Diff}}(S\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}X_{p+1}\ \textrm{rel}\ \partial(S\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}X_{p+1}))

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\text{Emb}((X_{p+1},I_{0}|_{[\frac{1}{2},1]}\cup I_{1}|_{[\frac{1}{2},1]}),(S\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}X_{p+1},I_{0}|_{[\frac{1}{2},1]}\cup I_{1}|_{[\frac{1}{2},1]}))

where the fiber identifies with

and where we note that

is built inductively by attaching disks along two intervals, or homotopically attaching arcs, and using the contractibility of the components of embeddings of arcs in a surface, as proved in
[

, Thm 5]
.

\square

5.
Braiding and homological stability for groups

In order to use the framework of Krannich
[
Kra19
]

to prove homological stability for a sequence of groups,
one needs the structure of an “
E_{1}
-module over an
E_{2}
-algebra”.
We give in Proposition
5.1
below a simple way to construct such a module structure,
in terms of Yang–Baxter operators.
Compared to earlier approaches to homological stability such as
[
RWW17
]
,
which Krannich’s work generalizes,
this has the advantage of being very lightweight.
Instead of having to provide the structure of a braiding on the monoidal category whose automorphism
groups one is interested in,
it suffices to provide a single morphism satisfying a simple
equation.

Our main example of a Yang–Baxter operator is the
inverse Dehn twist
T_{1}^{-1}\in{\operatorname{Aut}}_{{{\mathbf{M}}_{2}}}(D\mathbin{\text{{\#}}}D)
, defined in
Section
3.2
and used to prove our main result.
In Section
5.3
, we show that this Yang–Baxter operator is not part
of a braided monoidal structure on the category
{{\mathbf{M}}_{2}}
, but gives
instead a twisted version of such a structure.

5.1.
Yang–Baxter operators and braid groupoid actions

Let
\mathscr{X}=(\mathscr{X},\oplus,\mathbbm{1})
be a monoidal category.
A
Yang–Baxter operator
in
\mathscr{X}

is a pair
(X,\tau)
consisting of an object
X\in\mathscr{X}

and a morphism
\tau\in{\operatorname{Aut}}_{\mathscr{C}}(X\oplus X)
,
satisfying the Yang–Baxter equation

(\tau\oplus 1)(1\oplus\tau)(\tau\oplus 1)=(1\oplus\tau)(\tau\oplus 1)(1\oplus\tau)\in{\operatorname{Aut}}_{\mathscr{C}}(X\oplus X\oplus X),

where we suppress associators from the notation.

Yang–Baxter operators are closely related to the braid groupoid:
Recall from Section
3.2
the braid groupoid
\mathbf{B}
, with objects
the natural numbers and only non-trivial morphisms

{\operatorname{Aut}}_{\mathbf{B}}(n)=B_{n}
.
A variant of the coherence theorem for braided
monoidal categories says that the category of strong monoidal functors
from the braid groupoid into
\mathscr{X}
is equivalent to a naturally
defined category of Yang–Baxter operators in
\mathscr{X}

[
JS93
, Prop 2.2]
.
4
4

4

In other words, the pair consisting of the braid groupoid
\mathbf{B}

and the Yang–Baxter operator
\sigma_{1}\in\text{Aut}_{\mathbf{B}}(2)
,
is the initial monoidal category
with a distinguished Yang–Baxter element.

To a Yang–Baxter operator
(X,\tau)
in
\mathscr{X}
,
this equivalence associates
the strong monoidal functor
\Phi_{X,\tau}\colon\mathbf{B}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathscr{X}
given by

\Phi_{X,\tau}(n)=X^{\oplus n}
on objects,
and on morphisms by letting

\Phi_{X,\tau}\colon B_{n}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow{\operatorname{Aut}}_{\mathscr{X}}(X^{\oplus n})

send the
i
th standard generator
\sigma_{i}
to

\text{id}_{X^{\oplus i-1}}\oplus\tau\oplus\text{id}_{X^{\oplus n-i-1}}
,
where the required maps

\Phi_{X,\tau}(m)\oplus\Phi_{X,\tau}(n)\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\Phi_{X,\tau}(m+n)

are given by the monoidal structure of
\mathscr{X}
.

Suppose now that the monoidal category

\mathscr{X}
acts on a category
\mathscr{M}

via a functor
\mathscr{M}\times\mathscr{X}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathscr{M}
,
which we also denote by
\oplus
, compatible with the
monoidal sum in
\mathscr{X}
.
The following result shows that the choice of a Yang–Baxter operator
defines an action of the braid groupoid
\mathbf{B}
on
\mathscr{M}
, and hence is
appropriate data to apply the stability framework of
[
Kra19
]
:

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
(\mathscr{X},\oplus,\mathbbm{1})
be a monoidal category
with
\tau\in{\operatorname{Aut}}_{\mathscr{X}}(X\oplus X)

a Yang–Baxter operator in
\mathscr{X}
.
Suppose
\mathscr{X}
acts on a category
\mathscr{M}
.
Then there is an action of the braid groupoid

\alpha_{\tau}\colon\mathscr{M}\times\mathbf{B}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathscr{M}

given on objects by
\alpha_{\tau}(A,n)=A\oplus X^{\oplus n}

and determined on morphisms by

\alpha_{\tau}(f,\sigma_{i})=f\oplus\text{id}_{X^{\oplus i-1}}\oplus\tau\oplus\text{id}_{X^{\oplus n-i-1}},

for
\sigma_{i}
the
i
th elementary braid in
B_{n}
.
Furthermore, taking classifying spaces this endows
B\mathscr{M}

with the structure of an
E_{1}
-module over the
E_{2}
-algebra
B\mathbf{B}
.

Note that if we are interested in homological stability for
stabilization by
X
for the automorphism groups

G_{n}:={\operatorname{Aut}}_{\mathscr{M}}(A\oplus X^{\oplus n})

for some object
A
of
\mathscr{M}
,
only the full subcategory
\mathscr{M}_{A,X}\subseteq\mathscr{M}

spanned by objects of the form
A\oplus X^{\oplus n}
, is relevant.
So for stability purposes, it is enough to consider the
subfunctor

\alpha_{\tau}\colon\mathscr{M}_{A,X}\times\mathbf{B}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathscr{M}_{A,X}.

In fact, to make sure that the structure of
E_{1}
–module over the

E_{2}
–algebra
B\mathbf{B}
is graded, one can even replace the category

\mathscr{M}_{A,X}
by a category with objects the natural
numbers and setting
\text{Aut}(n)=\text{Aut}_{\mathscr{M}}(A\oplus X^{\oplus n})
, avoiding any potential issue coming from unwanted
equalities
A\oplus X^{\oplus n}=A\oplus X^{\oplus m}
for
m\neq n
.

Proof.

The functor

\alpha_{\tau}\colon\mathscr{M}\times\mathbf{B}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathscr{M}

is defined as the composite functor

\alpha(-,-)=(-)\oplus\Phi_{X,\tau}(-),

for

\Phi_{X,\tau}\colon\mathbf{B}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow\mathscr{X}
as above.
The result follows from
[

, Lem 7.2]
because
\alpha

makes
\mathscr{M}
into a module over
\mathbf{B}
and

is braided monoidal.

\square

Example 5.2

5.2.
Homological stability from Yang–Baxter elements

Suppose we are given the data of a monoidal category

(\mathscr{X},\oplus,\mathbbm{1})
acting on a category
\mathscr{M}
,
along with a choice of stabilizing object
X\in\mathscr{X}

and Yang–Baxter operator
\tau\in\text{Aut}_{\mathscr{X}}(X\oplus X)
.
Proposition
5.1
above allows to apply
[

, Thm

, which in this case says that for any

, there is a sequence of simplicial spaces

, for
n\geq 0
, so that if

is highly-connected

satisfies homological stability. Theorem B of the same paper gives in
addition a stability statement with twisted coefficients.
Under an injectivity assumption of the form of
Proposition
3.4
, this simplicial space is homotopy
discrete, and modeled by the space of destabilizations
as described in Definition
4.1
.

Remark 5.4
.

The fact that
(X,\tau)
is a Yang–Baxter operator
is precisely what is needed
for the collection of sets
W_{n}(A,X)_{p}
and maps

d_{i}\colon W_{n}(A,X)_{p}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow W_{n}(A,X)_{p-1}
,
defined as in Definition
4.1
,
to assemble into a semi-simplicial set;
indeed, the Yang–Baxter equation implies the necessary
simplicial identities.

For a fixed monoidal category

\mathscr{X}=(\mathscr{X},\oplus,\mathbbm{1})

acting on a category
\mathscr{M}
,
and a stabilizing object
X\in\mathscr{X}
,
the choice of Yang–Baxter
element will not affect the stabilizing map, but it will affect the
spaces
W_{n}(X,A)_{\bullet}
.
The identity map
1\in\text{Aut}_{\mathscr{X}}(X\oplus X)
is a trivial
choice of Yang–Baxter operator.
But, as is to be expected, this trivial twist is not useful for proving
stability:

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\mathscr{X},\mathscr{M}
,
A
and
X
be as above. If we choose
the Yang–Baxter operator
\tau\in{\operatorname{Aut}}_{\mathscr{X}}(X\oplus X)

to be the identity element,
then the semi-simplicial set
W_{n}(A,X)_{\bullet}
is connected if and only if
the map

G_{n-1}=\text{Aut}_{\mathscr{M}}(A\oplus X^{\oplus n-1})\xrightarrow{{-}\oplus X}\text{Aut}_{\mathscr{M}}(A\oplus X^{\oplus n})=G_{n}

is an isomorphism.

Proof.

If
\tau
is the identity element,
all face maps
d_{i}
are equal to the canonical map
G_{n}/G_{n-p-1}\mathrel{\mathchoice{{}\hbox{$\displaystyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\textstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptstyle{\meno}$}}{{}\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle{\meno}$}}}\mathrel{\mkern-3.0mu}\rightarrow G_{n}/G_{n-p}
.
In particular, the vertices of any
p
-simplex are all equal,
so the semi-simplicial set
W_{n}(A,X)_{\bullet}
is isomorphic to a disjoint union
of semi-simplicial sets, one for each
0
-simplex. The result
follows from the fact that the set of
0
-simplices is precisely
the quotient
G_{n}/G_{n-1}
.

\square

In fact,
Barucco proved in his master thesis a result that translates to the following
stronger statement (stated in the thesis in the context of a groupoid acting on itself,
i.e.
\mathscr{M}=\mathscr{X}
):

Lemma 5.6
.

[
Bar17
, Lem 3.1]

The space

is connected if and only if

and
G_{n-1}\oplus 1
together generate
G_{n}={\operatorname{Aut}}(A\oplus X^{\oplus n})
.

The connectivity of the semi-simplicial set
W_{n}(A,X)
(or of the associated simplical complex defined in
[
RWW17
, Def 2.8]
) can be thought of as a measure a form of
higher generation
of the group

by the cosets of the subgroups

for
p\geq 1
and braid subgroups generated by the chosen Yang–Baxter element

,
in a way similar to the notion of higher generation for a family of subgroups of a group defined in
[
AH93
, 2.1]
.
