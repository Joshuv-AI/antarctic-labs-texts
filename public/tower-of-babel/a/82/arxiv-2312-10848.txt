Global relaxation-based LP–Newton method for multiple hyperparameter selection in support vector classification with feature selection The first draft of the paper was complted while YQ was a visiting postgraduate student at the School of Mathematical Sciences at the University of Southampton, UK. Authors’ email addresses: Qingna Li ( qnl@bit.edu.cn ), Yaru Qian ( y.qian@soton.ac.uk ), and Alain Zemkoho ( a.b.zemkoho@soton.ac.uk )
Qingna Li, Yaru Qian, Alain Zemkoho

Abstract
Support vector classification (SVC) is an effective tool for classification tasks in machine learning. Its performance relies on the selection of appropriate hyperparameters. In this paper, our focus is on identifying the optimal value for the regularization hyperparameter C C , as well as determining the bounds on the features in a SVC problem. This implies that the number of hyperparameters in our SVC can potentially be very large. It is very well-known in machine learning that this could lead to the so-called curse of dimensionality . To address this challenge of multiple hyperparameter selection, the problem is formulated as a bilevel optimization problem, which is then transformed into a mathematical program with equilibrium constraints (MPEC). Our first contribution involves proving the fulfillment of a Mangasarian–Fromovitz constraint qualification tailored to the latter reformulation of the problem. Furthermore, we introduce a novel linear programming (LP)-Newton-based global relaxation method (GRLPN) for solving this problem and provide corresponding convergence results. Typically, in global relaxation methods for MPECs, the algorithm for the corresponding subproblem is treated as a blackbox. Possibly for the first time in the literature, the subproblem is specifically studied in detail. Numerical experiments substantiate the superiority of GRLPN over grid search and the global relaxation solved by the well-known nonlinear programming solver SNOPT.

Introduction

Support vector classification (SVC) is a widely used type of machine learning algorithms that is particularly effective for classification tasks. Classification tasks arise in a wide range of areas, including in economics, finance, and engineering, just to name a few. In engineering more specifically, applications of SVC have been discovered in chemical engineering

, and so on. In civil engineering, for example, SVC can be applied for the prediction of traffic flow and congestion in a road network

, seismic hazard assessment

, soil quality classification
liu2016comprehensive
, pavement condition evaluation

jozdani2019comparing
. Interested readers are referred to the survey

In SVC, the process of selecting the best hyperparameters is a crucial topic, and has been extensively studied by numerous researchers from both theoretical and practical perspectives

;

;

;
duan2003evaluation
;

Recently, bilevel optimization–based techniques to compute optimal hyperparameters have also been gaining some traction, given that they offer numerous advantages as is discussed in
kunapuli2008classification
, for example. However, solving bilevel programs numerically can be extremely challenging due to their inherent hierarchical structure. It is known that even when all the functions involved are linear, the computational complexity is already NP-hard
ben1990computational
. Two standard approaches exist for solving bilevel programming problems, with the first one being the gradient–based methods, which are generally divided into two categories: iterative differentiation
okuno2021lp
;
franceschi2017bridge
;
franceschi2018bilevel
;
shaban2019truncated
and implicit differentiation
foo2007efficient
;
mackay2019self
, depending on how the gradient (w.r.t. hyperparameters) can be computed.

In contrast, the second approach entails transforming the problem into a single-level problem, using various ways. One way is to use the lower-level problem to define an equivalent single–level problem
ye2010new
;
ye1995optimality
. One can also replace the lower-level problem by its first order optimality condition to solve the resulting problem as a mathematical program with equilibrium constraints (MPEC)
bennett2006model
;
kunapuli2008classification
;
kunapuli2008bilevel
;
conigliobilevel
;
wang2023fast
. Therefore, various algorithms for MPECs can be potentially applied to solve bilevel optimization problems. Next, we briefly summarize the algorithms for MPEC, which are closely related to the method used in our paper. Notice that the feasible set of MPECs exhibits a highly unique structure, which leads to the violation of the majority of conventional constraint qualifications, and as a result, specialized algorithms are typically employed to address MPECs effectively
fletcher2006local
;
stein2012lifting
;
scholtes1999exact
.

A distinguished category of algorithms for MPECs is known as relaxation (or regularization) methods
hoheisel2013theoretical
. The original global relaxation method for MPECs was developed by Scholtes
scholtes2001convergence
, and subsequent advancements have introduced various relaxation schemes aiming to alleviate the challenges posed by complementarity constraints in MPECs through different approaches. Certain recent schemes have demonstrated superior theoretical properties compared to the original global relaxation method. Nonetheless, empirical evidence indicates that the Scholtes global relaxation method continues to be one of the most efficient and dependable approaches
hoheisel2013theoretical
.

Some studies have utilized the Scholtes approach for selecting hyperparameters in SVC models; see, e.g.,
li2022bilevel
;
li2022unified
. In the latter papers, bilevel optimization is utilized to determine the optimal value of the hyperparameter
C
for special classes of
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
–based SVC problems. Here, the corresponding versions of the mathematical programming with equilibrium constraints–tailored Mangasarian-Fromovitz constraint qualification (MPEC-MFCQ) is established, and subsequently, the resulting relaxation subproblem is solved with the SNOPT solver.

Unlike in most bilevel optimization–based papers,
kunapuli2008classification
proposes a model to for simultaneously selecting the hyperparameter
C
and determining the bounds for features. Here, the authors utilize the FILTER nonlinear programming solver for solving the associated MPEC transformation of the problem. In this paper, we consider the same model as in
kunapuli2008classification
, which is to choose multiple hyperparameters in an
\ell_{1}
–based SVC model with feature selection that we denote as (
MHS-SVCFS
). We then reformulate it as a tractable bilevel optimization problem, which is then transferred into an MPEC. Different from the approach in
kunapuli2008classification
, we would like to ask the following questions: (a) Is there any theoretical property that the resulting MPEC enjoys? (b) Can we design an efficient numerical algorithm to solve the MPEC? If so, what is the convergence result? These questions serve as the primary impetus driving the work presented in this paper.

Based on these questions, the main contributions of this work are as follows. Firstly, for the first time, we prove the fulfilment of MPEC–MFCQ for the resulting MPEC. Secondly, in conjunction with the global relaxation approach, we introduce and analyze a LP Newton–based method for the associated subproblem. Thirdly, in terms of the numerical pe
rformance, our algorithm outperforms grid search—a pivotal technique for hyperparameter selection in machine learning—as well as the global relaxation method solved by the SNOPT solver.

(MHS-SVCFS)

In this model, the focus lies on selecting appropriate hyperparameters, which can be further divided into two components: the regularization parameter
C\in\mathbb{R}
and the feature selection parameter
\overline{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Given the hyperparameters

This measure is commonly known as the cross-validation error (CV error) for classification, which computes the average number of misclassified data points. It is important to highlight that compared to the traditional L1-SVC, a box constraint exists for each feature in the lower-level problem. If some components in
\overline{w}
are small or even zero, meaning that they do not significantly contribute to the final classifier, the corresponding feature will be deemed redundant or irrelevant.

3
Global relaxation-based LP–Newton method

In this section, we introduce an efficient numerical algorithm to solve the MPEC reformulation (
12
). Among the various approaches to solve the MPECs, the global relaxation method (GRM) proposed by Scholtes
scholtes2001convergence
stands out as one of the most widely adopted. Notably, Kanzow et al.
hoheisel2013theoretical
conducted a comparative analysis of five relaxation methods for solving MPECs, revealing that GRM exhibits superior theoretical properties in terms of convergence requirements and numerical performance. On the other hand, LP–Newton method
facchinei2014lp
;
fischer2016globally
;
fischer2016convergence
has been extensively studied for solving equation systems and demonstrate great advantages in solving nonsmooth equations. Therefore, a question is whether LP–Newton method can be explored to conjunction with GRM to solve (
12
). This motivates our GRLPN method, which is detailed below.

The main difficulty associated with solving MPECs pertains to the presence of complementarity constraints. Consequently, the basic idea behind GRM involves the relaxation of these complementarity constraints. At each iteration, the GRM aims to solve the following subproblem (parameterized in
t_{k}\downarrow 0
):

\begin{array}[]{cl}\min\limits_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{\overline{m}}}&f(v)\\
\hbox{s.t.}&G_{i}(v)\geq 0,\quad\ i=1,\ \cdots,\ \overline{m},\\
&H_{i}(v)\geq 0,\quad\ i=1,\ \cdots,\ \overline{m},\\
&G_{i}(v)H_{i}(v)\leq t_{k},\quad\ i=1,\ \cdots,\ \overline{m},\\
&g_{i}(v)\geq 0,\quad\ i=1,\ \cdots,\ \overline{n}.\end{array}

(NLP-
t_{k}
)

The literature (see, e.g.,
li2022unified
;
li2022bilevel
;
kunapuli2008classification
) encompasses the GRM–based methodology for addressing the hyperparameter selection problem. Nonetheless, we observe a scarcity of publications that specifically address the improvement of solving the subproblem (
NLP-

t
k

), as most of them directly rely on the NLP solvers. Notice that in (
NLP-

t
k

), the objective function is a linear function, and in terms of constraints, except the nonlinear constraints
G_{i}(v)H_{i}(v)\leq t_{k},\ i=1,\ ..,\ \overline{m}
, the rest are all linear constraints. Therefore, it is possible to design efficient algorithms to explore the above special structure in (
NLP-

t
k

). This is the motivation of our GRLPN algorithm.

As is discussed in
hoheisel2013theoretical
, the fulfilment of the MPEC-MFCQ ensures the satisfaction of the MFCQ for the relaxed problem, parametrized by sufficiently small values of
t_{k}
, and thus it is reasonable to consider the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions associated to problem (
NLP-

t
k

). The Lagrangian function for this subproblem and the corresponding KKT conditions (
14a
)–(
14e
) are as follows:

\displaystyle\nabla_{v}L(v,\lambda)=\mathbf{0},

(14a)

\displaystyle g(v)\geq\mathbf{0},\ \lambda^{g}\geq\mathbf{0},\ \sum_{i=1}^{\overline{n}}\lambda_{i}^{g}g_{i}(v)=0,

(14b)

\displaystyle G(v)\geq\mathbf{0},\ \lambda^{G}\geq\mathbf{0},\ \sum_{i=1}^{\overline{m}}\lambda_{i}^{G}G_{i}(v)=0,

(14c)

\displaystyle H(v)\geq\mathbf{0},\ \lambda^{H}\geq\mathbf{0},\ \sum_{i=1}^{\overline{m}}\lambda_{i}^{H}H_{i}(v)=0,

(14d)

\displaystyle t-G_{i}(v)H_{i}(v)\geq 0,\ \lambda^{GH}\geq\mathbf{0},\ \sum_{i=1}^{\overline{m}}\lambda_{i}^{GH}\left[t-G_{i}(v)H_{i}(v)\right]=0.

(14e)

For simplicity, we use
t
instead of
t_{k}
. Here,
L(v,\lambda)
is the Lagrangian function defined as

L(v,\lambda):=f(v)-\sum_{i=1}^{\overline{n}}\lambda_{i}^{g}g_{i}(v)-\sum_{i=1}^{\overline{m}}\lambda_{i}^{G}G_{i}(v)-\sum_{i=1}^{\overline{m}}\lambda_{i}^{H}H_{i}(v)+\sum_{i=1}^{\overline{m}}\lambda_{i}^{GH}[G_{i}(v)H_{i}(v)-t],

where
\lambda:=((\lambda^{g})^{\top},(\lambda^{G})^{\top},(\lambda^{H})^{\top},(\lambda^{GH})^{\top})^{\top}
.
Our subsequent objective is to reframe the KKT conditions (
14a
)–(
14e
) into a system of equations. Various approaches exist for achieving this, as discussed in
galantai2012properties
. Here, we adopt a minimization function for this reformulation:

K_{t}(v,\lambda):=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\nabla_{v}L(v,\lambda)\\
\min\{\lambda^{g},g(v)\}\\
\min\{\lambda^{G},G(v)\}\\
\min\{\lambda^{H},H(v)\}\\
\min\{\lambda^{GH},\mathbf{t}-G(v)H(v)\}\end{array}\right]=\mathbf{0},\\

(15)

where
G(v)H(v)-\mathbf{t}:=[G_{1}(v)H_{1}(v)-t,\ ...\ ,G_{\overline{m}}(v)H_{\overline{m}}(v)-t]^{\top}
. Note that as
K_{t}(v,\lambda)
is a nonsmooth vector–valued function, we employ the LP–Newton method
facchinei2014lp
;
fischer2016globally
;
fischer2016convergence
to solve (
15
). It has already been discussed in
fischer2016convergence
that in order to ensure the local quadratic convergence of the LP–Newton method, one should solve the following equivalent equation system instead:
K_{t}(v,\lambda)=\mathbf{0}
with
\left(v,\lambda\right)\in\bar{\Omega}
, where

\bar{\Omega}:=\{(v,\lambda)\mid\lambda\geq\mathbf{0},\;\;g(v)\geq\mathbf{0},\;\;G(v)\geq\mathbf{0},\;\;H(v)\geq\mathbf{0},\;\;\mathbf{t}-G(v)H(v)\geq 0\}.

However, the nonlinear term
\mathbf{t}-G(v)H(v)
makes it more difficult to directly apply LP–Newton–type framework. Therefore, we introduce a slack variable
u:=\mathbf{t}-G(v)H(v)\in\mathbb{R}^{\overline{m}},
and study the LP–Newton method associated to the following reformulation equivalent to (
15
):

F_{t}(z):=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\nabla_{v}L(v,\lambda)\\
u^{GH}+G(v)H(v)-\mathbf{t}\\
\min\{\lambda^{g},g(v)\}\\
\min\{\lambda^{G},G(v)\}\\
\min\{\lambda^{H},G(v)\}\\
\min\{\lambda^{GH},u\}\\
\end{array}\right]=\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{\overline{q}},\ z\in\Omega,

(16)

where
z:=(v,\lambda,u)\in\mathbb{R}^{\overline{q}}
,
\overline{q}=2\overline{n}+5\overline{m}
and

\Omega:=\{z\mid\lambda\geq\mathbf{0},\;\;u\geq\mathbf{0},\;\;g(v)\geq\mathbf{0},\;\;G(v)\geq\mathbf{0},\;\;H(v)\geq\mathbf{0}\}.

Since
F_{t}(z)
is a piecewise continuously differentiable function with at most
2^{3\overline{m}+2\overline{n}}
possible pieces, it holds that

F_{t}(z)\in\{F^{i}_{t}(z):\,i=1,\ldots,2^{3\overline{m}+2\overline{n}}\}
, where
each selection
F^{i}_{t}(z)
is a continuously differentiable quadratic function.

The iterative process of the LP–Newton method to solve system (
16
) begins with a current iterate
z^{j}\in\Omega
, and the subsequent iterate is determined as
z^{j+1}:=z^{j}+\theta^{m}d^{j}
, while the pair
(\gamma_{j},d^{j})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{\overline{q}}
is a solution to the linear programming problem defined by

\begin{array}[]{cl}\min\limits_{\gamma\in\mathbb{R},d\in\mathbb{R}^{\overline{q}}}&\gamma\\
\hbox{s.t.}&\|F_{t}(z^{j})+\mathcal{J}(z^{j})d\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\|F_{t}(z^{j})\|_{\infty}^{2},\\
&\|d\|_{\infty}\leq\gamma\|F_{t}(z^{j})\|_{\infty},\\
&z^{j}+d\in\Omega,\end{array}

(17)

where
\mathcal{J}(z^{j})\in\partial F_{t}(z^{j})
, and
\partial F_{t}(z^{j})
corresponds to the Clarke’s generalized Jacobian of
F_{t}(z^{j})

clarke1983nonsmooth
throughout the paper. Furthermore,
m\geq 0
is the minimum integer satisfying

\|F_{t}(z^{j}+\theta^{m}d^{j})\|_{\infty}\leq\|F_{t}(z^{j})\|_{\infty}+\sigma_{1}\theta^{m}\Delta_{j}

(18)

with
\sigma_{1}\in(0,1)
being a constant and

\Delta_{j}:=-\|F_{t}(z^{j})\|\left(1-\gamma\|F_{t}(z^{j})\|\right)

(19)

serving as an appropriate approximation of the directional derivative of
\|F(z^{j})\|
along direction
d^{j}
.

t_{0}
, parameters
\sigma_{1}\in(0,1),\ \sigma_{2}\in(0,1),\ \theta\in(0,1),\ t_{\min}>0
, point
(v^{0},\ \lambda^{0},\ u^{0}
).

k:=0
.;

while

do

with

as a solution of (

) with

at

.;

Let
m\geq 0
be the minimum integer satisfying (

).;

end while

Let
v^{k+1}=z_{v}^{j},\ {\lambda}^{k+1}=z_{\lambda}^{j},\ {u}^{k+1}=z_{u}^{j},\ t_{k+1}:=\sigma_{2}t_{k}
