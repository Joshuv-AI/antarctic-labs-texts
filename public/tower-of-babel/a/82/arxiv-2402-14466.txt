Magnitude homology is a derived functor Thanks: The first named author thanks S. Wakatsuki for a fruitful discussion.
Yasuhiko Asao, Sergei O. Ivanov

Abstract
. We prove that the magnitude (co)homology of an enriched category can, under some technical assumptions, be described in terms of derived functors between certain abelian categories. We show how this statement is specified for the cases of quasimetric spaces, finite quasimetric spaces, and finite digraphs. For quasimetric spaces, we define the notion of a distance module over a quasimetric space, define the functor of (co)invariants of a distance module and show that the magnitude (co)homology can be presented via its derived functors. As a corollary we obtain that the magnitude cohomology of a quasimetric space can be presented in terms of Ext functors in the category of distance modules. For finite quasimetric spaces, we show that magnitude (co)homology can be presented in terms of Tor and Ext functors over a certain graded algebra, called the distance algebra of the quasimetric space. For finite digraphs, the distance algebra is a bound quiver algebra. In addition, we show that the magnitude cohomology algebra of a finite quasimetric space can be described as a Yoneda algebra.

Introduction

In

14

Leinster introduced the notion of the Euler characteristic of a finite category. This Euler characteristic is a rational number coinciding with the Euler characteristic of the classifying space if it is a finite CW-complex. Later

It was shown that the coefficients of the formal power series can be described as a certain alternating sum, similar to the sum arising in the classical definition of the Euler characteristic. Having this analogy in mind, Hepworth and Willerton
[
10

defined the magnitude homology of a graph and showed that the coefficients of the magnitude power series are alternating sums of ranks of the magnitude homology groups. Leinster and Shulman

\mathcal{V}
-category

where
\mathcal{V}
is a semicartesian symmetric monoidal category equipped with a strong monoidal functor to an abelian symmetric monoidal category

As in the case of magnitude, a special case was that of metric space. They showed that the relation between the magnitude and the magnitude homology can be generalized from the case of graphs to the case of finite metric spaces. The dual theory of magnitude cohomology was developed by Hepworth
[
9
]
, where he introduces a product in the magnitude cohomology and shows that in the case of a finite metric space the magnitude cohomology algebra completely determines the metric space.

If

is the category of sets,

is the category of abelian groups and

[

which is a right exact functor between abelian categories, and the homology of

can be presented via it’s left derived functors

H_{n}(\mathcal{C})\cong(L_{n}{\rm colim})(\mathbb{Z}).

There is a similar statement about the cohomology of
\mathcal{C}
and the functor of limit. The goal of this work is to generalize these statements to the magnitude (co)homology of an enriched category, and point out the specifics that arise in the special cases of quasimetric space, finite quasimetric space and digraph. We also study the magnitude cohomology algebra of a finite quasimetric space and show that it can be presented as a Yoneda algebra.

2.8.
Nerve of an augmented category

For ordinary small category there is a notion of nerve, which is a simplicial set associated with a category. For enriched categories it is more complicated. One option is to assume that the underlying monoidal category is semicartesian. In this case the construction of nerve is well defined. This happens because any category enriched over a semicartesian monoidal category has a unique augmentation. If we want to consider the nerve of a category enriched over any monoidal category, we need to equip it by an augmentation.

In this subsection we assume that
\mathcal{V}
is cocomplete symmetric monoidal category (not necessarily Bénabou cosmos). Denote by
\mathsf{pt}
the
\mathcal{V}
-category with one object
*
and hom given by
\mathsf{pt}(*,*)=\mathbb{1}.
The composition and identity of
\mathsf{pt}
are defined as the standard isomorphism
\mathbb{1}\otimes\mathbb{1}\cong\mathbb{1}
and the identity morphism
\mathbb{1}\to\mathbb{1}.
An
augmented
\mathcal{V}
-category
is a small non-empty
\mathcal{V}
-category

\mathcal{C}
together with a

\mathcal{V}
-functor

\varepsilon:\mathcal{C}\to\mathsf{pt}
called augmentation.

The nerve of an augmented
\mathcal{V}
-category
\mathcal{C}
is a simplicial object
\mathscr{N}_{\bullet}(\mathcal{C})
in
\mathcal{V},
whose components are defined as

(2.47)

\mathscr{N}_{n}(\mathcal{C})=\coprod_{c_{0},\dots,c_{n}}\mathcal{C}(c_{0},c_{1})\otimes\dots\otimes\mathcal{C}(c_{n-1},c_{n}).

In particular, for
n=0
we have
\mathscr{N}_{0}(\mathcal{C})=\coprod_{c_{0}\in{\rm Ob}(\mathcal{C})}\mathbb{1}.

The face maps
d_{i}:\mathscr{N}_{n}(\mathcal{C})\to\mathscr{N}_{n-1}(\mathcal{C})
for
0\leq i\leq n
are defined as follows. For
0<i<n
they are induced by compositions

(2.48)

\mathcal{C}(c_{i-1},c_{i})\otimes\mathcal{C}(c_{i},c_{i+1})\overset{\cong}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{C}(c_{i},c_{i+1})\otimes\mathcal{C}(c_{i-1},c_{i})\overset{\circ}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{C}(c_{i-1},c_{i+1}).

The morphisms
d_{0}
and
d_{n}
are induced by the augmentation
\varepsilon:\mathcal{C}(c_{0},c_{1})\to\mathbb{1}
and
\varepsilon:\mathcal{C}(c_{n-1},c_{n})\to\mathbb{1}.
The degeneracy maps

are defined induced by the identity map
\mathbb{1}\to\mathcal{C}(c_{i},c_{i}).
We leave the verification of simplicial relations to the reader.

4.2.
Distance modules over a quasimetric space

A generalized metric space is a set
X
equipped with a distance map
d:X\times X\to[0,\infty]
such that

(4.11)

d(x,y)+d(y,z)\geq d(x,z),\hskip 28.45274ptd(x,x)=0

for any
x,y,z\in X.
A
quasimetric space
is a generalized metric space such that

(4.12)

d(x,y)=0\ \Leftrightarrow\ x=y.

The following theory could be developed for any generalized metric space, but for the case of a quasimetric space there are several simplifications. For example, the definition of invariants and coinvariants of distance modules for the general case of generalized metric spaces is more complicated. So, for simplicity, further we will assume that
X
is a quasimetric space. Elements of
X
are called points. We say that a point
y
lies between points
x
and
z,
denoted by

(4.13)

x\preccurlyeq y\preccurlyeq z,

if
d(x,y)+d(y,z)=d(x,z).

In this section we use the abelian Bénabou cosmos
\mathbb{R}\text{-}\mathrm{GrMod}(\mathbb{K})
of
\mathbb{R}
-graded modules over a commutative ring
\mathbb{K}
(for details see Subsection
7.1
in Appendix). A
distance module

M
over a quasimetric space
X
consists of:

(1)

a family
(M(x))_{x\in X}
of
\mathbb{R}
-graded modules indexed by points of
X;

(2)

morphisms
M(x,y):M(x)\to M(y)
of degree
d(x,y)
for each pair of points
x,y
such that
d(x,y)<\infty;

satisfying conditions:
M(x,x)=1_{M(x)}
and

(4.14)

M(y,z)\circ M(x,y)=\begin{cases}M(x,z),&x\preccurlyeq y\preccurlyeq z\\
0,&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

An element
m\in M(x)_{\ell}
will be called an element of
M
over the point
x
of degree
\ell
. For such an element and any point
y
such that
d(x,y)<\infty
we set

(4.15)

m\cdot y:=M(x,y)(m).

So any element
m
of
M
over
x
defines an element
m\cdot y
over
y
of degree

(4.16)

|m\cdot y|=|m|+d(x,y).

Then the equation (
4.14
) can be rewritten as

(4.17)

(m\cdot y)\cdot z=\begin{cases}m\cdot z,&x\preccurlyeq y\preccurlyeq z\\
0,&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

A morphism of distance modules
\varphi:M\to N
is a family
(\varphi(x):M(x)\to N(x))_{x\in X}
of morphisms of
\mathbb{R}
-graded modules of degree
0
such that for any element
m
of
M
over a point
x
and any point
y
such that
d(x,y)<\infty
we have

(4.18)

\varphi(x)(m)\cdot y=\varphi(y)(m\cdot y).

The category of distance modules over
X
is denoted by
\mathrm{DistMod}(X,\mathbb{K}).

For an
\mathbb{R}
-graded module
A
we denote by
\mathrm{Triv}(A)
the distance module such that

(4.19)

\mathrm{Triv}(A)(x)=A

and
\mathrm{Triv}(A)(x,y)=0
for any
x\neq y.
This construction defines a functor

(4.20)

\mathrm{Triv}:\mathbb{R}\text{-}\mathrm{GrMod}(\mathbb{K})\longrightarrow\mathrm{DistMod}(X,\mathbb{K}).

For a distance module
M
we define the
\mathbb{R}
-graded module of invariants as follows

(4.21)

\mathrm{Inv}(M)_{\ell}=\prod_{x\in X}\mathrm{Inv}_{x}(M)_{\ell},

where

(4.22)

\mathrm{Inv}_{x}(M)_{\ell}=\{m\in M(x)_{\ell}\mid m\cdot y=0\text{ for any }y\neq x,d(y,x)<\infty\}.

Dually we define coinvariants

(4.23)

\mathrm{Coinv}(M)_{\ell}=\bigoplus_{x\in X}\mathrm{Coinv}_{x}(M)_{\ell},

where

(4.24)

\mathrm{Coinv}_{x}(M)_{\ell}=M(x)_{\ell}/\langle m\cdot x\mid m\in M(y)_{\ell-d(y,x)},y\neq x,d(y,x)<\infty\rangle.

These two constructions define functors

(4.25)

\mathrm{Inv},\mathrm{Coinv}:\mathrm{DistMod}(X,\mathbb{K})\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}\text{-}\mathrm{GrMod}(\mathbb{K}).

Further we assume that
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is a subgroup such that
d(x,y)\in\mathbb{G}\cup\{\infty\}
for any points
x,y\in X
(for example, if
X
is a digraph, we can take
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{Z}
). Then we consider a full subcategory

(4.26)

\mathrm{DistMod}_{\mathbb{G}}(X,\mathbb{K})\subseteq\mathrm{DistMod}(X,\mathbb{K})

that consists of distance modules such that
M(x)_{\ell}=0
for any
\ell\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{G}.
Such distance modules will be called
\mathbb{G}
-graded. Then the functors of invariants and coinvariants of distance modules, and the functor of trivial distance module can be restricted to the functors

(4.27)

\mathrm{Inv},\mathrm{Coinv}:\mathrm{DistMod}_{\mathbb{G}}(X,\mathbb{K})\longrightarrow\mathbb{G}\text{-}\mathrm{GrMod}(\mathbb{K}),

(4.28)

\mathrm{Triv}:\mathbb{G}\text{-}\mathrm{GrMod}(\mathbb{K})\longrightarrow\mathrm{DistMod}_{\mathbb{G}}(X,\mathbb{K}).

Denote by
\Sigma_{\mathbb{G}}X
an augmented category enriched over
\mathbb{G}\text{-}\mathrm{GrMod}(\mathbb{K})
whose objects are points of
X
and hom-objects are defined as

(4.29)

(\Sigma_{\mathbb{G}}X)(x,y)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{K}[d(x,y)],&d(x,y)<\infty\\
0,&d(x,y)=\infty.\end{cases}

If we denote by
(x,y)
the generator of
(\Sigma_{\mathbb{G}}X)(x,y),
then
(x,x)=1_{x}
and the composition is defined so that

(4.30)

(y,z)\circ(x,y)=\begin{cases}(x,z),&x\preccurlyeq y\preccurlyeq z\\
0,&\text{else}.\end{cases}

The augmentation

\varepsilon_{x,y}:(\Sigma_{\mathbb{G}}X)(x,y)\to\mathbb{K}[0]

is defined by the formula

(4.31)

\varepsilon_{x,y}((x,y))=\begin{cases}1,&x=y,\\
0,&x\neq y.\end{cases}

The category of

(

\ell\geq 0

\mathbb{K}
-module

Now we give a definition of magnitude homology with coefficients in a distance module
M.
We consider a simplicial module

\widetilde{\mathrm{MC}}_{\bullet,\ell}(X,M)
whose components are defined by the formula

(4.45)

\widetilde{\mathrm{MC}}_{n,\ell}(X,M)=\bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}|x_{0},\dots,x_{n}|<\infty\end{subarray}}M(x_{0})_{\ell-|x_{0},\dots,x_{n}|}.

Here the summation is taken over all tuples
(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
of points of
X
such that
|x_{0},\dots,x_{n}|<\infty.
For an element

the corresponding element of
\widetilde{\mathrm{MC}}_{n,\ell}(X,M)
from the summand indexed by

The face maps

for

and for

(4.48)

0,&\text{else.}\end{cases}

s_{i}
are defined by

(4.50)

5.1.
Distance algebra of a finite quasimetric space

Let
X
be a finite quasimetric space (§
4.2
) and
\mathbb{G}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a subgroup of the additive group of real numbers such that
d(x,y)\in\mathbb{G}\cup\{\infty\}
for any points
x,y
of
X.
Consider a
\mathbb{G}
-graded algebra
\sigma X
which as a
\mathbb{K}
-module is freely generated by pairs of points
(x,y)
such that
d(x,y)<\infty
and the product is defined by

(5.1)

(x,y)\cdot(y^{\prime},z)=\begin{cases}(x,z),&y=y^{\prime},x\preccurlyeq y\preccurlyeq z\\
0,&\text{else.}\end{cases}

The algebra
\sigma X
will be referred to as the distance algebra. Note that the elements
e_{x}:=(x,x)
are orthogonal idempotents and
1=\sum_{x\in X}e_{x}
is the unit of this algebra. So the set
\{e_{x}\mid x\in X\}
is a complete set of orthogonal idempotents. Note that in order to define the unit, we need to use that
X
is finite. The
\mathbb{G}
-grading is defined by the formula
|(x,y)|=d(x,y).
In other words,
(\sigma X)_{\ell}
is spanned by pairs
(x,y)
such that
d(x,y)=\ell.
In particular,
(\sigma X)_{0}
is spanned by the idempotents
e_{x}.

Proposition 5.1
.

There is an equivalence of categories

(5.2)

\mathrm{DistMod}_{\mathbb{G}}(X)\simeq\mathbb{G}\text{-}\mathrm{GrMod}(\sigma X),\hskip 28.45274ptM\mapsto\bar{M}

that sends a
\mathbb{G}
-graded distance module
M
to a
\mathbb{G}
-graded right
\sigma X
-module

(5.3)

\bar{M}=\bigoplus_{x\in X}M(x),

where the structure of
\sigma X
-module is defined by
m\cdot(x,y)=m\cdot y,
if
m\in M(x),
and by
m\cdot(x,y)=0,
if
m\in M(x^{\prime}),x\neq x^{\prime}.

Proof.

It is easy to check that the construction
M\mapsto\hat{M}
from the statement defines a functor
\mathrm{DistMod}_{\mathbb{G}}(X)\to\mathbb{G}\text{-}\mathrm{GrMod}(\sigma X)
. Let us construct the functor in the opposite direction. If
N
is a graded right
\sigma X
-module, we construct a distance module
\hat{N}
so that
\hat{N}(x)=Ne_{x}
and
n\cdot y=n\cdot(x,y)
for
n\in\hat{N}(x).
The fact that
\{e_{x}\mid x\in X\}
is a complete set of orthogonal idempotents implies that
N=\bigoplus_{x\in X}Ne_{x}.
Using this, the proof that
N\mapsto\hat{N}
is a functor opposite to the functor
M\mapsto\bar{M}
is straightforward.
∎

5.2.
Magnitude homology of a finite quasimetric space

For a finite quasimetric space
X
we denote by
JX
the homogeneous ideal of
\sigma X
generated by all pairs of positive degree. It is easy to check that if

is the cardinality of

then

(5.4)

(JX)^{n}=0.

Therefore,
JX
is a nilpotent ideal. We set

(5.5)

As an algebra

is isomorphic to the product
\prod_{x\in X}\mathbb{K},
where each factor is generated by the corresponding idempotent
e_{x}.
If
\mathbb{K}
is a principal ideal domain, then the Jacobson radical of

is trivial. Using that
JX
is nilpotent, in this case we obtain that
JX
is the Jacobson radical of
\sigma X
(that is why this notation was chosen).

Since

is a quotient graded algebra of
\sigma X
, it can be treated as a graded right
\sigma X
-module and a graded left
\sigma X
-module. This graded module is concentrated in degree zero and it is equal to the direct sum of graded submodules

(5.6)

where

Using this formula we obtain that

S
corresponds to the trivial distance module
\tilde{\mathbb{K}}=\mathrm{Triv}(\mathbb{K}[0])

(5.7)

If

\mathbb{G}
-graded distance module over

Proof.

Since
S=\sigma X/JX,
we have
\bar{M}\otimes_{\sigma X}S\cong\bar{M}/(\bar{M}\cdot JX).
Since
\bar{M}=\bigoplus_{x}M(x),
we obtain that
\bar{M}/(\bar{M}\tilde{J}X)
is a direct sum of
\mathbb{K}
-modules

Comparing this with the formula (

The second isomorphism can be proved similarly.
∎

6.
Magnitude homology of a digraph

6.1.
Background on representations of quivers

A quiver is a quadruple
Q=(Q_{0},Q_{1},s,t)
such that
Q_{0},Q_{1}
are sets and
s,t:Q_{1}\to Q_{0}
are maps. Elements of
Q_{0}
are called vertices, elements of
Q_{1}
are arrows, for an arrow
\alpha\in Q_{1}
we say that
s(\alpha)
is the start of
\alpha,
and
t(\alpha)
is the target of
\alpha.
A quiver
Q
is called finite, if
Q_{0},Q_{1}
are finite.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a commutative ring. A representation
V
of a quiver
Q
is a collection of
\mathbb{K}
-modules indexed by vertices
V(x)
and a collection of homomorphisms
V(\alpha):V(x)\to V(y)
for each arrow
\alpha,
where
x=s(\alpha)
and
y=t(\alpha).
A morphism of representations
f:V\to U
is a collection of homomorphisms
f(x):V(x)\to U(x)
indexed by vertices such that
U(\alpha)f(x)=f(y)V(\alpha)
for any arrow
\alpha
from
x
to
y.
The category of representations will be denoted by
\mathrm{Rep}(Q).

A path of length
n\geq 1
in a quiver
Q
is a sequence of arrows
\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}
such that
t(\alpha_{i})=s(\alpha_{i+1}).
With each vertex
x\in Q_{0}
we also associate a trivial path
e_{x}
of length zero. For a finite quiver
Q
we define the path algebra
\mathbb{K}Q
as a
\mathbb{K}
-algebra, whose
\mathbb{K}
-basis consists of all paths and trivial paths. The product is defined by concatenation of paths, and it vanishes if the concatenation does not exist. The algebra has a unit defined as the sum of trivial paths
1=\sum_{x\in Q_{0}}e_{x}.
Note that in order to define the unit we use that
Q_{0}
is finite.
It is well-known that the category of representations of a finite quiver
Q
is equivalent to the category of right
\mathbb{K}Q
-modules

(6.1)

\mathrm{Rep}(Q)\simeq\mathrm{Mod}(\mathbb{K}Q)

(see
[
3
, Ch.III, Th.1.5]
,
[
5
, §1, Lemma]
,
[
8
, Theorem 2.4.1]
).
This equivalence sends a representation
V
to a
\mathbb{K}Q
-module
M=\bigoplus_{x}V(x)
such that

v\cdot\alpha=V(\alpha)(v)
for any
v\in V(x)
and any arrow
\alpha
such that
s(\alpha)=x;
and
v\cdot\alpha=0,
if
s(\alpha)\neq x.
The functor in the opposite direction sends a module
M
to a representation
V
defined by
V(x)=Me_{x}
and
V(\alpha)(m)=m\cdot\alpha.

A
quiver with relations
is a pair
(Q,I),
where
Q
is a finite quiver and
I
is an ideal of
\mathbb{K}Q
. We say that a representation
V
of a quiver satisfies relations from
I,
if the annihilator of the corresponding
\mathbb{K}Q
-module contains
I.
The full subcategory of representations satisfying relations from
I
is denoted by

(6.2)

\mathrm{Rep}(Q,I)\subseteq\mathrm{Rep}(Q).

The equivalence (
6.1
) can be restricted to an equivalence

(6.3)

\mathrm{Rep}(Q,I)\simeq\mathrm{Mod}(\mathbb{K}Q/I)

(see
[
3
, Ch.III, Prop.1.7]
,
[
2
, Ch.III, Th.1.6]
).

All this theory can be developed in the graded setting. In this section we assume that
\mathbb{G}=\mathbb{Z}
and “graded” means “
\mathbb{Z}
-graded”. A graded representation
V
of a quiver
Q
is a collection of graded
\mathbb{K}
-modules indexed by vertices
V(x)
and a collection of homomorphisms
V(\alpha):V(x)\to V(y)
of degree
1
for each arrow
\alpha,
where
x=s(\alpha)
and
y=t(\alpha).
A morphism of graded representations
f:V\to U
is a collection of homomorphisms
f(x):V(x)\to U(x)
of degree zero indexed by vertices such that
U(\alpha)f(x)=f(y)V(\alpha)
for any arrow
\alpha
from
x
to
y.
The category of graded representations will be denoted by
\mathrm{GrRep}(Q).

The path algebra
\mathbb{K}Q
has a natural grading, where the
n
-th homogeneous component
(\mathbb{K}Q)_{n}
consists of linear combinations of paths of length
n.
Similarly to the non-graded case we obtain an equivalence

(6.4)

\mathrm{GrRep}(Q)\simeq\mathrm{GrMod}(\mathbb{K}Q).

If we assume that
I
is a homogeneous ideal of
\mathbb{K}Q
, we can also similarly define a subcategory

(6.5)

\mathrm{GrRep}(Q,I)\subseteq\mathrm{GrRep}(Q)

such that there is an equivalence

(6.6)

\mathrm{GrRep}(Q,I)\simeq\mathrm{GrMod}(\mathbb{K}Q/I).

Set
\Lambda=\mathbb{K}Q/I.
Denote by
J
the ideal of
\mathbb{K}Q
generated by all non-trivial paths. The ideal
I
is called admissible if

(6.7)

J^{n}\subseteq I\subseteq J^{2}

for some
n
(see
[
2
, Ch. II, Def. 2.1]
). In this case
(Q,I)
is called bound quiver and
\Lambda
is the bound quiver algebra (usually this terminology is used assuming
\mathbb{K}
is a field). In this case
\Lambda
is finitely generated as a
\mathbb{K}
-module.
The quotient

(6.8)

has a natural structure of a (right and left)
\Lambda
-module. Note that
S
is the direct sum of submodules
S_{x}\cong\mathbb{K}e_{x}

(6.9)

S=\bigoplus_{x\in Q_{0}}S_{x}.

If
\mathbb{K}
is a field and
I
is admissible, the bound quiver algebra
\Lambda
is finite dimensional and
J(\Lambda)=J/I
is the Jacobson radical of
\Lambda
(see
[
3
, §III.1, Prop. 1.6]
,
[
2
, Ch.II, Lemma 2.1]
). Moreover, in this case

is a complete set of representatives of the isomorphism classes of the simple
\Lambda
-modules and

is the direct sum this set
[

, Ch. III, Lemma 2.1]
.

6.2.
Distance modules as representations of quivers

We define a digraph
G
as a quiver without loops and multiple arrows. In this case arrows can be identified with pairs of vertices
\alpha=(s(\alpha),t(\alpha)).
So we can assume that
G_{1}\subseteq G_{0}\times G_{0}
and
s,t
are projections. A finite digraph is a digraph such that
G_{0}
is finite. Further in this section we assume that
G
is a fixed finite digraph.

A path
\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}
in the path algebra
\mathbb{K}G
can be identified with a sequence of the vertices
(x_{0},\dots,x_{n}).
The trivial path
e_{x}
is identified with
(x).
A path
(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
is called shortest, if it is a shortest path from
x_{0}
to
x_{n}.
A path which is not shortest will be called non-shortest.
Denote by
R(G)\triangleleft\mathbb{K}G
a homogeneous ideal generated by two types of relations

(R1)

(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})=(y_{0},\dots,y_{n}),
if
(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
and
(y_{0},\dots,y_{n})
are two different shortest paths from
x_{0}=y_{0}
to
x_{n}=y_{n}.

(R2)

(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})=0,
if
(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
is a non-shortest path.

We say that a non-shortest path
(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
is a minimal non-shortest path, if
(x_{0},\dots,x_{n-1})
and
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
are shortest paths. It is easy to see that (R2) can be replaced by

(R2’)

(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})=0,
if
(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
is a minimal
non-shortest path.

The distance
d(x,y)
between vertices of
G
as the infimum of lengths of paths from
x
to
y.
So
G
defines a finite quasimetric space with integral distances that we denote by the same letter
G
. We will consider the category of
\mathbb{Z}
-graded distance modules
\mathrm{DistMod}_{\mathbb{Z}}(G).
In this section all distance modules are
\mathbb{Z}
-graded.

Proposition 6.1
.

The ideal
R(G)\triangleleft\mathbb{K}G
is admissible and there is an isomorphism of graded algebras

(6.10)

\sigma G\cong\mathbb{K}G/R(G).

Moreover, there is an equivalence of categories

(6.11)

\mathrm{DistMod}_{\mathbb{Z}}(G)\simeq\mathrm{GrRep}(G,R(G))

that sends a
\mathbb{Z}
-graded distance module
M
to a representation
\tilde{M}
such that
\tilde{M}(x)=M(x)
and
\tilde{M}((x,y))=M(x,y)
for each
(x,y)\in G_{1}.

Proof.

If there are a least two shortest paths from one vertex to another, then these paths have lengths
\geq 2.
Non-shortest paths also have length
\geq 2.
Therefore
R(G)\subseteq J^{2}.
There is a finite number of shortest paths in
G.
If we take
n,
which is larger than the length of any shortest path, then
J^{n}\subseteq R(G).
Hence
R(G)
is admissible.

Consider a
\mathbb{K}
-linear homomorphism
\mathbb{K}G\to\sigma G
that sends a shortest path
(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})
to
(x_{0},x_{n}),
and a non-shortest path to zero. In particular, it sends
e_{x}
to
(x,x).
It is easy to check that it is an algebra homomorphism and its kernel is
R(G).
Therefore
\sigma G\cong\mathbb{K}G/R(G).
The equivalence
\mathrm{DistMod}_{\mathbb{Z}}(G)\simeq\mathrm{GrRep}(G,R(G))
follows from Proposition
5.1
and equivalence (
6.6
).
∎

As a corollary we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a commutative ring and
G
be a finite digraph. Then there are isomorphisms

\mathrm{MH}_{n,\ell}(G)={\rm Tor}^{\mathbb{K}G/R(G)}_{n,\ell}(S,S),\hskip 28.45274pt\mathrm{MH}^{n,\ell}(G)={\rm Ext}_{\mathbb{K}G/R(G)}^{n,\ell}(S,S).

Moreover, the magnitude cohomology algebra is isomorphic to the Yoneda algebra of the left
\mathbb{K}G/R(G)
-module
S

(6.13)

\mathrm{MH}^{*,*}(G)=\mathcal{Y}^{*,*}_{\mathbb{K}G/R(G)}(S)

Proof.

It follows from Theorems
5.3
,
5.6
and Proposition
6.1
.

7.1.
Abelian cosmos of graded modules

Let
\mathbb{K}
be a commutative ring and
\mathbb{G}
be an abelian group. In this subsection we will introduce the Bénabou cosmos of
\mathbb{G}
-graded
\mathbb{K}
-modules
\mathbb{G}\text{-}\mathrm{GrMod}(\mathbb{K}).
In this subsection the word “module” means “
\mathbb{K}
-module”.

An
\mathbb{G}
-graded module is a family of modules
A=(A_{g})_{g\in\mathbb{G}}.
An element of
A_{g}
will be called a homogeneous element of
A
of degree
g.
The degree of a homogeneous element
a
will be denoted by
|a|.
A morphism

of
\mathbb{G}
-graded modules of degree

is defined as a family of homomorphisms
(f_{g}:A_{g}\to B_{g+d})_{g\in\mathbb{G}}.
The degree of a morphism will be denoted by
|f|=d.
Therefore for a homogeneous element
a
of
A
we have

|f(a)|=|f|+|a|.

The composition of such morphisms is defined in the obvious way so that

|gf|=|f|+|g|.

We denote by
[A,B]_{d}
the abelian group of homomorphisms of degree
d

(7.1)

[A,B]_{d}=\prod_{g_{2}-g_{1}=d}\mathrm{Hom}(A_{g_{1}},B_{g_{2}}),

and denote by
[A,B]
the
\mathbb{G}
-graded module, whose components are
[A,B]_{d}.

For two
\mathbb{G}
-graded modules
A
and
B
we denote by
A\otimes B
an
\mathbb{G}
-graded module such that

(7.2)

(A\otimes B)_{g}=\bigoplus_{g_{1}+g_{2}=g}A_{g_{1}}\otimes B_{g_{2}}.

Then it is easy to check that for any
\mathbb{G}
-graded modules
A,B,C
there is an isomorphism of
\mathbb{G}
-graded modules

(7.3)

[A\otimes B,C]\cong[A,[B,C]].

For an
\mathbb{G}
-graded module
A
and a

we denote by
A[\ell]
the shifted
\mathbb{G}
-graded module

(7.4)

(A[\ell])_{g}=A_{g-\ell}.

For any (non-graded) module
V
we also denote by
V[\ell]
the
\mathbb{G}
-graded module concentrated in degree
\ell

(7.5)

V[\ell]_{g}=\begin{cases}V,&g=\ell,\\
0,&g\neq\ell.\end{cases}

It is easy to see that for any
\mathbb{G}
-graded module
A
there are isomorphisms

(7.6)

The category of
\mathbb{G}
-graded modules is a category whose objects are
\mathbb{G}
-graded modules and morphisms are morphisms of degree
0.

(7.7)

\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{G}\text{-}\mathrm{GrMod}(\mathbb{K})}(A,B)=[A,B]_{0}.

It is easy to see that the tensor product

the inner hom

and the unit

make it an abelian Bénabou cosmos. One can say that it as a category it is a product of the categories of

-modules

Therefore, this category has enough of projectives and injectives.

7.2.
Graded modules over graded algebras

By a
\mathbb{G}
-graded
\mathbb{K}
-algebra
\Lambda
we mean a collection of
\mathbb{K}
-modules
(\Lambda_{g})_{g\in\mathbb{G}}
equipped with bilinear homomorphisms
\cdot:\Lambda_{g}\times\Lambda_{h}\to\Lambda_{g+h}
and an element

satisfying the associativity and unit axioms (one can say that a graded algebra is a monoid in the cosmos of
\mathbb{G}
-graded modules). Traditionally people consider an ordinary algebra
\Lambda^{\oplus}=\bigoplus_{g\in\mathbb{G}}\Lambda_{g}
associated with the graded algebra, and call it graded algebra. We will also allow such freedom of speech, replacing
\Lambda
by
\Lambda^{\oplus}
if it does not lead to confusion. However, the situation with tensor product and hom of
\mathbb{G}
-graded
\Lambda
-modules is more complex, and there such freedom of speech can lead to confusion. So let us describe in details relations between hom-sets and tensor products of
\mathbb{G}
-graded
\Lambda
-modules and
\Lambda^{\oplus}
-modules. Further we assume that the word “graded” means “
\mathbb{G}
-graded” for the fixed
\mathbb{G}
.

If
\Lambda
is a graded algebra, a right
\mathbb{G}
-graded
\Lambda
-module is a collection of
\mathbb{K}
-modules
M=(M_{g})_{g\in\mathbb{G}}
equipped with bilinear maps
\cdot:M_{g}\times\Lambda_{h}\longrightarrow M_{g+h}
satisfying the exterior associativity and unit axioms. A left graded
\Lambda
-module is defined similarly. If
M
and
N
are right graded
\Lambda
-modules, then

(7.8)

\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(M,N),

is a graded
\mathbb{K}
-module whose

\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(M,N)_{\ell}
consists of collections of
\mathbb{K}
-homomorphisms
(f_{g}:M_{g}\to N_{g+\ell})_{g\in\mathbb{G}}
such that
f_{g+h}(m\cdot a)=f_{g}(m)\cdot a
for any

The hom-set in the category of graded
\Lambda
-modules is the

-component of this graded module

(7.9)

\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{G}\text{-}\mathrm{GrMod}(\Lambda)}(M,N)=\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(M,N)_{0}.

So we can say that

(7.10)

\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(M,N)_{\ell}=\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{G}\text{-}\mathrm{GrMod}(\Lambda)}(M,N[-\ell]).

Now assume that
M
is a right graded
\Lambda
-module and

is a left graded
\Lambda
-module. Then the tensor product

(7.11)

\mathbb{K}
-module, whose
\ell

is defined as the quotient of the
\mathbb{K}
-module
\bigoplus_{g+h=\ell}M_{g}\otimes N_{h}
by the submodule generated by elements of the form

It is easy to see that

(7.12)

\Lambda\otimes_{\Lambda}N\cong N,\hskip 28.45274pt\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(\Lambda,M)\cong M.

For a graded
\Lambda
-module
M
we denote by

the corresponding

\Lambda^{\oplus}
-module.
We will also consider a bigger

\Lambda^{\oplus}

(7.13)

M^{\oplus}\subseteq M^{\Pi}.

The elements of

are families
m_{*}=(m_{g})_{g\in\mathbb{G}},
where
m_{g}\in M_{g}.

Then for

the product is defined by
(m_{*}\cdot a)_{g}=m_{g-h}a.

The same notations are used for graded
\mathbb{K}

Using that the tensor product over
\mathbb{K}

(7.14)

(M\otimes_{\Lambda}N)^{\oplus}\cong M^{\oplus}\otimes_{\Lambda^{\oplus}}N^{\oplus}.

However, for hom-sets the formula is more complicated

\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda^{\oplus}}(M^{\oplus},N^{\Pi})\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(M,N)^{\Pi}

It follows from the isomorphism

If both graded modules are non-trivial only in finitely many degrees, then products in the last formula can be replaced by direct sums.

The derived functors of the functors

(7.16)

The formula (

Note that this implies the symmetric description of the bigraded ext

(7.20)

\mathrm{Ext}^{n,\ell}_{\Lambda}(M,N)=(R^{n}\mathrm{Hom}_{\Lambda}(-,N)(M))_{-\ell}.

Using this, formulas (
7.14
), (
7.15
