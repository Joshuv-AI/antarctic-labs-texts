Abstract
. We construct and study branching fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter H ∈ ( 1 / 2 , 1 ) H\in(1/2,1) . The construction relies on a generalization of the discrete approximation of fractional Brownian motion (Hammond and Sheffield [ HS13 ] ) to power law Pólya urns indexed by trees. We show that the first order of the speed of branching fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter H H is c ​ t H + 1 / 2 ct^{H+1/2} where c c is explicit and only depends on the Hurst parameter. A notion of “branching property” for processes with memory emerges naturally from our construction.

1.
Introduction

Fractional Brownian Motion with Hurst parameter
H
, also known as
H
-fractional Brownian motion, is the unique centered Gaussian process
B^{H}
with stationary increments and variance
\Varo\big[B^{H}_{t}\big]=t^{2H}
. We will restrict to the case
H\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
, for which fractional Brownian Motion has positively correlated increments (and lacks the Markov property). By a well-known result of Mandelbrot and van Ness
[
MVN68
]
, fractional Brownian motion (FBM) has a kernel representation in terms of Wiener increments
\dif W(s)
:

(1.1)

B^{H}(t)=\int_{(-\infty,t]}K(s,t)\dif{W(s)},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}.

The definition of the kernel
K=K^{H}
will be recalled in Section
3.1
.
In the following we will construct and analyse
a
branching
version of fractional Brownian motion, more specifically
fractional Brownian motion indexed by a Yule tree
\mathfrak{Y}
. This construction works in two steps: the first is for a given realisation
\mathfrak{y}
of
\mathfrak{Y}
, the second is a randomisation over
\mathfrak{y}
.

Figure 1
.
This shows a simulation of the branching fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H=0.85
. The
y
-axis is measured in units of
\left(\sum_{l\geq 0}q_{l}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
for
q_{l}
defined by (
2.10
) and
\alpha=H-\tfrac{1}{2}
.
The simulation is based on the discrete approximation described in Section
2.2

The representation (
1.1
) lends itself for a construction of the
\mathfrak{y}
-indexed FBM. To this purpose, think of
\mathfrak{y}
as the disjoint union of countably many edges
\{h\}\times(\ell_{h},r_{h}]
, where
h\in\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\{0,1\}^{n}
is the Ulam-Harris name (see
[
Har63
, Section 2.1]
and Section
2.2
) of the edge and
(\ell_{h},r_{h}]
is the life time interval of the edge. For each
v=(h,t)\in\mathfrak{y}
, let
t(v)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=t
. In this way, the
ancestral lineage

\mathfrak{a}(v)\subset\mathfrak{y}
of each
v\in\mathfrak{y}
is in isometric correspondence with
(-\infty,t(v)]
. Let
dW_{\mathfrak{y}}(v),\,v\in\mathfrak{y}
, be a standard Gaussian white noise on
\mathfrak{y}
. Then the
\mathfrak{y}
-indexed FBM
has the representation

(1.2)

B_{\mathfrak{y}}^{H}(v)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{\mathfrak{a}(v)}K(t(u),t(v))\dif{W_{\mathfrak{y}}(u)},\quad v\in\mathfrak{y}.

This construction is implicit in work by Adler and Samorodnitsky, see
[
AS95
]
, who introduced branching fractional Brownian as a starting point for their construction of super fractional Brownian motion as a process of measures on the path space that can be seen as a high density limit of branching fractional Brownian motion. For related approaches to tree-indexed processes with memory see
[
KLS19
]
, who studied the long-term behaviour of their cloud of particles with a focus on the bulk.

Our paper has two main contributions. Firstly, in Theorem
3.2
we will construct discrete approximations of
\mathfrak{y}
-indexed FBM
B_{\mathfrak{y}}^{H}
(and thus also the branching fractional Brownian motion) for
H>\frac{1}{2}
via a discrete approximation. This approach is based on the power law Pólya’s urn of Hammond and Sheffield
[
HS13
]
and a recent analysis of the random genealogy that underlies this urn, see
[
IW23
]
.

In a nutshell, the approximation of
B^{H}
works as follows. For
\alpha\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=H-\frac{1}{2}
, consider a family
\left(R_{i}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
of independent
\mathbb{N}
-valued random variables with distribution

(1.3)

\mu\left(\left\{n,n+1,\ldots\right\}\right)=n^{-\alpha},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}.

The types (say
-1
or
+1
) of the individuals
i\in\mathbb{Z}
are determined recursively: Each individual
i
selects
i-R_{i}
as its parent and inherits the parent’s type. As it turns out, this leads to a forest of countably many trees, see Section
2.1
. If each of these trees is assigned the type
-1
or
+1
by a fair coin tossing,
it further turns out that the rescaled sums over the types of the individuals
1,\ldots,\lfloor tn\rfloor
,
t\geq 0
, converge to fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H
as
n\to\infty
.

The proof of Theorem
3.2
, which will be carried out in Section
6
, reveals interesting analytic identities. While the representation (
1.1
) ( as well as (
1.2
) ) has the flavour of a moving average, the Hammond-Sheffield approximations of FBM is more in the spirit of an autoregression. Indeed, as an aside of our proof of Theorem
3.2
, we will obtain a “microscopic” interpretation of the prediction formula of Gripenberg and Norros
[
GN96
]
.

The simulation depicted in Figure
1
is based on the discrete approximation provided by Theorem
3.2
.

Our second main contribution in this paper is the analysis of the speed of the right-most particle of branching fractional Brownian motion, in the spirit of McKean’s celebrated work
[
McK75
]
about branching Brownian motion, which was later substantially refined by Bramson
[
Bra78
]
and Lalley and Sellke
[
LS87
]
.
Specifically we will show that
the maximum
M^{H}(t)
of a BFBM with Hurst parameter
H>\tfrac{1}{2}
behaves asymptotically like

(1.4)

m(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=t^{H+\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{\pi}2^{2H+1}H}{\Gamma(1-H)\Gamma\left(H+\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(H+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}}

in the sense that for all
\varepsilon>0

(1.5)

\mathcal{P}\left(\left|\frac{M^{H}(T)}{m(t)}-1\right|>\varepsilon\right)\rightarrow 0\quad\mbox{ for }t\rightarrow\infty.

See Figure
2
for an illustration of
m(t)
for
t
fixed and varying
H
.
Note that for
H=\tfrac{1}{2}

m(t)
equals
\sqrt{2}t
, which is consistent with the results about branching Brownian motion.

Figure 2
.
This is an illustration of the behaviour of
m(t)/t^{H+\frac{1}{2}}
as a function of
H
.

Up to the constant factor in (
1.4
), the leading order
t^{H+\frac{1}{2}}
of the maximum can be easily explained. Indeed, for a number
\lfloor e^{t}\rfloor
of independent normally distributed random variables with variance
t^{2H}
the leading order of the maximum would be given by

\mathfrak{m}(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sqrt{2}\,\,t^{H+\frac{1}{2}}

as suggested by the estimate

e^{t}\WS\left(\mathcal{N}(0,t^{2H})\geq\mathfrak{m}(t)\right)\approx e^{t}\exp\left(-\frac{\mathfrak{m}(t)^{2}}{2t^{2H}}\right)=1.

Due to correlations we obtain (
1.2
) which only differs by the constant prefactor depending on
H
. The statement on
M^{H}(t)
is proved in Theorem
3.8
, which in turn is strongly connected to Theorem
3.6
, see Remark
3.9
.
The proof techniques of these theorems rely heavily on exploring the connection
between branching random walks and the so-called generalised random energy models (GREM). Those models were introduced and discussed by Derrida in the 1980’s in
[
Der80
]
,
[
Der85
]
and
[
Der81
]
. An introduction to those can be found in Lecture Notes by Kistler
[
Kis15
]
.

The connection between GREMs and branching random walks has first been explored by Arguin, Bovier and Kistler in
[
ABK11
]
for branching Brownian motion. In contrast to Brownian motion fractional Brownian motion is a process with memory. The corresponding GREM is then a continuous GREM with decreasing variance.
Of such a GREM, Bovier and Kurkova analyse the leading order of the maximum in
[
BK04b
]
. Their results lead to Theorem
3.6
, where the underlying tree is a deterministic binary branching tree, see Section
7
. For subleading orders work by Maillard and Zeitouni
[
MZ16
]
allows some conjectures in our regime, see Remark
3.12
. For the proof of Theorem
3.8
, where the underlying tree is a Yule tree, we rely on methods developed first in
[
ABK11
]
and later in
[
KS15
]
by Kistler and Schmidt.
In Remark
3.13
we give a more detailed account of (some of) the literature on the GREM, with an emphasis on work that is relevant for the ideas that are used in the present paper.

Our proof of Theorem
3.8
is conceptual in the sense that it makes use of the connection between GREMs
and branching random walks, Section
8
. As pointed out to us by an anonymous reviewer of a previous version of this paper,
Theorem
3.8
can also be deduced in an expedited manner from known results on branching Brownian motion by using the representation (
1.2
) and the Payley-Wiener partial integration formula, see Remark
3.11
.

2.1.
The Hammond-Sheffield random walk

We start by briefly recalling the urn model of
[
HS13
]
along the lines of
[
IW23
]
.

For
\alpha\in\left(0,\tfrac{1}{2}\right)
and a slowly varying function
L\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{+}
let
\mu\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mu_{\alpha,L}
be a probability measure on
\mathbb{N}
having regularly varying tails

(2.1)

\mathbf{\mu}(\{n,n+1,\ldots\})\sim n^{-\alpha}L(n),

and let
R
be an
\mathbb{N}
-valued random variable with distribution
\mu
.

Let
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}
be a random directed graph with vertex set
\mathbb{Z}
and edge set
E\left(\mathcal{G}_{\mu}\right)
generated in the following way: Let
\left(R_{i}\right)_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}
be a family of independent copies of
R
. The random set of edges
E\left(\mathcal{G}_{\mu}\right)
is then given by

(2.2)

E\left(\mathcal{G}_{\mu}\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{(i,i-R_{i})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}i\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

This induces the (random) equivalence relation

(2.3)

i\sim j\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Longleftrightarrow i\mbox{ and }j\mbox{ belong to the same connected component of }\mathcal{G}_{\mu}.

If
\alpha>1/2
there is only one connected component and this relation is trivial.

The individuals’ types arise as follows: Assume that each component of
\mathcal{G}_{\mu}
gets its type by an independent copy of a real-valued random variable
Y
with

(2.4)

\mathbf{E}[Y]=0\mbox{ and }0<\mathbf{E}[Y^{4}]<\infty.

In the situation of
[
HS13
]
,
Y
is a centered Rademacher
(p)
variable, i.e.

(2.5)

Y=\xi-(2p-1)\mbox{ with }\WS(\xi=+1)=p,\,\WS(\xi=-1)=1-p.

Denote by
\mathscr{C}_{i}
the component which contains
i
(note that
\mathscr{C}_{i}=\mathscr{C}_{j}
if
i\sim j
). For
i\in\mathbb{Z}
the type of the component
\mathscr{C}_{i}
will be denoted by
Y_{i}
.
Define the “random walk” (with dependent increments)

(2.6)

S_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i},\quad n=0,1,\ldots.

Let us analyze the covariance structure of this random walk to motivate its relation to fractional Brownian motion: By construction,

(2.7)

\sigma_{n}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbf{Var}[S_{n}]=\sum_{i,j\in[n]}\mathbf{Cov}[Y_{i},Y_{j}]=\mathbf{E}[Y^{2}]\,\sum_{i,j\in[n]}\mathbf{P}(i\sim j).

[
HS13
, Lemma 3.1]
shows by Fourier and Tauberian arguments that

(2.8)

\sum\limits_{i,j\in[n]}\mathbf{P}(i\sim j)\sim\frac{C_{1}}{\alpha(2\alpha+1)}\cdot\frac{n^{2\alpha+1}}{L(n)^{2}}\quad\mbox{ as }n\to\infty;

with
C_{1}
as in (
C.2
), see
[
IW23
, (1.8)]
.

For
\frac{i-1}{n}\leq t\leq\frac{i}{n}
,
i,n\in\mathbb{N}
, let

(2.9)

S^{(n)}(t)
be the linear interpolation of
S_{i}/\sigma_{n}
and
S_{i+1}/\sigma_{n}
.

Because
(S_{n})
has stationary increments by construction, it follows from (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) that
S^{(n)}
has, asymptotically as
n\to\infty
, the covariance structure of fractional Brownian motion with Hurst parameter
H\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tfrac{1}{2}+\alpha
. In order to prove that
S^{(n)}
converges (in the sense of finite dimensional distributions) to
fractional Brownian motion, it is shown in
[
IW23
]
that the finite dimensional distributions of
S^{(n)}
are asymptotically Gaussian. This is provided by
[
IW23
, Theorem 1.1]
, see also
[
HS13
, Theorem 1.1]
.

The renewal function,

(2.10)

q_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbf{P}\left(\tilde{R}_{1}+\ldots+\tilde{R}_{j}=n\mbox{ for some }j\geq 0\right)

with
\tilde{R}_{1},\tilde{R}_{2},\ldots
being independent copies of
R
, an
\mathbb{N}
-valued random variable with distribution
\mu
as in (
2.1
),
is the main ingredient to describe the random graph
G_{\mu}
. As in
[
IW23
]
we will work under the condition

(2.11)

q_{n}\sim\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\frac{n^{\alpha-1}}{L(n)}\quad\mbox{as }n\rightarrow\infty,

see
[
IW23
, Theorem 1.1(B)]
.
This is equivalent to the validity of the Strong Renewal Theorem for the renewal process with increment distribution (
2.1
), see Caravenna and Doney
[
CD19
]
, whose Theorem 1.4 gives necessary and sufficient conditions in terms of

for the validity of (

[

]

\sup_{n\geq 1}\frac{n\mathbf{P}(R=n)}{\mathbf{P}(R>n)}<\infty.

We will work under the more restrictive assumption of
L\equiv 1
and (
2.5
) with
p=\tfrac{1}{2}
to simplify notation. This means that we assign a type to each component of the random graph

either
+1
or
-1
with probability
\tfrac{1}{2}
.

Another key ingredient to analyse this model is the pair coalescence probability
\WS\left(0\sim n\right)
.
Under the above assumption we get

(2.13)

\WS\left(0\sim n\right)\sim\frac{1}{\sum_{l\geq 0}q_{l}^{2}}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(1-2\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(1-\alpha)^{3}}\cdot n^{2\alpha-1}

[

Main results

In this section we will describe the main contributions of this paper, which include a discrete approximation of branching fractional Brownian motion and the derivation of the speed of its maximum.

3.1.
Tree-indexed fractional Brownian motion

Remember the classical construction of fractional Brownian motion already stated in the Introduction, see (
1.1
):
Given a Brownian motion
W
, Gaussian white noise on
\mathbb{R}
, we construct a fractional Brownian Motion with Hurst parameter
H
by setting

(3.1)

B(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(s,t)\dif{W(s)}

with

(3.2)

K(s,t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=K^{H}(s,t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{C_{H}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{s\leq 0}\left((t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right)+\mathbf{1}_{t\geq s>0}(t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right],

and

(3.3)

C_{H}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left(-\frac{2^{-2H}\Gamma(-H)\Gamma\left(H+\frac{1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

see
[
MVN68
, Definition 2.1, Corollary 3.4]
, where this process is called
reduced fractional Brownian motion
. Note, that the normalization corresponds to the one used by Samorodnitsky and Taqqu in
[
ST17
, Chapter 7.2]
and differs from the one used in
[
MVN68
]
.
In the same way we can proceed and construct on top of branching Brownian motion
\left(\left(W_{b}(t)\right)_{[0,T]}\right)_{b\in\mathcal{B}}
, Gaussian white noise on an
\mathbb{R}
-tree
\mathfrak{y}\in\mathcal{T}
, fractional branching Brownian motion:

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\left(\left(W_{b}(t)\right)_{[0,T]}\right)_{b\in\mathcal{B}}
be Gaussian white noise on an
\mathbb{R}
-tree
\mathfrak{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
. The process

\left(\left(B_{b}(t)\right)_{[0,T]}\right)_{b\in\mathcal{B}}
defined by

(3.4)

B_{b}(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}K(s,t)\dif{W_{b}(s)}

is called
\mathfrak{y}
-indexed branching fractional Brownian motion
(BFBM).

Note that the above definition corresponds to the process defined in (
1.2
) and the construction of
[
KLS19
, Section 6]
.
If one now computes the covariance

(3.5)

\Cov\left[B_{b}\left(t_{1}\right),B_{\tilde{b}}\left(t_{2}\right)\right]

one obtains for branches
b
and
\tilde{b}
with
b\wedge\tilde{b}=s

(3.6)

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathrm{K}}\left(t_{1},t_{2},s\right)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=

\displaystyle\EW_{\mathfrak{y}}\left[B_{b}(t_{1})B_{\tilde{b}}(t_{2})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C_{H}^{2}}\EW_{\mathfrak{y}}\left[\left(\int_{-\infty}^{0}(t_{1}-\xi)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(-\xi)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\dif{B_{b}(\xi)}+\int_{0}^{t_{1}}(t_{1}-\xi)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\dif{B_{b}(\xi)}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.\cdot\left(\int_{-\infty}^{0}(t_{2}-\xi)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(-\xi)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\dif{B_{\tilde{b}}(\xi)}+\int_{0}^{t_{2}}(t_{2}-\xi)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\dif{B_{\tilde{b}}(\xi)}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C_{H}^{2}}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left((t_{1}-\xi)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(-\xi)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\left((t_{2}-\xi)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(-\xi)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\dif{\xi}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{C_{H}^{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{s}(t_{1}-\xi)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}(t_{2}-\xi)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\dif{\xi}\right).

For
t_{1}=t_{2}\equiv t>s
this gives

(3.7)

\displaystyle\rho^{\mathrm{K}}\left(t,t,s\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C_{H}^{2}}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{0}\left((t-\xi)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(-\xi)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{2}\dif{\xi}\right)+\frac{1}{C_{H}^{2}}\left(\int_{0}^{s}(t-\xi)^{2H-1}\dif{\xi}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{2H}-(t-s)^{2H}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}2^{2H-1}}{\Gamma(1-H)\Gamma\left(H+\frac{1}{2}\right)}=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}t^{2H}-C_{\rho}(t-s)^{2H}

Observe that the prefactor one of
t^{2H}
in (
3.7
) is due to the normalization used in (
3.2
).

3.4.
The maximal displacement of branching fractional Brownian motion

In the following we identify the leading order of
\max\limits_{b\in\mathcal{B}}B_{b}(t)
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\mathfrak{y}_{\mathrm{bin}}
be a deterministic binary branching tree (every branch branches into two after time 1, see Figure
7
), and recall the definition of
C_{H}
from (
3.3
).
The leading order of the maximum of a BFBM

(3.14)

\left(\left(B_{b}(t)\right)_{t\geq 0}\right)_{b\in\mathcal{B}}

with Hurst parameter
H\in\left(\tfrac{1}{2},1\right)
is

(3.15)

m(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=t^{H+\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\log(2)\sqrt{\pi}2^{2H+1}H}{\Gamma(1-H)\Gamma\left(H+\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(H+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}}=t^{H+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2\log(2)}}{C_{H}}\cdot\frac{1}{(H+\frac{1}{2})}

in the sense that

(3.16)

\EW_{\mathfrak{y}_{\mathrm{bin}}}\left[\frac{\max\limits_{b\in\mathcal{B}}B_{b}(t)}{m(t)}\right]\rightarrow 1\quad\mbox{ for }t\rightarrow\infty.

See Section
7
for a proof.

Remark 3.7
.

Arguments in the flavour of Arguin, Bovier and Kistler
[
ABK11
]
, which were continued in work by Kistler and Schmidt
[
KS15
]
, see
Section
8
, give us a similar statement for branching fractional Brownian motion on Yule trees, see the next theorem.
The idea of the proof is to first show that the maximum of a BFBM can only be attained by a trajectory staying very close to the maximum all along the way. For this purpose we will show exactly this for a series of Generalized random energy models (GREMs) approximating BFBM. Finally we observe that trajectories staying close to the maximum are indeed of the desired order.

In Remark
3.11
we give another proof of Theorem
3.8
which combines results of
[
ABK11
]
with the Mandelbrot-van-Ness-representation
of fractional Brownian motion in terms of Wiener integrals and the Payley-Wiener partial integration formula.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{Y}
be a (binary branching) Yule tree with branching rate
1
and let
\eta
denote its distribution. Then
the leading order of the maximum of a BFBM

(3.17)

\left(\left(B_{b}(t)\right)_{t\geq 0}\right)_{b\in\mathcal{B}}

with Hurst parameter
H\in\left(\tfrac{1}{2},1\right)
is

(3.18)

m(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=t^{H+\frac{1}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{\pi}2^{2H+1}H}{\Gamma(1-H)\Gamma\left(H+\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(H+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}}}=t^{H+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}}{C_{H}}\cdot\frac{1}{(H+\frac{1}{2})}

for

defined by (

)
in the sense that

(3.19)

\mathop{\text{\LARGE$\int_{\text{\normalsize$\scriptstyle\mathbb{T}$}}$}}\nolimits\WS_{\mathfrak{y}}\left(\left|\frac{\max\limits_{b\in\mathcal{B}}B_{b}(t)}{m(t)}-1\right|>\varepsilon\right)\eta\left(\dif\mathfrak{y}\right)\rightarrow 0\quad\mbox{ for }t\rightarrow\infty

and all

.

Note that this result means

\mathcal{P}\left(\left|\frac{\max\limits_{b\in\mathcal{B}}B_{b}(t)}{m(t)}-1\right|>\varepsilon\right)\rightarrow 0,

Observe that the only difference between the formulas (

is to always follow the maximum path. This allows to compute the order of the maximal displacement directly. For details we refer to Section

Remark 3.10
.

We conjecture that the above results hold almost surely.

Remark 3.11
.

The proof of Theorem
3.8
, which we will give in Section

, is conceptual in the sense that it relates the fractional branching Brownian motion rather directly
with a generalised random energy model, see Remark
3.7
. Here we give an alternative proof, which makes use of the Mandelbrot-van-Ness-representation
of fractional Brownian motion in terms of Wiener integrals and transforms these via the Payley-Wiener partial integration formula into weighted Riemann-Integrals of the paths
of branching Brownian motion and controls their asymptotics using results from
[

]
:
Remember that we can represent branching fractional Brownian motion by the Wiener integral

(3.21)

B_{b}(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{C_{H}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{s\leq 0}\left((t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right)+\mathbf{1}_{t\geq s>0}(t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right]\dif{W_{b}(s)}.

Now note that all branches have the integral over
\mathbb{R}_{\leq 0}
in common. Writing

(3.22)

Z_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}\frac{1}{C_{H}}\left((t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}-(-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\right)\dif{W_{0}(s)}

we thus obtain

(3.23)

B_{b}(t)=Z_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{C_{H}}(t-s)^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\dif{W_{b}(s)}.

By integration by parts
the latter integral is equal to

(3.24)

\int_{0}^{t}\frac{H-\frac{1}{2}}{C_{H}}(t-s)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}W_{b}(s)\dif{s},

and similarly

(3.25)

Z_{t}=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{-}}\frac{H-\frac{1}{2}}{C_{H}}\left((t-s)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}-(-s)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}\right)W_{0}(s)\dif{s}.

Since
W_{0}(s)
is just an ordinary Brownian motion
Z_{t}
is almost surely at most of order
t^{H}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
, then by
[
ABK11
, Corollary 2.6]
(see also
[
Bov15
, Theorem 3.8]
and
[
ABBS13
, Proposition 2.5]
) the integral in (

) fulfills

(3.26)

\mathcal{P}\left(\left|\max\limits_{b\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{\int_{0}^{t}\frac{H-\frac{1}{2}}{C_{H}}(t-s)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}W_{b}(s)\dif{s}}{\int_{0}^{t}\frac{H-\frac{1}{2}}{C_{H}}(t-s)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{2}s\dif{s}}-1\right|>\varepsilon\right)\rightarrow 0\mbox{ as }t\rightarrow\infty.

Substituting
u=\tfrac{s}{t}
gives

\displaystyle\frac{H-\frac{1}{2}}{C_{H}}\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}\sqrt{2}s\dif{s}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{H-\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{H-\frac{1}{2}}{C_{H}}\int_{0}^{1}u(1-u)^{H-3/2}\dif{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{H+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{H-\frac{1}{2}}{C_{H}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-u)u^{H-3/2}\dif{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{H+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{H-\frac{1}{2}}{C_{H}}\left[\int_{0}^{1}u^{H-3/2}\dif{u}-\int_{0}^{1}u^{H-1/2}\dif{u}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t^{H+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}}{C_{H}}\cdot\frac{1}{(H+\frac{1}{2})},

which gives the desired result.

[
ABK11

can also be used for a weak result in the second order term since it also gives that the maximum path of branching Brownian motion at time

is of order

\int_{0}^{t}(t-s)^{H-\frac{3}{2}}\left(\sqrt{s}\wedge\sqrt{t-s}\right)\dif{s}

this entails that

(3.28)

\limsup\limits_{t\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{P}\left(\frac{\max\limits_{b\in\mathcal{B}}B_{b}(t)-t^{H+\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{2}}{C_{H}}\cdot\frac{1}{(H+\frac{1}{2})}}{t^{H}}>-K\right)\rightarrow 1\mbox{ as }K\rightarrow\infty.

Remark 3.12
.

In
[
MZ16
]

]

behaves like

T\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\sigma(s)\dif{s}-T^{\frac{1}{3}}\cdot 2^{-\frac{1}{3}}\alpha_{1}\int_{0}^{1}\sigma(s)^{\frac{1}{3}}\left|\sigma^{\prime}(s)\right|^{\frac{2}{3}}\dif{s}-\log(T)\cdot\sigma(1),

\displaystyle\rho(s,t,t)

\displaystyle=

for

\sigma^{2}(x)=2H(1-x)^{2H-1}.

be a Brownian motion on

W^{\sigma^{2},T}\left(T\right)+\sqrt{1-C_{\rho}}t^{H}Z\overset{(d)}{=}B^{H}(t)

for

.

\max\limits_{b\in\mathcal{B}}W^{\sigma^{2},T}_{b}\left(T\right)+\sqrt{1-C_{\rho}}t^{H}Z\overset{(d)}{=}\max\limits_{b\in\mathcal{B}}B^{H}(t)_{b}.

This is due to the fact that two branches in
[
MZ16
]
which have split at time

have covariance

.
We conjecture that the results by Maillard and Zeitouni, see

, Theorem 1.1]
, give the second order term of the speed of the maximum of a BFBM even if the function

is not fulfilling the conditions

,

and

. By (

) This suggests that the second order term is of order

. We do not believe that the third order term will be the same as in

Remark 3.13
.

A precursor (and a special case) of the GREM is the REM, Random Energy Model, introduced by Derrida in
[
Der80
]
, continued in
[
Der81
]
.
This is the Gaussian random field
\left(X_{\sigma}\right)_{\sigma=1,\ldots,2^{N}}
for independent
X_{\sigma}
.
An overview and analysis of this can be found in lecture notes by Bolthausen and Sznitman, see
[
BS02
]
.

In
[
Der85

Derrida then introduced the GREM, a REM with multiple levels, which introduced a hierarchical structure.
For example a GREM with two levels is a correlated random field

\left(X_{\sigma}\right)_{\sigma\in\left\{1,\ldots,2^{N/2}\right\}\times\left\{1,\ldots,2^{N/2}\right\}}

with

X_{\sigma}\equiv Y_{\sigma_{1}}^{(1)}+Y_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}^{(2)}

for independent collections of independent random variables

The covariance of the random field then depends on the so called overlap. See

An analysis of the leading order of the maximum and a general overview can be found in

]

[

they studied the corresponding Gibbs measures. This analysis has been extended in recent work of Kistler and Sebastiani

The connection of the GREM to branching random walks, especially branching Brownian motion, got first explored by Arguin, Bovier and Kistler in
[

They were able to analyse the full extremal process, including the maximal particle, the second maximal, and so on. They showed that extremal particles descend from ancestors that split either shortly after zero or just before the observed time.
In

In
[

After this ground breaking work variations of BBM have been studied, for example by Bovier and Hartung in

In more recent work

In Theorem

The GREM and its refinements have also found applications in topics beyond branching random walks, see

Appendix D
Proof of Proposition
3.3

The proof of Proposition
3.3
is similar to the one in
[
IW23
, Proposition 2.1]
.
The idea is based on considering two independent ancestral lines, which means lines that do not coalesce even if they meet. For this two lines we estimate the number of common ancestors and decompose it with respect to their most common ancestor.

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

Assume without loss of generality
j>i
and let
b,\tilde{b}
be two branches with
b\wedge\tilde{b}=s
. Denote by
\tilde{A}_{(b,i)}
and
\tilde{A}_{(\tilde{b},j)}
two independent (non-coalescing) ancestral lines starting in
(b,i)
and in
(\tilde{b},j)
respectively.
Since those can not meet before
sn
we can write

(D.1)

\EW_{\mathfrak{y}}\left[\left|\tilde{A}_{(b,i)}\cap\tilde{A}_{(\tilde{b},j)}\right|\right]=\sum_{r\geq i-sn}q_{r}q_{r+j-i}.

By a decomposition with respect to the most recent common ancestor we can write

(D.2)

\EW_{\mathfrak{y}}\left[\left|\tilde{A}_{(b,i)}\cap\tilde{A}_{(\tilde{b},j)}\right|\right]=\WS_{\mathfrak{y}}\left((b,i)\sim(\tilde{b},j)\right)\sum_{r\geq 0}q_{r}^{2}.

Giving us

(D.3)

\WS_{\mathfrak{y}}\left((b,i)\sim(\tilde{b},j)\right)=C_{2}\sum_{r\geq(i\wedge j)-sn}q_{r}q_{r+|j-i|}=C_{2}\sum_{r\geq 0}q_{i-sn+r}q_{j-sn+r}.

∎
