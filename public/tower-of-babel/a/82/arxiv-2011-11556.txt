Towards a kinetic theory of a dark soliton gas in one-dimensional superfluids
Clara Pereira, João D. Rodrigues, José T. Mendonça, Hugo Terças

Abstract
Soliton hydrodynamics is an appealing tool to describe strong turbulence in low-dimensional systems. Strong turbulence in quasi-one dimensional spuerfluids, such as Bose-Einstein condensates, involves the dynamics of dark solitons and, therefore, the description of a statistical ensemble of dark-solitons, i.e. soliton gases, is necessary. In this work, we propose a phase-space (kinetic) description of dark-soliton gases, introducing a kinetic equation that is formally similar to the Vlasov equation in plasma physics. We show that the proposed kinetic theory can capture the dynamical features of soliton gases and show that it sustains an acoustic mode, a fact that we corroborate with the help of direct numerical simulations. Our findings motivate the investigation of the microscopic structure of out-of-equilibrium and turbulent regimes in low-dimensional superfluids.



I
Introduction

Unlike its classical analogue, which finds a comprehensive model in the Navier-Stokes equation, quantum turbulence (QT) does not fin in a unified and comprehensive description
Barenghi et al. 2014a
. The difficulties of establishing a suitable framework are rooted not only on the coexistence of normal and superfluid phases, but most relevantly on the topological nature of turbulent structures: while in classical systems they assume arbitrary shapes and sizes, with lengths spanning over several orders of magnitude, in the quantum regime vorticity is quantised
Alperin et al. 2019
;
Barenghi et al. 2014a
. As such, QT exhibits vortex tangling, as envisioned by Feynman, resulting in a vorticity distribution that is quite distinct from the continuous vorticity present in classical fluids, making the dynamics of QT rather complicated
Skrbek 2011
. Both theoretical and experimental methods have been developed to produce and investigate vortex tangling in Bose-Einstein condensates (BEC)
Yukalov et al. 2016
;
Castilho et al. 2019
. The investigation of the statistical properties of such systems has revealed that vortex tangling can, under certain circumstances, be at the origin of “classicality”, such as the Kolmogorov scaling of the energy spectrum
Barenghi et al. 2014b
.

The features of quantum turbulence change significantly due to dimensional constraints. In two-dimensional (2D) systems, QT shares with classical turbulence an intriguing feature: the so-called inverse cascade, where energy flows backwards from smaller to larger scales, in opposition to what is dictated by Kolmogorov cascade. A consequence of the inverse cascade is the vortex clustering observed in recent experiments
Gauthier et al. 2019
;
Johnstone et al. 2019
;
Madeira et al. 2020
;
Griffin et al. 2020
;
Groszek et al. 2020
, as first predicted in Onsager’s theory
Onsager 1949
;
Simula et al. 2014
. The situation is even more drastic in one-dimensional (1D) systems, since quantum fluctuations may play quite a significant role
Giamarchi and Press 2004
;
Dziarmaga and Sacha 2006
;
Edler et al. 2017
. Moreover, angular momentum quantization is not possible in one-dimensional systems, and the role of vortices is played by dark solitons (DS), the topological defects created by a phase jump in the order parameter
Frantzeskakis 2010
;
Ku et al. 2016
. Another interesting aspect of dark-solitons is the fact of being fermionic
Sato et al. 2012
;
Karpiuk et al. 2015
, being intimately related to the type-II excitations on top of a one-dimensional Bose gas as predicted by the Lieb-Liniger theory
Lieb 1963
. Moreover, the concept of “solitonic turbulence” is also present in some conditions of strong turbulence in classical systems
Hassaini and Mordant 2017
;
Cazaubiel et al. 2018
, which increases the interest around the development of statistical methods for solitons. Previous studies in 1D QT indeed exist, but are mostly (if not exclusively) performed in the weak turbulence regime
Nazarenko et al. 2001
. Similarly to what happens in 2D, Zakharov’s weak (or wave–wave) turbulence theory reveals the occurrence of inverse cascades in 1D
Connaughton et al. 2005
;
Sun et al. 2012
, but it is still not clear what is supposed to happen in a strong turbulence situation. Can we expect to observe the same sort of inverse cascades for dark solitons in 1D? If yes, how will their fermionic statistics work
Terças et al. 2013
;
Terças et al. 2014
?

With the aim of understanding the microscopic processes leading to strong turbulence in 1D superfluids, we will develop a kinetic theory of dark-soliton gases based on the Klimontovich approach
Schram 1991
;
Dufty and Bonitz 2005
, well-established in the context of plasma physics but recently applied to atomic systems
Mendonça and Serbêto 2020
. In integrable systems, multiple-soliton solutions, consisting of well-ordered soliton lattices, exist and are described by the inverse scattering transform method
Gardner et al. 1967
. In the case of randomly distributed solitons, a statistical description in terms of distribution functions is more desirable
El and Kamchatnov 2005
;
Bulchandani et al. 2019
. We start by reducing DS to particle-like objects of effective negative mass, and determine the Hamiltonian and the corresponding canonical structure for a dark-soliton pair. Then, we construct a distribution function describing a collection of DS in the phase space, and obtain the corresponding transport equation. Finally, by performing ensemble averages, we are able to obtain the mean-field dynamics of a DS gas and compute its excitation spectrum. We find that a gas of dark-solitons sustains a collective excitation, which is acoustic-like (massless) in the long-wavelength limit, in agreement with the Bethe ansatz solution of the Lieb-Liniger model. Our results open a venue towards a theoretical framework able to capture the spectral properties of strong turbulence in 1D systems.

II
Dark-soliton Hamiltonian

We start by considering a homogeneous, one-dimensional superfluid at zero temperature, which is governed by the Gross-Pitaevskii equation
(
Pitaevskii and Stringari 2003
)

i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi(x,t)}{\partial t}=\left(-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}+g|\Psi(x,t)|^{2}\right)\Psi(x,t),

(1)

with
\displaystyle\Psi(x,t)
being the superfluid order parameter, associated to the following Hamiltonian density

\mathcal{H}\{\Psi\}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left|\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right|^{2}+\frac{g}{2}\left|\Psi\right|^{4}.

(2)

Dark solitons constitute exact solutions to Eq. (
1
) parametrised by
\displaystyle s
and
\displaystyle v
, standing for the centroid position and velocity, respectively,
\displaystyle\Psi(x,t;s,v)=e^{-i\mu t/\hbar}\psi_{0}[x;s,v]
, where
\displaystyle\mu=gn_{0}
is the chemical potential, and
Pitaevskii and Stringari 2003

\psi_{0}[x;s,v]=\sqrt{n_{0}}\left[i\beta+\gamma^{-1}\tanh\left(\frac{x-s}{\gamma\xi}\right)\right].

(3)

Here,
\displaystyle\beta=v/c
,
\displaystyle\gamma=(1-\beta^{2})^{-1/2}
,
\displaystyle c=\sqrt{gn_{0}/m}
is the sound speed and
\displaystyle\xi=\hbar/\sqrt{gmn_{0}}
is the healing length. Because of the translational invariance of the solution, the soliton Hamiltonian is a function of the DS velocity only,

H(v)=\int\left(\mathcal{H}\{\Psi_{0}\}-\mu n_{0}\right)dx=\frac{2}{3}mc^{2}n_{0}\xi\left(1-\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}\right)^{3/2}.

(4)

For small velocities,
\displaystyle v\ll c
,
\displaystyle H(v)\simeq|M_{*}|c^{2}+M_{*}v^{2}/2
, where
\displaystyle M_{*}=-2mn_{0}\xi
is the effective mass of the soliton
(
Kivshar and Luther-Davies 1998
)
. Therefore, Eq. (
4
) suggests that DS may be regarded as relativistic hole-like particles, with
\displaystyle c
playing here the role of the speed of light. The canonical momentum may be obtained via the relation
\displaystyle v=\partial_{p}H(v)
, which can be readily inverted to provide

p=\int_{0}^{v}\frac{1}{u}\frac{\partial H(u)}{\partial u}du=M_{*}c\left(\frac{\beta}{\gamma}+\delta\right),

(5)

with
\displaystyle\delta=\arcsin(\beta)
being the phase jump in the order parameter from
\displaystyle x-s=-\infty
to
\displaystyle x-s=+\infty
. This shows that the parameter
\displaystyle v
is, indeed, the kinematic DS velocity,
\displaystyle v=\dot{s}
, since
\displaystyle s
and
\displaystyle p
constitute a canonical pair obeying the Poisson bracket
\displaystyle\{s,p\}=1
. As such, Eq. (
5
) allows us to change the functional dependence on the Hamiltonian,
\displaystyle H(v)\to H(p).

With the establishment of the canonical equations for a dark soliton, we are now in position to generalize to a set of
\displaystyle N
solitons. In order to avoid phase singularities within the order parameter, we consider the DS gas to be composed of an array kink
\displaystyle-
anti
\displaystyle-
kink pairs

\psi_{\rm gas}[\{s_{j};v_{j}\}]=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\psi_{0}[x_{j},(-1)^{j}v_{j}].

(6)

We can repeat the procedure of the single DS to formally obtain the equations of motion,

\dot{s}_{j}=\frac{\partial H_{\rm gas}}{\partial p_{j}}=v_{j},\quad\quad\dot{p}_{j}=-\frac{\partial H_{\rm gas}}{\partial s_{j}},

(7)

with
\displaystyle H_{\rm gas}
obtained from Eq. (
4
) as

H_{\rm gas}(s_{j},p_{j})=\sum_{k\neq j}H(s_{k},p_{k}).

(8)

Notice that the Hamiltonian is now a function of the soltion positions
\displaystyle s_{j}
and their momenta
\displaystyle p_{j}
, as a consequence of the breakdown of translational invariance for the case of randomly distributed soltions. Of course, this results in
\displaystyle 2N
coupled equations, which are of little use. Crucially, Eq. (
7
) now encodes the motion that a single soliton undergoes due to its interaction with all the others. In what follows, we implement Klimontovich procedure based on the dynamics of Eq. (
7
).

III
Microscopic phase-space distribution function

In order to construct a statistical description of DS gases, we define the phase-space distribution function
of the canonical variables
\displaystyle s
and
\displaystyle p
as
Nicholson 1983
;
Stix 1992

\rho(s,p,t)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta(s-s_{j}(t))\delta(p-p_{j}(t)),

(9)

satisfying the following relation with the total number of solitons in the gas

N=\iint\rho(s,p,t)~dsdp.

(10)

Computing the time derivative, we have

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\dot{s}_{j}\frac{\partial\delta(s-s_{j})}{\partial s_{j}}\delta(p-p_{j})+\dot{p}_{j}\frac{\partial\delta(p-p_{j})}{\partial p_{j}}\delta(s-s_{j}).

(11)

By using the property
\displaystyle\partial_{x}\delta(x-y)=\partial_{y}\delta(y-x)
, we can obtain

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+\dot{s}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial s}+\dot{p}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial p}=0,

(12)

which means that the DS phase-space distribution function may be regarded as an incompressible fluid,
\displaystyle\dot{\rho}=0
, in agreement with Liouville’s theorem. The key point now is to understand that we can go from the discrete dynamics to a continuous description in the phase space by making use of
\displaystyle\rho(s,p,t)
, and write Eq. (
7
) as
\displaystyle\dot{p}=-\partial_{s}V
, where
\displaystyle V
is obtained from Eq. (
8
) as

V(s,p,t)=\iint H_{\rm gas}(s-s^{\prime},p-p^{\prime})\rho(s^{\prime},p^{\prime},t)ds^{\prime}dp^{\prime}.

(13)

Notice that Eq. (
13
) defines a pseudo-potential depending on both
\displaystyle s
and
\displaystyle p
(or
\displaystyle v
), which is a consequence of the relativistic nature of DS: their mass depends on their velocity. Together with Eq. (
5
), establishing a relation between
\displaystyle p
and
\displaystyle v=\dot{s}
, we can recast the Klimontovich equation as

\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}+v\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial s}-\frac{\partial V}{\partial s}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial p}=0.

(14)

This approach is formally equivalent to the single-particle Liouville equation. It is very useful for numerical simulations
Birdsall et al. 1985
;
Pang and O 1997
;
Surendra and Graves 1991
, but quite complicated to handle analytically. To describe the mean-field behaviour of DS gases, we introduce ensemble averages
\displaystyle f\equiv\langle\rho\rangle
and
\displaystyle\langle V\rangle
and the corresponding deviations as

\delta\rho=\rho-f,\quad\quad\delta V=V-\langle V\rangle,

(15)

with
\displaystyle\langle V\rangle
obtained from Eq. (
13
) via the replacement
\displaystyle\rho\to f
. Inserting in Eq. (
14
), we obtain a kinetic equation for the smooth distribution function
\displaystyle f(s,p,t)
,

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+v\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\right)f-\frac{\partial\langle V\rangle}{\partial s}\frac{\partial f}{\partial p}=\Big\langle\frac{\partial\delta V}{\partial s}\frac{\partial\delta\rho}{\partial p}\Big\rangle.

(16)

The r.h.s of the latter defines the collision integral, which depends on the details of the short-range nature of DS collisions. It can be constructed, at different levels of approximations, by making use of the BBGKY hierarchy
Nicholson 1983
, thus yielding different kinetic equations. In what follows, we consider dilute soliton gases,
\displaystyle N_{0}\xi\ll 1
, where
\displaystyle N_{0}=1/\langle s\rangle
is the gas density determined by the averaged soliton separation
\displaystyle\langle s\rangle
. In that regime, we neglect the correlations between the multi-particle distributions and set the collision integral in Eq. (
16
) to zero. As such, we obtain the collisionless kinetic equation for the single-particle distribution function

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+v\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\right)f-\frac{\partial\langle V\rangle}{\partial s}\frac{\partial f}{\partial p}=0.

(17)

The effective pairwise pseudo-potential as a function of the soliton separation for
\displaystyle v=0
(solid line) and
\displaystyle v=0.5c
(dashed line).

The latter is formally equivalent to the Vlasov equation widely used to describe fully ionised plasmas
Stix 1992
;
Vlasov 1961
. The Vlasov equation has also been employed to describe photon-quasiparticles, where long-range interactions are absent
Mendonça 2014
. As we are about to see, the important difference stems from the fact that the mean-field soliton-soliton interaction is short-ranged, contrary to the case of electrons and ions in plasmas interacting through the Coulomb potencial.

In order to model the interaction potential in Eq. (
17
), we consider that DS interact via an averaged two-body potential. This is justified since dark solitons are localised objects of size
\displaystyle\sim\xi
, making their interaction to be finite-ranged
Frantzeskakis 2010
. As such, the two-body pseudo-potential is obtained from Eqs. (
6
) and (
8
) by setting
\displaystyle N=2
, and therefore defining
\displaystyle H_{\rm gas}\simeq H_{\rm pair}\equiv H(s,v)
, where

H(s,v)\simeq H_{\rm kin}(s,v)+H_{\rm int}(s,v)-\frac{8}{3}|M_{*}|c^{2}\left(1-\frac{v^{2}}{c^{2}}\right)^{-3/2}.

(18)

Here,

\begin{array}[]{ccl}H_{\rm kin}(s,v)&=&-\frac{1}{3}\frac{gn_{0}^{2}\xi}{\gamma^{3/2}}\left[2\beta^{2}-\cosh\left(\zeta\right)-1\right]\\
\\
&\times&\text{csch}\left(\zeta\right)\left[\cosh\left(2\zeta\right)-6\zeta\coth\left(\zeta\right)+5\right]\end{array}

(19)

is the contribution from the kinetic term in Eq. (
2
) and

\begin{array}[]{ccl}H_{\rm int}(s,v)&=&\frac{1}{48}\frac{gn_{0}^{2}\xi}{\gamma^{3/2}}\text{csch}^{7}(\zeta)\left[\left(16\beta^{2}-35\right)\sinh\left(5\zeta\right)\right.\\
\\
&+&\left.\sinh\left(7\zeta\right)+12\zeta\cosh\left(5\zeta\right)\right.\\
\\
&+&\left.\left(-432\beta^{4}+448\beta^{2}-171\right)\sinh\left(\zeta\right)\right.\\
\\
&+&\left.\left(-176\beta^{4}+464\beta^{2}-207\right)\sinh\left(3\zeta\right)\right.\\
\\
&+&\left.24\left(36\beta^{4}-64\beta^{2}+29\right)\zeta\cosh\left(\zeta\right)\right.\\
\\
&+&\left.12\left(8\beta^{4}-32\beta^{2}+21\right)\zeta\cosh\left(3\zeta\right)\right],\end{array}

(20)

results from the interaction (non-linear) term, where
\displaystyle\zeta=s/(\gamma\xi)
,
\displaystyle s
is the soliton separation and
\displaystyle v=(v_{1}+v_{2})/2
is the average velocity of the soliton pair. The third term in Eq. (
18
) corresponds to twice the energy of a single soliton,
\displaystyle s\to\infty
, and does not contribute to the force term in the Vlasov equation.
As it can be seen in Fig. (
1
), the pairwise DS potential is attractive. However, since the DS mass is negative (and hence the reduced mass of the DS pair), the resulting interaction is repulsive. Moreover, we can observe that the potential has a range of order
\displaystyle\sim\xi
, and becomes weaker for more relativistic (less massive, in modulus) solitons.

IV
Sound modes of the dark-soliton gas

In order to illustrate some of the features of the transport equation, we start by considering small amplitude perturbations around a certain equilibrium configuration,

f\simeq f_{0}+\tilde{f}_{1},\quad{\rm with}\quad f_{1}\ll f_{0}.

(21)

Inserting this into Eq. (
17
), we obtain the linearised Vlasov equation

\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}+v\frac{\partial}{\partial s}\right)f_{1}-\frac{\partial\langle V_{1}\rangle}{\partial s}\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial p}=0,

(22)

where
\displaystyle\langle V_{1}(s,p)\rangle=\int H(s-s^{\prime},p-p^{\prime})f_{1}(s^{\prime},p^{\prime})ds^{\prime}dp^{\prime}
with
\displaystyle H
being given by Eq. (
18
). Upon Fourier transforming the latter (i.e. by making
\displaystyle f_{1}(s,p,t)=\sum_{k,\omega}\tilde{f}_{1}(k,p,\omega)e^{i(ks-\omega t)}
, we formally obtain the kinetic dispersion relation of the DS gas as

1=k\int\frac{\tilde{H}(k,v)}{(\omega-kv)}\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial v}\frac{\partial v}{\partial p}~dv,

(23)

where
\displaystyle\tilde{H}(k,v)
is the spatial Fourier transform of Eq. (
18
). The Jacobian
\displaystyle\partial v/\partial p
allows us to eliminate
\displaystyle p
and corresponds to a generalised mass term that can be determined with the help of Eq. (
5
). The dispersion relation can be numerically solved for generic equilibrium configurations, accounting for i) the relativistic nature of dark solitons, ii) the velocity-dependence of their pairwise interaction, and iii) the negativity of their mass (“hole-like” nature). A particularly interesting and analytically tractable case is that of a non-relativistic DS gas, distributed such that
\displaystyle v\ll c
. In that case, we can set
\displaystyle H(k,v)\simeq\tilde{H}(k,0)
and
\displaystyle\partial p/\partial v\simeq-1/|M_{*}|
, which yields

1\simeq-\frac{k\tilde{H}(k,0)}{|M_{*}|}\int\frac{1}{(\omega-kv)}\frac{\partial f_{0}}{\partial v}~dv,

(24)

where

\tilde{H}(k,0)\simeq-\frac{1}{2}|M_{*}|c_{s}^{2}\xi\left(\frac{28}{9}+(7\pi^{2}-15)k^{2}\xi^{2}\right)+\mathcal{O}(k^{4}).

(25)

As we can immediately see, the negative signs in Eqs. (
24
) and (
25
) cancel, thus confirming that the nature of the DS interaction is, indeed, repulsive as earlier stated.

We consider fluctuations on top of homogeneous soliton gases,
\displaystyle f_{0}(s,v)=N_{0}g_{0}(v)
. An interesting situation corresponds to that of a cold dark-soliton gas, for which solitons are prepared at rest, distributed in velocity as
\displaystyle g_{0}(v)=\delta(v)
. Although this distribution is correct for classical particles, it may not be generally accurate to describe solitons. The reason stems from the fact that solitons are fermions, as one can immediately see from Eq. (
6
) for a two-DS wavefunction
Terças et al. 2013
,

\psi_{2}(x_{2},x_{1};0,0)=-\psi_{2}(x_{1},x_{2};0,0),

(26)

in agreement with Lieb-Liniger theory
Sato et al. 2012
;
Karpiuk et al. 2015
. The fermionisation of the DS gas must therefore be included in the equilibrium at a semi-classical level. For that task, we make use of the distribution function

g_{0}(v)=\frac{1}{2v_{F}}\Theta(v_{F}-|v|),

(27)

where
\displaystyle v_{F}=\pi\hbar N_{0}/|M_{*}|
is the 1D Fermi velocity of the DS gas. As a matter of fact, the later reduces to the Dirac-delta distributed gas in the limit
\displaystyle v_{F}\to 0
, i.e. for sufficiently diluted DS gases. The dispersion relation in (
24
) thus provides

\omega^{2}=v_{F}^{2}k^{2}+\frac{N_{0}k^{2}\tilde{H}(k,0)}{M_{*}}

(28)

In the long-wavelength limit
\displaystyle k\xi\to 0
, this corresponds to a
solitonic first sound
,
\displaystyle\omega\simeq v_{1}k
, where

v_{1}=c\sqrt{\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\Gamma^{2}+\frac{14}{9\pi}\Gamma}

(29)

is the first sound speed and
\displaystyle\Gamma=N_{0}\xi
is the DS gas dilution parameter. In the validity range of Eq. (
17
),
\displaystyle\Gamma\ll 1
,
\displaystyle v_{1}/c\simeq 0.703\sqrt{\Gamma}
, meaning that the effects of the Fermi pressure are not relevant for very dilute gases. Indeed, the effect of the fermionisation becomes relevant for densities of the order
\displaystyle\Gamma\gtrsim(56/(9\pi^{3}))\simeq 0.203
.

\displaystyle N_{0}\sim\xi

\displaystyle v\sim c

An additional feature can be observed in Fig. (

): the emergence of an energetic mode
\displaystyle\omega\simeq v_{2}k
, with
\displaystyle v_{2}>c
. This mode does not correspond to the low-lying (Bogoliubov) excitations on top of the superfluid - a fact that we verified numerically - and is certainly not that of a soliton gas (for which the slope is
\displaystyle v_{1}\ll c
, as discussed above). At this stage, we understand this mode to be a consequence of the hybridization between the Bogoliubov (fast) and the first-sound (slow) modes, as a result of the interactions between solitons and phonons. Although a microscopic theory remains to be developed, we can anticipate that our kinetic theory may be coupled to a Wigner-Moyal description of phonons
Mendonça and Terças 2012
. The resulting problem will consist on the dynamics under the effect of a ponderomotive force due to the modulations of the superfluid density by the phonons
Cary and Kaufman 1981
;
Shukla et al. 1986
.

V
Conclusions

We have established the foundations of a kinetic theory of dark soliton gases in one-dimensional superfluids based on the Klimontovich approach. By considering that dark solitons behave as particles of negative mass, and assuming that they interact via an ensemble averaged pairwise potential, we defined a kinetic equation governing the phase-space distribution function of an array of dark solitons, i.e. a dark soliton gas. Our approach allows us to describe solitons statistically in analytical grounds, a feature that is virtually impossible with studies based on the Gross-Pitaevskii equation. One important feature of the soliton gas is that it supports a first sound mode, which is much less energetic than the phonon modes on top of the condensate. This as consequence of solitons behaving as weakly interacting particles of negative mass. We believe to be setting the stage towards a more comprehensive, microscopic description of solitonic turbulence, an aspect of central importance when dealing with strong turbulence regimes in one-dimensional superfluids. In a near future, with the help of a hydrodynamic model that can be directly obtained from our kinetic equation, we believe to be able to describe solitonic turbulence as a Kolmogorov theory of weak turbulence of dark soliton gases. If successful, such a description will unveil important mechanisms underlying the spectral properties of strong quantum turbulence in low-dimensional superfluids.
