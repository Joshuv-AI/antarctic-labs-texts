Abstract
. We consider the minimum spanning tree problem on a weighted complete bipartite graph K n R , n B K_{n_{R},n_{B}} whose n = n R + n B n=n_{R}+n_{B} vertices are random, i.i.d. uniformly distributed points in the unit cube in d d dimensions and edge weights are the p p -th power of their Euclidean distance, with p > 0 p>0 . In the large n n limit with n R / n → α R n_{R}/n\to\alpha_{R} and 0 < α R < 1 0<\alpha_{R}<1 , we show that the maximum vertex degree of the tree grows logarithmically, in contrast with the classical, non-bipartite, case, where a uniform bound holds depending on d d only. Despite this difference, for p < d p<d , we are able to prove that the total edge costs normalized by the rate n 1 − p / d n^{1-p/d} converge to a limiting constant that can be represented as a series of integrals, thus extending a classical result of Avram and Bertsimas to the bipartite case and confirming a conjecture of Riva, Caracciolo and Malatesta.



1.
Introduction

The minimum spanning tree (MST) problem ranks among the simplest Combinatorial Optimization problems, with many applications, well beyond its historical introduction for network design
[
1
]
including approximation algorithms for more complex problems
[
2
,
3
]
and cluster analysis
[
4
]
.

Its formulation is straightforward: given a weighted undirected graph
G=(V,E,w)
with
w:E\to(0,\infty)
, find a sub-graph
T\subseteq E
that connects all nodes
V
and has a minimal total edge cost

\sum_{e\in T}w(e),

thus defining the MST cost functional
\mathcal{C}_{MST}(G)
. Minimality yields that redundant connections can be discarded, so that the resulting sub-graph
T
turns out to be a tree, i.e., connected and without cycles. Several algorithms have been proposed for its solution, from classical greedy to more efficient ones
[
5
]
, possibly randomized
[
6
]
.

Despite its apparent simplicity, a probabilistic analysis of the problem, i.e., assuming that weights are random variables with a given joint law and studying the resulting random costs and MST’s yields interesting results. Moreover, it may suggest mathematical tools to deal with more complex problems, such as the Steiner tree problem or the travelling salesperson problem, where one searches instead for a cycle connecting all points having minimum total edge weight.

The most investigated random model is surely that of i.i.d. weights with a regular density, as first studied by Frieze
[
7
]
, who showed in particular the following law of large numbers: if
G^{n}=(V^{n},E^{n},w^{n})
, with
(V^{n},E^{n})=K_{n}
the complete graph over
n
nodes and
w^{n}=\left(w_{ij}\right)_{i,j=1}^{n}
are independent and uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
, then almost surely

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{C}_{MST}(G^{n})=\zeta(3)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{3}}.

(1.1)

Another well studied setting is provided by Euclidean models, where nodes are i.i.d. sampled points in a region (say uniformly on a cube
[0,1]^{d}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for simplicity) and edge weights are functions of their distance, e.g.
w(x,y)=|x-y|^{p}
for some parameter
p>0
. This setting dates back at least to the seminal paper by Beardwood, Halton and Hammersley
[
8
]
where they focused on the travelling salesperson problem, but stated that other problems may be as well considered, including the MST one. A full analysis was later performed by Steele
[
9
]
who proved that, if the Euclidean graph consists of
n
nodes, then for every
0<p<d
, almost sure convergence holds

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{C}_{MST}(G^{n})}{n^{1-p/d}}=\beta_{MST}(p,d),

(1.2)

where
\beta_{MST}(p,d)\in(0,\infty)
is a constant. The rate
n^{1-p/d}
is intuitively clear due to the fact that there are
n-1
edges a tree over
n
points and the typical distance between two adjacent points is expected to be of order
n^{-1/d}
. The constraint
p<d
was removed by Aldous and Steele
[
10
]
and Yukich
[
11
]
, so that convergence holds in fact for any
p>0
. This result can be seen as an application of a general Euclidean additive functional theory
[
12
,
13
]
. However, such general methods that work for other combinatorial optimization problems give not much insight on the precise value of the limit constant
\beta_{MST}(p,d)
. The MST problem is known to be exceptional, for a (sort of) explicit series representation, analogue to (
1.1
), was obtained by Avram and Bertismas
[
14
]
, although only in the range
0<p<d
. The latter was used by Penrose
[
15
]
, in connection with continuum percolation, to study, among other things, the MST in the high dimensional regime
d\to\infty
. An alternative approach towards explicit formulas was proposed by Steele
[
16
]
, but limited to the case of i.i.d. weights, based on Tutte polynomials.

Aim of this paper is to investigate analogous results for
bipartite
Euclidean random models, i.e., when nodes correspond to two distinct families of sampled points (e.g., visually rendered by red/blue colourings) and weights, still given by a power of the distance, are only defined between points with different colours. Formally, we replace the underlying complete graph
K_{n}
with a complete bipartite graph
K_{n_{R},n_{B}}
with
n_{R}+n_{B}=n
.

A similar question was formulated and essentially solved in the model with independent weights by Frieze and McDiarmid
[
17
]
. In Euclidean models, however, it is known that such innocent looking variant may in fact cause quantitative differences in the corresponding asymptotic results. For example, in the Euclidean bipartite travelling salesperson problem with
d=1
and
d=2
, the correct asymptotic rates (for
p=1
) are known to be respectively
\sqrt{n}

[
18
]
and
\sqrt{n\log n}

[
19
]
, larger than the natural
n^{1-1/d}
for the non-bipartite problem. Similar results are known for other problems, such as the minimum matching problem
[
12
]
and its bipartite counterpart, also related to the optimal transport problem
[
20
,
21
,
22
,
23
,
24
]
. Barthe and Bordenave proposed a bipartite extension of the Euclidean additive functional theory
[
25
]
that allows to recover an analogue of (
1.2
) for many relevant combinatorial optimization problems on bipartite Euclidean random models, although its range of applicability is restricted to
0<p<d/2
(the cases
p=1
,
d\in\left\{1,2\right\}
are indeed outside this range) and anyway the MST problem does not fit in the theory. The main reason for the latter limitation is that there is no uniform bound on the maximum degree of a MST on a bipartite Euclidean random graph – their theory instead applies to a variant of the problem where a uniform bound on the maximum degree is imposed, which is in fact algorithmically more complex (if the bound is two it recovers essentially the travelling salesperson problem).

Main results

Our first main result describes precisely the asymptotic maximum degree of a MST on a bipartite Euclidean random graph, showing that it grows logarithmically in the total number of nodes, in the asymptotic regime where a fraction of points
\alpha_{R}\in(0,1)
is red and the remaining
\alpha_{B}=1-\alpha_{R}
is blue.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
d\geq 1
, let
n\geq 1
and
R^{n}=\left(X_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n_{R}}
,
B^{n}=\left(Y_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n_{B}}
be (jointly) i.i.d. uniformly distributed on
[0,1]^{d}
with
n_{R}+n_{B}=n
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n_{R}}{n}=\alpha_{R}\in(0,1),\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n_{B}}{n}=\alpha_{B}=1-\alpha_{R}.

Let
T^{n}
denote the MST over the complete bipartite graph with independent sets
R^{n}
,
B^{n}
and weights
w(X_{i},Y_{j})=|X_{i}-Y_{j}|
, and let
\Delta(T^{n})
denote its maximum vertex degree. Then, there exists a constant
C=C(d,\alpha_{R})>0
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}P\left(C^{-1}<\frac{\Delta(T^{n})}{\log(n)}<C\right)=1.

(Indeed, the structure of the MST does not depend on the specific choice of the exponent
p>0
, so we simply let
p=1
above). The proof is detailed in Section
3
.

Our second main result shows that, although the general theory of Barthe and Bordenave does not apply and the maximum degree indeed grows, the total weight cost for the bipartite Euclidean MST problem turns out to be much closer to the non-bipartite one, since no exceptional rates appear in low dimensions. Before we give the complete statement, let us introduce the following quantity, for
d\geq 1
,
k_{R}
,
k_{B}\geq 1
,
\alpha_{R}\in(0,1)
,

\begin{split}E(k_{R},k_{B},\alpha_{R})=&\int_{\Theta(k_{R},k_{B})}\left(\alpha_{R}|D(\left\{b_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{k_{B}})|+\alpha_{B}|D(\left\{r_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{k_{R}})|\right)^{-(k_{R}+k_{B})/d}\cdot\\
&\quad\cdot\left(\frac{k_{R}}{\alpha_{R}}\delta_{0}(r_{1})db_{1}+\frac{k_{B}}{\alpha_{B}}dr_{1}\delta_{0}(b_{1})\right)dr_{2}\ldots dr_{k_{R}}db_{2}\ldots db_{k_{B}},\end{split}

where
\alpha_{B}=1-\alpha_{R}
and we write

\Theta(k_{R},k_{B})\subseteq(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k_{R}}\times(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{k_{B}},

(1.3)

for the set of (ordered) points
(\left(r_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{k_{R}},\left(b_{j}\right)_{j=1}^{k_{B}})
such that, in the associated Euclidean bipartite graph with weights
\left(|r_{i}-b_{j}|\right)_{i,j}
, the subgraph with all edges having weight less than
1
is connected (or equivalently, there exists a bipartite Euclidean spanning tree having all edges with length weight less than
1
), and for a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we write

D(A)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,:\,\operatorname{dist}(A,x)\leq 1\right\},

(1.4)

and
|D(A)|
for its Lebesgue measure. Notice also that the overall integration is performed with respect to Lebesgue measure over
k_{R}+k_{B}-1
variables in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and one (either
r_{1}
or
b_{1}
) is instead with respect to a Dirac measure at
0
.

These quantities enter in the explicit formula for the limit constant in the bipartite analogue of (
1.2
), as our second main result shows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
d\geq 1
, let
n\geq 1
and
R^{n}=\left(X_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n_{R}}
,
B^{n}=\left(Y_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n_{B}}
be (jointly) i.i.d. uniformly distributed on
[0,1]^{d}
with
n_{R}+n_{B}=n
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n_{R}}{n}=\alpha_{R}\in(0,1),\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n_{B}}{n}=\alpha_{B}=1-\alpha_{R}.

Let
T^{n}
denote the MST over the complete bipartite graph with independent sets
R^{n}
,
B^{n}
and weights
w(X_{i},Y_{j})=|X_{i}-Y_{j}|
. Then, for every
p\in(0,d)
, the following convergence holds

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\left\{X_{i},Y_{j}\right\}\in T^{n}}|X_{i}-Y_{j}|^{p}\right]}{n^{1-p/d}}=\beta_{bMST}(d,p),

(1.5)

and the constant
\beta_{bMST}(d,p)\in(0,\infty)
is given by the series

\beta_{bMST}(d,p)=\frac{p}{d}\sum_{k_{R},k_{B}=1}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha_{R}^{k_{R}}}{k_{R}!}\frac{\alpha_{B}^{k_{B}}}{k_{B}!}\frac{\Gamma((k_{R}+k_{B})/d)}{k_{B}+k_{R}}E(k_{R},k_{B},\alpha_{R}).

(1.6)

Moreover, if
p<d/2
for
d\in\left\{1,2\right\}
or
p<d
for
d\geq 3
, convergence is almost sure:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{\left\{X_{i},Y_{j}\right\}\in T^{n}}|X_{i}-Y_{j}|^{p}}{n^{1-p/d}}=\beta_{bMST}(d,p).

The proof is detailed in Section
4
. The one-dimensional random bipartite Euclidean MST has been recently theoretically investigated in the statistical physics literature by Riva, Caracciolo and Malatesta
[
26
]
, together with extensive numerical simulations also in higher dimensions, hinting at the possibility of a non-exceptional rate
n^{1-p/d}
also for
d=2
. In particular, our result confirms this asymptotic rate in the two dimensional case, with a.s. convergence if
p<1
and just convergence of the expected costs if
1\leq p<2
– in fact we also have a general upper bound if
p\geq 2
(Lemma
4.5
).

Further questions and conjectures

Several extensions of the results contained in this work may be devised, for example by generalizing to
k
-partite models or more general block models, allowing for weights between the same coloured points but possibly with a different function, e.g. the same power of the distance function, but multiplied by a different pre-factor according to pair of blocks. An interesting question, also open for the non-bipartite case, is to extend the series representation for the limiting constant to the case
p\geq d
. On the other side, we suspect that additivity techniques may yield convergence in (
1.5
) also in the range
p\geq d
, without an explicit series, but we leave it for future explorations. A further question, that has no counterpart in the non-bipartite case, is what happens if the laws of different coloured points are different, say with densities
f_{R}
and
f_{B}
that are regular, uniformly positive and bounded. Assuming that
n_{R}/n\to 1/2
, a natural conjecture is that the limit holds with (
1.6
) obtained by replacing
\alpha_{R}
and
\alpha_{B}
with the “local” fraction of points
f_{R}(x)/2
,
f_{R}(x)/2
and then integrating with respect to
x\in[0,1]^{d}
, i.e.,

\frac{p}{d}\sum_{k_{R},k_{B}=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{k_{R}+k_{B}}}\int_{[0,1]^{d}}\frac{f_{R}^{k_{R}}}{k_{R}!}\frac{f_{B}^{k_{B}}}{k_{B}!}\frac{\Gamma((k_{R}+k_{B})/d)}{k_{B}+k_{R}}E(k_{R},k_{B},f_{R}/2,f_{B}/2).

(1.7)

Finally, a central limit theorem is known for MST problem
[
27
,
28
]
and it may be interesting to understand the possible role played by the additional fluctuations introduced in bipartite setting for analogue results.

Structure of the paper

In Section
2
we collect useful notation and properties of general MST’s, together with crucial observations in the metric setting (including the Euclidean one) and some useful probabilistic estimates. We try here to keep separate as much as possible probabilistic from deterministic results, to simplify the exposition. In Section
3
we prove Theorem
1.1
and in Section
4
we first extend
[
14
, Theorem 1]
to the bipartite case and then apply it in the Euclidean setting. An intermediate step requires to argue on the flat torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
to exploit further homogeneity. We finally use a concentration result to obtain almost sure convergence: since the vertex degree is not uniformly bounded, the standard inequalities were not sufficient to directly cover the case
p<1
, so we prove a simple variant of McDiarmid inequality in Appendix
A
that we did not find in the literature and may be of independent interest.

2.
Notation and preliminary results

2.1.
Minimum spanning trees

Although our focus is on weighted graphs induced by points in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the following general definition of minimum spanning trees will be useful.

Definition 2.1
.

Given a weighted undirected finite graph
G=(V,E,w)
, with
w:E\to[0,\infty]
, the MST cost functional is defined as

\mathcal{C}_{MST}(G)=\inf\left\{\sum_{e\in T}w(e)\,:\,\text{$T\subseteq E$ is a connected spanning subgraph}\right\},

(2.1)

Here and below, connected is in the sense that only edges with finite weight must be considered. We consider only minimizers
T
in (
2.1
) that are trees, i.e., connected and acyclic, otherwise removing the most expensive edge in a cycle would give a competitor with smaller cost (since we assume possibly null weights, there may be other minimizers). The following lemma is a special case of the cut property of minimum spanning trees, but will play a crucial role in several occasions, so we state it here.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
,
v\in V
and assume that
e\in\operatorname{argmin}\left\{w(f)\,:\,v\in f\right\}
is unique. Then,
e
belongs to every minimum spanning tree of
G
.

Proof.

Assume that
e
does not belong to a minimum spanning tree
T
. Addition of
e
to
T
induces a cycle that includes necessarily another edge
f
, with
v\in f
and by assumption
w(f)>w(e)
. By removing
f
, the cost of the resulting connected graph is strictly smaller that the cost of
T
, a contradiction.
∎

We write
\mathsf{n}_{G}(v)\in V
, or simply
\mathsf{n}(v)
if there are no ambiguities, for the closest node to
v
in
G
, i.e.,

e=\left\{v,\mathsf{n}(v)\right\}\in\operatorname{argmin}_{w\in E}\left\{w(f)\,:\,v\in f\right\},

assuming that such node is unique.

The subgraphs

G(z):=\left\{e\in E\,:\,w(e)\leq z\right\},\quad\text{for $z\geq 0$,}

are strongly related to the minimum spanning tree on
G
, since the execution of Kruskal’s algorithm yields the identity, already observed in
[
14
]
,

\mathcal{C}_{MST}(G)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(C_{G(z)}-1\right)dz,

(2.2)

where we write
C_{G}
denotes the number of connected components of a graph
G
. Indeed, the function
z\mapsto C_{G(z)}
is piecewise constant and decreasing from
|V|
towards
1
(assuming that all weights are strictly positive and
G
is connected). Assume for simplicity that all weights
\left\{w_{e}\right\}_{e\in E}
are different, so that
z\mapsto C_{G(z)}
has only unit jumps, on a set
J_{-}
. An integration by parts gives the identity

\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(C_{G(z)}-1\right)dz=\sum_{z\in J_{-}}z.

To argue that the right hand side is the cost of a MST, e.g., obtained by Kruskal’s algorithm, we may represent the connected components of
G(z)
as a function of
z
in a tree-like graph (see Fig.
1
): starting with components consisting of single nodes at
z=0
, whenever two components merge (i.e., at values
z\in J_{-}
) we connect the corresponding segments. This yields a (continuous) tree with leaves given by the nodes and a root at
z=\infty
. Since Kruskal’s algorithm returns exactly the tree consisting of the edges corresponding to such
z\in J_{-}
, we obtain (
2.2
).

Remark 2.3
.

The construction above also yields that the minimum spanning trees of
G=(V,E,w)
are also minimum spanning trees associated to the graph
G^{\psi}=(V,E,\psi\circ w)
, i.e., weights are
\psi(w(e))
where
\psi
is an increasing function. In particular, assuming that
\psi:[0,\infty)
is strictly increasing with
\psi(0)=0
, then
G^{\psi}(z)=G(\psi^{-1}(z))
, hence

\mathcal{C}_{MST}(G^{\psi})=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(C_{G^{\psi}(z)}-1\right)dz=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(C_{G(u)}-1\right)d\psi(u).

In particular, choosing
\psi(x)=x^{p}
and letting
p\to\infty
, we obtain that any minimum spanning tree
T
is also a minimum bottleneck spanning tree, i.e.,
T
minimizes the functional

\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{\infty}(G):=\inf\left\{\max_{e\in T}w(e)\,:\,\text{$T\subseteq E$ is a connected spanning subgraph}\right\}.

(2.3)

A similar argument
[
14
, Lemma 4]
yields an upper bound for a similar quantity where
C_{G}
is replaced with
C_{k,G}
, the number of connected components having at least
k
nodes.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
G=(V,E,w)
be connected with all distinct weights
(w(e))_{e\in E}
(if finite) and

2\leq k\leq|V|
. Then, there exists a partition
V=\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}C_{i}
such that letting
G_{k}
be the graph over the node set
\left\{C_{1},\ldots,C_{m}\right\}
with weights

w(C_{i},C_{j})=\inf\left\{w(e)\,:\,e=\left\{x,y\right\}\in V,x\in C_{i},y\in C_{j}\right\},\quad\text{for $i$, $j\in\left\{1,\ldots,m\right\}$,}

then

\int_{0}^{\infty}(C_{k,G(z)}-1)dz\leq\mathcal{C}_{MST}(G_{k}).

(2.4)

Moreover, for every
i=1,\ldots,m
,
|C_{i}|\geq k
, hence
m\leq|V|/k
, and there exists

such that
\mathsf{n}_{G}(v)\in C_{i}
and
\mathsf{n}_{G}(\mathsf{n}_{G}(v))=v

2.2.
Metric MST problem

If
(X,\mathsf{d})
is a metric space and
V\subseteq X
, then a natural choice for a weight is
w(\left\{x,y\right\})=\mathsf{d}(x,y)^{p}
, where
p>0
is fixed. If
V
,
R
,
B\subseteq X
, are finite sets and
p>0
, we write

\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(V)\quad\text{and respectively}\quad\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R,B),

for the the MST cost functional on the complete graph on
V
(and respectively, on the complete bipartite graph with independent sets
R
,
B
) and edge weights
w(\left\{x,y\right\})=\mathsf{d}(x,y)^{p}
, for
\left\{x,y\right\}\in E
. Notice that, by Remark
2.3
, the MST does not in fact depend on the choice of
p
, and moreover we may let
p\to\infty
and obtain

\left(\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(V)\right)^{1/p}\to\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{\infty}(V),\quad\left(\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R,B)\right)^{1/p}\to\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{\infty}(R,B),

where
\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{\infty}
is the minimum bottleneck spanning tree cost defined in (
2.3
) with edge weight given by the distance.

We denote by

\mathsf{d}(V,x)=\inf_{v\in V}\mathsf{d}(v,x)

and

\mathsf{d}(R,B)=\sup\left\{\sup_{r\in R}\inf_{b\in B}\mathsf{d}(r,b),\sup_{b\in B}\inf_{r\in R}\mathsf{d}(r,b)\right\}

respectively the distance function from
V
and the Hausdorff distance between
R
and
B
. Clearly,
\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R\cup B)\leq\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R,B)
. The following lemma provides a sort of converse inequality.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
p>0
. There exists a constant
C=C(p)>0
such that, for finite sets
R
,
B\subseteq X
,

\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R,B)\leq C\left(\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R)+\sum_{r\in R}\mathsf{d}(B,r)^{p}+\sum_{b\in B}\mathsf{d}(R,b)^{p}\right),

and, for some constant
C>0
,

\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{\infty}(R,B)\leq C\left(\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{\infty}(R)+\mathsf{d}(R,B)\right).

Proof.

For simplicity, we assume that all edge weights are different (otherwise a small perturbation of the weights and a suitable limit gives the thesis). Let
T_{R}
denote the MST for the vertex set
R
and fix
\bar{r}\in R
. For every
r\in R
, there exists a unique path in
T_{R}
with minimal length connecting
r
to
\bar{r}
. We associate to every
r\in R\setminus\left\{\bar{r}\right\}
the first edge
e(r)
of such path (so that
r\in e(r)
). The correspondence
r\mapsto e(r)
is a bijection.

We use this correspondence to define a connected spanning graph
S
(not necessarily a tree) on the bipartite graph with independent sets
R
,
B
. For every
r\in R\setminus\left\{\bar{r}\right\}
, if
e(r)=\left\{r,r^{\prime}\right\}
, we add the edge
\left\{\mathsf{n}(r),r^{\prime}\right\}
to
S
, where
\mathsf{n}(r)=\mathsf{n}_{G}(r)\in B
and
G
is the complete bipartite graph with independent sets
R
,
B
. Moreover, for every
r\in R
,
b\in B
we also add the edges
\left\{r,\mathsf{n}(r)\right\}
,
\left\{b,\mathsf{n}(b)\right\}
.
S
is connected because every
b\in B
is connected to
R
and the vertex set
R
is connected: any path on
T_{R}
naturally corresponds to a path on
S
using the pair of edges
\left\{r,\mathsf{n}(r)\right\}
,
\left\{\mathsf{n}(r),r^{\prime}\right\}
instead of an edge
e(r)=\left\{r,r^{\prime}\right\}
. The triangle inequality gives

\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{n}(r),r^{\prime})^{p}\leq\left(\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{n}(r),r)+\mathsf{d}(r,r^{\prime})\right)^{p}\leq C\left(\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{n}(r),r)^{p}+\mathsf{d}(r,r^{\prime})^{p}\right),

for some constant
C=C(p)>0
, hence

\sum_{\left\{r,b\right\}\in S}\mathsf{d}(r,b)^{p}\leq C\left(\sum_{\left\{r,r^{\prime}\right\}\in T}\mathsf{d}(r,r^{\prime})^{p}+\sum_{r\in R}\mathsf{d}(r,\mathsf{n}(r))^{p}+\sum_{b\in B}\mathsf{d}(b,\mathsf{n}(b))^{p}\right)

and the first claim follows. Taking the
p
-th root both sides and letting
p\to\infty
yields the second inequality.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

In the Euclidean setting
X=\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it is known
[
9
]
that, if
p<d
, there exists a constant
C=C(d,p)>0
such that

\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(V)\leq C|V|^{1-p/d},

(2.5)

for any
V\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
. A similar uniform bound cannot be true in the bipartite case, as simple examples show.

A second fundamental difference between the usual Euclidean MST and its bipartite variant is that for the latter its maximum vertex degree does not need to be uniformly bounded by a constant
C=C(d)>0
(again, examples are straightforward). The following result will be crucial to provide an upper bound in the random case. We say that
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a cube if
Q=\prod_{i=1}^{d}(x_{i},x_{i}+a)
with
x=(x_{i})_{i=1}^{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
a>0
is its side length. The diameter of
Q
is then
\sqrt{d}a
and its volume
|Q|=a^{d}
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
R
,
B\subseteq X
, let
T
be a MST on the bipartite graph with independent sets
R
,
B
and edge weight
w(x,y)=\mathsf{d}(x,y)
and let
\left\{r,b\right\}\in T
with
\delta:=\mathsf{d}(r,b)>\mathsf{d}(R,B)
. Then
S\cap R=\emptyset
, where

S=\left\{x\in X\,:\,\text{ $\mathsf{d}(x,r)<\delta-\mathsf{d}(R,B)$ and $\mathsf{d}(x,b)<\delta$}\right\}.

In particular, when
X=[0,1]^{d}
,
S
contains a cube
Q\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
with volume

|Q|=\min\left\{\left(\frac{\delta-\mathsf{d}(R,B)}{2\sqrt{d}}\right)^{d},1\right\}.

Proof.

Assume by contradiction that there exists
r^{\prime}\in S\cap R
, and consider the two connected components of the disconnected graph
T\setminus\left\{r,b\right\}
. If
r^{\prime}
is in the same component as
r
, then adding
\left\{r^{\prime},b\right\}
to
T\setminus\left\{r,b\right\}
yields a tree (hence, connected) with strictly smaller cost, since
\mathsf{d}(r^{\prime},b)<\delta
, hence a contradiction. If
r^{\prime}
is in the same component as
b
, then adding
\left\{\mathsf{n}(r),r^{\prime}\right\}
to
T\setminus\left\{r,b\right\}
again yields a tree with strictly smaller cost, since the triangle inequality gives

\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{n}(r),r^{\prime})\leq\mathsf{d}(\mathsf{n}(r),r)+\mathsf{d}(r,r^{\prime})<\mathsf{d}(R,B)+\delta-\mathsf{d}(R,B)<\delta.

To prove the last statement, notice first that by convexity of
[0,1]^{d}
, the point
x
on the segment connecting
r
and
b
at
(\delta-\mathsf{d}(R,B))/2
from
r
belongs to
[0,1]^{d}
. Moreover, the open ball centred at
x
with radius
(\delta-\mathsf{d}(R,B))/2
is entirely contained in
S
. Finally, intersection of any ball with radius
u\geq 0
and center
x\in[0,1]^{d}
contains at least a cube of side length
\min\{u/\sqrt{d},1\}
(the worst case is in general when
x
is a vertex of
[0,1]^{d}
).
∎

2.3.
Probabilistic estimates

In this section we collect some basic probabilistic bounds on distances between i.i.d. uniformly distributed random variables
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
on a cube
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Some of these facts are well known, especially for
d=1
, since they are related to order statistics, but we provide here short proofs for completeness. The basic observation is that, for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
0\leq\lambda\leq\left(|Q|/\omega_{d}\right)^{1/d}
, then

P(\left|x-X_{i}\right|>\lambda)=\frac{|Q\setminus B(x,\lambda)|}{|Q|}\geq 1-\omega_{d}\lambda^{d}/|Q|,

(2.6)

hence, by independence,

P\left(\min_{i=1,\ldots,n}\left|x-X_{i}\right|>\lambda\right)\geq\left(1-\omega_{d}\lambda^{d}/|Q|\right)^{n},\quad\text{for every $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$.}

If
x\in Q
, we also have the upper bound (the worst case being
x
a vertex of
Q
)

P(\left|x-X_{i}\right|>\lambda)\leq 1-\omega_{d}2^{-d}\lambda^{d}/|Q|,

hence,

P\left(\min_{i=1,\ldots,n}\left|x-X_{i}\right|>\lambda\right)\leq\left(1-\omega_{d}2^{-d}\lambda^{d}/|Q|\right)^{n},\quad\text{for every $x\in Q$.}

(2.7)

Assume
Q=[0,1]^{d}
. The layer-cake formula
\mathbb{E}\left[Z^{p}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}P(Z>\lambda)p\lambda^{p-1}d\lambda
yields, for every
p>0
, existence of a constant
C=C(d,p)>0
such that, for every
n\geq 1
,

C^{-1}n^{-p/d}\leq\sup_{x\in[0,1]^{d}}\mathbb{E}\left[\min_{i=1,\ldots,n}\left|x-X_{i}\right|^{p}\right]\leq Cn^{-p/d}.

(2.8)

For
A\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
, write
N(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}I_{A}(X_{i})
. Then
N(A)
has binomial law with parameters
(n,|A|)
. In particular, for every
t>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[t^{N(A)}\right]=(1+(t-1)|A|)^{n}.

Markov inequality yields that, letting
F(t)=\left(t\log t-t+1\right)
then, for every
t>1
,

P(N(A)>tn|A|)\leq\exp\left(-n|A|F(t)\right),

(2.9)

and, for
t<1
,

P(N(A)<tn|A|)\leq\exp\left(-n|A|F(t)\right).

(2.10)

We need some uniform bounds on
N(Q)
for every cube
Q\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
with sufficiently large or small volume. We write for brevity, for
v\geq 0
,

N^{*}(v):=\sup\left\{N(Q)\,:\,\text{ $Q\subseteq[0,1]^{d}$ cube with $|Q|\leq v$}\right\},

and

N_{*}(v):=\inf\left\{N(Q)\,:\,\text{ $Q\subseteq[0,1]^{d}$ cube with $|Q|\geq v$}\right\}.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be i.i.d. uniformly distributed on
[0,1]^{d}
. For every
v\in(0,1)
, there exist
C=C(v,d)\geq 1
such that, for every
n\geq C
, if
t>2^{2d}
, then

P\left(N^{*}(v)>tnv\right)\leq\frac{1}{2^{d}v}\exp\left(-nv2^{d}F(t2^{-2d})\right),

(2.11)

while, if
t<2^{-2d}
,

P\left(N_{*}(v)<tnv\right)\leq\frac{2^{2d}}{v}\exp\left(-nv2^{-2d}F(t2^{2d})\right).

(2.12)

Proof.

We prove (
2.11
) first. Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

2^{-k-2}\leq v^{1/d}<2^{-k-1},

so that every cube
Q
with
|Q|=v
is contained in a dyadic cube
\prod_{\ell=1}^{d}(n_{\ell}2^{-k},(n_{\ell}+1)2^{-k})
, with
(n_{\ell})_{i=1}^{d}\in\left\{0,\ldots,2^{k}-1\right\}^{d}
. It is then sufficient to consider the event
N(Q)>tnv
for at least one such dyadic cube, i.e., using the union bound (
2.9
), we bound from above

P\left(N^{*}(v)>tnv\right)\leq 2^{kd}P\left(N(Q)>tnv)\right).

Using that, for a dyadic cube with
|Q|=2^{-dk}
,

2^{kd}\leq 1/(2^{d}v)\quad\text{and}\quad|Q|2^{-2d}\leq v\leq 2^{-d}|Q|,

it follows that (
2.9
) applies with
2^{-2d}t
instead of
t
, yielding

\begin{split}2^{kd}P\left(N(Q)>tnv\right)&\leq 2^{kd}P\left(N(Q)>2^{-2d}tn|Q|\right)\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2^{d}v}\exp\left(-n|Q|F(t2^{-2d})\right)\\
&\leq\frac{1}{2^{d}v}\exp\left(-nv2^{d}F(t2^{-2d})\right).\end{split}

The argument for (
2.12
) is analogous. Let
k\in\mathbb{Z}
be such that

2^{-k+1}\leq v^{1/d}<2^{-k+2},

hence every cube
Q
with
|Q|\geq v
contains at least one dyadic cube with volume
2^{-kd}
, and we are reduced to consider the event that for such a cube
N(Q)<tnv
, i.e.,

P\left(N_{*}(v)<tnv\right)\leq 2^{kd}P\left(N(Q)<tnv\right).

Using that

2^{kd}\leq 2^{2d}/v\quad\text{and}\quad 2^{d}|Q|\leq v\leq 2^{2d}|Q|,

it follows that (
2.10
) applies with
2^{2d}t
instead of
t
, yielding

\begin{split}2^{kd}P\left(N(Q)<tnv\right)&\leq 2^{kd}P\left(N(Q)<2^{2d}tn|Q|\right)\\
&\leq\frac{2^{2d}}{v}\exp\left(-n|Q|F(t2^{2d})\right)\\
&\leq\frac{2^{2d}}{v}\exp\left(-nv2^{-2d}F(t2^{2d})\right).\qed\end{split}

In the following result we investigate the random variable (for
n\geq 2
)

M^{n}=\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}|X_{i}-\mathsf{n}(X_{i})|,

where
\mathsf{n}(X_{i})
denotes the closest point to
X_{i}
among
\left\{X_{j}\right\}_{j\neq i}
, so that

|X_{i}-\mathsf{n}(X_{i})|=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\leq n\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}|X_{i}-X_{j}|.

Heuristically, since
|X_{i}-\mathsf{n}(X_{i})|\sim n^{-1/d}
, and the random variables are almost independent, we still expect that
M^{n}\sim n^{-1/d}
, up to logarithmic factors. This is indeed the case.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
n\geq 2
,
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be i.i.d. uniformly distributed on
[0,1]^{d}
. Then, for every
a>0
, there exists a constant
C=C(a,d)\geq 1
such that

P\left(C^{-1}\leq\frac{M^{n}}{(\log(n)/n)^{1/d}}\leq C\right)\geq 1-\frac{C}{n^{a}}.

In particular, for every
q>0
, there exists
C=C(d,q)>0
such that, for every
n\geq 2
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(M^{n}\right)^{q}\right]\leq C\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{q/d}.

(2.13)

Proof.

For every
n
sufficiently large, we choose
\eta=\eta(d,n)\in((a+1)2^{2d+1},(a+1)2^{2d+2})
such that, defining
\delta=\left(\eta\log(n)/{n}\right)^{1/d}
, we have that
1/(3\delta)^{d}
is integer.

Consider a partition of
[0,1]^{d}
into cubes
\left\{Q_{j}\right\}_{j\in J}
of volume
|Q_{j}|=v:=\delta^{d}
, with
J=\left\{1,\ldots,\delta^{-d}\right\}
. Fix
\bar{t}>2^{2d}
such that
2^{d}F(\bar{t}2^{-2d})>a+1
. Lemma
2.8
entails that the event

E:=\bigcap_{j\in J}\left\{2\leq N(Q_{j})\leq\bar{t}\log n\right\}\supseteq\left\{N^{*}(v)\leq\bar{t}\log n\right\}\cap\left\{N_{*}(v)\geq 2\right\}.

has probability larger than
1-C/n^{a}
for some constant
C=C(d,a)
. Indeed, (
2.11
) gives

\begin{split}P(N^{*}(v)\leq\bar{t}\log n)&=P\left(N^{*}(v)\leq\bar{t}/\eta nv\right)\\
&\leq 2^{-d}\frac{n}{\eta\log n}\exp\left(-\log(n)\eta 2^{d}F(\bar{t}2^{2d}/\eta)\right)\\
&{}^{\leq}\frac{1}{2^{d+1}\eta n^{a}},\end{split}

having also used that
\eta\geq 1
. Similarly, by (
2.12
), with
t=2/(\log(n)\eta)
,

\begin{split}P(N_{*}(v)>2)&=P\left(N_{*}(v)>\left(2/(\log(n)\eta)\right)nv\right)\\
&\leq 2^{2d}\frac{n}{\eta\log n}\exp\left(-\log(n)\eta 2^{-2d}F(2/(\log(n)\eta))\right).\end{split}

For
n
sufficiently large,
F\left(2/(\log(n)\eta)\right)o>1/2
, hence

\begin{split}2^{2d}\frac{n}{\eta\log n}\exp\left(-\log(n)\eta 2^{-2d}F(2/(\log(n)\eta))\right)&\leq 2^{2d}\frac{n}{\eta\log n}\exp\left(-\log(n)\eta 2^{-2d-1}\right)\\
&\leq\frac{2^{2d}}{\eta n^{a}}.\end{split}

Hence, to prove the thesis, we can assume that
E
holds. In such a case, it follows at once that
M^{n}\leq\sqrt{d}v^{1/d}
, hence

P\left(\frac{M^{n}}{(\log(n)/n)^{1/d}}>\sqrt{d}\eta^{1/d}\right)\leq\frac{C}{n^{a}}.

Next, we introduce the random variables

M^{n}_{j}:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\leq n\\
X_{i}\in Q_{j}\end{subarray}}\,\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\leq n\\
0<|X_{i}-X_{\ell}|<\delta\end{subarray}}|X_{i}-X_{\ell}|,

i.e., we maximize the minimum distances between points in
Q_{j}
and those that are at distance at most
\delta
. These are not necessarily in
Q_{j}
but must belong to the union of all cubes
Q_{\ell}
covering
\left\{x|\mathsf{d}(x,Q_{j})\leq\delta\right\}
, that we denote by
Q_{j,\delta}
. Since every cube
\left\{Q_{j}\right\}_{j\in J}
has side length
\delta
, then
Q_{j,\delta}
is a cube of side length
3\delta
, hence
|Q_{j,\delta}|/|Q|=3^{d}
. Notice also that, since each cube
Q_{j}
contains at least two elements (for we assume that
E
holds) then

M^{n}=\max_{j\in J}M^{n}_{j}.

We now use the following fact: for every
f:\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}\to J
, conditioning upon the event

A_{f}=\left\{X_{i}\in Q_{f(i)}\quad\text{for every $i=1,\ldots,n$,}\right\},

the random variables
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
are independent, each
X_{i}
uniform on
Q_{f(i)}
. Thus, we further disintegrate upon the events
A_{f}
, and since
E
holds we consider only
f
’s such that, for every
j\in J
,

2\leq\left|\left\{f=j\right\}\right|\leq\bar{t}\log n.

We let
K\subseteq J
denote a subfamily consisting of
(\delta 3)^{-d}
cubes such that, for
j,k\in K
, with
j\neq k
,
Q_{j,\delta}\cap Q_{k,\delta}=\emptyset
so that the random variables
(M^{n}_{k})_{k\in K}
are independent (after conditioning upon
A_{f}
). Using

M^{n}=\max_{j\in J}M^{n}_{j}\geq\max_{k\in K}M^{n}_{k},

and independence we have, for every
\lambda>0
,

P(M^{n}\leq\lambda|A_{f})\leq P(\max_{k\in K}M^{n}_{k}\leq\lambda|A_{f})=\prod_{k\in K}P(M^{n}_{k}\leq\lambda|A_{f})

The probability
P(M^{n}_{k}\leq\lambda|A_{f})
clearly depends only on the number of elements in each
\left\{f=j\right\}
, i.e., on the number of points in each
Q_{j}
, for
j\in J
, not their labels. We may therefore assume that
f(1)=k
, i.e.,
X_{1}\in Q_{k}
and that

\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n}\cap Q_{k,\delta}=\left\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{\ell}\right\},

with

\ell=N(Q_{k,\delta})\leq 3^{d}\bar{t}\log n.

(2.14)

Then,

M^{n}_{k}\geq\min_{j=2,\ldots,\ell}|X_{j}-X_{1}|,

hence, further conditioning upon
X_{1}
and using independence,

\begin{split}P(M^{n}_{k}>\lambda|A_{f})&\geq\frac{1}{|Q_{k}|}\int_{Q_{k}}P\left(\min_{j=2,\ldots,\ell}|X_{j}-x|>\lambda|A_{f},X_{1}=x\right)dx\\
&\geq\frac{1}{|Q_{k}|}\int_{Q_{k}}\prod_{j=2}^{\ell}P(|X_{j}-x|>\lambda|A_{f},X_{1}=x)dx\\
&\geq\frac{1}{|Q_{k}|}\int_{Q_{k}}\prod_{j=2}^{\ell}\left(1-\omega_{d}\lambda^{d}/|Q_{f(j)}|\right)_{+}dx\quad\text{by \eqref{eq:steele-lower},}\\
&\geq\left(1-\omega_{d}(\lambda/\delta)^{d}\right)^{N(Q_{k,\delta})-1}_{+}=e^{-u(N(Q_{k,\delta})-1)},\end{split}

where in the last equality we choose
\lambda=\delta((1-e^{-u})/\omega_{d})^{1/d}
with
u=1/(2\cdot 3^{d}\bar{t})
. Indeed, this choice ensures that, by (
2.14
) we bound from above, for
n
sufficiently large,

P\left(M^{n}\leq\lambda|A_{f}\right)\leq\left(1-e^{-u(3^{d}\bar{t}\log(n)-1)}\right)^{(\delta 3)^{-d}}\leq\left(1-\frac{e^{u}}{n^{1/2}}\right)^{n/(3^{d}\log(n))}\leq\frac{C}{n^{a}}.

Finally, (
2.13
) follows since
M^{n}\leq\sqrt{d}
, hence, choosing
a=q/d
, we bound from above

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(M^{n}\right)^{q}\right]\leq d^{q/2}P(M^{n}>C(\log(n)/n)^{1/d})+C^{q}\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{q/d}\leq C^{\prime}\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{q/d}.\qed

A minor variation of the proof of the previous proposition yields the following bipartite analogue, where we replace
M^{n}
with the (random) Hausdorff distance between
R
and
B
.

Proposition 2.10
.

For
n\geq 1
, let
R^{n}=\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n_{R}}
,
B^{n}=\left\{Y_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n_{B}}
be (jointly) i.i.d. uniformly distributed on
[0,1]^{d}
with
n_{R}+n_{B}=n
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n_{R}}{n}=\alpha_{R}\in(0,1),\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n_{B}}{n}=\alpha_{B}=1-\alpha_{R}.

Then, for every
a>0
, there exists a constant
C=C(d,\alpha_{R},\alpha_{B},a)\geq 1
such that, for every
n
sufficiently large,

P\left(C^{-1}\leq\frac{\mathsf{d}(R^{n},B^{n})}{(\log(n)/n)^{1/d}}\leq C\right)\geq 1-\frac{C}{n^{a}}.

In particular, for every
q>0
, there exists
C=C(d,q,\alpha_{R})>0
such that, for every
n
sufficiently large,

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathsf{d}(R^{n},B^{n})^{q}\right]\leq C\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{q/d}.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Throughout this section, for
n\geq 1
, let
R^{n}=\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n_{R}}
,
B^{n}=\left\{Y_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n_{B}}
be (jointly) i.i.d. uniformly distributed on
[0,1]^{d}
with
n_{R}+n_{B}=n
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n_{R}}{n}=\alpha_{R}\in(0,1),\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n_{B}}{n}=\alpha_{B}=1-\alpha_{R}.

Let
T^{n}
denote the Euclidean bipartite MST on
R^{n}
,
B^{n}
,
and write
\Delta(T^{n})
be the maximum vertex degree of
T^{n}
.

We split the proof into two separate results.

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists
\epsilon>0
such that, as
n\to\infty
,

P\left(\Delta(T^{n})<\epsilon\log n\right)\to 0.

Proof.

For
n
sufficiently large, we have
n_{R}\geq\alpha_{R}n/2
,
n_{B}\geq\alpha_{B}n/2
. Fix any
a>0
and let
C_{1}=C(d,a)\geq 1
be as in Proposition
2.9
applied to the variables
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n_{R}}
, so that the event
E^{n}
, such that there exists
X_{i}\in R^{n}
with

\min_{j\neq i}|X_{i}-X_{j}|\geq C^{-1}_{1}\left(\log(n_{R})/n_{R}\right)^{1/d}\geq C^{-1}\left(\log(n)/n\right)^{1/d},

(the second inequality being true if
n
is sufficiently large) has probability
P(E^{n})\geq 1-C/n^{a}
as
n\to\infty
, for a suitable constant
C=C(d,\alpha_{R},a)>0
.
In particular, every point in the cube
Q
centred at
X_{i}
with side length

Theorem 4.2

), by dominated convergence it follows that

\begin{split}\lim_{n\to\infty}&\int_{\Theta(k_{R},k_{B})}\left(1-\frac{y}{n}|D(\left\{b_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{k_{B}})|\right)^{n_{R}-k_{R}}\left(1-\frac{y}{n}|D(\left\{r_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{k_{B}})|\right)^{n_{B}-k_{B}}\delta_{0}(r_{1})drdb\\
&=\int_{\Theta(k_{R},k_{B})}\exp\left(-y\left(\alpha_{R}|D(\left\{b_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{k_{B}})|+\alpha_{B}|D(\left\{r_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{k_{R}})|\right)\right)\delta_{0}(r_{1})drdb\\
&=:\mathcal{I}_{R}(k_{R},k_{B},\alpha_{R},y).\end{split}

Moreover,

\lim_{n\to\infty}{n_{R}\choose k_{R}-1}{n_{B}\choose k_{B}}\frac{1}{n^{k-1}}=\frac{\alpha_{R}^{k_{R}-1}}{(k_{R}-1)!}\frac{\alpha_{B}^{k_{B}}}{k_{B}!},

(4.5)

so that

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{k,R}^{n}((y/n)^{p/d})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{R}+k_{B}=k\\
k_{R}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{\alpha_{R}^{k_{R}-1}}{(k_{R}-1)!}\frac{\alpha_{B}^{k_{B}}}{k_{B}!}y^{(k-1)/d}\mathcal{I}_{R}(k_{R},k_{B},\alpha_{R},y).

To show that convergence is dominated, in view of (
4.4
) and the limit (
4.5
) it is sufficient to dominate each term

n^{k-1}I_{R}(k_{R},k_{R},(y/n)^{p/d}),

If
A\subseteq\mathbb{T}^{d}
is not empty, then
|D(A,z)|\geq\omega_{d}z^{d/p}
(assuming that
\omega_{d}z^{d/p}\leq 1
). Therefore, if also
k_{B}\geq 1
, we write

\begin{split}I_{R}(k_{R},k_{B},z)&=\int_{\Theta_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(k_{R},k_{B},z)}(1-|D(\left\{b_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{k_{B}},z)|)^{n_{R}-k_{R}}\left(1-|D(\left\{r_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{k_{R}},z)|\right)^{n_{B}-k_{B}}drdb\\
&\leq\int_{\Theta_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(k_{R},k_{B},z)}(1-\omega_{d}z^{d/p})^{n-k}_{+}drdb\\
&\leq\exp\left(-(n-k)z^{d/p}\right)|\Theta_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(k_{R},k_{B},z)|\\
&\leq k^{k-2}z^{d/p}\exp\left(-(n-k)z^{d/p}\right).\end{split}

where the bound
|\Theta_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(n_{R},n_{B},z)|\leq k^{k-2}z^{d/p}
follows since every tree (even not necessarily bipartite) with edge weights smaller than
z
can be iteratively obtained by choosing points
x_{i+1}\in D(\left\{x_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{i},z)
. Substituting
z=(y/n)^{p/d}
gives the required domination. Notice that, if
k_{B}=0
and
k_{R}\geq 2
, there is nothing to prove, since
\Theta_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(k_{R},k_{B},z)
is empty. For the case
k_{B}=0
,
k_{R}=1
we argue similarly obtaining

I_{R}(1,0,z)\leq\exp\left(-(n-1)z^{d/p}\right),

which is sufficient to conclude. Arguing similarly for
P_{k,B}^{n}
gives

\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{k,B}^{n}((y/n)^{p/d})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k_{R}+k_{B}=k\\
k_{B}\geq 1\end{subarray}}\frac{\alpha_{R}^{k_{R}}}{k_{R}!}\frac{\alpha_{B}^{k_{B}-1}}{(k_{B}-1)!}y^{(k-1)/d}\mathcal{I}_{B}(k_{R},k_{B},\alpha_{R},y),

with similar definitions. Thus, the validity of assumption
a
) is established.

We notice here that, exchanging the order of integration,

\begin{split}&\int_{0}^{\infty}y^{(k-1)/d}\mathcal{I}_{R}(k_{B},k_{R},\alpha_{R},y)y^{1/d-1}dy\\
&=\int_{\Theta(k_{R},k_{B})}\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-y\left(\alpha_{R}|D(\left\{b_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{k_{B}})|+\alpha_{B}D(\left\{r_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{k_{R}})|\right)\right)y^{k/d-1}dy\delta_{0}(r_{1})drdb\\
&=\Gamma(k/d)\int_{\Theta(k_{R},k_{B})}\left(\alpha_{R}|D(\left\{b_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{k_{B}})|+\alpha_{B}|D(\left\{r_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{k_{R}})|\right)^{-k/d}\delta_{0}(r_{1})drdb,\end{split}

which, after some manipulations, taking into account also the term with
\mathcal{I}_{B}
, gives the claimed expression for
\beta_{bMST}(p,d)
.

We next prove that assumption
b
) holds. A lower bound is straightforward, since the maximum weight of the edges is uniformly bounded (by some constant
M=M(d,p)>0
, e.g.
M=d^{p/2}
), it follows that
C_{k}^{n}(z)=1
if
z\geq M
and
n
is sufficiently large (recall that we must let first
n\to\infty
and then
k\to\infty
, so we can assume
n\geq k
).

It follows that

\frac{1}{n^{1-\frac{1}{d}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[C_{k}^{n}(z)\right]-1\right)dz\geq-\frac{M}{n^{1-\frac{1}{d}}}\to 0\quad\text{ as $n\to\infty$.}

To obtain an upper bound we use Lemma
2.4
on each
G^{n}
– we can assume
k\geq 2
and
n\geq k
. Given the sets
C_{i}
, for
i=1\ldots,m
with
m\leq n/k
, we choose elements
r_{i}\in C_{i}
such that
b_{i}:=\mathsf{n}_{G^{n}}(r_{i})\in C_{i}
and
\mathsf{n}_{G^{n}}(b_{i})=r_{i}
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
r_{i}\in R^{n}
,
b_{i}\in B^{n}
. We consider the induced subgraph of
G^{n}_{k}\subseteq G_{k}
obtained by restriction on the nodes
R^{n}_{k}:=\left\{r_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{m}
,
B^{n}_{k}:=\left\{b_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{m}
. With the notation of Lemma
2.4
, we have

w(i,j)\leq\mathsf{d}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(r_{i},b_{j})^{p},

hence

\frac{1}{n^{1-\frac{1}{d}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[C_{k}^{n}(z)\right]-1\right)dz\leq\mathcal{C}_{MST}(G^{n}_{k}).

We then use Lemma
2.5
(in fact applied on the metric space
\mathbb{T}^{d}
) to obtain that, for some constant
C=C(p)>0
,

\mathcal{C}_{MST}(G^{n}_{k})\leq C\left(\mathcal{C}_{MST}(R^{n}_{k})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathsf{d}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(r_{i},b_{i})^{p}\right),

where only one summation appears since
\mathsf{n}_{G^{n}_{k}}(b_{i})=\mathsf{n}_{G}(b_{i})=r_{i}
. Bounding from above the distance on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
with the Euclidean distance, and using Remark (
2.6
), we have the inequality, for some constant
C=C(d,p)>0
,

\mathcal{C}_{MST}(R^{n}_{k})+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathsf{d}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(r_{i},b_{i})^{p}\leq Cm^{1-p/d}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\min_{j=1,\ldots,n}|r_{i}-Y_{j}|^{p},

where we also used that
b_{i}=\mathsf{n}_{G^{n}}(r_{i})
. We finally apply (
2.8
) with
x=r_{i}
and the i.i.d. uniform random variables
(Y_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
to conclude that, again for some further constant
C=C(d,p)>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{C}_{MST}(R^{n}_{k},B^{n}_{k})\right]\leq C\left(m^{1-p/d}+mn^{-p/d}\right)\leq C\left(\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{1-p/d}+\frac{n}{k}n^{-p/d}\right).

Dividing by
n^{1-p/d}
and letting first
n\to\infty
and then
k\to\infty
gives the thesis.∎

To transfer the result from the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
to the cube
[0,1]^{d}
, we use the fact that points in
[0,1]^{d}
can be projected to
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, and
\mathsf{d}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(x,y)\leq|x-y|
, so that, for any
p>0
,
R
,
B\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
,

\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R,B|\mathbb{T}^{d})\leq\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R,B),

for any set of points
R
,
B\subseteq[0,1]^{d}
, where
\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R,B|\mathbb{T}^{d})
denotes the MST cost functional on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. Letting
p\to\infty
yields also
\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{\infty}(R,B|\mathbb{T}^{d})\leq\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{\infty}(R,B)
. A converse inequality is the following one.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
\delta\in(0,1/2)
is such that
\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{\infty}(R,B)\leq\delta
, then, for every
p>0
,

\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R,B)\leq\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R,B|\mathbb{T}^{d})+\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R_{\delta},B_{\delta}),

where
R_{\delta}=R\setminus[\delta,1-\delta]^{d}
,
B_{\delta}=B\setminus[\delta,1-\delta]^{d}
.

Proof.

Indeed, let
T
be a MST for
R
,
B
projected on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. The assumption gives that for every
\left\{r,b\right\}\in T
, since
|r-b|\leq\delta
, it must be
\mathsf{d}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(r,b)=|r-b|
if
r\in R\setminus R_{\delta}
or
b\in B\setminus B_{\delta}
. Therefore, the only obstacle to bound
\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R,B)
from above by
\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R,B|\mathbb{T}^{d})
is due to edges
\left\{r,b\right\}\in T
with
r\in R_{\delta}
and
b\in B_{\delta}
, for which
\mathsf{d}_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}(r,b)
may be much smaller than
|r-b|
. However, removing all these edges and adding all the edges of a bipartite Euclidean MST over
R_{\delta}
,
B_{\delta}
yields a connected graph and the desired upper bound.
∎

We combine the above lemma with the following asymptotic upper bounds.

Lemma 4.5
.

For
n\geq 1
, let
R^{n}=\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n_{R}}
,
B^{n}=\left\{Y_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n_{B}}
be (jointly) i.i.d. uniformly distributed on
[0,1]^{d}
with
n_{R}+n_{B}=n
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n_{R}}{n}=\alpha_{R}\in(0,1),\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n_{B}}{n}=\alpha_{B}=1-\alpha_{R}.

Then, for every
p>0
, there exists a constant
C=C(d,p,\alpha_{R})>0
such that, for
n
large enough,

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}\left(R^{n}\right)\right]\leq Cn^{1-p/d},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}\left(R^{n},B^{n}\right)\right]\leq Cn^{1-p/d},

as well as, for some constant
C=C(d,q,\alpha_{R})>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{\infty}\left(R^{n}\right)\right)^{q}\right]\leq C\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{q/d},\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{\infty}\left(R^{n},B^{n}\right)\right)^{q}\right]\leq C\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{q/d},

and finally, for every
a>0
, there exists
C=C(d,a,\alpha_{R})>0
, such that for
n
large enough,

P(\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{\infty}\left(R^{n}\right)>C(\log(n)/n)^{1/d})\leq\frac{C}{n^{a}},\quad P(\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{\infty}\left(R^{n},B^{n}\right)>C(\log(n)/n)^{1/d})\leq\frac{C}{n^{a}}.

(4.6)

Proof.

This follows from an application of the space-filling curve technique: consider
\gamma:[0,1]\to[0,1]^{d}
such that the push-forward of the uniform measure on
[0,1]^{d}
is the uniform measure on
[0,1]^{d}
and it is Hölder continuous with exponent
1/d
, i.e.,

C_{\gamma}:=\sup_{s\neq t}\frac{|\gamma(t)-\gamma(s)|^{1/d}}{|t-s|}<\infty.

While many constructions for
d=2
are historically well-known, the case of general
d
is established in detail e.g. in
[
29
]
. Let then
\left(Z_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{m}
be i.i.d. uniform on
[0,1]
, so that
\left(\gamma(Z_{i})\right)_{i=1}^{m}
are i.i.d. on
[0,1]^{d}
. Consider the order statistics

Z_{(1)}=\min_{i=1,\ldots,m}Z_{i}\leq Z_{(2)}\leq\ldots\leq Z_{(m)}=\max_{i=1,\ldots,m}Z_{i}

and let
T
be the connected graph on
\left\{\gamma(Z_{i})\right\}_{i=1}^{m}
with edges

\left\{\left\{\gamma(Z_{(i)}),\gamma(Z_{(i+1)})\right\}\,:\,i=1,\ldots,m-1\right\}.

We have

\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}\left(\left(\gamma(Z_{i})\right)_{i=1}^{m}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}|\gamma(Z_{(i+1)})-\gamma(Z_{(i)})|^{p}\leq C_{\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}|Z_{(i+1)}-Z_{(i)}|^{p}.

The law of each
Z_{(i+1)}-Z_{(i)}
is beta
B(1,n)
, so that, for every
q\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{(i+1)}-Z_{(i)}|^{q}\right]=\prod_{r=0}^{q-1}\frac{r+1}{m+r+1}\leq C(q)m^{-q}.

Bounding the
p
-th moment with the
\left\lceil p\right\rceil
-th moment gives that, for
p>0
, there exists a constant
C(p)
such that, for every
m
and
i
,

\mathbb{E}\left[|Z_{(i+1)}-Z_{(i)}|^{p}\right]\leq C(p)m^{-p}

The first inequality of the thesis thus follows by summation upon
i=1\ldots,m-1
and letting
m=n_{R}
. For the second inequality, we use Lemma
2.5
and (
2.8
). The remaining inequalities follow analogously, noticing that

\max_{i=1,\ldots,m-1}|Z_{(i+1)}-Z_{(i)}|=\max_{i=1,\ldots,m}|Z_{i}-\mathsf{n}(Z_{i})|=M^{m},

with the notation of Proposition
2.9
.
∎

The following result entails that the expectation of the bipartite Euclidean minimum spanning tree cost on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
and on the cube
[0,1]^{d}
are much closer than the rate
n^{1-p/d}
.

Proposition 4.6
.

For
n\geq 1
, let
R^{n}=\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n_{R}}
,
B^{n}=\left\{Y_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n_{B}}
be (jointly) i.i.d. uniformly distributed on
[0,1]^{d}
with
n_{R}+n_{B}=n
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n_{R}}{n}=\alpha_{R}\in(0,1),\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n_{B}}{n}=\alpha_{B}=1-\alpha_{R}.

Then, for every
p>0
, there exists a constant
C=C(p,d)>0
such that, for
n
large enough,

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R^{n},B^{n})\right]-\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R^{n},B^{n}|\mathbb{T}^{d})]\leq Cn^{1-(p+1)/d}(\log n)^{(p+1)/d}.

In particular, for every
p<d
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R^{n},B^{n})\right]}{n^{1-p/d}}=\beta_{bMST}(p,d)

with
\beta_{bMST}(p,d)
as in Theorem
1.2
.

Proof.

Let
C>0
be as in (
4.6
) with
a>p/d
and let
\delta=C(\log(n)/n)^{1/d}
. Since both
n_{R}
and
n_{B}
grow linearly with
n\to\infty
, an application of Lemma
2.8
as in the proof of Proposition
2.9
ensures that with probability larger than
1-C/n^{a}
, for every fixed
a>0
, in particular for
a>p/d
(and a suitable constant
C>0
) all cubes
Q
with
|Q|=\delta^{d}
have non-empty intersections both with
R^{n}
and
B^{n}
. Let
E
the event where both these conditions occur as well as
\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{\infty}(R^{n},B^{n})\leq\delta
. In the event
E
, we may apply Lemma
4.4
, otherwise we simply bound
\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R^{n},B^{n})\leq nd^{p/2}
and use the fact that
P(E^{c})\leq C/n^{a}
for
a
arbitrary large.

We are then reduced to bound from above

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}\left(R^{n}_{\delta},B^{n}_{\delta}\right)I_{E}\right]

where
R^{n}_{\delta}=R^{n}\setminus[\delta,1-\delta]^{d}
,
B^{n}_{\delta}=B^{n}\setminus[\delta,1-\delta]^{d}
.
We decompose
[0,1]^{d}\setminus[\delta,1-\delta]^{d}
into
\left(Q_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{m}
, cubes with side length
\delta
, with
m\leq C\delta^{-(d-1)}
, for some
C=C(d)>0
. We consider a bipartite Euclidean minimum spanning tree
T^{n}_{i}
on
R^{n}_{i}:=R^{n}\cap Q_{i}
,
B^{n}_{i}:=B^{n}\cap Q_{i}
– both are non empty if the event
E
occurs – and then add
m-1
edges, each with one node in a cube and an adjacent one, to connect all these trees (again this is possible since
E
occurs). This construction leads to a bipartite spanning tree on
R^{n}_{\delta}
,
B^{n}_{\delta}
, so that, in
E
,

\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}\left(R^{n}_{\delta},B^{n}_{\delta}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}\left(R^{n}_{i},B^{n}_{i}\right)+m\delta^{p}.

Taking expectation and rescaling from the cube
Q_{i}
to
[0,1]^{d}
, we bound each term in the sum using Lemma
4.5
, writing
N_{R}(Q_{i})=|R^{n}_{i}|
,
N_{B}(Q_{i})=|B^{n}_{i}|
, that are independent random variables with binomial laws with common parameters
(n,\delta^{d})
. It follows that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}\left(R^{n}_{i},B^{n}_{i}\right)I_{E}\right]&\leq\delta^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}\left(R^{n}_{i},B^{n}_{i}\right)I_{\left\{N_{R}(Q_{i})>0,N_{B}(Q_{i})>0\right\}}\right]\\
&\leq C\delta^{p}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[N_{R}(Q_{i})^{1-p/d}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[N_{B}(Q_{i})\right]\mathbb{E}\left[N_{R}(Q_{i})^{-p/d}I_{\left\{N_{R}(Q_{i})>0\right\}}\right]\right)\\
&\leq C\delta^{p}\log n,\end{split}

having used that

\mathbb{E}\left[N_{R}(Q_{i})^{1-p/d}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[N_{R}(Q_{i})\right]^{1-p/d}\leq C(\log n)^{1-p/d}

and

\mathbb{E}\left[N_{R}(Q_{i})^{-p/d}I_{\left\{N_{R}(Q_{i})>0\right\}}\right]\leq 1.

It follows that, for some (other) constant
C=C(p,d)>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}\left(R^{n}_{i},B^{n}_{i}\right)I_{E}\right]\leq Cm\delta^{p}\log n=C\delta^{p-d+1}\log n,

that eventually gives the thesis.
∎

We end the proof of Theorem
1.2
with a concentration result to improve from convergence of expectations to complete convergence.

Proposition 4.7
.

Let
d\geq 1
. For
n\geq 1
, let
R^{n}=\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n_{R}}
,
B^{n}=\left\{Y_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n_{B}}
be (jointly) i.i.d. uniformly distributed on
[0,1]^{d}
with
n_{R}+n_{B}=n
and

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n_{R}}{n}=\alpha_{R}\in(0,1),\quad\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n_{B}}{n}=\alpha_{B}=1-\alpha_{R}.

Then, for every
p\in(0,d/2)
if
d\in\left\{1,2\right\}
or any
p>0
if
d\geq 3
, complete convergence holds:

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R^{n},B^{n})-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}(R^{n},B^{n})\right]}{n^{1-p/d}}=0.

Proof.

Consider the function

\left([0,1]^{d}\right)^{n_{R}+n_{B}}\ni(x,y)=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n_{R}},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n_{B}})\mapsto f(x,y)=\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}\left(\left\{x_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n_{R}},\left\{y_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{n_{B}}\right).

We argue separately for
p<1
and
p\geq 1
. In the former case, if
(x,y)
,
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in\left([0,1]^{d}\right)^{n_{R}+n_{B}}
differ only on a single coordinate, say for simplicity
x_{1}\neq x_{1}^{\prime}
, then, letting
T
denote the Euclidean bipartite minimum spanning tree on
\left\{x_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n_{R}}
,
\left\{y_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{n_{B}}
,

\begin{split}f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})&\leq f(x,y)+\sum_{\left\{y_{j},x_{1}\right\}\in T}|y_{j}-x_{1}^{\prime}|^{p}-|y_{j}-x_{1}|^{p}\\
&\leq f(x,y)+\sum_{\left\{y_{j},x_{1}\right\}\in T}|x_{1}-x_{1}^{\prime}|^{p}\quad\text{(by the triangle inequality, since $p\leq 1$)}\\
&\leq f(x,y)+\deg_{T}(x_{1})|x_{1}-x^{\prime}_{1}|^{p}\\
&\leq f(x,y)+\Delta(T)d^{p/2},\end{split}

where
\Delta(T)
denote the maximum degree of
T
. Arguing symmetrically, we obtain

|f(x,y)-f(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|\leq\Delta(T)d^{p/2}+\Delta(T^{\prime})d^{p/2}.

By Lemma
A.1
with
E_{i}=[0,1]^{d}
and
g_{i}(x,y)=\Delta(T)d^{p/2}
(for every
i=1,\ldots,n
) we obtain, for every
q\geq 2
, that there exists
C=C(q)>0
such that

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}\left(R^{n},B^{n}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}\left(R^{n},B^{n}\right)\right]\right|^{q}\right]&\leq Cn^{q/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\Delta(T^{n})d^{p/2}\right)^{q}\right]\\
&\leq C^{\prime}n^{q/2}(\log n)^{q},\end{split}

where we used (
3.1
) and
C^{\prime}=C(d,q,\alpha_{R},\alpha_{b})>0
is a constant. Dividing both sides by
n^{q(1-p/d)}
and using Markov inequality yields, for every
\epsilon>0
,

P\left(\left|\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}\left(R^{n},B^{n}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}\left(R^{n},B^{n}\right)\right]\right|/n^{1-p/d}>\epsilon\right)\leq C^{\prime}\epsilon^{-q}n^{q(p/d-1/2)}(\log n)^{q},

that is summable if
p/d-1/2<0
, i.e.,
p<d/2
, and
q
is sufficiently large.

In the case
p\geq 1
, we use the fact that
f
is Lipschitz (being minimum of Lipschitz functions) with a.e. derivative given by

\nabla_{x_{i}}f(x,y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1,\ldots,n_{B}\\
\left\{x_{i},y_{j}\right\}\in T\end{subarray}}p|x_{i}-y_{j}|^{p-2}(x_{i}-y_{j}),

\nabla_{y_{j}}f(x,y)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,\ldots,n_{R}\\
\left\{x_{i},y_{j}\right\}\in T\end{subarray}}p|x_{i}-y_{j}|^{p-2}(x_{i}-y_{j}),

We bound from above, using Cauchy-Schwartz inequality

|\nabla_{x_{i}}f(x,y)|^{2}\leq p^{2}\Delta(T)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1,\ldots,n_{B}\\
\left\{x_{i},y_{j}\right\}\in T\end{subarray}}|x_{i}-y_{j}|^{2(p-1)}.

It follows that the (Euclidean) norm of the derivative is bounded above by

|\nabla f(x,y)|^{2}\leq 2p^{2}\Delta(T)\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{2(p-1)}(x,y)\leq 2p^{2}\Delta(T)\left(\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{\infty}(x,y)\right)^{2(p-1)}n,

where we used the fact that the minimum spanning tree
T
does not depend on the choice of
p
, see Remark
2.3
. For every
q>0
Lemma
3.2
and Lemma
4.5
yield that, for some constant
C=C(d,p,q)>0
and
n
sufficiently large,

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta(T^{n})^{q/2}\left(\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{\infty}(R^{n},B^{n})\right)^{q(p-1)}\right]&\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta(T^{n})^{q}\right]^{1/2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{\infty}(R^{n},B^{n})\right)^{2q(p-1)}\right]^{1/2}\\
&\leq C\log(n)^{q/2}\left(\frac{\log n}{n}\right)^{q(p-1)/d}\end{split}

It follows that (possibly for a larger constant
C
)

\mathbb{E}\left[|\nabla f(\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n},\left\{Y_{i}\right\}_{i=1})|^{q}\right]\leq C\log(n)^{q\left(1/2+(p-1)/d\right)}n^{q(1/2-(p-1)/d)}.

Poincaré inequality for the uniform measure on the unit cube, see e.g. the argument in
[
30
, Prop. 2.8]
, gives that, for some constant
C=C(q)>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|f(\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n},\left\{Y_{i}\right\}_{i=1})-\mathbb{E}\left[f(\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n},\left\{Y_{i}\right\}_{i=1})\right]\right|^{q}\right]\leq C(q)\mathbb{E}\left[|\nabla f(\left\{X_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{n},\left\{Y_{i}\right\}_{i=1})|^{q}\right],

thus using Markov inequality, for every
\epsilon>0
, we have

P\left(\left|\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}\left(R^{n},B^{n}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{C}_{MST}^{p}\left(R^{n},B^{n}\right)\right]\right|/n^{1-p/d}>\epsilon\right)\leq C\frac{(\log n)^{q\left(1/2+(p-1)/d\right)}}{\epsilon^{q}n^{q(1/2-1/d)}},

which is summable if
q
is large enough and
d\geq 3
.
∎

Appendix A
A concentration inequality in
L^{q}
.

McDiarmid inequality
[
31
]
is a simple but effective concentration inequality often used in random combinatorial optimization problems. An interpretation is that the oscillations of a function of many independent random variables are bounded by its Lipschitz norm (with respect to a Hamming-type distance). The usual proof relies concentration inequalities for discrete time exponential martingales. In this section we show an analogous result where we replace the Lipschitz condition with a “Sobolev” one and use Burkholder-Gundy inequalities instead.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\left((E_{i},\mathcal{E}_{i})\right)_{i=1}^{n}
be measurable spaces, set
E=\prod_{i=1}^{n}E_{i}
and let

f:E\to\mathbb{R},\quad g_{i}:E\to[0,\infty]\quad\text{for $i=1,\ldots,n$,}

be such that, for every
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,n\right\}
, for every
x
,
x^{\prime}\in E
with

x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},\ldots,x_{n})\quad x^{\prime}=(x_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{i},\ldots,x_{n})

then

\left|f(x)-f(x^{\prime})\right|\leq g_{i}(x)+g_{i}(x^{\prime}).

For every
q\geq 2
, there exists
C=C(q)>0
such that, if
X=(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
are independent random variables, with
X_{i}:\Omega\to E_{i}
, then

\mathbb{E}\left[|f(X)-\mathbb{E}\left[f(X)\right]|^{q}\right]\leq C(q)n^{q/2-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}(X)^{q}\right].

Proof.

Write
\mathbb{E}_{i}
for the conditional expectation with respect to the variables
\left(X_{j}\right)_{j\leq i}
. In particular,
\mathbb{E}_{0}=\mathbb{E}
and
\mathbb{E}_{n}
is the identity operator. We write
f(X)-\mathbb{E}\left[f(X)\right]
as a sum of martingale differences

f(X)-\mathbb{E}\left[f(X)\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[f(X)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{{i-1}}\left[f(X)\right].

Burkholder-Gundy inequality
[
32
]
gives, for some constant
C=C(q)\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[|f(X)-\mathbb{E}\left[f(X)\right]|^{q}\right]\leq C(q)\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[f(X)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{{i-1}}\left[f(X)\right]\right|^{2}\right)^{q/2}\right].

(A.1)

To simplify notation, we introduce a copy of the independent variables
(X_{i}^{\prime})_{i=1}^{n}
defined on a different space
\Omega^{\prime}
, and write
\mathbb{E}^{\prime}
for expectation with respect to such variables, so that, because of independence, for every
i=0,\ldots,n
,

\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[f(X)\right]=\mathbb{E}^{\prime}\left[f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{i},X^{\prime}_{i+1},\ldots X^{\prime}_{n})\right].

\begin{split}&\left|\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[f(X)\right]-\mathbb{E}_{i-1}\left[f(X)\right]\right|\\
&\quad=\left|\mathbb{E}^{\prime}\left[f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{i},X^{\prime}_{i+1},\ldots X^{\prime}_{n})\right]-\mathbb{E}^{\prime}\left[f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{i-1},X^{\prime}_{i},\ldots X^{\prime}_{n})\right]\right|\\
&\quad\leq\mathbb{E}^{\prime}\left[\left|f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{i},X^{\prime}_{i+1},\ldots X^{\prime}_{n})-f(X_{1},\ldots,X_{i-1},X^{\prime}_{i},\ldots X^{\prime}_{n})\right|\right]\\
&\quad\leq\mathbb{E}^{\prime}\left[g_{i}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{i},X^{\prime}_{i+1},\ldots,X^{\prime}_{n})+g_{i}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{i-1},X^{\prime}_{i},\ldots,X^{\prime}_{n})\right]\\
&\quad=\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[g_{i}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{{i-1}}\left[g_{i}\right].\end{split}

Using these inequalities in (
A.1
) yields

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\left[|f(X)-\mathbb{E}\left[f(X)\right]|^{q}\right]&\leq C(q)\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[g_{i}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{{i-1}}\left[g_{i}\right]\right)^{2}\right)^{q/2}\right]\\
&\leq C(q)n^{q/2-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[g_{i}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{{i-1}}\left[g_{i}\right]\right)^{q}\right]\\
&\leq\tilde{C}(q)n^{q/2-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}_{i}\left[g_{i}\right]^{q}+\mathbb{E}_{{i-1}}\left[g_{i}\right]^{q}\right]\\
&\leq 2\tilde{C}(q)n^{q/2-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{i}^{q}\right],\end{split}

hence the thesis.
∎
