New lower bound on ball packing density in high-dimensional hyperbolic spaces
Irene Gil Fernández, Jaehoon Kim, Hong Liu, Oleg Pikhurko

Abstract
We present a new lower bound on the Bowen–Radin maximal density of radius- R R ball packings in the m m -dimensional hyperbolic space, improving on the basic covering bound by factor Ω ⁡ ( m ⁡ ( R + ln ⁡ m ) ) \Omega(m(R+\ln m)) as m m tends to infinity. This is done by applying the recent theorem of Campos, Jenssen, Michelen and Sahasrabudhe on independent sets in graphs with sparse neighbourhoods.



1
Introduction

Let
R>0
be a positive real and let
(V,d)
be a metric space endowed with a Borel non-zero measure
\mu
.
An
R
-packing
in
V

is a subset
X\subseteq V
such that any two distinct points of
X
are at distance at least
2R
. If the measure
\mu
is
finite
(that is,
\mu(V)<\infty
), then we define the
density
of an
R
-packing
X
by

D_{R}(X):=\frac{\mu\left(B_{R}(X)\right)}{\mu(V)},

where we denote

B_{R}(X):=\{y\in V:\exists\,x\in X,\ d(x,y)\leqslant R\}.

In other words,
D_{R}(X)
is the fraction of the measure space
V
covered by closed radius-
R
balls around the points of
X
. The problem of determining or estimating the
R
-packing density

D_{R}(V)
of a given metric space
V
(that is, the supremum of the densities of
R
-packings in
V
) is the archetypical problem of coding theory which also has a large number of applications to other fields.

One important case is when
V={\mathbbm{R}}^{m}
, endowed with the Euclidean distance
and the Lebesgue (uniform) measure.
Since the measure is not finite here, one defines the
R
-packing density

D({\mathbbm{R}}^{m})
as the limit of the packing densities of growing cubes that exhaust
{\mathbbm{R}}^{m}
:

D({\mathbbm{R}}^{m}):=\lim_{n\to\infty}D_{R}\left(\,[-n,n]^{m}\,\right).

(1)

It is easy to see that the limit exists and, by the scaling properties of the Lebesgue measure, does not depend on the radius
R
.

It is clear that the packing density of the real line
D({\mathbbm{R}}^{1})=1
.
The packing density of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{m}
for
m=2,3,8,24
was determined by respectively Thue
[
30
]
, Hales
[
21
]
, Viazovska
[
34
]
, and Cohn, Kumar, Miller, Radchenko and Viazovska
[
12
]
.
The value of
D({\mathbbm{R}}^{m})
is still unknown for any other
m
, although the bounds for
m=4
(from
[
25
,
10
]
) are rather close to each other. We refer the reader to Cohn
[
14
]
for the table of the known bounds for
m\leqslant 48
.

When
m\to\infty
, the best known upper and lower bounds on
D({\mathbbm{R}}^{m})
are exponentially far apart. The best known upper bound
D({\mathbbm{R}}^{m})\leqslant 2^{-(0.5990...+o(1))\,m}
is due to Kabatjanskiĭ and Levenšteĭn
[
24
]
, see also
[
11
,
13
]
.

The trivial
covering lower bound

D({\mathbbm{R}}^{m})\geqslant 2^{-m}
(take a maximal packing and observe that balls of doubled radius cover the whole space) was improved by a factor of
\Omega(m)
by Rogers
[
29
]
. After a sequence of improvements in the constant factor
[
15
,
1
,
31
,
33
]
, Venkatesh
[
33
]
was the first to beat the
\Omega(\frac{m}{2^{m}})
lower bound by showing that
D({\mathbbm{R}}^{m})=\Omega(\frac{m\,\ln\ln m}{2^{m}})
for some infinite (sparse) sequence of dimensions
m
. In a recent breakthrough, Campos, Jenssen, Michelen and Sahasrabudhe
[
8
]
proved that
D({\mathbbm{R}}^{m})\geqslant(\frac{1}{2}-o(1))\,\frac{m\,\ln m}{2^{m}}
. This was done by discretising the problem and then applying their new result (which is stated as Theorem
12
here) on the existence of a large independent set in graphs with sparse neighbourhoods.

The aim of this paper is to observe that the method from
[
8
]
also applies to ball packing in hyperbolic spaces. Let
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
denote the
m
-dimensional hyperbolic space, equipped with the standard metric
d_{m}

and the standard (isometry invariant) measure
\mu_{m}
.
(See Section
2
for all formal definitions.)

The study of packings in
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
was held back for a long while by the absence of a good definition of the maximum packing density, see e.g. the discussion in
[
18
, Pages 831–834]
.
Analogues of (
1
) lack many desired properties because growing polygons or balls in
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
have most of their mass near their boundary.
So some “local” approaches were considered; see
[
19
, Chapter 11]
for a survey of known results from this point of view.
However, even here one has to be careful in view of the following striking example of Böröczky
[
3
]
(also described on Page 833 of
[
18
]
). Namely, Böröczky showed that
there is an
R
-packing
X\subseteq{\mathbbm{H}}^{2}
in the hyperbolic plane and two tilings
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{2}
of
{\mathbbm{H}}^{2}
by single polygonal tiles
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
respectively such that each tile of
{\mathcal{T}}_{i}
contains one radius-
R
disk centred at a point of
X
and is disjoint from all other such disks; at the same time,
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
have different areas. Thus if we measure the “density” of this packing
X
using the fraction occupied by the corresponding disks within each tile of
{\mathcal{T}}_{1}
(resp.
{\mathcal{T}}_{2}
), then we get two different numbers.

A solution was proposed by Bowen and Radin
[
5
, Definition 5]
who presented a new notion of density where, roughly speaking, one considers probability distributions on
R
-packings in
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
that are invariant under isometries and maximises the probability that a fixed point is covered by a ball. This parameter has many nice properties and is the one that will be used in this paper as the definition of the
R
-packing density
of
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
and thus denoted by
D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})
. We refer the reader to Section
3
for the formal definition of
D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})
and to
[
27
,
6
]
for further discussions and motivation behind it.

For dimension
m=2
and countably many radii
R
, Bowen and Radin
[
5
, Theorem 2]
were able to determine
D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{2})
. Namely, for every integer
n>6
, if we tile
{\mathbbm{H}}^{2}
by (equilateral) triangles having all three angles
2\pi/n
and take a “uniform shift” of the set of the triangles’ vertices then this distribution attains
D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{2})
, where
R=R_{n}
is the half of the side length of the triangles.
As far as we know, these are the only pairs
(m,R)
with
m\geqslant 2
for which the exact value of
D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})
is known.

Let us discuss the known upper bounds on
D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})
for
m\to\infty
.
Before Bowen and Radin’s paper
[
5
]
, Fejes Tóth
[
20
]
(for
m=2
), Böröczky and Florian
[
2
]
(for
m=3
) and Böröczky
[
4
]
(any
m
) proved that for every
R
-packing
X\subseteq{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
and any
x\in X
the fraction of volume occupied by the
R
-ball

B_{R}(x):=B_{R}(\{x\})
around
x

inside the Dirichlet-Voronoi cell of
x\in X
is at most
\delta_{m}(2R)
, which is defined to be the fraction of volume of a regular simplex of side length
2R
occupied by (touching) balls of radius
R
at its vertices. It follows from these results (see Lemma
4
here) that
D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})\leqslant\delta_{m}(2R)
.
More recently, Cohn and Zhao
[
13
, Theorem 4.1]
showed that, independently of
R>0
,
the Bowen–Radin density satisfies

D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})\leqslant\inf_{\pi/3\leqslant\theta\leqslant\pi}\sin^{m-1}(\theta/2)A(m,\theta),

(2)

where
A(m,\theta)
be the maximum number of unit vectors in the Euclidean space
{\mathbbm{R}}^{m}
such that the scalar product of every two is at most
\cos\theta
; in other words,
A(m,\theta)
is the maximum size of a spherical code with minimum angle
\theta
. In particular, the method of Kabatjanskiĭ and Levenšteĭn
[
24
]
(that also applies to spherical codes) gives that

D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})\leqslant 2^{-(0.5990...+o(1))\,m},

(3)

see
[
13
, Corollary 4.2]
. For large
m
, this bound is smaller than
\delta_{m}(0):=\lim_{R\to 0}\delta_{m}(R)
, which can be shown to be the volume ratio coming from
m+1
touching unit balls in
{\mathbbm{R}}^{m}
. Note that Marshall
[
26
, Theorem 2]
proved that, for all large
m
, the function
\delta_{m}(R)
is strictly increasing in
R
(and thus
\delta_{m}(0)\leqslant\delta_{m}(R)
for any
R>0
for such
m
).

The covering principle (that if
X
is a maximal
R
-packing then
2R
-balls around points of
X
cover the whole space) translates with some work (see Lemma
8
) into the basic lower bound

D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})\geqslant L_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m}):=\frac{\mu_{m}(B_{R})}{\mu_{m}(B_{2R})},

(4)

where
\mu_{m}(B_{r})
denotes the volume of some (equivalently, any)
r
-ball in
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
.

There are various constructions of packings in small dimensions (see e.g.
[
19
, Chapter 11]
for references) and it is plausible that their “locally” measured densities translate into lower bounds on the Bowen-Radin density. However, we are not aware of any improvements to (
4
) for large
m
apart that, as far as we can see, the method of Jenssen, Joos and Perkins
[
22
,
23
]
, which is based on the hard-sphere model of statistical physics, can be applied to improve the bound in (
4
) by factor
\Omega(m)
. Anyway, this is superseded by the main result of this paper as follows.

Theorem 1

For every
\varepsilon>0
there is
m_{0}
such that for any
m\geqslant m_{0}
it holds for every
R\in(0,\infty)
that

D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)\,m\ln\left(\sqrt{m}\cosh(2R)\right)\frac{\mu_{m}(B_{R})}{\mu_{m}(B_{2R})},

where
\ln
is the natural logarithm and
\cosh
is the hyperbolic cosine.

Note that
\ln(\cosh(2R))
is at least
2R-1
, so Theorem
1
improves the bound in (
4
) by factor at least

\Omega(m(R+\ln m))
. We prove Theorem
1
by reducing the lower bound problem to finding a packing in some finite-volume space (namely, the quotient of
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
by a large-girth lattice) and then (as it was done in
[
8
]
) discretising the problem by taking a Poisson point process.

Organisation of the paper.
We recall some notions related to
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
in Section
2
and define the Bowen-Radin density in Section
3
. We describe the method from
[
5
]
of lower bounding
D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})
via
R
-packings in some finite-volume space
M
in Section
4
. Various estimates are collected in Section
5
. Finally, Theorem
1
is proved in Section
6
.
Since
m
and
R
will be reserved, respectively, for the dimension of the studied hyperbolic space and the packing radius, we may omit them from our notation if the meaning is clear.

2
Hyperbolic space

This section contains just a bare minimum of material sufficient to formally define the hyperbolic space
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
and some needed related notions. For a detailed introduction to hyperbolic spaces see
e.g. Bridson and Haefliger
[
7
, Section 2]
or Ratcliffe
[
28
]
.

One representation
of
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
(for more details see e.g.
[
28
, Chapter 3]
) is to take the bilinear form

\langle u,v\rangle_{m,1}:=-u_{m+1}v_{m+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}v_{i},\quad u,v\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{m+1},

(5)

identify
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
with upper sheet of the hyperboloid

{\mathcal{H}}:=\{u\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{m+1}:\langle u,u\rangle_{m,1}=-1\}

(namely, the sheet where
u_{m+1}>0
), and
define the metric
d_{m}
by
\cosh d_{m}(u,v)=-\langle u,v\rangle_{m,1}
for
u,v\in{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
.
The group of isometries of
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}

can be identified with
O(m,1)_{0}
, the group of
(m+1)\times(m+1)
-matrices
A
which leave
the bilinear form in (
5
) invariant
and do not swap the two sheets of
{\mathcal{H}}
; see
[
7
, Theorem 2.24]
or
[
28
, Theorem 3.2.3]
.

Let
{\mathcal{G}}
be the group of orientation-preserving isometries of
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
; it corresponds to the
subgroup
\mathrm{SO}(m,1)_{0}
of index 2 in
O(m,1)_{0}
, which
consists of
matrices with determinant 1. It is a topological group with the topology inherited from
O(m,1)_{0}\subseteq{\mathbbm{R}}^{(m+1)^{2}}
.

We fix one point of
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
, say
(0,\dots,0,1)\in{\mathbbm{R}}^{m+1}
in the above representation, which we will denote by
{\mathcal{O}}
and refer to as the
origin
.

The space
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
is equipped with a Borel isometry-invariant measure
\mu_{m}
whose push-forward
under the projection on the first
m
coordinates of
{\mathbbm{R}}^{m+1}
has density

(1+(x_{1}^{2}+\hskip 0.9pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 0.3pt.\hskip 1.5pt+x_{m}^{2}))^{-1/2}

with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
{\mathbbm{R}}^{m}
.

The group
{\mathcal{G}}
is unimodular and locally compact, so there is a Haar measure
\mu_{{\mathcal{G}}}
on
{\mathcal{G}}
(which is both right- and left-invariant). It is a standard result (see e.g.
[
28
, Lemma 4 of Section 11.6]
) that, by scaling
\mu_{{\mathcal{G}}}
, we can assume that the projection map
\pi_{{\mathcal{O}}}:({\mathcal{G}},\mu_{{\mathcal{G}}})\to({\mathbbm{H}}^{m},\mu_{m})
, where
\gamma\mapsto\gamma.{\mathcal{O}}
, is measure-preserving.

We consider the natural (left) action
{\mathcal{G}}{\curvearrowright}{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
. For
\gamma\in{\mathcal{G}}
and
A\subseteq{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
, we denote

\gamma.A:=\{\gamma.x:x\in A\}
. For
x\in{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
, let

\Sigma_{x}:=\{\gamma\in{\mathcal{G}}:\gamma.x=x\}

be the
stabiliser
of
x
. In particular, we have the natural homeomorphism of topological spaces
{\mathcal{G}}/\Sigma_{{\mathcal{O}}}\cong{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
.

3
Definition of the Bowen–Radin density

In this section, we give the definition of the packing density
D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})
introduced by Bowen and Radin
[
5
]
. We also state some results from
[
5
]
, occasionally providing more details (usually when these were implicitly assumed but not stated in
[
5
]
).

Let
R>0
be a positive real. Let
S_{R}
consist of
R
-packings
X\subseteq{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
that are
relatively dense
, that is, such that, for every
x\in{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
, the (closed radius-
2R
) ball
B_{2R}(x)
around
x
contains at least one element of
X
.
This is slightly weaker than the notion of a maximal packing (namely, a relatively dense packing can have the distance from some
x
to
X
exactly
2R
and thus not be a maximal one).

Bowen and Radin
[
5
]
considered the following metric
d_{R}
on
S_{R}
:

d_{R}(X,Y):=\sup_{r\in[1,\infty)}\frac{1}{r}\,h\big(B_{r}({\mathcal{O}})\cap X,B_{r}({\mathcal{O}})\cap Y\big),\quad X,Y\in S_{R},

(6)

where

h(A,B):=\max\left\{\sup_{a\in A}\inf_{b\in B}d_{m}(a,b),\ \sup_{b\in B}\inf_{a\in A}d_{m}(a,b)\right\},\quad A,B\subseteq{\mathbbm{H}}^{m},

is the usual
Hausdorff distance
on the subsets of
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
. As noted in
[
5
, Page 25]
, the metric space
(S_{R},d_{R})
is compact. Let
{\mathcal{M}}(R)
be the set of Borel probability measures on
(S_{R},d_{R})
.

The group
{\mathcal{G}}
naturally acts on
S_{R}
and the corresponding map
{\mathcal{G}}\times S_{R}\to S_{R}
is continuous.
A measure
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}(R)
is called
{\mathcal{G}}
-invariant
if for every
\gamma\in{\mathcal{G}}
and every Borel set
E\subseteq S_{R}
we have
\mu(\gamma.E)=\mu(E)
. Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{I}(R)
consist of all
{\mathcal{G}}
-invariant measures in
{\mathcal{M}}(R)
. Let
{\mathcal{M}}_{I}^{\mathbf{e}}(R)
consist of those measures
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{I}(R)
that are
ergodic
, that is, are extreme points of the convex set
{\mathcal{M}}_{I}(R)
.

For
p\in{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
, define

F_{p}(X):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\mbox{if $d_{m}(x,X)\leqslant R$,}\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise},\end{array}\right.\quad\text{ for }X\in S_{R},

to be the indicator function that
R
-balls around
X
cover
p
.

Lemma 2

For every
p\in{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
, the function
F_{p}
is a Borel function on
(S_{R},d_{R})
.

Proof.
Let us show that
F_{p}^{-1}(1)=\{X\in S_{R}:F_{p}(X)=1\}
is a closed subset of
(S_{R},d_{R})
. Take any sequence
(X_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
F_{p}^{-1}(1)
convergent to some
X\in S_{R}
. Each
X_{n}
has a point
x_{n}
in
B_{R}(p)
. By the compactness of the closed ball
B_{R}(p)
, we can pass to a subsequence of
n
so that
x_{n}
converges to some
x\in B_{R}(p)
as
n\to\infty
. Since
X
has at most one point in
B_{R/2}(x)
, it follows from the definition of the Hausdorff distance by taking any
r>d_{m}(x,{\mathcal{O}})
in (
6
) that
x\in X
. Thus
F_{p}(X)=1
and the set
F_{p}^{-1}(1)
is closed (and thus Borel).

Since the function
F_{p}
is
\{0,1\}
-valued and the pre-image
F_{p}^{-1}(1)
is closed, the function
F_{p}
is Borel.

Thus for any
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{I}(R)
, we can define the
average density

D(\mu):=\int_{S_{R}}F_{{\mathcal{O}}}(X)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(X).

(7)

By the
{\mathcal{G}}
-invariance of
\mu
and the transitivity of
{\mathcal{G}}{\curvearrowright}{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
, we could have taken any other point of
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
instead of the origin in this definition, without changing the value.

Following Bowen and Radin
[
5
, Definition 5]
, we define the
R
-packing density of
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
as

D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m}):=\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{I}^{\mathbf{e}}(R)}D(\mu).

(8)

We refer the reader to
[
5
]
and also to
[
6
,
27
]
for discussion and further properties of this parameter. For example,
[
5
, Theorem 1]
implies that the supremum in (
8
) is attained by some measure
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{I}^{\mathbf{e}}(R)
.

Let us show that the definition in in (
8
) is not affected if we take the supremum over invariant (not necessarily ergodic) measures.

Lemma 3

For every
R\in(0,\infty)
and
m\in{\mathbbm{N}}
, we have that
D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})=\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{I}(R)}D(\mu)
.

Proof.
Trivially,
D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})\leqslant\sup_{\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{I}(R)}D(\mu)
, so let us show the converse
inequality.

The ergodic invariant decomposition theorem of Varadarajan
[
32
, Theorem 4.2]
(see also Farrell
[
16
, Theorem 5]
for a similar result) applies to Borel actions of locally compact groups on standard Borel spaces. When applied to
the action
{\mathcal{G}}{\curvearrowright}S_{R}
, it gives a Borel map
\beta:S_{R}\to{\mathcal{M}}_{I}^{\mathbf{e}}(R)
which is constant on every orbit of
{\mathcal{G}}
such that every measure
\nu
in the image of
\beta
assigns value
1
to the pre-image
\beta^{-1}(\nu)\subseteq S_{R}
and every
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{I}(R)
satisfies

\mu(A)=\int_{S_{R}}\beta(X)(A)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(X),\quad\mbox{for every Borel $A\subseteq S_{R}$}.

(9)

Take any
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{I}(R)
and let
A
be
\{X\in S_{R}:{\mathcal{O}}\in B_{R}(X)\}=F_{{\mathcal{O}}}^{-1}(1)
. By Lemma
2
,
A
is a Borel subset of
S_{R}
. Note that
\mu(A)=D(\mu)
. Thus, by (
9
) applied to
\mu
and
A
,
D(\mu)
is some average of
D(\nu)
over ergodic invariant measures
\nu=\beta({X})
for
X\in S_{R}
. Thus there is
\nu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{I}^{\mathbf{e}}(R)
with
D(\nu)\geqslant D(\mu)
and we have
D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})\geqslant D(\mu)
.
Since
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{I}(R)
was arbitrary, the desired inequality follows.

Also, it is plausible that one can replace
S_{R}
by the space of arbitrary (not necessarily relatively dense)
R
-packings by completing a random
R
-packing into a relatively dense one in a measurable and invariant way. However, this would require some extra work (and the definition of the distance in (
6
) would need some tweaking) so we stay with the definitions from
[
5
]
.

At this point, let us observe
that the classical “local” upper bounds from
[
20
,
2
,
4
]
also apply to
D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})
.

Lemma 4

For any real
R\geqslant 0
and integer
m
, we have
D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})\leqslant\delta_{m}(2R)
.

Proof.
Take any
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}_{I}^{\mathbf{e}}(R)
. Bowen and Radin
[
5
, Proposition 3]
showed that
D(\mu)
can be computed as the expectation over a random packing
X
distributed according to
\mu
of the ratio of volume of
B_{R}({\mathcal{O}})
to the volume of the Dirichlet-Voronoi cell of
X
containing the origin
{\mathcal{O}}\in{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
in its interior. (Note that, by the invariance of
\mu
, the probability that
{\mathcal{O}}
lies on the boundary of a Dirichlet-Voronoi cell of
X
is zero.)

By the results from
[
20
,
2
,
4
]

mentioned in the Introduction, the integrated
function is always at most
\delta_{m}(2R)
, so its average is at most
\delta_{m}(2R)
, as desired.

4
Lower bounds via a finite-volume space

Here we describe the method, implicit in
[
5
]
, of proving lower bounds on
D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})
coming from ball packings in some finite-volume space. We use (as in
[
17
]
) the following definition of a lattice which is stronger than the standard one.
Namely, let us call a subgroup

{\mathcal{L}}\subseteq{\mathcal{G}}
a
(uniform torsion-free) lattice
if (i) there is
g>0
such that for every
x\in{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
and every non-identity
\gamma\in{\mathcal{L}}
we have
d_{m}(x,\gamma.x)\geqslant g
; (ii) the quotient space
M:={\mathbbm{H}}^{m}/{\mathcal{L}}
is compact. Denote the supremum of
g
that work in (i) as

Various estimates

We will use various facts about hyperbolic functions
\cosh x:=({\mathrm{e}}^{x}+{\mathrm{e}}^{-x})/2
,
\sinh x:=({\mathrm{e}}^{x}-{\mathrm{e}}^{-x})/2
,
\tanh x:=\frac{\sinh x}{\cosh x}
and
\coth x:=1/\tanh x
. First, we have
\cosh^{2}x=1+\sinh^{2}x
. The following formulas for angle doubling are easy to check directly:

\cosh(2x)=2\cosh^{2}x-1\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\sinh(2x)=2\sinh x\cosh x.

Also, we will use the monotonicity of the above hyperbolic functions on
[0,\infty)
, approximations
\tanh x,\sinh x=(1+o(1))x
for
x\to 0
and the following inequalities that are routine to check:

\tanh x<1,\ \ 2\sinh x\leqslant\sinh(2x),\ \ \tanh(2x)\leqslant 2\tanh x,\quad\mbox{for $x\in[0,\infty)$.}

(11)

Furthermore, we will use the following formula (see e.g.
[
28
, Theorem 5.3.5]
for a proof).

Lemma 9
(The First Law of Cosines)

If
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
are the angles of a hyperbolic triangle and
a,b,c
are the lengths of the opposite sides, then

\cos\gamma=\frac{\cosh a\cosh b-\cosh c}{\sinh a\sinh b}.

(12)

Lemma 10

Let
x,u\in{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
be two distinct points and let
\tau:=d_{m}(x,u)>0
. Let
r>0

satisfy
\tau<2r
.
Then the intersection
B_{r}(x)\cap B_{r}(u)
is contained in (and thus has volume at most as that of) a hyperbolic ball of radius
\sigma=\sigma(\tau,r)
, where
\sigma>0
is defined by

\sinh^{2}(\sigma)=\sinh^{2}(r)-\cosh^{2}(r)\tanh^{2}\left(\tau/2\right).

(13)

Proof.
Let
w
be the point on the geodesic line through
x
and
u
such that
d_{m}(x,w)=d_{m}(w,u)
. We will show that
B_{\sigma}(w)
works in the lemma.
Let

be any point at distance

from

and
r_{2}
from
u
for some
r_{1},r_{2}\leqslant r
. By the convexity of
B_{r}(x)\cap B_{r}(u)
, we can assume that
y
is not equal to
x
nor
u
, i.e. that
r_{1},r_{2}>0
.
Consider the hyperbolic triangle with the vertices
x,y,u
and let
\alpha
be the angle at the vertex
u
in this triangle. Let
\rho=d_{m}(y,w)
be the distance between
y
and
w
.

Applying Lemma

to the hyperbolic triangle
xyu
and the hyperbolic triangle

, we obtain

\cos\alpha=\frac{\cosh\tau\cosh r_{2}-\cosh r_{1}}{\sinh\tau\sinh r_{2}}\kern 5.0pt\text{ and }\kern 5.0pt\cos\alpha=\frac{\cosh(\tau/2)\cosh r_{2}-\cosh\rho}{\sinh(\tau/2)\sinh r_{2}}.

As these two expressions have the equal value, using the equalities
\sinh\tau=2\sinh(\tau/2)\cosh(\tau/2)
and
\cosh\tau=2\cosh^{2}(\tau/2)-1
,
one can obtain

\displaystyle\cosh\rho

\displaystyle=\cosh(\tau/2)\cos r_{2}-\frac{1}{2\cosh(\tau/2)}\left(\cosh\tau\cosh r_{2}-\cosh r_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\cosh(\tau/2)\cosh r_{2}-\frac{(2\cosh^{2}(\tau/2)-1)\cosh r_{2}-\cosh r_{1}}{2\cosh(\tau/2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\cosh r_{2}+\cosh r_{1}}{2\cosh(\tau/2)}\leqslant\frac{\cosh r}{\cosh(\tau/2)}.

The final inequality holds as

is an increasing function of

and
r_{1},r_{2}\leqslant r
.
This yields

\displaystyle\sinh^{2}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\cosh^{2}(\rho)-1\leqslant\frac{\cosh^{2}(r)}{\cosh^{2}(\tau/2)}-1=\cosh^{2}(r)-1-\frac{\cosh^{2}(r)(\cosh^{2}(\tau/2)-1)}{\cosh^{2}(\tau/2)}

\displaystyle=\sinh^{2}(r)-\cosh^{2}(r)\tanh^{2}(\tau/2).

As
\sinh^{2}(x)
is an increasing function on

, this shows that

is at most
\sigma
. This proves that any point

has distance at most

from

, as desired.

The volume of an

-ball in

is given by the following formula (see e.g.

\mu_{m}(B_{r})=\mathrm{vol}(S^{m-1})\int_{0}^{r}\sinh^{m-1}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\eta,

Recall that

Moreover, we have for

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{r}\sinh^{m-1}\eta\,\mathrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle=(1+O({\mathrm{e}}^{-r(m-1)/2}))\int_{r/2}^{r}\left(\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{\eta}-{\mathrm{e}}^{-\eta}}{2}\right)^{m-1}\,\mathrm{d}\eta

\displaystyle=(1+O(m{\mathrm{e}}^{-r}))\int_{r/2}^{r}\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{(m-1)\eta}}{2^{m-1}}\,\mathrm{d}\eta=(1+O(m{\mathrm{e}}^{-r}))\,\frac{e^{(m-1)r}}{(m-1)2^{m-1}}.

\mu_{m}(B_{r})=(1+O(m{\mathrm{e}}^{-r}))\frac{{\mathrm{e}}^{(m-1)r}}{(m-1)2^{m-1}}\,\mathrm{vol}(S^{m-1})={\mathrm{e}}^{(m-1)r-(1/2+o_{r}(1))m\ln m}.

(14)

by (
19
), contradicting
\ln(\frac{\sinh(2R)}{\tau})=\omega(m)
. We obtain from (
19
) that
m=\Theta\left((R/\tau)^{2}\ln\ln(R/\tau)\right)
. Since
m\to\infty
, it follows that
R/\tau\to\infty
and thus
R/\tau=\Theta\left(\sqrt{m/\ln\ln m}\right)
, which again contradicts our assumption
\kappa\neq O(\ln m)
.

Now we define the parameters
\Delta
and
\lambda
as follows:

\Delta:=\frac{1}{m^{4}}\frac{\mu(B_{2R})}{\mu(B_{\tau})}\text{ and }\lambda:=\frac{\Delta}{\mu(B_{2R})}.

Recall that
\mu=\mu_{M}
denotes the measure on
M
and that, for every
0\leqslant r\leqslant 4R
, it assigns the same measure to an
r
-ball in
M
as the hyperbolic measure
\mu_{m}
assigns to an
r
-ball in
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
by the girth assumption on
{\mathcal{L}}
.

Let us show that

\displaystyle\ln\Delta=(1+o(1))m\ln(\sqrt{m}\cosh(2R)).

(20)

Indeed, if
R<m
then (
20
) is a consequence of Lemma
11
and Claim
12.1
, so suppose that
R\geqslant m
. Then we have
\tau>\ln m
and
\sinh\tau\leqslant\cosh\tau\leqslant 2\cosh^{2}(\tau/2)
, thus (
17
) implies

0<\ln(\sinh\tau)=O(\ln R+\ln m)=o(\ln(\sinh(2R))).

Thus
Lemma
11
implies that

\displaystyle\ln\Delta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(m+O(1))(\ln(\sinh(2R))-\ln(\sinh\tau))+O(\ln m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+o(1))m\ln(\sinh(2R)),

giving (
20
) since
|\sinh(2R)-\cosh(2R)|\leqslant 1
and

\ln(\sqrt{m})=o(\ln(\cosh(2R)))
.

In particular, we have that
\Delta\to\infty
as
m\to\infty
.

With these choices, we have the following lemma. Recall that
G_{Y}
is the graph on
Y
whose edge set is defined by (
16
).

Lemma 13

There exists
Y\subseteq M
such that

|Y|\geqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\frac{\Delta}{\mu(B_{2R})}\,\mu(M)

and the graph
G_{Y}
satisfies that

\Delta(G_{Y})\leqslant\Delta+\Delta^{2/3}\text{ and }\Delta_{2}(G_{Y})\leqslant\Delta\,(\ln\Delta)^{-10}.

With this lemma and Theorem
12
, we obtain that there is an
R
-packing in
M
of size at least

(1-o(1))\frac{|Y|\ln\left(\Delta+\Delta^{2/3}\right)}{\Delta+\Delta^{2/3}}\geqslant(1-1/m-o(1))\frac{\mu(M)\ln\Delta}{\mu(B_{2R})}.

Thus (
20
) implies that, as
m\to\infty
,

D_{R}({\mathbbm{H}}^{m})\geqslant D_{R}(M)\geqslant(1-o(1))\ln\Delta\cdot\frac{\mu(B_{R})}{\mu(B_{2R})}\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)m\ln(\sqrt{m}\cosh(2R))\frac{\mu(B_{R})}{\mu(B_{2R})}.

This proves (
15
) (that is, Theorem
1
). Thus it remains to prove Lemma
13
.

Proof of Lemma
13
.

We follow the argument in Section 2 of
[
8
]
.
In order to prove Lemma
13
, we sample a Poisson point process
X\subseteq M
with the intensity
\lambda\,\mu
,
and obtain a desired set
Y\subseteq X
by removing points
x\in X
which satisfy at least one of the following two conditions:

\displaystyle|X\cap B_{2R}(x)|\geqslant\Delta+\Delta^{2/3}\text{,\ \ or\ \ $\exists\,y\in X$\ \ }|X\cap B_{2R}(x)\cap B_{2R}(y)|\geqslant\Delta\,(\ln\Delta)^{-10}.

(21)

We will show that
Y
has almost the same size as
X
.
The following
identity of Mecke
will be useful: for any bounded measurable set
\Lambda\subseteq M
and events
(A_{x})_{x\in\Lambda}
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\{x\in X\cap\Lambda:A_{x}\text{ holds for }X\}\right|=\lambda\int_{\Lambda}\mathbb{P}[\,A_{x}\text{ holds for }X\cup\{x\}\,]\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x).

(22)

Also, the following tail bound for a Poisson random variable
Z
will be used:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\,Z-\mathbb{E}Z\geqslant t\,\mathbb{E}Z\,]\leqslant\exp\left(-\min\{t,t^{2}\}\frac{\mathbb{E}Z}{3}\right).

(23)

First, we show the following claim stating that, on average, only a small fraction of
x\in X
satisfies the first bad condition in (
21
).

Claim 13.1

Let
X\sim\mathrm{Po}_{\lambda}(M)
. Then

\mathbb{E}|\{x\in X:|X\cap B_{2R}(x)|\geqslant\Delta+\Delta^{2/3}\}|\leqslant\frac{1}{m^{2}}\,\mathbb{E}|X|.

Proof.
Take any
x\in M
.
Recall that
|X\cap B_{2R}(x)|
is a Poisson random variable of mean
\lambda\mu(B_{2R})=\Delta
. Thus using (
23
), we have

\mathbb{P}[\,|X\cap B_{2R}(x)|\geqslant\Delta+\Delta^{2/3}-1\,]\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{1}{4}\Delta^{1/3}\right)\leqslant m^{-2}.

Thus we have by Mecke’s identity that

\mathbb{E}\,\left|\{x\in X:|X\cap B_{2R}(x)|\geqslant\Delta+\Delta^{2/3}\}\right|=\lambda\int_{M}\mathbb{P}[\,|X\cap B_{2R}(x)|\geqslant\Delta+\Delta^{2/3}-1\,]\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\leqslant\frac{\lambda\,\mu(M)}{m^{2}},

giving the desired by
\mathbb{E}|X|=\lambda\,\mu(M)
.

Before considering the second bad condition in (
21
), we prove the following claim.

Claim 13.2

For any
x,y\in M
at distance
d_{M}(x,y)\geqslant\tau
, it holds that

\lambda\,\mu(B_{2R}(x)\cap B_{2R}(y))\leqslant\Delta\,(\ln\Delta)^{-15}.

Proof.
Note that we only have to consider the case where
\tau
is at most
4R
, as otherwise
B_{2R}(x)\cap B_{2R}(y)
is empty.
As the lattice
{\mathcal{L}}
has girth at least
16R
, Lemma
10
with
r=2R
applies to the points
x,y
in
M
(instead of
{\mathbbm{H}}^{m}
). Hence we obtain that the following where
\sigma=\sigma(2R,\tau)\in(0,2R)
was defined in (
13
):

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lambda\,\mu(B_{2R}(x)\cap B_{2R}(y))&\leqslant\lambda\,\mu(B_{\sigma})\leqslant\Delta\,\frac{\mu(B_{\sigma})}{\mu(B_{2R})}\\
&\leqslant\Delta\,\frac{\sinh^{m-1}(\sigma)}{\sinh^{m-1}(2R)}=\Delta\,\left(1-\coth^{2}(2R)\tanh^{2}\left(\tau/2\right)\right)^{(m-1)/2}.\end{split}

(24)

Here, the penultimate inequality is from Lemma
11
.

Recall that
\tau
satisfies
\gamma(\tau)=0
where
\gamma
was defined in (
17
). If
R<m
, then the final expression in (
24
) becomes

\displaystyle\Delta\left(1-50\cdot\frac{\ln m+\ln\ln(\frac{\sinh 2R}{\sinh\tau})}{m}\right)^{(m-1)/2}\leqslant\Delta\,\frac{1}{m^{20}}\cdot\ln\left(\frac{\sinh 2R}{\sinh\tau}\right)^{-20}\leqslant\Delta\,(\ln\Delta)^{-15},

as we want. Here, the final inequality holds as Lemma
11
implies
\ln\Delta\leqslant m\ln(\frac{\sinh 2R}{\sinh\tau})
.
If
R>m
, then
as
\coth^{2}(2R)\tanh^{2}(\tau/2)\geqslant\tanh^{2}(\tau/2)=1-\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(\tau/2)}
,
the final term in (
24
) is bounded from above by

\displaystyle\Delta\left(\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}{(\tau/2)}}\right)^{(m-1)/2}

\displaystyle=\Delta\left(\frac{1}{m\ln(\cosh(2R))}\right)^{(m-1)/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\Delta\,(\ln\Delta)^{-(m-1)/4}\leqslant\Delta\,(\ln\Delta)^{-15},

where the penultimate inequality follows from (
20
).
This proves the claim.

Now we bound the number of points satisfying the second bad condition in (
21
).

Claim 13.3

Let
X\sim\mathrm{Po}_{\lambda}(M)
and put
\eta:=(\ln\Delta)^{-10}
. Then we have that

\mathbb{E}|\{x\in X:|X\cap B_{2R}(x)\cap B_{2R}(y)|\geqslant\eta\Delta\text{ for some }y\in X\}|\leqslant\frac{1}{2m}\,\mathbb{E}|X|.

Proof.
Take any
x\in M
. For
y\in M
, let
I_{x,y}:=|X\cap B_{2R}(x)\cap B_{2R}(y)|
.
Using Markov’s inequality, we have

\mathbb{P}[\,\exists y\in X:I_{x,y}\geqslant\eta\Delta-1\,]\leqslant\mathbb{E}\,|B_{\tau}(x)\cap X|+\mathbb{E}\,|\{y\in X\setminus B_{\tau}(x):I_{x,y}\geqslant\eta\Delta-1\}|.

We bound each of these two terms.

For the first term, using Lemma
11
and the definition of
\Delta
, we have

\mathbb{E}\,|B_{\tau}(x)\cap X|\leqslant\lambda\,\mu(B_{\tau}(x))\leqslant\frac{\Delta\,\mu(B_{\tau})}{\mu(B_{2R})}=\frac{1}{m^{4}}.

For the second term, we only need to consider
y\in B_{4R}(x)
as otherwise
I_{x,y}=0<\eta\Delta-1
. (Note that
\eta\Delta\to\infty
.)
Using (
22
) and Markov’s inequality, the second term is

\displaystyle\lambda\,

\displaystyle\int_{M\setminus B_{\tau}(x)}\mathbb{P}[\,I_{x,y}\geqslant\eta\Delta-2\,]\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)=\lambda\,\int_{B_{4R}(x)\setminus B_{\tau}(x)}\mathbb{P}[\,I_{x,y}\geqslant\eta\Delta-2\,]\,\mathrm{d}\mu(y)

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda\,\mu(B_{4R})\ \sup_{y\in B_{4R}(x)\setminus B_{\tau}(x)}\mathbb{P}[\,I_{x,y}\geqslant\eta\Delta-2\,].

Using Claim
13.2
, we have for
y
with
d_{M}(y,x)\geqslant\tau
that

\mathbb{E}I_{x,y}=\lambda\,\mu(B_{2R}(x)\cap B_{2R}(y))\leqslant\Delta\,(\ln\Delta)^{-15}.

As
I_{x,y}
is a Poisson random variable, we can apply (
23
) to obtain that, rather roughly,

\displaystyle\lambda\,\mu(B_{4R})\ \mathbb{P}[\,I_{x,y}\geqslant\eta\Delta-2\,]

\displaystyle\leqslant\Delta\,\frac{\mu(B_{4R})}{\mu(B_{2R})}\exp\left(-\Delta(\ln\Delta)^{-15}\right)<\frac{1}{m^{2}}.

The last inequality follow by observing by Lemma
11
and (
20
) that

\frac{\mu(B_{4R})}{\mu(B_{2R})}\leqslant\left(\frac{\sinh(4R)}{\sinh(2R)}\right)^{m}\leqslant{\mathrm{e}}^{2Rm}\leqslant\frac{\exp\left(\Delta(\ln\Delta)^{-15}\right)}{\Delta\,m^{2}}.

Thus we have shown that, for every
x\in X
,

\mathbb{P}[\,\exists y\in X:I_{x,y}\geqslant\eta\Delta-1\,]\leqslant\frac{1}{m^{4}}+\frac{1}{m^{2}}<\frac{1}{2m}.

By Mecke’s idenity, we conclude that the expected number of
x\in X
satisfying the second bad condition in (
21
) is at most
\frac{1}{2m}\lambda\,\mu(M)
, proving the claim.

Now we prove Lemma
13
.
Let
X
be obtained from the Poisson point process in
M
with intensity
\lambda\,\mu
.
Let
S_{1},S_{2}\subseteq X
be the points that satisfy the first and second bad properties in (
21
). Let
Y:=X\setminus(S_{1}\cup S_{2})
.
Then the previous claims imply that

\mathbb{E}|Y|\geqslant\mathbb{E}|X|-\mathbb{E}|S_{1}|-\mathbb{E}|S_{2}|\geqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{m^{2}}-\frac{1}{2m}\right)\mathbb{E}|X|\geqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{m}\right)\frac{\Delta}{\mu(B_{2R})}\,\mu(M).

There is an outcome
X
such that
|Y|
is at least its expected value, finishing the proof of Lemma
13
.
