Connectivity-Aware Pheromone Mobility Model for Search and Rescue Operation in Autonomous UAV Swarms
Shreyas Devaraju, Alexander Ihler, Sunil Kumar

Abstract
In recent years, there has been a surge in the use of inexpensive micro UAV swarms for civilian and military applications such as search and rescue, surveillance and tracking, and as loitering ammunitions. To carry out these operations efficiently, there is a need to develop scalable, decentralized autonomous UAV swarm architectures with high network connectivity. However, the area coverage and the network connectivity requirements exhibit a fundamental trade-off. In this project, a connectivity-aware pheromone mobility model (CAP) is designed for search and rescue operations, which is capable of maintaining connectivity among UAVs in the UAV swarm. We use stigmergy-based digital pheromone maps along with distance-based local connectivity information to autonomously coordinate the UAV movements, in order to improve its map coverage efficiency while maintaining high network connectivity. We also apply a deep Q-learning approach to tune our connectivity-aware pheromone mobility model (CAP-DQN) to improve our coverage / connectivity trade-off in UAV swarms.

Introduction

UAVs may fly autonomously or be remotely controlled by human operator. Multiple UAVs operating together is called a UAV swarm. Recent advances in hardware and battery technology has led to the miniaturization of UAVs, and these inexpensive micro-UAV swarms are being used in civilian and military operations, such as search and rescue, surveillance and tracking, and loitering ammunition
[
1
,
2
]
. Micro UAVs equipped with self-localization and sensing capabilities can perform target search in rural, natural and structurally complex environments, where access to humans and large UAVs may be limited, dangerous or even impossible. In such cases, using a group of low-cost micro UAVs for target search
[
1
]
is more advantageous than using a single large and expensive UAV. This is because the UAV swarms are more scalable, and can execute the tasks more efficiently, sense simultaneously in an expanded area, and are more robust (without any single point of failure).

It is advisable to avoid centralized control and coordination of a UAV swarm as it leads to exponential increase in communication overhead and computational complexity
[
6
]
. In decentralized autonomous UAV swarm architectures, members of the swarm perform only local sensing and communication without global knowledge
[
10
]
. Also, decentralized autonomous swarms can require less communication bandwidth and are less complex to implement.

Swarm intelligence methodologies inspired by nature, such as the social behavior of insects, birds, and fish, can be used to solve complex problems cooperatively by exhibiting collective intelligence and coordination through simple rules and local interactions
[
7
]
. One such methodology is the use of stigmergic digital pheromones
[
3
,
4
]
, which act as spatiotemporal potential fields that are used to coordinate and control the UAVs movements in a swarm for search and tracking operations. The term “stigmergy” was coined by the French biologist Grassé
[
5
]
in the 1950’s to describe a broad class of multi-agent coordination mechanisms that rely on information exchange through a shared environment. In nature, stigmergic systems can generate robust, complex, intelligent behavior at the system level even when the individual agents are simple and non-intelligent. In these systems, intelligence does not reside in a single distinguished agent or in each individual agent, but in the interactions among the agents and the shared dynamical environment. Also, these type of systems are highly
scalable
(work with large number of agents),
robust
(failure of a few agents will not affect the swarm), and possess
simplicity
(simple mechanisms capable of coordinating complex behaviors in large swarm systems).

Though a multi-agent UAV swarm can increase operational performance in applications such as coverage and tracking compared to a single agent UAV system, it may also introduce communication issues, since smaller and low-cost UAVs have limited communication range. In some settings, even positioning a larger and more powerful UAV as a cluster head at higher altitudes to alleviate line-of-sight communication issues between UAVs and the base station may not be possible due to airspace regulations, complexity and cost. Therefore, the connectivity among the UAVs of a swarm should be maintained to allow the coordination and control.

In our work, we design a UAV mobility model which combines the pheromone mobility model with local connectivity information to optimize coverage and connectivity performance. We call this model as connectivity-aware pheromone mobility (CAP) model. Our mobility model works by selecting a UAV path that balances pheromone values with estimated connectivity values at a number of potential waypoints. Our basic approach (in CAP) uses a simple weighted average to select the next waypoint. The CAP model facilitates the communication and coordination among the UAVs in an autonomous swarm.

II
Related Work

Robustness, scalability, flexibility, and distributed sensing make UAV swarm systems well suited for search, rescue, and tracking. Several algorithms such as particle swarm optimization, artificial bee colony, ant colony optimization (ACO), and glow worm optimization used to control and coordinate swarms for various search and rescue applications are summarized in
[
10
]
. In this report, we focus on the widely used pheromone-based stigmergic mechanism for the coordination of UAVs to explore unknown environments. Pheromone-based stigmergic algorithms achieve quick exploration of completely unknown environments, and their decentralized nature makes them fault-tolerant and highly scalable
[
9
]
.

The published works on stigmergic mechanisms using pheromone for target search and other similar tasks can be grouped into the use of physical and digital pheromones. Digital pheromone models are used in UAV swarms.
In digital pheromone schemes, information about the pheromone map is communicated between agents in the swarm through direct or indirect communication. In the direct communication methods, each agent maintains a full or partial pheromone map representing the immediate vicinity of the agent. Updates in the pheromone map due to deposits or withdrawals need to be communicated only locally. One of the challenges of the direct communication schemes is that the bandwidth required may grow exponentially with the swarm size. Using the compressed local information and low frequency map updates can lower the bandwidth to communicate the required information between agents. In
[
19
]
, distributed stigmergic coordination of UAVs for automatic target recognition is done through direct communication. The UAVs mark potential targets and communicate the pheromone information to nearby neighbors using a decentralized gossip mechanism based on controlled flooding. In controlled flooding
[
20
]
, the probability of forwarding a message by a UAV is inversely proportional to the UAV’s distance from the source of the message. This scheme also uses repel pheromone as negative feedback when more than the predefined number of UAVs identify the same target.

Sauter et al.
[
4
]
is an example of an indirect communication scheme, which studies and demonstrates the use of digital pheromones for controlling and coordinating UAV swarm for surveillance, target acquisition, and tracking. Here the coordination of swarm members is based on digital pheromones maintained in an artificial pheromone map, and a centralized base station is used to communicate the global pheromone map to all the UAVs. There are two classes of agents called walker and avatars. The walkers move around and deposit, withdraw, and read the pheromones needed to make movement and action decisions. On the other hand, the avatars are other entities (friendly, enemy, neutral) that may perceive the environment from the pheromone map to perform specific tasks when their own sensor information is unavailable. Different flavors of pheromone (repel, attract) with different dynamics (evaporation, propagation, magnitude) are used depending on the applications.

Some schemes use a fusion of stigmergic pheromone algorithm and flocking behaviors to coordinate a group of UAVs performing decentralized target search
[
8
,
3
]
. Here UAVs deposit digital attract pheromones when a potential target(s) is detected to attract UAVs in the area; Repel pheromones are deposited when no target is found. They also follow Biods
[
17
]
flocking rules (coherence, separation, alignment) to organize the swarm for better perception and communications while tracking targets. An evolutionary algorithm is used in
[
3
]
for tuning parameters of the two behaviors to get optimal performance.

Shao et al.
[
22
]
designed a navigation algorithm by using the pheromone algorithm on top of the Olfati-Saber’s flocking algorithm
[
18
]
. Here the leader-follower scheme is used to achieve the flocking behavior.

Coverage and network connectivity for a UAV group using a random mobility model versus a pheromone guided mobility model are compared in
[
11
]
. While the random model follows a Markov process, the UAVs move to a low repel pheromone area in the pheromone model. The pheromone model provides a better coverage than the random model, but neither the pheromone nor the random model showed good connectivity.

The chaotic ant colony optimization for coverage and connectivity (CACOC
2
)
[
13
]
tries to optimize the two opposing criteria (coverage and connectivity) for the UAV swarm, where the ACO with a chaotic dynamical system, together with the Boids flocking model, tries to maximize the coverage while preserving the network connectivity. Similarly, Dual-pheromone Clustering Hybird Approach (DPCHA)
[
27
]
uses dual pheromones for target tracking and map coverage and clustering to maintain stable network connectivity. Messous et al.
[
28
]
try to alleviate the connectivity issue in UAV fleets by weighting a UAV’s tendency to follow its neighbor based on its connectivity, hop count to the base station, and energy level.

In
[
25
]
, the connectivity of UAV swarm to the base station is preserved during surveillance missions, where the movement of the UAVs is limited by the network connectivity constraints. A UAV may only move to neighborhoods where it remains connected to 1-hop neighbors which have the least hop count to the base station. Among these neighborhoods, it chooses the one with lowest repel pheromone to promote coverage.

Combining flocking algorithms to organize the swarm usually leads to a better connectivity, but it introduces additional complexity. In our work, we have designed a simple, scalable, distributed mobility algorithm to optimize coverage and connectivity using UAVs local pheromone and estimated 1-hop connectivity information, rather than using a traditional flocking or clustering mechanism to maintain swarm connectivity.

III
Overview of Pheromone Mobility Model

Our scheme uses repel pheromone to promote exploration and fast coverage of an area with no prior information. Each UAV deposits a repel pheromone of magnitude ‘1’ in cells scanned along its trajectory, while moving towards the cells with minimum repel pheromone. The deposited repel pheromone is allowed to diffuse to neighboring cells, which encourage UAVs to spread out and move towards the unvisited cells. In addition, if the map environment and target locations change with time, evaporation of the deposited repel pheromones over time allows for UAVs to revisit already scanned cells of the map after a certain time gap.

For simplicity, the UAVs are assumed to move in two-dimensional space to search a given area, which is divided in a grid of
C\textsuperscript{2}
cells, where each cell is identified by its (x,y) coordinates. Pheromones deposited by each UAV in the grid space are saved in a digital pheromone map. In a distributed UAV swarm system, connected UAVs exchange their digital pheromone maps with the help of periodic ’hello messages’.

A digital pheromone has the same characteristics of a natural pheromone, such as deposition, evaporation and diffusion. When a UAV deposits a pheromone in a cell (x,y): (1) it is added to its existing digital pheromone value; (2) it is then progressively diffused to the surrounding cells, with a constant diffusion rate
\delta\in[0,1]
; (3) it subsequently evaporates, decreasing in intensity over time by a constant rate
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
. Mathematically the pheromone dynamics (pheromone value
p_{(x,y)}

\partial p_{(x,y)}(t-1,t)+\partial d_{(x,y)}(t-1,t)]

(1)

where

Traditional pheromone mobility models achieve a fast coverage of the area, but the very nature of the repel pheromones used to encourage coverage by pushing the UAVs away from each other also leads to poor UAV swarm connectivity. Connectivity preservation is essential for the UAV swarm to distribute pheromone information among its members and to coordinate the swarm mobility. In the Fig.
1
, if a UAV follows the repel pheromone gradient to a region of low connectivity, it is more likely to visit a region that has not been recently visited by another UAV. This would improve the coverage but the UAV may lose connectivity with other UAVs. Conversely, moving to a region with high repel pheromone where more UAVs are present would increase its connectivity, but the coverage performance will suffer.

Fig. 1:
(a) Illustration of next-waypoint selection based on repel pheromone intensity and connectivity of the UAV.

In this paper, we propose a connectivity-aware pheromone model for distributed UAV swarms that can maintain a better UAV swarm connectivity while maintaining a good coverage performance. The improved connectivity would enable effective swarm coordination and communication between the UAVs.

IV-A

UAV Mobility: Next-Waypoint Selection

The UAVs move from one cell (waypoint) to another, and the cell corresponding to the waypoint to which a UAV decides to move to is called the next-waypoint cell. After reaching a previously selected next-waypoint cell, a UAV again selects its next-waypoint cell. The number of cells between the current and next waypoint cell is a function of the UAV speed and cell size.

The heading of the UAVs (0 to 360 degrees) is discretized into 8 directions (see Fig.
2
(a)), and the next-waypoint is chosen w.r.t the current heading of a UAV such that it satisfies the flight trajectory constraints of a fixed-wing UAV, giving smooth turn trajectories. As shown in Fig.
2

IV-B

“Look-Ahead” Pheromone Value

In most traditional pheromone mobility models, a UAV moves towards an unvisited region in the map by selecting the cell with the minimum repel pheromone as its next-waypoint. Instead, we propose the
‘look-ahead pheromone’
value of the next-waypoint which is the weighted average of the repel pheromone value of the next waypoint cell and its eight 1-hop neighboring cells. By selecting the next-waypoint with the minimum ’look-ahead pheromone’ value, the UAV is more likely to visit areas of the map that are less likely to have been visited before, thereby increasing its coverage performance.

At any given next-waypoint cell (x,y), the ’look-ahead pheromone’ value is calculated as,

Look\_Ahead\ pher{(x,y)}=\frac{1}{12}\cdot(4\cdot pher{(x,y)}+\\
(\sum_{(a,b\in[-1,1]\;\ except\ a=b=0)}pher{(x+a,y+b)}))

(3)

where
pher{(x+a,y+b)}
represents a pheromone value in the 1-hop neighbor of cell (x,y).

IV-C

Distance-Weighted Degree-of-Connectivity

The degree of connectivity of a UAV is a measure of the number of its 1-hop neighbors. For a UAV, the closer to the edge of the transmission boundary its 1-hop UAV is, the more likely it is to lose connectivity in near future. Therefore, we calculate the
distance-weighted connectivity

(\gamma_{uv})
between two UAVs (
u
and
v

(d_{uv})

\gamma_{uv}=\scalebox{.8}{
\begin{cases}1 &d_{uv} \leq(0.6\cdot Tx)\\
2.5(1-\frac{d_{uv}}{Tx}) &(0.6\cdot Tx) < d_{uv} \leq Tx\\
0 &d_{uv} > Tx\end{cases}
}

(4)

The value of
\gamma_{uv}
is set to 1 within 60% of the transmission range, where we have a high probability of UAV connectivity being maintained. The value of
\gamma_{uv}
decreases linearly after 60% of transmission range because the probability of a UAV maintaining connectivity decreases with an increase in
d_{uv}

Futher, the
distance-weighted degree-of-connectivity

K_{u}
of a UAV

\cal N

K_{u}=\sum_{v\in{\cal N}}\gamma_{uv}

(5)

IV-D

Distributed Information Exchange using ‘Hello Messages’

Hello message containing each UAV’s updated local information is propagated to its 1-hop neighbors. The local pheromone and connectivity information of a UAV’s neighbors is used to select its next-waypoint cells and coordinate with its neighbors.

In our distributed scheme, we assume that each UAV exchanges the ’Hello’ messages with its 1-hop neighbors every 2 seconds, which consist of the UAV Id, its current location, next waypoint cell, and local pheromone map (pheromone information of the 5x5 cells centered at the UAV’s current cell).

IV-E

Selecting Next-Waypoint based on Distance-Weighted Degree-of-Connectivity

’look-ahead pheromone’ value
(repel) and the

at the next-waypoint cells.

K_{i}

: It is the average time take to scan 90% of cells in the map. It represents how quickly a given model can cover the entire map.

•

Coverage Fairness (F)
: It represents how equally all the cells of the map are visited over a given time period and is measured using the Jain’s fairness index
[
29
]
,

F=\frac{(\sum_{i}x_{i})^{2}}{n\sum_{i}x_{i}^{2}}

(10)

where
x_{i}
is number of scans of cell (i) and
n
is total number of cells in the map.

The connectivity performance of UAV swarm is measured in terms of:

•

Number of Connected Components (NCC)
: It is an average value of the number of disconnected components in the UAV swarm, sampled every 10 s. NCC is a measure of how disconnected the UAV swarm is throughout the simulation.

•

Average Network Connectivity (ANC)
: It is is average degree of connectivity of each UAV in the swarm, sampled every 10 s. ANC is a measure of how many average links or connected neighbors the UAVs maintain throughout the simulation.

VII
CONCLUSION FOR CAP model

The CAP model was able to achieve efficient coverage of a given area while preserving the network connectivity in an autonomous UAV swarm. In the CAP model, the UAVs used local distance-weighted connectivity information and pheromone information to make mobility decisions to maintain a desired balance between connectivity and coverage.
Also, the designed model allows for some tuning of the connectivity and coverage of the UAV swarm, especially in lower range of coverage time (faster coverage).
Also, the CAP model was able to achieve a better trade-off between coverage and connectivity than the CACOC
2
model.

VIII
Using Deep Q-learning

In our basic CAP model we use local connectivity information to augment the pheromone information and balance the trade-off between coverage and connectivity in the UAV swarm. In this section, we use the framework of reinforcement learning, in particular Deep Q-Learning
[
30
,
31
]
to train a policy for the UAVs, in order to
explicitly
optimize the trade-off between coverage and connectivity using the same information sources.
Reinforcement learning (RL)
[
32
]
is a branch of machine learning in which we attempt to optimize an agent’s policy, represented as a distribution over actions given the current state, in order to maximize a (discounted) cumulative reward to that agent over time.

optimize our agents’ policy using standard modeling and training libraries such as PyTorch
[
34
]
.

Finally, although technically our UAV swarm environment is a “multi-agent” system, to facilitate the offline training we treat the problem as a single-agent task, in which we optimize each agent’s policy individually, treating the other agents’ behavior as environmental randomness.

VIII-A

DQN Problem Formulation

In Deep Q-Learning, we model the state-action value function
q(s,a)
using a neural network. The function
q
captures the long-term value associated with being in a state
s
and taking action
a
. Given a current estimate of
q
, our agent can select its next action
a
to maximize
q(s,a)
for its current state
s
.
In our setting, each UAV flies from its previous way-point to its selected next way-point. Upon reaching the next way-point, the UAV must choose a new next way-point from a set of five possibilities, based on the “look-ahead” pheremone and estimated distance-weighted degree of connectivity information for those way-points; see Section
IV
.
Thus, we select as our state information
s
the “look-ahead” pheremone and connectivity information at each waypoint (10 real values),
and our action space consists of five possible actions, corresponding to the five forward-facing directions with respect to the current UAV heading.

Our RL setting also requires us to define a set of rewards
r
, which are obtained when the agent takes action
a
from state
s
. We provide two sources of reward, one for coverage (
r_{c}
) and one for connectivity (
r_{k}
), with
r=r_{c}+r_{k}
. Here,
r_{c}
is defined to be the difference between the number of new cells scanned and the number of already-scanned cells along the path taken by the UAV beween its two way-points. This rewards paths that improve rapid coverage of the environment, under the assumtion that the UAVs scan any cells as they pass overhead. Our connectivity reward,
r_{k}
, is calculated based on the distance-weighted degree-of-connectivity,
K^{\prime}_{i}
, of the UAV when it reaches its next waypoint
i
; specifically,

r_{k}&=\scalebox{0.8}{\begin{cases}-20 &K'_{i} < 1\\
-10 &1 \leq K'_{i} < 2\\
0 &2 \leq K'_{i}.\end{cases}}

(11)

Since high swarm connectivity (e.g., low NCC and high ANC) correlates with high
K^{\prime}_{i}
values, we reward our agent for maintaining a baseline degree of connectivity (2 or better), with increasing penalties for less connectivity.

VIII-C

Training

We learn a single, universal policy from transition sequences stored in our memory, which may have been generated by multiple agents. The transition sequences from each agent is used to update a single DQN network, learning a single policy that will be followed by all the UAVs in the swarm. In this way, we ensure that our agents’ policies are not dependent on other agents and their actions, so that the policy can be applied locally in order to be robust and scalable.
