Sign regular matrices and variation diminution: single-vector tests and characterizations, following Schoenberg, Gantmacher–Krein, and Motzkin
Projesh Nath Choudhury and Shivangi Yadav

Abstract
. Variation diminution (VD) is a fundamental property in total positivity theory, first studied in 1912 by Fekete–Pólya for one-sided Pólya frequency sequences, followed by Schoenberg, and by Motzkin who characterized sign regular (SR) matrices using VD and some rank hypotheses. A classical theorem by Gantmacher–Krein characterized the strictly sign regular (SSR) m × n m\times n matrices for m > n m>n using this property. In this article we strengthen these results by characterizing all m × n m\times n SSR matrices using VD. We further characterize strict sign regularity of a given sign pattern in terms of VD together with a natural condition motivated by total positivity. We then refine Motzkin’s characterization of SR matrices by omitting the rank condition and specifying the sign pattern. This concludes a line of investigation on VD started by Fekete–Pólya [ Rend. Circ. Mat. Palermo 1912] and continued by Schoenberg [ Math. Z. 1930], Motzkin [PhD thesis, 1936], Gantmacher–Krein [1950 book], Brown–Johnstone–MacGibbon [ J. Amer. Stat. Assoc. 1981], and Choudhury [ Bull. London Math. Soc. 2022, Bull. Sci. Math. 2023]. In fact we show stronger characterizations, by employing single test vectors with alternating sign coordinates – i.e., lying in the alternating bi-orthant. We also show that test vectors chosen from any other orthant will not work.

SR and SSR matrices

enjoy the variation diminishing (VD) property, which states that the number of sign changes of

is bounded above by the number of sign changes of

)
<\infty

[

We now look back on some fundamental contributions in this direction: In 1930, Schoenberg
[

[

, Motzkin then characterized all

The following definitions and notations are essential in order to formulate our results.

Definition 1.1
.

Throughout this paper,

will denote fixed but arbitrary integers.

(1)

For
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we denote by

the number of sign changes in the sequence

a value of 1 or
-1

the maximum possible number of sign changes in the resulting sequence. We set
S^{+}(\mathbf{0}):=n
for
\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2)

A

is one whose rows and columns are indexed by consecutive integers.

(3)

Let
\adj(A)
denote the adjugate matrix of
A
. If

, we define
\adj(A):=1
to be the determinant of the empty matrix.

(4)

Let
\mathbf{e}^{j}
denote the unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose
j^{th}
entry is 1 and the rest are zero.

(5)

The
signature
or

is the ordered tuple
\epsilon=(\epsilon_{1},\dots,\epsilon_{\min\{m,n\}})
in a definition above, with
\epsilon_{r}
denoting the sign of all

minors of

. We set
\epsilon_{0}:=1
.

(6)

Given integers

and a sign pattern
\epsilon=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{k})
, an SSR
k
(SR
k
) matrix

is called SSR
{}_{k}({\epsilon})
(SR
{}_{k}({\epsilon})
) if the sign pattern of all non-zero minors of

of order at most

is given by
\epsilon
. If

then we simply write SSR
(\epsilon)
(SR
(\epsilon)
).

(7)

Let
A\begin{pmatrix}i_{1},\ldots,i_{p}\\
j_{1},\ldots,j_{q}\end{pmatrix}
denote the
p\times q
submatrix of an
m\times n
matrix

indexed by rows

and columns
1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{q}\leq n
, where
1\leq p\leq m
and
1\leq q\leq n
.

We now state our main results. The first characterizes SSR matrices of arbitrary sizes using the variation diminishing property, parallel to Motzkin’s 1936 result for SR matrices.

Theorem A
.

Given
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
A
satisfies the variation diminishing property,
S^{+}(A\mathbf{x})\leq S^{-}(\mathbf{x})
for all
\mathbf{0}\neq\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if and only if
A
is SSR.

The above theorem ensures that if a matrix
A
satisfies the variation diminishing property then it is SSR. However, it does not give any information about the signature/sign pattern of
A
. Our next result aims to fill this gap, and it guarantees that we can obtain an SSR matrix with a sign pattern of desired choice at the cost of imposing a natural condition along with the variation diminishing property, which we have stated in part (2) of Theorem
B
. Further, we have refined part (2) below in part (3) where exactly one vector
\mathbf{x}^{A_{k}}
for every contiguous square submatrix
A_{k}
of
A
is sufficient to ensure that
A
is SSR
(\epsilon)
. Thus, (3) reveals the extent to which (2) can be weakened without losing content.

Theorem B
.

Given
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\epsilon=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{\mathrm{min}\{m,n\}})
, the following statements are equivalent.

(1)

A
is SSR
({\epsilon})
.

(2)

For all
\mathbf{0}\neq\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have
S^{+}(A\mathbf{x})\leq S^{-}(\mathbf{x})
. Further, for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
A\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}
and
S^{+}(A\mathbf{x})=S^{-}(\mathbf{x})=r

, then the sign of the first (last) component of
A\mathbf{x}
(if zero, the unique sign given in determining
S^{+}(A\mathbf{x})
) agrees with
\epsilon_{r}\epsilon_{r+1}
times the sign of the first (last) non-zero component of
\mathbf{x}
.

In fact, these are also equivalent to the following assertion with a severely reduced test set:

(3)

For every contiguous square submatrix
A_{k}
of
A
of size
k\times k
, where
1\leq k\leq\mathrm{min}\{m,n\}
, and given any fixed vector
\mathbf{0}\neq\mathbf{v}:=(\alpha_{1},-\alpha_{2},\ldots,(-1)^{k-1}\alpha_{k})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
with all
\alpha_{j}\geq 0
, we define the vector

(1.1)

\mathbf{x}^{A_{k}}:=\adj(A_{k})\mathbf{v}.

Then
S^{+}(A_{k}\mathbf{x}^{A_{k}})\leq S^{-}(\mathbf{x}^{A_{k}})
. Moreover, if
S^{+}(A_{k}\mathbf{x}^{A_{k}})=S^{-}(\mathbf{x}^{A_{k}})=r
, and if
0\leq r\leq k-1
, then the sign of the first (last) component of
A_{k}\mathbf{x}^{A_{k}}
(if zero, the unique sign given in determining
S^{+}(A_{k}\mathbf{x}^{A_{k}})
) agrees with
\epsilon_{r}\epsilon_{r+1}
times the sign of the first (last) non-zero component of
\mathbf{x}^{A_{k}}
.

Remark 1.2
.

As a consequence of Theorem
B
, one can reduce the test set in Theorem
A
as well, to a single vector
\mathbf{x}^{A_{k}}:=\adj(A_{k})\mathbf{v}
for each contiguous square submatrix, and where
\mathbf{v}
is as in Theorem
B
, with alternate signed coordinates, i.e. from the alternating bi-orthant. As we show in Theorem
2.2
, single test vectors from other bi-orthant do not work.

Our final result is a “non-strict” variant of Theorem

\epsilon
by removing Motzkin’s rank-hypothesis. In fact we show a twofold strengthening of Motzkin’s result, by moreover working with a single test vector for each square submatrix.

Theorem C
.

Given
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
\epsilon=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{\mathrm{min}\{m,n\}})

(1)

(2)

For all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

Proof of Theorem

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be such that
S^{+}(A\mathbf{x})\leq S^{-}(\mathbf{x})
for all
\mathbf{0}\neq\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Our aim is to show all minors of the same size of
A
have the same sign. The proof is by induction on the size of the minors. First we show that all
p\times p
minors of
A
are non-zero for
1\leq p\leq\mathrm{min}\{m,n\}
. Assume to the contrary that
\det A\begin{pmatrix}i_{1},\ldots,i_{p}\\
j_{1},\ldots,j_{p}\end{pmatrix}=0
, where (henceforth)
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{p}\leq m

1\leq j_{1}<\cdots<j_{p}\leq n

\mathbf{0}\neq\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that

A\begin{pmatrix}i_{1},\ldots,i_{p}\\
j_{1},\ldots,j_{p}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{0}.

Define
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that the
j_{l}
component of
\mathbf{x}
is

for
l=1,\ldots,p
, and all other components are zero. Then
S^{-}(\mathbf{x})\leq p-1

, a contradiction. This shows the claim.

Next we show that all entries of

, and thus
S^{+}(A\mathbf{e}^{j})=0.
Therefore, all the elements of
A
present in the

A

\mathbf{a}^{i_{1}}
is positive while
\mathbf{a}^{i_{2}}
is negative. We can choose positive real numbers

such that at least one entry of the vector
\mathbf{v}=x_{i_{1}}\mathbf{a}^{i_{1}}+x_{i_{2}}\mathbf{a}^{i_{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is zero. Take
\mathbf{0}\neq\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with

and

and

, a contradiction. Thus
A
is SSR
{}_{1}.

A
is SSR

A
is SSR

have the same sign, and (b) this sign is independent of

and depends only on

.

Fix

. We will first prove that
\det A\begin{pmatrix}i_{1},\ldots,i_{p}\\

for all

have the same sign. If
p=m
, then we are done; if

, it suffices to show this for the
(p+1)
minors of size

that can be formed from an arbitrary choice of
(p+1)

(say). Consider the following

) of

A_{l}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{i_{1}j_{1}}&a_{i_{1}j_{2}}&\ldots&a_{i_{1}j_{p}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{i_{l-1}j_{1}}&a_{i_{l-1}j_{2}}&\ldots&a_{i_{l-1}j_{p}}\\
a_{i_{l+1}j_{1}}&a_{i_{l+1}j_{2}}&\ldots&a_{i_{l+1}j_{p}}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
a_{i_{p+1}j_{1}}&a_{i_{p+1}j_{2}}&\ldots&a_{i_{p+1}j_{p}}\\

We will show that

and depends only on

. Let us take

, with

, and let
\varepsilon^{\prime}_{r}:=\varepsilon^{\prime}(j_{1},\ldots,j_{r-1},j_{r+1},\ldots,j_{p+1}).
It suffices to prove

Define a vector

whose coordinates in position

. Now if

, and so

To prove the converse, let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be SSR. Take a non-zero vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and assume that
0\leq S^{-}(\mathbf{x})=r\leq n-1
. Therefore, we can partition

into

(2.1)

\displaystyle(x_{1},\ldots,x_{s_{1}}),\quad(x_{s_{1}+1},\ldots,x_{s_{2}}),\quad\ldots,\quad(x_{s_{r}+1},\ldots,x_{n}).

. Also, note that there is at least one non-zero element in each partition, otherwise

. Writing

, we obtain

A\mathbf{x}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\mathbf{a}^{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{r+1}(-1)^{j-1}\mathbf{y}^{j}

\mathbf{y}^{j}=\sum_{k=s_{j-1}+1}^{s_{j}}|x_{k}|\mathbf{a}^{k}\hskip 5.69046pt\mathrm{for}\hskip 5.69046ptj=1,\ldots,r+1,\hskip 5.69046pt\hskip 5.69046pts_{0}=0,\hskip 5.69046pt\mathrm{and}\hskip 5.69046pts_{r+1}=n.

Since

is SSR, the terms

have the same sign for all choices of

,

; and

for some choice of admissible
\{k_{1},\ldots,k_{p}\}

is SSR. Note that the minors of

With the above analysis in hand, we will show that
S^{+}(A\mathbf{x})\leq r
. Let
\mathbf{d}_{r+1}:=(1,-1,\ldots,(-1)^{r})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{r+1}
. Consider the following two cases.

Case I.

.

If

, then since

is SSR and hence invertible, hence

, and
S^{+}(A\mathbf{x})=S^{+}(Y\mathbf{d}_{r+1})\leq r=S^{-}(\mathbf{x})
. Otherwise,

. Then clearly
S^{+}(A\mathbf{x})\leq m\leq S^{-}(\mathbf{x}).

Case II.

m>r+1
.

Define
\mathbf{w}:=A\mathbf{x}=Y\mathbf{d}_{r+1}
and note that
\mathbf{w}\neq\mathbf{0}
, since

has rank
(r+1)
and hence independent columns. Now assume to the contrary that
S^{+}(A\mathbf{x})\geq r+1

and

such that

for

. Since

is SSR

can be zero; in particular, at least two of them are non-zero. Now, the determinant

\det\begin{pmatrix}w_{i_{1}}&y_{i_{1}1}&\ldots&y_{i_{1}r+1}\\
w_{i_{2}}&y_{i_{2}1}&\ldots&y_{i_{2}r+1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
w_{i_{r+2}}&y_{i_{r+2}1}&\ldots&y_{i_{r+2}r+1}\end{pmatrix}

0=\sum_{j=1}^{r+2}(-1)^{j-1}w_{i_{j}}\det Y\begin{pmatrix}i_{1},\ldots,i_{j-1},i_{j+1},\ldots,i_{r+2}\\
1,2,\ldots\hskip 28.45274pt\ldots,r,r+1\end{pmatrix}.

But the right hand side is non-zero, since

is SSR,
(-1)^{j-1}w_{i_{j}}
has the same sign
\theta
for

are zero. This contradiction implies that

.
∎

To prove our next main result, we recall a 1968 seminal result by Karlin for strict sign regularity, first proved in 1912 by Fekete

Theorem 2.1

(Karlin
[

]
)

Suppose

Proof of Theorem

(\epsilon)

2,3,\ldots\hskip 28.45274pt\ldots,r,r+1\end{pmatrix}}{\det Y\begin{pmatrix}i_{1},\ldots,i_{r+1}\\

From part (3) of Theorem

Theorem 2.2
.

Fix an integer

and a sign pattern

\mathbf{x}_{0}

\mathbf{x}_{0}

Write
\mathbf{x}_{0}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})^{T}
. Then
S^{-}(\mathbf{x}_{0})=r\leq n-2
and one can partition
\mathbf{x}_{0}
as in (

). Since
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is SSR
{}_{n-1}(\epsilon)
, by the argument following (

), the matrix

is SSR with sign pattern

2.1.
Variation Diminution for Sign Regular Matrices

We conclude by showing Theorem

, which characterizes all

sign regular matrices with a given sign pattern using the variation diminishing property. The proof requires a density theorem proved by Gantmacher–Krein in 1950 using the total positivity of the Gaussian kernel.

Theorem 2.3

(Gantmacher–Krein
[
14
]
)
.

Let
m,n\geq k\geq 1
be integers. Given a sign pattern
\epsilon=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{k})
, the set of
m\times n
SSR
{}_{k}(\epsilon)
matrices is dense in the set of
m\times n
SR
{}_{k}(\epsilon)
matrices.

The following basic lemma on sign changes of limits of vectors will also be needed in the proof.

Lemma 2.4
.

[
26

If
\displaystyle{\lim_{k\to\infty}}\mathbf{x}_{k}=\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then

\varliminf_{k\to\infty}S^{-}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\geq S^{-}(\mathbf{x})\hskip 5.69046pt\mathrm{and}\hskip 5.69046pt\varlimsup_{k\to\infty}S^{+}(\mathbf{x}_{k})\leq S^{+}(\mathbf{x}).

With these tools at hand, we now show:

C
.

We first show that (1)
\implies
(2). Since

is SR
(\epsilon)

(\epsilon)

converging entrywise to
A
.
For
\mathbf{0}\neq\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, using Theorem

S^{-}(A\mathbf{x})\leq\varliminf_{l\to\infty}S^{-}(A_{l}\mathbf{x})\leq\varliminf_{l\to\infty}S^{+}(A_{l}\mathbf{x})\leq\varliminf_{l\to\infty}S^{-}(\mathbf{x})=S^{-}(\mathbf{x}).

If
S^{-}(A\mathbf{x})=S^{-}(\mathbf{x})=r
where
0\leq r\leq\mathrm{min}\{m,n\}-1
and
A\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}
, then we necessarily have

r=S^{-}(A\mathbf{x})\leq S^{-}(A_{l}\mathbf{x})\leq S^{+}(A_{l}\mathbf{x})\leq S^{-}(\mathbf{x})=r

for all

sufficiently large, using Theorem

and Lemma

admit ‘partitions’ of the type (

times that of
\mathbf{x}
. By a limiting argument, the non-zero sign patterns of
A\mathbf{x}
and

That (2)
\implies
(1) is shown similarly to the proof of Theorem

\mathbf{e}^{j}

\epsilon_{1}

\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{p})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

j_{1},\ldots,j_{p}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{d}_{p}:=(1,-1,\ldots,(-1)^{p-1})^{T}.
By repeating the corresponding part of the proof of Theorem

S^{-}(A\mathbf{x})=S^{-}(\mathbf{x})=p-1

, which further implies

z_{1}=\frac{\displaystyle{\sum_{l=1}^{p}}\det A\begin{pmatrix}i_{1},\ldots,i_{l-1},i_{l+1},\ldots,i_{p}\\
j_{2},j_{3},\ldots\hskip 5.69046pt\ldots,j_{p-1},j_{p}\end{pmatrix}}{\det A\begin{pmatrix}i_{1},\ldots,i_{p}\\

, we obtain

0<\epsilon_{p-1}\epsilon_{p}z_{1}=\epsilon_{p-1}\epsilon_{p}\frac{\displaystyle{\sum_{l=1}^{p}}\det A\begin{pmatrix}i_{1},\ldots,i_{l-1},i_{l+1},\ldots,i_{p}\\

Thus the sum on the right must be non-zero. Moreover, by the induction hypothesis, it has sign
\epsilon_{p-1}
. Thus the sign of
\det A\begin{pmatrix}i_{1},\ldots,i_{p}\\
j_{1},\ldots,j_{p}\end{pmatrix}
is
\epsilon_{p}
. This completes the induction step.

Now we show that (2)
\implies
(3) – again more generally, for arbitrary vectors
\mathbf{0}\neq\mathbf{y}^{A_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
. The proof is similar to that of Theorem
B
. Fix
1\leq k\leq\mathrm{min}\{m,n\}
, and let

. Take

at position

for

S^{-}(A_{k}\mathbf{y}^{A_{k}})\leq S^{-}(A\mathbf{x})\leq S^{-}(\mathbf{x})=S^{-}(\mathbf{y}^{A_{k}}).

Now suppose
S^{-}(A_{k}\mathbf{y}^{A_{k}})=S^{-}(\mathbf{y}^{A_{k}})=r
, where

S^{-}(A_{k}\mathbf{y}^{A_{k}})=S^{-}(\mathbf{y}^{A_{k}})=S^{-}(\mathbf{x})=S^{-}(A\mathbf{x})=r\hskip 5.69046pt\mathrm{and}\hskip 5.69046ptA\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{0}.

Assume without loss of generality that the first and the last non-zero components of
A_{k}\mathbf{y}^{A_{k}}
are in positions

, respectively. Since
S^{-}(A\mathbf{x})=S^{-}(A_{k}\mathbf{y}^{A_{k}})=r

A_{k}\mathbf{y}^{A_{k}}

\epsilon_{r}\epsilon_{r+1}

Finally we show that (3)
\implies
(1). We show by induction on
r
that the sign of
\det A_{r}

\epsilon_{r}
for all
r\times r

\alpha_{1}>0

S^{-}(A_{1}y^{A_{1}})=S^{-}(y^{A_{1}})=0

\epsilon_{1}

\epsilon_{1}

For the induction step,
A_{r}

r-1

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},-\alpha_{2},\ldots,(-1)^{r-1}\alpha_{r})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}\hskip 5.69046pt\mathrm{with}\hskip 5.69046pt\mathrm{all}\hskip 5.69046pt\alpha_{j}>0
