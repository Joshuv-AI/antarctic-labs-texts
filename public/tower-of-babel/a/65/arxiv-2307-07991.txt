Examples of hyperbolic spaces without the properties of quasi-ball or bounded eccentricity
Qizheng You and Jiawen Zhang

Abstract
. In this note, we present examples of non-quasi-geodesic metric spaces which are hyperbolic ( i.e. , satisfying the Gromov’s 4 4 -point condition) while the intersection of any two metric balls therein does not either “look like” a ball or has uniformly bounded eccentricity. This answers an open question posed in [ 2 ] .



Keywords: Gromov’s hyperbolic spaces, Non-(quasi)-geodesic, Quasi-ball property, Bounded eccentricity property

1.
Introduction

In the seminal work
[
4
]
, Gromov introduced a notion of hyperbolicity for metric spaces which encodes the information of metric curvatures for the underlying spaces, with prototypes from classic hyperbolic geometry. Gromov’s hyperbolic spaces have attractd a lot of interest since they are discovered and have fruitful applications in various aspects of mathematics (see,
e.g.
,
[
1
,
3
]
).

Recall that a geodesic metric space
(X,d)
is called
hyperbolic
(in the sense of Gromov
[
4
]
) if there exists
\delta>0
such that for any geodesic triangle in
(X,d)
, the union of the
\delta
-neighbourhoods of any two sides of the triangle contains the third. Gromov also provided a characterisation for his hyperbolicity using the so-called
Gromov product
:

(x|y)_{p}=\frac{1}{2}(d(x,p)+d(y,p)-d(x,y))\quad\text{for}\quad x,y,p\in(X,d).

He proved in
[
4
]
that a geodesic metric space
(X,d)
is hyperbolic
if and only if
the following condition holds:

Gromov’s
4
-point condition:
There exists
\delta>0
such that

(x|y)_{p}\geq\min\{(x|z)_{p},(y|z)_{p}\}-\delta\quad\text{for all}\quad x,y,z,p\in(X,d).

Note that the statement of the Gromov’s
4
-point condition does not require that the underlying space
(X,d)
is geodesic. Hence in the general context, we say that a (not necessarily geodesic) metric space is
hyperbolic
if the Gromov’s
4
-point condition holds.

Later in
[
2
]
, Chatterji and Niblo discovered new characterisations of Gromov’s hyperbolicity for geodesic metric spaces using the geometry of intersections of balls. More precisely, for a geodesic metric space, they showed that it is hyperbolic in the sense of Gromov
if and only if
the following holds:

Quasi-ball property:
The intersection of any two metric balls is at uniformly bounded Hausdorff distance from a ball.

They also considered the eccentricity of the intersection of balls. Recall that for a metric space
(X,d)
and
\delta>0
, we say that the
eccentricity
of a subset
S
of
X
is less than
\delta
if there exist
c,c^{\prime}\in X
and
R\geq 0
such that

(1.1)

B(c,R)\subseteq S\subseteq B(c^{\prime},R+\delta).

Here we use
B(x,r):=\{y\in X:d(x,y)\leq r\}
to denote the metric ball. The
eccentricity
of
S
is the infimum of
\delta
satisfying (
1.1
). By convention, the eccentricity of the empty set is
0
. Chatterji and Niblo proved in
[
2
]
that a geodesic metric space is hyperbolic
if and only if
the following holds:

Bounded eccentricity property:
The intersection of any two metric balls has uniformly bounded eccentricity.

In
[
2
, Section 4]
, Chatterji and Niblo also discussed the situation of non-geodesic metric spaces. They recorded an example due to Viktor Schroeder (see
[
2
, Example 18]
) that there exists a non-geodesic metric space with the quasi-ball property but not hyperbolic (
i.e.
, does not satisfy the Gromov’s
4
-point condition). However, the other direction is unclear and hence, they asked the following:

Question 1.1
.

Does there exist a non-geodesic hyperbolic metric space which does not satisfy the quasi-ball property or the bounded eccentricity property?

In this short note, we provide an affirmative answer to Question
1.1
by constructing concrete examples. The main result is the following:

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists a non-quasi-geodesic hyperbolic (
i.e.
, satisfying the Gromov’s
4
-point condition) space which does not satisfy either the quasi-ball property or the bounded eccentricity property.

Our construction is motivated by Gromov’s observation in
[
4
, Section 1.2]
(also suggested in
[
2
, Section 4]
) that for a metric space
(X,d)
, we can endow another metric
d^{\prime}
on
X
defined by

(1.2)

d^{\prime}(x,y)=\ln(1+d(x,y))\quad\text{for}\quad x,y\in X

such that
(X,d^{\prime})
satisfies the Gromov’s
4
-point condition. We show that if
(X,d)
is geodesic and unbounded, then
(X,d^{\prime})
cannot be quasi-geodesic (see Corollary
3.3
). Then we study the relation of the quasi-ball property and the bounded eccentricity property between
(X,d)
and
(X,d^{\prime})
(see Lemma
3.5
, Lemma
3.7
and Lemma
3.9
). Finally, we show in Example
3.10
that for the Euclidean space
X=\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the Euclidean metric
d
, the construction
(X,d^{\prime})
in (
1.2
) provides an example to conclude Theorem
1.2
.

2.
Preliminaries

Here we collect some necessary notions and notation for this note.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. For
x\in X
and
R\geq 0
, denote the (metric) ball by
B(x,R)=\{y\in X:d(x,y)\leq R\}
. We say that
(X,d)
is
bounded
if there exist
x\in X
and
R\geq 0
such that
X=B(x,R)
, and
unbounded
if it is not bounded. For a subset
A\subset X
and
R\geq 0
, denote
\mathcal{N}_{R}(A)=\{x\in X:d(x,A)\leq R\}
the
R
-
neighbourhood
of
A
in
X
. For subsets
A,B\subset X
, the
Hausdorff distance
between
A
and
B
is

d_{H}(A,B)=\inf\{R\geq 0:A\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{R}(B)\text{ and }B\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{R}(A)\}.

Recall that a
path
in a metric space
(X,d)
is a continuous map
\gamma:[a,b]\to X
. A path
\gamma
is called
rectifiable
if its
length

\ell(\gamma):=\sup\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}d(\gamma(t_{i-1}),\gamma(t_{i})):a=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=b,n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

is finite. Usually it is convenient to change the parameter
t\in[a,b]
to the
standard arc parameter

s\in[0,\ell(\gamma)]
as follows. Define a map
\varphi:[a,b]\rightarrow[0,\ell(\gamma)]
by
t\mapsto s=\ell(\gamma|_{[a,t]})
, and set
\tilde{\gamma}:[0,\ell(\gamma)]\to X
by
\tilde{\gamma}:=\gamma\circ\varphi^{-1}
. Then we have
\ell(\tilde{\gamma}|_{[s_{1},s_{2}]})=|s_{1}-s_{2}|
for any
0\leq s_{1}\leq s_{2}\leq\ell(\gamma)
.

Now we recall the notion of (quasi-)geodesics.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space.

(1)

Given
x,y\in X
, a
geodesic
between
x
and
y
is an isometric embedding
\gamma:[0,d(x,y)]\rightarrow X
with
\gamma(0)=x
and
\gamma(d(x,y))=y
.
The space
(X,d)
is called
geodesic
if for any
x,y
in
X
, there exists a geodesic between
x
and
y
.

(2)

Given
x,y\in X,L\geq 1
and
C\geq 0
, an
(L,C)
-quasi-geodesic
between
x
and
y
is a map
\gamma:[0,T]\rightarrow X
such that
\gamma(0)=x,\gamma(T)=y
and

\frac{1}{L}|a-b|-C\leq d(\gamma(a),\gamma(b))\leq L|a-b|+C\quad\text{for all}\quad a,b\in[0,T].

The space
(X,d)
is called
(L,C)
-quasi-geodesic
if for any
x,y
in
X
, there exists an
(L,C)
-quasi-geodesic between
x
and
y
. We also say that
(X,d)
is
quasi-geodesic
if it is
(L,C)
-quasi-geodesic for some
L
and
C
.

We also need the notion of ultrametric space. Recall that a metric space
(X,d)
is called
ultrametric
if there exists
\delta>0
such that for any points
x,y,z\in X
, we have

d(x,y)\leq\mathrm{max}\{d(x,z),d(y,z)\}+\delta.

The following is due to Gromov:

Lemma 2.2

(
[
4
, Section 1.2]
)
.

An ultrametric space satisfies the Gromov’s
4
-point condition.

Recall from Section
1
that for a metric space
(X,d)
, Gromov considered another metric
d^{\prime}
on
X
defined in (
1.2
) and noticed that

\displaystyle d^{\prime}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ln(1+d(x,z)+d(z,y))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ln(2+2\mathrm{max}\{d(x,z),d(y,z)\})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{max}\{d^{\prime}(x,z),d^{\prime}(y,z)\}+\ln 2.

Combining with Lemma
2.2
, we obtain the following:

Lemma 2.3

(
[
4
, Section 1.2]
)
.

For a metric space
(X,d)
, the new metric
d^{\prime}
on
X
defined in (
1.2
) satisfies the Gromov’s
4
-point condition.

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2

This whole section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
1.2
, which is divided into several parts.

Firstly, we would like to study the property of (quasi-)geodesic for the new metric
d^{\prime}
defined in (
1.2
). To simplify the notation, for a path
\gamma
in
X
, we denote
\ell(\gamma)
and
\ell^{\prime}(\gamma)
its length with respect to the metric
d
and
d^{\prime}
, respectively.

We need the following lemma:

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and
d^{\prime}
be the metric on
X
defined in (
1.2
).
A path
\gamma:[a,b]\rightarrow X
in
X
is rectifiable with respect to
d

if and only if
it is rectifiable with respect to
d^{\prime}
. In this case, we have
\ell(\gamma)=\ell^{\prime}(\gamma)
.

Proof.

Firstly, we assume that
\gamma
is rectifiable with respect to
d
,
i.e.
,
\ell(\gamma)<\infty
. Note that
\ln(1+x)\leq x
holds for all
x\geq 0
. Hence for any partition
a=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=b
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}d^{\prime}(\gamma(t_{i-1}),\gamma(t_{i}))\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}d(\gamma(t_{i-1}),\gamma(t_{i}))\leq\ell(\gamma),

which implies that
\ell^{\prime}(\gamma)\leq\ell(\gamma)
. In particular,
\gamma
is rectifiable with respect to
d^{\prime}
.

Conversely, we assume that
\gamma
is rectifiable with respect to
d^{\prime}
,
i.e.
,
\ell^{\prime}(\gamma)<\infty
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\gamma
is parametrised by the standard arc parameter with
a=0
and
b=\ell^{\prime}(\gamma)
.
Note that for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
\alpha x\leq\ln(1+x)
holds for all
x\in[0,\delta]
.
Given a partition
a=s_{0}<s_{1}<\cdots<s_{m}=b
, we choose a refinement
a=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{n}=b
such that
|t_{i-1}-t_{i}|<\ln(1+\delta)
holds for all
i
. Hence we have

d^{\prime}(\gamma(t_{i-1}),\gamma(t_{i}))\leq l^{\prime}(\gamma|_{[t_{i-1},t_{i}]})=|t_{i-1}-t_{i}|<\ln(1+\delta),

which implies that
d(\gamma(t_{i-1}),\gamma(t_{i}))<\delta
due to (
1.2
).
Therefore, we obtain

\sum_{i=1}^{m}d(\gamma(s_{i-1}),\gamma(s_{i}))\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}d(\gamma(t_{i-1}),\gamma(t_{i}))\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{n}d^{\prime}(\gamma(t_{i-1}),\gamma(t_{i}))\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}\cdot\ell^{\prime}(\gamma)

for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Letting
\alpha\to 1
and taking the supremum of the left hand side, we obtain that
\ell(\gamma)\leq\ell^{\prime}(\gamma)
, which concludes the proof.
∎

As a direct corollary, we obtain the following:

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
(X,d)
be a geodesic metric space which contains at least two elements, and
d^{\prime}
be the metric defined in (
1.2
). Then the metric space
(X,d^{\prime})
is
not
geodesic.

Proof.

By assumption, we take two distinct points
x,y\in X
. If
(X,d^{\prime})
is geodesic, we choose a geodesic
\gamma
between
x
and
y
. In particular,
\gamma
is rectifiable with respect to
d^{\prime}
. Hence by Lemma
3.1
, we know that
\gamma
is also rectifiable with respect to
d
and we have

\ell^{\prime}(\gamma)=\ell(\gamma)\geq d(x,y)>d^{\prime}(x,y),

where the last inequality follows from the assumption that
x\neq y
.
This is a contradiction to the assumption that
\gamma
is a geodesic between
x
and
y
with respect to the metric
d^{\prime}
. Hence we conclude the proof.
∎

Moreover, with an extra hypothesis, the new metric
d^{\prime}
cannot be even quasi-geodesic.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
(X,d)
be an unbounded geodesic metric space, and
d^{\prime}
be the metric defined in (
1.2
).
Then for any
L\geq 1
and
C\geq 0
, the metric space
(X,d^{\prime})
cannot be
(L,C)
-quasi-geodesic.

To prove Corollary
3.3
, we need the following lemma to tame quasi-geodesics. The idea is similar to
[
1
, Lemma III.H.1.11]
, but the setting is slightly different.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
(X,d)
be a geodesic metric space and
d^{\prime}
be the metric defined in (
1.2
).
Given an
(L,C)
-quasi-geodesic
\gamma:[a,b]\rightarrow X
with respect to
d^{\prime}
, there exists a continuous and rectifiable
(L,C^{\prime})
-quasi-geodesic
\gamma^{\prime}:[a,b]\rightarrow X
with respect to
d^{\prime}
satisfying the following:

(1)

\gamma^{\prime}(a)=\gamma(a)
and
\gamma^{\prime}(b)=\gamma(b)
;

(2)

C^{\prime}=3(L+C)
;

(3)

\ell^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime}|_{[t,t^{\prime}]})\leq k_{1}d^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime}(t),\gamma^{\prime}(t^{\prime}))+k_{2}
for all
t,t^{\prime}\in[a,b]
, where
k_{1}=L(L+C)
and
k_{2}=(LC^{\prime}+4)(L+C)
;

(4)

d^{\prime}_{H}(\mathrm{Im}(\gamma),\mathrm{Im}(\gamma^{\prime}))\leq L+C
.

Careful readers might already notice that in the situation of
[
1
, Lemma III.H.1.11]
, we need to assume that the
new
metric
d^{\prime}
on
X
is geodesic instead of the current setting that the
original
metric
d
is geodesic. Although the proof is similar, here we also provide one for convenience to readers.

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

Define
\gamma^{\prime}
to agree with
\gamma
on
\Sigma:=\{a,b\}\cup(\mathbb{Z}\cap(a,b))
, then choose geodesic segments with respect to
d
joining the images of successive points in
\Sigma
and define
\gamma^{\prime}
by concatenating linear reparameterisations of these geodesic segments.

Let
[t]
denote the point of
\Sigma
closest to
t\in[a,b]
. Note that the
d^{\prime}
-distance of the images of successive points in
\Sigma
is at most
L+C
, and hence we have

d^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime}(t),\gamma^{\prime}([t]))=\ln\left(1+d(\gamma^{\prime}(t),\gamma^{\prime}([t]))\right)\leq\ln(1+\exp(L+C)-1)\leq L+C,

which implies that
d^{\prime}_{H}(\mathrm{Im}(\gamma),\mathrm{Im}(\gamma^{\prime}))\leq L+C
.
Since
\gamma
is an
(L,C)
-quasi-geodesic with respect to
d^{\prime}
, and
\gamma([t])=\gamma^{\prime}([t])
for all
t\in[a,b]
, we have

d^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime}(t),\gamma^{\prime}(t^{\prime}))\leq d^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime}([t]),\gamma^{\prime}([t^{\prime}]))+2(L+C)\leq L|[t]-[t^{\prime}]|+C+2(L+C)\leq L|t-t^{\prime}|+3(L+C),

and similarly, we have

(3.1)

d^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime}(t),\gamma^{\prime}(t^{\prime}))\geq\frac{1}{L}|t-t^{\prime}|-3(L+C)

for all
t,t^{\prime}\in[a,b]
. Hence
\gamma^{\prime}
is an
(L,C^{\prime})
-quasi-geodesic with respect to
d^{\prime}
.

For any integers
s,s^{\prime}\in\Sigma
with
s\leq s^{\prime}
, Lemma
3.1
tells us that

\ell^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime}|_{[s,s^{\prime}]})=\ell(\gamma^{\prime}|_{[s,s^{\prime}]})=\sum_{k=s}^{s^{\prime}-1}\ell(\gamma^{\prime}|_{[k,k+1]})\leq|s-s^{\prime}|(L+C).

Similarly for any
s,s^{\prime}\in\Sigma
, we have
\ell^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime}|_{[s,s^{\prime}]})\leq(|s-s^{\prime}|+2)(L+C)
. Hence for any
t,t^{\prime}\in[a,b]
, we have

\ell^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime}|_{[t,t^{\prime}]})\leq(|[t]-[t^{\prime}]|+2)(L+C)+(L+C)\leq(|t-t^{\prime}|+4)(L+C).

Combining with Inequality (
3.1
),
we obtain that
\ell(\gamma^{\prime}|_{[t,t^{\prime}]})\leq k_{1}d^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime}(t),\gamma^{\prime}(t^{\prime}))+k_{2}
for
k_{1},k_{2}
defined in (3). Hence we conclude the proof.
∎

Proof of Corollary
3.3
.

Assume that
(X,d^{\prime})
is
(L,C)
-quasi-geodesic for some
L\geq 1
and
C\geq 0
. For any
x,y\in X
, choose an
(L,C)
-quasi geodesic
\gamma:[0,T]\rightarrow X
(with respect to
d^{\prime}
) connecting them. Lemma
3.4
implies that there is a rectifiable path
\gamma^{\prime}:[0,T]\rightarrow X
which is an
(L,3L+3C)
-quasi-geodesic (with respect to
d^{\prime}
) connecting
x
and
y
. Moreover, we have
\ell^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime})\leq k_{1}d^{\prime}(x,y)+k_{2}
for
k_{1}=L(L+C)
and
k_{2}=(3L(L+C)+4)(L+C)
. Hence Lemma
3.1
implies that

k_{1}d^{\prime}(x,y)+k_{2}\geq\ell^{\prime}(\gamma^{\prime})=\ell(\gamma^{\prime})\geq d(x,y)=\exp(d^{\prime}(x,y))-1.

Note that
(X,d)
is unbounded, which implies that
(X,d^{\prime})
is also unbounded. Hence taking
d^{\prime}(x,y)\to\infty
, we conclude a contradiction and finish the proof.
∎

Next, we move to study the relation of the quasi-ball property and the bounded eccentricity property between the metric spaces
(X,d)
and
(X,d^{\prime})
. Again to save the notation, for
x\in X
and
r\geq 0
, we denote
B(x,r)
and
B^{\prime}(x,r)
the closed balls with respect to the metrics
d
and
d^{\prime}
, respectively. For
A\subset X
and
\delta\geq 0
, we denote
\mathcal{N}_{\delta}(A)
and
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}_{\delta}(A)
the
\delta
-neighbourhood of
A
with respect to the metrics
d
and
d^{\prime}
, respectively.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and
d^{\prime}
be the metric on
X
defined in (
1.2
). Then
(X,d)
has the quasi-ball property
if and only if

(X,d^{\prime})
has the quasi-ball property.

Proof.

Firstly, we assume that
(X,d^{\prime})
has the quasi-ball property,
i.e.
, there exists
\delta\geq 0
such that the intersection of any two balls in
(X,d^{\prime})
is
\delta
-close to another ball (
i.e.
, their Hausdorff distance is bounded by
\delta
). Note that there exists
\alpha=\alpha(\delta)>1
such that
x\leq\alpha\ln(1+x)
for all
x\in[0,\delta]
. This implies that for
A,B\subseteq X
with
A\subseteq\mathcal{N}^{\prime}_{\delta}(B)
, we have
A\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{\alpha\delta}(B)
.

Given two balls
B(x,s)
and
B(y,t)
in
(X,d)
, it is clear that
B(x,s)=B^{\prime}(x,\ln(1+s))
and
B(y,t)=B^{\prime}(y,\ln(1+t))
. Hence by the assumption, there exists another ball
B^{\prime}(c,r)
in
(X,d^{\prime})
such that

d^{\prime}_{H}\left(B^{\prime}(x,\ln(1+s))\cap B^{\prime}(y,\ln(1+t)),B^{\prime}(c,r)\right)\leq\delta.

It follows from the previous paragraph that in this case, we have

d_{H}\left(B(x,s)\cap B(y,t),B(c,\exp(r)-1)\right)=d_{H}\left(B^{\prime}(x,\ln(1+s))\cap B^{\prime}(y,\ln(1+t)),B^{\prime}(c,r)\right)\leq\alpha\delta.

Hence
(X,d)
has the quasi-ball property.

The converse holds similarly, using the fact that
\ln(1+x)\leq x
holds for all
x\geq 0
.
∎

Combining Lemma
2.3
, Corollary
3.3
and Lemma
3.5
, we obtain the following, which concludes part of Theorem
1.2
.

Corollary 3.6
.

Let
(X,d)
be a geodesic metric space which is not hyperbolic (
e.g.
, the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with the Euclidean metric) and
d^{\prime}
be the metric on
X
defined in (
1.2
). Then
(X,d^{\prime})
is a non-quasi-geodesic metric space which satisfies the Gromov’s
4
-point condition but
not
the quasi-ball property.

Concerning the bounded eccentricity property, we have the following:

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and
d^{\prime}
be the metric on
X
defined in (
1.2
). If
(X,d)
satisfies the bounded eccentricity property, so does
(X,d^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Given two balls
B^{\prime}(x,\ln(1+r))
and
B^{\prime}(y,\ln(1+s))
in
(X,d^{\prime})
, we assume that their intersection
Y
is non-empty. Note that

Y=B^{\prime}(x,\ln(1+r))\cap B^{\prime}(y,\ln(1+s))=B(x,r)\cap B(y,s)

is again an intersection of balls in
(X,d)
. Hence by the assumption, there exist
c,c^{\prime}\in X
and
R\geq 0
such that

B(c,R)\subseteq Y\subseteq B(c^{\prime},R+\delta_{0}).

Therefore in
(X,d^{\prime})
, we have

B^{\prime}(c,\ln(1+R))\subseteq Y\subseteq B^{\prime}(c^{\prime},\ln(1+R+\delta_{0})).

Then the eccentricity of
Y
in
(X,d^{\prime})
is bounded above by

\ln(1+R+\delta_{0})-\ln(1+R)=\ln(1+\frac{\delta_{0}}{1+R})\leq\ln(1+\delta_{0})\leq\delta_{0},

which concludes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

Readers might wonder whether the converse to Lemma
3.7
holds. Unfortunately, in general this is false. Note that if we have

B^{\prime}(c,\ln(1+R))\subseteq Y\subseteq B^{\prime}(c^{\prime},\ln(1+R)+\delta_{0})

in
(X,d^{\prime})
, then it implies that

B(c,R)\subseteq Y\subseteq B(c^{\prime},\exp(\delta_{0})\cdot(1+R)-1)

in
(X,d)
. Hence the eccentricity of
Y
in
(X,d)
is bounded by
(\exp(\delta_{0})-1)(1+R)
. However, if
R
(
i.e.
, the radius of the ball contained in
Y
) does not have a uniform upper bound, then neither does the eccentricity of
Y
.

Remark
3.8
suggests the following partial converse to Lemma
3.7
:

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and
d^{\prime}
be the metric on
X
defined in (
1.2
). Assume that there exists a sequence of subsets
\{Y_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
X
satisfying the following:

(1)

each
Y_{n}
is the intersection of two balls in
(X,d)
;

(2)

there exists
M>0
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the radius (with respect to
d
) of any ball contained in
Y_{n}
is bounded above by
M
;

(3)

the eccentricity of
Y_{n}
in
(X,d)
is not uniformly bounded.

Then
(X,d^{\prime})
does
not
satisfy the bounded eccentricity property.

Proof.

Assume the opposite,
i.e.
, there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that the eccentricity of the intersection of any two balls in
(X,d^{\prime})
is uniformly bounded by
\delta_{0}
. Hence by condition (1) we know that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist
c_{n},c_{n}^{\prime}\in X
and
r_{n}\geq 0
such that

B^{\prime}(c_{n},\ln(1+r_{n}))\subseteq Y_{n}\subseteq B^{\prime}(c_{n}^{\prime},\ln(1+r_{n})+\delta_{0}).

Hence in
(X,d)
, we have

B(c_{n},r_{n})\subseteq Y_{n}\subseteq B(c_{n},\exp(\delta_{0})\cdot(1+r_{n})-1).

By condition (2), we know that
r_{n}\leq M
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Therefore, we have

\exp(\delta_{0})\cdot(1+r_{n})-1-r_{n}=(\exp(\delta_{0})-1)(1+r_{n})\leq(\exp(\delta_{0})-1)(1+M),

which is a contradiction to condition (3) in the assumption.
∎

Example 3.10
.

Now we show that there exists a non-quasi-geodesic metric space which satisfies the Gromov’s
4
-point condition but
not
the bounded eccentricity property. For example, take
(X,d)
to be the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
equipped with the Euclidean metric, and let
d^{\prime}
be the metric on
X
defined in (
1.2
). Lemma
2.3
and Corollary
3.3
imply that
(X,d^{\prime})
is not quasi-geodesic but satisfies the Gromov’s
4
-point condition.

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, take
x_{n}=(0,0)
and
y_{n}=(2n,0)
and set

Y_{n}=B(x_{n},n+1)\cap B(y_{n},n+1).

It is easy to see that in
(X,d)
, the biggest ball contained in
Y_{n}
is
B((n,0),1)
. Moreover, the diameter of
Y_{n}
is

d\left((n,\sqrt{2n+1}),(n,-\sqrt{2n+1})\right)=2\sqrt{2n+1},

which implies that the eccentricity of
Y_{n}
cannot be uniformly bounded. Therefore applying Lemma
3.9
, we conclude the result.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Combining Corollary
3.6
and Example
3.10
, we conclude the proof for Theorem
1.2
.
∎

Finally, recall that in
[
5
]
the following weaker form of the bounded eccentricity property was considered: For a metric space
(X,d)
, there exist
\lambda>0
and
\delta>0
such that for any two balls
B_{1},B_{2}\subseteq X
with non-empty intersection there exist
z,z^{\prime}\in X
and
r\geq 0
such that

B(z,r)\subseteq B_{1}\cap B_{2}\subseteq B(z^{\prime},\lambda r+\delta).

Wenger showed in
[
5
]
that this condition also implies Gromov’s hyperbolicity for geodesic metric spaces.

We remark that this weaker form of the bounded eccentricity property does not hold either for the space
(X,d^{\prime})
constructed in Example
3.10
.
