Entropy factorization via curvature
Pietro Caputo, Justin Salez

Abstract
We develop a new framework for establishing approximate factorization of entropy on arbitrary probability spaces, using a geometric notion known as non-negative sectional curvature. The resulting estimates are equivalent to entropy subadditivity and generalized Brascamp-Lieb inequalities, and provide a sharp modified log-Sobolev inequality for the Gibbs sampler of several particle systems in both continuous and discrete settings. The method allows us to obtain simple proofs of known results, as well as some new inequalities. We illustrate this through various applications, including discrete Gaussian free fields on arbitrary networks, the down-up walk on uniform n n -sets, the uniform measure over permutations, and the uniform measure on the unit sphere in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} . Our method also yields a simple, coupling-based proof of the celebrated logarithmic Sobolev inequality for Langevin diffusions in a convex potential, which is one of the most emblematic applications of the Bakry-Émery criterion.

Introduction

1.1
Motivation

In this motivational section, we consider a product probability measure
\mathbf{P}
on a
n-
dimensional measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}(\Omega_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i})
.

Variance tensorization.

We write
{\mathbf{E}}[f]
and
{\mathrm{Var}}(f)
for the expectation and variance of a random variable
f\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P})
. For
i\in[n]
, we let
Z_{i}\colon\Omega\to\Omega_{i}
denote the projection onto the
i-
th coordinate, and we define
{\mathbf{E}}_{i}[f]:={\mathbf{E}}[f|Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{i-1},Z_{i+1},\ldots,Z_{n}]
and
{\mathrm{Var}}_{i}(f):={\mathbf{E}}_{i}[f^{2}]-{\mathbf{E}}^{2}_{i}[f]
. With this notation, the celebrated Efron-Stein inequality asserts that

\displaystyle\forall f\in L^{2},\qquad{\mathrm{Var}}\left(f\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{Var}}_{i}(f)\right].

(1)

This property is the starting point of the well-developed theory of concentration of Lipschitz observables on product spaces, as exposed in the comprehensive textbook
[
10
]
or the beautiful lecture notes
[
38
]
. From a dynamical viewpoint, it can also be regarded as a dimension-free Poincaré inequality for the Gibbs sampler associated with
\mathbf{P}
.

Entropy tensorization.

A far-reaching observation is that a similar relation holds between the entropy
{\mathrm{Ent}}(f):={\mathbf{E}}[f\log f]-{\mathbf{E}}[f]\log{\mathbf{E}}[f]
and its conditional versions
{\mathrm{Ent}}_{i}(f):={\mathbf{E}}_{i}[f\log f]-{\mathbf{E}}_{i}[f]\log{\mathbf{E}}_{i}[f]
,
i\in[n]
. Specifically, we have

\displaystyle\forall f\in L\log L,\qquad{\mathrm{Ent}}\left(f\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\mathbf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{Ent}}_{i}(f)\right],

(2)

where
L\log L
denotes the set of measurable functions
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
{\mathbf{E}}[|f\log f|]<\infty
. The functional inequality (
2
) implies (
1
) by a classical perturbation argument around constant functions. It admits several notable consequences, including a sub-Gaussian concentration estimate for Lipschitz observables, and a dimension-free modified log-Sobolev inequality for the Gibbs sampler associated with
\mathbf{P}
. We again refer the reader to
[
10
,
38
]
for details. In fact, this entropy tensorization happens to be a special case of an even more general inequality due to Shearer, which we refer to as
block factorization
of entropy.

Block factorization.

Given a set
A\subset[n]
, we write
{\mathbf{E}}_{A}[\cdot]:={\mathbf{E}}[\cdot|Z_{j}\colon j\in A^{c}]
for the conditional expectation given all coordinates in
A^{c}:=[n]\setminus A
, and
{\mathrm{Ent}}_{A}[f]:={\mathbf{E}}_{A}[f\log f]-{\mathbf{E}}_{A}[f]\log{\mathbf{E}}_{A}[f]
for the corresponding conditional entropy. With this notation at hand, Shearer’s inequality (see, e.g.,
[
30
]
) asserts that

\displaystyle\forall f\in L\log L,\qquad\theta_{\star}\,{\mathrm{Ent}}\left(f\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}\,{\mathbf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{Ent}}_{A}(f)\right],

(3)

for any non-negative weights
(\theta_{A})_{A\subset[n]}
, where
\theta_{\star}:=\min_{1\leq i\leq n}\sum_{A\ni i}\theta_{A}
is the smallest marginal weight. This functional inequality considerably generalizes the entropy tensorization (
2
), which corresponds to the choice
\theta_{A}={\bf 1}_{|A|=1}
. It implies a dimension-free modified log-Sobolev inequality for the
Block Dynamics
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P})
that consists in re-sampling any block of coordinates
(Z_{i}\colon i\in A)
at rate
\theta_{A}
according to its conditional law given
(Z_{j}\colon j\in A^{c})
.

Approximate block factorization.

The concept of entropy factorization, and its use in the analysis of log-Sobolev inequalities go back to classical work on the Glauber dynamics for non-product measures such as lattice spin systems
[
29
,
37
,
31
,
23
,
20
]
. This point of view was then revisited in
[
32
,
15
,
33
]
.
Motivated by the powerful consequences of Shearer’s inequality in probability, combinatorics, and functional analysis,
[
17
,
9
,
12
]
have recently investigated more systematically the possibility of establishing approximate versions of (
3
) that apply to non-product measures. More precisely, given a non-negative vector
\theta=(\theta_{A})_{A\subset[n]}
and an arbitrary probability measure
\mathbf{P}
on an
n-
dimensional measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}(\Omega_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i})
, one looks for a constant
\kappa=\kappa(\mathbf{P},\theta)>0
, as large as possible, such that

\displaystyle\forall f\in L\log L,\qquad\kappa\,{\mathrm{Ent}}\left(f\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}\,{\mathbf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{Ent}}_{A}(f)\right].

(4)

As in the case of Shearer’s inequality, one important motivation for studying approximate block factorizations is the fact that the constant
\kappa
in (
4
) provides a lower bound on the modified log-Sobolev constant for the block dynamics associated with the weights
\theta
. We refer the reader to the recent lecture notes
[
14
]
for a self-contained introduction to this active line of research.

Entropy subadditivity and Brascamp-Lieb inequalities.

Another important motivation stems from the equivalence between block factorizations, entropy subadditivity and the Brascamp-Lieb inequalities.
Indeed, using the classical
chain rule for entropy, for any sub
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{F}
we write

\displaystyle\forall f\in L\log L,\qquad{\mathrm{Ent}}(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[{\mathrm{Ent}}(f|{\cal G})]+{\mathrm{Ent}}({\mathbf{E}}[f|{\cal G}]),

(5)

where
{\mathbf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{Ent}}(f|\mathcal{G})\right]:={\mathbf{E}}\left[f\log(f/{\mathbf{E}}(f|\mathcal{G}))\right]
is the averaged conditional entropy of
f
with respect to
\mathcal{G}
.
Assuming (without loss of generality) that the weights
(\theta_{A})_{A\subset[n]}
are normalized so as to sum to
1
, we can rewrite the approximate entropy factorization (
4
) equivalently as an
entropy subadditivity
statement of the form:

\displaystyle\forall f\in L\log L,\qquad\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}\,{\mathrm{Ent}}\left({\mathbf{E}}_{A}[f]\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\kappa){\mathrm{Ent}}(f).

(6)

On the other hand, it is well known
[
18
]
that by Legendre duality, the subadditivity (
6
) is equivalent to the Brascamp-Lieb inequality

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\left[\textstyle{\prod_{A\subset[n]}}g_{A}(Z_{A^{c}})^{c_{A}}\right]\leq\textstyle{\prod_{A\subset[n]}}{\mathbf{E}}\left[g_{A}(Z_{A^{c}})\right]^{c_{A}}\,,

(7)

for all bounded measurable functions
g_{A}:\mathbb{R}^{|A^{c}|}\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
, where, for all
A\subset[n]
,
c_{A}:=\frac{\theta_{A}}{1-\kappa}
, and
Z_{A}
denotes the set of variables

(Z_{i},i\in A)
. We note that if
f
is a probability density with respect to
\mathbf{P}
, so that
f\mathbf{P}
defines a probability measure on
\Omega
, then
{\mathbf{E}}_{A}[f]
is the density of the pushforward of
f\mathbf{P}
under the projection
Z\mapsto Z_{A^{c}}
(the marginal of
f\mathbf{P}
on
Z_{A^{c}}
). In fact, the equivalence
between (
7
) and (
4
) applies more generally when the projection
Z\mapsto Z_{A^{c}}
is replaced by
an arbitrary measurable map
Z\mapsto B_{A}(Z)
, and
{\mathbf{E}}_{A}[f]
is replaced by the conditional expectation
{\mathbf{E}}[f|B_{A}(Z)]
; see
[
18
, Theorem 2.1]
.
The inequalities (
7
) obtained in this way are referred to as Brascamp-Lieb (B-L) type inequality, in analogy with the classical B-L inequality, which corresponds to the setting where
\mathbf{P}
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the measurable maps
B_{A}
are linear; see
[
11
,
27
,
5
,
8
,
26
]
.

Our contribution.

We adopt a more general viewpoint on the inequalities (
7
) and (
4
), by replacing conditional expectations
{\mathbf{E}}_{A}[f]

with arbitrary, not necessarily self-adjoint, Markov operators
f\mapsto T_{A}f
. It turns out that it is not hard to extend the duality to the new framework, thus generalizing the equivalence proved in
[
18
]
; see Section
1.2
.

Within this general framework, we propose a new approach towards establishing the approximate entropy factorization (
4
), based on
non-negative sectional curvature
. This simple probabilistic and geometric notion, an
L^{\infty}
version of Ollivier’s Ricci curvature
[
35
]
, has recently been shown to play a fundamental role in quantifying the entropy dissipation rate of Markov processes
[
36
,
34
,
16
]
. In fact, our main result, stated in Section
1.2
below, is sufficiently general to also contain the main finding of
[
16
]
.
We demonstrate the robustness of our method through various applications in both continuous and discrete settings.
For instance, the method enables us to derive optimal entropy factorizations for Gaussian free fields on arbitrary networks, providing optimal bounds on the spectral gap and relative entropy decay of any weighted block dynamics. We also discuss analogous bounds for the uniform measure over permutations and over the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Although obtaining optimal constants for these models with any choice of weights is significantly challenging, our method achieves bounds that match the state-of-the-art.
Additionally, we apply our method to the down-up walk for uniform

n
-sets, a special case of the well-studied down-up walk on the bases of a matroid. Recent studies have obtained remarkable entropy contraction bounds for these processes using properties of log-concave polynomials (see
[
22
,
2
]
). In the special case of uniform
n
-sets, our method produces a slightly better bound than that derived from log-concavity.
Finally, we highlight the versatility of our method by providing a straightforward coupling proof of the well known Bakry-Émery criterion for Langevin diffusions in a convex potential.

1.2
General framework

We consider a generalized version of the inequality (
6
) where
the conditional expectations are replaced with arbitrary Markov operators.

Markov operators.

From now on, we consider an arbitrary probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P})
, and we simply write
L^{p}
for
L^{p}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P})
,
p\in[1,\infty]
. We recall that a
transition kernel
on
\Omega
is a function
T\colon\Omega\times\mathcal{F}\to[0,1]
such that

(i)

for each point
x\in\Omega
, the function
E\mapsto T(x,E)
is a probability measure;

(ii)

for each event
E\in\mathcal{F}
, the function
x\mapsto T(x,E)
is measurable.

Integrating with respect to such a kernel naturally gives rise to a bounded linear operator on
L^{\infty}
, which is also denoted by
T
, and whose action is as follows:

\displaystyle\forall x\in\Omega,\qquad(Tf)(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f(y)\,T(x,{\rm d}y).

(8)

Of course,
Tf
is non-negative as soon as
f
is, and
T{\bf 1}={\bf 1}
: such operators are usually known as
Markov operators
. Note that the underlying transition kernel can be recovered from the operator via the formula
T(x,E)=(T{\bf 1}_{E})(x)
, so that the above identification is legit. We will always assume that
T
is
measure preserving
, in the sense that for all
f\in L^{\infty}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[Tf]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[f],

(9)

where we recall that
{\mathbf{E}}[\cdot]
denotes the expectation w.r.t.
\mathbf{P}
. Thanks to this stationarity and Jensen’s inequality, the formula (
8
) actually defines a bounded linear operator
T\colon L^{p}\to L^{p}
with operator norm
1
, for any
p\in[1,\infty]
. Its adjoint
T^{\star}
is also a measure-preserving Markov operator, characterized by the relation

\displaystyle\forall f,g\in L^{2},\qquad{\mathbf{E}}[(T^{\star}f)g]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[(Tg)f].

(10)

As above, we write
f\in L\log L
to mean that
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is measurable with
{\mathbf{E}}[|f\log f|]<\infty
. Note that
Tf
is then well-defined and in
L\log L
, with

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Ent}}(Tf)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Ent}}(f).

We are now in position to introduce the general inequality that we propose to investigate.

General functional inequality.

For an integer
M\geq 1
, we assume to be given a family
(T_{1},\ldots,T_{M})
of measure-preserving transition kernels on our workspace
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P})
, identified with the corresponding Markov operators, along with a probability vector
(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{M})
. Our aim is to find a constant
\kappa\in[0,1]
, as large as possible, such that

\displaystyle\forall f\in L\log L,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{M}\theta_{i}\,{\mathrm{Ent}}(T_{i}^{\star}f)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\kappa){\mathrm{Ent}}(f).

(11)

Since, for any
\sigma-
field
\mathcal{G}\subset\mathcal{F}
, the conditional expectation
f\mapsto{\mathbf{E}}[f|\cal G]
is a measure-preserving self-adjoint Markov operator, the inequality (
11
) contains (
6
) as a special case. Also note that, by the convexity of the functional
f\mapsto{\mathrm{Ent}}(f)
, the inequality (
11
) implies that the average Markov operator
T:=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\theta_{i}T_{i}
satisfies the
1
-step entropy contraction property

\displaystyle\forall f\in L\log L,\qquad{\mathrm{Ent}}(T^{\star}f)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\kappa){\mathrm{Ent}}(f),

(12)

which we can iterate to deduce exponential convergence to equilibrium, in relative entropy, at rate
\kappa
, for the Markov chain with transition kernel
T
. In light of those two observations, it seems of considerable interest to find simple and broadly applicable criteria that guarantee (
11
) with an explicit (and, ideally, sharp) constant
\kappa
. This is precisely the aim of the present paper.

Duality and generalized B-L inequalities.

Before presenting our main result, we observe that the equivalence
between (
7
) and (
4
) established in
[
18
]
admits the following generalization in our setting.

Theorem 1

(Duality)
.

Consider Markov operators
(T_{1},\dots,T_{M})
, a constant
\kappa\in(0,1)
, and a probability vector
(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{M})
, and define

c_{i}=\frac{\theta_{i}}{1-\kappa}\,,\qquad i=1,\dots,M.

The following statements are equivalent.

(a)

Entropy subadditivity:

\displaystyle\forall f\in L\log L,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{M}\theta_{i}\,{\mathrm{Ent}}(T_{i}^{\star}f)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\kappa){\mathrm{Ent}}(f)\,.

(13)

(b)

Generalized B-L inequality:

\displaystyle\forall\varphi_{1},\dots,\varphi_{M}\in L^{\infty},\qquad{\mathbf{E}}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{M}e^{c_{i}T_{i}\varphi_{i}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\prod_{i=1}^{M}{\mathbf{E}}\left[e^{\varphi_{i}}\right]^{c_{i}}\,.

(14)

We remark that when the Markov operators
T_{i}
are conditional expectations of the form
T_{i}f={\mathbf{E}}[f|\mathcal{F}_{i}]
for some sub
\sigma
-algebras
\mathcal{F}_{i}
, Theorem
1
yields the duality proved in
[
18
, Theorem 2.1]
.
In that case
T_{i}
is an orthogonal projection in
L^{2}
, and therefore
T_{i}
is self-adjoint, and by the chain rule (
5
) one has that both statements (1) and (2) are equivalent to an entropy factorization statement of the form

\displaystyle\forall f\in L\log L,\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{M}\theta_{i}\,{\mathbf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{Ent}}(f|\mathcal{F}_{i})\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\kappa\,{\mathrm{Ent}}(f)\,.

(15)

We note that the case where
T_{i}
is not a conditional expectation goes beyond the common B-L inequality setup.
The proof, however, boils down to essentially the same argument as in

[
18
]
, which is based on the well known variational principle for relative entropy stating that

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Ent}}(f)\;=\;\sup_{h}\;\left\{{\mathbf{E}}[fh]-\log{\mathbf{E}}[e^{h}]\right\},

(16)

where
h
ranges over all functions on
\Omega
such that
e^{h}\in L^{1}
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Suppose (b) holds. Let
f\in L\log L
and set
h=\sum_{i=1}^{M}c_{i}T_{i}\log(T_{i}^{\star}f)
. Without loss of generality we may assume that
f\in L^{\infty}
and
{\mathbf{E}}(f)=1
.
From the variational principle (
16
),
one has

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Ent}}f\geq{\mathbf{E}}[fh]-\log{\mathbf{E}}[e^{h}]=\sum_{i}c_{i}{\mathbf{E}}\left[fT_{i}\log(T_{i}^{\star}f)\right]-\log{\mathbf{E}}\left[\prod_{i}e^{c_{i}T_{i}\varphi_{i}}\right]\,,

(17)

where we define
\varphi_{i}:=\log(T_{i}^{\star}f)
. From the assumption (b) it follows that

\log{\mathbf{E}}\left[\prod_{i}e^{c_{i}T_{i}\varphi_{i}}\right]\leq\sum_{i}c_{i}\log{\mathbf{E}}\left[e^{\varphi_{i}}\right].

Since

{\mathbf{E}}\left[e^{\varphi_{i}}\right]={\mathbf{E}}\left[T_{i}^{\star}f\right]=1
,
using also
{\mathbf{E}}\left[fT_{i}\log(T_{i}^{\star}f)\right]={\mathrm{Ent}}(T_{i}^{\star}f)
, (
17
) implies

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Ent}}f\geq\sum_{i}c_{i}\,{\mathrm{Ent}}(T_{i}^{\star}f)\,.

(18)

This proves (b)
\implies
(a). To prove the converse, take arbitrary functions
\varphi_{i}\in L^{\infty}
, and set
h=\sum_{i=1}^{M}c_{i}T_{i}\varphi_{i}
.
Taking
f=e^{h}/{\mathbf{E}}[e^{h}]
, we observe that

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Ent}}f={\mathbf{E}}[fh]-\log{\mathbf{E}}[e^{h}]=\sum_{i}c_{i}{\mathbf{E}}[T_{i}^{\star}f\varphi_{i}]-\log{\mathbf{E}}\left[\prod_{i}e^{c_{i}T_{i}\varphi_{i}}\right].

(19)

From the assumption (a) and (
19
) it follows that

\displaystyle\log{\mathbf{E}}\left[\prod_{i}e^{c_{i}T_{i}\varphi_{i}}\right]\leq\sum_{i}c_{i}{\mathbf{E}}[T_{i}^{\star}f\varphi_{i}]-\sum_{i}c_{i}{\mathrm{Ent}}(T_{i}^{\star}f)\,.

(20)

From the variational principle (
16
), for each
i\in[n]
one has

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Ent}}(T_{i}^{\star}f)={\mathbf{E}}\left[T_{i}^{\star}f\log T_{i}^{\star}f\right]\geq{\mathbf{E}}\left[T_{i}^{\star}f\varphi_{i}\right]-\log{\mathbf{E}}\left[e^{\varphi_{i}}\right].

(21)

If we multiply by
c_{i}
and sum over
i
in (
21
), and then take exponentials in (
20
) we obtain

{\mathbf{E}}\left[\prod_{i}e^{c_{i}T_{i}\varphi_{i}}\right]\leq\prod_{i}{\mathbf{E}}\left[e^{\varphi_{i}}\right]^{c_{i}}\,.

This proves (a)
\implies
(b).

1
had already been observed; see
[
1
]
for an early result in the case
M=1
, and
[
28
]
for a general result analogous to the one considered here. We also refer to
[

for a list of interesting applications of this generalized framework, such as hypercontractivity, strong data processing inequalities and other information-theoretic questions.

1.3
Main result

We henceforth assume that
\Omega
is equipped with a metric
\,{\rm dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
, which we are free to choose as we want as long as the square-integrability condition

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\,{\rm dist}^{2}(o,x)\mathbf{P}({\rm d}x)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle+\infty,

(22)

holds for some (and hence every) point
o\in\Omega
.
We write
W_{1}
and
W_{\infty}
for the resulting
L^{1}
and
L^{\infty}-
Wasserstein distances, defined for probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
as follows:

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\inf_{X\sim\mu,Y\sim\nu}{\mathbb{E}}[\,{\rm dist}(X,Y)],

\displaystyle W_{\infty}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\inf_{X\sim\mu,Y\sim\nu}\mathrm{ess\,sup}\,{\rm dist}(X,Y),

where the infimum runs over all random pairs
(X,Y)
with marginals
\mu
and
\nu
. Also, we define the
essential Lipschitz constant
of a function
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}(f)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\inf_{N\in\mathcal{N}}\,\sup\left\{\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{\,{\rm dist}(x,y)}\colon(x,y)\in(\Omega\setminus N)^{2},x\neq y\right\},

where
\mathcal{N}\subset\mathcal{F}
denotes the set of zero-probability events, henceforth called
null sets
.

Theorem 2

(Main result)
.

Consider Markov operators
(T_{1},\dots,T_{M})
, and a probability vector
(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{M})
. Assume that, for some numbers
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{M}\geq 0
, and a constant
\kappa\in[0,1]
, the following conditions are satisfied:

(i)

(Sectional curvature). For each
1\leq i\leq M
and all
x,y
outside a null set,

\displaystyle W_{\infty}\left(T_{i}^{\star}(x,\cdot),T_{i}^{\star}(y,\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\ell_{i}\,{\rm dist}(x,y).

(ii)

(Average curvature). For all
x,y
outside a null set,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\theta_{i}\ell_{i}W_{1}\left(T_{i}(x,\cdot),T_{i}(y,\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(1-\kappa\right)\,{\rm dist}(x,y).

(23)

(iii)

(Regularity). For each
1\leq i\leq M,
we have

\displaystyle\forall f\in L^{\infty},\qquad\mathrm{Lip}(T_{i}f)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\infty.

Then, the entropy contraction property (
11
) holds with constant
\kappa
.

There are several important remarks to make about this result.

Remark 1

(Regularization)
.

Assumption (iii) trivially holds when
\Omega
is finite and, more generally, when the metric
\,{\rm dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
is discrete. When it fails, it can often be bypassed by an appropriate regularization. More precisely, suppose that our workspace
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P})
admits a family of measure-preserving transition kernels
(P_{\tau})_{\tau\in(0,1)}
such that

1.

(Continuity). For each
f\in L^{2}
, we have
P_{\tau}f\xrightarrow[\tau\to 0]{}f
in
L^{2}
.

2.

(Regularity). For each
\tau\in(0,1)
, and each
f\in L^{\infty}
, we have
\mathrm{Lip}(P_{\tau}f)<\infty
.

3.

(Non-negative sectional curvature). For some sequence
\epsilon_{\tau}\to 0
, as
\tau\to 0
, for all
x,y\in\Omega
outside a null set,

\displaystyle W_{\infty}\left(P_{\tau}(x,\cdot),P_{\tau}(y,\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon_{\tau})\,{\rm dist}(x,y)

\displaystyle W_{\infty}\left(P_{\tau}^{\star}(x,\cdot),P_{\tau}^{\star}(y,\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon_{\tau})\,{\rm dist}(x,y)\,.

Then, Assumption (iii) in Theorem
2
is unnecessary: indeed, we can apply the theorem to the operators
(P_{\tau}T_{i})_{1\leq i\leq M}
– which do fulfill all the requirements – instead of
(T_{i})_{1\leq i\leq M}
and then let
\tau\to 0
to obtain the desired conclusion. This regularization trick will be implemented in the Gaussian case in Section
3.1
, using the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semi-group.

Remark 2

(Peres-Tetali conjecture)
.

The result is already highly non-trivial in the special case where
M=1
,
\ell=1
and
\Omega
is finite: indeed, it is then exactly the main result of the recent preprint
[
16
]
, devoted to the celebrated Peres-Tetali conjecture. The present paper can be seen as a (powerful) refinement of the method used therein.

Remark 3

(The product case)
.

Recovering the original Shearer inequality (
3
) from Theorem
2
is easy: consider a product space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P})=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}(\Omega_{i},\mathcal{F}_{i},\mathbf{P}_{i})
, for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and define a family of self-adjoint Markov operators
(T_{A})_{A\subset[n]}

by the formula

\displaystyle T_{A}f

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{A}[f]\ =\ {\mathbf{E}}[f|Z_{i},i\in A^{c}],

where we recall that
Z=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n})
denotes the identity map on
\Omega
.
More explicitly,
T_{A}(x,\cdot)
is the law of the random vector obtained from
x
by replacing
x_{i}
with
Z_{i}
for each
i\in A
. Now, equip
\Omega
with the Hamming distance
\,{\rm dist}(x,y):=\#\{i\in[n]\colon x_{i}\neq y_{i}\}
. Then,

\displaystyle W_{\infty}\left(T_{A}(x,\cdot),T_{A}(y,\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\#\{i\in A^{c}\colon x_{i}\neq y_{i}\}\ \leq\ \,{\rm dist}(x,y),

as witnessed by the trivial coupling that consists in replacing both
x_{i}
and
y_{i}
by
Z_{i}
for each
i\in A
. Since
W_{1}\leq W_{\infty}
, we moreover have, for any probability vector
(\theta_{A})_{A\subset[n]}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}W_{1}\left(T_{A}(x,\cdot),T_{A}(y,\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf 1}_{x_{i}\neq y_{i}}\left(1-\sum_{A\ni i}\theta_{A}\right)\ \leq\ (1-\theta_{\star})\,{\rm dist}(x,y).

Thus, Theorem
2
applies with
M=2^{n}
,
\ell\equiv 1
,
\kappa=\theta_{\star}
. Therefore, for all
f\in L\log L
,

\displaystyle\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}\,{\mathrm{Ent}}\left({\mathbf{E}}_{A}[f]\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\theta_{\star}){\mathrm{Ent}}(f).

Using the chain rule (
5
), this is equivalent to (
3
).

1.4
Some examples

We consider several applications of our main result, to both continuous and discrete settings.

Discrete Gaussian Free Field.

The discrete Gaussian free field is a Gaussian probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with covariance of the form
\Gamma=({\rm Id}-P)^{-1}
, where
P
is a symmetric
n\times n
sub-stochastic matrix, that is
P_{i,j}\geq 0
for all
i,j\in[n]
,
\sum_{j}P_{i,j}\leq 1
for all
i\in[n]
, and such that
{\rm Id}-P
is invertible. We also assume that
P
is irreducible, that is for all
i,j
there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
P^{k}_{i,j}>0
. A well studied example in equilibrium statistical mechanics is obtained by taking
P
as the transition matrix of the random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
that is killed upon exiting a finite domain
V\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, see e.g.
[
24
, Chapter 8]
.

Thus, we fix a positive definite covariance matrix
\Gamma=(\Gamma_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
as above, and consider the probability space

\Omega:=\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad\mathcal{F}:=\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\qquad\mathbf{P}({\rm d}x):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}\det(\Gamma)}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{\top}\Gamma^{-1}x}{2}\right){\rm d}x.

(24)

As motivated in Section
1.1
, we assume to be given a probability vector
(\theta_{A})_{A\subset[n]}
, and we seek to estimate the optimal constants
\lambda
and
\kappa
in the inequalities

\displaystyle\forall f\in L^{2},\qquad\lambda\,{\mathrm{Var}}(f)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}{\mathbf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{Var}}_{A}(f)\right],

(25)

\displaystyle\forall f\in L\log L,\qquad\kappa\,{\mathrm{Ent}}(f)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}{\mathbf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{Ent}}_{A}(f)\right].

(26)

The constant
\lambda
coincides with the
spectral gap
of the
weighted block dynamics
, that is the Markov chain where at each step a block
A\subset[n]
is picked with probability
\theta_{A}
and the variables
(X_{i},i\in A)
are resampled according to the conditional distribution
\mathbf{P}(\cdot|X_{A^{c}})
. Indeed, (
25
) can be written in the form
\lambda\,{\mathrm{Var}}(f)\leq\mathcal{E}(f,f)
where
\mathcal{E}
denotes the Dirichlet form of the weighted block dynamics

\displaystyle\forall f,g\in L^{2},\qquad\mathcal{E}(f,g)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}{\mathbf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{Cov}}_{A}(f,g)\right],

(27)

and
{\mathrm{Cov}}_{A}(f,g)={\mathbf{E}}_{A}[fg]-{\mathbf{E}}_{A}[f]{\mathbf{E}}_{A}[g]
is the covariance w.r.t.
\mathbf{P}(\cdot|X_{A^{c}})
.
On the other hand, the constant
\kappa
in (
26
) provides a lower bound on the
modified log-Sobolev
constant, describing the rate of exponential decay of the relative entropy for the same dynamics in continuous time. Indeed, the modified log-Sobolev constant
\rho_{\rm MLS}
is defined as the largest
\rho\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\forall f\in L\log L,\qquad\rho\,{\mathrm{Ent}}(f)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f,\log f),

(28)

and, since by convexity
{\mathrm{Cov}}_{A}(f,\log f)\geq{\mathrm{Ent}}_{A}(f)
for all
f\in L\log L
,
A\subset[n]
, it follows that
\kappa\leq\rho_{\rm MLS}
. Moreover, it is well known that
\lambda\geq\kappa
and
2\lambda\geq\rho_{\rm MLS}
.
We refer e.g. to
[
14
]
for more background and a proof of these standard relations.

An application of our general framework will allow us to control
the
fundamental constants
\lambda,\kappa
in terms of the least eigenvalue of the “laplacian”
\Delta:={\rm Id}-P
, and to show that they actually coincide for the Glauber dynamics, that is the process obtained when
\theta_{A}=\frac{1}{n}\,{\bf 1}_{|A|=1}
.

Theorem 3
.

For any irreducible, symmetric, sub-stochastic matrix
P
such that
{\rm Id}-P
is invertible, the Gaussian free field
\mathbf{P}
with covariance
\Gamma=({\rm Id}-P)^{-1}
satisfies

\displaystyle\kappa\geq\delta\,\theta_{\star}\,,

(29)

where
\delta
denotes the smallest eigenvalue of the matrix
\Delta={\rm Id}-P
, and
\theta_{\star}=\min_{1\leq i\leq n}\sum_{A\ni i}\theta_{A}
.
Moreover, the Glauber dynamics satisfies

\displaystyle\lambda=\kappa=\frac{\delta}{n}\,.

(30)

Theorem
3
can be seen as the Gaussian Free Field version of Shearer inequality, which is shown to be optimal for the Glauber dynamics. In fact, as the proof will show, in that case the inequality (
25
) is saturated by a linear function
f(x)=z^{\top}x
, where
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is such that
\Delta z=\delta\,z
. Thus the spectral gap is achieved at a linear function. From the above mentioned inequalities
\kappa\leq\rho_{\rm MLS}\leq 2\lambda
, this also shows that the Glauber dynamics has a modified log-Sobolev constant satisfying

\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{n}\;\leq\;\rho_{\rm MLS}\;\leq\;\frac{2\delta}{n}\,\,.

(31)

Quantifying the speed of convergence to equilibrium of the
Glauber dynamics for Gaussian free fields is a natural problem which, to the best of our knowledge, has only been investigated in the two-dimensional square lattice
[
25
]
, that is when
P
is the transition matrix of the random walk on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
that is killed upon exiting a finite domain
V\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. As a consequence of Theorem
3
, one can compute explicitly both the spectral gap and the entropy contraction constant
\kappa
of the lattice Gaussian free field, for
any dimension
d\geq 1
, and for any domain
V
of the form
V=[n_{1}]\times\cdots\times[n_{d}]\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
since in that case
\lambda=\kappa=\delta/n
, and

\displaystyle\delta\;=\;\frac{2}{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left[1-\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n_{k}+1}\right)\right],

which behaves like
2\pi^{2}/n^{2}
in the regime
n_{1}=n_{2}=\cdots=n_{d}=n
and
n\to\infty
.

Remark 4
.

Combining Theorem
3
with Theorem
1
one obtains equivalent B-L inequalities for the gaussian measures. The latter were in fact already known, since they can be directly related to the classical B-L inequalities for Lebesgue measures, see e.g.
[
6
, Fact 6]
. A direct proof of the gaussian B-L inequality is also possible along the lines of
[
26
]
, see
[
21
, Theorem 3]
. Thus, one could have obtained Theorem
3
from these known estimates. However, the purpose of Theorem
3
is to illustrate that an entirely different approach based on curvature can be used to obtain optimal entropy factorization estimates, and, in principle, it can yield an alternative proof of the classical B-L inequalities as well. One caveat is that we assume the specific form
\Gamma=({\rm Id}-P)^{-1}
for the covariance matrix of our gaussian measure, whereas it would be desirable to have the result for all positive definite matrices
\Gamma
. For technical reasons, in this more general setup
at the moment we can only prove the desired estimate up to a factor
2
, that is we can show that
\sigma\leq 2\kappa\leq 2\sigma
, for an explicit spectral quantity
\sigma=\sigma(\Gamma,\theta)
, see Remark
5
for more details. We believe it should be possible to obtain
\kappa=\lambda=\sigma
for all
(\Gamma,\theta)
, as we prove in the case of
\Gamma=({\rm Id}-P)^{-1}
with Glauber dynamics in Theorem
3
, thus obtaining full applicability of our argument to derive Gaussian and classical B-L inequalities.

Permutations and weighted block shuffles.

The next example we consider is the case where
\mathbf{P}
is the uniform measure over
S_{n}
, the set of permutations of
[n]
:

\Omega:=S_{n},\qquad\mathbf{P}(\sigma):=\frac{1}{n!}\,,\quad\sigma\in\Omega\,.

(32)

As above, we consider the weighted block dynamics associated to a given probability vector
(\theta_{A},A\subset[n])
, with Dirichlet form formally given exactly by the expression in (
27
).
If we think of the configuration
\sigma\in\Omega
as representing the positions of
n
cards, the weighted block dynamics is the Markov chain where at each step a block
A
is chosen with probability
\theta_{A}

and all cards located in
A
get uniformly reshuffled among themselves. We refer to this as the
\theta
-weighted block shuffle. The case
\theta_{A}=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}{\bf 1}_{|A|=2}
is the well known random transposition dynamics.

Theorem 4
.

For any choice of the probability vector
(\theta_{A},A\subset[n])
,

\displaystyle\forall f\in L\log L,\qquad\theta_{\star\star}\,{\mathrm{Ent}}(f)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}{\mathbf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{Ent}}_{A}(f)\right],

(33)

where
\theta_{\star\star}:=\min_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\sum_{A\supset\{i,j\}}\theta_{A}
.

The estimate (
33
) is equivalent to the subadditivity statement

\displaystyle\forall f\in L\log L,\qquad\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}\,{\mathrm{Ent}}\left({\mathbf{E}}_{A}[f]\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\kappa){\mathrm{Ent}}(f)\,,

(34)

with
\kappa=\theta_{\star\star}
, and can be used to obtain equivalent B-L inequalities via Theorem
1
.
As we observed in (
28
), the bound (
33
) implies
\rho_{\rm MLS}\geq\theta_{\star\star}
, providing a lower bound on the rate of exponential decay to stationarity of the continuous time version of the
\theta
-weighted block shuffle.

It is interesting to note that the estimate (
33
) is equivalent to the B-L bounds proved in
[
6
, Proposition 21]
, which were obtained by an entirely different approach, namely using monotonicity along semigroup interpolations for the corresponding B-L inequality.
On the other hand, in the “mean field” case where

\theta_{A}
depends only on the size of
A
, that is
\theta_{A}=\varphi(|A|)
for some function
\varphi\geq 0
, optimal bounds on the constant
\kappa
in (
34
) were obtained recently in
[
12
]
. In particular,
[
12
, Theorem 1.9]
shows that for any
\ell\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, if
\theta_{A}=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{\ell}}\,{\bf 1}_{|A|=\ell}
, then
(
34
) holds with the optimal constant

\kappa=\kappa_{\ell,n}:=\frac{\log(\ell!)}{\log(n!)},

and that the only extremal functions are Dirac masses.
Here one has
\theta_{\star\star}=\frac{\ell(\ell-1)}{n(n-1)}
which is smaller than
\kappa_{\ell,n}
for
\ell\in\{2,\dots,n-1\}
, and therefore Theorem
4
is not optimal for such homogeneous weights. However,
Theorem
4
, as well as
[
6
, Proposition 21]
, addresses the entropy factorization problem for arbitrary weights
(\theta_{A})
, and in this generality they may provide the best known bounds.

Entropy of
\ell^{n}_{p}
–spherical marginals.

A celebrated result of Carlen-Lieb-Loss
[
19
]
states that the uniform probability measure on the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
satisfies a subadditivity estimate with a factor 2, independently of
n
, with respect to the bound satisfied by a product measure, thus quantifying the departure from independence for this distribution. An equivalent formulation of this is that when
\mathbf{P}
is the uniform probability measure on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
, then the inequality (
6
), taking the homogeneous weights
\theta_{A}:=\frac{1}{n}{\bf 1}_{|A|=n-1}
, holds with constant

\displaystyle\kappa=1-\frac{2}{n}\,,

(35)

for all
n\geq 2
. Moreover, the same authors also show that this constant is optimal. Extensions of this result were later proposed by
[
4
]
and
[
6
]
. In particular, it was shown in
[
6
, Proposition 11]
that for any choice of the probability vector
\theta
, one has the estimate (
6
) with
\kappa=\theta_{\star\star}
, where
\theta_{\star\star}:=\min_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\sum_{A\supset\{i,j\}}\theta_{A}
. Note that this holds for arbitrary
\theta
and it recovers the optimal bound (
35
) in the “all but one” case
\theta_{A}:=\frac{1}{n}{\bf 1}_{|A|=n-1}
. The proof of these bounds was based on an extension of the approach introduced in
[
19
]
, which used monotonicity along semigroup interpolations for the corresponding B-L inequality. In the special case
\theta_{A}:=\frac{1}{\binom{n}{2}}\,{\bf 1}_{|A|=2}
, the associated weighted block dynamics (
27
) is often referred to as the Kac walk on the sphere.
In this case the bound
\kappa=\theta_{\star\star}
gives

\displaystyle\kappa=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\,.

(36)

Letting
\rho_{\rm MLS}
denote the modified log-Sobolev constant from (
28
), the simple bound
\rho_{\rm MLS}\geq\kappa
allows one to obtain
\rho_{\rm MLS}\geq\frac{2}{n(n-1)}
, which recovers (and actually improves by a factor 2) an estimate previously shown by Villani in
[
39
, Theorem 6.1]
by a different type of semigroup interpolation.

Here we discuss how our alternative method could be applied to obtain the same estimates, in the general setting of
\ell^{n}_{p}
–spheres, for all
p>0
. Namely, for
p>0
, define the
\ell^{n}_{p}
–sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}_{p}
as

\displaystyle\Omega\;=\;{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}_{p}\;:=\;\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\;\|x\|_{p}=1\}\,,\qquad\|x\|_{p}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{p}\right)^{1/p}\,.

(37)

We let
\mathbf{P}
denote the law of the vector
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
obtained as

\displaystyle X=\frac{(G_{1},\dots,G_{n})}{S_{p}(G)^{1/p}}\,,\qquad S_{p}(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|x_{i}|^{p}\,,

(38)

where
G=(G_{1},\dots,G_{n})
is a vector of i.i.d. real random variables with density proportional to
e^{-|x|^{p}}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

\mathbf{P}
is also called the
cone measure
on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}_{p}
; see e.g.
[
7
]
. A characteristic property of this construction is that
X
and
S_{p}(G)
are independent. Note that, for
p=1,2
,
\mathbf{P}
coincides with the uniform distribution on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}_{1}
, and
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}_{2}={\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}
respectively.
We believe that it is possible to prove the following statement as a consequence of Theorem
2
.

Conjecture 1
.

For all
p>0
, all probability vectors
\theta
, the cone measure
\mathbf{P}
on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}_{p}
satisfies

\displaystyle\forall f\in L\log L,\qquad\theta_{\star\star}\,{\mathrm{Ent}}(f)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}{\mathbf{E}}\left[{\mathrm{Ent}}_{A}(f)\right].

(39)

For
p=2
this coincides with the result in
[
6
, Proposition 11]
. A related estimate for all
p>0
, under additional symmetry assumptions on
f
, was obtained in
[
6
, Corollary 16]
.
Note that the above statement is formally equivalent to Theorem
4
. In fact, to prove the conjecture one may proceed exactly as in our proof of Theorem
4
, namely by exhibiting a metric on
\Omega
for which Assumptions (i) and (ii) in Theorem
2
are satisfied with
\kappa=\theta_{\star\star}
, when the Markov operators are given by the conditional expectations
T_{A}f={\mathbf{E}}_{A}f
. As we show in
Section
3.3
, this can be achieved by taking the special metric

\,{\rm dist}(x,y):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left||x_{i}|^{p}-|y_{i}|^{p}\right|+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf 1}_{\{x_{i}y_{i}<0\}}\,.

Assumption (iii) however fails in this case. As in the case of Theorem
3
, the proof of Conjecture
1
would be complete if we could establish a regularizing procedure as described in Remark
1
, but we were unable to find the needed regularizing kernels for this model, since the natural diffusion process on
{\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}_{p}
does not seem to have nonnegatve sectional curvature with respect to our metric.

Down-up walk for uniform
n
-sets.

Let
{\bf S}
be a finite space with cardinality
N=|{\bf S}|
. Given
n\in\{1,\dots,N-1\}
, let
\Omega=\binom{{\bf S}}{n}
denote set of all subsets of
{\bf S}
with cardinality
n
, and call
\mathbf{P}
the uniform distribution over
\Omega
.
For a fixed integer
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we let
T_{k}
denote the Markov operator associated to the following resampling step. Given
X\in\Omega
, let
X_{-k}
denote a uniformly random subset of
X
with cardinality
n-k
, and, given
X_{-k}
, let
X^{\prime}\in\Omega
denote a uniformly random supset of
X_{-k}
with cardinality
n
. Thus
X^{\prime}
is obtained from
X
by first removing
k
elements chosen uniformly at random from
X
, thus obtaining
X_{-k}
, and then by adding
k
elements chosen uniformly at random from
{\bf S}\setminus X_{-k}
. The corresponding Markov operator
T_{k}
is then written as

T_{k}f(X)=\sum_{X^{\prime}\in\Omega}T_{k}(X,X^{\prime})f(X^{\prime})\,,\qquad X\in\Omega,

where
T_{k}(X,X^{\prime})
denotes the probability of the transition from
X
to
X^{\prime}
as a result of the the above described step. The Markov chain associated to
T_{k}
is also called the
(n\leftrightarrow n-k)

down-up walk
on uniform
n
-sets. By symmetry,
T_{k}=T_{k}^{\star}
and the walk is
\mathbf{P}
-reversible.

The
(n\leftrightarrow n-k)
down-up walk on uniform
n
-sets is a special case of the
(n\leftrightarrow n-k)
down-up walk on the
bases of a matroid
, an extensively studied Markov chain which is known to satisfy an entropy contraction with rate
\frac{k}{n}
, for all
k
, that is, for any
f:\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

{\mathrm{Ent}}(T_{k}f)\leq\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)\,{\mathrm{Ent}}f\,,

see
[
22
,
2
]
. Somewhat surprisingly, as a simple consequence of our main result (Theorem
2
), we obtain an improvement for the
(n\leftrightarrow n-k)
down-up walk on uniform
n
-sets, and show that for any
k
one has entropy contraction with the strictly larger rate
\frac{k}{n}+\frac{k}{N-(n-k)}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)
. We formulate this in the following slightly more general terms.

Theorem 5
.

For any
\theta=(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})
, with
\theta_{k}\geq 0
and
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{k}=1
, for any
f:\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{k}{\mathrm{Ent}}(T_{k}f)\leq(1-\kappa){\mathrm{Ent}}f,

(40)

with
\kappa:=\kappa_{0}(\theta)+\kappa_{1}(\theta)
, where

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}(\theta):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{k}\,\frac{k}{n}\,,\quad\kappa_{1}(\theta):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{k}\,\frac{k}{N-(n-k)}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)\,.

(41)

In particular, for any
k=1,\dots,n
, the
(n\leftrightarrow n-k)
down-up walk for uniform
n
-sets has entropy contraction with rate
\frac{k}{n}+\frac{k}{N-(n-k)}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)
.

The proof is based on showing that Assumptions (i) and (ii) of Theorem
2
are satisfied with the required constants. In Section
3.4
we provide the simple coupling argument for these curvature bounds.

Langevin diffusion in a convex potential.

Finally, we consider the
n-
dimensional Langevin diffusion in a convex potential, that is the stochastic differential equation

\displaystyle\,{\rm d}X_{t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\nabla V(X_{t})\,{\rm d}t+\sqrt{2}\,{\rm d}B_{t},

(42)

where
B=(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a standard
n-
dimensional Brownian motion and
V\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
a smooth (say, twice continuously differentiable) function, which is assumed to be
\rho-
convex:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Hess}(V)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\rho\,\mathrm{Id},

(43)

for some constant
\rho>0
.
The classical theory ensures that there is a unique strong solution
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
starting from any fixed condition
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and that the formula
(P_{t}f)(x):={\mathbf{E}}_{x}\left[f(X_{t})\right]
defines a self-adjoint Markov semi-group
(P_{t})_{t\geq 0}
on
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P})
, where

\displaystyle\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\qquad\mathbf{P}({\rm d}x)

\displaystyle\propto

\displaystyle e^{-V(x)}\,{\rm d}x.

(44)

The following fundamental result on the relative entropy decay for Langevin diffusions is one of the most emblematic applications of the Bakry-Émery theory. It is equivalent to a log-Sobolev inequality for the measure
\mathbf{P}
, with optimal constant in the case where
V
is quadratic, see e.g.
[
3
]
.

Theorem 6
.

Under assumption (
43
), one has

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Ent}}(P_{t}f)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{-2\rho t}\,{\mathrm{Ent}}(f),

for all times
t\geq 0
and all functions
f\in L\log L
.

In Section
3.5
we show how to use our framework to obtain an elementary probabilistic proof of Theorem
6
.

Proof of Theorem

2.1
Step 1: Lipschitz contraction

Our first step consists in converting the curvature assumptions into a crucial Lipschitz contraction property for the non-linear operator
\Lambda\colon L^{\infty}\to L^{\infty}
defined by

\displaystyle\Lambda f

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\theta_{i}T_{i}\log T_{i}^{\star}\exp f.

Proposition 1

(Lipschitz contraction)
.

Under Assumptions
(i)-(ii)
, we have

\displaystyle\forall f\in L^{\infty},\qquad\mathrm{Lip}\left(\Lambda f\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\kappa)\,\mathrm{Lip}(f).

(45)

We start with an elementary remark that will be used several times throughout the proof.

Claim 1

(Null sets)
.

If
T
is a measure-preserving transition kernel on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbf{P})
, and if
N\subset\Omega
is a null set, then so is
\left\{x\in\Omega\colon T(x,N)>0\right\}
.

Proof.

Simply choose
f={\bf 1}_{N}
in (
9
) to obtain

\int_{\Omega}T(x,N)\mathbf{P}(\,{\rm d}x)=\mathbf{P}(N)=0.

∎

Proof of Proposition
1
.

Fix
f\in L^{\infty}
. By definition, there is a null set
N\subset\Omega
such that

\displaystyle\forall(x,y)\in(\Omega\setminus N)^{2},\qquad|f(x)-f(y)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}(f)\,{\rm dist}(x,y).

Claim
1
ensures that

N^{\prime}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}\left\{x\in\Omega\colon T_{i}^{\star}(x,N)>0\right\}

is also a null set. Upon enlarging it if necessary, we may assume that
N^{\prime}
also contains the null sets appearing in Assumption (i). Now, fix two points
x,y\in\Omega\setminus N^{\prime}
, and an index
1\leq i\leq n
. By assumption, there is a coupling
(X^{\star},Y^{\star})
of
T_{i}^{\star}(x,\cdot)
and
T_{i}^{\star}(y,\cdot)
such that almost-surely,

\,{\rm dist}(X^{\star},Y^{\star})\leq\ell_{i}\,{\rm dist}(x,y)
and
X^{\star},Y^{\star}\notin N
. In particular, this implies that almost-surely,

\displaystyle f(X^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle f(Y^{\star})+\ell_{i}\mathrm{Lip}(f)\,{\rm dist}(x,y),

Taking exponentials, then expectations, and then logarithms, we arrive at the key inequality

\displaystyle(\log T_{i}^{\star}\exp f)(x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\log T_{i}^{\star}\exp f)(y)+\ell_{i}\mathrm{Lip}(f)\,{\rm dist}(x,y),

(46)

valid for any
1\leq i\leq M
and any
x,y\in\Omega\setminus N^{\prime}
. Now, invoking Claim
1
again, we know that
N^{\prime\prime}:=\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}\left\{x\in\Omega\colon T_{i}(x,N^{\prime})>0\right\}
is a null set and, upon enlarging it if needed, we may assume that it contains the null set appearing in Assumption (ii). Consider a pair
(x,y)\in(\Omega\setminus N^{\prime\prime})^{2}
, and let
(X,Y)
be any coupling of
T_{i}(x,\cdot)
and
T_{i}(y,\cdot)
. Then,
X,Y
are both in
\Omega\setminus N^{\prime}
almost-surely, so that (
46
) holds almost-surely with
x,y
replaced by
(X,Y)
. Taking expectations, we obtain

\displaystyle(T_{i}\log T_{i}^{\star}\exp f)(x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(T_{i}\log T_{i}^{\star}\exp f)(y)+\ell_{i}\mathrm{Lip}(f){\mathbf{E}}\left[\,{\rm dist}(X,Y)\right].

Since this is true for any choice of the coupling
(X,Y)
, we may replace
{\mathbf{E}}\left[\,{\rm dist}(X,Y)\right]
with
W_{1}\left(T_{i}(x,\cdot),T_{i}(y,\cdot)\right)
. Finally, we simply multiply through by
\theta_{i}
and sum over
1\leq i\leq M
. In view of Assumption (ii) and our definition of
\Lambda
, we obtain

\displaystyle(\Lambda f)(x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(\Lambda f)(y)+(1-\kappa)\,\mathrm{Lip}(f)\,{\rm dist}(x,y).

Since this is true for any

∎

2.2
Step 2: variance contraction

With Proposition
1
on hand, the proof of Theorem
2
boils down to establishing that the Lipschitz contraction
\mathrm{Lip}\left(\Lambda f\right)\leq(1-\kappa)\mathrm{Lip}(f)
and the regularity assumption (iii) together imply the desired entropy contraction (
11
). We first establish a weaker statement, obtained by replacing entropies with variances.

Proposition 2

(Variance contraction)
.

Under Assumption (iii), the contraction (
45
) implies

\displaystyle\forall f\in L^{2},\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{M}\theta_{i}{\mathrm{Var}}\left(T_{i}^{\star}f\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\kappa){\mathrm{Var}}\left(f\right).

(47)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we assume that
f\in L^{\infty}
and that
{\mathbf{E}}[f]=0
and
{\mathbf{E}}[f^{2}]=1
. Using the uniform approximation
e^{\varepsilon f}=1+\varepsilon f+o(\varepsilon)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Lambda(\varepsilon f)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\varepsilon\to 0]{L^{2}}

\displaystyle Tf,

where we have introduced the operator
T:=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\theta_{i}T_{i}T_{i}^{\star}
. It follows that
T
inherits the Lipschitz contraction property (
45
) from
\Lambda
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}\left(Tf\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\kappa)\,\mathrm{Lip}(f).

(48)

Now,
T
is clearly a non-negative self-adjoint Markov operator on the Hilbert space
L^{2}
, so the spectral theorem provides us with a Borel probability measure
\mu
on
[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle\forall n\in\mathbb{N},\qquad\int_{0}^{1}\lambda^{n}\mu({\rm d}\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle f,T^{n}f\rangle.

(49)

Iterating the Lipschitz contraction (
48
), we find

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\lambda^{2n}\mu({\rm d}\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Var}}(T^{n}f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega^{2}}\left[(T^{n}f)(x)-(T^{n}f)(y)\right]^{2}\mathbf{P}(dx)\mathbf{P}(dy)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{Lip}(T^{n}f)^{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega^{2}}\,{\rm dist}^{2}(x,y)\mathbf{P}(dx)\mathbf{P}(dy)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\kappa)^{2n-2}\,\frac{\mathrm{Lip}(Tf)^{2}}{2}\,\int_{\Omega^{2}}\,{\rm dist}^{2}(x,y)\mathbf{P}(dx)\mathbf{P}(dy).

Note that the right-hand side is finite thanks to Assumption (iii) and (
22
). We may thus safely raise both sides to the power
1/(2n)
and send
n\to\infty
to conclude that the measure
\mu
is actually supported on
[0,1-\kappa]
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\langle f,Tf\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\lambda\mu({\rm d}\lambda)\ \leq\ 1-\kappa.

By definition of
T
, the left-hand side is exactly
\sum_{i=1}^{M}\theta_{i}{\mathrm{Var}}(T_{i}^{\star}f)
, and the claim is proved.
∎

2.3
Step 3: entropy contraction

We henceforth fix a parameter
m\in(0,\infty)
and consider the compact set
K\subset L^{2}
formed by those functions
f
satisfying
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq m
and
{\mathbf{E}}[e^{f}]=1
. We then introduce the constant

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sup_{f\in K\setminus\{0\}}\mathcal{H}(f),\qquad\textrm{ where }\qquad\mathcal{H}(f):=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\theta_{i}{\mathrm{Ent}}(T_{i}^{\star}e^{f})}{{\mathrm{Ent}}(e^{f})}.

(50)

We will prove that
\rho\leq 1-\kappa
, independently of
m
. This establishes the desired entropy contraction property (
11
) for all positive measurable functions that are bounded away from
0
and
\infty
. By a straightforward approximation argument, the conclusion then extends to all functions in
L\log L
, and our proof of Theorem
2
is complete.

Proposition 3

(Entropy contraction)
.

Under Assumption (iii), the Lipschitz contraction (
45
) implies that
\rho\leq 1-\kappa
, independently of the parameter
m
.

An essential ingredient is the following structural property of optimizers of
\mathcal{H}
.

Lemma 2

(Optimizers)
.

If
f\in K\setminus\{0\}
satisfies
\mathcal{H}(f)=\rho
, then there is
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that

•

\Lambda f\leq\rho f+\alpha
a.-s. on the set
\{f\neq m\}
.

•

\Lambda f\geq\rho f+\alpha
a.-s. on the set
\{f\neq-m\}
.

In particular, we must have
\rho\,\mathrm{Lip}(f)\leq\mathrm{Lip}(\Lambda f)
.

Proof.

Fix
f\in K\setminus\{0\}
such that
\mathcal{H}(f)=\rho
, and let us introduce the short-hands

\displaystyle A:=\{f\neq m\},\qquad B:=\{f\neq-m\}\qquad\textrm{ and }\qquad\Psi

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\Lambda f-\rho f.

With this notation at hands, the two items in the statements are rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathrm{ess\,\sup}_{A}\Psi

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{ess\,\inf}_{B}\Psi.

(51)

Assume for a contradiction that (
51
) fails. This means that we can find
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(A\cap\{\Psi>\beta\}\right)>0

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}\left(B\cap\{\Psi<\beta\}\right)>0.

By monotone convergence, this remains true with
A
and
B
replaced by
A^{\prime}:=\{f\leq m-\delta\}
and
B^{\prime}:=\{f\geq-m+\delta\}
, provided
\delta>0
is small enough. Now, consider the function

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{{\bf 1}_{A^{\prime}\cap\{\Psi>\beta\}}}{\mathbf{P}(A^{\prime}\cap\{\Psi>\beta\})}-\frac{{\bf 1}_{B^{\prime}\cap\{\Psi<\beta\}}}{\mathbf{P}(B^{\prime}\cap\{\Psi<\beta\})}.

(52)

Note that
h\in L^{\infty}
, with
{\mathbf{E}}[h]=0
and
h\leq 0
on
(A^{\prime})^{c}
and
h\geq 0
on
(B^{\prime})^{c}
.
Those properties guarantee that the function

f_{\varepsilon}:=\log\left(e^{f}+\varepsilon h\right)

is in
K
for all small enough
\varepsilon>0
, and a Taylor expansion gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(f_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(f)+\frac{\varepsilon\langle h,\Psi\rangle}{{\mathrm{Ent}}(e^{f})}+o(\varepsilon)\qquad\textrm{ as }\qquad\varepsilon\to 0.

Thus, the maximality of
\mathcal{H}
at
f
imposes
\langle h,\Psi\rangle\leq 0
. In view of (
52
), this reads

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\left[\Psi|A^{\prime}\cap\{\Psi>\beta\}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}\left[\Psi|B^{\prime}\cap\{\Psi<\beta\}\right].

This inequality is clearly self-contradictory, and (
51
) is proved. This means that there is a null set
N\subset\Omega
such that for all
(x,y)\in(A\setminus N)\times(B\setminus N)
, we have
\Psi(x)\leq\Psi(y)
, hence

\displaystyle\rho f(y)-\rho f(x)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|(\Lambda f)(y)-(\Lambda f)(x)|.

On the other hand, this inequality trivially holds when
(x,y)\in\Omega^{2}\setminus(A\times B)
, because the left-hand side is then non-positive. The conclusion
\rho\,\mathrm{Lip}(f)\leq\mathrm{Lip}(\Lambda f)
follows.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3
.

By definition of
\rho
, there is a sequence
(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}
in
K\setminus\{0\}
such that

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathcal{H}(f_{k}).

(53)

Upon extracting a subsequence if needed, we may further assume that
(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}
has an almost-sure limit
f
. Note that
f
automatically inherits the properties
\|f\|_{\infty}\leq m
and
{\mathbf{E}}[e^{f}]=1
. If
f
is not a.s. zero, then
f\in K\setminus\{0\}
and

\mathcal{H}(f)=\rho
, so Lemma
2
and (
45
) together yield

\displaystyle\rho\,\mathrm{Lip}(f)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{Lip}\left(\Lambda f\right)\ \leq\ (1-\kappa)\,\mathrm{Lip}(f).

Moreover, Assumption (iii) ensures that the middle term is finite, so that
\mathrm{Lip}(f)<\infty
. Therefore, we may safely simplify through by
\mathrm{Lip}(f)
to obtain
\rho\leq 1-\kappa
, as desired. Consider now the degenerate case where
f=0
almost surely. We can then write
e^{f_{k}}=1+h_{k}
with
\|h_{k}\|_{\infty}\leq e^{m}
,
{\mathbf{E}}[h_{k}]=0
and
h_{k}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. But then, a Taylor expansion yields

\displaystyle{\mathrm{Ent}}\left(e^{f_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}{\mathrm{Var}}(h_{k})

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}\theta_{i}{\mathrm{Ent}}\left(T_{i}^{\star}e^{f_{k}}\right)

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\theta_{i}{\mathrm{Var}}(T^{\star}_{i}h_{k}),

where the notation
a_{k}\sim b_{k}
means that
a_{k}/b_{k}\to 1
as
k\to\infty
. In particular, (
53
) becomes

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{M}\theta_{i}{\mathrm{Var}}(T^{\star}_{i}h_{k})}{{\mathrm{Var}}(h_{k})}.

By Proposition
2
, the right-hand is at most
1-\kappa
, and the proof is complete.
∎

In this section we address the application of Theorem

to the main examples discussed in Section

.

3.1
Gaussian Free Fields: Proof of Theorem
3

Let us first show that the second part of the theorem follows from the first, namely that once we have
\kappa\geq\delta\theta_{\star}
for all choices of
\theta
, it follows that for Glauber dynamics one has
\kappa=\lambda=\delta/n
. Since
\theta_{\star}=1/n
in this case, and, in general,
\kappa\leq\lambda
, the claim (
30
) follows from (
29
) and the following observation.

Lemma 3
.

The Glauber dynamics satisfies
\lambda\leq\delta/n
.

Proof.

By definition,

\displaystyle\forall f\in L^{2},\qquad\lambda\,{\mathrm{Var}}(f)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(f,f)\ =\frac{1}{n}\,{\mathbf{E}}\left[f\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f-{\mathbf{E}}_{i}f)\right].

(54)

Let us apply this to the linear observable
f(x)=x^{\top}z
, where
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an eigenvector of
\Delta
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\delta
. If
u_{j}(x)=x_{j}
, then
{\mathbf{E}}_{i}[u_{j}](x)=x_{j}
for all
j\neq i
, and
{\mathbf{E}}_{i}[u_{j}](x)=(Px)_{i}
if
j=i
. Therefore, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f-{\mathbf{E}}_{i}f)(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}(\Delta x)_{i}\ =\ z^{\top}\Delta x\ =\ x^{\top}\Delta z\ =\ \delta f(x).

Thus, (
54
) forces
\lambda\leq\delta/n
, as desired.
∎

We turn to the proof of (
29
). Recall the notation introduced in (
24
), and
let
Z\colon\Omega\to\Omega
denote the identity map on
\Omega
, which forms a random vector with law
\mathbf{P}
.
For each set
A\subset[n]
, we consider the Markov operator
T_{A}\colon L^{2}\to L^{2}
defined by

\displaystyle T_{A}f

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[f|Z_{j},j\in A^{c}],

(55)

where
A^{c}=[n]\setminus A
. Note that
T_{A}
is a self-adjoint, measure-preserving, Markov operator.
In order to apply Theorem
2
, we need to investigate the curvature of the associated transition kernel.

A contractive coupling

Let us start with an explicit expression for the transition kernel associated to our Markov operator
T_{A}
.

Lemma 4

(Explicit representation)
.

For all
f\in L^{2}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle(T_{A}f)(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f\left(M_{A}z+({\rm Id}-M_{A})x\right)\mathbf{P}({\rm d}z),

(56)

where
M_{A}
is the
n\times n
matrix defined by the blocks

\begin{array}[]{rclrcl}M_{A\times A}&=&\mathrm{Id}&M_{A\times A^{c}}&=&-\Gamma_{A\times A^{c}}\,(\Gamma_{A^{c}\times A^{c}})^{-1}\\
M_{A^{c}\times A}&=&0&M_{A^{c}\times A^{c}}&=&0.\end{array}

(57)

Proof.

From the block definition of
M_{A}
above, we can readily compute
M_{A}\Gamma
:

\begin{array}[]{rclrcl}(M_{A}\Gamma)_{A\times A}&=&\Gamma_{A\times A}-\Gamma_{A\times A^{c}}\,(\Gamma_{A^{c}\times A^{c}})^{-1}\,\Gamma_{A^{c}\times A},&(M_{A}\Gamma)_{A\times A^{c}}&=&0,\\
(M_{A}\Gamma)_{A^{c}\times A}&=&0,&(M_{A}\Gamma)_{A^{c}\times A^{c}}&=&0.\end{array}

(58)

The second line implies that the random vector
M_{A}Z
is uncorrelated with
(Z_{j}\colon j\in A^{c})
, hence independent of
\mathcal{G}:=\sigma(Z_{j}\colon j\in A^{c})
.
On the other hand, the fact that
M_{A\times A}=\mathrm{Id}
ensures that the random vector
({\rm Id}-M_{A})Z
is
\mathcal{G}-
measurable. Thus, the decomposition

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({\rm Id}-M_{A})Z+M_{A}Z,

expresses
Z
as the sum of a
\mathcal{G}-
measurable vector and a
\mathcal{G}-
independent one. The claim now follows from a standard property of conditional expectation (see, e.g.
[
41
, Section 9.10]
).
∎

To ensure that
T_{A}
has non-negative sectional curvature, we equip
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the following distorted metric instead of the usual Euclidean norm. Let

\psi
denote an eigenvector of
\Delta
with eigenvalue
\delta
. Since
P
is irreducible with nonnegative entries, by Perron-Frobenius theorem we may choose
\psi
with positive entries:
\psi_{i}>0
for all
i\in[n]
. We then define the metric

\displaystyle\,{\rm dist}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}|(\Delta x)_{i}-(\Delta y)_{i}|.

(59)

Let
W_{1}
and
W_{\infty}
denote the Wasserstein distances associated with this metric.

Lemma 5
.

For any
A\subset[n]
, we have

\displaystyle\forall x,y\in\Omega,\qquad W_{\infty}\left(T_{A}(x,\cdot),T_{A}(y,\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,{\rm dist}(x,y)-\delta\sum_{i\in A}\psi_{i}|(\Delta x)_{i}-(\Delta y)_{i}|

In particular,
for any probability vector
(\theta_{A},\,A\subset[n])
, one has

\displaystyle\forall x,y\in\Omega,\qquad\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}W_{\infty}\left(T_{A}(x,\cdot),T_{A}(y,\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-\delta\theta_{\star})\,{\rm dist}(x,y)\,,

where
\theta_{\star}=\min_{i}\sum_{A\ni i}\theta_{A}
.

Proof.

The second inequality is an immediate consequence of the first. To prove the first, fix a subset
A\subset[n]
and two points
x,y\in\Omega
. Recalling that
Z\sim\mathbf{P}
, Lemma
4
shows that
the random vectors

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rlc}X&:=&({\rm Id}-M_{A})x+M_{A}Z\\
Y&:=&({\rm Id}-M_{A})y+M_{A}Z.\end{array}\right.

form a coupling of
T_{A}(x,\cdot)
and
T_{A}(y,\cdot)
. Thus, taking
z=x-y
, the proof boils down to showing that for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}|[\Delta({\rm Id}-M_{A})z]_{i}|\;\leq\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}|(\Delta z)_{i}|-\delta\sum_{i\in A}\psi_{i}|(\Delta z)_{i}|.

(60)

To prove this, recall that the matrix
M_{A}\Gamma
computed in the proof of the previous lemma was symmetric, i.e.
M_{A}\Gamma=\Gamma M^{\top}_{A}
or equivalently,
\Gamma^{-1}M_{A}=M^{\top}_{A}\Gamma^{-1}
. Combining this with the fact that
M^{2}_{A}=M_{A}
, we obtain
\Gamma^{-1}M_{A}=M^{\top}_{A}\Gamma^{-1}M_{A}
. In particular,
\Delta M_{A}
is symmetric and
\Delta({\rm Id}-M_{A})=({\rm Id}-M^{\top}_{A})\Delta
. Next, we observe that
{\rm Id}-M^{\top}_{A}
has nonnegative entries. To see this, write

{\rm Id}-M^{\top}_{A}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
(\Gamma_{A^{c}\times A^{c}})^{-1}\Gamma_{A^{c}\times A}&{\rm Id}\end{pmatrix},

and note that
\Gamma_{A^{c}\times A}\Delta_{A\times A}+\Gamma_{A^{c}\times A^{c}}\Delta_{A^{c}\times A}=(\Gamma\Delta)_{A^{c}\times A}=0
, or equivalently

(\Gamma_{A^{c}\times A^{c}})^{-1}\Gamma_{A^{c}\times A}\;=\;-\Delta_{A^{c}\times A}(\Delta_{A\times A})^{-1}.

Now,
-\Delta_{A^{c}\times A}=P_{A^{c}\times A}
has nonnegative entries, as well as

\displaystyle(\Delta_{A\times A})^{-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(P_{A\times A})^{k}\,.

(61)

This proves that
{\rm Id}-M^{\top}_{A}
has nonnegative entries. As a consequence,

\displaystyle[\Delta({\rm Id}-M_{A})z]_{i}

\displaystyle\;\leq\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}|({\rm Id}-M^{\top}_{A})_{i,j}(\Delta z)_{j}|

\displaystyle\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{n}({\rm Id}-M^{\top}_{A})_{i,j}|(\Delta z)_{j}|\;=\;|(\Delta z)_{i}|-\sum_{j=1}^{n}|(\Delta z)_{j}|(M_{A})_{j,i}\,.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}|[\Delta({\rm Id}-M_{A})z]_{i}|\;\leq\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}|(\Delta z)_{i}|-\sum_{j=1}^{n}(M_{A}\psi)_{j}|(\Delta z)_{j}|.

(62)

To conclude, observe that
M_{A}\psi=M_{A}\Gamma\Delta\psi=\delta M_{A}\Gamma\psi
, and that

M_{A}\Gamma=\begin{pmatrix}(\Delta_{A\times A})^{-1}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},

where the latter follows from a standard Schur complement computation and (
58
).
Thus,
M_{A}\psi=\delta(\Delta_{A\times A})^{-1}\psi_{A}
, and using (
61
), one finds

\displaystyle(M_{A}\psi)_{j}\;=\;\delta[(\Delta_{A\times A})^{-1}\psi_{A}]_{j}\;\geq\;\delta\,\psi_{j}\,,\qquad j\in A\,,

and
(M_{A}\psi)_{j}=0
otherwise. Inserting this in (
62
) we have proved (
60
).
∎

Since
W_{1}\left(T_{A}(x,\cdot),T_{A}(y,\cdot)\right)\leq W_{\infty}\left(T_{A}(x,\cdot),T_{A}(y,\cdot)\right)
, Lemma
5
implies that both Assumptions (i)-(ii) in Theorem
2
are verified.
It remains to check the regularity Assumption (iii). Unfortunately, Assumption (iii) fails, except in the degenerate case where
A=[n]
. To circumvent this, we use the regularization procedure outlined in Remark
1
, using the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semi-group. This will conclude the proof of Theorem
3
.

Ornstein-Uhlenbeck regularization

Given a parameter
\tau\in(0,1)
, we consider the Markov operator
P_{\tau}
defined by

\displaystyle(P_{\tau}f)(x)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f\left(\sqrt{1-\tau}x+\sqrt{\tau}z\right)\mathbf{P}({\rm d}z).

Note that
P_{\tau}
is measure-preserving, because the image of
\mathbf{P}\otimes\mathbf{P}
under the map
(x,z)\mapsto\sqrt{1-\tau}x+\sqrt{\tau}z
is
\mathbf{P}
. An easy change of variable yields the alternative expression

\displaystyle(P_{\tau}f)(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}p_{\tau}\left(x,y\right)f\left(y\right)\mathbf{P}({\rm d}y),

(63)

where the transition kernel
p_{\tau}\colon\Omega\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is explicitly given by

\displaystyle p_{\tau}(x,y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\tau^{-\frac{n}{2}}\,\exp\left\{\frac{\sqrt{1-\tau}}{\tau}x^{\top}\Gamma^{-1}y-\frac{1-\tau}{2\tau}x^{\top}\Gamma^{-1}x-\frac{1-\tau}{2\tau}y^{\top}\Gamma^{-1}y\right\}.

The fact that this formula is symmetric in
x
and
y
readily guarantees that
P_{\tau}
is self-adjoint.
We now verify that
(P_{\tau})_{0<\tau<1}
satisfies the three properties in Remark
1
.
For the continuity, we need to check that
for any
f\in L^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle P_{\tau}f

\displaystyle\xrightarrow[\tau\to 0]{L^{2}}

\displaystyle f.

The claim is clear when
f
is continuous and bounded. In the general case, consider a sequence
(f_{k})_{k\geq 1}
of bounded measurable functions that converges to
f
, and write

\displaystyle\|P_{\tau}f-f\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|P_{\tau}f_{k}-f_{k}\|_{2}+\|P_{\tau}f-P_{\tau}f_{k}\|_{2}+\|f_{k}-f\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|P_{\tau}f_{k}-f_{k}\|_{2}+2\|f_{k}-f\|_{2}.

Taking first a
\limsup
as
\tau\to 0
and then a limit as
k\to\infty
concludes the proof.

The second property in Remark
1
is the regularity
\mathrm{Lip}(P_{\tau}f)<\infty
for all
f\in L^{\infty}
,
\tau>0
. Since our metric is comparable to Euclidean norm, this follows from the well known regularizing property of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck semi-group, see e.g.
[
3
, Th. 4.7.2]
.

It remains to check the third property in Remark
1
, namely non-negative sectional curvature.

Lemma 6

(Non-negative sectional curvature)
.

For every
x,y\in\Omega
, we have

\displaystyle W_{\infty}\left(P_{\tau}(x,\cdot),P_{\tau}(y,\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\tau}\,{\rm dist}(x,y).

Proof.

With
Z\sim{\rm P}
, we deduce from the definition of
P_{\tau}
that the random vectors

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rlc}X&:=&\sqrt{1-\tau}x+\sqrt{\tau}Z\\
Y&:=&\sqrt{1-\tau}y+\sqrt{\tau}Z.\end{array}\right.

form a coupling of
P_{\tau}(x,\cdot)
and
P_{\tau}(y,\cdot)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\,{\rm dist}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\tau}\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}\,|[\Delta(x-y)]_{i}|=\sqrt{1-\tau}\,\,{\rm dist}(x,y)\,,

which implies the desired property.
∎

Remark 5
.

If instead of the gaussian free field with covariance

3.2
Permutations: proof of Theorem
4

Here
\mathbf{P}
is the uniform measure over
\Omega=S_{n}
, the set of permutations of
[n]
, see (
32
). A permutation
\sigma\in\Omega
is identified with the configuration
(\sigma_{i})_{i\in[n]}
. To prove Theorem
4
, we are going to apply Theorem
2
with Markov operators given by the conditional expectations
T_{A}\colon L^{2}\to L^{2}
,
A\subset[n]
, defined by

\displaystyle T_{A}f

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}_{A}[f]={\mathbf{E}}[f|\sigma_{j},j\in A^{c}],

(67)

where, as usual,
A^{c}=[n]\setminus A
.
We equip
\Omega=S_{n}
with the
transposition distance

\,{\rm dist}(\sigma,\eta)
,
\sigma,\eta\in\Omega
, which is the minimal number of transpositions needed to turn
\sigma
into
\eta
.
Fix a pair of permutations
(\sigma,\eta)
and let
\sigma^{\prime}
and
\eta^{\prime}
denote the random permutations with distribution
T_{A}(\sigma,\cdot)
and
T_{A}(\eta,\cdot)
. A coupling of
(\sigma^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})
can be obtained by simply replacing the pair
(\sigma,\eta)
by the pair
(\sigma^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})=(\sigma\pi,\eta\pi)
where
\pi
is a uniformly random permutation of the labels
\{j\in[n]:j\in A\}
. This produces a valid coupling with the property that
\,{\rm dist}(\sigma^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})=\,{\rm dist}(\sigma,\eta)
. Thus, hypothesis (i) of Theorem
2
is satisfied with
\ell\equiv 1
.

To check the positive curvature, we use a finer coupling. We first observe that
the metric
\,{\rm dist}(\sigma,\eta)
is generated by pairs of configurations that differ by exactly one swap, and thus using a telescopic decomposition, using the so-called
Gluing Lemma
(see, e.g.,
[
40
, Lemma 7.6]
), we may restrict to a pair
(\sigma,\eta)
such that
\sigma_{\ell}=\eta_{\ell}
, for all
\ell\neq i,j
, and
(\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j})=(\eta_{j},\eta_{i})
, for a fixed pair of distinct indexes
i,j\in[n]
. This reduction step is also known as the
path coupling
lemma
[
13
]
. Given such a pair
(\sigma,\eta)
, the coupling of
(\sigma^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})
is described as follows. Let
\pi
be a uniformly random permutation of the labels
\{j\in[n]:j\in A\}
. If either
i
or
j
(or both) are in
A^{c}
then we update as before by setting
(\sigma^{\prime},\eta^{\prime})=(\sigma\pi,\eta\pi)
. If instead

i,j
are both in
A
, then we ensure coalescence by replacing
(\sigma,\eta)
with
(\sigma\pi,\sigma\pi)
. This shows that

\displaystyle\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}\,W_{1}\left(T_{A}(\sigma,\cdot),T_{A}(\eta,\cdot)\right)\leq\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}\,{\bf 1}_{\{A\not\supset\{i,j\}\}}\leq 1-\theta_{\star\star},

(68)

where
\theta_{\star\star}:=\min_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\sum_{A\supset\{i,j\}}\theta_{A}
. Thus, we have checked the validity of hypothesis (ii) of Theorem
2
with
\kappa=\theta_{\star\star}
. This ends the proof of Theorem
4
.

3.3
Contractive coupling for
\ell^{n}_{p}
–spheres

Here we present a
contractive coupling towards the proof of Conjecture
1
.
As in (
37
),
for any
p>0
, we take
\Omega={\mathbb{S}}^{n-1}_{p}
, with the associated Borel
\sigma
-algebra, and write
\mathbf{P}
for the cone measure, i.e. the probability measure obtained as in (
38
). As usual, we write
Z:\Omega\mapsto\Omega
for the identity map and take

T_{A}f:=E[f|Z_{A^{c}}]
for the conditional expectation of
f\in L^{1}
given
Z_{A^{c}}
. We introduce the distance on
\Omega
given by

\displaystyle\,{\rm dist}(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}||x_{i}|^{p}-|y_{i}|^{p}|+\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\bf 1}_{x_{i}y_{i}<0}\,.

(69)

Under this metric,
T_{A}
has nonnegative sectional curvature. More precisely,

Lemma 7
.

For all
p>0
for all
x,y\in\Omega
, and all
A\subset[n]
,

W_{\infty}(T_{A}(x,\cdot),T_{A}(y,\cdot))=\sum_{i\in A^{c}}||x_{i}|^{p}-|y_{i}|^{p}|+\sum_{i\in A^{c}}{\bf 1}_{\{x_{i}y_{i}<0\}}+\left|\sum_{i\in A}(|x_{i}|^{p}-|y_{i}|^{p})\right|\,.

In particular,
W_{\infty}(T_{A}(x,\cdot),T_{A}(y,\cdot))\leq\,{\rm dist}(x,y)
. Moreover, the same identity applies to
W_{1}
.

Proof.

Fix
x,y\in\Omega
and consider any coupling
(X,Y)
of
T_{A}(x,\cdot)
and
T_{A}(y,\cdot)
. Since only the coordinates in
A
are modified, we have almost-surely

\displaystyle\,{\rm dist}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in A^{c}}||x_{i}|^{p}-|y_{i}|^{p}|+\sum_{i\in A^{c}}{\bf 1}_{\{x_{i}y_{i}<0\}}+\sum_{i\in A}||X_{i}|^{p}-|Y_{i}|^{p}|+\sum_{i\in A^{c}}{\bf 1}_{\{x_{i}y_{i}<0\}}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in A^{c}}||x_{i}|^{p}-|y_{i}|^{p}|+\sum_{i\in A^{c}}{\bf 1}_{\{x_{i}y_{i}<0\}}+\left|\sum_{i\in A}(|X_{i}|^{p}-|Y_{i}|^{p})\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in A^{c}}||x_{i}|^{p}-|y_{i}|^{p}|+\sum_{i\in A^{c}}{\bf 1}_{\{x_{i}y_{i}<0\}}+\left|\sum_{i\in A}(|x_{i}|^{p}-|y_{i}|^{p})\right|\,,

where the last line uses the fact that the norm is conserved
\|X_{A}\|_{p}=\|x_{A}\|_{p}
and
\|Y_{A}\|_{p}=\|y_{A}\|_{p}
.
Now, the middle inequality is an equality as soon as
X_{A}
is a positive multiple of
Y_{A}
. The latter fact is almost-surely the case under the following coupling:

(X_{i},Y_{i})=\begin{cases}\left(\tfrac{\|x_{A}\|_{p}}{\|Z_{A}\|_{p}}\,Z_{i}\,,\,\tfrac{\|y_{A}\|_{p}}{\|Z_{A}\|_{p}}\,Z_{i}\right)&\;\text{ if }\,i\in A\\
(x_{i},y_{i})&\;\text{ if }\,i\in A^{c}\end{cases}

where we recall that
Z
has law
\mathbf{P}
.
The fact that the above is a valid coupling follows from the resampling property of the Dirichlet-type random variable with distribution given by (
38
). Thus we have found an optimal coupling, and the proof is complete.
∎

We now fix a probability vector
\theta=(\theta_{A},A\subset[n])
, and we look for a constant
\kappa
as
large as possible, such that for all
x,y\in\Omega
,

\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}W_{1}(T_{A}(x,\cdot),T_{A}(y,\cdot))\;\leq\;(1-\kappa)\,{\rm dist}(x,y)\,.

(70)

The answer turns out to be remarkably explicit.

Lemma 8
.

The optimal constant in (
70
) is exactly
\kappa=\theta_{\star\star}=\min_{i<j}\sum_{A\supset\{i,j\}}\theta_{A}
.

Proof.

By the triangle inequality, and using the path coupling lemma
[
13
]
, the validity of the inequality (
70
) can be checked separately on two complementary cases: the “same sign” case where
x_{i}y_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
, and the “sign flip” case where
x,y
differ at a single coordinate (its sign being the only difference). More precisely, the optimal constant that we are looking for is just the minimum of the two optimal constants obtained by restricting our attention to same-sign pairs, or to sign-flip pairs, respectively. The sign-flip case is actually easy: if
x,y
differ at a single coordinate
i
, then
\,{\rm dist}(x,y)=1
and
W_{1}(T_{A}(x,\cdot),T_{A}(y,\cdot)=1_{i\in A^{c}}
, so that

\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}W_{1}(T_{A}(x,\cdot),T_{A}(y,\cdot))\;\leq\;(1-\theta_{\star})\,{\rm dist}(x,y)\,,

with equality when
i
realizes the minimum in the definition of
\theta_{\star}=\min_{i}\sum_{A\ni i}\theta_{A}
. Let us now turn to the “same sign” case: given a pair
(x,y)\in\Omega^{2}
, with
x\neq y
and
x_{i}y_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
, we introduce two probability vectors
\mu,\nu
on
[n]
, with disjoint supports, defined by

\mu_{i}:=\frac{2(|x_{i}|^{p}-|y_{i}|^{p})_{+}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}||x_{i}|^{p}-|y_{i}|^{p}|}\,,\qquad\nu_{i}:=\frac{2(|y_{i}|^{p}-|x_{i}|^{p})_{+}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}||x_{i}|^{p}-|y_{i}|^{p}|}\,,

where
(a)_{+}=\max\{a,0\}
denotes the positive part of
a\in\mathbb{R}
.
Using Lemma
7
, we then find

\displaystyle\frac{W_{1}(T_{A}(x,\cdot),T_{A}(y,\cdot))}{\,{\rm dist}(x,y)}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\sum_{i\in A}||x_{i}|^{p}-|y_{i}|^{p}|}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}||x_{i}|^{p}-|y_{i}|^{p}|}+\frac{\left|\sum_{i\in A}(|x_{i}|^{p}-|y_{i}|^{p})\right|}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}||x_{i}|^{p}-|y_{i}|^{p}|}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\mu(A)+\nu(A)-|\mu(A)-\nu(A)|}{2}

\displaystyle=1-\min\{\mu(A),\nu(A)\}\,.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}W_{1}(T_{A}(x,\cdot),T_{A}(y,\cdot))}{\,{\rm dist}(x,y)}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}\mu(A)\nu(A)

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{A\subset[n]}\theta_{A}\sum_{i\in A,j\in A}\mu_{i}\nu_{i}\leq 1-\theta_{\star\star}.

Moreover, the two inequalities appearing in this computation are both equalities if
x
and
y
are the
i
-th and
j
-th vectors of the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
(i,j)
being any pair that realizes the minimum in the definition of
\theta_{\star\star}
. Thus, the optimal constant in the “same sign” optimization problem is precisely
\theta_{\star\star}
. To conclude, it remains to note the simple fact that
\theta_{\star\star}\leq\theta_{\star}
.
∎

As we mentioned in Section
3
, Lemma
8
would be sufficient to prove Conjecture
1
if we could solve the technical problem of replacing Assumption (iii) in Theorem
2
by a suitable regularizing procedure as outlined in Remark
1
.

3.4
Down-Up walk on uniform
n
-sets: proof of Theorem
5

To prove the theorem we apply our general result from Theorem
2
. In fact, it will be sufficient to apply it for each
k
separately, and then obtain (
40
) by summing over
k
. Given
X,Y\in\Omega
we write
|X\cap Y|
for the number of elements in
X\cap Y
, and define

\,{\rm dist}(X,Y)=n-|X\cap Y|\,,

for the number of discrepancies in the two sets. Notice that this defines a distance in
\Omega
. Indeed, we can identify
X\in\Omega
with the function
\eta_{X}:{\bf S}\mapsto\{0,1\}
such that
\eta_{X}(x)={\bf 1}_{x\in X}
and in this representation one has

\,{\rm dist}(X,Y)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in{\bf S}}{\bf 1}_{\eta_{X}(x)\neq\eta_{Y}(x)}
. Therefore, Theorem
5
is an immediate consequence of Theorem
2
and the following lemma.

Lemma 9
.

For any
k=1,\dots,n
, for any
X,Y\in\Omega
,
W_{\infty}(T_{k}(X,\cdot),T_{k}(Y,\cdot))\leq\,{\rm dist}(X,Y)
, and

\displaystyle W_{1}(T_{k}(X,\cdot),T_{k}(Y,\cdot))\leq\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)\left(1-\frac{k}{N-(n-k)}\right)\,{\rm dist}(X,Y)\,.

(71)

Proof.

We want to estimate
{\mathbf{E}}[\,{\rm dist}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})]
, where
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
is
a suitable coupling of the laws
T_{k}(X,\cdot)
and
T_{k}(Y,\cdot)
respectively. By the path coupling lemma
[
13
]
we may restrict to the case where
X,Y\in\Omega
is an arbitrary pair such that
\,{\rm dist}(X,Y)=1
. Thus, there exist
x,y\in{\bf S}
such that
x\neq y
,
x\in X\cap Y^{c}
,
y\in Y\cap X^{c}
and
X\cap Y=X\setminus\{x\}=Y\setminus\{y\}
. Let
X_{-k}
denote a uniformly random subset of
X
with cardinality
n-k
. There are
\binom{n}{k}
possible choices,
\binom{n-1}{k-1}
of which do not contain
x
.
If
x\notin X_{-k}
then we may set
Y_{-k}=X_{-k}
and thus define
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=(X^{\prime},X^{\prime})
, where
X^{\prime}
is a uniformly random supset of
X_{-k}
with cardinality
n
. If instead
x\in X_{-k}
, we may take
Y_{-k}=(X_{-k}\setminus\{x\})\cup\{y\}
and we construct
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
as follows. Let
X^{\prime}=X_{-k}\cup X^{k}
, where
X^{k}
is a uniformly random subset of
{\bf S}\setminus X_{-k}
with cardinality
k
. There are
\binom{N-(n-k)}{k}
possible choices,
\binom{N-(n-k)-1}{k-1}
of which contain
y
. If
y\notin X^{k}
, then we set
Y^{\prime}=(X^{\prime}\setminus\{x\})\cup\{y\}
. If instead
y\in X^{k}
, then we set
Y^{\prime}=X^{\prime}
.

It is not hard to check that the above defines a valid coupling of
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
, such that
\,{\rm dist}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=\,{\rm dist}(X,Y)=1
with probability

\mathbf{P}(y\notin X^{k})=\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)\left(1-\frac{k}{N-(n-k)}\right)\,,

while with the remaining probability one has
\,{\rm dist}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=0
. In particular, it follows that

W_{\infty}(T_{k}(X,\cdot),T_{k}(Y,\cdot))\leq\,{\rm dist}(X,Y)\,,

and

\displaystyle{\mathbf{E}}[\,{\rm dist}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})]\leq\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)\left(1-\frac{k}{N-(n-k)}\right).

(72)

∎

3.5
Langevin diffusion: proof of Theorem
6

The proof is an immediate application of Theorem
2
. Given
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we may couple two Langevin diffusions
X
and
Y
starting from
X_{0}=x
and
Y_{0}=y
by using the same Brownian motion
B=(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, so that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\|X_{t}-Y_{t}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\langle X_{t}-Y_{t},\nabla V(X_{t})-\nabla V(Y_{t})\rangle\,{\rm d}t

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-2\rho\|X_{t}-Y_{t}\|^{2}\,{\rm d}t,

where the second line uses the
\rho-
convexity (
43
). Thus, the Euclidean distance
t\mapsto\|X_{t}-Y_{t}\|
decays at rate at least
\rho
, almost-surely. This shows that for all
t\geq 0
and all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

\displaystyle W_{\infty}\left(P_{t}(x,\cdot),P_{t}(y,\cdot)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{-\rho t}\|x-y\|.

(73)

We may now apply the case
M=1
of Theorem
2
to the operator
T=P_{t}
, for a fixed
t>0
. Since
P_{t}^{\star}=P_{t}
and
W_{1}\leq W_{\infty}
, Assumptions (i)-(ii) hold with
\ell=e^{-\rho t}
and
\kappa=1-e^{-2\rho t}
. Moreover, the regularity property (iii) classically holds in this setting (see, e.g.,
[
3
, Th. 4.7.2]
). The desired conclusion readily follows.
