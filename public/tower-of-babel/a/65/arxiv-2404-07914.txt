Convergence, divergence, and inherent oscillations in MFS solutions of two-dimensional Laplace-Neumann problems
Georgios D. Kolezas, George Fikioris, John A. Roumeliotis

Abstract
The method of fundamental solutions (MFS), also known as the method of auxiliary sources (MAS), is a well-known computational method for the solution of boundary-value problems. The final solution (“MAS solution”) is obtained once we have found the amplitudes of N N auxiliary “MAS sources.” Past studies have demonstrated that it is possible for the MAS solution to converge to the true solution even when the N N auxiliary sources diverge and oscillate. The present paper extends the past studies by demonstrating this possibility within the context of Laplace’s equation with Neumann boundary conditions. One can thus obtain the correct solution from sources that, when N N is large, must be considered unphysical. We carefully explain the underlying reasons for the unphysical results, distinguish from other difficulties that might concurrently arise, and point to significant differences with time-dependent problems that were studied in the past.

The method of fundamental solutions (MFS) is a popular numerical technique for the solution of certain types of boundary-value problems

The key concept of the method is the representation of the solution as a superposition of fundamental solutions of the governing differential equation whose singularities lie outside the problem’s domain. In the context of electromagnetics, the MFS is also known as the method of auxiliary sources (MAS) and has been extensively used for the modeling of two-dimensional (2D) scattering problems

In its simplest form, the 2D scatterer has a perfect electric or magnetic conductivity and is illuminated externally by a point source (exterior problem), or is a cavity surrounded by a perfectly conducting electric or magnetic space, and illuminated by a source inside the cavity (interior problem). The central idea of MAS is to represent the scattered electric/magnetic field or potential by

We use the language of magnetostatics for definiteness, but our Neumann problems are generic. We specifically deal with 2D exterior (or interior) Neumann problems satisfied by the vector potential
\mathbf{A}={\mathbf{\hat{z}}}A_{z}

Now let us, for definiteness, refer to exterior problems, so that the auxiliary surface is placed in the interior. Exterior potentials generated by smooth, continuous, 2D surface current densities
K_{z}
(units: A/m) are analytic functions of the space variables. Therefore no such
K_{z}

divergent
currents (even when normalized) are capable of generating a

potential. In most realistic problems, the singularities of the analytic continuation are not known beforehand. A circular boundary has the advantage of bringing out the aforesaid convergence unequivocally, because there are image singularities, and they are known explicitly. Thus much herein pertains to the question: What happens when the auxiliary surface
does not
enclose all singularities?, and to the corresponding question for interior problems.

In all our Neumann problems, the so-called self-consistency condition (i.e., the condition that must be satisfied by the integral of the Neumann boundary data for the problem to have a solution see, e.g.,
[

Throughout this paper, the terms MAS and MFS are used interchangeably.

\displaystyle\alpha^{(m)}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p=0}^{N-1}\alpha_{p}e^{-i2\pi mp/N},\qquad\alpha_{p}=\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}\alpha^{(m)}e^{i2\pi mp/N}.

3
Exterior circular problem: Problem formulation

We consider the 2D magnetostatic problem shown in
fig.

1
, where a circular cylinder of infinite permeability (
\mu=\infty
) is illuminated by a temporally constant line-current
I
.
The cylinder has radius
\rho_{\rm cyl}
, infinite extent along the
z
-direction, and axis the
z
-axis. The infinitely long current
I
is parallel to the
z
-axis and positioned at
(\rho,\phi)=(\rho_{\rm fil},0)
, with
\rho_{\rm fil}>\rho_{\rm cyl}
. The medium surrounding the cylinder is free space with permeability
\mu_{0}
.

Figure 1
:
Geometry of the exterior circular problem. Indicative positions of MAS currents and collocation points are depicted.

Because the examined geometry is invariant along the
z
-direction, the total magnetostatic vector potential
\mathbf{A}^{\rm tot}
only has a
z
-component and depends on the polar coordinates
(\rho,\phi)
, i.e.,
\mathbf{A}^{\rm tot}=\mathbf{\hat{z}}A_{z}^{\rm tot}(\rho,\phi)
. For
\rho>\rho_{\rm cyl}
and
(\rho,\phi)\neq(\rho_{\rm fil},0)
,

satisfies Laplace’s equation. Adopting terms used in electromagnetic scattering, the exterior to the cylinder

is the sum of the incident vector potential
A_{z}^{\rm inc}(\rho,\phi)
due to the current source and the vector potential
A_{z}^{\rm sc}(\rho,\phi)
scattered by the cylinder. The former is given by

\displaystyle A_{z}^{\rm inc}(\rho,\phi)=-\frac{\mu_{0}I}{2\pi}\ln\frac{R_{\rm fil}}{d_{\rm ref}},

(10)

where

\displaystyle R_{\rm fil}=\sqrt{\rho^{2}+\rho_{\rm fil}^{2}-2\rho\rho_{\rm fil}\cos\phi}

(11)

is the distance between the observation point
(\rho,\phi)
and the current source (see
fig.

1
), while
d_{\rm ref}
is an arbitrary reference distance (so that the ratio
R_{\rm fil}/d_{\rm ref}
be dimensionless and
A_{z}^{\rm inc}=0
whenever
R_{\rm fil}=d_{\rm ref}
).

Inside the infinitely permeable cylinder the magnetic field
\mathbf{H}
is zero and the magnetic flux density
\mathbf{B}
is finite. This means that the tangential component of
\mathbf{H}
is continuous across the surface of the cylinder and thus zero at
\rho=\rho_{\rm cyl}+0
. Since
\mathbf{H}=(1/\mu_{0})\nabla\times\mathbf{A}
, the tangential component of the magnetic field is
H_{\phi}=(-1/\mu_{0})\partial A_{z}/\partial\rho

\partial A_{z}^{\rm tot}/\partial\rho=0
must hold at
\rho=\rho_{\rm cyl}+0
.

Our problem can be reformulated in terms of
A_{z}^{\rm sc}
as follows. We require
A_{z}^{\rm sc}\rightarrow 0
as
\rho\rightarrow\infty
, consistent with the fact that no current flows inside the cylinder or on its surface (at infinity,
A_{z}^{\rm tot}
behaves as does
A_{z}^{\rm inc}
). For
\rho>\rho_{\rm cyl}
,
A_{z}^{\rm sc}
satisfies Laplace’s equation subject to the nonhomogeneous Neumann boundary condition

\displaystyle\frac{\partial A_{z}^{\rm sc}}{\partial\rho}=-\frac{\partial A_{z}^{\rm inc}}{\partial\rho},\,\,\,\text{at}\,\,\,\rho=\rho_{\rm cyl}+0,

(12)

in which the right-hand side is known from (
10
) and (
11
). Within the loop of radius
\rho_{\mathrm{cyl}}
, the incident
\mathbf{H}
-field surrounds no current. Therefore the self-consistency condition for our Neumann problem (see Introduction or
[
5
]
) is

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(-\frac{\partial A_{z}^{\rm inc}}{\partial\rho}\right)\,\rho_{\rm cyl}{\rm\,d}\phi=0,\,\,\,\text{at}\,\,\,\rho=\rho_{\rm cyl}+0.

(13)

We can independently verify (
13
) by substituting (
10
) and (
11
) into the integral of its left-hand side: The resulting integral can be evaluated using the top integral (
3

The boundary-value problem we formulated has a well-known solution, viz.,

\displaystyle A_{z}^{\rm sc}(\rho,\phi)=-\frac{\mu_{0}I}{2\pi}\ln\frac{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+\rho_{\rm cri}^{2}-2\rho\rho_{\rm cri}\cos\phi}}{\rho},

(14)

where

\rho_{\rm cri}=\frac{\rho_{\rm cyl}^{2}}{\rho_{\rm fil}}\quad\left(\rho_{\rm cri}<\rho_{\rm cyl}\right).

(15)

Equation

14

z
-axis, and the other at
(\rho,\phi)=(\rho_{\rm cri},0)

Proceeding as if this solution were not known, the next two sections apply two different MFS schemes to the above-described boundary-value problem.
In both schemes, the fundamental solutions originate from sources
I_{0},I_{1},\ldots,I_{N-1}
placed on an interior cylindrical surface
\rho=\rho_{\rm aux}
, so that

\rho_{\rm aux}<\rho_{\rm cyl}<\rho_{\rm fil},

(16)

see
fig.

1

As we will see, both our schemes arrive at the true potential (

last two
terms in the numerator. We thus obtain

I_{\ell}\sim\frac{I}{N}t^{N}+\frac{2I}{N}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}t^{\frac{N+1}{2}}(t-1)\cos\frac{\pi\ell}{N}}{t^{2}-2t\cos\frac{2\pi\ell}{N}+1},\quad\text{as}\,\,\,N\rightarrow\infty\quad(\rho_{\rm aux}<\rho_{\rm cri}).

(33)

In (
33
), the
(-1)^{\ell}
means that the
I_{\ell}
oscillate, very rapidly, about the mean value (dominant term)
\frac{I}{N}t^{N}
, which is exponentially large in
N
. The
t^{\frac{N+1}{2}}
shows that the deviations from the mean are themselves exponentially large. Such currents (however normalized) cannot converge to a continuous
K_{z}(\phi)
. For this reason, the case
\rho_{\rm aux}<\rho_{\rm cri}
is termed “unphysical” or “oscillating.”

•

Case 3: Critical case
\rho_{\rm aux}=\rho_{\rm cri}

When
t=1
or
\rho_{\rm aux}=\rho_{\rm cri}
, an evaluation of the right-hand side of (
31
) (which is indeterminate when
\ell=0
and equal to zero otherwise) yields
I_{0}\sim I
and, when
\ell\neq 0
,
I_{\ell}\to 0
. The meaning of this large-
N
result is clear and was to be expected, because the
exact solution
(
14
) coincides with a single term of the sum in (
17
), namely the term with
\ell=0

(
this is true because
\rho_{\rm aux}=\rho_{\rm cri}
implies
R_{0}=\sqrt{\rho^{2}+\rho_{\rm cri}^{2}-2\rho\rho_{\rm cri}\cos\phi}

)
. Consequently, the aforementioned large-
N
result amounts to

A_{z,N}^{\rm sc}\sim A_{z}^{\rm sc},\quad\text{as}\,\,\,N\rightarrow\infty,

(34)

with only the
\ell=0
term surviving in the limit.

4.3
Convergence of vector potential

Having shown
eq.

34
for Case 3,
we discuss the large-
N
behavior of
A_{z,N}^{\rm sc}
for Cases 1 and 2. Substitution of
eq.

18
into
eq.

17
and use of the identity
eq.

7
gives

A_{z,N}^{\rm sc}(\rho,\phi)=\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\sum_{\ell=0}^{N-1}I_{\ell}\Bigg[\sum_{m\neq 0}\frac{1}{|m|}\bigg(\frac{\rho_{\rm aux}}{\rho}\bigg)^{|m|}e^{im(\phi-2\pi\ell/N)}\Bigg]\\
=\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\sum_{m\neq 0}\frac{1}{|m|}\bigg(\frac{\rho_{\rm aux}}{\rho}\bigg)^{|m|}e^{im\phi}\sum_{\ell=0}^{N-1}I_{\ell}e^{-i2\pi m\ell/N}.

(35)

In the last expression, the finite sum (over
\ell
) is
N
-periodic in
m
; since
I_{\ell}=I_{N-\ell}
, this sum is also even in
m
. By the inverse-DFT relation in
eq.

8
, the sum equals the
N
-periodic and even quantity
NI^{(m)}
. We thus obtain the exact formula

\displaystyle A_{z,N}^{\rm sc}(\rho,\phi)=\frac{\mu_{0}}{4\pi}\sum_{m\neq 0}\frac{NI^{(m)}}{|m|}\bigg(\frac{\rho_{\rm aux}}{\rho}\bigg)^{|m|}e^{im\phi},

(36)

in which
I^{(0)}
does not appear. Note that the aforementioned periodicity (together with the inequality
\rho_{\rm aux}<\rho
) ensure rapid convergence of the series in
eq.

36
for any finite
N
, even in Case 2: The product
I^{(m)}(\rho_{\rm aux}/\rho)^{|m|}
is always bounded by a constant times the exponentially decreasing function
(\rho_{\rm aux}/\rho)^{|m|}
.

As
N\rightarrow\infty
, the asymptotic formula
eq.

29
(for
I^{(m)}
) holds for
all
m>0
and, upon replacing
m
by
|m|
, can be extended to all
m\neq 0
. Consequently,
eq.

36
yields

\displaystyle A_{z,N}^{\rm sc}(\rho,\phi)\sim\frac{\mu_{0}I}{4\pi}\sum_{m\neq 0}\frac{1}{|m|}\bigg(\frac{\rho_{\rm cri}}{\rho}\bigg)^{|m|}e^{im\phi},\quad\mathrm{as\ }N\to\infty.

(37)

The series converges because
\rho_{\rm cri}<\rho_{\rm cyl}<\rho
, see
eq.

15
, and can be evaluated (exactly) with the aid of
eq.

7
. The result is

\displaystyle A_{z,N}^{\rm sc}(\rho,\phi)\sim-\frac{\mu_{0}I}{2\pi}\ln\frac{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+\rho_{\rm cri}^{2}-2\rho\rho_{\rm cri}\cos\phi}}{\rho},\quad\mathrm{as\ }N\to\infty,

(38)

whose right-hand side coincides with the exact solution in
eq.

14
, demonstrating that
eq.

34
also holds in Cases 1 and 2.
The salient point is that one obtains the true potential
A_{z}^{\rm sc}
when
\rho_{\rm aux}<\rho_{\rm cri}
. This is peculiar, because
the correct solution is arrived at even in the unphysical case, in which the MAS currents diverge and oscillate rapidly.

Having thus clarified the particularities of the “unphysical” case, let us stress that some sort of anomalous behavior was to be expected, if only from the general considerations of our Introduction. The point
(\rho,\phi)=(\rho_{\rm cri},0)
is a singularity of the analytic continuation of the (exact) scattered potential to points interior to the circle, i.e., to points
(\rho,\phi)
with
\rho<\rho_{\rm cyl}
. The origin is another singularity. In this simple problem, the singularity positions can be deduced a priori from (
38
), and are consistent with the annulus of convergence of the Laurent series in (
37
).

4.4
Matrix condition number

We now briefly discuss the condition number of the matrix in (
20
). For similar results, see
[
28
]
for the interior circular Laplace-Neumann problem;
[
25
,
26
,
33
]
for the interior circular Laplace-Dirichlet problem;
[
27
]
for a stability analysis for the circular/noncircular interior Laplace problem with a mixed type of boundary conditions;
[
2
]
for the electrodynamic problem of scattering from a PEC cylinder, and
[
1
]
for a similar problem with a dielectric cylinder.

The DFT
B^{(m)}
of the first row of the circulant matrix in (
20
) is given in (
23
). As
N\to\infty
, the smallest
B^{(m)}
is
B^{(0)}\sim(\rho_{\rm aux}/\rho_{\rm cyl})^{N}
, while the largest is
B^{(1)}\sim\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\rm aux}/\rho_{\rm cyl}
. Since circulant matrices are normal, the
B^{(m)}
coincide with the matrix eigenvalues and singular values
[
9
,
19
]
, and the ratio
B^{(1)}/B^{(0)}
is the 2-norm condition number
\kappa
:

\kappa\sim\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\rho_{\rm cyl}}{\rho_{\rm aux}}\right)^{N-1},\quad\mathrm{as\ }N\to\infty.

(39)

In both cases, therefore (physical and unphysical),
\kappa
is exponentially large in
N
. The base (which, as expected, is independent of
\rho_{\rm fil}
) is seen to decrease with
\rho_{\rm aux}
.

4.5
Insensitivity of vector potential

Since
\kappa
is large, small errors (e.g., in the calculation of the boundary data/incident vector potential, or in the potentials generated by the auxiliary sources) will greatly affect the
I_{\ell}
. Will these resulting errors in
I_{\ell}
affect the calculation of
A_{z,N}^{\rm sc}
(which we find from (
17
), and is the final quantity of interest)? This is a separate issue (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
) that seems to have been first addressed quantitatively by Kitagawa in the 1991 paper
[
26
]
(in the context of interior Laplace-Dirichlet problems, but much carries over to the present case). Since errors are not our main focus—we rather focus on phenomena that would occur even with perfect hardware and software—we limit ourselves to some qualitative remarks for the unphysical/oscillating case (Case 2,
\rho_{\rm aux}<\rho_{\rm cri}
). We assume, of course, that
N
is large.

As we already saw, the main contribution to the sum in (
27
) (for
I_{\ell}-I^{(0)}
) comes from large values of
m
; hence the oscillations. By contrast, the main contribution to the rapidly-converging,
exact
infinite series (
36
) (for
A_{z,N}^{\rm sc}
) comes from small values of
|m|
. This allows us to view the factor
(\rho_{\rm aux}/\rho)^{|m|}
as a low-pass filter that blocks the large-
|m|
values of
I^{(m)}
.

Let us now assume that the
N\times N
system (
20
) is solved by a method that mostly introduces errors in the large-
|m|
values of
I^{(m)}
. A similar hypothesis is further discussed by Kitagawa in
[
26
]
, where it is phrased in terms of the matrix singular values
B^{(m)}
. In any case, the above assumption seems to be true for the standard MATLAB solver that we used. Subject to our assumption, the aforementioned small errors we started out with (which, due to the large
\kappa
, appear as large errors in the
I_{\ell}
) will be filtered out, and will not greatly affect the final result
A_{z,N}^{\rm sc}
. We call this phenomenon
insensitivity
. In fact, it is easily understood from (
36
) that more insensitivity occurs for larger
\rho
, in which case the cutoff of the low-pass filter becomes smaller.

Let us emphasize that insensitivity pertains to errors that arise from, e.g., roundoff, or transcendental-function computations, and that appear magnified in the
I_{\ell}
because of the large
\kappa
. The unphysical oscillations of
I_{\ell}
(Section
4.2
) are definitely not errors of this sort. In a sense, the two effects compete: Since large intermediate results should in
general be avoided
[
22
]
, the existence of oscillations is a hindrance to numerically obtaining the correct potential; by contrast, insensitivity helps.

5
Exterior circular problem: Traditional fundamental solutions

We now employ MAS with the “traditional” or “logarithmic” fundamental solutions
[
17
,
24
,
29
]
. This means approximating the scattered vector potential by

\displaystyle A^{\prime\rm sc}_{z,N}(\rho,\phi)=-\frac{\mu_{0}}{2\pi}\sum_{\ell=0}^{N-1}I^{\prime}_{\ell}\ln\frac{R_{\ell}}{d_{\rm ref}},

(40)

with the
R_{\ell}
defined in (
18
). Once again, we require the
I^{\prime}_{\ell}
to satisfy the boundary condition (
12
) at
(\rho_{\rm cyl},2\pi p/N)
. It is apparent from (
19
) that
I^{\prime}_{\ell}\neq I_{\ell}
. Following the steps of Section
4.1
, we obtain a new linear system of equations that reads

\sum_{\ell=0}^{N-1}B^{\prime}_{\ell-p}I^{\prime}_{\ell}=D_{p},\quad p=0,\dots,N-1,

(41)

with

B^{\prime}_{p}=\frac{\rho_{\rm cyl}^{2}-\rho_{\rm cyl}\rho_{\rm aux}\cos\frac{2\pi p}{N}}{\rho_{\rm cyl}^{2}+\rho_{\rm aux}^{2}-2\rho_{\rm cyl}\rho_{\rm aux}\cos\frac{2\pi p}{N}}=B_{p}+1.

(42)

Thus the right-hand side
D_{p}
remains the same, while the new circulant matrix is obtained by adding
1
to all matrix elements. It follows from (
8
) that
D^{\prime(m)}=D^{(m)}
for all
m
; and from (
8
), (
9
), and (
23
) that

B^{\prime(0)}=B^{(0)}+1=\frac{1}{1-\Big(\frac{\rho_{\rm aux}}{\rho_{\rm cyl}}\Big)^{N}},\quad B^{\prime(m)}=B^{(m)},\quad m=1,2,\ldots,N-1.

(43)

These relations and (
71
) in turn imply

\displaystyle I^{\prime(m)}=I^{(m)},\quad m=1,2,\ldots,N-1,

(44)

while, by (
71
), (
73
), and (
43
),

\displaystyle I^{\prime(0)}=\frac{D^{(0)}}{NB^{\prime(0)}}=\frac{I}{N}\frac{\Big(\frac{\rho_{\rm cyl}}{\rho_{\rm fil}}\Big)^{N}\bigg[1-\Big(\frac{\rho_{\rm aux}}{\rho_{\rm cyl}}\Big)^{N}\bigg]}{1-\Big(\frac{\rho_{\rm cyl}}{\rho_{\rm fil}}\Big)^{N}}.

(45)

The MAS currents
I^{\prime}_{\ell}
are thus given by (
24
) or (
27
), but with
I^{(0)}
replaced by the
I^{\prime(0)}
of (
45
). Accordingly, (
31
) is modified to

\displaystyle I^{\prime}_{\ell}\sim\frac{I}{N}\left[\left(\frac{\rho_{\rm cyl}}{\rho_{\rm fil}}\right)^{N}+2\frac{(-1)^{\ell}t^{\frac{N+1}{2}}(t-1)\cos\frac{\pi\ell}{N}-t^{2}+t\cos\frac{2\pi\ell}{N}}{t^{2}-2t\cos\frac{2\pi\ell}{N}+1}\right],\quad\text{as}\,\,\,N\rightarrow\infty,

(46)

with
t
as in (
30
).
The conclusions of Sections
4.2
–
4.4
are recast as follows.

•

Case 1: Physical case
\rho_{\rm aux}>\rho_{\rm cri}

When
\rho_{\rm aux}>\rho_{\rm cri}
, the asymptotic formula (
32
) remains the same. The
I^{\prime}_{l}
, when properly normalized, converge to (the same) surface current density
K_{z}(\phi)
, which can now be regarded as a (continuous) source located at
\rho=\rho_{\rm aux}
and generating the scattered vector potential. Since the only restriction on
\rho_{\rm aux}
is
\rho_{\rm cri}<\rho_{\rm aux}<\rho_{\rm cyl}
, this source is certainly not unique. We can further show that
K_{z}(\phi)
is the solution of an integral equation; we do not dwell on this, because the pertinent discussions within the context of electrodynamics
[
14
]
can easily be extended to the present case.

•

Case 2: Unphysical/oscillating case
\rho_{\rm aux}<\rho_{\rm cri}

In the more interesting case
\rho_{\rm aux}<\rho_{\rm cri}
, we must neglect the exponentially small term
\left(\rho_{\rm cyl}/\rho_{\rm fil}\right)^{N}
when simplifying (
46
). Accordingly, (
33
) is modified to

I^{\prime}_{\ell}\sim\frac{2I}{N}\frac{(-1)^{\ell}t^{\frac{N+1}{2}}(t-1)\cos\frac{\pi\ell}{N}}{t^{2}-2t\cos\frac{2\pi\ell}{N}+1},\quad\text{as}\,\,\,N\rightarrow\infty\quad(\rho_{\rm aux}<\rho_{\rm cri}).

(47)

Consequently, the MAS currents
I^{\prime}_{\ell}
still exhibit exponentially large oscillations, but the mean is zero (previously, the mean was exponentially large). There is no associated continuous
K_{z}(\phi)
, so these
I^{\prime}_{\ell}
are unphysical in the same sense as were the
I_{\ell}
.

•

Case 3: Critical case
\rho_{\rm aux}=\rho_{\rm cri}

Here, (
46
) leads to
I^{\prime}_{0}\sim I
and, when
\ell\neq 0
,
I^{\prime}_{\ell}\sim-I/N
. This time, the
\ell=0
term in (
40
) accounts for the image at the critical point
(\rho_{\rm cri},0)
—see the exact solution (
14
)—while, for
\ell\neq 0
, the normalized
I^{\prime}_{\ell}
amount to a
\phi
-independent surface-current density on the circle of radius
\rho_{\rm aux}=\rho_{\rm cri}
, which replaces the image at the origin.

•

In all three cases, it remains true that one obtains the true potential, i.e.,

A^{{}^{\prime}\rm sc}_{z,N}\sim A_{z}^{\rm sc},\quad\text{as}\,\,\,N\rightarrow\infty.

(48)

This can be verified by going through the steps of
section

4.3
. In particular, we have found (a posteriori) that the boundary condition at infinity is satisfied.

•

As
N\to\infty
, the condition number
\kappa^{\prime}
is large, but its order of magnitude differs. This is because the smallest
B^{(m)}
is
B^{((N-1)/2)}
, while the largest is the
B^{\prime(0)}
of (
43
)—this is
O(1)
, while
B^{(0)}
was exponentially small. Thus in place of (
39
) we have

\kappa^{\prime}\sim\left(\frac{\rho_{\rm cyl}}{\rho_{\rm aux}}\right)^{\frac{N}{2}}

(49)

While exponentially large, this
\kappa^{\prime}
is much smaller than the
\kappa
of (
39
).

As opposed to this second version of MAS, the first one had the advantage of a priori satisfying the boundary condition at infinity, for any finite
N
. Despite this, it presents the severe disadvantage of having a much larger matrix condition number. This affects the calculation of the auxiliary currents (when one finds them by solving the
N\times N
system, something necessary for noncircular problems). Furthermore, the currents of our first version oscillate about a mean value that is nonzero and large. This is disadvantageous, because stable algorithms should normally avoid large intermediate results
[
22
]
.

7
Extensions

We have discussed several aspects of the application of MAS to exterior circular Laplace-Neumann problems. We now extend our results in a number of different directions.

7.1
Purely numerical effects;
the use of overdetermined systems

Errors due to roundoff can in principle be alleviated by longer computer wordlengths. As we already discussed, the unphysical oscillations analyzed herein have nothing to do with roundoff; the existence of an asympotic formula predicting the oscillating values (see the left
fig.

2
) suffices to confirm this observation, which is also supported by the agreement—already mentioned in
section

6
—between the two methods of obtaining the
I_{\ell}
(namely, DFT and direct solution of the
N\times N
system). For the parameters in
fig.

2
, this agreement is not implausible, because the condition number is not prohibitively large:
eq.

39
gives
\kappa=5\cdot 10^{12}
(the MATLAB routine
cond
gives the same
\kappa
).

While modern computers can deal with systems having such condition numbers,
\kappa
rapidly increases with
N
. For example, if we increase the
N
of
fig.

2
to
N=101
, we have
\kappa\sim 10^{16}
, and the two methods of obtaining
I_{\ell}
no longer give the same results: DFT gives
I_{0}/I=4\cdot 10^{4}
, while directly solving
eq.

20
gives
I_{0}/I=1\cdot 10^{4}
. Now, roundoff (which was negligible in
fig.

2
) becomes important (at least in the calculation of
I_{\ell}
, recall the insensitivity of
section

4.5
).

The MAS-related literature (see for example
[
8
,
11
]
) often uses overdetermined systems. In our case, such schemes amount to enforcing
the boundary condition
eq.

12
at
M
collocation points
(\rho_{\rm cyl},2\pi p/M)
(
p=0,\dots,M-1
and
M>N
) and solving the resulting overdetermined
M\times N
system (for
I_{\ell}
) in the least-squares sense. We experimented with such schemes, and found no significant improvement as far as the oscillations in the unphysical case are concerned.

Let us return to square (
N\times N
) systems. For sufficiently large
N
(or sufficiently small
\rho_{\rm aux}
), we found that the
I_{\ell}
obtained by solving the system no longer agree with the asymptotic formula
eq.

33
. This means that solution behavior is clouded by roundoff: the
true
behavior (which would be obtained in a hypothetical computer with infinite wordlength) is, somewhat paradoxically, better predicted by the asymptotic formula. In other words, the effects caused by the large condition numbers are purely numerical; so are the mitigating effects due to insensitivity. These two effects depend on computer wordlength and the system solver and, more generally, on the specific software and hardware one uses.

Needless to say, whenever oscillations were observed, the auxiliary surface did not enclose the singularities of the scattered potential. These singularities can be easily determined from the (exterior) solution found in

7.3
Interior circular problem (cavity problem)

Now consider the circular “cavity” problem of
fig.

4
, in which a line current
I
is located at
(\rho,\phi)=(\rho_{\rm fil},0)

inside
a nonmagnetic (
\mu=\mu_{0}
) cylinder of radius
\rho_{\rm cyl}
, with the surrounding medium having infinite permeability (
\mu=\infty
). As in

3

\rho>\rho_{\rm cyl}
. However, the properties of
\mathbf{H}
and
\mathbf{B}
now differ. In the surrounding medium, with
\mu=\infty

\mathbf{H}
must be finite and so
\mathbf{B}=\mu\mathbf{H}
becomes infinite. In order for the tangential components of
\mathbf{H}
and the normal components of
\mathbf{B}
to be continuous across the surface of the cylinder
\rho=\rho_{\rm cyl}
(boundary conditions), the field lines of
\mathbf{H}
(and
\mathbf{B}

\rho=\rho_{\rm cyl}+0
, but not at
\rho=\rho_{\rm cyl}-0
. In the interior, where
\mu=\mu_{0}
, the direction of the field lines is not normal to the surface of the cylinder (as in the exterior circular problem), but is such that the aforementioned boundary conditions be valid there
[
21
,
36
]
. The component
H_{\phi}
of
\mathbf{H}
equals
I/(2\pi\rho)
for
\rho\geq\rho_{\rm cyl}

[
36
]
. Therefore the total interior potential satisfies
\partial A_{z}^{\rm tot}/\partial\rho=-\mu_{0}I/(2\pi\rho_{\rm cyl})
at
\rho=\rho_{\rm cyl}-0
.

:
Geometry of the interior circular problem. Indicative positions of MAS currents and collocation points are depicted.

Once again, we formulate our problem in terms of the scattered potential

\rho_{\rm fil}<\rho_{\rm cyl}

By Amperé’s circuital law,

\oint_{\rho=\rho_{\rm cyl}-0}\mathbf{H}^{\mathrm{inc}}\cdot{\rm d}\mathbf{l}=I

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left(-\frac{\partial A_{z}^{\rm inc}}{\partial\rho}-\frac{\mu_{0}I}{2\pi\rho_{\rm cyl}}\right)\rho_{\rm cyl}\,d\phi=0,\,\,\,\text{at}\,\,\ \rho=\rho_{\rm cyl}-0,

•

Case 1: Physical case
\rho_{\rm aux}<\rho_{\rm cri}

When
\rho_{\rm aux}<\rho_{\rm cri}
, the normalized

, just as in the interior problem. Furthermore (because the first term in

converges to a true solution of the problem, specifically to the right-hand side of

.

•

Case 2: Unphysical/oscillating case
\rho_{\rm aux}>\rho_{\rm cri}

Here, the
I_{\ell}
cannot converge to a surface current density; rather, their asymptotic behavior is given by
eq.

33
, but with
t=\rho_{\rm aux}/\rho_{\rm cri}>1
. Furthermore—with an exception to be discussed below—
A_{z,N}^{\rm sc}
diverges as

. The “divergent part”—i.e., the first term in (
59

, and independent of
\rho
and
\phi
, but is dependent on
\rho_{\rm aux}
.

For Case 2, it is worth stressing that the mean value of the oscillating
I_{\ell}
is large, as in the exterior problem with the
bounded
fundamental solutions, even if we used the
traditional
ones here. Yet what is really new is the divergence of the potential, something not encountered in any of the two schemes we applied to the exterior problem. We illustrate this divergence in

, where it is seen that three successive values of

give potentials that (due to the change of the first term in

59
) differ by a
\rho

In Case 2, choosing
\rho_{\rm aux}=d_{\rm ref}
has the desirable consequence of rendering the divergent part to be zero; this is the exceptional case we previously alluded to. It seems, however, that this case is only of theoretical interest because there is no straightforward way of extending the choice
\rho_{\rm aux}=d_{\rm ref}
to noncircular problems.

It is perhaps more interesting that the divergent part is independent of the observation point
(\rho,\phi)
: If it is
\mathbf{H}
that is finally desired, and if we calculate
\mathbf{H}
numerically from

, then all sufficiently large values of

will give the same

. (As large intermediate results should in general be avoided
[
22
]
, this is true with the proviso that
A_{z,N}^{\rm sc}
does not become too large.) Thus in Case 2, the first term in (
59
) somewhat resembles a nonzero

in the exact solution (
54
); but (since it is exponentially large) the said term does not
converge
to an
A_{0}
.

These findings render the MAS behavior of the present (interior circular Laplace-Neumann) problem distinctly different from that of the electrodynamics problems we have studied
[
14
,
16
,
17
,
35
,
18
]
.

8
Conclusions and future work

Previous works
[
14
,
16
,
17
,
35
,
18
]
have shown that it is possible for the final MAS solution to converge to the correct one despite the divergence and unphysical oscillations of an intermediate result, namely the MAS currents. Here, we extended these findings to the case of Laplace-Neumann problems. As in the previous works, the normalized MAS currents diverge and oscillate whenever the analytic continuation of the scattered potential presents a singularity between the auxiliary surface and the 2D boundary; in the circular problems, the oscillations are well-predicted by asymptotic formulas ((
33
) and (
47
)) and would therefore occur even with perfect hardware and software; these oscillations must be distinguished from effects (
section

7.1
) that are purely numerical in nature and depend on the hardware/software implementation.

The present analysis (for the Laplace-Neumann case) has certain particularities that do not pertain to the corresponding time-harmonic cases: The exact and asymptotic formulas are generally simpler, so that conclusions arise in a clearer way. We treated the exterior circular problem by two different methods and found noteworthy differences between the two arising unphysical/oscillating cases (in one of the two, the currents oscillate about a
large
mean value, whereas the mean is zero in the corresponding time-harmonic problems). The interior circular magnetostatic problem, which is known to require a different Neumann formulation from the exterior one (compare
eq.

12
to
eq.

52
), gave rise to a “divergent” vector potential (in the sense explained in
section

7.3
; no such divergence seems to occur in the corresponding time-harmonic problems). Finally, to extend our findings to noncircular problems, we chose an elliptic boundary, for which we developed a particularly simple closed-form solution (
appendix

C
), whose singularities can be deduced a priori.

The “insensitivity” of
section

4.5
was observed numerically. In fact, the numerical results suggest that the present (Laplace-Neumann) problem exhibits more insensitivity than certain time-harmonic problems we have studied
[
14
,
16
,
17
,
35
,
18
]
. For now, we make no attempt to state this empirical observation in a more precise manner, but we do plan to study it in the future. Such a study should consider insensitivity concurrently with the often overlooked phenomenon of oscillations (which, as we saw in
section

4.5
, can be thought of as a competing effect).
We also plan to study more carefully the manner in which the two combined phenomena affect the MFS solutions of noncircular problems.

Appendix C
Exact solution for elliptic cylinder

Here, we outline the derivation of the exact solution to the 2D problem of an elliptic cylinder of infinite permeability, illuminated by a line-current source
I
. The cross section of the cylinder is an ellipse with major and minor axes
2a
and
2b
, respectively. The current
I
is located at
(x,y)=(\rho_{\rm fil},0)
. The problem is solved subject to the Neumann boundary condition for the total vector potential at the surface of the cylinder. We employ the separation of variables in elliptic coordinates to obtain the solution in a particularly simple form.

The 2D elliptic coordinates
(\xi,\eta)
are connected to the Cartesian coordinates
(x,y)
by means of the transformation

\displaystyle x=c\cosh\xi\cos\eta,\quad y=c\sinh\xi\sin\eta,

(75)

where
\xi\geq 0
,
-\pi<\eta\leq\pi
, and
c
is a parameter that determines the focal points of the elliptic coordinate system. In the latter, the surfaces of constant
\xi
are confocal ellipses, while the surfaces of constant
\eta
are branches of confocal hyperbolas; in both cases the focal distance is
2c
.

In elliptic coordinates, the surface of the elliptic cylinder is described by
\xi=\xi_{0}
, where
\xi_{0}
is determined by either
\cosh\xi_{0}=a/c
or
\sinh\xi_{0}=b/c
, with
c=(a^{2}-b^{2})^{1/2}
half the focal distance. The respective coordinates of the current filament are
(\xi,\eta)=(\xi_{\rm fil},0)
, where
\cosh{\xi_{\rm fil}}=\rho_{\rm fil}/c
. For
\xi>\xi_{0}
and
(\xi,\eta)\neq(\xi_{\rm fil},0)
,
A_{z}^{\rm sc}
satisfies the 2D Laplace equation. In accordance with (
12
), the Neumann boundary condition is

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h_{\xi}}\frac{\partial A_{z}^{\rm sc}}{\partial\xi}=-\frac{1}{h_{\xi}}\frac{\partial A_{z}^{\rm inc}}{\partial\xi},\,\,\,\text{at}\,\,\,\xi=\xi_{0}+0.

(76)

where
h_{\xi}=c(\cosh^{2}\xi-\cos^{2}\eta)^{1/2}
is the appropriate scale factor.
Expression (
10
) for
A_{z}^{\rm inc}
is written in elliptic coordinates as

\displaystyle A_{z}^{\rm inc}(\xi,\eta)=-\frac{\mu_{0}I}{2\pi}\ln\frac{\sqrt{(c\cosh\xi\cos\eta-\rho_{\rm fil})^{2}+c^{2}\sinh^{2}\xi\sin^{2}\eta}}{d_{\rm ref}},

(77)

and we can easily verify that the Neumann boundary data (i.e., the right-hand side of (
76
)) satisfies the self-consistency condition: Indeed, the related integral is reduced to (
1
) with
m=0
,
x=\rho_{\rm fil}/a
, and
y=b/a
, and through (
2
) turns out to vanish.

Because the Laplace equation is separable in elliptic coordinates, the solution for
A_{z}^{\rm sc}
can be written in the form of the series

\displaystyle A_{z}^{\rm sc}(\xi,\eta)=-\frac{\mu_{0}I}{2\pi}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}e^{-m\xi}\big[C_{m}\cos(m\eta)+D_{m}\sin(m\eta)\big],

(78)

which satisfies the condition
A_{z}^{\rm sc}\rightarrow 0
as
\xi\rightarrow\infty
.
C_{m}
and
D_{m}
are unknown expansion coefficients. To determine them, substitute (
77
) and (
78
) into (
76
) and make use of the orthogonality of the trigonometric functions
\cos(m\eta)
and
\sin(m\eta)
. We find

\displaystyle C_{m}=\frac{e^{m\xi_{0}}}{m\pi}\frac{b}{a}J\left(m,\frac{\rho_{\rm fil}}{a},\frac{b}{a}\right),\quad D_{m}=0,\quad m=1,2,\dots.

(79)

where
J(m,x,y)
is the convergent integral defined in (
1
). Using (
79
) in conjunction with (
2
) and some algebra, we can see that

\displaystyle C_{m}=\frac{e^{-m\xi_{\rm fil}}-e^{m(2\xi_{0}-\xi_{\rm fil})}}{m}.

(80)

If we define

\displaystyle w_{1}=e^{2\xi_{0}-\xi_{\rm fil}-\xi},\quad w_{2}=e^{-\xi_{\rm fil}-\xi},

(81)

then (
78
) becomes the convergent-series solution

\displaystyle A_{z}^{\rm sc}(\xi,\eta)=-\frac{\mu_{0}I}{2\pi}\bigg[-\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{w_{1}^{m}}{m}\cos(m\eta)+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{w_{2}^{m}}{m}\cos(m\eta)\bigg],\quad\xi\geq\xi_{0},

(82)

which somewhat resembles the series solution found in
[
34
]
. Finally, use of the following corollary of (
7
),

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{w^{m}}{m}\cos(m\eta)=-\ln\sqrt{1-2w\cos{\eta}+w^{2}},\quad 0\leq w<1,\quad-\pi<\eta\leq\pi,

(83)

gives

\displaystyle A_{z}^{\rm sc}(\xi,\eta)=-\frac{\mu_{0}I}{4\pi}\ln\frac{1-2w_{1}\cos\eta+w_{1}^{2}}{1-2w_{2}\cos\eta+w_{2}^{2}}.

(84)
