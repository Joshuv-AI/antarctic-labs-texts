On entire f f -maximal graphs in the Lorentzian product 𝔾 n × ℝ 1 \mathbb{G}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{1}
H. V. Q. An, D. V. Cuong, N. T. M. Duyen, D. T. Hieu, T. L. Nam

Abstract
In the Lorentzian product 𝔾 n × ℝ 1 , \mathbb{G}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{1}, we give a comparison theorem between the f f -volume of an entire f f -maximal graph and the f f -volume of the hyperbolic H r + H_{r}^{+} under the condition that the gradient of the function defining the graph is bounded away from 1. This condition comes from an example of non-planar entire f f -maximal graph in 𝔾 n × ℝ 1 \mathbb{G}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{1} and is equivalent to the hyperbolic angle function of the graph being bounded. As a consequence, we obtain a Calabi-Bernstein type theorem for f f -maximal graphs in 𝔾 n × ℝ 1 . \mathbb{G}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{1}.

Introduction

Suppose that
\Sigma
is the graph of a
C^{2}
function
f

over a domain
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}.

The graph
\Sigma
is minimal if the function
f
satisfies the Minimal Surface Equation

\divv\left(\frac{\nabla f}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla f|^{2}}}\right)=0.

S. N. Bernstein (1915-1917) proved a surprising theorem in the case
n=2
, named after him, that such a function defined over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
must be affine. This means that a minimal graph over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
must be a plane. He conjectured that the theorem holds true for
n>2.

Bernstein’s conjecture has been a longstanding problem and it was proved to be true for
n\leq 7
by the works of De Giorgi, F. Almgren and J. Simons (see
[
6
]
,
[
11
]
and
[
21
]
). However, Bombieri, De Giorgi and Giusti
[
7
]

showed the existence of some entire minimal graphs other than the hyperplanes for
n\geq 8.

The Lorentzian version of the Bernstein’s Theorem for maximal graphs in the Lorentz-Minkowski spaces
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{1}
is called the Calabi-Bernstein’s Theorem. Quite different from the Euclidean case, the Calabi-Bernstein’s Theorem holds true for any dimension. The theorem was first proved by Calabi
[
8
]
for the case
n=2
and later by Cheng and Yau
[
10
]
for the case
n>2.

The Calabi-Bernstein’s Theorem has been generalized to Lorentzian product spaces (see
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
and
[
4
]
, for instance) as well as to other ambient spaces such as warped products, Robertson-Walker spacetimes, manifolds with density…(see
[
5
]
,
[
9
]
,
[
14
]
,
[
20
]
and
[
22
]
, for instance).
Albujer and Alías
[
3
]
proved a Calabi-Bernstein type result, that any entire maximal graph in
M^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{1},
where
M
is a Riemannian manifold with non-negative Gaussian curvature, must be totally geodesic. They also proved that if
M
is non-flat, then the graph must be a slice
M\times\{t_{0}\},t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.
In
[
1
]
, some non-planar entire maximal graphs in
\mathbb{H}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{1}
were constructed. This shows that without the assumption of nonnegative Gauss curvature of
M,
the theorem is no longer true.

The Calabi-Bernstein’s Theorem has been also generalized to manifolds with density.
L. Wang (see
[
22
]
) proved a Bernstein type theorem for self-shrinkers in
\mathbb{R}^{n},
i.e., for
f
-minimal hypersurfaces in Gauss space
\mathbb{G}^{n}.

By a calibration argument, without using second order differential equations, the last two authors of this paper (see
[
14
]
) proved a Bernstein type theorem for entire
f
-minimal graphs in
\mathbb{G}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}.
In these cases the theorem holds true without any condition.

The purpose of this paper is to establish a similar Calabi-Bernstein type theorem for entire
f
-maximal graphs in the Lorentzian product
\mathbb{G}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{1}
by using Lorentzian calibration arguments.

The results are quite different from the case of the Riemannian product. The first main result of the paper is that an example of a non-planar entire
f
-maximal graph in
\mathbb{G}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{1}
was found. We see that along the first coordinate axis, the gradient of the function determining the graph converges to 1. So it seems the condition for an entire
f
-maximal graph in
\mathbb{G}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{1}
being a hyperplane is that the gradient of the function determining the graph is bounded away from 1, or equivalently, the hyperbolic angle function of the graph is bounded. In fact, with this condition, a comparison theorem between the
f
-volume of an entire
f
-maximal graph and the
f
-volume of the hyperbolobic
H_{r}^{+}
is given. As a consequence, we obtain the second main result, a Calabi-Bernstein type theorem for
f
-maximal graphs in
\mathbb{G}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{1}.

2
f
-maximal graphs

Let
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{1}
be the
(n+1)
-dimensional Lorentz-Minkowski space, i.e.,
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
endowed with the Lorentzian scalar product

\langle\ ,\ \rangle=dx_{1}^{2}+dx_{2}^{2}+\ldots+dx_{n}^{2}-dx_{n+1}^{2}.

Since
\langle,\rangle
is
non-positive definite, for
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{1},

\langle\textbf{x},\textbf{x}\rangle
may be zero or negative. A nonzero
vector
\textbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{1}
is called spacelike, lightlike or timelike if
\langle\textbf{x},\textbf{x}\rangle>0
,
\langle\textbf{x},\textbf{x}\rangle=0
or
\langle\textbf{x},\textbf{x}\rangle<0
,
respectively. Two vectors
\textbf{x},\textbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{1}
are said to be Lorentzian orthogonal if
\langle\textbf{x},\textbf{y}\rangle=0.
The norm of a tangent vector
x
is defined by
\|\textbf{x}\|=\sqrt{|\langle\textbf{x},\textbf{x}\rangle|}.

We say that two timelike tangent vectors
x
and
y
lie in the same timelike cone if
\langle\textbf{x},\textbf{y}\rangle<0.
If
x
and
\textbf{e}_{n+1},
where
\textbf{e}_{n+1}
denotes the timelike coordinate vector field, lie in the same timelike cone, we say that
x
is future-directed. For timelike tangent vectors
x
and
\textbf{y},
we have the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

|\langle\textbf{x},\textbf{y}\rangle|\geq\sqrt{-\langle\textbf{x},\textbf{x}\rangle}\sqrt{-\langle\textbf{y},\textbf{y}\rangle},

and the equality holds if and only if
x
and
y
are proportional. If
x
and
y
lie in the same timelike cone, then there exists a unique number
\varphi\geq 0
such that

\langle\textbf{x},\textbf{y}\rangle=-|\textbf{x}||\textbf{y}|\cosh\varphi.

The number
\varphi
is called the hyperbolic angle between
x
and
\textbf{y}.

A hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{1}
is called spacelike if its induced metric
from
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{1}
is Riemannian. Equivalently, every tangent vector of the hypersurface is spacelike. A spacelike hypersurface is orientable, i.e., there exists a unit timelike normal vector field
N
on it. Moreover, we can suppose that
N
is future-directed and then we can define the hyperbolic angle function
\theta
of the hypersurface. The value of
\theta
at a point
p
on the hypersurface is the hyperbolic angle between
N(p)
and
\textbf{e}_{n+1}.

Let
\Sigma
be a spacelike hypersurface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{1}
and
N
be the unit timelike normal vector field.
Denote by
\overline{D}
and
D
the Levi-Civita connections of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{1}
and
\Sigma,
respectively. For
X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma),
we have

\overline{D}_{X}Y=D_{X}Y-\langle A(X),Y\rangle N,

and

A(X)=-\overline{D}_{X}N,

where
A:\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)\rightarrow\mathfrak{X}(\Sigma)
is the shape operator associated to
N.

The mean curvature of
\Sigma,
denoted by
H,
is defined by

H=-\frac{1}{n}\trace(A).

It is well-known that

nH=-\divv N.

A spacelike hypersurface is called
maximal
if its mean curvature
H
is zero everywhere.
When
\Sigma
is the graph of a function
u:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
where
U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},
then it is easy to see that

N=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}(\nabla u+\textbf{e}_{n+1}),

is the future-directed unit timelike normal vector field on
\Sigma,
where
\nabla u
denotes the gradient of the function
u
on
U.
Note that since
\Sigma
is spacelike,
|\nabla u|<1.
Hence the mean curvature of
\Sigma
is computed on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to be

H=-\frac{1}{n}{\rm div}_{{}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\right),

where
{\rm div}_{{}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}
is the divergence operator on
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Therefore,
\Sigma
is maximal if and only if
u
satisfies the maximal equation

{\rm div}_{{}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\right)=0.

A density on
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{1}
is a positive function
e^{-f},
and it is used to weight volumes of
k
-dimensional spacelike submanifolds. In
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{1}
with density
e^{-f},
the weighted volume, or
f
-volume, of a spacelike hypersurface
\Sigma,
denoted by
\Vol_{f}(\Sigma),
is defined by

\Vol_{f}(\Sigma)=\int_{\Sigma}e^{-f}dV_{\Sigma},

where
dV_{\Sigma}
is the volume element of the hypersurface. The weighted mean curvature or
f
-mean curvature of
\Sigma,
denoted by
H_{f},
is defined by

H_{f}=H+\frac{1}{n}\langle\nabla f,N\rangle.

Now,
\Sigma
is called
f
-maximal
provided that
H_{f}=0
everywhere, i.e.,
H=-\frac{1}{n}\langle\nabla f,N\rangle.

If
\Sigma
is the graph of a function
u:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R},
then
\Sigma
is
f
-maximal if and only if
u
satisfies the
f
-maximal equation

{\rm div}_{{}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\left(\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\right)-\langle\nabla f,N\rangle=0.

The Gauss space
\mathbb{G}^{n},
a typical example of manifolds with density, is just
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with Gaussian probability density

e^{-f(\textbf{x})}=e^{c-\frac{|{\bf x}|^{2}}{2}},

where
c=\log[(2\pi)^{-\frac{n}{2}}].
Gauss space has many applications to probability and statistics (see
[
17
]
and
[
18
]
, for instance).

The space
\mathbb{G}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{1}
is
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{1}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{1}
endowed with the Gaussian-Euclidean density

e^{-f(\textbf{x},t)}=e^{c-\frac{|{\bf x}|^{2}}{2}},

where
(\textbf{x},t)\in\mathbb{G}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{1},\ \textbf{x}\in\mathbb{G}^{n},\ t\in\mathbb{R}_{1}.

Below is the example about a non-planar entire
f
-maximal graph in
\mathbb{G}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{1}
mentioned in the introduction section.

Consider the entire graph
\Sigma_{0}
of the function
u:\mathbb{G}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R},\ \textbf{x}\longmapsto\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x_{1}}\sqrt{\dfrac{e^{\tau^{2}}}{1+e^{\tau^{2}}}}d\tau,
where
\textbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}).
The graph
\Sigma_{0}
has a parametrization as follows

\displaystyle X(\textbf{x},t)=\left(\textbf{x},u(\textbf{x})\right).

Since

1-|\nabla u|^{2}=\frac{1}{1+e^{x_{1}^{2}}}>0,

\Sigma_{0}
is spacelike.

It follows that the future-directed unit timelike normal vector field is

{N}=\left(\dfrac{u_{x_{1}}}{\sqrt{1-u_{x_{1}}^{2}}},0,\ldots,0,\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-u_{x_{1}}^{2}}}\right).

Therefore,
the mean curvature of
\Sigma_{0}
is

H=-\frac{1}{n}{\rm div}N=-\frac{1}{n}\frac{u_{x_{1}x_{1}}}{(1-u_{x_{1}}^{2})^{3/2}}=-\frac{x_{1}e^{x_{1}^{2}/2}}{n}.

Since

\langle\nabla f,N\rangle=\frac{x_{1}u_{x_{1}}}{\sqrt{1-u_{x_{1}}^{2}}}=x_{1}e^{x_{1}^{2}/2},

H_{f}=H+\frac{1}{n}\langle\nabla f,N\rangle=0,

i.e.,
\Sigma_{0}
is
f
-maximal.

This example shows that we need an assumption in order to obtain a uniqueness result.

3
Calabi-Bernstein result

Let
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{G}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{1}
be the graph of a function
u({\textbf{x}})=t
over
\mathbb{G}^{n},
that is
f
-maximal, and

N
be the future-directed unit timelike normal vector field of
\Sigma.
Consider the smooth extension of
N
by translations along
t
-axis, that we will also denote by
N,
and the
n
-differential form
w
defined by

w=i_{N}dV.

Similarly as in the Riemannian case, we obtain the following,

Lemma 1
.

The form
w
is a Lorentzian calibration that calibrates
\Sigma,
i.e.,

1.

d(e^{-f}w)=0;

2.

For orthonormal spacelike vector fields
X_{i},\ i=1,2,\ldots,n,

|w(X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n})|\geq 1,
and the equality holds at a point if and only if
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
are tangent to
\Sigma
at such a point.

Proof.

1.

Because
\Sigma
is
f
-maximal,

\displaystyle d(e^{-f}w)

\displaystyle=d(e^{-f}i_{N}dV)={\rm div}(e^{-f}{N})dV

\displaystyle=(e^{-f}{\rm div}N-e^{-f}\langle\nabla f,N\rangle)dV

\displaystyle=-e^{-f}(nH+\langle\nabla f,N\rangle)dV=0.

2.

At any point
p
of the Lorentz Minkowski spacetime, we can find a tangent vector
\overline{N}(p)
such that
\{X_{1}(p),\ldots,X_{n}(p),\overline{N}(p)\}
is a frame
[
19
, page 84]
. Then we can write

N=\pm\cosh\varphi\overline{N}+\sinh\varphi u_{0},

for
\varphi\in\mathbb{R},
where
\langle N,u_{0}\rangle=0,\ \langle u_{0},u_{0}\rangle=1,
and the sign depends on the timelike orientation. Hence,

\displaystyle|w(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})(p)|=|dV(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},N)(p)|=|dV(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},\pm\cosh\varphi\overline{N})|=\cosh\varphi.

The previous equation clearly proves this part of the lemma.

∎

By Stokes’ Theorem, as in the case of
f
-minimal submanifolds
[
13
, Theorem 2.1]
, it can be proved that
\Sigma
is
f
-area-maximizing, i.e., any compact portion of
\Sigma
has largest area among all spacelike hypersurfaces in its homology class, the Lorentzian version follows analogously.

As a next step, we establish a comparison between the
f
-volume of
\Sigma
and the
f
-volume of the spacelike Lorentzian hypersphere

H_{r}^{+}=\{({\textbf{x}},t)\in\mathbb{G}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{1}:\ \langle\textbf{x},\textbf{x}\rangle-\langle t,t\rangle=-r^{2},\ t>0\}.

Lemma 2
.

If
|\nabla u|
is bounded away form 1, then for every
r>0
,

\Vol_{f}(\Sigma)\geq\Vol_{f}(H^{+}_{r}).

Remark 3
.

Because
\langle N,\textbf{e}_{n+1}\rangle=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}},
it follows that
|\nabla u|
is bounded from 1 is equivalent to the hyperbolic angle function
\theta
of
\Sigma
being bounded. This condition appeared in some Calabi-Bernstein type results for constant mean curvature spacelike hypersurfaces in Lorentzian warped products (see
[
5
]
, for instance).

Proof.

Figure 1:
For the proof of Lemma 2

Since the density does not depend on the last coordinate, the
f
-volume and
f
-mean curvature are invariant under translations along the
x_{n+1}
-axis. Therefore, we can assume that the origin
O\in\Sigma.

Let
{B^{n}_{R}}
be the
n
-ball in
\mathbb{G}^{n}
with center
O
and radius
R
and
{\cal C}=B^{n}_{R}\times\mathbb{R}
be the
(n+1)
-cylinder over
{B^{n}_{R}}.

Since
\Sigma
is spacelike,
\widetilde{\Sigma}:=\Sigma\cap{\cal C}
is bounded and lies in the slab between parallel hyperplanes
t=\pm R.
Let
\Omega
be the region bounded by
{\cal C},\widetilde{\Sigma}
and
\widetilde{H^{+}_{r}}:=H^{+}_{r}\cap{\cal C}
with inward-pointing orientation;
\widetilde{\partial\cal C}
be the part of
\partial{\cal C}
between
\widetilde{\Sigma}
and
\widetilde{H^{+}_{r}}
and
w
be the Lorentzian calibration constructed as above. By Stokes’ Theorem (note that, the directions of both
\Sigma
and
H_{r}^{+}
are future-directed), we obtain

0=\int_{\Omega}d(e^{-f}w)=\int_{\partial\Omega}e^{-f}w=\int_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}e^{-f}w-\int_{\widetilde{H_{r}^{+}}}e^{-f}w+\int_{\widetilde{\partial\cal C}}e^{-f}w,

or

\int_{\widetilde{H_{r}^{+}}}e^{-f}w=\int_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}e^{-f}w+\int_{\widetilde{\partial\cal C}}e^{-f}w.

By Lemma
1
,
\int_{\widetilde{H_{r}^{+}}}e^{-f}w\geq\Vol_{f}({\widetilde{H_{r}^{+}}})
and
\int_{\widetilde{\Sigma}}e^{-f}w=\Vol_{f}(\widetilde{\Sigma}).
Thus, we obtain the following estimate

\Vol_{f}(\widetilde{H_{r}^{+}})\leq\Vol_{f}(\widetilde{\Sigma})+\int_{\widetilde{\partial\cal C}}e^{-f}w.

Since
\displaystyle\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\Vol_{f}(\widetilde{H_{r}^{+}})=\Vol_{f}(H_{r}^{+})
and
\displaystyle\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\Vol_{f}(\widetilde{\Sigma_{R}})=\Vol_{f}(\Sigma),
so the remain we have to prove is
\displaystyle\lim_{R\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\widetilde{\partial\cal C}}e^{-f}w=0.

Let
\overline{\partial{\cal C}}=S_{R}^{n-1}\times\left[-~\sqrt{R^{2}+r^{2}},\sqrt{R^{2}+r^{2}}\;\right]
be the part of
\partial{\cal C}
between two parallel hyperplanes
t=\pm\sqrt{R^{2}+r^{2}}.
Because
\widetilde{\partial{\cal C}}\subset\overline{\partial\cal C}
and the density on
\overline{\partial{\cal C}}
is
e^{c-\frac{R^{2}}{2}}
(a constant), we get

\int_{\widetilde{\partial\cal C}}e^{-f}w\leq e^{c-\frac{R^{2}}{2}}\int_{\overline{\partial\cal C}}w.

Note that a unit normal vector field of
{\cal C}
is of the form
(\textbf{x},0),
where
|\textbf{x}|=1.
Therefore

\int_{\overline{\partial\cal C}}w=\int_{\overline{\partial{\cal C}}}\frac{\langle\nabla u,\textbf{x}\rangle}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}.

By the assumption
|\nabla u|
is bounded away form 1, we have

\int_{\overline{\partial{\cal C}}}\frac{\langle\nabla u,\textbf{x}\rangle}{\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}}\leq K\Vol(\overline{\partial{\cal C}})=2K\sqrt{R^{2}+r^{2}}\Vol(S^{n-1}_{R}),

where
K>0
is a constant.
Finally, we get the following inequality

\int_{\widetilde{\partial\cal C}}e^{-f}w\leq 2e^{c-\frac{R^{2}}{2}}K\sqrt{R^{2}+r^{2}}\Vol(S^{n-1}_{R}).

It is easy to see that, the right-hand side of the inequality goes to zero when
R
approaches infinity and therefore the proof of the lemma is complete.
∎

As a consequence, we obtain the following Calabi-Bernstein type theorem.

Theorem 4
.

In
\mathbb{G}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}_{1},
the graph
\Sigma
of a function
u(\text{\bf x})=t
over
\mathbb{G}^{n},
with
|\nabla u|
is bounded away from 1, is
f
-maximal if and only if
u
is constant, i.e.,
\Sigma
is a spacelike hyperplane.

Proof.

It is clear that, if
u
is constant, then
\Sigma
is
f
-maximal.

Note that
H_{r}^{+}
is the graph of the function
g(\textbf{x})=\sqrt{{\textbf{x}}^{2}+r^{2}},
so

\Vol_{f}(H_{r}^{+})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-f}\sqrt{\frac{r^{2}}{\textbf{x}^{2}+r^{2}}}dV_{{}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}.

It is easy to see that

\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\Vol_{f}(H_{r}^{+})=1.

Since

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\Vol_{f}\mathbb{G}^{n}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-f}dV_{{}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-f}\sqrt{1-|\nabla u|^{2}}dV_{{}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}=\Vol_{f}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\Vol_{f}(H_{r}^{+})=1,

we conclude that
|\nabla u|^{2}=0,
i.e.,
u
is constant.
∎
