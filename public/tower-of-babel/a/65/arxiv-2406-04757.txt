Hulls of Projective Reed-Muller Codes
Nathan Kaplan

Abstract
Projective Reed-Muller codes are constructed from the family of projective hypersurfaces of a fixed degree over a finite field 𝔽 q \mathbb{F}_{q} . We consider the relationship between projective Reed-Muller codes and their duals. We determine when these codes are self-dual, when they are self-orthogonal, and when they are LCD. We then show that when q q is sufficiently large, the dimension of the hull of a projective Reed-Muller code is 1 less than the dimension of the code. We determine the dimension of the hull for a wider range of parameters and describe how this leads to a new proof of a recent result of Ruano and San José (2024).

Introduction

Affine Reed-Muller codes are the multivariate analogues of Reed-Solomon codes. Codewords come from evaluating polynomials in
n
variables at the points of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, or equivalently, they correspond to affine hypersurfaces. There is an extensive literature studying these codes, including foundational results of Delsarte, Goethals, and MacWilliams
[
6
]
, Kasami, Lin, and Peterson
[
17
]
, and many others.

This paper deals with projective analogues of these codes, the projective Reed-Muller codes, where codewords correspond to hypersurfaces of a fixed degree
k
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. These codes also have an extensive history going back to early work of Lachaud who studied codes from hyperplanes and codes from quadric hypersurfaces in
[
18
]
, and determined the parameters of these codes in the case where
k<q
in
[
19
]
. Sørensen determined the parameters of these codes in general, described the dual of a projective Reed-Muller code, and studied when these codes are cyclic
[
28
]
. Berger analyzes the automorphism groups of these codes in
[
4
]
. More recently, there has been extensive interest in determining the generalized Hamming weights of these codes, and in determining Hamming weight enumerators of these codes in certain cases; see for example
[
2
,
3
,
8
,
16
]
.

The main focus of this paper is on studying the hulls of projective Reed-Muller codes. The
hull of a linear code

C
is defined by
\operatorname{Hull}(C)=C\cap C^{\perp}
. Three particular cases of interest are when
C
is
self-dual
, which means
C=C^{\perp}=\operatorname{Hull}(C)
, when
C
is
self-orthogonal
, which means
C=\operatorname{Hull}(C)
, and when
C
is
LCD (linear code with complementary dual)
, which means
\operatorname{Hull}(C)
is trivial. In addition to the huge literature of self-dual codes, self-orthogonal codes, and LCD codes (see for example
[
7
,
9
,
22
,
23
]
), there have been many recent papers studying hulls of families of codes more generally, some of which is motivated by connections to quantum error-correcting codes.

The CSS construction gives a method for constructing a quantum error-correcting code from a classical linear code, but this construction requires that the classical code be self-orthogonal. Mathematicians have worked around this self-orthogonality restriction by using
entanglement assistance
. This technique allows for the construction of a quantum code from a pair of classical codes
C_{1},C_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, and the dimension of the
relative hull of
C_{1}
with respect to
C_{2}
,
C_{1}\cap C_{2}^{\perp}
, plays an important role in analyzing the parameters.

Ruano and San-José have recently determined the dimension of the hulls of projective Reed-Muller codes over the projective plane
[
26
]
. In fact, they study intersections of pairs of projective Reed-Muller codes over the projective plane more generally. We will review these contributions in Section
2.4
. We give a different proof of one of their results in Section
4
. The results of our paper apply not only to projective Reed-Muller codes over the projective plane, but also to projective Reed-Muller codes over higher-dimensional projective spaces. We also note recent work of Gao, Yue, Huang, and Zhang
[
10
]
, and of Chen, Ling, and Liu
[
5
]
, on hulls of generalized Reed-Solomon codes.

Ruano and San-José investigate applications of their results to the construction of quantum codes with good parameters in
[
26
]
. They study the construction of quantum codes from projective Reed-Muller codes further in
[
27
]
, which focuses on what they call the
hull variation problem for projective Reed-Muller codes
. Instead of restricting themselves to the study of
C\cap C^{\perp}
where
C
is a projective Reed-Muller code, or even to
C_{1}\cap C_{2}^{\perp}
where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are projective Reed-Muller codes, they consider
C_{1,\ell}\cap C_{2}^{\perp}
where
C_{2}
is a projective Reed-Muller code and
C_{1,\ell}
is a code that is monomially equivalent to a projective Reed-Muller code. By varying the first code one can vary the dimension of the intersection, which is sometimes desirable for applications. The idea of varying the intersection by replacing one code with a code monomially equivalent to it also occurs in work of Anderson et al.
[
1
, Theorem 4.6]
, and in work of Guenda et al.
[
13
, Theorem 2.2]
. We do not consider these hull variation problems or the application to quantum codes in this paper and leave this as a direction for future work.

The main contributions of our paper are as follows. Building on Sørensen’s analysis of the dual of a projective Reed-Muller code, we completely determine when projective Reed-Muller codes are self-dual, when they are self-orthogonal, and when they are LCD. We then show that when
q
is sufficiently large, the dimension of the hull of a projective Reed-Muller code is
1
less than the dimension of the code. We then determine the dimension of the hull for a wider range of parameters and explain how this leads to a new proof of a recent result of Ruano and San José
[
26
]
. We give examples throughout the paper to illustrate our results.

Our paper consists of four sections. Section
2
describes preliminaries on projective Reed-Muller codes, the hull of a linear code, and duals of projective Reed-Muller codes. We prove our main results in Sections
3
and
4
. We end the paper by highlighting parameters for which our results do not determine the dimension of the hull, which suggests a natural direction for future work.

2.1
Projective Reed-Muller Codes

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},\dots,P_{N}\}
be a subset of points of the affine space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}

and let
\mathcal{V}
be a finite subspace of polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. Consider the evaluation map:

\displaystyle{\mbox{ev}}_{\mathcal{P}}:\mathcal{V}

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N}

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle(f(P_{1}),\dots,f(P_{N})).

The
evaluation code

\operatorname{ev}(\mathcal{V},P)
is the image of this map. The
affine Reed-Muller code
of order
k
and length
q^{n}
, which we denote by
{\mbox{RM}}_{q}^{A}(k,n)
, comes from choosing
\mathcal{V}
to be

\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq k}
, the vector space of polynomials of degree at most
k
, and
\mathcal{P}
to be
the set of all points in the affine space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. This is sometimes referred to as a
generalized Reed-Muller code
in the literature. Note that
{\mbox{RM}}_{q}^{A}(k,n)
depends on a choice of the ordering of the points of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. Recall that two linear codes over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
are
monomially equivalent

[
15
]
if one code can be obtained from the other by permuting the coordinate positions and multiplying each coordinate by a nonzero element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. It is clear that the affine Reed-Muller codes that arise from different choices are all monomially equivalent.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},\dots,P_{N}\}
be a subset of points in the projective space
\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
.
It does not make sense to evaluate an element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]
at a projective point, so we make a choice of affine representative for each, giving a set
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\{P_{1}^{\prime},\dots,P_{N}^{\prime}\}
with each
P_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n+1}\setminus(0,0,\ldots,0)
.
Let
\mathcal{V}
be a subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]_{k}
, the
set of homogeneous polynomials of degree
k
(including the zero polynomial). We
now define an evaluation code as in the previous paragraph. When
\mathcal{P}
consists
of all points in
\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
and
\mathcal{V}
is all of
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}]_{k}
, this construction defines the
projective Reed-Muller code of order
k
and length

N=|\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})|=(q^{n+1}-1)/(q-1),

denoted
{\mbox{RM}}_{q}^{P}(k,n)
.
In this paper, we write
C_{n,k}^{q}
for
{\mbox{RM}}_{q}^{P}(k,n)

and
C_{n,k}^{A,q}
for
{\mbox{RM}}_{q}^{A}(k,n)
. As in the previous paragraph, the projective Reed-Muller codes that arise from different orderings of the points of
\mathcal{P}
and different choices of affine representatives in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
are monomially equivalent. In this paper we follow the convention of Lachaud
[
18
,
19
]
and Sørensen
[
28
]
, where for each projective point we choose the affine representative for which the left-most nonzero coordinate is equal to
1
. Throughout the rest of this paper, whenever we write
C_{n,k}^{q}
we will use this choice of affine representatives.

To give one basic example, when
k=1
the projective Reed-Muller code arises from evaluating linear forms on projective space and one can see that
C_{n,1}^{q}
is a
q
-ary simplex code. Lachaud discusses this example, and
C_{n,2}^{q}
where codewords come from evaluating quadratic polynomials, in his initial paper on this topic
[
18
]
.

Lachaud determined the parameters of the code
C_{n,k}^{q}
in the case where
1\leq k<q
.

Lemma 2.1
.

(
[
19
, Theorem 2]
)

Assume that
1\leq k<q
. Then the code
C_{n,k}^{q}
has parameters

length

N=\frac{q^{n+1}-1}{q-1}
,
dimension

K=\binom{n+k}{k}
,

distance

D=(q-k+1)q^{n-1}
.

If
k\geq n(q-1)+1
, then
C_{n,k}^{q}
is trivial, that is, the whole space
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N}

[
28
, Remark 3]
.
For
1\leq k\leq n(q-1)
, Sørensen computed the parameters of
C_{n,k}^{q}
. For a recent discussion of Sørensen’s computation of the minimum distance and also for a characterization of the minimal weight codewords of
C_{n,k}^{q}
, see the paper of Ghorpade and Ludhani
[
11
]
, and also the short note of Sørensen
[
29
]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

(
[
28
, Theorem 1]
)

Assume that
1\leq k\leq n(q-1)
. Then the code
C_{n,k}^{q}
has parameters

\displaystyle{\mbox{{\rm length} }}N

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{q^{n+1}-1}{q-1},

\displaystyle{\mbox{{\rm dimension} }}K

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\equiv k\hskip-6.25963pt\pmod{q-1}\\
0<t\leq k\end{subarray}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n+1}(-1)^{j}\binom{n+1}{j}\binom{t-jq+n}{t-jq}\right),

\displaystyle{\mbox{{\rm distance} }}D

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(q-s)q^{n-r-1},

where

k-1=r(q-1)+s,~~0\leq s<q-1.

We remark that if
k<q
, then
r=0
and
s=k-1
so that

D=(q-s)q^{n-r-1}=(q-(k-1))q^{n-1}=(q-k+1)q^{n-1},

which matches the expression for
D
in Lemma
2.1
.

A different expression for the dimension of
C_{n,k}^{q}
is given by Mercier and Rolland
[
24
]
. See
[
11
]
for a discussion of the equivalence between these two expressions.

Lemma 2.3
.

(
[
24
, Theorem 4]
)

Assume that
1\leq k\leq n(q-1)
. Then
C_{n,k}^{q}
has dimension

K=\binom{n+k}{k}-\sum_{j=2}^{n+1}(-1)^{j}\binom{n+1}{j}\sum_{i=0}^{j-2}\binom{k+(i+1)(q-1)-jq+n}{k+(i+1)(q-1)-jq}.

We briefly recall some notation related to polynomials over finite fields. Let
F(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
. The
reduced form of
F(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
, denoted by
\overline{F}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
, is the polynomial we get by replacing any factor
x_{j}^{t_{j}}
, where
t_{j}=a(q-1)+b
with
0<b\leq q-1
, with
x_{j}^{b}
. That is,
\overline{F}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})
is the polynomial we get by reducing modulo
x_{j}^{q}-x_{j}
for each
j
.

Proposition 2.4
.

For
k
satisfying
1\leq k<n(q-1)
, we have
\dim(C_{n,k}^{q})<\dim(C_{n,k+1}^{q})
.

We could give a proof of this using the expression for
\dim(C_{n,k}^{q})
given in Lemma
2.2
, or the one given in Lemma
2.3
, but because these expressions are complicated in general we give a more combinatorial argument.

Proof.

In the proof of
[
28
, Theorem 2]
, Sørensen explains that the dimension of
C_{n,k}^{q}
is equal to the number of distinct elements
\overline{m}
where
m
is a monomial in
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
of degree
k
. We call a monomial
m

reduced
if all of its exponents are at most
q-1
. This is equivalent to
\overline{m}=m
. We see that
\dim(C_{n,k}^{q})
is equal to the number of reduced monomials in
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
of degree
k-j(q-1)
where
j
can take any value satisfying
0\leq j<\frac{k}{q-1}
.

Let
\mathcal{R}_{n,k}^{q}
denote the set of reduced monomials
\overline{m}
as
m
runs through all monomials of degree
k
in
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
. We will give an injective function
\varphi\colon\mathcal{R}_{n,k}^{q}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{n,k+1}^{q}
and then will prove that there exists an element of
\mathcal{R}_{n,k+1}^{q}\setminus\varphi\left(\mathcal{R}_{n,k}^{q}\right)
. This shows that
|\mathcal{R}_{n,k+1}^{q}|>|\mathcal{R}_{n,k}^{q}|
, completing the proof.

Define
\varphi\colon\mathcal{R}_{n,k}^{q}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{n,k+1}^{q}
by
\varphi(m)=\overline{x_{0}m}
. There are two cases to consider.

1.

First suppose that
x_{0}m=\overline{x_{0}m}
. This occurs precisely when the exponent of
x_{0}
in
m
is less than
q-1
. If
m=\overline{F}
for some homogeneous polynomial
F
of degree
k
, then it is clear that
x_{0}m=\overline{x_{0}F}
. Therefore,
x_{0}m\in\mathcal{R}_{n,k+1}^{q}
.

2.

Now suppose that
x_{0}m\neq\overline{x_{0}m}
. This occurs precisely when the exponent of
x_{0}
in
m
is
q-1
. In this case,
\overline{x_{0}m}=\frac{x_{0}m}{x_{0}^{q-1}}
and
\frac{x_{0}m}{x_{0}^{q-1}}
is reduced. As in the previous case, if
m=\overline{F}
for some homogeneous polynomial
F
of degree
k
, then it is clear that
\overline{x_{0}m}=\overline{x_{0}F}
. Therefore,
\overline{x_{0}m}\in\mathcal{R}_{n,k+1}^{q}
.

We have shown that
\varphi\colon\mathcal{R}_{n,k}^{q}\rightarrow\mathcal{R}_{n,k+1}^{q}
is injective. We now show that there exists an element of
\mathcal{R}_{n,k+1}^{q}\setminus\varphi\left(\mathcal{R}_{n,k}^{q}\right)
.

The definition of
\varphi
implies that the exponent of
x_{0}
is nonzero in every monomial in
\varphi\left(\mathcal{R}_{n,k}^{q}\right)
. Since
k+1\leq n(q-1)
, there is at least one monomial
x_{0}^{0}x_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}
for which
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=k+1
and
0\leq a_{i}\leq q-1
for each
i
. That is, there is at least one reduced monomial of degree
k+1
in which the exponent of
x_{0}
is
0
. This is an element of
\mathcal{R}_{n,k+1}^{q}\setminus\varphi\left(\mathcal{R}_{n,k}^{q}\right)
.
∎

The following fact follows immediately. We will apply it in our proof of Theorem
3.5
.

Corollary 2.5
.

Suppose
a
and
b
are integers satisfying
1\leq a<b\leq n(q-1)
. Then
\dim(C_{n,b}^{q})-\dim(C_{n,a}^{q})\geq b-a
.

2.2
The Hull of a Linear Code

Suppose
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})
and
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{N})
are elements of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N}
. Let
\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}y_{i}
. The
dual
of a linear code
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N}
is

C^{\perp}=\{y\in\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N}\colon\langle y,c\rangle=0\ \text{ for all }c\in C\}.

The
hull
of a linear code
C
is defined by
\operatorname{Hull}(C)=C\cap C^{\perp}
. A linear code is
self-dual
if
C=C^{\perp}
and is
self-orthogonal
if
C\subseteq C^{\perp}
. A
K\times N
matrix
G
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a
generator matrix
for a linear code
C\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N}
of dimension
K
if the rows of
G
form a basis for
C
. We recall a basic fact about the dimension of the hull of a linear code
C
.

Proposition 2.6
.

(
[
14
, Proposition 3.1]
)

Let
C\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N}
be a linear code of dimension
K
and let
G
be a generator matrix for
C
. Then
\operatorname{rank}(GG^{T})=K-\dim(\operatorname{Hull}(C))
.

In particular,
C
is self-orthogonal if and only if
GG^{T}
is the zero matrix and
C
is LCD if and only if
GG^{T}
is invertible.

There is a result like this one that can be used to determine the dimension of
C_{1}\cap C_{2}^{\perp}
when
C_{1},C_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N}
are linear codes that are not necessarily equal
[
13
, Theorem 2.1]
. We will not apply this result in this paper, but at the end of Section
4
when we discuss further directions for our work we will return to the role that this theorem would play in our strategy.

2.3
Duals of Projective Reed-Muller Codes

In order to study hulls of projective Reed-Muller codes, we need to understand the dual of a projective Reed-Muller code. It is well known that the dual of an affine Reed-Muller code is also an affine Reed-Muller code. More precisely,
C_{n,k}^{A,q}\ {}^{\perp}=C_{n,n(q-1)-k-1}^{A,q}
; see for example
[
6
]
. Since
\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq k}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]_{\leq k+1}
, it is clear that if
\ell=n(q-1)-k-1
and
k^{\prime}=\min(k,\ell)
then
C_{n,k^{\prime}}^{q,A}
is self-orthogonal. Therefore, in all cases we can determine
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{A,q})
.

The projective analogue of this situation is more complicated since it is not always the case that the dual of a projective Reed-Muller code is a projective Reed-Muller code. Sørensen computes the dual of a projective Reed-Muller code in
[
28
]
. Throughout this paper, we write
{\bf 1}=(1,1,\dots,1)
. We recall the convention that
C_{n,0}^{q}=\mbox{Span}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\{{\bf 1}\}
, since the homogeneous polynomials of degree
0
in
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
are the constant functions.

Theorem 2.7
.

(
[
28
, Theorem 2]
)
Let
k
be an integer satisfying
1\leq k\leq n(q-1)
and let
\ell=n(q-1)-k
. Then

(i)

{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}=C_{n,\ell}^{q}
for
k\not\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
.

(ii)

{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}={{\operatorname{Span}}}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\{{\bf 1},C_{n,\ell}^{q}\}
for
k\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
.

Remark 2.8
.

Suppose
k
is an integer satisfying
1\leq k<n(q-1)
and
k\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
. Let
\ell=n(q-1)-k
. Theorem
2.7
implies that
{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}={\mbox{Span}}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\{{\bf 1},C_{n,\ell}^{q}\}
. In these cases
{\bf 1}\not\in C_{n,\ell}^{q}
, so we have
C_{n,\ell}^{q}\subsetneq{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}
. Sørensen does not explain why
{\bf 1}\not\in C_{n,\ell}^{q}
, so we give a quick proof of this fact.

Suppose
{\bf 1}\in C_{n,\ell}^{q}
. Note that
\ell\equiv-k\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
and that
\ell>0
. At the end of the proof of Theorem
2.7
, Sørensen shows that
{\bf 1}\in{C_{n,\ell}^{q}}^{\perp}
. If
m
is a positive integer we have
m\cdot 1=0
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
if and only if the characteristic of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
divides
m
. However,

\langle{\bf 1},{\bf 1}\rangle=\frac{q^{n+1}-1}{q-1}\cdot 1=(q^{n}+q^{n-1}+\cdots+q+1)\cdot 1.

Since
q
is a power of
\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
it is clear that
q^{n}+q^{n-1}+\cdots+q+1\equiv 1\pmod{\operatorname{char}(\mathbb{F}_{q})}
. This implies
\langle{\bf 1},{\bf 1}\rangle\neq 0
in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, contradicting the fact that
{\bf 1}\in{C_{n,\ell}^{q}}^{\perp}
.

Now suppose
k=n(q-1)
. Since
{C_{n,n(q-1)}^{q}}^{\perp}={\mbox{Span}}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\{{\bf 1}\}
, the argument in the previous paragraph shows that
{\bf 1}\not\in C_{n,n(q-1)}^{q}
. This observation implies that
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,n(q-1)}^{q})
is trivial, or equivalently, that
C_{n,n(q-1)}^{q}
is an LCD code.

2.4
Hulls of Projective Reed-Muller Codes over the Projective Plane

Ruano and San-José study projective Reed-Muller codes over the projective plane in
[
26
]
. That is, they focus on the
n=2
case of our general study of the codes
C_{n,k}^{q}
. Ruano and San-José give a basis for
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{2,k}^{q})
for any
1\leq k\leq q-1
in the following form.

Theorem 2.9
.

[
26
, Corollary 3.12]

Let
1\leq k\leq q-1
and let
Y=\{0,1,\ldots,\min\{k-1,q-1-k-1\}\}
.

•

If
2k\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
, then
C_{2,k}^{q}\cap{C_{2,k}^{q}}^{\perp}=C_{2,k}^{q}
.

•

If
2k\not\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
, a basis for
C_{2,k}^{q}\cap{C_{2,k}^{q}}^{\perp}
is given by

A_{1}^{k}\cup\left(\bigcup_{a_{2}\in Y}x_{1}^{k-a_{2}}x_{2}^{a_{2}}\right),

where

A_{1}^{k}=\{x_{0}^{a_{0}}x_{1}^{a_{1}}x_{2}^{a_{2}}\colon a_{0}>0,\ a_{0}+a_{1}+a_{2}=k,\ 0\leq a_{1},a_{2}\leq q-1\}.

Consequently,

\dim(\operatorname{Hull}(C_{2,k}^{q}))=\binom{k+1}{2}+\min\{k,q-k-1\}.

For any
k
satisfying
1\leq k<2(q-1)
except
k=q-1
, Theorem
2.7
(i) implies that
{C_{2,k}^{q}}^{\perp}=C_{2,2(q-1)-k}^{q}
. If
k
satisfies
q-1<k<2(q-1)
, then
{C_{2,k}^{q}}^{\perp}=C_{2,\ell}^{q}
where
\ell=2(q-1)-k
satisfies
1\leq\ell<q-1
, and
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{2,k}^{q})=\operatorname{Hull}(C_{2,\ell}^{q})
. In this way, we see that Theorem
2.9
gives a basis for
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{2,k}^{q})
for all
k
satisfying
1\leq k\leq 2(q-1)
.

Theorem
2.9
follows from the results we prove in Section
4
. We emphasize that our proof is quite different than the one given by Ruano and San-José. A main idea in
[
26
]
is to view codewords of projective Reed-Muller codes as classes of polynomials in a quotient ring. They then compute bases of some subspaces of quotient rings associated to projective Reed-Muller codes by using techniques from commutative algebra. In particular, the authors work with a particular basis of the vanishing ideal of the collection of points of
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. Our proofs work directly with the linear algebraic setup of Proposition
2.6
, taking sums of polynomials evaluated at the standard affine representatives of the points of
\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
to determine the rank of the relevant matrix.

We note that Ruano and San-José actually consider the more general problem of determining a basis for
C_{2,k_{1}}^{q}\cap C_{2,k_{2}}^{q}
where
1\leq k_{1},k_{2},\leq 2(q-1)
. Theorem
2.9
comes from specializing to the case where
k_{2}=2(q-1)-k_{1}

[
26
, Corollary 3.11]
. In Section
4
we discuss how one could adapt our techniques to ammept to give a different proof of this more general statement about projective Reed-Muller codes with
n=2
.

3
Special Classes of Projective Reed-Muller Codes

Our goal is to understand the following question.

Question 3.1
.

1.

For which parameters
(n,k,q)
is the projective Reed-Muller code
C_{n,k}^{q}
self-dual?

2.

For which parameters
(n,k,q)
is
C_{n,k}^{q}
self-orthogonal?

3.

For which parameters
(n,k,q)
is
C_{n,k}^{q}
LCD?

4.

In cases where
C_{n,k}^{q}
is not self-orthogonal, what can we say about
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q})
?

In this section we answer the first three questions. We consider the last one in Section
4
.

We begin this section by applying Theorem
2.7
to answer the first part of Question
3.1
and determine when a projective Reed-Muller code is self-dual.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
1\leq k\leq n(q-1)
. Then
C^{q}_{n,k}
is self-dual if and only if
q
and
n
are odd and
k=\frac{n(q-1)}{2}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
q
and
n
are odd and
k=\frac{n(q-1)}{2}
. Then
k\not\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
because
n
is odd. Note that
n(q-1)-k=k
. Theorem
2.7
(i) implies that
{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}={C_{n,k}^{q}}
, proving that
{C_{n,k}^{q}}
is self-dual.

Conversely, suppose that
C^{q}_{n,k}
is self-dual. Then its length
N=q^{n}+q^{n-1}+\cdots+1
is even. If
q
is even, then
N
is odd. Hence
q
is odd.
We complete the proof by considering two cases.

•

Suppose that
k\not\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
. Then by Theorem
2.7
(i), we have

{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}=C_{n,\ell}^{q}=C_{n,n(q-1)-k}^{q}.

Proposition
2.4
implies that for any
1\leq k,k^{\prime}\leq n(q-1)
with
k\neq k^{\prime}
the codes
C_{n,k}^{q}
and
C_{n,k^{\prime}}^{q}
have different dimensions.
Therefore,
{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}=C_{n,\ell}^{q}=C_{n,k}^{q}
implies
\ell=n(q-1)-k=k
, that is,
k=\frac{n(q-1)}{2}
.
If
n
is even, then
k=\frac{n(q-1)}{2}\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
, which is a contradiction. Hence,
n
is odd.

•

Suppose that
k\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
. Then we have

{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}={\mbox{Span}}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\{{\bf 1},C_{n,n(q-1)-k}^{q}\}
by Theorem
2.7
(ii).
Since
C^{q}_{n,k}
is self-dual, we know that

C^{q}_{n,k}={C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}={\mbox{Span}}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\{{\bf 1},C_{n,n(q-1)-k}^{q}\}.

This implies that
{\bf 1}\in C^{q}_{n,k}
. By the remark following Theorem
2.7
, this is not possible. Therefore,
C^{q}_{n,k}
is not self-dual.

∎

We now answer the second the second part of Question
3.1
and determine the projective Reed-Muller codes that are self-orthogonal.

Theorem 3.5
.

Suppose that
1\leq k\leq n(q-1)
. Then
C_{n,k}^{q}
is self-orthogonal if and only if
1\leq k\leq\frac{n(q-1)}{2}
and
2k\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
1\leq k\leq\frac{n(q-1)}{2}
and
2k\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
. Since
2k\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
and
k\leq\frac{n(q-1)}{2}
, we have
\ell=n(q-1)-k=k+r(q-1)
for some
r\geq 0
.
By Theorem
2.7
,
C_{n,n(q-1)-k}^{q}\subseteq{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}
.

In order to prove
C_{n,k}\subseteq{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}
, it suffices to show that
C_{n,k}\subseteq C_{n,n(q-1)-k}^{q}
. For each monomial
m
of degree
k
, we construct a monomial
m^{\prime}
of degree
n(q-1)-k
such that
\overline{m^{\prime}}=m
. Let
x_{0}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}
be a monomial of degree
k
, so
0\leq a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}\leq k
and
\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}=k
. Let
j
be the smallest positive integer such that
a_{j}>0
. The evaluation map sends a polynomial and the reduced form of that polynomial to the same element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{N}
. More precisely,
x_{j}^{r(q-1)+a_{j}}=x_{j}^{a_{j}}
at every element of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. Therefore

\operatorname{ev}_{\mathcal{P}}(x_{0}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}x_{j}^{r(q-1)})=\operatorname{ev}_{\mathcal{P}}(x_{0}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}).

We see that the span of
\operatorname{ev}_{\mathcal{P}}(x_{0}^{a_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}})
as we vary over all monomials in
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
of degree
k
is a subcode of
C_{n,n(q-1)-k}^{q}
, completing the proof that
C_{n,k}^{q}
is self-orthogonal.

We now show that
C_{n,k}^{q}
is not self-orthogonal in all other cases. Suppose
\frac{n(q-1)}{2}<k\leq n(q-1)
. Let
\ell=n(q-1)-k
, so
0\leq\ell<\frac{n(q-1)}{2}
and
k-\ell=2k-n(q-1)
. We consider two cases.

1.

Suppose
k-\ell=1
. This implies
k=\frac{n(q-1)+1}{2}
, so in particular
q
is even and
n
is odd. Note that
2k=n(q-1)+1\equiv 1\pmod{q-1}
. If
q\neq 2
, then
k\not\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
and Theorem
2.7
(i) implies that
{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}=C_{n,\ell}^{q}
. Proposition
2.4
implies that
\dim({C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp})<\dim(C_{n,k}^{q})
, and therefore
C_{n,k}^{q}
is not self-orthogonal.

Suppose
q=2
. If
n=1
, then
k=1
. It is easy to check that
C_{1,1}^{2}
is not self-orthogonal, so we suppose
n>1
. In particular,
k=\frac{n(q-1)+1}{2}<n(q-1)
. Theorem
2.7
implies that
{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}={\mbox{Span}}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\{{\bf 1},C_{n,\ell}^{q}\}
. The argument in Remark
2.8
implies that
{\bf 1}\not\in C_{n,\ell}^{q}
, so

\dim({C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp})=\dim(C_{n,\ell}^{q})+1\leq\dim(C_{n,k}^{q}).

If
C
is a linear code for which
\dim(C^{\perp})\leq\dim(C)
, then
C
is self-orthogonal if and only if it is self-dual. But,
{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}
contains
{\bf 1}
, and the argument in Remark
2.8
shows that
C_{n,k}^{q}
does not. We conclude that
C_{n,k}^{q}
is not self-orthogonal.

2.

Suppose
k-\ell\geq 2
. By Theorem
2.7
,
{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}\subseteq{\mbox{Span}}_{\mathbb{F}_{q}}\{{\bf 1},C_{n,\ell}^{q}\}
, which implies
\dim({C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp})\leq\dim(C_{n,\ell}^{q})+1
. By Corollary
2.5
,
\dim(C_{n,k}^{q})-\dim(C_{n,\ell}^{q})\geq k-\ell\geq 2
. We conclude that
\dim(C_{n,k}^{q})>\dim({C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp})
, so
C_{n,k}^{q}
is not self-orthogonal.

Now suppose that
1\leq k\leq\frac{n(q-1)}{2}
and that
2k\not\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
. Let
G
be a generator matrix for
C_{n,k}^{q}
. We will show that
GG^{T}
has a nonzero entry. Applying Proposition
2.6
then shows that
C_{n,k}^{q}
is not self-orthogonal.

Choose a basis for
C_{n,k}^{q}
including the monomial
x_{n}^{k}
. Let
G
be a generator matrix for
C_{n,k}^{q}
for which the first row is equal to
\operatorname{ev}(x_{n}^{k})
. Throughout the rest of this proof, let
f(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n})=x_{n}^{2k}
. The top left entry of

is equal to
\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}f(p)
.

We recall the basic fact that for any positive integer

, we have

\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\beta^{r}=\begin{cases}-1&\text{if }r\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

(1)

Because of our choice of affine representatives
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
and the fact that
2k\not\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
, we have
\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}f(p)=f(0,\ldots,0,1)=1^{2k}=1
. Therefore,
GG^{T}
has a nonzero entry and

is not self-orthogonal.
∎

Corollary 3.8

We next characterize the projective Reed-Muller codes that are LCD. That is, we determine the
(n,k,q)
for which
C^{q}_{n,k}\cap{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}
is trivial.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
1\leq k<n(q-1)
. Then
C^{q}_{n,k}\cap{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}
is nontrivial. That is,
C_{n,k}^{q}
is not LCD.

Remark 3.11
.

As discussed at the end of Remark
2.8
,
C_{n,n(q-1)}^{q}
is LCD. Taken together with Theorem
3.10
we see that if
1\leq k\leq n(q-1)
, then
C_{n,k}^{q}
is LCD if and only if

.

Proof of Theorem
3.10
.

We prove that
\operatorname{Hull}(C)
is nontrivial by showing that
\operatorname{ev}(x_{0}^{k})\in C^{q}_{n,k}\cap{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}
. Since
x_{0}^{k}
is a monomial of degree
k
, it is clear that
\operatorname{ev}(x_{0}^{k})\in C_{n,k}^{q}
. We need only show that that
\operatorname{ev}(x_{0}^{k})
is orthogonal to
\operatorname{ev}(m)
for every monomial
m=x_{0}^{b_{0}}x_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{b_{n}}
where each
b_{i}
satisfies
0\leq b_{i}\leq k
and
b_{0}+\cdots+b_{n}=k
.

We compute that
\langle\operatorname{ev}(x_{0}^{k}),\operatorname{ev}(m)\rangle
is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}(x_{0}^{k}m)(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}1^{k+b_{0}}\alpha_{1}^{b_{1}}\alpha_{2}^{b_{2}}\cdots\alpha_{n}^{b_{n}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}0^{k+b_{0}}1^{b_{1}}\alpha_{2}^{b_{2}}\cdots\alpha_{n}^{b_{n}}

\displaystyle+\cdots

\displaystyle+\sum_{\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(0^{k+b_{0}}\cdots 0^{b_{n-2}}\right)1^{b_{n-1}}\alpha_{n}^{b_{n}}

\displaystyle+\left(0^{k+b_{0}}\cdots 0^{b_{n-1}}\right)1^{b_{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\alpha_{1}^{b_{1}}\alpha_{2}^{b_{2}}\cdots\alpha_{n}^{b_{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{\alpha_{1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\alpha_{1}^{b_{1}}\right)\left(\sum_{\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\alpha_{2}^{b_{2}}\right)\cdots\left(\sum_{\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\alpha_{n}^{b_{n}}\right).

For a nonnegative integer
b
, Equation (
1
) implies that
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\alpha^{b}
is
0
unless
b\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
and
b
is positive. Since
b_{1}+\cdots+b_{n}\leq k<n(q-1)
, it is not possible for each of the sums
\sum_{\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\alpha_{i}^{b_{i}}
to simultaneously be nonzero. We conclude that
\operatorname{ev}(x_{0}^{k})
is orthogonal to
\operatorname{ev}(m)
. Since
m
was an arbitrary monomial of degree
k
, we see that
\operatorname{ev}(x_{0}^{k})\in{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}
.
∎

4
Hulls of Projective Reed-Muller Codes

In the previous section we determined when projective Reed-Muller codes have certain special properties, that is, when they are self-dual, when they are self-orthogonal, and when they are LCD. For most parameters, the projective Reed-Muller code
C_{n,k}^{q}
does not satisfy any of these special conditions, but we would still like to understand the dimension of its hull. In this section we will determine the dimension of
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q})
when
k<q-1
. Replacing
C_{n,k}^{q}
with its dual and noting that for any code
C
,
\operatorname{Hull}(C)=\operatorname{Hull}(C^{\perp})
, we will also determine the dimension of
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q})
when
(n-1)(q-1)<k<n(q-1)
. We then consider the case where
n=2
and see how this recovers a result of Ruano and San-José about dimensions of hulls of projective Reed-Muller codes over the projective plane. At the end of this section we summarize the range of parameters for which our results determine the dimension of
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q})
.

We begin by determining
\dim(\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q}))
when
k
is not too large relative to
q
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose that
q>2k+1
. Then

\dim(\operatorname{Hull}(C^{q}_{n,k}))=\dim(C^{q}_{n,k})-1=\binom{n+k}{k}-1.

Moreover, a basis for
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q})
is given by
\{\operatorname{ev}(m)\}_{m\in\mathcal{M}}
where
\mathcal{M}
is the set of monomials of degree
k
in
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
except for the monomial
x_{n}^{k}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
2.6
it suffices to show that
\operatorname{rank}(GG^{T})=1
where
G
is a generator matrix for
C^{q}_{n,k}
. Since
q>2k+1
, Lemma
2.1
implies that
C^{q}_{n,k}
has dimension
\binom{n+k}{k}
. We take the lexicographic ordering of the
\binom{n+k}{k}
monomials of degree
k
in
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
. We write this ordered list of monomials as
m_{1},\ldots,m_{\binom{n+k}{k}}
. Note that
m_{\binom{n+k}{k}}=x_{n}^{k}
. It is clear that
\operatorname{ev}(m_{1}),\ldots,\operatorname{ev}(m_{\binom{n+k}{k}})
form an ordered basis for
C_{n,k}^{q}
. These choices determine a generator matrix
G
. We will prove that
GG^{T}
has a single nonzero entry, so in particular,
\operatorname{rank}(GG^{T})=1
.

The
(i,j)
-entry of
GG^{T}
comes from taking the sum of the evaluations of the monomial
m_{i}m_{j}
over the points of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
. Suppose
m_{i}=x_{0}^{a_{0}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}
and
m_{j}=x_{0}^{b_{0}}\cdots x_{n}^{b_{n}}
where
0\leq a_{0},\ldots,a_{n},b_{0},\ldots,b_{n}\leq k
and
\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}a_{\ell}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}b_{\ell}=k
. Recall that
0^{0}=1
denotes the empty product, so if
r
is a nonnegative integer, then
0^{r}=1
when
r=0
and
0^{r}=0
otherwise.

Our choice of
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
implies that the
(i,j)
-entry of
GG^{T}
is

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}(m_{i}m_{j})(p)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}1^{a_{0}+b_{0}}\alpha_{1}^{a_{1}+b_{1}}\alpha_{2}^{a_{2}+b_{2}}\cdots\alpha_{n}^{a_{n}+b_{n}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}0^{a_{0}+b_{0}}1^{a_{1}+b_{1}}\alpha_{2}^{a_{2}+b_{2}}\cdots\alpha_{n}^{a_{n}+b_{n}}

\displaystyle+\cdots

\displaystyle+\sum_{\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(0^{a_{0}+b_{0}}\cdots 0^{a_{n-2}+b_{n-2}}\right)1^{a_{n-1}+b_{n-1}}\alpha_{n}^{a_{n}+b_{n}}

\displaystyle+\left(0^{a_{0}+b_{0}}\cdots 0^{a_{n-1}+b_{n-1}}\right)1^{a_{n}+b_{n}}.

For any
i
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha_{i+1},\ldots,\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\left(0^{a_{0}+b_{0}}\cdots 0^{a_{i-1}+b_{i-1}}\right)1^{a_{i}+b_{i}}\alpha_{i+1}^{a_{i+1}+b_{i+1}}\cdots\alpha_{n}^{a_{n}+b_{n}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(0^{a_{0}+b_{0}}\cdots 0^{a_{i-1}+b_{i-1}}\right)1^{a_{i}+b_{i}}\left(\sum_{\alpha_{i+1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\alpha_{i+1}^{a_{i+1}+b_{i+1}}\right)\cdots\left(\sum_{\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\alpha_{n}^{a_{n}+b_{n}}\right).

Recall from Equation (
1
) that for any nonnegative integer
r<q-1
, we have
\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\beta^{r}=0
. For any
i,\ a_{i}+b_{i}\leq 2k
. Since
2k<q-1
, we see that

\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}(m_{i}m_{j})(p)=\left(0^{a_{0}+b_{0}}\cdots 0^{a_{n-1}+b_{n-1}}\right)1^{a_{n}+b_{n}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }m_{i}=m_{j}=x_{n}^{k}\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}.

We conclude that
GG^{T}
has a single nonzero entry that is equal to
1
, so in particular this matrix has rank
1
. The claim about the basis for
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q})
follows from the fact that every entry of
GG^{T}
except for the one in the lower-right corner is
0
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

The assumption
q>2k+1
plays an important role in the proof of Theorem
4.1
as
\sum_{\beta\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\beta^{q-1}=-1\neq 0
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Suppose that
n(q-1)-\frac{q-1}{2}<k\leq n(q-1)
and let
\ell=n(q-1)-k
. Then

\dim(\operatorname{Hull}(C^{q}_{n,k}))=\dim(C^{q}_{n,\ell})-1=\binom{n+\ell}{\ell}-1.

Proof.

For a linear code
C
, it is clear that
\operatorname{Hull}(C)=\operatorname{Hull}(C^{\perp})
. The assumption on
k
implies that either
k=n(q-1)
, or
k\not\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
. In the first case,
{C_{n,n(q-1)}^{q}}^{\perp}
is the
1
-dimensional code spanned by
{\bf 1}
. In Remark
2.8
we note that
{\bf 1}\not\in C_{n,n(q-1)}^{q}
, so the hull is trivial in this case. In the second case, Theorem
2.7
(i) implies that
{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}=C_{n,\ell}^{q}
. We see that
1\leq\ell<\frac{q-1}{2}
, which implies
q>2\ell+1
. Applying Theorem
4.1
completes the proof.
∎

With additional effort, the ideas that go into the proof of Theorem
4.1
can be adapted to compute the dimension of the hull of
C_{n,k}^{q}
for a wider range of parameters.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
\frac{q-1}{2}<k<q-1
. Then

\dim(\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q}))=\dim(C_{n,k}^{q})-(2k+1-(q-1)).

Moreover, a basis for
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q})
is given by
\{\operatorname{ev}(m)\}_{m\in\mathcal{M}}
where
\mathcal{M}
is the set of monomials of degree
k
in
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
except for the monomials
x_{n-1}^{k-a}x_{n}^{a}
where
q-1-k\leq a\leq k
.

Proof.

We follow the strategy from the proof of Theorem
4.1
. Since
k<q
, Lemma
2.1
implies that
C^{q}_{n,k}
has dimension
\binom{n+k}{k}
. We take the lexicographic ordering of the
\binom{n+k}{k}
monomials of degree
k
in
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
and write this ordered list of monomials as
m_{1},\ldots,m_{\binom{n+k}{k}}
. It is clear that
\operatorname{ev}(m_{1}),\ldots,\operatorname{ev}(m_{\binom{n+k}{k}})
form an ordered basis for
C_{n,k}^{q}
. These choices determine a generator matrix
G
. We will characterize the nonzero entries of
GG^{T}
. The form of this matrix will make it clear that it has rank
2k+1-(q-1)
, and moreover, will make it clear which monomials of degree
k
correspond to codewords of
C_{n,k}^{q}
that also lie in
{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}
.

Suppose
m_{i}=x_{0}^{a_{0}}\cdots x_{n}^{a_{n}}
and
m_{j}=x_{0}^{b_{0}}\cdots x_{n}^{b_{n}}
where
0\leq a_{0},\ldots,a_{n},b_{0},\ldots,b_{n}\leq k
and
\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}a_{\ell}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{n}b_{\ell}=k
. Following the reasoning from the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we see that the
(i,j)
-entry of
GG^{T}
is given by

\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}(m_{i}m_{j})(p)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\left(\left(0^{a_{0}+b_{0}}\cdots 0^{a_{i-1}+b_{i-1}}\right)1^{a_{i}+b_{i}}\left(\sum_{\alpha_{i+1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\alpha_{i+1}^{a_{i+1}+b_{i+1}}\right)\cdots\left(\sum_{\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\alpha_{n}^{a_{n}+b_{n}}\right)\right).

Equation (
1
) implies that
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\alpha^{a+b}=0
unless
a+b
is positive and
a+b\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
. In particular, this is zero unless
a+b\geq q-1
. Since
2k<2(q-1)
, there is at most one
i
for which
a_{i}+b_{i}
is be both positive and divisible by
q-1
. This implies that for any
i\leq n-2
,

\left(0^{a_{0}+b_{0}}\cdots 0^{a_{i-1}+b_{i-1}}\right)1^{a_{i}+b_{i}}\left(\sum_{\alpha_{i+1}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\alpha_{i+1}^{a_{i+1}+b_{i+1}}\right)\cdots\left(\sum_{\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\alpha_{n}^{a_{n}+b_{n}}\right)=0.

We see that

\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}(m_{i}m_{j})(p)=\left(0^{a_{0}+b_{0}}\cdots 0^{a_{n-2}+b_{n-2}}\right)1^{a_{n-1}+b_{n-1}}\left(\sum_{\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\alpha_{n}^{a_{n}+b_{n}}\right)+0^{a_{0}+b_{0}}\cdots 0^{a_{n-1}+b_{n-1}}1^{a_{n}+b_{n}}.

As in the end of the proof of Theorem
4.1
, we have

\left(0^{a_{0}+b_{0}}\cdots 0^{a_{n-1}+b_{n-1}}\right)1^{a_{n}+b_{n}}=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }m_{i}=m_{j}=x_{n}^{k}\\
0&\text{ otherwise}\end{cases}.

We see that
GG^{T}
has a
1
in its lower-right corner, which corresponds to
m_{i}=m_{j}=x_{n}^{k}
.

We now assume that we are not in this special case, meaning that either
m_{i}
or
m_{j}
is not equal to
x_{n}^{k}
. Therefore,

\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}(m_{i}m_{j})(p)=\left(0^{a_{0}+b_{0}}\cdots 0^{a_{n-2}+b_{n-2}}\right)1^{a_{n-1}+b_{n-1}}\left(\sum_{\alpha_{n}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}}\alpha_{n}^{a_{n}+b_{n}}\right).

This expression is zero unless
a_{0},b_{0},a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{n-2},b_{n-2}=0
. In order for this expression to be nonzero it is also necessary that
a_{n}+b_{n}=q-1
. Suppose
m_{i}=x_{n-1}^{k-a}x_{n}^{a}
where
0\leq a\leq k
. Then
\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime}}(m_{i}m_{j})(p)=0
if
m_{j}
is not equal to
x_{n-1}^{k-(q-1-a)}x_{n}^{q-1-a}
. This is a monomial of degree
k
if and only if
k-(q-1-a)\geq 0
, which is equivalent to
a\geq q-1-k
.

Each integer
a
satisfying
(q-1)-k\leq a\leq k
gives a nonzero entry of
GG^{T}
. Together with the entry in the bottom-right corner of
GG^{T}
, this gives
2k-(q-1)+1+1
nonzero entries of this matrix. Because we used lexicographic order for the monomials of degree
k
when defining
G
, the nonzero entries of
GG^{T}
are contained in a submatrix in the lower-right corner of
GG^{T}
. In this submatrix there are nonzero entries on the main antidiagonal, and then a single
1
in the lower-right corner. Since the last row and column of this matrix also contain nonzero entries corresponding to
\{m_{i},m_{j}\}=\{x_{n}^{k},x_{n-1}^{k-(q-1-k)}x_{n}^{q-1-k}\}
, we conclude that the rank of
GG^{T}
is
2k-(q-1)+1
.

Every monomial
m
that is not of the form
x_{n-1}^{k-a}x_{n}^{a}
where
(q-1)-k\leq a\leq k
has the property that the entire row corresponding to
m
in
GG^{T}
is zero. The number of such monomials is the dimension of
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q})
, so these monomials form a basis for
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q})
.
∎

The proof of the following corollary follows the same strategy we used to deduce Corollary
4.3
from Theorem
4.1
.

Corollary 4.5
.

Suppose that
(n-1)(q-1)<k<n(q-1)-\frac{q-1}{2}
and let
\ell=n(q-1)-k
. Then

\dim(\operatorname{Hull}(C^{q}_{n,k}))=\dim(C^{q}_{n,\ell})-(2\ell+1-(q-1))=\binom{n+\ell}{\ell}-(2\ell+1-(q-1)).

Proof.

By the assumption on
k
, Theorem
2.7
(i) implies that
{C_{n,k}^{q}}^{\perp}=C_{n,\ell}^{q}
. We see that
\frac{q-1}{2}<\ell<q-1
. Applying Theorem
4.4
completes the proof.
∎

We now describe how the results of this section give a different proof of Theorem
2.9
of Ruano and San-José. First note that if
k\in\left\{\frac{q-1}{2},q-1,\frac{3(q-1)}{2}\right\}
, then Theorem
3.5
and Corollary
3.8
determine
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{2,k}^{q})
.

Now suppose that
1\leq k\leq 2(q-1)
and
2k\not\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
. Theorems
4.1
and
4.4
together with Corollaries
4.3
and
4.5
determine
\dim(\operatorname{Hull}(C_{2,k}^{q}))
. When
\min\{k,q-k-1\}=k
, the formula at the end of Theorem
2.9
is equal to the formula in Theorem
4.1
,
\binom{k+2}{2}-1
. When
\min\{k,q-k-1\}=q-1-k
, since

\binom{k+1}{2}+q-1-k=\binom{k+2}{2}-(2k+1-(q-1)),

the formula from Theorem
2.9
is equal to the formula in Theorem
4.4
.

•

In the case where
k<\frac{q-1}{2}
, we see how the basis described in Theorem
2.9
consists of all monomials in
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}
of degree
k
except
x_{2}^{k}
, matching the result of Theorem
4.1
.

•

In the case where
k
satisfies
\frac{q-1}{2}<k<q-1
, we see that the basis described in Theorem
2.9
consists of all monomials in
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}
of degree
k
except those of the form
x_{1}^{k-a}x_{2}^{a}
where
a\in\{q-1-k,q-k,\ldots,k\}
, which matches the set of monomials described in Theorem
4.4
.

This completes our proof of Theorem
2.9
.

Ruano and San-José not only describe a basis for
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{2,k}^{q})
for each
q
, but more generally, they describe a basis for
C_{2,k_{1}}^{q}\cap C_{2,k_{2}}^{q}
where
1\leq k_{1}<k_{2}\leq 2(q-1)

[
26
, Theorem 3.9]
. Our results in this paper for
n=2
do not recover this theorem because we focus only on
C_{2,k}^{q}\cap{C_{2,k}^{q}}^{\perp}
. We briefly describe how one could try to prove this more general result using our techniques. In place of Proposition
2.6
, we would want to apply
[
13
, Theorem 2.1]
. This would allow us to determine a basis for
C_{2,k_{1}}^{q}\cap{C_{2,k_{2}}^{q}}^{\perp}
in terms of the nonzero entries of
G_{1}G_{2}^{T}
, where
G_{1}
is a generator matrix for
C_{2,k_{1}}^{q}
and
G_{2}
is a generator matrix for
C_{2,k_{2}}^{q}
. The entries of this matrix correspond to summing a monomial of degree
k_{1}+k_{2}
over a collection of affine representatives of the points of
\mathbb{P}^{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q})
. The difficult case would be when both
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
are large, for example, when
q\leq k_{1}
. This does match up with
[
26
, Theorem 3.9]
where there is a special case that did not arise in our analysis of
C_{2,k}^{q}\cap{C_{2,k}^{q}}^{\perp}
. We do not attempt to carry out this argument here.

We close this section by summarizing the cases where the dimension of
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q})
is not determined by the results of this paper.

•

When
1\leq k<\frac{q-1}{2}
, Theorem
4.1
determines the dimension of
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q})
.

•

When
n(q-1)-\frac{q-1}{2}<k\leq n(q-1)
, Corollary
4.3
determines the dimension of
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q})
.

•

When
\frac{q-1}{2}<k<q-1
, Theorem
4.4
determines the dimension of
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q})
.

•

When
(n-1)(q-1)<k<n(q-1)-\frac{q-1}{2}
, Corollary
4.5
determines the dimension of
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q})
.

•

When
1\leq k\leq\frac{n(q-1)}{2}
and
2k\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
, Theorem
3.5
determines the dimension of
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q})
.

•

When
\frac{n(q-1)}{2}\leq k\leq n(q-1)
and
2k\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
but
k\not\equiv 0\pmod{q-1}
, Corollary
3.8
determines the dimension of
\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q})
.

In all other cases with
1\leq k<n(q-1)
, Theorem
3.5
implies that
C_{n,k}^{q}
is not self-orthogonal and Theorem
3.10
implies that
C_{n,k}^{q}
is not LCD. Therefore,
1\leq\dim(\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q}))<\dim(C_{n,k}^{q})
, but we do not know the precise value of
\dim(\operatorname{Hull}(C_{n,k}^{q}))
.

A natural next case to consider is when
k
satisfies
q-1<k<\frac{3(q-1)}{2}
. It seems likely that one could carry out an analysis similar to the one given in the proof of Theorem
4.4
, but the details would be more complicated. This is the kind of thing that would be necessary in order to prove an analogue of Theorem
2.9
for projective Reed-Muller codes
C_{3,k}^{q}
corresponding to surfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. Investigating this further could be an interesting problem for future work.
